A sequential quadratic programming method for nonsmooth stochastic optimization with upper- 𝒞 2 \mathcal{C}^{2} objective Thanks: Submitted to the editors. LLNL release number LLNL-JRNL-846514.
Jingyi Wang, Ignacio Aravena, Cosmin G. Petra

Abstract
We propose a sequential quadratic programming (SQP) method that can incorporate adaptive sampling for stochastic nonsmooth nonconvex optimization problems with upper- 𝒞 2 \mathcal{C}^{2} objectives. Upper- 𝒞 2 \mathcal{C}^{2} functions can be viewed as difference-of-convex (DC) functions with smooth convex parts. They are common among certain classes of solutions to parametric optimization problems, e.g. , recourse of stochastic programming and closest-point projection onto closed sets. Our proposed algorithm is a stochastic SQP with line search and bounded algorithmic parameters and is shown to achieve subsequential convergence in expectation for nonsmooth problems with upper- 𝒞 2 \mathcal{C}^{2} objectives. We discuss various sampling strategies, including an adaptive sampling one, that can potentially improve algorithm efficiency. The capabilities of our algorithm are demonstrated by solving a joint production, pricing and shipment problem, as well as a realistic optimal power flow problem as used in current power grid industry practice.

Introduction

In this paper, we consider the class of nonsmooth nonconvex optimization problems in the form of

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}x\end{subarray}\in C}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle f(x)+r(x),

(1)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle c(x)=0,

where the objective function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and equality constraint function
c:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
are continuously differentiable with Lipschitz gradients and
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a convex compact set.
The stochastic part of the objective is given as
r(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[R(x,\xi)\right]
, where
the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\xi}
is the expected value of the function
R(x,\xi)
with respect to
\xi
.
The random vector
\xi
is defined over a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, where
\Omega
is the sample space,
\mathcal{F}
the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the subspace of
\Omega
and
\mathbb{P}
a probability measure on
\mathcal{F}
.
In this work, we focus on
r:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}

that is Lipschitz continuous and
upper-
\mathcal{C}^{2}
(see monograph
[
40
]
and Section
2
), but potentially nonsmooth.

Upper-
\mathcal{C}^{2}
functions are a subset of DC functions, and is equivalent to weakly concave functions on a convex compact set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

[
12
]
.
Any finite, concave function is upper-
\mathcal{C}^{2}

[
40
, Theorem 10.33]
, as are all continuously differentiable functions
[
40
, Proposition 13.34]
.
Moreover, a squared distance function to a closed set, which is
the value function of a minimization problem, is upper-
\mathcal{C}^{2}

[
40
, Example 10.57]
.
The property exists in many important two-stage stochastic programming problems with recourse of the form of (
1
)
[
42
,
4
,
19
]
,
whose first-stage objective includes an upper-
\mathcal{C}^{2}
value function
r
of the second-stage problem.
The application that motivated us to look into this property is
the security-constrained alternating current optimal power flow (SCACOPF) problem
[
10
,
37
,
36
,
35
,
34
]
, which can be stated as a stochastic two-stage problem.
In this case, the nonsmooth part of the first-stage objective
r
becomes upper-
\mathcal{C}^{2}
through regularization of the second-stage problems, if it is not already so
[
47
]
.
We add that upper-
\mathcal{C}^{2}
does not guarantee differentiability, or (lower) regularity (see Section
2
).

The most successful nonsmooth optimization methods include subgradient methods
[
44
]
, bundle methods
[
28
,
20
]
, and DC algorithms
[
1
]
.
In particular, many bundle methods have been shown to enjoy asymptotic global convergence with lower-
\mathcal{C}^{1}
or upper-
\mathcal{C}^{1}
objectives
[
24
,
23
,
41
,
31
,
18
,
32
]
.
For constrained problems, bundle methods typically incorporate constraints into the objective through penalty or filter methods so that the bundle approximation is constructed for the new objective
[
17
,
15
]
. The extension to stochastic optimization is challenging as the piecewise-linear cutting planes are no longer exact. However, if the error from the finite approximation to the stochastic objective is bounded, bundle methods have been proven to converge to proximity of certain optimality points with inexact information
[
33
,
17
,
32
]
.

Recently, a significant body of literature has been dedicated to research of (nonsmooth) stochastic optimization methods, where prominent algorithms include stochastic gradient descent
[
7
]
and stochastic subgradient descent
[
16
,
38
,
29
]
.
For constrained smooth stochastic problems, sequential quadratic programming (SQP) has been applied to stochastic objective and deterministic constraints, including both equality and inequality constraints
[
3
]
.
Nonsmooth stochastic DC algorithms (DCA) have garnered great interest as well, typically through combining DCA with sample average approximation (SAA)
[
25
,
22
,
26
]
.
The smoothing of the objective is explored in
[
21
]
, where a primal-dual regularization of the objective combined with SAA provides a solution to problems with certain nonsmooth risk measures.

In addition to SAA, adaptive sampling, which aims to improve efficiency of algorithms by adaptive sample size, is an active research topic for stochastic gradient descent, augmented Lagrangian, and SQP methods
[
8
,
2
]
.
The sample size is commonly controlled via variance in the stochastic approximation of quantities such as the gradient. In the unconstrained setting, a well-established norm test
[
8
,
9
]
can be used.
Its theory and application to smooth objectives on convex feasible regions has been shown in
[
2
]
.

To the best of our knowledge, algorithm development for stochastic nonsmooth constrained problems, particularly with adaptive sampling, has been limited.
In
[
47
]
, we described a nonsmooth SQP method for upper-
\mathcal{C}^{2}
objectives. The method uses line search on the constraints and trust-region update rule to achieve convergence with practical success in solving SCACOPF
[
46
]
. In this paper, we extend the effort to stochastic optimization problems. In the stochastic setting, the trust-region update rule and serious step criterion are no longer available without exact function values. We point out that since both DCAs and our stochastic SQP linearizes the nonconvex part of the objective, the reader can also view the proposed SQP algorithm as a version of DCA designed for constrained problems with upper-
\mathcal{C}^{2}
objectives.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we describe the mathematical notations and background necessary for the stochastic optimization algorithm.
In Section
3
, we propose an algorithm for a simplified version of problem (
1
)
where the equality constraints
c(x)=0
are neglected, making line search unnecessary. We provide subsequential convergence analysis
for the algorithm with probability 1, under assumptions typically made for stochastic and nonsmooth optimization.
In Section
4
, we expand the algorithm to the full problem (
1
) based on
a line search algorithm the authors previously developed
[
47
]
. We show subsequential convergence with probability 1 of the algorithm
to Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) points (points that satisfy KKT conditions) under proper assumptions.
In section
5
, we present adaptive sampling criteria in theory and implementation that could lead to more efficient results in practice.
Numerical experiments are shown in Section
6
to illustrate the practical capabilities of the proposed algorithm.
Finally, Section
7
concludes the paper and suggests directions for future research.

Background and notations

In this section, we discuss the mathematical background and notations necessary for the analysis in this paper.
First, the lower regular subdifferential of a function
r:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
at point
\bar{x}
,
denoted as
\hat{\partial}r(\bar{x})
, is defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\partial}r(\bar{x})=\left\{g\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\liminf_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\to\bar{x}\\
x\neq\bar{x}\end{subarray}}\frac{r(x)-r(\bar{x})-\langle g,x-\bar{x}\rangle}{\left\lVert{x-\bar{x}}\right\rVert}\geq 0\right\},

(2)

where
\left\lVert{\cdot}\right\rVert
is the 2-norm and
\langle\cdot\rangle
is the inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

[
40
]
.
For a sequence
\{x^{\nu}\}
, the notation
x^{\nu}\to x
means
\left\lVert{x^{\nu}-x}\right\rVert\to 0
as
\nu\to\infty
.
If there exists a sequence
\{x^{\nu}\}
such that
x^{\nu}\xrightarrow[r]{}\bar{x}
and
g^{\nu}\in\hat{\partial}r(x^{\nu})
with

\{g^{\nu}\}
converging
g^{\nu}\to\bar{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then

\bar{g}
is a lower general subgradient of
r(\bar{x})
. The f-attentive convergence, denoted as
x^{\nu}\xrightarrow[r]{}\bar{x}
, is

\displaystyle x^{\nu}\xrightarrow[r]{}\bar{x}\quad\ratio\Leftrightarrow\quad x^{\nu}\to\bar{x}\quad\text{with}\quad r(x^{\nu})\to r(\bar{x}),

(3)

and is trivially satisfied for
x^{\nu}\to\bar{x}
when
r
is Lipschitz (see 8(2),
[
40
]
for details).
The lower general subdifferential at
\bar{x}
is denoted as
\partial r(\bar{x})
.

A Lipschitz function
r
is lower regular (or subdifferentially regular) if and only if
\partial r(\bar{x})=\hat{\partial}r(\bar{x})

[
40
, Corollary 8.11]
.
Due to its importance, lower general subgradient is often simply called general subgradient,
while a lower regular function is called regular.
On the other hand,
upper regular subdifferential
[
40
,
27
]
is defined as

\displaystyle\hat{\partial}^{+}r(\bar{x})=

\displaystyle-\hat{\partial}(-r)(\bar{x})=\left\{g\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\to\bar{x}\\
x\neq\bar{x}\end{subarray}}\frac{r(x)-r(\bar{x})-\langle g,x-\bar{x}\rangle}{\left\lVert{x-\bar{x}}\right\rVert}\leq 0\right\}.

(4)

The upper general subdifferential is
\partial^{+}r(\bar{x})=-\partial(-r)(\bar{x})
.
A function
r
is called upper regular if
-r
is lower regular.

In nonsmooth optimization literature, both Clarke subdifferential
[
11
]
, denoted as
\bar{\partial}r{(\bar{x})}
of
r
at
\bar{x}
, and
\partial r(\bar{x})
have been widely adopted.
If
r
is lower regular,
\partial r(\bar{x})=\bar{\partial}r{(\bar{x})}

[
40
, Theorem 8.6, 8.49, 9.61]
.
The same holds for an upper regular
r
as well (for proof see
[
47
]
) and lets one use its Clarke subgradient and upper general subgradient interchangeably. Thus, in the remainder of the paper, we simply refer to Clarke subgradient as subgradient.
Clarke subdifferential is outer/upper-semicontinuous, which is necessary in establishing subsequential convergence
[
40
, Proposition 6.6]
.
In addition, for a Lipschitz
r
,
\bar{\partial}r(\bar{x})
is locally bounded
[
40
, Theorem 9.13]
.

Next, we discuss lower-
\mathcal{C}^{k}
, introduced in
[
45
,
40
]

with equivalent definitions in
[
14
]
.
A function
r:O\to\mathbb{R}
, where
O\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open, is said to be lower-
\mathcal{C}^{k}
on
O
, if on some neighborhood
V
of each
x\in O
there is a representation

\displaystyle r(x)=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in T\end{subarray}}{\text{max}}\ r_{t}(x),

(5)

where
r_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{k}
on
V
and the index set
T
is a compact space such that
r_{t}

and all of its partial derivatives through order
k
are continuous on
(t,x)\in T\times V
.
A function is upper-
\mathcal{C}^{k}
if we replace the
max
with
min
in (
5
).
Let
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
be compact, the function
r
is upper-
\mathcal{C}^{2}
if it can be expressed as

\displaystyle r(x)=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in T\end{subarray}}{\text{min}}\ p(t,x)

(6)

for all
x\in O
, such that
p(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and its first- and second-order partial derivatives in
x

are continuous on
(t,x)
. Clearly, upper-/lower-
\mathcal{C}^{k}
imply upper-/lower-regularity.

Equivalently, a finite-valued function
r
is lower-
\mathcal{C}^{2}
on
O\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if there exists
\rho>0
such that

r(\cdot)+\frac{1}{2}\rho\left\lVert{\cdot}\right\rVert^{2}
is convex. Notice that this definition is already given on
O
with a uniform
\rho
.
If
r
is upper-
\mathcal{C}^{2}
,
e.g.
,
r(x)=-|x|,x\in\mathbb{R}
, by definition,
-r
is lower-
\mathcal{C}^{2}
.
Through simple arithmetic and convexity with subgradients, we have

\displaystyle r(x)-r(\bar{x})-\langle g,x-\bar{x}\rangle\leq\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert{x-\bar{x}}\right\rVert^{2},

(7)

where
g\in\bar{\partial}r(\bar{x})
.
We refer to (
7
) as the upper-
\mathcal{C}^{2}
inequality.
Without loss of generality, (
1
) can be simplified to

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in C\end{subarray}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle r(x)=\mathbb{E}\left[R(x,\xi)\right],

(8)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle c(x)=0.

There are multiple optimality conditions used for nonsmooth constrained problems,
e.g.
,
stationary point, KKT point, critical point.
In this paper, we assume linear independent constraint qualification (LICQ, Section
4
)
at local minima and accumulation points of the proposed algorithms for (
8
), due to presence of both equality
and inequality constraints. This enables us
to establish the necessary optimality condition in terms of subgradient
[
11
, Section 6.4]
.
For problem (
8
), at a local minimum
\bar{x}
, there exists
\bar{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

\displaystyle 0\in\bar{\partial}r(\bar{x})+\nabla c(\bar{x})\bar{\lambda}+\bar{\partial}i_{C}(\bar{x})

\displaystyle,

(9)

\displaystyle c_{j}(\bar{x})=0,j=1,\dots,m,\ \bar{x}\in C

\displaystyle,

where the matrix
\nabla c(\bar{x})
is of dimension
n\times m
, and
\bar{\partial}i_{C}(\cdot)
is
the (Clarke) subdifferential of the indicator function
i_{C}(\cdot)
defined as

\displaystyle i_{C}(x)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if}\ x\in C,\\
+\infty,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(10)

Note that because
C
is convex, so is
i_{C}(\cdot)
.
We call a point that satisfies (
9
) a KKT point of (
8
).
Since (
9
) is defined with Clarke subdifferential, a point that satisfies it can be referred to as a Clarke stationary point
[
12
]
.

3
Stochastic optimization with convex set constraint

In this section, we focus on a simplified problem

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in C\end{subarray}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle r(x)=\mathbb{E}\left[R(x,\xi)\right],

(11)

which permits simpler algorithms and assumptions necessary for convergence.
Motivated by the discussion in section
2
, two assumptions are formalized below.

Assumption 1
.

The Lipschitz continuous objective
r
in (
11
) is upper-
\mathcal{C}^{2}
.

In particular, inequality (
7
) is satisfied.

Assumption 2
.

The feasible region
C
is convex and compact.

Here, we opt for a compact
C
instead of a closed
C
with bounded iterates
\{x_{k}\}
for ease of presentation.
A bounded
C
would ensure bounded
\{x_{k}\}
and given Assumption
1
guarantee a bounded
r(\cdot)
as well.
Therefore, there exists
r_{\min}
such that
r_{\min}\leq r(x),\forall x\in C
.
It is assumed that
x\in C
can be enforced in the optimization subproblems,
e.g.
, through projection onto
C
.

3.1
Algorithm description

At iteration
k
and its iterate
x_{k}
, the local approximation model to the objective with a true subgradient
g_{k}\in\bar{\partial}r(x_{k})
is

\displaystyle\phi_{k}(x)=r(x_{k})+g_{k}^{T}(x-x_{k})+\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{x-x_{k}}\right\rVert^{2},

(12)

where
\alpha_{k}>0
is a scalar quadratic coefficient.
In the stochastic setting, however, we use a stochastic subgradient estimate
\bar{g}_{k}
of
g_{k}
and the local model
\bar{\phi}_{k}(\cdot)
is

\displaystyle\bar{\phi}_{k}(x)=\bar{r}_{k}+\bar{g}_{k}^{T}(x-x_{k})+\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{x-x_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}.

(13)

The stochastic estimate of function value
\bar{r}_{k}
does not affect the optimization subproblem and its solution. Hence, we hereby use it interchangeably with
r(x_{k})
.
Denoting
d=x-x_{k}
,
\bar{\phi}_{k}(x)
and
\phi_{k}(x)
can be rewritten as
\bar{\Phi}_{k}(d)
and
\Phi_{k}(d)
, respectively,

\displaystyle\bar{\Phi}_{k}(d)=\bar{r}_{k}+\bar{g}_{k}^{T}d+\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2},\ \Phi_{k}(d)=r(x_{k})+g_{k}^{T}d+\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{d}\right\rVert^{2}.

(14)

The subproblem to be solved at iteration
k
and
x_{k}
is thus

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{k}+d\in C\end{subarray}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle\bar{\Phi}_{k}(d).

(15)

Meanwhile, the corresponding subproblem with true subgradient is

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{k}+d\in C\end{subarray}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle\Phi_{k}(d).

(16)

Let
\bar{d}_{k}
be the solution to (
15
) and
d_{k}
the solution to (
16
).
The predicted change on the objective is defined as

\displaystyle\bar{\delta}_{k}=

\displaystyle\bar{\Phi}_{k}(0)-\bar{\Phi}_{k}(\bar{d}_{k})=-\bar{g}_{k}^{T}\bar{d}_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}.

(17)

Similarly,
\delta_{k}=-g_{k}^{T}d_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{d_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}
.
The choice of
\alpha_{k}
is independent of the stochastic subgradient.
We rely on knowledge of
r(\cdot)
such as its Lipschitz constant
to select
\alpha_{k}
such that
\alpha_{k}
is large enough to ensure convergence in expectation, in line with stochastic optimization literature
[
3
]
.
The first-order optimality conditions of subproblem (
15
) are

\displaystyle\bar{g}_{k}+\alpha_{k}\bar{d}_{k}+\bar{v}_{k}=0,

(18)

\displaystyle x_{k}+\bar{d}_{k}\in C,\bar{v}_{k}\in\bar{\partial}i_{C}(x_{k}+\bar{d}_{k}).

The first-order optimality conditions of subproblem (
16
) are

\displaystyle g_{k}+\alpha_{k}d_{k}+v_{k}=0,

(19)

\displaystyle x_{k}+d_{k}\in C,v_{k}\in\bar{\partial}i_{C}(x_{k}+d_{k}).

The stochastic quantities are estimated at each iteration with independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables
\xi
.
We use
S_{k}
to denote the set of samples of
\xi
at iteration
k
with realizations
\xi_{i},\xi_{i}\in S_{k}
.
The cardinality or sample size of
S_{k}
is defined as
N_{k}=|S_{k}|
.

The simplified stochastic SQP is presented in Algorithm
1
.
While we require
\{\alpha_{k}\}
to be monotonically non-decreasing, in step

, for a simpler analysis, it is possible to relax this requirement as long as
\rho\leq\alpha_{k}\leq\eta_{\alpha}\rho
is maintained.
Additionally, the quadratic term
\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{d_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}
can be replaced by

, where

is a positive definite matrix. We leave the exact update rule for

3.2
Convergence analysis

In this section, we assume Assumption
1
,
2
are valid.
The choice of
\alpha_{k}
in step 7 of Algorithm
1
leads to the following Lemma.

Lemma 4
.

The iterate
x_{k+1}
from Algorithm
1

satisfies
r(x_{k})-r(x_{k+1})\geq\Phi_{k}(0)-\Phi_{k}(\bar{d}_{k})
.

Proof.

From the upper-
\mathcal{C}^{2}
inequality (
7
), we have

\displaystyle r(x_{k}+d)-r(x_{k})-g_{k}^{T}d\leq\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert{d}\right\rVert^{2},

(20)

for
g_{k}\in\bar{\partial}r(x_{k})
,
\rho>0
and
x\in C
.
Since
\alpha_{k}
is monotonically non-decreasing and
\alpha_{0}\geq\rho
, we have

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}\geq\rho,\ \text{for all}\ k\in\mathbb{N}.

(21)

The inequalities (
20
) and (
21
) imply that at

\displaystyle r(x_{k})-r(x_{k}+\bar{d}_{k})\geq

\displaystyle-g_{k}^{T}\bar{d}_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\rho\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}

(22)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-g_{k}^{T}\bar{d}_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}=\Phi_{k}(0)-\Phi_{k}(\bar{d}_{k}).

To further the analysis, the estimation error bound in expectation from sampling needs to be established.
In
[
25
]
, with no specific form of
\bar{\phi}_{k}(\cdot)
, the variance of the stochastic objectives is assumed to be bounded.
The variance bounds then comes from sample size

.
Similarly, in
[
3
]
, an unbiased estimate with bounded variance is made on the gradient with SQP.
In
[
43
]
, the Monte Carlo estimate of the objective is assumed to be unbiased and its variance uniformly bounded.
Given our choice of SQP, without specifying the sampling strategy, we make the assumption that the stochastic estimate of the subgradient is unbiased, and its variance is bounded.

Assumption 5
.

For all iterations

, the stochastic subgradient approximation
\bar{g}_{k}
is an

, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{k}[\bar{g}_{k}]=g_{k}

Furthermore,
\bar{g}_{k}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\left\lVert{\bar{g}_{k}-g_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq M_{k},

(23)

where

for all
k
is a sequence independent of stochastic solutions. Here,
\mathbb{E}_{k}
denotes the expectation over

\xi
conditioned on iterate
x_{k}
at the
k

Lemma 6

(24)

Assumption

Lemma 7

For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
of Algorithm

,
\mathbb{E}_{k}[\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}-d_{k}}\right\rVert]\leq\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}\sqrt{M_{k}}
, and

\mathbb{E}_{k}[\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}-d_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}]\leq\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}^{2}}M_{k}

Proof.

From the definition,
d_{k}
and

are the solutions to (

Subtracting the optimality conditions (

\displaystyle\bar{g}_{k}-g_{k}+\alpha_{k}(\bar{d}_{k}-d_{k})+\bar{v}_{k}-v_{k}=0.

(25)

Taking dot product of (

\bar{d}_{k}-d_{k}
and we obtain

\displaystyle(\bar{g}_{k}-g_{k})^{T}(\bar{d}_{k}-d_{k})+\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}-d_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}+(\bar{v}_{k}-v_{k})^{T}(\bar{d}_{k}-d_{k})=0.

(26)

Given that

\displaystyle\left(\bar{v}_{k}-v_{k}\right)^{T}(\bar{d}_{k}-d_{k})\geq 0.

Applying (

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}-d_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-(\bar{g}_{k}-g_{k})^{T}(\bar{d}_{k}-d_{k})\leq\left\lVert{\bar{g}_{k}-g_{k}}\right\rVert\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}-d_{k}}\right\rVert.

(29)

Taking
\mathbb{E}_{k}

The expectation of change in the objective
r(\cdot)
observes the following Lemma.

Lemma 8
.

The step
x_{k+1}=x_{k}+\bar{d}_{k}
satisfies, for constant

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{k}[r(x_{k})-r(x_{k+1})]\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{k}[\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}]-c_{r}M_{k}.

Proof.

From Lemma

\displaystyle r(x_{k})-r(x_{k+1})\geq

\displaystyle-g_{k}^{T}\bar{d}_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}.

(31)

By the optimality conditions (

\bar{g}_{k}+\alpha_{k}\bar{d}_{k}=-\bar{v}_{k},\ \bar{v}_{k}\in\bar{\partial}i_{C}(x_{k}+\bar{d}_{k}).

Taking the inner product with
-\bar{d}_{k}
in (

, we have

\displaystyle-\bar{g}_{k}^{T}\bar{d}_{k}-\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\bar{v}_{k}^{T}\bar{d}_{k}=i_{C}(x_{k})-i_{C}(x_{k}+\bar{d}_{k})-\bar{v}_{k}^{T}(-\bar{d}_{k})\geq 0.

(33)

Thus,

\displaystyle-\bar{g}_{k}^{T}\bar{d}_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}.

The right-hand side of (

\displaystyle-g_{k}^{T}\bar{d}_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=-g_{k}^{T}\bar{d}_{k}+\bar{g}_{k}^{T}\bar{d}_{k}-\bar{g}_{k}^{T}\bar{d}_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}

(35)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\bar{g}_{k}-g_{k}\right)^{T}d_{k}+\left(\bar{g}_{k}-g_{k}\right)^{T}\left(\bar{d}_{k}-d_{k}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\bar{g}_{k}-g_{k}\right)^{T}d_{k}-\left\lVert{\bar{g}_{k}-g_{k}}\right\rVert\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}-d_{k}}\right\rVert+\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\bar{g}_{k}-g_{k}\right)^{T}d_{k}-\frac{1}{2}(\left\lVert{\bar{g}_{k}-g_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}-d_{k}}\right\rVert^{2})+\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}.

By Assumption
5
, Lemma

and Lemma

, the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{k}
of (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[-g_{k}^{T}\bar{d}_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\geq

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(1+\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}^{2}})M_{k}+\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}\right].

(36)

Let
c_{r}=\frac{1}{2}(1+\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}^{2}})
. From (

From Lemma
8
, it is clear that to obtain a convergent step
\bar{d}_{k}\to 0
, the sequence

needs to be controlled so that the right-hand side of (

Fortunately, this can achieved as standard practice through increasing the sample size

as

.
We present the convergence result in the following theorem.

Theorem 9
.

If the sequence
M_{k}
satisfies

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}M_{k}<\infty
,
then

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\left\lVert{x_{i+1}-x_{i}}\right\rVert^{2}\right]<\infty.

(37)

It follows that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert\right]=0
and
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert{\bar{g}_{k}+\bar{v}_{k}}\right\rVert\right]=0
.

Proof.

Taking the total expectation of (
30
) in Lemma
8
, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[r(x_{k+1})-r(x_{k})]\leq-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}]+\mathbb{E}\left[c_{r}M_{k}\right]\leq-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{0}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}\right]+c_{r}M_{k},

(38)

since
\alpha_{k}
is monotonically non-decreasing and bounded.
Summing up
i=0,1,\dots,k-1
of
r(x_{i+1})-r(x_{i})
and taking the total expectation, we have

\displaystyle-\infty<r_{\min}-r(x_{0})\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[r(x_{k})-r(x_{0})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\left(r(x_{i+1})-r(x_{i})\right)\right]

(39)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{0}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\left\lVert{x_{i+1}-x_{i}}\right\rVert^{2}\right]+c_{r}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}M_{i}.

Let
b=r(x_{0})-r_{\min}
. Then, from the condition of the theorem,

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{0}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\left\lVert{x_{i+1}-x_{i}}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq b+c_{r}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}M_{k}<\infty.

(40)

The first part of the theorem is proven.
Further,
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}]=0
.
By Jensen’s inequality,
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert]=0
.
By first equation in (
18
), the last result is proved.

It is possible to further show subsequential convergence of accumulation points of
\{x_{k}\}
.

Theorem 10
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
9
, any accumulation point of the sequence
\{x_{k}\}

produced by Algorithm
1
is a KKT point of (
11
) with probability 1.

Proof.

We first show that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\bar{d}_{k}=0
with probability 1.
From Theorem
9
, we know

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}]
is finite and
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\bar{d}_{k}]=0
.
We proceed by contradiction. Suppose there exists
\epsilon>0
and
a>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\limsup_{k\to\infty}\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert\geq\epsilon)\geq a.

(41)

By Chebyshev’s inequality, we have
\mathbb{P}(\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert\geq\epsilon)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}]}{\epsilon^{2}}
.
Since
\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}]
is finitely summable, there exists
N>0
such that

\sum_{k=N}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert\geq\epsilon)\leq\sum_{k=N}^{\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}]}{\epsilon^{2}}<a
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\limsup_{k\to\infty}\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert\geq\epsilon\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\limsup_{k\to\infty:k\geq N}\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert\geq\epsilon\right)\leq\sum_{k=N}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert\geq\epsilon)<a.

(42)

This is a contradiction against (
41
). Hence, we have

\lim_{k\to\infty}\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert=0
with probability 1.
Additionally, under the condition of Theorem
9
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert{\bar{g}_{k}-g_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}]<\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}M_{k}<\infty.

(43)

Using the same contradiction argument, we have
\lim_{k\to\infty}\bar{g}_{k}-g_{k}=0
with probability 1.

Let
\bar{x}
be an accumulation point of
\{x_{k}\}
. Then, passing on to a subsequence if necessary, we can assume
\lim_{k\to\infty}x_{k}=\bar{x}
, where
\bar{x}\in C
.
From (
18
), we have

\displaystyle\bar{g}_{k}+\alpha_{k}\bar{d_{k}}+\bar{v}_{k}=g_{k}+(\bar{g}_{k}-g_{k})+\alpha_{k}\bar{d}_{k}+\bar{v}_{k}=0.

(44)

Since
g_{k}\in\bar{\partial}r(x_{k})
is bounded, there exists at least one accumulation point for
\{g_{k}\}
. Passing on further to a subsequence if necessary, we may assume
g_{k}\to\bar{g}
. By the outer semicontinuity of Clark subdifferential, we have
\bar{g}\in\bar{\partial}r(\bar{x})
.
Therefore, (
44
) implies that with probability 1,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\bar{v}_{k}=-\bar{g}
.
Since
\bar{v}_{k}\in\bar{\partial}i_{C}(x_{k}+\bar{d}_{k})
, by the outer semicontinuity of
\bar{\partial}i_{C}(\cdot)
,
-\bar{g}\in\bar{\partial}i_{C}(\bar{x})
.
Thus,
0\in\bar{\partial}r(\bar{x})+\bar{\partial}i_{C}(\bar{x})
,
and
\bar{x}
is a KKT point with probability 1.

Stochastic optimization with equality constraint

We now turn to the full problem (

In addition to Assumptions

and

Assumption 12
.

The function
c(\cdot)
is continuously differentiable with Lipschitz continuous gradient. This means that there exists a constant
H\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle|c_{j}(x^{\prime})-c_{j}(x)-\nabla c_{j}(x)^{T}(x^{\prime}-x)|\leq

\displaystyle\frac{H}{2}\left\lVert{x-x^{\prime}}\right\rVert^{2},

(45)

for all
x,x^{\prime}\in C
and all
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\}

[
30
]
.

4.1
Algorithm description

The algorithm remains an iterative method with locally approximated convex quadratic objective.
At iteration
k
and its iterate
x_{k}
, the true model
\phi_{k}(\cdot)
is the same as
given in (
12
) while the stochastic model
\bar{\phi}_{k}(\cdot)
is (
13
).
Denoting
d=x-x_{k}
,
\phi_{k}(x)
can be rewritten as
\Phi_{k}(d)
,
while its stochastic counterpart is
\bar{\Phi}_{k}(d)
in (
14
).
Furthermore, the constraint
c(x)=0
in (
8
) is linearized.
The subproblem to be solved at iteration
k
is

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{k}+d\in C\end{subarray}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle r(x_{k})+\bar{g}_{k}^{T}d+\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{d}\right\rVert^{2}

(46)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle c(x_{k})+\nabla c(x_{k})^{T}d=0.

The corresponding subproblem with the true subgradient is

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{k}+d\in C\end{subarray}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle r(x_{k})+g_{k}^{T}d+\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{d}\right\rVert^{2}

(47)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle c(x_{k})+\nabla c(x_{k})^{T}d=0.

To measure progress in both the objective and constraints,
a
\ell_{1}
merit function is adopted in the form of

\displaystyle\varphi(x,\theta_{k})=r(x)+\theta_{k}\left\lVert{c(x)}\right\rVert_{1},

(48)

where
\left\lVert{\cdot}\right\rVert_{1}
is the 1-norm and
\theta_{k}>0
is a penalty parameter.
A line search step is needed in order to ensure progress
in the merit function (
48
).
The predicted change
\bar{\delta}_{k}
on the objective is again (
17
).
Let the line search step size for
\bar{d}_{k}
be
\bar{\beta}_{k}\in(0,1]
. Then, the
(k+1)
-th step taken is given as

x_{k+1}=x_{k}+\bar{\beta}_{k}\bar{d}_{k}
.
By letting
\bar{\delta}_{k}^{\beta}=\bar{\Phi}_{k}(0)-\bar{\Phi}_{k}(\bar{\beta}_{k}\bar{d}_{k})
, we have

\displaystyle\bar{\delta}_{k}^{\beta}=

\displaystyle\bar{\Phi}_{k}(0)-\bar{\Phi}_{k}(\bar{\beta}_{k}\bar{d}_{k})=-\bar{\beta}_{k}\bar{g}_{k}^{T}\bar{d}_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\bar{\beta}_{k}^{2}\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}.

(49)

The first-order optimality conditions of subproblem (
46
) are

\displaystyle\bar{g}_{k}+\alpha_{k}\bar{d}_{k}+\nabla c(x_{k})\bar{\lambda}^{k+1}+\bar{v}_{k}=0,

(50)

\displaystyle c(x_{k})+\nabla c(x_{k})^{T}\bar{d}_{k}=0,

\displaystyle x_{k},x_{k}+\bar{d}_{k}\in C,~\bar{v}_{k}\in\bar{\partial}i_{C}(x_{k}+\bar{d}_{k}),

where
\bar{\lambda}^{k+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the Lagrange multiplier for the equality constraint.
Similarly, the optimality conditions for (
47
) are

\displaystyle g_{k}+\alpha_{k}d_{k}+\nabla c(x_{k})\lambda^{k+1}+v_{k}=0,

(51)

\displaystyle c(x_{k})+\nabla c(x_{k})^{T}d_{k}=0,

\displaystyle x_{k},x_{k}+d_{k}\in C,~v_{k}\in\bar{\partial}i_{C}(x_{k}+d_{k}).

Due to the presence of both equality and inequality constraints, additional step size control is necessary to guarantee
convergence. Therefore, at
x_{k}
, the line search condition is presented and
executed with a scalar
\bar{\zeta}_{k}\in(0,1]
, and the actual step size is first set to
\bar{\beta}_{k}=\nu_{k}\bar{\zeta}_{k}
.
The sequence
\{\nu_{k}\}\subset(0,1]
is user-defined and deterministic.
The line search condition with
\bar{d}_{k}
and
\bar{\theta}_{k}
is

\displaystyle\bar{\theta}_{k}\left\lVert{c(x_{k})}\right\rVert_{1}-\bar{\zeta}_{k}\left|(\bar{\lambda}^{k+1})^{T}c(x_{k})\right|

\displaystyle\geq\bar{\theta}_{k}\left\lVert{c(x_{k}+\bar{\zeta}_{k}\bar{d}_{k})}\right\rVert_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\eta_{\beta}\alpha_{k}\bar{\zeta}_{k}\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}.

(52)

where
\eta_{\beta}\in(0,1)
is a parameter of the algorithm.
In addition, a user-defined upper bound on
\bar{\beta}_{k}
needs to be imposed to eventually force
\bar{\beta}_{k}
to be sufficiently small while having minimal variance. To that end, by using the ceiling function
\lceil\cdot\rceil
, which returns the least integer greater than the input, we define at iteration
k

\displaystyle\bar{\pi}_{k}:=\min\left\{1,\frac{1}{2}^{\lceil\log_{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\eta_{\beta}\alpha_{k}}{H\bar{\theta}_{k}m}\rceil}\right\}.

(53)

The
\frac{1}{2}
in definition (
53
) comes from our choice of reduction ratio for
\bar{\zeta}_{k}
in step 7 of the algorithm. It can easily be substituted with another number between
0
and
1
.
We will show later that
\bar{\zeta}_{k}=\bar{\pi}_{k}
satisfies the line search condition in Lemma
16
.
The upper bound on
\bar{\beta}_{k}
is set to
\nu_{k}(\bar{\pi}_{k}+\mu_{k})
, where
\{\mu_{k}\}\subset[0,1]
is another user-defined sequence, similar to
\nu_{k}
.
The parameters
\eta_{\beta}
,
\{\nu_{k}\}
and
\{\mu_{k}\}
help control the stochastic step size
\bar{\beta}_{k}
and its variance to ensure decrease in the merit function value in expectation, which in turn is critical for convergence.
The constant
H
in (
53
) needs to be estimated for the constraints when it is not known, similar to
\rho
.

Our proposed stochastic nonsmooth SQP is presented in Algorithm
2
.
An adaptive sampling criterion and its implementation is discussed in Section

.

\displaystyle-\bar{\beta}_{k}g_{k}^{T}\bar{d}_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\rho\bar{\beta}_{k}^{2}\left\lVert{\bar{d}_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}

7
Conclusions

In this paper, we have proposed and analyzed SQP algorithms for constrained stochastic nonsmooth nonconvex optimization problems
with upper-
\mathcal{C}^{2}
objectives. A range of important problems fit the mathematical setup, particularly two-stage stochastic optimization problems.
The proposed algorithms help to fill the gap of nonsmooth stochastic optimization algorithms with adaptive sampling. Furthermore, problems with and without smooth equality constraints are discussed separately with two distinct algorithms.
Both algorithms formulate a convex quadratic programming subproblem at each iteration based on
stochastic approximation of the objective. The equality constrained problem requires additional, carefully designed line search to ensure overall progress.
Subsequential convergence analysis of the proposed pair of algorithms with respect to expectation is provided using widely adopted assumptions.
The adaptive sampling algorithm can be used in practice to improve efficiency of the algorithm.
