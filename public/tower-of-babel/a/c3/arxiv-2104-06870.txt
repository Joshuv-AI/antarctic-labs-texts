Geometrical and topological properties of transmission resonance and artificial mirage
Youjun Deng, Hongyu Liu, Xianchao Wang, Wei Wu

Abstract
. Transmission eigenfunctions are certain interior resonant modes that are of central importance to the wave scattering theory. In this paper, we present the discovery of novel global rigidity properties of the transmission eigenfunctions associated with the Maxwell system. It is shown that the transmission eigenfunctions carry the geometrical and topological information of the underlying domain. We present both analytical and numerical results of these intriguing rigidity properties. As an interesting application, we propose an illusion scheme of artificially generating a mirage image of any given optical object. Keywords: electromagnetic scattering; Maxwell system; transmission eigenfunctions; surface localization; topological structure; artificial mirage 2010 Mathematics Subject Classification: 35P25, 58J50, 35R30, 78A40



1.
Introduction

We are concerned with the spectral geometry of the interior transmission eigenvalue problem arising in the time-harmonic electromagnetic (EM) scattering described by the Maxwell system. Let
\Omega
be a bounded Lipschitz domain in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that
\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega}
is connected. Let
\varepsilon
and
\mu
be bounded positive functions such that
\mathrm{supp}(1-\varepsilon)\cup\mathrm{supp}(1-\mu)\subset\Omega
. In the physical context,
\varepsilon
and
\mu
are the optical parameters of the space medium, and respectively signify the electric permittivity and magnetic permeability. Throughout, we assume that
\mu\equiv 1
. Consider a pair of incident EM waves
(\mathbf{E}^{i},\mathbf{H}^{i})
that are entire solutions to the following Maxwell system:

(1.1)

\nabla\wedge\mathbf{E}^{i}-\mathrm{i}k\mathbf{H}^{i}=\mathbf{0},\quad\nabla\wedge\mathbf{H}^{i}+\mathrm{i}k\mathbf{E}^{i}=\mathbf{0}\quad\mbox{in}\ \ \mathbb{R}^{3},

where
k\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\mathrm{i}:=\sqrt{-1}
. Here,
k
signifies the wavenumber of the EM wave propagation. The EM inhomogeneity
(\Omega,\varepsilon)
interrupts the incident fields and leads to the scattered fields
\mathbf{E}^{s}
and
\mathbf{H}^{s}
. Let
\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{E}^{i}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{E}^{s}(\mathbf{x})
and
\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{H}^{i}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{H}^{s}(\mathbf{x})
denote the total electric and magnetic fields, respectively. The EM scattering is governed by the following Maxwell system:

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &~~\nabla\wedge\mathbf{E}-\mathrm{i}k\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{0},&&\mbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{3},\\
&~~\nabla\wedge\mathbf{H}+\mathrm{i}k\varepsilon\mathbf{E}=\mathbf{0}&&\mbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{3},\\
&\lim_{|{\mathbf{x}}|\rightarrow\infty}\left(\mathbf{H}^{s}\wedge{\mathbf{x}}-|{\mathbf{x}}|\mathbf{E}^{s}\right)={\mathbf{0}}.\end{aligned}\right.

The last limit in (
1.2
) is known as the Silver-Müller radiation condition, which characterizes the outgoing nature of the scattered fields and holds uniformly in the angular variable
\hat{\mathbf{x}}:=\mathbf{x}/|\mathbf{x}|\in\mathbb{S}^{2}:=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3};|\mathbf{x}|=1\right\}
. The well-posedness of the scattering problem (
1.2
) can be conveniently found in
[
25
,
28
]
. There exists a unique pair of solutions
({\mathbf{E}},{\mathbf{H}})\in

H_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathrm{curl},\mathbb{R}^{3})\times H_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathrm{curl},\mathbb{R}^{3})
which admits the following asymptotic expansions as
|\mathbf{x}|\rightarrow\infty
:

(1.3)

\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{Q}^{i}(\mathbf{x})+\frac{{e}^{\mathrm{i}k|\mathbf{x}|}}{|\mathbf{x}|}\mathbf{Q}_{\infty}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\mathbf{x}|^{2}}\right),\quad\mathbf{Q}:=\mathbf{E},\mathbf{H}.

The functions
\mathbf{E}_{\infty}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})
and
\mathbf{H}_{\infty}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})
in (
1.3
) are respectively referred to as the electric and magnetic far field patterns, and satisfy the following one-to-one correspondence,

\mathbf{E}_{\infty}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})=-\hat{\mathbf{x}}\wedge\mathbf{H}_{\infty}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathbf{H}_{\infty}(\mathbf{x})=\hat{\mathbf{x}}\wedge\mathbf{E}_{\infty}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}),\quad\forall\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}.

We next consider a particular case that non-scattering, a.k.a invisibility, occurs, namely
\mathbf{Q}_{\infty}\equiv\mathbf{0}
. In such a case, by Rellich’s Theorem
[
15
]
, one has
\mathbf{Q}^{s}=\mathbf{0}
in
\mathbf{R}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega}
. With such an observation, one can directly verify that
\mathbf{Q}_{1}=\mathbf{Q}|_{\Omega}
and
\mathbf{Q}_{2}=\mathbf{Q}^{i}|_{\Omega}
fulfil the following PDE system:

(1.4)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\nabla\wedge\mathbf{E}_{1}-\mathrm{i}k\mathbf{H}_{1}=\mathbf{0},&&\nabla\wedge\mathbf{H}_{1}+\mathrm{i}k\varepsilon\mathbf{E}_{1}=\mathbf{0}\quad&&\mbox{in}\ \ \Omega,\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\\
&\nabla\wedge\mathbf{E}_{2}-\mathrm{i}k\mathbf{H}_{2}=\mathbf{0},&&\nabla\wedge\mathbf{H}_{2}+\mathrm{i}k\mathbf{E}_{2}=\mathbf{0}\quad&&\mbox{in}\ \ \Omega,\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\\
&\nu\wedge\mathbf{E}_{1}=\nu\wedge\mathbf{E}_{2},&&\nu\wedge\mathbf{H}_{1}=\nu\wedge\mathbf{H}_{2}\quad&&\mbox{on}\ \ \partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

where and also in what follows,
\nu
signifies the exterior unit normal vector to
\partial\Omega
. The system (
1.4
) is referred to as the Maxwell transmission eigenvalue problem. It is clear that
\mathbf{E}_{j}=\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{H}_{j}=\mathbf{0}
,
j=1,2
, are trivial solutions. If there exist nontrivial solutions,
(\mathbf{E}_{j},\mathbf{H}_{j})_{j=1,2}
are called the transmission eigenfunctions and
k
is the associated transmission eigenvalue. In this paper, we shall be mainly concerned with real transmission eigenvalues. Hence, if no scattering/invisibility occurs, then the restrictions of the incident and total EM fields are transmission eigenfunctions and the wavenumber is an eigenvalue. After eliminating
\mathbf{H}_{j}
,
j=1,2
, in (
1.4
), we have the following reduced formulation of the transmission eigenvalue problem for
\mathbf{E}_{j}\in H(\mathrm{curl},\Omega)
,
j=1,2
:

(1.5)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\nabla\wedge(\nabla\wedge\mathbf{E}_{1})-k^{2}\varepsilon\mathbf{E}_{1}=\mathbf{0},&&\nabla\cdot(\varepsilon\mathbf{E}_{1})=\mathbf{0}&&\mbox{in}\ \ \Omega,\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\\
&\nabla\wedge(\nabla\wedge\mathbf{E}_{2})-k^{2}\mathbf{E}_{2}=\mathbf{0},&&\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}_{2}=\mathbf{0}&&\mbox{in}\ \ \Omega,\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\\
&\nu\wedge\mathbf{E}_{1}=\nu\wedge\mathbf{E}_{2},&&\nu\wedge(\nabla\wedge\mathbf{E}_{1})=\nu\wedge(\nabla\wedge\mathbf{E}_{2})&&\mbox{on}\ \ \partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

For a convenient reference, if one considers the transverse EM scattering (cf.
[
17
]
), one can deduce the following transmission eigenvalue problem associated with the Helmholtz equation in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
:

(1.6)

\Delta w+k^{2}\mathbf{n}^{2}w=0,\ \ \Delta v+k^{2}v=0\ \ \text{in}\ \ \Omega;\ \ \ w=v,\ \ \partial_{\nu}w=\partial_{\nu}v\ \text{on}\ \ \partial\Omega,

where
\mathbf{n}:=\sqrt{\epsilon}
is known as the refractive index of the inhomogeneous medium, and
w,v
correspond to the transverse parts of
\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{H}
.

The spectral study of the transmission eigenvalue problems has a long and colourful history in the literature and has become a central topic in the inverse scattering theory.
The spectral properties of transmission eigenvalues have been intensively and extensively investigated in the literature, including the existence, infiniteness and Weyl’s law, and we refer to
[
9
,
10
,
14
]
and the references cited in for the state-of-the-art development on this aspect. Recently, the spectral geometry of transmission eigenfunctions has received considerable attentions in the literature in different physical contexts
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
16
,
18
,
20
]
, and we refer
[
24
]
for survey and review on this aspect. It is pointed out that most of the aforementioned works are concerned with local geometric properties of the transmission eigenfunctions except
[
13
,
19
]
, where global rigidity properties of the transmission eigenfunctions were discovered. Our study in the current article is strongly motivated by the related study in
[
13
]
, where it is established that the transmission eigenfunctions associated with the Helmholtz system (
1.6
) carry the geometric information of the underlying domain
\Omega
. In fact, it is shown that there exists an infinite sequence of transmission eigenfunctions whose
L^{2}
-energies concentrate on
\partial\Omega
. This geometrical property has been further used to develop a super-resolution wave imaging scheme associated with the acoustic scattering. In this paper, we shall show that the Maxwell transmission eigenfunctions in (
1.5
) carry both the geometrical and topological properties of the underlying domain
\Omega
. First, we construct sequences of eigenfunctions
(\mathbf{E}_{1}^{(n)},\mathbf{E}_{2}^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}}
associated with eigenvalues
k_{n}\rightarrow\infty
whose
L^{2}
-energies localize around
\partial\Omega
. In fact, we construct both mono-localized and bi-localized eigenfunctions. Here, by a mono-localized eigenfunction
(\mathbf{E}_{1}^{(n)},\mathbf{E}_{2}^{(n)})
, we mean that only one of the pair of eigenfunctions is surface-localized while the other one is not, and by bi-localization, we mean both
\mathbf{E}_{1}^{(n)}
and
\mathbf{E}_{2}^{(n)}
are surface-localized. In the radial case with constant refractive index, we give the analytical construction, whereas for the general case, we provide numerical verifications. Second, we show that the transmission eigenfunctions carry the topological structure of the underlying domain
\Omega
when it has multiple connected components. Using the obtained geometrical and topological results, we propose a novel and interesting application of generating the mirage imaging of a given optical object at a customized location. The main idea is to design suitable illuminating EM fields that are related to the underlying object as well as the associated EM transmission eigenfunctions.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Sections 2 and 3 are respectively devoted to the geometrical and topological properties of the EM transmission eigenfunctions. In Section 4, we consider the application to artificial mirage.

2.
Surface-localized transmission eigenmodes

To begin with, we introduce a qualitative definition of surface-localization for a function
\mathbf{Q}\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{3}
. In what follows, for a sufficiently small
\delta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we set

\mathcal{N}_{\delta}(\partial\Omega):=\{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega;\ \mathrm{dist}(\mathbf{x},\partial\Omega)<\delta\}.

Definition 2.1
.

Given a function
\mathbf{Q}\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{3}
. It is said to be surface-localized if there exists a sufficient small constant
\delta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, such that

(2.1)

\frac{\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\backslash\mathcal{N}_{\delta}(\partial\Omega))^{3}}}{\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{3}}}\ll 1.

By (
2.1
), if a function is surface-localized, then its
L^{2}
-energy concentrates on
\partial\Omega
. In what follows, we shall make the qualitative relation in (
2.1
) more quantitative.

Our main finding in this section can be summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a simply connected Lipschitz domain and
\varepsilon
be a positive constant with
\varepsilon\neq 1
. Consider the Maxwell transmission eigenvalue problem (
1.4
). Then there exists a sequence of mono-localized eigenfunctions
(\mathbf{E}_{1}^{(n)},\mathbf{E}_{2}^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}}
, and there also exists a sequence of bi-localized eigenfunctions
(\mathbf{E}_{1}^{(n)},\mathbf{E}_{2}^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}}
, associated with the transmission eigenvalues
k_{n}\rightarrow\infty
.

In what follows, we shall rigorously prove Theorem
2.1
in the case that
\Omega
is radially symmetric, whereas in the general case, we only provide numerical verifications. In the numerics, we can actually consider variable
\varepsilon
and the conclusion in Theorem
2.1
still holds true.

2.1.
Transmission eigenvalues for a ball

In this subsection, we briefly discuss the transmission eigenvalues for the Maxwell system (
1.5
) in the radially symmetric case associated with a constant refractive index. This has been derived in
[
29
]
. Nevertheless, some of the technical ingredients shall be needed in our subsequent analysis of the transmission eigenfunctions and for self-containedness and easy reference, we present them in what follows. Without loss of generality, we assume that
\Omega
centres at the origin, namely,
\Omega=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\,|\mathbf{x}|<R\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\}
. We also let
\epsilon_{0}=\sqrt{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Then (
1.5
) can be rewritten as

(2.2)

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\nabla\wedge(\nabla\wedge\mathbf{E}_{1})-k^{2}\epsilon_{0}^{2}\mathbf{E}_{1}=0,&&\nabla\cdot(\epsilon_{0}^{2}\mathbf{E}_{1})=0&&\mbox{in}\ \ \Omega,\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\\
&\nabla\wedge(\nabla\wedge\mathbf{E}_{2})-k^{2}\mathbf{E}_{2}=0,&&\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}_{2}=0&&\mbox{in}\ \ \Omega,\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\\
&\nu\wedge\mathbf{E}_{1}=\nu\wedge\mathbf{E}_{2},&&\nu\wedge(\nabla\wedge\mathbf{E}_{1})=\nu\wedge(\nabla\wedge\mathbf{E}_{2})&&\mbox{on}\ \ \partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

Since
\epsilon_{0}
is a positive constant, we can expand the solutions
\mathbf{E}_{1}
and
\mathbf{E}_{2}
of the system (
2.2
) into the Fourier series
[
15
]
,

\displaystyle\bf{\displaystyle E}_{1}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}a_{n}^{m}\mathcal{M}_{n}^{m}(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}b_{n}^{m}\mathcal{N}_{n}^{m}(\mathbf{x}),\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

\displaystyle\bf{\displaystyle E}_{2}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}\widetilde{a}_{n}^{m}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{m}(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}\widetilde{b}_{n}^{m}\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{n}^{m}(\mathbf{x}),\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

where

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{n}^{m}(\mathbf{x})=\nabla\wedge\{\mathbf{x}j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}|\mathbf{x}|)Y_{n}^{m}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})\},\quad\mathcal{N}_{n}^{m}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}k}\nabla\wedge\mathcal{M}_{n}^{m}(\mathbf{x}),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{m}(\mathbf{x})=\nabla\wedge\{\mathbf{x}j_{n}(k|\mathbf{x}|)Y_{n}^{m}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})\},\quad\ \ \,\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{n}^{m}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}k}\nabla\wedge\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{m}(\mathbf{x}).

Here,
j_{n}(\mathbf{x})
denotes the spherical Bessel functions of the first kind and
Y_{n}^{m}
denotes a spherical harmonic function of degree
n
and order
m
.
Moreover, one can verify that
\mathcal{M}_{n}^{m}
and
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{m}
are the solutions to

\nabla\wedge(\nabla\wedge\mathbf{E}_{1})-k^{2}\epsilon_{0}^{2}\mathbf{E}_{1}=0,\quad\nabla\cdot(\epsilon_{0}^{2}\mathbf{E}_{1})=0,

and
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{m}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{n}^{m}
are the solutions to

\nabla\wedge(\nabla\wedge\mathbf{E}_{2})-k^{2}\mathbf{E}_{2}=0,\ \ \ \ \nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}_{2}=0.

In particular,

\mathcal{M}_{n}^{m}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{m}
denote the TE (transverse electric) waves;
\mathcal{N}_{n}^{m}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{n}^{m}
denote the TM (transverse magnetic) waves.

For the TE modes, using the spherical coordinates
(\rho,\theta,\phi)
,
\mathcal{M}_{n}^{m}
can be rewritten as

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{n}^{m}

\displaystyle=\nabla\wedge\{\rho j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho)Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)\,\hat{\bm{\rho}}\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho^{2}\sin\theta}\left|\begin{array}[]{ccc}\hat{\bm{\rho}}&\rho\hat{\bm{\theta}}&\rho\sin\theta\hat{\bm{\phi}}\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}&\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}&\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\\
\rho j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho)Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)&0&0\end{array}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho)\frac{\partial Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\phi}\hat{\bm{\theta}}-j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho)\frac{\partial Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\theta}\hat{\bm{\phi}},

where
\rho=|\mathbf{x}|
and

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\rho}}=\sin\theta\cos\phi\,\bm{e}_{1}+\sin\theta\sin\phi\,\bm{e}_{2}+\cos\theta\,\bm{e}_{3},

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\theta}}=\cos\theta\cos\phi\,\bm{e}_{1}+\cos\theta\sin\phi\,\bm{e}_{2}-\sin\theta\,\bm{e}_{3},

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\phi}}=-\sin\phi\,\bm{e}_{1}+\cos\phi\,\bm{e}_{2}.

Due to

\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\theta}\right)+\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\theta}\frac{\partial^{2}Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\phi^{2}}+n(n+1)Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)=0,

we can derive that

(2.5)

\displaystyle\nabla\wedge\mathcal{M}_{n}^{m}=\frac{1}{\rho^{2}\sin\theta}\left|\begin{array}[]{ccc}\hat{\bm{\rho}}&\rho\hat{\bm{\theta}}&\rho\sin\theta\hat{\bm{\phi}}\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}&\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}&\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\\
0&\rho\frac{1}{\sin\theta}j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho)\frac{\partial Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\phi}&-\rho\sin\theta j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho)\frac{\partial Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\theta}\end{array}\right|

\displaystyle=-\frac{j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho)}{\rho}\left(\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\theta}\right)+\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\theta}\frac{\partial^{2}Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\phi^{2}}\right)\hat{\bm{\rho}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}(\rho j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho))\frac{\partial Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\theta}\hat{\bm{\theta}}+\frac{1}{\rho\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}(\rho j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho))\frac{\partial Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\phi}\hat{\bm{\phi}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho}j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho)n(n+1)Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)\hat{\bm{\rho}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}(\rho j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho))\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\theta}\hat{\bm{\theta}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}(\rho j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho))\frac{1}{\rho\sin\theta}\frac{\partial Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\phi}\hat{\bm{\phi}}.

Since the surface gradient in the spherical coordinates is given by

\nabla_{s}f=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}\hat{\bm{\theta}}+\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi}\hat{\bm{\phi}},

equations (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) can be rewritten as

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{n}^{m}

\displaystyle=j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho)\nabla_{s}Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)\wedge\hat{\bm{\rho}},

\displaystyle\nabla\wedge\mathcal{M}_{n}^{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho}j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho)n(n+1)Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)\hat{\bm{\rho}}+\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}(\rho j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho))\nabla_{s}Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi).

Similarly, one can deduce that

(2.7)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{m}

\displaystyle=j_{n}(k\rho)\nabla_{s}Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)\wedge\hat{\bm{\rho}},

\displaystyle\nabla\wedge\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho}j_{n}(k\rho)n(n+1)Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)\hat{\bm{\rho}}+\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}(\rho j_{n}(k\rho))\nabla_{s}Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi).

According to the boundary condition to (
2.2
), it yields

\displaystyle a_{n}^{m}\hat{\bm{\rho}}\wedge\mathcal{M}_{n}^{m}=\widetilde{a}_{n}^{m}\hat{\bm{\rho}}\wedge\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{m},

\displaystyle a_{n}^{m}\hat{\bm{\rho}}\wedge(\nabla\wedge\mathcal{M}_{n}^{m})=\widetilde{a}_{n}^{m}\hat{\bm{\rho}}\wedge(\nabla\wedge\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{m}).

Applying (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) with a straightforward calculation, one can obtain

\displaystyle\Big(a_{n}^{m}j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho)-\widetilde{a}_{n}^{m}j_{n}(k\rho)\Big)\,\nabla_{s}Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)=0,

\displaystyle\left(a_{n}^{m}\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}(\rho j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho))-\widetilde{a}_{n}^{m}\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}(\rho j_{n}(k\rho))\right)\hat{\rho}\wedge\nabla_{s}Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)=0,\quad\rho=R.

Noting that
a_{n}^{m}\neq 0
and
\widetilde{a}_{n}^{m}\neq 0
, the eigenvalues
k
’s of the TE modes are positive zeros of the following function

(2.8)

f_{n}^{\mathrm{TE}}(k)=\left(j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho)\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}(\rho j_{n}(k\rho))-j_{n}(k\rho)\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}(\rho j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho))\right)_{\rho=R}.

Using the recurrence relation of the derivatives of the spherical Bessel functions,

(2.9)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial j_{n}(k\rho)}{\partial\rho}=k\left(\frac{n}{k\rho}j_{n}(k\rho)-j_{n+1}(k\rho)\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho)}{\partial\rho}=k\epsilon_{0}\left(\frac{n}{k\epsilon_{0}\rho}j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho)-j_{n+1}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho)\right),

equation (
2.8
) can be rewritten as

(2.10)

f_{n}^{\mathrm{TE}}(k)=k\Big(\epsilon_{0}j_{n}(kR)j_{n+1}(k\epsilon_{0}R)-j_{n+1}(kR)j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}R)\Big),\quad n\geq 1.

On the other hand, for the TM modes, one can derive that

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{n}^{m}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}k}\nabla\wedge\mathcal{M}_{n}^{m}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}k\rho}j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho)n(n+1)Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)\hat{\bm{\rho}}+\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}k\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}(\rho j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho))\nabla_{s}Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi).

Noting that

\nabla\wedge\left(\nabla\wedge\mathcal{M}_{n}^{m}\right)-k^{2}\epsilon_{0}^{2}\mathcal{M}_{n}^{m}=0
,
together with (
2.3
) and (
2.6
), one can derive that

\nabla\wedge\mathcal{N}_{n}^{m}=\nabla\wedge\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}k}\nabla\wedge\mathcal{M}_{n}^{m}\right)=-\mathrm{i}k\epsilon_{0}^{2}\mathcal{M}_{n}^{m}=-\mathrm{i}k\epsilon_{0}^{2}j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho)\nabla_{s}Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)\wedge\hat{\bm{\rho}}.

Similar results also hold for
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{n}^{m}
. According to last equation of (
2.2
), it yields

\displaystyle b_{n}^{m}\hat{\bm{\rho}}\wedge\mathcal{N}_{n}^{m}=\widetilde{b}_{n}^{m}\hat{\bm{\rho}}\wedge\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{n}^{m},

\displaystyle b_{n}^{m}\hat{\bm{\rho}}\wedge(\nabla\wedge\mathcal{N}_{n}^{m})=\widetilde{b}_{n}^{m}\hat{\bm{\rho}}\wedge(\nabla\wedge\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{n}^{m}).

By following a similar argument as the TE model case, one has

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}k}\left(b_{n}^{m}\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}(\rho j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho))-\widetilde{b}_{n}^{m}\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}(\rho j_{n}(k\rho))\right)\hat{\bm{\rho}}\wedge\nabla_{s}Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)=0,

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}k\Big(b_{n}^{m}\epsilon_{0}^{2}j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho)-\widetilde{b}_{n}^{m}\,j_{n}(k\rho)\Big)\nabla_{s}Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)=0,\quad\rho=R.

Noting that
b_{n}^{m}\neq 0
and
\widetilde{b}_{n}^{m}\neq 0
, the eigenvalues
k
’s for TM modes are positive zeros of the following function

f_{n}^{\mathrm{TM}}(k)=\left(j_{n}(k\rho)\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}(\rho j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho))-\epsilon_{0}^{2}j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}\rho)\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}(\rho j_{n}(k\rho))\right)_{\rho=R}.

Using the recurrence relation (
2.9
), the last equation can be rewritten as

(2.11)

\displaystyle f_{n}^{\mathrm{TM}}(k)=

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\epsilon_{0}^{2})(1+n)}{\rho}j_{n}(kR)j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}R)

\displaystyle+k\epsilon_{0}\Big(\epsilon_{0}j_{n}(k\epsilon_{0}R)j_{n+1}(kR)-j_{n}(kR)j_{n+1}(k\epsilon_{0}R)\Big),\quad n\geq 1.

Based on the above discussion, the transmission eigenvalues are those

[\,t\,]

\displaystyle r_{n,s_{1}}=\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(1+C_{0}(n+1/2)^{2(\gamma_{1}-1)/3}+o((n+1/2)^{2(\gamma_{1}-1)/3})\right),

\displaystyle r_{n,s_{2}}=\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(1+C_{0}(n+1/2)^{2(\gamma_{2}-1)/3}+o((n+1/2)^{2(\gamma_{2}-1)/3})\right),

\displaystyle\cos\left((n+3/2)\mathcal{O}\Big((n+1/2)^{\varsigma_{2}}\Big)-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\cos\left((n+1/2)\left(\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}^{2}-1}-\frac{\pi}{2}+\arcsin\frac{1}{\epsilon_{0}}+\mathcal{O}\Big((n+1/2)^{\varsigma_{2}}\Big)\right)-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)<0.

Noting that the above two cosine functions never have the same frequency, so it is easy to realize (

\varsigma_{2}(\gamma_{2})

k_{n}\in(r_{n,s_{1}(n)}/R,r_{n,s_{2}(n)}/R)

From Lemma

Theorem 2.2

Let
\Omega=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:|\mathbf{x}|<R\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\}

\epsilon_{0}>1

Let

Let

For the TE models, the eigenfunctions are given by

(2.20)

(2.21)

Correspondingly, for the TM models, the eigenfunctions are given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{1}^{(n)}=\frac{{b}_{n}^{m}}{\mathrm{i}k_{n}\rho}\left(j_{n}(k_{n}\epsilon_{0}\rho)n(n+1)Y_{n}^{m}\hat{\bm{\rho}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\Big(\rho j_{n}(k_{n}\epsilon_{0}\rho)\Big)\nabla_{s}Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)\right),

To begin with, for a fixed

Next, we prove that the transmission eigenfunctions

From

Therefore, from (

\begin{split}j_{n}(k_{n}\tau)=&\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2k_{n}\tau}}J_{n+1/2}(k_{n}\tau)\\

<&C_{1}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{-1}\Big(1+o(1)\Big),\end{split}

\displaystyle n+\frac{1}{2}\leq r_{n,1}^{{}^{\prime}}<r_{n,1}<r_{n,2}^{{}^{\prime}}<r_{n,2}<r_{n,3}^{{}^{\prime}}<\cdots,

\displaystyle j^{\prime}_{n}(z)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}(z/2)^{n-1}}{4\Gamma(n+1/2)}\mathop{\Pi}\limits_{s=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{z^{2}}{r_{n,s}^{{}^{\prime}2}}\right),

j_{n}(k_{n}\rho)<j_{n}(k_{n}\tau)<C_{1}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{-1}\Big(1+o(1)\Big),\quad 0<\rho<\tau.

One the other hand, for sufficiently large

\tau_{1}

(2.29)

\tau_{1}=\frac{r_{n,1}^{\prime}}{k_{n}},\quad\tau<\tau_{1}<R.

Thus, we have

n+\frac{1}{2}<r_{n,1}^{\prime}=k_{n}\tau_{1}.

Using the asymptotic expansion (
2.18
), one can deduce that

(2.30)

\begin{split}&\int_{\tau}^{R}j_{n}^{2}(k_{n}\rho)\,\rho\,\mathrm{d}\rho\geq\int_{\tau_{1}}^{R}j_{n}^{2}(k_{n}\rho)\,\rho\,\mathrm{d}\rho\\

&\ \cdot\int_{\tau_{1}}^{R}\cos^{2}\left(\sqrt{k_{n}^{2}\rho^{2}-\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}-\frac{\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\pi}{2}+\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\arcsin\left(\frac{n+\frac{1}{2}}{k_{n}\rho}\right)-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\mathrm{d}\rho\\

\displaystyle\frac{\|\mathbf{E}_{2}^{(n)}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\tau})}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{E}_{2}^{(n)}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2C_{1}^{2}\tau^{3}}{3C_{2}\tau_{1}R(R-\tau_{1})}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{-1+\varsigma/2}\rightarrow 0,\quad\mathrm{as}\quad n\rightarrow\infty.

Moreover, for the TM models, according to the recurrence relation of spherical bessel function

\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}j_{n}(\rho)=\frac{j_{n-1}(\rho)-j_{n+1}(\rho)}{2},

and formula (
2.14
), then the eigenfunction (

(2.31)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{2}^{(n)}=

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{b}_{n}^{m}}{\mathrm{i}}\Big(\frac{n(n+1)}{k_{n}\rho}j_{n}(k_{n}\rho)Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)\bm{\hat{\rho}}+\frac{j_{n}(k_{n}\rho)}{k_{n}\rho}\nabla_{s}Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{b}_{n}^{m}}{\mathrm{i}}\Big(\frac{n(n+1)}{k_{n}\rho}j_{n}(k_{n}\rho)Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)\bm{\hat{\rho}}

\displaystyle\frac{\|\mathbf{E}_{2}^{(n)}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\tau})}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{E}_{2}^{(n)}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}

\mathbf{E}_{1}^{(n)}
are not

Without loss of generality, we only consider the TE models case and the TM models case can be proved in a similar manner.

From (

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}j_{n}^{2}(k_{n}\epsilon_{0}\rho)\rho^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\rho

\displaystyle<\frac{2}{\epsilon_{0}^{3}}\int_{\frac{r^{\prime}_{n,1}}{k_{n}}}^{\epsilon_{0}R}j_{n}^{2}(k_{n}\rho^{\prime})\rho^{\prime 2}\,\mathrm{d}\rho^{\prime}

\frac{r^{\prime}_{n,1}}{k_{n}\epsilon_{0}}<\frac{R}{\epsilon_{0}}<\tau<R,

and so for

\begin{split}&\int_{\frac{r^{\prime}_{n,1}}{k_{n}\epsilon_{0}}}^{t}j_{n}^{2}(k_{n}\epsilon_{0}\rho)\rho^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\rho\\
&=\int_{\frac{r^{\prime}_{n,1}}{k_{n}\epsilon_{0}}}^{t}\frac{\rho\cos^{2}\left(\sqrt{k_{n}^{2}\epsilon_{0}^{2}\rho^{2}-\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}-\frac{\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\pi}{2}+\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\arcsin\left(\frac{n+\frac{1}{2}}{k_{n}\rho}\right)-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}{k_{n}\epsilon_{0}\sqrt{k_{n}^{2}\epsilon_{0}^{2}\rho^{2}-(n+\frac{1}{2})^{2}}}\mathrm{d}\rho\\

Combining the last two estimations, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\|\mathbf{E}_{1}^{(n)}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\tau})}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{E}_{1}^{(n)}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}=

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Big|j_{n}(k_{n}\epsilon_{0}\rho)\,\nabla_{s}Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)\wedge\hat{\bm{\rho}}\Big|^{2}\rho^{2}\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi\mathrm{d}\theta\mathrm{d}\rho}{\int_{0}^{R}\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Big|j_{n}(k_{n}\epsilon_{0}\rho)\,\nabla_{s}Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)\wedge\hat{\bm{\rho}}\Big|^{2}\rho^{2}\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi\mathrm{d}\theta\mathrm{d}\rho}

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{\frac{r^{\prime}_{n,1}}{k_{n}\epsilon_{0}}}^{\tau}j_{n}^{2}(k_{n}\epsilon_{0}\rho)\rho^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\rho}{2\int_{\frac{r^{\prime}_{n,1}}{k_{n}\epsilon_{0}}}^{R}j_{n}^{2}(k_{n}\epsilon_{0}\rho)\rho^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\rho}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sqrt{k_{n}^{2}\,\epsilon_{0}^{2}\,\tau^{2}-\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}-\sqrt{r^{{}^{\prime}2}_{n,1}-\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}}{\sqrt{k_{n}^{2}\,\epsilon_{0}^{2}\,R^{2}-\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}-\sqrt{r^{{}^{\prime}2}_{n,1}-\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}^{2}(\tau/R)^{2}-1}}{\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}^{2}-1}}>0\quad n\rightarrow\infty.

The proof is complete.

2.3.
Bi-localized transmission eigenmodes

In this section, we also construct a sequence of transmission eigenvalues
\{k_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}}

\left(\mathbf{E}_{1}^{(n)},\mathbf{E}_{2}^{(n)}\right)

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\Omega=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:|\mathbf{x}|<R\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\}
and
\epsilon_{0}>1
be a constant. Let
E_{\Omega}^{\prime}
denote the set of transmission eigenvalues of (
2.2
). For a fixed value
s_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, there exists a subsequence
\{k_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}}\subset E_{\Omega}^{\prime}
, such that for sufficient large
n
, there holds

(2.32)

k_{n}\in\left(\frac{r_{n,s_{0}}}{\epsilon_{0}R},\frac{r_{n,s_{0}+1}}{\epsilon_{0}R}\right).

Moreover, one has

(2.33)

\frac{n+\frac{1}{2}}{\epsilon_{0}R}<k_{n}\leq\frac{n+\frac{1}{2}}{R},\quad n=1,2,3,\cdots.

Proof.

For any fixed
s_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, from (
2.16
) and (
2.26
), there exists a sufficiently large
n
such that

(2.34)

\frac{r_{n,s_{0}+1}}{\epsilon_{0}}\leq n+\frac{1}{2}\leq r_{n,1}^{\prime}.

By the monotonicity of the Bessel function before the first local maximal, we have

j_{n}(k)\geq 0,\quad k\in(0,r_{n,1}^{\prime}].

Noting that the positive root of
j_{n}(|x|)
are interlaced with those of
j_{n+1}(|x|)

[
27
]
, hence we have

j_{n+1}(r_{n,s_{0}})\cdot j_{n+1}(r_{n,s_{0}+1})<0.

Therefore, from the last two equations and (
2.10
), we can derive that

\displaystyle f_{n}^{\mathrm{TE}}\left(\frac{r_{n,s_{0}}}{\epsilon_{0}R}\right)f_{n}^{\mathrm{TE}}\left(\frac{r_{n,s_{0}+1}}{\epsilon_{0}R}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{n,s_{0}}r_{n,s_{0}+1}}{R^{2}}j_{n}\left(\frac{r_{n,s_{0}}}{\epsilon_{0}}\right)j_{n+1}(r_{n,s_{0}})\,j_{n}\left(\frac{r_{n,s_{0}+1}}{\epsilon_{0}}\right)j_{n+1}(r_{n,s_{0}+1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r_{n,s_{0}}r_{n,s_{0}+1}}{R^{2}}j_{n}^{2}\left(r_{n,1}^{\prime}\right)j_{n+1}(r_{n,s_{0}})j_{n+1}(r_{n,s_{0}+1})<0.

Hence, there exists
k_{n}\in\left({r_{n,s_{0}}}/{(\epsilon_{0}R)},{r_{n,s_{0}+1}}/{(\epsilon_{0}R)}\right)

such that
f_{n}^{\mathrm{TE}}\left(k_{n}\right)=0
.
By a similar argument, one can also verify that
f_{n}^{\mathrm{TM}}\left(k_{n}\right)=0

and it proves (
2.32
).
Furthermore, from (
2.26
) and (
2.34
), one can deduce (
2.33
) and it completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\Omega=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:|\mathbf{x}|<R\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\}
and
\epsilon_{0}>1
be a constant. Let
(\mathbf{E}_{1}^{(n)},\mathbf{E}_{2}^{(n)})
be the pair of transmission eigenfunctions associated with eigenvalue
k_{n}
in (
2.12
) for Maxwell’s transmission eigenvalue problem (
2.2
). Then it holds that both eigenfunctions
\mathbf{E}_{1}^{(n)}
and
\mathbf{E}_{2}^{(n)}
are surface-localized around the boundary
\partial\Omega
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we only consider the TE modes and the TM modes can be proved in a similar manner.
Let
\Omega_{\tau}:=\{\mathbf{x}:|\mathbf{x}|<\tau,\ \tau<R\}
and
k_{n}
be the eigenvalues defined in (
2.12
).
Using (
2.21
) and (
2.15
), one has

\displaystyle\frac{\|\mathbf{E}_{2}^{(n)}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\tau})}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{E}_{2}^{(n)}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Big|j_{n}(k_{n}\rho)\,\nabla_{s}Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)\wedge\hat{\bm{\rho}}\Big|^{2}\rho^{2}\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi\mathrm{d}\theta\mathrm{d}\rho}{\int_{0}^{R}\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Big|j_{n}(k_{n}\rho)\,\nabla_{s}Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)\wedge\hat{\bm{\rho}}\Big|^{2}\rho^{2}\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi\mathrm{d}\theta\mathrm{d}\rho}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{0}^{\tau}J_{n+\frac{1}{2}}^{2}(k_{n}\rho)\rho\,\mathrm{d}\rho}{\int_{0}^{R}J_{n+\frac{1}{2}}^{2}(k_{n}\rho)\rho\,\mathrm{d}\rho}.

Set
f(\rho)=J_{n+\frac{1}{2}}^{2}(k_{n}\rho)\rho
, through a straightforward calculation, one can verify that
f(\rho)
is a convex function on
[0,R]
for
k_{n}\leq(n+1/2)/R
.
Therefore, the area of the triangle under tangent of
f(R)
is smaller than
\int_{0}^{R}f(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho
, that is,

\frac{R^{2}J_{n+\frac{1}{2}}^{3}(k_{n}R)}{2J_{n+\frac{1}{2}}(k_{n}R)+4k_{n}RJ_{n+\frac{1}{2}}^{{}^{\prime}}(k_{n}R)}\leq\int_{0}^{R}J_{n+\frac{1}{2}}^{2}(k_{n}\rho)\rho\,\mathrm{d}\rho.

Thus it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{\|\mathbf{E}_{2}^{(n)}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\tau})}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{E}_{2}^{(n)}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tau^{2}J_{n+\frac{1}{2}}^{2}(k_{n}\tau)\Big(2J_{n+\frac{1}{2}}(k_{n}R)+4k_{n}RJ_{n+\frac{1}{2}}^{{}^{\prime}}(k_{n}R)\Big)}{R^{2}J_{n+\frac{1}{2}}^{3}(k_{n}R)}

\displaystyle\leq\tau^{2}\left(\frac{J_{n+\frac{1}{2}}^{2}(k_{n}\tau)}{J_{n+\frac{1}{2}}^{2}(k_{n}R)}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{2}{R^{2}}+\frac{4k_{n}}{R}\cdot\frac{J_{n+\frac{1}{2}}^{{}^{\prime}}(k_{n}R)}{J_{n+\frac{1}{2}}(k_{n}R)}\right).

By Lemma 2.3 in
[
19
]
, one can show that there exists positive constants
C_{3}
and
\gamma
such that

\frac{J_{n+\frac{1}{2}}^{{}^{\prime}}(k_{n}R)}{J_{n+\frac{1}{2}}(k_{n}R)}<C_{3}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\gamma}.

In addition, using the Calini formula, one can find that there exists positive constants
C_{4}
and
\delta(\tau,\epsilon_{0})\in(0,1)
, such that

\frac{J_{n+\frac{1}{2}}^{2}(k_{n}\tau)}{J_{n+\frac{1}{2}}^{2}(k_{n}R)}<C_{4}(1-\delta)^{n+\frac{1}{2}}.

Hence, combining the last three inequalities, one can obtain that

\frac{\|\mathbf{E}_{2}^{(n)}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\tau})}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{E}_{2}^{(n)}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}\rightarrow 0,\quad\mathrm{as}\ n\rightarrow\infty.

The proof is complete.
∎

Remark 2.1
.

We would like to point out that there is no contradiction between the mono-localized eigenmodes and bi-localized modes, since the transmission eigenvalues for mono-localized eigenmodes satisfy
k_{n}>\frac{n+1/2}{R}
while the transmission eigenvalues for bi-localized eigenmodes satisfy

\frac{n+1/2}{\epsilon_{0}R}<k_{n}<\frac{n+1/2}{R}
. In fact, for a fixed value
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
\epsilon_{0}>1
, the mono-localized eigenmodes would have a better chance for occurring.

Numerics

In this section, we present several numerical examples to show geometrical properties of transmission eigenfunctions.
Here, we shall use the curl conforming finite element method approximation as proposed in
[
29
]
and briefly describe it in the sequel. Multiplying the first two equation in (
1.5
) by a test functions
\varphi\in H_{0}^{1}(\mathrm{curl},\Omega)
and integrating by parts, one obtains

(2.35)

\displaystyle(\nabla\wedge\mathbf{E}_{1},\nabla\wedge\varphi)-k^{2}\varepsilon(\mathbf{E}_{1},\varphi)=0,\quad

\displaystyle\forall\varphi\in H_{0}^{1}(\mathrm{curl},\Omega),

\displaystyle(\nabla\wedge\mathbf{E}_{2},\nabla\wedge\varphi)-k^{2}(\mathbf{E}_{2},\varphi)=0,\quad

\displaystyle\forall\varphi\in H_{0}^{1}(\mathrm{curl},\Omega).

To enforce the boundary conditions
\nu\wedge(\nabla\wedge\mathbf{E}_{1})=\nu\wedge(\nabla\wedge\mathbf{E}_{2})
weakly, we multiply it by a test function
\psi\in H^{1}(\mathrm{curl},\Omega)
and integrate by parts

(2.36)

(\nabla\wedge\mathbf{E}_{1},\nabla\wedge\psi)-k^{2}\varepsilon(\mathbf{E}_{1},\psi)=(\nabla\wedge\mathbf{E}_{2},\nabla\wedge\psi)-k^{2}(\mathbf{E}_{2},\psi).

Hence, the variational formulation for (
2.2
) is to
find
(\mathbf{E}_{1},\mathbf{E}_{2})\in H(\mathrm{curl},\Omega)\times H(\mathrm{curl},\Omega)
satisfying (
2.35
) and (
2.36
),
together with the essential boundary condition
\nu\wedge\mathbf{E}_{1}=\nu\wedge\mathbf{E}_{2}
on
\partial\Omega
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be a regular tetrahedral mesh for
\Omega

where
P_{n}
denotes the space of homogeneous polynomials of order
n
.
We also define a subspace
V_{h}^{0}=V\cap H_{0}(\mathrm{curl},\Omega)
.
Let

\{\bm{\eta}_{i}:\,i=1,2,\cdots m\}
denote a basis for
V_{h}^{0}
and
\{\bm{\eta}_{i}:\,i=1,2,\cdots m\}\cup\{\bm{\zeta}_{j}:\,j=1,2,\cdots n\}
denote a basis for

\nu\wedge\mathbf{E}_{1}=\nu\wedge\mathbf{E}_{2}

\mathbf{E}_{1}^{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{1}^{(i)}\bm{\eta}_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}w^{(j)}\bm{\zeta}_{j},\quad\mathbf{E}_{2}^{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{2}^{(i)}\bm{\eta}_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}w^{(j)}\bm{\zeta}_{j},

(2.37)

A=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}S_{II}&0&S_{IB}\\

S_{IB}^{\top}&-S_{IB}^{\top}&0\\
\end{array}\right],\quad B=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}M_{2,II}&0&M_{2,IB}\\

Here, the stiffness and mass matrices are given by

\displaystyle S_{II}=(\nabla\wedge\bm{\eta}_{i},\nabla\wedge\bm{\eta}_{j}),

\displaystyle S_{IB}=(\nabla\wedge\bm{\eta}_{i},\nabla\wedge\bm{\zeta}_{j}),

\displaystyle M_{2,II}=(\varepsilon\bm{\eta}_{i},\bm{\eta}_{j}),

\displaystyle M_{1,II}=(\bm{\eta}_{i},\bm{\eta}_{j}),

\displaystyle M_{2,IB}=(\varepsilon\bm{\eta}_{i},\bm{\zeta}_{j}),

\displaystyle M_{1,IB}=(\bm{\eta}_{i},\bm{\zeta}_{j}),

3.
Topological properties of Maxwell transmission eigenfunctions

In what follows, with a bit abuse of notations, we let
\varepsilon,\mu
be symmetric-positive-definite-matrix valued functions in
\Omega
, and consider the Maxwell system

(3.1)

\nabla\wedge\mathbf{E}=\mathrm{i}k\mu\mathbf{H},\quad\nabla\wedge\mathbf{H}=-\mathrm{i}k\varepsilon\mathbf{E}\quad\mbox{in}\ \ \Omega.

We recall the following transformation-invariant property of the Maxwell system.

Lemma 3.1

(Lemma 2.2 in
[
26
]
)
.

Consider a bi-Lipschitz and orientation-preserving coordinate transformation
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}):\Omega\rightarrow\widetilde{\Omega}
. Let
D\mathbf{F}
denote the Jacobian matrix of
\mathbf{F}
. Assume that
(\mathbf{E},\mathbf{H})\in H(\mathrm{curl},\Omega)\times H(\mathrm{curl},\Omega)
are EM fields to (
3.1
); then for the pull-back fields given by

\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}=(\mathbf{F}^{-1})^{*}\mathbf{Q}:=(D\mathbf{F}^{T})\mathbf{Q}\circ\mathbf{F}^{-1},\quad\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{E},\mathbf{H},

we have
(\mathbf{E},\mathbf{H})\in H(\mathrm{curl},\Omega)\times H(\mathrm{curl},\Omega)
, which satisfy the Maxwell system

\widetilde{\nabla}\wedge\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}=\mathrm{i}k\tilde{\mu}\widetilde{\mathbf{H}},\quad\widetilde{\nabla}\wedge\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}=-\mathrm{i}k\widetilde{\varepsilon}\widetilde{\mathbf{E}},

where
\widetilde{\nabla}
signifies the differentiation in the
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}
-coordinate, and
\tilde{\varepsilon}
and
\tilde{\mu}
are the push-forwards of
\varepsilon
and
\mu
via
\mathbf{F}
, defined by

\tilde{\zeta}=\mathbf{F}_{*}\zeta:=|D\mathbf{F}|^{-1}D\mathbf{F}\cdot\zeta\cdot D\mathbf{F}^{T}\circ\mathbf{F}^{-1},\quad\zeta:=\varepsilon,\mu.

Using Lemma
3.1
, one can readily obtain the following transformation property of the transmission eigenvalue problem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\Omega,\widetilde{\Omega}
and
\mathbf{F}
be the same as those in Lemma
3.1
. Assume that
\mathbf{Q}_{j}\in H(\mathrm{curl},\Omega)
,
j=1,2
and
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{E},\mathbf{H}
, satisfy the following transmission eigenvalue problem:

(3.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\nabla\wedge\mathbf{E}_{j}-\mathrm{i}k\mu_{j}\mathbf{H}_{j}=\mathbf{0}&&\mbox{in}\ \Omega,\\
&\nabla\wedge\mathbf{H}_{j}+\mathrm{i}k\varepsilon_{j}\mathbf{E}_{j}=\mathbf{0}&&\mbox{in}\ \Omega,\\
&\nu\wedge\mathbf{E}_{j}=\nu\wedge\mathbf{H}_{j}&&\mbox{on}\ \partial\Omega,\ \ j=1,2,\end{aligned}\right.

the the pull-back fields
\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j}=(\mathbf{F}^{-1})^{*}\mathbf{Q}_{j}
are solutions to the following transmission eigenvalue problem:

(3.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\nabla\wedge\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}_{j}-\mathrm{i}k\tilde{\mu}_{j}\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_{j}=\mathbf{0},&&\mbox{in}\ \widetilde{\Omega},\\
&\nabla\wedge\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_{j}+\mathrm{i}k\tilde{\varepsilon}_{j}\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}_{j}=\mathbf{0}&&\mbox{in}\ \widetilde{\Omega},\\
&\nu\wedge\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}_{j}=\nu\wedge\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_{j}&&\mbox{on}\ \partial\widetilde{\Omega},\ \ j=1,2,\end{aligned}\right.

where
(\tilde{\varepsilon}_{j},\tilde{\mu}_{j})=\mathbf{F}_{*}(\varepsilon_{j},\mu_{j})
,
j=1,2
.

Proof.

Using Lemma
3.1
, one can readily have that the EM fields in (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) are related by the pull-back while the material parameters are related by the push-forward through the transformation
\mathbf{F}
. Hence, it is sufficient to show that the transmission conditions on
\partial\Omega
and
\partial\widetilde{\Omega}
are invariant under the transformation, which can be straightforwardly shown by following the argument in
[
26
]
in proving Lemma
3.1
.
∎

Let us consider a specific case by letting
\widetilde{\Omega}
be obtained through a translation of
\Omega
, namely
\widetilde{\Omega}=\mathbf{z}+\Omega
with
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. In such a case, we call
\widetilde{\Omega}
a copy of
\Omega
. Suppose
\mathbf{E}_{j}(\mathbf{x})
,
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega
and
j=1,2
, are transmission eigenfunctions to (
1.5
) associated with
(\Omega,\varepsilon)
. Using Theorem
3.1
, it is straightforward to verify that
\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}_{j}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z})
,
\mathbf{x}\in\widetilde{\Omega}
and
j=1,2
, are transmission eigenfunctions associated with
(\widetilde{\Omega},\tilde{\varepsilon})
, where
\tilde{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})=\varepsilon(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z})
for
\mathbf{x}\in\widetilde{\Omega}
. This fact shall be used in our subsequent study of the artificial electromagnetic mirage.

Next, we consider the case that the underlying domain
\Omega
has multiply connected components. To that end, we first recall that for a fixed transmission eigenvalue
k\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
in (
1.5
), the corresponding eigenspace is finite-dimensional
[
10
]
. In what follows, we let
\mathcal{S}_{k,(\Omega,\varepsilon)}
signify the eigenspace associated with the transmission eigenvalue
k
and the medium
(\Omega,\varepsilon)
in (
1.5
).

Theorem 3.2
.

Consider the transmission eigenvalue problem (
1.5
) and assume that
(\Omega,\varepsilon)=\cup_{j=1}^{l}(\Omega_{j},\varepsilon_{j})
, where each
\Omega_{j}
is a simply-connected component of
\Omega
. Then it holds that

\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{S}_{k,(\Omega_{j},\varepsilon_{j})})=p_{j}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{S}_{k,(\Omega_{j},\varepsilon_{j})}=\mathrm{Span}\{(\mathbf{E}_{1}^{\alpha_{j}},\mathbf{E}_{2}^{\alpha_{j}})_{\alpha_{j}=1}^{p_{j}}\}

if and only if

\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{S}_{k,(\Omega,\varepsilon)})=\sum_{j=1}^{l}p_{j}:=p\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{S}_{k,(\Omega,\varepsilon)}=\mathrm{Span}\{(\mathbf{E}_{1}^{\alpha},\mathbf{E}_{2}^{\alpha})_{\alpha=1}^{p}\},

where
(\mathbf{E}_{1}^{\alpha},\mathbf{E}_{2}^{\alpha})
is of the form

(\mathbf{E}_{1}^{\alpha},\mathbf{E}_{2}^{\alpha})=(\mathbf{E}_{1}^{\alpha_{j}},\mathbf{E}_{2}^{\alpha_{j}})\chi_{\Omega_{j}}+(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0})\chi_{\cup_{\beta=1,\beta\neq j}^{l}\Omega_{\beta}},

for a certain
1\leq j\leq l
.

Proof.

First, it is straightforward to verify that
k
is a transmission eigenvalue associated with
(\Omega,\varepsilon)
if and only if
k
is a transmission eigenvalue associated with each
(\Omega_{j},\varepsilon_{j})
for
j=1,2,\ldots,l
.

Next, by using (
1.5
), it is directly verified that the non-trivial functions defined in (
3.2
) are a pair of transmission eigenfunctions associated with
(\Omega,\varepsilon)
. On the other hand, if
(\mathbf{E}_{1},\mathbf{E}_{2})
are transmission eigenfunctions to (
1.5
), it can be shown that
(\mathbf{E}_{1}|_{\Omega_{j}},\mathbf{E}_{2}|_{\Omega_{j}})
are transmission eigenfunctions associated with
(\Omega_{j},\varepsilon_{j})
. Hence, one has

(\mathbf{E}_{1}|_{\Omega_{j}},\mathbf{E}_{2}|_{\Omega_{j}})\in\mathcal{S}_{k,(\Omega_{j},\varepsilon_{j})}=\mathrm{Span}\{(\mathbf{E}_{1}^{\alpha_{j}},\mathbf{E}_{2}^{\alpha_{j}})_{\alpha_{j}=1}^{p_{j}}\},

which readily implies that
\mathcal{S}_{k,(\Omega,\varepsilon)}
is spanned by the
p
functions defined in (
3.2
).

The proof is complete.

∎

Consider the special example again as above by letting
\Omega
be simply-connected and
\widetilde{\Omega}
be its copy. In order to further simplify the exposition, we let
\varepsilon
be constant. According to Theorem
3.2
, the multiplicity of the eigenvalue
k
associated with
(\Omega\cup\widetilde{\Omega},\varepsilon)
doubles that of
(\Omega,\varepsilon)
. Moreover, the corresponding eigenfunctions are given by

(3.4)

(\mathbf{E}_{1},\mathbf{E}_{2})\chi_{\Omega}+(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0})\chi_{\widetilde{\Omega}},\quad(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0})\chi_{\Omega}+(\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}_{1},\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}_{2})\chi_{\widetilde{\Omega}},

as well as the corresponding linear combination of the above two types of resonant modes. Here,
(\mathbf{E}_{1},\mathbf{E}_{2})
are the resonant modes associated with
(\Omega,\varepsilon)
and
(\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}_{1},\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}_{2})
are the resonant modes associated with
(\widetilde{\Omega},\varepsilon)
, and by Theorem
3.1
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}_{j}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{E}_{j}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z})
,
\mathbf{x}\in\widetilde{\Omega}
. This fact shall also be used in our subsequent study of the artificial mirage.

Finally, we would like to point out that Theorem
3.2
can easily extended to the case with more general transmission eigenvalue problems as considered in Theorem
3.1
. However, we shall not explore this point further.

4.
Artificial electromagnetic mirage

In this section, we propose a novel and interesting application of the geometrical and topological properties establish in the previous sections for the transmission resonances.
The practical scenario can be described as follows.

Let
(\Omega,\varepsilon)
be an optical object, where
\Omega
is simply-connected and
\varepsilon
is constant. Our goal is to generate certain incident electromagnetic field
(\mathbf{E}^{i},\mathbf{H}^{i})
impinging upon
(\Omega,\varepsilon)
with the following properties:

•

(\mathbf{E}^{i},\mathbf{H}^{i})
is a solution to (
1.1
);

•

the propagation of the EM fields is nearly non-interrupted by the presence of the physical object
(\Omega,\varepsilon)
;

•

the total wave field is localized on the boundary of a predefined set
\widetilde{\Omega}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\bar{\Omega}
, where
\widetilde{\Omega}=\Omega+\mathbf{z}
is simply a translation of
\Omega
. This copy is referred to as an artificial mirage image of the optical object
(\Omega,\varepsilon)
.

Now we introduce the scheme to get the desired
(\mathbf{E}^{i},\mathbf{H}^{i})
given
\Omega,\varepsilon
and
\mathbf{z}
. First, we recall the Fourier extension of the EM fields, which is also known as the Herglotz approximation. For a tangential vector field
\mathbf{g}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})^{3}
on the unit sphere, we define

(4.1)

\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x}):=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}e^{\mathrm{i}k\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{d}}\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{d})\,ds(\mathbf{d}),\quad\mathbf{H}_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}k}\nabla\wedge\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x})

to be the Herglotz fields associated with
\mathbf{g}
. For any regular domain
\Sigma
with a connected complement in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, it is known that the solution
(\mathbf{E},\mathbf{H})\in H(\mathrm{curl},\Sigma)\times H(\mathrm{curl},\Sigma)
to the following system

(4.2)

\nabla\wedge\mathbf{E}-\mathrm{i}k\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{0},\quad\nabla\wedge\mathbf{H}+\mathrm{i}k\mathbf{E}=\mathbf{0}\quad\mbox{in}\ \Sigma,

can be approximated by the Herglotz fields to an arbitrary accuracy; see
[
31
]
. It is noted that the Herglotz approximation result in
[
31
]
requires a bit stronger regularities on the EM fields in (
4.2
), which we shall always assume in our subsequent discussion.

Recall that
(\widetilde{\Omega},\varepsilon)
is an exact copy of
(\Omega,\varepsilon)
, where
\widetilde{\Omega}:=\mathbf{z}+\Omega
and
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Consider the transmission eigenvalue problem (
1.5
) associated with
(\Omega,\varepsilon)\cup(\widetilde{\Omega},\varepsilon)
. By Theorem
3.2
, and in particular the example discussed after the theorem, we know that the corresponding transmission eigenfunctions are given by (
3.4
). By the geometrical results in Theorem
2.3
in Section 2, we know that there exist infinitely many eigenfunctions associated with
(\Omega,\varepsilon)
which are surface-localized around
\partial\Omega
. Hence, we can choose one of such resonant modes such that
\mathbf{E}_{2}
is surface-localized around
\partial\Omega
and so
\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}_{2}
is surface-localized around
\partial\widetilde{\Omega}
, (see Fig.
5
for a schematic illustration).

Figure 5.
Contour plot of the transmission eigenfunction that is vanished on the left domain but localized on the right domain.

Next, we consider the second resonant mode in (
3.4
) and let
{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{g}}^{i}
be the Herglotz field associated with
\mathbf{0}\cdot\chi_{\Omega}+\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}_{2}\cdot\chi_{\widetilde{\Omega}}
, i.e.

(4.3)

\mathbf{E}^{i}_{\mathbf{g}}\approx\mathbf{0}\cdot\chi_{\Omega}+\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}_{2}\cdot\chi_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\quad\mbox{in}\ \ \Omega\cup\widetilde{\Omega}.

Let
\mathbf{H}_{\mathbf{g}}^{i}
be given according to the second formula in (
4.1
) associated with
\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{g}}^{i}
. Clearly,
(\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{g}}^{i},\mathbf{H}_{\mathbf{g}}^{i})
are entire solutions to (
1.1
), and can be used as incident fields, namely

(4.4)

(\mathbf{E}^{i},\mathbf{H}^{i})=(\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{g}}^{i},\mathbf{H}_{\mathbf{g}}^{i}).

Next, we consider the electromagnetic scattering problem (
1.2
) associated with the optical object
(\Omega,\varepsilon)
and incident field
(\mathbf{E}^{i},\mathbf{H}^{i})
. It is straightforward to deduce from (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) that the corresponding scattering field
(\mathbf{E}^{s},\mathbf{H}^{s})
satisfies

(4.5)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &~~\nabla\wedge\mathbf{E}^{s}-\mathrm{i}k\mathbf{H}^{s}=\mathbf{0}&&\mbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{3},\\
&~~\nabla\wedge\mathbf{H}^{s}+\mathrm{i}k\varepsilon\mathbf{E}^{s}=\mathrm{i}k(1-\varepsilon)\mathbf{E}^{i}&&\mbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{3},\\
&\lim_{|{\mathbf{x}}|\rightarrow\infty}(\mathbf{H}^{s}\wedge{\mathbf{x}}-|{\mathbf{x}}|\mathbf{E}^{s})={\mathbf{0}}.\end{aligned}\right.

By (
4.3
), we see that

\mathrm{i}k(1-\varepsilon)\mathbf{E}^{i}\approx\mathbf{0}\ \ \mbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{3}.

Therefore, by the well-posedness of the forward scattering system (
4.5
), we readily conclude that

\mathbf{E}^{s},\mathbf{H}^{s}\approx\mathbf{0}\quad\mbox{in}\ \ \mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega}.

That is, for the total fields
\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{H}
, one has

(4.6)

(\mathbf{E},\mathbf{H})\approx(\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{g}}^{i},\mathbf{H}_{\mathbf{g}}^{i})\quad\mbox{in}\ \ \mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega}.

Finally, by (
4.3
) and (
4.6
), we clearly have that the total electric field
\mathbf{E}
is localized around
\partial\widetilde{\Omega}
. That is, a “shining” area, i.e.
\partial\widetilde{\Omega}
, is produced and the electromagnetic fields inside
\widetilde{\Omega}
are nearly zero.

To summarize, given an optical object
(\Omega,\varepsilon)
, if one uses the incident fields designed in (
4.3
) and (
4.4
) to illuminate the object, an artificial mirage copy
(\widetilde{\Omega},\varepsilon)
will be produced. In what follows, we shall provide a few examples to illustrate the artificial mirage effect. However, in order to obtain eminent surface-localizing behaviour of the transmission eigenfunctions, one needs to compute the transmission eigenfunctions associated to (
1.5
) with either very large
\varepsilon
or very large
k
. This requires extensive and heavy costs in numerically computing the three-dimensional transmission eigenvalue problem (
1.5
) as well as the corresponding scattering system (
1.2
). Due to limited computational resources, we could only afford to conduct the two-dimensional simulations, which correspond to the TM (transverse magnetic) scattering.
