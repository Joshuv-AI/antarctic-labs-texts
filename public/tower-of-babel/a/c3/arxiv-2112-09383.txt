Intersection and Union Hierarchies of Deterministic Context-Free Languages and Pumping Lemmas 1 1 1 This exposition completes and corrects a preliminary report that appeared in the Proceedings of the 14th International Conference on Language and Automata Theory and Applications (LATA 2020), Lecture Notes in Computer Science, vol. 12038, pp. 341–353, 2020. A conference talk was given online during October 20–24, 2021 due to the coronavirus pandemic.


Abstract
We study the computational complexity of finite intersections and unions of deterministic context-free (dcf) languages. Earlier, Wotschke [J. Comput. System Sci. 16 (1978) 456–461] demonstrated that intersections of ( d + 1 ) (d+1) dcf languages are in general more powerful than intersections of d d dcf languages for any positive integer d d based on the intersection hierarchy separation of Liu and Weiner [Math. Systems Theory 7 (1973) 185–192]. The argument of Liu and Weiner, however, works only on bounded languages of particular forms, and therefore Wotschke’s result is not directly extendable to disprove any given language to be written in the form of d d intersection of dcf languages. To deal with the non-membership of a wide range of languages, we circumvent the specialization of their proof argument and devise a new and practical technical tool: two pumping lemmas for finite unions of dcf languages. Since the family of dcf languages is closed under complementation and also under intersection with regular languages, these pumping lemmas help us establish a non-membership relation of languages formed by finite intersections of non-bounded languages as well. We also refer to a relationship to deterministic limited automata of Hibbard [Inf. Control 11 (1967) 196–238] in this regard. Key words. deterministic pushdown automata, intersection and union hierarchies, pumping lemma, limited automata, iterative pair, stack-operational, state-stack pair

1.1
Intersection and Union Hierarchies and Historical Background

In formal languages and automata theory,
context-free languages
constitute a fundamental family,
\mathrm{CFL}
, which is situated in between the family
\mathrm{REG}
of regular languages and that of context-sensitive languages in Chomsky’s hierarchy.
Over a half century, a flurry of studies have been conducted toward a better understanding of the nature of the family
\mathrm{CFL}
. It has been well known that, among numerous structural properties,
\mathrm{CFL}
enjoys a closure property under the union operation but not the intersection operation since the non-context-free language
L_{abc}=\{a^{n}b^{n}c^{n}\mid n\geq 0\}

A_{1}=\{a^{n}b^{n}c^{p}\mid n,p\geq 0\}
and
A_{2}=\{a^{n}b^{p}c^{p}\mid n,p\geq 0\}

d
(
\geq 1
) context-free languages.
For later notational convenience, we here write
\mathrm{CFL}(d)
for the family of such intersection languages, in other words, the
d
intersection closure of
\mathrm{CFL}
(see, e.g.,
[
21
]
for this notation).
With the use of this succinct notation, we can say that the above language
L_{abc}
belongs to the difference
\mathrm{CFL}(2)-\mathrm{CFL}

L_{d}=\{a_{1}^{n_{1}}a_{2}^{n_{2}}\cdots a_{d}^{n_{d}}b_{1}^{n_{1}}b_{2}^{n_{2}}\cdots b_{d}^{n_{d}}\mid n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{d}\geq 0\}

\mathrm{CFL}(d)

d

of regular languages is closed under intersection, all unary languages in
\mathrm{DCFL}(\omega)
are regular as well. Although it is thus easy to find languages that are not in
\mathrm{DCFL}[\omega]
, such languages do not serve themselves to separate
\mathrm{CFL}
from
\mathrm{DCFL}(\omega)\cup\mathrm{DCFL}[\omega]
. As noted in Section
1.1
, Ginsburg and Greibach
[
4
]
remarked
with no proof
that the context-free language
Pal=\{ww^{R}\mid w\in\{0,1\}^{*}\}

is not in
\mathrm{DCFL}(\omega)
.
As another direct application of our pumping lemmas, we can provide a formal written proof of their remark. Furthermore, we can show that another context-free language
MPal^{\#}=\{w_{1}\#w_{2}\#\cdots\#w_{m}\#^{2}v_{1}\#v_{2}\#\cdots\#v_{n}\mid m,n\geq 1,\exists i\in[\min\{m,n\}](v_{i}=w_{i}^{R})\}
, where
w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{m},v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\in\{0,1\}^{*}
, is indeed outside of
\mathrm{DCFL}(\omega)\cup\mathrm{DCFL}[\omega]
.

Theorem 1.5

1.

The language
Pal
is in
\mathrm{CFL}\cap\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}\mathrm{CFL}
but not in
\mathrm{DCFL}[\omega]
.

2.

The language
MPal^{\#}
is in neither
\mathrm{DCFL}(\omega)
nor
\mathrm{DCFL}[\omega]
.

As an immediate consequence of Theorem
1.5
, we obtain Wotschke’s separation of
\mathrm{CFL}
from
\mathrm{DCFL}[\omega]
. Here, we stress that, unlike the work of Wotschke
[
19
,
20
]
, our proof does not depend on the main result (*) of Liu and Weiner. Actually, we can obtain a much stronger consequence from Theorem
1.5
(1) because
\mathrm{CFL}\cap\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}\mathrm{CFL}\subseteq\mathrm{DCFL}(\omega)
implies
\mathrm{CFL}\cap\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}\mathrm{CFL}=\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}(\mathrm{CFL}\cap\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}\mathrm{CFL})\subseteq\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}\mathrm{DCFL}(\omega)=\mathrm{DCFL}[\omega]
.

Corollary 1.6

\mathrm{CFL}\cap\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}\mathrm{CFL}\nsubseteq\mathrm{DCFL}(\omega)\cup\mathrm{DCFL}[\omega]
.

We then turn our interest to limited automata. For each index
d\geq 1
, we write
d\mbox{-}\mathrm{LDA}
for the family of all languages recognized by
d
-limited deterministic automata, in which their tape heads are allowed to rewrite tape symbols only during the first
d
accesses (except that, in the case where tape heads make a turn, we treat each turn as double visits).
Hibbard demonstrated that
d\mbox{-}\mathrm{LDA}\neq(d-1)\mbox{-}\mathrm{LDA}
for any integer
d\geq 3

[
7
]
.
We expand this separation result to the following in connection to the intersection hierarchy of dcf languages.

Proposition 1.7

For any
d\geq 2
,
d\mbox{-}\mathrm{LDA}\cap\mathrm{DCFL}[2^{d-1}]\nsubseteq(d-1)\mbox{-}\mathrm{LDA}\cup\mathrm{DCFL}[2^{d-1}-1]
.

The proofs of all the above-mentioned assertions will be given in Section
3
after introducing necessary notions and notation in the subsequent section.

Preparations: Notions and Notation

We begin with a detailed explanation of basic notions and notation, which are crucial in the rest of this exposition.

2.1
Fundamental Notions and Notation

The set of all
natural numbers
(including
0
) is denoted by
\mathbb{N}
. An
integer interval

[m,n]_{\mathbb{Z}}
for two integers
m
and
n
with
m\leq n
is the set
\{m,m+1,m+2,\ldots,n\}
.
For any integer
n\geq 1
,
[1,n]_{\mathbb{Z}}
is particularly abbreviated as
[n]
.
Given a set
S
, the notation
|S|
indicates the
cardinality
of
S
and the notation
{\cal P}(S)
refers to the
power set
of
S
.

An
alphabet
is a finite nonempty set of “symbols” or “letters”. A finite sequence of symbols in alphabet
\Sigma
is called a
string
over
\Sigma
. The
length
of such a string
x
, denoted
|x|
, is the total number of symbols occurring in
x
. The special symbol
\varepsilon
is used to denote the
empty string
of length
0
. We write
\Sigma^{*}
for the set of all strings over
\Sigma
and we set
\Sigma^{+}
to be
\Sigma^{*}-\{\varepsilon\}
. Given a number
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the notation
\Sigma^{n}
(resp.,
\Sigma^{\leq n}
) further denotes the set of all strings of length exactly
n
(resp., at most
n
) in
\Sigma^{*}
.
We say that
x
is a
substring
of
y
(denoted by
x\sqsubseteq y
) if
y=uxv
holds for certain strings
u
and
v
.
When a nonempty string
x
is expressed as
x=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{n}
with strings
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
, we call such an expression a
factorization
of
x
.

Any subset of
\Sigma^{*}
is called a
language
over
\Sigma
.
For a language
L
over
\Sigma
,
\Sigma^{*}-L
is the
complement
of
L
and expressed as
\overline{L}
as long as
\Sigma
is clear from the context. Given a family
{\cal F}
of languages,
\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}{\cal F}
expresses the complement family, which consists of the complements
\overline{L}
of all languages
L\in{\cal F}
.

For two language families
{\cal F}_{1}
and
{\cal F}_{2}
, the notation
{\cal F}_{1}\wedge{\cal F}_{2}
(resp.,
{\cal F}_{1}\vee{\cal F}_{2}
) denotes the family of all languages
L
over certain alphabets
\Sigma
such that there are two languages
L_{1}\in{\cal F}_{1}
and
L_{2}\in{\cal F}_{2}
over
\Sigma
satisfying
L=L_{1}\cap L_{2}
(resp.,
L=L_{1}\cup L_{2}
). Generally, for a given
k
-ary operation
op
over
k
languages, we say that a family
{\cal C}
of languages is closed under
op
if, for any
k
languages in
{\cal C}
,
op(L_{1},L_{2},\ldots,L_{k})
belongs to
{\cal C}
.

Given two binary strings
w
and
w^{\prime}
,
K(w)
means the
Kolmogorov complexity
of
w
and
K(w\mid w^{\prime})
expresses the
conditional Kolmogorov complexity
of
w
conditional to
w^{\prime}
. See, e.g.,
[
12
]
for basic properties.

2.2
Deterministic Pushdown Automata

The machine model of
one-way deterministic pushdown automata
(abbreviated as 1dpda’s) was introduced in 1966 by Ginsburg and Greibach
[
4
]
. Formally, a 1dpda
M
is a nonuple
(Q,\Sigma,\{|\!\!\mathrm{c},\$\},\Gamma,\delta,q_{0},Z_{0},Q_{acc},Q_{rej})
, where
Q
is a finite set of inner states,
\Sigma
is an input alphabet,
\Gamma
is a stack alphabet,
\delta
is a transition function from
(Q-Q_{halt})\times\check{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}\times\Gamma
to
{\cal P}(Q\times\Gamma^{*})
satisfying
|\delta(p,\sigma,a)|\leq 1
for any
(p,\sigma,a)
,
q_{0}
is the initial state in
Q
,
Z_{0}
is the bottom marker in
\Gamma
, and
Q_{acc}
and
Q_{rej}
are respectively sets of accepting states and of rejecting states, where
Q_{halt}=Q_{acc}\cup Q_{rej}
,
\check{\Sigma}=\Sigma\cup\{|\!\!\mathrm{c},\$\}
, and
\check{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}=\check{\Sigma}\cup\{\varepsilon\}
. Note that
|\!\!\mathrm{c}
and
\$
are respectively the left-endmarker and the right-endmarker. Let
\Gamma^{(-)}=\Gamma-\{Z_{0}\}
.
The
push size
of a 1ppda is the maximum length of any string pushed into a stack by any single move.
The 1dpda
M
must satisfy the following
deterministic requirement
:
|\delta(p,\sigma,a)\cup\delta(p,\varepsilon,a)|=1
holds for any
p\in Q
, any
a\in\Gamma
, and any nonempty symbol
\sigma\in\check{\Sigma}
.
When
\delta(q,\sigma,a)
is a singleton, say,
\{(p,w)\}
, we intentionally write “
\delta(p,\sigma,a)=(p,w)
”. This transition indicates that, when
M
is in inner state
q
scanning
\sigma
on an input tape and
a
on the top of a stack,
M
changes
q
to
p
, replace
a
by
w
, and move its tape head to the right whenever
\sigma\neq\varepsilon
.
Moreover, we require that the bottom marker
Z_{0}
is not removable, namely,
\delta(q,\sigma,Z_{0})\neq(p,\varepsilon)
for any
p,q\in Q
and
\sigma\in\check{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}
.
A content of a stack is expressed as
a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{k}
so that
a_{1}
is the topmost stack symbol,
a_{k}
is the bottom marker, and all
a_{i}
’s are placed in order from the top to the bottom in the stack.
The
stack height
refers to the size of a stack content, namely, the total number of symbols stored in the stack.
For instance, a stack content
a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{k}
with
a_{k}=Z_{0}
has stack height
k
. Since
Z_{0}
is not removable, the stack height is at least
1
at any step of a computation.

When
M
enters a halting state, which is either an accepting state or a rejecting state,
M
must
halt
. In the rest of this exposition, we always demand that
M
halts on all inputs. Given a string
w
, we say that
M

accepts
(resp.,
rejects
)
w
if
M
is in an accepting (resp., rejecting) state when it halts. We use the notation
L(M)
to denote the set of all strings accepted by
M
. If a language
L
satisfies
L=L(M)
,
M
is said to
recognize

L
. Such a language is called a
deterministic context-free (dcf) language
.

Given any number
d\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, a
d
-intersection deterministic context-free (dcf) language
refers to an intersection of
d
dcf languages. Let
\mathrm{DCFL}(d)
denote the family of all
d
-intersection dcf languages. Similarly, we define
d
-union dcf languages
and
\mathrm{DCFL}[d]
by substituting “union” for “intersection” in the above definition. It follows that
\mathrm{DCFL}[d]=\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}(\mathrm{DCFL}(d))
because of
\mathrm{DCFL}=\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}\mathrm{DCFL}
.

The following two lemmas will be quite useful in proving Theorems
1.3
and
1.5
together with Proposition
1.7
.

Lemma 2.1

[
19
,
20
]

\mathrm{DCFL}(d)
is closed under union, intersection with regular languages. In other words,
\mathrm{DCFL}(d)\wedge\mathrm{REG}\subseteq\mathrm{DCFL}(d)
and
\mathrm{DCFL}(d)\vee\mathrm{REG}\subseteq\mathrm{DCFL}(d)
. A similar statement holds for
\mathrm{DCFL}[d]
.

Proof.

For completeness, we provide the proof.
Take any language
L\in\mathrm{DCFL}[d]
and a regular language
A
. Take
d
appropriate languages
L_{1},L_{2},\ldots,L_{d}\in\mathrm{DCFL}
satisfying
L=\bigcap_{i\in[d]}L_{i}
. It then follows that
L\cap A=\bigcap_{i\in[d]}(L_{i}\cap A)
and
L\cup A=\bigcap_{i\in[d]}(L_{i}\cup A)
. Since
L_{i}\cap A
and
L_{i}\cup A
are both deterministic context-free, we conclude that
L\cap A
and
L\cup A
are both in
\mathrm{DCFL}(d)
. The case for
\mathrm{DCFL}[d]
is similarly treated.
∎

Lemma 2.2

Let
d\geq 1
be any integer.

1.

\mathrm{DCFL}(d)=\mathrm{DCFL}(d+1)
iff
\mathrm{DCFL}[d]=\mathrm{DCFL}[d+1]
.

2.

For any language
L\in\mathrm{DCFL}(d)
, it follows that
A\cap\overline{L}\in\mathrm{DCFL}[d]
for any language
A\in\mathrm{REG}
.

Proof.

(1) Assume that
\mathrm{DCFL}(d)=\mathrm{DCFL}(d+1)
. By taking complementation, it then follows that
\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}(\mathrm{DCFL}(d))=\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}(\mathrm{DCFL}(d+1))
. This is equivalent to
\mathrm{DCFL}[d]=\mathrm{DCFL}[d+1]
. The other direction is similarly proven.

(2) Assume that
L\in\mathrm{DCFL}(d)
. From this follows
\overline{L}\in\mathrm{DCFL}[d]
. Lemma
2.1
then yields
A\cap\overline{L}\in\mathrm{DCFL}[d]
for any regular language
A
.
∎

From Lemma
2.2
(1) follows Corollary
1.4
, provided that Theorem
1.3
is true. Theorem
1.3
itself will be proven in Section
3
.

2.3
Ideal Shape Lemma

We will give the proofs of the pumping lemmas for
\mathrm{DCFL}[d]
(Lemmas
1.1
and
1.2
) in Section
4
. To make the proofs simpler, we intend to use the fact that any 1dpda can be converted into a specific form.
Let us recall from
[
22
,
23
]
a special “push-pop-controlled” form (called an
ideal shape
), in which (i) pop operations always take place by first reading an input symbol and then making a series (one or more) of the pop operations without reading any further input symbol and (ii) push operations add single symbols
without altering any existing stack content.
The original notion was meant for
one-way probabilistic pushdown automata
(or 1ppda’s); however, in this exposition, we wish to apply this notion to 1dpda’s.
To be more formal, a 1ppda
in an ideal shape
is restricted to the following moves regarding its stack operations.
(1) Scanning
\sigma\in\Sigma
, preserve the topmost stack symbol (called a
stationary operation
). (2) Scanning
\sigma\in\Sigma
, push a new symbol
u
(
\in\Gamma^{(-)}
) without changing any other symbol in the stack. (3) Scanning
\sigma\in\Sigma
, pop the topmost stack symbol. (4) Without scanning an input symbol (i.e.,
\varepsilon
-move), pop the topmost stack symbol. (5) The stack operations (4) comes only after either (3) or (4).

It was shown in
[
22
,
23
]
that any 1ppda can be converted into its “equivalent” 1ppda in an ideal shape. We say that two 1dpda’s are
error-equivalent
if, for any input
x
, their acceptance/rejection coincide.

Lemma 2.3

[Ideal Shape Lemma for 1ppda’s
[
22
,
23
]
]

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. Any
n
-state 1ppda
M
with stack alphabet size
m
and push size
e
can be converted into another error-equivalent 1ppda
N
in an ideal shape with
O(en^{2}m^{2}(2m)^{2enm})
states and stack alphabet size
O(enm(2m)^{2enm})
.

As noted in
[
22
,
23
]
, by appropriately choosing an error probability of a 1ppda, Lemma
2.3
is applicable to 1dpda’s.

2.4
Deterministic Limited Automata

Hibbard
[
7
]
discussed another machine model of scan limited automata in 1967. In this exposition, we follow a reformulation of his model by Pighizzini and Pisoni
[
14
,
15
]
and Yamakami
[
22
]
under the name of
deterministic
d
-limited automata
(or
d
-lda’s, for short). Given
d\geq 1
, a
d
-lda
M
is formally an octuple
(Q,\Sigma,\{|\!\!\mathrm{c},\$\},\{\Gamma^{(e)}\}_{e\in[d]},\delta,q_{0},Q_{acc},Q_{rej})
with
\delta:(Q-Q_{halt})\times\check{\Sigma}\times\Gamma\to Q\times\Gamma\times D
, where
D=\{-1,+1\}
,
\Gamma=\bigcup_{e\in[0,d]_{\mathbb{Z}}}
with
\Gamma^{(0)}=\Sigma
,
\{|\!\!\mathrm{c},\$\}\subseteq\Gamma^{(d)}
, and
\Gamma^{(i)}\cap\Gamma^{(j)}=\varnothing
for any distinct pair
i,j\in[0,d]_{\mathbb{Z}}
.
We remark that
M
has a single rewritable input/work tape and moves its tape head in both directions. In particular,
M
makes no
\varepsilon
-move. If
\delta(q,\sigma)=(p,\tau,l)
, then
M
changes its inner state
q
to
p
, writes
\tau
over
\sigma
, and moves its tape head in direction
d
.
Assuming further that
\sigma\in\Gamma^{(i)}
and
\tau\in\Gamma^{(j)}
for
i,j\in[0,d]_{\mathbb{Z}}
, we demand that (1) if
i=d
, then
\sigma=\tau
and
j=d
, (2) if
i<d
and
i
is even, then
j=i+2^{(1-l)/2}
, and (3) if
i<d
and
i
is odd, then
j=i+2^{(1+l)/2}
. These requirements imply that no symbol in
\Gamma^{(d)}
is replaced by any other symbol.

Here, we reuse the terminology defined for 1dpda’s in Section
2.2
and we do not intend to repeat the same kinds
of definitions for
d
-lda’s.
The notation
d\mbox{-}\mathrm{LDA}
is used for the family of all languages accepted by
d
-lda’s. It is known that
\mathrm{DCFL}=2\mbox{-}\mathrm{LDA}

[
15
]
and
d\mbox{-}\mathrm{LDA}\neq(d+1)\mbox{-}\mathrm{LDA}
for any index
d\in\mathbb{N}^{+}

[
7
]
.

3
Proofs of Three Separation Claims

The proofs of the two pumping lemmas for
\mathrm{DCFL}[d]
(namely, Lemmas
1.1
and
1.2
) require a lengthy argument and we postpone the proofs of the lemmas until
Section
4
.
Meanwhile, we intend to concentrate on the three separation claims (Theorems
1.3
and
1.5
and Proposition
1.7
) announced in Section
2.1
and provide their detailed proofs in this section.
To understand these proofs better, we first demonstrate a simple and easy example of how to apply the first pumping lemma for
\mathrm{DCFL}[d]
(Lemma
1.1
) to obtain the desired separation between
\mathrm{DCFL}[d]

and
\mathrm{DCFL}[d-1]
.

Proposition 3.1

Let
d\geq 2
and let
L_{(d)}=\{a^{n}b^{kn}\mid k\in[d],n\geq 0\}
. It then follows that
L_{(d)}\in\mathrm{DCFL}[d]-\mathrm{DCFL}[d-1]
.

Proof.

Let
d\geq 2
. Since
L_{(d)}
can be expressed as
\bigcup_{k\in[d]}L^{(k)}
using the
d
languages
L^{(k)}=\{a^{n}b^{kn}\mid n\geq 0\}
for any number
k\in[d]
,
L_{(d)}
clearly belongs to
\mathrm{DCFL}[d]
.
Toward the non-membership
L_{(d)}\notin\mathrm{DCFL}[d-1]
, we assume otherwise and
apply the first pumping lemma for
\mathrm{DCFL}[d]
to
L_{(d)}
.
We take a pumping-lemma constant
c>0
that satisfies the lemma. Let
n=c+1
and consider
x=a^{n}
and
y^{(i)}=b^{in}
for each index
i\in[d]
. Since each string
w_{i}=a^{n}b^{in}
belongs to
L_{(d)}
, there is a special index pair
j,k\in[d]
with
j<k
such that
w_{j}
and
w_{k}
satisfy the lemma.

Consider the condition (1) of the lemma. Let
x^{\prime}=a^{n}b^{jn-1}
,
y=b
, and
z=b^{(k-j)n+1}
. Firstly, let us consider a factorization
x^{\prime}=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}x_{5}
with
|x_{2}x_{4}|\geq 1
and
|x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}|\leq c
. Since
x_{1}x_{2}^{i}x_{3}x_{4}^{i}x_{5}y\in L_{(d)}
holds for any
i\in\mathbb{N}
, it follows that
x_{2}\in\{a\}^{*}
and
x_{4}\in\{b\}^{*}
.
Because of the definition of
L_{(d)}
, we also conclude that
x_{2}\neq\varepsilon
and
x_{4}\neq\varepsilon
.
Let
x_{2}=a^{m}
and
x_{4}=b^{r}
for certain numbers
m,r\in[c]
. Note that
x_{1}x_{2}^{i}x_{3}x_{4}^{i}x_{5}y
equals
a^{n+(i-1)m}b^{jn+(i-1)r}
.
Since this string is in
L_{(d)}
,
n+(i-1)m=g(jn+(i-1)r)
follows for a certain
g\in[d]
.
This implies that
(jg-1)n=(m-gr)(i-1)
. We then obtain
jg-1=m-gr=0
, which further implies that
j=g=1
and
m=r
. Similarly, from
x_{1}x_{2}^{i}x_{3}x_{4}^{i}x_{5}z\in L_{(d)}
, it follows that
n+(i-1)m=g^{\prime}(kn+(i-1)r)
for an appropriate number
g^{\prime}\in[d]
. Thus,
(kg^{\prime}-1)n=(m-g^{\prime}r)(i-1)
. This implies
k=g^{\prime}=1
and
m=r
. Since
j\neq k
, we obtain a contradiction.

Secondly, let us consider the condition (2) with appropriate factorizations
x^{\prime}=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}
,
y=y_{1}y_{2}y_{3}
, and
z=z_{1}z_{2}z_{3}
with
|x_{2}|\geq 1
and
|x_{2}x_{3}|\leq c
such that
x_{1}x_{2}^{i}x_{3}y_{1}y_{2}^{i}y_{3}\in L_{(d)}
and
x_{1}x_{2}^{i}x_{3}z_{1}z_{2}^{i}z_{3}\in L_{(d)}
for any number
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
|x_{2}x_{3}|\leq c
, we obtain
x_{2}\in\{b\}^{+}
. Assume that
x_{2}=b^{m}
for a certain number
m\in[c]
. However, this is impossible because
x_{1}x_{2}^{i}x_{3}y_{1}y_{2}^{i}y_{3}
has the form
a^{n}b^{jn+(i-1)m}
and the exponent of
b
is not of the form
tn
for any number
t\in[d]
. Therefore,
L_{(d)}
is not in
\mathrm{DCFL}[d-1]
.
∎

Theorem
1.3
relates to the languages
L_{d}^{(\leq)}=\{a_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots a_{d}^{n_{d}}b_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots b_{d}^{m_{d}}\mid\forall i\in[d](n_{i}\leq m_{i})\}
over the alphabet
\Sigma_{d}=\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{d},b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{d}\}
and
NPal^{\#}_{d}=\{w_{1}\#\cdots\#w_{d}\#v_{1}\#\cdots\#v_{d}\mid\forall i\in[d](w_{i},v_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{*}\wedge v_{i}\neq w_{i}^{R})\}
over the alphabet
\{0,1,\#\}
. We employ the two pumping lemmas for
\mathrm{DCFL}[d]
to verify the theorem.

Theorem
1.3
.
(rephrased)

Let
d\geq 2
.

1.

The language
L_{d}^{(\leq)}
is not in
\mathrm{DCFL}(d-1)
.

2.

The language
NPal^{\#}_{d}
is not in
\mathrm{DCFL}(d-1)
.

Proof.

Let
d\geq 2
be any positive integer.

(1)
Our first target is the language
L^{(\leq)}_{d}
over the alphabet
\Sigma_{d}=\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{d},b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{d}\}
. For each index
i\in[d]
, we set
L^{(i)}=\{a_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots a_{d}^{n_{d}}b_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots b_{d}^{m_{d}}\mid n_{i}>m_{i}\geq 0\}
. It is clear that
L_{d}^{(\leq)}
coincides with
\bigcap_{i\in[d]}L^{(i)}
, and therefore
L_{d}^{(\leq)}
belongs to
\mathrm{DCFL}(d)
.
Our next goal is set to verify the non-membership of
L_{d}^{(\leq)}
to
\mathrm{DCFL}(d-1)

Next, we consider the condition (2), in which there are three factorizations

Theorem
1.5
.
(rephrased)

1.

The language
Pal

\mathrm{CFL}\cap\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}\mathrm{CFL}-\mathrm{DCFL}[\omega]
.

2.

The language
MPal^{\#}
is in
\mathrm{CFL}-(\mathrm{DCFL}(\omega)\cup\mathrm{DCFL}[\omega])

Proof.

(1)
It is easy to see that
Pal
is in
\mathrm{CFL}
.
We then claim that
Pal
is also in
\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}\mathrm{CFL}
. Let
NPal=\{xy\mid|x|=|y|,x,y\in\{0,1\}^{*},y\neq x^{R}\}
and
ODD=\{w\in\{0,1\}^{*}\mid|w|\text{ is odd }\}
. Note that
\overline{Pal}=ODD\cup(\overline{ODD}\cap NPal)

Since

, it suffices to verify that
\overline{ODD}\cap NPal\in\mathrm{CFL}
. Consider the following 1npda. Given an input

in

, slit

nondeterministically into

, store

into a stack, and pop

bit by bit while reading

. If

, then we reject

. Assume otherwise. If we discover any discrepancy between

and

Next, we intend to verify that
Pal\notin\mathrm{DCFL}[d]
for any

.
Toward a contradiction, we assume that
Pal\in\mathrm{DCFL}[d]
for a certain index

. We apply to

the first pumping lemma for

and take a pumping-lemma constant

.

Here, we use “blocks” of

and

. Choose
w_{k}=0^{n}1^{n}0^{n}1^{n}\cdots 0^{n}
(
2k+1

0^{n}
s and
1^{n}
s) for any index

. For example,
w_{1}=0^{n}1^{n}0^{n}
and
w_{2}=0^{n}1^{n}0^{n}1^{n}0^{n}
.
By the first pumping lemma, there is a distinct pair
i,j\in[d+1]
with
i<j
satisfying the lemma. We consider the first case where

and

are even.
Let
x^{\prime}=0^{n}1^{n}0^{n}\cdots 0^{n}
(

y=1^{n}0^{n}\cdots 0^{n}
(
i
blocks of
0^{n}
), and

(

blocks of
0^{n}
) so that

and

.
Consider the condition (1) of the lemma. There is a factorization

with

and
|x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}|\leq c
such that

and

for any number

. It follows that

must be a substring of

,

,

, ot

. In any case, nevertheless, we obtain a contradiction.

Let us consider the condition (2) with three factorizations

,

, and

with

and
|x_{2}x_{3}|\leq c
satisfying
x_{1}x_{2}^{i}x_{3}y_{1}y_{2}^{i}y_{3}\in Pal
and
x_{1}x_{2}^{i}x_{3}z_{1}z_{2}^{i}z_{3}\in Pal
for any number

. We then obtain

because of
|x_{2}x_{3}|\leq c
. Thus, in particular,
x_{1}x_{3}y_{1}y_{3}\notin Pal

(2)
The claim
MPal^{\#}\notin\mathrm{DCFL}[\omega]
comes from (1) since

Pal\in\mathrm{DCFL}[d]
for any

.
Next, we wish to show that

. Toward a contradiction, we assume otherwise. For convenience, we define

and set
NPal^{\#}=\{w_{1}\#\cdots\#w_{m}\#^{2}v_{1}\#\cdots\#v_{n}\mid m,n\geq 1,\forall i\in[\min\{m,n\}](v_{i}\neq w_{i}^{R})\}
, where
x_{1},\cdots x_{m},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\in\{0,1\}^{*}
.
Note that
NPal^{\#}=FORM\cap\overline{MPal^{\#}}
.
Since
FORM\in\mathrm{REG}
, by Lemma
2.2
(2), it follows from our assumption that
NPal^{\#}\in\mathrm{DCFL}[\omega]
.
We then take the minimum index
d\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
for which
NPal^{\#}
is in
\mathrm{DCFL}[d]
.
As a special case of
NPal^{\#}
, we consider
NPal^{\#}_{2}=\{w_{1}\#w_{2}\#v_{1}\#v_{2}\mid w_{1},w_{2},v_{1},v_{2}\in\{0,1\}^{*},v_{1}\neq w_{1}^{R},v_{2}\neq w_{2}^{R}\}

,

is written as the

.

We choose a sufficiently large integer

,

, and

by flipping the

. For instance,

,

for the set of all strings of the form

.

, we choose an index

Proposition

Proposition

.
(rephrased)

For any

,

In this proof, we intend to use a series of special languages used in
[

but not in
\mathrm{DCFL}[k-1]

.
Let us recall from
[

[

As shown in
[

, arXiv version]

belongs to

but it is not in

L_{k}\notin\mathrm{DCFL}[2^{k-1}-1]

Let

be any integer. For each fixed index

, we write

for an arbitrary string of the form

for any numbers

.
We set

to be composed of all strings

of the form

if

w_{2}\#w_{4}\#\cdots\#w_{k-1}\#w_{k}\#w_{k-2}\#\cdots\#w_{5}\#w_{3}\#w_{1}
if

is odd, together with the following conditions (a)–(c).

(a)

It holds that
n_{1}\leq m_{1}
.

(b)

Let

denote any index in
[2,k]_{\mathbb{Z}}
. If
n_{j}\leq m_{j}
, then either
n_{j-1}=m_{j-1}
or
m_{j-1}=p_{j-1}
holds. If
m_{j}\leq p_{j}
, then either
n_{j-1}<m_{j-1}
or
m_{j-1}<p_{j-1}
holds.

(c)

Either
n_{k}=m_{k}
or
m_{k}=p_{k}
holds.

For instance,
L_{2}
equals
\{a^{n_{2}}b^{m_{2}}c^{p_{2}}\#a^{n_{1}}b^{m_{1}}c^{p_{1}}\mid(n_{1}=m_{1}\wedge n_{2}=m_{2})\text{ or }(n_{1}<m_{1}\wedge m_{2}=p_{2})\}
. For convenience, we also define another language

obtained from

by replacing the last term

of

with
a^{p_{1}}b^{m_{1}}c^{n_{1}}
.

(1) As the first step, we prove the membership of both
L_{k}
and
L^{\prime}_{k}
to
\mathrm{DCFL}[2^{k-1}]
.

(i) When

, we define

, where

. Clearly,

equals

, and thus

belongs to
\mathrm{DCFL}[2]
. Similarly, we can prove that
L^{\prime}_{k}
is in
\mathrm{DCFL}[2]
.

(ii) Consider the case where

is an even number with

(iii) The case of odd

(2)
As the second step, we prove that

4.1
Boundaries, Turns, and
\varepsilon
-Enhanced Strings

Let us begin with two elementary notions of
boundaries
and
boundary blocks
, which are necessary to introduce other important notions.
For this purpose, we visualize a
single move
of a 1dpda
M
as a series of three consecutive actions: (i) firstly replacing a topmost stack symbol, (ii) secondly updating an inner state, and (iii) thirdly either moving a tape head to the right or making it stay still (in the case of a
\varepsilon
-move).

A
boundary
is a borderline between two adjacent tape cells (except for the case of the leftmost tape cell). We index all such boundaries from
0
to
||\!\!\mathrm{c}x\$|
as follows. The boundary
0
is located at the left of cell
0
and boundary
i+1
is located between cell
i
and cell
i+1
for each
i\geq 0
.
We often use both “cell indices” and “boundaries” to specify an area of the input tape interchangeably.
When a string
xy
is written in
|xy|
consecutive cells, the
(x,y)
-boundary
refers to the boundary
|x|+1
, which separates between
x
and
y
.
A
boundary block
between two boundaries
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
with
t_{1}<t_{2}
is a consecutive series of boundaries between
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
(including
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
). These numbers
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
are called the
ends
of this boundary block. For brevity, we write
[t_{1},t_{2}]
to denote such a boundary block between
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
.
For each boundary block
[t_{1},t_{2}]
, its
fringe
is either the boundary
t_{1}-1
or the boundary
t_{2}+1
if they actually exist.
The
(t_{1},t_{2})
-region
indicates all the consecutive “cells” located in the boundary block
[t_{1},t_{2}]
. When an input string
x
is written in this
(t_{1},t_{2})
-region, we conveniently call this region the
x
-region
as long as the region is clear from
the context. On the contrary,
x
is called the
(t_{1},t_{2})
-regional string
.

Figure 2:
A history of the changes of stack heights.

The
stack height
of
M
at boundary
t
is the length of the stack content while the tap head is passing though this boundary
t
.
Given
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
with
t_{1}<t_{2}
,
the boundary block
[t_{1},t_{2}]
is
flat
if the stack height does not change in the
(t_{1},t_{2})
-region. A boundary block
[t_{1},t_{2}]
is called
convex
if there is a boundary
s
between
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
(namely,
s\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
) such that there is no pop operation in the
(t_{1},s)
-region and there is no push operation in the
(s,t_{2})
-region.
A boundary block
[t_{1},t_{2}]
is
pseudo-convex
if the stack height at any boundary
s\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
does not go below
h_{1}+\frac{h_{2}-h_{1}}{t_{2}-t_{1}}(s-t_{1})
, where
h_{i}
is the stack height at the boundary
t_{i}
for any index
i\in\{1,2\}
. By their definitions, either convex or flat boundary blocks are pseudo-convex.

A
peak
(resp., a
pit
) is a boundary
t
for which the stack heights at the boundaries
t-1
and
t+1
are smaller (resp., greater) than the stack height at boundary
t
. A
plateau
is a boundary block
[t,t^{\prime}]
such that any stack height at boundary
i\in[t,t^{\prime}]
is the same.
This stack height is called the
height
of the plateau.
A
basin
(resp., an
elevated plateau
)
[t,t^{\prime}]
is a plateau and the stack heights at all fringes of this plateau are greater (resp., smaller) than the height of the plateau whenever the fringes exist.
A
hill
is a boundary block
[t,t^{\prime}]
such that (i) the stack height at the boundary
t
and the stack height at the boundary
t^{\prime}
coincide, (ii) there exists either a peak or an elevated plateau, but not both, in
[t,t^{\prime}]
, (iii) there is no pit or basin in
[t,t^{\prime}]
, and (iv) one of the ends of the hill is either a pit or the left edge of a basin. The lowest stack height of the hill is particularly called the
bottom height
.
The
height
of the hill is the difference between its bottom height and its highest stack height.
We briefly call either a peak or an elevated plateau existing in a hill by a
hill top
.
Those concepts are illustrated in
Fig.
2
.

An
upward slope
means a non-flat boundary block in which the stack height never decreases. In contrast, a
downward slope
is a non-flat boundary block in which no increase of the stack height occurs. A
slope
refers to either an upward slope or a downward slope.

A
turn
is intuitively a change of stack height from “nondecreasing” to “decreasing” discussed in 1966 by Ginsburg and Spanier
[
5
]
(who actually defined “turn” in a slightly different way).
We describe this notion in terms of boundary blocks and use it to partition an entire stack history.
A
turning point
is either a peak or the right edge of an elevated plateau.
A
turn
refers to a boundary block
[t_{1},t_{2}]
in which there is exactly one turning point such that the left fringe (if any) is a part of a downward slope and the right fringe is a part of either an upward slope or a basin.
The
height
of a turn is the difference between the maximum stack height and the minimum stack height in the turn. The minimum stack height in particular occurs at either end of the turn and is also called the
bottom height
.

Given strings over an alphabet
\Sigma
,
\varepsilon
-enhanced strings
are strings over the extended alphabet
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
(
=\Sigma\cup\{\varepsilon\}
). Here, the notation
\varepsilon
is treated as a special input symbol expressing the “absence” of symbols, not the “empty string.”
Even for an
\varepsilon
-enhanced string
x
, the notation
|x|
is used for the total number of non-
\varepsilon
-symbols in
x
. On the contrary, we use the new notation
|x|_{\varepsilon}
to denote the length of
x
by counting
\varepsilon
as an independent symbol. For instance, if
x=001\varepsilon 0110\varepsilon 10
, then
|x|=9
and
|x|_{\varepsilon}=11
.
An
\varepsilon
-enhanced 1dpda
(or an
\varepsilon
-1dpa, for short) is a 1dpda that takes
\varepsilon
-enhanced input strings and works as a standard 1dpda except that a tape head always moves to the right without stopping. This tape head movement is sometimes called “real time.”

Lemma 4.1

For any 1dpda
M
, there exists an
\varepsilon
-1dpda

such that, for any input string

, there is an appropriate
\varepsilon
-enhanced string

for which

accepts (resp., rejects)

accepts (resp., rejects)
\hat{x}
. Moreover,

\hat{x}
is uniquely determined from
x
and

. In this case,
\hat{x}
is said to be

from

by

. In addition,

can be made to be in an ideal shape.

Proof.

Let
M
be any 1dpda. By Lemma

, we can assume that

is in an ideal shape. The desired machine

is defined as follows. On any input, if

makes a non-
\varepsilon
-move, then

simulates this single step. On the contrary, in the case where

makes an
\varepsilon
-move, if

reads a symbol

on an input tape, then

simulates
M
’s move and then moves its tape head to the right; otherwise,

enters a rejecting state. This forces an input string to match

’s
\varepsilon
-moves and thus ensures the uniqueness of
\hat{x}
. We then define an
\varepsilon
-enhanced string
\hat{x}
so that
N
always reads
\varepsilon
whenever

makes an
\varepsilon
-move.
By the above constructions of

and

, it is obvious that

4.2
State-Stack Pairs and Mutual Correlations

We next introduce another important notion of state-stack pairs.
Let
M
denote either a 1dpda or an
\varepsilon
-1dpda with a set
Q
of inner states and a stack alphabet
\Gamma
, and assume that
M
is in an ideal shape. A
state-stack pair
at boundary
i
is a pair
(q,\gamma)
of inner state
q
and stack content
\gamma
(which is expressed as
a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{k}
from the top to the bottom of the stack with
a_{k}=Z_{0}
),
namely,
(q,\gamma)
in
Q\times(\Gamma^{(-)})^{*}Z_{0}
.
In particular,
(q_{0},Z_{0})
is the state-stack pair at the boundary
0
.
In a computation of
M
on input
x
, a state-stack pair
(q,\gamma)
at boundary
i
with
i\geq 1
refers to the machine’s current status where

M
is reading a certain input symbol, say,
\sigma
at cell
i-1
in a certain state, say,
p
with a certain stack content
a\gamma^{\prime}
for
a\in\Gamma
, and
M
then changes its inner state to
q
, modifying
a
by either pushing another symbol

satisfying
\gamma=ba\gamma^{\prime}
or popping

to make
\gamma=\gamma^{\prime}
.
It is important to remark that any computation of
M
on

can be expressed as a series of state-stack pairs at every boundary in the
|\!\!\mathrm{c}x\$
-region.
Consider a sequence
(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{m})
in which each
\alpha_{i}
is a state-stack pair at step

for any number

. Such a sequence completely describes a computation of
M
if

and

halts in exactly

Two boundaries
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
with

are

if (1) there are two state-stack pairs
(q,\gamma)
and
(p,\gamma)
at the boundaries
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
, respectively, and (2) the boundary block
[t_{1},t_{2}]
is pseudo-convex. For four boundaries

with
t_{1}<t_{2}<t_{3}<t_{4}
, the two boundary blocks
[t_{1},t_{2}]
and
[t_{3},t_{4}]
are
mutually correlated
if (1
′
)
[t_{1},t_{2}]
,
[t_{2},t_{3}]
, and
[t_{3},t_{4}]
are all pseudo-convex, (2
′
) there are two state-stack pairs
(q,\gamma)
and
(p,\alpha\gamma)
at the boundaries
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
, respectively, and (3
′
) there are two state-stack pairs
(s,\alpha\gamma)
and
(r,\gamma)
at the boundaries
t_{3}
and
t_{4}
, respectively, for appropriately chosen

,
\gamma\in(\Gamma^{(-)})^{*}Z_{0}
, and

.
Mutually correlated boundaries or boundary blocks are closely related to the iteration of certain parts of strings.
Furthermore, a pair
([t_{1},t_{2}],[t_{3},t_{4}])

Let
M
denote any
\varepsilon
-1dpda in an ideal shape and let

be any nonempty string.

1.

Let

is the

-boundary and

is the

-boundary. If the boundaries

and
t_{2}
are mutually correlated and inner states at the boundaries

.

1\leq t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots<t_{m}
for which
(q,\gamma),(q,\alpha_{1}\gamma),\ldots,(q,\alpha_{[m-2,1]}),(q,\alpha_{[m-1,1]}\gamma)
are state-stack pairs at the boundaries
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{m}
, respectively, and that there are boundaries
r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{m}
with
t_{m}\leq r_{1}<r_{2}<\cdots<r_{m}
for which
(p_{1},\alpha_{[m-1,1]}\gamma),(p_{2},\alpha_{[m-2,1]}\gamma),\ldots,(p_{m-1},\alpha_{1}\gamma),(p_{m},\gamma)
are state-stack pairs at the boundaries
r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{m}

and
[r_{i},r_{i+1}]
are pseudo-convex for each index

1.

If
m>|Q|
, then there exist two distinct indices
i,j\in[m]
satisfying
p_{i}=p_{j}
.

2.

If
m>|Q|^{2}
, then there exists a subset
C
of

of size more than

such that

holds for any pair

.

Proof.

(1) Consider the set
P=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{m}\}
of inner states. Since

, it follows that
|P|\leq|Q|<m
. Thus, there is a distinct pair

satisfying

by the pigeonhole principle.

(2) Assume that there is no such subset

stated in the lemma. For each inner state

for all

4.3
Stack-Operational Pairs

It is known that, for a context-free language
L
, a structural property given by an iterative pair for
L
is closely related to a generative property of a “grammar” generating
L
. This latter notion is described in terms of

grammatical pairs
in, e.g.,
[
1
, Section VIII.2]
.
In connection to nondegenerate iterative pairs for dcf languages, we introduce another notion of
stack-operational pairs
to describe a structural property given by the stack behaviors of 1dpda’s.

Let us consider a dcf language
L
and a 1dpda
M=(Q,\Sigma,\{|\!\!\mathrm{c},\$\},\Gamma,\delta,q_{0},Q_{acc},Q_{rej})
recognizing
L
. We further assume that
M
is in an ideal shape and consider a string
w\in\Sigma^{*}
and its factorization
uxvyz
.
We say that
(x,y)
is a
stack-operational pair w.r.t.
M
if the pair
([t_{1},t_{2}],[t_{3},t_{4}])
of boundary blocks is good, where
t_{1}
,
t_{2}
,
t_{3}
, and
t_{4}
represent the
(u,x)
-boundary, the
(x,v)
-boundary, the
(v,y)
-boundary, and the
(y,z)
-boundary, respectively.

Next, we consider two factorizations
uxvyz
and
u^{\prime}x^{\prime}v^{\prime}y^{\prime}z^{\prime}
. We say that
u^{\prime}x^{\prime}v^{\prime}y^{\prime}z^{\prime}
is
deduced from

uxvyz
if (1) there exists a number
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
such that
u^{\prime}x^{\prime}v^{\prime}y^{\prime}z^{\prime}=ux^{n_{0}}vy^{n_{0}}z
and (2) there are two numbers
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
and four strings
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}
such that
x=x_{1}x_{2}
,
y=y_{1}y_{2}
,
x^{\prime}=(x_{2}x_{1})^{p}
,
y^{\prime}=(y_{2}y_{1})^{q}
,
u^{\prime}\in ux^{*}x_{1}
,
v^{\prime}\in x_{2}x^{*}vy^{*}y_{1}
, and
z^{\prime}\in y_{2}y^{*}z
.
It instantly follows that
u^{\prime}(x^{\prime})^{k+1}v^{\prime}(y^{\prime})^{l+1}=ux^{n_{0}+pk}vy^{n_{0}+ql}z
for any pair
k,l\in\mathbb{N}
. With no proof, we also claim that, if
(x,y)
is a nondegenerate iterative pair of
uxvyz
for
L(M)
and
u^{\prime}x^{\prime}v^{\prime}y^{\prime}z^{\prime}
is deduced from

, then
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
is also a nondegenerate iterative pair of
u^{\prime}x^{\prime}v^{\prime}y^{\prime}z^{\prime}
for
L(M)
.

By Lemma
4.2
(2), any stack-operational pair is also an iterative pair. The converse in general does not hold. However, the deducibility further establishes a close relationship between iterative pairs and stack-operational pairs. This relation makes it possible to translate nondegenerate iterative pairs
to nondegenerate stack-operational pairs.

Proposition 4.4

Let
L\in\mathrm{DCFL}
and let

denote any 1dpda in an ideal shape recognizing

for

,
\bar{x}
,
\bar{v}
,
\bar{y}
, and

–

.
Therefore,
\bar{u}\bar{x}\bar{v}\bar{y}\bar{z}
is deduced from
uxvyz
. By the definition of
\bar{x}
and
\bar{y}
,
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is stack-operational w.r.t.
M
.
We intend to prove that
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is nondegenerate.
Let us assume otherwise and examine two separate cases.

Case 1: Consider the case where, for any fixed number
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there are infinitely many
l\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
\bar{u}\bar{x}^{k}\bar{v}\bar{y}^{l}\bar{z}\in L
. It then follows that
\bar{u}\bar{x}^{k}\bar{v}\bar{y}^{l}\bar{z}=ux^{\prime}(x^{d})^{k}x^{\prime\prime}vy^{\prime}(y^{e})^{l}y^{\prime\prime}z=ux^{k^{\prime}}vy^{l^{\prime}}z
for certain numbers
k^{\prime},l^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus,
(x,y)
is degenerate, a contradiction.

Case 2: Consider the case where for any
l\in\mathbb{N}
, there are infinitely many
k\in\mathbb{N}
for which
\bar{u}\bar{x}^{k}\bar{v}\bar{y}^{l}\bar{z}\in L
. This case can be handled similarly to Case 1.

\Box

4.4
Proof of the First Pumping Lemma for DCFL[
d
]

We are now ready to describe the proof of the first pumping lemma for
\mathrm{DCFL}[d]
(Lemma
1.1
). Our proof has two distinguished parts depending on the value of
d
. The first part of the proof targets the basis case of
d=1
.
As noted in Section
1.2
, this special case directly corresponds to Yu’s pumping lemma
[
24
, Lemma 1]
. To prove his lemma, Yu utilized a so-called
left-part theorem
of his own for
\mathrm{LR}(k)
grammars. As a caviar of this exposition, we intend to re-prove the lemma using 1dpda’s with no reference to
\mathrm{LR}(k)
grammars.

Let
\Sigma
be any alphabet and take any infinite dcf language
L
over
\Sigma
in
\mathrm{DCFL}[d]
.
Our proof argument is easily extendable to
one-way nondeterministic pushdown automata
(or 1npda’s) and to the “standard” pumping lemma for
\mathrm{CFL}
(cf.
[
8
]
).
The second part of the proof deals with the general case of
d\geq 2
.
Hereafter, we discuss these two parts separately.

Basis Case of
d=1
:

We begin our proof for the basis case of
d=1
.
By Lemma
4.1
, we take an appropriate
\varepsilon
-1dpda
M=(Q,\Sigma_{\varepsilon},\{|\!\!\mathrm{c},\$\},\Gamma,\delta,q_{0},Z_{0},Q_{acc},Q_{rej})
in an ideal shape that recognizes
L
. For the desired constant
c
, we set
c=2^{6|Q|^{6}}
.
Firstly, we take two arbitrary strings
w_{1}=xy
and
w_{2}=xz
in
L
over
\Sigma
with
|y|,|z|\geq 1
and
|x|>c
such that
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
have iterative pairs for
L
.
Following the use of an
\varepsilon
-enhanced machine, we naturally expand these given input strings to their corresponding
\varepsilon
-enhanced strings. For readability, we continue using the same notations
w_{1}
,
w_{1}
,
x
,
y
, and
z
to denote the corresponding
\varepsilon
-enhanced strings.

We assume that the condition (1) fails. Our goal is then to verify that the condition (2) of the lemma indeed holds.
There are four specific cases to deal with. Hereafter, we intend to discuss them separately. Remember that, since
M
’s tape head moves in only one direction, every state-stack pair at each boundary in the
x
-region does not depend on the choice of
y
and

.

Case 1: Consider the case where there are two boundaries
t_{1},t_{2}
with
1\leq t_{1}<t_{2}\leq|x|

|t_{2}-t_{1}|\leq c
such that (i) the boundaries
t_{1}
and

and

In this case, we can factorize

so that

is the
(x_{1},x_{2}x_{3})
-boundary and
t_{2}
is the
(x_{1}x_{2},x_{3})
-boundary. By Lemma

and
x_{1}x_{2}^{i}x_{3}z\in L
for any number

. Thus, the condition (1) holds. This is a contradiction against our assumption.

Case 2:
Consider the case where there are four boundaries
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4}
with
1\leq t_{1}<t_{2}<t_{3}<t_{4}\leq|x|
and
|t_{4}-t_{1}|\leq c
, and there are elements

,
\gamma\in(\Gamma^{(-)})^{*}Z_{0}
, and
\alpha\in(\Gamma^{(-)})^{*}
such that (i)
(q,\gamma)
and
(q,\alpha\gamma)
are state-stack pairs at the boundaries
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
, respectively, (ii)
(p,\alpha\gamma)
and

are two state-stack pairs at the boundaries
t_{3}
and
t_{4}
, respectively, and (iii)
[t_{i},t_{i+1}]
for every index
i\in[3]
is pseudo-convex.
We then factorize
x
into
x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}x_{5}
so that
t_{i}
is the
(x_{i},x_{i+1})
-boundary for each index
i\in[4]
. Note that
|x_{2}x_{4}|\geq 2
because of
t_{1}<t_{2}
and
t_{3}<t_{4}
.
Since
M
is in an ideal shape,
|x_{2}|\geq 1
also follows. By an application of Lemma
4.2
(2), it then follows that

(x_{2},x_{4})
is an iterative pair of both
xy
and
xz
for

.
This also contradicts our assumption.

Case 3: For convenience, we set
R=(|x|-c,|x|)
. Consider the simple case where there is no pop operation in the
R
-region. There exist a number
m\geq 1
and a series of
m
boundaries
s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m}
in the
R
-region with
1\leq s_{1}<s_{2}<\cdots<s_{m}
such that, for appropriately chosen elements
q\in Q
,
\gamma\in(\Gamma^{(-)})^{*}Z_{0}
, and
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m-1}\in(\Gamma^{(-)})^{*}
, there are state-stack pairs of the form
(q,\gamma),(q,\alpha_{1}\gamma),\ldots,(q,\alpha_{[m-1,1]}\gamma)
at the boundaries
s_{1},s_{2}\ldots,s_{m}
, respectively, where
\alpha_{[i,1]}
is the abbreviation of
\alpha_{i}\alpha_{i-1}\cdots\alpha_{1}
introduced in Section
4.2
. Note that the boundary blocks
[s_{1},s_{2}],[s_{2},s_{3}],\ldots,[s_{m-1},s_{m}]
are all convex. We maximize the value of
m
and we hereafter assume that

denotes this maximum value. Notice that

|Q|^{3}

.
If, for each

, the corresponding value

is at most

, then the size

of the

-region is at most
|Q|^{3}
. This is a contradiction, and thus we conclude that

.

Next, we choose
\{t_{i}\}_{i\in[m]}
and
\{r_{i}\}_{i\in[m]}
so that
t_{i}
and
r_{i}
are respectively boundaries in the
y
-region and the
z
-region satisfying that
(a)
s_{m}\leq t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots<t_{m}
and
s_{m}\leq r_{1}<r_{2}<\cdots<r_{m}
and
(b)
[s_{i},s_{i+1}]
is mutually correlated to
[t_{m-i},t_{m-i+1}]
in the
y
-region and also to
[r_{m-i},r_{m-i+1}]
in the
z
-region for any index
i\in[m-1]
.
This is possible because
M
is
\varepsilon
-enhanced and in an ideal shape.
Notice that the boundary blocks
[t_{1},t_{2}],\ldots,[t_{m-1},t_{m}]
and
[r_{1},r_{2}],\ldots,[r_{m-1},r_{m}]
are all pseudo-convex.
Assume that, at boundaries
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{m}
, the associated state-stack pairs are respectively of the form
(p_{1},\alpha_{[m-1,1]}\gamma),(p_{2},\alpha_{[m-2,1]}\gamma),\ldots,(p_{m-1},\alpha_{1}\gamma),(p_{m},\gamma)
for certain inner states
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{m}\in Q
.
Similarly, assume that
(e_{1},\alpha_{[m-1,1]}\gamma),(e_{2},\alpha_{[m-2,1]}\gamma),\ldots,(e_{m-1},\alpha_{1}\gamma),(e_{m},\gamma)
are state-stack pairs at the boundaries
r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{m}
,
respectively.

By Lemma
4.3
(2), since
m>|Q|^{2}
, there is a subset
C
of
[m]

such that, for any index pair

.
We then factorize the strings

,
y
, and
\hat{y}
as
x=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}
,

, and

so that
s_{j_{1}}
is an
(x_{1},x_{2})
-boundary,
s_{j_{2}}
is the
(x_{2},x_{3})
-boundary,
t_{j_{1}}
is the
(y_{1},y_{2})
-boundary,
t_{j_{2}}
is the
(y_{2},y_{3})
-boundary,
r_{j_{1}}
is the
(z_{2},z_{3})
-boundary, and

is the
(z_{2},z_{3})
-boundary.
It then follows that

(x_{2},y_{2})
and
(x_{2},z_{2})
are respectively iterative pairs of
xy
and
xz
for

. Therefore, the condition (2) follows.

Case 4:
Assume that Case 3 fails. Hence, at least one pop operation must take place in the
R
-region. We will proceed our proof by taking the following steps (1)–(5). In what follows, we focus our attention only on the
R
-region.

(1) We first claim that any flat boundary block has size at most
|Q|
.
This is because if a flat boundary block has size more than
|Q|
, then Case 1 occurs, a contradiction against our assumption. Therefore, the claim is true. This implies that there are at least
c/(|Q|+1)
operations of “pop” and “push” in the
R
-region.

(2)
Choose two boundaries
s
and
s^{\prime}
such that
|x|-c\leq s<s^{\prime}\leq|x|
, the boundary block
[s,s^{\prime}]
is pseudo-convex, and
[s,s^{\prime}]
can be partitioned into a number of turns.
Such a turning partition is illustrated in Fig.
3
. Hereafter, we consider the specific cases (a)–(b).

(a)
We first consider the case where
[s,s^{\prime}]
is
a single turn. We focus on a hill, say,
[t_{1},t_{2}]
that is contained in the turn and claim that this hill has height at most
|Q|^{2}
. This is shown as follows. If the hill has height more than
|Q|^{2}
, then
there exists an inner state
q\in Q
that appears at least
|Q|+1
state-stack pairs in the left part of the hill. Let
(q,\gamma),(q,\alpha_{1}\gamma),\ldots,(q,\alpha_{[|Q|,1]}\gamma)
denote those state-stack pairs for appropriate strings
\gamma,\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{|Q|+1}
. Associated with them, we also choose state-stack pairs
(p_{1},\alpha_{[|Q|,1]}\gamma),\ldots,(p_{|Q|},\alpha_{1}\gamma),(p_{|Q|+1},\gamma)
in the right part of the hill for certain inner states
p_{1},\ldots,p_{|Q|+1}
.
Lemma
4.2
(1) then guarantees the existence of a distinct pair
i,j\in[|Q|+1]
satisfying
p_{i}=p_{j}
.
This leads to Case 2, a contradiction.

As a consequence, the total size of the boundary blocks, each of which expresses one slope (either upward or downward) of the hill (excluding all plateaus), is at most
2|Q|^{2}
. Notice that each plateau has size at most
|Q|
by (1).
No distinct pair of plateaus in the hill has the same height since, otherwise, those two plateaus contain two state-stack pairs with the same inner state, and thus Case 1 occurs, a contradiction. Thus, the hill includes no more than
|Q|^{2}
plateaus. From this fact, it follows that
|t_{2}-t_{1}|\leq 2|Q|^{2}+|Q|^{2}\times|Q|\leq 3|Q|^{3}
.
Note that the size
s^{\prime}-s|
of the turn is the size of the hill plus the size of a certain slope extending one end (either the right or the left) of the hill. Such a slope cannot have size more than
|Q|^{2}
because, otherwise, we can conclude Condition (1). The size of the turn is therefore at most
|t_{2}-t_{1}|+|Q|^{2}\leq 3|Q|^{3}+|Q|^{2}\leq 4|Q|^{3}
. Similarly, the height of the turn is at most
|Q|^{2}+|Q|^{2}\leq 2|Q|^{2}
.

Moreover, the inner states at the boundaries
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
must be different because, otherwise, the corresponding state-stack pairs coincide, and thus Case 2 follows, a contradiction.

(b)
Next, we consider the case where
[s,s^{\prime}]
consists of a series
{\cal S}
of turns
[s_{1},s_{2}],[s_{2},s_{3}],\ldots,[s_{m-1},s_{m}]
with
s=s_{1}
,
s^{\prime}=s_{m}
, and
s_{1}<s_{2}<\cdots<s_{m}
. For each index
i\in[m-1]
, we take a hill
[t_{i},t^{\prime}_{i}]
lying in
[s_{i},s_{i+1}]
.
The height of such a hill is at most
|Q|^{2}
and its boundary-block size is at most
3|Q|^{3}
by (2a).
Recall that, for each turn
[s_{i},s_{i+1}]
, its bottom height is the stack height at either end of the turn, i.e.,
s_{i}
or
s_{i+1}
.
For convenience, we define the
gain
of the turn, denoted by
gain(s_{i},s_{i+1})
, to be the stack height at the boundary
s_{i}
minus the stack height at the boundary
s_{i+1}
.
It is easy to see that, when the gain of a turn equals
0
, the turn is merely a hill.
Let
(p_{i},\gamma_{i})
denote the state-stack pair at the boundary
s_{i}
. Assume that the gains of all the turns are zero and that their bottom heights are all equal. In this special case, we obtain
m\leq|Q|
. This is shown as follows. Since the stack height does not go below the bottom height and
M
is in an ideal shape,
\gamma_{i}=\gamma_{j}
follows for any pair
i,j\in[m-1]
. By Lemma
4.3
(1), if
m\geq|Q|+1
, then there are distinct indices
i,j\in[m]
for which
p_{i}=p_{j}
, a contradiction against Case 1. Hence,
m\leq|Q|
follows and
|s^{\prime}-s|
is upper-bounded by
m\cdot 4|Q|^{3}
, which is at most
4|Q|^{4}
.

(3) We estimate the total number of the turns whose bottom height is a fixed number
h
. Given such a number
h
, we define
bh(h)
to be the set of all indices
i\in[m-1]
such that the bottom height of
[s_{i},s_{i+1}]
is exactly
h
. Note that
|bh(0)|\leq|Q|
follows by (2b). We first assume that
|bh(h+1)|>|\bigcup_{i\in[0,h]_{\mathbb{Z}}}bh(i)|\cdot|Q|
holds for a certain number
h\in\mathbb{N}
. This implies the existence of more than
|Q|
turns in
bh(h+1)
, any pair of which contain no turn in
\bigcup_{i\in[0,h]_{\mathbb{Z}}}bh(i)
. Consider
|Q|+1
state-stack pairs at the boundaries whose height matches the bottom heights of those specific turns. By Lemma
4.3
(1), at least two of those state-stack pairs coincide, a contradiction.
Hence, we conclude that, for any
h\in\mathbb{N}
,
|bh(h+1)|\leq|\bigcup_{i\in[0,h]_{\mathbb{Z}}}bh(i)|\cdot|Q|
. We then assert that
|bh(h)|\leq 2^{h-1}|Q|^{h+1}
for any
h\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. Since
|bh(i)|\leq 2^{i-1}|Q|^{i+1}
holds for any
i\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, by induction hypothesis, it follows that
|bh(h)|\leq|Q|\cdot|\bigcup_{i\in[0,h-1]_{\mathbb{Z}}}bh(i)|\leq|Q|(|Q|+\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}2^{i-1}|Q|^{i+1})\leq(1+\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}2^{i-1})|Q|^{h+1}=2^{h-1}|Q|^{h+1}
.

(4)
We define another notion of “true gain” in the
(s,s^{\prime})
-region and intend to estimate its value.
Let us consider a series of consecutive turns
[s_{1},s_{2}],[s_{2},s_{3}],\ldots,[s_{m-1},s_{m}]
in the boundary block
[s,s^{\prime}]
, where
m
is the number that maximizes the length of this series.

For each turn, we consider its gain. Given an index
k\in[m]
, the
true gain
in
[s_{1},s_{k}]
, denoted by
tg(s_{1},s_{k})
, is set to be the sum
\sum_{i\in[k-1]}gain(s_{i},s_{i+1})
. In particular, we set
tg(s,s^{\prime})=tg(s_{1},s_{m})
. We want to evaluate the value of this true gain and prove that
tg(s,s^{\prime})>|Q|^{3}
. Assuming that
tg(s,s^{\prime})\leq|Q|^{3}
, we wish to reach a contradiction. We need to examine two separate cases.

(a)
Firstly, we consider the case where
tg(s_{1},s_{k})\geq 2|Q|^{3}
holds for a certain index
k\in[m-1]
.
We intend to partition all the turns according to the “sign” of their gains. For any pair
k,l\in[m]
with
k<l
, the notation
P_{s_{k},s_{l}}
(resp.,
N_{s_{k},s_{l}}
and
Z_{s_{k},s_{l}}
) denotes the set of all indices
i\in[k,l-1]_{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
gain(s_{i},s_{i+1})>0
(resp.,
gain(s_{i},s_{i+1})<0
and
gain(s_{i},s_{i+1})=0
).

Since
tg(s_{1},s_{k})\geq 2|Q|^{3}
, we can take elements
q\in Q
,
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{|Q|},\gamma
satisfying that, at certain
|Q|+1
boundaries in
\bigcup_{i\in P_{s_{1},s_{k}}}[s_{i},s_{i+1}]
, the state-stack pairs has the form
(q,\gamma),(q,\alpha_{1}\gamma),\ldots,(q,\alpha_{[|Q|,1]}\gamma)
. Associated with these state-stack pairs, there are also state-stack pairs
(p_{1},\alpha_{[|Q|,1]}\gamma),(p_{2},\alpha_{[|Q|-1,1]}\gamma),\ldots,(p_{|Q|+1},\gamma)
at certain
|Q|+1
boundaries in
\bigcup_{i\in N_{s_{k},s_{m}}}[s_{i},s_{i+1}]
for appropriately chosen inner states
p_{1},\ldots,p_{|Q|+1}
since
tg(s,s^{\prime})\leq|Q|^{3}
.
By Lemma
4.3
(1), we can find an appropriate distinct pair
i,j\in[|Q|+1]
for which
p_{i}=p_{j}
. This implies Case 3, a contradiction.

(b) Consider the case where, for any index
k\in[|Q|+1]
,
tg(s_{1},s_{k})<2|Q|^{3}
holds.
The number
m
of all the turns in the
R
-region is at most
|\bigcup_{i\in[0,2|Q|^{3}]_{\mathbb{Z}}}bh(i)|
. Thus, we conclude that
m\leq\sum_{i=0}^{2|Q|^{3}}|bh(i)\leq|Q|+\sum_{i=1}^{2|Q|^{3}}2^{i-1}|Q|^{i+1}\leq|Q|+2^{2|Q|^{3}}|Q|^{2|Q|^{3}+1}\leq 2^{4|Q|^{4}}
.

By (2b), there may be series of at most
|Q|
turns having gain
0
and those turns have size at most
4|Q|^{4}
.
Since
|s^{\prime}-s|
is the sum of the sizes of all turns, it follows that
|s^{\prime}-s|\leq|Q|\times 4|Q|^{4}+m\cdot 4|Q|^{4}\leq 4|Q|^{4}2^{4|Q|^{4}}\leq 2^{5|Q|^{5}}
.
Since each turn has size at most
4|Q|^{4}
and
m
is the maximum, it follows that
|s-(|x|-c)|<4|Q|^{4}
and
||x|-s^{\prime}|<4|Q|^{4}
. From these inequalities, we obtain
|s^{\prime}-s|\geq c-8|Q|^{4}
.
We then conclude that
c\leq 2^{5|Q|^{5}}+8|Q|^{4}
. This contradicts the definition of
c
.
From the cases (a)–(b), we conclude that
tg(s,s^{\prime})>|Q|^{3}
.

(5) Since
tg(s,s^{\prime})\geq|Q|^{3}+1
, there exists a series
(q,\gamma),(q,\alpha_{1}\gamma),\ldots,(q,\alpha_{[|Q|^{2},1]}\gamma)
of
|Q|^{2}+1
state-stack pairs respectively at the boundaries
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{|Q|^{2}+1}
in the
R
-region for appropriately chosen
q,\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{|Q|^{2}+1}
. Consider the
y
-region of
x^{\prime}y
. Let
r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{|Q|^{2}+1}
denote boundaries in the
y
-region such that the state-stack pairs at those boundaries have the form
(p_{1},\alpha_{[|Q|^{2},1]}\gamma),(p_{2},\alpha_{[|Q|^{2}-1,1]}\gamma),\ldots,(p_{|Q|^{2}+1},\gamma)
, respectively. By Lemma
4.3
(2), there exists a subset
C\subseteq[|Q|^{2}+1]
of more than
|Q|
such that
p_{i}=p_{j}
for any pair
i,j\in C
. Next, we consider the
z
-region of
x^{\prime}z
. Let
r^{\prime}_{1},r^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,r^{\prime}_{|Q|+1}
denote boundaries in the
z
-region such that the state-stack pairs at those boundaries have the form
(p^{\prime}_{1},\alpha^{\prime}_{[|Q|,1]}),(p_{2},\alpha^{\prime}_{|Q|_{1},1]}\gamma),\ldots,(p_{|Q|+1},\gamma)
, respectively. Lemma
4.3
(2) further implies the
p^{\prime}_{i_{0}}=p^{\prime}_{j_{0}}
for an appropriate distinct pair
i_{0},j_{0}\in[|Q|+1]
.
This clearly implies the condition (2) of the lemma.

This completes the proof of the case of
d=1
.

General Case of

:

We begin our argument by considering

dcf languages
L_{1},L_{2},\ldots,L_{d}
satisfying

.
Take
d+1
strings
w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{d+1}
of the form

in

with
|x|>c
for appropriate strings

and

.
Since there are only

and two distinct indices

for which

.
In what follows, we fix such a triplet
(j_{1},j_{2},e)
and set
w=w_{j_{1}}
,
w^{\prime}=w_{j_{2}}
, and

for simplicity.
Let us take any two factorizations

and

4.5
Proof of the Second Pumping Lemma for DCFL[
d
]

We have already proven in Section
4.4
the first pumping lemma for
\mathrm{DCFL}[d]
by conducting a detailed analysis on a stack history of a given 1dpda. In this section, we intend to prove the second pumping lemma for
\mathrm{DCFL}[d]
(Lemma
1.2
) by taking a slightly different strategy. Instead of applying Lemmas
4.2
–
4.3
, we here use Proposition
4.4
.

Take any integer
d\geq 1
and fix an arbitrary infinite language
L
in
\mathrm{DCFL}[d]
. We split the intended proof into two parts, depending on the value of
d
.

Basis Case of
d=1
:

We first consider the basis case of
d=1
. Fix a 1dpda
M=(Q,\Sigma,\{|\!\!\mathrm{c},\$\},\Gamma,\delta,q_{0},Q_{acc},Q_{rej})
in an ideal shape recognizing
L
. Consider two arbitrary strings
w=x^{\prime}y
and
w^{\prime}=x^{\prime}z
in
L
with
|x^{\prime}|,|y|,|z|\geq 1
.

Let us assume that the conditions (1)–(4) of the lemma all fail. It then suffices to verify the validity of the condition (5).
Since the condition (1) in particular fails, both
w
and
w^{\prime}
must have nondegenerate iterative pairs for
L
. We consider such nondegenerate iterative pairs of
x^{\prime}y
and
x^{\prime}z
for
L
. Note that, by our assumption, any of them must satisfy none of the conditions (2)–(4). The remaining case is that exactly one of the two nondegenerate iterative pairs of
x^{\prime}y
and
x^{\prime}z
is in the
x^{\prime}
-region.
Fix a string
u\in\{y,z\}
such that the
x^{\prime}
-region includes a nondegenerate iterative pair of
x^{\prime}u
. For the other string
x^{\prime}u^{(op)}
, where
u^{(op)}
is a unique string in
\{y,z\}-\{u\}
, a half pair of the nondegenerate iterative pair of
x^{\prime}u^{(op)}
must be in the
x^{\prime}
-region and the other half lies (at least partly) in the
z
-region.

We apply Proposition
4.4
to
w
and
w^{\prime}
and obtain stack-operational pairs of certain two strings deduced from
w
and
w^{\prime}
. For readability, we write those deduced strings as
w
and
w^{\prime}
as well and focus on their stack-operational pairs. In what follows, we assume that
x^{\prime}
and
u
are factorized into
x^{\prime}=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}x_{5}x_{6}x_{7}
and
u=u_{1}u_{2}u_{3}
, respectively. For readability, we use the notation
(\alpha_{1}|\alpha_{2}|\alpha_{3}|\alpha_{4}|\alpha_{5})
to denote a factorization
\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}\alpha_{4}\alpha_{5}
of a given string and we say that a quintuple
(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4})
of boundaries is
induced
by
(\alpha_{1}|\alpha_{2}|\alpha_{3}|\alpha_{4}|\alpha_{5})
if
t_{i}
is the
(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i+1})
-boundary for every index
i\in[4]
. We then examine the following cases (i)–(iv).

Case (i): Consider the case where
(x_{2}x_{3},x_{7}u_{1})
and
(x_{3}x_{4},x_{6}x_{7})
are respectively nondegenerate stack-operational pairs of
x^{\prime}u
and
x^{\prime}u^{(op)}
w.r.t.
M
. This case makes the condition (4) true, a contradiction.

Case (ii): Consider the case where
(x_{3}x_{4},u_{2})
and
(x_{2}x_{3},x_{6})
are respectively nondegenerate stack-operational pairs of
x^{\prime}u
and
x^{\prime}u^{(op)}
w.r.t.
M
.
By stack-operationalism,
M
can repeatedly push a series of the stack symbols corresponding to
x_{2}x_{3}
and then repeatedly pop the same series of symbols while reading a multiple copies of
x_{6}
. To do so, however,
M
need to pop all the symbols corresponding to
x_{3}x_{4}
. Hereafter, we state this argument more formally.

In relation to
x^{\prime}u^{(op)}
, we take a quintuple
(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4})
of boundaries induced by
(x_{1}|x_{2}x_{3}|x_{4}x_{5}|x_{6}|x_{7}u^{(op)})
.
Since
([t_{1},t_{2}],[t_{3},t_{4}])
is good, we can choose appropriate elements
q,p,\alpha,\gamma
such that
(q,\gamma)
and
(q,\alpha\gamma)
are respectively state-stack pairs at the boundaries
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
and
(p,\alpha\gamma)
and
(p,\gamma)
are respectively state-stack pairs at the boundaries
t_{3}
and
t_{4}
.
In a similar way, we take
(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},s_{4})
induced by
(x_{1}x_{2}|x_{3}x_{4}|x_{5}x_{6}x_{7}u_{1}|u_{2}|u_{3})
and take state-stack pairs
(q^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime})
and
(q^{\prime},\beta\gamma^{\prime})
at the boundaries
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
, respectively, and
(p^{\prime},\beta\gamma^{\prime})
and
(p^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime})
at the boundaries
s_{3}
and
s_{4}
, respectively.
If
|\gamma^{\prime}|\geq|\gamma|
, then
[s_{2},s_{3}]
cannot be pseudo-convex, and thus
[s_{1},s_{2}]
and
[s_{3},s_{4}]
are not mutually correlated.
This contradicts our assumption that
(x_{2}x_{3},x_{6})
is a stack-operational pair of
x^{\prime}u^{(op)}
. Thus,
|\gamma^{\prime}|<|\gamma|
follows. However, this implies that
[t_{2},t_{3}]
cannot be pseudo-convex. Hence,
[t_{1},t_{2}]
and
[t_{3},t_{4}]
are not mutually correlated. This is also absurd.
Therefore, this case (ii) never happens.

Similarly, even if we replace
(x_{2}x_{3},x_{6})
and
(x_{3}x_{4},u_{2})
respectively by
(x_{2},x_{6})
and
(x_{4},u_{2})
, we can obtain another contradiction.

Case (iii): Consider the case where
(x_{2},u_{2})
and
(x_{4},x_{6})
are respectively nondegenerate stack-operational pairs of
x^{\prime}u
and
x^{\prime}u^{(op)}
w.r.t.
M
. In this case,
we choose two quintuples
(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4})
and
(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},s_{4})
induced respectively by
(x_{1}|x_{2}|x_{3}x_{4}x_{5}x_{6}x_{7}u_{1}|u_{2}|u_{3})
and
(x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}|x_{4}|x_{5}|x_{6}|x_{7}u^{(op)})
. Since
[t_{2},t_{3}]
and
[s_{2},s_{3}]
are both pseudo-convex,
[s_{1},s_{2}]
and
[s_{3},s_{4}]
must appear inside
[t_{2},t_{3}]
. We can process
x_{2}
and
u_{2}
for the same number of times without affecting any other computation of
M
. Similarly, we can do the same for
x_{4}
and
x_{6}
. Thus, the case (a) is true.

Case (iv): Consider the case where
(x_{5}x_{6},u_{2})
and
(x_{2},x_{4}x_{5})
with
|x_{5}|\geq 1
are respectively nondegenerate stack-operational pairs of
x^{\prime}u
and
x^{\prime}u^{(op)}
w.r.t.
M
. As in the previous cases, let
(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4})
and
(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},s_{4})
denote two quintuples induced respectively by
(x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}|x_{5}x_{6}|x_{7}u_{1}|u_{2}|u_{3})
and
(x_{1}|x_{2}|x_{3}|x_{4}x_{5}|x_{6}x_{7}u^{(op)})
.
Note that there are a hill top in
[t_{2},t_{3}]
and another in
[s_{2},s_{3}]
.
It is thus possible for
M
to repeatedly push the stack symbols associated with
x_{2}
and then pop the same symbols while reading a multiple copies of
x_{4}x_{5}
. In a similar way,
M
can repeatedly process the same number of copies of
x_{5}x_{6}
and
u_{2}
.
These repetitive procedures do not influence the other computation of
M
. As a consequence, the case (b) is true.

Case (v): Consider the last case where
(x_{6},u_{2})
and
(x_{2},x_{4})
are respectively nondegenerate stack-operational pairs of
x^{\prime}u
and
x^{\prime}u^{(op)}
w.r.t.
M
. An argument used for Case (iv) also leads to the case (b).

General Case of
d\geq 2
:

We wish to prove the lemma for a general case of
d\geq 2
. Take
d+1
strings
w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{d+1}
in
L
. Assume that
L=\bigcup_{e\in[d]}L_{e}
for
d
dcf languages
L_{1},L_{2},\ldots,L_{d}
. Since the
d+1
strings fall into the union of these
d
languages, by the pigeonhole principle, there must be an index
e\in[d]
and a distinct pair
j_{1},j_{2}\in[d+1]
for which
w_{j_{1}}
and
w_{j_{2}}
both belong to
L_{e}
.
Consider a 1dpda
M
in an ideal shape that recognizes
L_{e}
. We then apply the basis case
d=1
to
(w_{j_{1}},w_{j_{2}},M)
. This completes the proof of the general case.

5
A Brief Discussion and Future Challenges

We have presented two new and practical lemmas, called the
pumping lemmas for
\mathrm{DCFL}[d]
. The first one (Lemma
1.1
) is in part viewed as a natural extension of Yu’s pumping lemma for
\mathrm{DCFL}

[
24
]
. This lemma has a quite simple form, describing a structural property of iterative pairs. In Section
3
, we have applied it to separate the intersection and the union hierarchies of deterministic context-free (dcf) languages. Our proof of this pumping lemma is solely founded on an analysis of the behaviors of one-way deterministic pushdown automata (or 1dpda’s) and it therefore provides an alternative proof to Yu’s pumping lemma whose proof actually utilizes
\mathrm{LR}(k)
grammars. In contrast, the second pumping lemma for
\mathrm{DCFL}[d]
(Lemma
1.2
) relates to a structural property of nondegenerate iterative pairs. The proof of this pumping lemma relies on the notion and the properties of stack-operational pairs. As a future task, it is desirable to study various aspects of iterative pairs particularly for dcf languages.

Although our pumping lemmas are quite useful, it is not as powerful as to precisely
characterize
all
d
-union dcf languages.
For instance, to prove that
NPal^{\#}
is not in
\mathrm{DCFL}[\omega]
in the proof of Theorem
1.5
(2), we have employed an argument, which relies on none of the pumping lemmas for
\mathrm{DCFL}[d]
. One of the most challenging tasks is to expand our pumping lemmas to characterize such languages. See, e.g.,
[
18
]
for the case of context-free languages.

Since finite intersections and unions have been intensively discussed so far, we here wish to discuss their simple extension to “infinite” intersections in a certain controlled way. To a given 1dpda
M=(Q,\Sigma,\{|\!\!\mathrm{c},\$\},\Gamma,\delta,q_{0},Z_{0},Q_{acc},Q_{rej})
, we first assign its
description size
, which equals the value
|Q||\Sigma||\Gamma^{\leq e}|
. We express this value as
des(M)
.
Let us consider an infinite sequence
\{M_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of 1dpda’s. Such a sequence is said to have
polynomial description size
if there exists a polynomial (with nonnegative coefficients)
p
such that
des(M_{n})\leq p(n)
holds for any number
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Given a function
\mu:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
, we concentrate on
\mu
-bounded intersection
of the dcf languages
\{L(M_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, which is defined as
\{x\mid\forall i\leq\mu(|x|)[x\in L(M_{i})]\}
. Recall from Section
1.1
the language
Pal
of even-length palindromes, which is not in
\mathrm{DCFL}[\omega]
by Theorem
1.5
(1). In spite of this fact, we can characterize
Pal
in terms of a certain
\mu
-bounded intersection of dcf languages as follows.

Lemma 5.1

There exists an infinite sequence
\{M_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of 1dpda’s having polynomial description size such that
Pal
coincides with the
\mu
-bounded intersection of
\{L(M_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
\mu(n)=\lceil n/2\rceil
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

We wish to construct a family
{\cal M}=\{M_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of 1dpda’s.
Let
M_{0}
denote a 1dpda that recognizes
\{x\mid|x|\text{ is even }\}
without using its stack. For any other
n\geq 1
, we define
M_{n}
in the following way. Given an input
x
, if
|x|\leq n
, then we reject
x
. Assume otherwise. We push all symbols of
x
one by one into a stack and remember the
n
th symbol of
x
, say,
a_{n}
in the form of inner states. This is possible because
n
is treated as a “constant” for
M_{n}
, not depending on the length of the input
x
. After reaching the endmarker
\$
, we start to pop the last
n
symbols of
x
from the stack. We then check if the
n
th symbol matches
a_{n}
. If so, then we accept
x
; otherwise, we reject
x
. Consider the
\mu
-bounded intersection of
{\cal M}
, namely,
\{x\mid\forall i\leq\mu(|x|)[x\in L(M_{i})]\}
. It is not difficult to show that the this intersection coincides with
Pal
.
∎

It is interesting to determine the precise computational complexity of
\mu
-bounded intersections of dcf languages for various choices of
\mu
.
