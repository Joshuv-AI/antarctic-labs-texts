Abstract
. In this paper, we investigate the ideal structure of uniform Roe algebras for general metric spaces beyond the scope of Yu’s property A. Inspired by the ideal of ghost operators coming from expander graphs and in contrast to the notion of geometric ideal, we introduce a notion of ghostly ideal in a uniform Roe algebra, whose elements are locally invisible in certain directions at infinity. We show that the geometric ideal and the ghostly ideal are respectively the smallest and the largest element in the lattice of ideals with a common invariant open subset of the unit space of the coarse groupoid by Skandalis-Tu-Yu, and hence the study of ideal structure can be reduced to classifying ideals between the geometric and the ghostly ones. As an application, we provide a concrete description for the maximal ideals in a uniform Roe algebra in terms of the minimal points in the Stone-Čech boundary of the space. We also provide a criterion to ensure that the geometric and the ghostly ideals have the same K K -theory, which helps to recover counterexamples to the Baum-Connes type conjectures. Moreover, we introduce a notion of partial Property A for a metric space to characterise the situation in which the geometric ideal coincides with the ghostly ideal.

1.
Introduction

Roe algebras are
C^{*}
-algebras associated to metric spaces, which encode the coarse geometry of the underlying spaces. They were introduced by Roe in his pioneering work on higher index theory
[
37
]
, where he discovered that the
K
-theory of Roe algebras serves as a receptacle for higher indices of elliptic differential operators on open manifolds.
Hence the computation for the
K
-theory of Roe algebras becomes crucial in the study of higher index theory, and a pragmatic and practical approach is to consult the Baum-Connes type conjectures
[
3
,
4
,
25
]
. There is also a uniform version of the Roe algebra, which equally plays a key role in higher index theory (see
[
46
,
48
]
). Over the last four decades, there have been a number of excellent works around this topic (
e.g.
,
[
19
,
24
,
28
,
53
,
54
]
), which lead to significant progresses in topology, geometry and analysis (see,
e.g.
,
[
38
,
39
]
).

On the other hand, the analytic structure of (uniform) Roe algebras reflects the coarse geometry of the underlying spaces, and the rigidity problem asks whether the coarse geometry of a metric space can be fully determined by the associated (uniform) Roe algebra. This problem was initially studied by Špakula and Willett in
[
49
]
, followed by a series of works in the last decade
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
30
]
. Recently this problem is completely solved in the uniform case by the profound work
[
2
]
, which again highlights the importance of uniform Roe algebras in coarse geometry. Meanwhile, uniform Roe algebras have also attained rapidly-growing interest from researchers in mathematical physics, especially in the theory of topological materials and topological insulators (see,
e.g.
,
[
20
]
and the references therein).

Due to their importance,
Chen and the first-named author initiated the study of the ideal structure for (uniform) Roe algebras
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
51
]
. They succeeded in obtaining a full description for the ideal structure of the uniform Roe algebra when the underlying space has Yu’s Property A (see
[
14
,
16
]
). However, the general picture is far from clear beyond the scope of Property A.

Note that when the underlying space comes from a discrete group equipped with a word length metric, then the associated uniform Roe algebra has the form of a crossed product by the group itself acting on an abelian
C^{*}
-algebra coming from its Stone-Čech compactification (
[
26
,
33
]
). This class of
C^{*}
-algebras plays an important role in dynamic systems, and their ideal structures have also been extensively studied (see,
e.g.
,
[
29
,
35
,
44
]
). More generally as shown below, uniform Roe algebras for general metric spaces can be realised as reduced groupoid
C^{*}
-algebras. Recently, rapidly-growing interest has arisen in the study of ideal structures for general reduced groupoid
C^{*}
-algebras (see,
e.g.
,
[
5
,
10
,
35
]
), partially due to their importance in the study of purely infiniteness for
C^{*}
-algebras.

In the present paper, we aim to provide a systematic study on the ideal structure of uniform Roe algebras for general discrete metric spaces. To outline our main results, let us first explain some notions.

Let
(X,d)
be a discrete metric space of bounded geometry (see Section
2.2
for precise definitions). Thinking of operators on
\ell^{2}(X)
as
X
-by-
X
matrices, we say that such an operator has
finite propagation
if the non-zero entries appear only in an entourage of finite width (measured by the metric on
X
) around the main diagonal (see Section
2.3
for full details). The set of all finite propagation operators forms a
\ast
-subalgebra of
\mathfrak{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
, and its norm closure is called the
uniform Roe algebra of
X
and denoted by
C^{*}_{u}(X)
.

There is another viewpoint on the uniform Roe algebra based on groupoids. Recall from
[
46
]
that Skandalis, Tu and Yu introduced a notion of coarse groupoid
G(X)
associated to a discrete metric space
X
, and they succeeded in relating coarse geometry to the theory of groupoids. The coarse groupoid
G(X)
is a locally compact, Hausdorff, étale and principal groupoid (see Section
2.5
for precise definitions), and the unit space of
G(X)
coincides with the Stone-Čech compactification
\beta X
of
X
. Moreover, the uniform Roe algebra
C^{*}_{u}(X)
can be interpreted as the reduced groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra of
G(X)
(see also
[
40
, Chapter 10]
).

In
[
14
]
, Chen and the first-named author concentrated on a class of ideals in the uniform Roe algebra in which finite propagation operators therein are dense, and they showed that these ideals can be described geometrically using the coarse groupoid. More precisely, recall that a subset
U\subseteq\beta X
is
invariant
if any element
\gamma
in
G(X)
with source in
U
also has its range in
U
(see Section
2.4
). As shown in
[
14
]
(see also Section
4
), for any ideal
I
in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
one can associate an invariant open subset
U(I)
of
\beta X
, and conversely for any invariant open subset
U\subseteq\beta X
one can associate an ideal
I(U)
in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
. Furthermore, these two procedures provide a one-to-one correspondence between invariant open subsets of
\beta X
and ideals in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
in which finite propagation operators therein are dense.

Based on
[
14
]
, the first-named author introduced the following notion in
[
51
, Definition 1.4]
:

Definition A

(Definition
4.4
)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a discrete metric space of bounded geometry. An ideal
I
in the uniform Roe algebra
C^{*}_{u}(X)
is called
geometric
if the set of all finite propagation operators in
I
is dense in
I
.

As explained above,
[
14
, Theorem 6.3]
(see also Proposition
4.9
) indicates that the geometric ideals in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
can be fully determined by invariant open subsets of
\beta X
, which explains the terminology. Consequently, the geometric ideals in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
are easy to handle and they must have the form of
I(U)
for some invariant open subset
U\subseteq\beta X
, called the
geometric ideal associated to
U
(see Definition
4.6
). Moreover, it follows from
[
16
, Theorem 4.4]
that all ideals in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
are geometric when
X
has Yu’s Property A.

However, things get complicated beyond the context of Property A. As noticed in
[
14
, Remark 6.5]
, when
X
comes from a sequence of expander graphs then the ideal
I_{G}
consisting of all ghost operators are
not
geometric (see also
[
24
]
). Recall that an operator
T\in\mathfrak{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
is a
ghost
if
T\in C_{0}(X\times X)
when regarding
T
as a function on
X\times X
. Ghost operators are introduced by Yu, and they are crucial to provide counterexamples to the coarse Baum-Connes conjecture (
[
24
]
).

Direct calculations show that the associated invariant open subsets for
I_{G}
and for the ideal of compact operators in
\mathfrak{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
are the same, both of which equal
X
(see also Example
4.8
and
5.5
). Hence for a general metric space
X
and an invariant open subset
U\subseteq\beta X
, there might be more than one ideal
I
in the uniform Roe algebra
C^{*}_{u}(X)
satisfying
U(I)=U
. Therefore, the study of the ideal structure for
C^{*}_{u}(X)
can be reduced to analyse the lattice (where the order is given by inclusion)

(1.1)

\mathfrak{I}_{U}:=\{I\mbox{~is~an~ideal~in~}C^{*}_{u}(X):U(I)=U\}

for each invariant open subset
U\subseteq\beta X
.

One of the main contributions of the present paper is to find the smallest and the largest elements in the lattice
\mathfrak{I}_{U}
. Following the discussions in
[
14
]
, it is easy to see that
I(U(I))\subseteq I
for any ideal
I
in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
, which implies that the geometric ideal
I(U)
is the smallest element in
\mathfrak{I}_{U}
(see Proposition
4.10
). To explore the largest element, we have to include every ideal
I
in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
with
U(I)=U
. Inspired by the definition of
U(I)
(see Equality (
4.1
)), we introduce the following key notion:

Definition B

(Definition
5.1
)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a discrete metric space of bounded geometry and
U
be an invariant open subset of
\beta X
. The
ghostly ideal associated to
U
is defined to be

\tilde{I}(U):=\{T\in C^{*}_{u}(X):\overline{\mathrm{r}(\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(T))}\subseteq U\mbox{~for~any~}\varepsilon>0\},

where
\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(T):=\{(x,y)\in X\times X:|T(x,y)|\geq\varepsilon\}
and
\mathrm{r}:X\times X\to X
is the projection onto the first coordinate.

We show that
\tilde{I}(U)
is indeed an ideal in the uniform Roe algebra
C^{*}_{u}(X)
(see Lemma
5.2
) and moreover, we obtain the following desired result:

Theorem C

(Theorem
5.4
)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a discrete metric space of bounded geometry and
U
be an invariant open subset of
\beta X
. Then any ideal
I
in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
with
U(I)=U
sits between
I(U)
and
\tilde{I}(U)
. More precisely, the geometric ideal
I(U)
is the smallest element while the ghostly ideal
\tilde{I}(U)
is the largest element in the lattice
\mathfrak{I}_{U}
in (
1.1
).

Theorem
C
draws the border of the lattice
\mathfrak{I}_{U}
in (
1.1
), as an important step to study the ideal structure of uniform Roe algebras for general metric spaces. More precisely, once we can bust every ideal between
I(U)
and
\tilde{I}(U)
for each invariant open subset
U\subseteq\beta X
, then we will obtain a full description for the ideal structure of the uniform Roe algebra
C^{*}_{u}(X)
. We pose it as an open question in Section
9
and hope this will be done in some future work.

Concerning the ghostly ideal
\tilde{I}(U)
, we also provide an alternative picture in terms of limit operators developed in
[
50
]
, showing that
\tilde{I}(U)
consists of operators which vanish in the
(\beta X\setminus U)
-direction (see Proposition
5.6
). Note that ghost operators vanish in all directions (see Corollary
5.7
), and hence operators in
\tilde{I}(U)
can be regarded as “partial” ghosts, which clarifies its terminology. Thanks to this viewpoint, we discover the deep reason behind the counterexample to the conjecture in
[
14
]
, constructed by the first-named author in
[
51
, Section 3]
(see Example
5.10
).

As an application, we manage to describe maximal ideals in the uniform Roe algebra. More precisely, it follows directly from Theorem
C
that maximal ideals correspond to minimal invariant closed subsets of the Stone-Čech boundary
\partial_{\beta}X:=\beta X\setminus X
. Moreover using the theory of limit spaces

For the integer group

Returning to the lattice
\mathfrak{I}_{U}
defined in (
1.1
), we already notice that generally
\mathfrak{I}_{U}
consists of more than one element. Hence it will be interesting and important to explore when
\mathfrak{I}_{U}
has only a single element, or equivalently (thanks to Theorem

), when the geometric ideal
I(U)
coincides with the ghostly ideal
\tilde{I}(U)
.

To study this problem, we start with an extra picture for geometric and ghostly ideals using the associated groupoid
C^{*}
-algebras (see Lemma

and Proposition

Proposition F

(Proposition

)
.

Let
X
be a discrete metric space of bounded geometry which can be coarsely embedded into some Hilbert space. Then for any invariant open subset

, we have an isomorphism

(\iota_{U})_{\ast}:K_{\ast}(I(U))\longrightarrow K_{\ast}(\tilde{I}(U))

for
\ast=0,1
, where

is the inclusion map.

Applying Proposition

to the case of

, we partially recover

[

for the coarse version and

[

). Hence presumably Proposition

will find further applications in higher index theory.

Conversely, it is natural to ask whether
I(U)=\tilde{I}(U)
implies that the restriction groupoid

is amenable. Note that when

,

implies that

is equivalent to that

has Property A (see also Example

), which is further equivalent to that the coarse groupoid

is amenable thanks to

. Inspired by these works, we introduce the following partial version of Property A:

Definition G

(Definition
8.1
)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a discrete metric space of bounded geometry and

be an invariant open subset. We say that
X
has
partial Property A towards
\partial_{\beta}X\setminus U
if
G(X)_{\partial_{\beta}X\setminus U}
is amenable.

Finally we reach the following, which recovers
[
42
, Theorem 1.3]
when
U=X
:

Theorem H

(Theorem
8.21
)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a strongly discrete metric space of bounded geometry and
U\subseteq\beta X
be a countably generated invariant open subset.
Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

X
has partial Property A towards
\beta X\setminus U
;

(2)

\tilde{I}(U)=I(U)
;

(3)

the ideal
I_{G}
of all ghost operators is contained in
I(U)
.

Note that there is a technical condition of countable generatedness (see Definition
8.16
) used in Theorem
H
, which holds for a number of examples (see Example
8.17
) including
X
itself. However as shown in Example
8.20
, there does exist an invariant open subset which is
not
countably generated.

The proof of Theorem
H
follows the outline of the case that
U=X
(cf.
[

, Theorem 1.3]
), and is divided into several steps. Firstly, we unpack the groupoid language of Definition

We also remark that in the proof of Theorem

2.2.
Notions from coarse geometry

Here we collect necessary notions from coarse geometry, and guide readers to
[

,

]
for more details.

For a discrete metric space
(X,d)
, denote the closed ball by
B_{X}(x,r):=\{y\in X:d(x,y)\leq r\}
for

and

. For a subset
A\subseteq X
and

, denote the
r
-neighbourhood of

in

by
\mathcal{N}_{r}(A):=\{x\in X:d_{X}(x,A)\leq r\}
. For

We say that
(X,d)

if for any

is finite. Also say that

is

.

Convention.
We say that “

Definition 2.1

(
[

]
)

A space

(1)

;

(2)

(3)

Using a standard normalisation argument, we have the following:

Lemma 2.2

A space

if and only if

\mathrm{supp}(f)\subseteq E_{S}

):

Lemma 2.3
.

A space
(X,d)
has Property A if and only if for any
R>0
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exist
S>0
and a kernel
k:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
of positive type satisfying the following:

(1)

for
x,y\in X
, we have
k(x,y)=k(y,x)
and
k(x,x)=1
;

(2)

for
x,y\in X
with
d(x,y)\geq S
, we have
k(x,y)=0
;

(3)

for
x,y\in X
with
d(x,y)\leq R
, we have
|1-k(x,y)|\leq\varepsilon
.

We also recall the notion of coarse embedding:

Definition 2.4
.

Let
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
be metric spaces and
f:X\to Y
be a map. We say that
f
is a
coarse embedding
if there exist functions
\rho_{\pm}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
with
\lim_{t\to+\infty}\rho_{\pm}(t)=+\infty
such that for any
x,y\in X
we have

\rho_{-}(d_{X}(x,y))\leq d_{Y}(f(x),f(y))\leq\rho_{+}(d_{X}(x,y)).

If additionally there exists
C>0
such that
Y=\mathcal{N}_{C}(f(X))
, then we say that
f
is a
coarse equivalence
and
(X,d_{X}),(Y,d_{Y})
are
coarsely equivalent
.

2.3.
Uniform Roe algebras

Let
(X,d)
be a discrete metric space. Each operator
T\in\mathfrak{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
can be written in the matrix form
T=(T(x,y))_{x,y\in X}
, where
T(x,y)=\langle T\delta_{y},\delta_{x}\rangle\in\mathbb{C}
. We also regard
T\in\mathfrak{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
as a bounded function on
X\times X
,
i.e.
, an element in
\ell^{\infty}(X\times X)
.
Denote by
\|T\|
the operator norm of
T
in
\mathfrak{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
, and
\|T\|_{\infty}
the supremum norm when regarding
T
as a function in
\ell^{\infty}(X\times X)
. It is clear that
\|T\|_{\infty}\leq\|T\|
for any
T\in\mathfrak{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
.

Given an operator
T\in\mathfrak{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
, we define the
support
of
T
to be

\mathrm{supp}(T):=\{(x,y)\in X\times X:T(x,y)\neq 0\},

and the
propagation
of
T
to be

\mathrm{prop}(T):=\sup\{d(x,y):(x,y)\in\mathrm{supp}(T)\}.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
(X,d)
be a space.

(1)

The set of all finite propagation operators in
\mathfrak{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
forms a
\ast
-algebra, called the
algebraic uniform Roe algebra of
X
and denoted by
\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]
. For each
R\geq 0
, denote the subset

\mathbb{C}_{u}^{R}[X]:=\{T\in\mathfrak{B}(\ell^{2}(X)):\mathrm{prop}(T)\leq R\}.

It is clear that
\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]=\bigcup_{R\geq 0}\mathbb{C}_{u}^{R}[X]
.

(2)

The
uniform Roe algebra of
X
is defined to be the operator norm closure of
\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]
in
\mathfrak{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
, which forms a
C^{*}
-algebra and is denoted by
C^{*}_{u}(X)
.

The following notion was originally introduced by Yu:

Definition 2.6
.

An operator
T\in C^{*}_{u}(X)
is called a
ghost
if
T\in C_{0}(X\times X)
when regarding
T
as a function in
\ell^{\infty}(X\times X)
. In other words, for any
\varepsilon
there exists a finite subset
F\subseteq X
such that for any
(x,y)\notin F\times F
, we have
|T(x,y)|<\varepsilon
.

It is easy to see that all the ghost operators in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
form an ideal in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
, denoted by
I_{G}
. Intuitively speaking, a ghost operator is locally invisible at infinity in all directions. This will be made more precise in the sequel.

2.4.
Groupoids and
C^{*}
-algebras

We collect here some basic notions and terminology on groupoids. Details can be found in
[
34
]
, or
[
45
]
in the étale case.

Recall that a
groupoid
is a small category, in which every morphism is invertible. More precisely, a groupoid consists of a set
\mathcal{G}
, a subset
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
called the
unit space
, two maps
\mathrm{s},\mathrm{r}:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
called the
source
and
range
maps respectively, a
composition law
:

\mathcal{G}^{(2)}:=\{(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\in\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}:\mathrm{s}(\gamma_{1})=\mathrm{r}(\gamma_{2})\}\ni(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\mapsto\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}\in\mathcal{G},

and an
inverse
map
\gamma\mapsto\gamma^{-1}
. These operations satisfy a couple of axioms, including associativity law and the fact that elements in
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
act as units.

For
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, denote
\mathcal{G}^{x}:=\mathrm{r}^{-1}(x)
and
\mathcal{G}_{x}:=\mathrm{s}^{-1}(x)
.
For
Y\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, denote
\mathcal{G}_{Y}^{Y}:=\mathrm{r}^{-1}(Y)\cap\mathrm{s}^{-1}(Y)
.
Note that
\mathcal{G}_{Y}^{Y}
is a subgroupoid of
\mathcal{G}
(in the sense that it is stable under multiplication and inverse), called the
reduction of
\mathcal{G}
by
Y
.
A subset
Y
is said to be
invariant
if
\mathrm{r}^{-1}(Y)=\mathrm{s}^{-1}(Y)
, and we write
\mathcal{G}_{Y}
instead of
\mathcal{G}_{Y}^{Y}
in this case.

A
locally compact Hausdorff
groupoid is a groupoid equipped with a locally compact and Hausdorff topology such that the structure maps (composition and inverse) are continuous with respect to the induced topologies.
Such a groupoid is called
étale
(also called
r
-discrete
) if the range (hence the source) map is a local homeomorphism. Clearly in this case, each fibre
\mathcal{G}^{x}
(and
\mathcal{G}_{x}
) is discrete with the induced topology, and
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is clopen in
\mathcal{G}
. The notion of étaleness for a groupoid can be regarded as an analogue of discreteness in the group case.

Example 2.7
.

Let
X
be a set. The
pair groupoid
of
X
is
X\times X
as a set, whose unit space is
\{(x,x)\in X\times X:x\in X\}
and identified with
X
for simplicity. The source map is the projection onto the second coordinate and the range map is the projection onto the first coordinate. The composition is given by
(x,y)\cdot(y,z)=(x,z)
for
x,y,z\in X
. When
X
is a discrete Hausdorff space, then
X\times X
is a locally compact Hausdorff étale groupoid.

Now we introduce the algebras associated to groupoids. Here we only focus on the case of étaleness, and guide readers to
[
34
]
for the general case.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact, Hausdorff and étale groupoid with unit space
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. Note that the space
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
is a
\ast
-involutive algebra with respect to the following operations: for
f,g\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
,

\displaystyle(f\ast g)(\gamma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{s}(\gamma)}}f(\gamma\alpha^{-1})g(\alpha),

\displaystyle f^{*}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{f(\gamma^{-1})}.

Consider the following algebraic norm on
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
defined by:

\|f\|_{I}:=\mathrm{max}\left\{\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}_{x}}|f(\gamma)|,~\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}_{x}}|f^{*}(\gamma)|\right\}.

The completion of
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{I}
is denoted by
L^{1}(\mathcal{G})
.

The
maximal (full) groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra

C^{*}_{\mathrm{max}}(\mathcal{G})
is defined to be the completion of
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
with respect to the norm:

\|f\|_{\mathrm{max}}:=\sup\|\pi(f)\|,

where the supremum is taken over all
\ast
-representations
\pi
of
L^{1}(\mathcal{G})
.

In order to define the reduced counterpart, we recall that for each
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
the
regular representation at
x
, denoted by
\lambda_{x}:C_{c}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathfrak{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x}))
, is defined as follows:

(2.1)

\big(\lambda_{x}(f)\xi\big)(\gamma):=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}_{x}}f(\gamma\alpha^{-1})\xi(\alpha),\quad\mbox{where}~f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})\mbox{~and~}\xi\in\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x}).

It is routine work to check that
\lambda_{x}
is a well-defined
\ast
-homomorphism. The
reduced norm
on
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
is

\|f\|_{r}:=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\|\lambda_{x}(f)\|,

and the
reduced groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra

C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
is defined to be the completion of the
\ast
-algebra
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
with respect to this norm. Clearly, each regular representation
\lambda_{x}
can be extended to a homomorphism
\lambda_{x}:C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathfrak{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x}))
automatically. It is also routine to check that there is a canonical surjective homomorphism from
C^{*}_{\mathrm{max}}(\mathcal{G})
to
C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
.

2.5.
Coarse groupoids

Let
(X,d)
be a space as in Section
2.2
. The coarse groupoid
G(X)
on
X
was introduced by Skandalis, Tu and Yu in
[
46
]
(see also
[
40
, Chapter 10]
) to relate coarse geometry to the theory of
groupoids. As a topological space,

G(X):=\bigcup_{r>0}{\overline{E_{r}}}^{\beta(X\times X)}\subseteq\beta(X\times X).

Recall from Example
2.7
that
X\times X
is the pair groupoid with source and range maps
\mathrm{s}(x,y)=y
and
\mathrm{r}(x,y)=x
. These maps extend to maps
G(X)\to\beta X
, still denoted by
\mathrm{r}
and
\mathrm{s}
.

Now consider
(\mathrm{r},\mathrm{s}):G(X)\to\beta X\times\beta X
. It was shown in
[
46
, Lemma 2.7]
that the map
(\mathrm{r},\mathrm{s})
is injective, and hence
G(X)
can be endowed with a groupoid structure induced by the pair groupoid
\beta X\times\beta X
, called the
coarse groupoid
of
X
. Therefore,
G(X)
can also be equivalently defined by

G(X):=\bigcup_{r>0}{\overline{E_{r}}}^{\beta X\times\beta X}\subseteq\beta X\times\beta X,

with the weak topology. It was also shown in
[
46
, Proposition 3.2]
that the coarse groupoid
G(X)
is locally compact, Hausdorff, étale and principal. Clearly, the unit space of
G(X)
can be identified with
\beta X
.

Given
f\in C_{c}(G(X))
, then
f
is a continuous function supported on
\overline{E_{r}}
for some
r>0
; equivalently, we can interpret
f
as a bounded function on
E_{r}
. Hence we define an operator
\theta(f)
on
\ell^{2}(X)
by setting its matrix coefficients to be
\theta(f)(x,y):=f(x,y)
for
x,y\in X
. We have the following:

Proposition 2.8

(
[
40
, Proposition 10.29]
)
.

The map
\theta
provides a
\ast
-isomorphism from
C_{c}(G(X))
to
\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]
, and extends to a
C^{*}
-isomorphism
\Theta:C^{*}_{r}(G(X))\to C^{*}_{u}(X)
. Note that
\Theta
maps the
C^{*}
-subalgebra
C^{*}_{r}(X\times X)
onto the compact operators
\mathfrak{K}(\ell^{2}(X))
.

Recall from Section
2.3
that an operator
T\in\mathfrak{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
can be regarded as an element in
\ell^{\infty}(X\times X)
. Following the notation from
[
14
]
, we denote by
\overline{T}
the continuous extension of
T
on
\beta(X\times X)
when regarding
T\in\ell^{\infty}(X\times X)
. Then

\mathrm{supp}(\overline{T})=\overline{\mathrm{supp}(T)}.

Note that
G(X)
is open in
\beta(X\times X)
, hence
C_{0}(G(X))
is a subalgebra in
C(\beta(X\times X))
. Restricting to
G(X)
, we also regard
\overline{T}
as a function on
G(X)
and hence we can talk of the value
\overline{T}(\alpha,\gamma)
for
(\alpha,\gamma)\in G(X)
. Moreover, we have the following:

Lemma 2.9
.

For
T\in\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]
, we have
\overline{T}\in C_{c}(G(X))
and
\theta(\overline{T})=T
. For
T\in C^{*}_{u}(X)
, we have
\overline{T}\in C_{0}(G(X))
and
\Theta(\overline{T})=T
.

Proof.

The first statement is a direct corollary of Proposition
2.8
. Since
\|T\|_{\infty}\leq\|T\|
for any
T\in\mathfrak{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
, the second follows from the first.
∎

2.6.
Amenability and a-T-menability for groupoids

Amenable groupoids comprise a large class of groupoids with relatively nice properties, which are literally the analogue of amenable groups in the world of groupoids. Here we only focus on the case of étaleness, in which the notion of amenability behaves quite well. A standard reference is
[
1
]
and another reference for just étale groupoids is
[
11
, Chapter 5.6]
.

Definition 2.10

(
[
1
]
)
.

A locally compact, Hausdorff and étale groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is said to be
(topologically) amenable
if for any
\varepsilon>0
and compact
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
, there exists
f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
with range in
[0,1]
such that for any
\gamma\in K
we have

\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{r}(\gamma)}}f(\alpha)=1\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{r}(\gamma)}}|f(\alpha)-f(\alpha\gamma)|<\varepsilon.

Similar to the case of Property A, we have the following by a standard normalisation argument:

Lemma 2.11
.

A locally compact, Hausdorff and étale groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is amenable
if and only if
for any
\varepsilon>0
and compact
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
, there exists
f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
with range in
[0,+\infty)
such that for any
\gamma\in K
we have

\big|\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{r}(\gamma)}}f(\alpha)-1\big|<\varepsilon\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{r}(\gamma)}}|f(\alpha)-f(\alpha\gamma)|<\varepsilon.

Amenability for étale groupoids enjoy similar permanence properties as in the case of groups. For example, open or closed subgroupoids of amenable étale groupoids are amenable, and amenability is preserved under taking groupoid extensions. See
[
1
, Section 5]
for details. Also recall that we have the following:

Proposition 2.12

(
[
11
, Corollary 5.6.17]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact, Hausdorff, étale and amenable groupoid. Then the natural quotient
C^{*}_{\mathrm{max}}(\mathcal{G})\to C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
is an isomorphism.

Now we recall the notion of a-T-menability for groupoids introduced by Tu
[
47
]
. Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact, Hausdorff and étale groupoid. A continuous function
f:\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be of
negative type
if

(1)

f|_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}=0
;

(2)

for any
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
,
f(\gamma)=f(\gamma^{-1})
;

(3)

Given
\gamma_{1},\cdots,\gamma_{n}\in\mathcal{G}
with the same range and
\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=0
, we have
\sum_{i,j}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}f(\gamma_{i}^{-1}\gamma_{j})\leq 0
.

A continuous function
f:\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{R}
is called
locally proper
if for any compact subset
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, the restriction of
f
on
\mathcal{G}_{K}^{K}
is proper.

Definition 2.13

(
[
47
, Section 3.3]
)
.

A locally compact, Hausdorff and étale groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is said to be
a-T-menable
if there exists a continuous locally proper function
f:\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{R}
of negative type on
\mathcal{G}
.

Analogous to the case of groups, Tu
[
47
]
proved that a locally compact,
\sigma
-compact, Hausdorff and étale groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is a-T-menable
if and only if
there exists a continuous field of Hilbert spaces over
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
with a proper affine action of
\mathcal{G}
. We also need the following significant result by Tu:

Proposition 2.14

(
[
47
, Théorème 0.1]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact,
\sigma
-compact, Hausdorff, étale and a-T-menable groupoid. Then
\mathcal{G}
is
K
-amenable
. In particular, the quotient map induces an isomorphism
K_{\ast}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{max}}(\mathcal{G}))\to K_{\ast}(C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G}))
for
\ast=0,1
.

Finally, we record the following result for coarse groupoids:

Proposition 2.15

(
[
46
, Theorem 5.3 and 5.4]
)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a space and
G(X)
be the associated coarse groupoid. Then:

(1)

X
has Property A
if and only if

G(X)
is amenable;

(2)

X
can be coarsely embedded into Hilbert space
if and only if

G(X)
is a-T-menable.

3.
Limit spaces and limit operators

In this section, we recall the theory of limit spaces and limit operators for metric spaces developed by Špakula and Willett in
[
50
]
, which becomes an important tool for later use.

Throughout the section, we always assume that
(X,d)
is a space (see “Convention” in Section
2.2
) and
G(X)
is its coarse groupoid. We will freely use the notion of ultrafilters on
X
, and related materials are recalled in Appendix
A
.

3.1.
Limit spaces

First recall that a function
t:D\to R
with
D,R\subseteq X
is called a
partial translation
if
t
is a bijection from
D
to
R
, and
\sup_{x\in X}d(x,t(x))
is finite. The
graph
of
t
is
\{(t(x),x):x\in D\}
, denoted by
\mathrm{gr}(t)
. It is well-known that each entourage
E
on
X
can be decomposed into finitely many graphs of partial translations (see,
e.g.
,
[
40
, Lemma 4.10]
) thanks to the bounded geometry of
X
.

Definition 3.1

(
[
50
, Definition 3.2 and 3.6]
)
.

Fix an ultrafilter
\omega\in\beta X
. A partial translation
t:D\to R
on
X
is
compatible with
\omega
if
\omega(D)=1
. In this case, regarding
t
as a function from
D
to
\beta X
, we define the following thanks to Lemma
A.2
:

t(\omega):=\lim_{\omega}t\in\beta X.

In other words, consider the extension
\overline{t}:\overline{D}\to\overline{R}
then
t(\omega)=\overline{t}(\omega)
.

An ultrafilter
\alpha\in\beta X
is
compatible with
\omega
if there exists a partial translation
t
compatible with
\omega
and
t(\omega)=\alpha
. Denote by
X(\omega)
the collection of all ultrafilters on
X
compatible with
\omega
. A
compatible family for
\omega
is a collection of partial translations
\{t_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in X(\omega)}
such that each
t_{\alpha}
is compatible with
\omega
and
t_{\alpha}(\omega)=\alpha
.

Fix an ultrafilter
\omega
on
X
, and a compatible family
\{t_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in X(\omega)}
. Define a function
d_{\omega}:X(\omega)\times X(\omega)\to[0,\infty)
by

d_{\omega}(\alpha,\beta):=\lim_{x\to\omega}d(t_{\alpha}(x),t_{\beta}(x)).

It is shown in
[
50
, Proposition 3.7]
that
d_{\omega}
is a uniformly discrete metric of bounded geometry on
X(\omega)
which does not depend on the choice of
\{t_{\alpha}\}
.

This leads to the following:

Definition 3.2

(
[
50
, Definition 3.8]
)
.

For each non-principal ultrafilter
\omega
on
X
, the metric space
(X(\omega),d_{\omega})
is called the
limit space
of
X
at
\omega
, which is a space in the sense of “Convention” in Section
2.2
.

It is shown in
[
50
, Proposition 3.9]
that for any
\alpha\in X(\omega)
, we have
X(\alpha)=X(\omega)
as metric spaces. Also note that when
\omega
is principal,
i.e.
,
\omega\in X
, then it is clear that
(X(\omega),d_{\omega})=(X,d)
.

We recall the following result, which reveals that the local geometry of
X
can be recaptured by those of the limit spaces.

Proposition 3.3

(
[
50
, Proposition 3.10]
)
.

Let
\omega
be a non-principal ultrafilter on
X
, and
\{t_{\alpha}:D_{\alpha}\to R_{\alpha}\}
a compatible family for
\omega
. Then for each finite
F\subseteq X(\omega)
, there exists a subset
Y\subseteq X
with
\omega(Y)=1
such that for each
y\in Y
, there is a finite subset
G(y)\subseteq X
such that the map

f_{y}:F\to G(y),\alpha\mapsto t_{\alpha}(y)

is a surjective isometry. Such a collection
\{f_{y}\}_{y\in Y}
is called a
local coordinate systerm
for
F
, and the maps
f_{y}
are called
local coordinates
.

Furthermore, if
F
is a metric ball
B(\omega,r)
, then there exist
Y\subseteq X
with
\omega(Y)=1
and a local coordinate system
\{f_{y}:F\to G(y)\}_{y\in Y}
such that each
G(y)
is the ball
B(y,r)
.

As shown in
[
50
, Appendix C]
, limit spaces can be described in terms of the coarse groupoid
G(X)
:

Lemma 3.4

(
[
50
, Lemma C.3]
)
.

Given a non-principal ultrafilter
\omega\in\beta X
, the map

F:X(\omega)\rightarrow G(X)_{\omega},\quad\alpha\mapsto(\alpha,\omega)

is a bijection. Hence
X(\omega)
is the smallest invariant subset of
\beta X
containing
\omega
. Here we consider
G(X)
as a subset of
\beta X\times\beta X
, as explained in Section
2.5
.

Consequently, we obtain the following:

Corollary 3.5
.

As a set, we have

G(X)=\big(X\times X\big)\sqcup\bigcup_{\omega\in\partial_{\beta}X}\big(X(\omega)\times X(\omega)\big).

Now we would like to provide a quantitative version for Corollary
3.5
. First we record the following observation, whose proof is straightforward and almost identical to that of Lemma
3.4
(originally from
[
40
, discussion in 10.18-10.24]
):

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
t:D\to R
be a partial translation on
X
. Then we have:

\overline{\mathrm{gr}(t)}^{\beta X\times\beta X}=\mathrm{gr}(t)\sqcup\bigcup_{\omega\in\partial_{\beta}X}\big\{(\alpha,\omega):\omega(D)=1\text{ and }\alpha=t(\omega)\big\}.

In general, we have the following:

Lemma 3.7
.

For any
S\geq 0
, we have:

\overline{E_{S}}^{\beta X\times\beta X}=E_{S}\sqcup\bigcup_{\omega\in\partial_{\beta}X}\big\{(\alpha,\gamma)\in X(\omega)\times X(\omega):d_{\omega}(\alpha,\gamma)\leq S\big\}.

Proof.

As explained at the beginning of this subsection, we can decompose

E_{S}=\mathrm{gr}(t_{1})\sqcup\cdots\sqcup\mathrm{gr}(t_{N})

where each
t_{i}:D_{i}\to R_{i}
is a partial translation. Hence we have

\overline{E_{S}}=\overline{\mathrm{gr}(t_{1})}\cup\cdots\cup\overline{\mathrm{gr}(t_{N})}.

Applying Lemma
3.6
, we obtain that
\overline{E_{S}}
is contained in the right hand side in the statement.

On the other hand, given
\omega\in\partial_{\beta}X
and
\alpha,\gamma\in X(\omega)
with
d_{\omega}(\alpha,\gamma)\leq S
, then we have
(X(\omega),d_{\omega})=(X(\gamma),d_{\gamma})
. Take a partial translation
t:D\to R
such that
\gamma(D)=1
and
\alpha=t(\gamma)
. Note that

d_{\gamma}(\alpha,\gamma)=\lim_{x\to\gamma}d(t(x),x)\leq S.

Hence for
D^{\prime}:=\{x\in D:d(t(x),x)\leq S\}
, we have
\gamma(D^{\prime})=1
. Consider the restriction of
t
on
D^{\prime}
, denoted by
t^{\prime}
. Then
t^{\prime}
is also a partial translation and
\alpha=t^{\prime}(\gamma)
. By Lemma
3.6
, we obtain that
(\alpha,\gamma)\in\overline{\mathrm{gr}(t^{\prime})}
, which is contained in
\overline{E_{S}}
as desired.
∎

Now we compute concrete examples of limit spaces. First we recall the case of groups from
[
50
, Appendix B]
. Let
\Gamma
be a countable discrete group, equipped with a left-invariant bounded geometry and strongly discrete metric
d
. For each
g\in\Gamma
, denote

\rho_{g}:\Gamma\to\Gamma,h\mapsto hg

the right translation map. Each
\rho_{g}
is a partial translation with full domain, and hence is compatible with every
\omega\in\beta\Gamma
. Moreover, we have the following:

Lemma 3.8

(
[
50
, Lemma B.1]
)
.

For each non-principal ultrafilter
\omega\in\beta\Gamma
, the map

b_{\omega}:\Gamma\longrightarrow\Gamma(\omega),g\mapsto\rho_{g}(\omega)

is an isometric bijection.

Inspired by Lemma
3.8
, we provide the following general method:

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
\{t_{\lambda}:D_{\lambda}\to R_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
be a family of partial translations on
X
satisfying the following: for each
S>0
, there exists a finite subset
\Lambda_{S}\subseteq\Lambda
such that
\mathrm{gr}(t_{\lambda})\cap\mathrm{gr}(t_{\mu})
is finite for
\lambda\neq\mu
in
\Lambda_{S}
and
E_{S}\setminus\big(\bigcup_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{S}}\mathrm{gr}(t_{\lambda})\big)
is finite. Then for any non-principal ultrafilter
\omega
on
X
and
\alpha\in X(\omega)
, there exists
\lambda\in\Lambda
such that
\omega(D_{\lambda})=1
and
\alpha=t_{\lambda}(\omega)
.

Proof.

By definition, we assume that
\alpha=t(\omega)
for some partial translation
t:D\to R
with
\omega(D)=1
. For
\lambda\in\Lambda
, set
\widetilde{D}_{\lambda}:=\{x\in D\cap D_{\lambda}:t(x)=t_{\lambda}(x)\}
. Choose
S>0
such that
\mathrm{gr}(t)\subseteq E_{S}
, and hence there exists a finite subset
F\subseteq E_{S}
such that

\mathrm{gr}(t)\subseteq\big(\bigcup_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{S}}\mathrm{gr}(t_{\lambda})\big)\sqcup F.

This implies that
D\subseteq\big(\bigcup_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{S}}\widetilde{D}_{\lambda}\big)\sqcup F^{\prime}
for some finite
F^{\prime}\subseteq X
. Since
\omega
is non-principal,
\Lambda_{S}
is finite and
\widetilde{D}_{\lambda}\cap\widetilde{D}_{\mu}
is finite for any
\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda_{S}
, there exists a unique
\lambda\in\Lambda_{S}
such that
\omega(\widetilde{D}_{\lambda})=1
. This implies that
\omega(D_{\lambda})=1
and
\alpha=t_{\lambda}(\omega)
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Back to the case of the group
\Gamma
, the set
\{\rho_{g}:\Gamma\to\Gamma\}_{g\in\Gamma}
satisfies the condition in Lemma
3.9
, and hence the map
b_{\omega}
in Lemma
3.8
is surjective. It is straightforward to check that
b_{\omega}
is isometric, which recovers the proof for Lemma
3.8
.

Example 3.10
.

Consider
X=\mathbb{N}
with the usual metric. For each
k\in\mathbb{Z}
with
k\geq 0
, define a partial translation

\rho_{k}:\mathbb{N}\longrightarrow\mathbb{N},\quad n\mapsto n+k.

For
k\in\mathbb{Z}
with
k<0
, define a partial translation

\rho_{k}:[-k,\infty)\cap\mathbb{N}\longrightarrow\mathbb{N},\quad n\mapsto n+k.

Then it is clear that the set
\{\rho_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
satisfies the condition in Lemma
3.9
. Now for a non-principal ultrafilter
\omega
on
\mathbb{N}
, consider the map

b_{\omega}:\mathbb{Z}\longrightarrow\mathbb{N}(\omega),\quad k\mapsto\rho_{k}(\omega).

Note that for
k,l\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

d_{\omega}(\rho_{k}(\omega),\rho_{l}(\omega))=\lim_{n\to\omega}|(k+n)-(l+n)|=|k-l|,

which implies that
b_{\omega}
is isometric. Moreover, Lemma
3.9
shows that
b_{\omega}
is surjective. Therefore, every limit space of
\mathbb{N}
is isometric to
\mathbb{Z}
. This provides a detailed proof for
[
50
, Example 3.14(2)]
.

Similar to the analysis in Example
3.10
, we can also apply Lemma
3.9
to obtain proofs for
[
50
, Example 3.14(3)-(5)]
. Details are left to readers.

3.2.
Limit operators

Now we recall the notion of limit operators for metric spaces introduced by Špakula and Willett:

Definition 3.11

(
[
50
, Definition 4.4]
)
.

For a non-principal ultrafilter
\omega
on
X
, fix a compatible family
\{t_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in X(\omega)}
for
\omega
and let
T\in C^{*}_{u}(X)
. The
limit operator of
T
at
\omega
, denoted by
\Phi_{\omega}(T)
, is an
X(\omega)
-by-
X(\omega)
indexed matrix defined by

\Phi_{\omega}(T)_{\alpha\gamma}:=\lim_{x\to\omega}T_{t_{\alpha}(x)t_{\gamma}(x)}\quad\text{for}\quad\alpha,\gamma\in X(\omega).

It was studied in
[
50
, Chapter 4]
that the above definition does not depend on the choice of the compatible family
\{t_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in X(\omega)}
for
\omega
. Furthermore, the limit operator
\Phi_{\omega}(T)
is indeed a bounded operator on
\ell^{2}(X(\omega))
, and belongs to the uniform Roe algebra
C^{*}_{u}(X(\omega))
.

Recall from Lemma
2.9
that for an operator
T\in C^{*}_{u}(X)
, the continuous extension
\overline{T}\in C_{0}(G(X))
. We have the following, which was implicitly mentioned in the proof of
[
50
, Lemma C.3]
.

Lemma 3.12
.

For a non-principal ultrafilter
\omega
on
X
and
T\in C^{*}_{u}(X)
, we have

\Phi_{\omega}(T)_{\alpha\gamma}=\overline{T}(\alpha,\gamma)\quad\text{for}\quad\alpha,\gamma\in X(\omega).

Proof.

Choose partial translations
t_{\alpha},t_{\gamma}
compatible with
\alpha,\gamma
such that
t_{\alpha}(\omega)=\alpha
,
t_{\gamma}(\omega)=\gamma
. By definition, we have

\Phi_{\omega}(T)_{\alpha\gamma}=\lim_{x\to\omega}T_{t_{\alpha}(x)t_{\gamma}(x)}=\lim_{x\to\omega}T(t_{\alpha}(x),t_{\gamma}(x))=\overline{T}(\alpha,\gamma)

where the last equality comes from the discussion before Lemma
2.9
.
∎

Since the limit operator
\Phi_{\omega}(T)
contains the information of the asymptotic behaviour of
T
“in the
\omega
-direction”, we introduce the following:

Definition 3.13
.

For an
\omega\in\partial_{\beta}X
, we say that an operator
T\in C^{*}_{u}(X)
is
locally invisible (
or
vanishes) in the
\omega
-direction
if
\Phi_{\omega}(T)=0
. For a subset
V\subseteq\partial_{\beta}X
, we say that
T
is
locally invisible (
or
vanishes) in the
V
-direction
if
\Phi_{\omega}(T)=0
for any
\omega\in V
.

Finally, we recall from Proposition
2.8
that there is a
C^{*}
-isomorphism
\Theta:C^{*}_{r}(G(X))\to C^{*}_{u}(X)
. This allows us to relate limit operators to left regular representations of
C^{*}_{r}(G(X))
:

Lemma 3.14

(
[
50
, Lemma C.3]
)
.

For a non-principal ultrafilter
\omega
on
X
, let
W_{\omega}:\ell^{2}(G(X)_{\omega})\to\ell^{2}(X(\omega))
be the unitary representation induced by
F
in Lemma
3.4
. Then we have the following commutative diagram:

\textstyle{C^{*}_{r}(G(X))\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\textstyle\lambda_{\omega}}
\scriptstyle{\textstyle\Theta}
\scriptstyle{\textstyle\cong}
\textstyle{\mathfrak{B}(\ell^{2}(G(X)_{\omega}))\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\textstyle\mathrm{Ad}_{W_{\omega}}}
\scriptstyle{\textstyle\cong}
\textstyle{C^{*}_{u}(X)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\textstyle\Phi_{\omega}}
\textstyle{\mathfrak{B}(\ell^{2}(X(\omega))),}

where
\lambda_{\omega}
is the left regular representation from (
2.1
).

4.
Geometric ideals

In this section, we recall the notion of geometric ideals, which was originally introduced by the first-named author in
[
51
]
(see also
[
14
]
).
Throughout the section, let
X
be a space in the sense of “Convention” in Section
2.2
.

Definition 4.1

(
[
14
, Definition 3.1, 3.3]
)
.

For an operator
T\in C^{*}_{u}(X)
and
\varepsilon>0
, the
\varepsilon
-support of
T
is defined to be

\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(T):=\{(x,y)\in X\times X:|T(x,y)|\geq\varepsilon\}.

Also define the
\varepsilon
-truncation of
T
to be

T_{\varepsilon}(x,y):=\begin{cases}~T(x,y),&\mbox{if~}|T(x,y)|\geq\varepsilon;\\
~0,&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

It is clear that
\mathrm{supp}(T_{\varepsilon})=\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(T)
. We also record the following elementary result for later use. The proof is straightforward, hence omitted.

Lemma 4.2
.

Given
T\in C^{*}_{u}(X)
and
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\overline{\mathrm{supp}(T_{\varepsilon})}\subseteq\{\tilde{\omega}\in\beta(X\times X):|\overline{T}(\tilde{\omega})|\geq\varepsilon\}\subseteq\overline{\mathrm{supp}(T_{\varepsilon/2})}.

The following is a key result in
[
14
]
:

Proposition 4.3

(
[
14
, Theorem 3.5]
)
.

Let
I
be an ideal in the uniform Roe algebra
C^{*}_{u}(X)
. For each
T\in I
and
\varepsilon>0
, we have
T_{\varepsilon}\in I\cap\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]
. Moreover, we have

\overline{I\cap\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]}=\overline{\{T_{\varepsilon}:T\in I,\varepsilon>0\}}

where the closures are taken with respect to the operator norm.

Now we recall the notion of geometric ideals from
[
51
]
:

Definition 4.4
.

An ideal
I
in the uniform Roe algebra
C^{*}_{u}(X)
is called
geometric
if
I\cap\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]
is dense in
I
.

In
[
14
]
, Chen and the first-named author provide a full description for geometric ideals in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
in terms of invariant open subsets of
G(X)^{(0)}=\beta X
.
To outline their work, let us start with the following elementary observation:

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
U
be a non-empty invariant open subset of
\beta X
, then
X\subseteq U
.

Proof.

Since
X
is dense in
\beta X
and
U
is open and non-empty, we obtain that
U\cap X\neq\emptyset
. Take an
x\in U\cap X
, then the pair
(x,y)\in G(X)
for any
y\in X
. Thanks to the invariance of
U
, we obtain that
y\in U
. This implies that
X\subseteq U
.
∎

Given an invariant open subset
U\subseteq\beta X
, denote
G(X)_{U}:=G(X)\cap s^{-1}(U)
. Following
[
14
]
, we define

\displaystyle I_{c}(U):

\displaystyle=\{f\in C_{c}(G(X)):f(\tilde{\omega})=0\mbox{~for~any~}\tilde{\omega}\notin G(X)_{U}\}

\displaystyle=\{T\in\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]:\overline{T}(\tilde{\omega})=0\mbox{~for~any~}\tilde{\omega}\notin G(X)_{U}\}.

Obviously,
I_{c}(U)
is a two-sided ideal in
C_{c}(G(X))
. Denote its closure in
C^{*}_{r}(G(X))
by
I(U)
, which is a geometric ideal in
C^{*}_{r}(G(X))\cong C^{*}_{u}(X)
from the definition (see also
[
14
, Lemma 5.1]
). This leads to the following:

Definition 4.6
.

For an invariant open subset
U\subseteq\beta X
, the ideal
I(U)
is called the
geometric ideal associated to
U
.

For later use, we record the following alternative description for the geometric ideal
I(U)
:

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
U
be an invariant open subset of
\beta X
. Then the ideal
I(U)
is isomorphic to the reduced groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}_{r}(G(X)_{U})
.

Proof.

This was implicitly contained in the proof of
[
14
, Proposition 5.5]
. For convenience to the readers, we include a proof here. By definition,
C^{*}_{r}(G(X)_{U})
is isomorphic to the norm closure of
C_{c}(G(X)_{U})
in
C^{*}_{r}(G(X))
. Note that

C_{c}(G(X)_{U})=\{T\in\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]:\mathrm{supp}(\overline{T})\subseteq G(X)_{U}\}\subseteq I_{c}(U).

On the other hand, for
T\in I_{c}(U)
we have
T=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}T_{\varepsilon}
since
T
has finite propagation. Note that
\mathrm{supp}(\overline{T_{\varepsilon}})\subseteq G(X)_{U}
, which implies
T_{\varepsilon}\in C_{c}(G(X)_{U})
. Hence we obtain that
C_{c}(G(X)_{U})
and
I_{c}(U)
have the same closure in
C^{*}_{r}(G(X))
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Example 4.8
.

For
U=X
, it follows directly from definition that
G(X)_{X}=X\times X
. Hence combining Proposition
2.8
and Lemma
4.7
, we obtain that the geometric ideal associated to
X
is
I(X)=\mathfrak{K}(\ell^{2}(X))
. On the other hand, for
U=\beta X
it is clear that
I(\beta X)=C^{*}_{u}(X)
.

Conversely, following
[
14
, Section 4 and 5]
we can associate an invariant open subset of
\beta X
to any ideal in the uniform Roe algebra. More precisely, let
I
be an ideal in the uniform Roe algebra
C^{*}_{u}(X)
. Define:

(4.1)

U(I):=\bigcup_{T\in I,\varepsilon>0}\overline{\mathrm{r}(\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(T))}=\bigcup_{T\in I\cap\mathbb{C}_{u}[X],\varepsilon>0}\overline{\mathrm{r}(\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(T))},

where the second equality follows directly from Proposition
4.3
. Also
[
14
, Lemma 5.2]
implies that
U(I)
is an invariant open subset of
\beta X
. Furthermore, as a special case of
[
14
, Theorem 6.3]
, we have the following:

Proposition 4.9
.

For a space
(X,d)
, the map
I\mapsto U(I)
provides an isomorphism between the lattice of all geometric ideals in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
and the lattice of all invariant open subsets of
\beta X
, with the inverse map given by
U\mapsto I(U)
.

Proposition
4.9
shows that geometric ideals in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
can be fully determined by invariant open subsets of
\beta X
. In contrast, general ideals in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
cannot be characterised merely by the associated subsets of
\beta X
. For example, direct calculations show that the associated invariant open subsets for the ideal
I_{G}
defined in Section
2.3
and for the ideal of compact operators in
\mathfrak{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
are the same, both of which equal
X
(see also Example
4.8
and
5.5
below).

Hence as pointed out in Section
1
, the study of the ideal structure for the uniform Roe algebra can be reduced to analyse the lattice (where the order is given by inclusion)

\mathfrak{I}_{U}=\{I\mbox{~is~an~ideal~in~}C^{*}_{u}(X):U(I)=U\}

in (
1.1
) for each invariant open subset
U\subseteq\beta X
. The following result busts the smallest element in
\mathfrak{I}_{U}
:

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
U
be an invariant open subset of
\beta X
.
Then the geometric ideal
I(U)
is the smallest element in the lattice
\mathfrak{I}_{U}
in (
1.1
).

The proof of Proposition
4.10
follows directly from the following lemma:

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
(X,d)
be a space and
I
an ideal in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
. Then we have

I(U(I))=\overline{I\cap\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]},

where the closure is taken in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
. Hence we have
I(U(I))\subseteq I
.

Proof.

Denoting
\mathring{I}:=\overline{I\cap\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]}
, it is clear that
\mathring{I}\cap\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]=I\cap\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]
. This implies that
\mathring{I}
is a geometric ideal in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
, and hence
\mathring{I}=I(U(\mathring{I}))
by Proposition
4.9
. By definition, we have

U(\mathring{I})=\bigcup_{T\in\mathring{I}\cap\mathbb{C}_{u}[X],\varepsilon>0}\overline{\mathrm{r}(\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(T))}=\bigcup_{T\in I\cap\mathbb{C}_{u}[X],\varepsilon>0}\overline{\mathrm{r}(\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(T))}=U(I).

Therefore, we obtain that
I(U(I))=I(U(\mathring{I}))=\mathring{I}=\overline{I\cap\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]}
as required.
∎

We would like to recall another description for geometric ideals based on the notion of ideals in spaces introduced in
[
14
]
. It has the advantage of playing within the given metric space, rather than going to the
mysterious
Stone-Čech boundary, and hence will help us to step over several technical gaps in Section
8
.

Definition 4.12

(
[
14
, Definition 6.1]
)
.

An
ideal
in a space
(X,d)
is a collection
{\bf L}
of subsets of
X
satisfying the following:

(1)

if
Y\in{\bf L}
and
Z\subseteq Y
, then
Z\in{\bf L}
;

(2)

if
R\geq 0
and
Y\in{\bf L}
, then
\mathcal{N}_{R}(Y)\in{\bf L}
;

(3)

if
Y,Z\in{\bf L}
, then
Y\cup Z\in{\bf L}
.

For an ideal
{\bf L}
in
X
, we define

U({\bf L}):=\bigcup_{Y\in{\bf L}}\overline{Y}^{\beta X}.

Conversely, given an invariant open subset
U
of
\beta X
, we define

{\bf L}(U):=\{Y\subseteq X:\overline{Y}^{\beta X}\subseteq U\}.

As a special case of
[
14
, Theorem 6.3]
, we have the following:

Proposition 4.13
.

For a space
(X,d)
, the map
{\bf L}\mapsto U({\bf L})
provides an isomorphism between the lattice of all ideals in
X
and the lattice of all invariant open subsets of
\beta X
, with the inverse map given by
U\mapsto{\bf L}(U)
.

Combining Proposition
4.9
and
4.13
, we obtain an isomorphism between the lattice of all ideals in
X
and the lattice of all geometric ideals in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
. Direct calculation shows (see also
[
14
, Theorem 6.4]
):

(4.2)

I(U({\bf L}))=\overline{\{T\in\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]:\mathrm{supp}(T)\subseteq Y\times Y\mbox{~for~some~}Y\in{\bf L}\}},

where the closure is taken in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
.

Now we consider a special class of geometric ideals coming from subspaces. Given a subspace
A\subseteq X
, recall from
[
27
, Section 5]
that there is an associated ideal
I_{A}
in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
whose
K
-theory is isomorphic to that of the uniform Roe algebra
C^{*}(A)
. More precisely, recall that an operator
T\in\mathfrak{B}(\ell^{2}(X))
is
near
A
if there exists
R>0
such that
\mathrm{supp}(T)\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{R}(A)\times\mathcal{N}_{R}(A)
, and the ideal
I_{A}
is defined to be the operator norm closure of all operators in
\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]
near
A
.

The ideal
I_{A}
is called
spatial
in
[
15
]
since it is related to a subspace in
X
. However, as shown in
[
15
, Example 2.1]
, there exist non-spatial ideals in general. On the other hand, spatial ideals play an important role in the computation of the
K
-theory of Roe algebras via the Mayer-Vietoris sequence argument (see
[
27
]
).

To show that spatial ideals are geometric, we observe that the smallest ideal in
X
containing
A
is
{\bf L}_{A}:=\{Z\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{R}(A):R>0\}
. Hence applying Proposition
4.13
, we immediately obtain the following:

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
A
be a subset of
X
. Then the set

(4.3)

U_{A}:=U({\bf L}_{A})=\bigcup_{R>0}\overline{\mathcal{N}_{R}(A)}

is an invariant open subset of
\beta X
. Moreover, if
U
is an invariant open subset of
\beta X
containing
A
, then
U_{A}\subseteq U
.

Consequently, combining with (
4.2
) we reach the following:

Corollary 4.15
.

Let
A
be a subset of
X
, then
I(U_{A})=I_{A}
. Hence the spatial ideal
I_{A}
is geometric.

For later use, we record the following result concerning the set
U_{A}
defined in (
4.3
). Recall from Corollary
A.8
that for a subset
Z\subseteq X
, the closure
\overline{Z}
in
\beta X
is homeomorphic to
\beta Z
.

Lemma 4.16
.

Let
A
be a subset of
X
. Then we have:

G(X)_{U_{A}}=\bigcup_{R>0}G(\mathcal{N}_{R}(A)).

Proof.

By definition, we have
G(X)_{U_{A}}=\bigcup_{R>0}G(X)_{\overline{\mathcal{N}_{R}(A)}}
. Note that for each
R>0
and
(\alpha,\omega)\in G(X)_{\overline{\mathcal{N}_{R}(A)}}
, we have
\omega\in\overline{\mathcal{N}_{R}(A)}
and there exists
S>0
such that
(\alpha,\omega)\in\overline{E_{S}}
due to Lemma
3.7
. Hence the pair
(\alpha,\omega)
in
\overline{\mathcal{N}_{R+S}(A)}\times\overline{\mathcal{N}_{R+S}(A)}
belongs to
G(\mathcal{N}_{R+S}(A))
, which concludes the proof.
∎

To end this section, we remark that for a given ideal
I
in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
, it is usually hard to compute the associated
U(I)
directly from definition. However, this is always achievable for principal ideals:

Lemma 4.17
.

Let
I=\langle T\rangle
be the principal ideal in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
generated by
T\in C^{*}_{u}(X)
. Denote

U:=\bigcup_{\varepsilon>0,R>0}\overline{\mathcal{N}_{R}(\mathrm{r}(\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(T)))}.

Then
U
is an invariant open subset of
\beta X
, and we have
U(I)=U
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.14
, it is clear that
U
is an invariant open subset of
\beta X
. By (
4.1
),
U(I)
contains
\overline{\mathrm{r}(\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(T))}
for any
\varepsilon>0
. Since
U(I)
is invariant, we obtain that
U(I)
contains
U
again by Lemma
4.14
.

On the other hand, we consider
S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}Tb_{i}
where
a_{i},b_{i}
are non-zero with supports being partial translations contained in
E_{R}
for some
R>0
. Hence for any
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\mathrm{r}\left(\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(S)\right)\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{r}\left(\mathrm{supp}_{\frac{\varepsilon}{n}}(a_{i}Tb_{i})\right)\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{N}_{R}\left(\mathrm{r}\left(\mathrm{supp}_{\frac{\varepsilon}{n\|a_{i}\|\cdot\|b_{i}\|}}(T)\right)\right),

which implies that
\overline{\mathrm{r}\left(\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(S)\right)}\subseteq U
. Note that operators of the form
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}Tb_{i}
as above are dense in
I
. Hence for a general element
\tilde{S}\in I
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}Tb_{i}
where
a_{i},b_{i}
are non-zero with supports being partial translations such that
\|\tilde{S}-S\|<\varepsilon/2
. Hence
\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{S})\subseteq\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon/2}(S)
, which concludes the proof.
∎

5.
Ghostly ideals

In the previous section, we observe that for an invariant open subset
U
of
\beta X
, the associated geometric ideal
I(U)
is the smallest element in the lattice

\mathfrak{I}_{U}=\{I\mbox{~is~an~ideal~in~}C^{*}_{u}(X):U(I)=U\}.

In this section, we would like to explore the largest element in
\mathfrak{I}_{U}
. As revealed in Section
1
, a natural idea is to include all operators
T\in C^{*}_{u}(X)
sitting in some ideal
I
with
U(I)=U
, which leads to the following:

Definition 5.1
.

For a space
(X,d)
and an invariant open subset
U
of
\beta X
, denote

\tilde{I}(U):=\{T\in C^{*}_{u}(X):\overline{\mathrm{r}(\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(T))}\subseteq U\mbox{~for~any~}\varepsilon>0\}.

We call
\tilde{I}(U)
the
ghostly ideal associated to
U
.

The terminology will become clear later (see Proposition
5.6
and Remark
5.8
below). First let us verify that
\tilde{I}(U)
is indeed an ideal in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
.

Lemma 5.2
.

For an invariant open subset
U
of
\beta X
,
\tilde{I}(U)
is an ideal in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
.

Proof.

For
T,S\in C^{*}_{u}(X)
and
\varepsilon>0
, we have
\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(T+S)\subseteq\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon/2}(T)\cup\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon/2}(S)
.
It follows that
\tilde{I}(U)
is a linear space in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
. Given
T\in\tilde{I}(U)
and
\varepsilon>0
, note that
\mathrm{r}(\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(T^{*}))=\mathrm{s}(\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(T))
and hence
T^{*}\in\tilde{I}(U)
since
U
is invariant. Now given a sequence
\{T_{n}\}_{n}
in
\tilde{I}(U)
converging to
T\in C^{*}_{u}(X)
and an
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(T)\subseteq\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon/2}(T_{n})
. Hence we obtain that
T\in\tilde{I}(U)
.

Finally, given
T\in\tilde{I}(U)
and
S\in C^{*}_{u}(X)
we need show that
TS
and
ST
belong to
\tilde{I}(U)
. Note that
ST=(T^{*}S^{*})^{*}
and
\tilde{I}(U)
is closed under taking the
\ast
-operation, hence it suffices to show that
TS\in\tilde{I}(U)
.
Since
\tilde{I}(U)
is closed in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
with respect to the operator norm, it suffices to consider the case that
S\in\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]
.
We can further assume that
S
is a partial translation since
\tilde{I}(U)
is closed under taking addition. In this case, we have
\mathrm{r}(\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(TS))\subseteq\mathrm{r}(\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon/\|S\|}(T))
for any
\varepsilon>0
. Hence we conclude the proof.
∎

Using the language of ideals in
X
, we record that for an ideal
{\bf L}
in
X
and
T\in C^{*}_{u}(X)
, then
T\in\tilde{I}(U({\bf L}))

if and only if
for any
\varepsilon>0
there exist
R>0
and
Y\in{\bf L}
such that for any
(x,y)\notin E_{R}\cap(Y\times Y)
then
|T(x,y)|<\varepsilon
.

The following result shows that the ghostly ideal
\tilde{I}(U)
is indeed the largest element in the lattice
\mathfrak{I}_{U}
:

Lemma 5.3
.

Given an invariant open subset
U
of
\beta X
, we have the following:

(1)

for an ideal
I
in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
with
U(I)=U
, then
I\subseteq\tilde{I}(U)
.

(2)

U(\tilde{I}(U))=U
.

Proof.

(1). Given
T\in I
, the condition
U(I)=U
implies that
\overline{\mathrm{r}(\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(T))}\subseteq U
for each
\varepsilon>0
. Hence by definition, we have
T\in\tilde{I}(U)
.

(2). Note that

U(\tilde{I}(U))=\bigcup_{T\in\tilde{I}(U),\varepsilon>0}\overline{\mathrm{r}(\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(T))}\subseteq U.

On the other hand, Proposition
4.9
shows that
U(I(U))=U
, which implies that
\tilde{I}(U)\supseteq I(U)
thanks to (1). Hence we have
U(\tilde{I}(U))\supseteq U(I(U))=U
again by Proposition
4.9
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Combining with Proposition
4.10
, we reach the following desired result:

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
(X,d)
be a space as in Section
2.2
, and
U
be an invariant open subset of
\beta X
. Then any ideal
I
in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
with
U(I)=U
sits between
I(U)
and
\tilde{I}(U)
. More precisely, the geometric ideal
I(U)
is the smallest element while the ghostly ideal
\tilde{I}(U)
is the largest element in the lattice
\mathfrak{I}_{U}
in (
1.1
).

As mentioned in Section
1
, Theorem
5.4
draws the border of the lattice
\mathfrak{I}_{U}
. Therefore, once we can bust every ideal between
I(U)
and
\tilde{I}(U)
for each invariant open subset
U\subseteq\beta X
, then we will obtain a full description for the ideal structure of the uniform Roe algebra
C^{*}_{u}(X)
(see Question
9.1
).

Now we aim to provide a geometric description for ghostly ideals, which helps to explain the terminology. Let us start with an easy example.

Example 5.5
.

Taking
U=X
, then
\tilde{I}(X)
is the ideal
I_{G}
defined in Section
2.3
. Indeed,
T\in\tilde{I}(X)
if and only if for any
\varepsilon>0
,
\mathrm{r}(\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(T))
is finite. This is equivalent to that
T\in C_{0}(X\times X)
since
T\in C^{*}_{u}(X)
. On the other hand, it is clear that
\tilde{I}(\beta X)=C^{*}_{u}(X)
.

More generally, we have the following result. Note that for any invariant open subset
U
of
\beta X
,
G(X)_{U}
is an open subset of
\beta(X\times X)
. Hence both
C_{0}(G(X)_{U})
and
I_{c}(U)
can be regarded as subalgebras in
C(\beta(X\times X))\cong\ell^{\infty}(X\times X)
.

Proposition 5.6
.

For an invariant open subset
U\subseteq\beta X
and
T\in C^{*}_{u}(X)
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

T\in\tilde{I}(U)
;

(2)

\overline{T}\in C_{0}(G(X)_{U})
;

(3)

\overline{T}\in\overline{I_{c}(U)}^{\|\cdot\|_{\infty}}
;

(4)

T
vanishes in the
(\beta X\setminus U)
-direction,
i.e.
,
\Phi_{\omega}(T)=0
for any
\omega\in\beta X\setminus U
.

Proof.

“(1)
\Rightarrow
(2)”: By definition, for any
\varepsilon>0
we have

\mathrm{r}(\mathrm{supp}(\overline{T_{\varepsilon}}))=\mathrm{r}(\overline{\mathrm{supp}(T_{\varepsilon})})=\overline{\mathrm{r}(\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(T))}\subseteq U.

Consider the compact set
K:=\{\tilde{\omega}\in\beta(X\times X):|\overline{T}(\tilde{\omega})|\geq 2\varepsilon\}
. By Lemma
4.2
, we have
\mathrm{r}(K)\subseteq\mathrm{r}(\mathrm{supp}(\overline{T_{\varepsilon}}))\subseteq U
, which implies that
K\subseteq G(X)_{U}
.
Moreover, we have
|\overline{T}(\tilde{\omega})|<2\varepsilon
for any
\tilde{\omega}\in\beta(X\times X)\setminus K
, which concludes that
\overline{T}\in C_{0}(G(X)_{U})
.

“(2)
\Rightarrow
(1)”: Assume that
\overline{T}\in C_{0}(G(X)_{U})\subseteq C(\beta(X\times X))
. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a compact subset
K\subseteq G(X)_{U}
such that for any
\tilde{\omega}\in\beta(X\times X)\setminus K
, we have
|\overline{T}(\tilde{\omega})|<\varepsilon
. This implies that
\{\tilde{\omega}\in\beta(X\times X):|\overline{T}(\tilde{\omega})|\geq\varepsilon\}\subseteq K
. Using Lemma
4.2
, we obtain that
\overline{\mathrm{supp}(T_{\varepsilon})}\subseteq K
, which implies that
\overline{\mathrm{r}(\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(T))}\subseteq U
. Hence
T\in\tilde{I}(U)
.

“(2)
\Leftrightarrow
(3)”: This is due to the fact that

\overline{I_{c}(U)}^{\|\cdot\|_{\infty}}=\{f\in C_{0}(G(X)):f(\tilde{\omega})=0\text{ for }\tilde{\omega}\notin G(X)_{U}\}.

“(3)
\Leftrightarrow
(4)”: By Lemma
3.12
, (4) holds if and only if
\overline{T}(\alpha,\gamma)=0
for any
\alpha,\gamma\in X(\omega)
and
\omega\in\beta X\setminus U
. Applying Lemma
2.9
and Lemma
3.4
, this holds if and only if
\overline{T}(\tilde{\omega})=0
whenever
\tilde{\omega}\notin G(X)_{U}
, which describes elements in
\overline{I_{c}(U)}^{\|\cdot\|_{\infty}}
. Hence we conclude the proof.
∎

Note that
G(X)_{X}=X\times X
. Hence as a direct corollary, we recover the following characterisation for ghost operators:

Corollary 5.7

(
[
50
, Proposition 8.2]
)
.

An operator
T\in C^{*}_{u}(X)
is a ghost
if and only if

\Phi_{\omega}(T)=0
for any non-principal ultrafilter
\omega
on
X
.

Remark 5.8
.

In other words, Corollary
5.7
shows that a ghost in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
is locally invisible in all directions. This suggests us to consider operators in
\tilde{I}(U)
as “partial” ghosts, which clarifies the terminology of “ghostly ideals”.

As an application of Proposition
5.6
, we now provide another description for ghostly ideals in terms of operator algebras, which will be used later in Section
7
. Let us start with the short exact sequences studied in
[
24
]
(see also
[
22
, Section 2]
).

Given an invariant open subset
U\subseteq\beta X
, notice that
U^{c}=\beta X\setminus U
is also invariant. Denote by
G(X)_{U^{c}}:=G(X)\cap\mathrm{s}^{-1}(U^{c})
and clearly, we have a decomposition:

G(X)=G(X)_{U}\sqcup G(X)_{U^{c}}.

Note that
G(X)_{U}
is open in
G(X)
, hence the above induces the following short exact sequence of
\ast
-algebras:

(5.1)

0\longrightarrow C_{c}(G(X)_{U})\longrightarrow C_{c}(G(X))\longrightarrow C_{c}(G(X)_{U^{c}})\longrightarrow 0

where the map
C_{c}(G(X)_{U})\longrightarrow C_{c}(G(X))
is the inclusion and the map
C_{c}(G(X))\longrightarrow C_{c}(G(X)_{U^{c}})
is the restriction.

We may complete the sequence (
5.1
) with respect to the maximal groupoid
C^{*}
-norms and obtain the following sequence:

(5.2)

0\longrightarrow C^{*}_{\rm max}(G(X)_{U})\longrightarrow C^{*}_{\rm max}(G(X))\longrightarrow C^{*}_{\rm max}(G(X)_{U^{c}})\longrightarrow 0,

which is easy to check by definition to be automatically exact (see,
e.g.
,
[
31
, Lemma 2.10]
). We may also complete this sequence with respect to the reduced groupoid
C^{*}
-norms and obtain the following sequence:

(5.3)

0\longrightarrow C^{*}_{r}(G(X)_{U})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i_{U}}}{{\longrightarrow}}C^{*}_{r}(G(X))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle q_{U}}}{{\longrightarrow}}C^{*}_{r}(G(X)_{U^{c}})\longrightarrow 0.

By construction,
i_{U}
is injective,
q_{U}
is surjective and
q_{U}\circ i_{U}=0
. Also recall from Lemma
4.7
that
i_{U}(C^{*}_{r}(G(X)_{U}))=I(U)
, the geometric ideal associated to
U
. However in general, (
5.3
) fails to be exact at the middle item. This is crucial in
[
24
]
to provide a counterexample to the Baum-Connes conjecture with coefficients.

More precisely when
U=X
, it is proved in
[
41
, Proposition 2.11]
for the group case and
[
22
, Proposition 4.4]
for the Roe algebraic case (see also
[
50
, Proposition 8.2]
) that
{\rm Ker}(q_{X})=I_{G}
,
i.e.
, the ideal consisting of all ghost operators in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
. Hence from Example
4.8
and Example
5.5
, the sequence (
5.3
) is exact for
U=X

if and only if

I(X)=\tilde{I}(X)
.
More generally, we have the following:

Proposition 5.9
.

Given an invariant open subset
U\subseteq\beta X
, the kernel of
q_{U}:C^{*}_{r}(G(X))\to C^{*}_{r}(G(X)_{U^{c}})
coincides with the ghostly ideal
\tilde{I}(U)
. Hence the sequence (
5.3
) is exact
if and only if

I(U)=\tilde{I}(U)
.

Proof.

It is easy to see that an operator
T\in C^{*}_{u}(X)\cong C^{*}_{r}(G(X))
belongs to the kernel of
q_{U}
if and only if
\lambda_{\omega}(T)=0
for any
\omega\in\beta X\setminus U
, where
\lambda_{\omega}
is the left regular representation from (
2.1
). Now Lemma
3.14
implies that
\Phi_{\omega}(T)=0
for any
\omega\in\beta X\setminus U
. Finally, we conclude the proof thanks to Proposition
5.6
.
∎

We end this section with an illuminating example from
[
51
, Section 3]
(see also
[
22
, Section 5]
), which is important to construct counterexamples to Baum-Connes type conjectures:

Example 5.10
.

Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of expander graphs or pertubed expander graphs (see
[
51
]
for the precise definition). Let
Y_{i,j}=X_{i}
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
and set
Y:=\bigsqcup_{i,j}Y_{i,j}
. We endow
Y
with a metric
d
such that it is the graph metric on each
Y_{i,j}
and satisfies
d(Y_{i,j},Y_{k,l})\to\infty
as
i+j+k+l\to\infty
.

Let
P_{i,j}\in\mathfrak{B}(\ell^{2}(Y_{i,j}))
be the orthogonal projection onto constant functions on
Y_{i,j}
, and we set
P
to be the direct sum of
P_{i,j}
in the strong operator topology. By the assumption on the expansion of
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, it is clear that
P\in C^{*}_{u}(Y)
. It is explained in
[
51
, Section 3]
(see also
[
22
, Lemma 5.1]
) that
P
is
not
a ghost,
i.e.
,
P\notin\tilde{I}(X)
. However intuitively,
P
should vanish “in the
i
-direction”. We will make it more precisely in the following.

Recall from
[
22
, Section 5.1]
that we have a surjective map
\beta Y\to\beta X\times\beta\mathbb{N}
induced by the bijection of
Y
with
X\times\mathbb{N}
and the universal property of
\beta Y
. Define:

f:\beta Y\longrightarrow\beta X\times\beta\mathbb{N}\longrightarrow\beta X

where the second map is just the projection onto the first coordinate. Denote
U=f^{-1}(X)
, which is open in
\beta Y
. Note that
U=\bigsqcup_{i}f^{-1}(X_{i})
, where each
f^{-1}(X_{i})
is homeomorphic to
X_{i}\times\beta\mathbb{N}
. On the other hand, note that

U=\bigsqcup_{i}f^{-1}(X_{i})=\bigcup_{i}\overline{\bigsqcup_{j}Y_{i,j}}=\bigcup_{\varepsilon>0}\overline{\mathrm{r}(\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(P))}=\bigcup_{\varepsilon>0,R>0}\overline{\mathcal{N}_{R}(\mathrm{r}(\mathrm{supp}_{\varepsilon}(P)))}.

Hence it follows from Lemma
4.17
that
U
is invariant (comparing with
[
22
, Lemma 5.2]
), and
U(\langle P\rangle)=U
. (Note that
P\in\tilde{I}(U)
was already implicitly proved in
[
22
, Theorem 5.5]
, thanks to Proposition
5.9
.) Since
U
contains
Y
as a proper subset, we reprove that
P
is not a ghost.

Moreover, it follows from Proposition
5.6
that
P
vanishes in the
(\partial_{\beta}Y\setminus U)
-direction. In particular, fixing an index
j_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and taking a sequence
\{x_{i}\in Y_{i,j_{0}}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, we choose a cluster point
\omega\in\overline{\{x_{i}\}_{i}}
. It is clear that
\omega\notin U
. Intuitively speaking, this means that
P
vanishes “in the
i
-direction”.

We remark that the first-named author proved in
[
51
, Section 3]
that the principal ideal
\langle P\rangle
cannot be decomposed into
I(U)+(I_{G}\cap\langle P\rangle)
, which provided a couterexample to the conjecture at the end of
[
14
]
. Our explanation above reveals that the reason behind this counterexample is that the ghostly part of
\langle P\rangle
could not be “exhausted” merely by ghostly elements associated to
X
(rather than
U
).

Finally, we remark that the groupoid
G(Y)_{U}
also plays a key role in constructing a counterexample to the boundary coarse Baum-Connes conjecture introduced in
[
22
]
(see Section 5.2 therein).

6.
Maximal ideals

In this section, we would like to study maximal ideals in uniform Roe algebras using the tools developed in Section
5
. Throughout this section, let
(X,d)
be a space as in Section
2.2
.

6.1.
Minimal points in the boundary

Recall from previous sections that ideals are closely related to invariant open subsets of the unit space
\beta X
. Hence we introduce the following:

Definition 6.1
.

An invariant open subset
U\subseteq\beta X
is called
maximal
if
U\neq\beta X
and
U
is not properly contained in any proper invariant open subset of
\beta X
. Similarly, an invariant closed subset
K\subseteq\beta X
is called
minimal
if
K\neq\emptyset
and
K
does not properly contain any non-empty invariant closed subset of
\beta X
.

Proposition 6.2
.

For any maximal invariant open subset
U\subset\beta X
, the ghostly ideal
\tilde{I}(U)
is a maximal ideal in the uniform Roe algebra
C^{*}_{u}(X)
. Conversely for any maximal ideal
I
in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
, the associated invariant open subset
U(I)
is maximal and we have
I=\tilde{I}(U(I))
.

Proof.

For any ideal
J
in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
containing
\tilde{I}(U)
, we have
U(J)\supseteq U(\tilde{I}(U))=U
by Lemma
5.3
(2). Since
U
is maximal, then either
U(J)=\beta X
or
U(J)=U
. If
U(J)=\beta X
, it follows from Lemma
4.11
that
J
contains
I(U(J))=C^{*}_{u}(X)
, which implies that
J=C^{*}_{u}(X)
. If
U(J)=U
, then it follows from Lemma
5.3
(1) that
J\subseteq\tilde{I}(U(J))=\tilde{I}(U)
, which implies that
J=\tilde{I}(U)
. This concludes that
\tilde{I}(U)
is maximal.

Conversely for any maximal ideal
I
in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
, we have
U(I)\neq\beta X
. For any open invariant subset
V\neq\beta X
containing
U
, we have
I\subseteq\tilde{I}(U)\subseteq\tilde{I}(V)
by Theorem
5.4
, and
\tilde{I}(V)\neq C^{*}_{u}(X)
. Hence due to the maximality of
I
, we obtain that
I=\tilde{I}(U)=\tilde{I}(V)
. This implies that
U=V
and also
I=\tilde{I}(U)
as required.
∎

Taking complements, we obtain the following:

Corollary 6.3
.

For any minimal invariant closed subset
K\subseteq\beta X
, the ghostly ideal
\tilde{I}(\beta X\setminus K)
is a maximal ideal in the uniform Roe algebra
C^{*}_{u}(X)
. Moreover, every maximal ideal in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
arises in this form.

Therefore, in order to describe maximal ideals in the uniform Roe algebra, it suffices to study minimal invariant closed subsets of the unit space
\beta X
. Recall from Lemma
3.4
that for each
\omega\in\partial_{\beta}X
, the limit space
X(\omega)
is the smallest invariant subset of
\beta X
containing
\omega
. However, note that
X(\omega)
might not be closed in general.

Definition 6.4
.

A point
\omega\in\partial_{\beta}X
is called
minimal
if the closure of the limit space
X(\omega)
in
\beta X
is minimal in the sense of Definition
6.1
.

The following result is straightforward, hence we omit the proof. It suggests us to study minimal points in the boundary.

Lemma 6.5
.

For a minimal invariant closed subset
K\subseteq\beta X
, there exists a minimal point
\omega\in\partial_{\beta}X
such that
K=\overline{X(\omega)}
. Conversely for any minimal point
\omega\in\partial_{\beta}X
, the set
\overline{X(\omega)}
is minimal.

One might wonder whether every
\omega\in\partial_{\beta}X
is minimal. However, things become very complicated after taking closures and we will show later that this does not hold even in the case of
X=\mathbb{Z}
. Firstly, we notice the following:

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
K
be an invariant closed subset of
\beta X
. Then
K
contains a minimal point. In particular, for any
\omega\in\partial_{\beta}X
there exists a minimal point
\omega^{\prime}
such that
\omega^{\prime}\in\overline{X(\omega)}
.

Proof.

This follows directly from the Zorn’s lemma together with the fact that
\beta X
is compact. Details are left to readers.
∎

Consequently, we obtain:

Corollary 6.7
.

For
\omega\in\partial_{\beta}X
,
\omega
is minimal
if and only if
for any
\alpha\in\overline{X(\omega)}
, we have
\overline{X(\alpha)}=\overline{X(\omega)}
. Writing
\overline{X(\omega)}=\bigsqcup_{\lambda\in\Lambda}X(\omega_{\lambda})
for certain
\omega_{\lambda}\in\partial_{\beta}X
, then
\omega
is minimal
if and only if

\overline{X(\omega_{\lambda})}=\overline{X(\omega)}
for any
\lambda\in\Lambda
.

As promised, now we study the case of
X=\mathbb{Z}
and show that it admits a number of non-minimal points. The following is the main result:

Theorem 6.8
.

For the integer group
\mathbb{Z}
with the usual metric, there exist non-minimal points in the boundary
\partial_{\beta}\mathbb{Z}
. More precisely, for any sequence
\{h_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{Z}
tending to infinity such that
|h_{n}-h_{m}|\to+\infty
when
n+m\to\infty
and
n\neq m
, and any
\omega\in\partial_{\beta}\mathbb{Z}
with
\omega(\{h_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}})=1
, then
\omega
is
not
a minimal point.

To prove Theorem
6.8
, we need some preparations. For future use, we record the following result in the context of a countable discrete group
\Gamma
equipped with a left-invariant word length metric.

Lemma 6.9
.

For
\omega,\alpha\in\partial_{\beta}\Gamma
, then
\alpha\in\overline{\Gamma(\omega)}

if and only if
for any
S\subseteq\Gamma
with
\alpha(S)=1
, there exists
g_{S}\in\Gamma
such that
\omega(S\cdot g_{S}^{-1})=1
.

Proof.

Recall from Lemma
3.8
that
\Gamma(\omega)=\{\rho_{g}(\omega):g\in\Gamma\}
, and from Appendix
A
that
\{\overline{S}:S\subseteq X\}
forms a basis for
\beta X
. Hence by definition, we obtain that
\alpha\in\overline{X(\omega)}
if and only if for any
S\subseteq X
with
\alpha\in\overline{S}
, there exists
g_{S}\in\Gamma
such that
\rho_{g_{S}}(\omega)\in\overline{S}
. Equivalently, this means that for any
S\subseteq X
with
\alpha(S)=1
, there exists
g_{S}\in\Gamma
such that
\rho_{g_{S}}(\omega)(S)=\omega(S\cdot g_{S}^{-1})=1
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Now we return to the case of
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}
and prove Theorem
6.8
:

Proof of Theorem
6.8
.

Fix a subset
H=\{h_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
tending to infinity such that
|h_{n}-h_{m}|\to+\infty
when
n+m\to\infty
and
n\neq m
. For any non-zero
g\in\mathbb{Z}
, note that
h\in(g+H)\cap H
if and only if there exists
h^{\prime}\in H
such that
h-h^{\prime}=g
. Since
g
is fixed and distances between different points in
H
tend to infinity, we obtain that
(g+H)\cap H
is finite. Hence for any
g_{1}\neq g_{2}
in
\mathbb{Z}
,
(g_{1}+H)\cap(g_{2}+H)
is finite.

Fixing a non-principal ultrafilter
\omega\in\partial_{\beta}\mathbb{Z}
with
\omega(H)=1
, we denote

\mathcal{U}:=\{B\subseteq H:\omega(B)=1\}.

We claim that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
g_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
B_{n}\in\mathcal{U}
such that
\{B_{n}+g_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are mutually disjoint. Indeed, we take
g_{0}=0
and
B_{0}=H
. Set
g_{1}=1
and
B_{1}:=H\setminus(H-g_{1})
. Since
H\cap(H-g_{1})
is finite by the previous paragraph, then
\omega(B_{1})=1
,
i.e.
,
B_{1}\in\mathcal{U}
. Similarly for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we take
g_{n}=n
and
B_{n}:=H\setminus\big((H-g_{1})\cup(H-g_{2})\cup\cdots\cup(H-g_{n})\big)
, which concludes the claim.

Consider
\widetilde{H}:=\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(B_{n}+g_{n})
, and denote
\mathcal{U}_{n}:=\{B\subseteq B_{n}:\omega(B)=1\}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. By Lemma
A.4
,
\mathcal{U}_{n}
is an ultrafilter on
B_{n}
. Choose a non-principal ultrafilter
\omega_{0}
on
\mathbb{N}
, and we consider:

\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}:=\big\{\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(A_{n}+g_{n})\subseteq\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(B_{n}+g_{n})=\widetilde{H}:\exists~J\subseteq\mathbb{N}\text{ with }\omega_{0}(J)=1\text{ s.t. }\forall n\in J,A_{n}\in\mathcal{U}_{n}\big\}.

Lemma
A.6
implies that
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}
is an ultrafilter on
\widetilde{H}
. We define a function
\alpha:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z})\to\{0,1\}
by setting
\alpha(S)=1
if and only if
S\cap\widetilde{H}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}
, which is indeed an ultrafilter on
\mathbb{Z}
thanks to Lemma
A.5
. Also note that
\alpha
is non-principal and
\alpha(\widetilde{H})=1
.

Now we show that
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Z}(\omega)}
while
\omega\notin\overline{\mathbb{Z}(\alpha)}
, and hence conclude the proof. To see that
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Z}(\omega)}
, we will consult Lemma
6.9
. For any
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
with
\alpha(S)=1
, by definition we have
S\cap\widetilde{H}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}
. Writing
S\cap\widetilde{H}=\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(A_{n}+g_{n})
with
A_{n}\subseteq B_{n}
, then there exists
J\in\omega_{0}
such that for any
n\in J
we have
A_{n}\in\mathcal{U}_{n}
. Hence for any
n\in J
, we have
S\supseteq S\cap\widetilde{H}\supseteq A_{n}+g_{n}
and
\omega(A_{n})=1
, which implies that
\omega(S-g_{n})=1
. Applying Lemma
6.9
, we conclude that
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Z}(\omega)}
.

Finally, it remains to check that
\omega\notin\overline{\mathbb{Z}(\alpha)}
. Assume the contrary, then Lemma
6.9
implies that there exists
g\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\widetilde{B}\subseteq\widetilde{H}
with
\alpha(\widetilde{B})=1
such that
H\supseteq\widetilde{B}-g
. Writing
\widetilde{B}=\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(A_{n}+g_{n})
with
A_{n}\subseteq B_{n}
, then there exists
J\in\omega_{0}
such that for any
n\in J
,
\omega(A_{n})=1
. This implies that

H+g\supseteq\widetilde{B}\supseteq\bigsqcup_{n\in J}(A_{n}+g_{n})\supseteq A_{n_{0}}+g_{n_{0}}

for some
n_{0}\in J
with
g_{n_{0}}\neq g
(this can be achieved since
J
is infinite). However, it follows from the first paragraph that
(H+g)\cap(H+g_{n_{0}})
is finite. While this intersection contains
A_{n_{0}}+g_{n_{0}}
, which is infinite since
\omega(A_{n_{0}})=1
. Therefore, we reach a contradiction and conclude the proof.
∎

6.2.
Maximal ideals via limit operators

We already see that maximal ideals in the uniform Roe algebra are related to minimal points in the Stone-Čech boundary of the underlying space and in Section
6.1
, we use topological methods to show the existence of non-minimal points. Now we turn to a
C^{*}
-algebraic viewpoint, and use the tool of limit operators to provide an alternative description for these ideals.

First recall from Corollary
6.3
and Lemma
6.5
that maximal ideals in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
arise in the form of
\tilde{I}(\beta X\setminus\overline{X(\omega)})
for some boundary point
\omega\in\partial_{\beta}X
. Moreover according to Proposition
5.9
,
\tilde{I}(\beta X\setminus\overline{X(\omega)})
is the kernel of the following surjective homomorphism:

q_{\beta X\setminus\overline{X(\omega)}}:C^{*}_{r}(G(X))\longrightarrow C^{*}_{r}(G(X)_{\overline{X(\omega)}}).

Hence we obtain the following:

Corollary 6.10
.

A point
\omega\in\partial_{\beta}X
is minimal
if and only if

C^{*}_{r}(G(X)_{\overline{X(\omega)}})
is simple.

Example 6.11
.

Consider the case of a countable discrete group
\Gamma
. For a point
\omega\in\partial_{\beta}\Gamma
, it follows from Lemma
3.8
that the limit space
\Gamma(\omega)
is identical to
\Gamma\omega
, and hence
C^{*}_{r}(G(\Gamma)_{\overline{\Gamma(\omega)}})
is
C^{*}
-isomorphic to the reduced crossed product
C(\overline{\Gamma\omega})\rtimes\Gamma
. Thanks to Corollary
6.10
, we obtain that
\omega
is minimal if and only if
C(\overline{\Gamma\omega})\rtimes\Gamma
is simple. Moreover, note that the action of
\Gamma
on
\beta\Gamma
is free (which is also a consequence of Lemma
3.8
). Hence it follows from
[
35
, Corollary 4.6]
that
C(\overline{\Gamma\omega})\rtimes\Gamma
is simple if and only if the action of
\Gamma
on
\overline{\Gamma\omega}
is minimal. In conclusion, we reach the following:

Corollary 6.12
.

In the case of a countable discrete group
\Gamma
, a point
\omega\in\partial_{\beta}\Gamma
is minimal
if and only if
the action of
\Gamma
on
\overline{\Gamma\omega}
is minimal.

Corollary
6.10
suggests an approach to distinguish minimal points via the simplicity of the reduced groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra. However, this
C^{*}
-algebra is still not easy to handle since it requires to consider all points in the
\overline{X(\omega)}
-direction (see Proposition
5.6
).
Now we show that this can be simplified by merely considering the
\omega
-direction:

Lemma 6.13
.

For
\omega\in\partial_{\beta}X
, an operator
T\in C^{*}_{u}(X)
belongs to the ideal
\tilde{I}(\beta X\setminus\overline{X(\omega)})

if and only if

T
vanishes in the
\omega
-direction,
i.e.
,
\Phi_{\omega}(T)=0
.

Proof.

We assume that
\Phi_{\omega}(T)=0
, and it suffices to show that
\Phi_{\alpha}(T)=0
for any
\alpha\in\overline{X(\omega)}
. Fixing such an
\alpha
, we take a net
\{\omega_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
in
X(\omega)
such that
\omega_{\lambda}\to\alpha
and it follows that
\Phi_{\omega_{\lambda}}(T)=0
. For any
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in X(\alpha)
, Lemma
3.4
and the fact that the coarse groupoid
G(X)
is étale imply that there exist
\gamma_{1,\lambda}
and
\gamma_{2,\lambda}
in
X(\omega_{\lambda})
for each
\lambda\in\Lambda
such that
\gamma_{1,\lambda}\to\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2,\lambda}\to\gamma_{2}
. Now Lemma
3.12
implies that

\Phi_{\alpha}(T)_{\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}}=\overline{T}((\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}))=\lim_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\overline{T}((\gamma_{1,\lambda},\gamma_{2,\lambda}))=\lim_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\Phi_{\omega_{\lambda}}(T)_{\gamma_{1,\lambda}\gamma_{2,\lambda}}=0,

which concludes the proof.
∎

Hence for
\omega\in\partial_{\beta}X
, Lemma
6.13
implies that the associated ideal
\tilde{I}(\beta\setminus\overline{X(\omega)})
coincides with the kernel of the following limit operator homomorphism (see also
[
50
, Theorem 4.10]
):

(6.1)

\Phi_{\omega}:C^{*}_{u}(X)\longrightarrow C^{*}_{u}(X(\omega)),\quad T\mapsto\Phi_{\omega}(T).

Consequently, we reach the following:

Corollary 6.14
.

A point
\omega\in\partial_{\beta}X
is minimal
if and only if
the image
\mathrm{Im}(\Phi_{\omega})
is simple. Hence when
X(\omega)
is infinite and
\Phi_{\omega}
is surjective, the point
\omega
is
not
minimal.

Proof.

For the last statement, it suffices to note that the ideal of compact operators is always contained in
C^{*}_{u}(X(\omega))
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 6.15
.

We remark that ideals of the form
\tilde{I}(\beta\setminus\overline{X(\omega)})
already appeared in the work of Georgescu
[
23
]
(with the notation
\mathscr{G}_{\omega}(X)
) to study the limit operator theory on general metric spaces.

Thanks to Corollary
6.14
, a special case of Theorem
6.8
can also be deduced from a recent work by Roch
[

]
. More precisely, combining
[
50
, Proposition B.6]
with
[

, Lemma 2.1]
, we have the following:

Proposition 6.16
.

Let
\{h_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in

tending to infinity such that

\|h_{n}-h_{k}\|_{\infty}\geq 3k\quad\text{for any}\quad k>n.

Then for any
\omega\in\partial_{\beta}\mathbb{Z}^{N}
with
\omega(\{h_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}})=1
, the map
\Phi_{\omega}:C^{*}_{u}(\mathbb{Z}^{N})\longrightarrow C^{*}_{u}(\mathbb{Z}^{N}(\omega))
is surjective.

Note that the limit space
\mathbb{Z}^{N}(\omega)
is bijective to
\mathbb{Z}^{N}
by Lemma

, and hence infinite. Therefore applying Corollary
6.14
, we obtain the following (when
N=1

6.8
).

.

For any sequence

in

, and any

is
not

Remark 6.18
.

We notice from the discussion above that for
\omega\in\partial_{\beta}X
, there is a
C^{*}
-monomorphism:

(6.2)

C^{*}_{r}(G(X)_{\overline{X(\omega)}})\cong C^{*}_{u}(X)/\tilde{I}(\beta X\setminus\overline{X(\omega)})\longrightarrow C^{*}_{u}(X(\omega))

where the first comes from Proposition
5.9
and the second comes from (
6.1
) together with Lemma
6.13
.

Now we provide another explanation for this map in terms of groupoids. Firstly, Corollary
3.5
implies that
G(X)_{X(\omega)}=X(\omega)\times X(\omega)
and hence
G(X)_{\overline{X(\omega)}}=\overline{X(\omega)\times X(\omega)}^{G(X)}
. Now we have

G(X)_{\overline{X(\omega)}}=\bigcup_{S>0}\big(\overline{E_{S}}^{G(X)}\cap\overline{X(\omega)\times X(\omega)}^{G(X)}\big)=\bigcup_{S>0}\overline{\overline{E_{S}}^{G(X)}\cap(X(\omega)\times X(\omega))}^{G(X)},

where the last inequality is due to the fact that
\overline{E_{S}}^{G(X)}
is clopen in
G(X)
. By Lemma
3.7
, we have

\overline{E_{S}}^{G(X)}\cap(X(\omega)\times X(\omega))=\{(\alpha,\gamma)\in X(\omega)\times X(\omega):d_{\omega}(\alpha,\gamma)\leq S\}=:E_{S}(X(\omega),d_{\omega})

i.e.
, the
S
-entourage in the limit space
(X(\omega),d_{\omega})
. Therefore, we conclude that

G(X)_{\overline{X(\omega)}}=\bigcup_{S>0}\overline{E_{S}(X(\omega),d_{\omega})}^{G(X)}.

On the other hand, we have (by definition) that

G(X(\omega))=\bigcup_{S>0}\overline{E_{S}(X(\omega),d_{\omega})}^{\beta(X(\omega)\times X(\omega))}.

By the universal property of the Stone-Čech compactification, there is a surjective continuous map
\overline{E_{S}(X(\omega),d_{\omega})}^{\beta(X(\omega)\times X(\omega))}\longrightarrow\overline{E_{S}(X(\omega),d_{\omega})}^{G(X)}
, which induces an injective map
C_{c}(G(X)_{\overline{X(\omega)}})\longrightarrow C_{c}(G(X(\omega)))
. Moreover, it is routine to check that it induces a
C^{*}
-monomorphism

C^{*}_{r}(G(X)_{\overline{X(\omega)}})\longrightarrow C^{*}_{r}(G(X(\omega)))\cong C^{*}_{u}(X(\omega)),

which can be verified to coincide with the map (
6.2
). Details are left to readers.

In particular, we consider a countable discrete group
X=\Gamma
. Fixing a point
\omega\in\partial_{\beta}\Gamma
, we mentioned in Example
6.11
that
C^{*}_{r}(G(\Gamma)_{\overline{\Gamma(\omega)}})\cong C(\overline{\Gamma\omega})\rtimes\Gamma
. On the other hand, we know from Lemma
3.8
that
C^{*}_{u}(\Gamma(\omega))\cong\ell^{\infty}(\Gamma\omega)\rtimes\Gamma
. In this case, one can check that the map (
6.2
) is induced by the natural embedding
C(\overline{\Gamma\omega})\hookrightarrow\ell^{\infty}(\Gamma\omega)
.

7.
Geometric ideals vs ghostly ideals

In this section, we return to the lattice
\mathfrak{I}_{U}
in (
1.1
). Recall from Theorem
5.4
that for an invariant open subset
U\subseteq\beta X
, the geometric ideal
I(U)
and the ghostly ideal
\tilde{I}(U)
are the smallest and the largest elements in
\mathfrak{I}_{U}
. Also as noticed before, generally
\mathfrak{I}_{U}
consists of more than one element.

Now we would like to study when
I(U)=\tilde{I}(U)
or equivalently, when
\mathfrak{I}_{U}
consists of a single element. Moreover, we will discuss their
K
-theories and provide a sufficient condition to ensure
K_{\ast}(I(U))=K_{\ast}(\tilde{I}(U))
for
\ast=0,1
.

First recall from Proposition
5.9
that we already have a characterisation for
I(U)=\tilde{I}(U)
using short exact sequences, while the condition therein still seems hard to check. Now we aim to search for a more practical criterion to ensure
I(U)=\tilde{I}(U)
.
We start with the following result, which combines
[
16
, Theorem 4.4]
and
[
42
, Theorem 1.3]
.

Proposition 7.1
.

For a space
(X,d)
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

X
has Property A;

(2)

G(X)
is amenable;

(3)

G(X)_{\partial_{\beta}X}
is amenable;

(4)

I(U)=\tilde{I}(U)
for any invariant open subset

;

(5)

I(X)=\tilde{I}(X)
.

\Rightarrow
(4)” was originally proved in
[

]
using approximations by kernels. Here we will take a shortcut, and the idea will also be used later.

.

is an isomorphism for

On the other hand, we claim that

is an isomorphism for

. To see, recall from

that there is a locally compact, second countable, ample and étale groupoid

such that

, which implies that

coincides with the maximal crossed product
C_{0}(U)\rtimes_{{\rm max}}\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
, and
C^{*}_{r}(G(X)_{U})
with the reduced one
C_{0}(U)\rtimes_{r}\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
. Note that the
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
-
C^{*}
-algebra

can be written as an inductive limit

of separable

-

-algebras. Moreover, it follows from

that

is a-T-menable since

is a-T-menable, and hence Proposition

is also

that the canonical map

For the last statement, we assume that

. By Lemma

. Also Lemma

can be decomposed as follows:

Therefore,

is injective for

∎

8.
Partial Property A and partial operator norm localisation property

In the previous section, we find a sufficient condition (Proposition
7.2
) to ensure
\tilde{I}(U)=I(U)
for a given invariant open subset
U\subseteq\beta X
. As promised in Remark
7.3
, now we study its converse and show that this is indeed an equivalent condition under an extra assumption.

Our strategy is to follow the outline of the case that
U=X
. More precisely, we introduce a notion called
partial Property A towards invariant subsets of the boundary
\partial_{\beta}X
, and then consider its counterpart in the context of operator norm localisation property to provide the desired characterisation.

8.1.
Partial Property A

Recall from Proposition
7.1
that a space
X
has Property A if and only if the groupoid
G(X)_{\partial_{\beta}X}
is amenable, which characterises
I(X)=\tilde{I}(X)
. Together with Proposition
7.2
, this inspires us to introduce the following:

Definition 8.1
.

Let
(X,d)
be a space and
U\subseteq\beta X
be an invariant open subset. We say that
X
has
partial Property A towards
\partial_{\beta}X\setminus U
if
G(X)_{\partial_{\beta}X\setminus U}
is amenable.

It is clear from definition that
X
has Property A if and only if it has partial Property A towards the whole boundary
\partial_{\beta}X
. On the other hand, it follows from Proposition
7.2
that if
X
has partial Property A towards
\partial_{\beta}X\setminus U
, then we have
I(U)=\tilde{I}(U)
. The rest of this section is devoted to studying the converse.

Firstly, we aim to unpack the groupoid language and provide a concrete geometric description for partial Property A, which resembles the definition of Property A (see Definition
2.1
). The following is the main result:

Proposition 8.2
.

Let
(X,d)
be a space and
U\subseteq\beta X
an invariant open subset. Then
X
has partial Property A towards
U^{c}=\partial_{\beta}X\setminus U

if and only if
for any
\varepsilon,R>0
, there exist
S>0
, a subset
D\subseteq X
with
\overline{D}\supseteq U^{c}
and a function
f:X\times X\to[0,1]
satisfying:

(1)

\mathrm{supp}(f)\subseteq E_{S}
;

(2)

for any
x\in D
, we have
\sum_{z\in X}f(z,x)=1
;

(3)

for any
x,y\in D
with
d(x,y)\leq R
, we have
\sum_{z\in X}|f(z,x)-f(z,y)|\leq\varepsilon
.

Comparing Proposition
8.2
with Definition
2.1
, it is clear that Property A implies partial Property A towards any invariant closed subset of
\partial_{\beta}X
.

To prove Proposition
8.2
, we start with the following lemma:

Lemma 8.3

With the same notation as above,
X
has partial Property A towards
U^{c}

Let

such that

The aim of the rest of this subsection is to show that partial ONL is equivalent to partial Property A. We will follow the outline of

To simplify the statement, denote

(resp.

. Hence we will not tell the difference in the sequel. For

B^{Y}_{S}:=\prod_{x\in X}\mathfrak{B}(\ell^{2}(B(x,S)\cap Y)),

whose elements will be written as

8.3.
Countable generatedness

Here we introduce the extra assumption we need to characterise
\tilde{I}(U)=I(U)
later.

Definition 8.16
.

For a set
\mathcal{S}
of subsets of
X
, denote
{\bf L}(\mathcal{S})
the smallest ideal in
X
containing
\mathcal{S}
, and we say that
{\bf L}(\mathcal{S})
is
generated by
\mathcal{S}
. An ideal
{\bf L}
in
X
is called
countably generated
if there exists a countable set
\mathcal{S}
such that
{\bf L}={\bf L}(\mathcal{S})
.

An invariant open subset
U\subseteq\beta X
is called
countably generated
if the associated ideal
{\bf L}(U)
is countably generated.

Example 8.17
.

For a space
X
and a subspace
A\subseteq X
, it follows from Lemma
4.14
that the spatial ideal
I_{A}
is countably generated. On the other hand, it follows from Lemma
4.17
that principal ideals are always countably generated. In particular, the ideal
\langle P\rangle
considered in
[
51
, Section 3]
(see also Example
5.10
) is countably generated.

The property of countable generatedness leads to the following:

Lemma 8.18
.

Let
{\bf L}
be a countably generated ideal in
X
. Then there exists a countable subset
\{Y_{1},Y_{2},\cdots,Y_{n},\cdots\}
in
{\bf L}
such that

{\bf L}=\{Z\subseteq X:~\exists~n\in\mathbb{N}\text{ such that }Z\subseteq Y_{n}\}.

To prove Lemma
8.18
, we need an auxiliary result on the structure of
{\bf L}(\mathcal{S})
:

Lemma 8.19
.

For a set
\mathcal{S}
of subsets of
X
, denote
\mathcal{S}^{(1)}:=\{A_{1}\cup\cdots\cup A_{n}:A_{i}\in\mathcal{S},n\in\mathbb{N}\}
and
\mathcal{S}^{(2)}:=\{\mathcal{N}_{k}(\tilde{A}):\tilde{A}\in\mathcal{S}^{(1)},k\in\mathbb{N}\}
. Then we have:

{\bf L}(\mathcal{S})=\{Z:\exists~Y\in\mathcal{S}^{(2)}\text{ such that }Z\subseteq Y\}.

Proof.

Denote
\mathcal{S}^{(3)}:=\{Z:\exists~Y\in\mathcal{S}^{(2)}\text{ such that }Z\subseteq Y\}
. It is clear that
\mathcal{S}^{(3)}\subseteq{\bf L}(\mathcal{S})
, and any ideal containing
\mathcal{S}
must contain
\mathcal{S}^{(3)}
. Hence it remains to check that
\mathcal{S}^{(3)}
is an ideal.

For
Z\in\mathcal{S}^{(3)}
and
W\subseteq Z
, there exists
Y\in\mathcal{S}^{(2)}
such that
Z\subseteq Y
. Hence
W\subseteq Y
, which implies that
W\in\mathcal{S}^{(3)}
. Also note that
Y\in\mathcal{S}^{(2)}
implies that there exists
\tilde{A}\in\mathcal{S}^{(1)}
and
k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
Y=\mathcal{N}_{k^{\prime}}(\tilde{A})
. Hence for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\mathcal{N}_{k}(Z)\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{k}(Y)\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{k+k^{\prime}}(\tilde{A})
, which implies that
\mathcal{N}_{k}(Z)\in\mathcal{S}^{(3)}
.

Finally for
Z_{1},Z_{2}\in\mathcal{S}^{(3)}
, there exist
\tilde{A}_{1},\tilde{A}_{2}\in\mathcal{S}^{(1)}
and
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
Z_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{k_{i}}(\tilde{A}_{i})
for
i=1,2
. Hence

Z_{1}\cup Z_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{k_{1}}(\tilde{A}_{1})\cup\mathcal{N}_{k_{2}}(\tilde{A}_{2})\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{k_{1}+k_{2}}(\tilde{A}_{1}\cup\tilde{A}_{2})\in\mathcal{S}^{(2)},

which implies that
Z_{1}\cup Z_{2}\in\mathcal{S}^{(3)}
. Therefore, we conclude the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma
8.18
.

By assumption, there exists a countable
\mathcal{S}
such that
{\bf L}={\bf L}(\mathcal{S})
. Using the notation of Lemma
8.19
, the set
\mathcal{S}^{(2)}
is countable as well. Hence the first statement follows directly from Lemma
8.19
.
∎

The following example shows that
not every
ideal in a space is countably generated.

Example 8.20
.

Let
X=\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
, equipped with the metric induced from the Euclidean metric
d_{E}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For each
\theta\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we define

\ell_{\theta}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}:y=\tan(\theta)x\}

and

S_{\theta,k}:=\{(x,y)\in X:d_{E}((x,y),\ell_{\theta})\leq k\}=\mathcal{N}_{k}(\ell_{\theta})\cap X.

Consider
\mathcal{S}:=\{S_{\theta,k}:\theta\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2}],k\in\mathbb{N}\}
and set
\mathcal{S}^{(1)}:=\{A_{1}\cup\cdots\cup A_{n}:A_{i}\in\mathcal{S},n\in\mathbb{N}\}
as in Lemma
8.19
. For any
R>0
and
A=A_{1}\cup\cdots\cup A_{n}\in\mathcal{S}^{(1)}
where
A_{i}\in\mathcal{S}
, we have
\mathcal{N}_{R}(A)=\mathcal{N}_{R}(A_{1})\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{N}_{R}(A_{n})
, which is contained in some element in
\mathcal{S}
. Hence applying Lemma
8.19
, the ideal
{\bf L}(\mathcal{S})
generated by
\mathcal{S}
is:

(8.4)

{\bf L}(\mathcal{S})=\{Z:\exists~Y\in\mathcal{S}^{(1)}\text{ such that }Z\subseteq Y\}.

We claim that
{\bf L}(\mathcal{S})
is
not
countably generated. Otherwise, there exists a countable subset
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
generating
{\bf L}(\mathcal{S})
. Moreover, according to (
8.4
) we can assume that

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\{Y_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\quad\text{where}\quad Y_{n}=S_{\theta_{n,1},k_{n}}\cup\cdots\cup S_{\theta_{n,p_{n}},k_{n}}\in\mathcal{S}^{(1)}.

Choose
\theta\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]\setminus\{\theta_{n,i}:i=1,2,\cdots,p_{n};n\in\mathbb{N}\}
, and consider
Y=S_{\theta,1}
. Since
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
generates
{\bf L}(\mathcal{S})
, it follows from Lemma

that there exist
R>0
and
Y_{m_{1}},\cdots,Y_{m_{l}}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
such that

S_{\theta,1}=Y\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{R}(Y_{m_{1}}\cup\cdots\cup Y_{m_{l}}).

Note that the right hand side is contained in a finite union of some
R^{\prime}
-neighbourhood of lines (in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
crossing the origin) with slopes in the set

\big\{\tan(\theta_{n,i}):i=1,2,\cdots,p_{n};n\in\mathbb{N}\big\}.

This leads to a contradiction due to the choice of

, which concludes that
{\bf L}(\mathcal{S})
cannot be countably generated.

Having established all the necessary ingredients above, now we present the main result of this section:

Theorem 8.21

Let
(X,d)
be a space as in Section
2.2
and
U\subseteq\beta X
be a countably generated invariant open subset.
Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

has partial Property A towards
\beta X\setminus U
;

(2)

\tilde{I}(U)=I(U)
;

(3)

the ideal

of all ghost operators is contained in

.

Note that
U=X
is countably generated, and hence Theorem
8.21
recovers
[

(see Example

and Example

).
Borrowing the language of
[

, condition (3) in Theorem
8.21
says that all the ghosts can be busted in the geometric ideal

.

We follow the outline of the proof for
[
42
, Theorem 1.3]
. Firstly, we need a modified version of
[
42
, Lemma 4.2]
:

Lemma 8.22
.

Let
(X,d)
be a space,
U\subseteq\beta X
be a countably generated invariant open subset and
{\bf L}={\bf L}(U)
the associated ideal in
X
. Assume that
X
does not have partial ONL towards
U^{c}
. Then there exist
\kappa\in(0,1)
,
R>0
, a sequence
(T_{n})
in
\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]
, a sequence
(B_{n})
of finite subsets of
X
and a sequence
(S_{n})
of positive real numbers such that:

(a)

(S_{n})
is an increasing sequence tending to infinity as
n\to\infty
;

(b)

each
T_{n}
is positive and has norm
1
;

(c)

for
n\neq m
, then
B_{n}\cap B_{m}=\emptyset
;

(d)

each
T_{n}
is supported in
B_{n}\times B_{n}
;

(e)

for each
n
and
\xi\in\ell^{2}(X)
with
\|\xi\|=1
and
\mathrm{diam}(\mathrm{supp}\xi)\leq S_{n}
, then
\|T_{n}\xi\|\leq\kappa
;

(f)

for each
Y\in{\bf L}(U)
, there exists
n
such that
B_{n}\cap Y=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Fixing a basepoint
x_{0}\in X
, consider the decomposition
X=X^{(1)}\cup X^{(2)}
with

X^{(1)}:=\bigsqcup_{m\text{ even}}\{x\in X:m^{2}\leq d(x,x_{0})<(m+1)^{2}\}

and

X^{(2)}:=\bigsqcup_{m\text{ odd}}\{x\in X:m^{2}\leq d(x,x_{0})<(m+1)^{2}\}.

Set
{\bf L}_{i}:=\{Y\cap X^{(i)}:Y\in{\bf L}\}
for
i=1,2
. By assumption,
X
does not have partial Property A towards
U^{c}
. Hence without loss of generality, we can assume that
X^{(1)}
does not have partial Property A towards
\beta X^{(1)}\setminus U({\bf L}_{1})
thanks to Proposition
8.13
. Note that this implies that
U({\bf L}_{1})\neq\beta X^{(1)}
.
It is clear that
{\bf L}_{1}
is also countably generated, and hence according to Lemma
8.18
there exists a countable subset
\{Y_{1},Y_{2},\cdots,Y_{n},\cdots\}
of
{\bf L}_{1}
such that

{\bf L}_{1}=\{Z\subseteq X^{(1)}:~\exists~n\in\mathbb{N}\text{ such that }Z\subseteq Y_{n}\}.

In the sequel, we fix such a sequence
\{Y_{1},Y_{2},\cdots,Y_{n},\cdots\}
.

Due to Lemma
8.11
, we know that there exist
c\in(0,1)
and
R>0
such that for any
Y\in{\bf L}_{1}
and
S>0
, there exists
T\in\mathbb{C}^{R}_{u}[X^{(1)}\setminus Y]
with
\|T\|=1
satisfying: for any
\xi\in\ell^{2}(X^{(1)})
with
\mathrm{diam}(\mathrm{supp}\xi)\leq S
and
\|\xi\|=1
, then
\|T\xi\|<c
. We call such an operator
(R,c,S,Y)
-localised
. Replacing
T
by
T^{*}T
(and
R
by
2R
and
c
by
\sqrt{c}
), we see that there exist
c\in(0,1)
and
R>0
such that for any
Y\in{\bf L}_{1}
and
S>0
, there exists a positive
(R,c,S,Y)
-localised operator of norm one. Let us fix such
c
and
R
in the rest of the proof, and set
\kappa:=\frac{2c}{1+c}<1
.

Note that
X^{(1)}
can be decomposed into:

X^{(1)}:=\bigsqcup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}X_{m}

where each
X_{m}
is finite and
d(X_{m},X_{n})>R
for any

. Hence each
T\in\mathbb{C}^{R}_{u}[X^{(1)}]
splits as a block diagonal sum of finite rank operators
T=\bigoplus_{m}T^{(m)}
where
T^{(m)}\in\mathfrak{B}(\ell^{2}(X_{m}))
, with respect to this decomposition.

Take
S_{1}=1
. By assumption, there exists a positive
(R,c,S_{1},Y_{1})
-localised operator
T\in\mathbb{C}^{R}_{u}[X^{(1)}\setminus Y_{1}]
with norm
1
. Note that
\|T\|=\sup_{m}\|T^{(m)}\|
, and then there exists
m_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\|T^{(m_{1})}\|>\frac{1+c}{2}
. We set
T_{1}:=T^{(m_{1})}/\|T^{(m_{1})}\|
and denote
B_{1}:=X_{m_{1}}\cap(X^{(1)}\setminus Y_{1})
, which is nonempty since
T
has support in
B_{1}\times B_{1}
by assumption. Then for any
\xi\in\ell^{2}(X^{(1)})
with
\|\xi\|=1
and
\mathrm{diam}(\mathrm{supp}(\xi))\leq S_{1}
, we have

\|T_{1}\xi\|\leq\frac{2c}{1+c}=\kappa<1.

Now we take
S_{2}>\mathrm{max}\big\{\mathrm{diam}\big(\bigsqcup_{k\leq m_{1}}X_{k}\big),2\big\}
. By assumption, there exists a positive
(R,c,S_{2},Y_{2})
-localised operator
T\in\mathbb{C}^{R}_{u}[X^{(1)}\setminus Y_{2}]
with norm

. Again there exists
m_{2}
such that

, which forces

. We set
T_{2}:=T^{(m_{2})}/\|T^{(m_{2})}\|
and denote
B_{2}:=X_{m_{2}}\cap(X^{(1)}\setminus Y_{2})
, which is nonempty since
T
has support in
B_{2}\times B_{2}
by assumption. Similarly for any

with
\|\xi\|=1
and

.

Inductively, we can construct a sequence
(T_{n})
in
\mathbb{C}_{u}[X^{(1)}]\subseteq\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]
, a sequence
(B_{n})
of finite subsets of

and a sequence

of positive real numbers satisfying condition (a)-(e) in the statement. Furthermore for each

which provides condition (f) and concludes the proof.

I_{G}=\tilde{I}(X)\subseteq\tilde{I}(U)
. Hence it suffices to show “(3)
\Rightarrow
(1)”, and we follow the outline of the proof for
[
42
, Theorem 1.3]
.

Assume that
X
does not have partial Property A towards
U^{c}
, then it follows from Proposition
8.12
that
X
does not have partial ONL towards
U^{c}
. Then from Lemma
8.22
, there exist
\kappa\in(0,1)
,
R>0
, a sequence
(T_{n})
in
\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]
, a sequence
(B_{n})
of finite subsets of
X
and a sequence
(S_{n})
of positive real numbers satisfying condition (a)-(f) therein. Now we consider the operator

T:=\bigoplus_{n}T_{n},

which is a positive operator in
\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]
of norm one.

Now we take a continuous function
f:[0,1]\to[0,1]
such that
\mathrm{supp}f\subseteq[\frac{1+\kappa}{2},1]
and
f(1)=1
. Consider the operator
f(T)\in C^{*}_{u}(X)
, which is positive, norm one, and admits a decomposition

f(T)=\bigoplus_{n}f(T_{n}),

where each
f(T_{n})\in\mathfrak{B}(\ell^{2}(B_{n}))
. We will show that
f(T)\in\tilde{I}(X)\setminus I(U)
, and hence conclude a contradiction.

First we show that
f(T)\notin I(U)
. Recall from (
4.2
) that

I(U)=\overline{\{T^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]:\mathrm{supp}(T^{\prime})\subseteq Y\times Y\mbox{~for~some~}Y\in{\bf L}(U)\}},

Now for any
T^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]
with
\mathrm{supp}(T^{\prime})\subseteq Y\times Y
for some
Y\in{\bf L}(U)
, condition (f) in Lemma
8.22
implies that there exists
n
such that
B_{n}\cap Y=\emptyset
. Hence we have:

\|f(T)-T^{\prime}\|\geq\|\chi_{B_{n}}f(T)\chi_{B_{n}}-\chi_{B_{n}}T^{\prime}\chi_{B_{n}}\|=\|f(T_{n})-0\|=1,

which implies that
f(T)\notin I(U)
.

On the other hand, using the same argument as for
[
42
, Theorem 1.3]
(since Lemma
8.22
provides all the conditions required in
[
42
, Lemma 4.2]
), we obtain that
f(T)
is a ghost operator. Hence according to Example
5.5
, we have
f(T)\in\tilde{I}(X)
. Therefore, we conclude the proof.
∎

9.
Open questions

Here we collect several open questions around this topic.

First recall from Theorem
5.4
that for a space
(X,d)
, any ideal
I
in the uniform Roe algebra
C^{*}_{u}(X)
must lie between
I(U)
and
\tilde{I}(U)
for
U=U(I)
. However, the structure of the lattice

\mathfrak{I}_{U}=\{I\mbox{~is~an~ideal~in~}C^{*}_{u}(X):U(I)=U\}

in (
1.1
) is still unclear. Note that for any invariant open subset
V\supseteq U
of
\beta X
, the ideal
I(V)\cap\tilde{I}(U)
belongs to the lattice
\mathfrak{I}_{U}
. Unfortunately, we do not know whether these ideals can bust every element in
\mathfrak{I}_{U}
. Hence we pose the following:

Question 9.1
.

Let
(X,d)
be a space and
U\subseteq\beta X
be an invariant open subset. Can we describe elements in the lattice
\mathfrak{I}_{U}=\{I\mbox{~is~an~ideal~in~}C^{*}_{u}(X):U(I)=U\}
in details? For
I\in\mathfrak{I}_{U}
, can we find an invariant open subset
V\supseteq U
such that
I=I(V)\cap\tilde{I}(U)
?

Note that an answer to the above question together with Theorem
5.4
will provide a full description for the ideal structure of the uniform Roe algebra.

Our next question concerns minimal points discussed in Section
6.1
. Recall that minimal points in the Stone-Čech boundary correspond to maximal ideals in the uniform Roe algebra. However as shown in Theorem
6.8
, there exist a number of non-minimal points in the boundary. Hence it would be interesting to explore a practical approach to distinguish minimal points.

Question 9.2
.

Given a space
(X,d)
, can we find a practical approach to distinguish minimal points in the Stone-Čech boundary
\partial_{\beta}X
?

Note from Theorem
6.8
that the answer might not be easy even in the elementary case that
X=\mathbb{Z}
.

The next question concerns the discussion on
K
-theory in Section
7
. Recall that in the second part of Proposition
7.4
, we prove that for an ideal
I
in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
,
(\iota_{I})_{\ast}:K_{\ast}(\overline{I\cap\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]})\longrightarrow K_{\ast}(I)
is injective when
X
is coarsely embeddable. Hence we pose the following:

Question 9.3
.

For an ideal
I
in
C^{*}_{u}(X)
with
X
coarsely embeddable, is the map
(\iota_{I})_{\ast}:K_{\ast}(\overline{I\cap\mathbb{C}_{u}[X]})\longrightarrow K_{\ast}(I)
surjective for
\ast=0,1
?

Our final question is designed for Theorem
8.21
. Recall that the assumption of countably generatedness plays an important role in Lemma
8.22
, which is in turn crucial in the proof of Theorem
8.21
. We wonder whether this assumption is necessary, and ask the following:

Question 9.4
.

Let
(X,d)
be a space and
U\subseteq\beta X
be an invariant open subset. If
\tilde{I}(U)=I(U)
, can we deduce that

Appendix A
Ultrafilters

Here we collect some basic knowledge on ultrafilters, which is used throughout the paper. The material should be fairly well-known (see,
e.g.
,
[
11
, Appendix A]
,
[
40
, Chapter 7.4]
or
[
50
, Appendix A]
). While some of the results might not be standard and we have not dug into the reference, we include the proofs for convenience to readers.

Definition A.1
.

Let
X
be a set and
\mathcal{P}(X)
be its power set. An
ultrafilter
on
X
is a family
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(X)
satisfying the following:

(I.1)

\emptyset\notin\mathcal{U}
;

(I.2)

for
A,B\in\mathcal{U}
, then
A\cap B\in\mathcal{U}
;

(I.3)

for
A\in\mathcal{U}
and
A\subseteq B
, then
B\in\mathcal{U}
;

(I.4)

for any
A\subseteq X
, either
A\in\mathcal{U}
or
X\setminus A\in\mathcal{U}
.

For an ultrafilter
\mathcal{U}
on
X
, we can associate a function
\omega:\mathcal{P}(X)\to\{0,1\}
by setting
\omega(A)=1
if and only if
A\in\mathcal{U}
. It follows from (I.1)-(I.4) above that
\omega
is a finitely additive
\{0,1\}
-valued probability measure on
\mathcal{P}(X)
. Conversely for such a function
\omega
on
\mathcal{P}(X)
, we can associate a family
\mathcal{U}_{\omega}:=\{A\subseteq X:\omega(A)=1\}
. It is clear that
\mathcal{U}_{\omega}
is an ultrafilter on
X
, and these two procedures are inverse to each other. Therefore throughout the paper, we slide between these two notions freely without further explanation.

For
a\in X
, it is clear that the family
\{A\in\mathcal{P}(X):a\in A\}
is an ultrafilter on
X
. Such an ultrafiler is called
principal
. An ultrafilter which is not principal is called
non-principal
. An argument using Zorn’s lemma shows that non-principal ultrafilters always exist whenever
X
is infinite.

The following is well-known (see,
e.g.
,
[
50
, Lemma A.2]
):

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\omega
be an ultrafilter on a set
X
, and
D\subseteq X
with
\omega(D)=1
. Let
f:D\to Y
be a function from
D
to a compact Hausdorff topological space
Y
. Then there exists a unique point
y\in Y
such that for any open neighbourhood
U
of
y
, we have
\omega(f^{-1}(U))=1
.

Definition A.3
.

The unique point in Lemma
A.2
is called the
ultralimit
of
f
along
\omega
or the
\omega
-limit
of
f
, denoted by
\lim_{\omega}f
or
\lim_{a\to\omega}f(a)
.

We record the following localisation result:

Lemma A.4
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be an ultrafilter on a set
X
, and
A\subseteq X
with
A\in\mathcal{U}
. Then we have:

(1)

\{S\cap A:S\in\mathcal{U}\}=\{S\subseteq A:S\in\mathcal{U}\}
is an ultrafilter on
A
, denoted by
\mathcal{U}_{A}
.

(2)

\mathcal{U}=\{S\subseteq X:S\cap A\in\mathcal{U}_{A}\}=\{S\subseteq X:\exists~S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{A}\text{ such that }S^{\prime}\subseteq S\}
.

Proof.

(1). It follows from Definition
A.1
that
\{S\cap A:S\in\mathcal{U}\}=\{S\subseteq A:S\in\mathcal{U}\}
, and hence (I.1)-(I.3) hold for
\mathcal{U}_{A}
. Concerning (I.4): given
B\subseteq A
, if
B\in\mathcal{U}
then
B\in\mathcal{U}_{A}
as well; if
B\notin\mathcal{U}
then
X\setminus B\in\mathcal{U}
, and hence
A\setminus B=A\cap(X\setminus B)\in\mathcal{U}_{A}
.

(2). This is straightforward, hence omitted.
∎

We can also extend an ultrafilter on a subset to the whole space. The proof is straightforward, hence omitted.

Lemma A.5
.

Let
Y
be a subset of a set
X
, and
\mathcal{U}_{0}
an ultrafilter on
Y
. Define

\mathcal{U}:=\{S\subseteq X:S\cap Y\in\mathcal{U}_{0}\}.

Then
\mathcal{U}
is an ultrafilter on
X
.

The following result provides an approach to combine a family of ultrafilters into a single one:

Lemma A.6
.

Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a family of sets, and
\mathcal{U}_{i}
be an ultrafilter on
X_{i}
for each
i\in I
. Let
\omega_{0}
be an ultrafilter on
I
. Consider the set
X:=\bigsqcup_{i\in I}X_{i}
and define:

\mathcal{U}:=\big\{\bigsqcup_{i\in I}A_{i}\subseteq\bigsqcup_{i\in I}X_{i}:\exists~J\subseteq I\text{ with }\omega_{0}(J)=1\text{ such that }\forall i\in J,A_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{i}\big\}.

Then
\mathcal{U}
is an ultrafilter on
X
.

Proof.

Firstly, it is clear that
\emptyset\notin\mathcal{U}
. Assume that
\bigsqcup_{i\in I}A_{i}
and
\bigsqcup_{i\in I}B_{i}\in\mathcal{U}
,
i.e.
, there exist
J_{A},J_{B}\subseteq I
with
\omega_{0}(J_{A})=\omega_{0}(J_{B})=1
such that
A_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{i}
for any
i\in J_{A}
and
B_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{i}
for any
i\in J_{B}
. Consider
(\bigsqcup_{i\in I}A_{i})\cap(\bigsqcup_{i\in I}B_{i})=\bigsqcup_{i\in I}(A_{i}\cap B_{i})
and
J=J_{A}\cap J_{B}
. Then
\omega_{0}(J)=1
and for each
i\in J
,
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
are in
\mathcal{U}_{i}
. This implies that
A_{i}\cap B_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{i}
, which concludes (I.2).

It is clear that (I.3) holds for
\mathcal{U}
and finally, we consider (I.4). Given
A=\bigsqcup_{i\in I}A_{i}\subseteq X
, denote
J:=\{i\in I:A_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{i}\}
. If
\omega_{0}(J)=1
, then it follows that
A\in\mathcal{U}
. Otherwise, assume that
\omega_{0}(J)=0
. Then we consider
X\setminus A=\bigsqcup_{i\in I}(X_{i}\setminus A_{i})
. Then
I\setminus J=\{i\in I:X_{i}\setminus A_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{i}\}
and
\omega_{0}(I\setminus J)=1
, which implies that
X\setminus A\in\mathcal{U}
and concludes the proof.
∎

Recall that ultrafilters can also be characterised by the Stone-Čech compactification. More precisely, we have the following (see,
e.g.
,
[
40
, Chapter 7.4]
):

Lemma A.7
.

Let
X
be a set and
\beta X
be the Stone-Čech compactification of
X
.

(1)

Given
\omega\in\beta X
, the family
\{A\subseteq X:\omega\in\overline{A}\}
is an ultrafilter on
X
.

(2)

Given an ultrafilter
\mathcal{U}
on
X
, the intersection
\bigcap_{A\in\mathcal{U}}\overline{A}
consists of a single point.

The procedures above are inverse to each other, and hence
\beta X
can be characterised by ultrafilters on
X
. Moreover, points in
\partial_{\beta}X
correspond to non-principal ultrafilters.

Thanks to Lemma
A.7
, we will also use ultrafilters and points in the Stone-Čech compactification freely without further explanation throughout the paper.

For convenience, we also record that for
D\subseteq X
, its closure
\overline{D}
in
\beta X
satisfies:

\overline{D}=\{\omega\in\beta X:\omega(D)=1\}

and
\overline{D}
is clopen in
\beta X
. The topology of
\beta X
is generated by
\{\overline{D}:D\subseteq X\}
.

Finally we recall the following, which can be proved either directly using the universal property of the Stone-Čech compactification or deduced directly from Lemma
A.4
,
A.5
and
A.7
:

Corollary A.8
.

For a subset
Z\subseteq X
, the closure
\overline{Z}
in
\beta X
is homeomorphic to
\beta Z
.
