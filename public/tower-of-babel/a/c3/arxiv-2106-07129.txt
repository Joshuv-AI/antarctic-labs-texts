A Path-Counting Analysis of Phase Shifts in Box-Ball Systems
Nicholas M. ERCOLANI and Jonathan RAMALHEIRA-TSU

Abstract
In this paper, we perform a detailed analysis of the phase shift phenomenon of the classical soliton cellular automaton known as the box-ball system, ultimately resulting in a statement and proof of a formula describing this phase shift. This phenomenon has been observed since the nineties, when the system was first introduced by Takahashi and Satsuma, but no explicit global description was made beyond its observation. By using the Gessel–Viennot–Lindström lemma and path-counting arguments, we present here a novel proof of the classical phase shift formula for the continuous-time Toda lattice, as discovered by Moser, and use this proof to derive a discrete-time Toda lattice analogue of the phase shift phenomenon. By carefully analysing the connection between the box-ball system and the discrete-time Toda lattice, through the mechanism of tropicalisation/dequantisation, we translate this discrete-time Toda lattice phase shift formula into our new formula for the box-ball system phase shift.

Introduction

In classical solitary wave theory there are three well-known signature features of the inherent nonlinearity of these waves that are inter-related. The first is that in long time, forward and backward, an exact

The second feature is that in long time these individual masses are ordered by their peak amplitudes, travelling with individual speeds asymptotically proportional to the square root of the amplitude. This is referred to as the

The sorting and phase shift properties are characteristic of

In the next two sections of this paper, we will discuss two other examples of these phenomena: the finite Toda lattice and the box-ball system (BBS). They turn out to be related to one another through a type of semi-classical limit known as

The precise sorting and phase shift formulas for the Toda lattice have been known for some time going back to Moser
[
20
]
. It is also the case that early work by Tokihiro et al.
[
29
]
derives results related to the explicit BBS phase shift formula that we will present. A comparison to the work of
[
29
]

[
13

However, rather than basing the proof on representation theoretic arguments, as Kostant does, our approach is based on a path counting construction. The potential advantages of this are, we feel, two-fold. First, Kostant’s extension of the phase shift formula depends on somewhat complicated determinantal formulas, whereas what we present is completely geometric and combinatorial thus making generalizations more natural. Secondly, we found, subsequent to our initial derivations, that the directed graphs we employ for our path counting are, in fact, those used by Postnikov
[
19

to coordinatize the totally positive Grassmannians and their tropicalization. This reinforces our belief that this is the right geometric picture for our second main result which is to derive the explicit, general phase shift formula for BBS by direct tropical limit of the classical Toda formula.

2.1
Box-ball systems

A cellular automaton is a special type of discrete dynamical system with both discrete time steps and a discrete (in fact finite) number of states. Of particular interest is the box-ball system (BBS) which was introduced in 1990 by Takahashi and Satsuma
[

.

The (basic) box-ball system consists of a one-dimensional infinite array of boxes with a finite number of the boxes filled with balls, and no more than one ball in each box (see, for example, Figure

More formally, the phase space of this system, which we denote by
BBS

\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}

1

0

2.2
The box-ball evolution

A simple evolution rule is provided for the box-ball dynamics:

(1)

Take the left-most ball that has not been moved and move it to the left-most empty box to its right.

(2)

Repeat (1) until all balls have been moved precisely once.

Since the algorithm requires one to know which balls have been moved, we can, without technically changing the algorithm, introduce a colour-coding based on whether balls have moved or not. Balls will be blue until they have moved, after which they will become red. When all balls are red, the colours should be reset to blue, ready for the next time step. Or, equivalently, a

2.3
Soliton behaviour and the sorting property

The box-ball system is sometimes referred to as a soliton cellular automaton. To appreciate this reference, we think of an entire box-ball configuration as being the soliton. In the classical setting, a soliton is thought of as being composed of masses (or pulses) that are nonlinearly related. In the box-ball setting, a “mass” corresponds to a consecutive sequence of balls. One may observe (see below) that such a block travels with velocity equal to the number of balls in it, so that larger blocks have velocity greater than smaller blocks. As with classical soliton masses, during the course of its evolution, the blocks may collide, and temporarily change their sizes. However, asymptotically in both forward and backward (discrete) time (
t
), the sizes of blocks comprising the soliton are the same. We will therefore refer to such a configuration as an
n
-soliton
if the total number of blocks asymptotically is
n
.

After blocks collide, they come out of the collision ordered with the longer blocks ahead of smaller blocks but having a
phase shift
due to the nonlinearity. By phase shift here, we mean the difference between where the block ends up after the collision and where the block would have been if it were not for the collision.

3
The Toda lattice

The Toda lattice
[
27
]
is a dynamical system on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, with coordinates
(p_{1},\dots,p_{n},q_{1},\dots,q_{n})
. The system is Hamiltonian with respect to the standard symplectic structure on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
with Hamiltonian

H(p_{1},\dots,p_{n},q_{1},\dots,q_{n})=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}{\rm e}^{q_{j}-q_{j+1}}.

The Toda lattice equations are then given by

\displaystyle\dot{q}_{j}=p_{j},\qquad j=1,\dots,n,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{j}=\begin{cases}-{\rm e}^{q_{1}-q_{2}}&\text{if }j=1,\\
{\rm e}^{q_{j-1}-q_{j}}-{\rm e}^{q_{j}-q_{j+1}}&\text{if }1<j<n,\\
{\rm e}^{q_{n-1}-q_{n}}&\text{if }j=n.\end{cases}

(3.2)

In this representation, boundary conditions of
q_{0}=-\infty
and
q_{n+1}=\infty
have been imposed, which, formally, result in
{\rm e}^{q_{0}-q_{1}}={\rm e}^{q_{n}-q_{n+1}}=0
. These boundary conditions are chosen to truncate the lattice to a finite system.

3.1
Flaschka’s transformation and isospectrality

Flaschka’s transformation
[
10
]
makes the variable replacement

(p_{1},\dots,p_{n},q_{1},\dots,q_{n})\mapsto(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{n-1})

given by setting
a_{j}=-p_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,n
, and
b_{j}={\rm e}^{q_{j}-q_{j+1}}
for
j=1,\dots,n-1
.
This is clearly a surjection of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
onto the open subset of

\mathbb{R}^{2n-1}
with
b_{i}>0
. It is evident that uniformly translating the position variables,
(q_{1},\dots,q_{n})\to(q_{1}+c,\dots,q_{n}+c)
leaves the equations
(
3.1
)–(
3.2
)
invariant. Fixing a center of mass for this particle system amounts to a particular cross-section of the fibration that the Flaschka transformation presents.

In Flaschka variables, the Toda lattice assumes the following simple form:

\displaystyle\dot{b}_{j}=(a_{j+1}-a_{j})b_{j},\qquad j=1,\dots,n-1,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\dot{a}_{j}=\begin{cases}b_{1}&\text{if }j=1,\\
b_{j}-b_{j-1}&\text{if }1<j<n,\\
-b_{n-1}&\text{if }j=n.\end{cases}

(3.4)

One can arrange the variables neatly into a tridiagonal Hessenberg matrix

X\doteq\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}a_{1}&1\\
b_{1}&a_{2}&\ddots\\
&\ddots&\ddots&1\\
&&b_{n-1}&a_{n}\end{array}\right].

It is immediate from (
3.4
) that
\text{tr}X
is a constant of motion. This is conservation of momentum corresponding to the translation symmetry mentioned above.

Equations (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) amount to the following matrix differential equation:

\left[\begin{matrix}a_{1}&1\\
b_{1}&a_{2}&\ddots\\
&\ddots&\ddots&1\\
&&b_{n-1}&a_{n}\end{matrix}\right]^{\bullet}=\left[\begin{matrix}b_{1}&0\\
(a_{2}-a_{1})b_{1}&b_{2}-b_{1}&\ddots\\
&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
&&(a_{n}-a_{n-1})b_{n-1}&-b_{n-1}\end{matrix}\right].

This matrix form of the Toda equations has the form of a
Lax equation
,

\dfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}X=[X,\pi_{-}(X)],\qquad X(0)=X_{0},

(3.5)

where
\pi_{-}(X)
denotes the projection of
X
into its strictly lower part:

\pi_{-}(X)=\left[\begin{matrix}0&\\
b_{1}&0&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&b_{n-1}&0\end{matrix}\right].

It is a straightforward application of the product rule using (
3.5
) to see that

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\text{tr}X^{k}=\text{tr}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}X^{k}=\text{tr}\big[X^{k},\pi_{-}(X)\big]=0.

This implies the so-called
isospectrality
of the Toda lattice: the eigenvalues of
X
remain invariant under the Toda flow.

3.2
Sorting for Toda

In Section
4
, we will see that for
t\to\infty
, the Toda solution
X(t)
limits to a matrix of the form

\epsilon_{\lambda}=\left[\begin{matrix}\lambda_{1}&1\\
&\lambda_{2}&\ddots\\
&&\ddots&1\\
&&&\lambda_{n}\end{matrix}\right],

(3.6)

where
\lambda_{1}>\dots>\lambda_{n}
are the eigenvalues of
X_{0}
which we will assume to be distinct in the remainder of this paper (by isospectrality these are the eigenvalues of
X(t)
for all
t
). It is immediate from (
3.5
) that
\epsilon_{\lambda}
is a fixed point of the Toda flow as are all the other
n!
matrices of this form with the eigenvalues permuted along the diagonal (and these are the only fixed points). We will also see in Section
4
that as
t\to-\infty
,
X(t)
limits to the fixed point associated to the longest permutation (which reverses the order of the eigenvalues along the diagonal). Finally, from the Flaschka representation, one sees that the asymptotic velocities of the original Toda variables, in forward and backward time, are the eigenvalues of
X(t)
. So, what we have just described is just the
sorting property
of the Toda lattice. Precisely, the asymptotic dynamics is

\displaystyle q_{k}(t)=\alpha_{k}^{+}t+\beta_{k}^{+}+O\big({\rm e}^{-\delta t}\big),

(3.7)

\displaystyle q_{k}(-t)=-\alpha_{k}^{-}t+\beta_{k}^{-}+O\big({\rm e}^{-\delta t}\big)

(3.8)

as
t\to+\infty
, where

\alpha_{k}^{+}=\lim_{t\to+\infty}p_{k}(t).

To relate this to the Hessenberg form of the Toda lattice, recall that

a_{k}(t)=-p_{k}(t),\qquad b_{k}(t)={\rm e}^{q_{k}(t)-q_{k+1}(t)}.

So, since the limiting form in forward time of
X(t)
is given by (
3.6
) (and by its reverse permutation in backward time), one has that

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}^{+}=-\lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}a_{k}(t)=-\lambda_{k},

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}^{-}=-\lim\limits_{t\to-\infty}a_{k}(t)=-\lambda_{n-k+1}

from which we note the symmetry
\alpha_{k}^{-}=\alpha_{n-k+1}^{+}
. Since

\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\cdots>\lambda_{n}
,

q_{k}(t)-q_{k+1}(t)\sim-(\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{k+1})t
as
t\to+\infty
. So, it is indeed the case that the particles spread apart linearly and sort, from which it follows that
b_{k}(t)\to 0
exponentially, consistent with the stated limiting form (
3.6
) of
X(t)
. A similar statement obtains asymptotically in reverse time.

3.3
Symes’s discrete-time matrix dynamics

and the discrete-time Toda lattice

In 1980, Symes
[
23
]
proposed a discrete time flow based on matrix factorization that commutes with the continuous time Toda flow described previously. This factorization mirrors that described in Theorem
4.1
, more commonly referred to as Gaussian elimination or the
LU
-decomposition.

Note
.

In this section and what follows, we continue to use
t
to denote discrete time, i.e.,
t\in\mathbb{Z}
(as well as for continuous time,
t\in\mathbb{R}
). The temporal domain intended should be clear from context.

We inductively define Symes’s dynamics as a two-step discrete evolution on Hessenberg matrices. If at (discrete) time
t
we have a matrix
X(t)
, we obtain
X(t+1)
as follows:

1.

Perform Gaussian elimination to factor
X(t)=L(t)R(t)
, with
L(t)
lower unipotent and
R(t)
upper triangular.

2.

Permute the factors to define
X(t+1)=R(t)L(t)
.

Remark 3.1
.

By construction, one has

X(t+1)=R(t)L(t)=\big(L(t)^{-1}X(t)\big)L(t)=L(t)^{-1}X(t)L(t).

(3.9)

Thus, this discrete evolution is given by conjugating a matrix by its lower unipotent factor. Since the spectrum of a matrix is invariant under conjugation, it follows that the eigenvalues are constants of motion for this discrete evolution; i.e., this discrete flow is isospectral.

Furthermore, if
X(t)
is tridiagonal, then one can show that
L(t)
is lower bi-diagonal with ones on its diagonal and
R(t)
is upper bi-diagonal with ones on its superdiagonal. Therefore, the product
X(t+1)=R(t)L(t)
is itself once again a tridiagonal Hessenberg matrix; i.e., this flow preserves the Toda lattice phase space.

It is of course not always the case that a given matrix has an
LU
-factorization in which the coefficients of the factors do not become singular. It is possible to continue the dynamics through these singularities, as we will see in Section
4
. For the phase shift behavior that we study, we are only interested in the long-time dynamics; for
t
of sufficiently large magnitude,
LU
-factorization always works.

Symes
[
23
]
observed that this discrete evolution extends to a continuous evolution with Lax equation of the same form as the Toda lattice Lax equation (equation (
3.5
)), but with
\pi_{-}(X)
replaced by
\pi_{-}(\log X)
:

\dfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}X=[X,\pi_{-}(\log X)].

(3.10)

Remark 3.2
.

In our applications here we will always take
X
to have positive eigenvalues so that
\log X
may be uniquely defined in terms of the principal branch of the logarithm along the positive real axis. From this it follows that this continuous flow is well-defined and commutes with the original Toda flow
[
5
,
31
]
. The Hamiltonian for (
3.10
) is
H_{LU}=\operatorname{Tr}(X\log X-X)
. This is discussed further in Section
4
.

To write the Symes discrete-time evolution out explicitly, let

L(t)=\left[\begin{matrix}1\\
V_{1}^{t}&1\\
&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&V_{n-1}^{t}&1\end{matrix}\right],\qquad\text{and}\qquad R(t)=\left[\begin{matrix}I_{1}^{t}&1\\
&I_{2}^{t}&\ddots\\
&&\ddots&1\\
&&&I_{n}^{t}\end{matrix}\right],

(3.11)

then Symes’s discrete-time evolution produces what has come to be known as the finite discrete-time Toda lattice:

Definition 3.3
.

The finite discrete-time Toda lattice is the system

\begin{cases}I_{i}^{t+1}=I_{i}^{t}+V_{i}^{t}-V_{i-1}^{t+1},&i=1,\dots,n,\\
V_{i}^{t+1}=\dfrac{I_{i+1}^{t}V_{i}^{t}}{I_{i}^{t+1}},&i=1,\dots,n-1,\\
V_{0}^{t}=V_{n}^{t}=0,&\end{cases}

which is expressible as

L(t+1)R(t+1)=R(t)L(t).

3.4
Maslov tropicalisation

Tropical mathematics (see, for example,
[
16
,
17
,
30
]
) is the study of the min-plus (or the max-plus) semiring, which we will now define. In this section, we follow the presentation given by Maslov
[
17
]
. The structure of the semiring
(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},+,\times)
is carried over to the set
\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
by a family of bijections
D_{\hbar}
, for
\hbar>0
, given by

D_{\hbar}(x)=\begin{cases}-\hbar\ln x&\text{if }x\neq 0,\\
-\infty&\text{if }x=0.\end{cases}

This induces a family of semirings, parametrised by
\hbar>0
,
(\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\},\oplus_{\hbar},\otimes_{\hbar})
with operations given by

\displaystyle a\oplus_{\hbar}b=D_{\hbar}\big(D_{\hbar}^{-1}(a)+D_{\hbar}^{-1}(b)\big)=\begin{cases}-\hbar\ln\big({\rm e}^{-a/\hbar}+{\rm e}^{-b/\hbar}\big)&\text{if }a,b\neq\infty,\\
\min(a,b)&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

\displaystyle a\otimes_{\hbar}b=D_{\hbar}\big(D_{\hbar}^{-1}(a)D_{\hbar}^{-1}(b)\big)=a+b.

In the limit,
\hbar\to 0
, Maslov ‘dequantises’
(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},+,\times)
to obtain the tropical semiring
(\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\},\allowbreak\min,+)
, where its addition is the usual
\min
operation and its multiplication operation is usual addition, hence the name “min-plus semiring”.

Maslov views this construction as an analogue of the correspondence principle from quantum mechanics, with
(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},+,\times)
as the quantum object and
(\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\},\min,+)
as its classical counterpart.

Remark 3.4
.

The limiting process through
D_{\hbar}
allows tropicalisation to be carried out precisely on equations by pulling the analogous tropical variables back, mapping the resulting equation back to
\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
, and then taking the limit as
\hbar\to 0
. This can be seen in action in equations (
3.12
)–(
3.19
), and again in Section
7
.

3.5
The ultradiscrete Toda lattice (udToda)

Following
[
28
]
, we perform the process of ultradiscretisation on the discrete-time Toda lattice to see that this results in the box-ball system.

Recall the discrete-time Toda lattice:

\begin{cases}V_{0}^{t}=V_{n}^{t}=0,&\\
I_{i}^{t+1}=I_{i}^{t}+V_{i}^{t}-V_{i-1}^{t+1},&i=1,\dots,n,\\
V_{i}^{t+1}I_{i}^{t+1}=I_{i+1}^{t}V_{i}^{t},&i=1,\dots,n-1.\end{cases}

It can be shown that the discrete-time Toda lattice is equivalent to the following system

\begin{cases}V_{0}^{t}=V_{n}^{t}=0,&\\
I_{i}^{t+1}=V_{i}^{t}+\dfrac{I_{i}^{t}\cdots I_{1}^{t}}{I_{i-1}^{t+1}\cdots I_{1}^{t+1}},&i=1,\dots,n,\\
V_{i}^{t+1}I_{i}^{t+1}=I_{i+1}^{t}V_{i}^{t},&i=1,\dots,n-1.\end{cases}

Making the change of variables

I_{i}^{t}={\rm e}^{-\frac{1}{\hbar}Q_{i}^{t}(\hbar)},\qquad V_{i}^{t}={\rm e}^{-\frac{1}{\hbar}W_{i}^{t}(\hbar)},

(3.12)

one obtains

\displaystyle W_{i}^{t+1}(\hbar)=Q_{i+1}^{t}(\hbar)+W_{i}^{t}(\hbar)-Q_{i}^{t+1}(\hbar),\qquad i=1,\dots,n-1,

(3.13)

\displaystyle Q_{i}^{t+1}(\hbar)=-\hbar\log\Big({\rm e}^{-\frac{1}{\hbar}W_{i}^{t}(\hbar)}+{\rm e}^{-\frac{1}{\hbar}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i}Q_{j}^{t}(\hbar)-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}Q_{j}^{t+1}(\hbar)\right)}\Big),\qquad i=1,\dots,n,

(3.14)

\displaystyle W_{0}^{t}(\hbar)=W_{n}^{t}(\hbar)=\infty.

(3.15)

Finally, assuming the limits

\displaystyle W_{i}^{t}:=\lim\limits_{\hbar\to 0^{+}}W_{i}^{t}(\hbar),\qquad

\displaystyle W_{i}^{t+1}:=\lim\limits_{\hbar\to 0^{+}}W_{i}^{t+1}(\hbar),

\displaystyle Q_{i}^{t}:=\lim\limits_{\hbar\to 0^{+}}Q_{i}^{t}(\hbar),\qquad

\displaystyle Q_{i}^{t+1}:=\lim\limits_{\hbar\to 0^{+}}Q_{i}^{t+1}(\hbar),

(3.16)

exist, one obtains

\displaystyle W_{i}^{t+1}=Q_{i+1}^{t}+W_{i}^{t}-Q_{i}^{t+1},\qquad i=1,\dots,n-1,

(3.17)

\displaystyle Q_{i}^{t+1}=\min\Bigg(W_{i}^{t},\sum_{j=1}^{i}Q_{j}^{t}-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}Q_{j}^{t+1}\Bigg),\qquad i=1,\dots,n,

(3.18)

\displaystyle W_{0}^{t}=W_{n}^{t}=\infty,

(3.19)

which are known as the ultradiscrete Toda (udToda) equations.

3.6
Centre of mass

By the isospectrality of dToda (
3.9
), the trace is also a constant of motion for dToda, in fact, it is a
Casimir
of the system. A quick computation of the diagonal entries of
L(t)R(t)
in (
3.11
) reveals that the corresponding tropicalisation of

\operatorname{tr}X(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}I_{i}^{t}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}V_{i}^{t}

is

\min\big(Q_{1}^{t},\dots,Q_{n}^{t},W_{1}^{t},\dots,W_{n-1}^{t}\big).

However, with the box-ball interpretation of the ultradiscretised dToda equations, the balls are seen to be moving right. In this interpretation, one can assign site numbers to the boxes (indexing them by
\mathbb{Z}
) and the sum of the site numbers of the first ball in each block can be seen to grow linearly with rate
M=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}^{t}
which is another constant of motion (the tropical analogue of the determinant of
X(t)=L(t)R(t)
). This observation is key to the main result of this paper and is stated precisely and proved later in Lemma
7.3
.

4.1
Kostant’s theorem and the principal embedding

The matrix reformulation of the Toda lattice in Flaschka’s variables led to an elegant Lie-theoretic method for its solution that extended to the solving of
Lax equations
more generally
[
1
,
13
,
24
]
). This is typically referred to as the Adler–Kostant–Symes (AKS) factorization theorem. Prior to stating that theorem we introduce some notation related to Lie theoretic factorizations.

We consider the Lie algebra decomposition of
n\times n

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{gl}(n,\mathbb{R})=\mathfrak{n}_{-}\oplus\mathfrak{b}_{+},

where
\mathfrak{n}_{-}
is the lower triangular nilradical subalgebra and
\mathfrak{b}_{+}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{n}_{-}=\left(\begin{matrix}0&&&&\\
*&0&&&\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&\\
*&\dots&\dots&*&0\end{matrix}\right),\qquad\mathfrak{b}_{+}=\left(\begin{matrix}*&*&\dots&\dots&*\\
&*&*&&\vdots\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
&&&\ddots&*\\
&&&&*\end{matrix}\right).

We will also use
\mathfrak{b}_{-}
, the transpose of
\mathfrak{b}_{+}
. Employing the regular nilpotent element,

\displaystyle\epsilon=\left(\begin{matrix}0&1&&&\\
&0&1&&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\\

\displaystyle\epsilon+\mathfrak{b}_{-}=\left(\begin{matrix}*&1&&&\\

\displaystyle\pi_{-}\colon\ \mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{n}_{-},\qquad\Pi_{-}\colon\ G\to N_{-},

\displaystyle\pi_{+}\colon\ \mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{b}_{+},\qquad\Pi_{+}\colon\ G\to B_{+},

and

is the invertible upper triangular matrices, is the exponential group of the algebra

Theorem 4.1

(the factorization theorem,

To solve

\dfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}X=[X,\pi_{-}(X)],\qquad X(0)=X_{0},

(
locally it is

X(t)=\Pi_{-}^{-1}\big({\rm e}^{X_{0}t}\big)X_{0}\Pi_{-}\big({\rm e}^{X_{0}t}\big).

(4.2)

The dynamical system (
4.1

is referred to as the

and, per the factorization theorem, its solution is locally given by (

This factorization result can be extended to discrete-time Toda. To avoid

Lemma 4.2

(

To solve the discrete-time Toda lattice with initial condition

for all

These factorizations provide the basis for our analysis of the phase shift formulas in both continuous and discrete time Toda. This is facilitated by a key result due to Kostant that provides a natural embedding of the Toda dynamics into a flag manifold that plays a role analogous to action-angle variables in classical integrable systems theory.

Theorem 4.3

(
[
13
]
)

For each

where

Set

the isospectral manifold, and define the

into a compact, homogeneous space, known as the

, to be

\displaystyle\kappa_{\lambda}\colon\ \mathcal{F}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\to G/B_{+},

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\mapsto L^{-1}\mod B_{+}.

This mapping simultaneously linearizes and completes the Toda flows

7
,
8
]

If
X_{0}=L_{0}\epsilon_{\lambda}L_{0}^{-1}
then

\displaystyle X(t)=\Pi_{-}^{-1}\big({\rm e}^{tX_{0}}\big)X_{0}\Pi_{-}\big({\rm e}^{tX_{0}}\big)=\Pi_{-}^{-1}\big({\rm e}^{tX_{0}}\big)L_{0}\epsilon_{\lambda}L_{0}^{-1}\Pi_{-}\big({\rm e}^{tX_{0}}\big),

\displaystyle\kappa_{\lambda}(X(t))=L_{0}^{-1}\Pi_{-}\big({\rm e}^{tX_{0}}\big)\mod B_{+}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\kappa_\lambda(X(t))}{}=L_{0}^{-1}\big({\rm e}^{tX_{0}}\big)\mod B_{+}

on

through the principal image of the initial value,

In Remark

we pointed out that the continuous Toda flow commutes with the discrete time Toda evolution. In fact, there is a hierarchy of continuous flows commuting with dToda and with one another given by Lax equations of the form

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t_{m}}X=\big[X,\pi_{-}X^{m}\big].

(4.3)

On

4.2
The fundamental element
\boldsymbol{\epsilon_{\lambda}}

As mentioned earlier, we are going to see that the Toda flow limits to
\epsilon_{\lambda}
(
3.6
) in large forward time. This element is distinguished by the fact that it lies in the intersection

(\epsilon+\mathfrak{b}_{-})_{\lambda}\cap\mathfrak{b_{+}}
, and in that regard it is essentially unique. (There are
n!
elements in this intersection corresponding to permutations of the distinct eigenvalues. In fact in large backwards time the Toda flow limits to the element corresponding to the longest permutation.) Since this element will play such a central role in our initial calculations, we take a few moments to discuss some of its relevant features.

4.2.1
Diagonalizing
\boldsymbol{\epsilon_{\lambda}}

Because we assume
\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{j}
for all
i\neq j
, we can of course diagonalise any matrix in
\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}
. The following result, which is an explicit form of Lemma 7 in
[
7
]
, describes a diagonalisation of
\epsilon_{\lambda}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

If
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
are distinct, then one has
\epsilon_{\lambda}=UD_{\lambda}U^{-1}
, where
U=(u_{ij})
is the upper triangular matrix given by

u_{ij}=\prod_{k=1}^{i-1}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k}),\qquad 1\leq i\leq j\leq n,

and
D_{\lambda}=\epsilon_{\lambda}-\epsilon=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
.

Proof.

The matrix
U
is clearly invertible since

\det(U)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{k=1}^{i-1}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k})=\prod_{1\leq k<i\leq n}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k})

and as stated above, we have assumed
\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{j}
for all
i\neq j
.

It just remains to show that
\epsilon_{\lambda}U=UD_{\lambda}
. Let
u_{j}=(u_{ij})_{1\leq i\leq n}
be the
j
-th column of
U
, then for
i<n
:

\displaystyle(\epsilon_{\lambda}u_{j})_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\epsilon_{\lambda})_{ik}u_{kj}=\lambda_{i}u_{ij}+u_{i+1,j}=\lambda_{i}\prod_{k=1}^{i-1}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k})+\prod_{k=1}^{i}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k})

\displaystyle=(\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i})\prod_{k=1}^{i-1}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k})=\lambda_{j}\prod_{k=1}^{i-1}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k})=\lambda_{j}(u_{j})_{i}.

For
i=n
, we simply have

\displaystyle(\epsilon_{\lambda}u_{j})_{n}=\lambda_{n}(u_{j})_{n}=\begin{cases}0,&j<n,\\
\lambda_{j}(u_{j})_{n},&j=n\end{cases}=\lambda_{j}(u_{j})_{n}.

Thus,
\epsilon_{\lambda}u_{j}=\lambda_{j}u_{j}
for each
j
.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

In the remainder of this paper, it is convenient to work with a unipotent matrix of eigenvectors for
\epsilon_{\lambda}
. Since
U
is triangular, we achieve this by scaling each column of the matrix
U
by the reciprocal of the diagonal entry for that column. The result is that we can choose

(U)_{ij}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\prod\limits_{i\leq m<j}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{m}}&\text{for }i\leq j,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and still maintain
\epsilon_{\lambda}=UD_{\lambda}U^{-1}
. From now on,
U
shall refer to this upper unipotent matrix.

4.2.2
Eigenvectors and the torus embedding

In this section we describe a slight modification of the principal embedding which displays more clearly the linearized character of the Toda flows that these embeddings reveal. This is the homogeneous space analogue of the linearization in terms of action-angle variables familiar from classical integrable systems theory
[
3
]
.

Combining Lemma
4.4
with Theorem
4.3
provides a diagonalization of
X\in\epsilon+\mathfrak{b}_{-}
:

\displaystyle X=LUD_{\lambda}U^{-1}L^{-1}.

(4.4)

Cross-multiplying in two different ways this is equivalent to each of two different representations

\displaystyle\big(U^{-1}L^{-1}\big)X=D_{\lambda}\big(U^{-1}L^{-1}\big),

(4.5)

\displaystyle X(LU)=(LU)D_{\lambda}.

(4.6)

The representation in (
4.5

as a matrix whose rows are the left eigenvectors of

while (
4.6

(LU)
as a matrix whose columns are the right eigenvectors of

\displaystyle\operatorname{tor}_{\lambda}\colon\ \mathcal{F}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\to G/B_{+},

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\mapsto U^{-1}L^{-1}\mod B_{+}

from

to its matrix of left eigenvectors. As before one can track the Toda dynamics through this embedding
and find that

\displaystyle\operatorname{tor}_{\lambda}(X(t))={\rm e}^{tD_{\lambda}}U^{-1}L^{-1}\mod B_{+}.

Also, as before, this can be extended to the commuting hierarchy of Toda flows (

\displaystyle\operatorname{tor}_{\lambda}(X(t_{1},\dots,t_{n}))=\exp\left({\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}t_{m}D^{m}_{\lambda}}\right)U^{-1}L^{-1}\mod B_{+}.

This amounts to a toric action
(left multiplication by
(\mathbb{R}^{*})^{n}
) on the flag manifold, explaining why

is referred to as the
torus embedding
. This is a replacement of the nonlinear Toda dynamics by a linear semigroup action on
G/B_{+}
.

4.3
Painlevé balances

It was pointed out in Theorem
4.1
that lower-upper factorization of
{\rm e}^{tX_{0}}
does not necessarily hold for all values of
t
. When this happens some entry of
X(t)
will become infinite. In fact these entries are meromorphic (which fact is referred to as the Painlevé property). We refer to
[
11
]
for further details about this. But as we saw in the previous section, the evolution under the torus embedding is just a multi-scaling action in the flag manifold, which is compact, and so the flows exist for all time.
A geometric understanding of what is happening is provided by the
Birkhoff cell decomposition
of
G/B_{+}
. The flag manifold may be written as a disjoint union,

\displaystyle G/B_{+}=\bigcup_{\widehat{w}\in W}N_{-}\widehat{w}B_{+}/B_{+}.

In this representation,
W
is the group of
n\times n

permutation matrices. The
n!
sets,
N_{-}\widehat{w}B_{+}/B_{+}
, are cells (homeomorphic to a Euclidean space) called Birkhoff cells. The cell
N_{-}B_{+}/B_{+}
is open and dense in
G/B_{+}
and referred to as the “big cell”. The other cells are lower dimensional with co-dimension equal to the
length of
\widehat{w}
(corresponding to the minimal number of pivots required to carry out Gaussian elimination of a representative group element for a point in that cell). By Kostant’s theorem
4.3
, all elements of
\epsilon+\mathfrak{b}_{-}
embed into the big cell. Under the Toda flow, “blow-up” of
X(t)
occurs precisely when
{\rm e}^{tX_{0}}
cannot be factored. However, under
\kappa_{\lambda}
, this flow simply continues to a smaller Birkhoff cell corresponding to the sequence of pivots required to perform Gaussian elimination on

{\rm e}^{tX_{0}}
. Thus the singularity is “resolved” and the flow may be continued to arbitrary times. A similar statement applies to the flows of the full Toda hierarchy. A consequence of this observation is that the meromorphic singularity structure of the Toda lattice solutions may be explicitly described as follows.

Proposition 4.6

(
[
11
]
)
.

Let
\Theta\subseteq\{1,\dots,n-1\}
be nonempty. There is a Laurent series solution of equations (
3.3
)–(
3.4
) having the form

\displaystyle b_{j}(t)=-\frac{\sigma_{j}^{\Theta}}{t^{2}}+\text{$($Taylor$)$ near}\ t=0,\qquad\text{if }j\in\Theta,

\displaystyle b_{j}(t)=\text{$($Taylor$)$ near}\ t=0,\qquad\text{if }j\not\in\Theta.

This solution depends on
2(n-1)-|\Theta|
free parameters.

Each type of series in the proposition is called a Painlevé balance
\Theta
of dimension equal to the number of free parameters.

Corollary 4.7

(
[
11
]
)
.

When
\Theta=\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, which is called the lowest balance, its dimension is
2(n-1)-(n-1)=n-1
.

Corollary 4.8

(
[
7
]
)
.

Restricted to the isospectral manifold
\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}
, the Toda flow,
{\rm e}^{\epsilon_{\lambda}t}\widehat{w}_{0}
,
passes through
\widehat{w}_{0}\mod B_{+}
as

, where
\widehat{w}_{0}
denotes the permutation matrix for the reverse permutation, i.e., the longest permutation

4.4
Centering the tridiagonal flows

In the remainder of this paper we will restrict attention to the tridiagonal flows we began with in Section

The phase shift formulas we describe do not depend on initial conditions. We have already seen this reflected in the fact (see Section

Representation theory and
\boldsymbol{\tau}
-functions

Let
G
denote the group
{\rm GL}(\ell+1)

(\rho_{n},V_{n})

\rho_{1}
is the

\rho_{1}(g)v=gv

\rho_{n}(g)v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{n}=gv_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge gv_{n}.

With respect to the standard basis
e_{1},\dots,e_{\ell+1}
of
\mathbb{C}^{\ell+1}

\langle e_{i_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{i_{n}},e_{j_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{j_{n}}\rangle=\delta_{i_{1},j_{1}}\cdots\delta_{i_{n},j_{n}}.

Set

and
v_{(n)}=e_{\ell-n+2}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{\ell+1}

We can now define the

\tau_{n}(t)=\big\langle\exp(tX_{0})v^{(n)},v^{(n)}\big\rangle,

which is just the
n^{\rm th}

.

The solution of the Toda lattice equations in Hessenberg form is then given
explicitly by

\displaystyle b_{n}(t)=b_{n}(0)\frac{\tau_{n-1}(t)\tau_{n+1}(t)}{\tau_{n}^{2}(t)},

(5.1)

\displaystyle a_{n}(t)=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\big(\log\tau_{n}(t)-\log\tau_{n-1}(t)\big).

6
Toda phase shift in terms of weighted path counting

We now come to our novel derivation of the phase shift formula for the continuous time Toda lattice. The original derivation was due to Moser
[
20
]
based on a continued fraction representation. Subsequently, Kostant
[
14
]
gave a fully Lie theoretic derivation. Here we present a direct combinatorial derivation based on the lemma of Gessel, Viennot and Lindström for enumerating weighted paths in directed graphs. We feel this approach is less
ad hoc
than Moser’s and less technical than Kostant’s while capturing the essential connection to the principal embedding through
\epsilon_{\lambda}
. This also makes it more amenable to ultradiscretization which is the main result of this paper and to future generalizations to other box ball systems.

The full details of our derivation are in the Appendices; this chapter just summarizes the essential results of that analysis, in Lemmas
6.3
and
6.4
, Corollary
6.5
, and shows how to use these to derive the continuous time phase shift. It is then straightforward, based on what we have already shown, to modify this formula for discrete time Toda (which is carried out in Section
6.2
).

Moser’s phase shift, in terms of the original Hamiltonian variables (see (
3.7
) and (
3.8
)), is given by

\beta_{n-k+1}^{+}=\beta_{k}^{-}+\sum_{j\neq k}\phi_{jk}(\alpha^{-}),

where

\displaystyle\phi_{jk}(\alpha^{-})=\begin{cases}\log(\alpha_{j}^{-}-\alpha_{k}^{-})^{2}&\text{for }j<k,\\
-\log(\alpha_{j}^{-}-\alpha_{k}^{-})^{2}&\text{for }j>k\end{cases}=\begin{cases}\log(\lambda_{n-k+1}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}&\text{for }j<k,\\
-\log(\lambda_{n-k+1}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}&\text{for }j>k.\end{cases}

We reformulate this in terms of the Hessenberg matrix form of Flaschka’s variables where it becomes equivalent to an elegant pairwise multiplicative formula for the sub-diagonal entries.

We have

b_{k}(-t)={\rm e}^{q_{k}(-t)-q_{k+1}(-t)}={\rm e}^{(\lambda_{n-k+1}-\lambda_{n-k})t}{\rm e}^{\beta_{k}^{-}-\beta_{k+1}^{-}+O({\rm e}^{-\delta t})},

and

b_{n-k}(t)={\rm e}^{q_{n-k}(t)-q_{n-k+1}(t)}={\rm e}^{(\lambda_{n-k+1}-\lambda_{n-k})t}{\rm e}^{\beta_{n-k}^{+}-\beta_{n-k+1}^{+}+O({\rm e}^{-\delta t})}.

Then consider

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}b_{n-k}(t)b_{k}(-t){\rm e}^{2(\lambda_{n-k}-\lambda_{n-k+1})t}={\rm e}^{(\beta_{n-k}^{+}-\beta_{k+1}^{-})-(\beta_{n-k+1}^{+}-\beta_{k}^{-})}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\exp\Bigg(\sum_{j\neq k+1}\phi_{j,k+1}(\alpha^{-})-\sum_{j\neq k}\phi_{j,k}(\alpha^{-})\Bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\exp\Bigg(\Bigg(\sum_{j<k+1}\log(\lambda_{n-k}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}-\sum_{j>k+1}\log(\lambda_{n-k}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad{}-\sum_{j<k}\log(\lambda_{n-k+1}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}+\sum_{j>k}\log(\lambda_{n-k+1}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\dfrac{\prod\limits_{j<k+1}(\lambda_{n-k}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}\prod\limits_{j>k}(\lambda_{n-k+1}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}}{\prod\limits_{j>k+1}(\lambda_{n-k}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}\prod\limits_{j<k}(\lambda_{n-k+1}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}}.

This leads us to the following analogue of the Moser’s phase shift formula for the off-diagonal entries, but in the Hessenberg setting:

Theorem 6.1
.

In the Hessenberg setting, the following asymptotic formula holds for the subdiagonal entries of the Toda flow:

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}b_{n-k}(t)b_{k}(-t){\rm e}^{2(\lambda_{n-k}-\lambda_{n-k+1})t}=\dfrac{\prod\limits_{j<k+1}\!(\lambda_{n-k}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}\!\prod\limits_{j>k}\!(\lambda_{n-k+1}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}}{\prod\limits_{j>k+1}\!(\lambda_{n-k}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}\!\prod\limits_{j<k}\!(\lambda_{n-k+1}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}}.\!\!\!

(6.1)

Although the analogy between this theorem and Moser’s original result is apparent, we will provide a novel proof for this result. This analysis proceeds from a direct deconstruction of
\tau
-functions appearing in the representation (
5.1
).

Recall that

\epsilon_{\lambda}=\left[\begin{matrix}\lambda_{1}&1\\
&\lambda_{2}&\ddots\\
&&\ddots&1\\
&&&\lambda_{n}\end{matrix}\right]=\epsilon+D_{\lambda},

where
D_{\lambda}=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
and, from Remark
4.5
, that it is diagonalised by the matrix

(U)_{ij}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\prod\limits_{i\leq m<j}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{m}}&\text{for }i\leq j,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

so that

\epsilon_{\lambda}=UD_{\lambda}U^{-1}.

We also recall here notation used by
[
21
]
for describing certain minor determinants of matrices:

Definition 6.2
.

For an
n\times n
matrix
X
and
1\leq k\leq m\leq n
, define

\Delta_{k}^{m}(X)=\det[x_{ij}]_{1\leq i\leq k,m-k+1\leq j\leq m},

where, by convention,
\Delta_{0}^{m}(X)
is taken to be
1
. In the particular case that
m=n
,
\Delta_{k}^{n}(X)
is the top-right
k\times k
minor determinant of
X
.

In proving Theorem
6.1
, the following three results will be key:

Lemma 6.3
.

\Delta_{k}^{n}(U)=\prod_{i=n-k+1}^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{n-k}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}}.

Lemma 6.4
.

(U^{-1})_{ij}=\begin{cases}0&i>j,\\
\displaystyle\prod_{i<m\leq j}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{m}}&i\leq j.\end{cases}

Corollary 6.5
.

\Delta_{k}^{n}(U^{-1})=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\prod_{i=k+1}^{n}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}}.

Corollary
6.5
will later be shown to follow from Lemmas
6.3
and
6.4
.

6.1
Proof of multiplicative formula for the classical Toda phase shift

We recall the level set parametrisation (
4.7
)

b_{0}:=\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\epsilon_{\lambda}^{i}t_{i}\right),

with
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\cdots>\lambda_{n}
,
define

L_{0}:=\Pi_{-}(b_{0}\widehat{w}_{0})
,
and let

X_{0}:=L_{0}^{-1}\epsilon_{\lambda}L_{0}

be the initial Hessenberg matrix.

Lemma 6.6
.

For each
k
, one has

b_{k}(0)=\dfrac{\Delta_{k+1}^{n}(b_{0})\Delta_{k-1}^{n}(b_{0})}{(\Delta_{k}^{n}(b_{0}))^{2}}.

Proof.

Define
B_{0}=\Pi_{+}(b_{0}\widehat{w}_{0})
, so that
b_{0}\widehat{w}_{0}=L_{0}B_{0}
. Then

\displaystyle B_{0}^{-1}X_{0}B_{0}

\displaystyle=(L_{0}B_{0})^{-1}\epsilon_{\lambda}L_{0}B_{0}=\widehat{w}_{0}^{-1}b_{0}^{-1}\epsilon_{\lambda}b_{0}\widehat{w}_{0}

\displaystyle=\widehat{w}_{0}^{-1}U{\rm e}^{-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n-1}t_{i}D_{\lambda}^{i}}U^{-1}UD_{\lambda}U^{-1}U{\rm e}^{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n-1}t_{i}D_{\lambda}^{i}}U^{-1}\widehat{w}_{0}

\displaystyle=\widehat{w}_{0}^{-1}UD_{\lambda}U^{-1}\widehat{w}_{0}=\widehat{w}_{0}^{-1}\epsilon_{\lambda}\widehat{w}_{0}.

From this, we see that

X_{0}=B_{0}\widehat{w}_{0}^{-1}\epsilon_{\lambda}\widehat{w}_{0}B_{0}^{-1}.

Splitting
\epsilon_{\lambda}=\epsilon+D_{\lambda}
, we study
\widehat{w}_{0}^{-1}\epsilon_{\lambda}\widehat{w}_{0}
as follows

\displaystyle\widehat{w}_{0}^{-1}\epsilon_{\lambda}\widehat{w}_{0}=\widehat{w}_{0}^{-1}\epsilon\widehat{w}_{0}+\widehat{w}_{0}^{-1}D_{\lambda}\widehat{w}_{0}.

By recalling that

\widehat{w}_{0}=\left[\begin{matrix}&&&&1\\
&&&1\\
&&1\\
&\iddots\\
1\end{matrix}\right],

one can check that
\widehat{w}_{0}^{-1}\epsilon_{\lambda}\widehat{w}_{0}=\hat{\epsilon}_{\lambda}
, where

\hat{\epsilon}_{\lambda}=\left[\begin{matrix}\lambda_{n}\\
1&\lambda_{n-1}\\
&1&\ddots\\
&&\ddots&\lambda_{2}\\
&&&1&\lambda_{1}\end{matrix}\right].

If one writes

B_{0}=\left[\begin{matrix}d_{1}&&*\\
&d_{2}\\
&&\cdots\\
&&&d_{n}\end{matrix}\right],

then

X_{0}=B_{0}\hat{\epsilon}_{\lambda}B_{0}^{-1}=\left[\begin{matrix}*&&&&*\\
d_{2}d_{1}^{-1}&*\\
&d_{3}d_{2}^{-1}&\ddots\\
&&\ddots&*&\\
&&&d_{n}d_{n-1}^{-1}&*\end{matrix}\right].

To conclude, we recall that the diagonal part of
L_{0}B_{0}
, which is
\operatorname{diag}(B_{0})
, is given by

d_{j}=\frac{\tau_{j}}{\tau_{j-1}},

where
\tau_{j}
is the
j\times j
principal minor of
b_{0}\widehat{w}_{0}
, hence
\tau_{j}=\Delta_{j}^{n}(b_{0})
.
∎

Remark 6.7
.

We note here that
b_{0}
is a totally positive element of
B_{+}
. (A matrix
b\in B_{+}
is defined to be
totally positive
with respect to
B_{+}
if every minor that does not identically vanish on
B_{+}
has a positive value.) The evident relation between principal minors of
b_{0}\widehat{w}_{0}
and upper-right minors of
b_{0}
reflects a deeper combinatorial relation studied by Lusztig
[
4
,
18
]
. This enables one to associate a kind of notion of positivity to elements of
\epsilon+\mathfrak{b}_{-}
(Hessenberg matrices).

Using the previous lemma and prior definitions we now proceed to expand the
\tau
-functions in terms of Plücker coordinates (minors) of
U
and
U^{-1}
. In this derivation we employ the refinement of the
LU
-decomposition to the
LDU
-decomposition for generic

:

g=[g]_{-}[g]_{0}[g]_{+},

where
[g]_{-}\in N_{-},[g]_{0}
is diagonal and
[g]_{+}\in N_{+}
, the subgroup of upper unipotent matrices.

We now proceed to the deconstruction of
\tau_{k}
with a brief explanation of the steps given at the end

\displaystyle\tau_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\big\langle{\rm e}^{tX_{0}}v^{(k)},v^{(k)}\big\rangle

(6.2)

\displaystyle=\big\langle L_{0}^{-1}U{\rm e}^{tD_{\lambda}}U^{-1}L_{0}v^{(k)},v^{(k)}\big\rangle

(6.3)

\displaystyle=\big\langle U{\rm e}^{tD_{\lambda}}U^{-1}[b_{0}\widehat{w}_{0}]_{-}v^{(k)},v^{(k)}\big\rangle

(6.4)

\displaystyle=\big\langle U{\rm e}^{tD_{\lambda}}U^{-1}b_{0}\widehat{w}_{0}[b_{0}\widehat{w}_{0}]_{+}^{-1}[b_{0}\widehat{w}_{0}]_{0}^{-1}v^{(k)},v^{(k)}\big\rangle

(6.5)

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k}d_{j}^{-1}\right)\big\langle U{\rm e}^{tD_{\lambda}}U^{-1}b_{0}\widehat{w}_{0}v^{(k)},v^{(k)}\big\rangle

(6.6)

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k}d_{j}^{-1}\right)\big\langle U{\rm e}^{tD_{\lambda}}U^{-1}b_{0}v_{(k)},v^{(k)}\big\rangle

(6.7)

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k}d_{j}^{-1}\right)\left\langle U{\rm e}^{tD_{\lambda}}U^{-1}\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\epsilon_{\lambda}^{i}t_{i}\right)v_{(k)},v^{(k)}\right\rangle

(6.8)

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k}d_{j}^{-1}\right)\left\langle U\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}D_{\lambda}^{i}(t_{i}+\delta_{i1}t)\right)U^{-1}v_{(k)},v^{(k)}\right\rangle.

(6.9)

In (
6.3
), the diagonalization (
4.4
) was applied to
X_{0}
to express it as
X_{0}=L_{0}^{-1}UD_{\lambda}U^{-1}L_{0}
. In step (
6.4
),
L_{0}^{-1}
is transposed to the right-hand side of the bracket, where it is an upper unipotent matrix acting on the highest weight vector
v^{(k)}
and therefore has no effect. Similarly, from its definition,
L_{0}
may be replaced by
[b_{0}\widehat{w}_{0}]_{-}
which is then rewritten in (
6.5
) using the
LDU
-decomposition. This then contracts to (
6.6
) where
d_{j}
is the
j
-th entry along the diagonal of
[b_{0}\widehat{w}_{0}]_{0}
. The reverse permutation
\widehat{w}_{0}
moves the highest weight vector to the lowest weight vector in (
6.7
). Finally, the last two steps follow from the definition of
b_{0}
and the diagonalization of
\epsilon_{\lambda}
, respectively.

By a well-known result from linear algebra (see, for example,
[
22
]
),
d_{j}
is given by

d_{j}=\dfrac{\Delta_{j}^{n}(b_{0})}{\Delta_{j-1}^{n}(b_{0})}.

Thus, the factor in front of the inner product is equal to
(\Delta_{k}^{n}(b_{0}))^{-1}
.

By the Cauchy–Binet formula, we get

\displaystyle\tau_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\Delta_{k}^{n}(b_{0})}\sum_{S\in{[n]\choose k}}\det\left(U\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}D_{\lambda}^{i}(t_{i}+\delta_{i1}t)\right)\right)_{[k],S}\det\big(U^{-1}\big)_{S,]k[}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\Delta_{k}^{n}(b_{0})}\sum_{S\in{[n]\choose k}}\exp\left(\sum_{s\in S}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{s}^{i}(t_{i}+\delta_{i1}t)\right)\det(U_{[k],S})\det\big(U^{-1}_{S,]k[}\big),

where
]k[{}:=\{n-k+1,n-k+2,\dots,n\}
.

There are two limits we wish to consider for the
\tau
-functions in what follows. One of these is the limit in which
t
tends to infinity while the eigenvalues,
\lambda_{i}
remain fixed and the other is the tropical limit in the eigenvalues while
t
is held fixed. In the former case, given the ordering
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\cdots>\lambda_{n}
, the term for
S=[k]
dominates as
t\to+\infty
, and the term for
S={}]k[
dominates as
t\to-\infty
. In the latter case, these same terms dominate for
t
fixed sufficiently positive and, respectively, for
t
fixed sufficiently negative. The only wrinkle in the latter case is that the minors
\det(U_{[k],S})
and

\det\big(U^{-1}_{S,]k[}\big)
depend on the eigenvalues. However, the growth of these terms is algebraic and may be crudely estimated to be no worse than
\lambda_{1}^{2k}
in one direction and
\lambda_{n}^{2k}
in the other. This algebraic growth is beaten by the exponential decay of the time-dependent factors in the tropical limit once the exponential factor associated to
S=[k]
, respectively for
S={}]k[
has been factored out of the Cauchy–Binet sum. Since in both the
t
and the tropical limits it is the same single term that dominates, the two limits manifestly commute.

Focusing now on the
t
limit, we let
\tau_{k}^{+}
and
\tau_{k}^{-}
denote these terms, respectively. By Lemma
6.3
and Corollary
6.5
, we have

\tau_{k}^{+}=\dfrac{1}{\Delta_{k}^{n}(b_{0})}\exp\left(\sum_{s=1}^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{s}^{i}(t_{i}+\delta_{i1}t)\right)\prod_{j=1}^{k}\prod_{i=k+1}^{n}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}}+o(1)

and

\tau_{k}^{-}=\dfrac{1}{\Delta_{k}^{n}(b_{0})}\exp\left(\sum_{s=n-k+1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{s}^{i}(t_{i}-\delta_{i1}t)\right)\prod_{i=n-k+1}^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{n-k}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}}+o(1).

As
t\to+\infty
, we therefore have

b_{k}(-t)\sim b_{k}^{-}:=b_{k}(0)\dfrac{\tau_{k+1}^{-}\tau_{k-1}^{-}}{(\tau_{k}^{-})^{2}},\qquad b_{n-k}(t)\sim b_{n-k}^{+}:=b_{n-k}(0)\dfrac{\tau_{n-k+1}^{+}\tau_{n-k-1}^{+}}{(\tau_{n-k}^{+})^{2}}.

Plugging in, and writing
\Delta_{j}
for
\Delta_{j}^{n}(b_{0})
, we have

\displaystyle b_{k}^{-}=b_{k}(0)\dfrac{(\Delta_{k})^{2}}{\Delta_{k-1}\Delta_{k+1}}\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\lambda_{n-k}^{i}-\lambda_{n-k+1}^{i})(t_{i}-\delta_{i1}t)\right)\times\Upsilon_{k}^{-},

where

\Upsilon_{k}^{-}=\dfrac{\left(\prod\limits_{i=n-k}^{n}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n-k-1}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}}\right)\left(\prod\limits_{i=n-k+2}^{n}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n-k+1}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}}\right)}{\left(\prod\limits_{i=n-k+1}^{n}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n-k}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}}\right)^{2}}

and

\displaystyle b_{n-k}^{+}=b_{n-k}(0)\dfrac{(\Delta_{n-k})^{2}}{\Delta_{n-k-1}\Delta_{n-k+1}}\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\lambda_{n-k+1}^{i}-\lambda_{n-k}^{i})(t_{i}+\delta_{i1}t)\right)\times\Upsilon_{n-k}^{+},

where

\Upsilon_{n-k}^{+}=\dfrac{\left(\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n-k+1}\prod\limits_{i=n-k+2}^{n}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}}\right)\left(\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n-k-1}\prod\limits_{i=n-k}^{n}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}}\right)}{\left(\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n-k}\prod\limits_{i=n-k+1}^{n}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}}\right)^{2}}.

Simplifying
\Upsilon_{k}^{-}
and
\Upsilon_{n-k}^{+}
, one has

\Upsilon_{k}^{-}=\dfrac{\left[\prod\limits_{i<n-k+1}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{n-k+1})\right]\left[\prod\limits_{i>n-k}(\lambda_{n-k}-\lambda_{i})\right]}{\left[\prod\limits_{i<n-k}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{n-k})\right]\left[\prod\limits_{i>n-k+1}(\lambda_{n-k+1}-\lambda_{i})\right]},

and

\Upsilon_{n-k}^{+}=\dfrac{\left[\prod\limits_{i>n-k}(\lambda_{n-k}-\lambda_{i})\right]\left[\prod\limits_{i<n-k+1}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{n-k+1})\right]}{\left[\prod\limits_{i>n-k+1}(\lambda_{n-k+1}-\lambda_{i})\right]\left[\prod\limits_{i<n-k}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{n-k})\right]}.

We see that
\Upsilon_{k}^{-}=\Upsilon_{n-k}^{+}
. Thus, the product
\Upsilon_{k}^{-}\Upsilon_{n-k}^{+}
simplifies to

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{k}^{-}\Upsilon_{n-k}^{+}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\left[\prod\limits_{i>n-k}(\lambda_{n-k}-\lambda_{i})^{2}\right]\left[\prod\limits_{i<n-k+1}(\lambda_{n-k+1}-\lambda_{i})^{2}\right]}{\left[\prod\limits_{i<n-k}(\lambda_{n-k}-\lambda_{i})^{2}\right]\left[\prod\limits_{i>n-k+1}(\lambda_{n-k+1}-\lambda_{i})^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\left[\prod\limits_{j<k+1}(\lambda_{n-k}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}\right]\left[\prod\limits_{j>k}(\lambda_{n-k+1}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}\right]}{\left[\prod\limits_{j>k+1}(\lambda_{n-k}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}\right]\left[\prod\limits_{j<k}(\lambda_{n-k+1}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}\right]}.

We also observe that

\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\lambda_{n-k}^{i}-\lambda_{n-k+1}^{i}\big)(t_{i}-\delta_{i1}t)\right)\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\lambda_{n-k+1}^{i}-\lambda_{n-k}^{i}\big)(t_{i}+\delta_{i1}t)\right)

is equal to

\displaystyle\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\lambda_{n-k}^{i}-\lambda_{n-k+1}^{i}\big)([t_{i}-\delta_{1i}]-[t_{i}+\delta_{i1}t])\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}-2\delta_{i1}t\big(\lambda_{n-k}^{i}-\lambda_{n-k+1}^{i}\big)\right)

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{2(\lambda_{n-k+1}-\lambda_{n-k})t}.

Putting all of the above together, one obtains

\displaystyle b_{k}^{-}b_{n-k}^{+}{\rm e}^{2(\lambda_{n-k}-\lambda_{n-k+1})t}=\dfrac{b_{k}(0)b_{n-k}(0)(\Delta_{k}\Delta_{n-k})^{2}}{\Delta_{k-1}\Delta_{k+1}\Delta_{n-k-1}\Delta_{n-k+1}}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad{}\times\dfrac{\left[\prod\limits_{j<k+1}(\lambda_{n-k}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}\right]\left[\prod\limits_{j>k}(\lambda_{n-k+1}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}\right]}{\left[\prod\limits_{j>k+1}(\lambda_{n-k}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}\right]\left[\prod\limits_{j<k}(\lambda_{n-k+1}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\dfrac{\left[\prod\limits_{j<k+1}(\lambda_{n-k}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}\right]\left[\prod\limits_{j>k}(\lambda_{n-k+1}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}\right]}{\left[\prod\limits_{j>k+1}(\lambda_{n-k}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}\right]\left[\prod\limits_{j<k}(\lambda_{n-k+1}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}\right]},

where the second equality holds by Lemma
6.6
, which completes the proof of Theorem
6.1
.

6.2
The discrete-time Toda lattice phase shift (dToda)

We now turn to proving the analogue of equation (
6.1
) for the discrete-time Toda lattice. In doing this we again emphasize that
t
will be discrete (
t\in\mathbb{Z}
) and that the eigenvalues of
X_{0}
are taken to be positive and distinct.

Theorem 6.8
.

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}b_{n-k}(t)b_{k}(-t)\left(\dfrac{\lambda_{n-k}}{\lambda_{n-k+1}}\right)^{2t}=\dfrac{\prod\limits_{j<k+1}(\lambda_{n-k}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}\prod\limits_{j>k}(\lambda_{n-k+1}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}}{\prod\limits_{j>k+1}(\lambda_{n-k}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}\prod\limits_{j<k}(\lambda_{n-k+1}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}}.\!\!\!

(6.10)

This essentially comes down to the analogous description of the subdiagonal entries in this setting:

b_{k}(t)=\dfrac{\Delta_{k+1}^{k+1}\big(X_{0}^{t}\big)\Delta_{k-1}^{k-1}\big(X_{0}^{t}\big)}{\big(\Delta_{k}^{k}\big(X_{0}^{t}\big)\big)^{2}}b_{k}(0).

In the above, we recall that
\Delta_{k}^{k}
is the
k\times k
principal minor determinant of a matrix. When one takes this into our proof of Moser’s continuous-time setting, the only difference is that the
{\rm e}^{t\epsilon_{\lambda}}
is replaced with
\epsilon_{\lambda}^{t}
.

When we compute these principal minor determinants, we still use
U
to diagonalise, we still have
[b_{0}\widehat{w}_{0}]_{-}
, everything is the same, except that we have
D_{\lambda}^{t}
instead of
{\rm e}^{D_{\lambda}t}
.

7
BBS phase shift

We use the basic properties of tropicalisation to tropicalise equation (
6.10
):

\lim_{t\to+\infty}b_{n-k}(t)b_{k}(-t)\left(\dfrac{\lambda_{n-k}}{\lambda_{n-k+1}}\right)^{2t}=\dfrac{\prod\limits_{j<k+1}(\lambda_{n-k}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}\prod\limits_{j>k}(\lambda_{n-k+1}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}}{\prod\limits_{j>k+1}(\lambda_{n-k}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}\prod\limits_{j<k}(\lambda_{n-k+1}-\lambda_{n-j+1})^{2}}.

We first recall that the phase space of dToda consists of tridiagonal Hessenberg matrices and we write a solution as

X(t)=\left[\begin{matrix}a_{1}(t)&1\\
b_{1}(t)&a_{2}(t)&\ddots\\
&\ddots&\ddots&1\\
&&b_{n-1}(t)&a_{n}(t)\end{matrix}\right].

(7.1)

Thus, the
b
’s are the subdiagonal entries of a solution at a given time.

It is important to recall that Tokihiro’s
[
28
]
tropicalisation is performed on the variables in the lower-upper factorisation description of dToda which we briefly recall from Sections
3.3
and
3.5
:

R(t)=\left[\begin{matrix}I_{1}^{t}&1\\
&I_{2}^{t}&\ddots\\
&&\ddots&1\\
&&&I_{n}^{t}\end{matrix}\right],\qquad L(t)=\left[\begin{matrix}1\\
V_{1}^{t}&1\\
&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&V_{n-1}^{t}&1\end{matrix}\right].

This evolves under the factorisation dynamics

L(t+1)R(t+1)=R(t)L(t).

By setting

X(t)=L(t)R(t)
,
one obtains a matrix of the form in (
7.1
). A quick comparison reveals

b_{k}(t)=V_{k}^{t}I_{k}^{t}

for all
k\in\{1,2,\dots,n-1\}
.

By Tokihiro’s calculation,
V_{k}^{t}
corresponds to the
k
-th finite gap and
I_{k}^{t}
corresponds to the
k
-th block size, each at time
t
. Therefore, the tropical analogue of
b_{k}(t)
is

W_{k}^{t}+Q_{k}^{t}
.

The other components in equation (
6.10
) to deal with are the eigenvalues. In the limit as
t\to+\infty
, we know that the matrix in (
7.1
) is

X(t)=\left[\begin{matrix}\lambda_{1}&1\\
&\lambda_{2}&\ddots\\
&&\ddots&1\\
&&&\lambda_{n}\end{matrix}\right].

We also know that
L(t)
limits to the identity matrix, so we know that
\lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}I_{k}^{t}=\lambda_{k}
for each
k
. Since the tropicalisation of
I_{k}^{t}
is
Q_{k}^{t}
(the
k
-th block size at time
t
), the tropical analogue of
\lambda_{k}
is

\lim_{t\to+\infty}Q_{k}^{t}=\mu_{k},

where

\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}<\cdots<\mu_{n}

are the sorted asymptotic block sizes of the BBS in forwards time.

Recalling that
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\cdots>\lambda_{n}>0
, we compute the tropicalisation of
(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})^{2}
for each pair
i\neq j
. For the sake of this calculation, assume
i<j
so that
\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}>0
. To detropicalise this quantity, we let
\lambda_{i}={\rm e}^{-\frac{1}{\hbar}\mu_{i}}
and
\lambda_{j}={\rm e}^{-\frac{1}{\hbar}\mu_{j}}
and compute

8
Comparison with the literature

Tokihiro and collaborators
[
29
]
have studied a related advanced version of the box-ball system in which balls are labelled and given priority in their movements based on the labels. Like the classic box-ball system, the advanced box-ball system also exhibits the solitonic scattering phenomenon, and they describe a soliton scattering rule that shows how the ball labels are permuted within the solitonic blocks in both forwards and backwards asymptotic time. Instead of the usual discrete-time Toda lattice with tridiagonal Hessenberg phase space, the advanced box-ball system is obtained via ultradiscretisation of the so-called
hungry Toda molecule equation
. This tropical correspondence allowed the dynamics and scattering rules of the advanced box-ball system to be understood by detropicalising, passing through the hungry Toda dynamics, and then re-tropicalising back to an advanced box-ball configuration. This, when restricted to just one box label, reduces to classical dToda and the classical box-ball system.

For the restriction to dToda and classical BBS,
[
29
]

observed
that the phase shift phenomenon decomposes into pairwise individual 2-soliton phase interactions and used this observation to justify restricting their attention to just 2-soliton phase shifts. However, this observation does not seem to be proved. With our approach, however, we do not make this assumption or observation
a priori
. Instead, we take an
n
-soliton configuration and prove an overall phase shift formula (equation (
7.9
)) which we recall below:

\hat{\Phi}_{i}=\sum_{j<i}2\mu_{j}-\sum_{j>i}2\mu_{i}

and, just as Moser did in
[
20
]
, we can interpret the formula as saying that one can think of the overall phase shift phenomenon as decomposing into pairwise phase shift interactions, even though we do not assume that only two blocks collide at a time. However, a major point to be made here is that one can easily construct a box-ball configuration in which multiple collisions occur simultaneously, and our proof here does not depend on assuming that such a multi-collision does not occur; the proof applies to such configurations.

Subsequent to the completion of this paper, we were made aware of another more recent approach to scattering properties of box-ball systems based on a purely combinatorial method known as the Kerov–Kirillov–Reshetikhin bijection. The works of
[
15
,
25
]
use this bijection to construct action-angle coordinates on (generalised) BBS, involving Young tableaux with
riggings
. This yields a linearisation of the BBS dynamics in terms of which the asymptotic behavior of the soliton blocks can be determined. It may be of interest in future studies to compare this approach with the diagrammatic phase shift analysis performed in this paper.

Lemma A.1

(Gessel–Viennot–Lindström)
.

Let

where

for some

such that

and

have no vertices in common for

Of particular interest to us will be path graphs of the following type:

(1,1)
(1,2)
(1,3)
(1,4)
(1,4)
(2,1)
(2,2)
(2,3)
(2,4)
(2,4)
(3,1)
(3,2)
(3,3)
(3,4)
(3,4)
(4,1)
(4,1)
(4,2)
(4,2)
(4,3)
(4,3)
(4,4)
(4,4)
(4,4)

In general, these will be graphs whose vertices form an

the vertices are labelled

for

, with

Lemma A.2
.

If one has a

\mathcal{A}=\{(n,a_{i})\colon 1\leq i\leq k\}
and a
k

\mathcal{B}=\{(b_{i},n)\colon 1\leq i\leq k\}

We assume

To complete the proof, we show that if
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{i}\colon(n,a_{i})\to(b_{\sigma(i)},n),\,1\leq i\leq k\}

\sigma(i)=i

\sigma(j+1)=j+1

The argument is the analogous to the base case: suppose

\sigma(j+1)>j+1

Lemma B.1
.

For all

\prod_{1\leq m<j}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{m}}=\sum_{m=1}^{j}\dfrac{\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{m}}\left(\prod_{1\leq p<m}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{p}}\right)\prod_{m\leq l<j}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{l}}.

Proof.

By multiplying both sides by

\prod_{1\leq i<j}(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{i})(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}),

the above is equivalent to showing

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{j-1}(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{j}\dfrac{\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{m}}\left(\prod_{p=m}^{j-1}(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{p})\right)\left(\prod_{l=1}^{m-1}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{l})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{j}\left(\prod_{p=m+1}^{j}(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{p})\right)\left(\prod_{l=1}^{m-1}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{l})\right).

This holds trivially for

(by convention for the empty product). For

\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{1}=(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{2})\times 1+1\times(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}).

We proceed by induction: suppose the above identity holds for each

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m=1}^{j-2}\left(\prod\limits_{p=m+1}^{j}(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{p})\right)\left(\prod\limits_{l=1}^{m-1}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{l})\right)+\prod\limits_{l=1}^{j-1}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{l})+(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{j})\prod\limits_{l=1}^{j-2}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{l})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m=1}^{j-2}\left(\prod\limits_{p=m+1}^{j}(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{p})\right)\left(\prod\limits_{l=1}^{m-1}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{l})\right)+(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{j-1}+\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{j})\prod\limits_{l=1}^{j-2}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{l})

\displaystyle=(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{j-1})\left[\sum_{m=1}^{j-2}\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=m+1\\
p\neq j-1\end{subarray}}^{j}(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{p})\right)\left(\prod_{l=1}^{m-1}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{l})\right)+\prod_{l=1}^{j-2}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{l})\right]

\displaystyle=(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{j-1})\prod_{i=1}^{j-2}(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{i})=\prod_{i=1}^{j-1}(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{i}),

where the penultimate equality was the induction hypothesis applied to the eigenvalues

\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{j-2},\lambda_{j},\dots,\lambda_{n}.

\hline\cr 0&1\end{array}\right],\qquad V=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}V_{[n-1],[n-1]}&\vec{v}_{n}\\
\hline\cr 0&1\end{array}\right],

where

(\vec{u}_{n})_{i}=\prod_{i\leq m<n}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{m}},\qquad i=1,2,\dots,n-1,

and

(\vec{v}_{n})_{i}=\prod_{i<m\leq n}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{m}},\qquad i=1,2,\dots,n-1.

U(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})\times V=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}U(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n-1})V_{[n-1],[n-1]}&U(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n-1})\vec{v}_{n}+\vec{u}_{n}\\

Thus, we see that an induction would be appropriate here: if we have shown the result for

The

0=\prod_{i\leq m<n}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{m}}+\sum_{j=i}^{n-1}\left(\prod\limits_{i\leq p<j}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{p}}\right)\left(\prod_{j<q\leq n}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{q}}\right),

which needs to be shown for each

Viewing the right-hand side as a rational function of
\lambda_{n}

\prod_{i\leq m<n}\dfrac{1}{x-\lambda_{m}}.

We set up a partial fraction decomposition:

\prod_{i\leq m<n}\dfrac{1}{x-\lambda_{m}}=\sum_{j=i}^{n-1}\dfrac{c_{j}}{x-\lambda_{j}}.

We multiply both sides by
\prod_{i\leq m<n}(x-\lambda_{m})

m\neq j\end{subarray}}(x-\lambda_{m}).

Plugging in

, we see that

c_{j}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\leq m<n\\
m\neq j\end{subarray}}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{m})=1

for each
j
. Hence,

c_{j}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\leq m<n\\
m\neq j\end{subarray}}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{m}}=\left(\prod\limits_{i\leq p<j}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{p}}\right)\left(\prod_{j<q<n}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{q}}\right).

To conclude, we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{j=i}^{n-1}\left(\prod\limits_{i\leq p<j}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{p}}\right)\left(\prod_{j<q<n}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{q}}\right)\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{j}}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{j=i}^{n-1}\left(\prod\limits_{i\leq p<j}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{p}}\right)\left(\prod_{j<q<n}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{q}}\right)\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{n}}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{j=i}^{n-1}\left(\prod\limits_{i\leq p<j}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{p}}\right)\left(\prod_{j<q\leq n}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{q}}\right).

This completes the induction, and therefore proves Lemma
6.4
.
∎

B.3
Proving Corollary
6.5

Lemma B.2
.

Let
V
be the matrix as in Lemma
6.4
:

(V)_{ij}=\begin{cases}0,&i>j,\\
\displaystyle\prod_{i<m\leq j}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{m}},&i\leq j.\end{cases}

Then

V_{ij}=\widehat{w}_{0}U(\lambda_{n},\dots,\lambda_{1})^{\rm T}\widehat{w}_{0}.

Proof.

Recall how we defined
U(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
:

(U)_{ij}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\prod\limits_{i\leq m<j}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{m}}&\text{for }i\leq j,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Therefore,

(U(\lambda_{n},\dots,\lambda_{1}))_{ij}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\prod\limits_{i\leq m<j}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{n-j+1}-\lambda_{n-m+1}}&\text{for }i\leq j,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We now prove that
V=\widehat{w}_{0}U(\lambda_{n},\dots,\lambda_{1})^{\rm T}\widehat{w}_{0}
by direct calculation:

\displaystyle\big(\widehat{w}_{0}U(\lambda_{n},\dots,\lambda_{1})^{\rm T}\widehat{w}_{0}\big)_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{n}(\widehat{w}_{0})_{il}\big(U(\lambda_{n},\dots,\lambda_{1})^{\rm T}\widehat{w}_{0}\big)_{lj}=\big(U(\lambda_{n},\dots,\lambda_{1})^{\rm T}\widehat{w}_{0}\big)_{n-i+1,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{n}\big(U(\lambda_{n},\dots,\lambda_{1})^{\rm T}\big)_{n-i+1,m}(\widehat{w}_{0})_{m,j}

\displaystyle=\big(U(\lambda_{n},\dots,\lambda_{1})^{\rm T}\big)_{n-i+1,n-j+1}=(U(\lambda_{n},\dots,\lambda_{1}))_{n-j+1,n-i+1}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\prod\limits_{n-j+1\leq m<n-i+1}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{n-m+1}}&\text{if }i\leq j,\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\prod\limits_{i<m\leq j}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{m}}&\text{for }i\leq j,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

which is indeed
(V)_{ij}
.
∎

Since
\widehat{w}_{0}^{\rm T}=\widehat{w}_{0}
, the above result can be equivalently expressed as

U(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})^{-1}=(\widehat{w}_{0}U(\lambda_{n},\dots,\lambda_{1})\widehat{w}_{0})^{\rm T}.

We now use this lemma to prove Corollary
6.5
:

Proof.

Since
V=U(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})^{-1}
,
\Delta_{k}^{n}(V)=\Delta_{k}^{n}\big(U^{-1}\big)
can be computed as follows

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}^{n}(V)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{k}^{n}\big(\widehat{w}_{0}U(\lambda_{n},\dots,\lambda_{1})^{\rm T}\widehat{w}_{0}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\in{[n]\choose k}}\det((\widehat{w}_{0})_{[k],S})\det\big((U(\lambda_{n},\dots,\lambda_{1})^{\rm T}\widehat{w}_{0})_{S,]k[}\big)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\det\big((U(\lambda_{n},\dots,\lambda_{1})^{\rm T}\widehat{w}_{0})_{]k[,]k[}\big)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\sum_{S\in{[n]\choose k}}\det\big(U(\lambda_{n},\dots,\lambda_{1})^{\rm T}\big)_{]k[,S})\det((\widehat{w}_{0})_{S,]k[})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{2\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\det\big(U(\lambda_{n},\dots,\lambda_{1})^{\rm T}\big)_{]k[,[k]})

\displaystyle=\det((U(\lambda_{n},\dots,\lambda_{1}))_{[k],]k[}).

Therefore, by Lemma
6.3
, we can deduce

\Delta_{k}^{n}(U^{-1})=\Delta_{k}^{n}(V)=\prod_{j=n-k+1}^{n}\prod_{i=1}^{n-k}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{n-j+1}-\lambda_{n-i+1}}=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\prod_{i=k+1}^{n}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}},

thus, completing the proof of Corollary
6.5
.
∎
