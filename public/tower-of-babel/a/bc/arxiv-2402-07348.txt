Carleman estimates for higher step Grushin operators
Hendrik De Bie, Pan Lian

Abstract
. The higher step Grushin operators Δ α \Delta_{\alpha} are a family of sub-elliptic operators which degenerate on a sub-manifold of ℝ n + m \mathbb{R}^{n+m} . This paper establishes Carleman-type inequalities for these operators. It is achieved by deriving a weighted L p − L q L^{p}-L^{q} estimate for the Grushin-harmonic projector. The crucial ingredient in the proof is the addition formula for Gegenbauer polynomials due to T. Koornwinder and Y. Xu. As a consequence, we obtain the strong unique continuation property for the Schrödinger operators − Δ α + V -\Delta_{\alpha}+V at points of the degeneracy manifold, where V V belongs to certain L loc r ​ ( ℝ n + m ) L^{r}_{{\rm loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m}) .

Primary 35H20, 35B60; Secondary 33C45

1.
Introduction

The study of unique continuation properties for partial differential operators traces back to Carleman’s seminal work
[
14
]
in 1939. Carleman proved that the time independent Schrödinger operator
\mathcal{H}=-\Delta+V
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with potential
V\in L^{\infty}_{{\rm loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, has the strong unique continuation property. Namely,
u\equiv 0
is the unique solution to
\mathcal{H}u=0
that has infinite order of vanishing at a point in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Since then, many unique continuation results have been established for elliptic operators. This development culminated with Jerison and Kenig’s celebrated result
[
35
]
, which establishes the strong unique continuation property for
\mathcal{H}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n},n\geq 3
, where
V\in L_{{\rm loc}}^{n/2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. The consideration of unbounded potentials
V
is prompted by situations of physical interests. Independent of the Carleman’s estimates, an alternative geometric approach for unique continuation was developed by Garofalo and Lin around 1986 in their profound works
[
22
,
23
]
. Their method is based on local doubling properties proved by the Almgren monotonicity formula. There exists extensive literature on unique continuation. For a comprehensive overview, we refer e.g. to the survey
[
46
]
and the references therein.

The unique continuation property for sub-elliptic operators is, in general, not true. A detailed understanding of the limitations or exceptions to this property is crucial for the broader comprehension of the behavior of sub-elliptic operators. Bahouri in
[
4
]
showed that unique continuation does not hold even for smooth and compactly supported perturbations of the sub-Laplacian on the Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. The closely related sub-elliptic operator (see
[
21
]
for their connection), known as the Grushin operator
\Delta_{\alpha}
, is defined by

(1.1)

\Delta_{\alpha}:=\Delta_{x}+|x|^{2\alpha}\Delta_{y},\qquad(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m},\,\alpha>0,

where
\Delta_{x}
is the ordinary Laplace operator on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
This operator was first introduced by Baoendi in
[
7
]
and later studied by Grushin in
[
29
,
30
]
, where hypoellipticity was obtained when
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
. The subclass with
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{1\}
is now commonly called the higher step Grushin operator. Geometrically, the higher step
\Delta_{\alpha}
arises via a submersion from a sub-Laplace operator on a nilpotent Lie group of step
\alpha+1
, see e.g.
[
9
]
. Over the last years,
many classical problems in harmonic analysis, such as restriction estimates and spectral multipliers theorems, have been
successfully addressed in the Grushin framework, see e.g.
[
15
,
16
,
36
]
. Moreover, the Grushin operator also has
fruitful connections with other areas of study, such as the obstacle and extension problems for the fractional Laplacian, see e.g. the influential works
[
12
,
13
]
.

In
[
21
]
, Garofalo initiated the study of strong unique continuation for zero-order perturbations of the Grushin operator, i.e.
-\Delta_{\alpha}+V
, where
\alpha>0
and the potential
V
is subjected to the condition

(1.2)

|V(x,y)|\leq C\psi(x,y).

Here
\psi
is the so-called angle function, which degenerates on
\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, see the subsequent formula (
2.29
) for its definition. Note that the condition (
1.2
) forces the potential
V
to vanish at
x=0
. Therefore, the permissible potentials are merely a subset of
L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}
. The results in
[
21
]
were subsequently extended to variable
coefficients Grushin operators in
[
27
]
.

Inspired by Jerison’s work
[
34
]
, Garofalo and Shen in 1994 established a delicate
L^{p}-L^{q}
Carleman estimate and thus obtained the strong continuation property in
[
25
]
for the significant specific case where
n\geq 2
,
m=1
and
\alpha=1
, i.e.

\Delta_{x}u+|x|^{2}\partial_{t}^{2}u=Vu,\qquad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R},

when
V\in L_{{\rm loc}}^{r}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\right)
, with

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}r>n,&\hbox{if $n$ is even;}\\
r>2n^{2}/(n+1),&\hbox{if $n$ is odd.}\end{array}\right.

Notably, the condition (
1.2
) in terms of the degenerate weight
\psi
is removed. The unresolved cases with
\alpha\neq 1
and
m\neq 1
are more challenging. Considerable efforts have been devoted to this problem over the last years, see for instance the work of Garofalo and his collaborators
[
5
,
6
]
. Moreover, as was pointed out in
[
6
]
, the main difficulty is to establish a suitable estimate for the Grushin-harmonic projector, which will be addressed in subsequent Section
4.2
. On the other hand, it is known by many researchers that even for the specific cases with
\alpha=1
and
m>1
, i.e. the unique continuation property for equations

\Delta_{x}u+|x|^{2}\Delta_{y}u=Vu,\qquad(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m},

takes on significance. For instance, it will lead to interesting applications to unique continuation properties for the sub-Laplacians on
H
-type groups, see e.g.
[
24
, Section 6]
and
[
26
]
.

In our work, following Garofalo and Shen
[
25
]
, we establish Carleman estimates for the zero-order perturbations of higher step Grushin operators, which lead to the strong unique continuation property for the Schrödinger operators
-\Delta_{\alpha}+V
, where
V
is in certain
L^{r}_{{\rm loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})
. Thus the restriction in terms of the degenerate weight
\psi
in (
1.2
) is successfully removed. Interestingly, although the degeneracy of
\Delta_{\alpha}
becomes increasingly stronger as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
, the strong unique continuation property still holds for
-\Delta_{\alpha}+V
at points of
\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
for all
V\in L^{\infty}_{{\rm loc}}
. It is worth noting that we were also able to remove the distinction between even and odd dimensions
n
in (
1
) from
[
25
]
. Consequently, the results in
[
25
]
for odd dimensions are improved. We collect the exponents
p
and
q
in Carleman estimates and the potential spaces valid for strong unique continuation in Table
1
.

Now we discuss the main novelty in our derivation of the Carleman estimates.

(I)
Orthogonal basis for Grushin-harmonic polynomials

The first key step is the explicit construction of an orthogonal basis for Grushin-harmonic polynomials in terms of Jacobi polynomials. The structure of Grushin spherical harmonics became clear when we recognized the underlying
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
structure through the connection between the Grushin operator and the so-called radially deformed Laplace operator, which arises from the study of the minimal representations of
{\rm O}(n,2)
in
[
10
]
, see Section
3.3
. The significant
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
structure allows us to generalize classical results in complex and harmonic analysis, such as Fischer decomposition, to the Grushin setting. Additionally, it is found that the Grushin-harmonics are also related to the theory of the Dunkl operator of rank 1 in
[
19
]
. When
\alpha=0
, we recover the addition formula for Gegenbauer polynomials, which was originally established by to Koornwinder in
[
37
]
and later generalized by Xu in
[
47
]
.

(II)
Estimates for Grushin-harmonic projection operator

The
L^{1}-L^{\infty}
estimates for the Grushin-harmonic projection operator are
derived using the aforementioned addition formula for Gegenbauer polynomials. The weighted
L^{2}-L^{2}
estimates are reduced to uniform estimates for the weighted
L^{2}
norms of Jacobi polynomials. The latter is achieved via two different approaches: by the Bernstein-type inequality recently obtained in
[
31
]
and by the connection formula for Jacobi polynomials respectively. Our proof is concise, elementary, and distinctly different from the method of Garofalo and Shen in
[
25
, Lemma 4.3]
. This part is the main contribution of this paper. Moreover, we provide a tighter weighted
L^{2}-L^{2}
estimate for the higher step Grushin-harmonic projector on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

We would like to mention that the weighted
L^{2}
-norms of Jacobi and Gegenbauer polynomials frequently appear in both physical and mathematical problems, for instance in the explicit computation of angular momentum, the generalization of Stolarsky’s invariance principle to projective spaces, and determinantal point processes, see e.g.
[
1
,
11
]
. In the present framework, the uniform norm estimates needed shall be viewed as a refined version of Askey’s famous transplantation theorem in the
L^{2}
setting
[
3
]
.

Finally, combining the estimates obtained for projection operators in Section 4 with the Sobolev inequality for the Grushin operator of
[
43
]
, we seamlessly derive the Carleman estimates and the strong unique continuation property. The proof follows without significant difficulties from the approach developed in Jerison’s work
[
34
]
, which was later refined by Garofalo and Shen in the sub-elliptic setting
[
25
]
.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows: In Section

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Grushin operator

In this subsection, we go through basic notions for Grushin operators. More details can be found in e.g.
[
17
,
21
,
41
]
.

The Grushin space, denoted by
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}=\{(x,y):x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\}
,
is the Carnot-Carathéodory space equipped with a system of vector fields

(2.1)

\displaystyle X_{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}},\,i=1,\ldots,n,\qquad Y_{j}=|x|^{\alpha}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}},\,j=1,2,\ldots,m.

Here
\alpha>0
is a given real number and
|x|=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}\right)^{1/2}
represents the standard Euclidean norm of
x
.

Definition 2.1
.

The Grushin operator
\Delta_{\alpha}
is a differential operator on
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
defined by

(2.2)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\alpha}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}Y_{j}^{2}=\Delta_{x}+|x|^{2\alpha}\Delta_{y}=\nabla_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla_{\alpha},

where
\nabla_{\alpha}
is the horizontal (or Grushin) gradient, that is

\displaystyle\nabla_{\alpha}:(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m})=(\nabla_{x},|x|^{\alpha}\nabla_{y}).

A function is called Grushin-harmonic if it is a solution of the equation
\Delta_{\alpha}u=0
.

Remark 2.2
.

(1)

When
\alpha=0,
the Grushin operator
\Delta_{\alpha}
reduces to the standard Laplacian on the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
. In the case where
\alpha=1
,
m=1
and
n
even, it is closely related to the Kohn Laplacian on the Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{\frac{n}{2}}
, see
[
21
,
25
]
.

(2)

For
\alpha>0
, the Grushin operator
\Delta_{\alpha}
is elliptic when
x\neq 0
and degenerate on the characteristic submanifold
\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
.

(3)

Grushin studied
\Delta_{\alpha}
in
[
29
,
30
]
for the case where
\alpha
is a non-negative integer and established its hypoellipticity.

(4)

When
\alpha=2k
with
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the Grushin operator

\Delta_{\alpha}
is a sum of squares of
C^{\infty}
vector fields that satisfy Hörmander finite rank
condition on the Lie algebra
Lie[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}]
, see

[
32
]
.

There exists a natural family of anisotropic dilations attached to the Grushin operator
\Delta_{\alpha}
, given by

(2.3)

\displaystyle\delta_{\lambda}(x,y):=(\lambda x,\lambda^{\alpha+1}y),\qquad\lambda>0,\,(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}.

From Eq. (
2.3
), we see that the degeneracy of
\Delta_{\alpha}
becomes increasingly stronger as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
.

The change of variable formula for Lebesgue measure yields

{\rm d}\circ\delta_{\lambda}(x,y)=\lambda^{Q}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y,

where

(2.4)

Q=n+m(\alpha+1).

The number
Q
is commonly referred to as the homogeneous dimension related to the vector fields (
2.1
). It plays an important role in the local analysis of
\Delta_{\alpha}
at points of the degeneracy manifold
\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Let us now introduce homogeneous functions in this framework, see e.g.
[
21
]
.

Definition 2.3
.

Suppose
u
is a function on
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
. We say that
u
is homogeneous of degree
k\in\mathbb{N}
with respect to (
2.3
) (or
u
is homogeneous of
\delta_{\lambda}
-degree
k
) if for each
\lambda>0
, it holds that

(2.5)

\displaystyle u\circ\delta_{\lambda}=\lambda^{k}u.

Equivalently, it can be characterized by

\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}u=ku,

where
\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}
is the generalized Euler’s operator introduced in
[
21
, Eq. (2.2)]
, given by

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}+(\alpha+1)\sum_{j=1}^{m}y_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}}.

The smooth vector field
\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}
is indeed the generator of the group
\{\delta_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda>0}
.

Remark 2.4
.

It is not difficult to verify that
X_{i}
and
Y_{j}
are homogeneous of degree one with respect to the dilations
\{\delta_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda>0}
in (
2.3
). That is,

X_{i}\circ\delta_{\lambda}=\lambda\delta_{\lambda}\circ X_{i},\qquad Y_{j}\circ\delta_{\lambda}=\lambda\delta_{\lambda}\circ Y_{j}.

Hence,
\Delta_{\alpha}
is homogeneous of degree two with respect to the dilations
\{\delta_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda>0}
.

Associated to the vector fields (
2.1
), for any
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
, the gauge norm is defined by

(2.7)

\displaystyle\rho(x,y):=\left(|x|^{2(\alpha+1)}+(\alpha+1)^{2}|y|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2(\alpha+1)}}.

Furthermore, the gauge-balls and gauge-spheres with respect to
\rho
centered at the origin with radius
r
are defined by

B_{r}:=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}|\rho(x,y)<r\right\}

and

\partial B_{r}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}|\rho(x,y)=r\}.

Note that these definitions depend on the value of
\alpha.

When
\alpha
is a non-negative integer, a Grushin-harmonic function
u(x,y)
can be expressed using its analyticity, as shown in
[
42
]
. Specifically, for
(x,y)
near the origin, one can write
u
as a series:

\displaystyle u(x,y)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}u_{k}(x,y),

where each
u_{k}
is a homogeneous polynomial of
\delta_{\lambda}
-degree
k
. This series converges uniformly and absolutely in some neighbourhood of the origin. It is not difficult to see that each
u_{k}
is also Grushin-harmonic. This means that every Grushin-harmonic function can be represented as an infinite sum of homogeneous Grushin-harmonic polynomials near the origin.

Based on the aforementioned analyticity considerations, we will always assume that
\alpha
is a non-negative integer in the rest of this paper, i.e.
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\{0,1,2,\cdots\}
. Furthermore, these cases have an interesting background from geometry. As mentioned earlier, the Grushin operator
\Delta_{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, arises through a submersion from a sub-Laplace operator on a nilpotent Lie group of step
\alpha+1
, see
[
9
, Proposition 3.1]
.

2.2.
Polar coordinates

In this subsection, we introduce suitable polar coordinates to derive a
decomposition of the Grushin operator
\Delta_{\alpha}
in (
2.2
). These coordinates for vector fields were first introduced by Greiner for the Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{1}
in
[
28
]
and later extended by Dunkl to
\mathbb{H}^{n}
in
[
18
]
. They were later adapted to the Grushin setting in
[
25
]
for
\alpha=m=1
and further studied for general cases, see e.g.
[
17
,
41
]
for
\alpha>0
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Let

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x_{1}=\rho\sin\phi(\sin^{2}\phi)^{-\frac{\alpha}{2\alpha+2}}\sin\theta_{1}\sin\theta_{2}\cdots\sin\theta_{n-2}\sin\theta_{n-1},\\
x_{2}=\rho\sin\phi(\sin^{2}\phi)^{-\frac{\alpha}{2\alpha+2}}\sin\theta_{1}\sin\theta_{2}\cdots\sin\theta_{n-2}\cos\theta_{n-1},\\
\vdots\\
x_{n}=\rho\sin\phi(\sin^{2}\phi)^{-\frac{\alpha}{2\alpha+2}}\cos\theta_{1};\\
y_{1}=\frac{1}{\alpha+1}\rho^{\alpha+1}\cos\phi\sin\beta_{1}\sin\beta_{2}\cdots\sin\beta_{m-2}\sin\beta_{m-1},\\
y_{2}=\frac{1}{\alpha+1}\rho^{\alpha+1}\cos\phi\sin\beta_{1}\sin\beta_{2}\cdots\sin\beta_{m-2}\cos\beta_{m-1},\\
\vdots\\
y_{m}=\frac{1}{\alpha+1}\rho^{\alpha+1}\cos\phi\cos\beta_{1},\end{array}\right.

where
\rho
is the gauge norm defined in (
2.7
)
,\phi\in(a_{\phi},b_{\phi}),0\leq\theta_{i}<\pi
,
i=1,\cdots,n-2
,
0\leq\beta_{j}<\pi
,

j=1,\cdots,m-2
,
0\leq\theta_{n-1}<2\pi
and
0\leq\beta_{m-1}<2\pi
. Here
a_{\phi}
and
b_{\phi}
depend on
n
and
m
, that is

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\phi\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2}),&\hbox{if $n,m\geq 2$;}\\
\phi\in(0,\pi),&\hbox{if $n\geq 2$ and $m=1$;}\\
\phi\in(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}),&\hbox{if $n=1$ and $m\geq 2$;}\\
\phi\in(0,2\pi),&\hbox{if $n=m=1$.}\end{array}\right.

Let
r_{1}=|x|
and
r_{2}=|y|
. When
n,m\geq 2
, it is seen from (
2.2
) that

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}r_{1}=|x|=\rho\sin^{\frac{1}{\alpha+1}}\phi,\\
r_{2}=|y|=\frac{1}{\alpha+1}\rho^{\alpha+1}\cos\phi.\end{array}\right.

A straightforward computation yields

(2.25)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(r_{1},r_{2})}{\partial(\rho,\phi)}=\begin{pmatrix}\sin^{\frac{1}{\alpha+1}}\phi&\frac{1}{\alpha+1}\rho\sin^{\frac{-\alpha}{\alpha+1}}\phi\cos\phi\\
\rho^{\alpha}\cos\phi&-\frac{1}{\alpha+1}\rho^{\alpha+1}\sin\phi\end{pmatrix}.

This gives

\displaystyle{\rm d}r_{1}{\rm d}r_{2}=\frac{1}{\alpha+1}\sin^{-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}}\phi\,\rho^{\alpha+1}\,{\rm d}\rho\,{\rm d}\phi,

and

(2.26)

\begin{split}{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y=&\,r_{1}^{n-1}r_{2}^{m-1}\,{\rm d}r_{1}\,{\rm d}\omega_{1}\,{\rm d}r_{2}\,{\rm d}\omega_{2}\\
=&\,\frac{1}{(\alpha+1)^{m}}\rho^{(\alpha+1)m+(n-1)}\sin^{\frac{n}{\alpha+1}-1}\phi\cos^{m-1}\phi\\
&\times{\rm d}\rho\,{\rm d}\phi\,{\rm d}\omega_{1}\,{\rm d}\omega_{2},\end{split}

where
{\rm d}\omega_{1}
and
{\rm d}\omega_{2}
are the Lebesgue measures on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
, respectively.

From Eq. (
2.2
), the gauge norm
\rho
in (
2.7
) and
\phi
are

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\rho=\left[r_{1}^{2(\alpha+1)}+(\alpha+1)^{2}r_{2}^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2(\alpha+1)}},\\
\phi=\arctan\frac{r_{1}^{\alpha+1}}{(\alpha+1)r_{2}}.\end{array}\right.

Again direct computation yields that

(2.28)

\displaystyle\,\frac{\partial(\rho,\phi)}{\partial(r_{1},r_{2})}=\begin{pmatrix}\sin^{\frac{2\alpha+1}{\alpha+1}}\phi&\rho^{-\alpha}\cos\phi\\
(\alpha+1)\rho^{-1}\sin^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}}\phi\cos\phi&-(\alpha+1)\rho^{-(\alpha+1)}\sin\phi\end{pmatrix}.

Alternatively, it is well-known that in the usual spherical coordinates,

\Delta_{x}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r_{1}^{2}}+\frac{n-1}{r_{1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{1}}+\frac{1}{r_{1}^{2}}\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}},

and

\Delta_{y}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r_{2}^{2}}+\frac{m-1}{r_{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{2}}+\frac{1}{r_{2}^{2}}\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}},

where
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}
denotes the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. Let
\psi(\phi)
be the angle function defined by

(2.29)

\displaystyle\psi(\phi):=\sin^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha+1}}\phi=\left(\frac{r_{1}}{\rho}\right)^{2\alpha}.

After a direct computation based on (
2.28
), the Grushin operator
\Delta_{\alpha}
in (
2.2
) can be expressed in the polar coordinates (
2.2
) as

(2.30)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\alpha}=\psi(\phi)\cdot\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\rho^{2}}+\frac{Q-1}{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\Delta_{\sigma}\right),

where
\sigma\in(\phi,\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
,
\omega_{1}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
,
\omega_{2}\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
, and

(2.31)

\begin{split}\Delta_{\sigma}=&\,(\alpha+1)^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\phi^{2}}+(n+\alpha-1)(\alpha+1)\cot\phi\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\\
&-(m-1)(\alpha+1)^{2}\tan\phi\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}+\sin^{-2}\phi\,\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\\
&+(\alpha+1)^{2}\cos^{-2}\phi\,\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}},\end{split}

see also
[
41
, Eq. (2.3)]
.

Remark 2.5
.

From Eq. (
2.30
), we see that if a function
u
depends only on the pseudo-distance
\rho
, i.e.
u(x,y)=f(\rho)
, then

\Delta_{\alpha}u=\sin^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha+1}}\phi\cdot\left(f^{\prime\prime}(\rho)+\frac{Q-1}{\rho}f^{\prime}(\rho)\right).

This implies that the Grushin operator does not map functions of
\rho
into functions of
\rho
, which is different with the Euclidean Laplacian. This feature of the Grushin operator, which was shared by the sub-Laplacian on the Heisenberg group, makes the analysis considerably harder than that of the Euclidean setting. This has been pointed out in several studies, see e.g.
[
6
,
21
,
25
]
.

2.3.
Jacobi polynomials

Gegenbauer polynomials have been used to construct the orthogonal basis for Grushin-harmonics on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, see
[
25
,
41
]
. In the general higher step setting, Jacobi polynomials will play a prominent role. We introduce several important properties of these polynomials in this subsection.

The Jacobi polynomials
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)
are a family of orthogonal polynomials defined on the interval
[-1,1]
with respect to the the weight
(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}
. They can be expressed using the terminating Gauss hypergeometric series as follows:

(2.32)

P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)=\frac{(\alpha+1)_{n}}{n!}\,{{}_{2}F_{1}}\left(-n,1+\alpha+\beta+n;\alpha+1;\frac{1-x}{2}\right),

where

(\alpha+1)_{n}:=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1+n)}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}

is the Pochhammer symbol. When
\alpha>-1
and
\beta>-1
, they satisfy the orthogonality condition, i.e.

(2.33)

\begin{split}&\int_{-1}^{1}(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}P_{m}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)\,{\rm d}x\\
=&\frac{2^{\alpha+\beta+1}}{2n+\alpha+\beta+1}\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)\Gamma(n+\beta+1)}{\Gamma(n+\alpha+\beta+1)n!}\delta_{n,m}.\end{split}

Here
\delta_{n,m}
is the Kronecker delta. Note that Jacobi polynomials include the Gegenbauer polynomials

(2.34)

\displaystyle C_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x)=\frac{(2\alpha)_{n}}{\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\right)_{n}}P_{n}^{(\alpha-1/2,\alpha-1/2)}(x)

as special cases. Furthermore, the Gegenbauer polynomials
C_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x)
reduce to the Legendre polynomials when
\alpha=1/2
.

Extensive research has been conducted on the asymptotic behavior of Jacobi polynomials for large values of
n
, see e.g.
[
45
, Chapter VIII]
. However, most of the existing formulas do not provide uniform estimates for
\alpha
and
\beta
. An important problem is to provide a uniform estimate for

(2.35)

\displaystyle(1-x)^{a}(1+x)^{b}\left|P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)\right|

over the entire interval
[-1,1]
, where
a
and
b
are non-negative constants. The first result of this type is Bernstein’s inequality for the Legendre polynomials (see e.g.
[
45
, Theorem 7.3.3]
). It can be stated as follows:

(2.36)

\displaystyle(1-x^{2})^{1/4}\left|P_{n}(x)\right|\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi(2n+1)}},\qquad\forall\,x\in[-1,1].

The constant
\sqrt{2/\pi}
in (
2.36
) is known to be sharp. This problem has attracted considerable attention over the last years, see e.g.
[
20
,
31
,
40
]
. For a comprehensive review of the progress made on this problem and more advanced estimates, we refer to
[
38
]
.

In this research, we need a uniform (in some sense) estimate for their weighted
L^{2}
-norms.
The following uniform estimate for Jacobi polynomials recently obtained in
[
31
]
will be used for that purpose. For
x\in[-1,1]
and
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
, let

(2.37)

\begin{split}g_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x):=&\left(\frac{\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma(n+\alpha+\beta+1)}{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)\Gamma(n+\beta+1)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\times\left(\frac{1-x}{2}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\left(\frac{1+x}{2}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{2}}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x).\end{split}

Then the following Bernstein-type inequality holds,

Theorem 2.6
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that

(2.38)

\displaystyle\left|(1-x^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}g_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)\right|\leq C(2n+\alpha+\beta+1)^{-\frac{1}{4}}

for all
x\in[-1,1]
, all
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
, and all non-negative integers
n
.

Remark 2.7
.

The optimal value of
C
is not yet known. However, it is known at least that
C<12
.

Remark 2.8
.

With suitable coordinates, the functions
g_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)

with non-negative integers
\alpha
and
\beta
form a natural and complete set of matrix coefficients for the irreducible
representations of
{\rm SU}(2)
. Interestingly, the value
2n+\alpha+\beta+1
in (
2.38
) is
exactly the dimension of the corresponding irreducible representation, see e.g.
[
2
, §9.14]
and
[
31
, Theorem 2.1]
. It follows that for all
x\in[-1,1]
,
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the inequality

(2.39)

\left|g_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)\right|\leq 1

holds. This was studied in depth and a tighter bound for
g_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)
was obtained, see
[
31
, p. 234 (20)]
.

3.
Spherical harmonics for the Grushin operator

In
[
25
]
, an orthogonal basis was constructed for Grushin-harmonic polynomials of the operator
\Delta_{\alpha}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, where
\alpha=1
, using Gegenbauer polynomials (
2.34
) and ordinary spherical harmonics. More recently, a similar orthogonal basis was discovered for general
\Delta_{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
in
[
41
]
. Meanwhile, it was observed in
[
41
]
that the Grushin harmonics on
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
, when
m>1
, exhibit increased complexity. In this section, we address this issue for
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
with
n,m\geq 2
.

3.1.
Orthogonal basis for Grushin-harmonics

Let
u_{k}
be a homogeneous polynomial of
\delta_{\lambda}
-degree
k
on
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
. We write it in the polar coordinates (
2.2
) as

(3.1)

\displaystyle u_{k}=\rho^{k}g(\phi,\omega_{1},\omega_{2}),

where
\omega_{1}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and
\omega_{2}\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
.

We first give a specific family of Grushin-harmonic polynomials using Jacobi polynomials and ordinary spherical harmonics.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose
n,m\geq 2
and
\alpha,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Let

Y_{\ell}(\omega_{1})
and
Y_{j}(\omega_{2})
be ordinary spherical harmonics of degree
\ell
on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and degree
j
on
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
, respectively. Here
\ell
and
j
are non-negative integers satisfying the condition

(3.2)

\displaystyle\widetilde{k}:=\frac{k-\ell-j(\alpha+1)}{2(\alpha+1)}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Then, the function

\displaystyle u_{k}=\rho^{k}h_{k,\ell,j}(\phi)Y_{\ell}(\omega_{1})Y_{j}(\omega_{2}),

where

(3.3)

\displaystyle h_{k,\ell,j}(\phi)=\cos^{j}\phi\,\sin^{\frac{\ell}{\alpha+1}}\phi\,P_{\widetilde{k}}^{(\mu,\gamma-1)}(\cos 2\phi),

with
\gamma=j+m/2
and
\mu=\frac{n-2+2\ell}{2(\alpha+1)}
, is a homogeneous Grushin-harmonic polynomial of
\delta_{\lambda}
-degree
k
.

Proof.

Using formula (
2.30
) for the Grushin operator
\Delta_{\alpha}
in polar coordinates, a homogeneous polynomial
u_{k}
of
\delta_{\lambda}
-degree
k
is a solution of

\Delta_{\alpha}u=0

if and only if
g(\phi,\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
in (
3.1
) is an eigenfunction of
\Delta_{\sigma}
in (
2.31
) with eigenvalue
-k(k+Q-2)
, i.e.

(3.4)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\sigma}g=-k(k+Q-2)g,

where
Q=n+m(\alpha+1)
is the homogeneous dimension.

According to (
2.31
) and the well-known property of ordinary spherical harmonics

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}Y_{\ell}(\omega_{1})=-\ell(\ell+n-2)Y_{\ell}(\omega_{1}),\\
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}Y_{j}(\omega_{2})=-j(j+m-2)Y_{j}(\omega_{2}),\end{array}\right.

it is seen that Eq. (
3.4
) holds if and only if

(3.6)

\begin{split}&(\alpha+1)^{2}h^{\prime\prime}(\phi)+(n+\alpha-1)(\alpha+1)(\cot\phi)h^{\prime}(\phi)\\
&-(m-1)(\alpha+1)^{2}(\tan\phi)h^{\prime}(\phi)+(\sin\phi)^{-2}(-\ell(\ell+n-2))h(\phi)\\
&+(\alpha+1)^{2}(\cos\phi)^{-2}(-j(j+m-2))h(\phi)+k(k+Q-2)h(\phi)=0.\end{split}

Letting
t=\cos\phi
and
h(\phi)=v(t)
, Eq. (
3.6
) transforms into

(3.7)

\begin{split}&(\alpha+1)^{2}(1-t^{2})\,\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}t^{2}}v(t)-(\alpha+1)(n+2\alpha)t\,\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}v(t)\\
&+(m-1)(\alpha+1)^{2}\frac{1-t^{2}}{t}\,\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}v(t)+\frac{1}{1-t^{2}}(-\ell(\ell+n-2))v(t)\\
&+(\alpha+1)^{2}(-j(j+m-2))\frac{1}{t^{2}}v(t)+k(k+Q-2)v(t)=0.\end{split}

Setting
w(t)=(1-t^{2})^{-\frac{\ell}{2(\alpha+1)}}v(t)
, then
w(t)
solves

(3.8)

\begin{split}&t^{2}(1-t^{2})\frac{{\rm d}^{2}w(t)}{{\rm d}t^{2}}+t\left[\left(1-m-\frac{2\ell+n+2\alpha}{\alpha+1}\right)t^{2}+m-1\right]\frac{{\rm d}w(t)}{{\rm d}t}\\
-&\left[c(\alpha,\ell,k)t^{2}+j(j+m-2)\right]w(t)=0,\end{split}

where

c(\alpha,\ell,k)=\frac{\ell(\ell+n+(\alpha+1)m-2)-k(k+n+(\alpha+1)m-2)}{(\alpha+1)^{2}}.

One hypergeometric solution of Eq. (
3.8
) is found to be

(3.9)

\displaystyle w(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t^{j}\,{{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{j(a+1)-k+\ell}{2(\alpha+1)},\frac{-2+j(\alpha+1)+k+\ell+m(\alpha+1)+n}{2(\alpha+1)},j+\frac{m}{2},t^{2}\right)}.

It is a polynomial when

\widetilde{k}:=-\frac{j(a+1)-k+\ell}{2(\alpha+1)}\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

or in other words when
\ell+(\alpha+1)j\equiv k\left({\rm mod}(2\alpha+2)\right)
. Using (
2.32
),
the solution (
3.9
) can be expressed in terms of Jacobi polynomials, i.e.

\displaystyle w(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t^{j}\cdot{{}_{2}F_{1}\left(-\widetilde{k},p+\widetilde{k},\gamma,t^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t^{j}\frac{\widetilde{k}!}{(\gamma)_{\widetilde{k}}}P_{\widetilde{k}}^{(\gamma-1,p-\gamma)}(1-2t^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{\widetilde{k}}t^{j}\frac{\widetilde{k}!}{(\gamma)_{\widetilde{k}}}P_{\widetilde{k}}^{(p-\gamma,\gamma-1)}(2t^{2}-1),

where
\gamma=j+m/2
and
p=\frac{n-2+2\ell}{2(\alpha+1)}+\gamma
.
Here the second equality is by (
2.32
) and the last step is by the property
P_{j}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(-x)=(-1)^{j}P_{j}^{(\beta,\alpha)}(x)
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

The solutions (
3.3
) can be verified directly by plugging them into Eq. (
3.7
) and then comparing with the equation with
\alpha=0
, which corresponds to the Euclidean case and is of course well-understood, see e.g.
[
37
, Eq. (4.3)]
.

In general, a second order ordinary differential equation possesses two linearly independent solutions. These solutions can be combined to derive the general solution. The significance of the hypergeometric solution (
3.9
) selected in Theorem
3.1
, can be seen from the following lemma. This lemma follows from the basic properties of Jacobi polynomials and changing variables. The proof is thus omitted.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
n,m\geq 2
. For fixed
\alpha,\ell,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
we denote
\gamma=j+m/2
and
\mu=\frac{n-2+2\ell}{2(\alpha+1)}
as in Theorem
3.1
. Then the set

(3.10)

\displaystyle\left\{h_{\widetilde{k}}(\phi):=\cos^{j}\phi\sin^{\frac{\ell}{\alpha+1}}\phi P_{\widetilde{k}}^{(\mu,\gamma-1)}(\cos 2\phi)\right\}_{\widetilde{k}=0}^{\infty}

forms a complete and orthogonal basis of
L^{2}\left([0,\pi/2],\sin^{\frac{n-2}{\alpha+1}+1}\phi\,\cos^{m-1}\phi\,{\rm d}\phi\right)
.
Moreover, it holds that

(3.11)

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{\pi/2}h_{\widetilde{k_{1}}}(\phi)h_{\widetilde{k_{2}}}(\phi)\sin^{\frac{n-2}{\alpha+1}+1}\phi\,\cos^{m-1}\phi\,{\rm d}\phi\\
=&\,\frac{1}{2(2\widetilde{k_{1}}+\mu+\gamma)}\frac{\Gamma(\widetilde{k_{1}}+\mu+1)\Gamma(\widetilde{k_{1}}+\gamma)}{\Gamma(\widetilde{k_{1}}+\mu+\gamma)\Gamma(\widetilde{k_{1}}+1)}\delta_{\widetilde{k_{1}},\widetilde{k_{2}}},\quad\gamma>0,\,\mu>-1.\end{split}

Remark 3.4
.

Note that the interval
[0,\pi/2]
is important. Indeed, the set
\{h_{\widetilde{k}}(\phi)\}_{\widetilde{k}=0}^{\infty}
does not form a complete basis for
L^{2}\left([-\pi/2,\pi/2],\sin^{\frac{n-2}{\alpha+1}+1}\phi\,\cos^{m-1}\phi\,{\rm d}\phi\right)
. A complete basis in this case where
\phi\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
would be obtained by employing the so-called generalized Gegenbauer polynomials in
[
19
, §1.5]
. This suggests that we should make a distinction between the cases listed in (
2.2
).

To construct a complete orthogonal basis for Grushin-harmonic polynomials, we introduce some notations.
Let

\{Y_{\ell,p}(\omega_{1})\}_{p=1,2,\ldots,d_{\ell}(n)}

be an orthonormal basis of ordinary spherical harmonics of degree
\ell
on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. Here
d_{\ell}(n)
is the dimension of the space of ordinary spherical harmonics of degree
\ell
on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, which is given by

(3.12)

\displaystyle d_{\ell}(n)=\frac{(n+2\ell-2)\Gamma(n+\ell-2)}{\Gamma(\ell+1)\Gamma(n-1)}.

By Stirling’s formula for the Gamma function, there exists a constant
C(n)
only depending on
n
such that

(3.13)

\displaystyle d_{\ell}(n)\leq C(n)(\ell+1)^{n-2}.

The set
\{Y_{j,q}(\omega_{2})\}_{q=1,2,\ldots,d_{j}(m)}
is defined similarly.

We introduce the following space spanned by homogeneous Grushin-harmonics obtained in Theorem
3.1
.

Definition 3.5
.

We define

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle:={\rm span}\bigg\{\rho^{k}h_{k,\ell,j}(\phi)Y_{\ell,p}(\omega_{1})Y_{j,q}(\omega_{2})\bigg|1\leq p\leq d_{\ell}(n),1\leq q\leq d_{j}(m),

\displaystyle\qquad 0\leq\ell,j\leq k,\,{\rm and}\,\,\ell+(\alpha+1)j\equiv k\left({\rm mod}(2\alpha+2)\right)\bigg\},

where
h_{k,\ell,j}
is given in (
3.3
).

Consider the measure on

\Omega:=\partial B_{1}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}|\rho(x,y)=1\}

given by

(3.14)

\displaystyle{\rm d}\Omega:=\sin^{\frac{n-2}{\alpha+1}+1}\phi\,\cos^{m-1}\phi\,{\rm d}\phi\,{\rm d}\omega_{1}\,{\rm d}\omega_{2},

which is from Lemma
3.3
.
It is worth noting that
{\rm d}\Omega
is not the usual Lebesgue measure on
\Omega
. It has the angular function
\psi(\phi)
in (
2.29
) as a weight. This sheds light on the reason of the presence of
\psi
in the condition (
1.2
).

Now, we have all the ingredients to state the main result of this subsection:

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
n,m\geq 2
.
The following orthogonal decomposition holds

\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega,{\rm d}\Omega)=\mathop{\bigoplus}_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}_{k}.

Proof.

Using the orthogonality of the functions
h_{\widetilde{k}}(\phi)
in (
3.10
) and the orthogonality of ordinary spherical harmonics of different degrees, we observe that the spaces
\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\alpha}
are mutually orthogonal in
L^{2}(\Omega,{\rm d}\Omega)
.
Thanks to Lemma
3.3
and the well-known completeness of ordinary spherical harmonics in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
, the completeness of
\mathop{\bigoplus}_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\alpha}
can be proven through a standard discussion.
In other words, we can show that if
f\in L^{2}(\Omega,{\rm d}\Omega)
is orthogonal to each
\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\alpha}
, then
f=0
a.e. on
\Omega
. We will not repeat the details.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

For the space
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, provided
\ell\equiv k({\rm mod}(\alpha+1))
, one polynomial solution of (
3.8
)
is given by the Gegenbauer polynomial

\displaystyle w(t)=P^{\left(\frac{2\ell+n-2}{2(\alpha+1)},\frac{2\ell+n-2}{2(\alpha+1)}\right)}_{\frac{k-1}{\alpha+1}}(t)\sim C^{\left(\frac{2\ell+n-2}{2(\alpha+1)}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}_{\frac{k-\ell}{\alpha+1}}(t).

If we consider the following measure on
\partial B_{1}
,

\displaystyle{\rm d}\Omega=\sin^{\frac{n-2}{\alpha+1}+1}\phi\,{\rm d}\phi\,{\rm d}\omega,

where
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, then an orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
is given by

\displaystyle\left\{\rho^{k}\left(\sin\phi\right)^{\frac{\ell}{\alpha+1}}C^{\left(\frac{2\ell+n-2}{2(\alpha+1)}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}_{\frac{k-\ell}{\alpha+1}}(\cos\phi)Y_{\ell,p}(\omega)\bigg|\right.

\displaystyle\qquad 0\leq p\leq d_{\ell}(n),\ell\equiv k({\rm mod}(\alpha+1))\biggr\}_{k=0}^{\infty},

where
\{Y_{\ell,p}\}_{p=1,2,\ldots d_{\ell}(n)}
is an orthonormal basis of spherical harmonics of degree
\ell
on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. We recover the result in
[
41
, Theorem 1.2]
.

The proof of Theorem
3.6
implies the following:

Theorem 3.8
.

When
n,m\geq 3
,
the space
\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\alpha}
decomposes under the action of
{\rm SO}(n)\times{\rm SO}(m)
into irreducible and mutually orthogonal pieces as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\alpha}=\mathop{\bigoplus}_{\beta}h_{k,\ell,j}(\phi)\mathcal{H}_{\ell}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})\otimes\mathcal{H}_{j}(\mathbb{S}^{m-1}),

where
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
is the space of ordinary spherical harmonics of degree
\ell
, the condition

\beta
means

\ell+(\alpha+1)j\equiv k\,({\rm mod}(2\alpha+2)),\qquad 0\leq\ell,j\leq k,

and
h_{k,\ell,j}(\phi)
is given in (
3.3
).

Remark 3.9
.

When
\alpha=0
, Theorem
3.8
reduces to the decomposition of ordinary spherical harmonics by Koornwinder in
[
37
, Theorem 4.2]
.

3.2.
The orthogonal projection and addition formula

In this subsection, we study the orthogonal projection

(3.15)

\displaystyle P_{k}:L^{2}(\Omega,{\rm d}\Omega)\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}_{k},

which is the projection from
L^{2}(\Omega,{\rm d}\Omega)
onto the
(k+1)
-th eigenspace of
\Delta_{\sigma}
defined in (
2.31
).

Recall that for
n\geq 2
, the reproducing kernel of the ordinary homogeneous spherical harmonic polynomials of degree
k
over
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is given by

(3.16)

\displaystyle K^{n}_{k}(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{k}(n)}Y_{k,j}(\zeta_{1})\overline{Y_{k,j}(\zeta_{2})}=\frac{d_{k}(n)}{\left|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\right|}\cdot\frac{C_{k}^{(\frac{n-2}{2})}(\cos\tau)}{C_{k}^{(\frac{n-2}{2})}(1)}.

Here
\tau
is the angle between
\zeta_{1}
and
\zeta_{2}
on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
,
0\leq\tau\leq\pi
,
C_{k}^{(\alpha)}(x)
is the Gegenbauer polynomial in (
2.34
) and
d_{k}(n)
is the dimension given in (
3.12
). See e.g.
[
2
, Theorem 9.6.3]
and
[
19
]
.

For convenience, we denote

(3.17)

\begin{split}B_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}:=&\int_{0}^{\pi/2}(\sin\phi)^{2\alpha+1}(\cos\phi)^{2\beta+1}\left(P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(\cos 2\phi)\right)^{2}{\rm d}\phi\\
=&\frac{1}{2(2n+\alpha+\beta+1)}\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)\Gamma(n+\beta+1)}{\Gamma(n+\alpha+\beta+1)n!},\end{split}

which is the square of the
L^{2}
-norm of the Jacobi polynomial
P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(x)
in trigonometric coordinates.

Now, let
\omega=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}),\eta=(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
and

{\rm d}\omega:={\rm d}\omega_{1}{\rm d}\omega_{2}
. It is not hard to see from Theorem
3.6
that the following holds.

Theorem 3.10
.

The orthogonal projector operator

P_{k}:L^{2}(\Omega,{\rm d}\Omega)\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}_{k}

coincides with the integral operator

(3.18)

\displaystyle P_{k}(g)(\phi,\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}G_{k}(\phi,\omega,\xi,\eta)g(\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle\times\sin^{\frac{n-2}{\alpha+1}+1}\xi\,\cos^{m-1}\xi\,{\rm d}\xi\,{\rm d}\eta,

where

(3.19)

\begin{split}&G_{k}(\phi,\omega,\xi,\eta)\\
=&\mathop{\sum_{\widetilde{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}}_{0\leq\ell,j\leq k}b_{k,\ell,j}^{2}h_{k,\ell,j}(\phi)h_{k,\ell,j}(\xi)K_{\ell}^{n}(\omega_{1},\eta_{1})K_{j}^{m}(\omega_{2},\eta_{2}),\end{split}

in which
b_{k,\ell,j}
is the normalization constant given by

(3.20)

\displaystyle b_{k,\ell,j}=\left(\frac{1}{B_{\widetilde{k}}^{\mu,\gamma-1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\left(\frac{2(2\widetilde{k}+\mu+\gamma)\Gamma(\widetilde{k}+\mu+\gamma)\Gamma(\widetilde{k}+1)}{\Gamma(\widetilde{k}+\mu+1)\Gamma(\widetilde{k}+\gamma)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Here
h_{k,\ell,j}(\phi),\widetilde{k},\mu,\gamma
are defined in Theorem
3.1
and
K_{k}^{n}(\cdot,\cdot)
is given in (
3.16
).

When
\alpha=0
, the integral kernel
G_{k}(\phi,\omega,\xi,\eta)
in (
3.19
) reduces to the reproducing kernel
K^{n+m}_{k}(\cdot,\cdot)
for the ordinary spherical harmonics of degree
k
in (
3.16
). Thus in this case, we recover the addition formula for Gegenbauer polynomials obtained first by Koornwinder in
[
37
, Eq. (4.7)]
using a group theoretical method without obtaining explicit constants. In 1997, Xu provided a generalization of the formula and an analytic proof in
[
47
, Eq. (2.5)]
. We adapt the notations to suit our purpose as follows.

Theorem 3.11
.

[
47
]

For
u>1
and
v>1
, the following addition formula holds

(3.21)

\displaystyle C_{k}^{\left(\frac{u+v}{2}-1\right)}(\cos\phi\cos\xi\cos\theta_{1}+\sin\phi\sin\xi\cos\theta_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\sum_{j+\ell=k-2i}c_{k,\ell,j}f_{k,\ell,j}(\cos 2\phi)f_{k,\ell,j}(\cos 2\xi)C_{j}^{(\frac{u}{2}-1)}(\cos\theta_{1})C_{\ell}^{(\frac{v}{2}-1)}(\cos\theta_{2}),

where

\displaystyle f_{k,\ell,j}(\cos 2\theta)=\left(\frac{1}{B_{\frac{k-\ell-j}{2}}^{\frac{v}{2}-1+j,\frac{u}{2}-1+\ell}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}(\cos\theta)^{j}(\sin\theta)^{\ell}P_{\frac{k-\ell-j}{2}}^{(\frac{v}{2}-1+\ell,\frac{u}{2}-1+j)}(\cos 2\theta),

and

\displaystyle c_{m,k,\ell}=\frac{(2j+u-2)(2\ell+v-2)\Gamma\left(\frac{u}{2}-1\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{v}{2}-1\right)}{4(2k+u+v-2)\Gamma\left(\frac{u+v}{2}-1\right)}.

Remark 3.12
.

The addition formula (
3.21
) will be crucial to establish the
L^{1}-L^{\infty}
estimates for the projector
P_{k}
in (
3.15
) in the next section.

3.3.
The
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
triple and Fischer decomposition

In this subsection, we further investigate Grushin-harmonics and the orthogonal projection (
3.15
). The interesting
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
structure allows us to generalize the classical harmonic analysis results which are governed by
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
to the present framework. We only point out some of them.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be the real vector space of all polynomials on
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
, and let
\mathcal{P}_{k}^{\alpha}
be
the subspace consisting of homogeneous polynomials of
\delta_{\lambda}
-degree
k
. The subsequent result has been implicitly used in literature. Since we have been unable to find a proof, we provide one here.

Theorem 3.13
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the mapping
f\rightarrow\Delta_{\alpha}f
establishes a surjective homomorphism from
\mathcal{P}_{k}^{\alpha}
to
\mathcal{P}_{k-2}^{\alpha}
.
(
The spaces
\mathcal{P}_{-1}^{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{P}_{-2}^{\alpha}
are assumed to be
\{0\}
.
)

Proof.

It is not hard to see that
\Delta_{\alpha}
preserves polynomials and is homogeneous of
\delta_{\lambda}
-degree two. Thus it is sufficient to show that this mapping is surjective.

This is true when
k\leq\alpha
, because now the polynomials in
\mathcal{P}_{k}^{\alpha}
are only those linear combinations of monomials
x_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{k_{n}}
with
k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}=k
and hence we can use the result for the classical Laplace operator.

Now, let us consider the general monomial
XY
, where

X:=x_{1}^{\ell_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\ell_{n}},\qquad Y:=y_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots y_{m}^{j_{m}}

with
\ell=\ell_{1}+\cdots+\ell_{n}
and
j=j_{1}+\cdots+j_{m}
. It is seen that
XY
is of
\delta_{\lambda}
-degree
\ell+(\alpha+1)j
.
Let
X_{1}
be a polynomial (not unique) such that
\Delta_{x}X_{1}=X
, and
X_{\ell}
be the polynomial such that
\Delta_{x}X_{\ell}=|x|^{2\alpha}X_{\ell-1}
for
\ell\geq 2
. The existence of such
X_{\ell}
follow from the Euclidean results.
Then,

\begin{split}&(\Delta_{x}+|x|^{2\alpha}\Delta_{y})\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor\frac{j+4}{2}\rfloor}(-1)^{\ell-1}X_{\ell}\Delta_{y}^{\ell-1}Y\\
=&\,XY+\sum_{\ell=2}^{\lfloor\frac{j+4}{2}\rfloor}(-1)^{\ell-1}|x|^{2\alpha}X_{\ell-1}\Delta_{y}^{\ell-1}Y+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor\frac{j+4}{2}\rfloor}(-1)^{\ell-1}|x|^{2\alpha}X_{\ell}\Delta_{y}^{\ell}Y\\
=&\,XY,\end{split}

where we have used
\Delta_{y}^{\lfloor\frac{j+4}{2}\rfloor}Y=0
in the last step. Since every polynomial is a linear combination of monomials, we complete the proof.
∎

Now we give some dimension discussions.

Lemma 3.14
.

The dimension of
\mathcal{P}_{k}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})
, denoted by
{\dim}\,\mathcal{P}_{k}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})
, satisfies

(3.22)

\frac{1}{(1-r^{\alpha+1})^{m}(1-r)^{n}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\dim}\,\mathcal{P}_{k}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})r^{k},\qquad|r|<1.

Explicitly, suppose
k=p(\alpha+1)+\ell
for some positive integer
p
and
\ell\in\{0,1,\cdots,\alpha\},
then we have

(3.23)

{\dim}\,\mathcal{P}_{k}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})=\sum_{j=0}^{p}\binom{m+j-1}{m-1}\cdot\binom{n+k-j(\alpha+1)-1}{n-1}.

Proof.

The formula (
3.23
) was already appeared in
[
41
, Lemma 2.1]
. It can be seen as follows. Since each
x_{i},i=1,2,\ldots,n
is homogeneous of
\delta_{\lambda}
-degree one and

y_{j},j=1,2,\ldots,m
is homogeneous of
\delta_{\lambda}
-degree
\alpha+1
, thus a basis for

\mathcal{P}_{k}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})
is given by

\displaystyle\{p_{k_{1}}(x)q_{k_{2}}(y):p_{k_{1}}(x)\in\mathcal{P}_{k_{1}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),q_{k_{2}}(y)\in\mathcal{P}_{k_{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{m}),k_{1}+k_{2}(\alpha+1)=k\}.

Using the well-known result for ordinary polynomials

{\rm dim}\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\binom{n+k-1}{n-1},
we are done.
The formula (
3.22
) can be seen from (
3.23
) and the following well-known generating function

\frac{1}{(1-r)^{n}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{n+k-1}{n-1}r^{k},\qquad|r|<1,

which generates the diagonals in Pascal’s Triangle.
∎

By Theorem
3.13
and Lemma
3.14
, we have,

Lemma 3.15
.

The dimension of
\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})
, denoted by
{\dim}\,\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})
, satisfies

(3.24)

\frac{1-r^{2}}{(1-r^{\alpha+1})^{m}(1-r)^{n}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\dim}\,\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})r^{k},\qquad|r|<1.

Explicitly, for
\alpha\neq 0
, let
k=p(\alpha+1)+\ell
for some positive integer
p
and
\ell\in\{0,1,\cdots,\alpha\},
then we have

\begin{split}{\dim}\,\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})=&\sum_{j=0}^{p}\binom{m+j-1}{m-1}\cdot\binom{n+k-j(\alpha+1)-1}{n-1}\\
&-\sum_{j=0}^{p}\binom{m+j-1}{m-1}\cdot\binom{n+k-j(\alpha+1)-3}{n-1},\end{split}

when
\ell\geq 2
,
and

\begin{split}{\dim}\,\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})=&\sum_{j=0}^{p}\binom{m+j-1}{m-1}\cdot\binom{n+k-j(\alpha+1)-1}{n-1}\\
&-\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}\binom{m+j-1}{m-1}\cdot\binom{n+k-j(\alpha+1)-3}{n-1},\end{split}

when
\ell<2
.

Proof.

Using Theorem
3.13
, we have

{\dim}\,\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})={\dim}\,\mathcal{P}_{k}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})-{\dim}\,\mathcal{P}_{k-2}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m}).

Together with (
3.22
) and (
3.23
), we obtain the explicit formulas.
∎

Remark 3.16
.

Rewriting the left-hand side of (
3.24
) as

\frac{1-r^{2}}{(1-r^{\alpha+1})^{m}(1-r)^{n}}=\frac{1}{1-r^{2(\alpha+1)}}\cdot\frac{1-r^{2(\alpha+1)}}{(1-r^{\alpha+1})^{m}}\cdot\frac{1-r^{2}}{(1-r)^{n}},

and then by the well-known relation for ordinary spherical harmonics (see
[
44
]
), i.e.

\frac{1-r^{2}}{(1-r)^{n}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\dim}\,\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})r^{k},\qquad|r|<1,

we have

{\dim}\,\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})=\sum_{\beta}{\dim}\,\mathcal{H}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cdot{\dim}\,\mathcal{H}_{j}(\mathbb{R}^{m}),

where
\beta
stands for
\ell+(\alpha+1)j\equiv k({\rm mod}(2\alpha+2)
,
0\leq\ell,j\leq k
and
{\dim}\,\mathcal{H}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the dimension of ordinary harmonic polynomials of degree
\ell
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
given in (
3.12
). This again implies that the orthogonal basis constructed in Section
3.1
is complete.

Theorems
3.6
and
3.13
together imply that the classical Fischer decomposition, a fundamental tool in classical harmonic analysis and complex analysis (see e.g.
[
19
]
), can be extended to the present Grushin setting with
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Theorem 3.17

(Fischer decomposition)
.

The following orthogonal decomposition holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{k}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})=\mathop{\bigoplus}_{p=0}^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}\rho^{2p}\,\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}_{k-2p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m}).

Obviously, when
\alpha=0
, this reduces to the classical result. On the other side, the classical Fischer decomposition is well understood by employing the Howe dual pair
({\rm SO}(n+m),\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
, see e.g.
[
33
]
. We show that the decomposition in Theorem
3.13
is again governed by a
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
triple. This will be achieved by introducing the following perturbed operator which maps functions of
\rho
into functions of
\rho
. Meanwhile, the Grushin-harmonic polynomials are included in the kernel of this new operator.

Definition 3.18
.

The perturbed Grushin operator
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\rho}{|x|}\right)^{2\alpha}\Delta_{\alpha}=\rho^{2\alpha}\left(|x|^{-2\alpha}\Delta_{x}+\Delta_{y}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\rho^{2}}+\frac{Q-1}{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\Delta_{\sigma}.

A straightforward calculation yields the following,

Theorem 3.19
.

The operators
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
,
\rho^{2}
and
\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}+Q/2
form an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
triple for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Namely, the following commutations hold

\displaystyle[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha},\rho^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\left(\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}+Q/2\right),

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha},\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}+Q/2\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\mathcal{L}_{\alpha},

\displaystyle\left[\rho^{2},\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}+Q/2\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\rho^{2},

where
\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}
is the analogue of classical Euler operator defined in (
2.6
).

Proof.

We only verify the first relation in Cartesian coordinates. Direct calculation yields

\begin{split}&\partial_{x_{i}}\rho^{2}=2\rho^{-2\alpha}|x|^{2\alpha}x_{i},\qquad\qquad 1\leq i\leq n,\\
&\partial_{y_{j}}\rho^{2}=2(\alpha+1)\rho^{-2\alpha}y_{j},\quad\qquad 1\leq j\leq m,\\
&\partial_{x_{i}}^{2}\rho^{2}=-4\alpha\rho^{4\alpha-2}|x|^{4\alpha}x_{i}^{2}+4\alpha\rho^{-2\alpha}|x|^{2\alpha-2}x_{i}^{2}+2\rho^{-2\alpha}|x|^{2\alpha},\\
&\partial_{y_{j}}^{2}\rho^{2}=-4\alpha(\alpha+1)^{2}\rho^{-4\alpha-2}y_{j}^{2}+2(\alpha+1)\rho^{-2\alpha}.\end{split}

It follows that

\displaystyle[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha},\rho^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho^{2\alpha}\left[|x|^{-2\alpha}\Delta_{x}+\Delta_{y},\rho^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho^{2\alpha}\left[|x|^{-2\alpha}\Delta_{x},\rho^{2}\right]+\rho^{2\alpha}\left[\Delta_{y},\rho^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}+2n+4\alpha-4\alpha\frac{|x|^{2(\alpha+1)}}{\rho^{2(\alpha+1)}}

\displaystyle+4(\alpha+1)\sum_{j=1}^{m}y_{j}\partial_{y_{j}}+2m(\alpha+1)-\frac{4\alpha(\alpha+1)^{2}|y|^{2}}{\rho^{2(\alpha+1)}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4(\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}+Q/2).

These relations can also be seen by using the polar coordinates.
∎

Remark 3.20
.

(1) The operator
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
can be further deformed as
\rho^{2-b}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
with
b>0
. Together with
\rho^{b}
and
\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}+c
, they will give a new family of realization of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
. In particular when
b=2+2\alpha
,
\rho^{2-b}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
reduces to the radially deformed Laplace operator on
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
in
[
10
]
plus the ordinary Laplace operator, i.e.
|x|^{-2\alpha}\Delta_{x}+\Delta_{y}
, which is also called the split Laplace operator.

(2) The
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
structure makes it possible to generalize many results to the present setting. For instance, a Fourier transform can be defined for
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
, using an exponential operator similarly as was done in
[
10
]
. Then the (sharp)
L^{2}
Hardy and Hardy-Rellich inequalities for the Grushin operator
\Delta_{\alpha}
, as well as several uncertainty inequalities will follow from this Fourier transform.

Recall that analogues of the classical Kelvin transform have been defined on the Heisenberg group and Grushin spaces via group theory methods
[
39
,
43
]
. Remarkably, these transforms preserve the class of functions annihilated by the sub-Laplacian and the Grushin operator respectively. Therefore Maxwell’s classical construction of harmonic polynomials (see e.g.
[
44
]
) can be extended to these settings
[
39
]
.
The
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
structure provides another way, i.e. via differential operators, to construct Grushin-harmonic polynomials.

Theorem 3.21
.

The projection operator, which maps a given homogeneous polynomial of
\delta_{\lambda}
-degree
k
to its harmonic component of degree
k-2\ell
, is given by

\displaystyle{\rm Proj}_{\ell}^{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor-\ell}\alpha_{j}\,\rho^{2j}\,\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{j+\ell},

where
\alpha_{j}=\frac{(-1)^{j}\left(Q/2+k-2\ell-1\right)}{4^{j+\ell}j!\ell!}\frac{\Gamma\left(Q/2+k-2\ell-j-1\right)}{\Gamma\left(Q/2+k-\ell\right)}.

Proof.

Let
p_{\ell}
be a homogeneous polynomial of
\delta_{\lambda}
-degree
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then by the commutator relations in Theorem
3.19
, we have

(3.25)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\left(\rho^{k}p_{\ell}\right)=k(k+Q+2\ell-2)\rho^{k-2}p_{\ell}+\rho^{k}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}p_{\ell}

where
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
k
being even. Furthermore, if
\Delta_{\alpha}p_{\ell}=0
, Eq. (
3.25
) reduces to

(3.26)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\left(\rho^{k}p_{\ell}\right)=k(k+Q+2\ell-2)\rho^{k-2}p_{\ell}.

By Corollary
3.17
, each homogeneous polynomial
f_{k}
of
\delta_{\lambda}
-degree
k
can be decomposed as

(3.27)

\displaystyle f_{k}=u_{k}+\rho^{2}u_{k-2}+\rho^{4}u_{k-4}+\cdots,

where each
u_{j}
is a homogeneous Grushin-harmonic of
\delta_{\lambda}
-degree
j
.
Making use of (
3.26
) for several times, we get

(3.28)

\begin{split}&\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}f_{k}=2(Q+2k-4)u_{k-2}+4(Q+2k-6)\rho^{2}u_{k-4}+\cdots,\\
&\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{2}f_{k}=8(Q+2k-6)(Q+2k-8)u_{k-4}+\cdots,\\
&\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{3}f_{k}=48(Q-2k-8)(Q-2k-10)(Q-2k-12)u_{k-6}+\cdots,\end{split}

and in general,

(3.29)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{v}f_{k}=\sum_{j=v}^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}\frac{(2j)!!}{(2j-2v)!!}\frac{(Q+2k-2j-2)!!}{(Q+2k-2j-2v-2)!!}\rho^{2j-2v}u_{k-2j},

where

\displaystyle j!!=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}j(j-2)(j-4)\cdot 4\times 2,&\hbox{$j$ is even;}\\
j(j-2)(j-4)\cdot 3\times 1,&\hbox{$j$ is odd.}\end{array}\right.

Eqs. (
3.28
) or (
3.29
) form a set of simultaneous equations. We solve these equations as was done for the ordinary case in
[
19
, Theorem 5.1.15]
which yields expressions for the homogeneous harmonic polynomials that occur in the decomposition (
3.27
).
∎

4.
Bounds for the Grushin-harmonic projection

The main purpose of this section is to establish the weighted
L^{p}-L^{q}
estimate for the projection operator
P_{k}
in (
3.15
), which is suitable for deriving the Carleman estimates.

4.1.
L^{1}-L^{\infty}
estimates

In this subsection, we focus on obtaining the
L^{1}-L^{\infty}
estimates for the projection operator in (
3.15
).
To achieve this, we first consider the point-wise bounds for the reproducing kernels of Grushin-harmonics in (
3.19
).
Recall that for ordinary spherical harmonics over
\mathbb{S}^{n+m-1}
, there exists a constant
C
independent of
k
such that

(4.1)

\displaystyle\left|K^{n+m}_{k}(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})\right|\leq C(k+1)^{n+m-2},

see, e.g.
[
2
]
. We show that such an estimate still remains valid in the (higher step) Grushin setting. It will be proven by extending the approach in
[
25
]
, developed there for the specific Grushin operator
\Delta_{1}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. However, it is worth noting that our proof heavily relies on the addition formula in Eq. (
3.21
), unavailable around the time
[
25
]
appeared.

We give some technical lemmas first.

Lemma 4.1
.

For
n\geq 4
and
m\geq 2
, there exists a positive constant
C
independent of
k
and
\phi
such that

(4.2)

\begin{split}&\mathop{\sum_{\widetilde{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}}_{0\leq\ell,j\leq k}b_{k,\ell,j}^{2}\left[\cos^{j}\phi\sin^{\frac{\ell}{\alpha+1}}\phi P_{\widetilde{k}}^{(\mu,\gamma-1)}(\cos 2\phi)\right]^{2}\\
&\times(\ell+1)^{n-2}(j+1)^{m-2}\leq C(k+1)^{n+m-2},\end{split}

for every
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, where
\widetilde{k},\mu,\gamma
are defined in Theorem
3.1
and
b_{k,\ell,j}
is the normalization constant given in (
3.20
).

Proof.

(I) First, we consider the case when
k\equiv 0({\rm mod}(\alpha+1))
. In this case by the condition

\widetilde{k}=\frac{k-\ell-j(\alpha+1)}{2(\alpha+1)}\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

we must have
\ell\equiv 0({\rm mod}(\alpha+1))
. Thus we can denote
\ell=(\alpha+1)\ell_{0}
and
k=(\alpha+1)k_{0}
with
\ell_{0},k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Now the left hand side of Eq. (
4.2
) becomes

(4.3)

\begin{split}&\sum_{i=0}^{\left\lfloor k_{0}/2\right\rfloor}\sum_{j+\ell_{0}=k_{0}-2i}b_{k,\ell,j}^{2}\left(\cos^{j}\phi\sin^{\ell_{0}}\phi P_{\frac{k_{0}-\ell_{0}-j}{2}}^{\left(\frac{n-2}{2(\alpha+1)}+\ell_{0},\frac{m}{2}+j-1\right)}(\cos 2\phi)\right)^{2}\\
&\times((\alpha+1)\ell_{0}+1)^{n-2}(j+1)^{m-2}.\end{split}

On the other side, the addition formula (
3.21
) can be equivalently written as

(4.4)

\begin{split}&e_{u+v,{\rm k}}\,C_{{\rm k}}^{\left(\frac{u+v}{2}-1\right)}(\cos\phi\cos\xi\cos\theta_{1}+\sin\phi\sin\xi\cos\theta_{2})\\
=&\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor{\rm k}/2\rfloor}\sum_{j+\ell={\rm k}-2i}f_{{\rm k},\ell,j}(\cos 2\phi)f_{{\rm k},\ell,j}(\cos 2\xi)\\
&\times e_{u,j}\,C_{j}^{(\frac{u}{2}-1)}(\cos\theta_{1})\,e_{v,\ell}\,C_{\ell}^{(\frac{v}{2}-1)}(\cos\theta_{2}),\end{split}

where
f_{{\rm k},\ell,j}
is given in Theorem
3.11
, and

e_{u,j}:=\frac{1}{2}\Gamma\left(\frac{u}{2}-1\right)\left(\frac{u}{2}-1+j\right).

Note that we used the sub-index notation
{\rm k}
in (
4.4
) on purpose, which is not the homogeneous degree
k
now. It will be set by different values related with the homogeneous degree
k
in the subsequent proof.

Now plugging
u=m
,
v=\frac{n-2}{\alpha+1}+2
,
{\rm k}=k_{0}
,
\xi=\phi
and
\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}=0
in (
4.4
), we obtain

(4.5)

\begin{split}&e_{u+v,k_{0}}C_{k_{0}}^{\left(\frac{u+v}{2}-1\right)}(1)=\sum_{i=0}^{\left\lfloor k_{0}/2\right\rfloor}\sum_{j+\ell_{0}=k_{0}-2i}e_{u,j}C_{j}^{(\frac{u}{2}-1)}(1)\,e_{v,\ell_{0}}C_{\ell_{0}}^{(\frac{v}{2}-1)}(1)\\
&\times b_{k,\ell,j}^{2}\left(\cos^{j}\phi\,\sin^{\ell_{0}}\phi\,P_{\frac{k_{0}-\ell_{0}-j}{2}}^{\left(\frac{n-2}{2(\alpha+1)}+\ell_{0},\frac{m}{2}+j-1\right)}(\cos 2\phi)\right)^{2}.\end{split}

Comparing (
4.3
) with the right hand side of (
4.5
), together with

(4.6)

\begin{split}e_{u,k}C_{k}^{(\frac{u}{2}-1)}(1)=&\frac{\left(u/2-1+k\right)\Gamma\left(u/2-1\right)\Gamma(k+u-2)}{\Gamma(u-2)\Gamma(k+1)}\\
\sim&(k+1)^{u-2},\end{split}

it follows that (
4.3
) is bounded by

\displaystyle C(k+1)^{n-2-\frac{n-2}{\alpha+1}}\sum_{i=0}^{\left\lfloor k_{0}/2\right\rfloor}\sum_{j+\ell_{0}=k_{0}-2i}(j+1)^{m-2}(\ell_{0}+1)^{\frac{n-2}{\alpha+1}}

\displaystyle\times\,b_{k,\ell,j}^{2}\left(\cos^{j}\phi\,\sin^{\ell_{0}}\phi\,P_{\frac{k_{0}-\ell_{0}-j}{2}}^{\left(\frac{n-2}{2(\alpha+1)}+\ell_{0},\frac{m}{2}+j-1\right)}(\cos 2\phi)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(k+1)^{n-2-\frac{n-2}{\alpha+1}}\left[e_{u+v,k_{0}}C_{k_{0}}^{\left(\frac{u+v}{2}-1\right)}(1)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(k+1)^{n-2-\frac{n-2}{\alpha+1}}\cdot(k+1)^{\frac{n-2}{\alpha+1}+m}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(k+1)^{n+m-2},

where in the second step we have used
n-2\geq\frac{n-2}{\alpha+1}
when
n\geq 2
. This proves the lemma when
k\equiv 0({\rm mod}(\alpha+1))
.

(II) For the cases when

k\equiv k_{\alpha}({\rm mod}(\alpha+1)),\quad k_{\alpha}\in\{1,\ldots,\alpha\},

or equivalently
k=k_{0}(\alpha+1)+k_{\alpha}
, we have

\ell=(\alpha+1)\ell_{0}+k_{\alpha},

by the condition
\widetilde{k}=\frac{k-\ell-j(\alpha+1)}{2(\alpha+1)}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Thus in these cases, the left hand side of (
4.2
) becomes

(4.7)

\begin{split}&\sin^{\frac{2k_{\alpha}}{\alpha+1}}\phi\sum_{i=0}^{\left\lfloor k_{0}/2\right\rfloor}\sum_{j+\ell_{0}=k_{0}-2i}\left(\cos^{j}\phi\,\sin^{\ell_{0}}\phi\,P_{\frac{k_{0}-\ell_{0}-j}{2}}^{\left(\frac{n-2+2k_{\alpha}}{2(\alpha+1)}+\ell_{0},\frac{m}{2}+j-1\right)}(\cos 2\phi)\right)^{2}\\
&\times b_{k,\ell,j}^{2}(\ell+1)^{n-2}(j+1)^{m-2}.\end{split}

Now setting
u=m
,
v=\frac{n-2+2k_{\alpha}}{\alpha+1}+2
,
{\rm k}=k_{0}
and
\phi=\xi
in the addition formula (
4.4
), we get

(4.8)

\displaystyle e_{u+v,k_{0}}C_{k_{0}}^{\left(\frac{m}{2}+\frac{n-2+2k_{\alpha}}{2(\alpha+1)}\right)}(\cos^{2}\phi\cos\theta_{1}+\sin^{2}\phi\cos\theta_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\left\lfloor k_{0}/2\right\rfloor}\sum_{j+\ell_{0}=k_{0}-2i}e_{u,j}C_{j}^{\left(\frac{m}{2}-1\right)}(\cos\theta_{1})\,e_{v,\ell_{0}}C_{\ell_{0}}^{\left(\frac{n-2+2k_{\alpha}}{2(\alpha+1)}\right)}(\cos\theta_{2})

\displaystyle\times b_{k,\ell,j}^{2}\left(\cos^{j}\phi\,\sin^{\ell_{0}}\phi\,P_{\frac{k_{0}-\ell_{0}-j}{2}}^{\left(\frac{n-2+2k_{\alpha}}{2(\alpha+1)}+\ell_{0},\frac{m}{2}+j-1\right)}(\cos 2\phi)\right)^{2}.

When
n\geq 4,
it is easy to see that

n-2\geq\frac{n-2+2k_{\alpha}}{\alpha+1}.

Similarly to the previous case, set
\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}=0
in (
4.8
). Then it follows that (
4.7
) is bounded by

\displaystyle C\left(\sin^{\frac{2k_{\alpha}}{\alpha+1}}\phi\right)(k+1)^{n-2-\frac{n-2+2k_{\alpha}}{\alpha+1}}\cdot e_{\frac{n-2+2k_{\alpha}}{\alpha+1}+m+2,k_{0}}C_{k_{0}}^{\left(\frac{m}{2}+\frac{n-2+2k_{\alpha}}{2(\alpha+1)}\right)}(1)

\displaystyle\leq C(k+1)^{n+m-2},

where we have used (
4.6
) again. This completes the proof.
∎

4.2.
Weighted

This subsection is devoted to deriving the weighted
L^{2}-L^{2}
estimates for the projector
P_{k}
in (
3.18
). The crucial ingredient is the weighted
L^{2}
-norm estimate for Jacobi polynomials. Here we provide two different approaches. The first one is based on the Bernstein-type inequality (
2.38
) for Jacobi polynomials obtained in
[
31
]
. The second one is based on the connection formula and some explicit calculations. Our proofs are short, straightforward, and completely different from the approach developed in
[
25
, Lemma 3.3]
for
\Delta_{1}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

Recall that the angle function in (
2.29
) is defined by

\displaystyle\psi(\phi):=\sin^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha+1}}\phi=\left(r_{1}/\rho\right)^{2\alpha}.

Lemma 4.7
.

Suppose
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
,
n,m\geq 2
and
\displaystyle 0\leq\beta<(\alpha+1)/(4\alpha)
, then there exists
C>0
depending only on
\alpha,\beta,n,
and
m
, such that, for every
g(\phi,\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\in\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\alpha}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\psi^{-\beta}(\phi)g\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,{\rm d}\Omega)}\leq C(k+1)^{\frac{1}{4}}\|g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,{\rm d}\Omega)}.

Proof.

For a fixed integer
k\geq 0
, let

(4.12)

\begin{split}&\bigg\{b_{k,\ell,j}h_{k,\ell,j}(\phi)Y_{\ell,p}(\omega_{1})Y_{j,q}(\omega_{2})\bigg|1\leq p\leq d_{\ell}(n),1\leq q\leq d_{j}(m),\\
&0\leq\ell,j\leq k,\,{\rm and}\,\,\ell+(\alpha+1)j\equiv k\left({\rm mod}(2\alpha+2)\right)\bigg\}\end{split}

be an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\alpha}\subset L^{2}(\Omega,{\rm d}\Omega)
. Note that (
4.12
) is also an orthogonal set in
L^{2}(\Omega,\psi^{-2\beta}(\phi){\rm d}\Omega)
. Thus, it is sufficient to show that there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
k
such that

(4.13)

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}|b_{k,\ell,j}h_{k,\ell,j}(\phi)|^{2}\left(\sin\phi\right)^{\frac{n-1+\alpha-4\alpha\beta}{\alpha+1}}\left(\cos\phi\right)^{m-1}{\rm d}\phi\\
=&\,\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}b_{k,\ell,j}^{2}\left(P_{\frac{k-\ell-j(\alpha+1)}{2(\alpha+1)}}^{\left(\frac{n-2+2\ell}{2(\alpha+1)},j+\frac{m}{2}-1\right)}(\cos 2\phi)\right)^{2}\\
&\times\left(\sin\phi\right)^{\frac{n-1+\alpha+2\ell-4\alpha\beta}{\alpha+1}}\left(\cos\phi\right)^{m-1+2j}{\rm d}\phi\\
\leq&\,C(k+1)^{\frac{1}{2}},\end{split}

for
\displaystyle 0\leq\beta<(\alpha+1)/(4\alpha)
.

On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}b_{k,\ell,j}^{2}\left(P_{\frac{k-\ell-j(\alpha+1)}{2(\alpha+1)}}^{\left(\frac{n-2+2\ell}{2(\alpha+1)},j+\frac{m}{2}-1\right)}(\cos 2\phi)\right)^{2}\left(\sin\phi\right)^{\frac{n-1+\alpha+2\ell-4\alpha\beta}{\alpha+1}}\left(\cos\phi\right)^{m-1+2j}{\rm d}\phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}b_{k,\ell,j}^{2}\left|P_{\frac{k-\ell-j(\alpha+1)}{2(\alpha+1)}}^{\left(\frac{n-2+2\ell}{2(\alpha+1)},j+\frac{m}{2}-1\right)}(x)\right|^{2}\left(\frac{1-x}{2}\right)^{\frac{n-2+2\ell-4\alpha\beta}{2(\alpha+1)}}\left(\frac{1+x}{2}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}-1+j}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}\left(\frac{k-\ell-j(\alpha+1)}{\alpha+1}+\frac{n-2+2\ell}{2(\alpha+1)}+j+\frac{m}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\times\int_{-1}^{1}\left|(1-x^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}g_{\frac{k-\ell-j(\alpha+1)}{2(\alpha+1)}}^{\left(\frac{n-2+2\ell}{2(\alpha+1)},j+\frac{m}{2}-1\right)}(x)\right|^{2}\left(\frac{1-x}{2}\right)^{-\frac{2\alpha\beta}{\alpha+1}-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{1+x}{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(k+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}\left(\frac{1-x}{2}\right)^{-\frac{2\alpha\beta}{\alpha+1}-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{1+x}{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(k+1)^{\frac{1}{2}},

where
g(x)
is defined in (
2.37
) and in the third step we used the the Bernstein-type estimate (
2.38
).
The last step is guaranteed by the condition
0\leq\beta<(\alpha+1)/(4\alpha)
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.8
.

It is seen that if
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
, then
1/2\leq(\alpha+1)/(4\alpha)
if and only if
\alpha=1
. We will show in the subsequent sections that the estimate in the above Lemma
4.7
is already enough to completely remove the degenerate weight
\psi
in (
1.2
) for the cases where
\alpha=1
. It will lead to interesting applications in the analysis of
H
-type groups.

Now, we start the second approach for the weighted
L^{2}-L^{2}
estimate for general
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
. Although the following explicit formulas on weighted
L^{2}
-norms of Jacobi polynomials are already known by experts, we provide a brief proof for the sake of completeness and to ensure self-containment.

Lemma 4.9
.

Assume
\gamma,\alpha,\beta>-1
. Then we have

(4.15)

\begin{split}I_{n}^{(\gamma,\alpha;\beta)}:=&\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left(P_{n}^{(\gamma,\beta)}(\cos 2\theta)\right)^{2}(\sin\theta)^{2\alpha+1}\,(\cos\theta)^{2\beta+1}\,{\rm d}\theta\\
=&\left(\frac{(\beta+1)_{n}}{(\alpha+\beta+2)_{n}}\right)^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(\frac{\alpha+\beta+2k+1}{\alpha+\beta+1}\right.\\
&\times\left.\frac{(\gamma-\alpha)_{n-k}(\alpha+\beta+1)_{k}(\beta+\gamma+n+1)_{k}}{(n-k)!(\beta+1)_{k}(\alpha+\beta+n+2)_{k}}\right)^{2}\\
&\times\frac{1}{2(2k+\alpha+\beta+1)}\frac{\Gamma(k+\alpha+1)\Gamma(k+\beta+1)}{\Gamma(k+\alpha+\beta+1)k!}.\end{split}

In particular when
\gamma>0
and
\beta>-1
, it holds that

(4.16)

\begin{split}I_{n}^{(\gamma,\gamma-1;\beta)}=&\,\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left(P_{n}^{(\gamma,\beta)}(\cos 2\theta)\right)^{2}(\sin\theta)^{2\gamma-1}(\cos\theta)^{2\beta+1}\,{\rm d}\theta\\
=&\,\frac{\Gamma(\gamma+n+1)\Gamma(\beta+n+1)}{2\gamma\Gamma(\gamma+\beta+n+1)n!}.\end{split}

Proof.

By the connection formula for Jacobi polynomials in
[
2
, Theorem 7.1.3]
, i.e.

\begin{split}P_{n}^{(\gamma,\beta)}(x)=&\frac{(\beta+1)_{n}}{(\alpha+\beta+2)_{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\alpha+\beta+2k+1}{\alpha+\beta+1}\\
&\times\frac{(\gamma-\alpha)_{n-k}(\alpha+\beta+1)_{k}(\beta+\gamma+n+1)_{k}}{(n-k)!(\beta+1)_{k}(\alpha+\beta+n+2)_{k}}P_{k}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x),\end{split}

and the orthogonal condition (
2.33
) (see also (
3.17
)), we obtain the first explicit formula (
4.15
). The other formula for
I_{n}^{(\gamma,\gamma-1;\beta)}
is obtained simply by changing variables in
[
8
, §16.4, (6)]
.
∎

The following bound is important for the second estimate.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
\gamma>0,\beta>-1
and
\gamma-1<\alpha<\gamma
. Then we have

\frac{I_{n}^{(\gamma,\alpha;\beta)}}{B_{n}^{\gamma,\beta}}\leq\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}(2n+\gamma+\beta+1)\right)^{\gamma-\alpha},

where
B_{n}^{\gamma,\beta}
and
I_{n}^{(\gamma,\alpha;\beta)}
are defined in (
3.17
) and (
4.15
), respectively.

Proof.

Denote
{\rm d}\mu(\theta)=\left(P_{n}^{(\gamma,\beta)}(\cos 2\theta)\right)^{2}(\sin\theta)^{2\gamma+1}(\cos\theta)^{2\beta+1}/B_{n}^{\gamma,\beta}\,{\rm d}\theta
. Obviously we have
\int_{0}^{\pi/2}{\rm d}\mu(\theta)=1
.

By Hölder’s inequality, we get

\begin{split}\frac{I_{n}^{(\gamma,\alpha;\beta)}}{B_{n}^{\gamma,\beta}}&=\frac{1}{B_{n}^{\gamma,\beta}}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left(P_{n}^{(\gamma,\beta)}(\cos 2\theta)\right)^{2}(\sin\theta)^{2\alpha+1}(\cos\theta)^{2\beta+1}\,{\rm d}\theta\\
&=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}1\cdot\frac{1}{(\sin\theta)^{2(\gamma-\alpha)}}\,{\rm d}\mu(\theta)\\
&\leq\left(\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{1}{(\sin\theta)^{2}}\,{\rm d}\mu(\theta)\right)^{\gamma-\alpha}\\
&=\left(\frac{I_{n}^{(\gamma,\gamma-1;\beta)}}{B_{n}^{\gamma,\beta}}\right)^{\gamma-\alpha}\\
&=\left((2n+\gamma+\beta+1)/\gamma\right)^{\gamma-\alpha}.\end{split}

The last step follows from the explicit constants in Eqs. (
3.17
) and (
4.16
).
∎

Remark 4.11
.

By Hilb’s approximation for Jacobi polynomials (see e.g.
[
45
, Theorem 8.21.12]
),
the asymptotic behaviour of
I_{n}^{(\gamma,\alpha;\beta)}
can be obtained (see
[
1
, p.1064]
),

\begin{split}I_{n}^{(\gamma,\alpha;\beta)}=\binom{n+\gamma}{n}^{2}\frac{\Gamma(2(\gamma-\alpha)-1)\Gamma(1+\alpha)}{2\Gamma(\gamma-\alpha)^{2}\Gamma(2\gamma-\alpha)}N^{-2(\alpha+1)}+\mathcal{O}(n^{\gamma-\alpha-\frac{3}{2}}),\end{split}

where
N=n+(\gamma+\beta+1)/2
. It suggests that the optimal estimate for
\gamma-1<\alpha<\gamma-1/2
in Lemma
4.10
should be

\frac{I_{n}^{(\gamma,\alpha;\beta)}}{B_{n}^{\gamma,\beta}}\leq C\left(2n+\gamma+\beta+1\right)^{2(\gamma-\alpha)-1},

with a constant
C
only depending on
\gamma-\alpha
. Unfortunately, it is currently unclear to the authors how to prove this.

Remark 4.12
.

For
\alpha,\beta>0
, the following relations hold

\frac{I_{n-1}^{(\gamma+1,\alpha+1;\beta+1)}}{B_{n-1}^{\gamma+1,\beta+1}}\leq\frac{I_{n}^{(\gamma,\alpha;\beta)}}{B_{n}^{\gamma,\beta}},\qquad\frac{I_{n-1}^{(\gamma+2,\alpha+2;\beta)}}{{B_{n-1}^{\gamma+2,\beta}}}\leq\frac{I_{n}^{(\gamma,\alpha;\beta)}}{B_{n}^{\gamma,\beta}}.

The proof is similar as the subsequent (
4.29
), hence we omit it.

The cases with
\gamma=0
are not included in Lemma
4.10
, because
I_{n}^{(0,-1;\beta)}
may not converge. Thanks to the bound given in (
2.39
), we still have bounds for the following cases.

Lemma 4.13
.

Suppose
\gamma,\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\gamma-1<\alpha<\gamma
. For any given
0<\varepsilon<1
,
there exists
C>0
only depending on
\varepsilon
and
\gamma-\alpha
such that

\frac{I_{n}^{(\gamma,\alpha;\beta)}}{B_{n}^{\gamma,\beta}}\leq C\left(2n+\gamma+\beta+1\right)^{\frac{\gamma-\alpha}{1-\varepsilon}},

where
B_{n}^{\gamma,\beta}
and
I_{n}^{(\gamma,\alpha;\beta)}
are defined in (
3.17
) and (
4.15
), respectively.

Proof.

By (
2.39
), we have

(4.17)

\frac{1}{B_{n}^{\gamma,\beta}}\left(P_{n}^{(\gamma,\beta)}(\cos 2\theta)\right)^{2}(\sin\theta)^{2\gamma}(\cos\theta)^{2\beta}\leq 2(2n+\gamma+\beta+1),

for all
\gamma,\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\theta\in[0,\pi/2]
.

Similarly as was done in Lemma
4.10
, by Hölder inequality, we have

\begin{split}\frac{I_{n}^{(\gamma,\alpha;\beta)}}{B_{n}^{\gamma,\beta}}&=\frac{1}{B_{n}^{\gamma,\beta}}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left(P_{n}^{(\gamma,\beta)}(\cos 2\theta)\right)^{2}(\sin\theta)^{2\alpha+1}(\cos\theta)^{2\beta+1}\,{\rm d}\theta\\
&=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}1\cdot\frac{1}{(\sin\theta)^{2(\gamma-\alpha)}}\,{\rm d}\mu(\theta)\\
&\leq\left(\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{1}{(\sin\theta)^{2(1-\varepsilon)}}\,{\rm d}\mu(\theta)\right)^{\frac{\gamma-\alpha}{1-\varepsilon}}\\
&\leq\left(2(2n+\gamma+\beta+1)\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{\sin\theta\cos\theta}{(\sin\theta)^{2(1-\varepsilon)}}\,{\rm d}\theta\right)^{\frac{\gamma-\alpha}{1-\varepsilon}}\\
&\leq C\left(2n+\gamma+\beta+1\right)^{\frac{\gamma-\alpha}{1-\varepsilon}},\end{split}

where we have used (
4.17
) in the fourth step.
∎

m\geq 2
is even, for a given

and

Theorem 4.15
.

Suppose
\displaystyle 0\leq\beta<1/2
. When
(1)

n\geq 3
,
m\geq 2
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
, or
(2)

n=2
,
m\geq 2
and
\alpha=1
, there exists a constant
C>0
, depending on
\alpha,\beta,n
and
m
, such that for every
g\in L^{2}(\Omega,{\rm d}\Omega)
, the following holds

(4.18)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left|\psi^{-\beta}(\phi)P_{k}\left(\psi^{-\beta}(\cdot)g\right)(\phi,\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\right|^{2}{\rm d}\Omega\leq C(k+1)^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha+1}}\int_{\Omega}|g|^{2}{\rm d}\Omega.

Proof.

Let

(4.19)

\displaystyle T_{\beta,k}(g)(\phi,\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=\psi^{-\beta}(\phi)P_{k}(g)(\phi,\omega_{1},\omega_{2}).

Then by Lemma
4.7
and Lemma

(4.20)

\begin{split}\|T_{\beta,k}(g)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,{\rm d}\Omega)}\leq&\,C(k+1)^{\frac{\alpha}{2(\alpha+1)}}\|P_{k}(g)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,{\rm d}\Omega)}\\
\leq&\,C(k+1)^{\frac{\alpha}{2(\alpha+1)}}\|g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,{\rm d}\Omega)}\end{split}

for every
g\in L^{2}(\Omega,{\rm d}\Omega)
.

On the other hand, the adjoint operator of
T_{\beta,k}
is given by

\displaystyle T_{\beta,k}^{*}(g)(\phi,\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=P_{k}\left(\psi^{-\beta}(\cdot)g\right)(\phi,\omega_{1},\omega_{2}).

Since
P_{k}
is a projection operator, we can write

\displaystyle\psi^{-\beta}(\phi)P_{k}\left(\psi^{-\beta}(\cdot)g\right)(\phi,\omega_{1},\omega_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi^{-\beta}(\phi)P_{k}\circ P_{k}(\psi^{-\beta}(\cdot)g)(\phi,\omega_{1},\omega_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T_{\beta,k}\circ T_{\beta,k}^{*}(g)(\phi,\omega_{1},\omega_{2}).

By (
4.20
) and a standard duality argument for the norm of
T_{\beta,k}^{*}
, we obtain the desired estimate (
4.18
).
∎

In this subsection, we present a tighter weighted

The main result of this subsection is as follows.

Theorem 4.18
.

Assume

.

,

Remark 4.19

Eq. (

Remark 4.20

When

Recall that for any

h_{k,\ell}(\phi)=b_{k,\ell}\sin^{\frac{\ell}{\alpha+1}}\phi\,C_{\frac{k-\ell}{\alpha+1}}^{\frac{2\ell+n-2}{2(\alpha+1)}+\frac{1}{2}}(\cos\phi),\quad 0\leq\ell\leq k,\;\ell\equiv k\,({\rm mod}(\alpha+1)),

where

\left\{h_{k,\ell}(\phi)Y_{\ell,j}(\omega)\,\arrowvert\,0\leq\ell\leq k,\ell\equiv k\,({\rm mod}(\alpha+1)),1\leq j\leq d_{\ell}\right\}

over
\partial B_{1}

3.7
. Similarly as done in Section
4.2
as well as in

4.18
will follow if we can show,

Lemma 4.21
.

Assume
n\geq 2
. There exists
C>0
only depending on
\alpha,\beta
and
n
, such that

(4.25)

\int_{0}^{\pi}\left|h_{k,\ell}(\phi)\right|^{2}(\sin\phi)^{\frac{n-2}{\alpha+1}+1-2\beta}\,{\rm d}\phi\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle C,&0\leq\beta<\frac{1}{2},\\
\displaystyle C(k+1)^{2\beta-1},&\frac{1}{2}<\beta<1.\end{array}\right.

To establish (
4.25
), we derive some estimates valid for Gegenbauer polynomials. First, we
recall the following results in
[
11
, Theorem 5 and 6]
.

Theorem 4.22
.

Let
\lambda>0
and
\mu>-1/2
. Then we have

(4.26)

\begin{split}J_{j}^{(\lambda;\mu)}:=&\int_{0}^{\pi}\left|C_{j}^{(\lambda)}(\cos\theta)\right|^{2}(\sin\theta)^{2\mu}\,{\rm d}\theta\\
=&\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\left(\mu+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\mu\Gamma(\mu+1)}\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\frac{j}{2}\rfloor}\frac{(\lambda)_{j-k}^{2}(\lambda-\mu)_{k}^{2}}{(\mu+1)_{j-k}^{2}(k!)^{2}}(j+\mu-2k)\frac{(2\mu)_{j-2k}}{(j-2k)!}.\end{split}

Furthermore,
J_{j}^{(\lambda;\mu)}
satisfies the asymptotic estimate

(4.27)

J_{j}^{(\lambda;\mu)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\mu+\frac{1}{2}-\lambda)}{2^{2\lambda-1}\Gamma(\lambda)^{2}\Gamma(\mu+1-\lambda)}j^{2\lambda-2}+\mathcal{O}(j^{\eta}),&\mu>\lambda-\frac{1}{2},\\
\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\lambda-\mu-\frac{1}{2})\Gamma(\mu+\frac{1}{2})}{2^{2\lambda-1}\Gamma(\lambda)^{2}\Gamma(\lambda-\mu)\Gamma(2\lambda-\mu-\frac{1}{2})}j^{4\lambda-2\mu-3}+\mathcal{O}(j^{\eta}),&\mu<\lambda-\frac{1}{2},\end{array}\right.

with

\eta=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\max(2\lambda-3,4\lambda-2\mu-3),&\mu>\lambda-\frac{1}{2},\\
\max(2\lambda-2,4\lambda-2\mu-4),&\mu<\lambda-\frac{1}{2}.\end{array}\right.

Remark 4.23
.

The explicit weighted
L^{2}
-norm of Gegenbauer polynomials in (
4.26
) is again derived by the connection formula (see
[
45
, (4.10.27)]
)

C_{j}^{(\lambda)}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\frac{j}{2}\rfloor}\frac{(\lambda)_{j-k}(\lambda-\mu)_{k}}{(\mu+1)_{j-k}k!}\frac{j+\mu-2k}{\mu}C_{j-2k}^{(\mu)}(x),

and the orthogonality relation (
2.33
).

For convenience, we set

\begin{split}b^{(\lambda)}_{j}:=\left(\int_{0}^{\pi}\left(C_{j}^{(\lambda)}(\cos\theta)\right)^{2}\sin^{2\lambda}\theta{\rm d}\theta\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\left(\frac{2^{1-2\lambda}\pi\Gamma(j+2\lambda)}{[\Gamma(\lambda)]^{2}(j+\lambda)\Gamma(j+1)}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\end{split}

for
\lambda>-1/2
and
\lambda\neq 0
.

Theorem 4.24
.

Let
\lambda>0
and
\mu>-1/2
. There exists a constant
C>0
only depending on
\lambda
and
\mu
, such that

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{\pi}\left|b_{j-\ell}^{(\lambda+\ell)}C_{j-\ell}^{(\lambda+\ell)}(\cos\theta)\right|^{2}(\sin\theta)^{2(\mu+\ell)}\,{\rm d}\theta\\
\leq&\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}C,&\displaystyle\lambda-1/2<\mu\leq\lambda,\\
C(j+1)^{2(\lambda-\mu)-1},&\displaystyle\mu<\lambda-1/2.\end{array}\right.\end{split}

for all
j,\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
j\geq\ell
.

Proof.

We prove the claim in two steps. First we prove the results for
\ell=0
and all
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then we derive the remaining by a decreasing relation.

(I) When
\lambda-1/2<\mu\leq\lambda
and
\ell=0
, by Theorem
4.22
, there exists a constant
C(\lambda,\mu)
such that

(4.28)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\pi}\left|b_{j}^{(\lambda)}C_{j}^{(\lambda)}(\cos\theta)\right|^{2}(\sin\theta)^{2\mu}\,{\rm d}\theta=&\,\left(\frac{2^{1-2\lambda}\pi\Gamma(j+2\lambda)}{[\Gamma(\lambda)]^{2}(j+\lambda)\Gamma(j+1)}\right)^{-1}\\
&\times\left(\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\mu+\frac{1}{2}-\lambda)}{2^{2\lambda-1}\Gamma(\lambda)^{2}\Gamma(\mu+1-\lambda)}j^{2\lambda-2}+\mathcal{O}(j^{\eta})\right)\\
\leq&\,C(\lambda,\mu)\end{split}

holds for all
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. This can also be seen from Askey’s transplantation theorem
[
3
]
.
Similarly, we obtain the estimate for
\mu<\lambda-1/2
for
\ell=0
.

(II) For
\mu<\lambda-1/2
or
\lambda-1/2<\mu\leq\lambda
, we consider the following ratio

(4.29)

\begin{split}&\frac{\left(b_{j-1}^{(\lambda+1)}\right)^{2}J_{j-1}^{\left(\lambda+1;\mu+1\right)}}{\left(b_{j}^{(\lambda)}\right)^{2}J_{j}^{(\lambda;\mu)}}=\frac{\int_{0}^{\pi}\left|b_{j-1}^{(\lambda+1)}C_{j-1}^{(\lambda+1)}(\cos\theta)\right|^{2}(\sin\theta)^{2(\mu+1)}\,{\rm d}\theta}{\int_{0}^{\pi}\left|b_{j}^{(\lambda)}C_{j}^{(\lambda)}(\cos\theta)\right|^{2}(\sin\theta)^{2\mu}\,{\rm d}\theta}\\
=&\frac{4\lambda^{2}}{j(j+2\lambda)}\cdot\frac{\mu\left(\mu+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{(\mu+1)^{2}}\cdot\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\frac{j-1}{2}\rfloor}\frac{(\lambda+1)_{j-1-k}^{2}(\lambda-\mu)_{k}^{2}}{(\mu+2)_{j-1-k}^{2}(k!)^{2}}(j+\mu-2k)\frac{(2\mu+2)_{j-1-2k}}{(j-1-2k)!}}{\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\frac{j}{2}\rfloor}\frac{(\lambda)_{j-k}^{2}(\lambda-\mu)_{k}^{2}}{(\mu+1)_{j-k}^{2}(k!)^{2}}(j+\mu-2k)\frac{(2\mu)_{j-2k}}{(j-2k)!}}.\end{split}

with
j\geq 1
.

Note that each term in the right-hand side of (
4.26
) is positive (due to
\mu>-1/2
and
(\mu)_{0}=1
). For a fixed
k
satisfying
0\leq k\leq\lfloor(j-1)/2)\rfloor
, we check the ratios of the corresponding terms in the two summations of the right-hand side of (
4.29
),

(4.30)

\begin{split}&\frac{4\lambda^{2}}{j(j+2\lambda)}\cdot\frac{\mu\left(\mu+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{(\mu+1)^{2}}\cdot\frac{\frac{(\lambda+1)_{j-1-k}^{2}(\lambda-\mu)_{k}^{2}}{(\mu+2)_{j-1-k}^{2}(k!)^{2}}(j+\mu-2k)\frac{(2\mu+2)_{j-1-2k}}{(j-1-2k)!}}{\frac{(\lambda)_{j-k}^{2}(\lambda-\mu)_{k}^{2}}{(\mu+1)_{j-k}^{2}(k!)^{2}}(j+\mu-2k)\frac{(2\mu)_{j-2k}}{(j-2k)!}}\\
=&\,\frac{4\lambda^{2}}{j(j+2\lambda)}\cdot\frac{\mu\left(\mu+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{(\mu+1)^{2}}\cdot\frac{(\mu+1)^{2}(2\mu+j-2k)(j-2k)}{2\mu(2\mu+1)\lambda^{2}}\\
=&\,\frac{(j-2k)(j+2\mu-2k)}{j(j+2\lambda)}\\
\leq&\,1.\end{split}

By (
4.30
) and the fact
\lfloor(j-1)/2\rfloor\leq\lfloor j/2\rfloor
as well as (
4.28
), we obtain

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{\pi}\left|b_{j-1}^{(\lambda+1)}C_{j-1}^{(\lambda+1)}(\cos\theta)\right|^{2}(\sin\theta)^{2(\mu+1)}\,{\rm d}\theta\\
\leq&\,\int_{0}^{\pi}\left|b_{j}^{(\lambda)}C_{j}^{(\lambda)}(\cos\theta)\right|^{2}(\sin\theta)^{2\mu}\,{\rm d}\theta\\
\leq&\,C(\lambda,\mu),\end{split}

for all
j-1\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Similarly, we obtain the estimate for
\mu<\lambda-1/2
.
By induction, we complete the proof.
∎

5.
Carleman estimates and unique continuation

5.1.
Carleman inequalities

Using the weighted
L^{p}-L^{q}
bounds from Theorem
4.17
for the projector
P_{k}
in (
3.18
), we establish Carleman estimates for the higher step Grushin operator
\Delta_{\alpha}
in this subsection.

Recall that
\psi(\phi)=\sin^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha+1}}\phi
and
Q=n+m(\alpha+1)
.
We first consider the cases when (1)
n\geq 4
,
m\geq 2
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
, or (2)
n\in\{2,3\}
,
m\geq 2
and
\alpha=1
. Let

(5.1)

p=\frac{2\left(n+m-2+\frac{1}{\alpha+1}\right)}{n+m-2+\frac{2}{\alpha+1}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad q=\frac{2\left(n+m-2+\frac{1}{\alpha+1}\right)}{n+m-2}.

It is seen that

\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1\quad\mbox{and}\quad\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{(\alpha+1)(n+m-2)+1}.

The main result of this subsection is as follows.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
0<\varepsilon<1/8
,
s>100
,
\delta={\rm dist}\,(s,\mathbb{N})>0
, and consider
p
and
q
as defined in (
5.1
). Then there exists a constant
C>0
depending only on
\alpha,\varepsilon,\delta,n
and
m
, such that

(5.2)

\begin{split}&\left\|\rho^{-s}\psi^{\varepsilon}g\right\|_{L^{q}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n+m},\rho^{-Q}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y\right)}\leq C\left\|\rho^{-s+2}\psi^{-\varepsilon}\Delta_{\alpha}(g)\right\|_{L^{p}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n+m},\rho^{-Q}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y\right)}\\
\end{split}

for
g\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m}\backslash\{0\})
.

Proof.

We prove it in four steps. Our proof follows the approach developed in
[
34
]
by Jerison, which was later extended to the Grushin setting by Garofalo and Shen in
[
25
, Theorem 5.1]
.

(I) We express the desired estimate (
5.2
) in an equivalent form.
Let
\rho=e^{t},t\in\mathbb{R}
. The Grushin operator
\Delta_{\alpha}
in (
2.30
) now can be written as

(5.3)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\alpha}=e^{-2t}\psi(\phi)\cdot\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}+(Q-2)\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\Delta_{\sigma}\right),

where
\Delta_{\sigma}
is given in (
2.31
). Thus for
u_{k}(\phi,\omega)\in\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\alpha}
where
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
, by (
3.4
) and (
5.3
), we have

(5.4)

\displaystyle\rho^{-s+2}\Delta_{\alpha}(\rho^{s}e^{it\eta}u_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{(-s+2)t}\Delta_{\alpha}(e^{st}e^{it\eta}u_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(k-(s+i\eta))(k+Q-2+s+i\eta)\,\psi\,e^{it\eta}\,u_{k}.

For later use, we denote

a_{s}(\eta,k)=-\left((k-(s+i\eta))(k+Q-2+s+i\eta)\right)^{-1}.

Moreover, for any
f(t,\phi,\omega)=g(e^{t},\phi,\omega)\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}\times\Omega)
, denote

\hat{f}(\eta,\phi,\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-it\eta}f(t,\phi,\omega)\,{\rm d}t.

Similarly as done in
[
25
, (5.11)]
and
[
34
, p. 130]
,
we introduce the following operator,

\begin{split}R_{s}(f)(t,\phi,\omega):=&\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}a_{s}(\eta,k)e^{it\eta}Q_{k}(\hat{f})(\eta,\phi,\omega)\,{\rm d}\eta\\
=&\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}a_{s}(\eta,k)e^{i(t-w)\eta}Q_{k}\,{\rm d}\eta\right)f(w,\phi,\omega)\,{\rm d}w,\end{split}

where
Q_{k}
is the operator given by

Q_{k}(f)(\eta,\phi,\omega):=P_{k}\left(\frac{f(\eta,\cdot,\cdot)}{\psi(\cdot)}\right)(\phi,\omega).

The operator
R_{s}
is the inverse of the operator
L_{s}(f):=\rho^{-s+2}\Delta_{\alpha}(\rho^{s}f)
at least formally.
It can be formally verified by (
5.4
) and the fact
f(t)=(1/2\pi)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{its}\hat{f}(s)\,{\rm d}s
.

Moreover, it is not hard to see from Eq. (
3.14
) for
{\rm d}\Omega
and Eq. (
2.26
) for
{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y
that

\rho^{-Q}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y=\frac{1}{(\alpha+1)^{m}}\psi^{-1}\,{\rm d}t\,{\rm d}\Omega.

Collecting everything, we see that the estimate (
5.2
) is equivalent to

(5.5)

\begin{split}&\|R_{s}(f)\|_{L^{q}\left(\mathbb{R}\times\Omega,\psi^{-1+\varepsilon q}\,{\rm d}t\,{\rm d}\Omega\right)}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{p}\left(\mathbb{R}\times\Omega,\psi^{-1-\varepsilon p}\,{\rm d}t\,{\rm d}\Omega\right)}\\
\end{split}

for all
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}\times\Omega)
.

(II) In this step, we introduce new operators based on a partition of unity in order to prove (
5.5
). Fix
s>100
with
{\rm dist}(s,\mathbb{N})=\delta>0
. Assume
2^{N}\leq(s/10)<2^{N+1}
.
We choose the same partition of unity

\{\Phi_{\gamma}\}_{\gamma=0}^{N}
for
\mathbb{R}_{+}
as in
[
25
, (5.14)]
and
[
34
, (9)]
, such that

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sum_{\gamma=0}^{N}\Phi_{\gamma}(r)=1,&\hbox{all $r>0$;}\\
{\rm supp}\,\Phi_{\gamma}\subset\{r:2^{\gamma-2}\leq r\leq 2^{\gamma}\},&\hbox{$\gamma=1,2,\ldots,N-1$;}\\
{\rm supp}\,\Phi_{0}\subset\{r:0<r\leq 1\},&\\
{\rm supp}\,\Phi_{N}\subset\{r:r\geq s/40\},&\end{array}\right.

and satisfying

(5.11)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{{\rm d}^{\ell}}{{\rm d}r^{\ell}}\Phi_{\gamma}(r)\right|\leq C_{\ell}2^{-\gamma\ell},\quad\ell=0,1,2,\ldots.

For
0\leq\gamma\leq N
, we let

(5.12)

\displaystyle a_{s}^{\gamma}(\eta,k):=\Phi_{\gamma}(|k-s-i\eta|)a_{s}(\eta,k)

and define

\displaystyle R_{s}^{\gamma}(f)(t,\phi,\omega)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}a_{s}^{\gamma}(\eta,k)e^{it\eta}Q_{k}(\tilde{f})(\eta,\phi,\omega)\,{\rm d}\eta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}a_{s}^{\gamma}(\eta,k)e^{i(t-w)\eta}Q_{k}\,{\rm d}\eta\right)f(w,\phi,\omega)\,{\rm d}w.

Obviously, it holds that

R_{s}=\sum_{\gamma=0}^{N}R_{s}^{\gamma}.

(III) We estimate
R_{s}^{\gamma}
for
0\leq\gamma\leq N-1
in this step.
Observing when
0\leq\gamma\leq N-1
,
a_{s}^{\gamma}(\eta,k)
is supported where
\delta 2^{\gamma-2}\leq|k-s-i\eta|\leq 2^{\gamma}
. Therefore, there are at most
2\cdot 2^{\gamma}
non-zero terms in the sum over
k
which defines
R_{s}^{\gamma}
. Furthermore, the value of
k
in each case is comparable to
s
. Now, by Eqs. (
5.11
), (
5.12
) and the aforementioned facts, it can be verified that

(5.13)

\displaystyle\left|\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\right)^{j}a_{s}^{\gamma}(\eta,k)\right|\leq C_{j}\cdot 2^{-(1+j)\gamma}\cdot s^{-1},\qquad j=0,1,\ldots.

The
L^{p}-L^{q}
estimate (
4.21
) in Theorem
4.17
implies that

\displaystyle\left\|Q_{k}(f)\right\|_{L^{q}\left(\Omega,\psi^{-\beta q}{\rm d}\Omega\right)}\leq C(k+1)^{\frac{n+m-2}{n+m-2+\frac{1}{\alpha+1}}}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}\left(\Omega,\psi^{\beta p-p}{\rm d}\Omega\right)}

for
0\leq\beta<1/q
. Letting
\varepsilon=1/q-\beta
, we get

(5.14)

\begin{split}&\left\|Q_{k}(f)\right\|_{L^{q}\left(\Omega,\psi^{-1+\varepsilon q}\,{\rm d}\Omega\right)}\\
\leq&\,C(k+1)^{\frac{n+m-2}{n+m-2+\frac{1}{\alpha+1}}}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}\left(\Omega,\psi^{-1-\varepsilon p}\,{\rm d}\Omega\right)}.\end{split}

Now performing integration by parts, we conclude from (
5.13
) and (
5.14
) that

\displaystyle\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i(t-w)\eta}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{s}^{\gamma}(\eta,k)Q_{k}(f)\,{\rm d}\eta\right\|_{L^{q}\left(\Omega,\psi^{-1+\varepsilon q}\,{\rm d}\Omega\right)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C}{|t-w|^{j}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\right)^{j}a_{s}^{\gamma}(\eta,k)\right|\,{\rm d}\eta\|Q_{k}(f)\|_{L^{q}\left(\Omega,\psi^{-1+\varepsilon q}\,{\rm d}\Omega\right)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C}{|t-w|^{j}}\cdot 2^{-j\gamma}\cdot 2^{\gamma}\cdot s^{-1+\frac{n+m-2}{n+m-2+\frac{1}{\alpha+1}}}\|f\|_{L^{p}\left(\Omega,\psi^{-1-\varepsilon p}\,{\rm d}\Omega\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{C}{\left(2^{\gamma}|t-w|\right)^{j}}\cdot 2^{\gamma}\cdot s^{-\frac{1}{(\alpha+1)(n+m-2)+1}}\|f\|_{L^{p}\left(\Omega,\psi^{-1-\varepsilon p}\,{\rm d}\Omega\right)}.

Here the second inequality is because the integration in
\eta
is over an interval of length
\leq 2\cdot 2^{\gamma}
and there are at most
2\cdot 2^{\gamma}
non-zero terms in the sum over
k
.

Now choosing
j=0
and
j=10
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i(t-w)\eta}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{s}^{\gamma}(\eta,k)Q_{k}(f)\,{\rm d}\eta\right\|_{L^{q}\left(\Omega,\psi^{-1+\varepsilon q}\,{\rm d}\Omega\right)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C}{(1+2^{\gamma}|t-w|)^{10}}\cdot s^{-\frac{1}{(\alpha+1)(n+m-2)+1}}\cdot 2^{\gamma}\|f\|_{L^{p}\left(\Omega,\psi^{-1-\varepsilon p}\,{\rm d}\Omega\right)}.

Thus, for
0\leq\gamma\leq N-1
, we have,

\displaystyle\left\|R_{s}^{\gamma}(f)\right\|_{L^{q}\left(\mathbb{R}\times\Omega,\psi^{-1+\varepsilon q}{\rm d}t{\rm d}\Omega\right)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\cdot s^{-\frac{1}{(\alpha+1)(n+m-2)+1}}\cdot 2^{\gamma}\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{(1+2^{\gamma}|t-w|)^{10}}\|f(t,\cdot,\cdot)\|_{L^{p}\left(\Omega,\psi^{-1-\varepsilon p}{\rm d}\Omega\right)}{\rm d}t\right\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R},{\rm d}t)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\cdot s^{-\frac{1}{(\alpha+1)(n+m-2)+1}}\cdot 2^{\gamma}\left\|\frac{1}{(1+2^{\gamma}|\cdot|)^{10}}\right\|_{L^{\frac{n+m-2+1/(\alpha+1)}{n+m-2}}(\mathbb{R},{\rm d}t)}\|f\|_{L^{p}\left(\mathbb{R}\times\Omega,\psi^{-1-\varepsilon p}{\rm d}t{\rm d}\Omega\right)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\cdot s^{-\frac{1}{(\alpha+1)(n+m-2)+1}}\cdot 2^{\frac{\gamma}{(\alpha+1)(n+m-2)+1}}\|f\|_{L^{p}\left(\mathbb{R}\times\Omega,\psi^{-1-\varepsilon p}{\rm d}t{\rm d}\Omega\right)},

where the first inequality is by Minkowski’s inequality and the second inequality is by Young’s convolution inequality, respectively.
Since

\sum_{\gamma=0}^{N-1}2^{\frac{\gamma}{(\alpha+1)(n+m-2)+1}}\leq s^{(\alpha+1)(n+m-2)+1)^{-1}},

we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma=0}^{N-1}\left\|R_{s}^{\gamma}(f)\right\|_{L^{q}\left(\mathbb{R}\times\Omega,\psi^{-1+\varepsilon q}\,{\rm d}t\,{\rm d}\Omega\right)}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{p}\left(\mathbb{R}\times\Omega,\psi^{-1-\varepsilon p}\,{\rm d}t\,{\rm d}\Omega\right)}.

(IV) We still need to estimate
R_{s}^{N}(g)
. It can be checked that

(5.15)

\displaystyle\left|\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\right)^{j}a_{s}^{N}(\eta,k)\right|\leq\frac{C_{j}}{(|\eta|+s+k)^{j+2}}.

By (
5.15
) and integration by parts, choosing
j=0
and
j=1
, it follows that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i(t-w)\eta}a_{s}^{N}(\eta,k)\,{\rm d}\eta\right|\leq\frac{C}{(k+s)[1+|t-w|(k+s)]}.

Furthermore, by the above estimate and the bound in (
5.14
) for
Q_{k}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i(t-w)\eta}a_{s}^{N}(\eta,k)Q_{k}(f)\,{\rm d}\eta\right\|_{L^{q}\left(\Omega,\psi^{-1+\varepsilon q}{\rm d}t{\rm d}\Omega\right)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(k+1)^{\frac{n+m-2}{n+m-2+\frac{1}{\alpha+1}}}}{(k+s)[1+|t-w|(k+s)]}\|f\|_{L^{p}\left(\Omega,\psi^{-1-\varepsilon p}{\rm d}t{\rm d}\Omega\right)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left\{\sum_{k\leq\frac{1}{|t-w|}}(k+1)^{-\frac{1}{(\alpha+1)(n+m-2)+1}}+\sum_{k>\frac{1}{|t-w|}}(k+1)^{-1-\frac{1}{(\alpha+1)(n+m-2)+1}}|t-w|^{-1}\right\}

\displaystyle\times\|f\|_{L^{p}\left(\Omega,\psi^{-1-\varepsilon p}{\rm d}t{\rm d}\Omega\right)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C}{|t-w|^{\frac{n+m-2}{n+m-2+\frac{1}{\alpha+1}}}}\|f\|_{L^{p}\left(\Omega,\psi^{-1-\varepsilon p}{\rm d}t{\rm d}\Omega\right).}

Now, the desired estimate for the last term
R_{s}^{N}
follows by applying the Minkowski’s inequality and using the well-known bounds on fractional integration.

Combining the estimates in (III) and (IV), we conclude the proof.
∎

5.2.
The strong unique continuation property

For
\rho>0
and
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we denote

\begin{split}B_{\rho}:=&B_{\rho}((0,y_{0}))\\
=&\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}\bigg|\left(|x|^{2(\alpha+1)}+(\alpha+1)^{2}|y-y_{0}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2(\alpha+1)}}<\rho\right\}.\end{split}

Let
S^{2}(B_{\rho})
be the closure of
C_{0}^{\infty}(B_{\rho})
under the norm

\displaystyle\|u\|_{S^{2}(B_{\rho})}=\left\{\int_{B_{\rho}}\left(|\nabla_{x}^{2}u|^{2}+\left||x|^{2\alpha}\nabla_{y}^{2}u\right|^{2}+|\nabla_{x}u|^{2}+\left||x|^{\alpha}\nabla_{y}u\right|^{2}+|u|^{2}\right)\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Thanks to the Sobolev inequality for the Grushin operator (see
[
43
, Eq. (1.3)]
), we have
u\in L^{p_{0}}(B_{\rho})
if
u\in S^{2}(B_{\rho})
, where
p_{0}=2Q/(Q-2)
in which
Q
is the homogeneous dimension in (
2.4
).

The strong unique continuation property is based on the following concept, see e.g.
[
21
,
25
]
.

Definition 5.3
.

We say that function
u
vanishes of infinite order at the point
(0,y_{0})
in the
L^{p}
mean, if for all
N>0
,

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\rho}((0,y_{0}))}|u|^{p}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y=\mathcal{O}(\rho^{N}),\qquad\mbox{as}\quad\rho\rightarrow 0.

Now, using the Carleman estimates obtained in Section
5.1
and the aforementioned Sobolev inequality, we can give the strong unique continuation property for the Schrödinger operators
-\Delta_{\alpha}+V
at points of the degeneracy manifold
\{(0,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}|y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\}
. In particular, we determine the allowed potential space
L_{{\rm loc}}^{r}
. Since it is obtained by adapting the proof in
[
25
, Theorem 6.4]
with some small changes, we do not repeat the details of the proof here.

.

Suppose that
u\in S^{2}(B_{\rho_{0}}((0,y_{0})))
for some
\rho_{0}>0
and
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Also, assume that

(5.17)

\displaystyle\left|\Delta_{x}u+|x|^{2\alpha}\Delta_{y}u\right|\leq|Vu|\qquad\mbox{in}\quad B_{\rho_{0}}=B_{\rho_{0}}((0,y_{0}))

for potential
V\in L_{{\rm loc}}^{r}

0\leq 1/r<1/p-1/q
with

and
5.2
. Then
u\equiv 0
in
B_{\rho_{0}}
if
u
vanishes of infinite order at
(0,y_{0})

mean.

Conclusions

We have constructed an orthogonal basis of Grushin-harmonics on
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
with
n,m\geq 2
in Section
3
. However, due to conditions in the polar coordinates (
2.2
) and (
2.2
), the cases for
n=1
and
m\geq 1
deserve to be discussed separately. As far as we see, it is closely related with the so-called generalized Gegenbauer polynomials arising in the study of Dunkl operator on the line
[
19
]
. It is worth mentioning that the
L^{2}-L^{2}
-weighted estimates for the cases where
n=2
,
m\geq 3
odd and
\alpha\geq 2
are not established yet. Finally, besides the potential applications in the study of unique continuation properties on
H
-type groups, our bounds in Section
4
will be useful to establish unique continuation results for the Schrödinger operators
-\Delta_{\alpha}+V
with singular potentials, for instance the Hardy-type potentials, as studied in the Euclidean setting.
