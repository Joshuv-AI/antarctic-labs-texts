\exp\left(-2\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{\mathbf{S}_{2k-1}(q)}{(2k)!}(-4X^{2})^{k}\right)=\prod_{j\geq 1}\left[1+\frac{4(\sin^{2}X)q^{j}}{(1-q^{j})^{2}}\right].

We also require the following convenient generating function for normalized Bernoulli numbers (for example, see equation (1.518.1) of
[
10
]
).

Lemma 4.2
.

As a power series in
X
, we have that

\begin{split}\frac{\sin(X)}{X}=\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{(-4)^{k}B_{2k}}{(2k)(2k)!}\cdot X^{2k}\right).\end{split}

4.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

The generating function for
M(m,n)
(see
[
4
,
9
]
) is given by

(4.1)

\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}M(m,n)z^{m}q^{n}=\prod_{n\geq 1}\frac{1-q^{n}}{(1-zq^{n})(1-z^{-1}q^{n})}.

After letting
z=e^{2iX},
we pair up conjugate terms to obtain

\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}M(m,n)q^{n}e^{2imX}=\prod_{n\geq 1}\frac{(1-q^{n})}{(1-2\cos(2X)q^{n}+q^{2n})}.

Thanks to the identity
-2\cos(2X)=-2+4\sin^{2}X,
after taking real parts of
z
we obtain

(4.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}M(m,n)q^{n}\cos(2mX)=\prod_{j\geq 1}\frac{1}{(1-q^{j})\left[1+\frac{4(\sin^{2}X)q^{j}}{(1-q^{j})^{2}}\right]}.

Combining Lemma
4.1
and (
4.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}M(m,n)q^{n}\cos(2mX)=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\cdot\exp\left(2\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{\mathbf{S}_{2k-1}(q)}{(2k)!}(-4X^{2})^{k}\right).

Thanks to (
1.8
) and Lemma
4.2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}M(m,n)q^{n}\cos(2mX)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\cdot\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{B_{2k}}{(2k)(2k)!}(-4X^{2})^{k}\right)\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{G_{2k}(\tau)}{(2k)!}(-4X^{2})^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\cdot\frac{\sin(X)}{X}\cdot\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{G_{2k}(\tau)}{(2k)!}(-4X^{2})^{k}\right).

We recognize this last expression in the context of Pólya’s cycle index polynomials. Namely,
Lemma
2.1
gives the identity

\exp\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}x_{j}\cdot\frac{y^{j}}{j}\right)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\left(\sum_{\lambda\vdash k}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{m_{j}!}\left(\frac{x_{j}}{j}\right)^{m_{j}}\right)y^{k},

which we apply with
x_{j}=\frac{G_{2j}(\tau)}{2(2j-1)!}
and
y=-4X^{2}.
Therefore, we find that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}M(m,n)q^{n}\cos(2mX)=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\cdot\frac{\sin(X)}{X}\cdot\sum_{k\geq 0}\left(\sum_{\lambda\vdash k}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{m_{j}!}\left(\frac{G_{2j}(\tau)}{(2j)!}\right)^{m_{j}}\right)(-4X^{2})^{k}.

Using the Taylor expansion of
\cos(2mX)
, a simple change of variables (i.e.
2X\rightarrow X
) gives

(4.3)

\sum_{k\geq 0}(-1)^{k}C_{2k}(q)\cdot\frac{X^{2k}}{(2k)!}=\frac{2\sin(\frac{X}{2})}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\cdot\sum_{k\geq 0}(-1)^{k}\Tr_{k}(\phi_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}c};\tau)\cdot X^{2k-1}.

4.3.
Proof of Corollary
1.3

By inserting
the Taylor expansion of
\sin(X/2)
in (
4.3
), a straightforward simplification gives

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 0}(-1)^{k}C_{2k}(q)\cdot\frac{X^{2k}}{(2k)!}=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{k}\frac{\Tr_{k-n}(\phi_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}c};\tau)}{4^{n}\cdot(2n+1)!}\right)\cdot X^{2k}.

By comparing the coefficient of
X^{2k}
on both sides and using the Pochhammer symbol to take into account

(2k)!
, we obtain the claimed formulas for
C_{2k}(q).

5.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.4
after recalling some essential preliminaries about Jacobi forms.

5.1.
Background on Jacobi forms

The definition of a meromorphic Jacobi form is given in the introduction. Here we give further basic properties of Jacobi forms to facilitate the proof of Theorem
1.4
. Namely, we require a universal factorization theorem for meromorphic Jacobi forms with torsional divisor.

5.1.1.
A key Jacobi form factorization

To this end, we begin by recalling the first example of a Jacobi form, the celebrated Jacobi theta function (see
[
8
,
12
]
)

\theta(z;\tau):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}u^{n}q^{n^{2}/2},

which is a Jacobi form for
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
of weight
1/2
and index
1/2
.
We work instead with a slightly modified version of this function. Namely, let
\eta(\tau):=q^{\frac{1}{24}}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{n}),
and then consider

(5.1)

\displaystyle\Theta(z,\tau)\

\displaystyle=\ (u^{1/2}-u^{-1/2})\prod_{n\geq 1}\frac{(1-uq^{n})(1-u^{-1}q^{n})}{(1-q^{n})^{2}}=\ \frac{1}{\eta(\tau)^{3}}\ \sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{n}u^{\frac{2n+1}{2}}q^{\frac{(2n+1)^{2}}{8}},

where we have employed the Jacobi Triple Product formula (see Theorem 2.8 of
[
3
]
). We note that this function arises naturally as
a factor of the generating function for the Andrews-Garvan crank (
4.1
), and
it is a Jacobi form for
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
with weight
-1
and index
1/2
.
The main reason for working with
\Theta(z;\tau)
is its close connection to the Weierstrass
\sigma
-function. To be precise, it satisfies the identity (see Theorem I.6.4 of
[
16
]
)

(5.2)

\Theta(z;\tau)=(2\pi i)\exp\left(-\frac{G_{2}(\tau)}{2}(2\pi iz)^{2}\right)\sigma(z;\tau).

As noted earlier, the divisor of a non-zero Jacobi form
F(z;\tau)
is the formal finite sum

\Div(F)=\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\tau}}a_{x}\cdot(x),

where each
a_{x}
is the the order of
F
at the point
x
. The
degree
of a divisor is the sum

\deg(D)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\tau}}a_{x}.

Using this terminology, we have the following factorization result for meromorphic Jacobi forms. This proposition is the key point that identifies the weakly holomorphic modular form
f_{F}
in Theorem
1.4
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
F(z;\tau)
be a non-zero meromorphic Jacobi form of weight
k
and index
m
for some subgroup
\Gamma\subseteq\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. If
F
has divisor
D=\Div(F)=\sum_{i=0}^{N}a_{x_{i}}\cdot(x_{i})
, with
x_{0}=0
,
then the following are true.

(1) The action of
\Gamma
fixes the divisor
D
. Moreover, we have that
\deg(D)=2m
, and as a sum of complex numbers, we have

(5.3)

\sum_{i=0}^{N}a_{x_{i}}\cdot x_{i}\equiv 0\pmod{\Lambda_{\tau}}.

(2) If representatives
x_{i}
of the divisor are chosen so that the sum in (
5.3
) vanishes, then the Jacobi form
F
factors as the product

F(z;\tau)=f_{F}(\tau)\cdot\Theta(z;\tau)^{a_{x_{0}}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\Theta(z-x_{i};\tau)^{a_{x_{i}}}}{\Theta(-x_{i};\tau)^{a_{x_{i}}}},

where
f_{F}(\tau)
is a meromorphic modular form on
\Gamma
with weight
k+a_{x_{0}}
.

Remark
.

We stress that
a_{x_{0}}
can of course be 0 in
\Div(F)
in Proposition
5.1
. We set
x_{0}=0
as this point requires separate treatment in the proof below.

Proof.

The points of
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\tau}
are fixed under the action of
\Gamma
unless, like torsion points, they depend on
\tau
. The modular transformation of
F
implies that
\mathrm{ord}_{x}(F(\tau))=\mathrm{ord}_{x}\big(F(\frac{z}{c\tau+d},\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d})\big)
for all
x\in\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\tau}
and
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma
, and so the action of
\Gamma
must fix
D
.

Now consider the Jacobi form

F_{1}(z;\tau):=F(z;\tau)\cdot\Theta(z;\tau)^{-2m},

which is a Jacobi form of index
0.
In other words, it is an elliptic function.
From
[
15
, Th. 2.2]
, we have that the degree of the divisor of
F_{1}
is
0,
and also that

\sum_{x\in\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\tau}}\mathrm{ord}_{x}(F_{1})\cdot x\equiv 0\pmod{\Lambda_{\tau}},

as a sum of genuine complex numbers (as opposed to a formal sum of points).
Since the divisor of
\Theta(z;\tau)
is just
(0)
, the proof of (1) is complete.

We now turn to the proof of (2).
To this end, we consider the function

f_{F}(z;\tau)=F(z;\tau)\cdot\Theta(z;\tau)^{-a_{x_{0}}}\prod_{i>0}\frac{\Theta(-x_{i};\tau)^{a_{x_{i}}}}{\Theta(z-x_{i};\tau)^{a_{x_{i}}}}.

The divisor of this function is trivial, and applying the elliptic transformation laws of
\Theta(z;\tau)
, we see that it is bounded as
z
varies. Thus
f_{F}
is constant in
z
. A short calculation using the transformation laws of
\Theta(z;\tau)
shows that the exponential terms from the Jacobi transformation laws cancel and so
f_{F}(\tau)
is modular on
\Gamma
with weight
k+a_{x_{0}}.

∎

5.1.2.
Eisenstein series for torsional divisors

Theorem
1.4
requires Eisenstein series that correspond to a torsional divisor
D
. These series arise naturally from
\Theta(z;\tau),

and thanks to Proposition
5.1

we are led
to Theorem
1.4
.
For convenience, we introduce the minor modification

(5.4)

\widetilde{\Theta}(z;\tau):=\exp\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\tfrac{z^{2}-|z|^{2}}{\Im(\tau)}\right)\Theta(z;\tau).

The Jacobi form transformation laws of
\Theta(z;\tau)
(i.e. weight
-1
and index
1/2
), imply that

\tau\widetilde{\Theta}\left(\frac{z}{\tau};\frac{-1}{\tau}\right)=\widetilde{\Theta}(z;\tau)=\widetilde{\Theta}(z;\tau+1)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left|\widetilde{\Theta}(z+1;\tau)\right|=\left|\widetilde{\Theta}(z+\tau;\tau)\right|=\left|\widetilde{\Theta}(z;\tau)\right|.

Therefore, the Taylor coefficients of
\log|\Theta(z;\tau)|
and
\log|\widetilde{\Theta}(z;\tau)|
are well-defined.
The Eisenstein series that will form the building blocks of (
1.9
) arise in this way.

To be precise, if
x
is a non-zero torsion point, we define the weight
k
Eisenstein series
\widetilde{G}_{k,x}(\tau)
using the Taylor coefficients of
\widetilde{\Theta}(z;\tau)
around
z=x:

(5.5)

\widetilde{G}_{k,x}(\tau)\ :=\ \left.-\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{d}{dz}\right)^{k}\log\left|\widetilde{\Theta}(z;\tau)\right|^{2}\right|_{z=x}.

Similarly, we define the holomorphic Eisenstein series

(5.6)

G_{k,x}(\tau)\ :=\ \left.-\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{d}{dz}\right)^{k}\log\left|\Theta(z;\tau)\right|^{2}\right|_{z=x}.

The following lemma lists the key properties that we require of these Eisenstein series.

Lemma 5.2
.

If
x=\alpha\tau+\beta,
where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Q},
then the following are true.

(1) If
x^{\prime}
is a torsion point with
x-x^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{\tau},
then

\widetilde{G}_{k,x}(\tau)=\widetilde{G}_{k,x^{\prime}}(\tau).

Moreover, if
\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, then

\widetilde{G}_{k,x}(\gamma\tau)=(c\tau+d)^{k}\widetilde{G}_{k,x^{\prime}}(\tau),

where
x^{\prime}=\alpha^{\prime}\tau+\beta^{\prime}
with
(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})=(\alpha,\beta)\gamma.

(2) The series
\widetilde{G}_{k,x}(\tau)
and
G_{k,x}(\tau)
are related by

\widetilde{G}_{k,x}(\tau)=\begin{cases}G_{k,x}(\tau)+\alpha&\ \ \ \text{ if }k=1,\\
G_{k,x}(\tau)+\frac{1}{4\pi\Im(\tau)}&\ \ \ \text{ if }k=2,\\
G_{k,x}(\tau)&\ \ \ \text{ if }k>2.\end{cases}

(3)
If
\alpha=0
, then
G_{k,x}(\tau)
has the Fourier expansion

G_{k,x}(\tau)=\begin{cases}\tfrac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\left(\zeta(1,-\beta)-\zeta(1,\beta)\right)+\displaystyle\sum_{n>0}\sum_{m>0}\left(\zeta_{\beta}^{n}-\zeta_{\beta}^{-n}\right)q^{mn}&\text{ if }k=1,\\
\frac{(k-1)!}{(2\pi i)^{k}}\left(\zeta(k,-\beta)+(-1)^{k}\zeta(k,\beta)\right)+\displaystyle\sum_{n>0}\sum_{m>0}n^{k-1}\left(\zeta_{\beta}^{n}+(-1)^{k}\zeta_{\beta}^{-n}\right)q^{mn}&\text{ if }k\geq 2,\end{cases}

where
\zeta_{\beta}:=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{\beta}},
and
\zeta(s,\beta)
is the Hurwitz zeta function.

(4) If
0<\alpha<1
, then
G_{k,x}(\tau)
has the Fourier expansion

G_{k,x}(\tau)=\begin{cases}\ \frac{1}{2}+\displaystyle\sum_{n>0}\sum_{m\geq 0}\zeta_{\beta}^{n}q^{(m+a)n}-\sum_{n>0}\sum_{m>0}\zeta_{\beta}^{-n}q^{(m-a)n}&\text{ if }k=1,\\
\ \displaystyle\sum_{n>0}\sum_{m\geq 0}n^{k-1}\zeta_{\beta}^{n}q^{(m+a)n}-\sum_{n>0}\sum_{m>0}(-n)^{k-1}\zeta_{\beta}^{-n}q^{(m-a)n}&\text{ if }k\geq 2.\end{cases}

Two Remarks
.

(1) We note that Lemma
5.2
(1) implies that
\widetilde{G}_{k,x}(\tau)
is modular with level
\lcm(\denom(\alpha),\denom(\beta)).
The same levels hold in all cases for
G_{k,x}(\tau).

(2) We emphasize the fact that
x-x^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{\tau}
does not imply that
G_{1,x}(\tau)
equals
G_{1,x^{\prime}}(\tau)
. In particular, Lemma
5.2
(1) and (2) show that
G_{1,x}(\tau)\neq G_{1,x+c\tau}(\tau)
if
c\neq 0
.

Proof.

The transformation law in (1) follows from the transformation law for
\widetilde{\Theta}(z;\tau)
, and the fact that

\alpha\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}+\beta=\frac{\alpha^{\prime}\tau+\beta^{\prime}}{c\tau+d}.

To prove (2), we simply note that the additional terms for
\widetilde{G}_{k,x}(\tau)
when
k=1
and
k=2
are simply the first and second logarithmic derivatives respectively of the exponential factor
\exp\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\tfrac{z^{2}-|z|^{2}}{\Im(\tau)}\right)
in the definition of
\widetilde{\Theta}(z;\tau)
in (
5.4
).

We take the logarithm of (
5.1
) to obtain

\log|\Theta(z;\tau)|^{2}=\log|u^{-1/2}|^{2}+\log|1-u|^{2}+\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\log|1-uq^{n}|^{2}+\log|1-u^{-1}q^{n}|^{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-\log|1-q^{n}|^{4}}\right).

Then applying the iterated derivative, and the substitution
u=\zeta_{\beta}q^{\alpha}
leads to the Fourier expansions in (3) and (4). The Hurwitz
\zeta
-function values in (3) are

\left.-\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{d}{dz}\right)^{k-1}\frac{u}{1-u}\right|_{z=\beta}=\frac{(k-1)!}{(2\pi i)^{k}}\left(\zeta(k,-\beta)+(-1)^{k}\zeta(k,\beta)\right).

This completes the proof.
∎

For Theorem
1.4
, we must extend the Eisenstein series
G_{k,x}(\tau)
to the weight
k
quasimodular Eisenstein series
G_{k,D}(\tau)
that we require.
To this end, we use the convention
G_{2k,0}(\tau)=G_{2k}(\tau)
and
G_{2k+1,0}(\tau)=0
. For nontrivial torsional divisors
D=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\tau}}a_{x}(x)
, we assume that representatives
x_{i}
are chosen so that
|\sum_{x\in\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\tau}}a_{x}x|
is minimized. Then we let

(5.7)

G_{k,D}(\tau):=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\tau}}a_{x}\,G_{k,x}(\tau).

Lemma 5.3
.

If
D
is a nontrivial torsional divisor for a Jacobi form
F
, then the following are true.

(1) If
k=1
, then

G_{1,D}(\tau)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\tau}}a_{x}\,\widetilde{G}_{1,x}(\tau)

is a weight 1 holomorphic modular form.

(2) For
k=2,
we have that
F
has index
m=0
if and only if

G_{2,D}(\tau)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\tau}}a_{x}\,\widetilde{G}_{2,x}(\tau)

is a weight 2 holomorphic modular form. Otherwise,
G_{2,D}(\tau)
is quasimodular.

(3) If
k\geq 3,
then
G_{k,D}(\tau)
is a weight
k
holomorphic modular form.

Proof.

In each case, we use the fact that
\widetilde{G}_{k,x}(\tau)
is a weight
k
(possibly non-holomorphic) modular form, and we then apply Lemma
5.2
(2) to account for the difference

G_{k,D}(\tau)-\sum_{x\in\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\tau}}a_{x}\widetilde{G}_{k,x}(\tau).

In (1), the difference vanishes due to the requirement that

\sum_{x\in\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\tau}}a_{x}\cdot x\in\Lambda_{\tau},
and is therefore
0
since we chose representatives
x
to minimize this sum. For (2), we use the fact that

\sum_{x\in\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\tau}}a_{x}=2m
, to see that the difference is
\frac{m}{2\pi\Im(\tau)}.

For (3), this difference is identically
0
.
∎

5.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

Suppose that
F(z;\tau)
is as in the statement of the Theorem
1.4
. Moreover, suppose that
\Div(F)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{x_{i}}(x_{i}),
and again that
x_{0}=0
.
Using the definition of
G_{k,x}(\tau)
above (i.e. (
5.5
) and (
5.6
)), for
z
near
0
we find that

\displaystyle\Theta(z-x;\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi i(z-x)\exp\left(-\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{G_{2k}(\tau)}{(2k)!}(2\pi i)^{2k}(z-x)^{2k}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\pi i\,x\cdot\exp\left(\log\left(1-\frac{z}{x}\right)-\sum_{k\geq 1}G_{2k}(\tau)(2\pi i)^{2k}\sum_{0\leq j\leq 2k}\frac{z^{j}}{j!}\frac{(-x)^{2k-j}}{(2k-j)!}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Theta(-x;\tau)\cdot\exp\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}(2\pi i)^{j}\,\frac{z^{j}}{j!}\left(-\frac{(j-1)!}{(2\pi i\,x)^{j}}-\sum_{k\geq 1}G_{2k}(\tau)\frac{(-2\pi ix)^{2k-j}}{(2k-j)!}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Theta(-x;\tau)\cdot\exp\left(-\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{(2\pi iz)^{j}}{j!}G_{j,x}(\tau)\right).

This implies then that

(5.8)

\frac{\Theta(z-x;\tau)}{\Theta(-x;\tau)}=\exp\left(-\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{(2\pi iz)^{j}}{j!}G_{j,x}(\tau)\right).

We now recall Proposition
5.1
, which gives

F(z;\tau)=f_{F}(\tau)\cdot\Theta(z;\tau)^{a_{x_{0}}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\Theta(z-x_{i};\tau)^{a_{x_{i}}}}{\Theta(-x_{i};\tau)^{a_{x_{i}}}}.

Therefore, by taking into account the points of
D
, we find that (
5.8
) gives

\log\frac{F(z;\tau)}{f_{F}(\tau)}=-\sum_{i=0}^{N}a_{x_{i}}\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{(2\pi iz)^{j}}{j!}G_{j,x_{i}}(\tau)=-\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{(2\pi iz)^{j}}{j!}G_{j,D}(\tau),

where in the last line we have used the definition of
G_{k,D}(\tau)
.
To complete the proof, we now apply Lemma
2.1
with

x_{j}=\frac{1}{(j-1)!}G_{j,D(\tau)}
and
y=2\pi iz
to the exponential of this last expression.
