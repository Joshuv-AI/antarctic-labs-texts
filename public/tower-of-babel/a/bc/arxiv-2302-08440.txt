1.
Introduction

A Schrödinger operator is the Hamiltonian that describes the dynamics of a conservative system of particles at quantum scale in absence of relativistic forces. Despite its phenomenological origin, theory of Schrödinger operators stands as an autonomous branch of mathematics. Its study links notions of differential equations, geometric analysis, and measure theory, among other fields. In this paper we study the spectral types of discrete one-dimensional Schrödinger operators. This question is of interest because of its physical interpretation: in the system described by a Schrödinger operator, bound states are associated with presence of point spectrum of the operator, while scattering states are associated with continuous spectrum. Discrete one-dimensional Schrödinger operators are defined as:

\displaystyle H:\mathcal{D}(H)\subseteq\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})

(1.1)

\displaystyle\psi

\displaystyle\mapsto(\Delta_{d}+V)\psi

where
\Delta_{d}
is the discrete Laplacian operator and
V
is the potential function (formal definition of these operators is presented in section
2
). The spectral theory of Schrödinger operators studies the spectrum of
H
according to the properties of the function
V
.

Given a dynamical system
\{\Omega,T\}
it is possible to define for each
\omega\in\Omega
a potential function
V_{\omega}(n)=f(T^{n}\omega)
, where
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. This approach relates the theory of dynamical systems with the spectral theory of Schrödinger operators. In this context, the analysis focuses on studying the properties of the family of operators
\{H_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
, where each
H_{\omega}
is given by:

\displaystyle H_{\omega}:\mathcal{D}(H_{\omega})\subseteq\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})

(1.2)

\displaystyle\psi

\displaystyle\mapsto(\Delta_{d}+V_{\omega})\psi.

If the transformation
T:\Omega\rightarrow\Omega
is ergodic, then
\{H_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
is a family of ergodic Schrödinger operators. The central question in this domain consists of two objectives: on the one hand, to determine the spectral types (point, absolutely continuous, and singular continuous) and the shape of the spectrum of the operator
H_{\omega}
(equation
1.2
), and on the other hand, to describe the dynamics of physical systems associated with
H_{\omega}
. A particular interest in the theory of ergodic Schrödinger operators lies in the study of properties that are satisfied for a generic element of
\{H_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
. The latter implies, from the topological point of view, studying the properties of an element in a residual subset of
\{H_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
, and from the measure-theoretical approach, studying the properties of an element in a subset of full measure of
\{H_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
. In this paper we study the spectral properties of a generic
H_{\omega}\in\{H_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
from the topological point of view.

The main background of this work is the research of Boshernitzan and Damanik
[
3
]
. They introduce the definition of topological and metric repetition property (
TRP
and
MRP
, respectively) on the system
\{\Omega,T\}
, and study its implications on the absence of point spectrum of
\{H_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
, where the potential
V_{\omega}(n)=f(T^{n}\omega)
is given by an ergodic homeomorphism
T
on a compact space
\Omega
. Another related research is the work of Avila and Damanik
[
1
]
. These authors show, using tools from harmonic analysis and Kotani Theory, that the absense of absolute continuous spectrum is a generic property of
\{H_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
, where
T:\Omega\rightarrow\Omega
is a nonperiodic homeomorphism.

The aim of this paper is to determine sufficient conditions for the purely continuous spectrum of a family of ergodic Schrödinger operators
\{H_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
to be a generic property. Our strategy is to show that a generic operator
H_{\omega}\in\{H_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
has no eigenvalues (and therefore its point spectrum is empty). This objective is similar to that of
[
3
]
and
[
1
]
, since we seek to rule out the presence of a certain spectral type in a generic Schrödinger operator. Our work is based on two theoretical tools:

(i)

Gordon’s Lemma
: this is a classical postulate of spectral theory that gives conditions for the absence of point spectrum of a Schrödinger operator.

(ii)

TRP
: this property provides the system
\{\Omega,T\}
with a structure that allows, using Gordon’s lemma, to show that a generic operator
H_{\omega}
of the family
\{H_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
has purely continuous spectrum (see definition
2.13
).

Our results extend the main theorem of Boshernitzan and Damanik (
[
3
]
, p.650). These authors demonstrate that purely continuous spectrum is a generic property of
\{H_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
using two hypotheses: that the system
\{\Omega,T\}
is minimal and satisfies
TRP
. We weaken the hypotheses of this result from two perspectives: first, we prove the previous result using only the hypothesis that
\{T^{n}\alpha\}_{n\geq 0}
is dense in
\Omega
for certain element
\alpha\in\Omega
(specifically, for
\alpha\in PRP(T)
.
See definition
2.9
). Then we demonstrate that
TRP
is sufficient condition for the purely continuous spectrum to be a generic property of the family of ergodic Schrödinger operators
\{H_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
. The following are the statements of the two main results of this paper.

Theorem A
.

Suppose that
\alpha\in PRP(T)
and
\{T^{n}\alpha\}_{n\geq 0}
is dense in
\Omega
. Then there exists a residual subset
\mathcal{F}
of
C(\Omega)
such that if
f\in\mathcal{F}
then there exists a residual subset
\Omega_{f}
of
\Omega
with the property that
f(T^{n}\omega)
is a Gordon potential, for every
\omega\in\Omega_{f}
.

Theorem B
.

Suppose that the dynamical system
\{\Omega,T\}
satisfies TRP. Then there exists a residual subset
\mathcal{F}
of
C(\Omega)
such that if
f\in\mathcal{F}
then there exists a residual subset
\Omega_{f}
of
\Omega
with the property that
f(T^{n}\omega)
is a Gordon potential, for every
\omega\in\Omega_{f}
.

In addition to this introduction, the paper is divided into five sections. In the second section we introduce the main definitions in which the work is framed. In the third, we state and demonstrate Gordon’s lemma. In the fourth and fifth sections, we prove theorems A and B, respectively. Finally, in the sixth section, we discuss some applications.

Setting and main results

In this section we introduce the formal definition of discrete Schrödinger operators. We also present the definition of
TRP
property and discuss some of its implications on the structure of a dynamical system

2.1.
Schrödinger operators.

Let
\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the space of square integrable functions, which is defined

\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\{\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\mid\int_{\mathcal{D}(\Psi)}\lVert\Psi(q)\rVert^{2}dq<\infty\}

where the inner product is given by:

\langle\Psi,\Phi\rangle=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\overline{\Psi(q)}\Phi(q)dq.

Discrete version of
\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is defined as:

\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})=\{\psi:\mathbb{Z}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\mid\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}|\psi(n)|^{2}<\infty\}

endowed with inner product:

\langle\psi,\varphi\rangle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\overline{\psi(n)}\varphi(n).

Definition 2.1
.

A Schrödinger operator is defined as a linear operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})\subseteq\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

(2.1)

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle\mapsto(\Delta+V)\Psi

where
\Delta
is the Laplacian in
\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
V:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
the potential function. Similary, discrete Schrödinger operators are defined as:

\displaystyle H:\mathcal{D}(H)\subseteq\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})

(2.2)

\displaystyle\psi

\displaystyle\mapsto(\Delta_{d}+V)\psi

with
\Delta_{d}
the discrete Laplacian in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
and
V:\mathbb{Z}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
the potential function.

In this paper we focus on the one-dimensional case, which leads to the following definition, considering
\Delta_{d}=\psi(n+1)+\psi(n-1)
as the discrete Laplacian in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Definition 2.2
.

The discrete one-dimensional Schrödinger operator:

\displaystyle H:\mathcal{D}(H)\subseteq\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})

(2.3)

\displaystyle\psi

\displaystyle\mapsto(\Delta_{d}+V)\psi

\displaystyle=\psi(n+1)+\psi(n-1)+V(n)\psi(n)

Proposition 2.3
.

The operator
H
(equation
2.3
) is self-adjoint.

Proof.

Let
\psi(n)
and
\varphi(n)\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Then, on the one hand:

\displaystyle\langle H\psi(n),\varphi(n)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(\Delta_{d}+V)\psi(n),\varphi(n)\rangle

\displaystyle\text{by definition of $H$}

(2.4)

\displaystyle=\langle\Delta_{d}\psi(n),\varphi(n)\rangle+\langle V(n)\cdot\psi(n),\varphi(n)\rangle

\displaystyle\text{by definition of $\langle,\rangle$}

\displaystyle=\langle\psi(n),\Delta_{d}\varphi(n)\rangle+\langle V(n)\cdot\psi(n),\varphi(n)\rangle

\displaystyle\text{as $\Delta_{d}$ is self-adjoint}

On the other hand, note that:

\displaystyle\langle V(n)\cdot\psi(n),\varphi(n)\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\overline{V(n)\psi(n)}\varphi(n)

\displaystyle\text{by definition of $\langle,\rangle$}

(2.5)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\overline{\psi(n)V(n)}\varphi(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\overline{\psi(n)}V(n)\varphi(n)

\displaystyle\text{since $V(n)\in\mathbb{R}$}

\displaystyle=\langle\psi(n),V(n)\cdot\varphi(n)\rangle

\displaystyle\text{by definition of $\langle,\rangle$}

Replacing (
2.5
) in (
2.3
) we obtain:

\displaystyle\langle H\psi(n),\varphi(n)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\psi(n),\Delta_{d}\varphi(n)\rangle+\langle\psi(n),V(n)\varphi(n)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\psi(n),(\Delta_{d}+V)\varphi(n)

\displaystyle\text{by definition of $\langle,\rangle$}

\displaystyle=\langle\psi(n),H\varphi(n)\rangle

\displaystyle\text{for definition $H$}

we conclude that
H
is self-adjoint.
∎

As a consequence of the previous proposition we have the next corollary.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
H=\Delta_{d}+V
be a discrete one-dimensional Schrödinger operator. If
V
is bounded, then
H
is a bounded self-adjoint operator.

2.2.
Discrete one-dimensional ergodic Schrödinger operators.

Let
\{\Omega,T\}
be a dynamical system and consider
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Each
\omega\in\Omega
and its orbit over
T
(
i.e.
the set
\{T^{n}\omega\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
) induces the definition of a potential function:

\displaystyle V_{\omega}:\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

(2.6)

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\mapsto f(T^{n}\omega).

Consequently, each
\omega\in\Omega
is associated with a Schrödinger operator

\displaystyle H_{\omega}:\mathcal{D}(H_{\omega})\subseteq\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})

(2.7)

\displaystyle\psi

\displaystyle\mapsto(\Delta_{d}+V_{\omega})\psi.

and
\{H_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
is called
family of Schrödinger operators with dynamically defined potential
.

We denote by
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})

the space of self-adjoint operators in a Hilbert space, that is:

\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})=\{H:\mathcal{D}(H)\subseteq\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}\mid\text{$H$ is self-adjoint}\}

The following definition relates the ergodic theory to the Schrödinger operators.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{B},\mu)
be a probability space and consider:

\displaystyle A:\Omega

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle\mapsto H_{\omega}

The operator
H_{\omega}
is an
ergodic operator
, if there exists a family of ergodic transformations
\{T_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
in
\Omega
and a family of unitary operators
\{U_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
en
\mathcal{H}

such that:

H_{T_{i}(\omega)}=U_{i}^{*}H_{\omega}U_{i}

where
U_{i}^{*}
is the adjoint operator of
U_{i}
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{B},\mu)
be a probability space,
\{\Omega,T\}
a dynamical system with
T
a
\mu-
ergodic transformation and
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
a given function. Then, the operator
H_{\omega}
(equation
2.7
) is ergodic.

Proof.

Consider the transformation:

\displaystyle A:\Omega

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle\mapsto H_{\omega}=\Delta_{d}+V_{\omega}

By hypothesis
T
is
\mu-
ergodic, so
\{T^{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a family of ergodic transformations on
\Omega
. To prove that
H_{\omega}
is an ergodic operator, it is necessary to find a family of unitary operators
\{U_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
such that:

H_{T^{t}(\omega)}=U_{t}^{*}H_{\omega}U_{t}

(2.8)

For each
t\in\mathbb{Z}
, consider the translation operator on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
:

\displaystyle U_{t}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle\psi(n)

\displaystyle\mapsto\psi(n+t)

Notice that the operator
U_{t}
is unitary:

\displaystyle\lVert U_{t}\rVert=\sup_{\psi\neq 0}\frac{\lVert U_{t}\psi(n)\rVert}{\lVert\psi(n)\rVert}=\sup_{\psi\neq 0}\frac{\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|\psi(n+t)|^{2}}{\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|\psi(n)|^{2}}=\sup_{\psi\neq 0}\frac{\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|\psi(n)|^{2}}{\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|\psi(n)|^{2}}=1

therefore,
\{U_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a family of unitary operators on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Additionally, we have that:

U_{t}^{*}=U_{-t},\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{Z}

Let
\psi(n)\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. then:

\displaystyle H_{T^{t}(\omega)}\psi(n)=(\Delta_{d}+V_{T^{t}\omega})\psi(n)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{d}\psi(n)+V_{T^{t}\omega}\psi(n)

(2.9)

\displaystyle=\psi(n+1)+\psi(n-1)+f(T^{n}[T^{t}\omega])\psi(n)

\displaystyle=\psi(n+1)+\psi(n-1)+f(T^{n+t}\omega)\psi(n)

furthermore:

\displaystyle U_{-t}H_{\omega}U_{t}\psi(n)=U_{-t}H_{\omega}\psi(n+t)

\displaystyle=U_{-t}[(\Delta_{d}+V_{\omega})\psi(n+t)]

(2.10)

\displaystyle=U_{-t}[\Delta_{d}\psi(n+t)+V_{\omega}\psi(n+t)]

\displaystyle=U_{-t}(\psi(n+t+1)+\psi(n+t-1)+f(T^{n+t}\omega)\psi(n+t))

\displaystyle=\psi(n+1)+\psi(n-1)+f(T^{n+t}\omega)\psi(n)

from equations (
2.9
) and (
2.10
):

H_{T^{t}(\omega)}\psi(n)=U_{t}^{*}H_{\omega}U_{t}\psi(n),\quad\forall\psi(n)\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})

∎

Lemma
2.6
allows us to conclude that
H_{\omega}
(equation
2.7
) is an ergodic operator. Therefore, if
T
is an ergodic homeomorphism on
\Omega
, then
\{H_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
is a family of discrete one-dimensional Schrödinger ergodic operators.

3.
Gordon’s lemma

Gordon’s lemma is the main analytical tool that supports theorems
A
and
B
. In this section we present the definition of Gordon potential and study some properties of matrix representation of Schrödinger operators. We also provide a detailed proof of Gordon’s lemma.

3.1.
Gordon potential

Definition 3.1
.

A bounded function
V:\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a
Gordon potential
if there exists a sequence of periodic functions
\{V_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}:\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, where
V_{m}(n)=V_{m}(n+T_{m})
and
T_{m}\rightarrow\infty
, that satisfies the following two conditions:

(i)

\sup_{n,m}|V_{m}(n)|<\infty
.

(ii)

\sup_{|n|\leq 2T_{m}}|V_{m}(n)-V(n)|\leq Cm^{-T_{m}}
, for a
C>0
.

A Gordon potential is essentially a function that, for
|n|\leq 2T_{m}
, can be approximated by a sequence of periodic functions. In addition to any periodic function, a nontrivial example of a Gordon potential is
V(n)=\sin(an)+\sin(bn)
where
a
and
b
are such that
\frac{a}{b}\notin\mathbb{Q}
. Gordon’s lemma, which is stated below, describes the spectral type of the discrete Schrödinger operator in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
:

H[\psi(n)]=\psi(n+1)+\psi(n-1)+V(n)\psi(n)

(3.1)

whose potential function
V(n)
satisfies the definition
3.1
.

Theorem 3.2
.

(Gordon’s, lemma) Let
V:\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a Gordon potential. For
E\in\mathbb{C}
, if
\psi
is a solution to:

\psi(n+1)+\psi(n-1)+V(n)\psi(n)=E\cdot\psi(n)

(3.2)

Then:

\limsup_{|n|\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\psi(n+1)^{2}+\psi(n)^{2}}{\psi(1)^{2}+\psi(0)^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{4}

(3.3)

Equation
3.3
implies that
\lim_{|n|\rightarrow\infty}\psi(n)>0
. Therefore:

\lVert\psi(n)\rVert^{2}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|\psi(n)|^{2}=\infty

(3.4)

as a consequence of equation
3.4
we conclude that
\psi(n)\notin\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, therefore the operator
H
in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
(equation
3.1
) has no eigenvalues (
i.e.
its point spectrum is empty). The following theorem provides a characterization of Gordon’s potential that will be useful later on the demonstration of Theorems A and B.

Theorem 3.3
.

A bounded function
V:\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a Gordon potential if and only if there exists a sequence
\{q_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
such that
q_{m}\rightarrow\infty
and for a
C>0
and every
m\geq 1
:

\max_{1\leq n\leq q_{m}}|V(n)-V(n+q_{m})|\leq Cm^{-q_{m}}\quad\text{and}\quad\max_{1\leq n\leq q_{m}}|V(n)-V(n-q_{m})|\leq Cm^{-q_{m}}

Proof.

“
\Rightarrow
” Suppose that
V(n)
is a Gordon potential. Using triangular inequality and the fact that
V_{m}
is a periodic function with period
T_{m}
:

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq n\leq T_{m}}|V(n)-V(n\pm T_{m})|

\displaystyle=\max_{1\leq n\leq T_{m}}|V(n)-V_{m}(n)+V_{m}(n\pm T_{m})-V(n\pm T_{m})|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{1\leq n\leq T_{m}}|V_{m}(n\pm T_{m})-V(n\pm T_{m})|+\sup_{1\leq n\leq T_{m}}|V_{m}(n)-V(n)|

\displaystyle\leq 2Cm^{-T_{m}}

by taking
q_{m}=T_{m}
and
\hat{C}=2C
, we conclude that:

\max_{1\leq n\leq q_{m}}|V(n)-V(n\pm q_{m})|\leq\hat{C}m^{-qm}

where
\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}q_{m}=\infty
.

“
\Leftarrow
” Suppose that there exists
C>0
such that
\max_{1\leq n\leq q_{m}}|V(n)-V(n\pm q_{m})|\leq Cm^{-q_{m}}
for a sequence of positive integers
\{q_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
such that
q_{m}\rightarrow\infty
. We want to construct, for each
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, a periodic function
V_{m}:\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with period
T_{m}
, such that
\sup_{n,m}|V_{m}(n)|<\infty
and
\sup_{|n|\leq 2T_{m}}|V_{m}(n)-V(n)|\leq Cm^{-T_{m}}
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. By hypothesis, for each
n\in\{1,\ldots,q_{m}\}
there exist
r_{1}(m)
y
r_{2}(m)
such that:

\displaystyle V(n-q_{m})+r_{1}(m)

\displaystyle=V(n),\quad

\displaystyle|r_{1}(m)|\leq Cm^{-q_{m}}\quad\text{y}

\displaystyle V(n)+r_{2}(m)

\displaystyle=V(n+q_{m}),\quad

\displaystyle|r_{2}(m)|\leq Cm^{-q_{m}}

Initially consider the function
V_{m}:\{1-q_{m},\ldots,2q_{m}\}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined as:

V_{m}(n)=\begin{cases}V(n)+r_{1}(m)\quad&\text{if $1-q_{m}\leq n\leq 0$}\\
V(n)\quad&\text{if $1\leq n\leq q_{m}$}\\
V(n)-r_{2}(m)\quad&\text{if $q_{m}+1\leq n\leq 2q_{m}$}\end{cases}

For all
n\in\{1,\ldots,q_{m}\}
the function
V_{m}
is periodic:

\displaystyle V_{m}(n+q_{m})

\displaystyle=V(n+q_{m})-r_{2}(m)

\displaystyle\text{definition of $V_{m}(n)$, because $q_{m}+1\leq n+q_{m}\leq 2q_{m}$}

\displaystyle=V(n)

\displaystyle\text{by hypothesis}

\displaystyle=V_{m}(n)

\displaystyle\text{definition of $V_{m}(n)$}

Similarly:

\displaystyle V_{m}(n-q_{m})

\displaystyle=V(n-q_{m})+r_{1}(m)

\displaystyle\text{definition of $V_{m}(n)$ because $1-q_{m}\leq n-q_{m}\leq 0$}

\displaystyle=V(n)

\displaystyle\text{by hypothesis}

\displaystyle=V_{m}(n)

\displaystyle\text{definition of $V_{m}(n)$}

therefore
V_{m}
is periodic, with period
q_{m}
.

Function
V_{m}
is extended to
\mathbb{Z}
by establishing, for each
j\in\mathbb{Z}:V_{m}(j)=V_{m}(n)
, where
n\in\{1,\ldots q_{m}\}
and
j\equiv_{q_{m}}n
. We conclude that
V_{m}:\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
is a periodic function with period
q_{m}
.

Additionally, since the function
V:\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is bounded, there exists
K
such that
V(n)\leq K
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Let
r(m)=\max\{r_{1}(m),r_{2}(m)\}
, note that for all
n
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}
:

\displaystyle|V_{m}(n)|\leq|V(n)|+|r(m)|\leq K+Cm^{-q_{m}}<\infty

then
\sup_{n,m}|V_{m}(n)|<\infty
. Finally:

|V_{m}(n)-V(n)|\leq|r(m)|\leq Cm^{-q_{m}}\Rightarrow\sup_{|n|\leq 2q_{m}}|V_{m}(n)-V(n)|\leq Cm^{-q_{m}}

Taking
q_{m}=T_{m}
, it is concluded that the sequence of functions
\{V_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}
satisfies definition
3.1
and therefore
V(n)
is a Gordon potential.
∎

3.2.
Matrix representation of Schrödinger operators

Let
V(n)
be a Gordon potential and
\Psi(n)
the column vector
(\psi(n),\psi(n+1))
where
\psi(n)
is a solution to the equation:

\psi(n+1)+\psi(n-1)+V(n)\psi(n)=E\psi(n)

(3.5)

which means that
E\in\mathbb{C}
is an eigenvalue of
H
(equation
3.1
) with given initial condition
\Psi(0)
. For
n>0
, equation (
3.5
) can be written in matrix form:

\Psi(n)=A(n)\cdots A(1)\Psi(0),\quad\text{where}\quad A(n)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&E-V(n)\end{pmatrix}

(3.6)

Similarly, let
\Psi_{m}(n)=(\psi_{m}(n),\psi_{m}(n+1))
and consider the equation:

\psi_{m}(n-1)+\psi_{m}(n+1)+V_{m}(n)\psi_{m}(n)=E\psi_{m}(n)

with initial condition
\Psi_{m}(0)=\Psi(0)
. Therefore:

\Psi_{m}(n)=A_{m}(n)\cdots A_{m}(1)\Psi(0),\quad\text{where}\quad A_{m}(n)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&E-V_{m}(n)\end{pmatrix}

(3.7)

In the following two lemmas we study some properties of
A_{m}(n)
and
A(n)
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
A(n)
and
A_{m}(n)
be according to the equations (
3.6
) and (
3.7
). Then:

\lVert A_{m}(n)\cdots A_{m}(1)-A(n)\cdots A(1)\rVert\leq n\cdot[\sup_{m,j}\lVert A_{m}(j)\rVert]^{n-1}\cdot[\sup_{1\leq j\leq n}\lVert A_{m}(j)-A(j)\rVert]

Proof.

Matrix
A_{m}(n)\cdots A_{m}(1)-A(n)\cdots A(1)
can be written as a telescopic sum:

\displaystyle A_{m}(n)\cdots A_{m}(1)-A(n)\cdots A(1)

(3.8)

\displaystyle=[A_{m}(n)-A(n)]\cdot[A_{m}(n-1)\cdots A_{m}(1)]

\displaystyle+[A(n)]\cdot[A_{m}(n-1)-A(n-1)]\cdot[A_{m}(n-2)\cdots A_{m}(1)]+\cdots

\displaystyle+[A(n)\cdots A(n-j+1)]\cdot[A_{m}(n-j)-A(n-j)]\cdot[A_{m}(n-j-1)\cdots A_{m}(1)]+\cdots

\displaystyle+[A(n)\cdots A(2)]\cdot[A_{m}(1)-A(1)]

Since
\lVert AB\rVert\leq\lVert A\rVert\cdot\lVert B\rVert=\lVert B\rVert\cdot\lVert A\rVert
, the norm of each summand on the right-hand side of the equation (
3.8
) can be bounded as follows, for all
0\leq j\leq n-1
:

\displaystyle\lVert[A(n)\cdots A(n-j+1)]\cdot[A_{m}(n-j)-A(n-j)]\cdot[A_{m}(n-j-1)\cdots A_{m}(1)]\rVert

(3.9)

\displaystyle\leq\lVert[A(n)\cdots A(n-j+1)]\rVert\cdot\lVert[A_{m}(n-j)-A(n-j)\rVert\cdot\lVert[A_{m}(n-j-1)\cdots A_{m}(1)]\rVert

\displaystyle=\lVert[A(n)\cdots A(n-j+1)]\rVert\cdot\lVert[A_{m}(n-j-1)\cdots A_{m}(1)]\rVert\lVert[A_{m}(n-j)-A(n-j)]\rVert

\displaystyle\leq[\sup_{m,j}\lVert A_{m}(j)\rVert]^{n-1}\cdot[\sup_{1\leq j\leq n}\lVert A_{m}(j)-A(j)\rVert]

In the above equation:

\sup_{m,j}\lVert A_{m}(j)\rVert=\sup_{1\leq j\leq n}\{\lVert A_{m}(j)\rVert,\lVert A(j)\rVert\}

From the equations (
3.8
) and (
3.9
) we obtain:

\lVert A_{m}(n)\cdots A_{m}(1)-A(n)\cdots A(1)\rVert\leq n[\sup_{m,j}\lVert A_{m}(j)\rVert]^{n-1}[\sup_{1\leq j\leq n}\lVert A_{m}(j)-A(j)\rVert]

∎

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
x
be a vector such that
\lVert x\rVert=1
and
B
an invertible
2\times 2
matrix. Then:

\max_{a=\pm 1,\pm 2}\lVert B^{a}x\rVert\geq\frac{1}{2}

In particular, for
\Psi_{m}(n)=A_{m}(n)\cdots A_{m}(1)\Psi(0)
, we have that:

\max_{a=\pm 1,\pm 2}\lVert\Psi_{m}(aT_{m})\rVert\geq\frac{1}{2}\lVert\Psi(0)\rVert

Proof.

Cayley-Hamilton theorem states that if
q(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}\lambda^{k}
is the characteristic polynomial of a linear transformation
T
in a vector space
V
of dimension
n
, then
q(A)=0
, where
A
is the square
n\times n
matrix associated to the operator
T
(Axler,
[
2
]
.
theorem 8.37
).
Therefore, if
q(\lambda)=a_{2}\lambda^{2}+a_{1}\lambda+a_{0}
is the characteristic polynomial associated with the matrix
B_{2\times 2}
, then:

a_{2}B^{2}+a_{1}B+a_{0}=0

(3.10)

To demonstrate theorem
3.5
we consider three cases. First
|a_{2}|=\max_{0\leq i\leq 2}|a_{i}|
. Multiplying right-hand side of the equation (
3.10
) by
\frac{1}{a_{2}}B^{-2}x
, and making
c_{1}=\frac{a_{1}}{a_{2}}
y
c_{0}=\frac{a_{0}}{a_{2}}
, we obtain:

x+c_{1}B^{-1}x+c_{0}B^{-2}x=0

where
|c_{1}|,|c_{0}|\leq 1
. Taking the norm of the above expression:

\displaystyle\lVert x\rVert=\lVert c_{1}B^{-1}x+c_{0}B^{-2}x\rVert

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad 1\leq|c_{1}|\cdot\lVert B^{-1}x\rVert+|c_{0}|\cdot\lVert B^{-2}x\rVert

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad\max\{\lVert B^{-1}x\rVert,\lVert B^{-2}x\rVert\}\geq\frac{1}{2}

The second case is
|a_{1}|=\max_{0\leq i\leq 2}|a_{i}|
. Multiplying equation (
3.10
) by
\frac{1}{a_{1}}B^{-1}x
:

d_{2}Bx+x+d_{0}B^{-1}x=0

where
|d_{2}|=\frac{a_{2}}{a_{1}}\leq 1
y
|d_{0}|=\frac{a_{0}}{a_{1}}\leq 1
. Then:

\displaystyle\lVert x\rVert=\lVert d_{2}Bx+d_{0}B^{-1}x\rVert

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad 1\leq|d_{2}|\cdot\lVert Bx\rVert+|d_{0}|\cdot\lVert B^{-1}x\rVert

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad\max\{\lVert Bx\rVert,\lVert B^{-1}x\rVert\}\geq\frac{1}{2}

The third case is
|a_{0}|=\max_{0\leq i\leq 2}|a_{i}|
. An analogous reasoning to the one just presented, multiplying now the equation (
3.10
) by
\frac{1}{a_{0}}x
allows us to affirm that:

\max\{\lVert B^{2}x\rVert,\lVert Bx\rVert\}\geq\frac{1}{2}

It is concluded that invertible
2\times 2
matrix
B
satisfies inequality:

\max_{a=\pm 1,\pm 2}\lVert B^{a}x\rVert\geq\frac{1}{2}

(3.11)

Let
B_{m}(n)=A_{m}(n)\cdots A_{m}(1)=\Pi_{j=1}^{n}A_{m}(j)
.

Note that
A_{m}(n)=A_{m}(n+T_{m})
, since
V_{m}(n)=V_{m}(n+T_{m})
. Then:

\displaystyle{B_{m}^{a}(T_{m})}=(\Pi_{j=1}^{T_{m}}A_{m}(j))^{a}

\displaystyle H_{\omega}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle\psi(n)

\displaystyle\mapsto\psi(n+1)+\psi(n-1)+f(T^{n}\omega)\psi(n)

Proof.

Consider
\omega\in\Omega

In this paper we studied the conditions that allow us to conclude that purely continuous spectrum is a generic property of ergodic Schrödinger operators. The theorems studied, as pointed out by Boshernitzan and Damanik
[
3
]
, support the notion that a repetition property is associated with the absence of point spectrum in Schrödinger operators. The topic covered in this paper can be expanded in multiple directions, for example:

Schrödinger operators in higher dimensions
. This domain has been covered by authors as Fan and Han
[
4
]
, who study the continuous spectrum of the Schrödinger operators in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
for a measurable potential function

.

Repetition properties and entropy
. A question of interest is to study what implications has the properties
TRP
and
MRP
on the entropy of a dynamical system. This issue was investigated by Huang et. al.
[
5
]
, who state that positive entropy of a dynamical system
\{\Omega,T\}
implies the presence of point spectrum on a generic Schrödinger operator
\{H_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
. Finally, remains the question of neccesary conditions on the dynamical system
\{\Omega,T\}
to guarantee the generic continuous spectrum of the family of Schrödinger operators
\{H_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
, topic that is framed in the theory of inverse problems in spectral theory.
