A consensus-based algorithm for non-convex multiplayer games
Enis Chenchene, Hui Huang, Jinniao Qiu

Abstract
In this paper, we present a novel consensus-based zeroth-order algorithm tailored for non-convex multiplayer games. The proposed method leverages a metaheuristic approach using concepts from swarm intelligence to reliably identify global Nash equilibria. We utilize a group of interacting particles, each agreeing on a specific consensus point, asymptotically converging to the corresponding optimal strategy. This paradigm permits a passage to the mean-field limit, allowing us to establish convergence guarantees under appropriate assumptions regarding initialization and objective functions. Finally, we conduct a series of numerical experiments to unveil the dependency of the proposed method on its parameters and apply it to solve a nonlinear Cournot oligopoly game involving multiple goods.



Keywords:
Non-convex, multiplayer games, Nash equilibrium, swarm optimization, Laplace’s principle.

Introduction

Multiplayer games
[
48

[
41

, social sciences

. In recent years, significant progress has also been made in developing advanced learning approaches within the field of artificial intelligence towards multiplayer scenarios. One notable example is the extension of adversarial learning, which was originally applied to settings with a single generator and discriminator, to accommodate multiple agents

,

,
43
]
. This process can also be observed in reinforcement learning, where multiplayer game theory has been integrated to enhance learning algorithms

.

In this paper, we focus on exploring a common class of non-convex games involving multiple players, specifically
M\geq 3
players. Each player, denoted by
m\in[M]:=\{1,\ldots,M\}
, aims to minimize their own cost function
\mathcal{E}_{m}(x_{m};\boldsymbol{x}_{-m}):\mathbb{R}^{Md}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. The cost function is influenced by two factors: the player’s own decision, represented by
x_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the decisions made by all other players, denoted as
\boldsymbol{x}_{-m}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m-1},x_{m},\dots,x_{M})\in\mathbb{R}^{(M-1)d}
. This setting has garnered significant attention in machine learning applications. For instance, in the context of sensor localization
[
40
,
60
]
, the decision variable
x_{m}
corresponds to a sensor node, and the cost function
\mathcal{E}_{m}
is instantiated as the Euclidean norm. Similarly, in the domain of robust neural network training
[
50
,
21
]
,
x_{m}
represents the model parameter, and
\mathcal{E}_{m}
is the cross-entropy function. Furthermore, the considered setting has the potential to inspire solutions for resource allocation problems in unmanned vehicles
[
59
]
and secure transmission
[
55
]
. In these contexts, the decision variable
x_{m}
represents the allocation of transmit resources, and the cost function
\mathcal{E}_{m}
captures the associated transmission cost.

With the formulation presented above, it is both natural and crucial, from the perspectives of both game theory and machine learning, to pursue the identification of the global Nash Equilibrium (NE)
[
49
]
, which represents a widely recognized concept of optimality in game theory, wherein no player can improve their outcome by unilaterally altering their strategy while keeping the strategies of others unchanged. We specialize this concept in the following definition:

Definition 1.1
.

Point
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}:=(x_{1}^{*},\dots,x_{M}^{*})
is a NE of cost functions
\{\mathcal{E}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{E}_{M}\}
if

x_{m}^{*}\in\arg\min_{x_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{E}_{m}(x_{m};\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{-m})\,,\quad\text{for all}\ m\in[M]\,.

(1.1)

The search for global NE in non-convex settings remains an open problem
[
46
,
45
]
. This challenge arises not only due to the absence of powerful tools compared to those available in convex scenarios but also due to the diversity of non-convex structures, which may require specific methodologies for resolution. While efficient techniques have been developed within convex conditions that have led to significant advancements in multiplayer game models
[
61
,
15
]
, these approaches may prove insufficient when confronted with non-convexity. In such cases, they may become trapped in local NE or approximate solutions while following pseudo-gradients, failing to converge to a global NE. Moreover, while inspiring breakthroughs have been made in solving non-convex two-player min-max games in different contexts, such as Polyak–Łojasiewicz cases
[
50
,
23
]
or concave cases
[
44
,
52
]
, these advancements may not readily apply to multiplayer settings. This is because the global stationary conditions in multiplayer games are intricately coupled and cannot be independently addressed by each player. Consequently, novel effective methods are required for attaining them, which is exactly the main contribution of this article

In this paper we introduce a novel zero-order consensus-based approach for finding global NE points in multiplayer games. This method draws inspiration from the consensus-based optimization (CBO) framework initially introduced in
[
10
,
51
]
. CBO belongs to the family of global optimization methodologies, which leverages systems of interacting particles to achieve consensus around global minimizers of the cost functions. As part of the broader class of metaheuristics
[
2
,
29
]
, CBO orchestrates interactions between local improvement procedures and global strategies, utilizing both deterministic and stochastic processes. This interplay ultimately results in an efficient and robust procedure for exploring the solution space of the cost functions.
More importantly, the CBO approach possesses an inherent advantage of being gradient-free. This characteristic makes it particularly desirable when dealing with cost functions that lack smoothness or when computing their derivatives is computationally expensive.

Motivated by diverse applications, researchers have extended and adapted the original CBO model to encompass various settings. These extensions include incorporating memory effects or gradient information
[
54
,
58
,
17
]
, integrating momentum
[
16
]
, and employing jump-diffusion processes
[
39
]
. Additionally, CBO has been extended to address global optimization on compact manifolds
[
24
,
33
]
, handle general constraints
[
6
,
14
]
, cost functions with multiple minimizers
[
7
]
, multi-objective problems
[
4
,
5
]
, and sampling from distributions
[
11
]
. Furthermore, CBO has also been applied to tackle high-dimensional machine learning problems
[
12
,
25
]
, saddle point problems
[
37
]
, asset allocation problems
[
1
]
, and more recently the clustered federated learning
[
13
]
. It has been demonstrated that CBO exhibits behavior similar to stochastic gradient descent (SGD)
[
53
]
.
Specifically, our CBO dynamic for
M
-player games is a collection of
M\cdot N
particles
\{(\overline{X}_{t}^{1,i},\dots,\overline{X}_{t}^{M,i})\}_{i\in[N]}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{Md}
satisfying the following Stochastic Differential Equations (SDEs)

d\overline{X}_{t}^{m,i}=-\lambda(\overline{X}_{t}^{m,i}-X_{\alpha}(\overline{\rho}_{t}^{m}))dt+\sigma D(\overline{X}_{t}^{m,i}-X_{\alpha}(\overline{\rho}_{t}^{m}))dB_{t}^{m,i}\,,\quad i\in[N]\,,\ m\in[M]\,,

(1.2)

where
\lambda,\sigma>0
are drift and diffusion parameters,
\{B_{t}^{m,i}\}_{m\in[M],i\in[N]}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
are standard independent Brownian motions, and
D(X):=\diag(X_{1},\dots,X_{d})
(anisotropic), or
D(X):=|X|
(isotropic), for all
X\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. However, in the subsequent analysis, we will specifically focus on the anisotropic case.
The system is complemented with identically and independently distributed (i.i.d.) initial data
\{(\overline{X}_{0}^{1,i},\dots,\overline{X}_{0}^{M,i})\}_{i\in[N]}
with respect to the common law
\overline{\rho}_{0}
.
The first term on the right hand-side of (
1.2
) is a deterministic drift that pulls the particles towards a current consensus point, computed as a convex combination of particles locations as

X_{\alpha}(\overline{\rho}_{t}^{m}):=\frac{1}{\|\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}_{m}}(\cdot;\overline{\boldsymbol{M}}_{t}^{-m})\|_{L^{1}(\overline{\rho}_{t}^{m})}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x_{m}\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}_{m}}(x_{m};\overline{\boldsymbol{M}}_{t}^{-m})\overline{\rho}_{t}^{m}(dx_{m})\,,

(1.3)

where
\overline{\rho}_{t}^{m}(dx_{m}):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\overline{X}_{t}^{m,i}}dx_{m}
is the empirical measure of the particle system at time
t\geq 0
,
\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}_{m}}
is a weight function defined as

\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}_{m}}(x;\boldsymbol{y}):=\exp\left(-\alpha\mathcal{E}_{m}(x;\boldsymbol{y})\right)\,,\quad\text{for all}\ x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,,\ \boldsymbol{y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(M-1)d}\,,

(1.4)

and
\overline{\boldsymbol{M}}_{t}
is a random vector given by

\overline{\boldsymbol{M}}_{t}:=\bigg(\frac{\overline{X}_{t}^{1,1}+\dots+\overline{X}_{t}^{1,N}}{N},\dots,\frac{\overline{X}_{t}^{M,1}+\dots+\overline{X}_{t}^{M,N}}{N}\bigg)\,.

(1.5)

The introduction of the second stochastic term in (
1.2
) is intended to promote exploration of the energy landscape of the cost function. When the consensus is achieved, meaning
\overline{X}_{t}^{m,i}=X_{\alpha}(\overline{\rho}_{t}^{m})
, both the drift and diffusion terms vanish. The choice of the weight function (
1.4
) comes from the well-known Laplace’s principle
[
47
,
20
]
, which states that for any probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there holds for any fixed
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(M-1)d}
,

\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{\alpha}\log\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{(M-1)d}}\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}_{m}}(x_{m};{\boldsymbol{y}})\mu(dx_{m})\right)\right)=\inf\limits_{x_{m}\in\rm{supp}(\mu)}\mathcal{E}_{m}(x_{m};{\boldsymbol{y}})\,.

(1.6)

The theoretical convergence analysis of a CBO method can be conducted using two different approaches. The first approach involves investigating the microscopic particle level directly, as demonstrated in
[
32
,
31
]
, by analyzing the particle system (
1.2
). The second approach, which the present paper adopts, is to consider the macroscopic level and focus on its corresponding mean-field equation in the large particle limit. This mean-field approach has been successfully utilized in previous works
[
26
,
28
,
27
,
36
]
.
Indeed, as the number of particles
N\to\infty
, the mean-field limit
[
3
,
35
,
9
,
57
,
38
]
result would imply that our CBO particle dynamic (
1.2
) well approximates solutions of the following mean-field kinetic Mckean–Vlasov type equations

dX_{t}^{m}=-\lambda(X_{t}^{m}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{m}))dt+\sigma D(X_{t}^{m}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{m}))dB_{t}^{m}\,,\quad m\in[M]\,,

(1.7)

where the consensus point is defined similarly to (
1.3
) as

X_{\alpha}({\rho}_{t}^{m}):=\frac{1}{\|\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}_{m}}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})\|_{L^{1}(\rho_{t}^{m})}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x_{m}\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}_{m}}(x_{m};\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m}){\rho}_{t}^{m}(dx_{m})\,,

(1.8)

where
\rho_{t}^{m}
is the distribution of
X_{t}^{m}
, and
\boldsymbol{M}_{t}
is the vector given by

\boldsymbol{M}_{t}:=\left(\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{1}],\dots,\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{M}]\right).

(1.9)

A direct application of Itô’s formula yields that the law
\rho_{t}
of
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}:=(X_{t}^{1},\dots,X_{t}^{M})
for
t\geq 0
is a weak solution to the following nonlinear Vlasov–Fokker–Plank equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\bigg(\lambda\nabla_{x_{m}}\cdot\left(\left(x_{m}-{X}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{m})\right)\rho_{t}\right)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\partial^{2}_{(x_{m})_{k}(x_{m})_{k}}\left((x_{m}-{X}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{m}))_{k}^{2}\rho_{t}\right)\bigg)\,,

(1.10)

with the initial data
\rho_{0}
being the law of
\boldsymbol{X}_{0}
. The current paper centers on investigating the convergence of the proposed CBO variant in the context of finding global NE in multiplayer games. While the rigorous mean-field approximation is an interesting avenue for further exploration, it falls outside the scope of the current work and is best pursued in future research endeavors. The well-posedness results regarding the CBO particle system (
1.2
) and its mean-field dynamics (
1.7
) have also been omitted from this work. However, it is worth noting that these results closely resemble the well-posedness theorems presented in
[
37
, Theorem 3, Theorem 6]
, where a CBO dynamic is introduced for two player zero-sum games. Moreover, in the following we shall assume the solution to (
1.7
) has the regularity
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}^{m}|^{4}]<\infty
for all
m\in[M]
and any time horizon
T>0
, which can be guaranteed by the assumption on the initial data
\mathbb{E}[|X_{0}^{m}|^{4}]<\infty
for all
m\in[M]
.

The main objective of this work is to establish the convergence of the dynamics
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
in (
1.7
) to the global NE point
(x_{1}^{*},\dots,x_{M}^{*})
as
t
approaches infinity. Inspired by
[
27
]
, we introduce the variance functions as follows:

V^{m}(t)=\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}^{m}-x_{m}^{*}|^{2}]\,,\quad\text{for each}\ m\in[M]\,,\quad\text{and}\quad V(t):=\sum_{m=1}^{M}V^{m}(t)\,,\quad\text{for}\ t>0\,.

(1.11)

By analyzing the decay behavior of the (cumulative) variance function
V(t)
we establish the convergence of the CBO dynamics to the global NE point. Specifically, we demonstrate that the function
V(t)
decays exponentially, with a decay rate controllable through the parameters of the CBO method. This also implies the convergence of the mean-field PDE (
1.10
) to a Dirac delta centering at the global NE with respect to the 2-Wasserstein distance, i.e.

W_{2}(\rho_{t},\delta_{(x_{1}^{*},\dots,x_{M}^{*})})\to 0\,,\quad\mbox{as }\alpha,\ t\to\infty\,.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we commence by outlining the assumptions governing the cost functions
\{\mathcal{E}_{m}\}_{m\in[M]}
(Assumptions
(A1)
–
(A4)
) and deriving an estimate for the variance function (
1.11
) (Lemma
2.1
). Subsequently, we establish a quantitative estimate of the Laplace principle (Proposition
2.1
) together with a result demonstrating that the probability mass around the target NE point remains bounded away from zero at any time
0<t<\infty
(Proposition
2.2
). By leveraging these outcomes, we finally demonstrate that the variance

exhibits exponential decay, achieving any desired level of accuracy
\varepsilon>0
(Theorem
2.3
). In Section
3
, we conduct a series of numerical experiments to evaluate the performance of our proposed CBO method and analyze its dependency on parameters. Additionally, we apply the method to solve a nonlinear non-convex Cournot’s oligopoly game involving multiple goods.

2
Global convergence

In this section, we present our main result about the global convergence in mean-field law for cost functions satisfying the following conditions.

Assumptions.

In this paper, the cost functions
\{\mathcal{E}_{m}\}_{m\in[M]}\subset\mathcal{C}({\mathbb{R}}^{Md})
satisfy:

(A1)

There exists a unique global NE point
(x_{1}^{*},\dots,x_{M}^{*})
and
|\mathcal{E}_{m}|\leq\overline{c}
for all
m\in M
.

(A2)

For any
m\in[M]
and
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(M-1)d}
, there exists a unique
\overline{x}_{m}({\boldsymbol{y}})
such that

\overline{x}_{m}({\boldsymbol{y}})=\arg\min_{x_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathcal{E}_{m}(x_{m};{\boldsymbol{y}})\,.

(2.1)

Especially, it holds that
\overline{x}_{m}(\boldsymbol{x}_{-m}^{*})=x_{m}^{*}
. Moreover, there exist
\overline{c}_{1},\overline{c}_{2}>0
independent of
m
such that

\displaystyle|\overline{x}_{m}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{1})-\overline{x}_{m}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{2})|\leq\overline{c}_{1}|{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{2}|\,,\quad\mbox{for any }m\in[M]\mbox{ and }\ {\boldsymbol{y}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{y}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{(M-1)d}

(2.2)

\displaystyle|\overline{x}_{m}({\boldsymbol{y}})|\leq\overline{c}_{2}\,,\quad\mbox{for any }m\in[M]\mbox{ and }\ {\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(M-1)d}\,.

(2.3)

(A3)

For each
m\in[M]
and
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(M-1)d}
, there exist
\mathcal{E}_{\infty},\eta,\nu,R_{0}>0
independent of
m
such that

\eta|x_{m}-\overline{x}_{m}({\boldsymbol{y}})|\leq|\mathcal{E}_{m}(x_{m};{\boldsymbol{y}})-\mathcal{E}_{m}(\overline{x}_{m}({\boldsymbol{y}});{\boldsymbol{y}})|^{\nu}\,,\quad\text{for all}\ x_{m}\in B_{R_{0}}(\overline{x}_{m}({\boldsymbol{y}}))\,,

(2.4)

\mathcal{E}_{\infty}<\mathcal{E}_{m}(x_{m};{\boldsymbol{y}})-\mathcal{E}_{m}(\overline{x}_{m}({\boldsymbol{y}});{\boldsymbol{y}})\,,\quad\text{for all}\ x_{m}\in B_{R_{0}}(\overline{x}_{m}({\boldsymbol{y}}))^{c}\,.

(2.5)

(A4)

For each
q>0
there exists some
r\in(0,R_{0}]
such that

\sup_{m\in[M]}\sup_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(M-1)d}}\sup_{\ x_{m}\in B_{r}(\overline{x}_{m}({\boldsymbol{y}}))}|\mathcal{E}_{m}(x_{m};{\boldsymbol{y}})-\mathcal{E}_{m}(\overline{x}_{m}({\boldsymbol{y}});{\boldsymbol{y}})|\leq q\,.

(2.6)

Note that
B_{r}(x)
, here and in the sequel, represents the ball centered in
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with radius
r>0
with respect to the
\ell^{\infty}
norm, i.e.,
B_{r}(x):=\{x^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\max_{k\in[d]}|(x^{\prime})^{k}-x^{k}|\leq r\}
.

Remark 2.1
.

The assumptions
(A1)
,
(A3)
, and
(A4)
above are standard in the analysis of the CBO methods. In fact, these also appear, e.g., in
[
27
, Definition 9]
. Assumption
(A2)
is tailored to our multiplayer setting, accounting for the fact that each player’s strategy is contingent upon the decisions made by the other players. Specifically, later in the analysis, we show that Assumption
(A2)
, coupled with a quantitative Laplace principle, is indeed crucial for obtaining a bound for
|x_{m}^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{m})|
, with
m\in[M]
. It is worth noting that our numerical experiments suggest that the assumption in (
2.2
) could potentially be relaxed or even dropped (cf. Section
3.1.2
), which we leave for future work. Moreover, the assumptions do not require any convexity/concavity or differentiability conditions. For instance, consider the nonconvex, nonconcave, and nondifferentiable functions
\mathcal{E}_{m}(x_{m};\boldsymbol{x}_{-m})=\Big|\tanh\Big(x_{m}-(4+|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{m}|)^{-1}\sin\Big(\frac{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{-m}}{2}\Big)\Big)\Big|\cdot(2+\sin(x_{m}))^{-1}
with
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{-m}=M^{-1}\sum_{j\neq m}x_{j}
for
m\in[M],\,d=1,\,(x_{m},\boldsymbol{x}_{-m})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{M-1}
, which lead to a unique global NE point with
x_{1}^{*}=x_{2}^{*}=\dots=x_{M}^{*}=0
and have all the assumption
(A1)
–
(A4)
satisfied.

We now present a differential inequality for the variance function
V^{m}(t)
according to (
1.11
) , whose proof is postponed to the Appendix.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\{\mathcal{E}_{m}\}_{m\in[M]}
satisfy Assumptions
(A1)
–
(A4)
, and we assume that
\lambda,\sigma
satisfy

2\lambda-\sigma^{2}>0
. Then for any
m\in[M]
the variance function
V^{m}(t)
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{dV^{m}(t)}{dt}\leq-(2\lambda-\sigma^{2})V^{m}(t)+2(\lambda+\sigma^{2})V^{m}(t)^{\frac{1}{2}}|x_{m}^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{m})|+\sigma^{2}|x_{m}^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{m})|^{2}\,.

(2.7)

The main idea underpinning our main convergence result consists of showing that

\frac{dV^{m}(t)}{dt}\leq-\frac{1}{2}(2\lambda-\sigma^{2})V^{m}(t)\,,

(2.8)

which formally requires that

2(\lambda+\sigma^{2})V^{m}(t)^{\frac{1}{2}}|x_{m}^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{m})|+\sigma^{2}|x_{m}^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{m})|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}(2\lambda-\sigma^{2})V^{m}(t)\,.

(2.9)

This condition is fulfilled by ensuring that
|x_{m}^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{m})|
remains sufficiently small. To establish this, we shall utilize a quantitative estimate of the Laplace principle in conjunction with (
2.2
) in Assumption
(A2)
. Indeed, for each
m\in[M]
, the former provides a bound for
|\overline{x}_{m}(\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{m})|
, while the latter allows us to turn it into a bound for
|x_{m}^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{m})|
. This reveals a distinction between our multiplayer game setting and the consensus-based methods for optimization (see, e.g.,
[
27
]
).

2.1
Quantitative Laplace principle

The following proposition yields a quantitative estimate of the Laplace principle (
1.6
) by using the inverse continuity Assumption
(A3)
. To do so, for each player
m\in[M]
, we need to quantify the maximum discrepancy for the cost function
\mathcal{E}_{m}
around the corresponding best strategy with respect to
\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m}
, namely, for
r>0
,

\mathcal{E}_{r}^{m}(\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m}):=\sup_{x\in B_{r}(\overline{x}_{m}(\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m}))}\left|\mathcal{E}_{m}(x;\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})-\mathcal{E}_{m}(\overline{x}_{m}(\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m});\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})\right|\,.

(2.10)

We have the following result:

Proposition 2.1
.

Assume that
\{\mathcal{E}_{m}\}_{m\in[M]}
satisfy Assumptions
(A1)
–
(A4)
. For any
m\in[M]
,
t>0
and
r\in(0,R_{0}]
, let
\mathcal{E}_{r}^{m}(\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})
be defined as in (
2.10
),
0<q\leq\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\infty}}{2}
, and
r:=\max\{s\in(0,R_{0}]:~\sup_{m\in[M],\boldsymbol{y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(M-1)d}}\mathcal{E}_{s}^{m}(\boldsymbol{y})\leq q\}
. Then, it holds that

\displaystyle|\overline{x}_{m}(\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{m})|\leq

\displaystyle\frac{(2q)^{\nu}}{\eta}+\frac{\exp\left(-\alpha q\right)}{\rho_{t}^{m}(B_{r}(\overline{x}_{m}(\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})))}\int|x-\overline{x}_{m}(\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})|\rho_{t}^{m}(dx)\,.

(2.11)

Remark 2.2
.

Such
r>0
exists because of Assumption
(A4)
, and it is independent of
m
and
\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m}
. The right hand-side of equation (
2.11
) can become small by selecting a sufficiently small value for
q
and a suitably large value for
\alpha
.

Proof.

To simplify notation, let us denote by
\overline{x}_{t}^{m}:=\overline{x}_{m}(\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})
. Let
\widetilde{r}\geq r>0
. Using Jensen’s inequality one can deduce

|\overline{x}_{t}^{m}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{m})|\leq\int_{B_{\widetilde{r}}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m})}|x-\overline{x}_{t}^{m}|\frac{\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}_{m}}(x;\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})\rho_{t}^{m}(dx)}{\|\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}_{m}}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})\|_{L_{1}(\rho_{t}^{m})}}+\int_{B_{\widetilde{r}}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m})^{c}}|x-\overline{x}_{t}^{m}|\frac{\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}_{m}}(x;\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})\rho_{t}^{m}(dx)}{\|\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}_{m}}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})\|_{L_{1}(\rho_{t}^{m})}}\,.

The first term is bounded by
\widetilde{r}
since
|x-\overline{x}_{t}^{m}|\leq\widetilde{r}
for all
x\in B_{\widetilde{r}}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m})
. Let us now tackle the second term. It follows from Markov’s inequality that

\displaystyle\|\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}_{m}}

\displaystyle(\cdot,\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})\|_{L_{1}(\rho_{t}^{m})}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\exp\Big(-\alpha(\mathcal{E}_{r}^{m}(\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})+\mathcal{E}_{m}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m};\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m}))\Big)\cdot

\displaystyle\quad\rho_{t}^{m}\left(\bigg\{x:\exp\left(-\alpha\mathcal{E}_{m}(x;\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})\right)\geq\exp\Big(-\alpha(\mathcal{E}_{r}^{m}(\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})+\mathcal{E}_{m}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m};\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m}))\Big)\bigg\}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\Big(-\alpha(\mathcal{E}_{r}^{m}(\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})+\mathcal{E}_{m}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m};\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m}))\Big)\cdot

\displaystyle\quad\rho_{t}^{m}\left(\bigg\{x:\mathcal{E}_{m}(x;\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})\leq\Big(\mathcal{E}_{r}^{m}(\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})+\mathcal{E}_{m}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m};\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})\Big)\bigg\}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\alpha\Big(\mathcal{E}_{r}^{m}(\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})+\mathcal{E}_{m}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m},\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})\Big)\right)\rho_{t}^{m}\left(B_{r}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m})\right)\,.

(2.12)

Hence, using that

\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}_{m}}(x;\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})\leq C_{\widetilde{r}}:=\exp\Big(-\alpha\inf_{x\in B_{\widetilde{r}}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m})^{c}}\mathcal{E}_{m}(x;\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})\Big)\,,\quad\text{for all}\ x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

and denoting by
C_{r}
the right hand-side of (
2.1
), for the second term we have

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\widetilde{r}}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m})^{c}}

\displaystyle|x-\overline{x}_{t}^{m}|\frac{\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}_{m}}(x;\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})\rho_{t}^{m}(dx)}{\|\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}_{m}}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})\|_{L_{1}(\rho_{t}^{m})}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{C_{r}}\int_{B_{\widetilde{r}}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m})^{c}}|x-\overline{x}_{t}^{m}|\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}_{m}}(x;\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})\rho_{t}^{m}(dx)\leq\frac{C_{\widetilde{r}}}{C_{r}}\int_{B_{\widetilde{r}}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m})^{c}}|x-\overline{x}_{t}^{m}|\rho_{t}^{m}(dx)\,.

(2.13)

Thus from (
2.1
), for any
\widetilde{r}\geq r>0
we obtain

|\overline{x}_{t}^{m}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{m})|\leq\widetilde{r}+\frac{C_{\widetilde{r}}}{C_{r}}\int_{B_{r}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m})}|x-\overline{x}_{t}^{m}|\rho_{t}^{m}(dx)\,,

(2.14)

where, recalling the definition of
C_{\widetilde{r}}
and
C_{r}
above,

\frac{C_{\widetilde{r}}}{C_{r}}=\frac{1}{\rho_{t}^{m}\left(B_{r}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m})\right)}\exp\left(-\alpha\Big(\inf_{x\in B_{\widetilde{r}}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m})^{c}}\mathcal{E}_{m}(x;\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})-\mathcal{E}_{r}^{m}(\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})-\mathcal{E}_{m}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m};\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})\Big)\right)\,.

(2.15)

Next, we choose
\widetilde{r}=\eta^{-1}(q+\mathcal{E}_{r}^{m}(\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m}))^{\nu}
, and note that by Assumption
(A2)
this is indeed a suitable choice since

\displaystyle\widetilde{r}

\displaystyle\geq\eta^{-1}\mathcal{E}_{r}^{m}(\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})^{\nu}

\displaystyle=\eta^{-1}\bigg(\sup_{x\in B_{r}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m})}|\mathcal{E}_{m}(x;\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})-\mathcal{E}_{m}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m};\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})|\bigg)^{\nu}\geq\sup_{x\in B_{r}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m})}|x-\overline{x}_{t}^{m}|=r\,.

It only remains to see that with this choice, (
2.14
) and (
2.15
) turn into (
2.11
). Notice that

\inf_{x\in B_{\widetilde{r}}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m})^{c}}\mathcal{E}_{m}(x;\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})-\mathcal{E}_{m}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m};\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})\geq\begin{cases}(\widetilde{r}\eta)^{\frac{1}{\nu}}\,,&\ \mbox{if}\ x\in B_{\widetilde{r}}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m})^{c}\cap B_{R_{0}}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m})\,,\\
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}\,,&\ \mbox{if}\ x\in B_{R_{0}}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m})^{c}\,.\end{cases}

Thus, since
(\widetilde{r}\eta)^{\frac{1}{\nu}}=q+\mathcal{E}_{r}^{m}(\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})\leq 2q\leq\mathcal{E}_{\infty}
, we have

\inf_{x\in B_{\widetilde{r}}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m})^{c}}\mathcal{E}_{m}(x;\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})-\mathcal{E}_{m}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m};\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})\geq q+\mathcal{E}_{r}^{m}(\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})\,.

Therefore

\inf_{x\in B_{\widetilde{r}}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m})^{c}}\mathcal{E}_{m}(x,\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})-\mathcal{E}_{r}^{m}(\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})-\mathcal{E}_{m}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m},\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})\geq q+\mathcal{E}_{r}^{m}(\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})-\mathcal{E}_{r}^{m}(\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})=q\,.

In particular, from (
2.15
) we get
C_{\widetilde{r}}/{C_{r}}\geq\exp(-\alpha q)[\rho_{t}^{m}(B_{r}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m}))]^{-1}
. Inserting the latter and the definition of
\widetilde{r}
into (
2.14
), we obtain the claim.
∎

To eventually apply the above proposition, one needs to ensure that
\rho_{t}^{m}(B_{r}(\overline{x}_{m}(\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})))
is bounded away from
0
for a finite time horizon
T
for every
m\in[M]
. To do so, we employ a rather technical argument inspired from
[
27
, Proposition 23]
, and introduce the mollifier
\phi_{r}^{\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{Md}\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined by

\phi_{r}^{\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\begin{cases}\prod_{m=1}^{M}\prod_{k=1}^{d}\exp\left(1-\frac{r^{2}}{r^{2}-\left(x_{m}-\overline{x}_{m}\right)_{k}^{2}}\right)\,,&\ \text{if}\ \boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in\boldsymbol{B}_{r}(\overline{\boldsymbol{x}})\,,\\
0\,,&\ \text{else}\,,\end{cases}

(2.16)

where
\boldsymbol{B}_{r}(\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}):=B_{r}(\overline{x}_{1})\times\dots\times B_{r}(\overline{x}_{m})
, for
r>0
, and
\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}:=(\overline{x}_{1},\dots,\overline{x}_{M})
is any point in
[-\overline{c}_{2},\overline{c}_{2}]^{M}
. Then, we get the following result, whose proof is postponed to the Appendix.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\phi_{r}
be the mollifier defined in (
2.16
) and
({\boldsymbol{X}}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
be the solution to (

) up to a time

. Assume that
\sup_{m\in[M],t\in[0,T]}\left|x_{m}^{*}-X_{\alpha}\left(\rho_{t}^{m}\right)\right|\leq B

. Then, there exists some constant
\vartheta>0
depending only on

and

such that

\inf_{\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}\in[-\overline{c}_{2},\overline{c}_{2}]^{M}}\mathbb{P}({\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}\in\boldsymbol{B}_{r}(\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}))\geq\inf_{\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}\in[-\overline{c}_{2},\overline{c}_{2}]^{M}}\mathbb{E}[\phi_{r}^{\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}}({\boldsymbol{X}}_{t})]\geq\inf_{\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}\in[-\overline{c}_{2},\overline{c}_{2}]^{M}}\mathbb{E}[\phi_{r}^{\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}}({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0})]\exp(-\vartheta t)

(2.17)

\boldsymbol{x}^{*}:=(x_{1}^{*},\dots,\overline{x}_{M}^{*})

2.2
Global convergence in mean-field law

Now we are ready to prove the global convergence result, which provides a rate of decreasing for the variance function
V(t)
defined in (
1.11
), for
t
within a prescribed time-range.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
({\boldsymbol{X}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a solution to (
1.7
). Assume that
\{\mathcal{E}_{m}\}_{m\in[M]}
satisfy Assumptions
(A1)
–
(A4)
, and
\lambda,\sigma
satisfy

2\lambda>\sigma^{2}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{c}_{1}\leq\frac{1}{4}\min\left\{\frac{2\lambda-\sigma^{2}}{8\sqrt{M}(\lambda+\sigma^{2})},\sqrt{\frac{2\lambda-\sigma^{2}}{4M\sigma^{2}}}\right\}\,.

(2.18)

Let
\varepsilon>0
be any accuracy, and assume that the initial data satisfies
V(0)\geq 2\varepsilon
,

\rho_{0}^{m}(B_{r_{0}}(\overline{x}_{m}(\textbf{M}_{0}^{-m})))>0\text{ for all }m\in[M],\quad\text{and}\quad\inf_{\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}\in[-\overline{c}_{2},\overline{c}_{2}]^{M}}\mathbb{E}[\phi_{r_{1}}^{\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}}({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0})]>0,

(2.19)

where
r_{0},r_{1}>0
are to be determined later. Then for
\alpha
sufficiently large there exists some
0<T_{\ast}\leq T_{\varepsilon}
such that

V(t)\leq V(0)\exp\left(-\frac{2\lambda-\sigma^{2}}{2}t\right)\,,\quad\mbox{for}\ t\in[0,T_{\ast})\,,

(2.20)

and
V(T_{\ast})\leq\varepsilon
. Here
T_{\varepsilon}:=\frac{2}{2\lambda-\sigma^{2}}\log(\frac{V(0)}{\varepsilon})
.

Remark 2.3
.

The theorem above asserts that for any given accuracy
\varepsilon>0
, we can choose
\alpha
to be sufficiently large (dependent on
\varepsilon
) such that the variance
V(t)
(dependent on
\alpha
) of the CBO dynamic decreases exponentially until it achieves the desired accuracy of
\varepsilon
. Conversely, for any fixed
\alpha
, the achievable accuracy
\varepsilon
is constrained and depends on the value of
\alpha
. This can be seen in (
2.28
). In addition, the initial preparation (
2.19
) may be satisfied for all
r_{0},r_{1}>0
if the density function of
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}
is continuous and has support in the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{Md}
; for instance, this may be easily satisfied when generating initial samples of
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}
with Gaussian distributions.

Proof.

First, we define

T_{\alpha}:=\inf\bigg\{t\geq 0\,:\,V(t)\leq\varepsilon\quad\mbox{or}\quad\sum_{m=1}^{M}|x_{m}^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{m})|\geq 2Mc_{3}V(0)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg\}\,,

(2.21)

where
c_{3}
is the constant given by

c_{3}:=\min\left\{\frac{2\lambda-\sigma^{2}}{8\sqrt{M}(\lambda+\sigma^{2})},\sqrt{\frac{2\lambda-\sigma^{2}}{4M\sigma^{2}}}\right\}\,.

(2.22)

As in Proposition
2.1
, we simplify notation denoting by
\overline{x}_{t}^{m}:=\overline{x}_{m}(\boldsymbol{M}_{t}^{-m})
for all
t\in[0,T_{\alpha})
. Firstly, it follows from Proposition
2.1
that for

q_{0}:=\frac{1}{2}\min\bigg\{\bigg(\frac{\eta c_{3}V(0)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{2}\bigg)^{1/\nu},\mathcal{E}_{\infty}\bigg\}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad r_{0}:=\max\left\{s\in[0,R_{0}]:\sup_{m\in[M]}\sup_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(M-1)d}}\mathcal{E}_{s}^{m}({\boldsymbol{y}})\leq q_{0}\right\}\,,

which, by construction, satisfy
r_{0}\leq R_{0}
and
\mathcal{E}_{r_{0}}^{m}(\boldsymbol{M}^{-m}_{0})\leq\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\infty}}{2}
with
\mathcal{E}_{r_{0}}^{m}(\boldsymbol{M}^{-m}_{0})
defined as in (
2.10
), we have the following estimate

|\overline{x}^{m}_{0}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{0}^{m})|\leq\frac{(2q_{0})^{\nu}}{\eta}+\frac{\exp\left(-\alpha q_{0}\right)}{\rho_{0}^{m}\left(B_{r_{0}}(\overline{x}^{m}_{0})\right)}\sqrt{2}(1+\overline{c}_{1})V(0)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,,

(2.23)

where we have used that

\int|x-\overline{x}^{m}_{0}|\rho_{0}^{m}(dx)\leq V^{m}(0)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\overline{c}_{1}\bigg(\sum_{k\neq m}^{M}V^{k}(0)\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\sqrt{2}(1+\overline{c}_{1})V(0)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,,

which holds since, by Assumption
(A2)
,

\displaystyle|x-\overline{x}^{m}_{0}|

\displaystyle\leq|x-x_{m}^{*}|+|x_{m}^{*}-\overline{x}^{m}_{0}|

\displaystyle=|x-x_{m}^{*}|+|\overline{x}_{m}(\boldsymbol{x}_{-m}^{*})-\overline{x}_{m}(\boldsymbol{M}^{-m}_{0})|\leq|x-x_{m}^{*}|+\overline{c}_{1}\bigg(\sum_{k\neq m}^{M}V^{k}(0)\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Then, using
\overline{c}_{1}\leq\frac{c_{3}}{4}
, Assumption
(A2)
and the definition of
q_{0}
, it holds that

\displaystyle|x_{m}^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{0}^{m})|

\displaystyle\leq|x_{m}^{*}-\overline{x}^{m}_{0}|+|\overline{x}^{m}_{0}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{0}^{m})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{3}}{4}V(0)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{c_{3}}{2}V(0)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{\exp\left(-\alpha q_{0}\right)}{\rho_{0}^{m}\left(B_{r_{0}}(\overline{x}^{m}_{0})\right)}\sqrt{2}(1+\overline{c}_{1})V(0)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\frac{3}{4}c_{3}+\frac{\exp\left(-\alpha q_{0}\right)}{\rho_{0}^{m}\left(B_{r_{0}}(\overline{x}^{m}_{0})\right)}\sqrt{2}(1+\overline{c}_{1})\bigg)V(0)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,.

If we now choose
\alpha
sufficiently large, e.g.,
\alpha\geq\sup_{m\in[M]}\alpha_{0}^{m}
with

\alpha_{0}^{m}=\frac{-\log(c_{3}\rho_{0}^{m}(B_{r_{0}}(\overline{x}^{m}_{0})))+\log(4\sqrt{2}(1+\overline{c}_{1}))}{q_{0}}\,,

one has

\sum_{m=1}^{M}|x_{m}^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{0}^{m})|\leq Mc_{3}V(0)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,.

This together with the assumption that
V(0)\geq 2\varepsilon
implies
T_{\alpha}>0
.

According to the definition of
T_{\alpha}
one has

V(t)>\varepsilon\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{m=1}^{M}|x_{m}^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{m})|<2Mc_{3}V(0)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,,\quad\text{for all}\ t\in[0,T_{\alpha})\,,

and at
t=T_{\alpha}
, it holds that
V(T_{\alpha})\leq\varepsilon
or
\sum_{m=1}^{M}|x_{m}^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{T_{\alpha}}^{m})|\geq 2Mc_{3}V(0)^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Next, we prove that
T_{\alpha}\leq T_{\varepsilon}
and

decreases exponentially on

.

Case

T_{\alpha}\leq T_{\varepsilon}
: Arguing as in (

), we get for every

\displaystyle|\overline{x}_{t}^{m}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{m})|\leq\frac{(2q_{1})^{\nu}}{\eta}+\frac{\exp\left(-\alpha q_{1}\right)}{\rho_{t}^{m}(B_{r_{1}}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m}))}\sqrt{2}(1+\overline{c}_{1})V(t)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,,

(2.24)

where
q_{1}
and

are constants defined by

(2.25)

Then, we obtain

\displaystyle|x_{m}^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{m})|

\displaystyle\leq|x_{m}^{*}-\overline{x}_{t}^{m}|+|\overline{x}_{t}^{m}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{m})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{3}}{4}V(t)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{c_{3}}{2}V(t)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{\exp\left(-\alpha q_{1}\right)}{\rho_{t}^{m}\left(B_{r_{1}}(\overline{x}_{t}^{m})\right)}\sqrt{2}(1+\overline{c}_{1})V(t)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3c_{3}}{4}V(t)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{\exp\left(-\alpha q_{1}\right)}{\inf_{|\overline{x}_{m}|\leq\overline{c}_{2}}\rho_{t}^{m}\left(B_{r_{1}}(\overline{x}_{m})\right)}\sqrt{2}(1+\overline{c}_{1})V(t)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,.

(2.26)

Moreover, (
2.17

\displaystyle\inf_{|\overline{x}_{m}|\leq\overline{c}_{2}}\rho_{t}^{m}\left(B_{r_{1}}(\overline{x}_{m})\right)=\inf_{|\overline{x}_{m}|\leq\overline{c}_{2}}\mathbb{P}\big(X_{t}^{m}\in B_{r_{1}}(\overline{x}_{m})\big)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\inf_{\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}\in[-\overline{c}_{2},\overline{c}_{2}]^{M}}\mathbb{P}({\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}\in\boldsymbol{B}_{r_{1}}(\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}))\geq\inf_{\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}\in[-\overline{c}_{2},\overline{c}_{2}]^{M}}\mathbb{E}[\phi_{r_{1}}^{\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}}({\boldsymbol{X}}_{t})]\geq\inf_{\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}\in[-\overline{c}_{2},\overline{c}_{2}]^{M}}\mathbb{E}[\phi_{r_{1}}^{\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}}({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0})]\exp(-\vartheta t)

depends only on

is independent of
\alpha
.
This allows us to conclude that for all

\displaystyle|x_{m}^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{m})|\leq\frac{3c_{3}}{4}V(t)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{2\exp\left(-\alpha q_{1}\right)\exp(\vartheta T_{\varepsilon})}{\inf_{\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}\in[-\overline{c}_{2},\overline{c}_{2}]^{M}}\mathbb{E}[\phi_{r_{1}}^{\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}}({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0})]}\sqrt{2}(1+\overline{c}_{1})V(t)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq c_{3}V(t)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,,

(2.27)

where we choose
\alpha\geq\alpha_{1}
with

(2.28)

From the upper bound for the time derivative of

V(t)\leq V(0)\exp\left(-\frac{2\lambda-\sigma^{2}}{2}t\right)\,,\quad\mbox{for}\ t\in[0,T_{\alpha})\,.

(2.30)

This implies

namely,
\sum_{m=1}^{M}|x_{m}^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{T_{\alpha}}^{m})|\leq Mc_{3}V(0)^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Then, according to the definition of
T_{\alpha}
we must have

.

Case

: By the definition of
T_{\alpha}
we know that
V(t)\geq\varepsilon
and
\sum_{m=1}^{M}|x_{m}^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{m})|\leq 2Mc_{3}V(0)^{\frac{1}{2}}

. Then, arguing as the in the first case, we conclude

This estimate and the fact that

imply
V(T_{\varepsilon})\leq\varepsilon
, which is a contradiction. Thus this case can never happen.
∎

3.2
Nonlinear oligopoly games with several goods

In this section, we present the numerical performance of Algorithm
1
to solve a nonlinear variant of a classical NE problem that arises from economics, especially, from Cournot’s model of oligopoly games with several goods
[
18

. We assume there are
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
economic agents holding
d\in{\mathbb{N}}

h\in[d]

quantity of good
h
. Every product incurs into a production cost quantified by
c_{h}>0
, for all
h\in[d]
. In the present model, we assume that the quantity of each good influences the price of the others as well. In this setting, a typical price function is of the form

p_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})=\max\{a-b\ell_{h}^{T}(x_{1}+\dots+x_{M}),0\}^{\beta}\,,\quad\text{for all}\ \boldsymbol{x}:=(x_{1},\dots,x_{M})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times M}\,,

(3.4)

where
\beta,a,b>0
, and
\ell_{h}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+}
for all
h\in[d]
. Let us denote by
\boldsymbol{p}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times M}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
the function defined by
\boldsymbol{p}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})=p_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})
for all
h\in[d]
and all
\boldsymbol{x}:=(x_{1},\dots,x_{M})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times M}
. The resulting cost function for agent
m

can indeed approximate an optimal solution efficiently; (ii) investigate the dependency of Algorithm

with respect to the dimension.

4
Conclusion

In this paper, we present a novel variant of the consensus-based particle method aimed at identifying global NE within non-convex multiplayer games. We establish the global convergence of the dynamics to the global NE, denoted as
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=(x_{1}^{*},\dots,x_{m}^{*})
, of the cost functions
\{\mathcal{E}_{m}\}_{m\in[M]}
in mean-field law (large particle limit). Our analysis focuses on the time-evolution of the distance between the mean-field dynamics
\{X_{t}^{m}\}_{m\in[M]}
and the unique global NE
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
, i.e.
V(t)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}^{m}-x_{m}^{*}|^{2}]
, demonstrating its exponential decay over time, achieving any prescribed accuracy
\varepsilon
under suitable assumptions. We illustrate the numerical performance of this method using a one-dimensional example as well as real-world applications rooted in economics, particularly oligopoly games involving multiple goods. These examples confirm that the theoretical predictions are indeed reflected in practice, underscoring the method’s competitive performance, particularly when dealing with highly nonlinear and non-convex problems in finite dimensions.
