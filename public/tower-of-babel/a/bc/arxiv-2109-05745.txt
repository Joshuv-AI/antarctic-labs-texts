The category of finite strings
Henning Krause

Abstract
. We introduce the category of finite strings and study its basic properties. The category is closely related to the augmented simplex category, and it models categories of linear representations. Each lattice of non-crossing partitions arises naturally as a lattice of subobjects.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
05E10 (primary), 16G20, 18N50 (secondary)

1.
Introduction

Strings are considered to be one of the most basic combinatorial
structures arising in representation theory of associative
algebras. In fact, many of the interactions with neighbouring fields
involve strings and their corresponding representations, which are
also known as string modules.

In this note we introduce a category of finite strings and establish
some connections. First of all, we notice that the category of
connected strings is equivalent to the augmented simplex category

\Delta
(cf.
[
9
,
17
]
), once the initial and terminal
objects in
\Delta
are identified. Then we show that the category of
finite strings models categories of linear representations. More
precisely, we provide an equivalence between finite strings and
certain abelian categories (hereditary and uniserial length categories
with only finitely many simple objects and split over a fixed field,
cf.
[
1
]
), where morphisms between strings correspond to
certain exact functors. In this context it is appropriate to include
cyclic strings which correspond to abelian categories of infinite
representation type. This is somewhat parallel to the cyclic category
of Connes and others
[
3
,
4
]
; however we add new objects
(cyclic strings) while the cyclic category keeps the objects of
\Delta

and only morphisms are added.

Any morphism in the category of finite strings admits an epi-mono
factorisation. Thus it is of interest to study the subobjects of a
given object, at least for any connected string. We show for a linear
string of length
n
that the lattice of subobjects is isomorphic to
the lattice
\operatorname{NC}(n+1)
of non-crossing partitions
[
16
]
, while
the lattice
\operatorname{NC}^{B}(n)
of type
B
non-crossing partitions

[
19
]
arises for a cyclic string of length
n
.

The correspondence between strings and categories of linear
representations identifies subobjects of strings with thick
subcategories of abelian categories. In this way we recover the
beautiful classification of thick subcategories for quiver
representations of type
A
due to Ingalls and Thomas
[
13
]
,
and we add a classification for nilpotent representations of cyclic
quivers which seems to be new. The cyclic case can be
used to complete the classification of all thick subcategories for
representations of any tame hereditary algebra, including the ones
that are not generated by exceptional sequences, and therefore
complementing the work in
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
. We
point out that the category of strings can be extended to include all
Dynkin types, beyond the type
A
in this work, and analogous to the
categorification of non-crossing partitions for all Dynkin types in

[
11
]
.

Finally, let us mention the connection with some recent work which is
concerned with Iyama’s higher Auslander algebras of type
A

[
14
]
. These algebras form a natural generalisation of the
hereditary algebras of type
A
arising in the present work. In

[
7
]
the authors point out the simplicial structure of the
representations for these higher Auslander algebras, using some
advanced categorical formalism. Wide subcategories of representations
generalise thick subcategories and these are studied for type
A

higher Auslander algebras in
[
10
]
.

2.
Connected strings

In this work we introduce the category of finite strings. For any
natural number
n
the connected string of length
n
is
denoted by
\Sigma_{n}
. Each string comes equipped with its set of
(connected) substrings, together with a multiplication on the set of
substrings given by concatenation. The objects of the category are
finite coproducts of connected strings, and the morphisms are maps
that preserve substrings and their multiplication.

A
basic string
is a pair
s=(s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime})
of integers
s^{\prime}\leq s^{\prime\prime}
.
We write
\ell(s)=s^{\prime\prime}-s^{\prime}+1
for the
length
of
s
and add a

zero string

*
satisfying
\ell(*)=0
. Strings of length one
are called
simple
and we set
s_{i}:=(i,i)
,
i\in\mathbb{Z}
. For a
string
s=(i,j)
we call the simple strings
s_{i},s_{i+1},\ldots,s_{j}
its

composition factors
.

A multiplication of basic strings is given by concatenation. For

s,t
set

st:=\begin{cases}(s^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})&\text{for }s=(s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}),\,t=(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime}),\,s^{\prime\prime}+1=t^{\prime},\\
t&\text{for }s=*,\\
s&\text{for }t=*,\\
*&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This multiplication is not associative. For instance,
we have

(s_{0}s_{1})s_{0}=(0,1)s_{0}=*\qquad\text{and}\qquad s_{0}(s_{1}s_{0})=s_{0}*=s_{0}.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
the
connected string
of length

n
is by definition the set of basic strings

\Sigma_{n}:=\{s=(s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime})\mid 0\leq s^{\prime}\leq s^{\prime\prime}<n\}\cup\{*\}.

A morphism
\phi\colon\Sigma_{m}\to\Sigma_{n}
is by definition a map such that
for all
s,t\in\Sigma_{m}

(2.1)

\phi(st)=\phi(s)\phi(t)\qquad\text{and}\qquad st\neq*=\phi(st)\implies\phi(s)=*=\phi(t).

The string
*
plays the
role of a base point. In fact, a morphism is base point preserving
since

\phi(*)=\phi(**)=\phi(*)\phi(*)=*.

Any morphism is determined by the
images of the simple strings but it need not preserve the length of
basic strings.

We define
standard morphisms
as follows. Let
n\geq 1
. The morphism

\delta^{i}_{n}\colon\Sigma_{n-1}\longrightarrow\Sigma_{n}\qquad(0\leq i\leq n)

is given by the unique injective map such that
s_{i-1}
and
s_{i}

are not in its image. Note that
\delta_{1}^{0}=\delta_{1}^{1}
. The
morphism

\sigma^{i}_{n}\colon\Sigma_{n}\longrightarrow\Sigma_{n-1}\qquad(0\leq i<n)

is
given by the unique surjective map such that
s_{i}
is sent to the
zero string.

The standard morphisms are analogues of the face and degeneracy maps
for simplices. In fact, they satisfy the following
simplicial identities

[
17
, VII.5]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

The standard morphisms satisfy the following identities:

(2.2)

\displaystyle\delta^{i}_{n+1}\circ\delta^{j}_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathrlap{\delta^{j+1}_{n+1}\circ\delta^{i}_{n}}\hphantom{\begin{cases}\delta^{i}_{n}\circ\sigma^{j-1}_{n}&\\
\operatorname{id}&\\
\delta^{i-1}_{n}\circ\sigma^{j}_{n}&\end{cases}}\kern-1.2pti\leq j

\displaystyle\sigma^{j}_{n}\circ\sigma^{i}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\mathrlap{\sigma^{i}_{n}\circ\sigma^{j+1}_{n+1}}\hphantom{\begin{cases}\delta^{i}_{n}\circ\sigma^{j-1}_{n}&\\
\operatorname{id}&\\
\delta^{i-1}_{n}\circ\sigma^{j}_{n}&\end{cases}}\kern-1.2pti\leq j

\displaystyle\sigma^{j}_{n}\circ\delta^{i}_{n}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\delta^{i}_{n}\circ\sigma^{j-1}_{n}&i<j\\
\operatorname{id}&i=j\text{ or }i=j+1\\
\delta^{i-1}_{n}\circ\sigma^{j}_{n}&i>j+1\end{cases}

Proof.

This is easily checked, for instance by tracing the images of the
simple strings. We have

(2.3)

\delta_{n}^{i}(s_{j})=\begin{cases}s_{j}&j<i-1\\
(j-1,j)&j=i-1\\
s_{j+1}&j>i-1\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma_{n}^{i}(s_{j})=\begin{cases}s_{j}&j<i\\
*&j=i\\
s_{j-1}&j>i.\end{cases}

It remains to note that any morphism
\phi
is determined by the
images
\phi(s_{j})
.
∎

We denote by
\Sigma
the
category of connected strings
with
objects given by the strings
\Sigma_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Any morphism can be written in some canonical form. First observe that
there is a canonical epi-mono factorisation.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\phi\colon\Sigma_{m}\to\Sigma_{n}
be a morphisms. Let

0\leq j_{v}<\cdots<j_{0}<m
be the indices
j
such that

\phi(s_{j})=*
and set
\phi^{\prime}=\sigma_{m-v}^{j_{v}}\circ\cdots\circ\sigma_{m-1}^{j_{1}}\circ\sigma_{m}^{j_{0}}
. Then there is a
factorisation
\phi=\phi^{\prime\prime}\circ\phi^{\prime}
such that
\phi^{\prime\prime}
is injective.

Proof.

When
\phi(s_{i})=*
for some simple string
s_{i}
, then

\phi(s_{i}s)=\phi(s)=\phi(ss_{i})
for all
s\in\Sigma_{m}
. This yields the
factorisation
\phi=\phi^{\prime\prime}\circ\phi^{\prime}
. Given strings
s,t
such that

\phi^{\prime\prime}(s)=\phi^{\prime\prime}(t)
, an induction on their length shows that
s=t
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Every morphism
\phi\colon\Sigma_{m}\to\Sigma_{n}
can be written uniquely as
composite

(2.4)

\phi=\delta_{n}^{i_{u}}\circ\delta_{n-1}^{i_{u-1}}\circ\cdots\circ\delta_{n-u}^{i_{0}}\circ\sigma_{m-v}^{j_{v}}\circ\cdots\circ\sigma_{m-1}^{j_{1}}\circ\sigma_{m}^{j_{0}}

with
0\leq i_{0}<\cdots<i_{u}\leq n
,
0\leq j_{v}<\cdots<j_{0}<m
, and

n-u=m-v
.
1
1

1

We need to exclude
\delta_{1}^{0}
as a factor and
choose instead
\delta_{1}^{1}
in order to achieve uniqueness.

We call (
2.4
) the
canonical decomposition
of
\phi

in
\Sigma
.

Proof.

Let
0\leq j_{v}<\cdots<j_{0}<m
be the indices
j
such that

\phi(s_{j})=*
. And let
0\leq i_{0}<\cdots<i_{u}\leq n
be the
indices such that for all
(s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime})\in\Sigma_{n}
in the image of
\phi
we
have
s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}+1\not\in\{i_{0},\ldots,i_{u}\}
. Then it is clear that
\phi

satisfies (
2.4
). Conversely, if
\phi
is written as in
(
2.4
), then the indices
i_{0},\cdots,i_{u}
and

j_{0},\cdots,j_{v}
are characterised as above.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

The category
\Sigma
is generated by the objects
\Sigma_{n}
, the
morphisms
\delta_{n}^{i}
,
\sigma_{m}^{j}
, plus the relations
\delta_{1}^{0}=\delta_{1}^{1}
and
(
2.2
).

Proof.

Let us denote by
\Sigma^{\prime}
the category generated by the objects

\Sigma_{n}
, the morphisms
\delta_{n}^{i}
,
\sigma_{m}^{j}
, plus the relations

\delta_{1}^{0}=\delta_{1}^{1}
and (
2.2
). Since the relations are
satisfied in
\Sigma
, there is a unique functor
\Sigma^{\prime}\to\Sigma
which
induces the identity on the objects and on the morphisms
\delta_{n}^{i}

and
\sigma_{m}^{j}
. Since every morphism in
\Sigma
is a composite of
morphisms
\delta_{n}^{i}
and
\sigma_{m}^{j}
, the induced map

\operatorname{Hom}_{\Sigma^{\prime}}(\Sigma_{m},\Sigma_{n})\to\operatorname{Hom}_{\Sigma}(\Sigma_{m},\Sigma_{n})
is surjective
for all
m,n
. To show injectivity, choose
\phi,\psi
in

\operatorname{Hom}_{\Sigma^{\prime}}(\Sigma_{m},\Sigma_{n})
with same image in
\Sigma
. Since the
relations
\delta_{1}^{0}=\delta_{1}^{1}
and (
2.2
) in
\Sigma^{\prime}
are satisfied, there are
decompositions (
2.4
) in
\Sigma^{\prime}
for
\phi
and

\psi
. These decompositions coincide in
\Sigma
by
Lemma
2.3
, and therefore
\phi=\psi
.
∎

3.
The simplicial category

Let
\Delta
denote the
simplicial category
, which is also known
as
augmented simplex category
(terminology and notation follows

[
17
, VII.5]
). The objects are given by the finite ordinals

[n]=\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and the morphisms

\phi\colon[m]\to[n]
are given by maps satisfying
\phi(i)\leq\phi(j)
for
all
0\leq i\leq j<m
. For
n\geq 0
there is the
face map

\bar{\delta}^{i}_{n}\colon[n]\longrightarrow[n+1]\qquad(0\leq i\leq n)

(the unique injective map not taking the value
i
) and for
n\geq 1

the
degeneracy map

\bar{\sigma}^{i}_{n}\colon[n+1]\longrightarrow[n]\qquad(0\leq i<n)

(the unique surjective map taking twice the value
i
) which are
known to satisfy the simplicial identities (
2.2
). In fact, the category

\Delta
is generated by the objects
[n]
, the morphisms
\bar{\delta}_{n}^{i}
,

\bar{\sigma}_{m}^{j}
, and the identities (
2.2
); see
[
9
, II.2]

or
[
17
, VII.5]
.

We write
\bar{\Delta}=\Delta[\alpha^{-1}]
for the category which is obtained
by formally inverting the morphism
\alpha=\bar{\delta}_{0}^{0}
. This amounts to identifying the initial and
the terminal object in
\Delta
.

Proposition 3.1
.

The assignments

[n]\mapsto\begin{cases}\Sigma_{0}&n=0\\
\Sigma_{n-1}&n>0,\end{cases}\qquad\bar{\delta}_{n}^{i}\mapsto\delta_{n}^{i},\qquad\bar{\delta}_{0}^{0}\mapsto\operatorname{id},\qquad\bar{\sigma}_{n}^{i}\mapsto\sigma_{n}^{i}

provide a functor
p\colon\Delta\to\Sigma
which induces an
equivalence
\bar{\Delta}\xrightarrow{\raisebox{-1.20552pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle{\sim}$}}\Sigma
.

Proof.

The functor
p
is well defined since it maps
generators to generators and the simplicial identities are satisfied
in both categories. The functor
p
inverts
\bar{\delta}_{0}^{0}
and induces
therefore a functor
\bar{\Delta}\to\Sigma
, which yields a bijection
between the isomorphism classes of objects. Also for the morphisms
we obtain bijections since the functor matches generators and
relations. Note that
\bar{\delta}_{1}^{0}=\bar{\delta}_{1}^{1}
in
\bar{\Delta}
since

\bar{\delta}_{0}^{0}
is invertible.
∎

The functor
p\colon\Delta\to\Sigma
admits two sections
s_{0}
and
s_{1}

that are given by

\Sigma_{n}\mapsto[n+1],\qquad\delta_{n}^{i}\mapsto\bar{\delta}_{n}^{i},\qquad\sigma_{n}^{i}\mapsto\bar{\sigma}_{n}^{i},

except that

s_{i}(\delta_{1}^{0})=\bar{\delta}_{1}^{i}=s_{i}(\delta_{1}^{1})
for
i=0,1
.

4.
Representations

The category of finite strings models certain categories of linear
representations. In the following we specify the relevant class of
abelian categories and the exact functors between them.

Let
P
be a poset and
k
a field. A
k
-linear
representation of
P
is by definition a functor
P\to\operatorname{mod}k
into the
category of finite dimensional
k
-spaces, where
P
is viewed as a category (with
objects the elements in
P
and a unique morphism
x\to y
iff

x\leq y
). Morphisms between representations are the natural
transformations, and we denote by
\operatorname{Rep}(P,k)
the category of all
finite dimensional
k
-linear representations.

For a

We wish to describe the subobjects of
\Sigma_{n}
in the category of
finite strings. This requires some preparations.

Let

. We call

if
*\in S
and for any

of non-zero strings we have
st\in S
, and moreover

(s^{\prime},t^{\prime}-1),(t^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}),(s^{\prime\prime}+1,t^{\prime\prime})\in S
provided that

s^{\prime}\leq t^{\prime}\leq s^{\prime\prime}\leq t^{\prime\prime}
. We denote by
\operatorname{Thick}(S)
the smallest thick

\Sigma_{n}
containing
S

A set

of non-zero strings
is called

provided that
s,t\in S
and

s^{\prime}\leq t^{\prime}\leq s^{\prime\prime}\leq t^{\prime\prime}

.

Lemma 6.1
.

The assignment

gives a bijection between the
non-crossing subsets and the thick subsets of
\Sigma_{n}
.

Proof.

The inverse map takes a thick subset
T\subseteq\Sigma_{n}
to the unique
non-crossing subset
S\subseteq T
with
\operatorname{Thick}(S)=T
.
∎

For non-crossing subsets

of
\Sigma_{n}
we set

S\leq S^{\prime}\quad:\iff\quad\operatorname{Thick}(S)\subseteq\operatorname{Thick}(S^{\prime}).

This yields
the structure of a poset. In fact, the non-crossing subsets form a
lattice since the thick subsets of
\Sigma_{n}
are closed under
intersections. We denote this lattice by
\operatorname{NC}(\Sigma_{n})
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. A
partition

P=(P_{\alpha})
of
[n]
is given by
pairwise disjoint non-empty subsets
P_{\alpha}
of
[n]
such that

\bigcup_{\alpha}P_{\alpha}=[n]
. Each partition is determined by the
corresponding set of strings

, where by

if for some

we have

and

for all

. This is
clear since any part

is determined by
the corresponding set of strings

S_{\alpha}=\{(a_{1},a_{2}-1),\ldots,(a_{r-1},a_{r}-1)\}
.

Call a subset

of non-zero strings

when for any

we have

such that

. This yields a bijective correspondence between partitions

to

.

This result could be deduced from

Proof.

The assignment

gives a well defined map from the

7.
Cyclic strings

We enlarge the category of finite strings and add cyclic strings as
follows. Let
\Sigma_{\mathbb{Z}}
denote the set of all basic strings. There is
a natural action of the group of integers given by

*^{z}:=*\qquad\text{and}\qquad s^{z}:=(s^{\prime}+z,s^{\prime\prime}+z)\qquad\text{for }s=(s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}),\,z\in\mathbb{Z}.

For
n>0
the
cyclic string
of length
n
is the set of orbits
with respect to the action of the subgroup
(n)=n\mathbb{Z}
. Thus

\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}:=\{s^{(n)}\mid s\in\Sigma_{\mathbb{Z}}\}=\{s^{ni}\mid s\in\Sigma_{\mathbb{Z}},\,i\in\mathbb{Z}\}

with multiplication given by

s^{(n)}t^{(n)}:=u^{(n)}

where
u=*
except when there is a pair of integers
i,j
such that

s^{ni}t^{nj}=u\neq*
. We set
\tilde{\Sigma}_{0}:=\{*\}
.

A morphism
\phi\colon\tilde{\Sigma}_{m}\to\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}
is by definition a map
satisfying (
2.1
). We define
standard morphisms
which
are given by their values on simple strings as in (
2.3
).
Let
n\geq 1
. Then the morphism

\tilde{\delta}^{i}_{n}\colon\tilde{\Sigma}_{n-1}\longrightarrow\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}\qquad(0\leq i<n)

is given by the injective map such that
s^{(n)}_{i-1}
and

s^{(n)}_{i}
are not in its image, and the
morphism

\tilde{\sigma}^{i}_{n}\colon\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}\longrightarrow\tilde{\Sigma}_{n-1}\qquad(0\leq i<n)

is given by the surjective map such that
s^{(n)}_{i}
is sent
to the zero string. The
cyclic
permutation

\tau^{i}_{n}\colon\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}\longrightarrow\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}\qquad(0\leq i<n)

is given by

s^{(n)}\mapsto(s^{i})^{(n)}
.

Lemma 7.1
.

The standard morphisms satisfy the identities (
2.2
), and
every morphism
\phi\colon\tilde{\Sigma}_{m}\to\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}
admits a unique

\phi=\delta_{n}^{i_{u}}\circ\delta_{n-1}^{i_{u-1}}\circ\cdots\circ\delta_{n-u}^{i_{0}}\circ\sigma_{m-v}^{j_{v}}\circ\cdots\circ\sigma_{m-1}^{j_{1}}\circ\sigma_{m}^{j_{0}}\circ\tau_{m}^{k}

Proof.

Adapt the proof of Lemmas
2.1

∎

is not in its image. We consider the morphism

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tilde{\sigma}_{2}^{1}}

Lemma 7.2
.

Let

First consider a morphism

. As before,

Theorem 7.4
.

Let
k
be a field. The assignments
\Sigma_{n}\mapsto\mathcal{A}_{n}
and

provide an equivalence between the

-linear abelian

Proof.

We adapt the proof of Theorem

. Any

\mathcal{A}_{n}
or
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
, respectively, by

and

and

8.
Non-crossing partitions of type
B

We wish to describe the subobjects of
\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}
in the enlarged category of
finite strings. This description is parallel to that for
\Sigma_{n}
and
involves the non-crossing partitions of type
B
.

Let
S\subseteq\Sigma_{\mathbb{Z}}
. We call
S

thick
if
*\in S
and for any
pair
s,t\in S
of non-zero strings we have
st\in S
, and moreover

(s^{\prime},t^{\prime}-1),(t^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}),(s^{\prime\prime}+1,t^{\prime\prime})\in S
provided that

s^{\prime}\leq t^{\prime}\leq s^{\prime\prime}\leq t^{\prime\prime}
. We denote by
\operatorname{Thick}(S)
the smallest thick
subset of
\Sigma_{\mathbb{Z}}
containing
S
.

A set
S\subseteq\Sigma_{\mathbb{Z}}
of non-zero strings
is called
non-crossing
provided that
s,t\in S
and

s^{\prime}\leq t^{\prime}\leq s^{\prime\prime}\leq t^{\prime\prime}
implies
s=t
.

For
n>0
consider the canonical projection

p\colon\Sigma_{\mathbb{Z}}\to\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}
. Then a subset

S\subseteq\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}
is
thick
if
p^{-1}(S)
is thick,
and
S
is
non-crossing
if
p^{-1}(S)
is non-crossing. Note
that
\ell(s)\leq n
for any
s^{(n)}
when
S
is non-crossing.

Lemma 8.1
.

The assignment
S\mapsto\operatorname{Thick}(S)
gives a bijection between the
non-crossing subsets and the thick subsets of
\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}
.

Proof.

The inverse map takes a thick subset
T\subseteq\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}
to the unique
non-crossing subset
S\subseteq T
with
\operatorname{Thick}(S)=T
.
∎

For non-crossing subsets
S,S^{\prime}
of
\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}
we set

S\leq S^{\prime}\quad:\iff\quad\operatorname{Thick}(S)\subseteq\operatorname{Thick}(S^{\prime}).

This yields
the structure of a poset. In fact, the non-crossing subsets form a
lattice since the thick subsets of
\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}
are closed under
intersections. We denote this lattice by
\operatorname{NC}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{n})
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We consider the set

[2n]=\{0,1,\cdots,n-1,\bar{0},\bar{1},\cdots,\overline{n-1}\},

where

\bar{x}
is identified with
x+n
for
0\leq x<n
, and

\bar{\bar{x}}:=x
. For a partition
P=(P_{\alpha})
of
[2n]
we require
that
\overline{P_{\alpha}}
is a part of
P
for each
\alpha
. Each partition
is determined by the corresponding set of strings

S(P)\subseteq\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}
, where by definition

s^{(n)}\in\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}
with
0\leq s^{\prime}<n
belongs to
S(P)
if for
some
\alpha
we have
s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}\in P_{\alpha}
and
i\not\in P_{\alpha}
for all

s^{\prime}<i\leq s^{\prime\prime}
. The partitions of
[2n]
are partially ordered via
refinement, and the non-crossing partitions then form a lattice which
is denoted by
\operatorname{NC}^{B}(n)
; cf.
[
19
,
21
]
.

Lemma 8.2
.

There is a lattice isomorphism

\operatorname{NC}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{n})\xrightarrow{\raisebox{-1.20552pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle{\sim}$}}\operatorname{NC}^{B}(n)
which is given by
S\mapsto P(S)
.

Proof.

Adapt the proof of Lemma
6.2
.
∎

Remark 8.3
.

Let
P=(P_{\alpha})
be a non-crossing partition of
[2n]
and denote by
S=(S_{\alpha})

the corresponding partition of
S=S(P)
. Then
P
has at most one part
P_{\alpha}

satisfying
\overline{P_{\alpha}}=P_{\alpha}
. In fact,
\overline{P_{\alpha}}=P_{\alpha}
holds
if and only if
\operatorname{Thick}(S_{\alpha})
is infinite.

As before, we write
P(S)
for the partition of
[2n]
corresponding
to a non-crossing set
S\subseteq\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}
. For a monomorphism

\phi\colon X\to\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}
in the category of finite strings we set

S(\phi):=\{\phi(s)\mid s\in X\text{ simple}\}.

Theorem 8.4
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The subobjects of
\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}
in the enlarged
category of finite strings form a lattice which is canonically
isomorphic to the lattice of non-crossing partitions

\operatorname{NC}^{B}(n)
. The isomorphism sends a monomorphism

\phi\colon X\to\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}
to
P(S(\phi))
.

Proof.

Adapt the proof of Theorem
6.6
.
∎

9.
Thick subcategories

Results about subobjects in categories of strings correspond to
statements about thick subcategories of abelian categories, because of
the correspondence from Theorem
7.4
.

Recall that a full subcategory of an abelian category is
thick

if it is closed under direct summands and the two out of three
property holds for any short exact sequence.

Lemma 9.1
.

Let
k
be a field and let
\mathcal{A}
be a
k
-linear abelian category
satisfying
(Ab2)–(Ab5)
. Then every thick subcategory of
\mathcal{A}

satisfies again
(Ab2)–(Ab5)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
be a thick subcategory. Then
\mathcal{C}
is closed
under images of morphisms in
\mathcal{C}
because
\mathcal{A}
is hereditary. It
follows that the category
\mathcal{C}
is abelian and again
hereditary. Also,
\mathcal{C}
is necessarily a length category. If

X\in\mathcal{C}
is simple, then
\operatorname{End}(X)
is isomorphic to
k[t]/(t^{p})
for
some
p\geq 1
, since
X
is indecomposable in
\mathcal{A}
. Schur’s lemma
then implies
p=1
. It remains to show that
\mathcal{C}
is a uniserial
category with finitely many simple objects. We may assume that
either
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{n}
or
\mathcal{A}=\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then a
representative set of simple objects in
\mathcal{C}
identifies with a
non-crossing subset
S
in
\Sigma_{n}
or
\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}
. The set
S
is
finite since the length of any string in
S
is bounded by
n
. Let

M_{s},M_{t}
be simple objects in
\mathcal{C}
corresponding to strings

s,t\in S
. Then
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(M_{t},M_{s})\neq 0
iff
st\neq*
. It is clear
that for each
s\in S
there is at most one
t\in S
with

st\neq*
, and dually there is at most one
r\in S
with

rs\neq*
. Then a criterion from
[
1
]
implies that
\mathcal{C}
is
uniserial.
∎

Now we can deduce classifications of thick subcategories from
Theorems
6.6
and
8.4
. The first part is due to
Ingalls and Thomas
[
13
]
and only included for completeness; the
second part seems to be new.

Corollary 9.2
.

Let
k
be a field and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(1)

There is a canonical isomorphism between the lattice of thick
subcategories of
\mathcal{A}_{n}
and the lattice
\operatorname{NC}(n+1)
.

(2)

There is a canonical isomorphism between the lattice of thick
subcategories of
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
and the lattice
\operatorname{NC}^{B}(n)
.

Proof.

We apply Lemma
9.1
. From the homological factorisation of
an exact functor it follows that each subobject of
\mathcal{A}_{n}
or

\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
is given by the inclusion of a thick subcategory. On
the other hand, all thick subcategories arise in this
way. Theorem
7.4
provides the correspondence with
subobjects of
\Sigma_{n}
and
\tilde{\Sigma}_{n}
, respectively. Then the
assertion follows for
\mathcal{A}_{n}
from Theorem
6.6
and for

\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
from Theorem
8.4
.
∎

Remark 9.3
.

(1) The classification of thick subcategories for abelian categories
of the form
\mathcal{A}_{n}
or
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
given by a field and

generalises to any connected hereditary and uniserial length

]
. For the module category of a tame hereditary algebra

subcategory of regular modules, provided it is not generated by an
exceptional sequence
[
5
]
. This yields a classification of
all thick subcategories, complementing the work in

[
11
,
12
,
13
]
.

(3) For an hereditary abelian category
\mathcal{A}
, thick subcategories of the
bounded derived category

correspond bijectively to thick
subcategories of
\mathcal{A}
via

\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mathcal{A})\supseteq\mathcal{C}\longmapsto\{H^{0}(X)\in\mathcal{A}\mid X\in\mathcal{C}\}\subseteq\mathcal{A},

see
[

.

Appendix A
Basic strings

The properties of basic strings and the connection with linear representations become
more transparent if we consider analogues of face and degeneracy maps for the poset
of integers. We view this poset as a category, and this
means that morphisms
\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}
are viewed as functors. For
i\in\mathbb{Z}

we define morphisms

\delta^{i}\colon\mathbb{Z}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z},\qquad j\mapsto\begin{cases}j&j<i\\
j+1&j\geq i\end{cases}

and

\sigma^{i}\colon\mathbb{Z}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z},\qquad j\mapsto\begin{cases}j&j\leq i\\
j-1&j>i.\end{cases}

These satisfy the simplicial identities
(
2.2
). Moreover, they are related via adjunctions:

\cdots\dashv\delta^{i+1}\dashv\sigma^{i}\dashv\delta^{i}\dashv\sigma^{i-1}\dashv\cdots

Let
k
be a field. Then for each
i\in\mathbb{Z}
precomposition with
\delta^{i}
and

\sigma^{i}
yields exact functors

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Rep}(\mathbb{Z}^{\mathrm{op}},k)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Rep}(\mathbb{Z}^{\mathrm{op}},k).}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\delta^{i})^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\sigma^{i})^{*}}

This assignment is contravariant and therefore reverses the directions of
functors. Thus the dual simplicial identities but the same adjunctions

\cdots\dashv(\delta^{i+1})^{*}\dashv(\sigma^{i})^{*}\dashv(\delta^{i})^{*}\dashv(\sigma^{i-1})^{*}\dashv\cdots

are satisfied.

For
i\in\mathbb{Z}
let
S_{i}
denote the simple representation concentrated
in
i
, that is,
S_{i}(j)=0
for all
j\neq i
. Let

n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then precomposition with the inclusion
[n]\to\mathbb{Z}
yields
an exact functor

\operatorname{Rep}(\mathbb{Z}^{\mathrm{op}},k)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Rep}([n]^{\mathrm{op}},k)=\mathcal{A}_{n}

which becomes an
equivalence when restricted to the full subcategory of objects in

\operatorname{Rep}(\mathbb{Z}^{\mathrm{op}},k)
with composition factors in

\{S_{0},\ldots,S_{n-1}\}
. Viewing this as an identification, the
functors
(\delta^{i})^{*}
and
(\sigma^{i-1})^{*}
restrict to exact functors

\mathcal{A}_{n}\xrightarrow{\ (\delta^{i})^{*}\ }\mathcal{A}_{n-1}\qquad(0\leq i<n)

and

\mathcal{A}_{n-1}\xrightarrow{\ (\sigma^{i-1})^{*}\ }\mathcal{A}_{n}\qquad(0\leq i\leq n).

Recall that
\Sigma_{\mathbb{Z}}
denotes the set of basic strings. Then

\Sigma_{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{*\}
identifies with the indecomposable objects of
finite length in
\operatorname{Rep}(\mathbb{Z}^{\mathrm{op}},k)
via
s\mapsto M_{s}
, as in
Lemma
4.2
. This identification yields maps

\Sigma_{\mathbb{Z}}\to\Sigma_{\mathbb{Z}}
which are induced by
(\delta^{i})^{*}
and
(\sigma^{i})^{*}
,
respectively. Restricting these maps for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
to the set

\Sigma_{n}
of basic strings with composition factors in

\{s_{0},\ldots,s_{n-1}\}
gives

\sigma_{n}^{i}=(\delta^{i})^{*}|_{\Sigma_{n}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\delta_{n}^{i}=(\sigma^{i-1})^{*}|_{\Sigma_{n-1}}.

Then the following elementary observation (reflecting a duality for
the simplicial category
\Delta
, cf.
[
18
, VIII.7]
) explains
the simplicial relations (and any further properties) for

\delta^{i}_{n}\colon\Sigma_{n-1}\to\Sigma_{n}
and
\sigma^{i}_{n}\colon\Sigma_{n}\to\Sigma_{n-1}
.

Lemma A.1
.

Consider symbols
(\delta^{i},\sigma^{i})
and
(d^{i},s^{i})
