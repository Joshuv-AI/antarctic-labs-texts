De Finetti’s control problem with a concave bound on the control rate
Félix Locas, Jean-François Renaud

Abstract
. We consider De Finetti’s control problem for absolutely continuous strategies with control rates bounded by a concave function and prove that a generalized mean-reverting strategy is optimal. In order to solve this problem, we need to deal with a nonlinear Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. Despite the level of generality of the bound imposed on the rate, an explicit expression for the value function is obtained up to the evaluation of two functions.This optimal control problem has those with control rates bounded by a constant and a linear function, respectively, as special cases.



1.
Introduction

Nowadays,
De Finetti’s control problem
refers to a family of stochastic optimal control problems concerned with the maximization of withdrawals made from a stochastic system. While it has interpretations in models of population dynamics and natural resources and in inventory models, it originates from the field of insurance mathematics. Usually, the performance function is the expected time-discounted value of all withdrawals made up to a first-passage stopping time. In the original insurance context, it is interpretated as follows: find the optimal way to pay out dividends, taken from the insurance surplus process, until ruin is declared. In this case, the performance function is the expectation of the total amount of discounted dividend payments made up to the time of ruin. Therefore, the difficulty consists in finding the optimal balance between paying out dividends as much (and as early) as possible while avoiding ruin to maintain those payments on the long run. Similar interpretations can be made in population dynamics (harvesting) and for natural resources extractions; see, e.g.,
[
3
,
10
]
. In any case, the overall objective is the identification of the optimal way to withdraw from the system (optimal strategy) and the derivation of an analytical expression for the optimal value function.

In this paper, we consider absolutely continuous strategies. Except for very recent contributions (see
[
1
,
4
,
14
]
), most of the literature has considered constant bounds on the control rates (see, e.g.,
[
5
,
11
,
8
]
). Following the direction originally taken by
[
3
]
and later on by
[
14
]
, we apply a much more general bound, namely a concave function applied to the current level of the system.

1.1.
Model and problem formulation

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbf{P})
be a filtered probability space. Fix
\mu,\sigma>0
. The state process
X=\left\{X_{t},t\geq 0\right\}
is given by

(1.1)

X_{t}=x+\mu t+\sigma W_{t},

where
x\in\mathbb{R}
and where
W=\left\{W_{t},t\geq 0\right\}
is an
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-adapted standard Brownian motion. For example, the process
X
can be interpreted as the (uncontrolled) density of a population or the (uncontrolled) surplus process of an insurance company. As alluded to above, we are interested in absolutely continuous control processes. More precisely, a control strategy
\pi
is characterized by a nonnegative and adapted control rate process
l^{\pi}=\left\{l^{\pi}_{t},t\geq 0\right\}
yielding the cumulative control process

L_{t}^{\pi}=\int_{0}^{t}l_{s}^{\pi}\mathrm{d}s.

Note that
L^{\pi}=\left\{L^{\pi}_{t},t\geq 0\right\}
is nondecreasing and such that
L^{\pi}_{0}=0
. The corresponding controlled process
U^{\pi}=\left\{U^{\pi}_{t},t\geq 0\right\}
is then given by

U_{t}^{\pi}=X_{t}-L_{t}^{\pi}.

In the above mentioned applications (e.g., harvesting and dividend payments), it makes sense to allow for higher rates when the underlying state process is far from its critical level and to allow for higher (relative) increase in this rate as early as possible. The situation is reminiscent of utility functions in economics. In this direction, let us fix an increasing and concave function
F\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
F(0)\geq 0
. For technical reasons, we assume
F
is a differentiable Lipschitz function. Finally, a strategy
\pi
is said to be admissible if its control rate is also such that

(1.2)

0\leq l_{t}^{\pi}\leq F(U_{t}^{\pi}),

for all
0\leq t\leq\tau_{0}^{\pi}
, where
\tau_{0}^{\pi}=\inf\left\{t>0\colon U_{t}^{\pi}<0\right\}
is the termination time. The termination level
0
is chosen only for simplicity. Let
\Pi^{F}
be the set of all admissible strategies.

From now, we will write
\mathbf{P}_{x}
for the probability measure associated with the starting point
x
and
\mathbf{E}_{x}
for the expectation with respect to
\mathbf{P}_{x}
. When
x=0
, we write
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{E}
.

Fix a time-preference parameter
q>0
and then define the value of a strategy
\pi\in\Pi^{F}
by

V_{\pi}(x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}^{\pi}}e^{-qt}l_{t}^{\pi}\mathrm{d}t\right],\quad x\geq 0.

Note that in our model, we have
V_{\pi}(0)=0
, for all
\pi\in\Pi^{F}
.

We want to find an optimal strategy, that is a strategy
\pi^{\ast}\in\Pi^{F}
such that, for all
x\geq 0
and for all
\pi\in\Pi^{F}
, we have
V_{\pi^{*}}(x)\geq V_{\pi}(x)
. We also want to compute the optimal value function given by

V(x)=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi^{F}}V_{\pi}(x),\quad x\geq 0.

Remark 1
.

The optimal value function and an optimal strategy (if it exists) should be independent of the behaviour of
F
when
x<0
. The Lipschitz condition assumption is a sufficient condition for the existence and the uniqueness of a (strong) solution to the stochastic differential equation (SDE) defined in (
2.3
); see, e.g.,
[
15
]
. In fact, any increasing differentiable concave function
F:(0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
such that
F(0)\geq 0
and
F^{\prime}(0+)<\infty
satisfy the conditions for our model: we can extend
F
as a Lipschitz continuous function by extending
F
, such that
F(x)=F^{\prime}(0+)x+F(0)
, for all
x\leq 0
.

1.2.
More related literature

De Finetti’s optimal control problems are adaptations and interpretations of Bruno de Finetti’s seminal work
[
9
]
. As they have been extensively studied in a variety of models, especially over the last 25 years, it is nearly impossible to provide a proper literature review on this topic in an introduction. See the review paper
[
2
]
for more details. Therefore, let us focus on the literature closer to our model and our problem.

In
[
11
,
5
]
, the problem for absolutely continuous strategies in a Brownian model is tackled under the following assumption on the control rate:

(1.3)

0\leq l_{t}^{\pi}\leq R,

where
R>0
is a given constant; see also
[
12
]
for the same problem in a Lévy model. For this problem, a threshold strategy is optimal: above the optimal barrier level, the maximal control rate
R
is applied, otherwise the system is left uncontrolled. In other words, a threshold strategy is characterized by a barrier level and the corresponding controlled process is a Brownian motion with a two-valued drift. Very recently, in
[
14
]
, the admissibility condition in (
1.3
) was replaced by the following one:

(1.4)

0\leq l_{t}^{\pi}\leq KU^{\pi}_{t},

where
K>0
is a given constant. In other words, the control rate is now bounded by a linear transformation of the current state. Note that this idea had already been considered in a biological context (but under a different model) in
[
3
]
. In
[
14
]
, it is proved that a mean-reverting strategy is optimal: above the optimal barrier level, the maximal control rate
KU^{\pi}_{t}
is applied, otherwise the system is left uncontrolled. Again, such a control strategy is characterized by a barrier level, but now the controlled process is a
refracted
diffusion process, switching between a Brownian motion with drift and an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process.

To the best of our knowledge, the concept of
mean-reverting strategy
first appeared in
[
6
]
. It was argued that, in an insurance context, this type of strategies has desirable properties for shareholders. Then, in
[
14
]
, the name
mean-reverting strategies
was used for a larger family of control strategies; in fact, the mean-reverting strategy in
[
6
]
is one member (when the barrier level is equal to zero) of this sub-family of strategies. As mentioned above, a mean-reverting strategy is optimal in the case
F(x)=Kx
. Following those lines, we will use the name
generalized mean-reverting strategy
for similar bang-bang strategies corresponding to the general case of an increasing and concave function
F
.

1.3.
Main results and outline of the paper

Obviously, the bounds imposed on the control rates in these last two problems, as given in (
1.3
) for the constant case and in (
1.4
) for the linear case, are special cases of the one considered in our problem and presented in (
1.2
). A solution to our general problem is given in Theorem
4.3
. As we will see, an optimal control strategy is provided by a generalized mean-reverting strategy, which is also characterized by a barrier level
b
. More specifically, for such a strategy, the optimal controlled process is given by the following diffusion process:

\mathrm{d}U_{t}^{b}=\left(\mu-F(U_{t}^{b}){\mathbf{1}}_{\left\{U_{t}^{b}>b\right\}}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

We use a guess-and-verify approach: first, we compute the value of any generalized mean-reverting strategy (Proposition
3.1
) using a Markovian decomposition and a perturbation approach; second, we find the optimal barrier level (Proposition
4.1
); finally, using a Verification Lemma (Lemma
2.3
), we prove that this
best
mean-reverting strategy is in fact an optimal strategy for our problem. Despite the level of generality (of
F
), our solution for this control problem is explicit up to the evaluation of two
special functions
strongly depending on
F
:
\varphi_{F}
, the solution to an ordinary differential equation, and
I_{F}
, an expectation functional. While
\varphi_{F}
is one of the two well-known fundamental solutions associated to a diffusion process (also the solution to a first-passage problem), the definition of
I_{F}
(see Equation (
2.4
)) is an intricate expectation for which many properties can be obtained (see Proposition
2.2
).

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we provide important preliminary results on the functions at the core of our main result and we state a Verification Lemma for the maximization problem. In Section 3, we introduce the family of generalized mean-reverting strategies and compute their value functions. In Section 4, we state and prove the main result, which is a solution to the control problem, before looking at specific examples. A proof of the Verification Lemma is provided in the Appendix.

2.
Preliminary results

First, let
G\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be an increasing and differentiable function. Consider the homogeneous ordinary differential equation (ODE)

(2.1)

\Gamma_{G}(f):=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(x)+(\mu-G(x))f^{\prime}(x)-qf(x)=0,\quad x>0,

and denote by
\psi_{G}
(resp.
\varphi_{G}
) a positive increasing (resp. decreasing) solution to (
2.1
), with
\psi_{G}(0)=\varphi_{G}(+\infty)=0
. Under the additional conditions that
\varphi_{G}(0)=1
and
\psi_{G}^{\prime}(0)=1
, it is known that
\psi_{G}
and
\varphi_{G}
are uniquely determined. It is also known that
\psi_{G},\varphi_{G}\in C^{3}[0,\infty)
.

We will write
\Gamma
,
\psi
and
\varphi
when
G\equiv 0
. In particular, it is easy to verify that, for
x\geq 0
,

(2.2)

\psi(x)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sqrt{\mu^{2}+2q\sigma^{2}}}\mathrm{e}^{-(\mu/\sigma^{2})x}\sinh\left((x/\sigma^{2})\sqrt{\mu^{2}+2q\sigma^{2}}\right).

In what follows, we will not manipulate this explicit expression for
\psi
. Instead, we will use its analytical properties (see Lemma
2.1
below).

Remark 2
.

The expression for
\psi
is proportional to the expression for
W^{(q)}
, the
q
-scale function of the Brownian motion with drift
X
, used in
[
14
]
. For more details, see
[
13
]
.

The next lemma gives analytical properties of the functions
\psi
and
\varphi_{G}
. See
[
10
]
for a complete proof.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
G\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be an increasing and differentiable function. The functions
\psi
and
\varphi_{G}
have the following analytical properties:

(a)

\psi
is strictly increasing and strictly concave-convex with a unique inflection point
\hat{b}\in(0,\infty)
;

(b)

\varphi_{G}
is strictly decreasing and strictly convex.

The value of the inflection point of
\psi
is known explicitly; see, e.g., Equation (4) in
[
14
]
. Our analysis does not depend on this specific value.

Recall from (
1.1
) that
X_{t}=x+\mu t+\sigma W_{t}
. Define
U=\left\{U_{t},t\geq 0\right\}
by

(2.3)

\mathrm{d}U_{t}=(\mu-F(U_{t}))\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

Under our assumptions on
F
, there exists a unique strong solution to this last stochastic differential equation. This is what we called a
nonlinear Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process
.

Now, for
a\geq 0
, define the following first-passage stopping times:

\tau_{a}=\inf\left\{t>0\colon X_{t}=a\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\tau^{F}_{a}=\inf\left\{t>0\colon U_{t}=a\right\}.

It is well known that, for
0\leq x\leq b
, we have

{\mathbf{E}_{x}}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{b}}{\mathbf{1}}_{\left\{\tau_{b}<\tau_{0}\right\}}\right]=\frac{\psi(x)}{\psi(b)},

and, for
x\geq 0
, we have

{\mathbf{E}_{x}}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau^{F}_{0}}{\mathbf{1}}_{\left\{\tau^{F}_{0}<\infty\right\}}\right]=\varphi_{F}(x),

since it is assumed
\varphi_{F}(0)=1
.

Finally, define

(2.4)

I_{F}(x)={\mathbf{E}_{x}}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}F(U_{t})\mathrm{d}t\right],\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

As alluded to above, the analysis of this functional is of paramount importance for the solution of our control problem. The main difficulty in computing this functional lies in the fact that the dynamics of
U
also depend on
F
.

Proposition 2.2
.

The function
I_{F}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a twice continuously differentiable, increasing and concave solution to the following ODE:

(2.5)

\Gamma_{F}(f)=-F,\quad x>0.

Moreover, we have
0\leq I_{F}^{\prime}(x)\leq 1
, for all
x\geq 0
.

Proof.

Under our assumptions on
F
, it is known that there exists a twice continuously differentiable solution
f
to (
2.5
). Applying Ito’s Lemma to
\mathrm{e}^{-qt}f(U_{t})
and taking expectations, we obtain

\mathbf{E}_{x}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-qt}f(U_{t})\right]=f(x)-{\mathbf{E}_{x}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{-qs}F(U_{s})\mathrm{d}s\right].

Letting
t\to\infty
, we get
f(x)=I_{F}(x)
.

In the rest of the proof, we will use the notation
U^{x}
for the solution to

(2.6)

\mathrm{d}U_{t}^{x}=(\mu-F(U_{t}^{x}))\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad U_{0}^{x}=x.

This is the dynamics given in (
2.3
).

Fix
x<y
. Note that
U_{0}^{x}<U_{0}^{y}
and define
\kappa=\inf\left\{t>0\colon U_{t}^{x}=U_{t}^{y}\right\}
. Since
U_{t}^{x}<U_{t}^{y}
for all
0\leq t\leq\kappa
and
F
is increasing, we have

\displaystyle I_{F}(y)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}F(U_{t}^{y})\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\kappa}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}F(U_{t}^{y})\mathrm{d}t\right]+\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{\kappa}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}F(U_{t}^{y})\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\kappa}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}F(U_{t}^{x})\mathrm{d}t\right]+\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{\kappa}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}F(U_{t}^{x})\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle=I_{F}(x),

where we used the fact that, for
t\geq\kappa
, we have
U_{t}^{x}=U_{t}^{y}
. This proves that
I_{F}
is increasing.

Now, fix
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
. Define
z=\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y
and

Y_{t}^{z}=\lambda U_{t}^{x}+(1-\lambda)U_{t}^{y}.

By linearity, we have

\mathrm{d}Y_{t}^{z}=(\mu-l_{t}^{Y})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad Y_{0}^{z}=z,

where
l_{t}^{Y}:=\lambda F(U_{t}^{x})+(1-\lambda)F(U_{t}^{y})
. Since
F
is concave, we have that
l_{t}^{Y}\leq F(Y_{t}^{z})
for all
t\geq 0
. We want to prove that, almost surely, we have
Y_{t}^{z}\geq U_{t}^{z}
for all
t\geq 0
.
First, for all
t\geq 0
, we have

(2.7)

Y_{t}^{z}-U_{t}^{z}=\int_{0}^{t}\left(F(U_{s}^{z})-l_{s}^{Y}\right)\mathrm{d}s.

Note that, by the concavity of
F
, since
U_{0}^{z}=Y_{0}^{z}=z
, we have
F(U_{0}^{z})\geq l_{0}^{Y}
. Now, define

\tau=\inf\left\{t>0\colon F(U_{t}^{z})>l_{t}^{Y}\right\}.

On
\left\{\tau=+\infty\right\}
, we have
Y_{t}^{z}=U_{t}^{z}
for all
t
. On
\left\{\tau<\infty\right\}
, since the mapping
s\mapsto F(U_{s}^{z})-l_{s}^{Y}
is continuous almost surely, it follows from (
2.7
) that there exists
\epsilon>0
for which
Y_{t}^{z}>U_{t}^{z}
for all
t\in]\tau,\tau+\epsilon[
. But we can apply the same argument for another time point
s>\tau
: if there exists
s>\tau
for which
Y_{s}^{z}=U_{s}^{z}
, then either we have
Y_{t}^{z}=U_{t}^{z}
for all
t>s
, or there exists
s^{\prime},\epsilon^{\prime}>0
, for which we have

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}Y_{t}^{z}=U_{t}^{z}&\mbox{if }s\leq t\leq s^{\prime},\\
Y_{t}^{z}>U_{t}^{z}&\mbox{if }s^{\prime}<t<s^{\prime}+\epsilon^{\prime}.\end{array}\right.

This proves that, for all
t\geq 0
, we have
Y_{t}^{z}\geq U_{t}^{z}
, which is equivalent to

\int_{0}^{t}F(U_{s}^{z})\mathrm{d}s\geq\int_{0}^{t}\left(\lambda F(U_{s}^{x})+(1-\lambda)F(U_{s}^{y})\right)\mathrm{d}s.

Since this is true for all
t\geq 0
, we further have that, for all
t\geq 0
,

(2.8)

\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{-qs}F(U_{s}^{z})\mathrm{d}s\geq\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{-qs}\left(\lambda F(U_{s}^{x})+(1-\lambda)F(U_{s}^{y})\right)\mathrm{d}s.

Letting
t\to\infty
, it follows that

I_{F}(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)\geq\lambda I_{F}(x)+(1-\lambda)I_{F}(y),

proving the concavity of
I_{F}
.

Let
x,h\geq 0
and define

\kappa^{h}=\inf\left\{t>0:U_{t}^{x+h}=U_{t}^{x}\right\}.

We can write

I_{F}(x+h)-I_{F}(x)=\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\left(F(U_{t}^{x+h})-F(U_{t}^{x})\right)\mathrm{d}t\right]\\
=\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\kappa^{h}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\left(F(U_{t}^{x+h})-F(U_{t}^{x})\right)\mathrm{d}t\right]+\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{\kappa^{h}}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\left(F(U_{t}^{x+h})-F(U_{t}^{x})\right)\mathrm{d}t\right].

If
\kappa^{h}
is finite, the second expectation is zero, because for all
t\geq\kappa^{h}
and for all
\omega\in\Omega
, we have
U_{t}^{x+h}(\omega)=U_{t}^{x}(\omega)
. Now, on
\left\{\kappa^{h}<\infty\right\}
, we have

\int_{0}^{\kappa^{h}}\left(F(U_{s}^{x+h})-F(U_{s}^{x})\right)\mathrm{d}s=h,

which implies that

\int_{0}^{\kappa^{h}}\mathrm{e}^{-qs}\left(F(U_{s}^{x+h})-F(U_{s}^{x})\right)\mathrm{d}s\leq h.

On
\left\{\kappa^{h}=\infty\right\}
, we have

\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{-qs}\left(F(U_{s}^{x+h})-F(U_{s}^{x})\right)\mathrm{d}s<h,

for all
t\geq 0
, which implies that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-qs}\left(F(U_{s}^{x+h})-F(U_{s}^{x})\right)\mathrm{d}s\leq h.

In conclusion, we have

\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\kappa^{h}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\left(F(U_{t}^{x+h})-F(U_{t}^{x})\right)\mathrm{d}t\right]\leq h.

Letting
h\to 0
, we find that
I_{F}^{\prime}(x)\leq 1
.
∎

Finally, here is a Verification Lemma for the stochastic control problem. The proof is given in Appendix A.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\pi^{*}\in\Pi^{F}
be such that
V_{\pi^{*}}\in C^{2}(0,\infty)
,
V_{\pi^{*}}^{\prime}
is bounded and, for all
x>0
,

(2.9)

\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}V_{\pi^{*}}^{\prime\prime}(x)+\mu V_{\pi^{*}}^{\prime}(x)-qV_{\pi^{*}}(x)+\sup_{0\leq u\leq F(x)}u(1-V_{\pi^{*}}^{\prime}(x))=0.

Then,
\pi^{*}
is an optimal strategy. In this case,
V\in C^{2}(0,+\infty)
,
V^{\prime}
is bounded and
V
satisfies (
2.9
).

3.
Generalized mean-reverting strategies

Since the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation in (
2.9
) is linear with respect to the control variable, we expect a bang-bang strategy to be optimal. Further, since from modelling reasons we expect the optimal value fonction
V
to be concave, then an optimal strategy must be of the form

(3.1)

l_{s}^{\pi}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}F(U_{s}^{\pi})&\mbox{if }U_{s}^{\pi}>b,\\
0&\mbox{if }U_{s}^{\pi}<b,\end{array}\right.

for some
b\geq 0
to be determined.

Consequently, and following the line of reasoning in
[
14
]
, let us define the family of
generalized mean-reverting strategies
. For a fixed
b\geq 0
, define the generalized mean-reverting strategy
\pi_{b}
and the corresponding controlled process
U^{b}:=U^{\pi_{b}}
by

(3.2)

\mathrm{d}U_{t}^{b}=\left(\mu-F(U_{t}^{b}){\mathbf{1}}_{\left\{U_{t}^{b}>b\right\}}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

In other words, the control rate
l^{b}:=l^{\pi_{b}}
associated to
\pi_{b}
is given by

l^{b}_{t}=F(U_{t}^{b}){\mathbf{1}}_{\left\{U_{t}^{b}>b\right\}}.

Similarly, we define the corresponding value function by
V_{b}:=V_{\pi_{b}}
.

Note that, if
b=0
, then
U^{0}=U
, with
U
already defined in (
2.3
).

Remark 3
.

As discussed in Remark
1
, there exists a unique strong solution to the SDE given in (
2.3
). When
b>0
, the drift function is not necessarily continuous, but it can be shown, using for example the same steps as in
[
14
]
, that a strong solution exists for (
3.2
).

The next proposition gives the value function of a generalized mean-reverting strategy.

Proposition 3.1
.

The value function
V_{0}
of the generalized mean-reverting strategy
\pi_{0}
is continuously differentiable and given by

(3.3)

V_{0}(x)=I_{F}(x)-I_{F}(0)\varphi_{F}(x),\quad x\geq 0.

If
b>0
, then the value function
V_{b}
of the generalized mean-reverting strategy
\pi_{b}
is continuously differentiable and given by

(3.4)

V_{b}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}C_{1}(b)\psi(x)&\mbox{if }0\leq x\leq b,\\
I_{F}(x)+C_{2}(b)\varphi_{F}(x)&\mbox{if }x\geq b,\end{array}\right.

where

\displaystyle C_{1}(b)

\displaystyle=\frac{I_{F}^{\prime}(b)\varphi_{F}(b)-I_{F}(b)\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(b)}{\psi^{\prime}(b)\varphi_{F}(b)-\psi(b)\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(b)},

\displaystyle C_{2}(b)

\displaystyle=\frac{I_{F}^{\prime}(b)\psi(b)-I_{F}(b)\psi^{\prime}(b)}{\psi^{\prime}(b)\varphi_{F}(b)-\psi(b)\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(b)}.

Proof.

The proof follows the same steps as the one for Proposition 2.1 in
[
14
]
.

Fix
b>0
. Using the strong Markov property, we have, for
x\leq b
,

V_{b}(x)={\mathbf{E}_{x}}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{b}}{\mathbf{1}}_{\left\{\tau_{b}<\tau_{0}\right\}}\right]V_{b}(b).

Using the strong Markov property again, we get, for all
x>b
,

V_{b}(x)={\mathbf{E}_{x}}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}F(U_{t})\mathrm{d}t\right]+{\mathbf{E}_{x}}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau^{F}_{0}}{\mathbf{1}}_{\left\{\tau^{F}_{0}<\infty\right\}}\right]\left(\frac{V_{b}(b)-{\mathbf{E}_{b}}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}F(U_{t})\mathrm{d}t\right]}{{\mathbf{E}_{b}}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau^{F}_{0}}{\mathbf{1}}_{\left\{\tau^{F}_{0}<\infty\right\}}\right]}\right).

Consequently, we can write

(3.5)

V_{b}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{\psi(x)}{\psi(b)}V_{b}(b)&\mbox{if }0\leq x\leq b,\\
I_{F}(x)+\frac{\varphi_{F}(x)}{\varphi_{F}(b)}(V_{b}(b)-I_{F}(b))&\mbox{if }x\geq b.\end{array}\right.

To conclude, we need to compute
V_{b}(b)
. For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
sufficiently large, consider the strategy
\pi_{b}^{n}
consisting of using the maximal control rate
F(U_{t}^{\pi_{b}^{n}})
when the controlled process is above
b
, until it goes below
b-1/n
. We apply again the maximal control rate when the controlled process reaches
b
again. Note that
\pi_{b}^{n}
is admissible. We denote its value function by
V_{b}^{n}
. We can show that:

\lim_{n\to\infty}V_{b}^{n}(b)=V_{b}(b).

Using similar arguments as above, we can write

V_{b}^{n}(b-1/n)=\psi_{b}(b-1/n)V_{b}^{n}(b)

and

\displaystyle V_{b}^{n}(b)

\displaystyle={\mathbf{E}_{b}}{\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{b-1/n}^{F}}e^{-qt}F(U_{t}^{\pi_{b}^{n}})\mathrm{d}t\right]}+{\mathbf{E}_{b}}{\left[e^{-q\tau_{b-1/n}^{F}}{\mathbf{1}}_{\left\{\tau_{b-1/n}^{F}<\infty\right\}}\right]}V_{b}^{n}(b-1/n)

\displaystyle=I_{F}(b)+\frac{\varphi_{F}(b)}{\varphi_{F}(b-1/n)}\left(V_{b}^{n}(b-1/n)-I_{F}(b-1/n)\right).

Solving for
V_{b}^{n}(b)
, we find

V_{b}^{n}(b)=\frac{I_{F}(b-1/n)\varphi_{F}(b)-I_{F}(b)\varphi_{F}(b-1/n)}{\psi_{b}(b-1/n)\varphi_{F}(b)-\psi_{b}(b)\varphi_{F}(b-1/n)},

where
\psi_{b}(x)=\psi(x)/\psi(b)
.
Define

G(y)=I_{F}(b-y)\varphi_{F}(b)-I_{F}(b)\varphi_{F}(b-y)

and

H(y)=\psi_{b}(b-y)\varphi_{F}(b)-\psi_{b}(b)\varphi_{F}(b-y).

Note that
G(0)=H(0)=0
. As
\psi_{b},\varphi_{F}
and
I_{F}
are differentiable functions, dividing the numerator and the denominator by
1/n
and taking the limit yields

V_{b}(b)=\lim_{n\to\infty}V_{b}^{n}(b)=\frac{G^{\prime}(0+)}{H^{\prime}(0+)},

which leads to

(3.6)

V_{b}(b)=\frac{I_{F}^{\prime}(b)\varphi_{F}(b)-I_{F}(b)\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(b)}{\psi_{b}^{\prime}(b)\varphi_{F}(b)-\psi_{b}(b)\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(b)}.

Substituting (
3.6
) into (
3.5
), we get:

(3.7)

V_{b}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}K_{1}(b)\psi_{b}(x)&\mbox{if }0\leq x\leq b,\\
I_{F}(x)+K_{2}(b)\varphi_{F}(x)&\mbox{if }x\geq b,\end{array}\right.

where

K_{1}(b)=\frac{I_{F}^{\prime}(b)\varphi_{F}(b)-I_{F}(b)\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(b)}{\psi_{b}^{\prime}(b)\varphi_{F}(b)-\psi_{b}(b)\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(b)}\quad\text{and}\quad K_{2}(b)=\frac{I_{F}^{\prime}(b)\psi_{b}(b)-I_{F}(b)\psi_{b}^{\prime}(b)}{\psi_{b}^{\prime}(b)\varphi_{F}(b)-\psi_{b}(b)\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(b)}.

Using the definition of
\psi_{b}
, (
3.7
) can be rewritten as follows:

V_{b}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}C_{1}(b)\psi(x)&\mbox{if }0\leq x\leq b,\\
I_{F}(x)+C_{2}(b)\varphi_{F}(x)&\mbox{if }x\geq b.\end{array}\right.

It is straightforward to check that
V_{b}\in C^{1}(0,+\infty)
, since elementary algebraic manipulations lead to
V_{b}^{\prime}(b-)=V_{b}^{\prime}(b+)
.

When
b=0
, using Markovian arguments as above, we can verify that

V_{0}(x)=I_{F}(x)-I_{F}(0)\varphi_{F}(x),\quad x\geq 0,

from which it is clear that
V_{0}\in C^{1}(0,+\infty)
.
∎

4.
Main results

We are now ready to provide a solution to the general control problem. As mentioned before, depending on the set of parameters, an optimal strategy will be given by the generalized mean-reverting strategy
\pi_{0}
or by a generalized mean-reverting strategy
\pi_{b^{\ast}}
, for a barrier level
b^{\ast}
to be determined.

First, let us consider the situation in which the parameters are such that

(4.1)

I_{F}^{\prime}(0)-I_{F}(0)\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(0)\leq 1.

Recalling from Proposition
3.1
, that

V_{0}(x)=I_{F}(x)-I_{F}(0)\varphi_{F}(x),\quad x\geq 0,

we deduce that
V_{0}^{\prime}(0)\leq 1
.

Also, we have that
V_{0}
is a concave function. Indeed, using the notation introduced in (
2.6
), we can write

V_{0}(x)=\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}^{x}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}F(U_{t}^{x})\mathrm{d}t\right],

where
\tau^{x}_{0}=\inf\left\{t>0\colon U^{x}_{t}=0\right\}
. Since the inequality in (
2.8
) holds for all
t\geq 0
, it holds true also for the stopping time
\tau^{x}_{0}
. As a consequence, we have that
V_{0}
is concave and we further have that
V_{0}^{\prime}(x)\leq 1
for all
x\geq 0
.

In conclusion, all the conditions of the Verification Lemma are satisfied and thus the generalized mean-reverting
\pi_{0}
is an optimal strategy.

Now, let us consider the situation in which the parameters are such that

(4.2)

I_{F}^{\prime}(0)-I_{F}(0)\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(0)>1.

From the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation (
2.9
), we expect an optimal barrier level
b^{*}
to be given by

V_{b^{*}}^{\prime}(b^{*})=1,

which is equivalent to

(4.3)

I_{F}(b^{*})-\frac{I_{F}^{\prime}(b^{*})\varphi_{F}(b^{*})}{\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(b^{*})}=\frac{\psi(b^{*})}{\psi^{\prime}(b^{*})}-\frac{\varphi_{F}(b^{*})}{\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(b^{*})}.

The next proposition is one of the most important results.

Proposition 4.1
.

If
I_{F}^{\prime}(0)-I_{F}(0)\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(0)>1
, then there exists a solution
b^{*}\in(0,\hat{b}]
to (
4.3
).

Proof.

Define

g(y)=I_{F}(y)-\frac{I_{F}^{\prime}(y)\varphi_{F}(y)}{\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(y)}

and

h(y)=\frac{\psi(y)}{\psi^{\prime}(y)}-\frac{\varphi_{F}(y)}{\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(y)}.

We see that
g(0)>h(0)
is equivalent to
I_{F}^{\prime}(0)-I_{F}(0)\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(0)>1
. We will show that
g(\hat{b})\leq h(\hat{b})
. The result will follow from the Intermediate Value Theorem. First, we have the following inequality:

(4.4)

\frac{\psi(\hat{b})}{\psi^{\prime}(\hat{b})}-\frac{\varphi_{F}(\hat{b})}{\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(\hat{b})}\geq\frac{F(\hat{b})}{q}.

Indeed, by definition of
\hat{b}
and
\psi
, we have
\frac{\psi(\hat{b})}{\psi^{\prime}(\hat{b})}=\frac{\mu}{q}
. Also, by definition of
\varphi_{F}
, we have

\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\frac{\varphi_{F}^{\prime\prime}(\hat{b})}{\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(\hat{b})}+\mu-q\frac{\varphi_{F}(\hat{b})}{\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(\hat{b})}-F(\hat{b})=0.

Since
\varphi_{F}
is convex and decreasing, (
4.4
) follows.

Now, using Proposition
2.2
, we can write

I_{F}(\hat{b})=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2q}I_{F}^{\prime\prime}(\hat{b})+\frac{\mu}{q}I_{F}^{\prime}(\hat{b})-\frac{F(\hat{b})}{q}\left(I_{F}^{\prime}(\hat{b})-1\right).

Since
I_{F}
is concave, it follows that

I_{F}(\hat{b})\leq\frac{\mu}{q}I_{F}^{\prime}(\hat{b})-\frac{F(\hat{b})}{q}\left(I_{F}^{\prime}(\hat{b})-1\right).

Also, since we have that
0\leq I_{F}^{\prime}(\hat{b})\leq 1
, using (
4.4
) yields

I_{F}(\hat{b})\leq\frac{\mu}{q}I_{F}^{\prime}(\hat{b})-\left(\frac{\psi(\hat{b})}{\psi^{\prime}(\hat{b})}-\frac{\varphi_{F}(\hat{b})}{\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(\hat{b})}\right)\left(I_{F}^{\prime}(\hat{b})-1\right).

This inequality is equivalent to
g(\hat{b})\leq h(\hat{b})
.
∎

The next result states that if
b^{*}
is a solution to (
4.3
), as in the previous proposition, then
\pi_{b^{*}}
satisfies the conditions of the Verification Lemma.

Proposition 4.2
.

If
I_{F}^{\prime}(0)-I_{F}(0)\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(0)>1
and if
b^{*}\in(0,\hat{b}]
is a solution to (
4.3
), then
V_{b^{*}}\in C^{2}(0,+\infty)
is concave.

Proof.

This proof relies heavily on the analytical properties of
\psi,\varphi_{F}
and
I_{F}
obtained in Lemma
2.1
and Proposition
2.2
.

First, let us show that
V_{b^{*}}\in C^{2}(0,+\infty)
. From Proposition
3.1
, we deduce that

V_{b^{*}}^{\prime\prime}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}C_{1}(b^{*})\psi^{\prime\prime}(x)&\mbox{if }0<x<b^{*},\\
I_{F}^{\prime\prime}(x)+C_{2}(b^{*})\varphi_{F}^{\prime\prime}(x)&\mbox{if }x>b^{*}.\end{array}\right.

Therefore, since
\psi
,
\varphi_{F}
and
I_{F}
are twice continuously differentiable functions, it is sufficient to show that
V_{b^{*}}^{\prime\prime}(b^{*}-)=V_{b^{*}}^{\prime\prime}(b^{*}+)
. We also have that
\psi
,
\varphi_{F}
and
I_{F}
are solutions to second-order ODEs, so it is equivalent to show that

\mu\left[C_{1}(b^{*})\psi^{\prime}(b^{*})-C_{2}(b^{*})\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(b^{*})-I_{F}^{\prime}(b^{*})\right]\\
-q\left[C_{1}(b^{*})\psi(b^{*})-C_{2}(b^{*})\varphi_{F}(b^{*})-I_{F}(b^{*})\right]\\
+F(b^{*})\left[C_{2}(b^{*})\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(b^{*})-I_{F}^{\prime}(b^{*})-1\right]=0.

The statement follows from the fact that
V_{b^{*}}
is continuously differentiable at
x=b^{*}
and because
V_{b^{*}}^{\prime}(b^{*}+)=1
.

Now, let us show that
V_{b^{*}}
is concave. Since
V_{b^{*}}^{\prime}(b^{*})=1
, it follows directly that

(4.5)

C_{1}(b^{*})=\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}(b^{*})}

and

(4.6)

C_{2}(b^{*})=\frac{1-I_{F}^{\prime}(b^{*})}{\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(b^{*})}.

Using the analytical properties of
\psi,\varphi_{F}
and
I_{F}
, it is clear that
C_{2}(b^{*})\leq 0<C_{1}(b^{*})
. Since
b^{*}\leq\hat{b}
, we have that
\psi
is concave on
(0,b^{*})
, and so
V_{b^{*}}^{\prime\prime}(x)\leq 0
, for all
x\in(0,b^{*})
. Finally, since
I_{F}
is concave,
\varphi_{F}
is convex, and
C_{2}(b^{*})\leq 0
, we have that
V_{b^{*}}^{\prime\prime}(x)\leq 0
, for all
x\in(b^{*},\infty)
. In other words,
V_{b^{*}}
is concave.
∎

We are now ready to state the main result, which is a solution to the general control problem.

Theorem 4.3
.

If
I_{F}^{\prime}(0)-I_{F}(0)\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(0)\leq 1
, then
\pi_{0}
is an optimal strategy and the optimal value function is given by

V(x)=I_{F}(x)-I_{F}(0)\varphi_{F}(x),\quad x\geq 0.

If
I_{F}^{\prime}(0)-I_{F}(0)\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(0)>1
, then
\pi_{b^{*}}
is an optimal strategy, with
b^{*}
a solution to (
4.3
), and the optimal value function is given by

V(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{\psi(x)}{\psi^{\prime}(b^{*})}&\mbox{if }0\leq x\leq b^{*},\\
I_{F}(x)+\frac{1-I_{F}^{\prime}(b^{*})}{\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(b^{*})}\varphi_{F}(x)&\mbox{if }x\geq b^{*}.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

All is left to justify is the expression for the optimal value function
V
under the condition
I_{F}^{\prime}(0)-I_{F}(0)\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(0)>1
. In that case, it suffices to use the general expression for
V_{b}
obtained in Proposition
3.1
together with the expressions for
C_{1}(b^{*})
and
C_{2}(b^{*})
given in Equations (
4.5
) and (
4.6
).
∎

As announced, given an increasing and concave function
F
, and recalling that
\psi
is independent of
F
and always known explicitly (see Equation (
2.2
)), this solution to the control problem is explicit up to the computations of the functions
\varphi_{F}
and
I_{F}
. It is interesting to note that these functions only depend on the dynamics of the nonlinear Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process
U
as given in (
2.3
); they are also solutions to ODEs.

4.1.
Solution to the problem with an affine bound

If we choose
F(x)=R+Kx
, with
K>0
and
R\geq 0
, then
U
is such that

\mathrm{d}U_{t}=(\mu-R+KU_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}W_{t},

i.e., it is a
standard
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. In this case,
\varphi_{F}
is known explicitly (see
[
7
]
):

\varphi_{F}(x)=\frac{H_{K}^{(q)}(x;\mu-R,\sigma)}{H_{K}^{(q)}(0;\mu-R,\sigma)},\quad x\geq 0,

where, using the notation in
[
14
]
,

H_{K}^{(q)}(x;m,\sigma)=\mathrm{e}^{K((x-m/K)^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}D_{-q/K}\left(\left(\frac{x-m/K}{\sigma}\right)\sqrt{2K}\right),

where
D_{-\lambda}
is the parabolic cylinder function given by

D_{-\lambda}(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\lambda)}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/4}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\lambda-1}\mathrm{e}^{-xt-t^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}t,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

On the other hand, we can compute the expectation in the definition of
I_{F}
:

(4.7)

I_{F}(x)=\frac{K}{q+K}\left(x+\frac{\mu}{q}\right)+\frac{Rq}{q+K}.

The following corollary is a generalization of both the results obtained in
[
11
,
5
]
and in
[
14
]
for a constant bound and a linear bound on control rates, respectively.

Corollary 4.4
.

Set
F(x)=R+Kx
, with
K>0
and
R\geq 0
. Define
\Delta=-\frac{H_{K}^{(q)}(0;\mu-R,\sigma)}{H_{K}^{(q)\prime}(0;\mu-R,\sigma)}
.

If
\Delta\geq\frac{K\mu}{q^{2}}+\frac{R}{q}
, then the mean-reverting strategy
\pi_{0}
is an optimal strategy and the optimal value function is given, for
x\geq 0
, by

V(x)=\frac{Kx}{q+K}-\left[\frac{K}{q+K}\left(\frac{\mu}{q}\right)+\frac{Rq}{q+K}\right]\left(1-\frac{H_{K}^{(q)}(0;\mu-R,\sigma)}{H_{K}^{(q)}(0;\mu-R,\sigma)}\right).

If
\Delta<\frac{K\mu}{q^{2}}+\frac{R}{q}
, then there exists a (unique) solution
b^{*}\in(0,\hat{b}]
to

\frac{\psi(b)}{\psi^{\prime}(b)}-\left(b+\frac{\mu}{q}\right)-\frac{R}{K}=-\frac{q}{K}\left(\frac{\psi(b)}{\psi^{\prime}(b)}-\frac{H_{K}^{(q)}(b;\mu-R,\sigma)}{H_{K}^{(q)\prime}(b;\mu-R,\sigma)}\right),

the mean-reverting strategy
\pi_{b^{*}}
is an optimal strategy and the optimal value function is given by

V(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{\psi(x)}{\psi^{\prime}(b^{*})}&\mbox{if }0\leq x\leq b^{*},\\
\frac{K}{q+K}\left(x+\frac{\mu}{q}\right)+\frac{q}{q+K}\left(R+\frac{H_{K}^{(q)}(x;\mu-R,\sigma)}{H_{K}^{(q)\prime}(b^{*};\mu-R,\sigma)}\right)&\mbox{if }x\geq b^{*}.\end{array}\right.

4.2.
Solution to the problem with a capped linear bound

If we choose
F(x)=\min(Kx,R)
, with
K,R>0
, then
U
is such that

dU_{t}=(\mu-\min(KU_{t},R))\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}W_{t},

i.e., it is a Brownian motion with drift
\mu-R
when
U_{t}>R/K
and a standard Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process when
U_{t}<R/K
.

Note that
F
is not differentiable at
x=R/K
, but there exists a decreasing sequence of differentiable and concave functions
F_{n}
such that, for all
x\geq 0
, we have
F_{n}(x)\to F(x)
when
n\to\infty
. By continuity, we have
\varphi_{F_{n}}\to\varphi_{F}
, and
I_{F_{n}}\to I_{F}
. Also, since
F
is continuous,
I_{F}
and
\varphi_{F}
are (at least) twice continuously differentiable.

We can compute
\varphi_{F}
and
I_{F}
explicitly, using the strong Markov property repeatedly:

\varphi_{F}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}B(x)+C(x)\varphi_{F}(R/K)&\mbox{if }0\leq x\leq R/K,\\
A(x)\varphi_{F}(R/K)&\mbox{if }x\geq R/K,\end{array}\right.

and

I_{F}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}I_{K}(x)-D(x)\left(I_{K}(R/K)-I_{F}(R/K)\right)&\mbox{if }x\leq R/K,\\
\frac{R}{q}\left(1-A(x)\right)+A(x)I_{F}(R/K)&\mbox{if }x\geq R/K,\end{array}\right.

where (see
[
7
]
)

\displaystyle A(x)

\displaystyle={\mathbf{E}_{x}}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{R/K}}{\mathbf{1}}_{\left\{\tau_{R/K}<\infty\right\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\tilde{\psi}(x-R/K)-\frac{2q}{\sqrt{(\mu-R)^{2}+2\sigma^{2}q}-(\mu-R)}\psi(x-R/K),

\displaystyle B(x)

\displaystyle={\mathbf{E}_{x}}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{0}^{F}}{\mathbf{1}}_{\left\{\tau_{0}^{F}<\tau_{R/K}^{F}\right\}}\right]=\frac{S\left(\frac{q}{K};\sqrt{\frac{2}{K}}\left(\frac{R-\mu}{\sigma}\right);\frac{x-\mu/K}{\sigma/\sqrt{2K}}\right)}{S\left(\frac{q}{K};\sqrt{\frac{2}{K}}\left(\frac{R-\mu}{\sigma}\right);\sqrt{\frac{2}{K}}\left(\frac{-\mu}{\sigma}\right)\right)},

\displaystyle C(x)

\displaystyle={\mathbf{E}_{x}}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{R/K}^{F}}{\mathbf{1}}_{\left\{\tau_{R/K}^{F}<\tau_{0}^{F}\right\}}\right]=\frac{S\left(\frac{q}{K};\frac{x-\mu/K}{\sigma/\sqrt{2K}};\sqrt{\frac{2}{K}}\left(\frac{-\mu}{\sigma}\right)\right)}{S\left(\frac{q}{K};\sqrt{\frac{2}{K}}\left(\frac{R-\mu}{\sigma}\right);\sqrt{\frac{2}{K}}\left(\frac{-\mu}{\sigma}\right)\right)},

\displaystyle D(x)

\displaystyle={\mathbf{E}_{x}}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{R/K}^{F}}{\mathbf{1}}_{\left\{\tau_{R/K}^{F}<\infty\right\}}\right]=\frac{H_{K}^{(q)}(x;\mu,-\sigma)}{H_{K}^{(q)}(R/K;\mu,-\sigma)},

where

\tilde{\psi}(x):=1+\frac{2q}{\sigma^{2}}\int_{0}^{x}\psi(y)\mathrm{d}y

and

S(\nu,x,y):=\frac{\Gamma(\nu)}{\pi}\mathrm{e}^{(x^{2}+y^{2})/4}\left(D_{-\nu}(-x)D_{-\nu}(y)-D_{-\nu}(x)D_{-\nu}(-y)\right),

and where

I_{K}(x):=\frac{K}{q+K}\left(x+\frac{\mu}{q}\right).

Note that
I_{K}
is the function
I_{F}
when
F(x)=Kx
. See (
4.7
) when
R=0
.

Since
\varphi_{F}
and
I_{F}
are continuously differentiable, we can deduce that

\varphi_{F}(R/K)=\frac{B^{\prime}(R/K)}{A^{\prime}(R/K)-C^{\prime}(R/K)}

and

I_{F}(R/K)=\frac{K/(q+K)-D^{\prime}(R/K)I_{K}(R/K)+(R/q)A^{\prime}(R/K)}{A^{\prime}(R/K)-D^{\prime}(R/K)}.

Corollary 4.5
.

Set
F(x)=\min(Kx,R)
, with
K,R>0
.

If
I_{F}^{\prime}(0)-I_{F}(0)\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(0)\leq 1
, then the generalized mean-reverting strategy
\pi_{0}
is optimal and the optimal value function is given, for
x\geq 0
, by

V(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}I_{K}(x)-D(x)(I_{K}\left(\frac{R}{K}\right)-I_{F}\left(\frac{R}{K}\right))-I_{F}(0)\left(B(x)+C(x)\varphi_{F}(\frac{R}{K})\right)&\mbox{if }x\leq\frac{R}{K},\\
\frac{R}{q}+A(x)\left(I_{F}\left(\frac{R}{K}\right)-I_{F}(0)\varphi_{F}\left(\frac{R}{K}\right)-\frac{R}{q}\right)&\mbox{if }x\geq\frac{R}{K}.\end{array}\right.

If
I_{F}^{\prime}(0)-I_{F}(0)\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(0)>1
, then the generalized mean-reverting strategy
\pi_{b^{*}}
is optimal, where
b^{*}\in(0,\hat{b}]
is the unique solution to (
4.3
), and the optimal value function is given by: if
b^{*}\leq\frac{R}{K}
, then

V(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{\psi(x)}{\psi^{\prime}(b^{*})}&\mbox{if }x\leq b^{*},\\
I_{K}(x)-D(x)\left(I_{K}\left(\frac{R}{K}\right)-I_{F}\left(\frac{R}{K}\right)\right)-\frac{1-I_{F}^{\prime}(b^{*})}{\varphi_{F}(b^{*})}\left(B(x)+C(x)\varphi_{F}\left(\frac{R}{K}\right)\right)&\mbox{if }b^{*}\leq x\leq\frac{R}{K},\\
\frac{R}{q}+A(x)\left(I_{F}\left(\frac{R}{K}\right)+\frac{1-I_{F}^{\prime}(b^{*})}{\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(b^{*})}\varphi_{F}\left(\frac{R}{K}\right)-\frac{R}{q}\right)&\mbox{if }x\geq\frac{R}{K},\end{array}\right.

and if
b^{*}>\frac{R}{K}
, then

V(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{\psi(x)}{\psi^{\prime}(b^{*})}&\mbox{if }x\leq b^{*},\\
\frac{R}{q}+A(x)\left(I_{F}\left(\frac{R}{K}\right)+\frac{1-I_{F}^{\prime}(b^{*})}{\varphi_{F}^{\prime}(b^{*})}\varphi_{F}\left(\frac{R}{K}\right)-\frac{R}{q}\right)&\mbox{if }x\geq b^{*}.\end{array}\right.

Appendix A. Proof of the Verification Lemma

The second statement of the lemma is a direct consequence of the definition of the optimal value function.

Now, let
\pi
be an arbitrary admissible strategy. Applying Ito’s Lemma to the continuous semi-martingale
(t,U_{t}^{\pi})
, using the function
g(t,y):=\mathrm{e}^{-qt}V_{\pi^{*}}(y)
, we find

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-q(t\wedge\tau_{0}^{\pi})}

\displaystyle V_{\pi^{*}}(U_{t\wedge\tau_{0}^{\pi}}^{\pi})

\displaystyle=V_{\pi^{*}}(x)+\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{0}^{\pi}}\mathrm{e}^{-qs}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}V_{\pi^{*}}^{\prime\prime}(U_{s}^{\pi})+\mu V_{\pi^{*}}^{\prime}(U_{s}^{\pi})-qV_{\pi^{*}}(U_{s}^{\pi})-l_{s}^{\pi}V_{\pi^{*}}^{\prime}(U_{s}^{\pi})\right)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{0}^{\pi}}\sigma\mathrm{e}^{-qs}V_{\pi^{*}}^{\prime}(U_{s}^{\pi})\mathrm{d}W_{s}.

Taking expectations on both sides, we find

\displaystyle V_{\pi^{*}}(x)

\displaystyle={\mathbf{E}_{x}}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q(t\wedge\tau_{0}^{\pi})}V_{\pi^{*}}(U_{t\wedge\tau_{0}^{\pi}}^{\pi})\right]
