Abstract
. In matching markets such as job posting and online dating platforms, the recommender system plays a critical role in the success of the platform. Unlike standard recommender systems that suggest items to users, reciprocal recommender systems (RRSs) that suggest other users must take into account the mutual interests of users. In addition, ensuring that recommendation opportunities do not disproportionately favor popular users is essential for the total number of matches and for fairness among users. Existing recommendation methods in matching markets, however, face computational challenges on real-world scale platforms and depend on specific examination functions in the position-based model (PBM). In this paper, we introduce the reciprocal recommendation method based on the matching with transferable utility (TU matching) model in the context of ranking recommendations in matching markets, and propose a faster and examination-agnostic algorithm. Furthermore, we evaluate our approach on experiments with synthetic data and real-world data from an online dating platform in Japan. Our method performs better than or as well as existing methods in terms of the total number of matches and works well even in relatively large datasets for which one existing method does not work.



1.
Introduction

In matching markets, such as job posting and online dating platforms, reciprocal recommender systems (RRSs) play a crucial role in the success of the platforms
(
Pizzato et al. 2010
;
Mine et al. 2013
)
.
Unlike conventional recommender systems that suggest items to users, RRSs that suggest other users need to take into account two aspects that do not appear in conventional recommender systems.
First, recommendation results need to be based on the mutual preferences of users.
In job posting platforms, for example, even if one job seeker has a strong interest in one job post, the employer might not be interested at all in the job candidate;
the “match” between the job seeker and the employer will not be established in this case.
Therefore, recommending job posts to a job seeker only according to the seeker’s interest is not effective,
and hence RRSs should recommend only when both users are mutually interested.
Second, recommendation opportunities should not concentrate on a few popular users.
The number of matches that one user can make is often physically limited because it is impossible for one employer to have interviews with all candidates.
Thus, distributing match opportunities widely among users is important to maximize the total number of matches, and fairness among users is important in matching markets.

To take into account the first aspect (i.e., the mutuality of preferences), most reciprocal recommendation methods aggregate two unilateral preference scores (of job candidates toward employers and vice versa) into a bilateral preference score via some aggregate functions.
Unilateral preference scores can be generally calculated by standard recommendation methods such as matrix factorization
(
Hu et al. 2008
;
Koren et al. 2009
)
.
Arithmetic mean, geometric mean, or harmonic mean are typically used as the aggregate function in the literature and in practice.
However, most of the aggregate functions do not reflect the second aspect, the necessity of avoiding concentration on a few popular users, and this issue has not been analyzed enough in the literature.

To tackle this issue,
Su et al. 2022
recently consider the problem of ranking recommendation in the matching markets, including job posting platforms.
They define the social welfare function as the expected total number of matches and propose a method to choose a stochastic ranking recommendation policy that maximizes the approximate social welfare function.
In the experiment simulating a matching market, their method substantially improves the total number of matches compared to both the naive method based on naive unilateral preferences and the reciprocal method based on the product of two unilateral preferences.
However, there are still two practical challenges to using
Su et al. 2022
’s method in real-world platforms.
First, their method involves a doubly stochastic matrix, whose size is quadratic in the number of users in the reactive side (e.g., employers), for each proactive side user (e.g., job candidates) as variables, and solves a convex optimization problem with constraints.
In real-world scale matching platforms, it is very expensive to directly solve the convex optimization problem of such sizes in terms of both space and time complexity.
In addition, implementing the stochastic ranking recommendation is challenging because the Birkhoff–von-Neumann decomposition
(
Birkhoff 1946
)
of doubly stochastic matrices for each proactive user is also computationally prohibitive.
Second, their method depends on the specific form of the examination functions in the position-based model (PBM).
In PBM, the examination function maps a ranking that an item listed on to a probability with which a user has attention to the item.
Although some methods to estimate the examination function are proposed, it is still hard in practice to estimate it correctly because the examination functions are specific to each application and may be different among individual users.

Su et al. 2022
use an examination function for both the objective function to optimize in their method and the evaluation via Monte Carlo simulations, and they do not consider the problem of misspecification of examination functions.

In the literature on the matching theory
(
Gale and Shapley 1962
;
Roth and Sotomayor 1992
)
, economists and computer scientists have developed various matching algorithms that seek to match two sides of agents appropriately according to the mutual preferences of agents and their matching capacity, which correspond to two aspects of RRSs discussed above.
Especially, matching with transferable utility (TU matching) models
(
Shapley and Shubik 1971
)
have been developed to analyze the job markets
(
Kelso Jr. and Crawford 1982
)
and the marriage markets
(
Becker 1973
;
Becker 1974
;
Choo and Siow 2006
)
.
In these models, monetary transfers are assumed to occur between two agents matched, and the amounts of transfers are adjusted so as to balance the demands from both sides in the matching market, just as prices in the standard economic markets.
The resulting matching, which is called “equilibrium matching” in this paper, reflects the mutuality of preferences and avoids the concentration of matching on a few popular people, and thus it would be effective to apply it in the reciprocal recommendations.

In this paper, we consider the reciprocal recommendation method based on the TU matching model of
Choo and Siow 2006
, in the setting of the ranking recommendation in the matching markets.
Our proposed
TU
method computes a deterministic ranking policy more efficiently in terms of both space and time complexity than
Su et al. 2022
’s method, and it is independent of a form of the examination function.

The contribution of this paper is as follows.
First, in the setting of ranking recommendation in the matching markets, we propose a reciprocal recommendation method based on TU matching model and develop an efficient algorithm.
Second, we evaluate our
TU
method in experiments with synthetic data and real-world data from an online dating platform in Japan, comparing with baselines including
SW
method of
Su et al. 2022
.
Our
TU
method performs at least as well as
SW
method in terms of the expected total number of matches, and outperforms the conventional RRS methods such as
Naive
and
Reciprocal
methods.
Moreover,
TU
method outperforms
SW
with the misspecified examination function in most cases, and it also works well even in relatively large datasets where
SW
method does not work.

1.1.
Related work

Reciprocal recommendation

Of particular distinction in reciprocal recommendation from the standard item recommendation is that a system must consider the bilateral preferences of proactive and reactive users.
Most of the conventional methods estimate the preferences of each side of users by using a corresponding model and then use the bilateral preference estimates to predict a reciprocal preference.
As the first step of unilateral preferences can be solved by standard recommender methods,
modeling reciprocal preferences is often reduced into the aggregation of bilateral preferences.
In this context, various aggregation functions have been proposed, e.g., arithmetic mean
(
Neve and Palomares 2019b
;
Neve and Palomares 2019a
)
, geometric mean
(
Neve and Palomares 2019b
;
Neve and Palomares 2019a
)
, harmonic mean
(
Pizzato et al. 2010
;
Xia et al. 2015
)
, cross-ratio uniform
(
Appel et al. 2017
)
, matrix multiplication
(
Jacobsen and Spanakis 2019
)
, the multiplicative inverse of rank multiplication
(
Mine et al. 2013
)
, and weighted mean with optimized weighting parameters
(
Kleinerman et al. 2018
)
.

Neve and Palomares 2019b
proposed an early method that optimizes two independent matrix factorization models of unilateral preferences and then aggregates two preference estimates using arithmetic, geometric, or harmonic means.
However, such aggregation functions highly depend on the scale and domain of unilateral preference estimates and lack a theoretical foundation.
By contrast, recent fairness-aware recommendation
do not consider such aggregation functions by directly maximizing a welfare function
(
Do et al. 2021
;
Do and Usunier 2022
)
.

Inefficiency issues in post-processing approach based on doubly stochastic matrices

Fairness-aware methods based on the post-processing approach often rely on the post-estimation of doubly stochastic matrices, which represent stochastic recommendation policies for users
(
Singh and Joachims 2018
;
Do et al. 2021
;
Usunier et al. 2022
;
Do and Usunier 2022
;
Saito and Joachims 2022
;
Su et al. 2022
)
.
Methods in this category may be impractical due to the quadratic computational cost with respect to the number of items for each user.
Recent methods alleviate this issue by using Frank-Wolfe algorithms that enable efficient parameter updates in the quasi-linear cost with respect to the number of items for each user
(
Do et al. 2021
;
Do and Usunier 2022
)
.
The practical implementation of probabilistic ranking systems is also challenging because the pre-computation step for sampling a ranked list from a doubly stochastic matrix can be costly due to the Birkhoff–von-Neumann decomposition
(
Birkhoff 1946
)
, which involves iteratively finding a perfect matching on the matrix.
Moreover, although the main interest of conventional studies is the fairness guarantee for algorithms, most of the provable guarantee does not hold in practice as the availability of the true preferences and true examination probabilities is often assumed.

Matching theory and reciprocal recommendations based on matching theory

Our methodology builds upon the literature of matching theory
(
Roth and Sotomayor 1992
;
Manlove 2013
)
originated by
Gale and Shapley 1962
, which has been widely applied to a variety of applications, including hospital-resident assignment
(
Roth and Peranson 1999
;
Kamada and Kojima 2015
)
, school matching
(
Abdulkadiroğlu et al. 2005b
;
Abdulkadiroğlu 2005

)

(

;

)
.
Especially, the matching with transferable utility model (TU model)
(
Shapley and Shubik 1971
;
Chiappori 2017
)
considers matching markets where agents matched can transfer utility or money between them. Economists have applied the TU matching models to analyze the job market
(
Kelso Jr. and Crawford 1982
)
and the marriage market
(
Becker 1973
;
Becker 1974
)
.

Choo and Siow 2006
develops an empirical model of the marriage market and applies it to the empirical study of the real marriage market in the US, and several works have developed its generalized model and estimation algorithms
(
Decker et al. 2013
;
Galichon and Salanié 2022
)
.

Although there are a few studies that apply the matching theory literature to the problem of reciprocal recommendation
(
Saini et al. 2019
;
Eskandanian and Mobasher 2020

Pizzato and Silvestrini 2011
)
, the interaction of reciprocal recommendation and matching theory has been relatively less analyzed.
The most similar work to this paper in terms of methodology is
Chen et al. 2023
, which considers recommending users based on the equilibrium matching of
Choo and Siow 2006
model in online dating.
However, the main method that they use in the online experiment separates users into several groups and estimates preferences for each group, and thus it does not correspond directly to the fully personalized recommendation

2.1.
Framework for recommendation in matching markets

Following
Su et al. 2022
, we describe the framework of matching markets in the context of job posting platforms.
This framework can be applied to other matching markets such as online dating platforms.
In this market, job candidates (proactive side) first browse a list of ranked job posts/employers and apply to the ones they are interested in. The employer (reactive side) receives a list of job candidates who applied to them and then selects the ones he/she considers qualified for the job.

We now present a formal model of this market.
Let
\mathcal{C}
be the set of job candidates, and
\mathcal{J}
be the set of job posts or employers.
We note that both sets are finite (
|\mathcal{C}|,|\mathcal{J}|<+\infty
).
Each candidate
c\in\mathcal{C}
has a preference over the set of job posts
\mathcal{J}
, and each employer
j\in\mathcal{J}
has a preference over the candidate set
\mathcal{C}
.

Although the platform does not know the true preferences of job candidates and employers, we assume that their estimations are available by using some recommendation methods.
Let
p_{c,j}\in[0,1]
be the estimated preference of candidate
c\in\mathcal{C}
to employer
j\in\mathcal{J}
, and
p_{j,c}\in[0,1]
be that of
j
to
c
.
The score
p_{c,j}
can be thought of as the probability with which
c
has relevance with
j
conditioning that
c
examined
j
;
a similar analogy can be applied to
p_{j,c}
.

The platform can choose a ranked list of employers sent to each job candidate.
Let
\Sigma_{|\mathcal{J}|}
be the set of all possible ranked lists of employers
\mathcal{J}
.
A deterministic ranking policy
\sigma:\mathcal{C}\to\Sigma_{|\mathcal{J}|}
is a mapping from each job candidate
c\in\mathcal{C}
to a ranked list of employers
\sigma(c)\in\Sigma_{|\mathcal{J}|}
that is sent to the candidate
c
.
A stochastic ranking policy
\pi:\mathcal{C}\to\Delta(\Sigma_{|\mathcal{J}|})
is a mapping from a candidate
c\in\mathcal{C}
to a probability distribution of ranked lists of employers
\pi(\cdot\mid c)\in\Delta(\Sigma_{|\mathcal{J}|})
.
In this work, we study how to design deterministic ranking policies (the naive, reciprocal, and our methods mentioned below), while the focus of
Su et al. 2022
is on stochastic ranking policies.
We also note that a deterministic ranking policy
\sigma
is a special case of stochastic ranking policies that take ranking
\sigma(c)
for each candidate
c\in\mathcal{C}
with probability
1
, and thus we explain the framework and objective in terms of stochastic policies for simplicity below.

After obtaining a ranked list of employers
\sigma(c)
, each candidate
c\in\mathcal{C}
chooses whether to apply for each employer.
In this framework, we use the position-based model (PBM)
(
Joachims et al. 2017
)
.
Conditioning on the ranked list
\sigma(c)
, the probability with which the candidate
c
applies to an employer
j
is

\mathbb{P}\left(c\text{ applies to }j\mid\sigma(c)\right)=v(\mathrm{rank}(j\mid\sigma(c)))\cdot p_{c,j},

where
\mathrm{rank}(j\mid\sigma(c))\in\{1,2,\dots,|\mathcal{J}|\}
is the rank of the employer
j
in the ranked list
\sigma(c)
, and
v
is an examination function, that is,
v(k)\in[0,1]
is a probability with which the candidate examines the employer positioning on rank
k
.
Typically,
v(x)=1/x
or
1/\log(1+x)
are used.
With a stochastic ranking policy
\pi
, the probability with which a candidate
c\in\mathcal{C}
applies to an employer
j\in\mathcal{J}
, denoted by
\mathbb{P}_{c,j}^{\pi}
can be calculated as below:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{c,j}^{\pi}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(c\text{ applies to }j\mid\pi\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma(c)\in\Sigma_{|\mathcal{J}|}}\pi(\sigma(c)\mid c)\cdot\mathbb{P}(c\text{ applies to }j\mid\sigma(c))

(1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma(c)\in\Sigma_{|\mathcal{J}|}}\pi(\sigma(c)\mid c)\cdot v(\mathrm{rank}(j\mid\sigma(c)))\cdot p_{c,j}.

After the application process by the job candidates, each employer
j\in\mathcal{J}
receives the list of candidates who applied to the employer
j
.
The list is sorted in descending order according to the employer’s preference
p_{j,\cdot}
, beforehand by the platform.
Employers examine the lists and then choose candidates to “match”, that is, to get in touch with or to conduct an interview with.
As in the case of job candidates, we also use the PBM for this employer’s process.
The probability with which the employer
j\in\mathcal{J}
matches to
c\in\mathcal{C}
conditioning on that
c
applied to
j
, denoted by
\mathbb{P}_{j,c}^{\pi}
is

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{j,c}^{\pi}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(j\text{ matches to }c\mid c\text{ applied to }j,\ \pi\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[v\left(\mathbf{rk}^{\pi}_{j}(c)\right)\right]\cdot p_{j,c}

where
\mathbf{rk}^{\pi}_{j}(c)
is a rank of
c
in the list for the employer
j
conditioning on the policy
\pi
and that
c
applied to
j
.
We note that
\mathbf{rk}^{\pi}_{j}(c)
is a random variable even if
\pi
is deterministic because it relies on the set of candidates who also applied for the employer
j
.

Given a policy
\pi
, the
social welfare
is defined as the expected total number of matches
(
Su et al. 2022
)
:

(2)

\mathbf{SW}(\pi)\coloneqq\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{c,j}\mathbb{P}_{j,c}^{\pi}.

The platform’s objective is to find an appropriate policy
\pi
such that the social welfare
\mathbf{SW}(\pi)
is maximized.

2.2.
Existing methods

Given the matching market explained above, we highlight popular existing methods, the naive methods, reciprocal methods, and the approximate social welfare maximization approach
(
Su et al. 2022
)
.

Naive method

One of the most popular methods in practice is to re-rank employers naively according to job candidates’ preferences.
For each job candidate
c\in\mathcal{C}
, let
\sigma^{\mathrm{naive}}(c)
be the ranked list of employers
\mathcal{J}
, which is sorted in descending order according to the candidate
c
’s preference score
p_{c,\cdot}
.

Reciprocal method

Many works in the RRSs use aggregation functions that aggregate two unilateral scores
p_{c,j},p_{j,c}
into a reciprocal score
p^{\mathrm{rec}}_{c,j}
to take into account the mutuality of preferences.
One of the most popular aggregation functions is the geometric mean, or equivalently in terms of the order, the product of job candidates’ and employers’ preference scores
p^{\mathrm{rec}}_{c,j}=p_{c,j}\cdot p_{j,c}

(
Neve and Palomares 2019a
;
Neve and Palomares 2019b
)
.
In the reciprocal method, the platform provides the job candidate
c
with the ranked list of employers
\sigma^{\mathrm{rec}}(c)\in\Sigma_{|\mathcal{J}|}
which is sorted in descending order according to the reciprocal scores
p^{\mathrm{rec}}_{c,\cdot}
.

Input:

preference matrices
(p_{c,j})_{(c,j)\in\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{J}}
and
(p_{j,c})_{(j,c)\in\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{C}}
, examination function
v(\cdot)
, timesteps
T
, learning rate
\eta_{t}

Output:

the doubly stochastic matrices
M_{\mathcal{C}}^{\pi}

initialize

M_{c}^{\pi}\leftarrow\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}/|\mathcal{J}|
,
\forall c\in\mathcal{C}
;

for

t=1,\dots T

do

S^{*}_{\mathcal{C}}\in\mathop{\rm arg~min}\limits_{S_{\mathcal{C}}}-\nabla\underline{\mathbf{SW}}(M_{\mathcal{C}}^{\pi})^{\mathsf{T}}S_{\mathcal{C}}
s.t.
\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}S_{c}=\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}},\ S_{c}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1},\ \forall c\in\mathcal{C},\ S_{\mathcal{C}}=(S_{c})_{c\in\mathcal{C}}
;

M_{\mathcal{C}}^{\pi}\leftarrow(1-\eta_{t})M_{\mathcal{C}}^{\pi}+\eta_{t}S^{*}_{\mathcal{C}}
;

end for

return

M_{\mathcal{C}}^{\pi}
;

Approximate social welfare maximization
(
Su et al. 2022
)

Unlike the naive and reciprocal methods, the approximate social welfare maximization method
(
Su et al. 2022
)
optimizes a stochastic policy directly. The probability (
1
) with which candidate
c\in\mathcal{C}
applies to employer
j\in\mathcal{J}
in a stochastic policy
\pi
can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{c,j}^{\pi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma(c)\in\Sigma_{|\mathcal{J}|}}\pi(\sigma(c)\mid c)\cdot v(\mathrm{rank}(j\mid\sigma(c)))\cdot p_{c,j}

\displaystyle=p_{c,j}\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{J}|}\mathbb{P}_{\sigma(c)\sim\pi(\cdot\mid c)}\Big(\mathrm{rank}\left(j\mid\sigma(c)\right)=k\Big)\cdot v(k)

(3)

\displaystyle=p_{c,j}\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{J}|}M^{\pi}_{c}(j,k)\cdot v(k)

where
M_{c}^{\pi}\in[0,1]^{|\mathcal{J}|\times|\mathcal{J}|}
is the doubly stochastic matrix reduced from
\pi(\cdot\mid c)
, that is,
M_{c}^{\pi}(j,k)
is the probability with which the employer
j
is ranked at position
k
for the candidate
c
in the stochastic policy
\pi
.
In the PBM, the probability with which
c
applies to
j
relies only on the position
k
where the employer
j
is displayed and preference score
p_{c,j}
, and not on other employers’ position.
Therefore, the application probability (
3
) can be expressed in terms of the doubly stochastic matrix
M_{c}^{\pi}
reduced from the stochastic policy
\pi(\cdot\mid c)
.

Then the social welfare (
2
) is:

\displaystyle\mathbf{SW}(\pi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{c,j}\mathbb{P}_{j,c}^{\pi}

(4)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}p_{c,j}\cdot p_{j,c}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[v\left(\mathbf{rk}^{\pi}_{j}(c)\right)\right]\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{J}|}M^{\pi}_{c}(j,k)\cdot v(k).

However, the social welfare (
4
) cannot be directly optimized because the rank of
c
in
j
’s list
\mathbf{rk}^{\pi}_{j}(c)
depends on other candidates’ applications and thus the term
\mathbb{E}\left[v\left(\mathbf{rk}^{\pi}_{j}(c)\right)\right]
is too complex to tackle with.

Su et al. 2022
derive a lower bounds
\underline{\mathbf{SW}}\left(M_{\mathcal{C}}^{\pi}\right)
of
\mathbf{SW}(\pi)
under the assumption of convexity of examination function
v
:

\displaystyle\underline{\mathbf{SW}}\left(M_{\mathcal{C}}^{\pi}\right)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}p_{c,j}\cdot p_{j,c}

\displaystyle\cdot v\left(1+\sum_{c^{\prime}:p_{j,c^{\prime}}>p_{j,c}}p_{c^{\prime},j}\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{J}|}M_{c^{\prime}}^{\pi}(j,k)\cdot v(k)\right)

(5)

\displaystyle\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{J}|}M_{c}^{\pi}(j,k)\cdot v(k),

where
M_{\mathcal{C}}^{\pi}\coloneqq(M_{c}^{\pi})_{c\in\mathcal{C}}
. They propose the
SW
method that optimizes this approximated social welfare instead of
\mathbf{SW}(\pi)
:

(6)

\displaystyle\max_{M^{\pi}_{\mathcal{C}}}\ \underline{\mathbf{SW}}\left(M_{\mathcal{C}}^{\pi}\right),\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\ \mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}M_{c}^{\pi}=\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}},\ M_{c}^{\pi}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1},\ \forall c\in\mathcal{C}.

Several approaches can be used to solve the convex optimization problem (
6
).

Su et al. 2022
use the Frank-Wolfe approach, which is summarized as Algorithm
1
.

However, there are some practical difficulties in implementing this social welfare optimization approach in real-world matching markets as discussed in Section
1
.
The objective function (
5
) includes the true examination function
v
, whose estimation is hard in practice.
Moreover, materializing the doubly stochastic matrices for all candidates is unrealistic due to its computational cost of
O(|\mathcal{C}||\mathcal{J}|^{2})
for each timestep, and the precomputation for efficient sampling of rankings from a doubly stochastic matrix involves Birkhoff–von-Neumann decomposition, which is very costly to solve for all job candidates; we further discuss this point in Section
3.2
.

)
in Section

3.1.
TU matching model

In the model discussed below, we interpret that estimated preference scores
p_{c,j}
and
p_{j,c}
are the estimation of surplus or gains that they obtain when they match with each other.
When the candidate
c\in\mathcal{C}
and the employer
j\in\mathcal{J}
match, they split surplus

p_{c,j}+\epsilon_{c,j}+p_{j,c}+\epsilon_{j,c}

where
\epsilon_{c,j}
and
\epsilon_{j,c}
are the estimation error of preferences
p_{c,j}
,
p_{j,c}
respectively, which are assumed to be identically and independently distributed from some probability distribution
F,G
.
In the TU matching model, we assume that monetary transfer occurs between a job candidate and an employer when they are matched.
Transfers are interpreted in physical markets as, for example, wages in the job market, household sharing in the marriage market, or gifts in dating markets.
Although there may not be such explicit transfers between users on online platforms, we assume that some implicit transfers occur and use virtual transfers as a tool to derive an appropriate matching.
Let
\tau_{c,j}
be the transfer amount that the employer
j
gives to
c
when they match.
In this case, the candidate
c
/the employer
j
obtains

p_{c,j}+\epsilon_{c,j}+\tau_{c,j},\qquad p_{j,c}+\epsilon_{j,c}-\tau_{c,j}

from the matching, respectively.
In this matching market, each agent chooses one that maximizes his/her gain from the set of opponents.
They also have an outside option, or remaining “unmatched” in this market, which is denoted as “
0
”.
The stochastic demand of the candidate
c\in\mathcal{C}
toward the employer
j\in\mathcal{J}\cup\{0\}
, that is, the probability with which
c
chooses
j
is

(7)

\mu_{c,j}=\mathbb{P}_{\epsilon_{c,\cdot}}\left(j\in\mathop{\rm arg~max}\limits_{j^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}\cup\{0\}}\left(p_{c,j^{\prime}}+\epsilon_{c,j^{\prime}}+\tau_{c,j^{\prime}}\right)\right),

and the stochastic demand of the employer
j\in\mathcal{J}
toward
c\in\mathcal{C}\cup\{0\}
(the probability with which
j
chooses
c
) is

(8)

\mu_{j,c}=\mathbb{P}_{\epsilon_{j,\cdot}}\left(c\in\mathop{\rm arg~max}\limits_{c^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}\cup\{0\}}\left(p_{j,c^{\prime}}+\epsilon_{j,c^{\prime}}-\tau_{c^{\prime},j}\right)\right),

where we assume
p_{c,0}=p_{0,j}=\tau_{c,0}=\tau_{0,j}=0
,
\epsilon_{c,0}\sim F
and
\epsilon_{j,0}\sim G
i.i.d. for any
c\in\mathcal{C},j\in\mathcal{J}
.
2
2

2

The existence of an outside option is not a crucial assumption because the model and method can be written and derived without the outside option “
0
”.
We contain
0
following the tradition of matching with transferable utility literature
(
Choo and Siow 2006
)
and for the simplicity of notation and method.

The equilibrium is a situation where both sides of stochastic demands
\mu_{c,j},\mu_{j,c}
coincide by the transfers’ adjustment, just as the market price in the standard physical market, which adjusts demands and supplies.
We call the adjusted transfers
\tau^{*}=(\tau^{*}_{c,j})_{(c,j)\in\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{J}}
and (fractional) matching
\mu^{*}=(\mu^{*}_{c,j})_{(c,j)\in\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{J}}
as equilibrium matching.

Definition 0.

An equilibrium matching
(\mu^{*},\tau^{*})
satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

\mu^{*}
satisfies

(9)

\displaystyle\mu_{c,0}^{*}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\mu^{*}_{c,j}=1\qquad\forall c\in\mathcal{C},

(10)

\displaystyle\mu_{0,j}^{*}+\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\mu^{*}_{c,j}=1\qquad\forall j\in\mathcal{J}.

(2)

\mu^{*}
equals both sides of demands:

\displaystyle\mu^{*}_{c,j}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\epsilon_{c,\cdot}}\left(j\in\mathop{\rm arg~max}\limits_{j^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}\cup\{0\}}\left(p_{c,j^{\prime}}+\epsilon_{c,j^{\prime}}+\tau^{*}_{c,j^{\prime}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\epsilon_{j,\cdot}}\left(c\in\mathop{\rm arg~max}\limits_{c^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}\cup\{0\}}\left(p_{j,c^{\prime}}+\epsilon_{j,c^{\prime}}-\tau^{*}_{c^{\prime},j}\right)\right)

for all
(c,j)\in\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{J}
.

The intuition of the equilibrium matching in TU matching model is as follows.
For a popular candidate
c
,
p_{j,c}
is generally high for many employers, and the demands of employers toward
c
(
8
) likely to exceed the demands of
c
toward employers (
7
).
In this case, transfers
\tau_{c,\cdot}
that the candidate
c
requests to employers increase to adjust the excess demands in the market.
On the other hand, a popular employer
j
with high
p_{c,j}
s tends to have excess demands from many candidates.
In this case, to adjust the excess demand toward
j
, the market would decrease
\tau_{\cdot,j}
that the employer needs to pay when he matches.
After these adjustment processes, demands from both sides are settled in equilibrium.
Economists consider that the equilibrium is achieved in real-world matching markets such as job markets or marriage markets, and analyzes this equilibrium matching in TU matching model.
Online matching markets might also suffer from the excess demands toward a few popular users.
Therefore, we consider that the equilibrium in TU matching would be effective in designing a desirable reciprocal recommendation that takes into account two aspects discussed in the Introduction: the mutuality of preferences and the avoidance of concentration on a few popular users.

Galichon and Salanié 2022
show that the equilibrium matching exists, can be computed as a solution to a convex optimization problem, and maximizes social gains under constraints.
However, solving this equilibrium matching, in general, is computationally difficult.
To derive tractable equations of equilibrium matching, we introduce the Gumbel specification
(
Choo and Siow 2006
)
of preference errors’ distribution.

Assumption 1.

Estimation errors of preference scores (
\epsilon_{c,j}
for each
(c,j)\in\mathcal{C}\times(\mathcal{J}\cup\{0\})
, and
\epsilon_{j,c}
for each
(j,c)\in\mathcal{J}\times(\mathcal{C}\cup\{0\})
) are identically and independently distributed from the Gumbel (type-I extreme value) distribution with location
0
and scale
\beta>0
.

Given Assumption
1
, we can show that
\mu^{*}_{c,j}
for each
(c,j)\in\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{C}
in equilibrium is

(11)

\mu^{*}_{c,j}=\exp\left(\frac{p_{c,j}+p_{j,c}}{2\beta}\right)\sqrt{\mu_{c,0}^{*}}\sqrt{\mu_{0,j}^{*}}

where
\mu_{c,0}^{*},\mu_{0,j}^{*}
is the stochastic demand of
c
/
j
toward the outside option
0
.
In our method, we compute this equilibrium matching
\mu_{c,j}^{*}
and generate the ranked list of employers in the descending order of
\mu_{c,\cdot}^{*}
to each candidate
c
.
Unlike standard reciprocal recommendation methods that use aggregate functions, such as the product function discussed Section
2.2
, the equilibrium matching (
11
) is computed with not only the preference scores
p_{c,j},p_{j,c}
between the two users, but also with
\mu_{c,0}^{*},\mu_{0,j}^{*}
determined by the overall balance of the market.

Input:

preference scores
(p_{c,j})_{(c,j)\in\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{J}}
and
(p_{j,c})_{(j,c)\in\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{C}}
, scale parameter
\beta>0
, timesteps
T

Output:

Equilibrium Matching
\mu^{*}=(\mu^{*}_{c,j})_{(c,j)\in\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{J}}

initialize

A_{c}\leftarrow 1\ \forall c\in\mathcal{C}
,
B_{j}\leftarrow 1\ \forall j\in\mathcal{J}
;

for

t=1,\dots T

do

A_{c}\leftarrow\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\exp\left(\frac{p_{c,j}+p_{j,c}}{2\beta}\right)B_{j}\right)^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\exp\left(\frac{p_{c,j}+p_{j,c}}{2\beta}\right)B_{j}
for each
c\in\mathcal{C}
;

B_{j}\leftarrow\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\exp\left(\frac{p_{c,j}+p_{j,c}}{2\beta}\right)A_{c}\right)^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\exp\left(\frac{p_{c,j}+p_{j,c}}{2\beta}\right)A_{c}
for each
j\in\mathcal{J}
;

end for

\mu_{c,j}^{*}\leftarrow\exp\left(\frac{p_{c,j}+p_{j,c}}{2\beta}\right)A_{c}B_{j}
for each
(c,j)\in C\times\mathcal{J}
;

return

\mu^{*}
;

Algorithm 2

Solving Choo–Siow Equilibrium Matching via IPFP

Now we can derive a system of equations to find the equilibrium matching.
Let
A_{c}\coloneqq\sqrt{\mu_{c,0}^{*}}
and
B_{j}\coloneqq\sqrt{\mu_{0,j}^{*}}
.
The equilibrium matching
\mu^{*}
satisfies the following system of equations:

(12)

\displaystyle A_{c}^{2}+A_{c}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\exp\left(\frac{p_{c,j}+p_{j,c}}{2\beta}\right)B_{j}=1\qquad\text{for each }c\in\mathcal{C},

(13)

\displaystyle B_{j}^{2}+B_{j}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\exp\left(\frac{p_{c,j}+p_{j,c}}{2\beta}\right)A_{c}=1\qquad\text{for each }j\in\mathcal{J},

(14)

\displaystyle\mu_{c,j}^{*}=\exp\left(\frac{p_{c,j}+p_{j,c}}{2\beta}\right)A_{c}B_{j}\qquad\text{for each }(c,j)\in\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{J},

The derivation of these equations is shown in Appendix
A.1
.

These equations can be solved in several methods.
In our experiments discussed later, we solve the equations via IPFP (Iterative Proportional Fitting Procedure)
(
Decker et al. 2013
;
Galichon and Salanié 2022
)
, which is summarized as Algorithm

3.2.
Computational efficiency in inference time

As mentioned above, the existing method requires precomputing Birkhoff–von-Neumann decomposition
(
Birkhoff 1946
)
of a doubly stochastic matrix for each job candidate to efficiently sample a deterministic ranking in inference time.
However, it leads to a prohibitively large computational cost, being infeasible in realistic applications.
The space cost for the precomputed set of top-
K
deterministic rankings for each job candidate may also be impractical.
By contrast, our method enables efficient real-time retrieval via vector search algorithms
(
Shrivastava and Li 2014
)
when employing two-tower/dot-product models
(
Rendle et al. 2020
)
, such as matrix factorization
(
Hu et al. 2008
;
Koren et al. 2009
)
, for preference scores; this is an essential property for large-scale applications (e.g.,
(
Covington et al. 2016
)
).
Assume that preference scores can be expressed in the following dot-product models,

\displaystyle p_{c,j}=\langle\phi_{1}(c),\psi_{1}(j)\rangle,\quad p_{j,c}=\langle\phi_{2}(c),\psi_{2}(j)\rangle.

Here,
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
are the
d
-dimensional feature mappings for job candidates in the two preference models,
while
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
are those for job posts.
Recall here that our method predicts a ranked list of
\mathcal{J}
for a candidate
c\in\mathcal{C}
according to the order of
\{\mu^{*}_{c,j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
, and hence it is equivalent to use
\{\log(\mu^{*}_{c,j})\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
to generate ranked lists due to the monotonicity of
\log(\cdot)
.
By using Equation (
11
),
this equivalent ranking score can be reformulated as follows:

\displaystyle\log(\mu^{*}_{c,j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\left(\exp\left(\frac{p_{c,j}+p_{j,c}}{2\beta}\right)\sqrt{\mu^{*}_{c,0}}\sqrt{\mu_{0,j}^{*}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{p_{c,j}+p_{j,c}}{2\beta}+\frac{1}{2}\log(\mu^{*}_{c,0})+\frac{1}{2}\log(\mu_{0,j}^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\beta}\left(\langle\phi_{1}(c),\psi_{1}(j)\rangle+\langle\phi_{2}(c),\psi_{2}(j)\rangle\right)+\frac{1}{2}\log(\mu^{*}_{c,0})+\frac{1}{2}\log(\mu_{0,j}^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\beta}\left\langle\left[\phi_{1}(c),\phi_{2}(c)\right],\left[\psi_{1}(j),\psi_{2}(j)\right]\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\log(\mu^{*}_{c,0})+\frac{1}{2}\log(\mu_{0,j}^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\beta}\left\langle\left[\phi_{1}(c),\phi_{2}(c),\beta\log(\mu^{*}_{c,0}),1\right],\left[\psi_{1}(j),\psi_{2}(j),1,\beta\log(\mu_{0,j}^{*})\right]\right\rangle,

where
[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}]
is the concatenation of two vectors
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
.
Therefore, our method maintains the desirable structure in dot-product models of preference scores by using the following
(2d+2)
-dimensional feature mappings,

\displaystyle\phi(c)=\left[\phi_{1}(c),\phi_{2}(c),\beta\log(\mu^{*}_{c,0}),1\right],

\displaystyle\psi(j)=\left[\psi_{1}(j),\psi_{2}(j),1,\beta\log(\mu_{0,j}^{*})\right].

Note here that we omitted the positive constant
1/2\beta
as it does not affect ranking predictions.

These results suggest that our

method would be beneficial in practice for real-world online dating or job posting platforms with more than thousands of users.

6.
Conclusions and Future works

In this paper, we introduce the TU matching-based reciprocal recommender system that does not rely on the specific form of examination functions, and works in a relatively large datasets for which one existing method does not work.
We evaluate the performance of our method in experiments with synthetic and real-world data from an online dating platform.
Our
TU
method outperforms the conventional
Naive
and
Reciprocal
methods, and performs at least as well as the
SW
method.
These experiments suggest that the
TU
method can be superior to
SW
in a realistic situation where
SW
uses a possibly misspecified examination function.

There are several considerable future directions in the field of reciprocal recommendation in matching markets.
First, in this paper, whereas we evaluate our
TU
method only in experiments that run Monte Carlo simulations that simulate the matching markets,

evaluation in online A/B experiments
in real-world platforms remains for future works.
Moreover, it is also important to develop offline/off-policy evaluation methods in reciprocal recommendation in matching markets.

Next,
more scalable ranking methods in RRSs
would also be a future direction.
Although our
TU
method is more efficient in terms of time and space complexity than the
Su et al. 2022
’s method, it still costs the order of computational complexity
O(|\mathcal{J}||\mathcal{C}|)
for each timestep in Algorithm.
2
, and we only conduct experiments with
1{,}000\times 1{,}000
datasets at most.
Since there are real-world matching platforms with tens or hundreds of thousands of users, a more efficient implementation of our
TU
method or other algorithms would be needed for such large-scale matching platforms.

Another future direction is
applications of other matching algorithms
.
Besides TU matching
(
Shapley and Shubik 1971
;
Choo and Siow 2006
;
Galichon and Salanié 2022
)
, many other matching algorithms have been developed in the field of matching with non-transferable utility (NTU matching), such as the differed acceptance (DA) algorithm of
Gale and Shapley 1962
.
Only a few works have developed a reciprocal recommender system based on matching algorithms like DA algorithm
(
Saini et al. 2019
;
Eskandanian and Mobasher 2020
;
Pizzato and Silvestrini 2011
)
.
Especially, there seems to be possible interaction between the issues of fairness in the reciprocal recommendation and the matching with constraints.
In the literature of matching theory, various algorithms with complex constraints regarding fairness issues have been recently developed, such as NTU matching with affirmative actions
(
Abdulkadiroğlu 2005
;
Hafalir et al. 2013
)
and with regional constraints
(
Kamada and Kojima 2015
;
Aziz et al. 2020
)
, and TU matching with regional constraints
(
Jalota 2022
;
Matsushita et al. 2022
)
.
These matching algorithms with constraints could be beneficial to improve the fairness of reciprocal recommendations.

Finally, the issues of
incentive and strategic behavior in reciprocal recommendation
would also be an important future direction.
In reciprocal recommendations, the problem could be whether users act according to their true preferences.
For example, a job seeker may not apply for a job slot in which she is strongly interested because the position seems to be popular among job seekers and highly competitive.
Thus, the action history obtained in reciprocal recommendations may not reflect the true preferences of users.
Furthermore, consider the situation where the reciprocal recommendation method other than the naive method that recommends naively according to the estimated preferences of proactive users is used.
Users may have incentives to manipulate their actions and reveal false preferences because the recommendation systems do not simply suggest users that they seem to be interested in.
The issue of strategic behavior has been extensively analyzed in matching theory, and many “strategy-proof” matching algorithms that incentivize users to reveal their true preferences have been developed
(
Roth and Sotomayor 1989
;
Sönmez 1994
)
.
Designing RRSs that incentivize users to act according to their true preferences based on strategy-proof matching algorithms could be an interesting future direction.
