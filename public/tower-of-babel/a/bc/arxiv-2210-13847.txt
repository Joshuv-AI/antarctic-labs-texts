Abstract
. We present a multidimensional extension of Kellerer’s theorem on the existence of mimicking Markov martingales for peacocks, a term derived from the French for stochastic processes increasing in convex order. For a continuous-time peacock in arbitrary dimension, after Gaussian regularization, we show that there exists a strongly Markovian mimicking martingale Itô diffusion. A novel compactness result for martingale diffusions is a key tool in our proof. Moreover, we provide counterexamples to show, in dimension d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 , that uniqueness may not hold, and that some regularization is necessary to guarantee existence of a mimicking Markov martingale.

1.
Introduction

Given a finite set of probability measures on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
that are increasing in convex order, Strassen
[
42
]
showed in 1965 that there exists a Markov martingale whose marginals coincide with the given probability measures. We call this latter property
mimicking
. For a family of measures indexed by
continuous time
that are increasing in convex order, also called a peacock (
Processus Croissant pour l’Ordre Convexe
), Kellerer
[
28
]
proved in 1972 that there exists a mimicking strong Markov martingale in dimension one. The questions of continuity and uniqueness for Kellerer’s mimicking martingale remained open until the work of Lowther
[
33
,
32
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
completely clarified the situation. Lowther showed that, in dimension one, there exists a
unique strong Markov
mimicking martingale and, moreover this process has
continuous paths
when the peacock is weakly continuous and the marginals have convex support. It is also known that the strong Markov property is required to obtain uniqueness; Beiglböck, Lowther, Pammer and Schachermayer
[
5
]
construct a one-dimensional continuous Markov martingale whose marginals coincide with those of Brownian motion but which does not have the strong Markov property.

While the problem of finding mimicking Markov martingales is thus very well understood for one-dimensional peacocks, the higher dimensional case has remained wide open, although 50 years have passed since the publication of Kellerer’s result. In this paper, to the best of our knowledge, we provide the first known multidimensional extension of Kellerer’s theorem. Given a peacock on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we show that, after some Gaussian regularization, there exists a
strongly Markovian martingale Itô diffusion
that mimics the regularized peacock.

To prove our result, we construct a martingale Itô diffusion that mimics the regularized peacock on the dyadics, and then pass to a limit in finite dimensional distributions. In order to take such a limit, we prove a compactness result for martingale Itô diffusions.

Additionally, we show that uniqueness does not necessarily hold in higher dimensions. We consider an example of a martingale Itô diffusion studied by Robinson
[
38
]
and Cox–Robinson
[
9
]
, and we show that this martingale mimics the marginals of a two-dimensional Brownian motion, while itself not being a Brownian motion. We also show, by means of counterexamples, that the Gaussian regularization is necessary to guarantee the existence of a mimicking Markov martingale.

For
\eta>0
, let
\gamma^{\eta}
denote the centered Gaussian law on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with covariance
\eta\mathrm{id}
, and let
\ast
denote the convolution operator between measures. Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.1

(existence of mimicking martingales)
.

Let
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
be a weakly continuous square-integrable peacock on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Fix
\delta,\varepsilon>0
and, for each
t\in[0,1]
, define the regularized measure
\mu^{\mathrm{r}}_{t}\coloneqq\mu_{t}\ast\gamma^{\varepsilon(t+\delta)}
. Then there exists a strongly Markovian martingale Itô diffusion
(M_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
mimicking the regularized peacock
(\mu^{\mathrm{r}}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
.

More precisely, there exists a measurable function
(t,x)\mapsto\sigma_{t}(x)
on
[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, taking values in the set of positive definite matrices such that, on any probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},{\mathbb{P}})
supporting a standard
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
-valued Brownian motion
(B_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
and an independent random variable
\xi\sim\mu_{0}
, the martingale
M
satisfies

(1.1)

\mathrm{d}M_{t}=\sigma_{t}(M_{t})\mathrm{d}B_{t},\quad M_{0}=\xi.

The map
(t,x)\mapsto\sigma_{t}(x)^{2}\coloneqq\sigma_{t}(x)\sigma_{t}(x)^{\top}
is locally Lipschitz continuous in the variable
x
, uniformly in
t\in[0,1]
and, for every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, there exist constants
c_{x},C_{x}>0
such that, for
t\in[0,1]
, we have the bounds

(1.2)

c_{x}\,\mathrm{id}\leq\sigma_{t}(x)^{2}\leq C_{x}\,\mathrm{id}.

Moreover, the martingale
M
is a Feller process.

Note that, in particular, the mimicking martingale that we construct in
Theorem

1.1
is continuous and strongly Markovian.
A key ingredient in the construction of this mimicking martingale is the following result that allows us to pass to limits of martingale Itô diffusions, the details of which are presented in
Section

5
.

Theorem 1.2

(compactness of martingale Itô diffusions)
.

A set of martingale Itô diffusions satisfying
Items

(A1)
–
(A5)
is precompact in the set of martingale Itô diffusions with respect to convergence in finite dimensional distributions.

Our next main result is that, in dimension
d\geq 2
, mimicking martingales of the form (
1.1
) may not be unique.

Theorem 1.3

(non-uniqueness of mimicking martingales)
.

Let
(B_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
be a standard Brownian motion on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
with initial law
\mathrm{Law}(B_{0})=\eta
, where
\eta
is rotationally invariant with finite second moment. Define a peacock
\mu
by
\mu_{t}=\mathrm{Law}(B_{t})
, for
t\in[0,1]
.

Then there exists a continuous strongly Markovian martingale diffusion
(M_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
of the form (
1.1
), that is not a Brownian motion, such that
\mathrm{Law}(M_{t})=\mu_{t}
, for all
t\in[0,1]
.

We further construct a series of counterexamples in dimension
d=4
which show that, without regularization,
Theorem

1.1
does not hold in full generality, even without imposing continuity of the mimicking martingale, let alone the Itô diffusion property.

Theorem 1.4

(necessity of regularization)
.

There exists a weakly continuous square-integrable peacock
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
such that, for the peacock
(\mu_{t}\ast\gamma^{t})_{t\geq 0}
, there exists no mimicking Markov martingale.

While previous authors have considered the problem of finding mimicking martingales in general dimensions, to the best of our knowledge the present work is the first to provide a multidimensional extension of Kellerer’s theorem. Prior to Kellerer’s work, Doob
[
15
]
proved the existence of mimicking martingales taking values in an abstract compact space in continuous time, but notably did not consider the Markov property. More recently, Hirsch and Roynette
[
22
]
proved existence for continuous-time peacocks on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
, with right-continuous paths, again without the Markov property.

Juillet
[
25
]
considered generalizing Kellerer’s theorem in two different directions, first showing that when a peacock on
{\mathbb{R}}
is indexed by a two-parameter family with some partial order, mimicking martingales may not exist at all. Moreover,
[
25
]
provides an example of a peacock on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
for which there exists no mimicking martingale that additionally satisfies the so-called
Lipschitz Markov
property, defined in
[
25
, Definition 6]
. The Lipschitz Markov property implies the Feller property and, for càdlàg processes, the strong Markov property; see
[
35
, Lemma 4.2]
. The key property of the class of càdlàg Lipschitz Markov processes is compactness with respect to convergence in finite dimensional distributions, as shown in
[
35
, Lemma 4.5]
. On the other hand, it is well known that the class of Markov martingales is not closed with respect to this mode of convergence; see, e.g.
[
3
, Example 1]
. All proofs of Kellerer’s theorem that are known to us make use of the compactness of Lipschitz Markov processes; see, e.g.
[
3
,
24
,
28
,
35
]
. In light of the result of
[
25
]
, the notion of Lipschitz Markovianity does not lend itself well to the higher-dimensional problem. In its place, we consider a class of Feller processes that are martingale Itô diffusions with particular properties. We show in
Theorem

1.2
that this set of processes is compact with respect to convergence in finite dimensional distributions.

We have seen that, in dimension one, uniqueness holds in the class of continuous strong Markov mimicking martingales when the marginals of the peacock have convex support.
Theorem

1.3
shows that strong Markovianity is not sufficient to guarantee uniqueness in higher dimensions, by exhibiting a continuous two-dimensional strong Markov martingale with Brownian marginals that is not itself a Brownian motion. The question of the existence of martingales distinct from Brownian motion that have Brownian marginals goes back to Hamza and Klebaner
[
21
]
, who showed that such a
fake Brownian motion
with discontinuous paths exists in one dimension. As already mentioned, the culmination of this one-dimensional investigation was the construction
[
5
]
of a continuous Markovian fake Brownian motion. Of course Brownian motion is the unique continuous
strong
Markov martingale with Brownian marginals in one dimension. In two dimensions however, we show in
Theorem

1.3
that there exists a fake Brownian motion that is continuous and strongly Markovian.

We remark that the mimicking martingale of
Theorem

1.1
is an Itô diffusion process with Markovian diffusion coefficient. Finding mimicking martingales of this form has also received extensive interest since the work of Krylov
[
29
]
and Gyöngy
[
20
]
. In fact we twice apply a more recent result of Brunick and Shreve
[
7
]
on mimicking Markovian diffusions in our construction in
Section

2
.

For a more detailed review of the existing literature, we refer the reader to the surveys of Hirsch, Roynette and Yor
[
24
]
and Beiglböck, Pammer and Schachermayer
[
4
]
, and the references therein.

The structure of the present article is as follows. In
Section

2
, we construct a strongly Markovian mimicking martingale Itô diffusion, thus proving
Theorem

1.1
. We then prove
Theorem

1.3
in
Section

3
, by providing a counterexample to uniqueness of mimicking martingales. We present further examples in
Section

4
, which show that existence may fail without regularization, thus proving
Theorem

1.4
. Finally, in
Section

5
, we prove the compactness result
Theorem

1.2
for martingale Itô diffusions, which is key to the proof of
Theorem

1.1
in
Section

2
.

We introduce some notation and terminology that will be used throughout the paper. The notation
|\cdot|
represents the Euclidean norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and
B_{R}
denotes the closed ball with radius
R>0
centered at the origin. We denote by
\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
the set of probability measures on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with finite second moment. We denote by
\mathcal{F}^{X}
the natural filtration of a stochastic process
X
, enlarged as necessary to satisfy the usual conditions. For measures
\mu,\nu
, we write
\mu\preceq\nu
to denote that
\mu
is dominated by
\nu
in convex order; i.e. for any convex function
f
,
\int f\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\int f\mathrm{d}\nu
. A family of measures
(\mu_{t})_{t\in I}
is called a

if it is increasing in the convex order.

.

2.
Construction of a mimicking martingale

Let
d\geq 2
and let
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
be a weakly continuous peacock in
\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
; i.e.
\mu_{t_{0}}\preceq\mu_{t_{1}}
for all
t_{0}\leq t_{1}
,
\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\int|x|^{2}\mu_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)=\int|x|^{2}\mu_{1}(\mathrm{d}x)<\infty
, and
t\mapsto\int f\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}
is continuous for any bounded continuous function
f
. Fix
\delta,\varepsilon>0
and define the regularized peacock
\mu^{\mathrm{r}}
by

(2.1)

\mu^{\mathrm{r}}_{t}=\mu_{t}\ast\gamma^{\varepsilon(t+\delta)},\quad t\in[0,1].

Note that the process
(\mu^{\mathrm{r}}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
is a peacock satisfying
\mu_{t}\preceq\mu^{\mathrm{r}}_{t}
, for all
t\in[0,1]
.

Remark 2.1
.

The relevant feature of the function

(2.2)

\varphi(t)=\varepsilon(t+\delta),\quad t\in[0,1],

is that
\phi(0)>0
and
t\mapsto\phi(t)
is strictly increasing. It will become clear from the construction below that we can replace (
2.2
) with any such function.

In this context, we also normalize the peacock
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
by making a deterministic time change so that
\int|x|^{2}\mu_{t+h}(\mathrm{d}x)-\int|x|^{2}\mu_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)=h
, for
t\in[0,1)
,
h>0
.
For convenience, we still take
\varphi
as in (
2.2
) after the time change.

Moreover, in place of the Gaussian family
(\gamma^{\varepsilon(t+\delta)})_{t\in[0,1]}
, one may take another family of centered probability measures
(\eta_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
that is weakly continuous and
strictly increasing
in convex order. Provided that these measures have smooth densities that are uniformly bounded from below on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, one could apply similar arguments to prove an analogue of
Theorem

1.1
. However, the proof would become significantly more involved.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and consider the dyadics
S^{n}\coloneqq\{2^{-n},2\cdot 2^{-n},\dotsc,2^{n}\cdot 2^{-n}\}\subseteq[0,1]
. For
k\in\{0,\dotsc,2^{n}\}
, denote
t^{n}_{k}\coloneqq k2^{-n}
. We will construct a martingale Itô diffusion that mimics
\mu^{\mathrm{r}}
on the dyadics
S^{n}
.
Theorem

5.4
will allow us to pass to a limit. We first construct martingale Itô diffusions on each dyadic interval, before concatenating these intervals. This step is rather standard; cf.
[
23
,
24
]
. For our purposes it is convenient to use the concept of
stretched Brownian motion
introduced in
[
1
]
. We now fix
k\in\{0,\dotsc,2^{n}-1\}
and consider the interval
[t^{n}_{k},t^{n}_{k+1})
. Recall that
B
denotes a standard Brownian motion on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
be a weakly continuous square-integrable peacock. Then there exists a strongly Markovian martingale diffusion
(\bar{M}^{n,k}_{t})_{t\in[t^{n}_{k},t^{n}_{k+1}]}
, with the representation

(2.3)

\mathrm{d}\bar{M}^{n,k}_{t}=\bar{\sigma}^{n,k}_{t}(\bar{M}^{n,k}_{t})\mathrm{d}B_{t},\quad\text{on}\quad[t^{n}_{k},t^{n}_{k+1}),

for some measurable function
(t,x)\mapsto\bar{\sigma}^{n,k}_{t}(x)
, taking values in the set of positive semidefinite matrices, such that
\mathrm{Law}(\bar{M}^{n,k}_{t^{n}_{k}})=\mu_{t^{n}_{k}}
and
\mathrm{Law}(\bar{M}^{n,k}_{t^{n}_{k+1}})=\mu_{t^{n}_{k+1}}
.

Proof.

Let
(\bar{M}^{n,k})_{t\in[t^{n}_{k},t^{n}_{k+1}]}
be the stretched Brownian motion with
\mathrm{Law}(\bar{M}^{n,k}_{t^{n}_{k}})=\mu_{t^{n}_{k}}
and
\mathrm{Law}(\tilde{M}^{n,k}_{t^{n}_{k+1}})=\mu_{t^{n}_{k+1}}
, as defined in
[
1
, Definition 1.6]
. By definition,
\bar{M}^{n,k}
is a martingale with the representation
\mathrm{d}\bar{M}^{n,k}_{t}=\theta^{n,k}_{t}\mathrm{d}B_{t}
, for some
\mathcal{F}^{B}
-predictable process
\theta^{n,k}
taking values in the set of positive semidefinite matrices. Moreover, by
[
1
, Corollary 2.5]
,
\bar{M}^{n,k}
is a strong Markov process. Thus
Lemma

A.2
gives the existence of a measurable function
\bar{\sigma}^{n,k}_{\cdot}\colon[t^{n}_{k},t^{n}_{k+1})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
such that (
2.3
) holds.
∎

We do not yet have a control on the matrix norm of
(\bar{\sigma}^{n,k}_{t}(\bar{M}^{n,k}))_{t\in[t^{n}_{k},t^{n}_{k+1}]}
. In order to achieve an upper bound on the diffusion matrix, we make a first convolution with a Gaussian. This will have an averaging effect and allow us to control the diffusion from above almost surely. Namely, we take a centered Gaussian random variable
\Gamma^{n,k}
with covariance matrix
(\varepsilon[t^{n}_{k}+\delta])\mathrm{id}
, independent of
\mathcal{F}^{B}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\bar{M}^{n,k}}
, and define

(2.4)

\tilde{M}^{n,k}_{t}\coloneqq\bar{M}^{n,k}_{t}+\Gamma^{n,k},\quad t\in[t^{n}_{k},t^{n}_{k+1}].

Then, for the initial law in this interval, we have
\mathrm{Law}(\tilde{M}^{n,k}_{t^{n}_{k}})=\mu_{t^{n}_{k}}\ast\gamma^{\varepsilon(t^{n}_{k}+\delta)}=\mu^{\mathrm{r}}_{t^{n}_{k}}
, and for the terminal law, we have the ordering
\mathrm{Law}(\tilde{M}^{n,k}_{t^{n}_{k+1}})=\mu_{t^{n}_{k+1}}\ast\gamma^{\varepsilon(t^{n}_{k}+\delta)}\preceq\mu_{t^{n}_{k+1}}\ast\gamma^{\varepsilon(t^{n}_{k+1}+\delta)}=\mu^{\mathrm{r}}_{t^{n}_{k+1}}
, where we recall the definition of
\mu^{\mathrm{r}}
from (
2.1
). Later we will make a second Gaussian convolution, which will allow us to also bound the squared diffusion matrix from below. We now prove that the square of the diffusion matrix obtained after the first convolution is locally bounded and locally Lipschitz.

Lemma 2.3
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\in\{0,\dotsc,2^{n}\}
and
\bar{\sigma}^{n,k}
as in (
2.3
), define the matrix-valued function
(t,x)\mapsto\tilde{\sigma}^{n,k}_{t}(x)
as the unique positive semidefinite square root of

(2.5)

\tilde{\sigma}^{n,k}_{t}(x)^{2}\coloneqq\frac{\int\bar{\sigma}^{n,k}_{t}(y)^{2}g^{n,k}(x-y)\bar{m}^{n,k}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y)}{\int g^{n,k}(x-y)\bar{m}^{n,k}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y)},\quad t\in[t^{n}_{k},t^{n}_{k+1}),x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

where
g^{n,k}
is the density of a Gaussian with mean zero and covariance matrix
(\varepsilon[t^{n}_{k}+\delta])\mathrm{id}
, and
\bar{m}^{n,k}_{t}=\mathrm{Law}(\bar{M}^{n,k}_{t})
.

Then for every compact set
K\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, there exist constants
C_{K},L_{K}
, independent of
t
,
k
and
n
, such that

(2.6)

\lVert\tilde{\sigma}^{n,k}_{t}(x)^{2}\rVert\leq C_{K},\quad(t,x)\in[t^{n}_{k},t^{n}_{k+1}]\times K,

and
x\mapsto\tilde{\sigma}^{n,k}_{t}(x)^{2}
is Lipschitz on
K
with Lipschitz constant
L_{K}
, for all
t\in[t^{n}_{k},t^{n}_{k+1}]
.

Proof.

Choose
R>0
such that
\int|x|\mathrm{d}(\mu_{1}\ast\gamma^{\varepsilon(1+\delta)})(x)\leq R/2
. Applying Doob’s maximal inequality and the convex ordering of the marginals gives the bound
\bar{m}^{n,k}_{t}(B_{R})\geq\frac{1}{2}
, for all
t\in[t^{n}_{k},t^{n}_{k+1}]
and all
k\in\{1,\dotsc,2^{n}-1\}
. Then, for an arbitrary compact set
K\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we can bound the normalising constant in the denominator of (
2.5
) by

\begin{split}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}g^{n,k}(x-y)\bar{m}^{n,k}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y)&\geq\int_{B_{R}}g^{n,k}(x-y)\bar{m}^{n,k}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y)\\
&\geq\frac{\bar{C}_{K}}{2}(2\pi\varepsilon[t^{n}_{k}+\delta])^{-\frac{d}{2}},\quad\text{for}\quad x\in K,\end{split}

where
\bar{C}_{K}\coloneqq\inf\!\left\{\exp\{-\varepsilon^{-1}\delta^{-1}\lvert x-y\rvert^{2}\}\colon\;x\in K,y\in B_{R}\right\}\!>0
, independent of
t
and
k
. We bound the numerator of (
2.5
) in the Hilbert–Schmidt norm by

\begin{split}\!\left\|\int\bar{\sigma}^{n,k}_{t}(y)^{2}g^{n,k}(x-y)\bar{m}^{n,k}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y)\right\rVert&\leq(2\pi\varepsilon[t^{n}_{k}+\delta])^{-\frac{d}{2}}{\mathbb{E}}[\lVert\bar{\sigma}^{n,k}_{t}(\bar{M}^{n,k}_{t})\rVert^{2}].\end{split}

Recall from
Remark

2.1
that
{\mathbb{E}}[\lvert\bar{M}^{n,k}_{t+h}\rvert^{2}-\lvert\bar{M}^{n,k}_{t}\rvert^{2}]=h
, for all
t\in[t^{n}_{k},t^{n}_{k+1})
and
h
sufficiently small. But, by the Itô isometry,

(2.7)

{\mathbb{E}}[\lvert\bar{M}^{n,k}_{t+h}\rvert^{2}-\lvert\bar{M}^{n,k}_{t}\rvert^{2}]=\int_{t}^{t+h}{\mathbb{E}}[\lVert\bar{\sigma}^{n,k}_{t}(\bar{M}^{n,k}_{t})\rVert^{2}]\mathrm{d}t,

and so
{\mathbb{E}}[\lVert\bar{\sigma}^{n,k}_{t}(\bar{M}^{n,k}_{t})\rVert^{2}]=1
, for all
t\in[t^{n}_{k},t^{n}_{k+1}]
. Altogether, we have the upper bound

(2.8)

\lVert\tilde{\sigma}^{n,k}_{t}(x)^{2}\rVert\leq\frac{2}{\bar{C}_{K}},\quad\text{for}\quad x\in K.

It remains to prove continuity. To save notation in the following calculation, we suppress the dependency on
n
and
k
, setting
g\coloneqq g^{n,k}
,
\bar{\sigma}\coloneqq\bar{\sigma}^{n,k}
,
\bar{m}\coloneqq\bar{m}^{n,k}
. Fix
t\in[t^{n}_{k},t^{n}_{k+1}]
and
x_{0},x_{1}\in K
, for some compact set
K\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Then, from the definition (
2.5
) and the bound (
2
), we calculate

\begin{split}&\!\left\lVert\tilde{\sigma}^{n,k}_{t}(x_{1})^{2}-\tilde{\sigma}^{n,k}_{t}(x_{0})^{2}\right\rVert\\
&\qquad\leq 4(2\pi\varepsilon[t^{n}_{k}+\delta])^{d}\bar{C}_{K}^{-2}\!\left\lVert\int g(x_{0}-y)\bar{m}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y)\int\bar{\sigma}_{t}(y)^{2}g(x_{1}-y)\bar{m}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y)\right.\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\!\left.-\int g(x_{1}-y)\bar{m}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y)\int\bar{\sigma}_{t}(y)^{2}g(x_{0}-y)\bar{m}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y)\right\rVert\\
&\qquad\leq 4(2\pi\varepsilon[t^{n}_{k}+\delta])^{d}\bar{C}_{K}^{-2}\!\left\lvert\int g(x_{0}-y)\bar{m}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y)\right\rvert\!\left\lVert\int\!\left[g(x_{1}-y)-g(x_{0}-y)\right]\!\bar{\sigma}_{t}(y)^{2}\bar{m}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y)\right\rVert\\
&\qquad\qquad+4(2\pi\varepsilon[t^{n}_{k}+\delta])^{d}\bar{C}_{K}^{-2}\!\left\lvert\int\!\left[g(x_{0}-y)-g(x_{1}-y)\right]\!\bar{m}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y)\right\rvert\!\left\lVert\int g(x_{0}-y)\bar{\sigma}_{t}(y)^{2}\bar{m}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y)\right\rVert.\end{split}

As in the proof of the upper bound, note that
\lVert\int\bar{\sigma}_{t}(y)^{2}\bar{m_{t}}(\mathrm{d}y)\rVert\leq 1
, by
Remark

2.1

3.
Non-uniqueness

We shall show that, even for the simple example of a two-dimensional Brownian motion, uniqueness does not hold in the class of continuous strong Markov mimicking martingales. In other words, the one-dimensional uniqueness result of Lowther
[
33
]
cannot be extended directly to higher dimensions.

Considering the problem of mimicking the marginals of a standard two-dimensional Brownian motion, the Brownian motion itself is of course a continuous strong Markov martingale with the required marginals. In order to disprove uniqueness, we seek another mimicking process with these properties. We will thus construct a two-dimensional continuous
fake Brownian motion
that is strongly Markovian.

Proposition 3.1
.

For every peacock
\mu=(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}

\mu

Let

(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}

(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}

For any

at time
t
a particle
X_{t}
starting either in
x=(x_{1},x_{2},0,0)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
or
x=(0,0,x_{3},x_{4})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
drifts in the direction
x
with speed
|x|^{2}
.
This drift is compensated with jumps of rate
\frac{1}{2|x|^{2}}
to a uniform distribution on
S^{2}_{|x|^{2}}
(resp.
S^{1}_{|x|^{2}}
).
For
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
define the rate function
\lambda
and its anti-derivative
\Lambda
by

(4.6)

\lambda_{t}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2t}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Lambda_{t}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\log(t).

To construct the process
X
, we first consider the peacock
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[t_{0},1]}
where
t_{0}\in(0,1)
and define a mimicking process
X^{t_{0}}
.
To this end, let
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
(U_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, and
(V_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be families of independent random variables such that
\xi_{n}\sim\exp(1)
,
U_{n}\sim\text{Unif}(S^{1}_{1})
, and
V_{n}\sim\text{Unif}(S^{2}_{1})
.
Given that
X_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}}=x\in S^{1}_{|x|^{2}}
,
U_{0}\coloneqq x/|x|
, and
t\in(t_{0},1]
, we set

(4.7)

X_{t}^{t_{0}}=\sqrt{t}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\xi_{k}\leq\Lambda_{t}-\Lambda_{t_{0}}<\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\xi_{k}\}}\!\left(\mathbbm{1}_{2\mathbb{Z}}(n)V_{n}+\mathbbm{1}_{2\mathbb{Z}+1}(n)U_{n}\right)\!,

where we use the convention that the sum over an empty index set is
-\infty
.
Similarly, when starting in
S^{2}_{|x|^{2}}
, we define
X_{t}^{t_{0}}
analogously to the displayed equation above but with the roles of odd and even integers reversed.
It is straightforward to show that this process is a Feller process and thus has the strong Markov property.

By
Lemma

B.1
, which we postpone to
Appendix

B
,
X^{t_{0}}
mimics
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[t_{0},1]}
and there exists a process
X
with the property that, for any
t_{1}\in(0,1]
,

(4.8)

(X_{t})_{t\in[t_{1},1]}\sim(X^{t_{1}}_{t})_{t\in[t_{1},1]}.

Hence
X
mimics
\mu
. We deduce from (
4.8
) and the strong Markov property of
X^{t_{1}}
that
X
has the strong Markov property for stopping times
\tau
with
\tau\geq t_{1}
and
t_{1}>0
. By
Lemma

B.2
, we also have that
X
is Markovian at time
0
. Hence, by Lemma
A.1
below,
X
is a strong Markov process.

Remark 4.5
.

Two referees, to whom we are indebted for their excellent remarks, have asked us to comment on the assumption that the usual conditions of right-continuity and completeness hold for the filtration
(\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
considered in
Proposition

4.2
. This example depends crucially on the assumption of right-continuity. Indeed, if we consider the natural filtration
(\mathcal{F}^{0}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
generated by the process
Y
constructed in the proof of
Proposition

4.2
without any augmentation, the sigma-algebra
\mathcal{F}^{0}_{0}
is trivial and the process
Y
is Markovian with respect to
\mathcal{F}^{0}
.

To address this issue, we start by referring to the one-dimensional result of Kellerer
[
28
]
. As noted in the introduction, the set of Markov martingales is not closed with respect to convergence in finite dimensional distributions. The original proof of
[
28
, Theorem 3]
therefore uses a stronger notion, which has subsequently been termed the
Lipschitz Markov
property. In fact Kellerer shows the existence of a one-dimensional Lipschitz Markov mimicking martingale. Lowther
[
35
, Lemma 4.5]
shows that the set of càdlàg Lipschitz Markov processes is closed with respect to convergence in finite dimensional distributions, and
[
35
, Lemma 4.2]
shows that a càdlàg Lipschitz Markov process has the strong Markov property with respect to the augmented filtration. In fact, as observed in
[
3
, Section 3.3]
, the Lipschitz Markov property implies the Feller property. Then, for a right continuous process, strong Markovianity with respect to the augmented filtration follows from Liggett
[
31
, Theorem 3.3]
. Moreover, the completed natural filtration of a Feller process is right-continuous. The notion of Lipschitz Markov martingales has been used in all known proofs of the one-dimensional Kellerer theorem; see, e.g.
[
3
,
24
]
. In the higher dimensional setting, the Lipschitz Markov property no longer holds in general. However, we argue that it is still natural to consider Feller processes.
Theorem

1.1
shows the existence of a mimicking martingale that is a Feller process in the regularised case, and
Propositions

4.2
,
4.6
and
4.7
give examples for which, in particular, no Feller mimicking martingale exists.

We next refer to the use of the usual conditions in the literature, which are now a standard assumption; see, e.g.
[
39
]
. The usual conditions, or
conditions habituelles
, of completeness and right-continuity of filtrations are fundamental to the
théorie générale
of semi-martingales developed by the Strasbourg school; see Dellacherie and Meyer
[
11
,
12
]
. In the context of Markov processes, Dellacherie and Meyer
[
13
]
work predominantly with processes satisfying the strong Markov and Feller properties, and they once again work under the usual conditions. These conditions were already present in the work of Blumenthal and Getoor
[
6
]
and Getoor
[
18
]
, where the authors consider strong Markov
right processes
; see also the later references of Dellacherie and Meyer
[
14
]
, Liggett
[
31
]
, and Sharpe
[
40
]
.

To give additional motivation for the use of the usual conditions, we finally put ourselves into the following financial context. Suppose that
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
models the price of a stock (or four stocks, as in
Proposition

4.2
) and an economic agent trades on the stock using predictable trading strategies, as described, for example, in the books
[
10
,
27
]
. Following the paradigm of
no arbitrage
, the process
X
must be a semi-martingale by
[
10
, Theorem 9.7.2]
. This places us in the setting of
[
12
]
described above, where the usual conditions are in force. An important role is played by the available information that is encoded in the filtration. Consider, for example, the announcement of some economic statistics that is revealed at a fixed time
t
(corresponding to
t=0
in the example of
Proposition

4.2
). We claim that the proper interpretation is that the agent may use the knowledge of these statistics from time
t
on, and not only from time
t+\varepsilon
for each
\varepsilon>0
. This point of view corresponds to the right continuity of the filtration. One may ask whether this gives too much information to the agent, since the stock prices
X_{t}
may be more favourable than
X_{t+\varepsilon}
for arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
. However, this difference in prices is negligible as
\varepsilon\to 0
, since semi-martingales are defined to be right-continuous; see
[
12
]
. Thus, the usual conditions fit well in this financial setting.

Nevertheless, for purely mathematical interest, one could and should of course ask whether it is possible to construct counterexamples of peacocks for which there exists no mimicking Markov martingale, when the Markov property is defined with respect to the raw filtration. We do not have an answer to this question, and we leave this as an open problem for future research.

4.2.
The general case

We now generalize the example given in
Proposition

4.2
to find a peacock for which there is no mimicking Markov martingale, even if we allow for jumps. We construct such a peacock by modifying the previous example in the following way. Let us partition the time interval
[0,1]
into the intervals
I_{1}\coloneqq\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathbb{N}\\
n\;\text{even}\end{subarray}}[2^{-(n+1)},2^{-n}]
,
I_{2}\coloneqq\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathbb{N}\\
n\;\text{odd}\end{subarray}}[2^{-(n+1)},2^{-n}]
. Define the functions

\begin{split}a_{1}(t)&=\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{I_{1}}(s)\mathrm{d}s,\quad a_{2}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{I_{2}}(s)\mathrm{d}s.\end{split}

Now time-change the processes
M^{1},M^{2}
from (
4.3
) to define a process
X
by

(4.9)

X_{t}=\begin{cases}\!\left(M^{1}_{a_{1}(t)},\;0\right)\!,\;&\xi=0,\\
\!\left(0,\;M^{2}_{a_{2}(t)}\right)\!,&\xi=1,\end{cases}\quad t\in[0,1],

and write
\mu_{t}=\mathrm{Law}(X_{t})
. Then, at time
t\in[0,1]
,
\mu_{t}
is the uniform measure on
S^{1}_{a_{1}(t)}\cup S^{2}_{a_{2}(t)}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
. Note that, for
t\in I_{1}
, the radius of
S^{1}_{a_{1}(t)}
is increasing deterministically at rate
\sqrt{t}
, while the radius of
S^{2}_{a_{2}(t)}
remains constant, with the roles reversed on the set of times
I_{2}
.

We will show that there is no Markov martingale mimicking
\mu
.

Proposition 4.6
.

There exists a peacock
\mu
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
such that there does not exist any
Markov martingale
mimicking
\mu
.

Proof.

Let
\mu_{t}=\mathrm{Law}(X_{t})
,
t\in[0,1]
, where
X
is defined by (
4.9
). Suppose that there exists a martingale
Y
mimicking
\mu
. As in
Proposition

4.2
, we will show that
Y
is not Markovian at time
0
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
even and
t_{0}\in[2^{-(n+1)},2^{-n})\subset I_{1}
. Then, for all
t\in[2^{-(n+1)},2^{-n}]
,
\mu_{t}
is supported on
S^{1}_{a_{1}(t)}\cup S^{2}_{a_{2}(t_{0})}
. Define a function
f\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{4}\to{\mathbb{R}}
by
f(x)=\sqrt{x_{3}^{2}+x_{4}^{2}}
, and note that
f
is convex. Also note that, for any
t\in[0,1]
,
f(x)=0
for
x\in S^{1}_{a_{1}(t)}
, and
f(x)=\sqrt{a_{2}(t)}
for
x\in S^{2}_{a_{2}(t)}
.
Let
t\in(t_{0},2^{-n}]
. Then, by convex ordering,

\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[f(Y_{t})\mid Y_{t_{0}}\in S^{2}_{a_{2}(t_{0})}\right]\!\geq{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[f(Y_{t_{0}})\mid Y_{t_{0}}\in S^{2}_{a_{2}(t_{0})}\right]\!&=\sqrt{a_{2}(t_{0})},\\
\text{and}\quad{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[f(Y_{t})\mid Y_{t_{0}}\in S^{1}_{a_{1}(t_{0})}\right]\!\geq{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[f(Y_{t_{0}})\mid Y_{t_{0}}\in S^{1}_{a_{1}(t_{0})}\right]\!&=0.\end{split}

We also have

\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}[f(Y_{t})]&=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{a_{2}(t)}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{a_{2}(t_{0})}={\mathbb{E}}[f(Y_{t_{0}})].\end{split}

Hence equality holds in each inequality in (
4.2
) and, in particular,

(4.10)

{\mathbb{P}}\!\left[Y_{t}\in S^{1}_{a_{1}(t_{0})}\mid Y_{t_{0}}\in S^{1}_{a_{1}(t_{0})}\right]\!=1.

This holds for any subinterval of
I_{1}
, and a symmetric argument applies to
I_{2}
.
Since the peacock
\mu
is weakly continuous, we can suppose that
Y
has càdlàg paths. Therefore

(4.11)

{\mathbb{P}}\!\left[Y_{t}\in S^{1}_{a_{1}(t)},\;\forall t\geq t_{0}\mid Y_{t_{0}}\in S^{1}_{a_{1}(t_{0})}\right]\!=1.

For
t\in(0,1]
, define
A^{1}_{t}\coloneqq\{Y_{s}\in S^{1}_{a_{1}(s)},\forall s\geq t\}
, and let
A^{1}\coloneqq\bigcap_{t>0}A^{1}_{t}
. Then, for each
t\in(0,1]
,
{\mathbb{P}}(A^{1}_{t})=1/2
, and so
{\mathbb{P}}(A^{1})=1/2
. Since
\sigma(Y_{0})
is trivial,
A^{1}\notin\sigma(Y_{0})
. On the other hand, we have
A^{1}_{t}\in\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}
. Hence
A^{1}\in\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{0}\coloneqq\bigcap_{s>0}\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{s}
.

We conclude as in
Proposition

4.2
. Observe that, with
f
defined as above, for any
t\in(0,1)
, we get

(4.12)

\frac{\sqrt{a_{2}(t)}}{2}={\mathbb{E}}[f(Y_{t})\mid\sigma(Y_{0})]\neq{\mathbb{E}}[f(Y_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{0}]=\begin{cases}0&\text{on}\;A^{1},\\
\sqrt{a_{2}(t)}&\text{on}\;\Omega\setminus A^{1}.\end{cases}

Hence
Y
is not Markovian at time
0
.
∎

4.3.
A partially regularized case

In this section, we present a final example, in which we regularize a peacock, which is defined similarly as in
Proposition

4.6
, by convolving the peacock at time
t\in[0,1]
with a centered Gaussian with covariance
t\,\mathrm{id}
. We will show that, even after such regularization, there exists no Markov martingale mimicking this peacock. Therefore, in order to guarantee existence of a mimicking Markov martingale, some further regularization is required, as in
Theorem

1.1
.

Throughout this section, we use the notation
\gamma^{\sigma}
for the
4
-dimensional centered Gaussian measure with covariance
\sigma\mathrm{id}
,
\sigma\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. Also let
\gamma^{0}
denote the Dirac measure at
0\in{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
, so that
\mu_{0}\ast\gamma^{0}=\mu_{0}
.

Proposition 4.7
.

There exists a peacock
\mu
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
such that there is no Markov martingale mimicking
\mu^{\mathrm{r}}
, which is defined by
\mu^{\mathrm{r}}_{t}\coloneqq\mu_{t}\ast\gamma^{t}
, for
t\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

Let
\nu_{t}=\mathrm{Law}(X_{t})
, where
X_{t}
is defined by (
4.9
), and define a regularized peacock
\nu^{\mathrm{r}}
by
\nu^{\mathrm{r}}_{t}\coloneqq\nu_{t}\ast\gamma^{t^{14}}
, for
t\in[0,1]
. Suppose that there exists a martingale
Y
mimicking
\nu^{\mathrm{r}}
. In the following, we will show that
Y
can not be Markovian at
0
. Finally, we will time-change
\nu
in order to find a peacock
\mu
such that any martingale mimicking
\mu^{\mathrm{r}}\coloneqq(\mu_{t}\ast\gamma^{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
is not Markovian.

Due to the convolution with a Gaussian
\nu^{\mathrm{r}}_{t}
is no longer concentrated on
S^{1}_{a_{1}(t)}\cup S^{2}_{a_{2}(t)}
; it rather has full support on
{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
, for all
t\in(0,1]
.
Since
Y
mimics
\nu^{\mathrm{r}}
we have, for each
t\in[0,1]
, that
\mathrm{Law}(Y_{t})=\mathrm{Law}(X_{t}+N_{t^{14}})
where
X_{t}\sim\text{Unif}(S^{1}_{a_{1}(t)}\cup S^{2}_{a_{2}(t)})
and
N_{t^{14}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,t^{14}\mathrm{id})
are independent. Note that, from the definitions of
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
, we can find constants
c,C>0
such that
c\,t\leq a_{i}(t)\leq C\,t
, for
t\in[0,1]
,
i=1,2
.
We also have the estimate

\begin{split}&{\mathbb{P}}[|N_{t^{14}}|\geq a]\leq\frac{t^{14}}{a^{2}},\quad a>0.\end{split}

Define the events
\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t}\coloneqq\{\exists x\in S^{i}_{a_{i}(t)}\colon\;|x-Y_{t}|<t\}
.
Using the independence of
X_{t}
and
N_{t^{14}}
, we have the bound

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}[\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t}]&\geq{\mathbb{P}}[X_{t}\in S^{1}_{a_{1}(t)},\,|N_{t^{14}}|<t]=\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{P}}[|N_{t^{14}}|<t]\geq\frac{1}{2}-\frac{t^{12}}{2}.\end{split}

On the other hand, note that

(4.13)

\{X_{t}\in S^{1}_{a_{1}(t)}\}=\{\exists x\in S^{1}_{a_{1}(t)}\colon\;|x-X_{t}|^{2}\leq a_{1}(t)\}.

Thus

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}[\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t}]&\leq t^{14}a_{1}(t)^{-1}{\mathbb{P}}[\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t};\,N_{t^{14}}\geq\sqrt{a_{1}(t)}]+{\mathbb{P}}[\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t};\,N_{t^{14}}\geq\sqrt{a_{1}(t)}]\\
&\leq c^{-1}t^{13}+{\mathbb{P}}[\exists x\in S^{1}_{a_{1}(t)}\colon\;|x-X_{t}|^{2}\leq a_{1}(t)]=c^{-1}t^{13}+\frac{1}{2}.\end{split}

Write
t_{k}\coloneqq 2^{-k}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
. By
Lemma

B.3
we have that,
for
t\in[0,1]
,

(4.14)

{\mathbb{P}}[\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{k}}\bigtriangleup\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{k+1}}]\leq Ct_{k},

where
A\bigtriangleup B\coloneqq(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)
denotes the symmetric difference between events
A
and
B
.
Therefore, for
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
m\geq n
, the bounds (
4.3
) and (
4.14
) imply

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}\!\left[\bigcap_{k=n}^{m}\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{k}}^{1}\right]\!&={\mathbb{P}}\!\left[\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{m}}^{1}\setminus\bigcup_{k=n}^{m-1}(\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{k}}^{1}\bigtriangleup\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{k+1}}^{1})\right]\!\geq{\mathbb{P}}[\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{m}}^{1}]-\sum_{k=n}^{m-1}{\mathbb{P}}[\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{k}}^{1}\bigtriangleup\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{k+1}}^{1}]\\
&\geq\frac{1}{2}-\frac{t_{m}^{12}}{2}-C\sum_{k=n}^{m-1}t_{k}\geq\frac{1}{2}-C\sum_{k=n}^{m}t_{k}\xrightarrow{m,n\to\infty}\frac{1}{2},\end{split}

since the sequence
(t_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable, and (
4.3
) gives

(4.15)

{\mathbb{P}}\!\left[\bigcap_{k=n}^{m}\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{k}}^{1}\right]\!\leq\inf_{n\leq k\leq m}{\mathbb{P}}[\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{k}}]\leq\frac{1}{2}+c^{-1}t_{m}^{13}\xrightarrow{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{2}.

Defining an increasing sequence of events
\mathcal{S}_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and its limit
\mathcal{S}
by

(4.16)

\mathcal{S}_{n}\coloneqq\bigcap_{k=n}^{\infty}\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{k}}^{1},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{S}\coloneqq\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{S}_{n},

we conclude that

(4.17)

{\mathbb{P}}[\mathcal{S}]=\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}[\mathcal{S}_{n}]=\frac{1}{2}.

Hence
\mathcal{S}\notin\sigma(Y_{0})
. However, we have that, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{k}}\in\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t_{k}}
, and so for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{S}_{n}\in\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t_{k}}
for all
k\geq n
. Hence
\mathcal{S}\in\bigcap_{k\geq n}\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t_{k}}=\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{0}
.

Now choose
k\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large that
{\mathbb{P}}[\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{k}]\leq 1/8
and
t_{k}\leq 1/8
.
Then we have

(4.18)

{\mathbb{P}}[\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{k}}\mid\mathcal{S}]={\mathbb{P}}[\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{k}}\cap\mathcal{S}]/{\mathbb{P}}[\mathcal{S}]\geq 2{\mathbb{P}}[\mathcal{S}_{k}\cap\mathcal{S}]=2({\mathbb{P}}[\mathcal{S}]-{\mathbb{P}}[\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{k}])\geq 2\!\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{8}\right)\!=\frac{3}{4},

while on the other hand,
{\mathbb{P}}[\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{k}}\mid\sigma(Y_{0})]\leq 1/2+t_{k}\leq 5/8<3/4
. Therefore
Y
cannot be Markovian at time
0
.

Now define a peacock
\mu
by a time-change of
\nu
such that
\mu_{t}\coloneqq\nu_{t^{14^{-1}}}
, and define a regularized peacock
\mu^{\mathrm{r}}
by
\mu^{\mathrm{r}}_{t}\coloneqq\mu_{t}\ast\gamma^{t}
,
t\in[0,1]
. Then, rescaling time by
t\mapsto t^{14^{-1}}
in all of the above arguments, we obtain the result that any martingale mimicking
\mu^{\mathrm{r}}
cannot be Markovian at time
0
.
∎

5.
Compactness of martingale Itô diffusions

In this section we prove a compactness result for martingale diffusions with respect to convergence in finite dimensional distributions (Theorem
5.4
).
We applied this result in the proof of
Theorem

1.1
in order to pass to a limit when constructing a mimicking martingale diffusion. This parallels the approach of Lowther
[
35
]
to the one-dimensional case.

Consider a sequence
(\sigma^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of positive semidefinite matrix-valued measurable functions
\sigma^{k}\colon[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
.
Let
\Sigma^{k}\colon[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
denote the integral

(5.1)

\Sigma^{k}_{t}(x)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}^{k}(x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Moreover, fix a sequence of initial distributions
(\mu_{0}^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and suppose that there exist weak solutions
(X^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of the SDEs

(5.2)

dX_{t}^{k}=\sigma^{k}_{t}(X_{t}^{k})\,\mathrm{d}B^{k}_{t},\quad\mbox{ with }X_{0}^{k}\sim\mu_{0}^{k},

where
B^{k}
denotes a standard
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
-valued Brownian motion, and
(X^{k},B^{k})
is defined on some probability space
(\Omega^{k},\mathcal{F}^{k},{\mathbb{P}}^{k})
, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
(\mu^{k}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
denote the marginal distributions of
X^{k}
, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

In the following we will use combinations of the following assumptions, which were satisfied in the setting of
Proposition

2.5
.

Assumption 5.1
.

(A1)

The map
x\mapsto\sigma^{k}_{t}(x)^{2}
is locally Lipschitz continuous, uniformly in
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in[0,1]
.

(A2)

For every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
the value of
\|\sigma^{k}_{t}(x)\|
is bounded, uniformly in
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in[0,1]
.

(A3)

The family of random variables
\{|X^{k}_{1}|^{2}\colon k\in\mathbb{N}\}
is uniformly integrable.

(A4)

The matrix
\sigma^{k}_{t}(x)^{2}
is positive definite with eigenvalues bounded away from zero, locally in
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, uniformly in
t\in\bigcup_{j=0}^{2^{k}-1}[j2^{-k},j2^{-k}+2^{-k-1}],
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

(A5)

The set of initial distributions
\{\mu_{0}^{k}\colon k\in\mathbb{N}\}
converges to
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

Remark 5.2
.

Due to
Items

(A1)
and
(A2)
and the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem, we can assume without loss of generality, by passing to subsequences, that
(\Sigma^{k}_{t}(x))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges for every
(t,x)\in[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Remark 5.3

(continuity of matrix square root)
.

Suppose that, for some domain

\pi^{n}_{m,k-1}(S_{t^{m}_{k-1}}^{m})
.
The discrete-time jump process
Z^{m}_{t}\coloneqq\sum_{l=1}^{2^{m}}\mathds{1}_{[0,t]}(t^{m}_{l})(S_{t^{m}_{l}}^{m}-S_{t^{m}_{l}-}^{m})
is a martingale in the underlying filtration and
\tilde{S}^{m}\coloneqq S^{m}-Z^{m}
is a continuous martingale.
Indeed

(5.24)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[Z^{m}_{\hat{t}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\!

\displaystyle=Z_{t}^{m}+\sum_{l=1}^{2^{m}}\mathds{1}_{(t,\hat{t}]}(t^{m}_{l})\mathbb{E}\!\left[S_{t^{m}_{l}}^{m}-S_{t^{m}_{l}-}^{m}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\!

(5.25)

\displaystyle=Z_{t}^{m}+\sum_{l=1}^{2^{m}}\mathds{1}_{(t,\hat{t}]}(t^{m}_{l})\mathbb{E}\!\left[S_{t^{m}_{l-1}}^{m}-S_{t^{m}_{l-1}}^{m}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\!=Z_{t}^{m}.

Moreover, by (
5.22
),
Z^{m}
admits the following estimate

(5.26)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[|Z^{m}_{1}|^{2}\right]\!^{\frac{1}{2}}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{k=0}^{2^{m}-1}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\!\left(\pi_{m,k}(X^{n(m)}_{t^{m}_{k}}),\pi^{n}_{m,k}(X_{t^{m}_{k}}^{n(m)})\right)\!\right]\!^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\tilde{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}2^{-\frac{m}{4}},

whence, by Doob’s maximal inequality, the term
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|Z^{m}_{t}|]
also vanishes as
m\to\infty
.
We claim that
(\tilde{S}^{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Cauchy sequence.
Indeed, using the Lipschitz property of
\sigma
and the bound (
5.21
), for
m,\hat{m}\in\mathbb{N}
,
\hat{m}\geq m
we have

(5.27)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[|\tilde{S}^{m}_{t}-\tilde{S}^{\hat{m}}_{t}|^{2}\right]\!

\displaystyle\leq 2\!\left(\mathbb{E}\!\left[|X^{n(m)}_{0}-X^{n(\hat{m})}_{0}|^{2}\right]\!+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{0}^{t}|\sigma_{t}(S^{m}_{\hat{t}})-\sigma_{t}(S^{\hat{m}}_{\hat{t}})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\hat{t}\right]\!\right)\!

(5.28)

\displaystyle\leq 2\!\left(\mathbb{E}\!\left[|X^{n(m)}_{0}-X^{n(\hat{m})}_{0}|^{2}\right]\!+\tilde{L}^{2}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{0}^{t}|S^{m}_{\hat{t}}-S^{\hat{m}}_{\hat{t}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\hat{t}\right]\!\right)\!

(5.29)

\displaystyle\leq 2\!\left(\mathbb{E}\!\left[|X^{n(m)}_{0}-X^{n(\hat{m})}_{0}|^{2}\right]\!+3\tilde{L}^{2}\!\left(\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{0}^{t}|\tilde{S}^{m}_{\hat{t}}-\tilde{S}^{\hat{m}}_{\hat{t}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\hat{t}\right]\!+\tilde{L}^{2}C^{2}2^{1-2m}\right)\!\right)\!.

By Grönwall’s lemma, we have that
(\tilde{S}^{m}_{1})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
is an
L^{2}
-Cauchy sequence.
Therefore, there exists a continuous
L^{2}
-martingale

such that
(\tilde{S}^{m}_{1})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in

to
S_{1}
.
As

vanishes uniformly, we get

(5.30)

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|S^{m}_{t}-S_{t}|^{2}\right]\!=0.

Since
S^{m}_{t}\sim\mu_{t}
for
t\in\{0,2^{-m},\ldots,1\}
, we have by continuity of
t\mapsto\mu_{t}
that
S_{t}\sim\mu_{t}
for every
t\in[0,1]
.
By the Lipschitz property of
\sigma
, for any
t\in[0,1]
, we find that

(5.31)

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{0}^{t}|\sigma_{t}(S^{m}_{s})-\sigma_{t}(S_{s})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\right]\!=0.

Thus, for any
t\in[0,1]
, the Itô isometry yields

(5.32)

\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}(S_{s})\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}=\lim_{m\to\infty}\int\sigma_{s}(S^{m}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}=\lim_{m\to\infty}\tilde{S}^{m}_{t}-X^{n(m)}_{0}=S_{t}-S_{0},

and so
S
is the unique strong solution of
dS_{t}=\sigma_{t}(S_{t})\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}
, with
S_{0}\sim\mu_{0}
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and
\hat{m}\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then there exists
M\geq\hat{m}
such that for all

(5.33)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|S^{m}_{t}-S_{t}|\right]\!<\varepsilon.

Recall that by construction we have
(S^{m}_{0},S^{m}_{t^{\hat{m}}_{1}},\ldots,S^{m}_{1})=(X^{n(m)}_{0},X^{n(m)}_{t^{\hat{m}}_{1}},\ldots,X^{n(m)}_{1})
in law, from which we deduce that

converges in finite dimensional distributions to

.

Finally, to see uniqueness of the limit, note that by Lemma

the sequence

is precompact with respect to convergence in finite dimensional distributions.
By the first part of the proof any subsequence of

admits a subsequence that converges in finite dimensional distributions to

.
Hence, we conclude that

is the unique limit by recalling that the finite dimensional distributions separate points on the space of probability measures on the Skorohod space

∎

Having established

in order to complete the proof of

.

Proof of Theorem

.

In order to apply Proposition

, for any radius

by

\sigma_{t}^{n,R}(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\sigma_{t}^{n}(x)&|x|\leq R,\\
\sigma_{t}^{n}\!\left(R\frac{x}{|x|}\right)\!&\text{else}.\end{cases}

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let

and define a process

by

Then

is a weak solution of the SDE

with

.
The map

is both Lipschitz continuous and bounded, uniformly in
(t,n)\in[0,1]\times\mathbb{N}
. By
Item

(A3)
,
C\coloneqq\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}[|X^{n}_{1}|^{2}]<\infty
, and thus by (

) and Doob’s martingale inequality,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|X^{n,R}_{t}-X^{n}_{t}|\wedge 1\right]\!\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|X^{n}_{t}|\geq R\right)\!\leq\frac{C}{R^{2}}.

Moreover,
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}{\mathbb{E}}[|X^{n,R}_{1}|^{2}]\leq C
.

We see that the conditions of
Proposition

5.8
are fulfilled, and thus there exists a diffusion coefficient
\sigma^{R}
such that the SDE

admits a unique strong solution

with
X^{R}_{0}\sim\mu_{0}
, and

converges in finite dimensional distributions to

.
Observe that, for

, the corresponding diffusion coefficients are compatible, in the sense that

(5.37)

\sigma^{R}_{t}(x)=\sigma^{R^{\prime}}_{t}(x)\quad\mbox{for every }x\in B_{R}\mbox{ and almost every }t\in[0,1].

Defining
\sigma_{t}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{R=0}^{\infty}\mathds{1}_{[R,R+1)}(|x|)\sigma_{t}^{R}(x)
, the properties of

given by

5.8
imply that
\sigma^{2}
is locally Lipschitz continuous, uniformly in

, and that, for each

such that

, for
t\in[0,1]
. In particular,
\sigma
is locally Lipschitz continuous, uniformly in

, by
Remark

5.3
. Skorohod

shows that weak existence and pathwise uniqueness hold for the SDE

. Therefore there exists a unique strong solution

[

, Corollary 1]
.
Defining
\tau^{R}\coloneqq\inf\{t>0\colon|X^{R}_{t}|\geq R\}
and

, pathwise uniqueness implies that, almost surely,

and

X^{R}_{t\wedge\tau^{R}}=X_{t\wedge\tau^{R}},\quad\text{for all }t\in[0,1].

Thus, by the continuity of the paths of

, the convergence of

, and Doob’s martingale inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|X_{t}|>R\right)\!

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|X_{t}^{R}|>R\right)\!\leq\sup_{I\subseteq\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sup_{t\in I}|X_{t}^{R}|>R\right)\!

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{I\subseteq\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]}\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sup_{t\in I}|X_{t}^{n,R}|>R\right)\!\leq\frac{C}{R^{2}}.

Therefore, similarly to (

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|X^{R}_{t}-X_{t}|\wedge 1\right]\!\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|X_{t}|\geq R\right)\!\leq\frac{C}{(R-1)^{2}}.

For any finite subset

, (

) imply that

(5.42)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{t\in I}|X_{t}^{n}-X_{t}|\wedge 1\right]\!

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|X_{t}^{n}-X_{t}^{n,R}|\wedge 1+\sup_{t\in I}|X_{t}^{n,R}-X_{t}^{R}|+\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|X_{t}^{R}-X_{t}|\wedge 1\right]\!

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\frac{2C}{(R-1)^{2}}+\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{t\in I}|X_{t}^{n,R}-X_{t}^{R}|\wedge 1\right]\!=\frac{2C}{(R-1)^{2}},

using convergence of

in finite dimensional distributions to

. Taking

converges to

in finite dimensional distributions.
∎

Appendix A
The Markov and strong Markov property

For the definitions and properties of Markov and strong Markov processes, we refer to
[
6
,
26
]
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
be a càdlàg process on a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},{\mathbb{P}},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]})
under the usual conditions.
Suppose that
X
satisfies the Markov property for all times
t\in[0,1]
and satisfies the strong Markov property for all
\varepsilon>0
and all finite stopping times
\tau\geq\varepsilon>0
. Then
X
is a strong Markov process.

Proof.

Let
g\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be measurable and bounded,
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, and fix
\varepsilon>0
.
For a given finite stopping time
\tau
,
we consider the event
A_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\{\tau\leq\varepsilon\}\in\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}
, and define the finite stopping time

(A.1)

\tau_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\varepsilon\mathbbm{1}_{A_{\varepsilon}}+\tau\mathbbm{1}_{A_{\varepsilon}^{\textrm{c}}}\geq\varepsilon.

In the following, all equalities should be understood
{\mathbb{P}}
-almost surely. On the event
A_{\varepsilon}^{\textrm{c}}
, we have by assumption that

(A.2)

{\mathbb{E}}[g(X_{\tau+t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{\tau}]={\mathbb{E}}[g(X_{\tau_{\varepsilon}+t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{\varepsilon}}]={\mathbb{E}}[g(X_{\tau_{\varepsilon}+t})\mid\sigma(X_{\tau_{\varepsilon}})]={\mathbb{E}}[g(X_{\tau+t})\mid\sigma(X_{\tau})].

In particular, since
A^{\textrm{c}}_{\varepsilon}\nearrow\{\tau>0\}
as
\varepsilon\searrow 0
, we find that

{\mathbb{E}}[g(X_{\tau+t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{\tau}]={\mathbb{E}}[g(X_{\tau+t})\mid\sigma(X_{\tau})]
on
\{\tau>0\}
.
On the other hand, we have by the Markov property of
X
that
{\mathbb{E}}[g(X_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{0}]={\mathbb{E}}[g(X_{t})\mid\sigma(X_{0})]
.
Combining these two observations yields that, for all finite stopping times
\tau
,

(A.3)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[g(X_{\tau})\mid\mathcal{F}_{\tau}]

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau=0\}}{\mathbb{E}}[g(X_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{0}]+\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau>0\}}{\mathbb{E}}[g(X_{\tau+t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{\tau}]

(A.4)

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau=0\}}{\mathbb{E}}[g(X_{t})\mid\sigma(X_{0})]+\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau>0\}}{\mathbb{E}}[g(X_{\tau+t})\mid\sigma(X_{\tau})]

(A.5)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[g(X_{\tau+t})\mid\sigma(X_{\tau})].

Hence
X
is a strong Markov process.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
B
be a standard Brownian motion on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
\tilde{\sigma}
an
\mathcal{F}^{B}
-predictable process taking values in the set of positive semidefinite matrices. Let
X
be a square-integrable Itô process satisfying
\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\tilde{\sigma}_{t}\mathrm{d}B_{t}
for
t\in[0,1]
. If
X
is a Markov process, then there exists a measurable function
\sigma\colon[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
taking values in the set of positive semidefinite matrices such that

(A.6)

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\sigma_{t}(X_{t})\mathrm{d}B_{t},\quad t\in[0,1].

Proof.

Fix
s\in[0,1]
. Then, for any
t\in[s,1]
,

(A.7)

{\mathbb{E}}[|X_{t}|^{2}-|X_{s}|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}^{X}_{s}]={\mathbb{E}}[|X_{t}|^{2}-|X_{s}|^{2}\mid\sigma(X_{s})],

almost surely, by the Markov property. Thus

\begin{split}\tilde{\sigma}_{s}^{2}&=\lim_{t\searrow s}\frac{1}{t-s}{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\int_{s}^{t}\tilde{\sigma}_{r}^{2}\mathrm{d}r\mid\mathcal{F}^{X}_{s}\right]\!=\lim_{t\searrow s}\frac{1}{t-s}{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\int_{s}^{t}\tilde{\sigma}_{r}^{2}\mathrm{d}r\mid\sigma(X_{s})\right]\!,\end{split}

almost surely. Hence
\tilde{\sigma}_{s}
is almost surely adapted to the sigma-algebra generated by
X_{s}
and the representation (
A.6
) holds.
∎

we get the lower bound

(B.14)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[|Y_{t_{0}}^{2}|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{0}}^{2}}]

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{E}}[|X_{t_{0}}+N_{t_{0}^{14}}|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{0}}^{2}};|N_{t_{0}^{14}}|<t_{0}^{2}]

(B.15)

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{E}}[|X_{t_{0}}+N_{t_{0}^{14}}|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{0}}^{2}}]-{\mathbb{E}}[|X_{t_{0}}+N_{t_{0}^{14}}|^{2};|N_{t_{0}^{14}}|\geq t_{0}^{2}]

(B.16)

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{E}}[|X_{t_{0}}|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{0}}^{2}}]-{\mathbb{E}}[|X_{t_{0}}|^{2}+|N_{t_{0}^{14}}|^{2};|N_{t_{0}^{14}}|\geq t_{0}^{2}],

using the independence of
X_{t_{0}}
and
N_{t_{0}^{14}}
. We use this independence again, together with the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the estimates (
B
), to show that

(B.17)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[|Y_{t_{0}}^{2}|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{0}}^{2}}]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}a_{2}(t_{0})-{\mathbb{E}}[|X_{t_{0}}|^{2}+|N_{t_{0}^{14}}|^{2};|N_{t_{0}^{14}}|\geq t_{0}^{2}]

(B.18)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}a_{2}(t_{0})-\!\left({\mathbb{P}}[|N_{t_{0}^{14}}|\geq t_{0}^{2}]{\mathbb{E}}[|X_{t_{0}}|^{4}+|N_{t_{0}^{14}}|^{4}]\right)\!^{\frac{1}{2}}

(B.19)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}a_{2}(t_{0})-Ct_{0}^{6}.

Recalling that
a_{2}
is constant on
[t_{0},t_{1}]
, (
B.13
) implies that
{\mathbb{E}}[|Y_{t_{1}}|^{2}]\leq\frac{1}{2}a_{2}(t_{0})+t_{1}^{14}
.
Since
Y
is a martingale we have

(B.20)

{\mathbb{E}}[|Y_{t_{1}}^{2}-Y^{2}_{t_{0}}|^{2}]={\mathbb{E}}[|Y_{t_{1}}^{2}|^{2}-|Y_{t_{0}}^{2}|^{2}]\leq{\mathbb{E}}[|Y_{t_{1}}^{2}|^{2}]-{\mathbb{E}}[|Y_{t_{0}}^{2}|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{0}}^{2}}].

Combining the upper and lower bounds above gives us

(B.21)

{\mathbb{E}}[|Y_{t_{1}}^{2}-Y^{2}_{t_{0}}|^{2}]\leq\frac{1}{2}a_{2}(t_{0})+t_{1}^{14}-\!\left(\frac{1}{2}a_{2}(t_{0})-Ct_{0}^{6}\right)\!\leq C_{0}t_{0}^{6}.

for some
C_{0}>0
.
By Doob’s maximal inequality we obtain

(B.22)

{\mathbb{P}}[\sup_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}|Y^{2}_{t}-Y_{t_{0}}^{2}|\geq t_{0}/2]\leq 4C_{0}t^{4}_{0}.

Next, we prove a similar bound to (
B.21
) when
i=1
. We claim that, for some constant
C_{1}>0
,

(B.23)

{\mathbb{E}}[|Y_{t_{1}}^{1}-Y_{t_{0}}^{1}|^{2};(\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{0}})^{\textrm{c}}]\leq C_{1}t_{0}^{3}.

Since
Y
is a martingale, we have

\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}[|Y_{t_{1}}^{1}-Y_{t_{0}}^{1}|^{2};(\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{0}})^{\textrm{c}}]&={\mathbb{E}}[|Y^{1}_{t_{1}}|^{2}-|Y^{1}_{t_{0}}|^{2};(\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{0}})^{\textrm{c}}]\leq{\mathbb{E}}[|Y^{1}_{t_{1}}|^{2};(\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{0}})^{\textrm{c}}]\\
&\leq{\mathbb{E}}[|Y^{1}_{t_{1}}|^{2};(\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{0}}\cup\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{2}_{t_{0}})^{\textrm{c}}]+{\mathbb{E}}[|Y^{1}_{t_{1}}|^{2};\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{2}_{t_{0}}]\end{split}

We estimate each term separately, using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the estimates (
B
) in each case. We first bound

(B.24)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[|Y_{t_{1}}^{1}|^{2};(\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{0}}\cup\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{2}_{t_{0}})^{\textrm{c}}]\leq{\mathbb{E}}[|Y_{t_{1}}|^{4}]^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbb{P}}[(\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{0}}\cup\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{2}_{t_{0}})^{\textrm{c}}]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq Ct_{1}{\mathbb{P}}[|N_{t^{14}_{0}}|\geq t_{0}^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C_{2}t_{0}^{6}.

To bound the second term, we additionally apply (
B.22
) to get

(B.25)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[|Y_{t_{1}}^{1}|^{2};\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{2}_{t_{0}}]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[|Y_{t_{1}}^{1}|^{2};\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{2}_{t_{0}},\;|Y^{2}_{t_{1}}-Y^{2}_{t_{0}}|\leq t_{0}/2]+{\mathbb{E}}[|Y_{t_{1}}^{1}|^{2};|Y^{2}_{t_{1}}-Y^{2}_{t_{0}}|\geq t_{0}/2]

(B.26)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}[|Y_{t_{1}}^{1}|^{2};(\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{1}})^{\textrm{c}}]+\!\left({\mathbb{E}}[|Y_{t_{1}}|^{4}]{\mathbb{P}}[|Y_{t_{1}}^{2}-Y_{t_{0}}^{2}|\geq t_{0}/2]\right)\!^{\frac{1}{2}}

(B.27)

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}t_{0}^{6}+\!\left(Ct_{0}^{2}\cdot 4C_{0}t_{0}^{4}\right)\!^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C_{3}t_{0}^{3}.

Combining the two preceding inequalities yields (
B.23
).
As before, Doob’s maximal inequality implies

(B.28)

{\mathbb{P}}[\sup_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}|Y_{t}^{1}-Y_{t_{0}}^{1}|\geq t_{0}/4;\;(\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{0}})^{\textrm{c}}]\leq 16C_{1}t_{0}.

Note that, for
i=1,2
,

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}[\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{i}_{t_{0}}\bigtriangleup\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{i}_{t_{0}}]&\leq{\mathbb{P}}[\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{i}_{t_{0}}\bigtriangleup\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{i}_{t_{1}}]+{\mathbb{P}}[\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{i}_{t_{0}}\setminus(\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{i}_{t_{0}}\cup\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{i}_{t_{1}})],\end{split}

and

(B.29)

{\mathbb{P}}[\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{i}_{t_{0}}\setminus(\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{i}_{t_{0}}\cup\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{i}_{t_{1}})]={\mathbb{P}}[t_{0}^{2}\leq|N_{t_{0}^{14}}|<t_{0},\;|N_{t_{1}^{14}}|\geq t_{1}]\leq t_{0}^{10}.

Hence, to prove the conclusion of the lemma, we only require bounds on
{\mathbb{P}}[\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{i}_{t_{0}}\bigtriangleup\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{i}_{t_{1}}]
, for
i=1,2
.

Now consider the events

(B.30)

A_{t_{0}}\coloneqq\{|Y_{t_{1}}^{2}-Y_{t_{0}}^{2}|\leq t_{0}/4\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad B_{t_{0}}\coloneqq\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{2}_{t_{0}}\cap\{|Y_{t_{1}}^{1}-Y_{t_{0}}^{1}|\leq t_{0}/4\},

and observe that (
B.22
) and (
B.28
) imply that

(B.31)

{\mathbb{P}}[A_{t_{0}}^{\textrm{c}}]\leq 16C_{0}t_{0}^{4}\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\mathbb{P}}[\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{2}_{t_{0}}\setminus B_{t_{0}}]\leq 16C_{1}t_{0}.

We calculate that

(B.32)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}[\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{0}}^{1}\bigtriangleup\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{1}}^{1}]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}[\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{0}}^{1}\setminus(\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{0}}^{1}\cap\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{1}}^{1})]+{\mathbb{P}}[\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{1}}^{1}\setminus(\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{0}}^{1}\cap\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{1}}^{1})]

(B.33)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}[\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{0}}]+{\mathbb{P}}[\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{1}}]-2{\mathbb{P}}[\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{0}}\cap\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{1}}]

(B.34)

\displaystyle\leq 2({\mathbb{P}}[\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{1}}^{1}]-{\mathbb{P}}[\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{0}}^{1}\cap\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{1}}^{1}])+t_{0}^{10},

since
{\mathbb{P}}[\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{1}}]\geq{\mathbb{P}}[\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{0}},|N_{t_{0}^{14}}|<t_{0}^{2}]\geq{\mathbb{P}}[\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{0}}]-t_{0}^{10}
.
Note that we have the inclusions

(B.35)

(\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{0}}^{1})^{\textrm{c}}\cap\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{1}}^{1}\subseteq(\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{0}}\cup\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{2}_{t_{0}})^{\textrm{c}}\cup(\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{0}}^{2}\cap\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{1}}^{1}),\quad\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{2}_{t_{0}}\cap\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{1}}\subseteq A_{t_{0}}^{\textrm{c}}.

Hence

(B.36)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}[\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{0}}^{1}\bigtriangleup\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{1}}^{1}]\leq 2{\mathbb{P}}[(\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{0}})^{\textrm{c}}\cap\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{1}_{t_{1}}]+t_{0}^{10}

\displaystyle\leq 2{\mathbb{P}}[(\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{0}}^{1}\cup\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{0}}^{2})^{\textrm{c}}\cup(\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{0}}^{2}\cap\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{1}}^{1})]+t_{0}^{10}

(B.37)

\displaystyle\leq 2{\mathbb{P}}[|N_{t^{14}}|\geq t_{0}^{2}]+2{\mathbb{P}}[A_{t_{0}}^{\textrm{c}}]+t_{0}

(B.38)

\displaystyle\leq 3t_{0}^{10}+32C_{0}t_{0}^{4}.

On the other hand, we have

(B.39)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}[\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{0}}^{2}\bigtriangleup\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{t_{1}}^{2}]\leq{\mathbb{P}}[\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{2}_{t_{0}}\setminus B_{t_{0}}]+{\mathbb{P}}[|N_{t_{0}^{14}}|\geq t_{0}]\leq Ct_{0}.

Thus we have shown the claim in the case that
n
is even. Due to symmetry of the arguments we also obtain the desired result when
n
is odd. ∎
