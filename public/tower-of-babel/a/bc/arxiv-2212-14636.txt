The suprema of selector processes with the application to positive infinitely divisible processes The authors were supported by National Science Centre, Poland grants 2019/35/B/ST1/04292 (W. Bednorz) and 2021/40/C/ST1/00330 (R. Meller).
Witold Bednorz, Rafał Martynek, Rafał Meller

Abstract
We provide an alternative proof of the recent result by Park and Pham (2022) on the expected suprema of positive selector and empirical processes. We extend it to positive infinitely divisible processes. Keywords: expected suprema, positive processes, p p -smallness. AMS MSC 2010: 60G15, 60G17.



1
Introduction

The recent results (see
[
8
]
,
[
2
]
) show that the size of expected suprema of empirical processes processes as well as of infinitely divisible processes can be explained by decomposing the initial index set into a sum of two new index sets. The size of the process defined on one of them is characterized with Talagrand’s gamma numbers while the process defined on the other index set is a positive process. The understanding of the size of positive processes was missing and some conjectures were posed by Talagrand. Recently, some of them were discussed and proved in
[
4
]
.

The general idea is to relate the size of the stochastic process with the geometry of the index set. This gives a rise to the very efficient method of chaining and in turn the characterization in terms of gamma functionals. The best known result in this area is the Majorizing Measure Theorem (see e.g.
[
8
, Theorem 2.10.1]
) concerning Gaussian processes. One of the geometric consequences of this result is that the construction of the partition provides also in some sense ”witnesses” of the size of the supremum. Let us briefly explain this concept (cf. discussion after Theorem 2.12.2 and Section 13.2 in
[
8
]
). Let
(G_{t})_{t\in T}
be a Gaussian process on some index set
T
. By the Majorizing Measure Theorem one can find a jointly Gaussian sequence
(u_{k})_{k\geqslant 1}
such that for some universal constant
L>0
it holds that

\left\{\sup_{t\in T}|G_{t}|\geqslant L\mathbf{E}\sup_{t\in T}|G_{t}|\right\}\subset\left\{u_{k}\geqslant 1\right\}

and

\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\mathbf{P}(u_{k}\geqslant 1)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}.

Let us emphasize that the above statement is weaker than the Majorizing Measure Theorem in the sense that it follows relatively easy from it however the other direction is not clear at all. Now, since chaining seems out of reach in the case when in the considered random series of functions we have no cancellations i.e. in the case of positive processes, the goal is to provide a characterization in the spirit of the above display.

The overall strategy for the proof is to construct the cover of
{\cal F}
based on the class of bad sets with fixed cardinality and show that its measure is appropriately small. To achieve this goal we need sets closely related to so-called fragments (cf.

Let

I_{j}=\{i_{l}\in I,\;1\leqslant l\leqslant j:\;\;\mu_{I}(i_{1})\geqslant\mu_{I}(i_{2})\geqslant\dots\geqslant\mu_{I}(i_{j})\},

Lemma 3.5
.

Let

be
c
-bad for some

Then, for any
I\in{\cal F}
there exists a number

such that

|I_{j(I,X)}\cap X|<c|I_{j(I,X)}|.

(11)

Proof.

Assume that
X
is non-empty, otherwise there is nothing to prove. Fix
I\in{\cal F}
. For
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
consider a mapping

\varepsilon\mapsto\sum_{i\in I\cap X}\mu_{I}(i)\wedge\varepsilon-c\sum_{i\in I}\mu_{I}(i)\wedge\varepsilon

and observe that it is continous. Moreover, for
\varepsilon=1
we have by (

\sum_{i\in I\cap X}\mu_{I}(i)-c\sum_{i\in I}\mu_{I}(i)<c-c\mu_{I}(I)=c-c=0

and obviously
\varepsilon=0
is mapped to

\varepsilon(I,X)\in[0,1)
such that

\sum_{i\in I\cap X}\mu_{I}(i)\wedge\varepsilon(I,X)\geqslant c\sum_{i\in I}\mu_{I}(i)\wedge\varepsilon(I,X)

(12)

and for
\varepsilon>\varepsilon(I,X)
the opposite inequality holds. Now, observe that for sufficiently small
\delta>0
we can write

\sum_{i\in I\cap X}\mu_{I}(i)\wedge(\varepsilon(I,X)+\delta)=\sum_{i\in I\cap X}\mu_{I}(i)\wedge\varepsilon(I,X)+\delta\sum_{i\in I\cap X}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{\mu_{I}(i)>\varepsilon(I,X)\}}

and

\sum_{i\in I}\mu_{I}(i)\wedge(\varepsilon(I,X)+\delta)=\sum_{i\in I}\mu_{I}(i)\wedge\varepsilon(I,X)+\delta\sum_{i\in I}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{\mu_{I}(i)>\varepsilon(I,X)\}}.

We combine the last two inequalities to get that

\sum_{i\in I\cap X}\mu_{I}(i)\wedge\varepsilon(I,X)+\delta\sum_{i\in I\cap X}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{\mu_{I}(i)>\varepsilon(I,X)\}}<c\sum_{i\in I}\mu_{I}(i)\wedge\varepsilon(I,X)+c\delta\sum_{i\in I}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{\mu_{I}(i)>\varepsilon(I,X)\}},

but because of (
12
) it follows that

\sum_{i\in I\cap X}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{\mu_{I}(i)>\varepsilon(I,X)\}}<c\sum_{i\in I}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{\mu_{I}(i)>\varepsilon(I,X)\}}.

Let

\tilde{I}:=\{i\in I:\;\mu_{I}(i)>\varepsilon(I,X)\}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;j(I,X):=|\tilde{I}|.

(13)

Obviously,

I_{j(I,X)}=\tilde{I}

(14)

so the result follows.

\blacksquare

The following definition is an analog of

We say that the event

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

The problem is difficult only when

. Therefore, we may assume that

\mathbf{E}|X|_{t}=\mathbf{E}\sum_{i\geqslant 1}|t(Y_{i})|=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{T}}|t(x)|\nu(dx)<\infty.

(22)

The proof is based on consecutive simplifications which finally allow us to apply Theorem
2.1
.

Step 1.
First we split the process into two parts

|X|_{t}=|X|^{(1)}_{t}+|X|^{(2)}_{t}=\sum_{i\geq 1}|t(Y_{i})|{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{|t(Y_{i})|\geq 2^{-N}\}}+\sum_{i\geq 1}|t(Y_{i})|{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{|t(Y_{i})|<2^{-N}\}},

\displaystyle\{\sup_{t\in T}|X|_{t}\geqslant KS\}

\displaystyle\subset\{\sup_{t\in T}|X|^{(1)}_{t}\geqslant KS\}\cup\bigcup_{t\in T}\{|X|^{(2)}_{t}\geqslant KS\}

\displaystyle=\{\sup_{t\in T}|X|^{(1)}_{t}\geqslant KS\}\cup\bigcup_{t\in T}\{|X|_{f(t)}\geqslant KS\}.

Markov’s inequality implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in T}\mathbf{P}(|X|_{f(t)}\geqslant KS)\leq\sum_{t\in T}\frac{\mathbf{E}|X|_{f(t)}}{KS}=\sum_{t\in T}\frac{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{T}}|t(x)|{\mathbbm{1}}_{|t(x)|<2^{-N}}d\nu(x)}{KS}.

Since

.
So we need to show that
\{\sup_{t\in T}|X|^{(1)}_{t}\geqslant KS\}

7/16

\nu(|t|\leq M)\leq 2^{N}\int_{R^{T}}|t(x)|d\nu(x)\leq 2^{N}S

(23)

Step 2.

The second step is to replace measurable functions

indexed by

\displaystyle{\cal N}\cup\bigcup_{A\in{\cal A}_{N}}A=\bigcap_{t\in T}\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T}:|t(x)|<M\},\textrm{ and }\nu({\cal N})=0.

Moreover, for any

(26)

Let also

(27)

Note that (

{\mathbb{R}}^{T}=\left(\bigcup_{A\in{\cal A}_{N}}A\right)\cup\left(\bigcup_{t\in T}\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T}:\;|t(x)|\geq M\}\right)\cup{\cal N},\textrm{ and }\nu({\cal N})=0.

Since, for every

The next step is to choose suitably small

) and that
\mu
is a probabilistic measure. Observe that the event that any of
X_{i}
’s belongs to
A_{*}
constitutes the final cover that we are constructing. Thus, it is enough to show that the event

\left\{\sup_{t\in T}\sum_{A\in{\cal A}_{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{m(A)}t(A)X(A_{k})\geqslant KS\right\}

(37)

is
5/16
-small.

Step 3.
Let

. Formulas (

for large enough

Thus, we can replace

).

Step 4.
In this step we replace the variables
X(A_{k})
by
0-1
variables (however not independent ones). Again, let

. By our construction
np\leq\sum_{A\in{\cal A}_{N}}\mu(A)\leq 1
. Thus, (
d
is fixed) we can pick
p
so small that
np^{2}d^{2}\leq 1/16
. For any
A\in{\cal A}_{N}
and
k\leq m(A)
let
g_{A_{k}}(x)={\mathbbm{1}}_{A_{k}}(x)
. We have that

\{X(A_{k})>1\}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}g_{A_{k}}(X_{i})\geq 2\right\},

so (as before) for

{\cal E}:=\left\{\sup_{t\in T}\sum_{A\in{\cal A}_{N}}\sum_{k=1}^{m(A)}t(A)X(A_{k})\geq KS^{\prime}\right\}\cap\bigcap_{A\in{\cal A}_{N},1\leqslant k\leqslant m(A)}\{X(A_{k})\in\{0,1\}\}

\left\{\sup_{t\in T}\sum_{A\in{\cal A}_{N}}\sum_{k=1}^{m(A)}t(A)X(A_{k})\geq KS^{\prime}\right\}\subset{\cal E}\cup\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}g_{A_{k}}(X_{i})\geq 2\right\}.

Since
(1-p)^{d}\geqslant 1-pd
, we have

\sum_{A\in{\cal A}_{N}}\sum_{k=1}^{m(A)}\mathbf{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}g_{A_{k}}(X_{i})\geq 2\right)=n(1-(1-p)^{d}-dp(1-p)^{d-1})\leqslant np^{2}d^{2}\leq\frac{1}{16}.

(38)

Since trivially

\bar{S}=\mathbf{E}\sup_{t\in T}\sum_{A\in{\cal A}_{N}}\sum_{k=1}^{m(A)}t(A)X(A_{k})\prod_{A\in{\cal A}_{N}}\prod_{k=1}^{m(A)}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{{X(A_{k})\in\{0,1\}\}}}\leqslant S^{\prime},

it is enough to show that the event

{\cal E}^{\prime}:=\left\{\sup_{t\in T}\sum_{A\in{\cal A}_{N}}\sum_{k=1}^{m(A)}t(A)X(A_{k})\geq K\bar{S}\right\}\cap\bigcap_{A\in{\cal A}_{N},1\leqslant k\leqslant m(A)}\{X(A_{k})\in\{0,1\}\}

is

-small.

Final Step.

Let
{\cal F}=\{A_{k}:A\in{\cal A}_{N},\;k\leq m(A)\}
. We denote by
\binom{{\cal F}}{d}
a family of
d

{\cal F}
. Let
Y
be uniformly chosen from
\binom{{\cal F}}{d}
. Finally, define for
A\in{\cal F}
,
Y(A)=1
if
A\in Y
and
0
otherwise. Let
X\in 2^{\cal F}
be a random set defined as
X=\{A\in{\cal F}:X(A)\geq 1\}
. Let us observe first that

\frac{8}{7}\mathbf{E}\sup_{t\in T}\sum_{A\in X}t(A)X(A)\geq\mathbf{E}\sup_{t\in T}\sum_{A\in Y}t(A).

(39)

If follows since by (
36
) and (
38
) we have

\mathbf{E}\sup_{t\in T}\sum_{A\in X}t(A)X(A)\geq\frac{7}{8}\mathbf{E}\left(\sup_{t\in T}\sum_{A\in X}t(A)X(A)\mid|X|=d,\ X(A)\in\{0,1\}:A\in{\cal F}\right).

Now, if we take any
I=\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{d}\}\in\binom{{\cal F}}{d}
and any other
J\in\binom{{\cal F}}{d}
we see that

\mathbf{P}(X=I)=\sum_{\pi\in\mathrm{Sym}_{d}}\mathbf{P}(X_{\pi(1)}\in A_{1},\ldots,X_{\pi(d)}\in A_{d})=d!p^{d}=\mathbf{P}(X=J).

Thus
X
conditioned on
\{|X|=d,\ X(A)\in\{0,1\}:A\in{\cal F}\}
has the same distribution as
Y
which concludes the argument for (
39
).

Now, by Proposition
5.5
there exists
\frac{4ed}{n}
-small familly
{\cal G}
which consists of sets
G=\{A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{|G|}:A_{i}\in{\cal F}\}
such that

\displaystyle\{Y\in\binom{{\cal F}}{d}:\sup_{t\in T}\sum_{A\in Y}t(A)\geq 4eL\mathbf{E}\sup_{t\in T}\sum_{A\in X}t(A)X(A)\}

\displaystyle\subset\{Y\in\binom{{\cal F}}{d}:\sup_{t\in T}\sum_{A\in Y}t(A)\geq 4eL\mathbf{E}\sup_{t\in T}\sum_{A\in Y}t(A)\}

\displaystyle\subset\bigcup_{G\in{\cal G}}\bigcap_{A\in G}\{Y(A)=1\}

and
\sum_{G\in{\cal G}}\left(\frac{Cd}{n}\right)^{|G|}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
.
Then clearly

{\cal E}^{\prime}\subset\bigcup_{G\in{\cal G}}\{X(A)=1,A\in G\}\cap\{X(A)\in\{0,1\};A\in{\cal F}\}.

We define
f_{G}(x)=\sum_{A\in G}{\mathbbm{1}}_{A}(x)
and see that

\{X(A)=1,A\in G\}\cap\{X(A)\in\{0,1\};\;A\in{\cal F}\}\subset\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}f_{G}(X_{i})\geq|G|\right\}.

Thus it is enough to show that

\sum_{G\in{\cal G}}\mathbf{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}f_{G}(X_{i})\geq|G|\right)\leq 1/4.

The variables
(f_{G}(X_{i}))_{i\leqslant d}
are independent Bernoulli random variables with probability of success equal to
|G|p
. So

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left(\sum_{i\leq d}f_{G}(X_{i})\geq|G|\right)=\sum_{k=|G|}^{d}\binom{d}{k}(p|G|)^{k}(1-p|G|)^{d-k}\leq\frac{1}{1-p}\binom{d}{|G|}(p|G|)^{|G|}.

The proof is completed the moment we show that

\frac{1}{1-p}\binom{d}{|G|}(p|G|)^{|G|}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{4ed}{n}\right)^{|G|}.

But

\left(\frac{4ed}{n}\right)^{|G|}=\left(\frac{4ed}{n}\right)^{|G|}\binom{d}{|G|}\frac{(d-|G|)!|G|!}{d!}\geqslant\binom{d}{|G|}\frac{|G|^{|G|}}{d^{|G|}}\left(\frac{4ed}{2en}\right)^{|G|},

so it is enough to show that

(2epn)^{|G|}\leqslant\frac{1-p}{2}(4e)^{|G|}.

The above is obviously true since
pn\leqslant 1
and
p
