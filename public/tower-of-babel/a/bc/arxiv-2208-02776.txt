Parallel block preconditioners for virtual element discretizations of the time-dependent Maxwell equations
Nicolás A. Barnafi, Franco Dassi, Simone Scacchi

Abstract
The focus of this study is the construction and numerical validation of parallel block preconditioners for low order virtual element discretizations of the three-dimensional Maxwell equations. The virtual element method (VEM) is a recent technology for the numerical approximation of partial differential equations (PDEs), that generalizes finite elements to polytopal computational grids. So far, VEM has been extended to several problems described by PDEs, and recently also to the time-dependent Maxwell equations. When the time discretization of PDEs is performed implicitly, at each time-step a large-scale and ill-conditioned linear system must be solved, that, in case of Maxwell equations, is particularly challenging, because of the presence of both H(div) and H(curl) discretization spaces. We propose here a parallel preconditioner, that exploits the Schur complement block factorization of the linear system matrix and consists of a Jacobi preconditioner for the H(div) block and an auxiliary space preconditioner for the H(curl) block. Several parallel numerical tests have been perfomed to study the robustness of the solver with respect to mesh refinement, shape of polyhedral elements, time step size and the VEM stabilization parameter.

Introduction

Finite element methods (FEM) have been widely used for the numerical approximation of Maxwell equations, see e.g.
[
27
,
10
,
22
,
25
]
, and the references therein. These theoretical studies have allowed the development of robust solvers, that have been successfully employed for numerical simulations of complex physical phenomena governed by Maxwell equations, such as the design of microwave devices
[
15
]
, electromagnetic scattering
[
23
]
, and antennas
[
21
]
.

In many applicative areas of electromagnetism, the computational domain presents complex geometric features, that might require the flexibility of general polytopal grids for an accurate representation or would otherwise requires a large number of simpler elements to be described (tetrahedra and/or hexahedra). The virtual element method (VEM), introduced in
[
7
]
, consists in a generalization of FEM to the case of polygonal or polyhedral grids constituted by elements of arbitrary shape. So far, VEM has been extended to several problems described by partial differential equations (PDEs), see e.g.
[
8
,
16
,
9
,
14
,
1
]
. Recently, a low order VEM discretization has been proposed in
[
6
]
for time-dependent Maxwell equations.

When the time discretization of PDEs is performed implicitly, at each time-step a large-scale and ill-conditioned linear system must be solved. In the case of Maxwell equations, this linear system is particularly challenging, because of the presence of both H(div) and H(curl) discretization spaces. A scalable effective linear solver for FEM discretizations of Maxwell equations has been proposed in
[
28
]
.

In the last five years, researchers have started developing ad-hoc preconditioners for VEM discretizations of different PDE problems: see
[
2
]
for multigrid,
[
13
]
for Additive Schwarz and
[
11
]
for BDDC and FETI-DP preconditioners for scalar elliptic equations; see
[
17
]
for parallel block algebraic multigrid (AMG) preconditioners for different saddle point problems; see
[
19
]
and
[
12
]
for BDDC preconditioners for mixed formulations of elliptic equations and the Stokes system, respectively. To the best of our knowledge, there are no references yet on effective linear solvers applied to VEM discretizations of Maxwell equations.

The aim and novelty of the present study is the construction and numerical validation of a parallel block preconditioner for a time-implicit low order VEM discretization of the Maxwell equations in three dimensions. The proposed preconditioner exploits the Schur complement block factorization of the linear system matrix and consists of a Jacobi preconditioner for the H(div) block and an auxiliary space preconditioner for the H(curl) block. Several parallel numerical tests have been perfomed to study the robustness of the solver with respect to mesh refinement, shape of polyhedral elements, time step size and the VEM stabilization parameter. A comparison with a tetrahedral FEM solver is also provided.

and

. With them, the homogeneous boundary condition spaces can be defined as

\displaystyle H_{0}(\curl,\Omega)

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathbfit{v}\in H(\curl,\Omega):\trace_{\curl}\mathbfit{v}=\mathbf{0}\right\},

\displaystyle H_{0}(\dive,\Omega)

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathbfit{v}\in H(\dive,\Omega):\trace_{\dive}\mathbfit{v}=0\right\}.

.

2
The continuous model

We consider the two field formulation of Maxwell’s equations, which can be stated as follows: Given the initial data
\mathbfit{E}^{0}
and
\mathbfit{B}^{0}
such that
\dive\mathbfit{B}^{0}=0
, find the electric field
\mathbfit{E}
and the magnetic induction field
\mathbfit{B}
such that

\displaystyle\varepsilon\mathbfit{E}_{t}+\sigma\mathbfit{E}-\curl\left(\mu^{-1}\mathbfit{B}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbfit{J}

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,\forall t\in(0,T),

(1)

\displaystyle\mathbfit{B}_{t}+\curl\left(\mathbfit{E}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,\forall t\in(0,T),

\displaystyle\mathbfit{E}(0)

\displaystyle=\mathbfit{E}^{0}

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\mathbfit{B}(0)

\displaystyle=\mathbfit{B}^{0}

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\trace_{\curl}\mathbfit{E}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle\forall t\in(0,T),

\displaystyle\trace_{\dive}\mathbfit{B}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\forall t\in(0,T),

where the subscript
t
means the first time derivative, i.e.
\psi_{t}=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}
for all functions
\psi
sufficiently regular. The function
\mathbfit{J}
represents the electric current density that is being externally applied, and the parameters are the electric permitivity
\varepsilon
, the electric conductivity
\sigma
and the magnetic permeability
\mu
.

Considering adequate test functions and integrating by parts, the weak formulation of problem (
1
) can be obtained: Given the initial data
\mathbfit{E}^{0}
and
\mathbfit{B}^{0}
such that
\dive\mathbfit{B}^{0}=0
, find the electric field
\mathbfit{E}\in H(\curl,\Omega)
and the magnetic induction field
\mathbfit{B}\in H(\dive,\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle\langle\varepsilon\mathbfit{E}_{t},\mathbfit{w}\rangle_{H(\curl,\Omega)}+(\sigma\mathbfit{E},\mathbfit{w})_{0,\Omega}+(\mu^{-1}\mathbfit{B},\curl\mathbfit{w})_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathbfit{J},\mathbfit{w}\rangle_{H(\curl,\Omega)}

\displaystyle\forall\mathbfit{w}\in H(\curl,\Omega),

(2)

\displaystyle(\mu^{-1}\mathbfit{B}_{t},\mathbfit{\psi})_{0,\Omega}+(\mu^{-1}\psi,\curl\mathbfit{E})_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\forall\mathbfit{\psi}\in H(\dive,\Omega).

We refer to
[
33
]
for specific hypotheses on the data and parameters such that problem (
2
) is well-posed. This problem presents a generalized saddle-point structure, and note that the order of the fields is fundamental for having a well-defined Schur complement operator as already hinted in
[
28
]
. Indeed, problem (
2
) can be written in operator form as

\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{A}&\mathcal{B}_{1}\\
\mathcal{B}_{2}&\mathcal{C}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbfit{E}\\
\mathbfit{B}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{J}\\
\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}

(3)

where the corresponding operators are formally given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\partial_{t}+\sigma\mathcal{I},

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\mu^{-1}\curl,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{2}

\displaystyle=\mu^{-1}\curl,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=\mu^{-1}\partial_{t},

where it holds that
\mathcal{B}_{1}^{T}=\mathcal{B}_{2}
. The matrix in (
3
) can be factorized as

\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{A}&\mathcal{B}_{1}\\
\mathcal{B}_{2}&\mathcal{C}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{I}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathcal{B}_{2}\mathcal{A}^{-1}&\mathcal{I}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{A}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{A}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{I}&\mathcal{A}^{-1}\mathcal{B}_{1}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathcal{I}\end{bmatrix}

(4)

where
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{A}}=\mathcal{C}-\mathcal{B}_{2}\mathcal{A}^{-1}\mathcal{B}_{1}
is known as the Schur complement. It is possible to define
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{C}}
analogously.

Space and time discretization

In this section we will give a brief description of the virtual element spaces used
to have the VEM numerical approximation of the problem defined in Equation (
2
).
For a more detailed analysis on the interpolation properties of the spaces,
the underling exact sequence structure and the convergence of fully-discrete scheme,
we refer the reader to
[
6
]
.

3.1
Virtual Element space discretization

Let
\Omega_{h}
be a partition of the three dimensional domain
\Omega
in generic polyhedral elements.
To discretize the magnetic and electric fields,
we proceed as the standard virtual element framework:
we define the local spaces and then we glue them together to build the whole functional space.
We describe this procedure for both the electric and magnetic fields in Sections
3.1.1
and
3.1.2
respectively.

3.1.1
Electric field - Edge space

Consider a generic polyhedron
P\in\Omega_{h}
,
we define the following space

\displaystyle\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{edge}}(P):=\bigg\{\mathbf{v}_{h}\in\big[L^{2}(P)\big]^{3}\>:\>

\displaystyle\dive(\mathbf{v}_{h})=0\,,\quad\curl(\curl(\mathbf{v}_{h}))\in\big[\mathbb{P}_{0}(P)\big]^{3}\,,

\displaystyle(\mathbf{n}_{F}\times\mathbf{v}_{h}|_{F})\times\mathbf{n}_{F}\in\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{edge}}(F),\hskip 11.49994pt\forall F\in\partial P\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{h}\cdot\mathbf{t}_{e}\in\mathbb{P}_{0}(e),\quad\forall e\in\upzeta P

\displaystyle\int_{P}\curl(\mathbf{v}_{h})\cdot(\mathbf{x}_{P}\times\mathbf{p}_{0})~\text{d}P=0\quad\forall\mathbf{p}_{0}\in[\mathbb{P}_{0}(P)]^{3}\>\bigg\}

(5)

where on each face
F\in\partial P
we have defined the following virtual space

\displaystyle\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{edge}}(F):=\bigg\{\mathbf{v}_{h}^{F}\in\big[L^{2}(P)\big]^{2}\>:\>

\displaystyle\dive_{F}(\mathbf{v}_{h}^{F})\in\mathbb{P}_{0}(F)\,,\quad\rot_{F}(\mathbf{v}_{h})\in\mathbb{P}_{0}(F)\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{h}^{F}\cdot\mathbf{t}_{e}\in\mathbb{P}_{0}(e)\quad\forall e\in\partial F,\quad\int_{F}\mathbf{v}_{h}^{F}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{F}~\text{d}F=0\>\bigg\}\,.

(6)

In the definition of
\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{edge}}(F)
we are considering function
\mathbf{v}_{h}^{F}
that are the restriction of
\mathbf{v}_{h}
on the face
F
, i.e.,

\mathbf{v}_{h}^{F}:=\mathbf{v}_{h}|_{F}
.
Moreover, both
\dive_{F}(\cdot)
and
\rot_{F}(\cdot)
refer to the divergence and rotor differential operators with respect to the two dimensional face coordinate system.

In such a space a generic function
\mathbf{v}_{h}
is uniquely determined by the constant values of
\mathbf{v}_{h}\cdot\mathbf{t}_{e}
on the skeleton of the polyhedron.
As a consequence the set of degrees of freedom we use is:

E1
)

the values of
\mathbf{v}_{h}\cdot\mathbf{t}_{e}
on each edge
e\in\upzeta P
.

Moreover, it can be proven that
constant vector fields belong to
\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{edge}}(P)
, see
[
5
]
.
Before defining the global functional space,
we underline that, although a generic function in
\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{edge}}(P)
is virtual,
it is possible to compute its
L^{2}
projection on constant fields.
More specifically,
it is possible to compute the projection operator
{\bm{\Pi}}^{0}_{\mathbf{E}}:\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{edge}}(P)\to[\mathbb{P}_{0}(P)]^{3}
as

({\bm{\Pi}}^{0}_{\mathbf{E}}\mathbf{v}_{h}\,,\mathbf{p}_{0})_{P}=(\mathbf{v}_{h}\,,\mathbf{p}_{0})_{P}\qquad\forall\mathbf{p}_{0}\in[\mathbb{P}_{0}(P)]^{3}\,,

(7)

starting from the degrees of freedom
E1
and
the integral properties plugged in both
\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{edge}}(P)
and
\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{edge}}(F)
spaces
[
5
]
.
Such projection operator is really important. Indeed, it is one of the key ingredient to define the bi-linear forms of the discrete weak formulation of the Maxwell’s equations.

Finally, the global discrete space for the electric field is

\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{edge}}(\Omega):=\left\{\mathbf{v}_{h}\in H_{0}(\curl,\Omega)\>:\>\mathbf{v}_{h}|_{P}\in\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{edge}}(P)\right\}\,,

where the local spaces are joined together in such a way that
the tangential component of the vector field on each edge of the mesh is continuous. The degrees of freedom of such a space are obviously the union of the local degrees of freedom
E1
. We underline that in this paper we are considering only the lowest order case. It is possible to generalise the definition of such a space for the general order case
[
4
]
.

3.1.2
Magnetic field - Face space

Now we describe the virtual element space we use to discretize the magnetic vector field.
Consider a polyhedron
P\in\Omega_{h}
, we define the following local space

\displaystyle\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{face}}(P):=\bigg\{\mathbf{w}_{h}\in\big[L^{2}(P)\big]^{3}\>:\>

\displaystyle\dive(\mathbf{w}_{h})\in\mathbb{P}_{0}(P)\,,\quad\curl(\mathbf{w}_{h})\in\big[\mathbb{P}_{0}(P)\big]^{3}\,,

\displaystyle(\mathbf{n}_{F}\cdot\mathbf{w}_{h}|_{F})\in\mathbb{P}_{0}(F),\quad\forall F\in\partial P\,,

\displaystyle\int_{P}\mathbf{w}_{h}\cdot(\mathbf{x}_{P}\times\mathbf{p}_{0})~\text{d}P=0\quad\forall\mathbf{p}_{0}\in[\mathbb{P}_{0}(P)]^{3}\>\bigg\}\,.

(8)

Also
\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{face}}(P)
contains not only constant vectorial polynomials but other functions that are virtual.
Thus, a generic function
\mathbf{w}_{h}\in\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{face}}(P)
is uniquely determined by

B1
)

the face moments on each polyhedron’s face

\int_{F}\mathbf{w}_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{F}~\text{d}F\,.

Also in this case it is possible to compute the projection operator
{\bm{\Pi}}^{0}_{\mathbf{B}}:\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{face}}(P)\to[\mathbb{P}_{0}(P)]^{3}
as

({\bm{\Pi}}^{0}_{\mathbf{B}}\mathbf{w}_{h}\,,\mathbf{p}_{0})_{P}=(\mathbf{w}_{h}\,,\mathbf{p}_{0})_{P}\qquad\forall\mathbf{p}_{0}\in[\mathbb{P}_{0}(P)]^{3}\,.

(9)

We refer the reader to
[
18
]
to see how
{\bm{\Pi}}^{0}_{\mathbf{B}}
is computed via the degrees of freedom of
\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{face}}(P)
.
In Section
3.2
we will see that
also this projection operator is essential to define the bi-linear forms involved in assembling the stiffness matrix.

Finally, as for the space
\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{edge}}(\Omega)
,
the global functional space is obtained by gluing each local space together

\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{face}}(\Omega):=\left\{\mathbf{w}_{h}\in H_{0}(\dive,\Omega)\>:\>\mathbf{w}_{h}|_{P}\in\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{face}}(P)\right\}\,.

However, the resulting vector field now has
the normal component continuous across each internal face.
We limit ourselves to the lowest order case, and refer the reader to
[
18
]
for a thorough description of the general case.

3.2
Discrete weak formulation

Before introducing the discrete counterpart of the problem defined in Equation (
2
),
we have to make two important remarks.

The first one is that
\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{edge}}(\Omega)
and
\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{face}}(\Omega)
are part of an exact sequence
[
6
]
.
As a consequence, the following identity holds

\curl\left(\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{edge}}(\Omega)\right)=\Big\{\mathbf{w}_{h}\in\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{face}}(\Omega)\>:\>\dive(\mathbf{w}_{h})=0\Big\}\,.

(10)

Such relation tells us that if we apply the
\curl
operator to any function in the space
\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{edge}}(\Omega)
,
we get a function in
\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{face}}(\Omega)
whose divergence is zero.

The second remark is the definition of the scalar products within
\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{edge}}(\Omega)
and
\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{face}}(\Omega)
spaces.
More specifically,
given the space
\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{edge}}(\Omega)
, we define the local discrete counterpart of the
L^{2}
inner product as

\left[\mathbf{v}_{h}^{1},\,\mathbf{v}_{h}^{2}\right]_{\text{edge},P}:=\int_{P}{\bm{\Pi}}^{0}_{\mathbf{E}}\mathbf{v}_{h}^{1}\cdot{\bm{\Pi}}^{0}_{\mathbf{E}}\mathbf{v}_{h}^{2}~\text{d}P+\mathcal{S}_{\text{edge},P}\left(\left(\mathbf{I}-{\bm{\Pi}}^{0}_{\mathbf{E}}\right)\mathbf{v}_{h}^{1},\,\left(\mathbf{I}-{\bm{\Pi}}^{0}_{\mathbf{E}}\right)\mathbf{v}_{h}^{2}\right)\,,

(11)

where
\mathbf{v}_{h}^{1},\,\mathbf{v}_{h}^{2}\in\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{edge}}(P)
and

\mathcal{S}_{\text{edge},P}
is the stabilization proposed in
[
5
, Equation 4.8]
.
In the same way, the scalar product of the space
\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{face}}(\Omega)
is defined as

\left[\mathbf{w}_{h}^{1},\,\mathbf{w}_{h}^{2}\right]_{\text{face},P}:=\int_{P}{\bm{\Pi}}^{0}_{\mathbf{B}}\mathbf{w}_{h}^{1}\cdot{\bm{\Pi}}^{0}_{\mathbf{B}}\mathbf{w}_{h}^{2}~\text{d}P+\mathcal{S}_{\text{face},P}\left(\left(\mathbf{I}-{\bm{\Pi}}^{0}_{\mathbf{B}}\right)\mathbf{w}_{h}^{1},\,\left(\mathbf{I}-{\bm{\Pi}}^{0}_{\mathbf{B}}\right)\mathbf{w}_{h}^{2}\right)\,,

(12)

where now
\mathbf{w}_{h}^{1},\,\mathbf{w}_{h}^{2}\in\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{face}}(P)
and

\mathcal{S}_{\text{face},P}
is the stabilization proposed in
[
5
, Equation 4.17]
.
Notice that these two scalar products differ by the projection operators (
{\bm{\Pi}}^{0}_{\mathbf{E}}
and
{\bm{\Pi}}^{0}_{\mathbf{B}}
) and the stabilization terms (
\mathcal{S}_{\text{edge},P}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\text{face},P}
).
Furthermore we underline that,
since the space inclusion defined in Equation (
10
) holds,
the inner product

\left[\curl(\mathbf{v}_{h}^{1}),\,\mathbf{w}_{h}^{2}\right]_{\text{face},P}\qquad\text{as well as}\qquad\left[\mathbf{w}_{h}^{1},\,\curl(\mathbf{v}_{h}^{2})\right]_{\text{face},P}

where
\mathbf{v}_{h}^{1},\mathbf{v}_{h}^{2}\in\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{edge}}(P)
and
\mathbf{w}_{h}^{1},\mathbf{w}_{h}^{2}\in\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{face}}(P)
are well defined.

Then, once each of these two scalar products are defined on a generic polyhedron
P\in\Omega_{h}
,
they are defined on the whole domain by summing each polyhedron’s contribution, i.e.,

\left[\mathbf{v}_{h}^{1},\,\mathbf{v}_{h}^{2}\right]_{\text{edge},\Omega}:=\sum_{P\in\Omega_{h}}\left[\mathbf{v}_{h}^{1},\,\mathbf{v}_{h}^{2}\right]_{\text{edge},P}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left[\mathbf{w}_{h}^{1},\,\mathbf{w}_{h}^{2}\right]_{\text{face},\Omega}:=\sum_{P\in\Omega_{h}}\left[\mathbf{w}_{h}^{1},\,\mathbf{w}_{h}^{2}\right]_{\text{face},P}

Starting from the previous observations,
we are now ready to state the discrete weak formulation of the problem at hand.
Consider a uniform partition of the time interval
[0,T]
in
n
sub-intervals of length
\tau

and an implicit Euler scheme to discretize the time derivative.
Then, the time dependent problem becomes: given the initial distribution of the fields
\mathbfit{E}_{h}^{0}
and
\mathbfit{B}_{h}^{0}
, find at each time step
m
the vector fields
\mathbfit{E}_{h}^{m}
and
\mathbfit{B}_{h}^{m}
such that

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{\tau}\left[\hat{\mu}^{-1}(\mathbfit{B}_{h}^{m}-\mathbfit{B}_{h}^{m-1}),\,\mathbf{w}_{h}\right]_{\text{face},\Omega}+\left[\curl(\mathbfit{E}_{h}^{m}),\,\hat{\mu}^{-1}\mathbf{w}_{h}\right]_{\text{face},\Omega}&=0\\
\frac{1}{\tau}\left[\hat{\epsilon}(\mathbfit{E}_{h}^{m}-\mathbfit{E}_{h}^{m-1}),\,\mathbf{v}_{h}\right]_{\text{edge},\Omega}+\left[\hat{\sigma}\mathbfit{E}_{h}^{m},\,\mathbf{v}_{h}\right]_{\text{edge},\Omega}-\left[\hat{\mu}^{-1}\mathbfit{B}_{h}^{m},\,\curl{\mathbf{v}_{h}}\right]_{\text{face},\Omega}&=\left[\bm{J}_{I}^{m},\,\mathbf{v}_{h}\right]_{\text{edge},\Omega}\end{aligned}\right.\,,

(13)

hold for each
\mathbf{w}_{h}\in\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{face}}(\Omega)
and
\mathbf{v}_{h}\in\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{edge}}(\Omega)
.
Here we have defined the element-wise constant functions
\hat{\mu},\hat{\epsilon}
and
\hat{\sigma}

that approximate the data
\mu,\epsilon
and
\sigma
, respectively, and

J_{I}^{m}\in\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{edge}}(\Omega)
is an interpolation of the current density at the time
t^{m}
.

We underline that we swap the equations in the discrete formulation of the problem,
compare Equation (
2
) and Equation (
13
).
This choice will become clearer in Section
4
,
when we define the novel preconditioner proposed in this paper to solve the linear system arising from the discretization of Equation (
13
).

4
Schur complement preconditioner

The point of departure for the development of an efficient block preconditioner for problem defined in Equation (
13
) is the Schur complement factorization shown in Equation (
4
), which provides an exact inverse.
Proceeding as in the continuous case, system (
13
) can be written as

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbfit{C}&\mathbfit{B}_{2}\\
\mathbfit{B}_{1}&\mathbfit{A}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbfit{B}_{h}\\
\mathbfit{E}_{h}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbfit{J}\end{bmatrix},

(14)

where the matrices will be given by

\displaystyle\mathbfit{A}

\displaystyle=(\varepsilon\tau^{-1}+\sigma)\mathbfit{I}_{E_{h}},

\displaystyle\mathbfit{B}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\mu^{-1}\curl_{h},

\displaystyle\mathbfit{B}_{2}

\displaystyle=\mu^{-1}\curl_{h},

\displaystyle\mathbfit{C}

\displaystyle=(\mu\tau)^{-1}\mathbfit{I}_{B_{h}},

with
\mathbfit{B}_{1}^{T}=\mathbfit{B}_{2}
. More specifically, The inverse of (
14
) is given by

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbfit{I}_{B_{h}}&-\mathbfit{C}^{-1}\mathbfit{B}_{2}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbfit{I}_{E_{h}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbfit{C}^{-1}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbfit{S}_{\mathbfit{C}}^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbfit{I}_{B_{h}}&\mathbf{0}\\
-\mathbfit{B}_{1}\mathbfit{C}^{-1}&\mathbfit{I}_{E_{h}}\end{bmatrix},

where the difficulty for the development of a preconditioner has been reduced to the development of efficient preconditioners for the operators
\mathbfit{C}
and
\mathbfit{S}_{\mathbfit{C}}
, the latter being given by

\mathbfit{S}_{\mathbfit{C}}=\mathbfit{A}-\mathbfit{B}_{1}\mathbfit{C}^{-1}\mathbfit{B}_{2}=(\varepsilon\tau^{-1}+\sigma)\mathbfit{I}_{E_{h}}+\tau\mu^{-1}\curl_{h}\mathbfit{I}_{B_{h}}\curl_{h}.

The last part of the preconditioner
\curl_{h}\mathbfit{I}_{B_{h}}\curl_{h}
is built combining the definition of the face inner product Equation (
12
) and commuting diagram property.
For more detail about we refer to Proposition 6 in
[
6
]
.

This yields two fundamental points: on one hand,
\mathbfit{S}_{\mathbfit{C}}
belongs to
(\mathbfit{V}_{h}^{\text{edge}})^{\prime}
and so it is well defined only on an edge functional space, which justifies the change of order considered for the problem variables. On the other hand, it is a differential operator that defines a simple bilinear form, and so it allows for a sparse representation for the Schur complement, which is ideal for the development of a preconditioner. In what follows we argue our choices of preconditioners for the matrices
\mathbfit{C}
and
\mathbfit{S}_{\mathbfit{C}}
.

Preconditioner for
\mathbfit{C}
.

This matrix is a scaled mass matrix, for which a simple diagonal preconditioner provides good performance in FEM formulations
[
32
]
. It is still not clear whether a diagonal preconditioner is a good preconditioner in VEM, so this will be evaluated as well in our study.

Preconditioner for
\mathbfit{S}_{\mathbfit{C}}
.

The preconditioning of the operator
I+\curl\curl
has been most successful through auxiliary space methods within multigrid methods
[
24
]
. This has been implemented in HYPRE
[
20
]
in the AMS solver (Auxiliary-space Maxwell solver), whose setup requires the coordinates of the degrees of freedom and a discrete gradient given by an orientation of the mesh edges.

We highlight that Schur complement preconditioners are attractive because the preconditioned problem has at most four distinct eigenvalues
[
26
]
in exact arithmetic. This is often not observed in practice unless a sparse representation of the Schur complement
\mathbfit{S}_{\mathbfit{C}}
is available, which in fact is our case.

5
Numerical tests

The scope of this section is twofold. On one hand, we want to explore the parallel performance of state-of-the-art preconditioners for VEM and their dependence on the timestep. On the other hand, we want to present a first comparison of the solution between FEM and VEM, for an increasing level of complexity of the meshes under consideration. For the FEM problem we consider the Firedrake library
[
29
]
, which already supports an efficient setup of the AMS preconditioner for the
I+\curl\curl
operator, and for the VEM problem we consider 5 meshes, starting from a comparable tetrahedral mesh to more complex ones.

The problem setup is the same in all cases: homogeneous initial and boundary conditions and
\mathbfit{J}=(1,1,1)
. The solver is given by a GMRES method without restart, the absolute and relative tolerances are given by
10^{-12}
and
10^{-6}
respectively, with divergence defined as more than 1000 iterations required for convergence. The block preconditioner was set using the PCFIELDSPLIT preconditioner from PETSc
[
3
]
with a lower-triangular factorization, and the sub-block preconditioners described in Section
4
. The implementation was done in an

5.3.1
Optimality tests

In this section we report the robustness of the proposed preconditioner with respect to the problem size. To obtain a better intuition on which block is the one yielding a higher contribution to the deterioration of the preconditioner, as well as validating the efficiency of the sparse representation of the Schur complement, we present the results in four different scenarios:

•

Exact-exact
: both
\mathbfit{C}
and
\mathbfit{S}_{\mathbfit{C}}
are solved exactly.

•

Jacobi-exact
: the inverse of
\mathbfit{C}
is approximated by the action of a Jacobi preconditioner and
\mathbfit{S}_{\mathbfit{C}}

•

Exact-AMS
: Matrix
\mathbfit{C}
is inverted exactly, whereas the inverse of
\mathbfit{S}_{\mathbfit{C}}
is approximated by the action of the AMS preconditioner.

•

Jacobi-AMS
: The inverse of both matrices
\mathbfit{C}
and
\mathbfit{S}_{\mathbfit{C}}
and approximated by the application of the previously described preconditioners.

Hexahedral structured mesh.

Octahedral mesh.

This is the first case considered with a truly polygonal mesh, and its results are shown in Table
6

Nonahedral mesh.

The results of this case are shown in Table
7
. This test is interesting as its trends change with the time step. More specifically, it is still true that the performance of the Exact-AMS preconditioner presents deterioration for the largest time step, but this instead does not hold for smaller time steps. Instead, in all cases there is a significant deterioration of the Jacobi preconditioner, as shown by the performance of the Jacobi-exact, on the finest mesh. As before, the Jacobi-AMS preconditioner presents similar number of iterations to the Jacobi-exact preconditioner, but as the latter is not robust in this case, neither is the former.

Irregular polyhedral mesh.

As expected, this case is the most difficult, with the results being shown in Table
8
. The ill-conditioning of the problem is severe for this type of mesh, which can be noted by the increase in GMRES iterations in the finest mesh for the largest time step (4 iterations instead of 2), despite the use of an exact inverse. In contrast to the nonahedral case, the Jacobi preconditioner is able to adequately handle the mass matrix, and instead the AMS preconditioner is not able to capture the physics, as observed by its poor convergence and even worse robustness with respect to the problem size, as shown by its divergence in most cases. This can be mildly alleviated by reducing the time step, but the overall time reduction is not significant and not worth the effort due to the lack of robustness.

We highlight that in all cases, an adequate performance is shown by the sparse representation of the Schur operator
\mathbfit{S}_{\mathbfit{C}}
, and in all cases but the irregular one, a slightly smaller time step results in a preconditioner with an adequate performance for large scale simulations. It is also fundamental to note that in all such cases, there is a difference of up to two orders of magnitude between the exact solver (Exact-exact) and the iterative one (Jacobi-AMS).

5.5
Sensitivity on stabilization in Schur operator

As shown in Section
3.1
, VEM has an intrinsic stabilization block required for the invertibility of the discrete operator. Still, the magnitude of the stabilization can be modulated through a parameter, which can have a great impact on the conditioning of the resulting problem, as studied in
[
1
]
. The sparse representation of the Schur complement operator we are considering is not the same matrix as the one that appears in the problem formulation, so it is not clear how much stabilization is required for this block. This is of course true not only here, but it holds for any Schur complement preconditioner used in a problem with a block structure as this one. This motivated this preliminary investigation on the effect of the Schur stabilization on the performance of the preconditioner.

We show the results of this study in Table
10
. We note that in all but the irregular case, the lowest iteration count is obtained by using the standard value
\alpha=1
. Still, this trend does not hold for the irregular case, where stabilization hinders performance. This last result is rather counter-intuitive, as a VEM block without stabilization is not invertible in theory. This is an interesting phenomenon that had not been observed before, and will indeed be the subject of future work.

6
Conclusions

This work presents an extension of an optimal FEM preconditioner to VEM, for a two field formulation of Maxwell’s equations based on the Schur complement. Its performance is robust and scalable for a sufficiently (but not too much) small time step, with usually only a mild deterioration in the GMRES iterations. The source of deterioration for most cases is the dominance of the
\curl\curl
operator in the Schur complement. When the meshes become too complex, this becomes the main driver of deterioration, as shown by the performance of the Jacobi preconditioner on the nonahedral mesh, or by the inability of the AMS preconditioner to function properly in the irregular mesh, which presents highly distorted elements. In all working cases, performance issues can be alleviated by reducing the time step, where the values required for an adequate performance are not too small, as we have observed a value of
0.05
seconds to suffice.

A comparison with finite elements, where the preconditioner used is known to be optimal, shows many interesting and still unresolved issues in the numerical approximation of PDEs using VEM. We highlight two: the first one is that the relationship between the performance and the elements used by the mesh are not trivial. Indeed, we expected the performance to deteriorate in the order tetrahedra, hexahedra, octahedra, nonahedra, and irregular, but the order between the first four is not like so. The second one is the preconditioning of the mass block, where usually a Jacobi preconditioner suffices, at least for first order elements. Instead, we observed that this approach was not effective with the nonahedral mesh. The latter point is fundamental for the simulation of time-dependent problems.
