Maximum Agreement Subtrees and Hölder homeomorphisms between Brownian trees
Thomas Budzinski, &, Delphin Sénizergues

Abstract
We prove that the size of the largest common subtree between two uniform, independent, leaf-labelled random binary trees of size n n is typically less than n 1 / 2 − ε n^{1/2-\varepsilon} for some ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 . Our proof relies on the coupling between discrete random trees and the Brownian tree and on a recursive decomposition of the Brownian tree due to Aldous. Along the way, we also show that almost surely, there is no ( 1 − ε ) (1-\varepsilon) -Hölder homeomorphism between two independent copies of the Brownian tree.

Maximum agreement subtree.

Let

Since then, it has been studied from algorithmic, extremal and probabilistic points of view.
In particular, the quantity
\mathrm{MAST}(t,t^{\prime})
can be computed in polynomial time in
n

[
29
]
.
On the extremal side, the minimal possible values of
\mathrm{MAST}(t,t^{\prime})
over all pairs
(t,t^{\prime})
of leaf-labelled binary trees of size
n
is known to be of order
\log n
(the upper bound was proved in
[

and the lower bound in
[

]
).

Maximum agreement subtree of random trees.

Another natural question is to understand the
typical
order of magnitude of the maximum agreement subtree, that is, the random variable
\mathrm{MAST}(T_{n},T^{\prime}_{n})
, where
T_{n}
and
T^{\prime}_{n}
are random trees of size
n
.
The most natural model is the one where
T_{n}
and
T^{\prime}_{n}
are independent and picked uniformly in the set of labelled binary trees of size
n
.
This model was first investigated by Bryant, McKenzie and Steel
[
10
]
, who proved by a first moment computation that
\mathrm{MAST}(T_{n},T^{\prime}_{n})
is
O(\sqrt{n})
with high probability.
They also provided numerical evidence that
\mathrm{MAST}(T_{n},T^{\prime}_{n})
should be of order
n^{\beta}
for some
\beta
close to
\frac{1}{2}
.
On the other hand, a polynomial lower bound of order
n^{1/8}
was obtained by Bernstein, Ho, Long, Steel, St. John and Sullivant in
[
7
]
. This lower bound was recently improved to
n^{\frac{\sqrt{3}-1}{2}}\approx n^{0.366}
by Aldous
[
5
]
and to
n^{0.4464}
by Khezeli
[
19
]
in expectation. Finally, we also mention that
\sqrt{n}
has been proved to be the right order of magnitude if the trees
T_{n}
and
T^{\prime}_{n}
are conditioned to have the same shape
[
25
]
, and that the upper bound in
\sqrt{n}
holds robustly for many random trees models arising from branching processes
[
27
]
.

Our main contribution in this paper is to show that the upper bound
\sqrt{n}
is actually not optimal in the independent model, which was conjectured by Aldous in
[
5
]
.

Theorem 1
.

For all
n\geq 3
, let
T_{n}
and
T^{\prime}_{n}
be two independent uniform labelled binary trees of size
n
. There exists a constant
\varepsilon_{\ref{thm_MAST_upper}}>0
such that we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{MAST}(T_{n},T^{\prime}_{n})<n^{1/2-\varepsilon_{\ref{thm_MAST_upper}}}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}1.

More explicitly, we find that we can take
\varepsilon_{\ref{thm_MAST_upper}}=10^{-338}
(see Section
5.1
for a discussion on explicit constants). We have not tried to optimize the constants and this value should be easy to improve, but we do not think that our strategy of proof can give a "reasonable" lower bound (like e.g.
\varepsilon_{\ref{thm_MAST_upper}}=10^{-6}
). We also mention that our arguments are sufficient to prove that the probability that
\mathrm{MAST}(T_{n},T^{\prime}_{n})
exceeds
n^{1/2-\varepsilon_{\ref{thm_MAST_upper}}}
is
O(n^{-a})
for some
a>0
, and that
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{MAST}(T_{n},T^{\prime}_{n})\right]\leq n^{1/2-\varepsilon}
for some
\varepsilon>0
(see Section
5.2
for a quick discussion).

Comparison with the Brownian tree.

As recalled before, it is proved in
[
25
]
that the MAST of two trees of the same shape is typically of order
\sqrt{n}
.
Therefore, our strategy relies on the fact that two independent large random trees have "different shapes" at every scale.
To formalize this, we make heavy use of the continuous scaling limit of
T_{n}
, which exhibits nice scale invariance properties, and on which more explicit computations can be performed.

More precisely, we denote by
\mathcal{T}
the
Brownian tree
, which is the scaling limit of
T_{n}
, seen as a measured metric space, where distances have been normalized by
\sqrt{n}
and masses by
n
.
This compact, continuous random tree with fractal dimension 2 was introduced in
[
2
]
and can be built in a natural way from a normalized Brownian excursion (see Section
2.2
for complete definitions). It also has the important property that its branching points all have degree
3
. We highlight that comparisons between the discrete trees
T_{n}
and the continuous object
\mathcal{T}
already play an important role in the proofs of the lower bounds of
[
5
]
and
[
19
]
.

Hölder homeomorphisms of Brownian tree.

Since proving Theorem
1
requires to compare the shapes of two independent copies of
\mathcal{T}
, we obtain along the way the following result of independent interest.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
be two independent copies of the Brownian tree. There exists a constant
\varepsilon_{\ref{thm_holder_homeo}}>0
such that almost surely, there is no
(1-\varepsilon_{\ref{thm_holder_homeo}})
-Hölder homeomorphism from
\mathcal{T}
to
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
.

Just like in Theorem
1
, we find that we can take
\varepsilon_{\ref{thm_holder_homeo}}=10^{-338}
, which we did not try to optimize.
Although none of Theorems
1
and
2
easily implies the other, they are closely related to each other.
Indeed, as can be seen on Figure
1
, a common subtree of two trees
T_{n}
and
T^{\prime}_{n}
gives a "correspondence" between a part of
T_{n}
and a part of
T^{\prime}_{n}
, which can be extended to a homeomorphism in the continuous limit.
This is not a completely new idea, as the arguments of
[
5
]
(and the improvements done in
[
19
]
) can already be interpreted as a proof of the existence of a homeomorphism from
\mathcal{T}
to
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
with a certain Hölder exponent.
As we check in Theorem
22
in the Appendix below, the actual Hölder exponent given by
[
5
]
turns out to be
5-2\sqrt{6}\approx 0.1010
.
More generally, statements similar to Theorem
2
on very different objects appear under the name of
Hölder equivalence
in the geometry literature.
In geometry, this problem is often of the following form: given a metric space
X
that is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, what is the optimal Hölder exponent of a homeomorphism from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
X
?
An immediate upper bound is
\frac{n}{\dim_{H}(X)}
, where
\dim_{H}(X)
is the Hausdorff dimension of
X
.
We refer to
[
16
]
for improved upper bounds in specific contexts such as sub-Riemannian or contact manifolds.
However, we are in a very different setting here, as the Brownian trees involved are not manifolds, and so our arguments do not share any commonalities.
Another difference between our setting and the one studied by Gromov is that we prove that the Hölder exponent cannot be arbitrarily close to
1
in a context where both sides of the homeomorphism have the same Hausdorff dimension.

We also note that Theorem
2
becomes quite easy if "
(1-\varepsilon)
-Hölder" is replaced by "Lipschitz"

Recursive decomposition of the Brownian tree.

In order to highlight more precisely what Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
have in common, we introduce an important tool in our proofs, which already crucially appears in
[
5
]
.
This is a randomized recursive decomposition of the Brownian tree
\mathcal{T}
, which was introduced by Aldous
[
4
]
.
The decomposition consists in picking
3
random uniform points
X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}
in
\mathcal{T}
, blowing up
\mathcal{T}
into three pieces at the unique branching point that separates
X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}
, and iterating the decomposition in each of the three pieces (see Section
2.5
for complete definitions).
After
k
steps, we obtain a (randomized) partition of
\mathcal{T}
into
3^{k}
regions, indexed by a set
\mathbb{T}_{3}^{k}
.
We denote those regions by
\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right)_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}^{k}}
.
This decomposition enjoys very nice independence properties that we will heavily rely on.

We can now state the key result that we will use to prove both Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
.
It roughly states that a homeomorphism between two independent realizations
\mathcal{T},\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
of the Brownian tree cannot be "almost measure-preserving", in the sense that it has to send "most" regions of
\mathcal{T}
to regions of
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
with a much smaller mass. We will denote by
|A|
the measure of a subset
A
of a Brownian tree.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
and
\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right)_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}^{k}}
be a Brownian tree and its recursive decomposition, and let
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
be an independent copy of
\mathcal{T}
. There exist constants
\xi,\eta>0
such that almost surely the following holds for
k
large enough:
For any homeomorphism
\Psi:\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
,
if we define the set
U^{k,\Psi}\subset\mathbb{T}_{3}^{k}
of indices as

\displaystyle U^{k,\Psi}:=\left\{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}^{k}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\left|\Psi\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right)\right|>e^{-\eta k}\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right|\right\},

then the subset

E^{k,\Psi}:=\bigcup_{\mathbf{i}\in U^{k,\Psi}}\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]
of
\mathcal{T}

has measure at most
e^{-\xi k}
.

Theorem
2
will follow almost immediately from this result.
On the other hand, in order to deduce Theorem
1
from Proposition
3
, we will rely on the nice coupling existing between the discrete random tree
T_{n}
and the continuous one
\mathcal{T}
.
We can then argue that a common subtree between
T_{n}
and
T^{\prime}_{n}
can be extended to a homeomorphism
\Psi
from
\mathcal{T}
to
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
.
Proposition
3
guarantees that for most of the regions
\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]
, either
\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]
or
\Psi(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}])
is very small, so only few labels can appear in both
\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]
and in
\Psi(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}])
when
T_{n}
is coupled to
\mathcal{T}
and
T^{\prime}_{n}
to
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
. We will conclude by using the classic square root upper bound for each of those regions (Lemma
19
).

Ideas of the proof of Proposition
3
.

Finally, let us mention some of the ideas behind the proof of Proposition
3
. The proof roughly consists in showing that a certain multiscale exploration of the tree
\mathcal{T}
has many "mismatches" with the analog exploration in
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, which we believe is the main innovation of the present work.
Fix a typical point
x\in\mathcal{T}
, and imagine that we try to build a "good" homeomorphism from
\mathcal{T}
to
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
. By looking at smaller and smaller regions of the recursive decomposition around the point
x
, we can encode the masses of a sequence of nested neighbourhoods of
x
by a sequence
\left(f_{j}(x)\right)_{j\geq 0}
of
i.i.d. numbers, where
j
represents decreasing scales.
We will argue that it is not possible to find
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
such that
f_{j}(x^{\prime})
is very close to
f_{j}(x)
for most of the scales
j
.
This will imply that the ratio between the mass of a small region around
x
and the mass of its image around
\Psi(x)
cannot stay "stationary" as the scale of that region decreases to
0
.
By a "martingale-like" argument, we will conclude that this ratio must decay quickly, which will yield Proposition
3
. This is somewhat reminiscent of some ideas of
[
6
]
, in the sense that "finding a large substructure is difficult because some positive proportion of the mass is lost at every scale".

Structure of the paper.

In Section
2
, we will introduce all the definitions of discrete and continuous objects that will be needed in the proofs, as well as some basic properties of the Brownian tree and of its recursive decomposition.
Section
3
is the central part of the paper and is devoted to the proof of Proposition
3
, which represents most of the work.
In Section
4
, we conclude the proofs of the main theorems.
In Section
5
, we discuss the quantitative values of
\varepsilon_{\ref{thm_MAST_upper}}
and
\varepsilon_{\ref{thm_holder_homeo}}
provided by our proof, as well as some open questions.
In Appendix
A
, we construct a Hölder homeomorphism between
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
.

Definitions and preliminaries

2.1
Labelled binary trees and maximum agreement subtree

A
finite tree
is a finite, connected graph with no cycles.
A
finite binary tree

t
is a finite tree where all the vertices have degree either
3
or
1
.
The vertices of degree
3
will be called the
nodes
of
t
, and the vertices of degree
1
will be called its
leaves
.
A
labelled binary tree of size
n\geq 2
is a finite binary tree with exactly
n
leaves (and therefore
n-2
nodes), some of which are labelled by integers so that each label appears at most once. By convention, we also say that a single vertex with no edge is a binary tree of size
1
, and the empty tree is a binary tree of size
0
.

For
n\geq 1
, we denote by
\mathcal{B}_{n}
the set of such trees where all
n
leaves are labelled and the labels are
1,\dots,n
, up to isomorphism (these are sometimes called
cladograms
in the literature).
We highlight that the trees that we consider are not rooted, and they are not plane trees (i.e. there is no clockwise ordering of the neighbours of a fixed vertex).

We recall that for all
n\geq 3
, we have

\#\mathcal{B}_{n}=(2n-5)!!=1\cdot 3\cdot\dots\cdot(2n-7)\cdot(2n-5).

In all the paper, we will denote by
T_{n}
a uniform random variable on
\mathcal{B}_{n}
.
We will also denote by
T^{\prime}_{n}
an independent copy of
T_{n}
. If
t\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
and
I
is a subset of
\{1,\dots,n\}
, we will denote by
t|_{I}
the
subtree of
t
induced by
I
.
More precisely, this is the labelled binary tree obtained from
t
by keeping only the paths joining the labels of
I
together, and by contracting the vertices of degree
2
that may appear in the process (see Figure
1
). Note that
t|_{I}
does not belong to
\mathcal{B}_{\#I}
, unless
I=\{1,\dots,k\}
. If
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
, we write

\mathrm{MAST}(t,t^{\prime})=\max\left\{\#I\,\big|\,I\subset\{1,\dots,n\}\mbox{ such that }t|_{I}=t^{\prime}|_{I}\right\},

where by
t|_{I}=t^{\prime}|_{I}
we mean that
t|_{I}
and
t^{\prime}|_{I}
are isomorphic as leaf-labelled trees.

If
v_{1},v_{2}
are nodes of a finite binary tree, a
region
r
of
t
delimited by
v_{1},v_{2}
is a connected component of the forest obtained by blowing up the nodes
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
into three leaves each. In particular, it is a labelled binary tree, where the leaves coming from
v_{1}
or
v_{2}
are unlabelled.
We write
\#r
for the number of original leaves of
t
that belong to the region
r
and call this quantity the
size
of
r
.
If
t,t^{\prime}
are two labelled binary trees and if
r\subset t
and
r^{\prime}\subset t^{\prime}
are two such regions, we may consider the quantity
\mathrm{MAST}(r,r^{\prime})
, which is the size of the largest subset
I
of
\{1,\dots,n\}
such that all the elements of
I
appear both in
r
and in
r^{\prime}
, and
r|_{I}=r^{\prime}|_{I}
.

2.2
The Brownian tree

We start with the construction of the Brownian tree
\mathcal{T}
introduced in
[
2
,
3
]
. Let
\mathbf{e}=(e_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}
be a normalized Brownian excursion (that is, a Brownian motion conditioned to stay positive in
(0,1)
and hit
0
at time
1
). For
s,t\in[0,1]
, we write
s\sim_{\mathbf{e}}t
if

e_{s}=e_{t}=\min_{[s\wedge t,s\vee t]}e_{u},

where
s\wedge t
and
s\vee t
stand respectively for
\min(s,t)
and
\max(s,t)
.
We also write

d_{\mathbf{e}}(s,t)=e_{s}+e_{t}-2\min_{[s\wedge t,s\vee t]}e_{u}.

Then
d_{\mathbf{e}}
is a pseudo-distance on
[0,1]
, i.e. it is symmetric and satisfies the triangle inequality. Moreover, we have
d_{\mathbf{e}}(s,t)=0
if and only if
s\sim_{\mathbf{e}}t
.
Then the quotient
\mathcal{T}=[0,1]/\sim_{\mathbf{e}}
equipped with
d_{\mathbf{e}}
is a random compact metric space, which we call the
Brownian tree
.
Moreover
\mathcal{T}
carries a natural probability measure, which is the pushforward of the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
under the canonical map from
[0,1]
to
\mathcal{T}
.
We will denote by
|\cdot|
this probability measure on
\mathcal{T}
, which has full support and no atom.
We recall that the metric space
\mathcal{T}
is almost surely a real tree, i.e. for all
x,y
in
\mathcal{T}
, there is a unique injective, continuous path from
x
to
y
, and this path is a geodesic.
For
m>0
, we say that a random measured metric space
(\mathcal{E},d,\mu)
is a
Brownian tree of mass
m
if
(\mathcal{E},m^{-1/2}d,m^{-1}\mu)
has the law of
\mathcal{T}
.
Note in particular that
\mathcal{T}
is a Brownian tree with mass
1
.

Let
k\geq 1
and let
(\mathcal{E},d,\mu)
be a Brownian tree with mass
m
. Conditionally on
(\mathcal{E},d,\mu)
, let
X_{1},\dots,X_{k}
be
k
i.i.d. points of
\mathcal{E}
, sampled according to (a normalized version of)
\mu
. Note that almost surely, the points
X_{i}
are all leaves, i.e.
\mathcal{E}\backslash\{X_{i}\}
is connected. We call
(\mathcal{E},d,\mu,X_{1},\dots X_{k})
a
k
-pointed Brownian tree with mass
m
.

In this work, decompositions of
\mathcal{T}
into several regions will play a crucial role. For this purpose, we will call
region of
\mathcal{T}
the closure of a connected component of
\mathcal{T}\backslash F
, where
F
is a subset of
\mathcal{T}
of cardinal
0
,
1
or
2
.

We recall that
\mathcal{T}
is almost surely a
binary
real tree, i.e. for all
x\in\mathcal{T}
, the space
\mathcal{T}\backslash\{x\}
has at most three connected components. A point
b\in\mathcal{T}
such that
\mathcal{T}\backslash\{b\}
has exactly three connected components will be called a
branching point
of
\mathcal{T}
. Moreover, we define the
size
of a branching point
b
as the measure of the smallest of the three connected components of
\mathcal{T}\backslash\{b\}
, and denote this quantity by
|b|_{\mathcal{T}}
.
Similarly, if
\mathcal{R}
is a region of
\mathcal{T}
and
b\in\mathcal{R}
is such that
\mathcal{R}\backslash\{b\}
has three connected components, the
relative size
of
b
in
\mathcal{R}
is the measure of the smallest such connected component, and is denoted by
|b|_{\mathcal{R}}
.

We recall here the definition of the Dirichlet and Beta distributions.

\frac{\Gamma(a_{1}+\dots+a_{d})}{\Gamma(a_{1})\dots\Gamma(a_{d})}\prod_{i=1}^{d}m_{i}^{a_{i}-1}

For
(W_{1},W_{2})\sim\mathrm{Dir}\left(a,b\right)

is said to have the

Lemma 5

Let

Then, with probability

\displaystyle\#R\leq n^{\varepsilon}\vee(n^{1+\varepsilon}\cdot|\mathcal{R}|).

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a region of
\mathcal{T}
delimited by at most two branching points.
This way the topological frontier
\partial\mathcal{R}
of the region
\mathcal{R}
contains at most two elements.
With the notation just above, for any
1\leq i\leq 2n-3
, we denote by
\overset{\circ}{\mathcal{T}_{i}}
the interior of the region
\mathcal{T}_{i}
, seen as a subset of
\mathcal{T}
.
We introduce

\displaystyle I=\left\{i\in\{1,\dots,2n-3\}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\overset{\circ}{\mathcal{T}_{i}}\cap\mathcal{R}\neq\emptyset\right\}.

We note that the set of edges
\{e_{i},i\in I\}
defines a region
\tilde{R}
of
T_{n}
delimited by at most two nodes; we denote
N:=\#\tilde{R}
.
By definition of
I
, the region
\tilde{R}
contains all the leaves with labels

that are such that

, so

and so

.
If

we have nothing to prove, so let us assume

.
Remark that since
\tilde{R}
is a region of
T_{n}
, it has at most two unlabelled leaves, so its number of edges satisfies
\#I\in\{2N-3,2N-1,2N+1\}

0
or
1
or
2

Now, for any

\overset{\circ}{\mathcal{T}_{i}}\cap\mathcal{R}\neq\overset{\circ}{\mathcal{T}_{i}}
then it means that
(\partial\mathcal{R})\cap\mathcal{T}_{i}\neq\emptyset

Since

has cardinality at most
2

and

(pick them arbitrarily if only

or
1

This entails
\mathcal{R}\supset\bigcup_{i\in I\setminus\{i_{1},i_{2}\}}\mathcal{T}_{i}
.

Now, let
(W_{i})_{1\leq i\leq 2n-3}
be as in the coupling of Theorem
4
(that is,
W_{i}
is the mass of the set of those points

such that

is the closest edge of

from

).
From the above reasoning, we have

\left|\mathcal{R}\right|\geq\sum_{i\in I\setminus\{i_{1},i_{2}\}}W_{i}.

(3)

We now condition on the discrete tree
T_{n}
. We recall that
(W_{i})_{1\leq i\leq 2n-3}
is independent of
T_{n}
and has Dirichlet distribution, so according to (

) we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I\setminus\{i_{1},i_{2}\}}W_{i}\sim\mathrm{Beta}\left(\frac{\#I-2}{2},\frac{2n-1-\#I}{2}\right)

conditionally on
T_{n}
. Let us now fix the region
\tilde{R}
of
T_{n}
. Using the explicit density of the
\mathrm{Beta}
distribution, we can write, recalling that
N=\#\tilde{R}\geq n^{\varepsilon}
and

:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i\in I\setminus\{i_{1},i_{2}\}}W_{j}\leq n^{-1-\varepsilon}\cdot N\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}T_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{2n-3}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{\#I-2}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{2n-1-\#I}{2}\right)}\int_{0}^{n^{-1-\varepsilon}\cdot N}x^{\frac{\#I-4}{2}}\cdot(1-x)^{\frac{2n-3-\#I}{2}}\mathrm{d}x.

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n^{\#I/2+1}}{(\#I/2)^{(\#I)/2}e^{O(\#I)}}\cdot(n^{-1-\varepsilon}N)^{\frac{\#I-4}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq n^{-\varepsilon n^{\varepsilon}+O(\frac{n^{\varepsilon}}{\log n})},

which decays faster than any polynomial in

In the above computation we have used the fact that
\frac{\Gamma(x)}{\Gamma(x-k)}\leq x^{k}
for any
x>0
and any integer

such that

, and in the end our assumption that

. Still conditionally on

corresponding to the removed edges. Combining this with (

and corresponding

\displaystyle|\mathcal{R}|\geq\sum_{i\in I\setminus{i_{1},i_{2}}}W_{i}\geq n^{-1-\varepsilon}\cdot N\geq n^{-1-\varepsilon}\cdot\#\tilde{R}\geq n^{-1-\varepsilon}\cdot\#R,

which is what we wanted to prove.

Remark 8
.

Often in the paper, it will be useful to re-interpret a quantity defined as the mass of a certain subset of
\mathcal{T}
, typically of the form
|\cup_{\mathbf{i}\in U^{k}}\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]|
with

, as the probability that a random point

falls into it. This will typically take the following form

\displaystyle\left|\bigcup_{\mathbf{i}\in U^{k}}\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right|=\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{i}_{k}(X)\in U^{k}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathcal{T},\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right)_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}}\right).

Estimates for the size of the regions.

We now present a very rough result controlling the sizes of the regions appearing in the Aldous recursive decomposition of
\mathcal{T}
.

Lemma 9
.

There are constants
C>c>0
such that almost surely, for
k
large enough, for all
\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}^{k}
, we have

e^{-Ck}\leq\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right|\leq e^{-ck}.

Proof.

The proof uses classical branching random walk arguments.
We first notice that, by the independence properties of our recursive decomposition, the process
\left(\log\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right|\right)_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}}
is a branching random walk.
That is, the vectors

\left(\log\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}1]\right|-\log\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right|,\log\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}2]\right|-\log\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right|,\log\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}3]\right|-\log\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right|\right)

for
\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}
are i.i.d. and have the law of
\left(\log W_{1},\log W_{2},\log W_{3}\right)
, where
(W_{1},W_{2},W_{3})
has distribution
\mathrm{Dir}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)
.
Therefore, for any
C>0
, using the Chernoff bound we can write, for any
\lambda>0
, for any
\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}^{k}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\log\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right|\leq-Ck\right)\leq e^{-\lambda Ck}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\lambda\log(W_{1})}\right]^{k}.

(6)

Since Dirichlet distributions have polynomial tails, we can find
\lambda
such that the expectation is finite.
We can then find
C
such that the right-hand side in the last equation is bounded by
4^{-k}
.
We conclude the proof by a union bound over
\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}^{k}
and then using the Borel–Cantelli lemma over
k
.

Similarly, for
c>0
and
\lambda>0
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\log\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right|\geq-ck\right)\leq e^{\lambda ck}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda\log(W_{1})}\right]^{k}.

Since
W_{1}
does not have an atom at zero, there exists
\lambda>0
such that
\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda\log(W_{1})}\right]<\frac{1}{5}
.
Once the value of
\lambda
is fixed, we can choose
c>0
sufficiently small so that the last right-hand side is bounded by
4^{-k}
.
This proves the other direction, again by union bound and Borel–Cantelli.
∎

1\pm\delta
at most of the scales
1\leq j\leq k
is of order
\delta^{k}
.
By choosing
\delta
small enough, we will be able to do a union bound over the possible candidate regions
r^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
.
This shows that for any small region
r^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, a homeomorphism sending
x
to a point of
r^{\prime}
will have "
\delta
-mismatches" between the ratios
\frac{\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j}(x)]\right|}{\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j-1}(x)]\right|}
and
\frac{\left|\Psi\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j}(x)]\right)\right|}{\left|\Psi\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j-1}(x)]\right)\right|}
for "many" scales
j
(this is Proposition
13
below, that we prove in Section
3.1
).
Moreover, the ratio

\frac{\left|\Psi\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{k}(x)]\right)\right|}{\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{k}(x)]\right|}

(8)

can be written as the telescopic product of those mismatches, so the existence of mismatches proves that the ratio (
8
) will vary "quite often" by a factor
1\pm\delta
.
To conclude, we will argue that for most points
x
, those mismatches cannot compensate each other.
Indeed, if for a region
\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]=\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}1]\cup\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}2]\cup\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}3]
we have
\frac{\left|\Psi\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right)\right|}{\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right|}\ll 1
, then if a mismatch makes the ratio
\frac{\left|\Psi\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}1]\right)\right|}{\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}1]\right|}
closer to
1
, it will make the ratios
\frac{\left|\Psi\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}2]\right)\right|}{\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}2]\right|}
and
\frac{\left|\Psi\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}3]\right)\right|}{\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}3]\right|}
even smaller. Another way to say this is that if
x
is picked uniformly at random in
\mathcal{T}
, the ratios (
8
) form a nonnegative martingale in
k
(see (
19
) below), so they converge almost surely. By existence of mismatches, they change by a factor
1\pm\delta
many times, so the limit of the martingale has to be
0
. This argument is done in a quantitative way in Section
3.2
.

3.1
Finding mismatches

Before giving the precise definition of mismatches, it will be convenient to restrict ourselves to a (not too small) subset of the intermediate scales. More precisely, we first notice that by construction of the Aldous recursive decomposition, if a word
\mathbf{i}
has its last letter equal to
3
, then the boundary of the region
\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]
in
\mathcal{T}
is a single point (see Figure
4
).

Definition 10
.

Let
\alpha>0
and let
\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}
with depth
k
.
We say that a scale
1\leq j\leq k-1
is
\alpha
-
good
for
\mathbf{i}
with respect to
\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right)_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}}
if
j
is odd, if
i_{j}=i_{j+1}=3
, and if furthermore

\min\left(\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j}1]\right|,\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j}2]\right|,\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j}3]\right|\right)\geq\alpha\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j}]\right|.

(9)

The reason why we want
i_{j}=3
is that it implies that there are two small regions
\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j}2],\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j}3]
whose boundary is the singleton
\{b[\mathbf{i}_{j}]\}
(see Figure
4
), and the ratio between the masses of those regions gives a convenient way to say that
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
have "different shapes" at a certain scale.
The point of the assumption (
9
) is that if the branching point
b[\mathbf{i}]
splits the region
\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]
in a very uneven way, there will be many possible choices for
\Psi(b[\mathbf{i}])
, whereas if
b[\mathbf{i}]
is "central" in
\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]
only few choices will be available.

The first step is to make sure that around most points of the tree
\mathcal{T}
, good scales represent a positive proportion of the scales.

Lemma 11
.

Let
\left(\mathcal{T},\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right)_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}}\right)
be a decomposed Brownian tree.
There exists constants
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>0
such that denoting

\displaystyle U^{k}:=\left\{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}^{k}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{i}\mbox{ has at least $\beta k$ scales that are $\alpha$-good}\right\}

and
E^{k}:=\bigcup_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}^{k}\backslash U^{k}}\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]
, then almost surely, for
k
large enough, we have

|E^{k}|<e^{-\gamma k}
.

Proof.

Let us prove that we can find
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
such that
\mathbb{E}\left[|E^{k}|\right]<e^{-2\gamma k}
, so that the conclusion of the lemma can be obtained using the Markov inequality and the Borel–Cantelli lemma.
Let
X
be a uniform point taken under the mass measure on
\mathcal{T}
independently of the decomposition.
By Remark
8
, we can rewrite
\mathbb{E}\left[|E^{k}|\right]
in the following way:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|E^{k}|\right]=\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{i}_{k}(X)\mbox{ has less than $\beta k$ scales that are $\alpha$-good}\right).

As in the proof of Lemma
7
, for
k\geq 0
, we denote by
\mathcal{G}_{k}
the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the word
\mathbf{i}_{k}(X)
and by the masses
\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j}(X)a]
for
0\leq j\leq k-1
and
a\in\{1,2,3\}
. It is clear that the event that
j
is a good scale for
\mathbf{i}_{k}(X)
belongs to
\mathcal{G}_{j+1}
.

Moreover, by Lemma
7
, for every odd
j
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mbox{$j$ is $\alpha$-good for $X$}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathcal{G}_{j-1}\right)=\frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{3}\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\min\left(W_{1},W_{2},W_{3}\right)\geq\alpha\right),

(10)

where
(W_{1},W_{2},W_{3})\sim\mathrm{Dir}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)
.
This probability is deterministic and does not depend on
j
so we denote it by
p
, and note that
p>0
if
\alpha>0
was chosen small enough. Therefore, the variables

\displaystyle\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mbox{the scale $2j+1$ is $\alpha$-good for $X$}\}}\right)_{0\leq j<\frac{k}{2}}

are i.i.d. Bernoulli variables with positive mean, which is sufficient to prove the lemma by a Chernoff bound.
∎

From now on, we will fix
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>0
that satisfy the conclusion of Lemma

and we will simply refer to an
\alpha
-good scale as a
good
scale.

Now let
0<\delta<\alpha
and let
\Psi:\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
be a homeomorphism.

Definition 12
.

Let
k\geq 0
and
\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}^{k}
. We say that a scale

is a
\delta
-
mismatch
for

at

with respect to

if it is a good scale and if we have

\max_{1\leq a\leq 3}\left|\frac{\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j}a]\right|}{\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j}]\right|}-\frac{\left|\Psi\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j}a]\right)\right|}{\left|\Psi\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j}]\right)\right|}\right|>\delta.

Since
\mathcal{T}
,

and

are fixed throughout the paper, we will often simply write "mismatch for

at
\mathbf{i}
" instead of "
\delta
-
mismatch
for
\Psi
at
\mathbf{i}
with respect to

".
Informally, the scale

at

indicates that when we perform one step of the Aldous recursive decomposition in

into three parts and its image by

Our next goal is the following result, which guarantees the existence of mismatches around most points for any homeomorphism.
It represents a significant proportion of the proof of Proposition
3
.

Proposition 13
.

There exists
\delta>0
such that almost surely, for

large enough, for any
\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}^{k}
that has at least
\beta k
good scales, for any homeomorphism

to

, one of the following holds:

1.

either
\left|\Psi\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right)\right|\leq e^{-k}\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right|

there are at least
\lfloor\frac{\beta}{2}k\rfloor
good scales in

\delta
-mismatches for
\Psi
at
\mathbf{i}

Proof.

Let
\delta\in\mathopen{(}0\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\alpha\mathclose{)}
, whose value will be specified later.
Let

.
Let

be the constant given by the lower bound in Lemma
9
, and let

be the event that for all
\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}

By Lemma

occurs for

Now let

, and let

We are interested in the existence of a homeomorphism

\displaystyle A_{J}(\mathbf{i})=\big\{\mbox{there exists a homeomorphism $\Psi:\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}^{\prime}$ such that (\ref{it:homeo:large enough branching points}), (\ref{it:homeo:image region large enough}), (\ref{it:homeo:not a mismatch}) hold for $\Psi$ and $\mathbf{i}$}\big\}.

Now let us consider what happens on the event where

.

On the event considered, (

occurs for

is good. Hence, for all

is a branching point of
\mathcal{T}
with the points

in the same connected component of
\mathcal{T}\backslash\{b_{h}\}
, and the points

Since
\Psi
is a homeomorphism, the same is true for the sequence
(c_{h})
in
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
. Moreover, we claim that the sizes of the branching points

cannot be too small.
More precisely, for all
1\leq h\leq\ell-1
, let
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{h}(\mathbf{c})
be the connected component of
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\backslash\{c_{h}\}
that contains

, with the convention that
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{c})=\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
.
In other words, we have
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{h}(\mathbf{c})=\Psi\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j_{h}+1}]\right)
.
Then for all
1\leq h\leq\ell
, we have

|c_{h}|_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{h-1}(\mathbf{c})}=|c_{h}|_{\Psi\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j_{h-1}+1}]\right)}\geq|c_{h}|_{\Psi\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j_{h}}]\right)}=\min_{a\in\{1,2,3\}}\left|\Psi\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j_{h}}a]\right)\right|.

Using successively the fact that

is not a
\delta
-mismatch and the fact that the scale

is
\alpha
-good, we deduce, for

|c_{h}|_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{h-1}(\mathbf{c})}\geq\left(\frac{\min_{a\in\{1,2,3\}}\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j_{h}}a]\right|}{\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j_{h}}]\right|}-\delta\right)\cdot\left|\Psi\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j_{h}}]\right)\right|\geq(\alpha-\delta)\left|\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{h}(\mathbf{c})\right|.

(12)

From now on, we assume
\delta<\frac{\alpha}{4}
, so that

\displaystyle|c_{h}|_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{h-1}(\mathbf{c})}\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}\left|\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{h}(\mathbf{c})\right|

for

.
For the same reasons, we also have

\displaystyle|c_{\ell}|_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\ell-1}(\mathbf{c})}\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}\left|\Psi\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j_{\ell}}]\right)\right|\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}\left|\Psi\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right)\right|

so, using (
ii
) and (

) in the definition of the event

, we have

\displaystyle|c_{\ell}|_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\ell-1}(\mathbf{c})}\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}e^{-k}\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right|\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}e^{-(C+1)k}.

Following what precedes, we define a
candidate sequence of length
\ell
as a sequence
(c_{h})_{1\leq h\leq\ell}
of branching points of
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
such that:

—

for all
1\leq h\leq\ell
, the points
c_{1},\dots,c_{h-1}
all lie in one connected component of
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\backslash\{c_{h}\}
, and the points
c_{h+1},\dots,c_{\ell}
lie in another one, denoted if

by
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{h}(\mathbf{c})
;

—

for all
1\leq h\leq\ell-1
, we have
|c_{h}|_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{h-1}(\mathbf{c})}\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}\left|\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{h}(\mathbf{c})\right|
;

—

we have
|c_{\ell}|_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\ell-1}(\mathbf{c})}\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}e^{-(C+1)k}
.

Note that crucially, the notion of candidate sequence does not depend on
\delta
. By the discussion above, any
\Psi:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
that makes the event
A_{J}(\mathbf{i})
occur must send
(b_{h})_{1\leq h\leq\ell}
to some candidate sequence of length

in
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
.
We will now provide a bound on the number of such candidate sequences in
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
.

\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
is a real tree with total mass

, which is almost surely the case for a realization of a Brownian tree.

Lemma 14
.

There exists a constant
K=K(C,\alpha,\beta)
such that almost surely, for all
\ell\geq 2
, the number of candidate sequences of length
\ell
in
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
is at most

.

The sequence
(b_{h})_{1\leq h\leq\ell}
and their positions relative to
\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j_{h}}2]
and
\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j_{h}}3]

Proof.

If
\mathbf{c}=(c_{h})_{1\leq h\leq\ell}
is a candidate sequence, we first define its
sequence of scales

\left(s_{h}(\mathbf{c})\right)_{0\leq h\leq\ell}
by

s_{h}(\mathbf{c})=\lfloor-\log\left|\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{h}(\mathbf{c})\right|\rfloor

for

, with the conventions
s_{0}(\mathbf{c})=0
and
s_{\ell}(\mathbf{c})=\lfloor-\log|c_{\ell}|_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\ell-1}(\mathbf{c})}\rfloor
.
Then
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{c})=\left(s_{h}(\mathbf{c})\right)_{1\leq h\leq\ell}
is a non-decreasing sequence of integers starting at
0
.
Moreover, by definition of a candidate sequence, the numbers
s_{h}(\mathbf{c})
are bounded above by

-\log\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}e^{-(C+1)k}\right)=-\log\frac{\alpha}{2}+(C+1)k.

Therefore, the number of possible values of the sequence
\left(s_{h}(\mathbf{c})\right)_{0\leq h\leq\ell}
is at most

\binom{\log(2/\alpha)+(C+1)k+\ell}{\ell}\leq\frac{2}{\alpha}2^{(C+2)k}.

(13)

On the other hand, for any such sequence
\mathbf{s}=(s_{h})_{0\leq h\leq\ell}
, let us bound the number of candidate sequences
\mathbf{c}
for which the scale sequence
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{c})
is
\mathbf{s}
.
For this, we start with an easy remark.
Let
\mathcal{R}
be a region of
\mathcal{T}
of mass
m_{0}
.
Then we claim the following

Claim
.

The number of branching points
c
in
\mathcal{R}
satisfying
|c|_{\mathcal{R}}\geq m
is at most
\frac{m_{0}}{m}
.

Indeed, let
S_{m}
be the set of branching points of size at least

in
\mathcal{R}
.
Then
\mathcal{R}
can be obtained by gluing the connected components of
\mathcal{R}\backslash S_{m}
along the structure of a finite binary tree.
The nodes of this binary tree correspond to the points of
S_{m}
, so this binary tree has
\#S_{m}
nodes and therefore
\#S_{m}+2
leaves.
Those
\#S_{m}+2
leaves correspond to
\#S_{m}+2
disjoint parts of
\mathcal{R}
with mass at least

each, so
\left(\#S_{m}+2\right)m\leq m_{0}
and the claim follows.

Now, let
\mathbf{s}=(s_{h})_{0\leq h\leq\ell}
be a non-decreasing sequence of integers with

s_{\ell}\leq\log\frac{2}{\alpha}+(C+1)\ell
.
Let us build step by step a candidate sequence

For
\mathbf{c}
to be a candidate sequence,

needs to satisfy

\displaystyle|c_{1}|_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{c})}=|c_{1}|_{\mathcal{T}}\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}\cdot|\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{1}(\mathbf{c})|\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}\cdot e^{-(s_{1}(\mathbf{c})+1)}

s_{1}(\mathbf{c})
.
Using the last display and the claim, the number of possible choices for
c_{1}
for which
s_{1}(\mathbf{c})=s_{1}
is at most
\frac{2}{\alpha}e^{s_{1}+1}
.

Let

and assume that

Then
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{h-2}(\mathbf{c})

is a connected component of
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{h-2}(\mathbf{c})\backslash\{c_{h-1}\}
, so there are only

\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{h-1}(\mathbf{c})
.
Moreover, once this region has been chosen, the point

must be a point of
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{h-1}(\mathbf{c})

|c_{h}|_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{h-1}(\mathbf{c})}\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}\left|\mathcal{R}_{h}^{\prime}(\mathbf{c})\right|\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}e^{-(s_{h}(\mathbf{c})+1)}

.

Finally, by the same reasoning, the number of possible choices for

is bounded above by

\frac{6}{\alpha}e^{(C+1)\ell-s_{\ell-1}}.

Using the above in cascade and reducing the telescopic product we get that, for any

, the number of candidate sequences
\mathbf{c}
such that

is bounded above by

\left(\frac{6}{\alpha}\right)^{\ell}e^{(C+2)\ell}.

Combined with (

), this proves the lemma, with

\displaystyle K=\frac{6}{\alpha}\cdot e^{C+2}\cdot 2^{\frac{2C+4}{\beta}}.

(14)

.

3.2
The martingale argument

For the next part of the argument, we need to introduce another notion of mismatch that is slightly looser than the one of Definition

From now on, we fix a value of
\delta>0
that satisfies the conclusion of Proposition

Suppose that
\left(\mathcal{T},\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right)_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}}\right)
is a decomposed Brownian tree and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
is another Brownian tree, and that
\Psi:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
is a homeomorphism.

Definition 15
.

Let
j\geq 1
, and let
\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}
of depth at least
j+1
. We say that the scale
j
is a weak mismatch for
\Psi
at

if
\frac{\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j}a]\right|}{\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j}]\right|}\geq\alpha
for all

and furthermore

\max_{a\in\{1,2,3\}}\left|\frac{|\Psi(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j}a])|}{|\Psi(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j}])|}-\frac{|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j}a]|}{|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j}]|}\right|>\delta.

In particular, a mismatch as defined in Definition
12
is also a weak mismatch.
The difference between the two definitions is that we have removed the "topological" part of the assumption that

has to be a good scale,
i.e.
that

(see Definition
10
).
In this section, we prove the following result.

Proposition 16
.

Let
\beta
be as in Lemma
11
.
There exists a constant
\eta>0
such that for any homeomorphism
\Psi
from
\mathcal{T}
to
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, for any

, the set

\displaystyle V^{k,\Psi}:=\left\{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}^{k}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\Psi\text{ has more than $\frac{\beta}{2}k$ weak mismatches at $\mathbf{i}$ and }\frac{\left|\Psi\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right)\right|}{\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right|}\geq e^{-\eta k}\right\}

is such that

\displaystyle\left|\bigcup_{\mathbf{i}\in V^{k,\Psi}}\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right|<e^{-\eta k}.

We highlight that the result is in fact deterministic: it holds almost surely for realizations of Brownian trees
\mathcal{T},\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, decomposition
(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}])_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}^{k}}
and any homeomorphism
\Psi
.
In what follows, we will refer to weak mismatches as simply mismatches.

Proof.

Although the result is deterministic, we will give a probabilistic proof
by interpreting the mass of a subset of
\mathcal{T}
as the probability that a point
X
sampled uniformly in
\mathcal{T}
belongs to that subset, as explained in Remark
8
.
In all the proof, we treat
\mathcal{T},\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
as deterministic, compact real trees, equipped with a nonatomic mass measure, and we also treat
\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]\right)_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}}
as deterministic.
We pick
X
in
\mathcal{T}
according to its mass measure.
For
j\geq 0
, we denote by
\mathcal{H}_{j}
the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathbf{i}_{j}(X)
, so that
(\mathcal{H}_{j})_{j\geq 0}
is a filtration.
Note that since
X
is uniform, we have for
j\geq 0
and

\mathbb{P}\left(i_{j+1}(X)=a\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathcal{H}_{j}\right)=\frac{\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j}(X)a]\right|}{\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j}(X)]\right|}.

(18)

We also note that if
0\leq j\leq k
, then the event that scale
j
is a mismatch for
\Psi
at
\mathbf{i}_{k}(X)
is
\mathcal{H}_{j}
-measurable
2
2

2

This is the reason why we are looking at the weak mismatches of Definition
15
, and not at the mismatches of Definition
12
: the assumption
i_{j+1}=3
in the definition of a good scale is not
\mathcal{H}_{j}
-measurable.
, since it only depends on
\mathbf{i}_{j}(X)
.
Now, for
j\geq 0
, we define

M_{j}:=\frac{\left|\Psi\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j}(X)]\right)\right|}{\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j}(X)]\right|}.

(19)

A simple computation using (
18

(M_{j})_{j\geq 0}
is an
(\mathcal{H}_{j})
-martingale.

The idea behind Proposition
16
is that a mismatch gives an opportunity for
M_{j+1}
to be significantly different from
M_{j}
. Since the martingale
M
is positive, it converges almost surely, and if its value changes often the limit has to be
0
.
To obtain a quantitative version of this intuition, we will study
(\log M_{j})_{j\geq 0}
, which is a supermartingale. We will use the fact that the steps corresponding to mismatches tend to bring the value of this process down by more than an additive constant in expectation. Hence, after a large number
k
of steps, either we have seen few mismatches or
\log M
has gone down by a lot.

More precisely, let us fix a constant
\mu>0
(to be precised later). For
j\geq 1
, we introduce

\displaystyle Z_{j}:=\log M_{j}-\log M_{j-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{Z}_{j}=Z_{j}+\mu\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\{\text{scale $j-1$ is a mismatch for $\mathbf{i}_{k}(X)$}\}}.

We will prove that if
\mu>0
is chosen sufficiently small, then for all

, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{Z}_{j}\right)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathcal{H}_{j-1}\right]\leq 1.

(20)

From here, the result follows from using a Chernoff bound. Indeed, using the last display in cascade, we obtain
\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\widetilde{Z}_{j}\right)\right]\leq 1
so that we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\widetilde{Z}_{j}\geq 2\eta k\right)\leq\exp(-\eta k).

Writing
\sum_{j=1}^{k}\widetilde{Z}_{j}=\log M_{k}+\mu\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\text{scale $j-1$ is a mismatch for $\mathbf{i}_{k}(X)$}\}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\log M_{k}\geq(2\eta-\mu\beta/2)k\text{ and }\mathbf{i}_{k}(X)\text{ has more than $\frac{\beta}{2}k$ mismatches}\right)\leq\exp(-\eta k),

which is what we want to prove if we set
\eta=\frac{\mu\beta}{8}
.

So now, we only have to prove (
20
) for some value
\mu>0
.
First, on the event that scale
j-1
is not a mismatch, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\widetilde{Z}_{j}}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathcal{H}_{j-1}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{Z_{j}}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathcal{H}_{j-1}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{M_{j}}{M_{j-1}}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathcal{H}_{j-1}\right]=1

by the martingale property. Therefore, by concavity of
x\mapsto x^{\frac{1}{2}}
and Jensen’s inequality, we have
\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{Z}_{j}}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathcal{H}_{j-1}\right]\leq 1^{\frac{1}{2}}=1
.
Hence we only have to focus on the event where scale

is a mismatch.
For this, we use the following lemma.

Lemma 17
.

There exists
\mu=\mu(\alpha,\delta)
such that the following holds.
Let
(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})
and
(q_{1},q_{2},q_{3})
be two elements of the simplex

, and let

be a random variable given by

\displaystyle Z=\log q_{I}-\log p_{I},\qquad\text{where for all }i\in\{1,2,3\},\quad\mathbb{P}\left(I=i\right)=p_{i}.

If
\max_{1\leq i\leq 3}|p_{i}-q_{i}|\geq\delta
and
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\geq\alpha
,
then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}(Z+\mu)\right)\right]\leq 1.

(21)

We then apply the lemma to the random variable
\widetilde{Z}_{j}
conditionally on
\mathcal{H}_{j-1}
, on the event that
j-1
is a mismatch, by taking
p_{i}=\frac{\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j-1}(X)i]\right|}{\left|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j-1}(X)]\right|}
and
q_{i}=\frac{\left|\Psi\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j-1}(X)i]\right)\right|}{\left|\Psi\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}_{j-1}(X)]\right)\right|}
.
This ensures that (
20
) is satisfied for the appropriate choice of
\mu
given in the lemma.
∎

Proof of Lemma
17
.

For
\mu>0
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}(Z+\mu)\right)\right]=e^{\frac{1}{2}\mu}\sum_{i=1}^{3}p_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot q_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

For each term, we have
p_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot q_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{p_{i}+q_{i}}{2}
. Moreover, let
a
be such that
|p_{a}-q_{a}|\geq\delta
. We have

p_{a}^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot q_{a}^{\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{p_{a}+q_{a}}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left(p_{a}^{\frac{1}{2}}-q_{a}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{p_{a}+q_{a}}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)^{2},

since
\sqrt{y}-\sqrt{x}\geq\frac{1}{2}(y-x)
for
0\leq x\leq y\leq 1
. Hence, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}(Z+\mu)\right)\right]=e^{\frac{1}{2}\mu}\sum_{i=1}^{3}p_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot q_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq e^{\frac{1}{2}\mu}\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}}{8}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{p_{i}+q_{i}}{2}\right)=e^{\frac{1}{2}\mu}\left(1-\frac{\delta^{2}}{8}\right).

This proves our claim, by taking
\mu>0
small enough.
∎

\displaystyle\mathrm{diam}(\Psi(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]))\underset{\eqref{eq:diam of region is smaller than mass to some power}}{\leq}|\Psi(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}])|^{\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}\underset{\eqref{eq:mass of Psi of a region is smaller than mass to some power}}{\leq}|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]|^{(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)(1+\frac{\eta}{C})}\underset{\eqref{eq:diam is at least mass to the half minus epsilon}}{\leq}\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}])^{(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)(2-\varepsilon)(1+\frac{\eta}{C})}.

4.2
Maximum agreement subtree bound

In this section, we prove Theorem

The first one, Corollary

We will only state this lemma here, and prove it in the next subsection.

Corollary 18
.

Let

be a decomposed Brownian tree and let
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
be an independent Brownian tree. There exists a constant

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}^{k}}\sqrt{|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]|\cdot|\Psi(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}])|}\leq\exp(-\rho\cdot k).

Proof.

Let
\xi,\eta>0
be given by Proposition
3

\Psi:\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
be a homeomorphism.
For

defined as in Proposition

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}^{k}}\sqrt{|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]|\cdot|\Psi(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}])|}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}^{k}\setminus U^{k,\Psi}}\sqrt{|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]|\cdot|\Psi(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}])|}+\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in U^{k,\Psi}}\sqrt{|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]|\cdot|\Psi(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}])|}

\displaystyle<e^{-\rho k},

where

4.3
The refined square root bound

This section is devoted to proving Lemma
19
.
Before getting to the proof, we will first state and prove two other intermediate results, Lemma
20
and Lemma
21
.

First, Lemma
20
, stated below, is a consequence of
[
10
, Lemma 4.1]
, see also
[
7
, Lemma 4.1, Proposition 4.2]
, but expressed in a slightly different context.
We provide a quick proof for completeness, adapted from the same references. We say that a random labelled tree is
exchangeable
if its distribution is invariant under uniform random permutation of the labels on the leaves.

Lemma 20
.

Suppose that
S_{m}
and
S_{m}^{\prime}
are independent exchangeable random variables on
\mathcal{B}_{m}
with
m
leaves (with possibly different distribution). Then for any
s\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{MAST}(S_{m},S_{m}^{\prime})\geq s\right)\leq\binom{m}{s}\frac{2^{s-2}s}{s!}.

In particular

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{MAST}(S_{m},S_{m}^{\prime})\geq 2e\sqrt{m}\right)\leq\exp\left(-2e(\log 2)\sqrt{m}+o(\sqrt{m})\right)

as
m\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof.

We use a first moment method to write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{MAST}(S_{m},S_{m}^{\prime})\geq s\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subset\{1,\dots,m\}\\
\#A=s\end{subarray}}\mathbbm{1}_{S_{m}|_{A}=S_{m}^{\prime}|_{A}}\right]

\displaystyle=\binom{m}{s}\mathbb{P}\left((S_{m})|_{\{1,\dots,s\}}=(S_{m}^{\prime})|_{\{1,\dots,s\}}\right),

where the last equality follows from the exchangeability of the leaf-labels.
Now, we can bound the probability appearing on the right-hand-side of the last display by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left((S_{m})|_{\{1,\dots,s\}}=(S_{m}^{\prime})|_{\{1,\dots,s\}}\right)\leq\sup_{t\in\mathcal{B}_{s}}\mathbb{P}\left((S_{m})|_{\{1,\dots,s\}}=t\right).

For
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{s}
we write
t\sim t^{\prime}
if
t^{\prime}
can be obtained from
t
by relabeling its leaves.
Note that this defines an equivalence relation on
\mathcal{B}_{s}
.
Then for any random variable
T_{s}
on
\mathcal{B}_{s}
that satisfy the exchangeability property, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(T_{s}=t\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(T_{s}\sim t\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(T_{s}=t\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}T_{s}\sim t\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(T_{s}=t\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}T_{s}\sim t\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\#\left\{t^{\prime}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}t^{\prime}\sim t\right\}}.

Since the distribution of
(S_{m})|_{\{1,\dots,s\}}
satisfies the exchangeability condition, we just need to check that the number appearing at the denominator on the right-hand-side of the inequality is bounded from below by
\frac{s!}{2^{s-2}s}
for any
t\in\mathcal{B}_{s}
.
For that, it suffices to prove that the number of graph automorphisms of any tree
t\in\mathcal{B}_{s}
is bounded below by
2^{s-2}s
. This follows from the fact that an automorphism of a tree is determined by the image of one leaf and a cyclic ordering of the edges around each node. This proves the first claim of the lemma, and the second follows by the Stirling formula.
∎

In the proof of Lemma
19
, the above result will be used to control the size of the MAST of two regions
R\subset T_{n}
and
R^{\prime}\subset T_{n}
in terms of their number of common labels.
The goal of the next result is to bound the number of these common labels in terms of
m=\#R
and
m^{\prime}=\#R^{\prime}
.

Lemma 21
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
.
Let
S
and
S^{\prime}
be two independent uniform random subsets of
\{1,\dots,n\}
of respective sizes
m,m^{\prime}
. Then we have the following bound:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\#(S\cap S^{\prime})\geq 8\cdot\left(n^{\varepsilon}\vee\frac{mm^{\prime}}{n}\right)\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2n^{\varepsilon}}{3}\right).

Proof.

We write
\#(S\cap S^{\prime})
as a sum of indicators
\#(S\cap S^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{i\in S\cap S^{\prime}}
. We also denote by
(\mathcal{H}_{i})_{0\leq i\leq n}
the filtration generated by the sequence
(\mathbbm{1}_{i\in S},\mathbbm{1}_{i\in S^{\prime}})_{1\leq i\leq n}
. We can check that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(i+1\in S\cap S^{\prime}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathcal{H}_{i}\right)=\frac{m-\sum_{j=1}^{i}\mathbbm{1}_{i\in S}}{n-i}\cdot\frac{m^{\prime}-\sum_{j=1}^{i}\mathbbm{1}_{i\in S^{\prime}}}{n-i}\leq\frac{mm^{\prime}}{(n-i)^{2}}.

Therefore, we have the following stochastic domination

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{n}{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{i\in S\cap S^{\prime}}\preceq_{\mathrm{st}}\mathrm{Bin}\left(\frac{n}{2},1\wedge\frac{4mm^{\prime}}{n^{2}}\right)\preceq_{\mathrm{st}}\mathrm{Bin}\left(\frac{n}{2},1\wedge 4\cdot\left(n^{\varepsilon-1}\vee\frac{mm^{\prime}}{n^{2}}\right)\right).

By shuffling the indices, the same domination holds for
\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{n}{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{i\in S\cap S^{\prime}}
, so we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\#(S\cap S^{\prime})\geq 8\cdot\left(n^{\varepsilon}\vee\frac{mm^{\prime}}{n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{n}{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{i\in S\cap S^{\prime}}\geq 4\cdot\left(n^{\varepsilon}\vee\frac{mm^{\prime}}{n}\right)\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=\frac{n}{2}+1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{i\in S\cap S^{\prime}}\geq 4\cdot\left(n^{\varepsilon}\vee\frac{mm^{\prime}}{n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2n^{\varepsilon}}{3}\right),

where for the last inequality we use that for a binomial random variable
X
with expectation
\mu
, we have
\mathbb{P}\left(X\geq(1+\varepsilon)\mu\right)\leq\exp(\frac{\varepsilon\mu}{3})
.
∎

We can finally prove Lemma
19
.

1
, we can take
\theta=\frac{1}{2C}=\frac{1}{15}
, and finally
\varepsilon_{\ref{thm_MAST_upper}}<\frac{\rho\theta}{2}
, so we can take
\varepsilon_{\ref{thm_MAST_upper}}=10^{-338}
.
Similarly
\varepsilon_{\ref{thm_holder_homeo}}
has to be chosen such that
\varepsilon_{\ref{thm_holder_homeo}}<\frac{\eta}{2C}=\frac{\eta}{15}
, so we can take
\varepsilon_{\ref{thm_holder_homeo}}=10^{-338}
.

5.2
Remarks and open questions

The expected maximum agreement subtree.

In all the arguments of the paper, the estimates that are stated with probability
1-o_{n}(1)
actually hold with probability
1-O(n^{-a})
for some (small)
a>0
(or with probability
1-O(e^{-ak})
, which is equivalent since we take
k
of order
\log n
). Hence, by Theorem
1
and Lemma
20
, we can write

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{MAST}(T_{n},T^{\prime}_{n})\geq n^{1/2-\varepsilon_{\ref{thm_MAST_upper}}}\right)\leq n^{-a},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{MAST}(T_{n},T^{\prime}_{n})\geq Cn^{1/2}\right)\leq e^{-c\sqrt{n}}

for some constants
C,c>0
. Therefore, since
\mathrm{MAST}(T_{n},T^{\prime}_{n})
is bounded by
n
, we can write

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{MAST}(T_{n},T^{\prime}_{n})\right]\leq n^{1/2-\varepsilon_{\ref{thm_MAST_upper}}}+n^{-a}\times C\sqrt{n}+e^{-c\sqrt{n}}\times n=O\left(n^{1/2-\min(\varepsilon_{\ref{thm_MAST_upper}},a)}\right),

so the
expected
MAST is also much less than
\sqrt{n}
.

Other models of random trees.

Another natural random tree model where the Maximum Agreement Subtree has been investigated
[
10
,
7
]
is the Yule–Harding model
Y_{n}
, i.e. the model where the binary tree
Y_{n}
is obtained from
Y_{n-1}
by choosing a leaf uniformly at random and splitting it into one node and two leaves. The best known lower and upper bounds for this model are given by
[
7
]
and are respectively of order
n^{0.344}
and
\sqrt{n}
.
It seems likely to us that adaptations of the ideas developed in the present paper could be used to prove Theorem
1
for the Yule–Harding model.

Beyond the binary case, another natural question would be to try to estimate the MAST between more general Galton–Watson trees.
We believe that similar results could be obtained provided the tail of the offspring distribution is light enough (with the technical difficulty that the coupling between the discrete model and the Brownian tree would not be as simple).
On the other hand, when the tail is very heavy, the MAST should become larger because of star-shaped subtrees.

Optimal regularity for homeomorphisms between continuous random structures.

It seems natural to introduce the following quantity

\displaystyle\gamma_{+}:=\inf\left\{\gamma\geq 0\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\text{there a.s. exists no homeomorphism $\Psi:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}^{\prime}$ that is $\gamma$-H\"{o}lder}\right\},

which, in some sense, captures how metrically different two independent realizations of the Brownian tree are.
Theorem
2
ensures that
\gamma_{+}\leq 1-10^{-338}
, and as it was pointed out in the introduction, Aldous’s construction in
[
5
]
amounts to constructing a
(5-2\sqrt{6})
-Hölder homeomorphism between
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
so
\gamma_{+}\geq 5-2\sqrt{6}\approx 0.1010
.
It would be an interesting direction of research to find tighter bounds on
\gamma_{+}
or a good heuristic as to what the value of
\gamma_{+}
may be.

The question of finding the optimal regularity for homeomorphisms between independent copies of random metric spaces can be asked for many other models.
It is for example natural to ask whether an analog of Theorem
2
could be proved for some models with the topology of the plane such as the Brownian map
[
22
,
24
]
, or more generally Liouville Quantum Gravity metrics
[
17
]
.

Appendix A
Construction of a Hölder homeomorphism

Theorem 22
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
be two independent copies of the Brownian tree.
There almost surely exists a homeomorphism
\Psi
from
\mathcal{T}
to
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
such that
\Psi
and
\Psi^{-1}
are both
\gamma
-Hölder, for any
\gamma<5-2\sqrt{6}
.

Proof.

Consider the Aldous decomposition of the tree
\mathcal{T}
, as described in Section
2.5
, with the associated collection
(x[\mathbf{i}])_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}\backslash\{\emptyset\}}
of points and the collection
(b[\mathbf{i}])_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}}
of branching points.
Let also
\left(\mathcal{T}^{\prime},(x^{\prime}[\mathbf{i}])_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}\backslash\{\emptyset\}},(b^{\prime}[\mathbf{i}])_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}}\right)
be an independent copy of
\left(\mathcal{T},(x[\mathbf{i}])_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}\backslash\{\emptyset\}},(b[\mathbf{i}])_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}}\right)
, so that in particular
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
are Brownian trees.
We construct a map
\Psi:\left\{b[\mathbf{i}]\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}\right\}\rightarrow\left\{b^{\prime}[\mathbf{i}]\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}\right\}
by setting
\Psi(b[\mathbf{i}])=b^{\prime}[\mathbf{i}]
.
We are going to show that for any
\gamma<5-2\sqrt{6}
, this map is almost surely
\gamma
-Hölder.
Then, by density of the set of branching points that we consider, we can extend
\Psi
in a unique way into a
\gamma
-Hölder map from
\mathcal{T}
to
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
.
By symmetry, the same can be done with
\Psi^{-1}
, so the map
\Psi
is indeed a homeomorphism, with
\Psi
and
\Psi^{-1}
that are
\gamma
-Hölder, almost surely.
Since this holds almost surely for a countable sequence of values of
\gamma
tending to
5-2\sqrt{6}
, the claim of the theorem holds.

From now on, we fix
\gamma<5-2\sqrt{6}
and introduce, for any
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle S_{k}:=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}\\
|\mathbf{i}|,|\mathbf{j}|\leq k\end{subarray}}\frac{d_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}(b^{\prime}[\mathbf{i}],b^{\prime}[\mathbf{j}])}{\left(d_{\mathcal{T}}(b[\mathbf{i}],b[\mathbf{j}])\right)^{\gamma}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad S:=\sup_{\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}}\frac{d_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}(b^{\prime}[\mathbf{i}],b^{\prime}[\mathbf{j}])}{\left(d_{\mathcal{T}}(b[\mathbf{i}],b[\mathbf{j}])\right)^{\gamma}}=\sup_{k\geq 1}S_{k}.

Proving that
\Psi
is a.s.
\gamma
-Hölder continuous on
\left\{b[\mathbf{i}]\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}\right\}
reduces to proving that
S
is almost surely finite.
We prove that claim in two steps:

—

First, we obtain a control on the distances between "neighbor branching points" of level
k
that will hold true simultaneously for all pairs of such points.
Such points appear as pairs of the form
(x[\mathbf{i}1],x[\mathbf{i}2])
in the decomposition described in Section
2.5
.
This estimate is the content of the next lemma.

—

Then, we use this estimate to bound
S_{k}-S_{k-1}
, so that
S_{k}
increases to a finite limit.

Lemma 23
.

Almost surely, for
k
large enough, for any
\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}^{k}
, we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}\left(x^{\prime}[\mathbf{i}1],x^{\prime}[\mathbf{i}2]\right)\leq k^{-2}\cdot d_{\mathcal{T}}\left(x[\mathbf{i}1],x[\mathbf{i}2]\right)^{\gamma}.

(28)

Proof of Lemma
23
.

Recall Proposition
6
, which states that for any
\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}^{k}
, conditionally on
\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]
, the region
(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}],x[\mathbf{i}1],x[\mathbf{i}2])
has the distribution of a bi-pointed Brownian tree of mass
|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]|
. Therefore, for any
\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}
, conditionally on
|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]|
, the distance
d_{\mathcal{T}}\left(x[\mathbf{i}1],x[\mathbf{i}2]\right)
, is distributed as

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{T}}\left(x[\mathbf{i}1],x[\mathbf{i}2]\right)\overset{\mathrm{d}}{=}|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]|^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot Y,

where
Y
has the Rayleigh distribution with density
4xe^{-2x^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}x
on
\mathopen{[}0\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\infty\mathclose{)}
, see for example
[
21
, Theorem 2.11]
.
The same is of course true for analogous quantities in
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
.

For any
\gamma\in\mathopen{(}0\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},1\mathclose{)}
and
\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}^{k}
we write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(d_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}\left(x^{\prime}[\mathbf{i}1],x^{\prime}[\mathbf{i}2]\right)>k^{-2}\cdot d_{\mathcal{T}}\left(x[\mathbf{i}1],x[\mathbf{i}2]\right)^{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{R}^{\prime}[\mathbf{i}]|^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot Y^{\prime}>k^{-2}\cdot\left(|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]|^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot Y\right)^{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{R}^{\prime}[\mathbf{i}]|^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot Y^{\prime}}{\left(\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]|^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot Y\right)^{\gamma}}>k^{-2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq k^{2\lambda}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathcal{R}^{\prime}[\mathbf{i}]|^{\frac{\lambda}{2}}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]|^{-\frac{\lambda\gamma}{2}}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[(Y^{\prime})^{\lambda}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[Y^{-\gamma\lambda}\right],

where in the last line we raised everything to some power
\lambda<\frac{2}{\gamma}
and used the Markov inequality. The condition
\lambda<\frac{2}{\gamma}
ensures that the quantity
C(\gamma,\lambda):=\mathbb{E}\left[(Y^{\prime})^{\lambda}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[Y^{-\gamma\lambda}\right]
is finite.
On the other hand, it follows from Proposition
6
that
|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]|
is the product of
k
i.i.d.
\mathrm{Beta}(1/2,1)
-random variables.
Since the
p
-th moment of such a Beta variable is given by
\frac{1}{2p+1}
for
p>-\frac{1}{2}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathcal{R}^{\prime}[\mathbf{i}]|^{\frac{\lambda}{2}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{i}]|^{-\frac{\lambda\gamma}{2}}\right]=\left(\frac{1}{(1+\lambda)(1-\gamma\lambda)}\right)^{k}.

The expression
(1+\lambda)(1-\gamma\lambda)
appearing in the denominator is maximized in
\lambda
at
\lambda=\frac{1-\gamma}{2\gamma}<\frac{2}{\gamma}
and the value of the maximum is
1+\frac{(1-\gamma)^{2}}{4\gamma}
.
This value decreases in
\gamma
and attains
3
at
5-2\sqrt{6}
.
Hence, for
\gamma<5-2\sqrt{6}
we can write a union bound over all
\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}^{k}
to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}^{k}:d_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}\left(x^{\prime}[\mathbf{i}1],x^{\prime}[\mathbf{i}2]\right)>k^{-2}\cdot d_{\mathcal{T}}\left(x[\mathbf{i}1],x[\mathbf{i}2]\right)^{\gamma}\right)\leq k^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}\cdot\left(\frac{3}{1+\frac{(1-\gamma)^{2}}{4\gamma}}\right)^{k}\cdot C(\gamma,\textstyle\frac{1-\gamma}{2\gamma}),

which is summable in
k\geq 1
. This ensures using the Borel-Cantelli lemma that our lemma holds true.
∎

From now on, we let
K_{0}
be the first time for which (
28
) holds for all
k\geq K_{0}
, and fix
k\geq K_{0}
.
Consider two indices in
\mathbb{T}_{3}
, say
\mathbf{i}
and
\mathbf{j}
, such that
|\mathbf{i}|,|\mathbf{j}|\leq k
, and consider the path
p_{\mathbf{i}\to\mathbf{j}}
in the tree
\mathcal{T}
going from
b[\mathbf{i}]
to
b[\mathbf{j}]
.
We denote by
\mathbf{l}
the index such that
b[\mathbf{l}]
is the first point of
\left\{b[\mathbf{i}]\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{3},\ |\mathbf{i}|\leq k-1\right\}
visited by
p_{\mathbf{i}\to\mathbf{j}}
(note that this index can possibly be
\mathbf{i}
itself, if
|\mathbf{i}|\leq k-1
).
Similarly we let
\mathbf{m}
be so that
b[\mathbf{m}]
is the last such point.
We can then write

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{T}}(b[\mathbf{i}],b[\mathbf{j}])=d_{\mathcal{T}}(b[\mathbf{i}],b[\mathbf{l}])+d_{\mathcal{T}}(b[\mathbf{l}],b[\mathbf{m}])+d_{\mathcal{T}}(b[\mathbf{m}],b[\mathbf{j}]).

Now, either
b[\mathbf{i}]=b[\mathbf{l}]
, in which case their distance is
0
, or
b[\mathbf{i}]\neq b[\mathbf{l}]
and in that case this pair of branching points can be written as
\{b[\mathbf{i}],b[\mathbf{l}]\}=\{x[\mathbf{u}1],x[\mathbf{u}2]\}
for some
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{T}_{3}
with
|\mathbf{u}|=k+1
. Indeed, two consecutive branching points of depth at most
k
along
p_{\mathbf{i}\to\mathbf{j}}
are always of this form, and it always holds that one has depth exactly
k
and the other at most
k-1
, so
b[\mathbf{l}]
is either
b[\mathbf{i}]
or the second branching point of
p_{\mathbf{i}\to\mathbf{j}}
. The same considerations hold in
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
so in any case, by Lemma
23
, we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}\left(b^{\prime}[\mathbf{i}],b^{\prime}[\mathbf{l}]\right)\leq(k+1)^{-2}\cdot d_{\mathcal{T}}\left(b[\mathbf{i}],b[\mathbf{l}]\right)^{\gamma},

and the analogous inequality is true for
d_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}(b^{\prime}[\mathbf{m}],b^{\prime}[\mathbf{j}])
.

Therefore, for
k\geq K_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}\left(b^{\prime}[\mathbf{i}],b^{\prime}[\mathbf{j}]\right)

\displaystyle=d_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}\left(b^{\prime}[\mathbf{i}],b^{\prime}[\mathbf{l}]\right)+d_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}\left(b^{\prime}[\mathbf{l}],b^{\prime}[\mathbf{m}]\right)+d_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}\left(b^{\prime}[\mathbf{m}],b^{\prime}[\mathbf{j}]\right)

\displaystyle\leq(k+1)^{-2}\cdot d_{\mathcal{T}}\left(b[\mathbf{i}],b[\mathbf{l}]\right)^{\gamma}+S_{k-1}\cdot d_{\mathcal{T}}\left(b[\mathbf{l}],b[\mathbf{m}]\right)^{\gamma}+(k+1)^{-2}\cdot d_{\mathcal{T}}\left(b[\mathbf{m}],b[\mathbf{j}]\right)^{\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq\left((k+1)^{-\frac{2}{1-\gamma}}+S_{k-1}^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}+(k+1)^{-\frac{2}{1-\gamma}}\right)^{1-\gamma}\cdot\left(d_{\mathcal{T}}(b[\mathbf{i}],b[\mathbf{l}])+d_{\mathcal{T}}(b[\mathbf{l}],b[\mathbf{m}])+d_{\mathcal{T}}(b[\mathbf{m}],b[\mathbf{j}])\right)^{\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq\left(S_{k-1}^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}+2(k+1)^{-\frac{2}{1-\gamma}}\right)^{1-\gamma}\cdot d_{\mathcal{T}}\left(b[\mathbf{i}],b[\mathbf{j}]\right)^{\gamma},

where we used the Hölder inequality with
p=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}
and
q=\frac{1}{\gamma}
.
This ensures that for
k\geq K_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle S_{k}\leq\left(S_{k-1}^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}+2(k+1)^{-\frac{2}{1-\gamma}}\right)^{1-\gamma}\leq S_{k-1}+\frac{2}{(k+1)^{2}},

and so
\sup_{k\geq 1}S_{k}<\infty
. This concludes the proof.
∎
