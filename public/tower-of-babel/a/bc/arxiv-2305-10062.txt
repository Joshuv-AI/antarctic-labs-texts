A generalization of Duistermaat and Van der Kallens theorem

1
Introduction

In 1939, Ott-Heinrich Keller
keller
formulated what is now known as the Jacobian conjecture:

Conjecture 1
(Jacobian conjecture)

Let
k
be a field of characteristic zero and
F\in k[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]^{n}
a polynomial mapping such that its Jacobian determinant
J_{F}
is a non-zero constant.
Then
F
has an inverse function
G\in k[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]^{n}
.

Since then, this conjecture remained unsolved (apart from the case
n=1
).
For a historical treaty and a collection of results around the Jacobian conjecture, one can read
Ess0
and
Ess2
.
In 1997, Olivier Mathieu showed in
Mat
that the Jacobian conjecture follows from what is now known as the Mathieu conjecture:

Conjecture 2
(Mathieu conjecture)

Let
G
be a compact connected real Lie group with Haar measure
\sigma
.
Let
f
be a complex valued
G
-finite function on
G
such that
\int_{G}f^{m}d\sigma=0
for all
m\geq 1
.
Then for every
G
-finite function
g
on
G
, also
\int_{G}gf^{m}d\sigma=0
for all large
m
.

The Mathieu conjecture has been proved for the case of connected abelian groups by Duistermaat and Van der Kallen in 1998
DuiKal
.

From the formulation of the Mathieu conjecture, Wenhua Zhao formulated the notion of Mathieu-Zhao spaces that are a generalization of ideals.
For history and details, see
Zhao1
and
Ess2
.

Together with this formulation, we manage to prove a generalization of the theorem of Duistermaat and Van der Kallen in dimension one.

Notations and conventions

We write
{\bf Z}
for the ring of integers,
{\bf C}

\mathbb{F}_{q}
for a finite field of
q

Furthermore, we always assume all rings to be commutative and to have an identity.

3
Mathieu-Zhao spaces and partial fractions decomposition

Because of the shape of the statement in the Mathieu conjecture
Mat
, we can define the following generalisation of ideals in associative algebras, which we call Mathieu-Zhao spaces.

Definition 1

Let
R
be any ring and
A
an
R
-algebra.
For any subset
M
of
A
we define the
radical of
M
by

\displaystyle r(M):=\{a\in A\mid\forall m\gg 0:a^{m}\in M\}

with
m\gg 0
meaning all
m\geq N
for some
N\geq 1
.

Remark 1

For an ideal
I
of
R
, this coincides with the usual definition of the radical of
I
, being:

\displaystyle\sqrt{I}:=\{a\in R\mid\exists n:a^{n}\in I\}.

It is well-known that the radical of an ideal is an ideal itself, and hence all
a^{n+i}
are elements of
I
for
i\geq 0
.

Definition 2

Let
R
be any ring and
A
an
R
-algebra.
For any subset
M
of
A
we define the
(left) strong radical of
M
by

\displaystyle sr(M):=\{a\in A\mid\forall b\in A\ \forall m\gg 0:ba^{m}\in M\}.

Remark 2

Note that if
a\in sr(M)
, then
1\cdot a^{m}\in M
for all
m\gg 0
, hence
a\in r(M)
.
So we always have
sr(M)\subset r(M)
.

Definition 3

Let
R
be any ring and
A
an
R
-algebra.
We say that an
R
-submodule
M
of
A
is a Mathieu-Zhao space of
A
(short: MZ-space) if and only if
sr(M)=r(M)
.

A little less is needed in practice.
Define
r^{\prime}(M)=\{a\in A\mid\forall m\geq 1:a^{m}\in M\}
. If
r^{\prime}(M)\subset sr(M)
, then
M
is an MZ-space of
A
.

Proposition 1
(
Ess2
)

Let
R
be a ring and
A
an
R
-algebra.
Let
M
be an additive subset of
A
and
I\subset A
an ideal of
A
such that
I\subset M
.
Then
M
is an MZ-space of
A
if and only if
M/I
is an MZ-space of
A/I
.

Example 1

Let
R
be a ring and
A
an
R
-algebra.
Let
I
be an ideal of
A
.
Then
I
is an MZ-space.

Proof.

We need to show that
r^{\prime}(M)\subset sr(M)
.
Thus, let
a\in r^{\prime}(I)
be arbitrary.
Then
a\in I
. Since
I
is an ideal, we have for any
b\in A
that
ba^{n}a\in I
for all
n\geq 0
.
Therefore
a\in sr(I)
.

\triangle

3.1
Zhao’s Idempotency Theorem

One of the most important theorems regarding MZ-spaces is Zhao’s Idempotency Theorem:

Theorem 3.1
(Zhao’s Idempotency Theorem
Zhao1
)

Let
k
be a field and
A
a
k
-algebra.
Let
M
be a
k
-linear subspace of
A
such that all elements of
r(M)
are algebraic over
k
.
Then
M
is an MZ-space of
A
if and only if
Ae\subset M
for all idempotents
e
which belong to
M
.

In particular, if
M
is an MZ-space of
A
and
1\in M
, then
M=A
.

Corollary 1

Let
A
be a
k
-algebra and suppose that all elements of
r(M)
are algebraic over
k
.
Then
M
is an MZ-space of
A
if and only if
r(M)
is an ideal of
A
.

Not all MZ-spaces are ideals, as the following example shows:

Example 2

The finite field
\mathbb{F}_{4}
has the following four MZ-spaces:

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{4}\qquad\{0\}\qquad\{0,x\}\qquad\{0,x+1\}.

Proof.

The sets
\{0\}
,
\{0,x\}
and
\{0,x+1\}
are the only
{\bf Z}
-linear subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{4}
that do not contain
1
.
Since
\mathbb{F}_{4}^{*}
is cyclic, we find that
r^{\prime}(M)=\{0\}
when
M
is a
{\bf Z}
-linear subspace that does not contain
1
.
It is clear that
r^{\prime}(M)\subset sr(M)
, thus
M
is an MZ-space.

\triangle

3.2
Partial fractions decomposition

We have the partial fractions decomposition of rational maps:

Theorem 3.2

(Partial Fractions Decomposition)

Let
k
be a field,
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in k
disinct and
\alpha\in k^{*}
.
Define
U(X)=\alpha(X-a_{1})\cdots(X-a_{n})
and let
V(X)\in k[X]
with
\deg_{X}V(X)<n
be arbitrary.
Then

\displaystyle\frac{V(X)}{U(X)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{A_{i}}{X-a_{i}}

with
A_{i}=\frac{V(a_{i})}{U^{\prime}(a_{i})}
.

Proof

Note first that
U^{\prime}(a_{i})\neq 0
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
.
This is since all
a_{i}
are distinct and we know that if both
U(a_{i})=0
and
U^{\prime}(a_{i})=0
, then
a_{i}
is a double root of
U
.
Since this is not the case, and we know that
U(a_{i})=0
, we must have
U^{\prime}(a_{i})\neq 0
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
.

Then consider the set
\mathcal{V}_{U(X)}:=\left\{\frac{V(X)}{U(X)}\mid\deg V(X)<n\right\}
as a subset of
k(X)
, the field of rational functions.
Since
\deg V(X)<n
, we see that
\mathcal{V}_{U(X)}
is spanned by the set
\left\{\frac{1}{U(X)},\frac{X}{U(X)},\ldots,\frac{X^{n-1}}{U(X)}\right\}
.
It is clearly a
k
-linear subspace of
k(X)
.
Indeed, the elements
\frac{1}{U(X)},\frac{X}{U(X)},\ldots,\frac{X^{n-1}}{U(X)}
are
k
-linearly independent as well.
Hence
\dim_{k}\mathcal{V}_{U(X)}=n
.

Now consider the elements
\frac{1}{X-a_{1}},\ldots,\frac{1}{X-a_{n}}
.
One easily verifies that they belong to
\mathcal{V}_{U(X)}
.
We show that they are linearly independent, and hence form a basis.
Therefore, suppose that there exist
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\in k
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{X-a_{i}}=0.

Then also

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{i}(X-a_{1})}{X-a_{i}}=0.

By substituting
a_{1}
for
X
, we find
\lambda_{1}=0
.

Then
\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{X-a_{i}}=0
and we repeat this procedure.
We find that indeed
\frac{1}{X-a_{1}},\ldots,\frac{1}{X-a_{n}}
are linearly independent.

Since now these elements constitute a basis for
\mathcal{V}_{U(X)}
, given any
V(X)
with
\deg V(X)<n
, we can write

\displaystyle\frac{V(X)}{U(X)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{A_{i}}{X-a_{i}}

for some
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\in k
.
We now determine those
A_{i}
.
By multiplying both sides with
(X-a_{j})
we get

\displaystyle\frac{V(X)}{U(X)}\cdot(X-a_{j})=A_{j}+\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{A_{i}(X-a_{j})}{X-a_{i}}.

Note that the
X-a_{j}
cancels with the
X-a_{j}
that occurs in
U(X)
.
So we can substitute
a_{j}
for
X
and obtain

\displaystyle\frac{V(a_{j})}{U^{\prime}(a_{j})}=A_{j},

as required.
Note that we used that
U^{\prime}(a_{j})=\alpha\prod_{i\neq j}(a_{j}-a_{i})
. ∎

4
The Theorem of Duistermaat and Van der Kallen

In this section we state the Theorem of Duistermaat and Van der Kallen in one dimension.
We shall prove a more general theorem later on, adapting a technique by Monsky.
Before we do that, we need to discuss some theorems, all of which are self-contained and needed for our proof.
At the end, we shall state the Theorem of Duistermaat and Van der Kallen.
The first necessary theorem is the Newton-Puiseux Theorem.
(See:
New
,
Pui
,
Pui2
)

Theorem 4.1
(Newton-Puiseux Theorem
New
,
Pui
,
Pui2
)

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero and
f(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}(t)X^{i}\in k((t))[X]
with
n:=\deg_{X}f(X)\geq 1
.
Then there exists some
p\geq 1
such that
f(X)
splits completely in linear factors over
k((t^{1/p}))
.

Next, we need a special ring for our proof:

Definition 4

Let
k
be a field and
v
a valuation on
k
such that
k
is complete with respect to
v
.
Define
k[[X,X^{-1}]]
to be the ring of formal series
\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_{n}X^{n}
such that
\lim_{|n|\to\infty}c_{n}=0
.

Proposition 2

Let
k={\bf C}((z^{1/p}))
and
v
the valuation of
k
such that
v(z^{1/p})=1/p
.
Then
{\bf C}[X,X^{-1}][[z]]
is a subring of
k[[X,X^{-1}]]
.

Proof

Note that
{\bf C}((z^{1/p}))
is complete with respect to
v
, one could write
z^{1/p}=t
, then
k={\bf C}((t))
and
v(t)=1/p
.
Hence
k[[X,X^{-1}]]
is defined.

Let
f(z)=a_{0}(X)+a_{1}(X)z+a_{2}(X)z^{2}+\ldots
be an arbitrary element of
{\bf C}[X,X^{-1}][[z]]
and let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary.
Write this element
f(z)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_{n}X^{n}
.

We want to determine
N
such that
|c_{n}|<\varepsilon
for all
|n|\geq N
.
First, observe that
v(z^{i})=iv(z)=i/p
.
So
|z^{i}|=2^{-v(z^{i})}=2^{-i/p}<\varepsilon
if
i>p\log_{2}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
.

Write
N(\varepsilon):=\lceil p\log_{2}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})\rceil
.
Then determine

\displaystyle N:=\max_{0\leq i\leq N(\varepsilon)}\{\deg a_{i}(X),\deg a_{i}(X^{-1})\}.

Then, if
|n|>N
, all
z^{i}
contributing to
c_{n}
come from
a_{i}(X)
with
i>N(\varepsilon)
.
Since
|z^{i}|<\varepsilon
if
i>N(\varepsilon)
, it follows that
|c_{n}|<\varepsilon
.
Hence
|c_{n}|<\varepsilon
if
|n|>N
.

Hence indeed,
f(z)\in k[[X,X^{-1}]]
, as required. ∎

The theorem of Duistermaat and Van der Kallen is as follows:

Theorem 4.2
(Duistermaat - Van der Kallen
DuiKal
)

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be
n
commutative variables and let
M
be the subspace of the Laurent polynomial algebra
{\bf C}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},X_{1}^{-1},\ldots,X_{n}^{-1}]
consisting of those Laurent polynomials with no constant term:

\displaystyle M=\{f\in{\bf C}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},X_{1}^{-1},\ldots,X_{n}^{-1}]\mid f_{0}=0\}.

Then
M
is an MZ-space of
{\bf C}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},X_{1}^{-1},\ldots,X_{n}^{-1}]
.

We omit the proof, and prove a more general theorem, for dimension
n=1
, in the next section, see Theorem
5.1
.

5
Kernels of linear maps: A generalisation of Duistermaat - Van der Kallen in dimension one

In this section we will discuss a generalisation of Duistermaat - Van der Kallen.
Note that

\displaystyle\varphi\colon{\bf C}[X,X^{-1}]\to{\bf C},f\mapsto f_{0}

is a linear map of
{\bf C}
-vector spaces for which
\varphi(X^{N})=0
for all
|N|\geq 1
.
The theorem of Duistermaat and Van der Kallen can thus be phrased as:
\operatorname{Ker}\varphi
is an MZ-space.

We now investigate arbitrary
{\bf C}
-linear maps of a form,
L\colon{\bf C}[X,X^{-1}]\to{\bf C}
or
L\colon{\bf C}[X]\to{\bf C}
.

Remark 3

1.

If
L
is injective, i.e.,
\operatorname{Ker}L=0
, then
\operatorname{Ker}L
is an MZ-space.

2.

If
L=0
, i.e., the trivial linear map, then
\operatorname{Ker}L={\bf C}[X,X^{-1}]
(or indeed
\operatorname{Ker}L={\bf C}[X]
), an MZ-space.

From now on, when we write
L
is a linear map, we mean a non-trivial non-injective linear map.

Lemma 1

Let
L\colon{\bf C}[X]\to{\bf C}
be a
{\bf C}
-linear map for which there exists an
N\geq 1
such that
L(X^{n})=0
for all
n\in{\bf Z}_{\geq N}
.
Then
\operatorname{Ker}L
is an MZ-space of
{\bf C}[X]
if and only if
L(1)\neq 0
.

Proof.

\Rightarrow:)
Suppose that
L(1)=0
, then
1\in\operatorname{Ker}L
.
Since
\operatorname{Ker}L
is an MZ-space of
{\bf C}[X]
, we find that
\operatorname{Ker}L={\bf C}[X]
, i.e.,
L=0
, a contradiction.
So
L(1)\neq 0
.

\Leftarrow:)
Note that
L(X^{N})=0
.
So for any
f=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}X^{i}
we have

\displaystyle L(fX^{N})=L\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}X^{i+N}\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}L(X^{i+N})=0,

hence
(X^{N})\subset\operatorname{Ker}L
.
Then by Corollary
1
it suffices to show that
\operatorname{Ker}L/(X^{N})
is an MZ-space of
{\bf C}[X]/(X^{N})
.
We will also use Zhao’s Theorem.
We therefore need to show that all elements in
r(\operatorname{Ker}L/(X^{N}))
are algebraic over
{\bf C}
and that
{\bf C}[X]/(X^{N})e\subset\operatorname{Ker}L/(X^{N})
for all idempotents
e
in
\operatorname{Ker}L/(X^{N})
.

Note first that
\operatorname{Ker}L/(X^{N})\subset{\bf C}[X]/(X^{N})
, hence any idempotent of
\operatorname{Ker}L/(X^{N})
is an idempotent of
{\bf C}[X]/(X^{N})
.
We start by determining the idempotents of
{\bf C}[X]/(X^{N})
.
Since
(X)\subset{\bf C}[X]
is a maximal ideal, we find that
{\bf C}[X]/(X^{N})
is a local ring.
A local ring has only idempotents
0,1
.
Since
L(1)\neq 0
, we see that
0
is the only idempotent in
\operatorname{Ker}L/(X^{N})
, and we trivially have
0\subset\operatorname{Ker}L/(X^{N})
.

Hence it remains to show that every element in
r(\operatorname{Ker}L/(X^{N}))
is algebraic over
{\bf C}
.
Note that

\displaystyle r(\operatorname{Ker}L/(X^{N}))\subset{\bf C}[X]/(X^{N}),

and every element of
{\bf C}[X]/(X^{N})
is algebraic over
{\bf C}
due to its dimension being finite.

Since we now satisfy the hypotheses of Zhao’s Theorem, we find that
\operatorname{Ker}L/(X^{N})
is an MZ-space of
{\bf C}[X]/(X^{N})
and hence
\operatorname{Ker}L
is an MZ-space of
{\bf C}[X]
.
\triangle

Note that the above lemma can be generalized to an arbitrary field
k
, with an identical proof.
The following is the generalization of the
1
-dimensional version of the theorem of Duistermaat and Van der Kallen.

Theorem 5.1

Let
L\colon{\bf C}[X,X^{-1}]\to{\bf C}
be a
{\bf C}
-linear map for which there exists an
N\geq 1
such that
L(X^{n})=0
for all
n\in{\bf Z}_{\geq N}
and all
n\in{\bf Z}_{\leq-N}
.
Then
\operatorname{Ker}L
is an MZ-space of
{\bf C}[X,X^{-1}]
if and only if
L(1)\neq 0
.

The proof of this theorem relies on the following lemmas and is based on the proof of the
1
-dimensional Duistermaat-Van der Kallen theorem by Monsky
Monskyproof
,
monskylemma
(Lemmas 3.6 and 3.7).

Lemma 2

Let
L\colon{\bf C}[X,X^{-1}]\to{\bf C}
be a
{\bf C}
-linear map for which there exists an
N\geq 1
such that
L(X^{n})=0
for all
n\in{\bf Z}_{\geq N}
and all
n\in{\bf Z}_{\leq-N}
.
If
L(1)\neq 0
and
f\in r^{\prime}(\operatorname{Ker}L)
, then
f\in{\bf C}[X]
or
f\in{\bf C}[X^{-1}]
.

Proof.

Suppose
f\not\in{\bf C}[X]
and
f\not\in{\bf C}[X^{-1}]
, i.e.
f=\alpha X^{-s}+\ldots+\beta X^{r}
with
\alpha,\beta\neq 0
and
s,r\geq 1
.
Then consider the power series

\displaystyle W(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}L(f^{j})z^{j}.

We will show that
W(z)\neq 1
.
That is,
L(f^{m})\neq 0
for some
m\geq 1
.
This contradicts
f\in r^{\prime}(\operatorname{Ker}L)
.

Write
U(X)=X^{s}(1-zf(X))\in{\bf C}(z)[X]\subset{\bf C}((z))[X]
and
n:=r+s
.
Then by the Newton-Puiseux Theorem (see Theorem
4.1
) there exists some
p\geq 1
such that

\displaystyle U(X)=(-\beta z)(X-a_{1})\cdots(X-a_{n}),\ \text{with all }a_{i}\in{\bf C}((z^{\frac{1}{p}})).

Write
k:={\bf C}((z^{\frac{1}{p}}))
.
There we have the valuation
\nu
defined by
\nu(z^{\frac{1}{p}})=\frac{1}{p}
and
k
is complete with respect to this valuation. Furthermore
k[[X,X^{-1}]]
is a ring, by Theorem
4
.
Extend
L
to
k[[X,X^{-1}]]
as

\displaystyle L\colon k[[X,X^{-1}]]\to k

by

\displaystyle L\left(\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_{n}X^{n}\right)=\sum_{n=-(N-1)}^{N-1}c_{n}L(X^{n}).

Since
{\bf C}[X,X^{-1}][[z]]
is a subring of
k[[X,X^{-1}]]
(see Proposition
2
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{m\geq 0}f^{m}z^{m}\in k[[X,X^{-1}]].

Then
1-zf
is invertible in
k[[X,X^{-1}]]
and so is
U(X)
.
Write

\displaystyle W(z)=L\left(\frac{1}{1-zf}\right)=L\left(\frac{X^{s}}{U(X)}\right).

We will now use the partial fractions decomposition of
X^{s}/U(X)
.
Therefore, note that since we have
(-1)^{n}a_{1}\cdots a_{n}=\alpha/\beta
, we find that
a_{i}\neq 0
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
.
From
U(a_{i})=0
we find then that
f(a_{i})=1/z
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
.

So
f^{\prime}(a_{i})a_{i}^{\prime}=-z^{-2}
.
Also
U^{\prime}(X)=sX^{s-1}(1-zf(X))+X^{s}(-z)f^{\prime}(X)
and
U^{\prime}(a_{i})=-za_{i}^{s}f^{\prime}(a_{i})\neq 0
.
Hence all
a_{i}
are distinct.
Then by Theorem
3.2
we get a partial fractions decomposition of the form

\displaystyle\frac{X^{s}}{U(X)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{A_{i}}{X-a_{i}}

with
A_{i}=\frac{a_{i}^{s}}{U^{\prime}(a_{i})}=-\frac{1}{zf^{\prime}(a_{i})}
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-zf(X)}=\frac{X^{s}}{U(X)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}-\frac{1}{zf^{\prime}(a_{i})(X-a_{i})}.

(1)

We now compute the inverse of each factor
X-a_{i}
in
k[[X,X^{-1}]]
.
Observe that
f(a_{i})=1/z
implies that
\nu(a_{i})\neq 0
. Then
\nu(a_{i})>0
or
\nu(a_{i})<0
.

If
\nu(a_{i})>0
, then we have

\displaystyle(X-a_{i})^{-1}=X^{-1}(1-a_{i}X^{-1})^{-1}=X^{-1}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}a_{i}^{m}X^{-m}\in k[[X,X^{-1}]]

while if
\nu(a_{i})<0
, then

\displaystyle(X-a_{i})^{-1}=-a_{i}^{-1}(1-a_{i}^{-1}X)^{-1}=-a_{i}^{-1}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(a_{i}^{-1}X)^{m}\in k[[X,X^{-1}]].
