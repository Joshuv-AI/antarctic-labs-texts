Rational lines on cubic hypersurfaces II
Julia Brandes, Rainer Dietmann

Abstract
. We show that any rational cubic hypersurface of dimension at least 33 33 vanishes on a projective rational line, reducing the previous lower bound of Wooley by two. The main ingredient is a result on linear spaces on quadratic forms over suitable imaginary quadratic field extensions of ℚ \mathbb{Q} .



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary: 11D72. Secondary: 11E76, 14G05, 14J70.

1.
Introduction

A celebrated theorem of Birch
[
2
]
asserts that any rationally defined projective hypersurface of odd degree contains large rational linear spaces, provided only that the dimension of the hypersurface be sufficiently large in terms of its degree and the dimension of the linear space. Unfortunately, his methods, although constructive, are famously inefficient and believed to be quite far from the truth. This is true even in some of the simplest cases, such as that of finding rational linear spaces, or even just lines, on cubic hypersurfaces. In particular, Wooley
[
12
, Theorem 2(b)]
proved that every cubic hypersurface defined by a rational homogeneous polynomial in at least
37
variables contains a rational line. Under the additional assumption that the cubic hypersurface is smooth, the present authors
[
3
, Theorem 1.1]
reduced that number to
31
. The aim of this note is to improve on Wooley’s result for the singular case.

For an imaginary quadratic field extension
K\mid\mathbb{Q}
denote by
\gamma_{K}
the least integer
n
with the property that any
K
-rational cubic form in at least
n
variables has a non-trivial
K
-rational zero. Define then

\displaystyle\gamma=\sup_{K}\gamma_{K},

where the supremum is over all imaginary quadratic extensions
K\mid\mathbb{Q}
. Note that this is well-defined since
\gamma_{K}\leq 16
for any number field
K
was confirmed by Pleasants
[
10
]
. In this notation, our main result is as follows.

Theorem 1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
C\in\mathbb{Q}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
be a cubic form. If
n\geq 2\gamma+3
, then
C
vanishes on a rational projective line.

Upon inserting the value
\gamma\leq 16
, we obtain the following simpler conclusion.

Corollary
.

Let
C(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\in\mathbb{Q}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
be a cubic form with
n\geq 35
. Then
C
vanishes on a rational projective line.

In addition, Bernert and Hochfilzer have indicated to us that they are able to show
\gamma\leq 14
, see their preprint
[
1
]
. Combining their result with our Theorem
1
, the number
35
of the Corollary can be reduced to
31
, thus matching the state of the art in the non-singular case. Observe that necessarily
\gamma\geq 10
, so
n\geq 23
is a hard limit of the method. We remark that it is quite unclear what the ‘true’ bound should be, but it was shown in
[
3
, Theorem 1.4]
that at the very least
12
variables are needed in order to avoid potential
p
-adic obstructions.

In our proof we follow largely the same strategy as in
[
12
]
. In other words, we begin by finding a point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
satisfying
C(\mathbf{x})=0
, and then seek another point
\mathbf{y}
having the property that the pair
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
spans a projective line on the hypersurface
C=0
. In order to find the point
\mathbf{y}
, we are led to find non-trivial solutions to a system of one linear, one quadratic and one cubic equation. The key to the strategy is to find a large linear space on which the quadratic equation vanishes, and then solve the cubic equation after restricting to this linear space. Since the quadratic equation may be definite, it has been customary to solve this problem over a suitable imaginary quadratic field extension
K\mid\mathbb{Q}
to ensure solubility. The novelty of our approach lies in how this choice is made. Unlike Wooley
[
12
]
, who used
K=\mathbb{Q}(i)
, we optimise our choice of
K
, which enables us to find high-dimensional
K
-linear spaces on the quadratic form more efficiently. Our main result here is the following.

Theorem 2
.

Let
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
Q(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\in\mathbb{Q}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
be a quadratic form. If
n\geq 2k+1
, then there exists an imaginary quadratic field extension
K
of
\mathbb{Q}
such that
Q
vanishes on an affine
K
-linear space of dimension at least
k
.

For comparison, it follows from Leep
[
7
, Corollary 2.4]
that
Q
vanishes on an affine
k
-dimensional linear space over
all
imaginary quadratic number fields when
n\geq 2k+3
.

Note that the condition
n\geq 2k+1
in Theorem
2
is sharp in general:
Suppose that
n=2k
with
k\geq 2
, and consider the quadratic form

Q(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{2}+3X_{3}^{2}-6X_{4}^{2}+X_{5}X_{6}+\ldots+X_{n-1}X_{n}.

Note that
Q
has already split off
n/2-2
hyperbolic planes, so in order for
Q
to vanish on an affine
K
-linear space of dimension
k=n/2
, the quadratic form

R(X_{1},\ldots,X_{4})=X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{2}+3X_{3}^{2}-6X_{4}^{2}

would have to vanish on a two-dimensional
K
-linear space for some quadratic field extension
K\mid\mathbb{Q}
. This is possible only if
\det R=-18=-2\times 3^{2}
is a square in
K
, which forces
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-2})
. Observe that since
\left(\frac{-2}{3}\right)=1
, it follows from Lemma
2.1
below that
\mathbb{Q}_{3}
is one of the completions of
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-2})
. However, it is not hard to show that
R
is anisotropic over
\mathbb{Q}_{3}
. We conclude that it is impossible to find a quadratic extension
K\mid\mathbb{Q}
such that
Q
vanishes on a
K
-linear space of dimension
k
.

2.
Notation and preliminaries

For a rational prime
p
, we write
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
for the field of
p
-adic numbers and ring of
p
-adic integers, respectively. The group of units in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is denoted as
\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{*}=\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. We refer to a vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
as primitive if it is non-vanishing modulo
p
. Abusing the notation, we will also write
\sqrt{a}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
for either of the two solutions to
x^{2}=a
in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
, and
\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\sqrt{a})
for the corresponding field extension of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
whenever
a
is not a square in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
. Referring to a number field
K
more in general, we denote by
|\cdot|_{\nu}
the valuation at the place
\nu
, and by
K_{\nu}
the corresponding completion.

We will be using in a crucial way the following two facts.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
K\mid\mathbb{Q}
be a quadratic field extension of the shape
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d})
. Then all completions of
K
are of the form
\mathbb{Q}_{\nu}(\sqrt{-d})
or
\mathbb{Q}_{\nu}
for
\nu
a rational prime or infinity, depending on whether or not
-d
is a square in
\mathbb{Q}_{\nu}
.

Proof.

This is immediate from
[
8
, Theorem II.8.2]
, upon observing that the polynomial
X^{2}+d
factors in
\mathbb{Q}_{\nu}
if and only if
-d
is a square in
\mathbb{Q}_{\nu}
.
∎

Our second main ingredient is a local-to-global principle for linear spaces on quadratic forms. This is an extension of the Hasse–Minkowski Theorem, see for example
[
9
, Theorem 66:1]
, but as we were unable to find a reference, we include a proof.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
k
and
n
be positive rational integers, let
K
be a number field, and let
Q(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\in K[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
be a non-singular quadratic form. Then
Q
vanishes on a
K
-linear space of dimension
k
if and only if for all completions
K_{\nu}
of
K
the form
Q
vanishes on a
K_{\nu}
-linear space of dimension
k
.

Proof.

It is clearly sufficient to show that local solubility implies global solubility, as the opposite direction is trivial. We follow the argument of
[
4
, p. 56]
, where the special case
K=\mathbb{Q}
and
k=2
was considered.
We prove Lemma
2.2
by induction on
k
. The statement for
k=1
immediately follows from the Hasse–Minkowski Theorem, see for example
[
9
, Theorem 66:1]
. Now suppose that the statement has already been established for
k-1\geq 1
. Let
K
be a number field and let
Q(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\in K[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
be a non-singular quadratic form such that for all completions
K_{\nu}
of
K
the form
Q
vanishes on a
K_{\nu}
-linear space of dimension
k
. In particular,
Q
vanishes on a
K_{\nu}
-linear space of dimension
k-1
for all completions
K_{\nu}
of
K
, so by our inductive assumption
Q
vanishes on a
K
-linear space of dimension
k-1
. Consequently,
Q
is equivalent (via a suitable non-singular
K
-linear transformation of the variables) to a quadratic form of the shape

(2.1)

F(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=X_{1}X_{2}+\ldots+X_{2k-3}X_{2k-2}+R(X_{2k-1},\ldots,X_{n}),

where
R(X_{2k-1},\ldots,X_{n})\in K[X_{2k-1},\ldots,X_{n}]
is a suitable non-singular quadratic form. Now let
K_{\nu}
be any completion of
K
. Since
F
is equivalent to
Q
over
K_{\nu}
, our assumption on the vanishing of
Q
implies that
F
vanishes on a
K_{\nu}
-linear space of dimension at least
k
. From (
2.1
) we then conclude that
R(X_{2k-1},\ldots,X_{n})
must be isotropic over
K_{\nu}
(see for example the discussion on page 99 of
[
9
]
, §42F). Hence
R(X_{2k-1},\ldots,X_{n})
is isotropic over all completions
K_{\nu}
of
K
. So by the Hasse–Minkowski Theorem
R(X_{2k-1},\ldots,X_{n})
is isotropic over
K
, and from (
2.1
) we conclude that
F
vanishes on a
K
-linear space of dimension
k
.
∎

We also record a little statement on squares in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
. Here, it is convenient to define

(2.2)

\displaystyle m_{p}=p\quad\text{for $p\neq 2$},\qquad m_{2}=8.

In this notation, we have the following.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
p
be a rational prime and let
a\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}_{p}
and
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
with
a\equiv b\pmod{m_{p}}
, where
m_{p}
is as in (
2.2
). Then
\frac{a}{b}
is a square in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
.

Proof.

The number
\frac{a}{b}
is a square in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
if and only if
ab
is. As
a\equiv b\pmod{m_{p}}
, the number
ab\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is a non-zero square modulo
p
if
p\neq 2
, and is congruent to
1
modulo
8
if
p=2
. In either case, an application of Hensel’s Lemma (see Theorems 3 and 4 in
[
11
, Chapter II]
) shows that
ab
, and thus also
\frac{a}{b}
, is a square in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
.
∎

Our last auxiliary result describes how hyperbolic planes can be split off quadratic forms. This procedure is standard, but we state it in the form we require for easier reference and include a proof for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
p
be a rational prime, let
u,v\in\mathbb{N}
with
v\geq 3
, and put
n=2u+v
. Let
Q(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
be a non-singular quadratic form. Then there exists a non-singular linear transformation
T:\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{n}\to\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{n}
as well as a non-singular quadratic form
R(X_{1},\ldots,X_{v})\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{v}]
having the property that

Q(T(\mathbf{X}))=X_{1}X_{2}+\ldots+X_{2u-1}X_{2u}+R(X_{2u+1},\ldots,X_{n}).

Moreover, if
p\nmid 2\det Q
then
p\nmid\det R
.

Proof.

We use induction on
u
.
Since for any
u\geq 1
we have
n\geq 5
, the form
Q
is isotropic over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
. Let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{n}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
be a primitive zero of
Q
. Then (see for example Lemma 2.1 in
[
5
]
, Chapter 9) there exists an
n\times n
-matrix
T_{1}
with entries in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
and with
p\nmid\det T_{1}
, and such that the first column of
T_{1}
is given by
\mathbf{x}
. Hence the form
Q^{\prime}(\mathbf{X})=Q(T_{1}(\mathbf{X}))
is of the shape

Q^{\prime}(\mathbf{X})=X_{1}L(X_{2},\ldots,X_{n})+Q_{0}(X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}),

where
L(X_{2},\ldots,X_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}[X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}]
and
Q_{0}(X_{2},\ldots,X_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}[X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}]
are linear and quadratic forms, respectively. Note that since
Q^{\prime}
is non-singular, the form
L
is non-trivial. We can therefore assume without loss of generality that
L
is non-constant in
X_{2}
, which ensures that the linear change of variables
\mathbf{Y}=\tau(\mathbf{X})
given by

Y_{1}=X_{1},\quad Y_{2}=L(X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}),\quad Y_{j}=X_{j}\quad(3\leq j\leq n)

is non-singular.
In this way we obtain a form
Q^{\prime\prime}(\mathbf{Y})=Q^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(\mathbf{Y}))
of the shape

Q^{\prime\prime}(\mathbf{Y})=Y_{2}(Y_{1}+M(Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{n}))+Q_{1}(Y_{3},\ldots,Y_{n})

for a suitable linear form
M(Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}[Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{n}]
and a suitable quadratic form
Q_{1}(Y_{3},\ldots,Y_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}[Y_{3},\ldots,Y_{n}]
. We apply another linear change of variables
\mathbf{Z}=\varphi(\mathbf{Y})
given by

Z_{1}=Y_{1}+M(Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{n}),\quad Z_{j}=Y_{j}\quad(2\leq j\leq n).

Clearly this transformation has
\det\varphi=1
. The form
Q^{\prime\prime\prime}(\mathbf{Z})=Q^{\prime\prime}(\varphi^{-1}(\mathbf{Z}))
thus obtained is given by

(2.3)

Q^{\prime\prime\prime}(\mathbf{Z})=Z_{1}Z_{2}+Q_{1}(Z_{3},\ldots,Z_{n}),

where
Q_{1}
is the quadratic form defined above. Since all linear transformations have been non-singular, it follows from the non-singularity of
Q^{\prime\prime\prime}
that
Q_{1}
must necessarily also be non-singular.
As long as
n-2\geq 5
, we may therefore iterate the process with
Q_{1}
in lieu of
Q
. This confirms the first statement of the lemma for any
u
in the range
1\leq u\leq(n-3)/2
.

We conclude the proof by remarking that in the above argument the transformations
T_{1}
and
\varphi
are non-singular modulo
p
. Moreover, if
p\nmid\det Q
then the property
p\nmid\det T_{1}
implies that not all coefficients of
L
are divisible by
p
, so we can assume without loss of generality that the coefficient of
X_{2}
in
L
is not divisible by
p
, which shows that in this case also the transformation
\tau
is non-singular modulo
p
. Consequently, if
p\nmid\det Q
it follows that
p\nmid\det Q^{\prime\prime\prime}
. Moreover, upon noting that

\det Q^{\prime\prime\prime}=\det\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1/2\\
1/2&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\det Q_{1}=-\tfrac{1}{4}\det Q_{1},

where
1/4\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
for all odd
p
, we see that that any odd prime not dividing
\det Q^{\prime\prime\prime}
is also not a divisor of
\det Q_{1}
, and by iterating as before one confirms the last conclusion of the lemma.
∎

3.
Quadratic forms over quadratic field extensions

When
p\nmid 2\det Q
we can now fairly easily show that
Q
vanishes on large
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-linear spaces.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\geq 2k+1
, and let
Q(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
be a quadratic form. Let
p
be a rational prime with
p\nmid 2\det Q
. Then
Q
vanishes on a
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-linear space of affine dimension
k
.

Proof.

Since
p\nmid\det Q
we can assume in particular that
\det Q\neq 0
, so
Q
is non-singular.
It follows from Lemma
2.4
with
u=k-1
that we may assume without loss of generality that
Q
is of the shape

Q(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=X_{1}X_{2}+\ldots+X_{2k-3}X_{2k-2}+R(X_{2k-1},\ldots,X_{n}).

Moreover, since
p\neq 2
, the form
R
can be diagonalised over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, so that on setting
v=n-2k+2
we may further assume that

R(X_{1},\ldots,X_{v})=a_{1}X_{1}^{2}+\ldots+a_{v}X_{v}^{2}

for some
a_{1},\ldots,a_{v}
with
p\nmid a_{1}\cdots a_{v}
.

By the hypothesis of the lemma we have
v\geq 3
, so it follows from Chevalley’s Theorem that the congruence
R(X_{1},\ldots,X_{v})\equiv 0\pmod{p}
has a non-trivial solution
\mathbf{x}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{n}
. As
p\nmid 2a_{1}\cdots a_{v}
, this solution can be lifted via Hensel’s lemma to a solution
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{v}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
of
R(\mathbf{x})=0
.

Let
\mathbf{z}_{k}=(0,\ldots,0,\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{n}
, and for
1\leq u\leq k-1
take
\mathbf{z}_{u}=\mathbf{e}_{2u}
, where
\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{n}
denote the usual unit basis vectors in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{n}
. It is then clear that
Q
vanishes on the
k
-dimensional linear space spanned by the points
\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{k-1},\mathbf{z}_{k}
, as postulated.
∎

The next lemma addresses the potentially problematic primes dividing
2\det Q
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
k
and
n
be positive integers with
n\geq 2k+1
, and let
Q(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
be a non-singular quadratic form. Let further
p
be a rational prime with
p\mid 2\det Q
. Then there exists a congruence class
\ell_{p,Q}\in(\mathbb{Z}/m_{p}\mathbb{Z})^{*}
having the following property:

(i)

If
\left(\frac{\ell_{p,Q}}{m_{p}}\right)=1
, then
Q
vanishes on a
k
-dimensional linear space over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
.

(ii)

If
\left(\frac{\ell_{p,Q}}{m_{p}}\right)=-1
, then
Q
vanishes on a
k
-dimensional linear space over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\sqrt{-d})
for all positive integers
d
satisfying
-d\equiv\ell_{p,Q}\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,m_{p})
.

Proof.

As in the previous case, an application of Lemma
2.4
with
u=k-1
and
v=n-2u
shows that we may assume without loss of generality that
Q
is of the form

Q(\mathbf{X})=X_{1}X_{2}+\ldots+X_{2k-3}X_{2k-2}+R(X_{2k-1},\ldots,X_{n}),

where
R(X_{1},\ldots,X_{v})\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{v}]
is a non-singular quadratic form in
v\geq 3
variables.

We consider first the form
R(X_{1},\ldots,X_{v})
. After diagonalising over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
and absorbing even powers of
p
from the coefficients into the variables, we may assume without loss of generality that
R
is of the shape

p^{\beta}R(\mathbf{X})=a_{1}p^{\alpha_{1}}X_{1}^{2}+\ldots+a_{v}p^{\alpha_{v}}X_{v}^{2}

for suitable
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}
,
a_{1},\ldots,a_{v}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
with
p\nmid a_{1}\cdots a_{v}
, and
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{v}\in\{0,1\}
. Since
v\geq 3
, we can assume without loss of generality that
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}
. Then it is sufficient to show that there exists a congruence class
\ell_{p,Q}\in(\mathbb{Z}/m_{p}\mathbb{Z})^{*}
with the property that
a_{1}X_{1}^{2}+a_{2}X_{2}^{2}
is isotropic over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\sqrt{-d})
for all
d\equiv-\ell_{p,Q}\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,m_{p})
. Note that this suffices for both conclusions since
\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\sqrt{-d})=\mathbb{Q}_{p}
whenever
-d
is a square in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
.

We take
\ell_{p,Q}\equiv-a_{1}a_{2}\pmod{m_{p}}
. Now, pick any
d\in\mathbb{N}
with
-d\equiv\ell_{p,Q}\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,m_{p})
, so that
d\equiv a_{1}a_{2}\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,m_{p})
. Working over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\sqrt{-d})
, we can substitute

X_{1}=Y_{1},\quad X_{2}=\frac{Y_{2}}{\sqrt{-d}}

and find

a_{1}X_{1}^{2}+a_{2}X_{2}^{2}=a_{1}Y_{1}^{2}-\frac{a_{2}}{d}Y_{2}^{2},

where the binary quadratic form
B(Y_{1},Y_{2})=a_{1}Y_{1}^{2}-\frac{a_{2}}{d}Y_{2}^{2}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}[Y_{1},Y_{2}]
has discriminant
\Delta=-\frac{a_{1}a_{2}}{d}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}
. Since
d\equiv a_{1}a_{2}\pmod{m_{p}}
, we see that
-\Delta
is a square in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
by Lemma
2.3
. Hence
B
is isotropic over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
and thus
a_{1}X_{1}^{2}+a_{2}X_{2}^{2}
is isotropic over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\sqrt{-d})
.

We now argue as in the previous lemma. Since
B
and consequently
R
are isotropic over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\sqrt{-d})
, we can find a non-trivial point
\mathbf{x}\in(\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\sqrt{-d}))^{v}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
with
R(\mathbf{x})=0
. Write now
\mathbf{z}_{k}=(0,\ldots,0,\mathbf{x})\in(\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\sqrt{-d}))^{n}
, and for
1\leq u\leq k-1
take
\mathbf{z}_{u}=\mathbf{e}_{2u}
. As before, we see that
Q
vanishes on the
k
-dimensional linear space spanned by the points
\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{k-1},\mathbf{z}_{k}
. Since
d
had been arbitrary with
-d\equiv\ell_{p,Q}\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,m_{k})
, such a linear space exists for all fields
K
of the shape
\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\sqrt{-d})
, as claimed.
∎

We are now ready to conclude the proof of Theorem
2
. Without loss of generality we can assume that
Q\in\mathbb{Z}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
. Recall also that
n\geq 2k+1
. By Lemma
2.2
it is sufficient to find an imaginary quadratic field extension
K\mid\mathbb{Q}
such that
Q
vanishes on a
K_{\nu}
-linear space of dimension
k
for all completions
K_{\nu}
of
K
.

Suppose first that
Q
is non-singular. We have three cases to consider.

Singular primes.
Consider first all primes
p
with
p\mid 2\det Q
. For each of these, choose a congruence class
\ell_{p,Q}\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,m_{p})
as in Lemma
3.2
, where
m_{p}
is given as in (
2.2
). By the Chinese Remainder Theorem we can then choose
d\in\mathbb{N}
such that
-d\equiv\ell_{p,Q}\pmod{m_{p}}
for all primes
p\mid 2\det Q
, and we take
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d})
. With this construction, it follows from Lemma
3.2
that
Q
vanishes on a
k
-dimensional linear space over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\sqrt{-d})
for all
p\mid 2\det Q
for which
\left(\frac{-d}{m_{p}}\right)=-1
, and over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
when
\left(\frac{-d}{m_{p}}\right)=1
. Thus, we conclude via Lemma
2.1
that
Q
vanishes on a
k
-dimensional linear space over all completions
K_{\nu}
with
|2\det Q|_{\nu}<1
.

Non-singular primes.
We next consider the primes
p
satisfying
p\nmid 2\det Q
. Here, it follows from Lemma
3.1
that
Q
vanishes on a
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-linear space of dimension
k
. When
\left(\frac{-d}{m_{p}}\right)=-1
, this implies
a forteriori
that
Q
also vanishes on a
k
-dimensional space over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\sqrt{-d})
, and again we conclude from Lemma
2.1
that
Q
vanishes on a
k
-dimensional linear space over all completions
K_{\nu}
with
|2\det Q|_{\nu}=1
.

The place at infinity.
As
K
is an imaginary quadratic field extension of
\mathbb{Q}
, the Archimedean completion of
K
is
\mathbb{C}
. One shows easily that any non-singular quadratic form
Q
in
n\geq 2k
variables vanishes on a
\mathbb{C}
-linear space of dimension
k
. This settles the remaining case of
|2\det Q|_{\nu}>1
.

We have thus confirmed that
Q
vanishes on a
k
-dimensional linear space over all completions
K_{\nu}
of
K
. By Lemma
2.2
we can now conclude that
Q
vanishes on a
K
-linear space of dimension
k
, which proves the theorem in the non-singular case.

Finally, we discuss the special case
\det Q=0
. In this case,
Q
is singular and hence there exists a non-singular linear map
T:\mathbb{Q}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}^{n}
in such a way that
Q(T(\mathbf{X}))=Q_{T}(\mathbf{X})
where
Q_{T}\in\mathbb{Q}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{j},\ldots,X_{n}]
is a quadratic form not depending on
X_{j+1},\ldots,X_{n}
, and such that
j<n
is minimal with this property. The form
Q_{T}
therefore vanishes trivially on the
(n-j)
-dimensional subspace corresponding to
X_{1}=\ldots=X_{j}=0
. If
n-j\geq k
, this is already sufficient. Otherwise, write
Q_{T}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Q}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{j}]
for the restriction of
Q_{T}
in the obvious way, and observe that the condition
n\geq 2k+1
together with the obvious bound
n\geq j
implies
2n\geq 2k+j+1
, which can be rewritten as
j\geq 2(k-(n-j))+1
. Since
Q^{\prime}_{T}
is non-singular due to the minimality of
j
, we conclude from our above arguments that there exists an imaginary quadratic field extension
K\mid\mathbb{Q}
such that
Q^{\prime}_{T}
vanishes on a
K
-linear space of dimension
k-(n-j)
. We can combine this linear space with the one already found and conclude that
Q
vanishes on a
K
-linear space of dimension
k
. This completes the proof of Theorem
2
.

4.
Proof of the main result

To prove Theorem
1
, recall that
C\in\mathbb{Q}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
is a rational cubic form with
n\geq 2\gamma+3
. As usual, we introduce a rational symmetric trilinear form
\Phi
such that
C(\mathbf{X})=\Phi(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X})
. As
\gamma\geq 10
we have
n\geq 2\gamma+3\geq 14
, so by
[
6
, Theorem 1]
there exists a base point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
with
C(\mathbf{x})=0
. Our task is now to find a point
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{Q}
, distinct from
\mathbf{x}
, and so that the pair
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
spans a line that is entirely contained in the hypersurface
C=0
.

Put

L(\mathbf{Y})=\Phi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{Y})\quad\text{ and }\quad Q(\mathbf{Y})=\Phi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Y}),

noting that

C(\mathbf{x}+\lambda\mathbf{Y})=3\lambda L(\mathbf{Y})+3\lambda^{2}Q(\mathbf{Y})+\lambda^{3}C(\mathbf{Y}).

To avoid the obvious solution
\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{x}
, choose any linear form
\Lambda\in\mathbb{Q}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
with
\Lambda(\mathbf{x})\neq 0
. We thus want to solve the system of two linear, one quadratic and one cubic rational Diophantine equations

\Lambda(\mathbf{y})=L(\mathbf{y})=Q(\mathbf{y})=C(\mathbf{y})=0.

Denote by
H\simeq\mathbb{Q}^{n-2}
the affine space defined by
L=\Lambda=0
, and denote by
C_{1}
and
Q_{1}
the restrictions of the forms
C
and
Q
to
H
.
If
n-2\geq 2\gamma+1
, Theorem
2
shows that there exists an imaginary quadratic field extension
K
of
\mathbb{Q}
such that
Q_{1}
vanishes on a
K
-linear space
V
of dimension at least
\gamma
. Writing
C_{2}
for the restriction of
C_{1}
to
V
, it now follows from the definition of
\gamma
that
C_{2}
has a non-trivial zero
\mathbf{y}_{1}\in V
. This point can in a natural manner be lifted to a non-zero point
\mathbf{y}\in K^{n}
. We thus have found a pair of non-zero points
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
and
\mathbf{y}\in K^{n}
, such that
C
vanishes on the
K
-rational linear space spanned by
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
. In this situation, Lemma 2.2 in
[
12
]
enables us to pull back the
K
-linear space spanned by
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
to a rational two-dimensional space. We thus obtain a rational projective line on
C=0
as required.
