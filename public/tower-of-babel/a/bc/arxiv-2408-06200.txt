Dirichlet improvability in L p L_{p} -norms
Nikolay Moshchevitin, Nikita Shulga

Abstract
. For a norm F F on ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} , we consider the set of F F -Dirichlet improvable numbers 𝐃𝐈 F \mathbf{DI}_{F} . In the most important case of F F being an L p L_{p} -norm with p = ∞ p=\infty , which is a supremum norm, it is well-known that 𝐃𝐈 F = 𝐁𝐀 ∪ ℚ \mathbf{DI}_{F}=\mathbf{BA}\cup\mathbb{Q} , where 𝐁𝐀 \mathbf{BA} is the set of badly approximable numbers. It is also known that 𝐁𝐀 \mathbf{BA} and each 𝐃𝐈 F \mathbf{DI}_{F} are of measure zero and of full Hausdorff dimension. Using the classification of critical lattices for unit balls in L p L_{p} , we provide a complete and effective characterization of 𝐃𝐈 p := 𝐃𝐈 F [ p ] \mathbf{DI}_{p}:=\mathbf{DI}_{F^{[p]}} in terms of the occurrence of patterns in regular continued fraction expansions, where F [ p ] F^{[p]} is an L p L_{p} -norm with p ∈ [ 1 , ∞ ) p\in[1,\infty) . This yields several corollaries. In particular, we resolve two open questions by Kleinbock and Rao by showing that the set 𝐃𝐈 p ∖ 𝐁𝐀 \mathbf{DI}_{p}\setminus\mathbf{BA} is of full Hausdorff dimension, and prove several results about the size of the difference 𝐃𝐈 p 1 ∖ 𝐃𝐈 p 2 \mathbf{DI}_{p_{1}}\setminus\mathbf{DI}_{p_{2}} . To be precise, we show that both 𝐃𝐈 2 ∖ 𝐃𝐈 1 \mathbf{DI}_{2}\setminus\mathbf{DI}_{1} and 𝐃𝐈 1 ∖ 𝐃𝐈 2 \mathbf{DI}_{1}\setminus\mathbf{DI}_{2} have full Hausdorff dimension. We also find all values of p p , for which the set 𝐃𝐈 p c ∩ 𝐁𝐀 \mathbf{DI}_{p}^{c}\cap\mathbf{BA} has full Hausdorff dimension. Finally, our characterization result implies that the number e e satisfies e ∈ 𝐃𝐈 p e\in\mathbf{DI}_{p} if and only if p ∈ ( 1 , 2 ) ∪ ( p 0 , ∞ ) p\in(1,2)\cup(p_{0},\infty) for some special constant p 0 ≈ 2.57 p_{0}\approx 2.57 .



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Classical background

The simplest result in the theory of Diophantine approximation is Dirichlet’s Approximation Theorem, which can be formulated as follows.

Theorem A.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. For any real
t\geqslant 1
there exists positive integer
q
such that

\begin{cases}||q\alpha||<\frac{1}{t},\cr 1\leqslant q\leqslant t.\end{cases}

Here
||\cdot||
is the distance to the nearest integer. It is easy to see that one cannot uniformly improve the constant
1
on the right-hand side of the first inequality.
However, for some particular numbers, Theorem A can be improved. A real number
\alpha
is called
Dirichlet improvable
if there exists a constant
c<1
, such that the system

\begin{cases}||q\alpha||<\frac{c}{t},\cr 1\leqslant q\leqslant t.\end{cases}

can be solved in
q\in\mathbb{N}
for any large real number
t
.
We denote the set of all real Dirichlet improvable numbers by
\mathbf{DI}_{\infty}
.

Recall that

\alpha
is called badly approximable if there exists
c>0
for which the inequality

\left|\alpha-\frac{p}{q}\right|>\frac{c}{q^{2}}

holds for all rational
\frac{p}{q}
. We denote the set of all badly approximable real numbers by
\mathbf{BA}
.
Classical results state that both
\mathbf{DI}_{\infty}
and
\mathbf{BA}
have zero Lebesgue measure and are of full Hausdorff dimension.

Davenport and Schmidt in
[
11
]
showed that these two sets are connected. One of their results is the following statement.

Theorem B.

An irrational number
\alpha
satisfies
\alpha\in\mathbf{DI}_{\infty}
if and only if it is badly approximable.

It is well-known that bad approximability is equivalent to the property of having bounded partial quotients in a regular continued fraction expansion, therefore, the last theorem can be restated as follows.

Theorem B
′
.
An irrational number
\alpha=[a_{0};a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},\ldots]
is Dirichlet non-improvable if and only if there is a subsequence
\{n_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, such that
a_{n_{k}}\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
.

1.2.
Dirichlet improvability with respect to a norm

Here we describe a setting formulated by Kleinbock and Rao
[
30
]

which goes back to the paper
[
4
]
by Andersen and Duke.
Our exposition is a bit different from the one in
[
4
,
30
]
, however, our objects are equivalent to the ones from these papers.

We consider a norm
F(x,y)
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}(x,y)
such that

F(x,y)=F(|x|,|y|)\,\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\,F(1,0)=F(0,1)=1.

In
[
4
]
such a norm is called
strongly symmetric
.
Denote the unit disk with respect to the norm
F
as

\mathcal{B}_{F}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:F(x,y)\leqslant 1\}.

Dani’s correspondence
[
10
]
allows to reformulate many diophantine statements in terms of trajectories in the space of two-dimensional lattices, and we use this principle below. For a fixed
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, we consider the family of lattices

(1)

\Lambda_{\alpha}(t)=G_{t}A_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z}^{2},\,\,\,\text{where}\,\,\,G_{t}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}t^{-1}&0\cr 0&t\end{array}\right),\,\,\,A_{\alpha}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\cr-\alpha&1\end{array}\right)

and successive minima

(2)

\lambda_{i}(t)=\lambda_{i}(\Lambda_{\alpha}(t),\mathcal{B}_{F}),\,\,\,i=1,2

of the lattice
\Lambda_{\alpha}(t)
with respect to the convex body
\mathcal{B}_{F}
as functions in
t
.
For every
t
, the fundamental volume of the lattice
\Lambda_{\alpha}(t)
is equal to one.

For the unit disk
\mathcal{B}_{F}
we consider its
critical determinant

\Delta_{F}=\inf\{{\rm det}\,\Lambda:\,\text{there are no non-zero points of $\Lambda$ inside }\,\mathcal{B}_{F}\}.

By compactness, this infimum is attained on some lattice which is called a
critical lattice
.
The set of all critical lattices
for
\mathcal{B}_{F}
forms a
critical locus
which is denoted by
\mathfrak{L}_{F}
.

Consider the value defined by

(3)

d_{F}(\alpha)=\limsup_{t\to\infty}\lambda_{1}(t)=\limsup_{t\to\infty}\lambda_{1}(\Lambda_{\alpha}(t),\mathcal{B}_{F}).

We call
d_{F}(\alpha)
a
Dirichlet constant of
\alpha
for the norm

F
.
In
[
4
]
, the authors dealt with the analogous values
\delta_{F}(\alpha)
.
In our notation, we have

\delta_{F}(\alpha)=\Delta_{F}\cdot(d_{F}(\alpha))^{2}
,
where
\Delta_{F}
is the
critical determinant
of
\mathcal{B}_{F}
.
It is clear that
\delta_{F}(\alpha)\leqslant 1
(or
d_{F}(\alpha)\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta_{F}}}
) for all
\alpha
.

A real number
\alpha
is called
F
-Dirichlet improvable
if
d_{F}(\alpha)<\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta_{F}}}
or, equivalently,
\delta_{F}(\alpha)<1
. Conversely, in the case

d_{F}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta_{F}}}
(or
\delta_{F}(\alpha)=1
) we say that
\alpha
is
F
-Dirichlet non-improvable
.

For a fixed norm
F
, we denote the set of
F
-Dirichlet improvable numbers by
\mathbf{DI}_{F}
. For the
L_{\infty}
-norm, this definition corresponds to regular Dirichlet improvability discussed in Subsection
1.1
above.

In the present paper, we thoroughly study the Dirichlet improvability in the most important case of
L_{p}
-norms

(4)

F^{[p]}(x,y)=(|x|^{p}+|y|^{p})^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Sup-norm, Euclidean norm, and taxicab norm

F^{[\infty]}(x,y)=\max(|x|,|y|),\,\,\,F^{[2]}(x,y)=(|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}},\,\,\,F^{[1]}(x,y)=|x|+|y|

are particular examples.

For a fixed
p
and norm
F^{[p]}
, we simply say that
\alpha
is
p
-Dirichlet improvable (or is
p
-Dirichlet non-improvable) and denote the set of
p
-Dirichlet improvable numbers by
\mathbf{DI}_{p}
. The set of
p
-Dirichlet non-improvable numbers is denoted by
\mathbf{DI}_{p}^{c}
.

In this paper, we tackle three problems. The main one is to deal with the following question.

Problem I.

Can one classify Dirichlet (non-)improvable numbers for other norms similarly to Theorem B
′
?

It turns out that the answer to the latter is positive, and we provide a complete and effective classification result of
p
-Dirichlet improvable numbers, see Theorem
3.1
below. This result deeply relies on the classification of critical lattices for unit balls
\mathcal{B}_{p}
in
L_{p}
, completed by Glazunov, Golovanov, and Malyshev in
[
13
]
.

Before formulating other problems, we recall an important metrical result that follows from Ergodic theory (see Theorem 1 from
[
4
]
by Andersen and Duke).

Theorem C.

For every strongly symmetric norm
F
, almost all
\alpha
in the sense of Lebesgue measure are not
F
-Dirichlet improvable, that is, for almost all
\alpha
we have the equality

d_{F}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta_{F}}}
.

One more crucial result was proven in
[
30
]
.
It has a general part that deals with
irreducible norms
and a corollary for the Euclidean norm.

Theorem D.

If
F
is an irreducible norm on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
whose unit ball is not a parallelogram, then the set of all badly approximable
F
-Dirichlet non-improvable numbers
\mathbf{DI}^{c}_{F}\cap\mathbf{BA}

has full Hausdorff dimension. In particular, the set of all badly approximable
2
-Dirichlet non-improvable numbers
\mathbf{DI}^{c}_{2}\cap\mathbf{BA}

has full Hausdorff dimension.

For the definition of
irreducible norm
see the original paper
[
33
]
and the recent one
[
28
]
. We do not want to go into the details here.
We should note that for
L_{p}
-norms, Theorem D gives a result only in the case
p=2
, because

L_{p}
-norms for
p\neq 1,2
are not irreducible (see Lemma 8 from
[
33
]
), while for
p=1
the unit ball
\mathcal{B}_{1}
is a parallelogram.

Also, in
[
29
]
Kleinbock and Rao proved the following result.

Theorem E.

For each norm
F
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the set
\mathbf{DI}_{F}
is of measure zero but winning in the sense of Schmidt. In particular,
\mathbf{DI}_{F}
has full Hausdorff dimension.

As the set of badly approximable numbers
\mathbf{BA}
is also winning, we get that for any norm
F
, the intersection
\mathbf{DI}_{F}\cap\mathbf{BA}
is winning and therefore it has full Hausdorff dimension.

Moreover, in
[
30
]
Kleinbock and Rao proved that either

\mathbf{BA}\subset\mathbf{DI}_{F}
or else
\mathbf{BA}\setminus\mathbf{DI}_{F}
has full Hausdorff dimension.

This motivated them to formulate the following open question, which is stated in
[
30
]
.

Problem II.

Does the difference
\mathbf{DI}_{2}\setminus\mathbf{BA}
have full Hausdorff dimension?

In this paper, we resolve this question in a more general form for any
L_{p}
-norm with
p\in[1,\infty)
. Namely, we prove

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
p\in[1,\infty)
, the set
\mathbf{DI}_{p}\setminus\mathbf{BA}
is of full Hausdorff dimension.

Note that for
p=\infty
, the statement of Theorem
1.1
does not hold, as
\mathbf{DI}_{\infty}\setminus\mathbf{BA}=\mathbb{Q}
.

In fact, Theorem
1.1
is a consequence, among many other corollaries, of the aforementioned complete characterization of
p
-Dirichlet improvability of
\alpha
in terms of regular continued fraction expansion (
5
) for any
p\in[1,\infty)
, that is Theorem
3.1
.

In
[
30
]
, the authors also formulated the following open problem about the sets of Dirichlet improvable numbers.

Problem III.

What can be said about the size of
\mathbf{DI}_{\nu_{1}}\setminus\mathbf{DI}_{\nu_{2}}
for two different norms
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
?

Our methods allow us to answer this question for two
L_{p}
-norms. In this introduction, we state the result for the two most interesting cases
\mathbf{DI}_{2}\setminus\mathbf{DI}_{1}
and
\mathbf{DI}_{1}\setminus\mathbf{DI}_{2}
, for which we prove full dimension results. We use the notation
\dim_{\mathrm{H}}X
for the Hausdorff dimension of a set
X
.

Theorem 1.2
.

We have

\dim_{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{DI}_{2}\setminus\mathbf{DI}_{1}=\dim_{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{DI}_{1}\setminus\mathbf{DI}_{2}=1.

Other results on this problem are listed in Theorem
3.7
in Section
3
.

2.
Auxiliary remarks

In this section, we collect together some basic facts related to the application of continued fractions theory to Diophantine Approximation and discuss the relations with Dirichlet improvability.

2.1.
Continued fractions and bad approximability

Let
\alpha
be a real irrational number. We consider its continued fraction expansion

(5)

\alpha=[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{\nu},a_{\nu+1},\ldots],\,\,\,\,\,a_{0}\in\mathbb{Z},\,\,a_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},\,j=1,2,3,\ldots

as well as the irrationality measure function

(6)

\psi_{\alpha}(t)=\min_{q\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}:\,q\leqslant t}||q\alpha||,\,\,\,\,\text{where}\,\,\,\,||x||=\min_{a\in\mathbb{Z}}|x-a|.

Let us consider
\nu
-th convergent to
\alpha
defined as

\frac{p_{\nu}}{q_{\nu}}=[a_{0};a_{1},\ldots,a_{\nu}]

and the values

(7)

\xi_{\nu}=||q_{\nu}\alpha||=|p_{\nu}-q_{\nu}\alpha|,\,\,\,\,\alpha_{\nu}=[a_{\nu};a_{\nu+1},a_{\nu+2},\ldots]=\frac{\xi_{\nu-2}}{\xi_{\nu-1}},\,\,\,\,\alpha_{\nu}^{*}=\frac{q_{\nu-1}}{q_{\nu}}=[0;a_{\nu},a_{\nu-1},\ldots,a_{2},a_{1}].

In this paper, we also use the notation

(8)

\mathfrak{z}_{\nu}=(q_{\nu},p_{\nu}-q_{\nu}\alpha)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

In particular,
for the points defined in (
8
) and for the lattice defined in (
1
)
we have
\mathfrak{z}_{\nu}\in\Lambda_{\alpha}(1)
.

For the values of the irrationality measure function, we have a nice equality

(9)

\psi_{\alpha}(t)=\xi_{\nu}=\frac{1}{q_{\nu}(\alpha_{\nu+1}+\alpha_{\nu}^{*})}=\frac{1}{q_{\nu+1}(1+\alpha_{\nu+2}^{-1}\alpha_{\nu+1}^{*})}\,\,\,\text{for}\,\,\,q_{\nu}\leqslant t<q_{\nu+1},

which is sometimes called Perron’s formula,
and the upper bound

\psi_{\alpha}(t)<\frac{1}{t}\,\,\,\,\,\forall\,t\geqslant 1.

The last inequality is equivalent to Dirichlet’s theorem (Theorem
A
).

For our purposes, the Lagrange constant

\lambda(\alpha)=\liminf_{t\to\infty}t\psi_{\alpha}(t)=\liminf_{\nu\to\infty}q_{\nu}\xi_{\nu}=\liminf_{\nu\to\infty}\frac{1}{\alpha_{\nu+1}+\alpha_{\nu}^{*}}

and the Dirichlet constant

(10)

d(\alpha)=\limsup_{t\to\infty}t\psi_{\alpha}(t)=\limsup_{\nu\to\infty}q_{\nu+1}\xi_{\nu}=\limsup_{\nu\to\infty}\frac{1}{1+\alpha_{\nu+1}^{-1}\cdot\alpha_{\nu}^{*}}

are of major importance.
The theorem of Hurwitz states that for all
\alpha
, one has

0\leqslant\lambda(\alpha)\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}},

while Szekeres’ theorem
[
44
]
claims that for all irrational
\alpha
one has

\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{5}}\leqslant d(\alpha)\leqslant 1.

There is an extended theory which deals with the study of the sets of possible values of
\lambda(\alpha)
and
d(\alpha)

which are called the
Lagrange spectrum
and the
Dirichlet spectrum
respectively.

It is well-known that the following five conditions for irrational
\alpha
are equivalent:

A.

\alpha\in\mathbf{BA}
;

B.

\inf_{q\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q||q\alpha||>0
;

C.

\sup_{\nu\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}a_{\nu}<\infty
;

D.

\lambda(\alpha)>0
;

E.

d(\alpha)<1
.

In particular,
the equivalence
A

\Longleftrightarrow

E
is a reformulation of Theorem B, whereas the equivalence
C

\Longleftrightarrow

E
is practically a Theorem B
′
.

Here we should note that Theorem B and its multidimensional generalization were proven by Davenport and Schmidt in
[
11
]
.

Beresnevich et al. in
[
5
]
deepened the result of Davenport and Schmidt, by showing that the set

\mathbf{FS}(n):=\mathbf{DI}(n)\setminus\Bigl(\mathbf{BA}(n)\cup\mathbf{Sing}(n)\Bigr)

for
n\geqslant 2
has continuum many points. Here
\mathbf{DI}(n)
,
\mathbf{BA}(n)
and
\mathbf{Sing}(n)
are
n
-dimensional analogues (in a sense of Diophantine properties) of
\mathbf{DI}_{\infty}
,
\mathbf{BA}
and
\mathbb{Q}
respectively.
As it was mentioned in
[
5
]
, in the case
n=2
this result also follows from a complete description of the two-dimensional Dirichlet spectrum
discovered by Akhunzhanov and Shatskov
[
2
]
, see also
[
1
]
.
Our Theorem
1.1
shows that if we stay in dimension one, but change the norm from supremum norm to other
L_{p}
-norms, the corresponding set also has continuum many points. Moreover, it has full Hausdorff dimension. The same full dimension result is conjectured for
\mathbf{FS}(n)
with
n\geqslant 2
in
[
5
]
.

All the facts mentioned above can be found in the classical books

[
6
,
9
,
40
,
41
]
.
In particular, a lot is known about the Lagrange spectrum
(see
[
9
,
38
]
). As for the Dirichlet spectrum, one may
consider papers

[
23
,
22
,
24
]
and references therein.

2.2.
Relations with different norms

For simplicity of notation, in the case of
L_{p}
-norms we use the convention and write
\mathcal{B}_{p}=\mathcal{B}_{F^{[p]}}
,
\mathfrak{L}_{p}=\mathfrak{L}_{F^{[p]}}
. Also, for the
L_{p}
-norm
F^{[p]}
we use the notation

d^{[p]}(\alpha)=\limsup_{t\to\infty}\lambda_{1}(t).

First, we note that for minima
\lambda_{i}(t)
defined in (
2
), we get that the dilated body

\lambda_{1}(t)\cdot\mathcal{B}_{F}

for any
t
has a non-trivial point of
\Lambda_{\alpha}(t)
on its boundary, while the body

\lambda_{2}(t)\cdot\mathcal{B}_{F}

contains a pair of linearly independent points of
\Lambda_{\alpha}(t)
.

Also note that if
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}
, then
\lambda_{1}(t)\to 0
when
t\to\infty
.
Thus,
d_{F}(\alpha)=0
for any rational
\alpha
and for any norm
F
, so every rational
\alpha
is trivially
F
-Dirichlet improvable for any
F
.

Now consider the following two examples.

When our norm is a sup-norm
F^{[\infty]}
, one can easily see that

(\lambda_{1}(t_{n}))^{2}=q_{n+1}||q_{n}\alpha||=\lim_{t\to q_{n+1}-}t\psi_{\alpha}(t)

for the irrationality measure function defined in (
6
).
In other words, taking into account the second equality from (
9
), one has

(\lambda_{1}(t_{n}))^{2}=D\left(\frac{1}{\alpha_{n+2}},\alpha_{n+1}^{*}\right),\,\,\,\,\text{where}\,\,\,\,D(x,y)=\frac{1}{1+xy}.

Therefore, the definition of Dirichlet constant (
10
) may be rewritten as

d(\alpha)=(\limsup_{t\to\infty}\lambda_{1}(t))^{2}=(d^{[\infty]}(\alpha))^{2}.

The value
d^{[2]}(\alpha)
was studied in
[
30
]
. The value
d^{[1]}(\alpha)
is related to the so-called Minkowski spectrum
\mathbb{M}
(see
[
39
]
), associated with Minkowski diagonal continued fraction. In particular, in the notation of
[
39
]
, one has

\mathfrak{m}(\alpha)=\limsup_{t\to\infty}t\cdot\mu_{\alpha}(t)=\frac{(d^{[1]}(\alpha))^{2}}{4}.

Also, we should note
that the set of values of
t
for which

\lambda_{1}(t)=\lambda_{2}(t)

forms an infinite strictly increasing sequence
\{t_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Moreover,

(11)

d_{F}(\alpha)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{1}(t_{n})=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{2}(t_{n}).

A classical geometric construction which goes up to Hermite
[
16
]
and Minkowski
[
36
,
37
]

describes an algorithm of constructing the sequence
\{t_{n}\}_{n}
. It is related to certain generalizations of regular continued fractions.
This construction was explained in detail in a recent paper by Andersen and Duke
[
4
]
.

3.
Main results and their corollaries

We begin this section with the formulation of our main classificational result.
The proof of this result is based on the classification of critical lattices for
\mathcal{B}_{p}
, completed by Glazunov, Golovanov, and Malyshev in
[
13
]
. Davis
[
12
]
observed that the structure of the set of critical lattices
\mathfrak{L}_{p}
differs depending on whether
p
is greater or smaller than
p_{0}
, where
p_{0}\approx 2.57
is some constant. The detailed description of critical lattices is given in Section
5
.

Theorem 3.1
.

(
a
)

Let
2<p<p_{0}
. Then
\alpha\in\mathbf{DI}_{p}^{c}

if and only if in the continued fraction for
\alpha
there are patterns of the type

(12)

x,1,1,y\,\,\,\,\,\text{or}\,\,\,\,\,x,2,y

with
\min(x,y)\to\infty
.

(
b
)

Let
p\in(1,2)\cup(p_{0},\infty)
. Consider
\varsigma_{p}
defined in (
23
).

(
b1

4.
Application of compactness argument

In this section, we recall well-known statements about critical lattices.

All the definitions concerning convergence in the space of lattices, compactness
and the related properties
are discussed
in Chapter V of the book

[
7
]
or in a modern survey by Kleinbock, Shash and Starkov
[
31
]
from a dynamical point of view
(see Subsection 1.3, example

from
[
31
]
).
We identify the space of unimodular lattices with
\mathfrak{X}={\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Here, we are interested in normalised critical lattices of the form

(19)

\overline{\Lambda}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta_{F}}}\cdot\Lambda,\,\,\,\,\,\Lambda\in\mathfrak{L}_{F},\,\,\,\,{\rm det}\,\overline{\Lambda}=1.

We define the set of all the lattices of the form (
19
) as

normalised critical locus
and denote it simply by
\overline{\mathfrak{L}}_{F}
.
As for discussion concerning critical loci, one can see a recent paper
[
28
]
.
In particular,
\overline{\mathfrak{L}}_{F}
is a compact set in the space of lattices
\mathfrak{X}
.

Recall that we always have the inequality

d_{F}(\alpha)\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta_{F}}}.

For our purpose, we need the following statement, which is an immediate corollary of
Mahler’s theory (see Chapter V from
[
7
]
) and compactness of
\overline{\mathfrak{L}}_{F}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Real number
\alpha
satisfies the equality

d_{F}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta_{F}}}

(is
F
-Dirichlet non-improvable)
if and only if there exists a lattice
\Lambda\in\mathfrak{L}_{F}

and a subsequence
\tau_{k}\to\infty,\,k\to\infty
such that the sequence of unimodular lattices

\Lambda_{\alpha}(\tau_{k})

converges to
\overline{\Lambda}
in the space of lattices.

Proof.

First, suppose that there is a sequence
\{\tau_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\Lambda_{\alpha}(\tau_{k})\to\overline{\Lambda}\in\overline{\mathfrak{L}}_{F}
.
Then, by continuity of the first successive minimum, we have

\lambda_{1}(\Lambda_{\alpha}(\tau_{k}),\mathcal{B}_{F})\to\lambda_{1}(\overline{\Lambda},\mathcal{B}_{F})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta_{F}}}
. This means that
d_{F}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta_{F}}}
and so
\alpha
is
F
-Dirichlet non-improvable.

On the other hand,
if
\alpha
is
F
-Dirichlet non-improvable, then there exists a sequence
\{\tau_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
with

\tau_{k}\to\infty
, such that

\lambda_{1}(\Lambda_{\alpha}(\tau_{k}),\mathcal{B}_{F})\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta_{F}}}

and

\lambda_{1}(\Lambda_{\alpha}(\tau_{k}),\mathcal{B}_{F})
increases monotonically. By the Mahler criterion
there exists a compact
\mathfrak{X}_{1}\subset\mathfrak{X}
such that
\Lambda_{\alpha}(\tau_{k})\in\mathfrak{X}_{1}
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
So we can choose a subsequence
\tau_{k_{l}},l\to\infty
such that

\lambda_{1}(\Lambda_{\alpha}(\tau_{k_{l}}),\mathcal{B}_{F})\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta_{F}}}

and
\Lambda_{\alpha}(\tau_{k_{l}})
converges to a certain
\overline{\Lambda}_{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{X}_{1}
.
Function
\overline{\Lambda}\mapsto\lambda_{1}(\overline{\Lambda},\mathcal{B}_{F})
is continuous in
\mathfrak{X}
.
So
\lambda_{1}(\overline{\Lambda}_{\alpha},\mathcal{B}_{F})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta_{F}}}
. But this means that

\Lambda_{\alpha}(\tau_{k_{l}})\to\overline{\Lambda}_{\alpha}\in\overline{\mathfrak{L}}_{F}

7.
More basic facts from continued fraction theory

In this section, we provide the necessary basic facts from the theory of continued fractions, which we use in the upcoming sections.

Recall that the
n
th convergent of
x
is defined as

\displaystyle\frac{p_{n}\left(x\right)}{q_{n}\left(x\right)}=\left[a_{1}\left(x\right),\ldots,a_{n}\left(x\right)\right].

For simplicity, we will denote
p_{n}=p_{n}\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\right)
,
q_{n}=q_{n}\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\right)
when there is no ambiguity. Both
p_{n}
and
q_{n}
satisfy the iterative formula, for any
k\geqslant 2
we have

(36)

\begin{split}p_{k}&=a_{k}p_{k-1}+p_{k-2},\\
q_{k}&=a_{k}q_{k-1}+q_{k-2},\end{split}

where
p_{0}=0,p_{1}=1
and
q_{0}=1,q_{1}=a_{1}
.

We will sometimes use a notation
\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle:=q_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
. For different values of
n\in\mathbb{N}
, this function
\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle:\mathbb{N}^{n}\to\mathbb{N}
depends on
n
variables
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
and is called
a continuant
. This is a strictly growing function with respect to any of its variables. Moreover, it is a strictly decreasing function upon deleting one of its variables. To be precise, for any
n
and any
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, we have

q_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{i-1},a_{i},a_{i+1},\ldots,a_{n})\geqslant q_{n-1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{i-1},a_{i+1},\ldots,a_{n})\text{ for any }i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}.

For
n\geqslant 1
and any
\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\right)\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, where
a_{i}\in\mathbb{N},1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, we define
\textit{a cylinder of order }n
, denoted by
I_{n}\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\right)
as

\displaystyle I_{n}\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\right):=\left\{x\in\left[0,1\right):a_{1}\left(x\right)=a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\left(x\right)=a_{n}\right\}.

We have the following standard facts about cylinders.

Proposition 7.1

(
[
27
]
)
.

For any
n\geqslant 1
and
\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\right)\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
,
p_{k},q_{k}
are defined recursively by
\left(\ref{rule}\right)
where
0\leqslant k\leqslant n
, we have

(37)

I_{n}\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left[\dfrac{p_{n}}{q_{n}},\dfrac{p_{n}+p_{n-1}}{q_{n}+q_{n-1}}\right)&{\rm if}\,n\textrm{ is even},\\
\left(\dfrac{p_{n}+p_{n-1}}{q_{n}+q_{n-1}},\dfrac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}\right]&{\rm if}\,n\textrm{ is odd}.\\
\end{array}\right.

Therefore, the length of a cylinder is given by

(38)

\left|I_{n}\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\right)\right|=\dfrac{1}{q_{n}\left(q_{n}+q_{n-1}\right)}.

For a better estimation, we need the following results. For any
n\geqslant 1
and
\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\right)\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, we have

Lemma 7.2

(
[
27
]
)
.

For any
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
, we have

(39)

\dfrac{a_{k}+1}{2}\leqslant\dfrac{q_{n}\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\right)}{q_{n-1}\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1},a_{k+1},\ldots,a_{n}\right)}\leqslant a_{k}+1.

Lemma 7.3

(
[
27
]
)
.

For any
k\geqslant 1
, we have

(40)

\begin{split}q_{n+k}\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},a_{n+1},\ldots,a_{n+k}\right)&\geqslant q_{n}\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\right)\cdot q_{k}\left(a_{n+1},\ldots,a_{n+k}\right),\\
q_{n+k}\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},a_{n+1},\ldots,a_{n+k}\right)&\leqslant 2q_{n}\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\right)\cdot q_{k}\left(a_{n+1},\ldots,a_{n+k}\right).\end{split}

For any
n\geqslant 1
, since
p_{n-1}q_{n}-p_{n}q_{n-1}=\left(-1\right)^{n}
, combined with a simple calculation, we get the bound

(41)

q_{n}\geqslant 2^{\left(n-1\right)/2}.

We will also use a trivial bound

(42)

a_{n}\cdot q_{n-1}\leqslant q_{n}\leqslant(a_{n}+1)\cdot q_{n-1}.

An easy corollary of the results listed above is the inequality

(43)

|I_{n+1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},b)|>\frac{1}{(b+1)^{2}}|I_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})|.

Consider an infinite sequence of finite patterns

\hskip 76.82234pt\text{such that }\,\,a_{\nu}=b_{t,1},a_{\nu+1}=b_{t,2},\ldots,a_{\nu+r_{t}-1}=b_{t,r_{t}}\}

The proof of Lemma

Proof of Lemma
8.1
.

For any
0<\varepsilon<1
, we give an explicit construction of the set

\mathbf{BA}_{W}(\varepsilon)\subset\mathbf{BA}_{W}
whose Hausdorff dimension is at least
1-\varepsilon
. This will be enough to conclude that the Hausdorff dimension of
\mathbf{BA}_{W}
is full. We choose the following parameters. Take
0<\varepsilon<1
and put

(45)

M=\left\lceil\frac{8}{\varepsilon\log 2}\right\rceil,\,\,\,\,\,Q_{t}=\prod_{j=1}^{r_{t}}(b_{t,j}+1)^{2},\,\,\,\,\,\nu_{t}=\left\lceil\frac{2\log Q_{t}}{\varepsilon\log 2}\right\rceil

and define a sequence
n_{t}
by

n_{1}=1,\,\,\,\,n_{t+1}=n_{t}+r_{t}+\nu_{t+1}+1.

Note that from our choice of parameters (
45
) it follows that

(46)

2^{\varepsilon}\left(1-\frac{2}{M}\right)\geqslant 2^{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\geqslant Q_{t}^{\frac{1}{\nu_{t}}}.

Define the set

\mathbf{BA}_{W}(\varepsilon)=\{\alpha=[a_{0};a_{1,1},\ldots,a_{1,\nu_{1}},b_{1,1},\ldots,b_{1,r_{1}},\ldots,a_{t,1},\ldots,a_{t,\nu_{t}},b_{t,1},\ldots,b_{t,r_{t}},\ldots]\}\subset\mathbf{BA}_{W}.

Here we mean that
\mathbf{BA}_{W}(\varepsilon)
consists of those
\alpha
for which in its continued fraction expansion
there are patterns (
44
) under our consideration with partial quotients
b_{i,j}
,
and
a_{i,j}
are arbitrary positive integers satisfying the condition
a_{i,j}\leqslant M
.

We take the following inequality from the famous paper by Jarnik
(see p. 104, lines 9-10 from

[
25
]
), where he proved that the set of badly approximable numbers
\mathbf{BA}
has full Hausdorff dimension:

(47)

\sum_{a=1}^{M}|I_{n+1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},a)|^{s}>2^{\varepsilon}\left(1-\frac{2}{M}\right)|I_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})|^{s}>Q_{t}^{\frac{1}{\nu_{t}}}|I_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})|^{s},\,\,\,\,\,s=1-\varepsilon,

where in the last inequality we used (
46
).

Inequalities
(
43
) and (
47
) are valid for arbitrary continued fractions with partial quotients

a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
. Now we use this fact for convergents of numbers from the set

\mathbf{BA}_{W}(\varepsilon)
.

We define two functions
f(n),\,g(n),\,n=1,2,3,\ldots
.
Let
a_{n}
be the
n
-th partial quotient for a number
\alpha\in\mathbf{BA}_{W}(\varepsilon)
.
Then either
a_{n}=a_{i,j}
and in this case we put

f(n)=1,\,\,\,\ g(n)=M,\,\,\,\,\,\text{so}\,\,\,\,f(n)\leqslant a_{n}\leqslant g(n),

or

a_{n}=b_{i,j}
and in this case we put

f(n)=g(n)=b_{i,j},\,\,\,\,\,\text{so again}\,\,\,\,f(n)\leqslant a_{n}\leqslant g(n).

We should note that this definition of
f(n),g(n)
does not depend on a particular choice of

\alpha\in\mathbf{BA}_{W}(\varepsilon)
. Now inequalities (
43
), (
47
) together with definition of

Q_{t}
(
45
) give us

\sum_{a:\,f(n+1)\leqslant a\leqslant g(n+1)}|I_{n+1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},a)|^{s}>F(n)\,|I_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})|^{s},\,\,\,\,\,s=1-\varepsilon

with

F(n)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{(b_{t,j}+1)^{2}}\,\,\text{if}\,\,\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}(\nu_{i}+r_{i})+\nu_{t}<n\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{t}(\nu_{i}+r_{i}),\cr Q_{t+1}^{\frac{1}{\nu_{t+1}}}\,\,\text{if}\,\,\sum_{i=1}^{t}(\nu_{i}+r_{i})<n\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{t}(\nu_{i}+r_{i})+\nu_{t+1}.\end{cases}

This function, by construction, satisfies

\prod_{k=1}^{n}F(k)\geqslant 1\,\,\,\,\,\text{for all}\,\,n\text{ sufficiently large}.

So we are just in the conditions of Lemma 5 from
[
15
]
. The conclusion of this lemma is that the Hausdorff dimension of the set

\mathbf{BA}_{W}(\varepsilon)
is at least
s=1-\varepsilon
.

9.
The Hausdorff dimension of
\mathbf{DI}_{p}\setminus\mathbf{BA}
and
\mathbf{DI}_{p_{1}}\setminus\mathbf{DI}_{p_{2}}

In this section, we prove Theorem

and Theorem
1.2
. To do this, we will utilize some symbolic spaces and facts from the theory of continued fractions.

Fix a fast-growing sparse sequence
\{n_{i}\}_{i}
of natural numbers. Next, fix a sequence of words of natural numbers
\mathcal{A}=(A_{1},A_{2},\ldots)
, so that for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
, a word

has length

(48)

\ell_{i}=|A_{i}|<\Delta_{i+1}=n_{i+1}-n_{i}

and each

Define a map
S^{\prime}=S^{\prime}(\{n_{i}\}_{i},\mathcal{A})\colon\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
acting by

(49)

(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n_{1}-1},a_{n_{1}},\ldots)\to(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n_{1}-1},A_{1},a_{n_{1}},\ldots,a_{n_{2}-\ell_{1}-1},A_{2},a_{n_{2}-\ell_{1}},\ldots)=(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots).

Basically,
S^{\prime}
"inserts" a word
A_{i}
starting at the place with index
n_{i}
, while moving apart
a_{k}
’s accordingly. In particular, for any
i\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
b_{n_{i}}=a_{i,1}
. For a fixed map (that is for fixed
\{n_{i}\}_{i}
and
\mathcal{A}
), we define a counting function

\omega(n):=\#\{k\leqslant n\colon\,b_{k}=a_{i,j}\text{ for some }(i,j)\}.

This function counts how many ’new’ elements appeared under
S
among the first
n
terms.

Consider an inverse map
P^{\prime}:=S^{\prime-1}
, so that

takes a sequence from

and for all

n_{i}
to

and moves all remaining parts together.

It is well-known that for any real irrational number
x\in[0,1)
, the continued fraction expansion is uniquely determined, so we can define a bijection
\theta:\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to[0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}
from a space of sequences of positive integers to real numbers by

\theta(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots)=[0;a_{1},a_{2},\ldots].

For any fixed set
B^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
, we will denote the image of this set under
\theta
by the same letter without the prime symbol, that is
B:=\theta(B^{\prime})
.

Also, define a map
P:[0,1]\to[0,1]
by
P(x)=\theta\circ P^{\prime}\circ\theta^{-1}(x)
. Similarly, we define
S(x)=\theta\circ S^{\prime}\circ\theta^{-1}(x)
.
Finally, define

E^{\prime}_{N}=\{x=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}:1\leqslant x_{n}\leqslant N\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\},

E_{N}=\theta(E^{\prime}_{N})=\{x\in[0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}:1\leqslant a_{n}(x)\leqslant N\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\},

and for
c>0
we let

\Omega(c)=\{x\in[0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}:\,1\leqslant a_{n}(x)\leqslant n^{c}\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\}.

The main instrument is the following statement.

Lemma 9.1
.

Fix a subset
B\subseteq\mathbf{BA}
and write
B_{N}=B\cap E_{N}
.

Fix a sequence
\{n_{i}\}_{i}
and a collection
\mathcal{A}=(A_{1},A_{2},\ldots)
.
Assume that the following three conditions are satisfied.

(1)

The sequence
\{n_{i}\}_{i}
and
\mathcal{A}=(A_{1},A_{2},\ldots)

(2)

For each

, there exists

(3)

The function

satisfies
\omega(n)=\overline{o}(n^{1-\epsilon_{1}})
for some
\epsilon_{1}>0
.

Then for any

and any

-Hölder condition, the map

\dim_{\mathrm{H}}S(B)=\dim_{\mathrm{H}}B.

We believe that Lemma
9.1
is of independent interest and is reusable in other settings of sets of continued fractions, for example, in the questions about sets with restrictions on the growth rate of functions of partial quotients, see
[
17
,
18

19

45
]
. We also note that in all of the applications of this lemma in the present paper, we take sets

. As one can see from the statement, this is not a necessary requirement. Also, this lemma can be seen as a generalization of Lemma

First, let us derive Theorem
1.1
from the comments after Theorem E, Lemma
9.1
, and Theorem
3.1
. We prove Lemma

in a separate Section

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

For each
p\in[1,\infty)
, let
B=B(p):=\mathbf{DI}_{p}\cap\mathbf{BA}
.
By the results of Kleinbock and Rao, we know that

\dim_{\mathrm{H}}B=1.

Now for
p\in\{1\}\cup\{2\}\cup(2,p_{0})\cup\{p_{0}\}
and for
p\in(1,2)\cup(p_{0},\infty)
with
\varsigma_{p}\notin\mathbb{Q}
, we apply Lemma
9.1
with this choice of
B
, and choose the parameters to be
n_{i}=2^{i}
and
A_{i}=i
for any
i\in\mathbb{N}
, that is we insert a single digit in the places with indices being powers of
2
. Note that for this choice of
n_{i}
, the quantity
\Delta_{i}=n_{i}-n_{i-1}
is an even number.

To highlight diverse types of applications of the lemma, in the case
p\in(1,2)\cup(p_{0},\infty)
with
\varsigma_{p}\in\mathbb{Q}
, we take the parameters to be
n_{i}=2^{i+100}
and
A_{i}=(2,i,4)
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, so that starting at places with indices being powers of
2
, we insert three digits, where the middle one is growing, and two on the sides are bounded. We note that in this case, we can also come to a contradiction by inserting the same pattern as in all previous cases.

By Lemma
9.1
, for the set
G=G(p):=S(B)
we get the equality

\dim_{\mathrm{H}}G=\dim_{\mathrm{H}}B=1.

The last thing to show is that
G\subset\mathbf{DI}_{p}\setminus\mathbf{BA}.

Clearly,
G
avoids the set of badly approximable numbers, as it has an infinite subsequence of growing partial quotients coming from the sequence
\mathcal{A}
. We need to show that any element
x\in G
satisfies
x\in\mathbf{DI}_{p}
.

By
distance
between two partial quotients
a
and
b
in the pattern, we mean the number of partial quotients lying strictly between
a
and
b
. We also refer to the partial quotients from
A_{i}
as
large
partial quotients (as they are growing to infinity).

Now, we distinguish cases of different values of
p
.

(1)

Case
p=1
. By definition of the map
S
, we need to make sure that the procedure (
49
) will not generate restricted patterns in a number

x=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n_{1}-1},A_{1},a_{n_{1}},\ldots,a_{n_{2}-2},A_{2},a_{n_{2}-1}\ldots),

provided that there were no restricted patterns in
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n_{1}-1},a_{n_{1}},\ldots)
. As we have three different types of patterns, let us consider three possibilities.

(a)

Patterns
b_{k},\ldots,b_{1},1,1,b_{1}+1,b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}
or
b_{k},\ldots,b_{2},b_{1}+1,1,1,b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}
for arbitrarily long
k
.

Clearly, those will not appear in
x
if they were not originally present in the preimage. This is because
x
has the same blocks of digits as the preimage except for a strictly growing sequence
(A_{1},A_{2},\ldots)
. As the restricted pattern of this case is (almost) symmetrical, the addition of strictly growing numbers cannot generate a restricted pattern.

(b)

Patterns of finite length
x,b_{k},\ldots,b_{1},1,1,b_{1}+1,b_{2},\ldots,b_{k},y
and others with
\min(x,y)\to\infty
.

All of these eight patterns in this case have two unbounded partial quotients on the sides. As all partial quotients in
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n_{1}-1},a_{n_{1}},\ldots)
are bounded, the only candidates for unbounded are
A_{i}
’s from
\mathcal{A}
. But one sees that the distance between large partial quotients
A_{i}
and
A_{i+1}
grows with
i
, so we cannot have infinitely many patterns of the same finite length.

(c)

Patterns

x,2,y
or
x,1,1,y
with
\min(x,y)\to\infty
.

Here, we are required to have two unbounded partial quotients with only one or two partial quotients between them. The only unbounded partial quotients in our case are
A_{i}
’s, which are located at a growing distance between two consecutive ones, thus this pattern cannot occur infinitely many times.

Therefore, we get that
G\subset\mathbf{DI}_{1}\setminus\mathbf{BA}
.

(2)

Case
2<p<p_{0}
. The only two patterns to be avoided in this case are the patterns with two growing partial quotients being at a distance 1 or 2 from each other infinitely many times, that is pattern
x,1,1,y
or
x,2,y
with
\min(x,y)\to\infty
. As the only growing partial quotients are located in the places with indices
2^{i}
, the distance between two consecutive large partial quotients is growing, hence this procedure will not generate infinitely many restricted patterns.

Therefore, we get that
G\subset\mathbf{DI}_{p}\setminus\mathbf{BA}
for
2<p<p_{0}
.

(3)

Case
p\in(1,2)\cup(p_{0},\infty)
with
\varsigma_{p}\in\mathbb{Q}
. All restricted patterns in this case have two growing partial quotients on the sides of it, while the rest of the partial quotients in between two large partial quotients form a symmetrical or almost symmetrical pattern. Once again, the growing partial quotients can only come from
A_{i}
’s, but for each restricted pattern, the partial quotient right after the first large partial quotient
x
and the last partial quotient before the last large partial quotient
y
are either equal to each other, or at least one of them is equal to
1
. However, we inserted patterns of the form
(2,i,4)
, so neither of the two options can happen. Thus, restricted patterns will never occur.

Alternatively, one can see that we also get a contradiction with Mahler’s compactness, as we need to have a subsequence of patterns with ’tails’ converging to some fixed numbers. This cannot happen as the distance between large partial quotients from different
A_{i}
’s grows to infinity.

Therefore, we get that
G\subset\mathbf{DI}_{p}\setminus\mathbf{BA}
for
p\in(1,2)\cup(p_{0},\infty)
with
\varsigma_{p}\in\mathbb{Q}
.

(4)

Case
p\in(1,2)\cup(p_{0},\infty)
with
\varsigma_{p}\notin\mathbb{Q}
. We only need to avoid a pattern
s_{\nu},\ldots,s_{1},1,s_{1},\ldots,s_{\nu}
. As this pattern is symmetric, and we inserted a strictly growing sequence of partial quotients, that restricted pattern cannot occur, as for any fixed number with bounded partial quotients, this new strictly growing sequence will eventually outgrow the bound on ’small’ partial quotients.

Therefore, we get that
G\subset\mathbf{DI}_{p}\setminus\mathbf{BA}
for
p\in(1,2)\cup(p_{0},\infty)
with
\varsigma_{p}\notin\mathbb{Q}
.

(5)

Case
p=p_{0}
. Recall the Remark
3.2
, stating that in the case
p=p_{0}
we need to avoid patterns
x,1,1,y
or
x,2,y
or the same patterns as in the case
p\in(1,2)\cup(p_{0},\infty)
. It is not known whether
\varsigma_{p_{0}}
is rational or not, so we cannot choose between cases
{\bf(b1)}
or
{\bf(b2)}
of Theorem
3.1
. However, we can still prove the result for
p_{0}
by showing that no matter if the value of
\varsigma_{p_{0}}
is rational or irrational, we will avoid restricted patterns of that case.

Once again, as we are inserting large partial quotients with a growing distance between two consecutive large ones, no patterns of finite length can occur infinitely often. This eliminates patterns
x,1,1,y
or
x,2,y
or the ones from
{\bf(b1)}
. The pattern
s_{\nu},\ldots,s_{1},1,s_{1},\ldots,s_{\nu}
with growing
\nu
is eliminated in the exact same way as before, because we cannot get a symmetrical pattern from bounded partial quotients with inserted strictly growing numbers.

Therefore, we get
G\subset\mathbf{DI}_{p_{0}}\setminus\mathbf{BA}
.

(6)

Case
p=2
. First, let us note that any patterns of finite lengths, that is
x,1,1,y
or
x,2,y
with
\min(x,y)\to\infty

\beta^{*}
and
\beta

2^{n}
, so the distance between two consecutive large partial quotients grows. Thus, no finite pattern can occur.

Now we need to deal with infinite patterns (
17

Both of the patterns (
17
) have a partial quotient
1
in the middle. We will call it a

partial quotient.

We will prove that every
x\in G
will avoid the patterns of this form by contradiction. Assume that one of them will appear infinitely many times. Clearly, those patterns should contain at least one partial quotient coming from
A_{i}
, because otherwise, we get a contradiction with the fact that the original number is an element of
\mathbf{DI}_{2}
. Indeed, if a restricted pattern appears and it doesn’t contain any partial quotients from
A_{i}
, then it consists only of bounded partial quotients of the original number, which doesn’t have any restricted pattern as an element of
\mathbf{DI}_{2}
.

Next, we claim that there exists a constant
D\in\mathbb{N}
, such that in each appearance of the pattern from (
17

is at most

. Indeed, assume the opposite, that there exists an integer sequence
\{D_{i}\}_{i}
with

, and a corresponding sequence of restricted patterns
\{P_{i}\}_{i}
with

when

, for which the distance between central partial quotient are the closest large partial quotient in the pattern

is equal to
D_{i}
. But this means that for any such pattern
P_{i}
, we can consider a subpattern

, which consists of a central partial quotient

, which tends to infinity as

. Moreover, this pattern has no large partial quotients in it. So we obtained a sequence of patterns

of arbitrarily large length with only bounded partial quotients in it, thus, we get a contradiction with the fact that the original number was an element of
\mathbf{DI}_{2}
and therefore avoided all restricted patterns of this case.

Now we can safely assume that we have a sequence of arbitrarily long patterns, for which the distance between their central partial quotient and the closest large partial quotient is bounded by some fixed constant
D
. By the structure of the map
S
, the distance between large partial quotients grows to infinity the further we go in the continued fraction expansion. Thus, eventually, we will have patterns of one of the two forms (
17
), such that on one side of the central partial quotient, we have a sequence of bounded partial quotients of growing length, whereas on the other side, we always have a growing partial quotient on a finite distance from the central partial quotient. This contradicts the compactness argument. Indeed, the compactness argument in the case of
p=2
states that there exist
\beta
and
\beta^{*}
for which there exists a sequence of patterns such that on one side of the central partial quotient, the subpattern tends to
\beta
and on the other side, the subpattern tends to
\beta^{*}
. In our case, as shown, one side tends to a rational number, while the other one tends to an irrational one. As in the product, they should give an integer number, we get a contradiction. Thus, the assumption that restricted patterns occurred was incorrect.

Therefore, we get that
G\subset\mathbf{DI}_{2}\setminus\mathbf{BA}
.

Therefore,

Now, let us deal with

Appendix A
Verification of Minkowski conjecture

A.1.
Introductory comments

The statement and the proof of Theorem
3.1
, the main characterization result, use the solution of the Minkowski conjecture. The proof of it is split across 50 years in a dozen manuscripts, with the final solution claimed in 1986 in
[
13
]
by Glazunov, Golovanov and Malyshev. The solution highly relies on computer-assisted computations, and as these were performed at a time when personal computers did not exist, the underlying code was not provided in the papers, not to mention that some computations were carried out on machines manufactured in the 1960s. In this appendix, we present a preliminary report on our confirmation of the proof of the Minkowski conjecture. In the process of doing so, we realized that the original proof of the Minkowski conjecture contained several mistakes, making some parts of the existing proof invalid. The second author is preparing a more detailed paper
[
43
]
on the full verification process, including the details of the interval calculations, more information on the problems in the original sources, and several technical tricks needed for the computation. We independently made all computations ourselves and claim that the Minkowski conjecture is indeed true, though the original strategy from
[
14
,
13
]
and many previous works contain non-trivial flaws.

We attach the link to the GitHub project
[
42
]
containing Julia code that can be run on any modern device. This code corresponds to interval calculations performed to verify the conjecture.

Nevertheless, we start by recording three significant mistakes (using the notation of the original papers) that we managed to find.

•

In
[

]

p
-region from (
58
) into parts where the general behaviour of
\Delta(p,\sigma)
is somewhat similar inside of each
p
-subregion as per Figure
4
.
These would be

(1)

1.01\leq p\leq 1.99
(the function is supposedly strictly increasing);

(2)

2.03407\leq p\leq 2.56809
(the function is either supposedly strictly decreasing or at least the minimum is attained for
\sigma=\sigma_{p}
);

(3)

2.56809\leq p\leq 2.642
(the function has a "bell" shape);

(4)

2.642\leq p\leq 6
(the function is supposedly strictly increasing or at least the minimum is attained for
\sigma=1
).

The remaining small neighborhoods of
p=1
and
p=2
are not covered by the cases (1)–(4) above and are dealt with separately. These are actually the hardest cases to deal with.

For each of the cases (1)–(4) (and in fact in the neigbourhoods of
p=1
and
p=2
, but with extra tricks to be introduced) the idea is to split the
(p,\sigma)
region into tiny rectangles, and in each one of them show the main inequality (
57
) by numerically bounding
\Delta(p,\sigma)
together with its derivatives on each of the rectangles. We did so using a technique called Interval Arithmetic, see the book
[
3
]
or the Wikipedia page
[
20
]
. We used the Julia programming language
[
26
]
(version 1.12.4), where we were able to find a pre-existing Interval Arithmetic library
[
32
]
(version v1.0.2), which has been updated until now.
All calculations were performed on a personal laptop MacBook Air, Apple M4 chip, 16 GB Memory. The theoretical strategy was fully developed by the authors, and the code for some programs was partially generated with the help of Gemini 3 Pro and ChatGPT 5.5.

In this appendix, we provide four tables with the values of parameters for which we ran our programs. Table
1
covers the four main
p
-ranges away from
p=1
and
p=2
. Tables
2
and
3
cover the neighbourhoods of
p=2
and
p=1
, respectively. Table
4
records the remaining endpoint check in the one-sided right neighbourhood of
p=2
. The full programs, as well as some other useful information, can be found in the GitHub repository
[
42
]
.

The purpose of the tables and pseudocode below is to make the verification independent of any particular implementation. The attached programs
[
42
]
provide our version of interval arithmetic verification of the stated inequalities, but it is possible to verify the inequalities from the tables by other means. Thus, even if the code was unavailable, the proof could be reproduced by verifying the same finite collection of inequalities by interval arithmetic or any other rigorous method.

First, let us introduce the functions used in the verification. Write

C_{p}(u)=(1+u^{p})^{-1/p}.

Then

A=C_{p}(\tau)-C_{p}(\sigma),\qquad B=\tau C_{p}(\tau)+\sigma C_{p}(\sigma),

and the critical branch is given by

F(p,\sigma,\tau)=A^{p}+B^{p}-1=0.

The derivatives of
F
with respect to
\sigma
and
\tau
are

\displaystyle F_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=p(1+\sigma^{p})^{-1-1/p}\left(B^{p-1}+\sigma^{p-1}A^{p-1}\right),

\displaystyle F_{\tau}

\displaystyle=p(1+\tau^{p})^{-1-1/p}\left(B^{p-1}-\tau^{p-1}A^{p-1}\right).

We need to analyze the derivative of
\Delta(p,\sigma)
with respect to
\sigma
. Direct differentiation gives

(59)

\frac{\partial\Delta(p,\sigma)}{\partial\sigma}=-\frac{g(p,\sigma)}{(1+\sigma^{p})^{1+1/p}\left(B^{p-1}-\tau^{p-1}A^{p-1}\right)},

where

(60)

g(p,\sigma)=(1+\sigma^{p})^{-1/p}(1-\sigma\tau^{p-1})\left(B^{p-1}+\sigma^{p-1}A^{p-1}\right)-(1+\tau^{p})^{-1/p}(1-\tau\sigma^{p-1})\left(B^{p-1}-\tau^{p-1}A^{p-1}\right).

Therefore,

\operatorname{sgn}g(p,\sigma)=-\operatorname{sgn}\frac{\partial\Delta(p,\sigma)}{\partial\sigma}.

In particular,
g(p,\sigma)<0
implies that
\Delta(p,\sigma)
is increasing in
\sigma
.

Following
[
13
]
, we define

(61)

h(p,\sigma):=\frac{\partial g(p,\sigma)}{\partial\sigma},

where, as above,
\tau=\tau(p,\sigma)
is determined by the critical-branch equation

F(p,\sigma,\tau)=0.

For computer verification purposes in the neighbourhoods of
p=1
and
p=2
, we also use the following functions:

\displaystyle h^{\prime}_{p}(p,\sigma)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\partial h(p,\sigma)}{\partial p},

\displaystyle l^{(1)}(p,\sigma)

\displaystyle:=\Delta(p,\sigma)-\Delta(p,1),

\displaystyle(l^{(1)})^{\prime}_{p}(p,\sigma)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\partial l^{(1)}(p,\sigma)}{\partial p},

\displaystyle(l^{(1)})^{\prime\prime}_{pp}(p,\tau)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\partial^{2}l^{(1)}(p,\tau)}{\partial p^{2}},

\displaystyle g^{\prime}_{p}(p,\sigma)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\partial g(p,\sigma)}{\partial p},

\displaystyle g^{\prime}_{p}(p,\tau)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\partial g(p,\tau)}{\partial p},

\displaystyle g^{\prime\prime}_{p\tau}(p,\tau)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\partial^{2}g(p,\tau)}{\partial p\,\partial\tau},

\displaystyle l^{(0)}(p,\sigma)

\displaystyle:=\Delta(p,\sigma)-\Delta(p,\sigma_{p}),

\displaystyle(l^{(0)})^{\prime}_{p}(p,\sigma)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\partial l^{(0)}(p,\sigma)}{\partial p},

\displaystyle h(p,\sigma):=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g(p,\tau)}{\partial\tau}.

When one of the functions above is written in variables
(p,\tau)
, the value of
\sigma=\sigma(p,\tau)
is determined by the equation
F(p,\sigma,\tau)=0
on the relevant branch.

It remains to introduce the extra notation used only in Table
4
. That table deals with the right-hand neighbourhood of
p=2
, more precisely with

2\leqslant p\leqslant 2.0002,\qquad 1.72\leqslant\sigma\leqslant\sigma_{p}.

This check is written in the variables
(p,\tau)
, as in Table
3
. The new difficulty is the endpoint
\tau=0
. When differentiating with respect to
p
, the factors

\tau^{p-2},\qquad\tau^{p-2}\log\tau

appear. To control these terms on intervals touching
\tau=0
, the program introduces

\delta=p-2,\qquad x=\tau^{\delta}=\tau^{p-2}.

The variable
x
is auxiliary and used in the interval estimates near
\tau=0
. In the expression for
h
, we replace

\tau^{p}=\tau^{2}x,\qquad\tau^{p-1}=\tau x,\qquad\tau^{p-2}=x.

Let
\widehat{h}=\widehat{h}(p,\sigma,\tau,x)
be the expression for
h
after these substitutions. In the partial derivatives

\widehat{h}_{p},\ \widehat{h}_{\sigma},\ \widehat{h}_{x}
, the variables
p,\sigma,x
are treated as independent variables and
\tau
is kept fixed.

The quantity checked in Table
4
is

H_{\delta}=\widehat{h}_{p}+\widehat{h}_{\sigma}\sigma_{\delta}+\widehat{h}_{x}x_{\delta},

where
x_{\delta}=x\log\tau
.
The value of
\sigma_{\delta}
is obtained from the equation
F(p,\sigma,\tau)=0
by differentiating with respect to
p
, keeping
\tau
fixed, so that
\sigma_{\delta}=-\frac{F_{p}}{F_{\sigma}}
.
Hence,

H_{\delta}=\widehat{h}_{p}-\widehat{h}_{\sigma}\frac{F_{p}}{F_{\sigma}}+\widehat{h}_{x}x\log\tau.
