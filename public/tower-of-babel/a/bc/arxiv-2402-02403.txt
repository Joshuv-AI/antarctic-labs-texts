Full Characterization of the Depth Overhead for Quantum Circuit Compilation with Arbitrary Qubit Connectivity Constraint
Pei Yuan, Shengyu Zhang

Abstract
In some physical implementations of quantum computers, 2-qubit operations can be applied only on certain pairs of qubits. Compilation of a quantum circuit into one compliant to such qubit connectivity constraint results in an increase of circuit depth. Various compilation algorithms were studied, yet what this depth overhead is remains elusive. In this paper, we fully characterize the depth overhead by the routing number of the underlying constraint graph, a graph-theoretic measure which has been studied for 3 decades. We also give reduction algorithms between different graphs, which allow compilation for one graph to be transferred to one for another. These results, when combined with existing routing algorithms, give asymptotically optimal compilation for all commonly seen connectivity graphs in quantum computing.

Hardware-compliant circuit construction and depth overhead upper bound

In this section, we first present a compiling algorithm by the maximum matching. Second, we give a graph partition algorithm. Third, based on the graph partition algorithm, we present the general compiling algorithm and the depth overhead upper bound.

Before diving into detailed constructions, let us first compare the measures

Note that Lemma
2
can already give compiling algorithms with depth overhead at most

for some specific graphs

. But for graphs with a small matching size (an extreme example is the star graph which has the maximum matching size

Lemma
2

is inevitable since a bottleneck in a graph does make simultaneous pebble moving inefficient due to traffic congestion. However, this bottleneck also affects

To improve it to the optimal bound of
O(\mathsf{rt}(G))
,
one idea is to partition the constraint graph into vertex-disjoint connected subgraphs, each having

diameter and containing at most
O(\mathsf{rt}(G))

(u_{i},v_{i})
on which a two-qubit gate acts to one of these subgraphs (different pairs may go to different subgraphs).
If this can be achieved, then we can implement two-qubit gates within one subgraph efficiently. Indeed, since each subgraph has diameter
O(1)
, it takes
O(1)
SWAP gates to implement one gate and since the subgraph has size
\mathsf{rt}(G)
, all the gates inside this subgraph can be done by
O(\mathsf{rt}(G))

O(\mathsf{rt}(G))
rounds. Also note that the routings in different subgraphs can be carried out in parallel. Thus the overall overhead is
O(\mathsf{rt}(G))
.

The challenge is that it is not always possible to achieve such a good partition of the constraint graph.
We present a good graph partition algorithm in Lemma
4

Theorem 5

For any connected graph
G
, we have
\mathsf{doh}(G)=O(\mathsf{rt}(G))
.

Proof.

For any graph
G=(V,E)
, we use Lemma
4
to find two families of sets
\mathcal{W}=\{W_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\penalty\ \forall i\in[s]\}
and
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}=\{W_{j}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\penalty\ \forall j\in[t]\}
satisfying the properties in the lemma. Since
|\bigcup_{i\in[s]}W_{i}|+|\bigcup_{j\in[t]}W^{\prime}_{j}|\geq n/2
, at least one of
|\bigcup_{i\in[s]}W_{i}|
and
|\bigcup_{j\in[t]}W^{\prime}_{j}|

. Without loss of generality, we assume that
|\bigcup_{i\in[s]}W_{i}|\geq n/4
. Consider any one layer of a given circuit. Suppose it has
k
(

) 2-qubit gates, which act on pairs
(i_{1},j_{1}),(i_{2},j_{2}),\ldots,(i_{k},j_{k})
of qubits. Note that qubits
i_{r}
and
j_{r}
are generally not adjacent on
G
. We call these the original gates, to distinguish from the compiled ones that are
G
-compliant, which will be referred to as
G
-gates
.

We now show how to compile one layer of 2-qubit original gates to a
G
-compliant circuit of depth at most
O(\mathsf{rt}(G))
.

1.

Take a permutation
\pi
that moves the first
\gamma_{1}=\lfloor|W_{1}|/2\rfloor
pairs
(i_{1},j_{1}),\ldots,(i_{\gamma_{1}},j_{\gamma_{1}})
to inside
W_{1}
, and the next
\gamma_{2}=\lfloor|W_{2}|/2\rfloor

(i_{\gamma_{1}+1},j_{\gamma_{1}+1}),\ldots,(i_{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}},j_{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}})
to inside
W_{2}
, and so on, until we move
\gamma_{s}=\lfloor|W_{s}|/2\rfloor
pairs to
W_{s}
. Implementing this permutation needs at most
\mathsf{rt}(G)
rounds (see Eq. (
9
)). Now the problem of implementing the
\sum_{s}\gamma_{s}
original gates

is reduced to implementing the corresponding gates

with
p
and
q
in some

.

2.

Inside
W_{i}
for each

, we can implement all
\gamma_{i}
2-qubit gates

in depth at most
3\gamma_{i}
. Indeed, for each gate
U_{p,q}
on qubits
(p,q)
in
W_{i}
, since

, either
(p,q)\in E
or there is another vertex

connecting

and

and

(r,q)
, and swap

and

G
-gates suffice to implement all
\gamma_{i}
2-qubit gates in

. Note that each

by Lemma

by Eq. (
8
).

3.

By repeating the above two steps

iterations, we can implement all of the original 2-qubit gates. Since

,
\gamma_{i}=\lfloor|W_{i}|/2\rfloor
, and
\sum_{i}|W_{i}|\geq n/4

O(1)
.

4.

Move all qubits to their original positions, which takes at most
\mathsf{rt}(G)
rounds.

Putting everything together, the depth overhead for compiling one layer of the original circuit is at most

. Applying this to all layers completes the proof.

\mathsf{doh}(G)
protocol can be done very efficiently. Since there are efficient routing protocols for many commonly seen specific graphs

For any connected graph

, we have
\mathsf{doh}(G)=\Omega(\mathsf{rt}(G))
.

Proof.

Suppose

, namely
\pi^{*}
is the hardest permutation in the definition of
\mathsf{rt}(G)
. By Lemma
1
,

of depth 2 realizing

.
By definition of

, we have

\mathsf{doh}(G)=\max_{C}\min_{C^{\prime}\sim C\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\atop G\text{-compliant}}\frac{d(C^{\prime})}{d(C)}\geq\min_{C^{\prime}\sim C\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\atop G\text{-compliant}}\frac{d(C^{\prime})}{d(C^{*})}=\min_{C^{\prime}\sim C\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\atop G\text{-compliant}}\frac{d(C^{\prime})}{2}.

Take a
C^{\prime}
achieving the minimum in the above ratio. Since
C^{*}
is a SWAP circuit and
C^{\prime}
is obtained from
C
by inserting SWAP gates,
C^{\prime}
is also a SWAP circuit. Therefore by the correspondence in Eq. (
24
), we obtain a routing algorithm
A^{*}
of

rounds which can realize
\pi^{*}
. Since this is one particular routing algorithm realizing
\pi^{*}
,
we have

\mathsf{rt}(G)=\mathsf{rt}(G,\pi^{*})\leq\text{ the number of rounds in }A^{*}=d(C^{\prime})=2\cdot\mathsf{doh}(G).

Therefore
\mathsf{doh}(G)\geq\mathsf{rt}(G)/2=\Omega(\mathsf{rt}(G))
.
∎

Combining Theorems
5
and
6

is fully characterized by its routing number, i.e.,
\mathsf{doh}(G)=\Theta(\mathsf{rt}(G))
.
This result shows that our algorithm in Theorem
5
is asymptotically optimal.
The characterization also gives quantitative guidance for the qubit layout and connectivity design of quantum processors when the depth overhead is considered.

5
Discussion

We have fully characterized the depth overhead when compiling a quantum circuit with all-to-all qubit connections to one under a connectivity graph constraint for any graph, by a well-studied graph measure called routing number. We also developed compiling algorithms to achieve the asymptotically optimal depth overhead. This enables us to utilize existing routing algorithms for various specific graphs such as paths, grids and trees, to construct hardware-compliant compilers. Constraint graphs like IBM’s brick walls and Rigetti’s cycle-grids can also be handled via some simple reductions. The characterization of the depth overhead can also be utilized in the design of quantum processors when a small depth overhead is crucially needed.

Appendix A
Hardness of computing depth overhead

In this section, we show the hardness of computing the depth overhead of a given circuit
C
and a constraint graph
G
.

First, we present a few initial concepts that will be utilized in the subsequent proof.

Quantum gates and circuits.

A SWAP gate implements the unitary which swaps the basis
|01\rangle
and
|10\rangle
and keeps
|00\rangle
and
|11\rangle
unchanged. A SWAP gate can be implemented by three CNOT gates.

Throughout this paper, we consider quantum circuits on
n
qubits. Qubit connectivity constraint is specified by an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, where
V
is the set of
n
vertices, often identified with the
n
qubits, and
E
is the set of edges, specifying pairs of qubits that two-qubit gates can act on. A circuit without any qubit connectivity constraint is called an
unconstrained circuit
. A circuit made of SWAP gates only is called a
SWAP circuit
. A SWAP circuit
C
implements a permutation on qubits in the sense that
C\ket{x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{n}}=\ket{x_{\pi(1)}x_{\pi(2)}\ldots x_{\pi(n)}}
for some qubit permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
.

For any qubit connectivity graph
G
and any permutation
\pi
of vertices, there is a natural one-one correspondence between the following two sets:

\{\text{$G$-compliant SWAP circuits $C$ of depth $d$ realizing $\pi$}\}\\
\leftrightarrow\{\text{routing algorithm $A$ of $d$ rounds on $G$ realizing $\pi$}\}.

(24)

Indeed, each layer of a SWAP circuit
C
consists of SWAP gates on distinct qubits, which 1-1 correspond to one round of a routing algorithm consisting of swapping pebbles. Note that
C
and
A
implement the same permutation, and are compliant with the same graph
G
.

Two quantum circuits
C
and
C^{\prime}
are equivalent if they realize the same unitary operation. In this paper, we are interested in a special
SWAP equivalence
, denoted by
C^{\prime}\sim C
, in which
C^{\prime}
is obtained from
C
by inserting SWAP gates and applying
C
’s gates on permuted qubits. More specifically, suppose that
C
=
\prod_{i=1}^{d}L_{i}
where
L_{i}
is the
i
-th layer, made of one- and two-qubit gates on distinct qubits. A circuit
C^{\prime}
satisfies
C^{\prime}\sim C
if
C^{\prime}=P(\pi)^{\dagger}\prod_{i=1}^{d}L_{i}^{\prime}
, where
L_{i}^{\prime}
is a circuit similar to
L_{i}
, but possibly with SWAP circuits inserted between the gates in
L_{i}
: If a gate
U
in
L_{i}
is on qubits
p
and
q
, then in
L_{i}^{\prime}
it should be
U
applied to qubits
\sigma(p)
and
\sigma(q)
, where
\sigma
is the permutation on qubits realized by the inserted SWAP gates in circuit
C^{\prime}
from beginning to right before

;

\pi
is the permutation of qubits by
all
SWAP gates in circuit
\prod_{i=1}^{d}L_{i}^{\prime}
, and
P_{\pi}^{\dagger}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\ket{x_{\pi(1)}x_{\pi(2)}\ldots x_{\pi(n)}}\mapsto\ket{x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{n}}
is just to permute the qubits back to their original positions.

Languages and decision problems.

A
language

L
is a set of strings of finite lengths over a finite alphabet
\Sigma
. The corresponding
decision problem
D_{L}
is to decide whether an input instance

belongs to the language
L
. A language

(or its decision problem

M
such that the following two statements are equivalent for any input

: (1)
x\in L
, and (2) there is a certificate string

of length polynomial in that of

such that

accepts
(x,y)

[
43
]
. A language
L
or its decision problem

is

if
L
is in NP and any other NP problem can be reduced to

in polynomial time. If an NP-complete language

in polynomial time, then

is also NP-complete.

It is known that computing the routing number
\mathsf{rt}(G,\pi)
of a given connected graph

is hard, as the following lemma states.

Lemma 10

(Routing deciding problem,
[
32
]
)
.

Deciding whether
\mathsf{rt}(G,\pi)\leq k
on a given graph
G

Second, using this result, we can show the hardness of computing the depth overhead.

.

\mathsf{doh}(G,C)\leq\alpha

Proof.

We first show that the hardness of deciding whether

\{(G,C,\alpha)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathsf{doh}(G,C)\leq\alpha\}

which is equivalent to

and obtained from

Next, for any given graph

of the routing deciding problem

Circuit

•

For any permutation

•

If

.

–

Define an unconstricted circuit
C_{\pi}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\text{SWAP}(a_{2i-1},a_{2i})
, which consists of
m
SWAP gates. Since all
a_{i}
’s are distinct, the depth of
C_{\pi}
is clearly
d(C_{\pi})=1
.

–

Let
\alpha=k
.

•

If
\pi^{2}\neq id
, then
\pi
is not a transposition. According to Lemma
1
,
\pi
can be decomposed as two transpositions
\pi=\sigma_{1}\circ\sigma_{2}
. As discussed above, we can construct two SWAP circuits of depth 1 for
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
.

–

Combining these SWAP circuits gives an unconstrained SWAP circuit
C_{\pi}
with depth
d(C_{\pi})=2
.

–

Let
\alpha=k/2
.

Observe that in either case, the circuit
C_{\pi}
realizes permutation
\pi
in the natural sense that
C_{\pi}\ket{x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{n}}=\ket{x_{\pi(1)}x_{\pi(2)}\ldots x_{\pi(n)}}
, and that
\alpha\cdot d(C_{\pi})=k
. Furthermore, the above algorithm mapping
(G,\pi,k)
to
(G,C_{\pi},\alpha)
can clearly be implemented in polynomial time. Now we will show that

\mathsf{rt}(G,\pi)\leq k\text{ if and only if }\mathsf{doh}(G,C_{\pi})\leq\alpha.

First, we claim that the following equality holds for this particular
C_{\pi}
.

\displaystyle\min\big\{d(C_{\pi}^{\prime})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C_{\pi}^{\prime}\sim C_{\pi},\text{ and $C_{\pi}^{\prime}$ is $G$-compliant}\big\}=\mathsf{rt}(G,\pi).

(25)

•

“
\geq
”: Take any
C_{\pi}^{\prime}
that achieves the minimum of the left-hand side. Since
C_{\pi}
realizes
\pi
and
C_{\pi}^{\prime}\sim C_{\pi}
, we have that
C_{\pi}^{\prime}
realizes
\pi
as well. Also note that
C_{\pi}
is a SWAP circuit, and
C_{\pi}^{\prime}
is obtained from
C_{\pi}
by inserting SWAP gates, therefore
C_{\pi}^{\prime}
is also a SWAP circuit. By the correspondence in Eq. (
24
),
C_{\pi}^{\prime}
induces a
d(C_{\pi}^{\prime})
-round routing algorithm realizing
\pi
, thus
\mathsf{rt}(G,\pi)
as the minimum number of rounds of such protocols is at most
d(C_{\pi}^{\prime})
.

•

“
\leq
”: Take any routing algorithm
A
with
\mathsf{rt}(G,\pi)
rounds. Again by the correspondence in Eq. (
24
),
A
induces a
\mathsf{rt}(G,\pi)
-depth SWAP circuit
C(A)
realizing
\pi
. Any
C_{\pi}^{\prime}\sim C_{\pi}
only inserts SWAP gates to
C_{\pi}
, thus is also a SWAP circuit. Therefore, the set in the left-hand side contains all
G
-compliant SWAP circuits realizing
\pi
, including
C(A)
. Thus the minimum depth
d(C_{\pi}^{\prime})
is at most the depth of
C(A)
, which is
\mathsf{rt}(G,\pi)
.

Now with Eq. (
25
), we can see that

\displaystyle\ \mathsf{doh}(G,C)\leq\alpha

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\ \min\big\{d(C_{\pi}^{\prime})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C_{\pi}^{\prime}\sim C_{\pi},\text{ and $C_{\pi}^{\prime}$ is $G$-compliant}\big\}/d(C_{\pi})\leq\alpha

\displaystyle(\text{by definition of }\mathsf{doh}(G,C))

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\ \frac{\mathsf{rt}(G,\pi)}{d(C_{\pi})}\leq\alpha

\displaystyle(\text{by Eq.\penalty\ \eqref{eq:npc-reduction}})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\ \mathsf{rt}(G,\pi)\leq k

\displaystyle(\text{by definition of }\alpha)

This completes the proof.
∎
