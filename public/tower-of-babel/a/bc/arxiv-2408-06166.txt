Detecting adversarial attacks on random samples
Gleb Smirnov

Abstract
. This paper studies the problem of detecting adversarial perturbations in a sequence of observations. Given a data sample X 1 , … , X n X_{1},\ldots,X_{n} drawn from a standard normal distribution, an adversary, after observing the sample, can perturb each observation by a fixed magnitude or leave it unchanged. We explore the relationship between the perturbation magnitude, the sparsity of the perturbation, and the detectability of the adversary’s actions, establishing precise thresholds for when detection becomes impossible.



1. Introduction

We consider the problem of testing a simple hypothesis against a complex alternative. Let
\boldsymbol{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
be a data sample drawn from the standard normal distribution,
N(0,1)
. An adversary, after observing
\boldsymbol{X}
, can either attack or leave the data unchanged based on a signal from a third party. If an attack occurs, the adversary adds a vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})
from a subset
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
\boldsymbol{X}
. The choice of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
may depend on the original data
\boldsymbol{X}
, unlike the typical hypothesis testing scenarios. The perturbed observation is denoted by
\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}
. We define the hypotheses as:

H_{0}:\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{X}\quad\text{(no attack)}

H_{1}:\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{X}+\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{X})\quad\text{(attack)}.

The classifier must decide, based on
\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}
and the set of possible perturbations
\Theta
, whether an attack occurred (
H_{1}
) or not (
H_{0}
). The test accepts
H_{0}
if
\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}
lies within a Borel subset
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

\text{Accept }H_{0}\text{ iff }\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\in\Omega.

The objective is to minimize the false negative rate,

P(\text{accept }H_{0}\mid H_{1}),

while keeping the false alarm rate below a threshold
\alpha
(e.g., 0.05):

P(\text{accept }H_{1}\mid H_{0})\leqslant\alpha.

The adversary has full access to the classifier’s test
(white-box attack), in principle allowing them to craft a highly effective perturbation. However, in the scenario considered here, this access is essentially useless — the adversary gains little to no advantage from knowing the test. The classifier knows the set
\Theta
but not the adversary’s strategy for choosing
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. However, in our example, revealing the adversary’s strategy does not benefit the classifier.

For the set of potential attacks
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
(again, known to both the classifier and the adversary), some regularity conditions are necessary to ensure that related sets are Borel measurable. In this paper, we consider two examples of
\Theta
: an
(n-1)
-dimensional sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a finite set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Both cases satisfy the required regularity.

The problem can also be framed geometrically. Given a Borel set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we translate
\Omega
by
-\boldsymbol{\theta}
for each
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta
and take the union of these translations:

\Omega-\Theta=\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Omega\quad\text{for some }\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta\right\}.

Here,
-\Theta
denotes the set obtained by applying the antipodal map to
\Theta
. Thus,
\Omega-\Theta
is the Minkowski sum of
\Omega
and
-\Theta
, hence the notation.

Let
\boldsymbol{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
be a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with iid components, each
N(0,1)
, and let
\gamma
be the corresponding Gaussian measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The task is to bound the Gaussian volume of
\Omega-\Theta
in terms of that of
\Omega
. Specifically, we have:

\gamma(\Omega)=1-P\left(\text{accept }H_{1}\mid H_{0}\right),\quad\gamma(\Omega-\Theta)=P\left(\text{accept }H_{0}\mid H_{1}\right).

We now describe the specific scenario considered in this paper. Fix a number
a>0
, for now constant, independent of
n
. For each
i=1,\ldots,n
, let

\theta_{i}\in\{\pm a,0\},

meaning the adversary can perturb each coordinate
X_{i}
by
\pm a
or leave it unchanged. The strength of the adversarial perturbation is measured using the sparsity ratio of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, defined as:

\text{{SR}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{\#\{i:\theta_{i}=0\}}{n}.

Intuitively, attacks with a very high sparsity ratio should be nearly impossible to detect. To ensure a meaningful problem setup, we should impose an upper bound on the perturbation strength. Conversely, when many data points are altered, the attack becomes easier for the classifier to detect. We seek to determine the threshold for the sparsity ratio above which the adversarial attack becomes undetectable by the classifier.

2.

To illustrate the approach used in §
5
,
we consider a simpler problem. Fix a radius
R>0
, known to the adversary and the classifier. The adversary observes a sample
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
,
X_{i}\sim N(0,1)
, and can select any data-dependent perturbation
(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})
such that

(2.1)

\sum_{i}\theta_{i}^{2}=R^{2}.

Proposition 3

(Sphere)
.

Based on the received observations, the adversary
can design an attack
\boldsymbol{\theta}
satisfying (
2.1
) such that, regardless of the
classifier’s test,

P\left(\text{{accept $H_{0}$}}\,|\,H_{1}\right)+P\left(\text{{accept $H_{1}$}}\,|\,H_{0}\right)>1-P(B_{R/2}),

where
B_{R/2}
is the ball of radius
R/2
, and
P(B_{R/2})
is the probability that
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
falls inside
B_{R/2}
.

Proof.

To begin, we define a random vector
\boldsymbol{Y}
as follows. If
\boldsymbol{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
falls outside the ball
B_{R/2}
, where
B_{R/2}
is the ball of radius
R/2
centered at the origin, we proceed by constructing a sphere
C_{R}
of radius
R
centered at
\boldsymbol{X}
. Let
S_{\|\boldsymbol{X}\|}
denote the sphere of radius
\|\boldsymbol{X}\|
centered at the origin. The intersection of the spheres
S_{\|\boldsymbol{X}\|}
and
C_{R}
forms another sphere, but of dimension
n-2
. We endow this resulting
(n-2)
-dimensional sphere with the uniform measure and choose
\boldsymbol{Y}
as a random point on it. On the other hand, if
\boldsymbol{X}
lies inside
B_{R/2}
, we set
\boldsymbol{Y}=\boldsymbol{X}
.

We claim that
\boldsymbol{Y}
is again a Gaussian vector with iid components, each
N(0,1)
. By construction, the norm of
\boldsymbol{Y}
equals that of
\boldsymbol{X}
, and the direction of
\boldsymbol{Y}
is uniformly distributed, as it is for
\boldsymbol{X}
. Additionally, the direction of
\boldsymbol{Y}
is independent of its norm. These properties characterize the Gaussian distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the claim follows.

The adversary’s strategy for constructing
\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}
is as follows: Given
\boldsymbol{X}
, the adversary evaluates
\boldsymbol{Y}
as described above. If
\boldsymbol{X}
lies outside
B_{R/2}
, then set
\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{Y}
. If
\boldsymbol{X}
is inside
B_{R/2}
, choose
\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}
to be any point at a distance
R
from
\boldsymbol{X}
. Under this strategy, we derive the following lower bound on the false-negative rate:

P\left(\text{{accept }}H_{0}\,|\,H_{1}\right)=P(\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\in\Omega)\geqslant P(\boldsymbol{X}\notin B_{R/2},\boldsymbol{Y}\in\Omega)\geqslant\\
P(\boldsymbol{X}\notin B_{R/2})+P(\boldsymbol{Y}\in\Omega)-1=P(\boldsymbol{X}\notin B_{R/2})+P(\boldsymbol{X}\in\Omega)-1.

By definition,
P\left(\text{{accept }}H_{1}\,|\,H_{0}\right)=1-P(\boldsymbol{X}\in\Omega)
, and the proposition follows. ∎

3. Coin testing

To establish a key lemma for §
4
, we consider adversarial attacks on Bernoulli-distributed data. Let
Z
be a random variable with
P(Z=1)=p>1/2
and
P(Z=-1)=1-p
. Both the adversary and the classifier know
p
and agree on a number
\varepsilon>0
, which will measure
the attack strength. The adversary observes independent trials
(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n})
from
Z
and selects a subset of
Z_{i}
, of fraction less than
(2p-1)-\varepsilon
, to leave intact while flipping the signs of the rest. The classifier receives the possibly contaminated sequence and must choose between
H_{0}
(no flips) and
H_{1}
(flipped). We show that if the fraction of unaffected points is less than
2p-1-\varepsilon
, reliable testing is always possible.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be independent of
n
and small enough that
(2p-1)-\varepsilon>0
. Suppose that the adversary flips all but a fraction less than
(2p-1)-\varepsilon
of
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
. Then for every
\alpha>0
, there exists a test such that for all large enough
n
,

P(\text{{accept }}H_{1}\,|\,H_{0})\leqslant\alpha,\quad\text{{and}}\quad P(\text{{accept }}H_{0}\,|\,H_{1})\leqslant\alpha.

Proof.

Consider
A_{n}(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n})=n^{-1}\sum_{i}Z_{i}
. Hoeffding’s inequality gives, for each
\lambda>0
,

(3.1)

P(A_{n}-\mathbb{E}(A_{n})\leqslant-\lambda/\sqrt{n})\leqslant e^{-\lambda^{2}/2},\quad\mathbb{E}(A_{n})=2p-1.

Choose
\lambda
such that
e^{-\lambda^{2}/2}<\alpha
. Let
Z^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,Z^{\prime}_{n}
be the observations received by the classifier, and define the test:

\Omega=\left\{\sqrt{n}(A_{n}(Z^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,Z^{\prime}_{n})-(2p-1))>-\lambda\right\}.

Under
H_{0}
(no attack), the false alarm probability is
\leqslant\alpha
. Under
H_{1}
(attack), we obtain:

n^{-1}\sum_{i}Z^{\prime}_{i}=n^{-1}\left(\sum_{\text{flipped}}Z^{\prime}_{i}+\sum_{\text{not flipped}}Z^{\prime}_{i}\right)=n^{-1}\left(-\sum_{\text{flipped}}Z_{i}+\sum_{\text{not flipped}}Z_{i}\right)=\\
=-n^{-1}\sum_{i}Z_{i}+2n^{-1}\sum_{\text{not flipped}}Z_{i}<-n^{-1}\sum_{i}Z_{i}+2(2p-1-\varepsilon),

where “flipped” refers to points changed by the adversary. Thus if
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
and
n
are such that

n^{-1}\sum_{i}Z_{i}-(2p-1)>-\lambda/\sqrt{n},\quad n>\lambda^{2}/\varepsilon^{2},

we get:

n^{-1}\sum_{i}Z^{\prime}_{i}-(2p-1)<-n^{-1}\sum_{i}Z_{i}+(2p-1)-2\varepsilon<\lambda/\sqrt{n}-2\varepsilon<\lambda/\sqrt{n}.

Consequently, under
H_{1}
, the events

\sqrt{n}(A_{n}(Z^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,Z^{\prime}_{n})-(2p-1))>-\lambda,\qquad\sqrt{n}(A_{n}(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n})-(2p-1))>-\lambda

are mutually exclusive, and the lemma follows. ∎
We now analyze the case where the adversary is forced to flip the signs of all observations
Z_{i}
, while letting
p
approach
1/2
, but not too quickly.

Lemma 2
.

Assume
\sqrt{n}(2p-1)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, and the adversary is no longer allowed to leave any
Z_{i}
intact. Then for every
\alpha>0
, there exists a test such that for all large enough
n
,

P(\text{{accept }}H_{1}\,|\,H_{0})\leqslant\alpha,\quad\text{{and}}\quad P(\text{{accept }}H_{0}\,|\,H_{1})\leqslant\alpha.

Proof.

We define a simpler test as:

\Omega=\left\{A_{n}(Z^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,Z^{\prime}_{n})>0\right\}.

Choose
n
large enough that
\sqrt{n}(2p-1)>\lambda
. From (
3.1
), we get:

A_{n}(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n})>A_{n}(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n})-\left[(2p-1)-\lambda/\sqrt{n}\right]>0

with probability at least
1-e^{-\lambda^{2}/2}
.
Under
H_{1}
, we obtain:

\sum_{i}Z^{\prime}_{i}=-\sum_{i}Z_{i}.

Consequently, under
H_{1}
, the events

A_{n}(Z^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,Z^{\prime}_{n})>0,\qquad A_{n}(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n})>0

are mutually exclusive, and the lemma follows. ∎

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be iid random variables, each

, representing the original observations. Partition the real line

into a countable union of bins

U_{k}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}:ka-\frac{a}{2}<x<ka+\frac{a}{2}\right\},\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

Whatever the values of
X_{i}
, chances are all of them lie within the union of

. Define the random variable
Z_{i}
associated with

Z_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }X_{i}\in U_{k}\text{ for some even }k,\\
-1&\text{if }X_{i}\in U_{k}\text{ for some odd }k.\end{cases}

Let

denote the probability measure of

, i.e.,

p_{k}=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\int_{ka-a/2}^{ka+a/2}e^{-x^{2}/2}\,dx.

Each

is a Bernoulli random variable with

(4.1)

P(Z_{i}=1)-P(Z_{i}=-1)=\sum_{k}(-1)^{k}p_{k}.

Using the function

\sum_{k}(-1)^{k}p_{k}=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\sum_{k}\int_{ka-a/2}^{ka+a/2}e^{-x^{2}/2}\,dx=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\sum_{k}\int_{-a/2}^{a/2}e^{-(x+ka)^{2}/2}\,dx=G(a).

In the event of an attack, the adversary replaces the original observations with contaminated observations

, where
X^{\prime}_{i}=X_{i}+\theta_{i}
, and

\theta_{i}\in\{-1,0,1\},\quad\frac{\#\{i:\theta_{i}=0\}}{n}<G(a)-\varepsilon.

Similar to

, define

as:

Z^{\prime}_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }X^{\prime}_{i}\in U_{k}\text{ for some even }k,\\
-1&\text{if }X^{\prime}_{i}\in U_{k}\text{ for some odd }k.\end{cases}

Observe that

Z^{\prime}_{i}=\begin{cases}Z_{i}&\text{if }\theta_{i}=0,\\
-Z_{i}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

Thus, under
H_{0}
(no attack), we have
Z^{\prime}_{i}=Z_{i}
, and the classifier observes a Bernoulli sequence characterized by (

). Under
H_{1}
(attack), the observed sequence

results from flipping the signs of

, with fewer than

of the observations not flipped.

As shown in Lemma

, it is possible to consistently distinguish between these two cases, regardless of how small
\varepsilon
is. This completes the proof of (1) of Proposition
1
.

Set
a=c/\sqrt{\ln{n}}
. To establish Proposition
2
, it suffices to show that if
c>\pi
, then
\sqrt{n}G(a)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. The proof then follows from Lemma
2
.

Substituting
a=c/\sqrt{\ln{n}}
into (
1.3
), we get:

G(a)=\dfrac{4}{\pi}\sum_{k\geqslant 0}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{1+2k}e^{-(1+2k)^{2}\cdot\pi^{2}/2a^{2}}>\dfrac{4}{\pi}\left[e^{-\pi^{2}/2a^{2}}-\frac{1}{3}e^{-9\cdot\pi^{2}/2a^{2}}+\ldots\right]>\dfrac{4}{\pi}e^{-\pi^{2}/2a^{2}}\left[1-\frac{1}{3}e^{-4\pi^{2}/a^{2}}\right]=\\
=\dfrac{4}{\pi}n^{-\pi^{2}/2c^{2}}\left[1-\frac{n^{-4\pi^{2}/c^{2}}}{3}\right]>n^{-\pi^{2}/2c^{2}}\quad\text{for $n$ large enough.}

This completes the proof of (2) of Proposition
2
, pending the proof of (
1.3
).

Lemma 3

((
1.3
))
.

For each
a>0
,

G(a)=\int_{-a/2}^{a/2}g(x)\,dx=\dfrac{4}{\pi}\sum_{k\geqslant 0}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{1+2k}\exp\left(-\frac{(1+2k)^{2}\pi^{2}}{2a^{2}}\right).

Proof.

This integral is computed
using the Poisson summation
formula:

(4.2)

\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}f(k)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f(y)e^{-2\pi iky}\,dy,

where
f
satisfies certain conditions (e.g., Schwartz). See § 6 in
[
6
]
.

We write:

g(x)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\sum_{k}(-1)^{k}e^{-(x+ka)^{2}/2}\,dx=(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-x^{2}/2}\sum_{k}e^{k(\pi i-ax)-k^{2}a^{2}/2},

and using (
4.2
),

(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-x^{2}/2}\sum_{k}e^{k(\pi i-ax)-k^{2}a^{2}/2}=(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-x^{2}/2}\sum_{k}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{y(\pi i-ax)-y^{2}a^{2}/2}\cdot e^{-2\pi iky}\,dy,

we find:

(2\pi)^{-1/2}\sum_{k}(-1)^{k}e^{-(x+ka)^{2}/2}\,dx=a^{-1}e^{-\pi^{2}/2a^{2}}\sum_{k}e^{(2k-1)\pi ix/a}\cdot e^{2\pi^{2}k/a^{2}-2\pi^{2}k^{2}/a^{2}}.

Calculating

\int_{-a/2}^{a/2}e^{(2k-1)\pi ix/a}\,dx=(-1)^{k}\dfrac{2a}{(1-2k)\pi},

we get:

\int_{-a/2}^{a/2}g(x)\,dx=\dfrac{2}{\pi}e^{-\pi^{2}/2a^{2}}\sum_{k}\dfrac{(-1)^{k}}{1+2k}e^{-2\pi^{2}k/a^{2}-2\pi^{2}k^{2}/a^{2}}=\\
=\dfrac{2}{\pi}\sum_{k}\dfrac{(-1)^{k}}{1+2k}e^{-\pi^{2}/2a^{2}\cdot(1+2k)^{2}}=\dfrac{4}{\pi}\sum_{k\geqslant 0}\dfrac{(-1)^{k}}{1+2k}e^{-\pi^{2}/2a^{2}\cdot(1+2k)^{2}},

and the lemma follows. ∎

5. Proof of (2) of Propositions
1
and
2

To design the adversary’s strategy, we use the initial sample
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
and the value
a
to generate a new normal sample
X^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,X^{\prime}_{n}
, where for each
i
,
X^{\prime}_{i}-X_{i}\in\{-a,0,a\}
. To begin with, we construct a discrete-time Markov chain on
\mathbb{R}
with the following properties. For
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, let
q(y|x)
denote the probability of transitioning to
y
from
x
. The transition probabilities are as follows:

q(x+a\mid x)=\varphi(x),\quad q(x\mid x)=\gamma(x),\quad q(x-a\mid x)=1-\varphi(x)-\gamma(x),

and
q(y\mid x)=0
for other values of
y
.

Let
p
be the density of the standard normal distribution
N(0,1)
,
defined as:

p(x)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-x^{2}/2}.

The functions
\varphi
and
\gamma
are chosen to satisfy the following condition:

(5.1)

\varphi(x-a)p(x-a)+\gamma(x)p(x)+\left[1-\varphi(x+a)-\gamma(x+a)\right]p(x+a)=p(x),

which ensures that the standard normal distribution is the stationary distribution of the Markov chain.

In addition, the function
\gamma
is designed to satisfy:

(5.2)

\int\gamma(x)p(x)\,dx=G(a),

where
G(a)
is defined by (
1.2
). In effect, this condition bounds the probability that the Markov chain remains in place after one step.

Before constructing the functions
\varphi
and
\gamma
, first show how they will be used in the proof. The adversary’s strategy for constructing
X^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,X^{\prime}_{n}
proceeds as follows: Given
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
, the adversary starts with
X_{1}
as the initial state of the Markov chain defined above. After one transition step,
X_{1}
moves to a new state, denoted
Y_{1}
. Since
X_{1}
is Gaussian,
Y_{1}
is also Gaussian. The adversary repeats this process for each observation, obtaining an iid sequence
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
, each

Y_{i}
being
N(0,1)
.

Let
T
be the event:

T=\left\{\frac{\#\{i:X_{i}=Y_{i}\}}{n}<G(a)+\varepsilon\right\}.

If
T
occurs, set
X^{\prime}_{i}=Y_{i}
for each
i
. Otherwise, choose
X^{\prime}_{i}
arbitrarily as either
X_{i}-a
or
X_{i}+a
. Conditioning on
T
, the adversary can challenge any test
\Omega
using
X^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,X^{\prime}_{n}
, achieving the following lower bound on the false-negative rate:

P\left(\text{{accept }}H_{0}\,|\,H_{1}\right)=P((X^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,X^{\prime}_{n})\in\Omega)\geqslant P(T\cap(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})\in\Omega)\geqslant\\
\geqslant P(T)+P((Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})\in\Omega)-1=P(T)+P((X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\in\Omega)-1.

Since
P\left(\text{{accept }}H_{1}\,|\,H_{0}\right)=1-P((X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
, it follows that

P\left(\text{{accept }}H_{0}\,|\,H_{1}\right)+P\left(\text{{accept }}H_{1}\,|\,H_{0}\right)\geqslant P(T).

It remains to show that
T
occurs with high probability. Define
Z_{i}
as:

Z_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }X_{i}=Y_{i},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Since
P(Z_{i}=1\,|\,X_{i})=\gamma(X_{i})
, it follows from (
5.2
) that

P(Z_{i}=1)=\int\gamma(x)p(x)\,dx=G(a).

Letting
S_{n}=Z_{1}+\cdots+Z_{n}
, Hoeffding’s bound gives:

P(\overline{T})=P(n^{-1}S_{n}-G(a)\geqslant\varepsilon)\leqslant e^{-2n\varepsilon^{2}},

completing the proof of Proposition
1
.

Set
a=c/\sqrt{\ln{n}}
. To establish Proposition
2
, it suffices to show
that if
c<\pi/\sqrt{2}
, then
S_{n}=0
with high probability. Indeed:

(5.3)

P(S_{n}=0)=(1-G(a))^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{G-int}}}{{=}}\left(1-\dfrac{4}{\pi}\sum_{k\geqslant 0}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{1+2k}e^{-(1+2k)^{2}\cdot\pi^{2}/2a^{2}}\right)^{n}>\left(1-\dfrac{4}{\pi}e^{-\pi^{2}/2a^{2}}\right)^{n}=\\
=\left(1-\dfrac{4}{\pi}n^{-\pi^{2}/2c^{2}}\right)^{n}=1-O\left(n^{-\pi^{2}/2c^{2}}\right).

The rest of this section shows the existence of functions
\gamma
and
\varphi
that satisfy the necessary conditions. To begin with, define
\gamma
as:

\gamma(x)=\begin{cases}\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}e^{-kax-k^{2}a^{2}/2},&\text{if }x\in(-a/2,a/2),\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

For
x
where
\gamma(x)\neq 0
, it relates to the function
g
defined by (
1.1
) via

p(x)\gamma(x)=g(x).

Thus, to establish (
5.2
), we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\gamma(x)p(x)\,dx=\int_{-a/2}^{a/2}g(x)\,dx=G(a).

Since
\gamma
represents a transition probability, it must satisfy

0\leqslant\gamma(x)\leqslant 1\quad\text{for each }x.

Let us first prove that
\gamma
is non-negative, i.e.,

(5.4)

\gamma(x)=\sum_{k}(-1)^{k}e^{-kax-k^{2}a^{2}/2}>0\quad\text{for all }|x|<a/2.

Setting
b=-a^{2}
and
z=\pi i-xa
, we can rewrite the series as:

\sum_{k}(-1)^{k}e^{-kax-k^{2}a^{2}/2}=\sum_{k}e^{\pi ik-kax-k^{2}a^{2}/2}=\sum_{k}e^{kz+bk^{2}/2}.

This series, representing the Jacobi theta
function
\vartheta_{3}(z)
, has zeros forming a lattice with basis
2\pi i
,
b
, centered at
z_{0}=\pi i+b/2
.
This classical result is found in § 2 in
[
13
]
. See also
[
6
]
. In the
x
-coordinate, the zeros are given by

\dfrac{a}{2}+\dfrac{2\pi i\,n}{a}+a\,m\quad\text{for all $n,m\in{\mathbb{Z}}$}.

Consequently,

(5.5)

\sum_{k}(-1)^{k}e^{-kax-k^{2}a^{2}/2}\neq 0\quad\text{for each $a>0$
and $|x|<a/2$,}

and this series vanishes at
x=\pm a/2
. Setting
x=0
, we find:

\sum_{k}(-1)^{k}e^{-k^{2}a^{2}/2}\to 1\quad\text{as $a\to\infty$.}

confirming (
5.4
) for
x=0
and large
a
. But then, using (
5.5
), we extend (
5.4
) to all
a>0
and
|x|<a/2
. Thus, we have shown that
\gamma
is non-negative.

Let us now show that
\gamma(x)\leqslant 1
for each
x
. Assuming
|x|<a/2
, we find:

\gamma(x)p(x)=\sum_{k}(-1)^{k}p(x+ka)=p(x)+\sum_{k\geqslant 1}(-1)^{k}p(x+ka)+\sum_{k\leqslant-1}(-1)^{k}p(x+ka)<p(x),

since
p
is an even function and monotonically decreases for
x>0
.

With
\gamma
constructed, we now find
\varphi
. To do this, we replace the stationarity condition (
5.1
) with the stronger
detailed balance
condition:

\varphi(x)p(x)=\left[1-\gamma(x+a)-\varphi(x+a)\right]p(x+a).

A Markov chain satisfying detailed balance for
p
necessarily has
p
as a stationary distribution. At the same time, this equation is straightforward to solve:

p(x)\varphi(x)=\sum_{k\geqslant 1}(-1)^{k+1}\left[1-\gamma(x+ka)\right]p(x+ka).

This formula recovers
\varphi
. It remains to verify that
0\leqslant\varphi(x)\leqslant\varphi(x)+\gamma(x)\leqslant 1
for all
x
. This is indeed true, and the verification follows similarly to the argument for
0\leqslant\gamma(x)\leqslant 1
above. We omit the detailed calculation for brevity.
