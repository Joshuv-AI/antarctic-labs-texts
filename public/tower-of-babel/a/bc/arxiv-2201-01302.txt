Evidence of large recoil velocity from a black hole merger signal
Vijay Varma, Sylvia Biscoveanu, Tousif Islam, Feroz H. Shaik, Carl-Johan Haster, Maximiliano Isi, Will M. Farr, Scott E. Field, Salvatore Vitale

Abstract
The final black hole left behind after a binary black hole merger can attain a recoil velocity, or a “kick”, reaching values up to 5000 km/s. This phenomenon has important implications for gravitational wave astronomy, black hole formation scenarios, testing general relativity, and galaxy evolution. We consider the gravitational wave signal from the binary black hole merger GW200129​ _ 065458 (henceforth referred to as GW200129), which has been shown to exhibit strong evidence of orbital precession. Using numerical relativity surrogate models, we constrain the kick velocity of GW200129 to v f ∼ 1542 − 1098 + 747 v_{f}\sim 1542^{+747}_{-1098} km/s or v f ≳ 698 v_{f}\gtrsim 698 km/s (one-sided limit), at 90% credibility. This marks the first identification of a large kick velocity for an individual gravitational wave event. Given the kick velocity of GW200129, we estimate that there is a less than 0.48 % 0.48\% ( 7.7 % 7.7\% ) probability that the remnant black hole after the merger would be retained by globular (nuclear star) clusters. Finally, we show that kick effects are not expected to cause biases in ringdown tests of general relativity for this event, although this may change in the future with improved detectors.



Introduction.
—
When two black holes (BHs) orbit each other, they emit gravitational waves
(GWs) which carry away energy and angular momentum. This causes the orbit to
shrink in a runaway process that culminates in the merger of the BHs into a
single remnant BH. At the same time, the GWs can also carry away linear
momentum from the binary, shifting its center of mass in the opposite
direction
Fitchett 1983
. Most of the linear momentum is lost near
the merger
Gonzalez et al. 2007a
, resulting in a recoil or “kick” velocity
imparted to the remnant BH.

Kicks are particularly striking for precessing binaries, in which the component
BH spins are tilted with respect to the orbital angular momentum. For these
systems, the spins interact with the orbital angular momentum as well as with
each other, causing the orbital plane to precess
Apostolatos et al. 1994
.
Numerical relativity (NR) simulations revealed that the kick velocities for
precessing binaries can reach values up to
\sim 5000

km/s
Campanelli et al. 2007
;
Gonzalez et al. 2007b
;
Lousto and Zlochower 2011
, large enough to
be ejected from any host galaxy
Merritt et al. 2004
.

Kicks have important implications for BH astrophysics. Following a supermassive
BH merger, the remnant BH can be displaced from the galactic center or ejected
entirely
Merritt et al. 2004
, impacting the galaxy’s
evolution
Komossa and Merritt 2008
, fraction of galaxies with central supermassive
BHs
Volonteri et al. 2010
, and event rates
Sesana 2007
for the
future LISA mission
Amaro-Seoane et al. 2017
. For stellar-mass BHs like those
observed by LIGO
Aasi et al. 2015
and
Virgo
Acernese et al. 2015
, kicks can limit the formation of heavy BHs. BH
masses greater than
{\sim}65M_{\odot}
are disfavored by supernova
simulations
Woosley 2017
;
Marchant et al. 2018
, but have been seen in GW
events
Abbott et al. 2020a
;
Abbott et al. 2021a
;
Abbott et al. 2021b
. This
could be explained by second-generation mergers
Gerosa and Fishbach 2021
, in which
one of the component BHs is itself a remnant from a previous merger, and is
thus more massive than the original stellar-mass progenitors. However, if the
kick from the first merger is large enough, the remnant BH would get ejected
from its host galaxy and would not participate in another merger.

Unfortunately, observational evidence of large kicks has been elusive. While
various candidates from electromagnetic observations have been identified,
their nature is debated
Bogdanovic et al. 2021
. Similarly, observing kicks
using GW signals has been challenging
Varma et al. 2020
;
Abbott et al. 2020b
;
Gerosa and Moore 2016
;
Calderón Bustillo et al. 2018
;
Lousto and Healy 2019
;
Healy et al. 2020
;
Mahapatra et al. 2021
. For example,

Varma et al. 2020

Varma et al. 2020
used accurate models based on
NR simulations to show that kicks from precessing binaries can be reliably
inferred with LIGO-Virgo operating at their design sensitivity. However, the
GW events analyzed in Ref.
Varma et al. 2020
, which only included signals in
the first two LIGO-Virgo observing runs
Abbott et al. 2019
, were not
loud enough to constrain the kick.

Since then, the LIGO-Virgo detectors have been further upgraded, and the GW data
from the third observing run were released in two stages,
O3a
Abbott et al. 2021a
and O3b
Abbott et al. 2021b
. Notably, O3a
provided the first evidence for precession in the ensemble population of merging
binaries
Abbott et al. 2021c
, even though none of the individual GW events
unambiguously exhibited precession
Abbott et al. 2021a
. Finally, in O3b, the
binary BH merger GW200129 was identified as the first individual GW event
showing strong evidence of precession
Abbott et al. 2021b
;
Hannam et al. 2021
. Similarly, support for large kicks was identified in the
ensemble population using the O3a data
Varma et al. 2021a
;
Doctor et al. 2021
,
even though the individual events were not loud enough for an unambiguous kick
inference
Varma et al. 2020
;
Islam et al. 2022
(with the exception of
GW190814
Abbott et al. 2020c
, which was found to have a
small
kick of

\sim 74^{+10}_{-7}
km/s at 90% credibility
Mahapatra et al. 2021
).

In this
Letter
, we use the method developed in Ref.
Varma et al. 2020

to show that GW200129 has a large kick velocity (
\mathchar 21016\,1542^{+747}_{-1098}
km/s at 90%
credibility). As an application of the kick constraint, we compute the
retention probability for the remnant BH of GW200129 in various host
environments, and discuss the implications for the formation of heavy
stellar-mass BHs. Finally, we show that Doppler effects due to the kick on the
remnant mass measurement are small for this event, and should not impact
ringdown tests of general relativity (GR).

NRSur7dq4

t_{\mathrm{ref}}/M\!=\!-100

Remnant mass and Doppler shifts.
—
Our method provides predictions for both the magnitude and direction of the
kick
Varma et al. 2020
. If the kick vector
\bm{v}_{f}
has a significant
component along (or opposite) the line-of-sight, the observed GW signal can be
influenced by the kick. At leading order, the kick’s effect can be described
as a Doppler shift of the GW frequency
Gerosa and Moore 2016
. However, as GR
lacks any intrinsic length scales, a uniform increase in signal frequency is
completely degenerate with a decrease in total mass
M
, and vice versa. Thus,
if not explicitly accounted for, a frequency shift due to a kick can bias mass
measurements. In particular, because the kick is mostly imparted near the
merger
Gonzalez et al. 2007a
, the Doppler shift only affects the merger and
ringdown part of the signal. This can lead to biases in the measurement of the
remnant mass
m_{f}

Varma et al. 2020
;
Ma et al. 2021
, and potentially impact
tests of GR using the ringdown signal
Abbott et al. 2021d
. However,
this effect is expected to be small for current detectors
Varma et al. 2020
;
Gerosa and Moore 2016
.

Conclusions.
—
We use NR surrogate models for the gravitational waveform and the remnant BH
properties to infer the kick velocity for the binary BH merger GW200129. The
kick magnitude is constrained to
v_{f}\sim 1542^{+747}_{-1098}
km/s or
v_{f}\gtrsim 698
km/s, at 90%
credibility. Given the kick velocity, we estimate that there is at most a

0.48\%
(
7.7\%
) probability that the remnant BH of GW200129 would be
retained by globular (nuclear star) clusters. Finally, we show that the
Doppler effect on the remnant mass is small compared to current measurement
uncertainty; therefore ringdown tests of GR are not expected to be
significantly impacted by the kick for this event.

Observational evidence for kicks has far reaching implications for BH
astrophysics. GW200129 is the first GW event identified as having a large kick
velocity. Large kicks like this have been previously predicted based on the
ensemble kick distribution of merging binary BHs
Varma et al. 2021a
;
Doctor et al. 2021
, and we can expect to see more such events as detector
sensitivity improves. In particular, such observations can help resolve the
mystery of the heavy BHs seen by LIGO-Virgo
Abbott et al. 2020a
;
Abbott et al. 2021a
;
Abbott et al. 2021b
, by constraining the rate of
second-generation mergers.
