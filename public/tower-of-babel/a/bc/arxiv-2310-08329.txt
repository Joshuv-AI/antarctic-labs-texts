Abstract
. It has been more than twenty years since Moshe Newman, based on work by Neil Calkin and Herbert Wilf, introduced an explicit bijection between the rational and natural numbers. Interestingly, this bijection is dynamic in nature. Indeed, Newman’s map has the property that the orbit of zero provides the required bijection. Claudio Bonanno and Stefano Isola, using continued fractions expansions, described the dynamics of its first return time map T T . They proved that it is topologically conjugated to the dyadic odometer. In this article, we prove that the correct numerical system needed to analyze this map is the backward continued fractions. Indeed, this approach has the advantage that it provides explicitly the action of T T on the expansion. As a by-product, we naturally obtain an explicit formula for Minkowski’s question mark function in terms of backward continued fractions. The whole point of Newman was to provide an explicit bijection, our approach shares the same taste for the explicit.

1.
Introduction.

We will essentially tell a
Monthly
tale. Interestingly, in Spanish the word for
counting
and that for
telling
is actually the same,
contar
. In 1873, Cantor established that the rational numbers are countable. This is a remarkable result not only because the natural numbers are strictly contained in the rationals, but because the later are dense in the (uncountable) set of real numbers. Most proofs of this fact do not provide an explicit bijection between
{\mathbb{N}}
and
{\mathbb{Q}}
. In this article we will be interested in an
explicit
bijection that was defined and studied at length in this journal.

The year 2000, in their
Monthly
article
[
CW
]
, Calkin and Wilf constructed a sequence of positive integers,
b(n)
, with the property that every positive rational appears only once as
b(n)/b(n+1)
. The numbers
b(n)/b(n+1)
were easily arranged in a dyadic tree. Moreover, simple rules to obtain the two descendants were given. This tree is now, sometimes, referred to as the Calkin and Wilf tree. In 2003, answering a question of Donald Knuth (
Monthly
Problem 10906), and inspired by the work of Calkin and Wilf, Newman
[
Kn
]
constructed a dynamical system with the property that the orbit of zero induces such a bijection. This means that he defined a function
F:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
with the property that the map
n\mapsto F^{n}(0)
, where
F^{n}(x)=(F\circ\dots\circ F)(x)
denotes the
n-
th iterate of
F
, is the required bijection.

We will study the first return map of the map
F
to the unit interval, which we denote by
T
. It turns out that
T
is nothing but the second iterate of
F
. Moreover, the orbit of zero with respect to
T
induces a bijection between the natural numbers and
{\mathbb{Q}}\cap[0,1)
. Bonnano and Isola
[
BI
]
, in the year 2009, studied this map in detail. They proved that, from a dynamical systems point of view, the map
T
is equivalent (topologically conjugated) to a well known system called
dyadic odometer
. This later system was apparently introduced by John von Neumann and also studied by Shizuo
Kakutani. All of its orbits are dense and it has a unique invariant probability measure. It acts in a similar way as car devices that count the kilometers driven, hence its name. The proof of Bonnano and Isola uses the continued fraction expansion of real numbers.

In this note we propose a proof of the result by Bonnano and Isola that instead of using continued fractions makes use of
backward continued fractions
, see Theorem
5.1
. We believe that this numeration system, studied in detail at least since the 1957 work of Alfred Renyi
[
R
]
, is better suited to study the map
T
. Indeed, the motivation to study the map
T
is that it provides an
explicit
bijection between natural numbers and rationals in the unit interval. Our approach, by means of backward continued fractions, provides an
explicit
action of
T
on this numeration system (see Theorem
2.6
). We hope to satisfy readers with a taste for the explicit.

Roughly speaking, the arguments by Bonanno and Isola relating the odometer with continued fractions expansions, runs through the following lines of thought. The Minkowski Question Mark function, denoted by
?
, conjugates the dynamics of the Tent map with that of the Farey map. Moreover, it turns out that the Gauss map is nothing but the
accelerated
Farey map (or the corresponding
jump transformation
). That is,

\text{Gauss map}\xleftrightarrow{accelerated}\text{Farey map}\xleftrightarrow{?}\text{Tent map}

Recall that the Gauss map is the dynamical system associated to the continued fraction expansion. On the other hand, the Tent map is the dynamical system corresponding to the
alternating binary expansion
(see
[
DKr
, Section 2.3.3]
). This latter expansion associates to each point of the unit interval a sequence of zeroes and ones. The alternating binary expansion can be identified, via the Farey map, with the continued fraction expansion of the point corresponding to
?^{-1}(x)
. In terms of expansions this can be summarized by,

\text{Continued fractions}\xleftrightarrow{accelerated}\text{Farey map}\xleftrightarrow{?}\text{Alternated Binary expansion}

These relations are studied in detail in
[
KMS
]
, see also
[
Is
, Section 2.1]
.
This circle of ideas allows for the proof of the fact that
T
is conjugated, via the Minkowski Question Mark function, to the odometer (as established in
[
BI
]
).

Nevertheless, the odometer is defined on the binary expansion of points in
[0,1]
. We propose an alternative approach, in which the binary expansion occurs naturally. The binary expansion is not directly related to the tent map, but to the doubling map. As above, the Minkowski Question Mark function makes a fundamental appearance. In what follows we will develop the following scheme,

\text{Renyi map}\xleftrightarrow{accelerated}\text{Back. Farey map}\xleftrightarrow{?}\text{Doubling map}

The Renyi map is the dynamical system associated to the backward continued fraction expansion. Thus, in terms of expansions this is,

\text{Backward continued fractions}\xleftrightarrow{accelerated}\text{Back. Farey map}\xleftrightarrow{?}\text{Binary expansion}

Therefore, the backward continued fraction expansion is the natural way to code if we are to conjugate to an odometer. That is why we obtain a simple and explicit formula for the odometric action of
T
.

These two approaches also lead to different expressions for the Minkowski Question Mark function. Indeed, during the 1930s, Arnaud Denjoy
[
D1
,
D2
]
obtained an analytical formula for the Question Mark function in terms of the continued fraction expansion of a number. In this article, we are naturally lead to a similar formula which, instead of continued fractions, describes the Question Mark function in terms of the backward continued fraction expansion, see Proposition
6.4
.

2.
Counting rationals and backward continued fractions.

While it is simple to prove that the rational numbers are countable, explicit bijections between
{\mathbb{N}}
are
{\mathbb{Q}}
are less well known. Newman, solving a problem posed by Knuth
[
Kn
]
, established the following,

2.1
.

The orbit of
x=0
with respect to the map
F:{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\cup\{0\}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
,

F(x)=\frac{1}{2[x]-x+1},

where
[x]
denotes the integer part of
x
, defines a bijection between
{\mathbb{N}}
and
{\mathbb{Q}}^{+}
. That is, the map
n\mapsto F^{n}(0)
is a bijection between the natural and the positive rational numbers.

The proof of this result is based on a construction done by Calkin and Wilf
[
CW
]
in which, using the Euclid tree, they define a sequence
b(n)
so that the map
n\mapsto\frac{b(n)}{b(n+1)}
is a bijection between
{\mathbb{N}}
and
{\mathbb{Q}}^{+}
. A detailed proof of Proposition
2.1
can be found in
[
AZ
, Chapter 17]
and in
[
MSZ
]
.

2.1.
Backward continued fractions.

There is a wide range of different expansions of real numbers each of which captures different arithmetical, algebraic, or dynamical properties. As it will be established in this article, the
natural
expansion associated to the map
F
that enumerates the rational numbers is the backward continued fraction. This expansion is related to the Gauss reduction theory of indefinite integer quadratic forms, when considered in matrix terms as
SL_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})-
equivalent matrices (see
[
Ka
,
Z
]
). Backward continued fraction expansions of real numbers have been extensively studied from the dynamical point of view at least since the 1957 work of Renyi
[
R
]
.

Every real number
x\in[0,1)
can be written as a
backward continued fraction
of the form

x=1-\textrm{ }\cfrac{1}{a_{1}-\cfrac{1}{a_{2}-\cfrac{1}{a_{3}-\dots}}}=\textrm{ }[a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\dots],

where
a_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
a_{i}\geq 2
. Irrational numbers have a unique backward continued fraction expansion which is infinite. Rational numbers,
p/q\in{\mathbb{Q}}
, have two different expansions: a finite one
p/q=[a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}]
and an infinite one of the form
p/q=[a_{1},\dots,a_{n}+1,2,2,\dots]
(that ends with a tail of
2
’s),
[
Ka
, Theorem 1.2]
. In this article, unless we state the contrary, we will always consider the infinite expansion of a rational number. Moreover, any sequence
(a_{i})_{i\geq 1}
with
a_{i}\geq 2
, corresponds to the backward continued fraction of a unique real number in
[0,1)
. We refer to
[
BL
,
Ka
]
for an introduction to backward continued fractions.

In his seminal article
[
R
]
, Renyi introduced the map
R:[0,1)\to[0,1)
defined by

R(x)=\frac{1}{1-x}-\left[\frac{1}{1-x}\right].

(2.1)

We will refer to
R
as the
Renyi map
. The coefficients
a_{i}
of the backward continued fraction of
x
can be obtained by the following recursion:

x=x_{1},\quad x_{n+1}=R(x_{n}),\quad a_{n}=\left[\frac{1}{1-x_{n}}\right]+1.

The Renyi map acts as the shift on the backward continued fraction (see
[
Ka
]
and
[
DK
, Chapter 11]
). That is,

\textrm{ if }x=[a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\dots]\textrm{ then }R(x)=[a_{2},a_{3},\dots].

The map
R
also occurs as a Poincaré map for certain cross sections of the geodesic flow on the modular surface
[
AF
,
Ka
]
. Ergodic properties of this map have been studied, among others, by Roy Adler and Leopold Flatto
[
AF
]
and by Renyi himself
[
R
]
, see also
[
GH
,
Io
]
. This is a map with infinitely many branches and infinite topological entropy. It has a fixed point at zero,
R(0)=0
, which is parabolic, that is
R^{\prime}(0)
=1. Renyi
[
R
]
showed that there exists an infinite
\sigma-
finite invariant measure,
\mu_{R},
absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. It is defined by

\mu_{R}(A)=\int_{A}\dfrac{1}{x}\ dx,

where
A\subset[0,1)
is a Borel set. There is no finite invariant measure absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. The map
R
, restricted to the irrational numbers is topologically conjugated to the full shift on a countable alphabet.

We introduce the following notation
\Sigma_{\geq n}=\{n,n+1,n+2,\dots\}^{{\mathbb{N}}}
. Endow the set
{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
with the discrete topology and
\Sigma_{\geq n}
with the product topology. The shift map
\sigma:\Sigma_{\geq n}\mapsto\Sigma_{\geq n}
is defined by
\sigma(w_{1}w_{2}\dots)=(w_{2}w_{3}\dots)
. Denote by
\pi_{R}:\Sigma_{\geq 2}\mapsto[0,1)
the coding map that to each sequence
\omega=(w_{1}w_{2}\dots)
associates the real number
\pi_{R}(\omega)=[w_{1},w_{2},\dots]
. Let
\Sigma_{\bar{2}}=\{(w_{i})_{i}\in\Sigma_{\geq 2}:\text{there exist }k\in{\mathbb{N}},\text{ such that }w_{i}=2\text{ for }i\geq k\}
.

2.2
.

The map
\pi_{R}:\Sigma_{\geq 2}\mapsto[0,1)
satisfies

R\circ\pi_{R}=\pi_{R}\circ\sigma.

(2.2)

Moreover,
\pi_{R}:\Sigma_{\geq 2}\setminus\Sigma_{\bar{2}}\mapsto[0,1)\setminus{\mathbb{Q}}
is a homeomorphism.

Recall that sequences in
\Sigma_{\bar{2}}
, those having a tail of 2’s, represent rational numbers in the backward continued fraction expansion.

2.2.
First return map of
F
.

In this article we will be interested in the map
T:[0,1)\to[0,1)
which corresponds to the first return time map of
F
to set
[0,1)
.

2.3
.

Let
T:[0,1)\to[0,1)
be the map defined by

T(x)=\frac{1}{2\left[\frac{1}{1-x}\right]+1-\frac{1}{1-x}}.

The map
T
is piecewise continuous in the partition of
[0,1)
given by
\{n/(n+1):n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
, see Figure
1
.

2.4
.

The following observations hold:

(a)

Let
r:[0,1)\to{\mathbb{N}}
be the
first return time
of
F
to
[0,1)
, which is defined by
r(x):=\inf\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:F^{n}(x)\in[0,1)\}
. We have that,
T(x)=F^{r(x)}(x)
.

(b)

F({\mathbb{Q}}\cap[0,1))={\mathbb{Q}}\cap[1,\infty)
and
F({\mathbb{Q}}\cap[1,\infty))={\mathbb{Q}}\cap(0,1)
.

(c)

The previous observation implies that for every
x\in[0,1)
we have
r(x)=2
. Thus,
T(x)=F^{2}(x)
.

(d)

It is a consequence of Proposition
2.1
and the form of
F
that the map
n\mapsto T^{n}(0)
is a bijection between the natural numbers
{\mathbb{N}}
and
{\mathbb{Q}}\cap(0,1)
.

2.3.
The action of
T
on the backward continued fraction.

The following results describe the action of the map
T
on the backward continued fraction of
x\in[0,1)
. Interestingly, it is just the use of backward continued fractions that allows for the proof of such a neat formula for
T
. In later sections we will prove that an action of the type described here corresponds to that of a simple dynamical system called the
odometer
. We stress, however, that we do not require that to obtain an explicit formula for the action of
T
.

2.5
.

If
x\in[0,1)
has a backward continued fraction
x=[a_{1},a_{2},\dots]
then

T(x)=\frac{1}{a_{1}-R(x)},

(2.3)

where
R(x)
is the Renyi map.

Proof.

Since
a_{1}=\left[\frac{1}{1-x}\right]+1
, we have

\displaystyle T(x)=\frac{1}{\left[\frac{1}{1-x}\right]+1-\left(\frac{1}{1-x}-\left[\frac{1}{1-x}\right]\right)}=\frac{1}{a_{1}-R(x)}.

∎

2.6
.

If
x=[a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\dots]\in[0,1)
then

T(x)=[\underbrace{2,2,\dots,2}_{(a_{1}-2)\textrm{-times}},a_{2}+1,a_{3},a_{4},\dots].

Proof.

Recall that if
x=[a_{1},a_{2},\dots]\in[0,1)
then

x=1-\textrm{ }\cfrac{1}{a_{1}-\cfrac{1}{a_{2}-\dots}}=1-\frac{1}{a_{1}-1+R(x)},

(2.4)

since the Renyi map acts as the shift on the backward continued fraction.
The proof will be done by induction on the first digit of the expansion. Let us first consider the case in which
a_{1}=2
. Due to Lemma
2.5
, we have that

\displaystyle T(x)=\frac{1}{2-R(x)}=\frac{1}{1+\frac{1}{a_{2}-1+R^{2}(x)}}=\frac{a_{2}-1+R^{2}(x)}{a_{2}+R^{2}(x)}=1-\frac{1}{a_{2}+R^{2}(x)},

which implies that

T(x)=[a_{2}+1,a_{3},\dots].

Thus, the case
a_{1}=2
is proved. Assume now that for
a_{1}=n\geq 2
we have

T(x)=[\underbrace{2,2,\dots,2}_{(n-2)\textrm{-times}},a_{2}+1,a_{3},a_{4},\dots].

Consider the case in which

a_{1}=n+1
. We have that

\displaystyle T(x)=\frac{1}{a_{1}-R(x)}=1-\frac{1}{\frac{a_{1}-R(x)}{a_{1}-R(x)-1}}=1-\frac{1}{2-\left(1-\frac{1}{a_{1}-1-R(x)}\right)}.

The inductive assumption on
a_{1}-1=n
implies that

\frac{1}{(a_{1}-1)-R(x)}=[\underbrace{2,2,\dots,2}_{(n-2)\textrm{-times}},a_{2}+1,a_{3},a_{4},\dots].

Therefore,

T(x)=[\underbrace{2,2,\dots,2}_{(n-1)\textrm{-times}},a_{2}+1,a_{3},a_{4},\dots].

This concludes the proof.
∎

3.
Binary expansions and the dyadic odometer.

From the topological point of view dynamical systems are classified by topological conjugacies. That is, two dynamical systems,
A:X\to X
and
B:Y\to Y
, share the same (topological) dynamical properties, if there exists a homeomorphism
g:X\to Y
such that
g\circ A\circ g^{-1}=B
. In Section
5
we will prove that the dynamical system
T
is topologically conjugated to a very well understood system called the
odometer
.

An odometer is a device used for measuring the distance traveled by a vehicle. It was traditionally made out of several disks that rotate moving in a unit. Once the numbers
9
passed the disk returned to zero and the rightmost disk moves in a unit. The mathematical odometer version we will be interested in consists of infinitely many disks and has only two digits. Endow the space
\{0,1\}
with the discrete topology and
\Sigma_{2}=\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{N}}}
with the product topology. The
symbolic dyadic odometer
is the map
D:\Sigma_{2}\to\Sigma_{2}
defined by

D((\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{k\textrm{-times}},0,w_{k+2},w_{k+3},\dots))=(\underbrace{0,\dots,0}_{k\textrm{-times}},1,w_{k+2},w_{k+3},\dots),

and
D((1,1,\dots))=(0,0,\dots)
. The map
D
is also known as the von Neumann-Kakutani odometer, adding machine or
2-
odometer. It can also be thought of as the system induced by the addition of
\underline{1}=(1,0,0,\dots)
. This group theoretic point of view allows for several generalizations. From the dynamical systems point of view the odometer is well understood. Among the many known properties of this system we will be interested in the following (see
[
Aa
, pp.25-26]
and
[
B
, Section 4.5.2]
).

3.1
.

The symbolic dyadic odometer satisfies:

(a)

For every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
w\in\Sigma_{2}
we have,

\left\{(D^{k}w)_{1},\dots,(D^{k}w)_{n}):0\leq k\leq 2^{n}-1\right\}=\{0,1\}^{n}.

(b)

The map
D
is minimal, that is, every orbit is dense.

(c)

The map
D
is uniquely ergodic and its unique invariant measure is the
(1/2,1/2)
Bernoulli measure.

(d)

The map
D
has zero topological entropy.

It readily follows from Proposition
3.1
(
a
) that the orbit of
w=(0,0,\dots)
with respect to
D
induces a bijection between the set of natural numbers and the set of all sequences in
\Sigma_{2}
ending with a tail of zeroes. Indeed, it suffices to consider
n\mapsto D^{n}(0,0,\dots)
. This odometer allow us to
count
sequences ending with a tail of zeroes.

The dyadic odometer can be realized in the unit interval. It should come as no surprise that this realization is related to the binary expansion of real numbers. Every
x\in[0,1)
can be written in binary form as

x=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{w_{i}}{2^{i}},

where
w_{i}\in\{0,1\}
. Thus, to every
x\in[0,1)
we can associate a sequence of zeroes and ones,

x=(w_{1},w_{2},w_{3},\dots).

Let
\mathbb{D}=\{x\in[0,1):x=\frac{k}{2^{n}}\text{ for }k\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\},n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
be the set of
dyadic numbers
in
[0,1)
. Points in
[0,1)\setminus\mathbb{D}
have a unique binary representation, while those in
\mathbb{D}
have exactly two representations, one ending with a tail of zeroes and the other with a tail of ones. Unless stated to the contrary, we will always consider the binary expansion ending with a tail of zeroes. In view of this, there is a map

\pi_{2}:[0,1)\to\Sigma_{2}
which assigns to each
x\in[0,1)
the unique sequence
w\in\Sigma_{2}

that corresponds to its binary expansion and does not end with a tail of ones. Moreover, if we denote by

\Sigma_{2}^{*}=\Sigma_{2}\setminus\{w\in\Sigma_{2}:w\text{ ends with a tail of }1^{\prime}s\text{ or of }0^{\prime}s\}
then the map

\pi_{2}:[0,1)\setminus\mathbb{D}\mapsto\Sigma_{2}^{*}

is a homeomorphism.

3.2
.

Let
(I_{n})_{n}
be the partition of the unit interval, where
I_{n}=[\frac{2^{n-1}-1}{2^{n-1}},\frac{2^{n}-1}{2^{n}})
, for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. The
interval realization of the
dyadic odometer
is the interval map
D_{2}:[0,1)\to[0,1)
defined by

D_{2}(x)=x+\frac{3}{2^{n}}-1,

for
x\in I_{n}
.

3.3
.

The map
D:\Sigma_{2}^{*}\to\Sigma_{2}^{*}
is topologically conjugated to
D_{2}:[0,1]\setminus\mathbb{D}\to[0,1)\setminus\mathbb{D}
via the map
\pi_{2}
. That is, if
x\in[0,1]\setminus\mathbb{D}
then

(D\circ\pi_{2})(x)=(\pi_{2}\circ D_{2})(x).

Proof.

Note that if
x\in I_{n}
then its binary expansion starts with
0.\underbrace{1\dots 1}_{n-1}0
. Also,

D_{2}(x)=x-\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}\right)+\frac{1}{2^{n}}=x-(0.\underbrace{1\dots 1}_{n-1})+(0.\underbrace{0\dots 0}_{n-1}1).

The result then follows.
∎

The map
D_{2}
is an infinite interval exchange transformation. From Proposition
3.3
and the properties of
D
the following holds.

3.4
.

The dyadic odometer
D_{2}
has zero topological entropy, it is minimal and uniquely ergodic. It preserves the Lebesgue measure of
[0,1)
.

3.5
.

Note that the orbit of
x=0
with respect to
D_{2}
induces a bijection between the natural numbers and the dyadic numbers.

4.
Accelerated binary expansions and the Linear Renyi map.

There is a natural way to relate to a dynamical system an
accelerated
version of it. This speed up system is sometimes called a
jump transformation
and was studied in detail by Fritz Schweiger
[
Sc
]
. Our starting system is the following map.

4.1
.

The
Doubling map
is the function
B:[0,1]\to[0,1]
defined by

\displaystyle B(x)=\begin{cases}2x&\text{ if }x\in[0,1/2)\\
2x-1&\text{ if }x\in[1/2,1].\end{cases}

This system is closely related to the binary expansion. Indeed, the map
B
acts as the shift in the binary expansion of a number in
[0,1]
.

Consider now the first hitting time map in
[0,1/2)
. This function defined away from the pre-images of
x=0
by

\tau_{B}(x):=1+\min\left\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}:B^{n}(x)\in\left[0,\frac{1}{2}\right)\right\}.

For every
x\in[0,1]
, not in the boundary of the partition defined by
(I_{n})_{n}
, we define the
linear Renyi map
(or the jump transformation for
B
) by

R_{2}(x)=B^{\tau_{B}(x)}(x).

The map
R_{2}
is piecewise linear and full-branched in the partition
(I_{n})_{n}
. It has infinite topological entropy and preserves the Lebesgue measure. It will be convenient to describe symbolically the action of
R_{2}
. For every integer
k\geq 0
we define the block

\displaystyle b_{k}=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }k=0,\\
\underbrace{1\dots 1}_{k\textrm{-times}}0&\text{ if }k\geq 1.\end{cases}

4.2
.

The binary expansion of every real number
x\in[0,1)
can be written in a unique way as an infinite concatenation of blocks
b_{k}
’s,

x=0.b_{k_{1}}b_{k_{2}}b_{k_{3}}\dots

For example,

0.\underbrace{0}_{k_{1}=0}\underbrace{0}_{k_{2}=0}\underbrace{1110}_{k_{3}=3}\underbrace{10}_{k_{4}=1}\underbrace{111110}_{k_{5}=5}\underbrace{10}_{k_{6}=1}\dots=0.b_{0}b_{0}b_{3}b_{1}b_{5}b_{1}\dots

For
x\geq 1
we define
[x]_{2}:=2^{[\log_{2}x]}
.

4.3
.

If
x=0.b_{k_{1}}b_{k_{2}}\dots\in[0,1)
then

\left[\frac{1}{1-x}\right]_{2}=2^{k_{1}}.

Proof.

For
x\in[0,1)
consider its binary representation
x=0.w_{1}w_{2}w_{3}\dots\
. With the notation
\bar{w}=1-w
, we obtain that

1-x=0.\bar{w}_{1}\bar{w}_{2}\bar{w}_{3}\dots

Define the blocks

\bar{b}_{k}=\underbrace{0\dots 0}_{k-\textrm{times}}1.

We thus have
1-x=0.\bar{b}_{k_{1}}\bar{b}_{k_{2}}\bar{b}_{k_{3}}\dots
. Setting,
r=0.b_{k_{2}}b_{k_{3}}\dots\in[0,1)

6.
Minkowski’s Question Mark function.

Continuous, non-decreasing functions mapping the unit interval onto itself and yet singular, that is, having derivative equal to zero Lebesgue almost everywhere, were constructed already in 1884 by Georg Cantor
[
C
]
. In 1904, Hermann Minkowski
[
M
]
introduced a function of the same type but with the remarkable property of being
strictly increasing
. The function
?:[0,1]\to[0,1]
, called
Question Mark function
can be defined inductively on the rational numbers in
[0,1]
by
?(0)=0
,
?(1)=1
and

?\left(\frac{p+p^{\prime}}{q+q^{\prime}}\right)=\frac{?(p/q)+?(p^{\prime}/q^{\prime})}{2},

whenever
p/q
and
p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}
are rational numbers in lowest terms with
p^{\prime}q-pq^{\prime}=1
. The definition for irrationals in
[0,1]
follows by continuity. Salem
[
S
]
proved that the question mark function is actually Hölder and conjectured that its Fourier-Stieltjes coefficients decay to zero at infinity with a polynomial rate. This was recently established by Thomas Jordan and Tuomas Sahlsten
[
JS
]
. Denjoy
[
D1
,
D2
]
, who actually proved the singularity of the function
?
, also obtained an explicit formula for it. In order to explain this, we briefly recall the definition and basic properties of the classical
continued fraction
expansion. Every real number
x\in(0,1)
can be written as a continued fraction of the form

x=\textrm{ }\cfrac{1}{a_{1}+\cfrac{1}{a_{2}+\cfrac{1}{a_{3}+\dots}}}=\textrm{ }[a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\dots]_{C},

where
a_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
. If
x\in(0,1)
is irrational then the continued fraction expansion is unique and it has infinitely many terms
[
HW
, Theorem 170]
. Rational numbers have two different expansions, both of them finite and one of them having last digit equal to
1
. As is the case of the backward continued fraction, there exits a dynamical system closely related to the continued fraction expansion. The
Gauss
map
G:(0,1]\to(0,1]
, is the interval map defined by

G(x)=\frac{1}{x}-\left[\frac{1}{x}\right].

The Gauss map acts as the shift map on the continued fraction expansion,

a_{n}=\left[\frac{1}{G^{n-1}(x)}\right].

We refer to Khinchine’s well known 1935 monograph on continued fractions
[
kh
]
for further properties. We can now state Denjoy’s result.

6.1
Denjoy.

If
x=[a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\dots]_{C}
is an irrational number in
[0,1]
then

?(x)=\frac{1}{2^{a_{1}-1}}-\frac{1}{2^{a_{1}+a_{2}-1}}+\frac{1}{2^{a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}-1}}-\dots=2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2^{a_{1}+a_{2}+\dots+a_{n}}}.

The following functional properties of the question mark function hold.

6.2
.

For every
x\in[0,1]
we have that

\displaystyle?\left(\frac{x}{x+1}\right)=\frac{?(x)}{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad?(1-x)=1-?(x).

A remarkable property of the Question Mark function, and one of the reasons for Minkowski to define it, is that it gives a bijection between quadratic irrationals of the unit interval and rationals in the same interval. Moreover, it provides a bijection between rational and dyadic numbers. This later property is, of course, of interest to us since we have studied a bijection between the natural numbers and the rational ones that passes through the dyadic numbers. The relation with the Question Mark function is not, as we will see below, a coincidence. In order to explain this we require the following result that relates the backward with the continued fraction expansions. This corresponds to
[
Ka
, Exercise 10. p.128]
.

6.3
.

If
x\in[0,1)
is an irrational number with infinite continued fraction expansion
x=[m_{1},m_{2},m_{3},\dots]_{C}
. Then, the backward continued fraction of
x
is

x=[\ \underbrace{2,\dots,2}_{m_{1}-1},\ (m_{2}+2),\ \underbrace{2,\dots,2}_{m_{3}-1},\ (m_{4}+2),\ \dots].

(6.1)

Proof.

Denote by
G^{2}(x)_{BCF}
the backward continued fraction of the second iterate of the Gauss map,
G^{2}(x)
. It suffices to show that the backward continued fraction of
x
verifies

x=[\ \underbrace{2,\dots,2}_{m_{1}-1},\ (m_{2}+2),\ G^{2}(x)_{BCF}].

We will prove this inductively on
m_{1}
. The following direct, but useful, identity will be crucial in our reasoning,

\frac{1}{1+\frac{1}{t}}=1-\frac{1}{t+1}.

Assume
m_{1}=1
. Then

\displaystyle[1,m_{2},m_{3},\dots]_{C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\frac{1}{m_{2}+G^{2}(x)}}=1-\frac{1}{m_{2}+1+G^{2}(x)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1}{m_{2}+2+G^{2}(x)-1}=[(m_{2}+2)\ G^{2}(x)_{BCF}].

Assume that (
6.1
) holds for
m_{1}\geq 1
. Then,

\displaystyle[(m_{1}+1),\ m_{2},m_{3},\dots]_{C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+m_{1}+\frac{1}{m_{2}+G^{2}(x)}}=\frac{1}{1+\frac{1}{\left(m_{1}+\frac{1}{m_{2}+G^{2}(x)}\right)^{-1}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1}{1+\left(m_{1}+\frac{1}{m_{2}+G^{2}(x)}\right)^{-1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1}{2+\frac{1}{\left(m_{1}+\frac{1}{m_{2}+G^{2}(x)}\right)}-1}=[2,\ [m_{1},m_{2},\dots]].

∎

As seen in Proposition
6.1
, Denjoy obtained an analytical formula for the question mark function associated to the continued fraction expansion. We now obtain the corresponding formula for the backward continued fraction.

6.4
.

If
x\in[0,1)
has backward continued fraction expansion
x=[a_{1},a_{2},\dots]
then

\displaystyle?(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{-j+a_{1}+a_{2}\dots+a_{j}}},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.b_{a_{1}-2}b_{a_{2}-2}\dots

Proof.

For
x=[a_{1},a_{2},\dots]
let

\hat{?}[a_{1},a_{2},\dots]=0.b_{a_{1}-2}b_{a_{2}-2}\dots.

Let
x=[m_{1},m_{2},\dots]_{C}
be an irrational real number
x\in[0,1)
. By Lemma
6.3
the backward continued fraction of
x
equals

x=[\underbrace{2,\dots,2}_{m_{1}-1},\ (m_{2}+2),\ \underbrace{2,\dots,2}_{m_{3}-1},\ (m_{4}+2),\ \dots].

Hence

\displaystyle\hat{?}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.\underbrace{b_{0}\dots b_{0}}_{m_{1}-1}b_{m_{2}}\underbrace{b_{0}\dots b_{0}}_{m_{3}-1}b_{m_{4}}\underbrace{b_{0}\dots b_{0}}_{m_{5}-1}\dots

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.\underbrace{0\dots 0}_{m_{1}-1}\underbrace{1\dots 1}_{m_{2}}0\underbrace{0\dots 0}_{m_{3}-1}\underbrace{1\dots 1}_{m_{4}}0\underbrace{0\dots 0}_{m_{5}-1}\dots

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{0.0\dots 0}_{m_{1}}\underbrace{1\dots 1}_{m_{2}}\underbrace{0\dots 0}_{m_{3}}\underbrace{1\dots 1}_{m_{4}}\underbrace{0\dots 0}_{m_{5}}\dots.

The above expression corresponds to the classic Question Mark function
?
for a real number written in continued fraction expansion, and hence
\hat{?}=?
on irrationals. The conclusion follows from continuity.
∎

7.
Backward Farey Map.

As indicated in the Introduction, the backward continued fraction expansion is naturally related with the binary expansion. This relation emerges from the following scheme,

\text{Renyi map}\xleftrightarrow{accelerated}\text{Backward Farey map}\xleftrightarrow{?}\text{Doubling map}

In this section we provide details to explain it.

7.1
.

The
Backward Farey map
is the function
J:[0,1]\to[0,1]
defined by

\displaystyle J(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{x}{1-x}&\text{ if }x\in[0,1/2)\\
\frac{2x-1}{x}&\text{ if }x\in[1/2,1].\end{cases}

7.2
.

This map has two
increasing
branches. The derivatives at
x=0
and at
x=1
are equal to
1
. If
G
denotes the Gauss map, then for
x\in[1/2,1]
we have that
J(x)=1-G(x)
. With a different purpose, this map was also studied by Bonanno and Isola
[
BI
]
.

7.3
.

The Backward Farey map is topologically conjugated to the doubling map via the Question Mark function. That is, for every
x\in[0,1]
we have

(B\circ?)(x)=(?\circ J)(x).

Proof.

Let us consider first the case in which
x\in[0,1/2)
. Note that the function
\frac{x}{1-x}
is the inverse of
\frac{x}{x+1}
. Thus, by Lemma
6.2
we obtain,

\displaystyle?(x)=?\left(\frac{\frac{x}{1-x}}{\frac{x}{1-x}+1}\right)=\frac{1}{2}?\left(\frac{x}{1-x}\right).

Therefore,

(B\circ?)(x)=2?(x)=?\left(\frac{x}{1-x}\right)=(?\circ J)(x).

Assume now that
x\in[1/2,1]
. In this case the continued fraction expansion of the point has the form
x=[1,a_{2},a_{3},\dots]_{C}
. By Dejoy’s formula for
?
we have,

\displaystyle?(x)=2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2^{1+a_{2}+a_{3}+\dots a_{n}}}=2\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2^{1+a_{1}}}+\frac{1}{2^{1+a_{1}+a_{2}}}-\dots\right)=

(7.1)

\displaystyle 2\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{a_{1}}}+\frac{1}{2^{a_{1}+a_{2}}}-\dots\right)\right)=1+\left(-\frac{1}{2^{a_{1}}}+\frac{1}{2^{a_{1}+a_{2}}}-\dots\right).

(7.2)

From (
7.1
) we have that:

2?(x)=2-?(G(x)).

In particular,

2?(x)-1=1-?(G(x)).

(7.3)

Note now that, for
x\in[1/2,1]
we have that
J(x)=1-G(x)
. Therefore, by Lemma
6.2
,

(?\circ J)(x)=?(1-G(x))=1-?(G(x)).

(7.4)

Combining equations (
7.3
) and (
7.4
) we have that if
x\in[1/2,1]
then

(B\circ?)(x)=(?\circ J)(x).

∎

It is well known, see for example
[
Is
, Section 2.1]
, that the Gauss map is an accelerated version of the Farey map. We now prove an analogous result for the Renyi map. We first consider the following partition of the unit interval. For every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let

P_{n}=\left[\frac{n-1}{n},\frac{n}{n+1}\right).

7.4
.

For every
n\geq 1
we have that
J(P_{n+1})=P_{n}
. Indeed,

J\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)=1-G\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)=1-\left(\frac{n+1}{n}-\left[\frac{n+1}{n}\right]\right)=\frac{n-1}{n}.

Consider now the first hitting time to
P_{1}
. This function is well defined away from the pre-images of
1
and is given by,

\tau(x):=1+\min\left\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}:J^{n}(x)\in P_{1}\right\}.

Note that if
x\in P_{n}
then
\tau(x)=n
, hence

\tau(x)=\left[\frac{1}{1-x}\right].

We have:

7.5
.

For every
x\in[0,1]
, not in the boundary of the partition defined by
(P_{n})_{n}
, we have

R(x)=J^{\tau(x)}(x).

Proof.

If
x\in P_{n}
then
\tau(x)=n
. Hence
J^{\tau(x)}(x)=J^{n}(x)=J\circ J^{n-1}(x)
.
Recall that the Renyi map has the following form:

R|_{P_{n}}(x)=\frac{nx-(n-1)}{1-x}.

On the other hand, if we denote the second branch of the backward Farey map by
\tilde{G}(x)=\frac{2x-1}{x}
, then

\tilde{G}^{n}(x)=\frac{(n+1)x-n}{nx-(n-1)}.

Thus, since the Renyi map and the map
J
coincide in
[0,1/2]
we have

\displaystyle J^{\tau(x)}(x)=J^{n}(x)=J\circ J^{n-1}(x)=R\left(\frac{nx-(n-1)}{(n-1)x-(n-2)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\frac{nx-(n-1)}{(n-1)x-(n-2)}}-1=\frac{nx-(n-1)}{1-x}.

∎
