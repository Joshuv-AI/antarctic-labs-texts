Berry curvature associated to Fermi arcs in continuum and lattice Weyl systems
Dennis Wawrzik, Jeroen van den Brink

Abstract
Recently it has been discovered that in Weyl semimetals the surface state Berry curvature can diverge in certain regions of momentum. This occurs in a continuum description of tilted Weyl cones, which for a slab geometry results in the Berry curvature dipole associated to the surface Fermi arcs growing linearly with slab thickness. Here we investigate analytically incarnations of lattice Weyl semimetals and demonstrate this diverging surface Berry curvature by solving for their surface states and connect these to their continuum descriptions. We show how the shape of the Fermi arc and the Berry curvature hot-line is determined and confirm the 1 / k 2 1/k^{2} divergence of the Berry curvature at the end of the Fermi arc as well as the finite size effects for the Berry curvature and its dipole, using finite slab calculations and surface Green’s function methods. We further establish that apart from affecting the second order, non-linear Hall effect, the divergent Berry curvature has a strong impact on other transport phenomena as the Magnus-Hall effect and the non-linear chiral anomaly.



I
Introduction

The Berry curvature associated to electronic band-like states is a fundamental quantity that gives rise to an anomalous electronic velocity and interesting (non-linear) electronic, heat and spin-transport properties
Karplus and Luttinger 1954
;
Culcer et al. 2004
;
Xiao et al. 2006
;
Xiao et al. 2010
;
Moore and Orenstein 2010
;
Sodemann and Fu 2015
;
Zhang et al. 2018
;
You et al. 2018
;
Yu et al. 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2021
. Recently it has been shown that electronic states at surface can have BC properties very different from bulk. For instance bulk BC-free systems exhibit a finite surface BC at certain surfaces, in particular ones of lower symmetry
Wawrzik et al. 2022
and surface Fermi arcs in Weyl semimetals have been shown to generically be accompanied by a surface Berry curvature that may diverge close to a hot line in the surface Brillouin zone connecting the projection of Weyl nodes with opposite chirality
Wawrzik et al. 2021
. In Weyl semi-metals such surface Berry curvature appears whenever a bulk node has a velocity tilt toward the surface of interest
Wawrzik et al. 2021
;
Ovalle et al. 2022
.

Here we consider such Weyl semimetals and the BC associated to their surface Fermi arcs in detail, in particular in the intrinsically regularized lattice setting with an even number of Weyl nodes. We first consider analytically a semi-infinite system –with a single surface– both on the lattice and in the continuum. We show that the BC of the Fermi arc diverges quadratically along a hot-line in reciprocal space in both cases. We show how the free parameter that appears in the boundary condition of the self-adjoint extension in the continuum setting is regularized and becomes fixed by the lattice. Based on this insight we provide a time reversal invariant lattice Hamiltonian that allows tuning of this parameter, which strongly affects the Fermi arcs and their connectivity.

Subsequently we consider a finite size slab of size
L
with both a bottom and top surface and show how the hybridization of the top and bottom Fermi arc and the size dependence of the BC dipole
D\propto L
on the lattice confirm continuum expectations. Furthermore, we investigate the surface BC using the surface Green’s function of the lattice model. Finally we consider Berry curvature mediated transport phenomena and show that the divergent surface BC has a significant influence on several phenomena like the Magnus Hall effect, second order Hall/Nernst effects, and non-linear chiral anomaly.

II
Berry curvature of surface states

To illustrate how the Berry curvature of surface states generally differs from that of bulk states, it is instructive to consider first a generic
Ansatz
for the wave function of a surface state
\Psi\left(z,{\bm{k}_{\parallel}}\right)
where
z<0
denotes the real space coordinate along the surface normal and
{\bm{k}_{\parallel}}
the momentum parallel to the surface
Wawrzik et al. 2022
:

\displaystyle\Psi\left(z,{\bm{k}_{\parallel}}\right)=c({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})\penalty\ f(z)e^{\lambda({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})z}\psi({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})

(1)

The surface state decays exponentially into the bulk where
\lambda({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})
is complex with Re
(\lambda)>0
and might be modulated by a general function
f(z)
. Without loss of generality we assume that the spinor part of the surface state
\psi^{\dagger}\psi=1
is normalized which leads to the normalization constant

\displaystyle c^{-2}=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\textup{d}z\penalty\ |f|^{2}e^{2\text{Re}(\lambda)z}.

(2)

The Berry connection
\bm{A}=-i\int_{-\infty}^{0}\penalty\ \Psi^{\dagger}\nabla\Psi
of the surface state becomes

\displaystyle\bm{A}

\displaystyle=\bm{A}^{\psi}-i\left[\frac{\nabla c}{c}+\bar{z}\nabla\lambda\right]

(3)

where
\bm{A}^{\psi}=-i\psi^{\dagger}\nabla\psi
is the expected Berry connection of the spinor
\psi({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})
and
\bar{z}({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})
the expectation value of
z
at a given momentum
{\bm{k}_{\parallel}}
:

\displaystyle\bar{z}({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})

\displaystyle=c^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\textup{d}z\penalty\ z\penalty\ |f|^{2}e^{2\text{Re}(\lambda)z}

(4)

While
\nabla c/c=\nabla\log(c)
is a pure gradient and vanishes in the Berry connection
\Omega=\nabla\times\bm{A}
, the last term in Eq. (
3
) depending on
\lambda
does not. This means, that the BC
\Omega=\Omega^{\psi}+\Omega^{\lambda}
of a surface state does not only depend on the BC of the spinor
\psi
but has, in contrast to bulk states, a contribution
\Omega^{\lambda}
originating from the momentum dependence of the localization of the surface state. More precisely, we have

\displaystyle\Omega^{\lambda}=2\text{Re}(\nabla\lambda)\times\text{Im}(\nabla\lambda)\penalty\ (\bar{z^{2}}-\bar{z}^{2})

(5)

where
\bar{z^{2}}
is defined analogous to Eq. (
4
). The BC is proportional to the variance
\bar{z^{2}}-\bar{z}^{2}
and at the transition from a surface to a bulk state the state will spread through the whole crystal, i.e. Re
(\lambda)\rightarrow 0
, and the BC diverges. This divergence can be seen more clearly in the example
f(z)=z^{n}
where
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Here the normalization constant is

\displaystyle c=2^{n}\sqrt{\frac{\left(\text{Re}(\lambda)\right)^{2n+1}}{n\penalty\ (2n-1)!}}

(6)

and
c=\sqrt{2\text{Re}(\lambda)}
for
n=0
. From this we can calculate the Berry connection
A^{\lambda}=i(2n+1)\nabla\lambda/\text{Re}(\lambda)
and the corresponding BC is

\displaystyle\Omega^{\lambda}=\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\penalty\ \frac{\text{Re}(\nabla\lambda)\times\text{Im}(\nabla\lambda)}{(\text{Re}(\lambda))^{2}}.

(7)

From this expression it is clear that the BC associated to the surface state may diverges at the line for which Re
(\lambda)=0
. As we will see later on, precisely this is case for Fermi arcs in tilted Weyl semimetals with
n=0
and
\lambda\propto{\bm{k}_{\parallel}}
: the end points of a Fermi arc always hit the BC hot-line such that the BC diverges quadratically
\Omega\propto{\bm{k}_{\parallel}}^{-2}
.

III
Surfaces of semi-infinite Weyl systems

The linear dispersion around a Weyl node has the remarkable consequence that on the surface a topological protected Fermi arc is induced, which connects the surface projections of two Weyl nodes of opposite chirality
Wan et al. 2011
;
Armitage et al. 2018
. Before investigating these surface states and their Berry curvature in a lattice setting, we will first consider the simpler case of a continuum description for a single tilted Weyl node and its associated surface Berry curvature.

III.1
Single tilted Weyl cone in continuum

Even if in condensed matter settings Weyl cones come in pairs of opposite chirality
Nielsen and Ninomiya 1981a
;
Nielsen and Ninomiya 1981b
, one may consider a low energy model of a single general, tilted Weyl cone with chirality
\chi=\pm 1
given by the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H=-i\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{r}}\cdot\left(\chi V\boldsymbol{\sigma}+\bm{u}\sigma_{0}\right)+\mu\sigma_{0}

(8)

where
\sigma_{i}
are the Pauli matrices,
\bm{u}
the tilting vector,
\mu
the energy of the node, and
V=V^{T}
a symmetric matrix with
\det(V)>0
allowing for an anisotropic velocity of the Weyl cone. We restrict our model to a type-I Weyl cone where
V^{2}-\bm{u}\bm{u}^{T}
is positive definite. Furthermore, it is convenient to simplify the model by setting
V
to the identity matrix,
\mu=0
, and
\bm{u}=(0,0,u_{z})
and restore everything later on. Without loss of generality we choose the surface to be at
z=0
and restrict the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
8
) to the lower half space
z<0
. Therefore, we split every vector in a parallel and perpendicular part with respect to the surface, i.e.
\bm{u}=\bm{u}_{\parallel}+u_{z}\hat{z}
.

In this setup, hermiticity
\left<\psi,H\psi\right>=\left<H\psi,\psi\right>
requires any surface state
\psi
to have
\psi^{\dagger}\left(\chi\sigma_{z}+u_{z}\right)\psi|_{z=0}=0
at the surface, i.e. the
z
-component of the pseudo-spin expectation value
s_{z}=-\chi u_{z}
compensates for the tilt such that there is no net current into the vacuum. Since there is no further constraint on
\bm{s}_{\parallel}
besides
|\bm{s}|=1
it can be parametrized by a real number
\alpha
(see also Section
III.3
) as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{s}

\displaystyle=\left<\psi_{\alpha}|\boldsymbol{\sigma}|\psi_{\alpha}\right>

\displaystyle=\left(\sqrt{1-u_{z}^{2}}\cos(\alpha),\sqrt{1-u_{z}^{2}}\sin(\alpha),-\chi u_{z}\right)

(9)

With this we are able to make an ansatz for a wave function
\Psi
that decays exponentially away from the surface with inverse decay length
\lambda
similar to Eq. (
1
):

\displaystyle\Psi\left(\bm{k_{\parallel}},z\right)=c\left({\bm{k}_{\parallel}}\right)e^{i{\bm{k}_{\parallel}}\cdot\bm{r}_{\parallel}+\lambda\left({\bm{k}_{\parallel}}\right)z}\psi_{\alpha}

(10)

Here,
\psi_{\alpha}
is the spinor fulfilling Eq. (
III.1
) so that the boundary condition is always satisfied. The normalization constant
c
is determined by
1=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\text{d}z\left|\Psi\right|^{2}
which gives
c^{2}=2
Re
(\lambda)
and Re
(\lambda)>0
. Before we can get the actual value of
\lambda
we first have to calculate the energy of the surface state

\displaystyle E({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})

\displaystyle=\left<\Psi|H|\Psi\right>

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{k}_{\parallel}-i\lambda\hat{z}\right)\cdot\left(\chi\bm{s}+u_{z}\hat{z}\right)

\displaystyle=\chi{\bm{k}_{\parallel}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\parallel}

(11)

where the part proportional to
-i\lambda
vanishes because of the boundary condition
s_{z}=-\chi u_{z}
.
For
E=0
this energy dispersion gives rise to a Fermi arc perpendicular to
\bm{s}_{\parallel}
, the exact direction will be fixed by the requirement Re
(\lambda)>0
. In order to find a solution for
\lambda({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})
it is convenient to multiply the Hamiltonian by
\sigma_{z}
, i.e. the equation
-\chi u_{z}E({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})=\left<\Psi\right|H\sigma_{z}\left|\Psi\right>
yields:

\displaystyle\lambda=-\frac{{\bm{k}_{\parallel}}\cdot\left(\hat{z}\times\bm{s}+i\chi u_{z}\bm{s}\right)}{1-u_{z}^{2}}

(12)

Now we have the full wave function and can calculate the BC
\Omega_{z}({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})=\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{{\bm{k}_{\parallel}}}\times\bm{A}_{\parallel}
of the surface state with Berry connection

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{\parallel}({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})=-i\int_{-\infty}^{0}\text{d}z\penalty\ \Psi^{\dagger}\left(\bm{k_{\parallel}},z\right)\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{{\bm{k}_{\parallel}}}\Psi\left(\bm{k_{\parallel}},z\right)

(13)

to be:

\displaystyle\Omega_{z}({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{Im}(\bm{\nabla}\lambda)\times\text{Re}(\bm{\nabla}\lambda)}{2\text{Re}^{2}(\lambda)}

(14)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\chi u_{z}\left(1-u_{z}^{2}\right)}{2\left({\bm{k}_{\parallel}}\cdot\left(\hat{z}\times\bm{s}\right)\right)^{2}}\hat{z}

(15)

The resulting BC is proportional to the tilt towards the surface and diverges at the BC hot-line where
{\bm{k}_{\parallel}}\propto\bm{s}_{\parallel}
.

Having the results of the simplified model, we can add the terms we skipped. The terms depending on
\bm{u}_{\parallel}
and
\mu
are proportional to
\sigma_{0}
and thus do not affect
\Psi
and
\lambda
but directly add to the energy
E({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})
. The easiest way to include the velocity matrix is to shift
V
in the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
8
) to the derivative which is basically done by substituting
({\bm{k}_{\parallel}},\hat{z})\rightarrow(V{\bm{k}_{\parallel}},V\hat{z})
and
\bm{u}\rightarrow V^{-1}\bm{u}
. The boundary condition then becomes
\bm{s}\cdot(V\hat{z})=-\chi u_{z}
and

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=\chi(V{\bm{k}_{\parallel}})\cdot\bm{s}_{\parallel}+{\bm{k}_{\parallel}}\cdot\bm{u}_{\parallel}+\mu

(16)

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=(V{\bm{k}_{\parallel}})\cdot\frac{\bm{s}\times V\hat{z}-i\left(V\hat{z}+\chi u_{z}\bm{s}\right)}{|V\hat{z}|^{2}-u_{z}^{2}}

(17)

yielding

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}=-\frac{\left(\left|V\hat{z}\right|^{2}-u_{z}^{2}\right)\left(V^{-1}\bm{s}\cdot\hat{z}\right)}{2\det(V)\left({\bm{k}_{\parallel}}\cdot\left(V^{-1}\bm{s}\times\hat{z}\right)\right)^{2}}\hat{z}.

(18)

For anisotropic Weyl cones it is possible that no principal axis of
V
is pointing along
\hat{z}
such that
(V{\bm{k}_{\parallel}})\cdot(V\hat{z})
is finite. Thus, we can have Im
(\bm{\nabla}\lambda)\neq 0
even if
u_{z}=0
and, according to Eq. (
14
), find BC without any tilt.

III.2
Lattice Weyl systems

As states above, according to the Nielsen-Ninomiya theorem
Nielsen and Ninomiya 1981a
;
Nielsen and Ninomiya 1981b
Weyl nodes in crystals always come in pairs with opposite chirality, which implies that our lattice model must have at least two nodes. Similar to Ref.
McCormick et al. 2017a
, we may define a tilted Weyl cone pair Hamiltonian
H_{\text{L}}(\bm{k})=H_{\text{cone}}+H_{\text{tilt}}+\mu\sigma_{0}
on a cubic lattice with lattice constant
a=1
as follows:

\displaystyle H_{\text{cone}}=

\displaystyle\chi\sin(k_{y})\left(V\bm{\sigma}\right)_{y}+\chi\sin(k_{z})\left(V\bm{\sigma}\right)_{z}+\left[\chi\left(\cot(k_{0})-\frac{\cos(k_{x})}{\sin(k_{0})}\right)+\Delta\left(2-\cos(k_{y})-\cos(k_{z})\right)\right]\left(V\bm{\sigma}\right)_{x}

(19)

\displaystyle H_{\text{tilt}}=

\displaystyle\left[u_{y}\sin(k_{y})+u_{z}\sin(k_{z})+\vphantom{\frac{1}{1}}u_{x}\left(\cot(k_{0})-\frac{\cos(k_{x})}{\sin(k_{0})}\right)\right]\sigma_{0}

(20)

This model has Weyl cones at
(\pm k_{0},0,0)
with chirality
\pm\chi
. The cone at
k_{x}=+k_{0}
has exactly the dispersion described by the
k\cdot p
model in Eq. (
8
) while the other one is related by a mirror symmetry
M_{x}
. Again, for simplicity we take
V=\mathbb{1}
,
k_{0}=\pi/2
,
\chi=\Delta=1
, and
u_{x}=u_{y}=0
. It is worth to notice that, compared to the continuum
k\cdot p
model, we have an extra term
\Delta\left(2-\cos(k_{y})-\cos(k_{z})\right)\sigma_{x}
. It vanishes at the Weyl nodes but is needed to open a gap of size
4\Delta
at
k_{y}=\pi
and
k_{z}=\pi
planes. As in the previous section, we construct a surface by constraining the Hamiltonian to
n\leq 0
where
n
labels the lattice sites in
z
-direction. In the vacuum
n>0
no hoppings are allowed. We can keep the Fourier transform in
{\bm{k}_{\parallel}}
-space parallel to the surface but have to use the real space hoppings perpendicular to it:

\displaystyle H({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{0}\bm{c}^{\dagger}_{n}h_{\parallel}({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})\bm{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{n}

(21)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{0}\left(\bm{c}^{\dagger}_{n}t\bm{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{n+1}+\bm{c}^{\dagger}_{n}t^{\dagger}\bm{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{n-1}\right)

where

\displaystyle h_{\parallel}({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})

\displaystyle=\left(2-\cos(k_{x})-\cos(k_{y})\right)\sigma_{x}+\sin(k_{y})\sigma_{y}

(22)

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\sigma_{x}+i\left(\sigma_{z}+u_{z}\sigma_{0}\right)\right)

(23)

and
\bm{c}^{\dagger}_{n}
is the creation operator for a particle in the
n
th layer with momentum
{\bm{k}_{\parallel}}
.

Now the solution of the surface state
\bm{\Psi}({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})
in this semi-infinite slab lattice model can be derived in a similar manner as of the continuum model. For any state
\bm{\psi}=\left(\dots,\psi_{n-1},\psi_{n},\psi_{n+1},\dots\right)
a hermitian Hamiltonian has to fulfil

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{\psi}|H\bm{\psi}\rangle-\langle H\bm{\psi}|\bm{\psi}\rangle

(24)

\displaystyle=2\,\text{Im}\left(\psi_{0}^{\dagger}t\psi^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{1}\right)

(25)

where all terms cancel except the ones at the boundary. If we assume that, as in the continuum case, the normalized 2-spinor
\psi_{n}\equiv\psi
is independent of
z
or rather
n
we arrive at the known boundary condition

\displaystyle\psi^{\dagger}\left(t-t^{\dagger}\right)\psi=0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad s_{z}=\psi^{\dagger}\sigma_{z}\psi=-u_{z}.

In addition, there should be effectively no current into vacuum, i.e.
\psi^{\dagger}t\psi=0
. This way, the additional
\Delta
term in Eq. (
19
) yields
s_{x}=0
and the previously free parameter
\alpha
is fixed to be
\alpha=\pm\pi/2
depending on the sign of
\Delta
. This is expected because the lattice Hamiltonian is a bounded operator, i.e. there exists an
E_{0}>0
such that
|H\Psi|\leq E_{0}|\Psi|
for all
\Psi
, and therefore does not need an extension.

As in Eq. (
10
) we make an ansatz for the surface wave function
\bm{\Psi}=\left(\dots,\Psi_{-2},\Psi_{-1},\Psi_{0},0,\dots\right)
which decays exponentially into the bulk and is proportional to the above
\psi
:

\displaystyle\Psi_{n}({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})=c({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})\penalty\ r^{-n}({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})\,\psi

(26)

with
n\leq 0
and
|r|<1
. We find
c^{2}=1-|r|^{2}
and

\displaystyle\left(H\bm{\Psi}\right)_{n}

\displaystyle=h_{\parallel}\Psi_{n}+t\Psi_{n+1}+t^{\dagger}\Psi_{n-1}

(27)

\displaystyle=\left(h_{\parallel}+rt+r^{-1}t^{\dagger}\right)\Psi_{n}

(28)

As in the continuum case we can use Schrödinger’s equation and
-u_{z}E=\Psi^{\dagger}_{n}H_{\text{eff}}\sigma_{z}\Psi^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{n}
to solve for energy
E({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})
and the complex number
r({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})
. We get

\displaystyle E({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})

\displaystyle=(-\sqrt{1-u_{z}^{2}}+u_{y})\penalty\ \sin(k_{y})+u_{x}\sin(k_{x})

(29)

\displaystyle r({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})

\displaystyle=\frac{z+\sqrt{z^{2}-u_{z}^{2}}}{1+\sqrt{1-u_{z}^{2}}}

(30)

where we restored
u_{x}
and
u_{y}
, and

\displaystyle z=2-\cos(k_{x})-\cos(k_{y})+iu_{z}\sin(k_{y}).

(31)

At the Weyl nodes
k_{x}=\pm\pi/2
and
k_{y}=0
we obtain
r=z=1
, i.e. the state becomes a surface state as expected. Without a tilt Eq. (
30
) simplifies to
r=z
everywhere and becomes a real number. The Berry curvature is given by

\displaystyle\Omega_{z}=2\,\frac{\text{Im}(\bm{\nabla}r)\times\text{Re}(\bm{\nabla}r)}{\left(1-|r|^{2}\right)^{2}}

(32)

and diverges at the hot-line where
|r|=1
as shown in Fig.
1
.

If the lattice becomes denser and denser we can connect the results from the lattice model to the continuum limit. Therefore, we introduce a small lattice constant
a
, i.e. using
z=na
and
\sin(ak_{i})
or
\cos(ak_{i})
where we let
a\rightarrow 0
. In this manner we find on the one hand from Eq. (
30
) close to the Weyl point at
k_{x}=\pi/2

\displaystyle r\approx 1-a\frac{k_{x}+iu_{z}k_{y}}{\sqrt{1-u_{z}^{2}}}

(33)

and on the other hand
\exp(\lambda z)\approx(1-a\lambda)^{-n}

which yields
r\approx 1-a\lambda
consistently with Eq. (
12
). This also gives
c^{2}=1-|r|^{2}\approx 2a\text{Re}(\lambda)
and
\nabla r\approx-a\nabla\lambda
. It turns out that we get the same BC as in Eq. (
32
) for the lattice model with finite

if we use the continuum BC in Eq. (
14
) and these approximations, even though both are valid close to the Weyl nodes and for

only.

III.3
Self-adjoint extensions and parameter
\alpha

Symmetric, unbound operators like the differential operator
P=-i\partial_{x}
in systems with boundaries are in general not self-adjoint
Bonneau et al. 2001
;
Hashimoto et al. 2017
;
Seradjeh and Vennettilli 2018
. Nevertheless, they can have self-adjoint extensions which usually come in form of boundary conditions. For example, if we consider wave functions
\psi
and
\phi
constraint to the interval
[0,1]
we find by partial integration

\displaystyle\left<\phi|P\psi\right>-\left<P\phi|\psi\right>=-i\left[\phi^{\dagger}(1)\psi(1)-\phi^{\dagger}(0)\psi(0)\right].

(34)

Even though
P^{\dagger}
(acting on
\phi
) formally looks the same as
P
(acting on
\psi
) they do not act on the same space of functions: for a particle in a box we usually take the boundary conditions
\psi(0)=\psi(1)=0
which always yield zero for the right hand side of Eq. (
34
) but do not give any restrictions on
\phi
. In order to make
P
self-adjoint we have to find a boundary condition for which the boundary term in Eq. (
34
) vanishes and which is the same for both
P
and
P^{\dagger}
. The easiest of these so-called self-adjoint extensions is
P_{0}
with periodic boundary conditions
\psi(0)=\psi(1)
. Indeed, there are infinitely many possible extensions parametrized by a phase
\theta
as
P_{\theta}
with
\psi(0)=e^{i\theta}\psi(1)
. According to von Neumann’s theorem
von Neumann 1930
, if an operator has a self-adjoint extension it is either unique or there are infinitely many extensions parametrized by a unitary matrix
\mathbb{U}(n)
.

As a consequence, we find a
\mathbb{U}(1)
phase
e^{i\alpha}
for the tilted Weyl cone model in Eq. (
8
) at the boundary
z=0

\displaystyle\psi_{\downarrow}=e^{i\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{1+\chi u_{z}}{1-\chi u_{z}}}\penalty\ \psi_{\uparrow}

(35)

which is equivalent to the condition
\left<\psi\right|\boldsymbol{\sigma}\left|\psi\right>=\bm{s}(\alpha)
in Eq. (
III.1
) yielding

\displaystyle\psi(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}e^{-i\alpha/2}\sqrt{1-\chi u_{z}}\\
e^{+i\alpha/2}\sqrt{1+\chi u_{z}}\end{pmatrix}.

(36)

Note that the choice of
\alpha
has direct physical consequences since it defines the direction of the Fermi arc in momentum space.

If a hermitian Hamiltonian is defined on the whole space it always is self-adjoint since the wave functions vanish at
\pm\infty
and there is no boundary term anymore. This means if we define the insulating phase at
z>0
it will fix a specific value of
\alpha
. As example, we take the Hamiltonian in Eqs. (
8
) and substitute
k_{y}
with

\displaystyle\tilde{k}_{y}(z)=\begin{cases}\frac{+k_{0}^{2}-k_{y}^{2}}{2k_{0}},&z\leq 0\\
\frac{-\Delta^{2}-k_{y}^{2}}{2\Delta},&z>0\end{cases}.

(37)

This yields two Weyl cones at
\bm{k}=(0,\pm k_{0},0)
with opposite chirality for
z<0
and an insulator with gap
2\Delta
for
z>0
, see SM of Ref.
Wawrzik et al. 2021
. Again, the wave function decays exponentially
\psi\propto e^{\lambda({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})z}
away from the surface
z=0
, i.e. Re
(\lambda z)<0
. From Eqs. (
III.1
) and (
12
) we find

\displaystyle\text{Re}(\lambda)\propto-\sin(\alpha)k_{x}+\cos(\alpha)\tilde{k}_{y}(z).

(38)

Thus, on the insulating site Re
(\lambda z)<0
is only satisfied for all
{\bm{k}_{\parallel}}
if
\sin(\alpha)=0
and
\cos(\alpha)>0
, i.e.
\alpha=0
. For the Weyl semimetal side the surface state has to be located at
|k_{y}|<k_{0}
and the Fermi arc becomes a straight line between the Weyl nodes. Even though this holds for the perfect vacuum
\Delta\rightarrow\infty
, too, other conventions for the insulator side will yield different values for
\alpha
.

As shown in the previous section, this parameter will be fixed in a lattice model, too. The model in Eq. (
19
) only allows for two different values
\alpha=\pm\pi/2
by tuning
\Delta
that opens a gap on the boundary of the Brillouin zone. However, introducing the time reversal invariant

V
Berry curvature mediated transport phenomena

The Berry curvature of Bloch wave functions has an influence on the velocity of Bloch electrons similar to a magnetic field yielding e.g. the anomalous Hall effect. For a Weyl semimetal the BC diverges quadratically at the BC hot-line and might give large responses in experiments. Furthermore, the BC is concentrated at the Weyl nodes and the
k\cdot p
model will give a good approximation for the following results.

If the WSM has broken TR symmetry it is possible to measure the anomalous Hall effect which is proportional to the integral of the BC over the first Brillouin zone. However, if there is a symmetry like TR or a mirror under which the BC is odd, this integral and the Hall response vanish. Nevertheless, we can have a dipole moment of Berry curvature at the Fermi surface giving rise to a second order anomalous Hall effect yielding a dc and an ac current with twice the applied frequency of the applied electric field
Sodemann and Fu 2015
. In two dimensions this is allowed if the maximal lattice symmetry is a single mirror line.
The Berry curvature dipole of the Fermi arc reads:

\displaystyle\bm{D}

\displaystyle=-\int\frac{\text{d}^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\penalty\ \bm{\nabla}f_{0}\penalty\ \Omega({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})

(53)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\chi u_{z}}{8\pi^{2}k_{c}}\sqrt{1-u_{z}^{2}}\frac{\bm{s}+\chi\bm{u}}{1+\chi\bm{s}\cdot\bm{u}}

(54)

In the limit
k_{c}\rightarrow 0
the BCD diverges due to the diverging BC and for a finite slab the BCD still increases linear in system size
L
. The dipole can not only be measured in the second order non-linear Hall effect
j\propto E^{2}L
, but also in the non-linear Nernst effect
j\propto(\bm{\nabla}T)^{2}L
and Kerr-rotations and circular dichroism using the effective orbital magnetization
\bm{M}\propto\bm{D}\cdot\bm{E}\penalty\ \hat{e}_{z}
.

Another important effect of the BC is the orbital magnetic moment
\bm{m}
which leads to a deformation of the Fermi surface
Sundaram and Niu 1999
and thus, has an influence on transport measurements. If we assume a
{\bm{k}_{\parallel}}
-dependent
\alpha
this yields for the WSM surface state:

\displaystyle\bm{m}

\displaystyle=\text{Im}\left(\left<\bm{\nabla}\Psi\right|\times(E-H)\left|\bm{\nabla}\Psi\right>\right)

(55)

\displaystyle=-4u_{z}\bm{\nabla}\alpha\times\bm{s}

(56)

The orbital magnetic moment does not diverge like the BCD and for a constant
\alpha
as we have in our models it even vanishes and therefore can be neglected. Regarding the surface state in Eq. (
1
), the orbital magnetic moment depends on
\psi({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})
but not on
\lambda({\bm{k}_{\parallel}})
.

A further widely known phenomenon is the chiral anomaly that can be seen if a parallel electric and a magnetic field
\bm{E}\parallel\bm{B}
is applied to a WSM and is caused by the different chiralities of the Weyl nodes. For tilted WSMs there is also a chiral non-linear current
Li et al. 2021

\displaystyle\bm{j}=\int\frac{\text{d}^{2}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\penalty\ \partial_{\epsilon}f_{0}\penalty\ (\bm{E}\times\bm{\Omega})((\bm{E}\times\bm{\Omega})\cdot(\bm{v}\times\bm{B})).

(57)

A natural question is if we can observe such a current caused by the Fermi arc. Since
\bm{\Omega}\perp\bm{v}
on the surface there is no contribution for
\bm{E}\parallel\bm{B}
. Nevertheless, for
\bm{B}=B\bm{n}
and
\bm{E}\parallel\bm{v}
this changes and we get

\displaystyle j_{x}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sqrt{1-u_{z}^{2}}u_{z}^{2}}{48\pi^{2}k_{c}^{3}}E^{2}B

(58)

\displaystyle\propto E^{2}BL^{3}

(59)

where for simplicity we chose
\bm{n}=\hat{z},\penalty\ \alpha=\pi/2
, and
\bm{u}_{\parallel}=0
. In contrast to the BCD the BC appears squared and the resulting current diverges even faster as
L^{3}
. Note that it has opposite signs on top and bottom surface and the bulk current vanishes in this case.

If we have a TR symmetric WSM there is still a chance to find a first order Hall-like response. The so-called Magnus Hall conductance
Papaj and Fu 2019
which is closely related to the BC dipole, yields

\displaystyle G_{MH}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}\Delta U\int_{v_{x}>0}\frac{\text{d}^{2}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\penalty\ \partial_{\epsilon}f_{0}\penalty\ \Omega

(60)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}\chi u_{z}}{8\hbar\pi^{2}k_{0}}\frac{1-u_{z}^{2}}{(1+\chi\bm{s}\cdot\bm{u})^{2}}

(61)

for the Fermi arc if the electric field is applied perpendicular to the mirror line and gives rise to a current in
y
-direction along the mirror line that is proportional to the slab thickness
L
.

VI
Conclusions and outlook

We have demonstrated that the surface state of a Weyl semimetal in a lattice model can be derived analytically in a similar manner as the continuum model. The free phase of the self-adjoint extension is fixed in the semi-infinite and finite lattice model resulting in a fully determined shape of the arc and the BC hot-line at which the BC diverges quadratically. We provided a lattice model in which a bulk parameter can change the phase on the surface. A slab calculation confirmed the that Fermi arcs on opposite surfaces hybridize and that the BC dipole grows linearly with the slab thickness
L
. The
1/k^{2}
behavior of the surface BC is also revealed using surface Green’s functions with different thicknesses of the surface layer. Finally, we showed that the diverging BC results in transport phenomena like the second order Hall effect and the Magnus-Hall conductance diverge linear with the thickness
L
. The second-order non-linear chiral current is even proportional to
L^{3}
if electric and magnetic field are perpendicular to each other.

As future direction, the lattice models we have investigated here are well suited for investigating how disorder influences the surface BC and the resulting transport phenomena. Furthermore, finite slabs as well as surface Green’s functions may be used to elaborate on the effect of surface reconstructions, which are relevant from an experimental point of view and may quantitatively alter surface Berry curvatures and their dipoles.
