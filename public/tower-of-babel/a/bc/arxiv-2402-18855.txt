Work Sum Rule for Open Quantum Systems
Parth Kumar, Caleb M. Webb, Charles A. Stafford

Abstract
A key question in the thermodynamics of open quantum systems is how to partition thermodynamic quantities such as entropy, work, and internal energy between the system and its environment. We show that the only partition under which entropy is non-singular is based on a partition of Hilbert-space, which assigns half the system-environment coupling to the system and half to the environment. However, quantum work partitions non-trivially under Hilbert-space partition, and we derive a Work Sum Rule that accounts for quantum work at a distance. All state functions of the system are shown to be path independent once this nonlocal quantum work is properly accounted for. Our results are illustrated with application to a driven resonant level strongly coupled to a reservoir.

In this Letter, we analyze the thermodynamics of a quasi-statically driven open quantum system strongly coupled to its environment, and
show that entropy is only well-defined under a Hilbert-space partition, which divides the system-environment coupling equally between the system and the environment.
Internal energy, chemical work, and entropy are partitioned straightforwardly under a partition of Hilbert space.
However, the partition of quantum work is non-trivial in this framework. We therefore derive a Work Sum Rule that accounts for the thermodynamic effect of quantum work at a distance.
All state functions of the system are shown to be path independent once this nonlocal quantum work is properly accounted for.

The Hamiltonian of the open quantum system is

H(t)=H_{S}(t)+H_{R}+H_{SR}(t)
,
where

H_{S}(t)=\sum_{n,m}[h_{S}(t)]_{nm}d^{\dagger}_{n}d_{m}

is the system Hamiltonian and

H_{R}=\sum_{k}\epsilon_{k}c_{k}^{\dagger}c_{k}

is the reservoir Hamiltonian.
Finally, the coupling Hamiltonian
H_{SR}(t)

H_{SR}(t)=\sum_{k,n}[V_{kn}(t)c_{k}^{\dagger}d_{n}+\mbox{h.c.}]

Definition (
12

H|_{S}(t)=H_{S}(t)+\frac{1}{2}H_{SR}(t),

(14)

The following Sum Rule for external work can be derived for open systems
(see Appendix
E

for a derivation)

\dot{W}_{ext}^{(1)}(t)=\dot{\Omega}_{S}^{(1)}(t)+\delta\dot{\Omega}_{R}^{(1)}(t)\,,

(15)

where
\delta\Omega_{R}=\Omega_{R}-\Omega_{R,0}

is the change in the reservoir grand potential induced due to the change in its spectrum as a result of its coupling to the system. A similar construction was introduced by Friedel
Friedel 1958
to describe the cloud of screening charge induced in a metal due to the presence of an impurity.

An interesting and important case to consider is that where only the system is time-dependent so that
\dot{H}|_{S}(t)=\dot{H}_{S}(t)
and
\dot{H}|_{R}(t)=0

\delta\dot{\Omega}_{R}(t)\neq 0

\langle\dot{H}_{S}(t)\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\dot{H}_{SR}=0}}{{=}}\dot{\Omega}_{S}^{(1)}(t)+\delta\dot{\Omega}_{R}^{(1)}(t)\,.

I^{W}_{S}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\dot{H}_{SR}=0}}{{=}}-\delta\dot{\Omega}^{(1)}_{R}

or minus the rate of nonlocal quantum work done on the reservoir.

Using the internal energy
U_{S}(t)=\langle H|_{S}(t)\rangle

)] of the work done on the system, which does not take into account the nonlocal quantum work
\int I_{S}^{W}dt
. Once the correct definition of work [Eq. (
18
)] is used, the
Hilbert-space partition (
\alpha=1/2
) of the statistical mechanical entropy [Eq. (
Work Sum Rule for Open Quantum Systems
)] satisfies the First Law [Eq. (
17
)], and is path independent, as shown in Fig.
3
(b).

In this Letter, we have derived a Work Sum Rule describing the thermodynamic effects of nonlocal quantum work.
An open quantum system is analogous to a quantum impurity problem, and nonlocal quantum work is a thermodynamic effect analogous to the screening charge that must be included in the Friedel sum rule
Friedel 1958
.
We emphasize that our thermodynamic partition of the entropy is different than the usual information-theoretic partition
based on the reduced state of a quantum subsystem
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
; due to the nonlocality of quantum information, the reduced-state description
introduces entanglement entropy
that is not associated with any thermodynamic process
Webb and Stafford 2024
.

The thermodynamic partition proposed in this Letter is based on a partition of Hilbert space, and
is consistent with the analysis of Ref.
Ludovico et al. 2014
, which circumnavigates the issue of partitioning quantum work.
Alternative attempts
Esposito et al. 2015
to partition the thermodynamics of open quantum systems have failed due to an incorrect partition of quantum work.

Appendix A
Derivation of Eq. 2

Using the inclusive definition of external work
Jarzynski 2007
;
Campisi et al. 2011
;
Talkner and Hänggi 2020
,
the quantum statistical average rate of external work done on a system with Hamiltonian
H(t)
is defined to be

\dot{W}_{ext}(t):=\frac{d}{dt}\langle H(t)\rangle,

where
H(t)
is the total Hamiltonian describing the subsystem of interest, the environment, and the system-environment coupling.
It is straightforward to show that the mean rate of external work is equal to the expectation value of the total power operator
[second equality in Eq. (
2
)]:

\dot{W}_{ext}(t)=\langle\dot{H}(t)\rangle\,.

(26)

This can be shown by first noting that since
\langle H(t)\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho(t)H(t)\}
, it follows from the product rule that

\frac{d}{dt}\langle H(t)\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}\{\dot{\rho}(t)H(t)+\rho(t)\dot{H}(t)\}\,.

(27)

Eq. (
26
) then follows from inserting the von Neumann equation of motion for the density matrix

i\hbar\dot{\rho}(t)=[H(t),\rho(t)]

(28)

in the above equation and using the cyclicity of the Trace.

Appendix B
Nonequilibrium Green’s functions in the quasi-static limit

B.1
Key definitions and relations

The general system lesser two-time Green’s function is defined as

G_{nm}^{<}(t,t^{\prime})=i\langle d_{m}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime})d_{n}(t)\rangle\,.

(29)

The advanced system, tunneling, and reservoir Green’s function are defined, respectively, as

G^{A}_{nm}(t,t^{\prime})=i\theta(t^{\prime}-t)\langle\{d_{n}(t),d^{\dagger}_{m}(t^{\prime})\}\rangle\,,

(30)

G^{A}_{nk}(t,t^{\prime})=i\theta(t^{\prime}-t)\langle\{d_{n}(t),c^{\dagger}_{k}(t^{\prime})\}\rangle\,,

(31)

and

G^{A}_{kk^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})=i\theta(t^{\prime}-t)\langle\{c_{k}(t),c^{\dagger}_{k^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\}\rangle\,,

(32)

where
\theta(x)
is the Heaviside step function.

The analysis of quasi-static driving is facilitated by performing the so-called Wigner transformation
Haug and Jauho 2007
;
Rammer 2007
of the time-dependent quantities defined by the substitutions

\tau=t-t^{\prime}\,,

(33)

and

\bar{t}=\frac{t+t^{\prime}}{2}\,,

(34)

where
\tau
is the fast dynamical quantum time-scale while
\bar{t}
is the slow time-scale of the quasi-static drive. We denote the Wigner transformed lesser Green’s function as
\widetilde{G}(\bar{t},\tau)
, and its Fourier transform with respect to the quantum time
\tau
is defined as

\mathcal{G}(\bar{t},\epsilon):=\int d\tau\,e^{i\epsilon\tau}\widetilde{G}(\bar{t},\tau)\,,

(35)

with the inverse transform is given by

\widetilde{G}(\bar{t},\tau)=\int\frac{d\epsilon}{2\pi}\,e^{-i\epsilon\tau}\mathcal{G}(\bar{t},\epsilon)\,.

(36)

The equal-time Green’s function is obtained by setting
\tau=0
and
\bar{t}=t
, so that

G^{<}_{nm}(t,t)=\widetilde{G}_{nm}^{<}(\bar{t}=t,0)=\int\frac{d\epsilon}{2\pi}\,\mathcal{G}_{nm}^{<}(\bar{t}=t,\epsilon)\,.

(37)

Derivatives of observables with respect to the quasi-static timescale
\bar{t}
are assumed to be infinitessimal, and
a series expansion of the Green’s function in powers of
\partial/\partial\bar{t}
can be developed
Haug and Jauho 2007

\widetilde{G}(\bar{t},\tau)=\widetilde{G}^{(0)}(\bar{t},\tau)+\widetilde{G}^{(1)}(\bar{t},\tau)+\widetilde{G}^{(2)}(\bar{t},\tau)+...\,,

(38)

where the superscript
(n)
denotes the power of
\partial/\partial\bar{t}
.
Only the lowest-order term in the above expansion is needed to describe
quasi-static driving.
In particular,

\mathcal{G}^{<(0)}(\bar{t},\epsilon)=2\pi if(\epsilon)A^{(0)}(\bar{t},\epsilon),

(39)

where the quasi-static spectral function is

A^{(0)}(\bar{t},\epsilon)=\delta(\epsilon-h(\bar{t}))
.

The submatrix of
A^{(0)}
within the system Hilbert-space may be expressed as

A^{(0)}(\bar{t},\epsilon)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}[\mathcal{G}^{A(0)}(\bar{t},\epsilon)-\mathcal{G}^{R(0)}(\bar{t},\epsilon)]\,,

(40)

where
\mathcal{G}^{A(0)}
and
\mathcal{G}^{R(0)}
are the Wigner-transformed advanced and retarded system Green’s functions, respectively, in the quasi-static limit

\mathcal{G}^{A/R(0)}(\bar{t},\epsilon)=\left[1\epsilon-h(\bar{t})-\Sigma^{A/R}(\bar{t},\epsilon)\right]^{-1},

(41)

and the (advanced) self-energy in the quasi-static limit is

\Sigma^{A}_{nm}(\bar{t},\epsilon)=\sum_{k}V_{kn}(\bar{t})\mathcal{G}^{A(0)}_{kk}(\epsilon)V^{*}_{km}(\bar{t})\,.

(42)

For the resonant-level model considered in this Letter,

\Sigma^{A}(\bar{t},\epsilon)=\Lambda(\bar{t},\epsilon)+i\frac{\Gamma(\bar{t},\epsilon)}{2}

(43)

is simply a complex number whose real and imaginary parts are shown in Fig.
4
and may be calculated explicitly as
Datta 2005a

\Lambda(\bar{t},\epsilon)=\frac{V^{2}(\bar{t})}{2t_{0}^{2}}\begin{cases}(\epsilon-\epsilon_{0})+[(\epsilon-\epsilon_{0})^{2}-4t_{0}^{2})]^{\frac{1}{2}},&\epsilon-\epsilon_{0}<-2t_{0},\\
(\epsilon-\epsilon_{0}),&|\epsilon-\epsilon_{0}|<2t_{0},\\
(\epsilon-\epsilon_{0})-[(\epsilon-\epsilon_{0})^{2}-4t_{0}^{2})]^{\frac{1}{2}},&\epsilon-\epsilon_{0}>2t_{0},\end{cases}

(44)

\Gamma(\bar{t},\epsilon)=\begin{cases}\frac{2V^{2}(\bar{t})}{t_{0}}\Big(1-\Big(\frac{\epsilon-\epsilon_{0}}{2t_{0}}\Big)^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}},&|\epsilon-\epsilon_{0}|<2t_{0},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(45)

Appendix C
Global Quasi-static dynamics and thermodynamics

The quantum adiabatic theorem
Sakurai and Napolitano 2011

tells us that the state of a system with an adiabatically varying Hamiltonian
h(t)
is given by a linear combination of vectors of the form

|\psi_{\nu}(t)\rangle=e^{i\theta_{\nu}(t)}e^{i\gamma_{\nu}(t)}|\nu(t)\rangle\,,

(47)

where
\theta_{\nu}(t)=-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\epsilon_{\nu}(t^{\prime})
is the so-called dynamical phase,
\gamma_{\nu}(t)=i\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\langle\psi_{\nu}(t^{\prime})|\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}\psi_{\nu}(t^{\prime})\rangle
is the so-called geometrical phase, and
|\nu(t)\rangle
solves the instantaneous Hamiltonian eigenvalue equation
h(t)|\nu(t)\rangle=\epsilon_{\nu}(t)|\nu(t)\rangle
.

C.1
Derivation of the Global Fundamental Thermodynamic Identity

Using
g(t,\epsilon)=\sum_{\nu}\delta(\epsilon-\epsilon_{\nu}(t))
, where
\epsilon_{\nu}(t)
are the instantaneous eigenvalues of
h(t)
, and it is understood that the sum becomes an integral for continuous spectra,
the thermodynamic quantities [Eqs. (
6
), (
8
), (
9
), and (
10
)], may be written

U(t)=\sum_{\nu}f_{\nu}(t)\epsilon_{\nu}(t)\,,

(48)

S(t)=-\sum_{\nu}\left\{f_{\nu}(t)\ln f_{\nu}(t)+(1-f_{\nu}(t))\ln[1-f_{\nu}(t)]\right\}\,,

(49)

N(t)=\sum_{\nu}f_{\nu}(t)\,,

(50)

and

\Omega(t)=-\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{\nu}\ln[1+e^{-\beta(\epsilon_{\nu}(t)-\mu)}]\,,

(51)

where
f_{\nu}(t)\equiv f(\epsilon_{\nu}(t))
.

It follows by the chain rule that the first variations can be computed as

\dot{U}^{(1)}(t)=\sum_{\nu}(\dot{f}_{\nu}(t)\epsilon_{\nu}(t)+f_{\nu}(t)\dot{\epsilon}_{\nu}(t))\,,

(52)

\dot{S}^{(1)}(t)=\beta\sum_{\nu}(\epsilon_{\nu}(t)-\mu)\dot{f}_{\nu}(t)\,,

(53)

\dot{N}^{(1)}(t)=\sum_{\nu}\dot{f}_{\nu}(t)\,,

(54)

and

\dot{\Omega}^{(1)}(t)=\sum_{\nu}f_{\nu}(t)\dot{\epsilon}_{\nu}(t)\,.

(55)

The global fundamental thermodynamic identity
\dot{\Omega}^{(1)}(t)=\dot{U}^{(1)}(t)-T\dot{S}^{(1)}(t)-\mu\dot{N}^{(1)}(t)

follows straightforwardly from the addition and rearrangement of the last four equations.

C.2
Derivation of Eq. (
11
)

Eq. (
26
) can be written in the instantaneous eigenbasis of the Hamiltonian as

\dot{W}_{ext}(t)=\sum_{\nu}f(\epsilon_{\nu}(t))\langle\nu(t)|\dot{h}(t)|\nu(t)\rangle\,.

(56)

The instantaneous eigenstates and eigenvalues of
h(t)
satisfy
Sakurai and Napolitano 2011

\langle\nu(t)|\dot{h}(t)|\mu(t)\rangle=\dot{\epsilon}_{\nu}(t)\delta_{\mu\nu}+(\epsilon_{\mu}(t)-\epsilon_{\nu}(t))\langle\nu(t)|\dot{\mu}(t)\rangle\,,

(57)

using which we may write

\dot{W}_{ext}(t)=\sum_{\nu}f(\epsilon_{\nu}(t))\dot{\epsilon}_{\nu}(t)\,,

(58)

where the RHS is identical to that of Eq. (
55
), thus establishing Eq. (
11
).

Appendix D
Motivation for the operator Hilbert-space partition [Eq. 12]

We begin by noting that the LDOS can be rewritten as

g_{\gamma}(t,\epsilon):=\mathbb{Tr}\{\Pi_{\gamma}A^{(0)}(t,\epsilon)\}=\int_{x\in\gamma}dx\,\langle x|\delta(\epsilon-h(t)|x\rangle\,,

(59)

which makes clear that the LDOS is simply the integral of the local spectrum over subspace
\gamma
.

To motivate the Hilbert-space partition of a quantum observable
Webb and Stafford 2024
, let us consider a dynamical observable
O
that is compatible with the Hamiltonian
H(t)
.
Then the corresponding Hilbert-space operator

o
commutes with
h(t)
,
and
o|\epsilon\rangle=o(\epsilon)|\epsilon\rangle
, where
h(t)|\epsilon\rangle=\epsilon|\epsilon\rangle
and
o(\epsilon)
is an eigenvalue of
o
.
In analogy with the definitions of
U_{\gamma}
and
N_{\gamma}
, define

O_{\gamma}^{(0)}(t):=\int d\epsilon\,g_{\gamma}(t,\epsilon)f(\epsilon)o(\epsilon)=\int d\epsilon\,\mathbb{Tr}\{\Pi_{\gamma}\delta(\epsilon-h(t))\}f(\epsilon)o(\epsilon).

(60)

Because
[o,h(t)]=0
, the operator
o
can be brought inside the trace so that

O_{\gamma}^{(0)}(t)=\int d\epsilon\,\mathbb{Tr}\{\Pi_{\gamma}\delta(\epsilon-h(t))o\}f(\epsilon).

(61)

Finally, from the cyclicity of the trace and the the compatibility of
o
and
h(t)
, this can be expressed using Eq. (
12
) as

O_{\gamma}^{(0)}(t)=\int d\epsilon\,\mathbb{Tr}\{o|_{\gamma}\,\delta(\epsilon-h(t))\}f(\epsilon)=\langle O|_{\gamma}(t)\rangle^{(0)}.

(62)

This completes the demonstration that the partition of thermodynamic quantities based on the local spectrum (LDOS) corresponds to the quantum statistical average of the partitioned observables defined by Eq. (
12
). This
equivalence holds for dynamical observables compatible with the Hamiltonian. For the application to statistical observables, such as entropy, see Appendix
G
. For dynamical observables that do not commute with the Hamiltonian, such as the power operator
\dot{H}(t)
, Eq. (
12
) is taken as the definition of the partitioned observable, but the quantum statistical average is no longer given by
Eq. (
60
), but is instead given by the more general Eq. (
62
).

Appendix F
Broad-band limit for the rate of nonlocal quantum work

The rate of nonlocal work done [Eq. (
21
)] vanishes in the broad-band limit provided the limit is taken in a physically meaningful manner. We may write the first term on the RHS of Eq. (
21
) using Eq. (
20
) in the broad-band limit as (since

)

\langle\dot{H}_{SR}\rangle=\int\frac{d\epsilon}{2\pi}\,f(\epsilon)\mathbb{Tr}[\dot{\Gamma}(t,\epsilon)\mathbb{Re}\{G^{A}(t,\epsilon)\}]\,.

(81)

We note first that

\Gamma(t,\epsilon)\leq\frac{2V^{2}(t)}{t_{0}}\theta(t_{0}-|\epsilon-\epsilon_{0}|).

It therefore follows in the limit

|\langle\dot{H}_{SR}\rangle|\leq\left|\frac{V\dot{V}}{\pi t_{0}}\ln\Bigg\{\frac{(\Gamma/2)^{2}+(\mu-\varepsilon_{s})^{2})}{(\Gamma/2)^{2}+(2t_{0}+\epsilon_{0}-\varepsilon_{s})^{2})}\Bigg\}\right|.

is

\langle\dot{H}_{SR}\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle t_{0}\rightarrow\infty}}{{\sim}}-\frac{2V\dot{V}}{\pi t_{0}}\ln\Bigg\{\frac{2t_{0}}{[(\Gamma/2)^{2}+(\mu-\varepsilon_{s})^{2})]^{1/2}}\Bigg\}\,.

(84)

t_{0}\rightarrow\infty

\psi_{\nu}^{\dagger}|0\rangle=|\nu(t)\rangle
, where
h(t)|\nu(t)\rangle=\varepsilon_{\nu}(t)|\nu(t)\rangle
and
f_{\nu}:=f(\varepsilon_{\nu}(t))
.
For such a system, the entropy operator has the form

\hat{S}^{(0)}=-\ln\hat{\rho}^{(0)}=\sum_{\nu}[-\ln f_{\nu}\psi_{\nu}^{\dagger}\psi_{\nu}-\ln(1-f_{\nu})\psi_{\nu}\psi_{\nu}^{\dagger}],

(86)

which consists of a particle-like piece
\hat{S}^{n}
and a hole-like piece
\hat{S}^{p}
, whose corresponding operators on the single-particle Hilbert-space are

\displaystyle s^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{\nu}\ln f_{\nu}|\nu\rangle\langle\nu|,

(87)

\displaystyle s^{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{\nu}\ln(1-f_{\nu})|\nu\rangle\langle\nu|.

(88)

These can be partitioned according to Eq. (
12
) to obtain the Hilbert-space partition of the entropy.

For the
\alpha
-partition of the entropy, we need to separate
s=s^{n}+s^{p}
into diagonal and off-diagonal pieces

s_{SS}:=\Pi_{S}(s^{n}+s^{p})\Pi_{S}

(89)

and

s_{SR}:=\Pi_{S}(s^{n}+s^{p})\Pi_{R}+\Pi_{R}(s^{n}+s^{p})\Pi_{S}.

(90)

The
\alpha
-partition corresponds to the Hilbert-space operator

s_{SS}+\alpha s_{SR}=(1-2\alpha)\Pi_{S}s\Pi_{S}+\alpha\{\Pi_{S},s\},

(91)

where we have used
1=2\alpha+(1-2\alpha)
and
\Pi_{S}+\Pi_{R}=1
. Clearly, for
\alpha=1/2
,
s_{SS}+\alpha s_{SR}=s|_{S}
, as defined by Eq. (
12
).

The Fock-space entropy operator under
\alpha
-partition is

\alpha\mbox{-}\hat{S}_{S}:=\hat{S}_{SS}+\alpha\hat{S}_{SR},

(92)

and taking its quantum statistical average

\alpha\mbox{-}S^{(0)}_{S}=\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{\rho}^{(0)}(\alpha\mbox{-}\hat{S}^{(0)}_{S})\}

(93)

yields

\displaystyle\alpha\mbox{-}S^{(0)}_{S}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\alpha\sum_{\nu}\langle\nu|\Pi_{S}|\nu\rangle s(\varepsilon_{\nu})

(94)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle(1-2\alpha)\sum_{\mu,\nu}|\langle\mu|\Pi_{S}|\nu\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle[-f_{\mu}\ln f_{\nu}-(1-f_{\mu})\ln(1-f_{\nu})]\,.

Taking the limit of a continuous spectrum gives Eq. (
Work Sum Rule for Open Quantum Systems
).
For additional details, see Ref.
Webb and Stafford 2024
.

Appendix H
Extension to multiple reservoirs

In this section, we extend the analysis presented in the main text to the case of a multi-level system coupled to multiple reservoirs, each with the same
temperature and chemical potential (for the case of reservoirs with different temperatures and chemical potentials, see Refs.
Shastry et al. 2020

Stafford 2016

Shastry and Stafford 2016

The change in the grand potential of the
\alpha^{th}
reservoir due to its coupling to the system can be written as

\delta\Omega^{(0)}_{R,{\alpha}}(t)=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\,\frac{d\epsilon}{\pi}\mathbb{ImTr}\Bigg\{\Bigg(\frac{\partial\Sigma^{A}_{\alpha}(t,\epsilon)}{\partial\epsilon}\Bigg)\mathcal{G}^{A(0)}(t,\epsilon)\Bigg\}\omega(\epsilon)\,,

(95)

where
\Sigma^{A}_{nm,\alpha}(t,\epsilon)=\sum_{k}V_{kn,\alpha}(t)\mathcal{G}^{A}_{kk,\alpha}(\epsilon)V^{*}_{km,\alpha}(t)

\alpha^{th}
reservoir (with
\mathcal{G}^{A}_{kk,\alpha}(\epsilon)
representing the advanced Green’s function and
V_{kn,\alpha}
representing the coupling element of the
n^{th}
system orbital to the
k^{th}
mode of the
\alpha^{th}
reservoir, respectively)
so that its first variation is

\displaystyle\dot{\Omega}_{R,\alpha}^{(1)}=

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\,\frac{d\epsilon}{\pi}\mathbb{ImTr}\Bigg\{\frac{\partial\Sigma^{A}_{\alpha}(t,\epsilon)}{\partial\epsilon}\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}^{A(0)}(t,\epsilon)}{\partial t}\Bigg\}\omega(\epsilon)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\,\frac{d\epsilon}{\pi}\mathbb{ImTr}\Bigg\{\frac{\partial^{2}\Sigma^{A}_{\alpha}(t,\epsilon)}{\partial t\partial\epsilon}\mathcal{G}^{A(0)}(t,\epsilon)\Bigg\}\omega(\epsilon)\,.
