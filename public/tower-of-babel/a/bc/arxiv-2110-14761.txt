The Grand Arc Graph
Assaf Bar-Natan and Yvon Verberne

Abstract
. In this article, we construct a new simplicial complex for infinite-type surfaces, which we call the grand arc graph . We show that if the end space of a surface has at least three different self-similar equivalence classes of maximal ends, then the grand arc graph is infinite-diameter and δ \delta -hyperbolic. In this case, we also show that the mapping class group acts on the grand arc graph by isometries and acts on the visible boundary continuously. When the surface has stable maximal ends, we also show that this action has finitely many orbits.



1.
Introduction

Let
\Sigma
be a connected, orientable, infinite-type surface.
The mapping class group of
\Sigma
, denoted by
\operatorname{Map}(\Sigma)
, is the group of orientation preserving homeomorphisms of
\Sigma
, up to isotopy.
Informally, an
end
of an infinite-type surface is a way of exiting every compact subsurface in the sense of Freudenthal
[
7
]
.

In
[
13
]
, Mann and Rafi define a partial order on the space of ends, allowing one to consider the space of
maximal ends
.
In this paper, we will consider surfaces which have finitely many self-similar equivalence classes of maximal ends. We call this splitting of
the end-space a
grand splitting
, denoted
\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)
, so that
the space of maximal ends is
equal to
\cup_{E\in\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)}E
. Since the grand splitting is preserved by
\operatorname{Map}(\Sigma)
, we are motivated to use it to construct a graph upon which the mapping class group acts by isometries.

A grand arc is a simple arc in the surface
\Sigma
such that the arc converges to exactly two ends, each of which are in different elements of the grand splitting.
We let the vertices of the grand arc graph,
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
, be isotopy classes of grand arcs, and we place an edge between two vertices if there exists disjoint representatives of the grand arcs. We show that for many surfaces, the grand arc graph is
\delta
-hyperbolic in the sense of Gromov and has infinite-diameter.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be an infinite-type surface with
|\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)|<\infty
,
which is not the once-punctured Cantor
tree. Then
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
is a nonempty,
connected, infinite-diameter
\delta
-hyperbolic metric space if and only if
|\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)|\geq 3
. Moreover, the constant
\delta
is independent of
\Sigma
.

Theorem
1.1
does hold in the case of the once punctured Cantor tree by the work of Bavard
[
3
]
, which uses different tools than those found in this paper. In Section
5
, we show that when
|\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)|=2
, the grand arc graph is not hyperbolic, but is still of infinite diameter. When
|\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)|=1
, the grand arc graph is empty.

For a finite-type surface
S
, the
curve graph
is a combinatorial tool used to study the mapping class group.
The curve graph was introduced by Harvey
[
10
]
and is defined to be the simplicial complex where every vertex is an isotopy class of an essential simple closed curve, and an edge between two vertices signifies disjointness. Masur-Minsky proved that the curve graph is hyperbolic
[
14
]
, and also used the curve graph to study the large-scale geometry of the mapping class group
[
15
]
.
The utility of the curve graph to aid in the study of mapping class groups of finite-type surfaces. Unfortunately, the curve graph of an infinite-type surface is of diameter
2
, which leads one to ask the following question.

Question 1.2

(AIM Workshop Problem 2.1)
.

What combinatorial objects are “good” analogues of the curve graph, either uniformly for all infinite-type surfaces or for some class of infinite-type surfaces? Here
“good” means that there exist relationships between topological properties of the mapping class and
dynamical properties of its action on the combinatorial object.

The grand arc graph can be defined for every infinite-type surface with maximal ends.
However, we will find that the grand arc graph has the desired properties outlined in Question
1.2
in the case where there are at least 3 but not infinitely-many self-similar equivalence classes of maximal ends.
For these cases, the grand arc graph is a reasonable candidate for answering Question

1.2
.

In the case of surfaces of finite-type, the mapping class group has the discrete topology and acts on the curve graph continuously by isometries.
By using the work of Fanoni–Ghaswala–McLeay
[
6
]
, it can be shown that
the mapping class group of an infinite-type surface does not act by continuous isometries on the grand arc graph.
However, we can prove that when the grand arc graph is
\delta
-hyperbolic, the action of the mapping class group on the boundary of the grand arc graph is continuous. We prove this result by showing that the action of the mapping class group on
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
is
3
-quasi-continuous
, a coarse relaxation of continuity.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\Sigma
be an infinite-type surface with

|\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)|<\infty
. Then the mapping class group acts on
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
by isometries. Moreover, if
|\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)|>2
, then the action on
\partial\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
is continuous. Moreover, when the maximal ends of
\Sigma
are stable, then the mapping class group acts on
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
with finitely many orbits.

1.1.
Prior results

Throughout this section, let
\Sigma
be an
infinite-type surface, and let
E
be its end space. Let
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
be the grand arc graph associated to
\Sigma
.

1.1.1.
Relationship to the ray graph

The
ray graph
was introduced by Calegari in
[
5
]
and is defined as follows. The ray graph is the graph whose vertex set is the
set of isotopy classes of proper rays, with interior in the complement of the Cantor
set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, from a point in
K
to infinity, and whose edges (of length 1) are the pairs of
such rays that can be realized disjointly.

In 2016, Bavard showed that the ray graph is
\delta
-hyperbolic, and used the ray graph to show the existence of non-trivial quasimorphisms on the once-punctured Cantor tree.
We note that the definition of the ray graph and the definition of the grand arc graph of the once-punctured Cantor tree agree, so in some sense, the grand arc graph is a generalization of the ray graph but for surfaces which have more than two types of maximal ends. For the ray graph, the point at infinity can be thought of as an isolated puncture, and therefore,
\mathbb{R}^{2}\smallsetminus K
is the once-punctured Cantor tree surface. All ends of the once-punctured Cantor tree are maximal, and the ends comprising of the Cantor set are all in the same self-similar equivalence class of maximal ends. Therefore, the ray graph is precisely the grand arc graph for the once-punctured Cantor tree. In this sense, the grand arc graph is a generalization of the ray graph, but for surfaces which have more than two types of maximal ends.

1.1.2.
Relationship to the omnipresent arc graph

The omnipresent arc graph was introduced by Fanoni, Ghaswala and McLeay
[
6
]
and is defined as follows.
A
one-cut surface
is a complementary component of a separating loop.
A one-cut subsurface which is homeomorphic to the entire surface is called a
one-cut homeomorphic subsurface
.
An
omnipresent arc
is an arc which joins two distinct ends and intersects all one-cut homeomorphic subsurfaces.
The
omnipresent arc graph
is the graph where vertices are homotopy classes of omnipresent arcs, and edges correspond to having disjoint representatives.

Fanoni–Ghaswala–McLeay show that if a surface
\Sigma
is stable (see Definitions 5.3 and 5.4, and Theorem B in
[
6
]
), then an arc is omnipresent if and only if it joins ends whose orbit under
\operatorname{Map}(\Sigma)
is finite. Additionally, note that finite orbit ends must be maximal, and by self-similarity of the sets in the grand splitting, they must lie in different elements of the grand splitting. In other words:

Fact 1.4
.

If
\Sigma
is a stable surface, and
\alpha
is an omnipresent arc in
\Sigma
, then
\alpha
is a grand arc.

In the general case when
\Sigma

is not stable, it is not necessarily true that omnipresent arcs are grand
1
1

1

This fact was communicated to the first author by Kasra Rafi, and follows from a construction of a surface with a single, unstable maximal end.
.
We also note that grand arcs aren’t necessarily omnipresent.
For instance, consider the surface which is
the connect sum of a Cantor tree and a
blooming Cantor tree where every end is
accumulated by genus. This stable
surface is characterized as having end space
isomorphic to
(E,E^{G})
, where
E
and
E^{G}

are Cantor sets.
In this case, no ends are of finite orbit,
and hence there are no omnipresent arcs. However,
any arc with one endpoint in
E
and one
endpoint in
E^{G}
is a grand arc.
This shows that the grand arc graph can be defined on surfaces where the omnipresent arc graph is empty.

1.1.3.
Loxodromic actions of big mapping class groups

In the literature, there has been a considerable amount of interest in studying loxodromic actions, weakly properly discontinuous (WPD) actions, or weakly WPD (WWPD) actions of big mapping class groups on various metric spaces
[
1
,
3
,
12
,
18
,
19
]
.

If
G
is a group acting on a
\delta
-hyperbolic path-connected metric space by isometries, we say that an element
g\in G
acts
loxodromically
if for any
x\in X
,
d(x,gx)
is uniformly bounded from below. For more information on isometries of
\delta
-hyperbolic spaces, we refer the reader to
[
8
]
.

In Section
6
, we will prove that there exist loxodromic actions of the mapping class group acting on the grand arc graph coming from pseudo-Anosov mapping classes on witnesses (see Section
2
for the definition of a witness).

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
W
be a witness, and let
\varphi

be a pseudo-Anosov mapping class that fixes the boundary of
W
. Let
\bar{\varphi}\in\operatorname{Map}(\Sigma)
be the homeomorphism fixing

W^{c}
and acting as
\varphi
on
W
. Then

\bar{\varphi}
acts loxodromically on
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
.

2.
Witnesses of the Grand Arc Graph

This section is an introduction of some concepts and terminology on infinite-type surfaces, with the goal of proving that the grand arc graph has witnesses in the sense of
[
21
]
.
To begin, we introduce some definitions which will be used throughout this paper. For more detail, we refer the reader to
[
13
]
.

2.1.
Surfaces, ends, and maximal ends

In this paper, a
surface
is
a 2-dimensional connected orientable topological manifold. If the surface has finitely-generated fundamental group, then it is called
finite-type
. Otherwise, it will be called
infinite-type
. Throughout this paper, we will denote infinite type surfaces by
\Sigma
, finite type surfaces by
S
, and witnesses by
W
.

The
space of ends
of a surface
\Sigma
, denoted
E=E(\Sigma)
, is defined as

E(\Sigma)=\varprojlim_{K\subset\Sigma}\pi_{0}(\Sigma\smallsetminus K)
,
where
K
ranges over all compact subsets of
\Sigma
,
and if
K_{i}\subset K_{j}
, then the map in the projective
limit is the inclusion

\varphi_{ij}:\pi_{0}(\Sigma\smallsetminus K_{j})\hookrightarrow\pi_{0}(\Sigma\smallsetminus K_{i})
.
Note that when
\pi_{0}(\Sigma\smallsetminus K)
is endowed with the discrete topology,
E(\Sigma)
becomes a totally disconnected, separable metrizable topological space. The
space
\bar{\Sigma}=\Sigma\sqcup E(\Sigma)
is called
the
end compactification
of
\Sigma

[
7
]
.
More concretely, an
end
of
\Sigma
can also be thought of as a sequence of nested open connected sets
\{U_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
which are eventually disjoint from
any compact
K\subset\Sigma
. For more detail, we refer the reader to
[
6
]
.

Let
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{\prime}
be two infinite type surfaces. Note that any continuous map
f:\Sigma^{\prime}\to\Sigma
, induces a continuous
map
f^{*}:E(\Sigma)\to E(\Sigma^{\prime})
because the inverse limit functor is contravariant. If
f:\Sigma^{\prime}\hookrightarrow\Sigma
is an embedding, then
f^{*}
is surjective.
Let
i:\Sigma^{\prime}\hookrightarrow\Sigma
be an inclusion. If an end of
\Sigma
has precisely one preimage under
i^{*}
, we say that it
lies in
the subsurface
\Sigma^{\prime}
.

An end
e
is said to be
accumulated by genus
if
every subsurface that it lies in cannot be embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Otherwise,
it is called
planar
. We denote the set of ends accumulated by genus by
E^{G}(\Sigma)
, and note that it is a closed subset of
E(\Sigma)
. By Richards’ classification of infinite-type surfaces
[
20
]
, infinite-type
surfaces are completely characterized by the triple
(E,E^{G},g)
,
where
0\leq g\leq\infty
is the
genus
of the surface.

In light of this, we think of a subset
U\subset E
as the pair
(U,U\cap E^{G})
,
and when we say
U\subset E(\Sigma_{1})
, and
V\subset E(\Sigma_{2})
are homeomorphic, we mean that there is a homeomorphism
\varphi:U\to V
such that
\varphi|_{U\cap E^{G}(\Sigma_{1})}
is a homeomorphism from
U\cap E^{G}(\Sigma_{1})
to
V\cap E^{G}(\Sigma_{2})
. A set
U\subset E
is
self-similar
if for any decomposition of
U
into two sets,
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
, which are clopen in the subspace topology of
U
, we have that
U
is homeomorphic to
U_{1}
or to
U_{2}
. We say that a subsurface
S\subset\Sigma

separates
two ends,
e,e^{\prime}\in E(\Sigma)
,
if
e
and
e^{\prime}
lie in different connected components
of
\Sigma\smallsetminus S
.

In
[
13
]
, Mann–Rafi define a
partial ordering on the space of ends
by setting
x\preccurlyeq y
, for
x,y\in E(\Sigma)
, if every clopen neighbourhood of
y

contains a clopen set homeomorphic to a neighbourhood of
x
.
Two ends are
equivalent
, or
are of the same type
if
x\preccurlyeq y
and
y\preccurlyeq x
.
This partial order has maximal elements, and we let

\mathcal{M}(\Sigma)
denote the space of maximal ends of
\Sigma
. A subset
X
of a group
G
is
coarsely bounded
, abbreviated CB, if every compatible left-invariant metric on
G
gives
X
finite diameter.
Mann–Rafi show that for infinite-type surfaces whose mapping class group is locally CB, one can write the space of maximal ends of
\Sigma
as a disjoint union of equivalence classes of self-similar ends.

Let
\Sigma
be an infinite-type surface
which has finitely many equivalence
classes of maximal ends.
In particular, this means we can write

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\Sigma)=\sqcup_{i=1}^{n}E_{i}

where the
E_{i}
’s are self-similar, meaning that they are either Cantor sets or singletons, and all of the ends
e\in E_{i}
are equivalent in the sense above.
We call such a splitting of
\mathcal{M}(\Sigma)
a

grand splitting
, denoted by
\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)
, and note that it is unique. Thus, if
\operatorname{Map}(\Sigma)
is locally CB, then
\Sigma
has a finite grand splitting.
We think of the grand splitting,

\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)
, of

\mathcal{M}(\Sigma)
as a
collection of subsets of ends, whereas we think of
\mathcal{M}(\Sigma)
as just a set of ends.

2.2.
Arcs, Loops, and Witnesses

Throughout this paper, an
arc
is a proper map
\gamma:(0,1)\to\Sigma
such that
\gamma
extends continuously to a map
\bar{\gamma}:[0,1]\to\bar{\Sigma}
, where
\bar{\gamma}(0),\bar{\gamma}(1)\in E(\Sigma)
.
We call the points
\bar{\gamma}(0)
and
\bar{\gamma}(1)
the

endpoints
of
\gamma
.
A
curve
in
\Sigma
is a map from
S^{1}
to
\Sigma
.
Throughout this paper, we often conflate curves and arcs with their images in the surface.
A curve or arc is called
simple
if
it does not intersect itself.

On a finite-type surface, arcs behave nicely, for example, they intersect one another at most finitely many times.
On infinite-type surfaces, this is not the case, as arcs can intersect infinitely often and become quite complicated.
Because of this, we restrict ourselves to focus on arcs which converge to two maximal ends.

Definition 2.1
.

An arc
\alpha

converges
to an end
e
if for any neighborhood
U
of
e
,
\alpha
eventually never leaves this neighborhood. For an infinite-type surface
\Sigma
, an arc
\alpha
is called
grand
if it is simple and converges to exactly two maximal ends, each of which is in a different element of
\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)
.

In other words, grand arcs are two-ended
arcs between maximal ends of different type, or of the same, finite-orbit type.
Using the definition of a grand arc, we are able to define the grand arc graph.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\Sigma
be an infinite-type surface. We denote the set of all grand arcs on
\Sigma
by
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
. The
grand arc graph
, denoted by
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
is the combinatorial graph given by the following data:

Vertices

There is one vertex for every isotopy class of grand arcs in
\Sigma
.

Edges

There is an edge between any two vertices corresponding to isotopy classes
\alpha
and
\beta
of
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
if
\alpha
and
\beta
have disjoint representatives.

We equip
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
with a metric by setting the length of each edge to
1
.
This gives the grand arc graph the structure of a complete metric space, and endows it with the discrete topology.

Remark 2.3
.

Notice that for the once-punctured Cantor tree, the grand arc graph is precisely equal to the ray graph defined by Bavard
[
3
]
.

Because mapping classes preserve disjointness of arcs, it follows trivially that the mapping class group acts by isometries on
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
.

The following two definitions are inspired by the works of Schleimer and Masur–Schleimer on witnesses and the curve complex for finite-type surfaces
[
16
,
21
]
.
In these works, arcs can be studied by their projections to subsurfaces. In our case, the relevant subsurfaces are finite-type subsurfaces of an infinite-type surface
\Sigma
. We would like these projections to be nonempty, prompting the following definition:

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\Sigma
be an infinite-type surface,

S\subset\Sigma
a subsurface, and
\alpha
some arc in
\Sigma
.

•

We say that
S
is
essential

if it is not homotopic to a point, a puncture, or a boundary component of
\Sigma
.

•

We say that
S

sees

\alpha
if every subsurface homotopic to
S
intersects every arc homotopic to
\alpha
.

•

We say that
S
is a
witness

of
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
if it is essential, and if it sees every arc in
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
.

If there exists a surface
W\subset\Sigma
which is a witness for
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
, then we say that
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
has a witness, or, that
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
admits a witness.

Example 2.5
.

The once-punctured Cantor tree of genus one admits two disjoint witnesses (Figure 1). Each is a witness, as every grand arc must converge to the puncture and to an end in the Cantor tree, and hence must intersect the surfaces that separate these sets of ends.

Figure 1.
Two disjoint witnesses on the once-punctured Cantor tree of genus one (cycloctopus).

In this paper, we will consider witnesses up to homotopy. In particular, when we say
disjoint
witnesses, we mean two witnesses that are not homotopically-equivalent, and that have disjoint representatives in their respective homotopy classes. Since witnesses see all grand arcs, it follows that they are necessarily separating. We next introduce some useful notation to extend the definition of “separating” to the end space.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
S\subset\Sigma
be a finite-type surface of
\Sigma
. If
E,E^{\prime}\subset E(\Sigma)
, we say that
S

separates

E
from
E^{\prime}
if the ends in
E_{1}
lie in different connected components of
S^{c}
from those in which the ends in
E_{2}
lie.

Using this definition, we see that a witness separates self-similar equivalence classes of maximal ends. We are now ready to prove that the grand arc graph has at least one witness.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\Sigma
be a surface of infinite type
with
|\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)|<\infty
.
If
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
is nonempty, then it admits a witness. Moreover, this witness can be chosen to be homeomorphic to the
|\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)|
-times punctured sphere.

Remark 2.8
.

If we assume that
\Sigma
is a surface whose mapping class group is CB-generated, then this
follows immediately from Proposition 5.4
of
[
13
]

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{M}(\Sigma)=E_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup E_{n}

be the grand splitting of
E=E(\Sigma)
,
and let
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}
be a disjoint clopen
cover of
E
such that
E_{i}\subset U_{i}
.

To see why the cover
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}
exists, we first note that due to Proposition 4.7 of
[
13
]
, the space of maximal ends is closed and the equivalence class of any maximal end is either finite or a Cantor set.
The end space is Hausdorff and compact, so it is a normal Hausdorff space.
Therefore, we can find a finite collection of disjoint open sets,
\{V_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, such that
E_{i}\subset V_{i}
.
Since
E
is a totally disconnected metrizable compact space,
E
has a
basis of clopen sets. Thus,
for every
i
and every
e\in E_{i}
,
there exists a clopen neighbourhood
e\in V_{e}\subset V_{i}
. By compactness of
E_{i}
, a finite union of
the neighborhoods
V_{e}
cover
E_{i}
and is contained in
V_{i}
. Define
U_{i}^{\prime}
to be this finite union.
Thus, we have found clopen sets

U_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,U_{n}^{\prime}
such that
E_{i}\subset U_{i}^{\prime}
.
Setting

U_{1}=U_{1}^{\prime}\cup(E\smallsetminus\cup_{i}U_{i})

and
U_{i}=U_{i}^{\prime}
for
i\geq 2
gives us the
desired cover.

Let
U_{i}^{G}
denote the ends of
\Sigma
accumulated
by genus that are in
U_{i}
. By Richard’s
classification
[
20
]
, there exist
surfaces
\Sigma_{i}
, each with a single
boundary component
\eta_{i}
, whose end spaces
are homeomorphic to
(U_{i},U_{i}^{G})
. If the genus of
\Sigma
is finite and equal
to
g
, we redefine
\Sigma_{1}
to be the
connect sum of
\Sigma_{1}
and a genus
g
surface.

Let
W
be the sphere with
n
connected boundary
components, labelled
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}
. By construction, and through Richard’s
classification,
\Sigma
is homeomorphic to
S\cup_{\eta_{i}\sim\gamma_{i}}\Sigma_{i}
, and the
image of
W
under any homeomorphism is a witness.
∎

3.
Disjoint Witnesses and the Grand Splitting

The goal of this section is to prove a lemma which describes when the grand arc graph contains two disjoint witnesses.
This lemma will help prove when the grand arc graph is
\delta
-hyperbolic.
For the remainder of this paper, let
\Sigma
be an infinite-type surface with a finite grand splitting, and let
E
be its end space.

To begin, we state a lemma which shows that there exists a simple closed curve which separates any two disjoint clopen neighborhoods in the end space.
This lemma is a restatement of Lemma 2.1 from
[
6
]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
E_{1},E_{2}
be two disjoint clopen neighbourhoods of the endspace,
E=E(\Sigma)
.
Then there exists a simple closed curve
\gamma
in
\Sigma
such that
\gamma
separates the ends in
E_{1}
from those in
E_{2}
.

Remark 3.2
.

If
E_{i}
consists of a single planar end
and
\Sigma
has genus, we can choose
\gamma
to be essential by shifting one genus to
\Sigma_{i}
. Furthermore, if
\Sigma
has at least
g
genus, we can choose
g
simple closed curves which are pairwise disjoint, and which separate
E_{1}
from
E_{2}
.

3.1.
Disjoint Witnesses and The Endspace

In this section, we prove that the grand splitting contains exactly two equivalence classes of maximal ends if and only if the grand arc graph admits at least two disjoint witnesses.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
be nonempty and let
|\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)|<\infty
. Suppose that
\Sigma
is neither the once-punctured Cantor tree, nor the cycloctopus (the once-punctured, genus 1 Cantor tree). Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

|\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)|=2
.

(2)

\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
has at least two disjoint witnesses.

(3)

\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
has at least two disjoint witnesses that are annuli.

(4)

has at least two disjoint witnesses,

and

Proof.

Using the classification of infinite-type surfaces

The image of

\implies

\implies

4.
Connectivity of the grand arc graph

In this section, we show that the grand arc graph has infinite diameter.
In addition, we show that for surfaces which have at least three elements in the grand splitting, the grand arc graph is connected.

To show that the grand arc graph has infinite diameter, we will use subsurface
projections, following the ideas of Schleimer
[
21
]
,
to project the grand arc graph to the arc graph of a witness. To show that
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
is connected, we will use unicorn paths as in
[
11
]
, but extended to the setting of infinite-type surfaces as in
[
6
]
.

To begin, we prove the following lemma which shows that the subsurface projection of a grand arc to a finite-type subsurface
{S\subset\Sigma}
will have finitely many connected components.
In the statement of the lemma, below, we let
\pi_{S}(\alpha)
denote the
subsurface projection of
\alpha
, as defined in
[
15
]
.
Namely, we define the projection map,
\pi_{S}(\alpha)
, from the grand arc graph,
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
, to the arc graph,
\mathcal{A}(S)
, by setting
\pi_{S}(\alpha)
to be the multi-arc
S\cap\alpha
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
be a grand arc, and let
S\subset\Sigma
be a finite-type
subsurface whose boundary is in minimal
position with
\alpha
.
Then
\pi_{S}(\alpha)
has finitely many
(possibly zero) connected components.

Remark 4.2
.

The condition on
\partial S
can be automatically satisfied
for any grand arc if we take a homotopy class of
S
with geodesic boundary with respect to some hyperbolic metric on
\Sigma
.

Proof.

The boundary of
S
is a finite collection of
simple closed curves on
\Sigma
in minimal
position with respect to
\alpha
. Reparametrizing the image of
\alpha
as
\alpha:\mathbb{R}\to\Sigma
, We find that there exists
an
M>0
such that the restriction of
\alpha
to
[-M,M]^{c}
,
\alpha_{[-M,M]^{c}}
, is disjoint from

\partial S
(this is because
\alpha
is a grand arc). Note
that the image of
[-M,M]
under
\alpha
is a compact
arc segment, so there exists a finite-type subsurface

K\subset\Sigma
containing
S,\partial S
and
\alpha_{[-M,M]}
.
The lemma follows by noticing that
\alpha
and

\partial S
can be realized as geodesics (in some hyperbolic metric on
\Sigma
) in minimal position on the finite type surface
K
.
∎

For a given finite-type surface
S\subset\Sigma
, let
\mathcal{A}(S)
be the arc complex of
S
, and let
\alpha
and
\beta
be two multi-arcs.
Then, as in
[
17
]
, we may define the distance between two multi-arcs by:

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{A}(S)}(\pi_{S}(\alpha),\pi_{S}(\beta))=\inf\{d_{\mathcal{A}(S)}(a,b):a\in\pi_{S}(\alpha),b\in\pi_{S}(\beta)\}

When
\alpha
and
\beta
are grand arcs, and
W
is a witness, the above infimum is realized as a minimum.

Let
W
be a witness of the grand arc graph.
The next lemma shows that the distance of two grand arcs in the grand arc graph is bounded below by the distance of their subsurface projections to the arc graph of the witness,
\mathcal{A}(W)
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
W\subset\Sigma
be a witness of
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
. Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
be grand arcs which are in minimal position
with respect to
\partial W
. Then

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{A}(W)}(\pi_{W}(\alpha),\pi_{W}(\beta))\leq d_{\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)}(\alpha,\beta).

Proof.

Let
\alpha=\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}=\beta
be a path in

\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
. Note that since
W
is a witness,
\pi_{S}(\alpha_{i})
must be nonempty.
For each
i
, choose an arc
a_{i}\in\pi_{S}(\alpha_{i})
.
Since
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
is a path between
\alpha
and
\beta
in the grand arc graph,
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
is a path between
\pi_{W}(\alpha)
and
\pi_{W}(\beta)
in
\mathcal{A}(W)
. The claim follows.
∎

Since we are able to define projections from
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
to
\mathcal{A}(W)
, where

is a witness of
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
, we can ask whether
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
is
quasi-isometric to the subcomplex of
\mathcal{A}(W)
where we only consider the arcs which start
and end at the boundary components which separate maximal ends.
While the general case is beyond the scope of this paper, there are examples where this is not the

, and

is a pair of pants. In this case,

has finite diameter, contradicting the following lemma:

If

Proof.

Let

Using Lemma

, we are now in a position where we can show that the grand arc graph is an infinite-diameter, connected metric space.

Proposition 4.5
.

If
|\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)|\geq 3
, then

\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
is an infinite-diameter,
connected metric space.

To prove this, we will use
unicorn paths
as introduced by Hensel–Przytycki–Webb
[
11
]
.
Unicorn paths were originally introduced for arcs which have a finite number of intersections
and were extended to the setting of infinitely many intersections by Fanoni–Ghaswala–McLeay
[
6
]
.

A
unicorn
in
\alpha
and
\beta
is an arc of the form
a\cup b
, where
a
is the closure of a connected component
of
\alpha{\smallsetminus}p
,
b
is a connected component of
\beta{\smallsetminus}p
, and
p
is an intersection point of
\alpha
and
\beta
. We call the
point
p
the
corner
of the unicorn.
Note that for
a\cup b
to be an arc,
a
and
b
can intersect only at
p
. In general, given two subarcs of
\alpha

and
\beta
with a common endpoint in
\Sigma
, there can be other intersection points, in which case the subarcs do not define a unicorn.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.4
, we have that
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
has infinite diameter when
\lvert\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)\rvert\geq 2
. Therefore, we need only show that
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
is a connected metric space. To do this, we show that between any two grand arcs
\alpha
and
\beta
, there exists a path in
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
between
\alpha
and
\beta
.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be grand arcs.
There are three main cases to consider:

(1)

The endpoints of
\alpha
and
\beta
all converge to different elements of the grand splitting.

(2)

One endpoint of
\alpha
converges to the same element of the grand splitting as one endpoint of
\beta
.

(3)

The endpoints of
\alpha
and
\beta

converge to the same elements of the grand splitting.

In the first case, if the endpoints of
\alpha

and of
\beta
are disjoint, then
\alpha

and
\beta
must intersect finitely many times.
This implies that there exists a finite unicorn path between
them. Note we can choose this unicorn path to be a path of grand arcs.

In the second case, let
e_{1},e_{2}
be the
endpoints of
\alpha
, and let
e_{1}^{\prime},e_{3}

be the endpoints of
\beta
. By assumption, there are
three elements of the grand splitting,
E_{1},E_{2},E_{3}

such that
e_{1}
and
e_{1}^{\prime}
converge to
E_{1}
,
e_{2}
converges to
E_{2}
, and
e_{3}
converges to
E_{3}
.
Let
\gamma_{1}
be the arc obtained by
fellow-travelling along
\alpha
from
e_{2}

to its first intersection point
p
with
\beta
,
then fellow-travelling along
\beta
from
p
converging to
e_{3}
. Note that
\gamma_{1}
is disjoint from

\beta
by construction. Moreover,

\gamma_{1}
is a grand arc intersecting
\alpha

finitely many times. We orient
\alpha

to start at
e_{1}
and end at
e_{2}
, and orient

\gamma_{1}
to start at
e_{3}
and end at
e_{2}
. Note
that since
|\gamma_{1}\cap\alpha|<\infty
,
there exists a unicorn path between these two oriented
arcs, which, together with
\gamma_{1}
, gives a
path from
\alpha
to
\beta
in
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
.

In the third case, let
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
be the
endpoints of
\alpha
and let
e_{1}^{\prime}
and

e_{2}^{\prime}
be the endpoints of
\beta
.
By assumption, we know that there are two elements of the grand splitting,
E_{1},E_{2}\in\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)
, such that
e_{1}
and
e_{1}^{\prime}
converge to
E_{1}
and
e_{2}
and
e_{2}^{\prime}
converge to
E_{2}
.
We will show that the third case reduces to the second case.
Indeed, notice that only two of the at least three equivalence classes of ends have
\alpha
and
\beta
converging towards them.
Choose one of the remaining equivalence classes of ends,
E_{3}
, and choose an arc
\gamma^{\prime}
which converges to the ends
e_{1}\in E_{1}
and
e_{3}\in E_{3}
.
If

\gamma^{\prime}
is disjoint from
\alpha
, then we are done.
If
\gamma^{\prime}
is not disjoint from
\alpha
, we set

\gamma
to be the unicorn obtained from

\alpha
and
\gamma^{\prime}
by fellow-travelling along

\gamma
until the first point of intersection
p

with
\alpha
, and then fellow-travelling from along
\alpha
from
p
and converging
towards
e_{1}
.
By construction,
\gamma
and
\alpha
are disjoint grand arcs so that only one end point of
\alpha
and one endpoint of
\beta
converge towards the same element of the grand splitting, which is precisely Case (2) and we have proven our claim.
∎

5.
Hyperbolicity of the grand arc graph

In this section, we provide a full characterization of when the grand arc graph is or is not hyperbolic. This, together with Proposition
4.5
will prove Theorem
1.1
.

5.1.
Grand arc graphs which are not hyperbolic

In this subsection, we prove that if a grand arc graph of a surface
\Sigma
admits two disjoint witnesses, then the grand arc graph is not
\delta
-hyperbolic.
To prove this result, we show that there exists a quasi-isometric embedding of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
into the grand arc graph for such a surface.
This is an analogue of Schleimer’s disjoint witnesses principle, which holds for surfaces of finite-type
[
21
]
. A shorter proof of this proposition can be found at the end of Subsection 6.2.

Proposition 5.1
.

Suppose that
|\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)|<\infty
.
If
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
is nonempty, and admits two disjoint witnesses

and
W_{2}

\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
is not
\delta
-hyperbolic.

Proof.

Since
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
are both witnesses, they must each
have at least two boundary components.
Let
\eta
be a grand arc which intersects exactly two of the boundary components of
\partial W_{1}
exactly once,
and exactly two of the boundary components of
\partial W_{2}
exactly once. Such an arc exists by taking simple arcs in
W_{i}
with appropriate boundaries, connecting them in the finite-type connected component of
{\Sigma\smallsetminus(W_{1}\cup W_{2})}
, and extending the resulting arc segment to a grand arc.

The

in

whose endpoints are

Using unicorn paths as in
[

]

Since

acts by isometries on the metric space

and
\varphi_{2}

Since the

]

[

]

Let

For any fixed

.

Since
\operatorname{Map}(\Sigma)
acts by isometries on
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
,
it follows that for any
m_{1},m_{2},m_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2C}(|m_{1}-m_{1}^{\prime}|+|m_{2}-m_{2}^{\prime}|)-D

\displaystyle\leq d_{\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)}(\eta,\eta^{m_{1}-m_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}-m_{2}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=d_{\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)}(\eta^{m_{1},m_{2}},\eta^{m_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\leq C(|m_{1}-m_{1}^{\prime}|+|m_{2}-m_{2}^{\prime}|)+D

which implies that
\{\eta^{m_{1},m_{2}}\}_{m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}}

forms a quasi-isometrically embedded copy of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
in

\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
. Thus,
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)

is not
\delta
-hyperbolic.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

The above proposition can be generalized to
cases when there are
k
(or infinitely-many) disjoint witnesses of sufficient
complexity, in which case

\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
has a quasi-isometrically
embedded copy of
\mathbb{Z}^{k}
(or
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}
).

A similar result along these lines can be found in
[
9
]

where it is shown that for surfaces containing an
essential shift
,
one can quasi-isometrically embed an infinite dimensional cube into the mapping class group of the surface.

5.2.
Grand arc graphs which are hyperbolic

In this section, we prove when the grand arc graph of a surface
\Sigma
is
\delta
-hyperbolic.
This section closely mirrors section
6 of
[
6
]
, which proves the hyperbolicity of the omnipresent arc graph.
The lemmas and corollaries in this section come from the work of Fanoni–Ghaswala–McLeay on omnipresent arcs, but the statements and proofs also hold in the case of grand arcs.

To begin, we define
grand unicorn paths
, an extension of the definition of unicorn paths which were introduced in Section
4
.
A
grand unicorn
is a unicorn that is
also a grand arc. If
\alpha
and
\beta
are grand
arcs, we let
\mathcal{U}_{G}(\alpha,\beta)
denote
the set of all grand unicorns originating
from
\alpha
and
\beta
and call this a
grand unicorn path
.

The following lemma is the analogue of Lemma 6.5 in the work of Fanoni–Ghaswala–McLeay
[
6
]
, but is based on Remark 3.2 of Hensel–Przytycki–Webb
[
11
]
.

Lemma 5.3

(Lemma 6.5 of
[
6
]
)
.

Let
x=a\cup b
be a grand unicorn in
\mathcal{U}_{G}(\alpha,\beta)
. Then one of the following holds:

•

\beta\smallsetminus b
intersects
a
, and then there exists a unicorn
y=a^{\prime}\cup b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{G}(\alpha,\beta)
which is disjoint from
x
, such that
a^{\prime}\subsetneq a
and
b\subsetneq b^{\prime}
. In particular, the corner of
y
belongs to the interior of
a
; or

•

\beta\smallsetminus b
and
a
are disjoint arc segments. In this case,
x
is disjoint from
\beta
.

A consequence of Lemma
5.3
is the following corollary, which is the analogue of Corollary 6.6 of
[
6
]
.
This corollary shows that if two grand arcs intersect a finite number of times, then the subgraph
\mathcal{U}_{G}(\alpha,\beta)
of
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
is connected.

Corollary 5.4

(Corollary 6.6 of
[
6
]
)
.

Suppose that
x=a\cup b\in\mathcal{U}_{G}(\alpha,\beta)
and
\beta\smallsetminus b
has finite intersection with
a
,
Then there exists a unicorn path between

x
and
\beta
in
\mathcal{U}_{G}(\alpha,\beta)
.

In the above lemma, we only considered the case where grand arcs intersect a finite number of times.
Therefore, it does not follow that
\mathcal{U}_{G}(\alpha,\beta)
is a connected subgraph of
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
when
\alpha
and
\beta
intersect an infinite number of times.
The next lemma shows that so long as
\alpha
and
\beta
intersect, then
\mathcal{U}_{G}(\alpha,\beta)
must contain a grand arc which is neither
\alpha
nor
\beta
.
While this lemma is stated in Fanoni–Ghaswala–McLeay in the case of omnipresent arcs, it extends to the case of grand arcs using the same reasoning.

Lemma 5.5

(Lemma 6.7 of
[
6
]
)
.

Let
\alpha,\beta
be two intersecting grand arcs. Then
\mathcal{U}_{G}(\alpha,\beta)
contains an arc
x
which is not equal to
\alpha
or
\beta

As noted earlier,
\mathcal{U}_{G}(\alpha,\beta)
may not be a connected subgraph of
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
.
However, the following lemma shows that the one-neighborhood of
\mathcal{U}_{G}(\alpha,\beta)
is a connected subgraph of
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
.

Lemma 5.6
.

Suppose
|\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)|\geq 3
. For any
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
, the full subgraph of
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
spanned by
N_{1}(\mathcal{U}_{G}(\alpha,\beta))

is connected.

The proof of this lemma is similar to the
proof of Lemma 6.8 in
[
6
]
,
where we replace
P
with
\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)
,

\mathcal{A}_{2}(\Sigma,P)
with
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
,

\mathcal{U}_{2}(\alpha,\beta)
with
\mathcal{U}_{G}(\alpha,\beta)
,
and the surface with a witness.

Proof.

Assume that
|\alpha\cap\beta|>0
, otherwise the result is clear. Fix a witness
W
.

Let
x_{0}=a_{0}\cup b_{0}\in\mathcal{U}_{G}(\alpha,\beta)

be a unicorn arc which is not equal to either
\alpha

or
\beta
, and let
p_{0}
be the corner of
x_{0}
.
Let
b_{0}^{\prime}
be the remaining arc segment of

\beta
, such that
\beta=b_{0}\cup b_{0}^{\prime}
.
If
|a_{0}\cap b_{0}^{\prime}|<\infty
, then
there is a finite unicorn path
from
x_{0}
to
\beta
by Corollary

5.4
. If not, then
there exists a sequence of unicorn paths,

x_{k}=b_{k}\cup a_{k}
such that

b_{k}\subsetneq b_{k+1}
, and whose
corners eventually leave
W
. In
particular, this means that for some
k
,

x_{k}\cap W=\beta\cap W
.

Let
E_{1},\ldots,E_{n}=\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)
be the
grand splitting of
\Sigma
.
Since
W
is a witness, for any

there exists
a finite multicurve,
C_{i}\subset\partial W
which separates
E_{i}
from
the other ends of
\Sigma
. Without
loss of generality, let the ends of

\beta
lie in
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
.

Let
\gamma_{S}
be a simple arc from a
boundary component in
C_{3}
to a point
in
S\cap\beta
. Let
\gamma_{3}
be an simple
arc starting at
\gamma_{S}\cap C_{3}
and converging
to an end in
E_{3}
, and let
b
be a
subarc of
\beta
starting at
\gamma_{3}\cap\beta

and converging to an end, such that

is
contained in

.
By construction,
\gamma_{3}*\gamma_{S}*b

is disjoint from
x_{k}
and from
\beta
,
proving the theorem.
∎

To prove that the grand arc graph is
\delta
-hyperbolic, we use a version of the “guessing geodesics lemma” by Bowditch
[
4
, Prop. 3.1]
and Masur–Schleimer
[
16
, Theorem 3.15]
.

Lemma 5.7
.

(Guessing geodesics lemma). Let
G
be a graph and suppose that for every pair of vertices

x,y\in G
there is an associated connected subgraph
g(x,y)
containing
x
and
y
. If there exists an
M>0
such
that:

(1)

if
d_{G}(x,y)\leq 1
, then
g(x,y)
has diameter at most
M
, and

(2)

for every
x,y,z\in G
,
g(x,y)
is contained in the
M
-neighbourhood of
g(x,z)\cup g(y,z)
,

then
G
is
\delta
-hyperbolic, where
\delta
depends only on
M
.

Similar to the work of Fanoni–Ghaswala–McLeay
[
6
]
, we can show that the above lemma applies in our situation by letting
g(\alpha,\beta)=\mathcal{N}_{1}(\mathcal{U}_{G}(\alpha,\beta))
.

Lemma 5.8
.

Assume
|\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)|\geq 3
. Then
g(\alpha,\beta)=\mathcal{N}_{1}(\mathcal{U}_{G}(\alpha,\beta))
yields connected subgraphs such that:

(1)

if
d_{\mathcal{G}}(\alpha,\beta)\leq 1
, then
g(\alpha,\beta)
has diameter at most 3, and

(2)

for every
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
,
g(\alpha,\beta)
is contained in the 3-neighbourhood of
g(\alpha,\gamma)\cup g(\beta,\gamma)
.

In particular, there is a uniform constant
\delta
(independent of
\Sigma
or
\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)
) such that if
|\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)|\geq 3
then
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)

is
\delta
-hyperbolic.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.6
,
g(\alpha,\beta)=\mathcal{N}_{1}(\mathcal{U}_{G}(\alpha,\beta))
is a connected subgraph of
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
.
Suppose first that
d_{\mathcal{G}}(\alpha,\beta)\leq 1
.
This implies that the grand arcs
\alpha
and
\beta
are disjoint.
Therefore,
g(\alpha,\beta)=\mathcal{N}_{1}(\mathcal{U}_{G}(\alpha,\beta))=\mathcal{N}_{1}(\alpha)\cup\mathcal{N}_{1}(\beta)
, which immediately implies that
g(\alpha,\beta)
has distance at most
3
, proving
(1)
.

We now wish to show that for every
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
, the subgraph
g(\alpha,\beta)
is contained in the 3-neighbourhood of
g(\alpha,\gamma)\cup g(\beta,\gamma)
.
This proof is the same as the proof by Fanoni-Ghaswala-McLeay, but in place of considering the precise endpoints of the grand arcs, we consider the elements of the grand splitting which the grand arc converges to.
∎

As a corollary to Lemma
5.8
, we find that if every two witnesses of the grand arc graph intersect, then the grand arc graph is
\delta
-hyperbolic.

We end this section by summarizing the results of the previous
lemmas and corollaries into a complete classification of when
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
is
\delta
-hyperbolic:

Proposition 5.9
.

Suppose
\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)
is finite. Then:

•

If
|\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)|\leq 1
, then
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
is empty.

•

If
1<|\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)|\leq 2
, and
\Sigma
is not the once-punctured cantor tree, then
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
is not
\delta
-hyperbolic.

•

If
|\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)|\geq 3
or
\Sigma
is the once-punctured
cantor tree, then
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
is
\delta
-hyperbolic.

This, together with Proposition
4.5
, gives a proof of Theorem
1.1
.

6.
The action of the mapping class group

The action of the mapping class group on
the grand arc graph is not continuous.
This can be shown by following the proof of Proposition 6.3 in
[
6
]
. For
example, if the maximal ends are finite-orbit ends, then the grand arc graph is precisely the omnipresent arc graph and the proof follows exactly.
However, in this section we show that the
action of the mapping class group on a
\delta
-hyperbolic grand arc graph is quasi-continuous, a weaker notion of continuity. Using this, we show that the action on the visible boundary of a
\delta
-hyperbolic
grand arc graph is continuous.

To begin this section, we introduce the notion of quasi-continuity.
We then prove a lemma which tells us that we can choose our witness to have some helpful properties.
This lemma will help us prove that the action of the mapping class group on a
\delta
-hyperbolic grand arc graph is quasi-continuous, and conclude that the action on the boundary is continuous.

Next, we construct examples of loxodromic actions of the mapping class group on the grand arc graph.
Finally, we will introduce the notion on stability and prove that for infinite-type surfaces which are stable, the mapping class group acts on the grand arc graph with finitely many orbits.

6.1.
Quasi-continuity & continuous action on the boundary

The goal of this subsection is to prove that the action of the mapping class group on a
\delta
-hyperbolic grand arc graph is quasi-continuous. Using this, we will show that the action of the mapping class group on the visible boundary of a
\delta
-hyperbolic grand arc graph is continuous.

Quasi-continuity

We begin this subsection by defining quasi-continuity by finding an equivalent condition for continuity of a topological group on a discrete graph, and then relaxing the definition.

Throughout this section, for a group
G
acting on a set
X
, if
U\subset G
, and
A\subset X
, then we denote
U\circ A=\{ga:g\in U,a\in A\}
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
G
be a topological group acting on a discrete metric space
X
. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

•

The action
G\times X\to X
defined by
\pi(g,x)=gx
is continuous.

•

For any
x\in X
there exists an open neighbourhood
id\in U_{x}\subset G
such that
U_{x}\circ\{x\}=\{x\}
.

Proof.

Let
x\in X
be arbitrary. If the action
\pi:G\times X\to X
is continuous, then in particular, we must have that
\pi^{-1}(\{x\})
is
open. Since
(id,x)\in\pi^{-1}(\{x\})
,
and by discreteness of
X
, there must be an open neighbourhood
U_{x}\times\{x\}\in\pi^{-1}(\{x\})
. By definition of
\pi
, it follows that
U_{x}\circ\{x\}=\{x\}
, and we’re done.

Next, we suppose that for any
x\in X
, there exists an open neighbourhood
id\in U_{x}\subset G
such that
U_{x}\circ\{x\}=\{x\}
.
Fix some
x\in X
.
We wish to show that
\pi^{-1}(\{x\})
is open. For
(h,y)\in\pi^{-1}(\{x\})\subset G\times X
we let
U_{y}
be an open neighbourhood of
id
such that

U_{y}\circ\{y\}=\{y\}
. Since the group action of
G
on itself is by homeomorphisms, it follows that

hU_{y}
is an open neighbourhood of
h
. Moreover,
hU_{y}\circ\{y\}=\{x\}
, meaning that

hU_{y}\times\{y\}
is an open neighbourhood of

(h,y)
contained in
\pi^{-1}(\{x\})
. Thus,

\pi^{-1}(\{x\})
is open, and since
X
is discrete, this completes the proof.
∎

To define quasi-continuity, we first recall the definition of Hausdorff distance.
Let
Y
and
Z
be two non-empty subsets of a metric space
(X,d)
.
The
Hausdorff distance
is defined to be

\displaystyle d_{X}(Y,Z)

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\sup\limits_{y\in Y}d(y,Z),\sup\limits_{z\in Z}d(Y,z)\right\}

where
d(a,B)=\inf\limits_{b\in B}d(a,b)
is defined for any
a\in X
and
B\subset X
.
Using the definition of Hausdorff distance, we define the notion of
quasi-continuity
:

Definition 6.2
.

Let
G
be a group acting on a metric space
X
. We
say that the action is
N
-quasi-continuous

if for any
x\in X
there exists an open
neighbourhood
id\in U_{x}\subset G
such that
d_{X}(U_{x}\circ\{x\},\{x\})\leq N
.
We say that the action is
quasi-continuous
if it is
N
-quasi-continuous for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

Remark 6.3
.

The action of a topological group on a metric
space is continuous if and only if for every

\varepsilon>0
, it is
\varepsilon
-quasi-continuous.

Remark 6.4
.

In the above definition, if an action is quasi-continuous with constant
N=0
, Lemma
6.1
asserts that the action is continuous.

Proposition 6.5
.

If
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
is
\delta
-hyperbolic, then the action
\pi:\operatorname{Map}(\Sigma)\times\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)\to\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
is quasi-continuous.

Before we prove Proposition
6.5
, we will prove the following technical lemma, which tells us that we are able to choose our witness to have some helpful properties:

Lemma 6.6
.

Suppose
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
is nonempty. Then for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
, there
exists a witness
S_{\alpha}\subset\Sigma
,
such that
S_{\alpha}\cap\alpha
is connected.

Proof.

Let
W
be a witness, and let
e_{1},e_{2}
be the endpoints of
\alpha
. Let
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
be two flare surfaces as in Lemma 3.1 of
[
6
]
for which
U_{i}\cap W=\emptyset
,
\partial U_{i}
is a simple closed curve,
|\partial U_{i}\cap\alpha|=1
, and
e_{i}
lies in
U_{i}
. Since
\alpha
converges to
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
, it follows that the arc segment
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha\cap(U_{1}\cup U_{2})^{c}
is compact. Let
X
be a regular neighbourhood of
\alpha^{\prime}\cup\partial U_{1}\cup\partial U_{2}
. Note that
X
is a finite-type surface intersecting

, since

is a witness. The surface
S_{\alpha}=W\cup X
is then the desired surface.
∎

Using Lemma
6.6
, we can now prove Proposition
6.5
.

Proof of Proposition
6.5
.

Let
\alpha
be a grand arc. We will find an

id\in U\subset G

\sup_{h\in U}d(h\alpha,\alpha)<3

Let

\Sigma_{1}

Thinking of the arc

Continuous Action on
\partial\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)

In this subsection, we show that the action of the mapping class group on the visible boundary is continuous.
Let
X
be a
\delta
-hyperbolic metric space, and let
\partial X
be its visible
boundary with respect to some basepoint

x_{0}\in X
.

Theorem 6.7
.

If
G
acts on
X
quasi-continuously by
isometries, then the action of
G
on
\partial X
is continuous in the sense that the map
\pi:G\times\partial X\to\partial X
is continuous.

Proof.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
G
acts on
X

N
-quasi-continuously. Let
\alpha:\mathbb{N}\to X
be a quasi-geodesic ray, and let
g_{0},g_{1},\ldots
be a sequence
of group elements converging to
g
. Without loss of generality, assume that

g_{n}\to\id
. We will show that the geodesics
\{g_{n}\circ\alpha\}_{n}

coarsely converge to
\alpha
uniformly on compact sets. Let
K\subset\mathbb{N}
be compact,
and contained in
\{0,\ldots,M\}
. Let
U_{\alpha(1)}\ldots,U_{\alpha(M)}

be neighbourhoods of the identity in
G
for which

d_{X}(U_{\alpha(i)}\circ\alpha(i),\alpha(i))<N
. In particular, it
follows that
U=\cap_{i\leq M}U_{\alpha(i)}
is an open neighbourhood of the
identity for which
d_{X}(U\circ\alpha(i),\alpha(i))<N
for any
i\leq M
. Since

\{g_{n}\}
converge to the identity, it follows that after a certain point,

g_{n}\in U
. Thus, for all
n>N_{0}
, we have
d_{X}(g_{n}\circ\alpha(i),\alpha(i))\leq N

for all
i\leq M
, meaning that
g_{n}\circ\alpha
converges to
\alpha
on compact
sets up to error bounded by
N
. In other words, the sequence
\{[g_{n}\alpha]\}

converges to
\alpha
in
\partial X
, and the action of
G
on
\partial X
is continuous.
∎

In particular, it follows that if
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
is
\delta
-hyperbolic, then the action of
\operatorname{Map}(\Sigma)
on
\partial\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
is continuous.

6.2.
Loxodromic actions

In this subsection, we construct examples of loxodromic actions of
\operatorname{Map}(\Sigma)
on the grand arc graph coming from pseudo-Anosov mapping classes defined on witnesses. More precisely, we prove the following theorem:

Theorem
1.5
.

Let
W
be a witness, and let
\varphi

be a pseudo-Anosov mapping class that fixes the boundary of
W
. Let
\bar{\varphi}\in\operatorname{Map}(\Sigma)
be the homeomorphism fixing

W^{c}
and acting as
\varphi
on
W
. Then

\bar{\varphi}
acts loxodromically on
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
is
\delta
-hyperbolic,
it suffices to show that the translation
length of
\bar{\varphi}
is bounded from below. To do this, we examine
\varphi
acting on the arc complex of a witness, like in the proof of Proposition
5.1
.

Let
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}=\bar{\varphi}(\alpha_{0})
be a path in

\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
from an arc
\alpha_{0}
to its image under
\bar{\varphi}
. We choose
\alpha_{i}
to lie in minimal position with respect to the boundary of
W
. For each
0\leq i<n
, let
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}(W)
be a connected component of
W\cap\alpha_{i}
, and let
a_{n}=\varphi(a_{0})
. Since the
\alpha_{i}
’s are in minimal position, this guarantees that
a_{0},\ldots,a_{n}
is a path in
\mathcal{A}(W)
. Since
\varphi
acts loxodromically on
\mathcal{A}(W)
, it follows that its translation length is bounded from below by some
K\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus,
n\geq K
, which proves the claim.
∎

In fact, the theorem above can be used to give a much shorter proof of Proposition
5.1
. Recall that Proposition
5.1
states that if
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
admits two disjoint witnesses, each with Euler characteristic less than
-2
, then
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
is not
\delta
-hyperbolic.
The proof is as follows:

Proof of Proposition
5.1
using Theorem
1.5
.

If
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
were

\delta
-hyperbolic,
then Theorem
1.5
would
give two commuting loxodromic actions coming from the witnesses
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
, contradicting hyperbolicity of the grand arc graph.
∎

While the above proof is much shorter, it also leaves out the explicit construction of a quasi-isometrically embedded copy of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
into
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
.

6.3.
Finitely many orbits

In this subsection, we prove that the mapping class group acts on the grand arc graph with finitely many orbits when
\Sigma
is a surface whose maximal ends are stable in the sense of
[
6
,
13
]
:

Definition 6.8
.

Let
e
be an end of a surface. A clopen neighbourhood
U
of
e
is
stable
if for every
clopen neighbourhood
V\subset U
there is a clopen neighbourhood
U^{\prime}\subset V
such that

(U,U\cap E^{G}(\Sigma))\cong(U^{\prime},U^{\prime}\cap E^{G}(\Sigma))
.
We say that an end is stable if it admits a stable neighbourhood, and is unstable otherwise.

By Lemma 5.1 in
[
6
]
, two ends are locally
homeomorphic if and only if they are equivalent in
the partial ordering on the space of ends defined by Mann-Rafi. As an immediate corollary,
it follows that there exists a homeomorphism of
the end-space of
\Sigma
sending a stable end
e
to a stable end
f
if and only if
e\sim f
.

Proposition 6.9
.

Let
\Sigma
be an infinite-type surface whose maximal ends are stable.
If
|\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)|\geq 2
, then

\operatorname{Map}(\Sigma)
acts on
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
with
finitely many orbits.

Proof.

To prove this claim, we show that if
\alpha
and
\beta
have endpoints which lie in the same equivalence classes of ends, then they are equal up to a homeomorphism.

Suppose first that
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)

have exactly the same endpoints,
e
and
f
.
Then by Lemma 3.3 of
[
6
]
and the classification of infinite-type surfaces
[
20
]
,
it follows that
\Sigma\smallsetminus\alpha

and
\Sigma\smallsetminus\beta
are both homeomorphic
to the surface
\Sigma^{\prime}
which has the same genus as
\Sigma

and has end space homeomorphic to
E(\Sigma)/(e\sim f)
.
Thus,
\alpha
and
\beta
are the same up to homeomorphism, by
[
2
]
.

Next, assume that
\alpha
and
\beta
each have one endpoint in common, and their remaining endpoints,

e_{\alpha}
and
e_{\beta}
respectively, lie in
the same equivalence class of maximal
ends. Each equivalence class of
maximal ends is either finite or a Cantor set
[
13
]
, and in particular, there must be a homeomorphism of
E(\Sigma)
sending
e_{\alpha}
to
e_{\beta}
. Such a homeomorphism is induced by a mapping class
[
2
]
.
Therefore, up to homeomorphism, we can can assume that
the endpoints of
\alpha
and the endpoints of
\beta
are the same.

Finally, let
e_{\alpha}
and
f_{\alpha}
be the endpoints of
\alpha
and
e_{\beta}
and
f_{\beta}
be the endpoints of
\beta
.
Assume that the endpoints
e_{\alpha}
and
e_{\beta}
lie in the same equivalence class of ends, and the endpoints
f_{\alpha}
and
f_{\beta}
lie in the same equivalence class of ends.
By the argument above, we may assume that, up to a homeomorphism,
\alpha
and
\beta
have the same endpoints.

Thus, up to applying mapping classes, there are precisely
\binom{m}{2}
orbits of grand arcs, where
m
is the number of equivalence classes of maximal ends, and the lemma follows.
∎

By combining Proposition
6.9
with Proposition
6.5
, we prove Theorem
1.3
which states that the mapping class group acts by isometries on the grand arc graph, that the action is of the mapping class group on a
\delta
-hyperbolic grand arc graph is quasi-continuous, and that if
\Sigma
is a stable surface of infinite-type then the mapping class group acts on the grand arc graph with finitely many orbits.

As a corollary of Proposition
6.9
, we find that when the maximal ends are stable, the quotient space
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)/\operatorname{Map}(\Sigma)
is a clique of size
\binom{m}{2}
, where
m
is the number equivalence classes of maximal ends.
To see that the quotient is a clique, we notice that for any quadruple of elements in the grand splitting,
E_{1},E_{2},E_{3}
, and
E_{4}
, we can construct disjoint arcs whose four endpoints lie in
E_{1},E_{2},E_{3}
and
E_{4}
.

Corollary 6.10
.

Suppose
\Sigma
is an infinite-type surface whose maximal ends are stable.
If
|\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)|<\infty
, then

\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)/\operatorname{Map}(\Sigma)
is a clique of
size
\binom{m}{2}
, where
m
is the number of equivalence classes of maximal ends.

In particular, when
\Sigma
has exactly one equivalence class of maximal ends, then the mapping class group acts transitively on
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
.
