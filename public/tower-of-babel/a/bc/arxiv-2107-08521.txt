Non-degeneracy and quantitative stability of half-harmonic maps from ℝ \mathbb{R} to 𝕊 \mathbb{S}
Bin Deng, Liming Sun, Jun-cheng Wei

Abstract
. We consider half-harmonic maps from ℝ \mathbb{R} (or 𝕊 \mathbb{S} ) to 𝕊 \mathbb{S} . We prove that all (finite energy) half-harmonic maps are non-degenerate. In other words, they are integrable critical points of the energy functional. A full description of the kernel of the linearized operator around each half-harmonic map is given. The second part of this paper devotes to studying the quantitative stability of half-harmonic maps. When its degree is ± 1 \pm 1 , we prove that the deviation of any map 𝒖 : ℝ → 𝕊 \boldsymbol{u}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{S} from Möbius transformations can be controlled uniformly by ‖ 𝒖 ‖ H ˙ 1 / 2 ​ ( ℝ ) 2 − deg ​ 𝒖 \|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}-\text{deg\,}\boldsymbol{u} . This result resembles the quantitative rigidity estimate of degree ± 1 \pm 1 harmonic maps ℝ 2 → 𝕊 2 \mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{S}^{2} which is proved recently. Furthermore, we address the quantitative stability for half-harmonic maps of higher degree. We prove that if 𝒖 \boldsymbol{u} is already near to a Blaschke product, then the deviation of 𝒖 \boldsymbol{u} to Blaschke products can be controlled by ‖ 𝒖 ‖ H ˙ 1 / 2 ​ ( ℝ ) 2 − deg ​ 𝒖 \|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}-\text{deg\,}\boldsymbol{u} . Additionally, a striking example is given to show that such quantitative estimate can not be true uniformly for all 𝒖 \boldsymbol{u} of degree 2. We conjecture similar things happen for harmonic maps ℝ 2 → 𝕊 2 \mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{S}^{2} .

Introduction

1.1.
Motivation and main results

The analysis of critical points of conformal invariant lagrangians has drawn much attention since 1950, due to their important applications in physics and geometry. One of the prominent examples is harmonic maps
\boldsymbol{u}:\Omega\to\mathbb{S}^{n}

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}dx.

When the domain
\Omega
is a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}

\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})
is conformally invariant and this plays a crucial role in the regularity theory of such maps (see
Hélein 1990
,
Rivière 2007
and references therein). The theory has been generalized to even-dimensional domains whose critical are called poly-harmonic maps.

In the recent years, many authors are interested in the analog of Dirichlet energy in odd-dimensional
cases, for instance,

Da Lio and Rivière 2011a

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{4}}\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}dx.

The second part of this paper deals with the quantitative stability of half-harmonic maps. To describe it, we note that half-harmonic maps achieve the minimum of
\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{S})
norm in its homotopy class. Namely

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose that
u\in\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{S})

\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\geq|\text{deg\,}u|
. If the equality holds, then

A natural question is that whether the discrepancy

from the half-harmonic maps. Naively, one expects a linear control as

(1.6)

\displaystyle\inf_{\vartheta\in\mathbb{S},\vec{\alpha}\in\mathbb{H}^{d}}\|u-\psi_{\vartheta,\vec{\alpha}}\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\leq C\left(\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}-|\text{deg\,}u|\right)

where
C
is independent of
u
.

Such type of question has been raised for many other topics. For instance,
Brezis and Lieb 1985
ask a similar question of the classical Sobolev inequality on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Later

24

2

. Reader can see their papers and reference therein.

Recently

similar to (

(1.7)

\displaystyle D(u)=\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}-|\text{deg\,}u|.

Theorem 1.3
.

For
u\in\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{S})
with
\text{deg\,}u=1
, there exists an
\alpha\in\mathbb{H}
and

(1.8)

\displaystyle\|u-\psi_{\vartheta,\alpha}\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\leq 36D(u).

If
\text{deg\,}u=-1
, then the above statement holds with
\bar{u}
.

The above theorem leaves us an intriguing question for half-harmonic maps with higher degree. The answer to this could shed some light to harmonic maps with higher degree. We find that there are some fundamental differences between the case degree
\pm 1
and higher degree. For instance, in degree 1,
\psi_{\vartheta,\alpha}
is equivalent to

For any

\displaystyle\inf_{\vartheta\in\mathbb{S},\vec{\alpha}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}}\|u-\psi_{\vartheta,\vec{\alpha}}\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\geq M\left(\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}-2\right).

For any compact set

1.2.
Comments on the proofs

We briefly sketch the main idea behind the proofs of Theorem
1.1
-
1.5
. To show the non-degeneracy, we start with the harmonic extension (denote as
\boldsymbol{U}
) of any half-harmonic map
\boldsymbol{u}
to
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
. It has been proven by
[
27
,
26
]
that the Hopf differential of
\boldsymbol{U}

(1.12)

\displaystyle\Phi=(|\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{U}|^{2}-|\partial_{y}\boldsymbol{U}|^{2})-2\textup{i\,}\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{U}\cdot\partial_{y}\boldsymbol{U}\equiv 0.

This actually implies
U
must be a holomorphic or anti-holomorphic function on
\mathbb{H}
.
If

, then we anticipate its harmonic extension

is also holomorphic or anti-holomorphic on
\mathbb{H}
. This is done by considering

(1.13)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}=2U_{z}\bar{V}_{z}=\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{U}\cdot\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{V}-\partial_{y}\boldsymbol{U}\cdot\partial_{y}\boldsymbol{V}-\textup{i\,}(\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{U}\cdot\partial_{y}\boldsymbol{V}+\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{V}\cdot\partial_{y}\boldsymbol{U}).

Note that
\tilde{\Phi}
can be thought of the derivative of
\Phi
in (
1.12
). One can show that
\tilde{\Phi}\equiv 0
which implies
V
is (anti-)holomorphic on
\mathbb{H}
when
U
is (anti-)holomorphic. A crucial step is defining
W=V/U
. Since
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=0
on
\mathbb{R}
, then
W
is purely imaginary on
\partial\mathbb{H}
. By Scharwz reflection, we can extend
W
to a meromorphic function on
\mathbb{C}
. One can show that
W
has no essential pole at infinity and thus
W
is a rational functions. By counting the dimension of admissible rational functions, we get
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\ker L_{u}=2|\text{deg\,}u|+1
. This exactly matches the dimension of parameters generating nearby half-harmonic maps.

The proof of Theorem
1.3
follows closely the approach in
[
22
]
. They use harmonic polynomials to do the computation there, while here we use Fourier series of functions
\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{S}
instead. Thanks to the fact that the
\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})
norm and degree of any map

can be written explicitly using the coefficients of Fourier series, the proof here is much shorter than that in
[
22
]
. The proof of Theorem
1.5
is a carefully refinement of the case degree
\pm 1
and induction. Non-degeneracy can be used to prove quantitative stability estimates in some cases. The reason behind this is that the linearized operator has a spectral gap on the orthogonal space of its kernel. For instance, one can see
[
4
]
. For similar approaches on fractional Sobolev inequality, one can see
[
9
]
. There might be possible to prove Theorem
1.5
using the non-degeneracy result we have shown. Since using Fourier series is more direct, we did not pursue this direction.

To get an example violating the uniform quantitative stability, we choose to perturb
\psi_{\vec{\alpha}}
with
\alpha_{1}=j^{2}+\textup{i\,}
and
\alpha_{2}=-j^{2}+\textup{i\,}
. For any

and the other part

in the orthogonal space. If

is almost orthogonal to the kernel of the linearized operator at

, then one still gets (

).
As

,
\psi_{\alpha_{1}}
and
\psi_{\alpha_{2}}
has very weak interaction. The four elements of

and

2.
Preliminary

In this section, we lay some foundations for half-harmonic maps. There are various perspective to define them.

2.1.
Formulations on the real line

Definition 2.1
.

For any
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define

(2.1)

\displaystyle(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}f(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}P.V.\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{|x-y|^{2}}dy.

We call
f\in\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})
if

(2.2)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}:=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\iint_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}dxdy<\infty.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},u_{2})
is a map from
\mathbb{R}
into
\mathbb{S}
. Here and the following we always assume
\mathbb{S}
is embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{2}=\mathbb{C}
.
Define the energy of
\boldsymbol{u}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{S}
by

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{u}dx.

Using
|\boldsymbol{u}|=1
, it is easy to verify that

(2.4)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}\cdot(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{u}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}P.V.\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|\boldsymbol{u}(x)-\boldsymbol{u}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}dy.

Consequently

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})=\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}=\|u_{1}\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}+\|u_{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}.

The functional
\mathcal{E}
is invariant under the trace of conformal maps keeping invariant the half-space
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
: the Möbius group.

The critical points of
\mathcal{E}
are called
half-harmonic maps
.

Definition 2.2
.

A map
\boldsymbol{u}\in\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{S})
is called a weak half-harmonic map if for any
\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\dot{H}^{1/2}\left(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{2}\right)\cap

L^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{2}\right)
there holds

\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\mathcal{E}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{u}+t\boldsymbol{\phi}}{|\boldsymbol{u}+t\boldsymbol{\phi}|}\right)=0.

Computing the associated Euler-Lagrange equation of (
2.3
), we obtain that if
\boldsymbol{u}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}
is a half-harmonic map, then
\boldsymbol{u}
satisfies the following equation:

(2.6)

\displaystyle\left(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{u}(x)=\left(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{u}\right)\boldsymbol{u}(x)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ in }\mathbb{R}.

All the half-harmonic maps (with finite energy) have been classified by
Millot and Sire 2015
.
In the sequel, we identify
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
writing

. We shall write

for any map
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},u_{2})

\displaystyle\psi_{\vartheta,\vec{\alpha}}:=e^{i\vartheta}\prod_{k=1}^{d}\frac{z-\alpha_{k}}{z-\bar{\alpha}_{k}}.

Furthermore,

(2.8)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\left|\nabla U\right|^{2}dz=d.

The above theorem says that the Stereographic projection

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(x)=\left(\frac{2x}{x^{2}+1},\frac{x^{2}-1}{x^{2}+1}\right):\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}

(2.10)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{S}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}P.V.\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathcal{S}(x)-\mathcal{S}(y)}{|x-y|^{2}}dy

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{4x}{\left(1+x^{2}\right)^{2}},\frac{2\left(x^{2}-1\right)}{\left(1+x^{2}\right)^{2}}\right),

(2.11)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|\mathcal{S}(x)-\mathcal{S}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}dy

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{1+x^{2}}.

It is easy to verify that
\mathcal{S}
satisfies (
2.6
). Therefore it is a half-harmonic map from
\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{S}
.

2.2.
Formulations on the unit circle

Using the Stereographic projection in (
2.9
),
one can reformulate the problem by the maps from
\mathbb{S}
to
\mathbb{S}
.
To that end, we parametrize
\mathbb{S}=\{e^{\textup{i\,}\theta}:\theta\in[0,2\pi)\}
. Let
\mathcal{S}(x)=e^{\textup{i\,}\theta}
,
\mathcal{S}(y)=e^{\textup{i\,}\vartheta}
, then

|e^{\textup{i\,}\theta}-e^{\textup{i\,}\vartheta}|^{2}=|\mathcal{S}(x)-\mathcal{S}(y)|^{2}=\frac{4(x-y)^{2}}{(x^{2}+1)(y^{2}+1)}.

Using
x=\mathcal{S}^{-1}(\theta):=\mathcal{S}^{-1}(e^{\textup{i\,}\theta})
, we can write
f(x)
defined on
\mathbb{R}
to
\tilde{f}(\theta)
defined on
\mathbb{S}
. By the above identity and (
2.1
), we obtain

(2.12)

\displaystyle(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}f(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}P.V.\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{|x-y|^{2}}dy=\frac{2}{1+x^{2}}\frac{1}{\pi}P.V.\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{\tilde{f}(\theta)-\tilde{f}(\vartheta)}{|e^{\textup{i\,}\theta}-e^{\textup{i\,}\vartheta}|^{2}}d\vartheta.

This leads to the definition of
(-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}})^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

Definition 2.4
.

For any
\tilde{f}:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define the

(2.13)

\displaystyle(-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{f}(\theta):=\frac{1+x^{2}}{2}(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}f(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}P.V.\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{\tilde{f}(\theta)-\tilde{f}(\vartheta)}{|e^{\textup{i\,}\theta}-e^{\textup{i\,}\vartheta}|^{2}}d\vartheta.

We call
\tilde{f}\in\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})
if and only if

\displaystyle\|\tilde{f}\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})}:=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\iint_{\mathbb{S}\times\mathbb{S}}\frac{|\tilde{f}(\theta)-\tilde{f}(\vartheta)|^{2}}{|e^{\textup{i\,}\theta}-e^{\textup{i\,}\vartheta}|^{2}}d\vartheta d\theta<\infty.

Using (
2.12
), it is easy to see that for any
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{S}
,

(2.14)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}=\|f\circ\mathcal{S}^{-1}\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})}.

Therefore
\mathcal{S}
is an isometric isomorphism of
\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})
and
\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})
.

Now for any map
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},u_{2}):\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{S}
, we still adopt the notation
u=u_{1}+\textup{i\,}u_{2}
is a complex-valued function, we define
(-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}})^{\frac{1}{2}}u=(-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}})^{\frac{1}{2}}u_{1}+\textup{i\,}(-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}})^{\frac{1}{2}}u_{2}
and

(2.15)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}:=\|u_{1}\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}+\|u_{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}=\textup{Re\,}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\bar{u}(-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}})^{\frac{1}{2}}u.

Here we have used the multiplication of two complex numbers and Re denotes the real part. One readily derive that
\|\text{id}_{\mathbb{S}}\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}=1
.

One also define the energy as
\mathcal{E}(u)=\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}
and the the critical points of
\mathcal{E}
are called half-harmonic maps
\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{S}
, which satisfy

(2.16)

\displaystyle(-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}})^{\frac{1}{2}}u(\theta)=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}P.V.\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{|u(\theta)-u(\vartheta)|^{2}}{|e^{\textup{i\,}\theta}-e^{\textup{i\,}\vartheta}|^{2}}d\vartheta\right)u(\theta)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on }\mathbb{S}.

The isometry in (
2.14
) infers the one-to-one correspondence of half-harmonic maps between
\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{S}
and
\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{S}
through the stereographic projection.

One of the great advantages of working on
\mathbb{S}
is that we have the Fourier expansion. Namely for any
u:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{C}
, we formally have

u=\sum_{-\infty}^{\infty}c_{k}e^{\textup{i\,}k\theta}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{k}z^{k},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where }c_{k}=\fint_{\mathbb{S}}uz^{-k}d\theta.

Using the fact that
(-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}})^{\frac{1}{2}}z^{k}=|k|z^{k}
for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
(for instance, see
[
34
]
), we have the following interpretation of
(-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}})^{\frac{1}{2}}
,

(-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}})^{\frac{1}{2}}u=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|k|c_{k}z^{k},

consequently (
2.15
) implies

(2.17)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|k||c_{k}|^{2}.

Furthermore, if
u
maps
\mathbb{S}
to
\mathbb{S}
, then
|u|=1
is equivalent to

(2.18)

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}|c_{j}|^{2}=1,\hskip 10.00002pt\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\bar{c}_{j}c_{j+k}=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\,k\in\mathbb{Z}.

Recall that the degree (or winding number) is well defined for
\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S};\mathbb{S})
by
de Monvel-Berthier et al. 1991
(see also
Brezis and Nirenberg 1995
). More precisely,

(2.19)

\displaystyle\text{deg\,}u=\frac{1}{2\pi\textup{i\,}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\bar{u}du=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}}u\wedge u_{\theta}d\theta=\sum_{-\infty}^{\infty}k|c_{k}|^{2}.

Throughout this paper, we use the notation
(z_{1}+\textup{i\,}z_{2})\wedge(w_{1}+\textup{i\,}w_{2})=z_{1}w_{2}-z_{2}w_{1}
.
From the above equation and (
2.17
), one can see that the degree is continuous in
\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S};\mathbb{S})
topology. For
u\in\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{S})
, we shall define
\text{deg\,}u=\text{deg\,}u\circ\mathcal{S}^{-1}
. We have the following simple fact.

Lemma 2.5
.

If
u,v\in\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S};\mathbb{S})
, then
\text{deg\,}(uv)=\text{deg\,}u+\text{deg\,}v
.

Proof.

Since
u,v
take values in
\mathbb{S}
, then using (
2.19
) to obtain

\displaystyle\text{deg\,}(uv)=\frac{1}{2\pi\textup{i\,}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\bar{u}\bar{v}d(uv)=\frac{1}{2\pi\textup{i\,}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\bar{u}du+\bar{v}dv=\text{deg\,}u+\text{deg\,}v.

∎

Define
\mathbb{D}=\{\xi=x+\textup{i\,}y:|\xi|<1\}
and consider
\mathbb{S}=\partial\mathbb{D}
. Recall that
f\in\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})
if and only if it is the trace of some function in
\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{D})
. The energy for
u\in\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S};\mathbb{S})
has a tractable representation

(2.20)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}=\inf\left\{\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{D}}|\nabla U|^{2}:U\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{D};\mathbb{D})\text{ with }\text{Tr\,}U=u\right\}.

This infimum is achieved by the harmonic extension of
u
. Since
\partial U/\partial\nu=(-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}})^{\frac{1}{2}}u
on
\mathbb{S}
, where
\nu
is the outward unit normal of
\partial\mathbb{D}
, then (
2.16
) is equivalent to

(2.21)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta U=0&\text{in }\mathbb{D},\\
\frac{\partial U}{\partial\nu}\wedge U=0&\text{on }\mathbb{S}.\end{cases}

All half-harmonic maps, as classified in Theorem
2.3
, are Blaschke products of
d
Möbius transformations of
\mathbb{D}
or their complex conjugate. This can seen from Cayley transformation. Recall that
Cayley transform
is a bi-holomorphic mapping
\mathfrak{C}:{\mathbb{H}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{D}}
which is defined as

(2.22)

\displaystyle\xi=\mathfrak{C}(z):=\textup{i\,}\frac{z-\textup{i\,}}{z+\textup{i\,}}.

Here and the following, we use
\xi
to denote the complex coordinates of
\mathbb{D}
and
z
to denote that of
\mathbb{H}
. Note that
the boundary mapping of
\mathfrak{C}(z)
is just the stereographic projection (
2.9
).

By the one-to-one correspondence of half-harmonic maps in
\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{S})
and
\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S};\mathbb{S})
, one can obtain all half-harmonic maps in
\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S};\mathbb{S})
from (
2.7
).
More precisely, it consists of Blaschke products
\{\phi_{\vartheta^{\prime},\vec{a}}:\vartheta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S},\,\vec{a}=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{d})\in\mathbb{D}^{d}\}
and their complex conjugates, where

(2.23)

\displaystyle\phi_{\vartheta^{\prime},\vec{a}}(\xi):=e^{i\vartheta^{\prime}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\xi-a_{j}}{1-\bar{a}_{j}\xi}.

The parameters in (
2.7
) and the above equation are related by

(2.24)

\displaystyle a_{j}=\mathfrak{C}(\alpha_{j}),\hskip 10.00002pt\vartheta^{\prime}=\vartheta+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\theta_{j},\hskip 10.00002pte^{\textup{i\,}\theta_{j}}=\frac{-\alpha_{j}-\textup{i\,}}{1+\textup{i\,}\bar{\alpha}_{j}}.

Using some integration by parts, the degree of
u
(see (
2.19
)) can also be defined by

(2.25)

\displaystyle\text{deg\,}u=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{D}}U_{x}\wedge U_{y}dxdy.

Notice the following identity when
U=U^{1}+\textup{i\,}U^{2}

(2.26)

\displaystyle|\nabla U|^{2}=\left(U_{x}^{1}-U_{y}^{2}\right)^{2}+\left(U_{y}^{1}+U_{x}^{2}\right)^{2}+2U_{x}\wedge U_{y}\geqslant 2U_{x}\wedge U_{y}.

Since
\phi_{\vartheta,\vec{a}}
is holomorphic in
\mathbb{D}
,
\phi_{\vartheta,\vec{a}}
achieves the identity for the above equation. Combining this with (
2.20
) and (
2.25
), we just proved (
2.8
) since

(2.27)

\displaystyle\|\phi_{\vartheta,\vec{a}}\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}=\text{deg\,}\phi_{\vartheta,\vec{a}}=d.

We show the proof of Theorem
1.2
to end this section.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

The inequality is trivial by (
2.26
). The proof of equality is essentially contained in
[
28
]
. For the reader’s convenience, we include it here. Suppose
U
is the harmonic extension of

. It follows from

and (

) that

By Weyl’s lemma, this is equivalent to

to

. Moreover,

∎

3.
Non-degeneracy of the linearized operator

Consider the half-harmonic map
u=u_{1}+\textup{i\,}u_{2}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{S}
, equation (
2.6
) is equivalent to

(3.1)

\displaystyle(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}u\wedge u=0.

The linearized operator at
u
is

(3.2)

\displaystyle L_{u}[v]=(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}u\wedge v+(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}v\wedge u.

We define

\ker L_{u}=\{v=v_{1}+\textup{i\,}v_{2}\in\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C}):L_{u}[v]=0,u_{1}v_{1}+u_{2}v_{2}=0\}.

Suppose
V=V_{1}+\textup{i\,}V_{2}
is the harmonic extension of
v
to the upper half space
\mathbb{H}
by Possion kernel, then
v\in\ker L_{u}
is equivalent to

(3.3)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta V=0&\text{in }\mathbb{H},\\
\partial_{y}U\wedge v+\partial_{y}V\wedge u=0&\text{on }\partial\mathbb{H}.\end{cases}

The stereographic projection induces an isometry between
\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})
and
\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})
. For a half-harmonic map
u:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{S}
, one can also define the linearized operator

(3.4)

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{u}[v]=(-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}})^{\frac{1}{2}}u\wedge v+(-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}})^{\frac{1}{2}}v\wedge u

and
\ker\tilde{L}_{u}=\{v\in\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S};\mathbb{C}):\tilde{L}_{u}[v]=0,\textup{Re\,}u\bar{v}=0\}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any half-harmonic map
u:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{S}
, let
\tilde{u}=u\circ\mathcal{S}^{-1}
, we have

L_{u}=J_{\mathcal{S}}(\tilde{L}_{\tilde{u}}\circ\mathcal{S}^{-1})

where
J_{\mathcal{S}}=(1-\sin\theta)
is the Jacobian of
\mathcal{S}
. Consequently
\text{dim}_{\mathbb{R}}\ker L_{u}=\text{dim}_{\mathbb{R}}\ker\tilde{L}_{\tilde{u}}
.

Proof.

It is easy to show that the Jacobian of
\mathcal{S}
is

J_{\mathcal{S}}=\frac{2}{x^{2}+1}=1-\sin\theta.

Recall from (
2.1
) that

(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}u(\mathcal{S}(\theta))=(1-\sin\theta){(-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{u}(\theta)}.

For any
v\in\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C})
, denote
\tilde{v}=v\circ\mathcal{S}^{-1}\in\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S};\mathbb{C})
, then

L_{u}[v](x)=(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}u\wedge v+(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}v\wedge u={(1-\sin\theta)}\tilde{L}_{\tilde{u}}[\tilde{v}].

∎

The operator
L_{u}
(or
\tilde{L}_{u}
) arise naturally in the linearization of the energy functional
\mathcal{E}
.
To see that, let us use
\boldsymbol{u}^{\perp}
denote the vector rotating
\boldsymbol{u}
counterclockwise by
\pi/2
. Any variation
\boldsymbol{\phi}
can be written as
\tilde{h}\boldsymbol{u}+h\boldsymbol{u}^{\perp}
for some real-valued function
\tilde{h}
and
h
in
\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose that
\boldsymbol{u}
is a half-harmonic map
\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{S}
and
\boldsymbol{\phi}=\tilde{h}\boldsymbol{u}+h\boldsymbol{u}^{\perp}
is a variation satisfying
|\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{\phi}|=1
. Then

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{\phi})=\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})+\int_{\mathbb{R}}h\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{u}}[h]+O(h^{3})

where the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{u}}
is given by

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{u}}=(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}-(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{u})+R

with the integral operator

(3.7)

\displaystyle(Rf)(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|\boldsymbol{u}(x)-\boldsymbol{u}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}f(y)dy.

Proof.

The constraint
|\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{\phi}|^{2}=1
a.e. implies that
2\tilde{h}+\tilde{h}^{2}+h^{2}=0
. Then
\tilde{h}=-\frac{1}{2}h^{2}+O(h^{3})
. We have

(3.8)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{\phi})=&\ \mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})+2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{u}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\phi}\\
=&\ \mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})-\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{u})h^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\phi}+O(h^{3}).\end{split}

It follows from (
2.1
) that

(3.9)

\displaystyle(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}(h\boldsymbol{u}^{\perp})(x)=((-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}h(x))\boldsymbol{u}^{\perp}(x)+\frac{1}{\pi}P.V.\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\boldsymbol{u}^{\perp}(x)-\boldsymbol{u}^{\perp}(y)}{|x-y|^{2}}h(y)dy.

Using
\boldsymbol{u}^{\perp}(x)\cdot(\boldsymbol{u}^{\perp}(x)-\boldsymbol{u}^{\perp}(y))=1-\boldsymbol{u}(x)\cdot\boldsymbol{u}(y)=\frac{1}{2}|\boldsymbol{u}(x)-\boldsymbol{u}(y)|^{2}
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\phi}=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\tilde{h}\boldsymbol{u}+h\boldsymbol{u}^{\perp})\cdot(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}(\tilde{h}\boldsymbol{u}+h\boldsymbol{u}^{\perp})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}h\boldsymbol{u}^{\perp}\cdot(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}(h\boldsymbol{u}^{\perp})+O(h^{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}h+\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}\frac{|\boldsymbol{u}(x)-\boldsymbol{u}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}h(x)h(y)dxdy+O(h^{3}).

Plugging it into (
3.8
), we get (
3.5
).
∎

For any
v=v_{1}+\textup{i\,}v_{2}\in\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C})
satisfying
u_{1}v_{1}+u_{2}v_{2}=0
, we can write
v=h\textup{i\,}u
for some real-valued function
h
on
\mathbb{R}
. Then one can verify that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&L_{u}[v]=(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}u\wedge(h\textup{i\,}u)+(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}(h\textup{i\,}u)\wedge u\\
=&[(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}u\wedge\textup{i\,}u]h-(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}h(\textup{i\,}u\wedge u)+\frac{1}{\pi}P.V.\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(\textup{i\,}u(x)-\textup{i\,}u(y))\wedge u(x)}{|x-y|^{2}}h(y)dy\\
=&(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{u})h-(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}h-Rh\\
=&-\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{u}}[h].\end{split}

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose
u
is a half-harmonic map from
\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{S}
, then
\text{dim}_{\mathbb{R}}\ker L_{u}\geq 2|\text{deg\,}u|+1
. More precisely, for instance, if
u=e^{\textup{i\,}\vartheta}\prod_{j=1}^{k}(\frac{x-\alpha_{j}}{x-\bar{\alpha}_{j}})^{d_{j}}
with
\{\alpha_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}
are distinct and
d_{j}\geq 1
, then

\displaystyle\ker L_{u}\supset\textup{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\left\{1,\textup{Re\,}\frac{1}{(x-\bar{\alpha}_{j})^{s}},\textup{Im\,}\frac{1}{(x-\bar{\alpha}_{j})^{s}}:s=1,\cdots,d_{j},j=1,\cdots,k.\right\}\textup{i\,}u.

Proof.

Assume
d=\text{deg\,}u>0
.
It is known that

u
takes form (
1.4
) if and only if there exists
c_{0},\cdots,c_{d-1}\in\mathbb{C}
and
\vartheta\in\mathbb{S}
such that

(3.10)

\displaystyle u=e^{\textup{i\,}\vartheta}\frac{x^{d}+c_{d-1}x^{d-1}+\cdots+c_{1}x+c_{0}}{x^{d}+\bar{c}_{d-1}x^{d-1}+\cdots+\bar{c}_{1}x+\bar{c}_{0}}

with
x^{d}+c_{d-1}x^{d-1}+\cdots+c_{1}x+c_{0}
has zeros all in
\mathbb{H}
. This fact comes from the theorem 3.3.2 in
[
19
]
(page 43) and conformal equivalence between
\mathbb{D}
and
\mathbb{H}
.

It is clear that changing the parameters
\vartheta,c_{d-1},\cdots,c_{0}
(complex numbers) of
u
continuously
yields a family of half-harmonic maps. Therefore, it generates kernel maps for the linearized operators
L_{u}
. Taking derivatives of
u
on
\vartheta
, we get
\{\textup{i\,}u\}\subset\ker L_{u}
. It is easy to see that for
l=0,\cdots,d-1
,

(3.11)

\displaystyle\partial_{c_{j}}u=e^{\textup{i\,}\vartheta}\frac{x^{l}}{x^{d}+\bar{c}_{d-1}x^{d-1}+\cdots+\bar{c}_{1}x+\bar{c}_{0}}=\frac{x^{l}u}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}(x-\alpha_{j})^{d_{j}}},

(3.12)

\displaystyle\partial_{\bar{c}_{j}}u=\frac{-x^{l}u}{x^{d}+\bar{c}_{d-1}x^{d-1}+\cdots+\bar{c}_{1}x+\bar{c}_{0}}=\frac{-x^{l}u}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}(x-\bar{\alpha}_{j})^{d_{j}}}.

Note that
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Taking derivative of
u
on the real part of
c_{j}
, we get

(3.13)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{c_{j}}u+\partial_{\bar{c}_{j}}u=2\textup{i\,}u\ \text{Im\,}\frac{x^{l}}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}(x-\bar{\alpha}_{j})^{d_{j}}}.\end{split}

Taking derivative of
u
on the imaginary part of
c_{j}
, we get

(3.14)

\displaystyle\textup{i\,}(\partial_{c_{j}}u-\partial_{\bar{c}_{j}}u)=2\textup{i\,}u\ \textup{Re\,}\frac{x^{l}}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}(x-\bar{\alpha}_{j})^{d_{j}}}.

Therefore, the
\mathbb{R}
-linear combination of them must belong to
\ker L_{u}
.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\ker L_{u}\supset&\ \text{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\left\{1,\textup{Re\,}\frac{x^{l}}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}(x-\bar{\alpha}_{j})^{d_{j}}},\text{Im\,}\frac{x^{l}}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}(x-\bar{\alpha}_{j})^{d_{j}}}:l=0,\cdots,d-1\right\}\textup{i\,}u\\
=&\ \textup{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\left\{1,\textup{Re\,}\frac{1}{(x-\bar{\alpha}_{j})^{s}},\textup{Im\,}\frac{1}{(x-\bar{\alpha}_{j})^{s}}:s=1,\cdots,d_{j},j=1,\cdots,k.\right\}\textup{i\,}u.\end{split}

Obviously they are all
\mathbb{R}
-linearly independent, therefore
\text{dim}_{\mathbb{R}}\ker L_{u}\geq 2|d|+1
.

If
d=0
, then obviously
\{\textup{i\,}u\}\subset\ker L_{u}
and thus
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\ker L_{u}\geq 1
. If
d<0
, one can prove similarly as above by working on the conjugate of
u
.
∎

Suppose that
\boldsymbol{V}=(V_{1},V_{2})
. We abuse the notation
V=V_{1}+\textup{i\,}V_{2}
and denote it a complex-valued function defined on upper half plane
\mathbb{H}
. We also adopt the notation
\partial_{z}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}-\textup{i\,}\partial_{y})
,
\partial_{\bar{z}}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}+\textup{i\,}\partial_{y})
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose
u
is half-harmonic map from
\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{S}
.
If
v\in\ker L_{u}
with
\text{deg\,}u>0
(
\text{deg\,}u<0
), then
V
is holomorphic (anti-holomorphic) in
\mathbb{H}
. Moreover,
V
can be extended to a meromorphic (anti-meromorphic) function with possible poles at poles of
U
. In addition,
V\circ\mathfrak{C}^{-1}
is smooth on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.

Proof.

We just prove the case
\text{deg\,}u>0
. Define the Hopf differential

(3.15)

\displaystyle\Phi=2U_{z}\bar{V}_{z}=\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{U}\cdot\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{V}-\partial_{y}\boldsymbol{U}\cdot\partial_{y}\boldsymbol{V}-\textup{i\,}(\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{U}\cdot\partial_{y}\boldsymbol{V}+\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{V}\cdot\partial_{y}\boldsymbol{U}).

Here in the middle we are using complex multiplication and
\bar{V}_{z}=\partial_{z}\bar{V}
. Since
U
is holomorphic, then

\Phi_{\bar{z}}=2U_{z\bar{z}}\bar{V}_{z}+2U_{z}\bar{V}_{z\bar{z}}=2U_{z}\overline{V_{z\bar{z}}}=\frac{1}{2}U_{z}\overline{\Delta V}=0.

Therefore
\Phi
is holomorphic in
\mathbb{H}
. We claim that
\text{Im}\,\Phi=0
on
\partial\mathbb{H}
. To see that, the boundary condition in (
3.3
) means

(3.16)

\displaystyle 0=\partial_{y}V\wedge u+(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\partial_{y}\boldsymbol{U})u\wedge v=(\partial_{y}V-(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\partial_{y}\boldsymbol{U})v)\wedge u\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on }\partial\mathbb{H},

which means

(3.17)

\displaystyle\partial_{y}V=(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\partial_{y}\boldsymbol{U})v+(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\partial_{y}\boldsymbol{V})u,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ on }\partial\mathbb{H}.

Since
\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}=0
and
\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{v}=0
on
\partial\mathbb{H}
, then

(3.18)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\partial_{y}\boldsymbol{V}+\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\partial_{y}\boldsymbol{U}=(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\partial_{y}\boldsymbol{U})\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}+(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\partial_{y}\boldsymbol{U})\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}=0.

Thus we have shown that
g(z):=\text{Im}\,\Phi
vanishes on
\partial\mathbb{H}
. By odd reflection across
\partial\mathbb{H}
, we can extend the harmonic function
g
to all of
\mathbb{C}
. However, since
g
is harmonic and
g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
because
U,V\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
, we conclude that
g\equiv 0
on
\mathbb{C}
. Thus
\Phi
is real-valued and holomorphic, which implies that
\Phi
is constant. Since
\Phi\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
, we deduce that
\Phi(z)\equiv 0
.

Now we have
\Phi=2U_{z}\bar{V}_{z}=0
. Since
U_{z}
only have isolated zeros in
\mathbb{H}
, then
\bar{V}_{z}=0
. Consequently,
V
is holomorphic.

We shall rewrite
U
as

(3.19)

\displaystyle U(z)=e^{\textup{i\,}\vartheta}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(\frac{z-\alpha_{j}}{z-\bar{\alpha}_{j}}\right)^{d_{j}}

with
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{H}
which are different from each other. Then
d_{1}+\cdots+d_{k}=d
.
Now define
W=V/U
. Then the previous argument implies that
W
is a meromorphic function on
\mathbb{H}
with possible poles at
\{\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{k}\}
. The orthogonality condition says that
u_{1}v_{1}+u_{2}v_{2}=0
on
\partial\mathbb{H}
. Therefore the real part of
W
vanishes on
\partial\mathbb{H}
because

(3.20)

\displaystyle W=\frac{v_{1}+\textup{i\,}v_{2}}{u_{1}+\textup{i\,}u_{2}}=\frac{u_{1}v_{1}+u_{2}v_{2}}{u_{1}^{2}+u_{2}^{2}}+\textup{i\,}\frac{u_{1}v_{2}-u_{2}v_{1}}{u_{1}^{2}+u_{2}^{2}}.

By Schwarz reflection principle, we can extend
W
to a meromorphic function on
\mathbb{C}
(we still denote it as
W
) by
W(z)=-\overline{W(\bar{z})}
for the lower half plane. Now
W
has possible poles at
\{\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{k},\bar{\alpha}_{1},\cdots,\bar{\alpha}_{k}\}
. The order of
W
at
\alpha_{j}
(or
\bar{\alpha}_{j}
) is at most
d_{j}
. Since
U
is a meromorphic function on
\mathbb{C}
, then so does
V=WU
. The poles of
V
are contained in
\{\bar{\alpha}_{1},\cdots,\bar{\alpha}_{k}\}
.

Note that
\bar{\alpha}_{j}
,
j=1,\cdots,k
, are all away from the
\partial\mathbb{H}
. Therefore
V
is holomorphic at any point on
\partial\mathbb{H}
. Since
\mathfrak{C}
is a holomorphic on
\overline{\mathbb{H}}
, then
V\circ\mathfrak{C}^{-1}
is holomorphic in
\overline{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\{\text{i}\}
. Because
\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C})
is isometric to
\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S};\mathbb{C})
through the stereographic projection, then
v\circ\mathfrak{C}^{-1}\in\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S};\mathbb{C})
. Thus
v\circ\mathfrak{C}^{-1}\in\ker\tilde{L}_{u\circ\mathfrak{C}^{-1}}
.
However, we can repeat the above whole process by using another bi-holomorphic mapping between
\mathbb{H}
and
\mathbb{D}
, say
(z-\textup{i\,})/(z+\textup{i\,})
, to show that
V\circ\mathfrak{C}^{-1}
is holomorphic in
\overline{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\{1\}
. Combining with the previous statement, we know
V\circ\mathfrak{C}^{-1}
is holomorphic in
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.
∎

Now we can prove the Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We shall assume
\text{deg\,}u>0
and
U
takes the form (
3.19
). Suppose
v\in\ker L_{u}
and
V
is the harmonic extension to
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
. Define
W=V/U
. Lemma
3.4
implies that
W
is meromorphic on
\mathbb{C}
with possible poles at
\{\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{k},\bar{\alpha}_{1},\cdots,\bar{\alpha}_{k}\}
. The order of
W
at
\alpha_{j}
(or
\bar{\alpha}_{j}
) is at most
d_{j}
.

We claim that
W
is bounded at infinity. Indeed, this just follows from the fact that
V\circ\mathfrak{C}^{-1}
is holomorphic in
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
and consequently
V
is bounded on
\overline{\mathbb{H}}
.

Thus
W
is a rational function. There exists some polynomial
P(x)
such that

(3.21)

\displaystyle W(z)=\frac{P(z)}{Q(z)},\hskip 10.00002ptQ(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{k}(z-a_{j})^{d_{j}}(z-\bar{a}_{j})^{d_{j}}.

The boundedness of
W
implies
\text{deg\,}P\leq 2d
. Since
W(z)=-\overline{W(\bar{z})}
and
Q(z)=\overline{Q(\bar{z})}
, it holds that
P(z)=-\overline{P(\bar{z})}
. If
P(z)=c_{0}+c_{1}z+\cdots+c_{2d}z^{2d}
, then one must have
c_{j}=-\bar{c}_{j}
. The dimension of all such polynomials is
2d+1
. Thus
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\ker L_{u}\leq 2|\text{deg\,}u|+1
. Applying Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
3.3
, we conclude
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\ker L_{u}=2|\text{deg\,}u|+1
. Furthermore, if
u=e^{\textup{i\,}\vartheta}\prod_{j=1}^{k}(\frac{x-\alpha_{j}}{x-\bar{\alpha}_{j}})^{d_{j}}
with
\{\alpha_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}
are distinct and
d_{j}\geq 1
, then

\displaystyle\ker L_{u}=\textup{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\left\{1,\textup{Re\,}\frac{1}{(x-\bar{\alpha}_{j})^{s}},\textup{Im\,}\frac{1}{(x-\bar{\alpha}_{j})^{s}}:s=1,\cdots,d_{j},j=1,\cdots,k.\right\}\textup{i\,}u.

∎

Remark 3.5
.

Translating the above results to half-harmonic map
\tilde{u}:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{S}
(see (
2.23
)), we can get

\displaystyle\ker\tilde{L}_{\tilde{u}}=\textup{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\left\{1,\textup{ Im\,}\frac{\xi^{j}}{\prod_{i=1}^{d}(\xi-a_{i})},\textup{Re\,}\frac{\xi^{j}}{\prod_{i=1}^{d}(\xi-a_{i})}:j=0,\cdots,d-1\right\}\textup{i\,}\tilde{u}.

In particular, if
\tilde{u}=\xi^{m}
, then

(3.22)

\displaystyle\ker\tilde{L}_{\xi^{m}}=\textup{i\,}\xi^{m}\ \textup{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{1,\textup{Im\,}\xi^{j-m},\textup{Re\,}\xi^{j-m}:j=0,\cdots,m-1\}.

Using
\xi=\cos\theta+\textup{i\,}\sin\theta
on
\mathbb{S}
, we can rewrite it as

(3.23)

\displaystyle\ker\tilde{L}_{\xi^{m}}=\textup{i\,}\xi^{m}\ \textup{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{1,\sin j\theta,\cos j\theta:j=1,\cdots,m\}.

This recovers the result of
Lenzmann and Schikorra 2018
.

4.
Stability of half-harmonic map

We only need to show the Theorem
1.3
for
d=1
. The negative case follows from taking complex conjugate in the proof. The case
d=\pm 1
has a flavor of the main theorem proved by
Hirsch and Zemas 2021
about the stability of harmonic maps
\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
with degree
\pm 1
. We will use induction to prove a local quantitative stability for higher degree.

Denote the set of all maps in
\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S};\mathbb{S})
with degree
d
by

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{S}}^{d}=\{u\in\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S};\mathbb{S}):\text{deg\,}\,u=d\}.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any
u\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{S}}^{d}
with
|d|\geq 1
, there exists
a_{0}\in\mathbb{D}
such that
\fint_{\mathbb{S}}u\circ\phi_{0,a_{0}}=0
.

Proof.

Assume first
u\in\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S};\mathbb{S})\cap C^{\infty}
. Since
|\text{deg\,}u|>1
, then
u
is surjective. We will write
\phi_{0,a}=\phi_{a}
for short. Define

(4.2)

\displaystyle F(a)=\fint_{\mathbb{S}}u\circ\phi_{-a}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}}u\circ\phi_{-a}.

One readily knows
F
is continuous and maps
\mathbb{D}
to
\mathbb{D}
and
F(0)=\fint_{\mathbb{S}}u
.
For any
\zeta\in\partial\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{S}
, one has

\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}\phi_{-r\zeta}(\theta)=\zeta,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\,\theta\neq-\zeta

and this convergence is uniform on
\{a\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}:|a+\zeta|\geq\epsilon\}
for any
\epsilon>0
. This implies that
\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}F(r\zeta)=u(\zeta)\in\mathbb{S}
uniformly in
\zeta
. Hence
F
is a continuous function on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. Moreover, the Lerary-Schauder degree of
F
with respect to 0 is the same as the winding number of
u
(see
[
29
]
). By Leray-Schauder degree theory, there exists
a_{0}\in\mathbb{D}
such that
F(a_{0})=0
.

In the general case of a map
u\in\mathcal{A}^{1}_{\mathbb{S}}
, one can approximate
u
by a sequence of
u_{j}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S};\mathbb{S})
with the property that

u_{j}\to u\text{ strongly with }u\in\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S};\mathbb{S})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and }\hskip 10.00002pt\text{deg\,}u_{j}=\text{deg\,}u.

Going to a subsequence if necessary, we can assume
u_{j}\to u
a.e.
\mathbb{S}
. By the previous paragraph, there exists
a_{j}\in\mathbb{D}
such that

\fint_{\mathbb{D}}u_{j}\circ\phi_{a_{j}}=0.

We must have
|a_{j}|<1-\epsilon_{0}
. Suppose not, then there is a subsequence, which we still label it as
a_{j}
, converging to some
-\zeta\in\partial\mathbb{D}
. Then
\phi_{a_{j}}\to\zeta
a.e on
\mathbb{S}
and
u_{j}\circ\phi_{a_{j}}\to u(\zeta)
a.e. on
\mathbb{S}
.
So we can use Dominated Convergence Theorem to infer that

u(\zeta)=\fint_{\mathbb{S}}u(\zeta)=\lim_{j\to\infty}\fint_{\mathbb{S}}u_{j}\circ\phi_{a_{j}}=0.

However, since
|u_{j}\circ\phi_{a_{j}}|\equiv 1
,

|u(\zeta)|=\fint_{\mathbb{S}}|u(\zeta)|=\lim_{j\to\infty}\fint_{\mathbb{S}}|u_{j}\circ\phi_{a_{j}}|=1.

This is a contradiction. Now we can assume
a_{j}\to a_{0}
where
a\in\mathbb{D}
and consequently

\fint_{\mathbb{S}}u\circ\phi_{a_{0}}=0.

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

By the Lemma
4.1
, it suffices to prove the stability (
1.8
) for
u\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
with the additional assumption
\fint_{\mathbb{S}}u=0
. This is due to the invariance of energy and the degree under Möbius transformation of
\mathbb{D}
, namely

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u)=\mathcal{E}(u\circ\phi_{a}),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{deg\,}u\circ\phi_{a}=\text{deg\,}u=1

holds for any
a\in\mathbb{D}
and the group structure of Möbius transformations

(4.4)

\displaystyle\phi_{b}\circ\phi_{a}=\phi_{\frac{a+b}{1+\bar{a}b}}.

If
D(u)\geq 1
, then

(4.5)

\displaystyle D(u)\geq 1=\text{deg\,}u=\|u\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})}-D(u),

which means
\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}\leq 2D(u)
. Consequently (
1.8
) holds. Thus for the following, we can assume
D(u)<1
.

Suppose that
u=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{k}z^{k}
. By the previous step, we can assume
c_{0}=0
. Since
u\equiv 1
a.e.
\mathbb{S}
, then

\displaystyle D(u)=

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}-\fint_{\mathbb{S}}u^{2}=\sum_{k\geq 1}[k-1](|c_{k}|^{2}+|c_{-k}|^{2})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\geq 2}k(|c_{k}|^{2}+|c_{-k}|^{2})=\frac{1}{2}\|u-c_{1}e^{\textup{i\,}\theta}-c_{-1}e^{-\textup{i\,}\theta}\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}.

That is

(4.6)

\displaystyle\|u-c_{1}e^{\textup{i\,}\theta}-c_{-1}e^{-\textup{i\,}\theta}\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}\leq 2D(u).

Again
|u|\equiv 1
a.e.
\mathbb{S}
and (
2.18
) implies

\sum_{k\geq 1}|c_{k}|^{2}+|c_{-k}|^{2}=1.

Combining with the consequence of (
2.19
), that is

1=\text{deg\,}u=\sum_{k\geq 1}k(|c_{k}|^{2}-|c_{-k}|^{2}),

one obtains

(4.7)

\displaystyle 1-|c_{1}|^{2}\leq\sum_{k\geq 2}(|c_{k}|^{2}+|c_{-k}|^{2})\leq D(u),

(4.8)

\displaystyle|c_{-1}|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\geq 2}k(|c_{k}|^{2}-|c_{-k}|^{2})\leq D(u).

Therefore plugging in (
4.7
) and (
4.8
) to (
4.6
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|u-e^{\textup{i\,}(\theta+\arg c_{1})}\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 9\left[2D(u)+\|c_{-1}e^{-\textup{i\,}\theta}\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}+\|(e^{\textup{i\,}\arg c_{1}}-c_{1})e^{\textup{i\,}\theta}\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 9[3D(u)+(1-|c_{1}|)^{2}]\leq 9[3D(u)+D(u)^{2}].

Since we assume
D(u)<1
, then the above inequality implies

\|u-e^{\textup{i\,}\vartheta}\text{id}_{\mathbb{S}}\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}\leq 36D(u),

for some
\vartheta
. Therefore (
1.8
) is established.

∎

Now we plan to prove Theorem
1.5
. Before that, let us make some preparations.
First, we can expand
\phi_{\vartheta,\vec{b}}
to be

(4.9)

\displaystyle\phi_{\vartheta,\vec{b}}=e^{\textup{i\,}\vartheta}\sum_{k\geq 0}A_{k}(\vec{b})z^{k}

where
A_{k}(\vec{b})
is some function for
\vec{b}
for each
k
. For instance, for any
a\in\mathbb{D}
,

(4.10)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\phi_{a}=\frac{z-a}{1-\bar{a}z}=(z-a)\sum_{k\geq 0}\bar{a}^{k}z^{k}=-a+\sum_{k\geq 1}(1-|a|^{2})\bar{a}^{k-1}z^{k}.\end{split}

Since
\phi_{\vartheta,\vec{b}}=e^{\textup{i\,}\vartheta}\phi_{b_{1}}\cdots\phi_{b_{d}}
, one knows
A_{0}(\vec{b})=(-1)^{d}\prod_{j=1}^{d}b_{j}
. For any vector
\vec{b}\in\mathbb{D}^{d}
and a set
\Omega\in\mathbb{D}
, we abuse the notation that
\vec{b}\in\Omega^{d}
if
b=(b_{1},\cdots,b_{d})
with
b_{i}\in\Omega
for
i=1,\cdots,d
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose
\Omega\Subset\mathbb{D}
is compact. There exists a
\epsilon_{\Omega}>0
and
N_{\Omega}
such that, for any Blaschke product
\phi_{\vartheta,\vec{b}}
with
\vec{b}\in\Omega^{d}
, we have

(4.11)

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i\leq N_{\Omega}}\{|A_{i}(\vec{b})|\}\geq\epsilon_{\Omega}.

Proof.

Let
\epsilon(N)=\sum_{1\leq k\leq N}|A_{k}(\vec{b})|^{2}
. By the assumption, there exists
\epsilon_{1}=\epsilon_{1}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle|A_{0}(\vec{b})|^{2}\leq 1-\epsilon_{1},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\,\vec{b}\in\Omega^{d}.

By the property (
2.18
), we get
\sum_{k\geq 0}|A_{k}(\vec{b})|^{2}=1
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq N+1}|A_{k}(\vec{b})|^{2}\geq\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon(N).

Since
\text{deg\,}\phi_{\vartheta,\vec{b}}=d
and (
2.19
), we get
\sum_{k\geq 1}k|A_{k}(\vec{b})|^{2}=d
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq N+1}k|A_{k}(\vec{b})|^{2}\leq d-\epsilon(N).

Compare the above two inequalities, we get

\displaystyle d-\epsilon(N)>(N+1)(\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon(N)).

Now we choose
N_{\Omega}
such that
(N_{\Omega}+1)\epsilon_{1}>d
. One readily see that

\displaystyle\epsilon(N_{\Omega})>\frac{1}{N_{\Omega}}[(N_{\Omega}+1)\epsilon_{1}-d].

This implies (
4.11
) holds for
\epsilon_{\Omega}=\left(\epsilon(N_{\Omega})/N_{\Omega}\right)^{1/2}
.
∎

Proposition 4.3
.

Suppose
\Omega\Subset\mathbb{D}
is compact. There exists a constant
C_{\Omega}
such that, for any
u\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{S}}^{d}
and
\vec{b}\in\Omega^{d}
, we have

(4.12)

\displaystyle\|u-\phi_{\vartheta,\vec{b}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}\leq C_{\Omega}\left(\|u-\phi_{\vartheta,\vec{b}}\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}+D(u)+D(u)^{2}\right).

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we will assume
\vartheta=0
in the assumption, and write
\phi_{\vartheta,\vec{b}}=\phi_{\vec{b}}
for short. Suppose that
u\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{S}}^{d}
with the Fourier expansion
u=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{k}z^{k}
.
By (
2.17
), (
2.19
) and (
1.7
),

(4.13)

\displaystyle D(u)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|k||c_{k}|^{2}-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}k|c_{k}|^{2}=2\sum_{k\leq-1}|k||c_{k}|^{2}.

Now since
u-\phi_{\vec{b}}=\sum_{k\geq 0}(c_{k}-A_{k})z^{k}+\sum_{k\leq-1}c_{k}z^{k}
, then

(4.14)

\displaystyle\|u-\phi_{\vec{b}}\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}=\sum_{k\geq 1}k|c_{k}-A_{k}(\vec{b})|^{2}+\sum_{k\leq-1}|k||c_{k}|^{2}

and

(4.15)

\displaystyle\|u-\phi_{\vec{b}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}=2\pi\sum_{k\geq 0}|c_{k}-A_{k}(\vec{b})|^{2}+2\pi\sum_{k\leq-1}|c_{k}|^{2}.

Combining (
4.13
), (
4.14
), we get

(4.16)

\displaystyle\|u-\phi_{\vec{b}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}\leq 2\pi\left(|c_{0}-A_{0}(\vec{b})|^{2}+\|u-\phi_{\vec{b}}\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}+D(u)\right).

It suffices to establish the following claim.

Claim 1
.

There exists a constant
C_{\Omega}
independent of
u
and
\vec{b}
such that

(4.17)

\displaystyle|A_{0}(\vec{b})-c_{0}|\leq C_{\Omega}\left(\|u-\phi_{\vec{b}}\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{S})}+D(u)+\sqrt{D(u)}\right).

In fact, Lemma
4.2
implies that there exists
1\leq k\leq N_{\Omega}
such that
|A_{k}(\vec{b})|\geq\epsilon_{\Omega}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume
k=1
and
|A_{1}(\vec{b})|\geq\epsilon_{\Omega}
. The following proof still works for other
k
with minor modification.

Since
|u|=1
and
|\phi_{\vec{b}}|=1
a.e. on
\mathbb{S}
, (
2.18
) implies

(4.18)

\displaystyle\bar{c}_{0}c_{1}=-\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\bar{c}_{j}c_{j+1},\hskip 10.00002pt\bar{A}_{0}A_{1}=-\sum_{j\geq 1}\bar{A}_{j}A_{j+1}.

Here for the time being we write
A_{k}(\vec{b})=A_{k}
for short.
Subtract two equations and make interpolation

\displaystyle A_{1}(\bar{c}_{0}-\bar{A}_{0})=

\displaystyle-\bar{c}_{0}(c_{1}-A_{1})-\sum_{j\geq 1}[\bar{c}_{j}c_{j+1}-\bar{A}_{j}A_{j+1}]-\sum_{j\leq-1}\bar{c}_{j}c_{j+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\bar{c}_{0}(c_{1}-A_{1})-\sum_{j\geq 1}[(\bar{c}_{j}-\bar{A}_{j})c_{j+1}+\bar{A}_{j}(c_{j+1}-A_{j+1})]-\sum_{j\leq-1}\bar{c}_{j}c_{j+1}.

Applying Hölder’s inequality to get

(4.19)

, then

(5.45)

\displaystyle\inf_{\vartheta\in\mathbb{S},\vec{\alpha}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}}\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\vartheta,\vec{\alpha}}\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}=\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}=\varepsilon^{2}\langle h_{\perp},h_{\perp}\rangle+O(\varepsilon^{3}).

Using (
5.31
), we obtain

(5.46)

\displaystyle\inf_{\vartheta\in\mathbb{S},\vec{\alpha}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}}\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\vartheta,\vec{\alpha}}\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}=\frac{10}{3}\pi\varepsilon^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{2}/\log j+\varepsilon^{3}).

On the other hand, Lemma
3.2
infers

(5.47)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})=\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j})+\varepsilon^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h_{\perp}\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}}[h_{\perp}]+O(\varepsilon^{3}).

Note (
2.3
) implies
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})=\|\boldsymbol{u}\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}
and
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j})=\|\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}=\text{deg\,}\boldsymbol{u}=2
. Combing with (
5.30
), it leads to

(5.48)

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}-2=\varepsilon^{2}O(1/\log j).

Now compare (
5.46
) and (
5.48
) to get

(5.49)

\displaystyle\inf_{\vartheta\in\mathbb{S},\vec{\alpha}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}}\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\vartheta,\vec{\alpha}}\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}\geq C(\log j)\left(\|u\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}-2\right).

Choosing
j
sufficiently large such that
C\log j\geq M
, our theorem is established.
∎

Proof of Lemma
5.6
.

Notice that if
|x-j^{2}|\geq j
and
x>0
, then

(A.1)

\displaystyle\rho_{j}(x)\leq\frac{C}{1+(x-j^{2})^{2}}.

Since
\rho_{j}
is even, then we have

(A.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{j}h^{2}=&\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{8(1+j^{4}+x^{2})h(x)^{2}}{[1+(x-j^{2})^{2}][1+(x+j^{2})^{2}]}dx\\
=&\int_{j^{2}-j}^{j^{2}+j}\frac{8(1+j^{4}+x^{2})}{[1+(x-j^{2})^{2}][1+(x+j^{2})^{2}]}dx+O(1/j)\\
=&\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{8(1+j^{4}+x^{2})}{[1+(x-j^{2})^{2}][1+(x+j^{2})^{2}]}dx+O(1/j)=\ 4\pi+O(1/j).\end{split}

Then we prove (
5.25
).
To prove (
5.26
), we
divide the support of
h(x)h(y)
, i.e.,
\{|x|\leq 2j^{2}\}\cap\{|y|\leq 2j^{2}\}
into
\Omega_{i}
,
i=1,\cdots,9
, according to their types.
Let

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Omega_{1}=\{|x-j^{2}|\leq j,j\leq|y-j^{2}|\leq j^{2}\}\cup\{j\leq|x-j^{2}|\leq j^{2},|y-j^{2}|\leq j\},\\
\Omega_{2}=\{|x-j^{2}|\leq j,j\leq|y+j^{2}|\leq j^{2}\}\cup\{j\leq|x-j^{2}|\leq j^{2},|y+j^{2}|\leq j\},\\
\Omega_{3}=\{|x+j^{2}|\leq j,j\leq|y+j^{2}|\leq j^{2}\}\cup\{j\leq|x+j^{2}|\leq j^{2},|y+j^{2}|\leq j\},\\
\Omega_{4}=\{|x+j^{2}|\leq j,j\leq|y-j^{2}|\leq j^{2}\}\cup\{j\leq|x+j^{2}|\leq j^{2},|y-j^{2}|\leq j\}.\end{split}

Using the expression (
5.8
), and there
|1+xy+j^{4}|\leq 8j^{4}
, it is easy to see that

\displaystyle Q_{j}(x,y)\leq C\begin{cases}[1+(x-j^{2})^{2}]^{-1}[1+(y-j^{2})^{2}]^{-1},\quad\Omega_{1},\\
[1+(x-j^{2})^{2}]^{-1}[1+(y+j^{2})^{2}]^{-1},\quad\Omega_{2},\\
[1+(x+j^{2})^{2}]^{-1}[1+(y+j^{2})^{2}]^{-1},\quad\Omega_{3},\\
[1+(x+j^{2})^{2}]^{-1}[1+(y-j^{2})^{2}]^{-1},\quad\Omega_{4}.\end{cases}

Consequently, for example, on
\Omega_{1}
we have, by the symmetry of
x
and
y
,

(A.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\iint_{\Omega_{1}}Q_{j}(x,y)h(x)h(y)dxdy\right|&\leq C\iint_{\{|x-j^{2}|\leq j,j\leq|y-j^{2}|\leq j^{2}\}}\frac{dxdy}{[1+(x-j^{2})^{2}][1+(y-j^{2})^{2}]}\\
&=4C\arctan j\int_{j}^{j^{2}}\frac{dy}{1+y^{2}}=O(1/j).\end{split}

Similarly, the integral on
\Omega_{i},i=2,3,4
is also of order
O(1/j)
.

On both of sets
\Omega_{5}=\{|x-j^{2}|\leq j\}\cap\{|y+j^{2}|\leq j\}
and
\Omega_{6}=\{|x+j^{2}|\leq j\}\cap\{|y-j^{2}|\leq j\}
, it holds that

(A.4)

\displaystyle|1+xy+j^{4}|\leq 4j^{3}.

It is easy to see that

\displaystyle Q_{j}(x,y)\leq\frac{C}{j^{2}}\begin{cases}[1+(x-j^{2})^{2}]^{-1}[1+(y+j^{2})^{2}]^{-1},\quad\Omega_{5},\\
[1+(x+j^{2})^{2}]^{-1}[1+(y-j^{2})^{2}]^{-1},\quad\Omega_{6}.\end{cases}

Then we obtain

(A.5)

\displaystyle\left|\iint_{\Omega_{i}}Q_{j}(x,y)h(x)h(y)dxdy\right|=O(1/j^{2}),\hskip 10.00002pti=5,6.

\Omega_{7}=\{|x-j^{2}|\leq j\}\cap\{|y-j^{2}|\leq j\}

. On

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\iint_{\Omega_{9}}Q_{j}(x,y)h(x)h(y)dxdy\right|&\leq C\int_{j}^{j^{2}}\int_{j}^{j^{2}}\frac{dxdy}{(1+x^{2})(1+y^{2})}=O(1/j^{2}).\end{split}

Together with (
A.3
), (

) and (
A.5

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{2\pi}&\iint_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}Q_{j}(x,y)h(x)h(y)dxdy\\
=&\ \frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_{\Omega_{7}}Q_{j}(x,y)dxdy+\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_{\Omega_{8}}Q_{j}(x,y)dxdy+O(1/j)\\
=&\ \frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}Q_{j}(x,y)dxdy+O(1/j)=4\pi+O(1/j).\end{split}

This is (
5.26
). Once we obtain (
5.25
), (
5.26
) and Lemma
5.5

(A.8)

Proof of Lemma
5.7
.

Since
h
is odd and
Q(x,y)=Q(y,x)=Q(-x,-y)
, it is easy to get

(A.10)

\displaystyle\langle h\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}^{\perp},K_{1}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}^{\perp}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}Q_{j}(x,y)h(x)dxdy=0.

By a direct computation, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle h\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}^{\perp},K_{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}^{\perp}\rangle&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{2}}K_{2}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}Q_{j}(x,y)h(x)K_{2}(y)dxdy\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{2(1-(x-j^{2})^{2})h(x)}{[1+(x-j^{2})^{2}]^{2}}dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{2(3+(x+j^{2})^{2})h(x)}{[1+(x-j^{2})^{2}][1+(x+j^{2})^{2}]}dx\\
&=I_{1}+I_{2}.\end{split}

Since

(A.11)

\displaystyle\begin{split}I_{1}=\int_{|x|\leq j}\frac{2(1-x^{2})h(x)}{(1+x^{2})^{2}}&\leq\left|\int_{|x|\leq j}\frac{2(1-x^{2})}{(1+x^{2})^{2}}dx\right|+\int_{|x|\geq j}\left|\frac{2(1-x^{2})}{(1+x^{2})^{2}}\right|dx\\
&=O(1/j),\end{split}

and

(A.12)

\displaystyle\begin{split}I_{2}&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{2h(x)}{1+(x-j^{2})^{2}}dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{4h(x)}{[1+(x-j^{2})^{2}][1+(x+j^{2})^{2}]}dx\\
&=2\arctan j+O(1/j^{2})=\pi+O(1/j).\end{split}

Then we get
\langle h\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}^{\perp},K_{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}^{\perp}\rangle=\pi+O(1/j)
.

We also have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle h\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}^{\perp},K_{3}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}^{\perp}\rangle&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{4(x-j^{2})h(x)}{[1+(x-j^{2})^{2}]^{2}}dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{4(x-j^{2})h(x)}{[1+(x-j^{2})^{2}][1+(x+j^{2})^{2}]}dx.\end{split}

Since

(A.13)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{4(x-j^{2})h(x)}{[1+(x-j^{2})^{2}]^{2}}dx\right|\leq\int_{|x|\geq j}\frac{4x}{(1+x^{2})^{2}}dx=O(1/j^{2}),\end{split}

and

(A.14)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{4(x-j^{2})h(x)}{[1+(x-j^{2})^{2}][1+(x+j^{2})^{2}]}dx\right|\\
&\leq\left|\int_{|x-j^{2}|\leq j}\frac{4(x-j^{2})}{j^{4}[1+(x-j^{2})^{2}]}dx\right|+\left|\int_{|x-j^{2}|\geq j}\frac{4}{j[1+(x+j^{2})^{2}]}dx\right|=O(1/j^{2}).\end{split}

Then we get
\langle h\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}^{\perp},K_{3}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}^{\perp}\rangle=O(1/j^{2})
.
Similarly, note that
h(x)=-1
when

, we can get

(A.15)

\displaystyle\langle h\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}^{\perp},K_{4}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}^{\perp}\rangle=-\pi+O(1/j),\hskip 10.00002pt\langle h\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}^{\perp},K_{5}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}^{\perp}\rangle=O(1/j^{2}).

∎
