Upper Bounds on Resolvent Degree and Its Growth Rate
Alexander J. Sutherland

Abstract
For each n n , let RD ​ ( n ) \text{RD}(n) denote the minimum d d for which there exists a formula for the general polynomial of degree n n in algebraic functions of at most d d variables. In 1945, Segre called for a better understanding of the large n n behavior of RD ​ ( n ) \text{RD}(n) . In this paper, we provide improved thresholds for upper bounds on RD ​ ( n ) \text{RD}(n) . Our techniques build upon classical algebraic geometry to provide new upper bounds for small n n and, in doing so, fix gaps in the proofs of A. Wiman and G.N. Chebotarev in [ Wim1927 ] and [ Che1954 ] .

Definition 2.1
.

(Polars of a Polynomial)

Let
f\in K[x_{0},\dotsc,x_{r}]
be a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d
and
P\in\mathbb{P}^{r}(K)
. For every
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, let

\displaystyle[n]=\left\{0,1,\dotsc,n\right\},

\displaystyle[n]^{*}=\left\{1,\dotsc,n\right\}.

For each

, we set

\displaystyle I_{k}:=\left\{(i_{0},\dotsc,i_{r})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r+1}\ |\ i_{0}+\cdots+i_{r}=k\right\}.

We have
r+1

\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{0}},\dotsc,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{r}}
. To obtain an (ordered)
k^{th}
partial derivative of
f
, we make
k

partial derivatives is indexed by
I_{k}^{*}=\text{Hom}_{{\textbf{Set}}}([k]^{*},[r])
. For any
\iota\in I_{k}^{*}
and

, we set

\displaystyle|\iota|(j):=\left|\iota^{-1}(j)\right|

and note that
(|\iota|(0),\dotsc,|\iota|(r))\in I_{k}
. Additionally, we use the shorthand

\displaystyle\partial_{0}^{j_{0}}\cdots\partial_{l}^{j_{l}}f:=\frac{\partial^{j_{0}+\cdots+j_{l}}f}{\partial x_{0}^{j_{0}}\cdots\partial x_{l}^{j_{l}}}.

With this notation set, the
k^{th}
polar of
f
at
P
is the homogeneous polynomial of degree
d-k

\displaystyle t(k,f,P)(y_{0},\dotsc,y_{r}):=\sum\limits_{\iota\in I_{d-k}^{*}}\left(\partial_{0}^{|\iota|(0)}\cdots\partial_{r}^{|\iota|(r)}f\right)\biggr\rvert_{P}\ y_{0}^{|\iota|(0)}\cdots y_{r}^{|\iota(r)|}.

.

(Polars of a Hypersurface)

Let

\displaystyle T(k,f,P):=\mathbb{V}\left(t(k,f,P)\right)\subseteq\mathbb{P}_{K}^{r}.

Example 2.3

Using the conventions of Definition

\displaystyle t(0,f,P)(x_{0},\dotsc,x_{r})

2.2
Resolvent Degree

Resolvent degree is an invariant introduced independently by Brauer in
[
Bra1975
]
and Arnol’d-Shimura in
[
AS1976
]
. Farb and Wolfson summarize the history of resolvent degree and significantly broaden the resolvent degree framework in
[
FW2019
]

\mathbb{C}
-varieties. We refer the reader to Section 4 of
[
Wol2021
]
for how to extend this definition to arbitrary dominant, rational maps. We also remind the reader that in this paper, we work only with fields that are finitely generated
\mathbb{C}
-algebras.

Definition 2.13
.

(Resolvent Degree of Field Extensions)

Let
L/K
be a finite extension of
\mathbb{C}
-fields. The
resolvent degree
of
L/K
, denoted
\text{RD}(L/K)
, is the minimum
d
for which there is a tower of finite field extensions

\displaystyle K=E_{0}\hookrightarrow E_{1}\hookrightarrow\cdots\hookrightarrow E_{\ell}

such that
L
embeds into
E_{\ell}
over
K
and for each
E_{i+1}/E_{i}
, there is a
finite extension of
\widetilde{F}_{i}/F_{i}
with
\text{tr.deg}_{\mathbb{C}}(F_{i})\leq d
such that

\displaystyle E_{i+1}\cong E_{i}\otimes_{F_{i}}\widetilde{F}_{i}.

Definition 2.14
.

(Resolvent Degree of Generically Finite, Dominant Maps)

Let
Y\dashrightarrow X
be a generically finite, dominant, rational map of
\mathbb{C}
-varieties. The
resolvent degree
of
Y\dashrightarrow X
, denoted
\text{RD}(Y\dashrightarrow X)
, is the minimum
d
for which there is a tower of generically finite, dominant, rational maps

\displaystyle E_{\ell}\dashrightarrow\cdots\dashrightarrow E_{1}\dashrightarrow E_{0}\subseteq X

such that
E_{0}\subseteq X
is a dense Zariski open;
E_{\ell}\dashrightarrow E_{0}
factors through
Y\dashrightarrow X
; and for each
\pi_{i}:E_{i+1}\dashrightarrow E_{i}
, there exists a surjective morphism
\widetilde{Z}_{i}\rightarrow Z_{i}
with
\dim\left(Z_{i}\right)\leq d
, a Zariski open
E_{i}^{\circ}\subseteq E_{i}
, and a morphism
E_{i}^{\circ}\rightarrow Z_{i}
such that

\displaystyle\pi_{i}^{-1}\left(E_{i}^{\circ}\right)\cong E_{i}^{\circ}\times_{Z_{i}}\widetilde{Z}_{i}.

(

We write

Remark 2.17
.

(RD of a Composition)

We will frequently use the following field-theoretic version of Lemma 2.7 of
[
FW2019
]
without explicit reference: given a tower of field extensions

\displaystyle E_{0}\hookrightarrow E_{1}\hookrightarrow\cdots E_{k},

the resolvent degree of the composition is the maximum of the resolvent degree of the components, e.g.

\displaystyle\text{RD}(E_{k}/E_{0})=\max\left\{\text{RD}(E_{j}/E_{j-1})\ |\ 1\leq j\leq k\right\}.

.

(Upper Bound on

Let

As

over

and observe that we can determine an

by construction. Lemma

Remark 2.20
.

(Extensions Given By Solving Polynomials)

Given Proposition

by solving a degree

2.3
Iterated Polar Cones and
k
-Polar Points

As we have established, for an intersection of hypersurfaces
V
and
P\in V(K)
, points of
\mathcal{C}(V;P)\setminus\{P\}
determine lines on

. Next, we will iterate the polar cone construction in a method to determine

-planes on intersections of hypersurfaces (as in Lemma

). To do so, we must first introduce additional definitions and notation.

Remark 2.21
.

(Linear Subvarieties)

•

For any points
P_{0},\dotsc,P_{k}\in\mathbb{P}^{r}(K)
, we denote the linear subsvariety they determine by
L(P_{0},\dotsc,P_{k})
. Note that
L(P_{0},\dotsc,P_{k})
has dimension at most
k
.

•

Henceforth, when we say
k
-plane, we mean a linear subvariety of
\mathbb{P}^{r}
of dimension
k
. When
k=1
, we say line instead of 1-plane and when
k=2
, we say plane instead of 2-plane.

Definition 2.22
.

(Iterated Polar Cones &
k
-Polar Points)

Let
V\subseteq\mathbb{P}_{K}^{r}
be an intersection of hypersurfaces and
P_{0}\in V(K)
; we set
\mathcal{C}^{1}(V;P_{0}):=\mathcal{C}(V;P_{0})
. Given additional points
P_{1},\dotsc,P_{k-1}\in V(K)
such that

P_{\ell-1}\in\mathcal{C}^{\ell-1}(V;P_{0},\dotsc,P_{\ell-2})\setminus L(P_{0},\dotsc,P_{\ell-2})

(2)

for
0\leq\ell\leq k-1
, the
k^{th}
polar cone of
V
at
P_{0},\dotsc,P_{k-1}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{k}(V;P_{0},\dotsc,P_{k-1}):=\mathcal{C}\left(\mathcal{C}^{k-1}(V;P_{0},\dotsc,P_{k-2}),P_{k-1}\right).

We refer to an ordered collection of points
\left(P_{0},\dotsc,P_{k}\right)
that satisfy (
2
) for each
0\leq\ell\leq k-1
as a
k
-polar point
of
V
.

When the points in question have already been specified, we simply refer to
the

k^{th}
polar cone of
V
. When such points exist but have not been specified, we refer to
a

k^{th}
polar cone of
V
. It is occasionally useful to refer to
V
itself as a zeroth polar cone of
V
(at any rational point of
V
).

Remark 2.23
.

(Iterated Polar Cones Are Nested)

Note that if
\left(P_{0},\dotsc,P_{k}\right)
is a
k
-polar point of an intersection of hypersurfaces

, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{k}(V;P_{0},\dotsc,P_{k-1})\subseteq\mathcal{C}^{k-1}(V;P_{0},\dotsc,P_{k-2})\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathcal{C}^{2}(V;P_{0},P_{1})\subseteq\mathcal{C}^{1}(V;P_{0})=\mathcal{C}(V;P_{0}).

Lemma 2.24
.

(Polar Point Lemma)

Let
V\subseteq\mathbb{P}_{K}^{r}
be an intersection of hypersurfaces and let
\left(P_{0},\dotsc,P_{k}\right)
be a
k
-polar point of
V
. Then,
L(P_{0},\dotsc,P_{k})\subseteq V
is a
k
-plane.

Proof.

We prove the claim by induction on
k
and observe that the case of
k=1
follows immediately from Lemma
2.11
. Now, consider arbitrary
k>1
and let
\left(P_{0},\dotsc,P_{k}\right)
be a
k
-polar point of
V
. Then,
\left(P_{1},\dotsc,P_{k}\right)
is a
(k-1)
-polar point of
\mathcal{C}(V;P_{0})
. Recall that
\mathcal{C}(V;P_{0})
is a cone and
L(P_{1},\dotsc,P_{k})\subseteq\mathcal{C}(V;P_{0})
is a
(k-1)
-plane which does not contain the vertex

, hence
L(P_{0},\dotsc,P_{k})\subseteq V
is a
k
-plane.
∎

Definition 2.25
.

(Type of an Intersection of Hypersurfaces)

Given an intersection of hypersurfaces

, we say that
V
is
of type
\left[\begin{matrix}d&d-1&\cdots&2&1\\
\ell_{d}&\ell_{d-1}&\cdots&\ell_{2}&\ell_{1}\end{matrix}\right]
if

has multi-degree

\displaystyle(\underbrace{d,\dotsc,d}_{\ell_{d}\text{ many}},\underbrace{d-1,\dotsc,d-1}_{\ell_{d-1}\text{ many}},\cdots,\underbrace{2,\dotsc,2}_{\ell_{2}\text{ many}},\underbrace{1,\dotsc,1}_{\ell_{1}\text{ many}}).

If
\ell_{j}=0
for any

, we may omit it in the presentation; e.g. an intersection of four quadrics is of type
\left[\begin{matrix}2\\
4\end{matrix}\right]
. When

and each

, we abbreviate the notation and say

is of type
(1,\dotsc,d)
. When
d\geq 2
,
\ell_{1}=0
, and the other
\ell_{j}=1
, we say

is of type
(2,\dotsc,d)
.

Consider an intersection of hypersurfaces
V=V_{d}\cap\cdots\cap V_{1}
of type
(1,\dotsc,d)
in
\mathbb{P}_{K}^{r}
with
\deg(V_{j})=j
. Note that
V_{1}\cong\mathbb{P}_{K}^{r-1}
and thus we can also consider

as an intersection of hypersurfaces of type
(2,\dotsc,d)
inside

.

Proposition 2.26
.

(Type of a
k^{th}
Polar Cone of an Intersection of Type
(1,\dotsc,d)
)

Let
V\subseteq\mathbb{P}_{K}^{r}
be an intersection of hypersurfaces of type
(1,\dotsc,d)
and take
k\geq 1
. A
k^{th}
polar cone
\mathcal{C}^{k}(V;P_{0},\dotsc,P_{k-1})
is of type

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}d&d-1&d-2&\cdots&3&2&1\\
1&k+1&\binom{k+2}{2}&\cdots&\binom{k+d-3}{d-3}&\binom{k+d-2}{d-2}&\binom{k+d-1}{d-1}\end{matrix}\right]

for
r\geq\binom{k+d}{d-1}
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
k
and note that the
k=1
case follows from the definition of a polar cone of an intersection of hypersurfaces, along with the observation that
\binom{1+j}{j}=1+j
for each
2\leq j\leq n-1
.

Now, suppose the claim is true for an arbitrary
k
. The number of hypersurfaces of degree
j
in a
(k+1)^{st}
polar cone is exactly the number of hypersurfaces of degree at least
j
in a
k^{th}
polar cone, e.g.
\sum\limits_{i=0}^{j}\binom{k+i}{i}
. However,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=0}^{j}\binom{k+i}{i}=\binom{k+j+1}{j}=\binom{(k+1)+j}{j},

by induction on
j
, using that
\binom{a}{b}=\binom{a-1}{b}+\binom{a-1}{b-1}
for positive integers
a>b
. This combinatorial argument also yields that
\sum\limits_{i=0}^{d-1}\binom{k+i}{i}=\binom{k+d}{d-1}
.
∎

In Subsection

, we will use iterated polar cones to establish new upper bounds on

. We first use iterated polar cones to determine

-planes on intersections of quadrics.

3
New Upper Bounds on
\text{RD}(n)

3.1
Tschirnhaus Transformations

We recall pertinent information about Tschirnhaus transformations here and refer the reader to
[
Wol2021
]
for a more complete treatment. Recall that
K_{n}=\mathbb{C}(a_{1},\dotsc,a_{n})
, a purely transcendental extension of
\mathbb{C}
with transcendence basis
a_{1},\dotsc,a_{n}
.

Definition 3.1
.

(General Polynomials)

The
general polynomial of degree
n
is the polynomial

\displaystyle\phi_{n}(z)=z^{n}+a_{1}z^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{n-1}z+a_{n}\in K_{n}[z].

Definition 3.2
.

(Tschirnhaus Transformations)

A
Tschirnhaus transformation
of the general degree
n
polynomial is an isomorphism of
K_{n}
-fields

\displaystyle K_{n}[z]/(\phi_{n}(z))\cong K_{n}[z]/(\psi(z)),

where

\displaystyle\psi(z)=z^{n}+b_{1}z^{n-1}+\cdots+b_{n-1}z+b_{n}.

It has
type
(j_{1},\dotsc,j_{k})
if
b_{j_{1}}=\cdots=b_{j_{k}}=0
.

Remark 3.3
.

(Description of Tschirnhaus Transformations)

Given primitive elements
\zeta_{\phi}
of
K_{n}[z]/(\phi_{n}(z))
and
\zeta_{\psi}
of
K_{n}[z]/(\psi(z))
, every
K_{n}
-algebra isomorphism
\Upsilon:K_{n}[z]/(\phi_{n}(z))\rightarrow K_{n}[z]/(\psi(z))
is determined by
\Upsilon(\zeta_{\phi})
, which is a
K_{n}
-linear combination of powers of
\zeta_{\psi}
:

\Upsilon(\zeta_{\phi})=w_{0}+w_{1}\zeta_{\psi}+\cdots+w_{n-1}\zeta_{\psi}^{n-1}.

Note that
\Upsilon
is an isomorphism exactly when there is some
j\geq 1
such that
w_{j}\not=0
. Let
\mathbb{A}_{K_{n}}^{n}
be the affine space with coordinates
w_{0},\dotsc,w_{n-1}
and denote the
w_{0}
-axis of
\mathbb{A}_{K_{n}}^{n}
by
\mathbb{A}_{K_{n},0}^{1}
. Corollary 3.3 of
[
Wol2021
]
shows that the space of Tschirnhaus transformations is exactly

\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{K_{n}}^{n}=\mathbb{A}_{K_{n}}^{n}\setminus\mathbb{A}_{K_{n},0}^{1}.

However, we need only work with Tschirnhaus transformations up to re-scaling. Let
\mathbb{P}_{K_{n}}^{n-1}
denote the projective space with coordinates
w_{0},\dotsc,w_{n-1}
and observe that the space of Tschirnhaus transformations up to re-scaling is

\mathcal{T}_{K_{n}}^{n}=\mathbb{P}_{K_{n}}^{n-1}\setminus\left\{[1:0:\cdots:0]\right\}.

Moreover, each
b_{m}
in Definition
3.2
is a homogenous polynomial of degree
m
in the
w_{0},\dotsc,w_{n-1}
with coefficients in
K_{n}
(e.g. an element an of
K_{n}[w_{0},\dotsc,w_{n-1}]_{(m)}
). In the following definition, we build upon the language used in
[
Wol2021
]
.

Definition 3.4
.

(Tschirnhaus Complete Intersections)

Fix
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
. For any
m\in\left\{1,\dotsc,n\right\}
, the
m^{th}
extended Tschirnhaus hypersurface
is

\tau_{m}:=\mathbb{V}(b_{m})\subseteq\mathbb{P}_{K_{n}}^{n},

and the
m^{th}
extended Tschirnhaus complete intersection
is

\tau_{1,\dotsc,m}:=\tau_{1}\cap\cdots\cap\tau_{m}\subseteq\mathbb{P}_{K_{n}}^{n}.

Similarly, the
m^{th}
Tschirnhaus hypersurface
is

\tau_{m}^{\circ}:=\tau_{m}\cap\mathcal{T}_{K_{n}}^{n}=\tau_{m}\setminus\left\{[1:0:\cdots:0]\right\},

and the
m^{th}
Tschirnhaus complete intersection
is

\tau_{1,\dotsc,m}^{\circ}:=\tau_{1,\dotsc,m}\cap\mathcal{T}_{K_{n}}^{n}\setminus\left\{[1:0:\cdots:0]\right\}.

Remark 3.5
.

(RD Bounds from Tschirnhaus Transformations)

Given a Tschirnhaus transformation
\Upsilon
(up to re-scaling) of type
(1,\dotsc,m-1)
of
\phi_{n}(z)
(i.e. a point of
\tau_{1,\dotsc,m-1}^{\circ}
), we need only consider the normal form

z^{n}+b_{m}z^{n-m}+\cdots+b_{n-1}z+b_{n},

(3)

as
\Upsilon^{-1}
takes the roots of (
3
) to the roots of
\phi_{n}
. We can then re-scale the roots over
\sqrt[n]{b_{n}}
(over a cyclic extension) and arrive at the normal form

z^{n}+c_{m}z^{n-m}+\cdots+c_{n-1}z+1.

(4)

Note that (
4
) is an algebraic function of
n-m
variables and so if we can determine an
L
-rational point of
\tau_{1,\dotsc,m-1}^{\circ}
over an extension
L/K_{n}
of sufficiently small resolvent degree, we can conclude that
\text{RD}(n)\leq n-m
.

3.2
New Bounds From Iterated Polar Cones

Definition 3.6
.

(Sub-Intersections of Given Level)

Let
V\subseteq\mathbb{P}_{K}^{r}
be an intersection of hypersurfaces of type
\left[\begin{matrix}d&d-1&\cdots&2&1\\
\ell_{d}&\ell_{d-1}&\cdots&\ell_{2}&\ell_{1}\end{matrix}\right]
defined by

\displaystyle V=\bigcap\limits_{i=1}^{d}\bigcap\limits_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\mathbb{V}(f_{i,j}),

with
\deg(f_{i,j})=i
for all

. For each
1\leq d^{\prime}\leq d
, the
sub-intersection of
V
of level

is the intersection of all defining hypersurfaces of
V
of degree
d^{\prime}
and is denoted

, i.e.

\displaystyle V_{d^{\prime}}:=\mathbb{V}(f_{d^{\prime},1})\cap\cdots\cap\mathbb{V}(f_{d^{\prime},\ell_{d^{\prime}}}).

Theorem 3.7
.

(The
n-6
Bound)

For
n\geq 21
,
\text{RD}(n)\leq n-6
.

Proof.

First, suppose that we can determine a plane
\Lambda\subseteq\tau_{1,2,3}^{\circ}
over an extension of
K_{n}
of low resolvent degree. Observe that
\deg\left(\Lambda\cap\tau_{1,\dotsc,5}\right)=20
and, by Proposition
2.19
, we can solve a polynomial of degree at most 20 to determine a point
Q
of
\Lambda\cap\tau_{1,\dotsc,5}
.

From Lemma
2.24
, it suffices to determine a 2-polar point

on

such that

for

when

Definition 3.11
.

(Parameter and Moduli Spaces of Hypersurfaces)

Fix

parameter space of degree

in

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(d;r)\cong\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\binom{r+d}{d}-1}

However, there is a natural action of

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{r}

in

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(d;r):=\mathcal{S}(d;r)/\text{PGL}(\mathbb{C},r+1).

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\circ}(d;r):=\mathcal{S}^{\circ}(d;r)/\text{ PGL}(\mathbb{C},r+1).

Lemma 3.29

(Upper Bound on

We will now introduce a constant

and give a bound on

Definition 3.31
.

(Constant for the Proof of Lemma

)

For each

, we set

\displaystyle C_{d}:=\max\left\{\binom{d+1}{i}\ |\ 0\leq i\leq d+1\right\}.

Lemma 3.32
.

(Bound on
\log(C_{d})
)

For each
d\geq 1
, it follows that

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{C_{d}}{d+1}\right)\leq d+\frac{3}{2}.

We will frequently use Stirling’s approximations for factorials, including in the proof of Lemma
3.32
, and thus state the version we use explicitly (a stronger version of which can be found in
[
Rob1955
]
):

Let
a
be an positive integer. Then,

\sqrt{2\pi}a^{a+\frac{1}{2}}e^{-a}\leq a!\leq a^{a+\frac{1}{2}}e^{1-a}.

(7)

Proof.

(Proof of Lemma
3.32
)

Our proof depends on the parity of
d+1
; we begin with the case where
d+1=2\ell
is even. Then,
C_{d}=\binom{2\ell}{\ell}=\frac{(2\ell)!}{(\ell!)^{2}}
and Stirling’s approximation yields

\displaystyle C_{d}\leq\frac{(2\ell)^{2\ell+\frac{1}{2}}e^{1-2\ell}}{\left(\sqrt{2\pi}\ell^{\ell+\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\ell}\right)^{2}}=\frac{2^{d+\frac{1}{2}}e}{\pi\sqrt{\ell}}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{C_{d}}{d+1}\right)\leq\log\left(\frac{2^{d-\frac{1}{2}}e}{\pi\sqrt{\ell}(d+1)}\right)\leq\log\left(\frac{e}{\pi(d+1)\sqrt{\ell}}\right)+\log\left(e^{d+\frac{1}{2}}\right)\leq d+\frac{1}{2}.

When
d+1=2\ell+1
is odd, we observe

\displaystyle C_{d}=\binom{2\ell+1}{\ell}\leq\binom{2\ell+2}{\ell+1}\leq\frac{2^{d+\frac{3}{2}}e}{\pi\sqrt{\ell+1}},

and thus

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{C_{d}}{d+1}\right)\leq\log\left(\frac{2^{d+\frac{3}{2}}e}{\pi\sqrt{\ell+1}}\right)\leq d+\frac{3}{2}.

∎

Recall that for each

and when

is large enough, we can determine an

over an extension

of degree

. Hence, we set

\displaystyle\varphi(d,m-d-1)=\max\left\{\frac{(m-1)!}{d!},\dim\left(\mathcal{M}(2,\dotsc,d;\vartheta(d,m-d-1))\right)+m\right\}

in Definition
3.26
. We next give a condition relating

and

Lemma 3.33
.

(The
\varphi
Condition)

Fix
d\geq 4
. For all
m\geq 2d^{2}+7d+6
, it follows that

\varphi(d+1,m-d-2)<\varphi(d,m-d-1).

(8)

Proof.

For any such

and
m
, it is always true that

\displaystyle\frac{(m-1)!}{(d+1)!}<\frac{(m-1)!}{d!}.

As a result, to conclude (
8
), we need only show that

\frac{(m-1)!}{d!}>\binom{m-1+\binom{m}{d+1}}{d+1},

(9)

since

\binom{m-1+\binom{m}{d+1}}{d+1}>\dim\left(\mathcal{H}(2,\dotsc,d;\vartheta(d,m-d-1))\right)>\dim\left(\mathcal{M}(2,\dotsc,d;\vartheta(d,m-d-1))\right).

Observe that

\binom{m-1+\binom{m}{d+1}}{d+1}=\frac{\left(m-1+\binom{m}{d+1}\right)!}{(d+1)!\left(m-d-2+\binom{m}{d+1}\right)}=\frac{1}{(d+1)!}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d+1}\left(m-i+\binom{m}{d+1}\right).

(10)

Next, we approximate
\binom{m}{d+1}
:

\binom{m}{d+1}=\frac{1}{(d+1)!}\cdot\frac{m!}{(m-d-1)!}=\frac{1}{(d+1)!}\prod\limits_{j=0}^{d+1}(m-j)\leq\frac{1}{(d+1)!}m^{d+2}\leq m^{d+2}.

(11)

By substituting the inequality (
11
) into equation (
10
) and using that
m-i\leq m
, we obtain the approximation

\displaystyle\binom{m-1+\binom{m}{d+1}}{d+1}\leq\frac{1}{(d+1)!}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d+1}\left(m+m^{d+2}\right),

which, after substituting into inequality (
9
) and multiplying both sides by

, yields the sufficient condition

(m-1)!>\frac{1}{d+1}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d+1}\left(m+m^{d+2}\right).

(12)

Notice that main term of the right side of inequality (
12
) is of the form
\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d+1}(a+b)=(a+b)^{d+1}
, hence applying the binomial theorem yields

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d+1}\left(m+m^{d+2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{d+1}\binom{d+1}{i}\left(m^{d+2}\right)^{i}m^{d+1-i},

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{d+1}\binom{d+1}{i}m^{di+2i}(m-1)^{d+1-i},

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{d+1}\binom{d+1}{i}m^{(d+1)i+d+1},

\displaystyle=m^{d+1}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{d+1}\binom{d+1}{i}\left(m^{d+1}\right)^{i}.

However, for any positive integer
a
and

it follows from induction
\sum\limits_{i=0}^{a+1}x^{i}\leq 2x^{a+1}.
Applying this to
(m-1)\sum\limits_{i=0}^{d+1}\binom{d+1}{i}\left((m-1)^{d}\right)^{i}
and recalling

yields

m^{d+1}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{d+1}\binom{d+1}{i}\left(m^{d+1}\right)^{i}\leq m^{d+1}C_{d}\left(2\left(m^{d+1}\right)^{d+1}\right).

(13)

Substituting inequality (
13
) into inequality (
12
) and simplifying, we obtain the condition

\displaystyle(m-1)!>\frac{2}{d+1}C_{d}m^{d^{2}+3d+2}.

Next, we apply Stirling’s approximation and re-arrange terms to arrive at the condition

\frac{(m-1)^{m-\frac{1}{2}}}{e^{m-1}}>\frac{2C_{d}}{\sqrt{2\pi}(d+1)}m^{d^{2}+3d+2}.

(14)

Observe that

for positive integers

. By requiring

\frac{m^{m-1}}{e^{m-1}}>\frac{2C_{d}}{\sqrt{2\pi}(d+1)}m^{d^{2}+3d+2}.

(15)

Multiplying both sides of inequality (

, and simplifying, we arrive at the condition

\left(\frac{m}{e}\right)^{m-d^{2}-3d-3}>\frac{2C_{d}e^{d^{2}+3d+2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}(d+1)}.

(16)

We take

Requiring

We note

\dim\left(\mathcal{M}\left(3;\psi(d,m-d-1)_{d-2}\right)\right)\leq\dim\left(\mathcal{M}\left(3;\psi(d+1,m-d-2)_{d-1}\right)\right),

(20)

which will then leave us to consider when

\frac{(m-1)!}{d!}<\dim\left(\mathcal{M}\left(3;\psi(d+1,m-d-2)_{d-1}\right)\right).

(21)

We begin by introducing auxiliary functions which will be useful for proving inequality (
20
) holds for
m\geq d+3
.

Definition 4.5
.

(Auxiliary Functions
\Psi(d;i)
)

For any
d\geq 2
, any
d-2\geq i\geq 1
and
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, we set

\displaystyle\Psi(d;i)(x)=x+\left\lfloor\frac{1}{x+1}\cdot\binom{x+d-i}{d-i}\right\rfloor.

Remark 4.6
.

(Key Properties of
\Psi(d;i)
)

The functions
\Psi(d,i)
satisfy

1.

\Psi(d;i+1)\left(\psi(d,m-d-1)_{i}\right)=\psi(d,m-d-1)_{i+1}
for
i\leq d-3
, and

2.

\Psi(d+1;i+1)=\Psi(d,i)
.

Lemma 4.7
.

(
\Psi(d;i)
are Non-Decreasing)

For each
m\geq d+1
with
d\geq 2
and each
d-2\geq i\geq 1
, the function
\Psi(d;i)(x)
is non-decreasing.

Proof.

First, observe that

\displaystyle\Psi(d;i)(x+1)-\Psi(d;i)(x)=1+\left\lfloor\frac{1}{x+2}\cdot\binom{x+1+d-i}{d-i}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{1}{x+1}\cdot\binom{x+d-i}{d-i}\right\rfloor,

thus

\displaystyle\Psi(d;i)(x+1)-\Psi(d;i)(x)\geq\frac{1}{x+2}\cdot\binom{x+1+d-i}{d-i}-\frac{1}{x+1}\cdot\binom{x+d-i}{d-i}.

Now, observe that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x+2}\cdot\binom{x+1+d-i}{d-i}

\displaystyle=\frac{(x+1+d-i)!}{(x+2)(d-i)!(x+1)!}=\frac{(x+d-i)!}{(d-i)!(x+1)!}\left(\frac{x+d-i+1}{x+2}\right),

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x+1}\cdot\binom{x+d-i}{d-i}

\displaystyle=\frac{(x+d-i)!}{(x+1)(d-i)!x!}=\frac{(x+d-i)!}{(d-i)!(x+1)!}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\Psi(d;i)(x+1)-\Psi(d;i)(x)\geq\frac{(x+d-i)!}{(d-i)!(x+1)!}\left(\frac{x+d-i+1}{x+2}-1\right),

and the right side is positive when
i\leq d-2
.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

(
\psi(d,m-d-1)_{d-2}
is Non-Decreasing in
d
)

Fix
m\geq 4
. Then,

\displaystyle\psi(2,m-3)_{0}\leq\psi(3,m-4)_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\psi(m-3,2)_{m-5}\leq\psi(m-2,1)_{m-4}.

Furthermore, for each
2\leq d\leq m-3
,

\displaystyle\dim\left(\mathcal{M}\left(3;\psi(d,m-d-1)_{d-2}\right)\right)\leq\dim\left(\mathcal{M}\left(3;\psi(d+1,m-d-2)_{d-1}\right)\right).

Proof.

In light of Remark
4.6
and Lemma
4.7
, it suffices to show

\displaystyle\psi(d+1,m-d-2)_{1}\geq\psi(d,m-d-1)_{0}

to prove the claim. However,

\displaystyle\psi(d+1,m-d-2)_{1}

\displaystyle=m-d-2+\left\lceil\frac{1}{m-d-1}\cdot\binom{m-2}{d-1}\right\rceil\geq m-d-1=\psi(d,m-d-1)_{0}.

∎

Remark 4.9
.

Having proved Lemma
4.8
, we now begin to work towards the second task outlined in Remark
4.4
: establishing a criterion for
m
in terms of
d
, which, when met, implies

\displaystyle\frac{(m-1)!}{d!}\leq\dim\left(\mathcal{M}\left(3;\psi(d+1,m-d-2)_{d-1}\right)\right).

Next, we establish simple functions which we use to approximate
\psi(d,m-d-1)_{d-2}
from below.

Definition 4.10
.

(Auxiliary Functions
\Omega
and
\omega(d,i)
)

For each
d\geq 4
and
d-3\geq i\geq 1
, define
\omega(d,i):\mathbb{R}_{>0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
by

\displaystyle\omega(d,i)(x)=\frac{1}{(d-i)!}x^{d-i-1}.

Similarly, for each pair
m\geq d
with
d\geq 4
, define the function
\Omega
by

\displaystyle\Omega(d,m)=\left(\omega(d,d-3)\circ\cdots\circ\omega(d,1)\right)(m-d-1).

Remark 4.11
.

(Bounding Properties of
\Omega
and
\omega(d,i)
)

Observe that for each
i
,

\displaystyle\omega(d,i+1)(\psi(d,m-d-1)_{i})\leq\psi(d,m-d-1)_{i+1}.

In particular,

\displaystyle\Omega(d,m)\leq\psi(d,m-d-1)_{d-2}.

Example 4.12
.

Consider the cases when
d=5
and
m=10,100
. Then,

\displaystyle\Omega(5,10)

\displaystyle\sim 1.185,

\displaystyle\Omega(5,100)

\displaystyle\sim 1.996\times 10^{8},

\displaystyle\psi(5,10)_{3}

\displaystyle=133,

\displaystyle\psi(5,100)_{3}

\displaystyle\sim 3.633\times 10^{8}.

Lemma 4.13
.

(Explicit Form of
\Omega
)

For any
d\geq 4
, set

\mathfrak{C}_{d}:=\prod\limits_{i=3}^{d-1}\frac{1}{(i!)^{(i-2)!}}.

For all
m\geq d+2
,

\Omega(d,m)=\mathfrak{C}_{d}(m-d-1)^{(d-2)!},

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
d
. When
d=4
, we have

\displaystyle\Omega(4,m)

\displaystyle=\rho(4,1)(m-5)=\frac{1}{3!}(m-5)^{2}=\mathfrak{C}_{2}(m-5)^{2!}.

For arbitrary
d
, recall

\displaystyle\Omega(d,m)

\displaystyle=\left(\omega(d,d-3)\circ\cdots\circ\omega(d,2)\circ\omega(d,1)\right)(m-d-1),

and

\displaystyle\Omega(d-1,m-1)

\displaystyle=\left(\omega(d-1,d-4)\circ\cdots\circ\omega(d-1,2)\circ\omega(d-1,1)\right)(m-d-1).

By definition, however,
\omega(d+1,i+1)=\omega(d,i)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\Omega(d,m)

\displaystyle=\left(\omega(d,d-3)\circ\cdots\circ\omega(d,2)\circ\omega(d,1)\right)(m-d-1),

\displaystyle=\left(\omega(d,d-3)\circ\cdots\circ\omega(d,2)\right)\left(\omega(d,1)(m-d-1)\right),

\displaystyle=\left(\omega(d-1,d-4)\circ\cdots\circ\omega(d-1,1)\right)\left(\omega(d,1)(m-d-1)\right),

\displaystyle=\Omega(d-1,\omega(d,1)(m-d-1)+d+1).

By induction, we know that

\displaystyle\Omega(d-1,\omega(d,1)(m-d-1)+d+1)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{C}_{d-1}\left(\omega(d,1)(m-d-1)\right)^{(d-3)!},

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{C}_{d-1}\left(\frac{1}{(d-1)!}(m-d-1)^{d-2}\right)^{(d-3)!},

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{C}_{d-1}\left(\frac{1}{(d-1)!}\right)^{(d-3)!}\left((m-d-1)^{d-2}\right)^{(d-3)!},

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{C}_{d}(m-d-1)^{(d-2)!}.

Consequently,

\Omega(d,m)=\Omega(d-1,\omega(d,1)(m-d-1)+d+1)=\mathfrak{C}_{d}(m-d-1)^{(d-2)!}.

∎

In the following lemma, we prove an inequality that will be useful in the proof of Proposition
4.15
, the proposition which establishes the criterion we seek.

Lemma 4.14
.

(Bounding
\log\left(\mathfrak{C}_{d}\right)
)

For each
d\geq 4
,

\log\left(\mathfrak{C}_{d}\right)\geq 2(d-2)!-2(d-2)!\log(d-1)-2(d-3)!\log(d-1).

Proof.

Observe

\log\left(\mathfrak{C}_{d}\right)=-\sum\limits_{i=3}^{d-1}(i-2)!\log(i!)\geq-\sum\limits_{i=3}^{d-1}(i-2)!\log\left(e^{1-i}i^{i}\right),

where the approximation is due to Stirling’s approximation (equation
7
). Hence,

\displaystyle\log\left(\mathfrak{C}_{d}\right)

\displaystyle\geq-\sum\limits_{i=3}^{d-1}(i-2)!\left(1-i+i\log(i)\right),

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=3}^{d-1}(i-1)!-\sum\limits_{i=3}^{d-1}(i-2)!(i)\log(i),

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d-3}(j+1)!-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d-3}j!(j+2)\log(j+2),

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d-3}(j+1)!-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d-3}j!(j+1)\log(j+2)-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d-3}j!\log(j+2),

\displaystyle\geq\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d-3}(j+1)!-\log(d-1)\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d-3}(j+1)!-\log(d-1)SL_{j=1}^{d-3}j!,

\displaystyle=(1-\log(d-1))\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d-3}(j+1)!-\log(d-1)\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d-3}j!.

Recall that for any positive integer
a
,
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{a}i!\leq 2(a!)
. Since
d\geq 4
, it follows that
1-\log(d-1)
and
-\log(d-1)
are negative, hence

\displaystyle\log\left(\mathfrak{C}_{d}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 2(1-\log(d-1))(d-2)!-2\log(d-1)(d-3)!,

\displaystyle=2(d-2)!-2(d-2)!\log(d-1)-2(d-3)!\log(d-1).

∎

Proposition 4.15
.

(The
\Omega
Condition)

Fix
d\geq 6
. For any
m\geq d^{2}-d+4
such that

\displaystyle m^{2}-\frac{5}{2}m+\frac{1}{2}<(d+1)+\log(d)\left(d+\frac{1}{2}\right)+6(d-3)!(d-2-\log(d-1)),

it follows that

\displaystyle\Phi(d,m-d-1)<\Phi(d+1,m-d-2).

Proof.

In light of Lemma
4.8
, it suffices to have

\frac{(m-1)!}{d!}<\dim\left(\mathcal{M}(3,\psi(d+1,m-d-2)_{d-1}\right)+m.

(22)

First, we approximate
\psi(d+1,m-d-2)_{d-1}
below by
\left\lceil\Omega(d+1,m-d-2)\right\rceil
to get

\dim\left(\mathcal{M}\left(3,\psi(d+1,m-d-2)_{d-1}\right)\right)+m\\
\geq\dim\left(\mathcal{M}\left(3,\left\lceil\Omega(d+1,m-d-2)\right\rceil\right)\right)+m.

(23)

Observe that

\displaystyle\dim\left(\mathcal{M}\left(3,\left\lceil\Omega(d+1,m-d-2)\right\rceil\right)\right)+m

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}\left(\left\lceil\Omega(d+1,m-d-2)\right\rceil^{3}+6\left\lceil\Omega(d+1,m-d-2)\right\rceil^{2}+11\left\lceil\Omega(d+1,m-d-2)\right\rceil+6\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{6}\left(\Omega(d+1,m-d-2)^{3}+6\Omega(d+1,m-d-2)^{2}+11\Omega(d+1,m-d-2)+6\right)

\displaystyle\ -\left(\Omega(d+1,m-d-2)+1\right)^{2}+m,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}\Omega(d+1,m-d-2)^{3}-\frac{1}{6}\Omega(d+1,m-d-2)+m.

Since
d\geq 6
and
m\geq d^{2}-d+4\geq 34
, it follows that

\displaystyle\Omega(d+1,m-d-2)\geq\Omega(6,m-8)\geq\Omega(6,26)>5.6\times 10^{12},

and consequently

\frac{1}{6}\Omega(d+1,m-d-2)^{3}-\frac{1}{6}\Omega(d+1,m-d-2)+m>\frac{1}{7}\Omega(d+1,m-d-2)^{3}.

(24)

Substituting inequality (
24
) into inequality (
22
) and re-arranging, we have the sufficient criterion

\displaystyle\frac{7(m-1)!}{d!}<\Omega(d+1,m-d-2)^{3}=\mathfrak{C}_{d}^{3}(m-d-2)^{3(d-2)!}.

Next, we apply Stirling’s approximations (equation
7
)and obtain

\displaystyle\frac{7(m-1)^{m-\frac{1}{2}}e^{2-m}}{\sqrt{2\pi}d^{d+\frac{1}{2}}e^{-d}}<\mathfrak{C}_{d}^{3}(m-d-2)^{3(d-2)!},

which we re-arrange as

\frac{(m-1)^{m-\frac{1}{2}}}{e^{m}(m-d-2)^{3(d-2)!}}<\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{7}\cdot e^{d-2}\cdot d^{d+\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\mathfrak{C}_{d}^{3}.

(25)

We take
\log
of both sides of inequality (
25
) and examine them individually. First, the right side of inequality (
25
) becomes

\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{7}\right)+(d+2)+\left(d+\frac{1}{2}\right)\log(d)+3\log\left(\mathfrak{C}_{d}\right).

(26)

By applying Lemma
4.14
to
\log\left(\mathfrak{C}_{d}\right)
, observing
\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{7}\right)\geq-1
, and simplifying, it suffices to replace expression (
26
) with

(d+1)+\left(d+\frac{1}{2}\right)\log(d)+6(d-2)!-6(d-2)!\log(d-1)-6(d-3)!\log(d-1).

(27)

The left side of inequality (
25
) becomes

\left(m-\frac{1}{2}\right)\log(m-1)-m-3(d-2)!\log(m-d-2).

(28)

For
m\geq d^{2}-d+4
,

\log(m-d-2)>\log\left(d^{2}-2d+1\right)=2\log(d-1),

and multiplying by
3(d-2)!
yields

3(d-2)!\log(m-d-2)>6(d-2)!\log(d-1).

(29)

By combining expressions (
27
) and (
28
) with inequality (
29
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left(m-\frac{1}{2}\right)\log(m-1)-m<(d+1)+\left(d+\frac{1}{2}\right)\log(d)+6(d-2)!-6(d-3)!\log(d).

Using the approximation
\log(m-1)<m-1
, we finally arrive at the condition

\displaystyle m^{2}-\frac{5}{2}m+\frac{1}{2}<(d+1)+\left(d+\frac{1}{2}\right)\log(d)+6(d-3)!\left(d-2-\log(d-1)\right),

as claimed above.
∎

Using the simple approximations
\log(d)\geq\log(6)>1
and
d-2-\log(d-1)>1
, we arrive at a simplified condition.

Corollary 4.16
.

(The Simplified
\Omega
Condition)

Fix
d\geq 6
. For any
m\geq d^{2}-d+4
such that

\displaystyle m^{2}-\frac{5}{2}m\leq 6(d-3)!+2d+1,

it follows that

\displaystyle\Phi(d,m-d-1)<\Phi(d+1,m-d-2).

Moreover,

\displaystyle F(m)\geq\frac{(m-1)!}{d!}.

Proof.

Together, Lemma
4.8
and Proposition
4.15
yield that

\displaystyle\Phi(d,m-d-1)<\Phi(d^{\prime},m-d^{\prime}-1)

for each
d<d^{\prime}<m-2
. For any
d^{\prime\prime}<d
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{(m-1)!}{d!}<\frac{(m-1)!}{(d^{\prime\prime})!}\leq\Phi(d^{\prime\prime},m-d^{\prime\prime}-1).

Hence,

\displaystyle F(m)\geq 2\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\Phi(d,m-d-1)\right\rfloor+1\geq\frac{(m-1)!}{d!}.

∎

We state and prove a corollary, then we recall and prove Theorem
4.1
.

Corollary 4.17
.

(Bounding the Ratio
\frac{F(m)}{G(m)})

For
d\geq 11
and
m\geq 2d^{2}+11d+15
,
\frac{F(m)}{G(m)}>d+1.

Remark 4.18
.

We expect that better estimates of
\frac{F(m)}{G(m)}
could reasonably be obtained. However, Corollary
4.17
suffices to prove Theorem
4.1
, which establishes that
G(m)
is the better bounding function and thus we do not need additional data on the growth rate of
F(m)
.

Proof.

(Proof of Corollary
4.17
)

Let
d\geq 11
. Recall that Corollary
3.34
applies for
m\geq 2d^{2}+7d+6
and so we set
m_{d}=2d^{2}+7d+6
. Similarly, Corollary
4.16
applies for
m\geq d^{2}-d+4
such that

\displaystyle m^{2}-\frac{5}{2}m\leq 6(d-3)!+2d+1.

Correspondingly, we set

\displaystyle M_{d}=\max\left\{m\in\mathbb{Z}\ |\ m^{2}-\frac{5}{2}m\leq 6(d-3)!+2d+1\right\}.

Observe that
2d^{2}+7d+6\geq d^{2}-d+4
and, since
d\geq 11
, we have

\displaystyle m_{d+1}=2d^{2}+11d+15<M_{d}.

Corollary
3.34
yields
G(m_{d+1})<\frac{(m_{d}-1)!}{(d+1)!}
and Corollary
4.16
yields that
F(m_{d+1})\geq\frac{(m_{d}-1)!}{d!}
. As a result, we have

\displaystyle\frac{F(m_{d+1})}{G(m_{d+1})}>\frac{\left(\frac{(m_{d}-1)!}{d!}\right)}{\left(\frac{(m_{d}-1)!}{(d+1)!}\right)}=\frac{(d+1)!}{d!}=d+1.

In fact,

\displaystyle\left(6(d-3)!+2d+1\right)-\left(m_{d+2}^{2}-\frac{5}{2}m_{d+2}\right)

is positive and strictly increasing for
d\geq 11
, so
m_{d+2}<M_{d}
. Hence, Corollaries
3.34
and
4.16
yield that

\displaystyle\frac{F(m)}{G(m)}>\frac{\left(\frac{(m-1)!}{d!}\right)}{\left(\frac{(m-1)!}{(d+1)!}\right)}=d+1.

for all
m\geq m_{d}=2d^{2}+7d+6
.
∎

Proof.

(Proof of Theorem
4.1
)

First, observe that Corollary
4.17
implies that

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{F(m)}{G(m)}=\infty,

and that
G(m)\leq F(m)
for

\displaystyle m\geq 2\left(11^{2}\right)+11(11)+15=378.

The verification of that
G(m)\leq F(m)
for
1\leq m\leq 59
comes from explicit computation and the relevant data is provided in Appendices
5.1
and
5.2
.

Finally, we address the cases of
60\leq m\leq 377
. Recall that Lemma
3.29
yields

\displaystyle\vartheta(d,m-d-1)\leq m-d-2+\binom{m}{d}.

For a fixed
d
,
\vartheta(m,d-m-1)
is bounded above by a polynomial of degree
d
in
m
with positive coefficients. Remark
3.15
yields that
\dim\left(\mathcal{M}(2,\dotsc,d;\vartheta(d,m-d-1)\right)
is also a polynomial in
m
, hence there is a polynomial
p_{d}(m)
with positive coefficients that bounds
\dim\left(\mathcal{M}(2,\dotsc,d;\vartheta(d,m-d-1)\right)
above. Hence, there is a minimal positive integer
a_{d}
such that
\frac{(a_{d}-1)!}{d!}>p_{d}(a_{d})
. Moreover,
\frac{m!}{d!}>p_{d}(m)
for all
m\geq a_{d}
. In particular,

\displaystyle G(m)\leq\frac{(m-1)!}{d!}

for all
m\geq a_{d}
.

We compute explicitly that for all
m\geq 57
,
\frac{(m-1)!}{9!}>\dim\left(\mathcal{M}(2,\dotsc,9;\vartheta(9,m-10)\right)
and thus

\displaystyle G(m)\leq\frac{(m-1)!}{9!}.

Additionally, we explicitly compute that for
m\leq 377
,

\displaystyle\frac{(m-1)!}{6!}<\dim\left(\mathcal{M}(3;\psi(7,m-d-1)_{5}\right).

and the same argument proving Corollary
4.16
yields that

\displaystyle F(m)\geq\frac{(m-1)!}{6!}.

As a consequence,

\displaystyle\frac{F(m)}{G(m)}\geq\frac{\frac{(m-1)!}{6!}}{\frac{(m-1)!}{9!}}\geq\frac{9!}{6!}=504

for all
60\leq m\leq 376
, which yields the theorem. ∎

Proof of Technical Lemma

We now recall the statement of Lemma
2.8
and provide a proof. We will continue to use the notation established in Definition
2.1
.

Lemma
2.8
. (Technical Lemma)

Let
P,Q\in\mathbb{P}^{r}(K)
and
f\in K[x_{0},\dotsc,x_{r}]
be a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d
. Applying a projective change of coordinates as necessary, we assume that

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=[1:p_{1}:\cdots:p_{r}],

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=[1:q_{1}:\cdots:q_{r}],

so that the line determined by
P
and
Q
is

\displaystyle L(P,Q)(K)=\left\{[1:\lambda p_{1}+\mu q_{1}:\cdots:\lambda p_{r}+\mu q_{r}]\ |\ [\lambda:\mu]\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(K)\right\}.

For any point
R_{\lambda:\mu}=[1:\lambda p_{1}+\mu q_{1}:\cdots:\lambda p_{r}+\mu q_{r}]\in L(P,Q)(K)
,

f\left(R_{\lambda:\mu}\right)=f(\lambda P)+f(\mu Q)+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{d-1}\frac{1}{k!}t(d-k,f,\lambda P)(\mu Q).

Proof.

Set
p_{0}=1
and
q_{0}=1
. Let
f\in K[x_{0},\dotsc,x_{r}]
be a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d
. When
r=1
, the claim follows immediately from the binomial formula. We thus assume
r\geq 2
. As partial derivatives are linear, it suffices to consider the case where
f
is a monomial:

\displaystyle f(x_{0},\dotsc,x_{r})=ax_{0}^{i_{0}}x_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots x_{r}^{i_{r}}.

Note that

\displaystyle f\left(R_{\lambda:\mu}\right)

\displaystyle=a\prod\limits_{j=0}^{r}\left(\lambda p_{j}+\mu q_{j}\right)^{i_{j}}

\displaystyle=a\prod\limits_{j=0}^{r}\sum\limits_{\ell_{j}=0}^{i_{j}}\binom{i_{j}}{\ell_{j}}(\lambda p_{j})^{\iota(j)-\ell_{j}}(\mu q_{j})^{\ell_{j}}

\displaystyle=a\sum\limits_{\ell_{0}=0}^{i_{0}}\cdots\sum\limits_{\ell_{r}=0}^{i_{r}}\left(\prod\limits_{j=0}^{r}\binom{i_{j}}{\ell_{j}}(\lambda p_{j})^{i_{j}-\ell_{j}}(\mu q_{j})^{\ell_{j}}\right).

To simplify notation, we denote an indexing set

\displaystyle I=\left\{(k_{0},\dotsc,k_{r})\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+1}\ |\ 0\leq k_{j}\leq i_{j}\right\}

and, in accordance with the notation of Definition
2.1
, partition it into subsets

\displaystyle I_{k}=\left\{(k_{0},\dotsc,k_{r})\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+1}\ |\ k_{0}+\cdots+k_{r}=k\right\}

for
0\leq k\leq d
. Thus, we write

\displaystyle f\left(R_{\lambda:\mu}\right)

\displaystyle=a\sum\limits_{I}\left(\prod\limits_{j=0}^{r}\binom{i_{j}}{k_{j}}(\lambda p_{j})^{\iota(j)-k_{j}}(\mu q_{j})^{k_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=a\sum\limits_{k=0}^{d}\sum\limits_{I_{k}}\left(\prod\limits_{j=0}^{r}\binom{i_{j}}{k_{j}}(\lambda p_{j})^{i_{j}-k_{j}}(\mu q_{j})^{k_{j}}\right)

Note that

\displaystyle f(\lambda P)

\displaystyle=a\left(\prod\limits_{j=0}^{r}(\lambda p_{j})^{i_{j}}\right)=a\sum\limits_{I_{0}}\left(\prod\limits_{j=0}^{r}\binom{i_{j}}{0}(\lambda p_{j})^{i_{j}-0}(\mu q_{j})^{0}\right),

\displaystyle f(\mu Q)

\displaystyle=a\left(\prod\limits_{j=0}^{r}(\mu q_{j})^{i_{j}}\right)=a\sum\limits_{I_{d}}\left(\prod\limits_{j=0}^{r}\binom{i_{j}}{i_{j}}(\lambda p_{j})^{i_{j}-i_{j}}(\mu q_{j})^{i_{j}}\right).

Thus,

\displaystyle f\left(R_{\lambda:\mu}\right)-f(\lambda P)-f(\mu Q)

\displaystyle=a\sum\limits_{k=1}^{d-1}\sum\limits_{I_{k}}\left(\prod\limits_{j=0}^{r}\binom{i_{j}}{l_{j}}(\lambda p_{j})^{i_{j}-k_{j}}(\mu q_{j})^{k_{j}}\right).

Consequently, it suffices to show that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k!}t(d-k,f,\lambda P)(\mu Q)=a\sum\limits_{I_{k}}\left(\prod\limits_{j=0}^{r}\binom{i_{j}}{l_{j}}(\lambda p_{j})^{i_{j}-k_{j}}(\mu q_{j})^{k_{j}}\right)

for each
1\leq k\leq d-1
. Recall that

\displaystyle[k]^{*}

\displaystyle=\left\{1,\dotsc,k\right\},

\displaystyle[r]

\displaystyle=\left\{0,1,\dotsc,r\right\},

\displaystyle I_{k}^{*}

\displaystyle=\text{Hom}_{{\textbf{Set}}}([k]^{*},[r])

and for
\iota\in I_{k}^{*}
, we set
|\iota|(j)=|\iota^{-1}(j)|
. Hence,

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{k!}t(d-k,f,\lambda P)(\mu Q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!}\sum\limits_{\iota\in I_{k}^{*}}\left(\partial_{0}^{|\iota|(0)}\cdots\partial_{r}^{|\iota|(0)}f\right)\biggr\rvert_{\lambda P}\ (\mu q_{0})^{|\iota|(0)}\cdots(\mu q_{r})^{|\iota(r)|}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!}\sum\limits_{\iota\in I_{k}^{*}}a\frac{i_{0}}{(i_{0}-|\iota|(0))!}\cdots\frac{i_{r}}{(i_{r}-|\iota|(r))!}(\lambda p_{0})^{i_{0}-|\iota|(0)}\cdots(\lambda p_{r})^{i_{r}-|\iota|(r)}(\mu q_{0})^{|\iota|(0)}\cdots(\mu q_{r})^{|\iota(r)|}

\displaystyle=\frac{a}{k!}\sum\limits_{\iota\in I_{k}^{*}}\prod\limits_{j=0}^{r}\frac{i_{j}}{(i_{j}-|\iota|(j))!}(\lambda p_{j})^{i_{j}-|\iota|(j)}(\mu q_{j})^{|\iota|(j)}

Note that for any
\iota,\iota^{\prime}\in I_{k}^{*}
whose fibers at every point of
[r]
have the same cardinality, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{0}^{|\iota|(0)}\cdots\partial_{r}^{|\iota|(0)}f=\partial_{0}^{|\iota^{\prime}|(0)}\cdots\partial_{r}^{|\iota^{\prime}|(0)}f

as partial derivatives of polynomials commute. Now, given any
\iota\in I_{k}^{*}
, we can apply a permutation of the symmetric group
S_{k}
to
[k]^{*}
and not change the number of fibers of each cardinality. However, to get a unique representative of each unordered class of partial derivative, we must identify any permutations which fix all of the fibers, but permute the elements within any such fiber. Thus, to a given
\iota
, there are

\displaystyle\frac{\left|S_{k}\right|}{\left|S_{|\iota|(0)}\right|\cdots\left|S_{|\iota(r)|}\right|}=k!\prod\limits_{l=0}^{r}\frac{1}{(|\iota|(j))!}

permutations which give the same unordered partial derivative. Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k!}t(d-k,f,\lambda P)(\mu Q)

\displaystyle=\frac{a}{k!}\sum\limits_{\iota\in I_{k}^{*}}\prod\limits_{j=0}^{r}\frac{i_{j}}{(i_{j}-|\iota|(j))!}(\lambda p_{j})^{i_{j}-|\iota|(j)}(\mu q_{j})^{|\iota|(j)}

\displaystyle=\frac{a}{k!}\sum\limits_{I_{k}}\left(k!\prod\limits_{l=0}^{r}\frac{1}{(|\iota|(j))!}\right)\prod\limits_{j=0}^{r}\frac{i_{j}}{(i_{j}-k_{j})!}(\lambda p_{j})^{i_{j}-k_{j}}(\mu q_{j})^{k_{j}}

\displaystyle=a\sum\limits_{I_{k}}\prod\limits_{j=0}^{r}\binom{i_{j}}{k_{j}}(\lambda p_{j})^{i_{j}-k_{j}}(\mu q_{j})^{k_{j}}

which yields the lemma.
∎
