Steiner Spanners.

A complementary direction is to study how Steiner points could help to construct spanners in planar domains. Here, Steiner points are points not in the input point set but in the ambient space. In
\mdmathbb{R}^{2}
(and generally in any
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}

[

showed that Steiner points could

[

We now describe our technique for constructing
(2+\varepsilon)

bound on the number of edges of
(2+\varepsilon)
-spanner requires a novel technique. Our starting point is to understand tree metrics. It is not so hard to see that tree metrics are a special case of polygonal domains. In this case, we are given a tree
T
and a subset

Non-Steiner Tree Covers for Trees.

In this problem, we are given an edge-weighted tree
T=(V_{T},E_{T},\omega_{T})
, and a set of
terminals

K\subseteq V_{T}
. We say that a collection
\mathcal{T}=\{T_{1},T_{2},\ldots,T_{\beta}\}
of
\beta

(\alpha,\beta)
-non-Steiner tree cover
if the following hold:

1.

Steiner free.
For every
i\in[\beta]
,
V(T_{i})=K
.

2.

Dominating.
For every
i\in[\beta]
,
d_{T_{i}}(x,y)\geq d_{T}(x,y)
for every two vertices
x,y\in K
.

3.

Low stretch.

\min_{i\in[\beta]}d_{T_{i}}(x,y)\leq\alpha\cdot d_{T}(x,y)
for every two vertices
x,y\in K
.

Parameter
\alpha
is called the
stretch
of the cover
\mathcal{T}
, and parameter
\beta
is called the
size
of the cover
\mathcal{T}
.

Our goal is to construct a non-Steiner tree cover with
small stretch and size
. As we will see later, the size-stretch trade-off for the non-Steiner tree cover of tree metrics is central to our construction of spanners in planar domains. While the weight of an edge
e=(x,y)
in a tree in the non-Steiner tree cover can be theoretically different from

d_{T}(x,y)
, it is always better to set the weight
w(e)=d_{T}(x,y)
since doing so will improve the stretch while preserving all the properties of a non-Steiner tree cover.

The special case of only having exactly one tree in the tree cover is the well-known Steiner Point Removal (SPR) problem for tree metrics introduced by Gupta
[
Gup01
]
. Gupta achieved stretch at most 8. This result has
applications in metric embedding
[
CGN+06
,
FRT04

and metric labeling
[
AFH+04
]
. Gupta also showed a stretch lower bound of
4-o(1)
. This lower bound was subsequently improved to

[

]
, matching the upper bound by Gupta
[
Gup01
]
.

Given that stretch 8 is the best possible for one tree, we ask if we could reduce the stretch by using more than one tree. And more generally:

Question 1.4

t

Locality Sensitive Ordering.

Chan, Har-Peled, and Jones
[
CHPJ20
]
introduced the notion of
locality-sensitive ordering
(

) and showed that for any point set in
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
, one can construct a locality-sensitive ordering, comprised of
O(1)
orderings for constant dimension

and stretch parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Their LSO has many surprising algorithmic applications, including dynamic spanners, dynamic approximate minimum spanning trees, dynamic bichromatic closest pairs, approximate nearest neighbors
[
CHPJ20
]
, and reliable spanners
[
BHO22
]
. However, for tree metrics, their notion of LSO is too strong: one needs
\Omega(n)
orderings in an LSO for any fixed
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. Filtser and Le
[
FL22a
]
introduced a more relaxed version of LSO tailored specifically for tree metrics, called
left-sided LSO
. As defined in
[
FL22a
]
, a
(\tau,\rho)
-left-sided LSO
for a tree metric
T
of
n
points is a collection
\Sigma
of
linear orderings
over
subsets
of

such that (i) every point
x
in
T
belongs to at most
\tau
linear orderings, and (ii) for any two points
x,y\in T
, there exists an ordering
\sigma\in\Sigma
with the following property: for any
x^{\prime}\preceq_{\sigma}x
and
y^{\prime}\preceq_{\sigma}y
,
\delta_{T}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq\rho\delta_{T}(x,y)
. That is, the distance of any two points
to the left
of
x
and
y
in
\sigma
is at most
\rho\cdot\delta_{T}(x,y)
. Parameter
\tau
is the
size
of the ordering
\Sigma
and
\rho
is the

. Filtser and Le
[
FL22a
]
showed that tree metrics admit a left-sided LSO with
O(\log n)
size and stretch
\rho=1
. A question raised by their work is: Could the size of the ordering be reduced to a
O(1)
? We answer this question negatively by showing that
O(\log n)
size is indeed
optimal
. We do so by using the technique developed in the proof of
Theorem

1.5
.

Theorem 1.9

There exists a tree metric
T
with
n
points such that any
(\tau,\rho)
-left-sided LSO for

with

must have

, matching the

upper bound by Filtser and Le
[
FL22a
]
.

Steiner spanners were studied for point sets in Euclidean plane with obstacles

]

Kapoor and Li

2.3
Stretch
2

In this subsection, we construct a tree cover of stretch
2
that has
O(\log n)
trees. We say that a vertex
v
is a
centroid
of a tree
T
if every connected component of
T\setminus\{v\}
has at most
n/2
vertices. Initially,
\mathcal{T}=\emptyset
. For each vertex
u
, we denote by
C(u)
the closest terminal to
u
. (If
u
is a terminal, then
C(u)=u
.) Our construction is recursive.

1.

Step 1.
If
T
contains a single terminal (the base case), then we simply return a singleton tree. Otherwise, we find a centroid
v
of
T
. Then we make a star
X_{v}
with center
C(v)
, and for every terminal
u\in K\setminus\{C(v)\}
, we add an edge
(C(v),u)
to
X_{v}
and set the weight
w_{X_{v}}(C(v),u)=d_{T}(u,C(v))
. Then we add
X_{v}
to
\mathcal{T}
.

2.

Step 2.
Let
\bar{T}_{1},\bar{T}_{2},\ldots,\bar{T}_{\kappa}
be all the connected components of
T\setminus\{v\}
. We recursively construct a non-Steiner tree cover
\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{j}
for each component
\bar{T}_{j}
, where
j\in[\kappa]
. Let
s=\max_{j\in[\kappa]}|\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{j}|
. By making duplicate copies if necessary, we assume that every cover
\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{j}
contains exactly
s
trees, denoted by
\{Y^{j}_{1},Y^{j}_{2},\ldots,Y^{j}_{s}\}
. Then, we create
s
trees
\{Z_{1},Z_{2}\ldots,Z_{s}\}
: for each
a\in[s]
, the
a
-th tree
Z_{a}
is formed by taking all the
a
-th trees

, one from each tree cover; we then connect
Y^{j}_{a}
to
Y^{1}_{a}
for every
j\in\{2,\ldots,\kappa\}
by adding an edge
(t_{j},t_{1})
from an (arbitrary) terminal
t_{j}\in Y^{j}_{a}
to an arbitrary terminal
t_{1}\in Y^{1}_{a}
. By adding all the edges
(t_{j},t_{1})
, we effectively connect every
Y^{j}_{a}
to
Y^{1}_{a}
, and finally get a tree
Z_{a}
. The weight of the edge
(t_{j},t_{1})
is

. We then add all the trees

to
\mathcal{T}
.

It follows directly from the construction that every tree in
\mathcal{T}
is non-Steiner. Furthermore, for every tree
X\in\mathcal{T}
, every edge
(x,y)\in X
has a weight

. Thus, by the triangle inequality,
X
is dominating. It remains to bound the number of trees, as well as the stretch of
\mathcal{T}
.

Bounding the Number of Trees in
\mathcal{T}
.

Let

be the number of trees in
\mathcal{T}
when applying the above algorithm to a tree

with

where the

in Step 1, and

is an upper bound for the size of all the covers
\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{1},\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{2},\ldots,\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{\kappa}
, as each connected component of

has at most
n/2

.

Analyzing the Stretch.

Let

and

. If

\begin{split}d_{X_{v}}(x,y)&=w_{X_{v}}(x,C(v))+w_{X_{v}}(y,C(v))\\
&=d_{T}(x,C(v))+d_{T}(y,C(v))\\
&\leq d_{T}(x,v)+d_{T}(v,C(v))+d_{T}(y,v)+d_{T}(v,C(v))\quad\text{(by triangle inequality)}\\
&=d_{T}(x,y)+2d_{T}(v,C(v))\\
&\leq d_{T}(x,y)+d_{T}(v,x)+d_{T}(v,y)\quad\text{(by definition, $d_{T}(v,C(v))\leq\min\{d_{T}(v,x),d_{T}(v,y)\}$)}\\
&=2d_{T}(x,y),\end{split}

(12)

implying that the stretch is at most
2
for the pair
x,y
. If
x
and
y
are in the same component of
T\setminus\{v\}
, then we get stretch
2
by induction.

3.1
Net Trees Based Spanners

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be an edge-weighted planar graph, and let
T\subset V
be a set of
n

terminals
. In this section, we work with the planar metric
(T,d_{G})
, where
d_{G}
is the shortest path distance between the terminals in
G
. The
aspect ratio
of the metric is the ratio of the maximum to the minimum distance between distinct vertices:
\rho=\max_{x,y\in T}d_{G}(x,y)/\min_{x,y\in T,x\neq y}d_{G}(x,y)
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that minimum distance between distinct terminals is 1, i.e.,
\min_{x,y\in T,x\neq y}d_{G}(x,y)=1
. In particular, the maximum distance between terminals is
\operatorname{diam}_{G}(V)=\rho
.

An
\delta
-net
in a metric space
(X,d)
is a subset
N\subset X
such that for every
x\in X
there exists
y\in N
such that
d(x,y)\leq\delta
(i.e., the closed balls of radius
\delta
centered in
N
cover
X
) and
\min_{x,y\in N,x\neq y}d(x,y)\geq\delta
(i.e., the open balls of radius
\delta/2
centered in
N
are pairwise disjoint).

For a given
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{4})
, we construct a hierarchy of nets on the terminals
T=N_{0}\supseteq N_{1}\supseteq\ldots\supseteq N_{\lceil\log_{2}\rho\rceil}
, where
N_{i}
is a
2^{i}
-net.
This hierarchy induces a
net-tree

\mathcal{T}
, where level
i
of the tree is the net
N_{i}
, and the parent of a vertex
v\in N_{i}
is
v\in N_{i+1}
(if
v\in N_{i-1}
) or another vertex
u\in N_{i+1}
such that
d_{G}(u,v)\leq 2^{i+1}
. Every vertex
v\in T
has a unique ancestor in the net
N_{i}
, that we denote by
v^{(i)}\in N_{i}
.
Using geometric series, we obtain

d_{G}(v,v^{(i)})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{i}d_{G}(v^{(j-1)},v^{(j)})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{i}2^{j}<2^{i+1}.

(13)

We construct a spanner
H_{\rm net}
for the planar metric
(T,d_{G})
as follows: At level
i=0
, we have
N_{0}=T
and we connect every terminal
u\in T
to all other terminal
v\in T
such that
d_{G}(u,v)\leq\frac{18}{\varepsilon}
. For every level
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,\lceil\log_{2}\rho\rceil\}
, let
\Delta_{i}=2^{i}/\varepsilon
; and connect every terminal
u\in N_{i}
to all other terminal
v\in N_{i}
such that

8\,\Delta_{i}\leq d_{G}(u,v)\leq 19\,\Delta_{i}.

Using a standard proof by induction, we show that
H_{\rm net}
is a
(1+\varepsilon)
-spanner on
T
.

Lemma 3.2

For
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{4})
, the graph
H_{\rm net}
is a
(1+\varepsilon)
-spanner for the metric
(T,d_{G})
.

Consider the
\binom{n}{2}
point pairs
\{x,y\}\subset T
sorted in nondecreasing order by distance
d_{G}(x,y)
. Let
\{x_{j},y_{j}\}
denote the
j

, that
d_{H_{\rm net}}(x_{j},y_{j})\leq(1+\varepsilon)d_{G}(x_{j},y_{j})
.

In the base case,
\{x_{1},y_{1}\}
is a closest pair in
G
, and we have
d_{G}(x_{1},y_{1})=1
by assumption. The edge
xy
was added to
H_{\rm net}
at level 0, and so
d_{H_{\rm net}}(x_{1},y_{1})=d_{G}(x_{1},y_{1})
. In fact, the same argument holds for all pairs
\{x_{j},y_{j}\}\subset V
with
d_{G}(x_{j},y_{j})\leq\frac{18}{\varepsilon}
.

For the induction step, consider a pair
\{x_{j},y_{j}\}\subset V
with
d_{G}(x_{j},y_{j})>\frac{18}{\varepsilon}
, and assume that
d_{H}(x,y)\leq(1+\varepsilon)d_{G}(x,y)
for all pairs
\{x,y\}\subset V
such that
d_{G}(x,y)<d_{G}(x_{j},y_{j})
. Then
x_{j}
and
y_{j}
are not adjacent at level 0.
Since
d_{G}(x_{j},y_{j})\leq\operatorname{diam}_{G}(V)\leq\rho
, there exists
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,\lceil\log_{2}\Delta\rceil\}
such that

9\,\Delta_{i}<d_{G}(x_{j},y_{j})\leq 18\,\Delta_{i}.

Considering the ancestors of
x_{j}
and
y_{j}
at level
i
, Equation (
13
) and the triangle inequality yield

\displaystyle d_{G}(x_{j}^{(i)},y_{j}^{(i)})

\displaystyle\leq d_{G}(x_{j}^{(i)},x_{j})+d_{G}(x_{j},y_{j})+d_{G}(y_{j},y_{j}^{(i)})\leq 18\,\Delta_{i}+2\cdot 2^{i+1}\leq\left(4+4\varepsilon\right)\Delta_{i}\leq 19\,\Delta_{i},

\displaystyle d_{G}(x_{j}^{(i)},y_{j}^{(i)})

\displaystyle\geq d_{G}(x_{j},y_{j})-d_{G}(x_{j}^{(i)},x_{j})-d_{G}(y_{j},y_{j}^{(i)})\geq 9\,\Delta_{i}-2\cdot 2^{i+1}\geq\left(2-4\varepsilon\right)\Delta_{i}\geq 8\,\Delta_{i},

for
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{4})
. Consequently, the edge
x_{j}^{(i)}y_{j}^{(i)}
has been added to
H_{\rm net}
at level
i
.

By the induction hypothesis,
H_{\rm net}
contains paths
\pi(x_{j},x_{j}^{(i)})
and
\pi(x_{j},x_{j}^{(i)})
of length at most
(1+\varepsilon)2^{i+1}
.
Concatenate the path
\pi(x_{j},x_{j}^{(i)})
, the edge
x_{j}^{(i)}y_{j}^{(i)}
, and the path
\pi(y_{j},y_{j}^{(i)})
. We obtain a path in
H_{\rm net}
between
x_{j}
and
y_{j}
of length

\displaystyle w(\pi(x_{j},x_{j}^{(i)}))+d_{G}(x_{j}^{(i)},y_{j}^{(i)})+w(\pi(y_{j},y_{j}^{(i)}))

\displaystyle\leq d_{G}(x_{j}^{(i)},y_{j}^{(i)})+2(1+\varepsilon)2^{i+1}

\displaystyle\leq d_{G}(x_{j}^{(i)},x_{j})+d_{G}(x_{j},y_{j})+d_{G}(y_{j},y_{j}^{(i)})+4(1+\varepsilon)2^{i}

\displaystyle\leq d_{G}(x_{j},y_{j})+2\cdot 2^{i+1}+4(1+\varepsilon)2^{i}

\displaystyle=d_{G}(x_{j},y_{j})+(8+4\varepsilon)2^{i}

\displaystyle\leq d_{G}(x_{j},y_{j})+(8+4\varepsilon)\varepsilon\Delta_{i}

\displaystyle\leq d_{G}(x_{j},y_{j})+(8+4\varepsilon)\varepsilon\cdot\frac{1}{9}\,d_{G}(x_{j},y_{j})

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{8+4\varepsilon}{9}\cdot\varepsilon\right)\cdot d_{G}(x_{j},y_{j})

\displaystyle\leq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot d_{G}(x_{j},y_{j}),

as required. This completes the induction step, hence the entire proof.

Recursive Shortest-Path Separators

Consider a single cluster,

Shortest Path Separators.

We recursively partition

along shortest path separators until each subgraph contains at most one net point in
N_{i}\cap C_{j}
. A

(for short,

has at most
\frac{2}{3}\cdot|V(G)|
vertices. According to a celebrated result by Lipton and Tarjan
[

A recursive partition of planar (or minor-free) graphs along balanced separators is well-known powerful technique; see
[

,
CCL+23
,
CCL+24
,
Fil20
,
JLN+05
,
KMS13
,
Tho04
]
for examples. Goodrich
[
Goo95
]
noticed that one can choose balanced separators in planar graph as the vertices of a

, which his composed of two shortest paths from a common endpoint such that the other two endpoints of the paths are adjacent in
G
(if

is a triangulation) or at least incident to a common face (in general). Such a balanced separator is called a

. Since can use a shortest path tree to recursively partition a planar graph
[

,
Tho04
]
, and maintain the additional property that the each subgraph in the recursion is bounded by
O(1)
shortest paths. We use the terminology presented in
[

]

Definition 3.7

Given an edge-weighted graph

For every piece

Shortcut Edges in RSPD.

Let
\Phi
be an
\eta
-RSPD for
G
with a constant
\eta
. It is a binary tree of height
O(\log n)
, and so its diameter is
O(\log n)
. We augment
\Phi
with

edges in order to reduce its diameter.

Chung and Garay
[
CG84
]
initiated the studied the minimum number of shortcut edges for paths and trees. Chazelle
[
Cha87
]
showed that a
n
-vertex tree can be augmented with
m
new edges to reduce the diameter to
O(\alpha(n,m))
, where
\alpha(n,m)
is the two-parameter inverse Ackermann function. This bound is the best possible
[
Yao82
]
, and was later rediscovered several times
[
AS87
,
BTS94
]
; see also
[
Bil22
,
FL22b
,
LMS22
,
Tho97
]
for algorithmic aspects and generalizations. Alternatively, the diameter of a tree with
n
vertices can be reduced to
2k
by adding
O(n\alpha_{k}(n))
edges,
where
\alpha_{k}(n)
is the inverse of a certain Ackermann-style function at the
\lfloor k/2\rfloor
th level of the primitive recursive hierarchy: Specifically,
\alpha_{0}(n)=\lceil n/2\rceil
,
\alpha_{1}(n)=\lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil
,
\alpha_{2}(n)=\lceil\log n\rceil
,
\alpha_{3}(n)=\lceil\log\log n\rceil
,
\alpha_{4}(n)=\log^{*}n
, etc.. Moreover,
\alpha_{2\alpha(n)+2}(n)\leq 4
, where
\alpha(n)
is the one-parameter inverse Ackermann function, which is an extremely slowly growing function
[
AS87
,
LMS22
,
Sol13
]
.

We apply the above results to the
\eta
-RSPD
\Phi
carefully. For our purposes, we distinguish between two types of shortcut edges:
type 1
shortcut edges between internal nodes of
\Phi
; and
type 2
shortcut edges between a leaf and an internal node. For our purposes (discussed below), a shortcut edge of type 1 costs roughly
O(\varepsilon^{-2})
; and one of type 2 costs roughly
O(\varepsilon^{-1})
. For this reason, we prefer shortcut edges of type 2. We add shortcut edges to
\Phi
as follows.

, with

|A_{H}|=|A|\geq 2n/3
. This implies
|E(G)|=\Omega(n\log n)
as desired.
