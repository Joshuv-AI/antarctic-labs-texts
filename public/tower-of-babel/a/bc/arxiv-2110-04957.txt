Provably Stable Full-Spectrum Dispersion Relation Preserving Schemes
Christopher Williams Kenneth Duru

Abstract
The dispersion error is often the dominant error for computed solutions of wave propagation problems with high-frequency components. In this paper, we define and give explicit examples of α \alpha -dispersion-relation-preserving schemes. These are dual-pair finite-difference schemes for systems of hyperbolic partial differential equations which preserve the dispersion-relation of the continuous problem uniformly to an α % \alpha\% -error tolerance. We give a general framework to design provably stable finite difference operators that preserve the dispersion relation for hyperbolic systems such as the elastic wave equation. The operators we derive here can resolve the highest frequency ( π \pi -mode) present on any equidistant grid at a tolerance of 5 % 5\% error. This significantly improves on the current standard that have a tolerance of 100 % 100\% error.



1
Introduction

It is well known that the dispersion error of a computational scheme has a great impact on the accuracy of a numerical simulation for hyperbolic partial differential equations (PDEs). Various efforts have been made to minimise the dispersion error for computational schemes
[
9
,
4
]
, but all of these efforts have been unable to resolve high-frequency wave modes, such as the highest-frequency mode on a computational grid called the
\pi
-mode.
These attempts have also exclusively used central finite difference (FD) stencils on co-located grids, as this has generally been accepted as necessary for a stable numerical scheme. Recently, the dual-pairing summation-by-parts (SBP) FD framework
[
5
,
1
]
has shown that this is not necessarily true. In some applications, staggered FD methods
[
11
,
10
]
on Cartesian meshes can minimise numerical dispersion errors.
However, the design of high order accurate and stable staggered FD methods in complex geometries is a challenge. See some progress in this direction
[
6
,
7
]
.

In this paper, we exploit the dual-pairing SBP FD framework on co-located grids to design FD schemes which can resolve high frequencies to an acceptable error tolerance for use in application. The dual-pairing SBP FD framework introduces additional degrees of freedom that can be tuned to diminish numerical dispersion errors.
In doing so we minimise two major numerical issues currently present in computational wave simulation, these are: the presence of spurious high-frequency wave modes; and, the reduced grid-refinement needed for the same error bounds by a factor of more than two in one-spatial dimension.
The result of this improvement is the absence of computationally fatal spurious wave modes in numerically computed solutions, and an efficiency increase that is exponential with the dimension of the problem.
For instance, in three-dimensions for a time-dependent problem our schemes achieve the same error bounds as traditional methods with
\approx 30
times less computational effort.

The structure of this paper is as follows.
First we recall the traditional SBP
[
3
,
8
]
and dual-pairing SBP
[
5
,
1
]
framework.
Here we identify what is necessary for our scheme to be provably stable.
Next we define what an
\alpha
-dispersion-relation-preserving scheme is within this framework.
We then turn to the construction of a
\alpha
-DRP scheme, giving a methodology for constructing upwind dual-pairing schemes that are able to resolve high-frequencies to
\alpha\%
error.
We find that this problem can be reduced to two optimisation problems.
The first optimisation pertains to the design of an interior stencil that is a non-convex smooth optimisation problem with a convex relaxation.
The second relates to the boundary stencils needed, and can be posed as a non-smooth convex minimisation problem.
We then compute explicit examples and present the numerical error properties of these schemes.
Small test examples are presented and compared to standard methods.

2
The Dual-Pairing SBP FD Framework

Previous work on FD methods has primarily focused on central stencil schemes, with carefully chosen boundary stencils so that the SBP property holds. It was thought that central FD stencils was necessary for provably stable schemes for general systems.
Recently, progression on skewed stencil schemes in the SBP framework has shown that non-central stencils may also be stable.
In this section we review what is necessary for a skewed stencil scheme to maintain stability.

2.2
Measure Approximation

Traditionally it has been the case that derivative operators have been approximated by FD quotients as an approximation of the limit given by a classical derivative.
Finite element methods were then formulated to solve PDE’s in their weak form, and provided a strong theoretical foundation for numerical methods.
The SBP FD framework
[
3
,
8
]
was then formulated as to mimic the theoretical guarantees of spectral methods for finite difference schemes.
It was found that SBP methods also produced quadrature rules for numerical integration.
Here, we generalise this idea by starting with an approximating discrete measure, and extrapolating differential operators in a flexible framework which allows for other critical components of the PDE to be approximated.

We will always use an atomic measure to approximate Lebesgue measure.
For simplicity we will restrict our attention to the interval
[-\pi,\pi]
and denote the Borel measurable subsets to be
\mathcal{B}([-\pi,\pi])
.

Definition 2.1
.

An atomic measure
\nu_{n}:\mathcal{B}([-\pi,\pi])\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a measure of the form

\displaystyle\nu_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{(\cdot)}(x_{i}),

(10)

where
h_{i}>0
are quadrature weights and
x_{i}\in[-\pi,\pi]
are the quadrature points.

A family of atomic measures
\{\nu_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
converges weakly to Lebesgue measure if for all continuous functions
f
on
[-\pi,\pi]
we have

\displaystyle\int fd\nu_{n}\rightarrow\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f.

(11)

See that

\displaystyle\int fd\nu_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}f(x_{i}).

(12)

In particular, we may form the inner-product

\displaystyle\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{\nu_{n}}\coloneqq\int fgd\nu_{n}=\left\langle\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}\right\rangle_{H}.

(13)

So we may view the functions as discrete evaluations, or the measures as discrete approximations to the continuous setting.
We make this clear as to inherit the theoretical framework from the finite element setting for finite difference operators.
To gain this in our dual-pairing SBP framework we demand two conditions on our approximating measure
\nu_{n}
, these are:

1.

\nu_{n}\xrightarrow{n\rightarrow\infty}dx
in the weak sense; and,

2.

there exists linear operators
D_{+},D_{-}:G_{n}\mapsto G_{n}
so that

\displaystyle\left\langle D_{+}\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}\right\rangle_{H}+\left\langle\mathbf{f},D_{-}\mathbf{g}\right\rangle_{H}=B(fg).

(14)

On the right hand side,
B:L^{\infty}([-\pi,\pi])\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is a boundary integral operator given through
B(f)\coloneqq f(\pi)-f(-\pi)
.
This operator is identically zero on the interior of our domain granting the negative duality

\displaystyle D_{+}^{T}=-D_{-},

(15)

when restricted to the interior of the domain.
There are three primary advantages to using the dual-pairing SBP framework in the finite difference setting, these are:

1.

numerical stability is preserved from the continuous setting;

2.

weak derivatives are approximated instead of strong derivatives in traditional finite difference schemes; and,

3.

the stencils can be designed to mimic the dispersion relation in the continuous setting.

The first and second properties come from the general dual-pairing framework, the last is the primary topic of this paper for the design of such schemes.
We briefly show the second property now.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{H}^{2}([-\pi,\pi])
and
g\in C^{\infty}_{0}([-\pi,\pi])
, then as
n\to\infty
we have that
D_{+}f\mapsto f^{\prime}\in L^{2}([-\pi,\pi])
.

Proof.

From the assumptions above we have

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle D_{+}f,g\right\rangle_{\nu_{n}}-\int f^{\prime}g\right|=\left|\left\langle f,D_{-}g\right\rangle_{\nu_{n}}-\int fg^{\prime}\right|.

(16)

The right hand side converges to zero as
n
tends to infinity.
This is because

\displaystyle D_{-}g(x)\rightarrow g^{\prime}(x),

(17)

strongly as
g
is smooth and
D_{-}
is of at least first order.
Write
D_{-}g=g^{\prime}+\varepsilon
where minimally
\varepsilon=O(1/n)
, then the convergence is given from the weak convergence of
\nu_{n}
as
f
is necessarily absolutely continuous.
∎

Thus even when the test function space comprises of smooth functions, the finite-difference operators
D_{+},D_{-}
may yield legitimate approximations to weak derivatives.
In summary, the dual-pairing framework allows for weak-derivative approximation and the preservation of the summation-by-parts.

2.3
Discrete Dual-Pairing SBP Operators

The matrix
B\coloneqq e_{n}e_{n}^{T}-e_{1}e_{1}^{T}
encodes the 1D boundary integral of the interval
[-\pi,\pi]
.
Again, throughout our study, the matrix
H\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is always a diagonal positive definite matrix.
We now may state the integration-by-parts and the summation-by-parts properties.

For
u,v\in C^{1}([-\pi,\pi])
we have the integration-by-parts property

\displaystyle\left\langle u^{\prime},v\right\rangle+\left\langle u,v^{\prime}\right\rangle=u(\pi)v(\pi)-u(-\pi)v(-\pi).

(18)

Let
D:\mathbb{R}^{n}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{n}
so that
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\approx D\mathbf{u}
approximates the first derivative grid function
\mathbf{u}
. The summation-by-parts property can be stated as

\displaystyle\left\langle D\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\right\rangle_{H}+\left\langle\mathbf{u},D\mathbf{v}\right\rangle_{H}=\mathbf{u}^{T}B\mathbf{v}=u_{n}v_{n}-u_{1}v_{1},

(19)

for suitable grid functions
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}
.
We may now separate the criteria of differentiation on a test function and the SBP criteria through considering the matrix equation

\displaystyle(HD)^{T}+HD=B.

(20)

This is the standard SBP framework
[
3
,
8
]
.
We will analyse the extension where we have two differential operators
D_{+}
,
D_{-}
that obey on test function vectors

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\approx D_{+}\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\approx D_{-}\mathbf{u},

(21)

and the modified SBP property

\displaystyle\left\langle D_{+}\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\right\rangle_{H}+\left\langle\mathbf{u},D_{-}\mathbf{v}\right\rangle_{H}=\left\langle\mathbf{u},B\mathbf{v}\right\rangle,

(22)

that translates to the matrix equation

\displaystyle(HD_{+})^{T}+HD_{-}=B.

(23)

As is standard notation, define the matrices
Q_{+}=HD_{+}
and
Q_{-}=HD_{-}
.
To simplify finding these operators we will put the additional constraint

\displaystyle D_{+}=H^{-1}(\overline{Q}_{+}+B/2)

\displaystyle D_{-}=H^{-1}(\overline{Q}_{-}+B/2).

(24)

Note that

HD_{+}=Q_{+}=\overline{Q}_{+}+B/2,\qquad HD_{-}=Q_{-}=\overline{Q}_{-}+B/2,

and

\overline{Q}_{+}=Q_{+}-B/2,\qquad\overline{Q}_{-}=Q_{-}-B/2.

We will assume that the stencil width for
D_{+}
is
|r_{2}+r_{1}|
for
-r_{1}\leq 0<r_{2}
.
When the matrix
H
is diagonal, it is necessarily true that for
n>>q
that
H
is identical to the identity operator for
e_{i}
with
i
away from one or
n
.
For the points such that
He_{i}=e_{i}
we call the position
i
an interior point of the stencil.
The compliment of this set is the boundary points of the operator.
Throughout this study, we will use polynomials to be the test functions considered.
We may now formally define a dual-pairing SBP framework.

Definition 2.3
.

A dual-pairing SBP operator of accuracy
q
is the 3-tuple
(D_{+},D_{-},H)
where
H>0
is a diagonal and positive matrix, and

\displaystyle D_{+}\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}^{\prime},\qquad D_{-}\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}^{\prime},

(25)

for polynomials of degree
q
or less on the interior points and polynomials of degree
\lfloor q/2\rfloor
on the boundary and the involved matrices obey

\displaystyle(HD_{+})^{T}+HD_{-}=B.

(26)

The high frequency modes (in the neighborhood of the
\pi
-modes) for both traditional stencils and the stencils we consider carries the majority of the numerical error.
It is useful to consider some form of artificial dissipation for high-frequency components.
For such scenarios we consider upwind dual-pairing SBP operators.

Definition 2.4
.

A upwind dual-pairing SBP operator is a dual-pairing SBP operator
(D_{+},D_{-},H)
that additionally satisfies

\displaystyle S\coloneqq\frac{\overline{Q}_{+}^{T}+\overline{Q}_{+}}{2},

(27)

is negative semi-definite.

Before we can define a dispersion-relation-preserving-scheme we must recall some basic properties of the dispersion relation for linear hyperbolic problems.

2.4
\alpha
-Dispersion Relation Preserving Schemes

In this section we recall what the dispersion relation is for a simple hyperbolic system.
We then define
\alpha
-dispersion-relation-preserving schemes (
\alpha
-DRP schemes).

Consider the simplest hyperbolic system

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial x},\qquad\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}.

(28)

Scaling constants and tensor products may be used to generalise our method to higher dimensional linear hyperbolic systems, but here we will only consider this simple case.
Using the notation
\mathbf{u}=(v,\sigma)^{T}
, take the Fourier solution basis of the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{\omega,k}(x,t)=\mathbf{u}_{0}\exp(-\mathrm{i}(\omega t-kx)).

(29)

Here
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
is the angular frequency,
k\in\mathbb{R}
is the spatial wave number and
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the constant polarisation vector.
For the continuous model problem (
28
), the wave-mode
\mathbf{u}_{\omega,k}
belongs to the solution if and only if

\displaystyle\omega^{2}=k^{2},\quad k\in\mathbb{R}.

(30)

This is the dispersion relation for this model problem (
28
).

Remark 2.5
.

We can take square roots in (
28
) yielding the linear dispersion relation

\displaystyle\omega=k,\quad k\in\mathbb{R}.

(31)

For the current study, we are only interested in analysing the dispersion relation for the interior of a given stencil.

We consider the
2\pi
-periodic functions in interval
x\in[-\pi,\pi]
and the uniform discretisation (
2
) of the interval.

Note that due to periodicity of the grid functions
\mathbf{v}
we must have

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{1-j}=\mathbf{v}_{n-j},\quad\mathbf{v}_{n+j}=\mathbf{v}_{1+j},\quad j=0,1,2,\cdots,n-1.

(32)

Definition 2.6
.

Let
(D_{+},D_{-},H)
be a dual-pairing SBP operator, and let
D_{\eta,\circ}
be the interior stencil for
\eta\in\{-,+\}
.
Let
\prescript{}{\infty}{\ell}_{\infty}
be the space of doubly infinite bounded sequences of real numbers, then
D_{\eta,\circ}:\prescript{}{\infty}{\ell}_{\infty}\mapsto\prescript{}{\infty}{\ell}_{\infty}
.
These operators are the
interior upwind operators
.

Recall for interior points
He_{i}=e_{i}
, where
e_{i}
is the
i
-th unit vector, and so for the interior operators we only need

\displaystyle D_{+,\circ}^{T}+D_{-,\circ}=\mathbf{0},

(33)

for the summation-by-parts criteria.

Now let us consider the semi-discrete counterpart

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}=D_{+,\circ}\boldsymbol{\sigma},\qquad\frac{d\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{dt}=D_{-,\circ}\mathbf{v},

(34)

where we have replaced the spatial derivatives with the finite difference operators
D_{+,\circ},D_{-,\circ}
.

We consider the interior stencils only, thus

\left(D_{+,\circ}\mathbf{v}\right)_{j}=\frac{1}{h}\sum_{l=-r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\alpha_{l}v_{j+l},\qquad\left(D_{-,\circ}\mathbf{v}\right)_{j}=\frac{1}{h}\sum_{l=-r_{2}}^{r_{1}}\beta_{l}v_{j+l},\qquad 0\leq r_{1}<r_{2},

with the consistency requirements

\sum_{l=-r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\alpha_{l}=\sum_{l=-r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\beta_{l}=0,\qquad\sum_{l=-r_{1}}^{r_{2}}l\alpha_{l}=\sum_{l=-r_{2}}^{r_{1}}l\beta_{l}=1.

Here,
h>0
is the uniform grid spacing,
\alpha_{l},\beta_{l}
are the non-dimensional constant coefficients defining the upwind finite difference stencils. Note that
\beta_{l}=-\alpha_{-l}
for
l=-r_{2},-(r_{2}-1),\cdots r_{1}
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N}:[-\pi,\pi]\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be the numerical dispersion relation for a given discrete operator for the semi-discrete approximation (
34
).
For a given dual-paring SBP scheme we may find an explicit expression for the discrete dispersion relation.

Let
(D_{+,\circ},D_{-,\circ})
be an internal upwind pairing, then introduce
\widetilde{k}=hk
the numerical wave number and define the discrete Fourier symbols

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}_{+}\coloneqq\sum_{l=-r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\alpha_{l}(\cos(l\widetilde{k})+\mathrm{i}\sin(l\widetilde{k})),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}_{-}\coloneqq-\sum_{l=-r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\alpha_{l}(\cos(l\widetilde{k})-\mathrm{i}\sin(l\widetilde{k})),

(35)

such that

\displaystyle h\left(D_{+,\circ}\mathbf{v}\right)_{j}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{+}\mathbf{v}_{j}(t),\qquad h\left(D_{-,\circ}\mathbf{v}\right)_{j}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{-}\mathbf{v}_{j}(t),

(36)

where

\mathbf{v}_{j}(t)=\widehat{v}_{0}\exp(-\mathrm{i}(\omega t-kx_{j})).

Lemma 2.7
.

Consider the semi-discrete approximation (
34
) with
(D_{+,\circ},D_{-,\circ})
. The semi-discrete discrete dispersion relation for (
34
) is given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}_{N}=-\boldsymbol{\omega}_{+}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{-}=\left(\sum_{l=-r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\alpha_{l}\cos(l\widetilde{k})\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{l=-r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\alpha_{l}\sin(l\widetilde{k})\right)^{2},

(37)

where
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N}=h\omega
is numerical frequency and
\widetilde{k}=hk
is the numerical wave number.

Proof.

Inserting the plane waves

\mathbf{v}_{j}(t)=\widehat{v}_{0}\exp(-\mathrm{i}(\omega t-kx_{j})),\quad\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}(t)=\widehat{\sigma}_{0}\exp(-\mathrm{i}(\omega t-kx_{j}))

in the semi-discretised PDE (
34
), we have

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\omega_{N}\mathbf{v}_{j}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{+}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j},\qquad-\mathrm{i}\omega_{N}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{-}\mathbf{v}_{j},

(38)

and the solvability condition

\displaystyle\text{det}\left(\begin{pmatrix}-\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N}&&-\boldsymbol{\omega}_{+}\\
-\boldsymbol{\omega}_{-}&&-\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N}\end{pmatrix}\right)=0\iff\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}_{N}+\boldsymbol{\omega}_{+}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{-}=0.

(39)

Re-arrangement of the terms to find
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N}
and using
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{+}
,
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{-}
as defined in (
35
) gives the result.
∎

We define the numerical dispersion relation

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}_{N}(k)=\left(\sum_{l=-r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\alpha_{l}\cos(l{k})\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{l=-r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\alpha_{l}\sin(l{k})\right)^{2},\qquad{k}\in[-\pi,\pi].

(40)

Similarly, for the traditional SBP operator we have the numerical dispersion relation

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}_{N}(k)=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r}2\gamma_{j}\sin(j{k})\right)^{2},\qquad{k}\in[-\pi,\pi],

(41)

where
\gamma_{j}
are the non-dimensional constant coefficients of the finite difference operator, with
\gamma_{-j}=-\gamma_{j}
,
\gamma_{0}=0
, and satisfying the consistency requirements

\sum_{l=-r}^{r}\gamma_{l}=0,\qquad\sum_{l=1}^{r}2l\gamma_{l}=1.

By comparing the numerical dispersion relation
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N}(k)
, defined in (
40
)–(
41
) and the continuous dispersion relation
\omega(k)
, defined in (
30
), we can determine the numerical dispersion error.
Define the point-wise dispersion error

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{d}(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N},k)=|\omega(k)-\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N}(k)|,\quad{k}\in[-\pi,\pi].

(42)

We are interested in schemes that are accurate across the whole spectrum
{k}\in[-\pi,\pi]
, so we define the maximal relative dispersion error to be

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N})=\max_{k\in[-\pi,\pi]}\frac{\varepsilon_{d}(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N},k)}{\omega(k)}.

(43)

Note that for the traditional SBP operators based on central finite difference scheme, from (
41
) we have
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N}(\pm\pi)=0
.
Therefore, all central stencil schemes have a maximal dispersion error of
100\%
, and are completely unable to resolve the highest frequency modes on a given computational grid
[
9
,
4
]
.

We are interested in constructing schemes whose dispersion error for high frequencies is acceptable for most application problems. This motivates our definition for a
\alpha
-DRP scheme.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
(D_{+,\circ},D_{-,\circ})
be an interior dual-pairing SBP operator.
We call
(D_{+,\circ},D_{-,\circ})
an
\alpha
-DRP operator if the maximal relative dispersion error is bounded by
\alpha
.
When a dual-pairing SBP operator has a
\alpha
-DRP operator on its interior, we call it a
\alpha
-DRP dual pairing SBP operator.

For many applications, the tolerance
\alpha=5\%
is an acceptable error.
Colloquially, we will call a scheme DRP-preserving if it is a
\alpha
-DRP scheme with
\alpha<5\%
.
In the next section we show how we can construct such a scheme.

3
Construction of
\alpha
-Dispersion Relation Preserving Schemes

In this section we show how to construct DRP-schemes which are provably stable through the dual-pairing SBP framework.
Previously work
[
9
,
4
]
has looked at optimising the dispersion relation for central stencil schemes.
All such attempts are
100\%
-DRP schemes that support spurious wave modes in their solution, being unable to resolve wave numbers higher than
\pi/2
.
Due to the dual-pairing framework, we may resolve the full spectrum to
5\%
error.

3.1
Interior Stencil

First we focus on designing the interior stencil of our scheme.
Recall we are interested in the minimisation

\displaystyle\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N}\in\Omega}|\omega(k)-\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N}(k)|

(44)

for a discrete dispersion relation
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N}
from a family of candidate models
\Omega
and all
k\in[-\pi,\pi]
.
Ideally, we would consider the infinity norm of this quantity but for computational feasibility we will consider the least-squares minimisation on the whole spectrum.
To the authors knowledge, this is the first successful attempt to optimise the dispersion error for the whole spectrum.
Note that continuous angular frequency
\omega(k)
and the numerical counterpart
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N}(k)
take the sign of its argument
k\in[-\pi,\pi]
.
We consider the following least squares optimisation

\displaystyle\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N}\in\Omega}\|\omega-\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N}\|_{2}^{2},

(45)

for an appropriate family of candidate models
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N}
.
It is important that our approximant is exact around
k=0
, as this determines the error convergence with grid refinement.

As stated above, for convenience we will work with quadratic positive functions
\omega^{2}(k)
,
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N}^{2}(k)
, however, using the following Lemma the analysis holds also for
\omega(k)
,
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N}(k)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in L^{2}([-\pi,\pi])
and
\omega_{1}(k)
,
\omega_{2}(k)
that take the sign of its argument
k\in[-\pi,\pi]
, then we have

\displaystyle\|\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\|\omega_{1}^{2}-\omega_{2}^{2}\|_{2}.

(46)

Proof.

First by the reverse triangle inequality and non-negativity,

\displaystyle\int|\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}|\leq\int|\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}|.

(47)

Now we have

\displaystyle\int|\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}|^{2}\leq\int|\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}||\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}|=\int|\omega_{1}^{2}-\omega_{2}^{2}|.

(48)

So we gain

\displaystyle\|\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\omega_{1}^{2}-\omega_{2}^{2}\|_{1},

(49)

but as we are compactly supported:

\displaystyle\|\omega_{1}^{2}-\omega_{2}^{2}\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\|\omega_{1}^{2}-\omega_{2}^{2}\|_{2}.

(50)

∎

If we now consider
\Omega
to be a convex set, then both

\displaystyle\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N}\in\Omega}\|\omega-\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N}\|_{2},

(51)

and

\displaystyle\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N}\in\Omega}\|\omega^{2}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}_{N}\|_{2},

(52)

are strictly convex and share the same minimiser if it is in the candidate model set due to Lemma
3.1
.

Now the function to be approximated,
k\mapsto k^{2}
, is smooth around zero.
To make our optimisation computationally feasible, we will place both
\omega
and our elements of
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N}\in\Omega
into orthonormal cosine bases. That is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}_{N}=\sum_{i}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{N})_{i}\cos(ki),\quad\omega^{2}=\sum_{i}(\beta)_{i}\cos(ki),\quad k\in[-\pi,\pi],

(53)

where
(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{N})_{i},(\beta)_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
are the Fourier coefficients.
This is a natural choice for the function we are aiming to approximate.
The coefficients
(\beta)_{i}
for the continuous dispersion relation have a closed form expression, namely

(\beta)_{i}=\frac{4(-1)^{i}}{i^{2}}.

The coefficients
(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{N})_{i}
for the numerical dispersion relation depend on the finite difference stencils and can be computed using a symbolic mathematical software, such as Maple. The coefficients form a finite square-summable sequence, and vanishes after a finite
i\geq j
, that is

(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{N})_{i}=0,\qquad\forall i\geq j.

Lemma 3.2
.

For positive functions
\omega,\boldsymbol{\omega}\in L^{2}([-\pi,\pi])
,

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\omega}-\omega\|_{2}^{4}\leq 2\pi\|\boldsymbol{\beta}-\beta_{j}\|_{2}^{2}+\mathbb{T}(j)

(54)

where
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\ell^{2}_{0,j}
the square summable space of sequences whose entries are zero after
j
entries,
\beta\in\ell^{2}
are such that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}=\sum_{i}(\boldsymbol{\beta})_{i}\cos(ki),\quad\omega^{2}=\sum_{i}(\beta)_{i}\cos(ki),\quad k\in[-\pi,\pi],

(55)

with
\beta_{j}
the projection of
\beta
into
\ell^{2}_{0,j}
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}(j)=O(1/j^{4}).

(56)

Proof.

Applying Lemma
3.2
and Parseval’s formula yields

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\omega}-\omega\|_{2}^{4}\leq 2\pi\|\boldsymbol{\beta}-\beta\|_{2}^{2}.

(57)

Now by Bessel’s equality for
(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\beta)_{\leq j}
the
j^{th}
level truncation of the sequence and
(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\beta_{j})_{>j}
its compliment we have

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\beta}-\beta\|_{2}^{2}=\|(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\beta)_{\leq j}\|_{2}^{2}+\|(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\beta_{j})_{>j}\|_{2}^{2}.

(58)

For
j^{\prime}>j
the entry in
(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\beta)_{j^{\prime}}
decays like
1/{(j^{\prime})^{2}}
as the extended periodic function
\omega^{2}=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}(k-2\pi l)^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{[l-\pi,l+\pi)}
on
\mathbb{R}
is once weakly differentiable, and so the square sum of these terms behaves like
1/j^{4}
completing the claim.
∎

Last, it would be beneficial if we could find a parameterised family that forms a linearly independent basis set.
For instance if
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N}^{2}:[-\pi,\pi]\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is of the form

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N}^{2}(k)=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\beta_{j}\cos(jk),

(59)

for

, then the functions

form a linearly independent basis for the family

\displaystyle\Omega=\left\{\sum_{r}\gamma_{r}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{r}^{2}\ |\ \gamma_{r}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

(60)

Further if
a=0
and as

,
\overline{\lim_{b\rightarrow\infty}\Omega}=L^{2}([-\pi,\pi])
.
This gives a approximation space for
L^{2}([-\pi,\pi])
, but it is still of great importance that the approximant
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}
is uniformly a good approximant to
\omega
around zero.
For our stencils, we fix

so for each member of the generated
\boldsymbol{\omega}
uniformly approximates
\omega
around

, then use the remaining free-parameters to approximate the function in the higher-frequency region.

All that is left is to find a family

Let

\displaystyle(D_{+})_{p}\coloneqq D_{p}-\frac{h^{p+1}}{\kappa_{p}}(\Delta_{+}\Delta_{-})^{p/2+1}-\frac{h^{p}}{\kappa_{p-1}}\Delta_{-}(\Delta_{+}\Delta_{-})^{p/2-1},

\displaystyle({D_{+,\circ}})_{p}^{T}+(D_{-,\circ})_{p}=\mathbf{0}.

Define

\displaystyle\Omega_{+}^{a,b}

\displaystyle\Omega_{-}^{a,b}

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\Omega^{a,b}
for any

for

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}=\sum_{j=0}^{N}c_{j}\cos(jk),

(66)

Proof.

It is sufficient to check

For

∎

Putting all of our work together we can prove the following bound.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\omega},\omega\in L^{2}([-\pi,\pi])
be positive functions whose squares are once weakly differentiable and have Fourier co-efficients
\boldsymbol{\beta},\beta\in\ell_{2}

\displaystyle\|\omega-\boldsymbol{\omega}\|_{2}^{4}\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\|\beta_{l}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}+O(1/l^{4}),

(68)

for

the

level truncation of
\boldsymbol{\beta},\beta
.

Currently, we have the strong assumption that our actual constraint set
\Omega^{a,b}
has the form
L^{\infty}([-\pi,\pi])\cap\mathcal{C}
, for a convex set
\mathcal{C}
.
This is not in general true, but we can note that

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\beta_{j})_{l}=\sum_{i,j}c_{i,j}\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j}+c_{0,0},

(69)

where we have the constraint set
\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}=1
.
An appropriate convex relaxation of this problem is to consider the cost of the form

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\beta_{j})_{l}=\sum_{i,j}c_{i,j}\pi_{i,j}+c_{0,0},

(70)

for
\pi_{i,j}
in
\mathbb{R}
such that
\pi_{i,j}\leq\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j}
and
\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}=1
.
Such a relaxation still has uniqueness and existence of minimiser, but as soon as we introduce the constraint of equality on
\pi_{i,j}=\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j}

Example 3.5
.

Consider the case
N=2
with the dummy variables
\pi_{1,1},\pi_{1,2},\pi_{2,2}

\displaystyle\pi_{1,1}=\gamma_{1}^{2},

\displaystyle\pi_{1,2}=\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2},

\displaystyle\pi_{2,2}=\gamma_{2}^{2}.

(71)

We will consider the three dimensional space spanned by
\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{3}
.
Recall
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
granting that the constraints given correspond to a parametric parabloid, meanwhile the constraint

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}=1,

(72)

is equivalently the plane given through the implicit equation

\displaystyle(\pi_{1,1},\pi_{1,2},\pi_{2,2})\mapsto(\pi_{1,1},\pi_{1,2},1-\pi_{1,1}-2\pi_{1,2}).

(73)

The intersection of these curves corresponds to a one-dimensional subspace

given through the parametric equation

\displaystyle(\pi_{1,1},\pi_{1,2},\pi_{2,2})=(u^{2},u(1-u),(1-u)^{2}),

(74)

for

.
Now consider the cost function

\displaystyle C(\pi_{1,1},\pi_{1,2},\pi_{2,2})=(\pi_{1,1}-1)^{2}+\pi_{1,2}^{2}+(\pi_{2,2}-1)^{2}.

(75)

This is a convex function in the parameters
\pi_{1,1},\pi_{1,2},\pi_{2,2}
, but our constrained optimisation yields two global minima.
Consider the sublevel sets

\displaystyle C_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\{(\pi_{1,1},\pi_{1,2},\pi_{2,2})\ |\ C(\pi_{1,1},\pi_{1,2},\pi_{2,2})\leq C(\pi_{1,1},\pi_{1,2},\pi_{2,2})\},

(76)

where it is clear

as

is convex.
We may reform our optimisation criteria as

\displaystyle\min_{\varepsilon\geq 0}\{\varepsilon\ |\ C_{\varepsilon}\cap P\neq\emptyset\}.

(77)

This occurs at

showing the minimum is one, and there are two points of intersection corresponding to

For the

Finally, we can state the (finite-dimensional) optimisation to be performed.

\displaystyle\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\Omega^{a,b}}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}(\boldsymbol{\pi})-\beta_{j}\|_{2}^{2}

(78)

\displaystyle\pi_{i,j}=\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j},

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}=1.

(79)

\pi_{i,j}

By a simple bootstrap argument, we may also consider the minimisation

\displaystyle\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\Omega^{a,b}}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}(\pi)-\beta_{j}\|_{\rho}^{2}

\displaystyle\pi_{i,j}=\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j},

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}=1,

Remark 3.7

A heuristic observed for designing interior operators is that higher accuracy can be found with increasing

3.2

In this section we show how to close the boundary stencil for a given interior stencil so that we keep the dual-pairing SBP framework.

We assume the ansatz

\displaystyle(Q_{+})_{i,j}=\begin{cases}q_{i,j},&\text{ if }i,j\leq s\\

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}

Define the linear operator

\displaystyle(S(\theta))_{i,j}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}(\theta_{i,j}+\theta_{j,i})/2,&\text{ if }i,j\leq s\\

(97)

for

Further define the sets

\displaystyle{C}_{\varepsilon_{1}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{x\in\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}\ |\ -\left\langle u,xv\right\rangle\geq\varepsilon_{1},\ \forall u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\};

(98)

\displaystyle{B}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{x\in\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}\ |\ x_{i,j}=s_{i,j}\ \forall(i,j)\in b\subsetneq\{1,\cdots,n\}^{2}\};

(99)

and

, and

\displaystyle{A}_{\varepsilon_{2}}\coloneqq\{\theta\in\mathcal{P}\ |\ A_{1}(\theta)=0,\ A_{2}(\theta)\geq\varepsilon_{2}\}.

(100)

\displaystyle\delta_{E}(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\infty&\text{if }x\in E\\

(101)

\displaystyle{C}_{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}}(\theta)\coloneqq\delta_{{A}_{\varepsilon_{2}}}(\theta)+\delta_{{B}}(S(\theta))+\delta_{{C}_{\varepsilon_{1}}}(S(\theta))+c(\theta),

Proof.

First, the operator

is convex as it is linear.
The set

\displaystyle-\left\langle u,(tx_{1}+(1-t)x_{2})v\right\rangle=-t\left\langle u,x_{1}v\right\rangle-(1-t)\left\langle u,x_{2}v\right\rangle\geq\varepsilon_{1}.

(103)

Similarly, the sets

The function
\delta_{E}
is convex whenever the set

Finally, the sum of convex and strongly convex functions is strongly convex completing the claim.

∎

Unfortunately, the indicator function is not smooth, and so we have posed a non-smooth convex optimisation problem.
We will use the ADMM projection method to solve this.

\displaystyle\theta^{*}=\argmin_{\theta\in\mathcal{P},\ \theta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime\prime}\in S(\mathcal{P})}\{\delta_{{A}_{\varepsilon_{2}}}(\theta)+\delta_{{B}}(\theta^{\prime\prime})+\delta_{{C}_{\varepsilon_{1}}}(\theta^{\prime})+c(\theta)\}

subject to

\displaystyle\theta^{\prime}=S(\theta),

\displaystyle\theta^{\prime\prime}=S(\theta).

(105)

Let
\|\cdot\|_{F}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta,\theta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime\prime};\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=C_{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}}(\theta,\theta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle-\left\langle\lambda_{1},S(\theta)-\theta^{\prime}\right\rangle_{F}-\left\langle\lambda_{2},S(\theta)-\theta^{\prime\prime}\right\rangle_{F}

(106)

\displaystyle+(t/2)\|S(\theta)-\theta^{\prime}\|_{F}^{2}+(t/2)\|S(\theta)-\theta^{\prime\prime}\|_{F}^{2}.

(107)

We have the three sub-problems to solve:

\displaystyle\argmin_{\theta^{\prime\prime}}\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta,\theta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime\prime};\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}),

(108)

\displaystyle\argmin_{\theta^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta,\theta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime\prime};\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}),

(109)

\displaystyle\argmin_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta,\theta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime\prime};\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}).

(110)

The last can be efficiently computed with a least squares solver as a convex least squares minimisation with convex constraints.
For our problem, the accuracy constraints are polynomials in the

and

variables.
These equations are of the form

\displaystyle\sum_{i}c_{i}q_{i,j}h_{i}=0,

(111)

for all stencils of accuracy

.
We may eliminate the

values from these equations as we know they are strictly positive and form a linear system, call this system
\mathbf{C}(\theta)=0
.
To ensure regularity on our solutions we consider the ridge-regression

\displaystyle\theta^{*}=\argmin_{\theta\in\mathcal{P}}\|\mathbf{C}(\theta)\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{c}\|\theta\|_{2}^{2}=\argmin_{\theta\in\mathcal{P}}c(\theta),

(112)

for a smoothing parameter
\lambda_{c}>0
.
The other two can be solved through using projective methods.
We firstly require the following results.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
P_{{C}_{\varepsilon_{1}}}:\mathcal{S}\mapsto{C}_{\varepsilon_{1}}
be the least squares projection operator onto
{C}_{\varepsilon_{1}}
, then

\displaystyle\argmin_{\theta^{\prime\prime}}\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta,\theta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime\prime};\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=P_{{C}_{\varepsilon_{1}}}(S(\theta_{k})-(\lambda_{1})_{k}/t),

(113)

where
t>0
.
Similarly,

\displaystyle\argmin_{\theta^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta,\theta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime\prime};\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=P_{{B}}(S(\theta_{k})-(\lambda_{1})_{k}/t),

(114)

for some
t>0
.

Proof.

The minimisation of the Lagrangian is equivalent to the proximal

\displaystyle\argmin_{\theta^{\prime\prime}}\{\delta_{{A}_{\varepsilon_{2}}}(\theta^{\prime\prime})+\left\langle\lambda_{2},\theta^{\prime\prime}\right\rangle_{F}+(t/2)\|S(\theta)-\theta^{\prime\prime}\|_{F}^{2}\}.

(115)

Restricting the argument values, shifting by a constant and completing the square yields

\displaystyle\argmin_{\theta^{\prime\prime}\in{A}_{\varepsilon_{2}}}\|S(\theta)-\lambda_{2}/t-\theta^{\prime\prime}\|,

(116)

which is the least squares projection operator.
For all
x\in\mathcal{S}
we have the decomposition
x=Q^{T}x_{d}Q
where
Q
is orthonormal and
x_{d}
is a diagonal matrix.
From here it is straight-forward to find that

\displaystyle\argmin_{\theta^{\prime\prime}\in{A}_{\varepsilon_{2}}}\|S(\theta)-\lambda_{2}/t-\theta^{\prime\prime}\|=Q^{T}(x_{d})_{\varepsilon_{2}}Q,

(117)

where the entries of
(x_{d})_{\varepsilon_{2}}
are the minimum of
-\varepsilon_{2}
and the diagonal entries of
x_{d}
.
An analogous argument holds for the second case with the appropriate projection operator simply being replacing the entries of
S(\theta)-\lambda_{1}/t-\theta^{\prime}
with those specified in
{B}
.
∎

This leads to the 3-step ADMM algorithm, which has guaranteed convergence due to the co-efficient matrices involved having one pair-wise orthogonal condition
[
chen2013convergence
]
.
Now the update rule for the ADMM method is:

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=P_{{C}_{\varepsilon_{1}}}(S(\theta_{k})-(\lambda_{1})_{k}/t)

(118)

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=P_{{B}}(S(\theta_{k})-(\lambda_{2})_{k}/t)

(119)

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\theta\in{A}_{\varepsilon_{2}}}\left\{\frac{2}{t}c(\theta)+\|S(\theta)-\theta_{k+1}^{\prime}-(\lambda_{1})_{k}/t\|_{F}^{2}+\|S(\theta)-\theta_{k+1}^{\prime\prime}-(\lambda_{2})_{k}/t\|_{F}^{2}\right\}

(120)

\displaystyle(\lambda_{1})_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(\lambda_{1})_{k}-t(S(\theta_{k+1})-\theta_{k+1}^{\prime})

(121)

\displaystyle(\lambda_{1})_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(\lambda_{2})_{k}-t(S(\theta_{k+1})-\theta_{k+1}^{\prime\prime}).

(122)

The results for orders
4,5,6,7
are given in the appendix.
These stencils can be found in rational arithmetic, but are presented for readability in floating point.

4
Semi-discrete analysis in 1D

4.1
Refinement Error Bounds

In this section we consider how the dispersion error acts under grid-refinement.
We see to gain asymptotic results, we only require the dispersion approximate
\boldsymbol{\omega}
to be well behaved around zero.
To see this, we consider the behaviour of
T_{h}\boldsymbol{\omega}
where
T_{h}
is an operator that maps
\boldsymbol{\omega}
to its refinement by a factor of
h
and compositions of these maps.
More rigorously, let

\displaystyle{h}\in\ell_{<1,\infty}\coloneqq\{{h}\in\ell_{\infty}\ |\ {h}_{i}<1\}.

(123)

For each
{h}\in\ell_{<1,\infty}
we associate the sequence of spaces

\displaystyle X_{{h}}=(X_{0},X_{1},\dots),

(124)

where
X_{0}=[-\pi,\pi]
and
X_{k}\coloneqq[-\prod_{j=1}^{k}(\frac{1}{h_{j}})\pi,\prod_{j=1}^{k}(\frac{1}{h_{j}})\pi]
.
Define the grid refinement operator
T_{{h},j}:L^{2}(X_{j-1})\mapsto L^{2}(X_{j})
through

\displaystyle T_{{h},j}(g)=\frac{1}{h_{j}}g(\ \cdot\ h_{j}).

(125)

The dispersion relations we aim to approximate are left invariant under this operator.
Further, this operator represents grid-refinement by a factor of
0<h_{j}<1
.
We must consider sequence of spaces to properly define the point-wise limit as the refinement factor approaches zero, as the linear opertor
T_{h,l}
becomes unbounded in this case.

Proposition 4.1
.

For any
{h}\in\ell_{<1,\infty}
, the associated sequence of operators
(T_{{h},1},T_{{h},2},\dots)
are bounded linear operators.
Furthermore, the functions of the form
g^{2}=|c\ \cdot\ |^{2}
obey

\displaystyle T_{{h},j}g\Big|_{X_{j-1}}=g\Big|_{X_{j}},

(126)

for all
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

First we have the bound

\displaystyle\|T_{{h},j}g\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{h_{i}^{2}}\|g\|_{2},

(127)

proving that
T_{{h},j}:L^{2}(X_{j-1})\mapsto L^{2}(X_{j})
and is bounded.
Further, it is straightforward to check
T_{{h},j}
is linear.
Now for
x>0
we have

\displaystyle T_{{h},j}(x|_{X_{j-1}}\mapsto cx|_{X_{j-1}})=x|_{X_{j-1}}\mapsto cx|_{X_{j}}.

(128)

An identical argument holds for
x<0
.
∎

Now we main prove our asymptotic result.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\omega,\boldsymbol{\omega}\in L^{\infty}(X_{0})
,
{h}\in\ell_{<1,\infty}
be given, if

\displaystyle|\omega(k^{\prime})-\boldsymbol{\omega}(k^{\prime})|=O({k^{\prime}}^{p})

(129)

for
k^{\prime}
in a small enough neighbourhood around
k=0
then for any
k\in[-\pi,\pi]
there is
m
large enough so

\displaystyle|T_{{h},l_{0}}\cdots T_{{h},l_{m}}(\omega(k)-\boldsymbol{\omega}(k))|<O(\prod_{l=0}^{m}\left(h_{l}\omega)^{p}\right).

(130)

Proof.

Using a
p-1
order Taylor series in
k
around zero gives the linear approximation.
Due to the invariance of
\omega
under
T_{{h},l}
for all
l
, there exists
m
sufficiently large so that any initial
k
can be mapped to a neighbourhood of zero through refinement.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\omega}
that is a
p^{th}
order approximant to
\omega
around zero, then

\displaystyle T_{{h},l_{0}}\cdots T_{{h},l_{m}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\xrightarrow[p.w]{m\rightarrow\infty}\omega|_{\mathbb{R}}.

(131)

This shows that higher order methods give better dispersion error bounds around zero for less refined grids.
We have not analysed the high frequency components on a grid however, we do this in the next section.

4.2
Uniform Error Bounds

In this section we derive key properties of the maximal dispersion error.
First we show that the defined quantity is invariant under grid refinement.
Next we show that for any wave mode on a given mesh, the maximal dispersion error bounds the dispersion error of any of the present wave modes.
Last, we show that the maximal dispersion error of the
\pi
-mode bounds all of the dispersion error quantities.

Proposition 4.4
.

The maximal dispersion error is invariant under the action of
T_{{h},{l}}
, that is

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\infty}(T_{{h},{l}}\boldsymbol{\omega})=\varepsilon_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\omega}).

(132)

Proof.

Directly

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\infty}(T_{{h},{l}}\boldsymbol{\omega})=\max_{k\in[-\pi,\pi]}\frac{\varepsilon_{d}(T_{{h},{l}}\boldsymbol{\omega},k)}{T_{{h},{l}}\omega(k)}=\max_{k\in[-\pi/h,\pi/h]}\frac{\varepsilon_{d}(\boldsymbol{\omega},h_{l}k)}{\omega(h_{l}k)}=\varepsilon_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\omega}).

(133)

∎

For traditional central stencil operators, it is always true that

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{d}(\boldsymbol{\omega},k^{\prime})\leq\varepsilon_{d}(\boldsymbol{\omega},k),

(134)

for
k^{\prime}\leq k
.
For our new operators, this is not the case and so we must introduce

\displaystyle\epsilon_{d}\coloneqq\max_{|k^{\prime}|\leq|k|}\varepsilon_{d}(\boldsymbol{\omega},k^{\prime}),

(135)

then
\epsilon_{d}
is monotonic in
k
.
Note that
\sup_{k}\epsilon_{d}(\boldsymbol{\omega},k)\leq\varepsilon_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
.
We may now state how our
\alpha
-DRP schemes control the dispersion error uniformly with mesh refinement.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
{h}\in\ell
,
T_{{h},l}
be the associated sequence of refinement operators, then

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{d}(T_{{h},l}(\boldsymbol{\omega}),k)\leq\epsilon_{d}(T_{{h},l}(\boldsymbol{\omega}),k)\leq\varepsilon_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\leq\alpha,

(136)

independent of
k
.

Last, we provide the quantitative means to compare the uniform bounds on our new operators to grid-refinement of traditional schemes.
For each of our new operators, we find

\displaystyle h^{*}(k)=\arg\max_{h\in(0,1]}\epsilon_{d}(T_{h}(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{trad}),k)\leq\delta_{k},

(137)

where
\delta_{k}=\epsilon_{d}(\boldsymbol{\omega},k)
for one of our newly derived operators
\boldsymbol{\omega}
.
The value
1/h^{*}(k)
represents the resolution refinement needed for the traditional operators to match the dispersion error or our newly derived operators for the wavenumber
k
.
In three spatial dimensions for a time dependant problem, the computational effort to achieve the same accuracy scales to be
\frac{1}{(h^{*}(k))^{3+1}}
for this level of accuracy.
