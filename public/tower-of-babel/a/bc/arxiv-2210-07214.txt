Before going further, we discuss the relation between the above mentioned approximation problem with polynomial convexity, which is a fundamental notion in several complex variables. Let
K
be a compact subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n}.
The
polynomial convex hull
of
K
is denoted by
\widehat{K}
and defined by
\widehat{K}:=\left\{\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:|p(\alpha)|\leq\max_{K}|p|~~\forall p\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},z_{2},\cdots,z_{n}]\right\}.
Clearly,
K\subset\widehat{K}.
We say
K
is
polynomially convex
if
\widehat{K}=K.
In
\mathbb{C},

\hat{K}=K
if and only if
\mathbb{C}\setminus K
is connected. Any convex compact subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is polynomially convex. In general, it is difficult to deduce if a given compact subset in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is polynomially convex. A closed subset

is
locally polynomially convex
at
p\in E
if there exists
r>0
such that
E\cap\overline{B(p,r)}
is polynomially convex. Let
K
be a compact set in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and let
\mathcal{C}(K)
be the class of of all continuous complex-valued functions on
K
. By
\mathscr{P}(K)
, we denote the space of all those functions on
K
which are uniform limits of polynomials in
z_{1},z_{2},\cdots,z_{n}
. One of the fundamental question in the theory of uniform algebras is to characterize compact subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
for which

(1.3)

\mathscr{P}(K)=\mathcal{C}(K).

From the theory of commutative Banach algebras (see
[
13
]
for details), we notice that

\mathscr{P}(K)=\mathcal{C}(K)\Longrightarrow\widehat{K}=K.

Therefore, polynomial convexity is a necessary condition for all compacts
K
of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
having property (
1.3
). Lavrentiev
[
20
]
showed that for

\mathscr{P}(K)=\mathcal{C}(K)
if and only if
\widehat{K}=K
and
int(K)=\emptyset.
In higher dimension, no such characterization is known. The condition
\mathbb{C}\setminus K
connected generalizes to polynomial convexity of
K.
A generalization of

condition is that the compact

is totally real except a small set of points. Recall that a

The manifold

and a subset

is said to be totally real if it is locally contained in a totally real manifold.

In case

In
[
3
]
, Bharali introduced a technique to study the polynomial convexity of graphs of functions in one variable. We observe here that the same technique can be generalized to higher-dimension. We will now state and prove this result. For that, we need some notations.

•

For a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{C}^{n},
by
\mathcal{O}(K;\mathbb{C}^{N})
we define the set of holomorphic functions from some neighborhood of
K
to
\mathbb{C}^{N}.
If
N=1,
we simply denote it
\mathcal{O}(K);
And for fix
\xi\in K,
we define a sub-class of
\mathcal{O}(K;\mathbb{C}^{N})
by
\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(K;\mathbb{C}^{N}):=\left\{F\in\mathcal{O}(K;\mathbb{C}^{N}):F(\xi)=0\right\}.

•

For
A\in\mathcal{O}(K)\text{ and }F=(f_{1},\cdots,f_{N})\in\mathcal{O}(K;\mathbb{C}^{N})
by
A(z)F(z)
, we mean
\left(A(z)f_{1}(z),\cdots,\right.

\left.A(z)f_{N}(z)\right)
and by
P(z)Q(z),
we mean
\sum_{j=1}^{N}p_{j}(z)q_{j}(z)
for
P=(p_{1},\cdots,p_{N})
and
Q=(q_{1},\cdots,q_{N});

•

Let
K
be a subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
F:=(f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{N}):K\to\mathbb{C}^{N}
be a function. We will denote the graph of
F
over
K
by

or by
Gr_{K}(f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{N}).

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
N\geq n\geq 1
and
F\in\mathcal{C}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{C}^{N}),
where
\Omega
is a bounded domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
with
\overline{\Omega}
is polynomially convex, and let
\xi\in\overline{\Omega}.
Suppose there exist a constant
p\geq 2,
a nowhere vanishing function
A\in\mathcal{O}(\overline{\Omega}),
and mappings

such that

(2.10)

\displaystyle\left|A(z)F(z)-A(\xi)F(\xi)+G(z)\right|^{p}\leq{\sf Re}\left(H(z)\left(A(z)F(z)-A(\xi)F(\xi)+G(z)\right)\right)~~\forall z\in\overline{\Omega}.

Then
Gr_{\overline{\Omega}}(F)
is polynomially convex.

Proof.

Since
A\in\mathcal{O}(\overline{\Omega})
and

there exists a neighborhood

of
\overline{\Omega}
such that the functions

are holomorphic on

Since
\overline{\Omega}
is polynomially convex, there exist an open polynomial polyhedron
\triangle
such that
\overline{\Omega}\subset\triangle\subset U.
Define a map

by

\displaystyle\Psi(z,w):=\left|A(z)w-A(\xi)F(\xi)+G(z)\right|^{p}-{\sf Re}\left(H(z)\left(A(z)w-A(\xi)F(\xi)+G(z)\right)\right).

Since
{\sf Re}\left(H(z)\left(A(z)w-A(\xi)F(\xi)+G(z)\right)\right)
is pluriharmonic in

and

on
\triangle\times\mathbb{C}^{N},

\Psi
is plurisubharmonic on
\triangle\times\mathbb{C}^{N}.
We set

. Since
\overline{\Omega}
is polynomially convex, we can say that
\widehat{\Gamma}\Subset{\overline{\Omega}}\times\mathbb{C}^{N}.

We consider the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}:=\big\{(z,w)\in{\overline{\Omega}}\times\mathbb{C}^{N}

\displaystyle:\Psi(z,w)\leq 0\big\}.

We now claim that
\widehat{\Gamma}\subset\mathcal{S}.
To prove this claim assume
(z_{0},w_{0})\in\widehat{\Gamma}
but
(z_{0},w_{0})\notin\mathcal{S}.
Therefore,
\Psi(z_{0},w_{0})>0
and by assumption (
2.10
), we get that
\sup_{\Gamma}\Psi(z,w)\leq 0.
Since
\triangle
is an open Runge and Stein neighborhood of
\overline{\Omega}
with
\Psi\in{\sf psh}(\triangle\times\mathbb{C}^{N})
such that
\Psi(z_{0},w_{0})>\sup_{\Gamma}\Psi(z,w),
we get that
(z_{0},w_{0})\notin\widehat{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{O}(\triangle\times\mathbb{C}^{N})}
and so
(z_{0},w_{0})\notin\widehat{\Gamma}.
Therefore, we obtain that
\widehat{\Gamma}\subset\mathcal{S}.
This also implies that

\displaystyle\left|A(z)w-A(\xi)F(\xi)+G(z)\right|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq|H(z)|\left|A(z)w-A(\xi)F(\xi)+G(z)\right|~~\forall(z,w)\in\widehat{\Gamma}.

Since
p\geq 2
, we obtain that

(2.11)

\displaystyle\widehat{\Gamma}\subset\left\{(z,w)\in\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{C}^{N}:\left|A(z)w-A(\xi)F(\xi)+G(z)\right|\leq|H(z)|^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\right\}.

Our next claim is:
\widehat{\Gamma}\cap\left(\{\xi\}\times\mathbb{C}^{N}\right)=\{(\xi,F(\xi))\}.

To prove this claim, assume
(\alpha,\beta)\in\widehat{\Gamma}\cap\left(\{\xi\}\times\mathbb{C}^{N}\right).
This implies
\alpha=\xi.
Since
(\xi,\beta)\in\widehat{\Gamma},
we get from (
2.11

\displaystyle\left|A(\xi)\beta-A(\xi)F(\xi)+G(\xi)\right|\leq|H(\xi)|^{\frac{1}{p-1}}.

In view of the assumption

we obtain that

|A(\xi)||\beta-F(\xi)|=0.
Since
A
is nowhere vanishing, we obtain
\beta=F(\xi).

This proves the claim. Since, by assumption, for each
\xi\in\overline{\Omega},
such functions

exists, therefore, we obtain that
\widehat{\Gamma}=\widehat{\Gamma}\cap\left(\cup_{\xi\in{\overline{\Omega}}}\left(\{\xi\}\times\mathbb{C}^{N}\right)\right)=\cup_{\xi\in{\overline{\Omega}}}\{(\xi,F(\xi))\}=\Gamma.

\Psi_{\xi}(z)
is

\displaystyle{\sf Re}(\Psi_{\xi}(z))=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|z^{m_{j}}_{j}-\xi_{j}^{m_{j}}\right|^{2}+{\sf Re}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(z^{m_{j}}_{j}-\xi^{m_{j}}_{j}\right)\left(R_{j}(z)-R_{j}(\xi)\right)\right).

We now compute:

\displaystyle{\sf Re}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(z^{m_{j}}_{j}-\xi^{m_{j}}_{j}\right)\left(R_{j}(z)-R_{j}(\xi)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|\left(z^{m_{j}}_{j}-\xi^{m_{j}}_{j}\right)\left(R_{j}(z)-R_{j}(\xi)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|z^{m_{j}}_{j}-\xi^{m_{j}}_{j}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}|R(z)-R(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq c\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|z^{m_{j}}_{j}-\xi^{m_{j}}_{j}\right|^{2}\right).

Here we use Cauchy-Schwarz for the second inequality and the last inequality follows from (
1.4
). From above calculations, we have

\displaystyle-c\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|z^{m_{j}}_{j}-\xi^{m_{j}}_{j}\right|^{2}\right)\leq{\sf Re}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(z^{m_{j}}_{j}-\xi^{m_{j}}_{j}\right)\left(R_{j}(z)-R_{j}(\xi)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq c\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|z^{m_{j}}_{j}-\xi^{m_{j}}_{j}\right|^{2}\right).

Therefore, we obtain that

(3.2)

\displaystyle 0\leq(1-c)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|z^{m_{j}}_{j}-\xi^{m_{j}}_{j}\right|^{2}\right)\leq{\sf Re}(\Psi_{\xi}(z))\leq(1+c)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|z^{m_{j}}_{j}-\xi^{m_{j}}_{j}\right|^{2}\right).

Let us compute:

\displaystyle\left|F(z)-F(\xi)\right|

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|\bar{z_{j}}^{m_{j}}+R_{j}(z)-\bar{\xi_{j}}^{m_{j}}-R_{j}(\xi)\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\left|\bar{z_{j}}^{m_{j}}-\bar{\xi_{j}}^{m_{j}}\right|+\left|R_{j}(z)-R_{j}(\xi)\right|\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|\bar{z_{j}}^{m_{j}}-\bar{\xi_{j}}^{m_{j}}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|R_{j}(z)-R_{j}(\xi)\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Here we use Minkowski inequality for the last inequality. Using (
1.4
), we obtain from above that

(3.3)

\displaystyle\left|F(z)-F(\xi)\right|^{2}\leq(1+c)^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|{z_{j}}^{m_{j}}-{\xi_{j}}^{m_{j}}\right|^{2}~~\forall(z,\xi)\in\overline{\Omega}\times\overline{\Omega}.

In view of (
3.2
) and (
3.3
), we get that

(3.4)

\displaystyle\left|F(z)-F(\xi)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1+c)^{2}}{(1-c)}{\sf Re}(\Psi_{\xi}(z))~~\forall(z,\xi)\in\overline{\Omega}\times\overline{\Omega}.

Therefore, we obtain that
|F(z)-F(\xi)|^{2}\leq\text{C}{\sf Re}\left(\Psi_{\xi}(z)\right),
where C is a constant on the right side of (
3.4
) which is independent of
z.
Hence, by
Proposition

2.7
, we conclude that
Gr_{\overline{\Omega}}(F)
is polynomially convex.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Assume that

and

is polynomially convex for each

\displaystyle\cup_{j\in\{1,\cdots,n\}\setminus\{l_{1},\cdots,l_{k}\}}\bigg(\cup_{I}X_{I}^{(l_{1},\cdots,l_{k},j)}\bigg)

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
1\leq l_{1}<l_{2}\cdots<l_{k}\leq n.
Clearly,

\displaystyle\cup_{j\in\{1,\cdots,n\}\setminus\{l_{1},\cdots,l_{k}\}}\bigg(\cup_{I}X_{I}^{(l_{1},\cdots,l_{k},j)}\bigg)=\cup_{j=1}^{k+1}A_{l_{j}},

\displaystyle A_{l_{1}}:

\displaystyle=\cup_{j=l_{r}+1}^{l_{r+1}-1}\bigg(\cup_{I}X_{I}^{(l_{1},\cdots,l_{k},j)}\bigg)~\text{ for }r=1,\cdots,k-1;\text{ and }

First, we prove that each

is polynomially convex. Without loss of generality, it is enough to show that

\displaystyle A_{l_{1}}:

\displaystyle=\cup_{j=1}^{l_{1}-1}\bigg(\cup_{I}X_{I}^{(l_{1},\cdots,l_{k},j)}\bigg)

is polynomially convex. We will now apply induction principle to show

is polynomially convex. Assume that
K_{1}:=\cup_{j=1}^{t}\bigg(\cup_{I}X_{I}^{(l_{1},\cdots,l_{k},j)}\bigg)
is polynomially convex for

.
We need to show that

\displaystyle\cup_{j=1}^{t+1}\bigg(\cup_{I}X_{I}^{(l_{1},\cdots,l_{k},j)}\bigg)=K_{1}\cup X_{I}^{(l_{1},\cdots,l_{k},t+1)}=:K_{1}\cup K_{2}

is polynomially convex. For this, we consider the polynomial

\displaystyle p_{t}(z,w)=\prod^{t}_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\notin\{l_{1},\cdots,l_{k}\}\\
\end{subarray}}z_{j}^{m_{n+j}}w_{j}.

Then we have the following:

•

p_{t}(K_{1})=\{0\}.

•

p_{t}(K_{2}\setminus K_{1})\not=\{0\}:
using
Lemma

5.2
, we get that for
(z,w)\in K_{2}\setminus K_{1},

p_{t}(z,w)=0
implies

\displaystyle\prod^{t}_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\notin\{l_{1},\cdots,l_{k}\}\\
\end{subarray}}z_{j}=0.
Since
(z,w)\notin K_{1},
this is not possible. Therefore,
p_{t}(K_{2}\setminus K_{1})\not=\{0\}
and
p_{t}(K_{1})\cap p_{t}(K_{2})=\{0\}.

•

Lemma

5.2
, we get that

and

Therefore,

Therefore, using Kallin’s lemma we get that

is polynomially convex.

Again, we will apply induction principle to show

is polynomially convex. Assume that

We consider the polynomial

\displaystyle p_{s}(z,w)=\prod^{s}_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\

\end{subarray}}z_{j}^{m_{n+j}}w_{j}.

Then we have the following:

•

p_{s}(E_{1})=\{0\}.

•

p_{s}(E_{2}\setminus E_{1})\not=\{0\}:
using
Lemma

5.2
, we get that for
(z,w)\in E_{2}\setminus E_{1},

p_{s}(z,w)=0
implies

\displaystyle\prod^{s}_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\notin\{l_{1},\cdots,l_{k}\}\\
\end{subarray}}z_{j}=0.
Since
(z,w)\notin E_{1},
this is not possible. Therefore,
p_{s}(E_{2}\setminus E_{1})\not=\{0\}
and
p_{s}(E_{1})\cap p_{s}(E_{2})=\{0\}.

•

Applying
Lemma

5.2
, we get that
p_{s}^{-1}\{0\}\cap E_{1}=E_{1}
and
p_{s}^{-1}\{0\}\cap E_{2}\subset E_{1}.
Therefore,

is polynomially convex.

Again, by Kallin’s lemma, we conclude that

is polynomially convex. Therefore, by induction principle, we obtained that

is polynomially convex, that is,

Proposition 5.4

,
\cup_{j\notin\{l_{1},\cdots,l_{k}\}}^{n}\bigg(\cup_{I}X_{I}^{(l_{1},\cdots,l_{k},j)}\bigg)
is polynomially convex.
We consider the polynomial

\displaystyle p(z,w)=\prod^{n}_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\notin\{l_{1},\cdots,l_{k}\}\\
\end{subarray}}z_{j}^{m_{n+j}}w_{j}.

Therefore

•

by
Lemma

5.1
,
p\left(X_{I}^{(l_{1},\cdots,l_{k})}\right)\subset\omega_{I}^{(l_{1},\cdots,l_{k})}~~~\forall I\in\{0,1,\cdots,m_{1}-1\}\times\{0,1,\cdots,m_{2}-1\}\times\cdots\times\{0,1,\cdots,m_{n}-1\};

•

Since
\omega_{I}^{(l_{1},\cdots,l_{k})}\cap\omega_{J}^{(l_{1},\cdots,l_{k})}=\{0\},
we have
p\left(X_{I}^{(l_{1},\cdots,l_{k})}\right)\cap p\left(X_{J}^{(l_{1},\cdots,l_{k})}\right)\subset\{0\};

•

by
Lemma

5.2
,
p^{-1}\{0\}\cap\left(\cup_{I}X_{I}^{(l_{1},\cdots,l_{k})}\right)=\bigcup_{j\notin\{l_{1},\cdots,l_{k}\}}^{n}\left(\cup_{I}X_{I}^{(l_{1},\cdots,l_{k},j)}\right),
which is polynomially convex by
Lemma

5.3
.

Therefore, by Kallin’s lemma, we infer that
\cup_{I}X_{I}^{(l_{1},\cdots,l_{k})}
is polynomially convex. ∎

Proof of
Theorem

1.12
.

Take
\delta:=\min\{\delta^{\prime},\delta_{0}\}.
We divide the proof of this theorem in three steps.

Step I:

Showing
\mathscr{P}(X_{I})=\mathcal{C}(X_{I})~~~\forall_{I}\in\{0,1,\cdots,m_{1}-1\}\times\{0,1,\cdots,m_{2}-1\}\times\cdots\times\{0,1,\cdots,m_{n}-1\}.

We have

\displaystyle X_{0}

\displaystyle=\{(z_{1},\cdots,z_{n},\bar{z_{1}}^{m_{n+1}}+R_{1}(z),\cdots,\bar{z_{n}}^{m_{2n}}+R_{n}(z)):z\in\overline{D(\delta)}\}

\displaystyle=Gr_{\overline{D(\delta)}}(\bar{z_{1}}^{m_{n+1}}+R_{1}(z),\cdots,\bar{z_{n}}^{m_{2n}}+R_{n}(z)).

For each
I=(t_{1},\cdots,t_{n}),
we define a map
\Psi_{I}:\mathbb{C}^{2n}\to\mathbb{C}^{2n}
by

\displaystyle\Psi_{I}(z_{1},\cdots,z_{n},w_{1},w_{2},\cdots,w_{n})=\left(e^{\frac{2i\pi t_{1}}{m_{1}}}z_{1},e^{\frac{2i\pi t_{2}}{m_{2}}}z_{2}\cdots,e^{\frac{2i\pi t_{n}}{m_{n}}}z_{n},w_{1},w_{2}\cdots,w_{n}\right).

Therefore,
\Psi_{I}(X_{0})=X_{I}.

Theorem

1.8
gives us that
\mathscr{P}(X_{0})=\mathcal{C}(X_{0}).
Since
\Psi_{I}
is a biholomorphism, using
Result

2.3
, we conclude that
\mathscr{P}(X_{I})=\mathcal{C}(X_{I}).

Step II:

Showing
\mathscr{P}(\Phi^{-1}(X))=\mathcal{C}(\Phi^{-1}(X)).

We consider the polynomial

\displaystyle p(z,w)=\prod^{n}_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\end{subarray}}z_{j}^{m_{n+j}}w_{j}.

For
\{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}\}=\emptyset,
in view of
Lemma

5.1
, we get that

•

For each
I=(t_{1},\cdots,t_{n})\in\{0,1,\cdots,m_{1}-1\}\times\cdots\times\{0,1,\cdots,m_{n}-1\},

p(X_{I})\subset\omega_{I},
where
\omega_{I}
is a closed sector in the complex plane with vertex at the origin and
L_{I}
as the angular bisector with argument argument
{\bigg({\displaystyle 2\pi\sum^{n}_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\end{subarray}}\frac{t_{j}m_{n+j}}{m_{j}}}\bigg)};

•

\omega_{I}\cap\omega_{J}=\{0\}~\text{ for all }{I\neq J}.

To apply Kallin’s lemma, we need to show that
p^{-1}\{0\}\cap\bigg(\cup_{I}X_{I}\bigg)=\cup^{n}_{j=1}\left(\cup_{I}X_{I}^{(j)}\right)
is polynomially convex. First we focus on the the polynomial convexity of
\cup_{I}X_{I}^{(j)}\text{ for }j\in{1,\cdots,n}.

Proposition

5.4
says that for each
i_{1}\in\{1,\cdots,n\},

\cup_{I}X_{I}^{(i_{1})}
is polynomially convex if for each
i_{2}\in\{1,\cdots,n\}\setminus\{i_{1}\},

\cup_{I}X_{I}^{(i_{1},i_{2})}
is polynomially convex. Again by
Proposition

5.4
,
\cup_{I}X_{I}^{(i_{1},i_{2})}
is polynomially convex if for each
i_{3}\in\{1,\cdots,n\}\setminus\{i_{1},i_{2}\},

\cup_{I}X_{I}^{(i_{1},i_{2},i_{3})}
is polynomially convex. Proceeding in this way, we arrive at this situation that
\cup_{I}X_{I}^{(i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{n-1})}
is polynomially convex if for each
i_{n}\in\{1,\cdots,n\}\setminus\{i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{n-1}\},

\cup_{I}X_{I}^{(i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{n-1},i_{n})}
is polynomially convex. But
\cup_{I}X_{I}^{(i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{n-1},i_{n})}=\{0\}
for all
I,
which is obviously polynomially convex. Hence, for each
j\in\{1,\cdots,n\},

\cup_{I}X_{I}^{(j)}
is polynomially convex. We will now apply induction on
n,
to conclude that
p^{-1}\{0\}\cap\left(\cup_{I}X_{I}\right)
is polynomially convex. Assume that
K_{1}:=\cup^{k}_{j=1}\left(\cup_{I}X_{I}^{(j)}\right)
is polynomially convex. We need to show that
\cup^{k+1}_{j=1}\left(\cup_{I}X_{I}^{(j)}\right)=K_{1}\cup\left(\cup_{I}X_{I}^{(k+1)}\right)=:K_{1}\cup K_{2}
is polynomially convex. For this we again apply Kallin’s lemma. We consider the polynomial

\displaystyle p_{1}(z,w)=\prod^{k}_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\end{subarray}}z_{j}^{m_{n+j}}w_{j}.

Then we have the following:

•

p_{1}(K_{1})=\{0\}.

•

p_{1}(K_{2}\setminus K_{1})\not=\{0\}:
using
Lemma

5.2
, we get that for
(z,w)\in K_{2}\setminus K_{1},

p_{1}(z,w)=0
implies

\displaystyle\prod^{k}_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
\end{subarray}}z_{j}=0.
Since
(z,w)\notin K_{1},
this is not possible. Therefore,
p_{1}(K_{2}\setminus K_{1})\not=\{0\}
and
p_{1}(K_{1})\cap p_{1}(K_{2})=\{0\}.

•

applying
Lemma

5.2
, we get that
p_{1}^{-1}\{0\}\cap K_{1}=K_{1}
and
p_{1}^{-1}\{0\}\cap K_{2}\subset K_{1}.
Hence
p_{1}^{-1}\{0\}\cap\left(K_{1}\cup K_{2}\right)=K_{1}
is polynomially convex.

Therefore, by Kallin’s lemma,
K_{1}\cup K_{2}
is polynomially convex. Again from
Step I
, we get that for each
I\in\left\{0,1,\cdots,m_{1}-1\right\}\times\cdots\times\left\{0,1,\cdots,m_{n}-1\right\},

\mathscr{P}(X_{I})=\mathcal{C}(X_{I}).

The above informations allow us to apply
Result

2.2
to conclude that

\mathscr{P}\left(\cup_{I}X_{I}\right)=\mathcal{C}\left(\cup_{I}X_{I}\right),~\text{ that is }\mathscr{P}\left(\Phi^{-1}(X)\right)=\mathcal{C}\left(\Phi^{-1}(X)\right).

Therefore, by
Result

2.3
, we get that

\mathscr{P}(X)=\mathcal{C}(X).

Step III:

Showing that
{z^{m_{1}}_{1},\cdots,z^{m_{n}}_{n},\bar{z_{1}}^{m_{n+1}}+R_{1}(z),\cdots,\bar{z_{n}}^{m_{2n}}+R_{n}(z)}
separates points on
\mathbf{\overline{D(\delta)}}.

Let
a=(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n}),b=(b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{n})\in\mathbf{\overline{D(\delta)}}
with
a\neq b
. Then there exists a set
\{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}\}\subseteq\{1,\cdots,n\}
such that
a_{j}\neq b_{j}~~\forall j\in\{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}\}
and
a_{j}=b_{j}~~\forall j\notin\{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}\}.
If
a^{m_{l}}_{l}\neq b^{m_{l}}_{l}\text{ for some }l\in\{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}\}
then
z^{m_{l}}_{l}
separates
a
and
b.
Next, we assume that
a^{m_{j}}_{j}=b^{m_{j}}_{j}\text{ for all }j\in\{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}\}.
We now show that, for some
j\in\{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}\},~~\bar{z_{j}}^{m_{n+j}}+R_{j}(z)
separates
a
and
b.
If possible, assume that

\displaystyle\left(\bar{z_{j}}^{m_{n+j}}+R_{j}\right)(a)=(\bar{z_{j}}^{m_{n+j}}+R_{j})(b)~~~\forall{j}\in\{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}\}.

This implies

(5.11)

\displaystyle\bar{a_{j}}^{m_{n+j}}-\bar{b_{j}}^{m_{n+j}}=R_{j}(b)-R_{j}(a)~~\forall{j}\in\{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}\}.

Now we compute:

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{j\in\{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}\}}|R_{j}(b)-R_{j}(a)|^{2}}\leq|R(b)-R(a)|

\displaystyle\leq c\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|b_{j}^{m_{n+j}}-a_{j}^{m_{n+j}}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=c\left(\sum_{j\in\{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}\}}\left|b_{j}^{m_{n+j}}-a_{j}^{m_{n+j}}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Therefore, using (
5.11
), we obtain from above that

(5.12)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{j\in\{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}\}}\left|b_{j}^{m_{n+j}}-a_{j}^{m_{n+j}}\right|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq c\left(\sum_{j\in\{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}\}}\left|b_{j}^{m_{n+j}}-a_{j}^{m_{n+j}}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Since
a_{j}\neq b_{j},~~a^{m_{j}}_{j}=b^{m_{j}}_{j}\text{ for all }j\in\{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}\}
and
\gcd(m_{j},m_{n+j})=1,
we can say that

\displaystyle{\sum_{j\in\{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}\}}\left|b_{j}^{m_{n+j}}-a_{j}^{m_{n+j}}\right|^{2}}\neq 0.

Therefore, from (
5.12
) we get that
c\geq 1.
This is a contradiction to our hypothesis.
Therefore,

\displaystyle[z^{m_{1}}_{1},\cdots,z^{m_{n}}_{n},\bar{z_{1}}^{m_{n+1}}+R_{1}(z),\cdots,\bar{z_{n}}^{m_{2n}}+R_{n}(z);\overline{D(\delta)}]=C(\overline{D(\delta)}).

∎
