Discrete harmonic functions for non-symmetric Laplace operators in the quarter plane Thanks: This project has received funding from the European Research Council (ERC) under the European Union’s Horizon 2020 research and innovation programme under the Grant Agreement No. 759702.
Viet Hung Hoang

Abstract
. We construct harmonic functions in the quarter plane for discrete Laplace operators. In particular, the functions are conditioned to vanish on the boundary and the Laplacians admit coefficients associated with transition probabilities of non-symmetric random walks. By solving a boundary value problem for generating functions of harmonic functions, we deduce explicit expressions for the generating functions in terms of conformal mappings. These mappings are yielded from a conformal welding problem with quasisymmetric shift and contain information about the growth of harmonic functions. Further, we describe the set of harmonic functions as a vector space isomorphic to the space of formal power series.

Introduction

Discrete harmonic functions

Let us first recall some definitions. A real-valued function
h
defined on a domain
D\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is called discrete harmonic (or preharmonic) with respect to the discrete Laplace operator

(1.1)

\Delta(h)(i,j)=\sum_{k,\ell}p_{k,\ell}h(i+k,j+\ell)-h(i,j),

with
\{p_{k,\ell}\}
fixed, if
\Delta(h)(i,j)=0
for all
(i,j)\in D
. For the sake of brevity, the words “harmonic function” and “Laplace operator” will always be understood in the discrete context. The word “discrete” will only be used in case where we want to emphasize it in relation to the continuous counterpart.

During the second half of the 20
th
century, queuing theory was of interest to many probabilists. Discrete harmonicity appeared vastly under the form of balance equations to model the stationary distribution of many quantities (see the book
[
25
]
of Kleinrock for an introduction of various models or the book
[
9
]
of Cohen for single server queues). Another important application is the connection to the concept of Doob’s transform. Biane in
[
4
]
, Eichelsbacher and König in
[
16
]
constructed processes conditioned to stay in cones from positive harmonic functions. Later, Denisov and Wachtel in
[
13
]
used the functions to find the tail asymptotics for the exit time of such conditioned walks. Harmonic functions are also found in some problems of population dynamics to model the extinction probabilities of the population (see
[
29
,
2
]
).

The references listed above mostly concern positive harmonic functions on account of their evident links to probabilistic quantities. However, some authors continued to extend the problem further to signed harmonic functions (namely, the sign may vary in some domains). Almansi in
[
1
]
proved that in the continuous context, polyharmonic functions can be decomposed into signed harmonic functions. The discrete analog of this result was achieved in
[
12
,
37
,
8
]
. Further in
[
8
]
, Chapon, Fusy and Raschel unveiled some connections between discrete polyharmonic functions and complete asymptotic expansions in walk enumeration problems. Therefore, studies of polyharmonic and/or signed harmonic functions become more relevant. Last but not least, one of our goals is to study the space of harmonic functions (see
[
24
]
), which gives us a general viewpoint on this discrete potential theory problem.

Overview of our main model and some studies of large jump random walks

Hoang, Raschel and Tarrago in
[
24
]
studied discrete harmonic functions for symmetric Laplace operators in the quarter plane. More precisely, they considered the Laplacian (
1.1
) with
p_{k,\ell}=p_{\ell,k}
for all
k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
. The present work aims at extending their results to a non-symmetric context. We therefore reintroduce our main problem and point out key differences between the models of this work and of
[
24
]
.

In this article, we consider the problem of finding discrete harmonic functions in the quarter plane (i.e.,
D=\mathbb{N}^{2}=\{1,2,3,...\}^{2}
) which vanish on the boundary axes. The weights
\{p_{k,\ell}\}
of the Laplacian (
1.1
) will be modeled by transition probabilities of random walks. This is motivated by the links between harmonic functions and various problems of queuing systems, population dynamics,… which can be reformulated as problems of random walks. In particular, we consider walks with arbitrary large negative jumps, small positive jumps, and moreover not being constrained by any symmetry conditions (see Table
1
). This is the main difference of this paper’s model in comparison with
[
24
]
. Due to the high order of the characteristic polynomial associated with large jumps, such walks are undoubtedly challenging to study. We refer the readers to some achievements in the subject: an analytic technique was developed in
[
11
,
10
]
by Cohen and Boxma to study walks with two-dimensional state space; asymptotics of multidimensional walks are investigated in
[
13
]
by Denisov and Wachtel,
[
15
]
by Duraj and Wachtel; and recently in combinatorics, walks with steps in
\{-2,-1,0,1\}^{2}
are systematically classified in
[
5
]
by Bostan, Bousquet-Mélou and Melczer, alongside with some related conjectures. In brief, the study of large step walks is still largely open for further development.

Analytic approach to find harmonic functions

We present here a complex analytic approach which will be the backbone of our work. The technique was pioneered by Malyshev in
[
32
]
, Fayolle and Iasnogorodski in
[
17
]
, to study the stationary distribution of small jump random walks in a two-dimensional state space. The global idea is as follows. The balance equations for the stationary probabilities lead to a functional equation for their generating functions. An important bivariate polynomial characterized by the transition probabilities of the walk appears in the equation and is usually called the kernel function. By studying the Riemann surface associated with this kernel, then constructing a uniformizing variable, one can solve the equation in certain regions, then continue meromorphically the solutions into a bigger region. In another direction proposed in
[
17
]
by Fayolle and Iasnogorodski, the algebraic curve associated with the kernel offers a large choice of branches, and with an appropriate subset, the functional equation can be reduced to boundary value problems (BVP). Such a technique is also discussed in deeper insights by the same authors in the book
[
18
]
.

Since then, the technique has been successfully applied to various areas, such as queuing systems
[
21
,
20
,
28
]
, potential theory
[
35
,
36
,
30
]
and enumerative combinatorics (counting walks in the quarter plane)
[
7
,
27
,
14
]
, leading to highly precise results (both exact and asymptotic results).

We also mention here the work
[
3
]
by Banderier and Flajolet. The authors considered combinatorial aspects of one-dimensional large jump walks as directed (large jump) two-dimensional walks. By the analytic approach, the counting generating functions are shown to have algebraic forms constructed by the branches of the associated algebraic curve.

And lastly, an alternative approach introduced by Cohen and Boxma in
[
11
]
contributes largely to the development of this technique (see also
[
10
]
by Cohen). The overall idea is the same as Fayolle and Iasnogorodski’s. However, the subset of the kernel’s zero set is chosen differently, in a way that this choice does not depend on the branches of the algebraic curve. Their construction overcomes the obstacle of studying precisely the Riemann surface, therefore it can cover a wide class of large jump random walks. This approach will also be the central point of our present analysis.

Non-symmetric walks with large negative jumps and harmonic functions

Consider a lattice walk on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with transition probabilities (or non-negative weights)
\{p_{k,\ell}\}_{(k,\ell)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}
summing to
1
such that:

(H1)

The walk has small positive jumps (at most
1
unit) and arbitrary large negative jumps, i.e.,
p_{k,\ell}=0
if
k\geqslant 2
or
\ell\geqslant 2
(see Table
1
);

(H2)

If
\big|\sum p_{k,\ell}x^{k}y^{\ell}\big|=1
and
|x|=|y|=1
, then
x=y=1
(which is a necessary condition for the irreducibility of the walk);

(H3)

\{p_{k,\ell}\}_{(k,\ell)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}
have finite moments of order
2
, i.e.,
\sum(k^{2}+\ell^{2})p_{k,\ell}<\infty
;

(H4)

The drift is zero, i.e.,
\sum kp_{k,\ell}=\sum\ell p_{k,\ell}=0
.

Now let
\Delta
be the discrete Laplacian (
1.1
) associated with
\{p_{k,\ell}\}_{(k,\ell)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}
and acting on functions
h:\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
. Our goal is to describe real-valued functions
\{h(i,j)\}_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}
satisfying the following conditions:

(H5)

h(i,j)
is harmonic with respect to the operator
\Delta
on the positive quadrant, i.e., for all
i,j\geqslant 1
,
\Delta(h)(i,j)=0
;

(H6)

h(i,j)
vanishes elsewhere, i.e., for all
(i,j)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with
i\leqslant 0
and/or
j\leqslant 0
,
h(i,j)=0
.

Random walks with such non-symmetric step sets have appeared throughout the literature. In
[
7
,
6
]
, the authors study the problem of counting walks for numerous small jump random walks. In
[
4
,
16
,
26
,
35
]
, walks staying in Weyl chambers are investigated. For example, walks in the duals of SU(3) and Sp(4) can be viewed as non-symmetric walks in the quadrant.

Continuous counterpart of the main problem

For future use, we recall some facts about continuous harmonic functions in cones. A real-valued function
f
defined on a domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is called harmonic with respect to the (continuous) Laplace operator

(1.2)

\nabla^{2}:=\partial_{xx}+\partial_{yy},

if
\nabla^{2}f=0
on
D
.

Consider the following Dirichlet problem on the cone
\mathscr{E}:=\{(r\cos\phi,r\sin\phi):r>0,0<\phi<\theta\}
of angle
\theta\in(0,\pi]
: find functions
f:\mathscr{E}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

(i)

f
is harmonic with respect to
\nabla^{2}
on
\mathscr{E}
;

(ii)

f
is continuous on the closure
\overline{\mathscr{E}}
of
\mathscr{E}
and vanishes on the boundary
\partial\mathscr{E}
.

If the cone is the upper half-plane
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
, then the problem can easily be solved by complex analysis: by Schwarz reflection principle, the function
f
can be extended to a harmonic function on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by the formula
f(x,y)=-f(x,-y)
for all
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{-}
. Moreover, a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is harmonic on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
if and only if there exists uniquely a complex analytic function
g
(up to an additive constant) on
\mathbb{C}
such that
f(x,y)=\Im g(x+yi)
(with
\Im
denoting the imaginary part) for any
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Therefore,
g(\overline{z})=\overline{g(z)}
for
z\in\mathbb{C}
by Schwarz reflection principle. In other words,
g(z)
admits the form
g(z)=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}a_{n}z^{n}
with
(a_{n})_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}
and
|a_{n}|^{1/n}\to 0
(which is a necessary and sufficient condition for
g(z)
to be analytic on
\mathbb{C}
). One can describe the set of its solutions as the vector space

\displaystyle H(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{+})=\{\sum_{n\geqslant 1}a_{n}\Im\big((x+iy)^{n}\big):(a_{n})_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}\text{ and }|a_{n}|^{1/n}\to 0\}.

By the mapping
z\mapsto z^{\theta/\pi}
which maps conformally the upper half-plane to a cone of angle
\theta
(in the complex plane), we then obtain a similar description for the solution set of the problem on the cone
\mathscr{E}
of angle
\theta
as

(1.3)

\displaystyle H(\mathscr{E})=\{\sum_{n\geqslant 1}a_{n}\Im\big((x+iy)^{n\pi/\theta}\big):(a_{n})_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}\text{ and }|a_{n}|^{1/n}\to 0\}.

Through the linear transform
(x,y)\mapsto(x\sin\theta-y\cos\theta,y)
which maps the cone of angle
\theta
to the first quadrant
\mathscr{Q}:=\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
, the problem on the cone can be reformulated into the one on the first quadrant: find functions
f:\mathscr{Q}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

(i)

f
is analytic and satisfies the equation

(1.4)

\partial_{xx}f-2\cos(\theta)\partial_{xy}f+\partial_{yy}f=0

on the first quadrant
\mathscr{Q}
;

(ii)

f
is continuous on
\overline{\mathscr{Q}}
and vanishes on the boundary
\partial\mathscr{Q}
.

As a result, the corresponding solution set of this problem is also isomorphic to the solution set
H(\mathscr{E})
and is described as:

(1.5)

H(\mathscr{Q})=\{\sum_{n\geqslant 1}a_{n}h_{\theta,n}:(a_{n})_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}\text{ and }|a_{n}|^{1/n}\to 0\},

where

(1.6)

h_{\theta,n}(x,y):=\Im\bigl((x/\sin\theta+y\cot\theta+{i}y)^{n\pi/\theta}\bigr).

The reason for mentioning these facts about the continuous problem is twofold: on one hand, we will see later the analogous description to (
1.5
) of the discrete counterpart; on the other hand, we will also emphasize a convergence of the discrete harmonic functions to the continuous ones.

Generating functions and the kernel

Let
h(i,j)
be any harmonic function satisfying the conditions
(H5)
and
(H6)
. We now introduce its generating function

(1.7)

H(x,y)=\sum_{i,j\geqslant 1}h(i,j)x^{i-1}y^{j-1},

and its sections

(1.8)

H(x,0)=\sum_{i\geqslant 1}h(i,1)x^{i-1}\quad\text{and}\quad H(0,y)=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}h(1,j)y^{j-1}.

From the Assumptions
(H1)
,
(H5)
, and
(H6)
, one has

\displaystyle H(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,\ell}p_{k,\ell}x^{-k}y^{-\ell}\sum_{i,j\geqslant 1}h(i+k,j+\ell)x^{i+k-1}y^{j+\ell-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,\ell\leqslant 1}p_{k,\ell}x^{-k}y^{-\ell}H(x,y)-\sum_{k\leqslant 1}p_{k,1}H(x,0)-\sum_{\ell\leqslant 1}p_{1,\ell}H(0,y)+p_{1,1}x^{-1}y^{-1}H(0,0).

In other words,
H(x,y)
satisfies the functional equation

(1.9)

K(x,y)H(x,y)=K(x,0)H(x,0)+K(0,y)H(0,y)-K(0,0)H(0,0),

where
K(x,y)
is called the kernel and is characterized by the weights
\{p_{k,\ell}\}_{(k,\ell)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}
:

(1.10)

K(x,y)=xy\left(1-\sum p_{k,\ell}x^{-k}y^{-\ell}\right)=xy-\sum p_{k,\ell}x^{-k+1}y^{-\ell+1}.

It is easily verified that solutions of Equation (
1.9
) take the form

(1.11)

H(x,y)=\frac{F(x)+G(y)}{K(x,y)},

for any power series
F,G\in\mathbb{C}[[t]]
(formal ring of power series with coefficients in
\mathbb{C}
). However, not every quotient
\big(F(x)+G(y)\big)/K(x,y)
defines a bivariate power series around the center
(0,0)
. This raises the task of finding appropriate
F,G\in\mathbb{C}[[t]]
such that the quotient is a power series, which also leads to the study of the kernel’s zero set, particularly near the point
(0,0)
.

A subset of the kernel’s zero set

We now introduce the subset of the kernel’s zero set under the approach of Cohen and Boxma in
[
11
]
:

(1.12)

\mathscr{K}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:K(x,y)=0\text{ and }|x|=|y|\leqslant 1\}.

As will be shown in Section
2
,
\mathscr{K}
behaves differently in three subcases and consists of a unique connected set, except in the case
p_{1,1}=0
,
p_{1,0}\neq p_{0,1}
, where it also consists of an isolated point
(0,0)
. The projections of its connected subsets along the first and second variables are respectively denoted by
\mathscr{S}_{1}
and
\mathscr{S}_{2}
. Let
X:\mathscr{S}_{2}\to\mathscr{S}_{1}
be the mapping such that for all
y\in\mathscr{S}_{2}
,
(X(y),y)\in\mathscr{K}
. Under Assumption
(K1)
,
\mathscr{S}_{1}
and
\mathscr{S}_{2}
are Jordan curves,
X(y)
is a well defined and one-to-one mapping from
\mathscr{S}_{2}
onto
\mathscr{S}_{1}
. Further, as
y
moves on
\mathscr{S}_{2}
,
X(y)
moves on
\mathscr{S}_{1}
in different orientation. Let
Y:\mathscr{S}_{1}\to\mathscr{S}_{2}
be the inverse of
X
. Under Assumption
(K1)
, we can first deduce further insights of the kernel’s zero set in the bidisk (Proposition
1
), and later define conformal mappings from the interior of
\mathscr{S}_{1}
and
\mathscr{S}_{2}
onto disjoint, complementary domains, which help constructing appropriate
F(x)
and
G(y)
in (
1.11
).

Main results

In order to state our theorems properly, we introduce here some new notations. The interior (resp. exterior) of the Jordan curve
\mathscr{S}_{1}
is denoted by
\mathscr{S}_{1}^{+}
(resp.
\mathscr{S}_{1}^{-}
).
\mathscr{S}_{2}^{+}
and
\mathscr{S}_{2}^{-}
are denoted similarly. By analytic continuation, we first have the following result concerning solutions of the kernel in the bidisk.

Proposition 1
.

Under Assumption
(K1)
,

(i)

The function
X:\mathscr{S}_{2}\to\mathscr{S}_{1}
, defined such that
(X(y),y)\in\mathscr{K}
for all
y\in\mathscr{S}_{2}
, can be continued analytically and is uni-valued on
\mathscr{S}_{2}^{-}\cap\mathscr{C}^{+}
. In other words, the extension of
X(y)
has no branch point on
\mathscr{S}_{2}^{-}\cap\mathscr{C}^{+}
. We have an analogous statement for
Y(x)
, which is the inverse of
X(y)
;

(ii)

K(x,y)
has no root on
\mathscr{S}_{1}^{+}\times\mathscr{S}_{2}^{+}
.

Proposition
1
may seem technical but it is crucial to indicate domains where
H(x,y)
in (
1.11
) is well defined. Particularly in the case
p_{1,1}=0
, we will show that the generating function
H(x,y)
, initially defined on
\mathscr{S}_{1}^{+}\times\mathscr{S}_{2}^{+}
, also admits an extension around
(0,0)
. It is also worth mentioning that its item
(i)
is a new statement, whereas its item
(ii)
has been stated for the symmetric cases of
[
24
]
, and will be proven in Section
2
for non-symmetric cases.

Our main theorems also are the main results in
[
24
]
, but now applied to non-symmetric cases under two technical assumptions
(K4)
and
(K5)
. Roughly speaking,
(K4)
ensures that
\mathscr{S}_{1}
and
\mathscr{S}_{2}
do not admit a cusp at
1
, and
(K5)
ensures that a “shift” function, appearing in a conformal welding problem (Section
3
), has power growth derivatives. Under the assumptions
(K1)
–
(K5)
,
\mathscr{S}_{1}^{+}
and
\mathscr{S}_{2}^{+}
are mapped conformally onto two disjoint complementary domains, respectively by two mappings
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
such that:

(i)

\psi_{1}^{+}(X(y))=\psi_{2}^{+}(y)
for all
y\in\mathscr{S}_{2}
, where
\psi_{1}^{+}
and
\psi_{2}^{+}
are respectively the continuous extension of
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
to
\mathscr{S}_{1}
and
\mathscr{S}_{2}
;

(ii)

\psi_{1}^{+}(1)=\psi_{2}^{+}(1)=\infty
;

(iii)

\psi_{1}(\overline{z})=\overline{\psi_{1}(z)}
for all
z\in\mathscr{S}_{1}^{+}
, and
\psi_{2}(\overline{z})=\overline{\psi_{2}(z)}
for all
z\in\mathscr{S}_{2}^{+}
.

\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
will be showed to be well defined around
0
whether or not
0
is in
\mathscr{S}_{1}^{+},\,\mathscr{S}_{2}^{+}
.

We also introduce a related angle:

(1.13)

\theta:=\arccos\frac{-\sum k\ell p_{k,\ell}}{\sqrt{\sum k^{2}p_{k,\ell}}\sqrt{\ell^{2}p_{k,\ell}}}.

It turns out that
\theta
is the arithmetic mean of the angles at
1
of
\mathscr{S}_{1}
and
\mathscr{S}_{2}
, and contains information about the growth of harmonic functions.

Finally, we introduce the following polynomials forming a basis of the space
\mathbb{R}[X]
:

(1.14)

\begin{split}&P_{2n+1}=(X-\psi_{1}(0))^{n+1}(X-\psi_{2}(0))^{n},n\geqslant 0,\\
&P_{2n}=(X-\psi_{1}(0))^{n}(X-\psi_{2}(0))^{n},n\geqslant 1.\end{split}

We then have our first theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Under the assumptions
(K1)
–
(K5)
, for any
n\geqslant 1
, the function

(1.15)

H_{n}(x,y):=\frac{P_{n}(\psi_{1}(x))-P_{n}(\psi_{2}(y))}{K(x,y)}

defines the generating function of a harmonic function
h_{n}(i,j)
satisfying
(H5)
and
(H6)
. Moreover,
H_{n}
is analytic in
\mathscr{S}_{1}^{+}\times\mathscr{S}_{2}^{+}
.

In particular, the discrete Laplace transform of normalized discrete harmonic functions converges pointwise to the continuous Laplace transform of continuous harmonic functions, that is,

(1.16)

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{c}{m^{n\pi/\theta+1}}\mathscr{L}h_{n}(\lfloor mx\rfloor,\lfloor my\rfloor)=\mathscr{L}h_{\theta,n}(x,y),

for any
x,y>0
, where
c
is a non-vanishing constant and
h_{n,\theta}
is defined in (
1.6
), with
\theta
in (
1.13
), and the Laplace transform
\mathscr{L}
is defined as follows

(1.17)

\mathscr{L}f(x,y):=\begin{cases}\sum_{u,v=0}^{\infty}f(u,v)e^{-(ux+vy)},&f:\mathbb{N}^{2}\to\mathbb{C},\\
\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(u,v)e^{-(ux+vy)}dudv,&f:\mathscr{Q}\to\mathbb{C}.\end{cases}

From the harmonic functions constructed in Theorem
2
, we can describe the set of harmonic functions as a vector space.

Theorem 3
.

Under the assumptions
(K1)
–
(K5)
, the space of discrete harmonic functions is isomorphic to the vector space of formal power series
\mathbb{R}_{0}[[t]]
with vanishing constant term, through the isomorphism

\Phi:\left\{\begin{matrix}\mathbb{R}[[t]]&\longrightarrow&H(\mathbb{N}^{2})\\
\sum_{n\geqslant 1}a_{n}t^{n}&\longmapsto&\sum_{n\geqslant 1}a_{n}h_{n}.\end{matrix}\right.

Among the additional assumptions
(K1)
–
(K5)
, the condition
(K5)
is particularly technical and difficult to verify, since it concerns the growth of a function appearing in the analysis. Therefore, we also introduce a sufficient condition asserting the moments of order 4 of the weights
\{p_{k,\ell}\}_{k,\ell}
(Assumption
(H7)
), under which the assumption
(K5)
holds true. Then Theorems
2
and
3
are also valid under Assumptions
(H1)
–
(H7)
,
(K1)
and
(K4)
.

The next lemma concerns the smoothness of
\mathscr{S}_{1}
and
\mathscr{S}_{2}
, particularly their shapes at
1
. Let us denote by
V[K]
the zero set of the kernel on the closed bidisk:

V[K]:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:K(x,y)=0,|x|\leqslant 1,|y|\leqslant 1\}.

Let us recall some key definitions from complex analysis. Firstly, a curve parametrized by a function
z:[a,b]\to\mathbb{C}
is smooth if
z^{\prime}(t)
exists, is continuous and does not vanish on
[a,b]
. Secondly, a singular point of
V[K]
is defined as a solution of the system
\partial_{x}K(x,y)=\partial_{y}K(x,y)=K(x,y)=0
. Otherwise, any point of
V[K]
is called non-singular or regular. Finally, the angle at a corner point formed by the two tangents will be simply referred to as the angle (of the curve) at that point.

Lemma 6
.

Under Assumptions
(H1)
–
(H4)
and
(K1)
, the curves
\mathscr{S}_{1}
and
\mathscr{S}_{2}
are smooth everywhere except at
1
, where they admit corner points (see Figure
3
) with the angles

(2.6)

\theta_{1}=\arccos\frac{AC-A^{2}-2B^{2}+2AB}{A(A-2B+C)}\quad\text{and}\quad\theta_{2}=\arccos\frac{AC-A^{2}-2B^{2}-2AB}{A(A+2B+C)},

where
A=\sum p_{k,\ell}(k+\ell)^{2}
,
B=\sum p_{k,\ell}(k^{2}-\ell^{2})
and
C=\sum p_{k,\ell}(k-\ell)^{2}
.
Moreover,
\mathscr{K}\setminus\{(1,1)\}
consists of non-singular points of
V[K]
.

Proof.

We first study the differentiability of
\eta(s)
. By Assumption
(H4)
,
\partial_{x}K
and
\partial_{y}K
are well defined for all
|x|,|y|<1
, then
\partial_{\eta}K(\eta s,\eta s^{-1})
and
\partial_{s}K(\eta s,\eta s^{-1})
are also well defined for all
|\eta|<1
,
|s|=1
.

In the case
p_{1,1}=0
, recall that
K(\eta s,\eta s^{-1})=\eta\widetilde{K}(\eta,s)
, where
\widetilde{K}
is defined in (
2.3
). By Lemma
4
, for any
s_{0}\in\mathscr{C}\setminus\{\pm 1\}
,
\eta(s_{0})
is the unique root of
\widetilde{K}(\eta,s_{0})
in
\mathscr{C}^{+}
, i.e., there exists a continuous function
u(\eta,s)
defined in a neighborhood of
(\eta(s_{0}),s_{0})
such that
\widetilde{K}(\eta,s)=(\eta-\eta(s_{0}))u(\eta,s)
and
u(\eta(s),s)\not=0
. This is equivalent to
\partial_{\eta}\widetilde{K}(\eta(s_{0}),s_{0})=u(\eta(s_{0}),s_{0})\not=0
. Thus,
\eta^{\prime}(s)
is well defined for all
s\in\mathscr{C}\setminus\{\pm 1\}
under the form

(2.7)

\eta^{\prime}(s)=-\frac{\partial_{s}\widetilde{K}(\eta(s),s)}{\partial_{\eta}\widetilde{K}(\eta(s),s)},

which is obtained by differentiating the identity
\widetilde{K}(\eta(s),s)=0
. Since
(\eta(s)s)^{\prime}
and
(\eta(s)s^{-1})^{\prime}
are non-vanishing on
\mathscr{C}\setminus\{\pm 1\}
(by Assumption
(K1)
), then
\mathscr{S}_{1}
and
\mathscr{S}_{2}
are smooth everywhere except at
1
.

In the case
p_{1,1}\not=0
, notice that for all
s\in\mathscr{C}
,
\eta(s)
and
\eta_{2}(s)
are distinct roots of
K(\eta s,\eta s^{-1})
. Indeed, if there exists
s_{0}\in\mathscr{C}
such that
\eta(s_{0})=\eta_{2}(s_{0})
, then Lemma
4
implies
\eta(s_{0})s_{0}=\eta(-s_{0})(-s_{0})
, i.e.,
\mathscr{S}_{1}
is self-intersecting, which contradicts Assumption
(K1)
. Hence, for any
s\in\mathscr{C}\setminus\{1\}
,
\eta(s)
is the unique root of
K(\eta s,\eta s^{-1})
in a neighborhood of
\eta(s)
, which is equivalent to
\partial\eta K(\eta(s)s,\eta(s)s^{-1})\not=0
by similar arguments as in the case
p_{1,1}=0
. Thus,
\eta^{\prime}(s)
is well defined for all
s\in\mathscr{C}\setminus\{1\}
. By Assumption
(K1)
, then
\mathscr{S}_{1}
and
\mathscr{S}_{2}
are smooth everywhere except at
1
.

We now prove that
\mathscr{K}\setminus\{(1,1)\}
consists of non-singular points of
V[K]
. Consider first the point
(0,0)\in\mathscr{K}
, which can only happen in the case
p_{1,1}=0
. Since
\partial_{x}K(0,0)
and
\partial_{y}K(0,0)
, which are respectively equal to
p_{0,1}
and
p_{1,0}
, cannot simultaneously vanish, then
(0,0)
is a non-singular point of
V[K]
. Now move to the other points of
\mathscr{K}\setminus\{(1,1)\}
and let
(\eta,s)
be a root of
K(\eta s,\eta s^{-1})
such that
\eta\in\overline{\mathscr{C}}\setminus\{0\}
and

, then as shown in the beginning of the proof,
\partial_{\eta}K(\eta,s)\neq 0
. Since

\partial_{\eta}K=\partial_{\eta}x\partial_{x}K+\partial_{\eta}y\partial_{y}K=s\partial_{x}K+s^{-1}\partial_{y}K,

we must have either
\partial_{x}K(\eta,s)
or
\partial_{y}K(\eta,s)
non-zero. The claim then follows.

We now investigate the shapes of
\mathscr{S}_{1}
and
\mathscr{S}_{2}
at
1
. In the case
p_{1,1}=0
, notice that one cannot evaluate the limit of
\eta^{\prime}(s)
as
s\to 1
directly from (

) since the quotient is undefined at

. We then differentiate the identity
\widetilde{K}(\eta(s),s)=0
twice and evaluate its limit as

. The limit
\lim_{s\to 1}\eta^{\prime}(s)
satisfies the equation

(2.8)

AX^{2}+2BX+C=0,

where

,

and

are defined in Lemma

and are finite by Assumption

. Since the above equation has two distinct roots, which indicates that

is semi-differentiable at

and admits left and right derivatives

\partial_{\pm}\eta(1):=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}s=\exp(i\phi)\\
\phi\to 0^{\pm}\end{subarray}}\frac{\eta(s)-1}{s-1}\in\left\{\frac{-B\pm{i}\sqrt{AC-B^{2}}}{A}\right\}.

In order to know which root of (
2.8
) corresponds to
\partial_{+}\eta(1)
, we take a look at the following form:

\partial_{+}(\eta(s)s)\big|_{s=1}=\partial_{+}\eta(1)+\eta(1)\in\left\{\frac{A-B\pm{i}\sqrt{AC-B^{2}}}{A}\right\}.

Since
\arg\partial_{+}(\eta(s)s)\big|_{s=1}
indicates the oriented angle between the tangent of

and
\mathscr{C}
at

of

\cos\theta_{1}=2\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}\right)-1=2\frac{AC-B^{2}}{(A-B)^{2}+(AC-B^{2})}-1.

Similarly, we have:

\displaystyle\partial_{\pm}(\eta(s)s^{-1})\big|_{s=1}=\frac{-A-B\pm{i}\sqrt{AC-B^{2}}}{A},

\displaystyle\cos\theta_{2}=2\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\theta_{2}}{2}\right)-1=2\frac{AC-B^{2}}{(A+B)^{2}+(AC-B^{2})}-1.

In the case

, by differentiating the identity

Proof.

We have:

\displaystyle\cos\frac{\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}}{2}

\displaystyle=\cos\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}\cos\frac{\theta_{2}}{2}-\sin\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}\sin\frac{\theta_{2}}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(AC-B^{2})-(A-B)(A+B)}{\sqrt{(A^{2}-2AB+AC)(A^{2}+2AB+AC)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{C-A}{\sqrt{(A-2B+C)(A+2B+C)}}=\cos\theta,

which implies that

.

Moving to the proof of the trichotomy, we first recall from Lemma

that

and

move respectively clockwise and counterclockwise as

under Assumption

where

(K1)
implies
-A\leqslant B\leqslant A

cannot hold. Otherwise, we have:
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\not=0
if
-A<B<A
;
\theta_{1}\not=0

\theta_{2}=0
if
-A<B=A
;
\theta_{1}=0
,
\theta_{2}\not=0
if
-A=B<A
.
∎

Now let

be the intersection of

and

. Assuming that there exists

such that

, we construct a path

such that:

•

•

\gamma((0,\tau))\in\mathscr{S}_{2}^{+}

3.
Conformal welding

This section aims at constructing conformal mappings from

We move to the properties of

Although the proof of Theorem

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Observe first that any infinite sum

, with

, evaluated at any point

for all

. Thus, the mapping
\Phi
in Theorem

is well defined. The injectivity of

is implied by Lemma

.

Now let

be any harmonic function and

be its generating function. To show the surjectivity of
\Phi
, we prove that there exists a sequence

such that

.

In the case

, let
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
be the sequence satisfying Lemma

and
\widetilde{H}(x,y)
be the generating function of

. We recall that

and

are determined by Eq. (

\displaystyle H(x,y)=\frac{K\cdot H(x,0)+K\cdot H(0,y)-K\cdot H(0,0)}{K(x,y)},

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}(x,y)=\frac{K\cdot\widetilde{H}(x,0)+K\cdot\widetilde{H}(0,y)-K\cdot\widetilde{H}(0,0)}{K(x,y)}.

Moreover, the quotients are well defined since

.

We move to the case

, and further assume

. Then the function

, there exists a unique sequence

. Let

Weighted simple random walk

\displaystyle\pi_{2}(z)=\mu\circ\omega_{2}\circ y^{-1}(z)=\frac{2T_{3}(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{z+1}{z-1})-1}{2T_{3}(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{z+1}{z-1})+1}.

and

in Lemma
