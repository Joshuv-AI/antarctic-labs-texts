Local Completeness Logic on Kleene Algebra with Tests
Marco Milanese, Francesco Ranzato

Abstract
Local Completeness Logic (LCL) has been put forward as a program logic for proving both the correctness and incorrectness of program specifications. LCL is an abstract logic, parameterized by an abstract domain that allows combining over- and under-approximations of program behaviors. It turns out that LCL instantiated to the trivial singleton abstraction boils down to O’Hearn incorrectness logic, which allows us to prove the presence of program bugs. It has been recently proved that suitable extensions of Kleene algebra with tests (KAT) allow representing both O’Hearn incorrectness and Hoare correctness program logics within the same equational framework. In this work, we generalize this result by showing how KATs extended either with a modal diamond operator or with a top element are able to represent the local completeness logic LCL. This is achieved by studying how these extended KATs can be endowed with an abstract domain so as to define the validity of correctness/incorrectness LCL triples and to show that the LCL proof system is logically sound and, under some hypotheses, complete.

Kleene algebra

Programs are modeled as elements of a KAT, so that their properties can be algebraically derived

The foundational study of Kozen

Furthermore, very recently, Zhang, De Amorim and Gaboardi

This stream of works made it possible to reason equationally on both program correctness and incorrectness in the

The Problem.

Recently, Bruni et al.
[
5
]
put forward a novel program logic, called local completeness logic

[

10

simultaneously combines over- and under-approximations of program behaviours.
This program logic leverages the notion of

abstract interpretation, meaning that the abstract interpretation of atomic
program commands, such as variable assignments and Boolean guards, is complete (i.e. with no false alarm)

to standard completeness
[

30

which must be satisfied

While a global completeness program logic was proposed in
[
15
]
,
Bruni et al.
[
5
]
design a proof system for inferring that a program analysis is locally complete.
It turns out

5

\LCL

the trivial store abstraction with a unique “don’t know” value abstracting any concrete store property,
boils down to O’Hearn incorrectness logic
[
26
]
. Moreover,
Bruni et al.
[
6
]
also show that abstract interpretations can be made locally complete through minimal
domain refinements that repair the lack of local completeness in a given program analysis.

In the original definition of
\LCL
in
[
5
]
program properties are represented
as elements of a concrete domain
C
and program semantics as functions of type
C\rightarrow C
.
Although straightforward, this approach determines a specific type of program semantics.
Vice versa, by exploiting a KAT, program properties are represented as tests and programs
as generic elements of the KAT. Hence, a KAT based formulation becomes agnostic w.r.t. the
underlying
semantics and can therefore admit multiple different models of computation (e.g., trace-based semantics, or even
models not related to program semantics as shown by the language-theoretic example in Section
3.5
).
Furthermore, KAT is a particularly
suitable formalism for compositionally reasoning on programs as all its basic composition operations on
programs (concatenation, choice and Kleene iteration) are directly modeled within the algebra:
this
allows us to represent composite programs and tests as elements of the KAT and, in particular, to check
for their equality and inclusion directly in the algebra. Thus,
following the KAT-based model of incorrectness logic
advocated by Möller, O’Hearn and Hoare
[
24
]
, this paper
pushes forward this line of work by studying an algebraic
formulation of
\LCL
program logic, with the objective of showing
that there is no need to leverage particular semantic
properties of programs to reason on their local completeness.

In this work, we show that the local completeness logic

Background on Kleene Algebra with Tests

A Kleene algebra with tests (KAT) is a purely algebraic structure that provides
an elegant equational framework for program reasoning.
A KAT consists of actions, playing the role of
programs, and tests, interpreted as pre/postconditions and Boolean guards. KAT elements can be combined with three
basic operations: nondeterministic choice
a_{1}+a_{2}
, sequential composition
a_{1};a_{2}
, and Kleene iteration
a^{*}
.
A standard model of KAT used to represent computations is the relational model, in which KAT elements are binary
relations on some set, thus modeling programs as a relation between input and output states.
Further models of KAT include regular languages over a finite alphabet,
square matrices over another Kleene algebra, and Kleene algebra modulo theories
[
16
]
.
In the following, we briefly recall some basics of KAT. For more details, the reader is referred to
[
8
,
19
,
11
]
.

An
idempotent-semiring
(

(A,+,\cdot,0,1)
where:
(1)
(A,+,0)
is a commutative monoid with an idempotent addition, i.e., for all
a\in A
,
a+a=a
;
(2)
(A,\cdot,1)
is a monoid, where the multiplication symbol
\cdot
is often omitted, such that,
for any
a\in A

0\cdot a=a\cdot 0
;
(3) multiplication distributes over addition (in both arguments).
In an i-semiring

, the relation

is a partial order, referred to
as the natural ordering, that we will implicitly use throughout the paper. Note that the addition

is the join w.r.t. this natural ordering.

A

is a tuple

(A,\Test(A),+,\cdot,\lnot,0,1)

of the Boolean algebra

and addition

A

is a tuple
(K,+,\cdot,\mathord{{}^{*}},0,1)
where:
(1)
(K,+,\cdot,0,1)
is an i-semiring; (2)

is a unary operation, called Kleene star or iteration,
satisfying the following conditions:

\displaystyle 1+aa^{*}\leq a^{*}

\displaystyle 1+a^{*}a\leq a^{*}

(

\displaystyle b+ac\leq c\Rightarrow a^{*}b\leq c

\displaystyle b+ca\leq c\Rightarrow ba^{*}\leq c

3
Local Completeness Logic in KAT

We investigate how the local completeness program logic
\LCL

[
5
]
can be interpreted on a KAT. To achieve this, we need to address
the following tasks:

•

To define a notion of abstract domain of a KAT, with the aim of abstracting
the set of program predicates, namely tests of a KAT;

•

To establish a concrete semantics and a corresponding sound abstract semantics of programs on KATs;

•

To adapt the local completeness proof system to attain valid triples on a KAT;

•

To prove logical soundness and completeness w.r.t. a KAT of this new proof system.

3.1
Program Properties in KAT

Program properties can be broadly classified as intensional and extensional. The former relate to how programs are written, while the latter concern the input-output relation of a program, i.e., its strongest postcondition denotational semantics.
Local completeness logic
\LCL
relies on
an abstract interpretation of programs which crucially depends on intensional properties of programs, meaning
that even if two programs share the same denotation, they could well have different abstract semantics.
Thus, we expect that an appropriate definition of abstract semantics based on a KAT model should also be intensional.
Given two elements
a
and
b
of a modal bdKAT playing the role of programs, we therefore expect that
their backward diamond functions might coincide, i.e.
\boldsymbol{\langle}a\boldsymbol{|}=\boldsymbol{\langle}b\boldsymbol{|}
, even if
a
and
b
encode different programs, i.e.
a\neq b
.
However, as shown by the following remark for the basic
relational model of KAT, it might happen that for certain classes of KAT models the backward diamond interpretation is injective.

In order to prove such result, we require an auxiliary Lemma to provide an explicit formula for the
\boldsymbol{\langle}\cdot\boldsymbol{|}
operator in the special case of full set of tests (i.e.,
\Test(K)=\wp(\{(x,x)\mid x\in X\})

\Leftarrow

\boldsymbol{\langle}\cdot\boldsymbol{|}
is a function on the first argument. Instead, we focus on the
\Rightarrow
direction.

Assume
\boldsymbol{\langle}a\boldsymbol{|}=\boldsymbol{\langle}b\boldsymbol{|}
. If
(x,y)\in a
, then by Lemma
2

\boldsymbol{\langle}a\boldsymbol{|}\{(x,x)\}=\boldsymbol{\langle}b\boldsymbol{|}\{(x,x)\}\supseteq\{(y,y)\}

This implies the existence of
z\in X
such that
(z,z)\in\{(x,x)\}
and
(z,y)\in b
, but the only possibility is
z=x
so that
(x,y)\in b
. By symmetry we can obtain
(x,y)\in b\Rightarrow(x,y)\in a
.

Notice that we can be sure that
\{(x,x)\}\in\Test(K)
because of the hypothesis
\Test(K)=\wp(\{(x,x)\mid x\in X\})
. ∎

This means that, at least for some fundamental KAT models, KAT elements are equal iff they are extensionally
equal, or, equivalently, they carry exclusively extensional program properties. In this case,
when a program is encoded with a KAT element all the intensional properties are lost and it is indistinguishable
from any other program with the same denotational semantics. Therefore, an abstract interpretation-based
semantics can not be defined directly on KAT elements.

3.2
KAT Language

As a consequence of the discussion in Section
3.1
, the concrete semantics cannot be directly
defined on KAT elements. A solution is to define it on an inductive language. Actually, in a language of programs, two elements are equal iff they are syntactically equal, or, in other terms, if the corresponding programs are written in the same way. This property makes a language an ideal basis upon which a semantics can be defined, because this brings the chance of depending on
intensional properties.

A natural choice for defining this language of programs is the so-called
KAT language
, as originally defined by Kozen and Smith
[
21
, Section 2.3]
, because it contains all and only the operators of a KAT, so that the interpretation of language terms as
KAT elements is the most natural one. This language
is inductively defined from two disjoint sets of primitive actions and tests through the basic elements/operations
0,1,+,\cdot,^{*}
of KATs.
More precisely, given a set
\Sigma
of
primitive actions
and a set
B
of
primitive tests
such that
\Sigma\cap B=\varnothing
, the corresponding
KAT language

T_{\Sigma,B}
of terms is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\Atom\ni\mathtt{a}::=a\in\mathit{\Sigma}\mid p\in\mathit{B}

\displaystyle T_{\Sigma,B}\ni\mathtt{t::=\mathtt{a}\mid 0\mid 1\mid t_{1}+t_{2}\mid t_{1}\cdot t_{2}\mid t^{*}}

For simplicity, we assume that
\mathtt{0}
and
\mathtt{1}
are primitive tests in
B
,
so that
\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\in\Atom
.
The notation
\Atom(\mathtt{t})\subseteq\Atom
will denote the set of atoms occurring in a term

\mathtt{t}\in T_{\Sigma,B}
.
Notice that a KAT language
T_{\Sigma,B}
is an equivalent representation of the language of regular commands
used in
[
26
,
5
]
for their program logics.

Given a KAT
K
, an evaluation of atoms in
K
is a mapping
u:\Atom\rightarrow K

such that
\mathtt{p}\in B\Rightarrow u(\mathtt{p})\in\Test(K)
.
An evaluation
u
induces an interpretation of terms
\llbracket\mathtt{\cdot}\rrbracket_{u}:T_{\Sigma,B}\rightarrow K
, which is inductively
defined as expected:

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathtt{a}\rrbracket_{u}\triangleq u(\mathtt{a})

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathtt{t_{1}+t_{2}}\rrbracket_{u}\triangleq\llbracket\mathtt{t_{1}}\rrbracket_{u}+\llbracket\mathtt{t_{2}}\rrbracket_{u}

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathtt{t_{1}\cdot t_{2}}\rrbracket_{u}\triangleq\llbracket\mathtt{t_{1}}\rrbracket_{u}\cdot\llbracket\mathtt{t_{2}}\rrbracket_{u}

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathtt{t^{*}}\rrbracket_{u}\triangleq\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket_{u}^{*}

In turn, the concrete semantic function

\llbracket\mathtt{\cdot}\rrbracket^{K}:T_{\Sigma,B}\rightarrow\big(\Test(K)\rightarrow\Test(K)\big)

models the strongest postcondition of a program, i.e. of a language term, for a
given precondition, i.e. a KAT test. This is therefore defined in terms of the backward diamond of a bdKAT as follows:

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket^{K}p\triangleq\boldsymbol{\langle}\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket_{u}\boldsymbol{|}p.

(11)

We will often use
\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket
to denote a concrete semantics, by omitting the superscript
K

when it is clear from the context.

The construction of the concrete semantic leverages the backward-diamond (
11
) and the interpretation
\llbracket\mathtt{\cdot}\rrbracket_{u}
, hence the properties of Proposition
1
can be transferred to
\llbracket\mathtt{\cdot}\rrbracket^{K}
. For completeness we summarize them in the following:

\displaystyle p\leq q\Rightarrow\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket p\leq\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket q

(12)

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathtt{t_{1}+t_{2}}\rrbracket p=\llbracket\mathtt{t_{1}}\rrbracket p+\llbracket\mathtt{t_{2}}\rrbracket p

(13)

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket(p+q)=\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket p+\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket q

(14)

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathtt{t_{1}\cdot t_{2}}\rrbracket p=\llbracket\mathtt{t_{2}}\rrbracket\llbracket\mathtt{t_{1}}\rrbracket p

(15)

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathtt{t^{*}}\rrbracket p=\bigvee_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket)^{n}p

(16)

\displaystyle p+\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket\llbracket\mathtt{t^{*}}\rrbracket p\leq\llbracket\mathtt{t^{*}}\rrbracket p

(17)

3.3
Kleene Abstractions

An abstract domain is used in abstract interpretation for approximating store properties, i.e., sets of program
stores form the concrete domain, likewise in our KAT model, the role of concrete domain is played by the set of tests
\Test(K)
of a KAT
K
, ordered by the natural
ordering induced by
K
.

Definition 3.1
(Kleene Abstract Domain)

A poset
(A,\leq_{A})
is a
Kleene abstract domain
of a bdKAT
K
if:

(i)

There exists a Galois insertion, defined by
a concretization map
\gamma:A\rightarrow\Test(K)
and an abstraction map
\alpha:\Test(K)\rightarrow A
,
of the poset
(A,\leq_{A})
into the poset

{(\Test(K),\leq_{K})}
;

(ii)

A
is countably-complete, i.e., any countable subset of
A
admits a lub. ∎

The abstract semantic function

\llbracket\mathtt{\cdot}\rrbracket_{A}^{\sharp}:T_{\Sigma,B}\rightarrow(A\rightarrow A)

defines how abstract preconditions are transformed into abstract postconditions. Likewise store-based abstract interpretation,
this abstract semantics is inductively defined as follows:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathtt{a}\rrbracket_{A}^{\sharp}p^{\sharp}\triangleq\alpha(\llbracket\mathtt{a}\rrbracket^{K}\gamma(p^{\sharp}))

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathtt{t_{\text{$1$}}+t_{\text{$2$}}}\rrbracket_{A}^{\sharp}p^{\sharp}\triangleq\llbracket\mathtt{t_{\text{$1$}}}\rrbracket_{A}^{\sharp}p^{\sharp}+\llbracket\mathtt{t_{\text{$2$}}}\rrbracket_{A}^{\sharp}p^{\sharp}

(18)

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathtt{t_{\text{$1$}}\cdot t_{\text{$2$}}}\rrbracket_{A}^{\sharp}p^{\sharp}\triangleq\llbracket\mathtt{t_{\text{$2$}}}\rrbracket_{A}^{\sharp}(\llbracket\mathtt{t_{\text{$1$}}}\rrbracket_{A}^{\sharp}p^{\sharp})

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathtt{t^{*}}\rrbracket_{A}^{\sharp}p^{\sharp}\triangleq\textstyle\bigvee_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket_{A}^{\sharp})^{n}p^{\sharp}

We recall in the following Proposition few properties of Galois Insertion. Proofs and a more in depth-presentation are available in

3.4
Local Completeness Logic on bdKAT

Given a Kleene abstract domain
A
, we will slightly abuse notation by using

•

is

for

It is known
[
15
]

that global completeness is hard to achieve in practice, even for simple programs.
Moreover, a complete and compositional (i.e., inductively defined on program structure)

[
4
]
. This motivated to study a local notion of completeness
in abstract interpretation
[

]
as a pragmatic and more attainable weakening of standard global completeness.

In our local completeness logic on a Kleene algebra, a triple

(2)

on the precondition

(3)

and

have the same over-approximation in

.

Theorem 3.4
(Logical Soundness of

\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket_{A}^{\sharp}\alpha(p)=\alpha(q)

The proof is on the structure of the derivation tree of

3.5
An Example of a Language-Theoretic KAT

To give an example digressing from programs and showing the generality of the KAT-based approach, we describe a language-theoretic model of Kleene algebra, early introduced by Kozen and Smith
[
21
, Section 3]
.

Let
\Sigma=\{\mathtt{u}\}
and
B=\{\mathtt{b}_{1},\mathtt{b}_{2}\}
be, resp., the sets of primitive actions and tests. An
atom
is a string
c_{1}c_{2}
, where
c_{i}\in\{\mathtt{b}_{i},\overline{\mathtt{b}}_{i}\}
. If
c_{i}=\mathtt{b}_{i}
, where
i\in\{1,2\}
, then
\mathtt{b}_{i}
appears positively in the atom
c_{1}c_{2}
, while if
c_{i}=\mathtt{\overline{b}}_{i}
it appears negatively. A
guarded string
is
either a single atom or
a string
\alpha_{0}\mathtt{a}_{1}\alpha_{1}...\mathtt{a}_{n}\alpha_{n}
, where
\alpha_{i}
are atoms and
\mathtt{a}_{i}\in\Sigma
. If we are only interested in the first (last) atom of a guarded string
\alpha\mathtt{a}_{1}\alpha_{1}...\mathtt{a}_{n}\beta
, we may refer to it through the syntax
\alpha x
(
x\beta
).
Concatenation of guarded strings is given by a coalesced product operation
\diamond
, which is partially defined as follows:

x\alpha\diamond\beta y\triangleq\begin{cases}x\alpha y&if $\alpha=\beta$\\
\textrm{undefined}&otherwise\end{cases}

The elements of this KAT
\mathcal{G}
are sets of guarded strings. Thus,
+
is set
union, the product is defined as pointwise coalesced product:

A\cdot B\triangleq\{x\diamond y\mid x\in A,y\in B\},

while the Kleene iteration is:

A^{*}\triangleq\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}A^{n}
.
The product identity corresponds to the whole set of atoms

1_{\mathcal{G}}\triangleq\{\mathtt{b}_{1}\mathtt{b}_{2},\mathtt{b}_{1}\overline{\mathtt{b}}_{2},\overline{\mathtt{b}}_{1}\mathtt{b}_{2},\overline{\mathtt{b}}_{1}\overline{\mathtt{b}}_{2}\}
, while

0_{\mathcal{G}}
is the empty set. The set of tests is
\Test(\mathcal{G})\triangleq\wp(1_{\mathcal{G}})
.

It turns out that
\mathcal{G}
is a bdKAT, whose backward diamond is as follows: for all
a\in{\mathcal{G}}
and

\boldsymbol{\langle}a\boldsymbol{|}p=\{\beta\mid x\beta\in pa\}.

(28)

Let

. By Lemma
1
, it is enough to show that
r
is the least

satisfying

.

Let
x\beta\in pa
. By definition,
\beta\in r
means that
x\beta\diamond\beta=x\beta
is contained in
par
. This therefore means that
pa\leq par
. The opposite inequality is trivial since
r
is a test, hence
r\leq 1_{\mathcal{G}}
, and by monotonicity of
\cdot
, we have that
pa\geq par
, thus implying
pa=par
.

Assume now, by contradiction, that there exists
t\in\Test(\mathcal{G})
such that
pa=pat
,
t\leq r
and

t\neq r
. This means that there is at least an atom
\beta
in
r
which is not in
t
. By definition of

, there is a guarded string
x\beta\in pa
. Since
pa=pat
, the last atom of all the guarded strings in

must be in

, but this does not hold for

as
\beta\notin t
. ∎

\displaystyle\triangleq\{\alpha\mathtt{a}\beta\mid\alpha,\beta\in 1_{\mathcal{G}}\},

The

\LCK

the composition exhibits a short-circuiting behavior, meaning that an error in the first command aborts the execution without executing the second one, while the Kleene star allows an error to occur after some error-free iterations. It is straightforward to check that this definition of abstract semantics is monotonic and sound.

5
Local Completeness Logic in TopKAT

We have shown in Section
3
how KAT extended with a modal backward-diamond operator allows us to interpret and
represent the local completeness program logic. This result follows the approach
by Moller, O’Hearn and Hoare
[
24
]
, who leverage a backward-diamond operator in their KAT interpretation
of correctness/incorrectness logics.
On the other hand, Zhang, de Amorim and
Gaboardi
[
34
]
have recently shown that incorrectness logic can be formulated
for a standard KAT, provided that it contains a top element, thus giving rise to a so-called TopKAT. In particular,
[
34
]
observed that a TopKAT is enough to express the codomain of relational KATs.
In this section, we take a similar path in studying an alternative formulation of local completeness logic based on a TopKAT.

5.1
Abstracting TopKATs

We expect that the base case of abstract semantics

\llbracket\mathtt{\mathtt{a}}\rrbracket_{A}^{\sharp}p^{\sharp}
for a basic action
\mathtt{a}\in\Atom
is
defined as best correct approximation in
A
of the concrete semantics of
\mathtt{a}
on the concretization of
p^{\sharp}
.
In a bdKAT this is achieved in definition
(
18
) through its backward-diamond operator, which is crucially used in (
11
) to define the
strongest postcondition as
\llbracket\mathtt{a}\rrbracket^{K}\gamma(p^{\sharp})=\boldsymbol{\langle}\llbracket\mathtt{a}\rrbracket_{u}\boldsymbol{|}\gamma(p^{\sharp})
.
Zhang et al.
[
34
]
observed that in a relational model of KAT, the codomain inclusion
\cod(q)\subseteq\cod(pa)
defining the meaning of an
under-approximation triple
[p]\,a\,[q]
can be expressed in a TopKAT as the inequality

\top q\leq\top pa
, thus hinting that this latter condition could be taken as definition of validity of incorrectness triples in a TopKAT. We follow here a similar approach by considering the element
\top p\llbracket\mathtt{a}\rrbracket_{u}
as a proxy for strongest postconditions in a TopKAT.
It is worth noticing that while in a bdKAT a strongest postcondition
\boldsymbol{\langle}\llbracket\mathtt{a}\rrbracket_{u}\boldsymbol{|}p
is always a test, in a TopKAT
K
,
given
p\in\Test(K)
and a term
\mathtt{t}\in T_{\Sigma,B}
,
it is not guaranteed that there exists a test
q\in\Test(K)
such that
\top p\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket_{u}=\top q
, as shown by the following example.

Example 5.1
(Strongest Postconditions in TopKAT)

Consider the Kleene algebra
A_{3}=\{0,1,a\}
consisting of 3 elements and characterized by Conway
[
8
, Chapter 12]
. This algebra can be lifted to a KAT by letting
\Test(A_{3})\triangleq\{0,1\}

and defining the KAT operators as follows:

\begin{array}[]{|c|ccc|}\hline\cr~+&~0&~1&~a\\
\hline\cr 0&0&1&a\\
1&1&1&1\\
a&a&1&a\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{|c|ccc|}\hline\cr~\cdot&~0&~1&~a\\
\hline\cr 0&0&0&0\\
1&0&1&a\\
a&0&a&0\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|}\hline\cr\rule{0.0pt}{12.0pt}~\mathord{0^{*}}\triangleq 1&~\mathord{1^{*}}\triangleq 1&~\mathord{a^{*}}\triangleq 1~\rule{0.0pt}{12.0pt}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

We have that
1\geq a
and
1\geq 0
, because
1+a=1
and
1+0=1
, so that
A_{3}
is a TopKAT with
\top=1
. Moreover,
\top\cdot 1\cdot a=1\cdot 1\cdot a=a
, whereas there exists no
q\in\Test(A_{3})
satisfying
\top\cdot q=a
. Indeed,
\top\cdot 1=1\cdot 1=1\neq a
and
\top\cdot 0=0\neq a
.
∎

In general, the lack of such a
q\in\Test(K)
implies that the abstract domain cannot be defined as an abstraction of
the set of topped-tests
\{\top p\mid p\in\Test(K)\}
, because in this case we could miss the abstraction

\alpha(\top p\llbracket\mathtt{a}\rrbracket_{u})
. To settle this issue, an abstract domain must provide an approximation of the larger set

\topp(K)\triangleq\{\top a\mid a\in K\}

which contains all the multiplicative elements of type
\top a
.

Definition 5.2
(Top Kleene Abstract Domain)

A poset
(A,\leq)
is a

of a TopKAT

(i)

and
\alpha:\topp(K)\rightarrow A
,
of the poset
(A,\leq_{A})
into the poset

(\topp(K),\leq_{K})
;

(ii)

\top b^{\prime}p\geq c^{\prime}
.

•

The (limit) rules of
\LCTK_{A}
and
\UL
differ on the distributivity condition. We assume that
distributivity also holds in
\UL
.

By instantiating to the trivial abstract domain
A_{tr}
, it turns out that the two proof systems become equivalent.

Theorem 5.7
(
\LCTK_{A_{tr}}\equiv\UL
)

Let
K
be a TopKAT
∗
. For any
a,b\in K
,
\mathtt{t}\in T_{\Sigma,B}
:

\vdash^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{TK}}}_{A_{tr}}[a]\;\mathtt{t}\;[b]\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\vdash_{\UL}[a]\;\mathtt{t}\;[b].

Proof

The
\Rightarrow
direction can be shown as in Theorem
3.6
. We focus instead on the opposite direction.

\Leftarrow
) The proof is by induction on the derivation tree of
\vdash_{\UL}[a]\;\mathtt{t}\;[b]
. The rules of Table
1
are immediate, meaning that only (empty),(choice),(disj),(iterate zero) and (iterate non-zero) are left to prove. As in Theorem
3.6
, we leverage the completeness of the proof system and likewise, in order to prove that a triple is valid, it is enough to show that condition (1) holds because (2) is trivially true.

(empty) :
\models^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{TK}}}_{A_{tr}}[a]\;\mathtt{t}\;[0]
is trivial since
\top\cdot 0=0\leq\top a\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket_{u}
is always true, therefore by completeness of
\LCTK_{A_{tr}}
,
\vdash^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{TK}}}_{A_{tr}}[a]\;\mathtt{t}\;[0]
.

(choice) : By induction we have, without loss of generality,
\vdash^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{TK}}}_{A_{tr}}[a]\;\mathtt{t_{1}}\;[b]
and we need to show
\vdash^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{TK}}}_{A_{tr}}[a]\;\mathtt{t_{1}+t_{2}}\;[b]
. Notice that for any
\mathtt{t_{2}}\in T_{\Sigma,B}
,
\models^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{TK}}}_{A_{tr}}[a]\;\mathtt{t_{2}}\;[0]
because
\top\cdot 0=0\leq\top a\llbracket\mathtt{t_{2}}\rrbracket_{u}
, therefore by completeness of
\LCTK_{A_{tr}}

\vdash^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{TK}}}_{A_{tr}}[a]\;\mathtt{t_{2}}\;[0]
. Finally, (join) yields the result
\vdash^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{TK}}}_{A_{tr}}[a]\;\mathtt{t_{1}+t_{2}}\;[b]
.

(disj) : By induction we have
\vdash^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{TK}}}_{A_{tr}}[a_{1}]\;\mathtt{t}\;[b_{1}]
and
\vdash^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{TK}}}_{A_{tr}}[a_{2}]\;\mathtt{t}\;[b_{2}]
and we need to show
\vdash^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{TK}}}_{A_{tr}}[a_{1}+a_{2}]\;\mathtt{t}\;[b_{1}+b_{2}]
. By soundness
\top b_{1}\leq\top a_{1}\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket_{u}
and
\top b_{2}\leq\top a_{2}\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket_{u}
, so that
\top(b_{1}+b_{2})\leq\top(a_{1}+a_{2})\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket_{u}
. Finally, by completeness we retrieve the result:
\vdash^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{TK}}}_{A_{tr}}[a_{1}+a_{2}]\;\mathtt{t}\;[b_{1}+b_{2}]
.

(iterate zero) : By (

∗

-unfold
)
\llbracket\mathtt{t^{*}}\rrbracket_{u}=(\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket_{u})^{*}\geq 1
, so that
\top a\llbracket\mathtt{t^{*}}\rrbracket_{u}\geq\top a
and by completeness
\vdash^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{TK}}}_{A_{tr}}[a]\;\mathtt{t^{*}}\;[a]
.

(iterate non-zero) : By (

∗

-unfold
)
\llbracket\mathtt{t^{*}}\rrbracket_{u}=(\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket_{u})^{*}\geq(\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket_{u})^{*}\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket_{u}=\llbracket\mathtt{t^{*}}\rrbracket_{u}\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket_{u}=\llbracket\mathtt{t^{*}\cdot t}\rrbracket_{u}
. Thus, by hypothesis
\top b\leq\top a\llbracket\mathtt{t^{*}\cdot t}\rrbracket_{u}\leq\top a\llbracket\mathtt{t^{*}}\rrbracket_{u}
and by completeness
\vdash^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{TK}}}_{A_{tr}}[a]\;\mathtt{t^{*}}\;[b]
.
∎

In turn, the logical soundness and completeness of
\UL
can be retrieved as a consequence of those of
\LCTK
.

Corollary 3

Under the same hypotheses of Theorem
5.7
, the proof system
\UL
is sound and complete, that is,

\vdash_{\UL}[p]\;\mathtt{t}\;[q]\;\Leftrightarrow\;\>\models_{\UL}[p]\;\mathtt{t}\;[q]
.

Proof

\displaystyle\vdash_{\UL}[p]\;\mathtt{t}\;[q]

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\qquad\textrm{[by Theorem \ref{thm:lctk_ul_equiv}]}

\displaystyle\vdash^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{TK}}}_{A_{tr}}[p]\;\mathtt{t}\;[q]

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\qquad\textrm{[by Theorems \ref{thm:lsound_top}, \ref{thm:lcompl_top}]}

\displaystyle\models^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{TK}}}_{A_{tr}}[p]\;\mathtt{t}\;[q]

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\models_{\UL}[p]\;\mathtt{t}\;[q]

∎

Incorrectness Logic in TopKAT

In Section
5
we proposed an alternative formulation of local completeness logic based on a TopKAT. Here, we take a similar path as in Section
4
in adding support for abnormal termination. Like in the modal formulation, each language term is interpreted as a pair of KAT elements, meaning that the interpretation function has signature
\llbracket\mathtt{\cdot}\rrbracket_{u}:T_{\Sigma,B}\rightarrow(K\times K)
and is defined inductively as in (
30
). Following the approach of Section
5
we consider an element
\top pa
as a proxy for the strongest postcondition and in particular we have two such elements corresponding to, resp., normal and abnormal termination:
\top p{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket_{u_{\textbf{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0} ok }}}}}
and
\top p{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket_{u_{\textbf{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0} err }}}}}
.

The abstract semantic is a function of type
\llbracket\mathtt{\cdot}\rrbracket_{A}^{\sharp}:T_{\Sigma,B}\rightarrow(A\rightarrow(A\times A))
, where
A
is a top Kleene abstract domain. As usual it is defined inductively, with base case
\mathtt{a}\in\Atom
defined as

\llbracket\mathtt{a}\rrbracket_{A}^{\sharp}p^{\sharp}\triangleq\textbf{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0} ok }}{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}:\alpha(\gamma(p^{\sharp}){\color[rgb]{0,1,0}\llbracket\mathtt{a}\rrbracket_{u_{\textbf{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0} ok }}}}})},\textbf{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0} err }}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}:\alpha(\gamma(p^{\sharp}){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\llbracket\mathtt{a}\rrbracket_{u_{\textbf{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0} err }}}}})}

and the inductive cases as in (
33
). It turns out that if
K
is a TopKAT
∗
then the abstract semantic is sound and monotone.

6.1
Local completeness incorrectness logic on TopKAT

The proof system
\LCIL
can be adapted to the TopKAT framework as follows. Let
A
be a top Kleene abstract domain on a TopKAT
∗

K
and a language
T_{\Sigma,B}
. If
a,b\in K

\mathtt{t}\in T_{\Sigma,B}
, then

•

The triple
[a]\;\mathtt{t}\;{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}[\mathbf{ok}:b]}

\top b\leq\top a{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket_{u_{\textbf{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0} ok }}}}}
, and (2)
{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket^{\sharp}_{A_{\textbf{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0} ok }}}}}\alpha(\top a)=\alpha(\top b)=\alpha(\top a{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket_{u_{\textbf{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0} ok }}}}})

The triple
[a]\;\mathtt{t}\;{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}[\mathbf{err}:c]}

\top c\leq\top a{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket_{u_{\textbf{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0} err }}}}}
, and (2)
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket^{\sharp}_{A_{\textbf{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0} err }}}}}\alpha(\top a)=\alpha(\top c)=\alpha(\top a{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\llbracket\mathtt{t}\rrbracket_{u_{\textbf{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0} err }}}}})

A triple

6.2
Relationship with Incorrectness logic

In Section
4.1
and
5.3
we have shown that the modal formulation of
\LCIL
generalizes
\IL
and
\LCTK
generalizes
\UL
. Likewise, it is possible to show that
\LCTIL
generalizes
\IL
. A TopKAT formulation of the
\IL
proof system was already proposed in
[
34
, Figure 4]
. We refer to such system for the proposed reduction, with the same minor changes that we discussed in Section

and
\top c^{\prime}\leq\top c

\IL
.

Similarly to the reductions already proposed in this paper, we let

, where

(

be a TopKAT

. For any

\vdash^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{TK}}}_{A_{tr}}[a]\;\mathtt{t}\;{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}[\mathbf{ok}:b]}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}[\mathbf{err}:c]}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\vdash_{\IL}[a]\;\mathtt{t}\;{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}[\mathbf{ok}:b]}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}[\mathbf{err}:c]}

Analogous to the proof of Theorem

7
Conclusion

This work has shown that the abstract interpretation-based local completeness logic introduced in
[
5
]
can be generalized to and interpreted
in Kleene algebra with tests. In particular, we proved that this can be achieved both for KATs extended
with a modal backward diamond operator playing the role of strongest postcondition,
and for KATs endowed with a
top element.
Our results generalize both
the modal
[
24
]

and top
[
34
]
KAT
approaches that encode Hoare correctness and O’Hearn incorrectness logic using different classes of KATs.
In particular, our KAT-based logic leverages an abstract interpretation
of KAT, a problem that was not studied so far.

Our plan for future work includes, but is not limited to, the following questions.

•

For a KAT with top
\top
, following the technical idea underlying the approach
by Zhang et al.
[
34
]
,
we defined an abstract domain as an approximation of all
the algebraic elements of type
\top\cdot a
, where
a
is any element of the KAT (cf. Definition
5.2
). Although this definition technically works, it is somehow artificial,
because the elements
\top\cdot a
do not carry a clear intuitive meaning. As an interesting future task, we would like
to characterize under which conditions an element
\top\cdot a
coincides with
\top\cdot p
for some test
p\in\Test(K)
,
and if such test
p
is unique.

•

This work is a first step towards an
algebraic and equational approach to abstract interpretation
.
We envisage that the reasoning made by an abstract interpreter of programs could be made purely equational within a KAT equipped
with a suitable collection of axioms. The ambition would be to conceive a notion of
abstract Kleene algebra
(AKA)
making this slogan true:
AKA is for the abstract interpretation of programs what KAT is for concrete interpretation of programs
.
