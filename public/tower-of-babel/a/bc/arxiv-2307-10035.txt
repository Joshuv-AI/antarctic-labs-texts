Trees with at least 6 ​ ℓ + 11 6\ell+11 vertices are ℓ \ell -reconstructible
Alexandr V. Kostochka, Mina Nahvi, Douglas B. West, Dara Zirlin

Abstract
The ( n − ℓ ) (n-\ell) -deck of an n n -vertex graph is the multiset of (unlabeled) subgraphs obtained from it by deleting ℓ \ell vertices. An n n -vertex graph is ℓ \ell -reconstructible if it is determined by its ( n − ℓ ) (n-\ell) -deck, meaning that no other graph has the same deck. We prove that every tree with at least 6 ​ ℓ + 11 6\ell+11 vertices is ℓ \ell -reconstructible.



1
Introduction

The
j
-deck
of a graph is the multiset of its
j
-vertex induced
subgraphs. We write this as the
(n-\ell)
-deck when the graph has
n

vertices and the focus is on deleting
\ell
vertices. An
n
-vertex graph is

\ell
-reconstructible
if it is determined by its
(n-\ell)
-deck.
Since every member of the
(j-1)
-deck arises

times by deleting a
vertex from a member of the
j
-deck, the
j
-deck of a graph determines its

(j-1)
-deck. Therefore, a natural reconstruction problem is to find for each
graph the maximum
\ell
such that it is
\ell
-reconstructible. For this
problem, Manvel
[
11
,
12
]
extended the classical Reconstruction
Conjecture of Kelly
[
6
]
and Ulam
[
17
]
.

Conjecture 1.1

(Manvel
[
11
,
12
]
)
.

For

, there exists a threshold

such that every graph with

M_{\ell}
vertices is

-reconstructible.

Manvel named this “Kelly’s Conjecture” in honor of the final sentence in

[
7

(n-2)
-deck. Manvel noted that Kelly may have expected the statement to

The classical Reconstruction Conjecture is
M_{1}=3
. Lacking a proof of
Conjecture
1.1
for any fixed

, we study threshold numbers of
vertices for
\ell
-reconstructibility of graphs in special classes. The
survey by Kostochka and West
[
8
]
describes prior such results. Here our
aim is to reduce the threshold number of vertices to guarantee

\ell
-reconstructibility of trees.

Reconstruction arguments for special families have two parts, named (when

\ell=1
) by Bondy and Hemminger
[
1
]
. When the

-deck
guarantees that all reconstructions or no reconstructions lie in the specified

\ell
-recognizable
. Separately, using the

family
weakly
\ell
-reconstructible
. Together, the two steps make
graphs in the family
\ell
-reconstructible.

Nýdl
[
14
]
conjectured that trees with at least

vertices are
weakly
\ell
-reconstructible, having presented in
[
13
]
two trees with

2\ell
vertices having the same

-deck, to make the conjecture sharp.
The two trees arise from a path with

vertices by adding one leaf,
adjacent either to the central vertex of the path or to one of its neighbors.
Kostochka and West
[
8
]
used the results of Spinoza and West
[

to

When

-recognizable

[

-vertex acyclic graphs are

-recognizable when

, except

-deck yields the

k
-deck whenever

, so the

-deck also yields the

-deck,
which fixes the number of edges. An

-vertex graph is a tree if and only if

-vertex trees is

-recognizable when

This suggests modifying Nýdl’s conjecture to say that trees with at least

). Indeed, Kelly
[

[

Groenland, Johnston, Scott, and Tan

(

When

For

.
Using reconstruction of rooted trees, Kostochka, Nahvi, West, and

We close this introduction by describing the structure of the paper.
Our proof is constructive; that is, we show how to obtain the unique

n
-vertex tree corresponding to a given
(n-\ell)
-deck
{\mathcal{D}}
.
As noted earlier, the family of
n
-vertex trees is

\ell
-recognizable when
n\geq 2\ell+1

[
10
]
, so we may assume that all
reconstructions from
{\mathcal{D}}
are trees.

We divide the proof into various cases depending on whether the diameter
of the tree is high or low and on whether various special structures are
present in the tree. It is important to note that we must show each case
is recognizable from the deck before we can make use of the hypotheses of
that case in reconstructing the tree.

In Section 2, we develop tools used in various cases. We first show that
several important parameters of
T
are
\ell
-reconstructible, including the
diameter (which we write as
r+1
), a number
k
that is within
1
of the
minimum radius among connected cards, and the number of vertices in
T
from
which three edge-dispoint paths of length
\ell+1
can be grown. We call such
vertices “spi-centers”, because a union of edge-disjoint paths with a common
endpoint is often called a
spider
. We also introduce a counting process
called an Exclusion Argument, which is a technique used repeatedly in later
sections to obtain various subtrees in
T
.

Sections 3–5 discuss the “high-diameter” case, defined by
r\geq n-3\ell
.
In this case,
k\geq\ell+1
, and the cards show all connected subgraphs of
T

with diameter “not too large”. Section 3 completes the case where
T
has a
spi-center, and Sections 4–5 consider the high-diameter trees without
spi-centers. In addition to spi-centers, we consider whether the deck has
a
sparse card
, which is a card containing an
r
-vertex path along
which the card has only one branch vertex. In Section 4, we find the maximal
subtrees of diameter
2k-2
that contain the end portions of every
r
-vertex
path in
T
. In Section 5, we finish reconstructing
T
by assembling these
two subtrees properly and finding the rest of the tree.

In Section 6, we consider the “small-diameter” case, meaning
r<n-3\ell
.
Subcases consider whether
T
has a sparse card or not, whether the branch
vertex along the
r
-vertex path in a sparse card has degree
3
or higher,
and whether
T
has various other cards in which there is a long path from
a leaf to the nearest branch vertex. Note that the existence of various kinds
of cards is immediately recognizable from the deck.

2
Vines and Diameter

In studying
\ell
-reconstructibility of
n
-vertex trees, we use different
methods for trees with large diameter and trees with small diameter. Along
the way, we introduce various structures in trees whose presence or absence
is determined by the
(n-\ell)
-deck, and we use their occurrence or
non-occurrence in resolving various cases.

Definition 2.1
.

In a graph
G
, the
distance
between two vertices is the minimum length
of a path containing them. The
eccentricity
of a vertex in a graph
G

is the maximum of the distances from it to other vertices. A
center
of

G
is a vertex of minimum eccentricity, and the minimum eccentricity is called
the
radius
of
G
. The
diameter
of
G
is the maximum eccentricity,
which is the maximum distance between vertices.

It is an elementary exercise that a tree has one center or two adjacent centers,
when the diameter is even or odd, respectively. The deck of an
n
-vertex tree
has no cards that are paths precisely when the tree has no paths with
n-\ell

vertices. We then know the diameter, because we all paths appear in the cards.
Later in this section we will determine the diameter from the deck also when it
is larger. We will need to count subtrees with various diameters, a technique
we used in
[
10
]
in the more general situation of
n
-vertex graphs
whose
(n-\ell)
-decks have no cards containing cycles.

Definition 2.2
.

A
j
-vine
is a tree with diameter
2j
.
A
j
-evine
is a tree with diameter
2j+1
.
A
j
-center
or
j
-central edge
is the central vertex or edge
in a
j
-vine or
j
-evine, respectively.

Lemma 2.3
.

In a tree
T
, every
j
-vine or
j
-evine
H
lies in a unique maximal

j
-vine or
j
-evine, respectively.

Proof.

The unique maximal such graph containing
H
is the subgraph induced by the set
of all vertices within distance
j
of its central vertex or central edge.
This is a
j
-vine or
j
-evine, respectively. Any other such subgraph
containing it would have to have the same center or central edge, but then it
cannot have any additional vertices.

\Box

For a family
{\mathcal{F}}
of graphs, an
{\mathcal{F}}
-subgraph
of a graph
G
is an
induced subgraph of
G
belonging to
{\mathcal{F}}
. For
F\in{\mathcal{F}}
, let
m(F,G)
be
the number of copies of
F
that are maximal
{\mathcal{F}}
-subgraphs in
G
. The case

\ell=1
of the next lemma is due to Greenwell and Hemminger
[
3
]
.
Similar statements for general
\ell
appear for example in
[
4
]
. We
include a proof for completeness; it is slightly simpler than proofs in the
literature involving inclusion chains.

Lemma 2.4

(
[
10
]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}
be a family of graphs such that every subgraph of
G
belonging to

{\mathcal{F}}
lies in a unique maximal subgraph of
G
belonging to
{\mathcal{F}}
. If for
every
F\in{\mathcal{F}}
with at least
n-\ell
vertices the value of
m(F,G)
is known
from the
(n-\ell)
-deck of
G
, then for all
F\in{\mathcal{F}}
the
(n-\ell)
-deck

{\mathcal{D}}
determines
m(F,G)
.

Proof.

Let
t=\left|V(G)\right|-\left|V(F)\right|
; we use induction on
t
. When
t\leq\ell
, the value

m(F,G)
is given. When
t>\ell
, group the induced subgraphs of
G

isomorphic to
F
according to the unique maximal
{\mathcal{F}}
-subgraph of
G

containing them (as an induced subgraph). Counting all copies of
F
then
yields

s(F,G)=\sum_{H\in{{\mathcal{F}}}}s(F,H)m(H,G).

Since
\left|V(F)\right|<n-\ell
, we know
s(F,G)
from the deck, and we know

s(F,H)
when
F
and
H
are known. By the induction hypothesis, we know all
values of the form
m(H,G)
when
F
is an induced subgraph of
H
except

m(F,G)
. Therefore, we can solve for
m(F,G)
.

\Box

Next we establish consistent notation for our subsequent discussion.

Definition 2.5
.

Always
{\mathcal{D}}
denotes the
(n-\ell)
-deck of an
n
-vertex tree

;
we call
{\mathcal{D}}
simply the

of

. Also

denotes always the
maximum number of vertices in a path in

A

vertices; it appears in a connected card.
We use “csc” for “connected subcard”.

Definition 2.6
.

Fix

to be the largest integer

such that

contains a

j
-evine and every
j
-evine in

has fewer than
n-\ell
vertices.

This fixes

Lemma 2.7
.

The value of

is determined by the deck of

.

Proof.

Each edge forms a
0
-evine (which are the only

-evines), and every

j
-evine with

contains a smaller

-evine, so the value of

is
well-defined. It remains to compute

.

All subgraphs with at most

The deck yields a largest value

such that some csc is a

-evine and all

-evine with fewer than

By the choice of

-evine in

-evine with more than

\Box

Lemma 2.8
.

In a reconstruction

from

, there exist

-vine
in

Proof.

By the definition of

If some

with

. Thus

has a vertex

at distance

from

. Note that

is

, but

\Box

Corollary 2.9
.

For
j\leq k
, the deck
{\mathcal{D}}
determines the numbers of maximal
j
-evines
and
j
-vines with each isomorphism type. All reconstructions from

Fix

By the definition of

\Box

Since

[

Corollary 2.10
.

For

, the degree list of any

-vertex tree is determined by

-deck.

Proof.

All
1
-vines are stars. Each vertex with degree at least
2

exactly one maximal
1
-vine. Suppose first that no star has at least
n-\ell

vertices. For

, by the counting argument (Lemma

) the

-vines having

in any reconstruction.

Now suppose that some star has at least
n-\ell
vertices. Since

,
we see in the deck that there are no

two stars share at most a common leaf or an edge joining the centers. Having

.

Hence only one vertex has degree at least
n-\ell-1
. Its degree is

if and
only if exactly

for

with at least

vertices.
Again Lemma
2.4
applies and we obtain the number of vertices with

.

Since every reconstruction is a tree, the remaining vertices have degree
1
.

\Box

.

Every connected card in
{\mathcal{D}}
has diameter at least

,
and some connected card has diameter at most
2k+3
.

Proof.

A connected card with diameter at most

would be a

For the second claim, let

2k+3
, then

contains a path with

,

-evine has at least

-evine outside a path with

-evine and has diameter

.

\Box

In a tree
T
, the number

.

.

(r-3)/2\geq k\geq(r-\ell-4)/2
. In particular, if

,

.

Proof.

.

.

Let

. By Lemma

Definition 2.14
.

A

A

Corollary 2.15

If

When

Definition 2.17
.

In a tree

from a vertex set

in

Lemma 2.18
.

Let

be a longest path in

, with vertices

. If

with

Since

is a spi-center,

has an offshoot from

at

with length
at least
\ell+1
. Since

is a longest path,

.

If a longest path

diverges from

between

and

We will often consider offshoots from a longest path

.
The union of a vertex

on

together with the offshoots from

at

is a rooted tree with

that we use in various situations to

of a tree being reconstructed.

Lemma 2.19

(The Exclusion Argument)
.

For a rooted tree

with root
z
, the
z
-offshoots are the
components of the graph

, rooted at the neighbors of

-offshoots of

-offshoots is determined by the multiset

of all rooted subtrees
obtained from individual

-offshoots.

Proof.

The

-offshoots are determined in nonincreasing order of number of vertices.
The largest

-offshoots are given initially. Having determined all

-offshoots with more than
p
vertices, let

be a rooted tree with
p

vertices in
\cal M
. Since we know all

-offshoots of

larger than

,

in
\cal M
arise from larger

-offshoots by

as a

-offshoot
is obtained from the number of copies of

in

by excluding those
that arise from larger

-offshoots.

\Box

6

In this section we consider trees with small diameter, in particular
with
r\leq n-3\ell-1
. First we consider those having sparse cards and then
those not having sparse cards. When
r\leq n-3\ell-1
, any connected card has
at least
2\ell+1
vertices outside any path.

We note first a case that we have already handled.

Corollary 6.1
.

If

and
r\leq n-\ell
and

contains a sparse card
that is a
3
-legged spider, then

is
\ell
-reconstructible.

Proof.

A
3
-legged spider has at most
(3r-1)/2
vertices. Such a tree as a card
requires
(3r-1)/2\geq n-\ell\geq 5\ell+5
, so
r\geq(10\ell+11)/3
. In

. By Lemma

,

has exactly one

-reconstructible.

\Box

Henceforth in this section we may assume that
T
has no sparse card that
is a
3
-legged spider.
Since all longest paths in a tree have the same center, the central vertices of
all long cards are the same. An
optimal
sparse card is a sparse card
such that, among all sparse cards, the primary vertex is closest to the center.

Suppose

Since all longest paths in a tree contain the center, when

Let

to

plus one offshoot from the central vertex

of degree

Hence we may assume that
{\hat{C}}

If

Hence we may assume

Lemma 6.10
.

Suppose
n\geq 6\ell+11
and
r\leq n-3\ell-1
. If
T
has a sparse card,
then
T
is
\ell
-reconstructible.

Proof.

As noted in Lemma
6.8
, we may assume that
T
has no sparse
card of degree 3 and every sparse card is optimal, with the same primary vertex

z
and primary value
j
. Since all sparse cards are visible in the deck, we
recognize such

.

Among all sparse cards, choose
C
and its
r
-path
P
to maximize the length

t
of a longest offshoot from
P
at the primary vertex, and within that
maximize the number of vertices in a largest offshoot of length
t
. We use
notation relative to

as in Definition
6.4
. Note that

.
Since we see all sparse cards, we know

.

Suppose first that

, which requires

.
By Lemma
6.8
,

has no long-legged card, which in the case

has no paddle.
Hence Lemma
6.9
applies to show that

is
\ell
-reconstructible.

Hence we may assume

.
Consider a largest subtree obtained as
C_{0}^{\prime}
in Lemma
6.6
(b), so

fits in a card, with

, and

shows us the subtree
T_{2}
in

C_{1}-z
containing
u_{j+1}
(we see both

and

in two such subtrees if

and they have the same size).

Case 1:

.
In this case

also shows us

, by Lemma
6.6
(b).
We now know all of

except some of the offshoots from
P
at
z
. We know
the total number
s
of vertices in offshoots from
P
at
z
, and we know
their maximum length,

.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
be the family of subtrees consisting of an
r
-path

\langle u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}\rangle
and one offshoot at

. Since

has no sparse card of

3
, all members of
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
have fewer than

vertices. Since we
know

and

, we know the number of peripheral vertices in those

(since

).
Since we know

, we also know all the members of
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
in which the

arises from

. In the remaining members,

and the

r
-path comes from

. Hence largest remaining members of

show us offshoots from

at

in
T
, seen in full. Excluding subtrees
from such offshoots with each smaller size, the exclusion argument now yields
all the offshoots from
P
at

in

, which completes the reconstruction of

.

Case 2:

t=j-1
with
j<(r+1)/2
.

We have noted that
C_{0}^{\prime}
satisfies
u_{j}=z
and shows us
T_{2}
.
The path
\langle u_{1},\ldots,u_{j-1}\rangle
in

may come from

or from

.

First we will use an exclusion argument to find all offshoots from
z
in
T

with length

. Let
{\mathcal{C}}_{3}
be the family of long subtrees that have
exactly one offshoot of length
j-1
from the vertex
u_{j}
in position
j
on
a specified
r
-path (and no other offshoot at
u_{j}
). Since
T
has no sparse
card of degree
3
, all members of
{\mathcal{C}}_{3}
fit in cards, and we see them.
Since we know
T_{2}
and the number of
r
-paths in

, and since by

every

-path has exactly one endpoint in

, we know
the number of peripheral vertices not in

. We can exclude (with

{\mathcal{C}}_{3}
in which

and the offshoot come from

(in particular, when

). In the remaining members of

, we have

from

Knowing

, the largest such offshoots (with multiplicity), and the number

Lemma 6.11
.

Suppose
n\geq 6\ell+11
and
r\leq n-3\ell-1
.
If
T
has no sparse card, then
T
is
\ell
-reconstructible.

Proof.

From the deck we recognize that
T
has no sparse card.
Recall that a paddle is a long card having a leg of length at least
r/2
.

Suppose first that
T
has no paddle. If
r
is odd, then Lemma
6.9

applies and
T
is
\ell
-reconstructible. If
r
is even, then let
C^{\prime}
be a
largest long subtree among those having a leg of length at least
r/2
. Since

T
has no paddle,
C^{\prime}
fits in a card and shows in full one branch of
T

obtained by deleting the central edge; call it
T^{\prime}
. If there are distinct
choices for
C^{\prime}
, then the two branches have the same size and we see them.
Otherwise, knowing
T^{\prime}
and the total number of
r
-paths gives the number of
peripheral vertices outside
T^{\prime}
, which with the number of copies of
C^{\prime}
tells
us whether the two branches are isomorphic. If the branches are not
isomorphic, then they do not have the same size, and we know the number
n^{\prime}
of
vertices in the smaller branch. Among the long cscs with one branch being a
path and the other having
n^{\prime}
vertices, we can exclude those whose non-path
branch comes from
T^{\prime}
. A largest remaining such csc shows the other branch,
completing the reconstruction.

Hence we may assume that
T
has a paddle. Among paddles whose long leg has
maximum length, choose
C^{*}
to maximize the length of a longest offshoot
from the
r
-path
P
at the branch vertex
z
at the end of the leg. Within
this, choose
C^{*}
to maximize the total number of
vertices in the offshoots from
P
at
z
. With
P=\langle v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}\rangle
, define
j

by
z=v_{j}
with
j<(r+1)/2
(the long leg implies
j\neq(r+1)/2
).
Although we have no sparse card, we still use “
z
” and “
j
” here, because
the items so designated in this argument play roles analogous to those played
by the items with those names earlier in this section.

Let
Q
be the union of the offshoots in
T
from
P
at
z
. Let
T_{1}
and

T_{2}
be the components of
T-z
containing
v_{j-1}
and
v_{j+1}
,
respectively. Since
T_{2}
contributes only a path to
C^{*}
and
T
has no
sparse card,
T_{1}
cannot contribute only a path
C^{*}
. Hence
C^{*}
shows us

Q
in full, since otherwise we could delete a vertex of
T_{1}
from
C^{*}
and
enlarge another offshoot. Note that
T_{1}
is a smallest offshoot from

T_{2}
at
z
among those with maximum length.

If we can determine
T_{2}
, then finding
T_{1}
will complete the reconstruction.
To see that, let
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
be the family of long subtrees having a leg of length
at least
r-j+1
. By the choice of
C^{*}
as a card with longest leg, every
member of
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
has fewer than
n-\ell
vertices, so we know all these
subtrees. Knowing
T_{2}
and
Q
, we can compute the number of peripheral
vertices in
T_{1}
(whether or not
Q
has length
j-1
). We can then exclude
from
{\mathcal{C}}_{4}
all members where the portion obtained by deleting the leg comes
from
T_{2}
or
Q
. A largest remaining member of
{\mathcal{C}}_{4}
shows us
T_{1}
.
Hence it suffices to find
T_{2}
.

Case 1:

Q
has in total at least
\ell+1
vertices.

Let
C_{2}
be a largest subtree containing an
r
-vertex path
P^{\prime}
with vertices

u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}
such that
\langle u_{1},\ldots,u_{j}\rangle
is a leg of
C_{2}
and the offshoots from

P^{\prime}
at
u_{j}
in
C_{2}
form a specified rooted subgraph of
Q
with exactly

\ell+1
vertices. There is such a subtree with
u_{j}=z
. Since
C^{*}
is a
card, there are at most
\ell
vertices in offshoots from any path of length

r-j
in
T_{2}
. Hence the
\ell+1
vertices from
Q
guarantee that
u_{j}
does
not lie in
T_{2}
. Also
u_{j}
cannot lie in

, since
C_{2}
has two
paths of length at least
j-1
from
u_{j}
. Hence
u_{j}
must equal
z
.

Since
u_{j}=z
and there is only one offshoot from
z
with length
r-j
,
the path
\langle u_{j+1},\ldots,u_{r}\rangle
in
C_{2}
must lie in
T_{2}
.
In
C_{2}
, there are exactly
j+\ell
vertices from

From

, we know that

has at most
r-j+\ell

Hence

has at most

vertices and fits in a card.
We see it as a csc, and its long offshoot from

is

.

Case 2:

Q
has in total at most
\ell
vertices.

As remarked earlier, we see
Q
in full in
C^{*}
.
Altogether there are at least
3\ell+1
vertices outside

in
T
.
Since
C^{*}
is a card, there are at most
\ell
vertices outside
P
in
T_{2}
,
and
Q
has at most
\ell
vertices. Hence
\left|V(T_{1})\right|\geq j+\ell
.

Let

be a largest subtree having an
r
-vertex path

with vertices

\langle u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}\rangle
such that

has degree

and the offshoots from

\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{j-1}\}

\ell+1
vertices. Since

Since

has at most
\ell
