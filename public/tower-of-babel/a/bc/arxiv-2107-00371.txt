Sparse GCA and Thresholded Gradient Descent
Sheng Gao and Zongming Ma

Abstract
Generalized correlation analysis (GCA) is concerned with uncovering linear relationships across multiple datasets. It generalizes canonical correlation analysis that is designed for two datasets. We study sparse GCA when there are potentially multiple leading generalized correlation tuples in data that are of interest and the loading matrix has a small number of nonzero rows. It includes sparse CCA and sparse PCA of correlation matrices as special cases. We first formulate sparse GCA as a generalized eigenvalue problem at both population and sample levels via a careful choice of normalization constraints. Based on a Lagrangian form of the sample optimization problem, we propose a thresholded gradient descent algorithm for estimating GCA loading vectors and matrices in high dimensions. We derive tight estimation error bounds for estimators generated by the algorithm with proper initialization. We also demonstrate the prowess of the algorithm on a number of synthetic datasets.

Introduction

With the advent of big data acquisition technology,
it has become increasingly important to integrate information across
multiple datasets collected on a common set of subjects.
Canonical correlation analysis (CCA), first proposed by
Hotelling 1992
, is a widely used statistical tool to integrate information from two datasets: It seeks linear combinations of variables within each dataset such that their correlation is maximized.

However, recent advances in
fields such as multi-omics and multimodal brain imaging have presented us with new challenges, since scientists are often able to collect more than two datasets on the same set of subjects nowadays.
To tackle these challenges, we turn to
a useful generalization of CCA called
generalized correlation analysis
(GCA)
[
24
]
which
aims to explore linear relationships across multiple data sources.

Kettenring 1971
proposed five different variants for generalized correlation analysis of multiple datasets,
where
different methods correspond to maximization of different objective functions of covariances and correlations,
subject to certain normalization constraints.

Tenenhaus and Tenenhaus 2011
extended
these approaches
to regularized versions by adding
l_{2}
penalties to the objective functions and proposed partial least squares algorithms for solving them.
In addition to unsupervised settings, it has been shown that GCA can be incorporated into supervised learning settings to improve efficiency and accuracy
[
27
]
.

Suppose that the
k
datasets are i.i.d. realizations of

k
random vectors
{X_{\{1\}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}},{X_{\{2\}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}},...,{X_{\{k\}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{k}}
.
Here and after, we use subscript
\{j\}
to denote the
j
th set of features and/or the
j
th dataset.
At the population level,
we propose to seek vectors
{a_{\{1\}}},{a_{\{2\}}},...,.{a_{\{k\}}}

(called
the first
k
-tuple of generalized loading vectors
) that solve

\displaystyle\underset{{l_{\{1\}},\dots,l_{\{k\}}}}{\text{maximize}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\text{cov}({l_{\{i\}}^{\top}}{X_{\{i\}}},{l_{\{j\}}^{\top}}{X_{\{j\}}})

(1)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\text{var}({l_{\{i\}}^{\top}}{X_{\{i\}}})=1.

An important difference between the foregoing formulation and those in
Kettenring 1971
is the normalization constraint.
We propose to normalize the sum of the variances of the linear combinations while
Kettenring 1971

requires
the individual variance of each linear combination to be one.
When
k=2
, the two normalizations essentially lead to the same solution.
One can show that the optimal solution to (
1
) necessarily has
\text{var}({a_{\{i\}}^{\top}X_{\{i\}}})=\frac{1}{2}
for
i=1
and
2
, and hence the optimal
{a_{\{i\}}}
’s are proportional to those from normalizing individual variances.
However, when
k\geq 3
, the solutions are usually different and we argue that
formulation
(
1
) is more appropriate for two reasons.
First, the normalization in (
1
) conforms with the “null principle”
[
12
]
in that if all linear combinations of the
X_{i}
’s are uncorrelated then the objective function reduces to the lefthand side of the normalization constraint.
In addition, it is more resilient to spurious solutions as illustrated by the following example.

Example 1.1
.

Suppose
k=3
and
p_{1}=p_{2}=p_{3}=m
.
Let
X_{\{1\}}=Yv_{\{1\}}+Z_{\{1\}}
,

X_{\{2\}}=Yv_{\{2\}}+Z_{\{2\}}
and
X_{\{3\}}=Z_{\{3\}}
where
v_{\{1\}}
and
v_{\{2\}}
are two deterministic unit vectors in

\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

Y\sim N(0,1)
,

Z_{\{i\}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}N_{m}(0,I_{m})
,
and they are mutually independent.
Simple linear algebra shows that the optimal solution to (
1
) is

a_{\{1\}}=\frac{1}{2}v_{\{1\}}
,

a_{\{2\}}=\frac{1}{2}v_{\{2\}}
and

a_{\{3\}}=0
.
In contrast, with the individual normalization constraint

\mathrm{var}(l_{\{i\}}^{\top}X_{\{i\}})=1
, the optimal solution would change to
a_{\{1\}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}v_{\{1\}}
,

a_{\{2\}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}v_{\{2\}}
and

a_{\{3\}}
is any vector in
\mathbb{B}^{m-1}

where
\mathbb{B}^{m-1}
is the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Comparing the solutions to two different normalizations, formulation (
1
) is advantageous in that it provides a meaningful and unique optimal
a_{\{3\}}
.

The solution to (
1
) gives the first
k
-tuple of leading GCA loading vectors.
More generally, we want to extract successive
k
-tuples of leading loading vectors subject to certain additional
constraints.
In view of (
1
), suppose we have found
({a_{\{1\}}^{(m)}},\dots,{a_{\{k\}}^{(m)}})
,
for
m=1,\dots,r-1
, as the first
(r-1)

k
-tuples of leading loading vectors.
We define the
r
th
k
-tuple
({a_{\{1\}}^{(r)}},\dots,{a_{\{k\}}^{(r)}})
as the solution to (
1
) with the following additional constraints:

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\text{cov}(({l_{\{i\}}^{(m)}})^{\top}X_{\{i\}},{l_{\{i\}}^{\top}}X_{\{i\}})=0,\qquad m=1,\dots,r-1.

To be more concise, let the columns of
L_{\{i\}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{i}\times r}

be the successive leading loading vectors for
X_{\{i\}}
for
i=1,\dots,k
.
The foregoing proposal for finding the first
r

leading
k
-tuples then reduces to the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\underset{L_{\{1\}},\dots,L_{\{k\}}}{\text{maximize}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\Tr({L_{\{i\}}^{\top}\Sigma_{\{ij\}}L_{\{j\}}})

(2)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}{L_{\{i\}}^{\top}\Sigma_{\{ii\}}L_{\{i\}}}=I_{r}.

Here
\Sigma_{\{ii\}}

denotes the covariance matrix of
X_{\{i\}}
and
\Sigma_{{\{ij\}}}=\text{cov}(X_{\{i\}},X_{\{j\}})
for
i\neq j
.
To write (
2
) more concisely, we define

L=[{L_{\{1\}}^{\top}},\dots,{L_{\{k\}}^{\top}}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}

where
p=\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}
,

\Sigma_{0}=\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma_{\{11\}},\dots,\Sigma_{\{kk\}})\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, and
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
where its
(i,j)
th block is
\Sigma_{\{ij\}}
and
i
th diagonal block is
\Sigma_{\{ii\}}
.
Then we can rewrite (
2
) as

\displaystyle\underset{L}{\text{maximize}}

\displaystyle\Tr(L^{\top}\Sigma L)

(3)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle L^{\top}\Sigma_{0}L=I_{r}.

In what follows,
A=[{A_{\{1\}}^{\top}},\dots,{A_{\{k\}}^{\top}}]^{\top}
denotes the solution to (
3
) where each
A_{\{i\}}\in\mathrm{R}^{p_{i}\times r}
and the
m
th column of
A_{\{i\}}
is
a^{(m)}_{\{i\}}
for
i=1,\dots,k
and
m=1,\dots,r
.

Informed readers might have already realized that (
3
) is closely connected to a generalized eigenvalue problem

|\Sigma-\lambda\Sigma_{0}|=0
,
where
|M|
stands for the determinant of a square matrix
M
.
In addition, when
\Sigma_{0}
is diagonal (i.e., when
k=p
), the problem is equivalent to principal component analysis (PCA) of the correlation matrix:
For any non-singular
\Sigma_{0}
, the columns of
\Sigma_{0}^{1/2}A
are the leading eigenvectors of the correlation matrix
\Sigma_{0}^{-1/2}\Sigma\Sigma_{0}^{-1/2}
.
When
k=2
, the solution to (
3
) is equivalent to CCA up to scaling.
Thus, program (
3
) provides a
unified
formulation for extracting leading linear covariation within one or across multiple datasets.

In practice,
one does not have direct knowledge of the joint sample covariance matrix
\Sigma
or even its block diagonal part
\Sigma_{0}
.
The natural choice is to replace them with their sample counterparts.
In potential modern applications of GCA, the dataset dimensions (i.e., the
p_{i}
’s) can be much larger than the sample size
n
.
Hence, one suffers from the curse of dimensionality if no further structural assumption is made
[
13
,
23
,
3
,
4
]
.
Due to its interpretability and practicality, a structural assumption
that has been widely adopted in both theory and practice
is
sparsity
: Most energy of the solution to (
1
)–(
3
) concentrates on a small number of entries
[
20
,
36
]
.
Let
A
denote the solution to (
3
).
In this manuscript, we adopt the assumption that at most
s
rows of
A
contain nonzero entries.
In other words, the target of estimation is also the solution to the following
sparse generalized correlation analysis
(Sparse GCA, or SGCA) problem:

\displaystyle\underset{L}{\text{maximize}}

\displaystyle\Tr(L^{\top}\Sigma L)

(4)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle L^{\top}\Sigma_{0}L=I_{r}\quad\|L\|_{2,0}\leq s.

Here and after, for any matrix
L
,
\|L\|_{2,0}
counts the number of nonzero rows in
L
.
In view of the discussion following (
3
), when
k=p
and
k=2
, (
4
) reduces to sparse PCA of correlation matrix and sparse CCA, respectively.

1.1
Main contributions

The main contributions of the present manuscript are the following.

First, we clarify the target of estimation in generalized correlation analysis (i.e., the solution
A
to (
3
)) by considering a natural latent variable model in which an
r
-dimensional latent variable drives the covariation of
k
random vectors.
Under mild conditions,
we show that the linear subspace spanned by the leading
r
generalized eigenvectors in our GCA formulation (i.e., the columns of
A
) coincides with the subspace spanned by a concrete functional of parameters in the latent variable model.
In addition, we characterize the behavior of generalized eigenvalues under the latent variable model.

Next, for sample sparse GCA, we propose a thresholded gradient descent algorithm for solving a Lagrangian version of the sample counterpart of (
4
).
The algorithm is intuitive and easy to implement.
In view of the discussion following (
3
), such an algorithm provides a unified approach to a number of different sparse unsupervised learning problems, including sparse PCA of correlation matrices and sparse CCA.

Furthermore, we provide a theoretical analysis of the thresholded gradient descent algorithm.
We show that with high probability, when initialized properly, estimation errors of the intermediate results after each iteration converge at a geometric rate until they arrive in a tight neighborhood of the population solution to (
4
).
Statistically, we establish tight estimation error bounds for the output of our algorithm as an estimator.
Numerically, this implies geometric convergence of our algorithm to such an estimator.
Finally, these theoretical findings are corroborated by numerical studies on simulated datasets.

Related works

In view of the discussion following (
3
), the present paper is closely connected to the sparse PCA and sparse CCA literature.
To date, there is a large literature devoted to various aspects of the sparse PCA problem, including algorithms
[
42
,
22
,
1
,
39
,
25
,
41
,
35
,
37
]
, information-theoretic limits
[
6
,
7
,
34
]
and computational theoretic limits
[
2
,
17
]
.
However, this literature has mostly focused on sparse PCA of covariance matrices.
Thus, the special case of
k=p
in our setting complements the existing literature by providing both theory and method for sparse PCA of correlation matrices.

In the case of
k=2
, the theory and method in this paper specialize to the sparse CCA setting
[
8
,
15
,
17
]
.
In this case,
we provide
a new iterative algorithm for sparse CCA that achieves optimal estimation rates derived in
Gao et al. 2015a
.
Therefore, for this special case, the present manuscript provides a competitive alternative to existing sparse CCA methods.

When
r=1
, (
4
) reduces to the population version of the sparse generalized eigenvalue problem considered in
Tan et al. 2018
.

Tan et al. 2018
proposed a truncated Rayleigh flow method for estimating the population solution in this special case, and established its rates of convergence.
It is not clear how their method can be generalized to estimate successive generalized eigenvectors or eigenspaces.
In contrast, our estimator is based on a different algorithm (thresholded gradient descent of a Lagrangian objective function).
It not only achieves fast converging estimation rates for estimating the first generalized eigenvector but also works for estimating leading generalized eigenspaces of fixed dimensions that are greater than one.

1.3
Organization of the paper

The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
In section 2 we examine the generalized eigenvalue problem underpinning sparse GCA under a latent variable model.
Section 3 proposes a thresholded gradient descent algorithm and its initialization via generalized Fantope projection.
Section 4 establishes the convergence rate of our algorithm under reasonable initialization.
Numerical results are presented in Section 5.
Technical proofs are deferred to appendices.

2
A Latent Variable Model

In this part, we aim to identify the solutions to (
3
) as functionals of parameters in the joint distribution of

({X_{\{1\}}^{\top}},\dots,{X_{\{k\}}^{\top}})^{\top}
.
To this end, we introduce an intuitive latent variable model.

The fundamental assumption underlying generalized correlation analysis is that there exists a shared low-dimensional latent variable which orchestrates the (linear) covariation of observed features across all datasets.
Let
z
be an
r
-dimensional latent variable.
The following latent variable model describes an idealized data generating process:

\displaystyle X_{\{i\}}

\displaystyle=U_{\{i\}}z+e_{\{i\}},\quad i=1,\dots,k,

(5)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\sim N_{r}(0,I_{r}),\quad e_{\{i\}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle ind}}{{\sim}}N_{p_{i}}(0,\Psi_{\{ii\}}).

Here the deterministic matrix
U_{\{i\}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{i}\times r}
for
i=1,\dots,k
,
\Psi_{\{ii\}}

is positive definite, and the latent variable
z
and the idiosyncratic noises
\{e_{\{i\}}\}_{i=1}^{k}
are mutually independent.
Under the foregoing latent variable model,
the joint covariance matrix of

({X_{\{1\}}^{\top}},\dots,{X_{\{k\}}^{\top}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is given by
\Sigma
with the
i
th diagonal block

\Sigma_{\{ii\}}=U_{\{i\}}U_{\{i\}}^{\top}+\Psi_{\{ii\}},\quad\mbox{for $i=1,\dots,k$,}

(6)

and the
(i,j)
th block

\Sigma_{\{ij\}}={U_{\{i\}}}{U_{\{j\}}^{\top}},\quad\mbox{for $1\leq i\neq j\leq k$}.

(7)

We denote by
\Psi
the block diagonal matrix with blocks
{\Psi_{\{ii\}}}
,
i=1,\dots,k
,
on the diagonal.
We also let
U=[{U_{\{1\}}^{\top}},{U_{\{2\}}^{\top}},...,{U_{\{k\}}^{\top}}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
.
In the rest of this section,
we assume that the observed datasets are generated by model (
5
).
In addition, we let

\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{p}\geq 0

(8)

denote the population generalized eigenvalues of
\Sigma
with respect to
\Sigma_{0}=\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma_{\{11\}},\dots,\Sigma_{\{kk\}})
.

The following key lemma identifies the connection between parameters in model (
5
) and the solution to (
3
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that
\Sigma
and
\Sigma_{0}
are specified by model (
5
)–(
7
).
Let
A=[{A_{\{1\}}^{\top}},\dots,{A_{\{k\}}^{\top}}]^{\top}
be the solution to (
3
).
If the
r
th generalized eigenvalue of
\Sigma
w.r.t.
\Sigma_{0}
is larger than
1
, i.e.,
\lambda_{r}>1
,
then
\mathrm{Col}(A_{\{i\}})\subset\mathrm{Col}({\Sigma_{\{ii\}}^{-1}}U_{\{i\}})
for all
i\in[k]
.
For any
i
, if further
{\mathrm{rank}}(A_{\{i\}})=r
, then

\mathrm{Col}(A_{\{i\}})=\mathrm{Col}({\Sigma_{\{ii\}}^{-1}}U_{\{i\}})
.

The next lemma describes the behavior of population generalized eigenvalues
under latent variable model (
5
).
It also identifies a sufficient condition for
\lambda_{r}>1
.
To this end, we start with
an assumption motivated by

[
14
]
.

Assumption 2.2
.

In latent variable model (
5
)–(
7
),
we assume the following:

•

The matrix
U
has full column rank, that is,
{\mathrm{rank}}(U)=r
;

•

\Sigma_{0}
and
U
satisfy
\sigma_{r}(\Sigma_{0}^{-1/2}U)\geq 1
.

Lemma 2.3
.

In model (
5
)–(
7
),
under Assumption
2.2
,
we have

r=\max\{j:\lambda_{j}>1,j\in[p]\}.

(9)

In other words, there are exactly

generalized eigenvalues greater than 1.
Moreover, define

Y=[{U_{\{1\}}^{\dagger}},{U_{\{2\}}^{\dagger}},\dots,{U_{\{k\}}^{\dagger}}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}

where
{U_{\{i\}}^{\dagger}}
is the

.
Then the multiplicity of

as a generalized eigenvalue is

\#\{j:\lambda_{j}=1\}=p-\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\mathrm{rank}}(U_{\{i\}})+r-{\mathrm{rank}}(U-UY^{\top}U).

(10)

The foregoing lemma guarantees an eigengap between the

th and the
(r+1)
th generalized eigenvalues, and so the leading rank

By the foregoing lemma, under model (
5

\Sigma=\Sigma_{0}K{\Lambda}K^{\top}\Sigma_{0}=\Sigma_{0}A\Lambda_{r}A^{\top}\Sigma_{0}+\Sigma_{0}B{\overline{\Lambda}}B^{\top}\Sigma_{0}.

Here

On the other hand, the decomposition (

Remark 2.4

In the case when

Remark 2.5
.

When

,
\Sigma_{0}
becomes a diagonal matrix where the diagonal entries are variances of the variables.
The problem (
3

Let

be the matrix containing the eigenvectors that span the leading

If

is the solution to (

\Sigma_{0}^{1/2}A
coincides with

(up to an

Thus, we essentially estimate the leading eigenspace of the correlation matrix.

3
Gradient Descent with Hard Thresholding

In this section, we present a thresholded gradient descent algorithm for simultaneously finding multiple leading sparse generalized eigenvectors.

3.1
Motivation

The sample counterpart of (
4
) can be recast as the following minimization problem:

\displaystyle\underset{L}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle-\langle\widehat{\Sigma},LL^{\top}\rangle

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle L^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}L=I_{r},\quad\|L\|_{2,0}\leq s.

Here
\widehat{\Sigma}
is the joint sample covariance matrix of all
k
sets of features.
We propose to solve its Lagrangian version:

\underset{{L\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}}}{\text{minimize}}\quad f(L)\quad\quad\quad\text{subject to}\quad\|L\|_{2,0}\leq s,

(12)

where the objective function
f(L)
is

f(L)=-\langle\widehat{\Sigma},LL^{\top}\rangle+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|L^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}L-I_{r}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.

(13)

Effectively, minimizing the first term in (
13
) maximizes the original objective function
whereas minimizing the second term controls the deviation from the normalization constraint in (
3
).
Here
\lambda
is a tuning parameter.
Intuitively, the larger it is, the more penalty we put on deviating from the normalization constraint and the closer
L^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}L
is to
I_{r}
.

To deal with the constraint in (
12
), we shall perform the following hard thresholding.

Definition 3.1
.

Given a matrix
U
and a natural number
k
,
we define the output of hard thresholding function
HT(U,k)
to be the matrix obtained by keeping the
k
rows with the largest
l_{2}
norms and replacing all other rows with zeros,
that is

HT(U,k)_{i*}=\begin{cases}U_{i*},&\text{if }i\in C_{k}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

where
C_{k}

is the index set of
k
rows of
U
with largest
l_{2}
norms.
When there is a tie, we always pick the smaller/smallest index.

3.2
Algorithm

Let
X^{(1)},X^{(2)}...,X^{(n)}
be i.i.d. observations generated from the latent variable model with covariance matrix
\Sigma
and its block diagonal part
\Sigma_{0}
.
Let
\widehat{\Sigma}
and
\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}
be the sample covariance matrix and its block diagonal part computed on
\{X^{(1)},X^{(2)}...,X^{(n)}\}
.

Algorithm
1
describes the proposed thresholded gradient descent procedure.
Given a proper initial estimator which we shall specify later, each iteration first performs a step of gradient descent on
f
defined in (
12
), then it keeps the
s^{\prime}
rows with the largest
l_{2}
norms and thresholds the remaining rows to zero.
By iteratively performing these two steps, the algorithm can be viewed as heuristics for solving the non-convex optimization problem (
4
).
Here
s^{\prime}
is a user-specified tuning parameter and is not necessarily equal to the true sparsity level
s
.
Step 2 in Algorithm
1
is a re-normalization step.
This is due to the fact that the stationary point of the objective function
f(L)
is not the same as the solution to the original optimization problem (
4
).
Thus, we first transform it for iterations and eventually transform back in Step 7 to define the final estimator.

Algorithm 1

Thresholded gradient descent for sparse GCA

Input
: Covariance matrix estimator
{\widehat{\Sigma}}
and its block diagonal part
{\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}}
; Initialization
\widehat{A}_{0}
.

Tuning Parameters
:
Step size
\eta
; Penalty
\lambda
; Sparsity level
s^{\prime}
; Number of iterations
T
;

1:

\widetilde{A}_{0}\leftarrow\widehat{A}_{0}(\widehat{A}_{0}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}\widehat{A}_{0})^{-1/2}

2:

\overline{V}_{1}\leftarrow\widetilde{A}_{0}(I+\frac{1}{\lambda}\widetilde{A}_{0}^{\top}{\widehat{\Sigma}}\widetilde{A}_{0})^{1/2}

3:

for

t=1,2,3,...,T

do

4:

V_{t+1}\leftarrow\overline{V}_{t}-\eta\nabla f(\overline{V}_{t})=\overline{V}_{t}-2\eta(-{\widehat{\Sigma}}\overline{V}_{t}+\lambda{\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}}\overline{V}_{t}(\overline{V}_{t}^{\top}{\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}}\overline{V}_{t}-I_{r}))

5:

\overline{V}_{t+1}\leftarrow HT(V_{t+1},s^{\prime})

Output
:
\widehat{A}_{T}=\overline{V}_{T}(\overline{V}_{T}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}\overline{V}_{T})^{-1/2}

In Algorithm
1
, each iteration is computationally efficient: line 4 is essentially matrix multiplication and addition, and line 5 requires calculating and sorting row
l_{2}
norms. Since
\widehat{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X^{(i)}-{\bar{X}})(X^{(i)}-{\bar{X}})^{\top}
where
{\bar{X}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X^{(i)}
, an efficient way to calculate
\widehat{\Sigma}\overline{V}_{t}
would be to calculate

Y_{t}^{(i)}=(X^{(i)}-{\bar{X}})^{\top}\overline{V}_{t}
first, followed by multiplying
(X^{(i)}-{\bar{X}})Y_{t}^{(i)}
,
resulting in
O(npr)
flops. Line 5 requires
O(rp+p\log p)
flops for selecting top
s^{\prime}
rows.
Hence a single iteration of thresholded gradient descent will require

O(npr+p\log p)
flops in total.

3.3
Initialization via generalized Fantope projection

Success of Algorithm
1
depends crucially on the quality of the initial estimator
\widehat{A}_{0}
.
Thus, we need to find an initial estimator that is relatively close to the true generalized eigenspace in some distance so that later iterations could further improve on estimation accuracy.
We now introduce such an initial estimator based on Fantope projection
[
35
]
.

Note that

\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}\Tr(A_{\{i\}}^{\top}\Sigma_{\{ij\}}A_{\{j\}})=\langle\Sigma,AA^{\top}\rangle.

The idea is to “lift”
AA^{\top}
into
\mathbb{S}^{p}
, the space of
p\times p
symmetric matrices, and hence to treat it as a single quantity.
Since we assume that
A
is row sparse,
F=AA^{\top}
have at most
s^{2}
nonzero entries which is much smaller than its number of elements.
To ensure sparsity of its solution,
we impose an entrywise
l_{1}
penalty to define the following objective function for initialization:

\underset{F\in\mathbb{S}^{p}}{\text{minimize}}~~-\langle{\widehat{\Sigma}},F\rangle+\rho\|F\|_{1}.

On the other hand, the normalization constraint on
A
implies that

({\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}})^{1/2}F({\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}})^{1/2}\in\mathcal{P}(p,r).

where

\mathcal{P}(p,r)
is defined to be the set of rank
r
projection matrices

\mathcal{P}(p,r)=\{PP^{\top},P\in\mathcal{O}(p,r)\}.

However, this direct generalization leads to a nonconvex feasible set since
\mathcal{P}(p,r)
is nonconvex.
To obtain a bona fide convex program,
in the light of
[
35
]
, we use the Fantope set introduced by
[
11
]
:

\mathcal{F}_{r}=\{X:0\preceq X\preceq I\quad\text{and}\quad\Tr{(X)}=r\}.

The motivation is the observation from
[
26
]
that

\mathcal{F}_{r}=\text{conv}(\mathcal{P}(p,r))
,
where
\text{conv}(A)
denotes the convex hull of
A.

To summarize, our initial estimator is the solution to the following program:

\displaystyle\underset{F\in\mathbb{S}^{p}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle-\langle{\widehat{\Sigma}},F\rangle+\rho\|F\|_{1},

(14)

\displaystyle\mbox{subject to}

\displaystyle({\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}})^{1/2}F({\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}})^{1/2}\in\mathcal{F}_{r}.

Upon obtaining
\widehat{F}
as the solution to (
14
), we collect the leading
r
eigenvectors of
\widehat{F}
as
\widetilde{U}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
and the corresponding leading
r
eigenvalues as entries of the diagonal matrix
\widetilde{D}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
.
Then let
A_{0}=\widetilde{U}_{r}\widetilde{D}_{r}^{1/2}
and

\widehat{A}_{0}=HT(A_{0},s^{\prime}).

(15)

This finishes initialization for Algorithm
1
.
The initialization procedure has
O(p^{3})
computation complexity due to the ADMM step involved in solving the generalized Fantope projection. See
[
17
]
for details of the ADMM algorithm.

As we shall show in next section,

\widehat{F}

suffers a relatively large estimation error rate for estimating
AA^{\top}
and hence
\widehat{A}_{0}
for
A
.
However, for Algorithm
1
to work, such an estimator serves well as an initial estimator under mild conditions.
In addition to PCA and the current setting, an analogous initialization via convex relaxation idea has appeared in
[
17
]
for performing sparse CCA which is asymmetric.

4
Theoretical Results

We provide theoretical justifications for our algorithms in this section.
We first state our main result on how each iteration of Algorithm
1
improves estimation accuracy,
followed by a corollary on error bounds achieved by the final estimator.
Analysis of generalized Fantope initialization follows our investigation of the main algorithm.
In addition, we include a lower bound for the finite
k
setting at the end of this section.

Parameter space

Under
covariance structure (
11
),
we define

\mathcal{F}(\{s_{i}\}_{1}^{k},\{p_{i}\}_{1}^{k},r,\{\lambda_{j}\}_{1}^{p};\nu)

as the collection of all covariance matrices
\Sigma
satisfying (
11
) and the following conditions:

\displaystyle\text{(i) Sparsity: $A_{\{i\}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{i}\times r}$ with $\|A_{\{i\}}\|_{2,0}\leq s_{i}$;}

(16)

\displaystyle\text{(ii) Bounded spectrum}:\frac{1}{\nu}\leq{s_{\min}(\Sigma)<s_{\max}(\Sigma)}\leq\nu,\frac{1}{\nu}\leq{s_{\min}(\Sigma_{0})<s_{\max}(\Sigma_{0})}\leq\nu;

\displaystyle\text{(iii) Eigengap}:\lambda_{r}-\lambda_{r+1}>0.

Here
\lambda_{i}
’s are generalized eigenvalues as defined in (
8
).
By Section
2
, under model (
5
) the eigengap condition is satisfied if Assumption
2.2
holds.
The parameter space is then defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n}(\{s_{i}\}_{1}^{k},\{p_{i}\}_{1}^{k},r,\{\lambda_{j}\}_{1}^{p};\nu)

\displaystyle=\big\{\mathcal{L}(X^{(1)},X^{(2)}...,X^{(n)}):X^{(i)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}N(0,\Sigma),

(17)

\displaystyle\Sigma\in\mathcal{F}(\{s_{i}\}_{1}^{k},\{p_{i}\}_{1}^{k},r,\{\lambda_{j}\}_{1}^{p};\nu)\big\}.

In what follows,
S
denotes the true row support of
A
.
While the parameter space requires normality, our theoretical analysis generalizes directly to sub-Gaussian distributions. We omit the generalization in this work as it is mostly formality.

Matrix distance

To measure estimation accuracy, we define the distance between two matrices
U
and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
as

\mathrm{dist}(U,V)=\min_{P\in\mathcal{O}(r)}\|UP-V\|_{\mathrm{F}}.

(18)

Here
\mathcal{O}(r)
is the collection of
r\times r
orthogonal matrices.
The matrix distance has been used previously in
[
18
]
,
[
33
]
and
[
19
]
, among others.

Corollary 4.2

Suppose the conditions of Theorem

We give some brief remarks on Theorem
4.1
and Corollary
4.2
.
The error bound for each step,
given by the right side of (
24
),
is composed of two parts:
a statistical error term independent of the iteration counter
t
,
and an optimization error that decreases geometrically as
t
increases. When
t\geq T_{0}
, the optimization error is dominated by the statistical error and we can achieve the estimation rate in Corollary
4.2
.
Thus,
T_{0}
can be interpreted as the minimum number of iterations needed for achieving the estimation error rate in Corollary
4.2
.
When the eigengap
\lambda_{r}-\lambda_{r+1}
is lower bounded by a positive constant, it is straightforward to verify that
T_{0}=O(\log(p+n))
.
In other words, thresholded gradient descent drives down the statistical error of the estimator to the desired rate after
O(\log(p+n))
iterates.
It is worth noting that the contraction rate
\xi
does not depend on ambient dimension
p
, which can be much larger than
r
and
s
.

Condition (
22
) on initial estimator is related to the notion of “basin of attraction”.
It has previously appeared in the literature of machining learning with nonconvex optimization in other contexts, e.g.,
[
9
,
10
,
37
]
.
The global landscape of (
12
) could be hard to handle due to non-convexity and sparsity constraints.
However, within the basin of attraction, the objective function
f(\cdot)
is locally smooth and strongly convex, which allows projected gradient descent to find a statistically sound solution at geometric convergence rate.

Outline of proof

To prove Theorem
4.1
, we track the estimator trajectory
\{\overline{V}_{t}:t\geq 0\}
over iteration.
To this end, we first characterize a global high probability event under which the entire trajectory would lie inside the basin of attraction within which the objective function is smooth and strongly convex.
On the event, we analyze in sequel the effects of the gradient step and the hard thresholding step in each iteration.
In particular,
we show that each gradient step drives down the distance between the target of estimation and the current estimator while the hard thresholding step projects the current estimator onto the feasible set.
For details, see Propositions
B.5
and
B.6
, respectively.
Combining the two propositions, we obtain a recursive inequality that characterizes the estimator trajectory as in (
24
).
This completes the major steps in the proof.
Finally,
Corollary
4.2
is a direct consequence of normalization on the same event that we performed the foregoing analysis of iteration.
For proof details, see Appendix
B
.

Sparse GCA with general covariance structure

In this section, we investigate the performance of our algorithm under a general covariance structure and demonstrate empirically that our algorithm works beyond latent variable models as long as
the eigengap condition holds.

We consider sparse GCA of three high-dimensional datasets with
p_{1}=500

p_{2}=p_{3}=200

\Sigma

p_{\min}=\min_{i}p_{i}=200
.
To generate covariance matrices
\Sigma
and
\Sigma_{0}
,
we first define a diagonal matrix
\Theta
with
\Theta_{ii}=2/i
for
i=1,2,\dots,p_{\min}
.
We then define
\Sigma
as a block matrix with

\Sigma_{\{ij\}}=T_{\{i\}}U_{\{i\}}\Theta U_{\{j\}}^{\top}T_{\{j\}},\quad\Sigma_{\{ii\}}=2T_{\{i\}},\quad\text{for $i\neq j\in\{1,2,3\}$},

U_{\{1\}}^{\top}T_{\{1\}}U_{\{1\}}=U_{\{2\}}^{\top}T_{\{2\}}U_{\{2\}}=U_{\{3\}}^{\top}T_{\{3\}}U_{\{3\}}=I.

Here, each
\Sigma_{\{ii\}}=2T_{\{i\}}
and each
T_{\{i\}}

(T_{\{k\}})_{ij}=\sigma_{k_{ij}}
where
\sigma_{k_{ij}}=a_{k}^{|i-j|}

a_{1}=0.5,a_{2}=0.7,a_{3}=0.9

U_{\{i\}}(2)\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{i}\times(p_{\min}-s_{i})}
.
The submatrices
U_{\{i\}}(1),U_{\{i\}}(2)

To generate each
U_{\{i\}}(1)

s_{i}

U_{\{i\}}(1)

U_{\{i\}}(1)

T_{\{i\}}

U_{\{i\}}(2)

\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\Sigma A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{\{1\}}+\Sigma^{-1}_{\{11\}}U_{\{1\}}\sum_{j\neq 1}U^{\top}_{\{j\}}A_{\{j\}}\\
\vdots\\
A_{\{k\}}+\Sigma^{-1}_{\{kk\}}U_{\{k\}}\sum_{j\neq k}U^{\top}_{\{j\}}A_{\{j\}}\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{\{1\}}\Lambda_{r}\\
\vdots\\
A_{\{k\}}\Lambda_{r}\end{bmatrix}=A\Lambda_{r}.

\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma^{-1}_{\{11\}}U_{\{1\}}\sum_{j\neq 1}U^{\top}_{\{j\}}A_{\{j\}}\\
\vdots\\
\Sigma^{-1}_{\{kk\}}U_{\{k\}}\sum_{j\neq k}U^{\top}_{\{j\}}A_{\{j\}}\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{\{1\}}(\Lambda_{r}-I_{r})\\

A.2
Proof of Lemma
2.3

Before the proof of the lemma, we recall Weyl’s inequality
[
38
]
stated as follows.

Lemma A.1
.

(Weyl’s inequality)

Let
A,B
be two
p\times p
Hermitian matrices and
s_{i}(A)
be the
i
th eigenvalue of
A
.
Then for
1\leq l,j\leq p
, we have

\displaystyle s_{l}(A+B)

\displaystyle\leq s_{j}(A)+s_{k}(B),\quad\text{for $l\geq j+k-1$},

\displaystyle s_{j}(A)+s_{l}(B)

\displaystyle\leq s_{j+l-p}(A+B),\quad\text{for $j+l\geq p$}.

Proof.

The proof is adapted from
[
14
]
.
It is composed of three parts.
First we show that if
i\geq r+1
,
\lambda_{i}\leq 1
.
Then we will prove that
\lambda_{r}>1
.
Finally we prove the result on multiplicity of 1.
Recall that the generalized eigenvalues are identical to the eigenvalues of
R=\Sigma_{0}^{-1/2}\Sigma\Sigma_{0}^{-1/2}
.
From now on we study the eigenvalues of
R
directly.

Step (1):

Note that we can write
R
as

R=\Sigma_{0}^{-1/2}\Sigma\Sigma_{0}^{-1/2}=\Sigma_{0}^{-1/2}(UU^{\top}+\Psi)\Sigma_{0}^{-1/2}=Q_{1}Q_{1}^{\top}+Q_{2}Q_{2}^{\top}

where

Q_{1}=\Sigma_{0}^{-1/2}U\quad Q_{2}=\Sigma_{0}^{-1/2}\Psi^{1/2}.

We first show that
\|Q_{2}Q_{2}^{\top}\|_{{\mathrm{op}}}\leq 1
.
To this end, note that

\|Q_{2}Q_{2}^{\top}\|_{{\mathrm{op}}}=\|Q_{2}^{\top}Q_{2}\|_{{\mathrm{op}}}=\|\Psi^{1/2}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\Psi^{1/2}\|_{{\mathrm{op}}}
.
Note that
\Psi^{1/2}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\Psi^{1/2}
is a block diagonal matrix with the
i
th block given by
\Psi^{1/2}_{\{ii\}}(\Psi_{\{ii\}}+U_{\{i\}}U^{\top}_{\{i\}})^{-1}\Psi^{1/2}_{\{ii\}}
.
Thus, it suffices to show that

\|\Psi^{1/2}_{\{ii\}}(\Psi_{\{ii\}}+U_{\{i\}}U^{\top}_{\{i\}})^{-1}\Psi^{1/2}_{\{ii\}}\|_{{\mathrm{op}}}\leq 1,\quad\mbox{for $i\in[k]$}.

To this end, by Woodbury matrix identity,

\displaystyle\Psi^{1/2}_{\{ii\}}(\Psi_{\{ii\}}+U_{\{i\}}U^{\top}_{\{i\}})^{-1}\Psi^{1/2}_{\{ii\}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Psi^{1/2}_{\{ii\}}[\Psi^{-1}_{\{ii\}}-\Psi^{-1}_{\{ii\}}U_{\{i\}}(I_{r}+U^{\top}_{\{i\}}\Psi^{-1}_{\{ii\}}U_{\{i\}})^{-1}U^{\top}_{\{i\}}\Psi^{-1}_{\{ii\}}]\Psi^{1/2}_{\{ii\}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I_{p_{i}}-\Psi^{-1/2}_{\{ii\}}U_{\{i\}}(I_{r}+U^{\top}_{\{i\}}\Psi^{-1}_{\{ii\}}U_{\{i\}})^{-1}U^{\top}_{\{i\}}\Psi^{-1/2}_{\{ii\}}.

Therefore,
0\preceq\Psi^{1/2}_{\{ii\}}(\Psi_{\{ii\}}+U_{\{i\}}U^{\top}_{\{i\}})^{-1}\Psi^{1/2}_{\{ii\}}\preceq I_{p_{i}}
, and so
\|Q_{2}Q_{2}^{\top}\|_{{\mathrm{op}}}\leq 1
.

By Lemma
A.1
, we have, for
i\geq j+k-1
,

\lambda_{i}=s_{i}(R)\leq s_{j}(Q_{1}Q_{1}^{\top})+s_{k}(Q_{2}Q_{2}^{\top}).

Now we set
j=r+1,k=1
and this gives

\lambda_{i}{=s_{i}(R)}\leq s_{r+1}(Q_{1}Q_{1}^{\top})+s_{1}(Q_{2}Q_{2}^{\top})=s_{1}(Q_{2}Q_{2}^{\top})\quad\text{for $i\geq r+1$}.

The last equality holds since we have
{\mathrm{rank}}(Q_{1}Q_{1}^{\top})=r
and so
s_{r+1}(Q_{1}Q_{1}^{\top})=0
.
As a result,

\lambda_{i}{=s_{i}(R)}\leq s_{1}(Q_{2}Q_{2}^{\top})=\|Q_{2}Q_{2}^{\top}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1.

This finishes the proof of the first step.

Step (2):

By Lemma
A.1
and Assumption
2.2
,
we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{r}{=s_{r}(R)}

\displaystyle=s_{r}(Q_{1}Q_{1}^{\top}+Q_{2}Q_{2}^{\top})\geq s_{r}(Q_{1}Q_{1}^{\top})+s_{p}(Q_{2}Q_{2}^{\top})

\displaystyle>s_{r}(Q_{1}Q_{1}^{\top})=\sigma_{r}^{2}(\Sigma_{0}^{-1/2}U)\geq 1.

Here, the strict inequality holds since
Q_{2}Q_{2}^{\top}
is of full rank.
This finishes the proof for the
r
th eigenvalue.

Step (3):

Now we calculate the multiplicity of
1
.
To this end, we resort to Theorem 2.2 in
[
32
]
, which gives

\displaystyle{\mathrm{rank}}(U^{\top}-U^{\top}YU^{\top})

\displaystyle={\mathrm{rank}}(U-UY^{\top}U)={\mathrm{rank}}(U^{\dagger}U-U^{\dagger}_{\{1\}}U_{\{1\}}-\dots-U^{\dagger}_{\{k\}}U_{\{k\}})

\displaystyle={\mathrm{rank}}(\widetilde{U})+{\mathrm{rank}}(U)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\mathrm{rank}}(U_{\{i\}}).

Here

\widetilde{U}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\Sigma_{\{12\}}&\Sigma_{\{13\}}&...&\Sigma_{\{1k\}}\\
\Sigma_{\{21\}}&0&\Sigma_{\{23\}}&...&\Sigma_{\{2k\}}\\
\Sigma_{\{31\}}&\Sigma_{\{32\}}&0&...&\Sigma_{\{3k\}}\\
...&...&...&...&...\\
\Sigma_{\{k1\}}&\Sigma_{\{k2\}}&\Sigma_{\{k3\}}&...&0\end{bmatrix}.

Note that we have

\Sigma_{\{ij\}}=U_{\{i\}}U^{\top}_{\{j\}},\quad{\mathrm{rank}}(U)=r,

by the latent variable model.
Moreover, it is easy to verify that multiplicity of
1
as an eigenvalue of
R
is the same as
multiplicity of
0
as an eigenvalue of
\widetilde{U}
, which in turn equals
p-{\mathrm{rank}}(\widetilde{U})
.
Thus, multiplicity of
1
is

\displaystyle p-{\mathrm{rank}}(\widetilde{U})

\displaystyle=p-{\mathrm{rank}}(U^{\top}-U^{\top}YU^{\top})+{\mathrm{rank}}(U)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\mathrm{rank}}(U_{\{i\}})

\displaystyle=p-\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\mathrm{rank}}(U_{\{i\}})+r-{\mathrm{rank}}(U-UY^{\top}U).

This finishes the proof of the lemma.
∎

\mathrm{dist}(\overline{V}_{t},V)

(44)

where
\|Q\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq C_{0}\epsilon_{n}.

Since
\overline{V}_{t}
is
s^{\prime}
row sparse and

is
s
row sparse,

The remaining proof is composed of two steps:

Step (1):

By definition we have

\displaystyle\overline{V}_{t}^{\top}{\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}}\overline{V}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(A\left(I+\frac{\Lambda_{r}}{\lambda}\right)^{1/2}P+Q\right)^{\top}{\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}}\left(A\left(I+\frac{\Lambda_{r}}{\lambda}\right)^{1/2}P+Q\right)

\displaystyle\|\overline{V}_{t}^{\top}{\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}}\overline{V}_{t}-P^{\top}(I+\frac{\Lambda_{r}}{\lambda})P\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq

\displaystyle\underbrace{\|P^{\top}(I+\frac{\Lambda_{r}}{\lambda})^{1/2}A^{\top}({\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}}-\Sigma_{0})A(I+\frac{\Lambda_{r}}{\lambda})^{1/2}P\|_{\mathrm{F}}}_{\text{{Term I}}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{2\|Q^{\top}{\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}}A(I+\frac{\Lambda_{r}}{\lambda})^{1/2}\|_{\mathrm{F}}}_{\text{Term II}}+\underbrace{\|Q^{\top}{\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}}Q\|_{\mathrm{F}}}_{\text{Term III}}.

A
is
s

\displaystyle\|P^{\top}(I+\frac{\Lambda_{r}}{\lambda})^{1/2}A^{\top}({\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}}-\Sigma_{0})A(I+\frac{\Lambda_{r}}{\lambda})^{1/2}P\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\sqrt{\frac{s\log p}{n}}.

A

\displaystyle\|P^{\top}(I+\frac{\Lambda_{r}}{\lambda})^{1/2}A^{\top}({\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}}-\Sigma_{0})A(I+\frac{\Lambda_{r}}{\lambda})^{1/2}P\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\sqrt{\frac{rs\log p}{n}}\leq C_{1}\epsilon_{n}.

For Term II, since

s+s^{\prime}

\displaystyle\|\Delta\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\leq\|(\overline{V}_{t}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}\overline{V}_{t})^{-1/2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|(\overline{V}_{t}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}\overline{V}_{t})^{1/2}-P^{\top}(I+\frac{\Lambda_{r}}{\lambda})^{1/2}P\|_{\mathrm{F}}\|P^{\top}(I+\frac{\Lambda_{r}}{\lambda})^{-1/2}P\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}\|\overline{V}_{t}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}\overline{V}_{t}-P^{\top}(I+\frac{\Lambda_{r}}{\lambda})P\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq C_{4}\epsilon_{n}.

[

.

Step (2):

Now we bound

.

\displaystyle\|\widehat{A}_{t}-AP\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle=\|\overline{V}_{t}(\overline{V}_{t}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}\overline{V}_{t})^{-1/2}-AP\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle=\|(A(I+\frac{\Lambda_{r}}{\lambda})^{1/2}P+Q)(P^{\top}(I+\frac{\Lambda_{r}}{\lambda})^{-1/2}P+\Delta)-AP\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle=\|AP+QP^{\top}(I+\frac{\Lambda_{r}}{\lambda})^{-1/2}P+A(I+\frac{\Lambda_{r}}{\lambda})^{1/2}P\Delta+Q\Delta-AP\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\leq\|QP^{\top}(I+\frac{\Lambda_{r}}{\lambda})^{-1/2}P\|_{\mathrm{F}}+\|A(I+\frac{\Lambda_{r}}{\lambda})^{1/2}P\Delta\|_{\mathrm{F}}+\|Q\Delta\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\leq\|Q\|_{\mathrm{F}}\|P^{\top}(I+\frac{\Lambda_{r}}{\lambda})^{-1/2}P\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\|A(I+\frac{\Lambda_{r}}{\lambda})^{1/2}P\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|\Delta\|_{\mathrm{F}}+\|Q\|_{\mathrm{F}}\|\Delta\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{5}\epsilon_{n}

for some constant

Combining the results above we deduce that

\mathrm{dist}(\widehat{A}_{t},A)\leq\|\widehat{A}_{t}-AP\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq C_{5}\epsilon_{n}

with probability at least

.
This finishes our proof.

By applying Holder’s inequality on Schatten norm and the definition of support set, we have

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(\|V_{F_{1}*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\|V_{t+1,F_{1}*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}+\|V_{F_{2}*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\|V_{t+1,F_{2}*}\|_{\mathrm{F}})^{2}=(x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{2})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2})X^{2}\leq(Y^{2}-y_{3}^{2})X^{2}\leq X^{2}Y^{2}-\frac{k_{3}}{k_{1}}X^{2}y_{1}^{2},

and this gives

y_{1}^{2}\leq\frac{k_{1}}{k_{3}X^{2}}(X^{2}Y^{2}-\Delta^{2})\leq\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}}{k_{3}+k_{2}}(Y^{2}-\Delta^{2}/X^{2})=\frac{s}{s^{\prime}}(Y^{2}-\Delta^{2}/X^{2}),

where the second inequality holds since
k_{1}\leq k_{3}
.
Now we split the arguments into two cases.

Case I:

\Delta<XY\sqrt{\frac{s}{s+s^{\prime}}}
.
In this case, we obtain that

\Delta<\sqrt{\frac{s}{s^{\prime}}}\sqrt{X^{2}Y^{2}-\Delta^{2}}.

This implies (
47
) immediately.

Case II:

We can now assume that
\Delta\geq XY\sqrt{\frac{s}{s+s^{\prime}}}
. Then we have

y_{1}^{2}\leq\frac{s}{s^{\prime}}(Y^{2}-\Delta^{2}/X^{2})\leq\frac{\Delta^{2}}{X^{2}}.

By definition we have

x_{1}y_{1}+\sqrt{Y^{2}-y_{1}^{2}}\sqrt{X^{2}-x_{1}^{2}}\geq x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{2}\geq\Delta.

Solving this inequality as a quadratic inequality in
x_{1}
yields

x_{1}\leq\frac{\Delta y_{1}+\sqrt{(X^{2}Y^{2}-\Delta^{2})(Y^{2}-y_{1}^{2}})}{Y^{2}}.

By definition of
X^{2}=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}
we have
x_{1}\leq X
. Also we have
\Delta\leq XY
by Holder inequality on Schatten norm.

Combining the above inequalities together, we have

x_{1}\leq\min\left(X,\frac{Xy_{1}+\sqrt{X^{2}Y^{2}-\Delta^{2}}}{Y}\right).

Note that
y_{1}\leq\sqrt{\frac{s}{s^{\prime}}}\frac{\sqrt{X^{2}Y^{2}-\Delta^{2}}}{X}
by previous calculation.
Substituting it into the above equation, we have

x_{1}\leq\min\left(X,\frac{\sqrt{\frac{s}{s^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{X^{2}Y^{2}-\Delta^{2}}}+\sqrt{X^{2}Y^{2}-\Delta^{2}}}{Y}\right).

Finally, we can compute that

\displaystyle x_{1}y_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{s}{s^{\prime}}}\frac{1}{X}\sqrt{X^{2}Y^{2}-\Delta^{2}}\,\min\left(X,\frac{1}{Y}\sqrt{X^{2}Y^{2}-\Delta^{2}}(1+\sqrt{\frac{s}{s^{\prime}}})\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{s}{s^{\prime}}}\min\left(\sqrt{X^{2}Y^{2}-\Delta^{2}},\,\frac{1+\sqrt{\frac{s}{s^{\prime}}}}{XY}(X^{2}Y^{2}-\Delta^{2})\right).

This is the error induced by the truncation step, finally we have

\displaystyle\Tr(|{V}^{\top}V_{t+1}|)-\Tr(|{V}^{\top}\overline{V}_{t+1}|)\leq

\displaystyle\Tr(|{V}^{\top}(\overline{V}_{t+1}-V_{t+1})|)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle x_{1}y_{1}\leq\sqrt{\frac{s}{s^{\prime}}}\min\left(\sqrt{X^{2}Y^{2}-\Delta^{2}},\frac{1+\sqrt{\frac{s}{s^{\prime}}}}{XY}(X^{2}Y^{2}-\Delta^{2})\right).

This finishes the proof for the claim.
∎

We now switch to work with the distance metric
\mathrm{dist}(U,V)=\min_{P\in{\mathcal{O}(r)}}\|UP-V\|_{\mathrm{F}}
.
We first expand the expression

\displaystyle\|UP-V\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|UP\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|V\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-2\langle UP,V\rangle

\displaystyle=\|U\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|V\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-2\Tr(P^{\top}U^{\top}V).

So, to minimize the distance metric we defined is to maximize
\Tr(P^{\top}U^{\top}V)
. To this end, let
ADB^{\top}
denote the singular value decomposition of
U^{\top}V
, then we have

\Tr(P^{\top}U^{\top}V)=\Tr(P^{\top}ADB^{\top})=\Tr(B^{\top}P^{\top}AD)=\Tr(ZD)

for
Z
being an orthogonal matrix. Since
D
is a diagonal matrix, we have
\Tr(ZD)=\sum_{i}Z_{ii}D_{ii}
and to maximize this value we would like to have
Z_{ii}=1
for all
i
since
D_{ii}
are non-negative. As a result, we have the optimal
P
being
AB^{\top}
and the optimal value is given by

\min\|UP-V\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\|U\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|V\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-2\Tr(D)=\|U\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|V\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-2\Tr(|U^{\top}V|).

Now we consider the subspace distance we have defined.
Recall that
\Delta=\Tr(|{V}^{\top}V_{t+1}|)
.
Then the subspace distance can be written as

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}^{2}(\overline{V}_{t+1},V)

\displaystyle=\|V\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|\overline{V}_{t+1}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-2\Tr|{V}^{\top}\overline{V}_{t+1}|

\displaystyle\leq\|V\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|\overline{V}_{t+1}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-2\Tr|{V}^{\top}V_{t+1}|+2\sqrt{\frac{s}{s^{\prime}}}\min\left(\sqrt{X^{2}Y^{2}-\Delta^{2}},\frac{1+\sqrt{\frac{s}{s^{\prime}}}}{XY}(X^{2}Y^{2}-\Delta^{2})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\|V\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|V_{t+1}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-2\Tr|{V}^{\top}V_{t+1}|+2\sqrt{\frac{s}{s^{\prime}}}\frac{1+\sqrt{\frac{s}{s^{\prime}}}}{XY}(X^{2}Y^{2}-\Delta^{2})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{dist}^{2}(V_{t+1},V)+2\sqrt{\frac{s}{s^{\prime}}}\frac{1+\sqrt{\frac{s}{s^{\prime}}}}{XY}(X^{2}Y^{2}-\Delta^{2}),

where the first equality follows from the previous expansion of distance, the first inequality follows from Claim (
47
) and we use the fact that truncation reduces the Frobenius norm in the second inequality. In the last equality, we use again the relationship between trace norm and distance. To deal with the extra error term, we use the following bound

\displaystyle X^{2}Y^{2}-\Delta^{2}

\displaystyle=(XY+\Delta)(XY-\Delta)\leq 2XY(XY-\Delta)\leq XY(X^{2}+Y^{2}-2\Delta)

\displaystyle=XY(\|V\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|V_{t+1}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-2\Tr(|{V}^{\top}V_{t+1}|)=XY\mathrm{dist}^{2}(V_{t+1},V).

Combining the inequalities, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}^{2}(\overline{V}_{t+1},V)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{dist}^{2}(V_{t+1},V)+2\sqrt{\frac{s}{s^{\prime}}}\frac{1+\sqrt{\frac{s}{s^{\prime}}}}{XY}(X^{2}Y^{2}-\Delta^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{dist}^{2}(V_{t+1},V)+2\sqrt{\frac{s}{s^{\prime}}}\frac{1+\sqrt{\frac{s}{s^{\prime}}}}{XY}XY\mathrm{dist}^{2}(V_{t+1},V)

\displaystyle=\left(1+2\sqrt{\frac{s}{s^{\prime}}}\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{s}{s^{\prime}}}\right)\right)\mathrm{dist}^{2}(V_{t+1},V).

This finishes our analysis for the hard thresholding step.
∎

\displaystyle=\left[U_{\{1\}}^{{(t)}^{\top}},U_{\{2\}}^{{(t)}^{\top}},\dots,U_{\{k\}}^{{(t)}^{\top}}\right]^{\top},

\displaystyle K_{i}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\left[-U_{\{1\}}^{{(t)}^{\top}},-U_{\{2\}}^{{(t)}^{\top}}\dots,-U_{\{i-1\}}^{{(t)}^{\top}},(k-1)U_{\{i\}}^{{(t)}^{\top}},-U_{\{i+1\}}^{{(t)}^{\top}},\dots,-U_{\{k\}}^{{(t)}^{\top}}\right]^{\top}.

That is,
K_{i}^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
is a block matrix with
j
th blocks being
-U_{\{j\}}^{(t)}
for
j\neq i
and the
i
th block being
(k-1)U_{\{i\}}^{(t)}
.
It is straightforward to verify that
\Sigma^{(t)}
yields the following decomposition

\Sigma^{(t)}=I+\frac{(k-1)\lambda}{k}U^{(t)}U^{{(t)}^{\top}}-\frac{\lambda}{k^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}K_{i}^{(t)}K_{i}^{{(t)}^{\top}}.

We then deduce that the eigen-structure of
\Sigma^{(t)}
is given by
r
eigenvalues equal to
1+\lambda(k-1)
,
(k-1)r
eigenvalues equal to
1-\lambda
and
p-kr
eigenvalues equal to 1. Furthermore, we notice that

\Sigma^{(t)}U^{(t)}=(1+(k-1)\lambda)U^{(t)},

hence the leading
r
dimensional eigenspace is given by the matrix
U^{(t)}
. This in particular implies that

\det\Sigma^{(1)}=\det\Sigma^{(2)}.

Now the KL divergence is given by

\displaystyle D(P^{(1)}||P^{(2)})

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2}\left(\Tr\left((\Sigma^{(2)})^{-1}\Sigma^{(1)}\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}-\log\det\left((\Sigma^{(2)})^{-1}\Sigma^{(1)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2}\left(\Tr\left((\Sigma^{(2)})^{-1}\Sigma^{(1)}\right)-p\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2}\left(\Tr\left((\Sigma^{(2)})^{-1}\left(\Sigma^{(1)}-\Sigma^{(2)}\right)\right)\right).

We now calculate the inverse of the block matrix
\Sigma^{(2)}
. To this end, we first guess the form of the inverse and then determine the coefficients. Specifically, we try to solve for the inverse with the following form

(\Sigma^{(2)})^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}I+aU_{\{1\}}^{(2)}U_{\{1\}}^{{(2)}^{\top}}&bU_{\{1\}}^{(2)}U_{\{2\}}^{{(2)}^{\top}}&\dots&\dots&bU_{\{1\}}^{(2)}U_{\{k\}}^{{(2)}^{\top}}\\
bU_{\{2\}}^{(2)}U_{\{1\}}^{{(2)}^{\top}}&I+aU_{\{2\}}^{(2)}U_{\{2\}}^{{(2)}^{\top}}&bU_{\{2\}}^{(2)}U_{\{3\}}^{{(2)}^{\top}}&\dots&bU_{\{2\}}^{(2)}U_{\{k\}}^{{(2)}^{\top}}\\
\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots\\
bU_{\{k\}}^{(2)}U_{\{1\}}^{{(2)}^{\top}}&\dots&\dots&\dots&I+aU_{\{k\}}^{(2)}U_{\{k\}}^{{(2)}^{\top}}\par\end{bmatrix}

with the
(i,j)
th block being
bU_{\{i\}}^{(2)}U_{\{j\}}^{{(2)}^{\top}}
for
i\neq j
and
i
th diagonal block being
I+aU_{\{i\}}^{(2)}U_{\{i\}}^{{(2)}^{\top}}
. To determine the values of
(a,b)
, we solve for the equation
(\Sigma^{(2)})^{-1}\Sigma^{(2)}=I_{p}
. This requires two conditions on the matrix product: the off-diagonal block to be 0 and the diagonal block to be
I
. For the
i
th diagonal block, we require

I+(k-1)b\lambda U_{\{i\}}^{(2)}(U_{\{i\}}^{(2)})^{\top}+aU_{\{i\}}^{(2)}(U_{\{i\}}^{(2)})^{\top}=I,

and for the
(i,j)
th off-diagonal block, we require

\lambda b(k-2)U_{\{i\}}^{(2)}(U_{\{j\}}^{(2)})^{\top}+bU_{\{i\}}^{(2)}(U_{\{j\}}^{(2)})^{\top}+\lambda U_{\{i\}}^{(2)}(U_{\{j\}}^{(2)})^{\top}+a\lambda U_{\{i\}}^{(2)}(U_{\{j\}}^{(2)})^{\top}=0.

Solving the above equations, we have

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=-(k-1)b\lambda,

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{(k-1)\lambda^{2}-\lambda(k-2)-1}.

As a result, we can simplify the expression for KL divergence as follows

\displaystyle D(P^{(1)}||P^{(2)})

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2}\left(\Tr\left((\Sigma^{(2)})^{-1}\left(\Sigma^{(1)}-\Sigma^{(2)}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda n}{2}\left(\Tr\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j\neq i}bU_{\{i\}}^{(2)}(U_{\{j\}}^{(2)})^{\top}\left(U_{\{j\}}^{(1)}(U_{\{i\}}^{(1)})^{\top}-U_{\{j\}}^{(2)}(U_{\{i\}}^{(2)})^{\top}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{b\lambda n}{2}\left(\sum_{i,j:i\neq j}\Tr\left(I-U_{\{i\}}^{(2)}(U_{\{j\}}^{(2)})^{\top}U_{\{j\}}^{(1)}(U_{\{i\}}^{(1)})^{\top}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{b\lambda n}{4}\sum_{i,j:i\neq j}\left\|U_{\{i\}}^{(1)}(U_{\{j\}}^{(1)})^{\top}-U_{\{i\}}^{(2)}(U_{\{j\}}^{(2)})^{\top}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda^{2}n}{4\left(-(k-1)\lambda^{2}+(k-2)\lambda+1\right)}\sum_{i,j:i\neq j}\left\|U_{\{i\}}^{(1)}(U_{\{j\}}^{(1)})^{\top}-U_{\{i\}}^{(2)}(U_{\{j\}}^{(2)})^{\top}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.

This finishes our proof for the Lemma. Note that when
k=2
, we recover Lemma 1 in
[
16
]
as a special case.

∎

C.1
Proof of Theorem
4.6

Proof.

The main body of the proof is adapted from
[
15
]
. The main tool for our proof is Fano’s Lemma. For the sake of completeness, we provide the following version of Fano’s Lemma from
[
40
]
.

Lemma C.2
.

Let
(\Theta,\rho)
be a metric space and
\{P_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
a collection of probability measures. For any totally bounded
T\in\Theta
, denoted by
\mathcal{M}(T,\rho,\epsilon)
the
\epsilon
-packing number of
T
with respect to
\rho
, i.e., the maximal number of points in
T
whose pairwise maximum distance in
\rho
is at least
\epsilon
. Define the Kullback-Leibler diameter
T
by

d_{\mathrm{KL}}(T)\triangleq\sup_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in T}D(P_{\theta}||D_{\theta^{\prime}}).

Then

\inf_{\theta}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}{\mathbf{E}}_{\theta}[\rho^{2}(\hat{\theta}(X),\theta)]\geq\sup_{T\in\Theta}\sup_{\epsilon>0}\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4}\left(1-\frac{d_{\mathrm{KL}}(T)+\log 2}{\log\mathcal{M}(T,\rho,\epsilon)}\right)

The proof is compose of two steps corresponding to different terms in the lower bound. Throughout the proof, we define
\lambda=\lambda_{r}-\lambda_{r+1}
for simplicity.

Step I.

We first establish the term involving
r\sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{i}
. To this end, let
U_{\{i\}0}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{r}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\in O(p_{i},r)
for each
i=1,2,\dots,k
. For some
\epsilon_{0}\in(0,\sqrt{r\wedge(s_{1}-r)}]
to be specified later, let

B(\epsilon_{0})=\{U_{\{1\}}\in O(p_{1},r):\mathrm{supp}(U_{\{1\}})\subset[s_{1}],\|U_{\{1\}}-U_{\{1\}0}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\epsilon_{0}\}

and

T_{0}=\left\{\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}I_{p_{1}}&\lambda U_{\{1\}}U_{\{2\}0}^{\top}&\lambda U_{\{1\}}U_{\{3\}0}^{\top}\dots&\lambda U_{\{1\}}U_{\{k\}0}^{\top}\\
\lambda U_{\{2\}0}U_{\{1\}}^{\top}&I_{p_{2}}&\dots&\lambda U_{\{2\}0}U_{\{k\}0}^{\top}\\
\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots\\
\lambda U_{\{k\}0}U_{\{1\}}^{\top}&\lambda U_{\{k\}0}U_{\{2\}0}^{\top}&\dots&I_{p_{k}}\end{pmatrix}:U_{\{1\}}\in B(\epsilon_{0})\right\},

with
U=\left[U_{\{1\}}^{\top},U_{\{2\}0}^{\top},\dots,U_{\{k\}0}^{\top}\right]^{\top}
. Since our target of estimation is
A
instead of
U
, we first establish the relationship between
U
and
A
under
T_{0}
. Note that under the construction of
\Sigma
, we have
\Sigma_{0}=I_{p}
so the generalized eigenspace coincides with eigenspace. From Lemma
C.1
, we deduce that the leading
r
dimensional eigenspace of
\Sigma
is given by
\mathrm{span}(U)
. From the normalization constraint such that
A^{\top}A=I
, we conclude that

\mathrm{dist}(A,\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}U)=0
.
From here on, we first derive the minimax lower bound for the estimation of
U
and the lower bound for estimating
A
under the matrix distance follows immediately by scaling.

The above analysis also implies that
T_{0}\subset\mathcal{F}
, where
\mathcal{F}
is our original parameter space. By Lemma
C.1
,

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{KL}}(T_{0})

\displaystyle=\sup_{U^{(1)}_{\{1\}},U^{(2)}_{\{1\}}\in B(\epsilon_{0})}D(P^{(1)}||P^{(2)})

\displaystyle=\sup_{U^{(1)}_{\{1\}},U^{(2)}_{\{1\}}\in B(\epsilon_{0})}\frac{\lambda^{2}n}{4\left(-(k-1)\lambda^{2}+(k-2)\lambda+1\right)}\sum_{i\neq j}\left\|U_{\{i\}}^{(1)}U_{\{j\}}^{{(1)}^{\top}}-U_{\{i\}}^{(2)}U_{\{j\}}^{{(2)}^{\top}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{U^{(1)}_{\{1\}},U^{(2)}_{\{1\}}\in B(\epsilon_{0})}\frac{\lambda^{2}n}{4\left(-(k-1)\lambda^{2}+(k-2)\lambda+1\right)}\sum_{j=2}^{k}2\left\|U_{\{1\}}^{(1)}U_{\{j\}0}^{{(1)}^{\top}}-U_{\{1\}}^{(2)}U_{\{j\}0}^{{(2)}^{\top}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{U^{(1)}_{\{1\}},U^{(2)}_{\{1\}}\in B(\epsilon_{0})}\frac{\lambda^{2}n}{2\left(-(k-1)\lambda^{2}+(k-2)\lambda+1\right)}\sum_{j=2}^{k}\left\|U_{\{1\}}^{(1)}-U_{\{1\}}^{(2)}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\lambda^{2}n(k-1)\epsilon_{0}^{2}}{-(k-1)\lambda^{2}+(k-2)\lambda+1}.

Here, the second to last equality follows from the definition of
B(\epsilon_{0})
.

We now establish a lower bound for the packing number of
T_{0}
. For some
\alpha\in(0,1)
which shall be specified later, we define
\{\widetilde{U}_{\{1\}}(1),\widetilde{U}_{\{1\}}(2),\dots,\widetilde{U}_{\{1\}}(N)\}\subset O(p_{1},r)
to be a maximal set such that
\mathrm{supp}(\widetilde{U}_{\{1\}}(i))\subset[s_{1}]
and for
\forall i\neq j
,

\|\widetilde{U}_{\{1\}}(i)\widetilde{U}_{\{1\}}^{\top}(i)-U_{\{1\}0}U_{\{1\}0}^{\top}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\epsilon_{0},\quad\|\widetilde{U}_{\{1\}}(i)\widetilde{U}_{\{1\}}^{\top}(i)-\widetilde{U}_{\{1\}}(j)\widetilde{U}_{\{1\}}^{\top}(j)\|_{\mathrm{F}}\geq\sqrt{2}\alpha\epsilon_{0}.

Then by Lemma 1 in
[
7
]
, for some absolute constant
C>1
,

N\geq\left(\frac{1}{C\alpha}\right)^{r(s_{1}-r)}.

For each
\widetilde{U}_{\{1\}}(i)
, we define
\overline{U}_{\{1\}}(i)
to be the matrix such that

\|U_{\{1\}0}-\overline{U}_{\{1\}}(i)\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\mathrm{dist}^{2}\left(\widetilde{U}_{\{1\}}(i),U_{\{1\}0}\right).

Then for any
i
, we deduce that
\overline{U}_{\{1\}}(i)\in O(p_{1},r)
,
\mathrm{supp}(\overline{U}_{\{1\}}(i))\subset[s_{1}]
and
\overline{U}_{\{1\}}(i)\overline{U}_{\{1\}}(i)^{\top}=\widetilde{U}_{\{1\}}(i)\widetilde{U}_{\{1\}}(i)^{\top}
.
In addition, Lemma 6.6 in
[
17
]
implies that

\|\overline{U}_{\{1\}}(i)-U_{\{1\}0}\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\mathrm{dist}\left(\widetilde{U}_{\{1\}}(i),U_{\{1\}0}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\|\widetilde{U}_{\{1\}}(i)\widetilde{U}_{\{1\}}^{\top}(i)-U_{\{1\}0}U_{\{1\}0}^{\top}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\epsilon_{0}

hence
\overline{U}_{\{1\}}(i)\in B(\epsilon_{0})
. On the other hand, note that from Lemma 6.6 in
[
17
]
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}\left(\overline{U}_{\{1\}}(i),\overline{U}_{\{1\}}(j)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\|\overline{U}_{\{1\}}(i)\overline{U}_{\{1\}}^{\top}(i)-\overline{U}_{\{1\}}(j)\overline{U}_{\{1\}}^{\top}(j)\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\|\widetilde{U}_{\{1\}}(i)\widetilde{U}_{\{1\}}^{\top}(i)-\widetilde{U}_{\{1\}}(j)\widetilde{U}_{\{1\}}^{\top}(j)\|_{\mathrm{F}}\geq\alpha\epsilon_{0}.

Define the metric to be
\rho(\Sigma^{(1)},\Sigma^{(2)})=\mathrm{dist}\left(U_{\{1\}}^{(1)},U_{\{1\}}^{(2)}\right)
. The above argument implies that for
\epsilon=\alpha\epsilon_{0}
,

\log\mathcal{M}(T_{0},\rho,\epsilon)\geq r(s_{1}-r)\log\frac{1}{C\alpha}.

Setting

\epsilon=c_{0}\left[\sqrt{r\wedge(s_{1}-r)}\wedge\sqrt{\frac{-(k-1)\lambda^{2}+(k-2)\lambda+1}{n(k-1)\lambda^{2}}r(s_{1}-r)}\right]

for sufficiently small constants
c_{0},\alpha
, we obtain a lower bound of the order

r\wedge(s_{1}-r)\wedge\frac{-(k-1)\lambda^{2}+(k-2)\lambda+1}{n(k-1)\lambda^{2}}r(s_{1}-r)

by applying Lemma
C.2
. By symmetry, we also have the lower bound with
r(s_{1}-r)
replaced by
r(s_{i}-r)
for
i=2,3,\dots,k
. Furthermore, recall that we have
r\leq\frac{1}{2}\min_{i}s_{i}
hence
r\leq(s_{i}-r)
, we obtain the lower bound of the order

r\wedge\frac{r\sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{i}}{n\lambda^{2}}

for the estimation of
U
with metric
\mathrm{dist}^{2}(U,\widehat{U})
, when
k
is finite.

Step II

We now turn to establish the lower bound term involving
s_{i}\log\frac{ep_{i}}{s_{i}}
. The step follows from the rank-one argument from
[
8
]
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
s_{i}\leq\frac{p_{i}}{2}
for any
i
, we then consider the following subset of the parameter space:

\displaystyle T_{1}=\left\{\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}I_{p_{1}}&\lambda U_{\{1\}}U_{\{2\}0}^{\top}&\lambda U_{\{1\}}U_{\{3\}0}^{\top}\dots&\lambda U_{\{1\}}U_{\{k\}0}^{\top}\\
\lambda U_{\{2\}0}U_{\{1\}}^{\top}&I_{p_{2}}&\dots&\lambda U_{\{2\}0}U_{\{k\}0}^{\top}\\
\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots\\
\lambda U_{\{k\}0}U_{\{1\}}^{\top}&\lambda U_{\{k\}0}U_{\{2\}0}^{\top}&\dots&I_{p_{k}}\end{pmatrix}:U_{\{1\}}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{r-1}&0\\
0&u_{r}\end{bmatrix}\right\},

for
u_{r}\in\mathbb{S}^{p_{1}-r+1}
and
|\mathrm{supp}(u_{r})|\leq s_{1}-r+1
. Restricting on the set
T_{1}
, the minimax risk for estimating
U
is the same as the minimax risk for estimating
u_{r}
under the squared error loss
\|u_{r}-\widehat{u}_{r}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
. Let
X_{\{i\}}=[X_{\{i\}1},X_{\{i\}2}]
for
X_{\{i\}1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times{(r-1)}}
and
X_{\{i\}2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times{(p_{i}-r+1)}}
. By the same argument in
[
15
]
, it is further equivalent to estimating
u_{r}
under the squared loss based on the observations from
X_{\{i\}2}
since
X_{\{i\}2}
for
i=1,2\dots,k
is a sufficient statistic for
u_{r}
. Applying the argument in
[
8
]
, we obtain the lower bound for
\mathrm{dist}^{2}(U,\widehat{U})
with the following term

\frac{1}{n\lambda^{2}}\left(s_{1}\log\frac{ep_{1}}{s_{1}}\right)\wedge 1.

By symmetry the same lower bound holds if we replace
s_{1},p_{1}
by
s_{i},p_{i}
for
i=2,3,\dots k
.

Combining all the above steps and noticing the
\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}
scaling between the estimation of
A
and
U
, we finally deduce that the lower bound for
\mathrm{dist}^{2}(A,\widehat{A})
is given by (
30
). This finishes our proof.
∎

Appendix D
Simulation Details for Sparse PCA of Correlation Matrices

Here we describe the procedure of generating a
p\times p
correlation matrix
R
with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{r}>\lambda_{r+1}=1\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{p}>0
and
s
-sparse leading eigenvectors.
Necessarily,
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{r}\leq s<p-1
and
s\geq r
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that
s=m\times 2^{l}
where
m
and
l
are positive integers and
2^{l}\geq r
.

Since
2^{l}\geq r
, there is a
2^{l}\times r
matrix
T_{0}
such that
T_{0,ij}\in\{\pm 1\}
and that the columns of
T_{0}
are orthogonal.
For example, we may take the first
r
columns of a
2^{l}\times 2^{l}
Hadamard matrix.
Fix such a
T_{0}
, we generate an
s\times r
matrix
T_{s}
as

T_{s}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}\begin{bmatrix}T_{0}^{\top}&\dots&T_{0}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

By our construction, each row of
T_{s}
has the same
l_{2}
norm and the columns are orthonormal.
Next, we define

R_{s}=T_{s}\,\mathrm{diag}(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{r})T_{s}^{\top}+\left(1-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}}{s}\right)I_{s}.

This decomposition ensures that the diagonal of
R_{s}
is equal to 1. We then solve for
\theta_{i}
such that the eigenvalues of
R_{s}
are given by
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r}.

Then we augment this
s\times s
positive definite matrix to a
p\times p
correlation matrix
R=\mathrm{diag}(R_{s},I_{p-s})
.
It is straightforward to verify that
R
has leading eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{r}>1
and the columns of
T=[T_{s}^{\top}\,O_{(p-s)\times r}^{\top}]^{\top}
are the corresponding eigenvectors.
