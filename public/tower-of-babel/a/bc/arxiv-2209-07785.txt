Radiation Hardness of a Wide Spectral Range SiPM with Quasi-Spherical Junction
Julius Römer, Erika Garutti, Wolfgang Schmailzl, Jörn Schwandt, Stephan Martens

Abstract
New pixel geometries are on the rise to achieve high sensitivity in near-infrared wavelengths with silicon photomultipliers (SiPMs). We test prototypes of the tip avalanche photo-diodes, which feature a quasi-spherical p-n junction and a high photodetection efficiency over a wide spectral range, and analyze the performance after neutron irradiation. The observed increase in dark count rate is significantly smaller than for a SiPM with a conventional design, indicating a good radiation hardness of the pixel geometry.

Introduction

Silicon photo-multipliers (SiPMs) have a wide range of applications, such as medical imaging
[
1
]
, particle physics experiments
[
2
]
, imaging in space
[
3

[

[

p-n junction

To overcome this limitation without sacrificing dynamic range, several attempts with a novel pixel geometry have been made
[

8

[

15\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
, a recovery time constant of
\tau=$4.5\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{s}$
and record photodetection efficiency (

73\text{\,}\%

and
22\text{\,}\%
at
900\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
have been reported
[
8
]

2
Analysis of charge spectra

To evaluate the charge spectra, the SiPM is wire-bonded on a ceramic with pin connectors, which is connected to a trans-impedance amplifier with an amplification of
G_{\text{TIA}}=$3000\text{\,}\Omega$
. The device is illuminated with a low-intensity laser pulse with a wavelength of
675\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
and a pulse length of
35\text{\,}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{s}
full-width half-maximum. The laser diode is driven at a frequency of
100\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
and the intensity of the light is tuned with neutral density filters. The outgoing signal is recorded with a Rohde + Schwarz RTO 2044 oscilloscope and the integral of the area beneath the signal of
250,000\text{\,}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{v}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{s}
is extracted to create charge histograms. The gain
G
is then evaluated as the difference between adjacent photo-electron peak positions in the charge spectrum. The turn-off voltage
V_{\text{off}}
is determined by extrapolating the linear gain-voltage dependence to
G=0

[
12
]
.

The gain-voltage behavior of both devices is shown in Fig.
2
. The TAPD gain is roughly a factor of 10 lower than that of the MP15. As the charge measurement setup is not temperature-controlled, the measurement has been conducted at
24.9\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
for the TAPD
0.6\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
and at
21.9\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
for the MP15. To compare the breakdown voltage, which is shown in section
3.2
, and the turn-off voltage, we add the product of the temperature difference and
\mathrm{d}V_{\text{BD}}/\mathrm{d}T
as shown in table
1
. After correcting for the temperature difference between the current and the charge measurements, we found that
V_{\text{off}}
is
0.72(\pm 0.06)\text{\,}\mathrm{V}
lower than
V_{\text{BD}}
for the non-irradiated MP15 sample, but up to
0.38(\pm 0.14)\text{\,}\mathrm{V}
larger for the non-irradiated TAPD sample. The relationship
V_{\text{off}}>V_{\text{BD}}
is unexpected, and a slightly non-linear
G(V)

Analysis of current-voltage measurements

In the

A Keithley 6517B is used to supply the bias voltage and to measure the current. Measurements have been conducted in forward and reverse bias at
-30\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}

20\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}

3.1
Quenching resistance

The measurements are conducted in forward bias to extract the quenching resistance

13
]

R_{\text{q}}=N_{\text{pix}}\cdot\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}I_{\text{f}}}{\mathrm{d}V}\right)^{-1}\,,

(1)

where
N_{\text{pix}}
is the number of pixels,
I_{\text{f}}
the measured current at applied forward biasing voltage
V
.

While the measurements of the 1 mm
2

R_{\text{q}}
with irradiation, only the measurements of single pixel structures showed the linear behavior required to apply Eq.
1
for retrieving the absolute value. Applying this method, a quenching resistance of
615(\pm 35)\text{\,}\mathrm{k\SIUnitSymbolOhm}

1.6\text{\,}\%5.8\text{\,}\%

-3.37(\pm 0.05)\text{\,}\mathrm{k\SIUnitSymbolOhm}\text{\,}{\mathrm{}}^{-1}

3.3
Photocurrent

To investigate the response to light, we use the photocurrent
I_{\text{photo}}=I_{\text{light}}-I_{\text{dark}}
and normalize it to

I_{\text{photo}}^{\text{norm}}=\frac{I_{\text{photo}}}{R_{\gamma}\cdot q_{0}}\,,

(3)

where
R_{\gamma}=I_{\text{photo,\,G=1}}/q_{0}
is the rate of generated photoelectrons, extracted at
I_{\text{photo,\,G=1}}\approx I_{\text{photo}}(V_{\text{bias}}=10\,\mathrm{V})
.
We can model this normalized photocurrent with

I_{\text{photo}}^{\text{norm}}=G\cdot PDE\cdot(1+CN)\,.

(4)

A comparison of
I_{\text{photo}}^{\text{norm}}
before and after irradiation indicates whether there has been a change in the parameters in Eq.
4
.

3.4
Dark current

To estimate the
DCR
and the pixel occupancy due to dark counts
\eta_{\text{DC}}
, we use a model for
I_{\text{dark}}
described in
[
10
]
:

\frac{I_{\text{dark}}}{G\cdot q_{0}}=DCR\cdot(1+CN)=\frac{N_{\text{pix}}\cdot\eta_{\text{DC}}}{\Delta t}\,,

(5)

where
G
is the gain,
q_{0}
is the elementary charge,
CN
the probability of correlated noise such as afterpulses and crosstalk, and
\Delta t
is the pixel recovery time after a Geiger discharge.
We assume
G\cdot q_{0}=(V_{\text{bias}}-V_{\text{off}})\cdot(C_{\text{pix}}+C_{\text{q}})
, where
V_{\text{off}}
is the turn-off voltage
[
12
]
,
V_{\text{bias}}
is the applied bias voltage,
C_{\text{q}}
is the capacitance of the pixel and
C_{\text{q}}
is the parasitic capacitance of the quenching resistor. For the recovery time, we assume
\Delta t=(C_{\text{pix}}+C_{\text{q}})\cdot R_{\text{q}}
. This enables us to calculate
\eta_{\text{DC}}
after rearranging Eq.
5

\eta_{\text{DC}}=\frac{I_{\text{dark}}\cdot\Delta t}{G\cdot q_{0}\cdot N_{\text{pix}}}=\frac{I_{\text{dark}}\cdot R_{\text{q}}}{(V_{\text{bias}}-V_{\text{off}})\cdot N_{\text{pix}}}\,.

(6)

With knowledge of the recovery time, we can also calculate an estimate for the DCR using

DCR\cdot(1+CN)=\frac{I_{\text{dark}}\cdot R_{\text{q}}}{(V_{\text{bias}}-V_{\text{off}})\cdot\Delta t}\,.

(7)

The turn-off voltage has been determined using charge histograms, while the recovery time constant for the TAPD of
\tau=$4.5\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{s}$
is taken from
[
8
]
and for the MP15
\tau=$11\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{s}$
as described in
[
14
]
.

4
Conclusion

We analyzed a promising SiPM prototype before and after neutron irradiation and compared it to the MP15, a SiPM with a conventional, planar
p-n
pixel design. At a fluence of
\Phi_{\text{eq}}=$1\text{\times}{10}^{12}\text{\,}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-2}$
, we found a comparable increase in quenching resistance and an increase of the breakdown voltage of the TAPD by
0.16(\pm 0.06)\text{\,}\%
. The increase in dark count rate with irradiation is in the order of
1\text{\times}{10}^{3}\text{\,}
. For comparison, the dark count rate of the MP15 increases by a factor of
1\text{\times}{10}^{5}\text{\,}
at the same temperature and overvoltage. This increased radiation hardness of the TAPD may be attributed to the geometry of the high-field region, which is roughly a factor of 3 smaller than that of the MP15.
