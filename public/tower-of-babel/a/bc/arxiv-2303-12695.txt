Adaptive Conformal Prediction by Reweighting Nonconformity Scores
Salim I. Amoukou, Nicolas J-B. Brunel

Abstract
Despite attractive theoretical guarantees and practical successes, Predictive Interval (PI) given by Conformal Prediction (CP) may not reflect the uncertainty of a given model. This limitation arises from CP methods using a constant correction for all test points, disregarding their individual uncertainties, to ensure coverage properties. To address this issue, we propose using a Quantile Regression Forest (QRF) to learn the distribution of nonconformity scores and utilizing the QRF’s weights to assign more importance to samples with residuals similar to the test point. This approach results in PI lengths that are more aligned with the model’s uncertainty. In addition, the weights learnt by the QRF provide a partition of the features space, allowing for more efficient computations and improved adaptiveness of the PI through groupwise conformalization. Our approach enjoys an assumption-free finite sample marginal and training-conditional coverage, and under suitable assumptions, it also ensures conditional coverage. Our methods work for any nonconformity score and are available as a Python package . We conduct experiments on simulated and real-world data that show significant improvements compared to existing methods.



1
Motivations

Machine learning techniques offer single point predictions, such as mean estimates for regression and class labels for classification, without providing any indication of uncertainty or reliability. This can be a major concern in high-stakes applications where precision is vital.

Consider a training set
\small\mathcal{D}_{m}=\{(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}
with
\small(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},Y_{i})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}
drawn exchangeably from
\small P=P_{\boldsymbol{X}}P_{Y|\boldsymbol{X}}
, and an algorithm
\small\mathcal{A}
that gives conditional mean or quantile estimate
\small\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}_{m})=\widehat{\mu}(\cdot)
. We consider the problem of constructing a predictive set
C(\cdot)
for the unseen response
\small Y_{n+1}
given a new feature
\small\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1}
. Conformal Prediction is a universal framework that constructs a prediction interval
\small C(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})
that covert
\small Y_{n+1}
with finite-sample (non-asymptotic) coverage guarantee without any assumption on
\small P
and
\small\widehat{\mu}
. CP methods can be broadly divided into two categories: those that involve retraining the model multiple times, such as full conformal
(
Vovk et al. 2005
)
or jackknife methods
(
Barber et al. 2021
)
, and those that use sample splitting, known as split conformal methods
(
Papadopoulos et al. 2002
;
Lei et al. 2016
)
. The latter is more computationally feasible at the cost of splitting the data. In this paper, we consider the split conformal approach (split-CP).

The foundation of the PI of the CP framework is the nonconformity score
\small\widehat{V}(\boldsymbol{X},Y)
that represents the error of the model
\small\widehat{\mu}
on
(\boldsymbol{X},Y)
. Given a calibration set
\small\mathcal{D}_{n}=\{(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
independent of the training set
\small\mathcal{D}_{m}=\{(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}
, and the scores
\small\widehat{V}_{i}:=\widehat{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},Y_{i})
for all
i\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
, the PI of
\small\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1}
at level
1-\alpha
given by the split-CP is:

\displaystyle\small C(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}:\widehat{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1},y)\leq\mathcal{Q}\left(1-\alpha;\;\widehat{F}_{n+1}\right)\right\},

(1)

where
\small\mathcal{Q}(1-\alpha;\;F)
denotes the
\small(1-\alpha)
-quantile of any cumulative distribution function (c.d.f)
F
, and
\small\widehat{F}_{n+1}(\cdot)
is the empirical c.d.f of the samples
\small\widehat{V}_{1:n}\cup\infty
defined as
\small\widehat{F}_{n+1}(r)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n+1}\mathds{1}_{\widehat{V}_{i}\leq r}+\frac{1}{n+1}\mathds{1}_{\infty\leq r}
.
By exchangeability of the
n+1
data points
(\boldsymbol{X}_{1},Y_{1}),\dots,(\boldsymbol{X}_{n},Y_{n}),(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1},Y_{n+1})
, we can show that the PI has marginal coverage, i.e.,

\displaystyle\small P^{n+1}\left(Y_{n+1}\in C(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})\right)\geq 1-\alpha.

P^{n+1}
denotes that the probability is taken with respect to the
\small n+1
data points and
\small\alpha\in(0,1)
is a predefined miscoverage rate. However, despite the marginal guarantees, split-CP cannot represent the variability of the model’s uncertainty given
\small\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1}
. Indeed, it constructs the PI of future test points
\small\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1}
through the uniform distribution over the calibration residuals
\small\widehat{F}_{n+1}(\cdot)
that treat all the calibration residuals as the same regardless of
\small\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1}
. To better illustrate the issue, consider a simple example where the true distribution of
Y
is homoskedastic, meaning that
\small Y=\mu(\boldsymbol{X})+\epsilon
, where
\small\boldsymbol{X}
and
\small\epsilon
are independent. In this case, the true residuals of the calibration samples
\small V_{i}:=V(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},Y_{i})=|Y_{i}-\mu(\boldsymbol{X}_{i})|=|\epsilon|
are independent of
\small\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
and
\small V_{i}\sim|\epsilon|
for
i\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
. Hence, we have
\small F_{V}(\cdot)=F_{V|\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x}}(\cdot)
. However, in practice, we only have the estimated residuals,
\small\widehat{V}_{i}:=\widehat{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},Y_{i})=|Y_{i}-\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i})|=|\mu(\boldsymbol{X}_{i})-\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i})+\epsilon|
, which do depend on
\small\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
as the accuracy of
\small\widehat{\mu}
can vary for different
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
. For example, if
\small\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
is in a high density region with a large amount of data,
\small\widehat{\mu}
is likely to be more accurate, while in a low density region with a small amount of data,
\small\widehat{\mu}
is likely to be less accurate. In contrast of the true residual, the conditional law of the estimated residuals
\small\widehat{V}|\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x}
is not equal to the marginal law of
\small\widehat{V}
, thus using the latter
\small F_{\widehat{V}}(\cdot)
as in split-CP to construct the PI of a given observation
\small\boldsymbol{x}
may produce under/over coverage PI as
\small\mathcal{Q}\left(1-\alpha;\;F_{\widehat{V}}\right)
may be greater or lower than
\small\mathcal{Q}\left(1-\alpha;\;F_{\widehat{V}|\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x}}\right)
.

Our goal is to construct Prediction Intervals (PIs) with valid coverage for the model of interest
\small\widehat{\mu}
, while adjusting the width of the intervals to help visualize and understand the source of uncertainty of the model
\small\widehat{\mu}
. In fact, the split-CP uses a constant correction term
\small\mathcal{Q}\left(1-\alpha;\;\widehat{F}_{n+1}\right)
for all test samples, while we aim to have an adaptive correction term that depends on the specific test observation
\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1}
. To achieve this, we propose to directly estimate the conditional distribution of the nonconformity score given
\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1}
by re-weighting the distribution
\small\widehat{F}_{n+1}(\cdot)
in order to favor the residuals
\{\widehat{V}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}
closer to the residual of
\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1}
. In Figure
1
, we show the correction terms of split-CP, our method, and the true error of the model
\small\widehat{\mu}
computed on california house price dataset
(
Kelley Pace and Barry 1997
)
. It shows that our corrections are more aligned with the true error of the model.

Figure 1:
Correction terms of SPLIT, Ours, and the true error on california house price dataset

We also aim to give PI with stronger coverage guarantee. Indeed, in practical applications, what is of interest is the coverage rate on future test points based on a given calibration set. However, the marginal coverage does not address this concern. It only bounds the coverage rate on average over all possible sets of calibration and test observations. In contrast, the training-conditional coverage ensures that with probability
1-\delta
over the calibration samples
\mathcal{D}_{n}
, the resulting coverage on future test observation is still above
1-\alpha
. Formally,

\displaystyle\small P^{n}\left(P\left(Y_{n+1}\in C(X_{n+1})\;|\;\mathcal{D}_{n}\right)\geq 1-\alpha\right)\geq 1-\delta.

This style of guarantee is also known as “Probably Approximately Correct” (PAC) predictive interval
(
Valiant 1984
)
. The roots of this type of guarantee can be traced back to the earlier works of
(
Wilks 1941
;
Wald 1943
)
. Despite the importance of training-conditional coverage in practice, only a few methods have been proven to achieve it.
Vovk 2012
was the first to establish this result for split conformal methods, and recently
Bian and Barber 2022
has shown that the K-fold CV+ method also achieves it. However, no analogous results are currently known for other CP methods, such as jackknife+
(
Barber et al. 2021
)
and full-conformal
(
Vovk et al. 2005
)
. Therefore, we propose a further calibration step such that our proposed adaptive PI also achieves training-conditional coverage.

There is another area of research that focuses on developing CP procedures for conditional coverage
\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1}\in C(X_{n+1})\;|\;\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1}=\boldsymbol{x})\geq 1-\alpha
. It is well known that obtaining nontrivial distribution-free conditional coverage is impossible with a finite sample
(
Lei and Wasserman 2014
;
Vovk 2012
)
. Consequently, we prove under suitable assumptions that our methods also achieve asymptotic conditional coverage.

2
Related works and contributions

For the sake of simplicity, we use the absolute residual as the nonconformity score
\small\widehat{V}_{i}:=\widehat{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},Y_{i})=|Y_{i}-\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i})|
, without loss of generality. As a result, the best (symmetric) PI that can be constructed with
\small\widehat{\mu}(\cdot)
and the score
\small\widehat{V}(\cdot)
is
\small C^{\star}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})=\big[\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})\pm q^{\star}_{1-\alpha}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})\big]
where
\small q^{\star}_{1-\alpha}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})
is the
\small(1-\alpha)
-quantile of
\small F_{\widehat{V}_{n+1}|\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1}}
. To construct adaptive PIs, we propose focusing on the estimated residuals of the calibration samples
\small\{\widehat{V}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}
, and approximate the distribution of
\small\widehat{V}|\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x}
or identify the stable regions
A
where
\small Var(\widehat{V}(\boldsymbol{X},Y)\;|\;\boldsymbol{X}\in A)\approx 0
, which would allow us to isolate the regions where there is high/low uncertainty of the model.

Recently,
Guan 2022
proposed Localized Conformal Prediction (LCP) and
Han et al. 2022
inspired by
(
Lin et al. 2021
)
proposed Split Localized Conformal Prediction (SLCP) which uses kernel-based weights
\small w_{h}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},x)
or Nadaraya-Watson (NW) estimator
(
Nadaraya 1964
)
to approximate the conditional c.d.f of
\small\widehat{V}|\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x}
. Both methods differ in how they learn the NW estimator, SLCP uses the training data
\small\mathcal{D}_{m}
to learn the estimator
\small\widehat{F}^{(S)}_{h}(r|\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{m}}w_{h}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{X}_{i})\mathds{1}_{\widehat{V}_{i}\leq r}
, while LCP uses the calibration data
\small\mathcal{D}_{n}
to learn the estimator
\small\widehat{F}^{(L)}_{h}(r|\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}w_{h}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{X}_{i})\mathds{1}_{\widehat{V}_{i}\leq r}
. The calibration step of these two methods is also different. The PI of
\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1}
given by SLCP is:

\displaystyle\small C^{S}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})=\Big[\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})\pm\mathcal{Q}\left(1-\alpha;\;\widehat{F}^{(S)}_{h}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})\right)+{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\widehat{Q}}\Big]

where
\small{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\widehat{Q}}
is the split-CP correction term to achieve marginal coverage. In constrast, LCP does not use split-CP but instead adapts the threshold
\small{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\tilde{\alpha}}=1-\alpha
in
\small\mathcal{Q}\left(1-\alpha;\;\widehat{F}^{(L)}_{h}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})\right)
to achieve the marginal coverage. LCP constructs the predictive interval for a new point
\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1}
as follows:

\displaystyle\small C^{L}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})=\Big[\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})\pm\mathcal{Q}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\tilde{\alpha}};\;\widehat{F}^{(L)}_{h}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})\right)\Big]

where
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\tilde{\alpha}}
is chosen to achieve the marginal coverage.
However, while both LCP and SLCP address the problem and guarantee marginal coverage, they have some limitations. A main limitation is that they are based on kernel methods, which are known to be limited in high dimensions due to the curse of dimensionality. Additionally, choosing the appropriate kernel bandwidth can be challenging and it can be difficult to define kernels that handle both categorical and continuous variables. Another limitation of SLCP is that it learns
\small\widehat{F}^{(S)}_{h}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x})
on the training data
\small\mathcal{D}_{m}
, which may result in overfitting and thus the calibration step using split-CP may produce large intervals to attain the marginal coverage. In contrast, LCP learns
\small\widehat{F}^{(L)}_{h}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x})
on the calibration data
\mathcal{D}_{n}
, but the calibration step that consists of finding the adaptive
\tilde{\alpha}
is computationally costly.

In this work, we propose to replace the Nadaraya-Watson (NW) estimator with the Quantile Regression Forest (QRF) algorithm
(
Meinshausen and Ridgeway 2006
)
to estimate the distribution
\widehat{V}|\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x}
and use the LCP approach to calibrate the PI. The QRF algorithm is an adaptation of the Random Forest (RF) algorithm
(
Breiman et al. 1984
)
, which can be seen as an adaptive neighborhood procedure
(
Lin and Jeon 2006
)
. It estimates the conditional c.d.f of
\widehat{V}|\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x}
as
\widehat{F}(r|\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i}w_{n}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{X}_{i})\mathds{1}_{\widehat{V}_{i}\leq r}
where the weights correspond to the average number of times where
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
falls in the same leaves of the RF as the observation
\boldsymbol{x}
. Unlike kernel-based methods, the weights given by the RF depend on both feature input
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
and the residual
\widehat{V}_{i}
due to the splits. We called this approach LCP-RF. This estimator has several advantages over the NW estimator. First, it is known to perform well in practice, even in high dimensions. It can handle both categorical and continuous variables. Additionally, it has interesting theoretical properties in high dimensions; under certain assumptions, it can be shown to be consistent and to adapt to the intrinsic dimension
(
Klusowski 2021
;
Scornet et al. 2015
)
. To illustrate, we compute the PI of these methods using a random forest fitted on a toy model with input
\boldsymbol{X}\in[0,7]^{21}
, and the target defined as
Y=\sin(X_{1})^{2}+0.1+0.6\times\epsilon\times\sin(2X_{1})
, where
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and
X_{i}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,7)
for all
i\in[21]
. As seen in Figure
2
, the competitors LCP and SLCP fail to perform even on this very simple example with 1 active and 20 noise features, while our method benefits from the power of the Random Forest algorithm on tabular data
(
Grinsztajn et al. 2022
)
.

Figure 2:
Predictive interval at level
1-\alpha
of SLCP, LCP and LCP-RF of a random Forest fitted on toy model
(\boldsymbol{X},Y),\boldsymbol{X}\in[0,7]^{21}
and the target is defined as
Y=\sin(X_{1})^{2}+0.1+0.6\times\epsilon\times\sin(2X_{1})
with
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and
X_{i}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,7)
for all
i\in[21].

Additionally, we show that the learned weights of the RF can be used to create a relevant partition or groups/clusters of the input space. This allows for a more efficient computation of the LCP calibration and also allows for groupwise conformalization to give a more adaptive PI. In practice, it is often desirable to have a stronger coverage guarantee than marginal coverage. Consequently, we propose a further calibration step such that our PI satisfies training-conditional coverage. We also show that it achieves conditional coverage guarantee under suitable assumptions.

An active area of research involves using a better nonconformity score to provide an adaptive prediction interval considering the variability of
\small Y|\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x}
. Several methods have been proposed such as Conformal Quantile Regression (CQR)
(
Romano et al. 2019
)
, which uses score functions based on estimated quantiles, Locally Adaptive Split Conformal methods
(
Lei et al. 2016
)
which use a scaled residual, and
Izbicki et al. 2020
proposed using the estimated conditional density as the conformity score. These methods incorporate different nonconformity scores
\small\widehat{V}(\cdot)
that are better suited for handling the variability of
\small Y
. However, the extracted residuals
\widehat{V}_{i}
of these nonconformity scores still depend on and vary according to
\small\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1}
. These methods are not competing with the LCP-RF approach as it can be applied to them to improve their PIs.

The main contributions of this paper are: (1) Developing an adaptive PI that better represents the uncertainty of a given model
\small\widehat{\mu}
by using a QRF to learn the conditional distribution of the residuals
\small\widehat{V}(\boldsymbol{X},Y)
, and utilizing the LCP framework to calibrate the resulting PI for marginal coverage, (2) Introducing a calibration step to achieve training-conditional coverage, (3) Exploiting the structure of the weights of the QRF to create groups for more adaptive PI and efficient computation through groupwise conformalization, (4) Showing that our methods achieve asymptotic conditional coverage under suitable conditions, (5) Demonstrating through simulations and real-world datasets that our methods outperform competitors LCP and SLCP, and providing a
Python package
for the methods.

3
Random Forest Localizer

In this section, we present the Random Forest Localizer for constructing adaptive PI that depends on the test point
\small\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1}
. The approach utilizes the learned weights of the RF and assigns higher weights to calibration samples that have residuals
\small\widehat{V}_{i}
similar to
\small\widehat{V}_{n+1}
. This is based on the RF algorithm’s ability to partition the input space by recursively splitting the data, resulting in similar observations with respect to the target variable (here the residuals) within each leaf node of the trees. The basic idea of the trees of the RF is to partition the input space into cells such that
\small Var(\widehat{V}(\boldsymbol{X},Y)\;|\;\boldsymbol{X}\in A)\approx 0
in each cell
\small A
. The corresponding weight of each calibration sample for
\small\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1}
is determined by the number of times it appears in the leaves of the trees where
\small\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1}
falls.

Random Forest (RF) is an ensemble learning method that utilizes the bagging principle
(
Breiman 1996
)
to combine k randomized trees derived from the CART algorithm
(
Breiman et al. 1984
)
. Each tree is constructed using a random sample of training data with replacement, and the best split at every node is identified by optimizing the CART-criterion among a random subset of variables. The predictions from all trees are then averaged to produce the final output of the forest. The Random Forest estimator can also be seen as an adaptive neighborhood procedure
(
Lin and Jeon 2006
)
. Let assume we have trained the RF on
\small\mathcal{D}_{n}
, then for every instance
\boldsymbol{x}
, the observations in
\small\mathcal{D}_{n}
are weighted by
\small w_{n}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{X}_{i})
,
i=1,\dots,n
. Therefore, the prediction of Random Forests and the weights can be rewritten as
\small m(\boldsymbol{x},\Theta_{1},\dots,\Theta_{k},\mathcal{D}_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{n}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{X}_{i})Y_{i}
and

\displaystyle\small w_{n}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{X}_{i})=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\frac{B_{n}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i};\Theta_{l})\;\mathds{1}_{\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\in A_{n}(\boldsymbol{x};\;\Theta_{l})}}{k\times N_{n}(\boldsymbol{x};\;\Theta_{l})}

where
\small\Theta_{l},l=1,\dots,k
are independent random vectors that represent the observations that are used to build each tree, i.e. the bootstrap samples, and the random subset of variables used in each node.
\small A_{n}(\boldsymbol{x};\;\Theta_{l})
is the tree cell (leaf) containing
\small\boldsymbol{x}
,
\small N_{n}(\boldsymbol{x};\;\Theta_{l})
is the number of bootstrap elements that fall into
\small A_{n}(\boldsymbol{x};\;\Theta_{l})
,
\small B_{n}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i};\;\Theta_{l})
is the number of times
\small\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
has been chosen from the training data, and
\small w_{n}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{X}_{i})
represents the average number of times
\small\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
appears in the same leaves as
\small\boldsymbol{x}
.

Random Forests can be used to estimate more complex quantities, such as cumulative hazard function
(
Ishwaran et al. 2008
)
, treatment effect
(
Wager and Athey 2017
)
, and conditional density
(
Du et al. 2021
)
. Quantile Regression Forests proposed by
Meinshausen and Ridgeway 2006
use the same weights
\small w_{n}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{X}_{i})
as Random Forests to approximate the c.d.f
\small F(y|\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x})
as:

\small\widehat{F}(y|\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{n}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{X}_{i})\mathds{1}_{Y_{i}\leq y}

(2)

Random Forest Localizer.
To approximate the estimated residuals
\widehat{V}|\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x}
, we propose to fit a Quantile Regression Forest
\widehat{F}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{x})
on the calibration data
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{n}=\{(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},\widehat{V}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and the estimator is defined as
\small\widehat{F}(r|\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}w_{n}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{X}_{i})\mathds{1}_{\widehat{V}_{i}\leq r}
, where
\widehat{V}_{n+1}=+\infty
unless specified. It’s worth noting that this estimator is slightly different from (
2
), as it includes the observation
\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1}
in the weighted sum. We will see later that this addition would be essential to prove the marginal coverage property of our method. Using this estimator, a natural PI for
\widehat{V}_{n+1}
is:

\displaystyle\small C_{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})=\left\{v:v\leq\mathcal{Q}\left(1-\alpha;\;\widehat{F}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})\right)\right\}

(3)

Recall that we obtain the prediction interval
\small C(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})
for
\small Y_{n+1}
by inverting the nonconformity score
\widehat{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1},\cdot)
using
C_{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})
as in equation (
1
). Thus, the real quantity of interest is
\small C_{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})
. The question at hand is whether the PI
\small C_{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})
defined in (
3
) satisfies marginal coverage. If
\small w_{n}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{X}_{i})=\frac{1}{n+1}
, we have
\small\mathcal{Q}\left(1-\alpha;\;\widehat{F}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})\right)=\widehat{V}_{(\lceil(1-\alpha)(n+1)\rceil)}
and thanks to the quantile lemma
(
Tibshirani et al. 2019
)
and exchangeability of the
\widehat{V}_{i}
, we have the marginal coverage. However, If
\small\widehat{F}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})
gives non-equal weights to the calibration samples, it is no longer the case. Recent methods have been proposed by
Tibshirani et al. 2019
and
Barber et al. 2022
that achieve marginal coverage when using reweighting. However, these methods cannot be applied to calibrate our PI, as they work under different assumptions. The method introduced by
Barber et al. 2022
assumes that the weights do not depend on the data, while the method proposed by
Tibshirani et al. 2019
handles data-dependent weights but assumes a covariate shift, where the training and test data have different input distributions but the same conditional distribution
P_{Y|\boldsymbol{X}}
.

To calibrate our PI, we use the Localized Conformal Prediction (LCP) framework
(
Guan 2022
)
to select an appropriate level
\tilde{\alpha}
of the quantile used in the PI (
3
) to ensure marginal coverage at level
1-\alpha
. Hence, the PI becomes

\displaystyle\small C_{V}(X_{n+1})=\left\{v:v\leq\mathcal{Q}\left(\tilde{\alpha};\;\widehat{F}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})\right)\right\}.

(4)

4
Weighted Conformal Prediction

In this section, we give a comprehensive overview of the LCP framework of
Guan 2022
with the Random Forest Localizer for completeness. Additionally, we describe our calibration approach that guarantees training-conditional coverage, and how we leverage the weights of the RF to improve the LCP calibration process and produce more adaptive prediction intervals. For ease of reading, we follow
Guan 2022
and introduce
\small\mathcal{F}_{i}=\widehat{F}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{X}_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{n}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},\boldsymbol{X}_{j})\mathds{1}_{\widehat{V}_{j}\leq\cdot}+w_{n}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})\mathds{1}_{\widehat{V}_{n+1}\leq\cdot}

as the estimated c.d.f of
\small\widehat{V}
given
\small\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
given by the Quantile Regression Forest. As
\widehat{V}_{n+1}
is not observed and we need to consider the possible values of
\small\widehat{V}_{n+1}
for constructing the PI, we introduce the additional notations
\small\mathcal{F}_{i}^{v}
for the c.d.f when
\small\widehat{V}_{n+1}=v
if
v
is finite, and
\small\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{n+1}^{\infty}
if
\small\widehat{V}_{n+1}=+\infty
.

4.1
Localized Conformal Prediction
(
Guan 2022
)

The following lemma is the cornerstone of the LCP framework. It shows how to achieve marginal coverage by properly selecting the level
\tilde{\alpha}
of the quantile of the localizer.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\tilde{\alpha}
be the smallest value in
\small\Gamma=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{k}w_{n}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},\boldsymbol{X}_{j}):i=1,\dots,n+1;\;k=1,\dots,n+1\right\}
such that

\small\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\frac{1}{n+1}\mathds{1}_{\widehat{V}_{i}\leq\mathcal{Q}(\tilde{\alpha};\;\mathcal{F}_{i})}\geq 1-\alpha,

then
\small P^{n+1}\left(\widehat{V}_{n+1}\leq\mathcal{Q}(\tilde{\alpha};\;\mathcal{F}_{n+1})\right)\geq 1-\alpha
, or equivalently
\small P^{n+1}\left(\widehat{V}_{n+1}\leq\mathcal{Q}(\tilde{\alpha};\;\mathcal{F})\right)\geq 1-\alpha
.

Remember that both
\small\tilde{\alpha}
and
\small\mathcal{F}_{n+1}
depends on
\small\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{n}=\{(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},\widehat{V}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\small(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1},\widehat{V}_{n+1})
, but we won’t specify them for clarity.
Now, we can use Lemma
4.1
to test
\small H_{0}:\widehat{V}_{n+1}=v
for each
\small v\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}
under exchangeability, then invert the test to construct the PI.
\small C_{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})
consists of all values
v
that are not rejected by this test. The resulting PI has marginal coverage as shown in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.2
.

At
\small\widehat{V}_{n+1}=v
, let define
\small\tilde{\alpha}
that depends on
\small\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{n}
and
\small(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1},v)
to be the smallest value
\small\tilde{\alpha}\in\Gamma
such that

\small\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\frac{1}{n+1}\mathds{1}_{\widehat{V}_{i}\leq\mathcal{Q}(\tilde{\alpha};\;\mathcal{F}^{v}_{i})}\geq 1-\alpha.

Set
\small C_{V}(V_{n+1})=\left\{v:v\leq\mathcal{Q}\left(\tilde{\alpha};\;\mathcal{F}\right)\right\}
and
\small C(Y_{n+1})=\left\{y:\widehat{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1},y)\leq\mathcal{Q}\left(\tilde{\alpha};\;\mathcal{F}\right)\right\}
, then by construction, Lemma
4.1
gives
\small P^{n+1}\left(Y_{n+1}\in C(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})\right)=P^{n+1}\left(\widehat{V}_{n+1}\in C_{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})\right)=P^{n+1}\left(\widehat{V}_{n+1}\leq\mathcal{Q}\left(\tilde{\alpha};\;\mathcal{F}\right)\right)\geq 1-\alpha
.

At this point, the LCP method is not practical as it requires computing
\small\tilde{\alpha}
for every possible value of
\small v\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}
in order to construct the prediction interval. This process can be extremely time-consuming and computationally intensive. However,
Guan 2022
shows that the computation of
\small C_{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})
can be done efficiently thanks to its interesting properties. Specifically, if
\small v
is accepted in
\small C_{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})
, all
\small v^{\prime}\leq v
are also accepted. Hence, it is sufficient to find the largest accepted value
v^{\star}
. Additionally, as
\small\mathcal{Q}(\tilde{\alpha};\;\mathcal{F}_{i}^{v})
is non-decreasing in both
\small\tilde{\alpha}
and
\small v
, and piece-wise constant in
\small\tilde{\alpha}
, with value changes only occurring at different
\small\widehat{V}_{i}
, it can be proven that the largest value is attained by one of the estimated residuals
\small\widehat{V}_{k^{\star}}
with
\small k^{\star}\in[n]
. Therefore, the closure
\small\bar{C}_{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})
of
\small C_{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})
is given by
\small\bar{C}_{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})=\left\{v:v\leq\widehat{V}_{k^{\star}}\right\}
for some
\small k^{\star}\in[n]
. The following Lemma shows how to find
V_{k^{\star}}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

We denote
\small\widehat{V}_{(1)},\dots,\widehat{V}_{(n)}
the order statistics of the nonconformity score of the calibration samples, set
\small\widehat{V}_{(n+1)}=+\infty
, and
\small\tilde{\theta}_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{n}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{X}_{i})\mathds{1}_{\widehat{V}_{i}<\widehat{V}_{(k)}}
. Let
\small k^{\star}\in\{1,\dots,n+1\}
the largest index such that

\small S(k):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n+1}\mathds{1}_{\widehat{V}_{i}\leq\mathcal{Q}\left(\tilde{\theta}_{k^{\star}};\;\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\widehat{V}_{(k^{\star})}}\right)}<\alpha.

(5)

Then,
\bar{C}_{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})=\left\{v:v\leq\widehat{V}_{(k^{\star})}\right\}
is the closure of
\small C_{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})
.

Guan 2022
also proposed an algorithm that computed
\small S(k)
in
\small\mathcal{O}(n\log(n))
time. The description of the algorithm can be found in the original paper.

4.2
Training-Conditional coverage for LCP-RF

Here, we consider training-conditional coverage or PAC predictive interval guarantees for the LCP-RF. Let’s consider the coverage rate given a calibration set
\mathcal{D}_{n}
as
\small\text{cov}(\mathcal{D}_{n})=P(\widehat{V}_{n+1}\in C_{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})\;|\;\mathcal{D}_{n})
where the probability is taken with respect to the test point
(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1},\widehat{V}_{n+1})
. The PAC predictive interval ensures that for most draws of the calibration samples
\mathcal{D}_{n}\sim P^{n}
, we have
\text{cov}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\geq 1-\alpha
. Formally,
\exists\delta
s.t.
P^{n}\left(\text{cov}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\geq 1-\alpha\right)\geq 1-\delta
.

We use a two-step approach to ensure training-conditional coverage for the LCP-RF. First, we use a portion of the calibration samples to ensure marginal coverage by applying the LCP approach. Next, we use a separate portion of the calibration samples to learn a correction term, which is then added to the LCP-RF approach to ensure training-conditional coverage. This approach is similar to the one used in
(
Kivaranovic et al. 2020
)
. We split the calibration set
\small\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{n}
into two sets
\small\widehat{\mathcal{D}}^{i}_{n_{i}}=\left\{(\boldsymbol{X}^{i}_{1},\widehat{V}^{i}_{1}),\dots,(\boldsymbol{X}^{i}_{n_{i}},\widehat{V}^{i}_{n_{i}})\right\}
for
\small i=1,2
with
\small n_{1}+n_{2}=n
. We train the Quantile Regression Forest on
\small\widehat{\mathcal{D}}^{1}_{n_{1}}
, and compute PI for the observations in the second set
\small\widehat{\mathcal{D}}^{2}_{n_{2}}
using the LCP-RF. The PI of each
\small i\in\widehat{\mathcal{D}}^{2}_{n_{2}}
is
\small C_{V}(\boldsymbol{X}^{2}_{i})=\left\{v:v\leq\mathcal{Q}(\tilde{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{X}^{2}_{i});\;\mathcal{F}^{2,\infty}_{i})\right\}
, where
\small\tilde{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{X}^{2}_{i})
is the adapted level
\small\tilde{\alpha}
to have marginal coverage if
\small\boldsymbol{X}^{2}_{i}
is the test point and
\small\mathcal{F}^{2,\infty}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}+1}w_{n}(\boldsymbol{X}^{2}_{i},\boldsymbol{X}^{1}_{j})\mathds{1}_{\widehat{V}^{1}_{j}\leq V}
is the estimated residual distribution learn on
\small\mathcal{D}^{1}_{n_{1}}
evalued on
\small\boldsymbol{X}^{2}_{i}
where we set
\small\boldsymbol{X}^{1}_{n_{1}+1}=\boldsymbol{X}^{2}_{i}
and
\small\widehat{V}^{1}_{n_{1}+1}=+\infty
. The following lemma shows how we can correct the corresponding
\small\tilde{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{X}^{2}_{i})
by adding a correction term
\small{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\widehat{\alpha}}
to ensure PAC coverage.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\epsilon>0
,
\alpha-\epsilon>0
and
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\widehat{\alpha}}
be the smalest value in
T=\{\alpha_{0}=0,\dots,\alpha_{K}=\alpha\}
s.t.

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_{2}}\frac{1}{n_{2}}\mathds{1}_{\widehat{V}^{2}_{i}\leq\mathcal{Q}\left(\tilde{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{X}^{2}_{i})+{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\widehat{\alpha}};\;\mathcal{F}^{2,\infty}_{i}\right)}\geq 1-\alpha.

(6)

Then, we have
P^{n_{1}}\left\{\text{cov}(\mathcal{D}_{n_{1}})\geq 1-\alpha-\epsilon\right\}\geq 1-\delta
with
\delta=K\exp(-2n_{2}\epsilon^{2})
and
\text{cov}(\mathcal{D}_{n_{1}})=P\left\{\widehat{V}_{n+1}\leq\mathcal{Q}\left(\tilde{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})+{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\widehat{\alpha}};\;\mathcal{F}^{\infty}_{n+1}\right)\;\big|\;D_{n_{1}}\right\}
.

Remark.
This result is valid under the i.i.d assumption and not under exchangeability as the other results of the paper. We suggest choosing a grid
T\subset[0,\alpha]
as we have observed in most practical scenarios that
\tilde{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})\approx 1-\alpha
. However, the central idea remains unaltered - to select a grid that enables transitioning from
\tilde{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})
to 1. Additionally, as
\tilde{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})+{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\widehat{\alpha}}
may be above
1
, we define
\tilde{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})+{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\widehat{\alpha}}:=(\tilde{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})+{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\widehat{\alpha}})\lor 1
.

4.3
Clustering using the weights of LCP-RF

In this section, we analyze the weights of the Random Forest Localizer and show that it offers several benefits compared to traditional kernel-based localizer. These benefits include faster computation and more adaptive PIs. One key difference between the RF localizer and kernel-based localizer is that the RF localizer’s weights are sparse, i.e., many weights being zero. For a given test point
\small\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1}
, if
\small w_{n}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{X}_{i})=0
, then the estimated c.d.f
\small\mathcal{F}_{i}
does not depend on the value of
\small\widehat{V}_{n+1}
. Thus, it may not be necessary to use
\mathcal{F}_{i}
in the LCP’s marginal calibration (eq.
5
in Lemma
4.3
).

The weights defined by the Random Forest Localizer have a structure that can be utilized to group similar observations together before applying the calibration steps. Indeed, we can view the weights of the RF on the calibration set as a transition matrix or a weighted adjacency matrix
\small G
where
\small G_{i,j}=w_{n}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},\boldsymbol{X}_{j})
and
\small\forall j\in[n],
we have
\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{n}(\boldsymbol{X}_{j},\boldsymbol{X}_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{n}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},\boldsymbol{X}_{j})=1.

To exploit this structure, we propose to group observations that are connected to each other and separate observations that are not connected. This can be done by considering the connected components of the graph represented by the matrix
G
. Assume that
G
has
L
connected components represented by the disjoint sets of vertices
\small G_{1},\dots,G_{L}
, defined such that for any
\small\boldsymbol{X}_{i},\boldsymbol{X}_{j}\in G_{l}
, there is a path from
\small\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
to
\small\boldsymbol{X}_{j}
, and they are connected to no other vertices outside the vertices in
\small G_{l}
. This leads to the existence of a partition of the input space
\small\cup_{i=1}^{L}R_{i}=\mathcal{X}
, where
\small\forall k,l\in[L],R_{l}\cap R_{q}=\varnothing
, and for all
\small\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\in R_{p},X_{j}\in R_{q}
, we have
\small w_{n}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},\boldsymbol{X}_{j})=0
. The regions
\small R_{i}
is defined as
\small R_{i}=\big\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\exists\boldsymbol{X}\in G_{i},w_{n}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{X})>0\text{ and }\forall\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\in G_{k},k\neq l,\;w_{n}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime})=0\big\}
.
By definition of the weights, we can also define
R_{i}
using the leaves of the RF as
\small R_{i}=\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\in G_{i}}\Big[\cup_{l=1}^{k}A_{n}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},\Theta_{l})\Big]
.
This shows that the
R_{i}
are connected space. Hence, we can apply the conformalization steps separately on each group and use only the observations that are connected to the test point. By using the conformalization by group, we reduce the computation of
S(k)
in Lemma
4.3
needed for the computation of the PI from
\small\mathcal{O}(n\log(n))
to
\small\mathcal{O}\left(\big|\textbf{R}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})\big|\log\big|\textbf{R}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})\big|\right)
, since we only use the observations in the region where
\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1}
belongs in the calibration step. This results in a more accurate and efficient PI. In addition, no coverage guarantees are lost as the
R_{i}
forms a partition. We prove the marginal coverage of the group-wise LCP-RF in the appendix.

In some cases, the graph may have a single connected component. Consequently, we propose to regroup calibration observations by (non-overlapping) communities using the weights of the RF. This involves grouping the nodes (calibration samples) of the graph into communities such that nodes within the same community are strongly connected to each other and weakly connected to nodes in other groups. Various methods exist for detecting communities in graphs, such as hierarchical clustering, spectral clustering, random walk, label propagation, and modularity maximization. A comprehensive overview of these methods can be found in
(
Schaeffer 2007
)
. Nonetheless, it is challenging to determine the most suitable approach as the selection depends on the particular problem and characteristics of the graph. In our experiments, we found that the popular Louvain-Leiden
(
Traag et al. 2019
)
method coupled with Markov Stability
(
Delvenne et al. 2010
)
is effective in detecting communities of the learned weights of the Random Forest. However, any clustering method can be used depending on the specific application and dataset.

Let’s assume a graph-clustering algorithm
C
that returns L disjoint clusters
C(\mathcal{D}_{n})=\{C_{1},\dots,C_{L}\}
. Note that contrary to connected components, we can have
\boldsymbol{X}\in C_{i},\;\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\in C_{j}
and
w_{n}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime})\neq 0
, therefore it’s more difficult to define the associated regions
R_{1},\dots R_{L}
that form a partition of
\mathcal{X}
s.t. for any
\boldsymbol{X}\in C_{i}
, then
\boldsymbol{X}\in R_{i}
. We define
R_{i}
as the set of points
\boldsymbol{x}
that assigns the highest weights to the observations in cluster
C_{i}
. As
w
can be interpreted as a transition matrix, we define
R_{i}
as the set of
\boldsymbol{X}
such that
\widehat{p}(\boldsymbol{X}\in C_{i})>\widehat{p}(\boldsymbol{X}\in C_{k})
for all
k\neq i
, where the probability is computed using the weights of the forest. Formally,
R_{i}
can be represented as

\small R_{i}=\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\sum_{j\in C_{i}}w_{n}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{X}_{j})>\sum_{j\in C_{k}}w_{n}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{X}_{j}),\;k\neq i\right\}.

However, we also need to define another set for observations that are "undecidable", i.e., belong to several groups at the same time. We define this set as
\small\bar{R}=\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\exists k,l\in[L],\sum_{j\in C_{l}}w_{n}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{X}_{j})=\sum_{j\in C_{k}}w_{n}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{X}_{j})\right\}
. Finally, we get marginal/PAC coverage as we do with the connected components case by applying the calibration step conditionally on the groups
\small R_{1},\dots,R_{L}
and
\bar{R}
.

5
Asymptotic conditional coverage

Here, we study the conditional coverage of LCP-RF. It is widely recognized that obtaining meaningful distribution-free conditional coverage is impossible with a finite sample
(
Lei and Wasserman 2014
;
Vovk 2012
)
. Below, we demonstrate the asymptotic conditional coverage of LCP-RF while making weaker assumptions than the original LCP based on kernel
(
Guan 2022
)
.

Assumption 5.1
.

\small\forall r\in\mathbb{R}
, the c.d.f
\boldsymbol{x}\rightarrow\small F(r|\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x})
is continuous.

Assumption
5.1
is necessary to get uniform convergence of the RF estimator.

Assumption 5.2
.

For
l\in[k]
, the variation of the conditional cumulative distribution function within any cell goes to
0
, i.e.,
\small\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\forall r\in\mathbb{R},\sup_{\boldsymbol{z}\in A_{n}(\boldsymbol{x};\;\Theta_{l})}|F(r|\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{z}})-F(r|\boldsymbol{x})|\overset{a.s}{\to}0
.

Assumption
5.2
allows for control of the approximation error of the RF estimator.
Scornet et al. 2015
show that this is true when the data come from additive regression models
(
Stone 1985
)
, and
Elie-Dit-Cosaque and Maume-Deschamps 2022
show that it holds for a more general class, such as product functions or sums of product functions. This result also applies to all regression functions, with a slightly modified version of RF, where there are at least a fraction
\gamma
observations in child nodes and the number of splitting candidate variables is set to 1 at each node with a small probability. Therefore, we do not need to assume that for all
r
,
F(r|.)
is Lipschitz, as required in LCP
(
Guan 2022
)
, which is a much stronger assumption.

Assumption 5.3
.

Let
\small k
and the number of bootstrap observations in a leaf node
\small N_{n}(\boldsymbol{x};\;\Theta_{l})
, s.t. there exists
\small k=\mathcal{O}(n^{\alpha})
, with
\small\alpha>0
, and
\small\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
N_{n}(\boldsymbol{x};\;\Theta_{l})=\Omega(\sqrt{n}(ln(n))^{\beta})
1
1

1

\footnotesize f(n)=\Omega(g(n))\iff\exists k>0,\exists n_{0}>0,\forall n\geq n_{0},|f(n)|\geq|g(n)|.

, with
\small\beta>1
a.s.

Assumption
5.3
allows us to control the estimation error and means that the cells should contain a sufficiently large number of points so that averaging among the observations is effective. It can be enforced by adjusting the hyperparameters of the RF.

Under these assumptions, we prove that the selected
\small\tilde{\alpha}
when
\small\widehat{V}_{n+1}=v
given by the LCP-RF converges to
\small 1-\alpha
, and the resulting PI achieves the target level
\small 1-\alpha
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
\small\tilde{\alpha}
and
\small C_{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})
define as in Theorem
4.2
. Under assumptions
5.1
-
5.3
, we have
\small\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}P\left(\widehat{V}_{n+1}\in C_{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1})\;|\;\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1}\right)=1-\alpha
and for all
\small\epsilon>0
,
\small\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}P\left(\max_{v}|\tilde{\alpha}-(1-\alpha)|<\epsilon\;|\;\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1}\right)=1
a.s.

6
Experiments

We evaluate the performance of our proposed methods: LCP-RF (Random Forest Localizer with marginal and training-conditional calibration), LCP-RF-G (LCP-RF with groupwise calibration) and QRF-TC (Random Forest Localizer with only training-conditional calibration) against their competitors SPLIT (split-CP), SLCP and LCP. We used the original implementation of SLCP and LCP and tuned the kernel widths as described in their respective papers. We test the methods on simulated data with heterogeneous output and 3 real-world datasets from UCI
(
Dua and Graff 2017
)

We consider two nonconformity scores: mean score

7
Conclusion

In this work, we have significantly enhanced the applicability of the Localized Conformal Prediction framework, which previously only worked on simple models with fewer than five variables, by adapting it for high-dimensional scenarios, accommodating categorical variables, and providing a PAC coverage guarantee. Our reweighting strategy based on the Random Forest algorithm can improve the PI computed using any nonconformity score. This results in more adaptive PI with marginal, training-conditional, and conditional coverage, making Conformal Predictive Intervals more similar to those produced by traditional statistics. This may ease their interpretation in terms of risks and give a clearer relationship between the length of the PI and the uncertainties of a given model
\widehat{\mu}
, thereby allowing for a better understanding of the limitations of
\widehat{\mu}
.

Proof of Lemma

The Lemma
4.2
, which is the cornerstone of the LCP framework, shows how to achieve marginal coverage by properly selecting the level
\tilde{\alpha}

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\tilde{\alpha}
be the smallest value in
\Gamma=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{k}w_{n}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},\boldsymbol{X}_{j}):i=1,\dots,n;\;k=1,\dots,n\right\}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\frac{1}{n+1}\mathds{1}_{\widehat{V}_{i}\leq\mathcal{Q}(\tilde{\alpha};\;\mathcal{F}_{i})}\geq 1-\alpha,

(7)

then
\mathbb{P}\Bigl\{\widehat{V}_{n+1}\leq\mathcal{Q}(\tilde{\alpha};\;\mathcal{F}_{n+1})\Bigl\}\geq 1-\alpha
, or equivalently
\mathbb{P}\Bigl\{\widehat{V}_{n+1}\leq\mathcal{Q}(\tilde{\alpha};\;\mathcal{F})\Bigl\}\geq 1-\alpha
.

It is important to keep in mind that both
\tilde{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{F}_{n+1}
depends on
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{n}=\left\{\widehat{Z}_{1},\dots,\widehat{Z}_{n}\right\}
and
(\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1},\widehat{V}_{n+1})
where
\widehat{Z}_{i}=(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},\widehat{V}_{i})
, but we will not specify them for ease of reading.

Proof.

Let define the event
E_{n+1}=\left\{\widehat{Z}_{1}=\widehat{z}_{1},\dots,\widehat{Z}_{n+1}=\widehat{z}_{n+1}\right\}
where
\widehat{Z}_{i}=(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},\widehat{V}_{i})
and
\widehat{z}_{i}=(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\widehat{v}_{i})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}
. The exchangeability of the residuals implies that
\widehat{V}_{n+1}|E_{n+1}
is uniform on the set
\left\{\widehat{v}_{1},\dots,\widehat{v}_{n+1}\right\}
, and

\displaystyle P^{n+1}\left\{\widehat{V}_{n+1}\leq\mathcal{Q}\left(\tilde{\alpha};\;\mathcal{F}_{n+1}\right)\;\Big|\;E_{n+1}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}P^{n+1}(\widehat{V}_{n+1}=\widehat{v}_{i}\;|\;\lx@nobreakspace E_{n+1})\mathds{1}_{\widehat{v}_{i}\leq\mathcal{Q}(\tilde{\alpha};\;\mathcal{F}_{i})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\frac{1}{n+1}\mathds{1}_{\widehat{v}_{i}\leq\mathcal{Q}(\tilde{\alpha};\;\mathcal{F}_{i})}\geq 1-\alpha\quad\text{(by construction, eq. \ref{eq:lcp_marg_sup})}

By marginalizing over the event

\mathbb{P}\Bigl\{\widehat{V}_{n+1}\leq\mathcal{Q}(\tilde{\alpha};\;\mathcal{F}_{n+1})\Bigl\}\geq 1-\alpha
. Additionally, we can remove the dependence on the unknown residuals
\widehat{V}_{n+1}
using the well-known fact that
\widehat{V}_{n+1}\leq\mathcal{Q}(\tilde{\alpha};\;\mathcal{F}_{n+1})\iff\widehat{V}_{n+1}\leq\mathcal{Q}(\tilde{\alpha};\;\mathcal{F})
(see lemma 1 of
[
Tibshirani et al. 2019
]
] or Lemma A.1 of
[
Guan 2022
]
] for proof). Thus, we also have
\mathbb{P}\Bigl\{\widehat{V}_{n+1}\leq\mathcal{Q}(\tilde{\alpha};\;\mathcal{F})\Bigl\}\geq 1-\alpha
.
∎

The bootstrap step in Random Forest makes its theoretical analysis difficult, which is why it has been replaced by subsampling without replacement in most studies that investigate the asymptotic properties of Random Forests
[

Wager and Athey 2017

Goehry 2020

The main idea is to use a second independent sample
