Abstract
. The dot-depth hierarchy of Brzozowski and Cohen classifies the star-free languages of finite words. By a theorem of McNaughton and Papert, these are also the first-order definable languages. The dot-depth rose to prominence following the work of Thomas, who proved an exact correspondence with the quantifier alternation hierarchy of first-order logic: each level in the dot-depth hierarchy consists of all languages that can be defined with a prescribed number of quantifier blocks. One of the most famous open problems in automata theory is to settle whether the membership problem is decidable for each level: is it possible to decide whether an input regular language belongs to this level? Despite a significant research effort, membership by itself has only been solved for low levels. A recent breakthrough was achieved by replacing membership with a more general problem: separation . Given two input languages, one has to decide whether there exists a third language in the investigated level containing the first language and disjoint from the second. The motivation for looking at separation is threefold: (1) while more difficult, it is more rewarding, as solving it requires a better understanding; (2) being more general, it provides a more convenient framework, and (3) all recent membership algorithms are actually reductions to separation for lower levels. We present a separation algorithm for dot-depth two. A key point is that while dot-depth two is our most prominent application, our theorem is more general. We consider a family of hierarchies, which includes the dot-depth: concatenation hierarchies. They are built through a generic construction process: one first chooses an initial class of languages, the basis, which serves as the lowest level in the hierarchy. Further levels are built by applying generic operations. Our main theorem states that for any concatenation hierarchy whose basis consists of finitely many languages, separation is decidable for level one. In the special case of the dot-depth, this can be lifted to level two using previously known results.

1.
Introduction

Concatenation hierarchies.

Many fundamental problems about regular languages raised in the 70s
[
jep-openreg35
]
led to considerable advances, not only in automata theory but also in logic and algebra, thanks to the discovery of deep connections between these areas. Even if some of these questions are now well understood, a few others remain wide open, despite a wealth of research work spanning several decades. This is the case for the fascinating dot-depth problem
[
jep-dd45
]
, which has two elementary formulations: a language-theoretic one and a logical one. The language-theoretic one is the older of the two. It takes its roots in a theorem of Schützenberger
[
sfo
]
(see also
[
dkrh-textbook
,
dgfo
]
), which gives an algorithm to decide whether a regular language is star-free,
i.e.
, can be expressed using union, complement and concatenation, but without the Kleene star operator. This celebrated result was highly influential for three reasons:

•

First, Schützenberger precisely formalized the objective of “understanding the expressive power of a formalism” through a decision problem called
membership
, which asks whether an input regular language belongs to the class under study.

•

Next, he developed a methodology for tackling it, which he applied to membership for the class of star-free languages.

•

Finally, McNaughton and Papert
[
mnpfo
]
established that star-free languages are exactly the first-order definable ones.

This work highlighted the robustness of the notion of regularity, underlining the ties between automata theory and logic, and revealing new links with algebra. It also established membership as the reference problem for investigating classes of languages.

Schützenberger’s theorem led Brzozowski and Cohen to define the dot-depth hierarchy
[
BrzoDot
]
, an infinite classification
[
BroKnaStrict
]
of all star-free languages counting the number of alternations between concatenations and complements needed to define them. This definition is a particular instance of a generic construction process, which was formalized later and named
concatenation hierarchies
. Any such hierarchy has a single parameter: a “level
0
class of languages” (its
basis
). Then, one uses two operations, polynomial and Boolean closure, to build two kinds of classes: half levels 1/2, 3/2, 5/2…and full levels 1, 2, 3…Given a class of languages
\mathcal{C}
, its
polynomial closure

{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
is the least class of languages containing
\mathcal{C}
that is closed under union and marked concatenation (
K,L\mapsto KaL
, where
a
is a letter). Its
Boolean closure

{Bool\/}(\mathcal{C})
is the least class containing
\mathcal{C}
and closed under union and complement. For any full level
n
, the next half and full levels are built as follows:

•

Level
n+\frac{1}{2}
is the polynomial closure of level
n
.

•

Level
n+1
is the Boolean closure of level
n+\frac{1}{2}
.

Thus, a concatenation hierarchy is fully determined by its basis. Here, we are interested in hierarchies with a
finite
basis,
i.e.
, consisting of a finite number of regular languages.

The most prominent hierarchies of this kind in the literature are the dot-depth itself, as well as the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy
[
StrauConcat
,
TheConcat
]
. They acquired this status when it was discovered
[
ThomEqu
,
PPOrder
]
that each of them coincides with the quantifier alternation hierarchy within an appropriate variant of first-order logic. These two variants have the same overall expressiveness but slightly different signatures (which impacts the properties that one can define at a given level of their quantifier alternation hierarchies).

These correspondences motivated a research program to solve membership for all levels of both hierarchies, thus also characterizing the alternation hierarchies of first-order logic. However, progress has been slow. Until recently, the classes that were solved for both variants are only level 1/2
[
arfi87
,
pwdelta
]
, level 1
[
simon75
,
knast83
]
and level 3/2
[
arfi87
,
pwdelta
,
gssig2
]
. See
[
dgk-smallfragments
]
for a survey. Following these results, membership for level 2 remained open for a long time and was named the “dot-depth 2 problem”.

Separation.
Recently
[
pzqalt
,
pseps3j
]
, solutions were found for levels 2, 5/2 and 7/2. The key ingredient is a new problem stronger than membership:
separation
. Rather than asking whether an input language belongs to the class
\mathcal{C}
under investigation, the
\mathcal{C}
-separation problem takes as input
two
languages, and asks whether there exists a third one
from
\mathcal{C}

containing the first and disjoint from the second. While the interest in separation is recent, it has quickly replaced membership as the central question. A first practical reason is that separation proved itself to be a key ingredient in obtaining all recent membership results. See
[
PZ:Siglog15
,
pzgenconcat
]
for an overview. A striking example is provided by a crucial theorem of
[
pzqalt
]
. It establishes a generic reduction from
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-separation to
\mathcal{C}
-membership which holds for any class
\mathcal{C}
. Combined with a separation algorithm for level 3/2 and a little extra work, this yields a membership algorithm for level 5/2.

However, the main reason is deeper. The primary motivation for considering such problems is to thoroughly understand the classes under investigation. In this respect, while harder, separation is also far more rewarding than membership. On one hand, a membership algorithm for a class
\mathcal{C}
only applies to languages of
\mathcal{C}
: it can detect them and build a description witnessing membership. On the other hand, a separation algorithm for
\mathcal{C}
is universal: it applies to
any
language. Indeed, one may view separation as an approximation problem: given an input pair
(L_{1},L_{2})
one wants to over-approximate
L_{1}
by a language in
\mathcal{C}
, and
L_{2}
serves to specify what a satisfying approximation is. This is why we look at separation: it yields a more robust understanding of the classes of languages than membership does.

The state of the art for separation is the following: it was shown to be decidable for levels 1/2, 1, 3/2 and 5/2 in the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy
[
martens
,
pvzmfcs13
,
pzqalt
,
pseps3j
]
. These results can be lifted to dot-depth using a generic transfer theorem
[
pzsucc2
]
. Notice the gap between levels 3/2 and 5/2: no algorithm is known for level 2. This is explained by the fact that obtaining separation algorithms presents very different challenges for half levels and for full levels. Indeed, it turns out that most separation algorithms rely heavily on closure under marked concatenation, which holds for half levels by definition, but not for full levels.

Contributions.
Our main result is a separation algorithm for level 2 in the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy. Furthermore, by the aforementioned transfer theorem
[
pzsucc2
]
, this can be lifted to separation for dot-depth 2. A crucial point is that this separation result is actually an instance of a generic theorem, which applies to any
finite
class
\mathcal{C}
satisfying a few standard properties (namely closure under Boolean operations and quotients). It states that for such a class
\mathcal{C}
, the class
{Bool\/}({Pol\/}(\mathcal{C}))
has decidable separation. This has two important consequences:

•

In
any
hierarchy whose basis is such a class, level 1 has decidable separation.

•

In the specific case of the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy, this extends to level 2, since it is also level 1 of another finitely based concatenation hierarchy
[
pin-straubing:upper
]
.

This generic result complements other recent results in a natural way. It has been shown in
[
pseps3j
]
that
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
- and
{Pol\/}({Bool\/}({Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})))
-separation are decidable for any finite class
\mathcal{C}
satisfying the aforementioned hypotheses. Combined with our results, this implies that for finitely based concatenation hierarchies, separation is decidable for all levels up to 3/2.

Our proof argument exploits a theorem of
[
pseps3j
]
about the simpler class
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
. However, the techniques that we use here are very different from the ones used in
[
pseps3j
]
, which rely heavily on the fact that
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
and
{Pol\/}({Bool\/}({Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})))
are closed under concatenation (again, this is not the case for
{Bool\/}({Pol\/}(\mathcal{C}))
).

Being generic, our approach yields separation algorithms for a whole family of classes. Moreover, it serves to pinpoint the key hypotheses which are critical in order to solve separation for dot-depth 2. Let us also stress that we obtain new direct proofs that separation is decidable for level 1 in both the dot-depth and Straubing-Thérien hierarchies. This is of particular interest for dot-depth 1 since the previous solution was indirect, as it relied on a transfer result from
[
pzsucc2
]
.

Finally, a key point is that all our results are stated and proved using a general framework that was recently introduced in
[
pzcovering2
]
. It is designed to handle the separation problem and to present the solutions in an elegant manner. In fact, this framework considers a third decision problem, which is even more general than separation:
covering
. Given a class
\mathcal{C}
, the
\mathcal{C}
-covering problem takes two objects as input, a single regular language
L
and a finite set of regular languages
\mathbf{L}
. It asks whether there exists a
\mathcal{C}
-cover of
L
(
i.e.
, a finite set of languages in
\mathcal{C}
whose union includes
L
) such that no language in this
\mathcal{C}
-cover intersects all languages in
\mathbf{L}
. It is simple to show that separation is the special case of covering when the set
\mathbf{L}
is a singleton. Our main theorem actually states that
{Bool\/}({Pol\/}(\mathcal{C}))
-covering is decidable for any finite class
\mathcal{C}
satisfying mild hypotheses.

Organization.
The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
gives preliminary definitions. In Section
3
, we introduce concatenation hierarchies and state our generic theorem:
{Bool\/}({Pol\/}(\mathcal{C}))
-covering is decidable for every finite quotient-closed Boolean algebra
\mathcal{C}
. The remainder of the paper is devoted to presenting the algorithm. In Section
4
, we recall the framework which was designed in
[
pzcovering2
]
to handle the covering problem. We use this framework in Section
5
to formulate our algorithm for
{Bool\/}({Pol\/}(\mathcal{C}))
-covering. The remaining sections are devoted to proving that this algorithm is correct.

This is the journal version of
[
pzboolpol
]
. The main result (
i.e.
, that
{Bool\/}({Pol\/}(\mathcal{C}))
-separation is decidable for every finite quotient-closed Boolean algebra
\mathcal{C}
) has been generalized to
{Bool\/}({Pol\/}(\mathcal{C}))
-covering. Moreover, both its presentation and its proof have been completely reworked. Our results are now formulated using the general framework introduced in
[

]
, designed to handle separation and covering problems.

pzsucc2
]
that separation and covering for any level in the dot-depth hierarchy reduce to the same problem for the corresponding level in the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy. Therefore, while the decidability of separation and covering for dot-depth one is not a new result, an advantage of Theorem
3.2
is that we obtain a new
direct
proof of this result.

However, these are not the main applications of Theorem
3.2
. It turns out that the theorem also applies to level two in the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy. Indeed, it is known that this level is also level one in another finitely based concatenation hierarchy. More precisely, recall the class and presented in Example
2.3
: it consists of all Boolean combinations of languages
A^{*}aA^{*}
, for some
a\in A
. It is straightforward to verify that and is a finite quotient-closed Boolean algebra. The following theorem was shown in
[
pin-straubing:upper
]
(see also
[
pzcovering2
]
for a recent proof).

Theorem
\thethm

(
[
pin-straubing:upper
]
)
.

Level two in the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy is exactly the class
{BPol\/}(\and)
.

In view of Theorem
3.3
, we obtain the following immediate corollary of Theorem
3.2
.

Corollary
\thethm
.

Separation and covering are decidable for level two in the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy.

Finally, this result may be lifted to dot-depth two using again the generic transfer theorem proved in
[
pzsucc2
]
. Hence, we obtain the following additional corollary.

Corollary
\thethm
.

Separation and covering are decidable for dot-depth two.

Remark
\thethm
.

Logical characterizations of these two hierarchies are known (see
[
ThomEqu
]
for the dot-depth hierarchy and
[
PPOrder
]
for the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy). Each of them corresponds to a quantifier alternation hierarchy within a particular variant of first-order logic over words. The two variants differ by the set of predicates which are allowed in sentences (they have the same overall expressive power, but this changes the levels in their respective quantifier alternation hierarchies). We refer the reader to
[
pzgenconcat
]
for details and a recent proof of these results.

In particular, level two in the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy corresponds to a logic denoted by
\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2}(<)
and dot-depth two corresponds to another logic denoted by
\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2}(<,+1)
. Hence, our results also imply that covering and separation are decidable for these two logics.

4.
Framework: rating maps and optimal covers

In this section, we present the framework which we use to formulate our covering algorithm for
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
(when
\mathcal{C}
is a finite quotient-closed Boolean algebra) announced in Theorem
3.2
. The framework itself was designed and applied to several specific classes in
[
pzcovering2
]
. Moreover, it was also used in
[
pseps3j
]
to formulate algorithms for
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
- and
{Pol\/}({BPol\/}(\mathcal{C}))
-covering. Here, we recall the part of this framework that we shall actually need in the paper. We refer the reader to
[
pzcovering2
]
for a complete and detailed presentation.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a lattice. In
\mathcal{D}
-covering, the input is a pair
(L,\mathbf{L})
where
L
is a regular language and
\mathbf{L}
a finite set of regular languages: we have to decide whether there exists a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
which is separating for
\mathbf{L}
. We design a two-step approach for tackling this problem.

The first step is to transform this decision problem into the following
computational
problem. Given an input
(L,\mathbf{L})
, we compute a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
, which is
optimal
with respect to
\mathbf{L}
in the following sense: for every subset
\mathbf{H}
of
\mathbf{L}
, this optimal cover is separating for
\mathbf{H}
if and only if
(L,\mathbf{H})
is
\mathcal{D}
-coverable. Thus, this single object encapsulates
all answers
to the
\mathcal{D}
-covering problem for inputs of the form
(L,\mathbf{H})
, with
\mathbf{H}\subseteq\mathbf{L}
. Such an optimal cover always exists when
\mathcal{D}
is a lattice. The set
\mathbf{L}
can be viewed as a parameter constraining this optimal cover, or dually, as a measure of the quality of this cover.

The second step in the approach of
[
pzcovering2
]
consists in replacing the set
\mathbf{L}
by a (more general) algebraic object called a
rating map
. Intuitively, rating maps play the same role as the set of languages
\mathbf{L}
from the above paragraph. Thus, they are also designed to measure the quality of
\mathcal{D}
-covers. Given a rating map
\rho
and a language
L
, we use
\rho
to rank the existing
\mathcal{D}
-covers of
L
.

We are then able to reformulate
\mathcal{D}
-covering with these notions. Instead of deciding whether
(L,\mathbf{L})
is
\mathcal{D}
-coverable, we compute an optimal
\mathcal{D}
-cover for
L
for a rating map
\rho
that we build from
\mathbf{L}
. An advantage of this approach is that it yields elegant formulations for the covering algorithms which are formulated with it. We refer the reader to
[
pzcovering2
]
and
[
pseps3j
]
for examples (in addition to
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{D})
, which is presented in this paper). Another important motivation for using this framework is that in order to handle
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{D})
, we require a result for the simpler class
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{D})
which is stronger than the decidability of covering (this result is proved in
[
pseps3j
]
). The framework of
[
pzcovering2
]
is designed to formulate this result.

We start by defining rating maps. Then, we explain how they are used to measure the quality of a cover and define optimal covers. Finally, we connect these notions to the covering problem. Let us point out that several statements presented here are without proof. We refer the reader to
[
pzcovering2
]
for these proofs.

4.1.
Rating maps

Rating maps involve commutative and idempotent monoids. We shall write such monoids
(R,+)
: we call the binary operation “
+
”
addition
and denote the neutral element by
0_{R}
. Being idempotent means that for all
r\in R
, we have
r+r=r
. Observe that for every commutative and idempotent monoid
(R,+)
, we may define a canonical ordering
\leq
over
R
:

\text{For all }r,s\in R,\quad r\leq s\text{ when }r+s=s.

It is straightforward to verify that
\leq
is a partial order which is compatible with addition. Moreover, we have the following fact which is immediate from the definitions.

Fact
\thethm
.

Let
(R,+)
and
(Q,+)
be two commutative and idempotent monoids. Moreover, let
\gamma:(R,+)\to(Q,+)
be a morphism. Then,
\gamma
is increasing: for every
s,t\in R
such that
s\leq t
, we have
\gamma(s)\leq\gamma(t)
.

Example
\thethm
.

For any set
E
, it is immediate that
(2^{E},\cup)
is an idempotent and commutative monoid. The neutral element is
\emptyset
. Moreover, the canonical ordering is set inclusion.

When manipulating the subsets of a commutative and idempotent monoid
(R,+)
we shall often need to apply a
downset operation
. Given
S\subseteq R
, we write
\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}S
for the set,

\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}S=\{r\in R\mid r\leq s\text{ for some $s\in S$}\}.

We extend this notation to Cartesian products of arbitrary sets with
R
. Given some set
X
and a subset
S\subseteq X\times R
, we write
\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}S
for the set,

\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}S=\{(x,r)\in X\times R\mid\text{there exists $q\in R$ such that $r\leq q$ and $(x,q)\in S$}\}.

We may now define rating maps. A
rating map
is a morphism
\rho:(2^{A^{*}},\cup)\to(R,+)
where
(R,+)
is a
finite
idempotent and commutative monoid called the
rating set of
\rho
. That is,
\rho
is a map from
2^{A^{*}}
to
R
satisfying the following properties:

(1)

\rho(\emptyset)=0_{R}
.

(2)

For all
K_{1},K_{2}\subseteq A^{*}
,
\rho(K_{1}\cup K_{2})=\rho(K_{1})+\rho(K_{2})
.

For the sake of improved readability, when applying a rating map
\rho
to a singleton set
K=\{w\}
, we shall write
\rho(w)
for
\rho(\{w\})
. Additionally, we write
\rho_{*}:A^{*}\to R
for the restriction of
\rho
to
A^{*}
: for every
w\in A^{*}
, we have
\rho_{*}(w)=\rho(w)
(this notation allows us to write
\rho_{*}^{-1}(r)\subseteq A^{*}
for the language of all words
w\in A^{*}
such that
\rho(w)=r
).

Most of the statements involved in our framework make sense for arbitrary rating maps. However, we shall often have to work with special rating maps which satisfy additional properties. We present them now.

Nice rating maps.
A rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
is
nice
when, for every language
K\subseteq A^{*}
, there exist finitely many words
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\in K
such that
\rho(K)=\rho(w_{1})+\cdots+\rho(w_{k})
.

Observe that in this case,
\rho
is characterized by the canonical map
\rho_{*}:A^{*}\to R
. Indeed, for every language
K
, we may consider the sum of all elements
\rho(w)
for
w\in K
: while it may be infinite, it boils down to a finite one since
R
is commutative and idempotent. The hypothesis that
\rho
is nice implies that
\rho(K)
is equal to this sum.

Multiplicative rating maps.
A rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
is
multiplicative
when its rating set
R
has more structure: it needs to be an
idempotent semiring
. Moreover,
\rho
has to satisfy an additional property connecting this structure to language concatenation. Namely, it has to be a morphism of semirings.

A
semiring
is a tuple
(R,+,\cdot)
where
R
is a set and “
+
” and “
\cdot
” are two binary operations called addition and multiplication, such that the following axioms are satisfied:

•

(R,+)
is a commutative monoid (the neutral element is denoted by
0_{R}
).

•

(R,\cdot)
is a monoid (the neutral element is denoted by
1_{R}
).

•

Multiplication distributes over addition:
r\cdot(s+t)=(r\cdot s)+(r\cdot t)
and
(r+s)\cdot t=(r\cdot t)+(s\cdot t)
for all
r,s,t\in R
.

•

The neutral element of
(R,+)
is a zero for
(R,\cdot)
:
0_{R}\cdot r=r\cdot 0_{R}=0_{R}
for all
r\in R
.

We say that a semiring
R
is
idempotent
when
r+r=r
for every
r\in R
,
i.e.
, when the additive monoid
(R,+)
is idempotent (on the other hand, there is no additional constraint on the multiplicative monoid
(R,\cdot)
).

Example
\thethm
.

A key example of an infinite idempotent semiring is the set
2^{A^{*}}
of all languages over
A
. Union is the addition (with
\emptyset
as neutral element) and language concatenation is the multiplication (with
\{\varepsilon\}
as neutral element).

Clearly, any finite idempotent semiring
(R,+,\cdot)
is in particular a rating set:
(R,+)
is an idempotent and commutative monoid. In particular, one may verify that the canonical ordering “
\leq
” on
R
, is compatible with multiplication as well.

We may now define multiplicative rating maps: as expected they are semiring morphisms. Let
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
be a rating map. By definition, this means that the rating set
(R,+)
is a finite idempotent commutative monoid and that
\rho
is a monoid morphism from
(2^{A^{*}},\cup)
to
(R,+)
. We say that
\rho
is multiplicative when the rating set
R
is equipped with a second binary operation “
\cdot
” such that
(R,+,\cdot)
is an idempotent semiring and
\rho
is also a monoid morphism from
(2^{A^{*}},\cdot)
to
(R,\cdot)
. In other words, the two following additional axioms hold:

(3)

\rho(\varepsilon)=1_{R}
.

(4)

For all
K_{1},K_{2}\subseteq A^{*}
, we have
\rho(K_{1}K_{2})=\rho(K_{1})\cdot\rho(K_{2})
.

Remark
\thethm
.

An important point is that the rating maps which are both nice and multiplicative are finitely representable. As we explained above, a nice rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
is characterized by the canonical map
\rho_{*}:A^{*}\to R
. Moreover, when
\rho
is multiplicative as well,
\rho_{*}
is finitely representable: it is a morphism from
A^{*}
into the finite monoid
(R,{\cdot})
. Thus, we may consider algorithms taking nice multiplicative rating maps as input. Let us point out that the rating maps which are not nice and multiplicative remain important. We often deal with them in our proofs.

4.2.
Imprints and optimal covers

We now explain how we use rating maps to measure the quality of covers. This involves an additional notion: “imprints”. Consider a rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
. For any finite set of languages
\mathbf{K}
, the
\rho
-imprint
of
\mathbf{K}
, denoted by
\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})
, is the following subset of
R
:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})&=&\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}\{\rho(K)\mid K\in\mathbf{K}\}\\
&=&\{r\in R\mid\text{there exists $K\in\mathbf{K}$ such that $r\leq\rho(K)$}\}.\end{array}

When using this notion, we shall have some language
L\subseteq A^{*}
in hand: our goal is to find the “best possible” cover
\mathbf{K}
of
L
. Intuitively,
\rho
-imprints measure the “quality” of candidate covers
\mathbf{K}
(the smaller the
\rho
-imprint, the better the quality).

This leads to the notion of optimality. Let
\mathcal{D}
be an arbitrary lattice. Given a language
L
, an
optimal
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
for
\rho
is a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
which has the smallest possible
\rho
-imprint (with respect to inclusion). That is,
\mathbf{K}
is an optimal
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
for
\rho
if and only if,

\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})\subseteq\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K}^{\prime})\quad\text{for every $\mathcal{D}$-cover $\mathbf{K}^{\prime}$ of $L$}.

Furthermore, in the special case when
L=A^{*}
, we speak of optimal
universal

\mathcal{D}
-cover for
\rho
.

If
\mathcal{D}
is a lattice, one can show that there always exists at least one optimal
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
for
\rho
(see
[
pzcovering2
, Lemma 4.15]
). In general, there are actually infinitely many of them.

Lemma
\thethm
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a lattice. Then, for any rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
and any language
L\subseteq A^{*}
, there exists an optimal
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
for
\rho
.

It is important to note that the proof of Lemma
4.2
is non-constructive: given
L
and
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
, computing an actual optimal
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
for
\rho
is a difficult problem in general. As we shall see below, getting such an algorithm (in the special case when
\rho
is nice and multiplicative) yields a procedure for
\mathcal{D}
-covering. Before we can establish this connection precisely, we require a key observation about optimal
\mathcal{D}
-covers.

Optimal imprints.
By definition, given a language
L
, all optimal
\mathcal{D}
-covers of
L
for
\rho
have the same
\rho
-imprint. Hence, this unique
\rho
-imprint is a
canonical
object for
\mathcal{D}
,
L
and
\rho
. We call it the
\mathcal{D}
-optimal
\rho
-imprint on
L
and we denote it by
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[L,\rho\right]
:

\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[L,\rho\right]=\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})\subseteq R\quad\text{for every optimal $\mathcal{D}$-cover $\mathbf{K}$ of $L$ for $\rho$}.

Additionally, in the particular case when
L=A^{*}
, we shall speak of
\mathcal{D}
-optimal universal
\rho
-imprint
and write
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\rho\right]
for
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[A^{*},\rho\right]
.

We complete these definitions with a few properties of optimal imprints. We start with a straightforward fact which compares the optimal imprints on languages which are comparable with inclusion. The proof is available in
[
pzcovering2
, Fact 4.17]
.

Fact
\thethm
.

Consider a rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
and a lattice
\mathcal{D}
. Let
H,L
be two languages such that
H\subseteq L
. Then,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[H,\rho\right]\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[L,\rho\right]
.

More precisely, the following fact connects optimal imprints with union of languages.

Fact
\thethm
.

Let
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
be a rating map and consider two languages
H,L
. Then, for every lattice
\mathcal{D}
, we have
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[H\cup L,\rho\right]=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[H,\rho\right]\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[L,\rho\right]
.

Proof.

We already know that
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[H,\rho\right]\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[H\cup L,\rho\right]
and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[L,\rho\right]\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[H\cup L,\rho\right]
by Fact
4.2
. Therefore, the inclusion
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[H,\rho\right]\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[L,\rho\right]\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[H\cup L,\rho\right]
is immediate. We prove the converse one. Let
r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[H\cup L,\rho\right]
. We let
\mathbf{K}_{H}
and
\mathbf{K}_{L}
be optimal
\mathcal{D}
-covers of
H
and
L
respectively (for
\rho
). Clearly,
\mathbf{K}_{H}\cup\mathbf{K}_{L}
is a cover
H\cup L
. Therefore,
r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[H\cup L,\rho\right]
implies that
r\in\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K}_{H}\cup\mathbf{K}_{L})
. Hence, there exists
K\in\mathbf{K}_{H}\cup\mathbf{K}_{L}
such that
r\leq\rho(K)
. Then, either
K\in\mathbf{K}_{H}
which implies
r\in\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K}_{H})=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[H,\rho\right]
or
K\in\mathbf{K}_{L}
which implies
r\in\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K}_{L})=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[L,\rho\right]
. Altogether, we get
r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[H,\rho\right]\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[L,\rho\right]
, finishing the proof.
∎

4.3.
Connection with the covering problem

Finally, we explain how rating maps are used for handling the covering problem.

Given a lattice
\mathcal{D}
, it turns out that the
\mathcal{D}
-covering problem reduces to another problem whose input is a nice multiplicative rating map. Let us point out that
two
reductions of this kind are presented in
[
pzcovering2
]
. The first one is simpler but restricted to Boolean algebras. On the other hand, the second one applies to any lattice, but requires working with more involved objects.

In the paper, we investigate classes of the form
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{D})
, which are Boolean algebras. Hence, we shall mostly work with the first variant whose statement is as follows.

{propC}

[
[
pzcovering2
]
]
Let
\mathcal{C}
be a Boolean algebra. Assume that there exists an algorithm for the following computational problem:

Input:

A nice multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
.

Output:

Compute the
\mathcal{C}
-optimal universal
\rho
-imprint,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[\rho\right]
.

Then,
\mathcal{C}
-covering is decidable.

Proof sketch.

The proof builds on several simple steps. The first one is the observation that when
\mathcal{C}
is a Boolean algebra, deciding whether a pair
(L,\mathbf{L})
is
\mathcal{C}
-coverable reduces to the case where
L=A^{*}
. Indeed, one may check that
(L,\mathbf{L})
is
\mathcal{C}
-coverable if and only if so is
(A^{*},\mathbf{L}\cup\{L\})
. We are left to show that one can decide whether a pair
(A^{*},\mathbf{L})
is
\mathcal{C}
-coverable.

Second, observe that
(2^{\mathbf{L}},\cup,\emptyset)
is a rating set. One may verify that the function
\rho_{\mathbf{L}}:2^{A^{*}}\to 2^{\mathbf{L}}
defined by
\rho_{\mathbf{L}}(K)=\{L\in\mathbf{L}\mid K\cap L\neq\emptyset\}
is a nice rating map. Furthermore, for any subset
\mathbf{H}
of
\mathbf{L}
, one may prove that
(A^{*},\mathbf{H})
is
\mathcal{C}
-coverable if and only if
\mathbf{H}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[\rho_{\mathbf{L}}\right]
.

The third step is to reduce the computation of
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[\rho_{\mathbf{L}}\right]
to that of
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[\rho^{\prime}_{\mathbf{L}}\right]
, where
\rho^{\prime}_{\mathbf{L}}
is a
multiplicative
nice rating map that we can build from
\rho_{\mathbf{L}}
. By the hypothesis of the statement, this last set is computable. We refer the reader to
[
pzcovering2
, Theorem 5.21]
for more details.
∎

Additionally, we shall need in Section
6
to apply a theorem of
[
pseps3j
]
for classes of the form
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
(which are lattices but not Boolean algebras) as a sub-result. Thus, we also recall the terminology associated to the generalized reduction which holds for arbitrary lattices, since we need it to state this theorem.

When working with an arbitrary lattice, one needs to consider slightly more involved objects. Given a lattice
\mathcal{D}
, a map
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
into a finite set
M
(in practice,
\alpha
will be a monoid morphism but this is not required for the definition) and a rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
, we write
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho]
for the following set,

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho]=\{(s,r)\in M\times R\mid r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho\right]\}\subseteq M\times R.

We call
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho]
the
\alpha
-pointed
\mathcal{D}
-optimal
\rho
-imprint
. Clearly, it encodes all sets
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho\right]
for
s\in M
.

The following statement is
[
pseps3j
, Proposition 5.18]
(see also
[
pzcovering2
, Proposition 7.2]
).

Proposition
\thethm
.

Consider a lattice
\mathcal{D}
and some finite quotient-closed Boolean algebra
\mathcal{C}
. Assume that there exists an algorithm for the following computational problem:

Input:

A
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
and

a nice multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
.

Output:

Compute the
\alpha
-pointed
\mathcal{D}
-optimal
\rho
-imprint,
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho]
.

Then,
\mathcal{D}
-covering is decidable.

5.
Characterization of
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-optimal imprints

We present a generic characterization of
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-optimal imprints which holds when
\mathcal{C}
is a finite quotient-closed Boolean algebra. For the sake of avoiding clutter, we assume that the finite quotient-closed Boolean algebra
\mathcal{C}
is fixed for the whole section.

Given a
nice
multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
, we want to characterize the set
\mathcal{I}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}\left[\rho\right]\subseteq R
. An important point is that we do not work directly with this set. Instead, we characterize the family of all sets
\mathcal{I}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}\left[D,\rho\right]\subseteq R
where
D\subseteq A^{*}
is a
\sim_{\mathcal{C}}
-class. Note that this family of sets record more information than just the set
\mathcal{I}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}\left[\rho\right]
. Indeed, by Fact
4.2
, we have,

\mathcal{I}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}\left[\rho\right]=\mathcal{I}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}\left[A^{*},\rho\right]=\bigcup_{D\in{A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}}}\mathcal{I}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}\left[D,\rho\right].

For the sake of convenience, we shall encode this family as a set of pairs in
({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R
. Given a multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
, we define:

\mathcal{P}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\mathcal{C}}[\rho]=\{(D,r)\in({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R\mid r\in\mathcal{I}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}\left[D,\rho\right]\}.

When
\rho
is nice, we characterize
\mathcal{P}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\mathcal{C}}[\rho]
as the
greatest
subset of
R
satisfying specific properties. From the statement, it is straightforward to obtain a greatest fixpoint procedure for computing
\mathcal{P}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\mathcal{C}}[\rho]
from
\rho
. In turns, this allows to compute
\mathcal{I}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}\left[\rho\right]
using the above equality. By Proposition
4.3
, this yields an algorithm for
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-covering, thus proving our main result: Theorem
3.2
.

Remark
\thethm
.

This characterization is rather unique among the results that have been obtained for other classes in
[
pzcovering2
]
and
[
pseps3j
]
. Typically, optimal imprints are characterized as
least
subsets, not greatest ones.

Notation
.

In our statements, we shall frequently manipulate subsets of
({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R
. When doing so, the following notation will be convenient. Given
S\subseteq({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R
and
D\in{A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}}
, we write,

S(D)=\{r\in R\mid(D,r)\in S\}

In particular, observe that
\mathcal{P}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\mathcal{C}}[\rho](D)=\mathcal{I}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}\left[D,\rho\right]
by definition.

Given a multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
, we define a notion of
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subset of
({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R
(for
\rho
). Our theorem then states that when
\rho
is nice, the greatest such subset is exactly
\mathcal{P}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\mathcal{C}}[\rho]
.

Remark
\thethm
.

The definition of
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated sets makes sense regardless of whether
\rho
is nice. However, we need this hypothesis for the greatest one to be
\mathcal{P}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\mathcal{C}}[\rho]
.

The definition is based on an intermediary notion. With every set
S\subseteq({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R
, we associate another set
\mathcal{R}^{\rho}_{S}\subseteq({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R\times 2^{R}
. For the definition, we need to recall a few properties. Given a word
w\in A^{*}
, we denote its
\sim_{\mathcal{C}}
class by
[w]_{\mathcal{C}}
. Moreover, since
\mathcal{C}
is closed under quotients, Lemma
2.3
yields that the equivalence
\sim_{\mathcal{C}}
is a congruence. We denote by “
\mathbin{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}
” the multiplication of
\sim_{\mathcal{C}}
-classes in the monoid
{A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}}
. Additionally, since
R
is a semiring,
2^{R}
is one as well for union as addition and the natural multiplication lifted from the one of
R
(for
U,V\in 2^{R}
,
UV=\{qr\mid q\in U\text{ and }r\in V\}
).

We may now present our definition. Consider a set
S\subseteq({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R
. We define
\mathcal{R}^{\rho}_{S}
as the least subset of
({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R\times 2^{R}
(with respect to inclusion) which satisfies the following properties:

•

Trivial elements.
For every
w\in A^{*}
, we have
([w]_{\mathcal{C}},\rho(w),\{\rho(w)\})\in\mathcal{R}^{\rho}_{S}
.

•

Extended downset.
For every
(C,q,U)\in\mathcal{R}^{\rho}_{S}
and
V\subseteq\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}U
, we have
(C,q,V)\in\mathcal{R}^{\rho}_{S}
.

•

Multiplication.
For every
(C,q,U),(D,r,V)\in\mathcal{R}^{\rho}_{S}
, we have
(C\mathbin{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}D,qr,UV)\in\mathcal{R}^{\rho}_{S}
.

•

S
-restricted closure.
For every triple of idempotents
(E,f,F)\in\mathcal{R}^{\rho}_{S}
, we have

(E,f,F\cdot S(E)\cdot F)\in\mathcal{R}^{\rho}_{S}
.

We are ready to define the
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subsets of
({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R
. Consider a set
S\subseteq({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R
. We say that
S
is
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated for
\rho
if the following property holds:

\begin{array}[]{c}\text{for every $(D,r)\in S$, there exist $r_{1},\dots,r_{k}\in R$ such that,}\\
\text{$r\leq r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}$ and $(D,r_{i},\{r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}\})\in\mathcal{R}^{\rho}_{S}$ for every $i\leq k$}\end{array}

(2)

We now state our characterization of
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-optimal imprints in the following theorem.

Theorem
\thethm
.

Let
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
be a nice multiplicative rating map. Then,
\mathcal{P}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\mathcal{C}}[\rho]
is the greatest
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subset of
({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R
for
\rho
.

Given as input a nice multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
, Theorem
5
yields an algorithm to compute
\mathcal{I}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}\left[\rho\right]
. Indeed, computing the greatest
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subset of
({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R
is achieved with a greatest fixpoint algorithm. One starts from the set
S_{0}=({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R
and computes a sequence
S_{0}\supseteq S_{1}\supseteq S_{2}\supseteq\cdots
of subsets. For every
n\in{\rm Nature}
,
S_{n+1}
is the set of all pairs
(D,r)\in S_{n}
satisfying (
2
) for
S=S_{n}
. That is,

\begin{array}[]{c}\text{there exist $r_{1},\dots,r_{k}\in R$ such that,}\\
\text{$r\leq r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}$ and $(D,r_{i},\{r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}\})\in\mathcal{R}^{\rho}_{S_{n}}$ for every $i\leq k$}\end{array}

Clearly,
S_{n+1}\subseteq S_{n}
for every
n\in{\rm Nature}
. Therefore, the computation eventually reaches a fixpoint which is the greatest
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subset of
({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R
by definition. Let us point out that the computation of
S_{n+1}
from
S_{n}
involves computing
\mathcal{R}^{\rho}_{S_{n}}
, which is achieved with a least fixpoint procedure by definition.

Altogether, it follows that Theorem
5
yields an algorithm for computing
\mathcal{P}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\mathcal{C}}[\rho]
(and therefore,
\mathcal{I}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}\left[\rho\right]
as well by Fact
4.2
) which alternates between a greatest fixpoint and a least fixpoint.

Remark
\thethm
.

The hypothesis that
\rho
is nice in Theorem
5
is mandatory: the result fails otherwise. This is actually apparent on the definition of
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated sets. One may verify from the definition of
\mathcal{R}^{\rho}_{S}
that for every triple
(D,q,U)\in\mathcal{R}^{\rho}_{S}
, there exists a word
w\in A^{*}
such that
D=[w]_{\mathcal{C}}
and
q=\rho(w)
. Therefore, it follows from (
2
) that for every
(D,r)\in({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R
belonging to a
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subset, its second component
r
must satisfy
r\leq\rho(w_{1})+\cdots+\rho(w_{k})
for some words
w_{1},\dots,w_{k}\in A^{*}
. Intuitively, this means that
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subsets only depend on the image of singletons. Therefore, using the notion only makes sense when
\rho
is characterized by these images: this is exactly the definition of nice rating maps.

This might seem to be a minor observation. Indeed, by Proposition
4.3
, being able to compute
\mathcal{I}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}\left[\rho\right]
from a nice multiplicative rating map suffices to meet our goal: getting an algorithm for
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-covering. Actually, it does not even make sense to speak of an algorithm which takes arbitrary multiplicative rating maps as input since we are not able to finitely represent them. However, from a theoretical point of view, the fact that we only manage to get a description of
\mathcal{I}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}\left[\rho\right]
when
\rho
is nice is significant. In the proof of Theorem
5
, we use a theorem of
[
pseps3j
]
as a subresult. Specifically, this theorem is a characterization of
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-optimal pointed imprints: given a
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
and a multiplicative rating map
\tau:2^{A^{*}}\to Q
, it describes the set
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\alpha}[\tau]
. A key point is that this characterization does
not
require
\tau
to be nice. This is crucial: in the proof of Theorem
5
, we consider auxiliary rating maps built from
\rho
which need
not
be nice.

In summary, we are able to handle
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
for all multiplicative rating maps, including those that are not nice, which is crucial in order be able to handle
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
. However, at this level, we are only able to deal with nice multiplicative rating maps (the situation is actually similar for
{Pol\/}({BPol\/}(\mathcal{C}))
as shown in
[
pseps3j
]
). This explains why the results presented in this paper and in
[
pseps3j
]
cannot be lifted to higher levels in concatenation hierarchies (at least not in a straightforward manner).

We turn to the proof of Theorem
5
. It spans the remaining four sections of the paper. Given a nice multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
, we have to show that
\mathcal{P}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\mathcal{C}}[\rho]
is the greatest
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subset of
({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R
for
\rho
. The main argument involves two directions which are proved independently. They correspond to soundness and completeness of the greatest fixpoint algorithm computing
\mathcal{P}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\mathcal{C}}[\rho]
.

•

The soundness argument shows that
\mathcal{P}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\mathcal{C}}[\rho]
contains every
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subset (this implies that the greatest fixpoint procedure only computes elements of
\mathcal{P}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\mathcal{C}}[\rho]
). We present it in Section
8
.

•

The completeness argument shows that
\mathcal{P}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\mathcal{C}}[\rho]
itself is
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated (this implies that the greatest fixpoint procedure computes all elements of
\mathcal{P}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\mathcal{C}}[\rho]
). We present it in Section
9
.

When put together, these two results yield as desired that
\mathcal{P}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\mathcal{C}}[\rho]
is the greatest
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subset of
R
, proving Theorem
5
.

However, before presenting the main argument, we require some additional material about rating maps. For both directions, we shall introduce auxiliary rating maps (built from
\rho
) and apply a characterization of
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-optimal imprints to them (taken from
[
pseps3j
]
). These auxiliary rating maps are built using generic constructions which are not specific to Theorem
5
. We present them in Section
6
. Then, we recall the theorem characterizing
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-optimal imprints from
[
pseps3j
]
in Section
7
(actually, we slightly generalize it, since we shall apply it for rating maps that are more general than the ones considered in
[
pseps3j
]
).

6.
Nesting of rating maps

In this section, we present two generic constructions. Each of them builds a new rating map out of an already existing one and a lattice
\mathcal{D}
. The constructions are new: they do not appear in
[
pzcovering2
]
(however, one of them generalizes and streamlines a technical construction used in
[
pseps3j
]
).

Remark
\thethm
.

As announced, we shall later rely on these constructions in the proof of Theorem
5
(we use them in the special cases when
\mathcal{D}
is either
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
or
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
). However, this section is independent from Theorem
5
: all definitions are presented in a general context.

We first present the constructions and then investigate the properties of the output rating maps they produce.

6.1.
Definition

We present two constructions. The first one involves two objects: a lattice
\mathcal{D}
and a rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
. We build a new rating map
\xi_{\mathcal{D}}[\rho]
whose rating set is
(2^{R},\cup)
, and which associates to a language its optimal
\mathcal{D}
-optimal
\rho
-imprint.

\begin{array}[]{llll}\xi_{\mathcal{D}}[\rho]:&(2^{A^{*}},\cup)&\to&(2^{R},\cup)\\
&K&\mapsto&\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[K,\rho\right]\end{array}

The fact that
\xi_{\mathcal{D}}[\rho]
is indeed a rating map is shown below in Proposition
6.1
.
The second construction involves an additional object: a map
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
where
M
is some arbitrary finite set (in practice,
\alpha
will be a monoid morphism, but this is not required for the definition). We build a new rating map
\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho]
with rating set
(2^{M\times R},\cup)
:

\begin{array}[]{llll}\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho]:&(2^{A^{*}},\cup)&\to&(2^{M\times R},\cup)\\
&K&\mapsto&\{(s,r)\mid r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\alpha^{-1}(s)\cap K,\rho\right]\}.\end{array}

Let us prove that these two maps are indeed rating maps. We state this result in the following proposition.

Proposition
\thethm
.

Consider a lattice
\mathcal{D}
, a map
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
into a finite set
M
and a rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
. Then,
\xi_{\mathcal{D}}[\rho]
and
\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho]
are rating maps.

Proof.

We start with
\xi_{\mathcal{D}}[\rho]
. It is immediate that
\xi_{\mathcal{D}}[\rho](\emptyset)=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\emptyset,\rho\right]=\emptyset
. Moreover, we obtain from Fact
4.2
that for every
H,L\subseteq A^{*}
,

\xi_{\mathcal{D}}[\rho](H\cup L)=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[H\cup L,\rho\right]=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[H,\rho\right]\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[L,\rho\right]=\xi_{\mathcal{D}}[\rho](H)\cup\xi_{\mathcal{D}}[\rho](L).

We conclude that
\xi_{\mathcal{D}}[\rho]
is indeed a rating map. We turn to
\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho]
. Clearly,

\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho](\emptyset)=\{(s,r)\mid r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\emptyset,\rho\right]\}=\{(s,r)\mid r\in\emptyset\}=\emptyset.

Moreover, given
H,L\subseteq A^{*}
,

\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho](H\cup L)=\{(s,r)\mid r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\alpha^{-1}(s)\cap(H\cup L),\rho\right]\}.

By Fact
4.2
, this yields,

\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho](H\cup L)=\{(s,r)\mid r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\alpha^{-1}(s)\cap H,\rho\right]\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\alpha^{-1}(s)\cap L,\rho\right]\}

This exactly says that
\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho](H\cup L)=\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho](H)\cup\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho](L)
, finishing the proof that
\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho]
is a rating map.
∎

A crucial observation is that the rating maps
\xi_{\mathcal{D}}[\rho]
and
\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho]
are not nice in general, even when the original rating map
\rho
is. Let us present a counter-example.

Example
\thethm
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be the Boolean algebra consisting of all languages which are either finite or co-finite (
i.e.
, their complement is finite). Moreover, let
T=\{0,1\}
and
R=2^{T}
. We define a nice rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
as follows (actually, it is simple to verify from the definition that
\rho
is also multiplicative). Since we are defining a nice rating map, it suffices to specify the evaluation of words: for any
w\in A^{*}
, we let
\rho(w)=\{0\}
if
w
has even length and
\rho(w)=\{1\}
if
w
has odd length. We show that the rating map
\xi_{\mathcal{D}}[\rho]:2^{A^{*}}\to 2^{R}
is not nice.

By definition,
\xi_{\mathcal{D}}[\rho](A^{*})=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[A^{*},\rho\right]
. Recall that
\mathcal{D}
contains only finite and co-finite languages. Moreover, covers may only contain finitely many languages. Hence, it is immediate that if
\mathbf{K}
is an optimal
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
A^{*}
for
\rho
, then there exists
K\in\mathbf{K}
containing a word of even length and a word of odd length. Therefore
\rho(K)=\{0,1\}
by definition of
\rho
, whence
\xi_{\mathcal{D}}[\rho](A^{*})=\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})=\{\{0,1\},\{0\},\{1\},\emptyset\}=R
.

Now observe that for any
w\in A^{*}
,
\{w\}\in\mathcal{D}
by definition (it is a finite language). Hence,
\{\{w\}\}
is a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
\{w\}
and since
\xi_{\mathcal{D}}[\rho](w)=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\{w\},\rho\right]
, we know that
\xi_{\mathcal{D}}[\rho](w)=\{\{0\},\emptyset\}
if
w
has even length and
\xi_{\mathcal{D}}[\rho](w)=\{\{1\},\emptyset\}
if
w
has odd length. Altogether, we obtain that,

\bigcup_{w\in A^{*}}\xi_{\mathcal{D}}[\rho](w)=\{\{0\},\{1\},\emptyset\}\neq\xi_{\mathcal{D}}[\rho](A^{*})

We conclude that
\xi_{\mathcal{D}}[\rho]
is not nice.

Another important question is whether
\xi_{\mathcal{D}}[\rho]
and
\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho]
are multiplicative. The remainder of the section is devoted to discussing this point.

6.2.
Multiplication

If
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
is a morphism into a finite monoid and
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
is a multiplicative rating map,
(M,\cdot)
is a monoid and
(R,+,\cdot)
is an idempotent semiring. We may lift the multiplication of
R
to
2^{R}
in the natural way: given
U,V\in 2^{R}
, we let
UV=\{qr\mid q\in U\text{ and }r\in V\}
. One may verify that
(2^{R},\cup,\cdot)
is an idempotent semiring. Similarly, we may lift the componentwise multiplication on
M\times R
to
2^{M\times R}
and
(2^{M\times R},\cup,\cdot)
is an idempotent semiring. Whenever we consider semiring structures for
2^{R}
and
2^{M\times R}
, these are the additions and multiplications that we shall use.

Unfortunately, even though
2^{R}
and
2^{M\times R}
are semirings,
neither

\xi_{\mathcal{D}}[\rho]:2^{A^{*}}\to 2^{R}
nor
\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho]:2^{A^{*}}\to 2^{M\times R}
are multiplicative: they are not monoid morphisms for multiplication. However, it turns out that they behave almost as multiplicative rating maps when the class
\mathcal{D}
satisfies appropriate properties related to closure under concatenation. We formalize this with a new notion: quasi-multiplicative rating maps.

Quasi-multiplicative rating maps.
Let
\rho:(2^{A^{*}},\cup)\to(R,+)
be a rating map whose rating set is equipped with a multiplication “
\cdot
” such that
(R,+,\cdot)
is a semiring (however,
\rho
is not required to be multiplicative for this multiplication). Finally, let
\mu_{\rho}
be an endomorphism of the additive monoid
(R,+)
(
i.e.
,
\mu_{\rho}(0_{R})=0_{R}
and for all
r,s\in R
,
\mu_{\rho}(r+s)=\mu_{\rho}(r)+\mu_{\rho}(s)
). We say that
\rho
is
quasi-multiplicative for
\mu_{\rho}

when the following axioms are satisfied:

(1)

For every
q,r,s\in R
,
\mu_{\rho}(q\mu_{\rho}(r)s)=\mu_{\rho}(qrs)
.

(2)

For every
K_{1},K_{2}\subseteq A^{*}
, we have
\rho(K_{1}K_{2})=\mu_{\rho}(\rho(K_{1})\cdot\rho(K_{2}))
.

For the sake of improved readability, we often abuse terminology and simply say that “the rating map
\rho
is quasi-multiplicative”, assuming implicitly that the endomorphism
\mu_{\rho}
is defined and fixed. Note however that this is slightly ambiguous as there might be several endomorphisms of
(R,+)
satisfying the above axioms for the same rating map
\rho
.

Remark
\thethm
.

Since
\mu_{\rho}
is an endomorphism of
(R,+)
, it preserves the canonical order on
R
. Given
q,r\in R
such that
q\leq r
, we have
\mu_{\rho}(q)\leq\mu_{\rho}(r)
. Indeed, by definition,
q\leq r
means that
q+r=r
. Therefore, since
\mu_{\rho}
is a morphism,
\mu_{\rho}(q)+\mu_{\rho}(r)=\mu_{\rho}(r)
which means that
\mu_{\rho}(q)\leq\mu_{\rho}(r)
. We use this property implicitly in proofs.

Remark
\thethm
.

Clearly, a true multiplicative rating map is always quasi-multiplicative. Indeed, in this case, it suffices to choose
\mu_{\rho}
as the identity;
\mu_{\rho}(r)=r
for all
r\in R
.

We have the following useful fact about quasi-multiplicative rating maps.

Fact
\thethm
.

Let
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
be a quasi-multiplicative rating map. For every language
H\subseteq A^{*}
, we have
\rho(H)=\mu_{\rho}(\rho(H))
.

Proof.

Axiom
2
in the definition yields
\rho(H)=\mu_{\rho}(\rho(H)\cdot\rho(\varepsilon))
. Thus, Axiom
1
yields
\mu_{\rho}(\rho(H))=\mu_{\rho}(\mu_{\rho}(\rho(H)\cdot\rho(\varepsilon)))=\mu_{\rho}(\rho(H)\cdot\rho(\varepsilon))=\rho(H)
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Additionally, we shall need the following lemma which is a straightforward adaptation of a result proved in
[
pzcovering2
, Lemma 5.8]
to quasi-multiplicative rating maps.

Lemma
\thethm
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a quotient-closed lattice and
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
be a quasi-multiplicative rating map. For every
H,L\subseteq A^{*}
,
q\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[H,\rho\right]
and
r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[L,\rho\right]
, we have
\mu_{\rho}(qr)\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[HL,\rho\right]
.

Proof.

Let
q\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[H,\rho\right]
and
r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[L,\rho\right]
. By definition, it suffices to prove that for every
\mathcal{D}
-cover
\mathbf{K}
of
HL
, we have
\mu_{\rho}(qr)\in\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})
. Let
\mathbf{K}
be a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
HL
, we have to find
K\in\mathbf{K}
such that
\mu_{\rho}(qr)\leq\rho(K)
. We use the following claim which is based on the Myhill-Nerode theorem.

Claim
.

There exists a language
G\in\mathcal{D}
which satisfies the following two properties:

(1)

For all
u\in H
, there exists
K\in\mathbf{K}
such that
G\subseteq u^{-1}K
.

(2)

r\leq\rho(G)

Proof of the claim.

For every
u\in H
, we let
\mathbf{Q}_{u}=\{u^{-1}K\mid K\in\mathbf{K}\}
. Clearly,
\mathbf{Q}_{u}
is a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
since
\mathbf{K}
is a cover of
HL
and
\mathcal{D}
is closed under quotients. Moreover, we know by hypothesis on
\mathcal{D}
that all languages in
\mathbf{K}
are regular. Therefore, it follows from the Myhill-Nerode theorem that they have finitely many quotients. Thus, while there might be infinitely many words
u\in H
, there are only finitely many distinct sets
\mathbf{Q}_{u}
. It follows that we may use finitely many intersections to build a
\mathcal{D}
-cover
\mathbf{Q}
of
L
such that for every
Q\in\mathbf{Q}
and every
u\in H
, there exists
K\in\mathbf{K}
satisfying
Q\subseteq u^{-1}K
. This means that all
Q\in\mathbf{Q}
satisfy the first item in the claim, we now pick one which satisfies the second one as well.

Since
r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[L,\rho\right]
, and
\mathbf{Q}
is a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
, we have
r\in\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{Q})
. Thus, we get
G\in\mathbf{Q}
such that
r\leq\rho(G)
by definition. This concludes the proof of the claim.
∎

We may now finish the proof of Lemma
6.2
. Let
G\in\mathcal{D}
be as defined in the claim and consider the following set:

\mathbf{G}=\left\{\bigcap_{v\in G}Kv^{-1}\mid K\in\mathbf{K}\right\}.

Observe that all languages in
\mathbf{G}
belong to
\mathcal{D}
. Indeed, by hypothesis on
\mathcal{D}
, every
K\in\mathbf{K}
is regular. Thus, it has finitely many right quotients by the Myhill-Nerode theorem and the language
\bigcap_{v\in H}Kv^{-1}
is the intersection of finitely many quotients of languages in
\mathcal{D}
. By closure under intersection and quotients, it follows that
\bigcap_{v\in G}Kv^{-1}\in\mathcal{D}
. Moreover,
\mathbf{G}
is a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
H
. Indeed, given
u\in H
, we have
K\in\mathbf{K}
such that
G\subseteq u^{-1}K
by the first assertion in the claim. Hence, for every
v\in G
, we have
u\in Kv^{-1}
and we obtain that
u\in\bigcap_{v\in G}Kv^{-1}
, which is an element of
\mathbf{G}
.

Therefore, since
q\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[H,\rho\right]
by hypothesis, we have
q\in\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{G})
and we obtain
G^{\prime}\in\mathbf{G}
such that
q\leq\rho(G^{\prime})
. Hence, since
r\leq\rho(G)
by the second item in the claim, we have
qr\leq\rho(G^{\prime})\cdot\rho(G)
. Since
\rho
is quasi-multiplicative over
\mathcal{D}
and
G,G^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
, it follows from Axiom
2
in the definition of quasi-multiplicative rating maps that,

\rho(G^{\prime}G)=\mu_{\rho}(\rho(G^{\prime})\cdot\rho(G))

Moreover, since
\mu_{\rho}
is an endomorphism of
(R,+)
and
qr\leq\rho(G^{\prime})\cdot\rho(G)
, we have by Fact
4.1
:

\mu_{\rho}(qr)\leq\mu_{\rho}(\rho(G^{\prime})\cdot\rho(G))

Altogether, we get
\mu_{\rho}(qr)\leq\rho(G^{\prime}G)
. Finally, observe that
G^{\prime}G\subseteq K
for some
K\in\mathbf{K}
. Indeed, if
w\in G^{\prime}G
, we have
w=uv
with
u\in G^{\prime}
and
v\in G
. Moreover,
G^{\prime}=\bigcap_{v\in G}Kv^{-1}
for some
K\in\mathbf{K}
by definition of
\mathbf{G}
. Hence,
u\in Kv^{-1}
which yields
w=uv\in K
. Altogether, we get that
\mu_{\rho}(qr)\leq\rho(G^{\prime}G)\leq\rho(K)
, which concludes the proof.
∎

We now prove that when
\mathcal{D}
is a quotient-closed lattice closed under concatenation, the rating map
\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho]
is quasi-multiplicative provided that
\alpha
is a morphism and
\rho
is already quasi-multiplicative (actually, this is also true for
\xi_{\mathcal{D}}[\rho]
but we do not need this result). This result is tailored to the situation in which we shall later use
\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho]
:
\mathcal{D}={Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
.

Lemma
\thethm
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a quotient-closed lattice closed under concatenation,
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
be a morphism and
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
be a quasi-multiplicative rating map. Then,
\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho]
is quasi-multiplicative for the following associated endomorphism
\mu_{\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho]}
of
(2^{M\times R},\cup)
:

\mu_{\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho]}(T)=\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}\{(s,\mu_{\rho}(r))\mid(s,r)\in T\}\quad\text{for every $T\in 2^{M\times R}$}.

Proof.

We already know from Proposition
6.1
that
\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho]
is a rating map. Hence, we have to prove that the axioms of quasi-multiplicative rating maps hold for the endomorphism
\mu_{\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho]}
of
(2^{M\times R},\cup)
described in the lemma (it is clear from the definition that this is indeed an endomorphism). For the sake of avoiding clutter, we write
\mu
for
\mu_{\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho]}
.

We start with the first axiom. Consider
T,U,V\in 2^{M\times R}
. We have to show that
\mu(T\mu(U)V)=\mu(TUV)
. Assume first that
(s,r)\in\mu(T\mu(U)V)
. By definition of
\mu
, this yields
(s_{1},r_{1})\in T
,
(s_{2},r_{2})\in\mu(U)
and
(s_{3},r_{3})\in V
such that
s=s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}
and
r\leq\mu_{\rho}(r_{1}r_{2}r_{3})
. Since
(s_{2},r_{2})\in\mu(U)
, we have
(s_{2},r^{\prime}_{2})\in U
such that
r_{2}\leq\mu_{\rho}(r^{\prime}_{2})
. It follows that
r_{1}r_{2}r_{3}\leq r_{1}\mu_{\rho}(r^{\prime}_{2})r_{2}
and since
\mu_{\rho}
is an endomorphism of
(R,+)
, we obtain,

r\leq\mu_{\rho}(r_{1}r_{2}r_{3})\leq\mu_{\rho}(r_{1}\mu_{\rho}(r^{\prime}_{2})r_{2}).

Since
\rho
is quasi-multiplicative, the first axiom in the definition yields that
\mu_{\rho}(r_{1}\mu_{\rho}(r^{\prime}_{2})r_{2})=\mu_{\rho}(r_{1}r^{\prime}_{2}r_{3})
and we get
r\leq\mu_{\rho}(r_{1}r^{\prime}_{2}r_{3})
. Since
(s_{1},r_{1})\in T
,
(s_{2},r^{\prime}_{2})\in U
and
(s_{3},r_{3})\in V
. This yields
(s,r)=(s_{1}s_{2}s_{3},r)\in\mu(TUV)
.

Conversely, assume that
(s,r)\in\mu(TUV)
. By definition of
\mu
, we get
(s_{1},r_{1})\in T
,
(s_{2},r_{2})\in U
and
(s_{3},r_{3})\in V
such that
s=s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}
and
r\leq\mu_{\rho}(r_{1}r_{2}r_{3})
. The first axiom in the definition of quasi-multiplicative rating maps yields that
\mu_{\rho}(r_{1}\mu_{\rho}(r_{2})r_{2})=\mu_{\rho}(r_{1}r_{2}r_{3})
. Therefore,
r\leq\mu_{\rho}(r_{1}\mu_{\rho}(r_{2})r_{3})
. Moreover, since
(s_{2},r_{2})\in U
, it is immediate that
(s_{2},\mu_{\rho}(r_{2}))\in\mu(U)
by definition of
\mu
. Altogether, this implies that
(s,r)\in\mu(T\mu(U)V)
.

It remains to establish that
\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho]
fulfills the Axiom
2
. Let
K_{1},K_{2}\subseteq A^{*}
. We show that,

\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho](K_{1}K_{2})=\mu(\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho](K_{1})\cdot\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho](K_{2})).

(3)

We start with the right to left inclusion. Consider
(s,r)\in\mu\big(\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho](K_{1})\cdot\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho](K_{2})\big)
. By definition of
\mu
we have
(s_{1},r_{1})\in\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho](K_{1})
and
(s_{2},r_{2})\in\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho](K_{2})
such that
s=s_{1}s_{2}
and
r\leq\mu_{\rho}(r_{1}r_{2})
. By definition of
\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho]
, this means that
r_{1}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[K_{1}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{1}),\rho\right]
and
r_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{2}),\rho\right]
. Since
\mathcal{D}
is a quotient-closed lattice, Lemma
6.2
yields that:

\mu_{\rho}(r_{1}r_{2})\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(K_{1}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{1}))\cdot(K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{2})),\rho\right].

Observe that
(K_{1}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{1}))\cdot(K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{2}))\subseteq K_{1}K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{1})\alpha^{-1}(s_{2})
. Moreover, since
\alpha
is a morphism, it is clear that
\alpha^{-1}(s_{1})\alpha^{-1}(s_{2})\subseteq\alpha^{-1}(s_{1}s_{2})=\alpha^{-1}(s)
. Altogether, this means that we have
(K_{1}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{1}))\cdot(K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{2}))\subseteq K_{1}K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s)
. Thus, Fact
4.2
yields
\mu_{\rho}(r_{1}r_{2})\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[K_{1}K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho\right]
. Since
r\leq\mu_{\rho}(r_{1}r_{2})
, this implies
r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[K_{1}K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho\right]
, which exactly says that
(s,r)\in\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho](K_{1}K_{2})
, concluding the proof for the left to right inclusion.

We turn to the converse inclusion. This is where we need
\mathcal{D}
to be closed under concatenation. Let
(s,r)\in\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho](K_{1}K_{2})
. We show that
(s,r)\in\mu\big(\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho](K_{1})\cdot\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho](K_{2})\big)
. By definition, we have
r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[K_{1}K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho\right]
. For every
t\in M
and
i\in\{1,2\}
, we define
\mathbf{H}_{i,t}
as an optimal
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
K_{i}\cap\alpha^{-1}(t)
. Consider the following finite set of languages
\mathbf{H}
,

\mathbf{H}=\{H_{1}H_{2}\mid\text{there exist $s_{1},s_{2}\in M$ such that $s_{1}s_{2}=s$, $H_{1}\in\mathbf{H}_{1,s_{1}}$ and $H_{2}\in\mathbf{H}_{2,s_{2}}$}\}.

We prove that
\mathbf{H}
is a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
K_{1}K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s)
. Clearly all languages in
\mathbf{H}
belong to
\mathcal{D}
since
\mathcal{D}
is closed under concatenation by Theorem
3.1
. Let us show that
\mathbf{H}
is a cover of
K_{1}K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s)
. Consider
w\in K_{1}K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s)
, we exhibit
H\in\mathbf{H}
such that
w\in H
. Since
w\in K_{1}K_{2}
, we have
w=w_{1}w_{2}
with
w_{1}\in K_{1}
and
w_{2}\in K_{2}
. Let
s_{1}=\alpha(w_{1})
and
s_{2}=\alpha(w_{2})
. Altogether, this means that
w_{1}\in K_{1}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{1})
and
w_{2}\in K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{2})
. Therefore, we have
H_{1}\in\mathbf{H}_{1,s_{1}}
and
H_{2}\in\mathbf{H}_{2,s_{2}}
such that
w_{1}\in H_{1}
and
w_{2}\in H_{2}
. This yields
w\in H_{1}H_{2}
. Finally,
s_{1}s_{2}=\alpha(w)=s
which yields that
H_{1}H_{2}\in\mathbf{H}
by definition.

We may now finish the argument and show that
(s,r)\in\mu(\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho](K_{1})\cdot\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho](K_{2}))
. Recall that
r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[K_{1}K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho\right]
. Thus, since
\mathbf{H}
is a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
K_{1}K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s)
, we have
r\in\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{H})
. It follows that there exists
H\in\mathbf{H}
such that
r\leq\rho(H)
. By definition of
\mathbf{H}
, we have
H=H_{1}H_{2}
with
H_{1}\in\mathbf{H}_{1,s_{1}}
and
H_{2}\in\mathbf{H}_{2,s_{2}}
where
s_{1},s_{2}\in M
satisfy
s_{1}s_{2}=s
. Let
r_{1}=\rho(H_{1})
and
r_{2}=\rho(H_{2})
. Since
\mathbf{H}_{1,s_{1}}
and
\mathbf{H}_{2,s_{2}}
are optimal
\mathcal{D}
-covers of
K_{1}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{1})
and
K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{2})
respectively, we have
r_{1}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[K_{1}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{1}),\rho\right]
and
r_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{2}),\rho\right]
. It follows that
(s_{1},r_{1})\in\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho](K_{1})
and
(s_{2},r_{2})\in\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho](K_{2})
. Consequently,
(s,r_{1}r_{2})=(s_{1}s_{2},r_{1}r_{2})\in\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho](K_{1})\cdot\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho](K_{2})
. Finally, by hypothesis and since
\rho
is quasi-multiplicative, we have,

r\leq\rho(H)=\rho(H_{1}H_{2})=\mu_{\rho}(\rho(H_{1})\cdot\rho(H_{2}))=\mu_{\rho}(r_{1}r_{2}).

By definition of
\mu
, this yields
(s,r)\in\mu(\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho](K_{1})\cdot\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho](K_{2}))
, concluding the proof.
∎

We present a second result for rating maps of the form
\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho]:2^{A^{*}}\to 2^{R}
. As expected, we shall consider the case
\mathcal{D}={Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
. In that case, we are not able to prove that
\xi_{\mathcal{D}}[\rho]
is quasi-multiplicative (the issue being that
{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})
is
not
closed under concatenation in general). We deal with this problem using two separate results. First, we show that when
\mathcal{D}
is a quotient-closed lattice of regular languages
closed under concatenation
, while
\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho]
might not be quasi-multiplicative itself, it coincides with a quasi-multiplicative rating map over languages in
\mathcal{D}
. Note that this result is where we use the weak concatenation principle that we presented for Boolean closure in Lemma
3.1
.

Lemma
\thethm
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a quotient-closed lattice closed under concatenation and
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
a multiplicative rating map. There exists a rating map
\tau:2^{A^{*}}\to 2^{R}
which satisfies the two following conditions:

(1)

\tau
is quasi-multiplicative for the endomorphism
\mu_{\tau}:U\mapsto\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}U
of
(2^{R},\cup)
, and,

(2)

for every
K\in\mathcal{D}
, we have
\tau(K)=\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho](K)
.

Proof.

We first define
\tau:2^{A^{*}}\to 2^{R}
. For every
K\subseteq A^{*}
, we let,

\tau(K)=\bigcap_{\{L\in\mathcal{D}\mid K\subseteq L\}}\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho](L)

It is immediate by definition that when
K\in\mathcal{D}
, we have
\tau(K)=\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho](K)
. We have to verify that
\tau
is a quasi-multiplicative rating map. We need the following fact.

Fact
\thethm
.

For all
K\subseteq A^{*}
, there exists
L\in\mathcal{D}
such that
K\subseteq L
and
\tau(K)=\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho](L)
.

Proof.

Since
2^{R}
is finite, there are finitely many languages
L_{1},\dots,L_{n}\in\mathcal{D}
such that
K\subseteq L_{i}
for every
i\leq n
and
\tau(K)=\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho](L_{1})\cap\cdots\cap\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho](L_{n})
. Since
\mathcal{D}
is a lattice by hypothesis, we have
L=L_{1}\cap\cdots\cap L_{n}\in\mathcal{D}
. Clearly,
K\subseteq L
which implies
\tau(K)\subseteq\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho](L)
by definition of
\tau
. Finally, since
L\subseteq L_{i}
for every
i
, we obtain that
\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho](L)\subseteq\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho](L_{1})\cap\cdots\cap\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho](L_{n})=\tau(K)
. Altogether, we conclude that
\tau(K)=\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho](L)
.
∎

Let us first prove that
\tau
is a rating map. Clearly,
\tau(\emptyset)=\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho](\emptyset)=\emptyset
. Let
K_{1},K_{2}\subseteq A^{*}
, we prove that
\tau(K_{1}\cup K_{2})=\tau(K_{1})\cup\tau(K_{2})
. Clearly, every
H\in\mathcal{D}
containing
K_{1}\cup K_{2}
contains
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
as well. Thus,
\tau(K_{1})\cup\tau(K_{2})\subseteq\tau(K_{1}\cup K_{2})
by definition of
\tau
. For the converse inclusion, Fact
6.2
yields
L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{D}
such that
K_{i}\subseteq L_{i}
and
\tau(K_{i})=\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho](L_{i})
for
i=1,2
. Since
\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho]
is a rating map by Proposition
6.1
, we get
\tau(K_{1})\cup\tau(K_{2})=\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho](L_{1}\cup L_{2})
. Since
K_{1}\cup K_{2}\subseteq L_{1}\cup L_{2}
, the definition of
\tau
then yields
\tau(K_{1}\cup K_{2})\subseteq\tau(K_{1})\cup\tau(K_{2})
.

It remains to prove that
\tau
is quasi-multiplicative for the endomorphism
\mu_{\tau}:U\mapsto\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}U
of
(2^{R},\cup)
. Axiom
1
is immediate: for every
T,U,V\in 2^{R}
, we have
\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}(T(\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}U)V)=\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}(TUV)
.

We turn to Axiom
2
. For
K_{1},K_{2}\subseteq A^{*}
, we prove that
\tau(K_{1}K_{2})=\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}(\tau(K_{1})\cdot\tau(K_{2}))
. We start with the right to left inclusion. Let
r\in\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}(\tau(K_{1})\cdot\tau(K_{2}))
. We show that
r\in\tau(K_{1}K_{2})
. By definition, this boils down to proving that
r\in\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho](H)
for every
H\in\mathcal{D}
such that
K_{1}K_{2}\subseteq H
. We fix
H
for the proof. Consider the two following languages,

U_{1}=\bigcap_{v\in K_{2}}Hv^{-1}\quad\text{and}\quad U_{2}=\bigcap_{u\in U_{1}}u^{-1}H.

Since
\mathcal{D}
is a quotient-closed lattice of regular languages and
H\in\mathcal{D}
, we have
U_{1},U_{2}\in\mathcal{D}
(recall that a regular language has finitely many quotients by the Myhill-Nerode theorem). Since
K_{1}K_{2}\subseteq H
, one may verify that
K_{i}\subseteq U_{i}
for
i=1,2
. This yields
\tau(K_{i})\subseteq\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho](U_{i})
by definition of
\tau
. Therefore, since we have
r\in\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}(\tau(K_{1})\cdot\tau(K_{2}))
, we obtain
r_{i}\in\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho](U_{i})
(
i.e.

r_{i}\in\mathcal{I}_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}\left[U_{i},\rho\right]
) for
i=1,2
such that
r\leq r_{1}r_{2}
. By Lemma
3.1
,
{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})
is a quotient-closed Boolean algebra. Thus, since
\rho
is a multiplicative rating map, Lemma
6.2
yields
r_{1}r_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}\left[U_{1}U_{2},\rho\right]
. Since
r\leq r_{1}r_{2}
, we obtain
r\in\mathcal{I}_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}\left[U_{1}U_{2},\rho\right]
by definition of imprints. Finally, we have
U_{1}U_{2}\subseteq H
by definition of
U_{2}
. Hence, Fact
4.2
yields
r\in\mathcal{I}_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}\left[H,\rho\right]=\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho](H)
.

We turn to the converse inclusion. Let
r\in\tau(K_{1}K_{2})
. We show that
r\in\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}(\tau(K_{1})\cdot\tau(K_{2}))
. For
i=1,2
, Fact
6.2
yields
L_{i}\in\mathcal{D}
such that
K_{i}\subseteq L_{i}
and
\tau(K_{i})=\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho](L_{i})
. We let
\mathbf{H}_{i}
as an optimal
{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})
-cover (for
\rho
) of
L_{i}
. By definition,
\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{H}_{i})=\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho](L_{i})=\tau(K_{i})
. Since
L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{D}
, we may apply Lemma
3.1
to build a
{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})
-cover
\mathbf{H}
of
L_{1}L_{2}
such that for every
H\in\mathbf{H}
, there exist
H_{1}\in\mathbf{H}_{1}
and
H_{2}\in\mathbf{H}_{2}
such that
H\subseteq H_{1}H_{2}
. By hypothesis
r\in\tau(K_{1}K_{2})
. Moreover, we have
K_{1}K_{2}\subseteq L_{1}L_{2}
and
L_{1}L_{2}\in\mathcal{D}
since
\mathcal{D}
is closed under concatenation. Thus,
r\in\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho](L_{1}L_{2})
by definition of
\tau
. It follows that
r\in\mathcal{I}_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}\left[L_{1}L_{2},\rho\right]
and since
\mathbf{H}
is a
{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})
-cover of
L_{1}L_{2}
, we get
H\in\mathbf{H}
such that
r\leq\rho(H)
. By definition of
\mathbf{H}
, there exist
H_{1}\in\mathbf{H}_{1}
and
H_{2}\in\mathbf{H}_{2}
such that
H\subseteq H_{1}H_{2}
. This implies that
r\leq\rho(H_{1})\cdot\rho(H_{2})
. Finally,
\rho(H_{i})\in\tau(K_{i})
for
i=1,2
by definition of
\mathbf{H}_{i}
. Thus, we get
r\in\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}(\tau(K_{1})\cdot\tau(K_{2}))
which concludes the proof.
∎

We complete Lemma
6.2
with another statement which requires that
\mathcal{D}
is closed under
marked
concatenation (this will be the case in practice since we apply these results for
\mathcal{D}={Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
). In this case as well, we use our weak concatenation principle for classes of the form
{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})
. Specifically, we apply the variant for marked concatenation (
i.e.
Lemma
3.1
).

Lemma
\thethm
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a quotient-closed lattice closed under marked concatenation and
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
a multiplicative rating map. For every
K_{1},K_{2}\in\mathcal{D}
and
a\in A
, we have,

\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho](K_{1}aK_{2})=\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}(\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho](K_{1})\cdot\{\rho(a)\}\cdot\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho](K_{2}))

Proof.

For the sake of avoiding clutter, we write
\xi
for
\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho]
. We start with the right to left inclusion. Let
r\in\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}(\xi(K_{1})\cdot\{\rho(a)\}\cdot\xi(K_{2}))
. We show that
r\in\xi(K_{1}aK_{2})
. By definition, we have
r_{i}\in\xi(K_{i})
for
i=1,2
such that
r\leq r_{1}\rho(a)r_{2}
. We have
r_{i}\in\mathcal{I}_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}\left[K_{i},\rho\right]
by definition of
\xi=\xi_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}[\rho]
. Clearly, we also have
\rho(a)\in\mathcal{I}_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}\left[\{a\},\rho\right]
. By Lemma
3.1
,
{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})
is a quotient-closed Boolean algebra. Thus, since
\rho
is a multiplicative rating map, Lemma
6.2
yields
r_{1}\rho(a)r_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}\left[K_{1}aK_{2},\rho\right]
. Since
r\leq r_{1}\rho(a)r_{2}
, we get
r\in\mathcal{I}_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}\left[K_{1}aK_{2},\rho\right]
. By definition, this exactly says that
r\in\xi(K_{1}aK_{2})
. We turn to the converse inclusion.

Let
r\in\xi(K_{1}aK_{2})
. We show that
r\in\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}(\xi(K_{1})\cdot\{\rho(a)\}\cdot\xi(K_{2}))
. For
i=1,2
, we let
\mathbf{H}_{i}
as an optimal
{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})
-cover (for
\rho
) of
K_{i}
for
i=1,2
. By definition, we have
\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{H}_{i})=\xi(K_{i})
. Since
K_{1},K_{2}\in\mathcal{D}
, we may apply Lemma

to build a
{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})
-cover

of

such that for every

. Therefore, since
\mathbf{H}
is a
{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})
-cover of

\mathbf{H}

7.
Characterization of
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-optimal imprints

In this section, we recall the theorem of
[
pseps3j
]
for classes of the form
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
(when
\mathcal{C}
is a finite quotient-closed Boolean algebra). It states a characterization of
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-optimal imprints: for a
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
and a multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
,
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\alpha}[\rho]
is characterized as the least subset of
M\times R
satisfying specific properties. When used in the special case when
\rho
is nice, one obtains a least fixpoint procedure for computing
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\alpha}[\rho]
from
\alpha
and
\rho
. By Proposition
4.3
, this yields an algorithm for solving
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-covering. However, deciding
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-covering is not our motivation here: we need this characterization in order to use it as a subresult when proving Theorem
5
.

Unfortunately, there are technical complications. When we apply the characterization as a subresult, we shall do so for rating maps which are not multiplicative, only quasi-multiplicative (as expected, we build them using the constructions presented in Section
6
). This case is not covered by the statement of
[
pseps3j
]
, which only deals with (true) multiplicative rating maps. Consequently, we have to generalize this statement. We avoid redoing the whole proof, by obtaining the generalized statement as a corollary of the original one from
[
pseps3j
]
. Additionally, we take this opportunity to slightly tweak the original statement in order to better accommodate our use of the theorem in Sections
8
and
9
.

We first present the theorem and then focus on its proof. We fix an arbitrary finite quotient-closed Boolean algebra
\mathcal{C}
for the presentation.

7.1.
Statement

Consider a
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
and a rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
which is quasi-multiplicative (there is no other constraint on
\rho
, in particular, it need not be nice). Recall that since
\alpha
is
\mathcal{C}
-compatible, we know that for every
s\in M
,
[s]_{\mathcal{C}}
is well-defined as a
\sim_{\mathcal{C}}
-class containing
\alpha^{-1}(s)
. We say that a subset
S\subseteq M\times R
is
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated
(for
\alpha
and
\rho
) when it satisfies the following properties:

(1)

Trivial elements:
For every
w\in A^{*}
,
(\alpha(w),\rho(w))\in S
.

(2)

Downset:
We have
S=\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}S
.

(3)

Multiplication
: For every
(s_{1},r_{1}),(s_{2},r_{2})\in S
, we have
(s_{1}s_{2},r_{1}r_{2})\in S
.

(4)

{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-closure
: For every pair of (multiplicative) idempotents
(e,f)\in S
, we have:

(e,f\cdot\rho([e]_{\mathcal{C}})\cdot f)\in S.

We prove the following statement as a corollary of
[
pseps3j
, Theorem 6.5]
.

Theorem
\thethm
.

Consider a
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
and a quasi-multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
. Moreover, let
S
be the least
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subset of
M\times R
. Then,

\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\alpha}[\rho]=\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}\big\{(s,\mu_{\rho}(r))\mid(s,r)\in S\big\}.

Recall that a (true) multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
is also quasi-multiplicative for the endomorphism
\mu_{\rho}
defined as the identity on
R
(see Remark
6.2
). Thus, in this case, Theorem
7.1
yields that
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\alpha}[\rho]=\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}S=S
where
S
is the least
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subset of
M\times R
. This is the original statement of
[
pseps3j
]
. When
\rho
is a nice multiplicative rating map, it is clear that one may compute the least
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subset of
M\times R
with a least fixpoint algorithm. Therefore, we get an algorithm for
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-covering by Proposition
4.3
.

Remark
\thethm
.

There is a difference between Theorem
7.1
for
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
and Theorem
5
for
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
: the latter is restricted to
nice
rating maps while this is not the case for the former. As explained in Remark
5
, while it is easy to miss, this difference is crucial.

7.2.
Proof of Theorem
7.1

We fix a
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
and a quasi-multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
. Since
\rho
is quasi-multiplicative, we have a semiring structure
(R,+,\cdot)
on
R
and an endomorphism
\mu_{\rho}
of
(R,+)
satisfying the appropriate axioms. Finally, we let
S
as the least
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subset of
M\times R
for
\alpha
and
\rho
. We show that
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\alpha}[\rho]=\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}\big\{(s,\mu_{\rho}(r))\mid(s,r)\in S\big\}
.

We first prove that the surjective restriction of
\rho
is a
true
multiplicative rating map (for a new multiplication on the rating set which is distinct from “
\cdot

[
pseps3j
]
.

We define a new multiplication on
R

\odot

q,r\in R

. It is immediate from Axiom

(R,+)

\rho(K_{1}K_{2})=\rho(K_{1})\odot\rho(K_{2})
. Thus, the rating map

(2^{A^{*}},\cdot)
to
(R,\cdot)
. Let
Q=\rho(2^{A^{*}})\subseteq R
and let

\rho

\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\alpha}[\rho]=\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}\left(\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\alpha}[\tau]\right)

[
pseps3j
]

\alpha

\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\alpha}[\tau]=T

\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}T=\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}\{(s,\mu_{\rho}(r))\mid(s,r)\in S\}.

(4)

Indeed, this clearly implies
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\alpha}[\rho]=\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}\big\{(s,\mu_{\rho}(r))\mid(s,r)\in S\big\}

Remark
\thethm
.

We are dealing with two strongly connected

and

It now remains to prove (

\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}T\subseteq\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}\{(s,\mu_{\rho}(r))\mid(s,r)\in S\}

r\in R

for
\alpha

8.
Soundness in Theorem
5

We may now start the proof of Theorem
5
. In this section, we establish that the statement is sound. The proof is divided in two parts. First, we present a preliminary result, which applies to the Boolean closure operation in general,
i.e.
, to classes of the form
{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})
when
\mathcal{D}
is an arbitrary lattice. Then, we apply this preliminary result in the special case when
\mathcal{D}={Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
(for
\mathcal{C}
a finite quotient-closed Boolean algebra) to establish the soundness direction in Theorem
5
.

8.1.
Preliminary result

We first introduce terminology that we need to state our result. We fix a lattice
\mathcal{D}
. Moreover, we let
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
be a rating map. Let us recall the definition of the rating map
\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho]
, defined page
6.1
, which also depends on a map
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
:

\begin{array}[]{llll}\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha}[\rho]:&(2^{A^{*}},{\cup})&\to&(2^{M\times R},\cup)\\
&K&\mapsto&\big\{(s,r)\mid r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\alpha^{-1}(s)\cap K,\rho\right]\big\}.\end{array}

Using induction, we define a rating map
\tau_{n}:2^{A^{*}}\to Q_{n}
for every
n\in{\rm Nature}
. When
n=0
, the rating set
Q_{0}
is
(2^{R},\cup)
and
\tau_{0}
is defined as follows,

\begin{array}[]{llll}\tau_{0}:&(2^{A^{*}},\cup)&\to&(2^{R},\cup)\\
&K&\mapsto&\{\rho(w)\mid w\in K\}.\end{array}

It is immediate by definition that
\tau_{0}
is indeed a rating map (
i.e.
, a monoid morphism).

Assume now that
n\geq 1
and that
\tau_{n-1}:2^{A^{*}}\to Q_{n-1}
is defined. Recall that
\rho_{*}:A^{*}\to R
denotes the canonical map associated to the rating map
\rho
. We define
\tau_{n}
as:

\tau_{n}=\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\rho_{*}}[\tau_{n-1}].

By Proposition
6.1
,
\tau_{n}
is indeed a rating map. By definition, this means that for all
n\geq 1
, the rating set
Q_{n}
of
\tau_{n}
is

Q_{n}=(2^{R\times Q_{n-1}},\cup).

We complete this definition with maps
f_{n}:Q_{n}\to 2^{R}
for
n\in{\rm Nature}
, defined by induction on
n
.

•

For
n=0
, let
T\in Q_{0}=2^{R}
. We define:

f_{0}(T)=\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}\{r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}\mid r_{1},\dots,r_{k}\in T\}.

•

For
n\geq 1
, let
T\in Q_{n}=2^{R\times Q_{n-1}}
. We define:

f_{n}(T)={\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}}\left\{r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}\mid\begin{array}[]{c}\text{there exist $(r_{1},T_{1}),\dots,(r_{k},T_{k})\in T$ such that}\\
\text{$r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}\in f_{n-1}(T_{i})$ for every $i\leq k$}\end{array}\right\}.

The following fact is immediate from the definition.

Fact
\thethm
.

For every
n\in{\rm Nature}
and
U,U^{\prime}\in Q_{n}
such that
U\subseteq U^{\prime}
, we have
f_{n}(U)\subseteq f_{n}(U^{\prime})
.

We may now state the preliminary result that we shall use in our soundness direction of Theorem
5
.

Proposition
\thethm
.

Consider a language
L\in\mathcal{D}
. Then, the following inclusion holds:

\bigcap_{n\in{\rm Nature}}f_{n}(\tau_{n}(L))\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}\left[L,\rho\right].

Proof.

The proof is based on the following more involved statement which is proved by induction on
n\in{\rm Nature}
.

Lemma
\thethm
.

Let
n\in{\rm Nature}
and
L,K_{0},\dots,K_{n},H_{0},\dots,H_{n}\in\mathcal{D}

\{K_{i}\setminus H_{i}\mid i\leq n\}

.

Before proving the lemma, let us use it to prove the first property described in Proposition
8.1
. Let
L\in\mathcal{D}
be a language. We write
S
for the set,

S=\bigcap_{n\in{\rm Nature}}f_{n}(\tau_{n}(L)).

We show that
S\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}\left[L,\rho\right]
. First, we prove the following fact which describes a special optimal
{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})
-cover of
L
for
\rho
.

Fact
\thethm
.

There exist
n\in{\rm Nature}
and
K_{0},\dots,K_{n},H_{0},\dots,H_{n}\in\mathcal{D}
such that
\{K_{i}\setminus H_{i}\mid i\leq n\}
is an optimal
{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})
-cover of
L
for
\rho
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{H}
be an arbitrary optimal
{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})
-cover of
L
for
\rho
. Each
V\in\mathbf{H}
is the Boolean combination of languages in
\mathcal{D}
. We put it in disjunctive normal form. Each disjunct is an intersection languages belonging to
\mathcal{D}
, or whose complement belongs to
\mathcal{D}
. Since
\mathcal{D}
is lattice, both
\mathcal{D}
and the complement class
{co\/}\textup{-}\mathcal{D}
are closed under intersection. Therefore, each disjunct in the disjunctive normal form of
V
is actually of the form
K\setminus H
, where
K,H
both belong to
\mathcal{D}
. We let
\mathbf{K}
as the set of all languages
K\setminus H
which are a disjunct in the disjunctive normal form of some
V\in\mathbf{H}
. Clearly,
\mathbf{K}
remains a
{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})
-cover of
L
since
\mathbf{H}
was one. Moreover, it is immediate that
\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})\subseteq\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{H})
since every language in
\mathbf{K}
is included in a language of
\mathbf{H}
. Hence,
\mathbf{K}
remains an optimal
{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})
-cover of
L
for
\rho
since
\mathbf{H}
was one.
∎

We let
n\in{\rm Nature}
and
K_{0},\dots,K_{n},H_{0},\dots,H_{n}\in\mathcal{D}
be as defined in Fact
8.1
. We may now prove that
S\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}\left[L,\rho\right]
. Let
s\in S
. By hypothesis on
S
, we have
s\in f_{2n}(\tau_{2n}(L))
. Therefore, since
\{K_{i}\setminus H_{i}\mid i\leq n\}
is by definition a cover of
L
, it is immediate from Lemma
8.1
that there exists
j\leq n
such that
s\leq\rho(K_{j}\setminus H_{j})
. Since
\{K_{i}\setminus H_{i}\mid i\leq n\}
is an optimal
{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})
-cover of
L
for
\rho
, this implies that
s\in\mathcal{I}_{{Bool\/}(\mathcal{D})}\left[L,\rho\right]
which concludes the main proof.

We turn to the proof of Lemma
8.1
. The argument is an induction on
n\in{\rm Nature}
. We start with the base case
n=0
.

Base case.
Consider
L,K_{0},H_{0}\in\mathcal{D}
such that
\{K_{0}\setminus H_{0}\}
is a cover of
L
and let
s\in f_{0}(\tau_{0}(L))
. We have to show that
s\leq\rho(K_{0}\setminus H_{0})
. By definition of
f_{0}
, we get
r_{1},\dots,r_{k}\in\tau_{0}(L)
such that
s\leq r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}
. Moreover, the definition of
\tau_{0}
yields that for every
i\leq k
,
r_{i}=\rho(w_{i})
for some
w_{i}\in L
. Therefore,
r_{i}\leq\rho(L)
for every
i\leq k
and since
R
is idempotent for addition,
s\leq r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}\leq\rho(L)
. Finally, since
\{K_{0}\setminus H_{0}\}
is a cover of
L
, we have
L\subseteq K_{0}\setminus H_{0}
and we get
s\leq\rho(K_{0}\setminus H_{0})
, finishing the argument for the base case.

Inductive step.
We now assume that
n\geq 1
. Let
L,K_{0},\dots,K_{n},H_{0},\dots,H_{n}\in\mathcal{D}
such that
\{K_{i}\setminus H_{i}\mid i\leq n\}
is a cover of
L
and let
s\in f_{2n}(\tau_{2n}(L))
. We have to exhibit
j\leq n
such that
s\leq\rho(K_{j}\setminus H_{j})
. Using the hypothesis that
s\in f_{2n}(\tau_{2n}(L))
, we prove the following fact.

Fact
\thethm
.

There exists
(r,U)\in\tau_{2n}(L)
such that
s\in f_{2n-1}(U)
.

Proof.

By definition of
f_{2n}
, the hypothesis that
s\in f_{2n}(\tau_{2n}(L))
yields
(r^{\prime}_{1},U^{\prime}_{1}),\dots,(r^{\prime}_{k},U^{\prime}_{k})\in\tau_{2n}(L)
such that
r^{\prime}_{1}+\cdots+r^{\prime}_{k}\in f_{2n-1}(U^{\prime}_{i})
for every
i\leq k
and
s\leq r^{\prime}_{1}+\cdots+r^{\prime}_{k}
. We let
(r,U)=(r^{\prime}_{1},U^{\prime}_{1})\in\tau_{2n}(L)
. We have
r^{\prime}_{1}+\cdots+r^{\prime}_{k}\in f_{2n-1}(U)
and
s\leq r^{\prime}_{1}+\cdots+r^{\prime}_{k}
. Hence, since
f_{2n-1}(U)
is closed under downset (by definition), this implies
s\in f_{2n-1}(U)
.
∎

Recall that by definition,
\tau_{2n}
is the rating map
\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\rho_{*}}[\tau_{2n-1}]
. Hence,
(r,U)\in\tau_{2n}(L)
means that:

U\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\rho_{*}^{-1}(r)\cap L,\tau_{2n-1}\right],

which yields, by Fact
4.2
, that,

U\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[L,\tau_{2n-1}\right].

Since
\{K_{i}\setminus H_{i}\mid i\leq n\}
is a cover of
L
,
L,K_{1},\dots,K_{n}\in\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}
is a lattice, it follows that
\{L\cap K_{i}\mid i\leq n\}
is a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
. Therefore, since
U\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[L,\tau_{2n-1}\right]
, we obtain some
\ell\leq n
such that
U\subseteq\tau_{2n-1}(L\cap K_{\ell})
.

Furthermore, since
s\in f_{2n-1}(U)
, we may unravel the definition of
f_{2n-1}
which yields
(r_{1},U_{1}),\dots,(r_{k},U_{k})\in U
such that
r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}\in f_{2(n-1)}(U_{m})
for every
m\leq k
and
s\leq r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}
. Note that by definition of
f_{2(n-1)}
, this also implies that
s\in f_{2(n-1)}(U_{m})
for every
m\leq k
. We now distinguish two sub-cases.

Sub-case 1:
Assume that for every
m\leq k
, we have,

\rho_{*}^{-1}(r_{m})\cap(K_{\ell}\setminus H_{\ell})\neq\emptyset

This means that for every
m\leq k
, we have
w_{m}\in K_{\ell}\setminus H_{\ell}
such that
\rho(w_{m})=r_{m}
. In particular,
r_{m}=\rho(w_{m})\leq\rho(K_{\ell}\setminus H_{\ell})
for every
m\leq k
. Finally, since
R
is idempotent for addition, we get
r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}\leq\rho(K_{\ell}\setminus H_{\ell})
. Since
s\leq r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}
, we get
s\leq\rho(K_{\ell}\setminus H_{\ell})
and Lemma
8.1
holds for
j=\ell
in this case.

Sub-case 2:
Conversely, assume that there exists
m\leq k
such that,

\rho_{*}^{-1}(r_{m})\cap(K_{\ell}\setminus H_{\ell})=\emptyset

This implies that
K_{\ell}\cap\rho_{*}^{-1}(r_{m})\subseteq K_{\ell}\cap H_{\ell}
. Recall that
(r_{m},U_{m})\in U
and that
U\subseteq\tau_{2n-1}(L\cap K_{\ell})
. Since
\tau_{2n-1}
is the rating map
\zeta_{\mathcal{D}}^{\rho_{*}}[\tau_{2(n-1)}]
by definition, it follows that,

U_{m}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\rho_{*}^{-1}(r_{m})\cap L\cap K_{\ell},\tau_{2(n-1)}\right]

Combined with the inclusion
K_{\ell}\cap\rho_{*}^{-1}(r_{m})\subseteq K_{\ell}\cap H_{\ell}
and Fact
4.2
, this yields that,

U_{m}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[L\cap K_{\ell}\cap H_{\ell},\tau_{2(n-1)}\right]

Since
\mathcal{D}
is a lattice, it is clear that
\{L\cap K_{\ell}\cap H_{\ell}\}
is a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L\cap K_{\ell}\cap H_{\ell}
. Thus, we obtain that
U_{m}\subseteq\tau_{2(n-1)}(L\cap K_{\ell}\cap H_{\ell})
. Therefore, since
s\in f_{2(n-1)}(U_{m})
by definition of
U_{m}
, we obtain from Fact
8.1
that,

s\in f_{2(n-1)}(\tau_{2(n-1)}(L\cap K_{\ell}\cap H_{\ell}))

Finally, since
\{K_{i}\setminus H_{i}\mid i\leq n\}
was a cover of
L
, it is clear that
\{K_{i}\setminus H_{i}\mid i\leq n\text{ and }i\neq\ell\}
(of size
n-1
) is a cover of
L\cap K_{\ell}\cap H_{\ell}
. Therefore, it follows by induction on
n
in Lemma
8.1
that there exists
j\leq n
(with
j\neq\ell
) such that
s\leq\rho(K_{j}\setminus H_{j})
, finishing the proof.
∎

8.2.
Soundness proof for Theorem
5

We may now come back to our main objective: soundness in Theorem
5
. We fix a finite quotient-closed Boolean algebra
\mathcal{C}
and a multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
. We show that for every
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subset
S\subseteq({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R
(for
\rho
), we have
S\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\mathcal{C}}[\rho]
.

Remark
\thethm
.

Note that we do not use the hypothesis that
\rho
is nice for this direction. This is only needed for completeness.

By Theorem
3.1
,
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
is a lattice. Therefore, we may instantiate the definitions and results presented at the beginning of the section for our nice multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
in the special case when
\mathcal{D}={Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
. We keep using the same notation: we have the rating maps
\tau_{n}:2^{A^{*}}\to Q_{n}
(as we prove below, they are now quasi-multiplicative since
\mathcal{D}={Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
and
\rho
is multiplicative) and the maps
f_{n}:Q_{n}\to 2^{R}
.

We complete Proposition
8.1
with another statement specific to this special case. In fact, the proof of this second proposition is based on Theorem
7.1
, the characterization of
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-optimal imprints (we apply it to the rating maps
\tau_{n}
). Together, these two results imply soundness in Theorem
5
.

Proposition
\thethm
.

Consider
S\subseteq({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R
which is
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated for
\rho
. Then, the following inclusion holds for every
D\in{A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}}
,

S(D)\subseteq\bigcap_{n\in{\rm Nature}}f_{n}(\tau_{n}(D)).

When put together, Proposition
8.1
and Proposition
8.2
imply soundness in Theorem
5
. Indeed, consider a
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated set
S\subseteq({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R
. We show that
S\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\mathcal{C}}[\rho]
. This amounts to proving that for every
D\in{A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}}
, we have,

S(D)\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\mathcal{C}}[\rho](D)=\mathcal{I}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}\left[D,\rho\right].

It is immediate from Proposition
8.2
that,

S(D)\subseteq\bigcap_{n\in{\rm Nature}}f_{n}(\tau_{n}(D)).

Moreover, since
D\in{A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}}
, we have
D\in\mathcal{C}
, which implies that
D\in{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
. Therefore, Proposition
8.1
yields that,

\bigcap_{n\in{\rm Nature}}f_{n}(\tau_{n}(D))\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}\left[D,\rho\right].

Altogether, we get the desired inclusion:
S(D)\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}\left[D,\rho\right]
. This concludes the soundness proof.

It remains to prove Proposition
8.2
. We start with a few additional results about the rating maps
\tau_{n}
that we are able to prove using our new hypotheses (
i.e.
, that
\rho
is multiplicative and
\mathcal{D}={Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
).

Preliminaries.
Recall that
\rho
is multiplicative. Hence,
R
is a semiring
(R,+,\cdot)
. Since
Q_{0}=2^{R}
and
Q_{n}=2^{R\times Q_{n-1}}
for all
n\geq 1
, we may lift the multiplication of
R
to all the rating sets
Q_{n}
in the natural way. It is simple to verify that
(Q_{n},\cup,\cdot)
is a semiring for every
n\in{\rm Nature}
. We first show that the rating maps
\tau_{n}
are quasi-multiplicative for these multiplications.

Lemma
\thethm
.

The rating map
\tau_{0}:2^{A^{*}}\to Q_{0}
is multiplicative. Moreover, for every
n\geq 1
, the rating map
\tau_{n}:2^{A^{*}}\to Q_{n}
is quasi-multiplicative for the following endomorphism
\mu_{\tau_{n}}
of
(Q_{n},\cup)
:

\mu_{\tau_{n}}(T)=\mathord{\downarrow_{Q_{n-1}}}\{(r,\mu_{\tau_{n-1}}(V))\mid(r,V)\in T\}\quad\text{for every $T\in Q_{n}=2^{R\times Q_{n-1}}$}.

Proof.

Recall that
Q_{0}=2^{R}
and
\tau_{0}(K)=\{\rho(w)\mid w\in K\}
for every language
K
. It is immediate that
\tau_{0}
is multiplicative since
\rho
is multiplicative by hypothesis. The result for the rating maps
\tau_{n}
for
n\geq 1
is then immediate from Lemma
6.2
using induction on
n
since
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
is a quotient-closed lattice closed under concatenation by Theorem
3.1
.
∎

We complete this result with two lemmas which connect the hypothesis that the rating maps
\tau_{n}
are quasi-multiplicative with the maps
f_{n}:Q_{n}\to 2^{R}
.

Lemma
\thethm
.

For every
n\in{\rm Nature}
and
T\in Q_{n}
, we have
f_{n}(T)\subseteq f_{n}(\mu_{\tau_{n}}(T))
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
n\in{\rm Nature}
. When
n=0
,
\tau_{0}
is multiplicative and the endomorphism
\mu_{\tau_{0}}
of
(Q_{0},\cup)
is the identity on
Q_{0}
. Hence, the lemma is immediate. Assume now that
n\geq 1
. Consider
T\in Q_{n}
. Let
r\in f_{n}(T)\subseteq R
. By definition, we have
(r_{1},T_{1}),\dots,(r_{k},T_{k})\in T
such that
r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}\in f_{n-1}(T_{i})
for every
i\leq k
and
r\leq r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}
. By definition of
\mu_{\tau_{n}}
in Lemma
8.2
, we have
(r_{1},\mu_{\tau_{n-1}}(T_{1})),\dots,(r_{k},\mu_{\tau_{n-1}}(T_{k}))\in\mu_{\tau_{n}}(T)
. Moreover, since
r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}\in f_{n-1}(T_{i})
for every
i\leq k
it is immediate by induction hypothesis that
r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}\in f_{n-1}(\mu_{\tau_{n-1}}(T_{i}))
for every
i\leq k
. Altogether, we obtain that
r\in f_{n}(\mu_{\tau_{n}}(T))
, finishing the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma
\thethm
.

For every
n\in{\rm Nature}
and
T,T^{\prime}\in Q_{n}
, we have
f_{n}(T)\cdot f_{n}(T^{\prime})\subseteq f_{n}(T\cdot T^{\prime})
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
n\in{\rm Nature}
. We first handle the case
n=0
. Let
r\in f_{0}(T)\cdot f_{0}(T^{\prime})
. We have
s\in f_{0}(T)
and
s^{\prime}\in f_{0}(T^{\prime})
such that
r=ss^{\prime}
. By definition, this yields
r_{1},\dots,r_{k}\in T
and
r^{\prime}_{1},\dots,r^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}\in T^{\prime}
such that
s\leq r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}
and
s^{\prime}\leq r^{\prime}_{1}+\cdots+r^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}
. It follows that
ss^{\prime}\leq\sum_{i\leq k}\sum_{j\leq k^{\prime}}r_{i}r^{\prime}_{j}
. Since
r_{i}r^{\prime}_{j}\in T\cdot T^{\prime}
for every
i\leq k
and
j\leq k^{\prime}
, it follows that
ss^{\prime}\in f_{0}(T\cdot T^{\prime})
.

Assume now that
n\geq 1
. Let
r\in f_{n}(T)\cdot f_{n}(T^{\prime})
. We have
s\in f_{n}(T)
and
s^{\prime}\in f_{n}(T^{\prime})
such that
r=ss^{\prime}
. By definition, this yields
(r_{1},T_{1}),\dots,(r_{k},T_{k})\in T
and
(r^{\prime}_{1},T^{\prime}_{1}),\dots,(r^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}},T^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}})\in T^{\prime}
such that,

•

s\leq r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}
and
r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}\in f_{n-1}(T_{i})
for every
i\leq k
.

•

s^{\prime}\leq r^{\prime}_{1}+\cdots+r^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}
and
r^{\prime}_{1}+\cdots+r^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}\in f_{n-1}(T^{\prime}_{j})
for every
j\leq k^{\prime}
.

Clearly, we have
ss^{\prime}\leq\sum_{i\leq k}\sum_{j\leq k^{\prime}}r_{i}r^{\prime}_{j}
. Moreover, for every
i\leq k
and
j\leq k^{\prime}
, we have,

\sum_{i\leq k}\sum_{j\leq k^{\prime}}r_{i}r^{\prime}_{j}\in f_{n-1}(T_{i})\cdot f_{n-1}(T^{\prime}_{j}).

By induction hypothesis, this yields,

\sum_{i\leq k}\sum_{j\leq k^{\prime}}r_{i}r^{\prime}_{j}\in f_{n-1}(T_{i}\cdot T^{\prime}_{j}).

Finally, it is immediate that for every
i\leq k
and
j\leq k^{\prime}
, we have
(r_{i}r^{\prime}_{j},T_{i}\cdot T^{\prime}_{j})\in T\cdot T^{\prime}
. Altogether, this yields
ss^{\prime}\in f_{n}(T\cdot T^{\prime})
by definition.
∎

Proof of Proposition
8.2
.
We now turn to the main argument. We fix
S\subseteq({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R
which is
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated (for
\rho
). We have to show that
S(D)\subseteq f_{n}(\tau_{n}(D))
for every
n\in{\rm Nature}
and
D\in{A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}}
. The argument is an induction on
n\in{\rm Nature}
.

Base case.
Assume that
n=0
and let
D\in{A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}}
. We show that
S(D)\subseteq f_{0}(\tau_{0}(D))
. Let
r\in S(D)
,
i.e.
,
(D,r)\in S
. Since
S
is
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated, we get from (
2
) that there exist
r_{1},\dots,r_{k}\in R
such that
r\leq r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}
and
(D,r_{i},\{r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}\})\in\mathcal{R}^{\rho}_{S}
for every
i\leq k
. For
i\leq k
, one may verify from the definition of
\mathcal{R}^{\rho}_{S}
that since
(D,r_{i},\{r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}\})\in\mathcal{R}^{\rho}_{S}
, we have
w_{i}\in A^{*}
such that
[w_{i}]_{\mathcal{C}}=D
and
\rho(w_{i})=r_{i}
. In particular,
w_{1},\dots,w_{k}\in D
and it is therefore immediate from the definition of
\tau_{0}
that
r_{1},\dots,r_{k}\in\tau_{0}(D)
. Since
r\leq r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}
, we obtain
r\in f_{0}(\tau_{0}(D))
by definition of
f_{0}
, which concludes the proof.

Inductive step.
We now assume that
n\geq 1
. The argument is based on the following lemma, which is where we use the characterization of
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-optimal imprints (
i.e.
Theorem
7.1
): we apply it to the quasi-multiplicative rating map
\tau_{n-1}
. Moreover, this is also where we apply induction on
n
in Proposition
8.2
.

Lemma
\thethm
.

For all
(D,q,U)\in\mathcal{R}^{\rho}_{S}
, we have
T\in Q_{n-1}
satisfying the following properties:

\text{$T\in\mathcal{I}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}\left[\rho_{*}^{-1}(q)\cap D,\tau_{n-1}\right]$ and $U\subseteq f_{n-1}(T)$}.

We start by explaining how Lemma
8.2
can be applied to complete the main proof. Consider
D\in{A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}}
. We show that
S(D)\subseteq f_{n}(\tau_{n}(D))
. Let
r\in S(D)
(
i.e.
,
(D,r)\in S
). We prove that
r\in f_{n}(\tau_{n}(D))
.

Since
S
is
{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated, we get from (
2
) that there exist
r_{1},\dots,r_{k}\in R
such that
r\leq r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}
and
(D,r_{i},\{r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}\})\in\mathcal{R}^{\rho}_{S}
for every
i\leq k
. Lemma
8.2
then yields
T_{i}\in Q_{n-1}
such that
T_{i}\in\mathcal{I}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}\left[\rho_{*}^{-1}(r_{i})\cap D,\tau_{n-1}\right]
and
r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}\in f_{n-1}(T_{i})
for every
i\leq k
. By definition,
\tau_{n}
is the rating map
\zeta_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\rho_{*}}[\tau_{n-1}]:2^{A^{*}}\to 2^{R\times Q_{n-1}}
. Thus, if we unravel the definition, the hypothesis that
T_{i}\in\mathcal{I}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}\left[\rho_{*}^{-1}(r_{i})\cap D,\tau_{n-1}\right]
exactly says that,

(r_{i},U_{i})\in\tau_{n}(D).

Since we also have
r\leq r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}
and
r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}\in f_{n-1}(T_{i})
for every
i\leq k
, it is immediate by definition of
f_{n}
that
r\in f_{n}(\tau_{n}(D))
, finishing the proof of Proposition
8.2
. It remains to prove Lemma
8.2
.

Proof of Lemma
8.2
.

We first apply Theorem
7.1
and then use the result to prove the lemma. Clearly, the Cartesian product
({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R
is a monoid when equipped with the componentwise multiplication. Let
\alpha:A^{*}\to({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R
be the morphism defined by
\alpha(w)=([w]_{\mathcal{C}},\rho(w))
. Clearly,
\alpha
is a
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphism: for every pair
(D,r)\in({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R
, it suffices to define
[(D,r)]_{\mathcal{C}}=D
. Consequently, since we also know that
\tau_{n-1}
is quasi-multiplicative by Lemma
8.2
, we may apply Theorem
7.1
to obtain a description of the set
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}^{\alpha}[\tau_{n-1}]\subseteq({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R\times Q_{n-1}
. Consider the least
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subset
X
of
({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})\times R\times Q_{n-1}
for

$$([a]_{\mathcal{C}},\rho(a),\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}\left(\tau([\varepsilon]_{\mathcal{C}})\cdot\{\rho(a)\}\cdot\tau([\varepsilon]_{\mathcal{C}})\right))\in\mathcal{R}^{\rho}_{S}$$We know that ${Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})$ is a quotient-closed lattice of regular languages closed under marked concatenation by Theorem\penalty\ \ref{thm:polclos}. Therefore, since $\tau$ and $\xi_{{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\rho]$ coincide over languages in ${Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})$, we obtain from Lemma\penalty\ \ref{lem:nest:bpolm} that,
$$\tau([\varepsilon]_{\mathcal{C}}a[\varepsilon]_{\mathcal{C}})=\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}\left(\tau([\varepsilon]_{\mathcal{C}})\cdot\{\rho(a)\}\cdot\tau([\varepsilon]_{\mathcal{C}})\right)$$Altogether, we obtain $([a]_{\mathcal{C}},\rho(a),\tau([\varepsilon]_{\mathcal{C}}a[\varepsilon]_{\mathcal{C}}))\in\mathcal{R}^{\rho}_{S}$. Moreover, we have $\tau(a)\subseteq\tau([\varepsilon]_{\mathcal{C}}a[\varepsilon]_{\mathcal{C}})$. Thus, we obtain from closure under downset that $([a]_{\mathcal{C}},\rho(a),\tau(a))\in\mathcal{R}^{\rho}_{S}$ as desired. Finally, assume that $w=a_{1}\cdots a_{n}$ for $n\geq 2$ and $a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in A$. We already established that $([a_{i}]_{\mathcal{C}},\rho(a_{i}),\tau(a_{i}))\in\mathcal{R}^{\rho}_{S}$ for every $i\leq n$. Therefore, closure under multiplication and extended downset yield,
$$([w]_{\mathcal{C}},\rho(w),\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}\left(\tau(a_{1})\cdots\tau(a_{n})\right))\in\mathcal{R}^{\rho}_{S}$$Since $\tau$ is quasi-multiplicative for the endomorphism $\mu_{\tau}:U\mapsto\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}U$ of $(2^{R},\cup)$, this yields $([w]_{\mathcal{C}},\rho(w),\tau(w))\in\mathcal{R}^{\rho}_{S}$, concluding the proof.
\end{proof}\par\par\@@numbered@section{section}{toc}{Conclusion}
We proved that separation and covering are decidable for all classes of the form ${BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})$ when $\mathcal{C}$ is a finite quotient-closed Boolean algebra. This yields separation and covering algorithms for a whole family of classes. Arguably, the most important one is the level two in the Straubing-Th\'{e}rien hierarchy (which corresponds to the logic $\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2}(<)$). Additionally, this result can be lifted to depth-two using an effective reduction of\penalty\ \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{pzsucc2}{}{}]} to the level two in the Straubing-Th\'{e}rien hierarchy.
\par An interesting consequence of our results is that since we proved the decidability of separation for the level two in the Straubing-Th\'{e}rien hierarchy, the main theorem of\penalty\ \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{pzqalt}{}{}]} is an immediate corollary: membership for this level is decidable. However, the algorithm of\penalty\ \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{pzqalt}{}{}]} was actually based on a characterization theorem: languages of level two in the Straubing-Th\'{e}rien hierarchy are characterized by a syntactic property of a canonical recognizer (\emph{i.e.}, their syntactic monoid). It turns out that one can also deduce this characterization theorem from our results (this does require some combinatorial work however). In fact, one may generalize it to all classes ${BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})$ when $\mathcal{C}$ is a finite quotient-closed Boolean algebra.
\par Finally, the main and most natural follow-up question is much harder: can our results be pushed to higher levels within concatenation hierarchies? For now, we know that given any finite quotient-closed Boolean algebra $\mathcal{C}$, ${Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})$, ${BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})$ and ${Pol\/}({BPol\/}(\mathcal{C}))$ have decidable covering (the former and the latter are results of\penalty\ \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{pseps3j}{}{}]}). Consequently, the next relevant levels are ${BPol\/}({BPol\/}(\mathcal{C}))$ and ${Pol\/}({BPol\/}({BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})))$.
