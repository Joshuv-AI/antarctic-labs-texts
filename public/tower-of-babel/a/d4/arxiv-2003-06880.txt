Enumerating Grammar-Based Extractions Journal: Discrete Applied Mathematics
Liat Peterfreund

Abstract
We propose a new grammar-based language for defining information extractors from documents (text) that is built upon the well-studied framework of document spanners for extracting structured data from text. While previously studied formalisms for document spanners are mainly based on regular expressions, we use an extension of context-free grammars, called extraction grammars, to define the new class of context-free spanners. Extraction grammars are simply context-free grammars extended with variables that capture interval positions of the document, namely spans. While regular expressions are efficient for tokenizing and tagging, context-free grammars are also efficient for capturing structural properties. Indeed, we show that context-free spanners are strictly more expressive than their regular counterparts. We reason about the expressive power of our new class and present a pushdown-automata model that captures it. We show that extraction grammars can be evaluated with polynomial data complexity. Nevertheless, as the degree of the polynomial depends on the query, we present an enumeration algorithm for unambiguous extraction grammars that, after quintic preprocessing, outputs the results sequentially, without repetitions, with a constant delay between every two consecutive ones.

The class of

To date, most research on document spanners has been focused on the regular representation that is based on regular expressions and finite state automata.
Regular expressions are useful for parsing tasks that involve simple patterns of strings. These tasks include segmentation and tokenization of simple data formats such as CSV files or log files. Nevertheless, regular languages fall short in intricate tasks such as syntax highlighting
[

Contribution

Indeed, we show that context-free spanners are strictly more expressive than regular
spanners, and that the restricted class of regular extraction grammars captures the regular
spanners. We compare the expressiveness of context-free spanners against core and generalized core
spanners and show that context-free spanners are incomparable to any of these classes.
In addition to extraction grammars, we present a pushdown automata
model that captures the context-free spanners.

In terms of evaluation of context-free spanners, we can evaluate extraction grammars in polynomial time in
data complexity
, where the spanner is regarded as fixed and the document as input. However, as the
degree of this polynomial depends on the query (in particular, on the number of variables in the relation it extracts),
we propose an enumeration algorithm for unambiguous extraction grammars. Our algorithm outputs the results consecutively, after quintic preprocessing, with constant delay between every two answers.
In the first step of the preprocessing stage, we manipulate the extraction grammar so that it will be adjusted to the input document. Then, in the second step of the preprocessing, we change it in a way that its non-terminals include extra information on the variable operations. This extra information enables us to skip sequences of productions that do not affect the output, hence obtaining a delay that is independent of the input document, and linear in the number of variables associated with the spanner.

Strings and Spans

We set an infinite set

3.

{\Sigma}

{P}
is a finite set of

For complexity analysis, we define the

We say that a derivation

Semantics of Extraction Grammars

Following Freydenberger

A ref-word

\dashv_{x}

Recall that a context-free grammar is said to be in

We extend this notion to extraction grammars. We say that an extraction grammar is in CNF if it is in CNF when viewed as a grammar over the extended alphabet
\Sigma\cup\Gamma_{X}

Proposition 2.2
.

Every extraction grammar
G
can be converted into an equivalent functional extraction grammar

in
O(|G|^{2}+3^{2k}|G|^{2})

is associated with. In addition,

is in CNF.

Proof.

Let

be an extraction grammar.
We start by converting
G
to CNF with the standard algorithm presented, e.g., in
[
51
, Section 7.1.5]
:
The algorithm consists of three steps:

1.

We omit
\epsilon
-productions, unit productions and useless symbols.

2.

We arrange that all right-hand sides of length 2 or more consists only of variables by adding productions of the form
A\rightarrow\sigma
;

3.

We break right-hand sides of length 3 or more into a cascade of productions, each with a right-hand side consisting of two variables. This is done by replacing rules of the form
A\rightarrow A_{1}\cdots A_{n}
into the cascade
A\rightarrow A_{1}B_{1},\,B_{1}\rightarrow A_{2}B_{2},\,\cdots\,B_{n-2}\rightarrow A_{n-1}A_{n}
.

By a slight abuse of notation, we refer to the resulting grammar by the same notation.

We next define an extraction grammar

whose non-terminals indicate which variable operations they generate.
Formally,
G^{\prime}
is associated with
X
, its non-terminals are
X\times 2^{\Gamma_{X}}
, its terminals are
{\Sigma}
, its start symbol is
(S,\Gamma_{X})
, and its production rules are the following:

1.

(A,X_{1})\rightarrow(B,X_{2})(C,X_{3})
whenever

(a)

A\rightarrow BC\in{P}
,

(b)

, and

(c)

for every

\dashv_{x}\in X_{2}
then
\vdash_{x}\not\in X_{3}
.

2.

(A,\emptyset)\rightarrow\sigma
whenever
A\rightarrow\sigma\in{P}
with
\sigma\in\Sigma
; and

3.

(A,\{\tau\})\rightarrow\tau
whenever
A\rightarrow\tau\in{P}
with
\tau\in\Gamma_{X}
.

We note that
G^{\prime}
is in CNF, and show now that

G^{\prime}
is both functional and equivalent to
G
.

G^{\prime}
is functional

A straightforward induction shows that whenever

(S,\Gamma_{X})\Rightarrow^{*}\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n}
with
\alpha_{i}

or a terminal

\dashv_{x}\in X_{i}

\vdash_{x}\not\in X_{j}
or
\beta_{j}\neq\vdash_{x}
. This allows us to conclude that in a terminal derivation

of

,
it holds that
\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n}
is a valid ref-word.

G^{\prime}
is equivalent to
G

Again, by induction on the length of production, we show that if

\alpha_{i}
either a pair
(A_{i},X_{i})
or

then

with
\alpha^{\prime}_{i}=A_{i}
whenever
\alpha_{i}:=(A_{i},X_{i})
, and
\alpha^{\prime}_{i}=\beta_{i}
whenever
\alpha_{i}:=\beta_{i}
.
Thus, if

then so is

.
For the other direction, a straightforward induction shows that if
A\Rightarrow^{*}_{G}\alpha^{\prime}_{1}\cdots\alpha^{\prime}_{n}
then
(A,Y)\Rightarrow^{*}_{G^{\prime}}\beta_{1}\cdots\beta_{n}

where
\beta_{i}=\alpha^{\prime}_{i}
whenever
\alpha^{\prime}_{i}\in{\Sigma}
, and
\beta_{i}=(\alpha^{\prime}_{i},Y_{i})

\alpha^{\prime}_{i}\in V

Thus, if

Complexity

For the complexity analysis we note that converting

into CNF requires

and that the resulting grammar is of size

. Since the

∎

2.6
Unambiguous Extraction Grammars

A grammar
G
is said to be unambiguous if every word it produces has a unique parse-tree.
We extend this definition to extraction grammars as follows.
An extraction grammar
G
is said to be
unambiguous
if for every document
\mathbf{d}
and every
(X,\mathbf{d})
-mapping
\mu\in\llbracket{G}\rrbracket(\mathbf{d})
it holds that

1.

there is a unique ref-word
\mathbf{r}
for which
\mu^{\mathbf{r}}=\mu
, and

2.

\mathbf{r}
has a unique parse-tree.

Unambiguous extraction grammars are less expressive than their ambiguous counterparts. Indeed, context-free grammars (which are extraction grammars associated with the empty set of variables) are less expressive than ambiguous context-free grammars
[
48
]
.

Example 2.8
.

The extraction grammar given in Example
2.7
is not unambiguous since
it produces the ref-words
\vdash_{x}\dashv_{x}\vdash_{y}\dashv_{y}
and
\vdash_{y}\dashv_{y}\vdash_{x}\dashv_{x}
that correspond to the same mapping.
It can be shown that replacing the derivation
B\rightarrow\epsilon
with
B\rightarrow\mathtt{a}\,\,\vline\,\,\mathtt{b}
results in an unambiguous extraction grammar which is equivalent to
disjEqLen
on any document different than
\epsilon
.
(Note however that this does not imply that the ref-languages both grammars produce are equal.)
∎

Our enumeration algorithm for extraction grammars presented in Section
6

relies on unambiguity and the following observation.

Proposition 2.3
.

In Proposition
2.2
, if
G
is unambiguous then so is
G^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Assume that
G
is unambiguous.

We first show that converting
G
to CNF results in an
unambiguous extraction grammar.
For that, let us recall the details of the algorithm presented in
[
51
, Section 7.1.5]
for converting a grammar to CNF.
The algorithm consists of three steps:

1.

We omit
\epsilon
-productions, unit productions and useless symbols.

2.

We arrange that all bodies of length 2 or more consists only of variables;

3.

We break bodies of length 3 or more into a cascade of productions, each with a body consisting of two variables.

In step
1
, we omit productions and thus if
G
is unambiguous then so is the resulting grammar.
In step
2
, we add productions of the form
A\rightarrow\sigma
but these do not affect unambiguity as these
A
s are fresh non-terminals and for each
\sigma
we have a unique such
A
.
In step
3
, we replace rules of the form
A\rightarrow A_{1}\cdots A_{n}
into the cascade
A\rightarrow A_{1}B_{1},B_{1}\rightarrow A_{2}B_{2},\cdots B_{n-2}\rightarrow A_{n-1}A_{n}
where all
B_{i}
s are fresh non-terminals. Thus, this step as well does not introduce ambiguity.

It is straightforward that the conversion to
G^{\prime}
preserves unambiguity, which completes the proof.
∎

3
Expressive Power and Closure Properties

In this section we compare the expressiveness of context-free spanners compared to other studied classes of spanners and discuss its evaluation.

3.1
Regular Spanners

The most studied language for specifying spanners is that of the regular spanners which are those definable by a nondeterministic
finite-state automaton that can open and close variables while running.
Formally, a
variable-set automaton

A
(or
vset-automaton
, for short)
is
a tuple

A:=(X,Q,q_{0},q_{f},\delta)
where
X\subseteq\mathsf{Vars}
is a finite set of variables also referred to as
\mathsf{Vars}(A)
,

Q
is the set of
states
,
q_{0},q_{f}\in Q
are the
initial
and
the
final
states, respectively, and

\delta\colon Q\times(\Sigma\cup\{\epsilon\}\cup\Gamma_{X})\to 2^{Q}
is the

transition function
.
The semantics of
A
is defined by interpreting
A
as a non-deterministic finite state automaton
over the extended alphabet

\Sigma\cup\Gamma_{X}
,
and defining
\mathcal{R}(A)
as the set of all
ref-words
\mathbf{r}\in(\Sigma\cup\Gamma_{X})^{*}
such that some path
from
q_{0}
to
q_{f}
is labeled with
\mathbf{r}
. Formally,

\mathcal{R}{(A)}:=\{\mathbf{r}\in({\Sigma}\cup\Gamma_{X})^{*}\,\,\vline\,\,q_{f}\in\delta(q_{0},\mathbf{r})\}\,.

Similarly for regex formulas, we define
\mathsf{Ref}(A,\mathbf{d})=\mathcal{R}(A)\cap\mathsf{Ref}(\mathbf{d})
. Finally, the result of applying the spanner
\llbracket{A}\rrbracket
on a document
\mathbf{d}\in\Sigma^{*}
is defined as

\llbracket{A}\rrbracket(\mathbf{d}):=\{\mu^{\mathbf{r}}\,\,\vline\,\,\mathbf{r}\in\mathsf{Ref}(A,\mathbf{d})\}.

The class of
regular spanners
equals the class of spanners that are expressible as a vset-automaton
[
10
]
.

Inspired by Chomsky’s hierarchy, we say that an extraction grammar
G
is
regular
if its productions are of the form
A\rightarrow\sigma B
and
A\rightarrow\sigma
where
A,B
are non-terminals and
\sigma\in(\Sigma\cup\Gamma_{X})
.
We then have the following equivalence that is strongly based on the equivalence of regular grammars and finite state automata.

Proposition 3.1
.

The class of spanners definable by regular extraction grammars is equal to the class of regular spanners.

Proof.

Let us consider a regular spanner definable by a vset-automaton
A
. Since every regular language is also context-free, we can construct a pushdown automaton
A^{\prime}
such that
\mathcal{R}(A^{\prime})=\mathcal{R}(A)
, which allows us to conclude that
\llbracket{A}\rrbracket=\llbracket{A^{\prime}}\rrbracket

For the other direction we have to show that every spanner definable by a regular extraction grammar is regular.
Let
G:=(X,V,{\Sigma},{P},{S})
be a regular extraction grammar.
We adapt the standard conversion of regular grammars into non-deterministic automata (cf
[
48
]
) to our settings by treating variable operations as terminal symbols.
Formally, the automaton is defined as follows. Its set of states consists of
q_{A}
for every non-terminal
A\in V
, and a fresh final state
q_{f}
. We set
q_{{S}}
as the initial state and define the transition function
\delta
as follows:
for every
A\rightarrow\sigma B
we set
\delta(q_{A},\sigma)=q_{B}
, and for
A\rightarrow\sigma
we set
\delta(q_{A},\sigma)=q_{f}
.
It holds that
\mathcal{R}(G)=\mathcal{L}(A)
, which allows us to conclude that
\llbracket{G}\rrbracket(\mathbf{d})=\llbracket{A}\rrbracket(\mathbf{d})
for every document
\mathbf{d}
.
∎

3.2
(Generalized) Core Spanners

In their efforts to capture the core of AQL which is IBM’s SystemT query language, Fagin et al.
[
10
]
defined the class of core spanners.
Core spanners
extend regular spanners with the string-equality selection. To define them properly, we present an
alternative way of defining regular spanners that is based on the notion of regex formulas:
A
regex formula

is defined recursively by

\alpha:=\hskip 3.5pt\emptyset\mid\epsilon\mid\sigma\mid\alpha\vee\alpha\mid\alpha\cdot\alpha\mid\alpha^{*}\mid\,\vdash_{x}\alpha\dashv_{x}

where
\sigma\in\Sigma
and
x\in\mathsf{Vars}
.
We
denote the set of variables whose variable operations occur in
\alpha
by
\mathsf{Vars}(\alpha)
,
and interpret each regex formula
\alpha
as a generator of a
ref-word
language
\mathcal{R}(\alpha)
over the extended alphabet

\Sigma\cup\Gamma_{\mathsf{Vars}(\alpha)}
.
For every
document
\mathbf{d}\in\Sigma^{*}
, we define

\mathsf{Ref}(\alpha,\mathbf{d})=\mathcal{R}(\alpha)\cap\mathsf{Ref}(\mathbf{d})
, and
the spanner
\llbracket{\alpha}\rrbracket

by

\llbracket{\alpha}\rrbracket(\mathbf{d}):=\{\mu^{\mathbf{r}}\,\,\vline\,\,\mathbf{r}\in\mathsf{Ref}(\alpha,\mathbf{d})\}.

The class of regular spanners is then defined as the closure of (spanners defined by) regex formulas under the algebraic operators: union, projection and natural join
[
10
]
.
Let
P
,
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
be spanners; the above operators are defined as follows:

Union

If
\mathsf{Vars}(P_{1})=\mathsf{Vars}(P_{2})
, their
union

(P_{1}\cup P_{2})
is defined by
\mathsf{Vars}(P_{1}\cup P_{2}):=\mathsf{Vars}(P_{1})

and
(P_{1}\cup P_{2})(\mathbf{d}):=P_{1}(\mathbf{d})\cup P_{2}(\mathbf{d})
for all

\mathbf{d}\in\Sigma^{*}
.

Projection

Let
Y\subseteq\mathsf{Vars}(P)
. The

projection

\pi_{Y}P
is defined by
\mathsf{Vars}(\pi_{Y}P):=Y
and

\pi_{Y}P(\mathbf{d}):={P|}_{Y}(\mathbf{d})
for all
\mathbf{d}\in\Sigma^{*}
, where
{P|}_{Y}(\mathbf{d})
is the restriction of all

\mu\in P(\mathbf{d})
to
Y
.

Natural join

Let
V_{i}:=\mathsf{Vars}(P_{i})
for
i\in\{1,2\}
. The
(natural) join

(P_{1}\bowtie P_{2})
of
P_{1}
and

P_{2}
is defined by
\mathsf{Vars}(P_{1}\bowtie P_{2}):=\mathsf{Vars}(P_{1})\cup\mathsf{Vars}(P_{2})
and, for all
\mathbf{d}\in\Sigma^{*}
,
(P_{1}\bowtie P_{2})(\mathbf{d})
is the set of all
(V_{1}\cup V_{2},\mathbf{d})
-records
\mu

for which there exist
\mu_{1}\in P_{1}(\mathbf{d})
and
\mu_{2}\in P_{2}(\mathbf{d})

with
{\mu|}_{V_{1}}(\mathbf{d})=\mu_{1}(\mathbf{d})
and
{\mu|}_{V_{2}}(\mathbf{d})=\mu_{2}(\mathbf{d})
.

The class of core spanners
is the closure of regex formulas under the positive operators, (i.e., union, natural join and projection) along
with the string equality selection that is defined as follows:

String-equality selection

Let
P
be a spanner and let
x,y\in\mathsf{Vars}(S)
, the
string equality selection

\zeta^{=}_{x,y}S
is defined by
\mathsf{Vars}(\zeta^{=}_{x,y}S)=\mathsf{Vars}(S)
and, for all
\mathbf{d}\in\Sigma^{*}
,
\zeta^{=}_{x,y}S(\mathbf{d})
is the set of all
\mu\in S(\mathbf{d})
where
\mathbf{d}_{\mu(x)}=\mathbf{d}_{\mu(y)}
.

Note that unlike the join operator that joins mappings that have identical spans in
their shared variables, the selection operator compares
the substrings of
\mathbf{d}
that are described by the spans, and does
not distinguish between different spans that span the same substrings.

The class of
generalized core spanners
is obtained by adding the difference operator.

Difference

Let
P_{1},P_{2}
be spanners. If
\mathsf{Vars}(P_{1})=\mathsf{Vars}(P_{2})
, their
difference

P_{1}\setminus P_{2}
is defined by
\mathsf{Vars}(P_{1}\setminus P_{2})=\mathsf{Vars}(P_{1})
and
(P_{1}\setminus P_{2})(\mathbf{d})=P_{1}(\mathbf{d})\setminus P_{2}(\mathbf{d})
.

Generalized core spanners are the closure of regex formulas under union, natural join, projection, string equality, and difference.

We say that two classes
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
of spanners are
incomparable
if both
\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{S}
are not empty.

Proposition 3.2
.

The classes of core spanners and generalized core spanners are each incomparable with the class of context-free spanners.

Proof.

We observe that extraction grammars
associated with the empty set of variables
are equivalent to context-free grammars and, hence, capture the context-free languages.

The class of core spanners
is not contained in the class of context-free spanners since the language
\{ww\,|\,w\in\Sigma^{*}\}
is not context-free yet there is a Boolean core spanner
P
that defines it
[
10
]
(that is,
P(w^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
if and only if
w^{\prime}\in\{ww\,|\,w\in\Sigma^{*}\}
).
This also shows that the class of generalized core spanners
is not contained in that of context-free spanners.

The class of context-free spanners is not contained in that of core spanners since
the language
\{\mathtt{a}^{n}\mathtt{b}^{n}|n\geq 1\}
is context free and yet it is not accepted by Boolean core spanners
[
10
]
, and not by generalized core spanners
[
15
]
.
∎

We conclude by an immediate result on closure properties.

Proposition 3.3
.

The class of context-free spanners is closed under union and projection,
but is
not closed under natural join and difference.

Proof.

To show closure under projection,
let
G:=(X,V,{\Sigma},{P},{S})
be an extraction grammar, and let
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
.
We define a morphism
\mathsf{clr}_{X\setminus X^{\prime}}:(\Sigma\cup V\cup\Gamma_{X})\rightarrow(\Sigma\cup V\cup\Gamma_{X^{\prime}})^{*}
by

\mathsf{clr}_{X\setminus X^{\prime}}(\alpha):=\left\{\begin{matrix}\alpha&\alpha\in\Sigma\cup\Gamma_{X^{\prime}}\\
\epsilon&\alpha\in\Gamma_{X\setminus X^{\prime}}\end{matrix}\right.

We then define the grammar
G^{\prime}=(X^{\prime},V,{\Sigma},{P}^{\prime},{S})
with
{P}^{\prime}
defined as follows: for every
A\rightarrow\alpha\in{P}
we have
A\rightarrow\mathsf{clr}_{X\setminus X^{\prime}}(\alpha)\in{P}^{\prime}
.
It is straightforward that
\llbracket{G^{\prime}}\rrbracket=\llbracket{\pi_{X^{\prime}}G_{X}}\rrbracket
.

Closure under union can be obtained straightforwardly.
Let
G_{1},G_{2}
be two extraction grammars with
\mathsf{Vars}(G_{1})=\mathsf{Vars}(G_{2})
.
We can construct a grammar
G
with
\mathcal{R}({G})=\mathcal{R}(G_{1})\cup\mathcal{R}(G_{2})
by defining its production rules as the union of those of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
with the addition of productions
S\rightarrow S_{1},S\rightarrow S_{2}
where
S
is a fresh non-terminal who is the start symbol of
G
, and
S_{1},S_{2}
are the start symbols of
G_{1},G_{2}
, respectively.

Non-closure properties are an immediate consequence of non-closure properties of context-free languages.
∎

4
Evaluation and Enumeration

The
evaluation
problem of extraction grammars is that of computing
\llbracket{G}\rrbracket(\mathbf{d})
where
\mathbf{d}
is a document and
G
is an extraction grammar.
Our first observation is the following.

Proposition 4.1
.

For every extraction grammar
G
and every document
\mathbf{d}
it holds that
\llbracket{G}\rrbracket(\mathbf{d})
can be computed in
O(|G|^{2}+|\mathbf{d}|^{2k+3}\,k^{3}\,k!\,|G|)
time where
k
is the number of variables
G
is associated with.

Proof.

Our evaluation algorithm relies on the celebrated
Cocke-Younger-Kasami (CYK) parsing algorithm for context-free grammars
[
48
]
in CNF, and
operates as follows: it iterates over all of the ref-words
\mathbf{r}
that are
(1)
valid for
\mathsf{Vars}(G)
and
(2)
mapped by
\mathsf{clr}
into
\mathbf{d}
. For each such ref-word, it uses the CYK algorithm to determine whether it is produced by
G
by treating
G
as a standard CFG over the extended alphabet
\Sigma\cup\Gamma_{X}
, after converting it to CNF.

Complexity

We convert
G
to CNF in
O(|G|^{2})
. There are
O(|d|^{2k}k!)
valid ref-words, and each is represented by a ref-word of length
O(|\mathbf{d}|+2k)
.
For each such ref-word, we use the CYK to check whether it belongs to the language of
G
in
O((|\mathbf{d}|+2k)^{3}|G|)
.
Since we repeat the process for every ref-word, we get a total complexity of
O(|G|^{2}+|\mathbf{d}|^{2k}k!(|\mathbf{d}|+2k)^{3}|G|)
.
∎

By replacing the CYK algorithm with Valiant’s parser
[
53
]
, we can decrease the complexity to
O(|G|^{2}+|\mathbf{d}|^{2k+\omega}\,k^{\omega}\,k!\,|G|)
with
\omega<2.373
the matrix multiplication exponent
[
54
]
.

While the evaluation of context-free spanners can be done in polynomial time in data complexity (where
G
is regarded as fixed and
\mathbf{d}
as input), the output size might be quite big. To be more precise, for an extraction grammar
G
associated with
k
variables, the output may consist of up to
O({(2k)}!|\mathbf{d}|^{2k})
mappings.
Instead of outputting these mappings altogether, we can output them sequentially (without repetitions) after some preprocessing.
This approach leads us to the main result of this paper:

Theorem 4.1
.

For every unambiguous extraction grammar
G
and every document
\mathbf{d}
there is an algorithm that outputs the mappings in
\llbracket{G}\rrbracket(\mathbf{d})
with delay
O(k)

after
O(|\mathbf{d}|^{5}|G|^{2}3^{4k})
preprocessing where
k
is the number of variables
G
is associated with.

In the rest of this paper, we describe the algorithm, proof its correctness, and analyze its complexity.

Our algorithm consists of two main stages: preprocessing and outputting.
In the preprocessing stage, we manipulate the extraction grammar and do some precomputations which are later exploited in the outputting stage in which we output the results sequentially.
We remark that unambiguity is crucial for outputting the mappings without repetition.

Through the lens of data complexity, our enumeration algorithm outputs the results with constant delay after quintic preprocessing. That should be contrasted with regular spanners for which there exists a constant delay enumeration algorithm whose preprocessing is linear
[
18
,
19
]
.

In the following sections, we present the enumeration algorithm and discuss its correctness but, before,
we deal with the special case
\mathbf{d}:=\epsilon
. In this case,
\llbracket{G}\rrbracket(\mathbf{d})
is either empty or contains exactly one mapping (since, by definition, the document
\epsilon
has exactly one span, namely
[1,1\rangle
).
Notice that
\llbracket{G}\rrbracket(\mathbf{d})
is empty if and only if
G
does not produce a ref-word that consists only of variable operations. To check if that is the case, it suffices to change the production rules of
G
by replacing every occurrence of
\tau\in\Gamma_{X}
with
\epsilon
, and to check whether the new grammar produces
\epsilon
. This can be done in linear time
[
51
]
, which completes the proof of this case.
So from now on it is assumed that
\mathbf{d}\neq\epsilon
.

5
Preprocessing Stage of the Enumeration Algorithm

The preprocessing stage consists of two steps: in the first we adjust the extraction grammar to a given document and add subscripts to non-terminals to track this connection, and in the second we use superscripts to capture extra information regarding the variable operations.
In addition, we compute a function, namely
jump
, that allows us to obtain the complexity guarantees on the delay between two consecutive outputs in the output stage.

5.1
Adjusting the Extraction Grammar to
\mathbf{d}

Let
{G}:=(X,V,{\Sigma},{P},{S})
be an extraction grammar in CNF, and
let
\mathbf{d}:=\sigma_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{n},n\geq 1
be a document.
The goal of this step is to restrict
G
so that it will produce only the ref-words which
\mathsf{clr}
maps to
\mathbf{d}
.
To this end, we define the grammar
G_{\mathbf{d}}
that is associated with the same set
X
of variables as
G
, and is defined as follows:

1.

The non-terminals are
\{A_{i,j}\,\,\vline\,\,A\in V,\,1\leq i\leq j\leq n\}\cup\{A_{\epsilon}\,\,\vline\,\,A\in V\}
,

2.

the terminals are
\Sigma
,

3.

the initial symbol is
S_{1,n}
, and

4.

the production rules are defined as follows:

(a)

A_{i,i}\rightarrow\sigma_{i}
for any
A\rightarrow\sigma_{i}\in{P}
,

(b)

A_{\epsilon}\rightarrow\gamma
for any
A\rightarrow\gamma\in{P}
with
\gamma\in\Gamma_{X}
,

(c)

A_{\epsilon}\rightarrow B_{\epsilon}C_{\epsilon}

for any

A\rightarrow BC\in{P}
,

(d)

A_{i,j}\rightarrow B_{i,j}C_{\epsilon}

for any
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
and any

A\rightarrow BC\in{P}
,

(e)

A_{i,j}\rightarrow B_{\epsilon}C_{i,j}

for any
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
and any

A\rightarrow BC\in{P}
,

(f)

A_{i,j}\rightarrow B_{i,i^{\prime}}C_{i^{\prime}+1,j}

for any
1\leq i\leq i^{\prime}<j\leq n
and

A\rightarrow BC\in{P}
.

We eliminate useless non-terminals from
G_{\mathbf{d}}
, and by a slight abuse of notation refer to the resulting grammar as
G_{\mathbf{d}}
. The intuition behind this construction is that if a subscript of a non-terminal is
i,j
then this non-terminal produces a ref-word that
\mathsf{clr}
maps to
\sigma_{i}\cdots\sigma_{j}
, and if it is
\epsilon
then it produces a ref-word that consists only of variable operations.

We establish the following connection between

Lemma 5.1
.

For every extraction grammar
G
in CNF, every document
\mathbf{d}:=\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{n}
, every non-terminal
A
of
G
, and every ref-word
\mathbf{r}\in(\Sigma\cup\Gamma_{X})^{*}
with
\mathsf{clr}(\mathbf{r})=\sigma_{i}\cdots\sigma_{j}
the following holds:

A\Rightarrow_{G}^{*}\mathbf{r}

if and only if
A_{i,j}\Rightarrow_{G_{\mathbf{d}}}^{*}\mathbf{r}

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
j-i
. In the base case we have
j=i
, and
\mathbf{r}=\sigma_{i}
. By definition,
A\Rightarrow^{*}_{G}\sigma_{i}
if and only if
A_{i,i}\Rightarrow^{*}_{G_{\mathbf{d}}}\sigma_{i}
.
For the induction step:
If
A\Rightarrow^{*}_{G}\mathbf{r}
with
\mathbf{r}=\sigma_{i}\cdots\sigma_{j}

then since
G
is in CNF we have
A\Rightarrow^{*}_{G}BC\Rightarrow^{*}_{G}\mathbf{r}_{1}\mathbf{r}_{2}
where
\mathsf{clr}(\mathbf{r}_{1})=\sigma_{i}\cdots\sigma_{i+\ell}
and

\mathsf{clr}(\mathbf{r}_{2})=\sigma_{i+\ell+1}\cdots\sigma_{j}
for some
0\leq\ell\leq j-i
.
By induction hypothesis,
B_{i,i+\ell}\Rightarrow^{*}_{G_{\mathbf{d}}}\mathbf{r}_{1}
and
C_{i+\ell+1,j}\Rightarrow^{*}_{G_{\mathbf{d}}}\mathbf{r}_{2}
. By definition of
G_{\mathbf{d}}
it holds that
A_{i,j}\Rightarrow^{*}_{G_{\mathbf{d}}}B_{i,i+\ell}C_{i+\ell+1,j}
, and thus we conclude

A_{i,j}\Rightarrow^{*}_{G_{\mathbf{d}}}\mathbf{r}
.

For the other direction, let us assume that
A_{i,j}\Rightarrow^{*}_{G_{\mathbf{d}}}\mathbf{r}
. By
G_{\mathbf{d}}
’s definition and since it is in CNF, it holds that
A_{i,j}\Rightarrow^{*}_{G_{\mathbf{d}}}B_{i,i+\ell}C_{i+\ell+1,j}
where
A\Rightarrow^{*}_{G}BC
. Thus, we obtain
A_{i,j}\Rightarrow^{*}_{G_{\mathbf{d}}}B_{i,i+\ell}C_{i+\ell+1,j}\Rightarrow^{*}_{G_{\mathbf{d}}}\mathbf{r}_{1}\mathbf{r}_{2}=\mathbf{r}
. By induction hypothesis,
B\Rightarrow^{*}_{G}\mathbf{r}_{1}
and
C\Rightarrow^{*}_{G}\mathbf{r}_{2}
. Combining the above we conclude
A\Rightarrow^{*}_{G}BC\Rightarrow^{*}_{G}\mathbf{r}_{1}\mathbf{r}_{2}=\mathbf{r}
.
∎

We can conclude the following.

Corollary 5.1
.

For every extraction grammar
G
in CNF and for every document
\mathbf{d}
, it holds that
\mathsf{Ref}(G,\mathbf{d})=\mathcal{L}(G_{\mathbf{d}})
.

We note that adjusting our extraction grammar to
\mathbf{d}
is somewhat similar to the CYK algorithm
[

]
and therefore it is valid on extraction grammars

in CNF.
For a similar reason, we obtain the following complexity which is cubic in

Proposition 5.1
.

For every extraction grammar
G
in CNF and for every document
\mathbf{d}
, it holds that
G_{\mathbf{d}}
can be constructed in
O({|\mathbf{d}|}^{3}|G|)

Proof.

For each production rule in

, there are at most
|\mathbf{d}|^{3}

. Thus, the total time required to produce

is

5.2
Constructing the Decorated Grammar

The goal of this step of preprocessing is to encode the information on the produced variable operations within the non-terminals.
We obtain from
G_{\mathbf{d}}
, constructed in the previous step, a new grammar, namely
\text{\sc{decorGrmr}}(G_{\mathbf{d}})
, that produces
decorated words
which are sequences of elements of the form

A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
where
A_{i,j}
is a non-terminal in
G_{\mathbf{d}}
and
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}
are sets of variable operations.
Intuitively,
A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
indicates that all variable operations in
\mathbf{x}
occur right before
\sigma_{i}
, and all those in
\mathbf{y}
right after
\sigma_{j}
.

To define
\text{\sc{decorGrmr}}(G_{\mathbf{d}})
, we need
G
to be functional. The following key observation is used in the formal definition of
\text{\sc{decorGrmr}}(G_{\mathbf{d}})
and is based on the functionality of
G
.

Proposition 5.2
.

For every functional extraction grammar
G
and every non-terminal
A
of
G
, there is a set
\mathbf{x}_{A}\subseteq\Gamma_{X}
of variable operations such that for every ref-word
\mathbf{r}
where
A\Rightarrow^{*}\mathbf{r}
the variable operations that appear in
\mathbf{r}
are exactly those in
\mathbf{x}_{A}
. Computing all sets
\mathbf{x}_{A}
can be done in
O(|G|)
.

Proof.

Assume by contradiction that there are
\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
that contain different variable operations such that
A\Rightarrow^{*}\mathbf{r}
and
A\Rightarrow^{*}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
.
Since
G
does not contain any unreachable non-terminal, and since every non-terminal of
G
is reachable from the initial symbol there exists a leftmost derivation (that is obtained by applying production to the leftmost non-terminal in each step. ) of the form:

S\Rightarrow^{*}\mathbf{r}_{1}A\gamma\Rightarrow^{*}\mathbf{r}_{1}\mathbf{r}\mathbf{r}_{2}

where
\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2}\in(\Sigma\cup\Gamma_{X})^{*}
and
\gamma\in(V\cup\Sigma\cup\Gamma_{X})^{*}
.
Thus, there is also the following derivation

S\Rightarrow^{*}\mathbf{r}_{1}A\gamma\Rightarrow^{*}\mathbf{r}_{1}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\mathbf{r}_{2}.

Recall that due to functionality both
\mathbf{r}_{1}\mathbf{r}\mathbf{r}_{2}
and
\mathbf{r}_{1}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\mathbf{r}_{2}
are valid, and thus the variable operations that occur in
\mathbf{r}
and
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
must be the same, which leads to the desired contradiction.

To compute the sets
\mathbf{x}_{A}
, we view the grammar as a directed graph whose nodes are the non-terminals and edges are the pairs
(A,B),(A,C)
whenever there is a production
A\rightarrow BC
, and
(A,\sigma)
whenever there is a production
A\rightarrow\sigma
with
\sigma\in\Gamma_{X}
.
We run a topological order on the graph, and iterate over the output of this order in an inverse order.
We then run a BFS on this graph and view the output sequence of non-terminals as a topological order. We then iterate over the non-terminals in an inverse order and accumulate for each the set of variable operations that consists of its descendants.
This whole process can be done in
O(|G|)
.
∎

In other words, for functional extraction grammars, the information on the variable operations is stored implicitly in the non-terminals. The set
\mathbf{x}_{A}
will serve us in the construction of
\text{\sc{decorGrmr}}(G_{\mathbf{d}})
.

5.2.1
Steps of the construction

The grammar
\text{\sc{decorGrmr}}(G_{\mathbf{d}})
is obtained from
G_{\mathbf{d}}
in two steps:

Step 1

For all pairwise disjoint subsets
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w}\subseteq\Gamma_{X}
, we add the following derivations to

1.

for every rule

in

,

2.

for every rule

in

with

3.

for every rule

in

4.

for every rule

in

with

and

defined as in Proposition

5.

with

,

5.3
Characterizing the Decorated Grammar

The decorated grammar
\text{\sc{decorGrmr}}(G_{\mathbf{d}})
encodes information on the original grammar
G
, and is used in the output stage of the algorithm.
We point at its characteristics which allow us to establish its connection to
G
.

Notice that the terminals of
\text{\sc{decorGrmr}}(G_{\mathbf{d}})
are stable non-terminals of
G^{\prime}_{\mathbf{d}}
. We now define the mapping that is defined by the words produced by
\text{\sc{decorGrmr}}(G_{\mathbf{d}})
.
We define the
(X,d)
-mapping
\mu^{w}
that corresponds with a decorated word

w:={(A^{1})}^{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1}}_{i_{1},j_{1}}\cdots{(A^{m})}^{\mathbf{x}_{m},\mathbf{y}_{m}}_{i_{m},j_{m}}

\mu^{w}(x):=[i,j\rangle
whenever the two following statements hold:

1.

there is
1\leq\ell\leq m
such that either (a)
\vdash_{x}\in\mathbf{x}_{\ell}
and
i=i_{\ell}
or (b)

\vdash_{x}\in\mathbf{y}_{\ell}
and
i=j_{\ell}+1
;

2.

there is
1\leq\ell\leq m
such that either (a)
\dashv_{x}\in\mathbf{x}_{\ell}
and
j=i_{\ell}
or (b)
\dashv_{x}\in\mathbf{y}_{\ell}
and
j=j_{\ell}+1
.

We say that a decorated word
w
is
valid
if
\mu^{w}(x)
is well-defined for every

.

G_{\mathbf{d}}
and
\text{\sc{decorGrmr}}(G_{\mathbf{d}})

Lemma 5.3
.

For every functional unambiguous extraction grammar
G
in CNF and for every document
\mathbf{d}

\text{\sc{decorGrmr}}(G_{\mathbf{d}})
is valid and that

\llbracket{G}\rrbracket(\mathbf{d})=\{\mu^{w}\,\,\vline\,\,S\Rightarrow^{*}_{\text{\sc{decorGrmr}}(G_{\mathbf{d}})}w\}.

Proof.

By Corollary

Before moving to the output stage of the algorithm, we discuss the ideas that allow us to obtain constant delay between every two consecutive outputs.

5.4
The Jump Function

When constructing the decorated grammar, we explained why we can stop the derivation when reaching stable non-terminals; It turns out, that our algorithm can also skip parts of the derivation.

Notice that if
G
is associated with
k
variables, there are exactly
2k
variable operations in each ref-word produced by
G
. While this allows us to obtain an upper bound for the number of non-stable non-terminals in a parse-tree, their depth can be linear in
|\mathbf{d}|
. We demonstrate this in the following example.

Example 5.4
.

Consider the non-stable non-terminal
F_{n-1,n}^{\emptyset,\emptyset}
in the partial parse-tree in Figure
7
. Observe that the depth of this non-terminal is linear in
n:=|\mathbf{d}|
.
∎

To obtain a delay independent of
\mathbf{d}
, we may
skip parts of the parse-tree in which no variable operation occurs.
This idea somewhat resembles the one used by Amarilli et al.
[
18
]
in their constant delay enumeration algorithm for regular spanners represented as vset-automata. There, they defined a function that ‘jumps’ from one state to the other if the path from the former to the latter does not contain any variable operation.
We extend this idea to extraction grammars by defining the notion of skippable productions.

Intuitively, for each non-terminal in a parse-tree the output mapping is affected either by its left subtree, or by its right subtree, or by the production applied on this non-terminal itself (or by any combination of the above).
If the mapping is affected exclusively by the left (right, respectively) subtree then we can skip the production applied on this non-terminal and move to check the left (right, respectively) subtree; We can continue recursively until we reach a production for which this is no longer the case (that is, the mapping is affected by more than one of the above).

Formally, a
skippable
production rule is of the form

A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\rightarrow B^{\mathbf{x},\emptyset}_{i,i^{\prime}}C^{\emptyset,\mathbf{y}}_{i^{\prime}+1,j}

where

(a)

A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
is non-stable,
and

(b)

exactly one of
B^{\mathbf{x},\emptyset}_{i,i^{\prime}},C^{\emptyset,\mathbf{y}}_{i^{\prime}+1,j}
is stable.

Intuitively,
(a)
assures that the parse-tree rooted in
A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
has an effect on the mapping; The empty sets in the superscripts of
B
and
C
assure that the production applied on
A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
does not have an effect on the mapping and
(b)
assures that exactly one subtree of
A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
(either the one rooted at
B^{\mathbf{x},\emptyset}_{i,i^{\prime}}
if
C^{\emptyset,\mathbf{y}}_{i^{\prime}+1,j}
is stable, or the one rooted at
C^{\emptyset,\mathbf{y}}_{i^{\prime}+1,j}
if
B^{\mathbf{x},\emptyset}_{i,i^{\prime}}
is stable) has an effect on the mapping.
We then say that a skippable production rule
\rho

follows
a skippable production rule
\rho^{\prime}
if the non-stable non-terminal in the right-hand side of
\rho^{\prime}
(i.e., after the
\rightarrow
) is the non-terminal in the left-hand side of
\rho
(i.e., before the
\rightarrow
).
We denote the set of non-terminals of
\text{\sc{decorGrmr}}(G_{\mathbf{d}})
by
V^{\text{\sc{dec}}}
, and define
the function
\text{\sc{jump}}\colon V^{\text{\sc{dec}}}\rightarrow 2^{V^{\text{\sc{dec}}}}
as follows:

B\in\text{\sc{jump}}(A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}})
if there is a sequence of skippable production rules

\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{m}
such that:

1.

\rho_{\iota}
follows
\rho_{\iota-1}
for every
\iota
,

2.

the left-hand side of
\rho_{1}
is
A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
,

3.

the non-stable non-terminal in the right-hand side of
\rho_{m}
is
B
,

4.

there is a production rule that is non-skippable whose left-hand side is
B
.

The proof of Claim 1 is more involved.
Notice that

Let us focus on the last

Complexity

We can check in
O(1)
whether a non-terminal is stable. To do so, we can run the algorithm described in the proof of Lemma
5.2
and store the data in a lookup table with non-terminals as keys and value which is either ‘stable’ or ‘non-stable’.
The runtime of this procedure is
O(|G_{\mathbf{d}}|5^{2k})
.
In addition, since we can check in
O(1)
whether a non-terminal is stable or not, we can check in
O(1)
whether a rule in
\text{\sc{decorGrmr}}(G_{\mathbf{d}})
is skippable just by verifying that the conditions in the definition holds (i.e., its left-hand side is non-stable and exactly one of the non-terminals in its right-hand side is stable). To do so, we can also use the above look-up table, and for each rule check whether all of the conditions of the definition hold in
O(1)
.
We can store this information in a lookup table with the rules as keys and the value is either ‘skippable’ or ‘non-skippable’.
The runtime of this procedure is also
O(|G_{\mathbf{d}}|5^{2k})
.

The first four
for-each
loops are used for initialization and require
O(|G_{\mathbf{d}}|5^{2k})=O(|\mathbf{d}|^{3}|G|5^{2k})
altogether as they iterate through all production rules of
\text{\sc{decorGrmr}}(G_{\mathbf{d}})
.
The topological sort of the production rules requires
O(|\mathbf{d}|^{3}|G|5^{2k})
.

In the last
for-each
loop, we iterate through all skippable rules of the decorated grammar
\text{\sc{decorGrmr}}(G_{\mathbf{d}})
ordered by their head in reverse topological order.
There are at most
O(|\mathbf{d}|^{3}|G|3^{2k})
such rules (since their form is restricted to
A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\rightarrow B_{i,\ell}^{\mathbf{x},\emptyset}C_{\ell+1,j}^{\emptyset,\mathbf{y}}
).
In each such iteration, we
compute set unions and set difference.
The size of each of these sets is bounded by
O(|{\mathbf{d}}|^{2}3^{2k}|G|)
.
Thus, the total time complexity of the second for loop is
O(|{\mathbf{d}}|^{5}3^{4k}|G|^{2})
.
And finally, the total complexity of the algorithm is

O(|{\mathbf{d}}|^{5}3^{4k}|G|^{2})
.
∎

According to Lemmas
5.2
and
5.4
the overall computation time required to find the
non-stable non-terminals as well as to compute the
jump
function is quintic in the document size and can therefore be included in the preprocessing stage.

It is important to note that if we can reduce the complexity of computing the
jump
function to cubic then we can reduce the whole preprocessing time to cubic.

6.2
Proof of Theorem
6.1

In order to proof the theorem, we need to take a careful look at different stages of the execution of
enumerate
which correspond with different stages of derivations.
To do that,
we define the mapping
\mu^{\alpha}
that corresponds with a finite sequence
\alpha
of terminals of
\text{\sc{decorGrmr}}(G_{\mathbf{d}})
:
\mu^{\alpha}
is defined by
(x,i)\in\mu^{\alpha}
whenever
\alpha
contains the terminal
A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
with
x\in\mathbf{x}
or the terminal
A_{i^{\prime},i-1}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}

with
x\in\mathbf{y}
.

We first wish to show how the run of
enumerate
reflects the derivation of the decorated grammar.

Lemma 6.1
.

The call
\text{\sc{enumerate}}(A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\alpha,\mathsf{map})
with
\mathsf{map}\supseteq\{(x,i)\,\,\vline\,\,x\in\mathbf{x}\}\cup\{(y,j+1)\,\,\vline\,\,y\in\mathbf{y}\}
provokes a call to

\text{\sc{enumerate}}(\alpha,\mathsf{map}\cup\mu^{w})
whenever there is a terminal derivation
A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\Rightarrow^{*}w
.

Proof.

We first observe that
for every call

\text{\sc{enumerate}}(A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\alpha,\mathsf{map})

if
A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
is stable then

i=1,j=n+1
and
A=S
.
Indeed, assume there is a call
\text{\sc{enumerate}}(A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\alpha,\mathsf{map})

with a stable
A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
. If
A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}=S_{1,n}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
then we are done. If not, this call was provoked by another call to
enumerate
. This implies that there is a pair
(\beta,\mathsf{map}^{\prime})\in B^{\prime}
that is not stable. Nevertheless,
B^{\prime}
is returned by
applyProd
and due to its definition this leads to a contradiction.

We prove the claim by induction on the length of the terminal derivation.
The basis holds trivially. For the induction step,
since
A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
is not stable, we can
distinguish between the following two possible cases:

If
A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
is skippable then

\text{\sc{jump}}(A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}})
returns
B_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}^{\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime}}
such that there is a leftmost derivation
A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\Rightarrow^{*}\alpha^{\prime}B_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}^{\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime}}
where
\mu^{\alpha^{\prime}}=\emptyset
(this can be formally proved by induction on the number of productions applied in the derivation). Thus,
\text{\sc{enumerate}}(B_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}^{\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime}}\alpha,\mathsf{map})
is provoked and the claim follows from the induction hypothesis.

If
A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
is non-skippable then
\text{\sc{jump}}(A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}})
returns
A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
itself, and for each production of the form
A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\rightarrow B_{i,i^{\prime}}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}C_{i^{\prime}+1,j}^{\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y}}
it holds that
\text{\sc{applyProd}}(A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}})
returns the pair
(\beta,\{(x,i^{\prime}+1)\,\,\,\vline\,\,\,i^{\prime}\in\mathbf{z}\cup\mathbf{w}\})
where
\beta
is either
B_{i,i^{\prime}}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}
or
C_{i^{\prime}+1,j}^{\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y}}
or their concatenation. In case
\beta
is
B_{i,i^{\prime}}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}
then the next call is to

\text{\sc{enumerate}}(B_{i,i^{\prime}}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}\alpha,\mathsf{map}\cup\{(x,i^{\prime}+1)\,\,\,\vline\,\,\,i^{\prime}\in\mathbf{z}\cup\mathbf{w}\})
. We can then apply the induction hypothesis and obtain that there is a call to
\text{\sc{enumerate}}(\alpha,\mathsf{map}\cup\{(x,i^{\prime}+1)\,\,\,\vline\,\,\,i^{\prime}\in\mathbf{z}\cup\mathbf{w}\}\cup\mu^{\beta})
where
B_{i,i^{\prime}}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}\Rightarrow^{*}\beta
. In this case, we have
A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\Rightarrow^{*}B_{i,i^{\prime}}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}C_{i^{\prime}+1,j}^{\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y}}\Rightarrow^{*}\beta\gamma
such that
\gamma
is of the form

D_{i^{\prime}+1,j}^{\mathbf{w},\emptyset}
.
Thus,
\mu^{\gamma}=\emptyset
which implies that
\mu^{\beta\gamma}=\mu^{\beta}
which, in turn, completes the proof of this case.
In case
\beta=C_{i^{\prime}+1,j}^{\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y}}
we show the claim similarly. And in case
\beta=B_{i,i^{\prime}}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}C_{i^{\prime}+1,j}^{\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y}}
we use twice the induction hypothesis in a similar way.
∎

This leads to the following direct conclusion which can be seen as proof of soundness of
enumerate
.

Corollary 6.1
.

The call
\text{\sc{enumerate}}(S_{1,n}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}},\mathsf{map})
with
\mathsf{map}\supseteq\{(x,1)\,\,\vline\,\,x\in\mathbf{x}\}\cup\{(y,n+1)\,\,\vline\,\,y\in\mathbf{y}\}
returns
\mu^{w}
whenever there is a terminal derivation
S_{1,n}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\Rightarrow^{*}w
.

To show that
enumerate
is also complete, that is, for each terminal derivation of the decorated grammar there is a run with corresponding output, we prove the following.

Lemma 6.2
.

For every terminal derivation
A\Rightarrow^{*}\alpha
, the call to the procedure
\text{\sc{enumerate}}(A\theta,\mathsf{map})

invokes once
\text{\sc{enumerate}}(\theta,\mathsf{map}\cup\mu^{\alpha})
.

Proof.

We prove this claim by induction on the number of non-skippable production rules that were applied in a leftmost derivation
A\Rightarrow^{*}\alpha
.

For the basis, if all of the production applied were skippable this implies that
A
was stable which implies that
A=S_{1,n}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
and that
\theta=\epsilon
and thus the claim holds (see the second if clause in
enumerate
).

Assume that there is at least one production rule that was applied and is non-skippable.
Thus, there is the following leftmost derivation

A\Rightarrow^{*}\alpha A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\delta\Rightarrow\alpha B_{i,i^{\prime}}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}C_{i^{\prime}+1,j}^{\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y}}\delta\Rightarrow^{*}\alpha\beta\gamma\delta

where the productions applied in
A\Rightarrow^{*}\alpha A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\delta
are all skippable, and the one applied in
\alpha A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\delta\Rightarrow\alpha B_{i,i^{\prime}}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}C_{i^{\prime}+1,j}\delta
(i.e.,
A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\rightarrow B_{i,i^{\prime}}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}C_{i^{\prime}+1,j}^{\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y}}
) is non-skippable.
Since all of the productions that were applied in
A\Rightarrow^{*}\alpha A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\delta
are skippable, we can show by a simple induction (on the number of productions applied) that
\mu^{\alpha}=\emptyset
.
By the definition of
enumerate
,
\text{\sc{applyProd}}(A_{i,j}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}})
is invoked and then
\text{\sc{enumerate}}(B_{i,i^{\prime}}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}C_{i^{\prime}+1,j}\delta,\mathsf{map})
is invoked (recall that
\mu^{\alpha}=\emptyset
).
By induction hypothesis (on
B_{i,i^{\prime}}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}\Rightarrow^{*}\beta)
, we conclude that
\text{\sc{enumerate}}(C_{i^{\prime}+1,j}\delta,\mathsf{map}\cup\mu^{\beta})
is invoked. Applying again the induction hypothesis (this time on
C_{i^{\prime}+1,j}^{\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y}}\Rightarrow^{*}\gamma)
shows that
\text{\sc{enumerate}}(\delta,\mathsf{map}\cup\mu^{\beta}\cup\mu^{\gamma})
is invoked.

Notice that the call to
\text{\sc{enumerate}}(\theta,\mathsf{map}\cup\mu^{\alpha})
is single due to the unambiguous nature of the decorated grammar.
∎

This leads us to the following direct conclusion that shows the desired completeness.

Corollary 6.2
.

For every terminal derivation
S_{1,n}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\Rightarrow^{*}\alpha
the call to the procedure
\text{\sc{enumerate}}(S_{1,n}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}},\mathsf{map})

with
\mathsf{map}:=\{(x,1),(y,n+1)\,\,\,\vline\,\,\,x\in\mathbf{x},y\in\mathbf{y}\}

outputs
\mu^{\alpha}
once.

Combining Corollaries
6.1
and
6.2
leads to the following key lemma which intuitively show that
enumerate
outputs exactly what is needed.

Lemma 6.3
.

The procedure
\text{\sc{enumerate}}(S_{1,n}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}},\mathsf{map})
with
\mathsf{map}:=\{(x,1)\,\,\vline\,\,x\in\mathbf{x}\}\cup\{(y,n+1)\,\,\vline\,\,y\in\mathbf{y}\}
outputs every and each mapping in

\{\mu^{w}\,\,\vline\,\,S_{1,n}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\Rightarrow^{*}w\}
exactly once.

We next discuss the delay between consecutive outputs of
enumerate
, and show that it is independent of the document length.

Lemma 6.4
.

Every two consecutive outputs of
\text{\sc{enumerate}}(S_{1,n}^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}},\mathsf{map})
are different, and
the delay between them is bounded by
O(k)
.

Proof.

Lemma
6.3
allows us to conclude the first part of the statement.
For the delay guarantees, notice that
(1)
each output of
enumerate
consists exactly of
2k
pairs. (This is also a straightforward conclusion from the same Lemma.) In addition,
(2)

the procedure
applyProd
outputs

(\beta,\mathsf{map})
with
\mathsf{map}\neq\emptyset
. This is due to the fact it
is called on a non-terminal
A
which was returned by
jump
. That is, there is a non-skippable rule with
A
on the left of
\rightarrow
. Therefore, with the notation used by the algorithm,
\mathbf{z}\cup\mathbf{w}\neq\emptyset
and
\mathsf{map}
is updated with at least one pair.

Combining
(1)
and
(2)
allows us to conclude that between each two outputs of
enumerate
, the procedure
applyProd
is called at most
2k
times.
We can thus conclude that the number of nested calls to
enumerate
between each two consecutive outputs is at most
2k
. Since the computation of
applyProd
is linear and since
jump
is computed as part of the preprocessing we obtain the desired result.
∎

Finally, to complete the proof of the theorem we combine Corollaries
6.1
and
6.2
, Lemma
6.4
, and the different calls to
enumerate
the main enumeration algorithm invokes.

7
Conclusion

In this paper
we propose a new grammar-based language for document spanners, namely extraction grammars. We compare the expressiveness of context-free spanners with previously studied classes of spanners and present a pushdown model for these spanners.
We present an enumeration algorithm for unambiguous grammars that outputs results with a constant delay after quintic preprocessing in data complexity.
We conclude by presenting recent advances and suggesting several future research directions.

While this paper was being under review, a follow-up paper
[
47
]
to the conference version of this paper
[
56
]
was published. This follow-up contains an extension of the enumeration algorithm that allows obtaining constant delay enumeration after a cubic (rather than quintic) preprocessing time.

To reach a full understanding of the expressiveness of context-free spanners, one should characterize the string relations that can be expressed with context-free spanners. This can be done by understanding
the expressiveness of context-free grammars enriched with string equality selection. We note that
there are some similarities between recursive Datalog over regex formulas
[
12
]
and extraction grammars. Yet, with the former, we reach the full expressiveness of polynomial time spanners (data complexity) whereas with the latter we cannot express string equality. Understanding the connection between these two formalisms better can be a step in understanding the expressive power of extraction grammars.

It can be interesting to examine more carefully whether the techniques used here for enumerating the derivations can be applied also for enumerating queries on trees, or enumerating queries beyond MSO on strings.
This connects to a recent line of work on efficient enumeration algorithms for monadic-second-order queries on trees
[
57
]
. Can our techniques be used to obtain efficient evaluation for more expressive queries?
