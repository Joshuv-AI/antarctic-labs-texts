Scaling limit of a drainage network model on perturbed lattice
Rahul Roy, Kumarjit Saha, Anish Sarkar

Abstract
Study of random networks generally requires the nodes to be independently and uniformly distributed such as a Poisson point process. In this work, we venture beyond this standard paradigm and investigate a stochastic forest obtained from a drainage network model constructed on a randomly perturbed subset of ℤ 2 {\mathbb{Z}}^{2} , where both horizontal and vertical perturbations are given by exponentially decaying unbounded discrete random variables and vertical perturbations are allowed in the upward direction only. We show that the resultant stochastic network is a single tree a.s. We further establish that as a collection of paths, under diffusive scaling the resultant network converges to the Brownian web.



1
Introduction and main results

Directed random networks, where edges have a preferred direction of propagation,
have long been an important class of models for understanding the large
scale behaviour of systems in a wide array of applications. These include, but are not limited
to, transport networks, power grids, various kinds of social networks, different types
of communication networks including wireless sensor networks, multicast communication
networks, peer-to-peer networks and drainage networks.

It has been empirically observed that river networks satisfy various scaling laws
and studying drainage networks is a
statistical approach to understand the unifying behaviour of river basins.
Leopold and Langbein
[
LL62
]
first carried out studies of drainage basins by simulating
drainage networks through random walks in a rectangular region.
Scheidegger
[
S67
]
was the first to introduce a directed network by imposing a preferential
flow condition where each source emptying to one of it’s two neighbours in a
preferred direction. Howard
[
H71
]
removed the restriction of drainage to
neighbouring sites only. Iturbe and Rinaldo
[
RR97
]
presented an excellent survey
of development in this field.

Informally drainage network models can be described in the following way.
A random subset of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
or
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is selected as a collection of ‘source’ vertices.
From each source
{\mathbf{x}}=({\mathbf{x}}(1),\cdots,{\mathbf{x}}(d))\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
exactly one directed edge is
drawn to another source vertex
{\mathbf{y}}
with
{\mathbf{y}}(d)>{\mathbf{x}}(d)
, representing the flow
from
{\mathbf{x}}
to
{\mathbf{y}}
. We briefly describe the Howard’s model here.
Each vertex in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
is a source vertex independently with probability

p\in(0,1)
and a source vertex
u\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
connects to the nearest source at the next level

\{{\mathbf{w}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}:{\mathbf{w}}(d)=u(d)+1\}
. If there are more than one closest source, one of them
is chosen uniformly. For this model Gangopadhyay et.al. [GRS04] showed that
the random graph is a (connected) tree a.s. for
d=2,3
and it is a forest with infinitely
many disjoint tree components for
d\geq 4
. Since then various other drainage network
models with complex dependencies in constructing edges have been proposed
and the tree-forest dichotomy problem depending on dimensions has been studied for these models
(see
[
FLT04
]
, [ARS08], [CT13],
[
RSS16A
]
).

In the mathematical study of such networks,
an important modelling hypothesis is the random distribution of their
nodes. Generally speaking, the distribution of the random nodes in stochastic networks is
often taken to be uniform over space and independently distributed over disjoint regions,
like, the Poisson point process and its variants. Such distribution of nodes is comparatively
easily amenable to rigorous mathematical treatment,
but is often limited in its effectiveness to model the reality, e.g., on a global scale the
homogeneous Poisson process exhibits clusters of points interspersed with vacant spaces,
whereas a more spatially uniform distribution might be a closer representation of the
reality (see, e.g.,
[
GL17
]
). However, little is understood about stochastic geometry of
networks arising from such strongly correlated point processes, principally because the tools
and techniques for studying the Poisson model heavily rely on its exact spatial independence
property.

This motivates the authors of
[
GS20
]
to study a drainage network model
constructed on a point process obtained from perturbations of lattice points where
perturbations are restricted to compact domains. The question of tree-forest dichotomy depending
on dimensions has been explored in
[
GS20
]
for the resultant network and
it’s scaling limit has also been studied. This work has initiated the analysis of directed
networks constructed on disordered lattice points, though working with
perturbations restricted to compact domain is certainly a limitation of
[
GS20
]
.
In this paper, we remove this limitation and examine a set of disordered lattice
points for
d=2
generated due to ‘unbounded’ i.i.d. perturbations along
x
-axis and
y
axis
with exponentially decaying tails. The generated set of perturbed lattice points
exhibit much greater measure of spatial homogeneity
compared to the Poisson process. It is important to observe that the generated
point process is hyperuniform.
Hyperuniformity of point processes have attracted a lot of interest in recent years, especially
in the statistical physics literature (see, e.g.,
[
GL17
]
,

[
T02
]
,
[
TS03
]
and the references therein). A point
process is said to be hyperuniform if the variance of the number of points in an expanding
domain scales like its surface area (or slower), rather than its volume, which is the case for
Poisson or any other extensive system that exhibits FKG-type properties. In fact, hyperuniformity
is closely related to negative association at the spatial level, which precludes the
application of many arguments that are ordinarily staple in stochastic geometry.
In the subsequent paragraphs, we lay out the details of the model and give an account of our principal
results.

We are going to define our model now.
In what follows, for
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, the notation
{\mathbf{x}}(i)

denotes the
i
-th coordinate of
{\mathbf{x}}
for
1\leq i\leq 2
.
We consider an i.i.d. family of random vectors

\displaystyle\{\Gamma_{\mathbf{u}}:=(B_{\mathbf{u}},R_{\mathbf{u}},\Lambda_{\mathbf{u}}):{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\}

(1)

such that the following holds:

(i)

B_{\mathbf{u}}
is a Bernoulli r.v. with success probability
p\in(0,1)

which indicates whether a lattice point
{\mathbf{u}}
is an open (source) vertex or not;

(ii)

R_{\mathbf{u}}
is a Rademacher r.v. associated to
{\mathbf{u}}
which helps to resolve a tie
(in case there is one) for deciding the Howard step;

(iii)

\Lambda_{\mathbf{u}}:=(X_{\mathbf{u}},Y_{\mathbf{u}})
denotes the perturbation random vector
associated to
{\mathbf{u}}
where
X_{\mathbf{u}}
and
Y_{\mathbf{u}}
denote the
x
coordinate perturbation
and
y
coordinate perturbation r.v.’s respectively. Set
\theta_{x},\theta_{y}\in(0,1)

and the respective p.m.f.’s for
X_{\mathbf{u}}
and
Y_{\mathbf{u}}
are given by:

\displaystyle P(X_{\mathbf{u}}=j)

\displaystyle=\theta_{x}((1-\theta_{x})/2)^{|j|}\text{ for }j\in{\mathbb{Z}}\text{ and }

\displaystyle P(Y_{\mathbf{u}}=j)

\displaystyle=\theta_{y}(1-\theta_{y})^{j}\text{ for }j\geq 0.

(2)

Let
\{{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}:B_{\mathbf{u}}=1\}
denote the collection of open (source) vertices. Using
(random) perturbation vector
\Lambda_{\mathbf{w}}=(X_{\mathbf{w}},Y_{\mathbf{w}})
a lattice point
{\mathbf{w}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}

gets perturbed to a new location
{\mathbf{w}}+\Lambda_{\mathbf{w}}
and this perturbed version
is denoted by
\tilde{{\mathbf{w}}}
.
Note that two vertices
{\mathbf{u}}
and
{\mathbf{w}}
may perturb to the same
location (i.e.,
\tilde{{\mathbf{u}}}=\tilde{{\mathbf{w}}}
) and in that case,
we don’t distinguish between them. We consider the set of

perturbed
open vertices denoted by
V
and defined as

\displaystyle V:=\{\tilde{{\mathbf{u}}}={\mathbf{u}}+\Lambda_{\mathbf{u}}:{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2},B_{\mathbf{u}}=1\}.

(3)

Based on the point process
V
we construct the Howard’s network as follows.
For
{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
we define the non-negative integer valued random variable
J({\mathbf{u}})
as

\displaystyle J({\mathbf{u}}):=\min\{|{\mathbf{w}}(1)-{\mathbf{u}}(1)|:{\mathbf{w}}\in V,{\mathbf{w}}(2)={\mathbf{u}}(2)+1\}.

(4)

In other words,
J({\mathbf{u}})
denotes the distance of the
nearest

point in
V
from
{\mathbf{u}}
having
y
coordinate as
{\mathbf{u}}(2)+1
.

Starting from
{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
, based on the set
V
we define
the ‘perturbed Howard’ (PH) step
h({\mathbf{u}})=h({\mathbf{u}},V)
as the almost
surely (a.s.) unique point in
V
with
h({\mathbf{u}})(2)={\mathbf{u}}(2)+1
such that:

\displaystyle h({\mathbf{u}},V)=h({\mathbf{u}}):=\begin{cases}{\mathbf{u}}+(J({\mathbf{u}}),1)&\text{ if }{\mathbf{u}}+(J({\mathbf{u}}),1)\in V\text{ and }{\mathbf{u}}+(-J({\mathbf{u}}),1)\notin V\\
{\mathbf{u}}+(-J({\mathbf{u}}),1)&\text{ if }{\mathbf{u}}+(-J({\mathbf{u}}),1)\in V\text{ and }{\mathbf{u}}+(J({\mathbf{u}}),1)\notin V\\
{\mathbf{u}}+(R_{\mathbf{u}}J({\mathbf{u}}),1)&\text{ if }{\mathbf{u}}+(-J({\mathbf{u}}),1),{\mathbf{u}}+(J({\mathbf{u}}),1)\in V.\end{cases}

(5)

We drop the point set
V
from the notation
h({\mathbf{u}},V)
when it is clear from the
context and denote it simply as
h({\mathbf{u}})
.
We consider the random graph
G:=(V,E)
with vertex set
V

and edge set
E:=\{\langle{\mathbf{u}},h({\mathbf{u}})\rangle:{\mathbf{u}}\in V\}
and call
it the perturbed Howard (PH) model.
We observe that each
{\mathbf{u}}\in V
has exactly one outgoing edge and therefore,
the generated random graph does not have any cycle or loop a.s.

In what follows, we assume that the i.i.d. collections
\{B_{\mathbf{u}}:{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\}
,

\{R_{\mathbf{u}}:{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\}
and
\{\Lambda_{\mathbf{u}}:{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\}
are
independent of each other. We do not require independence of
x
coordinate
and
y
coordinate perturbation random variables. Rather, we assume that the joint
distribution of the perturbation random vector
\Lambda_{\mathbf{u}}=(X_{\mathbf{u}},Y_{\mathbf{u}})

is such that we have
{\mathbb{P}}(X_{\mathbf{u}}=Y_{\mathbf{u}}=0)>0
and
X_{\mathbf{u}},Y_{\mathbf{u}}

respectively follow marginal distributions as specified in (
1
).
Based on these assumptions our first result shows that the random graph

G
is connected a.s.

Theorem 1.1
.

The perturbed Howard network
G=(V,E)
is connected and consists of a single tree a.s.
Further, there is no bi-infinite path in
G
a.s.

Our next main result is that the graph
G
observed as a collection of paths,
converges to the Brownian web under a suitable diffusive scaling. The standard Brownian
web is originated in the work of Arratia
[
A79
]
as the scaling limit of
the voter model on
{\mathbb{Z}}
. Intuitively,
the Brownian web can be thought of as a collection of one-dimensional coalescing Brownian
motions starting from every point in the space time plane
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.
Later
[
FINR04
]
provided
a framework in which the Brownian web is realized as a random variable taking values in a
Polish space. In Subsection
1.1

we present the relevant topological details from
[
FINR04
]
.

Set
h^{0}({\mathbf{u}})={\mathbf{u}}
and for
k\geq 1
, let
h^{k}({\mathbf{u}})=h(h^{k-1}({\mathbf{u}}))
denote
the
k
-th step starting from
{\mathbf{u}}
.
Joining successive steps
\langle h^{k-1}({\mathbf{u}}),h^{k}({\mathbf{u}})\rangle

for all
k\geq 1
linearly, we get the PH path

\pi^{\mathbf{u}}
starting from
{\mathbf{u}}
constructed using the point set
V
.
Sometimes we call this path as the process
\{h^{k}({\mathbf{u}}):k\geq 0\}
of successive
steps also. We consider this two-dimensional PH network as a
collection of paths and want to study it’s scaling limit under diffusive scaling.
We need some notations.

Let
{\cal X}:=\{\pi^{{\mathbf{u}}}:{\mathbf{u}}\in V\}
denote the collection of all PH paths.
For given
\gamma,\sigma>0
and for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the scaled path
is given by

\displaystyle\pi_{n}(\gamma,\sigma)(t):=\pi(n\gamma t)/(\sqrt{n}\sigma)\text{ and }{\cal X}_{n}(\gamma,\sigma):=\{\pi_{n}^{{\mathbf{u}}}(\gamma,\sigma):{\mathbf{u}}\in V\}

(6)

denotes the collection of the scaled paths.
Let
\bar{{\cal X}}_{n}(\gamma,\sigma)
denote the closure of
{\cal X}_{n}(\gamma,\sigma)

w.r.t. certain metric which is explained in detail in SubSection
1.1
.
Now we are ready to state our second theorem regarding the convergence of the diffusively
scaled PH network to the Brownian web.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exist
\sigma=\sigma(p,\theta_{x},\theta_{y})>0
and
\gamma=\gamma(p,\theta_{x},\theta_{y})>0

such that
\bar{{\cal X}}_{n}(\gamma,\sigma)
converges in distribution to the
Brownian web
{\cal W}
as
n\to\infty
.

We should mention here that though we have assumed particular distributions for
x
coordinate
and
y
coordinate perturbation random variables, our arguments hold in much generality as mentioned in
the following remark.

Remark 1.3
.

All our arguments hold and we have Theorem
1.1
and Theorem

1.2
as long as the i.i.d. collection of
perturbation random vectors
\{\Lambda_{\mathbf{u}}=(X_{\mathbf{u}},Y_{\mathbf{u}}):{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\}

satisfies the following assumptions:

(i)

{\mathbb{P}}(X_{\mathbf{u}}=Y_{\mathbf{u}}=0)>0
and
{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{\mathbf{u}}<0)=0
;

(ii)

{\mathbb{P}}(X_{\mathbf{u}}=j)={\mathbb{P}}(X_{\mathbf{u}}=-j)
for all
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
;

(iii)

There exist
C_{0},C_{1}>0
such that

{\mathbb{P}}(|X_{\mathbf{u}}|\geq n)\vee{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{\mathbf{u}}\geq n)\leq C_{0}\exp{(-C_{1}n)}\text{ for all }n.

In particular, if we consider an i.i.d. collection of
Gaussian random vectors
\{\Psi_{\mathbf{u}}=(\Psi_{\mathbf{u}}(1),\Psi_{\mathbf{u}}(2)):{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\}
and
take the perturbation random vector
\Lambda_{\mathbf{u}}=(X_{\mathbf{u}},Y_{\mathbf{u}})
defined as

still Theorem

and Theorem

hold for the Howard’s network
constructed on the point process of perturbed lattice points.

We should mention here that the point process of perturbed lattice points under
Gaussian perturbations have attracted a lot of interests in recent years.
In particular Holroyd and Soo
[

]
showed that in two dimensions,
the resulting Gaussian perturbed lattice point process is ‘rigid’ in the
sense that for this strongly correlated point process, for any bounded domain
{\cal D}

point process configuration on
{\cal D}^{c}
almost surely determines the number of points
inside
{\cal D}
. In three dimensions, Peres and Sly
[
PS14
]
examined rigidity properties of this point process in greater detail and establish a phase transition.

1.1
The Brownian web

Fontes
et. al.

[
FINR04
]
provided a suitable framework so that the Brownian
web (BW) can be regarded as a random variable taking values in a Polish space.
In this section, we recall the relevant topological details from
[
FINR04
]
.

Let
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{c}
denote the completion of the space time plane
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
with
respect to the metric

\rho((x_{1},t_{1}),(x_{2},t_{2}))=|\tanh(t_{1})-\tanh(t_{2})|\vee\Bigl|\frac{\tanh(x_{1})}{1+|t_{1}|}-\frac{\tanh(x_{2})}{1+|t_{2}|}\Bigr|.

As a topological space
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{c}
can be identified with the
continuous image of
[-\infty,\infty]^{2}
under a map that identifies the line

[-\infty,\infty]\times\{\infty\}
with the point
(\ast,\infty)
, and the line

[-\infty,\infty]\times\{-\infty\}
with the point
(\ast,-\infty)
.
A path
\pi
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{c}
with starting time
\sigma_{\pi}\in[-\infty,\infty]

is a mapping
\pi:[\sigma_{\pi},\infty]\rightarrow[-\infty,\infty]
such that

\pi(\infty)=\pi(-\infty)=\ast
and
t\rightarrow(\pi(t),t)
is a continuous
map from
[\sigma_{\pi},\infty]
to
({\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{c},\rho)
.
We then define
\Pi
to be the space of all paths in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{c}
with all possible starting times in
[-\infty,\infty]
equipped with the following metric,

d_{\Pi}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})=|\tanh(\sigma_{\pi_{1}})-\tanh(\sigma_{\pi_{2}})|\vee\sup_{t\geq\sigma_{\pi_{1}}\wedge\sigma_{\pi_{2}}}\Bigl|\frac{\tanh(\pi_{1}(t\vee\sigma_{\pi_{1}}))}{1+|t|}-\frac{\tanh(\pi_{2}(t\vee\sigma_{\pi_{2}}))}{1+|t|}\Bigr|,

for
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in\Pi
. This metric
makes
\Pi
a complete, separable metric space. Convergence in this
metric can be described as locally uniform convergence of paths as
well as convergence of starting times.
Let
{\cal H}
be the space of compact subsets of
(\Pi,d_{\Pi})
equipped with
the Hausdorff metric
d_{{\cal H}}
given by,

d_{{\cal H}}(K_{1},K_{2})=\sup_{\pi_{1}\in K_{1}}\inf_{\pi_{2}\in K_{2}}d_{\Pi}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})\vee\sup_{\pi_{2}\in K_{2}}\inf_{\pi_{1}\in K_{1}}d_{\Pi}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2}).

The space
({\cal H},d_{{\cal H}})
is a complete separable metric space. Let

B_{{\cal H}}
be the Borel
\sigma-
algebra on the metric space
({\cal H},d_{{\cal H}})
.
The Brownian web
{\mathcal{W}}
is then defined and characterized as an

({\cal H},B_{{\cal H}})
valued random variable by the following result:

Theorem 1.4

(Theorem 2.1 of
[
FINR04
]
)
.

There exists an
({\mathcal{H}},{\mathcal{B}}_{{\mathcal{H}}})
-valued random variable

{\mathcal{W}}
whose distribution is uniquely determined by
the following properties:

(a)

from any deterministic point
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, there is almost surely a unique path
\pi^{{\mathbf{x}}}\in{\mathcal{W}}
starting from
{\mathbf{x}}
;

(b)

for a finite set of deterministic points
{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\dotsc,{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, the collection
(\pi^{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}},\dotsc,\pi^{{\mathbf{x}}_{k}})
is distributed as coalescing
Brownian motions starting from
{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\dotsc,{\mathbf{x}}_{k}
;

(c)

for any countable deterministic dense set
{\mathcal{D}}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
,
{\mathcal{W}}
is the closure of
\{\pi^{{\mathbf{x}}}:{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{D}}\}
in
(\Pi,d_{\Pi})
almost surely.

The above theorem shows that the collection is almost surely determined by countably many coalescing Brownian motions.

We present a short introduction on the dual Brownian web
\widehat{{\mathcal{W}}}
.
As in case of forward paths, one can consider a similar metric space of collection of backward paths
denoted by
(\widehat{\Pi},d_{\widehat{\Pi}})
.
The notation
(\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}},d_{\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}})

denotes the corresponding Polish space of compact collections of backward paths with the induced
Hausdorff metric. The Brownian web and its dual denoted
by
({\mathcal{W}},\widehat{{\mathcal{W}}})
is a
({\mathcal{H}}\times\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}},{\mathcal{B}}_{{\mathcal{H}}}\times{\mathcal{B}}_{\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}})
-valued random variable such that:

(i)

\widehat{{\mathcal{W}}}
is distributed as the Brownian web
rotated
180^{0}
about the origin;

(ii)

{\mathcal{W}}
and
\widehat{{\mathcal{W}}}
uniquely determine each other
in the sense that the paths of
{\cal W}
a.s. do not cross with (backward) paths in

\widehat{{\cal W}}
. The interaction between the paths in
{\mathcal{W}}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{W}}
is that of Skorohod reflection (see
[
STW00
]
).

Before concluding this section, we explain the notion of ‘non-crossing’ paths
as this notion will be frequently used in this of the paper.
Two paths
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in\Pi

are said to be non-crossing
if there does not exist any
s_{1},s_{2}\in[\sigma_{\pi_{1}}\vee\sigma_{\pi_{2}},\infty)
such that

\displaystyle(\pi_{1}(s_{1})-\pi_{2}(s_{1}))(\pi_{1}(s_{2})-\pi_{2}(s_{2}))>0.

(7)

It follows that for the PH model, paths are a.s.
non-crossing. For any
n\geq 1
, clearly

{\cal X}_{n}(\gamma,\sigma)
a.s. forms a family of non-crossing paths and it’s
closure in
\Pi
denoted by
\overline{{\cal X}}_{n}(\gamma,\sigma)
which is
a
({\cal H},{\cal B}_{\cal H})
-valued random variable a.s. We will show that as
n\to\infty
,
the
({\cal H},{\cal B}_{\cal H})
-valued random variable

\overline{{\cal X}}_{n}(\gamma,\sigma)
converges
in distribution to the Brownian web
{\cal W}
(see Theorem
1.2
).

2
The joint exploration process and a sequence of ‘In’ steps

Fix
k\geq 1
. In this section we start from
k
many
lattice

points
{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
such that

{\mathbf{x}}_{1}(2)=\cdots={\mathbf{x}}_{k}(2)
and we consider the joint exploration process
of successive steps
\{h^{n}({\mathbf{x}}_{1}),\cdots,h^{n}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}):n\geq 0\}
. Note that the starting points
{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\mathbf{x}}_{k}
are not necessarily in
V
and
h^{n}({\mathbf{x}}_{i})=h^{n}({\mathbf{x}}_{i},V)
for all
1\leq i\leq k
.
Without loss of generality
we consider
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}(2)=\cdots={\mathbf{x}}_{k}(2)=0
.
For
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, let
{\cal F}_{n}
denote the
\sigma
field

\displaystyle{\cal F}_{n}:=\sigma\bigl(\Gamma_{\mathbf{w}}:{\mathbf{w}}(2)\leq n\bigl).

(8)

We observe that the joint exploration process
\{h^{n}({\mathbf{x}}_{1}),\cdots,h^{n}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}):n\geq 0\}

is measurable w.r.t. the filtration
\{{\cal F}_{n}:n\geq 0\}
.
We note that the filtration
\{{\cal F}_{n}:n\geq 0\}
is
not
the
natural or minimal filtration for the joint exploration process.
Nevertheless, we continue to work with this filtration.

We introduce some notations. For
l\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, let

\mathbb{H}^{+}(l):=\{{\mathbf{w}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}:{\mathbf{w}}(2)>l\}\text{ and }\mathbb{H}^{-}(l):=\{{\mathbf{w}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}:{\mathbf{w}}(2)\leq l\}

respectively denote the (open) upper and (closed) lower
half-planes w.r.t. the line
\{y=l\}
.
For any
n\geq 0
, we observe that the
\sigma
-field
{\cal F}_{n}

has ‘some’ information about certain points in the set
V\cap\mathbb{H}^{+}(n)
, viz.,
those vertices in
V
obtained as perturbations of open lattice points in

\mathbb{H}^{-}(n)
. The
\sigma
-field has information about all

\tilde{{\mathbf{w}}}\in V\cap\mathbb{H}^{+}(n)
with
{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{H}^{-}(n)
and
B_{\mathbf{w}}=1
.
Such points can affect the distribution of subsequent steps and consequently,
the joint exploration process
\{h^{n}({\mathbf{x}}_{1}),\cdots,h^{n}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}):n\geq 0\}
is
not
Markov. In the next subsection we show that,
together with this information about
{\cal F}_{n}
‘explored’ points in the upper half-plane

\mathbb{H}^{+}(n)
, the joint exploration process exhibits Markov property.

2.1
Markov property of the joint exploration process

We recall that we are studying the joint exploration process of
k
paths starting
from
{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\mathbf{x}}_{k}
with
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}(2)=\cdots={\mathbf{x}}_{k}(2)=0
.
We need to introduce some more notations. For

\displaystyle V^{+}_{l}

\displaystyle:=\{\tilde{{\mathbf{w}}}={\mathbf{w}}+\Lambda_{\mathbf{w}}:{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{H}^{+}(l),B_{\mathbf{w}}=1\}\text{ and }

\displaystyle V^{-}_{l}

\displaystyle:=\{\tilde{{\mathbf{w}}}={\mathbf{w}}+\Lambda_{\mathbf{w}}:{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{H}^{-}(l),B_{\mathbf{w}}=1\}.

(9)

We observe that

the
\sigma
-field

. On the other hand, the set

\displaystyle I_{l}:=V^{-}_{l}\cap\mathbb{H}^{+}(l)=\{\tilde{{\mathbf{w}}}={\mathbf{w}}+\Lambda_{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{H}^{+}(l):{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{H}^{-}(l),B_{\mathbf{w}}=1\}.

(10)

In other words,
I_{l}
represents the information that the
\sigma
-field

{\cal F}_{l}
has about the point set

Proposition 2.1
.

The process
\{(h^{n}({\mathbf{x}}_{1}),\cdots,h^{n}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}),I_{n}):n\geq 0\}

is Markovian.

Proof.

We consider independent collection of i.i.d. random vectors

\bigl\{\Gamma^{\text{ind}}_{\mathbf{w}}:=\bigl(B^{\text{ind}}_{\mathbf{w}},R^{\text{ind}}_{\mathbf{w}},\Lambda^{\text{ind}}_{\mathbf{w}}\bigr):{\mathbf{w}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\bigr\}

independent of the collection
\{\Gamma_{\mathbf{w}}:{\mathbf{w}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\}
.
Fix

. Conditional on the event
\{(h^{n}({\mathbf{x}}_{1}),\cdots,h^{n}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}),I_{n})=({\mathbf{w}}_{1},\cdots,{\mathbf{w}}_{k},\Delta)\}
, we consider the point process

\displaystyle\Psi=\Psi({\mathbf{w}}_{1},\cdots,{\mathbf{w}}_{k},\Delta):=\{{\mathbf{w}}+\Lambda^{\text{ind}}_{\mathbf{w}}:{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{H}^{+}(n),B^{\text{ind}}_{\mathbf{w}}=1\}\cup\Delta

in the upper half-plane
\mathbb{H}^{+}(n)
.

We observe that starting from
{\mathbf{w}}_{1},\cdots,{\mathbf{w}}_{k}
together with
the information set
\Delta

distribution as starting with the point process
\Psi
on the upper half-plane
\mathbb{H}^{+}(n)
.
In other words, we have the following

\displaystyle(h^{n+1}({\mathbf{x}}_{1}),\cdots,h^{n+1}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}),I_{n+1})\mid

\displaystyle\bigl((h^{n}({\mathbf{x}}_{1}),\cdots,h^{n}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}),I_{n})=({\mathbf{w}}_{1},\cdots,{\mathbf{w}}_{k},\Delta),{\cal F}_{n}\bigr)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}f\bigl(({\mathbf{w}}_{1},\cdots,{\mathbf{w}}_{k},\Delta),\{\Gamma^{\text{ind}}_{\mathbf{w}}:{\mathbf{w}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\}\bigr),

for some measurable function

.
Hence, by the random mapping theorem (see
[

follows.
∎

First we need to introduce certain notions of exponential tails for a general family
of random variables. We require these notions to prove Proposition
2.3

and they will be used repeatedly in later part of this paper .

Definition 2.4
.

We say that a family of r.v.’s
\{X_{i}:i\geq 1\}
has uniform exponential tail
decay if uniformly for all
i\geq 1
there exist constants
C_{0},C_{1}>0
such that

{\mathbb{P}}(X_{i}>n)\leq C_{0}\exp{(-C_{1}n)}\text{ for all }n,i\in{\mathbb{N}}.

We say that a family of r.v.’s
\{X_{i}:i\geq 1\}
has strong uniform exponential tail
decay if uniformly for all
i\geq 1
there exist constants
C_{0},C_{1}>0
such that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(X_{i}>n\mid(X_{i-1},\cdots,X_{1})\bigr)\leq C_{0}\exp{(-C_{1}n)}\text{ for all }n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(15)

We need Proposition
2.5
regarding tail decay of a random sum of r.v.’s with
exponential tail decay to prove Proposition
2.3
.

Corollary 2.5
.

Consider a family of r.v.’s
\{X_{i}:i\geq 1\}
with strong uniform exponential
tail decay and a exponentially decaying positive integer valued r.v.
Y
.
Then there exist
C_{0},C_{1}>0
such that for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
we have

{\mathbb{P}}(\sum_{i=1}^{Y}X_{i}>n)\leq C_{0}\exp{(-C_{1}n)}.

Proof.

Firstly, we observe that because of (
15
) in Definition

2.4
there exists a
r.v.
W
such that for all
i\geq 1
we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(X_{i}>n\mid(X_{i-1},\cdots,X_{1})\bigr)\leq{\mathbb{P}}(W>n)\leq C_{0}\exp{(-C_{1}n)}\text{ for all }n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(16)

Next, independent of the family
\{X_{i}:i\geq 1\}
,
we generate i.i.d. copies
W_{1},W_{2},\dotsc,
of
W
where
W
is as in (
16
).
Because of Lemma of 2.7
[
RSS16A
]
to prove Corollary
2.5

it is enough to show that for all
j\geq 1
we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\sum_{i=1}^{j}X_{i}>n)\leq{\mathbb{P}}(\sum_{i=1}^{j}W_{i}>n).

(17)

We prove (
17
) using the method of induction.
Clearly, the choice of
W_{1}
in (
17
) holds for
j=1
.
Assuming that (
17
) holds for
j\geq 1
we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\sum_{i=1}^{j+1}X_{i}>n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}P(\sum_{i=1}^{j}X_{i}=m)P(X_{j+1}>n-m|X_{1},X_{2},\dotsc,X_{j})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m}P(\sum_{i=1}^{j}X_{i}=m)P(W_{j+1}\geq n-m)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m}P(\sum_{i=1}^{j}W_{i}=m)P(W_{j+1}\geq n-m)

\displaystyle=P(\sum_{i=1}^{j+1}W_{i}\geq n).

The last inequality follows from the induction hypothesis.
This completes the proof.
∎

-th step,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(n<(\beta_{1}-\beta_{0})\mathbf{1}_{\{\beta_{0}<\infty\}}<\infty)={\mathbb{P}}(n<\beta_{1}<\infty)=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(\beta_{1}=n+l).

Regarding evolution of this process we obtain the following properties:

(i)

We obtain an important recursion relation (see (
33
))
in Subsection
3.1.1
regarding evolution of
\{L_{j}:j\geq 0\}
.

(ii)

Further, we show that the amount of increase in the height process,
i.e., the random quantity
(L_{j+1}-L_{j})\mathbf{1}_{\{L_{j+1}>L_{j}\}}

for
j\geq 0
exhibits strong uniform exponentially decaying tail behaviour.

(iii)

Lemma

gives us a strictly positive uniform lower bound
for the probability that

decreases by at least one at the next step.

These three properties together allow us to construct
a non-negative integer valued geometrically ergodic
Markov chain which stochastically dominates evolution of
\{L_{j}:j\geq 0\}

and prove Proposition
3.2
.
Details are given in Section

.

3.1.1
A (stochastic) bound to control increase in
L_{n}

Fix any
j\geq 0
. Given the
\sigma
-field
{\cal G}_{j}
,
we consider evolution of the joint process between
\tau_{j}
-th and
\tau_{j+1}
-th
step. Given
{\cal G}_{j}
, the ‘newly’ explored vertices are perturbed versions of
vertices in the set
\mathbb{H}^{-}(\tau_{j+1})\setminus\mathbb{H}^{-}(\tau_{j})
, i.e.,
vertices those are explored between the
\tau_{j}
-th and the
\tau_{j+1}
-th steps.
The contribution of these newly explored vertices to

L_{j+1}
is denoted by a random variable
{\cal N}_{j+1}
defined as

\displaystyle{\cal N}_{j+1}:=1+\sup\{(\tilde{{\mathbf{w}}}(2)-\tau_{j+1})\vee 0:{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{H}^{-}(\tau_{j+1})\setminus\mathbb{H}^{-}(\tau_{j}),\tilde{{\mathbf{w}}}\in\cup_{i=1}^{k}\nabla(h^{\tau_{j+1}}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}))\}.

(32)

Actually,
{\cal N}_{j+1}
represents
one excess
to the actual contribution
and the reason behind this modification is to ensure that the recursion relation (
33
) holds with probability
1
.
The following lemma gives a recursion relation which bounds the amount of
increase in
L_{j}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For all
j\geq 1
we have

L_{j+1}\leq\max\{L_{j},{\cal N}_{j+1}\}-1.

(33)

Proof.

For any
{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{H}^{-}(\tau_{j})
the corresponding random vector
\Gamma_{\mathbf{w}}

has been explored already by the
\sigma
-field
{\cal G}_{j}
and will be termed as an ‘older’ vertex henceforth. We observe that for any ‘older’ lattice point
{\mathbf{w}}
,
which does not contribute to
L_{j}
,
two situations can happen:

(i)

either
{\mathbf{w}}
is closed or

(ii)

{\mathbf{w}}
is open but
\tilde{{\mathbf{w}}}\notin\cup_{i=1}^{k}\nabla(h^{\tau_{j}}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}))
.

By the nesting property as in Remark
2.2
, we have that
\nabla(h^{\tau_{j+1}}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}))\subseteq\nabla(h^{\tau_{j}}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}))
for all
1\leq i\leq k
.
It follows that in both situations (i) and (ii), such an older vertex
{\mathbf{w}}
,
which does not contribute to
L_{j}
, cannot contribute to
L_{j+1}
as well.
Since at each time the PH path goes up by one step, for any older
vertex
{\mathbf{w}}
contributing to
L_{j}
, it’s contribution to
L_{j+1}

decreases by
at least
one compared to it’s contribution to
L_{j}
.

Further, the above discussion suggests that
we can have
L_{j+1}\geq L_{j}
due to contributions from newly explored
vertices
only
. Hence, the recursion relation (
33
)
follows from the definition of the random variable
{\cal N}_{j+1}
as in (
32
).
∎

The next lemma shows that at every
\tau
step, the probability that
the height random variable reduces by at least one is uniformly bounded away from zero.

Lemma 3.4
.

For any

given

, there exists

(which depends only on
parameters of the process and on

)
such that we have

{\mathbb{P}}((L_{j+1}-L_{j})\leq-1\mid{\cal G}_{j})\geq{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{N}}_{j+1}=1\mid{\cal G}_{j})\geq\tilde{p}.

Proof.

We prove it for
k=1
. The proof for general

is similar.
For
{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
we define the event

\displaystyle B({\mathbf{u}})=\{{\mathbf{u}}+(0,1)\in V_{\text{sp}}\}\cap\{\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}\notin\nabla({\mathbf{u}}+(0,1)):\text{ for all }{\mathbf{v}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2},{\mathbf{v}}(2)={\mathbf{u}}(2)+1\}.

Given
h^{\tau_{j}}({\mathbf{x}}_{1})={\mathbf{w}}_{j}
and
L_{j}>0
, we claim that

\displaystyle(B({\mathbf{w}}_{j})\cap A_{\text{sp}}({\mathbf{w}}_{j}+(0,1)))\subseteq\{(L_{j+1}-L_{j})\leq-1\}.

(34)

We need to justify this event inclusion.
The occurrence of the event
B({\mathbf{w}}_{j})
implies that the vertex
{\mathbf{w}}_{j}+(0,1)
must be a special point
and consequently we must have
h({\mathbf{w}}_{j})={\mathbf{w}}_{j}+(0,1)
.
Further, occurrence of the event
A_{\text{sp}}({\mathbf{w}}_{j}+(0,1))
ensures that ‘In’
event occurs at the next step which makes it a
\tau

\tau_{j+1}=\tau_{j}+1
. Finally, the event

can have their perturbed version in

33

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\bigl((L_{j+1}-L_{j})\leq-1\mid(h^{\tau_{1}}({\mathbf{x}}_{1}),\cdots,h^{\tau_{j}}({\mathbf{x}}_{1}))=({\mathbf{w}}_{1},\cdots,{\mathbf{w}}_{j})\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(B({\mathbf{w}}_{j})\cap A_{\text{sp}}({\mathbf{w}}_{j}+(0,1))\mid E({\mathbf{w}}_{1},\cdots,{\mathbf{w}}_{j})\cap\text{In}({\mathbf{w}}_{j})\bigr)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\bigl(B({\mathbf{w}}_{j})\cap A_{\text{sp}}({\mathbf{w}}_{j}+(0,1))\cap\text{In}({\mathbf{w}}_{j})\cap E({\mathbf{w}}_{1},\cdots,{\mathbf{w}}_{j})\bigr)/{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(\text{In}({\mathbf{w}}_{j})\cap E({\mathbf{w}}_{1},\cdots,{\mathbf{w}}_{j})\bigr)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\bigl(B({\mathbf{w}}_{j})\cap A_{\text{sp}}({\mathbf{w}}_{j}+(0,1))\bigr)\Bigl({\mathbb{P}}(E({\mathbf{w}}_{1},\cdots,{\mathbf{w}}_{j}))/{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(\text{In}({\mathbf{w}}_{j})\cap E({\mathbf{w}}_{1},\cdots,{\mathbf{w}}_{j})\bigr)\Bigr)

it is enough to show that there exist

such that

{\mathbb{P}}(\tau^{M}>n)\leq C_{0}\exp{(-C_{1}n)}.

Lemma
3.4
ensures that we have

{\mathbb{P}}(M_{j+1}\leq M_{j}-1\mid M_{j}=m)\geq\tilde{p}\text{ for all }m\geq 1.

Therefore, it is not difficult to see that the Markov chain
M_{j}
is irreducible.
It suffices to prove that, for some
\alpha>0
, we have
{\mathbb{E}}(\exp{(\alpha\tau M))}<\infty
.

Towards that using Proposition 5.5, Chapter 1 of Asmussen
[
A03
]
,
it suffices to show that there exist a non-negative
function
f:{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
,
n_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
and

r>1
such that
f(j)>\nu
for some
\nu>0
and
{\mathbb{E}}[f(M_{1})\mid M_{0}=j]<\infty
for all
j\leq n_{0}
, while for
j>n_{0}
,
{\mathbb{E}}[f(M_{1})\mid M_{0}=j]\leq f(j)/r
.

Taking
f:\{0,1,2,\cdots\}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
to be

f(i)=\exp{(\alpha i)}
, where
\alpha>0
is small enough so that

{\mathbb{E}}[\exp{(\alpha R_{1})}]<\infty
and
\exp{(-\alpha)}<1/r
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\alpha(M_{j+1}-M_{j})\mid M_{j}=m]

\displaystyle=\exp{(-\alpha)}{\mathbb{P}}(R_{1}\leq m)+\exp{(-\alpha m)}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{1}_{\{R_{j+1}>m\}}\exp{(\alpha R_{j+1})}]

\displaystyle<(1/r)+\exp{(-\alpha m)}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{1}_{\{R_{j+1}>m\}}\exp{(\alpha R_{j+1})}]

\displaystyle\leq(1/r)\text{ for }m\text{ sufficiently large}.

The last inequality follows because
{\mathbb{E}}[\exp(\alpha R_{1})]<\infty

guarantees

\exp{(-\alpha m)}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{1}_{\{R_{1}>m\}}\exp{(\alpha R_{1})}]\to 0\text{ as }m\to\infty.

∎

4
Renewal properties for a single path

In this section, we consider
k=1
and we explain renewal properties of the marginal process

\{h^{\sigma_{\ell}}({\mathbf{x}}_{1}):\ell\geq 0\}
.
More precisely, we will show that
the sequence of renewal steps gives rise to a random walk process with i.i.d.
increments. We define the sequence of successive renewal steps as

\displaystyle\{Y_{\ell+1}=Y_{\ell+1}({\mathbf{x}}_{1}):=h^{\sigma_{\ell+1}}({\mathbf{x}}_{1}):\ell\geq 0\}.

(39)

We observe that
\sigma_{\ell+1}({\mathbf{x}}_{1})-\sigma_{\ell}({\mathbf{x}}_{1})=(Y_{\ell+1}({\mathbf{x}}_{1})-Y_{\ell}({\mathbf{x}}_{1}))(2)
represents the number of steps elapsed, i.e.,
the total time taken between
\ell+1
-th and
\ell
-th renewal.
Our next proposition explains the renewal structure observed at these random steps.

Proposition 4.1
.

The sequence
\{(Y_{\ell+1}-Y_{\ell}):\ell\geq 1\}
gives a collection of
i.i.d. random vectors taking values in the space
{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{N}}

whose distribution does not depend on the choice of the
starting point
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
.

We observe that the above proposition gives i.i.d. increments with a lag.
Since, the starting condition is not the same as the renewal conditions,
the first increment random vector given by
(Y_{1}-{\mathbf{x}}_{1})
has a different distribution. We emphasize that
the above proposition also implies that
\{\sigma_{\ell+1}({\mathbf{x}}_{1})-\sigma_{\ell}({\mathbf{x}}_{1}):\ell\geq 1\}
forms an i.i.d.
sequence whose distribution does not depend on
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
.
This provides a strong uniform exponential tail decay (see Definition
2.4
)
for the family
\{\sigma_{\ell}({\mathbf{x}}_{1}):\ell\geq 1\}
.

Proposition
4.1
will be proved through a sequence of lemmas.
We need to introduce few notations first.
In what follows, together with the usual Howard step
h({\mathbf{u}})=h({\mathbf{u}},V)
we will
also consider
h({\mathbf{u}},V^{+}_{{\mathbf{u}}(2)})
, i.e., the step taken considering only the point set
V^{+}_{{\mathbf{u}}(2)}
. For simplicity of notation,

h({\mathbf{u}},V^{+}_{{\mathbf{u}}(2)})
will be simply denoted as
h({\mathbf{u}},V^{+})
and for any

,
the

-th step

V^{+}_{{\mathbf{u}}(2)}

-th Howard step
h^{j}({\mathbf{u}},V)=h^{j}({\mathbf{u}})

For
{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
we recall the definition of the event

which ensures that points from the set

must be out of the parabolic region
\nabla({\mathbf{u}})

On the event

This motivates us to define another event

similar to

involving the point set

\displaystyle\text{In}^{+}({\mathbf{u}}):=\{h^{j}({\mathbf{u}},V^{+})\in\nabla({\mathbf{u}})\text{ for all }j\geq 1\}.

Observation (

) allows us to have the following equality of events:

(42)

Similarly, the occurrence of the renewal event at the

-th step

can be equivalently represented as the occurrence of the event

\text{In}^{+}(h^{n}({\mathbf{x}}_{1}))\cap\text{Out}(h^{n}({\mathbf{x}}_{1}))

The usefulness of the representation is that the events

and

\text{Out}({\mathbf{u}})
are supported on disjoint sets of random vectors and therefore,

In order to have a better understanding of distribution of renewal increments,
we decouple our renewal event and express it as a joint occurrence of two
independent
events.
The same argument as that of Corollary
2.13
gives us the
next Corollary.

Corollary 4.2
.

For any
j\geq 1
we have the following equality of events

\displaystyle\bigcap_{l=1}^{j}\{\sigma_{l}=m_{l},h^{m_{l}}({\mathbf{x}}_{1})={\mathbf{v}}_{l}\}=

\displaystyle\Bigl[\bigcap_{l=1}^{j}\{h^{m_{l}}({\mathbf{x}}_{1})={\mathbf{v}}_{l},\text{Out}({\mathbf{v}}_{l})\}\cap\bigl\{\cap_{n=m_{l-1}+1}^{m_{l}-1}(\gamma(h^{n}({\mathbf{x}}_{1}))\leq m_{l}-n)

\displaystyle\cup(\text{Out}(h^{n}({\mathbf{x}}_{1})))^{c}\bigr\}\bigcap(\cap_{l=1}^{j-1}\text{In}^{m_{(l+1)}-m_{l}})\Bigr]\bigcap\text{In}^{+}({\mathbf{v}}_{j}),

(43)

and the event

\displaystyle\{\Lambda_{\mathbf{w}}:{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{H}^{+}({\mathbf{v}}_{\ell}(2))\}\mid(h^{\sigma_{\ell}}({\mathbf{x}}_{1})={\mathbf{v}}_{\ell})

6
Tail distribution for the coalescence time of two PH paths and the proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section we start with two points
{\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{x}}_{2}
in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
such that
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}(1)<{\mathbf{x}}_{2}(1)
and
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}(2)={\mathbf{x}}_{2}(2)=0
. A key result for proving the convergence of the PH
network to the BW, lies in a precise estimate for the tail distribution of the
coalescence time of two PH paths:

T({\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{x}}_{2}):=\inf\{t\geq 0:\pi^{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}(t)=\pi^{{\mathbf{x}}_{2}}(t)\}

(53)

where
\pi^{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}=(\pi^{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}(t))_{t\geq 0}
denotes the parametrization of the path
\pi^{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}
.
In this section we prove the following theorem on tail decay of coalescing
time
T({\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{x}}_{2})
of two perturbed Howard paths
\pi^{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}
and
\pi^{{\mathbf{x}}_{2}}
.

Theorem 6.1
.

For the above mentioned choice of
{\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{x}}_{2}
,
there exists a constant
C_{0}>0
which does not depend on
{\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{x}}_{2}
such that,
for any
t>0
,

{\mathbb{P}}(T({\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{x}}_{2})>t)\leq\frac{C_{0}({\mathbf{x}}_{2}(1)-{\mathbf{x}}_{1}(1))}{\sqrt{t}}.

In order to prove Theorem
6.1
we follow a robust technique
developed by Coupier et. al. in
[
CSST20
]
. This technique is applicable for a general class
of processes which need not be Markov but behave like mean zero random
walks away from origin and satisfy certain moment bounds (see Corollary
6.2
).
Proposition
5.1
ensures that the difference
between two PH paths observed at renewal steps satisfy these properties and allows us
to apply this technique to get a suitable tail decay in terms of number of (joint)

renewal
steps. With some additional work, we obtain the tail estimate
for coalescing time in terms of total number of steps.

For completeness we first quote the following corollary taken from
[
CSST20
]
regarding tail decay
of the coalescing time for a suitable class of processes.

Corollary 6.2

(Corollary 5.6 of
[
CSST20
]
)
.

Let
\{Y_{t}:t\geq 0\}
be a
\{{\cal G}_{t}:t\geq 0\}
adapted stochastic process taking values in
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. Let
\nu^{Y}:=\inf\{t\geq 1:Y_{t}=0\}
be the first hitting time to
0
. Suppose for any
t\geq 0
there exist positive constants
M_{0},C_{0},C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}
such that:

(i)

There exists an event
F_{t}
such that, on the event
\{Y_{t}>M_{0}\}
, we have
{\mathbb{P}}(F^{c}_{t}\mid{\cal G}_{t})\leq C_{0}/Y^{3}_{t}
and

{\mathbb{E}}\big[(Y_{t+1}-Y_{t})\mathbf{1}_{F_{t}}\mid{\cal G}_{t}\big]=0~.

(ii)

For any
t\geq 0
, on the event
\{Y_{t}\leq M_{0}\}
,

{\mathbb{E}}\big[(Y_{t+1}-Y_{t})\mid{\cal G}_{t}\big]\leq C_{1}~.

(iii)

For any
t\geq 0
and
m>0
, there exists
c_{m}>0
such that, on the event
\{Y_{t}\in(0,m]\}
,

{\mathbb{P}}\big(Y_{t+1}=0\mid{\cal G}_{t}\big)\geq c_{m}~.

(iv)

For any
t\geq 0
, on the event
\{Y_{t}>M_{0}\}
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}\bigl[(Y_{t+1}-Y_{t})^{2}\mid{\cal G}_{t}\bigr]\geq C_{2}\;\text{ and }\;{\mathbb{E}}\bigl[|Y_{t+1}-Y_{t}|^{3}\mid{\cal G}_{t}\bigr]\leq C_{3}~.

Then,
\nu^{Y}<\infty
almost surely. Further, there exist positive constants
C_{4},C_{5}
such that for any
y>0
and any integer
n
,

{\mathbb{P}}(\nu^{Y}>n\mid Y_{0}=y)\leq\frac{C_{4}+C_{5}y}{\sqrt{n}}~.

By Proposition
5.1
, the four hypotheses
(i)
-
(iv)
of Corollary
6.2
are satisfied by the process
\{Z_{\ell}:\ell\geq 0\}
defined in (
47
) and this gives us the required decay estimate in terms of number of
(joint) renewal steps before coalescing. Using this estimate, we proceed to establish Theorem
6.1
.

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

It is easy to observe that
h^{\sigma_{\ell}}({\mathbf{x}}_{1})=h^{\sigma_{\ell}}({\mathbf{x}}_{2})

implies that
h^{m}({\mathbf{x}}_{1})=h^{m}({\mathbf{x}}_{2})
for some
m
such that
m\leq\sigma_{\ell}
.
In other words

T_{\nu}:=\min\{\sigma_{\ell}:h^{\sigma_{\ell}}({\mathbf{x}}_{1})=h^{\sigma_{\ell}}({\mathbf{x}}_{2})\}

dominates the actual coalescing time
T({\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{x}}_{2})
of the two paths.
Consider an i.i.d. sequence
\{W_{\ell}:\ell\geq 1\}
with exponentially decaying tail
such that for each
\ell\geq 1
the conditional distribution of

(\sigma_{\ell+1}({\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{x}}_{2})-\sigma_{\ell}({\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{x}}_{2}))\mid{\cal S}_{\ell}

is stochastically dominated by
W_{\ell+1}
. Since the family
\{(\sigma_{\ell+1}({\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{x}}_{2})-\sigma_{\ell}({\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{x}}_{2})):\ell\geq 1\}
has strong uniform exponential tail decay
(see Remark
5.3
), we can always construct
such an i.i.d. sequence
\{W_{\ell}:\ell\geq 1\}
.
Choose
c=1/{\mathbb{E}}(2W_{1})
and we have,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(T_{\nu}>t)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor ct\rfloor+1}W_{\ell}\geq t\Big)+{\mathbb{P}}(\nu>ct)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor ct\rfloor+1}(W_{\ell}-{\mathbb{E}}(W_{1}))\geq t(1-c{\mathbb{E}}(W_{1}))\Big)+\frac{C_{0}}{\sqrt{ct}}({\mathbf{x}}_{2}(1)-{\mathbf{x}}_{1}(1))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\text{Var}\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor ct\rfloor+1}W_{\ell}\Big)}{(t(1-c{\mathbb{E}}(W_{1}))^{2}}+\frac{C_{0}}{\sqrt{ct}}({\mathbf{x}}_{2}(1)-{\mathbf{x}}_{1}(1))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(\lfloor ct\rfloor+1)\text{Var}(W_{1})}{(t/2)^{2}}+\frac{C_{0}}{\sqrt{ct}}({\mathbf{x}}_{2}(1)-{\mathbf{x}}_{1}(1))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\sqrt{t}}({\mathbf{x}}_{2}(1)-{\mathbf{x}}_{1}(1)),

for a suitable choice of constant
C_{1}>0
. This completes the proof.
∎

We observe that Theorem
6.1
is applicable
for PH paths starting from
{\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
with
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}(2)={\mathbf{x}}_{2}(2)
and with no information
about the collection of random vectors
\{\Gamma_{\mathbf{w}}:{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{H}^{-}({\mathbf{x}}_{1}(2))\}
.
On the other hand, while applying this result we need to estimate coalescing time of two PH paths
after they come close enough. By that time we gather enough information about
a large set of explored random vectors. Therefore, we need to develop a modified version of Theorem

6.1
which would be applicable in such situations.
Before ending this section, we state such a version of Theorem
6.1

that would be more useful while proving finite dimensional convergence to coalescing Brownian motions.
In order to do that we need to introduce some notations.

Fix any
\beta\in(0,1/2)
and lattice points

{\mathbf{w}}_{1},{\mathbf{w}}_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
with
{\mathbf{w}}_{1}(2)={\mathbf{w}}_{2}(2)=0

and
{\mathbf{w}}_{1}(1)<{\mathbf{w}}_{2}(1)
. Let
{\cal R}\subset\mathbb{H}^{-}(0)
be a bounded region.
Proposition
6.3
gives us that given any
realisations of the collection of random vectors
\{\Gamma_{\mathbf{w}}:{\mathbf{w}}\in{\cal R}_{n}\}

satisfying that
\tilde{{\mathbf{w}}}\in\mathbb{H}^{-}(n^{\beta})
for all
{\mathbf{w}}\in{\cal R}
,
the same tail estimate for coalescing time of PH paths
as in Theorem
6.1
holds. Proposition
6.3

would be more useful for proving convergence to coalescing Brownian motions.

Proposition 6.3
.

For
{\mathbf{w}}_{1},{\mathbf{w}}_{2}
chosen as above, there exists
C_{0}>0
(not depending on
{\mathbf{w}}_{1},{\mathbf{w}}_{2}
)
such that for all large
n

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl(T({\mathbf{w}}_{1},{\mathbf{w}}_{2})>n\mid\bigl(\{\Gamma_{\mathbf{w}}:{\mathbf{w}}\in{\cal R}\}

\displaystyle\text{ such that }\tilde{{\mathbf{w}}}\in\mathbb{H}^{-}(n^{\beta})\text{ for all }{\mathbf{w}}\in{\cal R}\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{0}({\mathbf{w}}_{2}(1)-{\mathbf{w}}_{1}(1))}{\sqrt{n}}.

Proof.

Choose
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
such that
\alpha>\beta
and we
define the events

and
B^{2}_{n}
respectively as

\displaystyle B^{1}_{n}

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem

Towards this we will apply a robust technique that was developed in

We recall here that the PH paths are non-crossing in the sense of (

Let

In other words,

, born before

that intersect

at time

Theorem 7.2

(Theorem 2.2 of
[
FINR04
]
)
.

Let
\{\Xi_{n}:n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
be a sequence of
({\mathcal{H}},B_{{\mathcal{H}}})
valued random variables with non-crossing paths. Assume that the following conditions hold:

(I_{1})

Fix a deterministic countable dense set
{\mathcal{D}}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. For each
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{D}}
, there exists
\pi_{n}^{{\mathbf{x}}}\in\Xi_{n}
such that for any finite set of points
{\mathbf{x}}^{1},\dotsc,{\mathbf{x}}^{k}\in{\mathcal{D}}
, as
n\to\infty
, we have

(\pi^{{\mathbf{x}}^{1}}_{n},\dotsc,\pi^{{\mathbf{x}}^{k}}_{n})
converges in distribution to
(W^{{\mathbf{x}}^{1}},\dotsc,W^{{\mathbf{x}}^{k}})
, where
(W^{{\mathbf{x}}^{1}},\dotsc,W^{{\mathbf{x}}^{k}})
denotes coalescing Brownian motions starting from the points
{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{x}}_{k}
.

(B_{1})

For all
t>0
,
\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{(a,t_{0})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}{\mathbb{P}}(\eta_{\Xi_{n}}(t_{0},t;a,a+\epsilon)\geq 2)\rightarrow 0
as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
.

(B_{2})

For all
t>0
,
\frac{1}{\epsilon}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{(a,t_{0})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}{\mathbb{P}}(\eta_{\Xi_{n}}(t_{0},t;a,a+\epsilon)\geq 3)\rightarrow 0
as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
.

Then
\Xi_{n}
converges in distribution to the standard Brownian web
{\mathcal{W}}
as
n\to\infty
.

Let us first mention that for a sequence of
({\cal H},{\cal B}_{{\cal H}})
-valued random variables
\{\Xi_{n}:n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
with non-crossing paths, Criterion
(I_{1})
implies
tightness (see Proposition B.2 in the Appendix of
[
FINR04
]
or Proposition 6.4 in
[
SSS19
]
) and hence sub-sequential limit(s) always exists. Moreover, Criterion
(B_{1})
has been shown to be redundant with
(I_{1})
for non-crossing path models (see Theorem 6.5 of
[
SSS19
]
). Actually Condition
(I_{1})

implies that subsequential limit contains coalescing Brownian motions
starting from all points with rational coordinates
and hence contain a copy of the standard BW
{\mathcal{W}}
.
Through Condition
(B_{2})
, we ensure that the limiting random variable does not
have extra paths other than the BW.

Criterion
(B_{2})
is often verified by applying an FKG type correlation inequality
together with an estimate on the distribution of the coalescence time between two paths. However,
FKG is a strong property and difficult to apply for models with complicate dependencies.
We will follow a more robust technique developed in
[
CSST20
]
and applicable only for non-crossing path models. The following theorem is taken from
[
CSST20
]
to
obtain joint convergence for the PH and its dual to the BW and its dual.

Theorem 7.3

(Theorem 6.3 of
[
CSST20
]
)
.

Let
\{(\Xi_{n},\widehat{\Xi}_{n}):n\geq 1\}
be a sequence of
({\cal H}\times\widehat{{\cal H}},{\cal B}_{{\cal H}\times\widehat{{\cal H}}})
-valued random variables with non-crossing paths only, satisfying the following assumptions:

(i)

For each

\Xi_{n}
do not cross (backward) paths in

almost surely, i.e., there does not exist any

(ii)

satisfies

\{(\widehat{\pi}_{n}(\sigma_{\widehat{\pi}_{n}}),\sigma_{\widehat{\pi}_{n}}):\widehat{\pi}_{n}\in\widehat{\Xi}_{n}\}

It is useful to mention here that there are several other approaches to replace Criterion
(B_{2})

Long before, Criterion

was proposed by Newman et al

which is applicable even for models with crossing paths as well. Schertzer et al.

for the diffusively scaled PH and its dual

Before proceeding further, we make the following remark regarding existence of bi-infinite path for
the PH network and this completes the proof of Theorem

.
We comment here that it is possible to obtain the same result by following a Burton-Keane argument.

Remark 7.4
.

From the construction of the dual graph it is evident that
the PH network has a bi-infinite path if and only if the dual graph is not connected.
If there are scaled dual paths which do not coalesce but converge
to coalescing Brownian motions then there must be
scaled forward paths entrapped between these scaled dual paths.
Further, joint convergence to the double Brownian web
({\mathcal{W}},\widehat{{\mathcal{W}}})

forces that there must be a limiting forward Brownian path approximating this sequence of entrapped forward scaled paths and this limiting forward Brownian path must spend positive Lebesgue measure time together with a backward (dual) Brownian path.
This leads to a contradiction and gives us that there is no bi-infinite path in the PH network
a.s.

at time
