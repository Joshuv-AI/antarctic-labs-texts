Matching with Transfers under Distributional Constraints
Devansh Jalota, Michael Ostrovsky, Marco Pavone

Abstract
We study two-sided many-to-one matching markets with transferable utilities, e.g., labor and rental housing markets, in which money can exchange hands between agents, subject to distributional constraints on the set of feasible allocations. In such markets, we establish the efficiency of equilibrium arrangements, specified by an assignment and transfers between agents on the two sides of the market, and study the conditions on the distributional constraints and agent preferences under which equilibria exist and can be computed efficiently. To this end, we first consider the setting when the number of institutions (e.g., firms in a labor market) is one and show that equilibrium arrangements exist irrespective of the nature of the constraint structure or the agents’ preferences. However, equilibrium arrangements may not exist in markets with multiple institutions even when agents on each side have linear (or additively separable) preferences over agents on the other side. Thus, for markets with linear preferences, we study sufficient conditions on the constraint structure that guarantee the existence of equilibria using linear programming duality. Our linear programming approach not only generalizes that of Shapley and Shubik [ 1 ] in the one-to-one matching setting to the many-to-one matching setting under distributional constraints but also provides a method to compute market equilibria efficiently.

1.1
Our Contributions

In this work, we study a two-sided many-to-one matching market wherein agents (firms and workers) have heterogeneous preferences, constraints are imposed on the set of feasible allocations, and transfers are allowed between agents on the two sides of the market. In such markets, we investigate the formation of stable arrangements under two notions of stability, one where we require the individual rationality of firms, which is our working definition throughout the paper, and another where we relax this condition (see Section
5.6
).

We begin our study of stable arrangements in such matching markets by establishing a connection between stability and the efficiency of the resulting assignment of workers to firms. In particular, we show that an assignment corresponding to any stable arrangement achieves the highest cumulative payoff for all firms and workers combined.

We then investigate the conditions on the distributional constraints and agent preferences under which stable arrangements exist and can be computed efficiently. To this end, we first consider the setting of one firm and show that stable arrangements exist irrespective of the nature of agents’ preferences or the constraint structure. We mention that this result holds despite the violation of the substitutes condition (see Section
4.1
). However, we then show that this general result on the existence of stable arrangements does not extend to the setting of multiple firms, even when firms have linear (or additively separable) preferences over workers.

As a result, in the multiple firm setting, we focus on markets where firms have linear preferences over workers and provide sufficient conditions on the constraint structure for stable arrangements to exist. In this setting, we develop a linear programming approach to compute stable arrangements in polynomial time in the number of firms, workers, and the cardinality of the constraint set. Our approach provides a connection between linear programming duality and the existence of stable arrangements, which generalizes the duality theory approach of Shapley and Shubik
[
1
]
in the one-to-one matching setting to the many-to-one matching setting under distributional constraints.

3
Model, Definitions, and Efficiency of Stable Arrangements

In this section, we present our model of two-sided many-to-one matching markets with transferable utilities and distributional constraints, introduce the notions of stability and efficiency that we use within this work, and show that assignments corresponding to stable arrangements are efficient.

3.1
General Model

We study a two-sided many-to-one matching market where (i) constraints are imposed on the set of feasible allocations, (ii) agents on each side of the market have heterogeneous preferences, and (iii) transfers are allowed between agents on the two sides of the market. Agents on one side of the market are referred to as firms, denoted
f\in F
, while agents on the other side are referred to as workers, denoted as
w\in W
. Each worker can be assigned (or matched) to at most one firm, while each firm can hire any feasible set of workers for that firm. For each firm
f
, we denote the feasibility collection
\mathcal{T}_{f}\subseteq 2^{W}
as the collection of all subsets of workers that are feasible for that firm, i.e., any set of workers
D\in\mathcal{T}_{f}
satisfies the distributional constraints on hiring for a firm
f
. We provide an example of such distributional constraints at the end of this section.

The agents on both sides of the market derive value from being matched to agents on the other side. To model agent preferences, we let
a_{w,f}
be the value that worker
w
obtains when matched to a firm
f
, and let
b_{D,f}
be the value firm
f
obtains when matched with a feasible set of workers
D\in\mathcal{T}_{f}
. Furthermore, without loss of generality, we normalize the value of agents that are unmatched to zero, i.e.,
a_{w,\emptyset}=0
and
b_{\emptyset,f}=0
, where
\emptyset
denotes the fact that the corresponding agent is unmatched.

An assignment of workers to firms is described by a matrix
\mathbf{X}=(x_{w,f})_{w\in W,f\in F}
, where
x_{w,f}\in\{0,1\}
denotes whether worker
w
is assigned to firm
f
. Such an assignment
\mathbf{X}
is
feasible
if each worker is assigned to at most one firm and the set of workers
D
assigned to each firm
f
satisfies its distributional constraints, i.e.,
D\in\mathcal{T}_{f}
, or firm
f
is unmatched, i.e.,
D=\emptyset
. For the ease of notation, let
f_{w}\in F\cup\emptyset
be the firm that a worker
w
is matched to (or that
w
is unmatched) and
D_{f}\in\mathcal{T}_{f}\cup\emptyset
be the set of workers that a firm
f
is matched to (or that
f
is unmatched) under an assignment
\mathbf{X}
.

In this market, we allow for transfers, which we refer to as salaries, between agents on the two sides of the market. We denote
s_{w,f}
as the salary received by worker
w
from firm
f
, and let
\bm{s}_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{|W|}
be the (prospective) salaries paid by firm
f
to all workers irrespective of whether they are matched or not. We mention that the salaries can take on any real number and thus be negative. In particular, if the salary
s_{w,f}<0
for some worker
w
and firm
f
, then we interpret this as a positive transfer received by firm
f
from worker
w
.

Each agent’s payoff is assumed to be quasi-linear in the salaries, and they are assumed to only derive value from the agent(s) they match with on the other side of the market. Under an assignment
\mathbf{X}
and salary profile
\bm{s}_{f}
for each firm
f
, the payoff
u_{w}
for a worker
w
and
v_{f}
for a firm
f
is given by

\displaystyle u_{w}(\mathbf{X},\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F})=a_{w,f_{w}}+s_{w,f_{w}},

(1)

\displaystyle v_{f}(\mathbf{X},\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F})=b_{D_{f},f}-\sum_{w\in D_{f}}s_{w,f},

(2)

where each worker
w
is assigned to firm
f_{w}
, and a set of workers
D_{f}
is assigned to each firm
f
. We note from the above payoff relation for the firms that the total salary paid by the firm to a set of workers
D_{f}
is the sum of the salaries paid to each worker
w\in D_{f}
by that firm. For brevity, we denote
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{|W|}
and
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{|F|}
as the vector of payoffs of the workers and firms, respectively. Furthermore, for notational simplicity, we will often drop the dependence of the payoffs of the workers and firms on the assignment and salary profile if it is sufficiently clear from the context.

We mention that in this more general two-sided matching setting we do not impose any structure on agents’ preferences or the distributional constraints that define the feasibility collection
\mathcal{T}_{f}
for each firm
f
. However, to elucidate our model, we present a class of agent preferences and distributional constraints, which will serve as the main focus of Section

of this paper.

An Example of Agent Preferences and Distributional Constraints

We consider the setting wherein firms have linear (or additively separable) preferences over workers and the distributional constraints are described by upper and lower bound constraints on hiring. To model each firm’s preferences, let
c_{w,f}
be the value firm
f
receives when hiring worker
w
. Then, for any feasible set of workers
D\in\mathcal{T}_{f}\cup\emptyset
, the total value received by firm
f
is
b_{D,f}=\sum_{w\in D}c_{w,f}
, which is a sum of the values received by the firm from each individual worker in the set
D
. Furthermore, for conciseness, we denote
\alpha_{w,f}=a_{w,f}+c_{w,f}
as the match value for the worker-firm pair
(w,f)

We now describe the upper and lower bound constraints on hiring that define the feasibility collection
\mathcal{T}_{f}
. To this end, for each firm

\mathcal{H}_{f}
denote the collection of all subsets of workers over which the firm has hiring constraints. Furthermore, for each set of workers

\underline{\lambda}_{D}\leq\bar{\lambda}_{D}

\bm{\lambda}_{f}=(\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{f},\bar{\bm{\lambda}}_{f})=(\underline{\lambda}_{D},\bar{\lambda}_{D})_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{f}}
be the vector of hiring quotas for that firm. Then, for each firm

\displaystyle\underline{\lambda}_{D}\leq\sum_{w\in{D}}x_{w,f}\leq\bar{\lambda}_{D},\quad\text{for all }D\in\mathcal{H}_{f}.

That is, for a set of workers
D\in\mathcal{H}_{f}
with quotas

\underline{\lambda}_{D}
and ceiling
\bar{\lambda}_{D}

\mathcal{T}_{f}
for each firm

is the collection of all subsets of workers that satisfy the upper and lower bound hiring constraints defined by the constraint structure
\mathcal{H}_{f}
and quotas
\bm{\lambda}_{f}

3.2
Definitions

Given an assignment
\mathbf{X}
and a vector

of salaries for each firm

(\mathbf{X},\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F})

An arrangement

is defined to be
feasible

\mathbf{X}
is feasible.

A feasible arrangement is
individually rational
for workers (firms) if the resulting payoffs for all the workers (firms) is non-negative, i.e.,
\bm{u}\geq\bm{0}
(
\bm{v}\geq\bm{0}
). Recall that the payoffs of the workers and firms are related to the assignment
\mathbf{X}
and the salaries

through Equations (

Finally, we introduce the notion of stability we use in this work, which is analogous to that considered in Kelso and Crawford
[
6
]
. In particular, stable arrangements are those under which no firm
f
and
feasible
group of workers
D\in\mathcal{T}_{f}\cup\emptyset
that can form a coalition and deviate in a manner that would make all agents in the coalition better off.

Definition 1

(Stable Arrangement)
.

A feasible arrangement
(\mathbf{X},\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F})
is stable if it is individually rational for all workers and firms, and there is no firm-worker set
\{f\}\cup D
, where
D
is feasible for firm
f
, i.e.,
D\in\mathcal{T}_{f}\cup\emptyset
, and vector of salaries
\bm{r}_{f}
, such that

\displaystyle a_{w,f}+r_{w,f}\geq a_{w,f_{w}}+s_{w,f_{w}},\quad\text{for all }w\in D,\text{ and }

\displaystyle b_{D,f}-\sum_{w\in D}s_{w,f}\geq b_{D_{f},f}-\sum_{w\in D_{f}}s_{w,f}

hold, with a strict inequality holding for at least one member in
D\cup\{f\}
. Here each worker
w
is assigned to firm
f_{w}
, and a set of workers
D_{f}
is assigned to each firm
f
under the assignment
\mathbf{X}
.

We reiterate here that the key difference between our definition of stability and that proposed by Kelso and Crawford
[
6
]
is that in our setting firms can only hire a feasible set of workers
D\in\mathcal{T}_{f}\cup\emptyset
rather than any set of workers, as in Kelso and Crawford
[
6
]
. We note that we could also have considered other natural notions of stability, e.g., where firms must all match with a feasible set of workers and do not have the option of remaining unmatched unless
\emptyset\in\mathcal{T}_{f}

[
32
]
. In Section
5.6
, we consider such an alternative notion of stability, as in Kojima et al.
[
32
]
, and present a generalization of our results under this stability notion in Appendix

When studying stable arrangements
(\mathbf{X},\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F})
, we are particularly interested in their
total match value

U
, i.e., the cumulative payoffs of all firms and workers combined, which is given by

\begin{split}U(\mathbf{X},\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F})&=\sum_{w\in W}u_{w}(\mathbf{X},\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F})+\sum_{f\in F}v_{f}(\mathbf{X},\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F}),\\
&=\sum_{w\in W}(a_{w,f_{w}}+s_{w,f_{w}})+\sum_{f\in F}(b_{D_{f},f}-\sum_{w\in D_{f}}s_{w,f}),\\
&=\sum_{w\in W}a_{w,f_{w}}+\sum_{f\in F}b_{D_{f},f},\\
&=U(\mathbf{X}),\end{split}

(3)

where, with slight abuse of notation, we denote the total match value
U
only as a function of the corresponding assignment. We do so, since the salaries do not influence the total match value of an arrangement. We now present the notion of efficiency that we use in this work.

Definition 2

(Efficiency of Assignments)
.

We define an assignment
\mathbf{X}^{*}
of workers to firms to be
efficient
if it maximizes the total match value among the class of all feasible assignments, i.e.,
U(\mathbf{X}^{*})\geq U(\mathbf{X})
for all feasible assignments
\mathbf{X}
.

3.3
Efficiency of Stable Arrangements

In this section, we present a consequence of stability on the efficiency of the resulting assignment. In particular, we establish that the assignment corresponding to any stable arrangement is efficient, i.e., it achieves the highest total match value among all feasible assignments.

Theorem 1

(Efficiency of Stable Arrangements)
.

If an arrangement
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F})
is stable, then the assignment
\mathbf{X}^{*}
is efficient.

While stable arrangements may, in general, not exist (see Section
5.1
), Theorem
1
establishes that if the market works at all, i.e., if stable arrangements exist, then the market works well as it results in efficient assignments. To prove Theorem
1
, we leverage the following necessary and sufficient condition for an arrangement to be stable.

Lemma 1

(Necessary and Sufficient Condition for Stability)
.

Let
\bm{u}=(u_{w})_{w\in W}
and
\bm{v}=(v_{f})_{f\in F}
represent the payoff vectors of the workers and the firms under the arrangement
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F})
. Then, an arrangement
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F})
is stable if and only if it is feasible, individually rational for workers and firms, and it holds for each feasible set of workers
D\in\mathcal{T}_{f}\cup\emptyset
for each firm
f\in F
that
\sum_{w\in D}a_{w,f}+b_{D,f}\leq\sum_{w\in D}u_{w}+v_{f}
, i.e., the sum of the payoffs of a firm
f
and any feasible set of workers
D
is at least the sum of their match values
\sum_{w\in D}a_{w,f}+b_{D,f}
.

For a proof of Lemma
1
, see Appendix
A.1
. Lemma
1
characterizes a necessary and sufficient condition such that no feasible coalition of workers and a firm can improve upon their payoffs in a manner that would make each agent in the coalition weakly better off and at least one agent in the coalition strictly better off. We now leverage Lemma
1
to prove Theorem
1

Proof.

To prove this claim, we show that the assignment
\mathbf{X}^{*}
has the highest total match value, i.e.,
U(\mathbf{X}^{*})\geq U(\mathbf{X})
for any other feasible assignment
\mathbf{X}
. To this end, let
\bm{u}=(u_{w})_{w\in W}
and

and feasible set of workers

that

\displaystyle\sum_{w\in D}u_{w}+v_{f}\geq b_{D,f}+\sum_{w\in D}a_{w,f}.

(4)

Next, consider an efficient assignment
\mathbf{Y}
, where
P_{f}^{*}
denotes the set of workers matched to firm
f
or the empty set in case no workers are matched to firm
f
under the efficient assignment. Then, it follows that the total match value of the efficient assignment is given by

\displaystyle U(\mathbf{Y})=\sum_{f\in F}\Bigg(\sum_{w\in P_{f}^{*}}a_{w,f}+b_{P_{f}^{*},f}\Bigg)=\sum_{f\in F:P_{f}^{*}\neq\emptyset}\Bigg(\sum_{w\in P_{f}^{*}}a_{w,f}+b_{P_{f}^{*},f}\Bigg).

Next, for each set of workers

it follows by Equation (

\displaystyle\sum_{w\in P_{f}^{*}}a_{w,f}+b_{P_{f}^{*},f}\leq\sum_{w\in P_{f}^{*}}u_{w}+v_{f},\quad\text{for all }f\in F\text{ such that }P_{f}^{*}\neq\emptyset.

\displaystyle U(\mathbf{Y})

where (a) follows from the individual rationality of workers and firms, and (b) follows from the sequence of equalities in Equation (

4
Existence of Stable Arrangements under General Constraints and Preferences

The substitutes condition is often critical for the stability of the corresponding market outcome in both matching markets without constraints
[
6
,
7
]
and matching with constraints
[
32
]
in the transferable utility setting. In contrast to earlier works that use the substitutes condition to establish the existence of stable arrangements, in this section, we study the question of whether stable arrangements can be guaranteed to exist under a general class of firm preferences and constraint structures beyond those that preserve the substitutes condition. To this end, we first present an example to demonstrate, even when the firm’s preferences are linear over the workers, that the substitutes condition may not hold under general constraints (Section
4.1
). Despite this result, we show in Section
4.2
that stable arrangements are guaranteed to exist in a setting with one firm under a general class of firm preferences and constraint structures.

4.1
Violation of Substitutes Condition

In this section, we present an example where firms have linear preferences over workers to show that the substitutes condition may be violated under general constraints. To elucidate this example, we first define the substitutes condition, which states that an increase in the salary of a given worker weakly increases the demand (of a firm) for all other workers. To this end, we denote the salary vector of all workers other than worker
w
as
\bm{s}_{-w,f}

Definition 3

such that
D\backslash\{w\}\subseteq D^{\prime}
.

We now show through a counterexample with one firm and three workers, where the firm has linear preferences over the workers, that raising the salary of one worker decreases the demand of that firm for another worker, thereby violating the substitutes condition. We refer to

4.2
Existence of Stable Arrangements for a One-Firm Setting

While Example
1
indicates that the substitutes condition may not hold, even under linear preferences, for general constraint structures, in this section, we show in a one-firm setting that stable arrangements exist irrespective of the nature of the constraint structure or the preferences of the firm
f
over workers.

Theorem 2

(Existence of Stable Arrangement for One Firm)
.

In a one-firm setting, there exists a stable arrangement under any feasibility collection
\mathcal{T}_{f}
and any vector of valuations
\bm{b}=(b_{D,f})_{D\in\mathcal{T}_{f}}
of the firm
f
.

Proof.

To prove this claim, we construct an arrangement
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\bm{s}_{f}^{*})
and show that this arrangement is stable. For the remainder of this proof, we drop the subscript
f
for the ease of notation since we are considering the setting of just one firm.

To define the arrangement
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\bm{s}^{*})
, consider the following problem

\displaystyle D^{*}=\argmax_{D\in\mathcal{T}\cup\emptyset}\Bigg\{\max_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{2}{\s: s_{w} + a_{w} \geq 0,}{\forall w \in D}}b_{D}-\sum_{w\in D}s_{w}\Bigg\}.

First observe that the solution of the inner maximization problem must satisfy
s_{w}=-a_{w}
, and thus, it follows that the above problem can be rewritten as

\displaystyle D^{*}=\argmax_{D\in\mathcal{T}\cup\emptyset}\Bigg\{b_{D}+\sum_{w\in D}a_{w}\Bigg\}.

(5)

Now, consider the assignment matrix
\mathbf{X}^{*}
that encodes the assignment of workers in the set
D^{*}
to the firm, and let the salaries
\bm{s}^{*}
be such that
s_{w}^{*}=-a_{w}
for
w\in D^{*}
and any
s_{w}^{*}<-a_{w}
for
w\notin D^{*}
. Then, we claim that the arrangement
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\bm{s}^{*})
is stable.

To see this, first observe that such an arrangement is individually rational for all workers since the payoff for matched workers
w\in D^{*}
is given by
a_{w}+s_{w}^{*}\geq 0
, while all unmatched workers obtain a payoff of zero. Next, the arrangement
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\bm{s}^{*})
is individually rational for the firm, since the payoff of the firm under the arrangement is at least its payoff if unmatched since
D^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{T}\cup\emptyset
. Furthermore, note that the assignment
\mathbf{X}^{*}
is feasible since the set of workers
D^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{T}\cup\emptyset
. Finally, to prove stability we claim that there is no set of workers
D\in\mathcal{T}\cup\emptyset
with corresponding salaries
\tilde{\bm{s}}
such that (i) all workers in the set
D
are weakly better off than under the arrangement
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\bm{s}^{*})
, and (ii) the firm is at least as well of as under the assignment
D^{*}
with salaries
\bm{s}^{*}
, i.e.,
b_{D}-\sum_{w\in D}s_{w}^{*}\geq b_{D^{*}}-\sum_{w\in D^{*}}\tilde{s}_{w}
, with at least one of the inequalities in (i) or (ii) being strict.

To establish this claim, we proceed by contradiction. In particular, suppose that there is a vector of salaries
\tilde{\bm{s}}
and a feasible set of workers
\tilde{D}\in\mathcal{T}\cup\emptyset
such that there is a worker
w^{\prime}\in\tilde{D}
that is strictly better off than under the arrangement
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\bm{s}^{*})
. Furthermore, suppose that the firm and all other workers
w\in\tilde{D}\backslash\{w^{\prime}\}
are weakly better off than under the arrangement
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\bm{s}^{*})
. That is, worker
w^{\prime}
receives a strictly positive payoff, i.e.,
\tilde{s}_{w^{\prime}}>-a_{w^{\prime}}
, while all other workers in
\tilde{D}
receive a non-negative payoff, i.e.,
\tilde{s}_{w}\geq-a_{w}
for all workers
w\in\tilde{D}\backslash\{w^{\prime}\}
. Note that the case where the firm is strictly better off is not possible since for any set of workers
\tilde{D}\in\mathcal{T}\cup\emptyset
the payoff of the firm under the matching with workers in
D^{*}
is at least that under any other feasible assignment by the definition of
D^{*}
. That is, the firm’s payoff
v
under the arrangement
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\bm{s}^{*})
satisfies

\displaystyle v(\mathbf{X}^{*},\{\bm{s}^{*}\})=b_{D^{*}}-\sum_{w\in D^{*}}s_{w}^{*}\geq b_{\tilde{D}}-\sum_{w\in\tilde{D}}\tilde{s}_{w}

for any vector of incomes
\tilde{\bm{s}}
such that
\tilde{s}_{w}+a_{w}\geq 0
and all sets of workers
\tilde{D}
by the definition of
D^{*}
.

Now, since worker
w^{\prime}
receives a strictly positive payoff while all other workers in
\tilde{D}
receive a non-negative payoff it follows that

\displaystyle b_{\tilde{D}}-\sum_{w\in\tilde{D}}\tilde{s}_{w}<b_{\tilde{D}}+\sum_{w\in\tilde{D}}a_{w}.

(6)

Finally, denoting
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}
as the assignment of workers in the set
\tilde{D}
to the firm and
\tilde{v}
as the payoff of the firm under the arrangement
(\tilde{\mathbf{X}},\tilde{\bm{s}})
, we get that

\displaystyle\tilde{v}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}},\tilde{\bm{s}})=b_{\tilde{D}}-\sum_{w\in\tilde{D}}\tilde{s}_{w}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{<}}b_{\tilde{D}}+\sum_{w\in\tilde{D}}a_{w}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}b_{D^{*}}+\sum_{w\in D^{*}}a_{w}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{=}}b_{D^{*}}-\sum_{w\in D^{*}}s_{w}^{*}=v(\mathbf{X}^{*},\bm{s}^{*}),

where (a) follows by Equation (
6
), (b) follows from the definition of
D^{*}
, and (c) follows from the definition of
\bm{s}^{*}
. This relation implies that if there is an arrangement that leads to weakly higher payoffs for all workers with one worker receiving a strictly positive payoff, then the firm will necessarily obtain a strictly lower payoff than under the arrangement
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\bm{s}^{*})
. Thus, we have arrived at our desired contradiction, which proves that the arrangement
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\bm{s}^{*})
is stable.
∎

Theorem
2
is particularly striking since it establishes that stable arrangements exist under arbitrary constraint structures and agent preferences, including those under which the substitutes condition may not hold, as elucidated in Example

5
Stable Arrangements under Linear Preferences and Hiring Quotas

In the previous section, we established that stable arrangements exist under general constraints and agent preferences in the setting of a single firm. In this section, we consider the multiple firm setting and focus on matching markets where firms have linear preferences over workers and upper and lower bound distributional constraints. Our motivation for studying this class of markets is two-fold. First, linear preferences have been studied extensively in matching markets
[
33
]
and serve as a direct analog to the matching market with additively separable preferences considered by Crawford and Knoer
[
14
]
. Next, upper and lower bound constraints on hiring are natural in many applications
[
34
]
and have been widely studied in two-sided matching markets without transfers
[
26

25
,
28
]
and in two-sided matching markets with transfers
[
32
]
. Notably, Kojima et al.
[
32
]
show that a sub-class of upper and lower bound hiring constraints are necessary and sufficient for the preservation of the substitutes condition, which guarantees the existence of stable arrangements.

We begin our study of this class of markets by establishing in Section
5.1
that stable arrangements are not guaranteed to exist even in a setting with two firms. This result on the non-existence of stable arrangements makes the study of this class of markets with linear preferences particularly relevant. As a result, we then study the conditions on the constraint structure under which stable arrangements exist and can be computed efficiently. To this end, we introduce a class of constraint structures in Section
5.2
under which the substitutes condition holds and use linear programming duality to establish the existence of and efficiently compute stable arrangements in Sections
5.3
-
5.5
. We mention here that our main contribution in this section is to provide a method to efficiently compute stable arrangements through a connection between linear programming duality and the existence of stable arrangements. For the ease of exposition, we focus on the setting with upper bound hiring constraints and present a generalization of our results under lower bound constraints in Appendix
5.6
. In the context of both upper and lower bound hiring constraints, we note that the condition on the constraint structure that we require to establish the existence of stable arrangements using linear programming is analogous to Kojima et al.’s
[
32
]
necessary and sufficient condition for the preservation of the substitutes condition. We refer to Remark
2
in Appendix

for a distinction between the sets of constraints we consider and those in Kojima et al.
[

5.1

5.2
Constraint Structures that Guarantee Existence of Stable Arrangements

In the previous section, we presented an example where stable arrangements did not exist under general upper bound constraints on hiring. As a result, in this section, we present classes of constraints under which the substitutes condition holds, which guarantees the existence of stable arrangements. In particular, we introduce
polymatroids
and

constraint structures, under which Kojima et al.
[

show that the substitutes condition is preserved. We reiterate here that we begin with the study of only upper bound constraints on hiring and present the generalization of our results to the setting with lower bound constraints in Section

We now introduce the notions of a polymatroid and a hierarchy. To this end, we begin by recalling that in the setting with upper bound constraints on hiring, an assignment
\mathbf{X}
is

To elucidate the types of restrictions on the constraint sets and quotas in the case of polymatroids, we first introduce the definitions of

\emptyset\notin\mathcal{H}^{\prime}

A\cap B,A\cup B\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}

to be

Definition 4

(Polymatroid

Let

\displaystyle P=\{\bm{y}:\bm{y}\geq\bm{0},\sum_{d\in D}y_{d}\leq g(D),\text{ for }D\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\}

Note that in the context of matching markets, the set

An important class of an intersecting family (of constraints)
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is a hierarchy, which is a family of subsets such that any two members of
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
are either disjoint or one is a subset of the other.

Definition 5

(Hierarchy)
.

A family (of constraints)

S\subseteq S^{\prime}

Remark 1

(Relation between Hierarchies and Polymatroids)
.

Hierarchical constraints are a special case of polymatroid constraints, i.e., for any non-negative integer-valued function
g^{\prime}
on a hierarchy
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
, the polyhedron
P=\{\bm{y}:\bm{y}\geq\bm{0},\sum_{d\in D}y_{d}\leq g^{\prime}(D),\text{ for }D\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\}
is a polymatroid
P(\mathcal{H}^{\prime},g^{\prime})
. To see this, note that no two members of
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
are intersecting subsets by the definition of a hierarchy, and thus (i)
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is an intersecting family, and (ii) the function
g^{\prime}
is submodular on intersecting sets.

Having noted this fundamental connection between polymatroids and hierarchies, we present an example in the context of the two-sided matching markets studied in this work to elucidate these notions and refer to

5.3
Linear Programming and Stable Arrangements

In this section, we present our main result on the connection between linear programming and the existence of stable arrangements. In particular, we use linear programming duality to establish that stable arrangements exist and can be computed efficiently under both polymatroid and hierarchical constraints.

We now present the main result of this section (Theorem

5.6

In this section, we present the generalization of Theorem

In this setting, note that in contrast to upper bound hiring constraints, where remaining unmatched is feasible and individually rational for all firms, assignments that respect the lower bound hiring constraints may not correspond to individually rational outcomes for firms. 6
Conclusion and Future Work

In this work, we studied two-sided many-to-one matching markets with transferable utilities subject to distributional constraints on the set of feasible allocations. In such markets, we established the efficiency of stable arrangements and, in the setting of one firm, showed their existence irrespective of the nature of the constraint structure or agent preferences. For markets with multiple firms, we showed that, even when firms have linear preferences over workers, stable arrangements may not exist under general constraint structures. Thus, for markets with linear preferences of firms, we investigated sufficient conditions on the constraint structure to guarantee the existence of stable arrangements and developed a method compute such arrangements efficiently. To this end, we developed an approach based on linear programming duality that extended Shapley and Shubik’s
[
1
]
duality theory approach in the one-to-one matching setting to the many-to-one matching setting under distributional constraints. This duality-based approach provided a method to compute stable arrangements efficiently through a connection between linear programming duality and stability.

There are various directions for future research. First, it would be worthwhile to study whether the linear programming approach can guarantee the existence of stable arrangements for constraints beyond polymatroid and generalized polymatroid constraints, i.e., for constraint structures that do not satisfy the substitutes condition. Next, it would be interesting to investigate whether the linear programming duality approach can be generalized to a broader range of firm preferences through methods such as convex programming. Finally, in many-to-one matching markets with transfers and distributional constraints, it would also be valuable to explore mechanisms that, in addition to preserving stability, are strategy-proof for agents on one side of the market.

\sum_{w\in D}a_{w,f}+b_{D,f}
if matched. Next, if for some some firm
f
and for some set of workers
D\in\mathcal{T}_{f}\cup\emptyset
, it holds under the arrangement
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F})
that

\displaystyle\sum_{w\in D}a_{w,f}+b_{D,f}>\sum_{w\in D}u_{w}+v_{f},

then the firm
f
and the workers in the set
D
can profitably deviate by matching with each other and obtaining a cumulative match value of at least
\sum_{w\in D}a_{w,f}+b_{D,f}
. Since
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F})
is stable, such a deviation by a coalition of workers and a firm is not possible, thereby proving one direction of this claim.

To prove the other direction, suppose that an arrangement
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F})
is feasible, individually rational for workers and firms, and for all feasible sets of workers
D
for all firms
f\in F
it holds that
\sum_{w\in D}a_{w,f}+b_{D,f}\leq\sum_{w\in D}u_{w}+v_{f}
. Then, we claim that there is no feasible set
D\in\mathcal{T}_{f}\cup\emptyset
of workers for a given firm
f
such that the firm
f
as well as all the workers in that set
D
are weakly better off with at least one worker in the set
D
or the firm
f
being strictly better off. Suppose for contradiction that such a coalition of workers
D^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{f}\cup\emptyset
and a firm
f
exists that can profitably deviate with at least one of the members of the coalition being strictly better off. To this end, let
\tilde{u}_{w}
for
w\in D^{\prime}
and
\tilde{v}_{f}
denote the payoffs for agents in the firm-worker set
\{f\}\cup D^{\prime}
when the workers in set
D^{\prime}
match with firm
f
. Then, the cumulative payoff received by this firm-worker set
\{f\}\cup D^{\prime}
is
\sum_{w\in D^{\prime}}a_{w,f}+b_{D^{\prime},f}
. Note that since this was a profitable deviation for this coalition, it must hold that
\tilde{u}_{w}\geq u_{w}
for all
w\in D^{\prime}
and
\tilde{v}_{f}\geq v_{f}
with at least one of the inequalities being strict. As a result, we get that

\displaystyle\sum_{w\in D^{\prime}}a_{w,f}+b_{D^{\prime},f}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\sum_{w\in D^{\prime}}\tilde{u}_{w}+\tilde{v}_{f}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{>}}\sum_{w\in D^{\prime}}u_{w}+v_{f}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\geq}}\sum_{w\in D^{\prime}}a_{w,f}+b_{D^{\prime},f},

a contradiction. Here (a) follows from the fact that the cumulative payoffs of agents in the firm-worker set
\{f\}\cup D^{\prime}
is
\sum_{w\in D^{\prime}}a_{w,f}+b_{D,f}
when workers in the set
D^{\prime}
match with the firm
f
, (b) follows from the fact that the coalition
\{f\}\cup D^{\prime}
had a profitable deviation, and (c) holds by the assumption on the arrangement
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F})
. Since we have obtained our desired contradiction, we have shown the second direction of our claim.
∎

Proof of Lemma

) is a special case of the linear program
\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E|}}\bm{c}^{T}\bm{y}\text{ s.t. }\bm{y}\in P_{1}\cap P_{2}
for two polymatroids
P_{1},P_{2}

\bm{q}
.

To this end, we define the set

, i.e., the set of all tuples of worker-firm pairs

\bm{q}
as the vector with entries
\alpha_{w,f}
and the decision variables
\bm{y}=(y_{j})_{j\in E}
as a vector
\bm{x}
with entries

\displaystyle P_{f}=\left\{\bm{x}:\bm{x}\geq\bm{0},\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{2}{(w, f) \in}{D \times\{ f \}}}x_{w,f}\leq g_{f}(D\times\{f\}),\text{ for }D\in\mathcal{H}_{f}\right\}

for each firm

is an intersecting family and the function

\mathcal{H}_{f}\times\{f\}

Next, we note that

for

that

We now show that the allocation Constraints (

for the workers given by the sets
\{w\}\times F
for all workers

A.3
Extensions of Results under r-Stability

In this section, we present the natural extensions of Lemma
1
, Theorem
1
, and Theorem
2
under the notion of r-stability. We omit the proofs of these extensions since their proofs are almost entirely analogous to the proofs of their counterparts under the stability notion of Definition
1
. We mention here that the primary difference in the proofs of the extensions of these results is that under the notion of r-stability, any firm
f
must hire a set of workers
D
that satisfy the hiring constraints, i.e.,
D\in\mathcal{T}_{f}
. Note that this is in contrast to the stability notion of Definition
1
, wherein firms had the option of remaining unmatched, i.e., any firm
f
could hire a set of workers
D\in\mathcal{T}_{f}\cup\emptyset
.

Necessary and Sufficient Condition for r-Stability

We first present the necessary and sufficient condition for r-stability, which generalizes Lemma
1
.

Lemma 4

(Necessary and Sufficient Condition for r-Stability)
.

Let
\bm{u}=(u_{w})_{w\in W}
and
\bm{v}=(v_{f})_{f\in F}
represent the payoff vectors of the workers and the firms under the arrangement
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F})
. Then, an arrangement
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F})
is r-stable if and only if it is r-feasible, individually rational for workers, and it holds for each r-feasible set of workers
D\in\mathcal{T}_{f}
for each firm
f\in F
that
\sum_{w\in D}a_{w,f}+b_{D,f}\leq\sum_{w\in D}u_{w}+v_{f}
, i.e., the sum of the payoffs of a firm
f
and any r-feasible set of workers
D
is at least the sum of their match values
\sum_{w\in D}a_{w,f}+b_{D,f}
.

Note that the above condition is analogous to the necessary and sufficient condition for stability in Lemma
1
other than the requirement of r-feasibility and the relaxation of the individual rationality of firms.

Efficiency of r-Stable Arrangements

To present the extension of Theorem
1
under this new notion of stability, we first define an appropriate notion of the efficiency of assignments in this context, which we refer to as r-efficiency.

Definition 7

(r-Efficiency of Assignments)
.

We define an assignment
\mathbf{X}^{*}
of workers to firms to be
r-efficient
if it maximizes the total match value among the class of all r-feasible assignments, i.e.,
U(\mathbf{X}^{*})\geq U(\mathbf{X})
for all r-feasible assignments
\mathbf{X}
.

Note that, as opposed to the efficiency of assignments, the notion of r-efficiency is defined with respect to the set of all r-feasible assignments, i.e., the assignment must be feasible with respect to the firm’s hiring constraints and thus firm
f
cannot remain unmatched unless
\emptyset\in\mathcal{T}_{f}
. We now present the efficiency result for r-stable arrangements.

Theorem 6

(r-Efficiency of r-Stable Arrangements)
.

If an arrangement
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F})
is r-stable, then the assignment
\mathbf{X}^{*}
is r-efficient.

Existence of r-Stable Arrangements in a One-Firm Setting

Finally, we present the extension of Theorem
2
, which, under the notion of stability in Definition
1
, establishes the existence of stable arrangements under general agent preferences and constraints, under r-stability.

Theorem 7

(Existence of r-Stable Arrangement for One Firm)
.

In a one-firm setting, there exists a r-stable arrangement under any vector of valuations
\bm{b}=(b_{D,f})_{D\in\mathcal{T}_{f}}
of the firm
f
and any feasibility collection
\mathcal{T}_{f}
for which there exists a r-feasible assignment.

We mention that, as opposed to Theorem
2
, we require here that an r-feasible assignment exists, as otherwise no r-stable arrangement can form. Note that no such feasibility requirement was needed for Theorem
2
since under the notion of stability in Definition
1
it was feasible for firms to remain unmatched.

A.4
Generalized Polymatroid Constraints

Here, we introduce the notion of generalized polymatroid constraints, as in Frank
[
35
]
.

Definition 8

(Generalized Polymatroid Constraints)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
be an intersecting family over a set S and
g,\rho
be integer-valued functions on
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
that are submodular and supermodular, respectively, on intersecting sets where
\rho(D)-\rho(D\backslash D^{\prime})\leq g(D^{\prime})-g(D^{\prime}\backslash D)
for all
D,D^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
. Then, the polyhedron

\displaystyle P=\{\bm{y}:\bm{y}\geq\bm{0},\rho(D)\leq\sum_{d\in D}y_{d}\leq g(D),\text{ for }D\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\}

defines a generalized polymatroid
P(\mathcal{H}^{\prime},g,\rho)
, and the corresponding constraints are generalized polymatroid constraints.

A.5
Proof of Theorem
5

Proof Outline

To prove Theorem
5
, we use Lemma
4
and Theorem
6
to show that there exists some r-stable arrangement
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\{s_{f}\}_{f\in F})
that can be computed via a linear program that has a number of decision variables and constraints that are polynomial in the number of firms, workers, and the cardinality of the constraint set given by
\sum_{f\in F}|\mathcal{H}_{f}|
. To compute the assignment
\mathbf{X}^{*}
, we note by the r-efficiency of r-stable arrangements (Theorem
6
) that the assignment must maximize the total match value. Thus, we formulate a linear program analogous to Problem (
11a
)-(
11d
) that also includes lower bound constraints on hiring to compute r-efficient fractional assignments. We then show that this linear program has an integral optimal solution under the specified condition on the constraint structure in the statement of the theorem. We mention that the number of decision variables and constraints of this linear program are polynomial in the number of firms, workers, and the cardinality of the constraint set. Then, we use linear programming duality to establish that there exist a vector of salaries
\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F}
such that the resulting payoffs of agents under the assignment
\mathbf{X}^{*}
and salaries
\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F}
satisfy the necessary and sufficient condition for r-stability (Lemma
4
), which proves our claim.

In the remainder of this section, we present the details of the above proof sketch.

Linear Programming to Compute r-Efficient Assignments

Since r-stable arrangements are r-efficient, we now present the following linear program with both upper and lower bound hiring constraints to compute r-efficient fractional assignments

\displaystyle\underset{\displaystyle\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{|W|\times|F|}}{\mathrm{max}}\quad U(\mathbf{X})=\sum_{w\in W}\sum_{f\in F}\alpha_{w,f}x_{w,f},

(13a)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{|W|\times|F|}}}{\mathrm{max}}$}][c]{\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\mathrm{max}$}][l]{\mathrm{\kern 1.00006pts.t.}}}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{f\in F}x_{w,f}\leq 1,\quad\forall w\in W,

(13b)

\displaystyle x_{w,f}\geq 0,\quad\forall w\in W,f\in F,

(13c)

\displaystyle\sum_{w\in D}x_{w,f}\leq\bar{\lambda}_{D},\quad\forall D\in\mathcal{H}_{f},f\in F,

(13d)

\displaystyle\sum_{w\in D}x_{w,f}\geq\bar{\lambda}_{D},\quad\forall D\in\mathcal{H}_{f},f\in F,

(13e)

Note that the linear Program is identical to Problem (
11a
)-(
11d
) other than that it additionally includes lower bound hiring Constraints (
13e
).

We now show that the solution to the linear Program (
13a
)-(
13e
) is integral when the upper and lower bound hiring constraints correspond to generalized polymatroid constraints (Definition
8
).

Lemma 5

(Integral Optimal Solutions With Lower Bound Constraints)
.

Suppose that for each firm
f
the constraint structure
\mathcal{H}_{f}\times\{f\}
is an intersecting family over
W\times F
, and there are integer-valued functions
g_{f}
,
\rho_{f}
on
\mathcal{H}_{f}\times\{f\}
, that are submodular and supermodular, respectively, on intersecting sets such that
\rho_{f}(D)-\rho_{f}(D\backslash D^{\prime})\leq g_{f}(D^{\prime})-g_{f}(D^{\prime}\backslash D)
for all
D,D^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{f}
and firms
f\in F
. Further, suppose that the upper and lower bound hiring quotas
\bar{\bm{\lambda}}_{f},\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{f}
satisfy
\bar{\lambda}_{D}=g_{f}(D\times\{f\})
and
\underline{\lambda}_{D}=\rho_{f}(D\times\{f\})
for all
D\in\mathcal{H}_{f}
and firms
f\in F
. Then there is an optimal solution
\mathbf{X}^{*}
of the linear Program (
13a
)-(
13e
) such that
x_{w,f}^{*}\in\{0,1\}
for all worker-firm pairs
(w,f)\in W\times F
.

Note here that the condition on the constraint structure and hiring quotas in the statement of Lemma
6
(and Theorem
5
) correspond to generalized polymatroid constraints. The proof of Lemma
5
follows from the generalized polymatroid intersection theorem
[
35
]
, which states that the optimal solution of a linear program under the intersection of two generalized polymatroids is integral.

Theorem 8

(Generalized Polymatroid Intersection Theorem
[
35
]
)
.

For a set
E
, a vector
\bm{q}
, and two generalized polymatroids
P_{1},P_{2}
, the linear program
\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E|}}\bm{q}^{T}\bm{y}\text{ s.t. }\bm{y}\in P_{1}\cap P_{2}
has an integral optimal solution.

Using Theorem
8
, we can apply an analogous reasoning to that used in the proof of Lemma
2
to establish Lemma
5
and so we omit the details here. In the following, we note that the result of Lemma
5
also extends to the setting of generalized polyhedral constraints introduced by Kojima et al.
[
32
]
.

Remark 2

(Extension of Lemma
5
under Generalized Polyhedral Constraints)
.

We mention that Lemma
5
extends to the setting of generalized polyhedral constraints, which are closely related to generalized polymatroid constraints. To this end, we note that generalized polyhedral constraints include not only upper and lower bound hiring constraints, with similar restrictions on the constraint structure as in the case of generalized polymatroid constraints, but also include “always-hiring” and “never-hiring” constraints. That is, in addition to the upper and lower bound hiring constraints given by
\mathcal{H}_{f}
for each firm
f
there are constraints that correspond to the setting when firms must hire a certain subset of workers
\bar{E}_{f}
or not hire a certain subset of workers
\underline{E}_{f}
. Here
\bar{E}_{f}\cap\underline{E}_{f}=\emptyset
, and for all subsets of workers
D\in\mathcal{H}_{f}
,
D\cap(\bar{E}_{f}\cup\underline{E}_{f})=\emptyset
, for all firms
f
. We note that the constraint structure of the linear program that additionally consider these “always” and “never” hiring constraints can also be shown to be the intersection of two generalized polymatroids in a manner similar to that used in the proof of Lemma
2
due to the condition on the constraint structure for all firms
f
and subsets of workers
D\in\mathcal{H}_{f}
that
D\cap(\bar{E}_{f}\cup\underline{E}_{f})=\emptyset
.

Computing Salaries using Duality

Finally, to complete the proof of Theorem
5
, we use linear programming duality to establish the existence of salaries
\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F}
such that the arrangement
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F})
is r-stable under the condition that the linear Program (
13a
)-(
13e
) has an integral optimal solution. To this end, we show that if there exists an integral optimal solution
\mathbf{X}^{*}
to the linear Program (
13a
)-(
13e
), then there exist salaries
\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F}
such that the arrangement
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F})
satisfies the necessary and sufficient condition for r-stability in Lemma
4
.

Lemma 6

(Duality and r-Stable Arrangements)
.

Suppose that there exists an optimal solution
\mathbf{X}^{*}
to the linear Program (
13a
)-(
13e
) such that
x_{w,f}^{*}\in\{0,1\}
for all worker-firm pairs
(w,f)
. Then, there exist salaries
\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F}
corresponding to payoff vectors
\bm{u},\bm{v}
for the workers and firms, respectively, such that the arrangement
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F})
is r-feasible, individually rational for workers, and
\sum_{w\in D}\alpha_{w,f}\leq\sum_{w\in D}u_{w}+v_{f}
for all feasible sets of workers
D\in\mathcal{T}_{f}
for each firm
f\in F
.

Proof.

The proof of this claim follows a similar argument to that of Lemma
3
other than the fact that there are additional dual variables introduced with the addition of the lower bound constraints that need to be used to define the payoffs of the firms and workers. To this end, we first define the dual of Problem (
13a
)-(
13e
) and then construct payoff vectors
\bm{u},\bm{v}
that satisfy the necessary and sufficient condition for r-stability in Lemma
4
.

To define the dual of Problem (
13a
)-(
13e
), we introduce the dual variables
\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{w})_{w\in W}
of the allocation Constraints (
13b
), the dual variables
\bar{\bm{\eta}}=\{\bar{\eta}_{D}\}_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{f},f\in F}
of the upper bound hiring Constraints (
13d
), and the dual variables
\underline{\bm{\eta}}=\{\underline{\eta}_{D}\}_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{f},f\in F}
of the lower bound hiring Constraints (
13e
). Then, the dual of the linear Program (
13a
)-(
13e
) is given by

\displaystyle\underset{\displaystyle\bm{\mu},\bar{\bm{\eta}}}{\mathrm{min}}\quad\sum_{w\in W}\mu_{w}+\sum_{f\in F}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{f}}\left(\bar{\eta}_{D}\bar{\lambda}_{D}+\underline{\eta}_{D}\underline{\lambda}_{D}\right),

(14a)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{\bm{\mu},\bar{\bm{\eta}}}}{\mathrm{min}}$}][c]{\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\mathrm{min}$}][l]{\mathrm{\kern 1.00006pts.t.}}}\quad

\displaystyle\alpha_{w,f}-\mu_{w}-\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{f}}\mathbbm{1}_{w\in D}\cdot\left(\bar{\eta}_{D}+\underline{\eta}_{D}\right)\leq 0,\quad\forall w\in W,f\in F,

(14b)

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}\geq\bm{0},\quad\bar{\bm{\eta}}\geq\bm{0},\quad\underline{\bm{\eta}}\leq\bm{0}.

(14c)

Here we let
\bm{\mu}^{*}
,
\bar{\bm{\eta}}^{*}
and
\underline{\bm{\eta}}^{*}
be the optimal solutions to the dual Problem (
14a
)-(
14c
). We now define the payoff vectors
\bm{u},\bm{v}
using the optimal dual solutions. In particular, let the payoffs of the workers
u_{w}=\mu_{w}^{*}
for all
w\in W
and that of the firms be given by
v_{f}=\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{f}}\left(\bar{\eta}_{D}^{*}\bar{\lambda}_{D}+\underline{\eta}_{D}^{*}\underline{\lambda}_{D}\right)
for all firms
f\in F
. Note that these payoffs can be realized under the salaries where
s_{w,f}=u_{w}-a_{w,f}
for all worker-firm pairs
(w,f)
such that
x_{w,f}^{*}=1
, and
s_{w,f}<-\max_{(w,f)\in W\times F}a_{w,f}
otherwise. We now show that the arrangement
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F})
is stable by leveraging Lemma
4
.

To see this, first observe that the arrangement is r-feasible since the assignment
\mathbf{X}^{*}
is r-feasible (it satisfies all the upper and lower bound hiring constraints), and the set of payoffs are individually rational for the workers since
u_{w}=\mu_{w}^{*}\geq 0
for all workers
w\in W
by the sign Constraints (
14c
). Note that the individual rationality of the firms cannot be guaranteed since
\underline{\bm{\eta}}^{*}\leq\bm{0}
.

Finally, we establish that the payoff vectors
\bm{u},\bm{v}
satisfy the necessary and sufficient condition for stability in Lemma
4
in a similar manner to that in the proof of Lemma
3
. To this end, fix a firm
f
and a set of workers
D\in\mathcal{T}_{f}
. Then, summing the dual feasibility Constraints (
14b
) over all workers
w\in D
, we get that

\displaystyle\sum_{w\in D}\alpha_{w,f}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{w\in D}\mu_{w}^{*}+\sum_{w\in D}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{f}}\mathbbm{1}_{w\in D}\left(\bar{\eta}_{D}^{*}+\underline{\eta}_{D}^{*}\right),

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\sum_{w\in D}\mu_{w}^{*}+\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{f}}\left(\bar{\eta}_{D}^{*}\bar{\lambda}_{D}+\underline{\eta}_{D}^{*}\underline{\lambda}_{D}\right),

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\sum_{w\in D}u_{w}+v_{f},

where (a) follows by the sign Constraints (
14c
) and the fact that for any feasible set of workers
D\in\mathcal{T}_{f}
it holds that
\underline{}{\lambda}_{D}\leq\sum_{w\in D}\mathbbm{1}_{w\in D}\leq\bar{\lambda}_{D}
, and (b) follows by the above definitions of the worker and firm payoffs,
\bm{u}
and
\bm{v}
, respectively.

Since
D
is an arbitrary r-feasible set of workers for firm
f
, we have that the above inequality
\sum_{w\in D}u_{w}+v_{f}\geq\sum_{w\in D}\alpha_{w,f}
holds for all r-feasible sets
D\in\mathcal{T}_{f}
for any firm
f\in F
. Thus, we have shown that the arrangement
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\{\bm{s}_{f}\}_{f\in F})
satisfies the necessary and sufficient condition for stability in Lemma
1
, which proves our claim.
∎

Jointly, Lemmas
5
and
6
establish Theorem
5
. We also note that, as in the case of polymatroid constraints, under generalized polymatroid constraints all the vertices of the polyhedron corresponding Constraints (
13b
)-(
13e
) are integral
[
38
]
. Thus, any polynomial time algorithm for linear programs, e.g., Karmarkar’s algorithm
[
39
]
or variants of the ellipsoid method by Khachiyan
[
37
]
, that finds basic optimal solutions, i.e., vertices of the polyhedron, can be used to compute r-stable arrangements in polynomial time. Furthermore, as in the case of Cororllary
1
, a direct consequence of Theorem
5
is that r-stable arrangements exist and can be computed efficiently for upper and lower bound constraints that correspond to a hierarchy. Finally, we mention that Theorem
5
focused on the case when the upper and lower bound constraints were over the same set
\mathcal{H}_{f}
of workers for the ease of exposition. We thus note that Theorem
5
can also be readily extended to the setting when the set of workers
\mathcal{H}_{f}
over which a firm has upper bound constraints are not necessarily the same as the set of workers over which the firm has lower bound constraints.

B.4
Non-Integrality of Solution of Linear Program

We construct an instance with upper bound constraints and show that there is no integral optimal solution of the linear Program (
11a
)-(
11d
) under these constraints. In particular, consider a setting with three workers, labelled
w_{1}
,
w_{2}
, and
w_{3}
, and one firm, labelled
f_{1}
. Further consider the following set of upper bound constraints: (i) at most one of workers
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
can be hired, i.e.,
x_{w_{1},f_{1}}+x_{w_{2},f_{1}}\leq 1
, (ii) at most one of workers
w_{2}
and
w_{3}
can be hired, i.e.,
x_{w_{2},f_{1}}+x_{w_{3},f_{1}}\leq 1
, and (iii) at most one of workers
w_{1}
and
w_{3}
can be hired, i.e.,
x_{w_{1},f_{1}}+x_{w_{3},f_{1}}\leq 1
, as depicted in Figure
4
. Furthermore, suppose that the match values of all the worker-firm pairs are equal, i.e.,
\alpha_{w_{1},f_{1}}=\alpha_{w_{2},f_{1}}=\alpha_{w_{3},f_{1}}=1
. Then, in this setting, the unique optimal solution to the linear Program (
11a
)-(
11d
) is
x_{w_{1},f_{1}}=0.5
,
x_{w_{2},f_{1}}=0.5
and
x_{w_{3},f_{1}}=0.5
which achieves a total match value of
1.5
, while any feasible integral assignment would result in a total match value of at most one. Thus, there are market instances when the optimal solution of the linear Program (
11a
)-(
11d
) may not be integral.

B.5
Distributional Constraints Alter the Properties of Stable Arrangements

B.5.1
Stable Arrangements Beyond LP Duality

We construct an instance to show that there may be payoff vectors
\bm{u},\bm{v}
for the workers and firms, respectively, that are not solutions to the dual linear Program (
12a
)-(
12c
) but are still associated with some stable arrangement. To this end, consider a setting of one worker
w
and one firm
f
, where the firm has an upper bound constraint on hiring at most two workers. Further, suppose that the value of the firm for the worker is given by
c_{w,f}=1
and the value of the worker for the firm is given by
a_{w,f}=0.5
. Then, under the solution of the dual linear Program (
12a
)-(
12c
) the payoff for the firm (
v_{f}=\bar{\eta}_{\{w\}}=0
) is zero by complimentary slackness since the upper bound constraint on hiring is not met with equality. However, one can observe that any salary
s_{w,f}
such that
-0.5\leq s_{w,f}\leq 1
will result in a stable arrangement where the worker and the firm are assigned to each other. That is, there are stable arrangements at which the payoffs of the workers and firms do not correspond to solutions of the dual linear Program (
12a
)-(
12c
), e.g., at a salary of
s_{w,f}=0.5
, the firm has a positive payoff.

B.5.2
Structure of Payoffs of Firms and Workers

We now present an example to show that in the many-to-one matching setting under distributional constraints, the payoffs of firms and workers do not form a lattice structure, as is the case in the one-to-one matching setting of Shapley and Shubik
[
1
]
. In particular, Shapley and Shubik
[
1
]
established in the one-to-one matching setting that if the payoffs
\bm{u},\bm{v}
and
\bm{u}^{\prime},\bm{v}^{\prime}
correspond to two stable arrangements, then the payoffs
\bar{\bm{u}},\underline{\bm{v}}
and
\underline{\bm{u}},\bar{\bm{v}}
also correspond to stable arrangements, where
\bar{u}_{w}=\max\{u_{w},u_{w}^{\prime}\}
,
\underline{u}_{w}=\min\{u_{w},u_{w}^{\prime}\}
,
\bar{v}_{f}=\max\{v_{f},v_{f}^{\prime}\}
, and
\underline{v}_{f}=\min\{v_{f},v_{f}^{\prime}\}
. We show that the payoffs
\bar{\bm{u}},\underline{\bm{v}}
and
\underline{\bm{u}},\bar{\bm{v}}
may not correspond to any stable arrangements in the many-to-one matching setting with distributional constraints through the following example.

Consider a setting of two workers, labeled
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
, and one firm, labeled
f_{1}
, where the firm has linear preferences and upper and lower bound constraints on hiring the two workers given by
2\leq x_{w_{1},f_{1}}+x_{w_{2},f_{1}}\leq 2
. These constraints imply that the only feasible allocation is when the firm hires both workers. Next, suppose that the values of the two workers for the firm are given by
a_{w_{1},f_{1}}=0
,
a_{w_{2},f_{1}}=0
, while the value of the firm for the two workers is given as
c_{w_{1},f_{1}}=2.5
and
c_{w_{2},f_{1}}=0.5
. Given these values, the efficient allocation
\mathbf{X}^{*}=(1,1)
is to match both workers to the firm rather than remaining unmatched. Furthermore, under the salaries
\bm{s}=(1,1)
and
\bm{s}^{\prime}=(2.5,0.5)
, we observe that the vector of payoffs are
\bm{u}=(1,1)
,
v_{f_{1}}=1
, and
\bm{u}^{\prime}=(2.5,0.5)
and
v_{f_{1}}^{\prime}=0
, respectively.

We first show that the arrangements
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\bm{s})
and
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\bm{s}^{\prime})
are stable and then establish that any arrangement corresponding to the assignment
\mathbf{X}^{*}
and the payoffs
\bar{\bm{u}},\underline{\bm{v}}
(or
\underline{\bm{u}},\bar{\bm{v}}
) is not stable.

To see that
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\bm{s})
is stable, we first note that the arrangement is feasible since the assignment
\mathbf{X}^{*}
is feasible as it respects the firm’s distributional constraints. Next, this arrangement is certainly individually rational for workers and firms. Finally, it is also clear that there is no other salary profile at which both the workers and firm could be weakly better off with at least one of the two workers or the firm being strictly better off. This is because a strict increase in the salary of any worker would result in a decreased payoff for the firm, and vice versa. Thus,
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\bm{s})
is a stable arrangement. Using a very similar line of reasoning it can be shown that
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\bm{s}^{\prime})
is also a stable arrangement.

Now, we establish that an arrangement corresponding to the payoffs
\bar{\bm{u}},\underline{\bm{v}}
is not stable. To see this, observe that
\bar{u}_{w_{1}}+\bar{u}_{w_{2}}+\underline{v}_{f_{1}}=2.5+1+0>3=\alpha_{w_{1},f_{1}}+\alpha_{w_{2},f_{1}}
, which contradicts stability since the sum of the payoffs must add up to the total match value.

Next, the arrangement corresponding to the payoffs
\underline{\bm{u}},\bar{\bm{v}}
is not stable. To see this, observe that
\underline{u}_{1}+\underline{u}_{2}+\bar{v}_{1}=1+0.5+1=2.5<3=\alpha_{w_{1},f_{1}}+\alpha_{w_{2},f_{1}}
, which again contradicts stability since the sum of the payoffs must add up to the total match value.

Thus, we have shown that in the many-to-one matching setting under distributional constraints, the payoffs of firms and workers do not form a lattice structure.

B.6
Individual Rationality and Feasibility May be at Odds with Lower Bound Constraints

We construct an instance to show that any feasible assignment that respects the lower bound constraints on hiring for a firm may not correspond to an individually rational outcome for that firm. To this end, consider a setting with one firm
f
and one worker
w
, where the firm has a lower bound constraint on hiring the worker with a quota of one, i.e.,
x_{w,f}\geq 1
. That is, assigning the worker to the firm is the only feasible assignment. Now, if the match value of the worker-firm pair is strictly less than zero, i.e.,
\alpha_{w,f}<0
, then it must hold that
u_{w}+v_{f}=\alpha_{w,f}<0
. Thus, the only feasible assignment must violate individual rationality for either the firm or the worker. If the resulting outcome is such that it is not individually rational for the worker, then the worker is better off remaining unmatched and thus receiving a payoff of zero. As a result, the only feasible assignment of hiring the worker can only be supported through a negative payoff to the firm.
