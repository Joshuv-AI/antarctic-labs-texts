Abstract
We study gapped 4d quantum field theories (QFTs) obtained from a relevant deformation of a UV conformal field theory (CFT). For simplicity, we assume the existence of a ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2} symmetry and a single ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2} -odd stable particle and no ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2} -even particles at low energies. Using unitarity, crossing and the assumption of maximal analyticity we compute numerically a lower bound on the value of the a a -anomaly of the UV CFT as a function of various non-perturbative parameters describing the two-to-two scattering amplitude of the particle.

2
Review of classic results

In this paper we work in
4d
Minkowski flat space with metric:

\eta_{\mu\nu}=\eta^{\mu\nu}=\text{diagonal}\{-1,+1,+1,+1\}.

(8)

All the quantum field theories have a very special operator called the stress-tensor
T^{\mu\nu}(x)
. It is symmetric in its two Lorentz indices and obeys conservation law, namely

T^{\mu\nu}(x)=T^{\nu\mu}(x),\qquad\partial_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}(x)=0.

(9)

2.1
Stress tensor and trace anomaly in CFTs

Let us start the discussion by considering conformally invariant quantum field theory. The conformal symmetry puts severe constraints on the form of correlation functions. In
Osborn:1993cr
it was shown that the most general two- and three-point functions of the stress tensor in CFTs have the following form

\displaystyle\langle 0|T^{\mu\nu}(x_{1})T^{\rho\sigma}(x_{2})|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{T}}{x_{12}^{8}}\times\mathbf{T}^{\mu\nu;\rho\sigma}_{0},

(10)

\displaystyle\langle 0|T^{\mu\nu}(x_{1})T^{\rho\sigma}(x_{2})T^{\alpha\beta}(x_{3})|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x_{12}^{4}x_{23}^{4}x_{31}^{4}}\Big(\mathbb{A}\mathbf{T}^{\mu\nu;\rho\sigma;\alpha\beta}_{1}+\mathbb{B}\mathbf{T}^{\mu\nu;\rho\sigma;\alpha\beta}_{2}+\mathbb{C}\mathbf{T}^{\mu\nu;\rho\sigma;\alpha\beta}_{3}\Big).

(11)

Here the objects
\mathbf{T}_{0}
,
\mathbf{T}_{1}
,
\mathbf{T}_{2}
and
\mathbf{T}_{3}
take care of the correct behaviour of the correlation functions under conformal transformations. They are called tensor structures. The standard basis for these tensor structures is defined in appendix
A
.
The coefficient
C_{T}\geq 0
is usually referred to as the central charge. The coefficients
\mathbb{A}
,
\mathbb{B}
and
\mathbb{C}
are called the OPE coefficients since they appear in the OPE expansion of the stress-tensor with itself. All of them are real quantities. Due to the conformal Ward identities
5
5

5

All the generators of the conformal transformation can be written as certain integral of the stress-tensor, see for example
Osborn:1993cr
. By performing appropriate integrals over one stress-tensor in (
11
) and using the properties of the generators we effectively obtain the two-point function (
10
).
the following relation holds

C_{T}\ =\ \frac{\pi^{2}}{3}\Big(14\mathbb{A}-2\mathbb{B}-5\mathbb{C}\Big).

(12)

Summarizing, there are three independent parameters describing the 2- and the 3-point function of the stress-tensor. One can choose these three parameters to be for example
\{C_{T},\;\mathbb{A},\;\mathbb{B}\}
. It is standard to also define the following quantities

a\equiv\frac{\pi^{4}}{64\times 90}\Big(9\mathbb{A}-2\mathbb{B}-10\mathbb{C}\Big),\qquad c\equiv\frac{\pi^{4}}{64\times 30}\Big(14\mathbb{A}-2\mathbb{B}-5\mathbb{C}\Big)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{64\times 10}C_{T}.

(13)

Finally we recall that in CFTs the trace of the stress-tensor vanishes, namely

T^{\mu}{}_{\mu}(x)=0.

(14)

Let us now discuss CFTs on the curved background which is described by the metric
g_{\mu\nu}(x)
. Conformal invariance on a curved background is achieved by requiring
\text{diff}\times\text{Weyl}
invariance. We recall that the Weyl transformation is defined as

g_{\mu\nu}(x)\rightarrow e^{2\sigma(x)}g_{\mu\nu}(x),\qquad\mathcal{O}(x)\rightarrow e^{-\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}\sigma(x)}\mathcal{O}(x),

(15)

where
\sigma(x)
is an arbitrary scalar function,
\mathcal{O}(x)
is a local scalar operator and
\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}
is the scaling dimension of the operator
\mathcal{O}(x)
.
Contrary to the flat space-time where (
14
) holds, for CFTs on the curved background we instead have

\langle 0|T^{\mu}{}_{\mu}(x)|0\rangle_{g}=\ -a\times E_{4}+c\times W^{2},

(16)

where
E_{4}
is the Euler density defined in (
177
) and
W^{2}
is the square of Weyl tensor defined in (
178
). The subscript
g
in the left-hand side of (
16
) indicates that the CFT is on the curved background rather than on the flat one. The coefficients
a
and
c
are exactly the ones introduced in
(
13
). They are called the Weyl anomalies as well as trace anomalies.
The name Weyl anomaly is appropriate because exact Weyl invariance implies
\langle 0|T^{\mu}{}_{\mu}(x)|0\rangle_{g}=0
.

2.2
Compensator field and the dilaton particle

Let us define a generic quantum field theory as the renormalization group flow from the UV to the IR fixed points which are described by the UV and the IR conformal field theories. Such a theory in curved background can be described by the action

A(g,M_{i})\equiv A_{\text{UV\;CFT}}(g)+A_{\text{deformation}}(g,M_{i}),

(17)

where the deformation of the UV CFT has the form

A_{\text{deformation}}(g,M_{i})=\sum_{i}\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\left(\lambda_{i}M_{i}^{4-\Delta_{i}}\mathcal{O}_{i}(x)\right).

(18)

Here
\mathcal{O}_{i}(x)
are relevant scalar UV CFT operators (operators obeying
\Delta_{i}<4
),
\lambda_{i}
are dimensionless coefficients and
M_{i}
are the mass scales which control when the deformation due to a particular operator becomes important. The explicit dependence on
g_{\mu\nu}(x)
indicates that we work on a generic curved background. The QFT in flat space-time is recovered by setting
g_{\mu\nu}(x)
to the flat metric (
8
).
The determinant of the metric is defined as follows

g\equiv\text{det}\ g_{\mu\nu}(x).

(19)

The action (
18
) is diff invariant by construction.

In curved background the stress-tensor is defined as

T^{\mu\nu}(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{-g}}\,\frac{\delta A(g,M_{i})}{\delta g_{\mu\nu}(x)}.

(20)

Under the Weyl transformation (
15
), the trace of the stress-tensor can be defined as a variation of the action with respect to the infinitesimal Weyl transformation parameter
\sigma
in the following way,

T^{\mu}_{\mu}(x)\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\,\frac{\delta_{W}A(g,M_{i})}{\delta\sigma(x)}.

(21)

Performing the Weyl transformation (
15
) in (
17
) and focusing on flat space-time we obtain the trace of the stress-tensor using the above definition, which reads

g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}:\qquad T^{\mu}{}_{\mu}(x)=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}(4-\Delta_{i})M_{i}^{4-\Delta_{i}}\mathcal{O}_{i}(x).

(22)

This is the standard result in QFT, namely the trace is proportional to the deforming operators.

The correlation functions of the stress-tensor both in the UV and IR are described by (
10
) and (
11
) where the coefficients
C_{T}
,
\mathbb{A}
,
\mathbb{B}
and
\mathbb{C}
have an additional label UV and IR respectively.
Out of all the above coefficients the
a
trace anomaly is the most interesting. In
Cardy:1988cwa
it was conjectured that

a^{\text{UV}}-a^{\text{IR}}\geq 0,

(23)

where the equality can hold only if there is no flow and the theory remains conformal, in other words if
A_{\text{deformation}}=0
in (
17
). The inequality (
23
) is known as the
a
-theorem. It was shown to hold in perturbation theory in
Osborn:1989td
;
Jack:1990eb
. It was proven non-perturbatively in
Komargodski:2011vj
, for further discussion see also
Komargodski:2011xv
;
Luty:2012ww
;
Komargodski:2015grt
. The proof of
Komargodski:2011vj
gives also the prescription on how to probe/compute the difference
(a^{\text{UV}}-a^{\text{IR}})
in a given QFT. One of the main ingredients of this proof is the compensator field and the associated particle which we call the dilaton. In the rest of this section we will define the compensator field and the dilaton particle.

Let us work with the action (
17
) on a curved background. It is diff invariant but not Weyl invariant. There are two sources which break the Weyl symmetry, namely the trace anomaly of the UV and IR CFTs given by (
16
) and the deformation part of the action
A_{\text{deformation}}
in (
17

The latter breaking can be compensated for in a modified theory with the following action

A^{\prime}(g,M_{i},\Omega)\equiv A(g,M_{i}\Omega(x))+A_{\text{dynamics}}(g,\Omega),

(24)

where
\Omega(x)
is a real scalar field called the compensator field and the action
A
was defined in (
17
). Both the metric
g_{\mu\nu}(x)
and the compensator field
\Omega(x)
are non-dynamical fields. We can however promote them to dynamical probe fields by adding a kinetic term
A_{\text{dynamics}}(g,\Omega)
. We will discuss possible convenient choices for this term in the end of this subsection. The compensator field
\Omega(x)
can be represented in the following two ways

\Omega(x)=e^{-\tau(x)}=1-\frac{\varphi(x)}{\sqrt{2}f}.

(25)

We refer to the real scalar fields
\tau(x)
and
\varphi(x)
also as the compensator or the dilaton fields interchangeably.
Here
f
is a new parameter with mass dimension one. The following relation holds

\tau(x)=\frac{\varphi(x)}{\sqrt{2}f}+O\left(\frac{1}{f^{2}}\right).

(26)

Let us now emphasize that the action (
24
) can be made invariant under the Weyl transformation (
15
)
given that the dilaton transforms in the following way

\tau(x)\rightarrow\tau(x)+\sigma(x)

(27)

and that the term
A_{\text{dynamics}}
is chosen appropriately.
The particle created by the compensator (or the dilaton) field
\varphi(x)
from the vacuum is called the dilaton particle. It will be denoted by
B
throughout this paper.

The simplest choice for
A_{\text{dynamics}}(g,\Omega)
, already used in
Komargodski:2011vj
, reads as

A_{\text{dynamics}}(g,\Omega)=\frac{1}{6}\,f^{2}\int d^{4}x\ \sqrt{-\widehat{g}}\,R(\widehat{g}),

(28)

where we have defined

\widehat{g}_{\mu\nu}\equiv e^{-2\tau}g_{\mu\nu}.

(29)

The action (
28
) is Weyl invariant at the classical level but not at the quantum level. One can simply see this for instance by taking the

28
) then reduces to the standard kinetic term describing free massless scalar. Free massless scalar gives a particular example of a CFT with trace anomalies

reported in (

2.3
a
-theorem

Let us now take the UV and IR limits of the action (
24
). They can be written as
7
7

7

Let us emphasize that we have made here a very non-trivial statement that the IR dilaton EFT action completely decouples from the IR CFT even though dilaton self interaction is present. One can argue for this at least in the limit of flat space-time: by construction (
24
), dilaton couples only to mass parameters, IR CFT instead does not have dimensionful parameters.

\displaystyle A^{\prime}_{\text{UV}}(g,\Omega)

\displaystyle\equiv A_{\text{UV CFT}}(g)+A_{\text{dynamics}}(g,\Omega),

(31)

\displaystyle A^{\prime}_{\text{IR}}(g,\Omega)

\displaystyle\equiv A_{\text{IR CFT}}(g)+A_{\text{dilaton EFT}}(g,\Omega)+A_{\text{dynamics}}(g,\Omega).

Here
A_{\text{dilaton EFT}}
is the effective field theory (EFT) action describing the dilaton interaction at low energy.
In order to obtain it in some explicit QFT model one needs to integrate out all the “massive” degrees of freedom throughout the RG flow which is almost impossible in practice. Luckily there is a model independent way to compute
A_{\text{dilaton EFT}}
which we will now review.

Consider the action (
24
). It breaks Weyl invariance in a very special way. The Weyl symmetry breaking is coming only from the UV and IR fixed points (
31
). Taking into account (
16
) we get

\displaystyle\delta_{W}A^{\prime}_{\text{UV}}(g,\Omega)

\displaystyle=\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\sigma(x)\,\Big(-a^{\text{UV}}\times E_{4}+c^{\text{UV}}\times W^{2}\Big)+\delta_{W}A_{\text{dynamics}}(g,\Omega),

(32)

\displaystyle\delta_{W}A^{\prime}_{\text{IR}}(g,\Omega)

\displaystyle=\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\sigma(x)\,\Big(-a^{\text{IR}}\times E_{4}+c^{\text{IR}}\times W^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle+\delta_{W}A_{\text{dilaton EFT}}(g,\Omega)+\delta_{W}A_{\text{dynamics}}(g,\Omega).

Here
\delta_{W}
stands for the infinitesimal Weyl variation.

Let us now assume that the Weyl anomaly in the UV matches the Weyl anomaly in the IR, in other words

\delta_{W}A^{\prime}_{\text{UV}}(g,\Omega)=\delta_{W}A^{\prime}_{\text{IR}}(g,\Omega).

(33)

Notice that contrary to the ’t Hooft anomaly matching, there is no proof for the Weyl anomaly matching (
33
) and it might not be true in general.
8
8

8

In fact the authors of
Niarchos:2020nxk
, see also
Andriolo:2022lcb
, found an apparent mismatch of the

-anomaly on the Higgs branch of

super-conformal field theory where conformal symmetry is spontaneously broken.
For further discussion on Weyl anomaly matching and its consequences see
karateev:2023
.
Plugging (
32
) in (
33
) we obtain the following variational equation

\delta_{W}A_{\text{dilaton EFT}}(g,\Omega)=\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\sigma(x)\,\Big(-\left(a^{\text{UV}}-a^{\text{IR}}\right)\times E_{4}+\left(c^{\text{UV}}-c^{\text{IR}}\right)\times W^{2}\Big).

(34)

The most general solution for this equation can be written in the following form

A_{\text{dilaton EFT}}(g,\Omega)=-(a^{\text{UV}}-a^{\text{IR}})\times A_{a}(g,\Omega)+(c^{\text{UV}}-c^{\text{IR}})\times A_{c}(g,\Omega)+\ A_{\text{invariant}}(g,\Omega),

(35)

where the two newly introduced terms
A_{a}(g,\Omega)
and
A_{c}(g,\Omega)
behave in the following way under the infinitesimal Weyl transformation

\delta_{W}A_{a}(g,\Omega)=\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\sigma(x)\,E_{4},\qquad\delta_{W}A_{c}(g,\Omega)=\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\sigma(x)\,W^{2}.

(36)

The term
A_{\text{invariant}}
instead remains completely invariant.
The solution to the above requirement was found in
Schwimmer:2010za
;
Komargodski:2011vj
, it reads

\displaystyle A_{a}(g,\Omega)

\displaystyle=\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\left(\tau E_{4}+4\left(R^{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}R\right)(\partial_{\mu}\tau)(\partial_{\nu}\tau)-4(\partial\tau)^{2}(\partial^{2}\tau)+2(\partial\tau)^{4}\right),

\displaystyle A_{c}(g,\Omega)

\displaystyle=\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\tau(x)\,W^{2}.

(37)

This solution is not easy to obtain but it is easy to check that it satisfies (
36
). It is also important to stress that even though (
33
) might not hold for every QFT, the weaker Wess-Zumino consistency condition exists, see
karateev:2023
, which implies that at the very least the first line in (
37
) always holds true.

The most general Weyl invariant action can be parametrized as follows

A_{\text{invariant}}(g,\Omega)=\int d^{4}x\ \sqrt{-\widehat{g}}\Bigg(M^{4}\lambda+M^{2}r_{0}\widehat{R}\ +\ r_{1}\widehat{R}^{2}+r_{2}\widehat{W}^{2}+r_{3}\widehat{E}_{4}+\ldots\Bigg).

(38)

Here the Ricci scalar, Weyl tensor and the Euler density are built out of the metric (
29
). The real dimensionless parameters
\lambda
,
r_{0}
,
r_{1}
,
r_{2}
and
r_{3}
depend on a particular QFT. The EFT cut-off scale
M
can be chosen to be the lowest deformation energy scale of the UV CFT. In spontaneously broken QFTs
\lambda=0
, but in generic QFTs
\lambda\neq 0
. However, when defining the action (
24
), if needed, one can fine tune the counterterms in such a way that
\lambda=0
.

In flat space the solution (
35
) together with (
37
) and (
38
) simply leads to

A_{\text{dilaton EFT}}(\varphi)=\frac{M^{4}\lambda}{4f^{4}}\int d^{4}x\big(\varphi(x))^{4}\ +\frac{6M^{2}r_{0}}{f^{2}}\int d^{4}x\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\varphi(x)\partial^{\mu}\varphi(x)\Big)\\
+36r_{1}\int d^{4}x\Big(\frac{1}{2f^{2}}+\frac{\varphi(x)}{\sqrt{2}f^{3}}+\frac{3\varphi(x)^{2}}{4f^{4}}\Big)(\partial^{2}\varphi(x))^{2}\\
+\frac{a^{\text{UV}}-a^{\text{IR}}}{2f^{4}}\times\int d^{4}x\ \big(\partial\varphi(x)\big)^{4}+\ O\big(f^{-5}\partial^{4}\varphi^{5}\ ,\ f^{-4}\partial^{6}\varphi^{4}\big).

(39)

The term proportional to
r_{0}
gives an
O(f^{-2})
correction to the dilaton kinetic term coming from
A_{\text{dynamics}}(g,\Omega)
. In the limit
f\rightarrow\infty
it should be neglected.
The interacting part of the dilaton scattering process
B(p_{1})B(p_{2})\rightarrow B(p_{3})B(p_{4})
at low energy is described by the effective action (
39
) and has the following form

\lim_{f\rightarrow\infty}f^{4}\mathcal{T}_{BB\rightarrow BB}(s,t,u)=6M^{4}\lambda+(a^{\text{UV}}-a^{\text{IR}})\times(s^{2}+t^{2}+u^{2})+O(s^{3}).

(40)

where
s=-(p_{1}+p_{2})^{2}\ ,\ t=-(p_{1}-p_{3})^{2}\ ,\ u=-(p_{1}-p_{4})^{2}
with
s+t+u=0
. Note, that the term proportional to
r_{1}
in (
39
) does not contribute to this scattering amplitude, since it vanishes under the dilaton equation of motion. Using the standard approach one can write the following dispersion relation in the
f\rightarrow\infty
limit

\displaystyle\displaystyle a^{\text{UV}}-a^{\text{IR}}\

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{f^{4}}{2}\ \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{0}\frac{ds}{s^{3}}\ \mathcal{T}_{BB\rightarrow BB}(s,0,-s)

(41)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{f^{4}}{\pi}\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{s^{3}}\ \text{Im}\mathcal{T}_{BB\rightarrow BB}(s,0,-s).

Since
\text{Im}\mathcal{T}_{BB\rightarrow BB}(s,0,-s)=s\ \sigma(s)\geq 0
where
\sigma(s)
is the total cross section for the scattering of
BB\rightarrow\text{anything}
,
(a^{\text{UV}}-a^{\text{IR}})
is non-negative. This proves the
a
-theorem.

Application in free scalar theory

As an application of the
a
-theorem consider the UV CFT which is generated by the free massless field
\Phi(x)
, namely we have the action

A_{\text{UV\;CFT}}=-\int d^{4}x\left(\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\Phi(x)\partial^{\mu}\Phi(x)\right).

(42)

It is straightforward to compute then the two- and tree-point correlation functions of the stress-tensor with itself. One obtains (
10
) and (
11
) with

C_{T}^{\text{UV}}=\frac{1}{3\pi^{4}},\qquad\mathbb{A}^{\text{UV}}=\frac{1}{27\pi^{6}}\ ,\qquad\mathbb{B}^{\text{UV}}=-\frac{4}{27\pi^{6}}\ ,\qquad\mathbb{C}^{\text{UV}}=-\frac{1}{27\pi^{6}}.

(43)

Let us now add the following deformation

A_{\text{deformation}}(m)=-\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\Phi(x)^{2},

(44)

where
m
becomes the mass of the field
\Phi
. This triggers the flow to an empty IR fixed point, thus in the deep IR we simply have

C_{T}^{\text{IR}}=0,\qquad\mathbb{A}^{\text{IR}}=0,\qquad\mathbb{B}^{\text{IR}}=0,\qquad\mathbb{C}^{\text{IR}}=0.

(45)

As a result according to (
13
) we get the following UV an IR a-anomaly

a^{\text{UV}}=\frac{1}{5760\pi^{2}},\qquad c^{\text{UV}}=\frac{1}{1920\pi^{2}},\qquad a^{\text{IR}}=0,\qquad c^{\text{IR}}=0.

(46)

Using the the modified action (
30
) we can also compute the dilaton scattering at low energies. We get

\lim_{f\rightarrow\infty}f^{4}\mathcal{T}_{BB\rightarrow BB}(s,t,u)=\frac{1}{5760\pi^{2}}\times(s^{2}+t^{2}+u^{2})+O(s^{3}).

(47)

The details of the computation are given in appendix
B
. The result (

) is in a perfect agreement with the
a
-theorem sum-rule (

) and (

).

3
Non-perturbative S-matrix bootstrap setup

In this section we explain our framework in details. We define scattering and partial amplitudes of particles
A
and
B
in section
3.1
. We write down all the crossing equations for the scattering amplitudes in section
3.2
. We construct unitarity conditions on the partial amplitudes in section
3.3
assuming that
B
is a generic massless particle. In section
3.4
we discuss further restrictions on the scattering amplitudes and modifications of the unitarity conditions in the case when
B
is the dilaton in the
f\rightarrow\infty
limit.

3.1
Scattering and partial amplitudes

Let us consider a QFT which contains two different scalar particles
A
and
B
with the masses

m_{A}=m,\qquad m_{B}=0.

(48)

Here and in the next two section we will not impose that
B
is the dilaton particle, it will be enough to treat it as a generic massless scalar particle. The requirement that
B
is the dilaton will be imposed only in section
3.4
. The particles
A
and
B
are described by the following asymptotic states

|A\rangle_{in}\equiv|m_{A},\vec{p}\,\rangle_{in},\quad|A\rangle_{out}\equiv|m_{A},\vec{p}\,\rangle_{out},\quad|B\rangle_{in}\equiv|m_{B},\vec{p}\,\rangle_{in},\quad|B\rangle_{out}\equiv|m_{B},\vec{p}\,\rangle_{out}.

(49)

For simplicity we assume the presence of the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry. We require that the particle of type
A
is odd and the particle of type
B
is even under this symmetry. In other words

\mathbb{Z}_{2}:\qquad|A\rangle_{in}\rightarrow-|A\rangle_{in},\quad|B\rangle_{in}\rightarrow+|B\rangle_{in}.

(50)

The same transformation properties hold for the out states.

In this work we will be interested in the following scattering amplitudes

AA\rightarrow AA,\qquad AA\rightarrow BB,\qquad AB\rightarrow AB,\qquad BB\rightarrow BB.

(51)

The presence of the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry required above forbids all the two to two scattering process with an odd number of particles of type
A
. We define the scattering amplitudes describing the processes (
51
) as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{AA\rightarrow AA}(s,t,u)\times(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{4}(p_{1}+p_{2}-p_{3}-p_{4})

\displaystyle\equiv{}_{out}\langle A_{3},A_{4}|A_{1},A_{2}\rangle_{in},

(52)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{AA\rightarrow BB}(s,t,u)\times(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{4}(p_{1}+p_{2}-p_{3}-p_{4})

\displaystyle\equiv{}_{out}\langle B_{3},B_{4}|A_{1},A_{2}\rangle_{in},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{AB\rightarrow AB}(s,t,u)\times(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{4}(p_{1}+p_{2}-p_{3}-p_{4})

\displaystyle\equiv{}_{out}\langle A_{3},B_{4}|A_{1},B_{2}\rangle_{in},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{BB\rightarrow BB}(s,t,u)\times(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{4}(p_{1}+p_{2}-p_{3}-p_{4})

\displaystyle\equiv{}_{out}\langle B_{3},B_{4}|B_{1},B_{2}\rangle_{in}.

Here we used the following short-hand notation for describing the two-particle in and out asymptotic states

\displaystyle|A_{1},A_{2}\rangle_{in}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Big(|m_{A},\vec{p}_{1}\,\rangle_{in}\otimes|m_{A},\vec{p}_{2}\,\rangle_{in}+|m_{A},\vec{p}_{2}\,\rangle_{in}\otimes|m_{A},\vec{p}_{1}\,\rangle_{in}\Big),

(53)

\displaystyle|A_{1},B_{2}\rangle_{in}

\displaystyle\equiv|m_{A},\vec{p}_{1}\,\rangle_{in}\otimes|m_{B},\vec{p}_{2}\,\rangle_{in},

\displaystyle|B_{1},B_{2}\rangle_{in}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Big(|m_{B},\vec{p}_{1}\,\rangle_{in}\otimes|m_{B},\vec{p}_{2}\,\rangle_{in}+|m_{B},\vec{p}_{2}\,\rangle_{in}\otimes|m_{B},\vec{p}_{1}\,\rangle_{in}\Big),

where
\otimes
denotes the ordered tensor product.
Notice that the two-particle states which consist of particles of type
A
or
B
only are totally symmetric. The factor
\sqrt{2}
is introduced to comply with the standard normalization conditions which will be provided below. The Mandelstam variables are defined as

s\equiv-(p_{1}+p_{2})^{2},\quad t\equiv-(p_{1}-p_{3})^{2},\quad u\equiv-(p_{1}-p_{4})^{2}.

(54)

As usual these are not linearly independent due to the following relations

s+t+u=m_{1}^{2}+m_{2}^{2}+m_{3}^{2}+m_{4}^{2},

(55)

where
m_{i}
are the masses of particles participating in the two to two scattering. This constraint is thus different for each process in (
52
). Let us write here this constraint precisely for each process separately

\displaystyle AA\rightarrow AA:

\displaystyle s+t+u=4m^{2},

(56)

\displaystyle AA\rightarrow BB:

\displaystyle s+t+u=2m^{2},

\displaystyle AB\rightarrow AB:

\displaystyle s+t+u=2m^{2},

\displaystyle BB\rightarrow BB:

\displaystyle s+t+u=0.

It is useful to write the four-momentum
p^{\mu}
in the spherical coordinates. One has

p^{\mu}=\{p^{0},\vec{p}\,\},\qquad\vec{p}=\{\boldsymbol{p}\cos\phi\sin\theta,\,\boldsymbol{p}\sin\phi\sin\theta,\,\boldsymbol{p}\cos\theta\},\qquad\boldsymbol{p}\equiv|\vec{p}\,|.

(57)

Let us discuss the normalization of states. The same normalizations holds for both in and out states. As a result below we write explicitly only the normalization of in states.
The one-particle states are normalized in the following way

\displaystyle{}_{in}\langle m_{A},\vec{p}_{1}|m_{A},\vec{p}_{2}\rangle_{in}

\displaystyle=2\,\sqrt{m^{2}+\boldsymbol{p}_{1}^{2}}\times(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\vec{p}_{1}-\vec{p}_{2}),

(58)

\displaystyle{}_{in}\langle m_{A},\vec{p}_{1}|m_{B},\vec{p}_{2}\rangle_{in}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle{}_{in}\langle m_{B},\vec{p}_{1}|m_{B},\vec{p}_{2}\rangle_{in}

\displaystyle=2\boldsymbol{p}_{1}\times(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\vec{p}_{1}-\vec{p}_{2}).

The normalization of two-particle states (
53
) follows immediately and read

\displaystyle{}_{in}\langle A_{3},A_{4}|A_{1},A_{2}\rangle_{in}

\displaystyle=4\,(2\pi)^{6}\,\sqrt{m^{2}+\boldsymbol{p}_{1}^{2}}\sqrt{m^{2}+\boldsymbol{p}_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\Big(\delta^{(3)}(\vec{p}_{1}-\vec{p}_{3})\delta^{(3)}(\vec{p}_{2}-\vec{p}_{4})+\delta^{(3)}(\vec{p}_{1}-\vec{p}_{4})\delta^{(3)}(\vec{p}_{2}-\vec{p}_{3})\Big),

\displaystyle{}_{in}\langle B_{3},B_{4}|A_{1},A_{2}\rangle_{in}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle{}_{in}\langle A_{3},B_{4}|A_{1},B_{2}\rangle_{in}

\displaystyle=4\,(2\pi)^{6}\,\boldsymbol{p}_{2}\,\sqrt{m^{2}+\boldsymbol{p}_{1}^{2}}\times\delta^{(3)}(\vec{p}_{1}-\vec{p}_{3})\delta^{(3)}(\vec{p}_{2}-\vec{p}_{4}),

(59)

\displaystyle{}_{in}\langle B_{3},B_{4}|B_{1},B_{2}\rangle_{in}

\displaystyle=4\,(2\pi)^{6}\,\boldsymbol{p}_{1}\boldsymbol{p}_{2}\times\Big(\delta^{(3)}(\vec{p}_{1}-\vec{p}_{3})\delta^{(3)}(\vec{p}_{2}-\vec{p}_{4})+\delta^{(3)}(\vec{p}_{1}-\vec{p}_{4})\delta^{(3)}(\vec{p}_{2}-\vec{p}_{3})\Big).

We can finally define the interacting part of the scattering amplitudes. This is done by subtracting the trivial normalization terms (which describe particles propagating without interacting), more precisely

\displaystyle i\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow AA}(s,t,u)\times(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{4}(p)

\displaystyle\equiv{}_{out}\langle A_{3},A_{4}|A_{1},A_{2}\rangle_{in}-{}_{in}\langle A_{3},A_{4}|A_{1},A_{2}\rangle_{in},

(60)

\displaystyle i\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow BB}(s,t,u)\times(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{4}(p)

\displaystyle\equiv{}_{out}\langle B_{3},B_{4}|A_{1},A_{2}\rangle_{in}-{}_{in}\langle B_{3},B_{4}|A_{1},A_{2}\rangle_{in},

\displaystyle i\mathcal{T}_{AB\rightarrow AB}(s,t,u)\times(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{4}(p)

\displaystyle\equiv{}_{out}\langle A_{3},B_{4}|A_{1},B_{2}\rangle_{in}-{}_{in}\langle A_{3},B_{4}|A_{1},B_{2}\rangle_{in},

\displaystyle i\mathcal{T}_{BB\rightarrow BB}(s,t,u)\times(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{4}(p)

\displaystyle\equiv{}_{out}\langle B_{3},B_{4}|B_{1},B_{2}\rangle_{in}-{}_{in}\langle B_{3},B_{4}|B_{1},B_{2}\rangle_{in},

where in order to make the formulas more compact we have introduced the following short-hand notation

\delta^{4}(p)\equiv\delta^{4}(p_{1}+p_{2}-p_{3}-p_{4}).

(61)

Notice the presence of the imaginary unit in the left-hand side which is introduced in order to match the standard conventions.

3.2
Crossing equations

The crossing equations for the process
AA\rightarrow AA
and
BB\rightarrow BB
are extremely simple. They require that the associated amplitudes are fully symmetric under any permutations of the Mandelstam variables, namely

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow AA}(s,t,u)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow AA}(t,s,u)\,=\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow AA}(u,t,s),

(62)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{BB\rightarrow BB}(s,t,u)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{BB\rightarrow BB}(t,s,u)=\mathcal{T}_{BB\rightarrow BB}(u,t,s).

(63)

Things are more complicated for the processes
AA\rightarrow BB
and
AB\rightarrow AB
. They are related by crossing. Recall that the exchange of particles
14
and
23
lead to the s-t crossing equations, instead the exchange of particles
13
and
24
lead to the s-u crossing equations. In other words

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{AB\rightarrow AB}(s,t,u)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow BB}(t,s,u)=\mathcal{T}_{BB\rightarrow AA}(t,s,u),

(64)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{AB\rightarrow AB}(s,t,u)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{AB\rightarrow AB}(u,t,s).

(65)

From the relation (
64
) it follows immediately that

\mathcal{T}_{BB\rightarrow AA}(s,t,u)=\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow BB}(s,t,u).

(66)

3.3
Unitarity

The two-particle states (
53
) do not transform in the irreducible representation of the Little group
SO(3)
. We can however decompose them into irreducible representations, see for example
Karateev:2019ymz
;
Hebbar:2020ukp
.

To begin with let us evaluate the states (
53
) in the center of mass frame, namely when the directions of particles are aligned and opposite to each other

\vec{p}_{1}=+\vec{p},\qquad\vec{p}_{2}=-\vec{p}.

(67)

In spherical coordinates the momentum
\vec{p}
reads as

+\vec{p}=(\boldsymbol{p},\,\theta,\,\phi),\quad-\vec{p}=(\boldsymbol{p},\,\pi-\theta,\,\pi+\phi),\quad\theta\in[0,\pi],\quad\phi\in[0,2\pi].

(68)

Here
\boldsymbol{p}\equiv|\vec{p}\,|
.
We denote the states (
53
) in this particular frame by

|A_{1},A_{2}\rangle_{in}^{com},\qquad|A_{1},B_{2}\rangle_{in}^{com},\qquad|B_{1},B_{2}\rangle_{in}^{com}.

(69)

These states depend on the masses
m_{A}
,
m_{B}
and the spherical coordinates
\boldsymbol{p}
,
\theta
and
\phi
.
The same notation holds for the out states.
According to equations (2.29) and (2.30) in
Hebbar:2020ukp
we can write

\displaystyle|A_{1},A_{2}\rangle_{in}^{com}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\sum_{\ell,\lambda}C_{\ell}^{AA}(\boldsymbol{p})e^{-i\lambda\phi}d^{(\ell)}_{\lambda 0}(\theta)|\sqrt{s},\vec{0};\ell,\lambda\rangle_{in}^{AA},

(70)

\displaystyle|A_{1},B_{2}\rangle_{in}^{com}

\displaystyle=\quad\;\,\sum_{\ell,\lambda}C_{\ell}^{AB}(\boldsymbol{p})\,e^{-i\lambda\phi}d^{(\ell)}_{\lambda 0}(\theta)|\sqrt{s},\vec{0};\ell,\lambda\rangle_{in}^{AB},

\displaystyle|B_{1},B_{2}\rangle_{in}^{com}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\sum_{\ell,\lambda}C_{\ell}^{BB}(\boldsymbol{p})e^{-i\lambda\phi}d^{(\ell)}_{\lambda 0}(\theta)|\sqrt{s},\vec{0};\ell,\lambda\rangle_{in}^{BB},

where
d_{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}^{(\ell)}(\theta)
is the small Wigner d matrices and
|\sqrt{s},\vec{0};\ell,\lambda\rangle
are states in the irreducible representation of the
SO(3)
Little group.
Here we have also introduced the object
C_{\ell}
which is defined as

\displaystyle C_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{p})^{2}\equiv 4\pi\,(2\ell+1)\times\frac{\sqrt{s}}{\boldsymbol{p}},

(71)

where
\sqrt{s}
is the total energy of the two-particle state which is

\displaystyle AA:\quad\sqrt{s}

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{m^{2}+\boldsymbol{p}^{2}},

(72)

\displaystyle AB:\quad\sqrt{s}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{m^{2}+\boldsymbol{p}^{2}}+\boldsymbol{p},

\displaystyle BB:\quad\sqrt{s}

\displaystyle=2\boldsymbol{p}.

The small Wigner
d
-matrices are defined for example in equation (2.9) of
Hebbar:2020ukp
.
The states in the right-hand side of (
70
) transform in the irreducible representation of the Lorentz group. They have the Little group spin
\ell
and helicity
\lambda=-\ell,\ldots,+\ell
. They also have the zero total spatial momentum
\vec{0}
. Their normalization is completely fixed by (
70
) and (
59
).

By using the orthogonality of the small Wigner d-matrices we can invert the decompositions (
70
) and write

\displaystyle|\sqrt{s},\vec{0};\ell,\lambda\rangle_{in}^{AA}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\ell}^{AA}|A_{1},A_{2}\rangle_{in}^{com},

(73)

\displaystyle|\sqrt{s},\vec{0};\ell,\lambda\rangle_{in}^{AB}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\ell}^{AB}|A_{1},B_{2}\rangle_{in}^{com},

\displaystyle|\sqrt{s},\vec{0};\ell,\lambda\rangle_{in}^{BB}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\ell}^{BB}|B_{1},B_{2}\rangle_{in}^{com},

where
\Pi_{\ell}
are the projectors to the definite Little group spin. Their explicit form reads

\displaystyle\Pi_{\ell}^{AA}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi\sqrt{2}C^{AA}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{p})}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\,e^{i\lambda\phi}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta\;d^{(\ell)}_{\lambda 0}(\theta),

(74)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\ell}^{AB}

\displaystyle\equiv\;\;\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi C^{AB}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{p})}\;\;\;\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\,e^{i\lambda\phi}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta\;d^{(\ell)}_{\lambda 0}(\theta),

\displaystyle\Pi_{\ell}^{BB}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi\sqrt{2}C^{BB}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{p})}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\,e^{i\lambda\phi}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta\;d^{(\ell)}_{\lambda 0}(\theta).

Let us define the partial amplitudes
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(s)
as the following matrix elements

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{AA\rightarrow AA}^{\ell}(s)\times\delta_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}\delta_{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda}(2\pi)^{4}\delta(\sqrt{s}^{\prime}-\sqrt{s})\delta^{3}(\vec{0})

\displaystyle\equiv{}_{out}^{AA}\langle\sqrt{s}^{\prime},\vec{0};\ell^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime}|\sqrt{s},\vec{0};\ell,\lambda\rangle_{in}^{AA},

(75)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{AA\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)\times\delta_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}\delta_{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda}(2\pi)^{4}\delta(\sqrt{s}^{\prime}-\sqrt{s})\delta^{3}(\vec{0})

\displaystyle\equiv{}_{out}^{BB}\langle\sqrt{s}^{\prime},\vec{0};\ell^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime}|\sqrt{s},\vec{0};\ell,\lambda\rangle_{in}^{AA},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{AB\rightarrow AB}^{\ell}(s)\times\delta_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}\delta_{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda}(2\pi)^{4}\delta(\sqrt{s}^{\prime}-\sqrt{s})\delta^{3}(\vec{0})

\displaystyle\equiv{}_{out}^{AB}\langle\sqrt{s}^{\prime},\vec{0};\ell^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime}|\sqrt{s},\vec{0};\ell,\lambda\rangle_{in}^{AB},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{BB\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)\times\delta_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}\delta_{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda}(2\pi)^{4}\delta(\sqrt{s}^{\prime}-\sqrt{s})\delta^{3}(\vec{0})

\displaystyle\equiv{}_{out}^{BB}\langle\sqrt{s}^{\prime},\vec{0};\ell^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime}|\sqrt{s},\vec{0};\ell,\lambda\rangle_{in}^{BB}.

Using (
73
) and (
74
) we obtain the explicit relations between the partial amplitudes and the scattering amplitudes. The detailed steps in this derivation are explained in section 2.5 in
Hebbar:2020ukp
. They read

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{AA\rightarrow AA}^{\ell}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{32\pi}\left(1-4m^{2}/s\right)^{1/2}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta P_{\ell}(\cos\theta)\mathcal{S}_{AA\rightarrow AA}(s,t,u),

(76)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{AA\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{32\pi}\left(1-4m^{2}/s\right)^{1/4}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta P_{\ell}(\cos\theta)\mathcal{S}_{AA\rightarrow BB}(s,t,u),

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{AB\rightarrow AB}^{\ell}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{16\pi}(1-m^{2}/s)\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta P_{\ell}(\cos\theta)\mathcal{S}_{AB\rightarrow AB}(s,t,u),

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{BB\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{32\pi}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta P_{\ell}(\cos\theta)\mathcal{S}_{BB\rightarrow BB}(s,t,u),

where
P_{\ell}(\cos\theta)
are the Legendre polynomials. They are related to the small Wigner d matrices as
P_{\ell}(\cos\theta)=d_{00}^{(\ell)}(\theta)
.
To complete these equations it is important to specify the relation between the Mandelstam variables and the scattering angle for each process. One has

\displaystyle AA\rightarrow AA:

\displaystyle\quad t=\frac{1}{2}(4m^{2}-s)(1-\cos\theta),\quad u=\frac{1}{2}(4m^{2}-s)(1+\cos\theta),

\displaystyle AA\rightarrow BB:

\displaystyle\quad t=m^{2}-\frac{s}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{s(s-4m^{2})}\cos\theta,\quad u=m^{2}-\frac{s}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{s(s-4m^{2})}\cos\theta,

\displaystyle AB\rightarrow AB:

\displaystyle\quad t=\frac{(s-m^{2})^{2}(\cos\theta-1)}{2s},\quad u=\frac{2m^{4}-(s-m^{2})^{2}(\cos\theta+1)}{2s},

\displaystyle BB\rightarrow BB:

\displaystyle\quad t=-\frac{s}{2}\,(1-\cos\theta),\quad u=-\frac{s}{2}\,(1+\cos\theta).

(77)

In (
76
) we found the relations between the partial amplitudes and the full scattering amplitudes. We can further rewrite them by splitting the scattering amplitudes into their trivial and their interacting parts given by (
59
) and (
60
). Performing the explicit integral over the trivial part we obtain the final expressions

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{AA\rightarrow AA}^{\ell}(s)

\displaystyle=1+i\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow AA}^{\ell}(s),

(78)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{AA\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)

\displaystyle=0+i\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s),

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{AB\rightarrow AB}^{\ell}(s)

\displaystyle=1+i\mathcal{T}_{AB\rightarrow AB}^{\ell}(s),

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{BB\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)

\displaystyle=1+i\mathcal{T}_{BB\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s),

where we have defined

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow AA}^{\ell}(s)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{32\pi}\left(1-4m^{2}/s\right)^{1/2}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta P_{\ell}(\cos\theta)\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow AA}(s,t,u),

(79)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{32\pi}\left(1-4m^{2}/s\right)^{1/4}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta P_{\ell}(\cos\theta)\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow BB}(s,t,u),

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{AB\rightarrow AB}^{\ell}(s)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{16\pi}(1-m^{2}/s)\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta P_{\ell}(\cos\theta)\mathcal{T}_{AB\rightarrow AB}(s,t,u),

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{BB\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{32\pi}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta P_{\ell}(\cos\theta)\mathcal{T}_{BB\rightarrow BB}(s,t,u),

We are now in position to address the unitarity constraints. Let us first consider the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
odd two-particle in and out states projected to the definite spin, namely

|\sqrt{s},\vec{0};\ell,\lambda\rangle_{in}^{AB},\qquad|\sqrt{s},\vec{0};\ell,\lambda\rangle_{out}^{AB}.

(80)

We take all possible inner products of these states. Unitarity requires that such a matrix is semipositive definite. Using the definition (
75
) we conclude that

\forall\ell\geq 0,\quad\forall s\in[m^{2},\infty):\qquad\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathcal{S}_{AB\rightarrow AB}^{*\ell}(s)\\
\mathcal{S}_{AB\rightarrow AB}^{\ell}(s)&1\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0.

(81)

Let us consider now the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
even two-particles in and out states projected to the definite spin, namely

|\sqrt{s},\vec{0};\ell,\lambda\rangle_{in}^{AA},\qquad|\sqrt{s},\vec{0};\ell,\lambda\rangle_{in}^{BB},\qquad|\sqrt{s},\vec{0};\ell,\lambda\rangle_{out}^{AA},\qquad|\sqrt{s},\vec{0};\ell,\lambda\rangle_{out}^{BB}.

(82)

Analogously we obtain the following unitarity constraint

\begin{aligned} \forall\ell=0,2,4,\ldots\\
\forall s\in[4m^{2},\infty)\end{aligned}:\qquad\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\mathcal{S}_{AA\rightarrow AA}^{*\ell}(s)&\mathcal{S}_{AA\rightarrow BB}^{*\ell}(s)\\
0&1&\mathcal{S}_{BB\rightarrow AA}^{*\ell}(s)&\mathcal{S}_{BB\rightarrow BB}^{*\ell}(s)\\
\mathcal{S}_{AA\rightarrow AA}^{\ell}(s)&\mathcal{S}_{BB\rightarrow AA}^{\ell}(s)&1&0\\
\mathcal{S}_{AA\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)&\mathcal{S}_{BB\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)&0&1\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0.

(83)

For the energy range
s\in[0,4m^{2})
the two particle state
AA
, do not exist, however the two particle state
BB
do. Considering the states

|\sqrt{s},\vec{0};\ell,\lambda\rangle_{in}^{BB},\qquad|\sqrt{s},\vec{0};\ell,\lambda\rangle_{out}^{BB},

(84)

we conclude that the following unitarity constraint also holds

\forall\ell=0,2,4,\ldots,\quad\forall s\in[0,4m^{2}):\qquad\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathcal{S}_{BB\rightarrow BB}^{*\ell}(s)\\
\mathcal{S}_{BB\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)&1\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0.

(85)

3.4
Dilaton scattering

So far the particle
B
was simply a generic massless scalar particle. We would like now to identify it with the dilaton. First, let us define rescaled amplitudes that remain finite in the probe limit
f\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{AA\rightarrow BB}(s,t,u)=\lim_{f\to\infty}f^{2}\,\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow BB}(s,t,u)\,,

(86)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{AB\rightarrow AB}(s,t,u)=\lim_{f\to\infty}f^{2}\,\mathcal{T}_{AB\rightarrow AB}(s,t,u)\,,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{BB\rightarrow BB}(s,t,u)=\lim_{f\to\infty}f^{4}\,\mathcal{T}_{BB\rightarrow BB}(s,t,u)\,.

The main result of
Komargodski:2011vj
which we use in this paper is the relation between the
a
-anomaly in the UV with the dilaton field scattering. More precisely

\lim_{f\rightarrow\infty}f^{4}\mathcal{T}_{BB\rightarrow BB}(s,t,u)=a^{\text{UV}}\times(s^{2}+t^{2}+u^{2})+O(s^{3}).

(87)

The limit
f\rightarrow\infty
corresponds to the full decoupling of the dilaton from the system. Nevertheless it still carries some non-trivial information about the system.

The goal of this section is to formulate the bootstrap setup in terms of amplitudes (
86
) and take explicitly the limit
f\rightarrow\infty
.

Analyticity and soft behavior

We start by noticing that dilatons in the limit
f\rightarrow\infty
cannot contribute to the scattering amplitudes as intermediate states. In practice this means that in all the scattering amplitudes there are no branch cuts
s\in[0,\infty]
due to
n
-dilaton particle states, where
n=1,2,3,4,\ldots
. In other words

s\in[4m^{2},\infty]:\qquad\begin{aligned} &\text{disc}_{s}\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow AA}(s,t,u)\neq 0,\\
&\text{disc}_{s}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{AA\rightarrow BB}(s,t,u)\neq 0,\\
&\text{disc}_{s}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{BB\rightarrow BB}(s,t,u)\neq 0,\end{aligned}

(88)

where the non-zero discontinuity is due to the
n
-particle states of type A, where
n\geq 2
is only allowed to be even due to the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry. Analogously we conclude that

s\in[9m^{2},\infty]:\qquad\text{disc}_{s}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{AB\rightarrow AB}(s,t,u)\neq 0,

(89)

where the discontinuity appears due to the
n
-particle states of type A, where
n\geq 3
is only allowed to be odd due to the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry. It is important also to discuss the contribution of the
n=1
particle state of type A to the scattering amplitudes describing the processes
AB\rightarrow AB
and
AA\rightarrow BB
. It appears as an s-channel pole in the amplitude, namely

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{AB\rightarrow AB}(s,t,u)

\displaystyle=-\frac{h^{2}}{s-m^{2}}+\ldots,

(90)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{AA\rightarrow BB}(s,t,u)

\displaystyle=\qquad 0\quad\;\,+\ldots,

where
h
is the real number which describes the interaction strength between the particle
A
and the dilaton.
The second amplitude in (
90
) does not have an s-channel pole due to the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry.
Imposing the crossing symmetry given by equations (
64
) and (
65
) we conclude that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{AB\rightarrow AB}(s,t,u)

\displaystyle=-\frac{h^{2}}{s-m^{2}}-\frac{h^{2}}{u-m^{2}}+g(s,t,u)

(91)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{AA\rightarrow BB}(s,t,u)

\displaystyle=-\frac{h^{2}}{t-m^{2}}-\frac{h^{2}}{u-m^{2}}+g(t,s,u),

where the function
g
describes the finite part of the amplitudes.
No poles in the other amplitudes are allowed either due to the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry or due to the limit
f\rightarrow\infty
. In appendix
C
we give a non-perturbative argument that the residue
h
has the following value

h^{2}=2m^{4}.

(92)

In the next section we will obtain a stronger result which not only fixes the residue of the poles but also the constant piece of the
AB\rightarrow AB
and
AA\rightarrow BB
amplitudes in the soft limit. The result reads

\displaystyle\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{AA\rightarrow BB}(s,t,u)\ =-\frac{2m^{4}}{t-m^{2}}-\frac{2m^{4}}{u-m^{2}}-m^{2}+\ O(u-m^{2},t-m^{2}).

(93)

Unitarity constraints

The final step is to write the unitarity constraints (
81
), (
83
) and (
85
) in the limit
f\rightarrow\infty
. Let us start with the constraint (
81
). We use the relations (
86
), compute the matrix eigenvalues and expand them in the inverse-powers of
f
to the sub-leading order. Semipositive definiteness requires that all the eigenvalues are non-negative. Explicitly we get

\displaystyle 0+f^{-2}\text{Im}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}^{\ell}_{AB\rightarrow AB}(s)+O(f^{-4})\geq 0,

(94)

\displaystyle 2-f^{-2}\text{Im}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}^{\ell}_{AB\rightarrow AB}(s)+O(f^{-4})\geq 0.

In the limit
f\rightarrow\infty
the second condition is satisfied automatically since the second term gives a negligible contribution. The leading term in the first condition vanishes however and we are forced to study the sub-leading contribution. Clearly in the limit
f\rightarrow\infty
the condition (
81
) simply reduces to

\forall\ell\geq 0,\quad\forall s\in[9m^{2},\infty):\qquad\text{Im}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}^{\ell}_{AB\rightarrow AB}(s)\geq 0.

(95)

Here we have also used (
89
).
Analogous reasoning holds for the condition (
85
). However due to the conditions (
88
) the imaginary part of
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}^{\ell}_{BB\rightarrow BB}(s)
vanishes in the interval
s\in[0,4m^{2}]
. As a consequence the condition (
85
) is trivially satisfied.

The best way to analyse the condition (
83
) is to cast it into a different form where the limit
f\rightarrow\infty
is obvious. Most of the manipulations bellow were already presented in appendix B in
Homrich:2019cbt
. First, due to (
78
) we can write (
83
) as

\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}&\mathbb{I}-i\mathbb{T}^{\dagger}(s)\\
\mathbb{I}+i\mathbb{T}(s)&\mathbb{I}\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0,

(96)

where we have defined

\mathbb{I}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbb{T}(s)\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow AA}^{\ell}(s)&\mathcal{T}_{BB\rightarrow AA}^{\ell}(s)\\
\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)&\mathcal{T}_{BB\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)\end{pmatrix}.

(97)

Second, we notice that the condition (
96
) is equivalent to

2\text{Im}\mathbb{T}-\mathbb{T}^{\dagger}(s)\mathbb{T}(s)\succeq 0.

(98)

Third, we rewrite this condition in another equivalent form

\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}&\mathbb{T}^{\dagger}(s)\\
\mathbb{T}(s)&2\text{Im}\mathbb{T}\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0.

(99)

The matrix appearing in the left-hand side of (
99
) is 4x4. Let us write it explicitly using (
97
) and (
86
). We get

\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow AA}^{*\ell}(s)&\frac{1}{f^{2}}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{AA\rightarrow BB}^{*\ell}(s)\\
0&1&\frac{1}{f^{2}}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{BB\rightarrow AA}^{*\ell}(s)&\frac{1}{f^{4}}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{BB\rightarrow BB}^{*\ell}(s)\\
\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow AA}^{\ell}(s)&\frac{1}{f^{2}}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{BB\rightarrow AA}^{\ell}(s)&2\text{Im}\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow AA}^{\ell}(s)&\frac{2}{f^{2}}\text{Im}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{BB\rightarrow AA}^{\ell}(s)\\
\frac{1}{f^{2}}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{AA\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)&\frac{1}{f^{4}}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{BB\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)&\frac{2}{f^{2}}\text{Im}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{AA\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)&\frac{2}{f^{4}}\text{Im}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{BB\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0.

(100)

We notice that multiplying the last row and subsequently the last column of the matrix in the left-hand side of (
100
) by
f^{2}
does not change its semidefinite positive property.
9
9

9

This can be proven in several different ways. For instance one can multiply the condition (
100
) by the diagonal matrix
\{1,1,1,f^{2}\}
to the left and to the right. Alternatively one can use the Sylvester’s criterion. Considering all the principal minors of the original matrix and the one with the last row and column multiplied by
f^{2}
we see that their non-negativity conditions are equivalent as long as
f^{2}>0
.

Taking the limit
f\rightarrow\infty
we obtain the unitarity condition (
83
) in its final form

\begin{aligned} \forall\ell=0,2,4,\ldots\\
\forall s\in[4m^{2},\infty)\end{aligned}:\qquad\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow AA}^{*\ell}(s)&\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{AA\rightarrow BB}^{*\ell}(s)\\
\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow AA}^{\ell}(s)&2\text{Im}\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow AA}^{\ell}(s)&2\text{Im}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{BB\rightarrow AA}^{\ell}(s)\\
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{AA\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)&2\text{Im}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{AA\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)&2\text{Im}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{BB\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0.

(101)

Notice that in going from (
100
) to (
101
) after taking the limit
f\rightarrow\infty
we dropped the second row and the second column which were simply
(0,1,0,0)
, did not affect the semidefinite positiveness.

Summarizing, the unitarity conditions which must be satisfied for the system of a particle and the dilaton are (
95
) and (
101
). We can use the Sylvester’s criterion in order to rewrite the latter condition as a set of inequalities. As a result the conditions (
95
) and (
101
) can be written as

\displaystyle\text{Im}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}^{\ell}_{AB\rightarrow AB}(s)\geq 0,

\displaystyle 2\text{Im}\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow AA}^{\ell}(s)\geq\left|\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow AA}^{\ell}(s)\right|^{2}\geq 0,\qquad 2\text{Im}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{BB\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)\geq\left|\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{AA\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)\right|^{2}\geq 0,

(102)

\displaystyle\text{Im}\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow AA}^{\ell}(s)\times\text{Im}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{BB\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)\geq\left(\text{Im}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{AA\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)\right)^{2}

together with

2\text{Im}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{BB\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)\times\left(2\text{Im}\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow AA}^{\ell}(s)-\left|\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow AA}^{\ell}(s)\right|^{2}\right)+\\
2\text{Im}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{AA\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)\times\left(\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow AA}^{\ell}(s)\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{AA\rightarrow BB}^{*\ell}(s)+\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow AA}^{*\ell}(s)\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{AA\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)\right)\geq\\
2\text{Im}\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow AA}^{\ell}(s)\times\left|\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{AA\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)\right|^{2}+4\left(\text{Im}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{AA\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)\right)^{2}.

(103)

The last condition comes from the determinant of the matrix (
95
).

Sum-rules for the

-anomaly

Equivalently using (

In

4.1
The most general effective action

The assumption of this paper is the presence of a single
\mathbb{Z}_{2}

\Phi(x)

We would like to find the most general low energy effective action

10
10

10

\varphi(x)

Following the discussion of section

\widehat{\Phi}(x)

Let us start from the tangent space with locally flat metric
\eta_{ab}

\Phi

11

11

\displaystyle K(\partial_{a})\rightarrow\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}^{a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{n}}E_{a_{1}}^{\mu_{1}}E_{a_{2}}^{\mu_{2}}\cdots E_{a_{n}}^{\mu_{n}}D_{\mu_{1}}D_{\mu_{2}}\cdots D_{\mu_{n}}\ \equiv F.

\Phi(x)\rightarrow\widehat{\Phi}(x)

5.2.2
Consistency checks

Before presenting more bounds let us perform several consistency checks.

First, we can set all the coefficients
\alpha
and
\beta
in the ansatz (
145
) to zero after imposing the first condition in (
151
). This situation corresponds to particle
A
being a free massive scalar, with the
AA\rightarrow BB
scattering amplitude given by (
108
). As explained below (
108
) this inevitably leads to
a/a_{\text{free}}=1
. We successfully reproduce this theoretical outcome numerically.
It is important to notice that the ansatz (
145
) requires very large numbers
N_{max}
in order to reproduce the free theory accurately. In practice it is very non-economical to work with such a big value of
N_{max}
. This was the motivation behind the introduction of the additional term (
154
) in the
BB\rightarrow BB
ansatz which improves the convergence with
N_{max}
drastically in this particular situation.

Second, let us set all the
\alpha
coefficients to zero and leave
\beta
coefficients to be completely free. One might expect the same outcome as before, however solving the optimization problem we obtain

a/a_{\text{free}}\approx 0.9071.

(161)

This result was obtained with
N_{max}=L_{max}=20
. For comparison we get
0.9107
for
N_{max}=L_{max}=10
.
This result is very stable under the change of
N_{max}
and
L_{max}
. The solution of the optimization problem gives the coefficients of the ansatz such that only

\beta_{00n}\neq 0.

(162)

Under closer inspection of the unitarity conditions one observes that setting
\alpha
to zero forces the imaginary part of the
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{AA\rightarrow BB}^{\ell}(s)
partial amplitude to be zero due to the last line in (
102
). However, the real part can still be non-zero. The solution (
162
) exactly reproduces such a situation. We, thus, conclude that the unitarity conditions we use do not fully constraint the behavior of the matter - dilaton scattering given the form of the matter scattering.

It is possible to guess analytically the
AA\rightarrow BB
scattering amplitude which gives the solution (
161
), (
162
). Let us assume no discontinuity in the s-channel of the amplitude
AA\rightarrow BB
(as follows from absence of
AA\to AA
scattering), we can then write the general ansatz

\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{AA\rightarrow BB}=-m^{2}-\frac{2m^{4}}{t-m^{2}}-\frac{2m^{4}}{u-m^{2}}-\int_{9m^{2}}^{\infty}dwq(w)\left(\frac{m^{2}}{t-w}+\frac{m^{2}}{u-w}-\frac{2m^{2}}{m^{2}-w}\right),

(163)

where

by unitarity. For the special case when
q(w)=Qm^{2}\delta(w-9m^{2})
we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{AA\rightarrow BB}=-m^{2}-\frac{2m^{4}}{t-m^{2}}-\frac{2m^{4}}{u-m^{2}}-Qm^{2}\left(\frac{m^{2}}{t-9m^{2}}+\frac{m^{2}}{u-9m^{2}}+\frac{1}{4}\right).

Plugging it into (
107

for

. The amplitude (

Finally, analogously to (
104

\Lambda_{2}=\frac{m^{4}}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{4m^{2}}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{s^{3}}\ \text{Im}\mathcal{T}_{AA\rightarrow AA}(s,0,4m^{2}-s).

(165)

Setting

process to be zero since the integrand is non-zero due to the first inequality in the second line in (

6
Discussion

We proposed a framework to study gapped QFTs defined as relevant deformations of an UV CFT. For simplicity we assumed the existence of a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry and a single stable
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-odd particle
at low energies denoted by
A
. By studying the unitarity constraints on the system of scattering amplitudes between the particle
A
and the dilaton particle
B
, as depicted in figure
1
, we related the physical scattering data for the particle
A
to the
a
-anomaly of the UV CFT.
This allowed us to derive lower bounds on the
a
-anomaly as a function of several parameters defined in (
3
) and (
4
) in terms of the amplitude
\mathcal{T}_{AA\to AA}
(see figures
2
and
3
).

In practice, we used the primal numerical S-matrix bootstrap method for multiple amplitudes.
14
14

14

For an example of the primal numerical setup with multiple amplitudes in 2d see
Homrich:2019cbt
.
This method requires extrapolation in the number of parameters of the ansatz (
N_{max}\to\infty
) and the number of unitarity constraints (
L_{max}\to\infty
).
As usual in S-matrix bootstrap problems with a mass gap, the extrapolation
L_{max}\to\infty
is easy. On the other hand, the extrapolation
N_{max}\to\infty
is more subtle and deserves further study. In this context, it would be extremely useful to have a dual S-matrix bootstrap method to approach the minimal
a
-anomaly from below. It should be possible to generalize the methods of
Lopez:1976zs
;
He:2021eqn
;
Guerrieri:2021tak
to our setup.

Our main result is the derivation of a universal lower bound on the
a
-anomaly. We estimated the bound to be
a/a_{\text{free}}>0.3
.
Combining this result with the conformal collider bound
\frac{31}{18}\geq\frac{a}{c}\geq\frac{1}{3}
from
Hofman:2008ar
, we obtain a lower bound
c>0.17\ a_{\text{free}}
.

It would be interesting to generalize our analysis by dropping some of the simplifying assumptions about the presence of the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry and the low energy spectrum of particles. For example, we can start by allowing more stable particles either even or odd under
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. More stable particles lead to extra poles in the ansatz (
145
) and change the branch point in
{\mathfrak{r}}_{2}
associated with the beginning of the continuum in the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
even sector (in practice
9m^{2}\to(m+m^{\prime})^{2}
where
m^{\prime}
is the mass of the lightest
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
even particle). Dropping the assumption of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry leads to similar effects: now particle
A
gives rise to a pole in every channel of every amplitude and all cuts start at
4m^{2}
. More ambitiously, we could include in our setup also the processes
\mathcal{T}_{AA\to AB}
and
\mathcal{T}_{AB\to BB}
. This would give rise to a stronger set of unitarity constraints.
Any of these more general scenarios must give rise to a weaker lower bound than
a/a_{\text{free}}>0.3
.
15
15

15

It may be possible to derive a stronger lower bound for QFTs with more than one stable particle by considering
2\to 2
scattering amplitudes with other external particles besides the dilaton
B
and the lightest particle
A
. This is similar to the discussion below about promoting particle
A
to a vector multiplet of
O(3)
.
This is intriguing because we do not know of any 4d QFT with
a<a_{\text{free}}
. On the one hand, one may interpret our results as pointing to the existence of an unknown QFT with
a<a_{\text{free}}
. On the other hand, it is also possible that our setup is just not constraining enough to approach the true minimal value of
a
in the space of QFTs.

Another interesting generalization would be to study QFTs with a continuous global symmetry.
In practice, this is achieved by promoting particle
A
to an irreducible representation of the global symmetry group. For example, we could study pions as a triplet of
O(3)
.
Building on
Guerrieri:2018uew
, we could impose the known value of the
a
-anomaly in QCD and study several observables like scattering lengths, chiral zeros and resonances.
Alternatively, we could inject some experimental data about
\pi-\pi
scattering and then minimize the
a
-anomaly.

It would also be interesting to apply our approach to QFTs with fermionic asymptotic states. For example, we can ask the question: what is the lowest
a_{UV}
that can give rise to massive fermions in the IR?
16
16

16

We thank H. Osborn for suggesting this question.

In practice, this would require a combination of the techniques of
Hebbar:2020ukp
with the present paper.

Our setup can easily accommodate a massless physical particle described by some Effective Field Theory (EFT) at low energies.
This would have several interesting applications. The simplest one is for particle
A
to be a Goldstone boson of spontaneous symmetry breaking. For example, particle
A
could describe massless pions like in
Guerrieri:2020bto
.
More challenging, would be to take particle
A
to be a photon. This would allows to ask the question: what is the minimum
a
-anomaly of the UV CFT that can give rise to a photon in the IR?
Our method creates a non-perturbative bridge between low-energy EFT and their UV completions (within the realm of QFT). Unfortunately, such applications will be very challenging numerically for the current methods. The main challenge is the extrapolation
L_{max}\to\infty
in gapless theories (see for instance
Guerrieri:2021ivu
).

In this paper, the dilaton particle
B
was an external probe. However, one can also study physical dilatons, which are the Goldstone bosons from spontaneous conformal symmetry breaking. In this case, the dilaton decay constant
f
would be finite and generically of order
m
. For this reason, dilatons can now appear as internal massless particles in the amplitudes. Such non-perturbative setup would be more challenging numerically.

So far, we restricted ourselves to 4 spacetime dimensions.
However, our setup can be easily adapted to 6d. The main difference with 4d is the low energy behavior of the dilaton scattering amplitude which reads in 6d as
Elvang:2012st

\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{BB\rightarrow BB}(s,t,u)=\frac{b}{8}\times(s^{2}+t^{2}+u^{2})+\frac{9}{8}(a^{\text{UV}}-a^{\text{IR}})\times stu+O(s^{4}).

(167)

Here
b
is some parameter of the theory. It is an outstanding open question to prove the
a
-theorem in 6d.
The paper
Kundu:2019zsl
made an interesting proposal: consider the RG flow on a fixed Anti-de Sitter background and formulate the
a
-theorem as a conformal bootstrap problem for its boundary correlation functions. Unfortunately, this approach has not yet succeeded.
In flat space, there seems to be no positive sum rule for
(a^{\text{UV}}-a^{\text{IR}})
in 6d
Elvang:2012st
.
Nevertheless, we can try to run our numerical approach and minimize
a^{\text{UV}}
in QFTs with a mass gap. We leave this exploration for the future.

Appendix F
Worldline action in dilaton background

In this appendix, we consider the effective worldline action for a massive particle moving in a background geometry with metric
g_{\mu\nu}=e^{-2\tau(x)}\eta_{\mu\nu}
. Writing
e^{-\tau(x)}=1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}f}\varphi(x)
, we shall show that the worldline action is universal up to two derivatives and quadratic order in the dilaton field
\varphi(x)
.

The most general coordinate invariant worldline action is

S=-m\int dt\left[1+c_{1}\ddot{x}^{\mu}\ddot{x}^{\nu}g_{\mu\nu}+c_{2}R+c_{3}\dot{x}^{\mu}\dot{x}^{\nu}R_{\mu\nu}+\dots\right]

(238)

where
m
is the mass of the particle and
c_{i}
are non-universal Wilson coefficients.
The 4-vector
\dot{x}^{\mu}
is equal to
\frac{dx^{\mu}}{dt}
with
t
the proper time defined by

dt^{2}=-g_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}\,.

(239)

R
(
R_{\mu\nu}
) stands for the Ricci scalar (tensor) of the background metric evaluated on the worldline, and the dots represent higher derivative terms.
Notice that the extrinsic curvature of a worldline is simply given in terms of
\dot{x}^{\mu}
and
\ddot{x}^{\mu}
.

For the conformally flat metric
g_{\mu\nu}=e^{-2\tau}\eta_{\mu\nu}
, the Riemann curvature tensor is

R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}=e^{-2\tau}(\eta_{\alpha\gamma}T_{\beta\delta}+\eta_{\beta\delta}T_{\alpha\gamma}-\eta_{\alpha\delta}T_{\beta\gamma}-\eta_{\beta\gamma}T_{\alpha\delta})\,,\\

(240)

with

\quad T_{\alpha\beta}=\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}\tau+\partial_{\alpha}\tau\partial_{\beta}\tau-\frac{1}{2}(\partial\tau)^{2}\eta_{\alpha\beta}\,.

(241)

Therefore, up to quadratic order in the dilaton field, both
R
and
R_{\mu\nu}
are of order
O(\partial^{2}\varphi,(\partial\varphi)^{2})
. Clearly, higher derivative terms will contain more derivatives (and more powers of
\varphi
in some cases). Notice that
\ddot{x}^{\mu}=0
is the leading order equation of motion, thus we can neglect the second term in (
238
).
We conclude that non-universal terms contribute to the scattering amplitude
\widetilde{T}_{AB\to AB}
at order at least
p^{2}
where
p
is dilaton 4-momentum.
This confirms the universality of the result (
143
).
