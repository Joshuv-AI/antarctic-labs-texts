Recovery Guarantees of Unsupervised Neural Networks for Inverse Problems trained with Gradient Descent
Nathan Buskulic, Jalal Fadili, Yvain Quéau

Abstract
Advanced machine learning methods, and more prominently neural networks, have become standard to solve inverse problems over the last years. However, the theoretical recovery guarantees of such methods are still scarce and difficult to achieve. Only recently did unsupervised methods such as Deep Image Prior (DIP) get equipped with convergence and recovery guarantees for generic loss functions when trained through gradient flow with an appropriate initialization. In this paper, we extend these results by proving that these guarantees hold true when using gradient descent with an appropriately chosen step-size/learning rate. We also show that the discretization only affects the overparametrization bound for a two-layer DIP network by a constant and thus that the different guarantees found for the gradient flow will hold for gradient descent.



keywords
Inverse problems, Deep Image/Inverse Prior, Overparametrization, Gradient descent, Unsupervised learning

1
Introduction

Problem statement.

In finite dimension, inverse problems are understood as the task of reliably recovering a vector
\overline{\mathbf{x}}
in a finite-dimensional vector space (throughout
\mathbb{R}^{n}
) from its indirect and noisy measurements

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}\overline{\mathbf{x}}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},

(1)

where
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the observation vector,
\mathbf{A}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a forward operator that we assume linear and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
is an additive noise. We will use the shorthand notation
\overline{\mathbf{y}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathbf{A}\overline{\mathbf{x}}
to denote the noiseless observation vector.

Due to the large number of scientific and engineering fields where inverse problems appear, it is natural that in recent years sophisticated machine learning algorithms, including those based on (deep) neural networks, were developed to solve them. These methods have shown promising results; for space limitation, we refer to the reviews
Arridge et al. 2019
;
Ongie et al. 2020
. Many of these approaches are based on the idea of optimizing a generator network
\mathbf{g}:(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\mapsto\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, equipped with an activation function
\phi
, to transform an input (latent) variable
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
into a vector
\mathbf{x}
as close as possible to the sought-after vector. The optimization/training takes the form of a (possibly stochastic) gradient descent on the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
of the network to minimize a loss function
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{y}}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbf{v}\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{v})
intended to capture the forward model (
1
) by measuring the discrepancy between the observation
\mathbf{y}
and an estimated observation
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\theta})
generated by the network with parameters/weights
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

Literature overview.

Several theoretical works emerged recently to study the optimization trajectory of overparametrized networks
Bartlett et al. 2021
;
Fang et al. 2021
. While the first attempts used unrealistic assumptions, such as the strong convexity of the loss when composed with the network, the next attempts were based on gradient dominated inequalities. This allowed to prove, for networks trained to minimize the mean square error (MSE), an exponential convergence rate to an optimal solution of either gradient flow
Chizat et al. 2019
;
Robin et al. 2022
, or gradient descent
Du et al. 2019
;
Arora et al. 2019
;
Oymak and Soltanolkotabi 2019
;
Oymak and Soltanolkotabi 2020
;
Liu et al. 2022
). These results were generalized in
Buskulic et al. 2023
to the inverse problem setting, providing both convergence and recovery guarantees. Furthermore, the latter work alleviates the restriction of using the MSE as the loss function and gives results for generic loss functions obeying the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz inequality (e.g., any semi-algebraic function or even definable on an o-minimal structure)
Lojasiewicz 1963
;
Łojasiewicz 1984
;
Kurdyka 1998
. However, these guarantees were only provided for the gradient flow and it is yet unknown how forward Euler discretization of this follows, giving rise to to gradient descent, affects them.

Contributions.

Gradient descent iteration to learn the network parameters reads

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\tau+1}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\tau}-\gamma\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\tau})),

(2)

with
\gamma
the (fixed) descent-size/learning rate. The goal of this work is to analyze under which conditions the iterates (
2
) converge, whether they converge to a zero-loss solution, and what can be said about the recovery guarantees of
\overline{\mathbf{x}}
.

In Section
3
, we first show that neural networks trained through gradient descent (
2
) can benefit from convergence and recovery guarantees for general loss functions verifying the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz (KL) property. That is, we prove that under a proper initialization and a well-chosen step-size
\gamma
, the network will converge to a zero-loss solution at a rate dependent on the desingularizing function of the KL property of the loss. We also provide a bound on the recovery error of the original vector
\overline{\mathbf{x}}
which requires a restricted injectivity condition to hold, and we emphasize the trade-off between this condition and the expressivity of the trained network. Then, we give a bound on the overparametrization necessary for a two-layer DIP
Ulyanov et al. 2018
network to benefit from all these guarantees with high probability. Our results match those of the gradient flow up to discretization errors that depend on the step-size. Section
4
eventually provides numerical experiments validating our theoretical findings.

Preliminaries

2.1
General Notations

For a matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{a\times b}
we denote by
\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{M})
and
\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{M})
its smallest and largest non-zero singular values, and by
\kappa(\mathbf{M})=\frac{\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{M})}{\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{M})}

\sigma_{\mathbf{A}}
to express
\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{A})
. We denote by
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
the Euclidian norm of a vector. With a slight abuse of notation
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
will also denote the spectral norm of a matrix. We use the notation
a\gtrsim b
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that
a\geq Cb
.
We also define
\mathbf{x}_{\tau}=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\tau})
and recall that
\mathbf{y}_{\tau}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{\tau}
. The Jacobian operator of
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u},\cdot)
is denoted
\mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{g}}
.
\mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{g}}(\tau)
is a shorthand notation of
\mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{g}}
evaluated at
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\tau}
. The local Lipschitz constant of a mapping on a ball of radius
R>0
around a point
\mathbf{z}
is denoted
\mathrm{Lip}_{\mathbb{B}(\mathbf{z},R)}(\cdot)
. We omit
R
in the notation when the Lipschitz constant is global. For a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, we use the notation for the sublevel set
[f<c]=\left\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\;f(\mathbf{z})<c\right\}
and
[c_{1}<f<c_{2}]=\left\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\;c_{1}<f(\mathbf{z})<c_{2}\right\}
. We set
C_{\phi}=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}\left[\phi(X)^{2}\right]}
and
C_{\phi^{\prime}}=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}\left[\phi^{\prime}(X)^{2}\right]}
.

For some
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we define
\Sigma_{\Theta}=\left\{\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\theta}):\;\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta\right\}
the set of vectors that the network
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u},\cdot)
can generate for all
\boldsymbol{\theta}
in the set of parameters
\Theta
.
\Sigma_{\Theta}
can thus be viewed as a parametric manifold. If
\Theta
is closed (resp. compact), so is
\Sigma_{\Theta}
. We denote
\mathrm{dist}(\cdot,\Sigma_{\Theta})
the distance to
\Sigma_{\Theta}
which is well defined if
\Theta
is closed and non-empty. For a vector
\mathbf{x}
,
\mathbf{x}_{\Sigma_{\Theta}}
is its projection on
\Sigma_{\Theta}
, i.e.
\mathbf{x}_{\Sigma_{\Theta}}\in\Argmin_{\mathbf{z}\in\Sigma_{\Theta}}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\right\rVert
. We also define
T_{\Sigma_{\Theta}}(\mathbf{x})
the tangent cone of
\Sigma_{\Theta}
at
\mathbf{x}\in\Sigma_{\Theta}
. The minimal (conic) singular value of a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
w.r.t. the cone
T_{\Sigma_{\Theta}}(\mathbf{x})
is then defined as

\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathbf{A};T_{\Sigma_{\Theta}}(\mathbf{x}))=\inf\{\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}\right\rVert/\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert:\mathbf{z}\in T_{\Sigma_{\Theta}}(\mathbf{x})\}.

Throughout,
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\theta})
is a feedforward neural network.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a component wise activation function. An
L
-layer fully connected neural network is a collection of weight matrices
\left(\mathbf{W}^{(l)}\right)_{l\in[L]}
where
\mathbf{W}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{l}\times N_{l-1}}
with
N_{l}\in\mathbb{N}
the number of neurons on layer
l
. We take
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, with
p=\sum_{l=1}^{L}N_{l-1}N_{l}
, a vector that gathers all these parameters. Then, a neural network parametrized by
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is the mapping
\mathbf{g}:(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\mapsto\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{L}}
with
N_{L}=n
, which is defined recursively as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathbf{g}^{(0)}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\theta})&=\mathbf{u},\\
\mathbf{g}^{(l)}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\theta})&=\phi\left(\mathbf{W}^{(l)}\mathbf{g}^{(l-1)}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\theta})\right),\quad l=1,\ldots,L-1,\\
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\theta})&=\mathbf{W}^{(L)}\mathbf{g}^{(L-1)}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\theta}).\end{cases}

2.2
KL inequality

We will work under a general condition of the loss function
\mathcal{L}
which includes non-convex ones. We will suppose that
\mathcal{L}
verifies a Kurdyka-Łojasewicz-type (KL for short) inequality
(
Kurdyka 1998
, Theorem 1)
.

Definition 2.2

(KL inequality)
.

A continuously differentiable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\min f=0
satisfies the KL inequality if there exists
r_{0}>0
and a strictly increasing function
\psi\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,r_{0}[)\cap\mathcal{C}^{1}(]0,r_{0}[)
with
\psi(0)=0
such that

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(f(\mathbf{z}))\left\lVert\nabla f(\mathbf{z})\right\rVert\geq 1,\quad\text{for all}\quad\mathbf{z}\in[f<r_{0}].

(3)

We use the shorthand notation
f\in\KL_{\psi}(r_{0})
for a function satisfying this inequality.

3
Recovery Guarantees with Gradient Descent

3.1
Main Assumptions

Throughout this paper, we will work under the following standing assumptions:

A-1
.

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{y}}(\cdot)\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
is bounded from below whose gradient is Lipschitz continuous on the bounded sets of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

A-2
.

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{y}}(\cdot)\in\KL_{\psi}(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y}_{0})+\eta)
for some
\eta>0
.

A-3
.

\min\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{y}}(\cdot)=0
.

A-4
.

\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
\exists B>0
such that
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|\phi^{\prime}(x)|\leq B
and
\phi^{\prime}
is
B
-Lipschitz continuous.

A-5
.

(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\tau})_{\tau\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded.

Notably, these assumptions are not restrictive.
A-1
and
A-4
ensure that
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u},\cdot))
is locally Lipschitz w.r.t
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and
A-2
is met by many classical loss functions (MSE, Kullback-Leibler and cross entropy to cite a few).
A-5
is quite mild as we do not require neither convexity nor coercivity of the objective or that
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u},\cdot))
is globally Lipschitz continuous. Indeed, the latter is widely assumed to ensure that the scheme (
2
) has the descent property, but this is unrealistic when training neural networks. Our assumption
A-5
, together with
A-1
and
A-4
, ensure the existence of a constant
L>0
such that
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u},\cdot))
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous w.r.t
\boldsymbol{\theta}
on the ball containing
(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\tau})_{\tau\in\mathbb{N}}
. This avoids an “egg and chicken” issue. Indeed, we can show that
(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\tau})_{\tau\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded if (
2
) has a global descent property, but this requires global Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u},\cdot))
.
A-5
resolves that issue.

3.3
Discussion and Consequences

We start by discussing the conditions of the theorem. The first condition
R^{\prime}<R
ensures that the loss at initialization is “small enough” so that the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
lie in the attraction basin of a minimizer. The condition
\gamma\in]0,1/L]
is classical and ensures that the scheme (
2
) has a descent property. In our context, avoiding big steps guarantees that it will not step out of the attraction basin of the minimizer.

Let us now comment on the different claims of the theorem. The first one ensures that the network converges to a zero-loss solution with a rate dictated by the mapping
\Psi^{-1}
(which is decreasing) applied to an affine increasing function of
\tau
. The choice of the loss function is generally dictated by a fidelity argument to the forward model (
1
) (e.g., noise
-
log-likelihood). On the other hand,
\Psi
only depends on the KL desingularizing function of the chosen loss function. Thus, this choice not only influences the theoretical convergence rate but also the condition (
4
) required for the theorem to hold. Our second claim shows that gradient descent provides a sequence of network parameters that converges to a global minimum of the loss. On the signal recovery side, our third result gives us a reconstruction error bound that holds under a restricted injectivity constraint. This bound depends on both the expressivity of the network (via the set
\Sigma^{\prime}
) and decreases with decreasing noise. Observe that a more expressive network, i.e., larger
\Sigma^{\prime}
, yields a lower
\mathrm{dist}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\Sigma^{\prime})
but may hinder the restricted injectivity condition
A-6
. The recovery bound (
(ii)
) also suggests to use an early stopping strategy to ensure that
\mathbf{x}(\tau)
for
\tau
large enough (whose expression is left to the reader), will lie in a ball around
\overline{\mathbf{x}}
.

Theorem
3.1
shows that gradient descent, which is nothing but an Euler forward time discretization of the gradient flow, inherits the recovery properties of latter proved in
Buskulic et al. 2023
. The price to be paid by gradient descent is in the requirement that the sequence of iterates a priori bounded, that
\gamma
is well-chosen, as well as by the constants
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
. As
\nu_{1}>1
and strictly increasing in
\gamma
, condition
R^{\prime}<R
in (
4
) is strictly worse that its counterpart for gradient flow, and the error bound (
7
) is also strictly larger. On the other hand, the convergence behaviour gets faster with decreasing
\nu_{2}
. If
\gamma
gets close to 0,
\nu_{1}
will get smaller but
\nu_{2}
will grow very large, entailing a very slow convergence. In fact,
\nu_{2}
is minimized when
\gamma=\frac{1}{2L}
in which case
\nu_{2}=6L
. As such, this choice of the step-size incurs a trade-off between the convergence speed and the requirements of the theorem.

3.4
Probabilistic Bounds For a Two-Layer DIP Network

We now study the case of a two-layer network in the DIP setting. We fix the latent input
\mathbf{u}
and learn the weights of a network to match the observation
\mathbf{y}
. We consider the two-layer network defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\mathbf{V}\phi(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{u})

(9)

with
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
and
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
. We will assume the following:

A-7
.

\mathbf{u}
is a uniform vector on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
;

A-8
.

\mathbf{W}_{0}
has iid entries from
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
C_{\phi},C_{\phi^{\prime}}<+\infty
;

A-9
.

\mathbf{V}_{0}
is independent from
\mathbf{W}_{0}
and
\mathbf{u}
and has iid columns with identity covariance and
D
-bounded centered entries.

Our main result gives a bound on the level of overparametrization which is sufficient for (
4
) to hold with high probability.

Corollary 3.2
.

Suppose that assumptions
A-1
and
A-4
hold. Let
C
,
C^{\prime}
two positive constants that depend only on the activation function and
D
. Let:

L_{\mathcal{L},0}=\max_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{B}\left(0,C\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert\sqrt{n\log(d)}+\sqrt{m}\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\overline{\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{\infty}\right)\right)}\frac{\left\lVert\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{v})\right\rVert}{\left\lVert\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert}.

Consider the one-hidden layer network (
9
) where both layers are trained with gradient descent using initialization satisfying
A-7
to
A-9
and the architecture parameters obeying

\displaystyle k\geq C^{\prime}\sigma_{\mathbf{A}}^{-4}n\psi\biggl(\frac{L_{\mathcal{L},0}}{2}\Bigl(

\displaystyle C\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert\sqrt{n\log(d)}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{m}\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\overline{\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{\infty}\right)\Bigl)^{2}\biggl)^{4}.

Then (
4
) holds with probability at least
1-2n^{-1}-d^{-1}
.

The overparametrization bound is strongly dependent on the chosen loss function via the associated desingularizing function
\psi
. As an example, for the MSE loss, we have
\psi(s)=cs^{1/2}
. In that setting, the dependency on the problem variables becomes
k\gtrsim\kappa(\mathbf{A})n^{2}m
which indicates naturally that the conditioning of the operator plays an important role in the reconstruction capabilities of the network. Furthermore, the dimension of the signal is more impactful on the bound than the dimension of the observation which seems intuitive as the network tries to reconstruct a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
while matching a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. This bound matches the one from the gradient flow case as the discretization error is absorbed in the constant. A more in-depth discussion can be found in
Buskulic et al. 2023
.

Local Lipschitz constant estimate.

Estimating the local Lipschitz constant
L
of
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u},\cdot))
is not easy in general. One can still derive crude bounds. To give some guidelines, and make the discussion easier, we here focus on the MSE loss and assume that
\phi
is uniformly bounded (this conforms to our numerics). In this case, we actually have global
L
-Lipschitz continuity
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u},\cdot))
and one can show that

L\lesssim\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert^{2}\frac{n}{k}+\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert\left\lVert\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}_{0}\right\rVert\sqrt{\frac{n}{k}},

(10)

where the constant in
\lesssim
depends only on
B
,
D
and the bound on
\phi
. As the level of overparametrization increases, the bound on
L
gets smaller and one can afford taking larger step-size
\gamma
. On the other hand, for the overparametrized regime,
L
depends essentially on
\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert
and the loss at initialization, which may themselves depend on the dimensions
(m,n)
, indicating that the choice of
\gamma
may be influenced by
(m,n)
, regardless of the choice of
k
large enough.

Numerical Experiments

We trained two-layer networks, equipped with the sigmoid activation, with varying architectures and on problems with varying dimensions. In the first experiments we set

.

5
Conclusion and Future Work

We studied the convergence of unsupervised networks in the inverse problem setting when trained through gradient descent. We showed that both convergence and recovery guarantees could be obtained which match the findings of the gradient flow case. Furthermore, we characterized the discretization error incurred which is of a constant factor at most. We also provided an overparametrization bound under which a two-layer DIP network will benefit from these guarantees with high probability. We would like in the future to study the supervised case in the setting where the network is seen as a push-forward measure to generate points on a manifold.
