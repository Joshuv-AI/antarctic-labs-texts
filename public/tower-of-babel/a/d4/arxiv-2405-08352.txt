Sibson α \alpha -Mutual Information and its Variational Representations
Amedeo Roberto Esposito, Michael Gastpar, Ibrahim Issa

Abstract
Information measures can be constructed from Rényi divergences much like mutual information from Kullback-Leibler divergence. One such information measure is known as Sibson α \alpha -mutual information and has received renewed attention recently in several contexts: concentration of measure under dependence, statistical learning, hypothesis testing, and estimation theory. In this paper, we survey and extend the state of the art. In particular, we introduce variational representations for Sibson α \alpha -mutual information and employ them in each described context to derive novel results. Namely, we produce generalized Transportation-Cost inequalities and Fano-type inequalities. We also present an overview of known applications, spanning from learning theory and Bayesian risk to universal prediction.

2.1
Rényi Entropy

Rényi entropy is a generalization of Shannon entropy; in fact it is the unique family of entropy measures that satisfy the additivity property (under some mild assumption on the form of the measure; for more details see

Given a discrete random variable

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}(X)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}P_{X}(x)^{\alpha}.

(2)

It is easy to check that Rényi entropy could be rewritten in terms of

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}(X)=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\log\left\|P_{X}\right\|_{\alpha}.

(3)

For
\alpha\geq 1
, the quantity inside the

is a norm; but it is only a quasi norm for

as it does not satisfy the triangle inequality. This form inspired Arimoto’s defintion of conditional Rényi entropy
[

Given a pair of discrete random variables

\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}(X|Y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{Y}}\left[\left\|P_{X|Y}(.|Y)\right\|_{\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\log\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}P_{XY}(x,y)^{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

Rényi Divergences

Rényi divergences

(12)

2.3
Variational Representations of KL and Rényi divergence

The Donsker-Varadhan variational representation of KL divergence
[
19
, Section 10]
can be stated as follows: let
P
and
Q
be two probability measures such that

(

D(P\|Q)=\sup_{f\in B(\mathcal{X})}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[f\right]-\log\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[e^{f}\right],

(20)

where
B(\mathcal{X})
is the space of bounded functions acting on
\mathcal{X}
.

One can derive analogous results for Rényi divergences. First, one can connect Rényi divergence to KL divergence via the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

For all
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty),

\displaystyle(1-\alpha)D_{\alpha}(P\|Q)=\inf_{R}\{\alpha D(R\|P)+(1-\alpha)D(R\|Q)\}.

(21)

Furthermore, the objective function in braces is a convex and continuous function of
R.

In this lemma, continuity should be understood in the sense of the discussion in
[
9
, Section III.D]
.
This lemma is well known, see e.g.
[
9
, Theorem 30]
or
[
55
]
. For completeness, a proof is included in
Section

B.1
. Moreover, Rényi divergence admits a representation akin to the Donsker-Varadhan representation of KL divergence, first observed by
[

]
. Indeed, as done in
[

]
, one can utilize
Lemma

to derive:

Lemma 2.2

(Variational Representation of Rényi divergence)
.

For all
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\alpha}D_{\alpha}(P||Q)=\sup_{f}\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[e^{(\alpha-1)f}\right]-\frac{1}{\alpha}\log\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[e^{\alpha f}\right].\end{split}

(22)

A simplified proof of this lemma is included in
Section

We begin by stating the definition of Sibson’s

Definition 3.1
.

Let
P_{XY}
be a joint distribution over
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
and denote with

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(X,Y)=\min_{Q_{Y}\in P(\mathcal{Y})}D_{\alpha}(P_{XY}\|P_{X}Q_{Y}),

(23)

where

denotes the set of probability distributions over
\mathcal{Y}
.

The definition is a generalization of Shannon’s mutual information, as
I_{1}(X,Y)=I(X;Y)
.

Remark 3.1
.

It is possible to see that

[

Let

\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}>0
. For
\alpha\geq 1
, the information radius of order
\alpha
is defined as:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\min_{\nu\ll\sum_{i}w_{i}\mu_{i}}\log\left(\sum_{i}w_{i}\exp((\alpha-1)D_{\alpha}(\mu_{i}\|\nu))\right).

In particular, for illustrative purposes, consider a discrete setting. Assume that one has two random variables
X,Y
jointly distributed according to
P_{XY}
, one can think of
\mu_{i}=P_{Y|X=i}
and
w_{i}=P_{X}(i)
. Leveraging then the geometric averaging present in Rényi’s information measures i.e.,

\displaystyle\hskip-10.00002ptD_{\alpha}(P_{X}P_{Y|X}\|P_{X}Q_{Y})=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}\left[\exp\left((\alpha-1)D_{\alpha}(P_{Y|X}\|Q_{Y}\right)\right],

(24)

one can see that the information radius is a special case of
Equation

23
which we consider as our de-facto definition of Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information.

Note that
D_{\alpha}(P_{XY}||P_{X}Q_{Y})
is convex in
Q_{Y}

[
9
, Theorem 12]
. Indeed, one can derive a closed-form expression for the minimizer
Q^{\star}_{Y}
and, consequently, a simple closed-form expression for
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)
:

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)
. Then

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=D_{\alpha}(P_{XY}\|P_{X}Q^{\star}_{Y})

(25)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{Y}}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}\left[\left(\frac{dP_{XY}}{dP_{X}P_{Y}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right],

(26)

where

\displaystyle\frac{dQ^{\star}_{Y}}{dP_{Y}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}\left[\left(\frac{dP_{XY}}{dP_{X}P_{Y}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{\mathbb{E}_{P_{Y}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}\left[\left(\frac{dP_{XY}}{dP_{X}P_{Y}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right]}.

(27)

If
\alpha\geq 1
and
P_{XY}
is not absolutely continuous with respect to
P_{X}P_{Y}
, then
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)=+\infty
.
Moreover,

\displaystyle I_{0}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=-\esssup_{P_{Y}}\log P_{X}\left(\frac{dP_{XY}}{dP_{X}P_{Y}}(X,Y)>0\right),

(28)

\displaystyle\text{and }I_{\infty}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}I_{\alpha}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{Y}}\left[\esssup_{P_{X}}\frac{dP_{XY}}{dP_{X}P_{Y}}\right].

(29)

The proof of this theorem is given in
Section

B.3
.

Remark 3.2
.

A more “rigorous” way of writing
Equation

26
would be to consider a dominating measure
\mu
and then write the Radon-Nikodym derivatives with respect to this measure. However, we opt for the above form as it is more illustrative, while measure-theoretic issues may hinder readability.

Remark 3.3
.

Equation

27
may not be well-defined if the denominator is infinite, in which case indeed
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)=\infty
.

If
X
and
Y
are discrete random variables, then the expression simplifies to

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\log\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P_{Y}(y)\left(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}P_{X}(x)\left(\frac{P_{Y|X}(y|x)}{P_{Y}(y)}\right)^{\alpha}\right)^{1/\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\log\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}P_{X}(x)P_{Y|X}(y|x)^{\alpha}\right)^{1/\alpha},

(30)

where, without loss of generality, we assumed that
P_{Y}
has full support over
\mathcal{Y}
.
Moreover, the minimizing probability mass function in
Equation

25
can be expressed as

\displaystyle Q_{Y}^{\star}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}P_{X}(x)P_{Y|X}(y|x)^{\alpha}\right)^{1/\alpha}}{\sum_{\tilde{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}P_{X}(x)P_{Y|X}(\tilde{y}|x)^{\alpha}\right)^{1/\alpha}}.

(31)

Similarly, if
X
and
Y
are jointly continuous random variables then we can replace sums with integrals and PMFs with pdfs in
Equation

30
.
The closed-form expression can be extended to
\alpha=\infty
:

I_{\infty}(X,Y)=\log\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}x:\\
P_{X}(x)>0\end{subarray}}P_{Y|X=x}(y)=\mathcal{L}\left(X\!\!\to\!\!Y\right),

(32)

where
\mathcal{L}\left(X\!\!\to\!\!Y\right)
denotes the maximal leakage between
X
and
Y

[
53
]
. A more extensive treatise on the connection between Maximal Leakage and
I_{\alpha}
is deferred to
Section

4.3.1
.

Remark 3.4
.

One could also attempt to extend the definition of Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information for negative values of
\alpha
. An informal discussion of this extension and its potential applications is provided in
Section

9.1
. Indeed, assuming a closed-form expression analogous to the positive case, such an object could naturally arise in contexts like those discussed in
Sections

6.1
and
7.3
. However, a rigorous formulation would involve additional technical challenges beyond the scope of this work. We therefore refrain from a formal treatment and simply refer the reader to instances where such an extension has been used, e.g.,
[
41
, Appendix D.2]
.

3.1
Examples

The closed-form expression for Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information makes it relatively simple to compute. We provide a few examples.

Example 3.1

(Binary Symmetric Channel)
.

Assume that
X=\text{Ber}(1/2)
, and that
P_{Y|X=x}(y)=\epsilon\mathbbm{1}_{x\neq y}+(1-\epsilon)\mathbbm{1}_{x=y}
, i.e. one has a Binary Symmetric Channel (BSC) with error probability
\epsilon
. If
Y
is the outcome of
X
after being passed through the BSC, one has that
Y=\text{Ber}(1/2)
. Then for
\alpha\in(0,+\infty)

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(X,Y)=I_{\alpha}(Y,X)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\frac{1}{2^{1-\alpha}}\left(\epsilon^{\alpha}+(1-\epsilon)^{\alpha}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\log 2+\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\epsilon^{\alpha}+(1-\epsilon)^{\alpha}\right)

(33)

\displaystyle=\log 2-h_{2,\alpha}(\epsilon),

where
h_{2,\alpha}(\epsilon)
denotes the binary Rényi Entropy of the distribution
(\epsilon,1-\epsilon)
.

For the limiting case
\alpha=0,
we find

\displaystyle I_{0}(X,Y)=I_{0}(Y,X)=0;

(34)

for the case
\alpha=1
, i.e., mutual information, we find

\displaystyle I_{1}(X,Y)=I_{1}(Y,X)

\displaystyle=I(X;Y)

\displaystyle=\log 2+(1-\epsilon)\log(1-\epsilon)+\epsilon\log(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\log 2-h_{2}(\epsilon),

(35)

and for the limiting case
\alpha=\infty,
we find

\displaystyle I_{\infty}(X,Y)=I_{\infty}(Y,X)

\displaystyle=\log\left(2\max\{\epsilon,1-\epsilon\}\right)

\displaystyle=\log 2-h_{2,\infty}(\epsilon).

(36)

I_{\alpha}(X,Y)

as a function of

.

The symmetry in the previous example is due to the choice of the joint distribution (a doubly symmetric binary source). However,
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)
is not necessarily symmetric as the following example shows:

Example 3.2

(Binary Erasure Channel)
.

Assume that
X=\text{Ber}(1/2)
and that
P_{Y|X=x}(y)=(1-\delta)\mathbbm{1}_{y=x}+\delta\mathbbm{1}_{y=e}
, i.e., one has a Binary Erasure Channel (BEC) with erasure probability
\delta
. Assume
Y
is the outcome of
X
after being passed through the BEC. Then, if
\alpha\in(0,+\infty)

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\log(\delta+(1-\delta)2^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}})

(37)

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(Y,X)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\left(\delta+(1-\delta)2^{\alpha-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right).

(38)

For the limiting case
\alpha=0,
we find

\displaystyle I_{0}(X,Y)=0\text{ and }I_{0}(Y,X)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\log\left(\frac{2}{1+\delta}\right)},

(39)

for the case
\alpha=1
, i.e., mutual information, we find

\displaystyle I_{1}(X,Y)=I_{1}(Y,X)=I(X;Y)

\displaystyle=(1-\delta)\log 2,

(40)

whereas

\displaystyle I_{\infty}(X,Y)=\log(2-\delta)\text{ and }I_{\infty}(Y,X)=\log 2.

(41)

Hence, this example illustrates the asymmetry of Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information.

One can also compute the Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information for continuous random variables.

Example 3.3

(Gaussian channel with Gaussian inputs)
.

Assume
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{X})
i.e.,
X
is a Gaussian random variable with mean
0
and variance
\sigma^{2}_{X}
. If
Y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{Y})
and
\alpha\in(0,+\infty)

I_{\alpha}(X,X+Y)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\alpha\frac{\sigma^{2}_{X}}{\sigma^{2}_{Y}}\right).

(42)

Clearly, for
\alpha=1,
this recovers the classic and well known mutual information expression for jointly Gaussian random variables. Moreover, as
\alpha
increases,
I_{\alpha}(X,X+Y)
increases without bound irrespective of the values of
\sigma_{X}^{2}>0
and
\sigma_{Y}^{2}.

As a last example, let us consider a setting in which
I(X;Y)=+\infty
but
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)<+\infty
for
0<\alpha<1
.

Example 3.4
.

Let
Y
be a discrete random variable with support on
[2,+\infty)\cap\mathbb{N}
and such that
P(y)=\frac{1}{C}\frac{1}{y(\log^{2}(y))}
for
y\geq 2
and with
C=2.10974
. Let now
X=g(Y)
with
g
deterministic and bijective. One has that
I(X;Y)=H(Y)=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{y=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{y\log^{2}(y)}\log\left(Cy\log^{2}(y)\right)=+\infty
(by the integral test).

However,

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(X,Y)=H_{1/\alpha}(Y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\log\sum_{y}P_{Y}(y)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\log\sum_{y}\frac{1}{C^{\hat{\alpha}}y^{\hat{\alpha}}\log^{2\hat{\alpha}}(y)}<+\infty,

(43)

where
\hat{\alpha}=1/\alpha
. Since
0<\alpha<1
one has that
\hat{\alpha}>1
and the sum in
Equation

43
converges for every
0<\alpha<1
.

3.2
Definitions Via (Quasi-)Norms

Another insightful perspective on Rényi
\alpha
-divergence and Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information is via norms. To that end, given a random variable
X
and a function
f
, the
L_{p}
-norm with respect to
P_{X}
is defined as follows,

\displaystyle\|f(X)\|_{L^{p}(P_{X})}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}[|f(X)|^{p}]\right)^{1/p},

(44)

where
p\geq 1
. For
p\in(0,1)
, the above definition yields a quasi-norm. Now, Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information can be written as:

Corollary 3.2
.

For all
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)
, we can express
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)
as

\displaystyle\frac{(\alpha-1)}{\alpha}I_{\alpha}(X,Y)=\log\left\|\left\|\frac{dP_{XY}}{dP_{X}P_{Y}}\right\|_{L^{\alpha}\left(P_{X}\right)}\right\|_{L^{1}(P_{Y})}.

(45)

For
\alpha>1
, the following alternative representation holds:

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(X,Y)=\log\left\|\left\|\frac{dP_{Y|X}}{dP_{Y}}\right\|_{L^{\alpha-1}\left(P_{X|Y}\right)}\right\|_{L^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}(P_{Y})}.

(46)

The following is well known (a proof is included for completeness in
Section

B.4
):

Lemma 3.3
.

For all
0\leq p\leq q,
we have

\|f\|_{L^{p}({P_{X}})}\leq\|f\|_{L^{q}({P_{X}})}.

When we combine the expressions given in
Corollary

3.2
with
Lemma

3.3
, we can conclude that
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)
is a non-decreasing function of
\alpha.

Another feature of the expressions given in
Corollary

3.2
is a natural connection to Shannon information measures.
To this end, we leverage the following lemma (proved, for completeness, in
Section

B.4
).

Lemma 3.4
.

\displaystyle\lim_{p\rightarrow 0+}\|X\|_{L^{p}(P_{X})}

\displaystyle=\exp\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}\left[\log|X|\right].

(47)

Corollary 3.5
.

If
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)<\infty
for some
\alpha>1
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to 1^{+}}I_{\alpha}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\log\exp\mathbb{E}_{P_{Y}}\left[\log\exp\mathbb{E}_{P_{X|Y}}\left[\log\frac{dP_{Y|X}}{dP_{Y}}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}\left[\log\frac{dP_{Y|X}}{dP_{Y}}\right]=I(X;Y).

(48)

Properties and Known Results

We now present properties and well-known results about Sibson
\alpha

I_{\alpha}
in terms of
\alpha

In

\alpha

Section

4.3
) and other generalizations of Shannon’s mutual information that stem from Rényi divergences (Aritmoto, Csiszár, Lapidoth-Pfister,
Section

4.4
). Some of the proofs for this Section can be found in
Appendix

C
. Other proofs will be omitted to avoid meaningful overlap with the literature. The material contained in this Section can also be found in

,

.

4.1
Basic properties

Theorem 4.1

(Axiomatic properties)
.

Given two jointly distributed random variables
X,Y
, and
\alpha\in(0,+\infty)
, Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)
satisfies the following set of properties:

1.

Non-negativity:
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)\geq 0
with
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)=0
if and only if
X
and
Y
are independent;

2.

Data processing inequality: Let
X,Y,Z
be three random variables jointly distributed according to
P_{XYZ}
. If
X-Y-Z
forms a Markov Chain then,

I_{\alpha}(X,Z)\leq\min\{I_{\alpha}(X,Y),I_{\alpha}(Y,Z)\};

(49)

3.

Invariance under injective transformations: Let
f:\mathcal{X}\to\tilde{\mathcal{X}}
and
g:\mathcal{Y}\to\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}
be two injective mappings, then

I_{\alpha}(f(X),g(Y))=I_{\alpha}(X,Y)

(50)

4.

Additivity: if
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
is a set of independent pairs of random variables then
I_{\alpha}(X^{n},Y^{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}I_{\alpha}(X_{i},Y_{i})
.

One may argue that the above properties should be satisfied by any “reasonable” information measure, and they are indeed satisfied by Sibson’s mutual information. They essentially follow from the properties of Rényi divergence. For completeness, a proof of this theorem is included in
Section

C.1
.

Moreover, intuitively, a finite random variable cannot have infinite mutual information with another variable. This holds true for Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information as well:

Theorem 4.2

(Boundedness)
.

Let
X,Y
be two random variables jointly distributed according to
P_{XY}
and assume that
P_{XY}
admits a pmf. If
\alpha\in(0,+\infty)
, then

I_{\alpha}(X,Y)\leq\min\{H_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(X),H_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(Y)\},

(51)

where equality to
H_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(Y)
holds if and only if
Y
is a deterministic function of
X
.

We note that, while the upper bounds appear in the literature, to the best of our knowledge, the condition after
Equation

51
has not been stated before. A proof is included in
Section

C.2
.

In the following, we provide analytical properties of Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information, in terms of the parameter
\alpha
, as well as in terms of
P_{X}
and
P_{Y|X}
.

Theorem 4.3

(Elementary properties)
.

Given two jointly distributed random variables
X,Y
, and
\alpha\in(0,+\infty)
, Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)
satisfies the following set of properties:

1.

Asymmetry:
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)\neq I_{\alpha}(Y,X)
;

2.

Continuity in
\alpha
: the mapping
\alpha\to I_{\alpha}(X,Y)
is continuous;

3.

Non-decreasability in
\alpha
: if
\alpha_{1}\leq\alpha_{2}
then
I_{\alpha_{1}}(X,Y)\leq I_{\alpha_{2}}(X,Y)\leq\mathcal{L}\left(X\!\!\to\!\!Y\right).

For completeness, a proof of this theorem is included in
Section

C.3
. It is particularly important to understand convexity properties for studying optimization problems involving Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information:

Theorem 4.4

(Convexity properties)
.

Given two random variables
X,Y
jointly distributed according to
P_{XY}
,
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)
satisfies the following convexity/concavity properties:

1.

if
\alpha\leq 1
then, given
P_{X}
,
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)
is convex in
P_{Y|X}
;

2.

if
\alpha\geq 1
then, given
P_{Y|X}
,
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)
is concave in
P_{X}
;

3.

\exp\left(\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}I_{\alpha}(X,Y)\right)
is

(a)

convex in
P_{Y|X}
for a given
P_{X}
if
\alpha\in(1,+\infty)
;

(b)

concave in
P_{Y|X}
for a given
P_{X}
if
\alpha\in(0,1)
;

(c)

concave in
P_{X}
for a given
P_{Y|X}
if
\alpha\in(1,+\infty)
;

(d)

convex in
P_{X}
for a given
P_{Y|X}
if
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

For completeness, a proof of this theorem is included in
Section

C.4
.

A statement resembling a data-processing inequality also holds for Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information, namely, for the special case of two independent random variables
X
and
Z,
and an arbitrarily dependent third random variable
Y.

Theorem 4.5

(
[
61
, Lemma 2]
)
.

If
X
and
Z
are independent, then

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(X,(Y,Z))

\displaystyle\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}I_{\alpha}((X,Z),Y)}.

(52)

For completeness, a proof is included in
Section

C.5
.

Finally, we provide a (seemingly new) tensorization result
for an ensemble of random variables satisfying a conditional independence property, linking Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information terms of various orders.

Theorem 4.6

(Tensorization bound in the conditionally independent case)
.

Let
Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{n}
be conditionally independent given
X
. For
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup\,(1,\infty)
,

\displaystyle(\alpha-1)I_{\alpha}(X,Y^{n})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\alpha-\frac{1}{\beta_{i}}\right)I_{\alpha\beta_{i}}(X,Y_{i}),

(53)

where
\beta_{i}>0
for all
i
, and
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\beta_{i}}=1
.

A proof is included in
Section

C.6
.
The above bound can be seen as a composition result for Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information. Similar composition results for mutual information measures (including Sibson’s) have been studied in
[
60
]
, where the limit and exponential rate of convergence are derived. As done here, these results also assume conditionally independent observations. By contrast, the above result differs by deriving finite
n
bounds (as opposed to asymptotic bounds) and using different Sibson orders
I_{\alpha\beta_{i}}(X,Y_{i})
. Considering the case of
n=2
and taking the limit as
\alpha
goes to infinity recovers the composition result of Maximal Leakage
[
53
, Lemma 6]
. Finite
n
composition bounds have been derived for a variant of Maximal Leakage called Binary Maximal Leakage
[
62
]
.

Moreover, composition results are particularly interesting in the context of differential privacy
[
63
,
64
]
. In that setup however,
adaptive
composition is allowed, i.e., the observations are not conditionally independent (with constraints imposed on
P_{Y_{i}|X,Y_{1}=y_{1},\ldots,Y_{i-1}=y_{i-1}}
). Adaptive composition bounds for Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information are not discussed in the literature, except for Maximal Leakage with binary inputs
[
65
]
.

4.2
Capacity and Coding Theorems

In this section, we briefly discuss applications of Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information in classical coding theorems.

4.2.1
Maximizing over Input Distributions

For a fixed channel
P_{Y|X}
, the Shannon capacity is given by

\displaystyle\sup_{P_{X}}I(X;Y)

\displaystyle=\sup_{P_{X}}\inf_{Q_{Y}}D(P_{XY}||P_{X}Q_{Y})

(54)

\displaystyle=\inf_{Q_{Y}}\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}D(P_{Y|X=x}||Q_{Y}),

(55)

where in the last equality, we assumed
\mathcal{X}
is finite. Maximizing Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information, which we may term the Sibson capacity, yields an analogous result:

Theorem 4.7

(
[
11
, Proposition 1]
)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{X}
is finite and fix
P_{Y|X}
. Then for all
\alpha>0
,

\displaystyle\sup_{P_{X}}I_{\alpha}(X,Y)=\inf_{Q_{Y}}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}D_{\alpha}(P_{Y|X=x}||Q_{Y}).

(56)

For
\alpha=1
, this is simply
Equation

54
. As observed by Csiszár
[
11
]
, this endows Sibson capacity with a geometrical interpretation analogous to that of Shannon capacity: it is the smallest radius that includes all
P_{Y|X=x}
“points”, where “distance” is measured according to
D_{\alpha}
. Csiszár’s proof can be found in
Section

C.7
.

For the special class of (weakly) symmetric channels, it is well known that mutual information is maximized by uniform inputs. This result carries over to Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information with a modification of the definition of symmetric channels. In particular, we introduce
\alpha
-weakly symmetric channels:

Definition 4.1

(
\alpha
-weakly symmetric channels)
.

A discrete input-output channel
P_{Y|X}
is
\alpha
-weakly symmetric if all the rows
P_{Y|X}(.|x)
are permutations of each other, and all the column
\alpha
-power sums
\sum_{x}P_{Y|X}(y|x)^{\alpha}
are equal.

Now, we get:

Theorem 4.8

(Capacity of
\alpha
-weakly symmetric channels)
.

For
X,Y
discrete and finite random variables and for any
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty),

if
P_{Y|X}
is symmetric in the sense that for any
x_{1}
and
x_{2},

p(y|x_{1})
is a permutation of
p(y|x_{2}),
then
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)
is maximized by uniform inputs.

The proof of this theorem is given in
Section

C.8
. Note that this recovers the result for the Shannon capacity for symmetric channels when
\alpha
is set to 1
[
7
]
. Nevertheless, the Sibson capacity does not have an operational interpretation akin to Shannon capacity. However, for
\alpha=0
,
\sup_{P_{X}}I_{0}(X,Y)
yields the zero-error capacity with feedback. Moreover, for
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the Sibson capacity is related to the sphere-packing and random coding exponents. For
\alpha\in(1,\infty)
, it may be given an operational interpretation in the context of information leakage measures, as discussed in
Section

4.3
.

4.2.2
Zero-Error Capacity with Feedback

It is well known that feedback does not increase the capacity of discrete memoryless channels
[
56
]
. However, the zero-error capacity with feedback can indeed be larger than the zero-error capacity (without feedback). Indeed, the former is given by a maximization of Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information of order zero. In this setting, feedback is available at the encoder instantaneously, so that the choice of the next symbol can depend on previously received symbols. More precisely, for a channel with input alphabet
\mathcal{X}
and output alphabet
\mathcal{Y}
, a (feedback-)code of blocklength
n
and rate
R
consists of
n
functions
\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
where
f_{i}:\{1,2,\ldots,2^{nR}\}\times\mathcal{Y}^{i-1}\to\mathcal{X}
.

Theorem 4.9

(
[
56
, Theorem 7]
)
.

Consider a discrete memoryless channel
P_{Y|X}
. Then the zero-error capacity with feedback of
P_{Y|X}
is given by

\displaystyle C_{0f}\left(P_{Y|X}\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{P_{X}}I_{0}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\sup_{P_{X}}\min_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\log\!\left(\sum_{x:P_{Y|X}(y|x)>0}\!\!P_{X}(x)\right)^{-1}\!.

(57)

4.2.3
Sphere-Packing and Random Coding Exponents

The sphere-packing exponent is defined for a rate
R
, prior
P_{X}
and conditional distribution
P_{Y|X}
by

\displaystyle E_{sp}(R,P_{X},P_{Y|X})=\!\!\!\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q_{Y|X}:\\
I(P_{X},Q_{Y|X})\leq R\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\!D(Q_{Y|X}||P_{Y|X}|P_{X}).

(58)

The
\sup_{P_{X}}E_{sp}(R,P_{X},P_{Y|X})
yields an upper bound on the optimal exponent of the probability of error in channel coding, and it can be obtained through the following optimization involving Sibson capacities of order
\alpha\in(0,1]
:

Theorem 4.10

(
[
16
, Theorem 8]
[
66
]
)
.

Given a discrete memoryless channel
P_{Y|X}
and
R\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\sup_{P_{X}}E_{sp}(R,P_{X},P_{Y|X})=\sup_{\rho\geq 0}\left\{\rho\sup_{P_{X}}I_{\frac{1}{1+\rho}}(X,Y)-\rho R\right\}.

(59)

Restricting the range of the optimization to consider
I_{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in[1/2,1]
yields the random-coding exponent, which lower-bounds the optimal exponent of the probability of error in channel coding and is defined as

\displaystyle E_{r}(R,P_{X},P_{Y|X})

\displaystyle=\min_{Q_{Y|X}}D(Q_{Y|X}||P_{Y|X}|P_{X})+[I(P_{X},Q_{Y|X})-R]^{+}.

(60)

Then,

Theorem 4.11

(
[
16
, Theorem 8]
[
67
, Corollary 3]
)
.

Given a discrete memoryless channel
P_{Y|X}
and
R\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\sup_{P_{X}}E_{r}(R,P_{X},P_{Y|X})=\sup_{\rho\in[0,1]}\left\{\rho\sup_{P_{X}}I_{\frac{1}{1+\rho}}(X,Y)-\rho R\right\}.

(61)

A more comprehensive treatment of the connection between Rényi information measures and error exponents is given by Verdú
[
68
]
. For a fixed input distribution, the sphere-packing and random-coding exponent functions are represented in terms of the Augustin–Csiszár mutual information (denoted by
I_{\alpha}^{C}
herein), with the optimizations ranging over
\rho\geq 0
and
\rho\in[0,1]
, respectively. Maximizing over the input distribution and using the equality between the Augustin–Csiszár and Sibson capacities recovers the expressions in
Equations

59
and
61
. Verdú further treats general alphabets and average input-cost constraints, thereby connecting Gallager’s modified
E_{0}
formulation, the large-deviation formulation in terms of conditional relative entropy, and the formulation based on Rényi information measures. In particular, a method is given for computing the cost-constrained Augustin–Csiszár capacity through an optimization involving Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information, with applications to Gaussian and additive exponential-noise channels.

4.3
Information Leakage Measures

Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information of order
\infty
and Sibson capacities of order
\alpha\in(1,\infty)
have been endowed with operational characterizations in the context of information leakage measures. In particular, Issa
et al.

[
53
]
define an information leakage measure in a “guessing” framework which turns out to equal Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information of order
\infty
. This framework was extended by Liao
et al.

[
51
]
who defined a family of information leakage measures that turn out to correspond to Sibson capacities of order
\alpha\in(1,\infty)
.

4.3.1
Maximal Leakage

For a given pair of random variables
(X,Y)
, Issa
et al.

[
53
]
define “maximal leakage” in the terms of the multiplicative advantage of guessing any function of
X
after observing
Y
:

Definition 4.2

(Maximal Leakage
[
53
, Definition 1]
)
.

Given a joint distribution
P_{XY}
on alphabets
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, the Maximal Leakage from
X
to
Y
is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(X\!\!\to\!\!Y)=\sup_{U:U-X-Y}\log\frac{\sup_{P_{\hat{U}|Y}}{\rm\mathbf{Pr}}(U=\hat{U})}{\max_{u}P_{U}(u)},

(62)

where the supremum is over all
U
taking values in a finite, but arbitrary, alphabet.

Maximal Leakage is motivated by the setting where
X
represents private/secret information (e.g., password, secret key, etc.), and
Y
represents information available to an adversary (typically through a “side-channel”). The adversary attempts to guess
X
, or some function of
X
which is not necessarily known to the system designer. As such, Maximal Leakage adopts a (conservative) worst-case approach by considering the maximum multiplicative guessing advantage, over all such (randomized) functions
[
53
]
.
It turns out that:

Theorem 4.12

(
[
53
, Theorem 1]
)
.

Given a joint distribution
P_{XY}
, the Maximal Leakage from
X
to
Y
is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(X\!\!\to\!\!Y)=I_{\infty}(X,Y).

(63)

Interestingly, another characterization was obtained by Braun
et al.

[
69
]
who consider the advantage of guessing
X
itself from
Y
, maximized over the input distribution
P_{X}
. That is,

Theorem 4.13

(
[
69
, Proposition 5.1]
)
.

\displaystyle I_{\infty}(X,Y)=\sup_{P_{X}}\log\frac{\sup_{P_{\hat{X}|Y}}{\rm\mathbf{Pr}}(X=\hat{X})}{\max_{x}P_{X}(x)}.

(64)

Maximal Leakage has subsequently received considerable attention, whether in terms of applications such as hypothesis testing
[
70
]
, generalization error
[
33
]
, biometric security
[
71
]
, and others
[
27
]
; or in terms of extensions such as generalized gain functions
[
52
]
, pointwise maximal leakage
[
72
]
, and Maximal
\alpha
-Leakage
[
51
]
, among others.

More recently, Maximal Leakage has also been connected to Doeblin coefficients, a fundamental tool to study convergence of Markov chains
[
73
]
. In particular, Makur and Singh
[
73
]
introduce the “max-Doeblin” coefficient, which equivalent to
e^{\mathcal{L}(X\to Y)}
. They show
[
73
, Theorem 4]
that max-Doeblin coefficient satisfies a coupling result, which generalizes the classical coupling result for total variation distance
[
74
, Proposition 4.7]
.

4.3.2
Maximal
\alpha
-Leakage

Liao
et al.

[
51
]
extend the Maximal Leakage framework by introducing a loss function parametrized by
\alpha\in(1,\infty)
:

\displaystyle\ell_{\alpha}(x,y,P_{\hat{X}|Y})=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\left(1-P_{\hat{X}|Y}(x|y)^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}\right).

(65)

The setting in which it was introduced assumes a Markov chain
X-Y-\hat{X}
, where
\hat{X}
is an estimator of
X
, and
P_{\hat{X}|Y}
denotes the strategy used to estimate
X
from observations
Y
. In particular,
P_{\hat{X}|Y}(x|y)
represents the probability of correctly estimating
X=x
given
Y=y
. The family of losses
\ell_{\alpha}
captures different ways of measuring the uncertainty associated with the strategy
P_{\hat{X}|Y}
.
In the limit as
\alpha\to 1
, the loss becomes

\ell_{1}(x,y,P_{\hat{X}|Y})=\log\frac{1}{P_{\hat{X}|Y}(x|y)},

while as
\alpha\to\infty
, it reduces to

\ell_{\infty}(x,y,P_{\hat{X}|Y})=1-P_{\hat{X}|Y}(x|y).

If
P_{\hat{X}|Y}(x|y)=1
, both losses (and indeed all
\ell_{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in[1,+\infty]
) are zero. However, if
P_{\hat{X}|Y}(x|y)=0
, the two losses reflect uncertainty very differently:
\ell_{1}(x,y,0)=+\infty
, while
\ell_{\infty}(x,y,0)=1
.
For intermediate values
\alpha\in(1,+\infty)
, the loss
\ell_{\alpha}
interpolates between these two extremes: smaller values of
\alpha
penalize incorrect estimates more heavily. Moreover, leveraging this tunable measure of loss

they define maximal
\alpha
-leakage as:

Definition 4.3

(Maximal
\alpha
-Leakage
[
51
]
)
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{max}}(X\!\!\to\!\!Y)=\sup_{U:U-X-Y}\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\log\frac{\sup_{P_{\hat{X}|Y}}\mathbb{E}\left[P_{\hat{X}|Y}(X|Y)^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}\right]}{\sup_{P_{\hat{X}}}\mathbb{E}\left[P_{\hat{X}}(X)^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}\right]}.

(66)

It turns out that

Theorem 4.14

(
[
51
]
)
.

Given a joint distribution
P_{XY}
and
\alpha\in(1,\infty)
, maximal
\alpha
-leakage is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{max}}(X\!\!\to\!\!Y)=\sup_{P_{\hat{X}}}I_{\alpha}(\hat{X},Y).

(67)

In particular,

\mathcal{L}_{1}^{\mathrm{max}}(X\!\!\to\!\!Y)=I(X;Y).

(68)

Remarkably, Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information again appears as (part of) the answer. The proof of Liao
et al.

[
51
]
, in fact, uses and is stated in terms of Arimoto’s mutual information
[
15
]
, rather than Sibson’s. However, the two notions are closely connected (they are equal when taking the supremum over
P_{X}
). We discuss this relationship and other generalized information measures in the next subsection.

4.4
Connection to Other Generalized Information Measures

There have been several attempts to generalize mutual information along the same lines as Rényi’s generalization of entropy and relative entropy. We summarize noteworthy attempts here and mention connections to Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information, where they exist.

4.4.1
Arimoto mutual information

Arimoto
[
15
]
generalized mutual information based on the following characterization of Shannon mutual information for discrete random variables:

\displaystyle I(X;Y)=H(X)-H(X|Y).

(69)

Definition 4.4

(Arimoto mutual information)
.

Given discrete random variables
X
and
Y
and
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)
, the Arimoto mutual information is defined as

\displaystyle I^{A}_{\alpha}(X,Y)=H_{\alpha}(X)-H_{\alpha}(X|Y).

(70)

It follows directly from
Equations

70
and
4
that

\displaystyle I^{A}_{\alpha}(X,Y)=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\log\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{P_{X}(x)^{\alpha}}{\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}P_{X}(x^{\prime})^{\alpha}}P_{Y|X}(y|x)^{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

(71)

By comparing with the expression given in
Equation

30
for Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information for discrete alphabets, we get

Proposition 4.15
.

Given two discrete random variables
(X,Y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
with joint distribution
P_{XY}=P_{X}P_{Y|X}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)
,

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}^{A}(X,Y)=I_{\alpha}(X_{\alpha},Y),

(72)

where
\displaystyle P_{X_{\alpha}}(x)=\frac{P_{X}(x)^{\alpha}}{\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}P_{X}(x^{\prime})^{\alpha}}
, and
P_{X_{\alpha}Y}=P_{X_{\alpha}}P_{Y|X}
.

That is, Arimoto mutual information is equivalent to Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information evaluated at a “tilted” input distribution
P_{X_{\alpha}}
which is proportional to
P_{X}^{\alpha}
(and the same conditional
P_{Y|X}
). The tilting operation is reversible so that we may also write

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(X,Y)=I^{A}_{\alpha}(X_{\frac{1}{\alpha}},Y),

(73)

where
P_{X_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}
is proportional to
P_{X}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}
.

Corollary 4.16
.

Given two discrete random variables
(X,Y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)
, if
P_{X}
is uniform, then

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(X,Y)=I_{\alpha}^{A}(X,Y).

(74)

Moreover, for fixed
P_{Y|X}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{P_{X}}I_{\alpha}(X,Y)=\sup_{P_{X}}I_{\alpha}^{A}(X,Y).

(75)

One can derive several properties of Arimoto mutual information using the equality in
Proposition

4.15
, including: non-negativity (with
I_{\alpha}^{A}(X,Y)=0
if and only if
X
and
Y
are independent), additivity, post-processing inequality (i.e.,
I_{\alpha}^{A}(X,Z)\leq I_{\alpha}^{A}(X,Y)
if
X-Y-Z
is a Markov chain), invariance under injective transformations, as well as convexity/concavity properties with respect to
P_{Y|X}
(for a fixed
P_{X}
). Moreover,

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}^{A}(X,Y)=I_{\alpha}(X_{\alpha},Y)\leq H_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(X_{\alpha})=H_{\alpha}(X),

(76)

where the last equality follows from simply plugging in the relevant expressions. It is worth noting, however, that
I_{\alpha}^{A}(X,Y)
does not satisfy the “pre-processing” inequality (also called linkage inequality). That is,

Proposition 4.17

(
[
60
, Proposition 1]
)
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)
, there exists a triple of random variables
(X,Y,Z)
satisfying the Markov chain
X-Y-Z
and
I_{\alpha}^{A}(X,Z)>I_{\alpha}^{A}(Y,Z)
.

4.4.2
Csiszár mutual information

Csiszár
[
11
]
considered the following characterization of Shannon mutual information

\displaystyle I(X;Y)=\min_{Q_{Y}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}\left[D\left(P_{Y|X}(.|X)\|Q_{Y}\right)\right]

(77)

and generalized it as follows:

Definition 4.5

(Csiszár mutual information)
.

Given a joint distribution
P_{XY}
on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)
, the Csiszár mutual information is defined as

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}^{C}(X,Y)=\min_{Q_{Y}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}\left[D_{\alpha}\left(P_{Y|X}(.|X)\|Q_{Y}\right)\right].

(78)

As opposed to Sibson and Arimoto mutual information, there is no known closed-form formula for
I_{\alpha}^{C}(X,Y)
. However, maximizing
I_{\alpha}^{C}(X,Y)
over
P_{X}
again yields the same result:

Proposition 4.18
.

Fix
P_{Y|X}
. Then for all
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)
,

\displaystyle\sup_{P_{X}}I_{\alpha}^{C}(X,Y)=\sup_{P_{X}}I_{\alpha}(X,Y)=\sup_{P_{X}}I^{A}_{\alpha}(X,Y).

(79)

Furthermore, Sibson and Csiszár mutual information obey a strict ordering:

Proposition 4.19
.

For all
\alpha>1
and all distributions
P_{XY}
,

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(X,Y)\geq I_{\alpha}^{C}(X,Y).

(80)

Moreover, for all
\alpha<1
and all distributions
P_{XY}
,

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(X,Y)\leq I_{\alpha}^{C}(X,Y).

(81)

4.4.3
Lapidoth-Pfister mutual information

Lapidoth and Pfister considered a minimization over product-of-marginals distributions
Q_{X}Q_{Y}
instead of over
Q_{Y}
only (as done in Sibson’s definition), so that:

Definition 4.6

(Lapidoth-Pfister mutual information
[
58
]
)
.

Given a joint distribution
P_{XY}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)
, the Lapidoth-Pfister mutual information is defined as

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}^{LP}(X,Y)=\min_{Q_{X}Q_{Y}}D_{\alpha}(P_{XY}||Q_{X}Q_{Y}).

(82)

It follows directly from the definitions that

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}^{LP}(X,Y)\leq I_{\alpha}(X,Y).

(83)

However, the capacities coincide for
\alpha>1
:

Proposition 4.20

(
[
59
, Theorem V.1]
)
.

Given a conditional distribution
P_{Y|X}
and
\alpha>1
,

\displaystyle\sup_{P_{X}}I_{\alpha}^{LP}(X,Y)=\sup_{P_{X}}I_{\alpha}(X,Y).

(84)

Moreover, Lapidoth-Pfister mutual information possesses several desirable properties, including: it is zero if and only if
X
and
Y
are independent, it satisfies the data processing inequality, it is bounded by
\log|\mathcal{X}|
, and it is additive over independent pairs. However, except for some special cases, there is no closed form for
I_{\alpha}^{LP}(X,Y).

5
Variational Representations

Variational representations of information measures have many applications and implications. The most notable one is probably Donsker-Varadhan’s representation of the Kullback-Leibler divergence
[
18
,
19
]
. This has been pivotal for linking the Kullback-Leibler divergence to hypothesis testing
[
28
]
, Wasserstein distances (and consequently, the concentration of measure phenomenon)
[
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
]
, generalization error of learning algorithms
[
46
,
47
]
, etc. In particular, these representations highlight the connection between information measures and spaces of functions and provide a powerful and elegant approach to linking information measures to various problems of applied nature (
e.g.
, compression, guessing, testing, etc.
[
25
]
).
We will now provide a variational representation of Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information.
In order to do so, one can undertake various paths:

•

leverage the connection between Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information and Rényi divergence (and, consequently, the connection between Rényi divergence and the Kullback-Leibler divergence
[
9
, Theorem 30]
). This is the approach undertaken to prove
Theorem

5.2
;

•

leverage the connection between Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information and norms, like we did in
Section

3.2
. This approach leads to
Theorem

5.4
.

In particular, one can see
Theorem

5.4
as a corollary of
Theorem

5.2
. However, given the power of applicability of
Theorem

5.4
and the insightful direct proof, it is stated as an independent result. Moreover, from
Theorem

5.4
one can recover most of the results that employ Sibson’s mutual information in applied settings:

•

exponential concentration bounds for functions of random variables when the function is not independent with respect to the variables (see
Section

6.1.1
), with consequent applications in learning theory settings (see
Section

6.2
);

•

relationships with the error exponent in hypothesis testing settings (see
Section

6.3
);

•

bounds on the expected generalization error of learning algorithms via transportation-cost like inequalities (see
Section

6.4
and
Corollary

6.8
);

•

applications in estimation theory via the construction of a generalized Fano’s method (see
Section

7.2
) and in Bayesian Risk Settings(see
Section

7.3
).

This clearly shows the potential of these representations.

5.1
Sibson
\alpha
-Mutual Information and Kullback-Leibler Divergence

By analogy to
Lemma

2.1
, Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information can be connected to Kullback-Leibler divergence. This leads to the first variational representation we introduce:

Theorem 5.1
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty),

\displaystyle(1-\alpha)I_{\alpha}(X,Y)=\min_{R_{XY}}\left\{\alpha D(R_{XY}\|P_{XY})+(1-\alpha)D(R_{XY}\|P_{X}R_{Y})\right\}

(85)

Hence,
(1-\alpha)I_{\alpha}(X,Y)
is the minimum of linear functions of
\alpha
so it is concave in
\alpha
.
Equality is attained by selecting

R_{XY}\propto P_{XY}^{\alpha}(P_{X}Q_{Y}^{\star})^{1-\alpha},

where
Q_{Y}^{\star}
is given in
Equation

27
, and we note that for this joint distribution, the marginal distribution on
Y
is given by
Q_{Y}^{\star}.

The proof of this result is given in
Section

D.1
.

In
[
55
]
a variational representation for
I_{\alpha}
in terms of Shannon information measures was also derived. In particular, the author introduces a variational representation for
I_{\alpha}(P,W)
(therein denoted as
K_{\alpha}(P,W)
) in terms of maxima of sums of the Kullback-Leibler divergence and Csiszár’s
\alpha
-mutual information (which, in turn, can be expressed as a maximum of a sum between Shannon’s mutual information and KL).
See
[
55
, Theorem 1]
.

5.2
Variational Representations of Sibson
\alpha
-Mutual Information

Theorem 5.2
.

Assume that
P_{XY}\ll P_{X}P_{Y}
. For
\alpha>1,
we have

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\sup_{f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}}\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}\left[e^{(\alpha-1)f(X,Y)}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{R_{Y}^{*}}\left[\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}\left[e^{\alpha f(X,Y)}\right]\right],

(86)

where

\displaystyle R_{Y}^{*}(y)

\displaystyle\propto\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}\left[P_{Y|X}(y|X)e^{(\alpha-1)f(X,y)}\right],

(87)

\displaystyle\frac{dR_{Y}^{*}}{dP_{Y}}(y)

\displaystyle\propto\mathbb{E}_{P_{X|Y=y}}\left[e^{(\alpha-1)f(X,y)}\right].

(88)

For
0<\alpha<1,
we have

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\sup_{f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}}\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}\left[e^{(\alpha-1)f(X,Y)}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{Q_{Y}^{\star}}\left[\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}\left[e^{\alpha f(X,Y)}\right]\right],

(89)

where
Q_{Y}^{\star}
is given in
Equation

27
.
In both cases, equality is attained by selecting

\displaystyle e^{\alpha f(x,y)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\frac{dP_{XY}}{dP_{X}P_{Y}}(x,y)\right)^{\alpha}}{\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}\left[\left(\frac{dP_{XY}}{dP_{X}P_{Y}}(X,y)\right)^{\alpha}\right]}.

(90)

The proof of this theorem can be found in
Section

D.2
.

Remark 5.1
.

Taking the limit of
Equation

86
(or
Equation

89
) as
\alpha\rightarrow 1
leads to the slightly strengthened Donsker-Varadhan representation of mutual information:

\displaystyle I(X;Y)

\displaystyle=\sup_{f}\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}[f(X,Y)]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{Y}}\left[\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}\left[e^{f(X,Y)}\right]\right].

(91)

We note that this formula can be readily obtained by applying the Donsker-Varadhan representation separately for every
y,
and then averaging over
Y.

The variational representation given in
Theorem

5.2
can be complemented by a second representation in a slightly different spirit. The inequality part of this second representation is, in fact, a weakening of
Theorem

5.2
through Jensen’s inequality.
Specifically, the following variational representation is established.

Theorem 5.3
.

Assume that
P_{XY}\ll P_{X}P_{Y}
. For
\alpha>1,
we have

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\sup_{f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}}\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}\left[e^{(\alpha-1)f(X,Y)}\right]-\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}R^{*}_{Y}}\left[e^{\alpha f(X,Y)}\right],

(92)

where
R_{Y}^{*}
is as in
Theorem

5.2
.
For
\alpha>0,
we have

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\sup_{f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}}\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}\left[e^{(\alpha-1)f(X,Y)}\right]-\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}Q_{Y}^{\star}}\left[e^{\alpha f(X,Y)}\right],

(93)

where
Q_{Y}^{\star}
is given in
Equation

27
.
In both cases, equality is attained by selecting the function
f(x,y)
as in
Equation

90
.

The proof of this theorem can be found in
Section

D.3
.

Remark 5.2
.

Notice that
Equation

93
can also be seen as a consequence of the variational representation of Rényi
\alpha
-divergence (
Lemma

2.2
). In fact, since
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)=D_{\alpha}(P_{XY}\|P_{X}Q_{Y}^{\star})
, one can use the variational representation of
D_{\alpha}
in order to recover
Equation

93
. To the best of our knowledge,
Equation

92
instead cannot be recovered with this approach and thus we provide an explicit proof in Appendix
D.3
.

In extension to the considerations above, we now establish a third variational representation, which is, in many ways, more compact and likely more useful than the previous two theorems. So far, we have consistently refined the various variational representations in order to render them more applicable in settings of interest. In going from
Theorem

5.1
to
Theorem

5.2
we leveraged the variational representation of Sibson
\alpha
-MI in terms of measures and KL divergences to obtain one involving expected values of functions. In many applied settings, one is indeed typically interested in bounding expected values of functions (probabilities of events, expected errors, etc.). Thus,
Theorem

5.2
is arguably easier to use than
Theorem

5.1
in said settings. Then, we weakened
Theorem

5.2
in order to achieve
Theorem

5.3
, approaching a form that is closer to what one could retrieve when providing a variational representation for Shannon’s mutual information via Donsker-Varadhan’s result. In particular, taking the limit of
\alpha\to 1
in
Theorem

5.3
one recovers exactly Donsker-Varadhan’s representation of mutual information,
i.e.
:

I(X;Y)=\sup_{f}\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}\left[f(X,Y)\right]-\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}P_{Y}}\left[e^{f(X,Y)}\right].

(94)

However,
Theorem

5.3
is still not immediate to employ in practical settings as one would have to compute
R_{Y}^{\star}
or
Q_{Y}^{\star}
. We can thus weaken the result further in order to obtain yet another variational representation that still preserves the asymmetry between
X
and
Y
and can be easily and successfully employed in a variety of practical settings, as we will see in the following sections.
In particular, this representation can be seen as a corollary of
Theorem

5.3
(see
Section

D.3.1
for the corresponding derivation), but it can also be directly proved in an independent and insightful fashion via Hölder’s inequality (see
Section

D.4
). Therefore, we prefer to state it as a separate theorem.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
\alpha>1
and assume that
P_{XY}\ll P_{X}P_{Y}
, then:

\exp\left(\!\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}I_{\alpha}(X,Y)\!\right)\!\!=\!\!\!\!\!\sup_{g:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}}\!\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}[g(X,Y)]}{\max_{y}\left(\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}[g^{\beta}(X,y)]\right)^{\frac{1}{\beta}}},

(95)

where
\beta=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}
.
Moreover, if
0<\alpha<1
then:

\displaystyle\exp\left(\!\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}I_{\alpha}(X,Y)\!\right)\!\!=\!\!\!\!\!\inf_{g:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}}\!\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}[g(X,Y)]}{\min_{y}\left(\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}[g^{\beta}(X,y)]\right)^{\frac{1}{\beta}}}.

(96)

Taking the limit of
\alpha\to\infty
one recovers a novel variational representation for Maximal Leakage, stated below for ease of reference:

\exp\left(\mathcal{L}\left(X\!\!\to\!\!Y\right)\right)=\sup_{g:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R^{+}}}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}[g(X,Y)]}{\max_{y}\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}[g(X,y)]}.

(97)

As indicated by this equivalence,
Theorem

5.4
is similar to formulations of privacy and security metrics. Indeed, it states that
I_{\alpha}
, for
\alpha>1
, is the multiplicative gain induced by observing
Y
, optimized over all gain functions (the case of
\alpha<1
can be seen as a multiplicative decrease over all non-negative cost functions). Note that the optimization over
g
subsumes the setup of guessing
X
from
Y
by choosing
g(X,Y)=\mathbf{1}\left\{X=\arg\max_{x^{\prime}}P_{X|Y}(x^{\prime}|Y)\right\}

(where ties should be broken, but can be broken arbitrarily). It also subsumes the setup of guessing any (possibly randomized) function of
X
by noting that
U-X-Y-\hat{U}
is a Markov chain implies
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)\geq I_{\alpha}(U,\hat{U})
and choosing
g(U,\hat{U})=\mathbf{1}\left\{U=\hat{U}\right\}
. This is in line with other recent variational approaches to privacy in general
[
75
,
76
]
, and using Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information in particular
[
77
,
78
]
.

It is worth noting here that the numerator in Theorem V.4 is an
L_{1}
-norm, whereas the denominator is an
L_{\beta}
-norm. This is akin to hypercontractivity bounds (where different norms are used at the input and output). As the
L_{\beta}
-norm is greater than the
L_{1}
-norm (
\beta>1
), this choice yields a smaller value of the optimization,
\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}I_{\alpha}(X,Y)
instead of
\mathcal{L}\left(X\!\!\to\!\!Y\right)
. As such, we get a trade-off between the choice of
\beta
and the induced left-hand side.

However, the function
g^{\star}
achieving equality in
Equation

97
is not obvious from the proof of
Theorem

5.4
and deserves an explicit treatise. One has that, in the case of
Equation

97
, equality is achieved by the following function:

g^{\star}(x,y)=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{argmax}\\
x\end{subarray}{\frac{dP_{XY}}{dP_{X}P_{Y}}(x,y)}\right\}}}{P_{X}\left(\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{argmax}\\
X\end{subarray}{\frac{dP_{XY}}{dP_{X}P_{Y}}(X,y)}\right\}\right)}.~

(98)

The argument to see why
Equation

98
is true follows from essentially re-writing
Equation

90
as follows:

g^{\star}(x,y)^{\beta}=\frac{\left(\frac{dP_{XY}}{dP_{X}P_{Y}}(x,y)\right)^{\alpha}}{\left\lVert\frac{dP_{XY}}{dP_{X}P_{Y}}(X,y)\right\rVert^{\alpha}_{L^{\alpha}(P_{X})}}.

(99)

One can then take the limit of
\alpha\to\infty
and retrieve
Equation

98
. Due to the presence of the power
\alpha
, the denominator in
Equation

99
no longer converges to an
\esssup
. However, for a given
y
the function
g^{\star}
behaves (due to the presence of the norm in the denominator and the fact that the norm is taken with respect to a probability measure)
as a normalized indicator function of
\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}
.
Another interesting consideration comes from taking the limit of
\alpha\to 1
. Clearly, doing so naively in
Equation

95
leads to a trivial bound of the form:
\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}[g(X,Y)]\leq\max_{y}\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}[g(X,Y)]
. However, massaging the expressions in
Theorem

5.4
and re-parametrising the set of functions one obtains the following:

\begin{split}\frac{I_{\alpha}(X,Y)}{\alpha}&=\sup_{f}\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}\left[e^{(\alpha-1)f}\right]-\frac{1}{\alpha}\log\max_{y}\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}\left[e^{\alpha f}\right].\end{split}

(100)

Taking then the limit of
\alpha\to 1
in
Equation

100
leads to the following:

I(X;Y)=\sup_{f}\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}\left[f(X,Y)\right]-\log\max_{y}\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}\left[e^{f(X,Y)}\right].

(101)

Equation

101
is a point-wise worse variational representation for mutual information with respect to the one that can be obtained with Donsker-Varadhan (see
Equation

94
).
Equality is achieved in both cases by
f^{\star}=\log\frac{dP_{XY}}{dP_{X}P_{Y}}
. Consequently, parametrised lower-bounds on the mutual information that one could achieve from
Equation

95
do not represent a suitable parametrised generalisation of expressions that one could obtain via
Equation

94
, as in the limit they would provide for a smaller lower-bound.
Also, the limit of
\alpha\to\infty
in
Equation

100
leads to a trivial result:
\esssup_{P_{XY}(x,y)}f(x,y)\leq\esssup_{P_{X}P_{Y}}f(x,y)
which follows from the absolute-continuity constraints necessary in order to define
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)
.

Remark 5.3
.

Recent research has shown growing interest in estimating information measures via neural networks
[
79
,
80
]
. To leverage
Theorem

5.4
for deriving an estimator of
I_{\alpha}
using polynomials or neural networks, it is essential to understand when the function achieving equality—namely the one in
Equation

98
or
Equation

99
—is bounded or smooth.

The function in
Equation

98
is generally not smooth. In fact, when the
\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}
,
x^{\star}
, is unique, a discontinuity appears around
x^{\star}
. However, it remains bounded if
P_{X}
is bounded away from zero (e.g., if the support of
X
is compact).

For the function in
Equation

99
, boundedness and smoothness depend on the Radon-Nikodym derivative. If
\left\lVert\frac{dP_{XY}}{dP_{X}P_{Y}}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(P_{X})}
is finite, the function is bounded. In particular, boundedness holds if there exists a constant
\kappa
such that for every measurable set
E\subseteq\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, we have
P_{XY}(E)\leq\kappa P_{X}P_{Y}(E)
.

Regarding smoothness, if
P_{X}
,
P_{Y}
, and
P_{XY}
admit smooth, bounded densities with respect to a common dominating measure (e.g., the Lebesgue measure), then the Radon-Nikodym derivative is smooth. This is the case, for instance, when
P_{X}
and
P_{Y}
have smooth and bounded densities and
Y=h(X)+N
, where
h
is smooth and
N
is independent of
X
and also has a smooth density.

Empirically, it appears feasible to estimate
I_{\alpha}
using
Equations

95
and
96
. Although the additional maximization in
Equation

95
increases complexity compared to Shannon’s mutual information (see
Equation

94
), this can be handled via a soft-max approximation, introducing a tunable parameter.

A simple architecture, similar to that used in
[
80
]
for Rényi divergences, a single-hidden-layer network with 64 hidden units and about 257 parameters, effectively approximates
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)
when
X
and
Y
are correlated Gaussians. The network used in the experiments was trained using Adam on
10^{4}
samples with mini-batches of size 512.

Remark 5.4
.

Neural network estimators of
Equation

95
(
Theorem

5.4
) can be leveraged for auditing differentially-private mechanisms
[
81
,
82
,
83
,
84
]
: Since
\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}I_{\alpha}(X,Y)
lower-bounds differential privacy, finding a function
g
such that the ratio in
Equation

95
exceeds
\varepsilon
implies a differential privacy violation. This may yield more robust auditing compared to estimators using mutual information
[
82
]
, as
I_{\alpha}
is larger. Developing and testing such auditing mechanisms is left as future work.

in the settings described a the beginning of

and show the versatility and potential it brings.

6
Applications: Concentration of Measure

Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information represents a fundamental object sitting at the intersection of Information Theory, Probability Theory, and Functional Analysis. As such, it is possible to provide fundamental results in concentration of measure and hypothesis testing settings. In particular, Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information allows us to link the expected values of a function of two random variables
X,Y
when the random variables are dependent, to the expected value of the same function under independence. This connection allows us to employ the measure in:

•

providing concentration results extending McDiarmid/Hoeffding’s inequality to functions of random variables in settings where the function depends on the random variables themselves. The concentration result can then in turn be used to provide bounds on the probability of having a large generalization error in Learning Theory settings;

•

composite hypothesis testing settings in which one is testing for independence but one of the two marginals is not fixed.

Similar results can be provided when one considers the conditional version of Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information (see
Section

8
).

6.1
Dependence vs Independence

6.1.1
Case
\alpha>1

An interesting feature of Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information comes from taking the norm-inspired perspective (
Section

3.2
) along with the Variational representations (
Section

5
). In particular, one can see
I_{\alpha}
as nested norms of the Radon-Nikodym derivative. For this section, consider
Equation

45
. Leveraging then
Theorem

5.4
, given a function
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
and two random variables
X,Y
one can prove results relating:

•

the expected value of
f
under a joint measure
P_{XY}
;

•

Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information between
X
and
Y
;

•

the nested norm of
f
under the product of the marginals
P_{X}P_{Y}
;

Part of the results presented in this section has already appeared in
[
33
]
.
One example of such a result is given in the following theorem:

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
P_{XY}
be a joint measure. Let
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a
P_{XY}
-measurable function, then one has that for every
\alpha>1
and denoting with
\beta=\alpha/(\alpha-1)
,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}\left[f(X,Y)\right]&\leq\max_{y}\mathbb{E}^{\frac{1}{\beta}}_{P_{X}}\left[f^{\beta}(X,y)\right]\exp\left(\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}I_{\alpha}(X,Y)\right).\end{split}

(102)

In particular, if one considers
\alpha\to\infty
one recovers the following:

\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}\left[f(X,Y)\right]\leq\max_{y}\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}\left[f(X,y)\right]\exp\left(\mathcal{L}\left(X\!\!\to\!\!Y\right)\right).

(103)

This result is particularly interesting when one selects
f
to be the indicator function of an event, which leads to the following corollary:

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
E
be a measurable event, then one has that for every
\alpha>1
and denoting with
\beta=\alpha/(\alpha-1)
,

P_{XY}(E)\leq\max_{y}\left(P_{X}(E_{y})\right)^{\frac{1}{\beta}}\exp\left(\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}I_{\alpha}(X,Y)\right).

(104)

In particular, if one considers
\alpha\to\infty
one recovers the following:

P_{XY}(E)\leq\max_{y}P_{X}(E_{y})\exp\left(\mathcal{L}\left(X\!\!\to\!\!Y\right)\right),

(105)

where, given
E\subseteq\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, one has that for a given
y\in\mathcal{Y}

E_{y}=\{x:(x,y)\in E\}
.

We will now provide three examples in which the bound expressed in
Equation

105
, is tight. The first setting represents an example in which the two random variables
X
and
Y
are independent and the bound is met with equality:

Example 6.1

(independent case)
.

Suppose that
E
is such that
P_{X}(E_{y})=\zeta
for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. In that case we have that, if
X
and
Y
are independent then
\mathcal{L}\left(X\!\!\to\!\!Y\right)=0
and:

\zeta=P_{XY}(E)\leq\max_{y}P_{X}(E_{y})\exp(\mathcal{L}\left(X\!\!\to\!\!Y\right))=\zeta.

(106)

The second example shows how even when the random variables are very dependent (
e.g.
,
X=Y
), the bound can still be met with equality:

Example 6.2

(strongly dependent case)
.

Consider the example presented in
[
85
]
: suppose
X=Y\sim\mathcal{U}([n])
then we have that
\mathcal{L}\left(X\!\!\to\!\!Y\right)=\log n
and if
E=\{(x,y)\in[n]\times[n]|x=y\}
then,

1=P_{XY}(E)\leq\frac{1}{n}\cdot n=1.

(107)

The last example is a classical information-theoretic setting in which the random variables are not independent nor one a deterministic function of the other:

Example 6.3
.

Suppose
(X,Y)
is a doubly-symmetric binary source (DSBS) with parameter
p
for some
p<1/2
i.e.,
X\sim
Ber
(1/2)
,
P_{Y|X}
is determined by a BSC
(p)
and, consequently,
Y\sim
Ber
(1/2)
. Let
E=\{(x,y):x=y\}
. Then,

1-p=P_{XY}(E)\leq\frac{1}{2}(2(1-p))=1-p.

(108)

The above examples essentially show that when the worst-case behavior (
i.e.
,
\max_{y}P_{X}(E_{y})
) matches with the average-case behavior (i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{P_{Y}}[P_{X}(E_{y})]=P_{X}P_{Y}(E)
) then the bound involving maximal leakage can be tight regardless of the degree of dependence between the random variables.
Moreover, the following proposition shows that the bound is tight in the following strong sense: if we want to bound the ratio
P_{XY}(E)/(\esssup_{P_{Y}}P_{X}(E_{y}))
as a function of
P_{Y|X}
only (i.e., independently of
P_{X}
and
E
), then
\exp\{\mathcal{L}\left(X\!\!\to\!\!Y\right)\}
is the best bound we could get (proof in
Section

D.5
):

Proposition 6.3
.

Given finite alphabets
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, and a fixed conditional distribution
P_{Y|X}
, then there exist
P_{X}
and
E
such that
Equation

105
is met with equality. That is,

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\max_{E\subseteq\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\max_{P_{X}}}\log\frac{P_{XY}(E)}{\esssup_{P_{Y}}P_{X}(E_{Y})}=\mathcal{L}\left(X\!\!\to\!\!Y\right).

(109)

Notably, the given
(P_{X},E)
which achieve the maximum is such that
P_{X}(x)>0\Rightarrow x\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{y}P_{Y|X}(y|x)
for some
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, and
P_{X}(E_{y})
is constant.

Unfortunately, a similar result does not seem to hold for
I_{\alpha}
with
\alpha<\infty
.

6.2
Concentration under Dependence and a Learning Setting

The results presented in the previous section lend themselves to applications to concentration of measure and, consequently, to applications in Statistical Learning settings. This consideration represents the backbone of
[
33
]
. Consider a classical concentration of measure setting: let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent random variables over a metric space
(\mathcal{X},d)
and
f:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
a
\kappa
-Lipschitz function with respect to
d

i.e.
,

\sup_{x^{n}\neq\hat{x}^{n}}\frac{|f(x^{n})-f(\hat{x}^{n})|}{d(x^{n},\hat{x}^{n})}=\kappa.

(111)

One can typically prove that, under assumptions over
P_{X_{i}}
that given a constant
\eta>0

\mathbb{P}(|f(X^{n})-\mathbb{E}[f(X^{n})]|\geq\eta)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{n\eta}{c\kappa^{2}}\right),

(112)

where
c
is a constant typically depending on the approach utilized to prove the concentration result (for a survey, see
[
32
]
). A natural question (arising from Statistical Learning settings) is:
can one prove concentration even if
f
is not independent of
X^{n}
? In particular, in Statistical Learning one typically assumes that one has access to a learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{Z}^{n}\to\mathcal{H}
with
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
which takes as input a sequence of iid samples
Z_{i}\sim P_{Z}
and produces as an output a
hypothesis

h\in\mathcal{H}
. The hypothesis is typically a function
h:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}

e.g.
,
h
is a classifier that takes as input a picture and produces as an outcome the corresponding label (the species of the animal or the category of the object one is trying to classify). For an extensive introduction to the topic, the reader is referred to
[
86
]
. One then typically wants to assess the performances of such a hypothesis
h
. A common way of doing so is considering the notion of
generalization error
. Consider a loss function
\ell:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
one can define the empirical risk of
h
on
Z^{n}=(X,Y)^{n}
as follows :
L_{Z^{n}}(h)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(h(X),Y)
. Similarly, one can define the expected risk
L_{P_{Z}}(h)=\mathbb{E}_{P_{Z}}[\ell(h(X),Y)]
. The generalization error of
\mathcal{A}
is then defined as follows:

\text{gen-err}(\mathcal{A},Z^{n})=L_{Z^{n}}(\mathcal{A}(X^{n}))-L_{P_{Z}}(\mathcal{A}(X^{n})).

(113)

One can then try to bound the generalization error in expectation, as it is done in a large body of the literature (
[
86
,
47
,
87
]
etc.). Alternatively, one can try to bound
Equation

113
in probability. In this case, one has the following probability:

\mathbb{P}(|\text{gen-err}(\mathcal{A},Z^{n})|\geq\eta)

(114)

and one can see
Equation

114
as an instance of the left-hand side of
Equation

112
with
f=\mathcal{A}(Z^{n})
. Hence, in this case, one has that
f
is
not
independent of
Z^{n}
. However, one can leverage the results presented in
Section

6.1
and, in particular,
Corollary

6.2
to provide a bound in this particular setting:

Corollary 6.5
.

In a Learning setting as the one described above, if
\ell(h(x),y)=\mathbbm{1}_{h(x)\neq y}
and for
\alpha>1
, one has the following:

\mathbb{P}(|\text{gen-err}(\mathcal{A},Z^{n})|\geq\eta)\leq 2^{\frac{1}{\beta}}\exp\left(-\frac{n(\alpha-1)}{\alpha}\left(2\eta^{2}-\frac{I_{\alpha}(Z^{n},\mathcal{A}(Z^{n}))}{n}\right)\right).

(115)

In particular, if
\alpha\to\infty
one recovers the following:

\mathbb{P}(|\text{gen-err}(\mathcal{A},Z^{n})|\geq\eta)\leq 2\exp\left(-n\left(2\eta^{2}-\frac{\mathcal{L}\left(Z^{n}\!\!\to\!\!\mathcal{A}(Z^{n})\right))}{n}\right)\right).

(116)

Remark 6.1
.

Corollary

6.5
holds in more general settings than the one described. In particular, the loss can be
\kappa
-Lipschitz with
\kappa<\infty
however the so-called
0-1
loss is a very popular choice in learning settings.

Remark 6.2
.

Re-considering the discussion at the beginning of the Section, one has that
Corollary

6.5
and, in particular
Equation

116
, represent a generalization of concentration inequalities when the function depends on the random variables themselves. Indeed, if
Z^{n}
is independent of
\mathcal{A}(Z^{n})
then one has that
I_{\alpha}(Z^{n},\mathcal{A}(Z^{n}))=0
for every
\alpha
. In particular,
\mathcal{L}\left(Z^{n}\!\!\to\!\!\mathcal{A}(Z^{n})\right)=0
and
Equation

116
specialises in

\mathbb{P}(|\text{gen-err}(Z^{n},\mathcal{A}(Z^{n}))|\geq\eta)\leq 2\exp(-2n\eta^{2})

(117)

i.e., it recovers McDiarmid’s Inequality.

The behaviour of
Equation

147
depends on the behaviour of
I_{\alpha}(Z^{n},\mathcal{A}(Z^{n}))
as a function of
n
. In general, one has that
I_{\alpha}(Z^{n},\mathcal{A}(Z^{n}))
is either finite and at most linear in
n
or infinite.
For instance, if
Z_{i}
is supported over the entire real line (
e.g.
,
Z_{i}
follows a Gaussian distribution) and
\mathcal{A}
is a deterministic mapping, then
I_{\alpha}(Z^{n},\mathcal{A}(Z^{n}))
is infinite.
It is possible to see that in a variety of settings that are of interest in learning settings (differentially-privacy algorithms, variants of Stochastic Gradient Descent algorithms, etc.) that Maximal Leakage (and, consequently, Sibson’s
I_{\alpha}
for every
\alpha<\infty
) are finite and sub-linear in
n
and thus provide exponential concentration in
Equation

115
.

Let us provide some concrete examples. Consider a noisy version of the popular Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) algorithm (cf.
[
33
, Corollary 5]
)
i.e.
, an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
such that

\mathcal{A}(Z^{n})=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{h\in\mathcal{H}}(L_{Z^{n}}(h)+N_{h}),

(118)

where
N_{h}
is independent exponential noise. Assume that
|\mathcal{H}|
is finite (or countably infinite) and indexed by naturals. Let
b_{j}=j^{1.1}/n^{1/3}
be the mean of noise corresponding the hypothesis
h_{j}
. Then

\mathcal{L}(S\to\mathcal{A}(Z^{n}))=\sum_{j=1}^{|\mathcal{H}|}\log\left(1+\frac{1}{b_{i}}\right).

Consequently, in this case one has that:

\mathbb{P}(|\text{gen-err}(Z^{n},\mathcal{A}(Z^{n})\geq\eta)\leq 2\exp\left(-n\left(2\eta^{2}-\frac{11}{n^{2/3}}\right)\right),

which will decay exponentially fast in
n
, for
n
large enough. The idea of adding carefully calibrated exponential noise is inspired by differentially private algorithms. Many such mechanisms exhibit Maximal Leakage that grows sub-linearly with
n
; see
[
33
, Lemmata 3,4,5, Corollary 12]
for formal statements. A broader set of examples is provided in
[
88
]
, where the Maximal Leakage of a class of learning algorithms—referred to as “Noisy, Iterative Algorithms” is shown to be bounded under various assumptions and noise models. This class includes noisy versions of SGD (Stochastic Gradient Descent), such as SGLD (Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics).

Notice that leveraging Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information (as opposed to Shannon’s mutual information) allows to provide generalization error bound for over-parametrized models (
e.g.
, deep neural networks) when trained using SGD-like algorithms even if the number of samples
n
is finite
[
88
]
.

6.3
Hypothesis Testing

Hypothesis testing is another context where both
Corollary

6.2
and
Corollary

8.2
can be utilized. In particular, composite hypothesis testing settings. Suppose one has access to a sequence of
n\geq 1
iid samples
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and suppose that one is interested in knowing, given a certain joint distribution
P_{XY}
, whether:

1.

(X,Y)
are jointly distributed according to
P_{XY}
(
null hypothesis
);

2.

X
is distributed according to
P_{X}
and independent of
Y
.
I.e.
, the couple
(X,Y)
is distributed according to
P_{X}Q_{Y}
where
Q_{Y}
is any probability distribution over the support of
Y
(
alternative hypothesis
)
.

This problem is connected to minimax converse results in channel coding theory and this connection has been formally stated in
[
89
, Section II]
.

In these testing settings,
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)
can be related to the error exponent in the following sense. Assume that, given
n\geq 1
,
T_{n}:\{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
is a decision function that, upon observing the sequence of samples
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
decides whether the null hypothesis is true (
T_{n}(\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\})=0
) or false (
T_{n}(\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\})=1
). One can commit two types of error in this case: the type-1 error, denoted with
p_{n}^{1}
, represent the probability of deciding the alternative hypothesis when the null hypothesis is true
i.e.
,
p_{n}^{1}=P_{XY}(T_{n}(\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\})=1)
and the type-2 error, denoted with
p_{n}^{2}
. In the particular setting advanced here, with type-2 error we will denote the maximum probability (over all the possible choices of
Q_{Y}
) of choosing the null hypothesis when the alternative is true
i.e.
,
p_{n}^{2}=\sup_{Q_{Y}}P_{X}Q_{Y}(T_{n}(\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\})=0).
The relevance of Sibson’s
I_{\alpha}
in this setting comes from asking the following question: if one assumes the type-2 error to decay exponentially fast with the number of samples
n
, what happens to the type-1 error? The reply to this question is encapsulated in the following result:

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
n>0
and
T_{n}:\{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
be a deterministic test, that upon observing the sequence
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
chooses either the null or the alternative hypothesis.
Assume that there exists an
R>0
such that for all
Q_{Y}
one has that
P_{X}Q_{Y}(T_{n}(\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n})=0)\leq\exp(-nR)
. If
\alpha\geq 1
,

\displaystyle 1-p^{1}_{n}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}n(R-I_{\alpha}(X,Y))\right).

(119)

We believe
Theorem

6.6
to be known, however we could not find an appropriate reference in the literature.

Equation

119
essentially states that if one requires an exponential decay for the type-2 error with a rate
R
larger than the Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information the probability of type-1 error will approach
1
. In a more technical sense, it is possible to define the so-called “error-exponent pairs” of the hypothesis testing problem (in the Hoeffding/Blahut sense
[
90
,
67
]
). These pairs can then be fully characterized via
I_{\alpha}
however, such a characterization is outside the scope of this work. For more information, we refer the reader to
[
91
,
58
]
.

6.4
Generalized Transportation-Cost Inequalities

Leveraging the variational representations that have been provided in this work, one can provide novel transportation-cost-like inequalities. In particular, these results can be linked, much like the ones involving the Kullback-Leibler divergence, to concentration of measure. This link to concentration is different from the one established in
Section

6
.
For instance, it is known that one can prove that having a sub-Gaussian-like cumulant generating function
f
(
i.e.
,
\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}P_{Y}}[\exp(\lambda f)]\leq\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}/2
) implies a result akin to Pinsker’s inequality:

\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}[f]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}P_{Y}}[f]\leq\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}I(X;Y)}.~

(120)

This property has been employed to relate the expected generalization error to information measures
[
47
,
27
]
. One can do something similar for
I_{\alpha}
for
\alpha>0
, leveraging
Equation

100
.
Let us briefly introduce an object instrumental for the next result:
\varphi
-Entropies. Given a convex functional
\varphi
and a random variable
X
with measure
\xi
then the
\varphi
-Entropy of
X
under
\xi
is defined as follows
[
92
]
:

\text{Ent}^{\xi}_{\varphi}(X)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\varphi(X)]-\varphi(\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[X])).

(121)

Notice that by Jensen’s inequality
\text{Ent}_{\varphi}(X)\geq 0
. With a slight abuse of notation, we will utilize the same notation even for concave functionals
\varphi
. The positivity of
\text{Ent}_{\varphi}
changes accordingly.
One can thus prove the following (see
Section

D.6
):

Theorem 6.7
.

Let
0<\alpha<1
and
\varphi(x)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log(x)
.
Let
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function such that there exists a constant
c
such that for every
y\in\mathbb{R}
and
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
one has that

\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}[\exp(\kappa f(\cdot,y))]\leq\frac{\kappa^{2}c}{2}-\text{Ent}^{P_{XY}}_{\varphi}(\exp((\alpha-1)\kappa f))

(122)

then

\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}[f]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}P_{Y}}[f]\leq\sqrt{\frac{2cI_{\alpha}(X,Y)}{\alpha}}.

(123)

Notice that, since
0<\alpha<1
,
\varphi
is a convex function and the corresponding Entropy is non-negative. Thus
Equation

122
is more restrictive than sub-Gaussianity but
Equation

123
can be tighter than
Equation

120
. Moreover, if
\alpha\to 1
then
\text{Ent}_{\varphi}^{P_{XY}}(\exp((\alpha-1)\kappa f))\to 0
as well as
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)/\alpha\to I(X;Y)
and then,
Theorem

6.7
boils down to
[
47
, Lemma 1]

i.e.

Equation

120
.

Remark 6.3
.

A different version of
Theorem

6.7
can be proved starting from
Equation

89
. In particular, instead of assuming that
Equation

122
holds for every
y
one can ask for it to hold in expectation with respect to
Q_{Y}^{\star}
as defined in
Equation

27
. This would represent a relaxation of
Equation

122
.

Example 6.4
.

Equation

122
is hard to verify in practice. However, if one has that
|f(X,Y)|\leq M
a.e.,
c=M^{2}(2-\alpha)
satisfies
Equation

122
for every
\kappa
. Indeed, assume that
f
is zero mean with respect to
P_{X}
for every
y
, as subtracting the mean of
f
with respect to
P_{X}
would not affect the argument we are about to present. The Hoeffding’s Lemma yields:

\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}[e^{\kappa f(X,y)}]\leq\frac{\kappa^{2}M^{2}}{2},

(124)

along with

\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}[e^{(\alpha-1)kf(X,Y)}]\leq(\alpha-1)\kappa\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}[f(X,Y)]+\frac{\kappa^{2}(\alpha-1)^{2}M^{2}}{2}.

(125)

Given
Equation

125
, one has that

\displaystyle\text{Ent}^{P_{XY}}_{\varphi}((\exp(\alpha-1)\kappa f))

\displaystyle=\kappa\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}[f(X,Y)]-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}[e^{(\alpha-1)\kappa f(X,Y)}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\kappa^{2}(1-\alpha)M^{2}}{2}.

(126)

Consequently, finding a constant
c
such that the following holds, suffices:

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}[e^{kf(X,y)}]+\text{Ent}^{P_{XY}}_{\varphi}((\exp(\alpha-1)\kappa f))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\kappa^{2}M^{2}}{2}+\frac{\kappa^{2}(1-\alpha)M^{2}}{2}

(127)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\kappa^{2}c}{2}.

(128)

Hence
c
can be chosen such that

c\geq M^{2}(2-\alpha).

(129)

and
Equation

122
would hold.

Clearly
Theorem

6.7
has been set so that one obtains a bound on the difference of expectation of some function
f
with respect to the joint and product of the marginals (see
Equation

123
). This result lends itself well to applications in learning theory. In particular, one can use it to provide a bound on the expected generalization error of a learning algorithm by setting
f
to be the loss function and assuming it satisfies
Equation

122

[
47
,
27
]
.

Corollary 6.8
.

Let
0<\alpha<1
.
Assume that
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\ell(h,Z_{i})
satisfies
Equation

122
under
P_{Z^{n}}
for every
h
in the hypothesis class, then

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\text{gen-err}(\mathcal{A},Z^{n})\right|\right]\leq\sqrt{\frac{2cI_{\alpha}(Z^{n},\mathcal{A}(Z^{n}))}{\alpha}}

(130)

Remark 6.4
.

An analogous result stemming from similar techniques to the ones employed in
Theorem

6.7
would connect the exponential integrability of a function
f
to the exponential integrability of the same function under the joint. I.e., if one can bound the cumulant generating function of
f
for every
y
under
P_{X}
then one can bound the cumulant generating function of
f
under
P_{XY}
with
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)/\alpha
. Said result would be reminiscent of
Corollary

6.2
, where an exponentially decaying bound on
P_{X}(E_{y})
for every
y
and a bounded
I_{\alpha}/\alpha
implies an exponentially decaying bound on
P_{XY}(E)
for every event
E
.

7
Applications: Estimation Theory

Another stream of applications comes from estimation theory. In particular, one can employ Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information to derive a generalization of Fano’s Inequality or in Bayesian estimation settings. In both cases, it is possible to establish impossibility results determining when estimation is not possible regardless of the number of observations one has access to. Moreover, although in a strict sense it does not represent an estimation setting, it is possible to link Sibson
\alpha
-MI to the problem of universal prediction.

7.1
Fano-Type Inequalities

Recall that the binary Rényi divergence is defined for
(p,q)\in[0,1]^{2}
as follows:

\displaystyle d_{\alpha}(p||q)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\left(p^{\alpha}q^{1-\alpha}+(1-p)^{\alpha}(1-q)^{1-\alpha}\right).

(131)

Using the data processing inequality for Rényi divergence, one can derive the following:

Theorem 7.1

(
[
93
,
94
]
)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{X}
is discrete, and let
P_{XY}
be a joint distribution on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
. Let
\varepsilon_{X|Y}
be the optimal probability of error of guessing
X
from
Y
(i.e., the error induced by the MAP rule). Then for any
\alpha\in[0,\infty]
,

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(X,Y)\geq d_{\alpha}\left(\varepsilon_{X|Y}\|1-p^{\star}\right),

(132)

where
p^{\star}=\max_{x}P_{X}(x)
. Moreover,
d_{\alpha}(\varepsilon\|\delta)
is a non-increasing function of
\varepsilon
on the interval
[0,\delta]
. Since
\varepsilon_{X|Y}\leq 1-p^{\star}
, then

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{X|Y}\geq d_{\alpha}^{-1}\left(I_{\alpha}(X,Y)\|1-p^{\star}\right),

(133)

where
d_{\alpha}^{-1}(\cdot\|1-p^{\star})
is the inverse of
f:[0,1-p^{\star}]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
where
f(\varepsilon)=d_{\alpha}(\varepsilon\|1-p^{\star})
.

Remark 7.1
.

Note that, for a fixed
\delta\in[0,1)
,
f(\varepsilon)=d_{\alpha}(\varepsilon,\delta)\leq-\log(1-\delta)
since it is a non-increasing function of
\varepsilon
on the interval
[0,\delta]
. As such, if
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)\geq-\log p^{\star}
, the right-hand side of
Equation

133
will be considered 0 by convention.

Remark 7.2
.

Theorem

7.1
was derived for uniform
P_{X}
by Polyanskiy and Verdú
[
93
, Theorem 5.3]
. The more general version above is due to Rioul
[
94
, Theorem 1]
. For completeness, a proof is included in
Section

E.1
.

A downside of the above inequality is that it does not have a closed form, so it may be difficult to analyze. Alternatively, one could use the variational representation to obtain a parameterized family of bounds by choosing
f=\beta\mathbbm{1}\{x=\hat{x}\}
in
Theorem

5.3
. Remarkably, optimizing the bound over
\beta
may outperform the bound in
Theorem

7.1
(as seen in
Figure

4
).

Theorem 7.2
.

Consider a joint distribution
P_{XY}
where
X
is a discrete random variable. Let
p^{\star}=\max_{x}P_{X}(x)
and
\hat{X}_{\mathrm{MAP}}
be the optimal estimator of
X
given
Y
. Then for all
\alpha>1
and
\gamma>0
,

\displaystyle{\rm\mathbf{Pr}}(X=\hat{X}_{\mathrm{MAP}}(Y))\leq\frac{\left(p^{\star}(\gamma+1)^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}+\!\!1-p^{\star}\right)^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}\!\!\exp\!\left\{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}I_{\alpha}(X,Y)\right\}-1}{\gamma}.

(134)

The proof is deferred to
Section

E.2
. Taking the limit as
\gamma
goes to infinity yields the following corollary:

Corollary 7.3
.

Under the setting of
Theorem

7.2
,

\displaystyle{\rm\mathbf{Pr}}(X=\hat{X}_{\mathrm{MAP}}(Y))\leq\left(p^{\star}e^{I_{\alpha}(X,Y)}\right)^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}.

(135)

Interestingly, whenever this bound is vacuous, so is the bound in
Theorem

7.1
, i.e., when
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)\geq-\log p^{\star}
. Otherwise, letting
\tilde{p}=
right-hand side of
Equation

135
, we get

\displaystyle d_{\alpha}(\tilde{p}\|p^{\star})\!=\!\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\!\log\!\left(\!e^{(\alpha-1)I_{\alpha}(X,Y)}\!+\!(1\!-p^{\star})\!\left(\frac{1-\tilde{p}}{1-p^{\star}}\right)^{\alpha}\right).

It is easy to check that
\tilde{p}>p^{\star}
, so that the second term inside the
\log
is smaller than 1, and the first term dominates (especially for large
\alpha
). As such,
Theorems

7.1
and
135
are very close.

As an illustration, consider
X^{n}\mathrel{{\mathop{\sim}\limits}}\mathrm{Ber}(1/2)
,
n=3
,
P_{Y|X}=\mathrm{BSC}(0.3)
. We plot the various bounds in
Figure

as function of
\alpha
.

7.1.1
Bound via Arimoto mutual information

Moreover, using the connection to Arimoto mutual information (cf.

) and the properties of conditional Rényi entropy, we get the following bound on the probability of error:

Theorem 7.4
.

Under the same setting of
Theorem

7.1
, for all
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)
,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{X|Y}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\exp\left\{-\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}\left(H_{\alpha}(X)-I^{A}_{\alpha}(X,Y)\right)\right\}

(136)

\displaystyle=1-\exp\left\{-\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}\left(H_{\alpha}(X)-I_{\alpha}(X_{\alpha},Y)\right)\right\},

(137)

where
\displaystyle P_{X_{\alpha}}(x)=\frac{P_{X}(x)^{\alpha}}{\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}P_{X}(x^{\prime})^{\alpha}}
.

Corollary

4.16

X

. The proof is deferred to

.

7.2
Generalized Fano Method

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a family of probability measures on an alphabet
\mathcal{Y}
, and
\theta(P)\in\Theta
be a parameter of interest. We are interested in estimating
\theta(P)
, with the quality of the estimate measured according to some given loss function
\ell:\Theta\times\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
which induces a pseudo-metric space
(\Theta,\ell)
. To that end, we observe
n
i.i.d samples
Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{n}
distributed according to (an unknown)
P\in\mathcal{P}
and generate an estimate
\hat{\theta}(X^{n})
to optimize a min-max objective:

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\theta(.)}}\max_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\ell(\theta(P),\hat{\theta}(Y^{n}))\right].

(138)

We are interested in the minimum number of samples necessary to guarantee the existence of an estimator with a small loss. A standard method to derive converse bounds in this setting is to reduce the estimation problem to a hypothesis testing problem on some subset of
\mathcal{P}

[
95
,
96
,
97
]
. As such, probability of guessing error bounds, and consequently mutual information bounds on the probability of guessing, have been used to derive converse bounds – as in Fano’s method
[
98
,
97
]
. In this section, we generalize Fano’s method using results of
Section

7.1
. First, we state a useful lemma:

Lemma 7.5
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,\infty]
. For fixed
P_{Y|X}
, if there exists a
Q_{Y}
such that for all
x
,

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}\left(P_{Y|X}(\cdot|x)\|Q_{Y}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\beta,

(139)

then for all
P_{X}
,

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(X,Y)

\displaystyle\leq\beta.

(140)

This is a generalization of the “standard” lemma used in the Fano method, which only considers KL divergence (i.e.,
\alpha=1
) and assumes
D(P_{Y|X}(.|x)||P_{Y|X}(.|x^{\prime}))\leq\beta
for all
x,x^{\prime}
. That is, we slightly relax the condition and allow for any
\alpha\in(0,\infty]
. The proof is deferred to
Section

E.4
.

We are now ready to state the generalized Fano method:

Theorem 7.6
.

[Generalized Fano method]
Fix
\alpha>0
. Let
r\geq 2
be an integer and set
\mathcal{J}=\{1,2,\ldots,r\}
. Consider
S=\{P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{r}\}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
such that there exists a distribution
P^{\star}
(not necessarily in
\mathcal{P}
) satisfying

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(P_{j}||P^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq\beta,\text{ for all }j=1,2,\ldots,r.

(141)

and

\displaystyle\ell\left(\theta(P_{j}),\theta(P_{i})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\gamma,\text{ for all }j\neq i.

(142)

Define
P_{Y|J}
as
P_{Y|J=j}=P_{j}
for all
j\in\mathcal{J}
. Then, for all distributions
Q
on
\mathcal{J}
,

\displaystyle\max_{j\in\{1,2,\ldots,r\}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{j}}\left[\ell(\theta(P_{j}),\hat{\theta}(Y))\right]\geq\frac{\gamma}{2}\varepsilon_{J|Y},

(143)

where
\varepsilon_{J|Y}
is the optimal probability of error of guessing
J
from
Y
. In particular, for all
\alpha>1
,

\displaystyle\max_{j\in\{1,2,\ldots,r\}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{j}}\left[\ell(\theta(P_{j}),\hat{\theta}(Y))\right]\geq\frac{\gamma}{2}\left(1-\left(\max_{j}Q_{J}(j)e^{I_{\alpha}(J,Y)}\right)^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}\right).

(144)

In the last step, we used the bound in
Equation

135
for illustrative purposes. Indeed, any bound on the probability of error, as given in
Theorems

7.2
,
7.1
and
7.4
would yield an analogous result. The proof follows along similar lines as
[
97
]
and is included in
Section

E.5
.

7.3
Bayesian Risk

Another example of application of
Corollary

6.2
comes from estimation procedures in a Bayesian setting. Let
\mathcal{W}
denote the
finite
parameter space and assume that we have access to a prior distribution over
\mathcal{W}
,
P_{W}
. Suppose that we observe
W\sim P_{W}
through the family of distributions
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{X|W=w}:w\in\mathcal{W}\}.
Given a function
\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\hat{\mathcal{W}}
, one can then estimate
W
from
X\sim P_{X|W}
via
\phi(X)=\hat{W}
. Let us denote with
\ell:\mathcal{W}\times\hat{\mathcal{W}}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
a loss function, the Bayesian risk is defined as:

R_{B}=\inf_{\phi}\mathbb{E}_{P_{W\hat{W}}}[\ell(W,\phi(X))]=\inf_{\phi}\mathbb{E}_{P_{W\hat{W}}}[\ell(W,\hat{W})].

(145)

Our purpose is to lower-bound
R_{B}
using Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information. In particular, one can connect the expected value of
\ell
under the joint
P_{W\hat{W}}
to

•

the expected value of the same function under the product of the marginals (
P_{W}P_{\hat{W}}
) or a “small-ball probability”;

•

the Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information, measuring how much information the observations retain about the parameter.

Small-ball probabilities date back to
[
99
]
with more recent advances and applications in
[
100
,
101
,
102
]
.

This particular technique allows us to retrieve a lower bound which is independent of the specific choice of the estimator
\phi
.
Let us denote the so-called small-ball probability as follows

L_{W}(\rho)=\sup_{\hat{w}\in\mathcal{\hat{W}}}L_{W}(\hat{w},\rho)=\sup_{\hat{w}\in\mathcal{\hat{W}}}P_{W}(\ell(W,\hat{w})<\rho).

(146)

Then one can prove the following:

Theorem 7.7
.

Consider the Bayesian framework just described. The following must hold for every
\alpha>1
and
\rho>0
:

R_{B}\geq\rho\left(1-\exp\left(\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}\left(I_{\alpha}(W,X)+\log(L_{W}(\rho))\right)\right)\right).

(147)

Taking the limit of
\alpha\to\infty
one recovers the following:

R_{B}\geq\sup_{\rho>0}\rho\left(1-\exp\left(\mathcal{L}\left(W\!\!\to\!\!X\right)+\log(L_{W}(\rho))\right)\right).

(148)

7.3.1
Bernoulli Bias Estimation

As an example of application of
Theorem

7.7
consider the following setting. Assume that
W
is uniformly distributed between
0
and
1
and that given
W=w
our observations
X_{i}
follow a Bernoulli distribution with parameter
w
. It is possible to see that
using
the sample mean estimator
i.e.
,
\hat{W}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
:

R_{B}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{6n}}.

(149)

Let us now provide a lower-bound on the problem leveraging
Theorem

7.7
. In this case one has that

\begin{split}&\exp\left(\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}I_{\alpha}(W,X^{n})\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\left(\frac{\Gamma(k\alpha+1)\Gamma((n-k)\alpha+1)}{\Gamma(n\alpha+2)}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}.\end{split}

(150)

Consider the loss function
\ell(w,\hat{w})=|w-\hat{w}|
, one can see that

L_{W}(\rho)=\sup_{\hat{w}}P_{W}(|W-\hat{w}|<\rho)=2\rho.

(151)

Consequently,
Equation

147
specializes into:

R_{B}\geq\sup_{\rho>0}\sup_{\alpha>1}\rho\left(1-(2\rho)^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}\exp\left(\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}I_{\alpha}(W,X^{n})\right)\right).

(152)

For any given
\alpha>1
the lower-bound in
Equation

152
will be larger than similar lower-bounds involving Shannon’s mutual information (cf.
[
47
]
). In
Figure

5
, one can see the improvement that
Equation

152
brings over
[
47
, Equation 19)]
when numerically optimized over
\rho
and
\alpha
. Given the difficulty in assessing the dependence on the various parameters in
Equation

150
, in order to better assess the power of
Theorem

7.7
one can, for example, consider the limit of
\alpha\to\infty
. In that case, one can prove that

\mathcal{L}\left(W\!\!\to\!\!X^{n}\right)\leq\log\left(2+\sqrt{\frac{\pi n}{2}}\right),

(153)

leading us to the following lower-bound on the Bayesian risk:

\displaystyle R_{B}\geq\sup_{\rho>0}\rho\left(1-\left(2+\sqrt{\frac{\pi n}{2}}\right)2\rho\right).

(154)

The quantity in
Equation

154
does not depend on
\alpha
anymore and can now be analytically optimized over
\rho
, leading to the following lower-bound:

R_{B}\geq\frac{1}{8\left(2+\sqrt{\frac{\pi n}{2}}\right)},

(155)

which, for
n
large enough (
i.e.
,
n\geq 127/\pi\approx 41
), can be further lower-bounded as follows

R_{B}\geq\frac{1}{5\sqrt{2\pi n}}.

Surprisingly, Maximal Leakage already offers a lower-bound that matches the upper-bound up to a constant (cf.
Equation

149
) and is simple to compute.
Equation

155
provides a larger lower-bound than the one provided using mutual information (cf.
[
47
, Corollary 2]
) for
n\geq 1
. Moreover, in order to achieve a lower-bound which behaves similarly to
Equation

149
in terms of the decay with respect to
n
, the authors in
[
47
]
need additional machinery and can only provide an
asymptotic
lower bound on the risk.
Another advantage of using the simple expression provided by Maximal Leakage is that Maximal Leakage depends on
P_{W}
only through the support. This means that if one has access to an upper-bound on
L_{W}(\rho)
that does not employ any additional knowledge of
P_{W}
, the resulting lower-bound on the risk would apply to any
W
whose support is the interval
[0,1]
. This would render the approach even more general than it already is. For more details and examples of application, the reader is referred to
[
41
]
.

7.4
Universal Prediction

In
[
103
,
104
]
, Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information is connected to the problem of universal prediction.
The quality of a universal predictor
\hat{p}
is assessed by its regret with respect to a model class containing distributions
p_{\theta}(x^{n}),
for all
\theta\in{\Theta},
where
\Theta
is the set of parameters.
To connect with Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information, we
consider the following generalized regret measure, defined for any
\alpha\geq 1
:

\begin{split}R_{\alpha}(\hat{p})&=\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}D_{\alpha}(p_{\theta}\|\hat{p})\\
&=\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\sum_{x^{n}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}}p_{\theta}(x^{n})\left(\frac{p_{\theta}(x^{n})}{\hat{p}(x^{n})}\right)^{\alpha-1}\end{split}

(156)

which we call
\alpha
-regret.
This regret measure itself is not standard, but interpolates between the two most important standard regret measures. Namely, the limit of
R_{\alpha}(\hat{p})
as
\alpha\rightarrow 1
is the standard average log-loss regret, while the limit of
R_{\alpha}(\hat{p})
as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
is the standard worst-case log-loss regret (see
[
104
]
for an in-depth discussion).
The connection is then that the minimum regret is precisely given by the maximum value of Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information.

Theorem 7.8
.

For universal prediction with respect to
\alpha
-regret and a class of distributions
\{p_{\theta}(x^{n}):\theta\in\Theta\},

assume that there exists a probability distribution
p^{*}(\theta)
on
\Theta
such that, with
W^{*}\sim p^{*}(\theta),

I_{\alpha}(W^{*},X^{n})=\sup_{V}I_{\alpha}(V,X^{n}),

(157)

where the supremum is over all random variables
V
on
\Theta,
and the conditional distribution of
X^{n}
is given by
p_{\theta}(x^{n}).

Then, the optimum predictor (minimizing the regret in
Equation

156
) is

\hat{p}_{\alpha}(x^{n})=\frac{\left\{\int_{\Theta}p^{*}(\theta)p_{\theta}^{\alpha}(x^{n})\,d\theta\right\}^{1/\alpha}}{\sum_{\tilde{x}^{n}}\left\{\int_{\Theta}p^{*}(\tilde{\theta})p_{\tilde{\theta}}^{\alpha}(\tilde{x}^{n})\,d\tilde{\theta}\right\}^{1/\alpha}},

(158)

which is also referred to as the
\alpha
-NML with prior
p^{*}(\theta)
, and the corresponding minimum regret is

R_{\alpha}(\hat{p}_{\alpha})=I_{\alpha}(W^{*},X^{n}).

(159)

This theorem is proved in
[
104
, Theorem 3]
.
The limiting cases of this theorem are well known.
For the limit
\alpha\rightarrow 1,
the result is due to Gallager
[
105
]
and often referred to as the capacity-redundancy theorem.
For the limit
\alpha\rightarrow\infty,
the result is due to Shtarkov
[
106
]
.
In this case, the optimum predictor is commonly referred to as
normalized maximum likelihood
(NML) predictor, and the corresponding regret as the
Shtarkov sum,
which in the language used here would be referred to as the maximal leakage from the parameter set
\Theta
to the sequences
X^{n},
denoted as
\mathcal{L}\left(\Theta\!\!\to\!\!X^{n}\right),
as in
Equation

32
above.
We note that in this limiting case, the distribution
p^{*}(\theta)
appearing in
Theorem

7.8
is immaterial since maximal leakage does not depend on the marginal distribution of the input.

Remark 7.3
.

By the connection between universal prediction and universal compression, an analogous result can be obtained for universal compression if one studies the source coding setting advocated by Campbell
[
107
]
. This is fully developed in
[
108
]
, where it is shown that the minimal redundancy is equal to the maximum value of Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information is a sense similar to
Theorem

7.8
.

8
Extension to Conditional Sibson
\alpha
-Mutual Information

There are multiple ways to define conditional mutual information, each highlighting different perspectives on dependence in a triplet of random variables. Given that Sibson’s
\alpha
-mutual information generalizes mutual information, one can draw inspiration from these various approaches to formulate a conditional version.
For example, one could define

I(X;Y|Z)=I(X,Z;Y)-I(Z;Y).

Or as another example, one can also define

I(X;Y|Z)=D(P_{XYZ}\|P_{X}P_{Z|X}P_{Y|Z}).

Or, as a third class of examples, one can define

\displaystyle I(X;Y|Z)

\displaystyle=\min_{Q_{Z|X}}D(P_{XYZ}\|P_{X}Q_{Z|X}P_{Y|Z})\text{ or }

\displaystyle I(X;Y|Z)

\displaystyle=\min_{Q_{Y|Z}}D(P_{XYZ}\|P_{X}P_{Z|X}Q_{Y|Z})\text{ or }

\displaystyle I(X;Y|Z)

\displaystyle=\min_{Q_{X}}D(P_{XYZ}\|Q_{X}P_{Z|X}P_{Y|Z}).

In the case of Kullback-Leibler divergence and Shannon’s mutual information, all of these definitions (including those via minimization) coincide.
However, when dealing with Rényi divergences, each of these definitions leads to a different quantity, as we will see in the rest of the Section.

Given
\alpha\neq 1
, it is somewhat unclear how one should define conditional information. Several candidate definitions exist and are all equally promising. In this section, we briefly discuss this context and point to some of the operational meanings that appear naturally as a consequence of the closed-form expressions that these information measures admit.
Mimicking the approach undertaken in
Definition

3.1
along with the considerations made about Shannon’s mutual information just above, we will define conditional Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information of
X
and
Y
given
Z
as a minimization of Rényi divergences measuring how far the joint is from the Markov chain
X-Z-Y
. Differently from the two variables case, one can choose to minimize over different measures
i.e.
, one can advance any of the following definitions

I_{\alpha}(X,Y|Z)\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\begin{cases}\min_{Q_{Y|Z}}D_{\alpha}(P_{XYZ}\|P_{X|Z}Q_{Y|Z}P_{Z})\text{ or }\\
\min_{Q_{X|Z}}D_{\alpha}(P_{XYZ}\|Q_{X|Z}P_{Y|Z}P_{Z})\text{ or }\\
\min_{Q_{Z}}D_{\alpha}(P_{XYZ}\|P_{X|Z}P_{Y|Z}Q_{Z}).\end{cases}

(160)

Moreover, one can consider different factorizations of the joint measure corresponding to the Markov chain
X-Z-Y
.
Rather than making an explicit choice or analyzing every possible definition, we will briefly consider the similarities and the properties these objects possess. To do so, let us go through some examples and extrapolate said properties. We will distinguish the resulting information measure with a superscript indicating the family of measures over which perform the minimization. For the remainder of this section, consider a discrete setting. For instance, minimizing over conditional measures
Q_{Y|Z}
leads to the following definition:

Definition 8.1
.

Let
X,Y,Z
be three discrete random variables jointly distributed according to
P_{XYZ}
. Denote with
P_{Z}
the corresponding marginal over the support of
Z
and, for a given
z
,
P_{X|Z=z}
the corresponding conditional distributions over the support of
X
. One can define the following conditional Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information:

I^{Y|Z}_{\alpha}(X,Y|Z)=\min_{Q_{Y|Z}}D_{\alpha}(P_{XYZ}\|P_{X|Z}Q_{Y|Z}P_{Z}),

(161)

where the minimization is over all the conditional distributions
Q_{Y|Z=\cdot}
over the support of
Y
.

Equation

161
admits a closed-form expression given in the following equation:

\begin{split}&I^{Y|Z}_{\alpha}(X,Y|Z)=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\log\mathbb{E}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}_{P_{Z}}\left[\mathbb{E}^{\alpha}_{P_{Y|Z}}\left[\mathbb{E}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}_{P_{X|Z}}\left[\left(\frac{P_{XYZ}}{P_{Z}P_{X|Z}P_{Y|Z}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]\right]\right].\end{split}

(162)

Similarly, if one chooses to minimize over
Q_{Z}
, the following definition arises:

Definition 8.2
.

Let
X,Y,Z
be three discrete random variables jointly distributed according to
P_{XYZ}
. For a given
z
, denote with
P_{X|Z=z}
the corresponding conditional distribution over the support of
X
and with
P_{Y|Z=z}
the corresponding conditional distribution over the support of
Y
. One can define the following conditional Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information:

I^{Z}_{\alpha}(X,Y|Z)=\min_{Q_{Z}}D_{\alpha}(P_{XYZ}\|P_{X|Z}P_{Y|Z}Q_{Z}),

(163)

where the minimization is over all the probability measures
Q_{Z}
over the support of
Z
.

Equation

163
also admits a closed-form expression given in the following equation:

\begin{split}&I^{Z}_{\alpha}(X,Y|Z)=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{Z}}\left[\mathbb{E}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}_{P_{Y|Z},P_{X|Z}}\left[\left(\frac{P_{XYZ}}{P_{Z}P_{X|Z}P_{Y|Z}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]\right].\end{split}

(164)

The proofs of these statements follow from adaptations of the proof of
Theorem

3.1
: every
Q_{\{\cdot\}}
we are minimizing over in the second argument of
D_{\alpha}
will have a corresponding expectation operator. Changing the measure of said expectation operator to the corresponding marginal
P_{\{\cdot\}}
(or conditional) obtained from
P_{XYZ}
, along with an application of Jensen’s inequality achieves the closed-form expression. Equality can then be proven by selecting
Q_{\{\cdot\}}^{\star}
to be a carefully chosen tilting with respect to
P_{\{\cdot\}}
(cf.
Equation

27
). For instance, if one minimizes over
Q_{Z}
, the corresponding
Q_{Z}^{\star}
is the following:

\begin{split}&Q_{Z}^{\star}(z)=\frac{P_{Z}(z)\!\left(\!\sum_{x,y}P_{XY|Z}(x,y,z)^{\alpha}(P_{X|Z}(x,z)P_{Y|Z}(y,z))^{1-\alpha}\!\right)^{\!\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{\mathbb{E}_{P_{Z}}\left[\!\left(\sum_{x,y}P_{XY|Z}(x,y,z)^{\alpha}(P_{X|Z}(x,z)P_{Y|Z}(y,z))^{1-\alpha}\!\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\!\right]}.\end{split}

(165)

Similarly, if one minimizes over
Q_{Y|Z}
then the corresponding tilting, for a given
z
is given in the following:

\begin{split}&Q_{Y|Z=z}^{\star}(y)=\frac{P_{Y|Z=z}(y)\left(\sum_{x}P_{XY|Z}(x,y,z)^{\alpha}(P_{X|Z}(x,z)P_{Z}(z))^{1-\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{\mathbb{E}_{P_{Y|Z=z}}\left[\left(\sum_{x}P_{XY|Z}(x,y,z)^{\alpha}(P_{X|Z}(x,z)P_{Z}(z))^{1-\alpha}\!\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right]}.\end{split}

(166)

The expressions in
Equation

162
and
Equation

164
are clearly distinct.
In particular, one can see that

\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}I_{\alpha}^{Y|Z}(X,Y|Z)=\mathcal{L}\left(X\!\!\to\!\!Y|Z\right),

while the same does not hold for
I_{\alpha}^{Z}(X,Y|Z)
.
However, for both of them is true that
\lim_{\alpha\to 1}I_{\alpha}^{\cdot}(X,Y|Z)=I(X;Y|Z)
.
Moreover, we do not know whether any of these satisfies a mutual information-like chain-rule
i.e.
,

I_{\alpha}(X,(Y,Z))\leq I_{\alpha}(X,Z)+I^{?}_{\alpha}(X,Y|Z),

(167)

even though, we do know that

\displaystyle I(X;(Y,Z))

\displaystyle=I(X;Z)+I(X;Y|Z)

(168)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(X\!\!\to\!\!(Y,Z)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}\left(X\!\!\to\!\!Z\right)+\mathcal{L}\left(X\!\!\to\!\!Y|Z\right).

(169)

Given the closed-form expressions and the technique leveraged in this work, we can, however, find applications for these quantities in hypothesis testing settings. Said links have been established in
[
91
,
34
]
. Moreover with a similar approach to
Section

6.1
, one can see
I_{\alpha}(X,Y|Z)
as three nested norms of the Radon-Nikodym derivative of the joint
P_{XYZ}
with respect to a measure corresponding to the Markov chain
X-Z-Y
. Similarly to the unconditional version, conditional
I_{\alpha}
also involves a minimization and there is a variety of choices with respect to which measure to minimize over,
Section

8
. Hence, as before, the norm corresponding to the measure we are minimizing over will be an
L^{1}
-norms, while the remaining two will be
L^{\alpha}
-norms. Similarly to the two variables case, given
P_{XYZ}
, one can relate via an inequality:

•

the expected value of a function (probability of event) under the joint
P_{XYZ}
;

•

with the nested norms of the same function (probability of event) under the Markovity assumption
X-Z-Y
;

•

nested norms of the Radon-Nikodym derivative of the joint with respect to the measure formalizing the Markov chain
X-Z-Y
(
e.g.
, conditional Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information).

For illustrative purposes, let us choose as a conditional version
I_{\alpha}^{Y|Z}
(see
Equation

161
). One can thus prove the following result:

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
P_{XYZ}
be a joint measure. Let
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times Z\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a
P_{XYZ}
-measurable function, then one has that for every
\alpha>1
and denoting with
\beta=\alpha/(\alpha-1)
,

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}_{P_{XYZ}}\left[f(X,Y,Z)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}^{\frac{1}{\beta}}_{P_{Z}}\left[\max_{y:P_{Y|Z}(y)>0}\mathbb{E}_{P_{X|Z}}\left[f^{\beta}(X,y,Z)\right]\right]\exp\left(\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}I^{Y|Z}_{\alpha}(X,Y|Z)\right).\end{split}

(170)

In particular, if one considers
\alpha\to\infty
one recovers the following:

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}_{P_{XYZ}}\left[f(X,Y,Z)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{P_{Z}}\left[\max_{y:P_{Y|Z}(y)>0}\mathbb{E}_{P_{X|Z}}\left[f(X,y,Z)\right]\right]\exp\left(\mathcal{L}\left(X\!\!\to\!\!Y|Z\right)\right).\end{split}

(171)

Consequently, similarly to before, one can relate probabilities of events under different distributions (joint and Markovian) selecting
f
to be the indicator function of the event in
Theorem

8.1
.

Corollary 8.2
.

Let
E
be a measurable event, then one has that for every
\alpha>1
and denoting with
\beta=\alpha/(\alpha-1)
,

\begin{split}&P_{XYZ}(E)\leq\mathbb{E}^{\frac{1}{\beta}}_{P_{Z}}\left[\max_{y:P_{Y|Z}(y)>0}P_{X|Z}(E_{y,Z})\right]\exp\left(\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}I_{\alpha}^{Y|Z}(X,Y|Z)\right).\end{split}

(172)

where, given
E\subseteq\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Z}
, one has that for a given
y\in\mathcal{Y}
and
z\in\mathcal{Z}
,
E_{y,z}=\{x:(x,y,z)\in E\}
.
In particular, if one considers
\alpha\to\infty
one recovers the following:

\begin{split}&P_{XYZ}(E)\leq\mathbb{E}_{P_{Z}}\left[\max_{y:P_{Y|Z}(y)>0}P_{X|Z}(E_{y,Z})\right]\exp\left(\mathcal{L}\left(X\!\!\to\!\!Y|Z\right)\right).\end{split}

(173)

9
Discussion, Extensions, and Open Problems

9.1
Negative Values of
\alpha

In this paper, we have restricted attention to non-negative values of
\alpha.
For this case, there is a rich and emerging literature. Needless to say, it is also tempting to consider negative values of
\alpha.
Indeed, many of the techniques presented here can be extended to negative values of
\alpha,
leading to interesting and useful results. However, providing a
meaningful definition
of Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information for negative values is non-trivial.
One can, for instance, define such an object following the classical approach, like in
Definition

3.1
. The first step in this direction would be to extend the definition of Rényi divergence to negative
\alpha.
Leaving the definition of
D_{\alpha}
unaltered would violate the fundamental properties of a divergence, like non-negativity and the data-processing inequality, see e.g.
[
9
, Section V]
.
Hence, in this case, one would need to either alter the definition of Rényi divergence itself or that of
I_{\alpha}
as expressed in
Definition

3.1
.

Another possibility comes from undertaking the norm perspective considered in
Section

3.2
. Indeed, one could extend the nested-norm functional in
Equation

45
to negative values of
\alpha
. This implies that the corresponding object will no longer be a norm (which is already the case when
0<\alpha<1
).
Moreover, said object would immediately find applications in bounds
e.g.
,
extending the result presented in
Section

6.1.2
to negative values of
\alpha
or in directly lower-bounding the Bayesian Risk (without employing Markov’s inequality like we did in
Theorem

7.7
). Some of these ideas were already advanced in
[
41
,
40
]
. However, one would incur into measure-theoretic subtleties and absolute continuity issues which are outside the scope of this work. Hence, these results will be formalised and presented in a separate manuscript.

9.2
Probabilistic Variational Representation

Drawing inspiration from
[
53
]
one can also try to achieve a representation for
I_{\alpha}
that mimics the one advanced in
Definition

4.2
. However, for finite
\alpha
(as opposed to
\alpha\to\infty

i.e.
, Maximal Leakage) one can only prove a lower bound:

Theorem 9.1
.

Let
X,Y
be two random variables jointly distributed according to
P_{XY}
and let
\alpha\in(1,+\infty]
. The following holds true:

\exp\left(\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}I_{\alpha}(X,Y)\right)\geq\sup_{U:U-X-Y}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P_{Y}}\left[\left\lVert P_{U|Y}\right\rVert_{L^{\alpha}(P_{U})}\right]}{\left\lVert\left\lVert P_{U|X}\right\rVert_{L^{\beta}(P_{X})}\right\rVert_{L^{\alpha}(P_{U})}}.

(174)

The following example shows the strictness of
Equation

174
for every finite order
\alpha>1
.

Example 9.1

(Strictness of
Equation

174
for every finite order)
.

Equality in
Equation

174
does not hold in general for any finite
\alpha>1
. Let

X\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right),\qquad Y=X\quad\text{almost surely},

and let
\beta=\alpha/(\alpha-1)
. For every auxiliary random variable
U
satisfying
U-X-Y
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P_{Y}}\left[\left\|\frac{dP_{U|Y}}{dP_{U}}\right\|_{L^{\alpha}(P_{U})}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\frac{dP_{U|X=0}}{dP_{U}}\right\|_{L^{\alpha}(P_{U})}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\frac{dP_{U|X=1}}{dP_{U}}\right\|_{L^{\alpha}(P_{U})}

\displaystyle\leq\left[\frac{1}{2}\left\|\frac{dP_{U|X=0}}{dP_{U}}\right\|_{L^{\alpha}(P_{U})}^{\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\frac{dP_{U|X=1}}{dP_{U}}\right\|_{L^{\alpha}(P_{U})}^{\alpha}\right]^{1/\alpha}

\displaystyle=\left\|\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{dP_{U|X=0}}{dP_{U}}\right)^{\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{dP_{U|X=1}}{dP_{U}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]^{1/\alpha}\right\|_{L^{\alpha}(P_{U})},

(175)

where the inequality follows from the power-mean inequality i.e., given
a,b\geq 0
and
\alpha>1
one has that due to Hölder’s inequality

\frac{a+b}{2}\leq\left(\frac{a^{\alpha}+b^{\alpha}}{2}\right)^{1/\alpha}.

(176)

Suppose first that
1<\alpha\leq 2
. In this case
\alpha\leq\beta
, and hence, for
P_{U}
-almost every
u
,

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{dP_{U|X=0}}{dP_{U}}(u)\right)^{\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{dP_{U|X=1}}{dP_{U}}(u)\right)^{\alpha}\right]^{1/\alpha}\leq\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{dP_{U|X=0}}{dP_{U}}(u)\right)^{\beta}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{dP_{U|X=1}}{dP_{U}}(u)\right)^{\beta}\right]^{1/\beta}.

(177)

Combining this inequality with
Equation

175
gives

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P_{Y}}\left[\left\|\frac{dP_{U|Y}}{dP_{U}}\right\|_{L^{\alpha}(P_{U})}\right]}{\left\|\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{dP_{U|X=0}}{dP_{U}}\right)^{\beta}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{dP_{U|X=1}}{dP_{U}}\right)^{\beta}\right]^{1/\beta}\right\|_{L^{\alpha}(P_{U})}}\leq 1.

(178)

Taking
U
to be constant gives

\frac{dP_{U|X=0}}{dP_{U}}=\frac{dP_{U|X=1}}{dP_{U}}=1,

so both the numerator and denominator are equal to
1
. Therefore, the upper bound is attained.

Suppose now that
2\leq\alpha<\infty
. For the uniform distribution on the two-point alphabet
\{0,1\}
, one has the standard estimate

\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L^{\alpha}(P_{U})}\leq 2^{1/\beta-1/\alpha}\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L^{\beta}(P_{U})}=2^{1-2/\alpha}\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L^{\beta}(P_{U})}.

Hence, for
P_{U}
-almost every
u
,

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{dP_{U|X=0}}{dP_{U}}(u)\right)^{\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{dP_{U|X=1}}{dP_{U}}(u)\right)^{\alpha}\right]^{1/\alpha}\leq 2^{\frac{1}{\beta}-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{dP_{U|X=0}}{dP_{U}}(u)\right)^{\beta}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{dP_{U|X=1}}{dP_{U}}(u)\right)^{\beta}\right]^{1/\beta}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P_{Y}}\left[\left\|\frac{dP_{U|Y}}{dP_{U}}\right\|_{L^{\alpha}(P_{U})}\right]}{\left\|\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{dP_{U|X=0}}{dP_{U}}\right)^{\beta}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{dP_{U|X=1}}{dP_{U}}\right)^{\beta}\right]^{1/\beta}\right\|_{L^{\alpha}(P_{U})}}\leq 2^{\frac{1}{\beta}-\frac{1}{\alpha}}=2^{1-\frac{2}{\alpha}}.

(179)

This upper bound is attained by taking
U=X
. Indeed,
P_{U}
is then uniform on
\{0,1\}
and

\frac{dP_{U|X=0}}{dP_{U}}(u)=2\mathbbm{1}\{u=0\},\qquad\frac{dP_{U|X=1}}{dP_{U}}(u)=2\mathbbm{1}\{u=1\}.

It follows that

\left\|\frac{dP_{U|X=0}}{dP_{U}}\right\|_{L^{\alpha}(P_{U})}=\left\|\frac{dP_{U|X=1}}{dP_{U}}\right\|_{L^{\alpha}(P_{U})}=2^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}},

whereas

\displaystyle\left\|\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{dP_{U|X=0}}{dP_{U}}\right)^{\beta}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{dP_{U|X=1}}{dP_{U}}\right)^{\beta}\right]^{1/\beta}\right\|_{L^{\alpha}(P_{U})}=2^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

(180)

Thus, the resulting ratio is

\frac{2^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{2^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}=2^{1-\frac{2}{\alpha}}.

We have therefore shown that

\sup_{U:U-X-Y}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P_{Y}}\left[\left\|\frac{dP_{U|Y}}{dP_{U}}\right\|_{L^{\alpha}(P_{U})}\right]}{\left\|\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{dP_{U|X=0}}{dP_{U}}\right)^{\beta}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{dP_{U|X=1}}{dP_{U}}\right)^{\beta}\right]^{1/\beta}\right\|_{L^{\alpha}(P_{U})}}=\begin{cases}1,&1<\alpha\leq 2,\\[2.84526pt]
2^{1-\frac{2}{\alpha}},&2\leq\alpha<\infty.\end{cases}

On the other hand, since
X
is uniform and
Y=X
,

I_{\alpha}(X,Y)=I_{\alpha}(X,X)=H_{1/\alpha}(X)=\log 2,

and therefore

\exp\left(\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}I_{\alpha}(X,Y)\right)=2^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

For every finite
\alpha>1
,

2^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}>\begin{cases}1,&1<\alpha\leq 2,\\[2.84526pt]
2^{1-\frac{2}{\alpha}},&2\leq\alpha<\infty.\end{cases}

Hence, the inequality in
Equation

174
is strict for every finite
\alpha>1
. As
\alpha\to\infty
, both sides converge to
2
, consistently with the equality known for maximal leakage.

Remark 9.1
.

Taking the limit of
\alpha\to 1^{+}
in
Equation

174
leads to a trivial result. Consider a discrete setting for simplicity. One has that taking said limit in
Equation

174
leads to the following inequality:

\sum_{y}\sum_{u}p_{U|Y=y}(u)p_{Y}(y)p_{U}(u)\leq\sum_{u}p_{U}(u)\max_{x}p_{U|X=x}(u).

(181)

One can easily see that
Equation

181
is always true as long as
U-X-Y
form a Markov chain. Indeed:

\displaystyle\sum_{y}p_{Y}(y)\sum_{u}p_{U|Y=y}(u)p_{U}(u)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y}p_{Y}(y)\sum_{u}p_{U}(u)\sum_{x}p_{U|X=x}(u)p_{X|Y=y}(x)

(182)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{y}p_{Y}(y)\sum_{u}p_{U}(u)\max_{x}p_{U|X=x}(u)\sum_{x}p_{X|Y=y}(x)

(183)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u}p_{U}(u)\max_{x}p_{U|X=x}(u).

(184)

Conclusions

We provided a reference document for Sibson
\alpha
-mutual information.

In

we presented its basic properties, along with fundamental and well-established results characterising the corresponding capacity, coding theorems as well as links to other generalized information measures.

In

, we introduced a variety of variational representations for

that, to the best of our knowledge, are entirely novel: the first links Sibson

-mutual information to the KL divergence and the other representations connect it to opportune functionals. Starting from

In particular, in

Appendix A
Hölder’s Inequality

Hölder’s inequality is a standard result, see e.g.
[
109
, p.80]
. We include a statement and proof outline since the result is central to many of the proofs presented above and since the second case in the following theorem is not explicitly treated in most standard textbooks on the topic (but is of interest to us here).

Theorem A.1

(Hölder’s Inequality)
.

Let
p
and
q
be Hölder conjugates, which means that they satisfy
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1.

Two cases are possible:

1.

p,q\in[1,\infty].
In this case,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[|XY|]

\displaystyle\leq\left({\mathbb{E}}[|X|^{p}]\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\left({\mathbb{E}}[|Y|^{q}]\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}.

(185)

In this case, the right hand side can be interpreted as the product of two norms.

2.

0<p<1,
and thus,
q<0.
In this case,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[|XY|]

\displaystyle\geq\left({\mathbb{E}}[|X|^{p}]\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\left({\mathbb{E}}[|Y|^{q}]\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}.

(186)

In both cases, equality is attained if and only if
|X|^{p}=\beta|Y|^{q}
(almost surely) for some real number
\beta.

Remark A.1
.

Both bounds hold not just for probability measures, but for general measures. Specifically, we will sometimes use the case of Lebesgue measure.

Proof.

Assume, without loss of generality,
0<{\mathbb{E}}[|Y|^{q}]<\infty
(otherwise the statement is trivial), and define

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{XY}(x,y)

\displaystyle=P_{XY}(x,y)\frac{|y|^{q}}{{\mathbb{E}}[|Y|^{q}]}.

(187)

With this,

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{XY}}[|XY|]}{{\mathbb{E}}[|Y|^{q}]}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{\tilde{P}_{XY}}[|X||Y|^{1-q}].

(188)

Now, if
p\geq 1
and for
x\geq 0,

x^{p}
is a convex function. Therefore, by Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{XY}}[|XY|]}{{\mathbb{E}}[|Y|^{q}]}\right)^{p}

\displaystyle=\left({\mathbb{E}}_{\tilde{P}_{XY}}[|X||Y|^{1-q}]\right)^{p}\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{\tilde{P}_{XY}}[|X|^{p}|Y|^{p(1-q)}].

(189)

Using the definition of
\tilde{P}_{XY},

\displaystyle\left(\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{XY}}[|XY|]}{{\mathbb{E}}[|Y|^{q}]}\right)^{p}\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{XY}}\left[|X|^{p}|Y|^{p(1-q)}\frac{|Y|^{q}}{{\mathbb{E}}[|Y|^{q}]}\right]=\frac{1}{{\mathbb{E}}[|Y|^{q}]}{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{XY}}\left[|X|^{p}|Y|^{p(1-q)+q}\right]

(190)

Now, note that
p+q-pq=0.
Taking the
p
-th root on both sides gives the desired result, since
1-1/p=1/q.

For
0\leq p<1,
the only change in the above is that
x^{p}
is a concave function (for
x\geq 0
), hence Jensen’s inequality is in the opposite direction.
∎

Proof of

A detailed proof can be found in
[
9
, Theorem 30]
. Herein, we demonstrate the main technique. In particular, we focus on the case where
D_{\alpha}(P||Q)<\infty
. Define a distribution
T
such that

\displaystyle\frac{dT}{dP}=\left(\frac{dP}{dQ}\right)^{\alpha-1}e^{-(\alpha-1)D_{\alpha}(P||Q)},

(191)

e.g., for discrete
\mathcal{X}
, we get
T(x)\propto P(x)^{\alpha}Q(x)^{1-\alpha}
. Then,

\displaystyle(1-\alpha)D_{\alpha}(P||Q)

\displaystyle=-\log\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\left(\frac{dP}{dQ}\right)^{\alpha-1}\right]

(192)

\displaystyle=-\log\mathbb{E}_{R}\left[\left(\frac{dP}{dR}\right)^{\alpha}\left(\frac{dQ}{dR}\right)^{1-\alpha}\right]

(193)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{R}\left[-\log\left(\frac{dP}{dR}\right)^{\alpha}-\log\left(\frac{dQ}{dR}\right)^{1-\alpha}\right]

(194)

\displaystyle=-\alpha\mathbb{E}_{R}\left[\log\left(\frac{dP}{dR}\right)\right]-(1-\alpha)\mathbb{E}_{R}\left[\log\left(\frac{dQ}{dR}\right)\right]

(195)

\displaystyle=\alpha D(R||P)+(1-\alpha)D(R||Q),

(196)

where the inequality is Jensen’s inequality.
Therefore, the condition for equality is simply to select
R
such that
\left(\frac{dP}{dR}\right)^{\alpha}\left(\frac{dQ}{dR}\right)^{1-\alpha}
is a constant, that is,
R=T
.

Indeed, one can rewrite

\displaystyle\alpha D(R||P)+(1-\alpha)D(R||Q)

\displaystyle=D(R\|T)+(1-\alpha)D_{\alpha}(P\|Q).

(197)

Finally, the convexity and continuity of (
196
) (as a function of
R
) follows from the convexity and continuity (in the first argument) of Kullback-Leibler divergence.

Appendix C
Proofs for
Section

4

C.1
Proof of
Theorem

4.1

Proof.

We provide short proofs for the individual properties given in
Theorem

4.1
.

1.

The non-negativity of Sibson’s
\alpha
-mutual information is a direct consequence of the non-negativity of Rényi divergence (see
[
9
, Theorem 8]
).

2.

To establish the data processing inequality, let
X-Y-Z
be a Markov chain and let
K_{Z|Y}
denote the corresponding Markov Kernel
K_{Z|Y}:(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{F})\to(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{\hat{F}})
induced by the transition probabilities
P_{Z|Y}
. Given any probability distribution over
Y,
denoted by
Q_{Y}
, one can construct a corresponding a probability distribution over
Z
via
K_{Z|Y}
as follows,
Q_{Z}=Q_{Y}K_{Z|Y}
where
Q_{Y}K_{Z|Y}(z)=\mathbb{E}_{Q_{Y}}[P_{Z|Y}(z)]
. By the Data-Processing Inequality for
D_{\alpha}
(
[
9
, Theorem 1]
), one has that for every
Q_{Y}
:

D_{\alpha}(P_{XZ}\|P_{X}Q_{Z})\leq D_{\alpha}(P_{XY}\|P_{X}Q_{Y})

(229)

Taking the
\min
with respect to
Q_{Y}
leads to

D_{\alpha}(P_{XZ}\|P_{X}Q_{Z})\leq I_{\alpha}(X,Y).

(230)

Moreover, since
I_{\alpha}(X,Z)\leq D_{\alpha}(P_{XZ}\|P_{X}\hat{Q}_{Z})
for every
\hat{Q}_{Z}
the statement follows by selecting
\hat{Q}_{Z}=Q_{Y}K_{Z|Y}
.
The other inequality follows by observing that considering the Kernel determined by
P_{XZ|XY}
and denoted by
K_{XZ|YZ}
one has that
P_{XZ}=P_{YZ}K_{XZ|YZ}
while
P_{X}Q_{Z}=(P_{X}Q_{Y})K_{XZ|YZ}
. Consequently by the Data-Processing Inequality for
D_{\alpha}
one has that

D_{\alpha}(P_{XZ}\|P_{X}Q_{Z})\leq D_{\alpha}(P_{YZ}\|P_{Y}Q_{Z}).

(231)

The statement follows from a similar argument as above.

3.

The invariance of Sibson’s
\alpha
-mutual information to injective transforms is a consequence of the data processing inequality. Indeed, one has that
X-Y-g(Y)
form a Markov Chain, thus
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)\geq I_{\alpha}(X,g(Y))
. Moreover, since
g
is injective and invertible, one also has
X-g(Y)-Y
and, consequently
I_{\alpha}(X,g(Y))\geq I_{\alpha}(X,Y)
. We thus have that
I_{\alpha}(X,Y)=I_{\alpha}(X,g(Y))
. Applying the same reasoning to
X
and
f(X)
yields the result.

4.

The additivity of Sibson’s
\alpha
-mutual information for independent pairs of random variables can be established by direct evaluation, as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}I_{\alpha}(X^{n},Y^{n})

\displaystyle=\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{Y^{n}}}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}_{P_{X^{n}}}\left[\left(\frac{dP_{X^{n}Y^{n}}}{dP_{X^{n}}P_{Y^{n}}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right]

(232)

\displaystyle\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{Y^{n}}}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}_{P_{X^{n}}}\left[\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{dP_{X_{i}Y_{i}}}{dP_{X_{i}}P_{Y_{i}}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right]

(233)

\displaystyle\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\log\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{P_{Y^{n}}}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}_{P_{X^{n}}}\left[\left(\frac{dP_{X_{i}Y_{i}}}{dP_{X_{i}}P_{Y_{i}}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right]

(234)
