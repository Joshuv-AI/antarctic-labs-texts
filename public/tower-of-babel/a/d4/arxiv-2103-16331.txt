The parabolic Anderson model on a Galton-Watson tree revisited
F. den Hollander

Abstract
In [ 1 ] a detailed analysis was given of the large-time asymptotics of the total mass of the solution to the parabolic Anderson model on a supercritical Galton-Watson random tree with an i.i.d. random potential whose marginal distribution is double-exponential. Under the assumption that the degree distribution has bounded support, two terms in the asymptotic expansion were identified under the quenched law, i.e., conditional on the realisation of the random tree and the random potential. The second term contains a variational formula indicating that the solution concentrates on a subtree with minimal degree according to a computable profile. The present paper extends the analysis to degree distributions with unbounded support. We identify the weakest condition on the tail of the degree distribution under which the arguments in [ 1 ] can be pushed through. To do so we need to control the occurrence of large degrees uniformly in large subtrees of the Galton-Watson tree. MSC2010: 60H25, 82B44, 05C80. Keywords: Parabolic Anderson model, Galton-Watson tree, double-exponential distribution, quenched Lyapunov exponent, variational formula. Acknowledgment: The work in this paper was supported through Gravitation-grant NETWORKS-024.002.003 of the Netherlands Organisation for Scientific Research (NWO). The authors thank Götz Kersting and Anton Wakolbinger for helpful discussions on large deviation properties of the Galton-Watson process.

1.1
The PAM and intermittency

The
parabolic Anderson model
(PAM) is the Cauchy problem

\partial_{t}u(x,t)=\Delta_{\mathscr{X}}u(x,t)+\xi(x)u(x,t),\qquad t>0,\,x\in\mathscr{X},

(1.1)

where
\mathscr{X}
is an ambient space,
\Delta_{\mathscr{X}}
is a Laplace operator acting on functions on
\mathscr{X}
, and
\xi
is a random potential on
\mathscr{X}
. Most of the literature considers the setting where
\mathscr{X}
is either
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
or
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\geq 1
(for mathematical surveys we refer the reader to
[
3
]
,
[
9
]
). More recently, other choices for
\mathscr{X}
have been considered as well: the complete graph
[
5
]
, the hypercube
[
2
]
, Galton-Watson trees
[
1
]
, and random graphs with prescribed degrees
[
1
]
.

The main target for the PAM is a description of
intermittency
: for large
t
the solution
u(\cdot,t)
of (
1.1
) concentrates on well-separated regions in
\mathscr{X}
, called
intermittent islands
. Much of the literature has focussed on a detailed description of the size, shape and location of these islands, and the profiles of the potential
\xi(\cdot)
and the solution
u(\cdot,t)
on them. A special role is played by the case where
\xi
is an i.i.d. random potential with a
double-exponential
marginal distribution

\mathrm{P}(\xi(0)>u)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{e}^{u/\varrho}},\qquad u\in\mathbb{R},

(1.2)

where
\varrho\in(0,\infty)
is a parameter. This distribution turns out to be critical, in the sense that the intermittent islands neither grow nor shrink with time, and therefore represents a class of its own.

The analysis of intermittency typically starts with a computation of the large-time asymptotics of the total mass, encapsulated in what are called
Lyapunov exponents
. There is an important distinction between the
annealed
setting (i.e., averaged over the random potential) and the
quenched
setting (i.e., almost surely with respect to the random potential). Often both types of Lyapunov exponents admit explicit descriptions in terms of
characteristic variational formulas
that contain information about where and how the mass concentrates in
\mathscr{X}
. These variational formulas contain a
spatial part
(identifying where the concentration on islands takes place) and a
profile part
(identifying what the size and shape of both the potential and the solution are on the islands).

In the present paper we focus on the case where
\mathscr{X}
is a Galton-Watson tree, in the quenched setting (i.e., almost surely with respect to the random tree and the random potential). In
[
1
]
the large-time asymptotics of the total mass was derived under the assumption that the degree distribution has bounded support. The goal of the present paper is to relax this assumption to unbounded degree distributions. In particular, we identify the
weakest condition on the tail of the degree distribution
under which the arguments in
[
1
]
can be pushed through.
To do so we need to control the occurrence of large degrees
uniformly in large subtrees
of the Galton-Watson tree.

1.2
The PAM on a graph

We begin with some basic definitions and notations (and refer the reader to
[
3
]
,
[
9
]
for more background).

Let
G=(V,E)
be a
simple connected undirected
graph, either finite or countably infinite. Let
\Delta_{G}
be the Laplacian on
G
, i.e.,

(\Delta_{G}f)(x):=\sum_{{y\in V:}\atop{\{x,y\}\in E}}[f(y)-f(x)],\qquad x\in V,\,f\colon\,V\to\mathbb{R}.

(1.3)

Our object of interest is the non-negative solution of the Cauchy problem with localised initial condition,

\begin{array}[]{llll}\partial_{t}u(x,t)&=&(\Delta_{G}u)(x,t)+\xi(x)u(x,t),&x\in V,\,t>0,\\
u(x,0)&=&\delta_{\mathcal{O}}(x),&x\in V,\end{array}

(1.4)

where
{\mathcal{O}}\in V
is referred to as the
root
of
G
. We say that
G
is
rooted at

{\mathcal{O}}
and call
G=(V,E,{\mathcal{O}})
a
rooted graph
. The quantity
u(x,t)
can be interpreted as the amount of mass present at time
t
at site
x
when initially there is unit mass at
{\mathcal{O}}
.

Criteria for existence and uniqueness of the non-negative solution to (
1.4
) are well-known (see
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
for the case
G=\mathbb{Z}^{d}
), and rely on the
Feynman-Kac formula

u(x,t)={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{O}}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(X_{s})\text{\rm d}\mkern 0.5mus}\,{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}\{X_{t}=x\}\right],

(1.5)

where
X=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the continuous-time random walk on the vertices
V
with jump rate
1
along the edges
E
, and
{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathcal{O}}
denotes the law of
X
given
X_{0}={\mathcal{O}}
. We are interested in the
total mass
of the solution,

U(t):=\sum_{x\in V}u(x,t)={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{O}}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(X_{s})\text{\rm d}\mkern 0.5mus}\right].

(1.6)

Often we suppress the dependence on
G,\xi
from the notation. Note that, by time reversal and the linearity of (
1.4
),
U(t)=\hat{u}(0,t)
with
\hat{u}
the solution of (
1.4
) with a different initial condition, namely,
\hat{u}(x,0)=1
for all
x\in V
.

As in
[
1
]
, throughout the paper we assume that the random potential
\xi=(\xi(x))_{x\in V}
consists of i.i.d. random variables with marginal distribution satisfying:

Assumption 1.1
.

[Asymptotic double-exponential potential]

For some
\varrho\in(0,\infty)
,

\mathrm{P}\left(\xi(0)\geq 0\right)=1,\qquad\mathrm{P}\left(\xi(0)>u\right)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{e}^{u/\varrho}}\;\;\text{for }u\text{ large enough.}

(1.7)

The restrictions in (
1.7
) are helpful to avoid certain technicalities that require no new ideas. In particular, (
1.7
) is enough to guarantee existence and uniqueness of the non-negative solution to (
1.4
) on any discrete graph with at most exponential growth (as can be inferred from the proof in
[
7
]
for the case
G=\mathbb{Z}^{d}
). All our results remain valid under milder restrictions (e.g.
[
7
, Assumption (F)]
plus an integrability condition on the lower tail of
\xi(0)
).

The following
characteristic variational formula
is important for the description of the asymptotics of
U(t)
when
\xi
has a double-exponential tail. Denote by
{\mathcal{P}}(V)
the set of probability measures on
V
. For
p\in{\mathcal{P}}(V)
, define

I_{E}(p):=\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}\left(\sqrt{p(x)}-\sqrt{p(y)}\,\right)^{2},\qquad J_{V}(p):=-\sum_{x\in V}p(x)\log p(x),

(1.8)

and set

\chi_{G}(\varrho):=\inf_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}(V)}[I_{E}(p)+\varrho J_{V}(p)],\qquad\varrho\in(0,\infty).

(1.9)

The first term in (
1.9
) is the quadratic form associated with the Laplacian, describing the solution
u(\cdot,t)
in the intermittent islands, while the second term in (
1.9
) is the Legendre transform of the rate function for the potential, describing the highest peaks of
\xi(\cdot)
in the intermittent islands.

1.3
The PAM on a Galton-Watson tree

Let
D
be a random variable taking values in
\mathbb{N}
. Start with a root vertex
{\mathcal{O}}
, and attach edges from
{\mathcal{O}}
to
D
first-generation vertices. Proceed recursively: after having attached the
n
-th generation of vertices, attach to each one of them independently a number of vertices that has distribution
D
, and declare the union of these vertices to be the
(n+1)
-th generation of vertices. Denote by
{\mathcal{G}\mathcal{W}}=(V,E)
the graph thus obtained and by
\mathfrak{P}
its probability law. Write
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{E}
to denote probability and expectation for
D
, and
\supp(D)
to denote the support of
\mathcal{P}
. The law of
D
is the offspring distribution of
{\mathcal{G}\mathcal{W}}
, the law of
D
is the degree distribution of
{\mathcal{G}\mathcal{W}}
.

Throughout the paper, we assume that the degree distribution satisfies:

Assumption 1.2
.

[Exponential tails]

(1)

d_{\min}:=\min\supp(D)\geq 2
and
\mathcal{E}[D]\in(2,\infty)
.

(2)

\mathcal{E}\big[\mathrm{e}^{aD}\big]<\infty
for all
a\in(0,\infty)
.

Under this assumption,
{\mathcal{G}\mathcal{W}}
is
\mathfrak{P}
-a.s. an infinite tree. Moreover,

\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log|B_{r}({\mathcal{O}})|}{r}=\log\mathcal{E}[D]=:\vartheta\in(0,\infty)\qquad\mathfrak{P}-a.s.,

(1.10)

where
B_{r}({\mathcal{O}})\subset V
is the ball of radius
r
around
{\mathcal{O}}
in the graph distance (see e.g.
[
10
, pp. 134–135]
). Note that this ball depends on
{\mathcal{G}\mathcal{W}}
and therefore is random. For our main result we need an assumption that is much stronger than Assumption
1.2
(2).

Assumption 1.3
.

[Super-double-exponential tails]

There exists a function
f\colon\,(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
satisfying
\lim_{s\to\infty}f(s)=0
and
\lim_{s\to\infty}f^{\prime}(s)=0
such that

\limsup_{s\to\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-s}\log\mathcal{P}(D>s^{f(s)})<-2\vartheta.

(1.11)

To state our main result, we define the constant

\widetilde{\chi}(\varrho):=\inf\big\{\chi_{T}(\varrho)\colon\,T\text{ is an infinite tree with degrees in }\supp(D)\big\},

(1.12)

with
\chi_{G}(\varrho)
defined in (
1.9
), and abbreviate

\mathfrak{r}_{t}=\frac{\varrho t}{\log\log t}.

(1.13)

Theorem 1.4
.

[Quenched Lyapunov exponent]

Subject to Assumptions
1.1
–
1.3
,

\frac{1}{t}\log U(t)=\varrho\log(\vartheta\mathfrak{r}_{t})-\varrho-\widetilde{\chi}(\varrho)+o(1),\quad t\to\infty,\qquad(\mathrm{P}\times\mathfrak{P})\text{-a.s.}

(1.14)

With Theorem
1.4
we have completed our task to relax the main result in
[
1
]
to degree distributions with unbounded support. The extension comes at the price of having to assume a tail that decays faster than double-exponential as shown in (
1.11
). This property is needed to control the occurrence of large degrees
uniformly in large subtrees
of
{\mathcal{G}\mathcal{W}}
. No doubt Assumption
1.3
is stronger than is needed, but to go beyond would require a major overhaul of the methods developed in
[
1
]
, which remains a challenge.

In (
1.4
) the initial mass is located at the root. The asymptotics in (
1.14
) is robust against different choices.

A heuristic explanation where the terms in (
1.14
) come from was given in
[
1
, Section 1.5]
. The asymptotics of
U(t)
is controlled by random walk paths in the Feynman-Kac formula in (
1.6
) that run within time
\mathfrak{r}_{t}/\varrho\log\mathfrak{r}_{t}
to an
intermittent island
at distance
\mathrm{r}_{t}
from
{\mathcal{O}}
, and afterwards stay near that island for the rest of the time. The intermittent island turns out to consist of a subtree with degree
d_{\min}
where the potential has a height
\varrho\log(\vartheta\mathfrak{r}_{t})
and a shape that is the solution of a variational formula restricted to that subtree. The first and third term in (
1.14
) are the contribution of the path after it has reached the island, the second term is the cost for reaching the island.

For
d\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}
, let
{\mathcal{T}}_{d}
be the infinite homogeneous tree in which every node has downward degree
d
. It was shown in
[
1
]
that if
\varrho\geq 1/\log(d_{\rm min}+1)
, then

\widetilde{\chi}(\varrho)=\chi_{{\mathcal{T}}_{d_{\min}}}(\varrho).

(1.15)

Presumably
{\mathcal{T}}_{d_{\min}}
is the
unique
minimizer of (
1.12
), but proving so would require more work.

Structural properties of the Galton-Watson tree

In the section we collect a few structural properties of
{\mathcal{G}\mathcal{W}}
that play an important role throughout the paper. None of these properties was needed in
[
1
]
. Section
2.1
looks at volumes, Section
2.2
at degrees, Section
2.3

2.1
Volumes

Let
Z_{k}
be the number of offspring in generation
k
, i.e.,

Z_{k}=|\{x\in V\colon\,d(x,{\mathcal{O}})=k\}|,

(2.1)

where
d(x,{\mathcal{O}})
is the distance from
{\mathcal{O}}
to
x
. Let
\mu=\mathcal{E}[D]
. Then there exists a random variable
W\in(0,\infty)
such that

W_{k}:=\mathrm{e}^{-k\vartheta}Z_{k}=\mu^{-k}Z_{k}\rightarrow W\qquad\mathfrak{P}\text{-a.s. as }k\to\infty.

(2.2)

It is shown in
[
13
, Theorem 5]
that

\exists\,C<\infty,c>0\colon\,\quad\mathfrak{P}(|W_{k}-W|\geq\varepsilon)\leq C\mathrm{e}^{-c\,\varepsilon^{2/3}\mu^{n/3}}\qquad\forall\,\varepsilon>0,\,k\in\mathbb{N}.

(2.3)

In addition, it is shown in
[
14
, Theorems 2–3]
that if
D
is bounded, then

\displaystyle-\log\mathfrak{P}(W\geq x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{\gamma^{+}/(\gamma^{+}-1)}\,[L^{+}(x)+o(1)],\qquad x\to\infty,

(2.4)

\displaystyle-\log\mathfrak{P}(W\leq x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{-\gamma^{-}/(1-\gamma^{-})}\,[L^{-}(x)+o(1)],\qquad x\downarrow 0,

(2.5)

where
\gamma^{+}\in(1,\infty)
and
\gamma^{-}\in(0,1)
are the unique solutions of the equations

\mu^{\gamma^{+}}=d_{\max},\qquad\mu^{\gamma^{-}}=d_{\min},

(2.6)

with
L^{+},L^{-}\colon(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
real-analytic functions that are multiplicatively periodic with period
\mu^{\gamma^{+}-1}
, respectively,
\mu^{1-\gamma^{-}}
. Note that Assumption
1.2
(1) guarantees that
\gamma^{-}\neq 1
.

The tail behaviour in (
2.4
) requires that
d_{\max}<\infty
. In our setting we have
d_{\max}=\infty
, which corresponds to
\gamma^{+}=\infty
, and so we expect exponential tail behaviour. The following lemma provides a rough bound.

Lemma 2.1
.

[Exponential tail for generation sizes]

If there exists an
a>0
such that
\mathcal{E}[\mathrm{e}^{aD}]<\infty
, then there exists an
a_{*}>0
such that
\mathfrak{E}[\mathrm{e}^{a_{*}W}]<\infty
.

Proof.

First note that if there exists an
a>0
such that
\mathcal{E}[\mathrm{e}^{aD}]<\infty
, then there exist
b>0
large and
c>0
small such that

\varphi(a):=\mathcal{E}[\mathrm{e}^{aD}]\leq\mathrm{e}^{\mu a+ba^{2}}\qquad\forall\,0<a<c.

(2.7)

Hence

\mathfrak{E}[\mathrm{e}^{aZ_{n+1}}]=\mathfrak{E}[\varphi(a)^{Z_{n}}]\leq\mathfrak{E}[\mathrm{e}^{(\mu a+ba^{2})Z_{n}}]

(2.8)

and consequently, because
\mu>1
,

\mathfrak{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{aW_{n+1}}\right]\leq\mathfrak{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{(a+ba^{2}\mu^{-(n+2)})W_{n}}\right]\leq\mathfrak{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{a\exp(bc\mu^{-(n+2)})W_{n}}\right].

(2.9)

Put
a_{n}:=c\exp(-bc\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mu^{-(k+2)})
, which satisfies
0<a_{n}\leq c
. From the last inequality in (
2.9
) it follows that

\mathfrak{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{a_{n+1}W_{n+1}}\right]\leq\mathfrak{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{a_{n}W_{n}}\right].

(2.10)

Since
n\mapsto a_{n}
is decreasing with
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}=a_{*}>0
, Fatou’s lemma gives

\mathfrak{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{a_{*}W}\right]\leq\mathfrak{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{a_{0}W_{0}}\right].

(2.11)

Because
\mathcal{E}[\mathrm{e}^{a_{0}W_{0}}]=\mathrm{e}^{a_{0}}<\infty
, we get the claim.
∎

The following lemma says that
\mathfrak{P}
-a.s. a ball of radius
R_{r}
centred anywhere in
B_{r}({\mathcal{O}})
has volume
\mathrm{e}^{\vartheta R_{r}+o(R_{r})}
as
r\to\infty
, provided
R_{r}
is large compared to
\log r
.

Lemma 2.2
.

[Volumes of large balls]

Subject to Assumption
1.2
(1)
, if there exists an
a>0
such that
\mathcal{E}[\mathrm{e}^{aD}]<\infty
, then for any
R_{r}
satisfying
\lim_{r\to\infty}R_{r}/\log r=\infty
,

\liminf_{r\to\infty}\frac{1}{R_{r}}\log\Big(\inf_{x\in B_{r}({\mathcal{O}})}|B_{R_{r}}(x)|\Big)=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{1}{R_{r}}\log\Big(\sup_{x\in B_{r}({\mathcal{O}})}|B_{R_{r}}(x)|\Big)=\vartheta\qquad\mathfrak{P}-a.s.

(2.12)

Proof.

We first prove the claim for lower balls. Afterwards we use a sandwich argument to get the claim for balls.

For
y\in{\mathcal{G}\mathcal{W}}
that lies
k
generations below
{\mathcal{O}}
, let
y[-i]
,
0\leq i\leq k
be the vertex that lies
i
generations above
y
. Define the
lower ball
of radius around
y
as

B^{\downarrow}_{r}(y):=\{x\in V\colon\,\exists\,0\leq i\leq r\ \text{with}\ x[-i]=y\}.

(2.13)

Note that
B^{\downarrow}_{r}({\mathcal{O}})=B_{r}({\mathcal{O}})
. Let
\mathcal{Z}_{k}
denote the vertices in the
k
-th generation. To get the upper bound, pick
\delta>0
and estimate

\displaystyle\mathfrak{P}\Big(\sup_{x\in B_{r}({\mathcal{O}})}|B_{R_{r}}^{\downarrow}(x)|\geq\mathrm{e}^{(1+\delta)\vartheta R_{r}}\Big)\leq\sum_{k=0}^{r}\mathfrak{P}\Big(\sup_{x\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}}|B_{R_{r}}^{\downarrow}(x)|\geq\mathrm{e}^{(1+\delta)\vartheta R_{r}}\Big)

(2.14)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{N}}\mathfrak{P}\Big(\sup_{x\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}}|B_{R_{r}}^{\downarrow}(x)|\geq\mathrm{e}^{(1+\delta)\vartheta R_{r}}\penalty\ \Big|\penalty\ Z_{k}=l\Big)\mathfrak{P}(Z_{k}=l)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{r}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{N}}l\ \mathfrak{P}\Big(|B^{\downarrow}_{R_{r}}({\mathcal{O}})|\geq\mathrm{e}^{(1+\delta)\vartheta R_{r}}\Big)\mathfrak{P}(Z_{k}=l)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{P}\Big(|B^{\downarrow}_{R_{r}}({\mathcal{O}})|\geq\mathrm{e}^{(1+\delta)\vartheta R_{r}}\Big)\sum_{k=0}^{r}\mathfrak{E}(Z_{k}).

By (
1.10
),
\sum_{k=0}^{r}\mathfrak{E}(Z_{k})=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\vartheta(r+1)}-1}{\mathrm{e}^{\vartheta}-1}=O(\mathrm{e}^{\vartheta r})
, and so in order to be able to apply the Borel-Cantelli lemma, it suffices to show that the probability in the last line decays faster than exponentially in
r
for any
\delta>0
. To that end, estimate

\displaystyle\mathfrak{P}\Big(|B^{\downarrow}_{R_{r}}({\mathcal{O}})|\geq\mathrm{e}^{(1+\delta)\vartheta R_{r}}\Big)=\mathfrak{P}\Big(\mathrm{e}^{-\vartheta R_{r}}\sum_{k=0}^{R_{r}}Z_{k}\geq\mathrm{e}^{\delta\vartheta R_{r}}\Big)

(2.15)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{P}\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{R_{r}}W_{k}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\vartheta(R_{r}-k)}\geq\mathrm{e}^{\delta\vartheta R_{r}}\Big)\leq\sum_{k=0}^{R_{r}}\mathfrak{P}\Big(W_{k}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\vartheta(R_{r}-k)}\geq\frac{1}{R_{r}+1}\mathrm{e}^{\delta\vartheta R_{r}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{R_{r}}\mathfrak{P}\Big(W+(W_{k}-W)\geq\frac{1}{R_{r}+1}\mathrm{e}^{\delta\vartheta R_{r}}\mathrm{e}^{\vartheta(R_{r}-k)}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{R_{r}}\mathfrak{P}\Big(W\geq\frac{1}{2(R_{r}+1)}\mathrm{e}^{\delta\vartheta R_{r}}\mathrm{e}^{\vartheta(R_{r}-k)}\Big)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=0}^{R_{r}}\mathfrak{P}\Big(|W_{k}-W|\geq\frac{1}{2(R_{r}+1)}\mathrm{e}^{\delta\vartheta R_{r}}\mathrm{e}^{\vartheta(R_{r}-k)}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathfrak{E}[\mathrm{e}^{a_{*}W}]\sum_{k=0}^{R_{r}}\exp\Big(-a_{*}\frac{1}{2(R_{r}+1)}\mathrm{e}^{\delta\vartheta R_{r}}\mathrm{e}^{\vartheta(R_{r}-k)}\Big)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=0}^{R_{r}}C\exp\Big(-c\Big[\frac{1}{2(R_{r}+1)}\,\mathrm{e}^{\delta\vartheta R_{r}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\vartheta(R_{r}-k)}\Big]^{2/3}(\mathrm{e}^{\vartheta})^{k/3}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathfrak{E}[\mathrm{e}^{a_{*}W}](R_{r}+1)\exp\Big(-a_{*}\frac{1}{2(R_{r}+1)}\mathrm{e}^{\delta\vartheta R_{r}}\Big)

\displaystyle+C(R_{r}+1)\exp\Big(-c\Big[\frac{1}{2(R_{r}+1)}\,\mathrm{e}^{\delta\vartheta R_{r}}\Big]^{2/3}\Big),

where we use (
2.3
) with
\mu=\mathrm{e}^{\vartheta}
. This produces the desired estimate.

To get the lower bound, pick
0<\delta<1
and estimate

\displaystyle\mathfrak{P}\Big(\inf_{x\in B_{r}({\mathcal{O}})}|B_{R_{r}}^{\downarrow}(x)|\leq\mathrm{e}^{(1-\delta)\vartheta R_{r}}\Big)\leq\sum_{k=0}^{r}\mathfrak{P}\Big(\inf_{x\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}}|B_{R_{r}}^{\downarrow}(x)|\leq\mathrm{e}^{(1-\delta)\vartheta R_{r}}\Big)

(2.16)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\sum\limits_{l\in\mathbb{N}}\mathfrak{P}\Big(\inf_{x\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}}|B_{R_{r}}^{\downarrow}(x)|\leq\mathrm{e}^{(1-\delta)\vartheta R_{r}}\penalty\ \Big|\penalty\ Z_{k}=l\Big)\mathfrak{P}(Z_{k}=l)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{r}\sum\limits_{l\in\mathbb{N}}l\,\mathfrak{P}\Big(|B^{\downarrow}_{R_{r}}({\mathcal{O}})|\leq\mathrm{e}^{(1-\delta)\vartheta R_{r}}\Big)\mathfrak{P}(Z_{k}=l)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{P}\Big(|B^{\downarrow}_{R_{r}}({\mathcal{O}})|\leq\mathrm{e}^{(1-\delta)\vartheta R_{r}}\Big)\sum\limits_{k=0}^{r}\mathfrak{E}(Z_{k}).

It again suffices to show that the probability in the last line decays faster than exponentially in
r
for any
\delta>0
. To that end, estimate

\displaystyle\mathfrak{P}\Big(|B^{\downarrow}_{R_{r}}({\mathcal{O}})|\leq\mathrm{e}^{(1-\delta)\vartheta R_{r}}\Big)=\mathfrak{P}\Big(\mathrm{e}^{-\vartheta R_{r}}\sum_{k=0}^{R_{r}}Z_{k}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\delta\vartheta R_{r}}\Big)

(2.17)

\displaystyle\leq\mathfrak{P}\Big(W_{R_{r}}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\delta\vartheta R_{r}}\Big)\leq\mathfrak{P}(W\leq 2\,\mathrm{e}^{-\delta\vartheta R_{r}})+\mathfrak{P}(W-W_{R_{r}}\geq\mathrm{e}^{-\delta\vartheta R_{r}})

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Big(-c^{-}(2\mathrm{e}^{\delta\vartheta R_{r}})^{\frac{\gamma^{-}}{1-\gamma^{-}}}[1+o(1)]\Big)+C\exp\Big(-c\,[\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2}{3}\delta\vartheta}(\mathrm{e}^{\vartheta})^{\frac{1}{3}}]^{R_{r}}\Big),

where we use (
2.5
), (
2.3
) with
\mu=\mathrm{e}^{\vartheta}
, and put
c^{-}:=\inf L^{-}\in(0,\infty)
. For
\delta
small enough this produces the desired estimate. This completes the proof of (
2.12
) for lower balls.

To get the claim for balls, we observe that

B_{r}^{\downarrow}(x)\subseteq B_{r}(x)\subseteq\bigcup_{k=0}^{r}B_{r}^{\downarrow}(x[-k]),

(2.18)

and therefore

|B_{r}^{\downarrow}(x)|\leq|B_{r}(x)|\leq\sum_{k=0}^{r}|B_{r}^{\downarrow}(x[-k])|.

(2.19)

It follows from (
2.19
) that

\inf_{x\in B_{r}({\mathcal{O}})}|B_{r}^{\downarrow}(x)|\leq\inf_{x\in B_{r}({\mathcal{O}})}|B_{r}(x)|\leq\sup_{x\in B_{r}({\mathcal{O}})}|B_{r}(x)|\leq(r+1)\sup_{x\in B_{r}({\mathcal{O}})}|B_{r}^{\downarrow}(x)|.

(2.20)

Hence we get (
2.12
).
∎

2.2

Write

to denote the degree of vertex

. The following lemma implies that,
\mathfrak{P}
-a.s. and for

,

is bounded by a vanishing power of

Lemma 2.3
.

[Maximal degree in a ball around the root]

(a) Subject to Assumption
1.2
(2)
, for every
\delta>0
,

\sum_{r\in\mathbb{N}}\mathfrak{P}\big(\exists\,x\in B_{2r}({\mathcal{O}})\colon\,D_{x}>\delta r\big)<\infty.

(2.21)

(b) Subject to Assumption
1.3
, there exists a function
\delta_{r}\colon\,(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
satisfying
\lim_{r\to\infty}\delta_{r}

=0
and
\lim_{r\to\infty}r\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}=0
such that

(2.22)

Proof.

(a) Estimate

\displaystyle\mathfrak{P}\big(\exists\,x\in B^{\downarrow}_{2r}({\mathcal{O}})\colon\,D_{x}>\delta r\big)\leq\sum_{k=0}^{2r}\mathfrak{P}\big(\exists\,x\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}\colon\,D_{x}>\delta r\big)

(2.23)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{2r}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{N}}\mathfrak{P}\big(\exists\,x\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}\colon\,D_{x}>\delta r\mid Z_{k}=l\big)\,\mathfrak{P}(Z_{k}=l)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{P}(D>\delta r)\sum_{k=0}^{2r}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{N}}l\,\mathfrak{P}\big(Z_{k}=l)=\mathcal{P}(D>\delta r)\sum_{k=0}^{2r}\mathfrak{E}(Z_{k}).

Since

, it suffices to show that
\mathcal{P}(D>\delta r)=O(\mathrm{e}^{-cr})
for some
c>2\vartheta
. Since
\mathcal{P}(D>\delta r)\leq\mathrm{e}^{-a\delta r}\mathcal{E}(\mathrm{e}^{aD})
, the latter is immediate from Assumption
1.2
(2) when we choose

.

(b) The only change is that in the last line

must be replaced by
\mathcal{P}(D>(\log r)^{\delta_{r}})
. To see that the latter is
O(\mathrm{e}^{-cr})
for some

, we use the tail condition in (
1.11
) with
\delta_{r}=f(s)
and
s=\log r
.
∎

2.3
Tree animals

For

and

, let

be the set of

of size

that contain
x
. Put

.

Lemma 2.4
.

[Number of tree animals]

Subject to Assumption
1.2
(2)
,
\mathfrak{P}
-a.s. there exists an

such that

for all

,

and

.

Proof.

We first prove the claim for lower tree animals. Afterwards we us a sandwich argument to get the claim for tree animals.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
x\in B^{\downarrow}_{r}({\mathcal{O}})
, let

\mathcal{A}^{\downarrow}_{n}(x)=\{\Lambda\subset B^{\downarrow}_{n}(x)\colon\,\Lambda\text{ is connected},\Lambda\ni x,|\Lambda|=n+1\}

(2.25)

be the set of
lower tree animals
of size
n+1
that contain
x
. Put

. Fix
\delta>0
. By Lemma
2.3
(a) and the Borel-Cantelli lemma,
\mathfrak{P}
-a.s. there exists an

3.2
Mass up to an exit time

Lemma 3.2
.

[Mass up to an exit time]

Subject to Assumption
1.2
(2)
,
\mathfrak{P}
-a.s. for any
\delta>0
,
r\geq r_{0}
,
y\in\Lambda\subset B_{r}({\mathcal{O}})
,

and
\gamma>\lambda_{\Lambda}=\lambda_{\Lambda}(\xi,{\mathcal{G}\mathcal{W}})

\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\int_{0}^{\tau_{\Lambda^{\text{\rm c}}}}(\xi(X_{s})-\gamma)\,\text{\rm d}\mkern 0.5mus}\right]\leq 1+\frac{(\delta r)\,|\Lambda|}{\gamma-\lambda_{\Lambda}}.

(3.13)

Proof.

We follow the proof of
[
7
, Lemma 2.18]
and
[
12
, Lemma 4.2]
. Define

u(x):=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\int_{0}^{\tau_{\Lambda^{\text{\rm c}}}}(\xi(X_{s})-\gamma)\,\text{\rm d}\mkern 0.5mus}\right].

(3.14)

This is the solution to the boundary value problem

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\Delta+\xi-\gamma)u&=0\quad\text{on}\ \Lambda\\
u&=1\quad\text{on}\ \Lambda^{\text{\rm c}}.\end{split}

(3.15)

Via the substitution
u=:1+v
, this turns into

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\Delta+\xi-\gamma)v&=\gamma-\xi\quad\text{on}\ \Lambda\\
v&=0\qquad\quad\text{on}\ \Lambda^{\text{\rm c}}.\end{split}

(3.16)

It is readily checked that for
\gamma>\lambda_{\Lambda}
the solution exists and is given by

v=\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}(\xi-\gamma),

(3.17)

where
\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}
denotes the resolvent of
\Delta+\xi
in
\ell^{2}(\Lambda)
with Dirichlet boundary condition. Hence

v(x)\leq(\delta r)\,(\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}\mathds{1})(x)\leq(\delta r)\,\langle\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}\mathds{1},\mathds{1}\rangle_{\Lambda}\leq\frac{(\delta r)\,|\Lambda|}{\gamma-\lambda_{\Lambda}},\quad x\in\Lambda,

(3.18)

where
\mathds{1}
denotes the constant function equal to
1
, and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\Lambda}
denotes the inner product in
\ell^{2}(\Lambda)
. To get the first inequality, we combine Lemma
2.3
(a) with the lower bound in (

) from Lemma

, to get
\xi-\gamma\leq\lambda_{\Lambda}+\delta r-\gamma\leq\delta r
on
\Lambda
. The positivity of the resolvent gives

0\leq[\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}(\delta\log r-(\xi-\gamma))](x)=(\delta r)\,[\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}\mathds{1}](x)-[\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}(\xi-\gamma)](x).

(3.19)

To get the second inequality, we write

(\delta r)\,(\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}\mathds{1})(x)\leq(\delta r)\sum_{x\in\Lambda}(\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}\mathds{1})(x)=(\delta r)\sum_{x\in\Lambda}(\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}\mathds{1})(x)\mathds{1}(x)=(\delta r)\,\langle\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}\mathds{1},\mathds{1}\rangle_{\Lambda}.

(3.20)

To get the third inequality, we use the Fourier expansion of the resolvent with respect to the orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions of
\Delta+\xi
in
\ell^{2}(\Lambda)
.

5.1
Upper bound

We follow
[
1
, Section 4.2]
. The proof of the upper bound in (

\lceil t^{\gamma}\rceil
for some

or leaving a ball of radius

have a negligible contribution to (

Lemma 5.1
.

[No long paths]

For any
\ell_{t}\geq t\log t
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{U^{*}(t)}\,{\mathbb{E}}_{{\mathcal{O}}}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s}{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{\tau_{[B_{\ell_{t}}]^{\text{\rm c}}}<t\}}\right]=0\quad(\mathrm{P}\times\mathfrak{P})-a.s.

(5.3)

Proof.

We follow
[
1
, Lemma 4.2]
. For
r\geq\ell_{t}
, let

\mathcal{B}_{r}:=\left\{\max_{x\in B_{r}({\mathcal{O}})}\xi(x)\geq a_{L_{r}}+2\varrho\right\}.

(5.4)

Since
\lim_{t\to\infty}\ell_{t}=\infty
, Lemma
3.5
gives that
\mathrm{P}
-a.s.

\bigcup_{r\geq\ell_{t}}\mathcal{B}_{r}\text{ does not occur eventually as }t\to\infty.

(5.5)

Therefore we can work on the event
\bigcap_{r\geq\ell_{t}}[\mathcal{B}_{r}]^{\text{\rm c}}
. On this event, we write

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{{\mathcal{O}}}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s}{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{\tau_{[B_{\ell_{t}}]^{\text{\rm c}}}<t\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\geq\ell_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}_{{\mathcal{O}}}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s}{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|X_{s}|=r\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{2\varrho t}\sum_{r\geq\ell_{t}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\varrho t\log r+\log(\delta_{r}\log\log r)}\,{\mathbb{P}}_{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(J_{t}\geq r\right),

(5.6)

where
J_{t}
is the number of jumps of
X
up to time
t
, and we use that
|B_{r}({\mathcal{O}})|\leq(\log r)^{\delta_{r}r}
. Next,

is stochastically dominated by a Poisson random variable with parameter
t(\log r)^{\delta_{r}}
. Hence

{\mathbb{P}}_{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(J_{t}\geq r\right)\leq\frac{[\mathrm{e}t\,(\log r)^{\delta_{r}}]^{r}}{r^{r}}\leq\exp\left\{-r\log\left(\frac{r}{\mathrm{e}t\,(\log r)^{\delta_{r}}}\right)\right\}

(5.7)

for large

. Using that

and

large enough,

\varrho t\log r-r\log\left(\frac{r}{\mathrm{e}t\,(\log r)^{\delta_{r}}}\right)<-3r,\qquad r\geq\ell_{t}.

(5.8)

Thus (
5.1
) is at most

\mathrm{e}^{2\varrho t}\sum_{r\geq\ell_{t}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-3r+\log(\delta_{r}\log\log r)}\,\leq\mathrm{e}^{2\varrho t}\sum_{r\geq\ell_{t}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-2r}\leq 2\,\mathrm{e}^{2\varrho t}\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\ell_{t}}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\ell_{t}}.

(5.9)

Since
\lim_{t\to\infty}\ell_{t}=\infty
and
\lim_{t\to\infty}U^{*}(t)=\infty
, this settles the claim.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

[No short paths]

For any
\gamma\in(0,1)
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{U^{*}(t)}\,{\mathbb{E}}_{{\mathcal{O}}}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s}{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{\tau_{[B_{\lceil t^{\gamma}\rceil}]^{\text{\rm c}}}>t\}}\right]=0\quad(\mathrm{P}\times\mathfrak{P})-a.s.

Proof.

We follow
[
1
, Lemma 4.3]
. By Lemma

where the second inequality uses that

\frac{1}{U^{*}(t)}\,{\mathbb{E}}_{{\mathcal{O}}}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s}{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{\tau_{[B_{\lceil t^{\gamma}\rceil}]^{\text{\rm c}}}>t\}}\right]\leq\frac{1}{U^{*}(t)}\,\mathrm{e}^{\gamma\varrho t\log t+O(1)}\leq\mathrm{e}^{(1-\gamma)\varrho t\log t+C\log\log\log t},\quad t\to\infty,

[Upper bound on

[

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{z}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{\int_{0}^{v}\xi(X_{u})\,\mathrm{d}u}{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{X_{u}\in B_{R}(z)\,\forall u\in[0,v]\}}\Big]

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{e}^{v\varrho\log\log|B_{\ell}|}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{Y}}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{\int_{0}^{v}q(X_{u})\,\mathrm{d}u}{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{X_{u}\in Q_{R}\,\forall u\in[0,v]\}}\Big]

(5.31)

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{e}^{v\varrho\log\log|B_{\ell}|}\mathrm{e}^{v\lambda_{Q_{R}}(q;T)}\phi^{{{\scriptscriptstyle{({1}})}}}_{Q_{R}}(\mathcal{Y})^{2}

\displaystyle>\exp\big\{v\left(\varrho\log\log|B_{\ell}|-\widetilde{\chi}(\varrho)-\varepsilon\right)\big\}

for large
v
, where we used that
B_{R+1}(z)
is isomorphic to
Q_{R+1}
for the indicators in the first inequality, and applied Lemma
B.2
and (
5.28
) to obtain the second and third inequalities, respectively. On the other hand, since
\xi\geq 0
,

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{O}}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{\int_{0}^{\tau_{z}}\xi(X_{u})\,\mathrm{d}u}{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}{\{\tau_{z}\leq s\}}\Big]\geq{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathcal{O}}(\tau_{z}\leq s),

(5.32)

and we can bound the latter probability from below by the probability that the random walk runs along a shortest path from the root
{\mathcal{O}}
to
z
within a time at most
s
. Such a path
(y_{i})_{i=0}^{|z|}
has
y_{0}={\mathcal{O}}
,
y_{|z|}=z
,
y_{i}\sim y_{i-1}
for
i=1,\ldots,|z|
, has at each step from
y_{i}
precisely
\deg(y_{i})
choices for the next step with equal probability, and the step is carried out after an exponential time
E_{i}
with parameter
\deg(y_{i})
. This gives

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}_{\mathcal{O}}(\tau_{z}\leq s)

\displaystyle\geq\Big(\prod_{i=1}^{|z|}\frac{1}{\deg(y_{i})}\Big)P\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{|z|}E_{i}\leq s\Big)\geq((\log|z|)^{\delta_{\ell}})^{-|z|}{\rm Poi}_{d_{\rm min}s}([|z|,\infty)),

(5.33)

where
{\rm Poi}_{\gamma}
is the Poisson distribution with parameter
\gamma
, and
P
is the generic symbol for probability. Summarising, we obtain

\displaystyle U(t)

\displaystyle\geq((\log|z|)^{\delta_{l}})^{-|z|}\mathrm{e}^{-d_{\rm min}s}\frac{(d_{\rm min}s)^{|z|}}{|z|!}\mathrm{e}^{(t-s)\left[\varrho\log\log|B_{\ell}|-\widetilde{\chi}(\varrho)-\varepsilon\right]}

(5.34)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left\{-d_{\min}s+(t-s)\left[\varrho\log\log|B_{\ell}|-\widetilde{\chi}(\varrho)-\varepsilon\right]-|z|\log\left(\frac{(\log|z|)^{\delta_{\ell}}}{d_{\min}}\frac{|z|}{s}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left\{-d_{\min}s+(t-s)\left[\varrho\log\log|B_{\ell}|-\widetilde{\chi}(\varrho)-\varepsilon\right]-\ell\log\left(\frac{(\log\ell)^{\delta_{\ell}}}{d_{\min}}\frac{\ell}{s}\right)\right\},

where in the last inequality we use that
s\leq|z|
and
\ell\geq|z|
. Further assuming that
\ell=o(t)
, we see that the optimum over
s
is obtained at

s=\frac{\ell}{d_{\min}+\varrho\log\log|B_{\ell}|-\widetilde{\chi}(\varrho)-\varepsilon}=o(t).

(5.35)

Note that, by Proposition
5.4
, this
s
indeed satisfies
s\leq|z|
. Applying (
1.10
) we get, after a straightforward computation,
(\mathfrak{P}\times\mathrm{P})
-a.s. eventually as
t\to\infty
,

\frac{1}{t}\log U(t)\geq\varrho\log\log|B_{\ell}|-\frac{\ell}{t}\log\log\ell-\frac{\ell}{t}\delta_{\ell}\log\log\ell-\widetilde{\chi}(\varrho)-\varepsilon+O\left(\frac{\ell}{t}\right).

(5.36)

Inserting
\log|B_{\ell}|\sim\vartheta\ell
, we get

\frac{1}{t}\log U(t)\geq F_{\ell}-\widetilde{\chi}(\varrho)-\varepsilon+o(1)+O\left(\frac{\ell}{t}\right)

(5.37)

with

F_{\ell}=\varrho\log(\vartheta\ell)-\frac{\ell}{t}\log\log\ell-\frac{\ell}{t}\delta_{\ell}\log\log\ell.

(5.38)

The optimal
\ell
for
F_{\ell}
satisfies

\varrho t=\ell\big[1+(\delta_{\ell}+\ell\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\ell}\delta_{\ell})]\log\log\ell+\frac{\ell\delta_{\ell}}{\log\ell}+\frac{\ell}{\log\ell},

(5.39)

i.e.,
\ell=\mathfrak{r}_{t}[1+o(1)]
. For this choice we obtain

\frac{1}{t}\log U(t)\geq\varrho\log(\vartheta\mathfrak{r}_{t})-\varrho-\widetilde{\chi}(\varrho)-\varepsilon+o(1).

(5.40)

Hence
(\mathfrak{P}\times\mathrm{P})
-a.s.

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\left\{\frac{1}{t}\log U(t)-\frac{1}{t}\log U^{*}(t)\right\}\geq-\varepsilon.

(5.41)

Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, this completes the proof of the lower bound in (
1.14
).

Appendix B
Largest eigenvalue

We recall the Rayleigh-Ritz formula for the principal eigenvalue of the Anderson Hamiltonian. For
\Lambda\subset V
and
q\colon\,V\to[-\infty,\infty)
, let
\lambda_{\Lambda}(q;G)
denote the largest eigenvalue of the operator
\Delta_{G}+q
in
\Lambda
with Dirichlet boundary conditions on
V\setminus\Lambda
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\Lambda}(q;G):=\sup\big\{\langle(\Delta_{G}+q)\phi,\phi\rangle_{\ell^{2}(V)}\colon\,\phi\in\mathbb{R}^{V},\,\supp\phi\subset\Lambda,\,\|\phi\|_{\ell^{2}(V)}=1\big\}.

(B.1)

Lemma B.1
.

[Spectral bounds]

(1)

For any
\Gamma\subset\Lambda\subset V
,

\max_{z\in\Gamma}q(z)-D_{\bar{z}}\leq\lambda_{\Gamma}(q;G)\leq\lambda_{\Lambda}(q;G)\leq\max_{z\in\Lambda}q(z)

(B.2)

with
\bar{z}=\mathrm{arg}\max_{z\in\Gamma}q(z)
and
D_{\bar{z}}
the degree of
\bar{z}
.

(2)

The eigenfunction corresponding to
\lambda_{\Lambda}(q;G)
can be taken to be non-negative.

(3)

If
q
is real-valued and
\Gamma\subsetneq\Lambda
is finite and connected in
G
, then the second inequality in (
B.2
) is strict and the eigenfunction corresponding to
\lambda_{\Lambda}(q;G)
is strictly positive.

Proof.

Write

\displaystyle\langle(\Delta_{G}+q)\phi,\phi\rangle_{\ell^{2}(V)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\left[(\Delta_{G}\phi)(x)+q(x)\phi(x)\right]\phi(x)

(B.3)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\sum_{{y\in\Lambda:}\atop{\{x,y\}\in E_{\Lambda}}}[\phi(y)-\phi(x)]\phi(x)+\sum_{x\in\Lambda}q(x)\phi(x)^{2}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{{x,y\in\Lambda:}\atop{\{x,y\}\in E_{\Lambda}}}[\phi(x)-\phi(y)]^{2}+\sum_{x\in\Lambda}q(x)\phi(x)^{2},

where the first sum in the last line runs over all ordered pairs
(x,y)
with
(x,y)\neq(y,x)
, which gives rise to the factor
\tfrac{1}{2}
. The upper bound in (
B.2
) follows from the estimate

\langle(\Delta_{G}+q)\phi,\phi\rangle\leq\sum_{x\in\Lambda}q(x)\phi(x)^{2}\leq\max_{z\in\Lambda}q(z)\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\phi(x)^{2}=\max_{z\in\Lambda}q(z).

(B.4)

To get the lower bound in (
B.2
), we use the fact that
\lambda_{\Lambda}
is non-decreasing in
q
. Hence,
replacing
q(z)
by
-\infty
for every
z\neq\bar{z}
and taking as test function
\phi=\bar{\phi}=\delta_{\bar{z}}
, we get from (
B.3
) that

\displaystyle\lambda_{\Lambda}(q;G)

\displaystyle\geq-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{{x,y\in\Lambda:}\atop{\{x,y\}\in E_{\Lambda}}}\left[\bar{\phi}(x)-\bar{\phi}(y)\right]^{2}+\sum_{x\in\Lambda}q(x)\bar{\phi}(x)^{2}

(B.5)

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{{y\in\Lambda:}\atop{\{\bar{z},y\}\in E_{\Lambda}}}1+q(\bar{z})=-D_{\bar{z}}+\max_{z\in\Lambda}q(z),

which settles the claim in (1). The claims in (2) and (3) are standard.
∎
