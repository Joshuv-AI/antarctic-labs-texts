Functional Acceleration for Policy Mirror Descent
Veronica Chelu, Doina Precup

Abstract
We apply functional acceleration to the Policy Mirror Descent (PMD) general family of algorithms, which cover a wide range of novel and fundamental methods in Reinforcement Learning (RL). Leveraging duality, we propose a momentum-based PMD update. By taking the functional route, our approach is independent of the policy parametrization and applicable to large-scale optimization, covering previous applications of momentum at the level of policy parameters as a special case. We theoretically analyze several properties of this approach and complement with a numerical ablation study, which serves to illustrate the policy optimization dynamics on the value polytope, relative to different algorithmic design choices in this space. We further characterize numerically several features of the problem setting relevant for functional acceleration, and lastly, we investigate the impact of approximation on their learning mechanics 1 1 1 Code is available at https://github.com/veronicachelu/functional-acceleration-for-pmd .

Introduction

The RL framework
(
Sutton & Barto, 2018
)
refers to the problem of solving sequential decision making tasks under uncertainty, together with a class of solution methods tailored for it. The RL problem has found applications in games
(
Tesauro, 1994
;
Mnih et al., 2013
;
Silver et al., 2014
;
Mnih et al., 2016
;
Silver et al., 2017
;
Hessel et al., 2017
;
Bellemare et al., 2017
;
Schrittwieser et al., 2019
;
Zahavy et al., 2023
)
, robotic manipulation
(
Schulman et al., 2015
;
Schulman et al., 2017
;
Haarnoja et al., 2018
)
, medicine
(
Jumper et al., 2021
;
Schaefer et al., 2004
;
Nie et al., 2020
)
and is formally described by means of discounted Markov Decision Processes (MDPs)
(
Puterman, 1994
)
. On the solution side, increased interest has been devoted to the study of policy-gradient (PG) methods based on optimizing a parameterised policy with respect to an objective
(
Williams, 1992
;
Konda & Borkar, 1999
;
Sutton et al., 1999
;
Agarwal et al., 2019
;
Bhandari & Russo, 2019
;
Kakade, 2001
;
Bhandari & Russo, 2021
;
Mei et al., 2020b
;
Mei et al., 2020a
)
.

Policy Mirror Descent (PMD)
(
Agarwal et al., 2019
;
Bhandari & Russo, 2021
;
Xiao, 2022
;
Johnson et al., 2023
;
Vaswani et al., 2021
)
is a general family of algorithms, specified by the choice of mirror map covering a wide range of novel and fundamental methods in RL.
PMD is a proximal algorithm
(
Parikh et al., 2014
)
and an instance of Mirror Descent (MD)
(
Beck & Teboulle, 2003
)
on the policy simplex
(
Bhandari & Russo, 2021
)
. MD is an iterative optimization algorithm that extends gradient descent (GD) by adapting to the geometry of the problem using different distance functions, particularly Bregman divergences
(
Amari, 1998
;
Bubeck, 2015
;
Banerjee et al., 2005
)
. PMD applies a proximal regularization, with a Bregman divergence, to the improvement step of Policy Iteration (PI), and converges to it as regularization decreases. In the discounted setting, with an adaptive step-size, PMD converges linearly at a rate determined by the discount factor, independent of the dimension of the state space or problem instance
(
Johnson et al., 2023
)
, recovering classical approaches, like PI and VI, as special cases.
PMD has been extended to linear approximation by
Yuan et al. (2023)
and to general function approximation by
Alfano et al. (2024)
.

Motivation
The running time of PMD algorithms scales with the number of iterations, and with a parametrized policy class, each iteration of an approximate PMD method generally requires multiple “inner-loop” updates to the policy parameter
(
Tomar et al., 2020
;
Vaswani et al., 2021
)
. Actor-critic (AC) methods
(
Sutton et al., 1999
;
Konda & Borkar, 1999
)
additionally require the computation or updating of an inexact critic at each iteration.
It is therefore desirable to design algorithms which converge in a smaller number of iterations, resulting in significant empirical speedups, as has been previously argued by
Johnson et al. (2023)
;
Xiao (2022)
;
Goyal & Grand-Clement (2021)
;
Russo (2022)
.

In this work,
we leverage duality and acceleration to build a novel surrogate objective for momentum-based PMD, leading to faster learning in terms of less iterations necessary to converge.
The novelty of our approach is the application of acceleration mechanics to the
direct
or
functional policy representation
\pi
—hence named
functional acceleration
, as opposed to classic acceleration applied to the policy parameter
\theta
(e.g.,
Mnih et al. (2016)
;
Hessel et al. (2017)
;
Schulman et al. (2017)
use Adam
(
Kingma & Ba, 2015
)
or RMSProp
(
Hinton et al., 2012
)
).
Specifically, we use momentum in the dual policy space to accelerate on “long ravines” or decelerate at “sharp curvatures” at the functional level of the policy optimization objective. Intuitively, adding momentum to the functional PG (the gradient of the policy performance objective with respect to the direct policy representation
\pi
) means applying, to the current directional policy derivative, a weighted version of the previous policy ascent direction, encouraging the method to adaptively accelerate according to the geometry of the optimization problem.

Contributions

•

We illustrate and analyze theoretically the impact of applying functional acceleration on the optimization dynamics of PMD, leading to a practical momentum-based PMD algorithm.

•

We
characterize the properties of the problem setting, and those intrinsic to the algorithm, for which applying functional acceleration is conducive to faster learning.

•

We study the influence of an inexact critic on the acceleration mechanism proposed.

Accelerated optimization methods

As far as we know, our idea of applying acceleration to the direct (functional) policy representation
\pi_{\theta}
—independent of the policy parametrization
\theta

Farahmand & Ghavamzadeh, 2021

Goyal & Grand-Clement, 2021

Mnih et al. (2016)
;
Hessel et al. (2017)
;
Schulman et al. (2017)
) use some form of adaptive gradient method, like Adam
(
Kingma & Ba, 2015
)
or RMSProp
(
Hinton et al., 2012
)
. The idea of acceleration generally relies on convexity of the objective relative to the representation of interest. The transformation from parameters
\theta
to functional representation of the policy as probabilities
\pi_{\theta}

(
Lu et al., 2017
)
(relative to the mirror map

) of the objective with respect to
\pi
when constructing surrogate models
(
Bhandari & Russo, 2019
;
Bhandari & Russo, 2021
;
Agarwal et al., 2019
;
Vaswani et al., 2021
)
. These properties suggests the functional acceleration mechanism is feasible and promising in our setting, since it is able to successfully accelerate convex optimization
(
Joulani et al., 2020b
)
.

Approximate PMD
PMD has been extended to function approximation by

)
,
rather than in proximal form
(
Beck & Teboulle, 2003
)
, as used by
Tomar et al. (2020)

Background & Preliminaries

RL
We consider a standard RL setting described by means of a Markov decision process (MDP)
\left(\mathscr{S},\mathscr{A},r,P,\gamma,\rho\right)
, with state space
\mathscr{S}
, action space
\mathscr{A}
, discount factor
\gamma\in[0,1)
, initial state distribution
\rho\in\Delta(S)
(
\Delta(\mathcal{X})
—the probability simplex over a set
\mathcal{X}
), rewards are sampled from a reward function
R\sim r(S,A),r:\mathscr{S}\times\mathscr{A}\to[0,R_{\max}]
, and next states from a transition probability distribution
S^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot|S,A)\in\Delta(\mathscr{S})
.
The RL problem
(
Sutton & Barto, 2018
)
consists in finding a policy
\textstyle\pi\!:\!\mathscr{S}\!\to\!\Delta_{\mathscr{A}}\!\in\!\Pi\!\equiv\!\Delta_{\mathscr{A}}^{|\mathscr{S}|}
, maximizing the performance objective defined as the discounted expected cumulative reward
V^{\rho}_{\pi}\doteq\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\rho}V_{\pi}(s)\in\mathbb{R}
,
where
\textstyle V_{\pi}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{|\mathscr{S}|}
and
\textstyle Q_{\pi}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{|\mathscr{S}|\times|\mathscr{A}|}
are the value and action-value functions of a policy
\pi
, such that
V_{\pi}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{i}R_{i+1}|S_{0}=s\right]
,
Q_{\pi}({s,a})\doteq\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{i}R_{i}|S_{0}=s,A_{0}=a\right]
and
V_{\pi}(s)\doteq\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}\left[Q(s,a)\right]
.
There exists an optimal deterministic policy
\pi^{*}
that simultaneously maximizes
V_{\pi}
and
Q_{\pi}

(
Bellman, 1957
)
.
Let
d_{\pi}
be the discounted visitation distribution
\textstyle d^{\rho}_{\pi}(s)\!=\!(1\!-\!\gamma)\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{i}\operatorname{Pr}(S_{i}=s|S_{0}\sim\rho,A_{j}\sim\pi({s_{j}}),\forall j\leq i)
. We use the shorthand notation
\textstyle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
—the dot product,
\textstyle\nabla f(x)\doteq\nabla_{x}f(x)
—gradients and partial derivatives,
\textstyle\nabla f(x,y)\doteq\nabla_{x}f(x,y)
,
\pi_{t}\doteq\pi_{\theta_{t}}
,
Q_{t}\doteq Q_{\pi_{t}}
,
V_{t}\doteq V_{\pi_{t}}
,
d^{\rho}_{t}\doteq d^{\rho}_{\pi_{t}}
,
\forall t
.

PG Algorithms
update the parameters
\theta\in\Theta
of a parametric policy
\pi_{\theta}
using surrogate objectives that are local approximations of the original performance. In the tabular setting, the direct parametrization associates a parameter to each state-action pair, allowing the shorthand notation
\pi\doteq\pi_{\theta}
. The gradient of the performance
V^{\rho}_{\pi}
with respect to the
direct/functional representation
\pi

(
Sutton et al., 1999
;
Agarwal et al., 2019
;
Bhandari & Russo, 2019
)
—which we call the
“functional” gradient
(to distinguish it from the gradient with respect to the policy parameter
\nabla_{\theta}V^{\rho}_{\pi_{\theta}}
), is

\nabla_{\pi({a|s})}V_{\rho}^{\pi}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{(1-\gamma)}}d^{\rho}_{\pi}(s)Q_{\pi}({s,a})\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathscr{A}|}
.
Then, we define
\nabla V^{\rho}_{\pi}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{|\mathscr{S}|\!\times\!|\mathscr{A}|}
as the concatenation of
\nabla_{\pi({a|s})}V^{\rho}_{\pi},\forall s\!\in\!\mathscr{S}
(yielding a PGT for directional derivatives— Lemma
2
in Appendix
B.1.1
).

Mirror Descent (MD)
is a general GD algorithm, applicable to constrained spaces
\mathcal{C}
, which relies on Fenchel conjugate duality to map the iterates of an optimization problem
x^{*}=\operatorname{argmin}_{x\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{C}}f(X)
, back and forth between a primal
\mathcal{X}
and a dual space
\mathcal{X}^{*}
. The algorithm uses a convex function of the Legendre-type (convex and smooth), called a mirror map
h
, to translate the MD iterates
x
to the dual space where the gradient update is performed
\nabla h(y)\doteq\nabla h(x)-\eta\nabla f(x)
, with
\eta
a step size. A new iterate satisfying the primal constraints is obtained using a Bregman projection
x^{\prime}\doteq\operatorname{proj}^{h}_{\mathcal{C}}(y)=\operatorname{argmin}_{x\in\mathcal{C}}D_{h}(x,\nabla h^{*}(\nabla h(y)))
of the updated dual iterate
\nabla h(y)
mapped back in the primal space using the Fenchel conjugate function of
h
,
h^{*}(x^{*})=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\langle x,x^{*}\rangle-h(x)
. This projection relies on a Bregman divergence
D_{h}(x,y)\doteq h(x)-h(y)-\langle\nabla h(y),x-y\rangle

(
Amari, 1998
;
Bubeck, 2015
;
Banerjee et al., 2005
)
.
The proximal formulation of MD merges the update and projection steps to
x^{\prime}\doteq\operatorname{argmin}_{\bar{x}\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{C}}\eta\langle\nabla f(x),\bar{x}\rangle+D_{h}(\bar{x},x)
(Lemma
3
in Appendix
B.1
).

PMD
is an instance of MD
(
Beck & Teboulle, 2003
)
, applying GD in a non-Euclidean geometry, using the proximal perspective of MD,

\pi_{t+1}\doteq\operatorname{argmin}_{\pi\in\Pi}-\langle\nabla V^{\rho}_{t},\pi\rangle+\eta^{\!-\!1}_{\pi_{t+1}}D_{{h}}(\pi,\pi_{t})
for some sequence of step-sizes
\eta_{\pi_{t+1}}\!>\!0
and initial policy
\pi_{0}
.
The visitation-distribution
d^{\rho}_{t}
in the gradient of the surrogate objective can lead to vanishing gradients in infrequently visited states under
\pi_{t}

(
Mei et al., 2020a
;
Bhandari & Russo, 2021
;
Johnson et al., 2023
)
, so PMD iteratively applies a variant that separates the objective per state

\displaystyle\textstyle\pi_{t+1}(s)\doteq\operatorname{argmin}_{\pi(s)\in\Delta(\mathscr{A})}-\langle{Q}_{t}(s),\pi(s)\rangle+\eta_{\pi_{t+1}}^{\!-\!1}D_{{h}}(\pi(s),\pi_{t}(s))

Inexact PMD replaces
{Q}
with a value estimator
\widehat{Q}
.
Using the negative Boltzmann-Shannon entropy
(
Shannon, 1948
)
as mirror map yields the Natural Policy Gradient (NPG)
(
Kakade, 2001
)
. With a null Bregman divergence, it recovers PI.

Approximate PMD
The standard PMD algorithm is adapted by
Tomar et al. (2020)
to general policy parametrization by updating the parameters of
\pi_{\theta}
using a surrogate composite objective
\textstyle\theta_{t+1}=\operatorname{argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\rho}_{t}}[-\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\theta}(s)}[{Q}_{t}({s,a})]+\eta_{\pi_{t+1}}^{\!-\!1}D_{h}(\pi_{\theta}(s),\pi_{t}(s))]
.

Alfano et al. (2024)
introduces the concept of Bregman policy class
\{\pi_{\theta}:\pi_{\theta}(s)=\operatorname{proj}^{h}_{\Delta(\mathscr{A})}(\nabla h^{*}(f_{\theta}(s))),s\in\mathscr{S}\}
, and uses a parametrized function
f_{\theta}
to approximate the dual update of MD
f_{t+1}(s)\doteq\nabla h(\pi_{t}(s))-\eta_{\pi_{t+1}}\widehat{Q}_{t}(s)
. To satisfy the simplex constraint,
(
Xiong et al., 2024
)
uses a Bregman projection on the dual approximation mapped back to the policy space

\pi_{\theta}(s)=\operatorname{proj}^{h}_{\Delta(\mathscr{A})}(\nabla h^{*}(f_{t+1}(s)))
, equivalent to

\theta_{t+1}=\operatorname{argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}D_{h}(\pi_{\theta}(s),\nabla h^{*}(f_{t+1}(s)))
.
Using the negative Boltzmann-Shannon entropy, yields the softmax policy class

{\pi}_{\theta}({s,a})\doteq\nicefrac{{\exp f_{\theta}({s,a})}}{{\|\exp f\theta({s})\|_{1}}},\forall s,a\in\mathscr{S}\times\mathscr{A}
.
For the approximate setting, we rely on the proximal formulation by
Tomar et al. (2020)
. Additional details on other are provided in Appendix
E
.

Limitations

First
, both PMD(+loo) and PMD(+ext) update two policies per iteration, both from from
\pi_{t}
(
\mu_{t}
to look ahead and
\pi_{t+1}
as the next policy iterate).
Second
, they require two policy evaluation procedures per iteration, for
Q_{t}(s)
and
(\mathcal{T}_{\mu_{t}}Q_{t})(s)
. We may use a value estimator for the former, but the latter would then need access to a model. The main issue of PMD(+ext) is that it needs information from two consecutive updates (
\pi_{t}
and
\mu_{t}
) to compute the update to the policy iterate
\pi_{t+1}
. In the next section, we propose a “lazy” practical algorithm which remedies these issues, using only one policy variable and applying a single policy evaluation procedure per iteration, at the expense of extra memory.

Extrapolation from the past

The idea is to rewrite the update to use a single evaluation per timestep plus a temporal-difference error between consecutive evaluation updates. Then, assuming past errors reflect future ones, reusing the prior errors (extrapolating from the past) should have the same effect as extrapolating from the future.
We briefly touch on the main steps of algorithm derivation, deferring details to Appendix
B.2.1
.
First
, we show the PMD(+ext) update can be rewritten as

\textstyle\pi_{t+1}(s)=\operatorname{argmin}_{\pi(s)\in\Delta(\mathscr{A})}\!-\!\langle(\mathcal{T}_{\mu_{t}}{Q}_{t})(s)\!-\!\eta^{\!-\!1}_{\pi_{t+1}}{\eta}_{\mu_{t}}{Q}_{t}(s),\pi(s)\rangle+{\eta}^{\!-\!1}_{\mu_{t}}D_{h}(\pi(s),\mu_{t}(s))
.

Next
, to remedy the need for two evaluation procedures, we recycle evaluation updates from previous iterations by replacing
Q_{t}
with the closest past evaluation, i.e.
\mathcal{T}_{\mu_{t-1}}Q_{t-1}
, twice in the updates of
\pi_{t+1}(s)
and
\mu_{t}
(Eq.
3
).
We
then
show the update can be written in terms of a one policy variable and a single evaluation procedure, henceforth denoted
\pi
and
{Q}

\displaystyle\pi_{t+1}(s)

\displaystyle\!=\!\operatorname{argmin}_{\pi(s)\in\Delta(\mathscr{A})}\!-\!\langle{Q}_{t}(s)\!+\!\eta^{\!-\!1}_{\pi_{t+1}}\eta_{\pi_{t}}({Q}_{t}(s)\!-\!{Q}_{t-1}(s)),\pi(s)\rangle\!+\!\eta^{\!-\!1}_{\pi_{t+1}}D_{h}(\pi(s),\pi_{t}(s))

In Fig.

These studies indicate (i) that it is possible to accelerate PMD, that the advantage of functional acceleration is proportional to: (iii) the ill-conditioning of the optimization surface, induced by the policy and MDP dynamics, and (ii) the quality of the “inner-loop” policy approximation.

6
Closing

Inspired by functional acceleration from convex optimization theory, we proposed a momentum-based PMD update applicable to general policy parametrization and large-scale optimization. We analyzed several design choices in ablation studies designed to characterize qualitatively the properties of the update, and illustrated numerically how the characteristics of the problem influence the added benefit of using acceleration. Finally we looked at how inexact critics impact the method. Further analysis with these methods using stochastic simulation and function approximation would be useful.

B.1.1
Functional Policy Gradient

The Performance Difference Lemma (PDL) is a property that relates the difference in values of policies to the policies themselves.

Lemma 1
.

(Performance Difference Lemma from
Kakade & Langford (2002)
)

For any policies
\pi_{t+1}
and
\pi_{t}
, and an initial distribution
\rho

\displaystyle V^{\rho}_{t+1}-V^{\rho}_{t}

\displaystyle=\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-\gamma}}\sum_{s\in\mathscr{S}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}d^{\rho}_{{t+1}}({s})(\pi_{t+1}({s,a})-\pi_{t}({s,a}),Q_{t}({s,a}))

\displaystyle=\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-\gamma}}\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\rho}_{t+1}}\left[\langle Q_{t}(s),\pi_{t+1}(s)-{\pi}_{t}(s)\rangle\right]

Proof.

According to the definition of the value function

\displaystyle V_{t+1}(s)-V_{t}(s)

\displaystyle=\langle Q_{t+1}(s),\pi_{t+1}(s)\rangle-\langle Q_{t}(s),\pi_{t}(s)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle Q_{t}(s),\pi_{t+1}(s)-\pi_{t}(s)\rangle+\langle Q_{t+1}(s)-Q_{t}(s),\pi_{t+1}(s)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle Q_{t}(s),\pi_{t+1}(s)-\pi_{t}(s)\rangle+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}\sum_{a}P(s^{\prime}|s,a)\pi_{t+1}({s,a})[V_{t+1}({s^{\prime}})-V_{t}({s^{\prime}})]

\displaystyle=\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-\gamma}}\sum_{s^{\prime}}d_{t+1}({s\to s^{\prime}})\langle Q_{t}({s^{\prime}}),\pi_{t+1}({s^{\prime}})-\pi_{t}({s^{\prime}})\rangle

∎

The following lemma is a version of the policy gradient theorem
(
Sutton et al., 1999
)
applied to the direct policy representation—the functional representation of the policy probabilities, and has appeared in various forms in
Agarwal et al. (2019)
;
Bhandari & Russo (2019)
;
Bhandari & Russo (2021)
;
Russo (2022)
.

Lemma 2
.

(Policy Gradient Theorem for Directional Derivatives)

For two policies
\pi_{t+1},{\pi}_{t}\in\Pi

\displaystyle\langle\nabla V^{\rho}_{t},\pi_{t+1}-{\pi}_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}d_{t}(s)Q_{t}({s,a})(\pi_{t+1}({s,a})-{\pi}_{t}({s,a}))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d_{t}}\left[\langle Q_{t}(s),\pi_{t+1}(s)-{\pi}_{t}(s)\rangle\right]

Proof.

A Taylor expansion using the Performance Difference Lemma
1
reveals

\displaystyle V^{\rho}_{t+1}-V^{\rho}_{t}

\displaystyle=\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-\gamma}}\sum_{s\in\mathscr{S}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}d^{\rho}_{{t+1}}({s})(\pi_{t+1}({s,a})-\pi_{t}({s,a}),Q_{t}({s,a}))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\rho}_{t+1}}\left[\langle Q_{t}(s),\pi_{t+1}(s)-{\pi}_{t}(s)\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-\gamma}}\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\rho}_{t}}\left[\langle Q_{t}(s),\pi_{t+1}(s)-{\pi}_{t}(s)\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-\gamma}}\underbrace{\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}(d^{\rho}_{{t+1}}({s})-d^{\rho}_{t}({s}))\langle Q_{t}(s),\pi_{t+1}(s)-{\pi}_{t}(s)\rangle}_{=O(\|\pi_{t+1}(s)-{\pi}_{t}(s)\|^{2})}

The last error term is second-order since

P_{\pi}
is linear in
\pi
and then
d^{\rho}_{\pi}

is differentiable in
\pi
.
∎

The next lemma states that the MD method minimizes the local linearization of a function while not moving too far away from the previous point, with distances measured via the Bregman divergence of the mirror map.

Lemma 3
.

(Proximal perspective on mirror descent)
The MD update for
x\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{C}
, with mirror map
h:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
for the minimization problem
\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{C}}f(x)
, with
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
can be rewritten in the following ways, for step-size
\eta\geq 0
and
t\geq 0

\displaystyle x_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{argmin}_{x\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{C}}D_{h}(x,\nabla h^{*}(\nabla h(x_{t})+\eta\nabla f(x_{t})))

(PGD)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{argmin}_{x\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{C}}\eta\left\langle\nabla f(x_{t}),x\right\rangle+D_{h}(x,x_{t})

(proximal perspective)

Proof.

\displaystyle x_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{argmin}_{x\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{C}}D_{h}(x,\nabla h^{*}(\nabla h(x_{t})+\eta\nabla f(x_{t})))

(generalized GD)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{argmin}_{x\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{C}}h(x)-\left\langle\nabla h(\nabla h^{*}(\nabla h(x_{t})+\eta\nabla f(x_{t}))),x\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\operatorname{argmin}_{x\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{C}}h(x)-\left\langle\nabla h(x_{t})+\eta\nabla f(x_{t}),x\right\rangle

(proximal perspective)

∎

B.1.2
Helpful Lemmas for Policy Mirror Descent

Key to the analysis of
Xiao (2022)
and

is the Three-Point Descent Lemma, that relates the improvement of the proximal gradient update compared to an arbitrary point. It originally comes from
Chen & Teboulle (1993)
(Lemma 3.2).

Lemma 4
.

(Three-Point Descent Lemma, Lemma 6 in
Xiao (2022)
)
. Suppose that
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a closed convex set,
\psi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a proper, closed convex function,
D_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Bregman divergence generated by a function
{h}
of Legendre type and
\operatorname{rint}\operatorname{dom}{h}\cap\mathcal{X}\neq\emptyset
. For any
x_{t}\in\operatorname{rint}\operatorname{dom}{h}
, let

\displaystyle x_{t+1}=\operatorname{argmin}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\psi(x)+D_{h}(x,x_{t})

Then
x_{t+1}\in\operatorname{rint}\operatorname{dom}{h}\cap\mathcal{X}
and
\forall x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\displaystyle\psi(x_{t+1})+D_{h}(x_{t+1},x_{t})\leq\psi(x)+D_{h}(x,x_{t})-D_{h}(x,x_{t+1})

The PMD update is an instance of the proximal minimisation with
\mathcal{X}=\Delta(\mathcal{A}),x_{t}=\pi_{t}(s)
and
\psi(x)=-\eta_{\pi_{t+1}}\langle Q_{t}(s),x\rangle
. Plugging these in, the Three-Point Descent Lemma relates the decrease in the proximal objective of
\pi_{t+1}(s)
to any other policy, i.e.
\forall\pi(s)\in\Delta(\mathcal{A})
,

\displaystyle-\eta_{\pi_{t+1}}\langle Q_{t}(s),\pi_{t+1}(s)-\pi(s)\rangle\leq D_{h}(\pi(s),{\pi}_{t}(s))-D_{h}(\pi_{t+1}(s),\pi_{t}(s))-D_{h}(\pi(s),{\pi}_{t+1}(s))

This equation is key to the analysis of convergence of exact PMD, leading to Lemma
6
regarding the monotonic improvement in Q-functions of PMD iterates.

Lemma 5
.

(Descent Property of PMD for Q-functions,
Lemma 7 in
Xiao (2022)

Consider the policies produced by the iterative updates of exact PMD. For any
t\geq 0

\displaystyle\langle Q_{t}(s),\pi_{t+1}(s)-\pi_{t}(s)\rangle\geq 0,\quad\forall s\in\mathcal{S},

Proof.

From the Three-Point Descent Lemma
4
of
Xiao (2022)
with
\pi=\pi_{t}
,

\displaystyle\eta_{\pi_{t+1}}\langle Q_{t}(s),\pi_{t+1}(s)-\pi_{t}(s)\rangle\geq D_{h}(\pi_{t}(s),\pi_{t+1}(s))+D_{h}(\pi_{t+1}(s),\pi_{t}(s))

since the Bregman divergences are non-negative and
\eta_{\pi_{t+1}}>0
,

\displaystyle\langle Q_{t}(s),\pi_{t+1}(s)-\pi_{t}(s)\rangle\geq 0

∎

Lemma 6
.

(Descent Property of PMD for Value Functions, Lemma A.2. from
Johnson et al. (2023)
)

Consider the policies produced by the iterative updates of exact PMD. Then for any

,

\displaystyle Q_{t+1}(s)

\displaystyle\geq Q_{t}(s),\quad\forall s\in\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle V^{\rho}_{t+1}

\displaystyle\geq V^{\rho}_{t},\quad\forall\rho\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})

Proof.

Follows from Lemma

\displaystyle V^{\rho}_{t+1}-V^{\rho}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\rho}_{t+1}}\left[\langle Q_{t}(s),\pi_{t+1}(s)-\pi_{t}(s)\rangle\right]\geq 0

∎

Derivation of the Suboptimality Decomposition and Convergence of PMD

Suboptimality decomposition

Fix a state

. For any
{{\pi}(s)},{\tilde{\pi}(s)}
, let
D_{-V}({{\pi}(s)},{\tilde{\pi}(s)})
be analogous to a standard Bregman divergence with mirror map

, capturing the curvature of

at
{{\pi}(s)}

\displaystyle D_{-V}({{\pi}(s)},{\tilde{\pi}(s)})

\displaystyle\doteq-V_{\pi}(s)-(-V_{\tilde{\pi}}(s))-\langle-Q_{\tilde{\pi}}(s),{{\pi}(s)}-\tilde{\pi}(s)\rangle

\displaystyle\doteq-V_{\pi}(s)+V_{\tilde{\pi}}(s)+\langle Q_{\tilde{\pi}}(s)(s),{{\pi}(s)}-{\tilde{\pi}(s)}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\langle Q_{\pi}(s)-Q_{\tilde{\pi}}(s),{{\pi}(s)}\rangle

(4)

(using Holder’s inequality)

(5)

\displaystyle\geq-\|Q_{\pi}(s)-Q_{\tilde{\pi}}(s)\|_{\infty}\|{{\pi}(s)}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\geq-\gamma\|V_{\pi}-V_{\tilde{\pi}}\|_{\infty}

(6)

For the general case, using the approximation
\widehat{Q}_{t}(s)\approx Q_{t}(s)
, the per-iteration suboptimality is

\displaystyle V^{*}(s)-V_{t}(s)

\displaystyle=-\langle\widehat{Q}_{t}(s),{\pi}_{t+1}(s)-{\pi}^{*}(s)\rangle-\langle\widehat{Q}_{t}(s),{\pi}_{t}(s)-{\pi}_{t+1}(s)\rangle-D_{-V}({\pi}^{*}(s),{\pi}_{t}(s))

\displaystyle\qquad-\langle Q_{t}(s)-\widehat{Q}_{t}(s),{\pi}_{t}(s)-{\pi}^{*}(s)\rangle

(7)

The first term,

If

\displaystyle-\langle\widehat{Q}_{t}(s),{\pi}_{t+1}(s)-{\pi}^{*}(s)\rangle

\displaystyle\leq-\langle\widehat{Q}_{t}(s),{\pi}_{t+1}(s)-\bar{\pi}_{t+1}(s)\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\langle\widehat{Q}_{t}(s),\bar{\pi}_{t+1}-\pi_{t+1}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\eta^{\!-\!1}_{\pi_{t+1}}D_{h}(\bar{\pi}_{t+1}(s),\pi_{t}(s))-\eta^{\!-\!1}_{\pi_{t+1}}D_{h}(\bar{\pi}_{t+1}(s),\pi_{t+1}(s))

Appendix E
Approximate Policy Mirror Descent as Projected Gradient Descent (PGD)

In this section we provide an alternative perspective on PMD—cf. Lemma
3
, stating that
the MD update can be rewritten in the following ways

\displaystyle x_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{argmin}_{x\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{C}}D_{h}(x,\nabla h^{*}(\nabla h(x_{t})+\eta\nabla f(x_{t})))

(PGD)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{argmin}_{x\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{C}}\eta\left\langle\nabla f(x_{t}),x\right\rangle+D_{h}(x,x_{t})

(proximal perspective)

Alfano et al. (2024)
introduces the concept of Bregman policy class
\{\pi_{\theta}:\pi_{\theta}(s)=\operatorname{proj}^{h}_{\Delta(\mathscr{A})}(\nabla h^{*}(f_{\theta}(s))),s\in\mathscr{S}\}
, and uses a parametrized function
f_{\theta}
to approximate the dual update of MD
f_{t+1}(s)\doteq\nabla h(\pi_{t}(s))-\eta_{\pi_{t+1}}\widehat{Q}_{t}(s)
. To satisfy the simplex constraint,
Alfano et al. (2024)
uses the L2 norm to measure function approximation errors, whereas
Xiong et al. (2024)
extends this to a Bregman projection on the dual approximation mapped back to the policy space

\pi_{\theta}(s)=\operatorname{proj}^{h}_{\Delta(\mathscr{A})}(\nabla h^{*}(f_{t+1}(s)))
, equivalent to

\theta_{t+1}=\operatorname{argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}D_{h}(\pi_{\theta}(s),\nabla h^{*}(f_{t+1}(s)))
(cf.
Amari (2016)
, the divergences derived from two convex functions are substantially the same, except for the order of the arguments
D_{h}(x,\nabla h^{*}(\nabla h(y)))=D_{h^{*}}(\nabla h(y),\nabla h^{*}(x))
).

Using the negative Boltzmann-Shannon entropy, yields the softmax policy class

{\pi}_{\theta}({s,a})\doteq\nicefrac{{\exp{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}_{\theta}({s,a})}}{{\|\exp{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}_{\theta}({s})\|_{1}}},\forall s,a\in\mathscr{S}\times\mathscr{A}
.

For the approximate setting, in the main text, we rely on the formulation introduced by
Tomar et al. (2020)
. Here, we provide additional details on the formulation by
Alfano et al. (2024)
and
Xiong et al. (2024)
.

Appendix F
Newton’s method

The Newton-Kantorovich theorem generalizes Newton’s method for solving nonlinear equations to infinite-dimensional Banach spaces.
It provides conditions under which Newton’s method converges and gives an estimate of the convergence rate. Newton-Kantorovich theorem deals with the convergence of Newton’s method for a nonlinear operator
F:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
, where
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are Banach spaces. The method iteratively solves
F(x)=0
using

\displaystyle x_{t+1}=x_{t}-(\nabla F)^{-1}F(x_{t})

where
\nabla F
is a generalization of the Jacobian of
F
, provided
F
is differentiable.
Intuitively, at each iteration, the method performs a linearization of
F(x)=0
close to
x
, using a first order Taylor expansion:

F(x+\Delta x)\approx F(x)+\nabla F(x)\Delta x
, where
F(x)+\nabla F(x)\Delta x=0\iff\Delta x=-(\nabla F)^{-1}F(x)
. For
x
close to
x^{*}
,
x-(\nabla F)^{-1}F(x)
is a good approximation of
x^{*}
. The iterative sequence
\{x_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
converges to
x^{*}
, assuming the Jacobian matrix exists, is invertible, and Lipschitz continuous.

Quasi-Newton methods

Any method that replaces the exact computation of the Jacobian matrices in the Newton’s method (or their inverses) with an approximation, is a quasi-Newton method. A quasi-Newton method constructs a sequence of iterates
\{x_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}

and a sequence
of matrices
\{J_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}

such that
J_{t}
is an approximation of the Jacobian
\nabla F(x_{t})

for any
t\geq 0
and

\displaystyle x_{t+1}=x_{t}-(J_{t})^{-1}F(x_{t})

In Anderson’s acceleration
(
Anderson, 1965
)
, information about the last iterates is used to update the approximation of
J_{t}
.

Policy iteration as Newton’s method

In the context of Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), policy iteration may be interpreted as Newton’s method with the following notations and analogies. First, using the Bellman optimality operator
\mathcal{T}V(s)\doteq\max_{a}[r({s,a})+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathscr{S}}P({s^{\prime}|s,a})V({s^{\prime}})]
, the aim is to find
V
such that
V=\mathcal{T}V
, which is akin to finding the roots
V
, such that
F(V)=V-\mathcal{T}V=(I-\mathcal{T})(V)=0
. We interpret
F=\nabla f
as the gradient of an unknown function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, despite the Bellman operator being non-differentiable in general due to the max. Where the greedy policy
{\pi}_{t}
attains the max in
\mathcal{T}V
, we obtain
J_{t}=\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P}_{{\pi}_{t}}
, which is invertible for
\gamma\in[0,1)
.
Expanding the Bellman operator, we have

\displaystyle V_{\pi_{t+1}}=r_{\pi_{t+1}}+\gamma P_{\pi_{t+1}}V_{\pi_{t+1}}\implies V_{\pi_{t+1}}

\displaystyle=(J_{t})^{-1}r_{\pi_{t+1}}

\displaystyle J_{t}V_{\pi_{t+1}}

\displaystyle=r_{\pi_{t+1}}

The values corresponding to two successive PI steps can be related in the following way by manipulating the equations
(
Puterman & Brumelle, 1979
;
Grand-Clément, 2021
)

\displaystyle V_{\pi_{t+1}}

\displaystyle=(J_{t})^{-1}r_{\pi_{t+1}}

\displaystyle=V_{\pi_{t}}-V_{\pi_{t}}+(J_{t})^{-1}r_{\pi_{t+1}}

\displaystyle=V_{\pi_{t}}-(J_{t})^{-1}J^{t}V_{\pi_{t}}+(J_{t})^{-1}r_{\pi_{t+1}}

\displaystyle=V_{\pi_{t}}-(J_{t})^{-1}(-r_{\pi_{t+1}}+J^{t}V_{\pi_{t}})

\displaystyle=V_{\pi_{t}}-(J_{t})^{-1}(-r_{\pi_{t+1}}+(I-\gamma P_{\pi_{t+1}})V_{\pi_{t}})

\displaystyle=V_{\pi_{t}}-(J_{t})^{-1}(V_{\pi_{t}}-r_{\pi_{t+1}}-\gamma P_{\pi_{t+1}}V_{\pi_{t}})

\displaystyle=V_{\pi_{t}}-(J_{t})^{-1}(V_{\pi_{t}}-\mathcal{T}V_{\pi_{t}})

In the main text we used the notation
\Psi_{t}\doteq(I-\gamma P_{\pi_{t}})^{-1}=(J_{t})^{-1}
, and applied the definition
\nabla f(V_{\pi_{t}})\doteq F(V_{\pi_{t}})=(I-\mathcal{T})(V_{\pi_{t}})=V_{\pi_{t}}-\mathcal{T}V_{\pi_{t}}
which yielded the expression

\displaystyle V_{\pi_{t+1}}=V_{\pi_{t}}-\Psi\nabla f(V_{\pi_{t}})

G.2
Details of Random Markov Decision Processes

We consider randomly constructed finite MDPs—
Random MDP
problems
(a.k.a. Garnet MDPs: Generalized Average Reward Non-stationary Environment Test-bench)

(
Archibald et al., 1995
;
Bhatnagar et al., 2009
)
, abstract, yet representative of the kind of MDP encountered in practice, which serve as a test-bench for RL algorithms
(
Goyal & Grand-Clement, 2021
;
Scherrer & Geist, 2014
;
Vieillard et al., 2019
)
. A
Random MDP
generator
\mathcal{M}\doteq(|\mathscr{S}|,|\mathscr{A}|,b,\gamma)
is parameterized by
4
parameters: number of states
|\mathscr{S}|
, number of actions
|\mathscr{A}|
, branching factor
b
specifying how many possible next states are possible for each state-action pair.

The transition probabilities
P(s_{0}|s,a)
are then computed as follows. First,
b
states (
s_{1},\dots s_{b}
) are chosen uniformly at random and transition probabilities are set by sampling uniform random
b\!-\!1
numbers (cut points) between
0
and
1
and sorted as
(p_{0}=0,p_{1},\dots p_{b-1},p_{b}=1)
. Then, the transition probabilities are assigned as
P(s_{i}|s,a)=p_{i}-p_{i-1}
for each
1\leq i\leq b
. The reward is state-dependent, and for each MDP, the per-state reward
r(s)
is uniformly sampled between
0
and
R_{\operatorname{max}}
, such that
r\sim(0,R_{\operatorname{max}})^{|\mathscr{S}|}
. The illustrations shown use
|\mathscr{S}|=100
and
R_{\operatorname{max}}=100
. Other choices yield similar results.
