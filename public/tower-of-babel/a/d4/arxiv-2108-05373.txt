Higher-Order Topological Superconductivity in Twisted Bilayer Graphene
Aaron Chew, Yijie Wang, B. Andrei Bernevig, Zhi-Da Song

Abstract
We show that introducing spin-singlet or spin-triplet superconductivity into twisted bilayer graphene induces higher-order topological superconductivity. C 2 ​ z ​ T C_{2z}T -protected corner states of Majorana Kramers pairs appear at the boundary between domains with opposite signs of pairing, and zero modes materialize in Abrikosov vortices. The topology of the superconducting phase originates from the anomaly Song et al. 2020 –—the absence of a lattice support—of the single-valley band structure of twisted bilayer graphene, which is protected by C 2 ​ z ​ T C_{2z}T and the particle-hole symmetry 𝒫 {\cal P} . We prove that any pairing (spin-singlet or spin-triplet) term preserving valley-U(1), spin-SU(2), time-reversal, C 2 ​ z ​ T C_{2z}T , and 𝒫 {\cal P} must drive the system into a higher-order topological superconductor phase. Here spin-SU(2) is the global spin-SU(2) for the singlet pairing and a combination of two SU(2)’s in the two valleys for the triplet pairing. Using a Dirac Hamiltonian, we demonstrate the existence of corner modes and confirm this with numerical calculations. These corner states are stable even if the approximate particle-hole symmetry 𝒫 \mathcal{P} is weakly broken, which is true in experimental setups. Finally, we suggest an experiment to detect the topological superconductivity: by observing the fractional Josephson effect in a TBG-TSC Josephson system.

Valley Symmetry Breaking.
Our realization of topological edges and Majorana corner modes requires that valley-U(1) symmetry be preserved, so the valleys do not couple.
In fact, we prove in Appendix

This problem can be circumvented by instead considering a domain wall between superconductors of phase difference
\pi

While the domain wall still introduces scattering that may link the valleys, the breaking of valley-U(1) is no longer enforced on general grounds.
We may consider, then, a slowly varying domain wall where the jump in superconducting phase is slow on the scale of the graphene unit cell, but rapid on the scale the Moiré unit cell.

Conclusion and Discussion.
We have shown that proximitizing twisted-bilayer graphene with the
A_{1}
spin-singlet (or
B_{1}
spin-triplet) superconductivity
must
yield a higher-order topological superconductor.
Multiple zero modes are bound to corners of the system.
We have explicitly demonstrated the topological phase and proved its existence with the Wilson loop formalism (
section

A.5
).
We have also examined the fate of the gapless edge states and zero modes under symmetric interactions, and concluded with possible experimental signatures of the zero modes (
appendix

I
).

Our work harnesses the unusual bulk topological properties of TBG in a special superconducting heterostructure.
By combining a relatively simple phase (a spin-singlet superconductor) with TBG, we engineer a higher-order topological superconductor with corner modes bound to domain walls.
Our work begs the question if other heterostructures can exploit the anomalous structure of TBG to yield even more exotic topological phases; for example, by using ferromagnets or quantum Hall systems.

We also conjecture that introducing spin-singlet or spin-triplet superconductivity into the recently realized mirror symmetric magic-angle twisted trilayer graphene (MATTG)
Park et al. 2020
also leads to topological superconductor because in MATTG a single valley has an odd number of Dirac points protected by the
C_{2z}T
symmetry, which is also anomalous and usually only appears as the surface state of the axion insulator.

c_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{Q},-\eta,\alpha,\downarrow}^{\dagger},\qquad&\text{if}\ s=\downarrow\end{cases}\

(33)

and write the Hamiltonian with pairing as

\hat{H}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}\\
\eta\alpha\beta ss^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{H}^{(\eta)}_{\mathbf{Q}\alpha s\,;\,\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}\beta s^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})\psi^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},\eta,\alpha,s}\psi_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\eta,\beta,s^{\prime}}\ .

(34)

We refer to
\mathcal{H}^{(\eta)}
as the BdG Hamiltonian.
Here the spin index also indicates the particle (
s=\uparrow
) and hole (
s=\downarrow
) degree of freedom.
We have assumed that the pairing happens only between different valleys such that the valley-U(1) is preserved and the BdG Hamiltonian matrix is diagonal in the valley index.
The full symmetry SU(2)
\times
SU(2), with spin conservation in each valley, would force pairing between fermions of the same valley, yielding a pair-density wave at finite momentum.
We do not wish to use this form of pairing, and so work with a reduced symmetry group.
In the singlet pairing case, only total spin-SU(2) is conserved.
Now we derive the explicit form of the free part of the BdG Hamiltonian,
i.e.
, the BdG Hamiltonian without pairing:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{k}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}\\
\eta\alpha\beta\end{subarray}}\Big(H^{(\eta)}_{\mathbf{Q}\alpha,\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}\beta}(\mathbf{k})\psi_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},\eta,\alpha,\uparrow}^{\dagger}\psi_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\eta,\beta,\uparrow}

\displaystyle+H^{(-\eta)}_{-\mathbf{Q}\alpha,-\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}\beta}(-\mathbf{k})\psi_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},\eta,\alpha,\downarrow}\psi_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\eta,\beta,\downarrow}^{\dagger}\Big).

(35)

By commuting
\psi_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},\eta,\alpha,\downarrow}
and
\psi_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\eta,\beta,\downarrow}^{\dagger}
in the second term, we have

\hat{H}_{0}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{k}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}\\
\eta\alpha\beta\end{subarray}}\mathcal{H}^{(\eta,0)}_{\mathbf{Q}\alpha s,\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}\beta s^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})\psi_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},\eta,\alpha,s}^{\dagger}\psi_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\eta,\beta,s^{\prime}}+const,

(36)

where

\small\mathcal{H}^{(\eta,0)}_{\mathbf{Q}\alpha s,\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}\beta s^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})=\delta_{ss^{\prime}}\big(\delta_{s\uparrow}H^{(\eta)}_{\mathbf{Q}\alpha,\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}\beta}(\mathbf{k})-\delta_{s\downarrow}H^{(-\eta)T}_{-\mathbf{Q}\alpha,-\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}\beta}(-\mathbf{k})\big).

(37)

Due to the spinless time-reversal symmetry
eq.

26
, the free Hamiltonian without pairing satisfies
H^{(\eta)}_{\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{k})=H^{(-\eta)*}_{-\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},-\mathbf{Q}}(-\mathbf{k})
, hence we can write the free part of the BdG Hamiltonian as

\mathcal{H}^{(\eta,0)}(\mathbf{k})=H^{(\eta)}(\mathbf{k})\cdot\xi_{z}

(38)

where
\xi_{z}
is the third Pauli matrix in the particle-hole space.

Then we consider generic BdG Hamiltonian with nonzero pairing term.
Besides the valley-U(1) symmetry, we further require
\hat{H}
to be invariant under spin-SU(2) and all the discrete symmetries defined in the above subsection.
According to
eqs.

31
and
26
, the time-reversal symmetry acts on the BdG basis as

\displaystyle T\psi_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},\eta,\alpha,s}T^{-1}=\begin{cases}c_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{Q},-\eta,\alpha,\uparrow},\qquad&\text{if}\ s=\uparrow\\
c_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},\eta,\alpha,\downarrow}^{\dagger},\qquad&\text{if}\ s=\downarrow\end{cases}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{Q},-\eta,\alpha,s}

(39)

Thus
\mathcal{H}^{(+)}
and
\mathcal{H}^{(-)}
, the BdG Hamiltonians with pairing, also are related by the spinless time-reversal symmetry as

\mathcal{H}^{(\eta)}_{\mathbf{Q}\alpha s\,;\,\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}\beta s^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})=\mathcal{H}^{(-\eta)*}_{-\mathbf{Q},\alpha,s\,;\,-\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\beta s^{\prime}}(-\mathbf{k})\ .

(40)

We find that the spin-SU(2) rotation
i\hat{s}_{y}
on
\psi
also interchanges the two valleys in
\psi
as

\displaystyle(i\hat{s}_{y})\psi_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},\eta,\alpha,s}(i\hat{s}_{y})^{-1}=\begin{cases}c_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},\eta,\alpha,\downarrow},\qquad&\text{if}\ s=\uparrow\\
-c_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{Q},-\eta,\alpha,\uparrow}^{\dagger},\qquad&\text{if}\ s=\downarrow\end{cases}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s^{\prime}}[i\xi_{y}]_{s^{\prime}s}\psi_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{Q},-\eta,\alpha,s^{\prime}}^{\dagger},

(41)

where
\xi_{y}
is the second Pauli matrix in the particle-hole space.
Implementing this symmetry to the BdG Hamiltonian, we have

[\xi_{y}\mathcal{H}^{(\eta)*}(\mathbf{k})\xi_{y}]_{\mathbf{Q},\alpha,s;\,\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\beta,s^{\prime}}=-\mathcal{H}^{(-\eta)}_{-\mathbf{Q},\alpha,s;\,-\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\beta,s^{\prime}}(-\mathbf{k})\ .

(42)

Combining
eqs.

40
and
42
we have the constraint imposed by
i\hat{s}_{y}T
(spinful time-reveral symmetry)

\{\xi_{y},\mathcal{H}^{(\eta)}\}=0.

(43)

Therefore, the
i\hat{s}_{y}T
symmetry, a spinful time-reversal symmetry on the original
c
fermions, is equivalent to a chiral symmetry
i\xi_{y}
in the single-particle first quantized BdG formalism in Eq.
33
. We gauge away the
i
in
i\xi_{y}
to define the chiral symmetry
S=\xi_{y}
, as chiral symmetry usually is chosen to square to
+1
. While this does not affect the physics of the system, choosing
S=\xi_{y}
instead of
S=i\xi_{y}
alters commutation relations with anti-unitary symmetries: for example,
[S,\mathcal{P}]=[\xi_{y},i\xi_{z}\mu_{y}\sigma_{x}K]=0
, while
\{i\xi_{y},i\xi_{z}\mu_{y}\sigma_{x}K\}=0
.

We turn to charge-conjugation symmetry
\mathcal{P}_{c}
.
Its action on the BdG basis is

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{c}\psi_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},\eta,\alpha,s}\mathcal{P}_{c}^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\sum_{\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}\eta^{\prime}\beta}D_{\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}\eta^{\prime}\beta,\mathbf{Q}\eta\alpha}^{*}(\mathcal{P})c^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\eta,\beta,\uparrow},\qquad&\text{if}\ s=\uparrow\\
\sum_{\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}\eta^{\prime}\beta}D_{-\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}\eta^{\prime}\beta,-\mathbf{Q}\eta\alpha}(\mathcal{P})c_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\eta,\beta,\downarrow},\qquad&\text{if}\ s=\downarrow\end{cases}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}\eta\beta s^{\prime}}\mathcal{D}^{*}_{\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}\eta^{\prime}\beta s^{\prime}\,;\,\mathbf{Q}\eta\alpha s}(\mathcal{P})\psi_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\eta^{\prime},s^{\prime}}^{\dagger}

(44)

where

\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\mathbf{Q}}(\mathcal{P})=\delta_{\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},-\mathbf{Q}}\zeta_{\mathbf{Q}}\cdot\tau_{0}\cdot\sigma_{x}\cdot\xi_{z}

(45)

according to
eq.

29
.
The term
\delta_{\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},-\mathbf{Q}}\zeta_{\mathbf{Q}}
switches layer and is represented by the Pauli matrix
i\mu_{y}
.
The
\xi_{z}
matrix in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\mathbf{Q}}(\mathcal{P})
comes from the fact that the actions of
\mathcal{P}
in the particle space and the hole space are opposite:
D_{\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\mathbf{Q}}(\mathcal{P})=-D_{-\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},-\mathbf{Q}}(\mathcal{P})
.
The relative negative sign that emerges can easily be seen from the action of
P
:

c_{\mathbf{k},m}\rightarrow c^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k},m^{\prime}}[i\mu_{y}\sigma_{x}]_{mm^{\prime}}
, with
m
short for the indices
\eta,\alpha,s
.
Applying the dagger reverses the sign because of the
i
:
P:c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},m}\rightarrow c_{-\mathbf{k},m^{\prime}}[-i\mu_{y}\sigma_{x}]_{mm^{\prime}}
.
Similar to the analysis of the
i\hat{s}_{y}T
symmetry, applying
\mathcal{P}_{c}
to the BdG Hamiltonian, we obtain

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P})\mathcal{H}^{{(\eta)}*}(\mathbf{k})\mathcal{D}^{T}(\mathcal{P})=-\mathcal{H}^{{(\eta)}}(-\mathbf{k}).

(46)

Thus the single-particle operator
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P})K
, where
K
is the complex conjugation, is a particle-hole symmetry for the BdG Hamiltonian.
Since
\mathcal{P}
commutes with the chiral symmetry
S
and leaves each valley invariant, we can define an emergent “time-reversal symmetry”
\tilde{T}=S\mathcal{P}
that leaves each valley unchanged.

\tilde{T}
squares to
-1
because
\tilde{T}^{2}=S\mathcal{P}S\mathcal{P}=S^{2}\mathcal{P}^{2}=-1
.
Therefore, we identify the effective Altland-Zirnbauer symmetry class as CII.

The actions of the crystalline symmetries preserving the valley index —
C_{3z}
,
C_{2x}
,
C_{2z}T
— on the BdG basis can be similarly derived.
Since the particle part of the BdG basis are annihilation operators in valley
\eta
and the hole part are creation operators in the valley
-\eta
, we have
\mathcal{D}^{(\eta)}(g)=D^{(\eta)}(g)\oplus D^{(-\eta)*}(g)
for these three symmetries.
On the other hand, due to the time-reversal symmetry, there must be
D^{(-\eta)*}(g)=D^{(\eta)}(g)
(one can check this from Eqs. (
23
) to (
26
)) and hence we have
\mathcal{D}^{(\eta)}(g)=D^{(\eta)}(g)\cdot\xi_{0}
.

To summarize, the two BdG Hamiltonian matrices in the two valleys are related by time-reversal
T
, and each valley has the symmetries
S
,
\mathcal{P}
,
C_{3z}
,
C_{2x}
,
C_{2z}T
.
Since
[S,\mathcal{P}]=0
and
\mathcal{P}^{2}=-1
, the effective symmetry class of each valley is CII.
The commutation and anti-commutation relations involving
S
and
\mathcal{P}
are

\small[S,g]=0,\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for}\ g=\mathcal{P},C_{3z},C_{2x},\hskip 9.24994pt\{S,C_{2z}T\}=0,

(47)

\{\mathcal{P},C_{2x}\}=0,\quad[\mathcal{P},C_{3z}]=0,\quad[\mathcal{P},C_{2z}T]=0.

(48)

A.4
Spin-singlet pairing

In this subsection we show that a uniform spin-singlet pairing satisfies all the BdG symmetries defined in
section

A.3
.
According to the Dirac theory in the main text, as a consequence of the anomaly of TBG, such a simple spin-singlet pairing will result in the higher order topological superconductor phase.
We consider spin-singlet pairing at at every carbon atom in TBG:

\Delta_{0}\sum_{\alpha,l}\sum_{\mathbf{R}\in l}c_{\mathbf{R},l,\alpha,\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf{R},l,\alpha,\downarrow}^{\dagger}+h.c.

(49)

Due to the time-reversal symmetry,
\Delta_{0}
must be a real number.
Applying Eq. (
21
), we can write the pairing in terms of
c_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},\eta,\alpha,s}
as

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}\frac{1}{N_{M}N_{0}}\sum_{\alpha,l}\sum_{\mathbf{R}\in l}\sum_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in{\rm MBZ}}\sum_{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\eta l}}\sum_{\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\eta^{\prime}l}}e^{-i(\eta\mathbf{K}_{l}+\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q}+\eta^{\prime}\mathbf{K}_{l}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{Q}^{\prime})\cdot(\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{t}_{\alpha})}c_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},\eta,\alpha,\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\eta^{\prime},\alpha,\downarrow}^{\dagger}+h.c.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}\sum_{\alpha,l}\sum_{\eta}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in{\rm MBZ}}\sum_{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\eta l}}c_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},\eta,\alpha,\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{Q},-\eta,\alpha,\downarrow}^{\dagger}+h.c.=\Delta_{0}\sum_{\eta,\alpha}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in{\rm MBZ}}\sum_{\mathbf{Q}}c_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},\eta,\alpha,\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{Q},-\eta,\alpha,\downarrow}^{\dagger}+h.c.

(50)

At the first equal sign, the summation over
\mathbf{R}
leads to the momentum conservation
\eta=-\eta^{\prime}
,
\mathbf{k}=-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}
,
\mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}
.
First
\eta=-\eta^{\prime}
, as no combination of the Moiré vectors will span the graphene vector
\mathbf{K}_{l}
.
The summation over
\mathbf{R}
then forces
\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}=0
.
Because
\eta=-\eta^{\prime}
,
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}
belong to opposite sublattices
{\cal Q}_{+},{\cal Q}_{-}
, meaning
\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}=n\mathbf{b}_{M1}+m\mathbf{b}_{M2},~n,m\in Z
, which is commensurate with the Moiré BZ lattice vectors.
Thus
\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime}=0
and
\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}=0
.
As this pairing operator adds a spin
\uparrow
in one valley and spin
\downarrow
in the other, it breaks spin and charge conservation in each valley. However,
total
charge and spin is still conserved.
In the BdG basis
eq.

33
, this pairing term can be written as

\hat{H}_{1}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}}\sum_{\eta\alpha\beta ss^{\prime}}\mathcal{H}^{(\eta,1)}_{\mathbf{Q}\alpha s,\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}\beta s^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})\psi^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},\alpha,s}\psi_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\beta,s^{\prime}}

(51)

where

\mathcal{H}^{(\eta,1)}_{\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})=\Delta_{0}\delta_{\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}}\cdot\sigma_{0}\cdot\xi_{x}.

(52)

One can verify that this pairing term satisfies the
T
,
S
,
\mathcal{P}
,
C_{3z}
,
C_{2x}
, and
C_{2z}T
symmetries.
The Bogoliubov bands with
\Delta_{0}=20
meV at
\theta=1.05^{\circ},1.2^{\circ},1.4^{\circ}
are shown in
fig.

4
(c-e), respectively.

In realistic systems, the Cooper pairing might not be exactly on-site.
We consider the pairing in form of

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\beta,l}\sum_{\mathbf{R}\in l}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{R}^{\prime}\in l\\
|\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{t}_{\alpha}-\mathbf{R}^{\prime}-\mathbf{t}_{\beta}|\leq a_{0}\end{subarray}}\Delta(|\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{t}_{\alpha}-\mathbf{R}^{\prime}-\mathbf{t}_{\beta}|)

\displaystyle\times c_{\mathbf{R},l,\alpha,\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf{R}^{\prime},l,\beta,\downarrow}^{\dagger}+h.c.,

(53)

where
a_{0}
is the lattice constant of single-layer graphene.
In general
\Delta(|\mathbf{d}|)
decays with
|\mathbf{d}|
, hence here we only keep the pairing to next nearest neighbor.

We denote the on-site pairing, the nearest neighbor pairing, and the next nearest neighbor pairing as
\bar{\Delta}_{0}=\Delta(0)
,
\bar{\Delta}_{1}=\Delta(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}a_{0})
, and
\bar{\Delta}_{2}=\Delta(a_{0})
, respectively.
For a simple nearly uniform spin-singlet pairing,
\bar{\Delta}_{0,1,2}
have the same sign.
Following the same calculation as in
section

A.4
, we obtain the pairing term in momentum space as

\displaystyle\sum_{\eta,\alpha}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in{\rm MBZ}}\sum_{\mathbf{Q}}\Big((\Delta_{0}+\widetilde{\Delta}^{(\eta)}_{2}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q}))c_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},\eta,\alpha,\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{Q},-\eta,\alpha,\downarrow}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle+\widetilde{\Delta}_{1}^{(\eta,\alpha)}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q})c_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},\eta,\alpha,\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{Q},-\eta,-\alpha,\downarrow}^{\dagger}\Big)+h.c.,

(54)

where
-\alpha=B,A
for
\alpha=A,B
and

\widetilde{\Delta}^{(\eta)}_{2}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q})=\bar{\Delta}_{2}\sum_{\mathbf{d}}^{\prime\prime}e^{i(\eta\mathbf{K}+\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q})\cdot\mathbf{d}}

(55)

\widetilde{\Delta}^{(\eta,\alpha)}_{1}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q})=\bar{\Delta}_{1}\sum_{\mathbf{d}_{\alpha}}^{\prime}e^{i(\eta\mathbf{K}+\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q})\cdot\mathbf{d}_{\alpha}}

(56)

with
\sum_{\mathbf{d}_{\alpha}}^{\prime}
and
\sum_{\mathbf{d}}^{\prime\prime}
summing over the three nearest neighbor vectors and the six next nearest neighbor vectors around
\mathbf{t}_{\alpha}
, respectively.
Since
|\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q}|\ll 1/a_{0}
in the low energy theory, the
\mathbf{k}
dependence of pairing can be neglected in the analysis of the topology of the superconductor phase.
However, in
appendix

F
, we find a weak
\mathbf{k}
-dependence is important for the appearance of Shiba states.
Thus for later reference, we expand
\widetilde{\Delta}^{(\eta)}_{2}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q})
and
\widetilde{\Delta}^{(\eta,\alpha)}_{1}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q})
to second order
a_{0}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q})
:

\widetilde{\Delta}^{(\eta)}_{2}(\mathbf{p})\approx-3\bar{\Delta}_{2}+\frac{3}{4}\bar{\Delta}_{2}a_{0}^{2}\mathbf{p}^{2},

(57)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Delta}^{(\eta,A)}_{1}(\mathbf{p})\approx\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\bar{\Delta}_{1}a_{0}(-\eta p_{x}+ip_{y})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{8}\bar{\Delta}_{1}a_{0}^{2}(p_{x}^{2}-p_{y}^{2}+2\eta ip_{x}p_{y})

(58)

\widetilde{\Delta}^{(\eta,B)}_{1}(\mathbf{p})=\widetilde{\Delta}^{(\eta,A)*}_{1}(\mathbf{p}),

(59)

where
\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q}
.
Defining
\Delta_{0}=\bar{\Delta}_{0}-3\bar{\Delta}_{2}
,
m=-\frac{3}{4}\bar{\Delta}_{2}a_{0}^{2}
,
v^{\prime}=-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\bar{\Delta}_{1}a_{0}
,
m^{\prime}=\frac{1}{8}\bar{\Delta}_{1}a_{0}^{2}
, we can write the BdG Hamiltonian as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{(\eta,1)}_{\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})=\delta_{\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}}\xi_{x}\Big((\Delta_{0}-m(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q})^{2})\sigma_{0}

\displaystyle+v^{\prime}\eta(k_{x}-Q_{x})\sigma_{x}+v^{\prime}(k_{y}-Q_{y})\sigma_{y}

\displaystyle+m^{\prime}((k_{x}-Q_{x})^{2}-(k_{y}-Q_{y})^{2})\sigma_{x}+

\displaystyle-2m^{\prime}\eta(k_{x}-Q_{x})(k_{y}-Q_{y})\sigma_{y}\Big).

(60)

We will assume
m=v^{\prime}=m^{\prime}=0
in the rest of this work unless specified otherwise.
As mentioned above, since
|\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q}|\ll 1/a_{0}
, the quadratic term
(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q})^{2}
in the mass term
\Delta_{0}-m(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q})^{2}
is always much smaller than
\Delta_{0}
within the cutoff of the single valley model.
Thus this quadratic term, as well as other
\mathbf{k}
-dependent terms in the pairing, can be safely neglected in the analysis of the band topology.

A.5
Wilson loop of the topological superconductor

The topological nature of TBG-TSC can be easily verified via the Wilson loop construction.
As discussed in Ref.
Song et al. 2020
, the two symmetries
C_{2z}T
and
{\cal P}
protect a stable topology in the Wilson loop spectrum.
When considering the Wannier Hamiltonian

\displaystyle W(k_{x})

\displaystyle=e^{iH_{W}(k_{x})}

(61)

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\prod_{j=0}^{N-1}U^{\dagger}(k_{x},\frac{2\pi j}{N})U(k_{x},\frac{2\pi(j+1)}{N}),

(62)

with
U
defined to be the matrix of coefficients of the
M
occupied BdG bands:

\displaystyle U(\mathbf{k})=(\ket{u_{1}(\mathbf{k})},\ket{u_{2}(\mathbf{k})},\cdots\ket{u_{M}(\mathbf{k})}),

(63)

defining
\ket{u_{m}(k_{x},k_{y})}
as the eigenket for the
m
th band.
The product of unitaries becomes

\displaystyle[U^{\dagger}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime})U(\mathbf{k})]_{mn}=\braket{u_{m}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime})|u_{n}(\mathbf{k})}.

(64)

One finds that
S\cal P
guarantees degeneracies in the Wilson loop spectrum at
k_{x}=0,\pi
, while
C_{2z}T
becomes a particle-hole symmetry and forces the bands to come in
E,-E
pairs.
To see this, we examine the action of
S\mathcal{P}
on the Wilson loop. As in Ref.
Song et al. 2020
, under
S\mathcal{P}
, defining the sewing matrix
B
to act as

\displaystyle S\mathcal{P}\ket{u_{n}(\mathbf{k})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\ket{u_{m}(-\mathbf{k})}B^{S\mathcal{P}}_{mn}(\mathbf{k}),

(65)

\displaystyle(S\mathcal{P})^{2}

\displaystyle=-1\implies B^{S\mathcal{P}*}(\mathbf{k})B^{S\mathcal{P}}(-\mathbf{k})=-1.

(66)

we find

\displaystyle\braket{u_{m}(\mathbf{k})|u_{n}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime})}=\braket{S\mathcal{P}u_{n}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime})|S\mathcal{P}u_{m}(\mathbf{k})}

(67)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}B^{S\mathcal{P}*}_{n^{\prime}n}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\braket{u_{n^{\prime}}(-\mathbf{k}^{\prime})|u_{m^{\prime}}(-\mathbf{k})}B^{S\mathcal{P}}_{m^{\prime}m}(\mathbf{k})

(68)

This implies that

\displaystyle W(k_{x})

\displaystyle=B^{S\mathcal{P}\dagger}(k_{x},0)W^{T}(-k_{x})B^{S\mathcal{P}}(k_{x},0).

(69)

Defining a time reversal symmetry
T^{\prime}(k_{x})=B^{S\mathcal{P}\dagger}(k_{x},0)K
, when
k_{x}=0,\pi
:

\displaystyle T^{\prime}(k_{x})H_{W}(-k_{x})T^{\prime-1}(k_{x})=H_{W}(k_{x}),

(70)

\displaystyle T^{\prime 2}=B^{S\mathcal{P}}(k_{x},0)B^{S\mathcal{P}*}(k_{x},0)=-1.

(71)

Thus a Kramers’ degeneracy is preserved at
k_{x}=0,\pi
.

Without
pairing, the singularity (the Dirac crossing) forces a jump in the Wannier spectrum by
\pi
as
k_{x}
crosses the Dirac point location, as the Berry phase enclosed in a loop around the Dirac point is
\pi
.
The two Dirac points at
K_{M},K_{M}^{\prime}
(we have restricted ourselves to the
\eta=+1
graphene valley) thus yield two jumps of
\pi
.
After pairing is added, the sharp discontinuities are smoothed out (as the spectrum is now gapped), but the two Wannier bands still criss-cross, yielding a nontrivial Dirac flow for two bands.
Regardless of how many pairs of trivial bands one adds to the spectrum, the Wannier flow is preserved and hence the superconductor remains topological.
If one breaks valley-U(1) symmetry, the superconductor will turn trivial.
See Fig.
5
for an illustration.

Figure 5:
Left panel:
Wannier band spectrum of TBG-TSC in a single valley
\tau=+1
. Before pairing, the energy spectrum has gapless points when the momentum crosses the Dirac points. Because there is one Dirac cone from
k_{x}\in[0,\pi)
, the Wannier bands’ value will jump by
\pi
. Adding pairing will smooth out the jump but cannot erase the nontrivial flow from left to right due to Kramers degeneracies at
k_{x}=0,\pi
.

Right panel:
Both valleys are allowed to hybridize with each other, with strong valley-U(1) breaking. This allows the nontrivial bands to pair up and avoid while still remaining Kramers’ partners at the TRI momenta. As twisted bilayer graphene is only anomalous within a single valley, this behavior is expected.

We verify numerically that the spin-singlet paired continuous TBG model is topological, using the Wilson loop.
Fixing
w_{1}=110
meV,
w_{0}=0.8w_{1}
,
v_{F}=5.944{\rm eV\cdot\mathring{A}}
, and
|\Delta_{0}|=20
meV, we calculated the Moiré bands and the Wilson loops of the -1 and -2 bands at three different twist angles,
\theta=1.05^{\circ},1.20^{\circ},1.40^{\circ}
, respectively, as is shown in Fig.
4
(c)-(h).
As expected, the spin-singlet superconducting phase at these angles are all topological.

Appendix B
Dirac theory of the topological superconductor

We use a simplified Dirac theory to demonstrate the zero modes on the phase boundary and in the vortex of the superconductor order parameters.
With no pairing, BdG Hamiltonian in each valley has two Dirac points at
K_{M}
and
K_{M}^{\prime}
.
Hence we can write the low energy model of the BdG Hamiltonian as

\mathcal{H}=k_{x}\xi_{z}\tau_{z}\mu_{0}\sigma_{x}+k_{y}\xi_{z}\tau_{0}\mu_{0}\sigma_{y}+\Delta\xi_{x}\tau_{0}\mu_{0}\sigma_{0}.

(72)

Here
\xi_{0,x,y,z}
,
\tau_{0,x,y,z}
,
\mu_{0,x,y,z}
,
\sigma_{0,x,y,z}
are the Pauli matrices (identity) in the space of particle-hole, valley, Moiré valley, and sublattice, respectively.

\mu_{z}=1
and
-1
correspond to the Dirac points at
K_{M}
and
K_{M}^{\prime}
, respectively.
Similar to
eq.

33
, the basis of the BdG Hamiltonian is defined as

\psi_{\mathbf{k},\eta,l,\alpha,s}=\begin{cases}c_{\mathbf{k},\eta,l,\alpha,\uparrow},\qquad&\text{if}\;s=\uparrow\\
c_{-\mathbf{k},-\eta,-l,\alpha,\downarrow}^{\dagger},\qquad&\text{if}\;s=\downarrow\end{cases}\ ,

(73)

where
\eta
,
l
,
\alpha
,
s
stands for the graphene valley, Moiré valley, sublattice, and spin (particle-hole) indices, respectively.
The symmetry actions on the BdG basis are same as in
sections

A.2
and
A.3
.
(One can think that the
\mu_{z}=\pm 1
Moiré valleys are contributed by states on the lattices
\mathcal{Q}_{\pm}
, respectively. Then all the symmetry operators defined in
sections

A.2
and
A.3
in Eqs.
23
-
29
,
A.3
-
46
also apply to the Dirac theory except that one needs to replace the matrices involving
\mathbf{Q}
vectors in
eq.

23
to (
29
) as
\delta_{\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},C_{3z}\mathbf{Q}}\to\mu_{0}
,

\delta_{\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},C_{2x}\mathbf{Q}}\to\mu_{x}
,

\delta_{\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\mathbf{Q}}\to\mu_{0}
,

\delta_{\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},-\mathbf{Q}}\to\mu_{x}
,

\delta_{\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},-\mathbf{Q}}\zeta_{\mathbf{Q}}\to i\mu_{y}
.)
The effective symmetry operators for the BdG Hamiltonian can be similarly obtained as

\displaystyle S=\xi_{y},\quad\mathcal{P}=i\xi_{z}\mu_{y}\sigma_{x}K,\quad T=\tau_{x}\mu_{x}K

(74)

\displaystyle C_{2z}T=\sigma_{x}K,\quad C_{2x}=\mu_{x}\sigma_{x},\quad C_{3z}=e^{i\frac{2\pi}{3}\tau_{z}\sigma_{z}}\ .

B.1
The pairing domain wall edge state in the
x
-direction

The BdG Hamiltonians in the two valleys are related by the time-reversal symmetry
T
(or the
s_{y}
spin rotation
i\hat{s}_{y}
).
The states in the valley
\eta=-1
are simply the time-reversal partners of the states in the valley
\eta=+1
.
Thus in the following we will mainly focus on the valley
\eta=+1
.
Notice that for a single graphene and Moiré valley Eq. (
72
) resembles the Hamiltonian of a quantum spin Hall edge, with time-reversal
T^{\prime}=i\sigma_{y}K
.
As the edge of a quantum spin Hall system carries a helical edge mode, TBG-TSC carries four, two for graphene valley and two for Moiré valley.
To derive the edge Hamiltonian, we model the domain wall with a jump in the pairing term
\Delta(x)=-\Delta_{0}\text{sgn}(x)
(take this to be the edge perpendicular to the
x
axis, so the bulk is to the left of the edge), and take the ansatz

\displaystyle\phi_{k_{y}}(x)\propto e^{ik_{y}y-\lambda|x|}u(k_{y}),\qquad(\lambda>0),

(75)

for some eight component vector
u
.
Since the translation symmetry along
y
is respected,
k_{y}
is still a good quantum number.
We first solve the zero modes at
k_{y}=0
and then apply the perturbation theory for finite
k_{y}
to obtain the effective Hamiltonian on the edge.
For
k_{y}=0
, with Eq.
72
with
\eta=+1
, taking
k_{x}\rightarrow-i\partial_{x}
, we obtain the eigenequation

\displaystyle(\lambda-\Delta_{0}\xi_{y}\mu_{0}\sigma_{x})u=0.

(76)

There are four degenerate bound states when
\lambda=\Delta_{0}
, and the wavefunctions are

\displaystyle u_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
i\end{bmatrix}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\end{bmatrix}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\end{bmatrix},\;u_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
i\end{bmatrix}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle u_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}-1\\
i\end{bmatrix}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\end{bmatrix}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}1\\
-1\end{bmatrix},\;u_{4}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}-1\\
i\end{bmatrix}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}1\\
-1\end{bmatrix}.

(77)

The effective Hamiltonian is obtained by projecting
k_{y}\xi_{z}\mu_{0}\sigma_{y}
onto the zero mode basis:

\mathcal{H}_{\rm eff}=k_{y}\xi_{y}^{\prime}\mu_{0}\ .

(78)

Here
\xi_{0,x,y,z}^{\prime}
and
\mu_{0,x,y,z}
are Pauli (identity) matrices defined in the projected four-dimensional space.
While
\mu
is the same as the original Pauli matrix used in the Dirac Hamiltonian,
\xi_{z}^{\prime}
is a combination of
\xi,\sigma
operators.
The projected symmetry operators for
S
,
\mathcal{P}
and
C_{2x}
are

S=\xi_{z}^{\prime},\quad\mathcal{P}=i\xi_{z}^{\prime}{\mu_{y}}K,\quad C_{2x}=\xi_{z}^{\prime}{\mu_{x}}\ .

(79)

The gapless edge state is protected by
S
,
\mathcal{P}
and
C_{2x}
.
There are eight terms that anti-commute with
\mathcal{H}_{\rm eff}
and hence would open full gaps:
\xi_{x}^{\prime}\mu_{0,x,y,z}
,
\xi_{z}^{\prime}\mu_{0,x,y,z}
.
Only the first four terms are allowed by
S=\xi_{z}^{\prime}
.
Among these four terms, only
\xi_{x}^{\prime}\mu_{0}
is allowed by
\mathcal{P}=i\xi_{z}^{\prime}\mu_{y}K
.
However, this term breaks the
C_{2x}=\xi_{z}^{\prime}\mu_{x}
symmetry.
Therefore, no gap term is allowed by the symmetries.
Terms like
\xi_{y}^{\prime}\mu_{y}
that commute with the Hamiltonian will not open up gaps, but shift the bands up and down relative to one another and move the crossing locations.

We can diagonalize the edge Hamiltonian.
Here we choose the wavefunctions of the two right movers (
\xi_{y}^{\prime}=1
) as
\frac{1+i}{2}(u_{1}+iu_{3})
,
\frac{1+i}{2}(u_{2}+iu_{4})
, and the wavefunctions of the two left movers (
\xi_{y}^{\prime}=-1
) as
\frac{1+i}{2}(u_{2}-iu_{4})
,
\frac{1+i}{2}(u_{1}-iu_{3})
.
The annihilation operators of the two right movers are given by

\psi_{1R\uparrow}(y)=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{2}\int dxe^{-\lambda|x|}(c_{+,+,A,\uparrow}(x,y)-ic_{+,+,B,\uparrow}(x,y)-c^{\dagger}_{-,-,A,\downarrow}(x,y)-ic^{\dagger}_{-,-,B,\downarrow}(x,y))

(80)

\psi_{2R\uparrow}(y)=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{2}\int dxe^{-\lambda|x|}(c_{+,-,A,\uparrow}(x,y)-ic_{+,-,B,\uparrow}(x,y)-c^{\dagger}_{-,+,A,\downarrow}(x,y)-ic^{\dagger}_{-,+,B,\downarrow}(x,y))

(81)

\psi_{1L\uparrow}(y)=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{2}\int dxe^{-\lambda|x|}(-ic_{+,-,A,\uparrow}(x,y)+c_{+,-,B,\uparrow}(x,y)-ic^{\dagger}_{-,+,A,\downarrow}(x,y)-c^{\dagger}_{-,+,B,\downarrow}(x,y))

(82)

\psi_{2L\uparrow}(y)=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{2}\int dxe^{-\lambda|x|}(-ic_{+,+,A,\uparrow}(x,y)+c_{+,+,B,\uparrow}(x,y)-ic^{\dagger}_{-,-,A,\downarrow}(x,y)-c^{\dagger}_{-,-,B,\downarrow}(x,y))

(83)

Here the subscripts
\eta,l,\alpha,s
in the operator
c_{\eta,l,\alpha,s}
represent the valley, Moiré valley, sublattice, and spin, respectively.
The spin of the operator
\psi
is chosen to be in the same direction as the annihilation operators
c
and opposite to the creation operators
c^{\dagger}
that compose
\psi
.
The four edge states in the other valley sector can be obtained by acting spin rotation (or time-reversal) on the above four states:

\psi_{\alpha,n,\downarrow}=-i\hat{s}_{y}\psi_{\alpha,n,\uparrow}(-i\hat{s}_{y})^{\dagger},\quad(\alpha=1,2,\ n=R,L)

(84)

with
-i\hat{s}_{y}
being the
\pi
-rotation of spin along the
y
-direction.
Therefore, we have in total four right movers and four left movers on the edge perpendicular to
x
-direction.
Each of the eight modes is a complex fermion, not a Majorana as we are working in the non-redundant basis.

B.2
Edge states in domain walls in other directions

We consider a
\Delta
domain wall rotated (anticlockwise) by an angle
\theta
from the edge perpendicular to the
x
direction.
We define
k_{\perp}=k_{x}\cos\theta+k_{y}\sin\theta
,
k_{\parallel}=-k_{x}\sin\theta+k_{y}\cos\theta
,
x_{\perp}=x\cos\theta+y\sin\theta
,
r_{\parallel}=-x\sin\theta+y\cos\theta
.
Then the 2D Dirac Hamiltonian can be rewritten as

\mathcal{H}^{(+)}=k_{\perp}\xi_{z}\mu_{0}\tilde{\sigma}_{x}^{(\theta)}+k_{\parallel}\xi_{z}\mu_{0}\tilde{\sigma}_{y}^{(\theta)}+\Delta(r_{\perp})\xi_{x}\mu_{0}\sigma_{0},

(85)

where
\Delta(r_{\perp})=-\Delta_{0}\mathrm{sgn}(r_{\perp})
,
\tilde{\sigma}_{x,y}^{(\theta)}=e^{-i\frac{\theta}{2}\sigma_{z}}\sigma_{x,y}e^{i\frac{\theta}{2}\sigma_{z}}
.
The basis of the low-energy states along this rotated edge is
u_{n}^{(\theta)}=e^{-i\frac{\theta}{2}\sigma_{z}}u_{n}
(
n=1,2,3,4
) with
u_{n}
defined in
section

B.1
such that the effective edge Hamiltonian and the
S
,
\mathcal{P}
symmetries take the same form as
eqs.

78
and
79
.
The
S
and
\mathcal{P}
symmetric gap term
\xi_{x}^{\prime}\mu_{0}
is forbidden by
C_{2x}
for
\theta=0
, as under
C_{2x}=\xi_{z}^{\prime}\mu_{x},M_{1}(y)\xi_{x}^{\prime}\mu_{0}\rightarrow-M_{1}(-y)\xi_{x}^{\prime}\mu_{0}
, forcing
M_{1}(y)=-M_{1}(y)
. With translation symmetry this forces
M_{1}=0
.
This term is also forbidden for
\theta=n\frac{2\pi}{6}
(
n=1\cdots 5
) due to the
C_{3z}
symmetry.
However, for generic
\theta
, no crystalline symmetry can forbid this gap term.
For
\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}
(
n\frac{2\pi}{6}+\frac{\pi}{6}
), the edge has the
C_{2y}T=C_{2x}\cdot C_{2z}T
symmetry (
C_{3z}^{n}C_{2y}TC_{3z}^{-n}
).
The symmetries obey

\displaystyle[S,\mathcal{P}]=0,~~\{\mathcal{P},C_{2y}T\}=0,~~\{S,C_{2y}T\}=0.

(86)

However, the
C_{2y}T
symmetry cannot protect the edge states.
To be specific, for
\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}
, we find the projected
C_{2y}T
operator as
\xi_{x}^{\prime}\mu_{x}K
.
(This is derived from the Pauli matrix form of
C_{2y}T=C_{2x}\cdot C_{2z}T=\mu_{x}K
, which projecting into the low-energy basis gives
\xi_{z}^{\prime}\mu_{x}K
.)
The gap term
\xi_{x}^{\prime}
is allowed by the
S
,
\mathcal{P}
,
C_{2y}T
symmetries.
Therefore, the edge state for generic
\theta\neq n\frac{2\pi}{6}
is unstable.

As shown in
appendix

C
, for the particular Hamiltonian of TBG and pairings that obey
\Delta(r_{\perp})=-\Delta(-r_{\perp})
, the system has an accidental inversion-like unitary particle-hole symmetry if the domain wall passes through a
C_{2z}
center of the Moiré unit cell.
This accidental symmetry, when present, will protect the gapless edge state in any direction.
As detailed in
appendix

C
, additional terms are added in the numerical calculation to remove this accidental symmetry.

B.3
Corner states and the higher-order topology

To conveniently see the corner states, let us break the translation symmetry along
y
but preserve
C_{2x}
symmetry on the edge perpendicular to
x
.
In practice, the translation breaking can be realized by a corner geometry (
fig.

7
) or a circular geometry (
fig.

2
b and
fig.

8
).
As discussed in
section

B.1
, for the edge Hamiltonian
eq.

78
, the only
S
and
\mathcal{P}
allowed gap term
M_{1}(y)\xi_{x}^{\prime}\mu_{0}
is forbidden by
C_{2x}
if the translation symmetry is assumed, which enforces
M_{1}(y)
to be a
y
-independent constant.
Since now the translation symmetry is broken, a
y
-dependent
M_{1}(y)
is allowed and
M_{1}(y)
must be odd in
y
due to
\{C_{2x},\xi_{x}^{\prime}\mu_{0}\}=0
.
The edge Hamiltonian now reads

\displaystyle{\cal H}=(-i\partial_{y})\xi_{y}^{\prime}\mu_{0}+M_{1}(y)\xi_{x}^{\prime}\mu_{0}.

(87)

Now we show that the oddness of
M_{1}(y)
gives rise two zero modes per valley, or four total.
This is expected from general arguments: a helical one-dimensional Dirac mode with a domain wall will bind a fermionic zero mode; four copies will bind four.
Without loss of generality, we assume
M_{1}(\infty)=-M_{1}(-\infty)>0
, taking ansatz

\displaystyle\phi(y)\propto e^{-\lambda\int_{0}^{y}dy^{\prime}M_{1}(y^{\prime})}v,

(88)

with
\lambda>0
and
v
being a four-component vector, we have the eigenequation

\displaystyle(\lambda-\xi_{z}^{\prime}\mu_{0})v=0.

(89)

There are two solutions for
\lambda=1
, and the corresponding wavefunctions are

v_{1}=(1,0,0,0)^{T},\qquad v_{2}=(0,1,0,0)^{T}\ .

(90)

The projected symmetry operators in this 2D space are

S=1,\qquad\mathcal{P}=i\mu_{y}^{\prime\prime}K,\qquad C_{2x}=\mu_{x}^{\prime\prime}\ ,

(91)

with
\mu_{x,y}^{\prime\prime}
being the Pauli matrices in the 2D space.
One can see that the two zero modes have the same chiral eigenvalue.
Due to the fact
\mathcal{P}
is local in real space, anti-unitary, and squares to
-1
, Kramers theorem guarantees that the two zero modes must locate at the same position.
We call the two zero modes a zero mode doublet.
Thus the two zero modes per valley are stable against any local perturbation that respects the chiral symmetry.
According to the wavefunctions
eqs.

90
and
B.1
, we can write the annihilation operators of the two zero modes as

\Phi_{1\uparrow}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}}}\int dxdy\ e^{-\Delta_{0}|x|-\int^{y}_{0}dy^{\prime}M_{1}(y^{\prime})}(c_{+,+,A,\uparrow}(x,y)+c_{+,+,B,\uparrow}(x,y)-ic_{-,-,A,\downarrow}^{\dagger}(x,y)-ic_{-,-,B,\downarrow}^{\dagger}(x,y)),

(92)

\Phi_{2\uparrow}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}}}\int dxdy\ e^{-\Delta_{0}|x|-\int^{y}_{0}dy^{\prime}M_{1}(y^{\prime})}(c_{+,-,A,\uparrow}(x,y)+c_{+,-,B,\uparrow}(x,y)-ic_{-,+,A,\downarrow}^{\dagger}(x,y)-ic_{-,+,B,\downarrow}^{\dagger}(x,y)),

(93)

where
\eta,l,\alpha,s
in the operator
c_{\eta,l,\alpha,s}
represent the valley, Moiré valley, sublattice, and spin, respectively, and
\mathcal{N}
is a normalized constant.
The two corner states in the other valley sector can be obtained by acting spin rotation (or time-reversal) on the above two states:

\Phi_{\alpha,\downarrow}=-i\hat{s}_{y}\Phi_{\alpha,\uparrow}(-i\hat{s}_{y})^{\dagger},\quad(\alpha=1,2),

(94)

with
-i\hat{s}_{y}
being the
\pi
-rotation of spin along the
y
-direction.
Therefore, we have in total four complex fermion zero modes (or eight Majorana zero modes) at this corner.

Now we consider a circular geometry of the phase domain wall shown in
fig.

2
b and
fig.

8
.
The above calculation applies to the corner at
y=0
.
Due to the
C_{3z}
and
C_{2z}T
symmetry, the system must have zero mode doublets at other five positions, which are rotated (anticlockwisely) from the corner in the
x
-direction by the angles
\theta=n\frac{2\pi}{6}
(
n=1\cdots 5
).
Since
[C_{3z},S]=0
and
\{C_{2z}T,S\}=0
, the zero mode doublets at
\theta=0,\frac{2\pi}{3},-\frac{2\pi}{3}
have the chiral eigenvalue
+1
; whereas zero mode doublets at
\theta=\frac{\pi}{3},\pi,-\frac{\pi}{3}
have the chiral eigenvalue
-1
.

We emphasize that the higher-order topology is protected by
C_{2z}T
,
\mathcal{P}
,
S
.
The presence of
C_{2x}
and
C_{3z}
only pins the corner states at the specific positions (
\theta=n\frac{2\pi}{6}
,
n=0\cdots 5
).
We can consider breaking
C_{3z}
to create (annihilate) a pair of zero mode doublets (in each valley) with opposite chiral eigenvalues at the angle
\theta
.
Then due to the
C_{2z}T
symmetry, we must create (annihilate) another pair of zero mode doublets (in each valley) at the angle
\theta+\pi
.
Hence we can change the number of zero mode doublets by 4.
Starting with 6 zero mode doublets, such
C_{2z}T
-symmetric process may change the number of zero mode doublets to
4n+2
(
n\in\mathbb{N}
).
Therefore, one cannot remove all the zero modes in our system without breaking
C_{2z}T
.
This is another proof that our system is always higher-order topological.

B.4
Robustness of the corner states against
\mathcal{P}
-breaking

Recall that the
\mathcal{P}
symmetry is an approximate, although very good, symmetry of the TBG Hamiltonian.
In practice, it is weakly broken by the small
\theta
dependence of the two Dirac Hamiltonians from the two graphene layers
Song et al. 2020
.
In the BdG formalism, the
\mathcal{P}
symmetry can also be broken by finite chemical potential.
In this section we study the robustness of the zero modes when
\mathcal{P}
is perturbatively broken.
As shown in
section

B.3
, the two zero modes (in each valley) at a corner have the same chiral eigenvalue.
Hence they must be stable against any perturbation that respects the chiral symmetry.
To see this, assume the states
\psi_{1,2}
are at nonzero energy
H\psi_{1}=E\psi_{1},H\psi_{2}=-E\psi_{2}
.
Since
\{S,H\}=0
, we have
S\psi_{1}=\psi_{2}
and
S\psi_{2}=\psi_{1}
up to a phase factor that can be gauged away.
This means that the combinations
\psi_{1}\pm\psi_{2}
are eigenvectors of
S
with eigenvalue
\pm 1
, which do
not
have the same chiral eigenvalue.
Thus if two states have the same chiral eigenvalue, they cannot tunnel into one another.

We also find that the
\mathcal{P}
-breaking term will not destroy the two zero modes, but only split them in real space.
To be specific, we consider a corner geometry described by the edge Hamiltonian
eq.

78
plus a translation breaking term
M_{1}(y)\xi_{x}^{\prime}\mu_{0}
.
There are two terms,
i.e.
,
\xi_{x}^{\prime}\mu_{y}
,
\xi_{x}^{\prime}\mu_{z}
, that break
\mathcal{P}
and preserve
S
and
C_{2x}
.
(The symmetry operators are given in
eq.

79
.)
Since the two terms are related by a gauge transformation that maps
\mu_{y}
to
\mu_{z}
and leaves the edge Hamiltonian
eq.

78
and
M_{1}(y)\xi_{x}^{\prime}\mu_{0}
unchanged, we only need to calculate one of them.
Thus we assume the Hamiltonian as

\mathcal{H}=-i\partial_{y}\xi_{y}^{\prime}\mu_{0}+M_{1}(y)\xi_{x}^{\prime}\mu_{0}+M_{2}(y)\xi_{x}^{\prime}\mu_{z},

(95)

where
M_{1}(y)
is odd in
y
(and yields the
2
corner modes per valley) and
M_{2}(y)
is even in
y
.
Without loss of generality, we assume
M_{1}(y)=m_{1}\tanh(y)
and
M_{2}(y)=m_{2}
.
For
m_{2}<m_{1}
, the gap term
M_{1}(y)\xi_{x}^{\prime}\mu_{0}+M_{2}(y)\xi_{x}^{\prime}\mu_{z}
has zero eigenvalues at
y_{\pm}=\pm\tanh^{-1}\frac{m_{2}}{m_{1}}
.
Therefore, the two zero modes per valley are split in space to the positions
y_{\pm}
.
We can see that, when
m_{2}\to m_{1}-0^{+}
, which means strong
\mathcal{P}
breaking, the two zero modes are sent to
\pm\infty
.
As discussed in the main text, for a circular domain wall, under a strong
\mathcal{P}
breaking, two zero modes with opposite chiral eigenvalues from two nearby corners will meet and annihilate each other.

B.5
Zero modes in the Abrikosov vortex

The two zero modes per valley also appear in the center of a vortex of the superconductor order parameter.
We introduce the polar coordinate system
x=r\cos\theta
,
y=r\sin\theta
and assume the pairing term as
\Delta(x,y)=e^{-i\theta}\Delta(r)
.
Substituting this pairing term into the Dirac Hamiltonian (
eq.

72
), we can write the BdG Hamiltonian (in valley
\eta=+
) as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{(\eta=+)}

\displaystyle=\xi_{z}\mu_{0}(k_{x}\sigma_{x}+k_{y}\sigma_{y})

\displaystyle+\Delta(r)(\cos{\theta}\xi_{x}+\sin{\theta}\xi_{y})\mu_{0}\sigma_{0}.

(96)

The new term
\Delta(r)\sin{\theta}\xi_{y}
, even choosing
\mathbf{r}
at a
C_{2z}
center, violates the symmetries
S,\mathcal{P}
defined in Eq. (
74
), while
\Delta(r)\cos{\theta}\xi_{x}
violates
C_{2z}T
(which rotates
\theta\rightarrow\theta+\pi
).

It will be convenient for us to define the operator
C_{2z}^{\prime}T=\xi_{z}C_{2z}T
; this operator fails to commute with
\Delta(r)\sin{\theta}\xi_{y}
instead of the
\xi_{x}
term. Since now all three of these symmetries
C_{2z}^{\prime}T,S,\mathcal{P}
all fail to commute with the
\Delta(r)\sin{\theta}\xi_{y}
operator, the combined symmetries,
C_{2z}^{\prime}TS,S\mathcal{P}
are preserved.

To solve the Hamiltonian, we switch to polar coordinates

\displaystyle\partial_{r}

\displaystyle=\cos{\theta}\partial_{x}+\sin{\theta}\partial_{y}

(97)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r}\partial_{\theta}

\displaystyle=-\sin{\theta}\partial_{x}+\cos{\theta}\partial_{y}

(98)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}+i\partial_{y}

\displaystyle=e^{i\theta}(\partial_{r}+\frac{i}{r}\partial_{\theta}):

(99)

\mathcal{H}^{(\eta=+)}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-ie^{-i\theta}(\partial_{r}-\frac{i}{r}\partial_{\theta})\mu_{0}&e^{-i\theta}\Delta(r)\mu_{0}&0\\
-ie^{i\theta}(\partial_{r}+\frac{i}{r}\partial_{\theta})\mu_{0}&0&0&e^{-i\theta}\Delta(r)\mu_{0}\\
e^{i\theta}\Delta(r)\mu_{0}&0&0&ie^{-i\theta}(\partial_{r}-\frac{i}{r}\partial_{\theta})\mu_{0}\\
0&e^{i\theta}\Delta(r)\mu_{0}&ie^{i\theta}(\partial_{r}+\frac{i}{r}\partial_{\theta})\mu_{0}&0\end{pmatrix},

(100)

where
\mu_{0}
is the two-by-two identity in the Moiré valley index.
Here we have ordered the basis as
\psi_{\eta=+,l=+,\alpha=A,\uparrow}
,
\psi_{+,-,A,\uparrow}
,
\psi_{+,+,B,\uparrow}
,
\psi_{+,-,B,\uparrow}
,

\psi_{+,+,A,\downarrow}
,
\psi_{+,-,A,\downarrow}
,
\psi_{+,+,B,\downarrow}
,
\psi_{+,-,B,\downarrow}
.
We take the ansatz of the zero modes as

\phi(x,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}}}[g_{1}(r),g_{2}(r),g_{3}(r),g_{4}(r)]^{T}\otimes v

(101)

where
v
is a two-component vector,
g_{1,2,3,4}(r)
are functions of
r
to be determined and
\mathcal{N}
is a normalization factor.
For either
v=(1,0)^{T}
or
v=(0,1)^{T}
, we find that the zero mode satisfies
g_{1}=g_{4}=0
and

-i\partial_{r}g_{2}+\Delta(r)g_{3}=0,\qquad\Delta(r)g_{2}+i\partial_{r}g_{3}=0.

(102)

Combining the two equations, we obtain the equations

(104)

nonzero.

The combined symmetries,

0

Appendix E
Valley-U(1) breaking on a hard edge

In this section we discuss the valley-U(1) symmetry, and how in a system of TBG with no pairing either the valley symmetry or the anti-unitary particle-hole
\mathcal{P}

must
be strongly broken on the edge.

Consider a circular sample of TBG from radius
0<r<a
.
The Hamiltonian per spin sector without pairing reads

\displaystyle H=k_{x}\tau_{z}\mu_{0}\sigma_{x}+k_{y}\tau_{0}\mu_{0}\sigma_{y},

(136)

with the symmetries

\displaystyle C_{2z}T=\sigma_{x}K,~~{\cal P}=i\mu_{y}\sigma_{x}K.

(137)

Outside the sample is a vacuum and is fully gapped.
The vacuum must respect
C_{2z}T
and we
assume
that the vacuum also respects
\mathcal{P}
.
Then there must be terms consistent with the symmetries in
eq.

137
to gap out the Dirac terms
outside
the sample to properly model the symmetry-preserving vacuum.

Working in graphene valley
\tau_{z}=+1
, we wish to gap the spectrum by adding terms that anti-commute with the Hamiltonian:
\mu_{i}\sigma_{z}
.
Terms that commute with
H
and affect Moiré valley only, like
\mu_{x,z}
, do not open up gaps; they simply shift the Dirac crossing points away from each other.
Forcing consistency with
\cal{P}
further restricts the gap terms to the form

\displaystyle M_{1}(\mathbf{r})\mu_{0}\sigma_{z},

(138)

which to be symmetric under
C_{2z}T
forces
M_{1}(\mathbf{r})=-M_{1}(-\mathbf{r})
, with origin at
\mathbf{r}=0
.
Adding this term will gap out the Dirac cones, except along lines where
M_{1}(\mathbf{r})
vanishes.
By
M_{1}(\mathbf{r})=-M_{1}(-\mathbf{r})
and the intermediate value theorem, there must be at least two lines extending from the sample to infinity that are gapless, leading to a contradiction.
For a simple way to see the gapless lines, take
\mathbf{r}=re^{i\theta}
, and consider a circle of constant radius
r_{0}
so that
\mathbf{r}
lies outside the sample of TBG.

C_{2z}T
symmetry forces
M_{1}(r_{0},\theta)=-M_{1}(r_{0},\theta+\pi)
.
If
M_{1}(r_{0},\theta_{0})>0
, then
M_{1}(r_{0},\theta_{0}+\pi)<0
, and there must be at least two diametric points along the circle of constant radius
r_{0}
where
M_{1}
vanishes.
This argument holds for all
r_{0}
outside the sample of TBG.

What this analysis implies is that outside the TBG sample, in order to reproduce the fully gapped vacuum that respects
\mathcal{P}
we must involve terms that mix the valleys: e.g.
M_{2}(\mathbf{r})\tau_{x}\mu_{x,z}\sigma_{x}
.
Alternatively, breaking
\mathcal{P}
allows the term
M_{3}(\mathbf{r})\mu_{y}\sigma_{z}
.
Both
M_{2}(\mathbf{r})
and
M_{3}(\mathbf{r})
will gap out the Dirac cone everywhere, because they are symmetric under
C_{2z}T
and is not required to vanish anywhere in the vacuum.
This means that along the edge of TBG, at those points where
M_{1}(\mathbf{r})
vanishes we must gap out the system instead with
M_{2}
or
M_{3}
, and will strongly break either valley-
U(1)
or
\mathcal{P}
.

Appendix F
Shiba States

We study the response of TBG-TSC to impurities.
Similar techniques involving Shiba states
Shiba 1968
have been proposed to detect topological superconductivity in 1D and topological insulators in higher dimensions
Sau and Demler 2013
;
Slager et al. 2015
, and even build topological phases
Pientka et al. 2013
.
These works study the Green’s function of the Hamiltonian and how it reacts to delta-function impurities.
However, the Dirac version of our Hamiltonian has a problem: it does not appropriately distinguish between the trivial and topological phases on its own.
The Dirac equation possess a symmetry
\Delta\rightarrow-\Delta
, with
\Delta
the mass, while the two phases are meant to be distinct.
The standard technique is to modify the Dirac Hamiltonian mass (i.e. superconducting gap) to
\Delta-mk^{2}
for some mass
m
(
section

A.4
)
Shen et al. 2011
.
This term is employed to properly calculate, for example, the Chern number for the Dirac theory of topological insulators and superconductors: with
m<0
for
\Delta>0
, the system is trivial, and with
m>0
, the system is topological
Shen et al. 2011
.
Notice that the topological phase has a band inversion while the trivial phase does not.
In this Appendix, we calculate the behavior of Shiba states for the Dirac Hamiltonian with quadratic correction, and demonstrate a qualitative difference between the
m<0
and
m>0
phases.

For TBG-TSC, however, we argue that
m<0
and
m>0

do not
correspond to the topological and trivial phases.
The distinction between topological and trivial phases works for non-anomalous systems, like a Chern insulator in two dimensions. In that situation
m<0
and
m>0
indeed do correspond to topological and trivial phases. In TBG-TSC however, the single-valley Hamiltonian is anomalous, analogous to the surface of a 3DTI. The two distinct phases correspond to
\Delta>0
and
\Delta<0
(not
m
), and even then are not topological/trivial themselves but only have topological distinction when compared to one another.
Properly capturing the topological-trivial distinction requires
|m\Lambda^{2}|\gg\Delta
, with
\Lambda
being the UV cutoff for
k
.
In this limit the band inversion is present and the Hamiltonian is well-defined even as
k\rightarrow\infty
.

The opposite limit,
|m\Lambda^{2}|\ll|\Delta|
, does not have a well-defined large momentum limit.
Only the difference between
\Delta<0
and
\Delta>0
has a well-defined topological invariant.
In this work we have assumed the second limit because the nearly uniform spin-singlet pairing (
section

A.4
) has no
\mathbf{k}
-dependence or very weak
\mathbf{k}
-dependence (
section

A.4
):
\Delta-m\mathbf{k}^{2}
is close to being uniform, so

for all

in the BZ.
This means that while Shiba states do distinguish between
m<0
and
m>0
phases (the former corresponding to singlet-pairing TBG-TSC), it does not offer a distinguishing signature of TBG-TSC. Nevertheless, we examine the behavior of impurities in the TBG-TSC Hamiltonian for different values of

F.5
Multiple Valleys

We have demonstrated that zero modes exist for arbitrary impurity strengths for
m\Delta>0
, while for
m\Delta<0
Shiba states will either not exist or can be pushed into the bulk with decreasing impurity strength.
As calculated in App.
A.4
, in TBG-TSC with nearly uniform pairing we have
m\Delta<0
, where the on-site pairing strength is much greater than the NNN pairing strength,
\bar{\Delta}_{0}\gg\bar{\Delta}_{2}
.
However, in TBG-TSC the observation of Shiba states is complicated by the presence of multiple valleys and zero modes.
A delta-function impurity itself will break valley-U(1) symmetry, allowing for the zero modes of different valleys to hybridize.

Take the real-space perturbation

\displaystyle{~~~~~~m\sum_{\alpha,l,s}\sum_{\mathbf{R}\in l}\delta_{\mathbf{R},0}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R},l,\alpha,s}c_{\mathbf{R},l,\alpha,s}}

(176)

\displaystyle={\frac{m}{N_{M}N_{0}}\sum_{\alpha,l,s,\eta,\eta^{\prime},\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}}e^{-i(\eta\mathbf{K}_{l}+\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q})\cdot\mathbf{t}_{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle{\times e^{i(\eta^{\prime}\mathbf{K}_{l}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{Q}^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{t}_{\alpha}}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},\eta,\alpha,s}c_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\eta^{\prime},\alpha,s}}.

(177)

We see that this perturbation mixes the valleys, and thus require that the perturbation not be a delta-function impurity, but ramp up gradually over the length scale of the graphene lattice (but quickly on the scale of the Moiré lattice).

Two important considerations needed to protect the
Z_{3}

\ket{1,1,1,0}_{\uparrow\downarrow},\ket{1,0,0,0}_{\uparrow\downarrow}

\ket{1,1,0}_{\uparrow\downarrow},\ket{1,0,0}_{\uparrow\downarrow}
