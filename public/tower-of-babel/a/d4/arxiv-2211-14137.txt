The randomization by Wishart laws and the Fisher information
Gérard Letac

Abstract
Consider the centered Gaussian vector X X in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} with covariance matrix Σ . \Sigma. Randomize Σ \Sigma such that Σ − 1 \Sigma^{-1} has a Wishart distribution with shape parameter p > ( n − 1 ) / 2 p>(n-1)/2 and mean p ​ σ . p\sigma. We compute the density f p , σ f_{p,\sigma} of X X as well as the Fisher information I p ​ ( σ ) I_{p}(\sigma) of the model ( f p , σ ) (f_{p,\sigma}) when σ \sigma is the parameter. For using the Cramér-Rao inequality, we also compute the inverse of I p ​ ( σ ) I_{p}(\sigma) . The important point of this note is the fact that this inverse is a linear combination of two simple operators on the space of symmetric matrices, namely ℙ ​ ( σ ) ​ ( s ) = σ ​ s ​ σ \mathbb{P}(\sigma)(s)=\sigma s\sigma and ( σ ⊗ σ ) ​ ( s ) = σ ​ trace ​ ( σ ​ s ) (\sigma\otimes\sigma)(s)=\sigma\,\mathrm{trace}(\sigma s) . The Fisher information itself is a linear combination ℙ ⁡ ( σ − 1 ) \mathbb{P}(\sigma^{-1}) and σ − 1 ⊗ σ − 1 . \sigma^{-1}\otimes\sigma^{-1}. Finally, by randomizing σ \sigma itself, we make explicit the minoration of the second moments of an estimator of σ \sigma by the Van Trees inequality: here again, linear combinations of ℙ ⁡ ( u ) \mathbb{P}(u) and u ⊗ u u\otimes u appear in the results.

Proposition 2.1.

The next step is the calculation of the Fisher information

I_{p}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{2(2p+3)}\left((2p+1)\mathbb{P}(\sigma^{-1})-\sigma^{-1}\otimes\sigma^{-1}\right)

Before stating a corollary of Proposition 3.1, we need a lemma:

Lemma 3.2.
Let
E
be the Euclidean space of symmetric matrices of order
n
and let
E_{+}\subset E
be the set of the positive definite ones. Fix
\sigma
in
E_{+}
and
c
a real number. Then

•

(\mathbb{P}(\sigma)-c\sigma\otimes\sigma)^{-1}
exists if and only if
c\neq 1/n
and is
\mathbb{P}(\sigma)+\frac{c}{1-nc}\sigma\otimes\sigma.

•

\mathbb{P}(\sigma)-c\sigma\otimes\sigma
is positive definite if and only if
c<1/n.

•

\det(\mathbb{P}(\sigma)-c\sigma\otimes\sigma)=(\det\sigma)^{n+1}(1-cn).

Proof.
Let us observe that
\mathbb{P}(\sigma^{-1/2})(\sigma)=I_{n}
and that
\|I_{n}\|^{2}=\,\textmd{tr}\,(I_{n})=n.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\sigma)-c\sigma\otimes\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\sigma^{1/2})\left(\mathrm{id}_{E}-c(\mathbb{P}(\sigma^{1/2})\sigma)\otimes(\mathbb{P}(\sigma^{1/2})\sigma)\right)\mathbb{P}(\sigma^{1/2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\sigma^{1/2})\left(\mathrm{id}_{E}-cI_{n}\otimes I_{n}\right)\mathbb{P}(\sigma^{1/2})

Applying Lemma 2.2 to
a=I_{n}
shows the first two points as well as
\det\left(\mathrm{id}_{E}-cI_{n}\otimes I_{n}\right)=1-cn.
For proving that
\det\mathbb{P}(\sigma)=(\det\sigma)^{n+1}
let us assume without loss of generality that
\sigma=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}).
This implies that if
v\in E
we have

\mathbb{P}(\sigma)(v)=\sigma v\sigma=(\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}v_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}.

(3
.
16)

If
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n},
we create a natural basis
f
for
E
made with the matrices
e_{ii}=e_{i}\otimes e_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
and
e_{ij}=e_{i}\otimes e_{j}+e_{j}\otimes e_{i}
for
1\leq i<j\leq n.
An interpretation of (
3.16
) is that
\mathbb{P}(\sigma)(e_{ij})=\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}e_{ij}
for
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
, or that
e_{ij}
is an eigenvector of
\mathbb{P}(\sigma)
for the eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}.
Since
(e_{ij})_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}
is a basis of

we can claim that

\det\mathbb{P}(\sigma)=\prod_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}=(\lambda_{1}\ldots\lambda_{n})^{n+1}=(\det\sigma)^{n+1},

which concludes the proof.
\square

Remarks.

•

From the very definition of
I_{p}(\sigma)
this symmetric endomorphism of the Euclidean space

must be positive definite. Lemma 3.2 gives a direct proof of the fact that

given by formula (

) is positive definite.

•

To insist on the ubiquity of the symbols
\mathbb{P}(u)
and

consider the simple problem of the calculation of the Fisher information
F_{1}(u)
and
F_{2}(v)
for the two Fisher models

\{N(0,u^{-1})(dx),u\in E_{+}\}
and
\{N(0,v)(dx),v\in E_{+}\}
. These models are unconcerned with the randomization of

by Wishart law

In the first case the log likelihood is
\ell_{x}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\,x^{t}ux+\frac{1}{2}\,\log\det u.
This leads to

\ell^{\prime}_{x}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\,xx^{t}+\frac{1}{2}\,u^{-1},\ \ell^{\prime}_{x}(u)=-\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{P}(u^{-1}),\ F_{1}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{P}(u^{-1}).

Instead of a direct calculation, it is shorter to deduce
F_{2}(v)
from

by the change of variable formula for Fisher information by

Since the differential of

is

we get

F_{2}(v)=(-\mathbb{P}(v^{-1}))^{T}\circ F_{1}(v^{-1})\circ(-\mathbb{P}(v^{-1}))=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{P}(v_{-1})\mathbb{P}(v)\mathbb{P}(v^{-1})=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{P}(v^{-1}).

Of course this direct proof can be avoided by observing that the model
\{N(0,u^{-1})(dx),u\in E_{+}\}
is an exponential family.

•

It can be noted that in formula (
3.11
) the dimension

does not appear in the coefficients.

Explicit form of the Cramér- Rao inequality

Consider a random variable
\Sigma
valued in
E
as an unbiased estimator of
\sigma
for the Fisher model
\{f_{p,\sigma}(x)dx;\sigma\in E_{+}\}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}.
The Cramér- Rao inequality says that

\mathbb{E}((\Sigma-\sigma)\otimes(\Sigma-\sigma))-I_{p}(\sigma)^{-1}

is semi positive definite, where
I_{p}(\sigma)
has been computed in (
3.11
). We show that
I_{p}(\sigma)^{-1}
is in the space
L_{\sigma}
and is quite explicit:

Proposition 4.1.

I_{p}(\sigma)^{-1}=\frac{2(2p+3)}{2p+1}\left(\mathbb{P}(\sigma)+\frac{1}{2p+1-n}\sigma\otimes\sigma\right).

Proof.
It follows from the formula (
3.11
) and from Lemma 3.2 where
\sigma
is replaced by
\sigma^{-1}
and where

\square

The density information and the Van Trees inequality

In this section we describe the Van Trees inequality discovered in
[
8
]
. To this end, we define first the density information. Consider the probability density
\theta\mapsto\lambda(\theta)
defined on an open set
\Theta\subset E
where
E
is a Euclidean space. We assume that

•

\lambda
is strictly positive on
\Theta
;

•

the differential
\theta\mapsto\lambda^{\prime}(\theta)
exists and is continuous;

•

\lambda
is zero at the boundary of
\Theta
and vanishes at infinity. More specifically, for any
\epsilon>0
there exists a compact
K_{\epsilon}\subset\Theta
such that
\lambda(\theta)\leq\epsilon
for all
\theta\in\Theta\setminus K_{\epsilon}.

As a consequence
g=\log\lambda
is well defined. The density information
I_{\lambda}
is the symmetric endomorphism of
E
defined by

I_{\lambda}=\int_{\Omega}(g^{\prime}(\theta)\otimes g^{\prime}(\theta))\lambda(\theta)d\theta.

In the particular case
\Theta=E
this density information could be considered as the Fisher information of the location model
\{\lambda(\theta-t)d\theta;t\in E\}.
However the result

I_{\lambda}=-\int_{\Omega}g^{\prime\prime}(\theta)\lambda(\theta)d\theta.

(5
.
17)

is not proved as easily as its analog for the Fisher information. A necessary condition for applying the Stokes formula is that
g^{\prime}(\theta)\lambda(\theta)
is zero on the boundary
\partial\Theta
so that we can write
0=\int_{\Theta}(g^{\prime}(\theta)\lambda(\theta))^{\prime}d\theta.

Independently, consider the Fisher model
P=\{P_{\theta}(dw);\theta\in\Theta\}
defined on some measured space
(\Omega,\nu)
. For convenience we write

P_{\theta}(dw)=e^{\ell_{w}(\theta)}\nu(dw)

and we consider its Fisher information
I_{P}(\theta)
which is the symmetric endomorphism of
E
equal to

I_{P}(\theta)=\int_{\Omega}\ell^{\prime}_{w}(\theta)\otimes\ell^{\prime}_{w}(\theta)P_{\theta}(dw)=-\int_{\Omega}\ell^{\prime\prime}_{w}(\theta)P_{\theta}(dw).

The Van Trees inequality is a Bayesian result. Let
X(w)
be any estimator of
\theta
-not necessarily unbiased. Suppose now that
\theta
is randomized by
\lambda(\theta)
.
What is the generalization of the Cramér-Rao theorem to this situation? That is: what is a minorant of the endomorphism of
E
equal to

C=\mathbb{E}((X-\theta)\otimes(X-\theta))

(in the sense of the partial ordering
A\geq B
of the symmetric endomorphisms of
E
defined by
B-A
semi positive definite)? Here, the expectation
C
is taken on
\Omega\times\Theta
with respect to the probability

P_{\theta}(dw)\lambda(\theta)d\theta
. The Van Trees inequality says that if

D=I_{\lambda}+\int_{\Theta}I_{P}(\theta)\lambda(\theta)d\theta,

then
C\geq D^{-1}
,
i.e.

C-D^{-1}
is semi positive definite. We give a proof in the appendix, with some comments explaining the differences with an ordinary Bayesian version of the Cramér- Rao inequality.

Proposition 6.1.

Let
U\sim\gamma_{p_{1},\sigma_{1}}
with
p_{1}-\frac{n+3}{2}>0.
and density
\lambda(u).
Let
I_{p}(\sigma)
as in (

Then there exists numbers
a=a(p,p_{1})
and
b=b(p,p_{1})
such that

\left(I_{\lambda}+\int_{E_{+}}I_{p}(u)\lambda(u)du)\right)^{-1}=a\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{1})+b\sigma_{1}\otimes\sigma_{1}.

Proof.
We begin with the calculation of the density information
I_{\lambda}.
We consider

\displaystyle g(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\lambda(u)=-\langle u,\sigma_{1}^{-1}\rangle+(p_{1}-\frac{n+1}{2})\log(\det u)+\mathrm{constant}

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sigma_{1}^{-1}+(p_{1}-\frac{n+1}{2})u^{-1},\ \ g^{\prime\prime}(u)=-(p_{1}-\frac{n+1}{2})\mathbb{P}(u^{-1}).

(6
.
21)

First we have to insure that we can use (
5.17
), by checking that if
u\to u_{0}\in\partial E_{+}
then
g^{\prime}(u)\lambda(u)
tends to 0. If
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
are the eigenvalues of
u
and
\lambda^{0}_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{0}_{n}
are the eigenvalues of
u_{0}
expressed in a decreasing order, we can claim that
\lambda_{i}\to_{u\to u_{0}}\lambda_{i}^{0}.
In particular
m=\min_{i}\lambda_{i}\to 0
and there exists
M>1
such that
\lambda_{i}<M
for all

and therefore
\det u<mM^{n}.
Furthermore the Frobenius norm of

\|u^{-1}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}^{2}}+\cdots+\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}^{2}}\leq\frac{n}{m^{2}}.

The fact that
p_{1}>\frac{n+3}{2}
implies that
p_{1}-\frac{n+1}{2}=1+\epsilon>1
. In particular
\lambda(u)\to_{u\to u_{0}}0
and

\|u^{-1}\|(\det u)^{p_{1}-\frac{n+1}{2}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{n}}{m}m^{1+\epsilon}M^{n(1+\epsilon)}\to_{u\to u_{0}}0.

Finally

\|g^{\prime}(u)\lambda(u)\|\leq\|\sigma_{1}^{-1}\|\lambda(u)+(p_{1}-\frac{n+1}{2})\|u^{-1}\|\lambda(u)\to_{u\to u_{0}}0.

In the sequel, to avoid unnecessarily complicated formulas we use the notation

5.17
) here and use (
6.20
) for computing

I_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{A_{3}A_{0}}\left(A_{1}\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{1}^{-1})+\frac{1}{2}\,\sigma_{1}^{-1}\otimes\sigma_{1}^{-1}\right).

\displaystyle\frac{2p+1}{2(2p+3)}\int_{E_{+}}\mathbb{P}(u^{-1})\gamma_{p_{1},\sigma_{1}}(du)-\frac{1}{2(2p+3)}\int_{E_{+}}(u^{-1}\otimes u^{-1})\gamma_{p_{1},\sigma_{1}}(du)

(6

\displaystyle\frac{2p+1}{2(2p+3)}\times\frac{1}{A_{3}A_{0}}P(\sigma_{1}^{-1})+\frac{2p+1}{2(2p+3)}\times\frac{1}{2A_{3}A_{1}A_{0}}(\sigma_{1}^{-1}\otimes\sigma_{1}^{-1})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2(2p+3)}\times\frac{1}{A_{3}A_{1}A_{0}}P(\sigma_{1}^{-1})-\frac{1}{2(2p+3)}\times\frac{A_{2}}{A_{3}A_{1}A_{0}}(\sigma_{1}^{-1}\otimes\sigma_{1}^{-1})

Appendix: proof of the Van Trees inequality

Proposition 7.1.
Let
(P_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
be a regular Fisher model on
\Omega
where
\Theta
is an open subset of the Euclidean space
E=\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Denote its Fisher information by
I(\theta).
Let
\lambda(\theta)
be a continuous probability density on
\overline{\Theta}
which is continuously differentiable and positive on
\Theta
, and which is zero on the boundary
\partial\Theta=\overline{\Theta}\setminus\Theta
and at infinity: that means that for any
\epsilon>0
there exists a compact subset
K_{\epsilon}
of
\Theta
such that
\lambda(\theta)\leq\epsilon
for
\theta\in\Theta\setminus K_{\epsilon}
. Denote its density information by
I_{\lambda}.
Let
\Omega\ni w\mapsto X(w)\in E
be an arbitrary estimator of
\theta.
Under these circumstances the following
2k
symmetric matrix

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\int_{\Omega\times\Theta}[(X(w)-\theta)\otimes(X(w)-\theta)]P_{\theta}(dw)\lambda(\theta)d\theta&I_{k}\\
I_{k}&I_{\lambda}+\int_{\Theta}I(\theta)\lambda(\theta)d\theta\end{array}\right]

(7
.
24)

is semi positive definite. In particular the following fact, called the
Van Trees inequality
holds: the
k
symmetric matrix

\int_{\Omega\times\Theta}[(X(w)-\theta)\otimes(X(w)-\theta)]P_{\theta}(dw)\lambda(\theta)d\theta-[I_{\lambda}+\int_{\Theta}I(\theta)\lambda(\theta)d\theta]^{-1}

(7
.
25)

is semi positive definite provided that the matrix
I_{\lambda}+\int_{\Theta}I(\theta)\lambda(\theta)d\theta
is invertible.

Proof.
Denote
P_{\theta}(dw)=e^{\ell_{w}(\theta)}\nu(dw)
as usual and write
\lambda(\theta)=e^{g(\theta)}.
The basic tool of the proof is Stokes’ theorem, which says that if
f
is a function on
\overline{\Theta}
valued in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
which is sufficiently regular and which is zero on
\partial\Theta
then

\int_{\Theta}f^{\prime}(\theta)d\theta=0.

We apply this principle to the two following functions

1.

f(\theta)=\lambda(\theta)e^{\ell_{w}(\theta)}
and thus
m=1.
Here

f^{\prime}(\theta)=(g^{\prime}(\theta)+\ell^{\prime}_{w}(\theta))\lambda(\theta)e^{\ell_{w}(\theta)}

(7
.
26)

2.

f(\theta)=\theta\lambda(\theta)e^{\ell_{w}(\theta)}
and thus
m=\dim E.
Here

f^{\prime}(\theta)=[I_{k}+\theta\otimes(g^{\prime}(\theta)+\ell^{\prime}_{w}(\theta))]\lambda(\theta)e^{\ell_{w}(\theta)}

(7
.
27)

From (
7.26
) and Stokes we get that
X(w)\otimes\int_{\Theta}(g^{\prime}(\theta)+\ell^{\prime}_{w}(\theta))\lambda(\theta)e^{\ell_{w}(\theta)}d\theta=0
and thus integrating with respect to
\nu(dw)
we have

\int_{\Omega\times\Theta}[(X(w)\otimes(g^{\prime}(\theta)+\ell^{\prime}_{w}(\theta))]\lambda(\theta)e^{\ell_{w}(\theta)}\nu(dw)d\theta=0.

(7
.
28)

From (
7.27
) and Stokes we get that

\int_{\Theta}[\theta\otimes(g^{\prime}(\theta)+\ell^{\prime}_{w}(\theta))]\lambda(\theta)e^{\ell_{w}(\theta)}d\theta=-I_{k}\int_{\Theta}\lambda(\theta)e^{\ell_{w}(\theta)}d\theta

and thus integrating with respect to
\nu(dw)
we have

\int_{\Omega\times\Theta}[\theta\otimes(g^{\prime}(\theta)+\ell^{\prime}_{w}(\theta))]\lambda(\theta)e^{\ell_{w}(\theta)}\nu(dw)d\theta=-I_{k}.

(7
.
29)

since
P(d\theta,dw)=\lambda(\theta)e^{\ell_{w}(\theta)}\nu(dw)d\theta
is a probability measure on
\Omega\times\Theta.

We now combine (
7.28
) and (
7.29
) to get finally

\int_{\Omega\times\Theta}[(X(w)-\theta)\otimes(g^{\prime}(\theta)+\ell^{\prime}_{w}(\theta))]\lambda(\theta)e^{\ell_{w}(\theta)}\nu(dw)d\theta=I_{k}.

Now consider the expectation under
P(d\theta,dw)
of the random variable

(\theta,w)\mapsto[(X(w)-\theta),(g^{\prime}(\theta)+\ell^{\prime}_{w}(\theta)]\otimes[(X(w)-\theta),(g^{\prime}(\theta)+\ell^{\prime}_{w}(\theta)]

which is valued in the set of semi positive definite matrices of order
2k.
This expectation is nothing but the matrix
(
7.24
) which is therefore semi positive definite also. To prove this, the only point left is to check that

\int_{\Omega\times\Theta}[(g^{\prime}(\theta)+\ell_{w}(\theta))\otimes(g^{\prime}(\theta)+\ell_{w}(\theta))]\lambda(\theta)e^{\ell_{w}(\theta)}\nu(dw)d\theta=I_{\lambda}+\int_{\Theta}I(\theta)\lambda(\theta)d\theta.

This comes from

\int_{\Omega}\ell^{\prime}_{w}(\theta)e^{\ell_{w}(\theta)}\nu(dw)=0\Rightarrow\int_{\Omega}[g^{\prime}(\theta)\otimes\ell^{\prime}_{w}(\theta)]e^{\ell_{w}(\theta)}\nu(dw)=0\Rightarrow\int_{\Omega\times\Theta}[g^{\prime}(\theta)\otimes\ell^{\prime}_{w}(\theta)]P(d\theta,dw)=0.

\square

Remarks.
Comparison with the Bayesian Cramér-Rao inequality is in order. This inequality says that if
X
is an unbiased estimator of
\theta
then

\int_{\Theta}(X-\theta)\otimes(X-\theta))\lambda(\theta)d\theta-\left(\int_{\Theta}I(\theta)\lambda(\theta)d\theta)\right)^{-1}

(7
.
30)

is semi positive definite.

The Van Trees inequality is about possibly
biased estimators
while the inequality (
7.30
) is about the unbiased case. Applying the Van Trees inequality to the unbiased case provides just a weaker result since saying by (
7.30
) that

A=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\int_{\Omega\times\Theta}[(X(w)-\theta)\otimes(X(w)-\theta)]P_{\theta}(dw)\lambda(\theta)d\theta&I_{k}\\
I_{k}&\int_{\Theta}I(\theta)\lambda(\theta)d\theta\end{array}\right]

is semi positive definite is stronger than saying that

A+\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
0&I_{\lambda}\end{array}\right]
is semi positive definite.

On the other hand, introduce the expectation
\psi(\theta)
of the biased estimator
X
of
\theta
. Consider the following semi positive definite matrix of order
k

C=\int_{\Theta}[(\psi(\theta)-\theta)\otimes(\psi(\theta)-\theta)]\lambda(\theta)d\theta.

Thus we have

\int_{\Omega\times\Theta}[(X(w)-\theta)\otimes(X(w)-\theta)]P_{\theta}(dw)\lambda(\theta)d\theta=C+\int_{\Theta}\mathrm{Cov}_{\theta}(X)\lambda(\theta)d\theta.

The general Bayesian Cramér-Rao inequality says that the matrix

B=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\int_{\Theta}\mathrm{Cov}_{\theta}(X)\lambda(\theta)d\theta&\int_{\Theta}\psi^{\prime}(\theta)\lambda(\theta)d\theta\\
\int_{\Theta}\psi^{\prime}(\theta)^{t}\lambda(\theta)d\theta&\int_{\Theta}I(\theta)\lambda(\theta)d\theta\end{array}\right]

is semi positive definite
and thus trivially if
B_{1}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}C&0\\
0&I_{\lambda}\end{array}\right]
then

B+B_{1}
is semi positive definite. The Van Trees inequality rather says that if we consider

B_{2}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}C&I_{k}-\int_{\Theta}\psi^{\prime}(\theta)\lambda(\theta)d\theta\\
I_{k}-\int_{\Theta}\psi^{\prime}(\theta)^{t}\lambda(\theta)d\theta&I_{\lambda}\end{array}\right]

then

B+B_{2}
is semi positive definite.
This fact is not trivial
since
B_{2}
is not positive definite in general.
