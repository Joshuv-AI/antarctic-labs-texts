Understanding Deep Contrastive Learning via Coordinate-wise Optimization
Yuandong Tian

Abstract
We show that Contrastive Learning (CL) under a broad family of loss functions (including InfoNCE) has a unified formulation of coordinate-wise optimization on the network parameter 𝜽 \bm{\theta} and pairwise importance α \alpha , where the max player 𝜽 \bm{\theta} learns representation for contrastiveness, and the min player α \alpha puts more weights on pairs of distinct samples that share similar representations. The resulting formulation, called 𝜶 \bm{\alpha} -CL , unifies not only various existing contrastive losses, which differ by how sample-pair importance α \alpha is constructed, but also is able to extrapolate to give novel contrastive losses beyond popular ones, opening a new avenue of contrastive loss design. These novel losses yield comparable (or better) performance on CIFAR10, STL-10 and CIFAR-100 than classic InfoNCE. Furthermore, we also analyze the max player in detail: we prove that with fixed α \alpha , max player is equivalent to Principal Component Analysis (PCA) for deep linear network, and almost all local minima are global and rank-1, recovering optimal PCA solutions. Finally, we extend our analysis on max player to 2-layer ReLU networks, showing that its fixed points can have higher ranks. Introduction

While contrastive self-supervised learning has been shown to learn good features
(
Chen et al. 2020
;
He et al. 2020
;
Oord et al. 2018
)
and in many cases, comparable with features learned from supervised learning, it remains an open problem what features it learns, in particular when deep nonlinear networks are used. Theory on this is quite sparse, mostly focusing on loss function
(
Arora et al. 2019
)
and treating the networks as a black-box function approximator.

In this paper, we present a novel perspective of contrastive learning (CL) for a broad family of contrastive loss functions
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})
: minimizing
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})
corresponds to a
coordinate-wise optimization
procedure on an objective
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})-\mathcal{R}(\alpha)
with respect to network parameter
\bm{\theta}
and
pairwise importance

\alpha
on batch samples, where
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})
is an energy function and
\mathcal{R}(\alpha)
is a regularizer, both associated with the original contrastive loss
\mathcal{L}
. In this view, the
max player

\bm{\theta}
learns a representation to maximize the contrastiveness of different samples and keep different augmentation view of the same sample similar, while the
min player

\alpha
puts more weights on pairs of different samples that appear similar in the representation space, subject to regularization. Empirically, this formulation, named P
a
ir-weighed
C
ontrastive
L
earning (
\bm{\alpha}
-CL
), when coupled with various regularization terms, yields novel contrastive losses that show comparable (or better) performance in CIFAR10
(
Krizhevsky et al. 2009
)
and STL-10
(
Coates et al. 2011
)
.

We then focus on the behavior of the max player who does
representation learning
via maximizing the energy function
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})
. When the underlying network is deep linear, we show that
\max_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})
is the loss function (under re-parameterization) of Principal Component Analysis (PCA)
(
Wold et al. 1987
)
, a century-old unsupervised dimension reduction method. To further show they are equivalent, we prove that the nonlinear training dynamics of CL with a linear multi-layer feedforward network (MLP) enjoys nice properties: with proper weight normalization, almost all its local optima are global, achieving optimal PCA objective, and are rank-1. The only difference here is that the data augmentation provides negative eigen-directions to avoid.

Furthermore, we extend our analysis to 2-layer ReLU network, to explore the difference between the rank-1 PCA solution and the solution learned by a nonlinear network. Assuming the data follow an orthogonal mixture model, the 2-layer ReLU networks enjoy similar dynamics as the linear one, except for a special
sticky weight rule
that keeps the low-layer weights to be non-negative and stays zero when touching zero. In the case of one hidden node, we prove that the solution in ReLU always picks a single mode from the mixtures. In the case of multiple hidden nodes, the resulting solution is not necessarily rank-1.

3
Contrastive Learning as Coordinate-wise Optimization

Notation
. Suppose we have
N
pairs of samples
\{{\bm{x}}[i]\}_{i=1}^{N}
and
\{{\bm{x}}[i^{\prime}]\}_{i=1}^{N}
. Both
{\bm{x}}[i]
and
{\bm{x}}[i^{\prime}]
are augmented samples from sample
i
and
{\bm{x}}
represents the input batch. These samples are sent to neural networks and
{\bm{z}}[i]
and
{\bm{z}}[i^{\prime}]
are their outputs. The goal of contrastive learning (CL) is to find the representation to maximize the squared distance
d^{2}_{ij}:=\|{\bm{z}}[i]-{\bm{z}}[j]\|_{2}^{2}/2
between distinct samples
i
and
j
, and minimize the squared distance
d^{2}_{i}:=\|{\bm{z}}[i]-{\bm{z}}[i^{\prime}]\|_{2}^{2}/2
between different data augmentations
{\bm{x}}[i]
and
{\bm{x}}[i^{\prime}]
of the same sample
i
.

3.1
A general family of contrastive loss

We consider minimizing a general family of loss functions
\mathcal{L}_{\phi,\psi}
, where
\phi
and
\psi
are monotonously increasing and differentiable scalar functions (define
\xi_{i}:=\sum_{j\neq i}\psi(d^{2}_{i}-d^{2}_{ij})
for notation brevity):

\min_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\phi,\psi}(\bm{\theta}):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi(\xi_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi\left(\sum_{j\neq i}\psi(d^{2}_{i}-d^{2}_{ij})\right)

(1)

Both
i
and
j
run from
1
to
N
. With different
\phi
and
\psi
, Eqn.
1
covers many loss functions (Tbl.
1
). In particular, setting
\phi(x)=\tau\log(\epsilon+x)
and
\psi(x)=\exp(x/\tau)
gives a generalized version of InfoNCE loss
(
Oord et al. 2018
)
:

\!\!\mathcal{L}_{nce}\!:=\!-\tau\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\frac{\exp(-d^{2}_{i}/\tau)}{\epsilon\exp(-d^{2}_{i}/\tau)\!+\!\sum_{j\neq i}\exp(-d^{2}_{ij}/\tau)}=\tau\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\left(\epsilon\!+\!\sum_{j\neq i}e^{\frac{d_{i}^{2}-d^{2}_{ij}}{\tau}}\right)

(2)

where
\epsilon>0
is some constant not related to
{\bm{z}}[i]
and
{\bm{z}}[i^{\prime}]
.
\epsilon=1
has been used in many works
(
He et al. 2020
;
Tian et al. 2020a
)
. Setting
\epsilon=0
yields SimCLR setting
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
where the denominator doesn’t contains
\exp(-d^{2}_{i}/\tau)
. This is also used in
(
Yeh et al. 2021
)
.

3.2
The other side of gradient descent of contrastive loss

To minimize
\mathcal{L}_{\phi,\psi}
, gradient descent follows its negative gradient direction. As a first discovery of this work, it turns out that the gradient descent of the loss function
\mathcal{L}
is the
gradient ascent
direction of another energy function
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
:

Theorem 1
.

For any differential mapping
{\bm{z}}={\bm{z}}({\bm{x}};\bm{\theta})
, gradient descent of
\mathcal{L}_{\phi,\psi}
is equivalent to
gradient ascent
of the objective
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}):=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}(\mathbb{C}_{\alpha}[{\bm{z}}(\bm{\theta}),{\bm{z}}(\bm{\theta})])
:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\phi,\psi}}{\partial\bm{\theta}}=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}}{\partial\bm{\theta}}\Big|_{\alpha=\alpha(\bm{\theta})}

(3)

Here the
pairwise importance

\alpha=\alpha(\bm{\theta}):=\{\alpha_{ij}(\bm{\theta})\}
is a function of input batch
{\bm{x}}
, defined as:

\alpha_{ij}(\bm{\theta}):=\phi^{\prime}(\xi_{i})\psi^{\prime}(d^{2}_{i}-d^{2}_{ij})\geq 0

(4)

where
\phi^{\prime},\psi^{\prime}\geq 0
are derivatives of
\phi,\psi
. The
contrastive covariance

\mathbb{C}_{\alpha}[\cdot,\cdot]
is defined as:

\mathbb{C}_{\alpha}[{\bm{a}},{\bm{b}}]:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{ij}({\bm{a}}[i]-{\bm{a}}[j])({\bm{b}}[i]-{\bm{b}}[j])^{\top}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{ij}\right)({\bm{a}}[i]-{\bm{a}}[i^{\prime}])({\bm{b}}[i]-{\bm{b}}[i^{\prime}])^{\top}

(5)

That is,
minimizing
the loss function
\mathcal{L}_{\phi,\psi}(\bm{\theta})
can be regarded as
maximizing
the energy function
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha=\mathrm{sg}(\alpha(\bm{\theta}))}(\bm{\theta})
with respect to
\bm{\theta}
. Here
\mathrm{sg}(\cdot)
means stop-gradient, i.e., the gradient of
\bm{\theta}
is not backpropagated into
\alpha(\bm{\theta})
.

Please check Supplementary Materials (SM) for all proofs. From the definition of energy
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})
, it is clear that
\alpha_{ij}
determines the importance of each sample pair
{\bm{x}}[i]
and
{\bm{x}}[j]
. For
(i,j)
-pair that “deserves attention”,
\alpha_{ij}
is large so that it plays a large role in the contrastive covariance term. In particular, for InfoNCE loss with
\epsilon=0
, the pairwise importance
\alpha
takes the following form:

\alpha_{ij}=\frac{\exp(-d^{2}_{ij}/\tau)}{\sum_{j\neq i}\exp(-d^{2}_{ij}/\tau)}>0

(6)

which means that InfoNCE focuses on
(i,j)
-pair with small squared distance
d^{2}_{ij}
. If both
\phi
and
\psi
are linear, then
\alpha_{ij}=\mathrm{const}
and
\mathcal{L}
is a simple subtraction of positive/negative squared distances.

From Thm.
1
, an important observation is that when propagating gradient w.r.t.
\bm{\theta}
using the objective
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})
during the backward pass, the gradient does not propagate into
\alpha(\bm{\theta})
, even if
\alpha(\bm{\theta})
is a function of
\bm{\theta}
in the forward pass. In fact, in Sec.
6
we show that propagating gradient through
\alpha(\bm{\theta})
yields worse empirical performance. This suggests that
\alpha
should be treated as an
independent
variable when optimizing
\bm{\theta}
. It turns out that if
\psi(x)
is an exponential function (as in most cases of Tbl.
1
), this is indeed true and
\alpha
can be determined by a separate optimization procedure:

Theorem 2
.

If
\psi(x)=e^{x/\tau}
, then the corresponding pairwise importance
\alpha
(Eqn.
4
) is the solution to the minimization problem:

\alpha(\bm{\theta})=\arg\min_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})-\mathcal{R}(\alpha),\quad\quad\mathcal{A}:=\left\{\alpha:\quad\forall i,\ \ \sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{ij}=\tau^{-1}\xi_{i}\phi^{\prime}(\xi_{i}),\ \ \alpha_{ij}\geq 0\right\}

(7)

Here the regularization
\mathcal{R}(\alpha)=\mathcal{R}_{H}(\alpha):=\tau\sum_{i=1}^{N}H(\alpha_{i\cdot})=-\tau\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{ij}\log\alpha_{ij}
.

For InfoNCE, the feasible set
\mathcal{A}
becomes
\{\alpha:\alpha\geq 0,\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{ij}=\xi_{i}/(\xi_{i}+\epsilon)\}
. This means that if
i
-th sample is already well-separated (small intra-augmentation distance
d_{i}
and large inter-augmentation distance
d_{ij}
), then
\xi_{i}
is small, the summation of weights
\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{ij}
associated with sample
i
is also small and such a sample is overall discounted. Setting
\epsilon=0
reduces to sample-agnostic constraint (i.e.,
\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{ij}=1
).

Thm.
2
leads to a novel perspective of
coordinate-wise optimization
for Contrastive Learning (CL):

Corollary 1

(Contrastive Learning as Coordinate-wise Optimization)
.

If
\psi(x)=e^{x/\tau}
, minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\phi,\psi}
is equivalent to the following iterative procedure:

\displaystyle\text{(Min-player $\alpha$)}\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\alpha_{t}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}_{t})-\mathcal{R}(\alpha)

(8a)

\displaystyle\text{(Max-player $\bm{\theta}$)}\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\bm{\theta}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\bm{\theta}_{t}+\eta\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{E}_{\alpha_{t}}(\bm{\theta})

(8b)

Intuitively, the max player
\bm{\theta}
(Eqn.
8b
) performs one-step gradient ascent for the objective
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})-\mathcal{R}(\alpha)
,
learns a representation
to maximize the distance of different samples and minimize the distance of the same sample with different augmentations (as suggested by
\mathbb{C}_{\alpha}[{\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}]
). On the other hand, the “min player”
\alpha
(Eqn.
8a
) finds optimal
\alpha
analytically, assigning high weights on confusing pairs for “max player” to solve.

Relation to max-min formulation
. While Corollary
1
looks very similar to max-min formulation, important differences exist. Different from traditional max-min formulation, in Corollary
1
there is asymmetry between
\bm{\theta}
and
\alpha
. First,
\bm{\theta}
only follows one step update along gradient ascent direction of
\max_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})
, while
\alpha
is solved analytically. Second, due to the stop-gradient operator, the gradient of
\bm{\theta}
contains no knowledge on how
\bm{\theta}
changes
\alpha
. This prevents
\bm{\theta}
from adapting to
\alpha
’s response on changing
\bm{\theta}
. Both give advantages to min-player
\alpha
to find the confusing sample pairs more effectively.

Relation to hard-negative samples
. While many previous works
(
Kalantidis et al. 2020
;
Robinson et al. 2021
)
focus on seeking and putting more weights on hard samples, Corollary
1
shows that contrastive losses already have such mechanism at the batch level, focusing on “hard-negative pairs” beyond hard-negative samples.

From this formulation, different pairwise importance
\alpha
corresponds to different loss functions within the loss family specified by Eqn.
1
, and choosing among this family (i.e., different
\phi
and
\psi
) can be regarded as choosing different
\alpha
when optimizing the
same
objective
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})
. Based on this observation, we now propose the following training framework called
\bm{\alpha}
-CL
:

Definition 1

(P
a
ir-weighed
C
ontrastive
L
earning (
\alpha
-CL))
.

Optimize
\bm{\theta}
by gradient ascent:
\bm{\theta}_{t+1}=\bm{\theta}_{t}+\eta\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{sg}(\alpha_{t})}(\bm{\theta})
, with the energy
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})
defined in Thm.
1
and pairwise importance
\alpha_{t}=\alpha(\bm{\theta}_{t})
.

In
\alpha
-CL, choosing
\alpha
can be achieved by either implicitly specifying a regularizer
\mathcal{R}(\alpha)
and solve Eqn.
8a
, or by a direct mapping
\alpha=\alpha(\bm{\theta})
without any optimization. This opens a novel revenue for CL loss design. Initial experiments (Sec.
6
) show that
\alpha
-CL gives comparable (or even better) downstream performance in CIFAR10 and STL-10, compared to vanilla InfoNCE loss.

4
Representation Learning in Deep Linear CL is PCA

In Corollary
1
, optimizing over
\alpha
is well-understood, since
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})
is
linear
w.r.t.
\alpha
and
\mathcal{R}(\alpha)
in general is a (strong) concave function. As a result,
\alpha
has a unique optimal. On the other hand, understanding the max player
\max_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})
is important since it performs
representation learning
in CL. It is also a hard problem because of non-convex optimization.

We start with a specific case when
{\bm{z}}
is a deep linear network, i.e.,
{\bm{z}}=W(\bm{\theta}){\bm{x}}
, where
W
is the equivalent linear mapping for the deep linear network, and
\bm{\theta}
is the parameters to be optimized. Note that this covers many different kinds of deep linear networks, including VGG-like
(
Saxe et al. 2014
)
, ResNet-like
(
Hardt & Ma 2017
)
and DenseNet-like
(
Huang et al. 2017
)
. For notation brevity, we define
\mathbb{C}_{\alpha}[{\bm{x}}]:=\mathbb{C}_{\alpha}[{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}]
.

Corollary 2

(Representation learning in Deep Linear CL reparameterizes Principal Component Analysis (PCA))
.

When
{\bm{z}}=W(\bm{\theta}){\bm{x}}
with a constraint
WW^{\top}=I
,
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
is the objective of Principal Component Analysis (PCA) with reparameterization
W=W(\bm{\theta})
:

\max_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}(W(\bm{\theta})X_{\alpha}W^{\top}(\bm{\theta}))\quad\mathrm{s.t.\ }WW^{\top}=I

(9)

here
X_{\alpha}:=\mathbb{C}_{\alpha}[{\bm{x}}]
is the contrastive covariance of input
{\bm{x}}
.

As a comparison, in traditional Principal Component Analysis, the objective is
(
Kokiopoulou et al. 2011
)
:
\frac{1}{2}\max_{W}\mathrm{tr}(W\mathbb{V}_{\mathrm{sample}}[{\bm{x}}]W^{\top})
subject to the constraint
WW^{\top}=I
, where
\mathbb{V}_{\mathrm{sample}}[{\bm{x}}]
is the empirical covariance of the dataset (here it is one batch). Therefore,
X_{\alpha}
can be regarded as a generalized covariance matrix, possibly containing negative eigenvalues. In the case of supervised CL (i.e,. pairs from the same/different labels are treated as positive/negative
(
Khosla et al. 2020
)
), then it is connected with Fisher’s Linear Discriminant Analysis
(
Fisher 1936
)
.

Here we show a mathematically rigorous connection between CL and dimensional reduction, as suggested intuitively in
(
Hadsell et al. 2006
)
. Unlike traditional PCA, due to the presence of data augmentation, while symmetric, the contrastive covariance
X_{\alpha}
is not necessarily a PSD matrix. Nevertheless, the intuition is the same: to find the direction that corresponds to maximal variation of the data.

Figure 2:
Dynamics of CL with multilayer (
L=5
) linear network (
DeepLin
) with fixed
\alpha
. Running the training dynamics (Lemma
1
) quickly leads to convergence towards the maximal eigenvalue of
X_{\alpha}
. For dynamics of singular value of
W_{l}
, the largest singular values (solid lines) converges to 1 while the second largest singular values (dashed lines) decay to
0
.

While it is interesting to discover that CL with deep linear network is essentially a reparameterization of PCA, it remains elusive that such a reparameterization leads to the same solution of PCA, in particular when the network is deep (and may contain local optima). Also, PCA has an overall end-to-end constraint
WW^{\top}=I
, while in network training, we instead use normalization layers and it is unclear whether they are equivalent or not.

In this section, we show for a specific deep linear model, almost all its local maxima of Eqn.
9
are global and it indeed solves PCA.

4.1
A concrete deep linear model

We study a concrete deep linear network with parameters/weights
\bm{\theta}:=\{W_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
:

{\bm{z}}[i]:=W_{L}W_{L-1}\ldots W_{1}{\bm{x}}[i]

(10)

Here
W_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}\times n_{l-1}}
,
n_{l}
is the number of nodes at layer
l
,
{\bm{z}}[i]
is the output of
{\bm{x}}[i]
and similarly
{\bm{z}}[i^{\prime}]
for
{\bm{x}}[i^{\prime}]
. We use
\bm{\theta}
to represent the collection of weights at all layers. For convenience, we define the
l
-th layer activation
{\bm{f}}_{l}[i]=W_{l}{\bm{f}}_{l-1}[i]
. With this notation
{\bm{f}}_{0}[i]={\bm{x}}[i]
is the input and
{\bm{z}}[i]=W_{L}{\bm{f}}_{L-1}[i]
.

We call this setting
DeepLin
. The Jacobian matrix
W_{>l}:=W_{L}W_{L-1}\ldots W_{l+1}
and
W:=W_{>0}=W_{L}W_{L-1}\ldots W_{1}
.

Lemma 1
.

The training dynamics in
DeepLin
is
\dot{W}_{l}=W_{>l}^{\top}W_{>l}W_{l}\mathbb{C}_{\alpha}[{\bm{f}}_{l-1}]

Note that
\mathbb{C}_{\alpha}[{\bm{f}}_{0}]=\mathbb{C}_{\alpha}[{\bm{x}}]=X_{\alpha}
. Similar to supervised learning
(
Arora et al. 2018
;
Du et al. 2018b
)
, nearby layers are also balanced:
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(W_{l}W_{l}^{\top}-W_{l+1}^{\top}W_{l+1}\right)=0
.

4.2
Normalization Constraints

Note that if we just run the training dynamics (Lemma
1
) without any constraints,
\|W_{l}\|_{F}
will go to infinity. Fortunately, empirical works already suggest various ways of normalization to stabilize the network training.

One popular technique in CL is
\ell_{2}
normalization. It is often put right after the output of the network and before the loss function
\mathcal{L}

(
Chen et al. 2020
;
Grill et al. 2020
;
He et al. 2020
)
, i.e.,
\hat{\bm{z}}[i]={\bm{z}}[i]/\|{\bm{z}}[i]\|_{2}
. Besides, LayerNorm
(
Ba et al. 2016
)
(i.e.,
\hat{\bm{f}}[i]=({\bm{f}}[i]-\mathrm{mean}({\bm{f}}[i]))/\mathrm{std}({\bm{f}}[i])
) is extensively used in Transformer-based models
(
Xiong et al. 2020
)
. Here we show that for gradient flow dynamics of MLP models, such normalization layers conserve
\|W_{l}\|_{F}
for any
l
below it, regardless of loss function.

Lemma 2
.

For MLP, if the weight
W_{l}
is below a
\ell_{2}
-norm or LayerNorm layer, then
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|W_{l}\|^{2}_{F}=0
.

Note that Lemma
2
also holds for nonlinear MLP with reversible activations, which includes ReLU (see SM). Therefore, without loss of generality, we consider the following complete objective for max player with
DeepLin
(here
\Theta
is the constraint set of the weights due to normalization):

\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}):=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}(WX_{\alpha}W^{\top}),\quad\Theta:=\{\bm{\theta}:\ \ \|W_{l}\|_{F}=1,1\leq l\leq L\}

(11)

4.3
Representation Learning with
DeepLin
is PCA

As one of our main contributions, the following theorem asserts that almost all local optimal solutions of Eqn.
11
are global, and the optimal objective corresponds to the PCA objective. Note that
(
Kawaguchi 2016
;
Laurent & Brecht 2018
)
proves no bad local optima for deep linear network in supervised learning, while here we give similar results for CL, and additionally we also give the (simple) rank-1 structure of all local optima.

Theorem 3

(Representation Learning with
DeepLin
is PCA)
.

If
\lambda_{\max}(X_{\alpha})>0
, then for any local maximum
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
of Eqn.
11
whose
W^{\top}_{>1}W_{>1}
has distinct maximal eigenvalue:

•

there exists a set of unit vectors
\{{\bm{v}}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L}
so that
W_{l}={\bm{v}}_{l}{\bm{v}}_{l-1}^{\top}
for
1\leq l\leq L
, in particular,
{\bm{v}}_{0}
is the unit eigenvector corresponding to
\lambda_{\max}(X_{\alpha})
,

•

\bm{\theta}
is global optimal with objective
2\mathcal{E}^{*}=\lambda_{\max}(X_{\alpha})
.

Corollary 3
.

If we additionally use per-filter normalization (i.e.,
\|{\bm{w}}_{lk}\|_{2}=1/\sqrt{n_{l}}
), then Thm.
3
holds and
{\bm{v}}_{l}
is more constrained:
[{\bm{v}}_{l}]_{k}=\pm 1/\sqrt{n_{l}}
for
1\leq l\leq L-1
.

Remark
. Here we prove that given fixed
\alpha
, maximizing
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})
gives rank-1 solutions for deep linear network. This conclusion is an extension of
(
Jing et al. 2022
)
, which shows weight collapsing happens if
\bm{\theta}
is 2-layer linear network and
\alpha
is fixed. If the pairwise importance
\alpha
is adversarial, then it may not lead to a rank-1 solution. In fact,
\alpha
can magnify minimal eigen-directions and change the eigenstructure of
X_{\alpha}
continuously. We leave it for future work.

Note that the condition that “
W^{\top}_{>1}W_{>1}
has distinct maximal eigenvalue” is important. Otherwise there are counterexamples. For example, consider 1-layer linear network
{\bm{z}}=W_{1}{\bm{x}}
, and
X_{\alpha}
has duplicated maximal eigenvalues (with
{\bm{u}}_{1}
and
{\bm{u}}_{2}
being corresponding orthogonal eigenvectors), then
W_{>1}^{\top}W_{>1}=I
(i.e., it has degenerated eigenvalues), and for any local maximal
W_{1}
, its row vector can be arbitrary linear combinations of
{\bm{u}}_{1}
and
{\bm{u}}_{2}
and thus
W_{1}
is not rank-1.

Compared to recent works
(
Ji et al. 2021
)
that also relates CL with PCA in linear representation setting using constant
\alpha
, our Theorem
3
has no statistical assumptions on data distribution and augmentation, and operates on vanilla InfoNCE loss and deep architectures.

5
How Representation Learning Differs in Two-layer ReLU Network

So far we have shown that the max player
\max_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}):=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}(\mathbb{C}_{\alpha}[{\bm{z}}(\bm{\theta})])
is essentially a PCA objective when the input-output mapping
{\bm{z}}=W(\bm{\theta}){\bm{x}}
is linear. A natural question arises. What is the benefit of CL if its representation learning component has such a simple nature? Why can it learn a good representation in practice beyond PCA?

For this, nonlinearity is the key but understanding its role is highly nontrivial. For example, when the neural network model is nonlinear, Thm.
1
and Corollary
1
holds but
not
Corollary
2
. Therefore, there is not even a well-defined
X_{\alpha}
due to the fact that multiple hidden nodes can be switched on/off given different data input. Previous works
(
Safran & Shamir 2018
;
Du et al. 2018a
)
also show that with nonlinearity, in supervised learning spurious local optima exist.

Here we take a first step to analyze nonlinear cases. We study 2-layer models with ReLU activation
h(x)=\max(x,0)
. We show that with a proper data assumption, the 2-layer model shares a
modified
version of dynamics with its linear version, and the contrastive covariance term
X_{\alpha}
(and its eigenstructure) remains well-defined and useful in nonlinear case.

5.1
The 2-layer ReLU network and data model

We consider the bottom-layer weight
W_{1}=[{\bm{w}}_{11},{\bm{w}}_{12},\ldots,{\bm{w}}_{1K}]^{\top}
with
{\bm{w}}_{1k}
being the
k
-th filter. For brevity, let
K=n_{1}
be the number of hidden nodes.
We still consider solution in the constraint set
\Theta
(Eqn.
11
), since Lemma
2
still holds for ReLU networks. This model is named
ReLU2Layer
.

In addition, we assume the following data model:

Assumption 1

(Orthogonal mixture model within receptive field
R_{k}
)
.

There exists a set of orthonormal bases
\{\bar{\bm{x}}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
so that any input data
{\bm{x}}[i]=\sum_{m}a_{m}[i]\bar{\bm{x}}_{m}
satisfies the property that
a_{m}[i]
is
Nonnegative
:
a_{m}[i]\geq 0
,
One-hot
: for any
k
,
a_{m}[i]>0
for at most one
m
and
Augmentation
only scales
{\bm{x}}_{k}
by a (sample-dependent) factor, i.e.,
{\bm{x}}[i^{\prime}]=\gamma[i]{\bm{x}}[i]
with
\gamma[i]>0
.

Since all
{\bm{x}}
appears in the inner-product with the weight vectors
{\bm{w}}_{1k}
, with a rotation of coordination, we can just set
\bar{\bm{x}}_{m}={\bm{e}}_{m}
, where
{\bm{e}}_{m}
is the one-hot vector with
m
-th component being 1. In this case,
{\bm{x}}\geq 0
is always a one-hot vector with only at most only one positive entry.

Intuitively, the model is motivated by sparsity: in each instantiation of
{\bm{x}}
, there are very small number of activated modes and their linear combination becomes the input signal
{\bm{x}}
. As we shall see, even with this simple model, the dynamics of ReLU network behaves very differently from the linear case.

With this assumption, we only need to consider nonnegative low-layer weights and
X_{\alpha}
is still a valid quantity for
ReLU2Layer
:

Lemma 3

(Evaluation of
ReLU2Layer
)
.

If Assumption
1
holds, setting
{\bm{w}}^{\prime}_{1k}=\max({\bm{w}}_{1k},0)
won’t change the output of
ReLU2Layer
. Furthermore, if
W_{1}\geq 0
, then the formula for linear network
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}(W_{2}W_{1}X_{\alpha}W_{1}^{\top}W_{2}^{\top})
still works for
ReLU2Layer
.

On the other hand, sharing the energy function
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
does not mean
ReLU2Layer
is completely identical to its linear version. In fact, the dynamics follows its linear counterparts, but with important modifications:

Theorem 4

(Dynamics of
ReLU2Layer
)
.

If Assumption
1
holds, then the dynamics of
ReLU2Layer
with
{\bm{w}}_{1k}\geq 0
is equivalent to linear dynamics with the
Sticky Weight rule
: any component that reaches 0 stays 0.

As we will see, this modification leads to very different dynamics and local optima in
ReLU2Layer
from linear cases, even when there is only one ReLU node.

5.2
Dynamics in One ReLU node

Now we consider the dynamics of the simplest case:
ReLU2Layer
with only 1 hidden node. In this case,
W^{\top}_{>1}W_{>1}
is a scalar and thus
W_{2}^{\top}W_{2}=\mathrm{tr}(W_{2}^{\top}W_{2})=1
. We only need to consider
{\bm{w}}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
, which is the only weight vector in the lower layer, under the constraint
\|W_{1}\|_{F}=\|{\bm{w}}_{1}\|_{2}=1
(Eqn.
11
). We denote this setting as
ReLU2Layer1Hid
.

The dynamics now becomes very different from linear setting. Under linear network, according to Theorem
3
,
{\bm{w}}_{1}
converges to the largest eigenvector of
X_{\alpha}=\mathbb{C}_{\alpha}[{\bm{x}}_{1}]
. For
ReLU2Layer1Hid
, situation differs drastically:

Theorem 5
.

If Assumption
1
holds, then in
ReLU2Layer1Hid
,
{\bm{w}}_{1}\rightarrow{\bm{e}}_{m}
for certain
m
.

Intuitively, this theorem is achieved by closely tracing the dynamics. When the number of positive entries of
{\bm{w}}_{1}
is more than 1, the linear dynamics always hits the boundary of the polytope
{\bm{w}}_{1}\geq 0
, making one of its entry be zero, and stick to zero due to sticky weight rule. This procedure repeats until there is only one survival positive entry in
{\bm{w}}_{1}
.

Overall, this simple case already shows that nonlinear landscape can lead to many local optima: for any
m
,
{\bm{w}}_{1}={\bm{e}}_{m}
is one local optimal. Which one the training falls into depends on weight initialization, and critically affects the properties of per-trained models.

Figure 3:
Theorem
6
shows that training
ReLU2Layer
could lead to more diverse hidden weight patterns beyond rank-1 solution obtained in the linear case (shown in right two figures: converged
W_{1}
and
W_{2}^{\top}W_{2}
).

5.3
Multiple hidden nodes

For complicated situations like multiple hidden units, completely characterizing the training dynamics like Theorem
5
becomes hard (if not impossible). Instead, we focus on fixed point analysis.

For deep linear model, using multiple hidden units does not lead to any better solutions. According to Thm.
3
, at local optimal,
W_{1}={\bm{v}}_{1}{\bm{v}}^{\top}_{0}
. This means that the weights
{\bm{w}}_{1k}
, which are row vectors of
W_{1}
, are just a scaled version of the maximal eigenvector
{\bm{v}}_{0}
of
X_{\alpha}
. Moreover, this is independent of the eigenstructure of
X_{\alpha}
as long as
\lambda_{\max}(X_{\alpha})>0
.

In
ReLU2Layer
, the situation is a bit different. Thm.
6
shows that these hidden nodes are (slightly) more diverse. Fig.
3
shows one such example. The intuition here is that in nonlinear case, rank-1 structure of the critical points may be replaced with low-rank structures.

Theorem 6

(
ReLU2Layer
encourages diversity)
.

If Assumption
1
holds, then for any local optimal
(W_{2},W_{1})\in\Theta
of
ReLU2Layer
with
\mathcal{E}>0
, either
W_{1}={\bm{v}}{\bm{e}}_{m}^{\top}
for some
m
and
{\bm{v}}\geq 0
, or
\mathrm{rank}(W_{1})>1
.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

We provide a novel perspective of contrastive learning (CL) via the lens of coordinate-wise optimization and propose a unified framework called
\alpha
-CL that not only covers a broad family of loss functions including InfoNCE, but also allows a direct set of importance of sample pairs. Preliminary experiments on CIFAR10/STL-10/CIFAR100 show comparable/better performance with the new loss than InfoNCE. Furthermore, we prove that with deep linear networks, the representation learning part is equivalent to Principal Component Analysis (PCA). In addition, we also extend our analysis to representation learning in 2-layer ReLU network, shedding light on the important difference in representation learning for linear/nonlinear cases.

Future work
. Our framework
\alpha
-CL turns various loss functions into a unified framework with different choices of pairwise importance
\alpha
and how to find good choices remains open. Also, we mainly focus on representation learning with fixed pairwise importance
\alpha
. However, in the actual training,
\alpha
and
\bm{\theta}
change concurrently. Understanding their interactions is an important next step. Finally, removing Assumption
1
in ReLU analysis is also an open problem to be addressed later.

Definition 4

A solution

Proof.

A necessary condition for

to be the local maximum is the critical point condition (here
\lambda_{l-1}
is some constant):

W^{\top}_{>l}W_{>l}W_{l}F_{l-1}=\lambda_{l-1}W_{l}

Right multiplying
W_{l}
on both sides of the critical point condition for

Therefore, all

are the same, denoted as

Now let’s consider

Applying
\mathrm{vec}(AXB)=(B^{\top}\otimes A)\mathrm{vec}(X)
, we have:

(X\otimes W^{\top}_{>1}W_{>1})\mathrm{vec}(W_{1})=\lambda\mathrm{vec}(W_{1})

with the constraint that

. Similarly, we have

.

We then prove that
\lambda
is the largest eigenvalue of

. We prove by contradiction. If not, then
\mathrm{vec}(W_{1})
is not the largest eigenvector, then there is always a direction
W_{1}
can move, while respecting the constraint
\|W_{1}\|_{F}=1
and keeping

fixed, to make

strictly larger. Therefore, for any local maximum

,

has to be the largest eigenvalue of

.

Let
\{{\bm{v}}_{0m}\}
be the orthonormal basis of the eigenspace of
\lambda_{\max}(X)
and
{\bm{u}}
be the (unique by the assumption) maximal unit eigenvector of
W^{\top}_{>1}W_{>1}
. Then
\mathrm{vec}(W_{1})=\sum_{m}c_{m}{\bm{v}}_{0m}\otimes{\bm{u}}

, notice that

is still the largest eigenvector of

. If not, i.e.,

, we know that

{\bm{u}}

\mathcal{W}_{>1}

(34)

Lemma 4

(Gradient Formula of contrastive Loss (Eqn.
1
) (extension of Lemma 2 in
(
Jing et al. 2022
)
)
.

Consider the loss function

\min_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\phi,\psi}(\bm{\theta}):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi\left(\sum_{j\neq i}\psi(d^{2}_{i}-d^{2}_{ij})\right)

(45)

Then for any matrix (or vector) variable
A
, we have:

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\phi,\psi}}{\partial{\bm{z}}[i]}A^{\top}[i]+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\phi,\psi}}{\partial{\bm{z}}[i^{\prime}]}A^{\top}[i^{\prime}]=-\mathbb{C}_{\alpha}[{\bm{z}},A]

(46)

and

\sum_{i=1}^{N}A[i]\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\phi,\psi}}{\partial{\bm{z}}[i]}+A[i^{\prime}]\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\phi,\psi}}{\partial{\bm{z}}[i^{\prime}]}=-\mathbb{C}_{\alpha}[A,{\bm{z}}^{\top}]

(47)

where
\mathbb{C}_{\alpha}[\cdot,\cdot]
is the
contrastive covariance
defined as (here
\beta_{i}:=\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{ij}
):

\mathbb{C}_{\alpha}[{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}]:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\alpha_{ij}({\bm{x}}[i]-{\bm{x}}[j])({\bm{y}}[i]-{\bm{y}}[j])^{\top}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\beta_{i}({\bm{x}}[i]-{\bm{x}}[i^{\prime}])({\bm{y}}[i]-{\bm{y}}[i^{\prime}])^{\top}

(48)

and
\alpha
is defined as the following:

\alpha_{ij}:=\phi^{\prime}\left(\sum_{j\neq i}\psi(d^{2}_{i}-d^{2}_{ij})\right)\psi^{\prime}(d^{2}_{i}-d^{2}_{ij})\geq 0

(49)

where
\phi^{\prime},\psi^{\prime}
are derivatives of
\phi,\psi
.

Proof.

Taking derivative of the loss function
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\phi,\psi}
w.r.t.
{\bm{z}}[i]
and
{\bm{z}}[i^{\prime}]
, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{z}}[i]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{ij}({\bm{z}}[j]-{\bm{z}}[i^{\prime}])+\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{ji}({\bm{z}}[j]-{\bm{z}}[i])

(50)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{z}}[i^{\prime}]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{ij}({\bm{z}}[i^{\prime}]-{\bm{z}}[i])=\beta_{i}({\bm{z}}[i^{\prime}]-{\bm{z}}[i])

(51)

We just need to check the following:

\sum_{i}\left(\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{ij}({\bm{z}}[j]-{\bm{z}}[i^{\prime}])+\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{ji}({\bm{z}}[j]-{\bm{z}}[i])\right)A^{\top}[i]+\sum_{i}\beta_{i}({\bm{z}}[i^{\prime}]-{\bm{z}}[i])A^{\top}[i^{\prime}]

(52)

To see this, we only need to check whether the following is true:

-\Sigma_{0}=\sum_{i}\left(\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{ij}({\bm{z}}[j]-{\bm{z}}[i^{\prime}])+\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{ji}({\bm{z}}[j]-{\bm{z}}[i])\right)A^{\top}[i]+\sum_{i}\beta_{i}({\bm{z}}[i^{\prime}]-{\bm{z}}[i])A^{\top}[i]

(53)

which means that

-\Sigma_{0}=\sum_{i}\left(\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{ij}({\bm{z}}[j]-{\bm{z}}[i])+\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{ji}({\bm{z}}[j]-{\bm{z}}[i])\right)A^{\top}[i]

(54)

Since
\alpha_{ii}({\bm{z}}[i]-{\bm{z}}[i])=0
for arbitrarily defined
\alpha_{ii}
,
j
can also take the value of
i
, this leads to

-\Sigma_{0}=\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{ij}({\bm{z}}[j]-{\bm{z}}[i])A^{\top}[i]+\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{ji}({\bm{z}}[j]-{\bm{z}}[i])A^{\top}[i]

(55)

Swapping indices for the second term, we have:

\displaystyle-\Sigma_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{ij}({\bm{z}}[j]-{\bm{z}}[i])A^{\top}[i]+\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{ij}({\bm{z}}[i]-{\bm{z}}[j])A^{\top}[j]

(56)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{ij}({\bm{z}}[j]-{\bm{z}}[i])A^{\top}[i]-\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{ij}({\bm{z}}[j]-{\bm{z}}[i])A^{\top}[j]

(57)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{ij}({\bm{z}}[j]-{\bm{z}}[i])(A^{\top}[j]-A^{\top}[i])

(58)

and the conclusion follows.
∎

Lemma 5
.

The following minimization problem:

\min_{p_{j}}\sum_{j}c_{j}p_{j}-\tau H(p)\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\sum_{j}p_{j}=\frac{1}{\tau}x_{0}\phi^{\prime}(x_{0})

(59)

where
H(p):=-\sum_{j}p_{j}\log p_{j}
is the entropy and
x_{0}:=\sum_{j}e^{-c_{j}/\tau}
, has close-form solution:

p_{j}=\frac{1}{\tau}\exp(-c_{j}/\tau)\phi^{\prime}\left(\sum_{j}\exp(-c_{j}/\tau)\right)

(60)

Proof.

Define the following Lagrangian multiplier:

\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\bm{\theta}):=\sum_{j}c_{j}p_{j}-\tau H(p)+\mu\left(\sum_{j}p_{j}-\frac{1}{\tau}x_{0}\phi^{\prime}(x_{0})\right)

(61)

Taking derivative w.r.t
p_{j}
and we have:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}}{\partial p_{j}}=c_{j}+\tau(\log p_{j}+1)-\mu=0

(62)

which gives the solution

p_{j}=\exp\left(\frac{\mu}{\tau}-1\right)\exp\left(-\frac{c_{j}}{\tau}\right):=Z\exp\left(-\frac{c_{j}}{\tau}\right)

(63)

where
Z
can be computed via the constraint:

Z=\frac{1}{\tau}\frac{x_{0}\phi^{\prime}(x_{0})}{\sum_{j}e^{-c_{j}/\tau}}=\frac{1}{\tau}\phi^{\prime}(x_{0})

(64)

∎

Lemma 7
.

Suppose the output of a linear layer (with a weight matrix
W_{l}
) connects to a
\ell_{2}
regularization or LayerNorm through reversible layers, then
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|W_{l}\|^{2}_{F}=0
.

Proof.

From Lemma, for each sample
i
, we have its gradient before/after the normalization layer (say it is layer
m
) to be the following:

{\bm{g}}_{m}[i]={J_{m}^{\mathrm{n}}[i]}^{\top}{\bm{g}}_{m}^{\mathrm{n}}[i]

(66)

where
{\bm{g}}_{m}[i]
is the gradient after back-propagating through normalization, and
{\bm{g}}_{m}^{\mathrm{n}}[i]
is the gradient sending from the top level.

Here
J_{m}^{\mathrm{n}}[i]=\frac{1}{\|P^{\perp}_{{\bm{1}}}{\bm{f}}_{m}[i]\|_{2}}P^{\perp}_{{\bm{f}}_{m}[i],{\bm{1}}}
for LayerNorm and
J_{m}^{\mathrm{n}}[i]=\frac{1}{\|{\bm{f}}_{m}[i]\|_{2}}P^{\perp}_{{\bm{f}}_{m}[i]}
for
\ell_{2}
normalization. For
W_{l}
, its gradient update rule is:

\dot{W}_{l}=\sum_{i}\tilde{\bm{g}}_{l}[i]{\bm{f}}^{\top}_{l-1}[i]

(67)

By reversibility, we know that
\tilde{\bm{g}}_{l}[i]=J_{(\tilde{l},m]}^{\top}[i]{\bm{g}}[i]
, where
J_{(\tilde{l},m]}[i]
is the Jacobian after the linear layer
\tilde{l}
till layer
m
, right before the normalization layer. Therefore, we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(W_{l}^{\top}\dot{W}_{l})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\mathrm{tr}(W_{l}^{\top}J_{(\tilde{l},m]}^{\top}[i]{J_{m}^{\mathrm{n}}[i]}^{\top}{\bm{g}}_{m}^{\mathrm{n}}[i]{\bm{f}}^{\top}_{l-1}[i])

(68)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\mathrm{tr}({\bm{f}}^{\top}_{l-1}[i]W_{l}^{\top}J_{(\tilde{l},m]}^{\top}[i]{J_{m}^{\mathrm{n}}[i]}^{\top}{\bm{g}}_{m}^{\mathrm{n}}[i])

(69)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\mathrm{tr}({\bm{f}}_{m}^{\top}[i]{J_{m}^{\mathrm{n}}[i]}^{\top}{\bm{g}}_{m}^{\mathrm{n}}[i])

(70)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(71)

The last two equality is due to reversibility
{\bm{f}}_{m}[i]=J_{(\tilde{l},m]}[i]W_{l}{\bm{f}}_{l-1}[i]
and the property of normalization layers:
J_{m}^{\mathrm{n}}[i]{\bm{f}}_{m}[i]=0
, since a vector projected to its own complementary space is always zero
P^{\perp}_{{\bm{f}}_{m}[i]}{\bm{f}}_{m}[i]=0
.

Then we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|W_{l}\|^{2}_{F}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{tr}(W_{l}^{\top}W_{l})=\mathrm{tr}(\dot{W}_{l}^{\top}W_{l})+\mathrm{tr}(W_{l}^{\top}\dot{W}_{l})=0

(72)

∎

Lemma 8
.

For every rank-1 matrix A with

, there exists
\|{\bm{u}}\|_{2}=\|{\bm{v}}\|_{2}=1
so that
A={\bm{u}}{\bm{v}}^{\top}
.

Since
A
is rank-1, it is clear that there exists
{\bm{u}}^{\prime}
and
{\bm{v}}^{\prime}
so that

. Since

. Therefore, taking
{\bm{u}}={\bm{u}}^{\prime}/\|{\bm{u}}^{\prime}\|_{2}

Lemma 9
.

If
\|W_{l}\|_{F}=1
for
1\leq l\leq L
, then
\|W_{L}W_{L-1}\ldots W_{1}\|_{2}=1
if any only if
W_{L},W_{L-1},\ldots,W_{1}
are aligned-rank-1 (Def.
4
).

Proof.

If
W_{L},W_{L-1},\ldots,W_{1}
are aligned-rank-1, then by its definition, there exists unit vectors
\{{\bm{v}}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L}
so that
W_{l}={\bm{v}}_{l}{\bm{v}}_{l-1}^{\top}
. Therefore,
\|W_{L}W_{L-1}\ldots W_{1}\|^{2}_{2}=\|{\bm{v}}_{L}{\bm{v}}_{0}^{\top}\|^{2}_{2}=\lambda_{\max}({\bm{v}}_{L}{\bm{v}}_{0}^{\top}{\bm{v}}_{0}{\bm{v}}_{L}^{\top})=\lambda_{\max}({\bm{v}}_{L}{\bm{v}}_{L}^{\top})=1
.

Then we prove the other direction. Note that

\|W_{L}W_{L-1}\ldots W_{1}\|_{2}\leq\prod_{l=1}^{L}\|W_{l}\|_{2}\leq\prod_{l=1}^{L}\|W_{l}\|_{F}=1

(73)

and the equality only holds when all
W_{l}
are rank-1. By Lemma
8
, for any
l
, there exists unit vectors
{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{l}
,
{\bm{v}}_{l-1}
so that
W_{l}={\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{l}{\bm{v}}_{l-1}^{\top}
. To show that they must be aligned (i.e.
{\bm{v}}_{l}=\pm{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{l}
), we prove by contradiction.

Suppose
\|W_{L}W_{L-1}\ldots W_{1}\|_{2}=1
but for some
l
,
{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{l}\neq\pm{\bm{v}}_{l}
and thus
|{\bm{v}}^{\top}_{l}{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{l}|<1
. Then
W_{l+1}W_{l}=({\bm{v}}^{\top}_{l}{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{l}){\bm{v}}_{l+1}{\bm{v}}^{\top}_{l-1}
and
\|W_{l+1}W_{l}\|_{2}\leq\|W_{l+1}W_{l}\|_{F}=|{\bm{v}}^{\top}_{l}{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{l}|<1
. Therefore,
\|W_{L}W_{L-1}\ldots W_{1}\|_{2}<1
, which is a contradiction.

Note that for
W_{l}=\pm{\bm{v}}_{l}{\bm{v}}_{l-1}^{\top}
, we can always move around the signs to either
{\bm{v}}_{0}
or
{\bm{v}}_{L}
to fit into the definition of aligned-rank-1.
∎

Lemma 10
.

For the following optimization problem with a given fixed vector
{\bm{u}}\neq 0
:

\max_{\mathcal{W}}\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{W};{\bm{u}}):=\|W_{L}W_{L-1}\ldots W_{1}{\bm{u}}\|_{2}\quad\mathrm{s.t.\ }\|W_{l}\|_{F}=1,

(74)

where
\mathcal{W}=\{W_{L},W_{L-1},\ldots,W_{1}\}
. If
\mathcal{W}^{*}
is a local maximum solution (i.e., there exists a neighborhood
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{W}^{*})
of
\mathcal{W}^{*}
so that for any
\mathcal{W}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{W}^{*})
,
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{W})\leq\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{W}^{*})
), and
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{W}^{*})>0
, then
\mathcal{W}^{*}
is an aligned-rank-1 solution (Def.
4
).

Proof.

Let
{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{L-1}:=W^{*}_{L-1}W^{*}_{L-2}\ldots W^{*}_{1}{\bm{u}}
. Note that
{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{L-1}\neq 0
(otherwise
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{W}^{*})
would be zero). Consider the following optimization subproblem (here we optimize over
W_{L}
and treat
{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{L-1}
as a fixed vector).

\max_{W_{L}}\mathcal{J}(W_{L};W^{*}_{-L})=\|W_{L}{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{L-1}\|_{2}\quad\mathrm{s.t.\ }\|W_{L}\|_{F}=1

(75)

By local optimality of
\mathcal{W}^{*}
,
W^{*}_{L}
must be the local maximum of Eqn.
75
and thus a critical point, since both the objective and the constraints are differentiable. Note that
\|W_{L}{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{L-1}\|_{2}
is a vector 2-norm and all critical points of Eqn.
75
must satisfy

W_{L}{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{L-1}{{\bm{v}}^{\prime}}^{\top}_{L-1}=\lambda W_{L}

(76)

for some constant
\lambda
. Notice that to satisfy this condition, each row of
W_{L}
must be an eigenvector of
{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{L-1}{{\bm{v}}^{\prime}}^{\top}_{L-1}
. For a solution to be local maximal,
\lambda
is the largest eigenvalue of
{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{L-1}{{\bm{v}}^{\prime}}^{\top}_{L-1}
, and each row of
W_{L}
is the corresponding eigenvector. It is clear that the rank-1 matrix
{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{L-1}{{\bm{v}}^{\prime}}^{\top}_{L-1}
has a unique maximum eigenvalue
\|{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{L-1}\|_{2}^{2}>0
with its corresponding one-dimensional eigenspace span by
{\bm{v}}_{L-1}:={\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{L-1}/\|{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{L-1}\|_{2}
(while all other eigenvalues are zeros). Therefore,
W^{*}_{L}
as the local maximum of Eqn.
75
, must have:

W^{*}_{L}={\bm{v}}_{L}{\bm{v}}^{\top}_{L-1}

(77)

for some
\|{\bm{v}}_{L}\|_{2}=1
.

Now let
{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{L-2}:=W^{*}_{L-2}\ldots W^{*}_{1}{\bm{u}}
. Similarly,
{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{L-2}\neq 0
(otherwise
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{W}^{*})
would be zero). Then
{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{L-1}=W^{*}_{L-1}{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{L-2}
. Treating
{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{L-2}
as a fixed vector and varying
W_{L-1}
and
W_{L}
simultaneously, then since
W^{*}_{L:1}
is a local maximal solution,
W^{*}_{L}
must take the form of Eqn.
77
given any
W^{*}_{L-1}
, which means that the objective function now becomes

\mathcal{J}(W_{L-1};W^{*}_{-(L-1)})=\|W^{*}_{L}{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{L-1}\|_{2}=\|{\bm{v}}_{L}{\bm{v}}^{\top}_{L-1}{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{L-1}\|_{2}=\|{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{L-1}\|_{2}=\|W_{L-1}{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{L-2}\|_{2}

(78)

and the subproblem becomes:

\max_{W_{L-1}}\|W_{L-1}{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{L-2}\|_{2}\quad\mathrm{s.t.\ }\|W_{L-1}\|_{F}=1

(79)

Repeating this process, we know
W^{*}_{L-1}
must satisfy:

W^{*}_{L-1}={\bm{v}}_{L-1}{\bm{v}}^{\top}_{L-2}

(80)

for
{\bm{v}}_{L-2}:={\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{L-2}/\|{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{L-2}\|_{2}
. This procedure can be repeated until
W_{1}
and the prove is complete.

∎

Lemma 11
.

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|{\bm{w}}_{k}\|^{2}_{2}=0
, if node
k
is under BatchNorm.

Proof.

For BN, it is a layer with reversibility on each filter
k
. We use
{\bm{f}}_{k},{\bm{g}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
to represent the activation/gradient at node
k
in a batch of size
N
. The forward/backward operation of BN can be written as:

{\bm{f}}^{\mathrm{n}}_{k}=J_{k}{\bm{f}}_{k},\quad{\bm{g}}_{k}=J_{k}^{\top}{\bm{g}}^{\mathrm{n}}_{k}

(81)

Here
J_{k}=J_{k}^{\top}=\frac{1}{\|P^{\perp}_{{\bm{1}}}{\bm{f}}_{k}\|_{2}}P^{\perp}_{{\bm{f}}_{k},{\bm{1}}}
is the Jacobian matrix at each node
k
.

We check how the weight
{\bm{w}}_{k}
changes under BatchNorm. Here we have
{\bm{f}}_{k}=h(F_{l-1}{\bm{w}}_{k})
where
h
is a reversible activation and
F_{l-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times n_{l-1}}
contains all output from the last layer. Then we have:

\dot{\bm{w}}_{k}=\sum_{i}h^{\prime}_{i}g_{k}[i]{\bm{f}}_{l-1}[i]=F^{\top}_{l-1}D_{k}{\bm{g}}_{k}=F^{\top}_{l-1}D_{k}J^{\top}_{k}{\bm{g}}^{\mathrm{n}}_{k}

(82)

where
D_{k}:=\mathrm{diag}([h^{\prime}_{i}]_{i=1}^{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
. Due to reversibility, we have
{\bm{f}}_{k}=h(F_{l-1}{\bm{w}}_{k})=D_{k}F_{l-1}{\bm{w}}_{k}
. Therefore,

{\bm{w}}_{k}^{\top}\dot{\bm{w}}_{k}={\bm{w}}_{k}^{\top}F^{\top}_{l-1}D_{k}J^{\top}_{k}{\bm{g}}^{\mathrm{n}}_{k}={\bm{f}}_{k}^{\top}J^{\top}_{k}{\bm{g}}^{\mathrm{n}}_{k}=0

(83)

∎

Lemma 12

(BatchNorm regularization)
.

Consider the following optimization problem with a fixed vector
{\bm{u}}\neq 0
:

\max_{\mathcal{W}}\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{W}):=\|W_{L}W_{L-1}\ldots W_{1}{\bm{u}}\|_{2}\quad\mathrm{s.t.\ }\|W_{L}\|_{F}=1,\quad\|{\bm{w}}_{lk}\|_{2}=1/\sqrt{n_{l}}

where

{\bm{w}}_{lk}

k
-th filter at layer

). Then Lemma
10

Lemma 14

1
holds,

{\bm{x}}_{1}
covers all

\alpha_{ij}>0

Lemma 15
.

If the receptive field
R_{k}
satisfies Assumption
1
, and the collection of

\{{\bm{x}}_{k}[i]\}_{i=1}^{N}

.

Lemma 16
.

If
{\bm{v}}>0
is an all positive
d
-dimensional vector,
{\bm{u}}^{\top}{\bm{v}}=0
, then

{\bm{u}}
