Abstract
. The main result is that the only non trivial closed ideal in the Banach algebra L ⁡ ( L p ) L(L^{p}) of bounded linear operators on L p ​ ( 0 , 1 ) L^{p}(0,1) , 1 ≤ p < ∞ 1\leq p<\infty , that has a left approximate identity is the ideal of compact operators. The algebra L ⁡ ( L 1 ) L(L^{1}) has at least one non trivial closed ideal that has a contractive right approximate identity as well as many, including the unique maximal ideal, that do not have a right approximate identity.

1.
Introduction

Arguably the most natural examples of
L^{p}
,

1\leq p<\infty
, operator algebras; i.e.,
closed subalgebras of the Banach algebra
L(L^{p})
of (always bounded, linear) operators on

L^{p}(\mu)

for some measure
\mu
, are the closed ideals in
L(L^{p})
. When
p=2
and the measure
\mu
is
separable, it is well-known that the only non trivial closed ideal in
L(L^{p})
is the ideal

{\mathcal{K}}(L^{p})
of compact operators.
Similarly,
{\mathcal{K}}(\ell^{p})
is the only non trivial closed ideal in
L(\ell^{p})
for other values of
1\leq p<\infty
. Here we focus on separable
L^{p}(\mu)
, in which case up to Banach space isomorphism the
only other examples are
L^{p}(0,1)
, so in the sequel we let
L^{p}
denote
L^{p}(0,1)
.

When
1<p\not=2<\infty
, the structure of the closed ideals in
L(L^{p})
is complicated and not well
understood. Given any Banach space
X
, every non trivial ideal in
L(X)
contains the ideal

{\mathcal{F}}(X)
of finite rank operators on
X
, and hence every non trivial closed ideal contains the
ideal
{\mathcal{K}}(X)
of compact operators on
X
when
X
has the approximation property; in particular,
when
X
is any
L^{p}
space,
1\leq p\leq\infty
. Sometimes
L(X)
has a largest non trivial ideal.
Let
{\mathcal{M}}(X)
denote the collection of all operators
T\in L(X)
such that the identity operator

I_{X}
on
X
does not factor through
T
(an operator
S:W\to Z
is said to factor through an
operator
T:X\to Y
provided there exist operators
B:W\to X
and
A:Y\to Z
such that
S=ATB
). It is easy to see that if
{\mathcal{M}}(X)
is closed under addition then
{\mathcal{M}}(X)
is an ideal, in
which case
{\mathcal{M}}(X)
is obviously the largest ideal in
L(L^{p})
. It is true but non trivial that

{\mathcal{M}}(L^{p})
is closed under addition for
1\leq p<\infty
(see
[
ES
]
for the case
p=1
and

[
JMST
]
for the cases
1<p<\infty
). Actually more is contained in the cited papers; namely,
that
{\mathcal{M}}(L^{p})
is the class of
L^{p}
–singular operators in
L(L^{p})
. (An operator
T:X\to Y
is
said to be
Z
–singular
provided there is no subspace
Z_{1}
of
X
such that
Z_{1}
is
isomorphic to
Z
and the restriction
T_{|Z_{1}}
of
T
to
Z_{1}
is an isomorphism. So
T
is
strictly singular
if it is
Z
-singular for every infinite dimensional
Z
.) This last description of

{\mathcal{M}}(L^{p})
will be used later in this note.

We take this oportunity to describe some of the ways to build many more exmaples of closed ideals in
L(L^{p})
.
As was pointed out by Pietsch
[
Pie
]
it follows from
[
Sch
]
that there are infinitely
many different closed ideals in
L(L^{p})
,
1<p\not=2<\infty
. In a similar way it follows from
[
BRS
]
that there
are
at least
\aleph_{1}
. These ideals are all
large
in the sense that they contain operators that
are not strictly singular. Schlumprecht and Zsák
[
SZ
]
proved that there are at least a
continuum
c:=2^{\aleph_{0}}
closed ideals in
L(L^{p})
,
1<p\not=2<\infty
. The ideals constructed
in
[
SZ
]
are all
small
; i.e., contained in the strictly singular operators. Recently,
the authors
[
JS
]

proved that there are
2^{c}
large closed ideals and also
2^{c}
small closed ideals in
L(L^{p})
when

1<p\not=2<\infty
.
The ideals constructed in
[
Sch
]
and
[
BRS
]
look much different from the ideals
constructed
in
[
SZ
]
and
[
JS
]
and illustrate somewhat different approaches to constructing ideals. In
the first approach one constructs a family

\mathcal{S}
of complemented subspaces of
L^{p}
that are mutually non isomorphic (in fact, for each two members of

\mathcal{S}
one of them does not embed complementably into any finite direct sum of the other). Given one of these
spaces
X
, let
\mathcal{I}_{X}
denote the space of all operators
in
L(L^{p})
that factor through some finite direct sum of
X
.
Then
\mathcal{I}_{X}
is obviously a (possibly non closed) ideal in
L(L^{p})
, and if an idempotent

P
is in the closure
\overline{\mathcal{I}_{X}}
of
\mathcal{I}_{X}
, then it is easy to see that
the range of

P
is isomorphic to a complemented subspace of some finite direct sum of
X
. Consequently,

\overline{\mathcal{I}_{X}}\not=\overline{\mathcal{I}_{Y}}
for distinct
X
,
Y
in
\mathcal{S}
.
In practice, it is a nuisance to have to consider all finite direct sums of the spaces
X
and so
one builds examples where it is easy to check that
X\oplus X
is isomorphic to
X
. In any case,
the ideals
\overline{\mathcal{I}_{X}}
are large ideals because, by construction, they contain
projections onto infinite dimensional subspaces, and such operators are obviously not strictly
singular.

In the second approach, one defines a specific operator
T
from some Banach space
X_{1}
into some
Banach space
X_{2}
and considers the collection
\mathcal{I}_{T}
of all operators
S
in
L(L^{p})

that factor through
T
; i.e.,
S
can be written as
ASB
for some bounded linear operators
B:L^{p}\to X_{1}
and
A:X_{2}\to L^{p}
. In practice one uses only
T
for which the direct sum operator

T\oplus T:X_{1}\oplus X_{1}\to X_{2}\oplus X_{2}
factors through
T
in order to guarantee that

\mathcal{I}_{T}
is a (possibly non closed) ideal in
L(L^{p})
so that the closure

\overline{\mathcal{I}_{T}}
is a closed ideal in
L(L^{p})
. Of course, the first approach is the
special case of the second approach where
T
is an idempotent in
L(L^{p})
, but in the second
approach what have been used are injections, including strictly singular operators such as the
formal injections
i_{s,r}

from
\ell_{s}
to
\ell_{r}
with
s<r
. When the injection
T
is strictly singular, the deal

\overline{\mathcal{I}_{T}}
consists of strictly singular operators and so is a small ideal.
In an earlier version of
[
JS
]
this approach was used to construct
c
large closed ideals
in
L(L^{p})
,
1<p\not=2<\infty
. The
injections used there are not strictly singular but are very far
from being idempotents.

In
[
JPS
]
, using this approach but with very different operators, it was proved that
L(L^{1})

has at least
c
small closed ideals. It is open
whether
L(L^{1})
has more than a continuum of closed ideals. There are known to exist only three
large ideals in
L(L^{1})
.

To build
2^{c}
closed ideals in
L(L^{p})
,
1<p\not=2<\infty
, a yet somewhat different approach was
used in
[
JS
]
. Each of these ideals is generated by a class of operators
\{T_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}
.
That is the closure of the set of operators of the form
\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}T_{\alpha_{i}}B_{i}
where n ranges
over the positive integers and
\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\}
over
{\mathcal{A}}^{n}
. This of course is not
very informative as any ideal is of that form. The main contribution of
[

]
is to find a way to produce

2^{c}
sets

that give rise to different ideals.

Although the classification of the closed ideals in
L(L^{p})
is far from being known, we can prove
some general facts about them. These facts involve the notion of approximate identity.

Definition 1.1
.

Given a Banach space
X
and an ideal
{\mathcal{I}}

in
L(X)
, we say that a net
(T_{\alpha})
of operators
in
{\mathcal{I}}
is a left; respectively, right, approximate identity for
{\mathcal{I}}
provided

\|T_{\alpha}S-S\|\to 0
; respectively,
\|ST_{\alpha}-S\|\to 0
, for every
S
in
{\mathcal{I}}
.

In particular, we prove in Section
2
that the only non
trivial ideal in
L(L^{p})
,
1\leq p<\infty
, that has a left approximate identity is the ideal

{\mathcal{K}}(L^{p})
of compact operators. If follows by duality that for
1<p<\infty
the ideal
{\mathcal{K}}(L^{p})
is
the only non trivial ideal that has a right approximate identity. In contrast to this last fact, in
Section
3
we prove that
L(L^{1})
has a non trivial ideal other than
{\mathcal{K}}(L^{1})
that has a
right approximate identity consisting of contractive idempotents. We also point out that no small
ideal in
L(L^{1})
has a right approximate identity and show that at least one of the other two
known large ideals in
L(L^{1})
, the unique maximal ideal
{\mathcal{M}}(L^{1})
, does not have a right approximate identity.

We thank N. C. Phillips for many discussions about the contents herein. In fact, Phillips raised the
question whether there are ideals in
L(L^{p})
other than
{\mathcal{K}}(L^{p})
that have a left or right
approximate identity. Part of his motivation was the open problem to determine which ideals
{\mathcal{I}}
in

L(L^{p})
have the property that
L(L^{p})/{\mathcal{I}}
are isomorphically homomorphic to a subalgebra of

L(L^{p}(\mu))
for some measure
\mu
. The proofs in
[
BJ
]
and
[
BP
]
that
{\mathcal{K}}(L^{p})
has this
property both use the fact that
{\mathcal{K}}(L^{p})
has a bounded approximate identity.

2.
Left approximate identities for ideals in
L(L^{p})

Here we prove

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a non trivial closed ideal in
L(L^{p})
,
1\leq p<\infty
, and assume that

\mathcal{I}
has a left approximate identity. Then
\mathcal{I}
is
{\mathcal{K}}(L^{p})
, the ideal of compact
operators.

Proof:
Since the Haar basis is a monotone Schauder basis for
L^{p}
, the ideal
{\mathcal{K}}(L^{p})
has a left
approximate identity consisting of finite rank projections of norm one. The converse direction
really consists of three theorems as the proofs for the cases
p=1
,
2<p<\infty
, and
1<p<2
are
very different. To call attention to the fact that we prove something stronger than an ideal has no
left approximate identity, we introduce some new language. Given a Banach space
X
, an ideal
{\mathcal{I}}

in
L(X)
, and a collection
{\mathcal{S}}
of operators in
L(X)
, we say that a net
(T_{\alpha})
of operators
in
L(X)
is an
{\mathcal{S}}
–left; respectively,
{\mathcal{S}}
–right, approximate identity for
{\mathcal{I}}
provided
each
T_{\alpha}
is in
{\mathcal{S}}
and
\|T_{\alpha}T-T\|\to 0
; respectively,
\|TT_{\alpha}-T\|\to 0
, for every
T
in
{\mathcal{I}}
.

Case
1
:
p=1
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a non trivial closed ideal in
L(L^{1})
. We treat first the case that
{\mathcal{I}}

is contained in the closed ideal
\mathcal{W}=\mathcal{W}(L^{1})
of weakly compact operators in
L(L^{1})
. In this case we show that there is no

{\mathcal{W}}
–left approximate identity for
{\mathcal{I}}
(the same argument shows that it also has no
{\mathcal{W}}
–right
approximate identity).

We use without further reference the facts that
\mathcal{W}
is equal to the ideal
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}(L^{1})
of strictly singular operators on
L^{1}
, and that an operator on
L^{1}
is weakly
compact if and only if it is
\ell^{1}
–singular; i.e., is not an isomorphism when restricted to any
subspace that is isomorphic to
\ell^{1}
. This background is presented in, for example,
[
AK
, Chapter
5]
.

Since
L^{1}
has the Dunford–Pettis property
[
AK
, Theorem 5.4.6]
, the product
\mathcal{W}\cdot\mathcal{I}
is a subset of the compact operators
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}(L^{1})
. Thus if

(S_{\alpha})
is a net of weakly compact operators and
\|S_{\alpha}T-T\|\to 0
, then
T
must be a
compact operator. This proves, in particular that
there is no
{\mathcal{W}}
–left approximate identity for
{\mathcal{I}}
unless

{\mathcal{I}}\subset{\mathcal{K}}
. This finishes the proof because
{\mathcal{K}}
is the smallest non trivial closed ideal
in
L(L^{1})
.

Next suppose that
{\mathcal{I}}
is the ideal
{\mathcal{W}}
of weakly compact operators on
L^{1}
. We have already
observed that
{\mathcal{W}}
does not have a left approximate identity (since there are weakly compact
operators on
L^{1}
that are not compact), but we want to show that there is no
{\mathcal{M}}
–left
approximate identity for
{\mathcal{W}}
, where

{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{M}}(L^{1})
is the unique maximal ideal in
L(L^{1})
described in the introduction. It then
follows that there is no
{\mathcal{M}}
–left approximate identity for any closed ideal in
L(L^{1})
that
contains
{\mathcal{W}}
. We then complete the proof of the
p=1
case of Theorem
2.1
by recalling
that a closed ideal in
L(L^{1})
that is not contained in
{\mathcal{W}}
must contain
{\mathcal{W}}
. This is true
because (1) the identity operator
I_{\ell^{1}}
on
\ell^{1}
factors through every non weakly compact
operator in
L(L^{1})

[
KP
]
, and (2) every weakly compact (even every representable) operator
from
L^{1}
to any Banach space factors through
\ell^{1}
. Again, this background is in the above
mention chapter in
[
AK
]
.

To prove that there is no
{\mathcal{M}}
–left approximate identity for
{\mathcal{W}}
,
it is enough to find
S\in{\mathcal{W}}
such that
S
is not in the closure of
{\mathcal{M}}\cdot S
. To do this,
first observe that
S:=I_{2,1}Q
is in
{\mathcal{W}}
, where
I_{2,1}
is the formal identity from
L^{2}

into
L^{1}
and
Q
is a quotient mapping from
L^{1}
onto
L^{2}
; that is,
Q
maps the open unit
ball
B^{\circ}_{L^{1}}
of
L^{1}
onto the open unit ball
B^{\circ}_{L^{2}}
of
L^{2}
. Thus

\overline{SB^{\circ}_{L^{1}}}
contains all measurable functions having constant modulus
1
. But if

T
is an operator on
L^{1}
that maps all measurable functions having constant modulus
1

into functions whose norms are bounded away from zero, then
T
is not in
{\mathcal{M}}
by
[
ES
]
,

[
Ros2
]
. In particular, no operator in
{\mathcal{M}}
can be close to the identity on the functions that
have constant modulus
1
. This shows that
S
is not in the closure of
{\mathcal{M}}\cdot S
.

This completes the proof of Theorem
2.1
in the case
p=1
. We just remark that we do not
really need the fact that a closed ideal
{\mathcal{I}}
in
L(L^{1})
that contains a non weakly compact
operator must contain
{\mathcal{W}}
, which uses the background facts (1) and (2). Only (1) is needed. (1)
says that
{\mathcal{I}}
contains a projection whose range is isomorphic to
\ell^{1}
, and it is easy to
construct an operator
U:\ell^{1}\to L^{1}
such that
UB_{\ell_{1}}
contains all measurable functions
that have constant modulus one.

Case 2:
2<p<\infty
.

We assume that
{\mathcal{I}}
is a non trivial ideal in
L(L^{p})
different from
{\mathcal{K}}={\mathcal{K}}(L^{p})
.
The main tool needed for the proof is the Kadec–Pełczyński dichotomy principle
[
KP
]
, which
says that every normalized weakly null sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
in
L^{p}
,
2<p<\infty
, contains a subsequence

\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
that is equivalent either to the unit vector basis of
\ell^{2}
or to the unit vector basis of

\ell^{p}
, and moreover that the closed linear space of
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is complemented in
L^{p}
. (This and
other standard background on
L^{p}
that we use is contained in the book
[
AK
]
.) Both
possibilities can occur because
L^{p}
contains complemented subspaces isomorphic to
\ell^{2}
and
contractively complemented subspaces isometrically isomorphic to
\ell_{p}
. By the K–P dichotomy,
if
T
is a non compact operator on
L^{p}
then either
i_{p}:=i_{p,p}
,
i_{2}:=i_{2,2}
, or
i_{2,p}

factors through
T
, where for
r\leq s
we denote by
i_{r,s}
the formal identity operator from

\ell^{r}
into
\ell^{s}
. If
T
lies in the ideal
{\mathcal{I}}
and
i_{2,p}
does not factor through
T
,
then the ideal property implies that
i_{2,p}
factors through some other operator in
{\mathcal{I}}
. So in
all cases we have
T\in{\mathcal{I}}
such that
i_{2,p}
factors through

T
. However, we get more information when either
i_{2}
or
i_{p}
factors through an operator in

{\mathcal{I}}
, so we discuss all three cases.

The easiest case is that
i_{2}
factors through some
T\in{\mathcal{I}}
. Thus in
TB_{L^{p}}
there is a
sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
that is equivalent to the unit vector basis for
\ell^{2}
and such that the closed
linear span
[\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}]
of
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is complemented in
L^{p}
. Let
\{h_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be the Haar basis for
L^{p}
,
normalized so that each
h_{n}
has norm one. Since
\{h_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is an unconditional basis for
L^{p}
and

L^{p}
has type
2
, the operator
x_{n}\mapsto h_{n}
extends to a bounded linear operator from
[\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}]

into
L^{p}
. Since
[\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}]
is complemented in
L^{p}
, this operator extends to an operator
S
on

L^{p}
. Thus the operator
U:=ST
is an operator in
{\mathcal{I}}
such that
UB_{L^{p}}
contains the Haar
basis
\{h_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
. We claim that
U
is not in the closure of
{\mathcal{M}}\cdot U
(where here
{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{M}}(L^{p})
). This claim is an immediate
consequence of Andrew’s result
[
And
]
that if
S\in L(L^{p})
and
\sup_{n}\|Sh_{n}-h_{n}\|<1
,
then
S
is not in
{\mathcal{M}}
. This proves that there is no
{\mathcal{M}}
–left approximate identity for
{\mathcal{I}}
.

Next assume that
i_{p}
factors through some
T\in{\mathcal{I}}
. It then follows from Proposition

2.3
below that there is another operator
U\in{\mathcal{I}}
such that
UB_{L^{p}}

contains the Haar basis
\{h_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
. The argument in the previous paragraph then gives that there is no

{\mathcal{M}}
–left approximate identity for
{\mathcal{I}}
.

Finally, assume that only
i_{2,p}
factors through an operator
T\in{\mathcal{I}}
. In this case we only
prove that if
{\mathcal{J}}
is a closed ideal such that there is a
{\mathcal{J}}
–left approximate identity for

{\mathcal{I}}
, then
i_{p}
factors through some operator in
{\mathcal{J}}
. We can assume that there is
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
in

TB_{L^{p}}
so that
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is equivalent to the unit vector basis of
\ell^{p}
and there is a
projection
P
from
L^{p}
onto
[\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}]
. Now suppose that there is a
{\mathcal{J}}
–left approximate
identity for
{\mathcal{I}}
. Given
\varepsilon>0
, there is
S\in{\mathcal{J}}
such that
\sup_{n}\|x_{n}-Sx_{n}\|<\varepsilon
.
Replacing
S
with
PS\in{\mathcal{J}}
, we can (since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary) assume that
S[\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}]\subset[\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}]
and so the restriction of
S
to
[\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}]
is equivalent to a necessarily non compact operator
on
\ell_{p}
and hence
i_{p}
factors through
S
.

This completes the proof of the case
2<p<\infty
. Notice that by the K-P result, an operator on

L^{p}
is strictly singular if and only if neither
i_{p}
nor
i_{2}
factors through the operator.
Thus any closed ideal for which there is a
{\mathcal{M}}
–approximate identity must be contained in the
ideal of strictly singular operators. However, we do not know whether any of these

2^{2^{\aleph_{0}}}
closed ideals other than
{\mathcal{K}}
has a
{\mathcal{M}}
–left approximate identity.

1<p<2
.

Let
{\mathcal{I}}
be any non trivial ideal in

other than

. We show that

does not have a

–left approximate identity. By duality from the

such that

factors through

. Let

extends to a contraction from

into

(here

such that

–left approximate identity for

, then for

such that

. But it was

–singular operator on

is in
{\mathcal{M}}
(this
just says that an operator on

that preserves some isomorph of

must send some isomorph of

L^{p}
isomorphically onto a complemented subspace of
L^{p}
). This completes the proof of the case

1<p<2
, but
we remark that one could avoid quoting the result from
[

]
by understanding what Andrews
proved but did not state explicitly in
[
And
]
.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{I}}
be a nontrivial closed ideal in
L(L^{p})
,
1\leq p<\infty
, other than the ideal of
compact operators. Then
{\mathcal{I}}
does not have a left approximate identity. When
p>1
,
{\mathcal{I}}
does not
have a right approximate identity. Moreover, if
1<p<2
, then
{\mathcal{I}}
does not have a
{\mathcal{M}}
–left
approximate identity and when
2<p<\infty
,
{\mathcal{I}}
does not have a
{\mathcal{M}}
–right approximate
identity. If
1<p<\infty
and
{\mathcal{I}}
is not contained in the ideal of strictly singular operators,
then
{\mathcal{I}}
does not have either a
{\mathcal{M}}
–left approximate identity or a
{\mathcal{M}}
–right approximate
identity.
If
p=1
and
{\mathcal{I}}
is not contained in the ideal of weakly compact operators, then
{\mathcal{I}}
does not
have a either a
{\mathcal{M}}
–left approximate identity or a
{\mathcal{M}}
–right approximate identity.

In the proof of Theorem
2.1
we used the fact that there exists
T\in L(\ell^{p},L^{p})
,

2<p<\infty
, such that
TB_{\ell^{p}}
contains the
L^{p}
normalized Haar basis for
L^{p}
. This fact
is immediate from Proposition
2.3
and the well known fact that for
1<p<\infty

the space
\ell^{p}
is isomorphic to
(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\ell^{2}_{n})_{p}
, or to any other
p
–sum of a
sequence of finite dimensional non zero Hilbert spaces
[
KP
]
.

Recall the definition of the Haar functions: For
n=0,1,\dots
and
i=1,\dots,2^{n}
, denote

h_{n,i}(t)=\begin{cases}1&\frac{i-1}{2^{n}}\leq t<\frac{2i-1}{2^{n+1}}\\
-1&\frac{2i-1}{2^{n+1}}\leq t<\frac{i}{2^{n}}.\end{cases}

As is well known, the Haar system,
{\bf 1}\cup\{h_{n,i}\}_{n=0,i=1}^{\infty\ \ \ 2^{n}}
, in its
lexicographic order, forms a Schauder basis for
L^{p}
,
1\leq p<\infty
and this basis is
unconditional iff
p>1
.

We also introduce the space
(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\oplus\ell_{2^{n}}^{2})_{p}
; i.e., the
\ell_{p}
sum of
Euclidean spaces of dimensions
2^{n}
,
n=0,1,\dots
. Its natural basis will be denoted

\{e_{n,i}\}_{n=0,i=1}^{\infty\ \ \ 2^{n}}
. In the next proposition we show that, for
p>2
, the
basis to basis map
e_{n,i}\to h_{n,i}/\|h_{n,i}\|_{p}
extends to a bounded operator.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
2<p<\infty
and define
T
from the finitely supported sequences in

(\sum\oplus\ell_{2^{n}}^{2})_{p}
to
L^{p}
as the linear extension of
Te_{n,i}=h_{n,i}/\|h_{n,i}\|_{p}
.
Then
T
is bounded and thus naturally extends to an operator from
(\sum\oplus\ell_{2^{n}}^{2})_{p}
to

L^{p}
.

Proof:
Recall (
[
Mul
]
, 1.2.6, page 37) that dyadic BMO is the space of all formal expansions

\sum_{n,i}b_{n,i}h_{n,i}
whose norm

\|\sum_{n,i}b_{n,i}h_{n,i}\|_{BMO}=\sup_{0\leq n<\infty,1\leq i\leq 2^{n}}(2^{n}\sum_{m=n}^{\infty}\sum_{\{j;|h_{m,j}|\leq|h_{n,i}|\}}b_{m,j}^{2}2^{-m})^{1/2}

Is finite. So,

\|\sum_{n,i}b_{n,i}h_{n,i}\|_{BMO}\leq\sup_{0\leq n<\infty}(2^{n}\sum_{m=n}^{\infty}2^{-m}\sum_{j=1}^{2^{m}}b_{m,j}^{2})^{1/2}\\
\leq\sup_{0\leq n<\infty}(2\sup_{m\geq n}\sum_{j=1}^{2^{m}}b_{m,j}^{2})^{1/2}=2^{1/2}\|\{b_{m,j}\}\|_{(\sum\oplus\ell_{2^{n}}^{2})_{\infty}}.

Recall that, for
1\leq p<\infty
, dyadic
H^{p}
is the space of all functions

\sum_{n,i}a_{n,i}h_{n,i}
for which

\|\sum_{n,i}a_{n,i}h_{n,i}\|_{H^{p}}=\|(\sum_{n,i}a_{n,i}^{2}h_{n,i}^{2})^{1/2}\|_{H^{p}}

is finite.

By the duality between dyadic
H^{1}
and dyadic BMO (see section 1.2 in
[
Mul
]
) we get that for
some universal constant
C
and all coefficients
\{a_{n,i}\}
,

(2.1)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\sum_{i=1}^{2^{n}}a_{n,i}^{2})^{1/2}\\
\leq C\|\sum_{n,i}a_{n,i}h_{n,i}/\|h_{n,i}\|_{L^{1}}\|_{H^{1}}=C\|(\sum_{n,i}a_{n,i}^{2}h_{n,i}^{2}/\|h_{n,i}\|_{L^{1}}^{2})^{1/2}\|_{L^{1}}.

We now interpolate between
H^{1}
and
L^{2}
to get that for some absolute constant
C
, for all

1<p<2
and all coefficients
\{a_{n,i}\}
,

(2.2)

\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\sum_{i=1}^{2^{n}}a_{n,i}^{2})^{p/2}\right)^{1/p}\\
\leq C\left\|\sum_{n,i}a_{n,i}h_{n,i}/\|h_{n,i}\|_{L^{p}}\right\|_{H^{p}}=C\left\|\big(\sum_{n,i}a_{n,i}^{2}h_{n,i}^{2}/\|h_{n,i}\|_{L^{p}}^{2}\big)^{1/2}\right\|_{L^{p}}.

More precisely, we use

Theorem

(2.3.4 in
[
Mul
]
)
.

For all
v=\sum_{n,i}v_{n,i}h_{n,i}\in H^{p},1<p<2
, there exists

x=\sum_{n,i}x_{n,i}h_{n,i}\in H^{1}
and
y=\sum_{n,i}y_{n,i}h_{n,i}\in L^{2}
satisfying

x_{n,i},y_{n,i}\geq 0
for all
n,i
,

|v_{n,i}|=x_{n,i}^{1-\theta}y_{n,i}^{\theta}\ \ \mbox{for all}\ \ n=0,1,\dots,\ \mbox{and}\ i=1,\dots,2^{n}

and

\|x\|_{H^{1}}^{1-\theta}\|y\|_{L^{2}}^{\theta}\leq 32\|v\|_{H^{p}},

where
\theta
is defined by
\frac{1}{p}=\frac{1-\theta}{1}+\frac{\theta}{2}
; i.e.,

\theta=2-\frac{2}{p}
.

To prove (
2.2
) we use this theorem with
v_{n,i}=a_{n,i}/\|h_{n,i}\|_{L^{p}}=2^{n/p}a_{n,i}
.
So there are non negative
x_{n,i},y_{n,i}
such that

2^{n/p}|a_{n,i}|=x_{n,i}^{1-\theta}y_{n,i}^{\theta}\ \ \mbox{for all}\ \ n=0,1,\dots,\ \mbox{and}\ i=1,\dots,2^{n}

and

(2.3)

\|(\sum_{n,i}x_{n,i}^{2}h_{n,i}^{2})^{1/2}\|_{L^{1}}^{1-\theta}\|(\sum_{n,i}y_{n,i}^{2}h_{n,i}^{2})^{1/2}\|_{L^{2}}^{\theta}\leq 32\|\sum_{n,i}a_{n,i}h_{n,i}/\|h_{n,i}\|_{L^{p}}\|_{H^{p}}.

Now,

\displaystyle(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(\sum_{i=1}^{2^{n}}a_{n,i}^{2})^{p/2})^{1/p}=(\sum_{n}2^{-n}(\sum_{i}x_{n,i}^{2(1-\theta)}y_{n,i}^{2\theta})^{p/2})^{1/p}

\displaystyle\phantom{aaaaa}\leq(\sum_{n}2^{-n}(\sum_{i}x_{n,i}^{2})^{\frac{(1-\theta)p}{2}}(\sum_{i}y_{n,i}^{2})^{\frac{\theta p}{2}})^{1/p}\ \ \mbox{by H\"{o}lder with}\ \ \frac{1}{1-\theta},\frac{1}{\theta}

\displaystyle\phantom{aaaaa}=(\sum_{n}(2^{-2n}\sum_{i}x_{n,i}^{2})^{\frac{(1-\theta)p}{2}}(2^{-n}\sum_{i}y_{n,i}^{2})^{\frac{\theta p}{2}})^{1/p}

\displaystyle\phantom{aaaaa}\leq(\sum_{n}(2^{-2n}\sum_{i}x_{n,i}^{2})^{1/2})^{1-\theta}(\sum_{n}2^{-n}\sum_{i}y_{n,i}^{2})^{\frac{\theta}{2}}

\displaystyle\phantom{aaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaa}\ \ \mbox{by H\"{o}lder with}\ \ \frac{1}{(1-\theta)p},\frac{2}{\theta p}

\displaystyle\leq C^{1-\theta}\|\sum_{n,i}2^{-n}x_{n,i}h_{n,i}/\|h_{n,i}\|_{L^{1}}\|_{H^{1}}^{1-\theta}\|\sum_{n,i}2^{-n/2}y_{n,i}h_{n,i}/\|h_{n,i}\|_{L^{2}}\|_{L^{2}}^{\theta}\ \ \mbox{by}\ (\ref{eq:h1})

\displaystyle=C^{1-\theta}\|\sum_{n,i}x_{n,i}h_{n,i}\|_{H^{1}}^{1-\theta}\|\sum_{n,i}y_{n,i}h_{n,i}\|_{L^{2}}^{\theta}

\displaystyle<32C\|\sum_{n,i}a_{n,i}h_{n,i}/\|h_{n,i}\|_{L^{p}}\|_{H^{p}}\ \ \mbox{by}\ (\ref{eq:int}).

The unconditionality of the Haar system in
L^{p}
implies that the last quantity is equivalent, with
constant depending on
p
only, to

\|\sum_{n,i}a_{n,i}h_{n,i}/\|h_{n,i}\|_{L^{p}}\|_{L^{p}}.

Dualizing again we get the required inequality showing that
T
is bounded.
∎

Although we focus here on separable
L^{p}(\mu)
spaces, it is worth remarking that if
L^{p}(\mu)
is
non separable, then there is a non trivial ideal
{\mathcal{I}}
in
L(L^{p}(\mu))
with
{\mathcal{I}}\not={\mathcal{K}}(L^{p}(\mu))

such that
{\mathcal{M}}
has a left approximate identity consisting of contractive projections; namely, let

{\mathcal{I}}
be the ideal of operators that have separable ranges. Since every closed sublattice of
L^{p}

is contractively complemented
[
LT2
, Lemma 1.b.9]
, the collection of contractive projections
onto separable sublattices forms a left approximate identity for
{\mathcal{I}}
.

3.
Right approximate identities for ideals in
L(L^{1})

It was stated in Corollary
2.2
that no small ideal (that is, contained in the ideal of
strictly singular operators) in
L(L^{1})
) other than the compact operators has a
{\mathcal{W}}(L^{1})
-right
approximate identity. In this section we consider two of the three known large closed ideals in

L(L^{1})
. We do not know whether the remaining large ideal, the ideal of Dunford-Pettis operators,
has a right approximate identity. Our guess is that it does not.

Background we use is contained in
[
DU
, section III]
. The main facts needed are that RNP
(representable) operators with domain an
L^{1}
space are the same as the operators that factor
through
\ell^{1}
, that every operator
B
from
L^{1}
to
\ell^{1}
is an RNP operator, and if
B
is
an RNP operator with domain
L^{1}
, then there is a partition
(E_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
of
(0,1)
such
that for each
n
the operator
B_{n}:=B_{|L^{1}(E_{n})}
is compact (this is how Lewis and Stegall

[
LS
]
showed that RNP operators from
L^{1}
factor through
\ell^{1}
–that operators from
L^{1}

into
\ell^{1}
are RNP operators was known before their work).

The two large ideals in
L(L^{1})
that we consider are

\Gamma_{\ell^{1}}(L^{1})
, the ideal of operators in
L(L^{1})
that factor through
\ell^{1}
; and

{\mathcal{M}}(L^{1})
, the unique maximal ideal in
L(L^{1})
. The ideal
{\mathcal{M}}(L^{1})
is the class of the
operators
T
in
L(L^{1})
such that the identity
I_{L^{1}}
does not factor through
T
. It was
already mentioned in the introduction that
{\mathcal{M}}(L^{1})
is indeed an ideal (so that it obviously is
maximal and the only maximal ideal) and that
{\mathcal{M}}(L^{1})
can also be described as the
L^{1}
-singular
operators on
L^{1}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

The ideal
{\mathcal{I}}:=\Gamma_{\ell^{1}}(L^{1})
in
L(L^{1})
has a right contractive approximate identity
consisting of contractive idempotents.

Proof:
Notice that
{\mathcal{I}}
is a large ideal and is closed because the RNP operators are closed. To
prove that
\mathcal{I}
has a right approximate identity consisting of contractive idempotents it
is enough to show that there is a net
\mathcal{N}
of contractive idempotents (i.e., conditional expectations)
in
\mathcal{I}
such that for all
B:L^{1}\to\ell^{1}
and each
\epsilon>0
there is a contractive
idempotent
P
in
\mathcal{N}
such that
\|BP-B\|\leq\epsilon
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be the net of all
P\in L(L^{1})
such that there exist a partition
\{E_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of
(0,1)

and, for each
n
, a finite rank idempotent
P_{n}
on
L^{1}(E_{n})
such that, for each
x\in L^{1}
and each
n
,
(Px)_{|E_{n}}=P_{n}(1_{E_{n}}x)
.

Since each
P_{n}
, being of finite rank, is in
\mathcal{I}
(formally,
P_{n}
preceded by the restriction to
E_{n}
and followed by the natural embedding of
L^{1}(E_{n})
into
L^{1}
) it is easy to see that
P
is in
\mathcal{I}
(for if
A_{1},A_{2},\dots
all
factor through
\ell^{1}
, then so does
A:=(A_{1},A_{2},\dots,):L^{1}\to(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}L^{1})_{1}
, where

Ax:=(A_{1}x,A_{2}x,\dots)
). Also,
\mathcal{I}
is clearly a net under the natural order on idempotents.

Finally, given a
B:L^{1}\to\ell^{1}
and
\epsilon>0

take a partition
(E_{n})
as in the result of Lewis and Stegall mentioned above and define
B_{n}:=B_{|L^{1}(E_{n})}
. Since
B_{n}
is compact there
is a contractive finite rank idempotent
P_{n}
on
{L^{1}(E_{n})}
such that

\|B_{n}-B_{n}P_{n}\|\leq\epsilon
. Define
P
on
L^{1}
by
(Px)_{|E_{n}}:=P_{n}(1_{E_{n}}x)
.
∎

Y. Choi
[
Choi
]
has used Theorem
3.1
to give a simple proof that
L(L^{1})
is not
amenable as a Banach algebra.

In order to see that
{\mathcal{M}}(L^{1})
does not have a right approximate identity, we need some further
background and notation.
Let
r_{1},r_{2},\dots
, denote the Rademacher functions, considered either as functions on
[0,1]
or
the coordinate projections on the Cantor group
\{-1,1\}^{N}
endowed with Haar measure. The
(
L^{1}
-normalized) Haar system (less the constant function), used already in the previous section,
can also be defined by:

h_{\phi}=r_{1},\quad\quad h_{\delta}=r_{2}(1+\delta r_{1}),\quad\delta=\pm 1,

and for
n=2,3,\dots
, by induction,

h_{\delta}=r_{n+1}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+\delta_{i}r_{i}),\quad\delta=(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n}),\quad\delta_{i}=\pm 1.

This system union a non zero constant function spans
L^{1}
and is a martingale difference sequence
in any order such that
(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n})
comes after
(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n-1})
for every

n>1
and every
\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n}
and
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n-1}
in
\{-1,1\}
(here and
elsewhere when
n=1
,
(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n-1})
is interpreted as
\phi
). Hence the Haar
system forms a monotone basis of
L^{1}
in any such order.

Claim 3.2
.

Let

\varphi:\{\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n}\}_{n=1,\delta_{i}=\pm 1}^{\infty}\to\{2,3,\dots\}
be a function such that

\varphi(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n})>\varphi(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n-1})

for every
n>1
and every
\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n}
and
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n-1}
in
\{-1,1\}
. Define

k_{\phi}=r_{1},\quad\quad k_{\delta}=r_{\varphi(\delta)}(1+\delta r_{1}),\quad\delta=\pm 1,

and for
n=2,3,\dots
, by recursion,

k_{\delta}=r_{\varphi(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n})}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+\delta_{i}r_{\varphi(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{i-1})}),\quad\delta=(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n}),\quad\delta_{i}=\pm 1.

Then
k_{\phi},\{k_{(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n})}\}
has the same joint distribution as the Haar system

h_{\phi},\{h_{(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n})}\}
.

Proof:
Define a map from the
\sigma
-field generated by
r_{1},\dots,r_{n+1}
into (but not necessarily
onto) the
\sigma
-field generated by
\{r_{1},r_{\varphi(\eta_{1})},\dots,r_{\varphi(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n})}\}_{\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n}\in\{-1,1\}}
by

\psi(\{t;\ r_{1}(t)=\delta_{1},\dots,r_{n}(t)=\delta_{n},r_{n+1}(t)=\delta_{n+1}\})\\
=\{t;\ r_{1}(t)=\delta_{1},r_{\varphi(\delta_{1})}=\delta_{2},\dots,r_{\varphi(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n})}(t)=\delta_{n+1}\}.

This is a measure preserving map and
k_{(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{m})}(\psi(t))=h_{(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{m})}(t)
for
all
t
,
m=1,\dots,n
and
\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n}\in\{-1,1\}
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence in
L^{1}
such that there is a sequence

\{x^{*}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
of norm one functionals with
x^{*}_{k}(x_{k})\geq a>0
for all
k
and

x^{*}_{k}(x_{l})=0
for
k\not=l
.
Let
\{h_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
be an enumeration of the Haar system in
L^{1}
and assume that

S(h_{k})=x_{k}
extends to an operator in
L(L^{1})
. Assume also that
T
in
L(L^{1})
satisfies

\|ST-S\|=b<a.

Then
T
is an Enflo operator; that is, there is a subspace
Y
of
L^{1}
so that the restriction

T_{|Y}
of
T
to
Y
is an isomorphism.

Proof:
By the assumptions,

x^{*}_{k}(STh_{k})\geq x^{*}_{k}(x_{k})-b\geq a-b.

Write

Th_{k}=\alpha h_{k}+\sum_{l\not=k}\alpha_{l}h_{l}

so that

x^{*}_{k}(STh_{k})=\alpha x^{*}_{k}(Sh_{k})\leq|\alpha|\|S\|

and hence

|\alpha|\geq(a-b)/\|S\|.

It now follows from
[
LMMS
]
that
T
is an Enflo operator.
∎

For
0<\varepsilon<1
, the biased coin operator
T_{\varepsilon}
is convolution with the measure

\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}(1+\varepsilon r_{i})
. The basic source for information about biased coin operators is
Bonami’s thesis
[
Bon
]
. Biased coin operators were introduced into Banach space theory by
Rosenthal
[
Ros1
]
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Suppose that
{\mathcal{I}}
is a closed ideal in
L(L^{1})
and there is
0<\varepsilon<1
such that the biased coin
operator
T_{\varepsilon}
is in
{\mathcal{I}}
. Then
{\mathcal{I}}
does not have a
{\mathcal{M}}(L^{1})
–right approximate identity. In
particular, the largest ideal
{\mathcal{M}}(L^{1})
does not have a right approximate identity.

Proof:
Recall that
{\mathcal{M}}(L^{1})
is the collection of non Enflo operators.
T_{\varepsilon}
is not an Enflo operator

[
Ros1
]
, so
T_{\varepsilon}
is contained in
{\mathcal{M}}(L^{1})
; so the “In particular” statement is clear. To
prove the Theorem itself,
by Lemma
3.3
it is enough to find an operator
S
in
{\mathcal{I}}
that sends the Haar system
to a biorthogonal sequence whose biorthogonal functionals are bounded. Note that
T_{\varepsilon}h_{(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n})}=\varepsilon r_{n+1}\prod_{n=1}^{n}(1+\varepsilon\delta_{i}r_{i})
, and
\|T_{\varepsilon}h_{(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n})}\|=\varepsilon
is indeed bounded away from zero. However, this sequence does not have a
bounded sequence of biorthogonal functionals. We overcome this difficulty by considering the action
of
T_{\varepsilon}
on an equivalent system of the kind discussed in Claim
3.2
.

Let the function
\varphi:\{\delta_{1}\dots,\delta_{n}\}_{n=1,\delta_{i}=\pm 1}^{\infty}\to\{2,3,\dots\}
in the
statement of Claim
3.2
also be one to one. Note that

T_{\varepsilon}k_{\delta}=\varepsilon r_{\varphi(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n})}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+\varepsilon\delta_{i}r_{\varphi(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{i-1})}).

It is still the case that
\|T_{\varepsilon}k_{\delta}\|=\varepsilon
, but now, due to the fact that

\varphi(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n})
are all different, the sequence
\{T_{\varepsilon}k_{\delta}\}
is a martingale
difference sequence and thus a montone basic sequence. Consequently, the biorthogonal functionals
to
\{T_{\varepsilon}k_{\delta}\}
all have norm at most
2/\varepsilon
. Thus we can apply Lemma
3.3
, using
for
the operator
S
the basis to basis map from the Haar system to the
k
-system followed by
T_{\varepsilon}
.
∎
