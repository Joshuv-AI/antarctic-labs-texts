Wave Map Null Form Estimates via Peter–Weyl Theory
Grigalius Taujanskas

Abstract
. We study spacetime estimates for the wave map null form Q 0 Q_{0} on ℝ × 𝕊 3 \mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3} . By using the Lie group structure of 𝕊 3 \mathbb{S}^{3} and Peter–Weyl theory, combined with the time-periodicity of the conformal wave equation on ℝ × 𝕊 3 \mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3} , we extend the classical ideas of Klainerman and Machedon to estimates on ℝ × 𝕊 3 \mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3} , allowing for a range of powers of natural (Laplacian and wave) Fourier multiplier operators. A key difference in these curved space estimates as compared to the flat case is a loss of an arbitrarily small amount of differentiability, attributable to a lack of dispersion of linear waves on ℝ × 𝕊 3 \mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3} . This arises in Fourier space from the product structure of irreducible representations of SU ⁡ ( 2 ) \mathrm{SU}(2) . We further show that our estimates imply weighted estimates for the null form on Minkowski space.

3.2
) with
F=0=G
is the estimate

(3.5)

\|Q_{0}(\phi,\psi)\|^{2}_{L^{2}([-\pi,\pi]\times\mathbb{S}^{3})}\lesssim\left(\|f_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}+\|f_{1}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}\right)\left(\|g_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{1+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}+\|g_{1}\|^{2}_{H^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}\right).

Theorem 3.4

(Estimates with Multipliers)
.

For solutions
\phi
,
\psi
of (
3.1
)–(
3.2
) there exists a universal constant
C=C(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\beta_{0},\beta_{w})>0
such that the estimate

\|J^{-\beta_{0}}W^{\beta_{w}}Q_{0}(\phi,\psi)\|_{L^{2}([-\pi,\pi]\times\mathbb{S}^{3})}\leqslant C\|f\|_{H^{\alpha_{1}}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}\|g\|_{H^{\alpha_{2}}(\mathbb{S}^{3})},

where
J=(1-\not{\Delta})^{1/2}
and
W=(2+\Box)
, holds provided the following bounds are satisfied:

(3.6)

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\beta_{0}

\displaystyle>1+2\beta_{w},

(3.7)

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant 1+2\beta_{w},

(3.8)

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}+\beta_{0}

\displaystyle\geqslant\beta_{w},

(3.9)

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}+\beta_{0}

\displaystyle\geqslant\beta_{w},

and additionally

(3.10)

\beta_{w}\geqslant-1

and

(3.11)

-\frac{1}{2}\leqslant\beta_{0}\leqslant 2\beta_{w}+\frac{3}{2}.

Remark 3.5
.

We remark that
Theorem

3.4
implies
Theorem

3.1
if we set
\beta_{w}=0
,
\beta_{0}=0
,
\alpha_{1}=1
, and
\alpha_{2}=\varepsilon
.

As a proof of concept, we also show that our estimates imply the following corollaries; of course these are not the most general estimates implied by
Theorem

3.4
(in particular we have set
\beta_{0}=\beta_{w}=0
in
Corollary

3.8
, and
\beta_{w}=0
,
\beta_{0}=-\varepsilon
in
Corollary

3.6
).

Corollary 3.6

(Forced Basic Estimate)
.

For any given
0<\varepsilon\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
there exists a universal constant
C(\varepsilon)>0
such that solutions
\phi
,
\psi
of (
3.1
)–(
3.2
) satisfy the estimate

\displaystyle\|Q_{0}(\phi,\psi)\|_{L^{2}([-\pi,\pi];H^{1+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{3}))}

\displaystyle\leqslant C(\varepsilon)\left(\|f_{0}\|_{H^{2+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}+\|f_{1}\|_{H^{1+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}+\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\|F(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{1+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 25.0pt\times\left(\|g_{0}\|_{H^{2+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}+\|g_{1}\|_{H^{1+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}+\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\|G(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{1+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}\,\mathrm{d}t\right).

As before, the time interval
[-\pi,\pi]
may also be replaced with any other interval, e.g.
[0,T]
for any
T>0
, when the constant
C(\varepsilon,T)
then depends only on
\varepsilon
and
T
.

Remark 3.7
.

In fact, we can prove slightly more, and also control the time derivative of
Q_{0}
in
L^{2}([-\pi,\pi];H^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{3}))
at the expense of replacing the norms on
F
and
G
on the right-hand side with
L^{2}([-\pi,\pi];H^{1+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{3}))
instead of
L^{1}([-\pi,\pi];H^{1+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{3}))
. See
eq.

8.7
.

Corollary 3.8

(Weighted Estimate on Minkowski Space)
.

Consider the linear waves
\Box_{\mathbb{R}^{1+3}}\tilde{\phi}=0=\Box_{\mathbb{R}^{1+3}}\tilde{\psi}
with initial data
(\tilde{\phi},\partial_{\tilde{t}}\tilde{\phi})|_{\tilde{t}=0}=(\tilde{f}_{0},\tilde{f}_{1})
and
(\tilde{\psi},\partial_{\tilde{t}}\tilde{\psi})|_{\tilde{t}=0}=(\tilde{g}_{0},\tilde{g}_{1})
, where
\tilde{t}
is the standard time coordinate on
\mathbb{R}^{1+3}
. Then for any sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
the null form

\widetilde{Q}_{0}(\tilde{\phi},\tilde{\psi})=\tilde{\nabla}_{a}\tilde{\phi}\tilde{\nabla}^{a}\tilde{\psi}=\partial_{\tilde{t}}\tilde{\phi}\partial_{\tilde{t}}\tilde{\psi}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\tilde{\phi}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\tilde{\psi}

satisfies the weighted estimate

\left\|\langle\tilde{t}-\tilde{r}\rangle\langle\tilde{t}+\tilde{r}\rangle\widetilde{Q}_{0}(\tilde{\phi},\tilde{\psi})\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{4})}\lesssim\left(\|\tilde{f}_{0}\|^{2}_{W^{2}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+\|\tilde{f}_{1}\|^{2}_{W^{1}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\right)\left(\|\tilde{g}_{0}\|^{2}_{W^{1+\varepsilon}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+\|\tilde{g}_{1}\|^{2}_{W^{\varepsilon}_{1+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\right).

Remark 3.9
.

The estimate of
Corollary

3.8
is a consequence of the basic estimate of
Theorem

3.1
.
Theorem

3.4
will imply similar, more general estimates on
\mathbb{R}^{1+3}
with respect to a hyperboloidal foliation.

4.
Classical Estimate on
\mathbb{R}^{1+3}

In this section we briefly review the main elements of the proof in
\mathbb{R}^{1+3}
, as this will help to motivate the strategy on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}
. For solutions
\tilde{\phi}
,
\tilde{\psi}
of

(4.1)

\Box_{\mathbb{R}^{1+3}}\tilde{\phi}=0=\Box_{\mathbb{R}^{1+3}}\tilde{\psi}

on
\mathbb{R}^{1+3}
with data
(\tilde{\phi},\partial_{\tilde{t}}\tilde{\phi})|_{\tilde{t}=0}=(0,\tilde{f})
,
(\tilde{\psi},\partial_{\tilde{t}}\tilde{\psi})|_{\tilde{t}=0}=(0,\tilde{g})
, the key result obtained in
[
KM93
]
is the estimate

(4.2)

\|\widetilde{Q}(\tilde{\phi},\tilde{\psi})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{4})}\leqslant C\|\tilde{f}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\tilde{g}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})},

where
\widetilde{Q}
is any of the classical null forms (
1.2
), (
1.3
) on
\mathbb{R}^{1+3}
. The method to obtain (
4.2
) may be broken down into the following steps. For the purpose of exposition we recap the case of
\widetilde{Q}_{0}(\tilde{\phi},\tilde{\phi})
. We start with data
\tilde{f}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
such that its Fourier transform is smooth and compactly supported, and vanishes in a neighbourhood of the origin; these assumptions may be removed at the end by a density argument.

4.1.
Positive/negative frequency splitting

The Fourier transform in space of (
4.1
) gives the solution
\hat{\tilde{\phi}}(\tilde{t},\xi)=(\sin(|\xi|\tilde{t})/|\xi|)\hat{\tilde{f}}(\xi)
. One considers the positive and negative frequency parts of this solution,

\hat{\tilde{\phi}}^{\pm}(\tilde{t},\xi)=\frac{e^{\pm i|\xi|\tilde{t}}}{|\xi|}\hat{\tilde{f}}(\xi),

or

\tilde{\phi}^{\pm}(\tilde{t},\tilde{x})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{e^{\pm i|\xi|\tilde{t}}}{|\xi|}\hat{\tilde{f}}(\xi)e^{i\tilde{x}\cdot\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\xi

in physical space, with the original solution given by
\tilde{\phi}=\frac{1}{2i}(\tilde{\phi}^{+}-\tilde{\phi}^{-})
. By the bilinearity of
\widetilde{Q}_{0}
, it is then enough to prove the estimate (
4.2
) for every pair
\widetilde{Q}_{0}^{\pm,\pm}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\widetilde{Q}_{0}(\tilde{\phi}^{\pm},\tilde{\phi}^{\pm})
. For brevity here we consider only
\widetilde{Q}_{0}^{+,+}
.

4.2.
Spacetime Fourier transform of
\tilde{\phi}^{\pm}

Taking now the spacetime Fourier transform of, say,
\tilde{\phi}^{+}
gives

\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{t}\to\tau,\,\tilde{x}\to\xi}(\tilde{\phi}^{+})=\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{t}\to\tau}\left(\frac{e^{i|\xi|\tilde{t}}}{|\xi|}\hat{\tilde{f}}(\xi)\right)=2\pi\frac{\hat{\tilde{f}}(\xi)}{|\xi|}\delta(\tau-|\xi|).

Using the homogeneity of
\delta
, this can be rewritten as

(4.3)

2\pi\frac{\hat{\tilde{f}}(\xi)}{|\xi|}\delta(\tau-|\xi|)=4\pi\hat{\tilde{f}}(\xi)\chi_{[0,\infty)}(\tau)\delta(\tau^{2}-|\xi|^{2}).

4.3.
Convolution in spacetime

Using
\Box_{\mathbb{R}^{1+3}}\tilde{\phi}=0
, one can rewrite the null form as
\widetilde{Q}_{0}^{+,+}=\nabla_{a}\tilde{\phi}^{+}\nabla^{a}\tilde{\phi}^{+}=\frac{1}{2}\Box_{\mathbb{R}^{1+3}}(\tilde{\phi}^{+})^{2}
. The spacetime Fourier transform of
\widetilde{Q}_{0}^{+,+}
is then

(4.4)

\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{t}\to\tau,\,\tilde{x}\to\xi}(\widetilde{Q}_{0}^{+,+})=\frac{1}{2}(\tau^{2}-|\xi|^{2})\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{t}\to\tau,\,\tilde{x}\to\xi}((\tilde{\phi}^{+})^{2})=\frac{1}{2}(\tau^{2}-|\xi|^{2})\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{t}\to\tau,\,\tilde{x}\to\xi}(\tilde{\phi}^{+})*\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{t}\to\tau,\,\tilde{x}\to\xi}(\tilde{\phi}^{+}).

To compute this convolution, one has the following lemma (the proof may be found in
[
KM93
]
, p. 1231).

Lemma 4.1
.

The spacetime Fourier transform of
(\tilde{\phi}^{+})^{2}
is given by

\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{t}\to\tau,\,\tilde{x}\to\xi}((\tilde{\phi}^{+})^{2})=\frac{2\pi^{2}}{\tau^{2}-|\xi|^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\alpha^{2}\hat{\tilde{f}}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\omega\right)\hat{\tilde{f}}\left(\xi-\frac{\alpha}{2}\omega\right)\mathrm{d}^{2}\omega

for
\tau\geqslant\xi
, and zero otherwise, where

\alpha=\alpha(\xi,\tau,\omega)=\frac{\tau^{2}-|\xi|^{2}}{\tau-\xi\cdot\omega}.

A key point is that the proof of
Lemma

4.1
relies on the inverse convolution formula (
4.4
) on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
.

4.4.
Spacetime Fourier transform of
\widetilde{Q}_{0}^{\pm,\pm}
and change of variables

Using the formula from
Lemma

4.1
, one then has for
\tau\geqslant|\xi|

\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{t}\to\tau,\,\tilde{x}\to\xi}(\widetilde{Q}_{0}^{+,+})=\pi^{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\alpha^{2}\hat{\tilde{f}}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\omega\right)\hat{\tilde{f}}\left(\xi-\frac{\alpha}{2}\omega\right)\mathrm{d}^{2}\omega,

so by Cauchy–Schwarz

|\mathcal{F}(\widetilde{Q}_{0}^{+,+})|^{2}\leqslant\pi^{2}|\mathbb{S}^{2}|\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\alpha^{4}\left|\hat{\tilde{f}}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\omega\right)\right|^{2}\left|\hat{\tilde{f}}\left(\xi-\frac{\alpha}{2}\omega\right)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}^{2}\omega.

One can show, from the definition of
\alpha
, that
\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\geqslant 1
, so changing variables
\mathrm{d}\tau=\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{\mathrm{d}\alpha}\mathrm{d}\alpha
, Plancherel’s theorem gives

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{Q}_{0}^{+,+}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\tilde{t},\tilde{x}})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\mathcal{F}(\widetilde{Q}_{0}^{+,+})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\tau,\xi})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}\xi\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\mathrm{d}^{2}\omega\,\alpha^{4}\left|\hat{\tilde{f}}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\omega\right)\right|^{2}\left|\hat{\tilde{f}}\left(\xi-\frac{\alpha}{2}\omega\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}\xi\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\alpha\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\mathrm{d}^{2}\omega\,\alpha^{4}\left|\hat{\tilde{f}}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\omega\right)\right|^{2}\left|\hat{\tilde{f}}\left(\xi-\frac{\alpha}{2}\omega\right)\right|^{2}.

Finally, reinstating the variable
\xi^{\prime}=\frac{\alpha}{2}\omega
,
\mathrm{d}\xi^{\prime}=\alpha^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\alpha\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\omega
and using Plancherel again, one arrives at

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{Q}_{0}^{+,+}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{4})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}\xi\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}\xi^{\prime}\,|\xi^{\prime}|^{2}|\hat{\tilde{f}}(\xi-\xi^{\prime})|^{2}|\hat{\tilde{f}}(\xi^{\prime})|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\tilde{f}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\tilde{f}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}.

An important point in this step is that the reinstating of the variable
\xi^{\prime}
involves mixing the time and space Fourier variables since
\alpha=\alpha(\xi,\tau,\omega)
. The parts
\widetilde{Q}_{0}^{+,-}
and
\widetilde{Q}_{0}^{-,-}
may be estimated similarly, and one concludes that

\|\widetilde{Q}_{0}(\tilde{\phi},\tilde{\phi})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})}\lesssim\|\tilde{f}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\tilde{f}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}.

5.
Setup on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}

On
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}
, the spatial Fourier variables will be discrete since
\mathbb{S}^{3}
is compact, however the temporal Fourier variable is in general continuous (cf.
Appendix

A
for our setup of Fourier transforms on Lie groups). To deal with this we use the time periodicity of (
3.4
).

5.1.
Time periodicity

Solutions to (
3.4
) are periodic in time; this may be seen at the level of twistor formulae (see §9.4 of
[
PR86
]
), or as follows. Suppose a
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3})
solution to (
3.4
) is given. Taking the operator-valued Fourier transform in the
\mathrm{SU}(2)
variables, one has

\partial_{t}^{2}\hat{\phi}(\pi_{m})+m(m+2)\hat{\phi}(\pi_{m})+\hat{\phi}(\pi_{m})=0,

where
m(m+2)+1=(m+1)^{2}
is a perfect square. This has the general solution

(5.1)

\hat{\phi}(\pi_{m})(t)=A_{m}\sin((m+1)t)+B_{m}\cos((m+1)t)

for constant
(m+1)\times(m+1)
matrices
A_{m}
and
B_{m}
, and in particular for every
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
the mode
\hat{\phi}(\pi_{m})(t)
is
2\pi
-periodic in
t
. By the Peter–Weyl theorem, the full solution
\phi
can be recovered from the Fourier series

\phi(t,x)=\sum_{m\geqslant 0}(m+1)\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\phi}(\pi_{m})(t)\pi_{m}(x)),

where
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathrm{SU}(2)
, and is also
2\pi
-periodic in
t
. It follows that solutions to (
3.4
) can be identified with a subspace of the space of real-valued functions on
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}\simeq\mathrm{U}(1)\times\mathrm{SU}(2)
.

5.2.
Positive/negative frequency splitting

In the case of data
(\phi,\partial_{t}\phi)|_{t=0}=(0,f)
, the solution (
5.1
) becomes

\hat{\phi}(\pi_{m})=\frac{\sin((m+1)t)}{(m+1)}\hat{f}(\pi_{m}).

In contrast to the continuous case, the denominator here never vanishes, so there is no need to consider a subspace of data
\hat{f}
which vanishes around the origin. Straight away we then have

\phi(t,x)=\sum_{m\geqslant 0}(m+1)\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\phi}(\pi_{m})(t)\pi_{m}(x))=\sum_{m\geqslant 0}\sin((m+1)t)\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{f}(\pi_{m})\pi_{m}(x)).

Analogously to before, we define the positive and negative frequency parts of
\phi
by

(5.2)

\phi^{\pm}(t,x)=\sum_{m\geqslant 0}e^{\pm i(m+1)t}\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{f}(\pi_{m})\pi_{m}(x)).

These solve
\Box\phi^{\pm}+\phi^{\pm}=0
and determine the full solution via
\phi=\frac{1}{2i}(\phi^{+}-\phi^{-})
, so we restrict our analysis to
\phi^{\pm}
.

5.3.
Spacetime Fourier transform of
\phi^{\pm}

To write
\phi^{\pm}
as a spacetime Fourier series, we first use the periodicity of
\phi^{\pm}(t,x)
in
t
. These can then be written as standard Fourier series on
\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{R}\simeq\mathbb{S}^{1}\simeq\mathrm{U}(1)
,

\phi^{\pm}(t,x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{int}\phi^{\pm}_{n}(x),

where, by comparing to (
5.2
),

(5.3)

\phi^{\pm}_{n}(x)=\begin{cases}\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{f}(\pi_{\pm n-1})\pi_{\pm n-1}(x))\qquad\text{for }\pm n\geqslant 1,\\
\hskip 50.0pt0\hskip 75.0pt\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Hence the spacetime Fourier coefficient
\mathcal{F}(\phi^{\pm})
of
\phi^{\pm}
is

\mathcal{F}(\phi^{\pm})(\pi_{m})_{n}=\frac{1}{(m+1)}\hat{f}(\pi_{m})\delta_{m,\pm n-1},

where
m\geqslant 0
is the
\mathrm{SU}(2)
index, and
n
is the
\mathrm{U}(1)
index.

5.4.
Spacetime Fourier transform of
Q_{0}^{\pm,\pm}

Given a non-abelian Lie group
\mathrm{G}
and two functions
f,\,g:\mathrm{G}\to\mathbb{R}
, their convolution is defined by

(f*g)(x)=\int_{\mathrm{G}}f(y)g(xy^{-1})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y),

from which it follows that

\widehat{(f*g)}(\pi)=\hat{f}(\pi)\hat{g}(\pi).

This is the
forward
convolution theorem. However, since
\hat{f}(\pi)
and
\hat{g}(\pi)
are operators, there is in general no natural way of defining the convolution
\hat{f}(\pi)*\hat{g}(\pi)
, as there is no group structure on the unitary dual
\hat{\mathrm{G}}
. Instead, we proceed in a different way to obtain the spacetime Fourier transform of
Q_{0}^{\pm,\pm}
, by using the commutativity of the
\mathrm{U}(1)
factor of
\mathrm{U}(1)\times\mathrm{SU}(2)
. The first step is to express
\mathcal{F}(Q_{0}^{\pm,\pm})
in terms of
\mathcal{F}(\phi^{\pm}\psi^{\pm})
.

Lemma 5.1
.

The spacetime Fourier coefficients
\mathcal{F}(Q_{0}^{\pm,\pm})(\pi_{m})_{n}
of
Q_{0}^{\pm,\pm}=Q_{0}(\phi^{\pm},\psi^{\pm})
are given by

\mathcal{F}(Q_{0}^{\pm,\pm})(\pi_{m})_{n}=\frac{1}{2}(1+(m+1)^{2}-n^{2})\mathcal{F}(\phi^{\pm}\psi^{\pm})(\pi_{m})_{n}.

Proof.

This follows from the identity

2Q_{0}(\phi^{\pm},\psi^{\pm})=(2+\Box)\phi^{\pm}\psi^{\pm}

and the fact that
\Box
is formally self-adjoint on
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}
. Taking the spacetime Fourier transform of this, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(Q_{0}^{\pm,\pm})(\pi_{m})_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}(\phi^{\pm}\psi^{\pm})(\pi_{m})_{n}+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\int_{\mathrm{SU}(2)}\mathrm{e}^{-int}\pi_{m}(x^{-1})(\Box(\phi^{\pm}\psi^{\pm}))(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}(\phi^{\pm}\psi^{\pm})(\pi_{m})_{n}+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\int_{\mathrm{SU}(2)}(\phi^{\pm}\psi^{\pm})(t,x)\Box(\mathrm{e}^{-int}\pi_{m}(x^{-1}))\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(1+(m+1)^{2}-n^{2})\mathcal{F}(\phi^{\pm}\psi^{\pm})(\pi_{m})_{n}.

∎

While an inverse convolution theorem on the whole of
\mathrm{U}(1)\times\mathrm{SU}(2)
is not available, it is still available on the abelian
\mathrm{U}(1)
factor. The
\mathrm{U}(1)
-Fourier coefficients of
(\phi^{\pm}\psi^{\pm})
are

(\phi^{\pm}\psi^{\pm})_{n}=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\phi^{\pm}_{l}\psi^{\pm}_{n-l},

so that, using (
5.3
),

(5.4)

(\phi^{+}\psi^{+})_{n}(x)=\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{f}(\pi_{l-1})\pi_{l-1}(x))\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{g}(\pi_{n-l-1})\pi_{n-l-1}(x))

for the
(+,+)
product and

(5.5)

(\phi^{+}\psi^{-})_{n}(x)=\sum_{l=n+1}^{\infty}\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{f}(\pi_{l-1})\pi_{l-1}(x))\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{g}(\pi_{l-n-1})\pi_{l-n-1}(x))

for the
(+,-)
product. The expressions for the
(-,+)
and
(-,-)
products are similar. Integrating (
5.4
), (
5.5
) against
\pi_{m}(x^{-1})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)
, we have

\mathcal{F}(\phi^{+}\psi^{+})(\pi_{m})_{n}=\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}\varpi_{l}^{+,+}(\pi_{m})_{n}~~\quad\text{and}~~\quad\mathcal{F}(\phi^{+}\psi^{-})(\pi_{m})_{n}=\sum_{l=n+1}^{\infty}\varpi^{+,-}_{l}(\pi_{m})_{n},

where

\displaystyle\varpi^{+,+}_{l}(\pi_{m})_{n}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{def}}{=}\int_{\mathrm{SU}(2)}\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{f}(\pi_{l-1})\pi_{l-1}(x))\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{g}(\pi_{n-l-1})\pi_{n-l-1}(x))\pi_{m}(x^{-1})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\hat{f}(\pi_{l-1})_{ji}\hat{g}(\pi_{n-l-1})_{sr}\int_{\mathrm{SU}(2)}(\pi_{l-1})_{ij}(\pi_{n-l-1})_{rs}\pi_{m}^{\dagger}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

and

\varpi^{+,-}_{l}(\pi_{m})_{n}=\hat{f}(\pi_{l-1})_{ji}\hat{g}(\pi_{l-n-1})_{sr}\int_{\mathrm{SU}(2)}(\pi_{l-1})_{ij}(\pi_{l-n-1})_{rs}\pi_{m}^{\dagger}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

Computing
\varpi_{l}^{+,\pm}(\pi_{m})_{n}
encapsulates the key novelty of this calculation as compared to the
\mathbb{R}^{1+3}
case: as
\mathrm{SU}(2)
is non-abelian, the tensor product
\pi_{l}\otimes\pi_{n-l}
is not isomorphic to a single irreducible representation
\pi_{n}
. Instead, for
\mathrm{SU}(2)
the product
\pi_{a}\otimes\pi_{b}
is isomorphic to the Clebsch–Gordan expansion

(5.6)

\pi_{a}\otimes\pi_{b}=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\min\{a,b\}}\pi_{|a-b|+2k}

(see, for example, §7 of
[
Far08
]
). For calculation we will need the expansion (
5.6
) with an explicit choice of the representations
\pi_{m}
; we use Wigner’s D-matrices
[
Wig59
]
.

5.5.
Wigner’s D-matrices and Clebsch–Gordan expansions

Wigner’s D-matrices are explicit unitary matrix representations of
\pi_{m}
,
m\geqslant 0
. The rows and columns of
(\pi_{m})_{ij}
are labelled by the integers and half-integers

i,j=-\frac{m}{2},-\frac{m}{2}+1,\dots,\frac{m}{2}-1,\frac{m}{2},

and a general expression for all
(\pi_{m})_{ij}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
, where
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
are the Euler angles parametrizing
\mathrm{SU}(2)
, can be found on p. 167 of
[
Wig59
]
. As an example, the first three representations are given by

\pi_{0}=1,\qquad\pi_{1}=\pm\left(\begin{array}[]{lr}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}i\alpha}\left(\cos\frac{1}{2}\beta\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}i\gamma}&-\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}i\alpha}\left(\sin\frac{1}{2}\beta\right)\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}i\gamma}\\
\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}i\alpha}\left(\sin\frac{1}{2}\beta\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}i\gamma}&\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}i\alpha}\left(\cos\frac{1}{2}\beta\right)\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}i\gamma}\end{array}\right),

and

\pi_{2}=\left(\begin{array}[]{lcr}\mathrm{e}^{-i\alpha}\frac{1}{2}(1+\cos\beta)\mathrm{e}^{-i\gamma}&-\mathrm{e}^{-i\alpha}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sin\beta&\mathrm{e}^{-i\alpha}\frac{1}{2}(1-\cos\beta)\mathrm{e}^{i\gamma}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\sin\beta)\mathrm{e}^{-i\gamma}&\cos\beta&-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\sin\beta)\mathrm{e}^{i\gamma}\\
\mathrm{e}^{i\alpha}\frac{1}{2}(1-\cos\beta)\mathrm{e}^{-i\gamma}&\mathrm{e}^{i\alpha}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sin\beta&\mathrm{e}^{i\alpha}\frac{1}{2}(1+\cos\beta)\mathrm{e}^{i\gamma}\end{array}\right).

Here
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in[-\pi,\pi]
, and the rows and columns of
\pi_{1}
are labelled by
-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}
, and of
\pi_{2}
by
-1,0,1
. In terms of Wigner’s D-matrices, an indexed version of the Clebsch–Gordan expansion (
5.6
) is given by (see eq. (17.16b) in
[
Wig59
]
, or, for example, eq. (4.25) in
[
Ros57
]
)

(5.7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\pi_{a})_{ij}(\pi_{b})_{rs}&=\sum_{k=0}^{\min\{a,b\}}\left\langle\left.\frac{a}{2}\,i\,\frac{b}{2}\,r\,\right|\frac{|a-b|+2k}{2}\,(i+r)\right\rangle\\
&\hskip 36.0pt\times\left\langle\left.\frac{a}{2}\,j\,\frac{b}{2}\,s\,\right|\frac{|a-b|+2k}{2}\,(j+s)\right\rangle(\pi_{|a-b|+2k})_{(i+r)(j+s)},\end{split}

where
\langle j_{1}\,m_{1}\,j_{2}\,m_{2}|j_{3}\,m_{3}\rangle
are the standard Clebsch–Gordan coefficients of
\mathrm{SU}(2)
. The Clebsch–Gordan coefficients are probability amplitudes, so in particular they are uniformly bounded by 1 in all six variables
j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}
. They do not vanish only if
-j_{1}\leqslant m_{1}\leqslant j_{1}
,
-j_{2}\leqslant m_{2}\leqslant j_{2}
,
-j_{3}\leqslant m_{3}\leqslant j_{3}
, and
m_{1}+m_{2}=m_{3}
, and further obey the orthogonality relations (cf. eqs. (3.7), (3.9) in
[
Ros57
]
)

(5.8)

\displaystyle\sum_{j_{3}}\sum_{m_{3}}\langle j_{1}\,m_{1}\,j_{2}\,m_{2}|j_{3}\,m_{3}\rangle\langle j_{1}\,m_{1}^{\prime}\,j_{2}\,m_{2}^{\prime}|j_{3}\,m_{3}\rangle=\delta_{m_{1},m_{1}^{\prime}}\delta_{m_{2},m_{2}^{\prime}},

(5.9)

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{1}}\sum_{m_{2}}\langle j_{1}\,m_{1}\,j_{2}\,m_{2}|j_{3}\,m_{3}\rangle\langle j_{1}\,m_{1}\,j_{2}\,m_{2}|j_{3}^{\prime}\,m_{3}^{\prime}\rangle=\delta_{j_{3},j_{3}^{\prime}}\delta_{m_{3},m_{3}^{\prime}},

where in (
5.8
) the sums run over
|j_{1}-j_{2}|\leqslant j_{3}\leqslant j_{1}+j_{2}
and
-j_{3}\leqslant m_{3}\leqslant j_{3}
, and in (
5.9
) the sums run over
-j_{1,2}\leqslant m_{1,2}\leqslant j_{1,2}
. Using (
5.7
) and the Schur orthogonality relations, we therefore have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{SU}(2)}(\pi_{l-1})_{ij}(\pi_{n-l-1})_{rs}(\pi^{\dagger}_{m})_{pq}\,\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\min\{l-1,n-l-1\}}\left\langle\left.\frac{l-1}{2}\,i\,\frac{n-l-1}{2}\,r\right|\frac{|n-2l|+2k}{2}\,(i+r)\right\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt\times\left\langle\left.\frac{l-1}{2}\,j\,\frac{n-l-1}{2}\,s\right|\frac{|n-2l|+2k}{2}\,(j+s)\right\rangle\int_{\mathrm{SU}(2)}(\pi_{|n-2l|+2k})_{(i+r)(j+s)}(\pi^{\dagger}_{m})_{pq}\,\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\min\{l-1,n-l-1\}}\left\langle\left.\frac{l-1}{2}\,i\,\frac{n-l-1}{2}\,r\right|\frac{|n-2l|+2k}{2}\,(i+r)\right\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt\times\left\langle\left.\frac{l-1}{2}\,j\,\frac{n-l-1}{2}\,s\right|\frac{|n-2l|+2k}{2}\,(j+s)\right\rangle\frac{1}{(m+1)}\delta_{i+r,p}\delta_{j+s,q}\delta_{|n-2l|+2k,m}

provided

|n-2l|\leqslant m\leqslant|n-2l|+2\min\{l-1,n-l-1\}\iff|n-2l|\leqslant m\leqslant n-2

so that
\pi_{m}
is in the range of the Clebsch–Gordan expansion (
5.7
), and

\frac{m-|n-2l|}{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\iff m\equiv n\mod 2.

We therefore obtain

(5.10)

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\varpi^{+,+}_{l}(\pi_{m})_{n})_{pq}&=\frac{1}{(m+1)}\hat{f}(\pi_{l-1})_{ji}\hat{g}(\pi_{n-l-1})_{(q-j)(p-i)}\\
&\times\left\langle\left.\frac{l-1}{2}\,i\,\frac{n-l-1}{2}\,(p-i)\right|\frac{m}{2}\,p\right\rangle\left\langle\left.\frac{l-1}{2}\,j\,\frac{n-l-1}{2}\,(q-j)\right|\frac{m}{2}\,q\right\rangle\chi_{1}\chi_{2},\end{split}

where
\chi_{1}=\chi(|n-2l|\leqslant m\leqslant n-2)
and
\chi_{2}=\chi(m\equiv n\mod 2)
, where
\chi
is the indicator function. Similarly, we obtain

(5.11)

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\varpi^{+,-}_{l}(\pi_{m})_{n})_{pq}&=\frac{1}{(m+1)}\hat{f}(\pi_{l-1})_{ji}\hat{g}(\pi_{l-n-1})_{(q-j)(p-i)}\\
&\times\left\langle\left.\frac{l-1}{2}\,i\,\frac{l-n-1}{2}\,(p-i)\,\right|\,\frac{m}{2}\,p\right\rangle\left\langle\left.\frac{l-1}{2}\,j\,\frac{l-n-1}{2}\,(q-j)\,\right|\,\frac{m}{2}\,q\right\rangle\chi_{2}\chi_{3},\end{split}

where
\chi_{3}=\chi(|n|\leqslant m\leqslant-n+2(l-1))
, and where in both cases for a well-defined representation
\pi_{l-1}
we require
l\geqslant 1
. The presence of
\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{3}
encodes the multi-dimensional nature of the irreducible representations of
\mathrm{SU}(2)
and the fact that the product
\pi_{l}\otimes\pi_{n-l}
has a non-trivial Clebsch–Gordan expansion. For the convolutions
\varpi^{+,\pm}_{l}(\pi_{m})_{n}
we have the following discrete Fourier-side version of Young’s inequality.

Lemma 5.2
.

For the matrices
\varpi^{+,\pm}_{l}(\pi_{m})_{n}
given by (
5.10
) and (
5.11
) we have the bounds

\sum_{m\geqslant 0}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(m+1)\varpi^{+,\pm}_{l}(\pi_{m})_{n}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\leqslant{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\hat{f}(\pi_{l-1})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\hat{g}(\pi_{\pm(n-l)-1})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}.

Proof.

We write down the proof for
\varpi^{+,+}_{l}(\pi_{m})_{n}
, the calculation for
\varpi^{+,-}_{l}(\pi_{m})_{n}
being the same. For more efficient notation, we write here

\sum_{m\geqslant 0}\chi_{1}\chi_{2}=\sum_{m}

and

\langle j_{1}\,m_{1}\,j_{2}\,m_{2}\,|\,j_{3}\,m_{3}\rangle=\mathcal{C}^{(j_{1}j_{2})}_{j_{3}m_{1}m_{2}},

with the understanding that the ranges of the sums below are restricted to the ranges in which the Clebsch–Gordan coefficients involved are non-zero. Then the orthogonality relations (
5.8
) and (
5.9
) read

(5.12)

\displaystyle\sum_{j_{3}}\sum_{m_{3}}\mathcal{C}^{(j_{1}j_{2})}_{j_{3}m_{1}m_{2}}\mathcal{C}^{(j_{1}j_{2})}_{j_{3}m^{\prime}_{1}m_{2}^{\prime}}=\delta_{m_{1},m_{1}^{\prime}}\delta_{m_{2},m_{2}^{\prime}},

(5.13)

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{1}}\sum_{m_{2}}\mathcal{C}^{(j_{1}j_{2})}_{j_{3}m_{1}m_{2}}\mathcal{C}^{(j_{1}j_{2})}_{j^{\prime}_{3}m_{1}m_{2}}=\delta_{j_{3},j_{3}^{\prime}}.

Explicitly expanding the Frobenius norm squared of
(m+1)\varpi^{+,+}_{l}(\pi_{m})_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{m\geqslant 0}

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(m+1)\varpi^{+,+}_{l}(\pi_{m})_{n}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}=\sum_{m}\sum_{p,q}\sum_{i,j}\sum_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\hat{f}(\pi_{l-1})_{ji}\overline{\hat{f}(\pi_{l-1})}_{j^{\prime}i^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\times\hat{g}(\pi_{n-l-1})_{(q-j)(p-i)}\overline{\hat{g}(\pi_{n-l-1})}_{(q-j^{\prime})(p-i^{\prime})}\mathcal{C}^{\left(\frac{l-1}{2}\frac{n-l-1}{2}\right)}_{\frac{m}{2}i(p-i)}\mathcal{C}^{\left(\frac{l-1}{2}\frac{n-l-1}{2}\right)}_{\frac{m}{2}i^{\prime}(p-i^{\prime})}\mathcal{C}^{\left(\frac{l-1}{2}\frac{n-l-1}{2}\right)}_{\frac{m}{2}j(q-j)}\mathcal{C}^{\left(\frac{l-1}{2}\frac{n-l-1}{2}\right)}_{\frac{m}{2}j^{\prime}(q-j^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\sum_{p,q}\left|\sum_{i,j}\mathcal{C}^{\left(\frac{l-1}{2}\frac{n-l-1}{2}\right)}_{\frac{m}{2}i(p-i)}\mathcal{C}^{\left(\frac{l-1}{2}\frac{n-l-1}{2}\right)}_{\frac{m}{2}j(q-j)}\hat{f}(\pi_{l-1})_{ji}\hat{g}(\pi_{n-l-1})_{(q-j)(p-i)}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\sum_{r,s}\left|\sum_{i,j}\mathcal{C}^{\left(\frac{l-1}{2}\frac{n-l-1}{2}\right)}_{\frac{m}{2}ir}\mathcal{C}^{\left(\frac{l-1}{2}\frac{n-l-1}{2}\right)}_{\frac{m}{2}js}\hat{f}(\pi_{l-1})_{ji}\hat{g}(\pi_{n-l-1})_{sr}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\hat{g}(\pi_{n-l-1})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\sum_{m}\sum_{r,s}\left|\sum_{i,j}\mathcal{C}^{\left(\frac{l-1}{2}\frac{n-l-1}{2}\right)}_{\frac{m}{2}ir}\mathcal{C}^{\left(\frac{l-1}{2}\frac{n-l-1}{2}\right)}_{\frac{m}{2}js}\hat{f}(\pi_{l-1})_{ji}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\hat{g}(\pi_{n-l-1})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\sum_{m}\sum_{m^{\prime}}\sum_{r,s}\left|\sum_{i,j}\mathcal{C}^{\left(\frac{l-1}{2}\frac{n-l-1}{2}\right)}_{\frac{m}{2}ir}\mathcal{C}^{\left(\frac{l-1}{2}\frac{n-l-1}{2}\right)}_{\frac{m^{\prime}}{2}js}\hat{f}(\pi_{l-1})_{ji}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle={\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\hat{g}(\pi_{n-l-1})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\sum_{m}\sum_{r}\sum_{i,i^{\prime}}\bigg[\mathcal{C}^{\left(\frac{l-1}{2}\frac{n-l-1}{2}\right)}_{\frac{m}{2}ir}\mathcal{C}^{\left(\frac{l-1}{2}\frac{n-l-1}{2}\right)}_{\frac{m}{2}i^{\prime}r}

\displaystyle\hskip 68.0pt\times\sum_{m^{\prime}}\sum_{s}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}}\mathcal{C}^{\left(\frac{l-1}{2}\frac{n-l-1}{2}\right)}_{\frac{m^{\prime}}{2}js}\mathcal{C}^{\left(\frac{l-1}{2}\frac{n-l-1}{2}\right)}_{\frac{m^{\prime}}{2}j^{\prime}s}\hat{f}(\pi_{l-1})_{ji}\overline{\hat{f}(\pi_{l-1})}_{j^{\prime}i^{\prime}}\bigg]

\displaystyle={\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\hat{f}(\pi_{l-1})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\hat{g}(\pi_{n-l-1})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2},

where in the last line we used (
5.12
) twice.
∎

6.
Proof of the Basic Estimate

We now turn to the proof of the basic estimate of
Theorem

3.1
, i.e.

\|Q_{0}(\phi,\psi)\|^{2}_{L^{2}([-\pi,\pi]\times\mathbb{S}^{3})}\lesssim\|f\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}\|g\|^{2}_{H^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}.

Noting that
-4Q_{0}(\phi,\psi)=Q_{0}^{+,+}-Q_{0}^{+,-}-Q_{0}^{-,+}+Q_{0}^{-,-}
, it is enough to estimate each of the four parts
Q_{0}^{\pm,\pm}
. Clearly the estimates for
Q_{0}^{+,+}
and
Q_{0}^{-,-}
are entirely analogous, as are those for
Q_{0}^{+,-}
and
Q_{0}^{-,+}
. It is therefore enough to show that
Q_{0}^{+,+}
and
Q_{0}^{+,-}
are suitably controlled. We record the following auxiliary lemma.

Lemma 6.1
.

For any fixed
n\in\mathbb{Z}
the function
\lambda:\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\lambda(m;n)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\frac{(1+(m+1)^{2}-n^{2})^{2}}{(m+1)}

is decreasing for
m\in[0,\sqrt{n^{2}-1}-1)
, has a global minimum
\lambda(m_{*})=0
at
m_{*}=\sqrt{n^{2}-1}-1
, and is increasing for
m\in(\sqrt{n^{2}-1}-1,\infty)
.

Proof.

This follows directly from computing the derivative in
m
,

\lambda^{\prime}(m;n)=\frac{(3(m+1)^{2}+(n^{2}-1))}{(m+1)^{2}}(1+(m+1)^{2}-n^{2}),

where the first factor in the above is manifestly positive for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
m\geqslant 0
. ∎

6.1.
Estimate for
Q_{0}^{+,+}

By Plancherel’s theorem, we compute, noting that we must have
n\geqslant 2
due to
\chi_{1}=\chi(|n-2l|\leqslant m\leqslant n-2)
,

(6.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|Q_{0}^{+,+}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{3})}&\simeq\sum_{n\geqslant 2}\sum_{m\geqslant 0}(m+1){\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathcal{F}(Q_{0}^{+,+})(\pi_{m})_{n}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\\
\ &\simeq\sum_{n\geqslant 2}\sum_{m\geqslant 0}(1+(m+1)^{2}-n^{2})^{2}(m+1){\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathcal{F}(\phi^{+}\psi^{+})(\pi_{m})_{n}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\\
&\simeq\sum_{n\geqslant 2}\sum_{m\geqslant 0}(1+(m+1)^{2}-n^{2})^{2}(m+1)^{-1}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}(m+1)\varpi^{+,+}_{l}(\pi_{m})_{n}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\end{split}

We now perform the splitting, for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}(m+1)\varpi^{+,+}_{l}(\pi_{m})_{n}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}\frac{(1+|n-2l|)l}{(n-l)^{1-2\varepsilon}}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(m+1)\varpi^{+,+}_{l}(\pi_{m})_{n}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{(n-k)^{1-2\varepsilon}}{(1+|n-2k|)k},

where the last factor in the above converges as
n\to\infty
for
\varepsilon>0
by
Lemma

B.1
with
\beta_{0}=0
,
\alpha=1
,
\beta=-1+2\varepsilon
. Now note that in estimate (
6.1
) the function
\lambda(m)=(1+(m+1)^{2}-n^{2})^{2}(m+1)^{-1}
is decreasing in
m
in the range
|n-2l|\leqslant m\leqslant n-2
(cf.
Lemma

6.1
), so its maximum in this range is attained at
m=|n-2l|
. Using
Lemma

5.2
, we have

(6.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|Q_{0}^{+,+}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{3})}&\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\sum_{n\geqslant 2}\sum_{m\geqslant 0}\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}(1+(m+1)^{2}-n^{2})^{2}(m+1)^{-1}\frac{(1+|n-2l|)l}{(n-l)^{1-2\varepsilon}}\\
&\times{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(m+1)\varpi^{+,+}_{l}(\pi_{m})_{n}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\\
&\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\sum_{n\geqslant 2}\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}\frac{(1+(|n-2l|+1)^{2}-n^{2})^{2}}{(n-l)^{1-2\varepsilon}}l{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\hat{f}(\pi_{l-1})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\hat{g}(\pi_{n-l-1})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\\

&\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\sum_{l\geqslant 1}\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\frac{k^{2}l^{2}}{k^{1-2\varepsilon}}l{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\hat{f}(\pi_{l-1})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\hat{g}(\pi_{k-1})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\\
&\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\|f\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}\|g\|^{2}_{H^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}.\end{split}

6.2.
Estimate for

Here we must split the estimate into the two cases

and

. We have

\displaystyle\|Q_{0}^{+,-}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{3})}

\displaystyle\simeq\bigg[\sum_{n\geqslant 0}+\sum_{n<0}\bigg]\underbrace{\sum_{m\geqslant 0}(1+(m+1)^{2}-n^{2})^{2}(m+1){\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\geqslant n+1\\

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\|f\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}\|g\|^{2}_{H^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}\,.

.

Here we proceed similarly; for any fixed

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geqslant 0}(\star)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\sum_{m\geqslant 0}(1+(m+1)^{2}-n^{2})(m+1)^{-1}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\sum_{l\geqslant n+1}(m+1)\varpi^{+,-}_{l}(\pi_{m})_{n}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\sum_{m\geqslant 0}(1+(m+1)^{2}-n^{2})^{2}(m+1)^{-1}\sum_{l\geqslant n+1}l(l-n)^{2\varepsilon}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(m+1)\varpi^{+,-}_{l}(\pi_{m})_{n}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{\lesssim}\times\sum_{k\geqslant n+1}k^{-1}(k-n)^{-2\varepsilon},

where now the range of

is

, so that

. Hence, using

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geqslant 0}(\star)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\sum_{m\geqslant 0}(1+(m+1)^{2}-n^{2})^{2}(m+1)^{-1}\sum_{l\geqslant n+1}l(l-n)^{2\varepsilon}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(m+1)\varpi^{+,-}_{l}(\pi_{m})_{n}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{\lesssim}\times\underbrace{\sum_{k\geqslant n+1}(k-n)^{-1-2\varepsilon}}_{\leqslant\zeta(1+2\varepsilon)<\infty}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\frac{(1+(2l-n-1))^{2}-n^{2})^{2}}{(2l-n-1)}\sum_{l\geqslant n+1}l(l-n)^{2\varepsilon}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\hat{g}(\pi_{l-n-1})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\hat{f}(\pi_{l-1})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\sum_{l\geqslant n+1}\frac{l^{2}(l-n)^{2}}{(l-n)}l(l-n)^{2\varepsilon}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\hat{g}(\pi_{l-n-1})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\hat{f}(\pi_{l-1})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\|f\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}\|g\|^{2}_{H^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}\,.

We have therefore shown that for any fixed

Putting this together with estimate (

For spatial derivatives it then follows trivially that

\not{\nabla}Q_{0}(\phi,\psi)(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}\not{\nabla}Q_{0}(\Phi_{s}(t-s,x),\Psi_{r}(t-r,x))\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}r.

3.6

(8.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\not{\nabla}Q_{0}(\phi,\psi)\|_{L^{2}([-\pi,\pi];H^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{3}))}&\lesssim\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\|\not{\nabla}Q_{0}(\Phi_{s}(t-s,x),\Psi_{r}(t-r,x)\|_{L^{2}([-\pi,\pi];H^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{3}))}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}r\\

&\times\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\bigg(\|\Psi_{r}(-r,\cdot)\|_{H^{2+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}+\|\partial_{t}\Psi_{r}(-r,\cdot)\|_{H^{1+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}\bigg)\mathrm{d}r\\
&\lesssim\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\|F(s,\cdot)\|_{H^{1+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}\,\mathrm{d}s\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\|G(r,\cdot)\|_{H^{1+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}\,\mathrm{d}r,\end{split}

where in the last line we used the energy inequality for
\Phi_{s}
and
\Psi_{r}
, in evolution from time
-s
(resp.
-r

(f_{0},0,0)

(0,f_{1},0)
and

(and the same for

)

.

9.
Proof of the Weighted Estimate on

Finally, we turn to the proof of the conformally weighted estimate of

\phi

\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}
with data
(f_{0},f_{1})
, then
\tilde{\phi}=\Omega\phi
satisfies

\Box_{\mathbb{R}^{1+3}}\tilde{\phi}=0

on
\mathbb{R}^{1+3}
with data
(\tilde{f}_{0},\tilde{f}_{1})=(\omega f_{0},\omega^{2}f_{1})
, where
\omega=2(1+\tilde{r}^{2})^{-1}=\Omega|_{\tilde{t}=0}
, and
\Omega
is the conformal factor

(9.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Omega&=2\left(1+(\tilde{t}-\tilde{r})^{2}\right)^{-1/2}\left(1+(\tilde{t}+\tilde{r})^{2}\right)^{-1/2}\\
&=2\cos\left(\frac{t-z}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{t+z}{2}\right),\end{split}

where
\tilde{t}
,
\tilde{r}
are the usual coordinates on
\mathbb{R}^{1+3}
and
z=\arctan(\tilde{t}+\tilde{r})-\arctan(\tilde{t}-\tilde{r})
is one of the angles on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
, the metric
g_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}
being given by
\mathrm{d}z^{2}+(\sin^{2}z)g_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}
. Note that
\Omega
is a smooth function on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}
, and we have

Q_{0}(\Omega,\Omega)=2\Omega\sin\left(\frac{t-z}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{t+z}{2}\right).

Writing
\widetilde{Q}_{0}(\tilde{\phi},\tilde{\psi})
to denote the null form on
(\mathbb{R}^{1+3},\eta_{ab})
,

\widetilde{Q}_{0}(\tilde{\phi},\tilde{\psi})=\tilde{\nabla}_{a}\tilde{\phi}\tilde{\nabla}^{a}\tilde{\psi},

a calculation shows

(9.2)

\widetilde{Q}_{0}(\tilde{\phi},\tilde{\psi})=\Omega^{4}Q_{0}(\phi,\psi)+\Omega^{3}\phi Q_{0}(\Omega,\psi)+\Omega^{3}\psi Q_{0}(\Omega,\phi)+\Omega^{2}\phi\psi Q_{0}(\Omega,\Omega),

or, taking into account the above expression for
Q_{0}(\Omega,\Omega)
,

(9.3)

\widetilde{Q}_{0}(\tilde{\phi},\tilde{\psi})=\Omega^{4}Q_{0}(\phi,\psi)+\Omega^{3}\left(\mathfrak{a}^{\mu}\phi\nabla_{\mu}\psi+\mathfrak{b}^{\mu}\psi\nabla_{\mu}\phi+\mathfrak{c}\phi\psi\right)

for a smooth function
\mathfrak{c}
and smooth vector fields
\mathfrak{a}^{\mu}
,
\mathfrak{b}^{\mu}
on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}
. Note that the standard foliation of
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}
which was used for our estimates in the previous sections corresponds to a hyperboloidal foliation on
\mathbb{R}^{1+3}
, given by the level sets
\Sigma_{t}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{3}
of the function

t=\arctan(\tilde{t}+\tilde{r})+\arctan(\tilde{t}-\tilde{r})\in(-\pi,\pi),

with unit normal (with respect to
\eta_{ab}=\mathrm{d}\tilde{t}^{2}-\mathrm{d}\tilde{r}^{2}-\tilde{r}^{2}g_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}
)

T^{a}\partial_{a}=\left(1+2(\tilde{t}^{2}+\tilde{r}^{2})+(\tilde{t}^{2}-\tilde{r}^{2})^{2}\right)^{-1}\left(2(1+\tilde{t}^{2}+\tilde{r}^{2})\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{t}}+4\tilde{t}\tilde{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{r}}\right).

The metric on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}
is related to the Minkowski metric
\eta_{ab}
by

g^{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}}_{ab}=\mathrm{d}t^{2}-g_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}=\Omega^{2}\eta_{ab},

so writing
\Omega^{-2}g_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}\overset{\text{def}}{=}g_{\Sigma_{t}}
, we have
\dvol_{\Sigma_{t}}=\Omega^{-3}\dvol_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}
, and

\eta_{ab}=\Omega^{-2}\mathrm{d}t^{2}-g_{\Sigma_{t}},

where the volume form of
g^{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}}
is related to the volume form of
\eta
by
\dvol_{\eta}=\Omega^{-4}\mathrm{d}t\wedge\dvol_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}=\Omega^{-1}\mathrm{d}t\wedge\dvol_{\Sigma_{t}}
. From (
9.3
), we have

\displaystyle\Omega^{-6}|\widetilde{Q}_{0}(\tilde{\phi},\tilde{\psi})|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Omega^{2}|Q_{0}(\phi,\psi)|^{2}+|\phi|^{2}(|\partial_{t}\psi|^{2}+|\not{\nabla}\psi|^{2})+|\psi|^{2}(|\partial_{t}\phi|^{2}+|\not{\nabla}\phi|^{2})+|\phi|^{2}|\psi|^{2},

so integrating with respect to
\dvol_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}
, at fixed
t
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{t}}\Omega^{-3}|\widetilde{Q}_{0}(\tilde{\phi},\tilde{\psi})|^{2}\dvol_{\Sigma_{t}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|Q_{0}(\phi,\psi)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}(t)

\displaystyle+\int_{\{t\}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}}\left(|\phi|^{2}(|\partial_{t}\psi|^{2}+|\not{\nabla}\psi|^{2})+|\psi|^{2}(|\partial_{t}\phi|^{2}+|\not{\nabla}\phi|^{2})+|\phi|^{2}|\psi|^{2}\right)\dvol_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|Q_{0}(\phi,\psi)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}(t)+\|\phi\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}(t)\left(\|\partial_{t}\psi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}(t)+\|\psi\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}(t)\right)

\displaystyle+\|\psi\|^{2}_{L^{6}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}(t)\left(\|\partial_{t}\phi\|^{2}_{L^{3}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}(t)+\|\not{\nabla}\phi\|^{2}_{L^{3}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}(t)\right),

where in the last line we used Hölder’s inequality with
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{6}+\frac{1}{3}=\frac{1}{2}
to control the terms of the form
|\psi|^{2}|\partial\phi|^{2}
. By the compactness of
\mathbb{S}^{3}
and using the embeddings
H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{3})\hookrightarrow L^{6}(\mathbb{S}^{3})
and

we obtain, after using the energy inequality (
3.3
) for
\phi
and

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{t}}\Omega^{-3}|\widetilde{Q}_{0}(\tilde{\phi},\tilde{\psi})|^{2}\dvol_{\Sigma_{t}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|Q_{0}(\phi,\psi)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}(t)

\displaystyle+\left(\|f_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}+\|f_{1}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}\right)\left(\|g_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}+\|g_{1}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}\right).

Hence integrating in
-\pi\leqslant t\leqslant\pi
and using (
3.5
) gives

\displaystyle\|\Omega^{-1}\widetilde{Q}_{0}(\tilde{\phi},\tilde{\psi})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{4})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\|f_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}+\|f_{1}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}\right)\left(\|g_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{1+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}+\|g_{1}\|^{2}_{H^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}\right),

or, using the definition of weighted Sobolev spaces (
2.1

and similarly for

\left\|\langle\tilde{t}-\tilde{r}\rangle\langle\tilde{t}+\tilde{r}\rangle\widetilde{Q}_{0}(\tilde{\phi},\tilde{\psi})\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{4})}\lesssim\left(\|\tilde{f}_{0}\|^{2}_{W^{2}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+\|\tilde{f}_{1}\|^{2}_{W^{1}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\right)\left(\|\tilde{g}_{0}\|^{2}_{W^{1+\varepsilon}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+\|\tilde{g}_{1}\|^{2}_{W^{\varepsilon}_{1+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\right).

This completes the proof of

Remark 9.1
.

Appendix A
Harmonic Analysis on Lie Groups

A.1.
Peter–Weyl Theorem

Here we recap briefly the fundamentals of harmonic analysis on Lie groups. The proofs of the statements below can be found in standard texts, e.g.
[
Far08
]
. Let
\mathrm{G}
be a compact matrix Lie group. A
representation
of
\mathrm{G}
on a finite dimensional complex vector space
V
is a group homomorphism
\pi:\mathrm{G}\to\mathrm{GL}(V)
such that the map
g\mapsto\pi(g)v
is continuous for all
v\in V
. We say two representations
(\pi_{1},V_{1})
and
(\pi_{2},V_{2})
are
equivalent
if there exists a continuous isomorphism
A:V_{1}\to V_{2}
such that
A\pi_{1}(g)=\pi_{2}(g)A
for all
g\in\mathrm{G}
. In this case we write
\pi_{1}\simeq\pi_{2}
. Let
(\pi,V)
be a representation of
\mathrm{G}
and let
W\subset V
be a subspace of
V
. The space
W
is said to be an
invariant subspace
of the representation
\pi
if
\pi(\mathrm{G})W\subset W
. One then defines an
irreducible
representation to be a representation which has no non-trivial invariant subspaces.

Next suppose
V
is a complex vector space equipped with a Hermitian inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. Recall that an operator
A
on
V
is
unitary
if
\langle Au,Aw\rangle=\langle v,w\rangle
for all
v,w\in V
. We say that a representation
\pi
of
\mathrm{G}
on
V
is
unitary
if for every
g\in\mathrm{G}
the operator
\pi(g)
is unitary.

Every compact Lie group
\mathrm{G}
admits a unique (up to multiplication by a positive constant) left-invariant measure
\mu
called the
Haar measure
. That is, the measure
\mu
satisfies

\int_{\mathrm{G}}f(kx)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=\int_{\mathrm{G}}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

for every measurable function
f:\mathrm{G}\to\mathbb{C}
and all
k\in\mathrm{G}
. We fix the Haar measure by the normalization

\int_{\mathrm{G}}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=1.

The Haar measure on any compact connected Lie group
\mathrm{G}
is also right-invariant.

Definition A.1
.

Let
\hat{\mathrm{G}}
denote the set of equivalence classes of unitary irreducible representations of a compact Lie group
\mathrm{G}
. The set
\hat{\mathrm{G}}
is called the
unitary dual
of
\mathrm{G}
.

Definition A.2
.

Let
\pi:\mathrm{G}\to\mathrm{GL}(V)
be a unitary
\mathbb{C}
-linear irreducible representation of
\mathrm{G}
on a complex vector space
V
of dimension
d_{\pi}
. Let
\{e_{i}\}_{1\leqslant i\leqslant d_{\pi}}
be an orthonormal basis for
V
. We call

\pi_{ij}(g)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\langle\pi(g)e_{j},e_{i}\rangle

the
matrix coefficients
of
\pi
with respect to
\{e_{i}\}_{i}
.

Proposition A.3

(Schur orthogonality)
.

For
u,v,u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in V
one has the orthogonality relations

\int_{\mathrm{G}}\langle\pi(g)u,v\rangle\overline{\langle\pi(g)u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\rangle}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(g)=\frac{1}{d_{\pi}}\langle u,u^{\prime}\rangle\overline{\langle v,v^{\prime}\rangle}.

As a consequence of Schur orthogonality, it is easy to check that the matrix coefficients of a unitary
\mathbb{C}
-linear irreducible representation
\pi
are orthogonal in
L^{2}(\mathrm{G})
,

(A.1)

\langle\pi_{ij},\pi_{kl}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathrm{G})}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\int_{\mathrm{G}}\pi_{ij}(g)\overline{\pi_{kl}(g)}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(g)=\frac{1}{d_{\pi}}\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{\pi}
denote the subspace of
L^{2}(\mathrm{G})
spanned by the matrix coefficients
\pi_{ij}
for
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant d_{\pi}
. It is not hard to see that
\mathcal{M}_{\pi}
is independent of the choice of orthonormal basis for
V
, and that, on account of (
A.1
),

\left\{\sqrt{d_{\pi}}\pi_{ij}\,:\,1\leqslant i,j\leqslant d_{\pi}\right\}

is an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{M}_{\pi}\subset L^{2}(\mathrm{G})
.

Proposition A.4
.

If
\pi\not\simeq\pi^{\prime}
are two inequivalent unitary irreducible representations of
\mathrm{G}
, then
\mathcal{M}_{\pi}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\pi^{\prime}}
are orthogonal in
L^{2}(\mathrm{G})
,

\int_{\mathrm{G}}\langle\pi(g)u,v\rangle\overline{\langle\pi^{\prime}(g)u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\rangle^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(g)=0

for all
u,v\in V
,
u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in V^{\prime}
.

Theorem A.5

(Peter–Weyl)
.

L^{2}(\mathrm{G})=\widehat{\bigoplus}_{\pi\in\hat{\mathrm{G}}}\mathcal{M}_{\pi},

where
\widehat{\bigoplus}_{\pi}\mathcal{M}_{\pi}
denotes the closure of
\bigoplus_{\pi}\mathcal{M}_{\pi}
in
L^{2}(\mathrm{G})
.

Definition A.6
.

Let
f\in L^{1}(\mathrm{G})
. For each
\pi\in\hat{\mathrm{G}}
the
Fourier coefficient

\hat{f}(\pi)
is the operator on
V
(the complex vector space associated with the representation
\pi
) defined by

\hat{f}(\pi)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\int_{\mathrm{G}}f(g)\pi(g^{-1})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(g).

For matrix Lie groups
\hat{f}(\pi)
can be thought of as a matrix.

By the Schur orthogonality relations and the Peter–Weyl theorem,

\mathcal{B}=\left\{\sqrt{d_{\pi}}\pi_{ij},\,\pi\in\hat{\mathrm{G}},\,1\leqslant i,j\leqslant d_{\pi}\right\}

is a Hilbert basis for
L^{2}(\mathrm{G})
. It is not difficult to check that for
f\in L^{2}(\mathrm{G})
we have

\langle f,\pi_{ij}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathrm{G})}=\langle\hat{f}(\pi)e_{j},e_{i}\rangle_{V}=\hat{f}(\pi)_{ij}.

This leads to the following.

Theorem A.7

(Plancherel Theorem)
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\mathrm{G})
. Then

f(g)=\sum_{\pi\in\hat{\mathrm{G}}}d_{\pi}\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{f}(g)\pi(g))

in
L^{2}(\mathrm{G})
, and furthermore

\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathrm{G})}=\sum_{\pi\in\hat{\mathrm{G}}}d_{\pi}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\hat{f}(\pi)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2},

where
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\hat{f}(\pi)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}
is the Frobenius norm of the matrix
\hat{f}(\pi)
defined by
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|A\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}=\operatorname{Tr}(AA^{\dagger})
.

A.2.
Sobolev spaces on
\mathrm{SU}(2)

We identify
\mathbb{S}^{3}\simeq\mathrm{SU}(2)
. The normalized Haar measure on
\mathrm{SU}(2)
is the volume form of the standard metric on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
,

(A.2)

\mathrm{d}\mu=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\dvol_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}.

For convenience we will define the Sobolev norms on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
with respect to the scaling (
A.2
) of the
\mathbb{S}^{3}
volume form. The finite-dimensional irreducible representations
\pi_{m}:\mathrm{SU}(2)\to\mathrm{GL}(V_{m})
of
\mathrm{SU}(2)
are labelled by
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, one for each
m
, and have dimensions
(m+1)
. They are exactly the eigenfunctions of the metric Laplacian
\not{\Delta}
on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
(or equivalently the Casimir operator on
\mathrm{SU}(2)
),

\not{\Delta}\pi_{m}=-m(m+2)\pi_{m}.

Since
\pi_{m}
is a matrix of size
(m+1)\times(m+1)
, the eigenvalue
-m(m+2)
has multiplicity
(m+1)^{2}
. The Schur orthogonality relations imply that

\int_{\mathrm{SU}(2)}(\pi_{m})_{ij}(x)\overline{(\pi_{n})_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=\frac{1}{(m+1)}\delta_{ii^{\prime}}\delta_{jj^{\prime}}\delta_{mn},

and the Peter–Weyl and Plancherel theorems state that any
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{3})
is expressible as its Fourier series

f(x)=\sum_{m\geqslant 0}(m+1)\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{f}(\pi_{m})\pi_{m}(x)),

interpreted as equality in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{3})
. Furthermore, the
L^{2}
norm of
f
is given by

\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}=\sum_{m\geqslant 0}(m+1)\,{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\hat{f}(\pi_{m})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}.

One can similarly define the Sobolev spaces
H^{k}(\mathbb{S}^{3})
using the
\mathrm{SU}(2)
Fourier coefficients. By Schur orthogonality,

\displaystyle\|\not{\nabla}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}

\displaystyle=\langle f,-\not{\Delta}f\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\geqslant 0}\sum_{n\geqslant 0}(n+1)(m+1)\hat{f}(\pi_{n})_{ij}\hat{f}(\pi_{m})_{rs}\langle(\pi_{n})_{ji},-\not{\Delta}(\pi_{m})_{sr}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\geqslant 0}\sum_{n\geqslant 0}(n+1)m(m+1)(m+2)\hat{f}(\pi_{n})_{ij}\hat{f}(\pi_{m})_{rs}\delta_{mn}\frac{1}{(m+1)}\delta_{js}\delta_{ir}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\geqslant 0}m(m+1)(m+2)\,{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\hat{f}(\pi_{m})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2},

so

\displaystyle\|f\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}

\displaystyle=\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}+\|\not{\nabla}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}=\sum_{m\geqslant 0}(m+1)^{3}\,{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\hat{f}(\pi_{m})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}.

It is not difficult to see that the usual higher differentiability Sobolev norms are equivalent to similar expressions,

\|f\|^{2}_{H^{k}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}\simeq\sum_{m\geqslant 0}(m+1)^{2k+1}\,{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\hat{f}(\pi_{m})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}.

We also use the above expression to define the spaces
H^{k}(\mathbb{S}^{3})
for non-integer
k
. This definition coincides with the usual one via
H^{k}(\mathbb{S}^{3})=(1-\not{\Delta})^{-k/2}(L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{3}))
.

Appendix B
An Auxiliary Lemma

Lemma B.1
.

The series

S(\beta_{0},\alpha,\beta;n)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{(1+|n-2k|)^{1+2\beta_{0}}}\frac{1}{k^{\alpha}}\frac{1}{(n-k)^{\beta}}

converges as
n\to\infty
if

(B.1)

\displaystyle\alpha+\beta+2\beta_{0}

\displaystyle>0,

(B.2)

\displaystyle\alpha+\beta

\displaystyle\geqslant 0,

(B.3)

\displaystyle 1+2\beta_{0}

\displaystyle\geqslant 0,

(B.4)

\displaystyle\alpha+1+2\beta_{0}

\displaystyle\geqslant 0,

(B.5)

\displaystyle\beta+1+2\beta_{0}

\displaystyle\geqslant 0.

Proof.

By splitting the sum into the two ranges
1\leqslant k\leqslant n/2
and
n/2<k\leqslant n-1
, we obtain

\displaystyle S(\beta_{0},\alpha,\beta;n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leqslant s\leqslant n-2}(1+s)^{-1-2\beta_{0}}(n+s)^{-\alpha}(n-s)^{-\beta}

\displaystyle+\sum_{0<s\leqslant n-2}(1+s)^{-1-2\beta_{0}}(n-s)^{-\alpha}(n+s)^{-\beta}.

By symmetry, it is enough to consider the convergence of

\tilde{S}(\beta_{0},\alpha,\beta;n)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\sum_{s=0}^{n-2}(1+s)^{-1-2\beta_{0}}(n+s)^{-\alpha}(n-s)^{-\beta}.

Necessity

By considering the first and last terms in the above series, we first observe that
\tilde{S}(\beta_{0},\alpha,\beta;n)\geqslant n^{-\alpha-\beta}+2^{-\alpha-\beta}(n-1)^{-1-\alpha-2\beta_{0}}
, so

(B.6)

\alpha+\beta\geqslant 0\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha+1+2\beta_{0}\geqslant 0

are necessary conditions. By symmetry, we then also need

(B.7)

\beta+1+2\beta_{0}\geqslant 0.

Together, these imply

\beta_{0}\geqslant-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4}(\alpha+\beta).

Sufficiency

Assume (
B.6
) and (
B.7
), and assume further that

(B.8)

\beta_{0}\geqslant-\frac{1}{2}

and

(B.9)

\alpha+\beta+2\beta_{0}>0.

Suppose first that two of the three exponents vanish. Then (
B.9
) implies that the remaining exponent is greater than
1
, and convergence follows.

Next, suppose that exactly one of the three exponents vanishes. If
\alpha=0
, then (
B.6
) implies that
\beta>0
and
1+2\beta_{0}>0
. By Hölder’s inequality, convergence follows if
\beta+2\beta_{0}>0
, which is guaranteed by (
B.9
). The other two cases are similar.

Suppose therefore that none of the three exponents vanish. We first treat the case when both
\alpha
and
\beta
are positive. Convergence follows trivially if
\alpha>1
,
\beta>1
, or
1+2\beta_{0}>1
, so suppose first that

0<\alpha,\,\beta,\,1+2\beta_{0}\leqslant 1.

Note that in this case
-1/2<\beta_{0}\leqslant 0
and that (
B.6
) and (
B.7
) are satisfied. Then we claim that convergence follows by two applications of Hölder’s inequality, first to separate the
(1+s)^{-1-2\beta_{0}}
term, and then to separate the two remaining terms. Indeed, applying Hölder’s inequality once, we wish to find
p,\,q>1
such that

p(1+2\beta_{0})>1,\qquad\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1.

Choose
\varepsilon>0
such that
\varepsilon<1+2\beta_{0}
, and put

p=\frac{1}{1+2\beta_{0}-\varepsilon}.

Then

\frac{1}{q}=1-\frac{1}{p}=\varepsilon-2\beta_{0}\in(0,1)

for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, so we may choose such a
q>1
. Now applying Hölder again, we wish to find exponents
r,\,s>1
such that

\alpha qr>1,\qquad\beta qs<1,\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{r}+\frac{1}{s}=1.

Choose
r>1
such that

\varepsilon-2\beta_{0}<\alpha r.

We then seek an
s>1
such that

\frac{1}{s}=1-\frac{1}{r}\in(1-\alpha(\varepsilon-2\beta_{0})^{-1},\,\beta q),

which is possible if

1-\frac{\alpha}{\varepsilon-2\beta_{0}}<\beta q=\frac{\beta}{\varepsilon-2\beta_{0}}.

This is satisfied if
\alpha+\beta+2\beta_{0}>\varepsilon
, which is possible by (
B.9
) if
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small.

Finally, suppose that either
\alpha
or
\beta
is allowed to be negative. Since we must have
\alpha+\beta\geqslant 0
, at least of one them must be non-negative, and because
(n+s)\geqslant(n-s)
, it is enough to treat the case
\beta\geqslant 0
and
\alpha\leqslant\beta
. Let

f(n;s)\overset{\text{def}}{=}(1+s)^{-1-2\beta_{0}}(n+s)^{-\alpha}(n-s)^{-\beta}.

The derivative of
f(n;s)
with respect to
s
is given by

\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}s}=-\frac{f(n;s)}{(n+s)(n-s)(1+s)}q(n,s),

where
q(n,s)
is the quadratic in
s
and
n

\displaystyle q(n,s)

\displaystyle=(n^{2}-s^{2})(1+2\beta_{0})+(1+s)(\beta(n+s)+\alpha(n-s))

\displaystyle=s^{2}(\beta-\alpha-1-2\beta_{0})+s((n-1)\alpha+(n+1)\beta)+(1+2\beta_{0})n^{2}+(\alpha+\beta)n.

Since
\beta_{0}\geqslant-1/2
and
0\leqslant s\leqslant n-2
,

q(n,s)\geqslant 4(n-1)(1+2\beta_{0})+\underbrace{(1+s)}_{\geqslant 1}((\alpha+\beta)n+\underbrace{(\beta-\alpha)s}_{\geqslant 0}),

so
q(n,s)\geqslant 0

\forall

0\leqslant s\leqslant n-2
by (
B.6
), (
B.8
), and the assumption
\beta-\alpha\geqslant 0
. So the function
f(n;s)
is decreasing in
s
in this case, and hence

\sum_{s=0}^{n-2}f(n;s)\leqslant n^{-(\alpha+\beta)}+I(\beta_{0},\alpha,\beta;n),

where

I(\beta_{0},\alpha,\beta;n)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\int_{0}^{n-2}(1+s)^{-1-2\beta_{0}}(n+s)^{-\alpha}(n-s)^{-\beta}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Making the change of variables
s=n\sigma
, the integral
I(\beta_{0},\alpha,\beta;n)
becomes

\displaystyle I(\beta_{0},\alpha,\beta;n)

\displaystyle=n^{-\alpha-\beta-2\beta_{0}}\int_{0}^{1-\frac{2}{n}}(n^{-1}+\sigma)^{-1-2\beta_{0}}(1+\sigma)^{-\alpha}(1-\sigma)^{-\beta}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle\lesssim n^{-\alpha-\beta-2\beta_{0}}\int_{0}^{1-\frac{2}{n}}(n^{-1}+\sigma)^{-1-2\beta_{0}}(1-\sigma)^{-\beta}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma,

where we noted that
I(\beta_{0},\alpha,\beta;n)
is manifestly positive and
(1+\sigma)\simeq 1
in the domain of integration (for
n\geqslant 2
). Splitting the range of integration into
[0,\frac{1}{2}]
and
[\frac{1}{2},1-\frac{2}{n}]
and noting that in the former range
(1-\sigma)\simeq 1
and in the latter range
(n^{-1}+\sigma)\simeq 1
, we deduce that
I(\beta_{0},\alpha,\beta;n)
converges as
n\to\infty
if

n^{-\alpha-\beta-2\beta_{0}}\left(\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}(n^{-1}+\sigma)^{-1-2\beta_{0}}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma+\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{1-\frac{2}{n}}(1-\sigma)^{-\beta}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)

converges. By direct integration, we conclude the following, distinguishing between the cases when the integrals lead to logarithmic terms. If
\beta_{0}\neq 0
and
\beta\neq 1
, we have convergence if
n^{-\alpha-\beta}+n^{-\alpha-2\beta_{0}-1}
converges, i.e. if
\alpha+\beta\geqslant 0
and
\alpha+2\beta_{0}+1\geqslant 0
. These are exactly (
B.2
) and (
B.4
). If
\beta_{0}=0
and
\beta\neq 1
, we have convergence of
I(\beta_{0},\alpha,\beta;n)
if
n^{-\alpha-\beta}\log n+n^{-\alpha-1}
converges, i.e. when
\alpha+\beta>0
and
\alpha\geqslant-1
; these are implied by (
B.1
) and (
B.4
). If, on the other hand,
\beta_{0}\neq 0
and
\beta=1
, convergence follows if
\alpha+2\beta_{0}+1>0
and
\alpha\geqslant-1
; these follow from (
B.1
) and (
B.2
). Finally, if
\beta_{0}=0
and
\beta=1
, we have convergence if
\alpha>-1
, which is implied by (
B.1
). ∎
