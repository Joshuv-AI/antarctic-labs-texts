Circuit locality from relativistic locality in scalar field mediated entanglement
Andrea Di Biagio, Richard Howl, Časlav Brukner, Carlo Rovelli, Marios Christodoulou

Abstract
Locality is a central notion in modern physics, but different disciplines understand it in different ways. Quantum field theory focuses on relativistic locality, based on spacetime regions, while quantum information theory focuses circuit locality, based on the notion of subsystems. Here, we investigate how spacetime and subsystem locality are related in the context of systems getting entangled while interacting via a scalar field. We show how, when the systems are put in a quantum-controlled superposition of localised states, relativistic locality (in the form of microcausality) gives rise to a specific kind of circuit. The relation between these forms of locality is relevant for understanding whether it is possible to formulate quantum field theory in quantum circuit language, and has bearing on the recent discussions on low-energy tests of quantum gravity.



The discovery that fundamental interactions are mediated by fields has led to the modern idea of locality: there is no action at a distance.
But action between what?

Relativistic locality says that no causal influence can propagate faster than the speed of light, and therefore there can be no causation between spacelike separated regions. In quantum field theory, this is ensured by
microcausality
, also known as Einstein causality, the vanishing of the commutator between field operators at spacelike separations:

[\hat{\phi}(x),\hat{\phi}(x^{\prime})]=0

(1)

if
x-x^{\prime}
is spacelike. This property, either shown to hold in physically relevant field theories or taken as an axiom in algebraic quantum field theory
Duncan 2017
, allows for simultaneous measurements at different locations to be compatible and prevents superluminal signalling
Eberhard and Ross 1989
. Relativistic locality is a spatiotemporal notion of locality.

One can also formulate a notion of locality based on subsystems, where information can propagate from a system
A
to system
B
either via direct interaction between
A
and
B
, or via a chain of successive interactions with some intermediary systems. We will call this notion
subsystem locality
. In quantum theory, subsystem locality is articulated in terms of
circuit locality
, where the evolution of several systems is modelled by a circuit, and the way the various gates are connected dictates the information flow between the subsystems
Lorenz and Barrett 2021
.

For example, if a unitary gate decomposes in the following circuit

\hat{U}
=
\penalty\ \hat{U}_{1}
\penalty\ \hat{U}_{2}

(2)

one is assured that measurements on the system on the left can be affected by the middle system only and not by the system on the right.
Circuit locality for circuits composed of unitary (or, more generally, completely positive trace-preserving maps) is a property of quantum mechanics. A version of circuit locality also holds in post-quantum theories, such as operational probabilistic theories
D’Ariano et al. 2017
and generalised probabilistic theories
Barrett 2007
.

What is the precise relation between these two ideas of locality, one native of quantum field theory and one native of quantum information theory? Circuit locality in quantum theory is sometimes taken to reflect, encode, or ensure relativistic locality; see
(
Coecke and Kissinger 2017
, Section 6.3)
and
Deutsch and Hayden 2000
. The idea is that if we can locate the various gates of a circuit in different spacetime regions and have connections between gates located in causally connected regions, then the mathematics of Hilbert space ensures relativistic locality via its enforcement of circuit locality. This would explain why it is not possible to superluminally signal using entangled systems. The motivation is also present in reverse: the very identification of subsystems with Hilbert space tensor factors is sometimes motivated via appeals to relativistic locality in works in the foundations of quantum theory
Raymond-Robichaud 2021
;
Barrett 2007
;
D’Ariano et al. 2017
. However, the relation is not quite clear, with the main formal obstruction in motivating circuit locality from relativistic locality being the well-known result that it is impossible to decompose the Hilbert space of a quantum field theory in a tensor product of spaces associated with different regions
Witten 2022
. Delineating the connection between these two notions of locality is an open problem
van der Lugt 2024
;
Vilasini and Renner 2024
.

This connection matters in the discussion on the recent idea of witnessing nonclassical behaviour of gravity via gravity-mediated entanglement: two spatially separated masses becoming entangled as a result of the gravitational interaction
Bose et al. 2017
;
Marletto and Vedral 2017
;
Krisnanda et al. 2017
;
Christodoulou and Rovelli 2018
;
Christodoulou et al. 2023
;
Martín-Martínez and Perche 2023
;
Fragkos et al. 2022
;
Huggett et al. 2022
. The study of these proposed experiments has created a new interface between the quantum gravity and quantum information research communities.
The following question arises. Suppose there are two systems,
A
and
B
, each coupled to a relativistically local field
\phi
but not to each other. Does it follow that the field
mediates
the interaction between
A
and
B
, in the information-theoretic sense that the evolution of the full system can be broken down into a product of unitaries each acting only on
A
and
\phi
, or on
B
and
\phi
, and never involving both
A
and
B
?

In a way, the answer is trivially yes. Indeed, thanks to the Suzuki-Trotter theorem, for a Hamiltonian

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{\phi A}+\hat{H}_{\phi B},

(3)

we have

e^{-i\hat{H}t}=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\left(e^{-i\hat{H}_{\phi A}t/N}e^{-i\hat{H}_{\phi B}t/N}\right)^{N}.

(4)

Since
\hat{H}_{\phi A}
acts only on
\phi
and
A
, and
\hat{H}_{\phi B}
only on
\phi
and
B
, it follows that the evolution can be approximated to any desired precision by an evolution with the required circuit locality.
This result however teaches us nothing about the relation between the two notions of locality we are interested in: the expansion relies only on general considerations about linear operators and relativistic locality plays no role in this.

In the following, we will answer ‘yes, approximately’ to the above question, but in a more informative way. We will show that, if the systems
A
and
B
are kept spatially separated, each in a quantum controlled superposition of semiclassically localised states, the evolution can be written as

\hat{U}(t_{\mathrm{i}},t_{\mathrm{f}})=\prod_{n=1}^{N-1}\left(\hat{U}_{B\phi}^{(n)}\,\hat{U}_{A\phi}^{(n)}\hat{U}_{\phi}^{(n)}\right),

(5)

where
\hat{U}_{\phi}
acts only on the field, and
\hat{U}_{A\phi}^{(n)}
and
\hat{U}_{B\phi}^{(n)}
act only on the field and one other system. Each value of
n
represents the evolution over a
finite time interval

(t_{n},t_{n+1})
in which the particles are localised

In contrast with the Suzuki-Trotter version of this decomposition, here the role of spacetime locality is evident: each term in the product in (
5
) evolves the system for a finite duration, given by the separation between the systems
A
and
B
. Given (
7
), we also see that during, this time interval,
A
and
B
cannot affect each other, but only the field, as one would expect for spacelike-separated systems.

The result is easily generalised to the case of an arbitrary number of particles. The approximations we employ to obtain this result are appropriate to describe a number of physically relevant regimes: quantum computing platforms, such as ion traps; quantum optics experiments, such as Bell tests; and the regime in which low energy tests of quantum gravity may be performed.

Figure 1:
If the particles are in a quantum-controlled superposition of well-localised trajectories, the evolution of the system takes the desired field mediation shape in each time interval where all trajectories of one particle are spacelike separated from all trajectories of the other particle.

In the following four sections we derive this result. After laying down some notation in Section
.1
, we define the required approximations and derive their consequences in Section
.2
. In Section
.3
, we derive an expression for the evolution of a quantum field coupled to a classical source, incidentally improving on textbook results by taking into account a phase which is often neglected. This phase plays a crucial role in our result, as we will see in Section
.4
, where we demonstrate how relativistic locality influences circuit locality in our system. In Section
.5
, we show how this setup allows for scalar field mediated entanglement by deriving the explicit, relativistic expression for the entangling phases. In the conclusion, we discuss the limitations and possible generalisations of our result. Throughout the paper, we refer the reader to the appendix, where we report more detailed derivations of the results.

.1
Setup

We consider a massive scalar field coupled to two quantum sources, coherently controlled by two control qudits (
d
-level systems with Hilbert space
\mathbb{C}^{d}
).
2
2

2

We work in units such that
\hbar=c=1
. We denote four-vectors as
x
and space vectors as
\bm{x}
. For brevity, we will sometimes suppress the explicit dependence on the interval
(t,t^{\prime})
for unitary evolution operators and other quantities. We will use hats to denote operators and distinguish them from c-numbers.
Coherent control with qudits is a typical setup in quantum information theory
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
. The Hilbert space for the field is the usual Fock space
\mathcal{F}
generated by the canonical creation-annihilation operators acting on the vacuum of the free Hamiltonian of the field. The Hilbert spaces for the particles are
A=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
B=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
, where
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
represents centre of mass motion and
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
some internal degrees of freedom of the particles.
The Hamiltonian of the system is taken to be

\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}_{A}(t)+\hat{H}_{B}(t)+\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}.

(8)

The Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{A}(t)
encodes the dynamics of
A
as influenced by a (possibly time-dependent) driving by the first control qudit. It acts only on these two systems and is of the form

\hat{H}_{A}(t)=\sum_{r=1}^{d}|r\rangle\!\langle r|\otimes\hat{H}^{r}_{A}(t),

(9)

with
|r\rangle
denoting the computational basis of the first qudit and
\hat{H}^{r}_{A}(t)
acting only on
A
; similar definitions hold for
\hat{H}_{B}(t)
. The free Hamiltonian of the field is given as usual by

\hat{H}_{0}=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\omega_{k}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\k{\hat}}a_{\k{,}}

(10)

with
{\omega_{k}=\sqrt{m^{2}+k^{2}}}
.

The interaction term couples the particle positions to the field. It is given by

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,\hat{\phi}({\bm{x}})(\hat{\rho}_{A}({\bm{x}})+\hat{\rho}_{B}({\bm{x}}))

(11)

where
\hat{\rho}_{A}
and
\hat{\rho}_{B}
are the charge density operators
3
3

3

This is the analog of electric charge density or mass density for the scalar field. For pointlike particles we would have
{\hat{\rho}_{A}({\bm{x}})=|{\bm{x}}\rangle\!\langle{\bm{x}}|_{A}}
, ie,
{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}=\hat{\phi}(\hat{\bm{x}}_{A})+\hat{\phi}(\hat{\bm{x}}_{B})}
. For something with spatial extent we would have
{\rho_{A}({\bm{x}})=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x_{A}\sigma_{A}({\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}_{A})|{\bm{x}}_{A}\rangle\!\langle{\bm{x}}_{A}|_{A}}
with
\sigma_{A}({\bm{x}})
a real-valued function of compact support that represents the charge distribution conditional on the particle being localised at the origin.

.2
Parametric approximations

We now assume that the back reaction of the particles on the qudits, on the one hand, and of the field on the particles on the other, can be safely ignored. This kind of approximation is sometimes known as
parametric
and is often used in quantum optics
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
;
Scully and Zubairy 1997
, and is also part of the Born-Oppenheimer approximation.

Concretely, we first assume that, at all times
t
, the state of the system is of the form

|\Psi(t)\rangle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{r,s\in\{1,\dots,d\}}|rs\rangle|\Psi^{rs}(t)\rangle,

(12)

where
|rs\rangle
are the states in the computational basis of the qudits and
|\Psi^{rs}(t)\rangle
are normalised states of the particles and field.
Since the states
|rs\rangle
are orthonormal and
\hat{H}(t)
is diagonal in this basis, we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}|\Psi^{rs}(t)\rangle=-i\hat{H}^{rs}(t)|\Psi^{rs}(t)\rangle,

(13)

with
\hat{H}^{rs}(t)=\langle rs|\hat{H}(t)|rs\rangle
.

To solve this equation, we further assume that, for all times
t
,

|\Psi^{rs}(t)\rangle=|\psi^{r}_{A}(t)\rangle|\psi^{s}_{B}(t)\rangle|\psi_{\phi}^{rs}(t)\rangle,

(14)

where
|\psi^{r}_{A}(t)\rangle,|\psi^{s}_{B}(t)\rangle,
and
|\psi_{\phi}^{rs}(t)\rangle
are states for the particles
A
and
B
, and the field, respectively.
In physical terms, this approximation amounts to disregarding any entanglement between the field and a particle caused by the quantum spread of the particle in each individual
|rs\rangle
branch. In other words, we assume that the particles are put in a quantum-controlled superposition of pointer states, in the sense of Zurek
Zurek 2003
. Note that this does not prevent the generation of entanglement in the full state (
12
) or the detection of entanglement between different subsystems; we will return to this point in section
.5
. Similarly, we assume that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}|\psi^{r}_{A}(t)\rangle=-i\hat{H}^{r}_{A}(t)|\psi^{r}_{A}(t)\rangle,

(15)

with
\hat{H}^{r}_{A}(t)=\langle r|\hat{H}_{A}(t)|r\rangle
and the same for
B
. It then follows that the field states
|\psi_{\phi}^{rs}(t)\rangle
also satisfy a Schrödinger equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}|\phi^{rs}(t)\rangle=-i\hat{H}^{rs}(t)|\psi_{\phi}^{rs}(t)\rangle,

(16)

with
\hat{H}^{rs}(t)=\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{H}^{rs}_{\mathrm{int}}(t)
and

\hat{H}^{rs}_{\mathrm{int}}(t)=\langle\psi^{r}_{A}(t)\psi^{s}_{B}(t)|\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}|\psi^{r}_{A}(t)\psi^{s}_{B}(t)\rangle.

(17)

Thus, the unitary evolution will be of the form

\hat{U}=\sum_{rs}|rs\rangle\!\langle rs|\otimes\hat{U}_{B}^{s}\,\hat{U}_{A}^{r}\,\hat{U}_{\phi}^{rs},

(18)

where
\hat{U}_{A}^{r}
and
\hat{U}_{B}^{s}
solve the Schrödinger equation (
15
) and the analogous one for
B
, and
\hat{U}_{\phi}^{rs}
solves (
16
).

Note that this is still not in the desired form, but it would be if we also had

\hat{U}_{\phi}^{rs}=\hat{U}_{\phi}^{s}\,\hat{U}_{\phi}^{r},

(19)

for some unitaries
\hat{U}_{\phi}^{s},\hat{U}_{\phi}^{r}
. Indeed, then we would have

\hat{U}=\underset{\hat{U}_{\phi B}}{\underbrace{\left(\sum_{s}|s\rangle\!\langle s|\hat{U}_{B}^{s}\hat{U}_{\phi}^{s}\right)}}\circ\underset{\hat{U}_{\phi A}}{\underbrace{\left(\sum_{r}|r\rangle\!\langle r|\hat{U}_{A}^{r}\hat{U}_{\phi}^{r}\right)}}\,.

(20)

We therefore need to investigate the properties of
\hat{U}_{\phi}^{rs}
.

.3
Evolution of the field states

In each
|rs\rangle
component, the field state evolves according to
\hat{H}^{rs}
. Putting together (
17
) and (
11
) we see that

\hat{H}^{rs}_{\mathrm{int}}(t)=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,\hat{\phi}({\bm{x}})\rho^{rs}(t,{\bm{x}}),

(21)

where

\rho^{rs}(t,{\bm{x}})=\langle\psi^{r}_{A}(t)\psi^{s}_{B}(t)|\hat{\rho}({\bm{x}})|\psi^{r}_{A}(t)\psi^{s}_{B}(t)\rangle,

(22)

with
\hat{\rho}=\hat{\rho}_{A}+\hat{\rho}_{B}
.
Thus,
\hat{H}^{rs}
describes the evolution of a quantum field coupled to a time-dependent classical source; this can be solved in closed form using standard methods. We will summarise the results in this section and detail the procedure in appendix
C
.

The propagator
\hat{U}^{rs}_{\phi}(t,t^{\prime})
that evolves a field state
|\phi^{rs}(t)\rangle
to
|\phi^{rs}(t^{\prime})\rangle
according to (
16
) can be written as

\hat{U}^{rs}_{\phi}(t,t^{\prime})=e^{-i\hat{H}_{0}t^{\prime}}\hat{U}^{rs}_{I}(t,t^{\prime})e^{i\hat{H}_{0}t},

(23)

where
\hat{U}_{I}^{rs}
denotes the interaction picture propagator. It is the solution to

\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}\hat{U}_{I}^{rs}(t,t^{\prime})=-i\hat{H}_{I}^{rs}(t^{\prime})\hat{U}^{rs}_{I}(t,t^{\prime}),

(24)

where

\hat{H}_{I}^{rs}(t)=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,\rho^{rs}(t,{\bm{x}})\hat{\phi}_{I}(t,{\bm{x}}),

(25)

with
\hat{\phi}_{I}(t,{\bm{x}})
the interaction picture field operators. Instead of expressing
\hat{U}^{rs}_{I}(t,t^{\prime})
as Dyson series time-ordered exponential, here it is advantageous to use the Magnus expansion

\hat{U}_{I}^{rs}(t,t^{\prime})=\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\hat{\Omega}^{rs}_{n}(t,t^{\prime})\right),

(26)

where the expression for the infinite sequence of operators
\hat{\Omega}^{rs}_{n}(t,t^{\prime})
can be found in
Blanes et al. 2009
.
In our case, thanks to the fact that both
\rho^{rs}
and
[\hat{\phi}_{I}(t,{\bm{x}}),\hat{\phi}(t^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})]
are c-numbers, we have that

[\hat{H}_{I}(t),\hat{H}_{I}(t^{\prime})]
is a c-number and one can obtain a relatively simple equation for the interaction picture propagators:

\hat{U}_{I}^{rs}(t,t^{\prime})=e^{\Omega^{rs}_{2}(t,t^{\prime})}e^{\hat{\Omega}^{rs}_{1}(t,t^{\prime})},

(27)

with
\hat{\Omega}^{rs}_{1}
and
\Omega^{rs}_{2}
given in (
84
) and (
85
), respectively.

In appendix
C.2
, we show that the Schrödinger picture propagators can then be expressed in a physically intuitive way, namely,

\hat{U}^{rs}_{\phi}(t,t^{\prime})=e^{\Omega_{2}^{rs}(t^{\prime}-t)}\hat{\mathcal{D}}[\rho^{rs}]e^{-i\hat{H}_{0}\cdot(t^{\prime}-t)},

(28)

where
e^{-i\hat{H}_{0}\cdot(t^{\prime}-t)}
is a free propagation of the field, followed by a displacement operator
\hat{\mathcal{D}}[\rho^{rs}]
that takes into account the effect of the particles on the field between
t
and
t^{\prime}
, the whole thing multiplied by a phase factor
e^{\Omega_{2}^{rs}(t^{\prime}-t)}
that does not depend on the state of the field.

This is the well-known result that classical sources create coherent states of the field, derived in standard references such as
Cohen-Tannoudji et al. 1989
;
Gerry and Knight 2005
. However, these standard treatments often omit the phase factor
e^{\Omega^{rs}_{2}}
. This is because, so long as we are only concerned with the effect of classical sources on the field, this is a global phase which has no observational consequences. Here—because we want to be able to deal with quantum superpositions of sources—this phase must be considered carefully. Additionally, this phase is necessary to ensure the correct groupoid property of the unitary operators,

{{\hat{U}^{rs}_{\phi}(t,t^{\prime\prime})=\hat{U}^{rs}_{\phi}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})}}\hat{U}^{rs}_{\phi}(t,t^{\prime})

(29)

for
t\leq t^{\prime}\leq t^{\prime\prime}
, whihc will allow us to break down the evolution over several time intervals and study its causal properties.

.4
Microcausality and circuit locality

We are now in a position to put everything together and see the connection between relativistic causality and circuit locality.

Recall that
{\hat{\rho}=\hat{\rho}_{A}+\hat{\rho}_{B}}
, from which follows that
{\rho^{rs}=\rho_{A}^{r}+\rho_{B}^{s}}
.
We can then use the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula to separate the contribution of each particle to the evolution of the field, obtaining (we refer the reader to Appendix
C.3
for more details)

\hat{U}^{rs}_{\phi}(t,t^{\prime})=e^{\Omega^{rs}(t,t^{\prime})}\hat{U}^{r}_{\phi}(t,t^{\prime})\,\hat{U}^{s}_{\phi}(t,t^{\prime})\,e^{-i\hat{H}_{0}\cdot(t^{\prime}-t)},

(30)

where
\hat{U}^{r}_{\phi}=e^{\Omega_{2}[\rho^{r}_{A}]}\hat{\mathcal{D}}[\rho_{A}^{r}]

computes the effect of the source
A
alone, and similarly for
\hat{U}^{s}_{B}
. This operator is almost in the desired form (
19
), the only obstruction being the pure phase
e^{\Omega^{rs}}
, given by

\displaystyle\Omega^{rs}(t,t^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\iint_{t}^{t^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}\tau\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\iint\mathrm{d}^{3}x\mathrm{d}^{3}x^{\prime}\,\rho_{A}^{r}(\tau,{\bm{x}})\rho_{B}^{s}(\tau^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\,[\hat{\phi}_{I}(\tau,{\bm{x}}),\hat{\phi}_{I}(\tau^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime},)]

(31)

\displaystyle+\int_{t}^{t^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}\tau\int_{t}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\iint\mathrm{d}^{3}x\mathrm{d}^{3}x^{\prime}\,\big(\rho_{A}^{r}(\tau,{\bm{x}})\rho_{B}^{s}(\tau^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})+\rho_{A}^{r}(\tau^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\rho_{B}^{s}(\tau,{\bm{x}})\big)\,[\hat{\phi}_{I}(\tau,{\bm{x}}),\hat{\phi}_{I}(\tau^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime},)],

with
{\rho^{r}_{A}(t,{\bm{x}})=\langle\psi^{r}_{A}(t)|\hat{\rho}_{A}({\bm{x}})|\psi^{r}_{A}(t)\rangle}
, and similarly for
\rho^{s}_{B}
.
The first term arises from the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula when splitting the displacement operator, the second term is due to the non-linearity of
\Omega_{2}^{rs}
; see (
85
).

It is this phase factor that can prevent the desired factorisation (
19
) of
\hat{U}^{rs}
, and therefore the circuit locality of the full evolution hinges on the properties of this phase.
Since the commutator of interaction picture fields appears explicitly here, this is where microcausality comes into play.

Assume that the supports of
\rho^{r}_{A}
and
\rho^{s}_{B}
are spacelike separated (for each
rs
pair) during the interval
[t,t^{\prime}]
. Products of the form
\rho_{A}^{r}(\tau,{\bm{x}})\rho_{B}^{s}(\tau^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})
will then only be non-zero for
(\tau-\tau^{\prime},{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime})
spacelike, precisely when the commutators
[\hat{\phi}_{I}(\tau,{\bm{x}}),\hat{\phi}_{I}(\tau^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})]
vanish due to microcausality. It follows that
{\Omega^{rs}=0}.
Then
\hat{U}^{rs}
factorises as in (
19
) and that the total evolution
\hat{U}
is a field mediation in this time interval.

If the supports are not spacelike separated for the whole interval
[t,t^{\prime}]
, they may still be spacelike separated in sub-intervals
(t_{n},t_{n+1})
of finite length, such that
\min_{n}(t_{n+1}-t_{n})>0
. The result would still hold because one can decompose the unitary evolution into many finite intervals in which the particles are not in causal contact using the group property (
29
).

Let
d^{rs}(t)
be the distance between the supports of
\rho_{A}^{r}
and
\rho_{B}^{s}
at time
t
, and define the distance of closest approach as
d_{\mathrm{min}}=\inf_{t}\min_{rs}d^{rs}(t)
. Within our approximations, then, the evolution falls into field mediation form if
d_{\mathrm{min}}>0
.

.5
Scalar field mediated entanglement

We now show that this setup is capable of reproducing the scalar analog of the gravity-mediated entanglement as originally proposed in
Bose et al. 2017
, where the control qudits are embedded in the particles, and used to put them in spatial superpositions.

Suppose that for
t<t_{\mathrm{i}}
the two particles are localised and at rest and the field is at equilibrium, so that the state is given by

|\Psi_{\mathrm{i}}\rangle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{rs}|rs\rangle|\psi_{A,\mathrm{i}}\rangle|\psi_{B,\mathrm{i}}\rangle|\phi_{\mathrm{i}}\rangle,

(32)

where
|\phi_{\mathrm{i}}\rangle
is the coherent state of the field peaked around the static classical configuration
\phi_{\mathrm{i}}
sourced by the particles at their initial locations.
Then between
t_{\mathrm{i}}
and
t_{\mathrm{f}}
, the driving Hamiltonians
\hat{H}_{A}(t)
and
\hat{H}_{B}(t)
are such that they put the particles in a superposition of semiclassical localised states moving along spatially separated trajectories; see figure
1
. The state of the system for
t\in[t_{\mathrm{i}},t_{\mathrm{f}}]
is

|\Psi(t)\rangle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{rs}|rs\rangle|\psi_{A}^{r}(t)\rangle|\psi_{B}^{s}(t)\rangle|\psi_{\phi}^{rs}(t)\rangle,

(33)

where each subsystem is entangled with the rest.
The superposition is of the particles is undone by
t_{\mathrm{f}}
and the final state will be

|\Psi_{\mathrm{i}}\rangle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{rs}e^{i\theta^{rs}}|rs\rangle|\psi_{A,\mathrm{f}}\rangle|\psi_{B,\mathrm{f}}\rangle|\phi_{\mathrm{f}}^{rs}\rangle,

(34)

where
|\phi_{\mathrm{f}}^{rs}\rangle
is the coherent field peaked around the classical configuration
\phi_{\mathrm{f}}^{rs}
obtained by solving the Klein-Gordon equation with source
\rho^{rs}
and initial condition
\phi_{\mathrm{i}}
. If we additionally assume that the experiment was done slowly enough such that the
|\psi^{rs}_{\mathrm{f}}\rangle
have quite large overlap with each other, the control qudits are effectively disentangled from the rest of the system and their state is well approximated by the state

\frac{1}{d}\sum_{rs}e^{i\theta^{rs}}|rs\rangle,

(35)

whose entanglement can be detected by standard methods
Bose et al. 2017
;
Polino et al. 2024
.
The phases
\theta^{rs}
responsible for the entanglement have a neat physical interpretation. They are given by

\theta^{rs}=-\frac{1}{2}\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\rho^{rs}(x)\phi^{rs}(x),

(36)

where
\phi^{rs}(t,{\bm{x}})
is the solution to the Klein-Gordon equation with initial conditions
\phi_{\mathrm{i}}
and source
\rho^{rs}
. Since the Klein-Gordon equation is hyperbolic, the effect of each particle on
\phi^{rs}
propagates causally, so that the phases can only entangle the two qudits only if there is enough time for light to travel from the region of one particle to that of the other, that is only if
t_{\mathrm{f}}-t_{\mathrm{i}}>d_{\mathrm{min}}
. This relativistic locality property is in complete alignment with that of the gravitational version of the experiment first derived in
Christodoulou et al. 2023
. In fact, the phases
\theta^{rs}
can be interpreted as the on-shell values of the field sector action

S_{\phi}^{rs}=\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\left(\frac{1}{2}\phi(\square-m^{2})\phi-\rho^{rs}\phi\right)

(37)

corresponding to the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}^{rs}_{\phi}
, as already remarked in
Christodoulou et al. 2023
.

.6
Discussion

We considered the evolution of a massive scalar field coupled to two quantum systems with positional and internal degrees of freedom, and we took these particles to be coherently controlled by a control qudit. Within this setting, we showed that the field is
mediating
an interaction between the particles, not only in the sense of no direct particle-particle coupling in the Hamiltonian, but also in the precise sense provided by the field mediation quantum circuit.

We emphasize the fact that we have taken the assumption that a particle’s effect on the field can be modelled, in each
|rs\rangle
branch, by a source with compact support. In quantum mechanics, however, the wavefunction of a particle will not have bounded support for any finite amount of time, unless it is confined by an infinite potential well (which itself needs to be perfectly localised). This implies that in the general situation the support of
\psi^{r}_{A}
and
\psi^{s}_{B}
will be overlapping, as will those of
\rho^{r}_{A}
and
\rho^{s}_{B}
, and the evolution
will not
take the field mediation shape.
From the perspective of the quantum field theoretical aspects of nature, the notions of local particles and qudits, and their circuit local evolution are only approximations. Nevertheless, in concrete situations, we know from experience this approximation is appropriate: for all practical purposes, the location of a particle, and its sourcing of a field, can be well approximated as being contained in a compact region.

Let us discuss the limitations of our computation.
The parametric approximation implies that we neglect the back-reaction of the field on the particles, and allowed us to do a “branch-by-branch” analysis. It also assumes that, in each
rs
branch, the particles and the field do not get entangled, which is a good approximation only if the states
|\psi^{r}_{A}(t)\rangle
and
|\psi^{s}_{B}(t)\rangle
are quite localised at all times. These assumptions are commonly used to model superpositions of semi-classical scenarios, and yield many interesting phenomena; see

Bose et al. 2017
;
Marletto and Vedral 2017
;
Mari et al. 2016
;
Belenchia et al. 2018
;
Danielson et al. 2022
;
Danielson et al. 2023
for a tiny sample.
Removing this approximation makes the analysis considerably more involved.
For example, when computing directly evolution due to the full Hamiltonian (
8
) using the Magnus expansion for the interaction picture, the non-trivial commutators of
\hat{\rho}
prevent the expansion from terminating. The result would still hold perturbatively, at low enough order, but we leave a complete analysis to future work.

Another limitation to the physical significance of our model is the fact that we have studied a massive scalar field, while the fundamental interactions in physics are via massless gauge fields. One issue that will arise in the massless theory is that the displacement operators
\hat{\mathcal{D}}
will no longer be well-defined unitary operators for all values of the source
\rho
. This is the well-known problem of infrared divergences in quantum field theory
Prabhu et al. 2022
;
Peskin and Schroeder 2019
. There are two ways to deal with this. One is to impose an infrared cutoff to the field, in which case the result holds immediately. Alternatively, one would have to embrace the fact that the algebra of observables will not be representable by operators on a single Hilbert space. We expect it should be possible to derive a similar result also in this setting, provided a notion of subsystem that does not rely explicitly on Hilbert spaces.

Since we worked with a scalar field, we did not have to deal with gauge constraints. In symmetry-reduced quantisation, the imposition of constraints can imply the presence of terms in the Hamiltonian that couple charged particles directly. For example, when quantising Maxwell’s theory in the Coulomb gauge, we would have the Coulomb term
\propto 1/|\hat{\bm{x}}_{A}-\hat{\bm{x}}_{B}|
spoiling mediation from the get go
Anastopoulos and Hu 2018
;
Anastopoulos et al. 2021
.
This is similar to what happens in the classical theory: the Coulomb gauge spoils the manifest Lorentz locality of electromagnetism in favour of computational efficiency. However, it is only ‘manifest’ Lorentz locality that is lost, all the physics remains unchanged and, in particular, relativistic causality still holds. Does a similar thing happen with subsystem locality in the quantum theory?

Dirac quantisation of gauge systems may be more suitable for keeping subsystem locality explicit. For example, in Gupta-Bleuler quantisation for the electromagnetic field
Cohen-Tannoudji et al. 1989
, we have four components each coupled to the sources with no interaction terms coupling the sources directly. Thus, formally, we have four massless scalar fields coupled to the sources and the dynamics resembles that studied in the main text. The gauge redundancy is removed instead by imposing a constraint on the physical states, a constraint that is maintained by the equations of motion. Perhaps a similar result can be derived in this setting.

Finally, let us briefly comment on the significance of this result on the discussion surrounding the low energy experimental tests of quantum gravity. The generation of entanglement between two massive quantum systems via gravity has been argued to provide a theory-independent witness of the nonclassicality of the gravitational field
Bose et al. 2017
;
Marletto and Vedral 2017
;
Krisnanda et al. 2017
;
Fragkos et al. 2022
;
Huggett et al. 2022
. This argument invokes theorems from quantum information theory and their post-quantum generalisations, which we may call ‘LOCC theorems’. These theorems state that a classical system cannot mediate the creation of entanglement between two systems. These no-go theorems are often presented as the dilemma: if gravity can create entanglement, then it is either non-local or non-classical.

The notion of locality at stake in these theorems is the quantum information notion of mediation, not that of relativistic locality. If no field theory obeyed the kind of locality in the LOCC theorems, the theorems would not have a strong bite, since a no-go theorem’s potency lies in the extent to which its conflicting assumptions are considered desirable properties of physical theories.
Granted the above limitations, our result shows that the particular form of the locality assumption in the LOCC theorems is indeed a property of a relativistic quantum field theory in the appropriate regime, thus strengthening the relevance of these theorems to the low-energy test of quantum gravity debate. The next step would be to prove a similar result in linearised quantum gravity.

We expect this work to spark further investigations on the above open questions in order to generalise the result given here to the degree possible. In general, a relativistic quantum field theory will not give rise to circuits in with unitary gates embedded in regions of spacetime. At a minimum, certain localisation conditions have to be satisfied by the states of such a theory.
The end result of the effort started here would hopefully be a clear demarcation of the approximations and assumptions needed in order for physically relevant quantum field theories to be cast in a quantum information—quantum circuit—language.

Appendix A
Parametric approximation

In the context of a composite system
\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}
evolving according to a Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{1}+\hat{H}_{2}+\hat{H}_{12}
, the parametric approximation consists of assuming that system
\mathcal{H}_{1}
evolves according to their free Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{1}
only, thus neglecting the effect of system
\mathcal{H}_{2}
on it.

A.1
One branch

Assume the systems initially start at
t=0
in a separable state

|\Psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle|\phi\rangle.

(38)

The parametric approximation consists of the following two assumptions.

1.

The state remains separable, that is, for all times, we may write

|\Psi(t)\rangle=|\psi(t)\rangle|\phi(t)\rangle.

(39)

2.

System
\mathcal{H}_{1}
evolves according to its free Hamiltonian:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}|\psi(t)\rangle=-i\hat{H}_{1}|\psi(t)\rangle.

(40)

Then, explicit differentiation of (
39
) gives

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}|\Psi(t)\rangle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big(|\psi(t)\rangle|\phi(t)\rangle\big)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big(|\psi(t)\rangle\big)|\phi(t)\rangle+|\psi(t)\rangle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}|\phi(t)\rangle,

(41)

while the Schrödinger equation gives

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}|\Psi(t)\rangle=-i\hat{H}|\Psi(t)\rangle=-i(\hat{H}_{1}+\hat{H}_{2}+\hat{H}_{12})|\psi(t)\rangle|\phi(t)\rangle.

(42)

Equating the right hand sides of these two formulas, using (
40
), and finally projecting onto
|\psi(t)\rangle
, we obtain a Schrödinger equation for the state of system
\mathcal{H}_{2}
:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}|\phi(t)\rangle=-i\left(\hat{H}_{1}+\langle\hat{H}_{12}\rangle^{\psi(t)}\right)|\phi(t)\rangle.

(43)

In this equation, the operator
\hat{H}_{12}
acting on the composite system
\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}
is replaced by the operator

\langle\hat{H}_{12}\rangle^{\psi(t)}=\langle\psi(t)|\hat{H}_{12}|\psi(t)\rangle

(44)

acting only on
\mathcal{H}_{2}
. One now can solve for the full evolution of the state of the composite system (
39
) by first solving the free evolution (
40
) of
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, and then the driven evolution (
43
) of
\mathcal{H}_{2}
.

A.2
Several Branches

Let us now consider a more general case that allows for entanglement. Let us assume that, that for all times
t\geq 0
, the state of the system can be written as

|\Psi(t)\rangle=\sum_{i}a_{i}|\psi_{i}(t)\rangle|\phi_{i}(t)\rangle,

(45)

where, for all
i
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}|\psi_{i}(t)\rangle=-i\hat{H}_{1}|\psi_{i}(t)\rangle.

(46)

Like in the previous case, we take the explicit time derivative of (
45
), and equate it to the Schrödinger equation, using (
46
) to cancel some terms. This time, we obtain

\sum_{i}a_{i}|\psi_{i}(t)\rangle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}|\phi_{i}(t)\rangle=-i\sum_{i}a_{i}\left(|\psi_{i}(t)\rangle\hat{H}_{2}|\phi_{i}(t)\rangle+\hat{H}_{12}|\psi_{i}(t)\rangle|\phi_{i}(t)\rangle\right).

(47)

Equations (
45
) and (
46
) are analogous to (
39
) and (
40
), respectively. We see that in this case we need two extra assumptions to get a separate Schrödinger equation for each
|\phi_{i}(t)\rangle
. First, we need to assume that the
|\psi_{i}(t)\rangle
are orthogonal. Assuming that they are, then, (or simply ignoring their overlap), we obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}|\phi_{i}(t)\rangle=-i\hat{H}_{2}|\phi_{i}(t)\rangle-\sum_{j}a_{j}\langle\psi_{i}(t)|\hat{H}_{12}|\psi_{j}(t)\rangle|\phi_{j}(t)\rangle.

(48)

We then also assume that

\langle\psi_{i}(t)|\hat{H}_{12}|\psi_{j}(t)\rangle=\delta_{ij}\langle\psi_{i}(t)|\hat{H}_{12}|\psi_{i}(t)\rangle.

(49)

Therefore, given the two assumptions (
45
) and (
46
), the orthonormality of the
|\psi_{i}\rangle
and the equation above, we obtain the equation we seek:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}|\phi_{i}(t)\rangle=-i\left(\hat{H}_{2}+\langle\hat{H}_{12}\rangle^{\psi_{i}(t)}\right)|\phi_{i}(t)\rangle.

(50)

In the system we consider in this work, the assumptions of orthogonality and (
49
) will be both taken care of by the presence of the control qudits.

Appendix B
Quantum field, quantum controlled sources

We now apply the previous discussion to the case relevant to the main text.

B.1
Setup

We may write the Hamiltonian of the full system as

\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}_{A}(t)+\hat{H}_{B}(t)+\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}},

(51)

where
\hat{H}_{A}(t)
acts only on particle
A
and the first qudit,
\hat{H}_{B}(t)
acts only on particle
B
and the second qudit,
\hat{H}_{0}
is the free Hamiltonian of the field and
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}
couples both particles to the field.

The time dependence in
\hat{H}_{A}(t)
represents a possible time-dependent driving of the particle
A
by the first qubit. We assume that

\hat{H}_{A}(t)=\sum_{r=1}^{d}|r\rangle\!\langle r|\otimes\hat{H}^{r}_{A}(t),

(52)

where
|r\rangle
are the eigenstates of the qudit in the computational basis. The same holds for for
\hat{H}_{B}(t)
.
The free Hamiltonian of the field is the standard

\hat{H}_{0}=\int\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.99997pt\bar{}\hskip 1.99997pt^{3}k\,\omega_{k}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\k{\hat}}a_{\k{,}}

(53)

with
\omega_{k}=\sqrt{m^{2}+k^{2}}
. Finally, the interaction Hamiltonian couples each particle to the field

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,\hat{\phi}({\bm{x}})\hat{\rho}({\bm{x}}),

(54)

where
\hat{\rho}({\bm{x}})=\hat{\rho}_{A}({\bm{x}})+\hat{\rho}_{B}({\bm{x}})
is the sum of two charge density operators, defined as

\hat{\rho}_{A}({\bm{x}})=\int\mathrm{d}x_{A}\,\sigma_{A}({\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}_{A})|{\bm{x}}_{A}\rangle\!\langle{\bm{x}}_{A}|,

(55)

with
|{\bm{x}}_{A}\rangle
the position eigenstates of particle
A
, and
\sigma_{A}({\bm{x}})
a real-valued function with compact support around
{\bm{x}}=\bm{0}
representing the charge distribution of particle
A
;
\hat{\rho}_{B}
is defined similarly.

B.2
Two parametric approximations

Write the initial state of the system as

|\Psi\rangle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{r,s\in\{1,\dots,d\}}|rs\rangle|\Psi^{rs}\rangle,

(56)

where
|\Psi^{rs}\rangle
is a state for the sources and the field. We take the multi-branch parametric approximation of
A.2
, with the qudits evolving freely. This represents the qudits acting as controls on the evolution of the particles and field, without suffering a back-reaction.

In practice, we assume that we can write the state at all times as

|\Psi(t)\rangle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{r,s\in\{1,\dots,d\}}|rs\rangle|\Psi^{rs}(t)\rangle.

(57)

This is the combination of both (
45
) and (
46
) (since
|rs\rangle
has no free Hamiltonian in this case, its free evolution is to stand still).
The different states
|rs\rangle
of the control qudits are naturally orthonormal, so we are left to verify that (
49
) holds, or, in other words that

\langle rs|\hat{H}(t)|r^{\prime}s^{\prime}\rangle=\delta_{rr^{\prime}}\delta_{ss^{\prime}}\langle rs|\hat{H}(t)|r^{\prime}s^{\prime}\rangle.

(58)

This is indeed the case, since
\hat{H}_{0}
and
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}
act trivially on the control qudits, and the
\hat{H}_{A}
and
\hat{H}_{B}
are block-diagonal on the computational basis. All the assumptions for the multi-branch parametric approximation hold, and we therefore obtain a Schrödinger equation for the sources and the field for each value of the control qudits:

|\Psi^{rs}(t)\rangle=-i\hat{H}^{rs}(t)|\Psi^{rs}(t)\rangle,

(59)

where
\hat{H}^{rs}(t)=\hat{H}_{A}^{r}(t)+\hat{H}_{B}^{s}(t)+\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}
.

We can now solve the
d^{2}
equations (
59
) by taking single branch parametric approximations. We assume that, at all times,

|\Psi^{rs}(t)\rangle=|\psi_{A}^{r}(t)\rangle|\psi_{B}^{s}(t)\rangle|\psi_{\phi}^{rs}(t)\rangle,

(60)

and that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}|\psi_{A}^{r}(t)\rangle=-i\hat{H}_{A}^{r}(t)|\psi_{A}^{r}(t)\rangle,\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}|\psi_{B}^{s}(t)\rangle=-i\hat{H}_{B}^{s}(t)|\psi_{B}^{s}(t)\rangle.

(61)

It then follows, by the arguments in
A.1
, that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}|\phi^{rs}(t)\rangle=-i\left(\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}^{rs}(t)\right)|\psi_{\phi}^{rs}(t)\rangle,

(62)

where

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}^{rs}(t)=\langle\psi_{A}^{r}(t)\psi_{B}^{s}(t)|\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}|\psi_{A}^{r}(t)\psi_{B}^{s}(t)\rangle.

(63)

The evolution of
|\phi^{rs}(t)\rangle
is that of a quantum field coupled to a prescribed time-evolving classical source
\rho^{rs}(t,{\bm{x}})
given by

\rho^{rs}(t,{\bm{x}})=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x_{A}\,\sigma_{A}({\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}_{A})|\psi_{A}^{r}(t,{\bm{x}}_{A})|^{2}+\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x_{B}\,\sigma_{B}({\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}_{B})|\psi_{B}^{s}(t,{\bm{x}}_{B})|^{2}\equiv\rho_{A}^{r}(t,{\bm{x}})+\rho_{B}^{s}(t,{\bm{x}}),

(64)

where
\psi_{A}^{r}(t,{\bm{x}}_{A})\equiv\langle{\bm{x}}_{a}|\psi_{A}^{r}(t)\rangle
is the wavefunction of the centre of mass of the source
A
, in position basis, and similarly for
\psi_{B}^{s}(t,{\bm{x}})
.

(70)

\hat{U}_{B\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})

Therefore, to study the subsystem locality of the full evolution

\hat{U}^{rs}_{\phi}

\hat{U}^{rs}_{\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})=e^{\Omega^{rs}(t_{1},t_{2})}\hat{U}^{s}_{\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})\hat{U}^{r}_{\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})e^{-i\hat{H}_{0}(t_{2}-t_{1})}

\hat{H}_{0}

\Omega^{rs}(t_{1},t_{2})

Therefore the unitary evolution of the full system can be written in field mediation form—

\Omega^{rs}(t_{1},t_{2})

In

\Omega^{rs}(t_{1},t_{2})

\rho_{A}^{r}
and
\rho_{B}^{s}
of the charge densities
\hat{\rho}_{A}
and
\hat{\rho}_{B}
are spacelike separated in all branches.
This is because the phase
\Omega^{rs}(t_{1},t_{2})
may be written as

\displaystyle\Omega^{rs}(t_{1},t_{2})=

\displaystyle\iint_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\iint\mathrm{d}^{3}x\mathrm{d}^{3}x^{\prime}\,\rho_{A}^{r}(t,{\bm{x}})\rho_{B}^{s}(t^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\cdot[\hat{\phi}_{I}(t,{\bm{x}}),\hat{\phi}_{I}(t^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})]

(72)

\displaystyle+\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathrm{d}t\int_{t_{1}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\iint\mathrm{d}^{3}x\mathrm{d}^{3}x^{\prime}\,\big(\rho_{A}^{r}(t,{\bm{x}})\rho_{B}^{s}(t^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})+\rho_{A}^{r}(t^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\rho_{B}^{s}(t,{\bm{x}})\big)\cdot[\hat{\phi}_{I}(t,{\bm{x}}),\hat{\phi}_{I}(t^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})].

By microcausality,
[\hat{\phi}_{I}(t,{\bm{x}}),\hat{\phi}_{I}(t^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})]
is required to vanish whenever
(t,{\bm{x}})
and
(t^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})
are spacelike separated. Therefore, if between
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
,
\rho_{A}^{r}
and
\rho_{B}^{s}
are only non-zero in spacelike separated regions, the whole integral vanishes identically.
If this is the case in each
rs
branch, then full the evolution can be written in field mediation form as

\hat{U}(t_{1},t_{2})=\hat{U}_{B\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})\circ\hat{U}_{A\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})\circ e^{-i\hat{H}_{0}(t_{2}-t_{1})}.

(73)

The charge density expectation values are defined as

\rho_{A}(t,{\bm{x}})=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x_{A}\,\sigma_{A}({\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}_{A})|\psi_{A}^{r}(t,{\bm{x}}_{A})|^{2},

(74)

and similarly for
\rho_{B}
, where
\sigma_{A}
is a real-valued function with compact support, representing the charge distribution of the particle. Due to quantum uncertainty, in non-relativistic quantum mechanics, the function
\psi_{A}^{r}
will not have compact support for any finite time, so neither will
\rho_{A}
. However, one can take an approximation where
|\psi_{A}|^{2}
is infinitesimal outside some region, and ignore its contribution. At that level of approximation, then,
\rho_{A}
will have compact support, and one can talk about spacetime separation.

Appendix C
Quantum field, classical sources

Let us solve the evolution of a quantum field in the presence of a classical source
\rho(t)
. We may rewrite the Hamiltonian in the Schrödinger picture for this system as

\hat{H}_{\phi}^{\rho}(t)=\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}^{\rho}(t)

(75)

and the field
\hat{\phi}
and the momentum
\hat{\pi}
are expressed as in terms of creation/annihilation operators as usual.

C.1
Evolution of the field in the presence of a classical source

We look for the family of propagators
\hat{U}^{\rho}_{\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})
satisfying the Schrödinger equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{2}}\hat{U}^{\rho}_{\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})=-i\hat{H}_{\phi}^{\rho}(t_{2})\hat{U}_{\phi}^{\rho}(t_{1},t_{2})

(76)

and the group property

\hat{U}_{\phi}^{\rho}(t_{2},t_{3})\hat{U}_{\phi}^{\rho}(t_{1},t_{2})=\hat{U}_{\phi}^{\rho}(t_{1},t_{3}).

(77)

To do so, we introduce the interaction picture

C.2
Physical interpretation

We can rewrite the Schrödinger picture propagator using (
86
) and the Magnus expansion as

\boxed{\hat{U}^{\rho}_{\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})=e^{\Omega^{\rho}_{2}(t_{1},t_{2})}\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{\rho}{(t_{1},t_{2})}e^{-i\hat{H}_{0}(t_{2}-t_{1})},}

(87)

where we have defined the operator

\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{\rho}{(t_{1},t_{2})}=e^{-i\hat{H}_{0}(t_{2}-t_{0})}e^{\hat{\Omega}^{\rho}_{1}(t_{1},t_{2})}e^{i\hat{H}_{0}(t_{2}-t_{0})}=\exp\left(e^{-i\hat{H}_{0}(t_{2}-t_{0})}\hat{\Omega}^{\rho}_{1}(t_{1},t_{2})e^{i\hat{H}_{0}(t_{2}-t_{0})}\right).

(88)

We will show that
\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{\rho}{(t_{1},t_{2})}
is a displacement operator. Therefore the full evolution
\hat{U}^{\rho}_{\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})
consists of the free evolution of the field followed by a displacement operator, up to a global phase that depends only on the classical source.

Note that in classic introductory texts, such as
Cohen-Tannoudji et al. 1989
;
Gerry and Knight 2005
the overall phase factor
e^{\Omega_{2}(t_{1},t_{2})}
is omitted when deriving the evolution of a quantum field coupled to a classical source. This factor is essential in ensuring the correct groupoid property
\hat{U}^{\rho}_{\phi}(t_{2},t_{3})\hat{U}^{\rho}_{\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})=\hat{U}^{\rho}_{\phi}(t_{1},t_{3})
. In the quantum case, this factor is also essential in deriving the correct evolution. However, the phase can be safely neglected when one is interested in a classical source, and evolving only from an initial time to a final time.

To see that
\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{\rho}{(t_{1},t_{2})}
is a displacement operator, we start by writing
\hat{\Omega}^{\rho}_{1}(t_{1},t_{2})
in terms of the creation/annihilation operators. Using the property
e^{i\hat{H}_{0}T}\hat{a}_{\k{e}}^{-i\hat{H}_{0}T}=\hat{a}_{\k{e}}^{-i\omega_{k}T}
, we may write

e^{-i\hat{H}_{0}(t_{2}-t_{0})}\hat{\Omega}^{\rho}_{1}(t_{1},t_{2})e^{i\hat{H}_{0}(t_{2}-t_{0})}=-i\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathrm{d}t\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,\rho(t,{\bm{x}})\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.99997pt\bar{}\hskip 1.99997pt^{3}k}{\sqrt{2\omega_{k}}}\left(\hat{a}_{\k{e}}^{i\k{\cdot}{\bm{x}}}e^{-i\omega_{k}(t-t_{2})}+\hat{a}_{\k{^}}\dagger e^{-i\k{\cdot}{\bm{x}}}e^{i\omega_{k}(t-t_{2})}\right).

(89)

Now, define

\alpha^{\rho}(\k{;}t_{1},t_{2})=-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2\omega_{k}}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathrm{d}t\,e^{-i\omega_{k}(t_{2}-t)}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,\rho(t,x)e^{-i\k{\cdot}{\bm{x}}},

(90)

so that

\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{\rho}{(t_{1},t_{2})}=\hat{D}[\alpha^{\rho}(t_{1},t_{2})],

(91)

where

\hat{D}[\alpha]=\exp\left(\int\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.99997pt\bar{}\hskip 1.99997pt^{3}k(\alpha(\k{)}\hat{a}_{\k{^}}\dagger-\alpha(\k{)}^{*}\hat{a}_{\k{)}}\right),

(92)

is a displacement operator.

The
\alpha^{\rho}(\k{;}t_{1},t_{2})
have a physical meaning in terms of the classical equations of motions: they are the normal variables of the field perturbation
\phi^{\rho}(t,{\bm{x}};t_{1})
caused by the sources from
t_{1}
to
t
. In other words,

\phi^{\rho}(t,{\bm{x}};t_{1})=\theta(t-t_{1})\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.99997pt\bar{}\hskip 1.99997pt^{3}k}{\sqrt{2\omega_{k}}}\left(\alpha^{\rho}(\k{;}t_{1},t)e^{i\k{\cdot}{\bm{x}}}+\alpha^{\rho}(\k{;}t_{1},t)^{*}e^{-i\k{\cdot}{\bm{x}}}\right)

(93)

is the unique solution of the classical Klein-Gordon equation
(\square-m^{2})\phi=\rho
that vanishes for
t\leq t_{1}
.

We can now characterise the effect of
\hat{U}^{\rho}_{\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})
on the creation/annihilation operators, and hence on the field observables. Note that

\hat{U}^{\rho}_{\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\k{\hat}}U^{\rho}_{\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})=\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{\rho}(t_{1},t_{2})^{\dagger}\left(\hat{a}_{\k{e}}^{-i\omega_{\k{(}}t_{2}-t_{1})}\right)\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{\rho}(t_{1},t_{2})=\hat{a}_{\k{e}}^{-i\omega_{\k{(}}t_{2}-t_{1})}+\alpha^{\rho}(\k{;}t_{1},t_{2})

(94)

and that, therefore

\hat{U}^{\rho}_{\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})^{\dagger}\hat{\phi}_{I}(t_{1},{\bm{x}})\hat{U}^{\rho}_{\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})=\hat{\phi}_{I}(t_{2},{\bm{x}})+\phi^{\rho}(t_{2},{\bm{x}};t_{1}).

(95)

Therefore,
\hat{U}^{\rho}_{\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})
freely evolves the state of the field from
t_{1}
to
t_{2}
and then displaces it by the classical field contributed by
\rho
during the interval
[t_{1},t_{2}]
, up to a phase.

C.3
Evolution of the field in the presence of two classical sources

We now consider the case where
\rho=\rho_{A}+\rho_{B}
, and find sufficient conditions to ensure that
\hat{U}^{\rho}_{\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})
split in terms that contain at most one of
\rho_{A}
and
\rho_{B}
(which will then translate in subsystem-locality in the quantum case).

First, note that
\Omega_{2}
splits into three terms,

\Omega^{\rho}_{2}(t_{1},t_{2})=\Omega^{\rho_{A}}_{2}(t_{1},t_{2})+\Omega^{\rho_{B}}_{2}(t_{1},t_{2})+\Omega_{2}^{\mathrm{cross}}(t_{1},t_{2};\rho_{A},\rho_{B}),

(96)

where
\Omega_{2}^{\rho_{A}}
and
\Omega_{2}^{\rho_{B}}
depend only on
\rho_{A}
and
\rho_{B}
, respectively, and the
\Omega^{\mathrm{cross}}
is

\Omega_{2}^{\mathrm{cross}}(t_{1},t_{2};\rho_{A},\rho_{B})=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathrm{d}t\int_{t_{1}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\iint\mathrm{d}^{3}x\mathrm{d}^{3}x^{\prime}\,\big(\rho_{A}(t,{\bm{x}})\rho_{B}(t^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})+\rho_{B}(t,{\bm{x}})\rho_{A}(t^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\big)[\hat{\phi}_{I}(t,{\bm{x}}),\hat{\phi}_{I}(t^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})].

(97)

Thanks to the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula,
e^{\hat{\Omega}^{\rho}_{1}}
also splits into three terms

e^{\hat{\Omega}^{\rho}_{1}(t_{1},t_{2})}=e^{\Omega^{\mathrm{cross}}_{1}(t_{1},t_{2};\rho_{A},\rho_{B})}e^{\hat{\Omega}^{\rho_{B}}_{1}(t_{1},t_{2})}e^{\hat{\Omega}^{\rho_{A}}_{1}(t_{1},t_{2})},

(98)

where

\!\!\!\!\Omega_{1}^{\mathrm{cross}}(t_{1},t_{2};\rho_{A},\rho_{B})=\frac{1}{2}[\hat{\Omega}^{\rho_{A}}_{1}(t_{1},t_{2}),\hat{\Omega}^{\rho_{B}}_{1}(t_{1},t_{2})]=-\frac{1}{2}\iint_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\iint\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,\mathrm{d}^{3}x^{\prime}\,\rho_{A}(t,{\bm{x}})\rho_{B}(t^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})[\phi_{I}(t,{\bm{x}}),\phi_{I}(t^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})].

(99)

Therefore, if we define

\Omega_{\mathrm{cross}}(t_{1},t_{2};\rho_{A},\rho_{B})=\Omega_{1}^{\mathrm{cross}}(t_{1},t_{2};\rho_{A},\rho_{B})+\Omega_{2}^{\mathrm{cross}}(t_{1},t_{2};\rho_{A},\rho_{B}),

(100)

we may write the interaction picture evolution of the field as a product of unitaries that depend on only one of
\rho_{A}
or
\rho_{B}
, up to a phase that depends on both:

\hat{U}_{I}^{\rho}(t_{1},t_{2})=e^{\Omega_{\mathrm{cross}}(t_{1},t_{2};\rho_{A},\rho_{B})}\hat{U}^{\rho_{B}}_{I}(t_{1},t_{2})\hat{U}^{\rho_{A}}_{I}(t_{1},t_{2}).

(101)

Repeating the derivation of the previous section, we can put the complete evolution of the field in physical terms

\hat{U}^{\rho}_{\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})=e^{\Omega_{\mathrm{cross}}(t_{1},t_{2};\rho_{A},\rho_{B})}\left(e^{\Omega^{\rho_{B}}_{2}(t_{1},t_{2})}\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{\rho_{B}}_{(t_{1},t_{2})}\right)\left(e^{\Omega^{\rho_{A}}_{2}(t_{1},t_{2})}\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{\rho_{A}}_{(t_{1},t_{2})}\right)e^{-i\hat{H}_{0}(t_{2}-t_{1})}.

(102)

We can write the above in more compact form as

\boxed{\hat{U}^{\rho}_{\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})=e^{\Omega_{\mathrm{cross}}(t_{1},t_{2};\rho_{A},\rho_{B})}\hat{U}^{\rho_{B}}_{\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})\hat{U}^{\rho_{A}}_{\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})e^{-i\hat{H}_{0}(t_{1},t_{2})},}

(103)

where
\hat{U}^{\rho_{A}}(t_{1},t_{2})=e^{\Omega^{\rho_{A}}_{2}(t_{1},t_{2})}\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{\rho_{A}}_{(t_{1},t_{2})}
, and similarly for
\hat{U}^{\rho_{A}}(t_{1},t_{2})
.

Appendix D
Scalar analog of the GME phases

Consider a coherent state of the field

|f\rangle=\hat{D}[\alpha_{f}]|0\rangle

(104)

peaked around a classical configuration with values

f({\bm{x}})=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.99997pt\bar{}\hskip 1.99997pt^{3}k}{\sqrt{2\omega_{k}}}\left(\alpha_{f}(\k{)}e^{i\k{\cdot}{\bm{x}}}+\alpha_{f}(\k{)}^{*}e^{-i\k{\cdot}{\bm{x}}}\right)

(105)

and momentum

\pi_{f}({\bm{x}})=-i\int\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.99997pt\bar{}\hskip 1.99997pt^{3}k\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{k}}{2}}\left(\alpha_{f}(\k{)}e^{i\k{\cdot}{\bm{x}}}-\alpha_{f}(\k{)}^{*}e^{-i\k{\cdot}{\bm{x}}}\right)

(106)

under the influence of a classical current
\rho
. We will show that the evolution
\hat{U}^{\rho}_{\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})
sends this coherent state to the coherent state peaked around the classical solution
\phi_{f}^{\rho}
to the Klein-Gordon equation with source
\rho
and initial conditions
\smash{(\phi,\dot{\phi})=(f,\pi_{f})}
:

\hat{U}^{\rho}_{\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})|f\rangle=e^{i\theta^{\rho}_{f}}|\phi^{\rho}_{f}\rangle,

(107)

where the phase
\theta^{\rho}_{f}
has a clear physical intepretation as the action of the on-shell trajectory of the field from
\phi^{\rho}_{f}(t_{1})
to
\phi^{\rho}_{f}(t_{2})
:

\theta^{\rho}_{f}=S_{\phi}(\phi^{\rho}_{f};t_{1},t_{2}),

(108)

First, using the definition (
87
) of
\hat{U}^{\rho}_{\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})
we have

\displaystyle\hat{U}^{\rho}_{\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})|f\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{\Omega^{\rho}_{2}(t_{1},t_{2})}\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{\rho}{(t_{1},t_{2})}e^{-i\hat{H}_{0}(t_{2}-t_{1})}\hat{D}[\alpha_{f}]e^{i\hat{H}_{0}(t_{2}-t_{1})}|0\rangle

(109)

\displaystyle=e^{\Omega^{\rho}_{2}(t_{1},t_{2})}\hat{D}[\alpha^{\rho}(t_{1},t_{2})]\hat{D}[\alpha_{f}e^{-i\omega(t_{2}-t_{1})}]|0\rangle,

where we used the fact that
e^{i\hat{H}_{0}t}|0\rangle=|0\rangle
and that
e^{i\hat{H}_{0}t}\hat{a}_{\k{e}}^{-i\hat{H}_{0}t}=\hat{a}_{\k{e}}^{-i\omega_{k}t}
to shift the argument of the first displacement operator (defined in (
92
)). This implements the free evolution of
|f\rangle
. Now, merging the two displacement operators, we obtain

\hat{U}^{\rho}_{\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})|f\rangle=e^{i\theta^{\rho}_{f}}|\phi_{f}^{\rho}(t_{1},t_{2})\rangle,

(110)

where,

|\phi_{f}^{\rho}(t_{1},t_{2})\rangle=\hat{D}[\alpha_{f}e^{-i\omega t}+\alpha^{\rho}(t_{1},t_{2})]|0\rangle,

(111)

and
\phi_{f}^{\rho}(t_{1},t_{2})
is the solution to the Klein Gordon equation with source
\rho
with initial conditions
(\phi,\dot{\phi})=(f,\pi_{f})
at
t_{1}
.

Let us look at the phase

\theta^{\rho}_{f}=-i\big(\Omega^{\rho}_{2}(t_{1},t_{2})+\varphi^{\rho}_{f}(t_{1},t_{2})\big),

(112)

where
\varphi^{\rho}_{f}(t_{1},t_{2})
appeared when merging the two displacement operators
using the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula:

\varphi^{\rho}_{f}(t_{1},t_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\int\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.99997pt\bar{}\hskip 1.99997pt^{3}k\left(\alpha^{\rho}(\k{;}t_{1},t_{2})^{*}\alpha_{f}e^{-i\omega(t_{2}-t_{1})}-c.c\right).

(113)

Using the definition (
90
) for
\alpha^{\rho}
we get

\varphi^{\rho}_{f}(t_{1},t_{2})=-\frac{i}{2}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathrm{d}t\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.99997pt\bar{}\hskip 1.99997pt^{3}k}{\sqrt{2\omega_{k}}}\left(\rho(t,\k{)}\alpha_{f}e^{-i\omega(t-t_{1})}+c.c\right).

(114)

To evaluate
\Omega_{2}^{\rho}
, recall (or verify) that

[\hat{\phi}_{I}(t,{\bm{x}}),\hat{\phi}_{I}(t^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})]=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.99997pt\bar{}\hskip 1.99997pt^{3}k}{2\omega_{k}}\left(e^{i\k{\cdot}({\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime})}e^{-i\omega_{k}(t-t^{\prime})}-c.c.\right),

(115)

and substitute this in the expression (
85
) for
\Omega_{2}^{\rho}
to obtain

\Omega_{2}^{\rho}(t_{1},t_{2})=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathrm{d}t\int_{t_{1}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.99997pt\bar{}\hskip 1.99997pt^{3}k}{2\omega_{k}}\left(\rho(t,\k{)}^{*}\rho(t^{\prime},\k{)}e^{-i\omega_{k}(t-t^{\prime})}-c.c.\right).

(116)

Using the definition (
90
) for the normal variables of the field generated by
\rho
, we get

\Omega_{2}^{\rho}(t_{1},t_{2})=-\frac{i}{2}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathrm{d}t\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.99997pt\bar{}\hskip 1.99997pt^{3}k}{\sqrt{2\omega_{k}}}\left(\rho(t,\k{)}^{*}\alpha^{\rho}(\k{;}t_{0},t)+c.c.\right).

(117)

So putting these two together, we get

\theta^{\rho}_{f}=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathrm{d}t\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,\rho(t,{\bm{x}})\phi^{\rho}_{f}(t,{\bm{x}};t_{1}),

(118)

where we used the relationship between the field’s normal variable and its Fourier transform
(\phi(\k{)}/2=\alpha(\k{)}/\sqrt{2\omega_{k}})
.
The field sector of the Lagrangian density corresponding to the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}^{\rho}
is

\mathcal{L}_{\phi}=\frac{1}{2}\phi(\square-m^{2})\phi-\rho\phi,

(119)

and when the field is on-shell and satisfies the field equations, we have

\mathcal{L}_{\phi}=-\frac{1}{2}\rho\phi.

(120)

So we see that the phases generated during the evolution are the values of the on-shell action of the field.
