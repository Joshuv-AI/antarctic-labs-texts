Multivariate polynomial splines on generalized oranges
Maritza Sirvent, Tatyana Sorokina, Nelly Villamizar, Beihui Yuan

Abstract
We consider spaces of multivariate splines defined on a particular type of simplicial partitions that we call (generalized) oranges . Such partitions are composed of a finite number of maximal faces with exactly one shared medial face. We reduce the problem of finding the dimension of splines on oranges to computing dimensions of splines on simpler, lower-dimensional partitions that we call projected oranges . We use both algebraic and Bernstein-Bézier tools.

1
Introduction and preliminaries

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial partition of an underlying domain
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
formed as a geometric realization of a
k
-dimensional simplicial complex. For integers
0\leqslant r\leqslant d
, the space of splines
S^{r}_{d}(\Delta)
is defined as the set of
C^{r}
-smooth piecewise polynomial functions of degree at most
d
on the partition
\Delta
.
The space
S^{r}_{d}(\Delta)
is a real vector space. These spline spaces are used throughout numerical analysis and approximation theory to solve diverse problems such as the interpolation and approximation of data, design of curves and surfaces, and, in the finite element method, for the solution of differential equations, among others.
A fundamental question is
to determine the dimension of such spaces. The problem has proven to be difficult due to the dependency of the dimension on the specific geometry of
\Delta
.

In 1973, Strang
[
12
]
, published a famous conjecture on the generic dimension of
S^{1}_{d}(\Delta)
for the bivariate case.
A simplicial partition
\Delta
is said to be generic provided that for all sufficiently small perturbations of the location of the vertices of
\Delta
, the resulting decomposition
\tilde{\Delta}
satisfies
\dim S^{r}_{d}(\tilde{\Delta})=\dim S^{r}_{d}(\Delta)
, see
[
6
]
where this definition was explicitly coined. Although the term generic dimension was not explicitly mentioned by Strang, he was aware of the fact that the dimension might increase for some particular configurations.
In
[
3
]
, Alfeld and Schumaker prove a dimension formula for
S^{r}_{d}(\Delta)
, when the polynomial degree
d\geqslant 4r+1
, and
\Delta
is a general triangulation. This result was later extended to
d\geqslant 3r+2
by Hong in
[
9
]
, and to
d\geqslant 3r+1
for generic triangulations by Alfeld and Schumaker in
[
4
]
. For
r<d<3r+2
, the only dimension known explicitly is the case when
r=1
and
d=4
, see
[
2
]
.

In 1988, Billera, see
[
7
]
, pioneered the use of algebraic homological methods to study multivariate splines, and proved the conjecture given by Strang.

For the trivariate case, Alfeld, Schumaker, and Whiteley, see
[
6
]
, use the Bernstein-Bézier analysis and algebraic homology and rigidity theory to give an explicit expression for the generic dimension of
S^{1}_{d}(\Delta)
, for
d\geqslant 8
.

For the spatial dimension
k\geqslant 3
, no explicit dimension formula is known for general triangulations. There are results on upper and lower bounds and exact dimensions for some specific triangulations. For example, in
[
8
]
, DiPasquale and Villamizar proved a lower bound for the dimension for splines on tetrahedral stars of a vertex.
A natural generalization of a star of a vertex is a star of a simplex.
A star of a simplex
\tau\in\Delta
is the set of simplices in
\Delta
that contain
\tau
. If a simplicial complex
\Delta
equals the closure of the star of
\tau
, we just call
\Delta
a star of
\tau
.
In this paper, we study stars of a simplex of arbitrary dimension. We call such stars
generalized oranges
or
oranges
.

Recall that a
k
-dimensional simplicial complex is
pure
is all its maximal faces are of dimension
k
.

Definition 1.1

(Generalized orange)
.

For integers
0\leqslant i\leqslant k
, a
(k,i)
-orange
is a pure
k
-dimensional simplicial complex
\mathcal{O}
composed by
n
maximal faces and with exactly one
face
\tau
of dimension
k-i
such that every maximal face of
\mathcal{O}
contains
\tau
.
We say that
\tau
is a
medial simplex
.

Note that the two extremal cases are
i=0
and
i=k
. For
i=0
,
\Delta=\tau
is a simplex. For
i=k
, the medial face
\tau
is a vertex, and
\Delta
is a star of a vertex.
Examples of
(3,2),(2,1)
, and
(3,1)
-oranges are shown in
Figs.
1
,
2
and
3
, respectively.

Figure 1:
Example of a
(3,2)
-orange, it is a 3-dimensional simplicial complex with medial simplex
\tau
, which is the
1
-face common to all tetrahedra.

Historically, the term “orange" was introduced in
[
5
]
to describe a tetrahedral partition with exactly one interior edge which is common to all tetrahedra in the partition, see Fig.
1
.
This matches our definition of a
(3,2)
-orange except that the medial edge does not have to be interior. The dimension for the spline spaces on a
(3,2)
-orange with interior medial edge was found in
[
5
]
using the trivariate cofactor method, see
[
10
]
for the proof as well. In
[
11
]
, it was noted that the dimension for the spline spaces on a
(3,2)
-orange with interior medial edge can be computed by adding up the dimensions of bivariate splines on a
planar vertex star obtained by projecting the orange along the common interior edge.

In this article we generalize the idea of the projection to an
i
-dimensional star of a vertex, and relate the dimension of the spline spaces over a
(k,i)
-orange
\mathcal{O}
to those over a projected orange
\mathcal{C}
, see Section
2
for the exact definition, by proving the following result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{i}
be the projected orange of a
(k,i)
-orange
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Then

\dim S^{r}_{d}(\mathcal{O})=\sum_{j=0}^{d}\binom{d+k-i-j-1}{k-i-1}\dim S^{r}_{i}(\mathcal{C}).

(1)

For the two extremal cases,
i=0
and
i=k
, Theorem
1.1
trivially holds. In the case
i=0
, the orange
\mathcal{O}=\tau
is a simplex,
\mathcal{C}
is a point, and

\dim S^{r}_{d}(\mathcal{O})=\sum_{j=0}^{d}\binom{d+k-j-1}{k-1}=\binom{d+k}{k}.

For
i=k
, we know that
\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{C}
. In this case, formula (
1
) is just the trivial identity
\dim S^{r}_{d}(\mathcal{O})=\dim S^{r}_{d}(\mathcal{C})
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we construct a projection
\mathcal{C}
of a
(k,i)
-orange
\mathcal{O}
along the medial face. We prove that the projection
\mathcal{C}
is also a simplicial complex. In Section
3
, we analyze the relation between the space of splines on
\mathcal{O}
and that on
\mathcal{C}
by using the so-called
cofactor criterion
. Finally, we prove our main result, Theorem
1.1
, by two methods: the algebraic method in Section
4
, and the Bernstein-Bézier method in Section
5
. The algebraic method is based on Hilbert series of spline spaces viewed as filtered vector spaces, while the Bernstein-Bézier method uses minimal determining sets and smoothness conditions in Bernstein-Bézier form.

2
Geometry

We embed a
(k,i)
-orange
\mathcal{O}
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, and we assume that the medial face
\tau
in
\mathcal{O}
of dimension
(k-i)
is in the
(k-i)
-dimensional coordinate subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
defined by
x_{1}=\cdots=x_{i}=0
.
As a simplifying assumption, we identify
\mathcal{O}
with its embedding in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
For this choice of coordinates, we define the following projection:

\begin{split}\pi\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}&\to\mathbb{R}^{i},\\
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})&\mapsto(x_{1},\dots,x_{i}).\end{split}

(2)

Additionally, we write
\mathcal{C}
for the collection of sets
\pi(\sigma)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{i}
of images under the projection
\pi
of the faces
\sigma\in\mathcal{O}
. Namely,

{\mathcal{C}}:=\pi(\mathcal{O})=\bigl\{\pi(\sigma)\colon\sigma\in\mathcal{O}\bigr\}.

(3)

We shall refer to
{\mathcal{C}}
as a
projected orange
. Note that the projection of the medial face
\pi(\tau)
is the origin
O
of
\mathbb{R}^{i}
.
For any
X,Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
, denote by
X*Y
the
join
of
X
and
Y
which is the union of all line segments joining the points in
X
to the points in
Y
. Note that

\pi(X*Y)=\pi(X)*\pi(Y).

(4)

If
X=\{v\}
and
Y=\{w\}
, we simply write
v*w
instead of
X*Y
.
Recall that the set of points
\{p_{1},\dots,p_{j}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
is said to be in
general position
if its span is a
(j-1)
-subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Note that every
m
-simplex
\sigma\in\mathcal{O}
can be written as the join of
m+1
points in general position.

Proposition 2.1
.

If
\mathcal{O}
is a
(k,i)
-orange, then the projected orange
\mathcal{C}
defined in (
3
) is a simplicial complex. Moreover,
\mathcal{C}
is a star of a vertex.

Before we prove Proposition
2.1
, we need some preliminary lemmas that will help us verify that every element in
\mathcal{C}
is a simplex, every face of a simplex in
\mathcal{C}
is also in
\mathcal{C}
, and that the intersection of any two elements in
\mathcal{C}
is a face of each of them.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{O}
be a
(k,i)
-orange with the medial simplex
\tau
. The image
\pi(\sigma)
of a simplex
\sigma\in\mathcal{O}
is a simplex. Moreover, if
\sigma
and
\tau
do not intersect, then
\pi(\sigma)
and
\sigma
have the same dimension. Otherwise,
\dim(\pi(\sigma))=\dim(\sigma)-\dim(\sigma\cap\tau)
.

Proof.

For a simplex
\sigma\in\mathcal{O}
, the vertices of
\sigma
can always be divided into two sets, one of which contains all vertices that are in
\tau
, the other contains those not in
\tau
. This means
\sigma
can be written in the form

\sigma=\alpha*\beta,

(5)

where
\alpha,\beta
are faces of
\sigma
such that
\alpha\cap\tau=\emptyset
and
\beta=\sigma\cap\tau
.
Then by (
4
), we have

\pi(\sigma)=\begin{cases}\pi(\alpha),&\mbox{if}~\beta=\emptyset,\\
\pi(\alpha)*O,&\mbox{if}~\beta\neq\emptyset.\end{cases}

(6)

Let
z_{i+1},\dots,z_{k+1}
be the vertices of
\tau
.
Without loss of generality assume that
z_{k+1}=O
.
Let
p_{1},\dots,p_{j}
be the vertices of
\alpha
, where
j\leqslant i
. Then
p_{1},\dots,p_{j},z_{i+1},\dots,z_{k+1}
are in general position and
p_{1},\dots,p_{j},z_{i+1},\dots,z_{k}
are linearly independent in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. We claim that then
\bigl\{\pi(p_{1}),\dots,\pi(p_{j})\bigr\}
is also linearly independent.
Note that in general the projection of a linearly independent set of vectors is not necessarily linearly independent.
Let
V=\mathrm{span}\{p_{1},\dots,p_{j},z_{i+1},\dots,z_{k}\}
, and let
U=\mathrm{span}\{z_{i+1},\dots,z_{k}\}
.
Then
\dim V=k+j-i
, and
\dim U=k-i
. Since
U
is the
(k-i)
-subspace containing
\tau
, we have
\mathrm{ker}(\pi)=U
, and
\dim(\pi(V))=j
.
Therefore, the set
\bigl\{\pi(p_{1}),\dots,\pi(p_{j})\bigr\}
spans
\pi(V)
, and it is linearly independent.

This shows that both
\pi(\alpha)

and
\pi(\alpha)*O
are simplices. Moreover, if
\beta=\emptyset
, then
\dim\pi(\sigma)=\dim\pi(\alpha)
. Otherwise,
\dim(\pi(\sigma))=\dim(\sigma)-\dim(\beta)
, and the proof is complete.
∎

Corollary 2.3
.

The projected orange
\mathcal{C}
is a collection of simplices. A face of an element in
\mathcal{C}
is also in
\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

It immediately follows from Lemma
2.2
that every element of
\mathcal{C}
is a simplex.
Now if a simplex
\omega\in\mathcal{C}
, then by Definition
3
, there is
\sigma\in\mathcal{O}
such that
\pi(\sigma)=\omega
.
We may assume
\sigma=v_{1}*\dots*v_{j}
, for vertices
v_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
j\leqslant k
.
Then
\pi(v_{1}*\dots*v_{j})=\pi(v_{1})*\cdots*\pi(v_{j})=\omega
.
Thus any face of
\omega
can be written as

\pi(v_{j_{1}})*\cdots*\pi(v_{j_{\ell}})=\pi(v_{j_{1}}*\cdots*v_{j_{\ell}})
and, hence, by Definition
3
, this face is in
\mathcal{C}
.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
v_{1},v_{2}
be two vertices in
\mathcal{O}
. If neither
v_{1}
nor
v_{2}
belongs to
\tau
, then
\pi(v_{1})=\pi(v_{2})
implies
v_{1}=v_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha_{1}=v_{1}*\tau
and
\alpha_{2}=v_{2}*\tau
. Both
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are
(k-i+1)
-faces in
\mathcal{O}
and
\tau\subseteq\alpha_{1}\cap\alpha_{2}
. Since
\mathcal{O}
is a simplicial complex and
\dim\tau=k-i
, then there are only two possibilities:

\tau=\alpha_{1}\cap\alpha_{2}\quad\text{or}\quad\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}.

(7)

Let
z_{i+1},\dots,z_{k+1}
be the vertices of
\tau
, where we again assume that
z_{k+1}
is the origin. Since
\pi(v_{1})=\pi(v_{2})
, we note that
v_{1}-v_{2}
is in the
(k-i)
-dimensional coordinate space spanned by
\tau
. Thus

v_{1}-v_{2}=\beta_{1}z_{i+1}+\dots+\beta_{k-i}z_{k}.

(8)

Without loss of generality, we assume that the first
j
coefficients
\bigl\{\beta_{\ell}\bigr\}_{\ell=1}^{j}
are negative, while the remaining
k-i-j
coefficients
\bigr\{\beta_{\ell}\bigr\}_{\ell=j+1}^{k-i}
are non-negative. We next rewrite (
8
) as follows:

v_{1}-\beta_{1}z_{i+1}-\dots-\beta_{j}z_{i+j}=v_{2}+\beta_{j+1}z_{i+j+1}+\dots+\beta_{k-i}z_{k}=v.

(9)

Let
m=\left(1+\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-i}|\beta_{\ell}|\right)^{-1}
, and let
u=mv
.

Since
u
can be written as two different convex combinations, one in the vertices of
\alpha_{1}
and a second in the vertices of
\alpha_{2}
, then
u\in\alpha_{1}\cap\alpha_{2}
.
From (
9
), it follows that
u\notin\tau
, so by (
7
) we must have
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}
, and hence
v_{1}=v_{2}
.
∎

Let

such that
\pi(\alpha)=\pi(\alpha^{\prime})=\omega

, we have
\alpha\cap\tau=\emptyset
, and

\dim(\alpha)=\dim(\alpha^{\prime})=\dim(\omega)=:j

\alpha^{\prime}=p^{\prime}_{1}*\dots*p^{\prime}_{j}

. Hence
\pi(p_{1})*\dots*\pi(p_{j})=\pi(p^{\prime}_{1})*\dots*\pi(p^{\prime}_{j})=\omega

\pi(p_{\ell})=\pi(p^{\prime}_{\ell})

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
\omega\in\mathcal{C}
, and let the origin

be contained in
\omega
. Then there is a unique face
\sigma\in\mathcal{O}
such that
\pi(\sigma)=\omega

Proof.

By Corollary

, every face

By Corollary

.

To prove the uniqueness, assume there is a face

Corollary 2.7

There is a one-to-one correspondence between the maximal faces of an orange

Lemma 2.8
.

For any two faces
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\mathcal{O}
containing
\tau
, we have

\pi(\sigma_{1})\cap\pi(\sigma_{2})=\pi(\sigma_{1}\cap\sigma_{2}).

(10)

In particular, (

) implies that
\pi(\sigma_{1})\cap\pi(\sigma_{2})\in\mathcal{C}
in this case.

Proof.

We only need to prove
\pi(\sigma_{1})\cap\pi(\sigma_{2})\subseteq\pi(\sigma_{1}\cap\sigma_{2})
.
Let

, and let

. Then there exist
a_{1}\in\sigma_{1}
and
a_{2}\in\sigma_{2}
such that

. If either

then

, and we are done.
Suppose that neither
a_{1}
nor
a_{2}
is in
\sigma_{1}\cap\sigma_{2}
. Then
(a_{1}*\tau)\cap\sigma_{2}=\tau
and
(a_{2}*\tau)\cap\sigma_{1}=\tau
. Since

, we have

(a_{1}*\tau)\cap(a_{2}*\tau)=(a_{1}*\tau)\cap(a_{2}*\tau)\cap\sigma_{1}=\tau.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
x_{1}(b)=1
and
x_{2}(b)=\dots=x_{i}(b)=0
. Note that

and
a_{2}*\tau
are in the same

. By a similar reasoning as in the proof of Lemma

Corollary 2.9
.

For any two faces
\alpha,\sigma\in\mathcal{O}
such that
\alpha\cap\tau=\emptyset
, we have

\pi(\alpha)\cap\pi(\sigma)=\pi(\alpha\cap\sigma).

In particular,
\pi(\alpha)\cap\pi(\sigma)\in\mathcal{C}
in this case.

Proof.

We only need to show that
\pi(\alpha)\cap\pi(\sigma)\subseteq\pi(\alpha\cap\sigma)
.
Note that
\sigma=\alpha^{\prime}*\beta^{\prime}
such that
\alpha^{\prime}\cap\tau=\emptyset
and
\beta^{\prime}\subseteq\tau
.
Since
\mathcal{O}
is a simplicial complex, then
\alpha\cap\sigma=\alpha\cap\alpha^{\prime}
, and
(\alpha*\tau)\cap(\alpha^{\prime}*\tau)=(\alpha\cap\alpha)*\tau
.
By Lemma

, we have that
\pi(\alpha\cap\alpha^{\prime})*\pi(\tau)=(\pi(\alpha)*\pi(\tau))\cap(\pi(\alpha^{\prime})*\pi(\tau))
.
This means that for any

, it holds

.
So we can assume that

is on the line segment

, where

.
This implies that both

and

are in
\pi(\alpha)
. Since

, then

.
This shows
\pi(\alpha)\cap\pi(\sigma)=\pi(\alpha\cap\sigma)
.

.

For any
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\mathcal{C}

If

Let

By Corollary

If

is in both
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
, then by Corollary

, there exists
\sigma_{l}\in\mathcal{O}
such that
\pi(\sigma_{l})=\omega_{l}
and

By Lemma

3
Cofactors

In this section and thereafter, we use the notion of filtered vector spaces.
Recall that a
filtered vector space
is a vector space
V
with a nested sequence of subspaces
\bigl\{V_{\leqslant d}\subseteq V\colon d=0,1,2,\dots\bigr\}
such that

\{0\}\subseteq V_{\leqslant 0}\subseteq V_{\leqslant 1}\subseteq V_{\leqslant 2}\subseteq\cdots

and that

V=\bigcup_{d\geqslant 0}V_{\leqslant d}.

In this article, we always assume
V_{\leqslant d}
is finite dimensional for each
d
.
Let
\Delta
be a fixed finite
k
-dimensional triangulation of a domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
If
r\geqslant 0
is an integer, the
total spline space
over
\Delta
is defined as

S^{r}(\Delta)=\bigcup_{d\geqslant 0}S^{r}_{d}(\Delta).

(11)

In particular,
S^{r}(\Delta)
is a filtered vector space. Denote by
\Delta_{j}
the set of

-dimensional faces (or
j
-faces) of

, for
0\leqslant j\leqslant k
.
We say that two

, or simply

(k-1)
-face of
\Delta
i.e., if
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
share (or have in common) a
(k-1)
-face of
\Delta
.

We recall from
[
7
]
that

f=(f_{\sigma}\colon\sigma\in\Delta_{k})\in S^{r}(\Delta)
if and only if for every pair
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\Delta_{k}
such that
\sigma\cap\sigma^{\prime}=\varepsilon\in\Delta_{k-1}
there exists a polynomial
c_{\varepsilon}
such that
f_{\sigma}-f_{\sigma^{\prime}}=c_{\varepsilon}\cdot\ell_{\varepsilon}^{r+1}
,
where
\ell_{\varepsilon}
is the linear polynomial defining the hyperplane containing
\varepsilon
.
This result is called the
cofactor criterion
for
C^{r}
-splines.

Throughout this section, we follow the notation introduced in Section
2
, and denote by
\mathcal{O}
a
(k,i)
-orange.
We embedded
\mathcal{O}
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, and up to a change of coordinates assume that the medial simplex
\tau
of
\mathcal{O}
satisfies
x_{1}=\cdots=x_{i}=0
.
In these coordinates, we consider the projection
\pi
along
\tau
given in (
2
).
We denote by
\mathcal{C}
the image of
\mathcal{O}
by
\pi
, as defined in (
3
).

The orange
\mathcal{O}
is by definition a
k
-dimensional simplicial complex (see Definition
1.1
), and Proposition
2.1
implies that
\mathcal{C}
is an
i
-dimensional simplicial complex.
Following the notation above, we denote by
\mathcal{O}_{k}
the set of
k
-dimensional (or maximal) faces of
\mathcal{O}
.

The following proposition shows how the total
C^{r}
-spline spaces over
\mathcal{O}
and
\mathcal{C}
are related.

Proposition 3.1
.

For any integer
r\geqslant 0
, the total spline space over
\mathcal{O}
satisfies

S^{r}(\mathcal{O})\simeq S^{r}(\mathcal{C})\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}[x_{i+1},\dots,x_{k}]

(12)

as filtered vector spaces, that is, for each degree
d

S^{r}_{d}(\mathcal{O})\simeq(S^{r}(\mathcal{C})\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}[x_{i+1},\dots,x_{k}])_{\leqslant d},

where
(S^{r}(\mathcal{C})\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}[x_{i+1},\dots,x_{k}])_{\leqslant d}
is the space spanned by

\bigl\{(g,h)\in S^{r}(\mathcal{C})\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}[x_{i+1},\dots,x_{k}]\colon\deg g+\deg h\leqslant d\bigr\}.

Proof.

By Corollary
2.7
, the projection
\pi
establishes a bijective correspondence between the maximal faces of
\mathcal{O}
and those of
\mathcal{C}
.
Hence, every spline
g\in S^{r}(\mathcal{C})
can be written as the tuple
g=\bigl(g_{\pi(\sigma)}\colon\sigma\in\mathcal{O}_{k}\bigr)
, where
g_{\pi(\sigma)}=g|_{\pi(\sigma)}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{i}]
.
Let
R=\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{k}]
, and consider the map

\displaystyle\varphi\colon(S^{r}(\mathcal{C})\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}[x_{i+1},\dots,x_{k}])_{\leqslant d}

\displaystyle\to\bigoplus_{\sigma\in\mathcal{O}_{k}}R,

\displaystyle\left(g,h\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto\bigl(hg_{\pi(\sigma)}\colon\sigma\in\mathcal{O}_{k}\bigr).

Notice that
S_{c}^{r}(\mathcal{O})\subseteq\bigoplus_{\sigma\in\mathcal{O}_{k}}R
.
We want to show that
\varphi
is an isomorphishm of vector spaces and
\mathrm{Im}\,\varphi=S_{d}^{r}(\mathcal{O})
.

It is clear that
\varphi
is
\mathbb{R}
-linear and it is injective because
S^{r}(\mathcal{C})\subseteq\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{i}]
, so none of the variables
x_{t}
for
t>i
is involved in the polynomials
g_{\pi}(\sigma)
.
First we show that
\mathrm{Im}\,\varphi\subseteq S^{r}_{d}(\mathcal{O})
.

Let
g\in S^{r}(\mathcal{C})
.
Then, for any
h\in\mathbb{R}[x_{i+1},\dots,x_{k}]
, and any pair of adjacent faces
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}_{k}
such that
\sigma\cap\sigma^{\prime}=\varepsilon\in\mathcal{O}_{k}
, the cofactor criterion implies
hg_{\pi(\sigma)}-hg_{\pi(\sigma^{\prime})}=hc_{\pi(\varepsilon)}\cdot\ell_{\pi(\varepsilon)}^{r+1}
,
for some polynomial
c_{\pi(\varepsilon)}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{i}]
, where
\ell_{\pi(\varepsilon)}
is the linear polynomial vanishing on
\pi(\varepsilon)
. Notice that by construction, the
(k-1)
-face
\varepsilon
contains the medial simplex
\tau
of
\mathcal{O}
.
Consequently, the linear polynomial
\ell_{\varepsilon}
vanishing on
\varepsilon
is in
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{i}]
, and hence
\ell_{\pi(\varepsilon)}=\ell_{\varepsilon}
.
Hence,
\varphi(g,h)\in S^{r}(\mathcal{O})
.
By filtering on the degree
we have that
\deg\varphi(g,h)\leqslant d
.
This shows that
\mathrm{Im}\,(\varphi)\subseteq S^{r}_{d}(\mathcal{O})
.

We now prove that
\mathrm{im}\varphi\supseteq S^{r}_{d}(\mathcal{O})
.
Let
f=(f_{\sigma}\colon\sigma\in\mathcal{O}_{k})\in S^{r}_{d}(\mathcal{O})
, where
f|_{\sigma}=f_{\sigma}\in R
and
\deg f_{\sigma}\leqslant d
.
Notice that we can rewrite each
f_{\sigma}
as a polynomial in
x_{i+1},\dots,x_{k}
with coefficients in
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{k}]
.
More precisely, we have

f_{\sigma}=\sum_{{\bm{j}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}^{k-i},\;|\bm{j}|\leqslant d}a_{\bm{j},\sigma}\,\bm{y}^{\bm{j}},\quad\text{for $a_{\bm{j},\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{i}]$},

where
\bm{y}=(x_{i+1},\dots,x_{k})
, and for a tuple of non-negative integers
\bm{j}=(j_{i+s})_{s=1}^{k-i}

we write
|\bm{j}|=\sum_{s=1}^{k-i}j_{s}
and
\bm{y}^{\bm{j}}=x_{i+1}^{j_{i+1}}\cdots x_{k}^{j_{k}}
.
Then, if
a_{\bm{j}}=\bigl(a_{\bm{j},\sigma}\colon\sigma\in\mathcal{O}_{k}\bigr)
, we can write

f=\sum_{{\bm{j}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}^{k-i},\;|\bm{j}|\leqslant d}{a}_{\bm{j}}\,\bm{y}^{\bm{j}},

(13)

where
\deg{a}_{\bm{j}}=\max\{\deg a_{\bm{j},\sigma}\colon\sigma\in\mathcal{O}_{k}\}
.
We prove that
a_{\bm{j}}\in S^{r}(\mathcal{C})
for every tuple
\bm{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
in (
13
) as follows.
Notice that for adjacent faces
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
as above, we have that
f_{\sigma}-f_{\sigma^{\prime}}=c_{\varepsilon}\cdot\ell_{\varepsilon}^{r+1}
, and we can write

c_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{{\bm{j}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}^{k-i},\;|\bm{j}|\leqslant d}b_{\bm{j},\varepsilon}\cdot\bm{\bm{y}}^{\bm{j}},\quad\text{where }b_{\bm{j},\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{i}].

Thus,

f_{\sigma}-f_{\sigma^{\prime}}=\sum_{{\bm{j}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}^{k-i},\;|\bm{j}|\leqslant d}(b_{\bm{j},\varepsilon}\ell_{\varepsilon}^{r+1})\cdot{\bm{y}}^{\bm{j}}=\sum_{{\bm{j}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}^{k-i},\;|\bm{j}|\leqslant d}(a_{\bm{j},\sigma}-a_{\bm{j},\sigma^{\prime}})\bm{y}^{\bm{j}}.

Hence, for every
\bm{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
, we have
a_{\bm{j},\sigma}-a_{\bm{j},\sigma^{\prime}}=b_{\bm{j},\varepsilon}\ell_{\varepsilon}^{r+1}
, which implies that

a_{\bm{j}}\in S^{r}(\mathcal{C})
.
Note that
\deg f_{\sigma}\leqslant d
, so
\deg a_{\bm{j}}+|\bm{j}|\leqslant d
.
This shows that
\mathrm{Im}\,\varphi\supseteq S^{r}_{d}(\mathcal{O})
, and it completes the proof.
∎

4
Hilbert series

In this section we prove the main result in this paper which is Theorem
1.1
.

Note that for any order simplicial complex
\Delta
and any order of continuity
r\geqslant 0
, we can see
S^{r}(\Delta)
as a filtered vector space for the sequence of spline spaces

\{0\}\subseteq S_{0}^{r}(\Delta)\subseteq S_{1}^{r}(\Delta)\subseteq\cdots
.
The
Hilbert series

\mathrm{Hilb}\bigl(S^{r}(\Delta),t\bigr)
of
S^{r}(\Delta)
is by definition

\mathrm{Hilb}\bigl(S^{r}(\Delta),t\bigr)=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\dim S^{r}_{d}(\Delta)\,t^{d}.

Following the notation in previous sections, if
0\leqslant i\leqslant k
we denote by
\mathcal{O}
a
(k,i)
-orange and by
\mathcal{C}
the image of
\mathcal{O}
by the projection
\pi\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{i}
in (
2
).
The following result is a consequence of Proposition
3.1
and relates the Hilbert series of the spline spaces
S^{r}(\mathcal{O})
and
S^{r}(\mathcal{C})
.

Corollary 4.1
.

If
\mathcal{O}
is a
(k,i)
-orange and
\mathcal{C}=\pi(\mathcal{O})
as defined in (
3
), then

\mathrm{Hilb}\bigl(S^{r}(\mathcal{O}),t\bigr)=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{k-i}}\mathrm{Hilb}\bigl(S^{r}(\mathcal{C}),t\bigr).

(14)

Proof.

By Proposition
3.1
, we know that
S^{r}(\mathcal{O})=S^{r}(\mathcal{C})\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}[x_{i+1},\dots,x_{k}]
and
S^{r}_{d}(\mathcal{O})\cong(S^{r}(\mathcal{C})\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}[x_{i+1},\dots,x_{k}])_{\leqslant d}
for every
d\geqslant 0
.
We will prove (
14
) by induction on the variables
x_{i+j}
for
1\leqslant j\leqslant k-i
.
Define
\phi\colon\bigl(S^{r}(\mathcal{C})\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}[x_{i+1}]\bigr)_{d}\to S^{r}_{d}(\mathcal{C})
by taking
\phi\bigl(x_{i+1}\bigr)=0
.
Then,
\ker_{d}(\phi)=\bigl(S^{r}(\mathcal{C})\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}[x_{i+1}]\bigr)_{d-1}\cdot x_{i+1}\cong\bigl(S^{r}(\mathcal{C})\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}[x_{i+1}]\bigr)_{d-1}
.
This holds for every degree
d\geqslant 0
, and therefore it implies

\mathrm{Hilb}(\ker(\phi),t)=\mathrm{Hilb}\bigl(S^{r}(\mathcal{C},t)\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}[x_{i+1}],t\bigr)\cdot t,

(15)

where
\ker(\phi)
is the filtered vector of
\ker_{d}(\phi)
.
But
\phi
is surjective and linear, hence

\dim\ker_{d}(\phi)+\dim S^{r}(\mathcal{C})_{d}=\dim\bigl(S^{r}(\mathcal{C})\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}[x_{i+1}]\bigr)_{d}
, and this together with (
15
) yields

\mathrm{Hilb}\bigl(S^{r}(\mathcal{C})\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}[x_{i+1}],t\bigr)(1-t)=\mathrm{Hilb}(S^{r}(\mathcal{C})).

Since
\mathbb{R}[x_{i+1},\dots,x_{k}]\cong\mathbb{R}[x_{i+1}]\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\dots\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}[x_{k}]
, and
S^{r}(\mathcal{O})=S^{r}(\mathcal{C})\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}[x_{i+1},\dots,x_{k}]
, we easily see by induction that
\mathrm{Hilb}\bigl(S^{r}(\mathcal{O}),t\bigr)(1-t)^{k-i}=\mathrm{Hilb}\bigl(S^{r}(\mathcal{C}),t\bigr)
, which proves (
14
).
∎
