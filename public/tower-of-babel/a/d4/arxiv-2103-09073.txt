Pruned inside-out polytopes, combinatorial reciprocity theorems and generalized permutahedra
Sophie Rehberg

Abstract
. Generalized permutahedra are a class of polytopes with many interesting combinatorial subclasses. We introduce pruned inside-out polytopes, a generalization of inside-out polytopes introduced by Beck–Zaslavsky (2006), which have many applications such as recovering the famous reciprocity result for graph colorings by Stanley. We study the integer point count of pruned inside-out polytopes by applying classical Ehrhart polynomials and Ehrhart–Macdonald reciprocity. This yields a geometric perspective on and a generalization of a combinatorial reciprocity theorem for generalized permutahedra by Aguiar–Ardila (2017), Billera–Jia–Reiner (2009), and Karaboghossian (2022). Applying this reciprocity theorem to hypergraphic polytopes allows to give a geometric proof of a combinatorial reciprocity theorem for hypergraph colorings by Aval–Karaboghossian–Tanasa (2020). This proof relies, aside from the reciprocity for generalized permutahedra, only on elementary geometric and combinatorial properties of hypergraphs and their associated polytopes.

1.
Introduction

Generalized permutahedra are an interesting class of polytopes containing numerous subclasses of polytopes defined via combinatorial structures,
such as graphic zonotopes, hypergraphic polytopes (Minkowski sums of simplices), simplicial complex polytopes, matroid polytopes, associahedra, and nestohedra.
Generalized permutahedra themselves are closely related to submodular functions, which have applications in optimization.

A
combinatorial reciprocity theorem
can be described as a result that relates two classes of combinatorial objects via their enumeration problems (see, e.g.,
[
Sta74
,
BS18
]
).
For example, the number of proper
m
-colorings of a graph
g=(I,E)
agrees with a polynomial
\chi(g)(m)
of degree
d=\lvert I\rvert
for positive integers
m\in\mathbbmss{Z}_{>0}
, and
(-1)^{d}\chi(g)(-m)
counts the number of pairs of compatible acyclic orientations and
m
-colorings of the graph
g

[
Sta73
]
.
For precise definitions see
Section

3.4
below.

One of our main results is a combinatorial reciprocity theorem for generalized permutahedra counting integral directions with
k
-dimensional maximal faces:

Theorem
3.4
.

For a generalized permutahedron
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbbmss{R}^{d}
and
k=0,\dots,d-1
,

\begin{split}\chi_{d,k}(\mathcal{P})(m)\coloneqq&\ \#{\big\{{y}\in[m]^{d}\ \colon\ {y}\text{-maximum face $\mathcal{P}_{y}$ is a $k$-face}\big\}}\end{split}

agrees with a polynomial of degree
d-k
, and

\begin{split}(-1)^{d-k}\chi_{d,k}(\mathcal{P})(-m)&=\sum_{{y}\in[m]^{d}}\#\left(k\text{-faces of }\mathcal{P}_{y}\right).\end{split}

We will use integer point counting in dissected and dilated cubes to prove this result
and comment on further generalizations in
3.5
.

The special case of this theorem for
k=0
, i.e., generic directions, was obtained by Aguiar and Ardila
[
AA17
]
, and earlier by Billera, Jia, and Reiner
[
BJR09
]
in a slightly different language.
The
k=0
case was also recently extended in
[
Kar22
]
.
As shown for some examples in
[
AA17
, Section 18]
the application of such a result to the various subclasses of generalized permutahedra yields already known combinatorial reciprocity theorems for their related combinatorial structures such as matroid polynomials
[
BJR09
]
, Bergmann polynomials of matroids and Stanley’s famous reciprocity theorem for graph colorings
[
Sta73
]
.

Aguiar and Ardila develop a Hopf monoid structure on the species of generalized permutahedra, work with polynomial invariants defined by characters, and apply their antipode formula to get the combinatorial interpretation of the reciprocity result for generalized permutahedra for
k=0
(
3.7
, below)
[
AA17
, Sections 16, 17]
. This method is also used in
[
Kar22
]
.
The approach in
[
BJR09
]
is similar to the one by Aguiar and Ardila. Billera, Jia, and Reiner use Hopf algebras of matroids and quasisymmetric functions, as well as a multivariate generating function as isomorphism invariants of matroids.
The reciprocity providing ingredient is again the antipode of a Hopf algebra together with Stanley’s reciprocity for
P
-partitions
[
BJR09
, Sections 6 and 9]
.

We give a different, geometric perspective.
In order to prove
3.4
we apply Ehrhart–Macdonald reciprocity to pruned inside-out polytopes.
A pruned inside-out polytope
{\mathcal{Q}\setminus\bigcup\mathcal{N}^{\co 1}}
consist of the points that lie inside a polytope
\mathcal{Q}
but not in the codimension one cones
\mathcal{N}^{\co 1}
of a complete polyhedral fan
\mathcal{N}
.
This is a generalization of inside-out polytopes introduced by Beck and Zaslavsky
[
BZ06b
]
.
An inside-out polytope
\mathcal{Q}\setminus\mathcal{H}
consists of the points in a polytope
\mathcal{Q}
but off the hyperplanes in the arrangement
\mathcal{H}
.
We think of the codimension-one cones
\mathcal{N}^{\co 1}
defining a pruned inside-out polytope as
pruned
hyperplanes, hence the name.
One of the many applications of inside-out polytopes
[
BZ06c
,
BZ06a
,
BZ10
,
BS18
]
is yet a different proof of Stanley’s reciprocity result for graph colorings
[
Sta73
]
.

Aval, Karaboghossian, and Tanasa presented a reciprocity theorem for hypergraph colorings
[
AKT20
]
, generalizing Stanley’s result for graph colorings.
A main tool in the paper is a Hopf monoid structure on hypergraphs defined in
[
AA17
, Section 20.1.]
and the associated basic polynomial invariant.
However, they do
not
use the antipode as reciprocity inducing element, but rather technical computations involving Bernoulli numbers.

In
Section

3.4
we show how the reciprocity theorem for hypergraph colorings in
[
AKT20
]
is a consequence of the reciprocity for generalized permutahedra.
Our main tool is a vertex description of hypergraphic polytopes in terms of acyclic orientations of hypergraphs (
3.9
).
More recent work by Karaboghossian
[
Kar20
,
Kar22
]
presents a more general version of the combinatorial reciprocity result for hypergraphs and an alternative proof with similar techniques as we present in
Section

3.4
.

As spelled out in
[
AA17
, Sections 21–25]
and
[
AKT20
, Section 4]
hypergraphs and hypergraphic polytopes contain a number of interesting combinatorial subclasses such as simple hypergraphs, graphs, simplicial complexes, building sets, set partitions, and paths, together with their associated polytopes such as graphical zonotopes, simplicial complex polytopes, nestohedra, and graph associahedra.

The paper is organized as follows:
In
Section

2
we introduce the notion of pruned inside-out polytopes, define two counting functions on pruned inside-out polytopes, and derive
(quasi-)polynomiality
and reciprocity results.

Section

3
provides two applications of the results in
Section

2
;
first, to generalized permutahedra, giving a new geometric perspective on reciprocity theorems in
[
BJR09
,
AA17
,
Kar22
]
and, moreover,
presenting generalized versions for arbitrary face dimensions (
Section

3.2
).
The relationship between our approach and the polynomial invariants for Hopf monoids is analyzed in
Section

3.3
.
Secondly, we apply the reciprocity theorem for generalized permutahedra to the subclass of hypergraphic polytopes giving an elementary combinatorial and geometric proof of the reciprocity theorem for hypergraph colorings in
[
AKT20
]
(
Section

3.4
).
In
Appendix

A
we provide more details about generalized permutahedra.
In particular we give a self-contained proof of the well known bijection between generalized permutahedra and submodular set functions.
We do not claim the proof to be either new or original, but it is hard to find in the literature.

2.
Pruned inside-out polytopes and Ehrhart theory

In
[
BZ06b
]
Beck and Zaslavsky develop the notion of an inside-out polytope, that is, a polytope dissected by hyperplanes.
Counting integer point in a polytope but off certain hyperplanes turns out to be a useful tool to derive (quasi-)polynomiality results and reciprocity laws for various applications such as graph colorings
and signed graph colorings, composition of integers, nowhere-zero flows on graphs and signed graphs, antimagic labellings, as well as magic, semimagic, and magic latin squares
[
BZ06c
,
BZ06a
,
BZ10
]
.
After reviewing the necessary notions from polytopes and Ehrhart theory (
Section

2.1
),
we introduce a generalization of inside-out polytopes, which we call pruned inside-out polytopes and develop Ehrhart-theoretic results (
Section

2.2
).

2.1.
Preliminaries: Polytopes and Ehrhart theory

First recall some basic notions from polytopes; for more detailed information consult, e.g.,
[
Zie98
,
Gru03
]
.
A
polyhedron

\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbbmss{R}^{d}
is the intersection of finitely many halfspaces.
If the intersection is bounded it is called a
polytope
and can equivalently be described as the convex hull of finitely many points in
\mathbbmss{R}^{d}
.
A
(polyhedral) cone

N
is a polyhedron such that for
x\in N
the point
\lambda x
is again contained in
N
for every
\lambda\in\mathbbmss{R}_{\geq 0}
.
A
supporting hyperplane

H
of a polyhedron
\mathcal{P}
is a hyperplane such that the polyhedron is contained in one of the closed halfspaces.
The intersection of a polyhedron
\mathcal{P}
with a supporting hyperplane
H
is a
face

F=H\cap\mathcal{P}
of
\mathcal{P}
.
The
dimension

\dim(\mathcal{P})
(resp.
\dim(F)
) of a polyhedron
\mathcal{P}
(resp. face
F
) is the dimension of the affine hull of the polytope
\mathcal{P}
(resp. face
F
),
0-dimensional faces are called
vertices
and
(\dim(\mathcal{P})-1)
-dimensional faces are called
facets
.
The
codimension

\codim(F)
of an polyhedron
F
is the difference between the dimension of the ambient space and the dimension of the polyhedron
\dim(F)
.
A polyhedron
\mathcal{P}
is a
rational polyhedron
, if all its facet defining hyperplanes
H
can be described as
H=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{d}\,\colon\,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x}=b\right\}
for some
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbbmss{Z}^{d}
and
b\in\mathbbmss{Z}
.

For a polytope
\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathbbmss{R}^{d}
and a positive integer
t\in\mathbbmss{Z}_{>0}
we define the
t
th
dilate of

\mathcal{Q}
as

(2.1)

t\mathcal{Q}\coloneqq\big\{x\in\mathbbmss{R}^{d}\,\colon\,\tfrac{1}{t}x\in\mathcal{Q}\big\}=\big\{tx\in\mathbbmss{R}^{d}\,\colon\,x\in\mathcal{Q}\big\}\,.

The
Ehrhart counting function

\ehr_{\mathcal{Q}}(t)
counts the number of integer point in the

t
th
dilate of the polytope
\mathcal{Q}
:

(2.2)

\ehr_{\mathcal{Q}}(t)\coloneqq\#\left(\tfrac{1}{t}\mathbbmss{Z}^{d}\cap\mathcal{Q}\right)=\#\left(\mathbbmss{Z}^{d}\cap t\mathcal{Q}\right)\,.

See
Figure

1
for an example.

Figure 1
.
The cube
[-1,1]^{2}
and its dilates
[-2,2]^{2}
and
[-3,3]^{2}

\ehr_{[-1,1]^{2}}(t)=(2t+1)^{2}
and
\ehr_{(-1,1)^{2}}(t)=(2t-1)^{2}
.

Recall that a
rational (resp. integral) polytope
has vertices with rational (resp. integral) coordinates.
We call the least common multiple of the denominators of all coordinates of all vertices of a rational polytope the
denominator of
\mathcal{Q}
.

A
quasipolynomial
of degree
d
is a function
q\colon\mathbbmss{Z}\to\mathbbmss{R}

of the form

q(t)=c_{d}(t)t^{d}+\dots+c_{1}(t)t+c_{0}(t)
where

c_{0},c_{1},\dots,c_{d}\colon\mathbbmss{Z}\to\mathbbmss{R}
are periodic functions.
The least common period of
c_{0}(n),c_{1}(n),\dots,c_{d}(n)
is the
period
of
q(t)
.

Theorem 2.1

(Ehrhart’s theorem
[
Ehr62
]
)
.

For a rational polytope
\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathbbmss{R}^{d}
the Ehrhart counting function
\ehr_{\mathcal{Q}}(t)
agrees with a quasipolynomial of degree equal to the dimension of
\mathcal{Q}
and period dividing the denominator of
\mathcal{Q}
for all
t\in\mathbbmss{Z}_{>0}
.

For an integer polytope
\mathcal{Q}
Ehrhart’s theorem implies that the Ehrhart counting function
\ehr_{\mathcal{Q}}
is a polynomial.
Therefore it is often called the
Ehrhart polynomial
.
The following reciprocity theorem was conjectured and proved for various special cases by Eugéne Ehrhart and proved by Ian G. Macdonald.
It is the foundation for the results in this paper.

Theorem 2.2

(Ehrhart–Macdonald reciprocity
[
Mac71
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathbbmss{R}^{d}
be a rational polytope and
t\in\mathbbmss{Z}_{>0}
. Then

(2.3)

(-1)^{\dim\mathcal{Q}}\ehr_{\mathcal{Q}}(-t)=\ehr_{\mathcal{Q}^{\circ}}(t)\coloneqq\#\left(\mathbbmss{Z}^{d}\cap t\mathcal{Q}^{\circ}\right)

where
\mathcal{Q}^{\circ}
is the (relative) interior of the polytope
\mathcal{Q}
.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}\setminus\left(\bigcup\mathcal{N}^{\co 1}\right)\subset\mathbbmss{R}^{d}
be a rational pruned inside-out polytope.
Then the inner pruned Ehrhart function
\Oc_{\mathcal{Q}^{\circ},\mathcal{N}^{\co 1}}(t)
and the cumulative pruned Ehrhart function
\Ex_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{N}^{\co 1}}(t)
agree with quasipolynomials in
t
of degree
d
for
t\in\mathbbmss{Z}_{>0}
and are related by reciprocity:

(2.12)

(-1)^{d}\Oc_{\mathcal{Q}^{\circ},\mathcal{N}^{\co 1}}(-t)=\Ex_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{N}^{\co 1}}(t).

Proof.

We first use
2.4
to get

(2.13)

\Oc_{\mathcal{Q}^{\circ},\mathcal{N}^{\co 1}}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\ehr_{R_{i}^{\circ}}(t)\,.

For every
i=1,\dots,k
we can apply Ehrhart’s
2.1
to
\ehr_{R_{i}^{\circ}}(t)
, hence the counting function
\Oc_{\mathcal{Q}^{\circ},\mathcal{N}^{\co 1}}(t)
is a sum of quasipolynomials, which is again a quasipolynomial.

For the second part of the claim
we use Ehrhart–Macdonald reciprocity (
2.2
) and compute

\begin{split}\Oc_{\mathcal{Q}^{\circ},\mathcal{N}^{\co 1}}(t)\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{k}\ehr_{R_{i}^{\circ}}(t)\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{k}(-1)^{d}\ehr_{\overline{R}_{i}}(-t)\ =\ (-1)^{d}\Ex_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{N}^{\co 1}}(-t),\end{split}

where the last equality follows from
2.5
.
∎

Remark 2.8
.

In the case that the polytope
Q
and the complete fan intersect such that all the closed regions

\overline{R}=\mathcal{Q}\cap N
of the pruned inside-out polytope
\mathcal{Q}\setminus\left(\bigcup\mathcal{N}^{\co 1}\right)
are integer polytopes, the counting functions
\Oc_{\mathcal{Q}^{\circ},\mathcal{N}^{\co 1}}(t)
and
\Ex_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{N}^{\co 1}}(t)
agree with a
polynomial
of degree

, by
2.1
and
2.2
. We will use this fact in the proof of
3.2
.

Remark 2.9
.

One can certainly generalize this setting, e.g., to polyhedral complexes. The framework here is motivated by the applications below.

After a short introduction to generalized permutahedra (

3.2.
Combinatorial reciprocity theorems for generalized permutahedra

We restate the combinatorial reciprocity result for generalized permutahedra by
[

, Propositions

and
17.4
]
in a slightly different language (see
3.7
for the original statement) and prove it using Ehrhart theory.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbbmss{R}^{d}
be a generalized permutahedron and
m\in\mathbbmss{Z}_{>0}
. Then

(3.6)

\chi_{d}(\mathcal{P})(m)\coloneqq\#\,\left(\mathcal{P}\text{-generic directions }{y}\in\big(\mathbbmss{R}^{d}\big)^{*}\text{ with }{y}\in[m]^{d}\right)

agrees with a polynomial in
m
of degree
d
. Moreover,

(3.7)

(-1)^{d}\chi_{d}(\mathcal{P})(-m)=\sum_{{y}\in[m]^{d}}\#\left(\text{vertices of }\mathcal{P}_{y}\right)\,.

While we will extend
3.2
(and our proof) in
3.4
and
3.5
below, we provide a self-contained proof here to present a flavor of our method.
In contrast to
[
BJR09
,
AA17
,
Kar22
]
we will prove these results without using any Hopf-algebraic method.
Our proof gives a geometric point of view by counting integer points in pruned inside-out cubes.
That is, we will consider the cube

(3.8)

[1,m]^{d}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{d}\,\colon\,1\leq x_{i}\leq m\text{ for }i=1,\dots,d\}\subset\mathbbmss{R}^{d}

and intersect it with the integer lattice:

(3.9)

[1,m]^{d}\cap\mathbbmss{Z}^{d}=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{d}\,\colon\,x_{i}\in\{1,\dots,m\}\text{ for }i=1,\dots,d\right\}=\{1,\dots,m\}^{d}=[m]^{d}.

The same holds in the dual space
\big(\mathbbmss{R}^{d}\big)^{*}
.
Now, a direction
y\colon[d]\to[m]\in\big(\mathbbmss{R}^{d}\big)^{*}
can be identified with an integer point
{y}
in the cube
\{1,\dots,m\}^{d}=[m]^{d}
in the dual space.
See
Figure

7
.
Before we start the proof of
3.2
we need the following result.

Lemma 3.3
.

The intersections of the unit cube
[0,1]^{d}
and the braid cones
\mathcal{B}_{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}}
for compositions
[d]=T_{1}\uplus\dots\uplus T_{k}
are integer polytopes.

Proof.

It is enough to consider the full-dimensional braid cones, since lower dimensional braid cones are faces of full-dimensional braid cones and faces of an integer polytope are integer polytopes.
Full-dimensional braid cones
\mathcal{B}_{T_{1},\dots,T_{d}}
correspond to permutations of the coordinates, i.e., total orders on
[d]
.
Hence, we can think of the intersection
[0,1]\cap\mathcal{B}_{T_{1},\dots,T_{d}}
as an order polytope (see, e.g.,
[
Sta86
, Definition
1.1
]
) of the total order on
[d]
given by
T_{1},\dots,T_{d}
.
Then Corollary
1.3
in
[
Sta86
]
implies that the vertices of
[0,1]\cap\mathcal{B}_{T_{1},\dots,T_{d}}
have coordinates equal to either
0
or
1
, hence they are integral.
∎

Proof of
3.2
.

We will argue
in the dual space
\big(\mathbbmss{R}^{d}\big)^{*}
and its integer lattice; to simplify notation we will not always explicitly point that out.
Let us recall that
{y}\in\big(\mathbbmss{R}^{d}\big)^{*}
being
\mathcal{P}
-generic means that the
{y}
-maximal face of
\mathcal{P}
is a vertex, that is,
{y}
is contained in a full-dimensional cone of the normal fan
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})
.
So the direction
{y}
is
not
contained in any cone
N
in the codimension-one fan
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})^{\co 1}
. Hence,

\begin{split}\chi_{d}(\mathcal{P})(m)&=\#\,\left(\mathcal{P}\text{-generic directions }{y}\in\big(\mathbbmss{R}^{d}\big)^{*}\text{ with }{y}\in[m]^{d}\right)\\
&=\#{\{{y}\in[1,m]^{d}\cap\mathbbmss{Z}^{d}\ \colon\ {y}\text{-maximum face $\mathcal{P}_{y}$ is a vertex}\}}\\
&=\#{\{{y}\in[1,m]^{d}\cap\mathbbmss{Z}^{d}\ \colon\ {y}\in N\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})\text{ with }N\text{ full-dimensional}\}}\\
&=\#{\{{y}\in[1,m]^{d}\cap\mathbbmss{Z}^{d}\ \colon\ {y}\notin N\text{ for all }N\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})\text{ with codimension }\geq 1\}}\\
&=\#{\left(\,\left([1,m]^{d}\setminus\bigcup\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})^{\co 1}\right)\cap\mathbbmss{Z}^{d}\,\right)}\\
&=\Oc_{(0,1)^{d},\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})^{\co 1}}(m+1)\,,\end{split}

where we use in the last line that
[1,m]^{d}\cap\mathbbmss{Z}^{d}=(0,m+1)^{d}\cap\mathbbmss{Z}^{d}=(m+1)\cdot(0,1)^{d}\cap\mathbbmss{Z}^{d}
.
With
3.3
we know that the unit cube and the normal fan
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})
intersect producing integer regions.
Therefore, using
2.7
and
2.8
, polynomiality of
\chi_{d}(\mathcal{P})(m)
follows.
With the above equality and
2.7
at hand, we compute

\begin{split}(-1)^{d}\chi_{d}(\mathcal{P})(-m)&=(-1)^{d}\Oc_{(0,1)^{d},\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})^{\co 1}}(-m+1)\\
&=(-1)^{d}\Oc_{(0,1)^{d},\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})^{\co 1}}(-(m-1))\\
&=\Ex_{[0,1]^{d},\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})^{\co 1}}(m-1)\\
&=\sum_{{y}\in\frac{1}{m-1}\mathbbmss{Z}^{d}}\mult_{[0,1]^{d},\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})^{\co 1}}({y})\\
&=\sum_{{y}\in\mathbbmss{Z}^{d}}\mult_{[0,m-1]^{d},\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})^{\co 1}}({y})\,.\end{split}

Every cone in the braid fan contains the line
L=\lambda(1,\dots,1)
.
Therefore, the fans
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})
and
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})^{\co 1}
are invariant under translations by vectors in the line
L
and scaling.
So we can shift the cube
[0,m-1]^{d}
to
[1,m]^{d}
and this bijection not only preserves the number of integer points but also their multiplicities
\mult_{[1,m]^{d},\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})^{\co 1}}
with respect to the fan
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})
. Hence,

\begin{split}(-1)^{d}\chi_{d}(\mathcal{P})(-m)&=\sum_{{y}\in\mathbbmss{Z}^{d}}\mult_{[1,m]^{d},\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})^{\co 1}}({y})\\
&=\sum_{{y}\in[1,m]^{d}\cap\mathbbmss{Z}^{d}}\#\left(\text{closed full-dimensional normal cones that contain }{y}\right)\\
&=\sum_{{y}\in[m]^{d}}\#\left(\text{closed normal cones of vertices that contain }{y}\right)\\
&=\sum_{{y}\in[m]^{d}}\#\left(\text{vertices of }\mathcal{P}_{y}\right)\,,\end{split}

where we make use of
3.1
.
∎

We can extend
3.2
above to faces of arbitrary dimension.

Theorem 3.4
.

For a generalized permutahedron
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbbmss{R}^{d}
and
k=0,\dots,d-1
,

\begin{split}\chi_{d,k}(\mathcal{P})(m)\coloneqq&\ \#{\{{y}\in[m]^{d}\ \colon\ {y}\text{-maximum face $\mathcal{P}_{y}$ is a $k$-face}\}}\end{split}

agrees with a polynomial of degree

, and

\begin{split}(-1)^{d-k}\chi_{d,k}(\mathcal{P})(-m)&=\sum_{{y}\in[m]^{d}}\#\left(k\text{-faces of }\mathcal{P}_{y}\right).\end{split}

Before we prove the theorem
we extend the notion of codimension-one fans to arbitrary dimensions by defining the
codimension-
\mathbf{k}
fan

\mathcal{N}^{\co k}
as

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}^{\co k}\coloneqq&\{N\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})\,\colon\,\codim(N)\geq k\},\end{split}

that is, for a polytope
\mathcal{P}
,

(3.10)

\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})^{\co k}=\{N_{\mathcal{P}}(F)\ \colon\ F\text{ a face of }\mathcal{P}\text{ with }\dim(F)\geq k\}\,.

For a polytope
\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathbbmss{R}^{d}
and
k\geq 0
we define the
k
-pruned inside-out polytope
as

\begin{split}\left(\mathcal{Q}\cap\bigcup\mathcal{N}^{\co k}\right)\setminus\left(\bigcup\mathcal{N}^{\co k+1}\right)=\mathcal{Q}\cap\biguplus\left\{N^{\circ}\,\colon\,N\in\mathcal{N}^{\co k}\right\}\,.\end{split}

Note this is consistent with the notation in the beginning of this section.
As before, for a polytope
\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathbbmss{R}^{d}
the open
k
-pruned inside-out polytope
\left(\mathcal{Q}^{\circ}\cap\bigcup\mathcal{N}^{\co k}\right)\setminus\left(\bigcup\mathcal{N}^{\co k+1}\right)
is the disjoint union of relatively open
(d-k)
-dimensional polytopes,
namely, the intersection of
\mathcal{Q}^{\circ}
with the relatively open cones in
\mathcal{N}
of codimension
k
.

Proof of
3.4
.

We compute

\begin{split}\chi_{d,k}(\mathcal{P})(m)&=\ \#{\big\{{y}\in[1,m]^{d}\cap\mathbbmss{Z}^{d}\ \colon\ {y}\text{-maximum face $\mathcal{P}_{y}$ is a $k$-face}\big\}}\\
&=\#{\Big(\Big(\mathbbmss{Z}^{d}\cap(0,m+1)^{d}\cap\bigcup\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})^{\co k}\Big)\setminus\Big(\bigcup\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})^{\co k+1}\Big)\Big)}\\
&=\#\Big(\Big(\biguplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})\\
\dim N=d-k\end{subarray}}N^{\circ}\cap(0,m+1)^{d}\Big)\cap\mathbbmss{Z}^{d}\Big)\,.\end{split}

The intersection
N^{\circ}\cap(0,m+1)^{d}
is the relative interior of a polytope.
Moreover, since
N^{\circ}
is an open cone containing the origin,

N^{\circ}\cap(0,m+1)^{d}
is the
(m+1)
st
dilate of
N^{\circ}\cap(0,1)^{d}
.
Hence,

(3.11)

\chi_{d,k}(\mathcal{P})(m)=\#\Big(\Big(\biguplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})\\
\dim N=d-k\end{subarray}}N^{\circ}\cap(0,m+1)^{d}\Big)\cap\mathbbmss{Z}^{d}\Big)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})\\
\dim N=d-k\end{subarray}}\ehr_{N^{\circ}\cap(0,1)^{d}}(m+1)\,.

Using again
3.3
and Ehrhart’s
2.1
we obtain polynomiality for
\chi_{d,k}(\mathcal{P})(m)
.

With Ehrhart–Macdonald reciprocity (
2.2
) we compute

(3.12)

\displaystyle(-1)^{d-k}\chi_{d,k}(\mathcal{P})(-m)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{d-k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})\\
\dim N=d-k\end{subarray}}\ehr_{N^{\circ}\cap(0,1)^{d}}(-m+1)

(3.13)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})\\
\dim N=d-k\end{subarray}}(-1)^{d-k}\ehr_{N^{\circ}\cap(0,1)^{d}}(-(m-1))

(3.14)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})\\
\dim N=d-k\end{subarray}}\ehr_{N\cap[0,1]^{d}}(m-1)

(3.15)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})\\
\dim N=d-k\end{subarray}}\#\big(N\cap[0,m-1]^{d}\cap\mathbbmss{Z}^{d}\big)\,.

Here we use, as in the proof of

\lambda(1,\dots,1)
for

.
So,

\begin{split}(-1)^{d-k}\chi_{d,k}(\mathcal{P})(-m)&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})\\

applying
3.1
in the last equality.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

At the heart of the proofs of
3.2
and
3.4
lie sums of Ehrhart polynomials and the reciprocity results are applications of Ehrhart-Macdonald reciprocity (
2.2
):
Recall (
3.11
) and (
3.14
) from the proof of
3.4
.
One can see that for a generalized permutahedron
\mathcal{P}
any combination of Ehrhart polynomials as in (
3.11
) and (
3.14
) results in a polynomial counting function

\begin{split}\chi_{d,\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathcal{P})(m)\coloneqq&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})\end{subarray}}\alpha_{N}\ehr_{N^{\circ}\cap(0,1)^{d}}(m+1)\\
=&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\text{ a face of }\mathcal{P}\end{subarray}}\alpha_{N_{\mathcal{P}}(F)}\ehr_{N_{\mathcal{P}}(F)^{\circ}\cap(0,1)^{d}}(m+1)\end{split}

for coefficients
\alpha_{N}
. This provides a combinatorial reciprocity result

\begin{split}\chi_{d,\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathcal{P})(-m)&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\dim N}\alpha_{N}\ehr_{N\cap[0,1]^{d}}(m-1)\\
&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\text{ a face of }\mathcal{P}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{d-\dim F}\alpha_{F}\ehr_{N_{\mathcal{P}}(F)\cap[0,1]^{d}}(m-1)\,.\end{split}

3.4
(and therefore also
3.2
) is a reformulation of this general result with coefficients

(3.16)

\alpha_{N}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }\dim N=d-k\\
0&\text{ else}\end{cases}\qquad\text{ for }k=0,1,\dots,d-1\,.

Remark 3.6
.

We observe that we used the following properties of generalized permutahedra in the proofs of
3.2
and
3.4

(i)

the intersection of the unit cube and the normal fan of a generalized permutahedron form integer pruned inside-out polytopes,

(ii)

every cone
N
in the normal fan
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})
of a generalized permutahedron
\mathcal{P}
contains the line
L=\left\{\lambda(1,\dots,1)\,\colon\,\lambda\in\mathbbmss{R}\right\}
.

The first property
(i)
can be weakened to rational intersections leading to a quasipolynomiality result.
Considering normal fans without property
(ii)
produces similar but more complicated statements, since the shift of the cube
[0,m-1]^{d}
to the cube

{y}

equals the number of acyclic headings of
h
compatible to the coloring
c_{y}
defined by the direction
{y}
.
We compute the
{y}
-maximum faces as in
Equations

3.35
and
3.36
:

(3.37)

\mathcal{P}(h)_{y}=\bigg(\sum_{e\in E}\Delta_{e}\bigg)_{y}=\sum_{e\in E}(\Delta_{e})_{y}=\sum_{e\in E}\conv\Big\{b_{i}\in\mathbbmss{R}I\ \colon\ i\in e,\ y(i)=\max_{j\in e}y(j)\Big\}\,.

From
Equation

3.37
we can see that a vertex of
\mathcal{P}(h)_{y}
corresponds to choosing for every hyperedge
e\in E
one of the nodes
i\in e
with maximal entry
{y}(i)
, i.e., maximal color
c_{y}(i)
.
This is, by definition, the same as constructing a compatible heading for the coloring
c_{y}
.
We know by
3.9
that vertices correspond to acyclic headings.
Hence, vertices of
\mathcal{P}(h)_{y}
correspond to acyclic headings compatible to the coloring
c_{y}
.
Vice versa, for a coloring
c
the compatible acyclic headings are those with heads of hyperedges having a maximal coloring.
That is, these acyclic headings correspond to those vertices, that are vertices of the maximum face
\mathcal{P}(h)_{{y}^{c}}
in direction
{y}^{c}
.
∎

Appendix A
Equivalent descriptions of permutahedra, generalized permutahedra, and hypergraphic polytopes

We compile some useful and nice information about permutahedra, generalized permutahedra and hypergraphic polytopes in this Appendix. This section is not original work, but the proof of
A.1
below might be hard to find in the literature.
As explained in
Section

3.3
we will use the notations for
\mathbbmss{R}^{d}
and
\mathbbmss{R}^{I}
interchangeably.

Recall that we defined the standard permutahedron
\pi_{d}
to be the convex hull of all the permutations of the point with entries
[d]
.
The facet description of the standard permutahedron is given by

\begin{split}\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}&=d+\left(d-1\right)+\dots+1=\frac{d(d+1)}{2}\\
\sum_{i\in T}x_{i}&\leq d+\left(d-1\right)+\dots+\left(d-\lvert T\rvert+1\right)\quad\text{for all }T\subseteq[d].\end{split}

Moreover, every face of the standard permutahedron can be described combinatorially by compositions, for details see, e.g.,
[
AA17
, Section 4.1.]
.
The standard permutahedron can equivalently be described as the Minkowski sum of line segments:

(A.1)

\pi_{d}=\sum_{i<j}\Delta_{\{i,j\}},

where
\Delta_{\{i,j\}}\coloneqq\conv\{e_{i},e_{j}\}
and
e_{i}
are standard basis vectors. This implies, in particular, that standard permutahedra are zonotopes.

Recall that generalized permutahedra are those polytopes that have a coarsening of the braid fan as normal fan.
Since the normal fan
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{Q})
of the Minkowski sum
\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{Q}
of two polytopes
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
is the common refinement of the two normal fans
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P})
and
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{Q})

[
Zie98
, Proposition 7.12]
,
generalized permutahedra are the (weak) Minkowski summands of standard permutahedra. That is,
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbbmss{R}^{d}
is a generalized permutahedron if and only if there exists a polytope
\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathbbmss{R}^{d}
and a real scalar
\lambda>0
such that
\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{Q}=\lambda\pi_{[d]}
.

One picturesque way of defining generalized permutahedra is by deforming standard permutahedra by parallel shifts of facets.
This deformation maintains the normal fan until a face degenerates, i.e., at least two vertices are merged into one vertex. In that case the corresponding normal cones of the vertices are glued together.
One example can be seen in
Figure

6
, where the top right edge degenerated and the two corresponding neighboring full-dimensional cones were combined.
A formal description of these deformations and a detailed proof of equivalence can be found in
[
PRW08
, Appendix]
.

Finally, generalized permutahedra can be uniquely described as the base polytopes of submodular functions
z\colon 2^{I}\to\mathbbmss{R}
with
z(\varnothing)=0
(
A.1
).
See, e.g.,
[
CL20
, Theorem 3.11 and 3.17]
.
For the sake of completeness and the convenience of the reader we include a self-contained proof of the well-known equivalence of the definitions of generalized permutahedra through braid fan coarsenings and submodular functions.
A set function
z\colon 2^{I}\to\mathbbmss{R}
is called
submodular
if for all
A,B\subseteq I

(A.2)

z(A)+z(B)\geq z(A\cup B)+z(A\cap B)\,.

We define the
base polytope

\mathsf{P}(z)
of a submodular function
z\colon 2^{I}\to\mathbbmss{R}
by

(A.3)

\mathsf{P}(z)\coloneqq\big\{x\in\mathbbmss{R}I\ \colon\ \sum_{i\in I}x_{i}=z(I)\ \text{ and }\ \sum_{i\in A}x_{i}\leq z(A)\ \text{ for all }A\subseteq I\big\}\,.

To simplify the proof of
A.1
we will use the following notation

(A.4)

x(A)\coloneqq\sum_{i\in A}x_{i}\qquad\text{ for }A\subseteq I\,.

With that notation at hand we can write the definition of the base polytopes as

(A.5)

\mathsf{P}(z)=\big\{x\in\mathbbmss{R}I\ \colon\ x(I)=z(I)\ \text{ and }\ x(A)\leq z(A)\ \text{ for all }A\subseteq I\big\}\,.

As mentioned above the standard permutahedron
\pi_{I}
is the base polytope of the submodular function

(A.6)

z(A)\coloneqq\lvert I\rvert+(\lvert I\rvert-1)+\dots+(\lvert I\rvert-\lvert A\rvert+1)\,.

Theorem A.1
.

A polytope
\mathcal{P}
is a generalized permutahedron if and only if it is the base polytope
\mathsf{P}(z)
of a submodular function
z\colon 2^{I}\to\mathbbmss{R}
with
z(\varnothing)=0
.

Before we start proving this theorem, we give a description of the faces in terms of set compositions.
A
composition
of a finite set
I
is an ordered sequence
(T_{1},\dots,T_{k})
of disjoint non-empty subsets
T_{i}\subseteq I
such that
I=T_{1}\uplus\dots\uplus T_{k}
.
Let
\mathbf{1}_{T}
for some
T\subseteq I
be the
0/1
-vector with entries equal to one for indices in the subset
T
and zero otherwise.

Lemma A.2
.

The faces of the braid arrangement
\mathcal{B}_{I}
, also called
braid cones
, can be described uniquely by compositions
I=T_{1}\uplus\dots\uplus T_{k}
:

\begin{split}\mathcal{B}_{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}}\coloneqq&\big\{y\in\mathbbmss{R}^{I}\ \colon\ y(i)=y(j)\text{ for all }i,j\in T_{a},\ y(i)\geq y(j)\text{ for }i\in T_{a},j\in T_{b}\text{ and }a<b\big\}\\
=&\cone\{\mathbf{1}_{T_{1}},\mathbf{1}_{T_{1}\cup T_{2}},\dots,\mathbf{1}_{T_{1}\cup\dots\cup T_{k-1}}\}+\spn\{\mathbf{1}_{I}\}\end{split}

with

(A.7)

\dim\mathcal{B}_{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}}=k\,,

where
\mathbf{1}_{T}
for some subset
T\subseteq I
is the
0/1
-vector with entries equal to one for indices in the subset
T
and zero otherwise.

Proof of
A.1
.

For a submodular function
z\colon 2^{I}\to\mathbbmss{R}
we show that
\mathsf{P}(z)
is a generalized permutahedron by showing that every braid cone
\mathcal{B}_{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}}\subset\mathbbmss{R}^{I}
is contained in a normal cone of
\mathsf{P}(z)
.
Since
\mathsf{P}(z)
is contained in the hyperplane
\{x\in\mathbbmss{R}I\ \colon\ x(I)=z(I)\}
the normal cone
N_{\mathsf{P}(z)}(\mathsf{P}(z))
contains the line spanned by
\mathbf{1}_{I}\in\mathbbmss{R}I
, hence every normal cone of
\mathsf{P}(z)
contains that line.

The following part of the proof relies on
[
FT83
]
.
Fujishige and Tomizawa show under which conditions a greedy-like algorithm gives an optimal solution in the base polytope of a submodular functions on a general distributive lattice.
We adapt the proof to our special case.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}}\subset\mathbbmss{R}^{I}
a braid cone.
Choose a maximal chain
\mathcal{C}\colon\emptyset=C_{0}\subset\dots\subset C_{n}=I
in the boolean lattice
2^{I}
such that
T_{1},T_{1}\sqcup T_{2},\dots,T_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup T_{k}
are sets in the chain
\mathcal{C}
.
Then

(A.8)

\lvert C_{j}\setminus C_{j-1}\rvert=1

for
j=1,\dots,n\coloneqq\lvert I\rvert

and we define a linear ordering on
I
by
i_{j}\coloneqq C_{j}\setminus C_{j-1}\in I
for
j=1,\dots,n
.
Now, consider the point
\tilde{x}\in\mathbbmss{R}I
defined by

(A.9)

\tilde{x}_{i_{j}}\coloneqq z(C_{j})-z(C_{j-1})\quad\text{für }j=1,\dots,n.

We will show

(i)

that
\tilde{x}(C_{j})=z(C_{j})
for
j=1,\dots,n
,
and that the point
\tilde{x}
lies in
\mathsf{P}(z)
,

(ii)

that
\tilde{x}
is maximal for all directions in the braid cone
\mathcal{B}_{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}}
.

Then it follows that the braid cone
\mathcal{B}_{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}}
is contained in the normal cone
N_{\mathsf{P}(z)}(F)
, where
F
is a face containing
\tilde{x}
.

For
j=1,\dots,n
we compute

(A.10)

\tilde{x}(C_{j})=\sum_{l=1}^{j}\tilde{x}_{i_{l}}=\sum_{l=1}^{j}\left(z(C_{l})-z(C_{l-1})\right)=z(C_{j}),

in particular,
\tilde{x}(I)=z(I)
.
We show by induction on the cardinality
\lvert A\rvert
of a subset
A\subseteq I
that
\tilde{x}(A)\leq z(A)
.
For the empty set we have
0=\tilde{x}(\varnothing)=z(\varnothing)
.
For an arbitrary set
A\subseteq I
let
j^{*}
be the minimal index such that
A\subseteq C_{j^{*}}
and define the element
i^{*}\coloneqq A\setminus C_{j^{*}-1}\in I
.
We compute using the induction hypothesis,
Equation

A.9
, and submodularity of
z
together with
A\setminus\{i^{*}\}=A\cap C_{j^{*}-1}
and
C_{j^{*}}=A\cup C_{j^{*}-1}
:

\begin{split}\tilde{x}(A)\ &=\ \tilde{x}(\{i^{*}\})+\tilde{x}(A\setminus\{i^{*}\})\ \leq\ \tilde{x}(\{i^{*}\})+z(A\setminus\{i^{*}\})\\
&=\ z(C_{j^{*}})-z(C_{j^{*}-1})+z(A\setminus\{i^{*}\})\ \leq\ z(A)\,.\end{split}

Hence,
\tilde{x}\in\mathsf{P}(z)
.

Now, choose an arbitrary direction
{y}\in\mathcal{B}_{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}}
.
By Lemma
A.2

y(i)=y(i^{\prime})
for
i,i^{\prime}\in T_{l}
so we can set
\hat{y}_{l}\coloneqq y(i)
for
i\in T_{l}
and
l=1,\dots,k
. Moreover,
\hat{y}_{l}\geq\hat{y}_{l+1}
.
For a point
x\in\mathsf{P}(z)
compute:

\begin{split}{y}(\tilde{x})-{y}(x)&=\sum_{i\in I}\tilde{x}_{i}{y}(i)-\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}{y}(i)=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\hat{y}_{l}\big(\tilde{x}(T_{l})-x(T_{l})\big)\\
&=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\Big(\hat{y}_{l}\big(\tilde{x}(T_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup T_{l})-x(T_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup T_{l})\big)\\
&\hskip 120.55518pt-\hat{y}_{l}\big(\tilde{x}(T_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup T_{l-1})-x(T_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup T_{l-1})\big)\Big)\\
&=\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}(\hat{y}_{l}-\hat{y}_{l+1})\big(\tilde{x}(T_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup T_{l})-x(T_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup T_{l})\big)+\hat{y}_{k}\big(\tilde{x}(I)-x(I)\big)\\
&=\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}(\underbrace{\hat{y}_{l}-\hat{y}_{l+1}}_{\geq 0})\big(\underbrace{z(T_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup T_{l})-x(T_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup T_{l})}_{\geq 0}\big)\geq 0,\end{split}

where we use in the last equality, that the sets
T_{1},T_{1}\sqcup T_{2},\dots,T_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup T_{k}
are contained in the chain
\mathcal{C}\colon\emptyset=C_{0}\subset\dots\subset C_{n}=I
and that we already know
\tilde{x}(C_{j})=z(C_{j})
for
j=1,\dots,n
.
Since the computation in
A
is independent from the actual values of the direction
{y}\in\mathcal{B}_{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}}
, the inequality
{y}(\tilde{x})\geq{y}(x)
holds for every direction
{y}\in\mathcal{B}_{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}}
.
So the braid cone
\mathcal{B}_{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}}
is contained in the normal cone
N_{\mathsf{P}(z)}(F)
, where
F
is a face containing
\tilde{x}
. Hence,
\mathsf{P}(z)
is a generalized permutahedron.

For the opposite implication let
\mathcal{P}
be a generalized permutahedron.
We will define a submodular function
z_{\mathcal{P}}
and show that
\mathcal{P}=\mathsf{P}(z_{\mathcal{P}})
.
Since the generalized permutahedron
\mathcal{P}
is contained in the hyperplane with constant coordinate sum, the following set function is well defined:

\begin{split}z_{\mathcal{P}}(I)&\coloneqq\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\quad\text{ for }x\in\mathcal{P}\\
z_{\mathcal{P}}(A)&\coloneqq\max_{x\in\mathcal{P}}\bigg(\sum_{i\in A}x_{i}\bigg)\quad\text{ for }A\subseteq I\,.\end{split}

We can immediately deduce that
z(\varnothing)=0
and
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathsf{P}(z_{\mathcal{P}})
.

First we show that
z_{\mathcal{P}}
is submodular.
For arbitrary
A,B\subseteq I
find a chain
\mathcal{C}\colon\emptyset=C_{0}\subset C_{1}\subset\dots\subset C_{k}=I
in the Boolean lattice
2^{I}
that contains
A\cap B
and
A\cup B
.
We set
T_{i}\coloneqq C_{i}\setminus C_{i-1}
for
i=1,\dots,k
and consider the braid cone
\mathcal{B}_{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}}=\cone\{\mathbf{1}_{T_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{1}_{T_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup T_{k-1}}\}+\spn\{\mathbf{1}_{I}\}
.
Then there exists a face
F
of
\mathcal{P}
such that the normal cone
N_{\mathcal{P}}(F)
contains the braid cone
\mathcal{B}_{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}}
and in particular every point
x\in F
is maximal in the direction
\mathbf{1}_{A\cap B},\mathbf{1}_{A\cup B}\in\mathcal{B}_{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}}
.
Then,

(A.11)

z_{\mathcal{P}}(A)+z_{\mathcal{P}}(B)\geq x(A)+x(B)=x(A\cup B)+x(A\cap B)=z_{\mathcal{P}}(A\cup B)+z_{\mathcal{P}}(A\cap B)

and
z_{\mathcal{P}}
is submodular.

Now it is left to show that
\mathcal{P}\supseteq\mathsf{P}(z_{\mathcal{P}})
.
The main idea for this part of the proof can be found in
[
DF10
]
.
For the sake of a contradiction, let us assume there is a point
u\in\mathsf{P}(z_{\mathcal{P}})\setminus\mathcal{P}
.
Then there exists a separating hyperplane
H_{{t},c}\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbbmss{R}I\ \colon\ {t}(x)=c\}
such that

(A.12)

{t}(u)=\sum_{i\in I}{t}_{i}u_{i}>c\quad\text{and}\quad{t}(p)=\sum_{i\in I}{t}_{i}p_{i}\leq c\quad\text{for all }p\in\mathcal{P}

Now choose a braid cone
\mathcal{B}_{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}}
such that
t\in\mathcal{B}_{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}}
and set again
\hat{t}_{l}\coloneqq t_{i}
for
i\in T_{l}
,
l=1,\dots,k
.
For points
q
in the
t
-maximal face
F\coloneqq\mathcal{P}_{t}
we know by the definition of
z
that
{q(T_{1}\cup\dots\cup T_{l})=z(T_{1}\cup\dots\cup T_{l})}
for
l=1,\dots,k
.
Using telescoping sums we compute

\begin{split}t(u)=\sum_{i\in I}t_{i}u_{i}>c\geq t\cdot q&=\sum_{i\in I}t_{i}q_{i}=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\hat{t}_{j}q(T_{l})\\
&=\hat{t}_{k}q(T_{1}\cup\dots\cup T_{k})+\sum_{l=k-1}^{1}(\hat{t}_{l}-\hat{t}_{l+1})q(T_{1}\cup\dots\cup T_{l})\\
&=\hat{t}_{k}z(T_{1}\cup\dots\cup T_{k})+\sum_{l=k-1}^{1}(\hat{t}_{l}-\hat{t}_{l+1})z(T_{1}\cup\dots\cup T_{l})\\
&\geq\hat{t}_{k}u(T_{1}\cup\dots\cup T_{k})+\sum_{l=k-1}^{1}(\hat{t}_{l}-\hat{t}_{l+1})u(T_{1}\cup\dots\cup T_{l})\\
&=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\hat{t}_{l}u(T_{l})=\sum_{i\in I}t_{i}u_{i}=t(u).\end{split}

This is a contradiction and completes the proof.
∎

Recall that the hypergraphic polytope
\mathcal{P}(h)\subset\mathbbmss{R}I
of a hypergraph

is defined as

(A.13)

\mathcal{P}(h)=\sum_{e\in E}\Delta_{e}\ \subset\mathbbmss{R}I

where

(A.14)

\Delta_{e}=\conv\{b_{i}\ \colon\ i\in e\},\quad\text{for a hyperedge }e\subseteq I\,

and

are the basis vectors for

.

Proposition A.3

(
[
Pos09
, Proposition 6.3.]
)
.

For a hypergraph
h=(I,E)
and its hypergraphic polytope
\mathcal{P}(h)
, the function
z\colon 2^{I}\to\mathbbmss{R}
defined by

(A.15)

z(T)\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\\
e\cap T\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}1=\#(\text{hyperedges in $h$ that intersect }T)\quad\text{for }T\subseteq I

is a submodular function with

\mathcal{P}(h)=\Big\{x\in\mathbbmss{R}I\ \colon\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}=z(I)\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i\in T}x_{i}\leq z(T)\quad\text{ for }T\subseteq I\Big\}\,.

Hence, hypergraphic polytopes are generalized permutahedra and in bijection with hypergraphs.
