Abstract
We propose a novel non-parametric learning paradigm for the identification of drift and diffusion coefficients of multi-dimensional non-linear stochastic differential equations, which relies upon discrete-time observations of the state. The key idea essentially consists of fitting a RKHS-based approximation of the corresponding Fokker-Planck equation to such observations, yielding theoretical estimates of non-asymptotic learning rates which, unlike previous works, become increasingly tighter when the regularity of the unknown drift and diffusion coefficients becomes higher. Our method being kernel-based, offline pre-processing may be profitably leveraged to enable efficient numerical implementation, offering excellent balance between precision and computational complexity.

Introduction

Consider non-linear
stochastic differential equations
of type

\mathrm{d}X(t)=b(t,X(t))\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{a(t,X(t))}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad t\in[0,T].

(1)

Here,
X(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the (stochastic) state of dimension
n\in\mathbb{N}
of the system at time
t\in[0,T]
, with
T>0
a fixed time horizon,
(W_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is a
n
–dimensional Wiener process, whereas
b:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
a:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\textnormal{Sym}_{++}(n)
are regular enough
drift
and
diffusion
coefficients, with
\textnormal{Sym}_{++}(n)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
the subset of symmetric positive-definite matrices.
Equations such as (
1
) may be profitably leveraged to accurately model complex phenomena in a wide range of applications, such as aerospace, finance, and robotics to name a few
[
41
,
55
,
8
,
7
,
51
]
.

In practice, both coefficients
a
and
b
might be completely unknown, e.g.,
a
might model external perturbations due to many different physical phenomena which affect the motion of the system, as it occurs in aerospace and robotics. Therefore, appropriate
stochastic system identification
procedures for both
a
and
b
must be devised.

1.1
Related Work

The problem of stochastic system identification has been investigated for several decades. The earliest methods mainly address either discrete-time models
[
25
,
35
]
or continuous-time models that are however linear in the state variable
[
20
]
, which thus do not fit the identification setting introduced by (
1
).

More recently, identification of non-linear stochastic differential equations such as (
1
) have seen an important surge of interest.
More specifically, besides some likelihood-based
[
29
]
or Kalman filtering-based estimation methods
[
48
,
6
,
45
]
, which assume continuous-time observation of the stochastic state, the vast majority of the existing methods leverage more realistic discrete-time observations of
X
. Efficient paradigms range from 1. non-parametric estimation
[
13
,
21
,
12
,
23
]
and Bayesian estimation
[
50
,
38
]
, to 2. maximum likelihood and quasi-likelihood methods
[
17
,
56
,
32
,
49
]
, generalized methods of moments
[
19
,
18
,
39
]
, and online gradient descent-based methods
[
22
,
34
]
, among others. On the one hand, although Group 1. of these works provides learning rates which become increasingly tighter when the number of observations of the state grows, estimates of the error between the unknown drift and diffusion coefficients and the learned ones are obtained by assuming diffusion coefficients are either known or have specific parametric structures. On the other hand, although Group 2. of the aforementioned works rely on generally milder assumptions to be implemented, they require working with given families of finite-dimensional parametric drift and diffusion coefficients, which might hinder the identification process in the case the family of parametric coefficients is not rich enough.

Importantly, none of the above works has investigated how to leverage the regularity, i.e., high order continuous differentiability of drift and diffusion coefficients to improve learning rates. Although such analysis has been recently undertaken in, e.g.,
[
1
,
37
,
2
,
30
]
, these studies often work with SDE that are either scalar or perturbed by constant diffusion coefficients, and they offer learning rates that are often only asymptotic. Therefore, designing methods for the identification of multi-dimensional non-linear SDE with non-constant diffusion coefficients under discrete-time observations of the state remains a challenging open question (note that multivariate settings are known to generally require very different techniques, see, e.g.,
[
37
,
2
]
). In particular, a key part of the challenge lies in developing non-asymptotic rates of convergence that become tighter not only when the number of observations of the state grows, but also when the regularity of drift and diffusion coefficients is higher.

Finally, from a numerical standpoint, learning-based methods, such as scalable gradient methods
[
26
]
and infinitely deep Bayesian neural networks
[
54
]
, have shown remarkable performance on complex stochastic differential equations. Unfortunately, it seems extremely challenging to endow these latter paradigms with theoretical guarantees of convergence, motivating investigation of numerically efficient identification methods for (
1
) which enjoy guarantees of accuracy under mild assumptions.

1.2
Outline and Contributions

We propose a non-parametric,
Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space
(RKHS)-based learning paradigm for the identification of drift and diffusion coefficients of the
multi-dimensional non-linear SDE
(
1
), which relies upon discrete-time observation of the state. In particular, motivated by classical likelihood-based methods
[
39
]
we propose a two-step, discrete-time observation-based scheme which entails fitting the
Fokker-Planck equation
related to (
1
).

Under assumptions of smoothness for the unknown drift and diffusion coefficients, we provide theoretical estimates of
non-asymptotic
learning rates which become increasingly tighter when the number of observations of the state grows. In particular, given the nature of our data set, which essentially depends on observations of the state process, we consider the error between solutions to (
1
) generated with the learned drift and diffusion coefficients and the unknown trajectories of (
1
) is a “good metric” to test the accuracy of our identification method. Importantly, under this accuracy metric we can additionally prove our learning rates become tighter when the regularity (in a Sobolev sense) of the unknown drift and diffusion coefficients is higher. Finally, from a numerical standpoint, our method being kernel-based, offline pre-processing may be successfully leveraged to enable efficient implementations,
offering excellent balance between precision and computational complexity (details are in Section
6
).

Our method is composed of two steps which are informally summarized below.

A.
Learning the laws of the stochastic differential equation through independent discrete-time observation of the state.

We assume there exist regular enough,
i.e. essentially
C^{2m+1}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, drift
b:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and diffusion
a:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\textnormal{Sym}_{++}(n)
, and a stochastic process
X:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
in some filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{G},\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, such that
X
solves (
1
) with drift
b
and diffusion
a
. In addition, we may assume we can sample
X
at
M\in\mathbb{N}
times
0=t_{1}<\dots<t_{M}=T
, and more specifically that at every time
t_{\ell}
,
\ell=1,\dots,M
, we have access to
N\in\mathbb{N}
samples
X_{\ell,1}\triangleq X(t_{\ell},\omega_{1}),\dots,X_{\ell,N}\triangleq X(t_{\ell},\omega_{N})
of the solution
X
which have been independently drawn from
\mathbb{P}_{X(t_{\ell},\cdot)}
.

In such setting, as first step we propose to approximate the unknown densities
p:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
of the laws of
X
through the (random) RKHS-based model:

\displaystyle\widehat{p}(t,x)\triangleq\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}c_{\ell}(t)\widehat{g}_{\ell}(x),\quad\textnormal{where}\quad\widehat{g}_{\ell}(x)\triangleq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\rho_{R}(x-X_{\ell,j}),

for appropriate coefficients
c_{\ell}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
and radial mappings
\rho_{R}
,
R>0
. Let
\mu_{X}
denote the probability measure which is generated by the process
X
. For every tuple of precision parameters
0<\varepsilon,\delta<1
, if the unknown drift
b
and diffusion
a
are regular enough, by leveraging RKHS approximation theory we can show appropriate values for
M
,
N
, and
R
(which depend on
\varepsilon

and
m
uniquely) may be selected so that the following learning rate holds with probability
\mu_{X}
at least
1-\delta
:

\int^{T}_{0}\left\|\frac{\partial\widehat{p}}{\partial t}(t,\cdot)-\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}(t,\cdot)\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\widehat{p}(t,\cdot)-p(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{H^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}t={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}O\left(\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\varepsilon\right)^{2}\right)},

(2)

where
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{H^{2}}
denote the norms of the Hilbert spaces
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
and
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
, respectively. Moreover, the higher the degree of smoothness of
b
and
a
is, the lower the values of
M
,
N
, and
R
needed to achieve this precision become.

The main benefit which comes with the model
\widehat{p}
consists of computing accurate finite-dimensional approximations of the laws of solutions to (
1
) without a priori resorting to conservative families of parametric densities. In particular, the model
\widehat{p}
being kernel-based, one may considerably reduce the computational effort by resorting to prior offline computations, which essentially boil down to simply inverting a
M\times M
matrix. As a byproduct, (
2
) provides a quantitative estimate of the approximation error which is key to derive theoretical guarantees for the accuracy of our identification method in the next step.

B.
Learning finite-dimensional models for the drift and diffusion coefficients by fitting approximated solutions to the Fokker-Planck equation.

If the unknown drift
b
and diffusion
a
are regular enough, the unknown densities
p
satisfy the following Fokker-Planck equation:

\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}(t,y)=(\mathcal{L}^{a,b}_{t})^{*}p(t,y),\quad(t,y)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},

(3)

where
(\mathcal{L}^{a,b}_{t})^{*}
is the dual operator of the Kolmogorov generator

\mathcal{L}^{a,b}_{t}\varphi(y)\triangleq\frac{1}{2}\sum^{n}_{i,j=1}a_{ij}(t,y)\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial y_{i}\partial y_{j}}(y)+\sum^{n}_{i=1}b_{i}(t,y)\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial y_{i}}(y),\quad\varphi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}).

Given the results at the previous step, it is then natural to learn models of the drift
\widehat{b}_{Q}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the diffusion
\widehat{a}_{Q}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\textnormal{Sym}_{++}(n)
which “best” match the Fokker-Planck equation when evaluated at
\widehat{p}
, that is as solutions to the following finite-dimensional convex optimization problem:

\underset{(\widehat{a}_{Q},\widehat{b}_{Q})\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}}{\min}\ \int^{T}_{0}\left\|\frac{\partial\widehat{p}}{\partial t}(t,\cdot)-(\mathcal{L}^{\widehat{a}_{Q},\widehat{b}_{Q}}_{t})^{*}\widehat{p}(t,\cdot)\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}t+\lambda\|(\widehat{a}_{Q},\widehat{b}_{Q})\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}},

(4)

where
\mathcal{H}_{Q}
is an appropriate finite-dimensional subspace of a RKHS
\mathcal{H}
with norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}
, in which the unknown drift and diffusion coefficients lie, whereas
\lambda>0
is a regularization weight to be appropriately selected.
As detailed in Section
6
, problem (
4
) admits a finite dimensional characterization that can be solved exactly, i.e., no approximation or integral discretization is needed, with reduced computational cost.

Thanks to estimate (
2
), one shows that, for appropriate choices of the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{Q}
and the regularization weight
\lambda
, the solution
(\widehat{a}_{Q},\widehat{b}_{Q})\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}
to problem (
4
) satisfies the following, with probability
\mu_{X}
at least
1-\delta
:

\displaystyle\int^{T}_{0}\left\|\frac{\partial\widehat{p}}{\partial t}(t,\cdot)-(\mathcal{L}^{\widehat{a}_{Q},\widehat{b}_{Q}}_{t})^{*}\widehat{p}(t,\cdot)\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}t+\lambda\|(\widehat{a}_{Q},\widehat{b}_{Q})\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}=

\displaystyle\hskip 215.2771pt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=O\left(\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\varepsilon\right)^{2}\right)}.

(5)

By combining estimate (
1.2
) with energy-type estimates for parabolic partial differential equations, we may infer theoretical estimates of learning rates for the identification of drift and diffusion coefficients of non-linear stochastic differential equations, which we informally summarize as follows.

As we mentioned previously, since our data consists of observation of the state process, the error between the unknown densities and the densities stemming from the learned coefficients is a “good metric” with which the convergence of an identification algorithm for stochastic differential equations which leverages observations of the state process may be tested. To better formalize this metric, let
X^{\widehat{a}_{Q},\widehat{b}_{Q}}
and
p_{\widehat{a}_{Q},\widehat{b}_{Q}}
respectively denote the solutions to (
1
) and (
3
) with coefficients
(\widehat{a}_{Q},\widehat{b}_{Q})\in\mathcal{H}_{H}
. We thus define the following metric to test the accuracy:

E(\widehat{a}_{Q},\widehat{b}_{Q})\triangleq\|p_{\widehat{a}_{Q},\widehat{b}_{Q}}-p\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=\int^{T}_{0}\|p_{\widehat{a}_{Q},\widehat{b}_{Q}}(t,\cdot)-p(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}t.

By adopting this metric, our main result on the accuracy of our learning method may be summarized in the following meta-theorem:

Meta-Theorem 1
.

Assume the unknown drift
b
and diffusion
a
coefficients are regular enough. The following estimate holds true with probability
\mu_{X}
at least
1-\delta
:

E(\widehat{a}_{Q},\widehat{b}_{Q})={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}O\left(\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\varepsilon\right)^{2}\right)}

In particular, for every regular enough function
f:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
:

\mathbb{E}\left[\int^{T}_{0}f(t,X(t))\;\mathrm{d}t\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int^{T}_{0}f(t,X^{\widehat{a}_{Q},\widehat{b}_{Q}}(t))\;\mathrm{d}t\right]+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}O\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\varepsilon\right)}.

(6)

Although the estimates provided in this meta-theorem are informal and need some clarification (see Section
5
for more rigorous statements), they show the aforementioned two-step identification method enjoys practical theoretical guarantees of accuracy, i.e., estimate (
6
): an observation/regulation metric
f
computed at the unknown state solution to (
1
) with unknown drift
b
and diffusion
a
may be rather observed through the process solution to (
1
) with model drift
\widehat{b}_{Q}
and diffusion
\widehat{a}_{Q}
up to an error
O\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\varepsilon\right)
. Such result has important implications in observation and regulation of stochastic differential equations
[
23
]
, and it may represent a good starting result to develop paradigms for the identification of
controlled stochastic differential equations
, which are crucial for the control of complex autonomous systems.

2
Notation and Preliminary Results

We fix the dimension
n\in\mathbb{N}
of the state space and a time horizon
T>0
for the identification process. We denote by
\textnormal{Sym}_{+}(n)
and
\textnormal{Sym}_{++}(n)
the subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
of symmetric semi-positive-definite and symmetric positive-definite matrices, respectively.

We assume stochastic differential equations are defined on a given filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{G}\triangleq\mathcal{F}_{T},\mathcal{F}\triangleq(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]},\mathbb{P})
, which is complete, and the noise is generated by a
\mathcal{F}
–adapted Wiener process
W:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
(e.g., we may consider the canonical process in the space
\Omega=C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{n})
, equipped with the Wiener measure). Moreover, we introduce the complete metric space
(S,\mathfrak{d})
, where

S\triangleq C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{n}),\quad\mathfrak{d}(w_{1},w_{2})\triangleq\underset{t\in[0,T]}{\sup}\ \|w_{1}(t)-w_{2}(t)\|,

and equip it with the Borel sigma-algebra
\mathcal{B}(S)
induced by the metric
\mathfrak{d}
.

For any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
,
r,s>0
, and any
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
, we denote by
H^{r,s}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},A)
the Sobolev (Hilbert) space of functions whose image is in
A
, and whose weak time derivatives are defined up to order
r
and whose weak space derivatives are defined up to order
s
; in particular, we denote
H^{r}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},A)\triangleq H^{r,r}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},A)
. Finally, we introduce the following Hilbert space and corresponding positive convex cone, in which we will assume the unknown drift and diffusion coefficients lie (this latter requirement will be made more explicit shortly):

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{m}\triangleq H^{d(m)}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})\times H^{d(m)}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{+}_{m}\triangleq\Big\{(a,b):[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\textnormal{Sym}_{+}(n)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ (a,b)\in\mathcal{H}_{m}\Big\},

for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
, where
d(m)\triangleq 2(m+1)+\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor\in\mathbb{N}
is the unique integer greater than
2m+1
such that
\left\lfloor d(m)-\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor=2m+1
. The choice of taking the same exponent
d(m)
for both the drift and the diffusion coefficients has been made without loss of generality for the sake of conciseness. The following regularity result will be crucial:

Theorem 2
.

There exists a constant
C>0
, which depends on
m
uniquely, such that every
(a,b)\in\mathcal{H}^{+}_{m}
satisfies the following properties:

1.

Differentiability:

a\in C^{2m+1}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}),\quad b\in C^{2m+1}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n}).

2.

Boundness of functions and their derivatives:

\sum^{2m+1}_{i=0}\Big(\|D^{i}_{(t,y)}a\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|D^{i}_{(t,y)}b\|_{L^{\infty}}\Big)\leq C\|(a,b)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{m}},

where
D^{i}_{(t,y)}
denotes the differential of order
i
with respect to
(t,y)
. In particular, the following refined bound holds:

\sum^{2}_{i=0}\Big(\|D^{i}_{(t,y)}a\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|D^{i}_{(t,y)}b\|_{L^{\infty}}\Big)\leq C\|(a,b)\|_{H^{d(m)-2m+1}}.

The proof of this result makes use of classical embedding arguments and it is reported in Appendix
A
for the sake of completeness.

3
Stochastic Differential and Fokker-Planck Equations

In this section, we summarize both classical and less classical results about stochastic differential equations, and corresponding relationships with the Fokker-Planck equation. In particular, we propose a minimally detailed discussion for the sake of conciseness, reporting a more structured exposition in Section
8
for the sake of completeness.

3.1
The Fokker-Planck equation

From now on, we fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
and a constant
\alpha>0
(to be specified later). Since we will need to work with diffusion coefficients that are never trivial,
we replace
\mathcal{H}^{+}_{m}
with

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,\alpha}_{m}}\triangleq(\alpha I,0)+\Big\{(a,b)\in\mathcal{H}^{+}_{m}:\ a(t,x)\succcurlyeq 0,\ (t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\Big\}.}

At this step, we fix a non-negative density
p_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
which will serve as appropriate initial condition, and we denote by
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the associate probability measure. Motivated by
[
47
,
16
]
, we recall the following notions of stochastic differential equation and its solutions (though they differ from the ones in
[
47
,
16
]
):

Theorem-Definition 3
.

There exists a measurable mapping
X:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\Omega\to S
such that each process
X_{x}(t,\omega)\triangleq X(x,\omega)(t)
is
\mathcal{F}
–progressively measurable for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and such that the following Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE)

\textnormal{SDE}_{x}\quad\begin{cases}\mathrm{d}X_{x}(t)=b(t,X_{x}(t))\;\mathrm{d}t+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sqrt{a(t,X_{x}(t))}}\;\mathrm{d}W_{t},\\[5.0pt]
\mathbb{P}\big(X_{x}(0)=x\big)=1,\end{cases}

holds in
(\Omega,\mathcal{G},\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
for
\mu_{0}
-almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We then say that
X
solves or is solution to SDE (with coefficients
(a,b)\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,\alpha}_{m}}
). The solution
X
to SDE is unique in the following sense: if a measurable mapping
Y:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\Omega\to S
solves SDE, then it holds that
X(x,\cdot)=Y(x,\cdot)
a.s., for
\mu_{0}
-almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

For the well-posedness of Theorem-Definition
3
, see Section
8
. Solutions to SDE share a close relationship with the solutions to the Fokker-Planck equation, which we introduce next. For every
(a,b)\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,\alpha}_{m}}
, we denote the Kolmogorov generator by

\mathcal{L}^{a,b}_{t}\varphi(y)\triangleq\frac{1}{2}\sum^{n}_{i,j=1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a}_{ij}(t,y)\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial y_{i}\partial y_{j}}(y)+\sum^{n}_{i=1}b_{i}(t,y)\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial y_{i}}(y),\quad\varphi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}).

Let
X:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\Omega\to S
be solution of SDE with coefficients
(a,b)\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,\alpha}_{m}}
. Thanks to specific regularity properties of
X
(we refer to Section
8
for these latter), we may define the curve
\mu:[0,T]\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
of probability measures

\mu_{t}(A)\triangleq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\Omega}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{x}(t)\in A\}}\;\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\;\mu_{0}(\mathrm{d}x),\quad A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

(7)

and note that
\mu
is narrowly continuous, i.e., for every
\varphi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
, the mapping

t\in[0,T]\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(y)\mu_{t}(\mathrm{d}y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\Omega}\varphi(X_{x}(t))\;\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\;\mu_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)\in\mathbb{R}

is continuous. By combining this latter property with the results in
[
16
]
, we introduce the following notions of Fokker-Planck equation, its solutions, and additional relationship between these solutions and the solutions to SDE (see also Section
8
):

Theorem-Definition 4
.

The curve
\mu:[0,T]\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
defined through (
7
) is the unique narrowly continuous curve satisfying the Fokker-Planck Equation

\textnormal{FPE}\quad\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(y)\mu_{t}(\mathrm{d}y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{L}^{a,b}_{t}\varphi(y)\mu_{t}(\mathrm{d}y),\quad\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}),\\[5.0pt]
\mu_{t=0}=\mu_{0}.\end{cases}

We then say that
\mu
solves or is solution to FPE (with coefficients
(a,b)\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,\alpha}_{m}}
). If
X
denotes the solution to SDE, the following representation formula holds:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(y)\mu_{t}(\mathrm{d}y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\Omega}\varphi(X_{x}(t))\;\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\;\mu_{0}(\mathrm{d}x),\quad\textnormal{for}\ t\in[0,T],\ \varphi\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}).

(8)

3.2
Absolutely continuous solutions to the Fokker-Planck equation

Thanks to the regularity of the coefficients
(a,b)\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,\alpha}_{m}}
which is offered through Theorem
2
, the solution
\mu
to FPE is absolutely continuous, i.e., it takes the form

\mu_{t}(A)=\int_{A}p(t,y)\;\mathrm{d}y,\quad A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

for an appropriate

Definition 5

Let

\textnormal{FPE}_{f}\quad\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(y)p(t,y)\;\mathrm{d}y=\\
\displaystyle\hskip 43.05542pt=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\mathcal{L}^{a,b}_{t}\varphi(y)p(t,y)+f(t,y)\varphi(y)\right)\;\mathrm{d}y,\quad\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}),\\[10.0pt]

The next theorem gathers important properties of the solution to

For any

\displaystyle\|p(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\int^{t}_{0}

\displaystyle\|p(s,\cdot)\|^{2}_{H^{1}}\;\mathrm{d}s\leq

\displaystyle\leq C\big({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha},\|({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a-\alpha I},b)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{m}}\big)\left(\|\bar{p}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\int^{t}_{0}\|f(s,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}s\right),\quad t\in[0,T],

(9)

Assume

Useful Results from RKHS Theory

In this section, we list classical results about Sobolev Spaces of functions with scattered zeros and Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHS), which will be extensively leveraged in the following sections and for which we mainly refer to
[
3
,
52
,
46
]
.

Let
r,s\in\mathbb{N}
such that
r>s/2
. Every function
u\in H^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{s},\mathbb{R})
satisfies

C\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|u\|_{H^{r}}=\|J_{r}(\cdot)({\cal F}u)(\cdot)\|_{L^{2}},

(12)

where
J_{r}(z)\triangleq(1+\|z\|^{2})^{r/2}/(2\pi)^{s/2}
,
z\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
denotes the Bessel potential and
{\cal F}u
the Fourier transform of
u
, whereas the constant
C>0
depends on
s
uniquely.

Let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
,
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{s}
be a bounded domain, and
u\in H^{r}(D,\mathbb{R})
be a function such that
u|_{\widehat{X}}=0
, where
\widehat{X}_{\ell}\triangleq\{x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{s}
is a finite set of
\ell
given points. For the
fill distance
, which is defined by

h_{\widehat{X}_{\ell},D}\triangleq\sup_{x\in D}\ \min_{i=1,\dots,\ell}\ \|x-x_{i}\|,

the following inequality holds true, e.g.,
[
52
, Theorem 11.32]
,

\|u\|_{H^{\nu}}\leq Ch_{\widehat{X}_{\ell},D}^{r-\nu}\|u\|_{H^{r}},\quad 0\leq\nu\leq r,

(13)

where the constant
C>0
depends on
\nu
,
r
, and
D
uniquely. Inequality
13
may be interpreted as follows: if
u
is zero on a well distributed set of points over
D
, i.e.,
h_{\widehat{X}_{\ell},D}
is small, and is very regular, i.e.,
r
is large, then any norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{\nu}}
,
0\leq\nu\leq r
, e.g., the
L^{2}
norm for
\nu=0
, is small over the whole domain
D
.

Given a set
D
, a RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{D}
is a separable Hilbert space of functions
u:D\to\mathbb{R}
such that the following
reproducing property
holds true:

Definition 8
(Reproducing property
[
3
]
).

For every point
x\in D
there exists a reproducing function
k_{x}\in\mathcal{H}_{D}
such that

f(x)={\langle{{f}},{{k_{x}}}\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}_{D}},\quad f\in\mathcal{H}_{D}.

We denote
K_{D}:D\times D\to\mathbb{R}
, the
reproducing kernel
associated to
\mathcal{H}_{D}
, i.e.,

K_{D}(x_{1},x_{2})\triangleq{\langle{{k_{x_{1}}}},{{k_{x_{2}}}}\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}_{D}},\quad x_{1},x_{2}\in D.

The following crucial result holds true, see, e.g.,
[
46
]
:

Theorem 9
.

Given a reproducing kernel for a RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{D}
, by the reproducing property, the reproducing function
k_{x}\in\mathcal{H}_{D}
for any
x\in D
corresponds to

k_{x}=K_{D}(x,\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}_{D}.

We recall that the Sobolev space
H^{r}(D,\mathbb{R})
is a RKHS for
r>s/2
, and
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{s}
be either
\mathbb{R}^{s}
or an open domain with Lipschitz boundary and
s\in\mathbb{N}
. In this case, the associated reproducing kernel has a known closed form, see, e.g.,
[
52
]
.

Learning Stochastic Differential Equations

In this section, we finally introduce and study the problem of learning drift and diffusion coefficients of a stochastic differential equation. For this, from now on we assume the following hypothesis, which naturally stems from our setting, to hold true (see also Definition
3
):

(A)

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
with
m\geq 1
,
\alpha>0
, and
p_{0}\in H^{2m+1}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})

(these will be considered as hyper-parameters in this work, see the next remark)

and a (unique) solution

to SDE with coefficients
(a_{*},b_{*})\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,\alpha}_{m}}

Remark 10
.

The quantities
m\in\mathbb{N}
with
m\geq 1
,
\alpha>0
, and
p_{0}\in H^{2m+1}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
, appearing in Assumption (A), play the role of hyper-parameters in this work. It is common and reasonable to assume prior knowledge of the initial distribution
p_{0}
and to have an informed estimate of the degree of smoothness
m

[
53
]
. Regarding the parameter
\alpha
, we emphasize that numerous examples of stochastic differential equations frequently employed in various applications have diffusion coefficients
a
that are uniformly lower-bounded by known
\alpha>0
. Notable examples include pricing in finance
[
5
]
, temperature modeling in electricity markets
[
40
]
, and dynamics modeling in planetary landing
[
15
]
, among others. Informed estimates of
\alpha
can be obtained using methods such as doubling trick-type algorithms
[
44
]
, which involve learning over a finite decreasing sequence of
\alpha
. Exploring more advanced estimation techniques for
\alpha
and analyzing how corresponding estimates influence the learning process lie beyond the scope of this work and are left for future research.

Thanks to the results we gathered in Section
3
, one readily checks that Assumption
(A)
yields the following characterization of the mapping
X
:

Corollary 11
.

With the notation
\mathcal{L}_{t}\triangleq\mathcal{L}^{a_{*},b_{*}}_{t}
, there exists a unique function
p\in H^{m+1,2(m+1)}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
which is non-negative and such that

\int_{A}p(t,x)\;\mathrm{d}x=\mu_{t}(A)\triangleq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\Omega}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{x}(t)\in A\}}\;\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\;\mu_{0}(\mathrm{d}x),\quad\textnormal{for all}\quad A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

and which satisfies:

\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}(t,y)=\mathcal{L}_{t}^{*}p(t,y),\quad\textnormal{a.e.}\quad(t,y)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}.

To achieve our goal, we proceed along three successive steps:

1.

First, we approximate the unknown curve of densities
p
from given samples
\{X_{x_{j}}(t_{\ell},\omega_{j})\}_{\ell=1,\dots,M,j=1,\dots,N}
of the unknown solution
X
to SDE, through a finite-dimensional RKHS-based model
\widehat{p}
. Thanks to this, all the quantities appearing in the learning problems we define in the next steps can be actually (tractably) computed. As is customary in many applications, we assume our data are collected by independently sampling
X
at pre-defined times.

2.

Second, we approximate the unknown coefficients
(a_{*},b_{*})\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,\alpha}_{m}}
through additional coefficients
(\widehat{a},\widehat{b})\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,\alpha}_{m}}
which “best” match SFPE when evaluated at
\widehat{p}
, through an appropriately well-posed infinite-dimensional learning problem. We then combine appropriate estimates with (
6
) to show the solution to SFPE with coefficients
(\widehat{a},\widehat{b})
well-approximates the unknown
p
.

3.

Third, we make the learning problem at the second step “tractable” by approximating the coefficients
(\widehat{a},\widehat{b})
through a finite-dimensional RKHS-based model
(\widehat{a}_{Q},\widehat{b}_{Q})
. We then combine the estimates we obtained at the second step with (
6
) to show the solution to SFPE with coefficients
(\widehat{a}_{Q},\widehat{b}_{Q})
well-approximates the unknown
p
. Finally, this latter property is combined with (
7
) to show theoretical error bounds in the context of observation and regulation of stochastic differential equations.

5.1
Approximating solutions to SDE via RKHS-based models

We start by defining our data set. We assume sampling happens at
M\in\mathbb{N}
fixed times

t_{\ell}\triangleq T(\ell-1)/(M-1)

At this step, fix

\displaystyle\widehat{p}(t,x)\triangleq\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}c_{\ell}(t)\widehat{g}_{\ell}(x),\quad\textnormal{where}\quad\widehat{g}_{\ell}(x)\triangleq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\rho_{R}(x-X_{\ell,j})

\int^{T}_{0}\|p_{\widehat{a},\widehat{b}}(t,\cdot)-p(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}t\leq C\big(\alpha,\|(\widehat{a}-\alpha I,\widehat{b})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{m}}\big)C(a_{*},b_{*})\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\varepsilon\right)^{2}.

At this step, from Lemma

5.3
The finite-dimensional learning problem and its fidelity

The learning problem we introduced in Section
5.2
(see Definition
14
) remains difficult to numerically solve. Here, we discuss appropriate finite-dimensional approximations of LP and error bounds ensuring the fidelity of this latter approximation, ultimately making our learning approach for stochastic differential equations accurate and tractable.

We start by recalling and adapting the approximation tools we introduced in Section
4
to our framework.
Let
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}D=[0,T]\times\overline{B^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}_{R_{*}+1}(0)}}
, and consider the RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{D}=H^{d(m)}(D,\mathbb{R})
with associated kernel
K_{D}
. Without loss of generality,
we may assume
K_{D}
to equal 1 on
[0,T]\times\partial B^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}_{R_{*}+1}(0)
and such that all its derivatives equal zero on
[0,T]\times\partial B^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}_{R_{*}+1}(0)
. While not limiting, pursuing learning with slightly larger domains, i.e.,
B^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}_{R_{*}+1}(0)
instead of
B^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}_{R_{*}}(0)
, is key to enable rates of convergence under positiveness constraints, as we will see shortly.
For any set of
Q\in\mathbb{N}
points

\widetilde{X}_{D}\triangleq\Big\{(t_{1},x_{1}),\dots,(t_{Q},x_{Q})\Big\},

we consider the following coordinate-wise finite dimensional models to approximate the candidate drift and diffusion coefficient solutions
(\widehat{a},\widehat{b})
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a_{A}}(t,y)&\triangleq\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}=1}^{Q}A_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\,K_{D}((t,y),(t_{\ell},x_{\ell}))\,K_{D}((t,y),(t_{\ell^{\prime}},x_{\ell^{\prime}})),\quad(t,y)\in D,\\
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}b_{B}}(t,x)&\triangleq\sum_{\ell=1}^{Q}B_{\ell}K_{D}((t,y),(t_{\ell},x_{\ell})),\quad(t,y)\in D,\end{split}

with
A_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},B_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Note in particular that, by defining
\Phi_{Q}:D\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times Qn}
to be the map
\Phi_{Q}(t,y)=\big(K_{D}((t,x),(t_{1},x_{1}))I_{n\times n}|\dots|K_{D}((t,y),(t_{Q},x_{Q}))I_{n\times n}\big)
, then

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a_{A}}(t,y)=\Phi_{Q}(t,y)A\Phi_{Q}(t,y)^{\top},\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}b_{B}}(t,y)=\Phi_{Q}(t,y)B,

(21)

with
B\triangleq(B_{1},\dots,B_{Q})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{Qn}
, and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{Qn\times Qn}
is the
Q\times Q
block matrix with block entries
A_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}
. Therefore, we define the finite dimensional convex subset in which

is approximated by

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,Q}_{m,R_{*}}}\triangleq\Bigg\{\bigg(\chi\left(a_{A}+\frac{\alpha}{2}I\right)+(1-\chi)\alpha I,\chi b_{B}\bigg):}\

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}A\in\mathbb{R}^{Qn\times Qn},}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}A\succeq 0,\ \textnormal{and}\ B\in\mathbb{R}^{Qn}\Bigg\}\subseteq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,\alpha/2}_{m}},}

where
\chi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n},[0,1])
is any given cut-off function satisfying
\chi|_{\overline{B^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}_{R_{*}}(0)}}=1
and
\textnormal{supp}\;\chi\subseteq\overline{B^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}_{R_{*}+1}(0)}
. Through the subset
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,Q}_{m,R_{*}}}
, we enlarge the space in which we look for finite-dimensional approximating coefficients, due to
a_{A}+\alpha/2
with
A\succeq 0
. Crucial to enable rates of convergence under positiveness constraint as we will see shortly, such strategy is not limiting, as it poorly affects computational complexity.

Before moving to the core of this section, by leveraging the facts we recalled in Section
4
, we provide a crucial approximation results for coefficients

Theorem 18

Denote the fill distance between

\displaystyle\|b-\chi b_{B}\|_{H^{d(m)-2m+1}}=\|b-\chi b_{B}\|_{H^{d(m)-2m+1}(D)}

Remark 24

To the best of our knowledge, the learning rates (

,
2

For the sake of completeness, one may want to compare (

7
Conclusion and Perspectives

In this paper, we propose a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space-based learning paradigm for the identification of drift and diffusion coefficients of non-linear stochastic differential equations, which relies upon discrete-time observation of the state. Under assumptions of smoothness for the unknown drift and diffusion coefficients, we provide theoretical estimates of learning rates which become increasingly tighter when both the number of observations of the state and the regularity of the unknown drift and diffusion coefficients grow.

Some possible improvements and perspectives are in order. Since our learning rates essentially apply to the laws of the state process, it would be interesting to understand whether and under what conditions our method may be extended to derive stronger
L^{p}
norm-based learning rates. Finally, it would be interesting to investigate extensions of our work for the identification of controlled stochastic differential equations: although these models are crucial for the control of complex systems, e.g., in aerospace and robotics, methods which offer relevant guarantees of accuracy and efficiency of the identification process still require extensive investigation.

\bar{\mu}:[0,T]\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
solves FPE, and thus Proposition
28
yields

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(y)\mu_{t}(\mathrm{d}y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(y)\bar{\mu}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\Omega}\varphi(X_{x}(t))\;\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\;\mu_{0}(\mathrm{d}x),

for every
t\in[0,T]
and
\varphi\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
, and the conclusion follows.

8.2
Absolutely continuous solutions to the Fokker-Planck equation

From what we showed, solutions to SDE may be found by solving FPE. In this section, we show the existence of narrowly continuous curves
\mu:[0,T]\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
of type

\mu_{t}(A)=\int_{A}p(t,y)\;\mathrm{d}y,\quad A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

for appropriate densities
p:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, which are solutions to FPE. Note that, if such solutions to FPE exist, then they are unique thanks to Proposition
28
. Although such existence result is classic (see, e.g.,
[
16
,
9
]
), we retrace its proof in Appendix
A
to characterize the constants appearing in some appropriate estimates and regularity properties which have been paramount to derive the results in Section
5
.

For this, let us first recall the broader definition of solution to FPE, which in particular encompasses Definition
27
as a sub-case (see also Theorem
31
below):

Definition 30
.

Let
f\in L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
and
\bar{p}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
. A (regular enough) function
p:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to solve the non-homogeneous Fokker-Planck Equation with coefficients
(a,b)\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,\alpha}_{m}}
, if

\textnormal{FPE}_{f}\quad\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(y)p(t,y)\;\mathrm{d}y=\\
\displaystyle\hskip 43.05542pt=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\mathcal{L}^{a,b}_{t}\varphi(y)p(t,y)+f(t,y)\varphi(y)\right)\;\mathrm{d}y,\quad\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}),\\[10.0pt]
p(0,\cdot)=\bar{p}(\cdot).\end{cases}

We gather results on the existence, uniqueness, and energy-type estimates for solutions to FPE
f
, and therefore for solutions to FPE, in the following:

Theorem 31
.

For every
f\in L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
and every
\bar{p}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
, there exists a unique mapping
p\in C(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}))
which solves FPE
f
with coefficients
(a,b)\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,\alpha}_{m}}
, and with
\displaystyle\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}))
. In addition, the following first parabolic estimate holds:

\displaystyle\|p(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\int^{t}_{0}

\displaystyle\|p(s,\cdot)\|^{2}_{H^{1}}\;\mathrm{d}s\leq

\displaystyle\leq C\big({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha},\|({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a-\alpha I},b)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{m}}\big)\left(\|\bar{p}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\int^{t}_{0}\|f(s,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}s\right),\quad t\in[0,T],

(29)

with
C\big({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha},\|(a-\alpha I,b)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{m}}\big)>0
continuously depending on
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha}
and
\|(a-\alpha I,b)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{m}}
. Finally, if
f=0
and
\bar{p}
is a non-negative density in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
, then

p(t,\cdot)\geq 0,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}p(t,y)\;\mathrm{d}y=1,\quad t\in[0,T],

and therefore, if for every
t\in[0,T]
we define

\mu_{t}(A)\triangleq\int_{A}p(t,y)\;\mathrm{d}y,\quad A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

then the curve
\mu:[0,T]\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is narrowly continuous and solves FPE.

Remark 32
.

The regularity of the mapping
f
may be weakened (see, e.g.,
[
27
,
9
]
), although the requirement
f\in L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
already fits our purpose.

The proof of Theorem
31
is reported in Appendix
A
and is based on the classical Lions scheme (see, e.g.,
[
27
,
11
,
14
]
). Among straightforward benefits, we recall Theorem
31
enables introducing rigorous criteria to establish satisfactory guarantees for our learning approach in many circumstances. Let us better introduce this concept below. As a matter of example, when dealing with stochastic differential equations in applications such as observation and regulation, one must often manipulate metrics

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{0}\times\mathbb{P}}\left[\int^{T}_{0}f(t,X_{x}(t))\;\mathrm{d}t\right]=\int^{T}_{0}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}f(t,y)\;\mathbb{P}_{X_{x}(t)}(\mathrm{d}y)\;\mu_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)\right)\;\mathrm{d}t,

(30)

where the measurable mapping
X:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\Omega\to S
solves SDE for some coefficients
(a,b)\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,\alpha}_{m}}
, and the mapping
f:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is regular enough. In the case the coefficients
(a,b)\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,\alpha}_{m}}
of SDE are to be learned, one does not have perfect knowledge of (
30
), and therefore the error between (
30
) and its counterpart in which
(X_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}
is replaced with the solution to SDE stemming from rather learned coefficients must be estimated. This gap is filled with the following:

Corollary 33
.

For any coefficients
(a,b)\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,\alpha}_{m}}
, let
X^{a,b}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\Omega\to S
denote the unique measurable mapping which solves SDE with coefficients
(a,b)\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,\alpha}_{m}}
(see Theorem
29
). There exists an operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}:\

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,\alpha}_{m}}\to L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})^{*})\cong L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}))

\displaystyle(a,b)\mapsto\mathcal{O}^{a,b}_{\cdot}(\cdot)

which, for every
t\in[0,T]
and every
\varphi\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
, satisfies

\mathcal{O}^{a,b}_{t}(\varphi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\varphi(y)\;\mathbb{P}_{X^{a,b}_{x}(t)}(\mathrm{d}y)\;\mu_{0}(\mathrm{d}x).

In addition, if
p_{a,b}\in C(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}))
denotes the unique solution to FDE
0
with coefficients
(a,b)\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,\alpha}_{m}}
and
\bar{p}=p_{0}
(which uniquely exists thanks to Theorem
31
), for every
f\in L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
the mapping

t\in[0,T]\mapsto\mathcal{O}^{a,b}_{t}\big(f(t,\cdot)\big)\in\mathbb{R}

is measurable and in
L^{1}([0,T],\mathbb{R})
, and it satisfies, for every
(a_{1},b_{1}),(a_{2},b_{2})\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,\alpha}_{m}}
,

\int^{T}_{0}\big|\mathcal{O}^{a_{1},b_{1}}_{t}\big(f(t,\cdot)\big)-\mathcal{O}^{a_{2},b_{2}}_{t}\big(f(t,\cdot)\big)\big|\;\mathrm{d}t\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}}\|p_{a_{1},b_{1}}-p_{a_{2},b_{2}}\|_{L^{2}}.

(31)

Remark 34
.

Thanks to the previous Corollary, we may provide (
30
) with a rigorous meaning by defining, for every
f\in L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{0}\times\mathbb{P}}\left[\int^{T}_{0}f(t,X^{a,b}_{x}(t))\;\mathrm{d}t\right]\triangleq\int^{T}_{0}\mathcal{O}^{a,b}_{t}\big(f(t,\cdot)\big)\;\mathrm{d}t.

As we showed in Section
5
, the estimate (
31
) enables the control of any estimation error occurring during the computation of the metric (
30
). Note that more refined estimates than (
31
) may be easily obtained by requiring more regularity on the function
f
(e.g., see the proof of Corollary
33
below).

Proof.

Fix any
(a,b)\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,\alpha}_{m}}
. For
t\in[0,T]
and
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
, we define

\mathcal{O}^{a,b}_{t}(\varphi)=\underset{k\to\infty}{\lim}\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\varphi_{k}(y)\;\mathbb{P}_{X^{a,b}_{x}(t)}(\mathrm{d}y)\;\mu_{0}(\mathrm{d}x),

where
(\varphi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
is any sequence which converges to
\varphi
for the strong topology of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
. This definition is well-posed thanks to Theorem
29
: indeed, the limit above uniquely exists, given that if
(\varphi^{1}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}},(\varphi^{2}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
are two sequences which converge to
\varphi
for the strong topology of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
, we may compute

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\varphi^{1}_{k}(y)\;\mathbb{P}_{X^{a,b}_{x}(t)}(\mathrm{d}y)\;\mu_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)-

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\varphi^{2}_{k}(y)\;\mathbb{P}_{X^{a,b}_{x}(t)}(\mathrm{d}y)\;\mu_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)\bigg|\leq

\displaystyle\leq\|p_{a,b}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}\|\varphi^{1}_{k}-\varphi^{2}_{k}\|_{L^{2}}\to 0,\quad k\to\infty.

Similarly, one may easily prove that
\mathcal{O}^{a,b}_{t}(\cdot)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})^{*}\cong L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
for every
t\in[0,T]
. In particular, note that thanks to (
31
), for
t\in[0,T]
and
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
,

|\mathcal{O}^{a,b}_{t}(\varphi)|\leq C(a,b)\|p_{0}\|_{L^{2}}\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}},

(32)

for some appropriate constant
C(a,b)>0
.

Finally, let
g\in L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
and let
(g_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq C_{c}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
converge to

for the strong topology of
L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
. In particular, up to some subsequence, we infer that each sequence
\big(g_{k}(t,\cdot)\big)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq C_{c}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
converges to
g(t,\cdot)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
for the strong topology of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
, for almost every
t\in[0,T]
. Hence, the definition of
\mathcal{O}^{a,b}_{t}(\cdot)
and Theorem
29
yield

\mathcal{O}^{a,b}_{t}\big(g(t,\cdot)\big)=\underset{k\to\infty}{\lim}\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g_{k}(t,y)p_{a,b}(t,y)\;\mathrm{d}y,\quad\textnormal{for almost every $t\in[0,T]$}.

(33)

On the one hand, combined with Theorem
31
and Pettis’ theorem, (
33
) yields the Bochner-measurability of the mapping

, and therefore from (

) we deduce that
\mathcal{O}^{a,b}_{\cdot}(\cdot)\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})^{*})
. On the other hand, (

) yields the measurability of the mapping
t\in[0,T]\mapsto\mathcal{O}^{a,b}_{t}\big(f(t,\cdot)\big)\in\mathbb{R}
, which together with (
32
) makes the mapping
t\in[0,T]\mapsto\mathcal{O}^{a,b}_{t}\big(f(t,\cdot)\big)\in\mathbb{R}
measurable and in
L^{1}([0,T],\mathbb{R})
. The conclusion of the proof follows from the fact that (
31
) is a straightforward consequence of (
33
) and Fatou’s lemma.

8.3
Additional regularity of solutions to the Fokker-Planck equation

In Section
5
, we dealt with solutions
p:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
of FPE
0
which enjoy higher regularity properties, and specifically for which
p\in H^{m+1,2(m+1)}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
, and for which FPE
0
holds pointwise. Below, we are going to show such additional attributes stem from the properties of coefficients
(a,b)\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,\alpha}_{m}}
listed in Theorem
2
.

First, for every
(a,b)\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,\alpha}_{m}}
and almost every
t\in[0,T]
we introduce the notation:

(\mathcal{L}^{a,b}_{t})^{*}u(t,y)\triangleq\frac{1}{2}\sum^{n}_{i,j=1}\frac{\partial^{2}\big(a_{ij}u\big)}{\partial y_{j}\partial y_{i}}(t,y)-\sum^{n}_{i=1}\frac{\partial\big(b_{i}u\big)}{\partial y_{i}}(t,y),\quad u\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})),

which denotes the dual operator of
\mathcal{L}^{a,b}_{t}
. This operator is well-defined with image in
L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
. Indeed, thanks to Theorem
2
, one readily shows that

\displaystyle b_{i}u\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})),\quad\textnormal{with}\quad\frac{\partial\big(b_{i}u\big)}{\partial y_{r}}=b_{i}\frac{\partial u}{\partial y_{r}}+u\frac{\partial b_{i}}{\partial y_{r}}\in L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}),

\displaystyle a_{ij}u\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})),\quad\textnormal{with}\quad\frac{\partial\big(a_{ij}u\big)}{\partial y_{r}}=a_{ij}\frac{\partial u}{\partial y_{r}}+u\frac{\partial a_{ij}}{\partial y_{r}}\in L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}),

\displaystyle\hskip 131.31903pt\textnormal{and}\quad\frac{\partial^{2}\big(a_{ij}u\big)}{\partial y_{r}\partial y_{s}}=a_{ij}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial y_{r}\partial y_{s}}+\frac{\partial a_{ij}}{\partial y_{s}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial y_{r}}

\displaystyle\hskip 161.45782pt+\frac{\partial a_{ij}}{\partial y_{r}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial y_{s}}+u\frac{\partial^{2}a_{ij}}{\partial y_{r}\partial y_{s}}\in L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}),

for
u\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}))
,
i,j,r,s=1,\dots,n
, thus the well-posedness of
(\mathcal{L}^{a,b}_{t})^{*}
.

Thanks to Theorem
2
, the following higher regularity result holds:

Theorem 35
.

Under the setting and notation of Theorem
31
, if furthermore
\bar{p}\in H^{2m+1}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
, then the unique solution
p:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
to FPE
0
with coefficients
(a,b)\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,\alpha}_{m}}
is additionally such that

p\in H^{m+1}(0,T;H^{2(m+1)}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})).

In particular, the mapping
p
additionally satisfies the following Strong Fokker-Planck Equations with coefficients
(a,b)\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}^{+,\alpha}_{m}}
:

\textnormal{SFPE}\quad\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}(t,y)=(\mathcal{L}^{a,b}_{t})^{*}p(t,y),\quad\textnormal{a.e.}\quad(t,y)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\\[10.0pt]
p(0,\cdot)=\bar{p}(\cdot).\end{cases}

Remark 36
.

This result is classically proved under either time-independent coefficients or bounded domains (e.g.,
[
14
,
28
]
). Although in classical reference which consider time-dependent coefficients such as
[
28
]
it is explicitly mentioned therein that their results may be extended to unbounded domains, we found such extension non-straightforward. We therefore decided to revisit and extend the regularity results for parabolic equations which are contained in
[
14
, Section 6.3]
to time-dependent coefficients and unbounded domains, and to report the proof in Appendix
A
.

\hskip 107.63855pt+A^{0,\dot{a},\dot{b}}(t;p(t,\cdot),\varphi)=0,\quad\varphi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}),\\
q(0,\cdot)=(\mathcal{L}^{a,b}_{0})^{*}\bar{p}(\cdot).\end{cases}

(47)

Problem (
47
) is well-posed. Indeed, since
p\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}))
, thanks to Theorem
2
one easily shows that

A^{0,\dot{a},\dot{b}}(t;p(t,\cdot),\varphi)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\mathcal{L}^{\dot{a},\dot{b}}_{t})^{*}p(t,y)\varphi(y)\;\mathrm{d}y,

for every
\varphi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
and almost every
t\in[0,T]
. Therefore, since
(\mathcal{L}^{\dot{a},\dot{b}}_{t})^{*}p\in L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
and
(\mathcal{L}^{a,b}_{0})^{*}\bar{p}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
, problem (
47
) fits the setting of Theorem
31
, thus from our previous computations there exists a unique solution
q\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}))
to (
47
), which additionally satisfies
\displaystyle\frac{\partial q}{\partial t}\in L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
.

At this step, we define the function

\displaystyle w:\

\displaystyle[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle(t,y)\mapsto\bar{p}(y)+\left(\int^{t}_{0}q(s,\cdot)\;\mathrm{d}s\right)(y)=\bar{p}(y)+\int^{t}_{0}q(s,y)\;\mathrm{d}s,

where the Lebesgue integral in the last equality stems from the properties of the Bochner integral for functions in
L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
. Clearly,
w\in C^{0}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}))
, and Fubini theorem yields, for every
\varphi_{1}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
and
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}([0,T],\mathbb{R})
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\int^{T}_{0}\left(\int^{t}_{0}q(s,y)\;\mathrm{d}s\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi}{\mathrm{d}t}(t)\;\mathrm{d}t+\int^{T}_{0}q(t,y)\psi(t)\;\mathrm{d}t\right)\varphi_{1}(y)\;\mathrm{d}y=0,

and, for almost every
t\in[0,T]
and every
\varphi_{2}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}w(t,y)\frac{\partial\varphi_{2}}{\partial y_{j}}(y)\;\mathrm{d}y=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\frac{\partial\bar{p}}{\partial y_{j}}(y)+\int^{t}_{0}\frac{\partial q}{\partial y_{j}}(s,y)\;\mathrm{d}s\right)\varphi_{2}(y)\;\mathrm{d}y,\quad j=1,\dots,n.

In particular, from such properties we readily deduce that, for every
j=1,\dots,n
,

\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}=q\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})),\quad\textnormal{with}\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}}\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}\right)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial y_{j}}\right).

(48)

Thanks to Theorem
2
and the properties listed in (
48
), for every
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
and almost every
t\in[0,T]
, we may compute

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(w(t,\cdot),\varphi)_{L^{2}}=(q(t,\cdot),\varphi)_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\big((\mathcal{L}^{a,b}_{0})^{*}\bar{p},\varphi\big)_{L^{2}}-\int^{t}_{0}A^{\alpha,a,b}(s;q(s,\cdot),\varphi)\;\mathrm{d}s-\int^{t}_{0}A^{0,\dot{a},\dot{b}}(s;p(s,\cdot)\pm w(s,\cdot),\varphi)\;\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\mathcal{L}^{a,b}_{0})^{*}\bar{p}(y)\varphi(y)\;\mathrm{d}y-

\displaystyle\quad-\sum^{n}_{i,j=1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}\left(\int^{t}_{0}\left(a_{ij}(s,y)\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial y_{j}}\right)(s,y)+\dot{a}_{ij}(s,y)\frac{\partial w}{\partial y_{j}}(s,y)\right)\mathrm{d}s\right)\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial y_{i}}(y)\;\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\quad-\sum^{n}_{j=1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Bigg(\int^{t}_{0}\Bigg(\Bigg(\sum^{n}_{j=1}\frac{\partial\dot{a}_{ij}}{\partial y_{j}}(s,y)-\dot{b}_{i}(s,y)\Bigg)w(s,y)

\displaystyle\hskip 123.78433pt+\Bigg(\sum^{n}_{j=1}\frac{\partial a_{ij}}{\partial y_{j}}(s,y)-b_{i}(s,y)\Bigg)\frac{\partial w}{\partial s}(s,y)\Bigg)\mathrm{d}s\Bigg)\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial y_{i}}(y)\;\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\quad-\int^{t}_{0}A^{0,\dot{a},\dot{b}}(s;(p-w)(s,\cdot),\varphi)\;\mathrm{d}s.

Therefore, from
[
11
, Theorem 11.5]
and the fact that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\mathcal{L}^{a,b}_{0})^{*}\bar{p}(y)\varphi(y)\;\mathrm{d}y=-A^{\alpha,a,b}(0;\bar{p},\varphi),

we conclude that
w
solves the following integro-differential variational problem

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(w(t,\cdot),\varphi)_{L^{2}}+A^{\alpha,a,b}(t;w(t,\cdot),\varphi)+\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle\hskip 43.05542pt+\int^{t}_{0}A^{0,\dot{a},\dot{b}}(s;(p-w)(s,\cdot),\varphi)\;\mathrm{d}s=0,\quad\varphi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}),\\[10.0pt]
w(0,\cdot)=\bar{p}(\cdot).\end{cases}

(49)

Clearly,
p=w
is solution to (

). In particular, if so we would obtain that
\displaystyle\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}))
and
\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial t^{2}}\in L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
, and thanks to the choice of the basis

and to a higher order elliptic regularity argument applied to (

)–(

) with

, we would also obtain that

, that is the sought second-order-in-time and fourth-order-in-space regularity.

Hence, to conclude we need to prove that the integro-differential variational problem (

) may have one solution at most. For this, let
z_{1},z_{2}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}))
, such that
\displaystyle\frac{\partial z_{1}}{\partial t},\frac{\partial z_{2}}{\partial t}\in L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})

). By defining the mapping

\displaystyle h:\

\displaystyle[0,T]\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle t\mapsto\int^{t}_{0}(\mathcal{L}^{\dot{a},\dot{b}}_{s})^{*}(z_{1}-z_{2})(s,\cdot)\;\mathrm{d}s,

it is readily checked that
h\in L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
, in particular satisfying

\displaystyle\int^{t}_{0}\|h(s,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}s\leq

\displaystyle\hskip 21.52771pt\leq C\big(\alpha,\|({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a-\alpha I},b)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{m}}\big)\int^{t}_{0}\left(\int^{s}_{0}\|(z_{1}-z_{2})(r,\cdot)\|^{2}_{H^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}r\right)\;\mathrm{d}s,\quad t\in[0,T],

(50)

and that

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}((z_{1}-z_{2})(t,\cdot),\varphi)_{L^{2}}+A^{\alpha,a,b}(t;(z_{1}-z_{2})(t,\cdot),\varphi)=\\[5.0pt]
\hskip 193.74939pt=(h(t,\cdot),\varphi)_{L^{2}},\quad\varphi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}),\\
(z_{1}-z_{2})(0,\cdot)=0.\end{cases}

Therefore, we are in the setting of Theorem

and of our previous computations. In particular, we may combine (
A.2

,

\displaystyle\int^{t}_{0}\|(z_{1}-z_{2})(s,\cdot)\|^{2}_{H^{2}}\;

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\alpha,\|({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a-\alpha I},b)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{m}}\big)\int^{t}_{0}\left(\int^{s}_{0}\|(z_{1}-z_{2})(r,\cdot)\|^{2}_{H^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}r\right)\;\mathrm{d}s,

and a routine application of Gronwäll’s inequality allows us to conclude that

4) Conclusion.
Given Theorem
2
, the regularity of the initial condition
\bar{p}\in H^{2m+1}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
, and the choice of the basis
(v_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq H^{2(m+1)}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
, one may easily iterate the previous computations by induction to define successive time derivatives of
p
via iteratively considering formal differentiation-in-time of the variational problem (
47
). In particular, it is clear how to extend properties (
48
) and define updated integro-differential variational problems (
49
) by induction, which, thanks to iteratively higher order elliptic regularity arguments applied to (
42
)–(
43
) and the elliptic estimate (
A.2
), provide that
\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{\ell}p}{\partial t^{\ell}}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{2(m+1-\ell)}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}))
, for
\ell=1,\dots,m+1
, and
p\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{2(m+1)}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}))
. The conclusion follows.
