Stability of Homomorphisms, Coverings and Cocycles I: Equivalence
Michael Chapman, Alexander Lubotzky

Abstract
. This paper is motivated by recent developments in group stability, high dimensional expansion, local testability of error correcting codes and topological property testing. In Part I, we formulate and motivate three stability problems: • Homomorphism stability : Are almost homomorphisms close to homomorphisms? • Covering stability : Are almost coverings of a cell complex close to genuine coverings of it? • Cocycle stability : Are 1 1 -cochains whose coboundary is small close to 1 1 -cocycles? We then prove that these three problems are equivalent . In Part II of this paper [ 21 ] , we present examples of stable (and unstable) complexes, discuss various applications of our new perspective, and provide a plethora of open problems and further research directions. In another companion paper [ 22 ] , we study the stability rates of random simplicial complexes in the Linial–Meshulam model.

Covering stability

Recall that a
polygonal complex
is a
2
-dimensional cell complex whose
2
-cells are polygons. The most popular example is that of

A combinatorial map

\mathcal{Y}

\mathcal{X}
in an incident preserving manner. Hence, by defining the map on

Assume

\pi
to a path

in

which originates at

and satisfies

. Even if the original path

was closed, the lifted path

may be either open or closed. Now,

is a genuine covering if and only if for every polygon
\pi
in
\mathcal{X}
and every
x^{\prime}\in f^{-1}(x)
the lifted path

is closed. Hence, we can define the

{\rm def}_{cover}(f)=\Pro_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in\overrightarrow{P}(\mathcal{X})\\
x^{\prime}\in f^{-1}(x)\end{subarray}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{The\ lift\ of}\ \pi\ \textrm{to\ }x^{\prime}\textrm{\ is\ open}}}\right],

where

is the set of oriented polygons in
\mathcal{X}
.

There is a natural measure of distance between graphs, which is called the (normalized)

4.1

with a fixed combinatorial map into
\mathcal{X}
. Thus, the

in the category of
\mathcal{X}
-labeled graphs. The complex

is

-covering stable

{\rm Def}_{cover}(f)\leq\rho({\rm def}_{cover}(f))

Cocycle stability

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a polygonal complex. An anti-symmetric map from

We already defined in (

Property testing

The goal of a property tester is to decide whether a given function satisfies a specific property by querying it at a few random evaluation points. Such a tester cannot decide with certainty that the function satisfies the property, since it may require reading all the values of the function. Hence, a property tester is good given that it satisfies the following: As the rejection probability of the test gets smaller, the closer the function in hand is to another function which has the property. We now make this paragraph precise. For a thorough introduction to the theory, see
[
38
]
.

Let
{\mathfrak{D}}
and
{\mathfrak{R}}
be collections of
finite
sets. Let
{\mathfrak{F}}
be the collection of functions whose domain is from
{\mathfrak{D}}
and range is from
{\mathfrak{R}}
, namely

{\mathfrak{F}}=\{f\colon D\to R\mid D\in{\mathfrak{D}},R\in{\mathfrak{R}}\}.

Let

and
f_{2}\colon D_{2}\to R_{2}
be two functions from
{\mathfrak{F}}

(2.1)

d_{H}(f_{1},f_{2})=|D_{2}\setminus D_{1}|+|D_{1}\setminus D_{2}|+|\{x\in D_{1}\cap D_{2}\mid f_{1}(x)\neq f_{2}(x)\}|.

When

and

d_{H}(f_{1},f_{2})=|\{x\in D_{1}\cup D_{2}\mid f_{1}(x)\neq f_{2}(x)\}|,

which is the usual way the Hamming distance is defined.
Though the Hamming distance with errors is a good metric, it outputs natural numbers and cannot tend to zero without being zero. Hence, we usually normalize it in some way. The most common normalization is the following, which we call the
normalized Hamming distance with errors

(2.2)

d_{h}(f_{1},f_{2})=\frac{d_{H}(f_{1},f_{2})}{|D_{1}\cup D_{2}|}=\Pro_{x\in D_{1}\cup D_{2}}[f_{1}(x)\neq f_{2}(x)],

where

is the uniform probability on
A

Homomorphism stability of group presentations

In 1940, Ulam raised the following problem:

Problem 3.1

(Ulam’s homomorphism stability problem
[
66
]
)
.

Given an almost homomorphism between two groups, is it close to a genuine homomorphism between them?

There are many (non-equivalent) ways to formulate Ulam’s problem (cf.
[
48
,
19
,
36
,
39
,
12
,
24
,
9
]
). In this section we study a finite group presentations variant of it.
8
8

8

Our notion of homomorphism stability is usually referred to in the literature as
pointwise flexible stability in permutations
(see
[
13
]
).

For a positive integer
n
, let
{\rm Sym}(n)
be the symmetric group acting on
[n]=\{1,...,n\}
. Given permutations
\sigma\in{\rm Sym}(n)
and
\tau\in{\rm Sym}(N)
where
N\geq n
, the normalized Hamming distance with errors between them (as defined in (

)) is

(3.1)

d_{h}(\sigma,\tau)=1-\frac{|\{i\in[n]\mid\sigma(i)=\tau(i)\}|}{N}=\Pro_{i\in[N]}[\sigma(i)\neq\tau(i)].

Let
\Gamma\cong\langle S|R\rangle
be a finite group presentation, and
\mathcal{F}(S)
the free group with basis
S
. Let
f\colon S\to{\rm Sym}(n)
be a function. By the universal property of free groups,
f
has a unique extension to a homomorphism from
\mathcal{F}(S)
to
{\rm Sym}(n)
, which we denote by
f
as well. This
f
factors through
\Gamma
if and only if for every
r\in R
we have
f(r)={\rm Id}.

Hence, the collection of functions
f\colon S\to{\rm Sym}(n)
that induce a homomorphism from
\Gamma
is

\textrm{Hom}(\Gamma,{\rm Sym})=\{f\colon S\to{\rm Sym}(n)\mid n\in\mathbb{N},\ \forall r\in R\colon f(r)={\rm Id}\}.

This suggests the following tester for deciding whether
f\colon S\to{\rm Sym}(n)
induces a homomorphism from
\Gamma
:

Algorithm 1

Homomorphism tester

Input:

f\colon S\to{\rm Sym}(n)
.

Output:
Accept or Reject.

1:

Pick
r\in R
unifromly at random.

2:

Pick
i\in[n]
uniformly at random.

3:

If
f(r).i=i
, then return Accept.

4:

Otherwise, return Reject.

Remark 3.2
.

Instead of sampling

3.1

7

(See Section

,

To fit Algorithm
1
to the setup of Section
2
, we need the input function

to be in the form

with the extra condition that

\begin{split}&{\mathfrak{F}}=\{f\colon S\times[n]\to[n]\mid n\in\mathbb{N},\forall s\in S\colon f(s,\cdot)\in{\rm Sym}(n)\},\\
&{\mathfrak{P}}=\textrm{Hom}(\Gamma,{\rm Sym}),\\
&\forall f_{1},f_{2}\in{\mathfrak{F}}\ \colon\ \ d_{h}(f_{1},f_{2})=\Ex_{s\in S}[d_{h}(f_{1}(s,\cdot),f_{2}(s,\cdot))].\end{split}

Hence, the
homomorphism local defect
of

, which is the rejection probability in the above test, is

{\rm def}_{hom}(f)=\Ex_{r\in R}[d_{h}(f(r),{\rm Id})].

Moreover, the

of

{\rm Def}_{hom}(f)=d_{h}(f,\textrm{Hom}(\Gamma,{\rm Sym}))=\inf\{d_{h}(f,\varphi)\mid\varphi\in\textrm{Hom}(\Gamma,{\rm Sym})\}.

Definition 3.3

Let

3.2.
Group soficity

In this section, we relate homomorphism stability to the well studied notion of

Fact 3.4

(Glebsky-Rivera

Let

Hence, we say that a group

Definition 3.5
.

A finitely presented group

and for every

13

13

\langle\langle R\rangle\rangle
is the normal subgroup generated by

If

Soficity is a

Though more sophisticated methods for constructing non-sofic groups were recently suggested (cf.

Covering stability of polygonal complexes

4.1.
Graphs a la Bass–Serre

Definition 4.1
.

A graph (a la Bass–Serre, see
[
63

)
\mathcal{X}
consists of the following data:

(1)

A set
V=V(\mathcal{X})
of vertices.

(2)

A set
\overrightarrow{E}=\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})
of directed edges, together with functions
\tau,\iota\colon\overrightarrow{E}\to V
and an involution
\overline{\ \ {}^{\ {}^{\ }}}\colon\overrightarrow{E}\to\overrightarrow{E}
satisfying

\forall e\in\overrightarrow{E}\ \colon\ \ \tau(\overline{e})=\iota(e).

The function
\tau
is the terminal point (or end point) function, the function
\iota
is the initial point (or origin point) function and
\overline{\ \ {}^{\ {}^{\ }}}
is the reverse edge function. Namely, an edge
e
can be visualized as

, where
x=\iota(e)
and
y=\tau(e)
. Moreover, we have
y\xrightarrow{\bar{e}}x
.

Remark 4.2
.

Note the following:

•

A graph (a la Bass-Serre) is essentially an undirected multi-graph. To make this statement precise, define
[e]=\{e,\bar{e}\}
and
E(\mathcal{X})=\{[e]\mid e\in\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})\}
. Then
(V(\mathcal{X}),E(\mathcal{X}))
is a multi-graph where the endopints of
[e]\in E(\mathcal{X})
are
\iota(e)
and
\tau(e)
.

Furthermore, multi-graphs can be viewed as
1

[

to be the

to be the

-dimensional cells, and with gluing maps induced by
\tau
and
\iota

•

We may abuse notation and write

, though there may be several edges with the exact same origin and endpoint.

Definition 4.3
.

A

\pi

is a sequence of edges

such that each
e_{i}\in\overrightarrow{E}
and for every

\tau(e_{i})=\iota(e_{i+1})
. By denoting
x_{i}:=\tau(e_{i})\in V

x_{0}\xrightarrow{e_{1}}x_{1}\xrightarrow{e_{2}}...\xrightarrow{e_{\ell}}x_{\ell}.

The integer
\ell(\pi)=\ell
is the

of the path.
The path
\pi
is

(or reduced) if
e_{i+1}\neq\bar{e}_{i}
for every

. For a path
\pi
, its

or

is

\bar{\pi}=x_{\ell}\xrightarrow{\bar{e}_{\ell}}...\xrightarrow{\bar{e}_{1}}x_{0}.

The path
\pi
is

if
x_{0}=x_{\ell}
, and
open
otherwise. When
\pi
is closed, it has

e_{k}...e_{\ell}e_{1}...e_{k-1}
and

. We call all these shifts and inverses the

of

. The path

Definition 4.4

(Combinatorial maps between graphs)
.

A
combinatorial map

between two graphs is a function that maps vertices of
\mathcal{Y}
to vertices of
\mathcal{X}
and edges of
\mathcal{Y}
to edges of
\mathcal{X}
, and is compatible with the structural data of the graph. Namely, it preserves initial points, terminal points and edge flips:

\forall e\in\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{Y})\ \colon\ \ \tau_{\mathcal{X}}(f(e))=f(\tau_{\mathcal{Y}}(e)),\ \iota_{\mathcal{X}}(f(e))=f(\iota_{\mathcal{Y}}(e)),\ f(\overline{e})=\overline{f(e)}.

Remark 4.5
.

Given a combinatorial map

, we can extend it to paths in
\mathcal{Y}
. Namely, if
\pi=e_{1}...e_{\ell}
is a path in
\mathcal{Y}
, then

is a path in
\mathcal{X}
. Moreover, combinatorial maps induce a continuous map between the graphs as topological spaces.

Recall that a continuous function

between two topological spaces is a (topological)

if for every point

there is an open neighborhood

is a disjoint union of open sets, each of which homeomorphic to

is (path) connected, then the

Definition 4.6

A combinatorial map

Remark 4.9
.

There is an encoding of graphs for which the edit distance (

5.1.
Non-commutative cohomology

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a polygonal complex. Recall our notation
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{X}}(i)
for the oriented
i
-cells of
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{X}(i)
for the unoriented versions, which were defined in (
4.3
) and (
4.4
). Moreover, for
i=1
or
2
, recall that
\bar{c}
is the reverse orientation of the cell
c\in\overrightarrow{\mathcal{X}}(i)
. Let
\Gamma
be a non trivial group and
d\colon\Gamma\times\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
a bi-invariant metric on
\Gamma
.

The
i
-cochains of
\mathcal{X}
with
\Gamma
coefficients
are the anti-symmetric assignments of elements of
\Gamma
to oriented
i
-cells. Namely,

C^{i}(\mathcal{X},\Gamma)=\{\alpha\colon\overrightarrow{\mathcal{X}}(i)\to\Gamma\mid\forall c\in\overrightarrow{\mathcal{X}}(i)\colon\alpha(\bar{c})=\alpha(c)^{-1}\}.

Since we did not define orientations on vertices, there are no anti-symmetricity conditions on
0
-cochains. Also, for
every
2
-cochain
\alpha\colon\overrightarrow{P}(\mathcal{X})\to\Gamma
and an orientation
\pi
of a polygon
[\pi]
, we assume that
\alpha(\pi)
is conjugate to
\alpha(\pi^{\prime})
, where
\pi^{\prime}
is a shift of
\pi
.

The
coboundary maps
are defined as follows. For a
0
-cochain
\alpha\colon V(\mathcal{X})\to\Gamma
, its coboundary
\delta\alpha
is the
1
-cochain

\forall x\xrightarrow{e}y\in\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})\ \colon\ \ \delta\alpha(e)=\alpha(x)^{-1}\alpha(y).

For a
1
-cochain
\alpha\colon\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})\to\Gamma
, its coboundary
\delta\alpha
is the
2
-cochain

\forall\pi=e_{1}...e_{\ell}\in\overrightarrow{P}(\mathcal{X})\ \colon\ \ \delta\alpha(\pi)=\alpha(e_{1})...\alpha(e_{\ell}).

Remark 5.1
.

Every map from
\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})
to a group
\Gamma
can be extended to paths in
G(\mathcal{X})
in the natural way: For
\pi=x_{0}\xrightarrow{e_{1}}...\xrightarrow{e_{\ell}}x_{\ell}
, we let
\alpha(\pi)=\alpha(e_{1})...\alpha(e_{\ell})
. Note that
\delta\alpha(\pi)
is exactly
\alpha(\pi)
in the extended sense, but restricted to the polygons of
\mathcal{X}
.

Next, we prove
exactness
, namely, that for
\alpha\in C^{0}(\mathcal{X},\Gamma)
,
\delta^{2}\alpha
is the constant identity function. For a polygon
\pi=x_{0}\xrightarrow{e_{1}}x_{1}\xrightarrow{e_{2}}...\xrightarrow{e_{\ell}}x_{\ell}=x_{0}
, we have

\delta^{2}\alpha(\pi)=\delta\alpha(e_{1})...\delta\alpha(e_{\ell})=\alpha(x_{0})^{-1}\alpha(x_{1})\alpha(x_{1})^{-1}\alpha(x_{2})...\alpha(x_{\ell-1})^{-1}\alpha(x_{\ell})=\alpha(x_{0})^{-1}\alpha(x_{\ell})={\rm Id}.

For any two
i
-cochains with
\Gamma
coefficients
\alpha
and
\beta
, the
distance
between them is

(5.1)

d(\alpha,\beta)=\Ex_{[x]\in\mathcal{X}(i)}\Ex_{x\in[x]}[d(\alpha(x),\beta(x))].

The
norm
of an
i
-cochain with
\Gamma
coefficients
\alpha
is its distance to the constant identity cochain, namely

(5.2)

\lVert\alpha\rVert=\Ex_{[x]\in\mathcal{X}(i)}\Ex_{x\in[x]}[d(\alpha(x),{\rm Id})].

An
i
-cocycle
is an
i
-cochain
\alpha
for which
\delta\alpha
is the constant identity function. We denote the collection of
i
-cocycles by

.
A
0
-cochain

is said to be a
0
-coboundary
if it is constant. Namely, for every

we have
\beta(x)=\beta(y)
. A

-cochain

is said to be a
1
-coboundary
if it is in the image of the coboundary operator
\delta\colon C^{0}(\mathcal{X},\Gamma)\to C^{1}(\mathcal{X},\Gamma)
.
Namely, there exists a
0
-cochain
\beta\colon V(\mathcal{X})\to\Gamma
such that for every

,
\alpha(xy)=\delta\beta(xy)=\beta(x)^{-1}\beta(y)
. We denote by
B^{i}(\mathcal{X},\Gamma)
the collection of
i
-coboundaries of
\mathcal{X}
. Note that, without assuming further assumptions on
\Gamma
, the only indices for which we define
Z^{i}(\mathcal{X},\Gamma)
and
B^{i}(\mathcal{X},\Gamma)
are
i=0\ \textrm{or}\ 1
.
We say that the
i^{\rm th}
cohomology of
\mathcal{X}
with
\Gamma

if
every
i
-cocycle of

is an

-coboundary.

5.3.
Cocycle stability in the
0^{\rm th}
dimension

Cocycle stability in the
0^{\rm th}
dimension depends only on the underlying graph of the complex. For every finite graph
\mathcal{X}
there is an associated adjacency matrix
M_{\mathcal{X}}\colon V(\mathcal{X})\times V(\mathcal{X})\to\mathbb{C}
that satisfies

(5.3)

M_{\mathcal{X}}(x,y)=|\{e\in\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})\mid\iota(e)=x,\tau(e)=y\}|.

Because of the reverse edge function, this matrix is symmetric. Assuming the graph is
k
-regular, the eigenvalues of this matrix are

k=\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq...\geq\lambda_{|V(\mathcal{X})|}\geq-k.

The normalized spectral gap of a
k
-regular graph
\mathcal{X}
is

\gamma(\mathcal{X})=\frac{k-\lambda_{2}}{k}.

Lemma 5.4

(
L^{2}
-Poincare-type inequality)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a finite regular graph. Let
\mathbb{H}
be a Hilbert space. Let
f\colon V(\mathcal{X})\to\mathbb{H}
be any function. Then

\Ex_{xy\in\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})}[\|f(x)-f(y)\|^{2}]\geq\gamma(\mathcal{X})\cdot\Ex_{x,y\in V(\mathcal{X})}[\|f(x)-f(y)\|^{2}].

Proof.

See Theorem 13.9 in
[
44
]
for a proof sketch, and Section 1.3 in
[
25
]
for a complete proof.
∎

Proposition 5.5
.

Every finite connected regular graph
\mathcal{X}
is
\rho
-cocycle stable in the
0^{\rm th}
dimension, with

\rho(\varepsilon)=\frac{\varepsilon}{\gamma(\mathcal{X})}.

Proof.

First of all, since
\mathcal{X}
is connected, all
0
-cocycles are
0
-coboundaries, namely constant functions. This is because
{\rm Id}=\delta\alpha(xy)=\alpha(x)^{-1}\alpha(y)
implies
\alpha(x)=\alpha(y)
along every edge.

Now, let
\alpha\colon V(\mathcal{X})\to{\rm Sym}(n)
be a
0
-cochain. The group
{\rm Sym}(n)
embeds naturally into
U(n)
, the
n\times n
unitary matrices, as permutation matrices. Moreover, for two permutations
\sigma,\tau\in{\rm Sym}(n)
, we have

d_{h}(\sigma,\tau)=\frac{1}{2}\|\sigma-\tau\|_{HS}^{2},

where on the right hand side we think of
\sigma
and
\tau
as the associated permutation matrices, and

(5.4)

\forall A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\ \colon\ \ \|A\|_{HS}^{2}=\frac{\textrm{tr}(A^{*}A)}{n}=\frac{\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}|A_{ij}|^{2}}{n}

is the normalized Hilbert–Schmidt norm, which is induced by an inner product on
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
that makes it a Hilbert space.
Hence, by Lemma
5.4
,

\begin{split}\|\delta\alpha\|&=\Ex_{xy\in\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})}[d_{h}(\delta\alpha(xy),{\rm Id})]\\
&=\Ex_{xy\in\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})}[d_{h}(\alpha(x)^{-1}\alpha(y),{\rm Id})]\\
&=\Ex_{xy\in\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})}[d_{h}(\alpha(y),\alpha(x)]\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\Ex_{xy\in\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})}[\|\alpha(y)-\alpha(x)\|_{HS}^{2}]\\
&\geq\frac{\gamma(\mathcal{X})}{2}\Ex_{x,y\in V(\mathcal{X})}[\|\alpha(y)-\alpha(x)\|_{HS}^{2}]\\
&=\gamma(\mathcal{X})\Ex_{x,y\in V(\mathcal{X})}[d_{h}(\alpha(y),\alpha(x))]\\
&\geq\gamma(\mathcal{X})\min_{x\in V(\mathcal{X})}\underbrace{\Ex_{y\in V(\mathcal{X})}[d_{h}(\alpha(y),\alpha(x))]}_{d_{h}(\alpha,\alpha(x))}\\
&\geq\gamma(\mathcal{X})d_{h}(\alpha,Z^{0}(\mathcal{X},{\rm Sym})),\end{split}

proving the claim.
∎

5.4.
Cheeger constants and (high dimensional) expansion

As was probably evident to an observant reader, Proposition
5.5
is a type of
Cheeger inequality
. To make this statement precise, we need a suitable notion of a Cheeger constant.

Definition 5.6
.

The
i^{\rm th}

cocycle Cheeger constant
of a complex
\mathcal{X}
with
\Gamma
coefficients, where
i=0
or
1
, is

(5.5)

h_{i}(\mathcal{X},\Gamma)=\inf\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{\|\delta\alpha\|}{d(\alpha,Z^{i}(\mathcal{X},\Gamma))}\ \middle|\ {\alpha\in C^{i}(\mathcal{X},\Gamma)},\ \|\delta\alpha\|\neq 0}}\right\}.

Similarly, the
i^{\rm th}

coboundary Cheeger constant
of a complex
\mathcal{X}
with
\Gamma
coefficients is

(5.6)

h^{B}_{i}(\mathcal{X},\Gamma)=\inf\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{\|\delta\alpha\|}{d(\alpha,B^{i}(\mathcal{X},\Gamma))}\ \middle|\ {\alpha\in C^{i}(\mathcal{X},\Gamma)}\setminus B^{i}(\mathcal{X},\Gamma)}}\right\}.

When
h_{i}(\mathcal{X},{\rm Sym})>0
we call
\mathcal{X}
an
i
-cocycle expander with
\Gamma
coefficients
, and when
h^{B}_{i}(\mathcal{X},{\rm Sym})>0
we call it an
i
-coboundary expander with
\Gamma
coefficients
.

When taking permutation coefficients, these notions genralize the classical Cheeger constant for graphs when
i=0
, as explained in Remark
5.7
. Moreover, when
i=1
and
\Gamma=\mathbb{F}_{2}
, these notions are closely related to Gromov’s topological overlapping property
[
40
,
33
]
. As we aim to develop further study of cocycle stability in permutations, we suggest in
[
21
]
a generalization of the search for bounded coboundary expanders.

Remark 5.7
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a finite connected
k
-regular graph. The
classical Cheeger constant
of
\mathcal{X}
is

h(\mathcal{X})=\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{|E(A,\bar{A})|}{\min(|A|,|\bar{A}|)}\ \middle|\ \emptyset\neq A\subsetneq V(\mathcal{X})}}\right\},

where
E(A,\bar{A})
is the set of edges with one endpoint in
A
and the other in its complement
\bar{A}
. Note that if
\alpha\colon V(\mathcal{X})\to\{0,1\}
is the characteristic function of
A
, and we identify
\{0,1\}
with
{\rm Sym}(2)
(as we did in Section
3.1
), then
\frac{|E(A,\bar{A})|}{|E(\mathcal{X})|}=\|\delta\alpha\|
. Moreover, the
0
-cocycles with
{\rm Sym}(2)
coefficients are exactly the characteristic functions of
V(\mathcal{X})
and
\emptyset
, which we denote by
{\bf 1}
and
{\bf 0}
for the moment. Now,
d_{h}(\alpha,{\bf 0})=\|\alpha\|=\frac{|A|}{|V(\mathcal{X})|}
and
d_{h}(\alpha,{\bf 1})=1-\|\alpha\|=\frac{|\bar{A}|}{|V(\mathcal{X})|}
. It can be verified that the closest constant function to
\alpha
is either
{\bf 0}
or
{\bf 1}
, even if we allow to range over all constant functions into
{\rm Sym}(n)
. Hence,
d_{h}(\alpha,Z^{0}(\mathcal{X},{\rm Sym}))=\frac{\min(|A|,|\bar{A}|)}{|V(\mathcal{X})|}
. All in all,

(5.7)

\begin{split}h(\mathcal{X})&=\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{|E(A,\bar{A})|}{\min(|A|,|\bar{A}|)}\ \middle|\ \emptyset\neq A\subsetneq V(\mathcal{X})}}\right\}\\
&=\frac{|E(\mathcal{X})|}{|V(\mathcal{X})|}\cdot\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{\nicefrac{{|E(A,\bar{A})|}}{{|E(\mathcal{X})|}}}{\nicefrac{{\min(|A|,|\bar{A}|)}}{{|V(\mathcal{X})|}}}\ \middle|\ \emptyset\neq A\subsetneq V(\mathcal{X})}}\right\}\\
&=\frac{k}{2}\cdot\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{\|\delta\alpha\|}{d_{h}(\alpha,Z^{0}(\mathcal{X},{\rm Sym}))}\ \middle|\ \alpha\in C^{0}(\mathcal{X},{\rm Sym}(2)),\|\delta\alpha\|\neq 0}}\right\}\\
&=\frac{k}{2}h_{0}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{F}_{2})\\
&\geq\frac{k}{2}h_{0}(\mathcal{X},{\rm Sym}).\end{split}

Namely, the classical Cheeger constant is (up to a factor of
\nicefrac{{k}}{{2}}
) the
0^{\rm th}
cocycle Cheeger constant with
\mathbb{F}_{2}\cong{\rm Sym}(2)
coefficients, which upper bounds the cocycle Cheeger constant with permutation coefficients.
By the proof of Proposition
5.5
,

(5.8)

5.5.
Coboundary expansion

In the beginning of the proof of Proposition
5.5
, we deduced the following:

Fact 5.9
.

The
0^{\rm th}
cohomology of
\mathcal{X}
with

\Gamma
coefficients vanishes if and only if
G(\mathcal{X})
is connected.

Hence, when we restricted the discussion in Proposition
5.5
and Remark
5.7
to the connected graph case, we forced the vanishing of the
0^{\rm th}
cohomology. In that case, as proved in Remark
5.7
, the cocycle Cheeger constant with
\mathbb{F}_{2}
coefficients is the same as the classical Cheeger constant. But, for disconnected graphs they differ, and the natural analogue is the coboundary Cheeger constant. The following is a straightforward observation:

Fact 5.10
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a polygonal complex. Then:

•

If
h^{B}_{i}(\mathcal{X},\Gamma)>0
, then, in particular, the
i^{\rm th}
cohomology of
\mathcal{X}
with
\Gamma
coefficients vanishes.

•

If the
i^{\rm th}
cohomology of
\mathcal{X}
with
\Gamma
coefficients vanishes, then
h^{B}_{i}(\mathcal{X},\Gamma)=h_{i}(\mathcal{X},\Gamma)
.

The first observation in Fact
5.10
(for
\mathbb{F}_{2}
coefficients) was a key ingredient in Linial–Meshulam’s work on cohomology vanishing of random simplicial complexes
[
51
]
. In a companion paper to this one
[
22
]
, we discuss it further, and suggest to study phase transition of the vanishing of the
1^{\rm st}
cohomology with permutation coefficients. But, what does it mean for the
1^{\rm st}
cohomology with permutation coefficients to vanish? The analysis in Section
6
provides a characterization:

Fact 5.11
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a connected polygonal complex. Then, the
1^{\rm st}
cohomology of
\mathcal{X}
with permutation coefficients vanishes if and only if the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{X},*)
has no proper finite index subgroups. Equivalently,
\widehat{\pi_{1}(\mathcal{X},*)}=1
, i.e., the profinite completion of the fundamental group is trivial.

As oppose to the
0^{\rm th}
dimensional case, where vanishing of the
0^{\rm th}
cohomology, namely connectedness, is the most basic property you would expect from an object which presumes to
expand
, this is not the case in higher dimensions. Out of the various suggested high dimensional expansion properties (cf.
[
54
]
), coboundary expansion is considered a very strong notion.
The motivation to study coboundary expansion over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
arised from the notion of topological overlapping
[
40
]
, but it also found applications in property testing
[
47
]
. As mentioned before, we further discuss these notions, as well as topological overlapping, in Part II
[
21
]
and in
[
22
]
, where we will also illustrate further the importance of coboundary expansion with permutation coefficients.

6.1.
Covering stability is
equivalent to cocycle stability

In this (sub) section, we prove Theorem
1.1
. Namely, we show that
\rho
-cocycle stability and
\rho
-covering stability of a polygonal complex
\mathcal{X}
are the same. We actually define a one to one correspondence between
1
-cochains of
\mathcal{X}
with
{\rm Sym}
coefficients (up to some equivalence relation) and coverings of the
1
-skeleton of
\mathcal{X}
(up to isomorphism). Under this correspondence,
1
-cocycles are mapped to genuine coverings. Moreover, the global defects are in correspondence. Lastly, the testers translate to one another under this correspondence, which in turn preserves the local defect. This idea of encoding graph coverings using
1
-cochains is classical (cf.
[
43
,
5
,
4
]
), and was specifically used in Dinur–Meshulam
[
29
]
in a similar way.

To make the claims from the previous paragraph precise, we need the following action of
0
-cochains of
\mathcal{X}
with
{\rm Sym}(n)
coefficients on
1
-cochains of
\mathcal{X}
with
{\rm Sym}(n)
coefficients:

(6.1)

\forall\alpha\colon\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})\to{\rm Sym}(n),\ \beta\colon V(\mathcal{X})\to{\rm Sym}(n)\ \colon\ \ \beta.\alpha(x\xrightarrow{e}y)=\beta(x)^{-1}\alpha(e)\beta(y).

Note that for every polygon
\pi=x\xrightarrow{e_{1}}...\xrightarrow{e_{\ell}}x\in\overrightarrow{P}(\mathcal{X})
, we have
\beta.\alpha(\pi)=\beta(x)^{-1}\alpha(\pi)\beta(x)
. In particular,
\|\delta(\beta.\alpha)\|=\|\delta\alpha\|
, and hence the action preserves
1
-cocycles.

Claim 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a polygonal complex. There is a one to one correspondence between coverings of
G(\mathcal{X})
and orbits of
1
-cochains of
\mathcal{X}
with permutation coefficients under the action described in (
6.1
).

Proof.

We first associate with every
1
-cochain
\alpha\colon\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})\to{\rm Sym}(n)
a corresponding covering
f\colon\mathcal{G}\to G(\mathcal{X})
as follows:

\begin{split}&V(\mathcal{G})=V(\mathcal{X})\times[n]\quad,\quad\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{G})=\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})\times[n];\\
&\forall x\xrightarrow{e}y\in\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X}),i\in[n]\ \colon\ \ \tau(e,i)=(y,i),\ \iota(e,i)=(x,\alpha(e).i),\ \overline{(e,i)}=(\bar{e},\alpha(e).i);\\
&\forall x\in V(\mathcal{X}),e\in\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X}),i\in[n]\ \colon\ \ f(x,i)=x,\quad f(e,i)=e.\end{split}

By Fact
4.8
,
f
is a covering.
21
21

21

Note that the permutation
\alpha(e)
tells us how the fiber over the terminal point
y
is mapped to the fiber over the origin point
x
and not the other way around. This is because of our choice of left actions.

On the other hand, if

is a degree
n
covering, then one can construct a
1
-cochain
\alpha\colon\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})\to{\rm Sym}(n)
as follows:
For every

,
|f^{-1}(x)|=n

by

n!
ways of choosing these labels. Now, for every

and

define

if
\tau(e^{\prime})=(y,i)
. Here we did not have any choice in the labeling. Now, define

to be the second coordinate of
\iota(e,i)
. Note that a different choice of labeling for the fibers

would give rise to another

∎

The encoding in Claim
6.1
allows us to analyze the lifted path
\pi^{\prime}
used in Algorithm
3
. Recall that for every path
x\xrightarrow{e_{1}}...\xrightarrow{e_{\ell}}y
in
\mathcal{X}
and every

, there exists a unique path
\pi^{\prime}

that starts at

. By choosing a

\begin{split}\pi^{\prime}=&(x,i)\xrightarrow{(e_{1},\alpha(\bar{e}_{1}).i)}(x_{1},\alpha(\bar{e}_{1}).i)\xrightarrow{(e_{2},\alpha(\bar{e}_{2})\circ\alpha(\bar{e}_{1}).i)}(x_{2},\alpha(\bar{e}_{2})\circ\alpha(\bar{e}_{1}).i)\to\dots\\

Note that

Corollary 6.2
.

Claim 6.3
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a polygonal complex. The

Proof.

Let
\alpha\colon\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})\to{\rm Sym}(n)

1

-labeled subgraph

Its edges are

Therefore, we found a

|\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{A})|=|\{(e,i)\mid e\in\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X}),\ i\in[N],\ \alpha(e).i=\beta(e).i\}|=|\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})|\cdot N\cdot d_{h}(\alpha,\beta).

Hence, recalling the definition of the normalized edit distance (

),

(6.2)

d_{edit}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{Y})\leq 1-\frac{|\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{A})|}{|\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{Y})|}=d_{h}(\alpha,\beta).

On the other hand, let
f\colon\mathcal{G}\to G(\mathcal{X})
be an
n
-covering and
\varphi\colon\mathcal{Y}\to G(\mathcal{X})
be an
N
-covering, where
N\geq n
. If
a\colon\mathcal{A}\to G(\mathcal{X})
is the largest

-labeled graph that embeds in both, then we can think of

. For every

then we can label its vertices as

\{(x,i)\}_{i=k_{x}+1}^{N}

into

. By this choice of labeling, we get

. But
\mathcal{A}
was the largest
G(\mathcal{X})
-labeled embedding into
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, which implies
\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{A})=\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{G})\cap\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{Y})

(6.3)

6.2.
Homomorphism stability is equivalent to cocycle stability

In this section we prove Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
.
Like in the previous section, there is a classical correspondence between presentations and polygonal complexes. One direction, from presentations to complexes, is via the
presentation complex
of the group (cf. Corollary 1.28, page 52, in
[
43
]
).
The other direction, from complexes to presentaions, is via the presentation of the fundamental group associated with the reatraction of a spanning tree. We now recall these constructions in detail.

Definition 6.4
.

Given a presentation
\Gamma=\langle S|R\rangle
, one can define the presentation complex
\mathcal{X}_{\langle S|R\rangle}
to be the following polygonal complex: It has a single vertex
*
, and an edge
e(s)
for every generator
s\in S
. Then, for every
r=s_{1}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\cdot...\cdot s_{\ell}^{\varepsilon_{\ell}}\in R
, we add a polygon (together with all its orientations)
\pi(r)=e(s_{1})^{\varepsilon_{1}}...e(s_{\ell})^{\varepsilon_{\ell}}
, where
e(s)^{-1}=\overline{e(s)}
and
e(s)^{1}=e(s)
. Note that since there is only one vertex, this is a closed path regardless of
r
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Every function
f\colon S\to{\rm Sym}(n)
can be extended to a
1
-cochain
\alpha_{f}
on
\mathcal{X}_{\langle S|R\rangle}
in an antisymmetric way. Namely,
\alpha_{f}(e(s))=f(s),\alpha_{f}(\overline{e(s)})=f(s)^{-1}
. Furthermore, for
r=s_{1}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\cdot...\cdot s_{\ell}^{\varepsilon_{\ell}}\in R
we get

\delta\alpha_{f}(\pi(r))=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{f}(e(s_{i}))^{\varepsilon_{i}}=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}f(s_{i})^{\varepsilon_{i}}=f(r).

Hence

\begin{split}{\rm def}_{cocyc}(\alpha_{f})&=\Ex_{[\pi]\in P(\mathcal{X}_{\langle S|R\rangle})}[d_{h}(\delta\alpha_{f}(\pi),{\rm Id})]\\
&=\Ex_{r\in R}[d_{h}(\delta\alpha_{f}(\pi(r)),{\rm Id})]\\
&=\Ex_{r\in R}[d_{h}(f(r),{\rm Id})]\\
&={\rm def}_{hom}(f).\end{split}

In particular, this correspondence
f\leftrightarrow\alpha_{f}
associates the homomorphisms
\textrm{Hom}(\Gamma,{\rm Sym})
with the cocycles
Z^{1}(\mathcal{X}_{\langle S|R\rangle},{\rm Sym})
.

Let
\varphi\colon S\to{\rm Sym}(N)
be another function, and let
\alpha_{\varphi}
be the associated
1
-cochain on
\mathcal{X}_{\langle S|R\rangle}
. Then,

\begin{split}d_{h}(\alpha_{f},\alpha_{\varphi})&=\Ex_{e\in\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X}_{\langle S|R\rangle})}[d_{h}(\alpha_{f}(e),\alpha_{\varphi}(e))]\\
&=\Ex_{s\in S}[d_{h}(\alpha_{f}(e(s)),\alpha_{\varphi}(e(s)))]\\
&=\Ex_{s\in S}[d_{h}(f(s),\varphi(s))]\\
&=d_{h}(f,\varphi).\end{split}

Therefore, the global defects are preserved.
∎

We now recall the presentation of the fundamental group associated with the retraction of a tree. This presentation can be attained by van Kampen’s Theorem (See Chapter 1.2 in
[
43
]
).

Definition 6.5
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a connected polygonal complex. Choose a base point
*\in V(\mathcal{X})
and a spanning tree

in

. There is a standard presentation
\langle S|R\rangle
of the fundamental group

with respect to

.

S

which is outside of the spanning tree

\pi

1.3
.

Let
\alpha\colon\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})\to{\rm Sym}(n)

\mathcal{X}
. We now define a

-cochain

and

\begin{split}\beta.\alpha(e)&=\beta(y)^{-1}\alpha(e)\beta(z)\\

But, the global defects may be different. If

\begin{split}d_{h}(f,\alpha|_{S})&=\Ex_{e\notin T}[d_{h}(f(e),\alpha(e))]\\

For clause
(2)

Let

and

\begin{split}d_{h}(\alpha|_{S}(e),\beta^{\prime}.\varphi|_{S}(e))&=d_{h}(\alpha(\pi_{y})^{-1}\alpha(e)\alpha(\pi_{z}),\varphi(\pi_{y})^{-1}\varphi(e)\varphi(\pi_{z}))\\

&=(\diamond).\end{split}

appears more than once. Hence,

\begin{split}(\diamond)\leq\sum_{e\in\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})}d_{h}(\alpha(e),\varphi(e))=|\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})|d_{h}(\alpha,\varphi).\end{split}

Therefore,

\begin{split}d_{h}(\alpha|_{S},\beta^{\prime}.\varphi|_{S})&=\Ex_{e\notin T}[d_{h}(\alpha|_{S}(e),\beta^{\prime}.\varphi|_{S}(e))]\\
&\leq\Ex_{e\notin T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})|d_{h}(\alpha,\varphi)}}\right]\\
&=|\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})|d_{h}(\alpha,\varphi),\end{split}

which finishes the proof.

.

)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be the
complete complex
on
d
vertices. Namely, it has a vertex set
[d]
, an edge set
\{ij\mid i\neq j\in[d]\}
and a triangle set
\{ijk\mid i\neq j\neq k\in[d]\}
. Then
\mathcal{X}
is
\rho
-cocycle stable with
\rho(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon
.

Remark 6.8
.

We now show that the bound in clause
(2)
of Theorem
1.3
is, up to a factor of
12
, tight. Let us consider the complete complex
\mathcal{X}
as in Fact
6.7
. Choose the spanning tree
T
to be the path
1\to 2\to 3\to...\to d
. Recall that
S=\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})\setminus T
, and let
\alpha\colon S\to\mathbb{F}_{2}\cong{\rm Sym}(2)
be the following:

\forall i\xrightarrow{e}j\notin T\ \colon\ \ \alpha(e)=\begin{cases}1&i\leq\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}},j>\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}},\\
1&j\leq\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}},i>\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}},\\
0&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Note that if we extend
\alpha
to be trivial on
T
, it is almost the characteristic function of the cut of
\mathcal{X}
separating
\{1,...,\lfloor\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}\rfloor\}
and the rest of
[d]
, except
\alpha(\lfloor\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}\rfloor\to\lfloor\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}\rfloor+1)=0
and not
1
(since this edge is part of
T
). It can be checked that the only triangles that are violated by
\alpha
are those which contain the edge
\lfloor\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}\rfloor\to\lfloor\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}\rfloor+1
, and there are
d-2
of them. Hence,
{\rm def}_{cocyc}(\alpha)={\rm def}_{hom}(\alpha)=\frac{d-2}{\binom{d}{3}}=\frac{6}{d(d-1)}.

On the other hand, since
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{X},*)=1
, there only homomorphisms from
S
to
{\rm Sym}(n)
are the constant identity ones, and clearly the closest one is the one into
{\rm Sym}(2)\cong\mathbb{F}_{2}
which is the constant
0
function.
Therefore,

{\rm Def}_{hom}(\alpha)=\Ex_{ij\notin T}[\alpha(ij)=1]=\frac{\lfloor\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}\rfloor\lceil\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}\rceil-1}{\binom{d-1}{2}-d+1}\approx\frac{1}{2}.

All in all,

\frac{{\rm Def}_{hom}(\alpha)}{{\rm def}_{hom}(\alpha)}\approx\frac{\frac{1}{2}}{\frac{6}{d(d-1)}}=\frac{|\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})|}{12},

which, together with Fact
6.7
, proves that the cocycle stability rate of
\mathcal{X}
is at least
\frac{|\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})|}{12}
better than the homomorphism stability of the presentation of
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{X},*)
obtained by retracting
T
.

This example is a bit weak, since there are better spanning trees that will yield a better (though, still quite a bad) deterioration parameter. We wonder whether there is a complex such that
all
of its spanning trees produce presentations with homomorphism stability rate deteriorating by
\Omega(|\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})|)
compared to the cocycle stability rate.

7.
Alternative distributions

Since we presented the property testing framework in a very general fashion in Section
2
, it was not natural to discuss various normalization and weighting systems other than the uniform ones. But, for other problems (cf.
[
21
]
), there is a natural way of extending the definitions to include weights.

Given a polygonal complex
\mathcal{X}
, we can provide with it probability distributions
\mu_{i}
on
\mathcal{X}(i)
. Then, the distance between the cochains
\alpha\colon\overrightarrow{\mathcal{X}}(i)\to{\rm Sym}(n)
and
\varphi\colon\overrightarrow{\mathcal{X}}(i)\to{\rm Sym}(N)
would be

(7.1)

d_{h}(\alpha,\varphi)=\Ex_{[x]\sim\mu_{i}}[d_{h}(\alpha(x),\varphi(x))].

Note that (
5.1
) is a special case of (
7.1
) by choosing
\mu_{i}
to be the uniform distributions on the un-oriented cells. Furthermore, the edit distance between
\mathcal{X}
-labeled graphs can be weighted according to
\mu_{1}
. The sampling procedure of a polygon in Algorithms
3
and
6
(with
i=1
) can be taken to be according to
\mu_{2}
.
The correspondence of Theorem
1.1
which was proved in Section
6.1
is still valid in this generalized setup, as long as the weights on the complex
\mathcal{X}
are fixed.

Similarly, one can associate a measure
\mu_{S}
on
S
and
\mu_{R}
on
R
. Then, define the distance between
f\colon S\to{\rm Sym}(n)
and
\varphi\colon S\to{\rm Sym}(N)
in a similar fashion to (
7.1
),

d_{h}(f,\varphi)=\Ex_{s\sim\mu_{S}}[d_{h}(f(s),\varphi(s))].

Furthermore, we can sample the relation in Algorithm
1
according to
\mu_{R}
. In this generalized framework, Theorem
1.2
can be proved as in Section
6.2
by associating with
X_{\langle S|R\rangle}
the measures
\mu_{1}=\mu_{S}
on its edges and
\mu_{2}=\mu_{R}
on its polygons. There is a weighted version of Theorem
1.3
as well, but we omit it from this discussion.

Though this generalized form does not imply any relation between the
\mu_{i}
’s, there is a natural choice which behaves well when perturbations are applied to the studied object. Let us focus on cocycle stability, which we now know is equivalent to the others under the appropriate corresondences of Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
and
1.3
. For an edge
e
and a polygon
\pi=e_{1}...e_{\ell}
, the occurrence number of
e
in
\pi
is

OC(e\prec\pi)=|\{i\in\{1,...,\ell\}\mid[e_{i}]=[e]\}|.

Note that the occurence number is independent of the chosen orientation of the polygon or edge.
Given a distribution
\mu_{2}
on un-oriented polygons of
\mathcal{X}
, let us define the following weighting system on edges

\begin{split}\forall[e]\in E(\mathcal{X})\ \colon\ \ w([e])&=\Ex_{[\pi]\sim\mu_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[OC(e\prec\pi)}}\right].\end{split}

Namely, the weight of
[e]
is the expected number of times it appears in a polygon sampled according to
\mu_{2}
.
Then, let

(7.2)

\begin{split}\forall[e]\in E(\mathcal{X})\ \colon\ \ \mu_{1}([e])&=\frac{w([e])}{\sum_{[e^{\prime}]\in E(\mathcal{X})}w(e^{\prime})}.\end{split}

The reason this weighting system is natural is that it behaves well under small perturbations, as the following claim shows.

Claim 7.1
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a polygonal complex, let
\mu_{2}
be some distribution on its polygons, and let
L
be the maximal length of a polygon in
\mathcal{X}
. Choose
\mu_{1}
to be the distribution from (
7.2
). Let
\alpha\colon\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})\to{\rm Sym}(n)
and
\varphi\colon\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})\to{\rm Sym}(N)
be two
1
-cochains. Then,

d_{h}(\delta\alpha,\delta\varphi)\leq\Ex_{[\pi]\sim\mu_{2}}[\ell(\pi)]\cdot d_{h}(\alpha,\varphi)\leq L\cdot d_{h}(\alpha,\varphi).

In particular, if two cochains are close, then their local defects are also close.

Proof.

Let us calculate,

\begin{split}d_{h}(\delta\alpha,\delta\varphi)&=\Ex_{[e_{1}...e_{\ell}]\sim\mu_{2}}[d_{h}(\delta\alpha(e_{1}...e_{\ell}),\delta\varphi(e_{1}...e_{\ell}))]\\
&=\Ex_{[e_{1}...e_{\ell}]\sim\mu_{2}}[d_{h}(\alpha(e_{1})...\alpha(e_{\ell}),\varphi(e_{1})...\varphi(e_{\ell}))]\\
&\leq\Ex_{[e_{1}...e_{\ell}]\sim\mu_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}d_{h}(\alpha(e_{j}),\varphi(e_{j}))}}\right]\\
&=\Ex_{[\pi]\sim\mu_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{[e]\in E(\mathcal{X})}OC(e\prec\pi)d_{h}(\alpha(e),\varphi(e))}}\right]\\
&=\sum_{[e]\in E(\mathcal{X})}\underbrace{\Ex_{[\pi]\sim\mu_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[OC(e\prec\pi)}}\right]}_{w([e])}d_{h}(\alpha(e),\varphi(e))\\
&=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{[e^{\prime}]\in E(\mathcal{X})}w(e^{\prime})}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{[e]\in E(\mathcal{X})}\mu_{1}([e])d_{h}(\alpha(e),\varphi(e))}}\right)\end{split}

Now, on the one hand,

\sum_{[e]\in E(\mathcal{X})}\mu_{1}([e])d_{h}(\alpha(e),\varphi(e))=\Ex_{[e]\sim\mu_{1}}[d_{h}(\alpha(e),\varphi(e))]=d_{h}(\alpha,\varphi).

On the other hand,

\sum_{[e^{\prime}]\in E(\mathcal{X})}w(e^{\prime})=\Ex_{[\pi]\sim\mu_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{[e^{\prime}]\in E(\mathcal{X})}OC(e\prec\pi)}}\right]=\Ex_{[\pi]\sim\mu_{2}}[\ell(\pi)]\leq L,

where
\ell(\pi)
is the length of the polygon
\pi
, defined in Definition
4.3
. This finishes the proof.
∎
