Abstract
Information-processing tasks modelled by homomorphisms between relational structures can witness quantum advantage when entanglement is used as a computational resource. We prove that the occurrence of quantum advantage is determined by the same type of algebraic structure (known as a minion) that captures the polymorphism identities of CSPs and, thus, CSP complexity. We investigate the connection between the minion of quantum advantage and other known minions controlling CSP tractability and width. In this way, we make use of complexity results from the algebraic theory of CSPs to characterise the occurrence of quantum advantage in the case of graphs, and to obtain new necessary and sufficient conditions in the case of arbitrary relational structures.

Introduction

The
constraint satisfaction problem
(CSP) is a computational paradigm that provides a common framework for studying the complexity of a variety of combinatorial problems. The input consists in a set of variables, a set of possible values for the variables, and a set of constraints between the variables; the goal is to determine whether there exists an assignment of values to the variables that satisfies the constraints. In its full generality, the CSP is NP-complete. Nevertheless, restricting the admissible constraints to a selected class can make the problem solvable in polynomial time. This is the case of linear equations, which can be formulated as a CSP where all constraints are linear relations between the variables.
The theory of CSP complexity aims at explaining the complexity of a CSP over some specific type of constraints in terms of the structure of the constraints.

An elegant way of formalising the CSP is through the notion of homomorphisms between relational structures. A relational structure consists of a set of vertices and a set of relations on the vertices; for example, a graph is a relational structure having a single binary symmetric irreflexive relation. A homomorphism
\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{Y}
between two relational structures
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
is a map between the vertex sets of
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
that preserves all relations, in the sense that the image of a tuple in a relation of
\mathbf{X}
must belong to the corresponding relation of
\mathbf{Y}
. We can then formulate the CSP
parameterised
by
\mathbf{Y}
as the problem of deciding if an arbitrary structure
\mathbf{X}
admits a homomorphism to
\mathbf{Y}
. In this setting, the relations of
\mathbf{X}
encode the tuples of variables that appear in some constraint of the CSP, while the relations of
\mathbf{Y}
encode the types of admissible constraints.

For example, linear equations over a finite field
\mathbb{F}
of
n
elements correspond to
\CSP(\mathbf{Y})
, where the domain of
\mathbf{Y}
is
\{1,\dots,n\}
, and the relations of
\mathbf{Y}
are affine hyperplanes in vector spaces over
\mathbb{F}
. If
\mathbf{Y}
is the
n
-clique
\mathbf{K}_{n}
,
\CSP(\mathbf{Y})
corresponds to another primary example of a CSP — namely, the
graph
n
-colouring
problem, which consists in testing whether the chromatic number of a graph
\mathbf{X}
is at most
n
.
A natural generalisation is the
graph
\mathbf{Y}
-colouring
problem, which corresponds to
\CSP(\mathbf{Y})
for some fixed graph
\mathbf{Y}
.
The complexity of graph
n
-colouring was characterised by Karp
[
48
]
: If
n=2
, the problem is in
\operatorname{P}
; otherwise, it is
{\operatorname{NP}}
-complete. Hell and Nešetřil
[
41
]
extended the dichotomy by proving that graph
\mathbf{Y}
-colouring is in
\operatorname{P}
if
\mathbf{Y}
is bipartite, and it is
{\operatorname{NP}}
-complete otherwise.
In
[
34
]
, Feder and Vardi proposed the conjecture that Karp’s and Hell–Nešetřil’s dichotomies could in fact extend to all CSPs.
Feder–Vardi’s Dichotomy Conjecture inspired a major research programme in the theory of CSPs, that culminated twenty years later with its positive resolution obtained by Bulatov

The advent of quantum computation led to the natural programme of understanding the type of advantage derived by having access to quantum resources — as opposed to classical resources — in computational tasks. In order to formalise this notion of
quantum advantage
for computational tasks consisting in constraint satisfaction problems, it shall be convenient to adopt an alternative description of homomorphisms between relational structures, in terms of winning strategies for non-local games.

For the sake of simplicity, suppose that
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
are two graphs. The
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}
-homomorphism game
, introduced in
[
53
]
, involves two players (Alice and Bob) and a Verifier. The Verifier sends Alice and Bob vertices
x_{A}
and
x_{B}
of
\mathbf{X}
, respectively. Alice and Bob (who cannot communicate during the game, but can agree on a strategy before the game starts) respond by sending the Verifier vertices
y_{A}
and
y_{B}
of
\mathbf{Y}
, respectively. They win the
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}
-homomorphism game if
y_{A}
and
y_{B}
are adjacent (resp. equal) vertices of
\mathbf{Y}
whenever
x_{A}
and
x_{B}
are adjacent (resp. equal) vertices of
\mathbf{X}
. If only resources of classical type are allowed, the existence of a perfect strategy for this game — i.e., a strategy that makes Alice and Bob win with probability
1
— is equivalent to the existence of a homomorphism
\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{Y}
. In fact, a classical perfect strategy simply consists in Alice and Bob sharing a protocol for selecting some homomorphism
f:\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{Y}
, and responding with the vertex
f(x)
whenever they are sent a vertex
x
by the Verifier.

Suppose now that Alice and Bob have access to quantum resources, modelled by an entangled state in the space
\mathbb{H}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{H}_{B}
, where
\mathbb{H}_{A}\simeq\mathbb{H}_{B}
are finite-dimensional (real or complex) Hilbert spaces. This state is shared by Alice and Bob; the separation between the two players corresponds to the fact that Alice (resp. Bob) can only perform measurements on
\mathbb{H}_{A}
(resp.
\mathbb{H}_{B}
). This extra resource allows the players to devise quantum-assisted strategies: Each player performs a positive operator-valued measure of their part of the state, which results in a vertex of
\mathbf{Y}
that is then sent to the Verifier. A
quantum perfect strategy
over the space
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{H}_{A}\simeq\mathbb{H}_{B}
for the
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}
-homomorphism game is a quantum-assisted strategy that makes Alice and Bob win with probability
1
. In this case,
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
are said to be
quantum homomorphic
over
\mathbb{H}
.
The extra correlation between the answers of Alice and Bob produced by the entanglement of the quantum state can result in strategies that are strictly stronger than classical strategies. In other words, it is possible for two graphs
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
to be quantum homomorphic even when they are not homomorphic in the classical sense.
In
[
53
]
, the question of understanding for which graphs
\mathbf{Y}
the notions of quantum and classical homomorphisms differ was posed. More precisely, we say that
\mathbf{Y}

has quantum advantage
if there exists some graph
\mathbf{X}
such that
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
are quantum homomorphic but not classically homomorphic.

The main conceptual contribution of this work is to establish that
the occurrence of quantum advantage is governed by
the same type of algebraic structure that determines the complexity of constraint satisfaction problems
— namely, the algebraic structure known as a
minion
.
We start by discussing the role of minions in CSP complexity.

The mathematical framework that allowed attacking and, eventually, proving Feder-Vardi’s Dichotomy
Conjecture was the so-called
algebraic approach to CSPs
, whose origins can be traced back to
[
46
,
45
,
56
]
. The gist of this approach is that the complexity of a CSP is entirely determined by a type of identities satisfied by its higher-order symmetries, known as
polymorphisms
. A polymorphism of a relational structure
\mathbf{Y}
is a homomorphism
\mathbf{Y}^{\ell}\to\mathbf{Y}
, where
\mathbf{Y}^{\ell}
is the
\ell
-fold direct power of
\mathbf{Y}
. If
\mathbf{Y}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}
are two structures such that the set
\Pol(\mathbf{Y})
of the polymorphisms of
\mathbf{Y}
is included in
\Pol(\mathbf{Y}^{\prime})
— thus meaning that
\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}
has “more symmetries” than
\mathbf{Y}
—
there exists a specific kind of polynomial-time reduction (known as a
gadget reduction
) from
\CSP(\mathbf{Y}^{\prime})
to
\CSP(\mathbf{Y})
(in symbols,
\CSP(\mathbf{Y}^{\prime})\leq_{\operatorname{g}}\CSP(\mathbf{Y})
).
This gives a powerful way of comparing the complexity of different CSPs, by looking at the properties of their polymorphisms.
The implication

\displaystyle\Pol(\mathbf{Y})\subseteq\Pol(\mathbf{Y}^{\prime})\;\;\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;\;\;\CSP(\mathbf{Y}^{\prime})\leq_{\operatorname{g}}\CSP(\mathbf{Y})

can in fact be strengthened: As established in
[
11
]
, in order for a gadget reduction to exist, it is enough for
\Pol(\mathbf{Y}^{\prime})
to satisfy all identities of a certain type (known as
height-1
or
minor
identities) that are satisfied in
\Pol(\mathbf{Y})
. One can then endow
\Pol(\mathbf{Y})
with a particular algebraic structure (known as
minion
or
minor-closed class

[
56
]
) that encodes minor identities. This results in the stronger implication

\displaystyle\Pol(\mathbf{Y})\to\Pol(\mathbf{Y}^{\prime})\;\;\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;\;\;\CSP(\mathbf{Y}^{\prime})\leq_{\operatorname{g}}\CSP(\mathbf{Y}),

where “
\to
” denotes a minor-preserving map (or
minion homomorphism
) between the minions
\Pol(\mathbf{Y})
and
\Pol(\mathbf{Y}^{\prime})
. Furthermore, it was shown in
[
11
]
that the converse of the implication above also holds.
In this sense, the polymorphism minion precisely captures CSP complexity.

Contributions

Our first contribution is
to show that quantum advantage is a property of the polymorphism minion, just like CSP complexity.
To that end,
we construct a minion
{{Q}}_{\mathbb{H}}
having the property that a structure
\mathbf{Y}
has quantum advantage over
\mathbb{H}
if and only if
{{Q}}_{\mathbb{H}}\not\to\Pol(\mathbf{Y})
. This
quantum minion
belongs to a particular class of minions identified in
[
28
]
, known as
linear minions
. Its objects are tuples of orthogonal subspaces of the Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
, while the minors are defined in terms of sums of linear spaces.

Using this connection, the question “
Which structures have quantum advantage?
” can be reformulated as “
Which minions admit a homomorphism from the quantum minion?
”. In other words, the problem of classifying the occurrence of quantum advantage is translated into the problem of locating
{{Q}}_{\mathbb{H}}
within the preorder on the class of minions induced by the minion homomorphism relation. We prove the following three results:

(i)
If
\mathbb{H}
has dimension
1
or
2
,
{{Q}}_{\mathbb{H}}
is equivalent to the so-called
dictator minion

{{D}}
(also known as projection minion). To show this, we make use of the Hopf fibration of the complex projective line
\mathbb{C}\textbf{P}^{1}
into the
2
-sphere
S^{2}
, which has the property of mapping pairs of orthogonal vectors into antipodal points. Then, we consider an antipodal partition of
S^{2}
. In this way, we can select in any element of
{{Q}}_{\mathbb{H}}
a distinguished coordinate — namely, the coordinate whose corresponding subspace is mapped by the Hopf fibration to, say, the first part of the partition. The map assigning to each element of
{{Q}}_{\mathbb{H}}
its distinguished coordinate is then shown to be a minion homomorphism from
{{Q}}_{\mathbb{H}}
to
{{D}}
.

(ii)
If
\mathbb{H}
has dimension at least
3
,
{{Q}}_{\mathbb{H}}
is not equivalent to the dictator minion. Our proof of this fact works by showing that a homomorphism
{{Q}}_{\mathbb{H}}\to{{D}}
would induce a Boolean measure on the subspaces of
\mathbb{H}
. By virtue of Gleason’s Theorem
[
39
]
, any measure
\mu
on the subspaces of
\mathbb{H}
must be of the form
\mu(v)=\tr(m\cdot\pr_{v})
for some positive semidefinite linear operator
m
(where
\pr_{v}
denotes the orthogonal projector onto the space
v
). Thus, in particular,
\mu
must have a continuous dependence on its inputs, which contradicts the fact that
\mu
is Boolean.

(iii)
For any
\mathbb{H}
,
{{Q}}_{\mathbb{H}}
admits a homomorphism to a linear minion
{{S}}
introduced in
[
28
]
and, independently, in
[
15
]
, which captures the power of the
standard semidefinite programming
relaxation for CSPs. The result is obtained by projecting a fixed vector of
\mathbb{H}
onto the spaces consitituing the elements of
{{Q}}_{\mathbb{H}}
. This yields a minion homomorphism from
{{Q}}_{\mathbb{H}}
to a complex version
{{S}}_{\mathbb{C}}
of
{{S}}
; then, we show that
{{S}}_{\mathbb{C}}
and
{{S}}
are in fact equivalent.

The minion-theoretic results on
{{Q}}_{\mathbb{H}}
listed above directly translate into results on the occurrence of quantum advantage. In particular,

(i)
implies that quantum advantage can only happen over Hilbert spaces of dimension
3
or higher — an instance of the known phenomenon that strong non-locality cannot be realised by two-qubit systems, see
[
18
,
1
]
.
For such spaces,
(ii)
and
(iii)
yield new sufficient and necessary conditions for the occurrence of quantum advantage in terms of the complexity of the corresponding CSP:

(ii)
implies that, if
\CSP(\mathbf{Y})
is not decided by a polynomial-time algorithm,
\mathbf{Y}
has quantum advantage;

(iii)
implies that, if
\mathbf{Y}
has quantum advantage,
\CSP(\mathbf{Y})
has
unbounded width
2

2

We also describe an alternative derivation of this result, by showing that
{{Q}}_{\mathbb{H}}
admits a homomorphism to the

introduced in
[

In the case of graphs, the combination of these results completely characterises the occurrence of quantum advantage: Using Hell–Nešetřil’s dichotomy for the complexity of graph CSPs (in Bulatov’s reformulation
[

in terms of polymorphisms), we show that, if
\dim(\mathbb{H})\geq 3

Finally, we introduce a natural generalisation of quantum advantage. A structure
\mathbf{Y}
having quantum advantage means that the presence of a quantum homomorphism from
\mathbf{X}

\mathbf{Y}

We show that our minion framework is also able to capture this more general notion of quantum advantage. As a consequence, we connect quantum advantage for pairs of structures to the complexity of a

In this way, we show that the two notions of quantum advantage do not collapse, in the sense that there exist two structures

does not.

Related work

Understanding the power of entanglement as a resource in non-local games (like the homomorphism game described above) is a well-established research programme in quantum information and theoretical computer science. A prominent example of this line of work is the “

Quantum perfect strategies in the special case of the graph

Boolean CSPs (i.e.,

]
. The relation between this notion of CSP satisfiability and quantum perfect strategies for non-local games was described in

Unlike their non-promise variant, the complexity of promise CSPs remains largely undiscovered: Even the complexity of the promise version of graph

Since the introduction of a minion-based algebraic approach to promise CSPs in

Transferring the geometric techniques we develop in this work for describing the structure of the quantum minion

Finally, we note that the dual version of Question

2.3
Quantum advantage

Given two
\sigma
-structures
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
,
consider a family
\{p_{x,y}\}_{x\in X,\,y\in Y}
of orthogonal projectors in
\Proj_{\mathbb{H}}
. For each
R\in\sigma
,
\mathbf{x}\in X^{\ar(R)}
, and
\mathbf{y}\in Y^{\ar(R)}
, define
p_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}=p_{x_{1},y_{1}}\cdot p_{x_{2},y_{2}}\cdot\ldots\cdot p_{x_{\ar(R)},y_{\ar(R)}}
. Consider the following three conditions:

(Q1)

\sum_{y\in Y}p_{x,y}=\id_{\mathbb{H}}
for each
x\in X
;

(Q2)

[p_{x_{i},y},p_{x_{j},y^{\prime}}]=\mathbx{0}_{\mathbb{H}}
for each
R\in\sigma
,
\mathbf{x}\in R^{\mathbf{X}}
,
i,j\in[\ar(R)]
, and
y,y^{\prime}\in Y
;

(Q3)

p_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}=\mathbx{0}_{\mathbb{H}}
for each
R\in\sigma
,
\mathbf{x}\in R^{\mathbf{X}}
, and
\mathbf{y}\in Y^{\ar(R)}\setminus R^{\mathbf{Y}}
.

Following
[
2
]
, we say that
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
are
quantum homomorphic
over
\mathbb{H}
(and write
\mathbf{X}\to_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbf{Y}
) if there exists a family
\{p_{x,y}\}_{x\in X,\,y\in Y}
having the three properties listed above. It was shown in
[
2
]
that
\mathbf{X}\to_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbf{Y}
if and only if there exists a
quantum perfect strategy
for the
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}
-homomorphism game mentioned in the Introduction. Observe that
\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{Y}
always implies
\mathbf{X}\to_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbf{Y}
.
Indeed, if
f:\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{Y}
is a homomorphism, letting
p_{x,y}=\id_{\mathbb{H}}
if
y=f(x)
and
p_{x,y}=\mathbx{0}_{\mathbb{H}}
otherwise yields a proper quantum homomorphism.
We say that
\mathbf{Y}

has quantum advantage over
\mathbb{H}
if the converse is not true; i.e., if there exists a
\sigma
-structure
\mathbf{X}
such that
\mathbf{X}\to_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbf{Y}
but
\mathbf{X}\not\to\mathbf{Y}
. In terms of the
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}
-homomorphism game, this means that Alice and Bob can win the game using a quantum-assisted strategy, but they cannot using a classical strategy.

5

5

We point out that, if

and

is more restrictive than the one given in

2.4
Minions

A minion
{{M}}
is the disjoint union of non-empty sets
{{M}}^{(\ell)}
for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, equipped with operations
(\cdot)_{/\pi}:{{M}}^{(\ell)}\to{{M}}^{(\ell^{\prime})}
(known as
minor operations
) for each pair of integers
\ell,\ell^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
and each map

\pi:[\ell]\to[\ell^{\prime}]
, which must satisfy the following two conditions:

•

M_{/\id}=M

for each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
M\in{{M}}^{(\ell)}
, where
\id
is the identity map on the set
[\ell]
;

•

(M_{/\pi})_{/\tilde{\pi}}=M_{/\tilde{\pi}\circ\pi}

for each
\ell,\ell^{\prime},\ell^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
,
M\in{{M}}^{(\ell)}
,
\pi:[\ell]\to[\ell^{\prime}]
,
and
\tilde{\pi}:[\ell^{\prime}]\to[\ell^{\prime\prime}]
.
6
6

6

Equivalently, we may define a minion as a functor from the skeleton category of non-empty finite sets to the category of non-empty sets. The definition of minion as we present it here was introduced in
[
16
]
, and it is an abstraction of the
minor-closed classes of functions
considered in
[
56
]
to study a Galois connection between functions and relations. In fact, the latter objects are precisely the function minions of Example
2
.

Given
M\in{{M}}
, the index
\ell
for which
M\in{{M}}^{(\ell)}
is referred to as the
arity
of
M
. A
subminion
of a minion
{{M}}
is a non-empty subset of
{{M}}
that is closed under the minor operations of
{{M}}
.
In this work, we shall consider two concrete types of minions, described in the two examples below.

Example 2

(Function minions)
.

Take two sets
S
and
T
. For any integer
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the set
{F}^{(\ell)}_{S,T}
of all functions from
S^{\ell}
to
T
. Given
f\in{F}^{(\ell)}_{S,T}
and
\pi:[\ell]\to[\ell^{\prime}]
, we let
f_{/\pi}\in{F}^{(\ell^{\prime})}_{S,T}
be the function defined as follows:

\displaystyle f_{/\pi}:S^{\ell^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\to T

\displaystyle(s_{1},\dots,s_{\ell^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\mapsto f(s_{\pi(1)},\dots,s_{\pi(\ell)}).

The set
{F}_{S,T}
consisting in the disjoint union of all sets
{F}^{(\ell)}_{S,T}
for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
is easily seen to be a minion. A
function minion
(over the sets
S
and
T
) is any subminion of
{F}_{S,T}
.

Example 3

(Linear minions)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an abelian monoid — i.e., a set equipped with a binary operation “
+_{\mathcal{A}}
” that is associative and commutative, and admits an identity element “
0_{\mathcal{A}}
”.
Following
[
28
]
, we let a
linear minion
over
\mathcal{A}
be a non-empty set
{{M}}
of matrices having entries in
\mathcal{A}
that is closed under the following elementary row operations:

•

swapping two rows;

•

replacing two rows with their sum;

•

inserting an extra zero row.

(Here, the sum is the entrywise application of the “
+_{\mathcal{A}}
” operation, while the zero row is a tuple of copies of “
0_{\mathcal{A}}
”.) We let
{{M}}^{(\ell)}
be the set of all matrices in
{{M}}
having exactly
\ell
-many rows. Moreover, given a matrix
M\in{{M}}^{(\ell)}
and a map
\pi:[\ell]\to[\ell^{\prime}]
, we define

as the matrix whose

-th row, for

having index in
\pi^{-1}(i)

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{i;\ell^{\prime}}^{\top}M_{/\pi}=\sum_{j\in\pi^{-1}(i)}\mathbf{e}_{j;\ell}^{\top}M.

Starting from

M_{/\pi}
through a sequence of the elementary row operations listed above. Therefore,

is a minion.

Let
{{M}}
and

be two minions. A

•

preserves the arity — i.e.,

•

preserves the minors — i.e.,
\xi(M_{/\pi})=\xi(M)_{/\pi}
if

and
\pi:[\ell]\to[\ell^{\prime}]
.
7
7

7

Equivalently, a minion homomorphism

Given a relational structure

By virtue of Theorem

, in order to investigate the occurrence of quantum advantage, one needs to understand the structure of the quantum minion
{{Q}}_{\mathbb{H}}
. “Minion homomorphism” is a binary, reflexive, and transitive relation; thus, it induces a preorder on the class of all minions. It shall be crucial to obtain information on where
{{Q}}_{\mathbb{H}}

\dim(\mathbb{H})\leq 2

of the preorder, as it is equivalent to a specific minion — known as the dictator minion — that admits a homomorphism to all minions.
This implies that no structure has quantum advantage over such Hilbert spaces.
Things get more interesting when

{{Q}}_{\mathbb{H}}
is essentially different from the dictator minion.
Through known results on the complexity of CSPs and the theory of their polymorphism minions, this separation shall lead to a sufficient condition for the occurrence of quantum advantage.

We start by defining the

5
{{Q}}_{\mathbb{H}}
and the bounded-width minion

As we have seen, no structure has quantum advantage over Hilbert spaces of dimension
1
or
2

3

provides a sufficient condition for the occurrence of quantum advantage, in terms of the complexity of
\CSP(\mathbf{Y})
. In this section, we establish a necessary condition, also formulated in terms of a complexity notion of
\CSP(\mathbf{Y})
— namely, its

.

The concept of width is central in the theory of constraint satisfaction.
On a high level, the width of a CSP expresses the power of local-consistency techniques for its solvability. More formally, given two similar relational structures
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
and some fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
the
k
-consistency algorithm provides a heuristic to check if
\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{Y}
, by testing for the existence of a non-empty family
of partial homomorphisms from substructures of
\mathbf{X}
of size at most
k
to
\mathbf{Y}
, that is closed under restriction and under extension up to size
k
. If such a family exists,
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
are said to be
k
-consistent.
16
16

16

We refer to
[
9
]
for the formal definition of the
k
-consistency algorithm.

As long as
k
is a constant, there exists a polynomial-time procedure (in the size of
\mathbf{X}
) to check whether
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
are
k
-consistent. If
\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{Y}
, a family of partial homomorphisms as described above always exists.
We say that
\CSP(\mathbf{Y})
has width
k
if the
k
-consistency algorithm always detects unsatisfiable instances; i.e.,
if
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
are
k
-consistent precisely when
\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{Y}
. If that is the case, this method yields a polynomial-time algorithm for the solution of
\CSP(\mathbf{Y})
. CSPs that are solvable in some constant width
k\in\mathbb{N}
are known as
bounded-width
CSPs, and constitute an important fragment of all tractable CSPs.

The goal of this section is to prove that the width of a CSP parameterised by a structure having quantum advantage over some finite-dimensional Hilbert space must be unbounded (Corollary
25
).
Our proof is in some sense specular to the argument in the previous section. Indeed, this time we need to show that the quantum minion
{{Q}}_{\mathbb{H}}
is located “left enough” in the minion homomorphism preorder, in that it always admits a homomorphism to one specific minion — recently introduced in
[
28
]
and, independently, in
[
15
]
— that is known to capture bounded width. Then, the result will follow from Theorem
6
. We shall conclude the section by outlining an alternative argument to obtain the same result, that makes use of a different minion introduced in
[
27
]
.

Remark 26

We now discuss an alternative proof of the result stated as Corollary

The algorithm is the

If the feasible region of the LP is empty, the assignment

CLP is at least as strong as the

The columns of the matrices in the minion correspond to the solutions of the LPs. For such LPs,

The second difference is that each matrix in

7
Quantum advantage and promise CSPs

Let
\mathbf{Y}
be a relational structure and, as usual, let
\mathbb{H}
be a finite-dimensional Hilbert space. If
\mathbf{Y}
does not have quantum advantage over
\mathbb{H}
, any structure
\mathbf{X}
such that
\mathbf{X}\to_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbf{Y}
admits a homomorphism to
\mathbf{Y}
. Suppose now that
\mathbf{Y}
does have quantum advantage over
\mathbb{H}
. Can we still get some partial information of classical type from the fact that a quantum homomorphism
\mathbf{X}\to_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbf{Y}
exists?
A natural way of expressing this partial information — as opposed to the complete information “
\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{Y}

\mathbf{X}\to_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbf{Y}
should at least guarantee the existence of a classical homomorphism
\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}
for
some
structure
\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}
. Notice that, if this is the case, we must have
\mathbf{Y}\to\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}
, since
\mathbf{Y}\to_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbf{Y}
. We now give a formal description of this more general version of quantum advantage.

Definition 31
.

Let
\mathbf{Y}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}
be two
\sigma
-structures such that
\mathbf{Y}\to\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}
. We say that the pair
(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Y}^{\prime})

has quantum advantage over
\mathbb{H}
if there exists a
\sigma
-structure
\mathbf{X}
such that
\mathbf{X}\to_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbf{Y}
but
\mathbf{X}\not\to\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}
.

In other words,
(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Y}^{\prime})
does
not
have quantum advantage over
\mathbb{H}
if and only if a quantum homomorphism
\mathbf{X}\to_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbf{Y}
guarantees at least a classical homomorphism
\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}
.
Since the composition of a quantum and a classical homomorphism is a quantum homomorphism, we easily see that, if
(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Y}^{\prime})
has quantum advantage over
\mathbb{H}
, then the same must hold for both
\mathbf{Y}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}
individually. We will prove that the converse implication is not true in general.

Theorem 32
.

If
\dim(\mathbb{H})\geq 3
,
there exists a pair of relational structures
\mathbf{Y}\to\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}
such that
\mathbf{Y}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}
, but not
(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Y}^{\prime})
, have quantum advantage over
\mathbb{H}
.

It turns out that the framework we developed in the previous sections can be smoothly readapted to capture this generalised version of quantum advantage. As a consequence, just like the occurrence of quantum advantage for a single structure is linked to CSP complexity, we shall see that the occurrence of quantum advantage for a pair of structures is linked to the complexity of a

For two

\mathbf{Y}

\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}

The fact that

This framework was formally introduced in

Lemma

(Lemma
11
restated)
.

If
\dim(\mathbb{H})=2
, there exists
C\subset\mathbb{H}
such that, given any two nonzero orthogonal
vectors
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{H}
, exactly one of
\mathbf{v}
and
\mathbf{w}
lies in
C
.

Proof.

Consider the map

\displaystyle f:\mathbb{C}^{2}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle(x,y)

\displaystyle\mapsto(x\bar{x}-y\bar{y},2x\bar{y}).

Let
S^{3}
be the subset of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
containing all pairs
(x,y)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
such that
x\bar{x}+y\bar{y}=1
, and let
S^{2}
be the subset of
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}
containing all pairs
(t,z)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}
such that
t^{2}+z\bar{z}=1
.
A straightforward calculation shows that
f
maps elements in
S^{3}
to elements in
S^{2}
. Moreover, given two orthogonal vectors
(x,y)
and
(p,q)
in
S^{3}
(i.e.,
x\bar{p}+y\bar{q}=0
), notice that the matrix

\displaystyle M=\begin{bmatrix}x&p\\
y&q\end{bmatrix}

satisfies
M^{*}M=I_{2}
; i.e.,
M
is unitary. As a consequence,

\displaystyle I_{2}=MM^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}x\bar{x}+p\bar{p}&x\bar{y}+p\bar{q}\\
y\bar{x}+q\bar{p}&y\bar{y}+q\bar{q}\end{bmatrix},

and we deduce that
f(x,y)=-f(p,q)
. In other words,
f
maps pairs of orthogonal vectors of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
having norm
1
to antipodal vectors in
S^{2}
.

Choose an antipodal partition of
S^{2}
; i.e., a partition
S^{2}=U\cup V
such that, for each
(t,z)\in S^{2}
,
(t,z)
and
(-t,-z)
lie in different parts. For example, we might let
U
be the set of vectors
(t,z)\in S^{2}
such that
t>0
, or
t=0
and
z_{R}>0
(where
z_{R}
is the real part of
z
), or
t=0
and
z=\mathrm{i}
, and
V=S^{2}\setminus U
.
Fix an orthonormal basis
B
for
\mathbb{H}
, and let
r:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
be the function mapping elements of
\mathbb{H}
to the corresponding vectors of coordinates in the basis
B
. Finally, consider the function
g:\mathbb{H}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}_{\mathbb{H}}\}\to S^{2}
mapping
\mathbf{v}
to the image of
\frac{\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|}
under the function
f\circ r
.
We define
C=g^{-1}(U)
. If
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}
are two nonzero orthogonal vectors in
\mathbb{H}
, the argument above implies that
g(\mathbf{v})=-g(\mathbf{w})
and, hence, exactly one of
g(\mathbf{v})
and
g(\mathbf{w})
lies in
U
. This shows that the set
C
has the required property.
∎
