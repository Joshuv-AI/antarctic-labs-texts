Hausdorff dimension of the Apollonian gasket
Polina L. Vytnova, Caroline L. Wormell

Abstract
The Apollonian gasket is a well-studied circle packing. Important properties of the packing, including the distribution of the circle radii, are governed by its Hausdorff dimension. No closed form is currently known for the Hausdorff dimension, and its computation is a special case of a more general and hard problem: effective, rigorous estimates of dimension of a parabolic limit set. In this paper we develop an efficient method for solving this problem which allows us to compute the dimension of the gasket to 128 128 decimal places and rigorously justify the error bounds. We expect our approach to generalise easily to other parabolic fractals.

Introduction

The Apollonian circle packing might be one of the earliest examples of fractals,
with a Hausdorff dimension strictly bigger than its topological dimension of one
and strictly less than the two dimensions of the plane of which it is a subset.
The construction dates back to
works by Apollonius of Perga, from 200 B.C., whose name is given to the fractal.
The theorem attributed to Apollonius states that given
3
mutually tangent
circles there are exactly two other circles tangent to all of them. This starts an iterative
process: at each step one takes
3
mutually tangent circles from the set of existing
ones and adds two more that are tangent to all of them. The resulting limiting set is now
called the Apollonian circle packing, and we shall denote it by
A
.
Recently, its statistical properties, such as the asymptotics of the number of
circles with curvature less than a given number, and number-theoretic properties,
such as possible curvatures in integer packings have attracted attention of the wide community.

In
[
4
]
Boyd gave the first and only rigorous estimate of the
Hausdorff dimension known to date, with an accuracy of
0.015
:

1.300197<d_{H}<1.314534.

Since then, there have been a number of non-rigorous attempts to improve this
bounds. These bounds were of varying success:

—

Various estimates have been made by counting circles in the Apollonian
packing, typically accurate only to a few digits
[
8
]
;

—

Thomas and Dhar
[
16
]
studied the transfer operator of dynamics
on quartets of tangent circles to obtain an estimate of

1.305686729(10)
. This dynamics is parabolic, and had consequently slow
convergence;

—

Later, McMullen
[
11
]
discretised the transfer operator of the
full Apollonian circle packing. This had slow convergence, again due to
parabolicity. He made a famous estimate of the dimension as
1.305688
,
which turned out to be accurate only to
5
decimal places;

—

More recently, Bai and Finch
[
1
]
discretised an induced
transfer operator on the complex unit circle in terms of functions of
the form
z^{n}\bar{z}^{m},n,m\geq 0
. This enabled them to make a
non-rigorous computation of the dimension to
30
decimal places. However,
they noted that there was no obvious way to make this computation
rigorous.

In the present manuscript we give an efficient and
effective method for computing the Hausdorff dimension of the limit set
of a conformal parabolic iterated function system. Using the new approach we
give an estimate of the Hausdorff dimension of the Apollonian circle packing
accurate to
128
decimal places.

Theorem 1.1
.

The Hausdorff dimension of the Apollonian circle packing is

\displaystyle\dim_{H}(A)=1.

\displaystyle 3056867280\,4987718464\,5986206851\,0408911060\,2644149646\,

\displaystyle 8296446188\,3889969864\,2050296986\,4545216123\,1505387132\,

\displaystyle 8079246688\,2421869101\,967305643\pm 10^{-129}.

The accuracy of this estimate could be easily improved using our algorithm subject to more computer resources and time.
Note that all circle packings arising from three mutually tangent circles have the same Hausdorff dimension as they are conformally equivalent.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is founded on the following ideas.

1.

The classical Ruelle–Bowen formula, which relates the Hausdorff
dimension to the maximal eigenvalue of a suitable linear operator acting on a
Hardy space. We rely here on an adaptation by Mauldin and Urbanski
[
10
]
of Ruelle’s classical
results to infinite iterated function systems.

2.

The linear operator’s analyticity improving property, which allows us to
approximate it numerically with operators of finite rank.
We establish exponential convergence of the
Chebyshev–Lagrange approximation in multiple dimensions
,
and compute the relevant constants explicitly. To our knowledge these results
are new and we state them in Theorem
3.3
in general form.
This is one of the key results of the paper and Section
3

is dedicated to it.

3.

An adaptation of min-max type estimates, which gives us a way
to obtain lower and upper bounds of the
maximal eigenvalue
of the linear operator
between Hardy spaces using the
eigenvector
of the finite rank
approximation. The idea to apply the min-max estimates to problems of this type appeared
in the work of the first author with M. Pollicott
[
13
]
. However, the version
we use here, presented in Section
4
, is a modification that
requires fewer computational resources.
We state it in Theorem
4.2
as a general result applicable
to other problems.

4.

In order to apply the min-max estimate in practice, it is necessary to be
able to evaluate the linear operator applied to a given function with
arbitrary precision. This is done using the classical Euler–Maclaurin formula, building on
[
18
]
.
In Section
5
we show that error terms arising in the
Euler–Maclaurin formula
can be effectively and efficiently estimated in general setting of
contracting infinite iterated function systems.
This is one of the key results of the paper that we state in
Theorem
5.9
. We give formulae that allow one to obtain estimates
for all constants involved in Theorem
6.2
.

In Section
6.1
we apply the general strategy outlined above to compute the
Hausdorff dimension of the Apollonian gasket. In particular, we evaluate
explicitly all necessary constants involved. We give an outline of the
algorithm used by our computer program and provide pseudocode in Section
7
.

approximation of the Apollonian circle packing’s transfer operator will be a
special case corresponding to the choices dimension
d=2
and spatial
\ell^{p}
metric
p=2
.

We will first define the Hardy spaces in which this approximation occurs; we
will then define the projection operator and give our main theorem on transfer
operators, Theorem
3.3
. Finally we will prove the main approximation
result, Proposition
3.2
.

3.1
Hardy spaces

It is easy to generalise the construction of the Bernstein ellipses in
\mathbb{C}^{2}

given in (
5
) to
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. In the future, it will be more convenient
to use the
\ell_{p}
norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
p\in[1,\infty]
. So let us define

\mathrm{Ball}_{r}^{d,p}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\|x\|_{p}<r\right\}.

By analogy with (
5
) we introduce
the
d
-
dimensional Bernstein ellipses

\mathcal{E}_{r}^{p,d}:=\cos(\mathbb{R}^{d}+i\mathrm{Ball}_{r}^{d,p})\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{r}^{d,\infty}=(\mathcal{E}_{r})^{d}\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}.

(10)

Note that when
d=1
or
d=2
this agrees with the
\mathcal{E}_{r}
and

\mathcal{E}_{r}^{2,2}
as defined before.

Next we want to consider a Hardy space of holomorphic functions on
the interior of
\mathcal{E}_{r}^{p,d}
continuous onto the boundary.
Similarly to the case
d=2
we introduce a
closed subspace consisting of functions that map the real cube

[-1,1]^{d}
to the real line. More precisely, we set

\mathcal{H}_{r}^{d,p}=\{\varphi\colon\mathcal{E}_{r}^{d,p}\to\mathbb{C}\mid\varphi\in C^{\omega}(\mathcal{E}_{r}^{d,p})\cap C^{0}(\overline{\mathcal{E}_{r}^{d,p}}),\ \varphi([-1,1]^{d})\subseteq\mathbb{R}\}.

(11)

with the supremum norm

\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{r}^{d,p}}=\sup_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{E}_{r}^{p,d}}|\varphi(\mathbf{z})|.

(12)

The transfer operator for the Apollonian packing defined in (
9
) is
a weighted composition operator acting
on
\mathcal{H}_{r}^{2,2}
spaces.

More generally, let us assume that the sizes of the ellipses
0<r<R
, as well
as
p
and
d
are fixed.
Given countable families of weight functions
w_{j}\in\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}
and
analytic maps
v_{j}\colon\mathcal{E}_{R}^{d,p}\to\mathcal{E}_{r}^{d,p}
, we define the transfer operator
in a general form

\mathcal{L}\colon\mathcal{H}_{r}^{d,p}\to\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p},\qquad\mathcal{L}\varphi=\sum_{j}w_{j}\cdot(\varphi\circ v_{j}).

(13)

The following proposition shows that in order to guarantee that the operator is
bounded it is sufficient to impose an assumption of
summability of weights.

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume that
W<\infty
is chosen such that

\sum_{\iota\in I}\bigl\|w_{\iota}\bigr\|_{\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}}\leq W
. Then
for
0<r<R
as above, the transfer operator (
13
) is a bounded operator
\mathcal{H}_{r}^{d,p}\to\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}
with

\|\mathcal{L}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{r}^{d,p}\to\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}}\leq W.

Proof.

From the definitions of
\mathcal{L}
and the norm on
\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}
we have

\|\mathcal{L}\psi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}}=\sup_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{E}_{R}^{p,d}}\Bigl|\sum_{\iota\in I}w_{\iota}(z)\ \psi(v_{\iota}(z))\Bigr|.

Since
v_{\iota}
maps
\mathcal{E}_{R}^{p,d}
into
\mathcal{E}_{r}^{p,d}
we can bound

\|\mathcal{L}\psi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}}\leq\sup_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{E}_{R}^{p,d}}\sum_{\iota\in I}\left|w_{\iota}(z)\right|\ \|\psi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{r}^{d,p}}\leq\sum_{\iota\in I}\left\|w_{\iota}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}}\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{r}^{d,p}}\leq W\cdot\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{r}^{d,p}},

as required.
∎

In the next section we explain how to approximate the transfer operator by
finite rank operators.

3.2
Chebyshev approximation in multiple dimensions

The Chebyshev polynomials

T_{k}(z)=\cos(k\arccos z),\ k\in\mathbb{N}

(14)

are a family of univariate polynomials. They are orthogonal with respect to
the inner product

\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\overline{\varphi(x)}\psi(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\mathrm{d}x.

They are very well-adapted to smooth function approximation on intervals,
which can be partly attributed to their close connection to Fourier series.
One consequence of this connection is that the polynomials
T_{k}
,

0\leq k<K
are mutually orthogonal with respect to the counting measure on the

Chebyshev nodes of the first kind
of order
K
:

x_{k,K}=\cos\tfrac{\pi(2k+1)}{2K},\ k=0,1,\ldots,K-1.

(15)

and form a basis in the space of polynomials of degree
K-1
.
The transformation between values of a function at
x_{k,K}
and its
coefficients in the basis of Chebyshev polynomials can be computed in
O(K\log K)

time using the Fast Fourier Transform
[
15
]
.

To approximate smooth functions on
d
-dimensional hypercubes we will use a
tensor product of Chebyshev bases, with the new basis functions being

T_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{z})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}T_{k_{j}}(z_{j}),\ \mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}.

(16)

The collection of tensor product functions

\{T_{\mathbf{k}}:\|\mathbf{k}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}<K\}
is orthogonal with respect to
counting measure on the (tensor product) Chebyshev nodes

\left\{\vec{x}_{\mathbf{k},K}=(x_{k_{1},K},x_{k_{2},K},\ldots,x_{k_{d},K})\mid\|\mathbf{k}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}<K\right\}.

By analogy with the one-dimensional case, we introduce the basis of the space
of polynomials
\langle T_{\mathbf{k}}\mid\|\mathbf{k}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}<K\rangle
consisting of the Chebyshev–Lagrange polynomials by

\left\{\ell_{\vec{k},K}(\vec{y})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\ell_{k_{j},K}(y_{j}),\|\mathbf{k}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}<K\,\Bigl|\,\ell_{k_{j},K}(y):=\prod_{i=1,\ldots,K,i\neq k_{j}}\tfrac{y-x_{i,K}}{x_{k,K}-x_{i,K}},\,\right\}.

(17)

Note that here we use an
\ell^{\infty}
norm independent of the choice of
p
parameter.

Let the projection operator
\mathcal{P}_{K}\colon\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{p,d}\to\langle T_{\mathbf{k}}\mid\|\mathbf{k}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}<K\rangle
be interpolation on these nodes, that is

(\mathcal{P}_{K}\phi)(\vec{y})=\sum_{\vec{k}\in\{1,2,\ldots,K\}^{d}}\phi(\vec{x}_{\vec{k},K})\ell_{\vec{k},K}(y)

(18)

The following results, extending those of
[
2
]
,
show that the transfer operator
\mathcal{L}
is nicely bounded in these Hardy spaces,
and is readily approximable using interpolation at Chebyshev nodes.

In what follows,
\mathcal{I}
stands for the natural embedding between the spaces
involved.

Proposition 3.2
.

For any
R>\rho\geq 0
and
K\in\mathbb{N}
the projection error is bounded by

\|\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{P}_{K}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}\to\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{d,p}}\leq E_{d,K}(R-\rho).

where
E_{d,K}
is the error function given by
3
3

3

The estimate
for
d\geq 4
can be improved by computing the incomplete Gamma function
exactly — see (
27
).

E_{d,K}(x)=\begin{cases}8e^{-(K-1)x}\cdot\frac{1}{x},&\mbox{ if }d=1;\\
16e^{-(K-1)x}\cdot\frac{1+Kx}{x^{2}},&\mbox{ if }d=2;\\
48e^{-(K-1)x}\cdot\frac{2+2Kx+K^{2}x^{2}}{x^{3}},&\mbox{ if }d=3;\\
2^{d}e^{1-(K-1)x}\cdot\frac{d^{2}K^{d-1}}{x}&\mbox{ if }d\geq 4.\end{cases}

(19)

In other words, the more the maps
v_{\iota}
contract,
the bigger the difference in sizes between the ellipses
\mathcal{E}_{R}^{d,p}
and
\mathcal{E}_{r}^{d,p}
that the contraction maps one into another
is; and consequently the smaller the projection error is.
Proposition
3.2
facilitates approximation of the transfer
operator by finite-rank operators. The proof of
Proposition
3.2
is rather technical and we postpone it until
§
3.3
. The most important consequence for us is the next
theorem, which is its immediate consequence.

Theorem 3.3
.

Given
0<r<R
,
p,d\in\mathbb{N}
and countable families of
weight functions
w_{j}\in\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}
and analytic maps
v_{j}\colon\mathcal{E}_{R}^{d,p}\to\mathcal{E}_{r}^{d,p}
satisfying
\sum\|w_{j}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}}\leq W
, consider the transfer operator

\mathcal{L}\varphi=\sum_{j}w_{j}\cdot(\varphi\circ v_{j}).

(
13
)

Then for all
K\in\mathbb{N}
and
\rho\in[0,R)
we have

\|\mathcal{P}_{K}\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{r}^{d,p}\to\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{d,p}}\leq W\cdot E_{d,K}(R-\rho),

where
E_{d,K}
is the error function given
by (
19
).

Proof.

Using Propositions
3.1
and
3.2
, we have

\|\mathcal{P}_{K}\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{r}^{d,p}\to\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{d,p}}\leq\|\mathcal{P}_{K}-\mathcal{I}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}\to\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{d,p}}\|\mathcal{L}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{r}^{d,p}\to\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}}\leq W\cdot E_{d,K}(R-\rho),

as required.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

Under the hypothesis of Theorem
3.3
the
operator
\mathcal{L}
is a compact endomorphism on
\mathcal{H}_{r}^{d,p}
.

Proof.

To show that
\mathcal{L}
is compact, we need only show that the inclusion
\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}\to\mathcal{H}_{r}^{d,p}
is compact. Proposition
3.2
gives that this
inclusion is approximable in operator norm by a sequence of finite-rank and
hence compact operators
\{\mathcal{P}_{K}\}_{K\in\mathbb{N}}
, and is therefore compact.
∎

Since the (non-trivial) eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{P}_{K}\mathcal{L}
lie in

\im\mathcal{P}_{K}=\spann\{T_{\mathbf{k}}\mid\|\mathbf{k}\|_{\infty}\leq K\}
,
the spectral data of
\mathcal{P}_{K}\mathcal{L}
can be obtained from the truly finite-dimensional
restriction
\mathcal{P}_{K}\mathcal{L}|_{\im\mathcal{P}_{K}}
, which is given by a matrix in the basis of
Chebyshev–Lagrange polynomials (
17
).
We shall use Theorem
3.3
to obtain a good approximation to the spectral radius of
\mathcal{L}
.

Remark 3.5
.

Extending the definition of C-expansion from
[
17
]
, we can expect
that, at least for a fixed
j
,
R
and
r
satisfying hypothesis of
Theorem
3.3
exist provided
v_{j}
and
w_{j}

are real-analytic with
v_{j}
obeying
the following C-contraction condition:

\sup_{{\bm{\theta}}\in[0,\pi]^{d}}\left\|(\arccos(v_{j}(\cos({\bm{\theta}})))^{\prime}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right\|_{\ell^{p}}<1

where
\cos
is applied component-wise, and
{\bm{\theta}}\in[0,\pi]^{d}
can
be thought of as the arc-cos of the position variable
\mathbf{x}\in[-1,1]^{d}
.
(The
\cos
connection arises from the connection between Fourier and
Chebyshev functions:
T_{k}(\cos\theta_{j})=\cos k\theta_{j}
.)

Remark 3.6
.

In practice, the ambient dimension
d
will be fixed by the problem and
generally low: the Apollonian circle packing IFS has

. We shall show later
in §

that for the Apollonian packing we can choose

and

. It turns out (see Table

it is

.

.

3.3
Proof of Proposition
3.2

Here we show that the projection operator (
18
) is close to the identity
and establish the formula (
19
) for the bound
E_{d,K}
on the error.

We need two easy technical statements that will simplify the exposition of the
argument.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
{\check{m}_{K}}(k_{j}):=\left|\left((k_{j}+K)\allowbreak\mathord{\rm mod}\allowbreak\,2K\right)-K\right|
, and define
{\check{m}_{K}}(\mathbf{k})

component-wise. Then, for all
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
and
q\in[1,\infty]
,

\|{\check{m}_{K}}(\mathbf{k})\|_{q}\leq\|\mathbf{k}\|_{q}
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
|{\check{m}_{K}}(k_{j})|\leq|k_{j}|
. Indeed, we have that
either
k_{j}\leq K
,
in which case
{\check{m}_{K}}(k_{j})=k_{j}
, or
k_{j}>K
, in which case
|{\check{m}_{K}}k_{j}|\leq K<|k_{j}|
.
∎

Lemma 3.8
.

For all
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
,
\mathcal{P}_{K}T_{\mathbf{k}}=c_{\mathbf{k},K}T_{{\check{m}_{K}}(\mathbf{k})}
, where

c_{\mathbf{k},K}\in\{-1,1,0\}
. In particular,
\mathcal{P}_{K}T_{\mathbf{k}}=T_{\mathbf{k}}
when

\|\mathbf{k}\|_{\ell^{\infty}}<K
and
c_{\mathbf{k},K}=0
when there exists
j
such
that
k_{j}\allowbreak\mathord{\rm mod}\allowbreak\,2K=K
.

Proof.

The statement is established in
[
15
]
for
d=1
. The case for higher
dimensions follows by multiplicativity.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

For all
\rho>0
and for all
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

q^{-1}+p^{-1}=1
we have

\|T_{\mathbf{k}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{d,p}}\leq e^{\rho\|\mathbf{k}\|_{q}}.

Proof.

Combining the definitions of the ellipses
\mathcal{E}_{\rho}^{d,p}
(
10
), the norm on
\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{d,p}
(
12
),
and the Chebyshev
polynomials
T_{k}
(
16
), with the Holder inequality

\sum k_{j}\vartheta_{j}\leq\|k\|_{q}\cdot\|\vartheta\|_{p}
,
we obtain an upper bound

\|T_{\mathbf{k}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{d,p}}=\sup_{x,\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\vartheta\|_{p}\leq 1}\prod_{j=1}^{d}|\cos(k_{j}(x_{j}+i\rho\vartheta_{j}))|\leq\sup_{\|\vartheta\|_{p}\leq 1}\prod_{j=1}^{d}e^{k_{j}\rho\vartheta_{j}}=e^{\rho\|\mathbf{k}\|_{q}}.

(20)

∎

Together with Lemma
3.7
, this yields

Corollary 3.10
.

\|T_{\check{m}_{K}\mathbf{k}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{d,p}}\leq e^{\rho\|\mathbf{k}\|_{q}}.

Corollary 3.11
.

For all
\rho>0
and for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{2,2}

\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{2,2}}\leq\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{2}}e^{\rho\|\mathbf{k}\|_{\ell^{2}}}|\check{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}|

whenever the right-hand side is finite

Proof.

We can formally expand
\varphi
in the Chebyshev series
\varphi=\sum\limits_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{2}}\check{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}T_{\mathbf{k}}
. We then have

\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{2,2}}\leq\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{2}}|\check{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}|\|T_{\mathbf{k}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{2,2}}\leq\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{2}}|\check{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}|e^{\rho\|\mathbf{k}\|_{\ell^{2}}}.

where in the last inequality we used (
20
) on
each term. The convergence of this sum justifies the use of the Chebyshev
series. ∎

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

By construction of
\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}
a function
\varphi\in\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}
is
continuous on
[-1,1]^{d}
, and therefore its Chebyshev series
converges. More precisely, we define the Chebyshev coefficients by

\check{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}:=\frac{t_{\mathbf{k}}}{\pi^{d}}\int_{[-1,1]^{d}}\frac{T_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})\varphi(\mathbf{x})}{\prod_{j=1}^{d}\sqrt{1-x_{j}^{2}}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},\quad\mbox{ where }t_{\mathbf{k}}:=\prod_{j=1}^{d}(2-\delta^{k_{j}}_{0}).

(21)

and the Chebyshev series expansion

\varphi=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}\check{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}T_{\mathbf{k}}.

(22)

Applying Lemma
3.8
we get

(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{P}_{K})\varphi=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}\check{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}(T_{\mathbf{k}}-\mathcal{P}_{K}T_{\mathbf{k}})=\sum_{\|\mathbf{k}\|_{\infty}\geq K}\check{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}(T_{\mathbf{k}}-c_{\mathbf{k},K}T_{\check{m}_{K}\mathbf{k}})

wherever (
22
) converges. Assuming the Chebyshev series
of
\varphi
converges on
\mathcal{E}_{\rho}^{p,d}
, we may write

\displaystyle\|(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{P}_{K})\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{d,p}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\|\mathbf{k}\|_{\infty}\geq K}|\check{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}|\cdot(\|T_{\mathbf{k}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{d,p}}+\|T_{\check{m}_{K}\mathbf{k}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{d,p}}).

(23)

Applying Corollary
3.10
we get

\|(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{P}_{K})\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{d,p}}\leq 2\sum_{\|\mathbf{k}\|_{\infty}\geq K}|\check{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}|\cdot e^{\rho\|\mathbf{k}\|_{q}}.

(24)

We see that if the Chebyshev coefficients
\check{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}
decay faster
than
e^{-\rho\|\mathbf{k}\|_{q}}
, then
\|(I-\mathcal{P}_{K})\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{d,p}}

converges to zero as
K\to\infty
.

It only remains to estimate the coefficients. Recall that
T_{k_{j}}(x_{j})=\cos(k_{j}\arccos x_{j})
, and let us denote
\varphi(\cos{\bm{\theta}}):=\varphi(\cos\theta_{1},\cos\theta_{2},\ldots,\cos\theta_{d})
.
We can rewrite Definition (
21
) as an integral in an angle variable

\theta_{j}=\arccos x_{j}
:

\check{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}=\frac{t_{\mathbf{k}}}{\pi^{d}}\int_{[0,\pi]^{d}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d}\cos k_{j}\theta_{j}\right)\,\varphi(\cos{\bm{\theta}})\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}.

Since the cosine is an even analytic
2\pi
-periodic function on the real line,
we can write

\check{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}=\frac{t_{\mathbf{k}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d}\cos k_{j}\theta_{j}\right)\,\varphi(\cos{\bm{\theta}})\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}.

Rewriting the cosine terms using
\cos(k_{j}\theta_{j})=\frac{1}{2}(e^{-ik_{j}\theta_{j}}+e^{ik_{j}\theta_{j}})
, we get

\check{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}=2^{-d}\sum_{\sigma\in\{-1,1\}^{d}}\frac{t_{\mathbf{k}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}e^{i\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}k_{j}\theta_{j}}\,\varphi(\cos{\bm{\theta}})\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}.

Note that
\varphi\in\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}
is analytic on
\mathcal{E}_{R}^{d.p}
and
the composition
\varphi(\cos(\cdot))
is analytic and
2\pi
-periodic on
\mathbb{R}^{d}+i\mathrm{Ball}^{d,p}_{R}
. Furthermore on this multidimensional strip

\sup|\varphi(\cos(\cdot))|=\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}}
and
thus we can make the exponential terms small by
moving the surface of integration.

Let us define a translation vector by
\bm{\kappa}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\|\bm{\kappa}\|_{p}=1
by

\kappa_{j}=\begin{cases}\sigma_{j}\cdot(\sgn k_{j})\cdot\left|k_{j}\right|\rule{0.0pt}{12.0pt}^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\cdot\|\mathbf{k}\|^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}_{q},&p>1\\
\sigma_{j}\cdot(\sgn k_{j}),&p=1;\end{cases}\qquad j=1,2,\ldots,d.

We want to
translate our surface of integration by
\bm{\kappa}R
in imaginary
direction

\check{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}=2^{-d}\sum_{\sigma\in\{-1,1\}^{d}}\frac{t_{\mathbf{k}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}e^{i\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}k_{j}(\theta_{j}+i\kappa_{j}R)}\,\varphi(\cos({\bm{\theta}}+i\bm{\kappa}R))\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}.

(25)

We can compute, using the choice of
\kappa_{j}
,

\displaystyle\left|e^{i\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}k_{j}(\theta_{j}+i\kappa_{j}R)}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|e^{-\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}k_{j}\kappa_{j}R}\right|

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-R\cdot\|\mathbf{k}\|_{q}^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}|k_{j}|^{\frac{1}{p-1}+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-R\cdot\|\mathbf{k}\|_{q}^{q-\frac{1}{p-1}}\right)=\exp(-R\|\mathbf{k}\|_{q}).

Therefore we get an upper bound on
|\check{\varphi}_{k}|
from (
25
)

\displaystyle|\check{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}|\leq 2^{-d}\sum_{\sigma\in\{-1,1\}^{d}}\frac{t_{\mathbf{k}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}\exp(-R\|\mathbf{k}\|_{q})\cdot\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}}=t_{\mathbf{k}}e^{-R\|\mathbf{k}\|_{q}}\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}}.

(26)

Substituting this into (
24
) and taking into account

|t_{\mathbf{k}}|<2^{d}
we get

\displaystyle\|(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{P}_{K})\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{d,p}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\|\mathbf{k}\|_{\infty}\geq K}2^{d}\cdot 2e^{-(R-\rho)\|\mathbf{k}\|_{q}}\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}}\leq\sum_{\|\mathbf{k}\|_{\infty}\geq K}2^{d+1}e^{-(R-\rho)\|\mathbf{k}\|_{\infty}}\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}}

\displaystyle=2^{d+1}\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}}\sum_{\hat{k}=K}^{\infty}\#\{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\mid\|\mathbf{k}\|_{\infty}=\hat{k}\}\cdot e^{-(R-\rho)\hat{k}}.

All that remains is to obtain a bound on the sum. Counting vertices in the
hypercube we get
\#\{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\mid\|\mathbf{k}\|_{\infty}=\hat{k}\}=(\hat{k}+1)^{d}-\hat{k}^{d}
. When
d=1
this just leads to a geometric series.
Otherwise by straightforward computation, since
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
d\geq 2

\displaystyle\sum_{\hat{k}=K}^{\infty}((\hat{k}+1)^{d}-\hat{k}^{d})e^{-(R-\rho)\hat{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\hat{k}=K}^{\infty}\int_{\hat{k}}^{\hat{k}+1}d\cdot u^{d-1}e^{-(R-\rho)\hat{k}}\,\mathrm{d}u\leq\sum_{\hat{k}=K}^{\infty}\int_{\hat{k}}^{\hat{k}+1}d\cdot u^{d-1}e^{-(R-\rho)(u-1)}\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=d\cdot e^{R-\rho}(R-\rho)^{1-d}\int_{K}^{\infty}((R-\rho)u)^{d-1}e^{-(R-\rho)u}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=d\cdot e^{R-\rho}(R-\rho)^{-d}\int_{K(R-\rho)}^{\infty}t^{d-1}e^{-t}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=d\cdot e^{R-\rho}(R-\rho)^{-d}\cdot\Gamma(d;K(R-\rho)).

(27)

It remains to get an upper bound on the incomplete Gamma function. Recall that
for
d=2
we get
\Gamma(2;K(R-\rho))=e^{-K(R-\rho)}(1+K(R-\rho))
and
for
d=3
we have
\Gamma(3;K(R-\rho))=e^{-K(R-\rho)}(2+2K(R-\rho)+K^{2}(R-\rho)^{2})
.

Therefore, in the case when
d=2
,

\sum_{\hat{k}=K}^{\infty}((\hat{k}+1)^{2}-\hat{k}^{2})e^{-(R-\rho)\hat{k}}\leq 2\cdot e^{-(K-1)(R-\rho)}\frac{1+K(R-\rho)}{(R-\rho)^{2}}

and thus

\|(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{P}_{K})\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}^{2,p}_{\rho}}\leq 16\cdot e^{-(K-1)(R-\rho)}\frac{1+K(R-\rho)}{(R-\rho)^{2}}\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}}.

Similarly, if
d=3
, then

\sum_{\hat{k}=K}^{\infty}((\hat{k}+1)^{2}-\hat{k}^{2})e^{-(R-\rho)\hat{k}}\leq 3\cdot e^{-(K-1)(R-\rho)}\frac{2+2K(R-\rho)+K^{2}(R-\rho)^{2}}{(R-\rho)^{3}}

and hence

\|(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{P}_{K})\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}^{3,p}_{\rho}}\leq 48\cdot e^{-(K-1)(R-\rho)}\frac{2+2K(R-\rho)+K^{2}(R-\rho)^{2}}{(R-\rho)^{3}}\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}}.

For
d\geq 4
we can use the bounds by Pinelis
[
12
]
.
In particular,
[
12
, Theorem 1.1]
gives

\Gamma(d,K(R-\rho))\leq G_{d}(K(R-\rho)),

where

G_{d}(x):=\frac{(x+\gamma)^{d}-x^{d}}{d\gamma}e^{-x}\ \mbox{ with }\ \gamma:=\sqrt[d-1]{\Gamma(d+1)}.

By the Stirling’s inequality,

\sqrt{2\pi d}\cdot d^{d}\cdot e^{\frac{1}{12d+1}-d}<\Gamma(d+1)=d!<\sqrt{2\pi d}\cdot d^{d}\cdot e^{\frac{1}{12d}-d}

therefore
\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{e^{2}}d\leq\gamma\leq\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{e}d
and hence provided
d<K(R-\rho)

\Gamma(d,K(R-\rho))\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}d^{2}}\Bigl(\Bigl(K(R-\rho)+\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{e}d\Bigr)^{d}-(K(R-\rho))^{d}\Bigr)e^{2-K(R-\rho)}\\
\leq dK^{d-1}(R-\rho)^{d-1}e^{1-K(R-\rho)}.

Substituting into (
27
) we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\hat{k}=K}^{\infty}((\hat{k}+1)^{d}-\hat{k}^{d})e^{-(R-\rho)\hat{k}}

\displaystyle\leq de^{R-\rho}(R-\rho)^{-d}\cdot dK^{d-1}(R-\rho)^{d-1}e^{1-K(R-\rho)}

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{2}K^{d-1}}{R-\rho}\cdot e^{1-(K-1)(R-\rho)}

Summing up for
d\geq 4
we have a bound

\|(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{P}_{K})\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{d,p}}\leq\frac{2^{d}d^{2}}{R-\rho}K^{d-1}e^{1-(K-1)(R-\rho)}\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{R}^{d,p}}.

as required.

Finally, the
O(e^{-R\|\mathbf{k}\|})
decay of Chebyshev coefficients we obtain
in (
26
) implies uniform convergence of the Chebyshev
series on
\mathcal{E}_{\rho}^{p,d}
, justifying its validity.
∎

4
Min-max method

In this section we explain how to use the finite rank
operator
\mathcal{P}_{K}\mathcal{L}
to get estimates on the spectral radius of
\mathcal{L}
. More precisely, we take an estimate
\varphi
of the leading eigenvector of
\mathcal{P}_{K}\mathcal{L}|_{\im\mathcal{P}_{K}}
to construct a function
to which we can apply the min-max method of
[

, modified to use our
rigorous Chebyshev–Lagrange discretisation results.

We will prove two theorems in this section. Theorem

is a simple
modification of
[

, which we do not use explicitly, but it
illustrates the way our method works and may be useful in other settings.
Theorem

adds to this idea an a priori bound on

\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}
(and hence the derivative of the spectral radius),
meaning that an upper and lower bound for the dimension that can be obtained using only
one evaluation of the transfer operator per step, as opposed to two, used in bisection
method.

Note that in Definition

of the norm on
\mathcal{H}_{r}^{p,d}

we may set

, which corresponds to the supremum norm on the cube

In what follows,
\rho(\mathcal{L})
stands for the spectral radius of an operator
\mathcal{L}

on
\mathcal{H}_{r}^{d,p}

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose that for some

, the transfer operator
\mathcal{L}
given
by (
13

\mathcal{H}_{r}^{d,p}
and preserves positive functions on
[-1,1]^{d}
.
Let
\lambda>0
be given. If there exist a function
\varphi\in\mathcal{H}_{r}^{d,p}
positive on
[-1,1]^{d}
and a number

such that

1.

If
\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in[-1,1]^{d}}(\mathcal{P}_{K}\mathcal{L}\varphi-\lambda\varphi)(\mathbf{x})\leq-\|\mathcal{L}\varphi-\mathcal{P}_{K}\mathcal{L}\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{0}^{d,p}}
,
then
\rho(\mathcal{L})\leq\lambda
;

2.

If
\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in[-1,1]^{d}}(\mathcal{P}_{K}\mathcal{L}\varphi-\lambda\varphi)(\mathbf{x})\geq\|\mathcal{L}\varphi-\mathcal{P}_{K}\mathcal{L}\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{0}^{d,p}}

then
\rho(\mathcal{L})\geq\lambda
.

Proof.

The argument below essentially repeats the proof of
[
13
, Lemma 3.1]
.
Assume that
1

write

\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in[-1,1]^{d}}(\mathcal{L}\varphi-\lambda\varphi)(\mathbf{x})\leq\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in[-1,1]^{d}}(\mathcal{P}_{K}\mathcal{L}\varphi-\lambda\varphi)(\mathbf{x})+\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in[-1,1]^{d}}(\mathcal{L}\varphi-\mathcal{P}_{K}\mathcal{L}\varphi)(\mathbf{x})\\

Thus,
(\mathcal{L}\varphi)(\mathbf{x})\leq\lambda\varphi(\mathbf{x})
for every
\mathbf{x}\in[-1,1]^{d}
. Since
\mathcal{L}
is positive, iterating the inequality we get
(\mathcal{L}^{n}\varphi)(\mathbf{x})\leq\lambda^{n}\varphi(\mathbf{x})
. Since
\mathcal{L}
has a spectral gap, there exists a positive eigenfunction

h\leq\varphi
corresponding to
\rho(\mathcal{L}_{s})
. Therefore we have

\rho(\mathcal{L})=\limsup_{n\to\infty}(\mathcal{L}^{n}h(\mathbf{x}))^{1/n}\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}(\mathcal{L}^{n}\varphi(\mathbf{x}))^{1/n}\leq\lambda
.

The proof of
2
is completely analogous.
∎

In practice (see Section
7
), we shall choose
\varphi
to be an
informed but non-rigorous best guess of the top eigenvector of
\mathcal{P}_{K}\mathcal{L}
, for
instance via a non-validated computation. This best guess will be of a reasonable norm in
some appropriate Hardy space
\mathcal{H}_{r}^{d,p}
, and so we can apply Theorem
3.3

to estimate the norm
\|\mathcal{L}\varphi-\mathcal{P}_{K}\mathcal{L}\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{0}^{d,p}}
.

However in our considerations we are not really interested in computing the
leading eigenvalue for the Apollonian transfer operator
\mathcal{A}_{s}
, but rather in finding
a parameter value
s
for which
the spectral radius
of the transfer
operator (
9
) has a given value
\rho(\mathcal{A}_{s})=1
. At first sight, the above method
would suggest that
\mathcal{P}_{K}\mathcal{A}_{s}\varphi
should be computed for at least two different values of

(for an upper and lower bound).

Fortunately, in dimension estimation problems for uniformly contracting IFS such as
the induced system we consider here, we can often exploit monotonicity of the positive
operators with respect to the parameter.

To formalise the idea let us introduce a parameter into the generic transfer
operator (
13
) by taking weight
functions
w_{j,s}

s\in(a,b)

\mathcal{L}_{s}\varphi:=\sum_{j}w_{j,s}\cdot(\varphi\circ v_{j})

In this case, for
s>s^{\prime}
for every

we can bound
\mathcal{L}_{s}\varphi(x)\leq\mathcal{L}_{s}^{\prime}\varphi(x)+c(s-s^{\prime})\varphi(x)

The next Theorem allows us to make additional estimates so that at the
verification step (see §
7
) we only need to compute
\mathcal{P}_{K}\mathcal{L}_{s}\varphi
for a single value of
s
.
The resulting bounds will be looser
than optimal by a constant factor, which is tolerable given that we are saving a
factor of at least two in computational expense (which in turn allows us to
increase
N
,
K
, etc. and obtain
10
–
15\%
more digits in our final
estimate).

Theorem 4.2
.

Suppose that there exist
r>0
such that for all
s\in[a,b]
the parameterised transfer
operator (
28
)
has a spectral gap in
\mathcal{H}_{r}^{d,p}
and preserves positive functions on
[-1,1]^{d}
.
Assume additionally that the operator

-\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\mathcal{L}_{s}\varphi:=-\sum_{j}(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}w_{j,s})\cdot(\varphi\circ v_{j})

is also a positive operator with the property that there exist constants

D^{\pm}>0
such that for every bounded
\psi>0
and all
\mathbf{x}\in[-1,1]^{d}

-D^{-}\sup\psi\leq\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}(\mathcal{L}_{s}\psi)(\mathbf{x})\leq-D^{+}\inf\psi<0.

(29)

Let the projection operator
\mathcal{P}_{K}
and parameter
s_{0}\in[a,b]
be given.
Assume that a function
\varphi\in\mathcal{H}_{r}^{d,p}
, bounded on
[-1,1]^{d}
with

for some
\varphi^{\pm}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and a number
\lambda>0

satisfies the double inequality
\varepsilon^{-}<(\mathcal{P}_{K}\mathcal{L}_{s_{0}}\varphi-\lambda\varphi)(\mathbf{x})<\varepsilon^{+}
for some
\varepsilon^{\pm}\in\mathbb{R}
and for all
\mathbf{x}\in[-1,1]^{d}
. Define

\mathrm{err}
to be the approximation error
\mathrm{err}:=\|\mathcal{L}_{s_{0}}\varphi-\mathcal{P}_{K}\mathcal{L}_{s_{0}}\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{0}^{d,p}}

Then the unique parameter value

for which

satisfies the double inequality

s_{0}+\frac{\varepsilon^{-}-\mathrm{err}}{D^{-}\varphi^{+}}<s_{*}<s_{0}+\frac{\varepsilon^{+}+\mathrm{err}}{D^{+}\varphi^{-}}.

Proof.

The inequality (
29
) implies that the spectral radius is
decreasing monotonically in
s
. Indeed, differentiating
\mathcal{L}_{s}\psi=\mu_{s}\psi
and taking into account
\psi>0

conclude that
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\mu_{s}<0
.

It follows from Theorem

.
Hence if for some

.

Therefore in order to show that

.

Integrating (

(\mathcal{L}_{s}\varphi)(\mathbf{x})-(\mathcal{L}_{s_{0}}\varphi)(\mathbf{x})=\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\mathcal{L}_{t}\varphi)(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\int_{s_{0}}^{s}-D^{+}\varphi^{-}\,\mathrm{d}t,

Consequently, if we set

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}_{s_{+}}\varphi)(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle-\lambda\varphi(\mathbf{x})\leq(\mathcal{L}_{s_{0}}\varphi)(\mathbf{x})-D^{+}\varphi^{-}(s_{+}-s_{0})-\lambda\varphi(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=((\mathcal{L}_{s_{0}}\varphi)(\mathbf{x})-(P_{K}\mathcal{L}_{s_{0}}\varphi)(\mathbf{x}))+((P_{K}\mathcal{L}_{s_{0}}\varphi)(\mathbf{x})-\lambda\varphi(\mathbf{x}))-D^{+}\varphi^{-}\frac{\varepsilon^{+}+\mathrm{err}}{D^{+}\varphi^{-}}

Because

5
The Euler–Maclaurin formula

A major obstacle to accurate computation of the Hausdorff dimension of the
Apollonian circle packing is the fact that the transfer operator
\mathcal{A}_{s}

defined by (
9
) as an infinite sum with the tail
that decays at a relatively slow polynomial rate (since
J_{n}(x,y)=O(n^{-2})
as

n\to\infty
). However, the analyticity of
J_{n}^{\pm}
and
G_{n}^{\pm}
with respect to
n

allows us to apply a kind of sum acceleration, the so-called
Euler–Maclaurin formula.

The idea is as follows. We will aim to approximate the infinite sum

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\psi(n).

We first choose
N>0
and compute the sum of the terms exactly up to
n=N-1
. Then, we want to
approximate the tail by an integral:

\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\psi_{n}\simeq I_{\psi}(N):=\int_{N}^{\infty}\psi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

(31)

There is obviously a substantial error here. We can iteratively correct the
error, let us say with
L+1
terms for
L\geq 0
. We choose the first to be
\tfrac{1}{2}\psi(N)
; the rest are odd derivatives of
\psi
at
N
, with
coefficients coming from Bernoulli numbers
B_{l}

D_{\psi}(L,N)=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\frac{B_{2l}}{(2l)!}\left.\frac{\partial^{2l-1}\psi(t)}{\partial^{2l-1}t}\right|_{t=N}.

(32)

This correction of the error is not perfect, and leaves a remainder
R_{\psi}(L,N)
, which, provided the appropriate
choices of
L
and
N
are made, will be very small and can be bounded with some extra information about the regularity of
\psi
.

Putting these terms altogether,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\psi(n)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\psi(n)+\tfrac{1}{2}\psi(N)+I_{\psi}(N)+D_{\psi}(L,N)+R_{\psi}(L,N).

(33)

Although (
33
) holds true for any

\psi\in C^{2L}(\mathbb{R})
, a holomorphic extension of the summand function helps us to
obtain a good estimate on the error term, as well as
very effective computable approximations of the other terms
I_{\psi}(N)
and
D_{\psi}(L,N)
involving only pointwise evaluations of
\psi(n)
at different
(complex) values of
n
.

We proceed through the error analysis in reverse order of the appearance of the terms.

5.1
Remainder term
R(L,N)

Proposition 5.1
.

Assume that on the right half-plane
\Re(z)\geq\nu
a function
\psi
is
analytic and bounded
|\psi(z)|\leq C
.
Then for all real
N>\nu
and for all integer
L\geq 1
,

|R_{\psi}(L,N)|\leq\frac{(2L+1)!C}{L(2\pi)^{2L+1}(N-\nu)^{2L}}.

(34)

Note that we do not require the function
\psi
to be analytic at infinity.

Proof.

A classical result states that the remainder term can be written using Bernoulli
polynomials
B_{n}(x)

R_{\psi}(L,N)=\int_{N}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2L+1)!}\frac{\partial^{2L+1}}{\partial t^{2L+1}}\psi(t)B_{2L+1}(\{t\})\,\mathrm{d}t.

where
\{t\}
stands for the
fractional part of
t
. Using Lehmer’s bound
[
7
]
for the Bernoulli polynomial

|B_{2L+1}(x)|\leq 2(2\pi)^{-(2L+1)}(2L+1)!
, we may write

|R_{\psi}(L,N)|\leq\frac{2}{(2\pi)^{2L+1}}\int_{N}^{\infty}\left|\frac{\partial^{2L+1}}{\partial t^{2L+1}}\psi(t)\right|\,\mathrm{d}t.

(35)

We can estimate the derivative at
t
via an integral along a complex circle
centred at
t
of radius
|\nu-t|
using the Cauchy’s formula. For
\Re(t)>\nu

this gives

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial^{2L+1}}{\partial t^{2L+1}}\psi(t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(2L+1)!}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|\psi(t+|\nu-t|e^{i\vartheta})|\,|t-\nu|^{-(2L+1)}\,\mathrm{d}\vartheta

\displaystyle\leq(2L+1)!\,|t-\nu|^{-(2L+1)}C.

Substituting this into (
35
), we get

\displaystyle|R_{\psi}(L,N)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{(2\pi)^{2L+1}}\int_{N}^{\infty}(2L+1)!\,|t-\nu|^{-(2L+1)}C\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{C}{2\pi L}(2L+1)!\cdot(2\pi\cdot|N-\nu|)^{-2L}

as required.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

For fixed
N
, the choice of
L
minimising the
Proposition
5.1
bound on
R(L,N)
is such that
2L+1\approx 2\pi(N-\nu)
. This gives exponentially small error with

|R(L,N)|=\exp\left(-2\pi(N-\nu)+O(\log(N-\nu))\right);

by the virtue of the Stirling formula
\log(n!)=n\log n-n+O(\log n)
.

5.2
Sum of derivatives term
D(L,N)

To numerically estimate
D(L,N)
we will need to be able to accurately
estimate high-order derivatives of an analytic function at a given
point
4
4

4

In the setting we consider
this is
\psi(j):=w_{j}(z)\varphi(v_{j}(z))
from (
13
) at
j=N
.
.

Fortunately the Taylor coefficients of a function
\psi
at a point
z_{0}
are close to the positive order Fourier
coefficients of
\psi
on a circle
z_{0}+\tau e^{i\theta}
,
0\leq\theta<2\pi
. We can therefore estimate the derivatives of
\psi
by
taking a discrete Fourier transform of the function on the
circle. The error bounds are given in the following lemma.

Lemma 5.3
.

Suppose that on a disc
\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid|z-z_{0}|<\sigma\}
, a function
\psi
is
holomorphic and bounded
|\psi(z)|<C
. Then for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

|\psi^{(k)}(z_{0})|\leq\frac{k!}{\sigma^{k}}C.

(36)

Furthermore, the
k
’th derivative at
z_{0}
can be approximated using evaluations at
M>k
equidistant points on a circle of radius
\tau<\sigma
:

\left|\psi^{(k)}(z_{0})-\frac{k!}{M\tau^{k}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}e^{-\pi ik\frac{2m-1}{M}}\psi\left(z_{0}+\tau e^{\pi i\frac{2m-1}{M}}\right)\right|\leq\frac{C\tau^{M}k!}{\sigma^{k}(\sigma^{M}-\tau^{M})}.

(37)

Proof.

The first bound follows immediately from the Cauchy’s integral theorem.

For the second bound, we essentially compute an aliasing error for the
discrete Fourier transform performed along the circle of the smaller
radius
\tau<\sigma
centred at
z_{0}
. Expanding
\psi
as a Taylor series
about
z_{0}
, we get

\displaystyle\psi\left(z_{0}+\tau e^{\pi i\frac{2m-1}{M}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}\psi^{(j)}(z_{0})\tau^{j}e^{\pi ij\frac{2m-1}{M}}.

Substituting to the sum on the left hand side of (
37
) and swapping the order of
summation, we obtain:

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}e^{-\pi ik\frac{2m-1}{M}}\psi\left(z_{0}+\tau e^{2\pi i\frac{2m-1}{M}}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\tau^{j}}{j!}\psi^{(j)}(z_{0})\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}e^{\pi i(j-k)\frac{2m-1}{M}}.

Applying the geometric series identity to the sum in
m
,

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}e^{\pi i(j-k)\frac{2m-1}{M}}=\begin{cases}(-1)^{j}&\mbox{ if }j=k+Mk^{\prime},\;k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z},\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise }.\end{cases}

Therefore

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}e^{-\pi ik\frac{2m-1}{M}}\psi\left(z_{0}+\tau e^{2\pi i\frac{2m-1}{M}}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}\tau^{k+Mj}}{(k+Mj)!}\psi^{(k+Mj)}(z_{0}).

Then, the left-hand side of (
37
) becomes

\displaystyle\left|\psi^{(k)}(z_{0})-k!\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}\tau^{Mj}\frac{\psi^{(k+Mj)}(z_{0})}{(k+Mj)!}\right|=\left|k!\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}\tau^{Mj}\frac{\psi^{(k+Mj)}(z_{0})}{(k+Mj)!}\right|

which is using the first bound (
36
) can be bounded by

\left|k!\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\tau^{Mj}\frac{\psi^{(k+Mj)}(z_{0})}{(k+Mj)!}(-1)^{j}\right|\leq k!\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\tau^{Mj}C\sigma^{-(k+Mj)}\leq\frac{C\tau^{M}k!}{\sigma^{k}(\sigma^{M}-\tau^{M})}

as required.
∎

We can now apply this general result to compute

Proposition 5.7

Remark 5.8
.

The error (

) is
\sim(\tfrac{\nu}{N})^{M^{\prime}}
. Thus
to obtain an error of the order of

, it is necessary to

. According to Remark

,

Proof.

Since
\phi
is analytic on

it can be
expanded in Taylor series at zero that converges absolutely in the disk

\phi(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}z^{k}\frac{\phi^{(k)}(0)}{k!}.

Since by assumption

we may write

First, we want to approximate
I_{\psi}(N)
by a weighted sum of derivatives.

\displaystyle\Bigl|I_{\psi}(N)-\sum_{k=0}^{M^{\prime}-1}\frac{\phi^{(k)}(0)}{k!N^{k}}\frac{N^{1-2s}}{k+2s-1}\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=M^{\prime}}^{\infty}\frac{|\phi^{(k)}(0)|}{k!N^{k}}\frac{N^{1-2s}}{(k+2s-1)}

by the first bound in Lemma
5.3
with

\sigma=\frac{1}{\nu}
,

\displaystyle\leq CN^{1-2s}\sum_{k=M^{\prime}}^{\infty}\frac{\nu^{k}}{(k+2s-1)N^{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{CN^{1-2s}}{(M^{\prime}+2s-1)(1-\tfrac{\nu}{N})}\left(\frac{\nu}{N}\right)^{M^{\prime}}=:\varepsilon_{1}.

(45)

We next want to apply the second part of Lemma
5.3
to estimate the difference between the

Note that by the choice of

and

\sum_{k=0}^{M^{\prime}-1}c_{k}^{\prime}\psi(z_{k}^{\prime})=\sum_{k=0}^{M^{\prime}-1}c_{k}^{\prime}\frac{1}{z_{k}^{2s}}\psi\left(\frac{1}{z_{k}}\right)\\
=N\sum_{k=0}^{M^{\prime}-1}e^{2\pi is\frac{2k+1}{M^{\prime}}}\frac{1}{N^{2s}}e^{-2\pi is\frac{2k+1}{M^{\prime}}}\phi\left(\frac{1}{N}e^{2\pi i\frac{2k+1}{M^{\prime}}}\right)\sum_{p=0}^{M^{\prime}-1}e^{-2\pi ip\frac{2k+1}{M^{\prime}}}\frac{1}{p+2s-1}\\
=N^{1-2s}\sum_{k=0}^{M^{\prime}-1}\frac{1}{k+2s-1}\sum_{p=0}^{M^{\prime}-1}e^{-\pi ik\frac{2p+1}{M^{\prime}}}\phi\left(\frac{1}{N}e^{2\pi i\frac{2p+1}{M^{\prime}}}\right)

(46)

Therefore we can rewrite the difference as follows

\displaystyle\Bigl|\sum_{k=0}^{M^{\prime}-1}\frac{\phi^{(k)}(0)}{k!N^{k}}\frac{N^{1-2s}}{k+2s-1}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}\sum_{k=0}^{M^{\prime}-1}c_{k}^{\prime}\psi(z_{k}^{\prime})\Bigr|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{k=0}^{M^{\prime}-1}\frac{N^{1-2s}}{k+2s-1}\left(\frac{\phi^{(k)}(0)}{k!N^{k}}-\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}\sum_{p=0}^{M^{\prime}-1}e^{-\pi ik\frac{2p+1}{M^{\prime}}}\phi\left(\frac{1}{N}e^{2\pi i\frac{2p+1}{M^{\prime}}}\right)\right)\right|.

Applying Lemma
5.3
with
\tau=\frac{1}{N}
and
\sigma=\frac{1}{\nu}

\displaystyle\Bigl|\sum_{k=0}^{M^{\prime}-1}\frac{\phi^{(k)}(0)}{k!N^{k}}\frac{N^{1-2s}}{k+2s-1}-\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}\sum_{k=0}^{M^{\prime}-1}c_{k}^{\prime}\psi(z_{k}^{\prime})\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{M^{\prime}-1}\frac{N^{1-2s}}{k+2s-1}\Bigl|\frac{CN^{k-M^{\prime}}}{\nu^{-k}(\nu^{-M^{\prime}}-N^{-M^{\prime}})}\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{CN^{1-2s}}{(2s-1)(1-\frac{\nu}{N})}\frac{1}{\left(\frac{N}{\nu}\right)^{M^{\prime}}-1}=:\varepsilon_{2}.

(47)

Putting the two bounds (

)
and (

which completes the proof.

7
Computer-assisted proof of the main theorem

Let us now describe the algorithm for the computer program used to obtain the bounds on the Apollonian gasket’s Hausdorff
dimension that we claim in Theorem
1.1
. It has two stages: a non-rigorous guess of the
dimension is found, and this guess is then rigorously validated. We begin by choosing a desired
precision
\varepsilon
and computing parameters
K,L,N,M,M^{\prime}
as specified in Table
1
.

The first step is an iterative search of a parameter value
s_{*}

such that the maximal eigenvalue of
\mathcal{P}_{K}\mathcal{A}_{s_{*}}
is
sufficiently close to
1
.

The search is initialised with two guesses
s_{1},s_{2}
close to
\dim_{H}A
. For each guess, we evaluate the action of our transfer
operator
\mathcal{A}_{s_{1},s_{2}}
on the Chebyshev–Lagrange basis at the Chebyshev nodes. In other words, for
s_{1},s_{2}
in turn
we construct a (complete) matrix representation of
\mathcal{P}_{K}\mathcal{A}_{s_{1},s_{2}}
in the
Chebyshev–Lagrange basis. We then compute the leading eigenvalues of this matrix as specified in
Algorithm
1
. By Theorem
3.3
we know that our
matrix representation is a good
approximation of the true transfer operator, and so its leading eigenvalue will be a
reasonable approximation of the true leading eigenvalue of
\mathcal{A}_{s}
.

The search proceeds with the secant method applied to the function from
Algorithm
1
as specified in Algorithm
2
. The
aim is to find a value of
s
for which the leading eigenvalue is sufficiently
close to
1
. We do not need to explicitly estimate any errors here, and so
work in extended floating point arithmetic (such that the machine precision is around
\epsilon
). Then both the value
s_{*}
and the corresponding
eigenvector, the polynomial
\varphi_{*}
, represented by its values at the Chebyshev nodes are
passed to the second step for verification.

The second step is a rigorous verification using the min-max method. Namely, we apply the
approximating operator
\mathcal{P}_{K}\mathcal{A}_{s}
to the guessed eigenvector
\varphi_{*}
, keeping
track of all errors via interval arithmetic. In order to invoke
Theorem
4.2
, we only need to obtain bounds on the discrepancy
\varepsilon^{-}<|\mathcal{P}_{K}\mathcal{A}_{s}\varphi_{*}-\varphi_{*}|<\varepsilon^{+}
, and to compute necessary constants using
Propositions
6.4
and
6.5
with

\psi=\varphi_{*}
and
s=s_{*}
. The verification step is realised in Algorithm
3
.

After the preliminary discussion, we are ready to justify the accuracy of the
dimension value claimed.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

The bounds on the dimension
s_{-}\leq\dim_{H}A\leq s_{+}
is the output of
Algorithm
3
, with all computations done
using the interval arithmetic so that
any round-off errors are taken into account at all times.

Let
s_{*}
and
\varphi_{*}
be an educated guess of the dimension value and
a polynomial eigenfunction, represented by its values at the Chebyshev nodes.
Theorem
6.2
allows us to evaluate
(\mathcal{A}_{s_{*}}\varphi_{*})(x,y)
for given values
x,y
with arbitrary precision using the
Euler–Maclaurin formula.
Hence we can calculate the polynomial
\mathcal{P}_{K}\mathcal{A}_{s_{*}}\varphi_{*}

numerically using evaluations of
\mathcal{A}_{s_{*}}\varphi_{*}
at
Chebyshev–Lagrange nodes.

Then Proposition
6.4
allows us
to obtain an estimate on
\|\varphi_{*}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{r_{A}}^{2,2}}
and thus bound the
norm of the difference
\|\mathcal{P}_{n}\mathcal{A}_{s_{*}}\varphi_{*}-\mathcal{A}_{s_{*}}\varphi_{*}\|_{L^{\infty}}

in terms of
\|\varphi_{*}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{r_{A}}^{2,2}}
.
At the same time, using Proposition
6.5
we compute
necessary constants in order to apply
Theorem
4.2
to operator
\mathcal{A}_{s_{*}}
. This gives us an interval
[s_{+},s_{-}]\subseteq[1.30,1.31]

containing an
s
such that
\rho(\mathcal{A}_{s})=1
.
Proposition
2.3
implies that this value of
s
is
unique, and is the Hausdorff dimension of the Apollonian circle packing.
∎

7.1
Computational expense

We will briefly comment on the computational power used by this algorithm. To
evaluate
\mathcal{A}_{s}\psi
at a given point takes around
O(N+L+M+M^{\prime})=O(\log\varepsilon^{-1})
evaluations of
\psi
and other
functions. Given that
K=O(\log\varepsilon^{-1})
, the construction of
a
K^{2}\times K^{2}
transfer operator in the nonrigorous step therefore takes
approximately
O((\log\varepsilon^{-1})^{5})
evaluations; so too does the
evaluation of
\mathcal{A}_{s}
applied to a sum of
K^{2}
Chebyshev–Lagrange polynomials at
K^{2}

points in the rigorous step. The secant method requires
O(\log\log\varepsilon^{-1})
steps to converge. The time taken to perform arithmetic
operations using
BigFloat
s (the default extended-precision floating point
arithmetic in Julia) appears empirically to be around
O((\log\varepsilon^{-1})^{1.5})
for precision
\varepsilon
.

This suggests that the computational power required to obtain a rigorous
Hausdorff dimension estimate of precision
\varepsilon
is
5
5

5

Here and
below by
o_{\varepsilon}(1)
we understand a quantity that
\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

O((\log\varepsilon^{-1})^{6.5+o_{\varepsilon}(1)})
: which is to say, doubling the number of
digits in the estimate requires around
100
times more computing power. Our
algorithms are of course extremely parallelisable.

We note that we were able to make some efficiency gains on this by using other
structure of the problem. For example, the transfer operator preserves
y

y
