Asymptotics of Bergman polynomials for domains with reflection-invariant corners
Erwin Miña-Díaz and Aron Wennman

Abstract
. We study the asymptotic behavior of the Bergman orthogonal polynomials ( p n ) n = 0 ∞ (p_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty} for a class of bounded simply connected domains D D . The class is defined by the requirement that conformal maps φ \varphi of D D onto the unit disk extend analytically across the boundary L L of D D , and that φ ′ \varphi^{\prime} has a finite number of zeros z 1 , … , z q z_{1},\ldots,z_{q} on L L . The boundary L L is then piecewise analytic with corners at the zeros of φ ′ \varphi^{\prime} . A result of Stylianopoulos implies that a Carleman-type strong asymptotic formula for p n p_{n} holds on the exterior domain ℂ ∖ D ¯ \mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{D} . We prove that the same formula remains valid across L ∖ { z 1 , … , z q } L\setminus\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{q}\} and on a maximal open subset of D D . As a consequence, the only boundary points that attract zeros of p n p_{n} are the corners. This is in stark contrast to the case when φ \varphi fails to admit an analytic extension past L L , since when this happens the zero counting measure of p n p_{n} is known to approach the equilibrium measure for L L along suitable subsequences.



1   Introduction

1.1  Zeros of Bergman polynomials for domains with corners

For a domain
D
in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
, we write
A^{2}(D)
for the Bergman space of
holomorphic functions
f
on
D
with finite norm

\|f\|_{L^{2}(D)}:=\left(\int_{D}|f(z)|^{2}{\rm dA}(z)\right)^{1/2}<\infty,

where
{\rm dA}(z)=\pi^{-1}{\rm d}x{\rm d}y
denotes the standard area measure normalized by
\pi
.
In this work,
D
will always stand for the interior domain of a Jordan curve
L
.
That is,
D
is the bounded component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus L
.
We will denote by
\mathbb{D}(z,r)
the open disk of radius
r
centered at
z
, by

\mathbb{T}(z,r)
its boundary, and by
\Delta(z,r)
the exterior disk

\Delta(z,r)=\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}(z,r)}
.

The space of polynomials forms a linear subspace of
A^{2}(D)
.
By applying the Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization procedure to the standard monomial sequence,
we obtain a unique sequence of polynomials
(p_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
such that
p_{n}
has
degree
n
and positive leading coefficient, and the following planar orthonormality
conditions are satisfied:

\displaystyle\int_{D}p_{n}(z)\overline{p_{m}(z)}\,{\rm dA}(z)=\begin{cases}0,&\ m\not=n,\\
1,&\ n=m.\end{cases}

These polynomials are often called the Bergman polynomials for the domain
D
.
Their study dates back at least to Carleman’s 1922 paper
[
2
]
,
and the name Carleman polynomials also occurs in the literature.

For a positive integer
N\geq 3
, we denote by
R_{N}
the regular
N
-gon with vertices
at the
N
-th roots of unity. The zeros of the associated Bergman polynomials
are known to exhibit a curious dichotomy depending on the value of
N
.
In numerical experiments carried out in
[
7
]
, it was observed that for
N\in\{3,4\}
,
the zeros of
p_{n}
appear to lie on the set
\Gamma_{N}
formed by
joining the
N
vertices of
R_{N}
to its center,
and indeed this observation was later confirmed to be a fact in
[
9
]
.
However, for
D=R_{N}
with
N\geq 5
, the experiments in
[
7
]
strongly indicate that the zeros of

p_{n}
move away from
\Gamma_{N}
as
n
increases.
The reason behind this dichotomy was partially explained in the work
[
8
]
,
and as we describe in what follows, it is related to the analytic continuation properties
of the interior conformal maps of
D
onto the unit disk.

Denote by
\nu_{n}
,
n\geq 1
, the normalized counting measure for the zeros set of
p_{n}
.
That is, if
\zeta_{1,n},\ldots,\zeta_{n,n}
denote the zeros of
p_{n}
(counted with multiplicity),
we put

\nu_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{\zeta_{j,n}}.

For a measure
\nu
, we write
\nu_{n}\wsc\nu
as
n\to\infty
to mean that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int f{\rm d}\nu_{n}=\int f{\rm d}\nu

for every compactly supported continuous function
f
on
\mathbb{C}
.
We denote by
Z
the set of points
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
with the property that for every open neighborhood
U

of
z_{0}
, one can find infinitely many polynomials in the sequence
(p_{n})
that have a zero in
U
.
It is easy to see that if
\nu_{n}\wsc\nu
, then every point in the support of
\nu
belongs to
Z
.
The following result was proven in
[
8
]
(see Theorem 2.1 and Fact B therein).

Theorem 1.1

(Levin, Saff, Stylianopoulos
[
8
]
)
.

Let
D
be the interior domain of a Jordan curve
L
,
and let
\sigma_{L}
be the equilibrium measure of
L
.
The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

No conformal map of
D
onto
\mathbb{D}(0,1)
can
be analytically continued to a domain containing
\overline{D}
.

(ii)

There exists a subsequence
(\nu_{n_{k}})
with

(1.1)

\displaystyle\nu_{n_{k}}\wsc\sigma_{L}\quad\mathrm{as}\quad k\to\infty.

(iii)

For every
z\in D
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}|p_{n}(z)|^{1/n}=1.

Note that (i) is equivalent to the existence
of
one
conformal map
\varphi
which cannot
be analytically continued past
L
.

We remark that there are classes of domains for which the convergence in (
1.1
) is
known to hold for the full sequence
(\nu_{n})
, see
[
14
, Cor. 3.1]
.

Since the support of
\sigma_{L}
is all of
L
, the convergence (
1.1
) implies that if

\varphi
has a singularity on
L
, then
L\subset Z
. When
D=R_{N}
with
N\geq 5
, the
conformal map
\varphi
has singularities at the vertices of
D
, so that every point
of
L
attracts zeros of the polynomials
p_{n}
. As a consequence, it is not possible
for the zeros to stay on the set
\Gamma_{N}
, as it otherwise happens when
D
is the
equilateral triangle (
D=R_{3}
) or the square (
D=R_{4}
). We note that when
N\in\{3,4\}
,
not only are the zeros of every
p_{n}
located on the set
\Gamma_{N}
, but we also
have that
\Gamma_{N}\subset Z
. This is a consequence of Theorem 9 of
[
9
]
.

If

The equilateral triangle and the square both belong to the class

.
We might also mention the example of

, and that

These examples suggest the hypothesis that for every

, the set

).
That this is indeed the case will follow from the main result of this paper, namely,

. We state this result in more precise terms in what follows.

1.2  Strong asymptotics on a maximal open set

Denote by
\Omega
the unbounded component of
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus L
,
so that
\Omega=\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\overline{D}
(see Figure
1
, left side).
We let
\phi:\Omega\to\Delta(0,1)
be the conformal map of

\Omega
onto the exterior disk
\Delta(0,1)
, such that
\phi(\infty)=\infty
and

\gamma:=\phi^{\prime}(\infty)>0
.
By Carathéodory’s theorem, the map
\phi
extends to a
homeomorphism of
\overline{\Omega}
onto
\overline{\Delta(0,1)}
.

In
[
15
]
, Stylianopoulos proved that if the orthogonality domain
D

is bounded by a piecewise analytic curve without cusps, then
p_{n}
satisfies
a Carleman-type strong asymptotic formula on the exterior domain
\Omega
.
The error term was later improved in
[
1
]
, and the
(sharp) asymptotic formula reads

(1.2)

\displaystyle p_{n}(z)=\sqrt{n+1}\phi^{\prime}(z)\phi(z)^{n}\big(1+\mathrm{O}(n^{-1})\big),\qquad z\in\Omega,

as
n\to\infty
, with the implicit constant being uniformly bounded on closed subsets of
\Omega
.
In particular, (
1.2
) applies whenever
D\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
.
Note that if the piecewise analytic curve
L
is such that an
interior conformal map
\varphi:D\to\mathbb{D}(0,1)
has a singularity on
L

(for instance, if the interior angle
\alpha
formed at a corner is such that
\pi/\alpha\not\in\mathbb{N}
),
then the asymptotic formula (
1.2
) cannot possibly hold on an open set
larger than
\Omega
. Indeed, by Theorem
1.1
every point of
L
attracts zeros of the
polynomials
p_{n}
, and furthermore, the
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{|p_{n}(z)|}

remains constant (equal to
1
) throughout
D
.
However, the opposite turns out to be true when
D\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
.
This is a direct consequence of the following theorem, which is the main
result of this paper.

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume
D\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
. There exists an open set

\Omega^{*}\supset\overline{\Omega}\setminus C(L)

and a univalent function
\Phi:\Omega^{*}\to\overline{\mathbb{C}}
with
\Phi|_{\Omega}=\phi
,
such that the asymptotic formula

(1.3)

\displaystyle p_{n}(z)=\sqrt{n+1}\Phi^{\prime}(z)\Phi(z)^{n}\left(1+\mathrm{O}\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)\right),\qquad z\in\Omega^{*},

holds uniformly on closed subsets of
\Omega^{*}
as
n\to\infty
.

The set
\Omega^{*}
is illustrated in Figure
1
(right side), and will be precisely defined in Section
2
. This set is maximal in the sense
that no formula such as (
1.3
) can
hold on an open set larger than
\Omega^{*}
, since

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{|p_{n}(z)|}

will be shown to remain constant on the interior of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega^{*}
.

In the examples where
D=R_{N}
with
N\in\{3,4\}
, we have
\Omega^{*}=\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Gamma_{N}
and
\Phi

is the analytic continuation to
\Omega^{*}
of the exterior conformal map
\phi
. However,
we do not know whether
\Omega^{*}
is always a connected set, and so we can only say that
\Phi

coincides with the analytic continuation of
\phi
on the unbounded component of
\Omega^{*}
.

Prior to
[
15
]
, asymptotic formulas like (
1.2
) (but with different
estimates of the error terms)
had been established for orthogonality domains
D
that are bounded either by an analytic Jordan
curve
[
2
]
, or by a curve with some degree of smoothness
[
16
]
.
For the class
\mathcal{A}_{2}
of domains with analytic boundary, Carleman found already in 1922

[
2
]
that (
1.2
) indeed holds on the largest domain

\Omega_{\rho}\supset\overline{\Omega}
that admits a univalent continuation of
\phi
with

\phi(\Omega_{\rho})=\Delta(0,\rho)
,
0\leq\rho<1
. More recently in
[
4
]
(see also
[
5
,
6
,
10
]
),
it was found that when
L
is analytic, it is possible to characterize the largest open set

\Omega^{*}\supset\Omega_{\rho}
on which a formula of the form
(
1.3
) is valid.

Notice that a domain
D
is in the union
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{1}\cup\mathcal{A}_{2}
precisely when a
conformal map of
D
onto
\mathbb{D}(0,1)
is analytic in
\overline{D}

(i.e., if the first statement of Theorem
1.1
does not hold).
For
D\in\mathcal{A}
, it turns out that the dominant behavior of
p_{n}(z)
on
D

as
n\to\infty
is given by the integral formula

(1.4)

\displaystyle p_{n}(z)\sim{}

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{n+1}\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{2\pi i}\int_{|w|=1}\frac{w^{n}}{h(w)-\varphi(z)}\,{\rm d}w,\qquad z\in D,

where
h
is the homeomorphism
h:\mathbb{T}(0,1)\to\mathbb{T}(0,1)
given by

\displaystyle h(w):=\varphi(\psi(w))

and
\psi
is the inverse of the exterior map
\phi
.
Since the right-hand side of (
1.4
) makes sense for every Jordan domain, it may well be that
this representation of
p_{n}
is valid also for other domains outside the class
\mathcal{A}
.
However, there is one feature which accounts for the distinguished behavior of the Bergman polynomials
for domains in the class
\mathcal{A}
.
Namely, the fact that
D\in\mathcal{A}
if and only if the function
h(w)
appearing in the denominator of
(
1.4
) is analytic in a neighborhood of the unit circle
\mathbb{T}(0,1)
.
This is the key feature that makes possible to extend the asymptotic formula
(
1.2
) beyond
\Omega
to a maximal subset of the orthogonality domain.

Theorem
1.3
has several implications for the limiting zero distribution
of the Bergman polynomial
p_{n}
for a domain
D\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
.
The most immediate is that for any closed subset
E
of
\Omega^{*}
,
there is an index
n_{E}
such that no polynomial
p_{n}
of degree
n>n_{E}
has a zero on
E
.
As a consequence, the zero limit set
Z
is contained in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega^{*}
,
and the only points of
L
that attract zeros of the
p_{n}
’s are the corners of
L
.

The fact that every corner point of
L
is in
Z
can easily be
seen by applying standard arguments from potential theory.
Indeed, the support
\mathrm{supp}(\nu)
of any measure
\nu

that is a weak
*
-limit of the sequence of zero counting measures

(\nu_{n})
is contained in
Z
, and
\nu
is an
inverse Balayage measure

of the equilibrium measure
\sigma_{L}
of
L
. This means that the logarithmic
potential
U^{\nu}(z)
of
\nu
equals the logarithmic potential

U^{\sigma_{L}}(z)
of
\sigma_{L}
for all
z\in\Omega
.
The potential
U^{\nu}
is harmonic outside
\mathrm{supp}(\nu)
,
and
U^{\sigma_{L}}(z)=\log\phi^{\prime}(\infty)-\log|\phi(z)|
for
z\in\Omega
.
Since
\log|\phi(z)|
cannot be harmonically extended past a corner of
L
,
every corner has to be an element of
\mathrm{supp}(\nu)
.

In a forthcoming work, we will carry out a more comprehensive analysis
on the distribution of the zeros of
p_{n}
and on the structure of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega^{*}
.
Some of the results are similar to those obtained in
[
4
]

for domains with analytic boundary, but others are more specific to the
class
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and a manifestation of the presence of corners on
\partial D
.

To conclude this section, we briefly mention that
the class
\mathcal{A}_{1}
can be characterized purely in geometric terms.
Suppose that
D
is a domain bounded by a piecewise analytic curve
L
,
and that for every corner
z
of
L
, two small sub-arcs
L_{z}^{0}
and
L_{z}^{1}
of

L
that meet at
z
form an interior angle of the form
\pi/m
, with
m\geq 2
an integer.
Applying a Schwarz reflection across
L_{z}^{1}
, we obtain a local holomorphic extension
of
\varphi
to a wedge-shaped region between
L_{z}^{0}
and a new arc
L_{z}^{2}

(the conformal reflection of
L_{z}^{0}
about
L_{z}^{1}
).
Iterating this, reflecting each time
L_{z}^{j}
about
L_{z}^{j+1}
,
we obtain after
2m-1
steps an arc
L_{z}^{2m}
which is tangent to
L_{z}^{0}
.
The domain
D
belongs to
\mathcal{A}_{1}
if and only if
each point of
L_{z}^{0}
is mapped to
itself by this
(2m-1)
-step reflection procedure,
so that in particular
L_{z}^{0}=L_{z}^{2m}
. This is the

reflection invariance
alluded to in the title.

2   Strong asymptotics across
L

2.1  Asymptotic behavior of
p_{n}
on
\Omega

Let
A_{n}(z)
be the analytic function on
\Omega
defined implicitly by the
relation

\displaystyle p_{n}(z)=\sqrt{n+1}\phi^{\prime}(z)\phi(z)^{n}\big(1+A_{n}(z)\big),\qquad z\in\Omega.

As mentioned in the introduction, it was proven in
[
15
]
that if
D
is
bounded by a piecewise analytic curve without cusps,
then
\lim_{n\to\infty}A_{n}(z)=0
and the convergence is uniform
on closed subsets of
\Omega
.
This is a consequence of the inequality (1.9) of
[
15
]
, which asserts
that for some constant
C
,

|A_{n}(z)|\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}(|\phi(z)|-1)}+\frac{C}{n},\qquad z\in\Omega,\quad n\geq 1.

We will make use of a different inequality which provides a better estimate on the rate of decay of
A_{n}(z)
for
z
in
\Omega
and away from the corners of
L
. We use
{\rm d}_{\mathbb{C}}(E,F)
to denote the Euclidean distance between two closed sets
E
and
F
, and when
E=\{z\}
is a singleton, we simply write
{\rm d}_{\mathbb{C}}(z,F)
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose that the boundary
L
of
D
is a piecewise analytic curve without cusps whose set of corners is denoted by
C(L)
. For every
\epsilon>0
, there exists a constant
c_{\epsilon}
such that

(2.1)

\displaystyle|A_{n}(z)|\leq\frac{c_{\epsilon}|\phi(z)|}{n(|\phi(z)|-1)},\qquad z\in\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon},\quad n>1,

where
\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}:=\{z\in\Omega:{\rm d}_{\mathbb{C}}(z,C(L))>\epsilon\}
. In particular,
A_{n}(z)=\mathrm{O}(1/n)
uniformly on closed subsets of
\Omega
as
n\to\infty
.

Though not explicitly stated, the inequality (
2.1
) is to a large extent contained in the proof of
Theorem 1.1 in
[
1
]
. We will briefly go over the details at the start of Section
4
.

2.2  Asymptotic behavior of
p_{n}
on
D

Among the conformal maps of
D
onto the unit disk
\mathbb{D}(0,1)
,
we fix any one of them and call it
\varphi
.

If
D\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
, then
L
is a piecewise analytic curve with corners at the zeros of
\varphi^{\prime}
on
L
. This set of corners is denoted by
C(L)
. In this case, the map
\phi
(and thus its derivative
\phi^{\prime}
as well) extends analytically to
an open set containing
\overline{\Omega}\setminus C(L)
. The derivative
\phi^{\prime}
is integrable over
L
, since

\int_{L}|\phi^{\prime}(z)|\,|{\rm d}z|=\int_{\mathbb{T}(0,1)}|{\rm d}w|=2\pi.

This allows us to define the functions

(2.2)

\displaystyle Q_{n}(z):=\frac{(n+1)\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{2\pi i}\int_{L}\frac{\phi^{\prime}(\zeta)\phi(\zeta)^{n}}{\varphi(\zeta)-\varphi(z)}\,{\rm d}\zeta,\qquad z\in D,\quad n\geq 0.

We denote by
\lambda_{n}
the leading coefficient of
p_{n}
, which is a positive number.
Recall also the notation
\gamma:=\phi^{\prime}(\infty)
. The first step towards extending
the exterior strong asymptotics is the following series representation of
p_{n}
on
D
.

Theorem 2.2
.

The Bergman polynomials
p_{n}
for a domain
D\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
admit the series representation

(2.3)

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{n}}{\gamma^{n+1}}p_{n}(z)={}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}h(n,j)Q_{n+j}(z),\qquad z\in D,\quad n\geq 0,

where the coefficients
h(n,j)
are numbers such that
h(n,0)=1
and

(2.4)

\displaystyle|h(n,j)|\leq\frac{B}{n+j+1}\left(1+\frac{j-1}{n+1}\right)^{B},\qquad j\geq 1,

for a certain constant
B>0
that is independent of
n
and
j
.

Formula (
2.3
) will be deduced from a well-known
connection between the Bergman polynomials and the reproducing kernel of the space
A^{2}(D)
,
that is, from the equality

\displaystyle\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)\overline{\varphi^{\prime}(\zeta)}}{(1-\varphi(z)\overline{\varphi(\zeta)})^{2}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\overline{p_{k}(\zeta)}p_{k}(z),\qquad z,\zeta\in D,

see Subsection
4.2
for details.
In doing so, we make use of some inequalities previously obtained in
[
15
]
.

The dominant term of the series in (
2.3
) happens to be the first one,
and the behavior of the leading coefficient
\lambda_{n}
is known from
[
15
]
:

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}=\sqrt{n+1}\gamma^{n+1}(1+\mathrm{O}(1/n)),\qquad n\to\infty.

With proper control over the error terms, this will lead us to conclude that for
z\in D
,

p_{n}(z)\sim(n+1)^{-1/2}Q_{n}(z)
as
n\to\infty
, which is the same as the integral formula (
1.4
).
This can be seen by making the change of variables
\zeta=\psi(w)
in (
2.2
).

By analyzing the large
n
behavior of the integral in (
1.4
),
we will extend the exterior asymptotics (
1.2
) to a certain maximal open set
D_{1}
of
D
.
The construction of
D_{1}
is carried out in what follows and applies more generally to any domain
D\in\mathcal{A}
. Let us set

L_{r}:=\{z\in\overline{\Omega}:|\phi(z)|=r\},\qquad r\in[1,\infty].

Each
L_{r}
with
1<r<\infty
is an analytic Jordan curve contained in
\Omega
,
L_{1}=L
, and
L_{\infty}=\{\infty\}
.
We denote by
\mathcal{D}_{r}
the component of
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus L_{r}
that does not contain
\infty
.
Then,
\mathcal{D}_{1}=D
and
\mathcal{D}_{\infty}=\mathbb{C}
.

Let
\mu\in[0,1]
be the smallest positive number with the property that
\varphi
extends meromorphically
to
\mathcal{D}_{1/\mu}
. Since, by assumption,
\varphi
extends analytically across
L
, we have
\mu<1
.
Recall that

\psi:\Delta(0,1)\to\Omega

denotes the inverse of
\phi
, which extends continuously to
\overline{\Delta(0,1)}
. The composite

h(w):=\varphi(\psi(w))

is well-defined and meromorphic in the annulus
1<|w|<1/\mu
, extending continuously
to
1\leq|w|<1/\mu
and mapping the unit circle
|w|=1
onto itself.
By the reflection principle (see e.g.
[
3
, Ch. 8]
),
h(w)
can be extended as a meromorphic function to the
bigger annulus
\mu<|w|<1/\mu
, the extension being given by

h(w):=\frac{1}{\overline{\varphi(\psi(1/\overline{w})})},\qquad\mu<|w|<1.

Moreover, it is not difficult to see that
\mu<|w|<1/\mu
is the largest annulus about
the origin that supports a meromorphic extension of
h
.

We next split
D
into subregions, determined by the zeros of the function

\displaystyle f_{z}(w):=h(w)-\varphi(z)\,.

First, we let
D_{0}
denote the set of points
z\in D
for which the function
f_{z}(w)
has no zeros in the annulus
\mu<|w|<1
.
Then, consider a point
z\in D\setminus D_{0}
. Among the zeros of
f_{z}(w)
in the region
\mu<|w|<1
, only finitely many,
say
w_{z,1},w_{z,2},\ldots,w_{z,s}
(
s\geq 1
), will have largest modulus.
Let
m_{z,k}
denote the multiplicity of
f_{z}(w)
at
w_{z,k}
.
For every integer
p\geq 1
, we define
D_{p}
as the set of points
z\in D\setminus D_{0}
such that

\displaystyle m_{z,1}+m_{z,2}+\cdots+m_{z,s}=p.

Thus,
D_{p}
consists of those
z\in D
for which
f_{z}(w)
has exactly
p
zeros in
\mu<|w|<1
of largest modulus,
counted according to multiplicity.

Define next the function
r:D\to[\mu,1)
by

(2.5)

r(z):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}|w_{z,1}|,&z\in D\setminus D_{0},\\
\mu,&z\in D_{0}.\end{array}\right.

If
z\in D_{1}
, then
f_{z}(w)
has exactly one zero in
\mu<|w|<1

of largest modulus, say
w_{z,1}
, and this zero is simple. Let us set

\displaystyle\phi_{1}(z):=w_{z,1},\qquad z\in D_{1},

so that
|\phi_{1}(z)|>\mu
.

One can easily verify by using Lemma
4.1
(see also Corollary 12 in

[
4
]
) that
D_{1}
and
D\setminus D_{0}
are open,
that the map
\phi_{1}
is a univalent function, and that the function
r(z)
is continuous.

Theorem 2.3
.

Assume
D\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
.
The analytic function
A_{n}:D_{1}\to\mathbb{D}(0,1)
implicitly defined by

(2.6)

\displaystyle p_{n}(z)=\sqrt{n+1}\phi_{1}^{\prime}(z)\phi_{1}(z)^{n}\left(1+A_{n}(z)\right),\qquad z\in D_{1},

satisfies that
A_{n}(z)=\mathrm{O}(n^{-1})
as
n\to\infty
, uniformly on compact subsets of
D_{1}
. Moreover,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}|p_{n}(z)|^{1/n}=r(z),\qquad z\in D.

It will be important to provide a more
precise interior bound for
|A_{n}(z)|
near
L
, similar to the exterior bound (
2.1
). We will obtain an inequality of the form

(2.8)

\displaystyle\left|A_{n}(z)\right|\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{M_{1}}{n(1-|\phi_{1}(z)|)}+\frac{M_{1}}{n^{B_{1}}(1-|\phi_{1}(z)|)^{B_{1}}},

valid for all
z\in D
sufficiently close to
L
but away from the corners,
see Section
4.4
below.
Here,
M_{1}
and
B_{1}>1
are some constants that do not depend on
n
.

2.3  Gluing interior and exterior asymptotics

As a consequence of Proposition
4.2
(the statement and proof of which we defer to
Section
4.4
), we have that the set

\Omega^{*}:=D_{1}\cup\overline{\Omega}\setminus C(L)

is open, and the function
\Phi:\Omega^{*}\to\Delta(0,\mu)
defined by

\Phi(z):=\begin{cases}\phi(z),&\ z\in\overline{\Omega},\\
\phi_{1}(z),&\ z\in D_{1},\end{cases}

is univalent. Hence

\displaystyle A_{n}(z):=\frac{p_{n}(z)}{\sqrt{n+1}\Phi^{\prime}(z)\Phi(z)^{n}}-1,\qquad z\in\Omega^{*},

is analytic in
\Omega^{*}
.
The main goal is to show that
A_{n}(z)\to 0
on
\Omega^{*}
.
To prove this, we start from the two
bounds (
2.1
) and (
2.8
), which both degenerate as
z
approaches
L
.
These bounds show that
A_{n}(z)
is small, except for possibly near
L
.
More concretely, we have that
A_{n}(z)=\mathrm{O}(n^{-1}\log n)
on the portion of
\Omega^{*}
outside the shrinking band

\Big\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{C}}(z,L)\leq\frac{1}{\log n}\Big\}

around
L
.
Applying a Phragmén–Lindelöf-type argument in
this narrow band but away from the corners,
we are able to deduce the following key lemma, which immediately implies
Theorem
1.3
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Assume
D\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
, and fix any
z_{0}\in L\setminus C(L)
.
There exists a neighborhood
\mathcal{U}_{z_{0}}\subset\Omega^{*}
of
z_{0}
and a corresponding constant
C_{z_{0}}
such that

\displaystyle|A_{n}(z)|\leq\frac{C_{z_{0}}\log n}{n}\qquad z\in\mathcal{U}_{z_{0}},\quad n>1.

Remark 2.5
.

Our extension of the strong asymptotics across
L
comes at the expense of a
factor
\log n
in the estimate of the rate of decay.
Without too much effort it is possible to improve Lemma
2.4

to give
A_{n}(z)=\mathrm{O}(n^{-1}\log^{\ell}(n))
for any fixed
\ell\geq 1
,
where
\log^{\ell}(n)
denotes the iterated logarithm

\log^{\ell}(n):=\underbrace{\log\log\ldots\log n}_{\ell\text{ times}}.

However, we will not present the details here,
since most likely the loss in precision is artificial.

The asymptotic results presented in this section as well as Theorem
1.3

will be proven in Section
4
. The proofs will rely on several auxiliary estimates
that will be convenient to have established beforehand.

3   Auxiliary estimates

In this section, we make use of some key estimates obtained in
[
15
]
,
and so for the most part we try to follow the notation employed therein.

Let
n\geq 0
be an integer. The Faber polynomial
F_{n}
associated to the map
\phi
is
the polynomial part of the Laurent expansion of
\phi(z)^{n}
at infinity.
Thus,
F_{n}(z)=\gamma^{n}z^{n}+\cdots
is a polynomial of degree
n
whose leading coefficient is
\gamma^{n}
.
Let
E_{n}(z)
be defined by the equation

\displaystyle\phi(z)^{n}=F_{n}(z)+E_{n}(z),\qquad z\in\Omega,

so that for every
r
such that
\Delta(0,r)\subset\Omega
, we have the Laurent expansion

(3.1)

\displaystyle E_{n}(z)=\frac{c_{n,1}}{z}+\frac{c_{n,2}}{z^{2}}+\frac{c_{n,3}}{z^{3}}+\cdots,\qquad z\in\Delta(0,r).

Note that since the map
\phi
is continuous on
\overline{\Omega}
, so is
E_{n}
.
If we differentiate the identity

\displaystyle\phi(z)^{n+1}=F_{n+1}(z)+E_{n+1}(z)

we get

(3.2)

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(z)\phi(z)^{n}=G_{n}(z)+H_{n}(z),\qquad z\in\Omega,

with

(3.3)

\displaystyle G_{n}(z):={}

\displaystyle\frac{F^{\prime}_{n+1}(z)}{n+1}=\gamma^{n+1}z^{n}+\cdots,\qquad n\geq 0,

\displaystyle H_{n}(z):={}

\displaystyle\frac{E^{\prime}_{n+1}(z)}{n+1}=\frac{a_{2,n}}{z^{2}}+\frac{a_{3,n}}{z^{3}}+\cdots,\qquad n\geq 0.

One can verify, see e.g.
[
15
, Lemma 2.1]
, that

\displaystyle H_{n}\in A^{2}(\Omega),\qquad n\geq 0.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that
L
is rectifiable. For integers
n\geq 0
and
m\geq-1
, we have

(3.4)

\displaystyle\int_{L}\phi^{m}(z)\phi^{\prime}(z)\overline{E_{n}(z)}\,{\rm d}z=0.

This is one of three identities stated in Lemma 2.2 of
[
15
]
. We quickly verify its validity.

By (
3.1
), we have
\lim_{z\to\infty}\phi(z)E_{n}(z)=\gamma c_{n,1}\in\mathbb{C}
,
so that we can write
E_{n}(z)=\phi(z)^{-1}E^{*}_{n}(z)
, with
E^{*}_{n}(z)
analytic in
\Omega

and continuous on
\overline{\Omega}
. Making the change of variables
z=\psi(w)

(recall that
\psi
is the inverse of
\phi
) and using that
w=1/\overline{w}
for
w
on the unit circle, we get

\displaystyle\int_{L}\phi(z)^{m}\phi^{\prime}(z)\overline{E_{n}(z)}\,{\rm d}z={}

\displaystyle\int_{|w|=1}w^{m+1}\overline{E^{*}_{n}(\psi(1/\overline{w}))}\,{\rm d}w.

The function
\overline{E^{*}_{n}(\psi(1/\overline{w}))}
is analytic in the unit disk
\mathbb{D}(0,1)
and
continuous on
\overline{\mathbb{D}(0,1)}
. It follows from Cauchy’s theorem that the latter integral equals zero, completing the proof of (
3.4
).

Let us now define the polynomial

\displaystyle q_{n-1}(z):=G_{n}(z)-\frac{\gamma^{n+1}}{\lambda_{n}}p_{n}(z),\qquad n\geq 0.

Note that
q_{-1}\equiv 0
and that
q_{n-1}
is a polynomial of degree
\leq n-1
for all
n\geq 1
.
We also define, for a rectifiable
L
, the quantities

(3.5)

\displaystyle\beta_{k,n}:=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{L}q_{k-1}(z)\overline{E_{n+1}(z)}\,{\rm d}z,\qquad k\geq n\geq 0,

and

(3.6)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{k,n}:=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{L}H_{k}(z)\overline{E_{n+1}(z)}\,{\rm d}z,\qquad k\geq n\geq 0.

Using the complex version of Green’s formula in the domain
\Omega

(see the proof of Lemma 2.3 of
[
15
]
), one finds the alternative expression

(3.7)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{k,n}=(n+1)\int_{\Omega}H_{k}(z)\overline{H_{n}(z)}\,{\rm dA}(z).

In particular,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n,n}=(n+1)\|H_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

One can also verify without much difficulty that

(3.9)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n,n}=1-(n+1)\|G_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}.

It was also proved in
[
15
, Eq. (2.22 )]
that

(3.10)

\displaystyle\beta_{n,n}=(n+1)\|q_{n-1}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}.

Let us now define, for integers
n,k\geq 0
, the quantities

(3.11)

\displaystyle\alpha_{n,k}:=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\overline{\int_{L}p_{k}(\zeta)\phi(\zeta)^{-n-1}{\rm d}\zeta}.

By an application of Cauchy’s theorem, it readily follows that

(3.12)

\displaystyle\alpha_{n,k}=\begin{cases}0,&\ 0\leq k<n,\\
\lambda_{n}/\gamma^{n+1},&\ k=n.\end{cases}

Lemma 3.2
.

If
L
is rectifiable, then we have

(3.13)

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma^{n+1}}{\lambda_{n}}\overline{\alpha_{n,n}}={}

\displaystyle(n+1)\frac{\gamma^{2(n+1)}}{\gamma^{2}_{n}}+\beta_{n,n}+\varepsilon_{n,n},\qquad n\geq 0,

(3.14)

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma^{k+1}}{\lambda_{k}}\overline{\alpha_{n,k}}={}

\displaystyle\beta_{k,n}+\varepsilon_{k,n},\qquad k>n\geq 0.

Proof.

We assume throughout the proof that
k\geq n\geq 0
. We have

(3.15)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\overline{\alpha_{n,k}}={}&\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{L}p_{k}(\zeta)\overline{F_{n+1}(\zeta)}\,{\rm d}\zeta+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{L}p_{k}(\zeta)\overline{E_{n+1}(\zeta)}\,{\rm d}\zeta\\
={}&\int_{D}p_{k}(z)\overline{F^{\prime}_{n+1}(z)}\,{\rm dA}(z)+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{L}p_{k}(\zeta)\overline{E_{n+1}(\zeta)}\,{\rm d}\zeta\\
={}&(n+1)\int_{D}p_{k}(z)\overline{G_{n}(z)}\,{\rm dA}(z)+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{L}p_{k}(\zeta)\overline{E_{n+1}(\zeta)}\,{\rm d}\zeta.\end{split}

By the orthonormality property of the polynomials
p_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{D}p_{k}(z)\overline{G_{n}(z)}\,{\rm dA}(z)=\begin{cases}\gamma^{n+1}/\lambda_{n},&k=n,\\
0,&k>n.\end{cases}

It follows from the latter equality and (
3.15
) that

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma^{k+1}}{\lambda_{k}}\overline{\alpha_{n,k}}={}

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma^{k+1}}{\lambda_{k}}\cdot\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{L}p_{k}(\zeta)\overline{E_{n+1}(\zeta)}\,{\rm d}\zeta+\begin{cases}\gamma^{2(n+1)}/\lambda^{2}_{n},&k=n,\\
0,&k>n.\end{cases}

Thus, the proof of Lemma
3.2
will be complete once we show that

(3.16)

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma^{k+1}}{\lambda_{k}}\cdot\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{L}p_{k}(\zeta)\overline{E_{n+1}(\zeta)}\,{\rm d}\zeta={}

\displaystyle\beta_{k,n}+\varepsilon_{k,n},\qquad k\geq n\geq 0.

Making the substitution

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma^{k+1}}{\lambda_{k}}p_{k}(z)=G_{k}(z)-q_{k-1}(z)

in (
3.16
) yields

(3.17)

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma^{k+1}}{\lambda_{k}}\cdot\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{L}p_{k}(\zeta)\overline{E_{n+1}(\zeta)}\,{\rm d}\zeta={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{L}G_{k}(\zeta)\overline{E_{n+1}(\zeta)}\,{\rm d}\zeta+\beta_{k,n}.

We can now use (
3.2
) and (
3.4
) to compute

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{L}G_{k}(\zeta)\overline{E_{n+1}(\zeta)}\,{\rm d}\zeta={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{L}\phi(\zeta)^{k}\phi^{\prime}(\zeta)\overline{E_{n+1}(\zeta)}\,{\rm d}\zeta

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{L}H_{k}(\zeta)\overline{E_{n+1}(\zeta)}\,{\rm d}\zeta

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{L}H_{k}(\zeta)\overline{E_{n+1}(\zeta)}\,{\rm d}\zeta=\varepsilon_{k,n},

which together with (
3.17
) yields (
3.16
), proving Lemma
3.2
. ∎

Since
\alpha_{n,n}=\lambda_{n}/\gamma^{n+1}
, we get from (
3.13
) that

(3.18)

\displaystyle(n+1)\frac{\gamma^{2(n+1)}}{\lambda_{n}^{2}}=1-(\beta_{n,n}+\varepsilon_{n,n}),\qquad n\geq 0.

This important identity was established in
[
15
, Lemma 2.4]
. It shows that for
L
rectifiable,

(3.19)

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma^{n+1}}{\lambda_{n}}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{n,n}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}},\qquad n\geq 0,

and that

\lim_{n\to\infty}(n+1)\frac{\gamma^{2(n+1)}}{\lambda_{n}^{2}}=1

if and only if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\beta_{n,n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\varepsilon_{n,n}=0
.

The quantity
\varepsilon_{n,n}
only depends on the geometry of the curve
L
via the exterior map

\phi
, while
\varepsilon_{n,n}
still depends on the polynomial
p_{n}
.
It turns out that if
L
is also a quasiconformal curve, then
\beta_{n,n}
can be
uniformly bounded by
\varepsilon_{n,n}
. This observation, made precise in our next proposition,
was first realized in
[
15
, Thm. 2.1]
.

A Jordan curve
L
is quasiconformal provided that there is a constant
M
such that
\diam L(z,\zeta)\leq M|z-\zeta|
for all
z,\zeta\in L
, where
L(z,\zeta)
is the arc of
L
between
z
and
\zeta
of smallest diameter. In particular, every piecewise analytic curve without cusps is quasiconformal. For the definition of the reflection factor
\kappa
of a quasiconformal curve and other details, see
[
15
]
.

Proposition 3.3
.

If
L
is a rectifiable quasiconformal curve, with a reflection factor of
\kappa
, then

(3.20)

\displaystyle 0\leq\beta_{n,n}\leq\frac{\kappa^{2}}{1-\kappa^{2}}\varepsilon_{n,n},\qquad n\geq 0.

The proof of (
3.20
) (take
k=n
in (
3.25
) below)
is based on the following inequality taken from
[
15
, Lemma 2.5]
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Suppose
L
is quasiconformal and rectifiable, and let
\kappa

denote the reflection factor of
L
. For every
f
analytic in
D
and continuous on
\overline{D}
,
and for every
g
analytic in
\Omega
and continuous on
\overline{\Omega}
, with
g^{\prime}\in A^{2}(\Omega)
, we have

(3.21)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{2i\pi}\int_{L}f(z)\overline{g(z)}\,{\rm d}z\right|\leq\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{1-\kappa^{2}}}\|f\|_{L^{2}(D)}\|g^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,.

We will moreover find use for the following result.

Proposition 3.5
.

Suppose
L
is quasiconformal and rectifiable, and let
\kappa

denote the reflection factor of
L
. The following inequalities hold true:

(3.22)

\displaystyle 0\leq 1-(n+1)\frac{\gamma^{2(n+1)}}{\lambda^{2}_{n}}\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\kappa^{2}}\varepsilon_{n,n},\qquad n\geq 0,

(3.23)

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma^{k+1}}{\lambda_{k}}|\alpha_{n,k}|\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\kappa^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_{k,k}}{k+1}}\sqrt{(n+1)\varepsilon_{n,n}},\qquad k>n\geq 0.

Proof.

The inequality (
3.22
) is a clear consequence of
(
3.18
) and (
3.20
).

To prove (
3.23
), we apply the triangle inequality to (
3.14
) to get

(3.24)

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma^{k+1}}{\lambda_{k}}|\alpha_{n,k}|\leq{}

\displaystyle|\beta_{k,n}|+|\varepsilon_{k,n}|,\qquad k>n\geq 0,

and proceed to estimate the right-hand side of (
3.24
).
First, we apply the inequality (
3.21
) to the integral that defines

\beta_{k,n}
in (
3.5
) to deduce that (recall (
3.8
) and (
3.10
))

\displaystyle|\beta_{k,n}|={}

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{L}q_{k-1}(\zeta)\overline{E_{n+1}(\zeta)}\,{\rm d}\zeta\right|

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{1-\kappa^{2}}}\|q_{k-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}(n+1)\|H_{n+1}\|_{L^{2}(D)}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{1-\kappa^{2}}}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{k,k}}{k+1}}\sqrt{(n+1)\varepsilon_{n,n}},

which in view of (
3.20
) yields

(3.25)

\displaystyle|\beta_{k,n}|\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa^{2}}{1-\kappa^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_{k,k}}{k+1}}\sqrt{(n+1)\varepsilon_{n,n}}.

Next, we take absolute values in (
3.7
) and apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to obtain

(3.26)

\displaystyle\begin{split}|\varepsilon_{k,n}|={}&(n+1)\left|\int_{\Omega}H_{k}(z)\overline{H_{n}(z)}\,{\rm dA}(z)\right|\\
\leq{}&(n+1)\|H_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|H_{n+1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
\leq{}&\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_{k,k}}{k+1}}\sqrt{(n+1)\varepsilon_{n,n}}.\end{split}

The inequality (
3.23
) now follows by replacing
|\beta_{k,n}|
and

|\varepsilon_{k,n}|
in (
3.24
) by their upper bounds found in (
3.25
) and (
3.26
).
∎

Having established (
3.23
), we then look for a good estimate for
\varepsilon_{n,n}
.
This is provided by the following result proven in
[
15
, Thm. 2.4]
.

Proposition 3.6
.

If
L
is piecewise analytic without cusps,
then there exists a constant
c_{1}
, that depends only on
L
, such that

\varepsilon_{n,n}\leq\frac{c_{1}}{n+1},\qquad n\geq 0.

As a consequence of Proposition
3.6
, one obtains from (
3.22
) that

(3.27)

\displaystyle\alpha_{n,n}=\frac{\lambda_{n}}{\gamma^{n+1}}=\sqrt{n+1}\big(1+\mathrm{O}(1/n)\big).

as
n\to\infty
.

Corollary 3.7
.

If
L
is piecewise analytic without cusps,
then there exists a constant
C
, that depends only on
L
, such that

(3.28)

\displaystyle|\alpha_{n,k}|\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{k+1}},\qquad k>n\geq 0.

Proof.

Using the estimate afforded by Proposition
3.6
on the right-hand side of
(
3.23
), we can find a constant
c_{2}
, depending only on
L
, such that

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma^{k+1}}{\lambda_{k}}|\alpha_{n,k}|\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{c_{2}}{k+1},\qquad k>n\geq 0.

This and (
3.27
) yield (
3.28
). ∎

We finish this section with a lemma about the asymptotic behavior of the polynomials
G_{n}
that were introduced in (
3.3
). The result is a direct corollary of the asymptotic analysis carried out in
[
12
]
for the Faber polynomials associated to a domain
\Omega
with piecewise analytic boundary.

Lemma 3.8
.

Suppose that
L
is a piecewise analytic Jordan curve without cusps, and let
C(L)
denote the corners of
L
. For every
\epsilon>0
, there exists a constant
m_{\epsilon}
such that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{G_{n}(z)}{\phi^{\prime}(z)\phi(z)^{n}}-1\right|\leq\frac{m_{\epsilon}}{n+1},\qquad z\in\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon},\quad n\geq 0,

where
\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}:=\{z\in\Omega:{\rm d}_{\mathbb{C}}(z,C(L))>\epsilon\}
.

Proof.

In
[
12
]
, fine asymptotic formulas were derived for the Faber polynomials
F_{n}
associated to a domain
\Omega
whose boundary
L
is a piecewise analytic curve without inner cusps (
L
not necessarily a Jordan curve). This is a more general assumption than the one we make in Lemma
3.8
. Let
\lambda\pi\in(0,2\pi)
be the smallest of the exterior angles formed at the corners of
L
. Since
\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{\epsilon}
is a closed subset of
\overline{\Omega}\setminus C(L)
, Theorem 2.4 of
[
12
]
implies that there exists an open set
U\supset\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{\epsilon}
such that
\phi
extends as a univalent function to
U
, and

F_{n+1}(z)=\phi(z)^{n+1}+\mathrm{O}(n^{-\lambda}),\qquad z\in U,\quad n\geq 1,

uniformly on closed subsets of
U
. By differentiating this relation, we find that

\frac{G_{n}(z)}{\phi^{\prime}(z)\phi(z)^{n}}=\frac{F^{\prime}_{n+1}(z)}{(n+1)\phi^{\prime}(z)\phi(z)^{n}}=1+\mathrm{O}(n^{-\lambda-1}|\phi(z)|^{-n}),\qquad n\geq 1,

uniformly on closed subsets of
U
, and the lemma follows since
|\phi(z)|>1
for
z\in\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}
.
∎

4   Proof of the asymptotic Results

4.1  Proof of Proposition
2.1

The inequality (
2.1
) is essentially contained in the proof of
Theorem 1.1 of
[
1
]
,
but the authors restrict attention to the exterior asymptotics away from
the boundary. To obtain Proposition
2.1
we
only need to go through their proof,
paying attention to precisely how their bounds degenerate
as we approach
\partial\Omega
.

In terms of the polynomials
G_{n}
introduced in (
3.3
), the function
A_{n}(z)
can be expressed as

\displaystyle A_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{G_{n}(z)}{\phi^{\prime}(z)\phi(z)^{n}}-1\right)+\frac{p_{n}(z)-\sqrt{n+1}G_{n}(z)}{\sqrt{n+1}\phi^{\prime}(z)\phi(z)^{n}}

\displaystyle=:A_{n}^{1}(z)+A_{n}^{2}(z).

This is the same as
[
1
, Eq. (4.1)]
, if we take into account that we are using a different normalization of the area measure, and that
the functions
f_{n}
in
[
1
]
are precisely defined as

f_{n}(z):=\frac{F_{n+1}^{\prime}(z)}{\sqrt{\pi(n+1)}}=\sqrt{\frac{n+1}{\pi}}G_{n}(z).

Let us fix
\epsilon>0
and let
\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}=\{z\in\Omega:{\rm d}_{\mathbb{C}}(z,C(L))>\epsilon\}
. By Lemma
3.8
, there exists a constant
m_{\epsilon}
such that

(4.1)

\displaystyle|A_{n}^{1}(z)|\leq\frac{m_{\epsilon}}{n+1},\qquad z\in\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon},\quad n\geq 0.

In particular, this implies that

(4.2)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{G_{n}(z)}{\phi^{\prime}(z)\phi(z)^{n}}\right|\leq 1+m_{\epsilon},\qquad z\in\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon},\quad n\geq 0.

In view of the inequality (2.14) of
[
1
]
and the one preceding (4.3) therein, we have the bound

(4.3)

\displaystyle|A_{n}^{2}(z)|\leq{}

\displaystyle c_{2}\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{n,n}}}{\sqrt{n+1}}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\sqrt{\varepsilon_{j,j}}\sqrt{j+1}|\phi(z)|^{j-n}\left|\frac{G_{j}(z)}{\phi^{\prime}(z)\phi(z)^{j}}\right|,\qquad z\in\Omega,

where
c_{2}
is a certain constant, and the numbers
\varepsilon_{n,n}
are those previously introduced in (
3.6
) (
\varepsilon_{n,n}
is called
\varepsilon_{n}
in
[
1
]
and defined by the alternative expression (
3.9
)).

Since
L
is piecewise analytic without cusps, we have in view of Proposition
3.6
(see also
[
1
, Eq. (1.12)]
) that
\varepsilon_{n,n}\leq c_{1}(n+1)^{-1}
for some constant
c_{1}
independent of

. Incorporating this and (
4.2
) into (
4.3
), we find

\displaystyle|A_{n}^{2}(z)|\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{c_{1}c_{2}(1+m_{\epsilon})}{n+1}\sum_{j=0}^{n}|\phi(z)|^{j-n},\qquad z\in\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon},\quad n\geq 0,

which together with (
4.1
) yields

\displaystyle|A_{n}^{1}(z)|+|A_{n}^{2}(z)|\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{\left(m_{\epsilon}+c_{1}c_{2}(1+m_{\epsilon})\right)|\phi(z)|}{(n+1)(|\phi(z)|-1)},\qquad z\in\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon},\quad n\geq 0,

completing the proof of Proposition
2.1
.

4.2  Proof of Theorem
2.2

The Bergman kernel of
A^{2}(D)
is the function
K(z,\zeta)

on
D\times D
which is analytic in
z
and conjugate-analytic in
\zeta
, with finite norm

\int_{D}|K(z,\zeta)|^{2}{\rm dA}(\zeta)<\infty,\qquad z\in D,

and such that for any
f\in A^{2}(D)
, the reproducing property holds:

f(z)=\int_{D}f(\zeta)K(z,\zeta)\,{\rm dA}(\zeta).

This kernel can be expressed in terms of the conformal map
\varphi
of
D
onto
\mathbb{D}(0,1)
by the formula

K(z,\zeta)=\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)\overline{\varphi^{\prime}(\zeta)}}{(1-\varphi(z)\overline{\varphi(\zeta)})^{2}}.

It is well-known that the polynomials are dense in
A^{2}(D)
, and therefore

(4.4)

\displaystyle\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)\overline{\varphi^{\prime}(\zeta)}}{(1-\varphi(z)\overline{\varphi(\zeta)})^{2}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\overline{p_{k}(\zeta)}p_{k}(z),\qquad z,\zeta\in D.

Here it is important to know that this equality actually holds in a bigger domain.
Indeed, for
\zeta\in D
fixed, we can easily see that
K(z,\zeta)
is analytic in the variable
z

in some open set that contains
\overline{D}
. Let
\tau(\zeta)<1
be the smallest number such that

K(\cdot,\zeta)
is analytic in the domain
\mathcal{D}_{1/\tau(\zeta)}
(the sets
\mathcal{D}_{r}
were
previously defined in Subsection
2.2
).
Then (
4.4
) holds for all
\zeta\in D
and
z\in\mathcal{D}_{1/\tau(\zeta)}
.
Moreover, for
\zeta\in D
fixed, the convergence is uniform for
z
on compact subsets
of
\mathcal{D}_{1/\tau(\zeta)}
. We also have

(4.5)

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}|p_{n}(\zeta)|^{1/n}=\tau(\zeta)<1,\qquad\zeta\in D.

These stronger statements follow from the overconvergence results of Walsh, see

[
17
, pp. 130-131]
(see also
[
13
, p. 336]
).

By the Hermitian symmetry of the kernel, for
z\in D
fixed, we have uniform convergence in
(
4.4
) for
\zeta\in L
. We then multiply (
4.4
) by

\frac{\phi(\zeta)^{n+1}\varphi^{\prime}(\zeta)\,{\rm d}\zeta}{\overline{\varphi^{\prime}(\zeta)}\,\varphi(\zeta)^{2}}

and integrate with respect to
\zeta
over
L=\partial D
to get (bearing in mind that
when
\zeta\in L
, we have
\overline{\varphi(\zeta)}=1/\varphi(\zeta)
)

(4.6)

\displaystyle\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{2\pi i}\int_{L}\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(\zeta)\phi(\zeta)^{n+1}}{(\varphi(\zeta)-\varphi(z))^{2}}\,{\rm d}\zeta={}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{p_{k}(z)}{2\pi i}\int_{L}\frac{\overline{p_{k}(\zeta)}\phi(\zeta)^{n+1}\varphi^{\prime}(\zeta)\,{\rm d}\zeta}{\overline{\varphi^{\prime}(\zeta)}\,\varphi(\zeta)^{2}},\qquad z\in D.

Now we note that

\overline{{\rm d}\zeta}=-\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(\zeta)\,{\rm d}\zeta}{\overline{\varphi^{\prime}(\zeta)}\,\varphi(\zeta)^{2}},

meaning that for every function
f
continuous on
L
,

\overline{\int_{L}f(\zeta)\,{\rm d}\zeta}=\int_{L}\overline{f(\zeta)}\underbrace{\left(-\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(\zeta)\,{\rm d}\zeta}{\overline{\varphi^{\prime}(\zeta)}\,\varphi(\zeta)^{2}}\right)}_{{\displaystyle``\,\overline{{\rm d}\zeta}\,"}}.

We then integrate by parts on the left-hand side of (
4.6
) to get

\displaystyle\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)(n+1)}{2\pi i}\int_{L}\frac{\phi^{\prime}(\zeta)\phi(\zeta)^{n}}{\varphi(\zeta)-\varphi(z)}\,{\rm d}\zeta={}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{n,k}p_{k}(z),\qquad z\in D,

where

\alpha_{n,k}=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{L}\overline{p_{k}(\zeta)}\phi(\zeta)^{n+1}\overline{{\rm d}\zeta}=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\overline{\int_{L}p_{k}(\zeta)\phi(\zeta)^{-n-1}{\rm d}\zeta}.

The coefficients
\alpha_{n,k}
are the same quantities previously introduced in
(
3.11
). From (
3.19
) and (
3.28
),
we know that

(4.7)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha_{n,n}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}},\qquad|\alpha_{n,k}|\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{k+1}},\qquad k>n\geq 0,

where
C
is a constant independent of
k
and
n
.
We have also seen in (
3.12
) that
\alpha_{n,k}=0
for
0\leq k<n
, so that we can write

(4.8)

\displaystyle\alpha_{n,n}p_{n}(z)={}

\displaystyle Q_{n}(z)-\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n,k}p_{k}(z),

with
Q_{n}(z)
as defined in (
2.2
).

The identity (
4.8
) is similar to the identity (6.5) obtained in
[
11
]

for polynomials orthogonal with weights on the unit disk. The remaining part of the proof is
essentially a repetition of the steps carried out in the proofs of Lemmas 6.1 and 6.2 of
[
11
]
,
but we will quickly summarize them for the sake of keeping the presentation
self-contained.

The idea is to iterate (
4.8
) to eliminate the polynomials
p_{k}
for all
k\geq n+1
,
and end up with the right-hand side of (
4.8
) written as a series in the
Q_{n}
’s.
The identity (
4.8
) for
n+1
in place of
n
reads

(4.9)

\displaystyle p_{n+1}(z)={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha_{n+1,n+1}}Q_{n+1}(z)-\frac{1}{\alpha_{n+1,n+1}}\sum_{k=n+2}^{\infty}\alpha_{n+1,k}p_{k}(z).

If we now substitute the right-hand side of (
4.9
) for
p_{n+1}
in the series of (
4.8
),
we get a new series where the only polynomials
p_{k}
that remain are those of degree
k\geq n+2
.
Repeating this process
m
times, we obtain (cf. Lemma 6.1 of
[
11
]
)

(4.10)

\displaystyle\alpha_{n,n}p_{n}(z)={}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{m}h(n,j)Q_{n+j}(z)+\sum_{k=n+m+1}^{\infty}g(n,m,k)p_{k}(z),

where the coefficients
h(n,j)
and
g(n,m,k)
are recursively defined by the relations

(4.11)

\displaystyle h(n,0)=1,\quad g(n,0,k)=-\alpha_{n,k},\qquad k>n\geq 0,

(4.12)

\displaystyle h(n,m+1)=\frac{g(n,m,n+m+1)}{\alpha_{n+m+1,n+m+1}}\,,

and

(4.13)

\displaystyle g(n,m+1,k)=g(n,m,k)-h(n,m+1)\alpha_{n+m+1,k},\qquad k\geq n+m+2.

Combining (
4.11
), (
4.12
), and (
4.13
) with (
4.7
) we find

(4.14)

\displaystyle h(n,0)=1,\quad|g(n,0,k)|\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{k+1}},\qquad k\geq n+1,

(4.15)

\displaystyle|h(n,m+1)|\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{|g(n,m,n+m+1)|}{\sqrt{n+m+2}},

(4.16)

\displaystyle|g(n,m+1,k)|\leq{}

\displaystyle|g(n,m,k)|+\frac{C}{\sqrt{k+1}}|h(n,m+1)|,

with the inequality (
4.16
) being valid for
k\geq n+m+2
.

By induction in the second variable
m
, we get from (
4.14
), (
4.15
)
and (
4.16
) that (c.f. Lemma 6.2 of
[
11
]
and its proof)

(4.17)

\displaystyle|h(n,j)|\leq\frac{C}{n+j+1}\prod_{\ell=1}^{j-1}\left(1+\frac{C}{n+\ell}\right),\qquad j\geq 1,

(4.18)

\displaystyle|g(n,m,k)|\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{k+1}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{m}\left(1+\frac{C}{n+\ell}\right),\qquad m\geq 0,\ k\geq n+m+1.

We now write

\displaystyle\prod_{\ell=1}^{m}\left(1+\frac{C}{n+\ell}\right)={}

\displaystyle\exp\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\log\left(1+\frac{C}{n+\ell}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\exp\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\frac{C}{n+\ell}\right)=\left(1+\frac{m}{n+1}\right)^{C}\exp(C\varrho_{n,m})

(4.19)

\displaystyle<{}

\displaystyle e^{C}\left(1+\frac{m}{n+1}\right)^{C},

where

\displaystyle\varrho_{n,m}=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n+m}\frac{1}{k}-\log(n+m+1)\right)-\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k}-\log(n+1)\right)<1.

Replacing the product in (
4.17
) by the estimate (
4.2
)
(with
m=j-1
) readily yields (
2.4
) with the choice of
B=Ce^{C}
.
Similarly, applying (
4.2
) to (
4.18
) we obtain

(4.20)

\displaystyle|g(n,m,k)|\leq\frac{B}{\sqrt{k}}\left(1+\frac{m}{n}\right)^{B},\qquad m\geq 0,\ k\geq n+m+1.

To finish the proof of Lemma
2.3
we just need to show that the second
series in (
4.10
) converges to zero as
m\to\infty
for
z\in D
.
Let us then think of
z\in D
as being fixed. Since

\tau(z)=\limsup_{k\to\infty}|p_{k}(z)|^{1/k}<1,

we can find
\epsilon>0
and a constant
M_{\epsilon}
such that
\tau(z)+\epsilon<1
and

|p_{k}(z)|\leq M_{\epsilon}(\tau(z)+\epsilon)^{k}
for all
k\geq 0
.
This together with (
4.20
) imply that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=n+m+1}^{\infty}|g(n,m,k)

\displaystyle p_{k}(z)|

\displaystyle\leq M_{\epsilon}B\left(1+\frac{m}{n}\right)^{B}\sum_{k=n+m+1}^{\infty}\frac{(\tau(z)+\epsilon)^{k}}{\sqrt{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M_{\epsilon}B(\tau(z)+\epsilon)^{n+1}}{\sqrt{n+m+1}(1-(\tau(z)+\epsilon))}\left(1+\frac{m}{n}\right)^{B}(\tau(z)+\epsilon)^{m}.

In view of the convergence

\lim_{m\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{m}{n}\right)^{B}(\tau(z)+\epsilon)^{m}=0,

we conclude that for
z\in D
, we have

\lim_{m\to\infty}\sum_{k=n+m+1}^{\infty}g(n,m,k)p_{k}(z)=0,

as desired.

4.3  Proof of Theorem
2.3

We begin by making the change of variable
\zeta=\psi(w)
in the integral
on the right-hand side of (
2.2
). This yields

(4.21)

\displaystyle Q_{n}(z)={}

\displaystyle\frac{(n+1)\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{2\pi i}\int_{|w|=1}\frac{w^{n}}{f_{z}(w)}\,{\rm d}w,\qquad z\in D.

We will use (
2.3
) to derive the behavior of
p_{n}
as

from that of
Q_{n}
. Broadly speaking, the dominant behavior of the integral in
(
4.21
) is found by computing the residues of its
integrand at the zeros of

of largest modulus.

We recall that
z\in D_{p}
means that among the zeros of the meromorphic function

f_{z}(w):=h(w)-\varphi(z)

in
\mu<|w|<1
, those of largest modulus, say
w_{z,1},\ldots,w_{z,s}
,
have a total multiplicity of
p
. That is, if
m_{z,k}
denotes the
multiplicity of

at
w_{z,k}
, then
\sum_{k=1}^{s}m_{z,k}=p
.

The following lemma is proven by a standard application of Rouché’s theorem.
It is practically a repetition of Lemma 11 of
[
4
]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
z_{0}\in D_{p}
be fixed, and let
\mu_{0}
be chosen in the range
\mu<\mu_{0}<r(z_{0})
such that
the only zeros of
f_{z_{0}}(w)
in the region
\mu_{0}\leq|w|<1
are precisely those of
largest modulus
w_{z_{0},1},\ldots,w_{z_{0},s}
,
1\leq s\leq p
.
Let
\delta>0
be small enough that the closed disks
\overline{\mathbb{D}(w_{z_{0},k},\delta)}
,

1\leq k\leq s
, are pairwise disjoint, contained in the annulus
\mu_{0}<|w|<1
, and such that

h^{\prime}(w)\not=0
whenever
0<|w-w_{z_{0},k}|\leq\delta
for some
1\leq k\leq s.

Then there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
\overline{\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\epsilon)}\subset D
and
for every
z\in\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\epsilon)
, the zeros of
f_{z}(w)
that lie in
\mu_{0}<|w|<1

are all simple and contained in
\cup_{k=1}^{s}\mathbb{D}(w_{z_{0},k},\delta)
, with each disk

\mathbb{D}(w_{z_{0},k},\delta)
containing a number
m_{z_{0},k}
of them.

It is easy to conclude from Lemma
4.1
(c.f. Corollary 12 in
[
11
]
)
that
D_{1}
and
D\setminus D_{0}
are open, that
\phi_{1}(z):=w_{z,1}
is univalent on
D_{1}
,
and that
r(z)
is continuous on
D
.

In order to prove Theorem
2.3
, it suffices to show that every
z_{0}\in D_{1}

has a neighborhood
\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\epsilon)
such that
A_{n}(z)=\mathrm{O}(n^{-1})
uniformly for
z\in\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\epsilon)

as
n\to\infty
. We then fix
z_{0}\in D_{1}
and pick
\delta
and
\epsilon
as specified in
Lemma
4.1
with the choice
p=1
. In particular, we have

(4.22)

\displaystyle\mu_{0}<|\phi_{1}(z_{0})|-\delta<|\phi_{1}(z)|<|\phi_{1}(z_{0})|+\delta<1,\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\epsilon).

For every
z\in\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\epsilon)
, the function
f_{z}(w)
has a single zero in
\mu_{0}\leq|w|\leq 1
,
say
w_{z,1}=\phi_{1}(z)
, which is simple and contained in
\mathbb{D}(w_{z_{0},1},\delta)
.
It follows that the integrand in (
4.21
) has a simple
pole at
w_{z,1}
and is analytic at any other point of the annulus
\mu_{0}\leq|w|\leq 1
.
We can then apply the residue theorem to deduce that for all

,

(4.23)

\displaystyle Q_{n}(z)=(n+1)\varphi^{\prime}(z)\frac{\phi_{1}(z)^{n}}{f_{z}^{\prime}(\phi_{1}(z))}+\frac{(n+1)\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{2\pi i}\int_{|w|=\mu_{0}}\frac{w^{n}}{f_{z}(w)}\,{\rm d}w.

The fact that

is a zero of

means that
h(\phi_{1}(z))=\varphi(z)
.
By differentiating this identity we obtain

(4.24)

\displaystyle f_{z}^{\prime}(\phi_{1}(z))=h^{\prime}(\phi_{1}(z))=\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\phi_{1}^{\prime}(z)},\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\epsilon).

Since
|h(w)-\varphi(z)|:\mathbb{T}(0,\mu_{0})\times\overline{\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\epsilon)}\to(0,\infty]
is
continuous in
(w,z)
and never vanishes, and since
|\varphi^{\prime}|
is bounded on
\overline{\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\epsilon)}
,
we deduce from (
4.23
) and (

) that

(4.25)

\displaystyle Q_{n}(z)={}

\displaystyle(n+1)\phi_{1}^{\prime}(z)\phi_{1}(z)^{n}+\mathrm{O}((n+1)\mu_{0}^{n}),\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\epsilon),

with the constant involved in the

-term being independent of
n
and
z
.
We now combine (
4.25
) with (
2.3
)
and (
3.27
) to find

(4.26)

\displaystyle\begin{split}A_{n}(z)={}&\frac{1+\mathrm{O}(1/n)}{n+1}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}h(n,j)(n+j+1)\phi_{1}(z)^{j}\\
&+\mathrm{O}\left(\tau_{0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{h(n,j)(n+j+1)}{n+1}\mu_{0}^{j}\right)+\mathrm{O}(1/n)\end{split}

uniformly for
z\in\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\epsilon)
as
n\to\infty
, where
\tau_{0}=\mu_{0}/(|\phi_{1}(z_{0})|-\delta)<1
.

By (
2.4
), we have that if

, then

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|h(n,j)|(n+j+1)|\zeta|^{j}\leq{}

\displaystyle B\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac{j-1}{n}\right)^{B}|\zeta|^{j}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle B2^{B}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\zeta|^{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{j-1}{n}\right)^{B}|\zeta|^{j}\right)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{B2^{B}}{1-|\zeta|}+\frac{B2^{B}}{n^{B}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}k^{B}|\zeta|^{k}.

By increasing

if necessary, we may assume that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(k+1)\cdots(k+B)\zeta^{j}=\left(\frac{\zeta^{B}}{1-\zeta}\right)^{(B)},

one easily verifies that

for some constant

. Therefore,

(4.27)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{|h(n,j)|(n+j+1)}{n+1}|\zeta|^{j}\leq\frac{E_{1}}{n(1-|\zeta|)}+\frac{E_{1}}{n^{B_{1}}(1-|\zeta|)^{B_{1}}},\end{split}

with

.
Since

and

)

.

It remains to prove (

). Fix

. By the very definition of

),

\displaystyle a_{-n}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|w|=1}\frac{w^{n-1}}{f_{z}(w)}\,{\rm d}w,\qquad n\geq 0,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}|a_{-n}(z)|^{1/n}=r(z).

By (

), we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}|Q_{n}(z)|^{1/n}=r(z).

Let

(recall (

)).
We need to show that

\displaystyle r(z)-\epsilon\leq\tau(z)\leq r(z)+\epsilon.

for all

), (

),
and (

\displaystyle|p_{n}(z)|\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}|h(n,j)||Q_{n+j}(z)|

\displaystyle\frac{(r(z)+\epsilon)^{n}}{\sqrt{n+1}}\left(1+\mathrm{O}(1/n)\right),

), (

),

) to find that for all

\displaystyle\alpha_{n,n}|p_{n}(z)|+\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}|\alpha_{n,k}||p_{k}(z)|

4.4  Interior asymptotic estimates near

Let us denote the corners of

\displaystyle\left|A_{n}(z)\right|\leq{}

Proof.

Recall that the function

\rho<|z|<1/\rho

Let us fix
\epsilon>0

), and let
\epsilon^{\prime}:=\epsilon/2
.
We choose
r_{\epsilon^{\prime}}<r^{\prime}<1

r^{\prime}>\rho
.
By the reflection principle, the map

,
so that the composite

(which then coincides with

) is univalent on

.

We now pick
r_{0}
such that
r^{\prime}<2r_{0}-1<1
, and observe that for any such choice,

\overline{U(r_{0},\epsilon)}\subset U(r^{\prime},\epsilon^{\prime})
,
and since

and

\displaystyle\beta_{0}:=\max\left\{|\varphi^{\prime}(z)|/|\phi^{\prime}(z)|:z\in\overline{U_{-}(r_{0},\epsilon)}\right\}<\infty.

We claim that if

, then for all

,

has at most one solution in

.
For otherwise, we are able to find a sequence

and a corresponding sequence

\displaystyle\beta_{1}:=\min\left\{|h(w)-\varphi(z)|:|w|=2r_{0}-1,\ z\in\overline{U_{-}(r_{0},\epsilon)}\right\}>0.

.

.

2.3
,

)

\displaystyle Q_{n}(z)={}

\displaystyle(n+1)\phi^{\prime}(z)\phi(z)^{n}+\frac{(n+1)\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{2\pi i}\int_{|w|=2r_{0}-1}\frac{w^{n}}{f_{z}(w)}\,{\rm d}w.

\displaystyle\begin{split}|A_{n}(z)|\leq{}&\left|\frac{\sqrt{n+1}}{\alpha_{n,n}}-1\right|+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{|h(n,j)|(n+j+1)|}{n+1}|\phi(z)|^{j}\\
&+\beta_{0}\beta_{1}\tau_{0}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{|h(n,j)|(n+j+1)|}{n+1}(2r_{0}-1)^{j+1},\end{split}

which is valid for

On

4.5  Proof of Lemma
2.4

As in the previous subsection, we use
z_{1},\ldots,z_{q}
to denote the corners of
L
.
We need to show that for every
z_{0}\in L\setminus\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{q}\}
,
there exists a neighborhood
\mathcal{U}_{z_{0}}
of
z_{0}

and a constant
C_{z_{0}}
such that
\mathcal{U}_{z_{0}}\subset\Omega^{*}
and

(4.37)

\displaystyle\left|A_{n}(z)\right|\leq\frac{C_{z_{0}}\log n}{n},\qquad z\in\mathcal{U}_{z_{0}}.

We use the notation introduced in (
4.30
), (
4.31
), and (
4.32
).
Let
\epsilon>0
be chosen so small that (
4.29
) is satisfied and

w_{0}:=\phi(z_{0})\not\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{q}\{e^{i\theta}:\theta_{k}-\epsilon\leq\theta\leq\theta_{k}+\epsilon\}.

By Proposition
4.2
, for such a
\epsilon

we can find
0<r<1
such that

(4.38)

\displaystyle\left|A_{n}(z)\right|\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{M_{1}}{n(1-|\phi(z)|)}+\frac{M_{1}}{n^{B_{1}}(1-|\phi(z)|)^{B_{1}}},\qquad z\in U_{-}(r,\epsilon),\quad n\geq 1,

where
M_{1}
and
B_{1}
are independent of
n
and
z
.
By increasing
r
if necessary, we can guarantee that
\overline{U(r,\epsilon)}\subset\Omega^{*}
, so that

(4.39)

\displaystyle\inf\{|\phi^{\prime}(z)|:z\in U(r,\epsilon)\}>0.

The estimate (
2.1
) holds on
U_{+}(r,\epsilon)
, and
|\phi(z)|<1/(1-r)
for all

z\in U_{+}(r,\epsilon)
. In combination with (
4.38
), this yields an inequality of the form

(4.40)

\displaystyle\left|A_{n}(z)\right|\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{M_{2}}{n(1-|\phi(z)|)}+\frac{M_{2}}{n^{B_{1}}(1-|\phi(z)|)^{B_{1}}},

which is valid for
z\in U_{-}(r,\epsilon)\cup U_{+}(r,\epsilon)
and
n\geq 1
, with
M_{2}
some constant.

By (
2.1
), on the level curve

L_{1/(1-r)}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|\phi(z)|=1/(1-r)\},

we have the bound

where the constant

, and in particular, on the set

. This and (
4.39
) imply that for all
n\geq 0
,

(4.41)

\displaystyle|A_{n}(z)|=\left|\frac{p_{n}(z)}{\sqrt{n+1}\phi^{\prime}(z)\phi(z)^{n}}-1\right|\leq\frac{M_{4}}{(1-r)^{2n}},\qquad z\in U(r,\epsilon),

where
M_{4}
is some constant.

For
\eta>0
, the Möbius transformation

J(\zeta)=w_{0}\frac{i-\eta\zeta}{i+\eta\zeta},

maps the real line onto the unit circle, while it takes the lower-half plane
H_{-}:=\{\zeta:\I(\zeta)<0\}
onto
the unit disk
\mathbb{D}(0,1)
, and the upper half-plane
H_{+}:=\{\zeta:\I(\zeta)>0\}
onto
\Delta(0,1)
.
Since
J(0)=w_{0}
, by choosing
\eta
sufficiently
small we can guarantee that
J(\Sigma_{1})\subset V(r,\epsilon)
,
where
\Sigma_{1}
is the open unit square, that is, the set corresponding to
\delta=1
in the definition

\Sigma_{\delta}:=\left\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}:|\R(\zeta)|<\delta,\,|\I(\zeta)|<\delta\right\}.

The set
\psi(J(\Sigma_{1}))
is open, contains
z_{0}
,
and we have the inclusions

\psi(J(\Sigma_{1}\cap H_{+}))\subset U_{+}(r,\epsilon),\qquad\psi(J(\Sigma_{1}\cap H_{-}))\subset U_{-}(r,\epsilon).

Combining (
4.40
) and (
4.41
) with the equality

\displaystyle|1-|J(\zeta)||=\frac{4\eta|\I(\zeta)|}{|i+\eta\zeta|^{2}(1+|J(\zeta)|)},

we find that the functions

u_{n}(t):=A_{n}(\psi(J(\zeta))),\qquad\zeta\in\Sigma_{1},

satisfy the following properties, where
M_{5}
is a certain constant independent of the index
n
:

(i)

u_{n}(\zeta)
is analytic in
\Sigma_{1}
;

(ii)

{\displaystyle|u_{n}(\zeta)|\leq\frac{M_{5}}{n|\I(\zeta)|}+\frac{M_{5}}{n^{B_{1}}|\I(\zeta)|^{B_{1}}}}
,

\zeta\in\Sigma_{1}\setminus(-1,1)
;

(iii)

{\displaystyle|u_{n}(\zeta)|\leq M_{5}a^{n}}
,
a=(1-r)^{-2}
,
\zeta\in\Sigma_{1}
.

If we prove that for every
0<\delta<1
, there is a constant

C_{\delta}
such that

(4.42)

\displaystyle\left|u_{n}(\zeta)\right|=C_{\delta}\frac{\log n}{n},\qquad\zeta\in\Sigma_{\delta},\quad n>1,

then (
4.37
) would hold with the choice of neighborhood

\mathcal{U}_{z_{0}}=\psi(J(\Sigma_{\delta}))
,
\delta<1
.
The assertion (
4.42
) is an immediate consequence of the following proposition.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\{u_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of holomorphic
functions on
\Sigma_{1}
, such that for some numbers
1=b_{1}\leq b_{2}\leq\cdots\leq b_{m}
, it holds that

(4.43)

|u_{n}(z)|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{n^{b_{j}}|\I(z)|^{b_{j}}},\qquad z\in\Sigma_{1}\setminus(-1,1).

Assume, in addition, that for some real number
a\geq 1
, we have the uniform bound

(4.44)

|u_{n}(z)|\leq a^{n},\qquad z\in\Sigma_{1}.

Then for any
0<\delta<1
, there exists
a constant
C_{\delta}
such that

\left|u_{n}(z)\right|\leq\frac{C_{\delta}\log n}{n},\qquad z\in\Sigma_{\delta},\quad n>1.

In particular,
u_{n}(z)
converges to
0
locally
uniformly on
\Sigma_{1}
.

Proof.

Fix
0<\delta<1
, and put

(4.45)

\displaystyle\sigma:=\frac{4(1+\epsilon)}{\pi(1-\delta)}>1,

where
\epsilon>0
is some arbitrary positive number.
For
n>1
, we
denote by
S_{n}
the thin rectangle

S_{n}=\left\{z\in\Sigma_{1}:|\I(z)|\leq\frac{1}{\sigma\log n}\right\}.

For
z\in\Sigma_{1}\setminus S_{n}
, we have
|\I(z)|\geq(\sigma\log n)^{-1}
,
so the bound (
4.43
)
gives

\displaystyle|u_{n}(z)|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\sigma^{j}(\log n)^{b_{j}}}{n^{b_{j}}}\leq m\sigma^{b_{m}}\frac{\log n}{n},\qquad z\in\Sigma_{1}\setminus S_{n}.

It remains to establish a similar bound on the set

\Sigma_{\delta}\cap S_{n}
.
For this, it is in turn sufficient to prove that
for any fixed
x_{0}\in(-\delta,\delta)
,
we have

\big|u_{n}(x_{0}+iy)\big|\leq\frac{C_{\delta}\log n}{n},\qquad|y|\leq\frac{1}{\sigma\log n},

where
C_{\delta}
is a constant independent of
x_{0}
.

The proof will be based on rescaling

\{z\in S_{n}\,:\,|\R(z)-x_{0}|<\delta^{\prime}\},\qquad\delta^{\prime}:=1-\delta,

to a rectangle of fixed (unit) height and growing width,
and then apply a quantitative analogue of the
Phragmén-Lindelöf principle in a strip.
To that end, we denote by
T_{n}
the rescaled rectangle

\displaystyle T_{n}

\displaystyle:=\left\{(\sigma\log n)(z-x_{0}):z\in S_{n},\ |\R(z)-x_{0}|\leq\delta^{\prime}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}:|\I(\zeta)|\leq 1,\ |\R(\zeta)|\leq\delta^{\prime}\sigma\log n\right\},

and consider the sequence
\{g_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
of holomorphic
functions on
T_{n}
defined by

g_{n}(\zeta):=u_{n}\big(x_{0}+\zeta(\sigma\log n)^{-1}\big),\qquad\zeta\in T_{n}.

On the horizontal parts of the boundary
\partial T_{n}
,
the bound (
4.43
) implies that

(4.46)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|g_{n}\left(x\pm i\right)\right|={}&\left|u_{n}\left(x_{0}+\frac{x}{\sigma\log n}\pm\frac{i}{\sigma\log n}\right)\right|\\
\leq{}&m\sigma^{b_{m}}\frac{\log n}{n},\qquad|x|\leq\delta^{\prime}\sigma\log n.\end{split}

In addition, the uniform bound (
4.44
) translates to

(4.47)

\left|g_{n}(\zeta)\right|\leq a^{n},\qquad\zeta\in T_{n}.

We next introduce the auxiliary function

h(\zeta):=\exp\left(-\sqrt{2}\left(e^{\pi\zeta/4}+e^{-\pi\zeta/4}\right)\right).

The function
h
is entire and
|h(x+iy)|
tends to zero rapidly as
|x|\to\infty
.
In analogy with standard proofs of the Phragmén-Lindelöf principle
in a strip, we will use
h
to subdue any growth of

|g_{n}(\zeta)|
in the horizontal direction,
allowing us to apply the maximum principle to
|g_{n}\,h|
in
T_{n}
.
More specifically, for
\zeta=x+iy\in T_{n}
, we have

(4.48)

\displaystyle\begin{split}|h(\zeta)|&=\exp\Big(-\sqrt{2}\cos(\pi y/4)\big(e^{\pi x/4}+e^{-\pi x/4}\big)\Big)\\
&\leq\exp\Big(-\exp\big(\pi|x|/4\big)\Big),\end{split}

where the inequality follows from the fact that
\cos(\pi y/4)\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}

for
|y|\leq 1
together with the elementary bound
e^{t}+e^{-t}\geq e^{|t|}
.

We have
|h(\zeta)|\leq 1
throughout the strip
\{\zeta:|\I(\zeta)|\leq 1\}
,
so (
4.46
) implies that

(4.49)

\displaystyle\begin{split}|h(\zeta)g_{n}(\zeta)|\leq{}m\sigma^{b_{m}}\frac{\log n}{n},\qquad\zeta=x\pm i\in\partial T_{n}.\end{split}

On the vertical parts of the boundary, (
4.48
) and (
4.45
) instead give

\displaystyle\left|h\left(\pm\delta^{\prime}\sigma\log n+iy\right)\right|\leq{}

\displaystyle\exp\left(-e^{\tfrac{\pi\delta^{\prime}\sigma\log n}{4}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\exp\left(-n^{1+\epsilon}\right),\qquad|y|\leq 1.

Combining this with the uniform bound (
4.47
),
we see that the product satisfies

(4.50)

\left|(g_{n}\cdot h)\left(\pm\delta^{\prime}\sigma\log n+iy\right)\right|\leq a^{n}\exp\left(-n^{1+\epsilon}\right),\qquad|y|\leq 1

on the vertical parts of
\partial T_{n}
.
Since the right-hand side of (
4.50
)
tends to
0
faster than
(\log n)/n
,
there exists some constant
C_{\delta}>m\sigma^{b_{m}}
such that

\left|(g_{n}\cdot h)\left(\pm\delta^{\prime}\sigma\log n+iy\right)\right|\leq\frac{C_{\delta}\log n}{n},\qquad|y|\leq 1,\quad n\geq 1.

When combined with (
4.49
),
(
4.50
) gives

\left|g_{n}(\zeta)h(\zeta)\right|\leq\frac{C_{\delta}\log n}{n},\qquad\zeta\in\partial T_{n},

which, by the standard maximum principle, also holds for all
\zeta\in T_{n}
.
In other words, for
\zeta=x+iy\in T_{n}
, we have

(4.51)

|g_{n}(\zeta)|\leq\frac{C_{\delta}\log n}{n|h(\zeta)|}=\frac{C_{\delta}\log n}{n}\exp\Big(\sqrt{2}\cos(\pi y/4)\,\big(e^{\pi x/4}+e^{-\pi x/4}\big)\Big).

Recall that we want to analyze the size of
u_{n}(z)
on the line
\R(z)=x_{0}
,
which corresponds to evaluating
g_{n}(\zeta)
along the imaginary axis.
Invoking the bound (
4.51
) for
\zeta=iy
and
|y|\leq 1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|u_{n}\left(x_{0}+iy(\sigma\log n)^{-1}\right)\right|={}

\displaystyle\big|g_{n}(iy)\big|\leq\frac{C_{\delta}\log n}{n}\exp\left(2\sqrt{2}\cos\left(\pi y/4\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{C_{\delta}e^{2\sqrt{2}}\log n}{n}.

This completes the proof.
∎

4.6  Proof of Theorem
1.3

2.1
) and (
2.6
) that
|A_{n}(z)|=\mathrm{O}(1/n)

A_{n}(z)=\mathrm{O}(\log n/n)
holds near any

away from the set of corners
C(L)
, which is precisely the assertion of Lemma
2.4
.
