Microwave-free wide-field magnetometry using nitrogen-vacancy centers
Joseph Shaji Rebeirro, Muhib Omar, Till Lenz, Omkar Dhungel, Peter Blümler, Dmitry Budker, Arne Wickenbrock

Abstract
A wide-field magnetometer utilizing nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centers in diamond that does not require microwaves is demonstrated. It is designed for applications where microwaves need to be avoided, such as magnetic imaging of biological or conductive samples. The system exploits a magnetically sensitive feature of NV centers near the ground state level anticrossing. An applied test field from a wire was mapped over an imaging area of ≈ 500 × 470 µ ​ m 2 \approx 500\times 470\text{\,}{\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}}^{2} . Analysis of the ground state level anticrossing lineshape allows to extract vector information of the applied field. The device allows micrometer-scale magnetic imaging at a spatial resolution dominated by the thickness of the NV layer (here 50 ​ µ ​ m 50\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$ ). For a pixel size of 4 ​ µ ​ m × 3.8 ​ µ ​ m 4\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$\times 3.8\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$ the estimated sensitivity is 4.8 µ ​ T − 1 4.8\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro T}\text{\,}{\mathrm{}}^{-1} . Two modalities for visualizing the magnetic fields, static and temporal, are presented along with a discussion of technical limitations and future extensions of the method.



I
Introduction

Negatively charged nitrogen vacancy (NV) centers in diamond
Maze2011
have attracted significant attention as a promising platform for sensing various physical quantities, such as temperature, pressure, magnetic and electric fields, at the nanoscale under various environmental conditions
Lesik2019
;
Acosta2010
;
Jarmola2012
. Magnetometry based on spin-dependent fluorescence of these nitrogen vacancy centers has demonstrated sensitivities down to
0.6\text{\,}\mathrm{pT}\text{\,}{\mathrm{}}^{-1}
for ensembles at room temperature
Chatzidrosos2017
;
Barry_2016
;
Barry2023
. By employing optically detected magnetic resonances with nitrogen vacancy centers, one can probe the magnetic fields generated by a wide range of samples, including biological systems, magnetic materials, current-carrying wires, and field progammable gate arrays
Karadas2018
;
Barry_2016
;
Webb2021
;
Lenz2021a
;
Levine_2019
. In optically detected magnetic resonance based magnetometry, a diamond sample is illuminated with laser light (for example, at 532 nm) and is subjected to a microwave field to drive population transfer between the differently bright spin states. When the microwave frequency matches a transition, there is a decrease of fluorescence. Measuring the resonant frequencies for transitions between the
m_{s}=0
and
m_{s}=\pm 1
states allows reconstruction of the magnetic field projection onto the NV axis
Jarmola2012
;
Doherty_2012
;
Ivady2021
.

However, there are scenarios where the use of microwaves is undesirable, such as when dealing with conductive materials or sensitive biological samples. To address this, recent developments have focused on microwave-free protocols that exploit energy-level crossings of nitrogen vacancy centers at different magnetic fields. These protocols include microwave-free magnetometry and vector magnetometry at zero-field, as well as the exploitation of specific features like the excited-state level anti-crossing feature at 51.2 mT and the ground state level anticrossing (GSLAC) feature at 102.45 mT
Wickenbrock_2016
;
Ivady2021
;
Auzinsh2018
. These kind of magnetically sensitive features have been successfully utilized for vector magnetometery, measuring eddy currents in conducting materials and performing nuclear magnetic resonance
Zheng2020
;
Zhang_2021
;
Wood2017
. In light of these advancements, this report presents a microwave-free wide-field magnetic microscope that leverages the ground state level anticrossing feature to probe and image samples where microwaves are detrimental.

The ground state level anticrossing feature originates from the mixing of ground-state levels at 102.45 mT. Due to the Zeeman splitting of the triplet ground state, as illustrated in
Fig.

1
a, the
m_{s}=-1
state becomes degenerate with the
m_{s}=0
state and mixing results in population transfer between these states. Mixing occurs due to hyperfine interaction, cross-relaxation with other defects as well as static and oscillating transversal magnetic fields. The population transfer is observed as a drop in fluorescence intensity. Our study aims to explore the potential of utilizing nitrogen vacancy centers without the need for microwaves to map magnetic fields. We create static magnetic field maps by analyzing the lineshape of the ground state level anticrossing resonance pixel by pixel. Furthermore, in another sensing modality, we investigate the sensitivity to dynamic field changes between individual image frames.

To demonstrate the utility of this magnetometer we map the magnetic field distribution of a direct current (DC) field generated by a straight current-carrying wire with a diameter of
200\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
placed on the diamond sample while we image it from underneath. This proof-of-concept experiment explores the application of nitrogen vacancy centers in a setup without microwaves. The lack of microwaves increases the usability of the device while reducing its technical complexity since no microwave components are needed. The results highlight the viability of diamond-based quantum sensors for a wide range of materials and biological applications.

III
Results and Discussions

III.1
Static Imaging

We demonstrate a static magnetic field imaging modality using the ground state level anticrossing by visualizing the field generated by a wire carrying electric current. The results are presented in
Fig.

3
b
To image , we illuminated the diamond with continuous wave 532 nm laser light with a Gaussian profile. The laser beam covered an area of
\approx 0.5\times 0.47\text{\,}{\mathrm{mm}}^{2}
.

The background magnetic field was swept over a range of
102.45\pm 1.3\text{\,}\mathrm{mT}
, covering the ground state level anticrossing feature while images where taken with the camera. This way the individual pixels contain a magnetic field scan over the ground state level anticrossing feature. The current-carrying wire generates both axial (
B_{\|}
) and transverse components (
B_{\bot}
) in the measurement region, thereby leading to alterations in the contrast (C), full width at half maximum and the center-field position of the ground state level anticrossing feature.

To analyze and quantify the observed changes, we fitted the experimental data pixel-by-pixel with a Lorentzian function
Petrakis_1967
;
Anishchik_2019

to extract three fitting parameters: contrast (
C
), full width at half maximum (
\Gamma
), and center-field (
b
).

To ensure synchronization between the magnetic field ramp and the data acquisition, we employed an external trigger at 185 Hz. This trigger served as a timing reference and triggered the camera to capture 70 frames during a 0.4 s magnetic field ramp.

Additionally, to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio, we repeated the acquisition 20 times and averaged the results.The acquisition procedure, which is similar to that of
Sengottuvel2022
, is illustrated in
Fig.

3
a.

During data analysis, we extract the ground state level anticrossing feature from individual pixels within a specific region measuring
\approx 0.47\times 0.50\text{\,}{\mathrm{mm}}^{2}
. This region comprises a total of
625\times 715
pixels, with each pixel corresponding to an area of
\approx 0.8\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$\times 0.69\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
on the diamond surface. The extracted data are averaged over a
5\times 5
pixels grid. This binning process reduces the optical resolution to approximately
\approx 4\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$\times 3.4\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
which is not limiting the spatial resolution for magnetic fields.

We utilize the Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm
Levenberg_1944
to fit the binned data on a pixel-by-pixel basis to a Lorentzian function. We extract the fitting parameters for each pixel, which provides information about the magnetic field distribution. The fitting of multiple pixels was done in parallel utilizing a threaded Python script. The resulting data-processing time was
136\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
per image. This is still significantly longer than the data acquisition time and requires further improvement. For example, deploying a parallel fitting routine on a graphics card or field progammable gate arrays.

IV
Conclusion

We developed a microwave-free diamond magnetic field microscope utilizing NV centers to measure spatially varying magnetic fields over a field of view of approximately
500\times 470\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
. We analyzed the GSLAC feature and can reconstruct longitudinal and transverse components of magnetic fields of samples. We implemented a fitting algorithm to extract the ground state level anticrossing feature and estimate magnetic fields in an image of
125\times 143
pixels post-binning.

Our results demonstrate the potential of microwave-free vector magnetometry with temporal resolution enabling recording of magnetic “movies”.
We envision that this approach will have a significant impact across a range of fields, including materials science, biology, and condensed matter physics.

It is important to acknowledge certain limitations in achieving high-resolution magnetic maps using the current methodology. One constraint is that only 1/4 of the NV-center orientations are available for this modality due to the requirement of on-axis alignment with the bias field. This leads to an overall drop in signal. Use of preferentially oriented NV centers could enhance the overall contrast and sensitivity
Osterkamp_2019
.

Another limitation arises from the camera, which imposes constraints in terms of its Full Well Capacity (FWC) and frames per second (fps). The mean per pixel shot-noise limit is estimated to be approximately
4.8\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro T}\text{\,}{\mathrm{}}^{-1}
for a pixel size of
4\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
, or
137.35\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro T}
for a volume-normalized estimate.
This can be compared to the best-reported volume-normalized sensitivities of comparable cameras using microwaves (including lock-in cameras) of approximately
31\text{\,}\mathrm{nT}

Kazi_2021
;
Hart2021
.
Considering these limits, future improvements may involve lock-in-based detection to boost sensitivity and pulse sequences for T1 relaxometry
Wood2017
.
