Large-time behaviour for anisotropic stable nonlocal diffusion problems with convection
Jørgen Endal, Liviu I. Ignat, Fernando Quirós

Abstract
. We study the large-time behaviour of nonnegative solutions to the Cauchy problem for a nonlocal heat equation with a nonlinear convection term. The diffusion operator is the infinitesimal generator of a stable Lévy process, which may be highly anisotropic. The initial data are assumed to be bounded and integrable. The mass of the solution is conserved along the evolution, and the large-time behaviour is given by the source-type solution with this mass of a limit equation that depends on the relative strength of convection and diffusion. When diffusion is stronger than convection the original equation simplifies asymptotically to the purely diffusive nonlocal heat equation. When convection dominates, it does so only in the direction of convection, and the limit equation is still diffusive in the subspace orthogonal to this direction, with a diffusion operator that is a “projection” of the original one onto the subspace. The determination of this projection is one of the main issues of the paper. When convection and diffusion are of the same order the limit equation coincides with the original one. Most of our results are new even in the isotropic case in which the diffusion operator is the fractional Laplacian. We are able to cover both the cases of slow and fast convection, as long as the mass is preserved. Fast convection, which corresponds to convection nonlinearities that are not locally Lipschitz, but only locally Hölder, has not been considered before in the nonlocal diffusion setting.

1.
Introduction and main results

We study the large-time behaviour of solutions to the nonlocal diffusion problem with nonlinear convection

(1.1)

\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{L}u+\nabla\cdot(F(u))=0\quad\textup{in }Q:=(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{N},\qquad u(0)=u_{0}\quad\textup{on }\mathbb{R}^{N},

where
\mathcal{L}
is a nonnegative symmetric
\alpha
-stable operator,
\alpha\in(0,2)
,

(1.2)

F(u):=\mathbf{a}|u|^{q-1}u,\quad\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\ |\mathbf{a}|=1,\quad 1-\frac{1}{N}<q\neq 1,

and
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Our goal is to describe the
intermediate asymptotics
, determining the rate at which solutions approach zero, and the limit profile, after scaling the solution to take into account the decay rate. The intermediate asymptotic behaviour depends strongly on the strength of diffusion, measured in terms of the parameter
\alpha
, when compared with convection, measured in terms of the parameter
q
.

1.1.
On the operator
\mathcal{L}

The nonlocal operator
\mathcal{L}
is defined, for smooth functions which do not grow too much at infinity, by

(\mathcal{L}\phi)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Big(\phi(x)-\frac{\phi(x+y)+\phi(x-y)}{2}\Big)\,{\rm d}\nu(y),

under the assumption:

(
\textup{A}_{\nu}
)

\left\{\ \begin{aligned} &\nu\geq 0\text{ is a Radon measure on }\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}.\\
&\text{For some measure }\mu\text{ on }\mathbb{S}^{N-1}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:|x|=1\},\text{ called the \emph{spectral measure}:}\\
&\hskip 22.76228pt\textup{(Polar decomposition)}\qquad{\rm d}\nu(r,\theta)=\frac{{\rm d}r}{r^{1+\alpha}}{\rm d}\mu(\theta)\text{ with $0<\alpha<2$;}\\
&\hskip 22.76228pt\textup{(Nondegenerate)}\qquad\hskip 28.45274pt\inf_{\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}|\xi\cdot\theta|^{\alpha}\,{\rm d}\mu(\theta)\geq\Lambda_{1}>0;\\
&\hskip 22.76228pt\textup{(Finite)}\qquad\hskip 73.12378pt\mu(\mathbb{S}^{N-1})=\Lambda_{2}<\infty.\end{aligned}\right.

Here, by a slight abuse of notation,
\nu(r,\theta)
denotes the measure on
(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
induced by
\nu(y)
and the change to polar coordinates,
r=|y|
,
\theta=y/|y|
.
We then immediately have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\min\{|y|^{2},1\}\,{\rm d}\nu(y)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\min\{r^{2},1\}\,\frac{{\rm d}r}{r^{1+\alpha}}\,{\rm d}\mu(\theta)<\infty,

and hence
\nu
is a
Lévy measure
.

Using the polar decomposition of the measure
\nu
, the operator can be written as

(1.3)

(\mathcal{L}\phi)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(\phi(x)-\frac{\phi(x+r\theta)+\phi(x-r\theta)}{2}\Big)\frac{{\rm d}r}{r^{1+\alpha}}\,{\rm d}\mu(\theta).

Notice that, although we do not impose any symmetry on the measure
\mu
, the operator will be symmetric, thanks to the way we write it using second differences.

Operators of this form arise as infinitesimal generators of symmetric stable Lévy processes
X=\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, which satisfy

\lambda X_{t}=X_{\lambda^{\alpha}t},\qquad\lambda>0,\;t\geq 0;

see e.g.
[
6
, Chapter 1]
.
These processes appear in Physics, Mathematical Finance and Biology, among other applications, and have been the subject of intensive research in the last years from the point of view both of Probability and Analysis; see for instance
[
46
,
63
,
57
]
.

Taking Fourier transform in the definition of the operator
\mathcal{L}
we get

(1.4)

\widehat{{\mathcal{L}}\phi}(\xi)=|\xi|^{\alpha}g\Big(\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\Big)\widehat{\phi}(\xi),\qquad g(\xi)=C_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}|\xi\cdot\theta|^{\alpha}\,{\rm d}\mu(\theta),\qquad\displaystyle C_{\alpha}=\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-\cos t)\frac{dt}{t^{1+\alpha}};

see for instance
[
17
]
.
Thanks to assumption (

A
ν

) we have
\Lambda_{1}\leq g(\xi/|\xi|)\leq\Lambda_{2}
. Hence, the multiplier of the operator
\mathcal{L}
,

(1.5)

m(\xi)=|\xi|^{\alpha}g\Big(\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\Big),

satisfies
m(\xi)\eqsim|\xi|^{\alpha}
(see paragraph
1.7
for an explanation of this and other notations in the paper). This implies that
\mathcal{L}
is nondegenerate, and hence that the diffusion operator is parabolic in all directions.

In the isotropic case,
{\rm d}\mu(\theta)=c\,{\rm d}\theta
for some constant
c>0
, the operator reduces to a multiple of the well-known fractional Laplacian,
\mathcal{L}=(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}
, whose symbol is
m(\xi)=|\xi|^{\alpha}
. This is the only possibility for an
\alpha
-stable operator if
N=1
. However, if
N>1
the spectral measure may be
anisotropic
. Nevertheless, the symbol, and hence the operator, is still homogeneous of order
\alpha
. The spectral measure is also allowed to be singular in some directions (the set of singular directions should have Lebesgue measure zero). As an example, we have the operator

\mathcal{L}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}(-\partial_{x_{j}x_{j}}^{2})^{\alpha/2},

that corresponds to the spectral measure
\mu(\theta)=c_{N,\alpha}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{e_{j}}(\theta)
, where

\{e_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
is the canonical basis in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
c_{N,\alpha}>0
is a normalization constant. The symbol is in this case a multiple of
\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\xi_{j}|^{\alpha}
.

1.2.
Assumptions on the initial data and the convection nonlinearity

Throughout the paper we will always assume that:

(
\textup{A}_{u_{0}}
)

0\leq u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\text{ with mass }M:=\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}.

The boundedness assumption is not essential, since the problem has an

Since we will always consider nonnegative initial data, and the concept of solution for the equation we will deal with has a comparison principle, see Theorem

Moreover, with a change of variables and a change of the measure,

(
\textup{A}_{q}
)

F(u):=\mathbf{a}f(u),\quad\text{where }\mathbf{a}=(0,\ldots,0,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\ f(u)=u^{q},\text{ and }1-\frac{1}{N}<q\neq 1.

The restriction on

, a case denoted in the literature as

Taking all the above into account, problem (

\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{L}u+\partial_{x_{N}}(u^{q})=0\quad\text{in }Q,\qquad u(0)=u_{0}\quad\text{on }\mathbb{R}^{N}.

To write down the definition of entropy solution, we split the nonlocal operator as follows:

\begin{split}&(\mathcal{L}\phi)(x)=(\mathcal{L}^{\leq\rho}\phi)(x)+(\mathcal{L}^{>\rho}\phi)(x)\quad\text{for all }\phi\in C_{\textup{c}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),\ \rho>0,\text{and }x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\text{ where}\\

Definition 1.1

(Entropy solution)
.

A function

is an

of (

) if:

(a)

(Regularity)
u\in L^{\infty}((0,\infty);L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}))\cap L^{\infty}_{\textup{loc}}((0,\infty);L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N}))

(b)

\begin{split}&\iint_{Q}\Big(|u-k|\partial_{t}\phi+\,{\rm sgn}(u-k)\big(F(u)-F(k)\big)\cdot\nabla\phi\Big)\\

(Initial data in the sense of bounded measures) For all

\esslim_{t\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u(t)\psi=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\psi\,{\rm d}u_{0}.

1.4.
Main results

The large-time behaviour of solutions to (
P

q

If

\begin{split}&(\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\phi)(x^{\prime},x_{N}):=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(\phi(x^{\prime},x_{N})-\frac{\phi(x^{\prime}+r\theta^{\prime},x_{N})+\phi(x^{\prime}-r\theta^{\prime},x_{N})}{2}\Big)\,\frac{{\rm d}r}{r^{1+\alpha}}{\rm d}\mu(\theta).\end{split}

Here,

(Large-time behaviour)

Assume (

1.6.
Comments and extensions

Solutions with sign changes.
In this paper we have only considered the case of nonnegative solutions. The case of nonpositive solutions is easily reduced to this one. Sign changes in the solutions offer some technical difficulties, which may be handled if the mass
M
is different from 0 following what was done for the local case in
[
30
,
10
]
. When
M=0
, things become more involved in the diffusion range, since the solutions of the limit problem decay faster than the fundamental solution. As a consequence, the critical line, where the decay rates in the diffusion and convection regimes are expected to coincide, should be different from that of the case
M\neq 0
. This situation in which solutions of the limit diffusion problem decay faster than the fundamental solution was considered for the local case in
[
50
]
. It may be interesting to see whether the techniques in that paper apply to the nonlocal case.

Nonintegrable initial data.
Problem (
1.1
) makes sense for initial data which are bounded but not necessarily in
L^{1}
. What is then the asymptotic behaviour? We still expect a diffusion regime and a convection one. However, in the diffusion regime the behaviour should not be given by a fundamental solution (solutions have infinite mass). If the initial data has a precise power-like nonintegrable decay at infinity, solutions of the purely diffusive problem converge towards a nonintegrable self-similar solution, with a decay that is different from the one of fundamental solutions; see
[
37
]
. This special solution is expected to give the behaviour in the diffusion regime. However, the critical line, where the decay rates of the limit diffusive and convective problems coincide, should change with respect to the integrable case.

Nonhomogeneous media.
It would be interesting to think about nonlocal models involving “non-constant diffusivities”, and to study their large-time behaviour. In the local case, if the diffusivity approaches a constant at infinity, there is an asymptotic simplification towards the problem with diffusivity equal to this constant; see
[
27
]
. In the nonlocal setting things might be very different.

Nonlinear diffusion.
The results in the local case have been extended by several authors to the case in which the diffusion operator is a nonlinear operator of porous medium type,
\mathcal{L}=-\Delta u^{m}
,
m>1
; see for instance
[
53
,
55
,
61
,
62
,
29
,
12
,
4
]
. Two different nonlocal nonlinear diffusion operators of porous medium-type have become popular in the last years,
\mathcal{L}u=(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}u^{m}
and
\mathcal{L}u=-\operatorname{div}(u^{m-1}\nabla(-\Delta)^{-\alpha/2}u)
; see for instance
[
18
]
for the former and
[
9
]
for the latter. Diffusion-convection problems for operators of this kind have only been considered for the second operator in spatial dimension
N=1
by means of entropy methods
[
34
]
. Such methods are not suitable for the first model, hence a different approach is needed.

Other convection nonlinearities.
It should be possible to extend our results to the case in which the convection nonlinearity is not exactly a power, as long as it behaves like one of them for
u\sim 0
; see e.g.
[
30
,
32
]
for the local case.

Multidirectional convection.
It would also be interesting to consider convection nonlinearities
F
in (
1.1
) having different behaviours for
u\sim 0
in different directions. To our knowledge, this situation has only been considered up to now in the local case, for problems involving different power-like convection nonlinearities in different directions, both with linear and nonlinear diffusion, under conditions that guarantee that asymptotically convection acts only in one direction; see
[
33
,
29
]
. The general case is more involved, as pointed out by the recent paper
[
64
]
, which shows that the multidimensional Burgers equation with a Dirac delta as initial data is not well-posed: either the solution does not exist or it is not self-similar. So, even if such an equation enjoys
L^{1}
–
L^{\infty}
-smoothing effects
[
65
]
, we cannot obtain its asymptotic behaviour with the methods developed in this paper.

Nonlocal convection.
The papers
[
44
,
26
,
39
]
study diffusion-convection problems with a diffusion operator of convolution type in which also the convection term is nonlocal. In both cases the involved kernel is integrable with some finite moments. The asymptotic behaviour of models involving singular kernels in both the diffusion and convection terms, and the manner in which they interact, remains to be analysed.

Numerical schemes.
It would be interesting to develop numerical schemes reproducing qualitatively the large-time behaviour of solutions of (
P
). Such issues have been considered in problems involving both local and nonlocal (of convolution type) diffusion operators for instance in
[
43
,
41
,
42
]
.

see (

). Then, integrating the above inequality in the last variable we get

\|\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\varphi\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\eqsim\|(-\Delta_{x^{\prime}})^{\alpha/2}\varphi\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}.

We show now that the map
a(\xi)=|\xi^{\prime}|^{\alpha}/|\xi|^{\alpha}
is an
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
multiplier for
1<p<\infty
. Indeed, since
a\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\})
and
a
is homogenous of order zero,
a(\lambda\xi)=a(\xi)
, we can apply Marcinkiewicz’s multiplier theorem (see
[
35
, Th. 5.2.4]
and the comments on page 366 of the same reference) to obtain:

(2.9)

\|\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\varphi\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\lesssim\|(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}\varphi\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\lesssim\|\varphi\|_{H^{\alpha,p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\quad\text{for all }\varphi\in H^{\alpha,p}(\mathbb{R}^{N}).

2.4.
Examples

As a first example, we consider the case in which the spectral measure
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue’s surface measure on
\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
,
\sigma_{N-1}
, so that
{\rm d}\mu(\theta)=h(\theta)\,{\rm d}\sigma_{N-1}(\theta)
for some density function
h\in L^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{N-1})
. Then the new measure
\tilde{\mu}
is given by

{\rm d}\tilde{\mu}(\sigma)=\Big(\sum_{\pm}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{s^{N-2+\alpha}}{(1-s^{2})^{1/2}}h(s\sigma,\pm(1-s^{2})^{1/2})\,{\rm d}s\Big)\,{\rm d}\sigma_{N-2}(\sigma),\quad\sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{N-2}.

As a second example, we consider the spectral measure
\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{a_{j}}
, which is concentrated at the points
\{a_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
. We assume that the hyperplane generated by these points does not contain the origin, so that the nondegeneracy condition in hypothesis (

A
ν

) is satisfied.
In this case

\tilde{\mu}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}|Pa_{k}|^{\alpha}\delta_{\frac{Pa_{k}}{|Pa_{k}|}},

where
Pa
denotes the projection of the point
a\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
onto its first
N-1
components. Note that if
a
is the north or south pole the corresponding term vanishes. Thus, if

\mathcal{L}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}(-\partial_{x_{j}x_{j}}^{2})^{\alpha/2},

an operator that corresponds to the spectral measure
\mu=c_{\alpha}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{e_{j}}
, where

\{e_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
is the canonical basis in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the measure corresponding to the projected operator is
\tilde{\mu}=c_{\alpha}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\delta_{e_{j}}
, and hence
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}(-\partial_{x_{j}x_{j}}^{2})^{\alpha/2}
.

Proof.

It follows the lines of
[
45
, Proposition 3.4]
. Lemma
3.1
gives that
\tilde{\varepsilon}\leq u\leq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}
and

Let us denote

where
\gamma\in(0,1]
will be chosen latter. Then

and

\partial_{t}z+\gamma z^{1-\frac{1}{\gamma}}\mathcal{L}(z^{\frac{1}{\gamma}})+qz^{\frac{q-1}{\gamma}}\partial_{x_{N}}z=\varepsilon\Big(\Delta z+\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}\frac{|\nabla z|^{2}}{z}\Big).

Let

and
\beta=\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}\geq 0
. Then
w\in C^{\infty}_{\textup{b}}(Q)
and

(3.4)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w+\frac{q(q-1)}{\gamma}z^{\frac{q-1-\gamma}{\gamma}}w^{2}+qz^{\frac{q-1}{\gamma}}\partial_{x_{N}}w+z^{-\frac{1}{\gamma}}A_{\beta}(w,z)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\Big(\Delta w-\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}\frac{|\nabla z|^{2}}{z^{2}}w+\frac{2(1-\gamma)}{\gamma}\frac{\nabla z\cdot\nabla w}{z}\Big),

\displaystyle\text{where }A_{\beta}(w,z)=z\mathcal{L}(z^{\beta}w)-\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}w\mathcal{L}(z^{1+\beta}).

Let us denote

. If
W(t)
is attained at some point
\overline{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

one can prove that

A_{\beta}(w(t,\overline{x}),z(t,\overline{x}))\geq w(t,\overline{x})\mathcal{I}_{z}(t,\overline{x})

for some nonnegative function
\mathcal{I}_{z}
. However, it may happen that for a fixed
t
the supremum is not attained, and we follow the strategy in
[
25
, Theorem 2, Remark 3]
and
[
49
, Lemma 1.17]
.
We consider points

such that

(3.5)

W(t)\geq w(t,x_{n})\geq W(t)-\frac{1}{n}.

Since

, it follows that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\nabla w(t,x_{n})=0,\qquad\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Delta w(t,x_{n})\leq 0.

Besides,
z(t,x_{n})\in[\tilde{\varepsilon}^{\gamma},\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}^{\gamma}]
for all

. Hence,
z(t,x_{n})\rightarrow p_{1}(t)\in[\tilde{\varepsilon}^{\gamma},\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}^{\gamma}]
up to a subsequence. Also, since

|\nabla z(t,x_{n})|\rightarrow p_{2}(t)
, again up to a subsequence.
We now make a claim which is proved some paragraphs below: once more up to a subsequence,

(3.6)

A_{\beta}(w(t,x_{n}),z(t,x_{n}))\geq W(t)\mathcal{I}_{n}(t)-o(1)\quad\text{as }n\to\infty

for some uniformly bounded nonnegative sequence
\mathcal{I}_{n}(t)
. We therefore also have
\mathcal{I}_{n}(t)\rightarrow p_{3}(t)\geq 0
up to a subsequence. Arguing as in
[
45
, Proposition 3.4]
, we obtain that

W^{\prime}(t)+\frac{q(q-1)}{\gamma}p_{1}^{\frac{q-1-\gamma}{\gamma}}(t)W^{2}(t)+p_{1}^{-\frac{1}{\gamma}}(t)p_{3}(t)W(t)+\frac{\varepsilon(1-\gamma)}{\gamma}\frac{p^{2}_{2}(t)}{p_{1}^{2}(t)}W(t)\leq 0\quad\text{for all }t>0.

Looking for supersolutions of the type

we get for

The choice

then gives (
3.2

Let us introduce
\tilde{w}=-w
. Using (
3.4

satisfies

\begin{gathered}\partial_{t}\tilde{w}+\frac{q(1-q)}{\gamma}z^{\frac{q-1-\gamma}{\gamma}}\tilde{w}^{2}+qz^{\frac{q-1}{\gamma}}\partial_{x_{N}}\tilde{w}+z^{-\frac{1}{\gamma}}A_{\beta}(\tilde{w},z)\\
=\varepsilon\Big(\Delta\tilde{w}-\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}\frac{|\nabla z|^{2}}{z^{2}}\tilde{w}+\frac{2(1-\gamma)}{\gamma}\frac{\nabla z\cdot\nabla\tilde{w}}{z}\Big).\end{gathered}

The same argument as above gives us, for

, in particular

\tilde{w}(t,x)\leq\frac{\gamma}{1-q}\frac{\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}^{\gamma-(q-1)}}{qt}\quad\text{for all }(t,x)\in Q.

The choice

then gives (
3.3

It remains to prove claim (
3.6

and

yields

\displaystyle A_{\beta}(w(t,x),

\displaystyle z(t,x))=\mathcal{J}^{>}(t,x,R)+\mathcal{J}^{<}(t,x,R),\quad\text{where }

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{>}(t,x,R)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}\int_{R}^{\infty}\Big[\frac{w(x)}{\beta+1}\Big(z^{\beta+1}(x)+\frac{\beta}{2}\big(z^{\beta+1}(x+r\theta)+z^{\beta+1}(x-r\theta)\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=z(x)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}\int_{0}^{R}\Big(z^{\beta}(x)w(x)-\frac{z^{\beta}(x+r\theta)w(x+r\theta)+z^{\beta}(x-r\theta)w(x-r\theta)}{2}\Big)\frac{{\rm d}r}{r^{1+\alpha}}{\rm d}\mu(\theta)

Using the strong convergence for the first term and the dominated convergence theorem for the last one we obtain that the right-hand side goes to zero as

.

As for the nonlinear term, if

, so that

\displaystyle\lambda^{-c(q,\alpha,N)}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|F(u_{\lambda})|

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-c(q,\alpha,N)}\int_{0}^{T}\|u_{\lambda}(t)\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}^{q}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{-c(q,\alpha,N)}\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda t}\Big)^{\frac{N}{\alpha}(q-1)}\,{\rm d}t\rightarrow 0\quad\text{as }\lambda\to\infty

when

, using Hölder’s inequality with exponents

and

and the conservation of mass,

We consider now the case

, for which

.
Observe that, on

, both

and

A.2
, we have

\begin{split}&\iint_{Q}|u_{\lambda}-k||\mathcal{L}^{\lambda,\leq\rho}\phi-\mathcal{L}^{\prime,\leq\rho}\phi|\leq\iint_{Q}|u_{\lambda}||\mathcal{L}^{\lambda,\leq\rho}\phi-\mathcal{L}^{\prime,\leq\rho}\phi|+|k|\iint_{Q}|\mathcal{L}^{\lambda,\leq\rho}\phi-\mathcal{L}^{\prime,\leq\rho}\phi|\\
&\qquad\leq MT\|\mathcal{L}^{\lambda,\leq\rho}\phi-\mathcal{L}^{\prime,\leq\rho}\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}+|k|\|\mathcal{L}^{\lambda,\leq\rho}\phi-\mathcal{L}^{\prime,\leq\rho}\phi\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\lesssim\lambda^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-\beta}.\end{split}

Then, by

\begin{split}\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big||u_{\lambda}-k|&-|U-k|\big||\mathcal{L}^{\prime,\leq\rho}\phi|\leq\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u_{\lambda}-U||\mathcal{L}^{\prime,\leq\rho}\phi|,\end{split}

we obtain that

\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u_{\lambda}-k|(\mathcal{L}^{\lambda,\leq\rho}\phi)\rightarrow\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|U-k|(\mathcal{L}^{\prime,\leq\rho}\phi)\quad\text{as }\lambda\to\infty.

Let us now consider the term

I_{\lambda}=\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\,{\rm sgn}(u_{\lambda}-k)\phi(\mathcal{L}^{\lambda,>\rho}u_{\lambda}).

We will prove that
I_{\lambda}\rightarrow I
as
\lambda\to\infty
, where

I=\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\,{\rm sgn}(U-k)\phi(\mathcal{L}^{\prime,>\rho}U).

The main ingredients are the following two uniform estimates in
\lambda
:

\|\mathcal{L}^{\lambda,>\rho}\phi\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\lesssim\rho^{-\alpha}\|\phi\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})},\ \|\mathcal{L}^{\lambda,>\rho}\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\lesssim\rho^{-\alpha}\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})},

which are easily obtained by Fubini’s theorem. We write the difference as
I_{1,\lambda}+I_{2,\lambda}+I_{3,\lambda}
where

\displaystyle I_{1,\lambda}

\displaystyle=\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\,{\rm sgn}(u_{\lambda}-k)\phi\big(\mathcal{L}^{\lambda,>\rho}u_{\lambda}-\mathcal{L}^{\lambda,>\rho}U\big),

\displaystyle I_{2,\lambda}

\displaystyle=\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big(\,{\rm sgn}(u_{\lambda}-k)-\,{\rm sgn}(U-k)\big)\phi(\mathcal{L}^{\lambda,>\rho}U),

\displaystyle I_{3,\lambda}

\displaystyle=\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\,{\rm sgn}(U-k)\phi\big(\mathcal{L}^{\lambda,>\rho}U-\mathcal{L}^{\prime,>\rho}U\big).

The first one goes to zero by using the strong
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
-convergence on
(\tau,T)
:

|I_{1,\lambda}|\lesssim\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\rho^{-\alpha}\int_{\tau}^{T}\|u_{\lambda}(t)-U(t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\,{\rm d}t\rightarrow 0\quad\text{as }\lambda\to\infty.

As for
I_{2,\lambda}
, we use
\|\mathcal{L}^{\lambda,>\rho}U\|_{L^{\infty}((\tau,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{N})}\lesssim\rho^{-\alpha}\|U\|_{L^{\infty}((\tau,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{N})}\lesssim C(\tau)
and the dominated convergence theorem, since
u_{\lambda}\rightarrow U
for a.e.
(t,x)
in

Q
. For
I_{3,\lambda}
, since
U(t)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, we use Lemma
A.1
to get
\mathcal{L}^{\lambda,>\rho}U(t)\rightarrow\mathcal{L}^{\prime,>\rho}U(t)
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, and the same holds for the whole time interval
(\tau,T)
.

We end this step by putting together all the above convergences to get that the limit profile
U
satisfies for all
k\in\mathbb{R}
, all
\rho>0
, and all
0\leq\phi\in C_{\textup{c}}^{\infty}(Q)
,

\begin{split}&\iint_{Q}\Big(|U-k|\partial_{t}\phi+\,{\rm sgn}(U-k)\big(F(U)-F(k)\big)\cdot\nabla\phi\Big)\\
&\qquad-\iint_{Q}\Big(\,{\rm sgn}(U-k)\phi(\mathcal{L}^{\prime,>\rho}U)+|U-k|(\mathcal{L}^{\prime,\leq\rho}\phi)\Big)\geq 0,\end{split}

i.e.,
U
solves (
1.13
) according to Definition
1.1
(b).

(iii)
Identification of the initial data.
Let us recall that
u_{\lambda}
is not only an entropy solution but also a very weak solution, i.e., for all
\phi\in C_{\textup{c}}^{\infty}(\overline{Q})
,

\iint_{Q}\Big(u_{\lambda}\partial_{t}\phi+u_{\lambda}^{q}\partial_{x_{N}}\phi-u_{\lambda}(\mathcal{L}^{\lambda}\phi)\Big)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u_{0,\lambda}\phi(0)=0.

As in the supercritical/critical case, we have that for all test functions
\psi\in C_{\textup{c}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u_{\lambda}(t)\psi-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u_{0,\lambda}\psi\bigg|\leq\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Big(|u_{\lambda}|^{q}|\partial_{x_{N}}\psi|+|u_{\lambda}||\mathcal{L}^{\lambda}\psi|\Big).

The estimate in (
4.10
) gives
\|\mathcal{L}^{\lambda}\psi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\lesssim\|\psi\|_{W^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}
. Thus
the same arguments as in the proof of the tail control in Proposition
4.4
(f) show that

\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u_{\lambda}(t)\psi-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u_{0,\lambda}\psi\bigg|\leq C(\varphi)(t+t^{a(\alpha,q,N)}).

By letting
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
, we get

\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}U(t)\psi-M\psi(0)\bigg|\leq C(\varphi)(t+t^{a(\alpha,q,N)})\quad\text{for a.e. }t>0,

which is then Definition
1.1
(c) for all function
\psi\in C_{\textup{c}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. To extend this result to all
\psi\in C_{\textup{b}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, we first obtain the tail control for the limit
U
. Letting
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
in
(
4.8
) gives us that
U
satisfies

\int_{|x|>2R}|U(t)|\lesssim\frac{t}{R^{\alpha}}+\frac{t^{a(\alpha,q,N)}}{R^{b(q,N)}}\quad\text{for a.e. }t>0\text{ if }R>0.

As in the supercritical/critical case,

\esslim_{t\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}U(t)\psi=M\psi(0)\quad\text{for all }\psi\in C_{\textup{b}}(\mathbb{R}^{N}).\qed

5.
Uniqueness of the limit problems

This section is devoted to prove that the kind of problems that may appear as limits of rescaled solutions have, under certain assumptions on
q
and
N
, a unique fundamental solution with mass
M
. This is the content of Theorem
1.5
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

(a) This was already proved in
[
17
, Section 3]
.

(b) The proof is very technical. Hence, we divide it into several steps. Assume that (
1.12
) has two entropy solutions
u,\overline{u}\in L^{\infty}((0,\infty);L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}))\cap L^{\infty}_{\textup{loc}}((0,\infty);L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N}))
with the same initial data
M\delta_{0}
.

(i)
Integrating in the direction
x_{N}
.
Using the ideas of the proof of Lemma
3.7
, it is easy to check that

(5.1)

v(t,x^{\prime}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(t,x^{\prime},x_{N})\,{\rm d}x_{N}\quad\textup{for a.e. }(t,x^{\prime})\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{N-1}

belongs to
L^{\infty}((0,\infty);L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N-1}))
and is the unique very weak solution of
\partial_{t}v+\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}v=0
in
(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
with initial data
M\delta_{0}
in the sense of bounded measures, where
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is the
(N-1)
-dimensional
\alpha
-stable operator introduced in (
2.2
). Thus,
v=M\Phi
, where
\Phi
is the unique fundamental solution of the equation with mass 1, which has a self-similar form,

\Phi(t,x^{\prime})=t^{-\frac{N-1}{\alpha}}\mathcal{G}(t^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}x^{\prime})\quad\textup{for all }(t,x^{\prime})\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{N-1}

for some smooth positive profile
\mathcal{G}
such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\mathcal{G}=1
; see
[
17
]
.
In terms of
u
and
\overline{u}
, we then have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(t,x^{\prime},x_{N})\,{\rm d}x_{N}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\overline{u}(t,x^{\prime},x_{N})\,{\rm d}x_{N}=M\Phi(t,x^{\prime})\quad\textup{for all }(t,x^{\prime})\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{N-1}.

(ii)
Approximation by entropy solutions with initial data in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.
We start by constructing the initial data for our approximations.
Inspired by
[
31
]
, for each
r>0
, we define

\begin{gathered}\varphi_{r}(t,x^{\prime})=\int_{-r}^{r}u(t,x^{\prime},x_{N})\,{\rm d}x_{N}-M\Phi(t,x^{\prime})=-\int_{|x_{N}|>r}u(t,x^{\prime},x_{N})\,{\rm d}x_{N},\\
u^{r}_{0,n}(x^{\prime},x_{N}):=\big(u(1/n,x^{\prime},x_{N})-\frac{1}{2r}\varphi_{r}(1/n,x^{\prime})\big)\mathds{1}_{(-r,r)}(x_{N}).\end{gathered}

Notice that
-M\Phi(t,x^{\prime})\leq\varphi_{r}(t,x^{\prime})\leq 0
. Hence, the functions
u^{r}_{0,n}
are nonnegative and bounded,

\|u^{r}_{0,n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\leq\|u(1/n)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}+\frac{M}{2r}\|\Phi(1/n)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N-1})}.

On the other hand, they have integral
M
since

(5.2)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{r}_{0,n}(x^{\prime},x_{N})\,{\rm d}x_{N}=M\Phi(1/n,x^{\prime})\quad\text{for a.e. }x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}.

Let
u_{n}
(we omit the dependence on the parameter
r
for simplicity) be the entropy solution of (
1.12
) with initial data
u_{0,n}^{r}
. Let us prove that, for any
t>0
, the sequence
u_{n}(t)
converges to
u(t)
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
as
n\to\infty
. To this aim we observe that both
u_{n}
and
u(\cdot+1/n)
are entropy solutions of (
1.12
), whose respective initial data,
u_{0,n}^{r}
and
u(1/n)
, belong to
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Then the
L^{1}
-contraction property, Theorem
B.7
(a)(ii), shows that

\displaystyle\big\|u_{n}(t)-u\big(t+1/n\big)\big\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}

\displaystyle\leq\|u^{r}_{0,n}-u(1/n)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}}\bigg(\int_{|x_{N}|>r}u(1/n,x^{\prime},x_{N})\,{\rm d}x_{N}+|\varphi_{r}(1/n,x^{\prime})|\bigg)\,{\rm d}x^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}}\int_{|x_{N}|>r}u(1/n,x^{\prime},x_{N})\,{\rm d}x_{N}{\rm d}x^{\prime}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{as $n\rightarrow\infty$.}

Since
u\in C((0,\infty);L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}))
(see Remark
1.2
), we have that
u(t+1/n)
converges to
u(t)
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Therefore,
u_{n}(t)\rightarrow u(t)
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. The contraction property implies that
u_{n}\rightarrow u
in
C([\tau,\infty);L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}))
as
n\rightarrow\infty
for all
\tau>0
.

Defining
\overline{u}_{0,n}^{r}
and then
\overline{u}_{n}
in a similar way, we get by the same reasoning that
\overline{u}_{n}(t)\rightarrow\overline{u}(t)
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

(iii)
Approximation by classical solutions.
If
q>1
, let
u_{n}^{\varepsilon}
be the solution of the regularized problem (

P
ε

) with initial data
u_{0,n}^{r}
and nonlinearity
f_{0}(u)=u^{q}
. If
q<1
, we consider the same problem but with nonlinearity

(5.3)

f_{\eta}(u)=(u^{2}+\eta)^{q/2}-\eta^{q/2},\quad\eta>0.

The corresponding solution will depend on
\varepsilon
and
\eta
but for simplicity
we still denote it by
u_{n}^{\varepsilon}
since
\eta=\eta_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow 0
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. Note that
f_{\eta}
is
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and satisfies the inequality

(5.4)

0\leq f_{\eta}(s)=(s^{2}+\eta)^{q/2}-\eta^{q/2}\leq s^{q},\quad\text{for all $s\geq 0$.}

The regularity results in Proposition
B.19
show that
u_{n}^{\varepsilon}
is a classical solution of (

P
ε

),
u_{n}^{\varepsilon}\in C^{1+\delta,2+2\delta}(Q)
. Besides, Lemma
4.3
and Lemma
4.5
with
\lambda=1
, proved in Sections
B.5
–
B.6
, and Lemma
B.17
show that
u_{n}^{\varepsilon}\rightarrow u_{n}
in
C((0,\infty);L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}))
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
. Hence, in view of the previous step,
u_{n}^{\varepsilon}\rightarrow u
in
C([\tau,\infty);L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}))
for all
\tau>0
. A similar result holds for
\overline{u}_{n}^{\varepsilon}
, which is defined analogously.

(iv)
The primitives are bounded solutions of the Hamilton-Jacobi equation.
It is easily checked that

v_{n}^{\varepsilon}(t,x^{\prime},x_{N}):=\int_{-\infty}^{x_{N}}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t,x^{\prime},y_{N})\,{\rm d}y_{N}\quad\text{for all }(t,x^{\prime},x_{N})\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\times\mathbb{R}

is a nonnegative classical solution of the Hamilton-Jacobi equation

(5.5)

\partial_{t}v+\mathcal{L}v-\varepsilon\Delta v=f_{\eta}(\partial_{x_{N}}v)\quad\text{in }Q.

Let us prove that it is bounded. Indeed, since
u^{\varepsilon}_{n}\in C([0,\infty);L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}))
is nonnegative,

(5.6)

v_{n}^{\varepsilon}(t,x^{\prime},x_{N})\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t,x^{\prime},y_{N})\,{\rm d}y_{N}=:w_{\varepsilon}^{n}(t,x^{\prime}),

where, by Lemma
3.7
(which also holds in the case of
f_{\eta}
, see Remark
B.18
) and (
5.2
), the function
w_{\varepsilon}^{n}
belongs to
C([0,\infty);L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N-1}))
and solves

(5.7)

\partial_{t}w+\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}w-\varepsilon\Delta_{x^{\prime}}w=0\quad\text{in $(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{N-1}$,}\qquad w(0)=M\Phi(1/n)\quad\text{on $\mathbb{R}^{N-1}$.}

The solution of this problem is
w_{\varepsilon}^{n}(t)=M\Phi(t)\ast G_{\varepsilon t}\ast\Phi(\frac{1}{n})
, where
G_{t}
is the classical Gaussian heat kernel. Hence,

\|w_{\varepsilon}^{n}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N-1})}\leq M\|\Phi(1/n)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N-1})}\leq Mc_{n}
for some positive constant
c_{n}
, and
v^{\varepsilon}_{n}
is bounded. The same results, with the same bound, hold for
\overline{v}^{\varepsilon}_{n}
, which is defined analogously.

(v)
Comparison of the traces.
We claim that, for a.e.
(x^{\prime},x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\times\mathbb{R}
,

(5.8)

\int_{-\infty}^{x_{N}-2r}u_{0,n}^{r}(x^{\prime},y_{N})\,{\rm d}y_{N}\leq\int_{-\infty}^{x_{N}}\overline{u}_{0,n}^{r}(x^{\prime},y_{N})\,{\rm d}y_{N}\leq\int_{-\infty}^{x_{N}+2r}u_{0,n}^{r}(x^{\prime},y_{N})\,{\rm d}y_{N}.

We prove only the first inequality since the second one can be obtained similarly.
By construction,
u_{0,n}^{r}(x^{\prime},\cdot)
is supported in
(-r,r)
. Thus, if
x_{N}<r
, the left-hand-side term vanishes, and, hence the inequality is true since
\overline{u}_{0,n}^{r}
is nonnegative. If
x_{N}>r
, using the support of

\overline{u}_{0,n}^{r}(x^{\prime},\cdot)
and (
5.2
),

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{x_{N}-2r}u_{0,n}^{r}(x^{\prime},y_{N})\,{\rm d}y_{N}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u_{0,n}^{r}(x^{\prime},y_{N})\,{\rm d}y_{N}=M\Phi(1/n,x^{\prime})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\overline{u}_{0,n}^{r}(x^{\prime},y_{N})\,{\rm d}y_{N}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{r}\overline{u}_{0,n}^{r}(x^{\prime},y_{N})\,{\rm d}y_{N}=\int_{-\infty}^{x_{N}}\overline{u}_{0,n}^{r}(x^{\prime},y_{N})\,{\rm d}y_{N},

and the inequality is also true.

On the other hand, since
u^{\varepsilon}_{n}\in C([0,\infty),L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}))
, then
u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t)\rightarrow u_{0,n}^{r}
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
as
t\rightarrow 0^{+}
. Thus,

v_{n}^{\varepsilon}(t,x^{\prime},x_{N})\rightarrow\int_{-\infty}^{x_{N}}u_{0,n}^{r}(x^{\prime},y_{N})\,{\rm d}y_{N}\quad\text{in }L^{1}_{x^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{N-1};L_{x_{N}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}))\text{ as }t\rightarrow 0^{+},

and, hence, a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\times\mathbb{R}
as well. Similar arguments hold for
\overline{v}^{\varepsilon}_{n}
. Therefore, (
5.8
) translates into

(5.9)

v^{\varepsilon}_{n}(0+,x^{\prime},x_{N}-2r)\leq\overline{v}^{\varepsilon}_{n}(0+,x^{\prime},x_{N})\leq v^{\varepsilon}_{n}(0+,x^{\prime},x_{N}+2r)\quad\text{for a.e}\ (x^{\prime},x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\times\mathbb{R}.

(vi)
Comparison of the primitives.
Let us now show that the inequalities (
5.9
) for the traces imply

(5.10)

v^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t,x^{\prime},x_{N}-2r)\leq\overline{v}^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t,x^{\prime},x_{N})\leq v^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t,x^{\prime},x_{N}+2r)\quad\text{for a.e. }(t,x^{\prime},x_{N})\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\times\mathbb{R}.

We only prove the first inequality, since the proof of the second is analogous. To this aim, we define

g(t,x^{\prime},x_{N}):=v^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t,x^{\prime},x_{N}-2r)-\overline{v}^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t,x^{\prime},x_{N})\quad\text{for a.e. }(t,x^{\prime},x_{N})\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\times\mathbb{R}.

By step (iv),
g
is uniformly bounded by
2Mc_{n}
. Moreover,
g\in C^{1+\delta,2+2\delta}(Q)
and satisfies

(5.11)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}g+\mathcal{L}g-\varepsilon\Delta g=a(t,x)\partial_{x_{N}}g\quad\text{in }Q,\qquad\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}g(t)\leq 0\quad\text{a.e. on }\mathbb{R}^{N},

where

a(t,x):=f_{\eta}^{\prime}(\zeta(t))\quad\text{with $\zeta(t)$ between $\partial_{x_{N}}v^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t)$ and $\partial_{x_{N}}\overline{v}^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t)$}.

Note that
|a(t,x)|\leq c_{\eta,q}
if
q<1
, and
|a(t,x)|\leq c_{n,q}
if
q>1
.

Following
[
5
, Theorem 3]
, we consider
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
smooth such that
\psi(x)=0
for
|x|\leq 1
,
\psi(x)>\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}
for
|x|\geq 2
, with
\psi,\nabla\psi
and
D^{2}\psi
bounded in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. With this choice we introduce, for all
\beta>0
,
\psi_{\beta}(x)=\psi(\beta x)
. It follows that
\psi_{\beta}(x)>\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}
for
|x|\geq 2/\beta
, and
\nabla\psi_{\beta}
,
D^{2}\psi_{\beta}
, and
\mathcal{L}\psi_{\beta}
all go to zero uniformly on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
when
\beta\rightarrow 0^{+}
. Let
\tilde{\delta}>0
and
h(t,x):=g(t,x)-\tilde{\delta}t-\psi_{\beta}(x)
. Then
h_{+}(t):=\max\{h(t),0\}
is smooth and satisfies
\nabla h_{+}(t)=\nabla h(t)
in the interior of its support, which is contained in the ball
|x|<2/\beta
for all
t>0
. Hence, by (
5.11
),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}h_{+}^{2}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}h_{+}(t)\partial_{t}h(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}h_{+}(t)(\partial_{t}g(t)-\tilde{\delta})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}h_{+}(t)(-\mathcal{L}g(t)+\varepsilon\Delta g(t)+a\partial_{x_{N}}g(t)-\tilde{\delta})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}h_{+}(t)\big(-\mathcal{L}h(t)+\varepsilon\Delta h(t)+a\partial_{x_{N}}h(t)\big)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}h_{+}(t)\big(-\mathcal{L}\psi_{\beta}+\varepsilon\Delta\psi_{\beta}+a\partial_{x_{N}}\psi_{\beta}-\tilde{\delta}\big).

Observe that for
\beta
small enough,
\beta<\beta(\tilde{\delta})
, we have

\big|-\mathcal{L}\psi_{\beta}+\varepsilon\Delta\psi_{\beta}+a\partial_{x_{N}}\psi_{\beta}\big|<\tilde{\delta}\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Moreover, for bounded and smooth
h
and compactly supported
h_{+}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}h_{+}(\mathcal{L}h)\,{\rm d}x=\mathcal{E}(h_{+},\mathcal{L}h)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big(h_{+}(x+y)-h_{+}(x)\big)\big(h(x+y)-h(x)\big)\,{\rm d}\nu(y){\rm d}x

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big(h_{+}(x)-h_{+}(x-y)\big)\big(h(x)-h(x-y)\big)\,{\rm d}\nu(y){\rm d}x\geq 0,

since the map
s\mapsto s_{+}
is increasing. Hence, for all
t>0
, Young’s inequality gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}h_{+}^{2}(t)

\displaystyle\leq-\varepsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\nabla h_{+}(t)\cdot\nabla h(t)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}ah_{+}(t)\partial_{x_{N}}h(t)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla h_{+}(t)|^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}ah_{+}(t)\partial_{x_{N}}h_{+}(t)

\displaystyle\leq-\varepsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla h_{+}(t)|^{2}+\frac{c^{2}_{n,q}}{2\vartheta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}h_{+}^{2}(t)+\frac{\vartheta}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\partial_{x_{N}}h_{+}(t)|^{2}\leq C(\varepsilon,n,q)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}h_{+}^{2}(t),

where we chose
\vartheta=2\varepsilon
. Then for all
0<s<t
we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}h_{+}^{2}(t)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}h_{+}^{2}(s)+C(\varepsilon,n,q)\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}h_{+}^{2}.

Observing that

\lim_{s\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}h_{+}^{2}(s)=\lim_{s\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{|x|<2/\beta}\big(g(s)-\tilde{\delta}s-\psi_{\beta}\big)_{+}^{2}=0,

we conclude that
h_{+}\equiv 0
, by Grönwall’s inequality, so that

g(t,x)\leq\tilde{\delta}t+\psi_{\beta}(x)\quad\text{for all }t>0,\text{ all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\text{ and all }\beta<\beta(\tilde{\delta}).

This implies that
g(t,x)\leq\tilde{\delta}t
on

Lemma A.1

\begin{split}I&\leq\iint_{Q}\Big(\Phi\frac{1}{p}u^{p}\partial_{t}\Theta+\Theta u^{p-1}F(u)\cdot\nabla\Phi+\Theta\Phi(p-1)^{\frac{1}{2}}u^{\frac{p}{2}-1}F(u)\cdot\Big(\frac{2(p-1)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{p}\nabla u^{\frac{p}{2}}\Big)\\
&\qquad-\Theta\frac{1}{p}u^{p}\big(\mathcal{L}\Phi\big)-\varepsilon\Theta(p-1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}u^{\frac{p}{2}}\Big(\frac{2(p-1)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{p}\nabla u^{\frac{p}{2}}\Big)\cdot\nabla\Phi\Big)\\
&\leq\iint_{Q}\Big(\Phi\frac{1}{p}u^{p}|\Theta^{\prime}|+\Theta u^{p-1}|F(u)||\nabla\Phi|+\Theta\Phi(p-1)^{\frac{1}{2}}u^{\frac{p}{2}-1}|F(u)|\Big|\frac{2(p-1)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{p}\nabla u^{\frac{p}{2}}\Big|\\

Using now Lemma

\begin{split}I&\leq\iint_{Q}\Big(\Phi\frac{1}{p}u^{p}|\Theta^{\prime}|+\Theta u^{p-1}|F(u)|\gamma\Phi+\Theta\Phi(p-1)^{\frac{1}{2}}u^{\frac{p}{2}-1}|F(u)|\frac{2(p-1)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{p}|\nabla u^{\frac{p}{2}}|\\
&\qquad+\Theta\frac{1}{p}u^{p}|\mathcal{L}\Phi|+\varepsilon\Theta(p-1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}u^{\frac{p}{2}}\frac{2(p-1)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{p}|\nabla u^{\frac{p}{2}}|\gamma\Phi\Big)\\
&=\iint_{Q}\bigg(\Phi\max\{|\Theta^{\prime}|,\Theta\}\Big(\frac{1}{p}u^{p}+\gamma u^{p-1}|F(u)|+\gamma\frac{1}{p}u^{p}\Big)\\
&\qquad+(\varepsilon\Theta\Phi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{2(p-1)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{p}|\nabla u^{\frac{p}{2}}|\Big(\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Theta\Phi)^{\frac{1}{2}}(p-1)^{\frac{1}{2}}u^{\frac{p}{2}-1}|F(u)|+(\varepsilon\Theta\Phi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma(p-1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}u^{\frac{p}{2}}\Big)\bigg)\\
&\qquad+\iint_{Q}\Theta\frac{1}{p}u^{p}|\mathcal{L}\Phi|.\end{split}

Now, apply Young’s inequality

to obtain

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}I&\leq\iint_{Q}\bigg(\Phi\max\{|\Theta^{\prime}|,\Theta\}\Big(\frac{1}{p}u^{p}+\gamma u^{p-1}|F(u)|+\gamma\frac{1}{p}u^{p}\Big)\\

&\leq\iint_{Q}\Phi\max\{|\Theta^{\prime}|,\Theta\}\Big(\frac{1}{p}u^{p}+\gamma u^{p-1}|F(u)|+\gamma\frac{1}{p}u^{p}\\
&\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\Big(\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}(p-1)^{\frac{1}{2}}u^{\frac{p}{2}-1}|F(u)|+\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma(p-1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}u^{\frac{p}{2}}\Big)^{2}\Big)+\iint_{Q}\Theta\frac{1}{p}u^{p}|\mathcal{L}\Phi|.\end{split}

Taking the maximum over the range of

B.5.

The initial data

-contraction also provides control of the time-translations:

Proposition B.15
.

Assume

, (

A
ν

Now, it is standard to choose

ε

\begin{split}\|u_{\delta}(t)-u_{\delta}(s)\|_{L^{1}(K)}&\leq\int_{K}\int_{s}^{t}\big|u(\tau)^{q}\ast\partial_{x_{N}}\omega_{\delta}-u(\tau)\ast(\mathcal{L}\omega_{\delta})+\varepsilon u(\tau)\ast\Delta\omega_{\delta}\big|\,{\rm d}\tau\\

Compact embedding of mixed spaces

Theorem C.1

(Aubin-Lions-Simon,
[
66
, Theorem 5]
)
.

Consider the three Banach spaces

X\hookrightarrow B\hookrightarrow Y,

where
X\hookrightarrow B
is compact. Assume
1\leq p\leq\infty
and:

(i)

\mathcal{F}\subset L^{p}((0,T);X)
is bounded.

(ii)

\textstyle{\int_{0}^{T-h}\|f(t+h)-f(t)\|_{Y}\,{\rm d}t\to 0}
as
h\to 0^{+}
uniformly for
f\in\mathcal{F}
.

Then
\mathcal{F}
is relatively compact in
L^{p}((0,T);B)
(and in
C([0,T];B)
if
p=\infty
).

Assume that
\alpha\in(0,2)
and
x=(x^{\prime},x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\times\mathbb{R}
, and define the Hilbert space

\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R};W^{\alpha/2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{N-1}))

\displaystyle:=\{\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\,:\,|\phi|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};W^{\alpha/2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{N-1}))}<\infty\},\quad\text{where }

\displaystyle|\phi|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};W^{\alpha/2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{N-1}))}

\displaystyle:=\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}}\frac{|\phi(x^{\prime}+z^{\prime},x_{N})-\phi(x^{\prime},x_{N})|^{2}}{|z^{\prime}|^{(N-1)+\alpha}}\,{\rm d}z^{\prime}{\rm d}(x^{\prime},x_{N})\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}},

.
It is classical that the above spaces are the same as the Bessel potential spaces, i.e.

.

Lemma C.2
.

Assume

,

,

and a set

(ii)

and some

\int_{|x^{\prime}|\leq R^{\prime},|x_{N}|\leq R}|\phi(x^{\prime},x_{N}+h_{N})-\phi(x^{\prime},x_{N})|^{2}\,{\rm d}x\leq C|h_{N}|^{\beta}

for some constant C independent of
\phi\in\mathcal{F}
.

Then
\mathcal{F}
is relatively compact in
L^{2}(B_{R^{\prime}}\times(-R,R))
.

Remark C.3
.

For any
R,R^{\prime}>0
the space
X_{R,R^{\prime}}\subset L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R};W^{\alpha/2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{N-1}))
endowed with the norm

\|\psi\|_{X_{R,R^{\prime}}}=\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};W^{\alpha/2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{N-1}))}+\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}+\sup_{|h_{N}|<R}\int_{|x^{\prime}|\leq R^{\prime},|x_{N}|\leq R}\frac{|\phi(x^{\prime},x_{N}+h_{N})-\phi(x^{\prime},x_{N})|}{|h_{N}|}\,{\rm d}x

is compactly embedded in
L^{2}(B_{R^{\prime}}\times(-R,R))
.

Proof.

We will employ the Fréchet-Kolmogorov-Riesz compactness theorem (see, e.g.,
[
36
]
); hence, we need uniform estimates on translations in
L^{2}
.

By the triangle inequality,

\begin{split}|\phi(x+h)-\phi(x)|^{2}&=|\phi(x+(h^{\prime},h_{N}))-\phi(x)|^{2}\\
&\leq|\phi(x+(h^{\prime},h_{N}))-\phi(x+(0,h_{N}))|^{2}+|\phi(x+(0,h_{N}))-\phi(x)|^{2}.\end{split}

Integrating the above inequality and using the change of variables
x+(0,h_{N})\mapsto x
gives

(C.1)

\begin{gathered}\int_{|x^{\prime}|\leq R^{\prime},|x_{N}|\leq R}|\phi(x+h)-\phi(x)|^{2}\,{\rm d}x\\
\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\phi(x+(h^{\prime},0))-\phi(x)|^{2}\,{\rm d}x+\int_{|x^{\prime}|\leq R^{\prime},|x_{N}|\leq R}|\phi(x+(0,h_{N}))-\phi(x)|^{2}\,{\rm d}x.\end{gathered}

The second term can already be estimated by assumption (ii), while the first term needs further consideration.
Using Plancherel’s identity and the inequality

|\textup{e}^{\textup{i}\xi^{\prime}\cdot h^{\prime}}-1|\leq\min\{2,|\xi^{\prime}\cdot h^{\prime}|\}\leq C_{\alpha}|\xi^{\prime}\cdot h^{\prime}|^{\alpha/2}\leq C_{\alpha}|\xi^{\prime}|^{\alpha/2}|h^{\prime}|^{\alpha/2},

we get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\phi(x+(h^{\prime},0))-\phi(x)|^{2}\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\textup{e}^{\textup{i}\xi^{\prime}\cdot h^{\prime}}-1|^{2}|\widehat{\phi}(\xi)|^{2}\,{\rm d}\xi\leq C_{\alpha}|h^{\prime}|^{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\xi^{\prime}|^{\alpha}|\widehat{\phi}(\xi)|^{2}\,{\rm d}\xi

\displaystyle=C_{\alpha}|h^{\prime}|^{\alpha}|\phi|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};W^{\alpha/2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{N-1}))}^{2}.

Going back to (
C.1
), we therefore get

(C.2)

\int_{|x^{\prime}|\leq R^{\prime},|x_{N}|\leq R}|\phi(x^{\prime}+h^{\prime},x_{N}+h_{N})-\phi(x^{\prime},x_{N})|^{2}\,{\rm d}(x^{\prime},x_{N})\leq C\big(|h^{\prime}|^{\alpha}+|h_{N}|^{\beta}\big),

where
C
is again independent of
\phi
by assumption (ii).

Now, define

\psi(x):=\phi(x)\mathds{1}_{B_{R^{\prime}}\times(-R,R)}(x).

In order to apply Fréchet-Kolmogorov-Riesz’s compactness theorem, we need to check that the family
\{\psi\,:\,\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is equicontinuous and equitight. The latter is automatically satisfied in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus(B_{R^{\prime}}\times(-R,R))
, and we are left with estimating the translations, for
h\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\times(-R,R)
:

\begin{split}\big\|\psi(\cdot+h)-\psi\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}&\leq\big\|\phi(\cdot+h)\big(\mathds{1}_{B_{R^{\prime}}\times(-R,R)}(\cdot+h)-\mathds{1}_{B_{R^{\prime}}\times(-R,R)}\big)\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\\
&\quad+\big\|\mathds{1}_{B_{R^{\prime}}\times(-R,R)}\big(\phi(\cdot+h)-\phi\big)\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\\
&\leq\big\|\phi\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\big\|\mathds{1}_{B_{R^{\prime}}\times(-R,R)}(\cdot+h)-\mathds{1}_{B_{R^{\prime}}\times(-R,R)}\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\\
&\quad+\big\|\phi(\cdot+h)-\phi\big\|_{L^{2}(B_{R^{\prime}}\times(-R,R))}.\end{split}

By assumption (i),
\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}
is uniformly bounded, and hence, the first term goes to zero as
h\to 0^{+}
uniformly. Moreover, the second term does so too since (
C.2
) holds. Hence, there is a convergent subsequence in
L^{2}(B_{R^{\prime}}\times(-R,R))
.
∎

Lemma D.1

\tilde{f}(x)=f(-x)

Let us first prove that a function
g\in L_{\rm loc}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
such that
g^{\prime}\leq C
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies

(D.2)

g(y)-g(x)\leq C(y-x)\quad\text{a.e. }x<y\in\mathbb{R}.

By density, in the inequality

-\int_{\mathbb{R}}g\varphi^{\prime}\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi\quad\text{for all }\varphi\in C^{1}_{\rm c}(\mathbb{R}),\ \varphi\geq 0,

we can use nonnegative and compactly supported test functions
\varphi
which are only
piecewise

C^{1}
. Fix two points
x<y
and a function
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
such that
\varphi_{\varepsilon}=1
in
(x+\varepsilon,y-\varepsilon)
, linear on
(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)\cup(y-\varepsilon,y+\varepsilon)
and vanishing identically outside the interval
(x-\varepsilon,y+\varepsilon)
. Then

-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{x-\varepsilon}^{x+\varepsilon}g+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{y-\varepsilon}^{y+\varepsilon}g\leq C\int_{x-\varepsilon}^{y+\varepsilon}\varphi_{\varepsilon}=C(y-x).

Since the function
g
is locally integrable, Lebesgue’s differentiation theorem guarantees that for a.e.
x
and
y
we can let
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
in the above inequality to obtain (
D.2
).

Let us now go back to function
f
. Since
f\in L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R})
, then
f^{k}\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R})
. Hence, we may apply the preliminary result to
g=f^{k}
to obtain, using the inequality (
D.1
),

f^{k}(y)-f^{k}(x)\leq C(y-x)\quad\text{a.e. }x<y\in\mathbb{R}.

Let us fix a point
y
for which the above inequality holds. Then

f^{k}(x)\geq C(x-\bar{y})\quad\text{a.e. }x<y,\quad\text{where }\bar{y}:=y-\frac{f^{k}(y)}{C}.

We use this inequality on the interval
(\bar{y},y)
to get, since
f
is nonnegative,

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\geq\int_{\bar{y}}^{y}f\geq C^{1/k}\int_{\bar{y}}^{y}(x-\bar{y})^{1/k}\,{\rm d}x=C^{1/k}\frac{k}{k+1}(y-\bar{y})^{1+1/k}=\frac{k}{C(k+1)}f^{k+1}(y),

from which the result follows immediately.
∎
