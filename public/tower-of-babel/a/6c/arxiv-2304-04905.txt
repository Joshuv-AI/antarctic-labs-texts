Levinson’s theorem as an index pairing
Angus Alexander, Adam Rennie

Abstract
We build on work of Kellendonk, Richard, Tiedra de Aldecoa and others to show that the wave operators for Schrödinger scattering theory on ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} generically have a particular form. As a consequence, Levinson’s theorem can be interpreted as the pairing of the K K -theory class of the unitary scattering operator and the K K -homology class of the generator of the group of dilations.



1
Introduction

Levinson’s theorem gives the number
N
of bound states
of the Schrödinger operator
-\Delta+V
, for suitably decaying potential
V
, as the total phase shift
[
29
]

N=\frac{1}{\pi}(\delta(0)-\delta(\infty)).

In this paper, for suitable potentials, we realise the number of bound states
N
as the pairing between the
K
-homology class
[D_{+}]\in K^{1}(C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\otimes\mathcal{K})
of the generator of dilations
D_{+}
on the half-line and the
K
-theory class
[S]\in K_{1}(C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\otimes\mathcal{K})
of the scattering operator
S
. In this language the number of bound states is given by

\langle[D_{+}],[S]\rangle=N.

The topological interpretation of Levinson’s theorem
was initiated in several low dimensional cases in
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
.
The technique in
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
is to use the form of the wave operator to deduce that
W_{-}
belongs to an algebra
E
fitting in to an exact sequence, with
\mathcal{K}
the compact operators,

0\to\mathcal{K}\to E\to C(\mathbb{S}^{1})\to 0.

As a consequence one may directly compute that the
K
-theory boundary map (the Index map)
K_{1}(C(\mathbb{S}^{1}))\to K_{0}(\mathcal{K})
takes the scattering operator to the index of the wave operator, which is known to be
-N
.

Realising
W_{-}
as an element of the algebra
E
also allows another topological interpretation, which is the starting point of this paper.
Following
[
25
,
26
,
27
,
36
]
, we show that for suitably decaying potentials, the wave operators for Schrödinger scattering on Euclidean space are generically of the form

\displaystyle W_{-}

\displaystyle=\textup{Id}+\varphi(D_{n})(S-\textup{Id})+K.

(1.1)

Here
K
is a compact operator,
D_{n}
denotes the generator of the dilation group on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
S=W_{+}^{*}W_{-}
the unitary scattering operator, and
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
is given by

\varphi(x)=\frac{1}{2}(1+\tanh{(\pi x)}-i\cosh{(\pi x)}^{-1}).

This form for the wave operator allows us to show that the number of bound states is equal to the index pairing between the
K
-theory class of the scattering operator and the
K
-homology class of the generator of dilations on the half-line. This index pairing requires care, as the algebras involved are nonunital, and we add the required detail in Section
4
.

That the wave operator has the form of Equation (
1.1
) has been shown in numerous low-dimensional Euclidean examples by
[
25
,
26
,
27
,
36
,
37
,
38
]
. Similar formulae for the wave operators have been developed in various other scattering models including rank-one perturbations
[
35
]
, the Friedrichs-Faddeev model
[
16
]
, Aharonov-Bohm operators
[
24
]
, lattice scattering
[
2
]
, half-line scattering
[
15
]
, discrete scattering
[
13
]
, scattering for an inverse-square potential
[
14
]
and for 1D Dirac operators with zero-range interactions
[
32
]
.

As well as the formula for the wave operator, which depends on having potentials with reasonable decay, we require that
S(0)=\textup{Id}
. This assumption rules out dimension 1 quite generally, and moreover the formula for the wave operator is slightly different in this case, due to the special properties of the zero-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{0}

[
26
]
. The only other case ruled out by the assumption
S(0)=\textup{Id}
is when there is a zero energy resonance in dimension 3.

Two other cases are ruled out by the intransigence of the low energy asymptotic expansion of the resolvent. These are the two dimensional case in the presence of zero energy
p
-resonances
[
37
,
38
]
and the case of resonances in dimension
n=4
(see Lemma
3.9
).

One sad feature of our result is that the one case (of dimension
n=1
) where we would have the summability to apply the local index formula
[
9
]
is explicitly ruled out. Trace class estimates of
[
41
, Section 8.1]
suggest that the scattering operator is sensitive to such summability data, and so the use of the local index formula in scattering theory is of interest for further study. Whilst we cannot directly use the local index formula, there are many analytic formulae for computing the number of bound states using scattering data, see
[
3
]
in dimension
3
,
[
5
, Theorem 6.3]
in dimension
2
and
[
10
, Theorem 3.66]
for higher (odd) dimensions.

The layout of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
we introduce the relevant concepts from scattering theory and fix our notation. In particular we discuss resolvent expansions and how they can be used to analyse the scattering matrix. In Section
3
we prove, via a number of technical results, that the wave operator in dimension
n=2
and
n\geq 4
is of the form of Equation (
1.1
), with dimension
n=3
having been considered already in
[
36
]
. In Section
4
we use the universal form of the wave operator to reinterpret Levinson’s theorem as an index pairing between the
K
-theory class of the scattering operator
S
and the
K
-homology class of the generator of dilations on the half-line
D_{+}
.

Preliminaries on scattering theory

2.1
Standing assumptions and notation

Throughout this document we will consider the scattering theory on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
associated to the operators

H_{0}=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}=-\Delta\quad\mbox{and}\quad H=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}+V

where the (multiplication operator by the)
potential
V
is real-valued and satisfies

\displaystyle|V(x)|

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|x|)^{-\rho}

(2.1)

for various values of
\rho
depending on the particular result and the dimension
n
.
We denote the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and its dual
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and recall the weighted Sobolev spaces

H^{s,t}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\Big\{f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{H^{s,t}}:=\left|\left|(1+|x|^{2})^{\frac{t}{2}}(\textup{Id}-\Delta)^{\frac{s}{2}}f\right|\right|<\infty\Big\}

with index
s\in\mathbb{R}
indicating derivatives and
t\in\mathbb{R}
associated to decay at infinity
[
1
, Section 4.1]
. With
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
the Euclidean inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we denote the Fourier transform by

\mathcal{F}_{n}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\qquad[\mathcal{F}_{n}f](\xi)=(2\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-i\langle x,\xi\rangle}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Note that the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is an isomorphism from
H^{s,t}
to
H^{t,s}
for any
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
. We will frequently drop the reference to the space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for simplicity of notation, as well as the subscript
n
from our Fourier transforms when the dimension is clear. We denote by
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})
and
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})
the bounded and compact operators from
\mathcal{H}_{1}
to
\mathcal{H}_{2}
.
For
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
, we let

R_{0}(z)=(H_{0}-z)^{-1},\qquad R(z)=(H-z)^{-1}

and the boundary values of the resolvent are defined as

\displaystyle R_{0}(\lambda\pm i0)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{R_{0}(\lambda\pm i\varepsilon)}\quad\mbox{and}\quad R(\lambda\pm i0)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{R(\lambda\pm i\varepsilon)}.

(2.2)

The limiting absorption principle
[
28
, Theorem 5.3.7]
tells us that these boundary values exist in
\mathcal{B}(H^{-1,t},H^{1,-t})
for any
t>\frac{1}{2}
and
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
. The operator
H_{0}
has purely absolutely continuous spectrum, and in particular no kernel. The operator
H
can have eigenvalues and for
V
satisfying Assumption (
2.1
) with
\rho>1
we have that these eigenvalues are negative, or zero
[
41
, Theorem 6.1.1]
. We let
P_{0}
be the kernel projection of
H
, which may be zero.

Much of the analysis of the asymptotics of
R(z)
is simplified by studying the operator (whose two expressions are related by resolvent identities)

T(z)=V(\textup{Id}+R_{0}(z)V)^{-1}=V-VR(z)V.

(2.3)

The operator
T(z)
appears in our analysis of the wave and scattering operators. The operator
T(z)
also appears in the scattering amplitude (the integral kernel of
S-\textup{Id}
, see Theorem
2.10
).

The one-parameter unitary group of dilations on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is
given on
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
by

\displaystyle[U_{n}(t)f](x)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{nt}{2}}f(\mathrm{e}^{t}x),\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.4)

We denote the self-adjoint generator of
U_{n}
by
D_{n}
.
The generator of the group
(U_{+}(t))
of dilations on the half-line
\mathbb{R}^{+}
is denoted
D_{+}

(which is
D_{1}
restricted to the positive half-line). The generators of the dilation groups are given by

\displaystyle D_{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{y}{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}+\frac{1}{2i}{\rm Id},\qquad D_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{x_{j}}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{n}{2i}{\rm Id}.

(2.5)

Since each of
D_{+},D_{n}
generate one-parameter groups, we can recognise functions of these operators. For
D_{+}
and
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
a bounded function whose Fourier transform has rapid decay, we have

\displaystyle[\varphi(D_{+})g](\rho)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{[\mathcal{F}^{*}\varphi](t)\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{2}}g(\mathrm{e}^{t}\rho)\,\mathrm{d}t},

with a similar formula for
D_{n}
.

Several Hilbert spaces recur, and we adopt the notation (following
[
20
, Section 2]
which contains an excellent discussion on the relations between the spaces and operators we introduce here)

\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\quad\mathcal{P}=L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1}),\quad\mathcal{H}_{spec}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathcal{P})\cong L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\otimes\mathcal{P}.

Here
\mathcal{H}_{spec}
provides the Hilbert space on which we can diagonalise the free Hamiltonian
H_{0}
.

Since
V
is bounded,
H=H_{0}+V
is self-adjoint with
\Dom(H)=\Dom(H_{0})
. The wave operators

W_{\pm}=s-\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}e^{itH}e^{-itH_{0}}

exist and are asymptotically complete if
\rho>1

[
33
, Theorem 12.1]
. We will use the stationary scattering theory, which coincides with the time-dependent approach
[
40
, Section 5.3]
given our assumptions.
For suitable
f,g\in\mathcal{H}
we can write
[
41
, Equation 0.6.9]

\displaystyle\langle W_{\pm}f,g\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\left(\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}\langle R_{0}(\lambda\pm i\varepsilon)f,R(\lambda\pm i\varepsilon)g\rangle}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda}.

(2.6)

For our analysis of the wave operator, we must describe the explicit unitaries which diagonalise our Hamiltonians.

For
\lambda>0
the trace operator
\gamma(\lambda):\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathcal{P}
defined by

[\gamma(\lambda)f](\omega):=f(\lambda\omega)
defines a bounded operator and for each
s>\frac{1}{2}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
extends to a bounded operator on
H^{s,t}
(see
[
28
, Theorem 2.4.3]
).

Definition 2.1
.

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
t>\frac{1}{2}
we define the operator

\Gamma_{0}(\lambda):H^{s,t}\to\mathcal{P}\quad\mbox{by}\quad[\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)f](\omega)=2^{-\frac{1}{2}}\lambda^{\frac{n-2}{4}}[\mathcal{F}f](\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\omega)

and the operator which diagonalises the free Hamiltonian
H_{0}
as

F_{0}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}_{spec}\quad\mbox{by}\quad[F_{0}f](\lambda,\omega)=[\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)f](\omega).

Lemma 2.2

(
[
20
, p. 439]
)
.

The operator
F_{0}
is unitary. Moreover for
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
,
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and
f\in\mathcal{H}_{spec}
we have

[F_{0}H_{0}F_{0}^{*}f](\lambda,\omega)=\lambda f(\lambda,\omega)=:[Mf](\lambda,\omega).

Here we have defined the operator
M
of multiplication by the spectral variable.

2.2
Resolvent expansions and resonances

Here we recall some known results regarding expansions related to the perturbed resolvent
R(z)
in the limit
z\to 0
. Only the terms in the expansion relevant to later computations will be shown, however we note that higher terms can be computed explicitly
[
18
,
19
,
21
]
. The low energy behaviour is sensitive to the presence of ‘zero-energy resonances’. These are essentially distributional solutions to
H\psi=0
which are not square-integrable but lie in some larger space. We will give the precise definition shortly.

The operator of interest to us is not
R(z)
but the related operator
T(z)=V(\textup{Id}+R_{0}(z)V)^{-1}
from (
2.3
). Before giving the low-energy expansions of
T(z)
, we note that the high-energy behaviour can be described rather simply in the following generalisation of
[
18
, Lemma 9.1]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose that
\rho>\frac{n+1}{2}
in Assumption (
2.1
) and define the complex domain
C_{+}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\textup{Re}(z)\geq 1\textup{ and }\textup{Im}(z)>0\}
and
t\in(\frac{1}{2},\rho-\frac{1}{2})
. Then
VR_{0}(z)
and
(\textup{Id}+VR_{0}(z))^{-1}
can be extended to continuous and uniformly bounded functions from
C_{+}
to
\mathcal{B}(H^{0,t},H^{0,t})
. Similarly,
R_{0}(z)V
and
(\textup{Id}+R_{0}(z)V)^{-1}
can be extended to continuous and uniformly bounded functions from
C_{+}
to
\mathcal{B}(H^{0,-t},H^{0,-t})
.

Proof.

Since for any
\rho>\frac{n+1}{2}
and
t\in(\frac{1}{2},\rho-\frac{1}{2})
we have
R_{0}(z)\in\mathcal{B}(H^{0,t},H^{0,t-\rho})
is continuous in
z\in C_{+}
by
[
19
, proof of Lemma 3.1]
, and since
V\in\mathcal{B}(H^{0,t-\rho},H^{0,t})
by assumption, we find that
VR_{0}(z)\in\mathcal{B}(H^{0,t},H^{0,t})
for any
t\in(\frac{1}{2},\rho-\frac{1}{2})
.

Since
VR_{0}(z)
is compact and has no eigenvalue
-1
for
z\in C_{+}
(see
[
28
, Proposition 5.2.1]
), the operator
(\textup{Id}+VR_{0}(z))^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(H^{0,t},H^{0,t})
exists and is continuous in
z\in C_{+}
. By
[
31
, Theorem 1]
we have that
VR_{0}(z)\to 0
as
|z|\to\infty
in
C_{+}
in the norm of
\mathcal{B}(H^{0,t},H^{0,t})
for any
t>\frac{1}{2}
, so that the operator
(\textup{Id}+VR_{0}(z))^{-1}
is uniformly bounded. A similar computation (or duality) can be used to prove the second claim.
∎

2.2.1
Resonances in dimensions 1,2,3

The formulae for wave operators in terms of dilations and scattering operator that we seek to prove have been established in dimensions
n=1,2,3
,
[
25
,
26
,
27
,
36
,
37
,
38
]
.
For this reason we do not need to describe the resolvent expansions in these cases, and refer the reader to
[
22
]
for relevant expansions in dimensions
n=1,2
and
[
18
]
for dimension
n=3
. The important takeaways from the low dimensional expansions are the following definitions of zero energy resonances.

Definition 2.4
.

1.

If
n=1
we say there is a resonance at zero energy if there exists a
0\neq\psi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that
H\psi=0
in the sense of distributions.

2.

If
n=2
we say there is an
s
-resonance at zero energy if there exists a
0\neq\psi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with
\psi\notin L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for all
q<\infty
and such that
H\psi=0
in the sense of distributions.

3.

If
n=2
we say there is a
p
-resonance at zero energy if for some
q>2
there exists a
0\neq\psi\in L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that
H\psi=0
in the sense of distributions.

4.

If
n=3
we say there is a resonance at zero energy of there exists
\psi\notin L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
such that
H\psi=0
in the sense of distributions.

Remark 2.5
.

The notation of
s
-resonances and
p
-resonances is motivated by the usual notation for angular momentum modes, although this intuition seems only applicable to rotationally invariant potentials. Here the
s
-resonances correspond to angular momentum
\ell=0
, and the
p
-resonances appear as moments in coordinate directions in an analogous manner to states of angular momentum
\ell=1
(see
[
22
, Theorem 6.2]
). In dimension
n=3
the exact form of resonances can be described explicitly, see
[
41
, Proposition 7.4.10]
and
[
10
, Lemma 3.22]
.

Resonances and resolvent in dimension 4

The expansion of the operator

and let

m+t>2

Let

\displaystyle\left|\left|V\psi\right|\right|_{1}

We note that by

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle:=1-2\gamma+i\pi-(4\pi)^{-2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}{\ln{\left(\frac{|x-y|}{2}\right)}[V\psi](x)\overline{[V\psi](y)}\,\mathrm{d}x}\,\mathrm{d}y}.

In order to discuss the resolvent expansions in general, we record the decay assumptions on the potential required in each dimension.

. The result of the expansion of

(

Suppose that

\displaystyle T(z)

\displaystyle=z^{-1}VP_{0}V+o(1)

in

.

Theorem 2.10

(
[
41
, Theorem 6.6.10]
)
.

Suppose that
V
satisfies Assumption (
2.1
) for some
\rho>1
. The scattering matrix
S(\lambda)
is given for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
by the equation

\displaystyle S(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\textup{Id}-2\pi i\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)(V-VR(\lambda+i0)V)\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)^{*}.

(2.8)

For each
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, the operator
S(\lambda)
is unitary in
\mathcal{P}=L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
and depends continuously (in the sense of norm) on
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

We note that resolvent identities yield
T(z)=V(\textup{Id}+R_{0}(z)V)^{-1}=V-VR(z)V
. This identity and the stationary formula
for the scattering matrix of Equation (

)
demonstrate why we need to study the asymptotics of

.
The resolvent expansions of the previous section and Equation (
2.8
) allow us to analyse the value of the scattering matrix at zero energy.
To do so we also need the low energy behaviour of the operator
\Gamma_{0}
, which we now describe.

For
\lambda>0
the trace operator
\gamma(\lambda):\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathcal{P}
is continuous and extends to a bounded operator in
\mathcal{B}(H^{s,t},\mathcal{P})
for each
s>\frac{1}{2}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}

[
28
, Theorem 2.4.3]
. We need the asymptotic development of
\gamma(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}})\mathcal{F}
as
\lambda\to 0
. We can compute for

the expansion

\displaystyle[\gamma(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}})\mathcal{F}f](\omega)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\langle x,\omega\rangle}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\sum_{j=0}^{K}{\frac{1}{j!}(i\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}})^{j}(-\langle x,\omega\rangle)^{j}f(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x}+O\left(\lambda^{\frac{K+1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=:\sum_{j=0}^{K}{(i\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}})^{j}[\gamma_{j}f](\omega)}+O\left(\lambda^{\frac{K+1}{2}}\right)

as
\lambda\to 0
in
\mathcal{B}(H^{s,t},\mathcal{P})
for appropriate

. The operators
\gamma_{j}
can be considered as operators in certain weighted Sobolev spaces, with higher terms in the series requiring convergence in Sobolev spaces with higher decay. Jensen
[
18
, Equation 5.4]
states the following result in dimension

and the result generalises in a straightforward manner to each dimension.

Lemma 2.11
.

Fix
j\in\mathbb{N}
. For each
s\geq 0
and
t>j+\frac{n}{2}
we have
\gamma_{j}\in\mathcal{B}(H^{s,t},\mathcal{P})
.

Proof.

For
t>j+\frac{n}{2}
and
s=0
, we can estimate that

\displaystyle\left|\left|\gamma_{j}f\right|\right|_{\mathcal{P}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{|[\gamma_{j}f](\omega)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\omega}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\left|(2\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}{(-\langle x,\omega\rangle)^{j}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\omega}

\displaystyle\leq(2\pi)^{-n}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\left|\langle\omega,x\rangle\right|^{j}|f(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\omega}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-n}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\left|\langle\omega,x\rangle\right|^{j}(1+|x|^{2})^{-\frac{t}{2}}(1+|x|^{2})^{\frac{t}{2}}|f(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\omega}.

We next use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to obtain the estimate

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{-n}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\left|\langle\omega,x\rangle\right|^{j}(1+|x|^{2})^{-\frac{t}{2}}(1+|x|^{2})^{\frac{t}{2}}|f(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\omega}

\displaystyle\leq(2\pi)^{-n}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}{|\langle\omega,x\rangle|^{2j}(1+|x|^{2})^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}x}\right)^{2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}{(1+|y|^{2})^{t}|f(y)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\omega}

\displaystyle=C_{j}\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{H^{0,t}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}.

Thus for
t>j+\frac{n}{2}
we find
\gamma_{j}\in\mathcal{B}(H^{0,t},\mathcal{P})
. For
s>0
we use the inclusion
H^{s,t}\subset H^{0,t}
.
∎

For each
\lambda>0
the operator
\Gamma_{0}(\lambda):\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathcal{P}
extends to an element of
\mathcal{B}(H^{s,t},\mathcal{P})
for each
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
t>\frac{1}{2}
, as well as
\lambda\mapsto\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)\in\mathcal{B}(H^{s,t},\mathcal{P})
is continuous. These follow immediately since
\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)=2^{-\frac{1}{2}}\lambda^{\frac{n-2}{4}}\gamma(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}})\mathcal{F}
.

Lemma 2.12
.

For
s\geq 0
and
t>\frac{n}{2}+1
we have the expansion

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=2^{-\frac{1}{2}}\lambda^{\frac{n-2}{4}}\gamma_{0}-2^{-\frac{1}{2}}\lambda^{\frac{n}{4}}\gamma_{1}+o\left(\lambda^{\frac{n+2}{4}}\right)

as
\lambda\to 0
in
\mathcal{B}(H^{s,t},\mathcal{P})
.

Combining Lemma

2.3.1

3
The form of the wave operator

We aim to show the wave operator is of the form

\displaystyle W_{-}

\displaystyle=\textup{Id}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\textup{Id}+\tanh{(\pi D_{n})}-i\cosh{(\pi D_{n})}^{-1}\right)(S-\textup{Id})+K,

(3.1)

where
D_{n}
is the generator of dilations on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
S
the scattering matrix and
K
a compact operator. We will frequently use the notation
\varphi(x)=\frac{1}{2}(1+\tanh{(\pi x)}-i\cosh{(\pi x)}^{-1})
.
The main result of this section is the following theorem, whose proof relies on several preparatory results.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
and suppose that
\rho
satisfies Assumption
2.8
, and let
V
satisfy
|V(x)|\leq C(1+|x|)^{-\rho}
for almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In dimension 2 we suppose also that there are no
p
-resonances and in dimension 4 we suppose there are no resonances.
Then in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle W_{-}

\displaystyle=\textup{Id}+\varphi(D_{n})(S-\textup{Id})+K,

where
K\in\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H})
and
\varphi(x)=\frac{1}{2}(1+\tanh{(\pi x)}-i\cosh{(\pi x)}^{-1})
.

We begin with the special case of dimension two, before setting the scene for the remainder of the proof. The main elements of the proof are: a careful analysis of the diagonalisation maps of Definition
2.1
; an analysis of the stationary (integral) formula for the wave operator using this information; an interchange of limits in the integral formula; finally an identification of a function of dilation and compact remainder. Our proof closely follows that of
[
36
]
.

3.1
Dimension 2

We first recall the known form of the wave operator in two dimensions in the presence of
s
-resonances. We then massage the formula from Theorem
3.2
to obtain the statement in Theorem
3.1
. The following is
[
38
, Theorem 1.3]

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
n=2
and
V
satisfy Assumption (
2.1
) for some
\rho>11
and suppose that there are no
p
-resonances. Then there exist two continuous functions
\eta,\tilde{\eta}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathcal{K}(L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1}))
vanishing at

and

and satisfying
\eta(H_{0})+\tilde{\eta}(H_{0})=S-\textup{Id}

\displaystyle W_{-}

\displaystyle=\textup{Id}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\textup{Id}+\tanh{\left(\frac{\pi D_{2}}{2}\right)}\right)\eta(H_{0})+\frac{1}{2}\left(\textup{Id}+\tanh{(\pi D_{2})}-i\cosh{(\pi D_{2})}^{-1}\right)\tilde{\eta}(H_{0})+K,

with

.

With the help of a technical result, we can recover our universal form of the wave operator from Theorem

rather simply.

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose that
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\cap C(\mathbb{R})
and
g\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{P}))
is such that
g(\pm\infty)=0
. Let
L
denote the (densely defined) operator of multiplication by the variable in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
. Then the operator
(f(D_{+})\otimes\textup{Id})g(\ln{(L)})
defines a compact operator on
\mathcal{H}_{spec}
. Similarly if
h\in C(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{P}))
is such that
h(0)=h(\infty)=0
, then
(f(D_{+})\otimes\textup{Id})h(L)
defines a compact operator on
\mathcal{H}_{spec}
.

Proof.

The algebraic tensor product
C_{0}(\mathbb{R})\odot\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{P})
is dense in
C_{0}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{P}))
by
[
34
, Theorem 1.15]
, when
C_{0}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{P}))
is equipped with the uniform topology. Thus it suffices to prove that for
a\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R})
and
b\in\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{P})
the operator

\displaystyle(f(D_{+})\otimes\textup{Id})(a(\ln{(L)})\otimes b)

\displaystyle=f(D_{+})a(\ln{(L)})\otimes b

is compact.
This follows from
[
39
, Theorem 4.1]
after a few steps. First we let
u_{m}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
be an approximate unit for
C_{0}(\mathbb{R})
. Then
u_{m}f
and
u_{m}a
are
L^{2}
-functions and so

(u_{m}f)(D_{+})(u_{m}a)(\ln{(L)})\otimes b

is compact by
[
39
, Theorem 4.1]
, and since
D_{+}
and
\frac{1}{2}\ln{(L)}
are canonically conjugate. Now because
f
and
a
vanish at
\pm\infty
we readily see that
(u_{m}f)(D_{+})\to f(D_{+})
in operator norm, and similarly for
(u_{m}a)(\ln{(L)})
. As the compacts are norm closed, the limit is compact. The final claim follows by writing
h=g\circ(\ln)
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
n=2
and
V
satisfy Assumption (
2.1
) for some
\rho>11
and suppose that there are no
p
-resonances. Then

is of the form given in Equation (

).

Proof.

Consider the difference

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=F_{0}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(\textup{Id}+\tanh{\left(\frac{\pi D_{2}}{2}\right)}\right)\eta(H_{0})-\frac{1}{2}\left(\textup{Id}+\tanh{(\pi D_{2})}-i\cosh{(\pi D_{2})}^{-1}\right)\eta(H_{0})\right)F_{0}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\textup{Id}+\tanh{\left(\pi D_{+}\right)}-\textup{Id}-\tanh{(2\pi D_{+})}+i\cosh{(2\pi D_{+})}^{-1}\right)\eta(L)

\displaystyle=:Y(D_{+})\eta(L).

The function
\eta
vanishes at
0
and
\infty
and the function

is continuous and square integrable. Thus an application of Lemma
3.3
gives that the operator
X
is compact. Hence

has the form given in Equation (
1.1
).
∎

3.2
Setting up the proof

We will now provide a number of preparatory results before proceeding to the proof of Theorem

for

The proof is broken up into a number of steps, whose ultimate goal is to factorise (in the spectral representation) the wave operator into the composition of several operators, whose mapping properties as operators between various weighted Sobolev spaces must be analysed. The properties of these factor operators depend heavily on their low energy behaviour and we use the resolvent expansions of Section
2.2
to determine this behaviour.

Definition 3.5
.

Define, for
\varepsilon>0
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the operator
\varphi_{\varepsilon}(H_{0}-\lambda)=\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}R_{0}(\lambda\mp i\varepsilon)R_{0}(\lambda\pm i\varepsilon)
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Similarly for
M=F_{0}H_{0}F_{0}^{*}
the operator of multiplication by the spectral variable, set
\varphi_{\varepsilon}(M-\lambda)=\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}(M-(\lambda\mp i\varepsilon))^{-1}(M-(\lambda\pm i\varepsilon))^{-1}
.

By
[
40
, Section 1.4]
, the limits
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(H_{0}-\lambda)f,g\rangle}
exist for almost every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Moreover the limit satisfies

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\left(\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\big\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(H_{0}-\lambda)f,g\big\rangle}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda}.

Using the resolvent identity
R(z)=(\textup{Id}+R_{0}(z)V)^{-1}R_{0}(z)
and the stationary formula for the wave operator in Equation (
2.6
) we can then show that

\displaystyle\langle(W_{\pm}-\textup{Id})f,g\rangle

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\left(\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\big\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(H_{0}-\lambda)f,(\textup{Id}+VR_{0}(\lambda\pm i\varepsilon))^{-1}VR_{0}(\lambda\pm i\varepsilon)g\big\rangle}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda}.

We now aim to derive formulas for the wave operators in the spectral representation of
H_{0}
, that is,
F_{0}(W_{-}-\textup{Id})F_{0}^{*}
. Recall from Lemma
2.2
the operator
M=F_{0}H_{0}F_{0}^{*}
of multiplication by the spectral variable. For suitable
f,g\in\mathcal{H}_{spec}
we compute that

\displaystyle-\langle F_{0}(W_{\pm}-\textup{Id})F_{0}^{*}f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{spec}}

\displaystyle=\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\!\left(\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\big\langle V(\textup{Id}+R_{0}(\lambda\mp i\varepsilon)V)^{-1}F_{0}^{*}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(M-\lambda)f,F_{0}^{*}(M-\lambda\mp i\varepsilon)^{-1}g\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda}

\displaystyle=\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\!\left(\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\int_{0}^{\infty}{\!\!\!\!\Big\langle\!\!\left[F_{0}V(\textup{Id}+R_{0}(\lambda\mp i\varepsilon)V)^{-1}F_{0}^{*}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(M-\lambda)f\right]\!(\mu,\cdot),(\mu-\lambda\mp i\varepsilon)^{-1}g(\mu,\cdot)\Big\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu}}\right)\!\mathrm{d}\lambda}.

With

for

we have

\displaystyle\langle F_{0}^{*}(W_{\pm}-\textup{Id})F_{0}^{*}f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{spec}}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\left(\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!{\big\langle\left[F_{0}T(\lambda\mp i\varepsilon)F_{0}^{*}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(M-\lambda)f\right](\mu,\cdot),(\mu-\lambda\mp i\varepsilon)^{-1}g(\mu,\cdot)\big\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda}.

(3.2)

To interchange the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
and the integral over
\mu
of Equation (
3.2
), which we do in

, we first need to analyse
F_{0}T(\lambda+i0)F_{0}^{*}
.

3.3
Analysing the diagonalisation maps

Recall that for
\lambda>0
the operator
\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)
(see subsection
2.1
) extends to an element
of
\mathcal{B}(H^{s,t},\mathcal{P})
for each

and

\lambda\mapsto\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)\in\mathcal{B}(H^{s,t},\mathcal{P})

We need stronger continuity and boundedness results for

.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
n\geq 4
,
s\geq 0
and
t>\frac{n}{2}
. Then, the functions
G_{\pm}:(0,\infty)\to\mathcal{B}(H^{s,t},\mathcal{P})

and
G_{-}(\lambda)=\lambda^{-\frac{1}{4}}\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)

Proof.

The continuity is immediate from the previous paragraph. For the boundedness, it is sufficient to check the boundedness of
G_{-}
in a neighbourhood of
0
and
G_{+}
in a neighbourhood of
\infty
. For the first bound, we need the asymptotic development of
\gamma(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}})
as
\lambda\to 0
. We recall that for
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we have

\displaystyle[\gamma(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}})\mathcal{F}f](\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{K}{(i\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}})^{j}[\gamma_{j}f](\omega)}+O\left(\lambda^{\frac{K+1}{2}}\right).

By Lemma
2.11
we have for
t>j+\frac{n}{2}
that
\gamma_{j}\in\mathcal{B}(H^{0,t},\mathcal{P})
. From this expansion we see that for
t>j+\frac{n}{2}
and for any
\alpha\leq\frac{n-2}{4}
we have

\displaystyle\lambda^{-\alpha}\left|\left|\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)\right|\right|

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\frac{n-2}{4}-\alpha}2^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left|\left|\gamma(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}})\right|\right|

(3.3)

is bounded in a neighbourhood of zero. Taking
\alpha=\frac{1}{4}
gives immediately the statement for
G_{-}
, since
n\geq 3
. For the second statement, we refer to
[
41
, Theorem 1.1.4]
for the estimate, valid for
t>\frac{1}{2}
,

\displaystyle r^{n-1}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{|u(r\omega)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\omega}

\displaystyle\leq C\left|\left|u\right|\right|^{2}_{H^{t,0}},

(3.4)

from which we obtain (with

and

) that

\displaystyle\left|\left|\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)f\right|\right|_{\mathcal{P}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{2^{-1}\lambda^{\frac{n-2}{2}}|[\mathcal{F}f](\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\omega)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\omega}=2^{-1}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\lambda^{\frac{n-1}{2}}|[\mathcal{F}f](\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\omega)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\omega}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-1}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}C\left|\left|\mathcal{F}f\right|\right|^{2}_{H^{t,0}}=2^{-1}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}C\left|\left|f\right|\right|^{2}_{H^{0,t}}.

This gives us the estimate

\displaystyle\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}\left|\left|\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)f\right|\right|

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{C}\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{H^{0,t}},

so that
G_{+}
is bounded as
\lambda\to\infty
. For

, we use the inclusion
H^{s,t}\subset H^{0,t}
.
∎

From the arguments above we immediately obtain

is continuous and bounded for
s\geq 0
and

. We can further strengthen Lemma

for

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
s\geq 0
and

. Then
\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)\in\mathcal{K}(H^{s,t},\mathcal{P})
for any

and the function

Proof.

The compactness statement follows from the compact embedding

for any

,

. See

For the continuity, the same argument as the previous lemma works. That

\gamma(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}})\mathcal{F}\to\gamma_{0}\mathcal{F}
in the norm of
\mathcal{B}(H^{s,t},\mathcal{P})
as
\lambda\to 0
. It remains to check the limit as
\lambda\to\infty
. The estimate (
3.4
) of the previous lemma tells us the behaviour of
\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)
as
\lambda\to\infty
in the norm of
\mathcal{B}(H^{0,t})
, so we analyse an equivalent limit in this norm. Define the operator
P_{s}=(\textup{Id}+H_{0})^{-\frac{s}{2}}
and note that for
\alpha\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle\lambda^{-\alpha}\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)P_{s}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-\alpha}(1+\lambda)^{-\frac{s}{2}}\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)

for each
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Thus we find

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}{\left|\left|\lambda^{-\alpha}\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)\right|\right|_{\mathcal{B}(H^{s,t})}}

\displaystyle=0

if and only if

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}{\left|\left|\lambda^{-\alpha}(1+\lambda)^{-\frac{s}{2}}\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)\right|\right|_{\mathcal{B}(H^{0,t})}}

\displaystyle=0.

Hence the claim follows from the estimate (
3.4
) of the previous lemma as long as
-\alpha-\frac{s}{2}<\frac{1}{4}
, which is the same as
s>-\frac{1}{2}-2\alpha
. Letting
\alpha=\frac{1}{4}
gives the desired result.
∎

We now consider the multiplication operator
G_{-}:C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{+},H^{s,t})\to\mathcal{H}_{spec}
given by

\displaystyle[G_{-}f](\lambda,\omega)

\displaystyle:=[G_{-}(\lambda)f](\lambda,\omega)=\lambda^{-\frac{1}{4}}[\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)f](\lambda,\omega).

(3.5)

Lemmas
3.6
and
3.7
show that the operator
G_{-}
extends, for
s\geq 0
and
t>\frac{1}{2}
to an element of
\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+},H^{s,t}),\mathcal{H}_{spec})
.

The next step is to deal with the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
of
\varphi_{\varepsilon}(M-\lambda)
from Definition
3.5
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Fix
n\geq 4
. Take
s\geq 0
and
t>\frac{n}{2}
. For
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathcal{P})
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\left|\left|F_{0}^{*}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(M-\lambda)f-\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)^{*}f(\lambda)\right|\right|_{H^{-s,-t}}}

\displaystyle=0.

We omit the proof, since it is identical to that of
[
36
, Lemma 2.3]
.

From now on we only consider the operator
W_{-}
since our resolvent results have only been in the upper half plane. Similar results could be derived in the lower half plane, however the operator
W_{+}
can be obtained in a much simpler manner from the relation
W_{+}=W_{-}S^{*}
at a later stage.

Lemma 3.9
.

Fix
n\geq 4
. For
z\notin\sigma(H_{0})
let
T(z)=V(\textup{Id}+R_{0}(z)V)^{-1}
. Suppose that
\rho,t
and
V
satisfy Assumption
2.8
, and that there are no resonances for
n=4
.
Then the function
B:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{P},H^{0,\rho-t})
defined by

\displaystyle B(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}T(\lambda+i0)\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)^{*}

is continuous and bounded, and the multiplication operator
B:C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathcal{P})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+},H^{0,\rho-t})
given by

\displaystyle[Bf](\lambda)

\displaystyle=B(\lambda)f(\lambda)

extends to an element of
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{spec},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+},H^{0,\rho-t}))
.

Proof.

The continuity of
B
follows from the limiting absorption principle
[
28
, Theorem 5.2.7]
. For boundedness, it will be sufficient to show that the map
\lambda\mapsto\left|\left|B(\lambda)\right|\right|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{P},H^{0,\rho-t})}
is bounded in a neighbourhood of
0
and in a neighbourhood of
\infty
.

For
\lambda\geq 1
, Lemma
2.3
shows that the function
\lambda\mapsto\left|\left|T(\lambda+i0)\right|\right|_{\mathcal{B}(H^{0,-t},H^{0,\rho-t})}
is bounded provided
\rho>\frac{n+1}{2}
, which is guaranteed by our assumptions. We also know from Lemma
3.6
that the function
\mathbb{R}^{+}\ni\lambda\mapsto\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}\left|\left|\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)^{*}\right|\right|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{P},H^{0,-t})}
is bounded. This shows that
\lambda\mapsto\left|\left|B(\lambda)\right|\right|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{P},H^{0,\rho-t})}
is bounded in a neighbourhood of
\infty
.

For
\lambda
in a neighbourhood of
0
, we use asymptotic developments for
T(\lambda+i0)
and
\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)^{*}
. The asymptotics of
\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)
are discussed in Lemma
2.12
. By taking adjoints in our Lemma
2.12
, we see that for each
s\geq 0
we have the expansion

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)^{*}

\displaystyle=2^{-\frac{1}{2}}\lambda^{\frac{n-2}{4}}(\gamma_{0}^{*}-i\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma_{1}^{*}+O(\lambda)^{\frac{1}{2}})

in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{P},H^{s,-t})
as
\lambda\to 0
.

The form of the asymptotic development of
T(\lambda+i0)
as
\lambda\to 0
depends heavily on the (non)existence of zero energy eigenvalues and zero energy resonances: the specific statements we use are summarised in Section
2.2
.

For
n\geq 5
the behaviour as
\lambda\to 0
of
T(\lambda+i0)
is described explicitly in Lemma
2.9
as

\displaystyle T(\lambda+i0)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-1}VP_{0}V+o(1)

in
\mathcal{B}(H^{1,-t},H^{0,-t})
as
\lambda\to 0
. The lowest order power of
\lambda
in the expansion of
\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}T(\lambda+i0)\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)^{*}
is thus the term
-\lambda^{\frac{n-5}{4}}\gamma_{0}VP_{0}V\gamma_{0}^{*}
. So we see that
\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}T(\lambda+i0)\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)^{*}
is bounded in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{P},H^{0,\rho-t})
as
\lambda\to 0
for
n\geq 5
.

We now consider
n=4
. For the asymptotic development of
T(\lambda+i0)
, we appeal to Lemma
2.7
. We obtain (with
\psi
the normalised zero energy resonance of Definition
2.6
and
a
the constant defined by Equation (
2.7
)) the expansion

\displaystyle T(\lambda+i0)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-1}VP_{0}V+\lambda^{-1}\left(a-\ln(\lambda)-i\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{-1}\langle V\psi,\cdot\rangle V\psi-\ln(\lambda)C_{1}+O(1)

as
\lambda\to 0
in
\mathcal{B}(H^{1,-t},H^{0,t})
. Hence we find as
\lambda\to 0
that

\displaystyle\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}T(\lambda+i0)\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)^{*}

\displaystyle=2^{-\frac{1}{2}}\lambda^{\frac{3}{4}}\left(\lambda^{-1}VP_{0}V+\lambda^{-1}\left(a-\ln(\lambda)-i\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{-1}\langle V\psi,\cdot\rangle V\psi-\ln(\lambda)C_{1}+O(1)\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(\gamma_{0}^{*}-i\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma_{1}^{*}+o(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}})\right)

\displaystyle=2^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\lambda^{-\frac{1}{4}}VP_{0}V\gamma_{0}^{*}+\lambda^{-\frac{1}{4}}\left(a-\ln(\lambda)-i\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{-1}\langle V\psi,\cdot\rangle V\psi\gamma_{0}^{*}+o(\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}\ln(\lambda))\right).

There are two terms of concern here as
\lambda\to 0
. The first is the term
\lambda^{-\frac{1}{4}}VP_{0}V\gamma_{0}^{*}
, which vanishes by
[
21
, Lemma 3.3]
. The second is the term containing
\psi
, which blows up as
\lambda\to 0
. Thus we must make the assumption
\psi=0
, that is there are no resonances.
∎

The case
n=2
has been resolved in
[
37
]
in the non-resonant case and
[
38
]
in the case of
s
-resonances but not
p
-resonances. The presence of certain resonances in dimensions
n=2
and
n=4
is well-known to produce complicated behaviour in scattering theory, see for instance
[
11
, Theorem III.3]
and
[
4
, Theorem 6.3]
, and so it is not surprising that we must exclude them in our analysis for dimension
n=4
, as is done in
[
38
]
for dimension
n=2
.

We also note the following essential corollary of Lemmas
3.7
and
3.9
.

Corollary 3.10
.

Suppose that
V
,
\rho
and
t
satisfy Assumption
2.8
. Then for
n\geq 2
we have the equality
S(\infty)=\textup{Id}
in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
.

Proof.

For dimension
n\geq 4
, we note that the operators
G_{-}(\lambda)
and
B(\lambda)
satisfy
G_{-}(\lambda)B=S(\lambda)-\textup{Id}
and thus, since
G_{-}
vanishes as

and

is bounded we have the desired result. In dimension
n=3
, a combination of
[
36
, Lemmas 2.2 and 2.4]
in an analogous fashion shows that

. In dimension
n=2
, the statement is an immediate corollary of
[
38
, Theorem 13]
.
∎

3.4
Interchanging limits

In this subsection we interchange the integral over
\lambda
and the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
in Equation (
3.2
).

For fixed
\varepsilon,\lambda>0
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
we define for
f,g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathcal{P})
the following functions

\displaystyle h_{\varepsilon}(\lambda)

\displaystyle:=\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}T(\lambda+i\varepsilon)F_{0}^{*}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(M-\lambda)f\in H^{s,0},\quad\textup{ and }

(3.6)

\displaystyle p(\lambda)

\displaystyle:=\lambda^{-\frac{1}{4}}\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)^{*}g(\lambda)\in H^{-s,0}.

We also denote by

q_{\lambda}(\nu)=\begin{cases}\left(\frac{\nu+\lambda}{\lambda}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}p(\nu+\lambda)&\nu>-\lambda\\
0&\nu\leq-\lambda\end{cases}

(3.7)

the extension by zero of the function
(-\lambda,\infty)\ni\nu\mapsto\left(\frac{\nu+\lambda}{\lambda}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}p(\nu+\lambda)\in H^{0,-s}
to all of
\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
n\geq 4
and suppose that
\rho,t
satisfy Assumption
2.8
, and that there are no resonances for
n=4
. If
|V(x)|\leq C(1+|x|)^{-\rho}
then one has the equality

\displaystyle\langle F_{0}(W_{-}-\textup{Id})F_{0}^{*}f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{spec}}

\displaystyle=-i\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}{\left\langle h_{0}(\lambda),\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}^{i\nu z}q_{\lambda}(\nu)\,\mathrm{d}\nu}\right\rangle_{H^{0,s},H^{0,-s}}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda}.

Proof.

Take
f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathcal{P})
and
g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\odot C(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
, and set
m=\rho-t
.
We may then write the expression (
3.2
), using the functions
h_{\varepsilon}
and
p
defined in Equation (
3.6
), as

\displaystyle\langle F_{0}(W_{-}-\textup{Id})F_{0}^{*}f,g\rangle

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\left(\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\int_{0}^{\infty}{\langle T(\lambda+i\varepsilon)F_{0}^{*}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(M-\lambda)f,(\mu-\lambda+i\varepsilon)^{-1}\Gamma_{0}(\mu)^{*}g(\mu)\rangle_{H^{0,m},H^{0,-m}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}{\left(\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}{\left\langle h_{\varepsilon}(\lambda),\frac{\lambda^{-\frac{1}{4}}\mu^{\frac{1}{4}}}{\mu-\lambda+i\varepsilon}p(\mu)\right\rangle_{H^{0,m},H^{0,-m}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda}.

We now use the formula

\displaystyle(\mu-\lambda+i\varepsilon)^{-1}

\displaystyle=-i\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}^{i(\mu-\lambda)z}\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon z}\,\mathrm{d}z}

and Fubini’s theorem to obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\int_{0}^{\infty}{\left\langle h_{\varepsilon}(\lambda),\frac{\lambda^{-\frac{1}{4}}\mu^{\frac{1}{4}}}{\mu-\lambda+i\varepsilon}p(\mu)\right\rangle_{H^{0,m},H^{0,-m}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu}}

\displaystyle=-i\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon z}\left(\left\langle h_{\varepsilon}(\lambda),\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}^{i(\mu-\lambda)z}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{4}}\mu^{\frac{1}{4}}p(\mu)\,\mathrm{d}\mu}\right\rangle_{H^{0,m},H^{0,-m}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}z}}

\displaystyle=-i\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon z}\left(\left\langle h_{\varepsilon}(\lambda),\int_{-\lambda}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}^{i\nu z}\left(\frac{\nu+\lambda}{\lambda}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}p(\nu+\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\nu}\right\rangle_{H^{0,m},H^{0,-m}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}z}},

(3.8)

where in the last line we have made a change of variables to recognise a Fourier transform. The
z
-integrand of (
3.4
) can be bounded independently of
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
explicitly with

\displaystyle\left|\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon z}\left\langle h_{\varepsilon}(\lambda),\int_{-\lambda}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}^{i\nu z}\left(\frac{\nu+\lambda}{\lambda}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}p(\nu+\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\nu}\right\rangle_{H^{0,m},H^{0,-m}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left|h_{\varepsilon}(\lambda)\right|\right|_{H^{0,m}}\left|\left|\int_{-\lambda}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}^{i\nu z}\left(\frac{\nu+\lambda}{\lambda}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}p(\nu+\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\nu}\right|\right|_{H^{0,-m}}

\displaystyle:=\left|\left|h_{\varepsilon}(\lambda)\right|\right|_{H^{0,m}}j_{\lambda}(z),

(3.9)

where we have used
\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon z}\leq 1
.
We also know from Lemma
3.8
that as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
h_{\varepsilon}(\lambda)
converges to
\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}T(\lambda+i0)\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)^{*}f(\lambda)
in
H^{0,m}
. Therefore the family
\left|\left|h_{\varepsilon}(\lambda)\right|\right|_{H^{0,m}}
(and thus the entire expression (
3.4
)) is bounded by a constant independent of
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.

In order to exchange the integral over
z
and the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
in Equation (
3.4
), it remains to show that the function
j_{\lambda}
of (
3.4
) is in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathrm{d}z)
. Recall
q_{\lambda}
from Equation (
3.7
). Note that
j_{\lambda}
in (
3.4
) can be rewritten as
j_{\lambda}(z)=(2\pi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left|\left|[\mathcal{F}^{*}q_{\lambda}](z)\right|\right|_{H^{0,-m}}
(here the Fourier transform is one-dimensional).

To estimate
j_{\lambda}
, we denote by
D=-i\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}
the self-adjoint operator acting (densely) on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and by
P=(\textup{Id}+D^{2})
. Then we have

\displaystyle\left|\left|[\mathcal{F}^{*}q_{\lambda}](z)\right|\right|_{H^{0,-m}}

\displaystyle=(1+z^{2})^{-1}\left|\left|[\mathcal{F}^{*}Pq_{\lambda}](z)\right|\right|_{H^{0,-m}}.

(3.10)

The function
z\mapsto(1+z^{2})^{-1}
is in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathrm{d}z)
and thus it suffices to show
\left|\left|[\mathcal{F}^{*}Pq_{\lambda}](z)\right|\right|_{H^{0,-m}}
is bounded independently of
z
.
Suppose that
g=\eta\otimes\xi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\otimes C(\mathcal{P})
is a simple tensor, so that we have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\nu+\lambda}{\lambda}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}[p(\nu+\lambda)](x)

\displaystyle=2^{-\frac{1}{2}}(2\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{4}}(\nu+\lambda)^{\frac{n-2}{4}}\eta(\nu+\lambda)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\mathrm{e}^{i(\nu+\lambda)^{\frac{1}{2}}\langle x,\omega\rangle}\xi(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\omega}.

Differentiating with respect to
\nu
twice shows that there are
\eta_{j,\lambda}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
such that

\displaystyle[Pq_{\lambda}](\nu)(x)

\displaystyle=\eta_{1,\lambda}(\nu)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\mathrm{e}^{i(\nu+\lambda)^{\frac{1}{2}}\langle x,\omega\rangle}\xi(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\omega}+\eta_{2,\lambda}(\nu)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\langle x,\omega\rangle\mathrm{e}^{i(\nu+\lambda)^{\frac{1}{2}}\langle x,\omega\rangle}\xi(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\omega}

\displaystyle+\eta_{3,\lambda}(\nu)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{(\langle x,\omega\rangle)^{2}\mathrm{e}^{i(\nu+\lambda)^{\frac{1}{2}}\langle x,\omega\rangle}\xi(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\omega}.

Then estimating each term individually we obtain
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},C>0
such that

\displaystyle|[\mathcal{F}^{*}Pq_{\lambda}](z)(x)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}+C_{2}|x|+C_{3}|x|^{2}\leq C(1+|x|^{2})

which gives

\displaystyle|[[\mathcal{F}^{*}Pq_{\lambda}](z)](x)|

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|x|^{2}).

For
\tau(x)=(1+|x|^{2})
, we compute that

\displaystyle\left|\left|\tau\right|\right|_{H^{0,-m}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}{(1+|x|^{2})^{-\frac{m}{2}}(1+|x|^{2})\,\mathrm{d}x}=\textup{Vol}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})\int_{0}^{\infty}{r^{n-1}(1+r^{2})^{1-\frac{m}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}r},

which is finite for
n-m-1<-1
, or
m>n+2
. Thus
\tau\in H^{0,-m}
for
m>n+2
.

We note in passing that the condition on
m=\rho-t
requires
\rho>n+t+2
, which is guaranteed by Assumption
2.8
.

By linearity, for each
g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\odot C(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
we find that
\left|\left|[\mathcal{F}^{*}Pq_{\lambda}](z)\right|\right|_{H^{0,-m}}
is bounded independently of
z
. Thus using Equation (
3.10
) we have
\left|\left|[\mathcal{F}^{*}q_{\lambda}](z)\right|\right|_{H^{0,-m}}\leq\tilde{C}(1+z^{2})^{-1}
and so
\left|\left|[\mathcal{F}^{*}q_{\lambda}](z)\right|\right|_{H^{0,-m}}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
.

As a consequence, we can apply Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem to exchange the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
with the integral over
z
in Equation (
3.4
) and obtain

\displaystyle-i\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon z}\left(\left\langle h_{\varepsilon}(\lambda),\int_{-\lambda}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}^{i\nu z}\left(\frac{\nu+\lambda}{\lambda}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}p(\nu+\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\nu}\right\rangle_{H^{0,m},H^{0,-m}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}z}}

\displaystyle=-i\left\langle h_{0}(\lambda),\int_{0}^{\infty}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathrm{e}^{i\nu z}q_{\lambda}(\nu)\,\mathrm{d}\nu}\,\mathrm{d}z}\right\rangle_{H^{0,m},H^{0,-m}}.

We have thus shown (on a dense set of

) the equality

\displaystyle\langle F_{0}(W_{-}-\textup{Id})F_{0}^{*}f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{spec}}

\displaystyle=-i\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}{\left\langle h_{0}(\lambda),\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}^{i\nu z}q_{\lambda}(\nu)\,\mathrm{d}\nu}\right\rangle_{H^{0,m},H^{0,-m}}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda}.\qed

3.5
Identification of function of dilation, completion of proof

Recall now the operator
D_{+}

using the group structure via the formula

\displaystyle[\psi(D_{+})f](x)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{[\mathcal{F}^{*}\psi](t)[U_{+}(t)f](x)\,\mathrm{d}t}.

\psi\in C(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
defined by

\displaystyle\psi(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\tanh{(2\pi x)}-i\cosh{(2\pi x)}^{-1}\right).

(3.11)

The Hilbert spaces
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+},H^{s,t})
and
\mathcal{H}_{spec}
can be naturally identified with the Hilbert spaces

and

. We recall the operator
G_{-}
of Equation (
3.5
) and
B
from Lemma
3.9
.

Theorem 3.13
.

n\geq 4
and suppose that
\rho,t
satisfy Assumption
2.8
, and that there are no resonances for
n=4
. If
|V(x)|\leq C(1+|x|)^{-\rho}

\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{spec})
the equality

\displaystyle F_{0}(W_{-}-\textup{Id})F_{0}^{*}

\displaystyle=-2\pi iG_{-}(\psi(D_{+})\otimes\textup{Id}_{H^{0,\rho-t}})B.

Take
f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathcal{P})
and
g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\odot C(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
, and set
m=\rho-t
. We will show that
\langle F_{0}(W_{-}-\textup{Id})F_{0}^{*}f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{spec}}
is equal to
\left\langle-2\pi iN(\psi(D_{+})\otimes\textup{Id}_{H^{0,m}})Bf,g\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{spec}}
. We write
\chi_{+}
for the characteristic function of
\mathbb{R}^{+}
and note that
q_{\lambda}
has compact support to obtain the following (in the sense of distributions with values in
H^{0,-m}
).

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathrm{e}^{i\nu z}q_{\lambda}(\nu)\,\mathrm{d}\nu}\,\mathrm{d}z}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{[\mathcal{F}^{*}\chi_{+}](\nu)q_{\lambda}(\nu)\,\mathrm{d}\nu}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{-\lambda}^{\infty}{[\mathcal{F}^{*}\chi_{+}](\nu)\left(\frac{\nu+\lambda}{\lambda}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}p(\nu+\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\nu}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{[\mathcal{F}^{*}\chi_{+}](\lambda(\mathrm{e}^{\mu}-1))\lambda\mathrm{e}^{\frac{5\mu}{4}}p(\mathrm{e}^{\mu}\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\mu}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{[\mathcal{F}^{*}\chi_{+}](\lambda(\mathrm{e}^{\mu}-1))\lambda\mathrm{e}^{\frac{3\mu}{4}}[(U_{+}(\mu)\otimes\textup{Id}_{H^{0,-m}})p](\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\mu}.

Next we note that the inverse Fourier transform of the characteristic function

is the distribution

(here

denotes the principal value), from which we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathrm{e}^{i\nu z}q_{\lambda}(\nu)\,\mathrm{d}\nu}\,\mathrm{d}z}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\left(\pi\delta(\mathrm{e}^{\mu}-1)+i\textup{Pv}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\frac{3\mu}{4}}}{\mathrm{e}^{\mu}-1}\right)[(U_{+}(\mu)\otimes\textup{Id}_{H^{0,-s}})p](\lambda)\Bigg\rangle_{H^{0,m},H^{0,-m}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu}

Next we note that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\frac{3\mu}{4}}}{\mathrm{e}^{\mu}-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1}{\sinh{\left(\frac{\mu}{4}\right)}}+\frac{1}{\cosh{\left(\frac{\mu}{4}\right)}}\right)

and the known Fourier transform of the function
\psi
of Equation

in
[

, Table 20.1]
as

\displaystyle[\mathcal{F}\psi](\mu)

\displaystyle=\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}2^{-\frac{1}{2}}\delta(\mathrm{e}^{\mu}-1)+\frac{i}{4}(2\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\textup{Pv}\left(\frac{1}{\sinh{\left(\frac{\mu}{4}\right)}}+\frac{1}{\cosh{\left(\frac{\mu}{4}\right)}}\right).

Combining these facts, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle F_{0}(W_{-}-\textup{Id}f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{spec}}=i\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}\Bigg\langle h_{0}(\lambda),

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Bigg(\pi\delta(\mathrm{e}^{\mu}-1)+\frac{1}{4}\textup{Pv}\Bigg(\frac{1}{\sinh{\left(\frac{\mu}{4}\right)}}+\frac{1}{\cosh{\left(\frac{\mu}{4}\right)}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\times[(U_{+}(\mu)\otimes\textup{Id}_{H^{0,-m}})p](\lambda)\Bigg)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\Bigg\rangle_{H^{0,m},H^{0,-m}}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda.

We now note that
[Bf](\lambda)=h_{0}(\lambda)
,
p=G_{-}^{*}g
and

\displaystyle(\psi(D_{+})\otimes\textup{Id}_{H^{0,m}})^{*}p

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{[\mathcal{F}\psi](\mu)(U_{+}(\mu)\otimes\textup{Id}_{H^{0,m}})p\,\mathrm{d}\mu}.

Combining these facts we obtain

\displaystyle\langle F_{0}(W_{-}-\textup{Id})F_{0}^{*}f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{spec}}

\displaystyle=2\pi i\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}{\left\langle[Bf](\lambda),[(\psi(D_{+})^{*}\otimes\textup{Id}_{H^{0,-s}})G_{-}^{*}g](\lambda)\right\rangle_{H^{0,m},H^{0,-m}}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda}

\displaystyle=\left\langle-2\pi iG_{-}(\psi(D_{+})\otimes\textup{Id}_{H^{0,m}})Bf,g\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{spec}}.

Since both
C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathcal{P})
and
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\odot C(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
are dense in
\mathcal{H}_{spec}
, this concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.14
.

Take
s\geq 0
and
t>\frac{n}{2}
, and suppose that there are no resonances for
n=4
. Then the difference

\displaystyle(\psi(D_{+})\otimes\textup{Id}_{\mathcal{P}})G_{-}-G_{-}(\psi(D_{+})\otimes\textup{Id}_{H^{s,t}})

(3.12)

is in
\mathcal{K}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+},H^{s,t}),\mathcal{H}_{spec})
.

This is a straightforward generalisation of
[

, Lemma 2.7]
and thus we omit the proof.

We now recall the action of the dilation group in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as
[U_{n}(t)f](x)=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{nt}{2}}f(\mathrm{e}^{t}x)
, and denote its self-adjoint generator by
D_{n}
. Then we have the relation
F_{0}D_{n}F_{0}^{*}=-2D_{+}\otimes\textup{Id}_{\mathcal{P}}
on
\Dom(D_{+}\otimes\textup{Id}_{\mathcal{P}})
. Thus if we define
\varphi(x)=\frac{1}{2}(1+\tanh{(\pi x)}-i\cosh{(\pi x)}^{-1})
, we find

\displaystyle F_{0}\varphi(D_{n})F_{0}^{*}

\displaystyle=\psi(D_{+})\otimes\textup{Id}_{\mathcal{P}}.

(3.13)

We can now prove the main result.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

We deduce from Theorem
3.13
and Lemma
3.14
that

\displaystyle W_{-}-\textup{Id}

\displaystyle=-2\pi iF_{0}^{*}G_{-}(\psi(D_{+})\otimes\textup{Id}_{H^{0,\rho-t}})BF_{0}

\displaystyle=-2\pi iF_{0}^{*}\big[G_{-}(\psi(D_{+})\otimes\textup{Id}_{H^{0,\rho-t}})-(\psi(D_{+})\otimes\textup{Id}_{\mathcal{P}})G_{-}+(\psi(D_{+})\otimes\textup{Id}_{\mathcal{P}})G_{-}\big]BF_{0}

\displaystyle=-2\pi iF_{0}^{*}(\psi(D_{+})\otimes\textup{Id}_{\mathcal{P}})G_{-}BF_{0}+K

\displaystyle=\varphi(D_{n})F_{0}^{*}(-2\pi i)G_{-}BF_{0}+K,

with
K:=-2\pi iF_{0}^{*}\big(G_{-}(\psi(D_{+})\otimes\textup{Id}_{H^{0,\rho-t}})-(\psi(D_{+})\otimes\textup{Id}_{\mathcal{P}})G_{-}\big)BF_{0}\in\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H})
. Note that the last equality follows from Equation (
3.13
).
We can compute explicitly that

\displaystyle-2\pi i[G_{-}Bf](\lambda,\omega)

\displaystyle=-2\pi i[\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)T(\lambda+i0)\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)^{*}f(\lambda,\cdot)](\omega)=[(S(\lambda)-\textup{Id})f(\lambda,\cdot)](\omega),

by Theorem
2.10
.
Thus we see that
F_{0}^{*}(-2\pi i)G_{-}BF_{0}=S-\textup{Id}
, proving the claim.
∎

We note that the case
n=4
differs from the case
n=2
in the contribution of different types of resonances. As shown in
[
38
]
in dimension
n=2
, we need to exclude
p
-resonances but not
s
-resonances to obtain our structural formula. This distinction comes from a detailed analysis of the low energy behaviour of the operator
T(z)
as in
[
22
]
for dimension
n=2
and
[
21
]
for dimension
n=4
. When computing a low energy expansion for
T(z)
in dimension four, all ‘bad’ behaviour in the expansion is confined to a single rank-one operator, whilst in dimension two all ‘bad’ behaviour in the expansion is confined to a rank-one and a rank-two operator which allows us to distinguish the different kinds of resonances. In dimensions
n=2,4
it is known that resonances give an integer contribution to statements of Levinson’s theorem
[
5
, Theorem 6.3]
in contrast to the half-integer contributions which occur in dimensions
n=1,3
.

We also note that as an immediate consequence of Theorem
3.1
we can view Levinson’s theorem in all dimensions as an index theorem in an identical manner to that of the case
n=3
discussed in
[
27
, Sections 4-6]
, to which we refer for details. In the next section, we instead use the result of Theorem
3.1
to give a new topological interpretation of Levinson’s theorem.

4
Levinson’s theorem as an index pairing

We now show how the formula for the wave operator implies that the number of bound states is the result of an index pairing between
K
-theory and
K
-homology, see
[
8
,
12
]
. We first recall the definition of a spectral triple.

Definition 4.1
.

An odd
spectral triple
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{H},\mathcal{D})
is given by a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, a

*
-subalgebra
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
acting on

\mathcal{H}
, and a densely defined unbounded self-adjoint operator
\mathcal{D}
such that:

1.
a\cdot{\rm dom}\,\mathcal{D}\subset{\rm dom}\,\mathcal{D}
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
, so that

da:=[\mathcal{D},a]
is densely defined. Moreover,
da
extends to a bounded operator for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
;

2.
a(1+\mathcal{D}^{2})^{-1/2}\in\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H})
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
.

Spectral triples define classes in the
K
-homology of the
norm closure
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
, a
C^{*}
-algebra. We will produce a spectral triple for
C_{c}^{\infty}((0,\infty),\mathcal{K})
where
\mathcal{K}
is the compact operators on
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
.

Lemma 4.2
.

The spaces
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathrm{d}x)
and
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathrm{d}y)
are unitarily equivalent via the map
W:L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
given by

\displaystyle[Wf](y)

\displaystyle=y^{-\frac{1}{2}}f(\ln(y))

(4.1)

with adjoint
W^{*}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathrm{d}y)\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathrm{d}x)
given by
[W^{*}g](x)=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{x}{2}}g(\mathrm{e}^{x}).

Proof.

This is a simple check.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

The two spectral triples

\Big(C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}),L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathrm{d}x),\frac{1}{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\Big)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Big(C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+}),L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathrm{d}y),\frac{y}{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}+\frac{1}{2i}\Big)

are unitarily equivalent.

Proof.

Conjugating
\frac{\lambda}{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}+\frac{1}{2i}
by
W:L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
gives
\frac{1}{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}
.
∎

Since
-i\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}
defines a non-trivial (indeed generating)
K
-homology class for
C_{0}(\mathbb{R})
, we see that the generator of dilations on the half-line defines a non-trivial
K
-homology class for
C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
.

Using this identification we immediately get the following in our context.

Corollary 4.4
.

The data
(C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\otimes\mathcal{K}(L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})),L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\otimes L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1}),D_{+}\otimes\textup{Id})
defines an odd spectral triple, and so a class
[D_{+}]
in odd
K
-homology.

Lemma 4.5
.

When
S(0)=\textup{Id}
the scattering operator defines an element of the odd
K
-theory
[S]\in K_{1}(C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\otimes\mathcal{K})
. For suitably decaying potentials, for example those satisfying Assumption
2.8
,
S(0)=\textup{Id}
in all dimensions except dimension 1 (generically) and dimension 3 (in the presence of resonances).

Corollary
4.4
and Lemma
4.5
tell us that we can pair the classes
[D_{+}]
and
[S]
to obtain an integer. See
[
12
, Section 8.7]
for details. Sadly the fact that our algebra
C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\otimes\mathcal{K}
is nonunital (both
C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
and
\mathcal{K}
!) means that we need to be careful about applying index pairing formulae. See
[
7
, Section 2.3]
for a description of the problems.

In
[
7
, Section 2.7]
it was shown that for spectral triples
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{H},\mathcal{D})
over a nonunital algebra, the index pairing with the class of a unitary
u
can be computed as
{\rm Index}(PuP-(\textup{Id}-P))
where
P=\chi_{[0,\infty)}(\mathcal{D})
is the non-negative spectral projection of
\mathcal{D}
.

In our setting, however, we will be using an approximation of the non-positive spectral projection of
D_{+}\otimes\textup{Id}
and we need to show that we still get a Fredholm operator.

Lemma 4.6
.

If
B=GG^{*}
is invertible modulo compacts then
G
is Fredholm.

Proof.

Write
B=A+K
where
A
is invertible and
K
is compact. Then
GG^{*}A^{-1}=BA^{-1}=\textup{Id}+KA^{-1}
and so
G
is Fredholm with approximate right inverse
G^{*}A^{-1}
.
∎

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
H=H_{0}+V
be such that the wave operators exist, are complete and are of the form of Equation (
1.1
) with
S(0)=\textup{Id}
. Let
S
be the corresponding scattering operator. We have the pairing

\displaystyle\langle[S],[D_{+}]\rangle

\displaystyle=-\textup{Index}(W_{-})=N,

where
N
is the number of bound states of
H
(eigenvalues counted with multiplicity).

Proof.

We know from
[
12
, Section 8.7]
that the pairing can be computed as

\displaystyle\langle[S],[D_{+}]\rangle

\displaystyle=\textup{Index}(PSP-(\textup{Id}-P))=\textup{Index}(PSP+(\textup{Id}-P)),

where
P
is the non-negative spectral projection for
D_{+}\otimes\textup{Id}
. The second equality follows from the homotopy
t\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{i\pi(1-t)}(\textup{Id}-P)
. We note that the pairing can also be computed using the non-positive spectral projection
P_{-}
for
D_{+}\otimes\textup{Id}
, at the expense of a minus sign, since
[2P_{+}-1]=-[2P_{-}-1]\in K^{1}(C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathcal{K}))
. The result is

\displaystyle\langle[S],[D_{+}]\rangle

\displaystyle=-\textup{Index}(P_{-}SP_{-}-(\textup{Id}-P_{-}))=-\textup{Index}(P_{-}SP_{-}+(\textup{Id}-P_{-})).

We will use this second form, and for convenience drop the subscript ‘
-
’.
To compute the pairing, we need to consider the wave operator in the spectral representation. We recall that the operator
D_{n}
satisfies
F_{0}D_{n}F_{0}^{*}=-2(D_{+}\otimes\textup{Id})
.

So we need to be able to approximate
P
with the operator
\varphi(-2D_{+})\otimes\textup{Id}
. We initially work with
D_{n}
for convenience. We set
T=\varphi(D_{n})
and
t=\tanh{(\pi D_{n})}
for simplicity. A quick computation shows that

\displaystyle TT^{*}

\displaystyle=T^{*}T=\frac{1}{2}(\textup{Id}+t).

Starting from
W_{-}
there is a compact operator
K
such that

\displaystyle W_{-}

\displaystyle=\textup{Id}-\frac{1}{2}(\textup{Id}+t)+TST^{*}+K.

We can also find a compact
K_{1}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\textup{Id}-t)TS^{*}T^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\textup{Id}-t)TT^{*}S^{*}+\frac{1}{2}(\textup{Id}-t)[T,S^{*}]T^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}(\textup{Id}-t^{2})S^{*}+\frac{1}{2}(\textup{Id}-t)[T,S^{*}]T^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}(\textup{Id}-t^{2})+\frac{1}{4}(\textup{Id}-t^{2})(S^{*}-\textup{Id})+\frac{1}{2}(\textup{Id}-t)[T,S^{*}]T^{*}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{4}(\textup{Id}-t^{2})+K_{1}.

Similarly we find compacts
K_{2},K_{3}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}TST^{*}(\textup{Id}-t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}(\textup{Id}-t^{2})+K_{2},

\displaystyle TST^{*}TS^{*}T^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}(\textup{Id}+t)^{2}+K_{3}.

We can then check (with all equalities mod compacts) that

\displaystyle W_{-}W_{-}^{*}=(\textup{Id}-\frac{1}{2}(\textup{Id}+t)+TST^{*})(\textup{Id}-\frac{1}{2}(\textup{Id}+t)+TS^{*}T^{*})

\displaystyle=\textup{Id}-\frac{1}{2}(\textup{Id}-t^{2})

which is invertible and hence
\textup{Id}-\frac{1}{2}(\textup{Id}+t)+TST^{*}
is invertible modulo compacts by Lemma
4.6
. Now we compare
\textup{Id}-\frac{1}{2}(\textup{Id}+t)+TST^{*}
and
PSP+(\textup{Id}-P)
to see that they have the same Fredholm index.
Taking commutators we have (mod compacts) the equalities

\displaystyle\textup{Id}-\frac{1}{2}(\textup{Id}+t)+TST^{*}

\displaystyle=\textup{Id}-TT^{*}+TST^{*}=\textup{Id}+T(S-\textup{Id})T^{*}

and

PSP+(\textup{Id}-P)=\textup{Id}+P(S-\textup{Id})P.

Define in the spectral representations the operators
\tilde{T}
and
\tilde{t}
by
\tilde{T}=F_{0}TF_{0}^{*}=\frac{1}{2}(\textup{Id}-\tanh{(2\pi D_{+})}-i\cosh{(2\pi D_{+})}^{-1})\otimes\textup{Id}
and
\tilde{t}=F_{0}tF_{0}^{*}=-\tanh{(2\pi D_{+})}\otimes\textup{Id}
. Taking the difference, and using
P=\frac{1}{2}(\textup{Id}-\textup{sign}(D_{+}))
we thus have

\displaystyle(P\otimes\textup{Id})S(P\otimes\textup{Id})+(\textup{Id}-(P\otimes\textup{Id}))-\big(\textup{Id}-\tilde{T}\tilde{T}^{*}+\tilde{T}S\tilde{T}^{*}\big)

\displaystyle=P(S-\textup{Id})P-\tilde{T}(S-\textup{Id})\tilde{T}^{*}

\displaystyle=(P-\tilde{T}\tilde{T}^{*})(S-\textup{Id})\bmod\mbox{compacts}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(-\textup{sign}+\tanh)(D_{+})(S-\textup{Id})\bmod\mbox{compacts}.

(4.2)

We note that the second equality, where we have commuted the exact spectral projection with
S
up to compacts, is justified by the arguments in
[
7
, Section 2.7]
.

To complete the argument, observe that
\frac{1}{2}(-\textup{sign}+\tanh)
is an
L^{2}
function, and for any compactly supported function
\chi\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
we have
\lambda\mapsto\chi(\lambda)\|S(\lambda)-\textup{Id}\|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{P})}
is
L^{2}
as well. Hence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(-\textup{sign}+\tanh)(D_{+})\chi(S(\cdot)-\textup{Id})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(-\textup{sign}+\tanh)(D_{+})\chi\|(S(\cdot)-\textup{Id})\|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{P})}\|(S(\cdot)-\textup{Id})\|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{P})}^{-1}(S(\cdot)-\textup{Id})

is a product of
L^{2}
functions, one of
D_{+}
and one of
H_{0}
, composed with a uniformly bounded compact operator-valued function
\|(S(\cdot)-\textup{Id})\|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{P})}^{-1}(S(\cdot)-\textup{Id})
. Applying the standard
f(x)g(\nabla)
result

[
39
, Theorem 4.1]
we obtain a compact operator times a uniformly bounded operator, which is then compact.
As
\lambda\mapsto\|(S(\lambda)-\textup{Id})\|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{P})}
is continuous and vanishes at
\lambda=0,\infty
, we can take an approximate unit
\chi_{m}\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
and see that

\frac{1}{2}(-\textup{sign}+\tanh)(D_{+})\chi_{m}\|(S(\cdot)-\textup{Id})\|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{P})}

converges in norm. Hence the limit
\frac{1}{2}(-\textup{sign}+\tanh)(D_{+})\|(S(\cdot)-\textup{Id})\|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{P})}
is compact.

Since the difference (
4.2
) is compact, the two Fredholm operators
W_{-}
and
P(S-\textup{Id})P
have the same index.
The fact that
\textup{Ind}(W_{-})=-N
follows immediately from the relations

\displaystyle W_{-}W_{-}^{*}

\displaystyle=\textup{Id}\quad\textup{ and }\quad W_{-}^{*}W_{-}=\textup{Id}-P_{p}(H),

where
P_{p}(H)
denotes the projection onto the point spectrum of
H
.
∎

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
V_{0}
and
V_{1}
be such that the wave operators exist, are complete and of the form of Equation (
1.1
). Suppose further that
S_{0}(0)=S_{1}(0)=\textup{Id}
, where
S_{0}
and
S_{1}
denote the scattering operators for the potentials
V_{0}
and
V_{1}
. If the number of eigenstates (counted with multiplicity) for
V_{0}
differs from that of
V_{1}
then their scattering matrices are not (stably) homotopic.

Corollary 4.9
.

Let
V_{0}
and
V_{1}
be such that the wave operators exist, are complete and of the form of Equation (
1.1
). Suppose further that
S_{0}(0)=S_{1}(0)=\textup{Id}
, where
S_{0}
and
S_{1}
denote the scattering operators for the potentials
V_{0}
and
V_{1}
. Consider the path
V_{t}=(1-t)V_{0}+tV_{1}
for
t\in[0,1]
with corresponding scattering operators
S_{t}
. If the number of eigenstates (counted with multiplicity) for
V_{0}
differs from that of
V_{1}
, then the path
S_{t}
is not norm continuous.

In fact the proof shows that there are a discrete number of points at which the path
S_{t}
fails to be norm continuous, corresponding to ‘jumps’ in the number of eigenvalues for the potential
V_{t}
. The norm holomorphy (which implies norm continuity) of the scattering operator as a function of
t
is discussed in
[
6
, Theorem 4.2]
where an equivalent condition to holomorphy of
S_{t}
is given. The points of failure of norm continuity in Corollary
4.9
are also points of failure of holomorphy in
[
6
, Theorem 4.2]
. In the case of a Rollnik class potential on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
holomorphy of the scattering matrix as a function of
t
has been studied in
[
23
, Theorem 6.1]
and
[
6
, Theorem 5.2]
.

The case
S(0)\neq\textup{Id}
cannot be handled by the simple pairing described in Theorem
4.7
, a more complicated object than simply the dilation operator is required to pair with the class of the scattering matrix. Such results will be discussed elsewhere.
