α − \alpha- surfaces and global coordinates in black hole spacetimes
Bernardo Araneda, Bernard F. Whiting

Abstract
We present a discussion of the periodicity in imaginary time of maximally extended black hole spacetimes without reference to Euclidean manifolds. As motivation, we first demonstrate our approach for the Rindler geometry in flat space before then applying it for the case of Schwarzschild time in the maximally extended Kruskal geometry. One notable advantage of our approach is that it can be utilized in the Kerr case, again without reference to a Euclidean manifold, and without complexifying the angular momentum parameter, a a . One unusual feature of our application in the Kerr case is that, even for the purely Lorentzian geometry, it is developed in terms of explicitly complex coordinates which are associated with distinct families of α − \alpha- surfaces. Moreover, coordinatization of these gives a single set of coordinates which cover the entire geometry outside the inner horizon.



1
Introduction

Periodicity in imaginary time for black hole spacetimes obviously implies considering some complexification of the spacetime coordinates, but does not require that one obtain a fully Euclidean section in the complexified manifold. However,
in the path integral approach to the quantization of gauge theories, the use of positive-definite (Euclidean) rather than Lorentzian manifolds can be motivated (see e.g.
[
1
]
) by the observation that the path integral is better behaved for the former class of metrics. The extension of this method to the quantization of gravitational fields leads to the Euclidean approach to quantum gravity
[
2
]
, where a similar motivation can be given
[
3
]
. An additional feature in the gravity case is that, since the path integral includes geometries such as black holes, one faces difficulties in the evaluation of the gravitational action due to spacetime singularities
[
4
]
. The Euclidean approach circumvents this issue by evaluating the action on a positive-definite section of the complexified geometry that is complete and free of singularities (a “gravitational instanton”
[
5
]
).

The Euclidean section of the Schwarzschild solution can be obtained by simply replacing
t
by
{-}{\rm i}\tau
in Schwarzschild coordinates (where
{\rm i}^{2}=-1
), and declaring
\tau
to be real. One then notices that the space is free of (conical) singularities if
\tau
is interpreted as an angular variable with certain periodicity. When trying to do the same for the Kerr solution, one finds that the replacement
t\to{-}{\rm i}\tau
(using now Boyer-Lindquist coordinates) produces a complex metric
[
4
]
,
unless
one simultaneously replaces the angular momentum parameter
a
by
{\rm i}a^{\prime}
and declares
a^{\prime}
to be real; this then gives the Euclidean section of Kerr
[
6
]
. The singularity-free requirement leads to certain identifications for the
\tau
and
\phi
variables of the Euclidean section
[
6
]
.

The use of imaginary time can also be motivated from quantum-mechanical and thermodynamical considerations different to those given above (e.g., the Schrödinger and heat equations are related by a Wick rotation; more generally see
[
7
]
). The approach to black hole thermodynamics from the Euclidean quantum gravity perspective would then seem to require finding real, positive-definite, complete and singularity-free sections of black hole spacetimes. However, it is perhaps hard to give a compelling physical argument for the replacement
a\to{\rm i}a^{\prime}
in the Kerr solution (see e.g.
[
8
,
9
]
), since
a
is a fixed parameter that is supposed to characterize the rotation of an astrophysical black hole. (In fact, the original derivation of the thermodynamical properties of rotating black holes in
[
4
]
uses directly the complex Kerr geometry.)

In this paper, we wish to show that periodicity in imaginary time, together with possible imaginary shifts in angular variables, can be derived from a unified treatment of the global, analytic, and “Hermitian” properties of both static and rotating black hole spacetimes, without any reference to Euclidean sections, replacements of the sort
a\to{\rm i}a^{\prime}
, or explicit requirements of absence of conical singularities.

The “global” and “analytic” properties are familiar in relativity; we are here referring to the fact that black holes have horizons that act as boundaries for geodesically incomplete regions, which leads one to look for analytic extensions across these boundaries and to the construction of a global black hole spacetime. As we highlight further below, the extension is constructed by exploiting the special structure of null geodesics of these black holes.

The “Hermitian” property is perhaps less known: it refers to the fact, discovered by Flaherty
[
10
,
11
]
, that black hole solutions in general relativity have the Lorentzian analog of an integrable almost-complex structure compatible with the metric.
This implies that any principal null geodesic in a black hole spacetime is a real ray in an
\alpha-
surface
(or
twistor surface
): a 2-complex-dimensional, totally null, totally geodesic, surface lying in the complexified manifold
[
12
,
13
]
.
As we will show,
\alpha-
surfaces provide special complex coordinate systems that: allow to extend the spacetime across future and past horizons, cover the entire geometry outside the inner horizon of Kerr, and lead to periodicity in imaginary time along with an imaginary shift in the azimuthal angle.

We began our investigation by using coordinates defined by geodesics. However, for the Kerr case, they proved to be insufficient for the task. Instead, it became apparent during the course of our work that the presence of families of
\alpha-
surfaces in the complexified geometries played a distinguished role in our approach. Black hole horizons are totally geodesic null surfaces.
Moreover, we will show that, in complexified black hole spacetimes, their horizons (future and past) lie within (distinct families of)
\alpha-
surfaces. Although we focus on the Schwarzschild and Kerr solutions, our method can in principle be applied to any black hole spacetime that admits
\alpha-
surfaces. This includes
[
14
]
the complete Plebański-Demiański family of solutions
[
15
]
, so one can add electromagnetics charges, cosmological constant, acceleration, and NUT parameters. These are all Petrov type D, but
\alpha-
surfaces turn out to also exist in black holes that are not algebraically special, such as Kerr-Sen and other solutions of supergravity
[
14
]
, and our procedure will also apply in these cases too.

In section
2
, we illustrate our method for the 2-dimensional (2D) Rindler spacetime. In
3
,
we apply our construction to the Kruskal extension of Schwarzschild. In section
4
we present our main results for Kerr. We conclude with a discussion in section
5
. We include an appendix
A
with further details about complex structures in Lorentz signature.

Schwarzschild, and its Kruskal Extension

\displaystyle{\rm d}{s}^{2}=-f(r){\rm d}{t}^{2}+\frac{{\rm d}{r}^{2}}{f(r)}+r^{2}({\rm d}\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta{\rm d}\phi^{2}),\qquad f(r):=1-\frac{2M}{r},

\displaystyle m^{a}\partial_{a}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{r\sqrt{2}}(\partial_{\theta}+\tfrac{{\rm i}}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\phi}),

(3.2c)

\displaystyle\bar{m}^{a}\partial_{a}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{r\sqrt{2}}(\partial_{\theta}-\tfrac{{\rm i}}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\phi}).

(3.2d)

4
Kerr

Preliminaries

4.2
Extended coordinates and
\alpha-
surfaces

In Schwarzschild, the procedure to cross the past and future horizons was to follow outgoing and ingoing geodesics, so we exchanged
(t,r)
by
(u,v)
, and since the angular variables were untouched, we could work with the coordinate system
(u,v,\theta,\phi)
. In Kerr, we see from (
4.7
) that

\displaystyle\text{outgoing:}\,\,\begin{cases}u=t-r^{*}\\
w=\phi-r^{\sharp}\\
\theta={\rm const.}\end{cases},\qquad\text{ingoing:}\,\,\begin{cases}v=t+r^{*}\\
z=\phi+r^{\sharp}\\
\theta={\rm const.}\end{cases},

(4.13)

so we have too many variables: we can work with
(u,v,w,\theta)
, or with
(u,v,z,\theta)
, or similar combinations, but none of these will be simultaneously adapted to both the outgoing and ingoing congruences. Therefore we need to do something different.

We thus define the following complex scalar fields (see
[
14
]
, and Appendix
A
for details):

\displaystyle\hat{u}:={}

\displaystyle u-{\rm i}a\cos\theta,

(4.14a)

\displaystyle\hat{v}:={}

\displaystyle v+{\rm i}a\cos\theta,

(4.14b)

\displaystyle\hat{w}:={}

\displaystyle w+{\rm i}\log\tan(\theta/2),

(4.14c)

\displaystyle\hat{z}:={}

\displaystyle z-{\rm i}\log\tan(\theta/2).

(4.14d)

Since the
\{\hat{u},\hat{v},\hat{w},\hat{z}\}
are functionally independent, they define a complex coordinate system for Kerr. Moreover, the Kerr line element (
4.1
) becomes:

\displaystyle{\rm d}{s}^{2}=g_{tt}{\rm d}{\hat{u}}{\rm d}{\hat{v}}+g_{t\phi}({\rm d}{\hat{u}}{\rm d}{\hat{z}}+{\rm d}{\hat{v}}{\rm d}{\hat{w}})+g_{\phi\phi}{\rm d}{\hat{w}}{\rm d}{\hat{z}}

(4.15)

This system is especially adapted to the principal null congruences: comparing (
4.14
) with (
4.13
), we see that if we fix an outgoing geodesic then we fix
(\hat{u},\hat{w})
(assuming that
(t,r,\theta,\phi)
are here real), while if we fix an ingoing geodesic then we fix
(\hat{v},\hat{z})
.

In the complexified Kerr spacetime, the set
(\hat{u},\hat{w})={\rm const.}
describes an
\alpha-
surface
– a 2D complex surface such that all tangent vectors are null and orthogonal
[
12
,
13
]
. Such surfaces are also totally geodesic. Their existence is equivalent to the existence of a shear-free null geodesic congruence. There is a 2-complex-parameter system of
\alpha-
surfaces associated to the outgoing congruence, see
[
13
, Prop. (7.3.18)]
. Analogously, there is a second set of
\alpha-
surfaces associated to the ingoing congruence, where each of these surfaces is described by
(\hat{v},\hat{z})={\rm const}
.

The coordinates
\hat{u}
and
\hat{w}
are naturally paired by the structure of
\alpha-
surfaces, and analogously for
\hat{v}
and
\hat{z}
. Moreover, one can perform holomorphic transformations of the form

\displaystyle(\hat{u},\hat{w})\to(\hat{u}^{\prime}(\hat{u},\hat{w}),\hat{w}^{\prime}(\hat{u},\hat{w})),\qquad(\hat{v},\hat{z})\to(\hat{v}^{\prime}(\hat{v},\hat{z}),\hat{z}^{\prime}(\hat{v},\hat{z})),

(4.16)

and the new coordinates
(\hat{u}^{\prime},\hat{w}^{\prime})
and
(\hat{v}^{\prime},\hat{z}^{\prime})
will describe the same families of
\alpha-
surfaces. That is, (
4.16
) preserves the complex structure underlying our construction (see eq. (
A.7
)).

Let us now focus on the region
{\rm I}\cup{\rm II}\cup{\rm I^{\prime}}\cup{\rm II^{\prime}}
(i.e.,
r>r_{-}
). Using (
4.14
), which are separately defined in all four regions, we define

\displaystyle U_{+}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\exp(-\kappa_{+}\hat{u}),\qquad&{\rm I,II^{\prime}}\\
-\exp(-\kappa_{+}\hat{u}),\qquad&{\rm I^{\prime},II}\end{cases}\qquad

\displaystyle V_{+}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\exp(\kappa_{+}\hat{v}),\qquad&{\rm I,II}\\
-\exp(\kappa_{+}\hat{v}),\qquad&{\rm I^{\prime},II^{\prime}}\end{cases}

(4.17a)

\displaystyle W_{+}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\exp(-\tfrac{\kappa_{+}}{\Omega_{+}}\hat{w}),\qquad&{\rm I,II^{\prime}}\\
-\exp(-\tfrac{\kappa_{+}}{\Omega_{+}}\hat{w}),\qquad&{\rm I^{\prime},II}\end{cases}\qquad

\displaystyle Z_{+}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\exp(\tfrac{\kappa_{+}}{\Omega_{+}}\hat{z}),\qquad&{\rm I,II}\\
-\exp(\tfrac{\kappa_{+}}{\Omega_{+}}\hat{z}),\qquad&{\rm I^{\prime},II^{\prime}}\end{cases}

(4.17b)

To gain some intuition, we can write these variables in region
{\rm I}
:

\displaystyle U_{+}\big|_{\rm I}

\displaystyle=e^{-\kappa_{+}(t-r-{\rm i}a\cos\theta)}\left(\frac{r}{r_{+}}-1\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{r}{r_{-}}-1\right)^{-\frac{\kappa_{+}}{2\kappa_{-}}},

(4.18a)

\displaystyle V_{+}\big|_{\rm I}

\displaystyle=e^{\kappa_{+}(t+r+{\rm i}a\cos\theta)}\left(\frac{r}{r_{+}}-1\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{r}{r_{-}}-1\right)^{-\frac{\kappa_{+}}{2\kappa_{-}}},

(4.18b)

\displaystyle W_{+}\big|_{\rm I}

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{\kappa_{+}}{\Omega_{+}}(\phi+{\rm i}\log\tan\frac{\theta}{2})}\left(\frac{r}{r_{+}}-1\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{r}{r_{-}}-1\right)^{-1/2},

(4.18c)

\displaystyle Z_{+}\big|_{\rm I}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{\kappa_{+}}{\Omega_{+}}(\phi-{\rm i}\log\tan\frac{\theta}{2})}\left(\frac{r}{r_{+}}-1\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{r}{r_{-}}-1\right)^{-1/2}.

(4.18d)

From the behaviour (
4.11
) (see also fig.
3
), we deduce

\displaystyle U_{+}\big|_{\mathcal{H}^{+,{\rm f}}}

\displaystyle=0,

(4.19a)

\displaystyle W_{+}\big|_{\mathcal{H}^{+,{\rm f}}}

\displaystyle=0,

(4.19b)

\displaystyle V_{+}\big|_{\mathcal{H}^{+,{\rm p}}}

\displaystyle=0,

(4.19c)

\displaystyle Z_{+}\big|_{\mathcal{H}^{+,{\rm p}}}

\displaystyle=0,

(4.19d)

and each is continuous across its respective horizon. Hence,
(U_{+},V_{+},W_{+},Z_{+})
cover
{\rm I}\cup{\rm II}\cup{\rm I^{\prime}}\cup{\rm II^{\prime}}
.

In terms of the variables (
4.17
), one family of
\alpha-
surfaces is now described by
(U_{+},W_{+})=const.
, and the other family of
\alpha-
surfaces is described by
(V_{+},Z_{+})=const.
(recall (
4.16
)). Since we see from (
4.19a
) and (
4.19b
) that at
\mathcal{H}^{+,{\rm f}}
we have
(U_{+},W_{+})=(0,0)
, then the future horizon lies in an
\alpha-
surface. The points of
\mathcal{H}^{+,{\rm f}}
are coordinatized by the two complex coordinates
(V_{+},Z_{+})
. One might think that there is an inconsistency here since
\mathcal{H}^{+,{\rm f}}
is a
three
-real-dimensional hypersurface and two complex coordinates generically amount to
four
real coordinates, however there is no
problem here since we see from (
4.17
) and (
4.14
) that the imaginary parts of
V_{+}
and
Z_{+}
are functionally dependent (they are both functions of
\theta
). In real terms,
\mathcal{H}^{+,{\rm f}}
is coordinatized by
(v,z,\theta)
. Analogously, we see from (
4.19c
)-(
4.19d
) that the past horizon
\mathcal{H}^{+,{\rm p}}
lies in an
\alpha-
surface (of the other family), and it is coordinatized by the two complex coordinates
(U_{+},W_{+})
or by the three real coordinates
(u,w,\theta)
.

It is not clear from (
4.19
) what happens at the intersection of the future and past horizons, i.e. at the bifurcation sphere. We will address this point in the next subsection, after we have discussed imaginary shifts, now in both
t
and
\phi
, across the horizons.

4.3
Imaginary shifts, periodicity, and the bifurcation sphere

To proceed as in the previous examples, we now introduce coordinates obtained by using
Log
rather than
\log
in the definitions of new radial functions
\widetilde{r^{*}}
and
\widetilde{r^{\sharp}}
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{r^{*}}(r):={}

\displaystyle r+\frac{1}{2\kappa_{+}}\text{Log}\left(\frac{r}{r_{+}}-1\right)-\frac{1}{2\kappa_{-}}\text{Log}\left(\frac{r}{r_{-}}-1\right),

(4.20)

\displaystyle\widetilde{r^{\sharp}}(r):={}

\displaystyle\frac{\Omega_{+}}{2\kappa_{+}}\text{Log}\left(\frac{r}{r_{+}}-1\right)-\frac{\Omega_{-}}{2\kappa_{-}}\text{Log}\left(\frac{r}{r_{-}}-1\right).

(4.21)

Then, our new tilde-coordinates will become:

\displaystyle\tilde{u}:={}

\displaystyle t-\widetilde{r^{*}}-{\rm i}a\cos\theta,

(4.22)

\displaystyle\tilde{v}:={}

\displaystyle t+\widetilde{r^{*}}+{\rm i}a\cos\theta,

(4.23)

\displaystyle\tilde{w}:={}

\displaystyle\phi-\widetilde{r^{\sharp}}+{\rm i}\log\tan(\theta/2),

(4.24)

\displaystyle\tilde{z}:={}

\displaystyle\phi+\widetilde{r^{\sharp}}-{\rm i}\log\tan(\theta/2).

(4.25)

As in the previous examples,
L=\text{Log}(r/r_{+}-1)
will receive a
\delta L=i\pi
jump on moving from region
{\rm I}
into region
{\rm II}
or
{\rm II}^{\prime}
. However, now we will have to consider all four coordinates, instead of just two. So, for
e^{\kappa_{+}\tilde{v}}
, and now also
e^{\kappa_{+}\tilde{z}/\Omega_{+}}
, to remain continuous across the future horizon
{\mathcal{H}^{+,{\rm f}}}
, and for
e^{-\kappa_{+}\tilde{u}}
, and now also
e^{\kappa_{+}\tilde{w}/\Omega_{+}}
, to remain continuous across the past horizon
{\mathcal{H}^{+,{\rm p}}}
in passing out of region
{\rm I}
, it is again necessary that
t
receive a
\delta t_{\rm I,II}=-{\rm i}\pi/(2\kappa_{+})
jump when passing into region
{\rm II}
, and a
\delta t_{\rm I,II^{\prime}}=+{\rm i}\pi/(2\kappa_{+})
jump when passing into region
{\rm II^{\prime}}
, while
\phi
must receive a jump
\delta\phi=\Omega_{+}\delta t
in both cases. As a consequence, in region
{\rm II}
,
e^{-\kappa_{+}\tilde{u}}
becomes
-e^{-\kappa_{+}\hat{u}}=U_{+}|_{\rm II}
and
e^{-\kappa_{+}\tilde{w}/\Omega_{+}}
becomes
-e^{-\kappa_{+}\hat{w}/\Omega_{+}}=W_{+}|_{\rm II}
, while in region
{\rm II^{\prime}}
,
e^{\kappa_{+}\tilde{v}}
becomes
-e^{\kappa_{+}\hat{v}}=V_{+}|_{\rm II^{\prime}}
and
e^{\kappa_{+}\tilde{z}/\Omega_{+}}
becomes
-e^{\kappa_{+}\hat{z}/\Omega_{+}}=Z_{+}|_{\rm II^{\prime}}
.

Similarly, in passing from regions
{\rm II}
or
{\rm II^{\prime}}
into region
{\rm I^{\prime}}
,
L=\text{Log}(r/r_{+}-1)
will receive a relative
\delta L=-{\rm i}\pi
jump, while
t
will receive an additional
\delta t_{\rm II,I^{\prime}}=-{\rm i}\pi/(2\kappa_{+})
jump, and
\phi
will receive an additional
\delta\phi=\Omega_{+}\delta t
jump, when passing from region
{\rm II}
into region
{\rm I^{\prime}}
, so that
e^{\kappa_{+}\tilde{v}}
becomes
-e^{\kappa_{+}\hat{v}}=V_{+}|_{\rm I^{\prime}}
and
e^{\kappa_{+}\tilde{z}/\Omega_{+}}
becomes
-e^{\kappa_{+}\hat{z}/\Omega_{+}}=Z_{+}|_{\rm I^{\prime}}
, while
e^{-\kappa_{+}\tilde{u}}
remains
-e^{-\kappa_{+}\hat{u}}=U_{+}|_{\rm I^{\prime}}
and
e^{-\kappa_{+}\tilde{w}/\Omega_{+}}
remains
-e^{-\kappa_{+}\hat{w}/\Omega_{+}}=W_{+}|_{\rm I^{\prime}}
; alternatively,
t
will receive an additional
\delta t_{\rm II^{\prime},I^{\prime}}={\rm i}\pi/(2\kappa_{+})
jump, and
\phi
will receive an additional
\delta\phi=\Omega_{+}\delta t
jump, when passing from region
{\rm II^{\prime}}
into region
{\rm I^{\prime}}
, so that
e^{-\kappa_{+}\tilde{u}}
becomes
-e^{-\kappa_{+}\hat{u}}=U_{+}|_{\rm I^{\prime}}
and
e^{-\kappa_{+}\tilde{w}/\Omega_{+}}
becomes
-e^{-\kappa_{+}\hat{w}/\Omega_{+}}=W_{+}|_{\rm I^{\prime}}
, while
e^{\kappa_{+}\tilde{v}}
remains
-e^{\kappa_{+}\hat{v}}=V_{+}|_{\rm I^{\prime}}
and
e^{\kappa_{+}\tilde{z}/\Omega_{+}}
remains
-e^{\kappa_{+}\hat{z}/\Omega_{+}}=Z_{+}|_{\rm I^{\prime}}
. These last two sets of results concerning
t
and
\phi
in region
{\rm I}^{\prime}
must be equivalent, that is, points obtained going anti-clockwise around the diagram and points obtained by going clockwise around the diagram must be identified, i.e., for
\beta_{+}=2\pi/\kappa_{+}
:

\displaystyle\left(t-\frac{i\beta_{+}}{2},\phi-\frac{i\beta_{+}}{2}\Omega_{+}\right)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\left(t+\frac{i\beta_{+}}{2},\phi+\frac{i\beta_{+}}{2}\Omega_{+}\right).

(4.26)

We can phrase this outcome more explicitly in terms of periodicity by defining the new quantity
\Phi_{+}=\phi-\Omega_{+}t
. Then:

\displaystyle(t,\Phi_{+})\sim(t,\Phi_{+}+2\pi)\sim(t+i\beta_{+},\Phi_{+})\sim(t+i\beta_{+},\Phi_{+}+2\pi),

(4.27)

so
t
must have imaginary periodicity
\beta_{+}=2\pi/\kappa_{+}
on the extended Kerr manifold, and
\phi
must also have a total imaginary shift of
\Omega_{+}\beta_{+}
.
Furthermore, with the understanding we have again developed for jumps in
t
,
and now also in
\phi
, between neighbouring regions, (
\tilde{u},\tilde{v},{\tilde{w},\tilde{z}}
) become global, piecewise continuous coordinates, and the identifications:

\displaystyle\tilde{U}_{+}=e^{-\kappa_{+}\tilde{u}},\qquad\tilde{V}_{+}=e^{\kappa_{+}\tilde{v}},\qquad{\tilde{W}_{+}=e^{-\kappa_{+}\tilde{w}/\Omega_{+}},\qquad\tilde{Z}_{+}=e^{\kappa_{+}\tilde{z}/\Omega_{+}}}

(4.28)

can be applied in all four regions simultaneously.

To address the issue of the bifurcation sphere, we introduce the following two coordinates:

\displaystyle{\check{w}_{+}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{w}-\Omega_{+}\tilde{u},

(4.29)

\displaystyle{\check{z}_{+}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{z}-\Omega_{+}\tilde{v}.

(4.30)

Note that
{\check{w}_{+}}
and
{\check{z}_{+}}
respect the structure of
\alpha-
surfaces (recall (
4.16
)). We have:

\displaystyle{\check{w}_{+}}={}

\displaystyle\Phi_{+}+\frac{(\Omega_{-}-\Omega_{+})}{2\kappa_{-}}\text{Log}\left(\frac{r}{r_{-}}-1\right)+{\rm i}\,(\log\tan(\theta/2)+a\Omega_{+}\cos\theta),

(4.31)

\displaystyle{\check{z}_{+}}={}

\displaystyle\Phi_{+}-\frac{(\Omega_{-}-\Omega_{+})}{2\kappa_{-}}\text{Log}\left(\frac{r}{r_{-}}-1\right)-{\rm i}\,(\log\tan(\theta/2)+a\Omega_{+}\cos\theta).

(4.32)

Thus, we see that
{\check{w}_{+}}
and
{\check{z}_{+}}
are regular at the bifurcation sphere. We can then use the coordinate system
(U_{+},V_{+},{\check{w}_{+}},{\check{z}_{+}})
to cover the entire region outside the inner horizon, and the bifurcation sphere corresponds to
U_{+}=0,V_{+}=0
and is coordinatized by
({\check{w}_{+}},{\check{z}_{+}})
.

We have focused on the outer horizon
r_{+}
, but an entirely analogous description can be developed for the inner horizon
r_{-}
, using the local complex coordinate system (
4.14
) to define the analogs of (
4.17
) but now replacing
\kappa_{+},\Omega_{+}
by
\kappa_{-},\Omega_{-}
. This does not introduce any complications, so we will omit the details here.

5
Discussion

The global structure of a maximally extended black hole spacetime is separated into a series of regions bounded by horizons, each region having a local “time” coordinate
t
and “azimuthal angle”
\phi
(more precisely,
t
and
\phi
parametrize the orbits of the Killing fields that are respectively timelike and spacelike —the latter with closed orbits— in the asymptotic infinity of the black hole exterior). In this paper, by exploiting the special null geodesic structure as understood from the perspective of
\alpha-
surfaces, we constructed coordinate systems that are continuous across horizons, cover the bifurcation sphere, and allow to show that the
t
’s and
\phi
’s in the different regions of rotating black holes are related by imaginary translations, while the horizons themselves are contained in
\alpha-
surfaces. This leads directly to imaginary time periodicity and similar shifts for
\phi
, without invoking Wick rotations, Euclidean sections, nor an artificial replacement of the angular momentum parameter.

Note that, in a somewhat different perspective, we can also interpret the imaginary translations in local Schwarzschild or Kerr coordinates as the imposition of different reality conditions in the complexifications of those coordinates. In other words, this point of view leads to a curious picture in which the different regions of a global black hole spacetime are different real slices of its holomorphic extension. (Similar ideas about imaginary translations and reality conditions are presented when describing Kerr as a —non-holomorphic— complex translation of Schwarzschild, according to the Newman-Janis shift
[
11
,
20
]
.) However, the above description is not exactly equivalent to the procedure we followed in this work, since it does not specify an unambiguous choice of analytic continuation across horizons, which is essential in our approach.

Apart from the initial example of the 2D Rindler spacetime, our discussion was centered around the extensions of the Schwarzschild and Kerr solutions. The horizon in the Rindler case is an acceleration horizon, whereas in the Schwarzschild and Kerr geometries it is an event horizon (including also a Cauchy horizon in the Kerr case). Since the key feature underlying our procedure is the existence of
\alpha-
surfaces, we can also apply our method to the other types of horizons that occur in spacetimes admitting
\alpha-
surfaces, such as the cosmological horizons of asymptotically de Sitter black holes, or the more complicated acceleration horizons of the C-metric and Plebański-Demiański solutions.

In the original works assigning an entropy and a temperature to black holes
[
21
,
22
]
, there was no reference to Euclideanization.
However, in the subsequent development of Euclidean quantum gravity, thermodynamical aspects of the gravitational field were often studied by employing positive-definite sections (e.g.
[
6
,
23
,
24
,
25
]
), and this sometimes involved unnatural complexifications of the parameters of a solution. By contrast, our use of
\alpha-
surfaces in this paper, which exposes a periodicity in imaginary
t
and
\phi
, directly supports a thermodynamic interpretation
[
4
,
8
]
; that is, our method applies to complexified geometries, regardless of whether they admit real sections with a particular signature.
In addition to the classical black holes of general relativity, our method of using
\alpha-
surfaces also applies to Lorentzian black holes that are not algebraically special, such as certain supergravity solutions (see
[
14
]
), and also gravitational instantons with Hermitian structures that do not have Lorentzian sections, but can still have physical effects (see
[
24
]
), such as the Chen-Teo instanton
[
26
]
, whose contribution to the quantum gravity partition function is as yet unknown.

Finally, the special complex coordinate systems that we constructed for Kerr will allow us to study various aspects of classical and quantum fields on the entire region outside the inner horizon of rotating black holes, analogously to what has been done in the static case for the extended Schwarzschild

27

Appendix A
Complex structures

In this appendix we summarize some properties about integrable complex structures in Lorentz signature, and we give further details for the Kerr case. We follow
[
11
]
and
[
14
]
, with the exception that we use the mostly plus convention for the metric signature,
(-+++)
.

Lorentzian complex structures.

Let
(M,g_{ab})
be a four-dimensional Lorentzian manifold. Consider a null tetrad
(\ell^{a},n^{a},m^{a},\bar{m}^{a})
, with
g_{ab}\ell^{a}n^{b}=-1
,
g_{ab}m^{a}\bar{m}^{b}=1
. The dual tetrad is
(\ell_{a},n_{a},m_{a},\bar{m}_{a})
. Define the tensor field

\displaystyle J^{a}{}_{b}:={\rm i}(\ell^{a}n_{b}-n^{a}\ell_{b}+m^{a}\bar{m}_{b}-\bar{m}^{a}m_{b}).

(A.1)

Then
J^{a}{}_{c}J^{c}{}_{b}=-\delta^{a}_{b}
and
g_{ab}J^{a}{}_{c}J^{b}{}_{d}=g_{cd}
, so
J^{a}{}_{b}
is an almost-complex structure compatible with
g_{ab}
. The tangent bundle decomposes as
TM\otimes\mathbb{C}=T^{+}\oplus T^{-}
, where
T^{\pm}
are the eigenspaces of
J^{a}{}_{b}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\pm{\rm i}
. One can check that

\displaystyle J^{a}{}_{b}\ell^{b}=-{\rm i}\,\ell^{a},\qquad J^{a}{}_{b}n^{b}=+{\rm i}\,n^{a},\qquad J^{a}{}_{b}m^{b}=+{\rm i}\,m^{a},\qquad J^{a}{}_{b}\bar{m}^{b}=-{\rm i}\,\bar{m}^{a},

(A.2)

so
T^{+}={\rm span}(n^{a},m^{a})
,
T^{-}={\rm span}(\ell^{a},\bar{m}^{a})
. We say that
J^{a}{}_{b}
is integrable iff
[T^{+},T^{+}]\subset T^{+}
and
[T^{-},T^{-}]\subset T^{-}
. If this is satisfied, there exist functions
A^{i},B^{i},\tilde{A}^{i},\tilde{B}^{i}
, with
i=0,1
, such that

\displaystyle A^{i}\ell_{a}{\rm d}{x}^{a}+B^{i}\bar{m}_{a}{\rm d}{x}^{a},\qquad\tilde{A}^{i}n_{a}{\rm d}{x}^{a}+\tilde{B}^{i}m_{a}{\rm d}{x}^{a}

(A.3)

are closed 1-forms, and thus locally exact. There are then complex scalar fields
z^{i},\tilde{z}^{i}
such that

\displaystyle A^{i}\ell_{a}{\rm d}{x}^{a}+B^{i}\bar{m}_{a}{\rm d}{x}^{a}={\rm d}{z}^{i},\qquad\tilde{A}^{i}n_{a}{\rm d}{x}^{a}+\tilde{B}^{i}m_{a}{\rm d}{x}^{a}={\rm d}\tilde{z}^{i}.

(A.4)

Denoting
g_{i\tilde{j}}:=g(\partial_{z^{i}},\partial_{\tilde{z}^{j}})
, with
i,j=0,1
, the metric is

\displaystyle{\rm d}{s}^{2}=2\,(\,g_{0\tilde{0}}{\rm d}{z}^{0}{\rm d}\tilde{z}^{0}+g_{0\tilde{1}}{\rm d}{z}^{0}{\rm d}\tilde{z}^{1}+g_{1\tilde{0}}{\rm d}{z}^{1}{\rm d}\tilde{z}^{0}+g_{1\tilde{1}}{\rm d}{z}^{1}{\rm d}\tilde{z}^{1}\,),

(A.5)

and the complex structure (
A.1
) becomes

\displaystyle J={\rm i}(\partial_{z^{0}}\otimes{\rm d}{z}^{0}+\partial_{z^{1}}\otimes{\rm d}{z}^{1}-\partial_{\tilde{z}^{0}}\otimes{\rm d}\tilde{z}^{0}-\partial_{\tilde{z}^{1}}\otimes{\rm d}\tilde{z}^{1}).

(A.6)

Note that (
A.6
) is preserved by any transformation of the form

\displaystyle z^{i}\to z^{\prime i}=f^{i}(z^{0},z^{1}),\qquad\tilde{z}^{i}\to\tilde{z}^{\prime i}=\tilde{f}^{i}(\tilde{z}^{0},\tilde{z}^{1}),

(A.7)

where
f^{i}
and
\tilde{f}^{i}
are any holomorphic functions of their arguments.

Shear-free congruences.

The integrability condition for
J^{a}{}_{b}
turns out to be equivalent to the condition that
\ell^{a}
and
n^{a}
define shear-free null geodesic congruences. For a spacetime satisfying the vacuum Einstein equations, the Goldberg-Sachs theorem then implies that an integrable
J^{a}{}_{b}
exists if and only if the spacetime is Petrov type D. In this case, the corresponding null tetrad that defines
J^{a}{}_{b}
via (
A.1
) is given by the principal null directions (PNDs). To find the complex coordinates
z^{i},\tilde{z}^{i}
in (
A.4
), one must then use a null tetrad aligned to the PNDs.

The Kerr spacetime.

For Kerr, a null tetrad aligned to the PNDs was given in (
4.5
). The dual tetrad is:

\displaystyle\ell_{a}{\rm d}{x}^{a}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\Delta}{2\Sigma}}\left[-{\rm d}{t}+\frac{\Sigma}{\Delta}\,{\rm d}{r}+a\sin^{2}\theta\,{\rm d}\phi\right],

(A.8a)

\displaystyle n_{a}{\rm d}{x}^{a}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\Delta}{2\Sigma}}\left[-{\rm d}{t}-\frac{\Sigma}{\Delta}\,{\rm d}{r}+a\sin^{2}\theta\,{\rm d}\phi\right],

(A.8b)

\displaystyle m_{a}{\rm d}{x}^{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\Sigma}}\left[-{\rm i}a\sin\theta\,{\rm d}{t}+\Sigma\,{\rm d}\theta+{\rm i}\sin\theta\,(r^{2}+a^{2})\,{\rm d}\phi\right],

(A.8c)

\displaystyle\bar{m}_{a}{\rm d}{x}^{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\Sigma}}\left[+{\rm i}a\sin\theta\,{\rm d}{t}+\Sigma\,{\rm d}\theta-{\rm i}\sin\theta\,(r^{2}+a^{2})\,{\rm d}\phi\right].

(A.8d)

To find complex coordinates for Kerr, we use (
A.4
) with (
A.8
) and

\displaystyle A^{0}=-(r^{2}+a^{2})\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\Sigma\Delta}},\qquad B^{0}=+{\rm i}a\sin\theta\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\Sigma}},\qquad A^{1}=-a\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\Sigma\Delta}},\qquad B^{1}=\tfrac{{\rm i}}{\sin\theta}\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\Sigma}},

(A.9)

\displaystyle\tilde{A}^{0}=A^{0},\qquad\tilde{B}^{0}=-B^{0},\qquad\tilde{A}^{1}=A^{1},\qquad\tilde{B}^{1}=-B^{1}.

We also find
g_{0\tilde{0}}=g_{tt}\,
,
g_{0\tilde{1}}=g_{1\tilde{0}}=g_{t\phi}\,
,
g_{1\tilde{1}}=g_{\phi\phi}
. To compare the notation in this appendix to that of section
4
(see eq.
4.14
), we must set

\displaystyle z^{0}\equiv\hat{u},\qquad\tilde{z}^{0}\equiv\hat{v},\qquad z^{1}\equiv\hat{w},\qquad\tilde{z}^{1}\equiv\hat{z}.

(A.10)
