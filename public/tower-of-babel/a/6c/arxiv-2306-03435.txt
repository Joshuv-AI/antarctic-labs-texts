On the Role of Attention in Prompt-tuning
Samet Oymak, Ankit Singh Rawat, Mahdi Soltanolkotabi, Christos Thrampoulidis

Abstract
Prompt-tuning is an emerging strategy to adapt large language models (LLM) to downstream tasks by learning a (soft-)prompt parameter from data. Despite its success in LLMs, there is limited theoretical understanding of the power of prompt-tuning and the role of the attention mechanism in prompting. In this work, we explore prompt-tuning for one-layer attention architectures and study contextual mixture-models where each input token belongs to a context-relevant or -irrelevant set. We isolate the role of prompt-tuning through a self-contained prompt-attention model. Our contributions are as follows: (1) We show that softmax-prompt-attention is provably more expressive than softmax-self-attention and linear-prompt-attention under our contextual data model. (2) We analyze the initial trajectory of gradient descent and show that it learns the prompt and prediction head with near-optimal sample complexity and demonstrate how the prompt can provably attend to sparse context-relevant tokens. (3) Assuming a known prompt but an unknown prediction head, we characterize the exact finite sample performance of prompt-attention which reveals the fundamental performance limits and the precise benefit of the context information. We also provide experiments that verify our theoretical insights on real datasets and demonstrate how prompt-tuning enables the model to attend to context-relevant information.

Introduction

Transformer models have achieved remarkable success in a wide array of machine learning domains spanning language modeling, vision, and decision making. Recently, one of the key techniques that has helped pave the way for the deployment of transformers to ever increasing application areas is their ability to adapt to multiple unseen tasks by conditioning their predictions through their inputs – a technique known as prompt-tuning
(
Lester et al. 2021
;
Li & Liang 2021
)
.
Concretely, prompt-tuning provides a more efficient (cheaper/faster) alternative to fine-tuning the entire weights of the transformer by instead training (fewer) so-called prompt parameters that are appended on the input and can be thought of as an input interface. In fact, several recent works have demonstrated experimentally that prompt-tuning is not only more efficient, but often even becomes competitive to fine-tuning in terms of accuracy
(
Lester et al. 2021
;
Liu et al. 2023
)
. However, there is currently limited formal justification of such observations. This motivates the first question of this paper:

How does prompt-tuning compare to fine-tuning in terms of expressive power? Are there scenarios prompt-tuning outperforms fine-tuning in that regard?

The core constituent of a transformer, and thus of prompt-tuning, is the attention mechanism. Through the attention layer, prompts get to interact with other input features, create/modify attention weights, and facilitate the model to attend on latent task-specific information. The standard attention layer relies on softmax nonlinearities. Operationally, the softmax function allows a model to selectively focus on certain parts of the input tokens when generating attention outputs. However, there is little rigorous understanding of attention-based prompt-tuning. Concretely,

What is the role of the softmax-attention in prompt-tuning in terms of optimization and generalization? How does it locate and extract relevant contextual information?

Contributions.
Our contributions are as follows:

•

We show that a particular form of attention which we refer to as
prompt-attention
naturally arises from the self-attention model with prompt-tuning.
We identify provable scenarios where it is more expressive than self-attention and linear-prompt-attention.

Our emphasis is on the role of attention (whether prompt- or self-). However, we analyze the general problem where attention weights are optimized jointly with the linear classifier head.
This separation result reveals insightful data models where prompt-tuning can be superior to fine-tuning with self-attention.

•

We develop new statistical foundations for gradient-based prompt-tuning: we study the optimization and generalization dynamics of the initial trajectory of gradient descent for optimizing prompt-attention. Concretely, we show the first few iterations learn the prompt and prediction head with near-optimal sample complexity while achieving high accuracy.

Related works.

Attention, specifically the so-called self-
attention
, is the backbone mechanism of transformers
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
. It differs from conventional models (e.g., multi-layer perceptrons and convolutional neural networks) in that it computes feature representations by globally modeling interactions between different parts of an input sequence. Despite tremendous empirical success
(see, e.g.,

;

, and references therein)
, the underlying mechanisms of the
attention
layer remain largely unknown: How does it learn? What makes it better (and when) compared to conventional architectures?
Yun et al. 2020
show that self-attention based transformers with large enough depth are universal approximators of seq2seq functions. Focusing on the self-attention component,
Edelman et al. 2021
show that self-
attention
can efficiently represent sparse functions of its input space, while
Sahiner et al. 2022
;
Ergen et al. 2022
analyze convex-relaxations of Self-
attention
, and
Baldi & Vershynin 2022
;
Dong et al. 2021
study the expressive ability of
attention
layers.
However, these works do
not
characterize the optimization and generalization dynamics of
attention
. To the best of our knowledge, the only prior works attempting this are
Jelassi et al. 2022
and
Li et al. 2023
.
Jelassi et al. 2022
assume a simplified
attention
structure in which the
attention
matrix is
not
directly parameterized in terms of the input sequence. Our paper also distinguishes itself from contemporaneous work by
Li et al. 2023
in several ways: (1) Unlike their data model, ours incorporates a context vector and employs a sub-Gaussian noise model instead of assuming bounded noise. (2) We provide a precise asymptotic analysis that elucidates the role of various problem parameters. (3) While

Motivation: Prompt-tuning

Consider a single-head self-
attention
layer

\displaystyle\bm{O}_{\text{pre}}=\phi({\bm{X}}\bm{W}_{Q}\bm{W}_{K}^{\top}{\bm{X}}^{\top}){\bm{X}}\bm{W}_{V}\,,

(1)

with input
{\bm{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d}
consisting of
T

and a softmax nonlinearity
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{T}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{T}
,
[\phi({\bm{v}})]_{t}=e^{v_{t}}/\sum_{t^{\prime}\in[T]}e^{v_{t^{\prime}}}
that acts row-wise when its argument is a
T\times T

attention

\displaystyle y_{\text{pre}}=\langle\bar{\bm{U}},\bm{O}_{\text{pre}}\rangle=\langle\bm{U},\phi({\bm{X}}\bm{W}_{Q}\bm{W}_{K}^{\top}{\bm{X}}^{\top}){\bm{X}}\rangle\,.

(2)

Note here that we implicitly subsume the value matrix
\bm{W}_{V}

We assume that the model above is pre-trained so that

Prompt-tuning appends a trainable prompt

\bm{O}=\phi({\bm{X}}_{{\bm{P}}}\bm{W}_{Q}\bm{W}_{K}^{\top}{\bm{X}}^{\top}){\bm{X}}.

Note that this is slightly different from (
1

between the augmented inputs
{\bm{X}}_{{\bm{P}}}
and the original inputs
{\bm{X}}
. This is also equivalent to self-attention on

after masking the prompt
{\bm{P}}
from keys. This masking is used to cleanly isolate the residual contribution of the prompt without impacting the frozen attention output. Concretely, let
\bm{W}_{\text{head}}
be the prediction head associated with the prompt tokens. As before, we scalarize the output by projecting with a linear head of size
(T+m)\times d
as follows:

\displaystyle y=\langle[{\bm{W}_{\text{head}}}^{\top}~\bm{U}^{\top}]^{\top},\phi({\bm{X}}_{{\bm{P}}}\bm{W}_{Q}\bm{W}_{K}^{\top}{\bm{X}}^{\top}){\bm{X}}\rangle

(3)

\displaystyle\hskip-7.22743pt=\underbrace{\langle{\bm{W}_{\text{head}}},\phi({\bm{P}}\bm{W}_{Q}\bm{W}_{K}^{\top}{\bm{X}}^{\top}){\bm{X}}\rangle}_{\text{prompt-attention $y_{\text{new}}$}}+\underbrace{\langle\bm{U},\phi({\bm{X}}\bm{W}_{Q}\bm{W}_{K}^{\top}{\bm{X}}^{\top}){\bm{X}}\rangle}_{\text{frozen self-attention $y_{\text{pre}}$}}.

Here,
y_{\text{new}}
captures the additive impact of prompt-tuning on the prediction. We denote the trainable parameters in the model above as
\bm{\theta}:=({\bm{W}_{\text{head}}},{\bm{P}})

\bm{W}_{\text{head}}
in addition to the prompt vectors
{\bm{P}}

Since the

Our goal is understanding the expressivity, training dynamics, and generalization properties of the above model. To simplify our analysis, we consider the following setting.

1.

We focus our attention on the novel component

of the model output in (

2.

We assume
\bm{W}_{K},\bm{W}_{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

m=1
.

Prompt-attention model.
Using these assumptions and setting

(or simply

\displaystyle f^{\textsc{att}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{X}})=\langle{\bm{w}},{\bm{X}}^{\top}\phi\left({\bm{X}}{\bm{q}}\right)\rangle,~\bm{\theta}=({\bm{w}},{\bm{q}}).

(4)

We shall see that this model exhibits interesting properties to learn rich contextual relationships within the data and can even be more expressive than a single self-attention layer.

We remark that the model above is of interest even beyond the context of prompting: the prompt-attention model in (

, the tokens’

and corresponding

2.2
Contextual data model

Consider supervised classification on IID data
({\bm{X}},y)\sim\mathcal{D}
with features
{\bm{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d}
and binary label
y\in\{\pm 1\}
.

Dataset model.
We assume the following about an example
({\bm{X}},y)
drawn from
\mathcal{D}
: The labels
y
are distributed as
\mathbb{P}(y=1)=1-\mathbb{P}(y=-1)=\pi
; for simplicity, we set
\pi=1/2
so that
\mathbb{E}[y]=0
. The tokens
\bm{x}_{t},t\in[T]
of input example
{\bm{X}}:=[\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{T}]
are split into a
context-relevant
set
\mathcal{R}\subset[T]
and
context-irrelevant
set
\mathcal{R}^{c}:=[T]-\mathcal{R}
. Specifically, conditioned on the labels and relevance set
\mathcal{R}
, tokens
\bm{x}_{t},t\in[T]
within
{\bm{X}}
are i.i.d. as follows

\bm{x}_{t}|y=\begin{cases}{\bm{q}}_{\star}+y{\bm{w}}_{\star},&t\in\mathcal{R}~~~~~~\text{(relevant token)}\\
-\delta^{q}{\bm{q}}_{\star}-\delta^{w}y{\bm{w}}_{\star}+\bm{z}_{t},&t\not\in\mathcal{R}~~~~~~\text{({ir}relevant token)}\,.\end{cases}

(DATA)

In the above,
{\bm{q}}_{\star}
is a context-vector that indicates token relevance and
{\bm{w}}_{\star}
is a regressor vector.
y,\delta:=(\delta^{q},\delta^{w}),(\bm{z}_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
are independent variables as follows:

•

\delta=(\delta^{q},\delta^{w})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a bounded random variable that obeys
\delta^{q}\geq 0
. Thus,
\delta
reflects
out-of-context
information within irrelevant tokens. However,
\delta
is allowed to expose label information through
\delta^{w}
. When
\delta=(0,0)
almost surely, we call the resulting distribution
core dataset model
.

•

\bm{z}_{t}
are independent centered subgaussian and random variables with covariance
\bm{\Sigma}
(see Ass.
1.a
). When
\bm{\Sigma}=0
, we call the resulting distribution
discrete dataset model
.

•

We allow the relevance set
\mathcal{R}
to be non-stochastic. This includes
\mathcal{R}
being adversarial to the classification model.

•

We assume constant fraction
\zeta=|\mathcal{R}|/T\in(0,1)
of label-relevant tokens for each input example
{\bm{X}}
drawn from
\mathcal{D}
. Thus,
\zeta
represents the sparsity of relevant signal tokens.

Training dataset
{\mathcal{S}}:=({\bm{X}}_{i},y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
.
We draw
n
i.i.d. samples from
\mathcal{D}
to form our training dataset
{\mathcal{S}}:=({\bm{X}}_{i},y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
. For
i
’th example
({\bm{X}}_{i},y_{i})
, we denote the tokens by
(\bm{x}_{i,t})_{t=1}^{T}
, noise by
(\bm{z}_{i,t})_{t=1}^{T}
, relevance set by
\mathcal{R}_{i}
, and out-of-context variable by
\delta_{i}=(\delta^{q}_{i},\delta^{w}_{i})
.

Ideally, for
i

and discard the rest. This would especially help when the signal-to-noise-ratio is small, i.e.
\zeta=|\mathcal{R}_{i}|/T\ll 1

{\bm{q}}_{\star}

{\bm{q}}_{\star}
whereas irrelevant tokens have non-positive correlation with

Remark 1

(Model interpretation)
.

(
DATA
) can be thought of as a simplified model for binary image classification with tokens being image patches of two types: ones revealing information about the label (set
\mathcal{R}
) and uninformative ones containing noise. Tokens in
\mathcal{R}
contain information indicating: (i) class-membership via signed-regressor
y{\bm{w}}_{\star}
and (ii) context-relevance via context-vector
{\bm{q}}_{\star}
. The signed-regressor differs across tokens of examples belonging to different classes
y\in\{\pm 1\}
, while the context-vector is common for all context-relevant tokes across classes. For a concrete example, consider images each depicting multiple, say
|\mathcal{R}|
, birds of one type surrounded by label-irrelevant/noisy background. The goal is to classify images according to one of two types of birds. Here, think of “context” as feature-information indicating corresponding pixels belong to “bird” (of either type) rather than “background,” while the “regressor” represents feature information useful to distinguish between two bird types. Alternatively, (
DATA
) may be modeling deep representations (rather than raw pixels) of the original images. Overall, simplified models similar to (
DATA
) have been used previously to analyze optimization and generalization dynamics of training fully-connected
(
Frei et al. 2022
)
and convolutional models
(
Cao et al. 2022
)
. Specifically, (
DATA
) is an extension of the commonly used (sub)-Gaussian mixture model customized to the nature of
attention
: each example is tokenized and context-relevant information is described in terms of both a regressor (differing between classes) and a context (common across classes).

Assumptions and notations

First, we formally state our assumptions on the noisy tokens. The more general condition is that noise is subgaussian and satisfies a mild zero third-moment condition.

Assumption 1.a
.

The noise vector

\sigma

For some of our results it will be convenient to further assume that noise is Gaussian since this leads to precise formulas that are easily interpretable.

Assumption 1.b
.

The noise vector

\sigma^{2}.

Contrasting prompt-attention to baselines

In this section, we establish separation results between prompt-attention (cf. (
4

6
)) and linear attention (cf. (

We first observe that if
\delta=(\delta^{q},\delta^{w})
admits a single value, even a linear model can solve the discrete dataset model.

Observation 1

(Linear model solves singleton)
.

Suppose
(\delta^{q},\delta^{w})=(\Delta^{q},\Delta^{w})
almost surely for
\Delta^{q},\Delta^{w}\in\mathbb{R}
. Set
{\bm{w}}_{\star}^{\prime}=(\bm{I}-\bar{{\bm{q}}}_{\star}\bar{{\bm{q}}}_{\star}^{\top}){\bm{w}}_{\star}

As long as

and
{\bm{w}}_{\star}^{\prime}\neq 0

or

achieves perfect accuracy.

Thus, to investigate the expressivity of

would need to admit two or more values. Perhaps surprisingly, we prove that, as soon as,
(\delta^{q},\delta^{w})
comes from a binary distribution, then both

and
f^{\textsc{lin}}
can indeed provably fail. Importantly, this happens in the regime
\delta^{q}\geq 0
where prompt-attention thrives.

(Population first step)

{\bm{w}}_{\star}.

\displaystyle=\gamma\alpha W^{2}(\zeta-\zeta^{2})\big(1+\frac{\alpha\sigma^{2}}{T}-\alpha\zeta(W^{2}+\rho^{2}Q^{2})\big){\bm{q}}_{\star}

\displaystyle a_{t}=[\phi({\bm{X}}{\bm{q}}_{1})]_{t}=e^{\gamma r_{t}}/S\begin{cases}=\frac{e^{\gamma B}}{S}\rightarrow\frac{1}{\zeta T}&t\in\mathcal{R}\\

4.3
Finite-sample analysis

Here, we investigate the behavior of the algorithm in (
9

. For convenience, we first introduce an additional de-biasing step after calculating the three gradients in (

6
Discussion

In light of remarkable success of attention architectures, we initiate a theoretical investigation of one of its core mechanisms: prompt-tuning. For a one-layer attention model, we motivate and analyze a simplified model for prompt-tuning, which we coin prompt-attention. Through this model, we developed new statistical foundations for gradient-based prompt-tuning, characterized its optimization and generalization dynamics, and explored how it facilitates attending to context-relevant information. We also showed that under (
DATA
) one-layer softmax-prompt-tuning can provably be superior to alternatives including one layer self-attention. Thorough experiments support our theory on how prompt-tuning attends to the context and how it can potentially outperform full fine-tuning. Our results also suggest many interesting future directions including 1) extension to deeper architectures by characterizing the role of softmax-attention in individual layers, 2) developing a stronger theoretical and empirical understanding of when/if prompt-tuning is superior to fine-tuning, 3) extending our model to include multiple prompt vectors (and perhaps extending (
DATA
) to include multiple context vectors), and 4) investigating the role of multi-head attention in prompt-tuning.

Appendix A
Sharp characterization of accuracy under known context

While the discrete dataset model is insightful, incorporating noise is crucial for understanding the fundamental limits of the benefits of context in attention. To this end, let us focus on the core dataset model where we set
\delta^{q}=\delta^{w}=0
and explore the role of noise in population accuracy. Also assume that noise is standard normal, i.e. Assumption
1.b
.

\bullet

Linear model.
The linear model aggregates tokens to obtain a simple Gaussian mixture distribution. Specifically, aggregated tokens are exactly distributed as
\frac{1}{T}{\bm{X}}^{\top}\mathds{1}_{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(\zeta{\bm{w}}_{\star},\frac{1-\zeta}{T}\bm{I})
,
leading to the following well-known result.

Fact A.1
.

For linear models, optimal accuracy obeys
\min_{{\bm{w}}}\textsc{err}(f^{\textsc{lin}}({\bm{w}}))=Q(\sqrt{\frac{\zeta^{2}W^{2}T}{1-\zeta}})
where
Q(\cdot)
is the tail function of the standard normal distribution.

\bullet

Prompt-attention model.
Since prompt-attention strictly generalizes the linear model, its accuracy is at least as good. The theorem below quantifies this and demonstrates how context vector can enable an optimal weighting of relevant and irrelevant tokens to maximize accuracy. A general version of this theorem is proven under a non-asymptotic setting (finite
T,d
) as Theorem
8
.

Theorem 4
.

Consider the prompt-attention model
f^{\textsc{att}}_{\bm{\theta}}
. Suppose
{\bm{w}}_{\star}\perp{\bm{q}}_{\star}
and let
\tau,{\bar{\tau}}>0
be hyperparameters. Consider the following algorithm which uses the hindsight knowledge of
{\bm{q}}_{\star}
to estimate
{\bm{w}}_{\star}
and make prediction:

Set
\hat{{\bm{w}}}=(\bm{I}-\bar{{\bm{q}}}_{\star}\bar{{\bm{q}}}_{\star})\nabla\mathcal{L}_{{\bm{w}}}(0,\tau\bar{{\bm{q}}}_{\star})

\bm{\theta}=(\hat{{\bm{w}}},{\bar{\tau}}\bar{{\bm{q}}}_{\star})

Suppose

each lie between two positive absolute constants. Suppose

is polynomially large in
n
and these constants and
{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(\cdot)
hides polynomial terms in
n
. Define

\texttt{ISNR}(\alpha,\tau)=\frac{(1-\zeta)e^{2\tau(\tau-Q)}}{\alpha\zeta^{2}W^{2}T}
.
In the limit

, the test error converges in probability to
\mathcal{Q}\left(\frac{e^{Q{\bar{\tau}}-{\bar{\tau}}^{2}}}{\sqrt{1+\texttt{ISNR}(\alpha,\tau)}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\zeta^{2}W^{2}T}{1-\zeta}}\right)
. In this limit, optimal hyperparameters are
\tau={\bar{\tau}}=Q/2
and leads to optimal
\texttt{ISNR}(\alpha):=\frac{(1-\zeta)e^{-Q^{2}/2}}{\alpha\zeta^{2}W^{2}T}
and the error

Here, a few remarks are in place. Note that
\texttt{rate}_{\textsc{lin}}:=\sqrt{\frac{\zeta^{2}W^{2}T}{1-\zeta}}

f^{\textsc{lin}}
from Fact
A.1

\alpha=n/d\rightarrow\infty

is simply given by
e^{Q/4}\texttt{rate}_{\textsc{lin}}
demonstrating the strict superiority of prompt-attention. Moreover setting
Q=0
(no prompt info), since feature-output of
f^{\textsc{lin}}
(i.e.
{\bm{X}}^{\top}\phi({\bm{X}}\mathds{1})

f^{\textsc{lin}}
classifier which has a finite-sample rate of
\texttt{rate}_{\textsc{lin}}/\sqrt{1+(1-\zeta)/(\alpha\zeta^{2}W^{2}T^{2})}
. Prompt-tuning also strictly improves this because our
\texttt{ISNR}(\alpha)
introduces an additional
e^{-Q^{2}/2}
multiplier.

Lemma 11

(From learning the context to learning good features)

C.4

With the characterization of the quality of learnt features in Theorem

Lemma 12

(Third step)
.

Suppose that the first and second gradient steps
\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{1},\widehat{{\bm{q}}}_{1}
are such that
the following hold. First, for absolute constant
c_{\eta}>0

\|\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{1}\|\leq c_{\eta}\zeta W/Q^{2}\,.

Second, for
\epsilon>0
and any fresh datapoint
({\bm{X}},y)
there exists
\delta
that does not depend on
\epsilon
such that

\mathbb{P}_{({\bm{X}},y)}\left(\|{\bm{X}}^{\top}\phi({\bm{X}}\widehat{{\bm{q}}}_{1})-({\bm{q}}_{\star}+y{\bm{w}}_{\star})\|\leq\epsilon\right)\geq 1-\delta.

Consider the third gradient step
\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{2}:=\eta\widehat{\bm{G}}_{\bm{q}}(\widehat{{\bm{q}}}_{1},\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{1})
. There exists absolute constants
c,C
such that, for all sufficiently small
\epsilon
and
c_{\eta}
, with probability at least
1-n\delta-2e^{cn}
,

\frac{\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{2}^{\top}{\bm{w}}_{\star}}{\|\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{2}\|}\geq C\,\frac{W^{2}}{Q}\,.

Proof.

Note that the lemma’s conclusion is insensitive to the choice of step-size
\eta
for the third gradient step. Thus, without loss of generality, assume below that
\eta=1
.

Recall the gradient formula

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{G}}_{\bm{w}}({\bm{q}},{\bm{w}}):=-\nabla_{{\bm{w}}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}(y_{i}-f_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{X}}_{i})){\bm{X}}_{i}^{\top}\phi(({\bm{X}}_{i}{\bm{q}}))

and denote for convenience

With this notation, we can conveniently rewrite the third gradient step evaluated at
{\bm{q}}:={\bm{q}}^{\gamma}:=\gamma\,\widehat{\bm{G}}_{\bm{q}}(0,\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{1})
for any
\gamma>0
as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{2}

\displaystyle:=\widehat{\bm{G}}_{\bm{w}}({\bm{q}},\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{1})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{i}{\bm{X}}_{i}^{\top}\phi({\bm{X}}_{i}{\bm{q}})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\left(\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{1}^{T}{\bm{X}}_{i}^{\top}\phi({\bm{X}}_{i}{\bm{q}})\right){\bm{X}}_{i}^{\top}\phi({\bm{X}}_{i}{\bm{q}})

\displaystyle={\bm{w}}_{\star}+{\bm{q}}_{\star}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{i}\right)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{i}\left({\bm{X}}_{i}^{\top}\phi({\bm{X}}_{i}{\bm{q}})-({\bm{q}}_{\star}+y_{i}{\bm{w}}_{\star})\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\left(\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{1}^{T}{\bm{X}}_{i}^{\top}\phi({\bm{X}}_{i}{\bm{q}})\right)({\bm{q}}_{\star}+y_{i}{\bm{w}}_{\star})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\left(\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{1}^{T}{\bm{X}}_{i}^{\top}\phi({\bm{X}}_{i}{\bm{q}})\right)\left({\bm{X}}_{i}^{\top}\phi({\bm{X}}_{i}{\bm{q}})-({\bm{q}}_{\star}+y_{i}{\bm{w}}_{\star})\right)\,

\displaystyle={\bm{w}}_{\star}+{\bm{q}}_{\star}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{i}\right)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{i}\bm{\epsilon}_{i}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{1}^{\top}\bm{\epsilon}_{i}\bm{\epsilon}_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{1}^{\top}({\bm{q}}_{\star}+y_{i}{\bm{w}}_{\star})\bm{\epsilon}_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{1}^{\top}\bm{\epsilon}_{i}({\bm{q}}_{\star}+y_{i}{\bm{w}}_{\star})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{1}^{\top}({\bm{q}}_{\star}+y_{i}{\bm{w}}_{\star})({\bm{q}}_{\star}+y_{i}{\bm{w}}_{\star})\,.

In order to control the correlation
\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{2}^{\top}{\bm{w}}_{\star}/\|\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{2}\|
it suffices to control
\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{2}^{\top}{\bm{v}}
for arbitrary
{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In view of the expression above, it will suffice bounding the two random terms below:

\displaystyle\text{Term}_{\rm{I}}

\displaystyle:=\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{i}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\text{Term}_{\rm{II}}

\displaystyle:=\|\bm{\epsilon}_{i}\|=\|{\bm{X}}_{i}^{\top}\phi({\bm{X}}_{i}{\bm{q}})-({\bm{q}}_{\star}+y{\bm{w}}_{\star})\|

For the first term, we have with probability at least

that

\text{Term}_{\rm{I}}\leq u_{1}/\sqrt{n}.

For the second term, we know by assumption that with probability at least
1-n\delta

\text{Term}_{\rm{II}}\leq\epsilon.

Putting the above together, with probability at least
1-2e^{-u^{2}/2}-n\delta
, we have that

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}_{\star}^{\top}\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{2}

\displaystyle\geq W^{2}-\left\lvert\rho\right\rvert QW\,\frac{u_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}-\epsilon\,W-\frac{3}{2}\eta\zeta\left(\epsilon^{2}W^{2}+2\epsilon W^{2}\left(Q+W\right)+W^{2}\left(Q+W\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq W^{2}\left(1-\left\lvert\rho\right\rvert\,\frac{Q}{W}\,\frac{u_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}-\frac{\epsilon}{W}-\frac{3}{2}\eta\zeta\left(\epsilon^{2}+2\epsilon\left(Q+W\right)+\left(Q+W\right)^{2}\right)\right)\,

where we also used the lemma’s assumption on
\|\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{1}\|\leq(3/2)\eta\zeta W
.

To further lower bound
{\bm{w}}_{\star}^{\top}\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{2}
, recall that
\left\lvert\rho\right\rvert\leq W/Q
,
W\gtrsim 1
and that
\epsilon
can be made arbitrarily small constant. Further pick

\displaystyle u_{1}=c_{1}\sqrt{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\eta=c_{\eta}/Q^{2}

(57)

for sufficiently small constants
c_{1}
and
c_{\eta}

that

\displaystyle\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{2}^{\top}{\bm{w}}_{\star}\gtrsim W^{2}\,.

(58)

Next, we use similar arguments to bound
\|\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{2}\|.
Conditioning on the event where the bounds derived above hold for
\text{Term}_{\rm{I}}
and
\text{Term}_{\rm{II}}
, we have from (
56

\displaystyle\|\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{2}\|\leq W+Q\frac{u_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}+\epsilon+\frac{3}{2}\eta\zeta W\left(\epsilon^{2}+2\epsilon\left(Q+W\right)+\left(Q+W\right)^{2}\right)\lesssim Q,

where in the second inequality, we chose
u_{1},\eta
as in (

All the above combined, shows that

\frac{\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{2}^{\top}{\bm{w}}_{\star}}{\|\widehat{{\bm{w}}}_{2}\|}\gtrsim\frac{W^{2}}{Q}.

Lemma 13

(Debiasing predictions)
.

For some
\epsilon>0
, suppose
{\bm{q}}_{1}
is such that a test example
(y,{\bm{X}})

1-n\delta
over the dataset

\|\bm{W}_{\perp}\hat{\bm{w}}-\bar{{\bm{v}}}/\sqrt{n}\|\leq{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(T^{-1/3}).

Thus,

(\sqrt{d}+t)^{2}+{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(T^{-1/3})\geq\frac{n\|\bm{W}_{\perp}\hat{\bm{w}}\|^{2}}{(1-\zeta)e^{2\tau^{2}}}\geq(\sqrt{d}-1-t)_{+}^{2}-{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(T^{-1/3}).

Using
\|{\bm{w}}_{\star}\|^{2}T=W
, We similarly have that

|\|\bar{{\bm{w}}}_{\star}^{\top}\hat{\bm{w}}\|^{2}-W\zeta^{2}e^{2\sqrt{Q}\tau}|\leq{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(T^{-1/3}).

To conclude, with probability
1-2e^{-t}-T^{-1/3}
,
\texttt{ISNR}(\hat{\bm{w}})
obeys

\frac{(\sqrt{d}+t)^{2}+{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(T^{-1/3})}{W\zeta^{2}e^{2\sqrt{Q}\tau}-{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(T^{-1/3})}\geq\frac{n}{(1-\zeta)e^{2\tau^{2}}}\texttt{ISNR}(\hat{\bm{w}})\geq\frac{(\sqrt{d}-1-t)_{+}^{2}-{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(T^{-1/3})}{W\zeta^{2}e^{2\sqrt{Q}\tau}+{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(T^{-1/3})}

Rewriting this bound, we find

\left(1+\frac{t}{\sqrt{d}}+{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(T^{-\frac{1}{3}})\right)^{2}\frac{(1-\zeta)e^{2\tau^{2}}}{\zeta^{2}W\alpha e^{2\sqrt{Q}\tau}}\geq\texttt{ISNR}(\hat{\bm{w}})\geq\left(1-\frac{1+t}{\sqrt{d}}-{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(T^{-\frac{1}{3}})\right)_{+}^{2}\frac{(1-\zeta)e^{2\tau^{2}}}{\zeta^{2}W\alpha e^{2\sqrt{Q}\tau}}.

Recalling the definition of
\texttt{ISNR}(\alpha,\tau)=\frac{(1-\zeta)e^{2\tau(\tau-\sqrt{Q})}}{\zeta^{2}W\alpha}
, we conclude with the bound.
∎

Step 2: Characterizing the error rate of
\bm{\theta}=(\hat{{\bm{w}}},{\bar{\tau}}{\bm{q}}_{\star})
.
To achieve this goal, we will leverage Theorem
9
. Since conditions of this theorem is satisfied (noticing that their
\gamma
is our
\texttt{ISNR}(\hat{\bm{w}})
which is upper bounded by a positive constant), for a new test point
(y,{\bm{X}})
, we have that

\left|\textsc{err}(f^{\textsc{att}}_{\bm{\theta}})-\mathcal{Q}\left(\frac{e^{\sqrt{Q}{\bar{\tau}}-{\bar{\tau}}^{2}}}{\sqrt{1+\texttt{ISNR}(\hat{\bm{w}})}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\zeta^{2}W}{1-\zeta}}\right)\right|\leq{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(T^{-1/3}).

Using the Lipschitzness of the Q-function (i.e.
\mathcal{Q}(x+\epsilon)-\mathcal{Q}(x)=\int_{x}^{x+\epsilon}e^{-t^{2}/2}dt\leq\epsilon
), as we have done in Theorem
9
, we pull out the perturbation term
{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(T^{-1/3})
within
\texttt{ISNR}(\hat{\bm{w}})
to obtain the advertised bound

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}\left(\frac{e^{\sqrt{Q}{\bar{\tau}}-{\bar{\tau}}^{2}}}{\sqrt{1+(1+\frac{t}{\sqrt{d}})\texttt{ISNR}(\alpha,\tau)}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\zeta^{2}W}{1-\zeta}}\right)-{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(T^{-1/3})\leq\textsc{err}(f^{\textsc{att}}_{\bm{\theta}})\leq

(85)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\mathcal{Q}\left(\frac{e^{\sqrt{Q}{\bar{\tau}}-{\bar{\tau}}^{2}}}{\sqrt{1+(1-\frac{1+t}{\sqrt{d}})_{+}\texttt{ISNR}(\alpha,\tau)}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\zeta^{2}W}{1-\zeta}}\right)+{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(T^{-1/3}).

(86)

To emphasize, this bound holds with probability
1-2e^{-t^{2}/2}-{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(T^{-1/3})
over a new test datapoint
(y,{\bm{X}})
. To see the optimal choices for
{\bar{\tau}},\tau
, we need to optimize the error bound. This results in

\displaystyle{\bar{\tau}}_{\star}=\arg\min_{{\bar{\tau}}}\sqrt{Q}{\bar{\tau}}-{\bar{\tau}}^{2}=\sqrt{Q}/2

(87)

\displaystyle\tau_{\star}=\arg\min_{\tau}\texttt{ISNR}(\alpha,\tau)=2\tau(\tau-\sqrt{Q})=\sqrt{Q}/2.

(88)

∎

Theorem 9
.

Consider the prompt-attention model
f^{\textsc{att}}_{\bm{\theta}}
where we set
\bm{\theta}=({\bm{w}}_{\star}+\bm{p},\tau\bar{{\bm{q}}}_{\star})
. Set
Q=\|{\bm{q}}_{\star}\|^{2},W=T\|{\bm{w}}_{\star}\|^{2}
. Here
\tau
is a tuning parameter and
\bm{p}
is a perturbation vector and assume all vectors are perpendicular i.e.
\bm{p}\perp{\bm{w}}_{\star}\perp{\bm{q}}_{\star}
. Set
\gamma:=\|\bm{p}\|^{2}/\|{\bm{w}}_{\star}\|^{2}
and suppose
1+\gamma,\zeta^{2}W,1-\zeta,e^{Q},e^{\tau}
each lie between two positive absolute constants.
{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(\cdot)
subsumes polynomial dependencies in these constants. We have that

\left|\textsc{err}(f^{\textsc{att}}_{\bm{\theta}})-\mathcal{Q}\left({\frac{e^{\sqrt{Q}\tau-\tau^{2}}}{\sqrt{1+\gamma}}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\zeta^{2}W}{1-\zeta}}\right)\right|\leq\mathcal{O}(T^{-1/3}).

Thus, as
T\rightarrow\infty
, the optimal tuning obeys
\tau_{\star}=\sqrt{Q}/2
and yields an error of
\mathcal{Q}\left({e^{Q/4}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\zeta^{2}W}{1-\zeta}}\right)
.

Proof.

Let us recap the notation of Theorem
4
. Without losing generality, assume first
\zeta T
tokens are relevant and remaining tokens are irrelevant. Consider
{\bm{X}}_{I}
of size
(1-\zeta)T\times d
induced by the irrelevant tokens with normal distribution. Using orthogonality of
{\bm{q}}_{\star},{\bm{w}}_{\star},\bm{p}
, observe that
{\bm{g}}={\bm{X}}_{I}(\bar{{\bm{w}}}_{\star}+\frac{\bm{p}}{\|{\bm{w}}_{\star}\|})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,(1+\gamma)\bm{I}_{(1-\zeta)T})
and
\mathbf{h}={\bm{X}}_{I}\bar{{\bm{q}}}_{\star}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\bm{I}_{(1-\zeta)T})
are independent vectors. Also for standard normal
g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, recall that moment-generating function is given by
\mathbb{E}[e^{\tau g}]=e^{\tau^{2}/2}
.

Note that, the attention weights have the form
\bm{a}=\phi(\tau\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{Q}\mathds{1}_{\zeta T}\\
\mathbf{h}\end{bmatrix})
. Here, the softmax denominator is
T\cdot D_{T}
where
D_{T}:=(\zeta e^{\sqrt{Q}\tau}+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{(1-\zeta)T}e^{\tau h_{i}})
. Define
e^{\tau\mathbf{h}}
to be the numerator corresponding to irrelevant tokens i.e.

e^{\tau\mathbf{h}}=[e^{\tau h_{1}}~\dots~e^{\tau h_{(1-\zeta)T}}].

Define the matrix
{\bm{Q}}_{\perp}=\bm{I}-\bar{{\bm{q}}}_{\star}\bar{{\bm{q}}}_{\star}^{\top}
,
\bm{W}_{\perp}=\bm{I}-\bar{{\bm{w}}}_{\star}\bar{{\bm{w}}}_{\star}^{\top}
. To proceed, observe that, the prediction with
\bm{\theta}=({\bm{w}}_{\star}+\bm{p},\tau\bar{{\bm{q}}}_{\star})
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{D_{T}}{\sqrt{T}\|{\bm{w}}_{\star}\|}yf^{\textsc{att}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{X}})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{T}(\bar{{\bm{w}}}_{\star}+\frac{\bm{p}}{\|{\bm{w}}_{\star}\|})^{\top}[\zeta e^{\|{\bm{q}}_{\star}\|\tau}({\bm{w}}_{\star}+y{\bm{q}}_{\star})+\frac{{\bm{X}}_{I}e^{\tau\mathbf{h}}}{T}]

(89)

\displaystyle=\zeta e^{\|{\bm{q}}_{\star}\|\tau}\sqrt{W}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}{\bm{g}}^{\top}e^{\tau\mathbf{h}}.

(90)

With this, conditioned on
e^{\tau\mathbf{h}}
observe that
{\bm{g}}^{\top}e^{\tau\mathbf{h}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{T}\|e^{\tau\mathbf{h}}\|^{2})
, thus,

\mathbb{P}_{\bm{g}}(yf^{\textsc{att}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{X}})>0)=1-\mathcal{Q}\left(\frac{\zeta e^{\sqrt{Q}\tau}\sqrt{W}}{\sqrt{1+\gamma}\|e^{\tau\mathbf{h}}\|/\sqrt{T}}\right).

To proceed, similar to Theorem
4
, we apply Chebyshev’s inequality over number of tokens
T
to find that with probability
1-{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(T^{-1/3})
over
\mathbf{h}
,

|\|e^{\tau\mathbf{h}}\|^{2}/T-e^{2\tau^{2}}|\leq{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(T^{-1/3}).

In aggregate, this implies that, with probability
1-{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(T^{-1/3})
over
\mathbf{h}
, we have that

1-\mathcal{Q}\left((1+{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(T^{-1/3}))\frac{\zeta e^{\sqrt{Q}\tau}\sqrt{W}}{\sqrt{1+\gamma}e^{\tau^{2}}}\right)\geq\mathbb{P}_{\bm{g}}(yf^{\textsc{att}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{X}})>0)\geq 1-\mathcal{Q}\left((1-{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(T^{-1/3}))\frac{\zeta e^{\sqrt{Q}\tau}\sqrt{W}}{\sqrt{1+\gamma}e^{\tau^{2}}}\right),

Finally, note that since
\frac{\zeta e^{\sqrt{Q}\tau}\sqrt{W}}{\sqrt{1+\gamma}e^{\tau^{2}}}
is upper/lower bounded by a positive constant, and since

\mathcal{Q}(x+\epsilon)-\mathcal{Q}(x)=\int_{x}^{x+\epsilon}e^{-t^{2}/2}dt\leq\epsilon
, we can rewrite

\left|\mathbb{P}_{\bm{g}}(yf^{\textsc{att}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{X}})>0)-\mathcal{Q}\left(\frac{\zeta e^{\sqrt{Q}\tau}\sqrt{W}}{\sqrt{1+\gamma}e^{\tau^{2}}}\right)\right|\leq{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(T^{-1/3}).

Union bounding with failure probability over
\mathbf{h}
, we conclude with the result.
∎

For a random variable
Z
and
\alpha>0
,
\|Z\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}
denotes its
\psi_{\alpha}
-norm for Orlicz function
\psi_{\alpha}(z)=e^{z^{\alpha}}-1

Fact G.1
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent zero-mean sub-gaussian or sub-exponential random variables with

for all

\left\|{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}X_{i}}\right\|_{\psi_{m}}\leq\frac{CK}{\sqrt{n}}.

.

(
Pollard 1990
)

The following identity holds for Orlitz norms

\displaystyle\|XY\|_{\psi_{\frac{\alpha\beta}{\alpha+\beta}}}\leq c\,\|X\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}\cdot\|Y\|_{\psi_{\beta}}

(91)

for a fixed numerical constant
c
.

Next we state a Lemma from Talagrand quoted directly from Lemma 22 of
(
Mohammadi et al. 2019
)
.

Fact G.3
.

(
Ledoux & Talagrand 1991
)
For any scalar
\alpha\in(0,1]
, there exists a constant
C_{\alpha}
such that for any sequence of independent random variables
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{N}
we have

\displaystyle\Big\|\sum_{i}\xi_{i}-\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{i}\xi_{i}\big]\Big\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}\leq C_{\alpha}\left(\max_{i}\|\xi_{i}\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}\right)\sqrt{N}\log N.

Fact G.4
.

Let
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a
K
-subgausssian vector i.e.
\bm{z}^{\top}{\bm{v}}
is
K
-subgaussian for fixed
\|{\bm{v}}\|=1
. Then, the following are true for a constant
c>0

\mathbb{P}\left(\|\bm{z}\|\geq cK({\sqrt{d}}+t)\right)\leq e^{-t^{2}}\,.

Proof.

For completeness, we provide a proof. Repeating Lemma 31 of
(
Oymak 2019
)
, we can pick a
1/2
cover
\mathcal{C}
of the unit Euclidean ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with size
\log|\mathcal{C}|\leq 2d
. For any
{\bm{v}}\in\mathcal{C}
subgaussianity implies
\mathbb{P}({\bm{v}}^{\top}\bm{z}\geq t)\leq\exp(-ct^{2}/K^{2})
. Setting
K^{\prime}=K/\sqrt{c}
,
t=K^{\prime}(\sqrt{2d}+\tau)
and union bounding over all
{\bm{v}}\in\mathcal{C}
, we find

\mathbb{P}(\sup_{{\bm{v}}\in\mathcal{C}}{\bm{v}}^{\top}\bm{z}\geq K^{\prime}(\sqrt{d}+\tau))\leq\exp(-\tau^{2}).

To proceed, set
{\bm{v}}(\bm{z})\in\mathcal{C}
to be the nearest point to
\bar{\bm{z}}=\bm{z}/\|\bm{z}\|
in
\mathcal{C}
. Since
\|{\bm{v}}(\bm{z})-\bm{z}\|\leq 0.5
, note that

\|\bm{z}\|=\bar{\bm{z}}^{\top}\bm{z}={\bm{v}}(\bm{z})^{\top}\bm{z}+(\bm{z}-{\bm{v}}(\bm{z}))^{\top}\bm{z}\leq{\bm{v}}(\bm{z})^{\top}\bm{z}+0.5\|\bm{z}\|.

Thus,
\|\bm{z}\|\leq 2K^{\prime}(\sqrt{d}+\tau)
with probability at least
1-\exp(-\tau^{2})
.

H.1
Additional details for image classification experiments

Dataset.
As mentioned in Section
5.2
, we construct three datasets by modifying the original images in CIFAR-10:

•

Full-Tiled
. Each examples consists of a 64x64 images obtained by arranging a 32x32 image from CIFAR-10 in a tiling pattern with four tiles (cf. Fig.
3(a)
).

•

Partial-Tiled
. This is dataset is similar to
Full-Tiled
with the exception that each image has at-least
T
out of 4 tiles replaced by patches of i.i.d. random Gaussian noise with mean zero and variance 0.2. Note that, for each example in the dataset,
T\in\{1,2,3\}
is a random number as well as the location of the noisy tiles (cf. Fig.
3(b)
).

•

Embed-in-ImageNet

(
Karp et al. 2021
)
. We construct an example by simply embedding a 32x32 image from CIFAR-10 at a random location in a 64x64 background corresponding to a randomly selected image from ImageNet
(
Russakovsky et al. 2015
)
. We also add i.i.d. random Gaussian noise with mean zero and variance 0.2 to the background (cf. Fig.
3(c)
).

By construction, each dataset has 50,000 train and 10,000 test examples corresponding to train and test set of CIFAR-10. We also consider data-limited settings where we keep the test set intact but subsample the train set by selecting a fixed number of images for each class. Note that all three datasets define 10-way multiclass classification tasks with CIFAR-10 classes as potential labels.

Model architecture.
We utilize a tiny variant of the Vision transformer model
(
Dosovitskiy et al. 2021
)
for our experiments. This variant has 12 transformer layers with its hidden dimension, MLP intermediate dimension, and number of heads per attention layer being equal to 192, 768, and 3, respectively. The patch size in our study is set to be 4x4. The model itself (without counting the trainable parameters/weights during prompt-tuning) has approximately 5.44M parameters. We rely on the CLS token to obtain the classification logits.

Training.
We rely on Scenic library
(
Dehghani et al. 2022
)
4
4

4

https://github.com/google-research/scenic
to conduct our experiments on image classification. Following the default settings in the library along with a coarse grid search, we employ Adam optimizer
(
Kingma & Ba 2014
)
with
\beta_{1}
= 0.9,
\beta_{2}
= 0.999, weight decay = 0.1, and batch size = 128 while training a
randomly initialized
model. Furthermore, we employ a linear warm-up of learning rate followed cosine learning rate schedule with base learning rate 3e-3. As for the fine-tuning and prompt-tuning experiments that (partially) initialize from a
pre-trained
model, we rely on SGD with momentum parameter 0.9 and batch size = 128 to update trainable parameters. Again, we utilize a linear warm-up of learning rate followed by cosine learning rate schedule. Throughout our experiments, the base learning rates for fine-tuning and prompt-tuning are 1e-3 and 0.1, respectively.
