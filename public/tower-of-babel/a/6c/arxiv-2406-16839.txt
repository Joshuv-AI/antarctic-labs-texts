Coactions of compact groups on M n M_{n}
S. Kaliszewski, Magnus B. Landstad, John Quigg

Abstract
. We prove that every coaction of a compact group on a finite-dimensional C ∗ C^{*} -algebra is associated with a Fell bundle. Every coaction of a compact group on a matrix algebra is implemented by a unitary operator. A coaction of a compact group on M n M_{n} is inner if and only if its fixed-point algebra has an abelian C ∗ C^{*} -subalgebra of dimension n n . Investigating the existence of effective ergodic coactions on M n M_{n} reveals that SO ⁡ ( 3 ) \operatorname{SO}(3) has them, while SU ⁡ ( 2 ) \operatorname{SU}(2) does not. We give explicit examples of the two smallest finite nonabelian groups having effective ergodic coactions on M n M_{n} .

1.
Introduction

Coactions of locally compact groups on
C^{*}
-algebras were invented to generalize crossed-product duality to nonabelian groups.
Coactions of abelian groups correspond to actions of the dual groups,
via Fourier transform,
and the theories correspond remarkably closely.
In particular,
coactions of discrete groups correspond to actions of compact groups,
and
coactions of compact groups correspond to actions of discrete groups.
Since actions of compact groups are the easiest, it is not surprising that coactions of discrete groups are also easiest; in fact, they are essentially the same as Fell bundles (over discrete groups).
On the other hand, actions of discrete groups can be quite hard, and correspondingly coactions of compact groups can be expected to be hard too.
So, we felt that it is a good time to look closely at coactions of compact groups.
As a first step, we compensate by restricting the
C^{*}
-algebra to be finite-dimensional — in fact, as our title indicates, we concentrate on
M_{n}
.
A long time ago Iain asked one of us exactly this question, which we now can answer.

As a reward, we show that coactions of compact groups on finite-dimensional
C^{*}
-algebras are given by Fell bundles (see Theorem
3.1
).
We remark (see Remark
3.2
) that in fact the proof would work for arbitrary amenable groups (amenability would be needed to ensure that the Fourier algebra
A(G)
separates points of
M(C^{*}(G))
),
but we do not go into details here since we did not want to break up the flow with compact groups.
By the way, a Fell bundle over a locally compact group is required to have a compatible topology. However, every Fell bundle we will encounter over our compact group
G
will have only finitely many nonzero fibres, so the topology of
G
becomes irrelevant.

Next, since actions of discrete groups on
M_{n}
are unitarily implemented, it is natural to expect something along these lines for coactions of compact groups, and we prove this in Theorem
4.1

(see also
[
Was88
, Theorem 3]
).
The key here is to note that the passage from actions of an abelian group
G
to coactions of its dual group
\widehat{G}
involve passing from
C_{0}(G)
to
C^{*}(G)
, and that the individual automorphisms of
G
can be recovered from
C_{0}(G)
by composing with irreducible representations.

Inner coactions of
G
are determined by homomorphisms of
C(G)
, and we use this to characterize the inner coactions on
M_{n}
as
those
whose fixed-point algebras contain a maximal abelian subalgebra of
M_{n}
(see Theorem
5.1
).
We illustrate our techniques by considering inner coactions on
M_{2}
and
M_{3}
(Examples
5.3
and
5.4
).

In some sense at the opposite extreme from inner coactions are the ergodic ones, and we might as well assume that our ergodic coactions are effective (that is, every spectral subspace
A_{x}
is nonzero), and this forces us down to finite groups — indeed, those of order
n^{2}
since our coactions are on
M_{n}
.
In fact, the existence of an effective ergodic coaction of
G
on
M_{n}
is characterized by a result of Kleppner
[
Kle62
]
regarding “regular elements”.
The case of
M_{p}
with
p
prime is unsurprisingly quite special, forcing
G=\mathbb{Z}_{p}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}
(see Theorem
6.4
).
As an application of our techniques, we show that
\operatorname{SU}(2)
has no ergodic coaction on any
M_{n}
(with
n>1
), while
\operatorname{SO}(3)
does (see Example
6.5
).
In Example
6.7
we give an explicit example of two finite nonabelian groups having
effective ergodic coactions on
M_{n}
.
The groups have order 16, and this is minimal.

In Section
7
we examine coactions on
M_{n}
of the smallest nonabelian (discrete) group
S_{3}
,
and in particular we characterize the effective inner coactions, which require
n\geq 4
(see Proposition
7.1
).
Finally, in Section
8
we briefly indicate further possible research (and we comment “Clearly, we have only scratched the surface”).

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\delta
be a coaction of a compact group

on a finite-dimensional

-algebra

Then there is a Fell bundle

is isomorphic to the dual coaction

Proof.

Let

(a)^{\circ}=\{\phi\in A(G):\phi\cdot a=0\}.

Note that

\delta(a)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}e_{i}\otimes c_{i},

where

\{e_{1},\dots,e_{p}\}

.
To ease the notational burden a bit, put
D=\{c_{1},\dots,c_{p}\}
.
Because the

are linearly independent it follows by slicing that
\phi\in(a)^{\circ}

if and only if

\phi
is in the annihilator
D^{\perp}
.

Now,
(a)^{\circ}
is an ideal of
A(G)
of finite codimension,
so is contained in only finitely many maximal ideals.
By
[
Eym64
, Théorèm 3.34]
, the maximal ideals of
A(G)
are of the form
I_{x}=\{\phi\in A(G):\phi(x)=0\}
, so the zero set of
(a)^{\circ}
can be identified with a finite subset
E\subseteq G
.
As we mentioned before this proof,

is spectral
because it is finite (see
[
War76
, Theorem 4]
), consequently the ideal

coincides with the intersection

I_{E}=\bigcap_{x\in E}I_{x}=\{\phi\in A(G):\phi(x)=0\text{ for all }x\in E\}.

Now, by general

-theory we can identify
A(G)=(C^{*}(G))^{*}
with the dual space of
M(C^{*}(G))
when the latter is given the strict topology.
Then we can interpret the above as saying that the two subsets

of

have the same annihilator in

D^{\perp}=E^{\perp}.

It then follows by the Bipolar Theorem that

is contained in the double polar of

Since

is finite, this double polar is just the linear span. Thus we have

Thus

\delta(a)\in A\otimes\operatorname{span}E,

and so there are

such that

(3.1)

\delta(a)=\sum_{x\in E}b_{x}\otimes x.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in E}\delta(b_{x})\otimes x

\displaystyle=(\delta\otimes\text{id})\circ\delta(a)

\displaystyle=(\text{id}\otimes\delta_{G})\circ\delta(a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in E}b_{x}\otimes x\otimes x.

Since

,
we conclude that

\delta(b_{x})=b_{x}\otimes x\quad\text{for all }x\in E,

i.e.,

Slicing (

) by

.
Letting

Now, the family

is a Fell bundle, and since

is finite-dimensional it must coincide with the full

.

4.
Unitary implementation

We give an elementary proof of an implementation theorem
(proved for arbitrary Type I factors in
[
Was88
, Theorem 3]
,
using more sophisticated machinery).

Theorem 4.1
.

Every coaction of a compact group on
M_{n}
is unitarily implemented.

Proof.

Let
\delta:A\to M(A\otimes C^{*}(G))
be a coaction where

A=M_{n}
.
Choose a decomposition of
C^{*}(G)
into a
c_{0}
-direct sum of matrix algebras:

C^{*}(G)=\bigoplus_{i\in S}M_{i}.

For each
i
we have a central projection
p_{i}\in C^{*}(G)
such that
p_{i}C^{*}(G)\simeq M_{i}
, and we fix such an isomorphism and in fact identify
p_{i}C^{*}(G)=M_{i}
.
Then we get
a homomorphism

\delta_{i}=(1\otimes p_{i})\delta:A\to A\otimes M_{i}.

Since

A
is
elementary,
we can apply the destabilization process:
write
C_{i}
for the relative commutant of
\delta_{i}(A)
in
M(A\otimes M_{i})
, so that

A\otimes M_{i}=\operatorname{span}\{\delta(A)C_{i}\}\simeq A\otimes C_{i}.

Since
A=M_{n}
, we see that
C_{i}
must also be a matrix algebra, and by counting
dimensions, we must have
C_{i}\simeq M_{i}
.
Fixing such an isomorphism, by composition we get an automorphism
\phi_{i}\in\operatorname{Aut}(A\otimes M_{i})
such that

\displaystyle\phi_{i}(A\otimes 1)=\delta_{i}(A)\quad\text{and}\quad\phi_{i}(1\otimes M_{i})=C_{i}.

Since
A\otimes M_{i}
is elementary,
we can choose a unitary
U_{i}
in
A\otimes M_{i}
such that

\phi_{i}=\operatorname{Ad}U_{i}
.
It follows that

\delta_{i}(a)=\operatorname{Ad}U_{i}(a\otimes 1)\quad\text{for all }a\in A.

Now we put them together: define a unitary

U=\sum_{i}U_{i}\in M(C^{*}(G)).

Then we have

\delta(a)=\sum_{i}\delta_{i}(a)=\sum_{i}\operatorname{Ad}U_{i}(a\otimes 1_{M_{i}})=\operatorname{Ad}U(a\otimes 1_{M(C^{*}(G))}),

so that
U
implements
\delta
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

With the above notation, if
U
is a unitary implementing
\delta
then
the set of all unitaries implementing
\delta
is

\{U(1\otimes u):u\in UM(C^{*}(G))\}.

Proof.

This follows quickly from doing it on each quotient by
1\otimes p_{i}
,
then assembling them.
∎

5.
Inner

In the preceding section we saw that all coactions of
G
on
M_{n}
are implemented by unitaries
U\in M(M_{n}\otimes C^{*}(G))
.
The inner coactions of
G
on
M_{n}
are precisely those
for which we can take

U=(\mu\otimes\text{id})(w_{G})

for a unital homomorphism
\mu:C(G)\to M_{n}
.

We pause to see what
\mu
must look like.
By the Spectral Theorem we can choose
orthogonal minimal
projections
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}

and
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in G
such that

\mu(f)=\sum_{i}f(x_{i})p_{i}.

Moreover, any such choice of
p_{i}
and
x_{i}
will give a homomorphism
\mu:C(G)\to M_{n}
.

For any coaction
\delta
of
G
on
M_{n}
, observe that
A^{\delta}
is a unital
C^{*}
-subalgebra of
M_{n}
;
we get some information by
looking at its maximal abelian
C^{*}
-subalgebras.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\delta
be a coaction of
G
on
A=M_{n}
with
n>1
.
Then
\delta
is inner if and only if
its fixed-point algebra
M_{n}^{\delta}
has an abelian
C^{*}
-subalgebra of dimension
n
.

Proof.

First suppose that

M^{\delta}_{n}
has
an abelian
C^{*}
-subalgebra of dimension
n
.
Then we can find rank-one projections
p_{1},\dots p_{n}\in A^{\delta}
such that
\sum_{i}p_{i}=1
.
Choose matrix units
e_{ij}
such that
e_{ii}=p_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n
.
By Theorem
4.1
we can choose a unitary
U
implementing
\delta
.
We can
write

U=\sum_{i,j}e_{ij}\otimes y_{ij}\quad\text{with }y_{ij}\in M(C^{*}(G)).

For each
k
we have

\displaystyle p_{k}\otimes 1

\displaystyle=\delta(p_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,r,j,s}e_{ir}p_{k}e_{sj}\otimes y_{ir}y_{js}^{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}e_{ij}\otimes y_{ik}y_{jk}^{*}.

Because the
e_{ij}
are linearly independent,
all terms except when
i=j=k

are zero,
and

y_{kk}y_{kk}^{*}=1
, so
y_{kk}
is a unitary.
On the other hand, with
i\neq k
and
j=k
we get

0=y_{ik}y_{kk}^{*},

so
y_{ik}=0
since
y_{kk}\in UM(C^{*}(G))
.
Thus

U=\sum_{i}p_{i}\otimes y_{i},

where we have written
y_{i}=y_{ii}
to
simplify the notation.
For
all
i,j

we have

\delta(e_{ij})=\sum_{r,s}p_{r}e_{ij}p_{s}\otimes y_{r}y_{s}^{*}=e_{ij}\otimes y_{i}y_{j}^{*}.

The coaction identity
implies that

\delta_{G}(y_{i}y_{j}^{*})=y_{i}y_{j}^{*}\otimes y_{i}y_{j}^{*}
, so
y_{i}y_{j}^{*}\in G
and
e_{ij}\in A_{y_{i}y_{j}^{*}}
.

Now we adjust
U
so that
y_{i}
itself is in
G
:
recall that if
U^{\prime}
is any unitary implementing
\delta
then
U^{\prime}=U(1\otimes u)
for some
u\in UM(C^{*}(G))
,
and moreover every choice of
u\in UM(C^{*}(G))
gives a suitable
U^{\prime}
.
If we take
u=y_{n}^{*}
,
then

x_{i}:=y_{i}y_{n}^{*}\in G
for
i=1,\dots,n

(and
x_{n}=e
),
and therefore we now can
replace the original
U
by

U=\sum_{i}p_{i}\otimes x_{i}

with
x_{i}\in G
.

We can define a homomorphism
\mu:C(G)\to M_{n}
by

\mu(f)=\sum_{i}p_{i}f(x_{i}),

and then

U=(\mu\otimes\text{id})(w_{G}),

so
\delta
is inner.

Conversely, suppose that
\delta
is inner, determined by a unital homomorphism
\mu:C(G)\to A
.
Choose
mutually orthogonal minimal projections
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\in M_{n}

and
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in G
such that

\mu(f)=\sum_{i}f(x_{i})p_{i}.

Thus
\delta
is implemented by

(\mu\otimes\text{id})(w_{G})=\sum_{i}p_{i}\otimes x_{i},

and
a quick computation shows that

\delta(a)=\sum_{i,j}p_{i}ap_{j}\otimes x_{i}x_{j}^{-1}.

Choosing matrix units
e_{ij}
such that
e_{ii}=p_{i}
, we have
e_{ij}\in A_{x_{i}x_{j}^{-1}}
.
In particular,
p_{i}=e_{ii}\in A_{e}=A^{\delta}
for all
i
, so
the fixed-point algebra
A^{\delta}
has an abelian
C^{*}
-subalgebra of dimension
n
.
∎

Paraphrasing the above theorem and its proof, we see that an inner coaction
\delta
on
A=M_{n}
is implemented by

U=\sum_{1}^{n}p_{i}\otimes x_{i},

where
p_{i}=e_{ii}
and
x_{i}\in G
.
For the matrix units we have

e_{ij}\in A_{x_{i}x_{j}^{-1}}.

Moreover, the
x_{i}
’s are only unique up to multiplying (on the right) by any fixed
x\in G
.
Here are some easy consequences:

Proposition 5.2
.

With the above notation, we have:

(1)

e_{ij}\in A^{\delta}
if and only if
x_{i}=x_{j}
;

(2)

For any particular pair
i,j
, if
e_{ij}\in A^{\delta}
then without loss of generality we can modify the
x
’s so that
x_{i}=x_{j}=e
,
and then for all
k\neq i,j
we have

\displaystyle e_{ik}

\displaystyle\in A_{x_{k}^{-1}}

\displaystyle e_{kj}

\displaystyle\in A_{x_{k}}.

Example 5.3
.

For inner coactions
\delta
of
G
on
A=M_{2}
,
since
e_{22}\in A^{\delta}

the only choice is the element
x\in G
for which
e_{12}\in A_{x}
,
and then the spectrum is
\operatorname{sp}\delta=\{e,x,x^{-1}\}
.
If
x=x^{-1}
then
\operatorname{sp}\delta=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.
In general
\operatorname{sp}\delta
may not be a subgroup of
G
,
unless
x^{2}=x^{-1}
, in which case
\operatorname{sp}\delta=\mathbb{Z}_{3}
.
Note that if
x^{2}\neq x^{-1}
we could still “regard” this as coming from an effective
coaction of
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
,
but this is of questionable value since
G
might not have
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
as a subgroup.

Of course, if
x=e
then
\delta
is trivial and
A^{\delta}=A
.

Example 5.4
.

For inner coactions
\delta
of
G
on
A=M_{3}
,
if for example

A^{\delta}=\operatorname{span}\{e_{ij}:1\leq i,j\leq 2\text{ or }i=j=3\},

then
\delta
is implemented by a corepresentation of the form

U=e_{11}\otimes 1+e_{22}\otimes 1+e_{33}\otimes x

for some

.
This is a routine application of Proposition
5.2
and the discussion immediately preceding it.

6.
Ergodic

Focusing on coactions of our compact group
G
on
M_{n}
, there are some immediate, easy facts.

Lemma 6.1
.

If
G
has
an effective ergodic coaction on
M_{n}
with
n>1
,
then:

(1)

G
has order
|G|=n^{2}
.

(2)

If
G
is nonabelian then
n>2
.

(3)

G
is noncyclic.

Proof.

(1)
If
\delta
is an ergodic coaction of
G
on
A=M_{n}
,
then (see
[
KS82
]
)
every spectral subspace
A_{x}
is spanned by a unitary, and
the subspaces
\{A_{x}:x\in G\}
are linearly independent,
so
|G|=n^{2}
.

(2)
This follows immediately from (1).

(3)
This follows quickly from the triviality of the 2-cohomology
H^{2}(G,\mathbb{T})
when
G
is cyclic; see
[
FD88a
, Proposition III.5.11]
for example.
But here is a quick and dirty argument:
Suppose
G
is cyclic with generator
x
, and pick a unitary
u\in A_{x}
.
Then
\{u^{i}:i=1,\dots,n\}
is a choice of unitaries in the spectral subspaces,
from which it follows that
M_{n}
is commutative, which is absurd.
∎

Lemma
6.1
is almost trivial.
The (seemingly) slightly stronger
Proposition
6.2
below is easy but relies on somewhat more sophisticated technology.

Proposition 6.2
.

If
G
has an ergodic coaction on
M_{n}
with
n>1
,
then
G
has a finite abelian noncyclic subgroup.

Proof.

Suppose that
\delta
is an ergodic coaction on
A=M_{n}
.
Then the spectrum
\operatorname{sp}\delta
is a subgroup of order
n^{2}
,
and without loss of generality we suppose that
G=\operatorname{sp}\delta
.
Choose spanning unitaries
U_{x}
for the spectral subspaces
A_{x}
,
and let
\omega
be the associated 2-cocycle.
Then
U
is
an irreducible
\omega
-representation of
G
.
In
[
Kle62
, page 559]
Kleppner defines
x\in G
to be
\omega
-regular
if

\omega(x,y)=\omega(y,x)\quad\text{whenever }xy=yx.

and it follows from

[
Kle62
, Corollary 1]
that

G\setminus\{e\}
contains no
\omega
-regular elements.
Thus, picking any
x\neq e
we can find
y\neq e
such that
xy=yx
and
\omega(x,y)\neq\omega(y,x)
.
Let
H=\langle x,y\rangle
be the abelian subgroup generated by
x
and
y
, so
\omega
restricted to
H\times H
is a non-trivial cocycle.
As we mentioned in the preceding proof,

H
must be noncyclic.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

When
G
is abelian, Kleppner’s characterization mentioned in the above proof
can actually be reformulated as

[
OPT80
, Theorem 5.9]
.
Even if
G
is not assumed to be abelian, it is possible to give a characterization generalizing that in
[
OPT80
]
.

Proposition 6.4
.

If
G
has
an irreducible projective representation
U
on
\mathbb{C}^{p}

with
p
prime
and
\{U_{x}:x\in G\}
linearly independent, then

G=\mathbb{Z}_{p}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.With
\gamma=\exp(2\pi i/p)
, then up to equivalence

U(1,0)=\operatorname{diag}(\gamma^{j}\mid j=1\cdots p)\quad\text{and}\quad U(0,1)=\{\delta(j,j+1)\mid j\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\}.

Proof.

From the assumptions there are
x,y\in G
with
yx=xy
and

\omega(x,y)\neq\omega(y,x).

Since

|G|=p^{2}
,
the order of
x
and
y
must be
1
,
p
or
p^{2}
(remember that we assume
G
is finite).
One quickly rules out
1
and
p^{2}
. Then one realizes that one can pick
x,y
such that

\omega(x,y)\overline{\omega(y,x)}=\exp(2\pi i/p)=:\gamma.

We must have
H\cap K=\{e\}
, so

|HK|=|H|\,|K|=p^{2}=|G|
, and hence
G=\mathbb{Z}_{p}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.
We leave as an exercise to show that
U
as described is (up to equivalence) the only irreducible projective representation of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}
satisfying the theorem.
∎

Example 6.5
.

It follows from Proposition
6.2
that
\operatorname{SU}(2)
has no ergodic coactions on any
M_{n}
with
n>1
,
because every finite abelian subgroup is cyclic (folklore).
Interestingly, the quotient group
\operatorname{SO}(3)
has an ergodic coaction on
M_{2}
,
because it contains the Klein-4 group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
as a subgroup;
for example, the elements

\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&-1&0\\
0&0&-1\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0\\
0&-1&0\\

are suitable generators.

Remark 6.6
.

[
Kle62
, Discussion following Corollary 1]

shows that if
H
is any abelian group of order
n
,
then there is a 2-cocycle
\omega
on
G=H\times\widehat{H}
given by

\omega\bigl((x,\chi),(y,\sigma)\bigr)=\chi(y),

and no element of

is
\omega

G

is of central type

.

is a special case.
In fact, Frucht proved in
[

This was rediscovered somewhat later by Olesen, Pedersen, and Takesaki

.

Ng

]

G
is such a group, with generators

and

\langle a\rangle
and
\langle b\rangle

Moreover, if

So, for

7.
G=S_{3}

It is interesting to examine coactions
\delta
of the smallest nonabelian group
S_{3}
(the permutations of a set of three elements) on
A=M_{n}
.
We fix a presentation

S_{3}=\langle\alpha,\beta\mid\alpha^{3}=\beta^{2}=e,\alpha\beta=\beta\alpha^{2}\rangle.

Since all proper subgroups of
S_{3}
are cyclic, to avoid trivialities we look for effective coactions.
By Lemma
6.1
this requires
n\geq 3
,
and we cannot expect
\delta
to be ergodic.

Here’s where we can make some progress: look for effective
inner
coactions.
As we have observed, we need
n\geq 3
.
But even
n=3
will not work:
in the notation of the proof of Theorem
5.1
,
we have
\operatorname{sp}\delta=\{x_{i}x_{j}^{-1}\}
with
x_{i}\in S_{3}
, and we can assume
x_{3}=1
.
So, we can choose
x_{1},x_{2}
, then
we have
e_{12}\in A_{x_{1}x_{2}^{-1}}
.
Since
S_{3}
has three self-inverse elements, trivial computations show that we can have at most
three non-identity elements
in
\operatorname{sp}\delta
, which is absurd.
Therefore we cannot have an effective inner coaction of
S_{3}
on
M_{3}
.

Therefore, an effective inner coaction of
S_{3}
on
M_{n}
requires
n\geq 4
.
And then it is easy to generate examples:
as we mentioned above, we might as well choose
x_{4}=1
.
Then, for example, we can choose

x_{1}=\alpha,\quad x_{2}=\beta,\quad\text{and}\quad x_{3}=\alpha\beta.

Then
\{x_{i}x_{j}^{-1}:i,j=1,\dots,3\}
will contain

x_{1}x_{2}^{-1}=\alpha\beta\quad\text{and}\quad x_{2}x_{3}^{-1}=\beta\alpha=\alpha^{2}\beta,

and we have accounted for all six elements of
S_{3}
.
Trivially we could have made other choices, and we could do something similar for any
n\geq 5
as well.
Conclusion:

Proposition 7.1
.

For any
n\geq 4
the above procedure gives all effective inner coactions of
S_{3}
on
M_{n}
,
but there are none for
n<4
.

8.
Conclusion

Clearly, we have only scratched the surface here;
there are many aspects of compact coactions on
M_{n}
that need more investigation.
For example, Section
5
leads to the following train of thought:
the
center of the
fixed-point algebra
A^{\delta}

is spanned by
projections
\{p_{i}\}_{i\in S}
,
and the coaction
\delta
restricts to the corners
p_{i}Ap_{i}
.
But a bit more thought reveals that we have a “coaction” on a Fell bundle
\{p_{i}Ap_{j}\}_{i,j\in S}
over the groupoid

S\times S
with multiplication
(i,j)(j,k)=(i,k)
.
It is tantalizing to think that this observation could yield significant further insight into the coaction
\delta
.

Also, coactions of compact groups have been recently shown to be
Pedersen rigid
in the sense that the outer conjugacy class is completely determined by the isomorphism class of the dual action (see
[
KOQT23
]
). How is this related to our results in this paper?

A next step in the study of coactions of the compact group
G
is to “ramp up” from
M_{n}
to
\mathcal{K}
(the compact operators on a separable infinite-dimensional Hilbert space).
Perhaps one should even investigate more general AF-algebras in this connection?
In particular, how about coactions of
G
on UHF algebras, e.g.,
M_{n}\otimes M_{n}\otimes\cdots
,
investigated by Masuda in
[
Mas08
]
?

If
U
is a unitary implementing the coaction
\delta
as in Theorem
4.1
, it is possible to show that there is a unique unitary
u\in M(C^{*}(G)\otimes C^{*}(G))
such that

(\text{id}\otimes\delta_{G})(U)=U_{12}U_{13}(1\otimes u).

This looks like some sort of “2-cocycle” for
U
,
and perhaps it would be fruitful to regard
U
as a sort of “twisted corepresentation”?
This would perhaps lead to a cohomology theory of coactions.
In fact, this is explored in
[
Lan92
,
Was88
]
.
The ultimate goal here would be to classify
coactions of a compact group
G
on matrix algebras
M_{n}
.
