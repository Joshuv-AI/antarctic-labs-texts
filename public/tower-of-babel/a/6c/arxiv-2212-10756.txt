Abstract
Partitions with initial repetitions were introduced by George Andrews. We consider a subclass of these partitions and find Legendre theorems associated with their respective partition functions. The results in turn provide partition theoretic interpretations of some Rogers-Ramanujan identities due to Lucy J. Slater.



Legendre theorems for a class of partitions with initial repetitions

Key words
: partition; generating function, initial repetition, bjiection.

Introduction

A partition of
n
is a non-increasing sequence of positive integers:
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\cdots,\lambda_{s})
such that
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{s}\lambda_{i}=n
. The summands
\lambda_{i}
’s are called
parts
and the
length
of a partition is the total number of parts (counting multiplicity). Instead of the ‘vector notation’ , we sometimes use the multiplicity notation
(\mu_{1}^{m_{1}},\mu_{2}^{m_{2}},\mu_{3}^{m_{3}},\cdots,\mu_{\ell}^{m_{\ell}})
in which
m_{i}
denotes the multiplicity of the part
\mu_{i}
and
\mu_{1}>\mu_{2}>\cdots>\mu_{\ell}
. If
m_{i}=1
for all
i
, we have a partition into distinct parts.

The
union
of two partitions
\lambda
and
\beta
, denoted by
\lambda\cup\beta
, is simply the multiset union where
\lambda
and
\beta
are treated as multisets. For instance, if
\lambda=(9^{3},7^{2},1^{3})
and
\beta=(7^{4},5^{3},4,1^{3})
, then
\lambda\cup\beta=(9^{3},7^{6},5^{3},4,1^{6})
.

For a partition
\lambda=(\mu_{1}^{m_{1}},\mu_{2}^{m_{2}},\mu_{3}^{m_{3}},\cdots,\mu_{\ell}^{m_{\ell}})
, the conjugate of
\lambda
is denoted by
\lambda^{\prime}
and it is given by:

\lambda^{\prime}=\left(\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}m_{i}\right)^{\mu_{\ell}},\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\ell-1}m_{i}\right)^{\mu_{\ell-1}-\mu_{\ell}},\cdots,m_{1}^{\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}}\right).

We shall use upper case letters for sets and lower case for counting functions. If
A(n)
denotes the set of partitions of
n
with a certain property, then
a(n)
denotes the cardinality of
A(n)
, i.e.
a(n)=|A(n)|
. An element of
A(n)
shall be referred to as an
a(n)
-partition.

We shall use
D(n)
to denote the set of partitions of
n
into distinct parts and so by our notation above,
d(n)=|D(n)|
. For instance,
d(5)=3
and the
d(5)
-partitions are
(5),(4,1)
and
(3,2)
. Let
d_{e}(n)
(resp.
d_{o}(n)
) be the number of
d(n)
-partitions with even (resp. odd) length. Legendre
[
17
]
proved that

d_{e}(n)-d_{o}(n)=\begin{cases}(-1)^{j},&\text{if $n=j(3j\pm 1)/2,j\geq 0$;}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(1.1)

a result that later became known as Legendre’s theorem. Note that (
1.1
) is also known as the pentagonal number theorem because the numbers
j(3j\pm 1)/2,j\in\mathbb{Z}
are the generalized pentagonal numbers. In fact, the numbers appearing in all our theorems in this paper are generalized polygonal numbers (or multiples thereof).

An interesting bijective proof of (
1.1
) was given by J. Franklin (see
[
7
]
). For related work in partition theory, see
[
1
,

,

,

,

,

]
.

Fine
[
3
]
studied partitions without gaps. A partition without gaps whose parts are in the set
A
is one in which every part is in
A
and every positive integer that is less than the largest part appears as a part. For instance, for
A=\{1+2j:j=0,1,2,3,\ldots\}
, we can talk about partitions into odd parts without gaps. We shall also use the terminology
gap-free partitions
to mean partitions without gaps. If the set
A
is not explicitly stated, we assume that
A=\{1,2,3,4,5,\ldots\}
.

Motivated by Fine’s work on partitions into odd parts without gaps and an observation that partitions without gaps are in one-to-one correspondence with partitions into distinct parts, George Andrews
[
6
]
introduced partitions with initial repetitions. His definition is given as follows:

Definition 1.1
.

A partition of
n
with initial
k
-repetitions is one in which either

a)

every part appears at most
k-1
times

or

b)

there is some part
j
which appears at least
k
times and every positive integer less than
j
appears at least
k
times as a part, and all parts greater than
j
appear at most
k-1
times.

For example, the partition
(10^{2},7,4^{3},3^{3},2^{5},1^{4})
is a partition with initial 3-repetitions.

Among several results, in the same paper
[
6
]
, Andrews was able to show that if
f_{e}(m,n)
(rep.
f_{o}(m,n)
) denotes the number of partitions of
n
with initial 2-repetitions with
m
different parts and an even (resp. odd) number of distinct parts, then

f_{e}(m,n)-f_{o}(m,n)=\begin{cases}(-1)^{j},&\text{if $m=j,n=j(j+1)/2,j\geq 0$;}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Statements of the type (
1.1
) are called Legendre theorems or identities of Euler pentagonal type.

In this paper, we find Legendre theorems associated with partitions with initial repetitions. Our first consideration is Andrews’ partitions with initial 2-repetitions. We look at subsets of these partitions in Section 2 and derive an interesting identity. In Section 3, we find several Legendre theorems. Consequently, these theorems provide partition-theoretic interpretations of the following identities of Rogers-Ramanujan type due to Slater

\prod\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{n})\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{n(n+1)}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{n}}=\prod\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{4n})(1-q^{4n-1})(1-q^{4n-3})

(1.2)

\prod\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{2n})\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{n(2n+1)}}{(q;q)_{2n+1}}=\prod\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{4n})(1+q^{4n-1})(1+q^{4n-3})

(1.3)

\prod\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{n})\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{n(n+1)}}{(q;q)_{n}}=\prod\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{5n})(1-q^{5n-1})(1-q^{5n-4})

(1.4)

\prod\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{2n})\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{2n(n+1)}}{(q;q)_{2n+1}}=\prod\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{8n})(1+q^{8n-1})(1+q^{8n-7})

(1.5)

\prod\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{4n})\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{4n^{2}}(q;q^{2})_{2n}}{(q^{4};q^{4})_{2n}}=\prod\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{12n})(1-q^{12n-5})(1-q^{12n-7})

(1.6)

\prod\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{4n})\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{4n(n+1)}(q;q^{2})_{2n+1}}{(q^{4};q^{4})_{2n+1}}=\prod\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{12n})(1-q^{12n-1})(1-q^{12n-11})

(1.7)

\prod\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{4n})\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{4n(n+1)}(-q;q^{2})_{2n+1}}{(q^{4};q^{4})_{2n+1}}=\prod\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{12n})(1+q^{12n-1})(1+q^{12n-11})

(1.8)

\prod\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-q^{2n})}{(1+q^{2n-1})}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{n(n+2)}(-q;q^{2})_{n+1}(-q^{2};q^{4})_{n}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{2n+1}}=\prod\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{16n})(1-q^{16n-4})(1+q^{16n-12})

(1.9)

Throughout our discussion, we assume that

and some of the tools that we use include the following identities:

\sum\limits_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}z^{n}q^{n(n+1)/2}=\prod\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{n})(1+zq^{n})(1+z^{-1}q^{n-1})

(1.10)

where

and

\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{n^{2}}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1-q^{n}}{1+q^{n}}.

On Andrews’ partitions with 2-initial repetitions

We first record the following result.

Lemma 2.1
.

For
|q|<1
, the following factorisations hold:

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{2n^{2}-n}}{(q;q)_{2n-1}}=(-q;q)_{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n+1}q^{n^{2}},

(2.1)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{2n^{2}+n}}{(q;q)_{2n}}=(-q;q)_{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{n^{2}}.

(2.2)

Proof.

Since
d(n)=d_{e}(n)-d_{o}(n)+2d_{o}(n)
, we have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d(n)q^{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(d_{e}(n)-d_{o}(n))q^{n}+2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{o}(n)q^{n}.

\displaystyle 2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{o}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=(-q;q)_{\infty}-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(d_{e}(n)-d_{o}(n))q^{n}

\displaystyle=(-q;q)_{\infty}-(q;q)_{\infty}

\displaystyle=(-q;q)_{\infty}\left(1-\frac{(q;q)_{\infty}}{(-q;q)_{\infty}}\right)

\displaystyle=(-q;q)_{\infty}\left(1-\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{n^{2}}\right)\,\,(\text{by}\,\,\eqref{gauss})

\displaystyle=2(-q;q)_{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n+1}q^{n^{2}}.

Thus it is not difficult to see that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{o}(n)q^{n}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{n(2n-1)}}{(q;q)_{2n-1}}

from which (
2.1
) follows. For (
2.2
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{e}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d(n)q^{n}-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{o}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=(-q;q)_{\infty}+(-q;q)_{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{n^{2}}\,\,(\text{by}\,\,\eqref{factor1})

\displaystyle=(-q;q)_{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{n^{2}}.

It can easily be shown that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{e}(n)q^{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{n(2n+1)}}{(q;q)_{2n}}

and so (
2.2
) follows.
∎

We prove the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
b^{e}(n)
be the number of partitions of
n
with initial 2-repetitions in which either all parts are distinct or the largest repeated part is even. Similarly, let
b^{o}(n)
denote the number of partitions of
n
with initial 2-repetitions in which at least one part is repeated and the largest repeated part is odd. Then

b^{e}(n)-b^{o}(n)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if}\,\,n=\frac{j(j+1)}{2};\\
0\,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b^{e}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}(1+q^{j})+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{2(1+2+3+\ldots+2m)}}{(q;q)_{2m}}\prod_{j=2m+1}^{\infty}(1+q^{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{2(1+2+3+\ldots+2m)}}{(q;q)_{2m}}\prod_{j=2m+1}^{\infty}(1+q^{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{2m(2m+1)}}{(q;q)_{2m}}\prod_{j=2m+1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-q^{2j})}{(1-q^{j})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{2m(2m+1)}}{(q;q)_{2m}(q^{2m+1};q)_{\infty}}\prod_{j=2m+1}^{\infty}(1-q^{2j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}q^{2m(2m+1)}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{2j})}{\prod_{j=1}^{2m}(1-q^{2j})}

\displaystyle=\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{2m(2m+1)}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{2m}}

and

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}b^{o}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{2(1+2+3+\ldots+2m-1})}{(q;q)_{2m-1}}\prod_{j=2m}^{\infty}(1+q^{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{2(1+2+3+\ldots+2m-1)}}{(q;q)_{2m-1}}\prod_{j=2m}^{\infty}\frac{1-q^{2j}}{1-q^{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{2m(2m-1)}}{(q;q)_{2m-1}(q^{2m};q)_{\infty}}\prod_{j=2m}^{\infty}(1-q^{2j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{(q;q)_{\infty}}}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}q^{2m(2m-1)}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{2j})}{\prod_{j=1}^{2m-1}(1-q^{2j})}

\displaystyle=\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{2m(2m-1)}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{2m-1}}.

Thus

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}(b^{e}(n)-b^{o}(n))q^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{2n(2n+1)}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{2n}}-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{2n(2n-1)}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{2n-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{2n^{2}}-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n+1}q^{2n^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\left(\text{by}\,\,\eqref{factor1}\,\,\text{and}\,\,\eqref{factor2}\,\,\text{with $q$ replaced by $q^{2}$}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\left(\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{2n^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(-q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}\,\,(\text{by}\,\,\eqref{gauss}\,\,\text{with $q$ replaced by $q^{2}$})

\displaystyle=\frac{(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}(-q;q)_{\infty}}{(-q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}(-q;q^{4})_{\infty}(-q^{3};q^{4})_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}q^{2n^{2}+n}\,\,(\text{by}\,\,\eqref{jacobi}\,\,\text{with $q$ replaced by $q^{4}$ and $z$ replaced by $q^{-1}$})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{n(n+1)/2}.

For the last equality, see Equation (1.4.9) of
[
12
]
.
∎

3
Combinatorial proof of Theorem
2.1

Let
B(n)
be the set of partitions of
n
with initial
2
-repetitions. If
\lambda\in B(n)
, write
\lambda=\alpha\cup\beta
with
\alpha
a distinct partition and
\beta
a partition whose parts have even multiplicity. Then
\beta
is gap-free and
\beta^{\prime}
(the conjugate of
\beta
) is a distinct partition with even parts. The goal is to prove that

\displaystyle|\{\lambda=\alpha\cup\beta\in B(n):

\displaystyle\ \ell(\beta^{\prime})\text{ even}\}|-|\{\lambda=\alpha\cup\beta\in B(n):\ell(\beta^{\prime})\text{ odd}\}|

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&\text{ if }n=\frac{j(j+1)}{2}\\
0&\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

We further write
\alpha=(\alpha^{o},\alpha^{e})
, where
\alpha^{o}
(respectively
\alpha^{e}
) consists of the odd (respectively even) parts of
\alpha
.
Since
(\alpha^{e},\beta)
is a pair of distinct partitions with even parts, by doubling all parts in partitions in the proof of
[
8
, Proposition 2]
, one proves that

\displaystyle|\{\lambda

\displaystyle=\alpha^{o}\cup\alpha^{e}\cup\beta\in B(n):\ell(\beta^{\prime})\text{ even}\}|-|\{\lambda=\alpha^{o}\cup\alpha^{e}\cup\beta\in B(n):\ell(\beta^{\prime})\text{ odd}\}|

\displaystyle=|\{\lambda=\alpha^{o}\cup\gamma\in C(n):\ell(\gamma)\text{ even}\}|-|\{\lambda=\alpha^{o}\cup\gamma\in C(n):\ell(\gamma)\text{ odd}\}|,

where
C(n)
is the set of partitions
\mu=\alpha^{o}\cup\gamma
of
n
with
\alpha^{o}
a partition into distinct odd pats and
\gamma
a partition into parts divisible by
4
.

This shows combinatorially that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(b^{e}(n)-b^{o}(n))q^{n}=(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}(-q;q^{2})_{\infty}=(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}(-q;q^{4})_{\infty}(-q^{3};q^{4})_{\infty}.

To finish the combinatorial proof, one uses a combinatorial proof of the Jacobi triple product. For example, one can use the proof in
[
16
]
. Note that
[
16
]
gives a combinatorial proof for

(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}(q;q^{4})_{\infty}(q^{3};q^{4})_{\infty}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{T_{n}}q^{T_{n}},

where
T_{n}=n(n+1)/2
, but the parity of the number of odd parts in a partition is determined by the parity of the size.

Let
c_{1}(n)
denote the number of partitions of
n
in which

(a)

all parts are distinct, the only odd part that may appear is 1 and even parts are at least 8 and divisible by 4

(b)

Let

Theorem 4.1
.

For all

c_{1,e}(n)-c_{1,o}(n)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $n=j(6j+5),j\in\mathbb{Z}$};\\

Proof.

Since

\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{1}(n)q^{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{4(2+4+6+\ldots+2n)}(-q;q^{2})_{2n+1}(-q^{8n+8};q^{4})_{\infty},

we must have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}(c_{1,e}(n)-c_{1,o}(n))q^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{4(2+4+6+\ldots+2n)}(-q;q^{2})_{2n+1}(q^{8n+8};q^{4})_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{4n(n+1)}(-q;q^{2})_{2n+1}(q^{4(2n+2)};q^{4})_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{4n(n+1)}(-q;q^{2})_{2n+1}\frac{(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}}{(q^{4};q^{4})_{2n+1}}

\displaystyle=(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-q;q^{2})_{2n+1}\frac{q^{4n(n+1)}}{(q^{4};q^{4})_{2n+1}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1+q^{12n-11})(1+q^{12n-1})(1-q^{12n})\,\,\,\,\,(\text{by}\,\,\,\,\eqref{eq57})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}q^{n(6n+5)}\,\,\,\,\,\,(\text{by}\,\,\,\,\eqref{jacobi}).

∎

c_{2}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}q^{4n^{2}+3n}.

n\geq 0
,

c_{3,e}(n)-c_{3,o}(n)=\begin{cases}1,&n=2j^{2}+j,j\in\mathbb{Z};\\
\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{3}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{\overbrace{(2n+1)+(2n+1)+(2n+1)+\ldots+(2n+1)}^{n\rm\,times}}}{(1-q)(1-q^{3})\ldots(1-q^{2n+1})}(-q^{2n+2};q^{2})_{\infty}

and thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(c_{3,e}(n)-c_{3,o}(n))q^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{\overbrace{(2n+1)+(2n+1)+(2n+1)+\ldots+(2n+1)}^{n\rm\,times}}}{(1-q)(1-q^{3})\ldots(1-q^{2n+1})}(q^{2n+2};q^{2})_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{q^{n(2n+1)}(q^{2n+2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q;q^{2})_{n+1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{q^{n(2n+1)}(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q;q^{2})_{n+1}(q^{2};q^{2})_{n}}

\displaystyle=(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{q^{n(2n+1)}}{(q;q)_{2n+1}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1+q^{4n-1})(1+q^{4n-3})(1-q^{4n})\,\,\,(\text{by}\,\,\,\,\eqref{eq9})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}q^{2n^{2}+n}.

∎

Let
c_{4}(n)
be the number of partitions of
n
in which either

(a)

all parts are distinct

or

(b)

the largest repeated part
j
appears twice, all positive integers less than
j
appear twice. Note that parts greater than
j
are distinct.

Similar to the previous formulations, let
c_{4,e}(n)
(resp.
c_{4,o}(n)
) be the number of
c_{4}(n)
-partitions with an even (resp. odd) number of distinct parts.

Theorem 4.4
.

For all
n\geq 0
,

c_{4,e}(n)-c_{4,o}(n)=\begin{cases}(-1)^{j},&n=(5j^{2}+3j)/2,j\in\mathbb{Z};\\
\\
\ 0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Clearly,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{4}(n)q^{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{1+1+2+2+3+3+\cdots+n+n}(-q^{n+1};q)_{\infty}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{n(n+1)}(-q^{n+1};q)_{\infty}

so that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(c_{4,e}(n)-c_{4,o}(n))q^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{n(n+1)}(q^{n+1};q)_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{q^{n(n+1)}(q;q)_{\infty}}{(q;q)_{n}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-q^{5n-1}\right)\left(1-q^{5n-4}\right)\left(1-q^{5n}\right)\,\,\,(\text{by}\,\,\,\,\eqref{eq14})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{\frac{5n^{2}+3n}{2}}.

∎
