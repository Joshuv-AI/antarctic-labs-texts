Quasirandom quantum channels
Tom Bannink, Jop Briët, Farrokh Labib, Hans Maassen

Abstract
Mixing (or quasirandom) properties of the natural transition matrix associated to a graph can be quantified by its distance to the complete graph. Different mixing properties correspond to different norms to measure this distance. For dense graphs, two such properties known as spectral expansion and uniformity were shown to be equivalent in seminal 1989 work of Chung, Graham and Wilson. Recently, Conlon and Zhao extended this equivalence to the case of sparse vertex transitive graphs using the famous Grothendieck inequality. Here we generalize these results to the non-commutative, or ‘quantum’, case, where a transition matrix becomes a quantum channel. In particular, we show that for irreducibly covariant quantum channels, expansion is equivalent to a natural analog of uniformity for graphs, generalizing the result of Conlon and Zhao. Moreover, we show that in these results, the non-commutative and commutative (resp.) Grothendieck inequalities yield the best-possible constants.

Spectral expansion and uniformity.

Spectral expansion is a linear-algebraic property given in terms of the transition matrix of a graph.
This transition matrix is the normalized adjacency matrix, which for a
d
-regular graph
G=(V,E)
is given by
A_{uv}=e(\{u\},\{v\})/d
, where
e(S,T)
denotes the number of edges connecting subsets
S,T\subseteq V
.
We say that the graph
G
is an
(n,d,\lambda)
graph if
|V|=n
, it is
d
-regular and all but the largest eigenvalue of
A
, which is always 1, have modulus at most
\lambda
.
The smallest value of
\lambda
for which this holds is denoted by
\lambda(G)
.
Spectral expansion then refers to the property that
\lambda(G)
is much smaller than 1, in which case
G
is referred to as a (spectral) expander.
Expanders have many important applications in mathematics and computer science (we refer to
[
23
]
for an extensive survey).
One such application is in randomized algorithms,
which can exploit the fact that a random walk on an expander rapidly mixes (i.e. quickly converges to its limit distribution) to significantly reduce the amount of randomness needed.

Uniformity is a combinatorial property of the configuration of the edges.
An
n
-vertex
d
-regular graph
G=(V,E)
is
\epsilon
-uniform if for all
S,T\subseteq V
,

\displaystyle\Big|e(S,T)-\frac{d}{n}|S|\,|T|\Big|\leq\epsilon dn

(1)

and
\epsilon(G)
denotes the smallest value of
\epsilon
for which this holds.
Uniformity then refers to the property that this parameter is much smaller than 1; trivially any graph is 1-uniform.
Intuitively, this says that for any two vertex subsets, the number of edges between those sets is close to the expected number of edges in a random graph with the same edge density.

A basic result known as the Expander Mixing Lemma
[
23
]
shows that for any regular graph
G
we have
\epsilon(G)\leq\lambda(G)
,
which is to say that spectral expansion implies uniformity.
A sequence
G_{n}
of
d_{n}
-regular graphs is called

if
d_{n}\geq\Omega(n)
, and

if
d_{n}/n\longrightarrow 0
.
It was shown in
[
8
]
that in the dense case, a converse to the Expander Mixing Lemma
\epsilon(G_{n})\leq o(1)\Rightarrow\lambda(G_{n})\leq o(1)

also holds.
In contrast, Krivelevich and Sudakov
[
25
]
showed that this is false for sparse graphs, thereby answering the question posed in
[
7
]
.
Their counterexample is not regular, however (and a later one from
[
4
]
is not connected).
But in
[
9
]
it was shown that even regular sparse graphs (where

) can simultaneously satisfy
\epsilon(G_{n})\leq o(1)
and
\lambda(G_{n})\geq\Omega(1)
.
Surprisingly, Kohayakawa, Rödl, and Schacht
[
24
]
showed that Cayley graphs over abelian groups, including sparse ones, do again admit such a converse.
Cayley graphs are an important class of regular graphs that include for instance the famous Ramanujan graphs of Margulis
[
27
]
and Lubotzky, Phillips and Sarnak
[
26
]
.
Conlon and Zhao
[
9
]
generalized this to all Cayley graphs and showed that this implies the same for all vertex-transitive graphs in general, for which they showed that
\lambda(G)\leq 4K_{G}\epsilon(G)
, where

is the famous

, whose exact value is currently unknown; the bounds shown here are the best known and were shown by Davie and Reeds (independently) in
[
11

30
]
and Braverman et al. in
[
5
]

Spectral expansion and uniformity are thus equivalent notions of quasirandomness for dense graphs and vertex-transitive graphs.

A transition matrix, such as the normalized adjacency matrix of a graph, maps probability vectors

In independent work, Hastings

Also quantum expanders found many applications, one of which is again randomness reduction, where randomness takes on the form of random unitary matrices.
Since a
k

A 1-design is a fixed collection of unitaries

Quantum expanders can be used to construct

\Phi(X)
and
\Pi(X)
are close in trace distance

2

The trace distance is the distance induced by the Schatten-1 norm, defined in
Section

2
.
instead of precisely equal. Another application is in cryptography where Ambainis and Smith
[
1

used quantum expanders to construct short quantum one-time pads.
It was shown in
[
18

that truly random quantum channels (given by independent Haar-uniform

In this work we introduce a natural notion of uniformity for superoperators, informally given by how well they mimic the action of

This is similar to Hasting’s notion of edge expansion for quantum channels

In particular, we say that

|\langle P_{V},(\Phi-\Pi)(P_{W})\rangle|\leq\epsilon.

(3)

Let
\epsilon(\Phi)
denote the smallest

As we show in Section

\lambda(\Phi)
and
\epsilon(\Phi)

Finally, also symmetry, which in the graph-theoretic context takes the form of vertex transitivity, is an important property of quantum channels.
In particular,

quantum channels, which turn out to generalize vertex-transitive graphs (see Section

[
22

A now famous result of Hastings
[
19
]
shows that the minimum output capacity in general does not have the intuitively natural property of being sub-additive under tensor products.
However, it was shown earlier by Holevo
[
21

Summary of our results.

In this work we make a first step in the study of the equivalence of quasirandom properties for quantum channels, or superoperators in general, and show optimality in the case of vertex-transitive graphs and covariant quantum channels.

•

(

3.2
) Our main result shows that under irreducible covariance, expansion and uniformity are equivalent for superoperators.
In particular, while a simple analogue of the classical Expander Mixing Lemma implies that

, that for this class of superoperators, also

always holds. This implies the same result for vertex-transitive graphs with

-weighted edges, essentially proved in

]
with the factor 2 replaced by the

.

•

(

(

We sometimes omit the group and simply say

In

Theorem 3.1

(Conlon–Zhao)
.

Let

be a linear map that is transitive covariant with respect to
\Gamma

\|A\|_{L_{2}\to L_{2}}\leq K_{G}^{\mathbb{C}}\|A\|_{L_{\infty}\to L_{1}}\;.

Here we give a somewhat more streamlined proof of this result based on a well-known factorization version of Grothendieck’s inequality

4
4

4

The main difference is that in
[

In our setting the inequality asserts the following

Theorem 3.2

(Commutative Grothendieck inequality (factorization))
.

Let
S
be as above and let

be a linear map. Then, there exist probability measures
\lambda,\nu
on

, we have

\displaystyle|\langle g,Af\rangle|\leq K_{G}^{\mathbb{C}}\|A\|_{L_{\infty}\to L_{1}}\left(\int_{S}|f(s)|^{2}\;d\lambda(s)\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{S}|g(s)|^{2}d\nu(s)\right)^{1/2}.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

It follows from the triangle inequality and transitivity that

\displaystyle|\langle g,Af\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\E_{\pi\in\Gamma}|\langle g,A^{\pi}f\rangle|=\E_{\pi\in\Gamma}|\langle g^{\pi},Af^{\pi}\rangle|.

By Theorem
3.2
and the AM-GM inequality there are probability measures
\lambda,\nu
on
S
such that the above right-hand side is at most

\displaystyle\frac{K_{G}^{\mathbb{C}}\|A\|_{L_{\infty}\to L_{1}}}{2}\E_{\pi\in\Gamma}\left(\int_{S}|f^{\pi}(s)|^{2}d\lambda(s)+\int_{S}|g^{\pi}(s)|^{2}d\nu(s)\right)=\frac{K_{G}^{\mathbb{C}}\|A\|_{L_{\infty}\to L_{1}}}{2}(\|f\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+\|g\|_{L_{2}}^{2}),

where we switched the order of the integrals (using Tonelli’s theorem) and the expression (
5

norm. For

this shows

.
∎

3.2
Non-commutative case

Our main technical result is as follows.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let

As projectors have Schatten-

The second inequality is proved at the end of this section and in

With Lemma

, which relates the uniformity parameter

to

then immediately gives the following result stated in the introduction.

Corollary 3.4

(Converse Quantum Expander Mixing Lemma)
.

Let
\Phi:M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be an irreducibly covariant superoperator. Then,
\lambda(\Phi)\leq 2\pi^{2}\epsilon(\Phi)

In this non-commutative setting we use the following analog of Theorem

A density matrix is a positive semidefinite matrix with trace equal to 1.

Theorem 3.5

(Haagerup)
.

Let

be a superoperator. Then, there exist density matrices

\displaystyle|\langle Y,\Phi(X)\rangle|\leq\|\Phi\|_{S_{\infty}\to S_{1}}\left(\mathrm{Tr}[\rho_{1}X^{*}X]+\mathrm{Tr}[\rho_{2}XX^{*}]\right)^{1/2}\left(\mathrm{Tr}[\sigma_{1}Y^{*}Y]+\mathrm{Tr}[\sigma_{2}YY^{*}]\right)^{1/2}.

(6)

We also use the following lemma.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\Gamma
be a compact group.
Then, a unitary representation
U:\Gamma\to U(n)
is irreducible if and only if for any
X\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, we have

\E_{g\in\Gamma}U(g)XU(g)^{*}=\mathrm{Tr}(X)\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Id}.

Proof.

By Schur’s lemma, if
U
is an irreducible representation, then for

\displaystyle\Big[\forall g\in\Gamma\quad U(g)TU(g)^{*}=T\Big]\iff\Big[\exists\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\quad T=\lambda\>\mathrm{Id}\Big].

Let
T_{X}=\E_{g\in\Gamma}U(g)XU(g)^{*}
, then by the group structure we have
U(g)T_{X}U(g)^{*}=T_{X}
for all
g\in\Gamma
. Therefore, if
U
is irreducible then
T_{X}=\lambda_{X}\>\mathrm{Id}
. By taking the trace, it follows that
\lambda_{X}=\mathrm{Tr}(X)/n
. In the other direction, if
U
is reducible then there exists a projector
P
onto an irreducible subspace that is left invariant, i.e.
U(g)PU(g)^{*}=P
for all

T_{P}\neq\lambda\mathrm{Id}

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Denote by
\Gamma
and

the group and irreducible representations such that
\Phi
is irreducibly covariant with respect to
\Gamma
(see Definition

).
For any

write

and

, then we have

\displaystyle|\langle Y,\Phi(X)\rangle|

\displaystyle=\E_{g\in\Gamma}|\langle Y_{g},\Phi(X_{g})\rangle|.

By Theorem
3.5
and the AM-GM inequality, there exist density matrices
\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
such that the right hand side is bounded from above by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\Phi\|_{S_{\infty}\to S_{1}}\E_{g\in\Gamma}\left(\mathrm{Tr}[\rho_{1}X_{g}^{*}X_{g}]+\mathrm{Tr}[\rho_{2}X_{g}X_{g}^{*}]+\mathrm{Tr}[\sigma_{1}Y_{g}^{*}Y_{g}]+\mathrm{Tr}[\sigma_{2}Y_{g}Y_{g}^{*}]\right).

By Lemma

\E_{g\in\Gamma}X_{g}^{*}X_{g}=\E_{g\in\Gamma}U(g)X^{*}XU^{*}(g)=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Tr}[X^{*}X]\mathrm{Id}=\|X\|_{S_{2}}^{2}\mathrm{Id}
. Let

\E_{g\in\Gamma}\mathrm{Tr}[\rho X_{g}^{*}X_{g}]=\|X\|_{S_{2}}^{2}

The same holds for

but with

\displaystyle\|\Phi\|_{S_{\infty}\to S_{1}}\left(\|X\|_{S_{2}}^{2}+\|Y\|_{S_{2}}^{2}\right).

If

we obtain

.
∎

3.3
Embedding graphs into quantum channels

In this subsection, we elucidate the claim that quantum channels generalize graphs and prove the result stated in the second bullet in the introduction, namely that there are non-irreducible quantum channels for which a converse expander mixing lemma does not hold.

We consider the following embeddings.
For
A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, define
\Phi_{A}:M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
as

\displaystyle\Phi_{A}(X)=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}X_{jj}E_{ii},

(7)

where
E_{ij}
is the matrix with a single
1
at position
(i,j)
.
When
A
is a transition matrix, i.e., its column sums are 1, then it is not hard to see that
\Phi_{A}
is completely positive and trace preserving and that
\Phi_{\frac{1}{n}J}=\Pi
.
Several other ways exist to create quantum expanders from expander graphs, see for example
[
20
]
and
[
17
]
, but as we show below, our embedding given above carries over all relevant properties of the graph we consider here.

Conlon and Zhao
[
9
]
give an infinite sequence of
d
-regular graphs
G_{n}
that are
o(1)
-uniform but for which
\lambda(G_{n})\geq 1/2
.
Combined with the following proposition, this immediately gives the result stated in the second bullet in the introduction.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and
p,q\in[1,\infty]
. Then, for
\Phi_{A}
as in (
7
), we have

\|\Phi_{A}-\Pi\|_{S_{p}\to S_{q}}=\|A-\frac{1}{n}J\|_{L_{p}\to L_{q}}\quad\text{and}\quad\|\Phi_{A}-\Pi\|_{\mathrm{cut}}=\|A-\frac{1}{n}J\|_{\mathrm{cut}}.

Proof.

Let
B=A-\frac{1}{n}J
, then
\Phi_{A}-\Pi=\Phi_{B}
.
By compactness and definition of
\|\cdot\|_{S_{p}\to S_{q}}
we can assume there is an
X\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
such that
\|\Phi_{B}\|_{S_{p}\to S_{q}}=\|\Phi_{B}(X)\|_{S_{q}}/\|X\|_{S_{p}}
.
Write
X=\mathrm{diag}(x)+X_{\mathrm{other}}
where
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
is the diagonal of
X
, and
X_{\mathrm{other}}
are the off-diagonal entries. Note that by definition of
\Phi_{B}
we have
\Phi_{B}(X)=\Phi_{B}(\mathrm{diag}(x))=\mathrm{diag}(Bx)
.
By definition of Schatten norms,
\|\mathrm{diag}(x)\|_{S_{p}}=\|x\|_{L_{p}}
and by Proposition
2.1
we have
\|X\|_{S_{p}}\geq\|x\|_{L_{p}}
.
We have

\displaystyle\|B\|_{L_{p}\to L_{q}}\geq\frac{\|Bx\|_{L_{q}}}{\|x\|_{L_{p}}}\geq\frac{\|\mathrm{diag}(Bx)\|_{S_{q}}}{\|X\|_{S_{p}}}=\frac{\|\Phi_{B}(X)\|_{S_{q}}}{\|X\|_{S_{p}}}=\|\Phi_{B}\|_{S_{p}\to S_{q}}

Now let
y\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
be such that
\|B\|_{L_{p}\to L_{q}}=\|By\|_{L_{q}}/\|y\|_{L_{p}}
. Then

\displaystyle\|\Phi_{B}\|_{S_{p}\to S_{q}}\geq\frac{\|\Phi_{B}(\mathrm{diag}(y))\|_{S_{q}}}{\|\mathrm{diag}(y)\|_{S_{p}}}=\frac{\|\mathrm{diag}(By)\|_{S_{q}}}{\|y\|_{L_{p}}}=\frac{\|By\|_{L_{q}}}{\|y\|_{L_{p}}}=\|B\|_{L_{p}\to L_{q}}.

This proves the first part.

The cut norm of a matrix takes the supremum over
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. Instead we can relax this to
x,y\in[0,1]^{n}
, since by linearity the supremum will always be attained by the extreme points. Similarly, for the superoperator case, we use Equation (
4
). Then, there exist
x,y\in[0,1]^{n}
such that
\|B\|_{\mathrm{cut}}=|\langle Bx,y\rangle|
. We have
\mathrm{diag}(x),\mathrm{diag}(y)\succeq 0
and
\|\mathrm{diag}(x)\|_{S_{\infty}},\|\mathrm{diag}(y)\|_{S_{\infty}}\leq 1
. Therefore

\displaystyle\|\Phi_{B}\|_{\mathrm{cut}}\geq|\langle\mathrm{diag}(y),\Phi_{B}(\mathrm{diag}(x))\rangle|=|\langle\mathrm{diag}(y),\mathrm{diag}(Bx)\rangle|=|\langle y,Bx\rangle|=\|B\|_{\mathrm{cut}}.

In the other direction, let
X,Y\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
such that
X,Y\succeq 0
and
\|X\|_{S_{\infty}},\|Y\|_{S_{\infty}}\leq 1
. Define
x,y
to be the diagonals of
X,Y
, i.e.
x_{i}=X_{ii}
and
y_{i}=Y_{ii}
. By Proposition
2.1
we have
\|x\|_{L_{\infty}},\|y\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq 1
. Since
X,Y\succeq 0
we know all diagonal entries of
X
and
Y
are real and non-negative, so we have
x,y\in[0,1]^{n}
. We conclude

\displaystyle\|B\|_{\mathrm{cut}}\geq|\langle y,Bx\rangle|=|\langle\mathrm{diag}(y),\mathrm{diag}(Bx)\rangle|=|\langle Y,\Phi_{B}(X)\rangle|=\|\Phi_{B}\|_{\mathrm{cut}},

completing the proof.
∎

Note that
\|A-\frac{1}{n}J\|_{L_{2}\to L_{2}}
is the second largest eigenvalue in absolute value of the matrix
A
, so spectral expansion is preserved under the embedding of graphs into quantum channels. Also, uniformity is preserved since the cut-norm does not change.

The following proposition shows that the embedding (
7
) preserves transitivity.
This shows that our Theorem
3.3
generalizes the main result of
[
9
]
, albeit with a slightly worse constant.

Proposition 3.8
.

For any
A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
,
A
is vertex transitive if and only if
\Phi_{A}
is irreducibly covariant.

Proof.

Suppose
A
is vertex transitive.
Let
\pi\in\mathrm{Aut}(A)
be a permutation and
P_{\pi}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be the associated permutation matrix, so that
P_{\pi}AP_{\pi}^{*}=A
.
Then,

\displaystyle\Phi_{A}(P_{\pi}XP_{\pi}^{*})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}(P_{\pi}XP_{\pi}^{*})_{jj}E_{ii}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}X_{\pi^{-1}(j)\pi^{-1}(j)}E_{ii}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}A_{i\pi(j)}X_{jj}E_{ii}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}A_{\pi(i)\pi(j)}X_{jj}E_{\pi(i)\pi(i)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}A_{\pi(i)\pi(j)}X_{jj}(P_{\pi}E_{ii}P_{\pi}^{*})=P_{\pi}\Phi_{A}(X)P_{\pi}^{*}.

This shows that for all
\pi\in\mathrm{Aut}(A)
we have
\Phi_{A}(P_{\pi}XP_{\pi}^{*})=P_{\pi}\Phi_{A}(X)P_{\pi}^{*}
.

Let
\mathbb{T}=\{c\in\mathbb{C}:\,|c|=1\}
be the complex unit circle.
For
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
, define
U_{\alpha}:=\mathrm{diag}(\alpha)
.
We have
U_{\alpha}E_{ii}U_{\alpha}^{*}=|\alpha_{i}|^{2}E_{ii}=E_{ii}
and
(U_{\alpha}XU_{\alpha}^{*})_{ii}=|\alpha_{i}|^{2}X_{ii}=X_{ii}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\Phi_{A}(U_{\alpha}XU_{\alpha}^{*})=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}(U_{\alpha}XU_{\alpha}^{*})_{jj}E_{ii}=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}X_{jj}U_{\alpha}E_{ii}U_{\alpha}^{*}=U_{\alpha}\Phi_{A}(X)U_{\alpha}^{*}.

We combine these two observations as follows. First we have that

\displaystyle\left(\E_{\alpha\in\mathbb{T}^{n}}U_{\alpha}XU_{\alpha}^{*}\right)_{ij}=\E_{\alpha\in\mathbb{T}^{n}}\alpha_{i}X_{ij}\overline{\alpha_{j}}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{i\theta_{i}}X_{ij}e^{-i\theta_{j}}\;d\theta_{i}d\theta_{j}=X_{ii}\delta_{ij}

If
A
is vertex transitive then for all
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
we have
\E_{\pi\in\mathrm{Aut}(A)}P_{\pi}\,\mathrm{diag}(x)\,P_{\pi}^{*}=(\E_{i}x_{i})\;\mathrm{Id}
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in\mathrm{Aut}(A)\\
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}^{n}\end{subarray}}(P_{\pi}U_{\alpha})X(P_{\pi}U_{\alpha})^{*}=\E_{\pi\in\mathrm{Aut}(A)}P_{\pi}\left(\E_{\alpha\in\mathbb{T}^{n}}U_{\alpha}XU_{\alpha}^{*}\right)P_{\pi}^{*}=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(X)}{n}\mathrm{Id}.

Letting
G\subset M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be the subgroup generated by the
U_{\alpha}
and
P_{\pi}
for
\pi\in\mathrm{Aut}(A)
, we see that for any
g\in G

\Phi_{A}(gXg^{*})=g\Phi_{A}(X)g^{*}

and by the previous equation and Lemma
3.6
,
G
acts irreducibly on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
(and it is unitary).
This proves
\Phi
is irreducibly covariant with respect to the group
G
with equal representations.

For the other direction, let
U:G\to U(n)
be the irreducible representation such that
\Phi_{A}
is irreducibly covariant, i.e.
\Phi_{A}(U(g)XU^{*}(g))=U(g)\Phi_{A}(X)U^{*}(g)
for all
g\in G
. Define
P_{g}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
as
(P_{g})_{ij}=|U(g)_{ij}|^{2}
so that
(U(g)E_{jj}U(g)^{*})_{ii}=(P_{g})_{ij}
.
Then

\displaystyle A_{kl}=\mathrm{Tr}[E_{kk}\Phi_{A}(E_{ll})]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}[U(g)E_{kk}U(g)^{*}\;\Phi_{A}(U(g)E_{ll}U(g)^{*})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}A_{ij}(P_{g})_{jl}(P_{g})_{ik}=(P_{g}^{T}AP_{g})_{kl},

showing
P_{g}^{T}AP_{g}=A
. Since
U(g)
is unitary,
P_{g}
is doubly stochastic so by Birkhoff’s Theorem
P_{g}
is a convex combination of permutation matrices, i.e.,
P_{g}=\E_{i}\Pi_{i}
for some (not necessarily uniform) probability distribution and where
\Pi_{i}
is a permutation matrix. We have

\displaystyle A_{kl}=(P_{g}^{T}AP_{g})_{kl}=\E_{i}\E_{j}(\Pi_{i}^{T}A\Pi_{j})_{kl}=\E_{i}\E_{j}A_{\pi_{i}(k)\>\pi_{j}(l)}.

Since
A
is
\{0,1\}
-valued, it follows that if
A_{kl}=1
then all elements of the convex combination on the right-hand side must be
1
, and if
A_{kl}=0
then all elements of the right hand side must be 0. Therefore, for all
i
we have
\Pi_{i}^{T}A\Pi_{i}=A
.
By irreducibility, we have for all
k,l
that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}[E_{kk}]}{n}\mathrm{Id}_{ll}

\displaystyle=\left(\E_{g\in G}U(g)E_{kk}U^{*}(g)\right)_{ll}=\E_{g\in G}\left|U(g)_{lk}\right|^{2},

showing
\E_{g\in G}(P_{g})_{lk}=1/n
. It follows that there is a
g\in G
such that
(P_{g})_{lk}>0
. Decomposing
P_{g}
into permutation matrices shows there is a
\Pi\in\mathrm{Aut}(A)
such that
\Pi_{lk}=1
. This holds for all
k,l
, proving the lemma.

3.4
Randomizing superoperators

We prove the following analogue of one of the results from
[
8
]
showing that for any
d
-regular
graph
G
, it holds that
\lambda(G)\leq\left(2\epsilon(G)/\delta^{2}\right)^{1/4}
, where
\delta=d/n
is the edge density.
This in particular establishes a tight relation between spectral expansion and uniformity for sequences of graphs with
\delta_{n}\geq\Omega(1)
.
For
A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, we have
\|A\|_{L_{1}\to L_{\infty}}=n\sup_{ij}|A_{ij}|
, and for an
n
-vertex
d
-regular graph with normalized adjacency matrix
A
we have
\sup_{ij}|A_{ij}|=\frac{1}{d}
so
\|A-J/n\|_{L_{1}\to L_{\infty}}=\frac{1}{\delta}-1
with
J
being the all-ones matrix. Therefore, a sequence of graphs with normalized adjacency matrices
A_{n}
is dense exactly when
\|A_{n}-J_{n}/n\|_{L_{1}\to L_{\infty}}\leq\mathcal{O}(1)
, where
J_{n}
is the all-ones
n
by
n
matrix .

Let
\Pi
be the projector onto the identity matrix. A superoperator
\Phi
is said to be
\eta
-
randomizing
if
\|\Phi-\Pi\|_{S_{1}\to S_{\infty}}\leq\eta
, which when
\eta\leq\mathcal{O}(1)
, may thus be seen as an analogue of density.
Note that by
Proposition

3.7
the embedding of any dense graph is
\mathcal{O}(1)
-randomizing.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
\Phi:M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be a superoperator that is
\mathcal{O}(1)
-randomizing.
Then,
\lambda(\Phi)\leq\mathcal{O}(\epsilon(\Phi)^{1/4})
.

To prove Proposition
3.9
, we require the following lemma.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\Phi:M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be a superoperator and let
C=\|\Phi\|_{S_{1}\to S_{\infty}}
.
Then we have
\|\Phi\|_{S_{2}\to S_{2}}\leq\Big(C^{3}\|\Phi\|_{S_{\infty}\to S_{1}}\Big)^{1/4}
.

Proof.

Note that by definition of
C
we have
|\langle Q,\Phi(P)\rangle|\leq C\|Q\|_{S_{1}}\|P\|_{S_{1}}
.
Let
X,Y\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be such that
\langle Y,\Phi(X)\rangle=\|\Phi\|_{S_{2}\to S_{2}}
with
\|X\|_{S_{2}}=\|Y\|_{S_{2}}=1
. Write
X=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}P_{i}
and
Y=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}Q_{i}
with
P_{i},Q_{i}
rank-1 matrices with
\|Q_{i}\|_{S_{1}}=\|P_{i}\|_{S_{1}}=1
. We have
\|\lambda\|_{L_{2}}=\|\mu\|_{L_{2}}=1
and by applying Cauchy-Schwarz twice,

\displaystyle|\langle Y,\Phi(X)\rangle|^{4}

\displaystyle=\Big|\E_{ij}\lambda_{i}\mu_{j}\langle Q_{j},\Phi(P_{i})\rangle\Big|^{4}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\E_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}\Big)^{2}\;\Big(\E_{i}\big|\E_{j}\mu_{j}\langle Q_{j},\Phi(P_{i})\rangle\big|^{2}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big(\E_{i,j,j^{\prime}}\mu_{j}\mu_{j^{\prime}}\langle Q_{j},\Phi(P_{i})\rangle\langle P_{i},\Phi^{*}(Q_{j^{\prime}})\rangle\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\E_{j,j^{\prime}}\mu_{j}^{2}\mu_{j^{\prime}}^{2}\Big)\Big(\E_{j,j^{\prime}}\Big|\E_{i}\langle Q_{j},\Phi(P_{i})\rangle\langle P_{i},\Phi^{*}(Q_{j^{\prime}})\rangle\Big|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\E_{i,i^{\prime},j,j^{\prime}}\langle Q_{j},\Phi(P_{i})\rangle\langle P_{i},\Phi^{*}(Q_{j^{\prime}})\rangle\langle Q_{j^{\prime}},\Phi(P_{i^{\prime}})\rangle\langle P_{i^{\prime}},\Phi^{*}(Q_{j})\rangle,

where all indices are averaged from
1
to
n
. Now we see

\displaystyle|\langle Y,\Phi(X)\rangle|^{4}

\displaystyle\leq\E_{i,j}\langle Q_{j},\Phi(P_{i})\rangle\Big\langle\E_{j^{\prime}}\langle Q_{j^{\prime}},\Phi(P_{i})\rangle Q_{j^{\prime}},\Phi\big(\E_{i^{\prime}}\langle P_{i^{\prime}},\Phi^{*}(Q_{j})\rangle P_{i^{\prime}}\big)\Big\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\E_{i,j}|\langle Q_{j},\Phi(P_{i})\rangle|\;\|\Phi\|_{S_{\infty}\to S_{1}}\;\|\E_{j^{\prime}}\langle Q_{j^{\prime}},\Phi(P_{i})\rangle Q_{j^{\prime}}\|_{S_{\infty}}\;\|\E_{i^{\prime}}\langle P_{i^{\prime}},\Phi^{*}(Q_{j})\rangle P_{i^{\prime}}\|_{S_{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\E_{i,j}|\langle Q_{j},\Phi(P_{i})\rangle|\;\|\Phi\|_{S_{\infty}\to S_{1}}\;\max_{j^{\prime}}|\langle Q_{j^{\prime}},\Phi(P_{i})\rangle|\;\max_{i^{\prime}}|\langle Q_{j},\Phi(P_{i^{\prime}})\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq C^{3}\|\Phi\|_{S_{\infty}\to S_{1}}.

Proof of
Proposition

3.9
.

Let
\Pi(X)=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Tr}[X]\mathrm{Id}
be the projector on to the identity. By assumption, we have
\|\Phi-\Pi\|_{\mathrm{cut}}=\epsilon(\Phi)
. Define
C=\|\Phi-\Pi\|_{S_{1}\to S_{\infty}}
. Using Lemma
2.2
and Lemma
3.10
applied to
\Phi-\Pi
we find
\|\Phi-\Pi\|_{S_{2}\to S_{2}}\leq(C^{3}\pi^{2}\epsilon(\Phi))^{1/4}
.
∎

4
Optimality of constants

4.1
Commutative case

In this section we prove the fourth bullet point in our introduction.
Theorem
3.1
shows that
K_{G}^{\mathbb{C}}
bounds the ratio of the
L_{2}\to L_{2}
and
L_{\infty}\to L_{1}
norms, and Lemma
2.2
(the matrix version) shows that
\pi^{2}
bounds the ratio of the
L_{\infty}\to L_{1}
norm and the cut norm. We now prove the optimality of the combined inequality.

Let
S^{m-1}=\{x\in\mathbb{C}^{m}:\,\|x\|_{L_{2}}=1\}
denote the
(m-1)
-dimensional unit sphere endowed with its Haar probability measure
\mu
.

Theorem 4.1
.

For any
\epsilon>0
there exist positive integers
m,k
and a transitive covariant linear map
M:C(S^{m-1}\times[k])\to C(S^{m-1}\times[k])
such that
\|M\|_{L_{2}\to L_{2}}\geq(\pi^{2}K_{G}^{\mathbb{C}}-\epsilon)\|M\|_{\mathrm{cut}}
.

The optimality of
\pi^{2}
between the
L_{\infty}\to L_{1}
norm and the cut norm is already covered in Lemma
2.2
. We show that
K_{G}^{\mathbb{C}}
is optimal in the sense that Theorem
3.1
cannot be improved (despite the fact that the exact value of the Grothendieck constant
K_{G}^{\mathbb{C}}
is unknown). We do this in Lemma
4.2
below. Then in
Lemma

4.3
we show that any map can be lifted to one on a bigger space with appropriately bounded cut norm. The combination of these lemmas proves our theorem.

In the introduction we also mentioned the optimal constant
4K_{G}
in the case where the field is
\mathbb{R}
instead of
\mathbb{C}
. The proofs below still apply in this case, with only small modifications.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
\epsilon>0
there exists a positive integer
m
and a transitive covariant linear map
B:C(S^{m-1})\to C(S^{m-1})
such that
\|B\|_{L_{2}\to L_{2}}\geq(K_{G}^{\mathbb{C}}-\epsilon)\|B\|_{L_{\infty}\to L_{1}}
.

Proof.

By definition of the Grothendieck constant, for any
\epsilon>0
there exists an
n\in\mathbb{N}
and a linear map
A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
such that
\|A\|_{\mathrm{G}}\geq(K_{G}^{\mathbb{C}}-\epsilon)\|A\|_{L_{\infty}\to L_{1}}
.
This map
A
might not be transitive covariant, so from it we will now construct a transitive covariant linear map
B:C(S^{2n-1})\to C(S^{2n-1})
such that
\|B\|_{L_{\infty}\to L_{1}}\leq\|A\|_{L_{\infty}\to L_{1}}
and
\|B\|_{L_{2}\to L_{2}}\geq\|A\|_{\mathrm{G}}
.
This idea is based on a lemma found in
[
6
]
.

Let
x^{i},y^{j}\in S^{2n-1}
be the vectors that attain the Grothendieck norm for
A
, which can always be assumed to be
2n
-dimensional since there are only
2n
of them, so

\displaystyle\|A\|_{G}=\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}\langle x^{i},y^{j}\rangle\Big|.

Define the map
B
by

\displaystyle\langle f,B(g)\rangle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}\int_{U(2n)}f(Ux^{i})g(Uy^{j})dU.

To bound
\|B\|_{L_{\infty}\to L_{1}}
we have to bound
|\langle f,B(g)\rangle|
for
f,g:S^{2n-1}\to[-1,1]
.
By the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle|\langle f,B(g)\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{U(2n)}\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}f(Ux^{i})g(Uy^{j})\Big|dU\leq\int_{U(2n)}\|A\|_{L_{\infty}\to L_{1}}dU\leq\|A\|_{L_{\infty}\to L_{1}}.

Now for each
i\in[2n]
let
f_{i}\in C(S^{2n-1})
be given by
f_{i}(x)=x_{i}
(i.e. the
i
-th coordinate). Then,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\langle f_{i},B(f_{i})\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\|B\|_{L_{2}\to L_{2}}\|f_{i}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|B\|_{L_{2}\to L_{2}}\int_{S^{2n-1}}\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}x_{i}^{2}d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\|B\|_{L_{2}\to L_{2}}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\langle f_{i},B(f_{i})\rangle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}\int_{U(2n)}\langle Ux^{i},Uy^{j}\rangle dU=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}\langle x^{i},y^{j}\rangle=\|A\|_{G},

so we conclude
\|B\|_{L_{2}\to L_{2}}\geq\|A\|_{G}
.
We will show
B
is transitive covariant with respect to
\Gamma=U(2n)
. To show
B
is invariant, we have to prove that for all
V\in U(2n)
we have
\langle f^{V},B(g^{V})\rangle=\langle f,B(g)\rangle
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\langle f^{V},B(g^{V})\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}\int_{U(2n)}f(VUx^{i})g(VUy^{j})dU

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}\int_{U(2n)}f(U^{\prime}x^{i})g(U^{\prime}y^{j})dU^{\prime}=\langle f,B(g)\rangle,

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
S
be any compact set and let
B:C(S)\to C(S)
be a linear map. For any
\epsilon>0
there exists a
k\in\mathbb{N}
and a linear map
M:C(S\times[k])\to C(S\times[k])
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\|M\|_{\mathrm{cut}}}{\|M\|_{L_{2}\to L_{2}}}\leq\big(\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}+\epsilon\big)\frac{\|B\|_{L_{\infty}\to L_{1}}}{\|B\|_{L_{2}\to L_{2}}}

and if
B
is transitive covariant then so is
M
.

Proof.

We will choose
k
large enough, to be determined later.
For any
f,g\in C(S\times[k])
define
f^{i}\in C(S)
as
f^{i}(s):=f(s,i)
, and similar for
g^{i}
.
Define
\omega=e^{2\pi i/k}
.
Define a linear map
M:C(S\times[k])\to C(S\times[k])
as

\displaystyle\big(M(f)\big)(t,j):=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\omega^{i-j}B(f^{i})(t),\quad\text{for }t\in S\text{ and }j\in[k].

We then have

\displaystyle\langle g,M(f)\rangle_{S\times[k]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\Big\langle\sum_{i}\omega^{i}g^{i},B\big(\sum_{j}\omega^{j}f^{j}\big)\Big\rangle_{S}

where one factor of
\frac{1}{k}
comes from our normalization of the inner product. This implies

\displaystyle\big|\langle g,M(f)\rangle_{S\times[k]}\big|

\displaystyle\leq\|B\|_{L_{\infty}\to L_{1}}\Big\|\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\omega^{i}g^{i}\Big\|_{L_{\infty}}\Big\|\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\omega^{j}f^{j}\Big\|_{L_{\infty}}.

(8)

If
f,g\in C(S\times[k])
are the
[0,1]
-valued functions that attain the cut norm of
M
, then by (
8
)

\displaystyle\|M\|_{\mathrm{cut}}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}+\epsilon\Big)\|B\|_{L_{\infty}\to L_{1}},

where we used
Lemma

4.4
to bound
\Big\|\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\omega^{i}g^{i}\Big\|_{L_{\infty}}
.

Let
u,v\in C(S)
with
\|u\|_{L_{2}}=\|v\|_{L_{2}}=1
be such that
\|B\|_{L_{2}\to L_{2}}=\langle v,B(u)\rangle_{S}
.
Now define
f_{(u)},g_{(v)}\in C(S\times[k])
as
f_{(u)}(s,i):=\omega^{-i}u(s)
and
g_{(v)}(s,i):=\omega^{-i}v(s)
, which also have
L_{2}
-norm equal to 1. We then see

\displaystyle\|M\|_{L_{2}\to L_{2}}\geq\big\langle g_{(v)},M(f_{(u)})\big\rangle_{S\times[k]}=\langle v,B(u)\rangle_{S}=\|B\|_{L_{2}\to L_{2}}.

The combination of these observations completes the first part of the proof.
Now assume
B
is transitive covariant with respect to
\Gamma
, so
B(f^{\pi})(\pi^{-1}(s))=B(f)(s)
for all
s\in S
and
\pi\in\Gamma
. Define a new group
\Gamma^{\prime}
as the cartesian product
\Gamma^{\prime}=\Gamma\times\mathbb{Z}_{k}
. For
(\pi,m)\in\Gamma^{\prime}
define the action
(\pi,m):S\times[k]\to S\times[k]
as
(\pi,m)(s,i)=(\pi(s),i+m)
. By entering
f^{(\pi,m)}
into the definition of
M
it follows that
M^{(\pi,m)}=M
, so
M
is transitive covariant with respect to
\Gamma^{\prime}
, completing the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\epsilon>0
, then there exists a
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
for all
k\geq k_{0}
and
x\in[0,1]^{k}
we have

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{2\pi i\,j/k}x_{j}\Big|\leq\frac{1}{\pi}+\epsilon.

Proof.

First let
k_{0}
be arbitrary, to be determined later and
k\geq k_{0}
.
Define
y\in[-1,1]^{k}
as
y_{i}=2x_{i}-1
, then

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{2\pi i\,j/k}x_{j}\Big|=\frac{1}{2}\Big|\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{2\pi i\,j/k}y_{j}\Big|=\frac{1}{2}e^{2\pi i\phi}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{2\pi i\,j/k}y_{j}.

In the first equality we used that
\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{2\pi i\,j/k}=0
.
In the second equality we used that there exists a
\phi
such that the full expression becomes real and positive. Since
e^{i\theta}=\cos(\theta)+i\sin(\theta)
and the full expression is real, we know the
\sin
component vanishes and therefore

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{2\pi i(\phi+j/k)}y_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\cos(2\pi(\phi+j/k))y_{j}.

Now note that
\cos(2\pi(\phi+j/k))y_{j}\leq\big|\cos(2\pi(\phi+j/k))\big|
and hence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big|\cos(2\pi(\phi+j/k))\big|\;\;\overset{k\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\;\;\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\big|\cos\big(2\pi(\phi+x)\big)\big|dx=\frac{1}{\pi}.

This completes the proof.
∎

4.2
Non-commutative case

In the non-commutative case we show optimality of
Theorem

3.3
.
By
Lemma

2.2
, the factor
\pi^{2}
between the cut-norm and
S_{\infty}\to S_{1}
-norm is also optimal.
In contrast with the commutative case, our work leaves the optimality of the combined inequality in
Corollary

3.4
as an open problem.
Straightforward analogues of the techniques employed in
Lemma

4.3
did not follow through in the non-commutative case.

Proposition 4.5
.

For any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a positive integer
n
and an irreducibly covariant superoperator
\Phi:M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
such that
\|\Phi\|_{S_{2}\to S_{2}}\geq(2-\epsilon)\|\Phi\|_{S_{\infty}\to S_{1}}
.

One of the forms of the non-commutative Grothendieck inequality, equivalent to Theorem
3.5
, is the following
[
29
]
. Let
\Phi:M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be a linear map and
x_{i},y_{j}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
finite sets of matrices. Then,

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{i}\langle x_{i},\Phi(y_{i})\rangle\Big|\leq K_{G}^{\prime}\|\Phi\|_{S_{\infty}\to S_{1}}\left(\frac{\|\sum_{i}x_{i}^{*}x_{i}\|+\|\sum_{i}x_{i}x_{i}^{*}\|}{2}\!\cdot\!\frac{\|\sum_{i}y_{i}^{*}y_{i}\|+\|\sum_{i}y_{i}y_{i}^{*}\|}{2}\right)^{1/2}

(9)

where
K_{G}^{\prime}\leq 2
and the norms on the right hand side are operator norms
\|\cdot\|_{S_{\infty}}
.
To show tightness, i.e.
K_{G}^{\prime}\geq 2
, Haagerup and Itoh
[
16
]
(see
[
29
]
for a survey) gave an explicit family of operators for which (
9
) gives a lower bound of
K_{G}^{\prime}
approaching
2
.
We will show that slight modifications of these operators are irreducibly covariant, which proves Proposition
4.5
.
It is instructive to repeat their construction.
The proof uses techniques familiar in the context of the antisymmetric Fock space, but our proof is self contained.

Lemma 4.6

(
[
16
]
)
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a
d\in\mathbb{N}
and a linear map
\Phi:M_{d}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
with sets of matrices
\{x_{i}\}
,
\{y_{i}\}
such that (
9
) yields
K_{G}^{\prime}\geq(2n+1)/(n+1)
.

Proof.

Let
H~=~\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}
and consider the antisymmetric
k
-fold tensor product
H^{\wedge k}
which is a linear subspace of the
k
-fold tensor product
H^{\otimes k}
.
A basis of
H^{\wedge k}
is formed by vectors
e_{i_{1}}\wedge e_{i_{2}}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{i_{k}}
with
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}
where the
e_{i}
are standard basis vectors of
H
. Here
\wedge
is the wedge product or exterior product, which has the property
x\wedge y=-y\wedge x
and is given by
x\wedge y=x\otimes y-y\otimes x
, for
x,y\in H
.
We will consider
k=n
and
k=n+1
so that the dimension of
H^{\wedge k}
is
d=\binom{2n+1}{n}
for both
k=n
and
k=n+1
.

For
1\leq i\leq(2n+1)
, define
c_{i}:H^{\wedge n}\to H^{\wedge(n+1)}
as
c_{i}(x):=e_{i}\wedge x
, which physicists call the fermionic creation operator. Its adjoint
c_{i}^{*}:H^{\wedge(n+1)}\to H^{\wedge n}
is known as the annihilation operator. By the antisymmetric property,
c_{i}(x)=0
whenever
e_{i}
was present in
x
, i.e., when
x=e_{i}\wedge x^{\prime}
. The operator
c_{i}c_{i}^{*}
, also known as the number operator, is a projector onto the space spanned by basis vectors in which
e_{i}
is present. The operator
c_{i}^{*}c_{i}
is a projector onto the space where
e_{i}
is
not
present. Since there are always
(n+1)
vectors present in
H^{\wedge(n+1)}
and
(n+1)
vectors
not
present in
H^{\wedge n}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2n+1}c_{i}c_{i}^{*}=(n+1)\mathrm{Id}_{H^{\wedge(n+1)}}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{2n+1}c_{i}^{*}c_{i}=(n+1)\mathrm{Id}_{H^{\wedge n}}.

We will now argue that

\displaystyle\langle c_{i},c_{j}\rangle:=\frac{1}{d}\Tr(c_{i}^{*}c_{j})

\displaystyle=\delta_{i,j}\frac{n+1}{2n+1},

(10)

\displaystyle\|\sum_{i=1}^{2n+1}\alpha_{i}c_{i}\|_{S_{1}}

\displaystyle=\|\alpha\|_{L_{2}}\frac{n+1}{\sqrt{2n+1}}\qquad\text{for }\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}.

(11)

The
\delta_{i,j}
in (
10
) follows because
\langle x,c_{i}^{*}c_{j}x\rangle=0
for any
x=e_{k_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{k_{n}}
when
i\neq j
.
The factor
\frac{n+1}{2n+1}
follows by taking the trace of one of the sums above and noting that by symmetry in
i
, every term of the sum must have the same trace.
To prove (
11
), first note that for any unitary
U\in\mathrm{U}(2n+1)
we have

\displaystyle U^{\otimes(n+1)}\cdot c_{i}\cdot(U^{\otimes n})^{-1}=\sum_{j}U_{ji}c_{j},

(12)

which can be shown by proving it for all basis states:

\displaystyle U^{\otimes(n+1)}c_{i}(U^{\otimes n})^{-1}(e_{k_{1}}\wedge...\wedge e_{k_{n}})

\displaystyle=U^{\otimes(n+1)}c_{i}(U^{-1}e_{k_{1}}\wedge...\wedge U^{-1}e_{k_{n}})

\displaystyle=U^{\otimes(n+1)}(e_{i}\wedge U^{-1}e_{k_{1}}\wedge...\wedge U^{-1}e_{k_{n}})

\displaystyle=(Ue_{i}\wedge e_{k_{1}}\wedge...\wedge e_{k_{n}})

\displaystyle=(\sum_{j}U_{ji}e_{j}\wedge e_{k_{1}}\wedge...\wedge e_{k_{n}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}U_{ji}c_{j}(e_{k_{1}}\wedge...\wedge e_{k_{n}}).

The trace-norm is unitarily invariant, so (
12
) implies
\|c_{i}\|_{S_{1}}=\|\sum_{j}U_{ji}c_{j}\|_{S_{1}}
. Since
c_{i}^{*}c_{i}
is a projector, we have
\sqrt{c_{i}^{*}c_{i}}=c_{i}^{*}c_{i}
and hence
\|c_{i}\|_{S_{1}}=\frac{1}{d}\Tr(c_{i}^{*}c_{i})
. Now let
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}
with
\sum_{i}|\alpha_{i}|^{2}=1
, then there is a unitary
U\in\mathrm{U}(2n+1)
such that the
i
-th row of
U
is
\alpha
. Note that
\|\alpha\|_{L_{2}}=1/\sqrt{2n+1}
since we use normalized
L_{2}
-norms, which implies (
11
).

Since the dimensions of
H^{\wedge n}
and
H^{\wedge(n+1)}
are equal, we can identify the space of linear maps
L(H^{\wedge n},H^{\wedge(n+1)})
with
M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
(by choosing bases for
H^{\wedge n}
and
H^{\wedge(n+1)}
), and define the following operator
\Phi:M_{d}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
,

\displaystyle\Phi(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{2n+1}\langle c_{i},x\rangle\,c_{i}.

Consider (
9
) for
\Phi
with
x_{i}=y_{i}=c_{i}
.
For the left hand side, note that by (
10
) we have

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{j=1}^{2n+1}\langle c_{j},\Phi(c_{j})\rangle\Big|=\Big|\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n+1}\langle c_{i},c_{j}\rangle\,\langle c_{j},c_{i}\rangle\Big|=\frac{(n+1)^{2}}{2n+1}.

For the right-hand side of (
9
), we require
\|\Phi\|_{S_{\infty}\to S_{1}}=\sup_{\|x\|_{S_{\infty}}=1}\|\Phi(x)\|_{S_{1}}
.
For any
x\in M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
, define
v^{(x)}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}
as
v^{(x)}_{i}=\langle c_{i},x\rangle
.
Note that
\|v\|_{L_{2}}=\sup_{\|\alpha\|_{L_{2}}=1}|\langle v,\alpha\rangle|
.
First apply (
11
) to obtain

\displaystyle\|\Phi(x)\|_{S_{1}}=\|\sum_{i=1}^{2n+1}\langle c_{i},x\rangle c_{i}\|_{S_{1}}=\|v^{(x)}\|_{L_{2}}\frac{n+1}{\sqrt{2n+1}}=\sup_{\|\alpha\|_{L_{2}}=1}|\langle v^{(x)},\alpha\rangle|\frac{n+1}{\sqrt{2n+1}}.

Using (
11
) again, we compute
\sup_{\|x\|_{S_{\infty}}=1}|\langle v^{(x)},\alpha\rangle|
for arbitrary
\alpha
with
\|\alpha\|_{L_{2}}=1
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\|x\|_{S_{\infty}}=1}|\langle v^{(x)},\alpha\rangle|=\sup_{\|x\|_{S_{\infty}}=1}\frac{1}{2n+1}\big|\langle x,\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}c_{i}\rangle\big|=\frac{1}{2n+1}\|\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}c_{i}\|_{S_{1}}=\frac{n+1}{(2n+1)\sqrt{2n+1}}.

We obtain
\|\Phi\|_{S_{\infty}\to S_{1}}=(n+1)^{2}/(2n+1)^{2}
. Now (
9
) yields
\frac{(n+1)^{2}}{2n+1}\leq K_{G}^{\prime}\frac{(n+1)^{2}}{(2n+1)^{2}}\cdot(n+1)
and therefore
\frac{2n+1}{n+1}\leq K_{G}^{\prime}
.
∎

We use the following fact from
[
12
, Theorem 19.14]
, about the representations of the odd dimensional complex special orthogonal groups on wedge products of
complex
vector spaces.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
,
N:=2n+1
and let
R_{k}:\mathrm{SO}(N,\mathbb{C})\to\mathrm{GL}((\mathbb{C}^{N})^{\wedge k})
be given by
A\mapsto A^{\otimes k}
. This representation is irreducible.

Below, we actually need that the
real
special orthogonal group
\mathrm{SO}(N,\mathbb{R})
acts irreducibly on the same anti-symmetric space.
Fortunately, this is implied by Lemma
4.7
; see
[
12
, pp. 439]
.
We will also use the fact that
R_{k}
and
R_{N-k}
are
unitarily
equivalent to each other.
This is the content of the following proposition
[
32
, Proposition IX.10.4]
.

Proposition 4.8
.

For positive integer
n
and
N=2n+1
and
k\in\{1,\dots,N\}
,
let
R_{k}
be the representation as in lemma
4.7
.
Then, there exists an isometry
V_{k}\colon(\mathbb{C}^{N})^{\wedge k}\to(\mathbb{C}^{N})^{\wedge(N-k)}
such that

\displaystyle V_{k}R_{k}(A)=R_{N-k}(A)V_{k},\quad\forall A\in\mathrm{SO}(N,\mathbb{R}).

Proof of Proposition
4.5
.

Let
d
be the dimension of
(\mathbb{C}^{N})^{\wedge n}
and let
\Phi:M_{d}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
be as in the proof of Lemma
4.6
.
For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
R_{k}:\mathrm{SO}(N,\mathbb{R})\to\mathrm{GL}(H^{\wedge k})
be the representation
A\mapsto A^{\otimes k}
, which is irreducible by
Lemma

4.7
. Define, for notational convenience,
\pi:=R_{n+1}
and
\rho:=R_{n}
.
We first show that for all
A\in\mathrm{SO}(N,\mathbb{R})
, we have

\displaystyle\Phi(\pi(A)x\rho^{*}(A))=\pi(A)\;\Phi(x)\;\rho^{*}(A).

(13)

For the left-hand side, note that

\displaystyle\Phi(\pi(A)x\rho^{*}(A))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\big\langle c_{i},\pi(A)x\rho^{*}(A)\big\rangle\,c_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\big\langle\pi(A)^{*}c_{i}\rho(A),x\rangle\,c_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\Big\langle\sum_{j}A_{ij}c_{j},x\Big\rangle\,c_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}A_{ij}\langle c_{j},x\rangle\,c_{i},

where we used (
12
) from the proof of Lemma
4.6
and noting that
\mathrm{SO}(N,\mathbb{R})\subset\mathrm{U}(N)
is a subgroup.
Using (
12
) again for the right-hand side, we have

\displaystyle\pi(A)\;\Phi(x)\;\rho^{*}(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\langle c_{i},x\rangle\,\pi(A)c_{i}\rho^{*}(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\langle c_{i},x\rangle\,\sum_{j}A_{ji}c_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}A_{ij}\langle c_{j},x\rangle\,c_{i}.

which proves (
13
).

Define a new superoperator
\Phi^{\prime}\colon M_{d}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
by

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}(x)=\Phi(xV^{*})V,

where
V:=V_{n+1}
is the isometry as in Proposition
4.8
(we view
