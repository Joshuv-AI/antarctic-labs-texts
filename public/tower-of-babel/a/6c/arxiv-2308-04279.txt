Perturbation Field in The Presence of Uniform Mean Flow: Doppler Effect for Flows and Acoustics
Tapan K. Sengupta, Aditi Sengupta, Bhavna Joshi, Prasannabalaji Sundaram

Abstract
Having developed the perturbation equation for a dissipative quiescent medium for planar propagation using the linearized compressible Navier-Stokes equation without the Stokes’ hypothesis Sengupta, Sengupta, and Joshi 2023 , here the same is extended where a uniform mean flow is present in the ambiance to explore the propagation properties for the Doppler effect.



keywords:
fluid flow; acoustic field; dissipative medium; Doppler effect

I
Introduction

For problems of sound propagation, the use of the compressible Navier-Stokes equation is mandatory, with the disturbance treated as a small perturbation following the conservation of mass and momentum. In a flow with constant velocity, homogeneous medium, one can consider the equilibrium state with the following ansatz: disturbance velocity
\vec{V}^{\prime}
and disturbance density
\rho^{\prime}
develop with the constant mean flow (
\vec{\bar{V}}=V_{o}\hat{i}
) and a steady state for the unperturbed density prevails i.e.,
\left(\frac{\partial\bar{\rho}}{\partial t}=0\right)
. The only assumption used here is the neglect of temperature perturbation following the small perturbation imposed on the pressure field, and this is the reason for not considering the conservation of energy equation.

For the overall field, the conservation of mass is given by,

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\rho\vec{V}=0

(1)

The corresponding conservation of momentum equation without any body force is given by,

\displaystyle\rho\left(\frac{\partial\vec{V}}{\partial t}+\left(\vec{V}\cdot\nabla\right)\vec{V}\right)=-\nabla p

\displaystyle+\nabla\cdot\left(\lambda\left(\nabla\cdot\vec{V}\right)\bf{I}\right)+\nabla\cdot\left[\mu\left(\nabla\vec{V}+\nabla\vec{V}^{T}\right)\right]

(2)

Here,
\bf{I}
is an identity matrix of rank three. If one considers the acoustic signal as a small perturbation over the mean flow, then the velocity, the density, and the pressure can be expressed as a superposition of the unperturbed equilibrium state with the small perturbation given by,
\vec{V}=\vec{\bar{V}}+\epsilon\vec{V}^{\prime};~\rho=\bar{\rho}+\epsilon\rho^{\prime};~p=\bar{p}+\epsilon p^{\prime}
.

Without any loss of generality, one can consider the propagation of disturbances over a constant mean flow (i.e.,
\vec{\bar{V}}=V_{o}\hat{i}
) in a homogeneous medium (i.e. constant
\bar{\rho}
), so that,

\vec{V}=V_{o}\hat{i}+\epsilon\vec{V}^{\prime}.

(3)

The
O(\epsilon)
equation resulting from the conservation of mass equation, Eq. (
1
) yields,

\frac{\partial\rho^{\prime}}{\partial t}+\bar{\rho}\nabla\cdot\vec{V}^{\prime}+V_{o}\frac{\partial\rho^{\prime}}{\partial x}=0

(4)

Similarly, the
O(\epsilon)
equation resulting from the conservation of momentum equation, Eq. (
2
) yields (with
\lambda
and
\mu
treated as constant in the absence of heat transfer),

\bar{\rho}\frac{\partial\vec{V}^{\prime}}{\partial t}+\bar{\rho}V_{o}\frac{\partial\vec{V}^{\prime}}{\partial x}=0=-\nabla p^{\prime}+\nabla\cdot\left(\lambda\left(\nabla\cdot\vec{V}^{\prime}\right)\bf{I}\right)+\nabla\cdot\left[\mu\left(\nabla\vec{V}^{\prime}+\nabla\vec{V}^{{}^{\prime}T}\right)\right]

(5)

From Eq. (
4
) one gets,
\nabla\cdot\vec{V}^{\prime}=-\frac{1}{\bar{\rho}}\frac{\partial\rho^{\prime}}{\partial t}-\frac{V_{o}}{\bar{\rho}}\frac{\partial\rho^{\prime}}{\partial x}
; and differentiating this equation with respect to time yields,

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\nabla\cdot\vec{V}^{\prime}\right)=-\frac{1}{\bar{\rho}}\frac{\partial^{2}\rho^{\prime}}{\partial t^{2}}-\frac{V_{o}}{\bar{\rho}}\frac{\partial^{2}\rho^{\prime}}{\partial t\partial x}

(6)

By taking divergence of Eq. (
5
) one gets

\bar{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\nabla\cdot\vec{V}^{\prime}\right)+\bar{\rho}V_{o}\left(\nabla\cdot\vec{V}^{\prime}\right)=-\nabla^{2}p^{\prime}+\lambda\nabla^{2}\left(\nabla\cdot\vec{V}^{\prime}\right)+2\mu\nabla^{2}\left(\nabla\cdot\vec{V}^{\prime}\right)

(7)

Using Eq. (
6
) in Eq. (
7
) one gets,

-\frac{\partial^{2}\rho^{\prime}}{\partial t^{2}}-2V_{o}\frac{\partial^{2}\rho^{\prime}}{\partial t\partial x}-V_{o}^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\rho^{\prime}}{\partial^{2}x}=-\nabla^{2}p^{\prime}-\left(\lambda+2\mu\right)\left(\nabla^{2}\frac{1}{\bar{\rho}}\frac{\partial\rho^{\prime}}{\partial t}+\frac{V_{O}}{\bar{\rho}}\nabla^{2}\frac{\partial\rho^{\prime}}{\partial x}\right)

(8)

Following the reasoning given in Ref.
Sengupta, Sengupta, and Joshi 2023
, One can invoke the polytropic relation between perturbation pressure and perturbation density (by neglecting temperature perturbation) as,

\frac{\partial\rho^{\prime}}{\partial t}=\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial p^{\prime}}{\partial t}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\frac{\partial\rho^{\prime}}{\partial x}=\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial p^{\prime}}{\partial x}

(9)

In the absence of viscous losses, the medium’s speed of sound (
c
) is for an isentropic process, such that one can treat a homogeneous temperature field for the propagation of other disturbances
Feynman 1965
;
Feynman 1969
. Therefore, eliminating
\rho^{\prime}
using this relation in Eq. (
8
), one gets

\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}p^{\prime}}{\partial t^{2}}+\frac{2V_{o}}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}p^{\prime}}{\partial t\partial x}+\frac{V_{o}^{2}}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}p^{\prime}}{\partial^{2}x}=\nabla^{2}p^{\prime}+\frac{\lambda+2\mu}{\bar{\rho}c^{2}}\left(V_{o}\nabla^{2}\frac{\partial p^{\prime}}{\partial x}+\nabla^{2}\frac{\partial p^{\prime}}{\partial t}\right)

(10)

which can be further simplified as,

\frac{\partial^{2}p^{\prime}}{\partial t^{2}}+2V_{o}\frac{\partial^{2}p^{\prime}}{\partial t\partial x}+V_{o}^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}p^{\prime}}{\partial^{2}x}=c^{2}\nabla^{2}p^{\prime}+\nu_{l}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\nabla^{2}p^{\prime}+V_{o}\nabla^{2}\frac{\partial p^{\prime}}{\partial x}\right)

(11)

where the generalized viscosity is defined as before
Sengupta et al. 2023
to be given by,
\nu_{l}=\frac{\lambda+2\mu}{\bar{\rho}}
, including the effects of the dissipative medium accounting for all forms of viscous losses.

I.1
Characteristics of Perturbation Pressure Equation

To elucidate the fundamentals of Doppler effects, attention is focused here in Eq. (
11
) for the planar propagation of the disturbance field by considering a one-dimensional version of the perturbation equation, as it has been done for a quiescent ambiance
Blackstock 2000
;
Sengupta et al. 2023
given by,

\frac{\partial^{2}p^{\prime}}{\partial t^{2}}+V_{o}^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}p^{\prime}}{\partial x^{2}}+2V_{o}\frac{\partial^{2}p^{\prime}}{\partial x\partial t}-c^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}p^{\prime}}{\partial x^{2}}-\nu_{l}\left(V_{o}\frac{\partial^{3}p^{\prime}}{\partial x^{3}}+\frac{\partial^{2}p^{\prime}}{\partial t\partial x^{2}}\right)=0

(12)

For the purpose of dispersion and dissipation analysis, we represent the fluctuating pressure by,

p^{\prime}(x,t)=\int\int\hat{p}(k,\omega)e^{i(kx-\omega t)}dk~d\omega

(13)

Rewriting Eq.(
12
) in the spectral plane by using the above representation in Eq. (
13
), one gets the quadratic dispersion relation as,

\omega^{2}-k\omega\left(2V_{o}-i\nu_{l}k\right)+V_{o}k^{2}-i\nu_{l}k^{3}V_{o}-c^{2}k^{2}=0

(14)

This yields the dispersion relation for the two components of the solution as

\omega_{1,2}=V_{o}k-\frac{i\nu_{l}k^{2}}{2}\pm kcf

(15)

where we denote,
f=\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{\nu_{l}k}{2c}\right)^{2}}
. Treating the wavenumber
k
, as the independent variable, the dispersion relation in Eq. (
15
), provides the following amplification factors as
Sengupta 2013
;
Suman et al. 2017
,

G_{1,2}=e^{-i\omega_{1,2}\tau_{s}}

(16)

for the introduced time scale
\tau_{s}
. For
f>0
, these complex exponents in Eq. (
16
) the amplification factors are given for the real and imaginary parts for the first and second components by,

\displaystyle G_{1,real}=e^{-k^{2}\nu_{l}\tau_{s}/2}\cos k(V_{o}\tau_{s}+fc\tau_{s})

(17)

\displaystyle G_{1,imag}=e^{-k^{2}\nu_{l}\tau_{s}/2}\sin k(V_{o}\tau_{s}+fc\tau_{s})

(18)

\displaystyle G_{2,real}=e^{-k^{2}\nu_{l}\tau_{s}/2}\cos k(V_{o}\tau_{s}-fc\tau_{s})

(19)

\displaystyle G_{2,imag}=e^{-k^{2}\nu_{l}\tau_{s}/2}\sin k(V_{o}\tau_{s}-fc\tau_{s})

(20)

These real and imaginary parts of the amplification factors imposes phase shift for each components, and these can be obtained as given in the following
Sengupta et al. 2023
,

\beta_{1,2}=V_{o}k\tau_{s}\pm kcf\tau_{s},

(21)

By introducing the Mach number of the unperturbed flow as
M_{o}=V_{o}/c
, the non-dimensional phase speeds of the perturbation equation is given by,

\frac{c_{ph1,2}}{c}=\frac{\beta_{1,2}}{kc\tau_{s}}=M_{o}\pm f

(22)

The corresponding group velocity components (
v_{g1,2}
), as given in the literature
Rayleigh 1945
;
Lighthill 2001
;
Brillouin 1960
;
Sengupta 2021
, of the perturbation equation are obtained from Eq. (
15
) as,

v_{g1,2}=\frac{d\omega_{1,2}}{dk}=V_{o}\pm cf\mp\frac{\left(k\nu_{l}\right)^{2}}{4fc}-i\nu_{l}k

(23)

This can be represented in the non-dimensional form as

\frac{v_{g1,2}}{c}=M_{0}\pm f\mp\frac{2}{f}(\frac{k}{k_{c}})^{2}-\frac{ik\nu_{l}}{c}

(24)

Where we have introduced the cut-off wavenumber as before
Sengupta et al. 2023
;
Sengupta, Sengupta, and Joshi 2023
,
k_{c}=2c/\nu_{l}
. As has been explained before
Suman et al. 2017
;
Sengupta et al. 2023
, the imaginary part of the group velocity is irrelevant for physical systems which do not exhibit anti-diffusion
Sengupta 2013
, and will not be considered henceforth.

If the imaginary part of the physical amplification factor is absent in equation (
20
), then there will be no phase shift in the time interval of
\tau_{s}
. Such a situation can arise for
\beta_{1}=m\pi
, for all integral values of
m
, including zero, i.e.

\displaystyle k_{m}\tau_{s}\left(V_{o}+cf_{m}\right)=m\pi\;\;\;{\rm for}~m=1,2,....\infty

(25)

\displaystyle k_{m}\tau_{s}\left(V_{o}-cf_{m}\right)=m\pi\;\;\;{\rm for}~m=1,2,....\infty

(26)

III
Summary and Conclusions

The perturbation field created by a pulse in quiescent ambiance has been reported recently
Sengupta et al. 2023
, where it has been shown that there exists a cut-off wavenumber (
k_{c}
), above which the governing wave equation for the perturbation pressure transforms to a diffusion equation, i.e. the acoustic wave diffuses the perturbation pressure. Furthermore, it has also been reported that such switching from wave equation to diffusion equation also occurs for sub-critical wavenumbers, explained as a multi-modal phenomenon
Sengupta, Sengupta, and Joshi 2023
. All these significant results have been obtained from the governing equation of the perturbation field by including viscous diffusion, which was not included earlier,
Feynman 1965
;
Feynman 1969
;
Whitham 1974
. The viscous loss terms were included for planar propagation in
Blackstock 2000
;
Trusler 1991
;
Morse and Ingard 1986
, but comprehensive details were communicated for the first time
Sengupta et al. 2023
for quiescent ambiance, by an analysis using global spectral analysis (GSA)
Sagaut et al. 2023
;
Sengupta 2013
to obtain the properties of the perturbation equation. GSA involves obtaining the metrics, such as the amplification factor, phase speed, and group velocity, by mapping the governing equation in the hybrid Fourier transform plane. The energy of a wave packet propagates with the group velocity, as noted
Brillouin 1960
;
Lighthill 2001
;
Rayleigh 1945
for electromagnetic waves, waves in fluids, and sound waves, respectively. Here, GSA has been extended in the presence of uniform mean flow, to quantify the well-known Doppler effect in acoustic. Detailed formulation of the perturbation equation has been provided here, with the uniform flow speed (or the Mach number, when non-dimensional quantities are presented), acting as a parameter.

Unlike the isotropy of propagation for the wave equation for the case of quiescent ambiance, here the non-isotropy due to the Doppler effect is demonstrated by considering the case of planar propagation, as shown in Eq. (
15
) for the dispersion relation. The corresponding plot is shown in Fig.
1
, and its effect on the amplification factor is shown in the nondimensional wavenumber and frequency plane, in Fig.
2
for both the sub- and super-critical wavenumbers ranges. In Fig.
2
and in all subsequent figures, the Doppler effect is demonstrated by comparing the quiescent case with a case for which the source is moving at a uniform speed given by the Mach number,
M_{0}=0.01
. All the figures are shown for a fixed generalized kinematic viscosity coefficient of
\nu_{l}=0.0002

m^{2}/s
, typical of Helium as the working fluid with Stokes’ hypothesis implemented. The Doppler effect is noted in the asymmetric attenuation for the left-running and right-running waves, as well as, for the modal morphing of the wave equation to diffusion equation for the sub-critical wavenumbers.

The asymmetric theoretical properties are shown for Doppler effects by noting the phase speed and group velocities in Figs.
3
-
4
and Figs.
5
-
6
, respectively, where these are compared with the quiescent ambiance case.

The study’s implications extend to fluid dynamics and acoustics, offering insights into the propagation of sound, when it’s source is in constant motion, both qualitatively and quantitatively for the first time. These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the complex interplay between disturbances, flow conditions, and wave behavior in compressible fluid media. The results presented here contribute to advancing our knowledge precisely of wave phenomena
Whitham 1974
;
Brillouin 1960
;
Lighthill 2001
and their practical implications for acoustics
Blackstock 2000
;
Sengupta and Bhumkar 2020
. It is proposed that the present research be extended to other types of mean flow fields, including for wall-bounded and free-shear layer flows.
