On the dynamics of an epidemic patch model with mass-action transmission mechanism and asymmetric dispersal patterns
Rachidi B. Salako, and, Yixiang Wu

Abstract
This paper examines an epidemic patch model with mass-action transmission mechanism and asymmetric connectivity matrix. Results on the global dynamics of solutions and the spatial structures of endemic solutions are obtained. In particular, we show that when the basic reproduction number ℛ 0 \mathcal{R}_{0} is less than one and the dispersal rate of the susceptible population d S d_{S} is large, the population would eventually stabilize at the disease-free equilibrium. However, the disease may persist if d S d_{S} is small, even if ℛ 0 < 1 \mathcal{R}_{0}<1 . In such a scenario, explicit conditions on the model parameters that lead to the existence of multiple endemic equilibria are identified. These results provide new insights into the dynamics of infectious diseases in multi-patch environments. Moreover, results in [ 27 ] , which is for the same model but with symmetric connectivity matrix, are generalized and improved.

Asymptotic Behavior; Persistenc; Multiplicity of Equilibria.

computed the basic reproduction number of a multi-city epidemic model; Wang and Zhao

used an SIS patch model to illustrate the importance of dispersal in the spread of disease; Guo, Li and Shuai
[
21
]
developed a graph-theoretical method to construct Lyapunov functions and show the stability of the endemic equilibrium of multigroup epidemic models. We refer the interested readers to the survey papers
[

,

]
for more works on epidemic patch models.

In the pioneering work
[
1
]
, Allen
et al
. proposed the following SIS epidemic patch model with standard-incidence transmission mechanism to study the impact of environmental heterogeneity and population movement on the spread of an infectious disease:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{dS_{i}}{dt}=d_{S}\sum_{j\in\Omega,j\neq i}(L_{ij}S_{j}-L_{ji}S_{i})-\beta_{i}\frac{S_{i}I_{i}}{S_{i}+I_{i}}+\gamma_{i}I_{i},&i\in\Omega,\ t>0,\cr\displaystyle\frac{dI_{i}}{dt}=d_{I}\sum_{j\in\Omega,j\neq i}(L_{ij}I_{j}-L_{ji}I_{i})+\beta_{i}\frac{S_{i}I_{i}}{S_{i}+I_{i}}-\gamma_{i}I_{i},&i\in\Omega,\ t>0.\end{cases}

(1.1)

Here, the population is supposed to live in patches (e.g., cities, countries), and
\Omega=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
is the collection of patch labels, where
n\geq 2
;
S_{i}
and
I_{i}
are the number of susceptible and infected people in patch
i
, respectively;
\beta_{i}
and
\gamma_{i}
are disease transmission and recovery rates, respectively;
L_{ij}
,
i\neq j
, is the degree of movement of individuals from patch
j
to patch
i
;
d_{S}
and
d_{I}
are the dispersal rates of susceptible and infected individuals, respectively. In
[
1
]
, the authors defined a basic reproduction number
\mathcal{R}_{0}
and showed that
\mathcal{R}_{0}
serves as a threshold parameter: the disease free equilibrium (DFE) is globally asymptotically stable if
\mathcal{R}_{0}<1
, and the model has a unique endemic equilibrium (EE) if
\mathcal{R}_{0}>1
.
Most importantly, they showed that the disease component of the endemic equilibrium approaches zero if
d_{S}\to 0
under the assumption that
\{i\in\Omega:\beta_{i}-\gamma_{i}<0\}
and
\{i\in\Omega:\beta_{i}-\gamma_{i}>0\}
are non-empty. Biologically, this suggests that one may control the disease by limiting the movement of susceptible people. Later, Li and Peng
[
26
]
studied the asymptotic profiles of the EE as
d_{S}
and/or
d_{I}
approach zero; Chen
et al.

[
10
]
revisited model (
1.1
) without assuming that the connectivity matrix
L
is symmetric; the monotonicity of
\mathcal{R}_{0}
in
d_{I}
has been studied in
[
19
,
18
,
10
]
; The impact of dispersal rates on the number of infected cases was investigated in
[
19
,
20
]
.

In this paper, we consider the following epidemic patch model with mass-action transmission mechanism:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{dS_{i}}{dt}=d_{S}\sum_{j\in\Omega,j\neq i}(L_{ij}S_{j}-L_{ji}S_{i})-\beta_{i}{S_{i}I_{i}}+\gamma_{i}I_{i},&i\in\Omega,\ t>0,\cr\displaystyle\frac{dI_{i}}{dt}=d_{I}\sum_{j\in\Omega,j\neq i}(L_{ij}I_{j}-L_{ji}I_{i})+\beta_{i}{S_{i}I_{i}}-\gamma_{i}I_{i},&i\in\Omega,\ t>0.\end{cases}

All the parameters and variables carry the same biological meaning with model (
1.1
) except that the standard-incidence transmission mechanism
\beta_{i}S_{i}I_{i}/(S_{i}+I_{i})
is replaced by the mass-action transmission mechanism
\beta_{i}S_{i}I_{i}
.
For simplicity, let
L_{ii}=-\sum_{j\in\Omega,j\neq i}L_{ji}
, which is the total degree of movement out from patch
i
. Then the model can be written as follows:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{dS_{i}}{dt}=d_{S}\sum_{j\in\Omega}L_{ij}S_{j}-\beta_{i}{S_{i}I_{i}}+\gamma_{i}I_{i},&i\in\Omega,\ t>0,\cr\displaystyle\frac{dI_{i}}{dt}=d_{I}\sum_{j\in\Omega}L_{ij}I_{j}+\beta_{i}{S_{i}I_{i}}-\gamma_{i}I_{i},&i\in\Omega,\ t>0.\end{cases}

(1.2)

This model has been studied in
[
27
]
when the connectivity matrix
\mathcal{L}=(L_{ij})
is symmetric. In particular, the authors defined a basic reproduction number
\mathcal{R}_{0}
and studied the global dynamics of the model if
d_{S}=d_{I}
or
(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})
is a multiple of
(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n})
. They proved the existence of EE when
\mathcal{R}_{0}>1
and showed that it was unique with some additional assumptions, and they studied the asymptotic profiles of the EE as
d_{S}
and/or
d_{I}
approach zero.

If (
1.2
) has only one patch, then it is the following classic ordinary differential equation epidemic model by Kermack and McKendrick
[
24
]
:

\begin{cases}S^{\prime}=-\beta SI+\gamma I,\\
I^{\prime}=\beta SI-\gamma I,\\
S+I=N.\end{cases}

(1.3)

For this model, one can define a basic reproduction number
R_{0}=N\beta/\gamma
, which is the average number of cases one infected case generates after it is introduced to a population without the disease. The basic reproduction number of (
1.3
) serves as a threshold value as it does for many epidemic models: if
R_{0}<1
, the DFE
(N,0)
of (
1.3
) is globally asymptotically stable; if
R_{0}>1
, the model (
1.3
) has a unique globally stable EE
(\gamma/\beta,N-\gamma/\beta)
. We may expect that such a threshold result is true for the patch model (
1.2
). However, we will show that it is possible for (
1.2
) to have multiple EEs when
\mathcal{R}_{0}<1
under certain conditions on the parameters. Our results indicate that the disease dynamics can be more complicated in a patchy environment.

The objective of the current paper is two fold. Firstly, we will obtain and improve the results in
[
27
]
without the assumption that the matrix
L
is symmetric. If
L
is not symmetric, many techniques (e.g., construction of Lyapunov functions) adopted in
[
27
]
cannot be carried over. So we will develop novel techniques to overcome the asymmetry of
L
, which may be applied to study similar patch models. Some of our global stability results on the DFE seem to be new even in the symmetric case. Secondly, we will study the uniqueness/non-uniqueness of the EE solutions. As shown in Theorem
3.5
, using
d_{S}
as a bifurcation parameter, it is possible that when
\mathcal{R}_{0}<1
the model has multiple EE solutions if
d_{S}
is small, while there is no EE solutions and the DFE is globally asymptotially stable if
d_{S}
is large. Our result also suggests that models with mass-action mechanism may have very different behavior compared with models with standard incidence mechanism (in model
1.1
, the DFE is always globally asymptotically stable when
\mathcal{R}_{0}<1
). We refer the readers to the last section for more discussions about the novelty and implications of our results.

We remark that there are many recent works on reaction-diffusion epidemic models that are related to models (
1.1
) and (
1.2
). In particular, the reaction-diffusion counterpart of (
1.1
) has been analyzed in
[

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

, while the reaction-diffusion version of (

The rest of our paper is organized as follows. In section 2, we introduce the notations and present some preliminary results. In section 3, we study the global stability of the model and the existence and uniqueness/non-uniqueness of the EE solutions. In section 4, we study the asymptotic profiles of the EE solutions as
d_{S}
and/or
d_{I}
approach zero. In section 5, we give some concluding remarks.

2.1
Notations, assumptions, and definitions

Throughout the paper, given a real number

, we use the conventional notations
x_{+}=\max\{x,0\}

x_{-}:=\max\{0,-x\}

. A bold letter always represents a column vector in

, and its no-bold form with a numeric subscript will be a component of it. For example, for any
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\bm{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})^{T}
, where

for

.
For convenience, we write
(0,\dots,0)^{T}
as
{\bm{0}}
and
(1,\dots,1)^{T}
as
{\bm{1}}
. For
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define

{\bm{X}}_{m}:=\min_{j=1,\cdots,n}X_{j},\quad{\bm{X}}_{M}:=\max_{j=1\cdots,n}X_{j},\quad\|\bm{X}\|_{1}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}|X_{i}|,\quad\text{and}\quad\|\bm{X}\|_{\infty}:=\max_{j\in\Omega}|X_{j}|.

We denote by

the diagonal matrix with diagonal entries

We denote by

the inner product on

, that is

\big<\bm{X},\bm{Y}\big>=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}Y_{i},\quad\forall\ \bm{X},\ \bm{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

For
\bm{X},\bm{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define the Hadamard product
\bm{X}\circ\bm{Y}:=(X_{1}Y_{1},\cdots,X_{n}Y_{n})^{T}

{\bm{X}}/{\bm{Y}}=(X_{1}/Y_{1},\cdots,

X_{n}/Y_{n})^{T}
if

for all

.
Adopting these notations, system (
1.2
) can be rewritten in the compact form

\begin{cases}\bm{S}^{\prime}=d_{S}\mathcal{L}\bm{S}-\bm{\beta}\circ\bm{I}\circ\bm{S}+\bm{\gamma}\circ\bm{I},&t>0,\cr\bm{I}^{\prime}=d_{I}\mathcal{L}\bm{I}+\bm{\beta}\circ\bm{I}\circ\bm{S}-\bm{\gamma}\circ\bm{I},&t>0.\end{cases}

Throughout this work, we make the following assumptions:

(A1)

The matrix
\mathcal{L}=(L_{i,j})_{i,j=1}^{K}
is quasi-positive (i.e.,
L_{ij}\geq 0
for any
i\neq j
) and irreducible.

(A2)

\bm{S}^{0},\bm{I}^{0}\geq\bm{0}
, and
\sum_{j\in\Omega}(S^{0}_{j}+I^{0}_{j}):=N
, where
N
is some fixed positive constant.

(A3)

\bm{\beta},\bm{\gamma}\gg\bm{0}
and
d_{S},d_{I}>0
.

Due to biological interpretations of the vectors
\bm{S}
and
\bm{I}
, we will only be interested in nonnegative solutions of (
1.2
). Let
\mathbb{R}_{+}
denote the set of nonnegative real numbers. For any initial data
(\bm{S}(0),\bm{I}(0))=(\bm{S}^{0},\bm{I}^{0})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}

) has a unique nonnegative solution

defined on a maximal interval of existence

. Summing up all the equations in (

), we find that
\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{i\in\Omega}(S_{i}+I_{i})=0
, which means that the total population is preserved. Therefore, for any initial data
(\bm{S}^{0},\bm{I}^{0})
satisfying
(A2)
, the solution satisfies

\sum_{j\in\Omega}(S_{j}(t)+I_{j}(t))=N,\quad\forall\ t>0.

(2.1)

This means that the solution of (
1.2
) exists globally and
T_{\max}=\infty
. Thanks to (
2.1
) and the fact that the positive constant
N
is fixed, the semiflow generated by solutions of (

) leaves invariant the set

\mathcal{E}:=\Big\{(\bm{S},\bm{I})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\ :\ \sum_{j\in\Omega}(S_{j}+I_{j})=N\Big\}.

It is easy to see that if
\bm{I}^{0}=\bm{0}
then
\bm{I}(t)=\bm{0}
for all
t\geq 0
. Assumption
(A1)
indicates that the patches are strongly connected, i.e. for any two patches the individuals can move directly or indirectly from one patch to another. By
(A1)
and the comparison principle, if
\bm{I}^{0}>\bm{0}
, then
\bm{S}(t)\gg\bm{0}
and
\bm{I}(t)\gg\bm{0}
for all
t>0
.

For
n\times n
real-valued square matrix
M
, let
\sigma(M)
be the set of eigenvalues of
M
,
\sigma_{*}(M)
be the spectral bound, i.e.,

\sigma_{*}(M):=\max\{\mathfrak{R}e(\lambda)\ :\ \lambda\in\sigma(M)\},

where
\mathfrak{R}e(\lambda)
is the real part of
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
and
\rho(M)
be the spectral radius, i.e.,

\rho(M):=\max\{|\lambda|\ :\ \lambda\in\sigma(M)\}.

Since
\mathcal{L}
is quasi-positive and irreducible, it generates a strongly-positive matrix-semigroup
\{e^{t\mathcal{L}}\}_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover since
\sum_{i\in\Omega}\mathcal{L}_{ij}=0
for each
j\in\Omega
, by the Perron-Frobenius theorem,
\sigma_{*}(\mathcal{L})=0
is a simple eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}
. Furthermore, there is an eigenvector
\bm{\alpha}
associated with
\sigma_{*}(\mathcal{L})
satisfying

\mathcal{L}\bm{\alpha}=\bm{0},\quad\sum_{j\in\Omega}{\alpha}_{j}=1,\quad\text{and}\quad{\alpha}_{j}>0,\ \forall\ j\in\Omega,

(2.2)

and
\bm{\alpha}
is the unique nonnegative eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}
with
\sum_{j\in\Omega}{\alpha}_{j}=1
. Throughout the paper,
\bm{\alpha}
is fixed and satisfies (
2.2
).

An equilibrium solution
(\bm{S},\bm{I})
of (
1.2
) is called a
disease free equilibrium
(DFE) if
\bm{I}=\bm{0}
and an
endemic equilibrium
(EE) if
\bm{I}\neq\bm{0}
. It is easy to check that
(N\bm{\alpha},\bm{0})
is the unique DFE. To define the basic reproduction number of (
1.2
), we linearize the model at DFE and find that the stability of DFE is determined by
\sigma_{*}(d_{I}\mathcal{L}+{\rm diag}(N\bm{\alpha}\circ\bm{\beta}-\bm{\gamma}))
. So define

{F}={\rm diag}(N\bm{\alpha}\circ\bm{\beta})\quad\text{and}\quad{V}:={\rm diag}(\bm{\gamma})-d_{I}\mathcal{L}.

(2.3)

Then
{V}
is invertible. Following the next generation matrix approach
[
42
,
16
]
, the
basic reproduction number

\mathcal{R}_{0}
of (
1.2
) is given by

\mathcal{R}_{0}:=\rho({F}{V}^{-1}).

(2.4)

It is well known that
\mathcal{R}_{0}-1
and
\sigma_{*}(-V+F)=\sigma_{*}(d_{I}\mathcal{L}+{\rm diag}(N\bm{\alpha}\circ\bm{\beta}-\bm{\gamma}))
have the same sign, and the DFE is locally asymptotically stable if
\mathcal{R}_{0}<1
and unstable if
\mathcal{R}_{0}>1

[
42
]
. By
[
10
,
18
]
,
\mathcal{R}_{0}
is decreasing in
d_{I}
with

\lim_{d_{I}\to 0}\mathcal{R}_{0}=\max_{j\in\Omega}\frac{N\alpha_{j}\beta_{j}}{\gamma_{j}}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \lim_{d_{I}\to\infty}\mathcal{R}_{0}=\frac{\sum_{j\in\Omega}N\alpha_{j}^{2}\beta_{j}}{\sum_{j\in\Omega}\alpha_{j}\gamma_{j}}.

(2.5)

Moreover,
\mathcal{R}_{0}
is strictly decreasing in
d_{I}
if and only if
\bm{\gamma}
is not a multiple of
\bm{\alpha}\circ\bm{\beta}
.

For convenience, we define
\bm{r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with

{r}_{j}:=\frac{\gamma_{j}}{\beta_{j}},\quad\forall\;j\in\Omega.

Biologically,

measures the risk of patch
j
when the patches are isolated, i.e.

.

2.2
Preliminary results

Recall that
\bm{\alpha}
is the eigenvector associated with
\sigma_{*}(\mathcal{L})=0
as described in (
2.2
). By classical results in matrix theory, there is a subspace
\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
invariant under
\mathcal{L}
such that

\mathbb{R}^{n}={\rm span}(\bm{\alpha})\oplus\mathcal{Z}.

So there is projection map
P:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to{\rm span}(\bm{\alpha})
such that
P\bm{\alpha}=\bm{\alpha}
and
P\bm{Z}={\bf 0}
for any
\bm{Z}\in\mathcal{Z}
. Moreover,

P\mathcal{L}\bm{X}=\bm{0},\quad\forall\;\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(2.6)

Note that
P\bm{X}\in{\rm span}(\bm{\alpha})
and
({\rm id}-P)\bm{X}\in\mathcal{Z}
for any
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Furthermore, there is positive number
\nu>0
and
M_{\nu}>0

[
22
, Theorem 1.5.3]
such that

\|e^{t\mathcal{L}}({\rm id}-P)\bm{X}\|_{1}\leq M_{\nu}e^{-\nu t}\|\bm{X}\|_{1},\quad\forall\ \bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(2.7)

In particular, observing that
e^{t\mathcal{L}}P\bm{X}=P\bm{X}
, we have that
e^{t\mathcal{L}}\bm{X}\to P\bm{X}
as
t\to\infty
for any
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The following lemma will be useful in the proofs of our main results.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that
(A1)
holds.
Let
d>0
and
\bm{F}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a continuous map satisfying
\|\bm{F}(t)\|_{1}\to 0
as
t\to\infty
. If
\bm{X}(t)
be a bounded solution of the system

\begin{cases}\bm{X}^{\prime}(t)=d\mathcal{L}\bm{X}(t)+\bm{F}(t),\ t>0,\cr\bm{X}(0)=\bm{X}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\end{cases}

then
\bm{X}(t)-({\sum_{j\in\Omega}X_{j}(t)})\bm{\alpha}\to\bm{0}
as
t\to\infty
.
In particular if
\bm{F}(t)={\bf 0}
for all
t\geq 0
, then
\bm{X}(t)\to({\sum_{j\in\Omega}X^{0}_{j}})\bm{\alpha}
as
t\to\infty
.

Proof.

We write
\bm{X}(t)=P\bm{X}(t)+({\rm id}-P)\bm{X}(t)
for any
t\geq 0
, where
P
is as in (
2.7
). Then
P\bm{X}(t)=g_{X}(t)\bm{\alpha}
for some
g_{X}(t)\in\mathbb{R}
,
t\geq 0
. Next, thanks to (
2.6
),

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}({\rm id}-P)\bm{X}=

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{X}}{dt}-P\frac{d\bm{X}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\mathcal{L}({\rm id}-P)\bm{X}+\bm{F}(t)-dP\mathcal{L}\bm{X}-P\bm{F}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\mathcal{L}({\rm id}-P)\bm{X}+({\rm id}-P)\bm{F}(t).

Hence, by the variation of constant formula,

({\rm id}-P)\bm{X}(t+\tau)=e^{d\tau\mathcal{L}}({\rm id}-P)\bm{X}(t)+\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{d(\tau-s)\mathcal{L}}({\rm id}-P)\bm{F}(t+s)ds,\quad t\geq 0,\tau>0.

(2.8)

By (
2.7
) and (
2.8
),

\displaystyle\|({\rm id}-P)\bm{X}(t+\tau)\|_{1}\leq

\displaystyle M_{\nu}e^{-\tau\nu d}\|\bm{X}(t)\|_{1}+M_{\nu}\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-d\nu(\tau-s)}\|\bm{F}(t+s)\|_{1}ds

(2.9)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle M_{\nu}e^{-d\tau\nu}\sup_{t^{\prime}\geq 0}\|\bm{X}(t^{\prime})\|_{1}+M_{\nu}\Big[\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-d\nu(\tau-s)}ds\Big]\sup_{t^{\prime}\geq t}\|\bm{F}(t^{\prime})\|_{1}

(2.10)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle M_{\nu}e^{-d\tau\nu}\sup_{t^{\prime}\geq 0}\|\bm{X}(t^{\prime})\|_{1}+\frac{M_{\nu}}{d\nu}\sup_{t^{\prime}\geq t}\|\bm{F}(t^{\prime})\|_{1}.

(2.11)

Since
\|\bm{F}(t)\|_{1}\to 0
as
t\to\infty
, we conclude from (
2.9
) that
\|({\rm id}-P)\bm{X}(t)\|_{1}\to 0
as
t\to\infty
. As a result, since
\bm{X}(t)=g_{X}(t)\bm{\alpha}+({\rm id}-P)\bm{X}(t)
, then

\sum_{j\in\Omega}X_{j}(t)=g_{X}(t)+\sum_{j\in\Omega}\big<\bm{e}_{j},({\rm id}-P)\bm{X}(t)\big>,\quad\forall\ t\geq 0,

where
\{\bm{e}_{1},\cdots,\bm{e}_{n}\}
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Hence,

\Big|g_{X}(t)-{\sum_{j\in\Omega}X_{j}(t)}\Big|\leq\|({\rm id}-P)\bm{X}(t)\|_{1}\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad t\to\infty.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\Big\|\bm{X}(t)-\bm{\alpha}{\sum_{j\in\Omega}X_{j}}\Big\|_{1}=

\displaystyle\Big\|P\bm{X}(t)+({\rm id}-P)\bm{X}(t)-\bm{\alpha}{\sum_{j\in\Omega}X_{j}}\Big\|_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big\|({\rm id}-P)\bm{X}(t)+\Big(g_{X}(t)-{\sum_{j\in\Omega}X_{j}}\Big)\bm{\alpha}\Big\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|({\rm id}-P)\bm{X}(t)\|_{1}+\Big|g_{X}(t)-({\sum_{j\in\Omega}X_{j}})\Big|\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad t\to\infty.

This completes the proof of the first assertion of the lemma. If in addition
\bm{F}(t)={\bf 0}
for any
t\geq 0
, then

\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{j\in\Omega}X_{j}(t)=0,\quad t>0.

Thus
\sum_{j\in\Omega}X_{i}(t)=\sum_{j=1}X^{0}_{j}
for all
t\geq 0
, which completes the proof of the lemma.

(2.13)

We will also need the following result.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
F:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be a Hölder continuous function satisfying

\sup_{t\geq a}F(t)e^{\int_{a}^{t}F(s)ds}<\infty

for some nonnegative number
a\geq 0
. Then
F(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.

Proof.

Let
\bar{F}:=\limsup_{t\to\infty}F(t)
. It suffices to show that
\bar{F}=0
. Suppose to the contrary that
\bar{F}\neq 0
. Then there is a sequence
\{t_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
converging to infinity such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}F(t_{n})=\bar{F}\in(0,\infty]
. Define
K:=\sup_{t\geq a}F(t)e^{\int_{a}^{t}F(s)ds}
. Then

\int_{a}^{t_{n}}F(s)ds\leq\ln\Big(\frac{K}{F(t_{n})}\Big),\quad\quad\forall\ n\gg 1.

(2.14)

Since
F(t_{n})\leq K
for
n\gg 1
,
\bar{F}<\infty
. It then follows from (
2.14
) that

\int_{a}^{\infty}F(s)ds\leq\ln(K/{\bar{F}})<\infty
. Since
F(t)\geq 0
for all
t\geq 0
and
F
is Hölder continuous, we have
F(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
. This is a contradiction. Therefore
\bar{F}=0
, which yields the desired result.
∎

3
Global stability and EE solutions

For simple epidemic differential equation models, one usually expects that the global dynamics is determined by
\mathcal{R}_{0}
: if
\mathcal{R}_{0}<1
, the DFE is globally stable; if
\mathcal{R}_{0}>1
, the model has a globally stable (and unique) EE. This is true if model (
1.2
) has only one patch. However when
n\geq 2
, there are certain parameter ranges that do not fit the expectations.

3.1
Case
\mathcal{R}_{0}\leq 1

Recall that the DFE
{\bf E}^{0}:=({N}\bm{\alpha},{\bf 0})
is unique. Firstly, we find parameter ranges such that the DFE is globally stable when
\mathcal{R}_{0}\leq 1
.

Theorem 3.1

(Global stability of DFE when
\mathcal{R}_{0}\leq 1
)
.

Suppose that
(A1)-(A3)
holds. The DFE is linearly stable if
\mathcal{R}_{0}<1
and unstable if
\mathcal{R}_{0}>1
. Furthermore, the DFE is globally asymptotically stable if one of the following conditions hold:

(i)

N\rho({\rm diag}(\bm{\beta})\mathcal{V}^{-1})\leq 1
;

(ii)

\mathcal{R}_{0}<1
and
d_{S}>d_{\rm low}(d_{I},N)
, for some positive number
d_{\rm low}(d_{I},N)
depending on
d_{I}
and
N
;

(iii)

\mathcal{R}_{0}\leq 1
and
d_{S}=d_{I}
;

(iv)

\mathcal{R}_{0}\leq 1
and
\bm{\gamma}=m\bm{\beta}\circ\bm{\alpha}
for some positive number
m>0
.

Proof.

The linear stability/instability of the DFE in terms of
\mathcal{R}_{0}
is standard
[
42
, Theorem 2]
. So it remains to prove the global stability of the DFE if one of (i)-(iv) holds.

(i) Suppose that
N\rho({\rm diag}(\bm{\beta})\mathcal{V}^{-1})\leq 1
. Let
(\bm{S}(t),\bm{I}(t))
be a solution of (
1.2
). Let
\tilde{\bm{E}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the positive eigenvector associated with
\sigma_{*}(d_{I}\mathcal{\mathcal{}}{L}+{\rm diag}(N\bm{\beta}-\bm{\gamma}))
with
\tilde{\bm{E}}_{m}=1
. Define

\bar{\bm{I}}(t):=e^{t\sigma_{*}(d_{I}\mathcal{\mathcal{}}{L}+{\rm diag}(N\bm{\beta}-\bm{\gamma}))}\tilde{\bm{E}},\quad\forall\ t\geq 0.

It holds that

\frac{d\bar{\bm{I}}}{dt}=d_{I}\mathcal{L}\bar{\bm{I}}+(N\bm{\beta}-\gamma)\circ\bar{\bm{I}},\quad\forall\ t\geq 0.

(3.1)

Let

c(t):=\inf\{\tilde{c}>0:\ \bm{I}(t)\leq\tilde{c}\tilde{\bm{E}}\},\quad t\geq 0.

Then,

\bm{I}(t)\leq c(t)\tilde{\bm{E}},\quad\forall\ t\geq 0.

(3.2)

By the definition of
c(t)
, there is
j_{t}\in\Omega
such that
I_{j_{t}}(t)=c(t)\tilde{E}_{j_{t}}
for all
t\geq 0
.
It follows that

\sum_{j\in\Omega}I_{j}(t)\geq I_{j_{t}}(t)=c(t)\tilde{E}_{j_{t}}\geq c(t)\min_{j\in\Omega}\tilde{E}_{j}=c(t)\tilde{\bm{E}}_{m}=c(t),\quad\forall\ t\geq 0.

As a result,

\sum_{j\in\Omega}S_{j}(t)=N-\sum_{j\in\Omega}I_{j}(t)\leq N-c(t),\quad\forall\ t\geq 0.

This in turn implies that

\bm{I}^{\prime}\leq d_{I}\mathcal{L}\bm{I}+((N-c(t))\bm{\beta}-\bm{\gamma})\circ\bm{I}\leq d_{I}\mathcal{L}\bm{I}+(N\bm{\beta}-\bm{\gamma})\circ\bm{I}-\bm{\beta}_{m}c(t)\bm{I},\quad\forall\ t>0.

(3.3)

Hence, setting

\underline{\bm{I}}(t+t_{0};t_{0}):=e^{\bm{\beta}_{m}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+t}c(s)ds}\bm{I}(t+t_{0}),\quad\ \forall\,t,t_{0}\geq 0,

we obtain from (

) that

\displaystyle\frac{d\underline{\bm{I}}(t+t_{0};t_{0})}{dt}\leq d_{I}\mathcal{L}\underline{\bm{I}}(t+t_{0};t_{0})+(N\bm{\beta}-\gamma)\circ\underline{\bm{I}}(t+t_{0};t_{0}),\quad\forall\,t>0,t_{0}\geq 0.

\underline{\bm{I}}(t_{0};t_{0})=\bm{I}(t_{0})\leq c(t_{0})\tilde{\bf E}=c(t_{0})\bar{\bm{I}}(0),

it follows from (
3.1
), (
3.4

\underline{\bm{I}}(t+t_{0};t_{0})\leq c(t_{0})\bar{\bm{I}}(t),\quad\forall\,t>0,t_{0}\geq 0.

Equivalently,

Remark 3.10
.

In the absence of the disease, the population will ideally be distributed according to the DFE (see Lemma
2.1
), in which case the susceptible population will be distributed proportionally to
\bm{\alpha}
. If the distribution of the risk function is proportional to
\bm{\alpha}
, hence proportional to the distribution of the susceptible population at the DFE, then the relative dispersal rates
d_{S}
and
d_{I}
of the infected and susceptible individuals become insignificant and the global dynamics is governed by the local dynamics. In this case, the local reproduction number, defined as
\mathcal{R}_{i}:=\frac{N\beta_{i}\alpha_{i}}{\gamma_{i}}=\frac{N\alpha_{i}}{r_{i}}
,
i\in\Omega
, is patch independent and
\mathcal{R}_{0}=\mathcal{R}_{i}
for all
i\in\Omega
. In this case, Theorem
3.1
-
(iv)
and Theorem
3.9
-
(i)
confirm that the global dynamics of the disease is solely determined by the sign of
\mathcal{R}_{0}-1
. When both the susceptible and infected populations have the same dispersal rate, that is
d=d_{I}=d_{S}
, Theorem
3.1
-
(iii)
and Theorem
3.9
-
(ii)
suggest that the global dynamics of the disease is again determine by the sign of
\mathcal{R}_{0}-1
. Note in the latter case that
\mathcal{R}_{0}
depends on
d
.

5
Discussions

We studied the dynamics of solutions of an epidemic model (
1.2
) with mass-action transmission mechanism in discrete-space environment. We defined the basic reproduction number
\mathcal{R}_{0}
(formula (
2.4
)) and studied the threshold dynamics of the model in terms of
\mathcal{R}_{0}
. In particular, when
\mathcal{R}_{0}\leq 1
, we established results on the global stability of the DFE (Theorem
3.1
), and the existence and multiplicity of EE solutions (Theorems
3.4
-
3.5
). When
\mathcal{R}_{0}>1
, we obtained results on the existence and uniqueness of EE solution (Theorem
3.8
). The global stability of the EE solution is established under some additional hypotheses (Theorem
3.9
). In the second part of the manuscript, we studied the impact of restricting population movement on the dynamics of the disease by investigating the asymptotic profiles of the EE solutions as
d_{S}
or/and
d_{I}
approach zero (Theorems
4.1
,
4.4
, and
4.6
). This study may help in taking informed decisions to implementing effective disease control strategies.

Our results are novel in two aspects. On one hand, we drop the assumption that the connectivity matrix between the patches is symmetric. As a consequence, some of our results generalize previous results obtained by Li and Peng
[
27
]
. On the other hand, some of our results are new even when the connectivity matrix is symmetric.
Firstly, the results on the global stability of DFE in Theorem
3.1
-(i)& (ii) seem to be new. Secondly, the results on the existence of multiple EE solutions are new. In simple epidemic models such as model (
1.2
) with one patch, it is usually expected that the DFE is globally stable if
\mathcal{R}_{0}<1
. Our Theorem
3.4
indicates that model (
1.2
) with more than one patch may have multiple EE solutions and the disease can persist even if
\mathcal{R}_{0}<1
. This result highlights a major difference on the predictions of the disease dynamics from models using the standard transmission mechanism: in model (
1.1
), the disease always extinct if
\mathcal{R}_{0}<1

[
1
,
10
]
. Another implication of this result is that the disease dynamics can be more complicated in patchy environment.

Our results are also interesting when using the dispersal rate
d_{S}
or total population
N
as bifurcation parameters. Notice that
\mathcal{R}_{0}
is independent of
d_{S}
. If
\mathcal{R}_{0}<1
, Theorem
3.1
-(ii) states that the DFE is globally asymptotically stable if
d_{S}
is sufficiently large; Theorem
3.4
indicates that it may be possible for the disease to persist if
d_{S}
is sufficiently small. If
\mathcal{R}_{0}>1
, there is some critical number
d_{I}^{*}=(1-m_{d_{I}})d_{I}
, where
m_{d_{I}}\in(0,1]
depends on the dispersal rate
d_{I}
, such that the (unique) EE solution exists if and only if
\mathcal{R}_{0}>1
(Theorem
3.8
). Interestingly,
d_{I}^{*}
is independent of
N
and equals zero if
\bm{r}\in{\rm span}(\bm{\alpha})
. Moreover, by Theorem
3.5
and Remark
3.7
, we have
d^{*}_{I}>0
if
\sum_{j\in\Omega}\gamma_{j}/\beta_{j}<(\sum_{j\in\Omega}\alpha_{j}\gamma_{j})/(\sum_{j\in\Omega}\beta_{i}\alpha_{j}^{2})
and
d_{I}
is large. It appears to be a difficult problem to understand the dependence of
d_{I}^{*}
on
d_{I}
. It is worth pointing out that when the connectivity matrix
\mathcal{L}
is symmetric,
[
27
, Theorem 4-(i)
\&
(iii) ]
established the existence and uniqueness of the EE when
\mathcal{R}_{0}>1
and
d_{S}>(1-|\Omega|/(\sum_{j\in\Omega}\beta_{j})^{1/3}(\sum_{j\in\Omega}\beta_{j}^{-1/2})^{2/3})d_{I}
. Hence, when
\mathcal{L}
is symmetric we have an apriori upper bound for

:

d_{I}^{*}\leq\Big(1-|\Omega|/(\sum_{j\in\Omega}\beta_{j})^{1/3}(\sum_{j\in\Omega}\beta_{j}^{-1/2})^{2/3}\Big)d_{I}.

We also note that
\mathcal{R}_{0}
is strictly decreasing in total population
N
. If
N
is sufficiently small (
\mathcal{R}_{0}\leq 1
in this case), Theorem
3.1
-(i) shows that the DFE is globally asymptotically stable. If

and

, model (

) has multiple EE solutions. When limiting the movement of susceptible people,

also plays an important role according to Theorem

[

9

.

When the disease eventually persists, it is critical to develop and implement adequate control strategies to alleviate the impact of the disease. One of the common disease control strategy is to restrict population movement, which was the case during the last global Covid-19 pandemic. One way to assess the effectiveness of such disease control strategies from a mathematical point of view is to study the profiles of the EE with respect to small dispersal rates of the population. In this direction, we first fixed the dispersal rate of the infected population and then examined the limit of EE as
d_{S}\to 0
. Our result in Theorem
4.1
indicates that if the total size of the population is below some critical number, then the disease can be significantly controlled by lowering the dispersal rate of the susceptible population. However, if the total population size exceeds this critical number, then disease may still persist even if the dispersal rate of the susceptible population is reduced. In fact, Proposition
4.2
confirms that the disease will persist if the total population size is large and the dispersal rate of the susceptible population is significantly small. Next, we fixed the diffusion rate of the susceptible population and then investigated the profiles of the EE as
d_{I}\to 0
. Theorem
4.4
suggests that if the dispersal rate of the susceptible population is small and the dispersal rate of the infected is significantly smaller, then the infected population will concentrate exactly on the highest-risk patches. Third, we restricted simultaneously the movement rates of both susceptible and infected people and examined the profiles of the EE solutions in Theorem
4.6
. This result shows that the ratio of the dispersal rates play an important role on the dynamics of the disease.
