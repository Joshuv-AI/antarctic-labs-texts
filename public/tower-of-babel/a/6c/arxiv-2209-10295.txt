Hidden Symmetries and Model Reduction in Markov Decision Processes: Explained and Applied to the Multi-period Newsvendor Problem
Tobias Joosten, Karl-Heinz Küfer

Abstract
Symmetry breaking is a common approach for model reduction of Markov decision processes (MDPs). This approach only uses directly accessible symmetries such as geometric symmetries. For some MDPs, it is possible to transform them equivalently such that symmetries become accessible – we call this type of symmetries hidden symmetries . For these MDPs, hidden symmetries allow substantially better model reduction compared to directly accessible symmetries. The main idea is to reveal a hidden symmetry by altering the reward structure and then exploit the revealed symmetry by forming a quotient MDP. The quotient MDP is the reduced MDP, since it is sufficient to solve the quotient MDP instead of the original one. In this paper, we introduce hidden symmetries and the associated concept of model reduction. We demonstrate this concept on the multi-period newsvendor problem, which is the newsvendor problem considered over an infinite number of days. In this way, we show that hidden symmetries can reduce problems that directly accessible symmetries cannot, and present a basic idea of revealing hidden symmetries in multi-period problems. The presented approach can be extended to more sophisticated problems.

Markov decision process, model reduction, hidden symmetry, newsvendor problem, multi-period problem

1
Introduction

A
Markov decision process
(MDP) is a stochastic framework that models the interaction of a decision maker with an environment.
The environment is in a certain state and the decision maker has to take an action. The outcome of the taken action is a new state and a reward, which are partially random and only depend on the current state.
The objective is to compute an policy/behavior for the decision maker that maximizes the expected total discounted reward; such a policy/behavior is called
optimal
.
This computation can be done using algorithms from
dynamic programming
or
reinforcement learning
depending on what information about the MDP is given, as stated by
Sutton and Barto 2017
.
Many real-world problems are too complex to be solved in a reasonable amount of time using these algorithms.
For example,

Boutilier and Dearden 1995
discussed this for planning problems where the number of states grows exponentially with the number of variables relevant to the problem.
This is especially due to the fact that the common algorithms for solving them run in time polynomial in the size of the state space
(
Puterman 2014
)
.
Therefore, if you want to solve these problems, you have to reduce them.
An elementary and theoretical question that arises is: What options do we have to reduce MDPs?

The simplest way is to aggregate states in an MDP that can simulate each other. This general concept is called
bisimulation
.

Dean and Givan 1997
did this by using partitions of the state space that fulfill
stochastic bisimulation homogeneity
, as their framework. This property is related to the
substitution property
for finite automata
(
Hartmanis and Stearns 1966
)
, the
lumpability
for Markov chains
(
Kemeny and Snell 1976
)
and
bisimulation equivalence
for transition systems
(
Lee and Yannakakis 1992
)
.

Givan et al. 2003
did this by using
stochastic bisimulation relations
, which is a general concept for transition systems
(
Castro and Precup 2010
;
Larsen and Skou 1991
)
.
The weakness of bisimulation is that the naming of actions has a strong impact on which states are equivalent to each other.
Therefore, the naming of actions affects which states are aggregated together.
For example, the games tic-tac-toe and Go contain rotation and mirror symmetries. However, they cannot be reduced using stochastic bisimulation because of the name of the actions.
Here, the concept of
symmetries
is needed.

Symmetries are a more general concept than bisimulation.
They can be used to reduce MDPs similar to symmetry breaking in combinatorial programming
(
Walsh 2006
;
Walsh 2012
)
.
There are several approaches to model symmetries in MDPs:

Zinkevich and Balch 2001
defined particular equivalence relations to model symmetries.

Ravindran and Barto 2001
took a different approach and introduced homomorphisms between MDPs to define symmetries.
These two definitions of symmetries are equivalent.
They include only the directly accessible/visible symmetries in an MDP
and not the symmetries that are visible after an equivalent transformation of the MDP.
We refer to this type of symmetry as
visible symmetry
.
As an example,
Mahajan and Tulabandhula 2017
investigated reflection symmetry in the Cart-Pole problem or fold symmetries in grid worlds. These are classical examples for visible symmetries.

Bisimulation and visible symmetries aggregate states or state-action pairs of an MDP that have the same reward structure.
These aggregations partition the state space or set of state-action pairs.

Dean and Givan 1997
and
Ravindran and Barto 2001
stated that these partitions induce
quotient MDPs
.
This is basically about the transitions between blocks of aggregated states, since the reward structure is the same in each block.
Quotient MDPs have the property that solutions to them can be transferred to solutions to the original MDP.
This is done using a
pullback
, a mapping that maps an optimal policy in the quotient MDP to an optimal policy in the original MDP
(
Ravindran and Barto 2001
;
Givan et al. 2003
)
.
The quotient MDP in combination with the pullback forms the
reduction
of the original MDP.
In addition, there is a maximally reduced MDP, giving rise to the concept of model
minimization
, which was discussed by
Dean and Givan 1997
with respect to bisimulation and
Ravindran and Barto 2001
with respect to visible symmetries.

Overall, bisimulation and visible symmetries are our current options for reducing MDPs.
However, there are problems that have no visible symmetries but where a symmetry is hidden.
Therefore, their MDPs cannot be reduced using the above techniques.
In this paper, we introduce a new type of symmetry that captures these hidden symmetries and allows us to reduce these problems:

Hidden Symmetries

The main idea is to reveal hidden symmetries by transforming the given MDP.
Two types of transformations are allowed: a) altering the reward structure while keeping optimal policies, and b) relabeling of states and actions.
Since these transformations do not change the main structure of the MDP,
we call MDPs
equivalent
if they can be transformed into each other by these transformations.
Indeed, there is a bijective mapping between the sets of optimal policies of equivalent MDPs.

A
hidden symmetry
of an MDP
\mathcal{M}
is basically a visible symmetry of an equivalent MDP. Therefore, it is formally defined as a tuple
(\mathcal{M}^{\prime},\sim)
where
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is equivalent to
\mathcal{M}
and
\sim
is a visible symmetry of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.
This tuple naturally induces the associated quotient MDP
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}/\!\!\sim
and a pullback of optimal policies from
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}/\!\!\sim
to
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.
This means, it is sufficient to solve
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}/\!\!\sim
instead of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.
Furthermore,
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
are equivalent.
Thus, there is bijective mapping between their optimal policies.
This yields a pullback from optimal policies in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}/\!\!\sim
to optimal policies in
\mathcal{M}
.
This means, it is sufficient to solve
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}/\!\!\sim
instead of
\mathcal{M}
.
Altogether, the quotient MDP
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}/\!\!\sim
in combination with this pullback forms the reduction of the original MDP
\mathcal{M}
using the hidden symmetry
(\mathcal{M}^{\prime},\sim)
.
This approach describes the concept of
model reduction using hidden symmetries
.
Model reduction can be extended to model
minimization
, but we do not discuss this topic in this paper.

Hidden symmetries are an extension of visible symmetries because for trivial transformations hidden symmetries are basically visible symmetries.
Therefore, hidden symmetries extend the model reduction and model minimization using visible symmetries of
Zinkevich and Balch 2001
and
Ravindran and Barto 2001
.

The most important part of hidden symmetries is the transformation that alters the reward structure while keeping optimal policies. This transformation causes hidden symmetries to extend visible symmetries.
This type of transformation is called
policy invariant reward shaping
and was introduced by
Ng et al. 1999
.
The more general concept of altering the reward structure without regard to optimal policies is called
reward shaping
.
However, reward shaping is a transformation that is not suitable for hidden symmetries. This is because reward shaping in general results in a non-equivalent MDP that provides incorrect solutions to the original problem, as showed by
Randløv and Alstrøm 1998
.
In the literature, the general focus and motivation of (policy invariant) reward shaping is to speed up the learning process of reinforcement learning algorithms

(
Ng et al. 1999
;
Behboudian et al. 2021
;
Laud and DeJong 2003
;
Laud 2004
)
.
In contrast, our motivation is to reveal a hidden symmetry to reduce the given MDP.

Hidden symmetries exist in various problems.
Since factored MDPs
(
Boutilier et al. 2000
)
received some attention in the literature on model minimization in terms of bisimulation
(
Dean and Givan 1997
)
and visible symmetries
(
Givan et al. 2003
)
,
we are interested in a similar field where hidden symmetries exist.
It appears that control problems in discrete-time dynamical systems
is such a field.
These problems are basically multi-period problems that operate deterministically and are perturbed stochastically from the outside
(
Puterman 2014
)
. They arise frequently in various domains such as supply chains, logistics and other economic sectors.
A simple
problem of this problem class is the
multi-period newsvendor problem
, which is an extension of the newsvendor problem that was first introduced by
Edgeworth 1888
.
The newsvendor problem is a classical problem in the literature and is often used for analysis (see
Khouja 1999
and
Qin et al. 2011
for review articles).

Our ultimate goal is to use the multi-period newsvendor problem to show that hidden symmetries exist, how they can be revealed, and that they allow us to reduce problems.
This problem is well-suited for this demonstration because it has no visible symmetries but a hidden symmetry.

This paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
introduces basics about MDPs and the required tools needed for revealing and exploiting hidden symmetries.
This includes visible symmetries in MDPs and their quotient MDPs, reward shaping and relabeling.
Then, Section
3
introduces hidden symmetries and the main theorem of this paper that shows how to reduce an MDP using a hidden symmetry.
The concept of reducing an MDP using a hidden symmetry is then demonstrated in Section
4
on the multi-period newsvendor problem.
In addition, we briefly discuss the multi-period newsvendor problem with a 5-day cycle and show that our reduction method works here as well.
We finish with a conclusion in Section
5
.

2
Preliminaries

This section introduces the
Markov Decision Process
(MDP) in its very general form defined by
Sutton and Barto 2017
plus the tools that are needed to define hidden symmetries in MDPs.

2.1
Markov Decision Process

A
Markov Decision Process
is a 5-tuple
\mathcal{M}\doteq(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\Psi,\mathcal{R},p)
where

\mathcal{S}
is the set of states,

\mathcal{A}
is the set of actions,

\Psi\subseteq\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
is the set of admissible state-action pairs such that for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
there exists an
a\in\mathcal{A}
with
(s,a)\in\Psi,

\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is a bounded subset and is the set of rewards, and

p
is the dynamic of the MDP given as a map
p\colon\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{R}\times\Psi\to[0,1]
so that
\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S},r\in\mathcal{R}}p(s^{\prime},r,(s,a))=1
holds for all
(s,a)\in\Psi
.
The value
p(s^{\prime},r,(s,a))
describes the probability of going
to state
s^{\prime}
and receiving reward
r
when starting in state
s
and taking action
a
;
thus, we use the notation
p(s^{\prime},r|s,a)\doteq p(s^{\prime},r,(s,a))
. The state transition function is encoded in the dynamic
p
. It is given by the function

\mathcal{S}\times\Psi\to[0,1]
,
(s^{\prime},(s,a))\mapsto\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}p(s^{\prime},r|s,a)
that we also denote by the letter
p
.
Because
p(s^{\prime},(s,a))
describes the probability of going from state
s
to state
s^{\prime}
with action
a
, we have a conditional probability and use the notation

p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\doteq p(s^{\prime},(s,a))
.
Furthermore, we denote by
\mathcal{A}(s)\doteq\left\{\,a\in\mathcal{A}\,\middle|\,(s,a)\in\Psi\,\right\}
the admissible actions in the state
s\in\mathcal{S}
.
In this work, we assume that the set
\mathcal{S}
is countable and
\mathcal{A}(s)
is finite for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
.

A
policy

\pi
is a mapping from
\Psi
to the real interval
[0,1]
such that for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
the equation
\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}(s)}\pi((s,a))=1
is true.
The value
\pi((s,a))
describes the probability of taking action
a
in state
s
; therefore, we use the notation
\pi(a|s)\doteq\pi((s,a))
. Furthermore, we denote the
set of all polices in
\mathcal{M}
by
\Pi(\mathcal{M})
.
A policy
\pi
is called
deterministic
if it only takes values in
\left\{\,0,1\,\right\}
. Then, there exists for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
exactly one
a\in\mathcal{A}
with
\pi(a|s)=1
.
Therefore, we can write a deterministic policy
\pi
also as a function from
\mathcal{S}
to
\mathcal{A}
with
\pi(s)=a
for all
(s,a)\in\Psi
with
\pi(a|s)=1
.

The
trajectory
that is induced in an MDP by a policy starting in state
S_{0}
is denoted by
(S_{0},A_{0},R_{1},S_{1},A_{1},R_{2},\dots)
where
S_{i}
,
A_{i}
,
R_{i}
are random variables of state, action and reward, respectively.

As the optimality criterion we use the expected total discounted reward.
Therefore, the
value function
for a given policy
\pi
in the MDP
\mathcal{M}
is given by

V_{\pi}^{\mathcal{M}}\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
with

V_{\pi}^{\mathcal{M}}(s)\doteq\mathbb{E}_{\pi,p}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}R_{k+1}\middle|S_{0}=s\right].

The
optimal value function
is defined as
V_{*}^{\mathcal{M}}(s)\doteq\sup_{\pi\in\Pi(\mathcal{M})}V_{\pi}^{\mathcal{M}}(s)
, and an
optimal policy
is defined as a policy
\pi
with
V_{\pi}^{\mathcal{M}}(s)=V_{*}^{\mathcal{M}}(s)
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
.
We denote the
set of all optimal policies in
\mathcal{M}
by
\Pi_{*}(\mathcal{M})
.

Lastly, we define the
expected reward of taking action
a
in state
s
by

R^{\mathcal{M}}(s,a)
.

2.2
Visible symmetries in MDPs and their Quotient MDPs

This subsection defines visible symmetries in MDPs based on the definition of MDP symmetries by
Zinkevich and Balch 2001
.
The term visible symmetry is used to better distinguish between visible and hidden symmetries.
It is also shown how to create a quotient MDP out of an MDP and an associated visible symmetry, and how to pull policies from the quotient MDP back to the original MDP.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\doteq(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\Psi,\mathcal{R},p)
be an MDP.
A
visible symmetry
of
\mathcal{M}
is a tuple
\sim\doteq(\sim_{\mathcal{S}},\sim_{\Psi})
where

\sim_{\mathcal{S}}
is an equivalence relation of
\mathcal{S}
, and
\sim_{\Psi}
is an equivalence relation of
\Psi
such that

•

for all
(s_{1},a_{1})\in\Psi
and
s_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
with
s_{1}\sim_{\mathcal{S}}s_{2}
exists an action
a_{2}\in\mathcal{A}(s_{2})
with
(s_{1},a_{1})\sim_{\Psi}(s_{2},a_{2})
, and

•

for all
(s_{1},a_{1}),(s_{2},a_{2})\in\Psi
with
(s_{1},a_{1})\sim_{\Psi}(s_{2},a_{2})
it holds
s_{1}\sim_{\mathcal{S}}s_{2}
and
the statement

\displaystyle\forall X\in\mathcal{S}/\!\!\sim_{\mathcal{S}},r\in\mathcal{R}:p(X,r|s_{1},a_{1})=p(X,r|s_{2},a_{2})

with
p(X,r|s_{i},a_{i})\doteq\sum_{\tilde{s}\in X}p(\tilde{s},r|s_{i},a_{i})

is true.

Additionally, we call a visible symmetry
simple
if

(s_{1},a_{1})\sim_{\Psi}(s_{2},a_{2})
implies
a_{1}=a_{2}
.

Since a visible symmetry of an MDP can identify several actions in a state with each other, we capture the number of actions identified with each other for a state-action pair
(s,a)\in\Psi
by

N_{\Psi}(s,a)\doteq\left\lvert\left\{\,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}(s)\,\middle|\,(s,a)\sim_{\Psi}(s,a^{\prime})\,\right\}\right\rvert
.

Remark 2.2
.

Simple visible symmetries are basically stochastic bisimulations.
For these,
N_{\Psi}(s,a)
is always equal to 1.

Definition 2.3
.

A visible symmetry
\sim\doteq(\sim_{\mathcal{S}},\sim_{\Psi})
of the MDP
\mathcal{M}
induces the
quotient MDP

\mathcal{M}/\!\!\sim
, which is defined
(by abuse of notation) as the MDP
\mathcal{M}/\!\!\sim\doteq({\overline{\mathcal{S}}},\mathcal{A},{\overline{\Psi}},\mathcal{R},{\overline{p}})

with
{\overline{\mathcal{S}}}\doteq\mathcal{S}/\!\!\sim_{\mathcal{S}}
,
{\overline{\Psi}}\doteq\Psi/\!\!\sim_{\Psi}

and the dynamic

{\overline{p}}\colon{\overline{\mathcal{S}}}\times\mathcal{R}\times{\overline{\Psi}}\to[0,1]

is given by

\displaystyle{\overline{p}}([s^{\prime}],r|[(s,a)])

\displaystyle\doteq p([s^{\prime}],r|s,a)\doteq\sum_{\tilde{s}\in[s]}p(\tilde{s},r|s,a).

If we have a simple visible symmetry, the quotient MDP simplifies and we can write

{\overline{p}}([s^{\prime}],r|[s],a)
instead of
{\overline{p}}([s^{\prime}],r|[(s,a)])
and
\pi(a|[s])
instead of
\pi([(s,a)])
where
\pi
is a policy in
\mathcal{M}/\!\!\sim
.

The abuse of notation refers to the fact that by definition
{\overline{\Psi}}\subseteq{\overline{\mathcal{S}}}\times\mathcal{A}
must hold.
This is fine because by choosing representatives
[(s,a)]
for all elements in
{\overline{\Psi}}
and using the mapping
[(s,a)]\mapsto([s],a)
, we get
{\overline{\Psi}}\subseteq{\overline{\mathcal{S}}}\times\mathcal{A}
.
However, such a determination of representatives is not necessary for the quotient MDP.

Policies in the quotient MDP can be
pulled back
to policies to the original MDP. Moreover, the pullback preserves the optimality property.
Therefore, it is sufficient to solve the quotient MDP instead of the original one. We refer to a theorem of
Ravindran and Barto 2001
for this:

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\doteq(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\Psi,\mathcal{R},p)
be an MDP and
\sim
a visible symmetry of
\mathcal{M}
.
Let
\pi
be a policy in the quotient MDP
\mathcal{M}/\!\!\sim
.
The pullback of the policy
\pi
is the policy
\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi(\mathcal{M})
with

\displaystyle\pi^{\prime}\colon\Psi\longrightarrow[0,1],\quad(s,a)\longmapsto\frac{\pi([(s,a)]_{\Psi})}{N_{\Psi}(s,a)}.

If
\pi
is optimal in
\mathcal{M}/\!\!\sim
, then the pullback
\pi^{\prime}
is optimal in
\mathcal{M}
. Moreover, if
\sim
is a simple visible symmetry, the pullback simplifies to
\pi^{\prime}(s,a)=\pi(a|[s])
.

Proof.

Ravindran and Barto showed this only for finite MDPs using MDP homomorphisms
(
Ravindran and Barto 2001
, Theorem 2)
. However, their result can be extended to our kind of MDP.
Finally, by translating the quotient map

\Psi\longrightarrow\Psi/\!\!\sim,\quad(s,a)\longmapsto[(s,a)]

into an MDP homomorphism, we can apply their statement and the theorem follows.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

Note that it is sufficient to define the pullback of
\pi
such that the equation

\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\left\{\,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}(s)\,\middle|\,(s,a)\sim_{\Psi}(s,a^{\prime})\,\right\}}\pi^{\prime}(s,a^{\prime})=\pi([(s,a)]_{\Psi})

is true for all
(s,a)\in\Psi
. We use the definition above to make the pullback unique.

2.3
Policy Invariant Reward Shaping

This subsection introduces reward shaping as well as policy invariant reward shaping.
These concepts are converted into concepts of transition structures of MDPs, as they are more practical.

Reward shaping
is the technique to alter the dynamic of an MDP without changing its transition structure; thus, only the reward structure is changed.
From another point of view, this means:
Two MDPs can create each other by reward shaping if and only if they have the same transition structure (this includes that the set of states, actions and admissible state-action pairs are the same).
Therefore, we define the following property for two MDPs.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\doteq(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\Psi,\mathcal{R},p)
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\doteq(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\Psi,\mathcal{R}^{\prime},p^{\prime})
be MDPs.
The MDPs
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}

have the same transition structure
if the statement

\forall(s,a)\in\Psi,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}:p(s^{\prime}|s,a)=p^{\prime}(s^{\prime}|s,a)

is true.

Policy invariant reward shaping
is reward shaping while keeping optimal policies.
From another point of view, this means:
Two MDPs
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
can create each other by policy invariant reward shaping if and only if they have the same transition structure and the same optimal policies
\Pi_{*}(\mathcal{M})=\Pi_{*}(\mathcal{M}^{\prime})
.

2.4
Relabeling

This subsection introduces relabeling and the associated transfer of optimal policies.

Definition 2.7
.

A
relabeling
of an MDP
\mathcal{M}\doteq(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\Psi,\mathcal{R},p)
to another MDP
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\doteq(\mathcal{S}^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\Psi^{\prime},\mathcal{R},p^{\prime})
is a tuple
h\doteq(f,\left\{\,g_{s}\,\middle|\,s\in\mathcal{S}\,\right\})
such that

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\colon\mathcal{S}\longrightarrow\mathcal{S}^{\prime}

\displaystyle g_{s}

\displaystyle\colon\mathcal{A}(s)\longrightarrow\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(f(s)),\quad s\in\mathcal{S}

are bijective functions and the equation

\displaystyle p(\tilde{s},r|s,a)=p^{\prime}(f(\tilde{s}),r|f(s),g_{s}(a))

is true for all
(s,a)\in\Psi
,
\tilde{s}\in\mathcal{S}
and
r\in\mathcal{R}
.
We then say that the MDP
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is a
relabeled variant of

\mathcal{M}
.

The following statement about the relationship of optimal policies in
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
holds:

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\doteq(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\Psi,\mathcal{R},p)
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\doteq(\mathcal{S}^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\Psi^{\prime},\mathcal{R},p^{\prime})
be MDPs and
h\doteq(f,\left\{\,g_{s}\,\middle|\,s\in\mathcal{S}\,\right\})
be a relabeling of
\mathcal{M}
to
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.
The mapping

\Pi_{*}(\mathcal{M}^{\prime})\longrightarrow\Pi_{*}(\mathcal{M}),\quad\pi_{*}\longmapsto(\pi_{*}\circ h)

with
(\pi_{*}\circ h)(s,a)\doteq\pi_{*}(g_{s}(a)|f(s))
for all
(s,a)\in\Psi
is bijective.

Proof.

The proposition follows from the fact that the relabeling
h
is only a renaming of states and actions without changing any structure of the MDP.
∎

3
Hidden Symmetries

This subsection introduces hidden symmetries of MDPs and shows that it is sufficient to solve the associated quotient MDP of a hidden symmetry instead of the original MDP.

We start with the definition of equivalence for MDPs.

Definition 3.1
.

We call two MDPs
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}

equivalent
if

1.

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is just a relabeled variant of
\mathcal{M}
, or

2.

\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
have the same transition structure and the same optimal policies (the reward structure can be different!), or

3.

it exists an MDP
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
such that
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
is a relabeled variant of
\mathcal{M}
, and
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
have the same transition structure and the same optimal policies, or

4.

it exists an MDP
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
such that

\mathcal{M}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
have the same transition structure and the same optimal policies, and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is a relabeled variant of
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
.

(Statements
3
and
4
result from the combination of
1
and
2
.)

In Statement
1
,
3
and
4
, there is a relabeled variant. Thus, there is a relabeling of MDPs
h
.
In Statement
2
, we set
h
trivial.
This relabeling
h
is a relabeling of the set of state-action pairs of
\mathcal{M}
to the set of state-action pairs of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.
Therefore, we call
h

relabeling of the sets of state-action pairs in respect to the equivalent definition of MDPs
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.
In general,
h
is no relabeling of
\mathcal{M}
to
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.

Using this definition, we can introduce hidden symmetries.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be an MDP.
A tuple
(\mathcal{M}^{\prime},\sim)
is called
hidden symmetry of
\mathcal{M}
if
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
are equivalent and
\sim
is a visible symmetry of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.

Equivalent MDPs have the following relationship between their optimal policies.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\doteq(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\Psi,\mathcal{R},p)
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\doteq(\mathcal{S}^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\Psi^{\prime},\mathcal{R}^{\prime},p^{\prime})
be equivalent MDPs
and let
h\doteq(f,\left\{\,g_{s}\,\middle|\,s\in\mathcal{S}\,\right\})
be a relabeling of
\Psi
to
\Psi^{\prime}
in respect to the equivalent definition of the MDPs
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.
Then, the mapping

\Pi_{*}(\mathcal{M}^{\prime})\longrightarrow\Pi_{*}(\mathcal{M}),\quad\pi_{*}\longmapsto(\pi_{*}\circ h)

is bijective.
Recall that
(\pi_{*}\circ h)(s,a)\doteq\pi_{*}(g_{s}(a)|f(s))
holds.

Proof.

We define the map

\Phi\colon\Pi_{*}(\mathcal{M}^{\prime})\longrightarrow\Pi_{*}(\mathcal{M}),\quad\pi_{*}\longmapsto(\pi_{*}\circ h).

We have to show that
\Phi
is bijective.

Since the MDPs
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
are equivalent, one of the four statements of Definition
3.1
holds.

If Statement
1
holds, then
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is a relabeled variant of
\mathcal{M}
and
h
is a relabeling of these MDPs.
By applying Proposition
2.8
, we obtain that

\Phi
is bijective.

If Statement
2
holds, then
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
have the same optimal policies, the equation
\Psi=\Psi^{\prime}
is true and
h
is trivial.
Hence, the map
\Phi
is bijective.

Since the Statements
3
and
4
are combinations of the statements
1
and
2
,
the map
\Phi
is bijective. ∎

Now, we can state the main theorem of this paper.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\doteq(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\Psi,\mathcal{R},p)
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\doteq(\mathcal{S}^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\Psi^{\prime},\mathcal{R}^{\prime},p^{\prime})
be MDPs.
Furthermore, let
(\mathcal{M}^{\prime},\sim)
be a hidden symmetry of
\mathcal{M}
. Every optimal policy in the quotient MDP
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}/\!\!\sim
can be pulled back to an optimal policy in the MDP
\mathcal{M}
.

To be more precise:
Let
h=(f,\left\{\,g_{s}\,\middle|\,s\in\mathcal{S}\,\right\})
be a relabeling from
\Psi
to
\Psi^{\prime}
in respect to the equivalent definition of the MDPs
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.
The associated pullback of a policy
\pi\in\Pi(\mathcal{M}^{\prime}/\!\!\sim)
to a policy
\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi(\mathcal{M})
is defined as

\displaystyle\pi^{\prime}(s,a)\doteq\frac{\pi([(f(s),g_{s}(a))]_{\Psi})}{N_{\Psi}(f(s),g_{s}(a))}

for all
(s,a)\in\Psi
.
If
\pi
is optimal in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}/\!\!\sim
, then
\pi^{\prime}
is optimal in
\mathcal{M}
.

Moreover, if
\sim
is simple, the pullback simplifies to
\pi^{\prime}(s,a)\doteq\pi(g_{s}(a)|[f(s)])
.

Proof.

By combining Theorem
2.4
and Lemma
3.3
, this theorem follows.
∎

Theorem
3.4
shows that it is sufficient to solve the associated quotient MDP of an hidden symmetry instead of the original MDP.
It indirectly provides an injective mapping from
\Pi_{*}(\mathcal{M}^{\prime}/\!\!\sim)
to
\Pi_{*}(\mathcal{M})
, which completes the reduction using hidden symmetries.

4
Reduction of the Multi-Period Newsvendor Problem using a Hidden Symmetry

This section applies the concept of model reduction using hidden symmetries to the multi-period newsvendor problem.
The multi-period newsvendor problem is an easy-to-understand problem that has no visible symmetries but a hidden symmetry.
Therefore, it is well-suited to show that hidden symmetries can reduce problems that cannot be reduced by the common methods of model reduction using visible symmetries.
In addition, we present a general approach to reveal a hidden symmetry, which is the crucial part of this new concept.

This section is organized as follows. Subsection
4.1
introduces the multi-period newsvendor problem and models it as an MDP.
Subsection
4.2
applies the procedure of reduction to it and briefly states the advantages of the reduction.
Finally, Subsection
4.3
applies the same procedure of reduction to the multi-period newsvendor problem with a 5-day cycle (i.e., the deviations depend on a 5-day cycle).

4.1
Modeling the Multi-Period Newsvendor Problem

Problem Description

The multi-period newsvendor problem is a mathematical model that represents the situation of a newsvendor over an infinite number of days:
A newsvendor has to decide, based on a forecast, how many newspapers they are going to purchase for the next day. However, the real demand may differ from the forecast by a deviation and all unsold newspapers are worth nothing.
These deviations behave independent and identically at each time step.
The newspapers have a fixed purchase and sale price. The aim is now to determine the optimal purchase quantity for each day such that the newsvendor maximizes their expected profit.
So one can say, the multi-period newsvendor problem is infinitely often the newsvendor problem with different forecasts for each time step but with the same deviation distribution at each time step.

Notation

The
time steps
or
days
are denoted by
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
The
deviation
at time step
t
is denoted by
\delta_{t}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The
(\delta_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
are i.i.d. random variables given by a probability distribution such that for all
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
the value
\delta_{t}
is bounded. We denote the possible values of
\delta_{t}
by
\mathbb{Z}_{\delta}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
.
The
forecast
at time step
t
is a natural number
F_{t}
.
The forecasts are bounded such that there exists a constant
C
with
0\leq F_{t}+\delta_{t}<C
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
The
demand
at a time step
t
is given by the sum

D_{t}\doteq F_{t}+\delta_{t}.

The
quantity of newspapers to be purchased at a time step
t
is the number of
ordered newspaper
, which is denoted by
O_{t}
.
We allow only
O_{t}\in F_{t}+\mathbb{Z}_{\delta}
because that is the range of the demand at time step
t
.
As reward, we simply use the daily profit:
We denote the
purchase cost per newspaper
by
C_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and the
selling price per newspaper
by
C_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
To form a reasonable model, the purchase cost must be less than the selling price, so we request
C_{p}<C_{s}
. The profit of the newsvendor at time step
t
with order
O_{t}=a
is given by the
reward function

\displaystyle\operatorname{Rew}_{t}(a)\doteq\min\left\{\,D_{t},a\,\right\}C_{s}-aC_{p}.

To be able to consider an arbitrary deviation in the reward function, we define the function

\displaystyle\operatorname{Rew}_{t}(a,d)\doteq\min\left\{\,F_{t}+d,a)\,\right\}C_{s}-aC_{p},

where
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\delta}
describes the arbitrary deviation and
a
the order of newspaper.
Obviously the relation
\operatorname{Rew}_{t}(a)=\operatorname{Rew}_{t}(a,\delta_{t})
holds.

The objective is to specify for all time steps
t
an order quantity
O_{t}=a_{t}
such that
the expected profit
\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{Rew}_{t}(a_{t})\right]
is maximized.

Remark 4.1
.

Instead of using the daily profit as the reward function, any reward function that satisfies the following
reward function condition
can be used:

Let
t
be any time step and
\operatorname{Rew}_{t}\colon(F_{t}+\mathbb{Z}_{\delta})\times\mathbb{Z}_{\delta}\to\mathbb{R}
be the reward function at that time step of the multi-period newsvendor problem.
There exists an
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{\delta}
such that the statement

\displaystyle\forall t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:x+F_{t}\in\argmax_{a\in F_{t}+\mathbb{Z}_{\delta}}\operatorname{Rew}_{t}(a,0)

is true
and for all
t,l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
a\in F_{t}+\mathbb{Z}_{\delta}
,
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\delta}
the equation

\displaystyle\operatorname{Rew}_{t}(a,d)-\operatorname{Rew}_{t}(x+F_{t},d)=\operatorname{Rew}_{l}(a-F_{t}+F_{l},d)-\operatorname{Rew}_{l}(x+F_{l},d)

holds.

For example, reward functions that punish deficits and surpluses more individually can also be used, as long as they fulfill the condition above. This is relevant, if for instance a certain level of safety in the sale is desired.

The Problem as an MDP

The MDP of the multi-period newsvendor problem
\mathcal{M}\doteq(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\Psi,\mathcal{R},p)
is defined as follows:
The set of states is given by
\mathcal{S}\doteq\left\{\,s_{t}\,\middle|\,t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\,\right\}
, where
s_{t}
represents the current time step
t
. The admissible actions in state
s_{t}
are the possible orders of newspapers at time step
t
and thus given by
\mathcal{A}(s_{t})\doteq F_{t}+\mathbb{Z}_{\delta}
.
This implies
\mathcal{A}\doteq\bigcup_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{A}(s)
and
\Psi\doteq\left\{\,(s,a)\,\middle|\,s\in\mathcal{S},a\in\mathcal{A}(s)\,\right\}.
The set of rewards is the bounded set
\mathcal{R}\doteq\bigcup_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\operatorname{Rew}_{t}(F_{t}+\mathbb{Z}_{\delta},\mathbb{Z}_{\delta})
. For all
r\in\mathcal{R}
,
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
a\in\mathcal{A}(s_{t})
, we define the set

D(r,t,a)\doteq\left\{\,d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\delta}\,\middle|\,r=\operatorname{Rew}_{t}(a,d)\,\right\},

which contains all possible deviation such that the reward
r
is received in the state
s_{t}
under action
a
. Using this set, the dynamic
p\colon\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{R}\times\Psi\to[0,1]
is defined as

\displaystyle p(s_{l},r|s_{t},a)\doteq\begin{cases}\sum_{d\in D(r,t,a)}\Pr\left\{\,\delta_{t}=d\,\right\},&\text{if }l=t+1,\\
0,&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Hence, the state transition function
p\colon\mathcal{S}\times\Psi\to[0,1]
is given by

\displaystyle p(s_{l}|s_{t},a)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }l=t+1,\\
0,&\text{else.}\end{cases}

The objective in the multi-period newsvendor problem is to specify for all
t
an order
a_{t}
that maximizes
\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{Rew}_{t}(a_{t})\right]
. This corresponds to finding an optimal policy in
\mathcal{M}
with discount factor
\gamma=0
.
Hence, we set the discount factor to 0. Therefore, the value function is simply given by

V_{\pi}^{\mathcal{M}}(s_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi,p}\left[R_{1}\middle|S_{0}=s_{t}\right]

for all
\pi\in\Pi(\mathcal{M})
,
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
.

Proposition 4.2
.

The multi-period newsvendor problem
\mathcal{M}
has no visible symmetries in general.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathcal{M}
has a visible symmetry.
Then there exist states
s_{t}
and
s_{l}
with
t\neq l
which are symmetric to each other. This implies that
s_{t}
and
s_{l}
have the same reward function; thus
F_{t}=F_{l}
holds.
Furthermore, the visible symmetry implies that the states
s_{t+k}
and
s_{l+k}
have the same reward function for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
; thus
F_{t+k}=F_{l+k}
holds for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
This is generally not true.
∎

4.2
Applying the Procedure to Reduction

The multi-period newsvendor problem was modeled as the MDP
\mathcal{M}
and it has no visible symmetry.
This subsection shows that the MDP
\mathcal{M}
has a hidden symmetry and how to reduce the MDP with it.

Outline of the Procedure

The steps of the procedure of revealing and exploiting the hidden symmetry are as follows:

1.

Solve the
deterministic case
of the multi-period newsvendor problem, which is the problem without stochastics.
The solution is a policy, which we denote by
{\pi_{*}^{\det}}
.

2.

Do a policy invariant reward shaping using the policy
{\pi_{*}^{\det}}
.

3.

Do a relabeling of the actions using the policy
{\pi_{*}^{\det}}
.

4.

Determine a hidden symmetry.

5.

Create the related quotient MDP.

6.

Determine how an optimal policy in the quotient MDP is pulled back to the original MDP.

Step 1. Solution to the Deterministic Case

In the
deterministic case
, we assume
\delta_{t}=0
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Thus, the demand always equals the forecast
(i.e.
F_{t}=D_{t}
),
and the reward function is given by

\displaystyle\operatorname{Rew}_{t}(a)=\min\left\{\,F_{t},a\,\right\}C_{s}-aC_{p}.

for all
t\in I
,
a\in\mathcal{A}(s_{t})
.
Clearly, the reward is maximum for
a=F_{t}
.
Therefore, the optimal policy in the deterministic case is
{\pi_{*}^{\det}}\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{A}
with
{\pi_{*}^{\det}}(s_{t})=F_{t}
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. We will use this policy to align rewards and actions with it in a certain way.

Step 2. Policy Invariant Reward Shaping

We will change the reward structure of
\mathcal{M}\doteq(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\Psi,\mathcal{R},p)
as follows:
If the decision maker is in the state
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
and takes an action
a\in\mathcal{A}(s_{t})
, the new reward received should be

\displaystyle\operatorname{Rew}_{t}(a)-\operatorname{Rew}_{t}({\pi_{*}^{\det}}(s_{t}))

instead of
\operatorname{Rew}_{t}(a)
.
Since the deviation
\delta_{t}
is hidden in the function
\operatorname{Rew}_{t}\colon\mathcal{A}(s_{t})\to\mathcal{R}
, the new reward in the state
s_{t}
is modeled by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(s_{t})\times\mathbb{Z}_{\delta}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\left\{\,r_{1}-r_{2}\,\middle|\,r_{1},r_{2}\in\mathcal{R}\,\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle(a,d)

\displaystyle\longmapsto\operatorname{Rew}_{t}(a,d)-\operatorname{Rew}_{t}({\pi_{*}^{\det}}(s_{t}),d),

where this new reward occurs with probability
\Pr\left\{\,\delta_{t}=d\,\right\}
.

To define the MDP
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
with this new reward structure, we need the following sets beforehand:
For all
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
a\in\mathcal{A}(s_{t})
and
r\in\left\{\,r_{1}-r_{2}\,\middle|\,r_{1},r_{2}\in\mathcal{R}\,\right\}
we define the set

\displaystyle D^{\prime}(r,t,a)\doteq\left\{\,d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\delta}\,\middle|\,r=\operatorname{Rew}_{t}(a,d)-\operatorname{Rew}_{t}({\pi_{*}^{\det}}(s_{t}),d)\,\right\},

which contains all possible deviations for which the new reward
r
is received in the state
s_{t}
under action
a
.

We define the MDP
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\doteq(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\Psi,\mathcal{R}^{\prime},p^{\prime})
with set of rewards

\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\doteq\left\{\,r_{1}-r_{2}\,\middle|\,r_{1},r_{2}\in\mathcal{R}\,\right\}

and the dynamic
p^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\times\Psi\to[0,1]
is given by

\displaystyle p^{\prime}(s_{l},r|s_{t},a)

\displaystyle\doteq\begin{cases}\sum_{d\in D^{\prime}(r,t,a)}\Pr\left\{\,\delta_{t}=d\,\right\},&\text{if }l=t+1,\\
0,&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Hence, the state transition function
p^{\prime}(s_{l}|s_{t},a)
is equal to 1 if
l=t+1
, and 0 otherwise. Thus,
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
have the same transition structure.

Since policies are defined over the set of state-action pairs, the two MDPs
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
have the same set of policies; i.e.
\Pi(\mathcal{M})=\Pi(\mathcal{M}^{\prime})
.

Lemma 4.3
.

The two MDPs
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
have the same optimal policies.

Proof.

Because the discount factor is zero, an optimal policy
\pi
in
\mathcal{M}
maximizes

V_{\pi}^{\mathcal{M}}(s_{t})\doteq\mathbb{E}_{\pi,p}\left[R_{1}|S_{0}=s_{t}\right]
for all
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
.
Therefore, it also maximizes the term

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi,p}\left[R_{1}|S_{0}=s_{t}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{{\pi_{*}^{\det}},p}\left[R_{1}|S_{0}=s_{t}\right]

(1)

for all
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
.
By definition of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
, the term (
1
) is equal to

\mathbb{E}_{\pi,p^{\prime}}\left[R_{1}|S_{0}=s_{t}\right]\doteq V_{\pi}^{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}(s_{t})
.
Since
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
have the same set of policies, a policy is optimal in
\mathcal{M}
if and only if it is optimal in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
; i.e.
\Pi_{*}(\mathcal{M})=\Pi_{*}(\mathcal{M}^{\prime})
.
∎

In total, we did a policy invariant reward shaping because the MDPs
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
have the same transition structure and the same optimal policies.

Step 3. Relabeling of Actions

We will relabel the actions of the current MDP
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\doteq(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\Psi,\mathcal{R}^{\prime},p^{\prime})
as follows:
In a state
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
, the new action
0
shall be the optimal action of the deterministic case
{\pi_{*}^{\det}}(s_{t})
, and the new action
n
shall be optimal action of the deterministic case plus
n
, i.e.
{\pi_{*}^{\det}}(s_{t})+n
.
This means, we want to use a different denotation for the actions which is encoded by the bijective mapping

\displaystyle g_{s_{t}}\colon\mathcal{A}(s_{t})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathcal{A}(s_{t})-{\pi_{*}^{\det}}(s_{t})

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\longmapsto a-{\pi_{*}^{\det}}(s_{t})

for all
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
.
Especially,
\mathcal{A}(s_{t})-{\pi_{*}^{\det}}(s_{t})=\mathbb{Z}_{\delta}
holds.

\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}\doteq(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\Psi^{\prime},\mathcal{R}^{\prime},p^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle p^{\prime\prime}(s_{l},r|s_{t},a^{\prime})\doteq p^{\prime}(s_{l},r|s_{t},a^{\prime}+{\pi_{*}^{\det}}(s_{t})).

Since we only renamed the actions, the relabeling of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
to
\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}
is given by the tuple
h\doteq(f,\left\{\,g_{s_{t}}\,\middle|\,s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}\,\right\})
with
f\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{S}
given by
f(s_{t})=s_{t}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}
is a relabeled variant of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
, and by proposition

2.8
,
the mapping

\displaystyle\Pi_{*}(\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime})\longrightarrow\Pi_{*}(\mathcal{M}^{\prime}),\quad\pi_{*}\longmapsto{\pi}_{*}\circ h

is bijective.

In total, we relabeled the actions. This was the last transformation needed to reveal a hidden symmetry of the newsvendor problem, as we will see.
Moreover, the MDPs
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}
are equivalent and

Step 4. Determining a Hidden Symmetry

As we can see in Example
4.5
, we revealed some kind of symmetry.
We capture this by the following lemma.

Lemma 4.6
.

The equation

\displaystyle p^{\prime\prime}(s_{l},r|s_{t},a^{\prime})=p^{\prime\prime}(s_{l+k},r|s_{t+k},a^{\prime})

(2)

holds for all

t,l,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

a\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
,
r\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

If
l\neq t+1
, we get
0=0
. Let
l=t+1
.
By definition of
p^{\prime\prime}
and
p^{\prime}
, we get

p^{\prime\prime}(s_{t+1},r|s_{t},a^{\prime})\doteq p^{\prime}(s_{t+1},r|s_{t},a^{\prime}+F_{t})\doteq\sum_{d\in D^{\prime}(r,t,a^{\prime}+F_{t})}\Pr\left\{\,\delta_{t}=d\,\right\}.

If the set
D^{\prime}(r,t,a^{\prime}+F_{t})
is independent of
t
, then the equation (
2
) is true because
(\delta_{t})_{t\in I}
are i.i.d. random variables.
By definition, we have

\displaystyle D^{\prime}(r,t,a^{\prime}+F_{t})\doteq\left\{\,d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\delta}\,\middle|\,r=\operatorname{Rew}_{t}(a^{\prime}+F_{t},d)-\operatorname{Rew}_{t}(F_{t},d)\,\right\}.

We calculate

\displaystyle\operatorname{Rew}_{t}(a^{\prime}+F_{t},d)-\operatorname{Rew}_{t}(F_{t},d)=\left(\min\left\{\,d,a^{\prime}\,\right\}-\min\left\{\,d,0\,\right\}\right)C_{s}-a^{\prime}C_{p},

and thus, the set
D^{\prime}(r,t,a^{\prime}+F_{t})
is independent of
t
.
Hence, the equation (
2
) is true.
∎

In Lemma
4.6
, we see that every state behaves the same.
Therefore, we define an equivalence relation
\sim_{\mathcal{S}}
that identifies all states with each other, and an equivalence relation
\sim_{\Psi^{\prime}}
that identifies all state-action pairs with each other that have the same action.
Therefore

\displaystyle\sim_{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle\doteq\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle\sim_{\Psi^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\doteq\left\{\,((s_{1},a_{1}),(s_{2},a_{2}))\in\Psi^{\prime}\times\Psi^{\prime}\,\middle|\,a_{1}=a_{2}\,\right\}.

The tuple
\sim\doteq(\sim_{\mathcal{S}},\sim_{\Psi^{\prime}})
is a candidate for a visible symmetry.
In fact,
\sim
is a simple visible symmetry in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}
because for all
(s_{t},a_{t})\sim_{\Psi^{\prime}}(s_{l},a_{l})
the statement

\forall r\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime}:p^{\prime\prime}(\mathcal{S},r|s_{t},a_{t})=p^{\prime\prime}(\mathcal{S},r|s_{l},a_{l})

is true (because of Lemma
4.6
), and
a_{t}=a_{l}
holds.

Since the MDPs
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}
are equivalent, the tuple
(\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime},\sim)
is a hidden symmetry of
\mathcal{M}
.

Step 5. Quotient MDP

With the hidden symmetry
(\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime},\sim)
of the MDP
\mathcal{M}
, we can create the associated quotient MDP
\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}/\!\!\sim
.
The quotient MDP
\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}/\!\!\sim\doteq({\overline{\mathcal{S}}},{\overline{\mathcal{A}}},{\overline{\Psi}},{\overline{\mathcal{R}}},{\overline{p}})
is given as follows:
The set of states is
{\overline{\mathcal{S}}}\doteq\left\{\,\mathcal{S}\,\right\}
.
The set of actions is
{\overline{\mathcal{A}}}\doteq\mathbb{Z}_{\delta}
, and the set of admissible state-actions pairs is
{\overline{\Psi}}\doteq\left\{\,\mathcal{S}\,\right\}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\delta}
. The set of rewards is
{\overline{\mathcal{R}}}\doteq\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
.
The dynamic
{\overline{p}}\colon{\overline{\mathcal{S}}}\times{\overline{\mathcal{R}}}\times{\overline{\Psi}}\to[0,1]
is given by

\displaystyle{\overline{p}}(\mathcal{S},r|\mathcal{S},a)\doteq p^{\prime\prime}(s_{1},r|s_{0},a).

Hence, we created the quotient MDP
\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}/\!\!\sim
, which is very simple since it consists of only one single state.
In a sense, the quotient MDP reflects the stochastic of the original problem.

Step 6. Pullback of Optimal Policies

To complete the reduction of the multi-period newsvendor problem, we state how to pull an optimal policy in the quotient MDP
\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}/\!\!\sim
back to the original MDP
\mathcal{M}
.

By Theorem
3.4
, we get the injective mapping

\Lambda\colon\Pi_{*}(\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}/\!\!\sim)\to\Pi_{*}(\mathcal{M})
given by

\displaystyle\Lambda(\pi)(a|s_{t})\doteq\pi(a-{\pi_{*}^{\det}}(s_{t})|[s_{t}])=\pi(a-F_{t}|\mathcal{S})

for all
(s_{t},a)\in\Psi
.
Moreover, the sets
\Pi_{*}(\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}/\!\!\sim)
and
\Pi_{*}(\mathcal{M})
are not empty (this holds by
(
Puterman 2014
, Theorem 6.2.10)
).

By considering the map
\Lambda
with a deterministic policy
\pi\in\Pi_{*}(\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}/\!\!\sim)
as an argument, one gets a better intuition of the pullback of the optimal policies from
\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}/\!\!\sim
to
\mathcal{M}
. Then,
\Lambda(\pi)
is a deterministic optimal policy in
\mathcal{M}
and is given by

\Lambda(\pi)(s_{t})\doteq\pi(\mathcal{S})+{\pi_{*}^{\det}}(s_{t})=\pi(\mathcal{S})+F_{t}.

This pullback completes the reduction of the multi-period newsvendor problem using a hidden symmetry.

Advantages of the Reduction

The multi-period newsvendor problem
\mathcal{M}
has been significantly reduced:
We reduced the MDP
\mathcal{M}
, which consists of infinitely many states, to the MDP
\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}/\!\!\sim
, which consists of only one state.
Moreover, the reduced MDP
\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}/\!\!\sim
is as complex as one state of
\mathcal{M}
.

Both MDPs are
bandit problems

(
Sutton and Barto 2017
)
, and thus, it is easy to
compare them: The MDP
\mathcal{M}
is a contextual bandit problem with
\left\lvert\left\{\,F_{t}\,\middle|\,t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\,\right\}\right\rvert
multi-
armed bandits, and the MDP
\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}/\!\!\sim
is just one multi-armed bandit.
Therefore, the MDP
\mathcal{M}
needs at least
\left\lvert\left\{\,F_{t}\,\middle|\,t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\,\right\}\right\rvert
times as many steps as the MDP
\mathcal{M}
to be solved with the same bandit solving algorithm, depending on the occurrence of the values in the sequence
(F_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.

4.3
Problem Variant: The 5-day Cycle Multi-Period Newsvendor Problem

This subsection applies the same procedure of reduction as above to a variant of the multi-period newsvendor problem.
This variant has deviations that follow a cyclic behavior.
In this way, we can better understand the role of deviations for our procedure of reduction and hidden symmetries.
We will see that this cyclical property of the deviations is sufficient.

Applying the Reduction Procedure

The
5-day cycle multi-period newsvendor problem
follows a 5-day cycle for the deviations as follows.
We assume that the newsvendor works 5 days a week, say Monday to Friday, and the deviations depend on the respective days of the week.
Thus, the deviations of each set
D_{b}\doteq\left\{\,\delta_{b+5k}\,\middle|\,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\,\right\}
with
b\in\left\{\,0,1,2,3,4\,\right\}
are i.i.d. random variables.

As in the standard multi-period newsvendor problem, the optimal policy of the deterministic case is again
{\pi_{*}^{\det}}
, and the transformations are done in the same way.
Therefore, we obtain the MDP
\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}\doteq(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\Psi^{\prime},\mathcal{R}^{\prime},p^{\prime\prime})
as described in Subsection
4.2
.
The only difference is that
the equation

p^{\prime\prime}(s_{l},r|s_{t},a^{\prime})=p^{\prime\prime}(s_{l+k},r|s_{t+k},a^{\prime})

from Lemma
4.6

does not hold for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Instead the equation only holds for all
k\in 5\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
This is because the deviations of each set
D_{b}

with
b\in\left\{\,0,1,2,3,4\,\right\}
are i.i.d. random variables.

In contrast to the original multi-period newsvendor problem,
the simple visible symmetry of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}
here does not aggregate all states/days into one but all Mondays, Tuesdays, …, Fridays.
The aggregations of the state-action pairs are done in the same way as in the original multi-period newsvendor problem but with the addition that the states must have to be the same day of the week.
Mathematically, the simple visible symmetry is given by
\sim\doteq(\sim_{\mathcal{S}},\sim_{\Psi^{\prime}})
with

\displaystyle\sim_{\mathcal{S}}\doteq

\displaystyle\left\{\,(s_{t_{1}},s_{t_{2}})\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}\,\middle|\,t_{1}-t_{2}\in 5\mathbb{Z}\,\right\}

\displaystyle\sim_{\Psi^{\prime}}\doteq

\displaystyle\left\{\,((s_{t_{1}},a_{1}^{\prime}),(s_{t_{2}},a_{2}^{\prime}))\in\Psi^{\prime}\times\Psi^{\prime}\,\middle|\,t_{1}-t_{2}\in 5\mathbb{Z},a_{1}^{\prime}=a_{2}^{\prime}\,\right\}.

Due to this, the quotient MDP consists of 5 states.
The pullback of optimal policies from the quotient MDP to the original MDP is done via the same map

\Lambda
.

Gained Insights

In the normal variant, the deviations are i.i.d. random variables.
It turns out that this condition is not required for our procedure of reduction and the existence of a hidden symmetry.
Instead, a cyclic behavior of the deviations, as in this variant, is sufficient.

The cyclic behavior causes the quotient MDP to have five states instead of one.
Interestingly, the pullback is completely the same.

5
Conclusion

This paper introduced hidden symmetries, which are a new type of symmetries for MDPs.
They naturally provide a model reduction framework for MDPs.
We showed how this model reduction framework using hidden symmetries works by applying it to the multi-period newsvendor problem.
In this way, we showed that hidden symmetries can reduce problems that visible symmetries cannot reduce. This is because the multi-period newsvendor problem has no visible symmetries but a hidden symmetry.
We drastically reduced the problem by using hidden symmetries. The standard variant has a reduced MDP consisting of only one state.
This highlights the advantage of hidden symmetries over the common visible ones.

Our procedure of reduction of the multi-period newsvendor problem followed some concrete steps.
The main idea here was to equivalently transform the MDP based on a fixed policy.
The presented procedure took advantage of the fact that the discount factor is zero.
This caused that the policy invariant reward shaping step was simple.

We suppose that the approach of using a fixed policy to reveal a hidden symmetry will work for many other problems as well.
For example, we think that planning problems modeled as control problems in discrete-time dynamical systems will benefit from this approach.
Clearly, if the discount factor is greater than zero, the policy invariant reward shaping step must be adjusted.
Currently, we are working on such a generalized version of the procedure presented in this paper.
