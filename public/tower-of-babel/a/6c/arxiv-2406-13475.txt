Abstract
. We study the analogue of Kummer distribution in free probability. We prove characterization of free-Kummer and free Poisson distributions by freeness properties together with some assumptions about conditional moments. Our main tools are subordination of free multiplicative convolution and Boolean cumulants.



1.
Introduction

Recall that the Gamma law
G(a,c)
with parameters
a,c>0
is a probability measure that has the density

\frac{c^{a}}{\Gamma(a)}x^{a-1}e^{-cx}\mathbbm{1}_{(0,\infty)}(x)

and the Kummer law
K(a,b,c)
with parameters
a,c>0
,
b\in\mathbb{R}
is a probability measure that has the density

C\frac{x^{a-1}}{(1+x)^{a+b}}e^{-cx}\mathbbm{1}_{(0,\infty)}(x),

where
C=C(a,b,c)
is a normalizing constant.

It was observed in
[
3
]
by Hamza and Vallois that if
X,Y
are independent,
X\sim K(a,b,c)
and
\ Y\sim G(a+b,c)
then

U=\frac{Y}{1+X}\ \ \ \mbox{and}\ \ \ V=X(1+U)

are independent and distributed as
U\sim K(a+b,-b,c)
and
V\sim G(a,c)
. We will refer to this as HV property. In
[
7
]
the converse was proved under some technical assumptions for densities of
X
and
Y
, namely assume that
X
and
Y
are independent, positive and such that logarithms of their densities are locally integrable on
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
. If
U
and
V
are independent then
X\sim K(a,b-a,c),\ Y\sim G(b,c)
for some parameters
a,b,c>0
. Later the same authors dropped the assumption of existence of densities and replaced independence of
U
and
V
by constancy of regressions. See
[
8
]
for more details.

The HV property has its analogue for random matrices. It involves matrix version of Kummer distribution and Wishart distribution. Before we state the property we recall some basic facts. Let
\Omega
denote the open cone of real, symmetric and positive definite matrices. Then a matrix Kummer distribution
K_{n}(a,b,\Sigma)
with parameters
a>\frac{n-1}{2},b\in{\mathbb{R}},\Sigma\in\Omega
is a probability measure supported on
\overline{\Omega}
that has the density

c(\det x)^{a-\frac{n+1}{2}}(\det(I+x))^{-a-b}e^{-\left<\Sigma,x\right>}\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega}(x),

where
I
is the
n\times n
identity matrix,
\left<x,y\right>=\textrm{tr}(xy)
and
c=c(a,b,\Sigma)
is a normalizing constant. Similarly a Wishart distribution
W_{n}(b,\Sigma)
with parameters
b\in\left\{0,\frac{1}{2},1,\frac{3}{2},\ldots,\frac{n-1}{2}\right\}\cup\left(\frac{n-1}{2},\infty\right)
and
\Sigma\in\Omega
is a probability measure supported on
\overline{\Omega}
with Laplace transform given by the following formula

\mathbb{E}e^{\left<s,Y\right>}=\left(\frac{\det\Sigma}{\det(\Sigma-s)}\right)^{b},\ \mbox{for all}\ s\in\Omega\ \mbox{such that }\ \Sigma-s\in\Omega.

When
b>\frac{n-1}{2}
the Wishart distribution
W_{n}(b,\Sigma)
has the density

\frac{(\det\Sigma)^{b}}{\Gamma_{\Omega}(b)}(\det x)^{b-\frac{n+1}{2}}e^{-\left<\Sigma,x\right>}\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega}(x),

where
\Gamma_{\Omega}(b)=\pi^{n(n-1)/4}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\Gamma\left(b-\frac{k-1}{2}\right)
is the multivariate Gamma function.

The HV property for random matrices was established in
[
4
]
and it states that if
X\sim K_{n}(a,b,cI)
and
Y\sim W_{n}(a+b,cI)
with
a>\frac{n-1}{2}
,
a+b>\frac{n-1}{2}
,
c>0
are independent then
U
and
V
defined as

U=(I+X)^{-\frac{1}{2}}Y(I+X)^{-\frac{1}{2}},\ \ V=(I+U)^{\frac{1}{2}}X(I+U)^{\frac{1}{2}},

are also independent and distributed as
K_{n}(a+b,-b,cI)
and
W_{n}(a,cI)
respectively. In the same paper the converse (characterization) was established under the assumption that
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}
have positive densities.

The HV property in free probability was proved using the concept of asymptotic freeness that, roughly speaking, states that large independent random matrices behave like free random variables. To be more precise, for a
n\times n
matrix
A
let
\mu_{A}
be an empirical spectral distribution of
A
i.e.

\mu_{A}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\delta_{\lambda_{k}},

where
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
are all eigenvalues of
A
. Then the following well known theorem proved by Voiculescu
[
13
]
holds.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose for all
n\geq 1
the random matrices
X_{n}
and
Y_{n}
are independent and the distribution of
Y_{n}
is unitary invariant. Assume also that the sequences
(X_{n})_{n}
and
(Y_{n})
have almost surely an asymptotic spectral distributions, say
\mu_{X_{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\to}}\mu
and
\mu_{Y_{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\to}}\nu
weakly. Then for all polynomials
P(x,y)
of two non-commutative variables almost surely we have

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mbox{tr}\left(P(X_{n},Y_{n})\right)=\varphi(P(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})),

where
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}
are two free random variables in a non-commutative probability space
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
such that
\mathbf{X}\sim\mu
and
\mathbf{Y}\sim\nu
.

The existence of a limiting spectral distribution for Kummer matrices was established in
[
6
]
. It was proved that for
X_{n}\sim K_{n}(a_{n},b_{n},c_{n})
where parameters
a_{n}>\frac{n-1}{2}
,
b_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
c_{n}>0
are such that
\frac{2a_{n}}{n}\to\alpha>1
,
\frac{2b_{n}}{n}\to\beta
,
\frac{2a_{n}}{n}\to\gamma>0
then the sequence of empirical spectral distributions
\mu_{X_{n}}
converges almost surely to what we call in this paper the free-Kummer distribution
\mathcal{K}(\alpha,\alpha+\beta,\gamma)
. Recall also the well known fact that for Wishart matrices
Y_{n}\sim W_{n}(\lambda_{n},\alpha_{n}I)
with
\frac{2\lambda_{n}}{n}\to\lambda
and
\frac{2\alpha_{n}}{n}\to\alpha
the limiting empirical spectral distribution is the free Poisson (Marchenko-Pastur) distribution
\nu(\lambda,1/\alpha)
.

In the same paper the HV property in free probability was proved.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
6
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
be self-adjoint random variables in some
C^{*}
probability space, such that
\mathbf{X}
has the free-Kummer distribution
\mathcal{K}(\alpha,\alpha+\beta,\gamma)
and the distribution of
\mathbf{Y}
is free-Poisson
\nu(\alpha+\beta,1/\gamma,)
for some
\alpha>1
,
\gamma>0
and
\beta>1-\alpha
. If
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}
are free, then
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}
defined as

(1.1)

\mathbf{U}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{X})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{X})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\ \mbox{and}\ \mathbf{V}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U})^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U})^{\frac{1}{2}}

are free. Moreover
\mathbf{U}\sim\mathcal{K}(\alpha+\beta,\alpha,\gamma)
and
\mathbf{V}\sim\nu(\alpha,1/\gamma)
.

In this paper we show that the definition of free-Kummer distribution
\mathcal{K}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
can be extended in a consistent way also for
\alpha\in(0,1]
,
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
\gamma>0
and
prove the following characterization theorem which can be thought of as the converse statement to Theorem
1.2
. Note that freeness of of
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}
is replaced by weaker condition of constancy of (two) conditional moments of
\mathbf{V}
given
\mathbf{U}
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
(k,l)\in\left\{(1,-1),(1,2),(-1,-2)\right\}
be fixed and let
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}
be free, positive, non-degenerated and self-adjoint random variables in some
W^{*}
probability space
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
. If
\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}
are defined by (
1.1
) and

\varphi\left(\mathbf{V}^{k}\mid\mathbf{U}\right)=m_{k}\mathbf{I},\ \ \mbox{and}\ \ \varphi\left(\mathbf{V}^{l}\mid\mathbf{U}\right)=m_{l}\mathbf{I},

for some constants
m_{k},m_{l}\in{\mathbb{R}}
then
\mathbf{X}
has free-Kummer distribution
\mathcal{K}\left(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\right)
and
\mathbf{Y}
has free Poisson distribution
\nu\left(\beta,1/\gamma\right)
for some
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>0
.

It is worth to note that in Theorem
1.2
both
\alpha
and
\alpha+\beta
are assumed to be greater than
1
and consequently both
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
are invertible. Our Theorem
1.3
suggest that this theorem holds more generally for
\alpha,\alpha+\beta>0
. See Remark
5.10
for more details.

Our main technical result needed for the proof of Theorem
1.3
is calculation of the following expression

(1.2)

\varphi\left(g_{1}(\mathbf{R})\left(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{-1}g_{2}(\mathbf{R})\left(\mathbf{I}-w\mathbf{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{-1}\right)\ \textrm{for}\ \ z,w\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}_{+},

in terms of the subordination functions for free multiplicative convolution. Here
\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y}
are assumed to be free and positive non-commutative random variables and
g_{1}(\mathbf{R}),g_{2}(\mathbf{R})
belong to unital subalgebra generated by
\mathbf{R}
. This is done in Theorem
4.1
.

We also prove that (for
\beta>0
) the distribution
\mathcal{K}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
is the only distribution supported on
[0,\infty)
for which the Cauchy-Stieltjes transform
G(z)
the equation that has the form

(1.3)

z(z+1)G^{2}(z)-(\gamma z(z+1)-(\alpha-1)(z+1)+\beta z)G(z)+\gamma z+\delta=0,

for some
\delta\in{\mathbb{R}}
. See Proposition
5.5
for more details. Problems of this type where the Cauchy-Stieltjes transform emerges as solution to a certain quadratic equation that depends on unknown parameters, appear frequently in characterization problems in free probability. See for example already mentioned article
[
6
]
or
[
9
]
where characterization the free-GiG and free-Poisson distributions was studied. The proof of uniqueness in those two papers rely on the study of roots of
\Delta(z)
-the discriminant of equation for
G(z)
and the geometric argument is given to show that
\Delta(z)
has a desired form. In our paper we present new and a very simple analytic proof of this problem for
\mathcal{K}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
that avoids geometric arguments.

The organization of this paper is as follows. In chapter 2 we recall some basic facts from free probability theory that are needed to understand this article. In chapter 3 we discuss definitions and properties of the free-Kummer definition and the free-Poisson distributions. In Chapter 4 we compute the expression given by formula
1.2
. Chapter 5 is devoted to characterization theorems and the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

2.
Background and notation

In this section we introduce basic notions and facts from non-commutative probability theory that are needed to understand this paper. We assume we are given a
\textrm{C}^{\ast}
-probability space
\left(\mathcal{A},\varphi\right)
i.e.
\mathcal{A}
is a unital
\textrm{C}^{\ast}
-algebra and
\varphi:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{C}
is positive, tracial and faithful linear functional (state) such that
\varphi(\mathbf{I})=1
where
\mathbf{I}
is the unit of
\mathcal{A}
.

Elements of
\mathcal{A}
are called (non-commutative) random variables and in this paper are denoted as
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z}
etc.

2.1.
Freeness and Boolean cumulants

Freeness is one of the basic concepts that serves as the analogue of independence from classic probability theory and was introduced by Voiculescu in
[
12
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that unital subalgebras
\mathcal{A}_{1},...,\mathcal{A}_{n}
of
\mathcal{A}
are free if for every
m
and for every choice of centered random variables
\mathbf{X}_{k}\in\mathcal{A}_{i_{k}}
i.e.
\varphi(\mathbf{X}_{k})=0
,
k=1,2,\ldots,m
, such that
i_{1}\neq i_{2}\neq\ldots\neq i_{m}
we have

\varphi\left(\mathbf{X}_{1}\mathbf{X}_{2}\cdots\mathbf{X}_{m}\right)=0.

We say that random variables
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\in\mathcal{A}
are free if unital subalgebras generated by those elements are free.

The definition of freeness can be viewed as a rule for computing joint moments. For example if
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}
are free, then
\varphi(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y})=\varphi(\mathbf{X})\varphi(\mathbf{Y})
.

For a positive integer
n
let us denote
[n]=\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}
.

Definition 2.2
.

(1)

A partition
\pi
of
[n]
is a set
\pi=\left\{B_{1},...,B_{k}\right\}
of non-empty and pairwise disjoint subsets of
[n]
such that
[n]=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}B_{i}
. Elements
B_{1},\ldots,B_{k}
are called blocks of
\pi
. The set of all partition of
[n]
is denoted by
\mathcal{P}(n)
.

(2)

A partition
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(n)
is called an interval partition if every block
B
of
\pi
is of the form
[n]\cap I
for some interval
I
. The set of all
2^{n-1}
interval partitions of
[n]
is denoted by
Int(n)
.

Remark 2.3
.

The set
Int(n)
has a lattice structure induced by the so-called reversed refinement order. We say that
\pi_{1}\leq\pi_{2}
if every block of the partition
\pi_{1}
is contained in some block of
\pi_{2}
.
The partition
1_{n}
with one block
[n]
is the maximum element in this lattice.

Definition 2.4
.

For
n\geq 1
the Boolean cumulant functional
\beta_{n}:\mathcal{A}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
is defined recursively by

\forall\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n}\in\mathcal{A}:\varphi\left(\mathbf{X}_{1}\mathbf{X}_{2}\cdots\mathbf{X}_{n}\right)=\sum_{\pi\in Int(n)}\beta_{\pi}\left(\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n}\right),

where for
\pi=\left\{B_{1},\ldots,B_{k}\right\}

\beta_{\pi}\left(\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\beta_{|B_{j}|}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}:i\in B_{j}\right).

In particular
\beta_{1}=\varphi
and
\beta_{2}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})=\varphi(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y})-\varphi(\mathbf{X})\varphi(\mathbf{Y})
.

We will need two formulas involving Boolean cumulants. They can be found in
[
2
]
and
[
5
]
and were used also in
[
10
]
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Assume we are given two collections of random variables
\{\mathbf{X}_{1},\mathbf{X}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n+1}\}
and
\{\mathbf{Y}_{1},\mathbf{Y}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{Y}_{n}\}
that are free,
n\geq 1
. Then

(2.1)

\varphi\left(\mathbf{Y}_{1}\mathbf{X}_{1}\cdots\mathbf{Y}_{n}\mathbf{X}_{n}\right)=\\
=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{0=j_{0}<j_{1}<\ldots<j_{k+1}=n}\varphi\left(\mathbf{X}_{j_{1}}\cdots\mathbf{X}_{j_{k+1}}\right)\prod_{l=0}^{k}\beta_{2(j_{l+1}-j_{l})-1}\left(\mathbf{Y}_{j_{l}+1},\mathbf{X}_{j_{l}+1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{j_{l+1}-1},\mathbf{Y}_{j_{l+1}}\right)

and

(2.2)

\beta_{2n+1}\left(\mathbf{X}_{1},\mathbf{Y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n},\mathbf{Y}_{n},\mathbf{X}_{n+1}\right)=\\
=\sum_{k=2}^{n+1}\sum_{1=j_{1}<\ldots<j_{k}=n}\beta_{k}\left(\mathbf{X}_{j_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{j_{k}}\right)\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}\beta_{2(j_{l+1}-j_{l})-1}\left(\mathbf{Y}_{j_{l}},\mathbf{X}_{j_{l}+1},\mathbf{Y}_{j_{l}+1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{j_{l+1}-1},\mathbf{Y}_{j_{l+1}-1}\right).

We will also need the following formula for Boolean cumulant with product as entries.

Proposition 2.6

(Proposition 2.12 in
[
2
]
)
.

Let
1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<...<i_{m}=n
be positive integers. Then

\beta_{m}(\mathbf{X}_{1}\cdots\mathbf{X}_{i_{1}},\mathbf{X}_{i_{1}+1}\cdots\mathbf{X}_{i_{2}},...,\mathbf{X}_{i_{m-1}+1}\cdots\mathbf{X}_{n})=\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi\in Int(n)}}{{\sigma\vee\pi=1_{n}}}}\beta_{\pi}(\mathbf{X}_{1},\mathbf{X}_{2},...,\mathbf{X}_{n})

where

\sigma=\left\{\{1,2...,i_{1}\},\{i_{1}+1,...,i_{2}\},...,\{i_{m-1}+1,...,n\}\right\}\in Int(n)

and
\vee
is join of partitions (smallest upper bound of two partitions in
Int(n)
).

2.2.
Conditional expectations

Assume that
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
is a
W^{*}
probability space, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}
is a finite von Neumann algebra and

\varphi
a faithful, normal, tracial state. If
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{A}
is von Neumann subalgebra, we denote by
\varphi\left(\cdot\mid\mathcal{B}\right)
the conditional expectation with respect to
\mathcal{B}
. That is
\varphi\left(\cdot\mid\mathcal{B}\right):\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}
is a faithful, normal projection such that
\varphi\circ\left[\varphi\left(\cdot\mid\mathcal{B}\right)\right]=\varphi(\cdot)
. The map
\varphi\left(\cdot\mid\mathcal{B}\right)
is a
\mathcal{B}
-bimodule map i.e.

\varphi\left(\mathbf{Y}_{1}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}_{2}\mid\mathcal{B}\right)=\mathbf{Y}_{1}\varphi\left(\mathbf{X}\mid\mathcal{B}\right)\mathbf{Y}_{2}

for all
\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{A}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{1},\mathbf{Y}_{2}\in\mathcal{B}
. For the existence of the conditional expectation see e.g (
[
11
]
, Proposition 2.36).

The conditional expectation has the following important property

(2.3)

\varphi\left(\mathbf{X}\mid\mathcal{B}\right)=\mathbf{Y}\iff\ \ \mathbf{Y}\in\mathcal{B}\ \ \mbox{and}\ \ \forall\,\mathbf{Z}\in\mathcal{B}:\ \varphi(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Z})=\varphi(\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Z}).

2.3.
Distribution of a random variable and analytic tools.

Definition 2.7
.

The distribution of a self-adjoint random variable
\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{A}
is a uniquely determined, compactly supported, probability measure
\mu_{\mathbf{X}}
on the real line such that for all
n\geq 1

\varphi\left(\mathbf{X}^{n}\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{n}\mu_{\mathbf{X}}(dx).

We list here some analytic tools and their properties that we use in this paper.

(1)

The Cauchy-Stieltjes transform of a compactly supported measure
\mu
on the real line is the map

G_{\mu}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mu(dx)}{z-x},

defined for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\textrm{supp}(\mu)
.
It is known that the Cauchy-Stieltjes transform is an analytic map on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\textrm{supp}(\mu)
and
G_{\mu}:\mathbb{C}^{+}\to\mathbb{C}^{-}
.

If
\mathbf{X}
is a self-adjoint random variable we write
G_{\mathbf{X}}
for
G_{\mu_{\mathbf{X}}}
. Note that

G_{\mathbf{X}}(z)=\varphi\left((z\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{X})^{-1}\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mu_{\mathbf{X}}(dx)}{z-x}.

(2)

The moment transform of
\mathbf{X}
is defined for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{X}
is invertible as

M_{\mathbf{X}}(z)=\varphi\left(z\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{X})^{-1}\right).

M_{\mathbf{X}}
is an an analytic function in some neighborhood of
0
and one has

M_{\mathbf{X}}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\varphi(\mathbf{X}^{k})z^{k}.

The moment transform
M_{\mathbf{X}}
and the Cauchy-Stieltjes
G_{\mathbf{X}}(z)
transform are related by the equation

(2.4)

G_{\mathbf{X}}\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)=z(1+M_{\mathbf{X}}(z)).

(3)

For a self adjoint, positive but non-zero random variable
\mathbf{X}
one can define so called
S
-transform. The
S
-transform
S_{\mathbf{X}}
is defined in the neighborhood of
(\mu_{\mathbf{X}}(\{0\})-1,0)
in
{\mathbb{C}}
as

S_{\mathbf{X}}(z)=\frac{z+1}{z}M^{\langle-1\rangle}_{\mathbf{X}}(z),

where
M^{\langle-1\rangle}_{\mathbf{X}}
is the inverse function of
M_{\mathbf{X}}
. The
S
-transform has the following useful property

(2.5)

S_{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}}=S_{\mathbf{X}}\cdot S_{\mathbf{Y}}

when
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
are free.

(4)

The
\eta
-transform of
\mathbf{X}
is defined by

\eta_{\mathbf{X}}(z)=\frac{M_{\mathbf{X}}(z)}{M_{\mathbf{X}}(z)+1}.

In some neighborhood of
0
one has

\eta_{\mathbf{X}}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\beta_{k}(\mathbf{X},...,\mathbf{X})z^{k}.

All of these transformations uniquely determine moments of a self-adjoint random variable
\mathbf{X}
and thus also uniquely determine its distribution.

2.4.
Subordination

Let
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}
be free self-adjoint and positive random variables. In
[
1
]
Biane establish a fundamental connection between moment transforms of
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}
and
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}
. It involves two functions
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}:{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{C}}
which can be defined as unique holomorphic functions on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
satisfying
\omega_{k}{(\overline{z})}=\overline{\omega_{k}(z)}
and
\arg(\omega_{k}(z))\geq\arg(z)
for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{+}
and
k=1,2
and

(2.6)

M_{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}}(z)=M_{\mathbf{Y}}(\omega_{1}(z))=M_{\mathbf{X}}(\omega_{2}(z)).

Because of the last property
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}
are called the subordination functions.

In the framework of von Neumann algebras (
2.6
) can be stated in a more general way

(2.7)

\varphi\left((\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{X}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}^{\frac{1}{2}})^{-1}\mid\mathbf{X}\right)=\left(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{X}\right)^{-1}.

The subordination functions
\omega_{1}(z),\omega_{2}(z)
can be expanded near the origin into a Taylor series with Bolean cumulants as coefficients:

(2.8)

\displaystyle\omega_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\beta_{2n-1}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{X},\ldots,\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{X})z^{n},

(2.9)

\displaystyle\omega_{2}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\beta_{2n-1}(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\ldots,\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})z^{n}.

See
[
5
]
for details.

The
S
-transform property
S_{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}}=S_{\mathbf{X}}\cdot S_{\mathbf{Y}}
and the subordination property (
2.6
) imply also the following useful identity

(2.10)

\omega_{1}(z)\omega_{2}(z)=z\cdot\eta_{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}}(z)=z\frac{M_{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}}(z)}{M_{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}}(z)+1},\ \mbox{for}\ z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}_{+}.

3.
Free–Poisson and free–Kummer distributions and their properties

3.1.
Free-Poisson Distribution

We say that a probability measure
\nu
is free Poisson
\nu(\lambda,\gamma)
or Marchenko-Pastur distribution with parameters
\lambda\geq 0,\gamma>0
if

\nu=\max\{0,1-\lambda\}\delta_{0}+\lambda\nu_{1},

where
\nu_{1}
is a measure with density

\frac{1}{2\pi\gamma x}\sqrt{4\lambda\gamma^{2}-(x-\gamma(1+\lambda))^{2}}\ \mathbbm{1}_{\left(\gamma(1-\sqrt{\lambda})^{2},\gamma(1+\sqrt{\lambda})^{2}\right)}(x).

The
S
-transform of the free Poisson distribution
\nu(\lambda,\gamma)
is equal

S(z)=\frac{1}{\gamma z+\lambda\gamma}.

3.2.
Free-Kummer distribution

For
\alpha>0
,
\alpha\neq 1
,
\gamma>0
,
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}
the free-Kummer distribution
\mathcal{K}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
is a probability measure
\mu_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}
defined as

\mu_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}=\max\{0,1-\alpha\}\delta_{0}+\alpha\mu_{1},

where
\mu_{1}
is a measure with the density

(3.1)

\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha}\sqrt{(x-a)(b-x)}\left(\tfrac{|\alpha-1|}{x\sqrt{ab}}-\tfrac{\beta}{(1+x)\sqrt{(a+1)(b+1)}}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{(a,b)}(x),

and
(a,b)
is the unique solution of

(3.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}\gamma+\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{(a+1)(b+1)}}-\frac{|\alpha-1|}{\sqrt{ab}}&=0,\\
\gamma\frac{a+b}{2}-\alpha+1+\beta-\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{(a+1)(b+1)}}&=2,\end{array}\right.

satisfying
0<a<b
.
The free-Kummer distribution was defined for
\alpha>1
in
[
6
]
as a limit of empirical spectral distribution of Kummer matrices. The above definition extends the definition from that paper for
\alpha\in(0,1)
and one can easily check that for
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
\gamma>0
we have

\mu_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}=(1-\alpha)\delta_{0}+\alpha\mu_{\frac{1}{\alpha},\frac{\beta}{\alpha},\frac{\gamma}{\alpha}}.

We note here that in
[
6
]
the parameter
\alpha
was assumed to be positive but not necessarily greater than
1
. One can see that the condition
\alpha\leq 1
contradicts some assumptions made in that paper and the definition of free-Kummer distribution does not work in this case. To reflect this fact we changed formulation of Theorem
1.2
by adding assmuptions
\alpha,\alpha+\beta>1
.

We will show now how to extend the definition of
\mathcal{K}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
for
\alpha=1
. The reader can find more details in the Appendix.

For
\alpha=1
and
\beta<1-(1+\sqrt{\gamma})^{2}
the above definition still works and in this case
\mathcal{K}(1,\beta,\gamma)
is the distribution of
X-1
where
X
has the free-Poisson distribution
X\sim\nu(1-\beta,1/\gamma)
.

For
\alpha=1
and
\beta\geq 1-(1+\sqrt{\gamma})^{2}
we define
\mathcal{K}(1,\beta,\gamma)

to be a probability measure which has the density

(3.3)

\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{x(b-x)}\left(\tfrac{\sigma}{x}-\tfrac{\beta}{(1+x)\sqrt{b+1}}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{(0,b)}(x),

where
\sigma=\gamma+\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{b+1}}
and
b
is the unique positive solution of

(3.4)

\gamma\frac{b}{2}+\beta-\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{(b+1)}}=2.

The Cauchy-Stieltjes transform of
\mathcal{K}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
for
\alpha>1
was calculated in
[
6
]
and is given by the following formula

(3.5)

G_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}(z)=\tfrac{1}{2}\left[\gamma-\tfrac{\alpha-1}{z}+\tfrac{\beta}{z+1}+\sqrt{(z-a)(z-b)}\left(\tfrac{\beta}{(z+1)\sqrt{(a+1)(b+1)}}-\tfrac{|\alpha-1|}{z\sqrt{ab}}\right)\right].

For
0<\alpha<1
we have
G_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}(z)=\frac{1-\alpha}{z}+\alpha G_{\frac{1}{\alpha},\frac{\beta}{\alpha},\frac{\gamma}{\alpha}}(z)
and in consequence the formula (
3.5
) remains valid also for
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

For
\alpha=1
,
\beta>1-(1+\sqrt{\gamma})^{2}
,
\gamma>0
one can check that

G_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}(z)=\tfrac{1}{2}\left[\gamma+\tfrac{\beta}{z+1}+\sqrt{z(z-b)}\left(\tfrac{\beta}{(z+1)\sqrt{b+1}}-\tfrac{\sigma}{z}\right)\right].

Remark 3.1
.

Using (
3.2
) for
\alpha\neq 1
or (
3.4
) when
\alpha=1
one can check that
G=G_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}
solves the following quadratic equation

(3.6)

z(z+1)G^{2}(z)-(\gamma z(z+1)-(\alpha-1)(z+1)+\beta z)G(z)+\gamma z+\delta=0.

Later we will show that, under some conditions, this equation characterizes free-Kummer distribution. See Proposition
5.5
for more details.

4.
The main technical results

Let
\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{Y}
be free, self-adjoint and positive random variables from some
W^{*}
probability space
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
. Let
g_{1}(\mathbf{R})
and
g_{2}(\mathbf{R})
be two elements in the unital subalgebra generated by
\mathbf{R}
. We also denote
\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}
. The goal of this section is to evaluate the function following function in terms of subordination functions

(4.1)

k(z,w)=\varphi\left(g_{1}(\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}g_{2}(\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{I}-w\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right).

Theorem 4.1
.

For
z,w
in some neighborhood of
0
in
{\mathbb{C}}
we have

k(z,w)=k_{1}(z,w)+k_{2}(z,w),

with

\displaystyle k_{1}(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi\left(g_{1}(\mathbf{R})g_{2}(\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(w)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right),

\displaystyle k_{2}(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\left(w\omega_{2}(z)-z\omega_{2}(w)\right)\left(\omega_{2}(z)-\omega_{2}(w)\right)}{\left(M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-M_{\mathbf{U}}(w)\right)(z-w)}A_{1}(z,w)A_{2}(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{w\omega_{2}(z)-z\omega_{2}(w)}{z-w}\frac{A_{1}(z,w)A_{2}(z,w)}{B(z,w)},

where

\displaystyle A_{k}(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi\left(\mathbf{R}g_{k}(\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(w)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right),\ \ k=1,2,

\displaystyle B(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi\left(\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(w)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)=\frac{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-M_{\mathbf{U}}(w)}{\omega_{2}(z)-\omega_{2}(w)}.

Here
\omega_{2}(z)
is the subordination function satisfying
M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)=M_{\mathbf{R}}(\omega_{2}(z))
.

Remark 4.2
.

The function
k(z,w)
is continuous for
(z,w)\in({\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}_{+})^{2}
and analytic in both
z
and
w
. For fixed
w
in some neighborhood of
0
the function
k_{1}(z,w)+k_{2}(z,w)
is meromorphic in
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and agrees with
k(z,w)
in the neighborhood of
0
.
Uniqueness of analytic continuation shows that
k_{1}(\cdot,w)+k_{2}(\cdot,w)
has only removable singularities and
k(z,w)=k_{1}(z,w)+k_{2}(z,w)
holds for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. Fixing
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and repeating the argument shows that
k=k_{1}+k_{2}
on
({\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}_{+})^{2}.

An immediate consequence of Theorem
4.1
and property (
2.3
) is the following Corollary.

Corollary 4.3
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
4.1

\varphi\left((\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}g(\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{I}-w\mathbf{U})^{-1}\mid\mathbf{R}\right)=g(\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(w)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\\
+\frac{w\omega_{2}(z)-z\omega_{2}(w)}{z-w}\frac{A(z,w)}{B(z,w)}\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(w)\mathbf{R})^{-1},

where

\displaystyle A(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi\left(\mathbf{R}g(\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(w)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right),

\displaystyle B(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi\left(\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(w)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right).

4.1.
Proof of Theorem
4.1

The proof will be divided into a series of lemmas and propositions.
Before we start we introduce some notation. Let
h(\mathbf{R})
belong to the unital subalgebra generated by
\mathbf{R}
. We define

(4.2)

\displaystyle\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\beta_{k+1}\left(h(\mathbf{R}),\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},\ldots,\mathbf{R}}_{k}\right)z^{k},

(4.3)

\displaystyle\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(z,w)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l\geq 0}\beta_{k+l+1}\left(\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}}_{l},h(\mathbf{R}),\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}}_{k}\right)z^{l}w^{k}.

Both
\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(z)
and
\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(z,w)
are well-defined near the origin. The function
\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(z)
was defined in
[
10
]
and the authors showed a method of calculating this function when

is a holomorphic function of
\mathbf{R}
.

The function
\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(z,w)
generalizes the function
\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(z)
in the sense that
\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(z,0)=\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(z)
. Since Boolean cumulants are invariant under reflections i.e.
\beta_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{1},\mathbf{X}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n})=\beta_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{n},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{2},\mathbf{X}_{1})
then it is clear that
\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(z,w)=\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(w,z)
. Lemma
4.9
shows that
\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(z,w)
can be expressed in terms of
\eta_{\mathbf{R}}^{h}(z)
, furthermore Lemma
4.10
shows that

\displaystyle\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\varphi\left(h(\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)}{\varphi\left((\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)},

\displaystyle\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\varphi\left(h(\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{R})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-w\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)}{\varphi\left((\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)\varphi\left((\mathbf{I}-w\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)},

which allows us to extend both functions analytically for
z,w\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and provides a way of calculating them at least when
h(\mathbf{R})
is some nice function of
\mathbf{R}
.

We will also use the following notation for Boolean cumulants that will frequently appear in the proof:

\displaystyle\beta_{n}(\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{n}(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Y},...,\mathbf{Y}),

\displaystyle y_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{2n+1}\left(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y},\ldots,\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y}\right),

\displaystyle r_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{2n+1}\left(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{R},\ldots,\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{R}\right),

\displaystyle s^{h}_{m,n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{2m+2n+3}(\underbrace{\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y},...,\mathbf{Y}}_{2m+1},h(\mathbf{R}),\underbrace{\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y},...,\mathbf{Y}}_{2n+1}),

\displaystyle t^{h}_{m,n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{2m+2n+1}(\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y},...,\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y}}_{2m},h(\mathbf{R}),\underbrace{\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{R}}_{2n}).

For technical reasons it will be easier to work with

f(z,w)=\varphi\left(g_{1}(\mathbf{R})z\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}g_{2}(\mathbf{R})w\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{I}-w\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right).

Since
(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}=\mathbf{I}+z\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}
, the difference
k(z,w)-f(z,w)
is equal

\varphi\left(g_{1}(\mathbf{R})g_{2}(\mathbf{R})\right)+\varphi\left(g_{1}(\mathbf{R})z\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}g_{2}(\mathbf{R})\right)+\varphi\left(g_{1}(\mathbf{R})g_{2}(\mathbf{R})w\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{I}-w\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right)

and can be easily calculated using (
2.7
). See the proof of Lemma
4.4
for more details.

For
z,w
is some neighborhood of
0
in
{\mathbb{C}}
we can write

f(z,w)=\sum_{m,n\geq 1}\varphi\left(g_{1}(\mathbf{R})\mathbf{U}^{m}g_{2}(\mathbf{R})\mathbf{U}^{n}\right)z^{m}w^{n}.

For any
m,n\geq 1
by traciality of
\varphi
we have

\displaystyle\varphi\left(g_{1}(\mathbf{R})\mathbf{U}^{m}g_{2}(\mathbf{R})\mathbf{U}^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi\left(g_{1}(\mathbf{R})\left(\mathbf{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{m}g_{2}(\mathbf{R})\left(\mathbf{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi\left(\underbrace{\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Y}\cdots\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Y}}_{2m-1}h_{2}(\mathbf{R})\underbrace{\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Y}\cdots\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Y}}_{2n-1}h_{1}(\mathbf{R})\right),

where
h_{k}(\mathbf{R})=\mathbf{R}g_{k}(\mathbf{R})
for

.
Therefore

f(z,w)=\sum_{m,n\geq 1}\varphi\left(\underbrace{\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Y}\cdots\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Y}}_{2m-1}h_{2}(\mathbf{R})\underbrace{\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Y}\cdots\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Y}}_{2n-1}h_{1}(\mathbf{R})\right)z^{m}w^{n}.

To evaluate

we apply formula (
2.1
) for the following two free collections of random variables

\{\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}}_{m-1},h_{2}(\mathbf{R}),\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}}_{n-1},h_{1}(\mathbf{R})\}\ \mbox{and}\ \{\underbrace{\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Y},..,\mathbf{Y}}_{m+n}\}.

In order to apply this formula we have to consider two cases whether the sequence
1\leq j_{1}<\ldots<j_{k+1}=m+n
appearing in formula (

) contains
m
or not. (This is due to the fact that

\varphi\left(\underbrace{\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Y}\cdots\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Y}}_{2m-1}h_{2}(\mathbf{R})\underbrace{\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Y}\cdots\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Y}}_{2n-1}h_{1}(\mathbf{R})\right)=S_{m,n}^{(1)}+S_{m,n}^{(2)},

where
S_{m,n}^{(1)}
is a sum over all sequences
1\leq j_{1}<\ldots<j_{k+1}=m+n
containing
m
and
S_{m,n}^{(2)}
contains all other elements.

Explicitly we have

(4.4)

S_{m,n}^{(1)}=\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\varphi\left(h_{1}(\mathbf{R})h_{2}(\mathbf{R})\mathbf{R}^{k+l}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{0}+\cdots+m_{l}=m-1-l\\
n_{0}+\cdots+n_{k}=n-1-k\end{subarray}}y_{m_{0}}y_{m_{1}}\cdots y_{m_{l}}\cdot y_{n_{0}}y_{n_{1}}\cdots y_{n_{k}},

(4.5)

S_{m,n}^{(2)}=\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\varphi\left(h_{1}(\mathbf{R})\mathbf{R}^{k+l}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{0}+\cdots+m_{l}=m-1-l\\

The indices
m_{0},..,m_{l},n_{0},...,n_{k}
appearing in the most inner sums are assumed to be non-negative integers.

We can now write
f(z,w)=\sum_{m,n\geq 1}S^{(1)}_{m,n}z^{m}w^{n}+\sum_{m,n\geq 1}S^{(2)}_{m,n}z^{m}w^{n}
. In the next two lemmas we calculate both series.

Lemma 4.4
.

For
z,w
in some neighborhood of
0
in
{\mathbb{C}}

is equal

(4.6)

f_{1}(z,w)=\omega_{2}(z)\omega_{2}(w)\varphi\left(h_{1}(\mathbf{R})h_{2}(\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(w)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right).

Using (
4.4
), changing variables and the order of summation several times yields

\displaystyle f_{1}(z,w)=\sum_{m,n\geq 1}S^{(1)}_{mn}z^{m}w^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m,n\geq 1}z^{m}w^{n}\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\varphi\left(h_{1}(\mathbf{R})h_{2}(\mathbf{R})\mathbf{R}^{k+l}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{0}+\cdots+m_{l}=m-1-l\\

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k,l\geq 0}\varphi\left(h_{1}(\mathbf{R})h_{2}(\mathbf{R})\mathbf{R}^{k+l}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\geq l+1\\
n\geq k+1\end{subarray}}z^{m}w^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{0}+\cdots+m_{l}=m-1-l\\

\displaystyle f_{1}(z,w)

\displaystyle\sum_{m,n\geq 1}z^{m}w^{n}\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\varphi\left(h_{1}(\mathbf{R})\mathbf{R}^{k+l}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{0}+\cdots+m_{l}=m-1-l\\

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l,k\geq 0}\varphi\left(h_{1}(\mathbf{R})\mathbf{R}^{k+l}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\geq l+1\\
n\geq k+1\end{subarray}}z^{m}w^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{0}+\cdots+m_{l}=m-1-l\\
n_{0}+\cdots+n_{k}=n-1-k\end{subarray}}y_{m_{0}}y_{m_{1}}\cdots y_{m_{l-1}}\cdot s^{h_{2}}_{m_{l},n_{0}}\cdot y_{n_{1}}\cdots y_{n_{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l,k\geq 0}\varphi\left(h_{1}(\mathbf{R})\mathbf{R}^{k+l}\right)z^{l+1}w^{k+1}\sum_{m,n\geq 0}z^{m}w^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{0}+\cdots+m_{l}=m\\
n_{0}+\cdots+n_{k}=n\end{subarray}}y_{m_{0}}y_{m_{1}}\cdots y_{m_{l-1}}\cdot s^{h_{2}}_{m_{l},n_{0}}\cdot y_{n_{1}}\cdots y_{n_{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l,k\geq 0}\varphi\left(h_{1}(\mathbf{R})\mathbf{R}^{k+l}\right)z^{l}w^{k}G(z,w)B(z)^{l}B(w)^{k},

where as in the previous proof

B(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}y_{n}z^{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\beta_{2n+1}(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y},...,\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y})z^{n}=\frac{\omega_{2}(z)}{z}

and

\displaystyle G(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle zw\sum_{m,n\geq 0}s^{h_{2}}_{m,n}z^{m}w^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m,n\geq 0}\beta_{2m+2n+3}\left(\underbrace{\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y},....,\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y}}_{2m+1},h_{2}(\mathbf{R}),\underbrace{\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y},....,\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y}}_{2n+1}\right)z^{m+1}w^{n+1}.

Hence

\displaystyle k_{2}(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G(z,w)\sum_{l,k\geq 0}\varphi\left(h_{1}(\mathbf{R})\mathbf{R}^{k+l}\right)\omega_{2}(z)^{l}\omega_{2}(w)^{l}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G(z,w)\varphi\left(h_{1}(\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(w)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right).

∎

The next step is to find the closed form for the series

(4.9)

G(z,w)=\sum_{m,n\geq 1}\beta_{2m+2n+1}\left(\underbrace{\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y},....,\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y}}_{2m-1},h(\mathbf{R}),\underbrace{\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y},....,\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y}}_{2n-1}\right)z^{m}w^{n}.

As always
h(\mathbf{R})
is a fixed element in the unital subalgebra generated by
\mathbf{R}
. It turns out that
G(z,w)
is closely related to the following double series

(4.10)

H(z,w)=\sum_{m,n\geq 0}\beta_{2m+2n+1}\left(\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y},....,\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y}}_{2m},h(\mathbf{R}),\underbrace{\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y},....,\mathbf{R}}_{2n}\right)z^{m}w^{n}

so we consider both at the same time.

We start with the following lemma which shows that
G(z,w)
can be expressed in terms of
H(z,w)
and the subordination functions.

Lemma 4.6
.

For
z,w
in some neighborhood of
0
in
{\mathbb{C}}
such that
\omega_{1}(z)\neq\omega_{1}(w)

G(z,w)=H(z,w)\frac{w\omega_{2}(z)-z\omega_{2}(w)}{\omega_{1}(z)-\omega_{1}(w)}

where
\omega_{1}(z),\omega_{2}(z)
are the subordination functions satisfying
M_{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Y}}(z)=M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)=M_{\mathbf{Y}}(\omega_{1}(z))=M_{\mathbf{R}}(\omega_{2}(z))
.

Proof.

We start by applying formula (
2.2
) to the following free collections of random variables

\{\underbrace{\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Y},..,\mathbf{Y}}_{m+n}\}\ \mbox{and}\ \{\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}}_{m-1},h(\mathbf{R}),\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}}_{n-1}\}.

Thus for
m,n\geq 1
we obtain

\beta_{2m+2n-1}(\underbrace{\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y},....,\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y}}_{2m-1},h(\mathbf{R}),\underbrace{\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y},....,\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y}}_{2n-1})\\
=\sum_{l=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\beta_{k+l}(\mathbf{Y})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}+m_{2}+...+m_{l}=m-l\\
n_{1}+n_{2}+...+n_{k}=n-k\end{subarray}}r_{m_{1}}\cdots r_{m_{l-1}}\cdot t^{h}_{m_{l},n_{1}}\cdot r_{n_{2}}\cdots r_{n_{l}}

where appropriate products of
r
’s are assumed to be
1
if either
k=1
or
l=1
. Application of the above formula and standard power series manipulations yield

\displaystyle G(z,w)=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m,n\geq 1}z^{m}w^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\beta_{k+l}(\mathbf{Y})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}+m_{2}+...+m_{l}=m-l\\
n_{1}+n_{2}+...+n_{k}=n-k\end{subarray}}r_{m_{1}}\cdots r_{m_{l-1}}\cdot t^{h}_{m_{l},n_{1}}\cdot r_{n_{2}}\cdots r_{n_{l}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k,l\geq 1}\beta_{k+l}(\mathbf{Y})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=l\\
n=k\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}+m_{2}+...+m_{l}=m-l\\
n_{1}+n_{2}+...+n_{k}=n-k\end{subarray}}r_{m_{1}}\cdots r_{m_{l-1}}\cdot t^{h}_{m_{l},n_{1}}\cdot r_{n_{2}}\cdots r_{n_{l}}\right)z^{m}w^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k,l\geq 1}\beta_{k+l}(\mathbf{Y})z^{l}w^{k}\sum_{m,n\geq 0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}+m_{2}+...+m_{l}=m\\
n_{1}+n_{2}+...+n_{k}=n\end{subarray}}r_{m_{1}}\cdots r_{m_{l-1}}\cdot t^{h}_{m_{l},n_{1}}\cdot r_{n_{2}}\cdots r_{n_{l}}\right)z^{m}w^{n}.

Note that the inner ”double” sum equals

A(z)^{l-1}A(w)^{k-1}H(z,w),

where by (
2.8
)

A(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}r_{n}z^{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\beta_{2n+1}(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{R})z^{n}=\frac{\omega_{1}(z)}{z}

and
H(z,w)=\sum_{m,n\geq 0}t^{h}_{m,n}z^{m}w^{n}
which is (
4.10
). Hence

\displaystyle G(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\beta_{k+l}(\mathbf{Y})z^{l}w^{k}A(z)^{l-1}A(w)^{k-1}H(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle zwH(z,w)\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\beta_{k+l}(\mathbf{Y})\omega_{1}(z)^{l-1}\omega_{1}(w)^{k-1}.

Now if
\omega_{1}(z)\neq\omega_{1}(w)
then

\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\beta_{k+l}(\mathbf{Y})\omega_{1}(z)^{k-1}\omega_{1}(z)^{l-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\beta_{n+2}(\mathbf{Y})\frac{\omega_{1}(z)^{n+1}-\omega_{1}(w)^{n+1}}{\omega_{1}(z)-\omega_{1}(w)}.

The rest is straightforward:

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\beta_{n+2}(\mathbf{Y})z^{n+1}=\frac{\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\beta_{n}(\mathbf{Y})z^{n}}{z}=\frac{\eta_{\mathbf{Y}}(z)}{z}-\varphi(\mathbf{Y}).

Hence

G(z,w)=zwH(z,w)\frac{\omega_{1}(w)\eta_{\mathbf{Y}}(\omega_{1}(z))-\omega_{1}(z)\eta_{\mathbf{Y}}(\omega_{1}(w))}{\omega_{1}(z)\omega_{1}(w)(\omega_{1}(z)-\omega_{1}(w))}.

To finish the proof we apply formula (
2.10
) that can be written as
\omega_{1}(z)\omega_{2}(z)=\eta_{\mathbf{Y}}(\omega_{1}(z))z
to get

zw\left[\omega_{1}(w)\eta_{\mathbf{Y}}(\omega_{1}(z))-\omega_{1}(z)\eta_{\mathbf{Y}}(\omega_{1}(w))\right]=\omega_{1}(z)\omega_{1}(w)\left(w\omega_{2}(z)-z\omega_{2}(w)\right).

∎

We will show now that similar calculation yields another formula that links
H(z,w)
with
G(z,w)
. Before we state the lemma we will express

\displaystyle H(z,w)=\sum_{m,n\geq 0}\beta_{2m+2n+1}\left(\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y},....,\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y}}_{2m},h(\mathbf{R}),\underbrace{\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y},....,\mathbf{R}}_{2n}\right)z^{m}w^{n},

as
H(z,w)=H_{1}(z,w)+C(z)+C(w)-\varphi(h(\mathbf{R}))

where

\displaystyle H_{1}(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m,n\geq 1}\beta_{2m+2n+1}\left(\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y},....,\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y}}_{2m},h(\mathbf{R}),\underbrace{\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y},....,\mathbf{R}}_{2n}\right)z^{m}w^{n},

\displaystyle C(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m\geq 0}\beta_{2m+1}\left(h(\mathbf{R}),\underbrace{\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y},....,\mathbf{R}}_{2m}\right)z^{m}.

We do this because it is easier to work with
H_{1}(z,w)
rather than
H(z,w)
. The reason is that
h(\mathbf{R})
is always the middle term in Boolean cumulant appearing in
H_{1}(z,w)
while it is not always the case for
H(z,w)
. The function
C(z)
is equal

(4.11)

C(z)=\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(\omega_{2}(z)),

where
\eta_{\mathbf{R}}^{h}(z)
is given by the series form formula (
4.2
). This was shown in
[
10
]
using (
2.2
) so we skip the details.

Lemma 4.7
.

For
z,w
in some neighborhood of
0
in
{\mathbb{C}}
such that
\omega_{2}(z)\neq\omega_{2}(w)

H(z,w)=\frac{G(z,w)}{zw}\frac{w\omega_{1}(z)-z\omega_{1}(w)}{\omega_{2}(z)-\omega_{2}(w)}+\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(\omega_{2}(z),\omega_{2}(w)),

where
\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(z,w)
is given by formula (
4.3
).

Proof.

We start by applying formula (
2.2
) to
t^{h}_{m,n}
with
m,n\geq 1
to get

t^{h}_{m,n}=\beta_{2m+2n+1}\left(\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y},....,\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y}}_{2m},h(\mathbf{R}),\underbrace{\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Y},....,\mathbf{R}}_{2n}\right)=E_{m,n}+F_{m,n}

where

\displaystyle E_{m,n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq l\leq m\\
1\leq k\leq n\end{subarray}}\beta_{k+l+1}\left(\underbrace{\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}}_{l},h(\mathbf{R}),\underbrace{\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}}_{k}\right)\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}+\cdots+m_{l}=m-l\\
n_{1}+\cdots+n_{k}=n-k\end{subarray}}y_{m_{1}}\cdots y_{m_{l}}\cdot y_{n_{1}}\cdots y_{m_{k}},

\displaystyle F_{m,m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq l\leq m\\
1\leq k\leq n\end{subarray}}\beta_{k+l}(\mathbf{R})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}+m_{2}+...+m_{l}=m-l\\
n_{1}+n_{2}+...+n_{k}=n-k\end{subarray}}y_{m_{1}}\cdots y_{m_{l-1}}\cdot s^{h}_{m_{l},n_{1}}\cdot y_{n_{2}}\cdots y_{m_{k}}.

Let us denote

E(z,w)=\sum_{m,n\geq 1}E_{m,n}z^{m}w^{n},\ \ \mbox{and}\ \ F(z,w)=\sum_{m,n\geq 1}F_{m,n}z^{m}w^{n}.

Consequently
H(z,w)=E(z,w)+F(z,w)+C(z)+C(w)-\varphi(h(\mathbf{R}))
.
The above formulas for
E_{m,n},F_{m,n}
allow us to evaluate
E(z,w)
and
F(z,w)
. One can show that

\displaystyle E(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k,l\geq 1}\beta_{k+l+1}\left(\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}}_{l},h(\mathbf{R}),\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}}_{k}\right)\omega_{2}(z)^{l}\omega_{2}(w)^{k},

\displaystyle F(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{G(z,w)}{zw}\frac{w\omega_{1}(z)-z\omega_{1}(w)}{\omega_{2}(z)-\omega_{2}(w)},

where
G(z,w)=\sum_{m,n\geq 0}s^{h}_{m,n}z^{m+1}w^{n+1}
is the function from (
4.9
). The proof of these identities is similar to the ones from Lemma
4.6
and we skip it.

To finish the proof note that (
4.11
) implies that
E(z,w)+C(z)+C(w)-\varphi(h(\mathbf{R}))
is exactly
\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(\omega_{2}(z),\omega_{2}(w))
.
∎

Combining Lemmas
4.6
and
4.7
yields the following Corollary.

Corollary 4.8
.

The functions
G(z,w),H(z,w)
given by formulas (
4.9
) and (
4.10
) are equal

G(z,w)=\frac{\left(w\omega_{2}(z)-z\omega_{2}(w)\right)\left(\omega_{2}(z)-\omega_{2}(w)\right)}{\left(\eta_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-\eta_{\mathbf{U}}(w)\right)(z-w)}\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(\omega_{2}(z),\omega_{2}(w)),

H(z,w)=\frac{(\omega_{1}(z)-\omega_{1}(w))\left(\omega_{2}(z)-\omega_{2}(w)\right)}{\left(\eta_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-\eta_{\mathbf{U}}(w)\right)(z-w)}\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(\omega_{2}(z),\omega_{2}(w)).

Proof.

Lemmas
4.6
and
4.7
imply that
G(z,w)
and
H(z,w)
satisfy the following system of equations

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}G(z,w)&=H(z,w)\frac{w\omega_{2}(z)-z\omega_{2}(w)}{\omega_{1}(z)-\omega_{1}(w)},\\
H(z,w)&=\frac{G(z,w)}{zw}\frac{w\omega_{1}(z)-z\omega_{1}(w)}{\omega_{2}(z)-\omega_{2}(w)}+\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(\omega_{2}(z),\omega_{2}(w)).\end{array}\right.

Hence

G(z,w)\left(1-\frac{1}{zw}\frac{w\omega_{2}(z)-z\omega_{2}(w)}{\omega_{1}(z)-\omega_{1}(w)}\frac{w\omega_{1}(z)-z\omega_{1}(w)}{\omega_{2}(z)-\omega_{2}(w)}\right)=\frac{w\omega_{2}(z)-z\omega_{2}(w)}{\omega_{1}(z)-\omega_{1}(w)}\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(\omega_{2}(z),\omega_{2}(w)).

Simple algebra and (
2.10
) i.e.
\omega_{1}(z)\omega_{2}(z)=z\eta_{\mathbf{U}}(z)
show that the expression in the parenthesis is equal to

\frac{(z-w)(\eta_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-\eta_{\mathbf{U}}(w))}{(\omega_{1}(z)-\omega_{1}(w))(\omega_{2}(z)-\omega_{2}(w))}.

This ends the proof.
∎

In the next two lemmas we will show that
\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(\cdot,\cdot)
can be expressed in terms of
\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(\cdot)
and
\eta_{\mathbf{R}}(\cdot)
. We also give explicit formulas for both functions.

Lemma 4.9
.

For
z\neq w
in some neighborhood of
0
in
{\mathbb{C}}
the following identity holds

(4.13)

\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(z,w)=\frac{z\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(z)-w\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(w)}{z-w}+\frac{\eta_{\mathbf{R}}(z)w\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(w)-\eta_{\mathbf{R}}(w)z\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(z)}{z-w}.

Proof.

Let
g(\mathbf{R})
be any element in the unital subalgebra generated by
\mathbf{R}
and let
h(\mathbf{R})=\mathbf{R}\cdot g(\mathbf{R})
. Then

\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(z,w)=\sum_{k,l\geq 0}\beta_{k+l+1}\left(\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}}_{l},\mathbf{R}g(\mathbf{R}),\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}}_{k}\right)z^{l}w^{k}.

Applying Proposition
2.6
we get

\beta_{k+l+1}\left(\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}}_{l},\mathbf{R}g(\mathbf{R}),\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}}_{k}\right)=\beta_{k+l+1+1}\left(\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}}_{l+1},g(\mathbf{R}),\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}}_{k}\right)+

+\beta_{l+1}\left(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}\right)\cdot\beta_{k+1}\left(g(\mathbf{R}),\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}}_{k}\right).

Hence
\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(z,w)=S_{1}+S_{2}
where

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k,l\geq 0}\beta_{k+l+1+1}\left(\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}}_{l+1},g(\mathbf{R}),\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}}_{k}\right)z^{l}w^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{z}\sum_{k\geq 0,l\geq 1}\beta_{k+l+1}\left(\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}}_{l},g(\mathbf{R}),\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}}_{k}\right)z^{l}w^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{z}\eta^{g}_{\mathbf{R}}(z,w)-\frac{1}{z}\eta^{g}_{\mathbf{R}}(w),

and

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k,l\geq 0}\beta_{l+1}\left(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}\right)\cdot\beta_{k+1}\left(g(\mathbf{R}),\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}}_{k}\right)z^{l}w^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{l\geq 0}\beta_{l+1}\left(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}\right)z^{l}\right)\sum_{k\geq 0}\beta_{k+1}\left(g(\mathbf{R}),\underbrace{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R}}_{k}\right)w^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{z}\eta_{\mathbf{R}}(z)\eta^{g}_{\mathbf{R}}(w).

Therefore

\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(z,w)=\frac{1}{z}\eta^{g}_{\mathbf{R}}(z,w)-\frac{1}{z}\eta^{g}_{\mathbf{R}}(w)+\frac{1}{z}\eta_{\mathbf{R}}(z)\eta^{g}_{\mathbf{R}}(w).

Since the functions
\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(z,w)
and
\eta^{g}_{\mathbf{R}}(z,w)
are symmetric in
z,w
we get

\frac{1}{w}\eta^{g}_{\mathbf{R}}(z,w)-\frac{1}{w}\eta^{g}_{\mathbf{R}}(z)+\frac{1}{w}\eta_{\mathbf{R}}(w)\eta^{g}_{\mathbf{R}}(z)=\frac{1}{z}\eta^{g}_{\mathbf{R}}(z,w)-\frac{1}{z}\eta^{g}_{\mathbf{R}}(w)+\frac{1}{z}\eta_{\mathbf{R}}(z)\eta^{g}_{\mathbf{R}}(w).

After some algebra we get

(4.14)

(z-w)\eta^{g}_{\mathbf{R}}(z,w)=z\eta^{g}_{\mathbf{R}}(z)-w\eta^{g}_{\mathbf{R}}(w)+\eta_{\mathbf{R}}(z)w\eta^{g}_{\mathbf{R}}(w)-\eta_{\mathbf{R}}(w)z\eta^{g}_{\mathbf{R}}(z).

This proves formula (
4.13
) (for
g
instead of
h
).
∎

Lemma 4.10
.

For
z,w
in some neighborhood of
0
in
{\mathbb{C}}

(4.15)

\displaystyle\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varphi\left(h(\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)}{\varphi\left((\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)},

(4.16)

\displaystyle\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(z,w)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varphi\left(h(\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{R})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-w\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)}{\varphi\left((\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)\varphi\left((\mathbf{I}-w\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)}.

Proof.

For
z
near the origin we have

\varphi\left(h(\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\varphi\left(h(\mathbf{R})\mathbf{R}^{n}\right)z^{n}.

By the definition of Boolean cumulants we have

\displaystyle\varphi\left(h(\mathbf{R})\mathbf{R}^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle j_{1}+j_{2}+\cdots+j_{k}=n+1}}{{j_{1},...,j_{k}\geq 1}}}\beta_{j_{1}}(h(\mathbf{R}),\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R})\prod_{m=2}^{k}\beta_{j_{m}}(\mathbf{R})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i_{1}+i_{2}+\cdots+i_{k}=n+1-k}}{{i_{1},...,i_{k}\geq 0}}}\beta_{i_{1}+1}(h(\mathbf{R}),\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R})\prod_{m=2}^{k}\beta_{i_{m}+1}(\mathbf{R}).

Thus

\displaystyle\varphi\left(h(\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i_{1}+i_{2}+\cdots+i_{k}=n+1-k}}{{i_{1},...,i_{k}\geq 0}}}\beta_{i_{1}+1}(h(\mathbf{R}),\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R})\prod_{m=2}^{k}\beta_{i_{m}+1}(\mathbf{R})z^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=k-1}^{\infty}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i_{1}+i_{2}+\cdots+i_{k}=n+1-k}}{{i_{1},...,i_{k}\geq 0}}}\beta_{i_{1}+1}(h(\mathbf{R}),\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R})\prod_{m=2}^{k}\beta_{i_{m}+1}(\mathbf{R})z^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{k-1}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i_{1}+i_{2}+\cdots+i_{k}=n}}{{i_{1},...,i_{k}\geq 0}}}\beta_{i_{1}+1}(h(\mathbf{R}),\mathbf{R},...,\mathbf{R})\prod_{m=2}^{k}\beta_{i_{m}+1}(\mathbf{R})z^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}z^{k-1}\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(z)\left(\frac{\eta_{\mathbf{R}}(z)}{z}\right)^{k-1}=\frac{\eta^{h}_{\mathbf{R}}(z)}{1-\eta_{\mathbf{R}}(z)}.

This proves (
4.15
) since
\frac{1}{1-\eta_{\mathbf{R}}(z)}=1+M_{\mathbf{R}}(z)=\varphi\left((\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)
. The formula (
4.16
) follows now from combining (
4.15
) with formula (
4.13
).
∎

The following Corollary ends the proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Corollary 4.11
.

For
z,w
in some neighborhood of
0
in
{\mathbb{C}}
the sum
k_{2}(z,w)
from Lemma
4.5
is equal

\displaystyle k_{2}(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\left(w\omega_{2}(z)-z\omega_{2}(w)\right)\left(\omega_{2}(z)-\omega_{2}(w)\right)}{\left(M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-M_{\mathbf{U}}(w)\right)(z-w)}A_{1}(z,w)A_{2}(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{w\omega_{2}(z)-z\omega_{2}(w)}{z-w}\frac{A_{1}(z,w)A_{2}(z,w)}{B(z,w)},

where

\displaystyle A_{k}(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi\left(\mathbf{R}g_{k}(\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(w)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right),

\displaystyle B(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi\left(\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(w)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right).

Proof.

Recall that
h_{k}(\mathbf{R})=\mathbf{R}g_{k}(\mathbf{R})
. From Lemma
4.5
and Corollary
4.8
we see that

k_{2}(z,w)=\frac{\left(w\omega_{2}(z)-z\omega_{2}(w)\right)\left(\omega_{2}(z)-\omega_{2}(w)\right)}{\left(\eta_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-\eta_{\mathbf{U}}(w)\right)(z-w)}\eta^{h_{2}}_{\mathbf{R}}(\omega_{2}(z),\omega_{2}(w))A_{1}(z,w).

From formula (
4.16
) we have

\eta^{h_{2}}_{\mathbf{R}}(\omega_{2}(z),\omega_{2}(w))=\frac{A_{2}(z,w)}{(M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+1)(M_{\mathbf{U}}(w)+1)}.

To finish the proof we note that

\eta_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-\eta_{\mathbf{U}}(w)=\frac{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-M_{\mathbf{U}}(w)}{(M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+1)(M_{\mathbf{U}}(w)+1)}

and

\displaystyle M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-M_{\mathbf{U}}(w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle M_{\mathbf{R}}(\omega_{2}(z))-M_{\mathbf{R}}(\omega_{2}(w))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\omega_{2}(z)-\omega_{2}(z))\varphi\left(\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(w)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\omega_{2}(z)-\omega_{2}(z))B(z,w).

∎

We will also an state the explicit formula for
k(z,w)
in the special case when
g(\mathbf{R})=\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{R})^{-1}
. This will be important later when we consider characterization theorems.

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{Y}
be free, self-adjoint and positive random variables such that
\mathbf{R}<\mathbf{I}
. Denote
g(\mathbf{R})=\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{R})^{-1}
and
\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
Then the expectation

k(z,w)=\varphi\left(g(\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{I}-w\mathbf{U})^{-1}g(\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right),

is equal

(4.17)

k(z,w)=k_{1}(z,w)+k_{2}(z,w),

where

\displaystyle k_{1}(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{2}(z)-\omega_{2}(w)}\left(\omega_{2}(z)\frac{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-\varphi(g(\mathbf{R}))}{(\omega_{2}(z)-1)^{2}}-\omega_{2}(w)\frac{M_{\mathbf{U}}(w)-\varphi(g(\mathbf{R}))}{(\omega_{2}(w)-1)^{2}}\right)+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\omega_{2}(z)-1)(\omega_{2}(w)-1)}\left(\varphi(g(\mathbf{R}))+\varphi(g(\mathbf{R})^{2})\right),

k_{2}(z,w)=\frac{\left(w\omega_{2}(z)-z\omega_{2}(w)\right)}{\left(M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-M_{\mathbf{U}}(w)\right)(\omega_{2}(z)-\omega_{2}(w))(z-w)}\left(\frac{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-\varphi(g(\mathbf{R}))}{\omega_{2}(z)-1}-\frac{M_{\mathbf{U}}(w)-\varphi(g(\mathbf{R}))}{\omega_{2}(w)-1}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

We need to evaluate
k_{1}(z,w)
and
k_{2}(z,w)
from Theorem
4.1
.

The function
k_{1}(z,w)
satisfies

k_{1}(z,w)=\varphi\left(g(\mathbf{R})^{2}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(w)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right).

We start by writing

(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(w)\mathbf{R})^{-1}=\frac{1}{\omega_{2}(z)-\omega_{2}(w)}\left(\omega_{2}(z)(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}-\omega_{2}(w)(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(w)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right).

Hence

k_{1}(z,w)=\frac{1}{\omega_{2}(z)-\omega_{2}(w)}\left(\varphi\left(\omega_{2}(z)g(\mathbf{R})^{2}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)-\varphi\left(\omega_{2}(w)g(\mathbf{R})^{2}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(w)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)\right).

The identity

\tfrac{zx^{2}}{(1-x)^{2}(1-zx)}=\tfrac{z}{(z-1)^{2}}\left(\tfrac{zx}{1-zx}-\tfrac{x}{1-x}\right)-\tfrac{z}{z-1}\left(\tfrac{x}{1-x}+\tfrac{x^{2}}{(1-x)^{2}}\right)

implies that

\varphi\left(\omega_{2}(z)g(\mathbf{R})^{2}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)=\frac{\omega_{2}(z)}{(\omega_{2}(z)-1)^{2}}\left(M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-\varphi(g(\mathbf{R}))\right)-\frac{\omega_{2}(z)}{\omega_{2}(z)-1}\left(\varphi(g(\mathbf{R}))+\varphi(g(\mathbf{R})^{2})\right).

After some algebra we get

\displaystyle k_{1}(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{2}(z)-\omega_{2}(w)}\left(\omega_{2}(z)\frac{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-\varphi(g(\mathbf{R}))}{(\omega_{2}(z)-1)^{2}}-\omega_{2}(w)\frac{M_{\mathbf{U}}(w)-\varphi(g(\mathbf{R}))}{(\omega_{2}(w)-1)^{2}}\right)+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\omega_{2}(z)-1)(\omega_{2}(w)-1)}\left(\varphi(g(\mathbf{R}))+\varphi(g(\mathbf{R})^{2})\right).

For
k_{2}(z,w)
we have

\displaystyle k_{2}(z,w)=\frac{\left(w\omega_{2}(z)-z\omega_{2}(w)\right)\left(\omega_{2}(z)-\omega_{2}(w)\right)}{\left(M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-M_{\mathbf{U}}(w)\right)(z-w)}A(z,w)^{2},

where

A(z,w)=\varphi\left(\mathbf{R}g(\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(w)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right).

As earlier, after using the formula

(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(w)\mathbf{R})^{-1}=\frac{1}{\omega_{2}(z)-\omega_{2}(w)}\left(\omega_{2}(z)(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}-\omega_{2}(w)(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(w)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)

and the identity

\frac{zx^{2}}{(1-x)(1-zx)}=\frac{zx}{(z-1)(1-zx)}-\frac{zx}{(z-1)(1-x)}

we get

\displaystyle\varphi\left(\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R}g(\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}{\omega_{2}(z)-1}-\frac{\omega_{2}(z)}{\omega_{2}(z)-1}\varphi(\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{R})^{-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}{\omega_{2}(z)-1}-\frac{1}{\omega_{2}(z)-1}\varphi(\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{R})^{-1})-\varphi(\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{R})^{-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-\varphi(g(\mathbf{R}))}{\omega_{2}(z)-1}-\varphi(g(\mathbf{R})).

Hence

A(z,w)=\left[\frac{1}{\omega_{2}(z)-\omega_{2}(w)}\left(\frac{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-\varphi(g(\mathbf{R}))}{\omega_{2}(z)-1}-\frac{M_{\mathbf{U}}(w)-\varphi(g(\mathbf{R}))}{\omega_{2}(w)-1}\right)\right].

This ends the proof.
∎

5.
Main problem: the characterization theorems

Throughout this section we assume we are given two free, self-adjoint, positive and non-degenerated variables
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}
in a
W^{*}
-probability space. We also assume
\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}
are defined by

(5.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathbf{U}&=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{X})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{X})^{-\frac{1}{2}},\\
\mathbf{V}&=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U})^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U})^{\frac{1}{2}}.\end{array}\right.

We consider three sets of regression conditions

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\varphi\left(\mathbf{V}\mid\mathbf{U}\right)&=a\mathbf{I},\\
\varphi\left(\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mid\mathbf{U}\right)&=c\mathbf{I},\end{array}\right.\ \ \mbox{or}\ \ \left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\varphi\left(\mathbf{V}\mid\mathbf{U}\right)&=a\mathbf{I},\\
\varphi\left(\mathbf{V}^{2}\mid\mathbf{U}\right)&=b\mathbf{I},\end{array}\right.\ \ \mbox{or}\ \ \left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\varphi\left(\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mid\mathbf{U}\right)&=c\mathbf{I},\\
\varphi\left(\mathbf{V}^{-2}\mid\mathbf{U}\right)&=d\mathbf{I},\end{array}\right.

where
a,b,c,d\in{\mathbb{R}}
are some constants. The goal is to show that any of these three sets of conditions implies that
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}
have free-Kummer and free-Poisson distribution respectively.

As earlier we denote
\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{X})^{-1}
which is equivalent to
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{R}^{-1}-1
. Thus we can express
\mathbf{U}
as
\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
Note that the first and the third set of conditions involves
\mathbf{V}^{-1}
and therefore we require to assume that
\mathbf{X}
is strictly positive. In this case
\mathbf{R}<\mathbf{I}
and
\mathbf{X}^{-1}=\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{R})^{-1}
.

As before
\omega_{1}(z),\omega_{2}(z)
denote the subordination functions such that
M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)=M_{\mathbf{Y}}(\omega_{1}(z))=M_{\mathbf{R}}(\omega_{2}(z))
.

Before we state our characterization theorem we show in the next few lemmas that each considered condition of the form
\varphi\left(\mathbf{V}^{k}\mid\mathbf{U}\right)=c_{k}\mathbf{I}
implies certain algebraic equation involving
\omega_{2}(z)
,
M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)
and various constants. It is worth to note that for
k=1,-1
these equations are derived using only the technique of subordination for free multiplicative convolution. For
k=2,-2
relying solely on subordination does not work and derivation of these equations is based on Theorem
4.1
.

Since any two considered conditions give us two equations with two unknown functions, in theory we should be able to find them and obtain various transforms for considered random variables and thus determine their distribution. This is done in Subsection
5.2
. See Proposition
5.5
and Lemmas
5.7
and
5.8
for details.

5.1.
Functional equations implied by regression conditions

Lemma 5.1
.

Assume that

(5.2)

\varphi\left(\mathbf{V}\mid\mathbf{U}\right)=a\mathbf{I},

for some constant
a
. Then the identity

(5.3)

(\omega_{2}(z)-1)M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+\omega_{2}(z)=\frac{z}{z+1}\left(aM_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+a-\varphi(\mathbf{X})\right)

holds for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.

Proof.

We start by rewriting (
5.2
) as

(5.4)

\varphi\left(\mathbf{X}\mid\mathbf{U}\right)=a(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U})^{-1}.

Multiplying both sides by
(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}
and applying expectation gives

(5.5)

\varphi\left(\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right)=a\varphi\left((\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right).

It is easy to check that for
z\neq-1

(1+\mathbf{U})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}=\frac{1}{z+1}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U})^{-1}+\frac{z}{z+1}z\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}+\frac{z}{z+1}\mathbf{I}.

Hence the right hand side equals

(5.6)

a\varphi\left((1+\mathbf{U})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right)=\frac{\varphi(\mathbf{X})}{z+1}+\frac{az}{z+1}M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+\frac{az}{z+1},

since (
5.4
) implies that
a\varphi((\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U})^{-1})=\varphi(\mathbf{X})
.

By the subordination property (formula (
2.7
)) the left hand side of (
5.5
) is equal

\displaystyle\varphi\left(\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi\left(\mathbf{X}\varphi\left((\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\mid\mathbf{R}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi\left((\mathbf{R}^{-1}-\mathbf{I})(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi\left(\mathbf{R}^{-1}+(\omega_{2}(z)-1)(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathbf{X})+1+(\omega_{2}(z)-1)(1+M_{\mathbf{R}}(\omega_{2}(z)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathbf{X})+\omega_{2}(z)+(\omega_{2}(z)-1)M_{\mathbf{U}}(z).

Hence (
5.5
) yields

(5.7)

\varphi(\mathbf{X})+\omega_{2}(z)+(\omega_{2}(z)-1)M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)=\frac{\varphi(\mathbf{X})}{z+1}+\frac{az}{z+1}M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+\frac{az}{z+1},

which is equivalent to (
5.3
).
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Assume that

(5.8)

\varphi\left(\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mid\mathbf{U}\right)=c\mathbf{I},

for some constant
c
. Then the identity

(5.9)

\frac{z}{\omega_{2}(z)-1}\left(M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-\varphi(\mathbf{X}^{-1})\right)=cz+c(z+1)M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)

holds for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.

Proof.

The condition

\varphi\left(\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mid\mathbf{U}\right)=c\mathbf{I},

is equivalent to

(5.10)

\varphi\left(\mathbf{X}^{-1}\mid\mathbf{U}\right)=c(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U}).

We multiply both sides by
z(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}
and apply the state
\varphi
to get

z\varphi\left(\mathbf{X}^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right)=cz\varphi\left((\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U})(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right).

We now evaluate both sides. The right hand side is equal

\displaystyle cz\varphi\left((\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U})(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle cz\varphi\left(\mathbf{I}+z\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right)+c\varphi\left(z\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle cz+czM_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+cM_{\mathbf{U}}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle cz+c(z+1)M_{\mathbf{U}}(z).

For the left hand side we apply formula (
2.7
) to get

\displaystyle z\varphi\left(\mathbf{X}^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z\varphi\left(\mathbf{X}^{-1}\varphi((\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\mid\mathbf{R})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z\varphi\left(\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{R})^{-1}(1-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right).

From the identity

\frac{x}{(1-x)(1-zx)}=\frac{1}{(z-1)}\frac{zx}{(1-zx)}-\frac{1}{(z-1)}\frac{x}{(1-x)},

we see that

\displaystyle z\varphi\left(\mathbf{X}^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{z}{\omega_{2}(z)-1}\left(M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-\varphi(\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{R})^{-1})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{z}{\omega_{2}(z)-1}\left(M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-\varphi(\mathbf{X}^{-1})\right).

∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Assume that

(5.11)

\varphi\left(\mathbf{V}^{-2}\mid\mathbf{U}\right)=d\mathbf{I},

for some constant
d
. Then the following identity holds

k(z,-1)=d+d\left(1+\frac{1}{z}\right)M_{\mathbf{U}}(z),

for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, where
k(z,w)
is given by the formula (
4.17
).

Proof.

We start by rewriting the condition (
5.11
) as

(5.12)

\varphi\left(\mathbf{X}^{-1}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{-1}\mid\mathbf{U}\right)=d(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U}).

Multiplying both sides by
(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}
and applying the state
\varphi
to both sides gives

\varphi\left(\mathbf{X}^{-1}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right)=d\varphi\left((\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U})(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right).

The middle term is exactly
k(z,-1)
by formula (
4.1
) which defines the function
k
. For the term on the right hand side we have

d\varphi\left((\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U})(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right)=d+d\left(1+\frac{1}{z}\right)M_{\mathbf{U}}(z).

This ends the proof of the Lemma.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Assume that

(5.13)

\varphi\left(\mathbf{V}^{2}\mid\mathbf{U}\right)=b\mathbf{I},

for some constant
b
. Denote
D(z)=(\omega_{2}(z)-1)M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+\omega_{2}(z)
. Then the identity

(5.14)

\varphi(\mathbf{X}^{2})+\varphi(\mathbf{Y})\varphi(\mathbf{X})+\omega_{2}(z)\varphi(\mathbf{X})+(\omega_{2}(z)-1)D(z)+\frac{D(z)^{2}}{zM_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}\\
=\frac{b\varphi((\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U})^{-1})}{z+1}+\frac{bz}{z+1}(M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+1)+\varphi(\mathbf{Y})\frac{D(z)}{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}

holds for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{V}^{2}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U})^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U})\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U})^{\frac{1}{2}}
, the condition (
5.13
) is equivalent to

(5.15)

\varphi\left(\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U})\mathbf{X}\mid\mathbf{U}\right)=b(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U})^{-1}.

We multiply both sides by
(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}
and apply
\varphi
to get

(5.16)

\varphi\left(\mathbf{X}^{2}(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right)+\varphi\left(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right)=b\varphi\left((\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right).

The right hand side was calculated in the previous Lemma and is equal

b\varphi\left((\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right)=\frac{b\varphi((\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U})^{-1})}{z+1}+\frac{bz}{z+1}(M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+1).

The first term of (
5.16
) can be calculated using formula (
2.7
) as follows

\displaystyle\varphi\left(\mathbf{X}^{2}(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi\left(\mathbf{X}^{2}\varphi\left((\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\mid\mathbf{R}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi\left((\mathbf{R}^{-1}-\mathbf{I})^{2}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi\left((\mathbf{R}^{-1}-\mathbf{I})^{2}+\omega_{2}(z)(\mathbf{R}^{-1}-1)+(\omega_{2}(z)-1)\mathbf{I}+(\omega_{2}(z)-1)^{2}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathbf{X}^{2})+\omega_{2}(z)\varphi(\mathbf{X})+\omega_{2}(z)-1+(\omega_{2}(z)-1)^{2}(1+M_{\mathbf{U}}(z))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathbf{X}^{2})+\omega_{2}(z)\varphi(\mathbf{X})+(\omega_{2}(z)-1)D(z),

The middle term of (
5.16
) is equal

\varphi\left(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right)=\frac{\partial}{\partial w}k(z,w)\Bigr|_{w=0},

where

k(z,w)=\varphi\left(\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{I}-w\mathbf{U})^{-1}\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right)
. Since
\mathbf{X}=g(\mathbf{R})=\mathbf{R}^{-1}-1
by Theorem
4.1
we have
k(z,w)=k_{1}(z,w)+k_{2}(z,w)
where

\displaystyle k_{1}(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi\left((\mathbf{R}^{-1}-\mathbf{I})^{2}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(w)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right),

\displaystyle k_{2}(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\left(w\omega_{2}(z)-z\omega_{2}(w)\right)\left(\omega_{2}(z)-\omega_{2}(w)\right)}{\left(M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-M_{\mathbf{U}}(w)\right)(z-w)}\varphi\left((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(w)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)^{2}.

Since
\omega_{2}^{\prime}(0)=\varphi(\mathbf{Y})
(see formula (
2.9
) for example) we conclude that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial w}k_{1}(z,w)\Bigr|_{w=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{2}^{\prime}(0)\varphi\left((\mathbf{R}^{-1}-\mathbf{I})^{2}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\mathbf{R}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathbf{Y})\varphi\left(-\frac{1}{\omega_{2}(z)}\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{R}^{-1}+\frac{(\omega_{2}(z)-1)^{2}}{\omega_{2}(z)}(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathbf{Y})\left(-\frac{1}{\omega_{2}(z)}+\varphi(\mathbf{X})+1+\frac{(\omega_{2}(z)-1)^{2}}{\omega_{2}(z)}(1+M_{\mathbf{U}}(z))\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathbf{Y})\varphi(\mathbf{X})+\frac{\varphi(\mathbf{Y})}{\omega_{2}(z)}(\omega_{2}(z)-1)D(z).

The function
w\omega_{2}(z)-z\omega_{2}(w)
is zero when
w=0
therefore the product rule for derivatives implies that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial w}k_{2}(z,w)\Bigr|_{w=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\omega_{2}(z)-\varphi(\mathbf{Y})z)\frac{\omega_{2}(z)}{zM_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}\varphi\left((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)^{2}.

Next we have

\varphi\left((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{I}-\omega_{2}(z)\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)=1+M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-\frac{1}{\omega_{2}(z)}M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)=\frac{D(z)}{\omega_{2}(z)}.

Consequently

\frac{\partial}{\partial w}k_{2}(z,w)\Bigr|_{w=0}=\frac{\omega_{2}(z)-\varphi(\mathbf{Y})z}{z\omega_{2}(z)M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}D(z)^{2}.

After some algebra we get

\varphi\left(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{U})^{-1}\right)=\varphi(\mathbf{Y})\varphi(\mathbf{X})+\frac{D(z)^{2}}{zM_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}-\varphi(\mathbf{Y})\frac{D(z)}{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}.

Combining and rearranging all terms gives (
5.14
).
∎

5.2.
Determination of a distribution from functional equations

In the proofs of all three characterizations we will encounter similar equations that will allow us to determine the distributions of
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
. In order to not repeat ourselves we will write down the common part of the proof in a form of few lemmas. We start with the following proposition that characterizes free-Kummer distribution as the one for which its Cauchy-Stieltjes transform is a solution of a certain quadratic equation. (See also Remark
3.1
.)

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
G=G(z)
be a Cauchy-Stieltjes transform of a positive random variable
\mathbf{Z}
. Suppose
G
satisfies the following equation

(5.17)

z(z+1)G^{2}(z)-(\gamma z(z+1)-(\alpha-1)(z+1)+\beta z)G(z)+\gamma z+\delta=0,

for some
\alpha,\gamma>0
,
\beta,\delta\in{\mathbb{R}}
. If either
\beta\geq 0
or
\alpha>1
then
\delta
is uniquely determined by
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
and
\mathbf{Z}
has the free-Kummer distribution
\mathcal{K}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
.

Proof.

Let us denote

\displaystyle p(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma z(z+1)-(\alpha-1)(z+1)+\beta z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma z^{2}-(\alpha-1-\beta-\gamma)z-(\alpha-1).

Let also
H=H(z)
be the Cauchy-Stieltjes transform of
\mathcal{K}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
. Then
H
solves the equation (
5.17
) with the same
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
but possibly different
\delta
say

z(z+1)H^{2}(z)-p(z)H(z)+\gamma z+\delta_{1}=0.

Subtracting the above equation from (
5.17
) gives the following identity

(5.18)

(G(z)-H(z))\left[z(z+1)(G(z)+H(z))-p(z)\right]=\delta_{1}-\delta,

which holds for
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
since both
G
and
H
are Cauchy-Stieltjes transforms of a positive random variable.

Let us denote
f(z)=z(z+1)(G(z)+H(z))-p(z)
. Note that the function
f(x)
takes only real values for
x\in(-\infty,0)
. Since
p(x)
is positive when
x\to-\infty
and both
G
and
H
are negative on
(-\infty,0)
we conclude that
f(x)
is also negative when
x\to-\infty
.

Since
f(-1)=-p(-1)=\beta
and

\limsup\limits_{x\to 0^{-}}f(x)=\limsup\limits_{x\to 0^{-}}\underbrace{x(x+1)(G(x)+H(x))}_{\textrm{positive on}\ (-1,0)}-p(0)\geq\alpha-1

we conclude that when either
\beta\geq 0
or
\alpha>1
there exists
x_{0}\in(-\infty,0)
such that
f(x_{0})=0
. This implies
\delta=\delta_{1}
and consequently

(5.19)

(G(z)-H(z))f(z)=0\ \ \mbox{on}\ {\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}_{+}.

Since
f(z)
is non-zero analytic function we conclude that
G=H
.
∎

Using (
2.4
) we can reformulate the previous Proposition in terms of a moment transform.

Corollary 5.6
.

Assume the function
M=M(z)
is a moment transform of a positive random variable
\mathbf{Z}
which satisfies the equation

z(z+1)M^{2}(z)+((\alpha+1)z(z+1)-\gamma(z+1)-\beta z)M(z)+\alpha z(z+1)+(\delta-\gamma-\beta)z=0,

for some
\alpha,\gamma>0
,
\beta,\delta\in{\mathbb{R}}
. If either
\beta\geq 0
or
\alpha>1
then
\delta
is uniquely determined by
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
and
\mathbf{Z}
has the free-Kummer distribution
\mathcal{K}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
.

Lemma 5.7
.

Suppose

(5.20)

\gamma\omega_{2}(z)=zM_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+\alpha z,

for some
\gamma>0
and
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Then
\alpha>0
and
\mathbf{Y}\sim\nu\left(\alpha,1/\gamma\right)
.

Proof.

By differentiating (
5.20
) at
z=0
we get
\gamma\varphi(\mathbf{Y})=\alpha>0
.
From (
2.10
) we get
z=\frac{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+1}{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}\omega_{1}(z)\omega_{2}(z)
.
Plugging this into (
5.20
) allows us to cancel out
\omega_{2}(z)
and yields

\gamma=(M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+1)\omega_{1}(z)+\alpha\tfrac{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+1}{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}\omega_{1}(z).

This gives

\omega_{1}(z)=\frac{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}{(M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+1)\left[1/\gamma M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+\alpha/\gamma\right]}.

Since
M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)=M_{\mathbf{Y}}(\omega_{1}(z))
this implies

M^{\langle-1\rangle}_{\mathbf{Y}}(z)=\frac{z}{(z+1)\left[1/\gamma\cdot z+\alpha/\gamma\right]}.

Hence

S_{\mathbf{Y}}(z)=\frac{1}{1/\gamma\cdot z+\alpha/\gamma}.

Since
S
-transform uniquely determines the distribution we get
\mathbf{Y}\sim\nu\left(\alpha,1/\gamma\right)
.
∎

From the above proof it is easy to see that if
\mathbf{Y}\sim\nu\left(\alpha,1/\gamma\right)
then (
5.20
) holds. We will use this fact in the proof of the following Lemma.

Lemma 5.8
.

Assume
M=M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)
satisfies

(5.21)

z(z+1)M^{2}+((\alpha+1)z(z+1)-\gamma(z+1)-\beta z)M+\alpha z(z+1)+\rho z=0,

for some
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>0
then
\mathbf{U}\sim\mathcal{K}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
. Moreover if
\mathbf{Y}\sim\nu\left(\alpha,1/\gamma\right)
then

\mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{K}(\beta,\alpha,\gamma)
.

Proof.

The first claim follows directly from Corollary
5.6
. For the second claim we note that the assumption
\mathbf{Y}\sim\nu\left(\alpha,1/\gamma\right)
implies identity (
5.20
) i.e.

z=\frac{\gamma\omega_{2}(z)}{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+\alpha}.

We plug this into (
5.21
) and after some easy algebra we get

(\omega_{2}(z)-1)M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)^{2}+\left[\gamma\omega_{2}(z)(\omega_{2}(z)-1)-(\beta-1)\omega_{2}(z)+\alpha(\omega_{2}(z)-1)\right]M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)\\
+\gamma\omega_{2}(z)(\omega_{2}(z)-1)+(\alpha+\rho)\omega_{2}(z)=0.

Since
M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)=M_{\mathbf{R}}(\omega_{2}(z))
we conclude that
M_{\mathbf{R}}(z)
satisfies

(5.22)

(z-1)M_{\mathbf{R}}(z)^{2}+\left[\gamma z(z-1)-(\beta-1)z+\alpha(z-1)\right]M_{\mathbf{R}}(z)+\gamma z(z-1)+(\alpha+\rho)z=0.

Now since
\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{X})^{-1}
then

M_{\mathbf{R}}(z)=\varphi\left(z\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{I}-z\mathbf{R})^{-1}\right)=z\varphi\left((\mathbf{X}+(1-z)\mathbf{I})^{-1}\right)=-zG_{\mathbf{X}}(z-1).

Thus we can rewrite the equation (
5.22
) for
M_{\mathbf{R}}(z)
as an equation for
G_{\mathbf{X}}(z)
.
After some algebra we get

z(z+1)G_{\mathbf{X}}(z)^{2}-\left(\gamma z(z+1)-(\beta-1)(z+1)+\alpha z\right)G_{\mathbf{X}}(z)+\gamma z+\alpha+\gamma+\rho=0.

The lemma follows now from Proposition
5.5
.
∎

5.3.
The first characterization

Theorem 5.9
.

Let
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}
be free, non-degenerated and self-adjoint random variables. Let also
\mathbf{Y}
be positive and
\mathbf{X}
strictly positive. If
\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}
are defined by (
5.1
) and

\varphi\left(\mathbf{V}\mid\mathbf{U}\right)=a\mathbf{I},

\varphi\left(\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mid\mathbf{U}\right)=c\mathbf{I},

for some constants
a
and
c
, then
ac>1
and
\mathbf{X}
has free-Kummer distribution
\mathcal{K}\left(\frac{ac}{ac-1},\frac{\lambda}{ac-1},\frac{c}{ac-1}\right)
and
\mathbf{Y}
has free Poisson distribution
\nu\left(\frac{\lambda}{ac-1},\frac{ac-1}{c}\right)
, where
\lambda
is some positive constant.

Proof.

We have
a=\varphi(\mathbf{V})>0
,
c=\varphi(\mathbf{V}^{-1})>0
then
ac>1
by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. (The inequality is strict because
V
is non-degenerated and
\varphi
is faithful.)
Under the assumptions of Theorem
5.9
, Lemmas
5.1
and
5.2
imply the following system of equations

(5.23)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}(\omega_{2}(z)-1)M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+\omega_{2}(z)&=&\frac{z}{z+1}\left(aM_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+a-p\right),\\
\frac{cz}{z+1}(\omega_{2}(z)-1)+c(\omega_{2}(z)-1)M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)&=&\frac{z}{z+1}\left(M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-q\right),\end{array}\right.

where we denoted
p=\varphi(\mathbf{X}),q=\varphi(\mathbf{X}^{-1})
.
Multiplying the first equation by
c
and subtracting second one from it gives

c\frac{z+\omega_{2}(z)}{z+1}=(ac-1)\frac{z}{z+1}M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+(\lambda+c)\frac{z}{z+1},

where
\lambda=c(a-p)+q-c
. Hence

(5.24)

c\omega_{2}(z)=(ac-1)zM_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+\lambda z.

Lemma
5.7
implies that
\lambda>0
and
\mathbf{Y}\sim\nu\left(\frac{\lambda}{ac-1},\frac{ac-1}{c}\right)
.

We plug
\omega_{2}(z)
from (
5.24
) into the second equation of (
5.23
) to get the following equation

(ac-1)z(z+1)M^{2}_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+\left[(ac-1+\lambda)z(z+1)-c(z+1)-acz\right]M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+\lambda z(z+1)+c(p-a)z=0.

We recognize here the equation (
5.21
) from Lemma
5.8
with parameters
\alpha=\frac{\lambda}{ac-1}
,
\beta=\frac{ac}{ac-1}
,
\gamma=\frac{c}{ac-1}
and
\rho=\frac{c(p-a)}{ac-1}
. Clearly
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>0
. Lemma
5.8
implies
\mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{K}\left(\frac{ac}{ac-1},\frac{\lambda}{ac-1},\frac{c}{ac-1}\right)
.

∎

5.4.
The second characterization

Theorem 5.12
.

Let
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}
be free, non-degenerated and self-adjoint random variables. Let also
\mathbf{Y}
be positive and
\mathbf{X}
strictly positive. If
\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}
are defined by (
5.1
) and

\varphi\left(\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mid\mathbf{U}\right)=c\mathbf{I},

\varphi\left(\mathbf{V}^{-2}\mid\mathbf{U}\right)=d\mathbf{I},

for some constants
c
and
d
, then
d>c^{2}
and
\mathbf{X}
has free-Kummer distribution
\mathcal{K}\left(\frac{d}{d-c^{2}},\frac{\lambda}{d-c^{2}},\frac{c^{3}}{d-c^{2}}\right)
and
\mathbf{Y}
has free Poisson distribution
\nu\left(\frac{\lambda}{d-c^{2}},\frac{d-c^{2}}{c^{3}}\right)
, where
\lambda
is some positive constant.

Proof.

Let us denote
a=\varphi(\mathbf{X}^{-1})
,
b=\varphi(\mathbf{X}^{-2})
,
K=M_{\mathbf{U}}(-1)
,
p=\omega_{2}(-1)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
5.12
, Lemmas
5.2
and
5.3
imply the following equations

(5.25)

\displaystyle\frac{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-a}{\omega_{2}(z)-1}=c+c\left(1+\frac{1}{z}\right)M_{\mathbf{U}}(z),

(5.26)

\displaystyle k(z,-1)=d\left(1+\frac{1}{z}\right)M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+d,

where
k(z,w)=k_{1}(z,w)+k_{2}(z,w)
is a function from Proposition
4.12
. Explicitly we have

\displaystyle k_{1}(z,-1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a+b}{(\omega_{2}(z)-1)(p-1)}+\frac{1}{\omega_{2}(z)-p}\left(\omega_{2}(z)\tfrac{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-a}{(\omega_{2}(z)-1)^{2}}-p\tfrac{K-a}{(p-1)^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle k_{2}(z,-1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-\left(\omega_{2}(z)+pz\right)}{\left(M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-K\right)(\omega_{2}(z)-p)(z+1)}\left(\frac{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-a}{\omega_{2}(z)-1}-\tfrac{K-a}{p-1}\right)^{2}.

The inequality
d>c^{2}
follows from Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, non-degeneracy of
\mathbf{V}
and faithfulness of
\varphi
. The constants
K
and
p
can be expressed in terms of
a,b,c,d
. From (
5.10
) we get
\varphi(\mathbf{U})=\frac{a}{c}-1
and (
5.25
) implies that
\frac{K-a}{p-1}=c
i.e.
K=a+c(p-1)
. Taking the limit
z\to 0
in (
5.26
) yields

d\varphi(\mathbf{U})+d=k_{1}(0,-1)=-\frac{a+b}{p-1}+\frac{K-a}{(p-1)^{2}}=\frac{c-a-b}{p-1}.

Consequently

(5.27)

p-1=\frac{c(c-a-b)}{ad},\ \ \ K=a+c(p-1).

The next step it to simplify (
5.26
). From (
5.25
) and (
5.27
) we get

k_{2}(z,-1)=\frac{-c^{2}\left(\omega_{2}(z)+pz\right)(z+1)M^{2}_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}{\left(M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-K\right)(\omega_{2}(z)-p)z^{2}},

and

k_{1}(z,-1)=c\left(1+\frac{1}{z}\right)M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)\frac{\omega_{2}(z)}{(\omega_{2}(z)-1)(\omega_{2}(z)-p)}+\frac{a+b-c}{p-1}\frac{1}{\omega_{2}(z)-1}.

Hence (
5.26
) is equivalent to

c\left(1+\frac{1}{z}\right)M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)\frac{\omega_{2}(z)}{(\omega_{2}(z)-1)(\omega_{2}(z)-p)}+\frac{a+b-c}{p-1}\frac{1}{\omega_{2}(z)-1}+k_{2}(z,-1)=d\left(1+\frac{1}{z}\right)M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+d.

By (
5.27
) we have
\frac{a+b-c}{p-1}=-\frac{ad}{c}
and this allows us to rewrite the last equation as

(5.28)

c\left(1+\frac{1}{z}\right)M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)\frac{\omega_{2}(z)}{(\omega_{2}(z)-1)(\omega_{2}(z)-p)}+k_{2}(z,-1)=d\left(1+\frac{1}{z}\right)M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+d+\frac{ad}{c}\frac{1}{\omega_{2}(z)-1}.

Using (
5.25
) we see that the right hand side of (
5.28
) equals

\frac{d}{c}\frac{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-a}{\omega_{2}(z)-1}+\frac{ad}{c}\frac{1}{\omega_{2}(z)-1}=\frac{d}{c}\frac{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}{\omega_{2}(z)-1}.

Consequently (
5.28
) takes the following equivalent form

c\left(1+\frac{1}{z}\right)M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)\frac{\omega_{2}(z)}{(\omega_{2}(z)-1)(\omega_{2}(z)-p)}-\frac{c^{2}\left(\omega_{2}(z)+pz\right)(z+1)M^{2}_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}{\left(M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-K\right)(\omega_{2}(z)-p)z^{2}}=\frac{d}{c}\frac{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}{\omega_{2}(z)-1}.

We cancel out
M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)
and multiply both sides by
z(\omega_{2}(z)-1)(\omega_{2}(z)-p)
which gives

(5.29)

c(z+1)\omega_{2}(z)-\frac{c^{2}(\omega_{2}(z)+pz)(z+1)(\omega_{2}(z)-1)M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}{(M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-K)z}=\frac{d}{c}z(\omega_{2}(z)-p).

We rewrite (
5.25
) as

\displaystyle c(z+1)(\omega_{2}(z)-1)M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z(M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-a)-cz(\omega_{2}(z)-1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z\left[(M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-K)-c(\omega_{2}(z)-p)\right],

and apply it to the middle term of (
5.29
) to get

\displaystyle c(z+1)\omega_{2}(z)-c(\omega_{2}(z)+zp)+\frac{c^{2}(\omega_{2}(z)+pz)(\omega_{2}(z)-p)}{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-K}=\frac{d}{c}z(\omega_{2}(z)-p).

Since
c(z+1)\omega_{2}(z)-c(\omega_{2}(z)+zp)=cz(\omega_{2}(z)-p)
we get

cz+\frac{c^{2}(\omega_{2}(z)+pz)}{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-K}=\frac{d}{c}z.

After some simple algebra we arrive at

(5.30)

c^{3}\omega_{2}(z)=(d-c^{2})zM_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+\lambda z,

where

(5.31)

\lambda=c^{2}K-c^{3}p-dK=ac^{2}-c^{3}-dK,

since by (
5.27
)
cp=K+c-a
. Lemma
5.7
implies
\lambda>0
and

\mathbf{Y}\sim\nu\left(\frac{\lambda}{d-c^{2}},\frac{d-c^{2}}{c^{3}}\right)
.

Combining (
5.25
) with (
5.30
) gives the following equation for
M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)
:

(d-c^{2})z(z+1)M^{2}_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+\left[(\lambda+d-c^{2})z(z+1)-c^{3}(1+z)-dz\right]M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+\\
+\lambda z(z+1)+(c^{2}a-c^{3}-\lambda)z=0.

We recognize here the equation (
5.21
) from Lemma
5.8
with parameters

\alpha=\frac{\lambda}{d-c^{2}}
,
\beta=\frac{d}{d-c^{2}}
,
\gamma=\frac{c^{3}}{d-c^{2}}
and
\rho=\frac{c^{2}a-c^{3}-\lambda}{d-c^{2}}
. Obviously
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>0

so Lemma
5.8
implies that
\mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{K}\left(\tfrac{d}{d-c^{2}},\tfrac{\lambda}{d-c^{2}},\tfrac{c^{3}}{d-c^{2}}\right)
.
∎

5.5.
The third characterization

Theorem 5.13
.

Let
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}
be free, positive, non-degenerated and self-adjoint random variables. If
\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}
are defined by (
5.1
) and

\varphi\left(\mathbf{V}\mid\mathbf{U}\right)=a\mathbf{I},

\varphi\left(\mathbf{V}^{2}\mid\mathbf{U}\right)=b\mathbf{I},

for some constants
a
and
b
, then
b>a^{2}
and
\mathbf{X}
has free-Kummer distribution
\mathcal{K}\left(\frac{a^{2}}{b-a^{2}},\frac{a\lambda}{b-a^{2}},\frac{a}{b-a^{2}}\right)
and
\mathbf{Y}

\nu\left(\frac{\lambda a}{b-a^{2}},\frac{b-a^{2}}{a}\right)
, where
\lambda
is some positive constant.

Proof.

The inequality
b>a^{2}
follows from Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
Under the assumptions of the Theorem
5.13
, Lemmas
5.1
and
5.4
imply the following identities

(5.32)

D(z):=(\omega_{2}(z)-1)M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+\omega_{2}(z)=\frac{z}{z+1}\left(aM_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+a-p\right),

(5.33)

q+\lambda p+p\omega_{2}(z)+(\omega_{2}(z)-1)D(z)+\frac{D(z)^{2}}{zM_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}=\\
=\frac{bp}{a(z+1)}+\frac{bz}{z+1}(M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+1)+\lambda\frac{D(z)}{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}.

where we denote
p=\varphi(\mathbf{X}),q=\varphi(\mathbf{X}^{2}),\lambda=\varphi(\mathbf{Y})>0
. We also used the fact that (
5.4
) implies that
\varphi((\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U})^{-1})=\frac{p}{a}
.

Before we continue we will show that
q
can be expressed by
a,b,p
and
\lambda
. From
(
5.4
)
and freeness of
\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{Y}
we obtain that

(5.34)

a=\varphi(\mathbf{X})+\varphi(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{U})=p+\varphi((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{R})\mathbf{Y})=p+\lambda(1-\varphi(\mathbf{R})).

Similarly (
5.15
) implies

\frac{bp}{a}=\varphi(\mathbf{X}^{2})+\varphi(\mathbf{X}^{2}\mathbf{U})=q+\varphi((\mathbf{R}^{-1}-\mathbf{I})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{R})\mathbf{Y})=q+\lambda(p-1+\varphi(\mathbf{R})).

Combining both identities we obtain

(5.35)

\frac{bp}{a}=q+\lambda p-(a-p).

The next step is to simplify (
5.33
). We start by noting that

\frac{bp}{a(z+1)}+\frac{bz}{z+1}(M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+1)=\frac{b}{a}\left[\frac{p+pz}{z+1}+\frac{z}{z+1}(aM_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+a-p)\right]=\frac{bp}{a}+\frac{b}{a}D(z).

The above identity together with (
5.35
) allows us to write (
5.33

z(a-p)+pz\omega_{2}(z)+z(\omega_{2}(z)-1)D(z)+\frac{D(z)^{2}}{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}=\frac{b}{a}zD(z)+\lambda z\frac{D(z)}{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}.

Now we note that (
5.32
) can be written in the following two forms

z(a-p)=(z+1)D(z)-azM_{\mathbf{U}}(z)\ \ \mbox{or}\ \ z(D(z)+p)=azM_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-D(z).

Consequently the left hand side of (
5.36
) is equal

\displaystyle z(a-p)+pz\omega_{2}(z)+z(\omega_{2}(z)-1)D(z)+D(z)\left(\omega_{2}(z)-1+\frac{\omega_{2}(z)}{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z(a-p)+z(p+D(z))\omega_{2}(z)-zD(z)+(\omega_{2}(z)-1)D(z)+\frac{\omega_{2}(z)}{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}D(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(z+1)D(z)-azM_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+(azM_{\mathbf{U}}(z)-D(z))\omega_{2}(z)-zD(z)+(\omega_{2}(z)-1)D(z)+\frac{\omega_{2}(z)}{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}D(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\omega_{2}(z)}{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}D(z)+az\left[\omega_{2}(z)(M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+1)-M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)\right]=\frac{\omega_{2}(z)}{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}D(z)+azD(z).

and thus (
5.36

\frac{\omega_{2}(z)}{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}D(z)+azD(z)=\frac{b}{a}zD(z)+\lambda z\frac{D(z)}{M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)}.

D(z)

\omega_{2}(z)=\frac{b-a^{2}}{a}zM_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+\lambda z.

5.7

\mathbf{Y}\sim\nu\left(\frac{\lambda a}{b-a^{2}},\frac{b-a^{2}}{a}\right)
.
Plugging
\omega_{2}(z)
from (
5.37
) into (
5.32
) yields the following equation for

(b-a^{2})z(z+1)M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)^{2}+\left[(b-a^{2}+a\lambda)z(z+1)-a(1+z)-a^{2}z\right]M_{\mathbf{U}}(z)+\\
a\lambda z(z+1)+a(p-a)z=0.

This is the equation (
5.21
) from Lemma
5.8
with parameters

\alpha=\frac{a\lambda}{b-a^{2}}
,
\beta=\frac{a^{2}}{b-a^{2}}
,
\gamma=\frac{a}{b-a^{2}}
and
\rho=\frac{a(p-a)}{b-a^{2}}
. Since

we conclude from Lemma
5.8

\mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{K}\left(\frac{a^{2}}{b-a^{2}},\frac{a\lambda}{b-a^{2}},\frac{a}{b-a^{2}}\right)
.

6.

6.1.
Free-Kummer distribution
\mathcal{K}(1,\beta,\gamma)

We will explain now in more details the definition of free-Kummer distribution for
\alpha=1
.
In that case the system of equations (
3.2
) takes the following form

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}\gamma+\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{(a+1)(b+1)}}&=0,\\
\gamma\frac{a+b}{2}+\beta-\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{(a+1)(b+1)}}&=2.\end{array}\right.

and can easily be solved explicitly. A solution exists when
\beta<0
and assuming
a<b
we have

a=-1+\tfrac{1}{\gamma}(1-\sqrt{1-\beta})^{2}\ \ \ \mbox{and}\ \ \ b=-1+\tfrac{1}{\gamma}(1+\sqrt{1-\beta})^{2}.

Form this we easily see that
a>0
if and only if
1-\beta>(1+\sqrt{\gamma})^{2}
.
Thus we define
\mathcal{K}(1,\beta,\gamma)
with
1-\beta>(1+\sqrt{\gamma})^{2}
to be a probability measure with the density (
3.1
) that takes now the form

(6.1)

\frac{\gamma}{2\pi}\frac{\sqrt{(x-a)(b-x)}}{x+1}\mathbbm{1}_{(a,b)}(x).

It’s easy to check that the above function is the density of
X-1
where
X
has the free-Poisson distribution
\nu(1-\beta,1/\gamma)
.

When
1-\beta\leq(1+\sqrt{\gamma})^{2}
we defined
\mathcal{K}(1,\beta,\gamma)

to be a probability measure that has the density

(6.2)

f_{\beta,\gamma}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{x(b-x)}\left(\tfrac{\sigma}{x}-\tfrac{\beta}{(1+x)\sqrt{b+1}}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{(0,b)}(x),

where
\sigma=\gamma+\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{b+1}}
and
b
is the unique positive solution of

(6.3)

\gamma\tfrac{b}{2}+\beta-\tfrac{\beta}{\sqrt{(b+1)}}=2.

(Existence of such solution is easy to see. Uniqueness follows from the fact the RHS is a strictly increasing function of
b
for
\beta\geq 0
and strictly convex function of
b
and equal
0
at
b=0
for
\beta<0
.) We will show now that (
6.2
) is a density function if and only if
1-\beta\leq(1+\sqrt{\gamma})^{2}
.

For
b>0
we have

\int_{0}^{b}\frac{\sqrt{x(b-x)}}{x}dx=\frac{\pi b}{2}\ \ \mbox{and}\ \ \ \int_{0}^{b}\frac{\sqrt{x(b-x)}}{x+1}dx=\frac{\pi(\sqrt{1+b}-1)^{2}}{2}

and we can check that
f_{\beta,\gamma}
integrates to
1
whenever
b
is a solution of (
6.3
). To finish, we will show the following fact.

Fact 6.1
.

f_{\beta,\gamma}
is non-negative if and only if
1-\beta\leq(1+\sqrt{\gamma})^{2}
.

Proof.

Note that

\tfrac{\sigma}{x}-\tfrac{\beta}{(1+x)\sqrt{b+1}}=\tfrac{\sigma}{x}-\tfrac{\sigma-\gamma}{x+1}=\tfrac{\sigma+\gamma x}{x(x+1)}\geq 0\ \mbox{for}\ x\in(0,b)\iff\sigma\geq 0,

so the goal is to show
\sigma\geq 0\iff\beta\geq-\gamma-2\sqrt{\gamma}
. It’s obvious that
\sigma\geq 0
when
\beta\geq 0
or
\beta<0
but
\beta+\gamma\geq 0
. So we can assume
\beta<-\gamma<0
.

Let us denote
u=\gamma-\sigma=-\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{b+1}}
. Note that
u>0
since we assumed
\beta<-\gamma<0
. The condition
b>0
is equivalent to
u=-\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{b+1}}<-\beta
. Since
b=\frac{\beta^{2}}{u^{2}}-1
we conclude that
u
is the unique solution of

(6.4)

\tfrac{\gamma\beta^{2}}{2u^{2}}-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}+\beta+u=2,

such that
u\in(0,-\beta)
. If we denote
h(u)=\frac{\gamma\beta^{2}}{2u^{2}}-\frac{\gamma}{2}+\beta+u
then
\lim\limits_{u\to 0^{-}}h(u)=+\infty
and
h(-\beta)=0
. Let us consider two cases

•

If
\beta\in[-\gamma-2\sqrt{\gamma},-\gamma)
then
\gamma\in(0,-\beta)
and
(\beta+\gamma)^{2}\leq 4\gamma
. Observe that

h(\gamma)=\tfrac{\beta^{2}}{2\gamma}-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}+\beta+\gamma=\tfrac{(\beta+\gamma)^{2}}{2\gamma}\leq 2.

Since there is only one solution to
h(x)=2
for
x\in(0,-\beta)
we conclude that
u=\gamma-\sigma\leq\gamma
which proves that
\sigma\geq 0
.

•

Similarly if
\beta<-\gamma-2\sqrt{\gamma}
then still
\gamma\in(0,-\beta)
but now
(\beta+\gamma)^{2}>2\gamma
so
h(\gamma)>2
and hence
u>\gamma
and
\sigma<0
.

∎

Remark 6.2
.

If
\beta=-\gamma-2\sqrt{\gamma}
then
h(\gamma)=2
and therefore
\sigma=0
. For
\beta\in(-\gamma-2\sqrt{\gamma},-\gamma)
we have
h(\gamma)<2
and thus
\sigma>0
(also trivially
\sigma>0
for
\beta\geq-\gamma)
.
