Sharp discrete isoperimetric inequalities in periodic graphs via discrete PDE and Semidiscrete Optimal Transport
Mircea Petrache, Matías Gómez

Abstract
We develop criteria based on a calibration argument via discrete PDE and semidiscrete optimal transport, for finding sharp isoperimetric inequalities of the form ( ♯ ​ Ω ) d − 1 ≤ C ​ ( ♯ ​ ∂ Ω → ) d (\sharp\Omega)^{d-1}\leq C(\sharp\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega})^{d} where Ω \Omega is a subset of vertices of a graph and ∂ Ω → \overrightarrow{\partial\Omega} is the oriented edge-boundary of Ω \Omega , as well as the optimum isoperimetric shapes Ω \Omega . The method is a discrete counterpart to Optimal Transport and ABP method proofs valid in the continuum, and answers a question appearing in Hamamuki [ 32 ] , extending that work valid for rectangular grids, to a larger class of graphs, including graphs dual to simplicial meshes of equal volume. We also connect the problem to the theory Voronoi tessellations and of Aleksandrov solutions from semidiscrete optimal transport. The role of the geometric-arithmetic inequality that was used in previous works in the continuum case and in the ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} -graph case is now played by a geometric cell-optimization constant, where the optimization problem is like in Minkowski’s proof of his classical theorem for convex polyhedra. Finally, we study the optimal constant in the related discrete Neumann boundary problem, and present a series of possible directions for a further classification of discrete edge-isoperimetric constants and shapes.

Introduction

1.1
Minimum energy and discrete Wulff shape in a graph

In the study of crystals and of other problems, one is lead to study the optimum shape of
N
-point systems at fixed
N
, minimizing an energy given by simple pairwise interactions, and its asymptotic in the limit
N\to\infty
.

As a basic model problem consider the following. If we study the shape of a model of crystallized material, we consider configurations which are subsets of a lattice
V=M\mathbb{Z}^{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
M:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
an invertible linear operator. We further imagine that points in
V
only interact in pairs, and that only
x,y
such that
x-y\in\mathcal{V}
for a fixed finite
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e. pairs of particles that are nearest-neighbors in finitely many fixed “lattice-coordinate”, will be bonded together. If a symmetric
g:V\times V\to[0,+\infty)
of the form
g(x,y)=\phi(x-y)
measures how much energy is required to break bonds in the system, then a perfect crystal shape will be the one requiring the most energy to completely break apart, and breaking apart a finite configuration
\Omega\subset V
would require energy

\mathcal{E}_{g}(\Omega)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\Omega\\
x-y\in\mathcal{V}\end{subarray}}g(x,y)=\sharp\Omega\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\phi(v)-\sum_{(x,y)\in\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}}\phi(x-y),

(1.1)

where

\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}:=\{(x,y)\in\Lambda\times\Lambda:\ x\in\Omega,y\notin\Omega,x-y\in\mathcal{V}\}.

Note that in
V
each point may require at most a total of
\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\phi(v)
to be separated from its neighbors, thus the first term in (
1.1
) is the “bulk energy of the configuration” and is independent of the shape of
\Omega
. Then finding the most robust crystal shape of
\Omega\subset\Lambda
at fixed
\sharp\Omega
is equivalent to minimizing the ”discrete weighted surface area” here represented by

\sharp_{g}\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}:=-\sum_{(x,y)\in\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}}g(x,y).

(1.2)

If a minimum-energy
\Omega
exists at fixed
\sharp\Omega
and
\sharp_{g}\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}
has growth
O\left((\sharp\Omega)^{\frac{d-1}{d}}\right)
like an area term, similarly to the continuum case, then we also have an isoperimetric inequality which has the following scaling-invariant form:

(\sharp\Omega)^{d-1}\leq C_{iso}(\sharp_{g}\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega})^{d}.

(1.3)

More generally we work in the following setup:

•

Let
G=(V,A,E)
be an ambient graph, with
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
a discrete set,
A:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}
a symmetric weight function, and edge set
E=\{(x,y)\in V\times V:\ A(x,y)\neq 0\}
.

•

Consider a symmetric function
g:E\to[0,+\infty)
which represents the cost of edges of
G
.

Then we define
\sharp_{g}\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}
as in (
1.2
) and we desire to find sharp inequalities of the form (
1.3
) and to characterize the shape of configurations
\Omega\subset V
for wihch equality holds in (
1.3
). The optimum value
C_{iso}
will be called the sharp isoperimetric constant of the weighted graph
G
.

1.2
Wulff shape theory in the continuum

The continuum analogue of the cardinality
\sharp\Omega

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

|\Omega|=\mathcal{L}^{d}(\Omega)

\mathrm{Per}_{\phi}(\Omega):=\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}\phi(\nu(x))d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(x),

H_{\phi}:=\{p:\ \forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ p\cdot x<\phi(x)\}.

(1.4)

This is not the most general setup for the study of Wulff shapes: convexity and symmetry of

1.2.2
The Mass Transportation proof, following strategy (a)

A first way, based on to obtain the isoperimetric inequality is using the basic setup from mass transport theory, for more references see above. We consider a transport plan
T:\Omega\to H_{\phi}
, such that the pushforward under
T
of the measure
\mu
with density
1_{\Omega}
to the measure
\nu
with density
\frac{|\Omega|}{|H_{\phi}|}1_{H_{\phi}}
. Then we have

d|\Omega|\left(\frac{|H_{\phi}|}{|\Omega|}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}=d\int_{\Omega}(\mathrm{det}\nabla T)^{\frac{1}{d}}\leq\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{div}T=\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}T\cdot\nu_{\Omega}\ d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(x)=\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}|T|_{H_{\phi}}\phi(\nu_{\Omega})d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(x)\leq\mathrm{Per}_{\phi}(\Omega),

(1.5)

where we used, in order, the transport condition on
T
,
then the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality for the eigenvalues of the matrix
\nabla T
,
the divergence theorem, and then we introduce the quasinorm
|x|_{H}:=\inf\{\lambda>0:\ x/\lambda\in H\}
,
and
|T(x)|_{H_{\phi}}\leq 1
for
x\in\Omega
because
T(\Omega)\subset H_{\phi}
by definition, and the definition of
\mathrm{Per}_{\phi}(\Omega)
.

Note that in general the first inequality in (
1.5
) is an equality if and only if
\nabla T
almost everywhere is a multiple of the identity matrix, and the second inequality of (
1.5
) is an equality if and only if
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}
-almost everywhere on
\partial^{*}\Omega
we have
T\cdot\nu_{\Omega}=|T|_{H_{\phi}}\phi(\nu_{\Omega})
, which implies the characterization of the isoperimetric shape

\Omega=H_{\phi}
, of the optimal
T
, and of the precise constant in (
1.5
).

1.2.3
The PDE approach, following strategy (b)

A second approach is linked to the first one (at least for the case of convex
\Omega
and monotone
T
) via the Brenier characterization of the (optimal) transport map as subdifferential of convex function, thus to use
T=\nabla u
as the “parameter” in our inequalities. This second approach focuses on the linear
\mathrm{div}T=\Delta u
part of the inequalities, rather than on the nonlinear
\mathrm{det}\nabla T
part, achieving the same conclusion even for general
\Omega
. We pass to describe this second strategy.

We consider solutions
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta u=\frac{P_{\phi}(\Omega)}{|\Omega|}&\text{ on }\Omega\\[8.53581pt]
\partial_{\nu_{\Omega}}u=\phi(\nu_{\Omega})&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

(1.6)

Note that imposing that
\Delta u
is constant, the value of the constant is determined by the second equation of (
1.6
) via the divergence theorem applied to
\nabla u
. Rather than the previous
T(\Omega)\subset H_{\phi}
, we now have the condition that
H_{\phi}\subset\partial u(\Omega)
, where
\partial u(\cdot)
is the subdifferential measurable mapping associated to
u
:

\partial u(E):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ \exists x_{0}\in E,\ \forall x\in\overline{\Omega},\ u(x)\geq u(x_{0})+p\cdot(x-x_{0})\}&\text{ if }u\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})\\[8.53581pt]
\{\nabla u(x_{0}):\ x_{0}\in E,\ \forall x\in\overline{\Omega},\ u(x)\geq u(x_{0})+\nabla u(x_{0})\cdot(x-x_{0})\}&\text{ if }u\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega}).\end{array}\right.

For the case
u\in C^{1}
, the condition that with notation (
1.4
) we have
H_{\phi}\subset\partial u(\Omega)
, follows from the boundary condition in (
1.6
): if
p\in H_{\phi}
then either
u(x)-p\cdot x
achieves its minimum over
\overline{\Omega}
at some interior point
x_{0}\in\Omega
then
p=\nabla u(x_{0})\in\partial u(\Omega)
. If the minimum is only achieved at points
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
then
\partial_{\nu_{\Omega}}(u(x)-p\cdot x)\leq 0
, which together with the boundary datum in (
1.6
) at
x_{0}
gives
\phi(\nu_{\Omega}(x_{0}))=\partial_{\nu_{\Omega}}u(x_{0})\leq p\cdot\nu_{\Omega}(x_{0})
, which implies that
p\notin H_{\phi}
, a contradiction.

Now we can write, with notation
\Gamma_{u}:=\{x_{0}\in\Omega:\ \forall x\in\overline{\Omega},u(x)\geq u(x_{0})+\nabla u(x_{0})\cdot(x-x_{0})\}
,

|H_{\phi}|\leq|\partial u(\overline{\Omega})|=\int_{\nabla u(\Gamma_{u})}dp\leq\int_{\Gamma_{u}}|\mathrm{det}(\nabla^{2}u)|\leq\int_{\Gamma_{u}}\left(\frac{\Delta u}{d}\right)^{d}\leq|\Gamma_{u}|\left(\frac{d\ P_{\phi}(\Omega)}{|\Omega|}\right)^{d}\leq\frac{1}{d^{d}}\frac{[P_{\phi}(\Omega)]^{d}}{|\Omega|^{d-1}}.

(1.7)

Besides the above justified property that
H_{\phi}\subset\partial u(\Omega)=\nabla u(\Gamma_{u})
, we use
T_{u}:=\nabla u
as a change of variables, then we use the arithmetic-geometric inequality for the eigenvalues of
\nabla^{2}u
. The forelast equality follows from (
1.6
) and the last one follows because
\Gamma_{u}\subset\Omega
. As above, the inequalities become equalities only if
\Gamma_{u}=\Omega,\nabla^{2}u=\lambda\mathrm{Id}
and
\Omega=\lambda H_{\phi}
for some
\lambda>0
.

However, virtually all versions of the inequalities do not treat the question of determining the isoperimetric shapes, or discussing cases in which equality in the isoperimetric inequality can be reached. The only exception which we are aware of is the paper
[
32
]
, which treats the case of a lattice
V=D\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, with
D=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
a diagonal matrix, links only between nearest neighbors in the coordinate directions, and edge weights equal to surface areas of a box with sides parallel to the coordinate directions. The setup in that case is based on the Cabré method from (
1.7
). The maximum principle tools from
[
32
]
can be seen as fitting within the broader theory of discretization of PDE tools, for which we refer to the above work for a broad list of references, and here we only mention the works of Merkov
[
45
]
and the in-depth treatment by Kuo and Trudinger, see e.g.
[
38
]
,
[
39
]
,
[
40
]
and the references therein.

In the “product case”
D\mathbb{Z}^{d}
it is straightforward to obtain the discrete counterpart of the geometric-arithmetic mean inequality for eigenvalues of the Hessian, which in the continuum case reads

|\mathrm{det}(\nabla^{2}u)|^{1/d}=|\lambda_{1}\cdots\lambda_{d}|^{\frac{1}{d}}\leq\frac{\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{d}}{d}=\frac{\Delta u}{d}.

(1.8)

Note that if
V=\lambda\mathbb{Z}
the absolute value of the discrete Laplacian
|\Delta_{\lambda\mathbb{Z}}u(x)|=|u(x-\lambda)-2u(x)+u(x+\lambda)|
is equal to the size of the “proximal” subdifferential and we have

\displaystyle\{p\in\mathbb{R}:\forall y\in\overline{\Omega},\ u(y)\geq u(x)+p\cdot(x-y)\}=\partial u(x)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\partial^{\mathrm{prox}}u(x):=\{p\in\mathbb{R}:u(x\pm\lambda)\geq u(x)\pm\lambda p\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda}[u(x)-u(x-\lambda),u(x+\lambda)-u(x)].

For
D\mathbb{Z}^{d}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}\mathbb{Z}
the nearest-neighbor differential is a product of the above, which allows to use the geometric-arithmetic mean inequality directly.

In
[
32
]
the question of correctly geneneralizing the result to obtain isoperimetric constants in other lattices is mentioned as an interesting open problem at the end of their paper. This is the main motivation and direction of the present work.

The strategy: chain of inequalities (

1.4.1
Discrete chain of inequality paralleling (
1.7
)

If
\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}:=\{(x,y):\ x\in\Omega,\ y\notin\Omega,\ A(x,y)\neq 0\}
and
g:\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
is fixed, we solve a discrete Neumann value problem formulated as follows, for
\Omega\subset V
. As noted by
[
32
]
, the continuum equation (
1.6
) must be replaced by the below differential inequality (
1.9
) in the discrete case. The upper bound on
\Delta_{A}u
in (
1.9
) is fixed as in
[
32
]
so that we are sure that a solution exists, however in some cases it is not optimal. We give a first characterization the sharp optimal constant in Section
6
, however this is not explicit enough to help in practice, and further studies are left to future work. Therefore, we use the below bound (
1.9
) for our study.

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\Delta_{A}u(x):=\sum_{y\in V}A^{2}(y,x)(u(x)-u(y))\leq\frac{1}{\sharp\Omega}\sum_{(x,y)\in\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}}g(x,y)\quad\text{ for }x\in\Omega\\[8.53581pt]
\frac{\partial u}{\partial_{\nu_{\Omega}}}=\frac{g}{A}\quad\text{ on }\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}.\end{array}\right.

(1.9)

The boundary condition in (
1.9
) has two intepretations:

•

either the naïve one, stating that
u(y)-u(x)=\frac{g(x,y)}{A(x,y)}
for all
(x,y)\in\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}
,

•

or the one set up by Hamamuki in
[
32
]
, requiring that for each
y
outside
\Omega
having some neighbor in
\Omega
, there exist such neighbors
x
such that
u(y)-u(x)=\frac{g(x,y)}{A(x,y)}
.

The naïve interpretation will be discarded as it has the drawback that for general nonconvex domains
\Omega
a solution to (
1.9
) may not exist, whereas the Hamamuki interpretation allows to find a solution. A more detailed explanation is included in Sections
2.2
and
2.3
.

We assume that
u
is a solution to (
1.9
) with Hamamuki interpretation for the boundary condition. To this discrete
u
we associate subdifferentials
\partial_{\Omega}u(x)
depending on the values of
u
on
\Omega
and a target shape determined by the boundary values in (
1.9
):

\displaystyle\partial_{\Omega}u(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ (\forall\ z\in\overline{\Omega}),\ u(x)\leq u(z)+p\cdot(x-z)\},

\displaystyle\partial^{\mathrm{prox}}u(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ (\forall\ z:\ z\sim x),\ u(x)\leq u(z)+p\cdot(x-z)\},

\displaystyle H_{g}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ \forall(x,y)\in\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}\ p\cdot(y-x)\leq\frac{g(x,y)}{A(x,y)}\right\}.

(1.10)

We will also write
\partial u(x)
rather than
\partial_{\Omega}u(x)
if the choice of
\Omega
is clear from the context.

The discrete analogue of (
1.7
) states that the following holds

|H_{g}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\left|\bigcup_{x\in\Omega}\partial u(x)\right|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\sum_{x\in\Omega}|\partial u(x)|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leq}}\sum_{x\in\Omega}|\partial^{\mathrm{prox}}u(x)|\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{\leq}}\sum_{x\in\Omega}c_{x}\left(\Delta_{A}u(x)\right)^{d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(e)}}{{\leq}}(\max_{x\in\Omega}c_{x})\sharp\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\sharp\Omega}\sum_{(x,y)\in\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}}g(x,y)\right)^{d}:=\max_{x\in\Omega}c_{x}\frac{\left(\sharp_{g}\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}\right)^{d}}{(\sharp\Omega)^{d-1}}.

(1.11)

The above inequalities are proved and discussed as follows:

(a):

The boundary conditions of (
1.9
) ensure that
H_{g}\subset\bigcup_{x\in\Omega}\partial u(x):=\partial u(\Omega)
, like in the continuous case: fixing an element
p\in H_{g}
, if
u(x)-p\cdot x
achieves its minimum over
x\in\overline{\Omega}
on
\Omega
then
p\in\partial u(\Omega)
; if not, then there exists
(x,y)\in\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}
such that
u(y)-p\cdot x<u(x)-p\cdot x
: we then have

\frac{g(x,y)}{A(x,y)}=u(y)-u(x)<p\cdot(y-x),

contradicting the assumption that
p\in H_{g}
. Furthermore, note that if equality holds in (a) and
u
is the restriction of a convex function, then
H_{g}=\partial u(\Omega)
and
\Omega
is of the form
\Omega=K\cap V
with
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
a convex set.

(b):

The subdifferentials of
u
are essentially disjoint (i.e. they have zero measure intersection), as we show in Lemma
2.2
. If equality also holds in (a) then the
\{\partial u(x),x\in\Omega\}
, form a partition of
H_{g}
.

(c):

In general, the proximal subdifferential
\partial^{\mathrm{prox}}u(x)
includes
\partial_{\Omega}u(x)
but is not equal to it. We find in Proposition
2.1
a condition on the underlying graph edges
\vec{E}=\{(x,y):\ A(x,y)\neq 0\}
which ensure that
\partial^{\mathrm{prox}}u(x)=\partial_{\Omega}u(x)\neq\emptyset
holds at all
x\in\Omega
only if
u
is convex.

(d):

This step is a geometric discrete arithmetic-geometric mean inequality, which depends on the neighborhood geometry near
x\in\Omega
. Indeed, the volume of the polyhedron
\partial^{\mathrm{prox}}u(x)
and the discrete Laplacian
\Delta_{A}u(x)=\sum_{y:y\sim x}A^{2}(x,y)(u(x)-u(y))
coincide with an arithmetic and geometric means in the case of a rectangular lattice treated in
[
32
]
. In general, we have a dilation-invariant bound

|\partial^{\mathrm{prox}}u(x)|\leq c_{x}\left(\Delta_{A}u(x)\right)^{d},

(1.12)

where the optimal constant
c_{x}=C_{\mathcal{V}_{x}}
depends on the geometry of the neighbor directions
\mathcal{V}_{x}:=\{(y-x)/|y-x|:\ y\simeq x\}
and on
A
, and is related to a version of the Minkowski theorem from convex geometry, described in Section
3
. As indicated in Theorem
3.1
, the optimum shape of
\partial^{\mathrm{prox}}u(x)
so that an equality appears in this step, is unique up to translation: it will depend only on
\mathcal{V}_{x},A
.

(e):

The quantity
C(\Omega,g)
in (
1.9
) obtained formally from the divergence theorem for the operator
\Delta_{A}
, is not necessarily optimal for general geometries, however allows to solve (
1.9
) as discussed in
[
32
]
in the product case. This value of
C(\Omega,g)
is sufficient for sharp bounds in several important examples, as indicated in Section
5
. We find a characterization of the optimal constant in the general case in Theorem
1.2
, however we do not investigate its applications here, as it is based on a not well-understood discrete equation (
6.6
) on directed graphs. See section
6
for details. Finally, note that if the
G
is connected and there is an equality in (d), then the

are all equal to each other. See Theorem
1.1
and Section
3
for details.

1.4.2
Interpretation via Semidiscrete Optimal Transport, following (
1.5
)

We first recall, following
[
4
]
, the theory of semidiscrete optimal transport solutions in our setting. To imitate the framework (
1.5

\mu:=\frac{|H_{\phi}|}{\sharp\Omega}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\delta_{x},\quad\nu:=1_{H_{\phi}}(x)dx.

However, rather than finding a map satisfying
T_{\sharp}\mu=\nu
(which is impossible since the pushforward by a measurable map of an atomic measure is always an atomic measure), we look for
T
going in the opposite direction, and consider an “optimal” map
T:H_{\phi}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
T_{\sharp}\nu=\mu
, i.e.
T
is also minimizing the transport cost
\int_{H_{\phi}}|x-T(x)|^{2}d\nu(x)
under the constraint that
T_{\sharp}\nu=\mu
. In this case the map exists and, due to Brenier’s theory
[
11
]
, it can be represented as
T=\nabla\phi
for a (unique up to summing constant) convex
\phi:H_{\phi}\to\mathbb{R}
. The map
\phi
is a so-called
Pogorelov solution
to the optimal transport problem between
\nu
and
\mu
with cost
|x-y|^{2}
. An
Aleksandrov solution
to the same problem is a convex function
\phi^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that the subdifferential map
\partial\phi^{*}
satisfies
|\partial\phi^{*}(E)|=\mu(E)
for
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\partial\phi^{*}(\Omega)=H_{\phi}
. For the Pogorelov solution there exist
c_{x}\in\mathbb{R},C_{x}\subset H_{\phi}
with
|C_{x}|=|H_{\phi}|/\sharp\Omega
for all
x\in\Omega
, which are related to
\phi
as follows:

\forall y\in H_{\phi},\ \phi(y)=\max_{x\in\Omega}\{y\cdot x-c_{x}\},\quad\quad\forall x\in\Omega,\ C_{x}=\{y\in H_{\phi}:\ \nabla\phi(y)=x\}=\{y\in H_{\phi}:\ \phi(y)=y\cdot x-c_{x}\}.

The Legendre transform of a Pogorelov solution is a so-called Aleksandrov solution
\phi^{*}(x):=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\{x\cdot y-\phi(y)\}
, which satisfies

\forall x\in\Omega,\quad\phi^{*}(x)=c_{x},\quad\text{and}\quad\partial\phi^{*}(x)=C_{x}.

2.1
Some useful convex analysis lemmas

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
d\geq 1
be an integer and let
G=(V,L)
be a graph where
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Suppose that
\Omega\subset V
is finite. For any function
u:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
. For all
x\in\Omega
there holds

\displaystyle\partial_{\Omega}u(x)\subset\bigcap_{v:\{x,x+v\}\in L}\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:p\cdot v\leq u(x+v)-u(x)\}.

Proof.

We assume that
\partial_{\Omega}^{-}u(x)\neq\emptyset
, else there is nothing to prove. Let
p\in\partial_{\Omega}u(x)
, which means that for all
z\in\overline{\Omega}
we have that

u(x)\leq u(z)-p\cdot(z-x).

If we test the above for
z=x+v
with
v
such that
\{x,v\}\in L
, we have that
z\in\overline{\Omega}
, by the definition of
\overline{\Omega}
, thus the above inequality holds for these choices, and it reads

u(x+v)\geq p\cdot v+u(x).

We thus have, reordening the inequality, for each
v
as above,

p\cdot v\leq u(x+v)-u(x).

(2.1)

∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Assume that
\overline{\Omega}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a closed set and for
u:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
and
x\in\overline{\Omega}
define

\partial_{\overline{\Omega}}u(x):=\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ (\forall y\in\overline{\Omega}),\ \langle p,y-x\rangle\leq u(y)-u(x)\}.

Then for
x\neq z\in\overline{\Omega}
the sets
\partial_{\overline{\Omega}}u(x),\partial_{\overline{\Omega}}u(z)
intersect at most their boundaries.

Proof.

The condition
p\in\partial_{\overline{\Omega}}u(x)\cap\partial_{\overline{\Omega}}u(z)
directly translates into

(\forall y\in\overline{\Omega}),\ \langle p,y-x\rangle\leq u(y)-u(x)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\langle p,y-z\rangle\leq u(y)-u(z).

In particular, we test the first condition with
y=z
and the second with
y=x
and we find

\langle p,x-z\rangle=u(x)-u(z).

This implies that
p
belongs to the boundaries of the two subdifferentials, because the equality in the inequality from the definition of the subdifferentials is achieved at
p
.
∎

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
G
be a graph with vertices a discrete set
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and edges
(x,y)
such that for all
x\in V
, denoting
y\sim x
the neighborhood relationship in
G
, there holds

\mathrm{conv}\left(\{y\in V:\ x\sim y\}\right)\cap V=\{x\}\cup\{y\in V:\ x\sim y\}.

(2.2)

If
u:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies
\partial^{\mathrm{prox}}u(x)=\partial_{\Omega}u(x)\neq\emptyset
for all
x\in\Omega
, then
u
is the restriction of a convex function
\tilde{u}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

We first note that
u
is the restriction of a convex
\tilde{u}
if and only if
\tilde{u}^{\Omega}|_{\overline{\Omega}}=u
, where the convex envelope
\tilde{u}^{\Omega}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined via the condition

\mathrm{epi}(\tilde{u}^{\Omega})=\bigcap\{H_{\pi}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}:\ \pi\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\text{ is a hyperplane such that }\pi\cap\mathrm{epi}(u)=\emptyset\},

where for
f:A\to\mathbb{R}
and
\pi=\{(x,t):\ t=\langle p,x\rangle\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
a non-vertical hyperplane, we define

\mathrm{epi}f:=\{(x,t):\ x\in\mathrm{A},\ t\in\mathbb{R},\ t\geq\tilde{u}(x)\},\quad H_{\pi}:=\{(x,t):\ t\geq\langle p,x\rangle\}.

We call a plane
\pi
as above such that
\partial H_{\pi}\cap\partial\mathrm{epi}(\tilde{u}^{\Omega})
has dimension
d
a
nondegenerate support plane of
\mathrm{epi}(\tilde{u}^{\Omega})
.

Now assume that
G
,
u
are as in the proposition but that

, which as seen above is equivalent to saying that

is not the restriction of a convex function. Then there exist
x_{0}\sim y_{0}\in\Omega
such that
u(x_{0})=\tilde{u}^{\Omega}(x_{0})
and
u(y_{0})\neq\tilde{u}^{\Omega}(y_{0})
.

We note that
\partial\,\mathrm{epi}(\tilde{u}^{\Omega})
is a polytope with vertices of the form

with

and

, and faces contained in its nondegenerate support planes. Up to reducing to an equivalent problem in lower dimension, and using condition (
2.2
), we may assume that there are

such that
u(x_{i})=\tilde{u}^{\Omega}(x_{i}),i=1,\dots,d

.
Then we have

K:=\mathrm{conv}\{(x_{0},u(x_{0})),\dots,(x_{d},u(x_{d}))\}\subset\partial\,\mathrm{epi}(\tilde{u}^{\Omega}).

(2.3)

On the other hand, note that for each

there holds

\mathrm{epi}(\tilde{u}^{\Omega})\subset\bigcap\{H_{\pi}:\ \pi=\{(x,t):t=u(x_{0})+\langle p,(x-x_{0})\rangle\},p\in\partial_{\Omega}u(x)\},

(2.4)

and thus due to the hypotheses that

for all

K\nsubseteq\bigcap\{H_{\pi}:\ \pi=\{(x,t):t=u(x_{0})+\langle p,(x-y_{0})\rangle\},\ p\in\partial^{\mathrm{prox}}u(y_{0})\}.

(2.5)

Now (

) and (

which is a contradiction, as desired.

∎

2.2
Naïve discrete Neumann boundary value condition

This subsection is includes two well-known elementary results, which we include as a motivation for the next subsection, and for future reference.

Let
G,A
be as in the introduction and consider functions
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
,
g:\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
and
u:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
. We are going to define what we mean when we say that
u
is a “naïve” discrete solution of the Neumann boundary value problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\Delta_{A}u=f&\text{in }\Omega,\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}=\frac{g}{A}&\text{on }\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}.\end{array}\right.

(2.6)

Our naïve choice is that the first equation in (
2.6
) means that
\Delta_{A}u(x)=f(x)
for all
x\in\Omega
and the second equation will mean that for all
(x,y)\in\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}
, there holds
u(y)-u(x)=\frac{g(x,y)}{A(x,y)}
. The divergence theorem applied to the combinatorial vector field
\nabla_{A}u(x,y)=A(x,y)(u(y)-u(x))
, implies that a necessary condition for a solution of (
2.6
) to exist is that

\sum_{x\in\Omega}f(x)=\sum_{(x,y)\in\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}}g(x,y).

(2.7)

Unfortunately, condition (
2.7
) is not sufficient, unlike what happens in the continuous case. This follows by comparing degrees of freedom of the two sides of (
2.6
):
u
is determined by the choice of its
\sharp\overline{\Omega}
independent values over its domain, whereas
f
and
g
are similarly determined by
\sharp\Omega+\sharp\partial\Omega
independent values. We can prove the following simple and very general result

Lemma 2.3
.

If
G=(V,E)
is a graph with vertices
V
and unoriented edges
E
. For any finite
\Omega\subset V
, defining the oriented boundary
\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}:=\{(x,y):\ \{x,y\}\in E,\ x\in\Omega,y\notin\Omega\}
and
\overline{\Omega}:=\Omega\cup\{x\in V:\ \exists y\in\Omega\mbox{ such that }\{x,y\}\in E\}
, there holds

\sharp(\overline{\Omega}\setminus\Omega)\leq\sharp\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega},

(2.8)

with equality if and only if for each
y\in\overline{\Omega}\setminus\Omega
there exists exactly one
x\in\Omega
such that
\{x,y\}\in E_{\Omega}
.

Proof.

To prove (
2.8
), we show that the map
\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}\ni(x,y)\mapsto y\in\overline{\Omega}\setminus\Omega
is a surjection. Indeed, by definition any
y\in\overline{\Omega}\setminus\Omega
is an extreme of an edge
(x,y)
with the
x\in\Omega
, thus
y
is in the image of the above map. The map is bijective precisely if such
x
is unique, completing the proof.
∎

We consider first the case in which equality holds in (
2.8
). In this case we have the following result:

Lemma 2.4
.

Assume that
\sharp\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}=\sharp(\overline{\Omega}\setminus\Omega)
. Then for any data
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
such that condition (
2.7
) holds on every connected component of
\Omega
, there exists exactly one solution to (
2.6
), up to addition of a function which is constant on each connected component of
\overline{\Omega}
.

Proof.

As
\sharp\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}=\sharp(\overline{\Omega}\setminus\Omega)
, for each
x\in\overline{\Omega}\setminus\Omega
there exists a unique
y_{x}\in\Omega
such that
(y_{x},x)\in\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}
. With this notation we define

L:\{u:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}\}\to\{u:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}\},\quad(Lu)(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta_{A}u(x)&\mbox{ if }x\in\Omega,\\[8.53581pt]
A^{2}(x,y_{x})(u(y_{x})-u(x))&\mbox{ if }x\in\overline{\Omega}\setminus\Omega.\end{array}\right.

We first show that
L
is self adjoint with respect to the scalar product given by

\langle u,v\rangle:=\sum_{x\in\Omega}u(x)v(x)+\sum_{x\in\overline{\Omega}\setminus\Omega}u(x)v(x).

Indeed we have, denoting
d_{x}:=\sum_{y\in\overline{\Omega}}A^{2}(x,y)
the total squared weight of neighbors of a vertex
x
in
\overline{\Omega}
,

\displaystyle\langle Lu,v\rangle=-\sum_{x\in\Omega}\sum_{y:\{x,y\}\in E}A^{2}(x,y)(u(y)-u(x))v(x)+\sum_{(x,y)\in\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}}A^{2}(x,y)(u(y)-u(x))v(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\Omega}d_{x}\ u(x)v(x)-\sum_{x\in\Omega}\sum_{y\in\overline{\Omega}:\{x,y\}\in E}A^{2}(x,y)u(y)v(x)+\sum_{x\in\overline{\Omega}\setminus\Omega}A^{2}(y_{x},x)u(x)v(x)-\sum_{x\in\overline{\Omega}\setminus\Omega}\sum_{y\in\overline{\Omega}:\{x,y\}\in E}A^{2}(x,y)u(x)v(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}d_{x}\ u(x)v(x)-\sum_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}\sum_{y\in\overline{\Omega}:\{x,y\}\in E}A^{2}(x,y)u(y)v(x).

The last sum is completely symmetric in
u,v
, because
(x,y)\in\vec{E}
if and only if
(y,x)\in\vec{E}
as well. This proves that
\langle Lu,v\rangle=\langle v,Lu\rangle
, as desired.

Next, we show that a function is in the kernel of
L
if and only if it is constant on every connected component of
\overline{\Omega}
. Otherwise, suppose that
Lu=0
and that
u
has a strict local maximum at
y\in\overline{\Omega}
. If
y\in\Omega
then the condition
\Delta_{A}u(y)=0
implies that the value
u(y)
is the
A^{2}
-weighted average of the values of
u
at the neighbors of
y
in
\overline{\Omega}
, which contradicts the fact that
y
is a strict local maximum. If
x\in\overline{\Omega}\setminus\Omega
then
u(y_{x})=u(x)
and by our hypothesis
y_{x}
is the only neighbor of
x
, again contradicting the local maximum property. This shows that
Lu=0
implies that
u
is constant on each connected component of
\overline{\Omega}
.

Let
\bar{U}_{j}
with
1\leq j\leq k
be an enumeration of the connected components of
\overline{\Omega}
. Then there exists a partition of
\Omega
given by
U_{j},1\leq j\leq k
such that
\bar{U}_{j}
is really the closure of
U_{j}
, as the notation indicates. As the sets
\bar{U}_{j}
form a partition of
\overline{\Omega}
, considering the equation
Lu(x)=b(x)
for all
x\in\overline{\Omega}
translates into requiring
Lu(x)=b(x)
for
x\in\bar{U}_{j}
and
1\leq j\leq k
. For fixed
j
, we have
\sharp\overrightarrow{\partial U_{j}}=\sharp((\overline{\Omega}\setminus\Omega)\cap U_{j})
. Thus we can replicate the procedure from the previous paragraph to show that for

is selfadjoint. Therefore the system given by

for all

has a solution if and only if

. Without loss of generality, we can consider an ordered version of

at
U_{j}
and the final coordinates are the values of

on the edges of
\overrightarrow{\partial U_{j}}
. Then the fact that

has a solution is equivalent to

\sum_{x\in U_{j}}f(x)=\sum_{(x,y)\in\overrightarrow{\partial U_{j}}}g(x,y)

As

is selfadjoint, the image of

∎

Remark 2.2

(Need of a different boundary value definition)
.

In many cases of interest, such as the triangular graph, or the case of non-convex domains

2.3
A discrete Neumann boundary value condition adapted to subdifferential compraisons (following

3
An optimization result for polyhedra in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
related to Minkowski’s theorem

We introduce a finite-dimensional optimization problem which it is useful to study for the final discussion. To formulate the problem in its general form, we use the following notation for a halfspace with normal vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and boundary the hyperplane
\{p:\ p\cdot v=c\}
:

H_{v}(c):=\{p:\langle p,v\rangle\leq c\}.

where
c\in\mathbb{R}
is a constant.

If we fix
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
appropriately, there holds

\left|\bigcap_{v\in\mathcal{V}}H_{v}(c_{v})\right|\leq C_{\mathcal{V}}\left(\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}c_{v}\right)^{d},

(3.1)

which for
\mathcal{V}=\{\pm e_{1},\ldots,\pm e_{d}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is equivalent to the arithmetic-geometric inequality, for which we have optimal values
c_{v}=\frac{1}{2d}
for all
v\in\mathcal{V}
and
C_{\mathcal{V}}=\frac{1}{d^{d}}
. Thus (
3.1
) generalizes this inequality.

The optimal constant in (
3.1
) can be expressed as follows:

C_{\mathcal{V}}=\max\left\{\left|\bigcap_{v\in\mathcal{V}}H_{v}(c_{v})\right|:\vec{c}=(c_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}},\ \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}c_{v}=1\right\}.

(3.2)

In the above,
|\cdot|
represents the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the maximum exists only if the convex hull
\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{V})
has the origin as an interior point. We will only study this problem for
finite
\mathcal{V}
, although it can be studied also in higher generality. We will denote from now on

\mathcal{V}=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{N}\}.

(3.3)

The below results turned out to be equivalent to the proof of Minkowski’s theorem which describes convex polyhedra with given facet normals and areas, see
[
1
, Ch. 7]
. Minkowski introduced a problem equivalent to (
3.2
) as a tool for constructing polyhedra and showing that they cannot be constructed in some cases. In our case we follow the opposite direction: we start from (
3.2
) and are interested in the characterization of the solutions. We will state the theorems here, and we sketch the proofs in the appendix. The interested reader can find a large overlap with the above cited book of Alexandrov.

Lemma 3.1
.

With the above notation the following hold:

1.

The problem (
3.2
) has a finite maximum value if and only if

\mathrm{Span}\mathcal{V}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}v=0.

(3.4)

2.

If (
3.4
) holds and
\mathcal{R}_{\vec{c}}:=\bigcap_{v\in\mathcal{V}}H_{v}(c_{v})
is an optimizer, then
\mathcal{R}_{\vec{c}}+w
is also an optimizer for each
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We also provide a proof of the above lemma, for completeness.

Proof.

We start by proving the second statement of the theorem. It suffices to show that
\mathcal{R}_{\vec{c}}+w
is also a solution to (
3.2
) if
\mathcal{R}_{\vec{c}}
is. For this, note that

w+H_{v}(c_{v})=H_{v}(c_{v}+\langle w,v\rangle).

(3.5)

Therefore
\mathcal{R}_{\vec{c}}+w=\mathcal{R}_{\vec{c}^{\prime}}
, where
c^{\prime}_{v}=c_{v}+\langle w,v\rangle
for
v\in\mathcal{V}
. In particular

\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}c^{\prime}_{v}=1\ +\ \left\langle w,\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}v\right\rangle=0,

which shows that
\mathcal{R}_{\vec{c}}+w
is also a solution to (
3.2
) if
\mathcal{R}_{\vec{c}}
is, as desired.

To prove the necessity of (
3.4
) for the existence of a maximizer in (
3.2
), assume that (
3.4
) does not hold, and let

\bar{v}:=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}v\neq 0.

Then we use again (
3.5
) and for
w=-\lambda\bar{v}
if
\vec{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}}
satisfies
\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}c_{v}=1
and
|\mathcal{R}_{\vec{c}}|>0
then we find

w+\mathcal{R}_{\vec{c}}=\mathcal{R}_{\vec{c}^{\prime}}:=\mathcal{R}_{\vec{c}-\lambda|\bar{v}|^{2}\vec{1}},

in particular if for a fixed
\epsilon\in(0,1)
we choose
\lambda=\frac{1-\epsilon}{|\bar{v}|^{2}\sharp\mathcal{V}}
then
\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}c_{v}^{\prime}=\epsilon
, and thus we have found
w=w_{\epsilon}
as above, such that

\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\vec{c}}+w_{\epsilon}\right)\quad\mbox{ is a competitor in \eqref{subdopt1}}.

(3.6)

But due to the dilation by
1/\epsilon
, the competitor (
3.6
) has volume
1/\epsilon^{d}
times larger than the one of
\mathcal{R}_{\vec{c}}
. This construction can be done for
\epsilon
arbitrarily close to
0
, which shows that (
3.2
) has no solution if (
3.4
) fails.

It remains to prove that if (
3.4
) holds then a solution to (
3.2
) exists. First note that the vectors
v\in\mathcal{V}
have
0
in their convex hull, and thus taking all the
c_{v}\geq 0
(for example
c_{v}=1/\sharp\mathcal{V}
for all
v\in\mathcal{V}
), we find
\mathcal{R}_{\vec{c}}
with nonempty interior and contained in the ball centered at zero with radius
\max_{v\in\mathcal{V}}|v|
, and thus it has bounded volume.

A consequence of the above is that competitors with nonempty interior exist, and they are the only ones with nonzero volume because we are in a space of finite dimension. Now by the result of the already proved item 2 of the lemma, under condition (
3.4
) any competitor of nonempty interior to (
3.2
) can be translated so that the origin is in its interior, in which case we have
c_{v}\geq 0
for all
v\in\mathcal{V}
and find an upper bound of
|\mathcal{R}_{\vec{c}}|
as in the last paragraph. Now classical arguments allow to prove the existence of minimizers, and we skip them.
∎

We now state a characterization of the optimizers of (
3.2
):

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{V}=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{N}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be such that (
3.4
) holds, so that problem (
3.2
) has a solution. Then polyhedron
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{V}}
realizing the maximum in (
3.2
) is unique up to translation, with face normals
v/|v|
with
v\in\mathcal{V}
. Furthermore, there exists
\alpha_{\mathcal{V}}>0
depending only on
\mathcal{V}
such that for each
v\in\mathcal{V}

\text{The face $F_{v}$ of }\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{V}}\text{ that has exterior normal }\frac{v}{|v|}\text{ has }\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(F_{v})=\alpha_{\mathcal{V}}|v|.

(3.7)

Furthermore,
C_{\mathcal{V}}=|\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{V}}|=\frac{\alpha_{\mathcal{V}}}{d}
.

The main ingredients of the proof are the following:

•

The optimizer exists and is bounded due to Lemma (
3.1
), and we call it
\mathcal{R}_{\vec{c}}
.

•

For each
k=1,\ldots,N
the hyperplane
\partial H_{v_{k}}(c_{k})
intersects
\partial R_{\vec{c}}
because otherwise there would be the freedom to diminish
c_{k}
and increase the
c_{j},j\neq k
, which would increase the volume of
\mathcal{R}_{\vec{c}}
.

•

We have
\frac{\partial R_{\vec{c}}}{\partial c_{k}}=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial H_{v_{k}}(c_{k})\cap\mathcal{R}_{\vec{c}})}{|v_{k}|}
for each
k
. Indeed if we increase
c_{k}
by
\epsilon>0
small enough, then
\mathcal{R}_{\vec{c}}
will increase by a region which can be approximated as a prism with height
\epsilon/|v_{k}|
and base
\partial H_{v_{k}}(c_{k})\cap\mathcal{R}_{\vec{c}}
. For more details see
[
1
, 7.2.2]
.

•

Now the theorem follows by using Lagrange multipliers.

•

Up to translation,
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{V}}
contains the origin and then
c_{k}\geq 0
for all
1\leq k\leq N
. Then
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{V}}
is the union of pyramids with one vertex at the origin, heights
c_{k}/|v_{k}|
and bases
F_{k}
. Thus we have

|\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{V}}|=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{c_{k}}{|v_{k}|}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(F_{k})=\frac{\alpha_{\mathcal{V}}}{d}\sum_{k}c_{k}=\frac{\alpha_{\mathcal{V}}}{d},

which gives the last statement of the theorem.

For more details we again refer the reader to
[
1
, 7.2]
.

Remark 3.1

(Computation of the maximum value
\alpha_{\mathcal{V}}/d
)
.

We are not aware of a general formula for
\alpha_{\mathcal{V}}
in terms of
\mathcal{V}
. This problem is equivalent to the one of computing the volume of a general convex polyhedron given its normals and face areas. See however iterative algorithms present in
[
41
]
and for the

[
51
]
.

We also note that a corollary of Theorem
3.1
, we have Minkowski’s famous theorem:

Theorem 3.2

(Minkowski)
.

If
\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{N}
are unit vectors in an Euclidean space and
f_{1},\ldots,f_{N}>0
are positive numbers, then a convex polyhedron with face normals
\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{N}
and
d-1
-dimensional area of the face with normal
\nu_{k}
equal to
f_{k}
for
1\leq k\leq N
exists if and only if

\sum_{k=1}^{N}f_{i}\nu_{i}=0,

(3.8)

and is unique up to translations.

The connection of Theorem
3.2
to the setup of the rest of this section is that
\nu_{k}=v_{k}/|v_{k}|
and (
3.8
) then corresponds to (
3.4
). As mentioned before, Minkowski’s proof idea was equivalent to the one approach presented here, and in a way our presentation just reformulates Minkowski’s proof in our setting.

3.1
Comparison to a related “Wulff shape” problem

The condition
\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}c_{v}=1
relates directly to our setup for involving the discrete graph laplacian. It is interesting to compare the minimization (
3.2
) itself to an isotropic isoperimetric problem. What seems the smallest perturbation of our problem in that direction would be to replace the constraint in (
3.2
) by the fixed-area constraint

\mathcal{H}^{d-1}\left(\partial\bigcap_{v\in\mathcal{V}}H_{v}(c_{v})\right)=1.

(3.9)

Then, if we optimize with respec to the
c_{v}
the same quantity as in (
3.2
) with constraint (
3.9
), we find that the optimizer exists if and only if the one in the initial problem (
3.2
) exists, and minimizers of the new and old problem are related by translation and dilation by a factor depending only on
\mathcal{V}
. Then we could proceed as follows:

1.

The problem constructed in the previous step has a solution then this is up to dilation also the solution of the dual problem

\min\left\{\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial R):\ \exists\vec{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}},\ \mathcal{R}=\bigcap_{v\in\mathcal{V}}H_{v}(c_{v}),|\mathcal{R}|=1\right\}.

(3.10)

2.

Problem (
3.10
) can be rephrased as an anisotropic perimeter problem (below
\nu
is a
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}
-measurable function which equals almost everywhere on

:

\min\left\{\int_{\partial R}F(\nu(x))\ d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(x):\ \partial\mathcal{R}\mbox{ rectifiable, }|\mathcal{R}|=1\right\},

(3.11a)

where

F:\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\to[0,+\infty],\qquad F(\nu)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{ if }\nu\in\mathcal{V},\\

(3.11b)

The solution to (

(3.12)

The simple expression (

Coming back to the solution

4.1
Survey: Isomorphisms between Reciprocals, Liftings, Pogorelov and Aleksandrov solutions, for general rectilinear cell decompositions

We introduce here some results of Rybnikov
[
48
]
and Aurenhammer
[
2
]
related to our setting, as a way of introducing semidiscrete optimal transport notation. The connection of this setup to Optimal Transport appears in
[
44
]
,
[
4
]
.

We invite the reader to consult
[
48
]
, who develops the very elegant fundamental setup in which one can define ad-hoc obstructions for the extension of given objects (such as convex functions, dual complexes, suitable liftings, etc.) around combinatorial cycles, and in providing conditions under which they vanish. Note that Rybnikov’s results are available for general piecewise-linear realizations of manifolds with vanishing first
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-homology group and satisfying local combinatorial non-degeneracy conditions. For simplicity (especially, in order to avoid all discussions about fixing orientations) we restrict to the case of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
here, although extensions of our methods and results to Rybnikov’s more general setting would be also possible.

Here are the notations and definitions that we need. In the below,
\mathcal{K}
will denote a
d
-dimensional simplicial complex and
K
will be its piecewise-linear realization of
\mathcal{K}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. By this we mean that each
k
-dimensional cell of
K
is a
k
-dimensional polyhedron identified bijectively to a
k
-cell in
\mathcal{K}
, so that the combinatorial cell complex structure of
\mathcal{K}
is conjugated by this bijection to the piecewise affine cell complex structure of
K
. We assume that orientations are defined on cells of
K
and are compatible with the combinatorial boundary operators. Note that no immersion or embedding requirements are made on this identification at this point. A further regularity condition is needed on our complexes. We only apply the below results for the case of convex bounded
k
-cells, however Rybnikov’s weaker assumption is that each
k
-cell has a homology
(k-1)
-sphere as boundary.

•

A
lifting of
K
is an assignment to each
k
-cell
C
of
K
, for all
k=0,\ldots,d
, of an affine
\mathbb{R}
-valued function
L(C)
, so that
L(C)|_{F}=L(F)
for each
F\in\partial C
. A lifting is called
locally convex
if for each subcomplex
K^{\prime}
of
K
which is embedded into
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the restrictions of
L
to cells in
K^{\prime}
coincide with a convex function. A lifting is called
sharp
if
\nabla L(C)\neq\nabla L(C^{\prime})
whenever
C,C^{\prime}
are adjacent
d
-cells in
K
.

•

The
combinatorial dual graph
of
\mathcal{K}
, denoted
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{K})
, is a graph whose vertices correspond to
d
-cells of
\mathcal{K}
, and whose edges correspond to internal
(d-1)
-cells of
\mathcal{K}
.

•

A
reciprocal of
K
is a piecewise-linear realization
R
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the combinatorial dual graph
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{K})
such that the edges of
R
are segments perpendicular to the corresponding
(d-1)
-dimensional facets of
K
. If none of the edges of a reciprocal collapses into a point, then the reciprocal is called
non-degenerate
. We assume that an orientation on
R
is defined and is compatible with the orientation on
K
, and if furthermore
R
is an embedding then we call it
convex
.

Theorem 4.1

(convex liftings
\simeq
convex reciprocals
[
48
, Thm. 6.1]
)
.

If
K
is a face-to-face polyhedral decomposition of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
L:K\to\mathrm{Aff}(d)
is a lifting of
K
, then to
L
we associate those realizations of
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{K})
that satisfy the property

R(C)-R(C^{\prime})=\nabla L(C)-\nabla L(C^{\prime})\quad\text{whenever}\quad C,C^{\prime}\quad\text{are adjacent $d$-cells of $K$.}

This association establishes a bijection between reciprocals of
K
defined up to a translation and liftings of
K
defined up to summing an affine function. Moreover, sharp liftings correspond to non-degenerate reciprocals, and convex liftings correspond to convex reciprocals under the above association.

•

Given a discrete point configuration
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a map
w:V\to\mathbb{R}
, the associated
weighted Voronoi diagram
(also found under the names of:
power diagram
,
Laguerre diagram
,
weighted Dirichlet decomposition
) is the collection of cells, indexed by
V
, where the cell associated to a given
p\in V
is given by

C(p):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ |p-x|^{2}-w(p)\leq|q-x|^{2}-w(q)\text{ for all $q\in V$}\}.

(4.1)

Note that
C(p)
is necessarily convex for all
p\in V
.

The proof idea for the second part of the following is present in Aurenhammer
[
2
]
, and Rybnikov
[
48

notes that it can be extended to the present setting:

Theorem 4.2

(convex liftings and weighted Voronoi diagrams
[
2
]
,
[
48
]
)
.

Let
K
be a face-to-face decomposition of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
into convex finite polyhedra, and that

is a lifting. Then the following hold:

1.

L
is locally convex if and only if there exists a convex function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\phi|_{C}=L(C)|_{C}
for each cell

of

2.

If

is a reciprocal associated to

as in Theorem
4.1
and

are the vertices of

are the

-cells of

. This association iduces a bijection between convex liftings defined up to an affine summand and weights defined up to additive constants.

Finally, we note the connection to classical Optimal Transport theory. In optimal transport theory the result is usually proved for the case of finite

[

(convex lifitings and semidiscrete Optimal Transport solutions)

Assume that

4.2
Discrete isoperimetric inequality and discussion of equality cases

We now formulate sufficient conditions for the existence of the optimal isoperimetric shape. These conditions will be further discussed and accompanied by several examples in Section
5
. Note that only the case
g=1
will be explored in details, however the result for general
g
may be of interest and requires no essential extra effort.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a discrete set and
A:V\times V\to[0,+\infty)
be a symmetric nonnegative weight. Let
G
be the graph with vertices
V
and edges
E_{A}:=\{(x,y):\ A(x,y)\neq 0\}
. Assume that condition (
2.2
) holds at each
x\in V
and that
G
is locally finite. Let
\Omega\subset V
be a finite subset and let
\overline{\Omega}
be the closure of
\Omega
in
G
. Let
g:E_{A}\to[0,+\infty)
be a second weight, and set
\sharp_{g}\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}:=\sum_{(x,y)\in\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}}g(x,y)
, where
\sharp_{g}\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}=\sharp\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}
for
g\equiv 1
.

Let
u:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
be a solution, in the sense (
2.9
), of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta_{A}u(x)\leq\frac{\sharp_{g}\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}}{\sharp\Omega}&\text{ if }x\in\Omega,\\[8.53581pt]
\partial_{\nu}u(x)=\frac{g(x,y)}{A(x,y)}&\text{ if }(x,y)\in\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}.\end{array}\right.

(4.2)

Then we have, with notations (
3.2
) and (
3.13
),

\left|\partial u(\Omega)\right|\leq\sum_{x\in\Omega}|\partial^{\mathrm{prox}}u(x)|\leq\sum_{x\in\Omega}C_{\mathcal{V}_{x}}\left(\Delta_{A}u(x)\right)^{d}\leq\left(\max_{x\in\Omega}C_{\mathcal{V}_{x}}\right)\frac{(\sharp_{g}\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega})^{d}}{(\sharp\Omega)^{d-1}}.

(4.3)

Necessary conditions for equality.
If equality holds in (
4.3
) then all
C_{\mathcal{V}_{x}},x\in\Omega
are equal,
G|_{\overline{\Omega}}
is the reciprocal of a face-to-face decomposition of
\partial u(\Omega)
into convex polyhedra
\partial^{\mathrm{prox}}u(x),x\in\Omega
of equal volume, and
u
satisfies
\Delta_{A}u(x)=\sharp_{g}\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}/\sharp\Omega
in
\Omega
,
u(y)-u(x)=\frac{g(x,y)}{A(x,y)}
for all
(x,y)\in\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}
.

Sufficient conditions for equality.
Let
\Omega\subset V
be connected within
G
. Equality in the isoperimetric inequality in graph
G
with weight
A
is achieved by
\Omega
if the following geometric conditions are met by
G,A
:

1.

The complex made of vertices and edges of
G|_{\Omega}\cup\overrightarrow{\partial\Omega}
(note that vertices in
\overline{\Omega}\setminus\Omega
are not included) is reciprocal to the collection of
d
- and
(d-1)
-cells of an equal-volume face-to-face polyhedral tessellation
K
of a convex polyhedron
H
.

2.

If
F_{x,y}
denotes the
(d-1)
-dimensional facet of the tessellation
K
which is dual to edge
(x,y)
of
G
, then the
\frac{A^{2}(x,y)|y-x|}{\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(F_{x,y})}
takes the same value for all edges of
G
.

Under the above conditions, functions
u
achieving equality in (
4.3
) are precisely those of the form
u=\lambda u_{\mathrm{Alek}}+\ell
where
\lambda>0
,
\ell
is an affine function and
u_{\mathrm{Alek}}
is the Aleksandrov solution to the optimal transport problem between
\frac{|H|}{\sharp\Omega}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\delta_{x}
and
1_{H}(x)dx
with cost
|x-y|^{2}
.

Proof.

1.

Proof of (
4.3
).

The inclusion
\partial u(x)\subset\partial^{\mathrm{prox}}u(x)
follows because the latter is defined by a subset of the constraints of the former. This in turn implies the first inequality in (
4.3
). Proposition
3.1
gives the second inequality in (
4.3
). The last inequality in (
4.3
) follows by estimating
C_{\mathcal{V}_{x}}
by its maximum over

and
\Delta_{A}u(x)
by its upper bound imposed in (
4.2
).

2.

Necessary conditions for equality in (
4.3
).

If equality holds in the first inequality in (
4.3
) then we must have that
\partial u(\Omega)
si the disjoint union of the
\partial u(x),x\in\Omega
and that for all
x\in\Omega
there holds
\partial u(x)=\partial^{\mathrm{prox}}u(x)
. Therefore
\partial u(\Omega)
is decomposed into the convex polyhedra
\partial^{\mathrm{prox}}u(x)
. Moreover, due to Proposition

, we have that

is the restriction of a convex function defined on

for all

If the last inequality in (

is constant over

and

is independent of

. Moreover, due to the divergence theorem, equality in (
4.2

u(y)-u(x)=\frac{g(x,y)}{A(x,y)}
for all

3.

Sufficient conditions for equality in (
4.3
).
Assume now that
G,A,H
are as in the last part of the theorem, and denote by

the equal-volume tessellation of point (1) of the theorem statement. We start with two observations, which are useful later.

Firstly, note that by Theorem

Secondly, let

We now show that a suitable rescaling of the Aleksandrov solution

determined by

. Indeed, if
\partial^{\mathrm{prox}}u_{\mathrm{Alek}}(x)=C(x)

We now show that any function

By the already proved necessary conditions for equality, we have

x\in V

We then proceed as in the last part of Theorem

5
Isoperimetric inequalities in periodic graphs

In this section we will present a few examples, in which the hypotheses of Proposition
4.1
are satisfied and in which equality in (
4.10
) can be achieved and characterized. We do not aim at exhausting all possibilities, but rather at giving a hint at some possible applications. A more thorough study and classification is left to future work.

For each case we will present
V,A,g
, the associated constant
C_{G,A}
from Proposition
4.1
point (3), the region
H
as in (
4.9
) and its volume.

In some cases, obstructions to the achievability of equality in (
4.10
) appear, while a discrete isoperimetric shape indeed exists nevertheless, leaving open possible directions of improvement of the theory. This suggests that alternative criteria alternative to Theorem
4.4
and Proposition
4.1
may exist. However such improvements go beyond the use of our PDE/Semidiscrete Optimal Transport approach, and thus we do not pursue them here.

We also restrict to periodic graphs only, and do not explore the whole range of possibilities left open by Theorem
4.4
and Proposition
4.1
.

We emphasize that even only restricting to the periodic case, the range of possible examples is very large, including the previous work
[
32
]
as a very special case. The whole classification of the periodic graphs in which Proposition
4.1
applies to yield sharp isoperimetric inequality in (
4.10
) goes beyond the scope of this work, and leads to open questions of independent interest, of which we mention a few.

Besides a range of specific examples, we present three general methods for producing new cases of applicability of Proposition
4.1
from known ones, by either taking products, enriching the graph by subdividing the dual polyhedral tessellation, or stacking periodic graphs along new coordinate directions.

5.1.2
Deformations of the honeycomb graph example

The combinatorial inequality obtained in the previous subsection begs the question of whether our method can be applied to other combinatorially equivalent rectilinear graphs with different side lenghts and weights. We verify that this is the case for any affine deformation of the corresponding graph

Another option for creating new examples is to modify the weights

5.1.3
An interesting problematic case in the plane

Isoperimetric shapes in graphs may exist and may have a form more complicated than (

This is the case of the triangular lattice graph, i.e. the

The examples in this section include the ones from the previous sections as special cases. A

Directed Laplacian.
A directed graph
\vec{G}=(V,\vec{E})
is defined by a set of vertices
V
and a set of edges
\vec{E}\subset V\times V
. We assume that
\vec{G}
is such that for each
x\in V
the set
\{y\in V:\ (x,y)\in\vec{E}\}
is nonempty, i.e. that
\vec{G}
has no sinks. The directed Laplacian
\vec{\Delta}
of the directed graph
\vec{G}
is an operator sending the set of functions
u:V\to\mathbb{R}
to itself, defined by

\vec{\Delta}u(x):=\sum_{y:(x,y)\in\vec{E}}(u(y)-u(x)).

If furthermore a weight
A(x,y)
is present on edges
(x,y)\in\vec{E}
, then we can define
\vec{\Delta}_{A}u(x):=\sum_{y:(x,y)\in\vec{E}}A^{2}(x,y)(u(y)-u(x))
.

As the modifications for general symmetric weights
A(x,y)
are straightforward, from now on we restrict to the case that
A(x,y)\equiv 1
on
\vec{E}
, and leave the extensions to the general case to the reader.
We thus denote
\vec{\Delta}_{A}=\vec{\Delta}
. In order to avoid confusion, we mention here that below we will also use the notation
\vec{\Delta}_{\vec{E}^{\prime}}
when it is important to keep in mind the set of edges
\vec{E}^{\prime}
as in (
6.5
).

C(g,\Omega,\vec{E}^{\prime})
reexpressed in terms of directed Laplacian.
For
\vec{E}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
, the operator
A_{\vec{E}^{\prime}}
sending
\mathbb{R}^{\overline{\Omega}}
to itself and defined by

(A_{\vec{E}^{\prime}}u)(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta u(x)&\mbox{ if }x\in\Omega,\\[8.53581pt]
u(y)-u(x)&\mbox{ if }x\in\overline{\Omega}\setminus\Omega,(x,y)\in\vec{E}^{\prime},\end{array}\right.

is the same as the directed Laplacian
\vec{\Delta}_{\vec{E}^{\prime}}
on the directed graph
\vec{G}_{\vec{E}^{\prime}}
with vertices
\overline{\Omega}
and edges

\{(x,y):\ x\in\Omega,(x,y)\in\vec{E}_{\overline{\Omega}}\}\cup\{(y,x):\ (x,y)\in\vec{E}^{\prime}\}.

Properties of
A_{\vec{E}^{\prime}}
.
We note that like for the Laplacian, the directed Laplacian over a graph can be identically zero only on functions that are constant on each connected component of the graph, where now for testing connectedness only oriented paths are allowed. Indeed, if
\vec{\Delta}u\equiv 0
at all vertices, then at each vertex
x_{0}\in G
, this says that
u(x_{0})
is equal to the average of
u
over all
y
such that
(x_{0},y)\in\vec{E}^{\prime}
, no
u
cannot have strict local minima or maxima.

From now on we assume
\Omega
connected,
for simplicity, as in the case of
\Omega
with multiple components we can reason componentwise as in In this case, we find that
\mathrm{Ker}(A_{\vec{E}^{\prime}})
is formed by just the constant functions
u
over
\overline{\Omega}
, thus is
1
-dimensional. The operator
A_{\vec{E}^{\prime}}
is in general not self-adjoint, but we can nevertheless gain the information that the image of
A_{\vec{E}^{\prime}}
has codimension
1
.

Compatibility condition and the image of
A_{\vec{E}^{\prime}}
.
We now characterize the orthogonal to the image of
A_{\vec{E}^{\prime}}
with respect to the usual inner product
\langle u,u^{\prime}\rangle=\sum_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}u(x)u^{\prime}(x)
. We know from the previous paragraph that the space of functions
v:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
orthogonal complement of
\mathrm{Im}A_{\vec{E}^{\prime}}
has dimension
1
, thus contains a nonzero function, unique up to scalar multiples. The condition determining this function is
\langle\vec{\Delta}_{\vec{E}^{\prime}}u,v\rangle=0
for all
u
. This can be explicitly written (note that the computation below holds on any connected directed graph):

\displaystyle(\forall u),\ 0=\sum_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}v(x)\vec{\Delta}_{\vec{E}^{\prime}}u(x)=\sum_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}v(x)\sum_{y:x\to y}(u(y)-u(x))

(6.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}v(x)\left(-d_{x}^{out}u(x)+\sum_{y:x\to y}u(y)\right)=\sum_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}u(x)\left(-d_{x}^{out}v(x)+\sum_{y:y\to x}v(y)\right).

In the above

(usually called the “outgoing degree at

6.6
) must hold for all choices of

d_{x}^{out}v(x)=\sum_{y:y\to x}v(y).

(6.7)

This equation seems to not have been studied in the literature, however in our case we know that a solution exists because we control the kernel of

We will reserve the notation “

) over

We say that two vertices

Proposition 6.1
.

Assume that
\vec{E}^{\prime}

Proof.

We will assume that

then there exists a solution

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\vec{\Delta}_{E^{\prime}}u(x_{0})=1,&\\[8.53581pt]
\vec{\Delta}_{E^{\prime}}u(x)=0,&\mbox{ if }x\in\Omega\setminus\{x_{0}\},\\[8.53581pt]
u(y)-u(x)=0,&\mbox{ if }(x,y)\in\vec{E}^{\prime}.\end{array}\right.

(6.8)

Let
\vec{G}^{\prime}
be a connected component of

is constant on
\vec{G}^{\prime}
. Since

, there exists

such that

. We have

and since

But

A similar reasoning shows that

Condition for optimality in (

Note that the problem

\min\left\{\max_{x\in\Omega}\Delta u(x):\ (\forall(x,y)\in\vec{E}^{\prime}),u(y)-u(x)=g(y)\right\}=\min\left\{z:\ B\vec{u}=\vec{g},\ (\forall x\in\Omega)z\geq A_{x}\cdot\vec{u}\right\},

\vec{u},z
with

(u(y)-u(x))_{y\in\overline{\Omega}\setminus\Omega}

We see that
\bar{u},\bar{z}
form an optimizer in (
6.10
) if and only if

1.

We have
B\bar{u}=\vec{g}
and
(\forall x\in\Omega)\bar{z}\geq A_{x}\cdot\bar{u}
.

2.

If
(\tilde{u},\tilde{z})
is a perturbation respecting the constraint, i.e. satisfying

, and such that
\tilde{z}<0
, then it violates some of the inequalities which were saturated at

, i.e.

\tilde{z}\geq A_{x}\cdot\tilde{u}\mbox{ for some }x\in\Omega\mbox{ at which }\bar{z}=A_{x}\cdot\bar{u}.

Indeed, were this not the case, a small perturbation along
\tilde{u},\tilde{z}
would decrease the value of the

\bar{u},\bar{z}
.

Note that the variable
\tilde{z}
can be eliminated in the point (2) above, and the resulting optimality condition can be then expressed in terms of our discrete equation as follows:

Lemma 6.2
.

\mbox{For all }\tilde{u}\mbox{ with $\vec{E}^{\prime}$-boundary datum zero},\quad(\exists x\in\mathrm{argmax}_{\Omega}\Delta\bar{u})\mbox{ at which }\Delta\tilde{u}(x)\geq 0.

(6.11)
