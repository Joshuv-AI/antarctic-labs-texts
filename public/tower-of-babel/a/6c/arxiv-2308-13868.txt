A Graph-Theoretic Model for a Generic Three Jug Puzzle
Suresh Manjanath Hegde, Shashanka Kulamarva

Abstract
In a classic three jug puzzle we have three jugs A A , B B , and C C with some fixed capacities. The jug A A is fully filled with wine to its capacity. The goal of the puzzle is to divide the wine into two equal halves by pouring it from one jug to another without using any other measuring devices. However, we consider a generic version of the three jug puzzle and present an independent graph theoretic model to determine whether the puzzle has a solution at first place. If it has a solution, then the same can be determined using this model. We also present the sketch of an algorithm to determine the solution of the puzzle. Keywords: Three jug puzzle; Generic three jug puzzle; graph theoretic model; directed graphs Mathematics Subject Classification: 05C20, 05C90

Introduction

Mathematics can be used as a tool to solve several puzzles, riddles and open questions. In most of the cases, mathematical modeling helps in this regard. A three jug puzzle is one of the ancient and familiar puzzles. A formal statement of the puzzle goes as follows:

Three Jug Puzzle
: There are three jugs
A
,
B
, and
C

a

c

For better understanding of the puzzle, we come up with the following definition. A pour of wine from jug
X
to jug

and

Y
becomes full to its capacity. As the name suggests, a measurable pour keeps track of the volumes of wine in each of the jugs after pouring. One can easily see that if we don’t perform a measurable pour and stop the pouring somewhere in between, since we don’t have other measuring devices, we will loose the information on the amount of wine in each of the jugs. Since we need to keep track of the amount of wine in each of the jugs without using any other measuring devices, every pour should be a measurable pour. Thus we can rewrite the puzzle as follows:

Main Results

Moving ahead, the idea is to utilize the graph

Theorem 3.1
.

Let

be a generic three jug puzzle with

(i,j)

Proof.

First, assume that the puzzle

(b^{\prime},c^{\prime})
through a sequence of intermediate distributions obtained exactly by the sequence of measurable pours performed along the process. For every single change of distribution in this sequence of pouring, we have a directed edge between the corresponding vertices, by Theorem
3.1
. By taking the union of all these directed edges corresponding to the entire sequence that is used to reach the distribution

from the distribution
(b^{\prime},c^{\prime})

(b^{\prime},c^{\prime})

Conversely, assume that there exists a directed path from the vertex

to the vertex
(\frac{d}{2},0)

G_{P}
. But a directed path is nothing but a sequence of directed edges. For each edge in this sequence, by Theorem

, we can obtain the corresponding ordered pair of distributions such that the latter can be obtained from the first by a single valid pour of the wine from one jug to another. This sequence of ordered pairs starting from

, generates the sequential order of pouring of wine between the jugs which gives us the desired solution to the puzzle

4
Sketch of an Algorithm

Now, if we are provided with a generic three jug puzzle, one can just extract an ordered quadruple out of it and obtain the graph model presented in Section
2
. Once that is done, it all boils down to determining whether the puzzle has a solution or equivalently whether the graph has the corresponding directed path. In other words, by Corollary
3.2
, we can leave aside the puzzle and just look at the fairly simple graph model and look at its structural properties and infer whether the original generic three jug puzzle had a solution or not; if it had a solution we can determine the same using the graph model.

Once we are sure that the puzzle has a solution, then one can think of the problem of minimizing the number of measurable pours, i.e., to determine the minimum number of measurable pours that is necessary to obtain a solution. Again, by using Corollary
3.2
, this boils down to determining the shortest directed path in the graph model. Observe that whenever a directed path exists in a graph, a shortest directed path also exists in the graph. Alternatively, whenever a solution exists for the puzzle, a solution with the minimum number of measurable pours also exists for the puzzle. Therefore, the model can also be used to obtain the optimum solution, if it exists.

Conclusion

As mentioned earlier, people have been studying the variants of the jug puzzle since 16th century
[
4
]
. Determining whether these kind of puzzles have a solution or not is an interesting research problem to be tackled. Several variants of the puzzle have been shown to be solvable using various methods and algorithms. In view of this paper, a generic version of the three jug puzzle is considered and an independent directed graph model is proposed such that the solvability of the puzzle can be determined by using the model. If a solution exists, then the same can be determined by using the same model. It is important to note that the graph model as such do not depend on the puzzle in any way.

In future, one can consider a different variant of the puzzle or a completely different puzzle and try to determine the solvability of the same by using a similar technique or the idea of the model presented in the paper. Our motive was to utilize graph theory as a tool to tackle the generic three jug puzzle. In future, researchers may utilize some other field of mathematics to tackle this puzzle and try to come up with a simpler model or a better technique.
