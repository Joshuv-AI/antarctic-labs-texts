Nonlinear feedback, double bracket dissipation and port control of Lie-Poisson systems
Simon Hochgerner

Abstract
. Methods from controlled Lagrangians, double bracket dissipation and interconnection and damping assignment – passivity based control (IDA-PBC) are used to construct nonlinear feedback controls which (asymptotically) stabilize previously unstable equilibria of Lie-Poisson Hamiltonian systems. The results are applied to find an asymptotically stabilizing control for the rotor driven satellite, and a stabilizing control for Hall magnetohydrodynamic flow.



1.
Introduction

This paper is concerned with two strands of feedback control: the theory of controlled Lagrangians initiated in
[
21
]
and further elaborated by
[
4
,
5
]
, and the method of interconnection and damping assignment – passivity based control (IDA-PBC) developed by
[
27
,
28
,
9
]
in the context of port controlled Hamiltonian systems. The relation between these approaches has been studied in
[
2
,
10
]
, and it has been shown, in the framework of almost Poisson structures, that these techniques are equivalent when regarded in sufficient generality.

The controlled Lagrangian/Hamiltonian approach was first formulated in the framework of Hamiltonian mechanics (e.g.,
[
8
]
) and later studied systematically from the Lagrangian point of view (
[
4
,
5
]
). Both perspectives are equivalent (for the kinetic energy functions considered in this paper) and will be referred to collectively as the controlled Lagrangian (CL) approach.

The CL method is particularly well suited to symmetry arguments and the tools of geometric mechanics (
[
31
,
7
,
8
,
3
,
11
]
).
The original idea of CL is to start from a mechanical system defined by a kinetic energy function of Kaluza-Klein type and to modify this structure (kinetic energy shaping) so that the corresponding equations of motion have force terms only in a given symmetry direction. In
[
15
,
16
]
this procedure has been reversed: associated to a given Lie group action is a force acting in symmetry directions and the goal is to reformulate this as a mechanical system associated to a new kinetic energy function. When such a function can be found, these approaches are clearly equivalent. However, as shown presently, more general forces along symmetry directions may be considered and this leads to not only a new energy function but also a new almost Poisson structure and a new symmetric tensor responsible for energy dissipation. This fits the framework of IDA-PBC, and has the additional feature of being adapted to the symmetry methods of geometric mechanics.

This paper deals with Kaluza-Klein Hamiltonian systems in the Lie-Poisson setting. This implies that the phase space of the mechanical system is a direct product dual Lie algebra
\mathfrak{p}^{*}=\mathfrak{d}^{*}\times\mathfrak{g}^{*}
where
\mathfrak{d}
and
\mathfrak{g}
are Lie algebras. Both,
\mathfrak{d}
and
\mathfrak{g}
, may be finite or infinite dimensional and non-Abelian. The component
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
represents the symmetry directions where the actuating force is applied.
The contributions, which are new to the best of the author’s knowledge, are the following:

(1)

In Proposition
2.5
matching conditions are derived to characterize the relationship between symmetry actuation and modified Kaluza-Klein Hamiltonians. These generalize the Euler-Poincaré matching conditions of
[
5
]
to the non-Abelian and coordinate independent setting (Section
2.D
).

(2)

Section
2.E
introduces a nonlinear feedback control based on double bracket dissipation (
[
6
]
). This yields controlled energy dissipation. In Theorem
2.13
this construction is recognized as an IDA-PBC system and the corresponding structure consisting of a new Hamiltonian, a new almost Poisson tensor and a new symmetric tensor is given explicitly. This method is fully constructive, and depends neither on the matching conditions of CL theory (
[
4
,
5
]
) nor on the solution of a PDE (
[
27
]
). In fact, the Hamiltonian found in this manner is of fourth order and does not satisfy the matching conditions (i.e. Proposition
2.5
).

(3)

Theorem
2.13
is applied in Section
3
to asymptotically stabilize the satellite with a rotor in the full phase space. This conclusion is the same as in
[
3
]
but reached through a different method.

(4)

Section
4
contains an application of Theorem
2.13
to Hall magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) flow. This is used in Section
4.D
to stabilize a previously unstable equilibrium of planar shear flow. As in Section
3
, stability is achieved in the full phase space of fluid velocity and magnetic field variables, but asymptotic stability cannot be concluded in this case.

The main idea underlying these points is that the under-actuation associated to the the given symmetry group direction and the double bracket construction of
[
6
]
give rise to a new conserved quantity when the dynamics are considered as a mechanical system on the cotangent bundle
T^{*}\mathcal{P}
, where
\mathcal{P}
is the Lie group of
\mathfrak{p}
. This in turn leads to a nonlinear coordinate transformation
\Phi:\mathfrak{p}^{*}\to\mathfrak{p}^{*}
,
(\nu,\alpha)\mapsto(\nu,\beta)
. In the new coordinates, there is a natural candidate,
g_{C}
, for a modified kinetic energy function. Therefore, the IDA-PBC problem can be solved algebraically, and we obtain a controlled interconnection and damping structure
\Pi_{C}
and
\mathcal{R}_{C}
. This structure can now be transformed to the original coordinates via pullback to yield the desired structure: the fourth order Hamiltonian function
h_{d}=\Phi^{*}g_{C}
, the almost Poisson tensor
\Pi_{d}=\Phi^{*}\Pi_{C}
and the symmetric tensor
\mathcal{R}_{d}=\Phi^{*}\mathcal{R}_{C}
, cf. Theorem
2.13
. This construction relies on the controlled conserved quantity
J_{\mathcal{G}}+j
described in Proposition
2.2
, it is therefore a synthesis of CL and the approach via canonical transformations to algebraic solutions of IDA-PBC outlined in
[
12
]
.

Moreover, the closed-loop dynamics, associated to the feedback control, appear in the
(\nu,\beta)
-variables in a particularly appealing format: the double bracket dissipation acts on the
\nu
-variables such that coadjoint orbits in
\mathfrak{d}^{*}
are preserved; on the other hand, in
\beta
it is desirable to asymptotically restore the origin, corresponding to the closed loop
\alpha=-C\nu-N(\nu)
, and accordingly decay of coadjoint orbits in
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is incorporated; the relevant control force is specified in terms of a linear map
C:\mathfrak{d}^{*}\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
leading to a new inner product on
\mathfrak{d}^{*}
, and a nonlinear map
N:\mathfrak{d}^{*}\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
encoding the double bracket structure.

Other recent approaches to algebraic IDA-PBC include
[
9
]
and
[
24
]
. The latter also introduces the notion of geometric PBC, but assumes a finite dimensional set-up and that the symmetry group action is Abelian. The method proposed in this paper differs from these approaches in the following ways: the coadjoint orbit structure in
\mathfrak{d}^{*}
is used to obtain double bracket dissipation; the controlled momentum map yields a nonlinear diffeomorphism such that the transformed closed-loop system can be stabilized in the full phase space (non-actuated and actuated variables) by means of the energy-Casimir method; non-Abelian symmetry groups are allowed; and the construction is coordinate independent so that it may be applied also to infinite dimensional problems such as Hall MHD flow.

This paper is restricted to mechanical systems of Lie-Poisson type which includes a large class of systems (
[
4
,
5
,
19
,
23
]
). However, because of independence from (Euler-Poincaré or Lie-Poisson) matching conditions and since the controlled momentum map exists for symmetry actuation on any cotangent bundle (Proposition
2.2
), the construction in Theorem
2.13
may carry over to more general phase spaces
T^{*}\mathcal{P}
where
\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{D}
is a principal fiber bundle with structure group
\mathcal{G}
.

2.
Feedback control of Lie-Poisson systems

2.A.
Lie-Poisson systems

Let
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{G}
be finite or infinite dimensional Lie groups and consider the direct product Lie group
\mathcal{P}:=\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{G}
. In the following
\mathcal{D}
will be thought of as the base and
\mathcal{G}
as the symmetry group so that the projection
\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{D}
is a principal fiber bundle with structure group
\mathcal{G}
.

For example, in Section
3
,
\mathcal{D}
will correspond to
\mbox{$\textup{SO}$}(3)
describing the satellite’s orientation and
\mathcal{G}
will coincide with
S^{1}
to represent the rotor’s angle, cf. also Remark
2.1
below.

Tangent and cotangent bundles can be represented via left or right multiplication as
T\mathcal{P}\cong\mathcal{P}\times\mathfrak{p}=\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{G}\times\mathfrak{d}\times\mathfrak{g}
and
T^{*}\mathcal{P}\cong\mathcal{P}\times\mathfrak{p}^{*}=\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{G}\times\mathfrak{d}^{*}\times\mathfrak{g}^{*}
where
\mathfrak{p}
,
\mathfrak{d}
,
\mathfrak{g}
denote the Lie algebras associated to
\mathcal{P}
,
\mathcal{D}
,
\mathcal{G}
respectively. The corresponding dual Lie algebras,
\mathfrak{p}^{*}
,
\mathfrak{d}^{*}
,
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, are defined as smooth duals (also called: the regular part) such that there exist isomorphisms
\mathfrak{p}\cong\mathfrak{p}^{*}
,
\mathfrak{d}\cong\mathfrak{d}^{*}
,
\mathfrak{g}\cong\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, even in the infinite dimensional case (
[
25
]
). The left trivialization is called body representation (often used for examples involving rigid bodies), and the right trivialization is called space representation (often used for fluid dynamical examples). See
[
19
]
.

From now on it is assumed that one such representation, left or right, has been chosen. Whenever the representation induces a specific sign this will be marked by
\pm
where
+
corresponds to ‘left’ and
-
to ‘right’, and vice versa for
\mp
. Thus we can identify
T^{*}\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}\times\mathfrak{p}^{*}
and elements
\Pi\in T^{*}\mathcal{P}
are written as
\Pi=(\phi,g,\nu,\alpha)
.

Let
M^{\pm}
denote left (or right) multiplication in
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{D}
, i.e.
M^{+}_{\psi}\phi=\psi\phi
and
M^{-}_{\psi}\phi=\phi\psi
in
\mathcal{G}
. The variational derivative,
\delta f/\delta\nu\in\mathfrak{d}
, of a function
f:\mathfrak{d}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by the dual pairing

\left<df(\nu),\eta\right>_{\mathfrak{d}^{**}\times\mathfrak{d}^{*}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big|_{0}f(\nu+t\eta)=\left<\eta,\frac{\delta f}{\delta\nu}\right>_{\mathfrak{d}^{*}\times\mathfrak{d}}

where
\nu,\eta\in\mathfrak{d}^{*}
,
df
is the exterior derivative (full differential) and the source space of the pairing is indicated. The variational derivative of the pull back
\Phi^{*}f=f\circ\Phi
along a function
\Phi:\mathfrak{d}^{*}\to\mathfrak{d}^{*}
is given by

(2.1)

\frac{\delta\Phi^{*}f}{\delta\nu}=(T\Phi)^{*}\frac{\delta f}{\delta\Phi(\nu)}.

The conjugation by left and right multiplication in
\mathcal{G}
gives rise to the adjoint action
\mbox{$\text{{Ad}}$}(g):\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
. Indeed, let
\textup{Conj}_{g}h=ghg^{-1}
for
g,h\in\mathcal{G}
. Then
\mbox{$\text{{Ad}}$}(g):=T_{e}\textup{Conj}_{g}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
, and the coadjoint action
\mbox{$\text{{Ad}}$}(g)^{*}:\mathfrak{g}^{*}\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is given by
\mbox{$\text{{Ad}}$}(g)^{*}\alpha=\alpha\circ\mbox{$\text{{Ad}}$}(g)
. Moreover, we define
\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(X)Y=\partial\mbox{$\text{{Ad}}$}(\textup{exp}(tX))Y/\partial t|_{0}
, and
\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(X)^{*}\alpha=\alpha\circ\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(X)
. The corresponding operations associated to
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{D}
will be denoted by the same symbols. In the infinite dimensional case these operations have to be restricted to their appropriate domains of existence (
[
25
]
).

On the cotangent bundle
T^{*}\mathcal{P}
there exists a canonical exact symplectic form,
\Omega^{T^{*}\mathcal{P}}
. Let
h:T^{*}\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}
be a Hamiltonian function which is left (or right) invariant with respect to multiplication,
M^{\pm}
, of
\mathcal{P}
on itself. The corresponding equations of motion, in left (or right) representation, are given by

(2.2)

\displaystyle\Big(TM^{\pm}_{\phi^{-1}}\dot{\phi},TM^{\pm}_{g^{-1}}\dot{g},\dot{\nu},\dot{\alpha}\Big)=X_{h}(\Pi)=\Big(\mbox{$\frac{\delta h}{\delta\nu}$},\mbox{$\frac{\delta h}{\delta\alpha}$},\pm\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(\mbox{$\frac{\delta h}{\delta\nu}$})^{*}\nu,\pm\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(\mbox{$\frac{\delta h}{\delta\alpha}$})^{*}\alpha\Big)

where
X_{h}=(\Omega^{T^{*}\mathcal{P}})^{-1}dh
is the Hamiltonian vector field,

\Pi=(\phi,g,\nu,\alpha)\in\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{G}\times\mathfrak{d}^{*}\times\mathfrak{g}^{*}
.
The canonical momentum map
J_{\mathcal{P}}:T^{*}P\to\mathfrak{p}^{*}
corresponding to left (or right) multiplication is:

J_{\mathcal{P}}=(J_{\mathcal{D}},J_{\mathcal{G}}):T^{*}\mathcal{P}\to\mathfrak{d}^{*}\times\mathfrak{g}^{*},\qquad(\phi,g,\nu,\alpha)\mapsto(\mbox{$\text{{Ad}}$}(\phi^{\mp})^{*}\nu,\mbox{$\text{{Ad}}$}(g^{\mp})^{*}\alpha)

See
[
23
]
.

For the remainder of this section, it is assumed that
h:T^{*}\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}
is left (or right) invariant.

Remark 2.1

(Satellite with a rotor)
.

The prime example for the theory developed in this section is that of the rigid body with a rotor attached to one of the principal axis. This example is treated in detail in Section
3
where bibliographical references are provided. However, to make the abstract formulation of the Lie-Poisson setting more accessible, the main points will be sketched for the satellite example also in this section. For this example,
\mathcal{D}=\mbox{$\textup{SO}$}(3)
corresponds to the rigid body and
\mathcal{G}=S^{1}
corresponds to the rotor attached to the short axis. We use the body representation whence the top sign is the relevant one. The Lie algebra structure on
\mathfrak{d}=\mbox{$\mathfrak{so}$}(3)
, which is identified with
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, is given by the cross product, i.e.
\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(u)v=u\times v
, and the bracket on
\mathfrak{g}=\mathbb{R}
is the Abelian, i.e. trivial, one. Thus the equations of motion (
2.2
) for a curve
(\nu_{t},\alpha_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}
are given by
\dot{\nu}=-\frac{\delta h}{\delta\nu}\times\nu
and
\dot{\alpha}=0
.

2.B.
Controlled equations of motion

Let
G:\mathfrak{g}^{*}\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
be an isomorphism, which is assumed to be linear but not necessarily equivariant. Let
C:\mathfrak{d}^{*}\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
be a linear map. Let
N:\mathfrak{d}^{*}\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
be a smooth map, which may be nonlinear.
Following the construction in
[
16
]
, we define a map
j:T^{*}\mathcal{P}\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
through

(2.3)

j(\phi,g,\nu,\alpha)=\mbox{$\text{{Ad}}$}(g^{\mp})^{*}G^{-1}\Big(C\nu+N(\nu)+(1-G)\alpha\Big).

Associated to
j
is a control force
f:T^{*}\mathcal{P}\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
via

(2.4)

f(\Pi)=-\mbox{$\text{{Ad}}$}(g^{\mp})^{*}\,G\,\mbox{$\text{{Ad}}$}(g^{\pm})^{*}\,dj\,X_{h}(\Pi)

and a corresponding closed-loop control (in the terminology of
[
10
, Def. 3.1]
)
\mathcal{U}:T^{*}\mathcal{P}\to T^{*}\mathcal{P}
as

(2.5)

\mathcal{U}(\Pi)=\Big(\phi,g,0,\mbox{$\text{{Ad}}$}(g^{\pm})^{*}f(\Pi)\Big).

The control force
F\in\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}(T^{*}\mathcal{P})
, which is a vector field on
T^{*}\mathcal{P}
, is now defined as the vertical lift of
\mathcal{U}
at
\Pi
, that is

(2.6)

F(\Pi):=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\Big|_{s=0}\Big(\Pi+s\,\mathcal{U}(\Pi)\Big)=\Big(0,0,0,\mbox{$\text{{Ad}}$}(g^{\pm})^{*}f(\Pi)\Big)\in T_{\Pi}T^{*}\mathcal{P}

and the controlled equations of motion are

(2.7)

\dot{\Pi}=X_{h}(\Pi)+F(\Pi).

Proposition 2.2
.

The following are true.

(1)

F
is vertical with respect to the cotangent projection
\tau:T^{*}\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{P}
, i.e.
T\tau\,F=0
.

(2)

dJ_{\mathcal{D}}\,F=0
and
dJ_{\mathcal{G}}\,F=f
.

(3)

The momentum map
J_{\mathcal{D}}
and the controlled momentum map
J_{\mathcal{G}}+j
are conserved along solutions of equation (
2.7
):

(2.8)

\displaystyle dJ_{\mathcal{D}}\,(X_{h}+F)

\displaystyle=0

(2.9)

\displaystyle d(J_{\mathcal{G}}+j)\,(X_{h}+F)

\displaystyle=0

Proof.

Items (1) and (2) follow because
F
is defined as a vertical lift of
\mathcal{U}
. Equation (
2.8
) is
dJ_{\mathcal{D}}\,X_{h}=0
which holds since
h
is, in particular,
\mathcal{D}
-invariant. Equation (
2.9
) is shown in
[
16
]
under the assumption of
j:T^{*}\mathcal{P}\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
being fiber linear. However, the calculation extends to the present case (because linearity of
G
suffices):

\displaystyle dj\,F(\Pi)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\Big|_{s=0}\,j\Big(\Pi+s\,\mathcal{U}(\Pi)\Big)=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\Big|_{s=0}\,\mbox{$\text{{Ad}}$}(g^{\mp})^{*}G^{-1}\Big(C\nu+N(\nu)+(1-G)(\alpha+s\,\mbox{$\text{{Ad}}$}(g^{\pm})^{*}\,f)\Big)

\displaystyle=-dj\,X_{h}(\Pi)+\mbox{$\text{{Ad}}$}(g^{\mp})^{*}G\mbox{$\text{{Ad}}$}(g^{\pm})^{*}dj\,X_{h}(\Pi)=-dj\,X_{h}(\Pi)-f(\Pi)

whence

d(J_{\mathcal{G}}+j)\,(X_{h}+F)=0+f+dj\,X_{h}+dj\,F=0
.
∎

To express (
2.7
) in the Lie-Poisson setting, note that (
2.2
) yields

\displaystyle dj\,X_{h}(\Pi)

\displaystyle=\mp\mbox{$\text{{Ad}}$}(g^{\mp})^{*}\Big(\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(X)^{*}G^{-1}(C\nu+N(\nu)+\alpha)-G^{-1}C\,\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(u)^{*}\nu-G^{-1}\,dN(\nu)\,\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(u)^{*}\nu-G^{-1}\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(X)^{*}\alpha\Big)

where
u=\delta h/\delta\nu
and
X=\delta h/\delta\alpha
.
The corresponding force in the Lie-Poisson formulation is

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\textup{LP}}(\Pi)

\displaystyle:=\mbox{$\text{{Ad}}$}(g^{\pm})^{*}f(\Pi)=-G\mbox{$\text{{Ad}}$}(g^{\pm})^{*}\,dj\,X_{h}(\Pi)

\displaystyle=\pm\Big(G\,\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(X)^{*}G^{-1}(C\nu+N(\nu)+\alpha)-C\,\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(u)^{*}\nu-dN(\nu)\,\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(u)^{*}\nu-\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(X)^{*}\alpha\Big)

which is independent of the base point,
\mathcal{U}_{\textup{LP}}(\Pi)=\mathcal{U}_{\textup{LP}}(\nu,\alpha)
, as it should be in order for (
2.7
) to be left (or right) invariant. Hence (
2.7
) may be expressed equivalently in the left (or right) representation as

(2.11)

\displaystyle\dot{\nu}

\displaystyle=\pm\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(u)^{*}\nu

(2.12)

\displaystyle\dot{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\pm\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(X)^{*}\alpha+\mathcal{U}_{\textup{LP}}(\nu,\alpha)=\pm\Big(G\,\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(X)^{*}G^{-1}(C\nu+N(\nu)+\alpha)-C\,\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(u)^{*}\nu-dN(\nu)\,\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(u)^{*}\nu\Big)

where

and

, and

Remark 2.4
.

If

and
X
can be written as functional derivatives with respect to a function depending on
\nu
and

then (
2.14
)-(

.

Following
[
4
,
5
,
7
,
8
]
, we assume from now on that the Hamiltonian
h_{0}
is not only left (or right)
\mathcal{P}
-invariant but also of Kaluza-Klein type. This means that
h_{0}
is the kinetic energy of a
\mathcal{P}
-left- (or right-) invariant Kaluza-Klein metric
\mu_{0}^{\mathcal{P}}
, i.e.
h_{0}(\Pi)=\langle(\nu,\alpha)^{\top},(\mu_{0}^{\mathcal{P}})^{-1}(\nu,\alpha)^{\top}\rangle/2

Thus

is assumed to be a Kaluza-Klein metric on the principal fiber bundle

where the symmetry group
\mathcal{G}
acts by left (or right) multiplication on itself. The metric
\mu_{0}^{\mathcal{P}}
is determined by the following data:

(1)

A Riemannian metric on
\mathcal{D}
which is invariant with respect to the left (or right) multiplication of
\mathcal{D}
on itself. Hence the metric is determined by an isomorphism
\mu_{0}:\mathfrak{d}\to\mathfrak{d}^{*}
such that the bilinear form

(2)

An isomorphism
\mathbb{I}_{0}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
such that the associated bilinear form on

is symmetric and positive definite.

(3)

A principal bundle connection
T\mathcal{P}\to\mathfrak{g}
,
(\phi,g,u,X)\mapsto\mbox{$\text{{Ad}}$}(g^{\pm})(X+\mathcal{A}_{0}u)
determined by a linear map
\mathcal{A}_{0}:\mathfrak{d}\to\mathfrak{g}
.

Identifying the metric with the induced isomorphism to the (regular) dual,
this means that
\mu_{0}^{\mathcal{P}}:\mathfrak{p}\to\mathfrak{p}^{*}
is given in matrix form:

(2.16)

\mu_{0}^{\mathcal{P}}=\left(\begin{matrix}\mu_{0}+\mathcal{A}_{0}^{*}\mathbb{I}_{0}\mathcal{A}_{0}&\mathcal{A}_{0}^{*}\mathbb{I}_{0}\\
\mathbb{I}_{0}\mathcal{A}_{0}&\mathbb{I}_{0}\end{matrix}\right)

This entails

(2.17)

\Big(\frac{\delta h_{0}}{\delta\nu},\frac{\delta h_{0}}{\delta\alpha}\Big)=\Big(u,X\Big)=(\mu_{0}^{\mathcal{P}})^{-1}\Big(\nu,\alpha\Big)=\Big(\mu_{0}^{-1}(\nu-\mathcal{A}_{0}^{*}\alpha),\mathbb{I}_{0}^{-1}\alpha-\mathcal{A}_{0}u\Big).

B
are the Maxwell equations without displacement term. We assume charge neutrality,
\sigma=0
, and that the current is a sum of induced and externally controlled components, i.e.

(4.46)

\mathcal{J}=eu^{i}-eu^{e}+\mathcal{J}_{\textup{ext}}.

The Hall MHD description is obtained from the two fluid system by neglecting the electron inertia,
m^{e}Du^{e}/Dt=0
. This yields Ohm’s law with Hall term:

E=-e^{-1}\nabla p^{e}-u^{e}\times B=-e^{-1}\nabla p^{e}-\Big(u^{i}-e^{-1}\textup{curl}\,B+e^{-1}\mathcal{J}_{\textup{ext}}\Big)\times B

where we have substituted
u^{e}=u^{i}-e^{-1}\textup{curl}\,B+e^{-1}\mathcal{J}_{\textup{ext}}
. Dropping the superscript
i
and inserting this equation for
E
in the system (
4.45
) leads to the single fluid equations of motion for ion flow:

(4.47)

\displaystyle\dot{u}

\displaystyle=-\nabla p-\langle u,\nabla\rangle u+\textup{curl}\,B\times B+\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle\dot{A}

\displaystyle=e^{-1}\nabla p^{e}+\Big(u-e^{-1}\textup{curl}\,B\Big)\times B-e^{-1}\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{J}_{\textup{ext}}\times B

with
B=\textup{curl}\,A
, and
p
and
p^{e}
are determined by
\textup{div}\,u=\textup{div}\,A=0
. Without external control,
\mathcal{F}=0
, these are the Hall MHD equations (
[
22
,
18
,
20
,
26
]
).

Let
\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{0}(M)
be the space of smooth vectorfields
X\in\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}(M)
such that
\textup{div}\,X=0
and
\langle X,n\rangle|\partial M=0
where
n
is the outward normal unit vector at the boundary
\partial M
. Let
\mathbb{P}:\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}(M)\to\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{0}(M)
,
X\mapsto X-\nabla\Delta^{-1}\textup{div}\,X
be the Leray-Hodge-Helmholtz projection ensuring the solenoidal property and the given boundary conditions. Thus (
4.47
) can be reformulated as

(4.48)

\displaystyle\dot{u}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Big(-\langle u,\nabla\rangle u+\textup{curl}\,B\times B+\mathcal{F}\Big)

\displaystyle\dot{A}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big(u-e^{-1}\textup{curl}\,B\Big)\times B-e^{-1}\mathcal{F}\Big)

with
B=\textup{curl}\,A
.

Given an unstable equilibrium
(u_{e},A_{e})
of the free Hall MHD system (i.e.
\mathcal{F}=0
) the goal is now to find a feedback law
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}(u,A)
such that
(u_{e},A_{e})
is a stable equilibrium of (
4.48
). It is, however, not insisted that
\mathcal{F}
should be of the form
-\mathcal{J}_{\textup{ext}}\times B
.

4.B.
Forced Lie-Poisson system

Let
\mathcal{D}:=\textup{Diff}_{0}(M)=:\mathcal{G}
be the infinite dimensional Lie group of volume and boundary preserving diffeomorphisms. The groups
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{G}
coincide as spaces but their roles are different, namely as in Section
2
, hence the distinction will be made throughout.

The Lie algebras and (regular) dual algebras are
\mathfrak{d}=\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{0}(M)=\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{d}^{*}=\Omega^{1}(M)/d\mbox{$C^{\infty}$}(M)=\mathfrak{g}^{*}
where
\Omega^{1}(M)/d\mbox{$C^{\infty}$}(M)
is the regular dual consisting of one-forms modulo exact one-forms. The regular dual is isomorphic to the Lie algebra, and the isomorphism is given by the flat operator (lowering indices) followed by projecting onto the equivalence class modulo exact one-forms,
[\cdot]\circ\flat:\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{0}(D)\to\Omega^{1}(M)/d\mbox{$C^{\infty}$}(M)
,
u\mapsto[u^{\flat}]=[\nu]
with inverse
[\nu]\mapsto\mathbb{P}\nu^{\sharp}=u
;
equivalence classes coincide,
[\nu]=[\kappa]
, if, and only if,
\nu-\kappa=df
for
f\in\mbox{$C^{\infty}$}(M)
.

The Lie bracket on
\mathfrak{d}
is the negative of the vector field bracket. That is,
\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(u)v=-\langle u,\nabla\rangle v+\langle v,\nabla\rangle u
for
u,v\in\mathfrak{d}
. The coadjoint representation is given by the Lie derivative of one-forms,
\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(u)^{*}[\nu]=[L_{u}\nu]=[di_{u}\nu+i_{u}d\nu]
where
u\in\mathfrak{d}
and
[\nu]\in\mathfrak{d}^{*}
.
Here
i_{u}
is the insertion (contraction) operation and
d
is the exterior derivative.
The adjoint and coadjoint represenations on
\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
are given by the same formulas and the notation will also be identical.

The equivalence class notation will be omitted from now on. E.g.,
\nu\in\mathfrak{d}^{*}
will refer to
[\nu]\in\Omega^{1}(M)/d\mbox{$C^{\infty}$}(M)
with the tacit understanding that
\nu
is only fixed up to an exact one-form.

Let

\mu_{0}:=\flat:\mathfrak{d}\to\mathfrak{d}^{*},\qquad\mathbb{I}_{0}:=-e^{2}\,\flat\circ\Delta^{-1},\qquad\mathcal{A}_{0}:=-1

where
\Delta=-\textup{curl}^{2}:\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{0}(M)\to\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{0}(M)

is the vector Laplacian (since
\textup{div}\,X=0
for
X\in\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{0}(M)
).
The Kaluza-Klein type Hamiltonian function
h_{0}:\mathfrak{d}^{*}\times\mathfrak{g}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as

(4.49)

\displaystyle h_{0}(\nu,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{M}||\nu+\alpha||^{2}\,dx+\frac{1}{2e^{2}}\int_{M}||d\alpha||^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{M}\left\langle\left(\begin{matrix}\mu_{0}^{-1}&-\mu_{0}^{-1}\mathcal{A}_{0}^{*}\\
-\mathcal{A}_{0}\mu_{0}^{-1}&\mathbb{I}_{0}^{-1}+\mathcal{A}_{0}\mu_{0}^{-1}\mathcal{A}_{0}^{*}\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}\nu\\
\alpha\end{matrix}\right),\left(\begin{matrix}\nu\\
\alpha\end{matrix}\right)\right\rangle\,dx

which has the same structure as (
3.37
).
Let
B=\textup{curl}\,A
with
\textup{div}\,A=0
. Then the identifications

\nu=u^{\flat}+eA^{\flat},\qquad\alpha=-eA^{\flat}

imply that equations (
4.47
) are equivalent to the system of forced Lie-Poisson equations

(4.50)

\displaystyle\dot{\nu}

\displaystyle=-\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(u)^{*}\nu,\qquad u=\delta h_{0}/\delta\nu=\mu_{0}^{-1}(\nu-\mathcal{A}_{0}^{*}\alpha)

\displaystyle\dot{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(X)^{*}\alpha+\mathcal{F}^{\flat},\qquad X=\delta h_{0}/\delta\alpha=\mathbb{I}_{0}^{-1}\alpha-\mathcal{A}_{0}u

on
\mathfrak{d}^{*}\times\mathfrak{g}^{*}
.
The proof of this equivalency follows either by direct calculation or, from a structural point of view, by adapting the Hamiltonian structure for Hall MHD found by
[
18
]
to the incompressible case.

4.C.
Closed-loop dynamics and double bracket IDA-PBC

Concerning the choice of sign in Section
2
, the bottom sign is now the relevant one since (
4.50
) is formulated in the space frame, that is
\pm 1
becomes
-1
.

In order to make use of Theorem
2.13
, we assume that
\mathcal{F}^{\flat}=\mathcal{F}(u,A)^{\flat}=\mathcal{U}_{\textup{LP}}(\nu,\alpha)+\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{\textup{diss}}(\nu,\alpha)
as in definitions (
2.10
) and (
2.24
) with
G=1
. The map
C:\mathfrak{d}^{*}\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
and the sign
s=\pm 1
are to be defined in accordance with the control target. It follows that, in the new variables
(\nu,\beta)=\Phi(\nu,\alpha)=(\nu,\alpha+C\nu+N(\nu))
, system (
4.50
) is equivalent to to the dissipative port controlled Hamiltonian system (
2.34
), given by

(4.51)

\displaystyle\dot{\nu}

\displaystyle=-\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(\mu_{C}^{-1}\nu)^{*}\nu+\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(\mu_{0}^{-1}\mathcal{A}_{0}^{*}\beta)^{*}\nu-\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(\mu_{0}^{-1}\mathcal{A}_{0}^{*}N(\nu))^{*}\nu

(4.52)

\displaystyle\dot{\beta}

\displaystyle=-N(\nu)-\beta

with
N(\nu)=-s\mathbb{I}_{0}\mathcal{A}_{0}^{*}\mu_{0}^{-1}\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(\mu_{C}^{-1}\nu)^{*}\nu
. Definition (
2.25
) yields

(4.53)

g_{C}(\nu,\beta)=\frac{1}{2}\langle\nu,\mu_{C}^{-1}\nu\rangle+\frac{s}{2}\langle\beta,\mathbb{I}_{0}^{-1}\beta\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{M}\langle\nu,\mu_{C}^{-1}\nu\rangle\,dx+\frac{s}{2e^{2}}\int_{M}||d\beta||^{2}\,dx

where the angle bracket is used to denote, both, the
L^{2}
- and the pointwise Euclidean pairing.

Depending on whether the goal is to stabilize an energy minimum or maximum the sign is chosen as
s=1
or
s=-1
, respectively.

4.D.
Controlled stability of planar shear flow

Consider now the Hall MHD system (
4.48
) for the case of flow along the horizontal strip
M=\{(x,y):0\leq x\leq L\pi,0\leq y\leq W\pi\}
, where length,
L\pi
, and width,
W\pi
, are fixed, in the presence of a vertical magnetic field,
B=b(x,y)\,e_{3}=\textup{curl}\,A
. That is,

(4.54)

\displaystyle\dot{u}

\displaystyle=-\nabla(p+\frac{1}{2}b^{2})-\langle u,\nabla u\rangle+\mathcal{F}=\mathbb{P}\Big(-\langle u,\nabla u\rangle+\mathcal{F}\Big)

\displaystyle\dot{A}

\displaystyle=-e^{-1}\nabla(p^{e}-\frac{1}{2}b^{2})+u\times B-e^{-1}\mathcal{F}=\mathbb{P}\Big(u\times B-e^{-1}\mathcal{F}\Big)

where
\mathbb{P}
is the Leray-Hodge-Helmholtz projection ensuring
\textup{div}\,u=\textup{div}\,A=0
and the boundary conditions.

Remark 4.1
.

Planar incompressible Hall MHD flow coincides with ordinary MHD since
\textup{curl}\,B\times B=-\nabla b^{2}/2
for a vertical field. But since (
4.54
) is derived as the two-dimensional version of (
4.48
) it will also be referred to as controlled Hall MHD flow.

Let
0\leq\gamma<1
and consider equilibrium solutions,
u_{e}
and
A_{e}
, of the uncontrolled equations, (
4.54
) with
E_{\textup{ext}}=0
, given by

(4.55)

u_{e}=\left(\begin{matrix}\sin(y)\\
0\end{matrix}\right),\qquad A_{e}=-\gamma e^{-1}\left(\begin{matrix}\sin(y)\\
0\end{matrix}\right).

Since
\textup{curl}\,B\times B=-\nabla b^{2}/2
is absorbed by the pressure term in the Euler equation the presence of an uncontrolled vertical magnetic field does not alter the stability properties of incompressible horizontal flow (
[
17
, Section 6.1]
). Hence, for
\gamma=0
, this equilibrium is stable if
\lambda_{1}>1
where
\lambda_{1}=1/L^{2}+1/W^{2}
is the minimal eigenvalue of
-\Delta
in the domain
M
; it is unstable otherwise. See
[
17
, Section 3.3]
. Given an arbitrarily long channel, the goal is to find
\gamma
and
\mathcal{F}(u,A)
such that
(u_{e},A_{e})
is stable.

Define
\Delta_{\gamma}:=\partial_{x}\partial_{x}+(1-\gamma)\partial_{y}\partial_{y}
and
\mu_{C}:\mathfrak{d}\to\mathfrak{d}^{*}
by

(4.56)

\mu_{C}\left(\begin{matrix}v^{1}\\
v^{2}\end{matrix}\right)=\mu_{0}\Delta^{-1}\Delta_{\gamma}\left(\begin{matrix}v^{1}\\
v^{2}\end{matrix}\right)=\mu_{0}\mathbb{P}\left(\begin{matrix}(1-\gamma)v^{1}\\
v^{2}\end{matrix}\right)

where
\Delta
and
\Delta_{\gamma}
operate component wise on vector fields. Because of
\mu_{C}^{-1}=\mu_{0}^{-1}(1+\mathcal{A}_{0}^{*}C)
and
\mathcal{A}_{0}=-1
this implies
C=1-\mu_{0}\Delta_{\gamma}^{-1}\Delta\mu_{0}^{-1}
.
Notice that

C\Big(\nu^{1}(y)\,dx\Big)=-\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\nu^{1}(y)\,dx.

Remark 4.2
.

The idea behind this choice of metric is that it makes the channel appear shorter as
\gamma
approaches
1
from below. Measured with respect to
\mu_{C}
the channel length is
\sqrt{1-\gamma}L\pi
.

Consider now the corresponding feedback controlled system (
4.54
) with control force

(4.57)

\displaystyle\mathcal{f}(u,A)

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}^{-1}\Big(\mathcal{U}_{\textup{LP}}(\nu,\alpha)+\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{\textup{diss}}(\nu,\alpha)\Big)=\mu_{0}^{-1}\Big(\mathcal{U}_{\textup{LP}}(\nu,\alpha)+\mathcal{U}_{\textup{diss}}(\nu,\alpha+C\nu+N(\nu))\Big)

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}^{-1}\Big(C\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(u)^{*}\nu-dN(\nu)\,\mbox{$\text{{ad}}$}(u)^{*}\nu+2N(\nu)+\alpha+C\nu\Big)

The equations of motion in the
(\nu,\beta)=\Phi(\nu,\alpha)
variables are (
4.51
)-(
4.52
). The equilibrium is mapped to

(\nu_{e},\beta_{e})=\Phi(\nu_{e},\alpha_{e})=(\mu_{c}u_{e},0)

since
N(\nu_{e})=0
and
\alpha_{e}=-C\nu_{e}
.

To show nonlinear stability of
(\nu_{e},\beta_{e})
we use the energy-Casimir method.
The first variation of
g_{C}
at the equilibrium is

(4.58)

D^{1}g_{C}(\nu_{e},\beta_{e})(\delta\nu,\delta\beta)=\langle\delta\nu,\mu_{C}^{-1}\nu_{e}\rangle=\langle\delta\nu,u_{e}\rangle=-\int_{M}\psi_{e}(\star d\delta\nu)\,dxdy=-\int_{M}\psi_{e}\delta\omega\,dxdy

where
\psi_{e}
is the stream function of
u_{e}
,
u_{e}=\nabla^{s}\psi_{e}=(-\partial_{y}\psi_{e},\partial_{x}\psi_{e})
, and
\star
is the Hodge star operator,
\star f(x,y)dx\wedge dy=f(x,y)
, and
\star d\delta\nu=\delta\omega
is the vorticity associated to
\delta\nu
.
This expression is nonzero whence a Casimir function,
K_{C}
, is needed such that
D^{1}(g_{C}+K_{C})(\nu_{e},\beta_{e})=0
. A suitable choice is

(4.59)

K_{C}(\nu,\beta)=K_{C}(\nu)=-\frac{1}{2(1-\gamma)}\int_{M}(\star d\nu)^{2}\,dxdy

where
\star d\nu
is the vorticity associated to
\nu
. Vorticity integrals are Casimir functions on
\mathfrak{d}^{*}
, hence
K_{C}
is constant along solutions of (
4.51
).
Coadjoint orbits in
\mathfrak{d}^{*}
are of the form
\mathcal{O}_{\nu_{e}}=\{\nu\in\mathfrak{d}^{*}:*d\nu=(*d\nu_{e})\circ\phi,\;\phi\in\mathcal{D}\}
, i.e., those elements whose vorticities are related by an area preserving diffeomorphism.

Moreover,
\omega_{e}=\star d\nu_{e}=\star d\mu_{C}\nabla^{s}\psi_{e}=\Delta_{\gamma}\psi_{e}
and
\psi_{e}
are related by
\omega_{e}=-(1-\gamma)\psi_{e}
. Therefore,

(4.60)

D^{1}(g_{C}+K_{C})(\nu_{e},\beta_{e})(\delta\nu,\delta\beta)=-\int_{M}\psi_{e}\delta\omega\,dxdy-\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\int_{M}\omega_{e}\delta\omega\,dxdy=0.

Let
\delta\psi
be the stream function associated to a perturbation
\delta\nu
, i.e.
\delta\nu=\mu_{C}\nabla^{s}\delta\psi
, and
\delta\omega=\star d\delta\nu=\Delta_{\gamma}\delta\psi
the vorticity.
The second variation is now estimated by the Poincaré inequality as

\displaystyle D^{2}(g_{C}+K_{C})(\nu_{e},\beta_{e})((\delta\nu,\delta\beta),(\delta\nu,\delta\beta))

\displaystyle=\langle\delta\nu,\mu_{C}^{-1}\delta\nu\rangle-\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\int_{M}(\delta\omega)^{2}\,dxdy+s\langle\delta\beta,\mathbb{I}_{0}^{-1}\delta\beta\rangle

\displaystyle=-\int_{M}\delta\psi\Delta_{\gamma}\delta\psi\,dxdy-\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\int_{M}(\delta\omega)^{2}\,dxdy+s\langle\delta\beta,\mathbb{I}_{0}^{-1}\delta\beta\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{1}(\gamma)^{-1}\int_{M}(\Delta_{\gamma}\delta\psi)^{2}\,dxdy-\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\int_{M}(\delta\omega)^{2}\,dxdy+s\langle\delta\beta,\mathbb{I}_{0}^{-1}\delta\beta\rangle

where

\lambda_{1}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{L^{2}}+\frac{1-\gamma}{W^{2}}

is the smallest eigenvalue of
-\Delta_{\gamma}
on
M
.
The quadratic form
D^{2}(g_{C}+K_{C})(\nu_{e},\beta_{e})
may be indefinite for
\gamma=0
due to the Casimir contribution. However, it is negative definite if

(4.61)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-\gamma)L^{2}}+\frac{1}{W^{2}}

\displaystyle>1

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=-1

With these choices,
L_{C}(\nu,\beta):=-g_{C}(\nu,\beta)-K_{C}(\nu)+g_{C}(\nu_{e},\beta_{e})+K_{C}(\nu_{e})
is a Lyapunov function: it is positive in a punctured neighborhood of
(\nu_{e},\beta_{e})
and decreasing along solutions.
Let
(\nu_{t},\beta_{t})=(\nu_{e}+\delta\nu_{t},\beta_{e}+\delta\beta_{t})
be a perturbed solution of (
4.51
)-(
4.52
). It follows that

L_{C}(\nu_{e}+\delta\nu_{0},\beta_{e}+\delta\beta_{0})\geq L_{C}(\nu_{t},\beta_{t})\geq\lambda_{1}(\gamma)^{-1}\Big(\frac{1}{(1-\gamma)L^{2}}+\frac{1}{W^{2}}-1\Big)\int_{M}(\delta\omega_{t})^{2}+\int_{M}(\nabla\delta\beta_{t})^{2}

and hence
(\nu_{e},\beta_{e})
is a nonlinearly stable equilibrium, with respect to the Sobolev
H^{1}
norm, when conditions (
4.61
) are satisfied.

Moreover, (
2.26
) implies that

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}L_{C}(\nu_{t},\beta_{t})=-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}g_{C}(\nu_{t},\beta_{t})=-||N(\nu_{t})||_{\mathbb{I}_{0}^{-1}}^{2}-||\delta\beta_{t}||_{\mathbb{I}_{0}^{-1}}^{2}

converges to
0
since
L_{C}
is nonnegative and decreasing along solutions. Hence
N(\nu_{t})\to 0
and
\beta_{t}\to\beta_{e}=0
asymptotically in the
H^{1}
-norm. It is not obvious that this implies also convergence of
\nu_{t}
to an equilibrium
\widetilde{\nu}_{e}
,
hence asymptotic stability is not concluded.

However, even if
\nu_{t}\to\widetilde{\nu}_{e}
it need not be the case that
\delta\omega_{t}\to 0
, i.e.
\widetilde{\nu}_{e}=\nu_{e}
: the reason for this is the same as in the satellite example in Section
3
, perturbations to different coadjoint orbits in
\mathfrak{d}^{*}
cannot be restored since this structure is preserved by the dynamics.

5.
Comparison to CL and IDA-PBC techniques

This paper rests on ideas from CL theory and IDA-PBC techniques, it therefore makes sense to compare the results to these two approaches.

Concerning CL theory, and specifically the satellite example, we note that
[
8
]
conclude stability of the controlled satellite by showing that it is equivalent to a Hamiltonian system, up to a certain factor (cf. sentence immediately below
[
8
, Equ. (3.7)]
). In the present approach, there is no need to introduce such a factor ex post due to the isomorphism
G
. Thus in contrast to ‘classical’ CL theory, the matching construction of Proposition
2.5
yields a direct identification of the feedback controlled and the corresponding Lie-Poisson systems. However, for the conclusion of stability the manner of this identification does, at least for the satellite example, not make any difference.

We emphasize that stability of the controlled satellite was shown in Section
3.C
with respect to the feedback law (
2.36
) which
does not
satisfy the matching conditions. Thus this control approach is independent of matching conditions which need not always have a (non-trivial) solution.

The same holds for the conclusion of asymptotic stability. In
[
3
]
this was shown by first proving stability via the matching construction and then adding a dissipative control to achieve asymptotic stability. Again, the present approach is quite different since it is not only independent of matching conditions but also because dissipation is included by the double bracket construction (via the nonlinear map
N
in equation (
2.21
)) and a restoring force in the closed loop variable
\beta
(via
\mathcal{U}_{\textup{diss}}
in equation (
2.22
)).

Concerning IDA-PBC approaches to Hamiltonian systems, it is not a priori clear how to include symmetry arguments in the construction of the interconnection and damping structures. In the present context this rests on the geometric formulation of the control force in Proposition
2.2
which encodes the symmetries ab initio in the desired manner. From this we arrive at the closed loop equations (
2.30
)-(
2.31
) constructively such that the IDA-PBC structure may be read off since these equations are linear in the first order derivatives of the quadratic function
g_{C}
defined in (
2.25
).

Thus, while the construction of Theorem
2.13
is covered by the IDA-PBC methodology from an abstract point of view, the benefit of the present geometric approach is that it is constructive and makes use of available symmetries.

Moreover, the coordinate independence of the geometric formulation allows to apply Theorem
2.13
to the infinite dimensional problem of feedback stabilization of Hall MHD flow (Section
4
). This system has been treated previously neither from the CL nor from the IDA-PBC point of view, although there are many works concerned with feedback control and (linear) stability of (Hall) MHD flow (e.g.,
[
14
,
13
,
32
,
30
,
29
]
). The results of this paper are restricted to Lie-Poisson systems defined on direct product Lie algebras. A superficial inspection shows that the derivation of Theorem
2.13
depends only on structural arguments which may also be formulated for
\mathcal{G}
-invariant Hamiltonian systems on the cotangent bundle of a general principal fiber bundle
\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{D}
with structure group
\mathcal{G}
, when the Hamiltonian is associated to a
\mathcal{G}
-invariant Riemannian metric on
\mathcal{P}
and there exists natural principal bundle connection specifying the control directions. Thus it may be hoped that the approach can be generalized such that other examples can be treated in a similar manner.
