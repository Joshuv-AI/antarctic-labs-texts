A relative trace formula approach to the Kuznetsov formula on GSp 4 \text{GSp}_{4}
Félicien Comtat

Abstract
. Relative trace formulas play a central role in studying automorphic forms. In this paper, we use a relative trace formula approach to derive a Kuznetsov type formula for the group GSp 4 \text{GSp}_{4} . We focus on giving a final formula that is as explicit as possible, and we plan on returning to applications elsewhere.

1.
Introduction

In this work we develop a Kuznetsov formula for the group
\text{GSp}_{4}
.
To motivate our results, we first recall the Kuznetsov formula for
\text{GL}_{2}
, an identity relating spectral information about the quotient space

\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
(where
\Gamma
is a congruence subgroup) to some arithmetic input.

For arbitrarily chosen nonzero integers
n
and
m
and any reasonable test function
h
, the spectral side involves
the quantity

(1)

h(t_{u})a_{m}(u)\overline{a_{n}(u)},

where
u
ranges over eigenfunctions of the Laplace operator involved in the
spectral decomposition of
L^{2}(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})
,
a_{m}(u)
is the
m
-th Fourier coefficient of
u
,
and
t_{u}
is the corresponding spectral parameter.
More precisely, the spectrum of
L^{2}(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})
can be described as the direct sum of the

discrete spectrum
and the
continuous spectrum
.
The discrete spectrum is the direct sum of
1
-dimensional subspaces spanned by cuspidal Maaß forms
(the
cuspidal spectrum
) plus the constant function (the
residual spectrum
).
The continuous spectrum is a direct integral of
1
-dimensional subspaces spanned by the Eisenstein series.
The spectral side of the Kuznetsov formula correspondingly splits as a discrete sum over Maaß forms plus a
continuous integral over Eisenstein series.

The arithmetic-geometric side is a sum of two contributions, that may be seen as the contributions from the
two elements of the Weyl group of
\text{GL}_{2}
.
The identity contribution is given by the delta symbol
\delta_{n,m}
times the integral of the spectral test function

h
against the spectral measure
\frac{t}{\pi^{2}}\tanh(\pi t)dt
.
For this reason, the Kuznetsov formula may be viewed as a result of quasi-orthogonality for the Fourier coefficients
a_{m}(\cdot)

and
a_{n}(\cdot)
, provided the remaining contribution can be controlled.
The latter consists of a sum of Kloosterman sums weighted by some integral transform of the test function
h
, involving Bessel
functions.

Applications of the Kuznetsov formula involve using known results about any of the two sides to derive information
about the other side.
On one hand, the flexibility allowed by the choice of the test function
h
enables one to use known bounds about the Kloosterman
sums to study the distribution of the discrete spectrum and the size of the Fourier coefficients of Maaß forms.
On the other hand, understanding the Fourier coefficients of Maaß forms as well as the integral transform appearing on
the geometric side yields strong bounds for sums of Kloosterman sums.

Recently, Kuznetsov formulae for
\text{GL}_{3}
have been developed by Blomer and Buttcane, with similar applications as described above.
It would thus be interesting to establish the corresponding formulae for other groups.

In the classical proof of the
\text{GL}_{2}
Kuznetsov formula, one computes the inner product of two Poincaré series in two
different ways, one involving the spectral decomposition of
L^{2}(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})
, and the other one by computing
the Fourier coefficients of the Poincaré series and unfolding. This gives a “pre-Kuznetsov formula”, that one then
proceeds to integrate against the test function
h
, obtaining on the geometric side the integral transforms of
h

mentioned above.

Another approach, which one may call the relative trace formula approach to the Kuznetsov formula, builds upon the relative trace formula introduced by
Jacquet
[
6
]
.
In the case of
\text{GL}_{2}
, the relative trace formula approach to the Kuznetsov formula is apparently based on unpublished work of Zagier,
detailed in
[
7
]
.
This approach is developed in the adelic framework in
[
9
]
for the congruence subgroup
\Gamma=\Gamma_{1}(N)
.
It proceeds by integrating an automorphic kernel

K_{f}(x,y)=\sum_{\gamma\in\text{PGL}_{2}(\mathbb{Q})}f(x^{-1}\gamma y),

where
f
is a suitable test function.
The spectral expansion of the kernel will then involve the quantity
\tilde{f}(t_{u})u(x)\overline{u(y)}
, where
u
ranges
over the eigenfunctions involved in the spectral decomposition of
L^{2}(\Gamma(N)\backslash\mathbb{H})
,

t_{u}
is the spectral parameter of
u
, and
\tilde{f}
is the
spherical transform
of
f
.
Thus integrating
K_{f}(x,y)
against a suitable character on
U\times U
, where
U={\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&*\\
&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]}
, one gets the
quantity (
1
) with
h=\tilde{f}
.
On the other hand, using the Bruhat decomposition for
\text{PGL}_{2}(\mathbb{Q})
, one can decompose the integral over
U\times U
as
a sum over elements of the Weyl group and over diagonal matrices in
\text{PGL}_{2}(\mathbb{Q})
of some orbital integrals.
In the case of the identity element, at most one diagonal matrix will have a non-zero contribution, which will turn out
to be a delta symbol times some integral transform of the function
f
. In the case of the longest element in the Weyl
group, each positive integer in
N\mathbb{Z}
will have a nonzero contribution, given by a Kloosterman sum times a second kind of
integral transform of
f
. A more refined version is then obtained by taking the Mellin transform of the primitive formula
obtained.
Note that in this approach, one gets on the geometric side some integral transforms of the function
f
, hence one has to do
some extra work to relate these to the test function
h=\tilde{f}
appearing in the spectral side.

A couple of remarks are in order about the choice of
f
. Firstly, the spectral expansion of the kernel involves the spectral
decomposition of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{>0}\text{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Q})\backslash\text{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{A}))
rather than
L^{2}(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})
.
By restricting
f
to be left and right
K_{\infty}
-invariant
(where
K_{\infty}=\text{SO}_{2}
), only right-
K_{\infty}
-invariant automorphic forms
\phi

(thus corresponding to adelization of functions on the homogeneous space
\mathbb{H}=SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})/K_{\infty}
)
will show up in the spectral expansion of the kernel, but other choices are possible.
Also one may choose the test function
f
at unramified places so as to get a final formula that include the Hecke eigenvalues
of a fixed Hecke operator of index coprime to the level
N
.

Our plan is to implement the relative trace formula approach in the case of
\text{GSp}_{4}
.
In contrast to the case of
\text{GL}_{2}
, there is more than one non-conjugate unipotent subgroups
U
.
Choosing
U
to be the unipotent radical of the Borel subgroup (that is the minimal parabolic subgroup)
will yield Whittaker coefficients of the automorphic forms involved (instead of the Fourier coefficients).
The Whittaker coefficients have a “multiplicity one” property, which ensures that the global Whittaker coefficients factor
into a product of local coefficients.
These local Whittaker coefficients can be written down in terms of local Satake parameters, which is important for applications.
Also in contrast to the case of
\text{GL}_{2}
, not every automorphic form has non-identically zero Whittaker coefficients.
For instance, Siegel modular form give rise to automorphic forms whose Whittaker coefficients vanish identically.
Thus, only
generic
automorphic forms (
i.e,
with non-identically zero Whittaker coefficients)
will survive the integration on
U\times U
and contribute to the final formula.

Let us briefly sketch some similarities and differences with the Kuznetsov formula for
\text{GL}_{3}
, which also has
rank
2
.
On the spectral side, the continuous contribution is in both cases given on the one hand by
minimal Eisenstein series
,
(that is, attached to the minimal parabolic subgroup), and on the other hand by Eisenstein series induced from non-minimal parabolics by Maaß forms
on
\text{GL}_{2}
. However, in the case of
\text{GL}_{3}
, the two non-minimal proper standard parabolic subgroup are
associated
,
hence by Langlands theory their Eisenstein series are essentially the same. On the other hand, for
\text{GSp}_{4}
, we have
two distinct non-associated such parabolic subgroups, giving rise to two distinct kinds of Eisenstein series.
As for as the geometric side, the Weyl group of
\text{GL}_{3}
has six elements, while the Weyl goup of
\text{GSp}_{4}
has eight.
However, it seems interesting to notice that in both case, only the identity element and the longest three elements
in the Weyl group have a non-zero contribution, thus eventually giving in total four distinct terms.

Finally, let us mention that Siu Hang Man has independently derived a Kuznetsov formula for
\text{Sp}_{4}
using the more classical technique
of computing the inner product of Poincaré series, and has derived some applications towards the Ramanujan Conjecture
[
11
]
.
However, because the techniques employed and the final formulae differ, the author believes that our works are complementary rather than redundant.
Indeed, the flexibility offered by the adelic framework enables us to treat the test function differently at each place.
As a result, by choosing an appropriate test function at finite places, our formula might incorporate the eigenvalues of an arbitrary Hecke operator.
Furthermore, at the Archimedean place, we make use of two deep theorems of functional analysis on real reductive groups (namely Harish-Chandra inversion theorem
and Wallach’s Whittaker inversion theorem) in order to produce an arbitrary Paley-Wiener test function on the spectral side, and relate it explicitly to its transform
appearing on the arithmetic side.
As a last point, working with
\text{GSp}_{4}
instead of
\text{Sp}_{4}
enables us to work with a central character.

The primary goal of this work is to write down the main Kuznetsov formula (in Theorem
6.0.1
) as explicitly as possible.
Applications of Kuznetsov formulae classically include counts of the cuspidal spectrum,
bounds towards the Ramanujan conjecture and bounds for the number of automorphic forms violating the Ramanujan conjecture,
large sieve inequalities involving the Hecke eigenvalues,
estimates for moments of
L
-functions,
and distribution of the Whittaker coefficients.
Some of these have been developed in
[
11
]
, and we hope to work out further applications in future work.

Definition 2.0.1
.

The general symplectic group of degree 2 over a field
\mathbb{F}
is the group

\text{GSp}_{4}(\mathbb{F})=\{M\in\text{Mat}_{4}(\mathbb{F}):\exists\mu\in\mathbb{F}^{\times},{}^{\top}MJM=\mu J\},

where

-I_{2}&\end{smallmatrix}\right]}
and

denotes the transpose matrix of

.

Note that some authors use different realizations of

[

]

(to which we refer, along with

to ours by the matrix
{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\begin{smallmatrix}&1\\

&\begin{smallmatrix}1&\\
&1\end{smallmatrix}\end{smallmatrix}\right]}

From now on we denote

The

The scalar

The centre of

.

\text{Sp}_{4}(\mathbb{F})=\{M\in G(\mathbb{F}):\mu(M)=1\}.

2.2.
Compact subgroups

A choice of maximal compact subgroup of

An easy computation shows

For each prime

2.3.

Parabolic subgroups are subgroups such that

Given a minimal parabolic subgroup

(with respect to
P_{0}

If

of

where

is a reductive subgroup and

We give here the three non-trivial standard (with respect to our choice of

Lie algebras and characters

Following Arthur

3.1.
Generic representations

3.1.1.
Generic characters

A character
\psi
of
U(\mathbb{Q})\backslash U(\mathbb{A})
is said to be
generic
if its differential is non-trivial
on each of the eigenspaces
\mathfrak{n}_{\alpha}
corresponding to simple roots
\alpha
.
Explicitly, If
\theta
is the standard additive character of
\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},m_{2})\in(\mathbb{Q}^{\times})^{2}
,
generic characters of
U(\mathbb{A})
are given by

(4)

\psi_{\mathbf{m}}\left(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&&c&a-cx\\
x&1&a&b\\
&&1&-x\\
&&&1\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right)=\theta(m_{1}x+m_{2}c).

Note that all generic characters may be obtained from each other by conjugation by an element of
T/Z
,
as we have for all
u\in U(\mathbb{A})

(5)

\psi_{\mathbf{m}}\left(u\right)=\psi_{\mathbf{1}}\left(t_{\mathbf{m}}^{-1}ut_{\mathbf{m}}\right),

where

(6)

t_{\mathbf{m}}={\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\begin{smallmatrix}m_{1}&\\
&1\end{smallmatrix}&\\
&\begin{smallmatrix}m_{1}m_{2}&\\
&m_{1}^{2}m_{2}\end{smallmatrix}\end{smallmatrix}\right]}.

3.1.2.
Whittaker coefficients and generic representations

If
\phi
is any automorphic form on
G(\mathbb{A})
and
\psi
a generic character, the
\psi
-Whittaker coefficient

of
\phi
is by definition

(7)

\mathcal{W}_{\psi}(\phi)(g)=\int_{U(\mathbb{Q})\backslash U(\mathbb{A})}\phi(ug)\psi(u)^{-1}du.

\phi
is called
\psi
-generic if
\mathcal{W}_{\psi}
is not identically zero as a function of
g
.
Changing variable and using the left-
G(\mathbb{Q})
-invariance of
\phi
, note that we have

\mathcal{W}_{\psi_{\mathbf{m}}}(\phi)(g)=\frac{1}{|m_{1}^{4}m_{2}^{3}|}\mathcal{W}_{\psi_{\mathbf{1}}}(\phi)(t_{\mathbf{m}}^{-1}g)

In particular,
\phi
is
\psi
-generic for some generic character
\psi
if and only if it is
\psi
-generic for any
generic character
\psi
, henceforth we shall just say
\phi
is
generic
.
An irreducible automorphic representation
(\pi,V_{\pi})
is called generic if

contains a generic automorphic form

\phi
.
Equivalently, every automorphic form in the space of a generic irreducible automorphic representation
\pi
is generic,
since otherwise the kernel of the map
\phi\mapsto W_{\psi}(\phi)
would be lead to a non-trivial invariant subspace of
\pi
,
contradicting the irreducibility of
\pi
.
Since
U
may as well be viewed as the unipotent part of the minimal parabolic subgroup of
\text{Sp}_{4}
,
we can define the Whittaker coefficients of automorphic forms
\phi
on
\text{Sp}_{4}
in the exact same way as (

to
\text{Sp}_{4}(\mathbb{A})
. This gives a similar notion of generic automorphic forms and generic representations for
\text{Sp}_{4}
.
Later on, we shall restrict automorphic forms on
\text{GSp}_{4}
to
\text{Sp}_{4}
.
Let us briefly explain the corresponding operations on automorphic representations.

Definition 3.1.1
.

Let
(\pi,V_{\pi})
be an automorphic representation of
\text{GSp}_{4}(\mathbb{A})
realized by right translation on a subspace of
L^{2}(G(\mathbb{Q})Z(\mathbb{R})\backslash G(\mathbb{A}))
.
We define a representation
\text{res }\pi
of
\text{Sp}_{4}(\mathbb{A})
as the action of
\text{Sp}_{4}(\mathbb{A})
on
\left\{\phi_{|\text{Sp}_{4}(\mathbb{A})}:\phi\in V_{\pi}\right\}
.
It is a quotient of the restriction
\text{Res }\pi=\pi_{|\text{Sp}_{4}(\mathbb{A})}
.

The representation
\text{res }\pi
does not have finite length in general. However, the following shall be useful later on.

Lemma 3.1.1
.

Let
\pi
be an irreducible automorphic representation
\pi
of
G(\mathbb{A})
that occurs discretely in
L^{2}(G(\mathbb{Q})Z(\mathbb{R})\backslash G(\mathbb{A}))
.
Then
\pi
is generic if and only if
\text{res }\pi
has a generic constituent.

Proof.

Fix a generic character
\psi
.
Note that for any automorphic form
\psi
on
G(\mathbb{A})
we have

\mathcal{W}_{\psi}(\phi_{|\text{Sp}_{4}})=\left(\mathcal{W}_{\psi}(\phi)\right)_{|\text{Sp}_{4}}
.
From this, it is clear that if
\text{res }\pi
has a generic constituent then
\pi
is generic.
Let us show the converse.
Assume no constituent of
\text{res }\pi
is generic, so for all
\phi\in V_{\pi}
,

\mathcal{W}_{\psi}(\phi)_{|\text{Sp}_{4}(\mathbb{A})}=0.

Let
\phi\in\pi
and
g\in G(\mathbb{A})
. Then
\pi(g)\phi\in V_{\pi}
hence

\mathcal{W}_{\psi}(\phi)(g)=\mathcal{W}_{\psi}(\pi(g)\phi)(1)=0.

Thus
\pi
is not generic.
∎

We now prove a similar lemma for the restriction of non-cuspidal representations.

Lemma 3.1.2
.

Let
\pi
be an irreducible automorphic representation
\pi
of
G(\mathbb{A})
that occurs discretely in
L^{2}(G(\mathbb{Q})Z(\mathbb{R})\backslash G(\mathbb{A}))
.
Then
\pi
is non-cuspidal if and only if
\text{res }\pi
has no cuspidal constituent.

Proof.

Recall
\pi
is cuspidal if the constant term

C_{\phi}(g)=\int_{U(\mathbb{Q})\backslash U(\mathbb{A})}\phi(ug)du

of some (equivalently, any, since
\pi
is irreducible) function
\phi
in the space of
\pi
vanishes identically.
The exact same proof as Lemma
3.1.1
, replacing the generic character
\psi
by
1
, shows that
\pi
is
non-cuspidal if and only if
\text{res }\pi
has a non-cuspidal component.
However, we want to show that if
\pi
is non-cuspidal, then
\text{res }\pi
has no cuspidal component.
So suppose that
\text{res }\pi
has a cuspidal component.
This means there is
\phi\in V_{\pi}
such that
(C_{\phi})_{|\text{Sp}_{4}(\mathbb{A})}=0
. We want to show that
C_{\phi}
is identically zero on
\text{GSp}_{4}(\mathbb{A})
.
Now changing variables and using the left-invariance of
\phi
under
\text{GSp}_{4}(\mathbb{Q})
, if
t\in T(\mathbb{Q})
then we have
C_{\phi}(tg)=C_{\phi}(g)
.
In addition, if
z\in Z(\mathbb{A})
then
C_{\phi}(zg)=\omega_{\pi}(z)C_{\phi}(g)
. Moreover, since
\pi
is an admissible representation,
\phi
is right-invariant by
\text{GSp}_{4}(\mathbb{Z}_{p})
for almost all prime

.
It follows that there exists a finite set of places

such that for any

,
if
\mu(g)\in\mathbb{Q}^{\times}(\mathbb{A}^{\times})^{2}\prod_{p\not\in S}\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times}
then
C_{\phi}(g)=0
.
The following lemma concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.1.3
.

Let
S
be any finite set of places containing
\infty
.
We have
\mathbb{Q}^{\times}(\mathbb{A}^{\times})^{2}\prod_{p\not\in S}\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times}=\mathbb{A}^{\times}
.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathbb{A}^{\times}
.
By strong approximation, we have
x=qu
, with
q\in\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
and
u\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\prod_{p<\infty}\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times}
.
Now by the Chinese Remainders Theorem, there exists an integer
n>0
such that for all finite
p\in S
, we have
nu_{p}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times})^{2}
.
For all
p\not\in S
, let
\epsilon_{p}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times}
such that
\epsilon_{p}nu_{p}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times})^{2}
.
Define
\epsilon_{p}=1
for

.
Then
n\epsilon u\in(\mathbb{A}^{\times})^{2}
, and
x=(qn^{-1})(n\epsilon u)\prod_{p\not\in S}\epsilon_{p}^{-1}
.
∎

3.2.
The basic kernel

The group
Z(\mathbb{Q})Z(\mathbb{R})\backslash Z(\mathbb{A})
is compact and acts on the Hilbert space
L^{2}(G(\mathbb{Q})Z(\mathbb{R})\backslash G(\mathbb{A}))
by
right translation.
Since
Z(\mathbb{Q})Z(\mathbb{R})\backslash Z(\mathbb{A})
is abelian, its irreducible representations are characters, thus
by Peter-Weyl theorem we have

L^{2}(G(\mathbb{Q})Z(\mathbb{R})\backslash G(\mathbb{A}))=\bigoplus_{\omega}L^{2}(G(\mathbb{Q})Z(\mathbb{R})\backslash G(\mathbb{A}),\omega),

where the orthogonal direct sum ranges characters of
Z(\mathbb{A})
that are trivial on
Z(\mathbb{Q})Z(\mathbb{R})
, and

L^{2}(G(\mathbb{Q})Z(\mathbb{R})\backslash G(\mathbb{A}),\omega)
is the subspace of
L^{2}(G(\mathbb{Q})Z(\mathbb{R})\backslash G(\mathbb{A}))

of functions
\phi
satisfying

\phi(gz)=\omega(z)\phi(g)

for all
z\in Z(\mathbb{A})
.
Fix such a character
\omega
. If
f:G(\mathbb{A})\to\mathbb{C}
is a measurable function that satisfies

•

f(gz)=\overline{\omega}(z)f(g)
for all
z\in Z(\mathbb{A})
,

•

f
is compactly supported modulo
Z(\mathbb{A})
,

then we define an operator
R(f)
on
L^{2}(G(\mathbb{Q})Z(\mathbb{R})\backslash G(\mathbb{A}),\omega)
by

R(f)\phi(x)=\int_{\overline{G}(\mathbb{A})}f(y)\phi(xy)dy,

where
\overline{G}
denotes
G/Z
.
By
G(\mathbb{Q})
-invariance of
\phi
, we have

\displaystyle R(f)\phi(x)=\int_{\overline{G}(\mathbb{A})}f(x^{-1}y)\phi(y)dy=\sum_{\gamma\in\overline{G}(\mathbb{Q})}\int_{\overline{G}(\mathbb{Q})\backslash\overline{G}(\mathbb{A})}f(x^{-1}\gamma y)\phi(y)dy

Hence, setting

(8)

K_{f}(x,y)=\sum_{\gamma\in\overline{G}(\mathbb{Q})}f(x^{-1}\gamma y),

we have

(9)

R(f)\phi(x)=\int_{\overline{G}(\mathbb{Q})\backslash\overline{G}(\mathbb{A})}K_{f}(x,y)\phi(y)dy.

Now let us argue informally to motivate the more technical actual reasoning.
Let us pretend that
K_{f}(x,.)
is an element of
L^{2}(G(\mathbb{Q})Z(\mathbb{R})\backslash G(\mathbb{A}),\omega)
, and that

L^{2}(G(\mathbb{Q})Z(\mathbb{R})\backslash G(\mathbb{A}),\omega)
has a Hilbert orthonormal base
\mathcal{B}
.
Then we would have

K(x,.)=\sum_{\phi\in\mathcal{B}}\langle K(x,.)|\overline{\phi}\rangle\overline{\phi}.

But equation (
9
) says that
\langle K(x,.)|\overline{\phi}\rangle=R(f)\phi(x)
.
Thus we might expect a spectral expansion of the kernel of the form

(10)

K(x,y)=\sum_{\phi\in\mathcal{B}}R(f)\phi(x)\overline{\phi(y)}.

If moreover each element
\phi
of our base
\mathcal{B}
is an eigenfunction of the operator
R(f)
, say

(11)

R(f)\phi=\lambda_{f}(\phi)

then the above expansion becomes

K(x,y)=\sum_{\phi\in\mathcal{B}}\lambda_{f}(\phi)\phi(x)\overline{\phi(y)}.

Finally, integrating
K(x,y)
on
U\times U
against a character
\overline{\psi_{1}(x)}\psi_{2}(y)
would then yield
a spectral equality involving the Whittaker coefficients and the eigenvalues
\lambda_{f}(\phi)
, of the form

(12)

\int_{(U(\mathbb{Q})\backslash U(\mathbb{A}))^{2}}K(x,y)\overline{\psi_{1}(x)}\psi_{2}(y)dxdy=\sum_{\phi\in\mathcal{B}}\lambda_{f}(\phi)\mathcal{W}_{\psi_{1}}(\phi)\overline{\mathcal{W}_{\psi_{2}}(\phi)}.

Note that in the last step we need (
10
) to hold not only in the
L^{2}
sense, but pointwise, as

(U(\mathbb{Q})\backslash U(\mathbb{A}))^{2}
has measure zero.

Of course,
L^{2}(G(\mathbb{Q})Z(\mathbb{R})\backslash G(\mathbb{A}),\omega)

does not
have a Hilbert orthonormal base, due to the presence
of continuous spectrum. However, after adding the proper continuous contribution,
a spectral expansion of the form (
10
) has been proved by Arthur
[
1
]
*pages 928-934,
building on the spectral decomposition of
L^{2}(G(\mathbb{Q})Z(\mathbb{R})\backslash G(\mathbb{A}),\omega)
by Langlands.
We may then reduce from global to local as follows.
By general theory, we may choose automorphic forms
\phi
appearing in the spectral expansion of the kernel
to be factorizable vectors
\phi_{\infty}\otimes\bigotimes_{p}\phi_{p}
.
If moreover we take
f
factorizable, say
f=f_{\infty}\prod_{p}f_{p}
, then the computation of
R(f)\phi
reduces
to the computation of the action of each local component
f_{v}
on
\phi_{v}
.
By choosing the local components
f_{v}
appropriately, we can ensure that each
\phi_{v}
is an eigenvector of the
operator corresponding to
f_{v}
, so that (
11
) holds.
The determination of
\lambda_{f}(\phi)
then amounts, at the infinite place, to the study
of the spherical transform of
f_{\infty}
, and at finite places
p
, of the action of the local Hecke algebra.
Specifically, from now on we assume
f
is as follows.

Assumption 3.2.1
.

From now on we assume

where

•

is any smooth, left and right

-invariant and

for all

•

whenever

f_{p}(g)=\begin{cases}\frac{\overline{\omega_{p}}(z)}{Vol(\overline{\Gamma_{p}})}\text{ if there exists }z\in Z(\mathbb{Q}_{p})\text{ such that }g\in z\Gamma_{p}\\
0\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note that this assumption can be fulfilled if and only if we have the following compatibility condition

For all prime

\omega_{p}
to

3.4.
The Archimedean representation

We first show that in our situation the representation at the Archimedean place must be an irreducible principal series representation, that is full induced from the Borel subgroup.

Proposition 3.4.1

Any generic irreducible spherical representation

Proof.

As explained at the end of

In particular the maximal Gelfand-Kirillov dimension is 4, and the irreducible large representations are either discrete series

Now the multiplicity of each possible

It is then known that the trivial

\overline{\pi}(f_{\infty})v=\int_{\overline{G}(\mathbb{R})}f_{\infty}(g)\pi(g)vdg=\int_{\text{Sp}_{4}(\mathbb{R})}f_{\infty}(g)\pi(g)vdg.

If

-fixed function satisfying (

.
Using the integration formula
[
4
]
*Ch. I Corollary 5.3 and right-

\displaystyle\pi(f_{\infty})\phi(1)

\displaystyle=\int_{\text{Sp}_{4}(\mathbb{R})}f_{\infty}(g)\pi(g)\phi(1)dg

\displaystyle=\int_{K_{\infty}}\int_{UA^{+}}f_{\infty}(an)\phi(an)dadndk=\int_{UA^{+}}f_{\infty}(an)e^{(\rho_{\text{B}}+\nu)(\log(a))}dadn\phi(1),

where

Therefore, using the Iwasawa decomposition and left-

the eigenvalue

\displaystyle\lambda_{\pi}(f)

\displaystyle=\int_{\text{Sp}_{4}(\mathbb{R})}f_{\infty}(g)e^{(\rho+\nu)(A(g))}dg

∎

The spherical transform

On the other hand, the geometric side will involve some different integral transform of our test function

.
It is therefore natural to investigate the analytic properties of

, and to seek to recover

from

.
This can be achieved by the Paley-Wiener theorem and Harish-Chandra inversion theorem.

3.4.2.
The Paley-Wiener theorem and Harish-Chandra inversion theorem.

The material in this section is taken from

.
Let us introduce a bit of notation.
We denote by

Explicitly the Killing form is given by

Harish-Chandra’s

-function
captures the asymptotic behaviour of the spherical function and it gives the Plancherel measure.
More precisely, by Theorem 6.14 of

]
*Chap. IV, if

and

Moreover,

,
and has meromorphic continuation to

given in our situation by the expression

c(-i\nu)=c_{0}\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi}\frac{2^{-\langle i\nu,\alpha_{0}\rangle}\Gamma(\langle i\nu,\alpha_{0}\rangle)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{\frac{3}{2}+\langle i\nu,\alpha_{0}\rangle}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{\frac{1}{2}+\langle i\nu,\alpha_{0}\rangle}{2}\right)},

Using the duplication formula

3.4.3.

As mentioned above, the Whittaker function is a non-zero

|W(\nu,g,\psi)|\leq\int_{U(\mathbb{R})}|e^{(\nu+\rho)(A(Jug))}|du=e^{(\rho-\Re(\nu))(A(g))}c(\Re(\nu))

Moreover, it has meromorphic continuation to all

Ishii

computed explicit integral representations for the normalized Jacquet integral

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(\nu,a,\psi)=2a_{1}a_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{\frac{\nu_{2}-\nu_{1}}{2}}(2\pi v_{1})K_{\frac{\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}}{2}}(2\pi v_{2})

\displaystyle\times\exp\left(-\pi\left(\frac{a_{2}^{2}}{v_{1}v_{2}}+\frac{v_{1}v_{2}}{a_{1}^{2}}+a_{1}^{2}\left(\frac{v_{1}}{v_{2}}+\frac{v_{2}}{v_{1}}\right)\right)\right)\frac{dv_{1}dv_{2}}{v_{1}v_{2}}.

The following theorem is a consequence of
[
19
]
*Ch. 15.
Let

3.4.5.
An integral transform

Let
g\in G(\mathbb{R})
,
t\in A^{+}(\mathbb{R})
and
\psi
a generic character of
U(\mathbb{R})
.
When dealing with the geometric side of the relative trace formula,
we shall be interested in the integral

Using expression (
15
) and applying Theorem 5.20 of
[

*Ch.I that relates integration

\phi_{\nu}(g)=\int_{U(\mathbb{R})}e^{(\rho+\nu)(A(Jug)}e^{(\rho-\nu)(A(Ju))}du.

From this identity, ignoring convergence issues and treating integrals as if they were

(28)

\int_{U(\mathbb{R})}\phi_{\nu}(tug)\overline{\psi(u)}du=W(\nu,g,\psi)W(-\nu,t^{-1},\overline{\psi}).

However, the domain of absolute convergence of the two Jacquet integral in the right hand side are complementary from each other,
and the integral in the left hand side is likely not absolutely convergent, making such a result,
where the left hand side is (optimistically) a semi-convergent integral and the right-hand side is defined by meromorphic continuation,
likely difficult to prove.

Carrying on with this heuristic and using Theorem
3.4.2
, let us write

\displaystyle cI(f_{\infty})

\displaystyle=\int_{U(\mathbb{R})}\int_{\mathfrak{a}^{*}}\tilde{f_{\infty}}(-i\nu)\phi_{-i\nu}(tug)\frac{d\nu}{c(i\nu)c(-i\nu)}\overline{\psi}(u)du

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathfrak{a}^{*}}\tilde{f_{\infty}}(-i\nu)\int_{U(\mathbb{R})}\phi_{-i\nu}(tug)\overline{\psi}(u)du\frac{d\nu}{c(i\nu)c(-i\nu)}

Unlike (
28

f_{\infty}
is compactly supported,
and in the right hand side

has rapid decay.
We now give a rigorous proof of the following.

.

be a smooth, bi-

Both sides transform on the left by

\displaystyle duda

Estimates for the Whittaker function

We close this section with some estimates for the Whittaker function to be used later on.
We begin with recalling the following estimate for Bessel

Lemma 3.4.1
.

Let

Proof.

Follows trivially from the explicit integral representation (

Proposition 3.4.3
.

Let
a\in A^{+}(\mathbb{R})
.
Then, for
\Re(\nu)

\Re(\nu)\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{+}

).
Next, if
\Re(\nu)

\sigma\in\Omega

The functional equation (

Lemma 5.2.1
.

For each

and

\displaystyle\varphi:D_{\sigma}(\mathbb{A})

\displaystyle\to D_{\sigma}(\mathbb{A})

\displaystyle(u_{1},u_{2})

\displaystyle\mapsto(u_{1},\delta_{\sigma}^{-1}u_{1}^{-1}\delta_{\sigma}u_{2}).

induces a bijective map

\displaystyle H_{\delta_{\sigma}}(\mathbb{Q})\backslash D_{\sigma}(\mathbb{A})\to

\displaystyle\left(U_{\sigma}(\mathbb{Q})\times\{1\}\right)\backslash D_{\sigma}(\mathbb{A})

\displaystyle\cong\left(U_{\sigma}(\mathbb{Q})\backslash U_{\sigma}(\mathbb{A})\right)\times\left(\sigma^{-1}U_{\sigma}(\mathbb{A})\sigma\right)

preserving the quotient measures.

Proof.

To prove
\varphi
is well defined it is sufficient to prove that for any

we have

\varphi_{2}(u_{1},u_{2})=\delta_{\sigma}^{-1}u_{1}^{-1}\delta_{\sigma}u_{2}\in\sigma^{-1}U_{\sigma}(\mathbb{A})

This is equivalent to the condition
\sigma\varphi_{2}(u_{1},u_{2})\sigma^{-1}\in U_{\sigma}(\mathbb{A})
, which in turn is equivalent to

\begin{cases}\sigma\varphi_{2}(u_{1},u_{2})\sigma^{-1}\in U(\mathbb{A})\\
\varphi_{2}(u_{1},u_{2})\in U(\mathbb{A}).\end{cases}

But
\varphi_{2}(u_{1},u_{2})=\delta_{1}^{-1}\sigma^{-1}u_{1}^{-1}\sigma\delta_{1}u_{2}
, and since
u_{1}\in U_{\sigma}(\mathbb{A})
,
we have
\sigma^{-1}u_{1}^{-1}\sigma\in U(\mathbb{A})
and it follows
\varphi_{2}(u_{1},u_{2})\in U(\mathbb{A})
as desired.
On the other hand,

\displaystyle\sigma\varphi_{2}(u_{1},u_{2})\sigma^{-1}

\displaystyle=\sigma\delta_{1}^{-1}\sigma^{-1}u_{1}^{-1}\sigma\delta_{1}u_{2}\sigma^{-1}

\displaystyle=(\sigma\delta_{1}\sigma^{-1})^{-1}u_{1}^{-1}(\sigma\delta_{1}\sigma^{-1})\sigma u_{2}\sigma^{-1}

By definition of the Weyl group,

\sigma u_{2}\sigma^{-1}\in U_{\sigma}(\mathbb{A})\subset U(\mathbb{A})
and it also follows that
\sigma\varphi_{2}(u_{1},u_{2})\sigma^{-1}\in U(\mathbb{A}).

Next, for

, we clearly have
\varphi(h)=(h_{1},1)
, and

\displaystyle\varphi(h(u_{1},u_{2}))

\displaystyle=(h_{1}u_{1},\delta_{\sigma}^{-1}u_{1}^{-1}\underbrace{h_{1}^{-1}\delta_{\sigma}h_{2}}_{=\delta_{\sigma}}u_{2})

\displaystyle=\varphi(h)\varphi(u_{1},u_{2}).

Finally if we define

, then

is an involution, and in particular
\varphi
is bijective, which establishes the lemma.
∎

The identity contribution

Definition 5.4.1
.

Let

be integers such that

.
Then the following sum is well-defined

Lemma 5.4.2
.

Let
a,b,d,N
be integers such that
d\mid N
.
Write
a=\prod_{i}p_{i}^{a_{i}}
, where
p_{i}
are distinct primes and
a_{i}
are integers, and similarly for
b,d,N
.
Then we have

S(a,b,d,N)=\prod_{i}S(p_{i}^{a_{i}},p_{i}^{b_{i}},p_{i}^{d_{i}},p_{i}^{N_{i}}).

Moreover if
n
is a positive integer,
i,j,k
are non-negative integers with
k\leq n
and
p
is a prime, then we have

\displaystyle S(p^{i},p^{j},p^{k},p^{n})

\displaystyle=p^{2n-k}(1-p^{-1})\max(0,k+1-\max(0,n-i)-\max(0,n-j))

\displaystyle+p^{2n-k-1}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\geq n\\
j\geq n\end{subarray}}-\mathbbm{1}_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<n\\
j<n\\
i+j\geq 2n-k-1\end{subarray}}\right).

In particular, it follows that
S(p^{i},p^{j},p^{k},p^{n})
is non-zero only if

(46)

(n-i)+(n-j)\leq k+1.

Proof.

The factorization is immediate from the Chinese remainders theorem.
Now let us evaluate
S=S(p^{i},p^{j},p^{k},p^{n})
.
We have (here, abusing slightly notations, we set
v_{p}(0)=n
)

S=\sum_{h=0}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}\\
v_{p}(x)=h\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{p^{i}x}{p^{n}}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}\\
v_{p}(y)\geq k-h\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{p^{j}y}{p^{n}}\right)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}\\
v_{p}(x)\geq k+1\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{p^{i}x}{p^{n}}\right)\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}}e\left(\frac{p^{j}y}{p^{n}}\right).

Now if
\ell
is any non-negative integer, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}\\
v_{p}(y)\geq\ell\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{p^{j}y}{p^{n}}\right)=\begin{cases}p^{n-\ell}\text{ if }j+\ell\geq n\text{ and }\ell\leq n\\
0\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Hence

S=\sum_{h=0}^{k-\max(0,n-j)}p^{n-k+h}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}\\
v_{p}(x)=h\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{p^{i}x}{p^{n}}\right)+p^{2n-k-1}\mathbbm{1}_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geq n\\
i+k+1\geq n\\
k<n\end{subarray}}.

Now

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}\\
v_{p}(x)=h\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{p^{i}x}{p^{n}}\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}\\
v_{p}(x)\geq h\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{p^{i}x}{p^{n}}\right)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}\\
v_{p}(x)\geq h+1\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{p^{i}x}{p^{n}}\right),

hence the
h
-sum becomes

\left(\sum_{h=\max(0,n-i)}^{k-\max(0,n-j)}p^{2n-k}(1-p^{-1})\right)-p^{2n-k-1}\mathbbm{1}_{0\leq n-i-1\leq k-\max(0,n-j)}+p^{2n-k-1}\mathbbm{1}_{k-\max(0,n-j)=n},

so

\displaystyle S=p^{2n-k}(1-p^{-1})\max(0,k+1-\max(0,n-i)-\max(0,n-j))

\displaystyle+p^{2n-k-1}(\mathbbm{1}_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geq n\\
i+k+1\geq n\\
k<n\end{subarray}}-\mathbbm{1}_{0\leq n-i-1\leq k-\max(0,n-j)}+\mathbbm{1}_{k-\max(0,n-j)=n}).

Finally, it can be checked by inspection of cases that

\mathbbm{1}_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geq n\\
i+k+1\geq n\\
k<n\end{subarray}}-\mathbbm{1}_{0\leq n-i-1\leq k-\max(0,n-j)}+\mathbbm{1}_{k-\max(0,n-j)=n}=\mathbbm{1}_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\geq n\\
j\geq n\end{subarray}}-\mathbbm{1}_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<n\\
j<n\\
i+j\geq 2n-k-1\end{subarray}}.

∎
