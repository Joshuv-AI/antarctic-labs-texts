A Multivariate Complexity Analysis of Qualitative Reasoning Problems
Leif Eriksson, Victor Lagerkvist

Abstract
. Qualitative reasoning is an important subfield of artificial intelligence where one describes relationships with qualitative, rather than numerical, relations. Many such reasoning tasks, e.g., Allen’s interval algebra , can be solved in 2 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ⋅ log ⁡ n ) 2^{{\mathcal{O}}(n\cdot\log n)} time, but single-exponential running times 2 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ) 2^{{\mathcal{O}}(n)} are currently far out of reach. In this paper we consider single-exponential algorithms via a multivariate analysis consisting of a fine-grained parameter n n (e.g., the number of variables) and a coarse-grained parameter k k expected to be relatively small. We introduce the classes 𝐅𝐏𝐄 \mathbf{FPE} and 𝐗𝐄 \mathbf{X}\mathbf{E} of problems solvable in f ⁡ ( k ) ⋅ 2 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ) f(k)\cdot 2^{{\mathcal{O}}(n)} , respectively f ​ ( k ) n f(k)^{n} , time, and prove several fundamental properties of these classes. We proceed by studying temporal reasoning problems and (1) show that the partially ordered time problem of effective width k k is solvable in 16 k ​ n 16^{kn} time and is thus included in 𝐗𝐄 \mathbf{X}\mathbf{E} , and (2) that the network consistency problem for Allen’s interval algebra with no interval overlapping with more than k k others is solvable in ( 2 ​ n ​ k ) 2 ​ k ⋅ 2 n (2nk)^{2k}\cdot 2^{n} time and is included in 𝐅𝐏𝐄 \mathbf{FPE} . Our multivariate approach is in no way limited to these to specific problems and may be a generally useful approach for obtaining single-exponential algorithms.



1.
Introduction

Qualitative reasoning
is an important formalism in artificial intelligence where the objective is to reason about continuous properties given certain relations between the unknown entities, expressed qualitatively rather than quantitatively (e.g., numerically). Two important subfields are
spatial
reasoning, where basic objects e.g. may be regions in space defined in terms of other regions, and
temporal
reasoning, where one wishes to describe the relationship between time points, and more generally, between intervals of time points. Examples of the former include the
region connection calculus
and the
cardinal direction calculus
, and to exemplify the latter we may e.g. mention
Allen’s interval algebra
, the
branching time
problem, and the
partially ordered time
problem. For many more examples and practical applications of these formalisms, cf. the survey by Dylla et al.
[
13
]
.
The classical complexity of qualitative reasoning is well understood: they are generally NP-hard but form non-trivial tractable fragments by restricting the types of basic relations. However, the tractable cases have limited strength, and in practice we therefore also need to solve NP-hard reasoning tasks. Hence, is there any hope of solving these intractable problems, or do we have to be contempt with heuristics?
Here, modern complexity theory tells a more nuanced story than the classical theory, where NP-hardness is the beginning rather than the end. There are two prominent views:

(1)

Fine-grained complexity: for a
fine-grained
parameter
n
, e.g., the number of variables, how fast can the problem be solved, and are existing algorithms close to being optimal (given complexity theoretical assumptions such as
the (strong) exponential-time hypothesis
((S)ETH))?

(2)

Parameterized complexity: for a
coarse-grained
parameter
k
, e.g., tree-width, which problems are
fixed-parameter tractable
(
\mathbf{FPT}
) — are in P if one is allowed unlimited computational resources (with respect to
k
) to preprocess the instance?

Crucially,
n
is expected to grow with the size of the instance while
k
is expected to stay small. Hence, fine-grained and parameterized complexity are not competing methods but tackle different aspects of intractability, with diverging algorithmic toolboxes.
Unfortunately, neither approach seem fit for qualitative reasoning problems. On the one hand, they are solvable in
2^{{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2})}
time or
2^{{\mathcal{O}}(n\cdot\log n)}
time in certain cases
[
20
]
, but we can currently only rule out subexponential
2^{o(n)}
algorithms under ETH
[
23
]
. On the other hand, despite the immense success of parameterized complexity, there is a lack of natural
\mathbf{FPT}
algorithms for qualitative reasoning, and we are only aware of a handful of less surprising examples such as tree-width
[
6
,
10
]
.

Hence, are we asking the right questions by attacking these problems with parameterized and fine-grained complexity, or are current attempts misguided?
Could it be the case that they simply are too hard to admit natural
\mathbf{FPT}
algorithms? Similarly, could it be the case that (e.g.)
Allen’s interval algebra
is not solvable in single-exponential time while still being fundamentally too different from 3-SAT to admit stronger lower bounds than
2^{o(n)}
? In this article we bridge the gap between fine-grained and parameterized complexity and attack this question by a
multivariate
complexity analysis of single-exponential time solvability: which NP-hard qualitative reasoning problems admit single-exponential time algorithms (with respect to a fine-grained parameter
n
) once preprocessed with respect to a coarse-grained parameter
k
? This approach is a natural continuation of Bringmann & Künnemann
[
7
]
who studied the complexity of the
longest common subsequence
problem in order to investigate which natural parameters could possibly break the SETH barrier. However, the exponential world of NP-hard qualitative reasoning problems is very different from the tractable
longest common subsequence
problem and requires new tools and techniques.
Our paper has two main contributions. First, in Section
3
we initiate a systematic investigation of complexity classes taking both a coarse-grained parameter
k
and a fine-grained parameter
n
into consideration. We identify two natural classes:
\mathbf{FPE}
, problems solvable in
f(k)\cdot 2^{{\mathcal{O}}(n)}
time, and
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}
, problems solvable in
f(k)^{n}
time. Naturally,
\mathbf{FPE}
is more desirable due to the complete separation of the parameter
n
and
k
and should be viewed as the exponential analogue of
\mathbf{FPT}
, while
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}
corresponds to the less desirable class
\mathbf{XP}
where the two parameters are intertwined.
Second, in Section
4
we begin the multivariate analysis of single-exponential time solvability by classifying parameterized problems as belonging to
\mathbf{FPE}
,
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}
, or neither (under the ETH). We first consider the finite-domain
constraint satisfaction problem
(CSP) and manage to rule out inclusion in both
\mathbf{FPE}
and
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}
under the ETH for several natural parameterizations. We then turn to two well-known problems from temporal reasoning: the
partially ordered time
problem
[
4
]
, which is the problem of determining whether there exists a partial order subjected to a set of constraints over a variable set, and
Allen’s interval algebra

[
1
]
, the problem of determining whether a set of 2-dimensional intervals described by 1-dimensional temporal constraints over the start- and end-points, is consistent or not.
Allen’s interval algebra
has seen applications in e.g. planning
[
2
,
12
,
26
]
, natural language processing
[
11
,
28
]
, and molecular biology
[
18
]
, while the
partially ordered time
problem occurs naturally in distributed systems where time is not totally ordered, cf. Lamport’s classical interprocessor communication model
[
24
,
3
]
. Both of these problems can be solved in
2^{{\mathcal{O}}(n\cdot\log n)}
time by enumeration, and
Allen’s interval algebra
is additionally known to admit an improved
{\mathcal{O}}(1.0615n^{n})
algorithm
[
14
]
. For
partially ordered time
we consider
effective width
as parameter, which in strength lies between the well-known properties of
width
and
dimension
. Here, we construct an
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}
-algorithm with a running time dominated by
16^{kn}
where
k
is the effective width of the partial order. The algorithm is non-trivial and attempts to construct a partial order of effective width
k
by guessing a suitable partition of the variable set and the ordering among all variables, which can be efficiently implemented via a recursive strategy making use of dynamic programming. For
Allen’s interval algebra
we define a parameter
k
based on the maximum number of overlapping intervals and construct a
(2nk)^{2k}\cdot 2^{n}
time algorithm, and thus prove membership in
\mathbf{FPE}
. Hence, for instances where
k
stays reasonably small our algorithm is effectively as good as a
2^{n}
-time algorithm, which is a major improvement over the aforementioned
{\mathcal{O}}(1.0615n^{n})
time algorithm. It is worth mentioning that both of these algorithms also solve the more involved problem of
counting
the number of solutions.

Our work opens up several directions for future research, which we describe in greater detail in Section
5
. Most importantly, which parameterized problems in qualitative reasoning, and artificial intelligence as a whole, belong to
\mathbf{FPE}
, and which belong to
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}
?

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout, let
\Sigma
be an alphabet, i.e., a finite set of symbols. For
x\in\Sigma^{*}
we let
|x|
be its length.

Definition 1
.

A parameterized problem is a subset of
\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
where
k\in\mathbb{N}
is called the parameter.

The goal of parameterized complexity is then to describe the complexity in terms of the parameter, and, ideally, design algorithms which decouples the parameter to
|x|
.

Definition 2
.

We introduce the following running times and the corresponding classes from parameterized complexity:

(1)

f(k)\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
time by a deterministic algorithm (
\mathbf{FPT}
),

(2)

|x|^{f(k)}
time by a deterministic algorithm (
\mathbf{XP}
),

where
f\colon\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
is a computable function.

\mathbf{FPT}
can be seen as the parameterized version of P where the parameter is completely decoupled from the rest of the instance via the factor
f(k)
. The class
\mathbf{XP}
, in comparison, still yields a polynomial-time algorithm for every fixed
k
but the dependency on
k
is much worse.

If a
fine-grained
parameter such as the number of variable
n
is used as parameter then the objective is typically to obtain precise upper and lower bounds on running times of the form
f(n)\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
, and the resulting field is sometimes called
fine-grained complexity
. If we let
\mathbf{SE}
be the subclass of
\mathbf{FPT}
allowing subexponential running times of the form
2^{o(n)}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
then the conjecture that 3-SAT is not included in
\mathbf{SE}
is known as the
exponential-time hypothesis
(ETH).
The ETH is widely believed conjecture and is a powerful assumption for proving conditional lower bounds. However, sometimes stronger assumptions are necessary. For
k\geq 3
let
c_{k}
be the infimum of all constants
c
such that
k
-
SAT
is solvable in
c^{n}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
time. The conjecture that the limit of the sequence
c_{3},c_{4},\ldots
tends to
2
is known as the
strong exponential-time hypothesis
(SETH). Importantly, the SETH implies that
CNF-SAT
, the satisfiability problem where we do not have any restrictions on clause lengths, is not solvable in
c^{n}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
time for
any

c<2
.

Before introducing the qualitative reasoning problems under consideration in this paper we introduce the more general class of

(CSPs).

3.
Multivariate Complexity Classes

Our main interest are problems solvable in
2^{f(n)}
time for
f\in{\mathcal{O}}(n)
(henceforth written
2^{{\mathcal{O}}(n)}
). This naturally leads to the following parameterized variant of the classical complexity class
\mathbf{E}
, which is typically defined via the parameter
|x|
.

Definition 4
.

\mathbf{E}
is the class of parameterized problems solvable in
2^{{\mathcal{O}}(n)}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
time.

The choice of parameter
n
greatly influences the existence of a
2^{{\mathcal{O}}(n)}
algorithm. For example, if we use the number of constraints
m
as the parameter then CSP
(\mathcal{A})
is in
\mathbf{E}
via a trivial backtracking algorithm, but it is only known to be solvable in
2^{{\mathcal{O}}(n\cdot\log n)}
time. Similarly, a
c^{n}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
algorithm for
c<2
for
CNF-SAT
would constitute a major break through in complexity theory, but if we instead use the number of clauses
m
as the complexity parameter then the problem can be solved in
{\mathcal{O}}(1.2226^{m})
time
[
9
]
.

Example 5
.

The number of problems admitting non-trivial single-exponential time algorithms is vast and we only consider a handful of illustrative examples.

•

The

time by the Held-Karp algorithm. Similarly,

can be solved in
k^{n}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
time, where

n^{n}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}

time via an inclusion-exclusion based algorithm
[
5
]
.

•

For each

k

be the variant of

-coloring

Importantly, no NP-hard qualitative reasoning tasks are known to be solvable in single-exponential time, necessitating a multivariate analysis where the complexity is measured with respect to several parameters.

3.1.
Multi-Parameterized Problems

We begin by defining a multi-parameterized problem following the style frequently used in classical parameterized complexity.

Definition 6
.

A multi-parameterized problem is a subset of
\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}^{c}
where
n,k_{1},\dots,k_{c-1}\in\mathbb{N}
are called the parameters.
A bi-parameterized problem is a multi-parameterized problem with only two parameters:
n
and
k
.

For simplicity we, given an instance
(x,n,k)
of a bi-parameterized problem, will always view the first parameter
n
as the fine-grained parameter, and the second parameter
k
as the coarse-grained parameter.
Inspired by
\mathbf{FPT}
and
\mathbf{XP}
we then define the following bi-parameterized analogues.

Definition 7
.

We introduce the following two classes.

(1)

\mathbf{FPE}
: problems solvable in
f(k)\cdot 2^{{\mathcal{O}}(n)}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
time, and

(2)

\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}
: problems solvable in
f(k)^{n}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
time,

where
f\colon\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
is a computable function.

Note that these running times are not symmetric with respect to the two parameters since we expect the parameter
k
to stay reasonably small, while
n
, naturally, grows rapidly with the size of the instance. Hence, the common trick in parameterized complexity to collapse two parameters into a single parameter
n+k
is not suitable for defining these classes.

Example 8
.

Let us first consider finite-domain CSPs. Clearly, if the coarse-grained parameter is the size of the domain, and the fine-grained parameter is the number of variables, then the resulting problem is solvable in
k^{n}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
time and is thus in
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}
. However, this problem is
not in
\mathbf{FPE}
unless the ETH fails (see Theorem
20
). For a less trivial
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}
example, consider the problem of finding a homomorphism between two graphs
G
and
H
over vertices
V_{G}
and
V_{H}
. If we let
n
be
|V_{G}|
, and
k
be the tree-width of
H
, then the problem is solvable in
(k+3)^{n}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
time, and if
n=\max\{V_{G},V_{H}\}
and
k
is the clique-width of
H
then it is solvable in
(2k+1)^{n}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
time
[
17
,
30
]
. Hence, both these parameterisations yield problems in
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}
.
Faster algorithms are known for certain types of graphs

: if
H
is the complete clique on
k

H
is tantamount to finding a
k
-coloring of

, which is well-known to be solvable in

[

]
(see Example

\mathbf{FPE}
, but is

\mathbf{E}
even if

Interestingly, if we let the fine-grained parameter

be the tree-width of

k^{n+1}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}

{\mathcal{O}}(((k-\varepsilon)^{n+1})\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)})
for any
\varepsilon>0
under the SETH
[
27
]
, and in particular it is not included in
\mathbf{FPE}
.

From Example

channel assignment problem
which is in
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}
when parameterized by the edge-length

of the input graph
[
25
]
.

Curiously, while
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}
-algorithms appear to be abundant in the literature,
\mathbf{FPE}
-algorithms are much rarer and we are not aware of any non-trivial examples, i.e., problems included in

but not in any smaller class such as
\mathbf{E}
.
However, before we turn to studying concrete problems in these classes we begin by establishing several fundamental properties.
In Section

we derive
\mathbf{FPE}
as a class of the form
\text{para-}\mathbf{E}
using standard constructions in parameterised complexity and prove that
\mathbf{FPE}
can equivalently be defined via running times of the form
n^{f(k)}\cdot 2^{{\mathcal{O}}(n)}
. In Section
3.3
we investigate the relationship between
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{E}
in greater detail and prove that the former can be defined via piecewise slices of the latter. Last, in Section

we consider reductions compatible with bi-parameterized problems, with a particular focus on obtaining relevant reductions for problems in
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}
.

3.2.
\mathbf{FPE}
:
f(k)\cdot 2^{{\mathcal{O}}(n)}
time

In this section we introduce the more desirable class of problems solvable in
f(k)\cdot 2^{{\mathcal{O}}(n)}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
time, and will see that the resulting class can be obtained via natural constructions in parameterized complexity.

Definition 10
.

We define the following concepts/classes.

(1)

A bi-parameterized problem
P\subseteq\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
is in
\mathbf{E}
after a pre-computation of the parameter
k
if there exists an alphabet
\Delta
and a computable function
f\colon\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\Delta^{*}
and a problem
Q\subseteq\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}\times\Delta^{*},Q\in\mathbf{E}
where

(x,n,k)\in P\Leftrightarrow(x,n,f(k))\in Q.

(2)

\text{para-}\mathbf{E}
is the class of bi-parameterized problems which are in
\mathbf{E}
after a precomputation of the parameter.

As we will now prove,
\text{para-}\mathbf{E}
is the natural exponential counter part to
\mathbf{FPT}
, and due to this correspondence we typically write
\mathbf{FPE}
rather than
\text{para-}\mathbf{E}
.

Lemma 11
.

A bi-parameterized problem
P\subseteq\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
with parameters
n
and
k
is in
\text{para-}\mathbf{E}
(
\mathbf{FPE}
) after a pre-computation of the parameter
k
if and only if it is solvable in
f(k)\cdot 2^{{\mathcal{O}}(n)}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
time for computable
f\colon\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the
\text{para-}C
construction in Flum & Grohe when
C
is a classical complexity class
[
15
]
, but we provide a complete proof to ensure that we get the necessary time bound.
Assume that
P
is solvable in
f(k)\cdot 2^{cn+{\mathcal{O}}(1)}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
time for computable
f\colon\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
and
c\geq 1
, and let
A
denote this algorithm. Thus, the algorithm
A
takes an instance
x\in\Sigma^{*}
together with two parameters
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
as input. We will prove that
P
is in
\text{para-}\mathbf{E}
after a pre-computation of the parameter
k
. Let
\Delta=\{1,:\}
where
:
is a symbol not occurring in
\Sigma
. Define the function
\pi(k)=k:f(k)
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, where
k
and
f(k)
are written in unary. This function is clearly computable since
f
is computable. Consider the language
Q\subseteq\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}\times\Delta^{*}
accepted by the following algorithm
B
:

(1)

Let
(x,n,y)\in\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}\times\Delta^{*}
be an instance.

(2)

Check if the string
y
is of the form
k:u
for some
{u\in\{1\}^{*}}
.

(3)

If not, answer no, otherwise, continue.

(4)

Simulate the algorithm
A
with
(x,n,k)
as input for at most
|u|\cdot 2^{cn+{\mathcal{O}}(1)}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
steps. If
A
answers yes within this time, answer yes, otherwise answer no.

We begin by analysing the running time of the algorithm

. Since
u
is part of the input, the simulation of the algorithm
A
in step (4) can be accomplished with a polynomial overhead, and step (2) takes only linear time with respect to
y
. For correctness, assume that
(x,n,k)
is a yes-instance of
P
. We claim that
(x,n,\pi(k))
is a yes-instance of
Q
. In this case the algorithm will reach step (4) and begin the simulation of
A
for
|u|\cdot 2^{cn+{\mathcal{O}}(1)}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
steps.
Assume that
(x,n,\pi(k))\in Q
. It follows that
A
answers yes after at most
f(k)\cdot 2^{cn+{\mathcal{O}}(1)}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
steps, and
(x,n,k)
must therefore be a yes-instance of
P
.

For the other direction, assume that
P
is in
\text{para-}\mathbf{E}
after a pre-computation of the parameter
k
, and let
{Q\in\mathbf{E}\subseteq\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}\times\Delta^{*}}
and the function
\pi\colon\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\Delta^{*}
witness this. Furthermore, let
A
be an algorithm running in time
2^{{\mathcal{O}}(n)}\cdot(|x|+|y|)^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
for input
(x,n,y)\in\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}\times\Delta^{*}
. Now, consider the following algorithm
B
for
P
:

(1)

Let
(x,n,k)\in\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
be an instance of
P
.

(2)

Call the algorithm
A
with input
(x,n,\pi(k))
.

(3)

Answer yes if
A
answers yes, and otherwise no.

This takes
\pi(k)+2^{{\mathcal{O}}(n)}\cdot(|x|+|\pi(k)|)^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}=\pi(k)+2^{{\mathcal{O}}(n)}\cdot(|x|)^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}\cdot(|\pi(k)|)^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
time which can be expressed as
f(k)\cdot 2^{{\mathcal{O}}(n)}\cdot(|x|)^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
for some computable
f\colon\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
(which depends on
\pi
).
∎

We remark that running times of the form
{f(k)\cdot 2^{{\mathcal{O}}(n)}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}}
easily allows us to hide factors of the form

for computable

. Consider a running time of the form
{n^{f(k)}\cdot 2^{cn+{\mathcal{O}}(1)}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}}
for a computable function
f\colon\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
. We claim that it is always bounded by
g(k)\cdot 2^{cn+{\mathcal{O}}(1)}\cdot 2^{n}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
where
g(k)=f(k)^{{f(k)}^{f(k)}}
. If
2^{n}\leq n^{f(k)}
then
f(k)\geq\frac{n}{\log n}
and
f(k)^{f(k)}\geq n
, and it follows that
g(k)\geq n^{f(k)}
.

Theorem 12
.

A bi-parameterized problem is in
\text{para-}\mathbf{E}
if and only if it is solvable in
n^{f(k)}\cdot 2^{{\mathcal{O}}(n)}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
time for computable
f\colon\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
.

Note that if we had instead attempted to define
\mathbf{FPE}
as the single-parameterized problems in
\mathbf{E}
with respect to a parameter
n+k
in
\mathbf{E}
then the factor
n^{f(k)}
, necessary for the CSP
(\mathcal{A})
algorithm in the main paper, would be prohibited. A similar attempt of defining
\mathbf{FPE}
as the single-parameterized problems solvable in
f(k)\cdot|x|^{k}\cdot 2^{{\mathcal{O}}(|x|)}
time would also be unsuitable since
|x|
is not a meaningful parameter for single-exponential time algorithms.

3.3.
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}
:
f(k)^{n}
time

In this section we investigate bi-parameterized problems solvable in
f(k)^{n}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
time for computable
f\colon\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
.

Definition 13
.

Let
P\subseteq\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
be a bi-parameterized problem.
For
k\geq 1
the
k
th slice of

is the parameterized problem

P_{k}=\{(x,n)\mid(x,n,k)\in P\}\subseteq\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
.

Definition 14
.

We define the following complexity classes.

(1)

\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{NU}}
is the set of all bi-parameterized problems
P
such that
P_{k}\in\mathbf{E}
for each
k\geq 1
.

(2)

\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{U}}
is the set all bi-parameterized problems where (1) each slice
P_{k}\in\mathbf{E}
can be solved by an algorithm
A_{k}
, and (2) there exists a computable function
{g\colon\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\{(A_{1},c_{1}),(A_{2},c_{2})\ldots\}}
which associates each
k
with the algorithm
A_{k}
for
P_{k}
running in
2^{c_{k}n+{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
time.

Since we in this paper do not care about undecidable problems we henceforth only consider the uniform class
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{U}}
and write
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}
instead of
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{U}}
.

Lemma 15
.

A bi-parameterized problem
P
is in
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}
if and only if it is solvable in
f(k)^{n}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
time for some computable function
f\colon\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

For the first direction, let
(x,n,k)
be an instance of the problem. We begin by determining the algorithm
A_{k}
and the function
f_{k}
which takes
g(k)
time for a fixed, computable function

. Next, we run the algorithm
A_{k}
with the input
(x,n)
for
2^{c_{k}n+{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
steps, and answer yes if and only if it accepts. Hence, we obtain a running time
g(k)+2^{c_{k}n+{\mathcal{O}}(1)}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
time, which can be expressed as
f(k)^{n}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
for a computable
f
which depends on
g
and
c_{k}
.
For the other direction we simply observe that a running time of the form
f(k)^{n}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
clearly implies that each slice

since
k
is fixed.
∎

3.4.
Reductions for Bi-Parameterized Problems

We proceed by describing reductions compatible with problems in
\mathbf{FPE}
. The complicating factor is that we have two parameters to take into consideration: the parameter
k
which may increase substantially as long as it is bounded by a computable function in
k
, and the parameter

which is only allowed to increase linearly — in order to preserve running times of the form

.

Definition 16
.

Let
P\subseteq\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
and

be two bi-parameterized problem. An

-reduction with respect to

such that

(1)

(x,n,k)\in P
if and only if
R((x,n,k))\in Q
.

(2)

There exists
c\in\mathbb{N}
such that
n^{\prime}=c\cdot n+{\mathcal{O}}(1)
for all instances
(x,n,k)
and
R((x,n,k))=(x^{\prime},n^{\prime},k^{\prime})
of
P
and
Q
.

(3)

There exists a computable function

such that
R((x,n,k))
is computable in
f(k)\cdot 2^{{\mathcal{O}}(n)}\cdot|x|^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
time for any instance
(x,n,k)
of
P
.

(4)

There exists a computable function
g\colon\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
such that

for all instances
(x,n,k)
and
{R((x,n,k))=(x^{\prime},n^{\prime},k^{\prime})}
of
P
and

.

We write

if

is
\mathbf{FPE}
-reducible to

and

are two single-parameterized problems then we say that

is
\mathbf{E}
-reducible

-reduction is a single-exponential time reduction which may increase the number of variables by a constant factor.

Theorem 17
.

The following statements hold.

(1)

\mathbf{E}
is closed under
\mathbf{E}
-reductions.

(2)

\mathbf{FPE}
is closed under
\mathbf{FPE}
-reductions.

Proof.

Since the first statement can be seen as a special case of the second statement we only prove the former. Hence, assume that
P\in\mathbf{FPE}
and that
Q
is a bi-parameterized problem such that

. We will prove that
Q\in\mathbf{FPE}
. Since
{P\in\mathbf{FPE}}
there exists computable

and constants

and

such that

f_{P}(k_{P})\cdot 2^{c_{P}n_{P}+{\mathcal{O}}(1)}\cdot|x|^{d_{P}}
time for any instance
(x,n_{P},k_{P})
of
P
. Let
R
be the
\mathbf{FPE}
-reduction from
Q
to
P
, with respect to (1) a constant

which linearly bounds the parameter

and (2) a computable function

and constants

such that

We refrain from formally introducing

Naturally, it is straightforward to modify the algorithm to actually return a solution, e.g., via back-pointers, or any other standard methods to iteratively build a solution.

5.
Concluding Remarks

We explored how a multivariate complexity analysis can generate interesting cases with significant complexity improvements compared to the classical case of single parameters. This led to the introduction of the classes
\mathbf{FPE}
and
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}
which turned out to be the natural exponential-time analogoues of the well-known classes
\mathbf{FPT}
and
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{P}
. We proved several fundamental properties of these classes and gave examples of
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{FPE}
from qualitative reasoning.
These algorithms are significantly faster than existing methods (provided the coarse-grained parameters stay relatively small) and constitute an important breakthrough for single-exponential time algorithms in qualitative reasoning.

Systematically classifying
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{FPE}
.

The overarching open question is to classify problems and parameters in terms of
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}
or
\mathbf{FPE}
membership (or neither, under assumptions such as the ETH). Here, qualitative spatial reasoning problems (such as the
region connection calculus
) seem to be a promising continuation since the NP-hard cases in general are not known to be solvable in single-exponential time. More generally, is it possible to find canonical parameters which result in
\mathbf{FPE}
algorithms, similarly to how parameters such as tree-width almost always results in
\mathbf{FPT}
algorithms? Identifying such parameters could open up entirely new algorithmic approaches for qualitative reasoning, in particular, and infinite-domain CSPs, in general.

Limited versus unlimited equality.

The algorithm in Section
4.3
for
k
-CSP(
\mathcal{A}
) has similarities to the one in
[
25
]
for the
channel assignment problem
with bounded edge-length, making the two problems interesting to compare.
One interpretation is that
k
-CSP(
\mathcal{A}
) belongs to
\mathbf{FPE}
because the problem bounds the number of intervals that may be equal (since they overlap), which is not true for
channel assignment
, which seemingly prevents our approach from proving
\mathbf{FPE}
membership.
However, if one limits the size of equivalence classes in the same sense in the
channel assignment problem
, this new problem would naturally fall into
\mathbf{FPE}
, using just a slight modification of the original algorithm. Is this a problem specific behaviour, or is it a deeper difference between
\mathbf{FPE}
and
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}
?

Extending and improving the algorithms.

The algorithm from Section
4.2
proves membership in
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}
but can be asymptotically improved.
First, the algorithm ignores any potential symmetry between different inputs, which could be taken into account and improve the complexity by a factor of
k!
. This is in line with
\mathbf{FPT}
algorithms of the form
f(k)\cdot|x|^{O(1)}
where the function
f
in a first attempt may be a tower of exponential, but which through clever algorithmic techniques in some cases can be improved to a single-exponential function
2^{ck}
for reasonably small
c>0

[
16
]
.
The algorithm in Section
4.2
and Section
4.3
can likely be improved to handle higher-arity constraints, although it would make the presentation and the analysis significantly more complex.
To handle e.g. arity three constraints the number of sets to keep track of would almost double, as we would, for
4.2
, also need to know how variables
outside
of the temporary waists (those that in the algorithm are represented by
T_{1}^{<},\dots,T_{k}^{<}
and
T_{1}^{>},\dots,T_{k}^{>}
)
relate to one of said waists.
Another interesting direction is to consider different parameters: the algorithm from Section
4.2
can very likely be modify to use the width of the partial order as the coarse-grained parameter instead of effective width, but it is less clear whether the dimension of a partial order is sufficient for an
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}
-algorithm.
