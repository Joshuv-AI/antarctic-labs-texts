Abstract
. We study the Bloch and the little Bloch spaces of harmonic functions on the real hyperbolic ball. We show that the Bergman projections from L ∞ ​ ( 𝔹 ) L^{\infty}(\mathbb{B}) to ℬ \mathcal{B} , and from C 0 ​ ( 𝔹 ) C_{0}(\mathbb{B}) to ℬ 0 \mathcal{B}_{0} are onto. We verify that the dual space of the hyperbolic harmonic Bergman space ℬ α 1 \mathcal{B}^{1}_{\alpha} is ℬ \mathcal{B} and its predual is ℬ 0 \mathcal{B}_{0} . Finally, we obtain an atomic decomposition of Bloch functions as a series of Bergman reproducing kernels.

1.
Introduction

For
n\geq 2
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let

\langle x,y\rangle=x_{1}y_{1}+\dots+x_{n}y_{n}
be the Euclidean inner
product and
\lvert x\rvert=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle}
the
corresponding norm.
Let
\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{B}_{n}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\lvert x\rvert<1\}

be the unit ball and
\mathbb{S}=\partial\mathbb{B}
the unit sphere.

The hyperbolic ball is
\mathbb{B}
equipped with the hyperbolic metric

ds^{2}=\frac{4}{(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}dx_{i}^{2}.

For a
C^{2}
function
f
, the hyperbolic (invariant) Laplacian

\Delta_{h}
is defined by

\Delta_{h}f(a)=\Delta(f\circ\varphi_{a})(0)\qquad(a\in\mathbb{B}),

where
\Delta=\partial^{2}/\partial x_{1}^{2}+\dots+\partial^{2}/\partial x_{n}^{2}

is the Euclidean Laplacian and
\varphi_{a}
is the involutory Möbius
transformation given in (
7
) that exchanges
a
and
0
.
Up to a factor
1/4
,
\Delta_{h}
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator
associated with the hyperbolic metric.
A straightforward calculation shows

\Delta_{h}f(a)=(1-\lvert a\rvert^{2})^{2}\Delta f(a)+2(n-2)(1-\lvert a\rvert^{2})\langle a,\nabla f(a)\rangle,

where

\nabla=\big(\partial/\partial x_{1},\dots,\partial/\partial x_{n}\big)

is the Euclidean gradient.
We refer the reader to
[
16
, Chapter 3]
for details.

A
C^{2}
function
f\colon\mathbb{B}\to\mathbb{C}
is called
hyperbolic harmonic or
\mathcal{H}
-harmonic on
\mathbb{B}

if
\Delta_{h}f(x)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{B}
.
We denote by
\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{B})
the space of all

\mathcal{H}
-harmonic functions equipped with the topology of
uniform convergence on compact subsets.

Let
\nu
be the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{B}
normalized so that

\nu(\mathbb{B})=1
and for
\alpha>-1
, let

d\nu_{\alpha}(x)=(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{\alpha}d\nu(x)
.
For
0<p<\infty
, denote the Lebesgue classes with respect to

d\nu_{\alpha}
by
L^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B})
.
The
\mathcal{H}
-harmonic weighted Bergman space

\mathcal{B}^{p}_{\alpha}
is the subspace

L^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B})\cap\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{B})
.
When
p=2
,
\mathcal{B}^{2}_{\alpha}
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space
and for each
x\in\mathbb{B}
, there exists

\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}(x,\cdot)\in\mathcal{B}^{2}_{\alpha}
such that

(1)

f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{B}}f(y)\overline{\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}(x,y)}\,d\nu_{\alpha}(y)\qquad(f\in\mathcal{B}^{2}_{\alpha}).

The reproducing kernel
\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}
is real-valued and the
conjugation above can be deleted.

\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}(x,y)=\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}(y,x)
and so
\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}

is
\mathcal{H}
-harmonic as a function of each variable.
We refer the reader to
[
15
]
and
[
16
, Chapter 10]
for details.

For
\alpha>-1
and
\phi\in L^{1}_{\alpha}
, the Bergman projection
operator
P_{\alpha}
is defined by

P_{\alpha}\phi(x)=\int_{\mathbb{B}}\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}(x,y)\phi(y)\,d\nu_{\alpha}(y).

In
[
19
]
, estimates for the reproducing kernels have been obtained,
and it is shown that
P_{\gamma}:L^{p}_{\alpha}\to\mathcal{B}^{p}_{\alpha}
is
bounded if and only
\alpha+1<p(\gamma+1)
.
Further properties of the spaces
\mathcal{B}^{p}_{\alpha}
including their
atomic decomposition have been obtained in
[
20
]
.

The purpose of this paper is to consider the
p=\infty
, i.e., the
Bloch space, case.
For a
C^{1}
function
f
, the hyperbolic gradient
\nabla^{h}
is defined by

\nabla^{h}f(a)=-\nabla(f\circ\varphi_{a})(0)=(1-\lvert a\rvert^{2})\nabla f(a).

The
\mathcal{H}
-harmonic Bloch space
\mathcal{B}
consists of all

f\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{B})
such that

(2)

p_{\mathcal{B}}(f)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{B}}\lvert\nabla^{h}f(x)\rvert=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{B}}(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})\lvert\nabla f(x)\rvert<\infty.

p_{\mathcal{B}}
is a seminorm and

\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}=\lvert f(0)\rvert+p_{\mathcal{B}}(f)
is a norm
on
\mathcal{B}
.
The little Bloch space
\mathcal{B}_{0}
is the subspace
consisting of functions
f
with

\lim_{\lvert x\rvert\to 1^{-}}\lvert(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})\lvert\nabla f(x)\rvert=0
.

The properties we state below for the
\mathcal{H}
-harmonic Bloch space

\mathcal{B}
are similar to the holomorphic or the harmonic case.
However, we would like to point out that there are differences between
these and the
\mathcal{H}
-harmonic case.
For example, it is well known that polynomials are dense in the holomorphic
little Bloch space and similarly harmonic polynomials are dense
in the harmonic little Bloch space.
However, this is not true in the
\mathcal{H}
-harmonic case.
In fact, when the dimension
n
is odd, there are not any non-constant

\mathcal{H}
-harmonic polynomials (see Lemma
4.1
).
Besides, some elementary properties of harmonic (or holomorphic)
functions do not hold for
\mathcal{H}
-harmonic functions.
For example, if
f
is harmonic, then the partial derivative

\partial f/\partial x_{i}
and the dilation
f_{r}(x)=f(rx)

are also harmonic.
However, neither of these are true for
\mathcal{H}
-harmonic functions.
Therefore, even if the final results are similar, some of the proofs
in the harmonic (or holomorphic) case do not directly carry over to
the
\mathcal{H}
-harmonic case.

Our first result is about projections onto
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{B}_{0}
.
Let
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{B})
be the Lebesgue space of essentially bounded
functions,
C(\overline{\mathbb{B}})
be the space of functions continuous
on
\overline{\mathbb{B}}
, and
C_{0}(\mathbb{B})
be its subspace consisting
of functions vanishing on
\partial\mathbb{B}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For every
\alpha>-1
,
P_{\alpha}
maps
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{B})
boundedly
onto
\mathcal{B}
.
It also maps
C(\overline{\mathbb{B}})
and
C_{0}(\mathbb{B})
boundedly
onto
\mathcal{B}_{0}
.

It has already been verified in
[
19
, Theorem 1.5]
that

P_{\alpha}\colon L^{\infty}(\mathbb{B})\to\mathcal{B}
is bounded and the main
aspect of the above theorem is the surjectivity.
To achieve this we first characterize
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{B}_{0}
in
terms of certain fractional differential operators that are defined in
Section
3
.
These operators are compatible with
\mathcal{H}
-harmonic functions and
the reproducing kernels, and to understand the properties of
\mathcal{B}
,
they are more adequate than
\nabla^{h}
or
\nabla
used
in (
2
).

We next consider the duality problem.
For
1<p<\infty
, the dual of the hyperbolic Bergman space

\mathcal{B}^{p}_{\alpha}
can be identified with
\mathcal{B}^{p^{\prime}}_{\alpha}
,
where
p^{\prime}=p/(p-1)
is the conjugate exponent of
p

(see
[
19
, Corollary 1.4]
).
We complete the missing
p=1
case.

Theorem 1.2
.

For
\alpha>-1
, the dual of
\mathcal{B}^{1}_{\alpha}
can be
identified with
\mathcal{B}
under the pairing

(3)

\langle f,g\rangle_{\alpha}=\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}\int_{r\mathbb{B}}f(x)g(x)\,d\nu_{\alpha}(x).

More precisely, to each
\Lambda\in(\mathcal{B}^{1}_{\alpha})^{*}
,
there corresponds a unique
g\in\mathcal{B}
with

\|g\|_{\mathcal{B}}
equivalent to
\|\Lambda\|
such that

\Lambda(f)=\langle f,g\rangle_{\alpha}
.
Similarly, for every
\alpha>-1
the dual of
\mathcal{B}_{0}

can be identified with
\mathcal{B}^{1}_{\alpha}
under the
pairing (
3
).

For an unbounded
g\in\mathcal{B}
and
f\in\mathcal{B}^{1}_{\alpha}
, the
integral
\int_{\mathbb{B}}f(x)g(x)d\nu_{\alpha}(x)
may not absolutely
converge; however the limit in (
3
) always exists.
In the case of the
holomorphic
Bloch space on the unit
ball of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
g(z)=\log 1/(1-z_{1})
is an unbounded
Bloch function.
On the other hand, it is not obvious whether there exists an unbounded

\mathcal{H}
-harmonic Bloch function.
We give an example in Lemma
6.2
.

As our final result we prove atomic decomposition of

\mathcal{H}
-harmonic Bloch functions.
Atomic decomposition of
harmonic
Bergman and Bloch functions
have been obtained in
[
4
]
(see also
[
3
]
).
In the
\mathcal{H}
-harmonic case, see
[
8
]
for atomic
decomposition of Hardy spaces and
[
20
]
for Bergman spaces.

For
a,b\in\mathbb{B}
, the pseudo-hyperbolic metric is

\rho(a,b)=\lvert\varphi_{a}(b)\rvert
.
For
0<r<1
, let
E_{r}(a)=\{\,x\in\mathbb{B}:\rho(x,a)<r\,\}
be the
pseudo-hyperbolic ball with center
a
and radius
r
.
A sequence
\{a_{m}\}
of points of
\mathbb{B}
is called
r
-separated
if
\rho(a_{m},a_{k})\geq r
for
m\neq k
.
An
r
-separated sequence is called an
r
-lattice if

\bigcup_{m=1}^{\infty}E_{r}(a_{m})=\mathbb{B}
.
Denote by
\ell^{\infty}
, the space of bounded sequences with norm

\|\{\lambda_{m}\}\|_{\ell^{\infty}}=\sup_{m\geq 1}\lvert\lambda_{m}\rvert
,
and by
c_{0}
the subspace consisting of sequences that converge to
0
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\alpha>-1
.
There is an
r_{0}<1/2
depending only on
n
and
\alpha
such that
if
\{a_{m}\}
is an
r
-lattice with
r<r_{0}
, then for every

f\in\mathcal{B}
(resp.
\mathcal{B}_{0}
), there exits

\{\lambda_{m}\}\in\ell^{\infty}
(resp.
c_{0}
) such that

(4)

f(x)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{m}\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}(x,a_{m})}{\|\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}(\cdot,a_{m})\|_{\mathcal{B}}}\qquad(x\in\mathbb{B}),

where the series converges absolutely and uniformly on compact
subsets of
\mathbb{B}
and the norm
\|\{\lambda_{m}\}\|_{\ell^{\infty}}

is equivalent to the norm
\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}
.

By Lemma
7.1
, the norm

\|\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}(\cdot,a_{m})\|_{\mathcal{B}}
is equivalent
to
(1-\lvert a_{m}\rvert^{2})^{-(\alpha+n)}
and the theorem remains true
if one uses the representation

(5)

f(x)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{m}(1-\lvert a_{m}\rvert^{2})^{\alpha+n}\,\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}(x,a_{m})\qquad(x\in\mathbb{B})

instead of (
4
).

2.
Preliminaries

We denote positive constants whose exact values are inessential by
the letter
C
.
The value of
C
may be different in different occurrences.
For two positive expressions
X
and
Y
, we write
X\lesssim Y

to mean
X\leq CY
.
If both
X\leq CY
and
Y\leq CX
, we write
X\sim Y
.

For
x,y\in\mathbb{B}
, we define

[x,y]:=\sqrt{1-2\langle x,y\rangle+\lvert x\rvert^{2}\lvert y\rvert^{2}}.

[x,y]
is symmetric,
[x,0]=1
, and if
y\neq 0
, then

[x,y]=\bigl\lvert\lvert y\rvert x-y/\lvert y\rvert\bigr\rvert
.
Therefore

(6)

[x,y]\geq 1-\lvert x\rvert\lvert y\rvert\qquad(x,y\in\mathbb{B}).

Denote by
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{B})
the group of Möbius
transformations that preserve
\mathbb{B}
.
For
a\in\mathbb{B}
, the canonical Möbius transformation
that exchanges
a
and
0
is given by

(7)

\varphi_{a}(x)=\frac{a\lvert x-a\rvert^{2}+(1-\lvert a\rvert^{2})(a-x)}{[x,a]^{2}}\qquad(x\in\mathbb{B}).

It is an involution,
\varphi_{a}^{-1}=\varphi_{a}
, and
for all
x\in\mathbb{B}
, the identity

(8)

1-\lvert\varphi_{a}(x)\rvert^{2}=\frac{(1-\lvert a\rvert^{2})(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})}{[x,a]^{2}}

holds.
The determinant of the Jacobian matrix of
\varphi_{a}
satisfies
(
[
16
, Theorem 3.3.1]
)

(9)

\lvert\det J\varphi_{a}(x)\rvert=\frac{(1-\lvert\varphi_{a}(x)\rvert^{2})^{n}}{(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{n}}.

The equality

(10)

[a,\varphi_{a}(x)]=\frac{1-\lvert a\rvert^{2}}{[x,a]}

follows from (
8
)
(see
[
13
, Theorem 1.1]
or
[
20
, Lemma 2.1]
).

For
a,b\in\mathbb{B}
, the pseudo-hyperbolic metric

\rho(a,b)=\lvert\varphi_{a}(b)\rvert
satisfies the
equality

(11)

\rho(a,b)=\frac{\lvert a-b\rvert}{[a,b]}.

The pseudo-hyperbolic ball

E_{r}(a)=\{\,x\in\mathbb{B}:\rho(x,a)<r\,\}
is also a Euclidean ball
with (see
[
16
, Theorem 2.2.2]
)

(12)

\text{center}=\frac{(1-r^{2})a}{1-\lvert a\rvert^{2}r^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\text{radius}=\frac{(1-\lvert a\rvert^{2})r}{1-\lvert a\rvert^{2}r^{2}}.

For a proof of the following lemma,
see
[
2
, Lemma 2.1 and 2.2]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

(i) For all
a,b\in\mathbb{B}
,

\frac{1-\rho(a,b)}{1+\rho(a,b)}\leq\frac{1-\lvert a\rvert}{1-\lvert b\rvert}\leq\frac{1+\rho(a,b)}{1-\rho(a,b)}.

(ii) For all
a,b,x\in\mathbb{B}
,

\frac{1-\rho(a,b)}{1+\rho(a,b)}\leq\frac{[x,a]}{[x,b]}\leq\frac{1+\rho(a,b)}{1-\rho(a,b)}.

The hyperbolic metric on
\mathbb{B}
is given by

\beta(a,b)=\log\frac{1+\rho(a,b)}{1-\rho(a,b)}\qquad(a,b\in\mathbb{B}).

Both metrics
\rho
and
\beta
are Möbius invariant.

Finally, we mention two integral estimates.
Let
\sigma
be the normalized surface measure on
\mathbb{S}
.
For a proof of the following lemma see
[
11
, Proposition 2.2]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
s>-1
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
For all
x\in\mathbb{B}
,

\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{d\sigma(\zeta)}{\lvert x-\zeta\rvert^{n-1+t}}\sim\int_{\mathbb{B}}\frac{(1-\lvert y\rvert^{2})^{s}}{[x,y]^{n+s+t}}d\nu(y)\sim\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}},&\text{if $t>0$};\\
1+\log\dfrac{1}{1-\lvert x\rvert^{2}},&\text{if $t=0$};\\
1,&\text{if $t<0$},\end{cases}

where the implied constants depend only on
n,s,t
.

3.
Reproducing Kernels and Fractional Differential Operators

In this section we review the properties of the reproducing
kernels and define a family of differential operators
D^{t}_{s}
.

Denote by
H_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the space of all homogeneous
(Euclidean) harmonic polynomials of degree
m
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
It is finite dimensional with
\text{dim}\,H_{m}\sim m^{n-2}

(m\geq 1)
.
By homogeneity,
q_{m}\in H_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is determined by its
restriction on
\mathbb{S}
which is called a spherical harmonic
and the space of spherical harmonics of degree
m
is denoted
by
H_{m}(\mathbb{S})
.
Spherical harmonics of different degrees are orthogonal
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
,

(13)

\int_{\mathbb{S}}q_{m}(\zeta)q_{k}(\zeta)\,d\sigma(\zeta)=0\qquad(m\neq k,q_{m}\in H_{m}(\mathbb{S}),q_{k}\in H_{k}(\mathbb{S})).

Point evaluation functionals are bounded on
H_{m}(\mathbb{S})
and
so for every
\eta\in\mathbb{S}
, there exists

Z_{m}(\eta,\cdot)\in H_{m}(\mathbb{S})
, called the zonal harmonic
of degree
m
with pole
\eta
, such that for all

q_{m}\in H_{m}(\mathbb{S})
,

(14)

q_{m}(\eta)=\int_{\mathbb{S}}q_{m}(\zeta)Z_{m}(\eta,\zeta)\,d\sigma(\zeta).

Z_{m}(\cdot,\cdot)
is real valued, symmetric, and homogeneous of degree

m
in each variable.
On the diagonal,
Z_{m}(\zeta,\zeta)=\text{dim}\,H_{m}
, and in general

\lvert Z_{m}(\eta,\zeta)\rvert\leq Z_{m}(\zeta,\zeta)
.
Thus

(15)

\lvert Z_{m}(\eta,\zeta)\rvert\lesssim m^{n-2}\qquad(m\geq 1).

For details we refer the reader to
[
1
, Chapter 5]
.

For
q_{m}\in H_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the solution of the
\mathcal{H}
-harmonic
Dirichlet problem on
\mathbb{B}
with boundary data

q_{m}|_{\mathbb{S}}
is given by (
[
16
, Theorem 6.1.1]
)

(16)

g(x)=S_{m}(\lvert x\rvert)q_{m}(x)\qquad(x\in\overline{\mathbb{B}}).

That is,
g
is
\mathcal{H}
-harmonic on
\mathbb{B}
, continuous on

\overline{\mathbb{B}}
and equals
q_{m}
on
\mathbb{S}
.
Here, the factor
S_{m}(r)

(0\leq r\leq 1)
is given by

(17)

S_{m}(r)=\frac{F(m,1-\tfrac{1}{2}n;m+\tfrac{1}{2}n;r^{2})}{F(m,1-\tfrac{1}{2}n;m+\tfrac{1}{2}n;1)},

where

(18)

F(a,b;c;z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a)_{k}(b)_{k}}{(c)_{k}k!}\,z^{k}

is the Gauss hypergeometric function.

S_{m}
depends also on the dimension
n
but we do not write this
for shortness.
When the dimension
n
is even the hypergeometric series terminates
and
S_{m}
is a polynomial, but this is not true in odd dimensions.

S_{m}(r)
is a decreasing function of
r
, and is normalized so that

S_{m}(1)=1
.

S_{0}\equiv 1
and when
m\geq 1
, the estimate
(
[
15
, Proposition I.6]
or
[
18
, Lemma 2.6]
)

(19)

1\leq S_{m}(r)\leq Cm^{n/2-1}\qquad(0\leq r\leq 1)

holds, where
C=C(n)
is a constant depending only on
n
.

Every
\mathcal{H}
-harmonic function on
\mathbb{B}
can be written
as a series of terms of the form (
16
).
More precisely, for every
f\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{B})
, there exists
a unique sequence of polynomials
q_{m}\in H_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
(see
[
7
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
16
, Theorem 6.3.1]
)

f(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}S_{m}(\lvert x\rvert)q_{m}(x)\qquad(x\in\mathbb{B}),

where the series converges absolutely and uniformly on compact
subsets of
\mathbb{B}
.

The hyperbolic Poisson kernel and its series expansion are
given by
[
16
, Theorem 6.2.2]

(20)

P_{h}(x,\zeta)=\frac{(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{n-1}}{\lvert x-\zeta\rvert^{2(n-1)}}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}S_{m}(\lvert x\rvert)Z_{m}(x,\zeta)\qquad(x\in\mathbb{B},\zeta\in\mathbb{S}).

For the Bergman reproducing kernels
\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}(x,y)

a closed formula is not known, however, the following series
expansion holds (
[
15
, Corollary III.5]
,

[
17
, Theorem 5.3]
)

(21)

\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}(x,y)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}c_{m}(\alpha)S_{m}(\lvert x\rvert)S_{m}(\lvert y\rvert)Z_{m}(x,y)\qquad(\alpha>-1,x,y\in\mathbb{B}),

where the coefficients
c_{m}(\alpha)
are determined by

(22)

\frac{1}{c_{m}(\alpha)}=n\int_{0}^{1}r^{2m+n-1}S_{m}^{2}(r)(1-r^{2})^{\alpha}\,dr.

A formula for the above integral is not known either.
However, the estimate

(23)

c_{m}(\alpha)\sim m^{\alpha+1}\qquad(m\to\infty)

holds (see (
[
15
, Theorem III.6]
) from which it follows
that the series in (
21
) converges absolutely and
uniformly on
K\times\overline{\mathbb{B}}
for every compact

K\subset\mathbb{B}
.

It is clear from (
22
) that
c_{m}(\alpha)>0
.
Using these coefficients we define a family of fractional
differential operators following
[
9
]
and
[
5
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
s>-1
and
s+t>-1
.
If
f\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{B})
has the series expansion

f(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}S_{m}(\lvert x\rvert)q_{m}(x)
,
then define

(24)

D^{t}_{s}f(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{c_{m}(s+t)}{c_{m}(s)}S_{m}(\lvert x\rvert)q_{m}(x).

The operator
D^{t}_{s}
multiplies the
m^{\text{th}}
term of the
series expansion of
f
with the coefficient

c_{m}(s+t)/c_{m}(s)\sim m^{t}
by (
23
).
Similar types of operators are frequently used in the theory of
holomorphic and harmonic Bergman spaces and act as
differential operators of order
t
(integral if
t<0
).
For
\mathcal{H}
-harmonic functions slightly different operators with
multipliers
\Gamma(m+s)/\Gamma(m+\beta+s)
are used in

[
14
]
and Hardy-Littlewood inequalities are obtained.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
f\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{B})
, the series in (
24
)
absolutely and uniformly converges on compact subsets of

\mathbb{B}
and so
D^{t}_{s}f\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{B})
.
In addition,

D^{t}_{s}:\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{B})\to\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{B})
is
continuous when
\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{B})
is equipped with the
topology of uniform convergence on compact subsets.

This lemma can be verified in the same way as

[
5
, Theorems 3.1 and 3.2]
.
An additional term
S_{m}(r)
appears but it can easily be
handled with the estimate (
19
).

The operator
D^{t}_{s}
is invertible with

(25)

D^{-t}_{s+t}D^{t}_{s}=D^{t}_{s}D^{-t}_{s+t}=\textrm{Id}.

The role of
s
is minor and one reason for its inclusion is to
simplify the action of
D^{t}_{s}
on the reproducing kernel
\mathcal{R}_{s}
,

(26)

D^{t}_{s}\mathcal{R}_{s}(x,y)=\mathcal{R}_{s+t}(x,y).

If
f\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{B})
is also integrable, then

D^{t}_{s}
can be written as an integral operator.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
s>-1
,
s+t>-1
and
f\in L^{1}_{s}(\mathbb{B})
.

(i)

D^{t}_{s}P_{s}f(x)=D^{t}_{s}\int_{\mathbb{B}}\mathcal{R}_{s}(x,y)f(y)d\nu_{s}(y)=\int_{\mathbb{B}}\mathcal{R}_{s+t}(x,y)f(y)d\nu_{s}(y).

(ii)

If
f
is also in
\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{B})
, then

D^{t}_{s}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{B}}\mathcal{R}_{s+t}(x,y)f(y)d\nu_{s}(y).

Proof.

For fixed
x\in\mathbb{B}
, the series in (
21
) uniformly
converges for
y\in\mathbb{B}
.
Thus

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{B}}\mathcal{R}_{s}(x,y)f(y)\,d\nu_{s}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}c_{m}(s)S_{m}(\lvert x\rvert)\int_{\mathbb{B}}Z_{m}(x,y)S_{m}(\lvert y\rvert)f(y)\,d\nu_{s}(y)

(27)

\displaystyle=:\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}c_{m}(s)S_{m}(\lvert x\rvert)q_{m}(x).

The function
q_{m}
is in
H_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and the series
in (
3
) absolutely and uniformly converges on
compact subsets of
\mathbb{B}
.
This follows from (
19
) and (
23
), and
the fact that

\lvert Z_{m}(x,y)\rvert\lesssim\lvert x\rvert^{m}m^{n-2}
by (
15
).
Thus, the series in (
3
) is the (unique) series expansion
and by (
24
),

D^{t}_{s}\int_{\mathbb{B}}\mathcal{R}_{s}(x,y)f(y)d\nu_{s}(y)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}c_{m}(s+t)S_{m}(\lvert x\rvert)q_{m}(x)
.
This series equals

\int_{\mathbb{B}}\mathcal{R}_{s+t}(x,y)f(y)d\nu_{s}(y)

by the same reasoning.

If
f\in L^{1}_{s}\cap\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{B})
, then
P_{s}f=f
because
the reproducing property in (
1
) holds also for

f\in\mathcal{B}^{1}_{\alpha}
(
[
19
, Lemma 7.1]
).
∎

The following upper estimates of the reproducing kernels

\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}
have been obtained in

[
19
, Theorem 1.2]
.
Here,
\nabla_{x}
means that the gradient is taken with respect to
x
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\alpha>-1
.
There exists a constant
C=C(n,\alpha)>0
such that
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{B}
,

(a)

\lvert\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}(x,y)\rvert\leq\dfrac{C}{[x,y]^{\alpha+n}}
,

(b)

\lvert\nabla_{x}\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}(x,y)\rvert\leq\dfrac{C}{[x,y]^{\alpha+n+1}}
.

These estimates lead to the following projection theorem
(see
[
19
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
and
\alpha,\gamma>-1
.
The operator
P_{\gamma}\colon L^{p}_{\alpha}\to\mathcal{B}^{p}_{\alpha}

is bounded if and only if
\alpha+1<p(\gamma+1)
.
In this case
P_{\gamma}f=f
for

f\in L^{p}_{\alpha}\cap\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{B})
.

4.
Elementary Properties of the Bloch Space

We first mention a few basic basic properties of
\mathcal{B}
and

\mathcal{B}_{0}
.
The verifications are omitted as they are straightforward and are
similar to the holomorphic or the Euclidean harmonic case.
The space
\mathcal{B}
is a Banach space with respect to the norm

\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{B}}
and
\mathcal{B}_{0}
is a closed subspace of

\mathcal{B}
.
The seminorm
p_{\mathcal{B}}
is Möbius invariant, i.e.,

p_{\mathcal{B}}(f\circ\psi)=p_{\mathcal{B}}(f)
for all

\psi\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{B})
.
If
f\in\mathcal{B}
, then for every
x,y\in\mathbb{B}
,

(28)

\lvert f(x)-f(y)\rvert\leq\frac{1}{2}\,p_{\mathcal{B}}(f)\beta(x,y).

In particular, taking
y=0
and using

\beta(x,0)\leq 1+\log 1/(1-\lvert x\rvert)
shows

(29)

\lvert f(x)\rvert\leq\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}\Big(1+\log\frac{1}{1-\lvert x\rvert}\Big)\qquad(f\in\mathcal{B},x\in\mathbb{B}).

There are various results in
[
6
]
and
[
8
]
that show
that
\mathcal{H}
-harmonic functions can have different behaviours
depending on whether the dimension
n
is odd or even.
We show here one more difference.
Let
q_{m}\in H_{m}(\mathbb{S})
.
If the dimension
n
is even,
S_{m}(\lvert x\rvert)
is a
polynomial and the Poisson extension

P_{h}[q_{m}](x)=S_{m}(\lvert x\rvert)q_{m}(x)
is
an
\mathcal{H}
-harmonic polynomial.
This is not true when the dimension
n
is odd.
In fact, in this case a non-constant polynomial can not be

\mathcal{H}
-harmonic on
\mathbb{B}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

In odd dimensions, there are no non-constant polynomials in

\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{B})
.

Proof.

Suppose that
p
is a polynomial of degree
M\geq 1
and

p\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{B})
.
Then
p=\sum_{m=0}^{M}p^{(m)}
, where
p^{(m)}
is a homogeneous
polynomial of degree
m
and
by
[
1
, Theorem 5.7]
, each
p^{(m)}
can be written in the form

p^{(m)}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{[m/2]}\lvert x\rvert^{2j}q_{m-2j}^{(m)}(x),

with
q_{m-2j}^{(m)}\in H_{m-2j}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Thus

(30)

p(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{M}\sum_{j=0}^{[m/2]}\lvert x\rvert^{2j}q_{m-2j}^{(m)}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{M}\sum_{j=0}^{[(M-k)/2]}\lvert x\rvert^{2j}q_{k}^{(k+2j)}(x),

where we make the change of index
k=m-2j
.
On the boundary
\mathbb{S}
,

p(\zeta)=\sum_{k=0}^{M}\sum_{j=0}^{[(M-k)/2]}q_{k}^{(k+2j)}(\zeta)=:\sum_{k=0}^{M}Q_{k}(\zeta)\qquad(\zeta\in\mathbb{S}),

with
Q_{k}\in H_{k}(\mathbb{S})
, and since
p
is
\mathcal{H}
-harmonic,

p=P_{h}[p|_{\mathbb{S}}]
and so

(31)

p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{M}S_{k}(\lvert x\rvert)Q_{k}(x)\qquad(x\in\mathbb{B}).

Because
p
is non-constant, there exist
1\leq i\leq M
and

\eta\in\mathbb{S}
such that
Q_{i}(\eta)\neq 0
.
For
0\leq r\leq 1
, we compute the integral

I=\int_{\mathbb{S}}p(r\zeta)Z_{i}(\eta,\zeta)\,d\sigma(\zeta)

in two ways, using (
30
) and then (
31
).
First, by (
30
),

I=\sum_{k=0}^{M}\sum_{j=0}^{[(M-k)/2]}r^{k+2j}\int_{\mathbb{S}}q_{k}^{(k+2j)}(\zeta)Z_{i}(\eta,\zeta)\,d\sigma(\zeta)=r^{i}\sum_{j=0}^{[(M-i)/2]}r^{2j}q_{i}^{(i+2j)}(\eta),

by (
13
) and (
14
).
Next, by (
31
),

I=\sum_{k=0}^{M}S_{k}(r)r^{k}\int_{\mathbb{S}}Q_{k}(\zeta)Z_{i}(\eta,\zeta)\,d\sigma(\zeta)=r^{i}S_{i}(r)Q_{i}(\eta).

Combining these we see that

S_{i}(r)Q_{i}(\eta)=\sum_{j=0}^{[(M-i)/2]}r^{2j}q_{i}^{(i+2j)}(\eta)

for all
0<r\leq 1
.
Because
Q_{i}(\eta)\neq 0
, this shows that
S_{i}(r)
is a
polynomial of
r
.
This is a contradiction because when the dimension
n
is odd,

S_{i}
is not a polynomial for
i\geq 1
since its
hypergeometric series do not terminate.
∎

Let
q_{m}\in H_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
When the dimension
n
is even,

P_{h}[q_{m}|_{\mathbb{S}}](x)=S_{m}(\lvert x\rvert)q_{m}(x)
is
a polynomial and so is in
\mathcal{B}_{0}
.
It is also in
\mathcal{B}_{0}
when the dimension is odd.
This follows from the following two elementary facts about
hypergeometric series.
First,

(32)

\frac{d}{dz}F(a,b;c;z)=\frac{ab}{c}F(a+1,b+1;c+1;z)

and second, if
\Re\{c-a-b\}>0
, then
F(a,b;c;z)
uniformly converges
and so is bounded on the closed disk
\{z:\lvert z\rvert\leq 1\}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

If
q_{m}\in H_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then

P_{h}[q_{m}|_{\mathbb{S}}](x)=S_{m}(\lvert x\rvert)q_{m}(x)
is in
\mathcal{B}_{0}
.

Proof.

The case
n=2
is obvious and we assume
n\geq 3
.
By (
32
),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}S_{m}(\lvert x\rvert)q_{m}(x)={}

\displaystyle 2x_{i}\frac{m(1-\tfrac{1}{2}n)}{m+\tfrac{1}{2}n}\,\frac{F(m+1,2-\tfrac{1}{2}n;m+\tfrac{1}{2}n+1;\lvert x\rvert^{2})}{F(m,1-\tfrac{1}{2}n;m+\tfrac{1}{2}n;1)}\,q_{m}(x)

\displaystyle+S_{m}(\lvert x\rvert)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}q_{m}(x).

The hypergeometric function in the first term is bounded since

\Re\{c-a-b\}=n-2>0
.
Since the second term is also bounded, the result follows.
∎

We next show that Bergman projections of polynomials are in
\mathcal{B}_{0}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

P_{\alpha}p\in\mathcal{B}_{0}
for every polynomial
p
and
\alpha>-1
.

Proof.

We can assume
p
is homogeneous.
By
[
1
, Theorem 5.7]
again,
p
can be written in the form

p=q_{m}+\lvert x\rvert^{2}q_{m-2}+\cdots+\lvert x\rvert^{2k}q_{m-2k}
,
where
k=[m/2]
and
q_{j}\in H_{j}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Thus, it suffices to show that
P_{\alpha}(\lvert x\rvert^{k}q_{j})\in\mathcal{B}_{0}

for every
k\geq 0
and
q_{j}\in H_{j}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Now, using the uniform convergence of the series in (
21
), integrating
in polar coordinates and then using (
13
) and (
14
), we obtain

\displaystyle P_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\bigl(\lvert x\rvert^{k}q_{j}\bigr)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{B}}\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}(x,y)\lvert y\rvert^{k}q_{j}(y)\,d\nu_{\alpha}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}c_{m}(\alpha)S_{m}(\lvert x\rvert)\int_{0}^{1}nr^{n-1}S_{m}(r)r^{k+m+j}(1-r^{2})^{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{S}}Z_{m}(x,\zeta)q_{j}(\zeta)\,d\sigma(\zeta)dr

\displaystyle=c_{j}(\alpha)S_{j}(\lvert x\rvert)q_{j}(x)\int_{0}^{1}nr^{n-1}S_{j}(r)r^{k+2j}(1-r^{2})^{\alpha}\,dr

\displaystyle=CS_{j}(\lvert x\rvert)q_{j}(x),

which belongs to
\mathcal{B}_{0}
by Lemma
4.2
.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

The above proof shows also that for every polynomial
p
and
\alpha>-1
,

P_{\alpha}p\in\text{span}\bigl\{S_{m}(\lvert x\rvert)q_{m}(x)\,|\,q_{m}\in H_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),m=0,1,2\dotsc\bigr\}
.

5.
Projections onto the Bloch and the Little Bloch Space

The aim of this is section is to prove Theorem
1.1
.
The main issue is to show that the projection

P_{\alpha}\colon L^{\infty}\to\mathcal{B}
is onto.
To achieve this we first characterize
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{B}_{0}

in terms of the differential operators
D^{t}_{s}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For every
\alpha>-1
,
P_{\alpha}\colon L^{\infty}(\mathbb{B})\to\mathcal{B}

is bounded.
In addition, if
f\in C(\overline{\mathbb{B}})
,
then
P_{\alpha}f\in\mathcal{B}_{0}
.

Proof.

The estimate in Lemma
3.4
(b) together with Lemma
2.2

immediately implies that
P_{\alpha}
maps
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{B})
boundedly
into
\mathcal{B}
(see
[
19
, Theorem 1.5]
).
That
P_{\alpha}
maps
C(\overline{\mathbb{B}})
into
\mathcal{B}_{0}
follows
from the Stone-Weierstrass theorem and Lemma
4.3
.
∎

We next obtain two estimates.
One is similar to Lemma
2.2
, and
the other to
[
20
, Lemma 4.3]
, but include an extra term

\beta(x,y)
, the hyperbolic distance between
x
and
y
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
s>-1
and
t>0
.

(i)

There exists a constant
C=C(n,s,t)>0

such that for all
x\in\mathbb{B}
,

\int_{\mathbb{B}}\frac{\beta(x,y)\,(1-\lvert y\rvert^{2})^{s}}{[x,y]^{n+s+t}}\,d\nu(y)\leq\frac{C}{(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}}.

(ii)

Given
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
0<r_{\varepsilon}<1
such that
for all
r
with
r_{\varepsilon}<r<1
and all
x\in\mathbb{B}
,

\int_{\mathbb{B}\backslash E_{r}(x)}\frac{\beta(x,y)\,(1-\lvert y\rvert^{2})^{s}}{[x,y]^{n+s+t}}\,d\nu(y)<\frac{\varepsilon}{(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}}.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the proof of
[
20
, Lemma 4.3]
, requiring
only a minor modification.
For
0\leq r<1
, let

I_{r}(x)=(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}\int_{\mathbb{B}\backslash E_{r}(x)}\frac{\beta(x,y)\,(1-\lvert y\rvert^{2})^{s}}{[x,y]^{n+s+t}}\,d\nu(y),

where for
r=0
,
\mathbb{B}\backslash E_{0}(x)=\mathbb{B}
.
In the integral make the change of variable
y=\varphi_{x}(z)
.
Note that

\varphi_{x}^{-1}(\mathbb{B}\backslash E_{r}(x))=\mathbb{B}\backslash\mathbb{B}_{r}
.
Employing (
8
), (
9
) and (
10
),
and the fact that
\beta(x,\varphi_{x}(z))=\beta(0,z)

by the Möbius-invariance of
\beta
, we obtain that

I_{r}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{B}\backslash\mathbb{B}_{r}}\frac{\beta(0,z)\,(1-\lvert z\rvert^{2})^{s}}{[x,z]^{n+s-t}}\,d\nu(z).

Next, using
\beta(0,z)\leq 1+\log 1/(1-\lvert z\rvert)
and
integrating in polar coordinates shows

I_{r}(x)\leq\int_{r}^{1}n\tau^{n-1}\Big(1+\log\frac{1}{1-\tau}\Big)\,(1-\tau^{2})^{s}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{d\sigma(\zeta)}{\lvert\tau x-\zeta\rvert^{n+s-t}}\,d\tau.

Estimating the inner integral with Lemma
2.2
in three cases
and using the inequality
1-\tau^{2}\lvert x\rvert^{2}\geq 1-\tau^{2}
, we see that

\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{d\sigma(\zeta)}{\lvert\tau x-\zeta\rvert^{n+s-t}}\leq Cg(\tau):=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{(1-\tau^{2})^{1+s-t}},&\text{if $1+s-t>0$};\\
1+\log\dfrac{1}{1-\tau^{2}},&\text{if $1+s-t=0$};\\
1,&\text{if $1+s-t<0$},\end{cases}

where
C
depends only on
n,s,t
.
Thus

I_{r}(x)\leq C\int_{r}^{1}n\tau^{n-1}\Big(1+\log\frac{1}{1-\tau}\Big)\,(1-\tau^{2})^{s}g(\tau)\,d\tau.

Because
s>-1
and
t>0
, in all the three cases, the above
integral is finite when
r=0
.
This proves both parts of the lemma.
∎

We prove one more estimate.
In the lemma below we consider the integral over
r\mathbb{B}

for
0<r\leq 1
, not just
\mathbb{B}
.
This generality will be needed later.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
s>-1
and
t>0
.
There exists a constant
C=C(n,s,t)>0
such that for all
0<r\leq 1

and
f\in\mathcal{B}
,

(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}\,\Bigl\lvert\int_{r\mathbb{B}}\mathcal{R}_{s+t}(x,y)f(y)\,d\nu_{s}(y)\Bigr\rvert\leq C\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}.

Proof.

We write

\displaystyle\int_{r\mathbb{B}}\mathcal{R}_{s+t}(x,y)f(y)d\nu_{s}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{r\mathbb{B}}\mathcal{R}_{s+t}(x,y)f(x)d\nu_{s}(y)

\displaystyle+\int_{r\mathbb{B}}\mathcal{R}_{s+t}(x,y)\big(f(y)-f(x)\big)d\nu_{s}(y)=:h_{1,r}(x)+h_{2,r}(x).

Integrating in polar coordinates gives

h_{1,r}(x)=f(x)\int_{0}^{r}n\tau^{n-1}(1-\tau^{2})^{s}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\mathcal{R}_{s+t}(x,\tau\zeta)\,d\sigma(\zeta)\,d\tau.

Now, by the mean-value theorem for
\mathcal{H}
-harmonic functions,
the inner integral is
\mathcal{R}_{s+t}(x,0)
and by
(
21
),
\mathcal{R}_{s+t}(x,0)=c_{0}(s+t)
for all

x\in\mathbb{B}
because
Z_{m}(x,0)=0
for
m\geq 1
,
Z_{0}\equiv 1

and
S_{0}\equiv 1
.
Therefore, using also (
29
), we obtain

(33)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bigl\lvert h_{1,r}(x)\bigr\rvert&\leq c_{0}(s+t)\lvert f(x)\rvert\int_{0}^{1}n\tau^{n-1}(1-\tau^{2})^{s}\,d\tau=C\lvert f(x)\rvert\\
&\leq C\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}\Bigl(1+\log\frac{1}{1-\lvert x\rvert}\Bigr),\end{split}

with
C
depending only on
n,s,t
.
This shows

(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}\lvert h_{1,r}(x)\rvert\lesssim\|f|_{\mathcal{B}}
.

Next, by (
28
) and Lemma
3.4
(a),

\lvert h_{2,r}(x)\rvert\lesssim p_{\mathcal{B}}(f)\int_{r\mathbb{B}}\frac{\beta(x,y)\,d\nu_{s}(y)}{[x,y]^{n+s+t}}\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{\mathbb{B}}\frac{\beta(x,y)\,d\nu_{s}(y)}{[x,y]^{n+s+t}}\lesssim\frac{\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}}{(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}},

where in the last inequality we use Lemma
5.2
(i).
This proves the lemma.
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
s>-1
,
t>0
and
f\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{B})
.

(i)

f\in\mathcal{B}
if and only if

(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}D^{t}_{s}f(x)\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{B})
.
In this case,

\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}\sim\|(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}D^{t}_{s}f(x)\|_{L^{\infty}},

where the implied constants depend only on
n,s,t
, and are
independent of
f
.
In addition,

(34)

P_{s}\bigl[(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}D^{t}_{s}f(x)\bigr]=f.

(ii)

f\in\mathcal{B}_{0}
if and only if

(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}D^{t}_{s}f(x)\in C_{0}(\mathbb{B})
.

This proposition verifies the onto part of Theorem
1.1

and completes its proof.
It also shows that for every
s>-1
and
t>0
,

\|(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}D^{t}_{s}f(x)\|_{L^{\infty}}
is a norm on

\mathcal{B}
equivalent to
\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}
.
In the rest of the paper we mostly employ these norms as they are
easier to work with than
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{B}}
.

Proof.

Suppose
f\in\mathcal{B}
.
Then
f\in L^{1}_{s}\cap\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{B})
by (
29
)
and by Lemma
3.3
(ii) we have

D^{t}_{s}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{B}}\mathcal{R}_{s+t}(x,y)f(y)d\nu_{s}(y)
.
That

\|(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}D^{t}_{s}f(x)\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}

follows now from Lemma
5.3
.

Suppose now that
(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}D^{t}_{s}f(x)\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{B})
.
We first show (
34
) which holds because

D^{t}_{s}f\in L^{1}_{s+t}(\mathbb{B})\cap\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{B})

and by Lemma
3.3
(ii) and (
25
),

P_{s}\bigl[(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}D^{t}_{s}f(x)\bigr](x)=\int_{\mathbb{B}}\mathcal{R}_{s}(x,y)D^{t}_{s}f(y)\,d\nu_{s+t}(y)=D^{-t}_{s+t}(D^{t}_{s}f)(x)=f(x).

It now follows from Lemma
5.1
that
f\in\mathcal{B}

and
\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq\|P_{s}\|\,\|(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}D^{t}_{s}f(x)\|_{L^{\infty}}
.

(ii) Suppose
f\in\mathcal{B}_{0}
.
For
\varepsilon>0
, pick
r>r_{\varepsilon}
with
r_{\varepsilon}

is as given in Lemma
5.2
(ii).
As in the proof of Lemma
5.3
, we write

D^{t}_{s}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{B}}\mathcal{R}_{s+t}(x,y)f(y)d\nu_{s}(y)

in the form

\displaystyle D^{t}_{s}f(x)={}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{B}}\mathcal{R}_{s+t}(x,y)f(x)\,d\nu_{s}(y)+\int_{\mathbb{B}\backslash E_{r}(x)}\mathcal{R}_{s+t}(x,y)\big(f(y)-f(x)\big)\,d\nu_{s}(y)

\displaystyle+\int_{E_{r}(x)}\mathcal{R}_{s+t}(x,y)\big(f(y)-f(x)\big)\,d\nu_{s}(y)=:h_{1}(x)+h_{2}(x)+h_{3}(x).

We have
(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}h_{1}(x)\in C_{0}(\mathbb{B})
by (
33
).
Next, applying (
28
), Lemma
3.4
(a) and then
Lemma
5.2
(ii) shows that for some constant
C=C(n,s,t)
,

\lvert h_{2}(x)\rvert\leq Cp_{\mathcal{B}}(f)\int_{\mathbb{B}\backslash E_{r}(x)}\frac{\beta(x,y)\,(1-\lvert y\rvert^{2})^{s}}{[x,y]^{n+s+t}}\,d\nu(y)<Cp_{\mathcal{B}}(f)\frac{\varepsilon}{(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}}.

Thus
(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}\lvert h_{2}(x)\rvert\lesssim\varepsilon
.

To estimate
h_{3}
note that for
y\in E_{r}(x)
, by the mean-value inequality,

\lvert f(y)-f(x)\rvert\leq\lvert y-x\rvert\sup_{z\in E_{r}(x)}\lvert\nabla f(z)\rvert

and by (
11
),

\lvert y-x\rvert=\rho(x,y)[x,y]<r[x,y]\lesssim r(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})
since
by part (ii) of Lemma
2.1
,
[x,y]\sim[x,x]=1-\lvert x\rvert^{2}
.
Therefore

\lvert f(y)-f(x)\rvert\lesssim(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})\sup_{z\in E_{r}(x)}\lvert\nabla f(z)\rvert\lesssim\sup_{z\in E_{r}(x)}(1-\lvert z\rvert^{2})\lvert\nabla f(z)\rvert,

where the last inequality follows from Lemma

(i).
Hence, by Lemma

\displaystyle(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}\lvert h_{3}(x)\rvert

\displaystyle\lesssim\sup_{z\in E_{r}(x)}(1-\lvert z\rvert^{2})\lvert\nabla f(z)\rvert\,(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}\int_{\mathbb{B}}\frac{d\nu_{s}(y)}{[x,y]^{n+s+t}}

Now, by (

we have

\lvert z\rvert\geq\dfrac{\bigl\lvert\lvert x\rvert-r\bigr\rvert}{1-r\lvert x\rvert}

and the right-hand side tends to

as
\lvert x\rvert\to 1^{-}
.
Since

, this shows that

\lim_{\lvert x\rvert\to 1^{-}}(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}\lvert h_{3}(x)\rvert=0

and we conclude that
(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}D^{t}_{s}f(x)\in C_{0}(\mathbb{B})
.

Conversely, if
(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}D^{t}_{s}f(x)\in C_{0}(\mathbb{B})
, then

f\in\mathcal{B}_{0}
by Lemma
5.1
and (
34
).
∎

We begin with writing the pairing in (

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\alpha>-1
,

For every

Proof.

Since the reproducing property in (

in
\mathcal{B}^{1}_{\alpha}
and

\lvert x\rvert\leq r

\mathcal{R}_{\alpha+t}(x,y)

is bounded by a constant independent of

and

Lemma
5.3
and the fact that
\mathcal{R}_{\alpha+t}
is
symmetric.
Thus, by the dominated convergence theorem, we can push the

\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}\int_{r\mathbb{B}}f(x)g(x)\,d\nu_{\alpha}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{B}}f(y)(1-\lvert y\rvert^{2})^{t}\int_{\mathbb{B}}\mathcal{R}_{\alpha+t}(x,y)g(x)\,d\nu_{\alpha}(x)\,d\nu_{\alpha}(y).

(ii).
∎

.

.
Note that

TUf(x)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}D^{t}_{\alpha}f(a_{m})\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}(x,a_{m})\,\nu_{\alpha+t}(E_{m})

and the series converges absolutely and uniformly on compact subsets
of
\mathbb{B}
.
By continuity we can push
D^{t}_{\alpha}
into the series,
and using (
26
) obtain

\displaystyle D^{t}_{\alpha}(TUf)(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}D^{t}_{\alpha}f(a_{m})\mathcal{R}_{\alpha+t}(x,a_{m})\,\nu_{\alpha+t}(E_{m})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\int_{E_{m}}D^{t}_{\alpha}f(a_{m})\mathcal{R}_{\alpha+t}(x,a_{m})\,\nu_{\alpha+t}(y).

Further, since
D^{t}_{\alpha}f\in L^{1}_{\alpha+t}(\mathbb{B})
, by the
reproducing property,

D^{t}_{\alpha}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{B}}\mathcal{R}_{\alpha+t}(x,y)D^{t}_{\alpha}f(y)d\nu_{\alpha+t}(y)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\int_{E_{m}}\mathcal{R}_{\alpha+t}(x,y)D^{t}_{\alpha}f(y)d\nu_{\alpha+t}(y).

Thus

\displaystyle D^{t}_{\alpha}(I-TU)f(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\int_{E_{m}}\bigl(\mathcal{R}_{\alpha+t}(x,y)-\mathcal{R}_{\alpha+t}(x,a_{m})\bigr)D^{t}_{\alpha}f(y)\,d\nu_{\alpha+t}(y)

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\int_{E_{m}}\bigl(D^{t}_{\alpha}f(y)-D^{t}_{\alpha}f(a_{m})\bigr)\mathcal{R}_{\alpha+t}(x,a_{m})\,d\nu_{\alpha+t}(y)

\displaystyle=:h_{1}(x)+h_{2}(x).

We first estimate
h_{1}
.
Pick
y\in E_{m}
.
Since
E_{m}\subset E_{r}(a_{m})
, a convex set, by the mean value inequality

\bigl\lvert\mathcal{R}_{\alpha+t}(x,y)-\mathcal{R}_{\alpha+t}(x,a_{m})\bigr\rvert\leq\lvert y-a_{m}\rvert\sup_{z\in E_{r}(a_{m})}\lvert\nabla_{z}\mathcal{R}_{\alpha+t}(x,z)\rvert.

By (
11
),
\lvert y-a_{m}\rvert=\rho(y,a_{m})[y,a_{m}]
and since

\rho(y,a_{m})<r<1/2
, we have
[y,a_{m}]\sim[y,y]=1-\lvert y\rvert^{2}
by
Lemma
2.1
(ii), with the suppressed constants not
depending on
r
.
Thus
\lvert y-a_{m}\rvert\lesssim r(1-\lvert y\rvert^{2})
.
Similarly, for all
x\in\mathbb{B}
and
z\in E_{r}(a_{m})
, we have

[x,z]\sim[x,a_{m}]\sim[x,y]
by Lemma
2.1
(ii).
Hence, by Lemma
3.4
(b),

\bigl\lvert\nabla_{z}\mathcal{R}_{\alpha+t}(x,z)\bigr\rvert\lesssim\frac{1}{[x,z]^{\alpha+t+n+1}}\sim\frac{1}{[x,y]^{\alpha+t+n+1}}.

We conclude that for all
x\in\mathbb{B}
and
y\in E_{m}
,

(40)

\bigl\lvert\mathcal{R}_{\alpha+t}(x,y)-\mathcal{R}_{\alpha+t}(x,a_{m})\bigr\rvert\lesssim r\frac{1-\lvert y\rvert^{2}}{[x,y]^{\alpha+t+n+1}}\lesssim\frac{r}{[x,y]^{\alpha+t+n}},

where in the last inequality we use
[x,y]\geq 1-\lvert y\rvert
by
(
6
).
Thus

\displaystyle\lvert h_{1}(x)\rvert

\displaystyle\lesssim r\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\int_{E_{m}}\frac{(1-\lvert y\rvert^{2})^{\alpha+t}\lvert D^{t}_{\alpha}f(y)\rvert}{[x,y]^{\alpha+t+n}}\,d\nu(y)\lesssim r\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{\mathbb{B}}\frac{(1-\lvert y\rvert^{2})^{\alpha}}{[x,y]^{\alpha+t+n}}\,d\nu(y)

\displaystyle\lesssim r\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}\frac{1}{(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}},

where the last inequality follows from Lemma
2.2
.

To estimate
h_{2}
, note first that by the reproducing property, we have

D^{t}_{\alpha}f(y)-D^{t}_{\alpha}f(a_{m})=\int_{\mathbb{B}}\bigl(\mathcal{R}_{\alpha+t}(y,z)-\mathcal{R}_{\alpha+t}(a_{m},z)\bigr)D^{t}_{\alpha}f(z)\,d\nu_{\alpha+t}(z).

Therefore, by (
40
) with the symmetry of
\mathcal{R}_{\alpha+t}
,
and Lemma
2.2
,

\lvert D^{t}_{\alpha}f(y)-D^{t}_{\alpha}f(a_{m})\rvert\lesssim r\int_{\mathbb{B}}\frac{(1-\lvert z\rvert^{2})^{\alpha+t}\lvert D^{t}_{\alpha}f(z)\rvert}{[y,z]^{\alpha+t+n}}\,d\nu(z)\lesssim r\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}\frac{1}{(1-\lvert y\rvert^{2})^{t}}.

Hence, using also Lemma
3.4
, the fact that
[x,a_{m}]\sim[x,y]

for all
x\in\mathbb{B}
and
y\in E_{m}
, and finally Lemma
2.2
,
we deduce

\displaystyle\lvert h_{2}(x)\rvert

\displaystyle\lesssim r\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\int_{E_{m}}\frac{(1-\lvert y\rvert^{2})^{\alpha}}{[x,a_{m}]^{\alpha+t+n}}\,d\nu(y)\sim r\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\int_{E_{m}}\frac{(1-\lvert y\rvert^{2})^{\alpha}}{[x,y]^{\alpha+t+n}}\,d\nu(y)

\displaystyle=r\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{\mathbb{B}}\frac{(1-\lvert y\rvert^{2})^{\alpha}}{[x,y]^{\alpha+t+n}}\,d\nu(y)\lesssim r\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}\frac{1}{(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}}.

Thus,

\|(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{t}D^{t}_{\alpha}(I-TU)f(x)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq Cr\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}

and the proof is completed.
∎

To see that the representation (
5
) can be used instead

and

.
The proofs of the Lemmas
7.2
and

become simpler;
and
TU
and the proof of Theorem
