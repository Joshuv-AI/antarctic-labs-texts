Abstract
. The aim of this work is to report on several ladder operators for generalized Zernike polynomials which are orthogonal polynomials on the unit disk 𝐃 = { ( x , y ) ∈ ℝ 2 : x 2 + y 2 ⩽ 1 } \mathbf{D}\,=\,\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\;x^{2}+y^{2}\leqslant 1\} with respect to the weight function W μ ​ ( x , y ) = ( 1 − x 2 − y 2 ) μ W_{\mu}(x,y)\,=\,(1-x^{2}-y^{2})^{\mu} where μ > − 1 \mu>-1 . These polynomials can be expressed in terms of the univariate Jacobi polynomials and, thus, we start by deducing several ladder operators for the Jacobi polynomials. Due to the symmetry of the disk and the weight function W μ W_{\mu} , it turns out that it is more convenient to use complex variables z = x + i ​ y z\,=\,x+iy and z ¯ = x − i ​ y \bar{z}\,=\,x-iy . Indeed, this allows us to systematically use the univariate ladder operators to deduce analogous ones for the complex generalized Zernike polynomials. Some of these univariate and bivariate ladder operators already appear in the literature. However, to the best of our knowledge, the proofs presented here are new. Lastly, we illustrate the use of ladder operators in the study of the orthogonal structure of some Sobolev spaces.



1.
Introduction

The so-called Zernike polynomials, originally introduced by Frits Zernike in 1934 (
[
20
]
) to describe the diffraction of the wavefront in the phase contrast image microscope, are used to characterize higher-order errors observed in interferometric analysis in precision optical manufacturing to achieve the desired performance of the system. In ophthalmological practice, the Hartmann-Shack sensor (or wavefront sensor) is used to determine the refractive errors of the human optical system, measuring slopes or normals of the wavefront at different points starting from the displacement of some luminous points in a target. A systematic method of classifying forms of aberration is to express the corresponding function in an appropriate basis. Zernike polynomials are recognized as the standard basis of wavefront developments by the Optical Society of America, (OSA). Any sufficiently regular phase function defined on the unit disk can be represented by its Fourier expansion in terms of the Zernike polynomials with certain coefficients. The alteration of these coefficients allows detection of the possible aberrations of the studied optical system. They are also used to describe the aberrations of the cornea or lens from an ideal spherical shape in optometry and ophthalmology. Finally, they can be effectively used in adaptive optics to cancel
atmospheric distortion, allowing images to be improved in IR or visual astronomy and satellite
images.

In
[
14
]
, an application in quantum optics of the “generalized Zernike or disk polynomials” (see, for instance,
[
16
]
) is shown, resorting to the Lie algebra based investigation of the dynamics of quantum systems driven by two-mode interaction Hamiltonians. This leads to
su
(2) and

su
(1,1) Lie algebraic structures, whose generators, in fact, can appropriately be realized in terms of creation and annihilation operators through raising and lowering operators. Hence, studying raising and lowering operators (collectively known as ladder operators), which is one the main goals of this paper, is of much interest in the theory of quantum optics.

From our point of view, Zernike polynomials are polynomials in two variables which are orthogonal on the unit disk with respect to the Lebesgue measure. They are represented in polar coordinates as a product of a radial polynomial part times a trigonometric function. The even polynomials are multiples of the cosine, and the odd polynomials are multiples of the sine. Generalized Zernike polynomials are bivariate polynomials which are orthogonal in the unit disk
x^{2}+y^{2}\leqslant 1
with respect to the weight function
(1-x^{2}-y^{2})^{\mu}
, where
\mu>-1
is a parameter and the special case
\mu=0
is equivalent to the usual Zernike polynomials. In turn, these polynomials can be generalized to several variables represented as a product of univariate Jacobi polynomials shifted to the interval
[0,1]
and harmonic polynomials (see
[
3
]
).

Another way of studying the generalized Zernike polynomials is to express them in one complex variable, for which we identify
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
\mathbb{C}
by setting
z=x+iy
and consider the unit disk as a subset of
\mathbb{C}
. Expressing orthogonal polynomials in a complex variable can be more convenient, even essential, in some cases, and may result in more elegant formulas and relations as well as simpler proofs. It is worth noting that the use of complex variables instead of cartesian ones to achieve results connected with orthogonal polynomials is discussed in
[
19
]
.

In this paper, we use the complex version of the generalized Zernike polynomials to show that they satisfy several ladder operators. These polynomials are represented in terms of univariate Jacobi polynomials and powers of the complex variable. We take advantage of this fact to deduce the ladder operators presented in the sequel and, thus, we also study several ladder operators satisfied by the Jacobi polynomials. We also include a brief discussion about the relevance of ladder operators in the study of orthogonal polynomials associated with Sobolev inner products, which are inner products involving the derivatives of the polynomials.

The outline of this paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we provide the basic background on bivariate orthogonal polynomials and the generalized Zernike polynomials. Section
3
is concerned with the ladder operators for Jacobi polynomials. We note that some of these univariate ladder operators already appear in the literature. However, to the best of our knowledge, the proofs presented here are new. We collect the ladder operators for the generalized Zernike polynomials in Section
4
and, in Section
5
, we show that fundamental differential and recurrence relations for the generalized Zernike polynomials can be deduced from the ladder operators. We dedicate the last section to discuss how ladder operators can be used to study the orthogonal polynomials associated with two Sobolev inner products defined on the unit disk. These Sobolev orthognal polynomials have been studied previously without involving bivariate ladder operators. It turns out that our proofs are simpler, which justifies this brief sidenote.

2.1.
Bivariate orthogonal polynomials.

We denote by
\Pi^{2}
the linear space of real bivariate polynomials. For
n\geqslant 0
, let
\Pi^{2}_{n}
denote the linear space of real bivariate polynomials of total degree at most
n
. Evidentely,

\text{dim}\,\Pi_{n}^{2}\,=\,\binom{n+2}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\Pi^{2}\,=\,\bigcup_{n\geqslant 0}\Pi_{n}^{2}.

We say that a sequence
\mathcal{P}\,=\,\{P_{n,m}(x,y):\,n\geqslant 0,\,0\leqslant m\leqslant n\}
of polynomials in
\Pi^{2}
is a
polynomial system (PS)
if for all
n\geqslant 0
, the set
\mathcal{P}_{n}\,=\{P_{n,m}(x,y):0\leqslant m\leqslant n\}
consists of
n+1
linearly independent polynomials of total degree
n
, that is,
\deg P_{n,m}\,=\,n
,
0\leqslant m\leqslant n
. In this way, a PS
\mathcal{P}
is a basis of
\Pi^{2}
.

Let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\Pi^{2}\times\Pi^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be an inner product defined on polynomials. A polynomial
P
of degree
n
is called an
orthogonal polynomial
with respect to the inner product if

\langle P,Q\rangle\,=\,0,\quad\forall Q\in\Pi^{2}_{n-1}.

Given an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and a PS
\mathcal{P}\,=\,\{P_{n,m}(x,y):\,n\geqslant 0,\,0\leqslant m\leqslant n\}
, we will say that
\mathcal{P}
is
orthogonal with respect to the inner product
if

\langle P_{n,m},Q\rangle\,=\,0,\quad\forall Q\in\Pi^{2}_{n-1},

for all
n\geqslant 0
and
0\leqslant m\leqslant n
. Moreover, if

\langle P_{n,m},P_{i,j}\rangle\,=\,H_{n,m}\,\delta_{n,i}\,\delta_{m,j},

where
H_{n,m}\,\neq\,0
for
n\geqslant 0
, then we say that
\mathcal{P}
is a
mutually orthogonal polynomial system
. Here,
\delta_{n,k}
denotes the Kronecker delta.

Let
W(x,y)
be a weight function defined on a domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
. That is,
W(x,y)>0
for all
(x,y)\in\Omega
, and
\Omega
has a nonempty interior. If an inner product is given by

\langle P,Q\rangle\,=\,\int\int_{\Omega}P(x,y)\,Q(x,y)\,W(x,y)\,dxdy,\quad\forall P,Q\in\Pi^{2},

we say that the orthogonal polynomials, whenever they exist, are orthogonal with respect to the weight function
W
.

2.2.
Generalized Zernike polynomials

Let

\mathbf{D}\,=\,\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\;x^{2}+y^{2}\leqslant 1\}

denote the unit disk in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For
\mu>-1
, we define the weight function

W_{\mu}(x,y)\,=\,(1-x^{2}-y^{2})^{\mu},\quad(x,y)\in\mathbf{D},

and the inner product

\langle P,Q\rangle_{\mu}\,=\,b_{\mu}\,\int\int_{\mathbf{D}}P(x,y)\,Q(x,y)\,W_{\mu}(x,y)\,dxdy,

where

b_{\mu}\,=\,\left(\int\int_{\mathbf{D}}W_{\mu}(x,y)\,dxdy\right)^{-1}\,=\,\frac{\mu+1}{\pi}.

A mutually orthogonal polynomial system on the unit disk can be given in terms of the Jacobi polynomials in the polar coordinates
(x,y)\,=\,(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)
,
0\leqslant r\leqslant 1
and
0\leqslant\theta\leqslant 2\pi
. Recall that the Jacobi polynomial of degree

P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)=\frac{1}{n!}\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(k+\alpha+1)_{n-k}(n+\alpha+\beta+1)_{k}\left(\dfrac{t-1}{2}\right)^{k},

where, as usual,

(a)_{0}=1,\quad(a)_{k}=a\,(a+1)\cdots(a+k-1),\quad k\geqslant 1,

denotes the Pochhammer symbol. These polynomials are orthogonal with respect to the univariate weight function

w_{\alpha,\beta}(t)\,=\,(1-t)^{\alpha}(1+t)^{\beta},\quad\alpha,\beta>-1,\quad t\in[-1,1],

and satisfy the differential equation

L^{\alpha,\beta}[y]\equiv(1-t^{2})y^{\prime\prime}+(\beta-\alpha-(\alpha+\beta+2)\,t)\,y^{\prime}\,=\,-n\,(n+\alpha+\beta+1)\,y.

2.3.
Complex generalized Zernike polynomials

Due to the symmetry of the unit disk, expressing the generalized Zernike polynomials in complex variables can be more convinient for our study and results in more elegant formulas and relations.

For
z\in\mathbb{C}
we write
z=x+iy
and, hence, considering the unit disk as a subset of
\mathbb{C}
, we have

\mathbf{D}\,=\,\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\,z\bar{z}\leqslant 1\}.

Definition 2.2
.

For
k,j\geqslant 0
, we define

Q_{k,j}^{\mu}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,\frac{j!}{(\mu+1)_{j}}\,z^{k-j}\,P_{j}^{(\mu,k-j)}(2z\bar{z}-1),\quad k>j.

(3)

The polynomials (
3
) are normalized by
Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(1,1)\,=\,1
. We note that we can write (
[
15
]
)

Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,\frac{k!}{(\mu+1)_{k}}\,\bar{z}^{j-k}\,P_{k}^{(\mu,j-k)}(2z\bar{z}-1).

(4)

They constitute a mutually orthogonal system with respect to the weight function

w_{\mu}(z)\,=\,(1-z\bar{z})^{\mu},\quad\mu>-1,\quad z\in\mathbf{D},

which satisfies
w_{\mu}(z)\,=\,W_{\mu}(x,y)
, hence,
w_{\mu}(z)\,dz\,=\,W_{\mu}(x,y)\,dxdy
. More precisely, the orthogonality is given by

b_{\mu}\,\int_{\mathbf{D}}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})\,\overline{Q^{\mu}_{m,\ell}(z,\bar{z})}\,w_{\mu}(z)\,dz\,=\,h^{\mu}_{k,j}\,\delta_{k,m}\,\delta_{j,\ell},

(5)

where

h^{\mu}_{k,j}\,=\,\frac{\mu+1}{\mu+k+j+1}\frac{k!\,j!}{(\mu+1)_{k}\,(\mu+1)_{j}}.

(6)

By (
3
),

\displaystyle\text{Re}\{Q^{\mu}_{n-j,j}(z,\bar{z})\}

\displaystyle=\,\frac{j!}{(\mu+1)_{j}}\,P^{n,\mu}_{j,1}(x,y),

\displaystyle 0\leqslant j\leqslant\frac{n}{2},

\displaystyle\text{Im}\{Q^{\mu}_{n-j,j}(z,\bar{z})\}

\displaystyle=\,\frac{j!}{(\mu+1)_{j}}\,P^{n,\mu}_{j,2}(x,y),

\displaystyle 0\leqslant j\leqslant\frac{n}{2},

which are, up to a constant, the orthogonal polynomials of two real variables defined in (
2
).

We must note that the polynomials
Q_{k,j}^{\mu}(z,\bar{z})
are invariant under the simultaneous permutations of the variables
\{z,\bar{z}\}
and the subindices
\{k,j\}
, that is,

Q_{k,j}^{\mu}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,Q_{j,k}^{\mu}(\bar{z},z).

(7)

This invariance will permeate the results presented in the sequel.

3.
Ladder operators for Jacobi polynomials

Throughout this section, we will introduce pairs of related ladder operators for the univariate Jacobi polynomials and, in the next section, we use them to deduce ladder operators for the complex generalized Zernike polynomials. Some of these univariate and bivariate ladder operators already appear in the literature. However, to the best of our knowledge, the proofs presented here are new.

Define the operators

A_{1}[u]\,=\,\frac{du}{dt}\quad\text{and}\quad A_{2}[u]\,=\,(1-t^{2})\frac{du}{dt}+[\beta\,(1-t)-\alpha\,(1+t)]\,u.

The action of these operators over the Jacobi polynomials is well known (
[
10
, 18.9.15, 18.9.16]
). The classical proof for the following result is based on the orthogonality of the Jacobi polynomials. However, we provide an operational proof.

Proposition 3.1
.

The univariate Jacobi polynomials satisfy

\displaystyle A_{1}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]

\displaystyle=\,\frac{1}{2}(n+\alpha+\beta+1)\,P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1)}(t),

\displaystyle\alpha,\beta>-1,

(8)

\displaystyle A_{2}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]

\displaystyle=\,-2(n+1)\,P_{n+1}^{(\alpha-1,\beta-1)}(t),

\displaystyle\alpha,\beta>0.

Proof.

Using the differential operator
L^{\alpha,\beta}
defined in (
1
), we compute

L^{\alpha,\beta}A_{1}[u]-A_{1}L^{\alpha,\beta}[u]\,=\,\left(2t\frac{d}{dt}+\alpha+\beta+2\right)A_{1}[u],

therefore,

L^{\alpha+1,\beta+1}A_{1}[u]\,=\,A_{1}\left[L^{\alpha,\beta}[u]+(\alpha+\beta+2)\,u\right].

Letting
u=P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)
and using (
1
), we get

\displaystyle L^{\alpha+1,\beta+1}A_{1}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]

\displaystyle\,=\,A_{1}L^{\alpha,\beta}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]+(\alpha+\beta+2)A_{1}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]

\displaystyle\,=\,\left(-n\,(n+\alpha+\beta+1)+\alpha+\beta+2\right)\,A_{1}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]

\displaystyle\,=\,-(n-1)(n+\alpha+\beta+2)\,A_{1}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)],

which implies that
A_{1}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]\,=\,c_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}\,P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1)}(t)
for some constant
c_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}
. From the explicit expression of the Jacobi polynomials and comparing the leading coefficients on both sides of this last equation, we get

c_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}\,=\,\frac{1}{2}(n+\alpha+\beta+1).

Similarly,

L^{\alpha,\beta}A_{2}[u]-A_{2}L^{\alpha,\beta}[u]\,=\,\left(2t\frac{d}{dt}-\alpha-\beta\right)A_{2}[u],

or, equivalently,

L^{\alpha-1,\beta-1}A_{2}[u]\,=\,A_{2}\left[L^{\alpha,\beta}[u]-(\alpha+\beta)\,u\right].

Thus,

\displaystyle L^{\alpha-1,\beta-1}A_{2}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]

\displaystyle\,=\,\left(-n\,(n+\alpha+\beta+1)-\alpha-\beta\right)\,A_{2}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]

\displaystyle\,=\,-(n+1)(n+\alpha+\beta)\,A_{2}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)],

which implies that
A_{2}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]\,=\,d_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}\,P_{n+1}^{(\alpha-1,\beta-1)}(t)
for some constant
d_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}
. In order to deduce the value of
d_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}
, we note that we can write

A_{2}[u]\,=\,-(t-1)^{2}\,\frac{du}{dt}-(\alpha+\beta)(t-1)\,u-2(t-1)\frac{du}{dt}-\alpha\,u.

Using this last identity and the explicit expression of the Jacobi polynomials, we compare leading coefficients on both sides of
A_{2}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]\,=\,d_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}\,P_{n+1}^{(\alpha-1,\beta-1)}(t)
and deduce that
d_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}\,=\,-2\,(n+1)
.
∎

For
n\geqslant 0
, let

B_{1}[u]\,=\,(1+t)\,\frac{du}{dt}+(n+\alpha+\beta+1)\,u\quad\text{and}\quad B_{2}[u]\,=\,(1-t^{2})\frac{du}{dt}-[2\alpha+(1-t)\,n]\,u.

We note that the coefficients of these operators are not fixed and depend on
n
.

Proposition 3.2
.

For
\beta>-1
, the univariate Jacobi polynomials satisfy

\displaystyle B_{1}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]

\displaystyle=\,(n+\alpha+\beta+1)\,P_{n}^{(\alpha+1,\beta)}(t),

\displaystyle\alpha>-1,

(9)

\displaystyle B_{2}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]

\displaystyle=\,-2(n+\alpha)\,P_{n}^{(\alpha-1,\beta)}(t),

\displaystyle\alpha>0.

Proof.

First, we note that we can write

\displaystyle L^{\alpha,\beta}[u]

\displaystyle\,=\,[2(1+t)-(t+1)^{2}]\frac{d^{2}u}{dt^{2}}+[2(\beta+1)-(\alpha+\beta+2)(1+t)]\frac{du}{dt}

\displaystyle\,=\,2(1+t)\frac{d^{2}u}{dt^{2}}+2(\beta+1)\frac{du}{dt}-\left\{(1+t)\frac{d}{dt}-n\right\}B_{1}[u]-n(n+\alpha+\beta+1)\,u.

From here, if we commute
L^{\alpha,\beta}
with
B_{1}
, we get

\displaystyle L^{\alpha,\beta}B_{1}[u]-B_{1}L^{\alpha,\beta}[u]

\displaystyle\,=\,2(1+t)\frac{d^{2}u}{dt^{2}}+2(\beta+1)\frac{du}{dt}

\displaystyle\,=\,\left\{(1+t)\frac{d}{dt}-n\right\}B_{1}[u]+L^{\alpha,\beta}[u]+n(n+\alpha+\beta+1)\,u,

or, equivalently,

L^{\alpha+1,\beta}B_{1}[u]\,=\,B_{1}\left(L^{\alpha,\beta}[u]-n\,u\right)+L^{\alpha,\beta}[u]+n(n+\alpha+\beta+1)\,u.

From (
1
), it follows that

L^{\alpha+1,\beta}B_{1}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]\,=\,-n(n+\alpha+\beta+2)\,B_{1}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)],

which implies that
B_{1}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]=c_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}\,P_{n}^{(\alpha+1,\beta)}(t)
. Writing,

B_{1}[u]\,=\,(t-1)\frac{du}{dt}+(n+\alpha+\beta+1)\,u+2\frac{du}{dt},

we can compare leading coefficients and obtain

c_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}\,=\,n+\alpha+\beta+1.

Notice that we can write

B_{2}[u]\,=\,-(t-1)^{2}\frac{du}{dt}+n\,(t-1)\,u-2(t-1)\frac{du}{dt}-2\,\alpha\,u.

Hence,

B_{2}\left[\left(\frac{t-1}{2}\right)^{n}\right]\,=\,-2(n+\alpha)\,\left(\frac{t-1}{2}\right)^{n},

which implies that
B_{2}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]
is a polynomial of degree
n
. By the orthogonality of
\{P_{n}^{(\alpha-1,\beta)}(t)\}_{n\geqslant 0}
and comparing leading coefficients, we have

B_{2}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]\,=\,-2(n+\alpha)P_{n}^{(\alpha-1,\beta)}(t)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d_{k}^{\alpha,\beta}\,P_{k}^{(\alpha-1,\beta)}(t),

where

d_{k}^{\alpha,\beta}\,h_{k}^{\alpha-1,\beta}\,=\,\int_{-1}^{1}B_{2}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]\,P_{k}^{(\alpha-1,\beta)}(t)\,w_{\alpha-1,\beta}(t)\,dt,\quad 0\leqslant k\leqslant n-1,

with

h_{k}^{\alpha-1,\beta}\,=\,\int_{-1}^{1}\left(P_{k}^{(\alpha-1,\beta)}(t)\right)^{2}\,w_{\alpha-1,\beta}(t)\,dt,

which is non zero for
\alpha-1,\beta>-1
(see
[
13
]
). After integrating by parts and observing that
p(t)w_{\alpha,\beta}(t)|_{-1}^{1}\,=\,0
for every polynomial
p(t)
, we obtain

\int_{-1}^{1}B_{2}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]\,P_{k}^{(\alpha-1,\beta)}(t)\,w_{\alpha-1,\beta}(t)\,dt\,=\,-\int_{-1}^{1}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)\,B_{1}[P_{k}^{(\alpha-1,\beta)}(t)]\,w_{\alpha,\beta}(t)\,dt,

and, therefore,

d_{k}^{\alpha,\beta}\,h_{k}^{\alpha-1,\beta}\,=\,-\int_{-1}^{1}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)\,B_{1}[P_{k}^{(\alpha-1,\beta)}(t)]\,w_{\alpha,\beta}(t)\,dt.

But
B_{1}[P_{k}^{(\alpha-1,\beta)}(t)]
is a polynomial of degree at most
k
. From the orthogonality of the Jacobi polynomials, we deduce that
d_{k}^{\alpha,\beta}\,=\,0
for
0\leqslant k\leqslant n-1
, and (
9
) is now proved.
∎

Notice that in (
9
), only the first parameter of the Jacobi polynomials is shifted by one unit. Recall that the univariate classical Jacobi polynomials following reflection formula

P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)\,=\,(-1)^{n}\,P_{n}^{(\beta,\alpha)}(-t).

(10)

Using (
10

n\geqslant 0
, the operators defined as

C_{1}[u]\,=\,(1-t)\,\frac{du}{dt}-(n+\alpha+\beta+1)\,u\quad\text{and}\quad C_{2}[u]\,=\,(1-t^{2})\frac{du}{dt}+[2\beta+(1+t)\,n]\,u,

act by shifting only the second parameter.

Proposition 3.3
.

For
\alpha>-1
, the univariate Jacobi polynomials satisfy

\displaystyle C_{1}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]

\displaystyle=\,-(n+\alpha+\beta+1)\,P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+1)}(t),

\displaystyle\beta>-1,

(11)

\displaystyle C_{2}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]

\displaystyle=\,2(n+\beta)\,P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta-1)}(t),

\displaystyle\beta>0.

On one hand, the operators in (
9
) and (

) shift one parameter and leave the degree of the polynomial unchanged. In contrast, the following univariate operators defined for

as

D_{1}[u]\,=\,(1+t)\,\frac{du}{dt}-n\,u\quad\text{and}\quad D_{2}[u]\,=\,(1-t^{2})\frac{du}{dt}+[(n+\beta+1)(1-t)-\alpha\,(1+t)]\,u,

shift the parameter

as well as the degree of the polynomial.

Proposition 3.4
.

For
\beta>-1
, the univariate Jacobi polynomials satisfy

\displaystyle D_{1}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]

\displaystyle=\,(n+\beta)\,P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta)}(t),

\displaystyle\alpha>-1,

(12)

\displaystyle D_{2}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]

\displaystyle=\,-2(n+1)\,P_{n+1}^{(\alpha-1,\beta)}(t),

\displaystyle\alpha>0.

Proof.

Observe that

D_{1}[u]\,=\,B_{1}[u]-(2n+\alpha+\beta+1)\,u.

Therefore,

\displaystyle L^{\alpha,\beta}D_{1}[u]-D_{1}L^{\alpha,\beta}[u]\,=\,L^{\alpha,\beta}B_{1}[u]-B_{1}L^{\alpha,\beta}[u]

\displaystyle\,=\,\left\{(1+t)\frac{d}{dt}+n+\alpha+\beta+1\right\}D_{1}[u]+L^{\alpha,\beta}[u]+n(n+\alpha+\beta+1)\,u

or, equivalently,

L^{\alpha+1,\beta}D_{1}[u]\,=\,D_{1}\left(L^{\alpha,\beta}[u]+(n+\alpha+\beta+1)\,u\right)+L^{\alpha,\beta}[u]+n(n+\alpha+\beta+1)\,u.

From (
1
), it follows that

L^{\alpha+1,\beta}D_{1}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]\,=\,-(n-1)\,(n+\alpha+\beta+1)\,D_{1}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)],

which implies that
D_{1}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]=c_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}\,P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta)}(t)
. Writing,

D_{1}[u]\,=\,(t-1)\frac{du}{dt}-n\,u+2\frac{du}{dt},

(13)

we can compare leading coefficients and obtain
c_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}\,=\,n+\beta
.

Moreover, notice that we can write

D_{2}[u]\,=\,-(t-1)^{2}\frac{du}{dt}-(n+\alpha+\beta+1)\,(t-1)\,u-2(t-1)\frac{du}{dt}-2\,\alpha\,u.

Hence,

D_{2}\left[\left(\frac{t-1}{2}\right)^{n}\right]\,=\,-2(2n+\alpha+\beta+1)\,\left(\frac{t-1}{2}\right)^{n+1}-2(n+\alpha)\,\left(\frac{t-1}{2}\right)^{n},

which implies that
D_{2}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]
is a polynomial of degree
n+1
. By the orthogonality of
\{P_{n}^{(\alpha-1,\beta)}(t)\}_{n\geqslant 0}
and comparing leading coefficients, we have

D_{2}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]\,=\,-2(n+1)P_{n+1}^{(\alpha-1,\beta)}(t)+\sum_{k=0}^{n}d_{k}^{\alpha,\beta}\,P_{k}^{(\alpha-1,\beta)}(t),

where

d_{k}^{\alpha,\beta}\,h_{k}^{\alpha-1,\beta}\,=\,\int_{-1}^{1}D_{2}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]\,P_{k}^{(\alpha-1,\beta)}(t)\,w_{\alpha-1,\beta}(t)\,dt,\quad 0\leqslant k\leqslant n.

Integrating by parts as in the proof of Proposition
3.2
, we obtain

d_{k}^{\alpha,\beta}\,h_{k}^{\alpha-1,\beta}\,=\,-\int_{-1}^{1}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)\,D_{1}[P_{k}^{(\alpha-1,\beta)}(t)]\,w_{\alpha,\beta}(t)\,dt.

On one hand,
D_{1}[P_{k}^{(\alpha-1,\beta)}(t)]
is a polynomial of degree at most
k
for
0\leqslant k\leqslant n-1
. From the orthogonality of the Jacobi polynomials, we deduce that
d_{k}^{\alpha,\beta}\,=\,0
for
0\leqslant k\leqslant n-1
. On the other hand, by (
13
), we have

D_{1}\left[\left(\frac{t-1}{2}\right)^{n}\right]\,=\,n\left(\frac{t-1}{2}\right)^{n-1}.

Therefore,
D_{1}[P_{n}^{(\alpha-1,\beta)}(t)]
is a polynomial of degree at most
n-1
and, by orthogonality,
d_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}\,=\,0
. This proves (
12
).
∎

Using (
12
) and (
10
), we can show that the operators defined for
n\geqslant 0
as

E_{1}[u]\,=\,(1-t)\frac{du}{dt}+n\,u\quad\text{and}\quad E_{2}[u]\,=\,(1-t^{2})\frac{du}{dt}+[(n+\alpha+1)(1+t)-\beta(1-t)]\,u,

shift the parameter
\beta
as well as the degree of the Jacobi polynomials.

Proposition 3.5
.

For
\alpha>-1
, the univariate Jacobi polynomials satisfy

\displaystyle E_{1}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]

\displaystyle=\,(n+\alpha)\,P_{n-1}^{(\alpha,\beta+1)}(t),

\displaystyle\beta>-1,

(14)

\displaystyle E_{2}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]

\displaystyle=\,-2(n+1)\,P_{n+1}^{(\alpha,\beta-1)}(t),

\displaystyle\beta>0.

We will now finish our list of ladder operators for Jacobi polynomials. We define the two operators with fixed coefficients:

F_{1}[u]\,=\,(1+t)\frac{du}{dt}+\beta\,u\quad\text{and}\quad F_{2}[u]\,=\,(1-t)\frac{du}{dt}-\alpha\,u.

Proposition 3.6
.

The univariate Jacobi polynomials satisfy

\displaystyle F_{1}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]

\displaystyle=\,(n+\beta)\,P_{n}^{(\alpha+1,\beta-1)}(t),

\displaystyle\alpha,\,\beta-1>-1,

(15)

\displaystyle F_{2}[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]

\displaystyle=\,-(n+\alpha)\,P_{n}^{(\alpha-1,\beta+1)}(t),

\displaystyle\alpha-1,\beta>-1.

Proof.

We compute

\displaystyle L^{\alpha,\beta}F_{1}[u]-F_{1}L^{\alpha,\beta}[u]\,=\,2\frac{d}{dt}F_{1}[u],

or, equivalently,

L^{\alpha+1,\beta-1}F_{1}[u]\,=\,F_{1}L^{\alpha,\beta}[u].

From here together with (
7
), we deduce (
15
).
∎

4.
Ladder operators for Zernike polynomials

In this section, we derive ladder operators for the generalized Zernike polynomials by directly employing ladder operators satisfied by the univariate Jacobi polynomials. We give several ladder operators that shift the parameter and degree of the complex generalized Zernike polynomials by at most one unit, that is, each ladder operator maps
Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})
to
Q^{\eta}_{m,\ell}(z,\bar{z})
with
|\mu-\eta|\leqslant 1
,
|k-m|\leqslant 1
,
|j-\ell|\leqslant 1
.

The first two ladder operators for the complex generalized Zernike polynomials are deduced in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

The complex generalized Zernike polynomials satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,\frac{k\,(j+\mu+1)}{\mu+1}Q^{\mu+1}_{k-1,j}(z,\bar{z}),

\displaystyle\mu>-1,

(16)

\displaystyle\left\{(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}-\mu\,z\right\}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,-\mu\,Q^{\mu-1}_{k+1,j}(z,\bar{z}),

\displaystyle\mu>0.

Proof.

Using (
4
), we compute

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,2\,\frac{k!}{(\mu+1)_{k}}\bar{z}^{j-k+1}\left(P_{k}^{(\mu,j-k)}\right)^{\prime}(2z\bar{z}-1).

Notice that if we set
t=2r-1
where
r\,=\,z\bar{z}
, then

A_{1}[P_{k}^{(\mu,j-k)}(t)]\,=\,2\left(P_{k}^{(\mu,j-k)}\right)^{\prime}(2r-1).

Then, from (
8
), we get

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle\,=\,(j+\mu+1)\frac{k!}{(\mu+1)_{k}}\bar{z}^{j-k+1}\,P_{k-1}^{(\mu+1,j-k+1)}(2z\bar{z}-1)

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{k\,(j+\mu+1)}{\mu+1}Q^{\mu+1}_{k-1,j}(z,\bar{z}),

and we obtain the first identity in (
16
).

Using (
4
) again, we compute

\displaystyle\left\{(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}-\mu\,z\right\}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle=\,\frac{k!\,\bar{z}^{j-k-1}}{(\mu+1)_{k}}\left[2(1-z\bar{z})z\bar{z}\left(P_{k}^{(\mu,j-k)}\right)^{\prime}+\left[(j-k)(1-z\bar{z})-\mu\,z\bar{z}\right]P_{k}^{(\mu,j-k)}\right](2z\bar{z}-1).

The expression in brackets is precisely
\frac{1}{2}A_{2}[P_{k}^{(\mu,j-k)}(t)]
under the same change of variable as before. Then, from (
8
) it follows that

\displaystyle\left\{(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial}{\partial z}-\mu\,z\right\}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle=\,\frac{1}{2}\frac{k!}{(\mu+1)_{k}}\,\bar{z}^{j-k-1}A_{2}[P_{k}^{(\mu,j-k)}](2z\bar{z}-1)

\displaystyle=\,-\frac{\,(k+1)!}{(\mu+1)_{k}}\,\bar{z}^{j-k-1}P_{k+1}^{(\mu-1,j-k-1)}(2z\bar{z}-1)

\displaystyle=\,-\mu\,Q^{\mu-1}_{k+1,j}(z,\bar{z}),

and the second identity in (
16
) follows.

We remark that this result can also be obtained by computing with (
3
) and using
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
instead of
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
, respectively. We omit the details.
∎

The following corollary is a consequence of the previous theorem and (
7
).

Corollary 4.2
.

The complex generalized Zernike polynomials satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,\frac{j\,(k+\mu+1)}{\mu+1}Q^{\mu+1}_{k,j-1}(z,\bar{z}),

\displaystyle\mu>-1,

(17)

\displaystyle\left\{(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}-\mu\,\bar{z}\right\}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,-\mu\,Q^{\mu-1}_{k,j+1}(z,\bar{z}),

\displaystyle\mu>0.

The ladder operators in the following theorem shift the parameter
\mu
by one unit, but keep the other two paramenter
k
and
j
unchanged and, hence, the total degree of the polynomials is preserved under the action of these operators.

Theorem 4.3
.

The complex generalized Zernike polynomials satisfy

\displaystyle\left\{z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+j+\mu+1\right\}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,\frac{(k+\mu+1)\,(j+\mu+1)}{\mu+1}Q^{\mu+1}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z}),

\displaystyle\mu>-1,

(18)

\displaystyle\left\{(1-z\bar{z})\,z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}-k\,(1-z\bar{z})-\mu\right\}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,-\mu\,Q^{\mu-1}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z}),

\displaystyle\mu>0.

Proof.

Using (
3
), we compute

\displaystyle\left\{z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+j+\mu+1\right\}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{j!}{(\mu+1)_{j}}z^{k-j}\left[2z\bar{z}\left(P_{j}^{(\mu,k-j)}\right)^{\prime}+(k+\mu+1)P_{j}^{(\mu,k-j)}\right](2z\bar{z}-1),

where the expression in brackets is
B_{1}[P_{j}^{(\mu,k-j)}(t)]
under the change of variables
t=2r-1
where
r\,=\,z\bar{z}
. By (
9
), we have

\displaystyle\left\{z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+j+\mu+1\right\}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{j!}{(\mu+1)_{j}}(k+\mu+1)z^{k-j}\,P_{j}^{(\mu+1,k-j)}(2z\bar{z}-1),

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{(k+\mu+1)\,(j+\mu+1)}{\mu+1}Q^{\mu+1}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z}).

Now, we compute

\displaystyle\left\{(1-z\bar{z})\,z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}-k\,(1-z\bar{z})-\mu\right\}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{j!}{(\mu+1)_{j}}z^{k-j}\left[2(1-z\bar{z})z\bar{z}\left(P_{j}^{(\mu,k-j)}\right)^{\prime}-\bigg(j\,(1-z\bar{z})+\alpha\bigg)\,P_{j}^{(\mu,k-j)}\right](2z\bar{z}-1).

The expression in brackets is
\frac{1}{2}B_{2}[P_{j}^{(\mu,k-j)}(t)]
under the same change of variable as before. Hence, from (
9
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left\{(1-z\bar{z})\,z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}-k\,(1-z\bar{z})-\mu\right\}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle\,=\,-(j+\alpha)\frac{j!}{(\mu+1)_{j}}z^{k-j}\,P_{j}^{(\mu-1,k-j)}(2z\bar{z}-1)\,=\,-\mu\,Q^{\mu-1}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z}).

We have now proved both equations in (
18
).
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

The complex generalized Zernike polynomials satisfy

\displaystyle\left\{\bar{z}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}+k+\mu+1\right\}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,\frac{(k+\mu+1)\,(j+\mu+1)}{\mu+1}Q^{\mu+1}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z}),

\displaystyle\mu>-1,

(19)

\displaystyle\left\{(1-z\bar{z})\,\bar{z}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}-j\,(1-z\bar{z})-\mu\right\}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,-\mu\,Q^{\mu-1}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z}),

\displaystyle\mu>0.

In contrast to the previous ladder operators, the follwing ones only shift the parameters
k
and
j
, and leave
\mu
unchanged.

Theorem 4.5
.

For
\mu>-1
, the complex generalized Zernike polynomials satisfy

\displaystyle\left\{(1-z\bar{z})\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+k\bar{z}\right\}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,k\,Q^{\mu}_{k-1,j}(z,\bar{z}),

(20)

\displaystyle\left\{(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}-(k+\mu+1)z\right\}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,-(k+\mu+1)\,Q^{\mu}_{k+1,j}(z,\bar{z}).

Proof.

From the representation (
3
), we get

\displaystyle\left\{(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}-(k+\mu+1)z\right\}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{j!}{(\mu+1)_{j}}z^{k-j+1}\left[2(1-z\bar{z})\left(P_{j}^{(\mu,k-j)}\right)^{\prime}-(k+\mu+1)P_{j}^{(\mu,k-j)}\right](2z\bar{z}-1).

Since the expression in brackets coincides with
C_{1}[P_{j}^{(\mu,k-j)}(t)]
where
t=2r-1
and
r\,=\,z\bar{z}
, it follows from (
11

\displaystyle\left\{(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}-(k+\mu+1)z\right\}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle\,=\,-(k+\mu+1)\frac{j!}{(\mu+1)_{j}}z^{k-j+1}P_{j}^{(\mu,k-j+1)}(2z\bar{z}-1)\,=\,-(k+\mu+1)\,Q^{\mu}_{k+1,j}(z,\bar{z}).

Similarly, we compute

\displaystyle\left\{(1-z\bar{z})\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+k\bar{z}\right\}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{j!}{(\mu+1)_{j}}z^{k-j-1}\left[2(1-z\bar{z})z\bar{z}\left(P_{j}^{(\mu,k-j)}\right)^{\prime}+(jz\bar{z}+k-j)P_{j}^{(\mu,k-j)}\right](2z\bar{z}-1).

Since the expression in brackets coincides with
\frac{1}{2}C_{2}[P_{j}^{(\mu,k-j)}(t)]
under the same change of variables as before, it follows from (
11

\displaystyle\left\{(1-z\bar{z})\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+k\bar{z}\right\}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{k\,j!}{(\mu+1)_{j}}z^{k-j-1}P_{j}^{(\mu,k-j-1)}(2z\bar{z}-1)

\displaystyle\,=\,k\,Q^{\mu}_{k-1,j}(z,\bar{z}).

This proves (
20

4

and

instead of

and

Corollary 4.6

(21)

Theorem 4.7

\displaystyle\left\{z\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z}-k\right\}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,\frac{k\,j}{\mu+1}\,Q^{\mu+1}_{k-1,j-1}(z,\bar{z}),

(22)

\displaystyle\left\{(1-z\bar{z})z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+(j+1)(1-z\bar{z})-\mu z\bar{z}\right\}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,-\mu\,Q^{\mu-1}_{k+1,j+1}(z,\bar{z}).

\displaystyle D_{1}[P_{k}^{(\mu,j-k)}(t)]\,=\,\left\{2z\bar{z}\left(P_{k}^{(\mu,j-k)}\right)^{\prime}-k\,P_{k}^{(\mu,j-k)}\right\}(2z\bar{z}-1),

\displaystyle D_{2}[P_{j}^{(\mu,k-j)}(t)]\,=\,\left\{4(1-z\bar{z})z\bar{z}\left(P_{j}^{(\mu,k-j)}\right)^{\prime}+2[(k+1)(1-z\bar{z})-\mu\,z\bar{z}]P_{j}^{(\mu,k-j)}\right\}(2z\bar{z}-1).

From here, we get

\displaystyle\left\{z\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z}-k\right\}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{k!}{(\mu+1)_{k}}\bar{z}^{j-k}D_{1}[P_{k}^{(\mu,j-k)}](2z\bar{z}-1)

\displaystyle\,=\,j\frac{k!}{(\mu+1)_{k}}\bar{z}^{j-k}P_{k-1}^{(\mu+1,j-k)}(2z\bar{z}-1)

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{k\,j}{\mu+1}\,Q^{\mu+1}_{k-1,j-1}(z,\bar{z}),

\displaystyle\left\{(1-z\bar{z})z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+(j+1)(1-z\bar{z})-\mu z\bar{z}\right\}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\,=\,\frac{1}{2}\frac{j!}{(\mu+1)_{j}}z^{k-j}D_{2}[P_{j}^{(\mu,k-j)}](2z\bar{z}-1)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\,=\,-(j+1)\frac{j!}{(\mu+1)_{j}}z^{k-j}P_{j+1}^{(\mu-1,k-j)}(2z\bar{z}-1)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\,=\,-\mu\,Q^{\mu-1}_{k+1,j+1}(z,\bar{z}),

5.
Differential and recurrence relations for
Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})

We can combine the ladder operators introduced in the previous section to deduce differential and recurrence relations satisfied by the complex generalized Zernike polynomials. Some of the relations involve polynomials with different parameters.

First, we define the following differential operator

L_{\mu}\,=\,2(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z\,\partial\bar{z}}-(\mu+1)\,\left(z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+\bar{z}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}\right).

Proposition 5.1
.

The complex generalized Zernike polynomials satisfy

L_{\mu}Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,\lambda_{k,j}^{\mu}\,Q^{\mu}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z}),

where
\lambda_{k,j}^{\mu}=-2kj-(\mu+1)(k+j)
.

Proof.

Observe that we can write

L_{\mu}\,=\,\left\{(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}-(\mu+1)z\right\}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+\left\{(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial}{\partial z}-(\mu+1)\bar{z}\right\}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}.

Then, the result follows from (
16
) and (
17
).
∎

Notice that the differential equation
L_{\mu}P\,=\,\lambda^{\mu}\,P
makes sense for
\mu\,=\,-1
. In fact, its polynomial solutions can be given in terms of the complex generalized Zernike polynomials (
[
12
]
). However, these polynomials solutions are not standard orthogonal polynomials (
[
4
]
).

Proposition 5.2
.

For
k,j\geqslant 0
, define

Q^{-1}_{0,0}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,1,\qquad Q^{-1}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,(1-z\bar{z})\,Q^{1}_{k-1,j-1}(z,\bar{z}),\quad k,j\geqslant 1.

(24)

Then
\{Q^{-1}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})\}_{k,j\geqslant 0}
satisfy

L_{-1}Q^{-1}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,\lambda^{-1}_{k,j}Q^{-1}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z}),

with
\lambda_{k,j}^{-1}\,=\,-2kj
.

Proof.

Note that

L_{-1}\,=\,2(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z\,\partial\bar{z}}.

Clearly, since
\lambda_{0,0}^{-1}\,=\,0
, we have
L_{-1}Q^{-1}_{0,0}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,\lambda^{-1}_{0,0}Q^{-1}_{0,0}(z,\bar{z})
. For
k,j\geqslant 1
, we have

\displaystyle L_{-1}Q^{-1}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle\,=\,2(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z\,\partial\bar{z}}(1-z\bar{z})Q^{1}_{k-1,j-1}(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle\,=\,(1-z\bar{z})\left\{2(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z\,\partial\bar{z}}-2\,\left(z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+\bar{z}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}\right)-2\right\}Q^{1}_{k-1,j-1}(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle\,=\,(1-z\bar{z})\left\{L_{1}-2\right\}Q^{1}_{k-1,j-1}(z,\bar{z}).

From Porposition
5.1
, we get

\displaystyle L_{-1}Q^{-1}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,(\lambda_{k-1,j-1}^{1}-2)Q^{-1}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,\lambda_{k,j}^{-1}Q^{-1}_{k,j}(z,\bar{z}),

which proves the announced result.
∎

We remark that it was shown in
[
12
]
that if
\mu\leqslant-2
is an integer, then
L_{\mu}
is not guaranteed to have a comples polynomial system solution (see also Remark 6.1 in
[
4
]
).

The basic three term relations for multivariate orthogonal polynomials (
[
3
]
) can be deduced for the complex generalized Zernike polynomials.

Proposition 5.3
.

The complex generalized Zernike polynomials satisfy the following three term relations

\displaystyle(k+j+\mu+1)z\,Q_{k,j}^{\mu}(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle\,=\,(k+\mu+1)\,Q_{k+1,j}^{\mu}(z,\bar{z})+j\,Q_{k,j-1}^{\mu}(z,\bar{z}),

\displaystyle(k+j+\mu+1)\bar{z}\,Q_{k,j}^{\mu}(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle\,=\,(j+\mu+1)\,Q_{k,j+1}^{\mu}(z,\bar{z})+k\,Q_{k-1,j}^{\mu}(z,\bar{z}).

Proof.

The result follows from writing

\displaystyle(k+j+\mu+1)z

\displaystyle\,=\,\left\{(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+j\,\bar{z}\right\}-\left\{(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial}{\partial z}-(k+\mu+1)\,\bar{z}\right\},

\displaystyle(k+j+\mu+1)\bar{z}

\displaystyle\,=\,\left\{(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}+k\,z\right\}-\left\{(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}-(j+\mu+1)\,z\right\},

and the using (
20
) and (
21
).
∎

We can also deduce the linear relation between families of complex generalized Zernike polynomials with parameters
\mu
and
\mu+1
. We remark that this linear relation is of fixed length for all values of
k
and
j
.

Proposition 5.4
.

The complex generalized Zernike polynomials satisfy

(k+j+\mu+1)\,Q_{k,j}^{\mu}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,\frac{(k+\mu+1)(j+\mu+1)}{\mu+1}Q_{k,j}^{\mu+1}(z,\bar{z})-\frac{k\,j}{\mu+1}Q_{k-1,j-1}^{\mu+1}(z,\bar{z}).

Proof.

Since

(k+j+\mu+1)\,Q_{k,j}^{\mu}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,\left\{z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+j+\mu+1\right\}Q_{k,j}^{\mu}(z,\bar{z})-\left\{z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}-k\right\}Q_{k,j}^{\mu}(z,\bar{z}),

the result follows from (
18
) and (
22
).
∎

Ladder operators can be combined to obtain the so-called structure relations, that is, linear relations of fixed length involving the partial derivatives of the polynomials.

Proposition 5.5
.

The complex generalized Zernike polynomials satisfy the following structure relations

(k+j+\mu+1)(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial}{\partial z}Q_{k,j}^{\mu}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,k(j+\mu+1)\left(Q_{k-1,j}^{\mu}(z,\bar{z})-Q_{k,j+1}^{\mu}(z,\bar{z})\right),

and

(k+j+\mu+1)(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}Q_{k,j}^{\mu}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,j(k+\mu+1)\left(Q_{k,j-1}^{\mu}(z,\bar{z})-Q_{k+1,j}^{\mu}(z,\bar{z})\right).

Proof.

The first structure relation follows from writing

(k+j+\mu+1)(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\,=\,k\left\{(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial}{\partial z}-(j+\mu+1)\bar{z}\right\}+(j+\mu+1)\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+k\bar{z}\right\},

and using (
20
) and (
21
). The second structure relation follows similarly.
∎

From the structure relations and (
16
), we get the following corollary.

Corollary 5.6
.

The complex generalized Zernike polynomials satisfy

(k+j+\mu+2)(1-z\bar{z})Q_{k,j}^{\mu+1}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,(\mu+1)\left(Q_{k,j}^{\mu}(z,\bar{z})-Q_{k+1,j+1}^{\mu}(z,\bar{z})\right).

Since the right hand side of the first equation in (
22
) and the first equation in (
23
) are equal, we readily get the following fundamental relation.

Proposition 5.7
.

The complex generalized Zernike polynomials satisfy

\left\{z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}-\bar{z}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}\right\}Q_{k,j}^{\mu}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,(k-j)\,Q_{k,j}^{\mu}(z,\bar{z}).

6.
A note about Sobolev orthogonality

Univariate orthogonal polynomials with respect to a Sobolev inner product (i.e., an inner product involving the derivatives of the polynomials) have been studied extensively in the past few decades. In contrast to one variable, Sobolev orthogonal polynomials of several variables are studied only recently. We refer the interested reader to the survey
[
7
]
for a recent presentation of the state of the art on Sobolev orthogonal polynomials. Several authors have taken an interest on Sobolev orthogonal polynomials on the unit ball of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
which, clearly, include the generalized Zernike polynomials when
n=2
(see, for instance,
[
1
,
2
,
5
,
6
,
8
,
9
,
11
,
18
,
17
]
). In this section, we illustrate the use of ladder operators in the study of Sobolev orthogonal polynomials on the disk.

6.1.
Example 1

Consider the following Sobolev inner product

(f,g)_{1}\,=\,\frac{\lambda}{\pi}\int_{\mathbf{D}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}(z,\bar{z})\,\overline{\frac{\partial g}{\partial z}(z,\bar{z})}\,dz+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(e^{i\theta},e^{-i\theta})\,\overline{g(e^{i\theta},e^{-i\theta})}\,d\theta,\quad\lambda>0.

We remark that this inner product is a complex version of one of the Sobolev inner products studied in
[
18
]
given by

(\tilde{f},\tilde{g})\,=\,\frac{\lambda}{\pi}\int_{\mathbf{D}}\nabla\tilde{f}(x,y)\cdot\nabla\tilde{g}(x,y)\,dxdy+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\tilde{f}(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\,\tilde{g}(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\,d\theta,\quad\lambda>0,

for real-valued functions
\tilde{f}(x,y)\,=\,f(z,\bar{z})
and
\tilde{g}(x,y)\,=\,g(z,\bar{z})
under the change of variable
z\,=\,x+iy\,=\,r\,e^{i\theta}
.

Now we use ladder operators for complex generalized Zernike polynomials to show that the polynomials defined in (
24
) are orthogonal with respect to
(\cdot,\cdot)_{1}
.

Lemma 6.1
.

The polynomials defined in (
24
) satisfy

\frac{\partial}{\partial z}Q_{k,j}^{-1}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,-Q_{k-1,j}^{0}(z,\bar{z}),\quad k,j\geqslant 1.

Proof.

We compute,

\frac{\partial}{\partial z}Q_{k,j}^{-1}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,\left\{(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial}{\partial z}-\bar{z}\right\}Q_{k-1,j-1}^{1}(z,\bar{z}).

Our result follows from (
17
).
∎

Proposition 6.2
.

The polynomials defined in (
24
) constitute a mutually orthogonal polynomial system with respect to
(\cdot,\cdot)_{1}
. Moreover,

(Q_{k,j}^{-1},Q_{m,\ell}^{-1})_{1}\,=\,\tilde{h}_{k,j}(\lambda)\,\delta_{k,m}\,\delta_{j,\ell},

with

\tilde{h}_{k,j}(\lambda)\,=\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2,&k=j=0,\\[10.0pt]
\lambda\,h^{0}_{k-1,j},&k,j\geqslant 1,\end{array}\right.

where
h^{0}_{k-1,j}
is given in (
6
).

Proof.

Clearly,

(Q_{0,0}^{-1},Q_{0,0}^{-1})_{1}\,=\,(1,1)_{1}\,=\,\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta\,=\,2.

For
k,j\geqslant 1
,

Q_{k,j}^{-1}(e^{i\theta},e^{-i\theta})\,=\,0,

since the factor
(1-z\bar{z})
vanishes when
z=e^{i\theta}
,
0\leqslant\theta\leqslant 2\pi
. Therefore,

(Q_{k,j}^{-1},Q_{0,0}^{-1})_{1}\,=\,0,\quad k,j\geqslant 0.

Using Lemma (
6.1
), we get

(Q_{k,j}^{-1},Q_{m,\ell}^{-1})_{1}\,=\,\frac{\lambda}{\pi}\int_{\mathbf{D}}Q_{k-1,j}^{0}(z,\bar{z})\,\overline{Q_{m-1,\ell}^{0}(z,\bar{z})}\,dz\,=\,\lambda\,h^{0}_{k-1,j}\,\delta_{k,m}\,\delta_{j,\ell},

where the last equality follows from (
5
).
∎

6.2.
Example 2

Now consider the following Sobolev inner product

(f,g)_{2}\,=\,\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbf{D}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z\partial\bar{z}}[(1-z\bar{z})f(z,\bar{z})]\,\overline{\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z\partial\bar{z}}[(1-z\bar{z})g(z,\bar{z})]}dz.

Recall that the two-dimensional Laplace operator can be represented in complex variables as

\Delta\,=\,4\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z\partial\bar{z}}.

Hence, for real-valued functions
\tilde{f}(x,y)\,=\,f(z,\bar{z})
and
\tilde{g}(x,y)\,=\,g(z,\bar{z})
, this inner product is equivalent to

(\tilde{f},\tilde{g})_{\Delta}\,=\,\frac{1}{16\pi}\int_{\mathbf{D}}\Delta[(1-x^{2}-y^{2})\tilde{f}(x,y)]\,\Delta[(1-x^{2}-y^{2})\tilde{g}(x,y)]\,dxdy.

A mutually orthogonal polynomial system with respect to
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\Delta}
is constructed and studied in
[
17
]
. Here, we study a complex mutually orthogonal polynomial system relative to
(\cdot,\cdot)_{2}
via ladder operators.

Define the polynomials

\displaystyle U_{0,0}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,1,\quad U_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,(1-z\bar{z})Q^{2}_{k-1,j-1}(z,\bar{z}),\quad k,j\geqslant 1.

(25)

Lemma 6.3
.

For
k,j\geqslant 0
, the polynomials defined in (
25
) satisfy

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z\partial\bar{z}}[(1-z\bar{z})U_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})]\,=\,c_{k,j}\,Q_{k,j}^{0}(z,\bar{z}),

with

c_{k,j}\,=\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{rr}-1,&k=j=0,\\
2,&k,j\geqslant 1,\end{array}\right.

and

(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z\partial\bar{z}}Q_{k,j}^{0}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,d_{k,j}U_{k,j}(z,\bar{z}),

with

d_{k,j}\,=\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&k=j=0,\\[10.0pt]
\dfrac{1}{2}k\,j\,(k+1)\,(j+1),&k,j\geqslant 1.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

We compute,

\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}[(1-z\bar{z})U_{0,0}(z,\bar{z})]\,=\,-z.

Then,

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z\partial\bar{z}}[(1-z\bar{z})U_{0,0}(z,\bar{z})]\,=\,-1\,=\,-Q_{0,0}^{0}(z,\bar{z}).

For
k,j\geqslant 1
, we compute

\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}[(1-z\bar{z})U_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})]\,=\,(1-z\bar{z})\left\{(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}-2z\right\}Q_{k-1,j-1}^{2}(z,\bar{z}).

It follows from (
16
) that

\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}[(1-z\bar{z})U_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})]\,=\,-2(1-z\bar{z})Q_{k,j-1}^{1}(z,\bar{z}).

Then,

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z\partial\bar{z}}[(1-z\bar{z})U_{k,j}(z,\bar{z})]\,=\,-2\left\{(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial}{\partial z}-\bar{z}\right\}Q_{k,j-1}^{1}(z,\bar{z})\,=\,2\,Q_{k,j}^{0}(z,\bar{z}),

where the last equality follows from (
17
).

Moreover, using (
16
) and (
17
), we obtain

\displaystyle(1-z\bar{z})\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z\partial\bar{z}}Q_{k,j}^{0}(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{1}{2}k\,j\,(k+1)\,(j+1)(1-z\bar{z})Q_{k-1,j-1}^{2}(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{1}{2}k\,j\,(k+1)\,(j+1)\,U_{k,j}(z,\bar{z}).

∎

Proposition 6.4
.

The polynomials defined in (
25
) constitute a mutually orthogonal polynomial system with respect to
(\cdot,\cdot)_{2}
. Moreover,

(U_{0,0},U_{0,0})_{2}\,=\,1,\quad(U_{k,j},U_{m,\ell})_{2}\,=\,\frac{4}{k+j+1}\,\delta_{k,m}\,\delta_{j,\ell},\quad k,j\geqslant 1.

Proof.

By Lemma (
6.3
), we have

(U_{k,j},U_{m,\ell})_{2}\,=\,c_{k,j}^{2}\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbf{D}}Q_{k,j}^{0}(z,\bar{z})\,\overline{Q_{m,\ell}^{0}(z,\bar{z})}\,dz\,=\,c_{k,j}^{2}\,h_{k,j}^{0}\,\delta_{k,m}\,\delta_{j,\ell},

where
h^{0}_{k,j}
is given in (
6
).
∎
