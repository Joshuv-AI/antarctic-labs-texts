Abstract
. The framework of graded semantics uses graded monads to capture behavioural equivalences of varying granularity, for example as found in the linear-time / branching-time spectrum, over general system types. We describe a generic Spoiler-Duplicator game for graded semantics that is extracted from the given graded monad, and may be seen as playing out an equational proof; instances include standard pebble games for simulation and bisimulation as well as games for trace-like equivalences and coalgebraic behavioural equivalence. Considerations on an infinite variant of such games lead to a novel notion of infinite-depth graded semantics . Under reasonable restrictions, the infinite-depth graded semantics associated to a given graded equivalence can be characterized in terms of a determinization construction for coalgebras under the equivalence at hand.

1.
Introduction

The classical linear-time / branching-time
spectrum
(
Glabbeek 1990
)
organizes a plethora of notions of
behavioural equivalence on labelled transition systems at various
levels of granularity ranging from (strong) bisimilarity to trace
equivalence. Similar spectra appear in other system types, e.g. on
probabilistic systems, again ranging from branching-time equivalence
such as probabilistic bisimilarity to linear-time ones such as
probabilistic trace equivalence
(
Jou and Smolka 1990
)
. While the
variation in system types (nondeterministic, probabilistic, etc.) is
captured within the framework of
universal
coalgebra

(
Rutten 2000
)
, the variation in the granularity of
equivalence, which we shall generally refer to as the
semantics

of systems, has been tackled, in coalgebraic generality, in a variety
of approaches
(
Hasuo
et al. 2007
;
Klin and Rot 2015
;
Jacobs
et al. 2015
;
Jacobs
et al. 2018
)
. One setting that
manages to accommodate large portions of the
linear-time / branching-time spectrum, notably including also
intermediate equivalences such as ready similarity, is based on

graded monads

(
Milius
et al. 2015
;
Dorsch
et al. 2019
;
Ford
et al. 2021
)
.

An important role in the theoretical and algorithmic treatment of a
behavioural equivalence is classically played by equivalence
games
(
Glabbeek 1990
;
Stirling 1999
)
, e.g. in partial-order
techniques
(
Huhn
et al. 1998
)
or in on-the-fly
equivalence checking
(
Hirschkoff 2001
)
. In the present
work, we contribute to
graded semantics
in the sense indicated
above by showing that, under mild conditions, we can extract from a
given graded monad a Spoiler-Duplicator game
(
Stirling 1999
)
that
characterizes the respective equivalence, i.e. ensures that two states
are equivalent under the semantics iff Duplicator wins the game.

As the name suggests, graded monads provide an
algebraic
view
on system equivalence; they correspond to
graded theories
,
i.e. algebraic theories equipped with a notion of
depth
on
their operations. It has been noticed early on
(
Milius
et al. 2015
)
that many
desirable properties of a semantics depend on this theory being

depth-1
, i.e. having only equations between terms that are
uniformly of depth 1. Standard examples include distribution of
actions over non-deterministic choice (trace semantics) or
monotonicity of actions w.r.t. the choice ordering
(similarity)
(
Dorsch
et al. 2019
)
. Put simply, our generic equivalence game
plays out an equational proof in a depth-1 equational theory in a
somewhat nontraditional manner: Duplicator starts a round by playing a set of equational assumptions
she claims to hold at the level of successors of the present state,
and Spoiler then challenges one of these assumptions.

In many concrete cases, the game can be rearranged in a straightforward
manner to let Spoiler move first as usual; in this view, the equational claims
of Duplicator roughly correspond to a short-term strategy determining the
responses she commits to playing after Spoiler’s next move. In particular,
the game instantiates, after such rearrangement, to the standard pebble
game for bisimilarity. We analyse additional cases, including similarity
and trace equivalence, in more detail. In the latter case, several natural
variants of the game arise by suitably restricting strategies played by
Duplicator.

It turns out that the game is morally played on a form of
pre-determinization of the given coalgebra, which lives in the
Eilenberg-Moore category of the zero-th level of the graded monad, and as
such generalizes a determinization construction that applies in
certain instances of coalgebraic language semantics of
automata
(
Jacobs
et al. 2015
)
. Under suitable conditions on the graded monad,
this pre-determinization indeed functions as an actual
determinization, i.e. it turns the graded semantics into standard
coalgebraic behavioural equivalence for a functor that we construct on
the Eilenberg-Moore category. This construction simultaneously
generalizes, for instance, the standard determinization of serial labelled
transition systems for trace equivalence and the identification of
similarity as behavioural equivalence for a suitable functor on
posets
(
Kapulkin
et al. 2012
)
(specialized to join
semilattices).

While graded semantics has so far been constrained to apply only to
finite-depth equivalences (finite-depth bisimilarity, finite trace
equivalence, etc.), we obtain, under the mentioned conditions on the
graded monad, a new notion of infinite-depth equivalence induced by a
graded semantics, namely via the (pre-)determinization. It turns out
the natural infinite version of our equivalence game captures
precisely this infinite-depth equivalence. This entails a fixpoint characterization of graded semantics on finite systems,
giving rise to perspectives for a generic algorithmic treatment.

Related Work.

Game characterizations of process
equivalences are an established theme in concurrency theory; they tend to be systematic but not generic
(
Glabbeek 1990
;
Chen and Deng 2008
)
.
Work on games for spectra of quantitative equivalences is positioned
similarly
(
Fahrenberg
et al. 2011
;
Fahrenberg and
Legay 2014
)
. The idea of developing
(bi)
simulation
games in coalgebraic generality goes back to work on branching-time
simulations based on relators
(
Baltag 2000
)
. There
is recent highly general work, conducted in a fibrational setting, on
so-called codensity games for various notions of
bisimilarity
(
Komorida et al. 2019
)
. The emphasis in this work
is on generality w.r.t. the measure of bisimilarity, covering,
e.g. two-valued equivalences, metrics, pre-orders, and topologies,
while, viewed through the lens of spectra of equivalences, the
focus remains on branching time. The style of the codensity game is
inspired by modal logic, in the spirit of coalgebraic Kantorovich
liftings
(
Baldan et al. 2018
;
Wild and
Schröder 2020
)
; Spoiler plays predicates thought of as arguments of modalities.
Work focused more specifically on games for Kantorovich-style
coalgebraic behavioural equivalence and behavioural
metrics
(
König and
Mika-Michalski 2018
)
similarly concentrates on
the branching-time case. A related game-theoretic characterization is
implicit in work on
\Lambda
-(bi)similarity
(
Gorín
and Schröder 2013
)
,
also effectively limited to branching-time. Comonadic game
semantics
(
Abramsky
et al. 2017
;
Abramsky and Shah 2018
;
Ó Conghaile
and Dawar 2021
)
proceeds in the opposite way
compared to the mentioned work and ours: It takes existing games as
the point of departure, and then aims to develop categorical models.

Graded semantics was developed in a line of work mentioned
above
(
Milius
et al. 2015
;
Dorsch
et al. 2019
;
Ford
et al. 2021
)
. The underlying notion of
graded monad stems from algebro-geometric work
(
Smirnov 2008
)

and was introduced into computer science (in substantially higher generality)
in work on the semantics of effects
(
Katsumata 2014
)
. Our pre-determinization
construction relates to work on coalgebras over algebras
(
Balan and Kurz 2011
)
.

The category of sets.

Unless explicitly mentioned otherwise, we will
work in the category
\mathbf{Set}
of sets and functions
(or
maps
), which is both complete and
cocomplete. We fix a terminal object
1=\{\star\}

and use
!_{X}
(or just
!
if confusion is
unlikely) for the unique map
X\to 1
.

In the subsequent sections, we will mostly draw examples from (slight
modifications of) the following (endo-)functors on
\mathbf{Set}
. The

powerset functor

\mathscr{P}
sends each set
X
to its set of
subsets
\mathscr{P}X
, and acts on a map
f\colon X\to Y
by taking direct
images, i.e.
\mathscr{P}f(S):=f[S]
for
S\in\mathscr{P}X
.
We write
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}
for the
finitary powerset functor
which sends
each set to its set of finite subsets; the action of
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}
on maps is
again given by taking direct images. Similarly,
\mathscr{P}^{+}
denotes the
non-empty powerset functor
(
\mathscr{P}^{+}(X)=\{Y\in\mathscr{P}(X)\mid Y\neq\emptyset\}
), and
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}^{+}
its
finitary subfunctor
(
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}^{+}(X)=\{Y\in\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}(X)\mid Y\neq\emptyset\}
).

We write
\mathcal{D}X
for the set of
distributions
on a set
X
:
maps
\mu\colon X\to[0,1]
such that
\sum_{x\in X}\mu(x)=1
. A
distribution
\mu
is
finitely supported
if the set

\{x\in X~|~\mu(x)\neq 0\}
is finite. The set of finitely supported
distributions on
X
is denoted
\mathcal{D}_{f}X
. The assignment

X\mapsto\mathcal{D}X
is the object-part of a functor: given

f\colon X\to Y
, the map
\mathcal{D}f\colon\mathcal{D}X\to\mathcal{D}Y
assigns to a
distribution
\mu\in\mathcal{D}X
the
image
distribution

\mathcal{D}f(\mu)\colon Y\to[0,1]
defined by

\mathcal{D}f(\mu)(y)=\sum_{x\in X\mid f(x)=y}\mu(x)
. Then,

\mathcal{D}f(\mu)
is finitely supported if
\mu
is, so
\mathcal{D}_{f}
is
functorial as well.

Coalgebra.

We will review the
basic definitions and results of
universal
coalgebra

(
Rutten 2000
)
, a categorical framework for the uniform
treatment of a variety of reactive system types.

Definition 2.1.

For an endofunctor
G\colon\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{C}
on a
category
\mathscr{C}
, a
G
-coalgebra
(or just

coalgebra
) is a pair
(X,\gamma)
consisting
of an object
X
in
\mathscr{C}
and a morphism

\gamma\colon X\to GX
.
A
(coalgebra) morphism
from
(X,\gamma)
to
a coalgebra
(Y,\delta)
is a morphism
h\colon X\to Y

such that
\delta\cdot h=Fh\cdot\gamma
.

Thus, for
\mathscr{C}=\mathbf{Set}
, a coalgebra consists of a set
X

of
states
and a map
\gamma\colon X\to GX
, which we view as a
transition structure that assigns to each state
x\in X
a structured
collection
\gamma(x)\in GX
of
successors
in
X
.

Example 2.2.

We describe some examples of functors on
\mathbf{Set}
and their coalgebras
for consideration in the subsequent. Fix a finite set
\mathcal{A}
of

actions
.

(1)

Coalgebras for the functor
G=\mathscr{P}(\mathcal{A}\times-)

are just
\mathcal{A}
-labelled transition systems (LTS)
: Given such a
coalgebra
(X,\gamma)
, we can view the elements

(a,y)\in\gamma(x)
as the
a
-successors of
x
. We call

(X,\gamma)

finitely branching
(resp.
serial

if
\gamma(x)
is finite (resp. non-empty) for all
x\in X
.
Finitely branching (resp. serial) LTS are coalgebras for
the functor
G=\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}(\mathcal{A}\times-)
(resp.
\mathscr{P}^{+}(\mathcal{A}\times-)
).

(2)

A coalgebra
(X,\gamma)
for the functor
G=\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}\times-)

is a
(generative) probabilistic transition system
(PTS): The
transition structure
\gamma
assigns to each state
x\in X
a
distribution
\gamma(x)
on pairs
(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times X
. We think of

\gamma(x)(a,y)
as the probability of executing an
a
-transition
to state
y
while sitting in state
x
.
A PTS
(X,\gamma)
is
finitely branching
if
\gamma(x)
is
finitely supported for all
x\in X
; then, finitely branching PTS
are coalgebras for
\mathcal{D}_{f}(\mathcal{A}\times-)
.

Given coalgebras
(X,\gamma)
and
(Y,\delta)
for an endofunctor

G
on
\mathbf{Set}
, states
x\in X
and
y\in Y
are

G
-behaviourally equivalent
if there exist coalgebra morphisms

(X,\gamma)\xrightarrow{~f~}(Z,\zeta)\xleftarrow{~g~}(Y,\delta)

such that
f(x)=g(y)
. Behavioural equivalence can be approximated
via the (initial
\omega
-segment of the)
final chain

(G^{n}1)_{n\in\omega}
, where
G^{n}
denotes
n
-fold application of

G
. The
canonical cone
of a coalgebra

(X,\gamma)
is then the family of maps
\gamma_{n}\colon X\to G^{n}1

defined inductively for
n\in\omega
by

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}

\displaystyle=\big(X\xrightarrow{~!~}1\big),\text{and}

\displaystyle\gamma_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\big(X\xrightarrow{~\gamma~}GX\xrightarrow{~G\gamma_{n}~}GG^{n}1=G^{n+1}1\big).

States
x,y\in X
are
finite-depth behaviourally equivalent
if

\gamma_{n}(x)=\gamma_{n}(y)
for all
n\in\omega
.

Remark 2.3.

It follows from results of Worrell
(
Worrell 2005
)
that behavioural
equivalence and finite-depth behavioural equivalence coincide for
finitary functors on
\mathbf{Set}
, where a functor
G
on
\mathbf{Set}
is

finitary
if it preserves filtered colimits. Equivalently, for
every set
X
and each
x\in GX
there exists a finite subset
Y\subseteq X
such that
x=Gi[GY]
, where
i\colon Y\hookrightarrow X
is
the inclusion map
(
Adámek et al. 2019
, Cor. 3.3)
.

Bisimilarity games.

We briefly recapitulate the classical
bisimilarity game
, a
two-player graph game between the players Duplicator (D) and Spoiler
(S); player D tries to show that two given states are bisimilar,
while S tries to refute this.
Configurations
of the game are
pairs
(x,y)\in X\times X
of states in a LTS
(X,\gamma)
. The game
proceeds in rounds, starting from the
initial configuration
,
which is just the contested pair of states. In each round, starting
from a configuration
(x,y)
, S picks one of the sides, say,
x
, and
then selects an action
a\in\mathcal{A}
and an
a
-successor
x^{\prime}
of
x
;
player D then selects a corresponding successor on the other side, in
this case an
a
-successor
y^{\prime}
of
y
. The game then reaches the new
configuration
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
. If a player gets stuck, the play is

winning
for their opponent, whereas any infinite play is
winning for D.

It is well known (e.g.
(
Stirling 1999
)
) that D has a
winning strategy in the bisimilarity game at a configuration
(x,y)

iff
(x,y)
is a pair of bisimilar states. Moreover, for finitely
branching LTS, an equivalent formulation may be given in terms of the

n
-round bisimilarity game
:
the rules of the
n
-round game are the same as those above, only
now D wins as soon as at most
n
rounds have been played. In fact, a
configuration
(x,y)
is a bisimilar pair precisely if D has
a winning strategy in the
n
-round bisimilarity game for all

n\in\omega
.

We mention just one obvious variation of this game that characterizes
a different spot on the linear-time/branching-time spectrum: The

mutual-simulation game
is set up just like the bisimulation
game, except that S may only choose his side once, in the first round,
and then has to move on that side in all subsequent rounds (in the
bisimulation game, he can switch sides in every round if he
desires). It is easily checked that states
x,y
are mutually similar
iff S wins the position
(x,y)
in the mutual-simulation game. We will
see that both these games (and many others) are obtained
as instances of our generic notion of graded equivalence game.

Graded monads.

We now review some background material on graded monads

(
Smirnov 2008
;
Milius
et al. 2015
)

Definition 2.4.

A
graded monad

\mathbb{M}
on a category
\mathscr{C}
is a triple

(M,\eta,\mu)
where
M
is a family of functors

M_{n}\colon\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{C}
on
\mathscr{C}
(
n\in\omega
),

\eta\colon\mathsf{id}\to M_{0}
is a natural transformation (the

unit
), and
\mu
is a family of natural transformations

(
n,k\in\omega
)

\mu^{n,k}\colon M_{n}M_{k}\to M_{n+k}

(the
multiplication
) such that the following diagrams commute
for all
n,m,k\in\omega
:

(2.1)

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu^{0,n}}

) and (

)
as the

and

\mathbb{M}
,
respectively. We call
\mathbb{M}

The above notion of graded monad is due to Smirnov
(
Smirnov 2008
)
.
Katsumata
(
Katsumata 2014
)
, Fujii et al.
(

)
, and

(

consider a more general notion of graded (or

\mathscr{M}
(representing the system of grades) on a

\mathscr{C}
. Graded monads in the above sense are recovered by

\mathscr{M}
to be the (discrete category induced by the) monoid

(\mathds{N},+,0)
.

The graded monad laws imply that the triple
(M_{0},\eta,\mu^{0,0})

is a (plain) monad on the base category
\mathscr{C}
; we use this freely
without further mention.

Example 2.5.

We review some salient constructions
(
Milius
et al. 2015
)
of graded
monads on
\mathbf{Set}
for later use.

(1)

Every endofunctor
G
on
\mathbf{Set}

induces a graded monad
\mathbb{M}_{G}
with underlying endofunctors

M_{n}:=G^{n}
(the
n
-fold composite of
G
with itself); the unit

\eta_{X}\colon X\to G^{0}X=X
and multiplication

\mu_{X}^{n,k}\colon G^{n}G^{k}X\to G^{n+k}X

We will later see that
\mathbb{M}_{G}
captures (finite-depth)

-behavioural

(2)

Let
(T,\eta,\mu)
be a monad on
\mathbf{Set}
, let
F
be

\mathbf{Set}
, and let
\lambda\colon FT\to TF

be a natural transformation such that

\lambda\cdot F\eta=\eta F\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lambda\cdot F\mu=\mu F\cdot T\lambda\cdot\lambda T

\lambda
is a distributive law of the functor
F
over the

T
). For each
n\in\omega
, let

\lambda^{n}\colon F^{n}T\to TF^{n}

\lambda^{0}:=\mathsf{id}_{T};\qquad\lambda^{n+1}:=\lambda^{n}F\cdot F^{n}\lambda.

M_{n}:=TF^{n}
, unit
\eta
, and
components
\mu^{n,k}
of the multiplication given as the

TF^{n}TF^{k}\xrightarrow{~T\lambda^{n}F^{k}~}TTF^{n}F^{k}=TTF^{n+k}\xrightarrow{~\mu F^{n+k}~}TF^{n+k}.

(3)

We obtain (by instance of the example above) a graded monad

on

\mathbf{Set}

. Thus,

is a graded monad for

; e.g. for

, we will
see that
\mathbb{M}_{T}(\mathcal{A})
captures probabilistic trace equivalence on
PTS.

(4)

Similarly, given a monad

, an
endofunctor
F
, both on the same category
\mathscr{C}
, and a
distributive law
\lambda\colon TF\to FT
of

over
F
, we
obtain a graded monad with
M_{n}:=F^{n}T
, unit and
multiplication given analogously as in
item
(2)
above
(see
(
Milius
et al. 2015
, Ex. 5.2.6)
). Such graded monads relate strongly
to Eilenberg-Moore-style coalgebraic language semantics
(

et al. 2013
)
.

Graded theories.

Graded theories, in a generalized form in which arities of operations
are not restricted to be finite, have been proved equivalent to graded
monads on
\mathbf{Set}

(
Milius
et al. 2015
)
(the finitary case was implicitly
covered already by Smirnov
(
Smirnov 2008
)
). We work primarily with
the finitary theories below; we consider infinitary variants of such
theories only when considering infinite-depth equivalences
(
Section

7
).

Definition 2.7.

(1)

A
graded signature
is a set
\Sigma

operations

\mathsf{ar}(f)\in\omega

An operation of arity 0 is

.

(2)

Let

be a set of

and let

of

is a term of uniform depth

and, for

is a
\Sigma

of uniform depth

(3)

A

\Sigma

Notation 2.8.

A
uniform-depth substitution
is a map

,
where
k\in\omega
and
X,Y
are sets.
Then
\sigma
extends to a family of maps

(

) defined recursively by

\bar{\sigma}_{n}(f(t_{1},\dots,t_{\mathsf{ar}(f)}))=f(\bar{\sigma}_{m}(t_{1}),\dots,\bar{\sigma}_{m}(t_{\mathsf{ar}(f)})),

where
t_{i}\in\Terms_{\!\Sigma,m}
and

. For a term

we also write

Given a graded theory
\mathcal{T}=(\Sigma,\mathcal{E})
, we have essentially the
standard notion of equational derivation (sound and complete over
graded algebras, cf.
Section

), restricted to
uniform-depth equations. Specifically, the system includes the
expected rules for reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity, and
congruence, and moreover allows substituted introduction of axioms: If

is in
\mathcal{E}
and
\sigma
is a uniform-depth substitution, then
derive the (uniform-depth) equation

. (A substitution
rule that more generally allows uniform-depth substitution into
derived equations is then admissible.) For a set

of uniform-depth
equations, we write

s=t
is derivable from equations in

\mathcal{E}
, the
equations in

cannot be substituted into in such a derivation (they
constitute assumptions on the variables occurring in

).

We then see that
\mathcal{T}
induces a graded monad

\Terms_{\!\Sigma,n}(X)
modulo derivable
equality under

given by the inclusion of variables as depth-0 terms and the

monad arises from a graded theory in this way

6.
Behavioural Equivalence Games

Let
\mathcal{S}=(\alpha,\mathbb{M})
be a depth-1 graded semantics for an
endofunctor
G
on
\mathbf{Set}
. We are going to describe a game for playing
out depth-
n
behavioural equivalence under
\mathcal{S}
-semantics on
states in
G
-coalgebras.

We first give a description of the game in the syntactic language of
graded equational reasoning, and then present a more abstract
categorical definition. Given a coalgebra
(X,\gamma)
, we will see
the states in
X
as variables, and the map
\alpha_{X}\cdot\gamma
as
assigning to each variable
x
a depth-1 term over
X
; we can regard
this assignment as a (uniform-depth) substitution
\sigma
. A
configuration of the game is a pair of depth-0 terms over
X
; to play
out the equivalence of states
x,y\in X
, the game is started from the
initial configuration
(x,y)
. Each round of the game then proceeds
in two steps: First, Duplicator plays a set
Z
of equalities between
depth-0 terms over
X
that she claims to hold under the
semantics. This move is admissible in the configuration
(s,t)

if
Z\vdash s\sigma=t\sigma
. Then, Spoiler challenges one of the
equalities claimed by Duplicator, i.e. picks an
element
(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})\in Z
, which then becomes the next configuration. Any
player who cannot move, loses. After
n
rounds have been played,
reaching the final configuration
(s,t)
, Duplicator wins if

s\theta=t\theta
is a valid equality, where
\theta
is a
substitution that identifies all variables. We refer to this last
check as
calling the bluff
. Thus, the game plays out an
equational proof between terms obtained by unfolding depth-0 terms
according to
\sigma
, cutting off after
n
steps.

We introduce some technical notation to capture the notion of
admissibility of
Z
abstractly:

Notation 6.1.

Let
Z\subseteq M_{0}X\times M_{0}X
be a relation, and let

c_{Z}\colon M_{0}X\to C_{Z}
be the coequalizer in
\Alg_{0}(\mathbb{M})
of the
homomorphisms
\ell_{0}^{*},r_{0}^{*}\colon M_{0}Z\to M_{0}X
given by
applying the Kleisli star (
2.3
) to the
projections
\ell,r\colon Z\to M_{0}X
. We define a homomorphism

\mathrlap{\hskip 1.75pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\hphantom{$Z$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}Z\colon M_{0}X\to M_{1}C_{Z}
in
\Alg_{0}(\mathbb{M})
by

(6.1)

\mathrlap{\hskip 1.75pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\hphantom{$Z$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}Z=\big(M_{0}X\xrightarrow{~(\alpha_{X}\cdot\gamma)^{*}_{0}~}M_{1}X=\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{\hphantom{$M$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}M_{1}M_{0}X\xrightarrow{~\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-2.5muM\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{1}c_{Z}~}\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{\hphantom{$M$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}M_{1}C_{Z}\big)

(omitting algebra structures, and again using the Kleisli star).

Remark 6.2.

Using designators as in
6.1
, we note:

(1)

By the universal property of

, an

associated to the relation

may be understood as
follows. As per the discussion above, we view the states of the

\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{\hphantom{$M$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}M_{1}c_{Z}

in (

) then essentially applies the relations given by
the closure of

under congruence w.r.t. depth-0 operations,
embodied in

as per
(1)
, under depth-1 operations
in (equivalence classes of) of depth-1 terms in

\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{\hphantom{$M$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}M_{1}c_{Z}

in a depth-1 theory presenting

(in notation as

Definition 6.3.

For

n

\mathcal{S}
-behavioural

\mathcal{G}_{n}(\gamma)

G
-coalgebra

Configurations

(s,t)\in M_{0}(X)\times M_{0}(X)

: First, D chooses a relation

Remark 6.4.

By the description of
\mathrlap{\hskip 1.75pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\hphantom{$Z$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}Z

Remark 6.5.

Soundness and completeness of the game with respect to

Theorem 6.6

Let

G

\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{\hphantom{$M$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}M_{1}
preserves monomorphisms, and let
(X,\gamma)
be
a

in

\mathcal{G}_{n}(\gamma)

.

Corollary 6.7
.

States
x,y
in a
G

(X,\gamma)
are

\mathcal{S}

(\eta(x),\eta(y))
for all

.

Remark 6.8.

In algebraic terms, the condition that
\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{\hphantom{$M$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}M_{1}
preserves
monomorphisms amounts to the following: In the derivation of an
equality of depth-1 terms

over
X
from depth-0 relations
over
X
(i.e. from a presentation of an
M_{0}
-algebra by relations
on generators
X
), if
X
is included in a larger set
Y
of
variables with relations that conservatively extend those on
X
,
i.e. do not imply additional relations on
X
, then it does not
matter whether the derivation is conducted over
X
or more
liberally over
Y
. Intuitively, this property is needed because not
all possible
n
-step behaviours, i.e. elements of
Y=M_{n}1
, are
realized by states in a given coalgebra on

. Preservation of
monos by
\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{\hphantom{$M$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}M_{1}
is automatic for graded monads of the form
\mathbb{M}_{G}

(
2.5
.
(1)
), since
M_{0}=\mathsf{Id}

in this case. In the other running examples, preservation of monos
is by the respective descriptions of
\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{\hphantom{$M$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}M_{1}
given in

5.3
.

Example 6.9.

We take a brief look at the instance of the generic game for the
case of bisimilarity on finitely branching LTS (more extensive
examples are in
Section

8
), i.e. we consider the depth-1
graded semantics
(\mathsf{id},\mathbb{M}_{G})
for the functor

. In this case,
M_{0}=\mathsf{Id}
, so when playing on
a coalgebra
(X,\gamma)
, D plays relations

. If the successor structures of states

are represented by
depth-1 terms
\sum_{i}a_{i}(x_{i})
and

,
respectively, in the theory
\JSL(\mathcal{A})

(

.
(1)
), then D is allowed to

iff the equality

7.
Infinite-depth behavioural
equivalence

We have seen in
Section

5
that in case

M_{0}1=1
,
(\alpha,\mathbb{M})
-behavioural equivalence
on
G
-coalgebras coincides, via a determization construction, with
finite-depth behavioural equivalence on
\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{\hphantom{$M$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}M_{1}
-coalgebras for a
functor
\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{\hphantom{$M$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}M_{1}
on
M_{0}
-algebras constructed from
\mathbb{M}
. If
G
is
finitary, then finite-depth behavioural equivalence coincides with
full behavioural equivalence (
2.3
), but in
general, finite-depth behavioural equivalence is strictly
coarser. Previous treatments of graded semantics stopped at this
point, in the sense that for non-finitary functors (which describe
infinitely branching systems), they did not offer a handle on
infinite-depth equivalences such as full bisimilarity.
In case
M_{0}1=1
, a candidate for a notion
of infinite-depth equivalence induced by a graded semantics arises via
full behavioural equivalence of
\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{\hphantom{$M$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}M_{1}
-coalgebras. We fix this notion
explicitly:

Definition 7.1.

States
x,y
in a
G
-coalgebra
(X,\gamma)
are

infinite-depth
(\alpha,\mathbb{M}
)-behaviourally equivalent

if
\eta(x)
and
\eta(y)
are behaviourally equivalent in the
pre-determinization of
(X,\gamma)
as described in

Section

6
.

We hasten to re-emphasize that this notion in general only
makes sense in case
M_{0}1=1
. We proceed to show that infinite-depth
equivalence is in fact captured by an infinite variant of the
behavioural equivalence game of
Section

6
.

Since infinite depth-equivalences differ from finite-depth ones only
in settings with infinite branching, we do not assume in this section
that
G
or
\mathbb{M}
are finitary, and correspondingly work with
generalized graded theories where operations may have infinite
arities
(
Milius
et al. 2015
)
; we assume arities to be cardinal numbers. We
continue to be interested only in depth-1 graded monads and theories,
and we fix such a graded monad
\mathbb{M}
and associated graded theory for the
rest of this section. The notion of derivation is essentially the same
as in the finitary case, the most notable difference being that the
congruence rule is now infinitary, as it has one premise for each
argument position of a given possibly infinitary operator. We do not
impose any cardinal bound on the arity of operations; if all
operations have arity less than
\kappa
for a regular
cardinal
\kappa
, then we say that the monad is

\kappa
-ary
.

Remark 7.2.

One can show using tools from the theory of locally presentable
categories that
\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{\hphantom{$M$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}M_{1}
has a final coalgebra if
\mathbb{M}
is

\kappa
-ary in the above sense. To see this, first note that

\Alg_{0}(\mathbb{M})
is locally
\kappa
-presentable if
M_{0}
is

\kappa
-accessible
(
Adámek and
Rosický 1994
, Remark 2.78)
. Using a somewhat
similar argument one can prove that
\Alg_{1}(\mathbb{M})
is also locally

\kappa
-presentable. Moreover, the functor
\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{\hphantom{$M$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}M_{1}
is

\kappa
-accessible, being the composite (
5.1
) of the left
adjoint
E\colon\Alg_{0}(\mathbb{M})\to\Alg_{1}(\mathbb{M})
(which preserves all
colimits) and the
1
-part functor

(-)_{1}\colon\Alg_{1}(\mathbb{M})\to\Alg_{0}(\mathbb{M})
, which preserves

\kappa
-filtered colimits since those are formed componentwise.
It follows that
\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{\hphantom{$M$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}M_{1}
has a final
coalgebra
(
Adámek and
Rosický 1994
, Exercise 2j)
. Alternatively, existence of a
final
\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{\hphantom{$M$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}M_{1}
-coalgebra will follow from
7.12

below.

Like before, we
assume that
\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{\hphantom{$M$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}M_{1}
preserves monomorphisms
.

Example 7.3.

We continue to use largely the same example theories as in

2.10
, except that we allow operations to be
infinitary. For instance, the
graded theory of complete join
semilattices over
\mathcal{A}
has as depth-1 operations all formal sums

\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}(-)
where
I
is now some (possibly infinite)
index set; the axioms are then given in the same way as in

2.10
.
(1)
, and all depth-1
equations

\textstyle\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}(x)=\sum_{j\in J}b_{j}(y)

such that
\{(a_{i},x_{i})\mid i\in I\}=\{(b_{j},y_{j})\mid j\in J\}
.
This theory presents the graded monad
\mathbb{M}_{G}
for

G=\mathscr{P}(\mathcal{A}\times(-))
.

The infinite game may then be seen as defining a notion of
derivable equality on infinite-depth terms by playing out a
non-standard, infinite-depth equational proof; we will make this view
explicit further below. In a less explicitly syntactic version, the
game is defined as follows.

Definition 7.4 (Infinite behavioural equivalence game).

The
infinite
(\alpha,\mathbb{M}
)-behavioural equivalence game

\mathcal{G}_{\infty}(\gamma)
on a
G
-coalgebra
(X,\gamma)
is played by
Spoiler (S) and Duplicator (D) in the same way as the finite
behavioural equivalence game (
6.3
) except that the
game continues forever unless one of the players cannot move. Any
player who cannot move, loses. Infinite matches are won by D.

As indicated above, this game captures infinite-depth

(\alpha,\mathbb{M})
-behavioural equivalence (under the running assumption
that
\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{\hphantom{$M$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}M_{1}
preserves monomorphisms):

Theorem 7.5
.

Given a
G
-coalgebra
(X,\gamma)
, two states
s,t
in the
pre-determinization of
\gamma
are behaviourally equivalent iff D
wins the infinite
(\alpha,\mathbb{M}
)-behavioural equivalence game

\mathcal{G}_{\infty}(\gamma)
from the initial configuration
(s,t)
.

Corollary 7.6
.

Two states
x,y
in a
G
-coalgebra
(X,\gamma)
are infinite-depth

(\alpha,\mathbb{M})
-behaviourally equivalent iff D wins the infinite

(\alpha,\mathbb{M}
)-behavioural equivalence game
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}(\gamma)

from the initial configuration
(\eta(x),\eta(y))
.

Remark 7.7.

Like infinite-depth
(\alpha,\mathbb{M})
-behavioural equivalence, the
infinite
(\alpha,\mathbb{M}
)-behavioural equivalence game is sensible only
in case
M_{0}1=1
. For instance, as noted in

Section

5
, in the graded monad for trace
semantics (
2.10
.
(2)
), which does not
satisfy this condition, behavioural equivalence of

\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{\hphantom{$M$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}M_{1}
-coalgebras is trivial. In terms of the game, D wins every
position in
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}(\gamma)
by playing

Z=\{(t,0)\mid t\in M_{0}X\}
– since the actions preserve the bottom
element
0
, this is always an admissible move. In the terminology
introduced at the beginning of
Section

6
, the reason that D
wins in this way is that in the infinite game, her bluff is never
called (
M_{0}!(t)
will in general not equal
M_{0}!(0)=0
). However, see

7.8
.
(1)
below.

Example 7.8.

(1)

As noted in

5.2
.
(1)
, the graded
monad for trace semantics can be modified to satisfy the
condition
M_{0}1=1
by restricting to serial labelled transition
systems.
By modification of
5.3
.
(2)
, we
obtain that in this setting,
\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{\hphantom{$M$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}M_{1}X
consists of partial maps

\mathcal{A}\rightharpoonup X
that are not everywhere undefined. It
follows that in this case, infinite-depth

(\alpha,\mathbb{M})
-behavioural equivalence is just finite trace
equivalence, and thus coincides with plain

(\alpha,\mathbb{M})
-behavioural equivalence.

(2)

In the case of graded monads
\mathbb{M}_{G}

(
2.5
.
(1)
), which so far
were used to capture finite-depth behavioural equivalence in the

; in particular,

G
-coalgebras. Effectively, a winning strategy
of D in the infinite game

on a

amounts to a
relation
R\subseteq X\times X
(the positions of D actually

)
.

Remark 7.9 (Fixpoint computation).

Via its game characterization (
7.5
),
infinite-depth
(\alpha,\mathbb{M})
-behavioural equivalence can be cast as
a greatest fixpoint, specifically of the monotone function
F
on

\mathscr{P}(M_{0}X\times M_{0}X)
given by

F(Z)=\{(s,t)\in M_{0}X\times M_{0}X\mid\mathrlap{\hskip 1.75pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\hphantom{$Z$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}Z(s)=\mathrlap{\hskip 1.75pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\hphantom{$Z$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}Z(t)\}.

If
M_{0}
preserves finite sets, then this fixpoint can be computed
on a finite coalgebra
(X,\gamma)
by fixpoint iteration; since

F(Z)
is clearly always an equivalence relation, the iteration
converges after at most
|M_{0}X|
steps, e.g. in exponentially many
steps in case
M_{0}=\mathscr{P}
. In case
M_{0}X
is infinite (e.g. if

M_{0}=\mathcal{D}
), then one will need to work with finite representations
of subspaces of
M_{0}X\times M_{0}X
. We leave a more careful analysis
of the algorithmics and complexity of solving infinite

(\alpha,\mathbb{M})
-behavioural equivalence games to future work. We do
note that on finite coalgebras, we may assume w.l.o.g. that both the
coalgebra functor
G
and graded monad
\mathbb{M}
are finitary, as we can
replace them with their finitary parts if needed (e.g. the powerset
functor
\mathscr{P}
and the finite powerset functor
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}
have
essentially the same finite coalgebras). If additionally
M_{0}1=1
,
then
(\alpha,\mathbb{M})
-behavioural equivalence coincides with
infinite-depth
(\alpha,\mathbb{M})
-behavioural equivalence, so that we
obtain also an algorithmic treatment of
(\alpha,\mathbb{M})
-behavioural
equivalence. By comparison, such a treatment is not immediate from
the finite version of the game, in which the number of rounds is
effectively chosen by Spoiler in the beginning.

Assume from now on that
\mathbb{M}
is
\kappa
-ary.
We note that in this case, we can describe the final
\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{\hphantom{$M$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}M_{1}
-coalgebra
in terms of a syntactic variant of the infinite game that is played on
infinite-depth terms, defined as follows.

Definition 7.10 (Infinite-depth terms).

Recall that we are assuming a graded signature
\Sigma
with
operations of arity less than
\kappa
. A
(uniform)
infinite-depth
(
\Sigma
-)

is an infinite tree with
ordered branching where each node is labelled with an
operation

by the arity of
f

will conflate nodes with (occurrences of) operations. We require
moreover that every infinite path in the tree contains infinitely
many depth-1 operations (finite full paths necessarily end in
constants). We write
\Terms_{\!\Sigma,\infty}
for the set of
infinite-depth
\Sigma
-terms. By cutting off at the top-most
depth-1 operations, we obtain for every
t\in\Terms_{\!\Sigma,\infty}

a
top-level decomposition

t=t_{1}\sigma
into a depth-1 term

t_{1}\in\Terms_{\!\Sigma,1}(X)
, for some set
X
, and a substitution

\sigma\colon X\to\Terms_{\!\Sigma,\infty}
.

Definition 7.11.

The
syntactic infinite
(\alpha,\mathbb{M}
)-behavioural equivalence
game

\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{syn}}_{\infty}
is played by S and D. Configurations of
the game are pairs
(s,t)
of infinite-depth
\Sigma
-terms. For
such
(s,t)
, we can assume, by the running assumption that
\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{\hphantom{$M$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}M_{1}

preserves monomorphisms, that the top level decompositions

s=s_{1}\sigma
,
t=t_{1}\sigma
are such that

s_{1},t_{1}\in\Terms_{\!\Sigma,1}(X)
,

\sigma\colon X\to\Terms_{\!\Sigma,\infty}
for the
same
X,\sigma
. Starting from a designated initial configuration,
the game proceeds in rounds. In each round, starting from a current
such configuration
(s,t)
, D first chooses a
relation
Z\subseteq\Terms_{\!\Sigma,0}(X)\times\Terms_{\!\Sigma,0}(X)

such that
Z\vdash s_{1}=t_{1}
in the graded theory that
presents
\mathbb{M}
(cf.
Section

2
).
Then, S
selects an
element
(u,v)\in Z
, upon which the game reaches the new
configuration
(u\sigma,v\sigma)
. The game proceeds forever unless
a player cannot move. Again, any player who cannot move, loses, and

if D
wins
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{syn}}_{\infty}
from position
(s,t)
.

We construct an
\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{\hphantom{$M$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}M_{1}
-coalgebra on the set

of infinite-depth terms modulo the winning region of D as follows. We
make

into an

-algebra by letting depth-0 operations act by

(using Kleisli star as per

is a top-level decomposition of an infinite-depth term,
with

;

[-]\colon\Terms_{\!\Sigma,1}(X)\to M_{1}X=\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{\hphantom{$M$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}M_{1}M_{0}X\quad\text{and}\quad q\colon\Terms_{\!\Sigma,\infty}\to U

denote canonical quotient maps.
These data are well-defined.

.

8.
Case studies

We have already seen (
6.9
) how the standard
bisimilation game arises as an instance of our generic game. We
elaborate on some further examples.

Simulation equivalence.

We illustrate how the infinite
(\alpha,\mathbb{M}
)-behavioural equivalence
game can be used to characterise simulation
equivalence
(
Glabbeek 1990
)
on serial LTS. We have described the
graded theory of simulation
in
3.3
.
(4)
. Recall that it requires
actions to be monotone, via the depth-1 equation

a(x+y)=a(x+y)+a(x).
When trying to show that depth-1
terms
\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}(t_{i})
and
\sum_{j\in J}b_{j}(s_{k})
are
equal, D

may exploit that over join semilattices, inequalities can be expressed
as equalities (
x\leq y
iff
x+y=y
), and instead endeavour to show
inequalities in both directions. By the monotonicity of actions,

\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}(t_{i})\leq\sum_{j\in J}b_{j}(s_{k})
is implied by D
claiming, for each
i
, that
t_{i}\leq s_{j}
for some
j
such that

a_{i}=b_{j}
; symmetrically for
\geq
(and by the description of the
relevant graded monad as per

2.10
.
(4)
, this proof
principle is complete). Once S challenges either a claim of the form

t_{i}\leq s_{j}
or one of the form
t_{i}\geq s_{j}
, the direction of
inequalities is fixed for the rest of the game; this corresponds to
the well-known phenomenon that in the standard pebble game for
similarity, S cannot switch sides after the first move. Like for
bisimilarity (
6.9
), the game can be modified
to let S move first: S first picks, say, one of the terms
t_{i}
, and D
responds with an
s_{j}
such that
a_{i}=b_{j}
, for which she claims

t_{i}\leq s_{j}
. Overall, the game is played on positions in

\mathscr{P}^{+}(X)\times\mathscr{P}^{+}(X)
, but if started on two states
x,y
of the
given labelled transition systems, i.e. in a position of the form

(\{x\},\{y\})
, the game forever remains in positions where both
components are singletons, and thus is effectively played on pairs of
states. Summing up, we recover exactly the usual pebble game for
mutual similarity. Variants such as complete, failure, or ready
simulation are captured by minor modifications of the graded
semantics
(
Dorsch
et al. 2019
)
.

T-structured trace equivalence.

Fix a set
\mathcal{A}
and a finitary monad
T
on
\mathbf{Set}
. We are going to
consider the
(\mathsf{id},\mathbb{M}_{T}(\mathcal{A}))
-behavioural equivalence game on
coalgebras for the functor
T(\mathcal{A}\times-)

(cf.
2.5
.
(3)
).

Notation 8.1.

Fix a presentation
(\Sigma^{\prime},E^{\prime})
of
T
(i.e. an equational theory
in the sense of universal algebra). We generalize the graded trace
theory described in
2.10
.
(3)
to
a graded theory
\mathcal{T}=(\Sigma,\mathcal{E})
for
\mathbb{M}_{T}(\mathcal{A})
as follows:

(\Sigma^{\prime},E^{\prime})
forms the depth-0 part of
\mathcal{T}
and, at depth-1,
\mathcal{T}

has unary actions
a(-)
which distribute over all operations

f\in\Sigma^{\prime}
:

a(f(s_{1},\cdots,s_{\mathsf{ar}(f)}))=f(a(s_{1}),\cdots,a(s_{\mathsf{ar}(f)}))

The arising theory
\mathcal{T}
presents
\mathbb{M}_{T}(\mathcal{A})
.

Recall from
6.2
.
(1)
that since, in
this setting,
M_{0}=T
, a legal move for D in position

(s,t)\in TX\times TX
is a relation
Z
on
TX
such
that equality of the respective successors
(\alpha_{X}\cdot\gamma)^{*}_{0}(s)

and
(\alpha_{X}\cdot\gamma)^{*}_{0}(t)
, viewed
as (equivalence classes of) depth-1 terms, is derivable in the theory

\mathcal{T}
under assumptions
Z
.

Remark 8.2.

A natural question is whether there exist algorithms for deciding if
a pair
(\alpha_{X}\cdot\gamma)^{*}_{0}(s),(\alpha_{X}\cdot\gamma)^{*}_{0}(t)

sits in the congruence closure of
Z
. In fact, there are
algorithms to check congruence closure of depth-0 terms for the
powerset monad
T=\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}

(
Bonchi and Pous 2013
)
and for
certain semiring monads
(
Bonchi
et al. 2017
)
. The idea behind
those algorithms is to obtain rewrite rules from pairs in
Z
, and
two elements are in the congruence closure if and only if they can
be rewritten to the same
normal form
. Applying depth-1
equations to normal forms could potentially yield a method to check
automatically whether a given pair of
M_{1}
-terms lies in the
congruence closure of
Z
.

Finite-trace equivalence.

More concretely, we examine the behavioural equivalence game for trace
equivalence on finitely branching LTS (i.e.

(\mathsf{id},\mathbb{M}_{\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}}(\mathcal{A}))
-semantics as

Failure equivalence.

Let
\gamma\colon X\to\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}(\mathcal{A}\times X)
be an LTS.

of a state

to a state

such that

fails
to perform some action

(the

they have the same set of failure pairs.

The

(

et al. 2019
)

extends the graded theory of traces by depth-1 constants

for
each

(failure sets) and depth-1
equations

for each

(failure sets are downwards closed). The resulting graded monad
(

et al. 2019
)

has

and
{M_{1}X=\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}^{\downarrow}(\mathcal{A}\times X+\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}\mathcal{A})}
,

\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}\mathcal{A}

It is clear that
\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{\hphantom{$M$}\vphantom{\rule{0.0pt}{5.5pt}}}}}M_{1}
still preserves monos since we have only expanded

is then described similarly as the one for plain traces above; the key

9.
Conclusions and Future Work

We have shown how to extract characteristic games for a
given graded behavioural equivalence, such as similarity, trace
equivalence, or probablistic trace equivalence, from the underlying
graded monad, effectively letting Spoiler and Duplicator play out an
equational proof. The method requires only fairly mild assumptions on
the graded monad; specifically, the extension of the first level of
the graded monad to algebras for the zero-th level needs to preserve
monomorphisms. This condition is not completely for free but appears to
be unproblematic in typical application scenarios. In case the zero-th
level of the graded monad preserves the terminal object (i.e. the
singleton set), it turns out that the induced graded behavioural
equivalence can be recast as standard coalgebraic behavioural
equivalence in a category of Eilenberg-Moore algebras, and is then
characterized by an infinite version of the generic equivalence
game. A promising direction for future work is to develop the generic
algorithmics and complexity theory of the infinite equivalence game,
which has computational content via the implied fixpoint
characterization. Moreover, we will extend the framework to cover
further notions of process comparison such as behavioural
preorders
(
Ford
et al. 2021
)
and, via a graded version of quantitative
algebra
(
Mardare
et al. 2016
)
, behavioural metrics.

Details for

We will prove that for every accessible graded monad

on a locally
presentable category, the category
\Alg_{n}(\mathbb{M})
,

, is locally
presentable. Furthermore, we show that the functor

Our proof makes use of several facts from the theory of accessible
and locally presentable categories which we tersely recall from
Adámek and Rosický’s book
(

Rosický 1994
)
.

As before, we fix a regular cardinal
\kappa
. A diagram

D\colon\mathscr{D}\to\mathscr{C}
is
\kappa
-filtered
if its diagram
scheme
\mathscr{D}
is a
\kappa
-filtered
category, that is a
category in which every subcategory with less than
\kappa
objects
and morphisms has a cocone. (In particular,
\mathscr{D}
is then non-empty
since a cocone for the empty subcategory is some object of
\mathscr{D}
.) A
functor
F\colon\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{D}
is
\kappa
-accessible
if it
preserves
\kappa
-filtered colimits, and a monad is

\kappa
-accessible if so is its underlying functor. An object
X
of
a category
\mathscr{C}
is
\kappa
-presentable
if its hom-functor

\mathscr{C}(X,-)\colon\mathscr{C}\to\mathbf{Set}
is
\kappa
-accessible.

Remark A.4.

We shall need the following facts about
\kappa
-presentable
objects and left adjoints.

(1)

A left adjoint
L\colon\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{D}
with a
\kappa
-accessible right adjoint

R
preserves
\kappa
-presentable objects. Indeed, for every

\kappa
-presentable object
C
in
\mathscr{C}
and every

\kappa
-filtered diagram
D
in
\mathscr{D}
we have the following chain
of natural isomorphisms

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}(LC,\colim D)

\displaystyle\cong\mathscr{C}(C,R(\colim D))

\displaystyle\cong\mathscr{C}(C,\colim RD)

\displaystyle\cong\colim\mathscr{C}(C,RD)

\displaystyle\cong\colim\mathscr{D}(LC,D).

(2)

Recall (e.g.
(
Adámek
et al. 1990
, Def. 7.74)
) that an
epimorphism
e
is
extremal
if whenever
e=m\cdot f
for
a monomorphism
m
, then
m
is an isomorphism. Let

L\dashv R\colon\mathscr{D}\to\mathscr{C}
be an adjunction where the
right adjoint
R
is faithful and reflects isomorphisms. Then the
adjoint transpose of every extremal epimorphism
e\colon C\twoheadrightarrow RD

is an extremal epimorphism, too.

Indeed, to see that the adjoint transpose
\bar{e}\colon LC\to D

is epic take a pair of morphisms
f,g\colon D\to D^{\prime}
such that

f\cdot\bar{e}=g\cdot\bar{e}
. Then
Uf\cdot e=Ug\cdot e

by adjoint transposition, whence
Uf=Ug
since
e
is epic, and
therefore
f=g
since
U
is faithful.

Now suppose that
\bar{e}=m\cdot f
for some monomorphism
m
in

\mathscr{D}
. Then
e=Um\cdot\bar{f}
in
\mathscr{C}
, where
Um
is
monic since right adjoints preserve monos. Thus,
Um
is an
isomorphism in
\mathscr{C}
, whence
m
is an isomorphism in
\mathscr{D}

since
U
reflects isomorphisms.

A category
\mathscr{C}
is
\kappa
-accessible
if it has

\kappa
-filtered colimits and a set of
\kappa
-presentable objects
such that every object of
\mathscr{C}
is a
\kappa
-filtered colimit of
object from that set. The category
\mathscr{C}
is locally

\kappa
-presentable if it is locally
\kappa
-accessible and
cocomplete. We say that a category is
accessible
(
locally presentable
)
if it is
\kappa
-accessible (locally
\kappa
-presentable) for some
regular cardinal
\kappa
.

Remark A.5.

We now collect several facts used in the proof of
A.6
below.

(1)

A category is locally
\kappa
-presentable iff
is is
\kappa
-accessible and complete
(
Adámek and
Rosický 1994
, Cor. 2.47)
.

(2)

A
strong generator
in a cocomplete
category
\mathscr{C}
is a set
\mathscr{G}
of objects such that every
object is an extremal quotient of a coproduct of object from

\mathscr{G}
. A cocomplete category is locally
\kappa
-presentable iff
it has a strong generator formed by
\kappa
-presentable
objects
(
Adámek and
Rosický 1994
, Thm. 1.20)
.

(3)

For a
\kappa
-accessible monad

on a

\kappa
-presentable category

, the category

\Alg(T)
of all Eilenberg-Moore algebras is locally

\kappa
-presentable
(
Adámek and
Rosický 1994
, Thm. & Rem. 2.78)
.

(4)

For every locally
\kappa
-presentable
category
\mathscr{C}
the product category
\mathscr{C}\times\mathscr{C}
is
locally
\kappa
-presentable. Indeed, first
\mathscr{C}\times\mathscr{C}

is
\kappa
-accessible
(
Adámek and
Rosický 1994
, Prop. 2.67)
. In addition, the
product is (co)complete since so is

and (co)limits are
formed componentwise in the product category. Moreover, the product
projection functors are clearly
\kappa
-accessible.

(5)

Let
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{K}
be locally

\kappa
-presentable, and let

be

\kappa
-accessible. The
inserter category
of
F,G
has
objects
(K,f)
where
K
is an object of
\mathscr{K}
and

is a morphism of
\mathscr{C}
; morphisms

are morphisms

of

\mathscr{K}
such that
f^{\prime}\cdot Fk=Gk\cdot f
. The inserter
category is
\lambda
-accessible for some regular cardinal

\lambda\geq\kappa

(
Adámek and
Rosický 1994
, Thm. 2.72)
. (Note that, in
general,
\lambda\neq\kappa
.)

(6)

Let
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{K}
be
\kappa
-accessible, and
let

be
\kappa
-accessible functors. The

of a pair

of natural

\mathscr{K}
given by all
objects

such that

. This category is

\kappa
-accessible and its inclusion functor into

is

\kappa
-accessible. The same holds, more generally,
for any set of pairs

)
and any set of pairs

(
Adámek and
Rosický 1994
, Lem. 2.76)
.

We say that a graded monad
\mathbb{M}
on
\mathscr{C}
is

\kappa
-accessible
if every of its endofunctors

(

) is
\kappa
-accessible.
