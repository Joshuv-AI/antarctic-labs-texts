Anatomy of real intermediate state-subtraction scheme Preprint: HIP-2023-14/TH
Kalle Ala-Mattinen, Matti Heikinheimo, Kimmo Kainulainen, Kimmo Tuominen

Abstract
We study the origin of the real intermediate state subtraction problem and compare its different solutions. We show that the ambiguity in subtraction schemes arises from the on-shell approximation for the 2-point functions that reduces the Schwinger-Dyson equations to the Boltzmann limit. We also suggest a new subtraction scheme which, unlike the earlier definitions, never leads to negative scattering rates. This scheme also quantifies the validity of the on-shell limit in terms of an effective one-particle weight function R ⁡ ( Δ ) {R}(\Delta) , where Δ \Delta measures the region around the resonance associated with the real state.



I
Introduction

There is a long-standing issue in setting up consistent kinetic equations for particle distribution functions when relevant scattering processes involve real particles that also appear as intermediate states. The problem is that the resonant on-shell scattering processes overlap with the decay contributions in the kinetic equations, which results in double counting unless some subtraction procedure is established for the scattering rates. Such a procedure, referred to as the real intermediate state (RIS) subtraction, was first set up in
Kolb and Wolfram 1980a
;
Kolb and Wolfram 1980b
;
Fry et al. 1980
. The RIS subtraction has since been discussed in various different forms
Harvey et al. 1982
;
Luty 1992
;
Cline et al. 1994
;
Bouzas et al. 1997
;
Plumacher 1997
;
Papavassiliou and Pilaftsis 1996
;
Pilaftsis 1997
;
Hambye et al. 2000
;
Giudice et al. 2004
;
Pilaftsis and Underwood 2004
;
Sawyer 2004
;
Buchmuller et al. 2005
;
Nardi et al. 2007
;
Davidson et al. 2008
;
Deppisch and Pilaftsis 2011
;
Kiessig and Plumacher 2012
;
Frossard et al. 2013
;
Iso et al. 2014
;
Iso and Shimada 2014
;
Lavignac and Schmauch 2015
;
Cline et al. 2017
;
Biondini et al. 2018
;
Dev et al. 2018
;
Bodeker and Buchmuller 2021
;
Matak 2022
;
Maták 2023
;
Ala-Mattinen et al. 2022
, and an alternative method that does not rely on the RIS subtraction was recently suggested in
Laine 2023
. Typically the RIS subtraction is performed at the level of Boltzmann equations, implementing some formal way to isolate and remove the on-shell part,
D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{on}}(s)
, from the full Breit-Wigner (BW) propagator
D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{BW}}(s)
, thus replacing it with the off-shell part,
D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{off}}(s)
, that is used for scattering amplitudes. However, this procedure is inherently ambiguous due to the ambiguity in the definition of a “real" particle with unstable states. As a result, there is large freedom in the definition of the RIS subtraction, and different RIS subtraction schemes are even known to lead to apparently unphysical negative scattering cross sections
Cline et al. 2017
.

In this article we study and compare different solutions to the RIS problem. We also study the emergence of the problem in the context of Boltzmann equations (BE) and then from a more fundamental perspective of the Schwinger-Dyson (SD) equations for the 2-point functions. SD equations are a fully consistent setup for studying unstable particles with no notion of the real intermediate states. We will show how the problem arises when the SD equations are reduced to the spectral limit. This also makes the effect of RIS subtraction to different reaction channels evident. We then suggest a new definition for the RIS subtraction, which does not suffer from the negative cross sections. The new scheme quantifies the ambiguity in the RIS subtraction in terms of an effective one-particle weight function
{R}(\Delta)<1
for the approximate real states, where
\Delta
measures the size of the kinematic region around the resonance that is counted to contribute to the real particle. For the real-particle picture to be a good approximation, one should be able to choose
\Delta
, which is much smaller than the characteristic energy scale in the system, such that
{R}(\Delta)\approx 1
. Failing to satisfy these conditions signals the breakdown of the on-shell limit, and a BE network with the decay channel should not be used, or should be interpreted with due care.

we study the origin of the RIS problem in the Schwinger-Dyson formalism in the case of the Yukawa theory. We show how the RIS problem arises in the on-shell limit, when the resonance overlaps with the kinematic region of interest for the problem at hand. This then motivates us to suggest our new RIS-subtraction scheme in Sec.
section

V
. In Sec.
section

VI
we perform numerical comparisons between different subtraction methods including our new proposal and conclude in Sec.
section

VII

II
Review of the RIS-subtraction schemes

The goal of the RIS-subtraction procedure is to somehow eliminate the on-shell part from the finite-width Breit-Wigner propagator:

D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{BW}}(s)=\frac{1}{s-m^{2}+im\Gamma}\rightarrow D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{off}}(s)\,,

(1)

where
s
is the Mandelstam variable,
m
is the mass of the propagating intermediate particle, and
\Gamma
is its total decay width. The on-shell part
D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{on}}(s)=D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{BW}}(s)-D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{off}}(s)
is then reduced to a delta function in the limit
\Gamma\rightarrow 0
. It is associated with the decay processes, while the remainder
D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{off}}(s)
is thought to describe the off-shell scattering processes. For this task, different technical schemes have been proposed. Let us begin with the
standard RIS-subtraction
(SRS) scheme that is applied at the level of the propagator function squared.

The standard subtraction scheme is based on the simple observation that the squared BW propagator itself has a formal delta-function limit:

|D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{BW}}(s)|^{2}=\frac{1}{m\Gamma}\frac{m\Gamma}{(s\!-\!m^{2})^{2}\!+\!(m\Gamma)^{2}}\approx\frac{\pi}{m\Gamma}\delta(s\!-\!m^{2})\,,

(2)

where the last equality holds approximately for small
\Gamma
. Interpreting the right-hand side of
eq.

2
as the on-shell propagator for a finite
\Gamma
can then be used as the definition for the
squared
off-shell propagator:

|D^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SRS}}}_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{off}}(s)|^{2}=|D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{BW}}(s)|^{2}-\frac{\pi}{m\Gamma}\delta(s-m^{2}).

(3)

This procedure works for the resonances isolated in a single channel. However, we may have for example a process with
s
- and
t
-channel contributions where the
s
-channel is resonant. Then the collision integral is proportional to

|\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}|^{2}+|\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{t}}|^{2}+2\mathfrak{R\!e}[\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}^{*}\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{t}}]\,.

(4)

The rule
eq.

3
is not sufficient to deal with the resonant propagator in the mixing term, as it only tells how to remove the on-shell part from the propagator squared contributions.

To avoid this issue, a subtraction procedure at the level of the propagator is needed.
This was discussed in
Harvey et al. 1982
but, to our knowledge, properly proposed later in
Luty 1992
. Here one starts by writing

\displaystyle D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{BW}}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{s-m^{2}}{(s-m^{2})^{2}+(m\Gamma)^{2}}-i\frac{m\Gamma}{(s-m^{2})^{2}+(m\Gamma)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{R\!e}\!\left[D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{BW}}(s)\right]+i\mathfrak{I\!m}\!\left[D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{BW}}(s)\right]

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathfrak{R\!e}\!\left[D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{BW}}(s)\right]-i\pi\delta(s-m^{2}).

(5)

In the last line the limit
\Gamma\rightarrow 0
was assumed (only) in the imaginary part of the propagator. One can use this result to complete the SRS scheme for the interference term in
eq.

4
, setting

D^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SRS}}}_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{off}}(s)\equiv D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{BW}}(s)+i\pi\delta(s-m^{2})

(6)

at the propagator level, whenever a single resonant propagator (as opposed to a squared one) is encountered. In the
principal value subtraction
(PVS) scheme suggested in
Luty 1992
one reverses the logic and assumes that the off-shell propagator is just the real part of the Breit-Wigner propagator:

D^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{PVS}}}_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{off}}(s)\equiv\mathfrak{R\!e}\!\left[D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{BW}}(s)\right].

(7)

Also in the PVS scheme different definitions for the propagator and for the squared propagator are needed, which require some care. One might naively think that the on-shell part of the squared propagator would be just
|D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{on}}|^{2}\equiv|D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{BW}}|^{2}-\left|\mathfrak{R\!e}\!\left[D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{BW}}\right]\right|^{2}\!=\left|\mathfrak{I\!m}[D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{BW}}]\right|^{2}
, but this is
not
the correct subtraction, because the imaginary part squared produces only half of the on-shell contribution:

\displaystyle\left|\mathfrak{I\!m}\!\left[D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{BW}}\right]\right|^{2}\rightarrow\frac{\pi}{2m\Gamma}\delta(s-m^{2})\,.

(8)

As pointed out in
Giudice et al. 2004
;
Pilaftsis and Underwood 2004
;
Sawyer 2004
, this issue is particularly relevant in the resonant leptogenesis literature.

The problem is that when we extract a distribution from a function the remainder is also a distribution, and one must be careful when taking the square. The explicit case at point here is that

\Big({\mathrm{PV}}\!\Big\{\frac{1}{x}\Big\}\Big)^{2}\neq{\mathrm{PV}}\!\Big\{\frac{1}{x^{2}}\Big\}\,,

(9)

where PV refers to the principal value part of the function. Indeed, while
1/x
has the principal value sequence
{\mathrm{PV}}(1/x)=\mathfrak{R\!e}(1/(x+i\varepsilon))=x/(x^{2}+\epsilon^{2})
,
the corresponding sequence for
1/x^{2}
is

{\mathrm{PV}}\!\Big\{\frac{1}{x^{2}}\Big\}=\,\mathfrak{R\!e}\Big(\frac{1}{(x\pm i\varepsilon)^{2}}\Big)=\frac{x^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}}{(x^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})^{2}}\,.

(10)

In these expressions, the limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
is of course assumed. From this exercise one infers that the correct squared propagator in the PVS scheme for a finite
\Gamma
is

D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{off}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{PVS}}(s)=\frac{(s-m^{2})^{2}-(m\Gamma)^{2}}{((s-m^{2})^{2}+(m\Gamma)^{2})^{2}}

(11)

as was indeed suggested in
Cline et al. 1994
. Subtracting this off-shell part from the full BW propagator one finds that

\displaystyle|D^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{PVS}}_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{on}}(s)|^{2}

\displaystyle=|D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{BW}}(s)|^{2}-|D^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{PVS}}_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{off}}(s)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(m\Gamma)^{2}}{((s-m^{2})^{2}+(m\Gamma)^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\frac{\pi}{m\Gamma}\delta(s-m^{2})\,.

(12)

That is, the narrow-width limit in the PVS scheme requires the use of the delta sequence
\pi\delta(x)=2\varepsilon^{3}/[x^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}]^{2}
.

The formulas for the SRS scheme
eqs.

3
and
6
and for the PVS scheme
eqs.

11
and
7
are equivalent up to order
\Gamma^{2}
. The PVS scheme also has a well defined
\Gamma\rightarrow 0
limit for the off-shell propagators:

\displaystyle D^{{\scriptscriptstyle\Gamma=0}}_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{off}}(s)

\displaystyle\rightarrow{\rm PV}\Big\{\frac{1}{s-m^{2}}\Big\}

\displaystyle|D^{{\scriptscriptstyle\Gamma=0}}_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{off}}(s)|^{2}

\displaystyle\rightarrow{\rm PV}\Big\{\frac{1}{(s-m^{2})^{2}}\Big\}.

(13)

The limiting case
eq.

13
can be understood as yet another subtraction scheme. We stress that the subtraction scheme dependence affects only the off-shell propagators. All schemes were designed to have the same on-shell limits:

\displaystyle D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{on}}(s)

\displaystyle=-i\pi\delta(s-m^{2})

\displaystyle|D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{on}}(s)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{m\Gamma}\delta(s-m^{2}).

(14)

There is no
a priori
reason to prefer one scheme over the other, although the limiting scheme
eq.

13
is perhaps conceptually most consistent, as we shall argue later. It seems that most confusion related to RIS subtraction in the literature has resulted from a failure to realize that each scheme requires separate formulas for the off-shell propagator and the squared off-shell propagator functions. Finally, we point out that the above discussion is not restricted to
s
-channel processes. Resonances can appear also in
t
- and
u
-channels. When this happens, the subtraction should be performed as in the
s
-channel case.

A fundamental problem in all above schemes is that they can occasionally lead to
negative
reaction rates. This can happen because the off-shell propagators are negative near on-shell; if the scattering process is enhanced there, the integrated rates may also become negative. This is an apparently unphysical result, but it does not necessarily lead to a failure of the kinetic equation network. We shall return to these issues in Sec.
section

VI
.

III
A minimal example of double counting

Here we show that the decay contribution to kinetic equations indeed corresponds to the contribution from the on-shell propagator in the scattering channel. To this end we consider a setting with unspecified particles
a
,
b
and
c
, and for demonstrative purpose focus only on the processes shown in Fig.
1
. We want to track the distribution function of species
c
, whose kinetic equation can be written as

\mathrm{L}\left[f_{c}(p_{1})\right]=\mathcal{C}_{aa\leftrightarrow cc}^{c}(p_{1})+\mathcal{C}_{b\leftrightarrow cc}^{c}(p_{1})\,,

(15)

where the Liouville operator
\mathrm{L}\left[f(p)\right]\equiv(\partial_{t}-Hp\partial_{p})f(p)
. To allow for analytic calculation, we work in the Maxwell-Boltzmann (MB) approximation,
1\pm f_{i}\approx 1
and
f_{i}^{\mathrm{eq}}=e^{-\beta E_{i}}
for
i={a,b,c}
and assume that particles
a
and
b
are in equilibrium.
We will prove that when the intermediate
b
particle is treated at the idealized resonant limit, the equilibrium contribution of the scattering term reduces exactly to the equilibrium decay term.

We start by evaluating the equilibrium scattering term in the MB limit:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{c}(a_{\mathrm{eq}}a_{\mathrm{eq}}\leftrightarrow cc)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2E^{c}_{p_{1}}}\int_{\scriptscriptstyle p_{2},k_{1},k_{2}}\!\!\!(2\pi)^{4}\,\delta^{(4)}(k_{1}+k_{2}-p_{1}-p_{2})\,

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\times|\mathcal{M}_{aa\leftrightarrow cc}|^{2}\left[f_{a}^{\mathrm{eq}}(k_{1})f_{a}^{\mathrm{eq}}(k_{2})-f_{c}(p_{1})f_{c}(p_{2})\right],

(16)

where
\smash{\int_{p}\equiv\int{\rm d}^{3}p/[2E_{p}(2\pi)^{3}]}
. The integral over momenta
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
is easily performed when one demands that the detailed balance holds:
f_{a}^{\mathrm{eq}}(k_{1})f_{a}^{\mathrm{eq}}(k_{2})=f_{c}^{\mathrm{eq}}(p_{1})f_{c}^{\mathrm{eq}}(p_{2})
. Furthermore, factoring the squared matrix element as
|\mathcal{M}_{aa\leftrightarrow cc}|^{2}=|\mathcal{M}_{aa\leftrightarrow b}|^{2}|D_{b}|^{2}|\mathcal{M}_{b\leftrightarrow cc}|^{2}
we end up with

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{c}(a_{\mathrm{eq}}a_{\mathrm{eq}}\leftrightarrow cc)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2E^{c}_{p_{1}}}\int_{p_{2}}\raisebox{-0.4pt}{\parbox[b]{172.79733pt}{\hbox to172.79733pt{\vrule height=0.4pt,width=3.0pt\leaders{\hbox to6.0pt{\hfill\rule{3.0pt}{0.4pt}\hfill}}{\hfill}\vrule height=0.4pt,width=3.0pt}\kern-0.4pt\par\parbox{0.4pt}{\vbox to18.17891pt{\hrule height=3.0pt,width=0.4pt\leaders{\vbox to6.0pt{\vfill\rule{0.4pt}{3.0pt}\vfill}}{\vfill}\hrule height=3.0pt,width=0.4pt}}\kern 3.0pt\parbox{165.99734pt}{\vskip 3.0pt\hbox{$\dfrac{1}{8\pi s}\lambda^{1/2}(s,m_{a}^{2},m_{a}^{2})\,|\mathcal{M}_{aa\leftrightarrow b}|^{2}|D^{b}_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{BW}}|^{2}$}\vskip 3.0pt}\kern 3.0pt\parbox{0.4pt}{\vbox to18.17891pt{\hrule height=3.0pt,width=0.4pt\leaders{\vbox to6.0pt{\vfill\rule{0.4pt}{3.0pt}\vfill}}{\vfill}\hrule height=3.0pt,width=0.4pt}}\par\kern-0.4pt\hbox to172.79733pt{\vrule height=0.4pt,width=3.0pt\leaders{\hbox to6.0pt{\hfill\rule{3.0pt}{0.4pt}\hfill}}{\hfill}\vrule height=0.4pt,width=3.0pt}}}

\displaystyle\quad\;\;\times|\mathcal{M}_{b\leftrightarrow cc}|^{2}\left[f_{c}^{\mathrm{eq}}(p_{1})f_{c}^{\mathrm{eq}}(p_{2})-f_{c}(p_{1})f_{c}(p_{2})\right],

(17)

where
\lambda(x,y,z)=(x-y-z)^{2}-4yz
. To compare with the decay term, we need to evaluate

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{c}(b_{\mathrm{eq}}\leftrightarrow cc)=\frac{1}{2E^{c}_{p_{1}}}\int_{p_{2},q}\!\!\!(2\pi)^{4}\,\delta^{(4)}(q-p_{1}-p_{2})\,

(18)

The detailed balance condition again allows us to replace
f_{b}^{\mathrm{eq}}(q)
by
f_{c}^{\mathrm{eq}}(p_{1})f_{c}^{\mathrm{eq}}(p_{2})
. Writing the three-dimensional integral over momentum

\smash{\delta(q^{2}-m_{b}^{2})\,(2\pi)^{-3}\mathrm{d}^{4}}q

\delta^{(4)}(q-p_{1}-p_{2})
to carry out integration over
\smash{\mathrm{d}^{4}\!q}
, the decay term then becomes

) and Eq. (

) amounts to establishing equality of the boxed quantities. To this end, we write the
|\mathcal{M}_{aa\leftrightarrow b}|^{2}

) in terms of the decay width:

\frac{1}{8\pi}|\mathcal{M}_{aa\leftrightarrow b}|^{2}=\frac{2m_{b}^{3}}{\lambda^{1/2}(m_{b}^{2},m_{a}^{2},m_{a}^{2})}\mathrm{BR}_{b\rightarrow aa}\Gamma\,,

(20)

where
\Gamma\equiv\sum_{i}\Gamma_{b\rightarrow\{i\}}
is the full decay width of the
b
particle and
\smash{\mathrm{BR_{b\rightarrow\{i\}}}=\Gamma_{b\rightarrow\{i\}}/\Gamma}
is the branching ratio to final state
{i}
. Furthermore, writing the propagator

, where the off- and on-shell results are given in (

) and (

), the boxed part of the scattering term (

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{8\pi s}\lambda^{1/2}(s,m_{a}^{2},m_{a}^{2})\,|\mathcal{M}_{aa\leftrightarrow b}|^{2}|D^{b}_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{BW}}|^{2}

(21)

where we defined

h(s)=\frac{m_{b}^{2}}{s}\frac{\lambda^{1/2}(s,m_{a}^{2},m_{a}^{2})}{\lambda^{1/2}(m_{b}^{2},m_{a}^{2},m_{a}^{2})}\,.

(22)

In the on-shell limit
s\rightarrow m_{b}^{2}
the function

proving that

\mathcal{C}^{c}(a_{\mathrm{eq}}a_{\mathrm{eq}}\leftrightarrow b_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{on}}\leftrightarrow cc)=\mathrm{BR}_{b\rightarrow aa}\,\mathcal{C}^{c}(b_{\mathrm{eq}}\leftrightarrow cc)\,.

(23)

This is the doubly counted state which the RIS subtraction is devised to remove via

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{c}(aa\leftrightarrow cc)+\mathcal{C}^{c}(b\leftrightarrow cc)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathcal{C}^{c}(aa\leftrightarrow b_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{off}}\leftrightarrow cc)+\mathcal{C}^{c}(b\leftrightarrow cc)\,,

(24)

i.e. by dropping the on-shell part of the scattering term. Finally all 2-2 process with different initial states, including elastic channels, should be included so that their corresponding branching ratios, as in Eq. (
23
), sum up
\sum_{i}\mathrm{BR}_{i}=1
.

IV
The origin of the RIS problem

The previous example verified the double counting between the decay and scattering channels, and showed that the on-shell
s
-channel propagator defined in
eq.

14
indeed corresponds to the decay contribution. In this section we shall take a look at the problem from a more general point of view.

IV.1
Spectral functions

A simple interacting system where the RIS problem may arise is the Yukawa theory with a scalar field
\varphi
and a fermion field
\psi
. If
\varphi
is too light to decay into a fermion pair, isolated poles exist for both
\varphi
and
\psi
, and no RIS problem exists, however. In this case the vacuum scalar spectral functions
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}
can be written as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\pi\epsilon(k_{0})\big(R_{\varphi}\delta(s-m_{\varphi}^{2})+\rho_{\varphi}(s)\big)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\psi}

\displaystyle=\pi\epsilon(k_{0})(\not{k}+m_{\psi})\Big(R_{\psi}\delta(s-m_{\psi}^{2})+\rho_{\psi}(s)\Big),

(25)

where
\rho_{\varphi,\psi}(s)
are the continuum (off-shell) parts to the spectral functions and the one-particle weight functions
R_{\varphi,\psi}
are constrained to be less than unity by the spectral sum rules. Indeed,
\smash{\frac{1}{\pi}\int{\rm d}k_{0}k_{0}\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}=1}
and
\smash{\frac{1}{\pi}\int{\rm d}k_{0}\mathcal{A}_{\psi}=\gamma^{0}}
imply that

R_{\varphi,\psi}=1-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int{\rm d}s\rho_{\varphi,\psi}(s)<1.

(26)

The continuum parts
\rho_{\varphi,\psi}(s)
may to a good accuracy be computed perturbatively using spectral free-theory propagators. In this case one can also derive a good BE approximation for kinetic equations reducing the SD equations to the on-shell limit using the spectral propagators:

\displaystyle i\Delta_{\varphi}^{\scriptscriptstyle<}

\displaystyle=2\pi\epsilon(k_{0})R_{\varphi}f_{\varphi}(k)\delta(s-m_{\varphi}^{2})

\displaystyle iS_{\psi}^{\scriptscriptstyle<}

\displaystyle=2\pi\epsilon(k_{0})(\not{k}+m_{\psi})R_{\psi}f_{\psi}(k)\delta(s-m_{\varphi}^{2})

(27)

and moreover

\displaystyle\Delta_{\varphi}^{\rm H}

\displaystyle={\mathrm{PV}}\Big\{\frac{R_{\varphi}}{s-m_{\varphi}^{2}}\Big\}

\displaystyle i\Delta_{\varphi}^{11}

\displaystyle=i\Delta_{\varphi}^{\rm H}+2\pi\epsilon(k_{0})R_{\varphi}(f_{\varphi}(k)+{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}})\delta(s-m_{\varphi}^{2}),

(28)

where
\smash{\Delta_{\varphi}^{11}}
is the Feynman propagator, whose Hermitian component is given by
\smash{\Delta_{\varphi}^{\rm H}}
. Similar equations hold for the fermion
\psi
. One can usually set
\smash{R_{\varphi,\psi}=1}
to a good approximation for perturbative couplings. We kept them here to emphasize that in an interacting theory the one-particle states do not exhaust the entire state space of the system. However, in the current setup where isolated poles exist, the on-shell formulas
eq.

27
and
eq.

28
provide a good parametrization for the system.

When
m_{\varphi}>2m_{\psi}
,
\varphi
is unstable and no longer has an isolated pole. In this case the spectral solutions do not strictly speaking exist, and it is not possible to derive the BE limit for the
\varphi
field without further approximations. For example, if the energy scales one is interested in do not overlap with the
\varphi
resonance, one may be able to derive a good BE limit for the problem based on the spectral parametrization for the fermion and treating
\varphi
only as an intermediate resonance. The RIS problem becomes acute only when the relevant energy scales
do
overlap with the resonance
and
one implements an on-shell kinetic equation also for
\varphi
, as we shall see in the next section.

The on-shell limit can often be a good approximation also for unstable particles. When this is so, it would seem natural to use the spectral limit for all propagator functions, including the Hermitian parts. This would give rise to the limiting subtraction prescription
eq.

13
, which in this sense is the most natural scheme to use. But this choice is not unique, as we shall see below.

IV.2
Schwinger-Dyson equations

We now discuss the RIS problem in the context of SD equations. We emphasize that the full SD equations, which span the entire phase space of the 2-point functions, do not need RIS subtraction. The issue emerges only when the SD equations for unstable particles are reduced to the on-shell limit. The SD equations for the Yukawa theory can be schematically written as

\displaystyle\Delta_{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\varphi,0}+\Delta_{\varphi,0}\otimes\Pi_{\varphi}\otimes\Delta_{\varphi}

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=S_{\psi,0}+S_{\psi,0}\otimes\Sigma_{\psi}\otimes S{\psi}

(29)

where
\Delta_{\varphi,0}
and
S_{\psi,0}
are the free propagators and
\Delta_{\varphi}
and

are the full propagators. In the direct space representation the convolutions are defined as

lives on the complex time contour. In real time the SD equations split into Kadanoff-Baym (KB) equations for the pole functions and for the Wightman functions. In the bosonic case we get:

\begin{split}(\Delta_{\varphi,0}^{-1}-\Pi_{\varphi}^{p})\otimes\Delta_{\varphi}^{p}&=1\\
(\Delta_{\varphi,0}^{-1}-\Pi_{\varphi}^{\rm H})\otimes\Delta_{\varphi}^{s}&=\Pi_{\varphi}^{s}\otimes\Delta_{\varphi}^{\rm H}+\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}^{s},\end{split}

(30)

where

refer to the retarded and advanced pole functions and
s=<,>
to the statistical Wightman functions, and we defined the collision terms

\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}^{\scriptscriptstyle<}=\frac{1}{2}\big(\Pi_{\varphi}^{\scriptscriptstyle>}\otimes\Delta_{\varphi}^{\scriptscriptstyle<}-\Pi_{\varphi}^{\scriptscriptstyle<}\otimes\Delta_{\varphi}^{\scriptscriptstyle>}\big),

(31)

and
\smash{\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}^{\scriptscriptstyle>}=-\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}^{\scriptscriptstyle<}}
. Similar decomposition can be derived for the fermionic correlation functions
S_{\psi}^{p,s}
; see
e.g.

Jukkala et al. 2021
. The KB equations usually cannot be solved without further approximations, such as the spectral ansatz
eq.

27

Jukkala et al. 2021
;
Kainulainen and Parkkinen 2023a
;
Kainulainen and Parkkinen 2023b
. However, to understand the RIS problem, it is sufficient to concentrate on
the collision terms
{\cal C}_{\varphi,\psi}^{\scriptscriptstyle<}
, which in the end emerge as the collision integrals in Boltzmann equations.

V
Cut-subtraction scheme

In the previous section we saw that both the need for and the ambiguity of the RIS subtraction arise when the full solutions to the SD equations are approximated by spectral solutions. The validity of the spectral limit clearly depends on the resonance width
\Gamma
. For a small width, excitations around the resonance may give nearly identical contributions to physical processes, allowing them to be treated as an effective single-particle state. If
\Gamma
is very small, the integrated weight of such states may even saturate the spectral sum rule. This is the case when the usual spectral reduction of the SD equations to the BE limit with free theory normalization
R=1
is valid. If the physical process changes significantly over the resonance region, however, the spectral approximation starts to break down.

We quantify these simple observations by defining the following cut-subtraction scheme:

D^{\Delta}_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{off}}(a)=\big[1-\Theta(a\!-\!m^{2},\Delta)\big]D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{BW}}(a),

(32)

where
a=s,t
, or
u
, depending on the channel one is looking at, and the cut function
\Theta
singles out a region around the resonance. The simplest choice is the top-hat function:

\Theta(x,\Delta)=\theta(\Delta-x)\theta(x+\Delta).

(33)

The squared off-shell propagator does not need a separate rule in this scheme. The propagator in Eq.
eq.

32
is again assumed to replace the Hermitian parts in the Feynman and anti-Feynman propagators in spectral decomposition,
e.g.
in Eq.
eq.

28
. Combined with this prescription, the statistical propagators in Eq.
eq.

27
(as well as the spectral parts of the Feynman and anti-Feynman propagators), are rescaled by a weight function:

{R}(\Delta)\equiv\frac{1}{\pi}\int\mathrm{d}s\,\Theta(s\!-\!m^{2},\Delta)\,m\Gamma|D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{BW}}(s)|^{2}.

(34)

It should be obvious that none of these changes interfere with the on-shell reduction of the SD equations.

The precise form of the cut function in Eq.
eq.

33
is not relevant, and the characteristic width
\Delta
will depend on the problem. The weight function will give a
quantitative measure
for the validity of the spectral limit. To see how this works, consider some physical quantity
\mathcal{F}
, which is an integral over, say, the Mandelstam variable
s
:

\mathcal{F}=\int\mathrm{d}s\,F(s)\,|D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{BW}}(s)|^{2}\,.

(35)

For example the vacuum contribution to the
\psi
collision integral from process
\psi\bar{\psi}\rightarrow\psi\bar{\psi}
, coming from the cut in the last diagram in Fig.
fig.

3
would have this form. We can now divide
\mathcal{F}
into on- and off-shell contributions
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{on}}+\mathcal{F}_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{off}}
, where

\mathcal{F}_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{off}}=\int\mathrm{d}s\,F(s)\,|D^{\Delta}_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{off}}(s)|^{2}\,.

(36)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{on}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m\Gamma}\int\mathrm{d}s\,F(s)\Theta(s\!-\!m^{2},\Delta)m\Gamma|D_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}}(s)|^{2}

\displaystyle\approx{R}(\Delta)\pi\frac{F(m_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}}^{2})}{m_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}}\Gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}}}\,.

(37)

In the second line we assumed that
F(s)
remains essentially a constant in the cut region and then used Eq.
eq.

34
. This clearly corresponds to replacing
|D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{BW}}(s)|^{2}
inside the on-shell region with a weighted delta function:
\pi{R}(\Delta)\delta(s-m^{2})/(m\Gamma)
.

If
\mathcal{F}
indeed described the process
\psi\bar{\psi}\rightarrow\psi\bar{\psi}
, then the on-shell part in Eq.
eq.

37
would be exactly canceled by the on-shell vacuum decay term
\varphi\rightarrow\psi\bar{\psi}
arising from the one-loop diagram in the fermion SD equation in Fig.
fig.

2
. Indeed, because of our rule for weighting the statistical propagators by
{R}(\Delta)
, the latter is given by the standard vacuum decay term multiplied by
{R}(\Delta)
. The exact calculation equating the two is essentially the same we presented in Sec.
section

III
, with
b=\varphi
and
aa,cc\rightarrow\psi\bar{\psi}
.

Our scheme in corresponds to setting
\smash{\Delta^{\rm H}_{\varphi}\rightarrow D^{\Delta}_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{off}}}

and

R_{\varphi}\rightarrow{R}(\Delta)
in Eqs.
eq.

27
and
eq.

28
. All collision integrals can then be computed from these functions following standard finite temperature field theory methods. One then recovers the standard tree level rules for computing collision integrals, augmented with the scaling of the phase space factors
f_{\varphi}\rightarrow{R}(\Delta)f_{\varphi}
and replacing
D_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{BW}}\rightarrow D^{\Delta}_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{off}}
for all intermediate scalar propagators. Extensions to more complicated theories where also fermions can be unstable should be obvious.

We should emphasize one important difference between our scheme and the other subtraction schemes. Consider the case where
F
is a constant. In this case all standard schemes give
\mathcal{F}_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{off}}=0
, whereas in the cut scheme
\mathcal{F}_{\scriptstyle\mathrm{off}}=F(1-{R}(\Delta))
. The latter one is the qualitatively correct result. The standard schemes thus
overestimate
the weight of the effective one-particle states, which forces the flat-weight integrals of the off-shell parts to vanish in compensation. The effect underestimates the off-shell contributions also for a nonconstant
F(s)
and may even lead to a negative cross section if the corresponding
F(s)
is enhanced near the resonance. The cut-subtraction scheme does not suffer from these problems by construction.

The particle picture makes sense only if the approximation in Eq.
eq.

37
holds with a large enough
\Delta
, such as
{R}(\Delta)\simeq 1
. Indeed, if
F(s)
changed rapidly in the scale
m\Gamma
, we would be forced to use very small
\Delta\ll m\Gamma
to extract
F(s)
outside the integral. This would then give
{R}(\Delta)\ll 1
, showing that the one-particle contribution to the process is very small. Of course the particle picture would not make sense in this limit. Nevertheless, the cut-subtraction procedure allows for a continuous mapping between the limit where the particle picture is valid [
{R}(\Delta)\simeq 1
] and the resonance limit where
\varphi
no longer is part of the thermal bath [
{R}(\Delta)\simeq 0
].

VII
Conclusions

In this article we revisited the well-known RIS subtraction problem. The RIS subtraction is necessary for the resonant scattering channels when the intermediate state causing the resonance is also included as a real state in the on-shell kinetic equation network. Over the years many different methods to perform the RIS subtraction have been proposed, with some of them leading to erroneous results and sometimes to negative scattering cross sections.

We took a fresh look into the different RIS schemes that have appeared in the literature. We pointed out how to extend the standard RIS-subtraction scheme to interference matrix elements and emphasized the need to use the correct sequences for different propagator functions in the principal value scheme. It indeed seems that most errors in the literature associated with these schemes have arisen from not realizing that the off-shell propagator and the squared off-shell propagator need separate definitions.

We then discussed the origin of RIS problem from the Schwinger-Dyson equation point of view. The freedom in the definition of the RIS-subtracted propagators corresponds to a freedom in defining the Hermitian part of the pole functions when Schwinger-Dyson equations for unstable particles are reduced to the spectral limit. We used this insight to propose a more physical cut-subtraction scheme, where the modes within distance
\Delta
from the resonance are associated with the on-shell state and those outside this interval are treated as virtual states. This method avoids the negative cross sections and provides a direct measure, in terms of an on-shell weight function
{R}(\Delta)
, to estimate when the on-shell particle picture is a consistent description.

We finished by a detailed numerical comparison of the different subtraction schemes in a toy model which qualitatively reproduces the various issues that can be encountered with the different subtraction schemes. We pointed out that the negative cross sections in the SRS and PVS schemes, while unphysical at face value, arise to compensate for the overestimated on-shell contributions in these schemes. This problem never appears in the cut scheme. It would obviously be interesting to make detailed comparisons of the different approaches in more realistic setups and with full out-of-equilibrium computations.
