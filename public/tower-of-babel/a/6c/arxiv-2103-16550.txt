Matroid stratifications of hypergraph varieties, their realization spaces, and discrete conditional independence models
Oliver Clarke, Kevin Grace, Fatemeh Mohammadi, Harshit J Motwani

Abstract
We study varieties associated to hypergraphs from the point of view of projective geometry and matroid theory. We describe their decompositions into matroid varieties, which may be reducible and can have arbitrary singularities by the Mnëv–Sturmfels universality theorem. We focus on various families of hypergraph varieties for which we explicitly compute an irredundant irreducible decomposition. Our main findings in this direction are threefold: (1) we describe minimal matroids of such hypergraphs; (2) we prove that the varieties of these matroids are irreducible and their union is the hypergraph variety; and (3) we show that every such matroid is realizable over real numbers. As corollaries, we give conceptual decompositions of various, previously-studied, varieties associated with graphs, hypergraphs, and adjacent minors of generic matrices. In particular, our decomposition strategy gives immediate matroid interpretations of the irreducible components of multiple families of varieties associated to conditional independence (CI) models in statistical theory and unravels their symmetric structures which hugely simplifies the computations.

Following

Then, the universality theorem states that point and line configurations exhibit all equivalence classes of this relation called singularity types.

We also note that the defining equations of matroid varieties are in general very difficult to calculate. These equations often arise from geometric constraints satisfied by the matroid. Some of them can be interpreted as rank conditions on certain submatrices, however this is not true in general.
Example
3.6

2.1 Hypergraph varieties.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a field,

be two positive integers,

X=(x_{ij})
be a

matrix of indeterminates and

We denote by

Definition 2.1

A (simple) hypergraph

The elements of

•

The

•

The variety of

which is given by

V_{\Delta}=\{X\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}:\ \textrm{Rank}(X_{F})<|F|\text{ for each }F\text{ in }\Delta\}.

The ideal

and its variety

depend on the value

, i.e. the dimension of the ambient space. However, to keep our notation concise, we suppress

from our notation. Unless otherwise stated, all results for hypergraph ideals and their varieties hold for all

as long as

Problem 2.2
.

2.2 Matroid varieties.

In this subsection, we recall the definitions of the realization space of a matroid and its associated variety. We refer the reader to

Matroid stratifications of hypergraph varieties

3.1 The general decomposition theorem.

We now use our notation from §
2
to decompose any hypergraph variety into certain matroid varieties. In subsequent sections, we will investigate techniques which will allow us to strengthen this statement to an irredundant irreducible decomposition. The following theorem may be interpreted from the perspective of the stratification of Grassmannians by matroid varieties. See Remark
3.2
.

Theorem 3.1

(Decomposition Theorem)
.

Let
\Delta\subseteq 2^{[n]}
be a hypergraph on
[n]
and let
\mathcal{M}(\Delta)
be the collection of realizable matroids whose collections of dependent sets contain
\Delta
. Then,

V_{\Delta}=\bigcup_{M\in\mathcal{M}(\Delta)}V_{M}.

In particular, we have
\sqrt{I_{\Delta}}=\bigcap_{M\in\mathcal{M}(\Delta)}I_{M}
.

Proof.

Let
M\in\mathcal{M}(\Delta)
. We begin by showing that
I_{\Delta}\subseteq I_{M}
.
By definition,
I_{\Delta}
is generated by minors, so let
f\in I_{\Delta}
be any such minor. We may assume without loss of generality that
f
is a maximal minor of the submatrix
X_{F}
for some
F\in\Delta
. For each matroid
M\in\mathcal{M}(\Delta)
and each point
A
in the realization space
\Gamma_{M}
, the columns of
A_{F}
are linearly dependent since
F
is a dependent set of
M
. Thus
f
vanishes on
\Gamma_{M}
, and by definition,
f
vanishes on its Zariski closure which is
V_{M}
. Thus, we have that
f\in I(V_{M})=I_{M}
, so
I_{\Delta}\subseteq I_{M}
. Hence,
V_{M}\subseteq V_{\Delta}
.
Let
A
be any point in the variety
V_{\Delta}
. The matrix
A
is a realization of a matroid, which we denote
M_{A}
, on
[n]
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. For each
F\in\Delta
, the ideal
I_{\Delta}
contains all
|F|
-minors of
X_{F}
, so all
|F|
-minors of
A_{F}
vanish. Therefore, the columns of
A_{F}
are linearly dependent. Hence
F
is a dependent set in
M_{A}
, so
M_{A}\in\mathcal{M}(\Delta)
.
∎

3.2 Combinatorial closure.

In Theorem
3.1
, we decomposed the hypergraph variety
V_{\Delta}
into matroid varieties. But this decomposition may have redundant components, because
V_{M^{\prime}}\subset V_{M}
for some
M,M^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(\Delta)
. To solve Problem
2.2
, we must determine the matroids that are necessary in the decomposition. It is clear that all matroids which are minimal with respect to the dependency order are necessary in the decomposition. One could conjecture that these are enough. Unfortunately this is
not
true, as we will see in Example
3.6
. Nevertheless, we will define an alternative decomposition for which the minimal matroids correspond to irredundant parts of a decomposition. We do this by introducing the
combinatorial closure
of a matroid. This is a generalization of the
weak realization space
of a matroid
[
BS89
, page 70]
to an ambient space of dimension greater than the rank of the matroid.

In the following remark we connect our setting to that of the stratification of the Grassmannian by matroids.

Remark 3.2
.

The Grassmannian
\Gr(d,n+d)
admits a stratification

[

is the matroid stratum of

, where

is the matroid associated to the point

. Let us restrict to the standard affine open patch

of

where the matrices are of the form

such that

is a

\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}

For example, take

So,

In this setting, we may restate Theorem

\bigcup_{\mathcal{M}}S_{\mathcal{M}}\cap V(I_{\Delta})\cap U_{0}=\bigcup_{M\in\mathcal{M}(\Delta)}V_{M}.

Definition 3.3
.

We define the
combinatorial closure

V_{M}^{\mathrm{comb}}
of a matroid
M
to be the union of all matroid varieties
V_{M^{\prime}}
for which
M^{\prime}\geq M
. In other words,

V_{M}^{\mathrm{comb}}=\bigcup_{M^{\prime}\geq M}V_{M^{\prime}}

We will denote the ideal of the combinatorial closure by
I_{M}^{\mathrm{comb}}
.

Remark 3.4
.

(i)

Note that
V_{M}^{\mathrm{comb}}
might not be a matroid variety itself. The inclusion
V_{M}\subseteq V_{M}^{\mathrm{comb}}
holds in general, however the equality might not hold. (See Example
3.6
below.) We will call
V_{M}
the
central component
of
V_{M}^{\mathrm{comb}}
and matroid varieties which intersect the complement will be called
non-central components
.

(ii)

The combinatorial closure is the closure of the union of realization spaces, i.e.
V_{M}^{\mathrm{comb}}=\overline{\bigcup_{M^{\prime}\geq M}\Gamma_{M^{\prime}}}
. This follows from the topological fact that a finite union of closures coincides with the closure of a union of sets.

The ideal of the combinatorial closure of a matroid
M
can be seen as follows. The collection of circuits
\mathcal{C}(M)
of
M
can be considered as a hypergraph. So the hypergraph ideal
I_{\mathcal{C}(M)}
for a given
d
, is defined as:

I_{\mathcal{C}(M)}=\big\langle[A|B]_{X}:\ A\subseteq[d],B\in\mathcal{C}(M),|A|=|B|\big\rangle.

Lemma 3.5
.

For any matroid
M
we have that

V_{M}^{\mathrm{comb}}=V_{\mathcal{C}(M)}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ or equivalently }\hskip 10.00002ptI_{M}^{\mathrm{comb}}=\sqrt{I_{\mathcal{C}(M)}}\ .

Proof.

Let
X\in V_{M}^{\mathrm{comb}}
be a collection of vectors, then
X\in\Gamma_{M^{\prime}}
for some
M^{\prime}\geq M
. So, any
B\in\mathcal{C}(M)
is a dependent set of
M^{\prime}
, and therefore
[A|B]_{X}=0
for any
A\subseteq[d]
with
|A|=|B|
. This shows that
V_{M}^{\mathrm{comb}}\subseteq V_{\mathcal{C}(M)}
.

Similarly, let
X
be a point in
V_{\mathcal{C}(M)}
, and let
M_{X}
be the matroid represented by
X
. For any
B\in\mathcal{C}(M)
and any subset
A\subseteq[d]
with
|A|=|B|
, we have
[A|B]_{X}=0
. Therefore, any
B\in\mathcal{C}(M)
is also a dependent set of
M_{X}
, hence
M_{X}\geq M
. This shows that
V_{\mathcal{C}(M)}\subseteq V_{M}^{\mathrm{comb}}
.
∎

In general, the combinatorial closures of matroid varieties are
reducible
.

Remark 3.7

We note that Example

We recall the definition of ideal quotients and saturation. See, e.g.

Lemma 3.8
.

Consider a polynomial ring

. Let

be a radical ideal and

any ideal. Then
I:J=I:J^{\infty}

For a matroid
M
, let us define its
bases ideal

J_{M}=\sqrt{\prod_{B\text{ basis of }M}\big\langle[A|B]_{X}:\ A\subseteq[d],|A|=|B|\big\rangle}.

If

is
generated by the product of all maximal minors of

whose columns are bases of

Proposition 3.9
.

The ideal of the matroid variety can be obtained by saturating
I(V_{M}^{\mathrm{comb}})
with respect to

:

I_{M}=I(V_{M}^{\mathrm{comb}}):J_{M}^{\infty}.

Proof.

Geometrically, the saturation
I(V_{M}^{\mathrm{comb}}):J_{M}^{\infty}
corresponds to the ideal of
\overline{V_{M}^{\mathrm{comb}}\setminus V(J_{M})}
; see
[

, §4, Theorem 10(iii)]
. By Lemma

, if

is a radical ideal, then
I:J=I:J^{\infty}
.
So it is enough to show that the difference
V_{M}^{\mathrm{comb}}\setminus V(J_{M})
is the realization space
\Gamma_{M}
.
For this, notice that the variety

consists of collections of vectors for which at least one of the bases
B
of

is dependent. Therefore, the difference
V_{M}^{\mathrm{comb}}\setminus V(J_{M})
consists of collections of vectors

for which all bases

of

are independent, which is by definition the realization space

.
∎

The above observation is shown in

under the identification of

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
\Delta\subseteq 2^{[n]}
be a hypergraph on
[n]
and let

be the collection of minimal realizable matroids whose dependent sets contain
\Delta
. Then

\sqrt{I_{\Delta}}=\bigcap_{M\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(\Delta)}I_{M}^{\mathrm{comb}}\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{or equivalently}\hskip 10.00002ptV_{\Delta}=\bigcup_{M\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(\Delta)}V^{\mathrm{comb}}_{M}.

Proof.

Following the notation of Theorem

, let
\mathcal{M}(\Delta)
be the collection of realizable matroids whose collections of dependent sets contain
\Delta
. We have that
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(\Delta)
consists of the minimal matroids in
\mathcal{M}(\Delta)
. We also have that if

then

. So it follows that,

\bigcap_{M\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(\Delta)}I_{M}^{\mathrm{comb}}=\bigcap_{M\in\mathcal{M}(\Delta)}I_{M}^{\mathrm{comb}}.

And so the proof is complete by Theorem

.
∎

3.3 Decomposition strategy.

We can now make precise the decomposition strategy detailed
in §
1
.
By Proposition
3.10
, we have a decomposition of the hypergraph variety
V_{\Delta}
into combinatorial closures of minimally dependent matroids for
\Delta
. To upgrade this to an irreducible decomposition, there are several remaining steps.

First, one has to find the minimal collection of matroid varieties which cover
V_{M}^{\mathrm{comb}}
. In particular, one has to show that the matroids appearing in this collection are realizable, since otherwise they are clearly redundant. In general, these matroid varieties might not be irreducible, so one has to either prove their irreducibility (which we will do in several examples) or find their irreducible decomposition.
By going through these steps, we will obtain such decompositions
for various families of hypergraphs. It still might be the case that some of the components are redundant. So to obtain the actual minimal decomposition, one has to check which ones are necessary.
We remark that step (ii) of the decomposition strategy, i.e. writing
V^{\mathrm{comb}}_{M}
as a minimal union of matroid varieties, involves showing that the matroid varieties whose union is
V_{M}^{\mathrm{comb}}
are nonempty. In other words, we must show that the matroids are realizable. In particular, if
V_{M}^{\mathrm{comb}}=V_{M}
for each minimally dependent matroid
M
of
\Delta
, then step (ii) can be accomplished by showing that all of the minimally dependent matroids for
\Delta
are realizable.

4
Irreducible point and line configurations

In this section, we will introduce point and line configurations. These are a family of matroids whose ideals appear as prime components of many examples of hypergraph ideals. We will prove Theorem
4.5
which allows us to inductively build up configurations with irreducible varieties. In particular, using this theorem, we will show that all configurations with at most
6
lines have irreducible varieties; see Theorem
4.2
.

Notation
.

We write
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{I})
for a configuration of points and lines in the plane, or simply a configuration. The set
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}
is the collection of
points
,
\mathcal{L}=\{\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{k}\}
is the collection of
lines
and
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{L}
is the set of
incidences
of points and lines. If the point
p
lies on the line
\ell
then
(p,\ell)\in\mathcal{I}
and we say that
p
is incident to line
\ell
, or
\ell
passes through
p
. We assume that any two distinct lines meet in at most one point.

For each point
p\in\mathcal{P}
, we write
\mathcal{L}_{p}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
for the set of lines which pass through the point
p
. With a slight abuse of notation, we will often identify the line
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
with the set of points which lie on the line
\ell
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{I})
be a configuration. For a line
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
we define
\mathcal{C}\backslash\ell=(\mathcal{P}^{\prime},\mathcal{L}\backslash\ell,\mathcal{I}\cap(\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\times(\mathcal{L}\backslash\ell))
to be the configuration where
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
is the collection of points of
\mathcal{C}
which do not lie solely on
\ell
.

For each configuration
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{I})
, there is a simple matroid of rank at most
3
associated to it. The matroid has ground set
\mathcal{P}
, and is defined such that a
3
-subset
D\subseteq\mathcal{P}
is dependent if and only if there exists a line
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
with
D\subseteq\ell
. The realization spaces for point and line configurations, are the realization spaces for the matroid; see Definition
2.3
.
In the projective plane, any two distinct points determine a line and so any pair of points in
\mathcal{P}
can be taken to lie on a line. However, if there is not a third element of
\mathcal{P}
on the line, then this line does not give rise to any dependent set in the matroid. Therefore, in the remainder of this section, we require that each line in
\mathcal{L}
contain three or more points in
\mathcal{P}
.

We note that configurations
\mathcal{C}
are purely combinatorial objects. In particular, they need not be realizable, i.e. we may have
\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}=\emptyset
. For example, the Fano plane, see Example
3.11
, is the smallest non-realizable configuration over
\mathbb{C}
. In the following sections, the purpose of using configurations is to give a parametrization of the irreducible components of certain hypergraph ideals via their realization spaces
\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}
. For these cases, we may assume that the configurations are realizable. However, for families of configurations that we study directly, such as forest-like configurations, we will prove that they are indeed realizable.

Theorem 4.2
.

For any configuration
\mathcal{C}
with at most
6
lines,
\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}
is irreducible with respect to the Zariski topology.

We prove this theorem using the Grassmann-Cayley algebra.
We review some of its theory following
[
Stu08
]
.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
V
be a vector space of dimension
d
over
\mathbb{C}
and let
\bigwedge(V)
be the exterior algebra of
V
. The Grassmann-Cayley algebra is the vector space
\bigwedge(V)
together with the two operations
\wedge
and
\vee
.
We denote by
a_{1}\vee a_{2}\vee\dots\vee a_{k}\in\bigwedge(V)
the extensor of length
k
which is also referred to as the join of
a_{1},a_{2}\dots,a_{k}
. The meet operation
\wedge
is a binary operation on
two extensors
A
and
B
of length
j,k
with
j+k\geq d
as:

A\wedge B:=\sum_{\sigma}{\rm sign}(\sigma)[a_{\sigma(1)},\dots,a_{\sigma(d-k)},b_{1},\dots,b_{k}]a_{\sigma(d-k+1)}\vee\cdots\vee a_{\sigma(j)},

where the sum is taken over all permutations
\sigma
of
\{1,2,\dots,j\}
.

Any extensor
A=a_{1}\vee\dots\vee a_{k}
has an associated vector space
\overline{A}={\rm span}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
with following properties.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
A
and
B
be two extensors. Then the following hold:

•

Any extensor
A
is uniquely determined from
\overline{A}
up to a scalar multiple
[
Stu08
, Section 3.3]
.

•

The meet of two extensors is again an extensor
[
Stu08
, Theorem 3.3.2 (b)]
.

•

We have that
A\wedge B\neq 0
if and only if
\overline{A}+\overline{B}=V
. In this case
\overline{A\wedge B}=\overline{A}\cap\overline{B}

[
Stu08
, Theorem 3.3.2 (c)]
.

In order to prove Theorem
4.2
, we will take cases on the possible configurations. We begin by proving the following theorem which allows us to build new irreducible configurations from old. The cases in the following theorem are depicted in Figure
4
and explained further in Example
4.6
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{I})
be a configuration and
\ell
a line in
\mathcal{L}
. Let
S=\{p\in\ell:|\mathcal{L}_{p}|\geq 3\}
be set of the points on
\ell
which lie on at least
2
other lines. Suppose that
|S|\leq 2
. If
\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}\backslash\ell}
is irreducible then so is
\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}
.

Proof.

Let
n
be the number of points in
\mathcal{C}\backslash\ell
and
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{m}
be the lines of
\mathcal{C}\backslash\ell
which intersect
\ell
in
\mathcal{L}
at a unique point. That is, if
\ell_{i}
intersects
\ell
at the point
p
, then there are no other lines through
p
, i.e.
\mathcal{L}_{p}=\{\ell,\ell_{i}\}
.
For each line
\ell_{i}
we fix two distinct points
p_{i,1}
and
p_{i,2}
in
\mathcal{P}\backslash\ell
which lie on
\ell_{i}
.
For each realization
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}\backslash\ell}
we write
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
for the points of
\gamma
and
\gamma_{\ell_{1}},\dots,\gamma_{\ell_{m}}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{3}
for the
2
-dimensional linear subspaces corresponding to the lines. Note that the subspace
\gamma_{\ell_{i}}
is the linear span of the points
\gamma_{p_{i,1}}
and
\gamma_{p_{i,2}}
. We denote by
r
the number of points of
\mathcal{C}
which lie on
\ell
and no other lines.
We proceed by taking cases on
|S|\in\{0,1,2\}
.

Case 1.
Assume that
|S|=0
. We define the space

X=\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}\backslash\ell}\times\textrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{3})\times(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{r}\times\mathbb{C}^{m},

where
\textrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{3})
denotes the set of linear maps from
\mathbb{C}^{2}
to
\mathbb{C}^{3}
. Here, we think of a linear map
\phi\in\textrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{3})
as a
3\times 2

matrix with entries in
\mathbb{C}
. We define the subset
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
to be the collection of
(\gamma,\phi,(y_{1},\dots,y_{r}),(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}))\in X
such that the following conditions hold:

(a)

\rank(\phi)=2
,

(b)

for each
i\in[n]
, the point
\gamma_{i}
does not lie in
\phi(\mathbb{C}^{2})\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{3}
,

(c)

for each
i\in[r]
, the point
\phi(y_{i})
does not lie in any of the subspaces
\gamma_{\ell_{1}},\dots,\gamma_{\ell_{m}}
,

(d)

for any
i,j\in[r]
, if
\phi(y_{i})=\phi(y_{j})
then
i=j
.

Since
\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}\backslash\ell}
,
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{r}
and
\textrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{3})
are irreducible, we have that
X
is a product of irreducible varieties over an algebraically closed field. Hence,
X
is also irreducible. By construction, each of the conditions for the subset
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
above is a rank constraint on certain submatrices. Explicitly we have that:

•

\rank(\phi)\neq 2
if all of the
2
-minors of
\phi
are zero,

•

Assuming that
\rank(\phi)=2
, a point
\gamma_{i}
lies inside
\phi(\mathbb{C}^{2})
if the
3
-minor
[\gamma_{i},\phi(1,0),\phi(0,1)]
is zero,

•

A point
\phi(y_{i})
lies inside the subspace
\gamma_{\ell_{i}}
if the
3
-minor
[\phi(y_{i}),\gamma_{p_{i,1}},\gamma_{p_{i,2}}]
is zero,

•

If
\rank(\phi)=2
then it follows that
\phi
is injective.

Therefore
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
is an open subset which implies that
X^{\prime}
is also irreducible.

Now, for each
(\gamma,\phi,(y_{1},\dots,y_{r}),(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}))\in X^{\prime}
and for each
i\in[m]
we fix a non-zero point
q_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
which lies in the intersection of
\gamma_{\ell_{i}}
and
\phi(\mathbb{C}^{2})
. Note that the ambient space is
\mathbb{C}^{3}
, hence any pair of
2
-dimensional linear subspaces intersect. By construction of
X^{\prime}
, we have that
\phi(\mathbb{C}^{2})
and
\gamma_{\ell_{i}}
do not coincide. Hence,
\dim(\phi(\mathbb{C}^{2})\cap\gamma_{\ell_{i}})=1
and
q_{i}
is unique up to a non-zero scalar multiple. We give an explicit formula for
q_{i}
using the Grassmann-Cayley algebra as follows.
Recall that
\gamma_{\ell_{i}}
is the span of
a_{1}:=\gamma_{p_{i,1}}
,
a_{2}:=\gamma_{p_{i,2}}
and
\phi(\mathbb{C}^{2})
is the span of
b_{1}:=\phi(1,0)
,
b_{2}:=\phi(0,1)
. Since the spaces
\gamma_{\ell_{i}}
and
\phi(\mathbb{C}^{2})
do not coincide, they must span the entire space
\mathbb{C}^{3}
. Since
\dim(\phi(\mathbb{C}^{2})\cap\gamma_{\ell_{i}})=1
, by Lemma
4.4
, we have that
q_{i}:=[a_{1},b_{1},b_{2}]a_{2}-[a_{2},b_{1},b_{2}]a_{1}\in\gamma_{\ell_{i}}\cap\phi(\mathbb{C}^{2})
is a non-zero vector lying in the intersection.
We define the map

\psi:X^{\prime}\rightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{3})^{n+m+r}:(\gamma,\phi,(y_{1},\dots,y_{r}),(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}))\mapsto(\gamma,\lambda_{1}q_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}q_{m},\phi(y_{1}),\dots,\phi(y_{r})).

By construction,
\phi
is a linear map and the coordinates of the points
q_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
are polynomials in the entries of
\gamma
. Thus
\psi
is a polynomial map and so it is continuous.

It remains to show that

.

Case 2.
Assume that

where

and

5
Combinatorial closures of configurations

In this section, we will focus on point and line configurations whose underlying graph is a forest.
As in the previous section, if
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{I})
is a point and line configuration, then each line in
\mathcal{L}
contains at least three points in
\mathcal{P}
, unless stated otherwise.

We will give a complete description of their combinatorial closures by describing their components. Our main tool for this section is the
perturbation argument
which gives a way to determine when a particular point in the combinatorial closure belongs to a specific realization space. Let us begin by making precise our notation.

Let
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{I})
be a point and line configuration with the ordered set of points
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\}
. Let
G_{\mathcal{C}}
be a graph with vertex set
\mathcal{P}
and edges
\{p_{i},p_{j}\}
such that

•

both points lie on the same line
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
(which is necessarily unique) and

•

there is no point
p_{k}
lying on
\ell
with
i<k<j
(or
j<k<i
).

Intuitively,
G_{\mathcal{C}}
is obtained from
\mathcal{C}
by making points into vertices and lines into paths. Moreover, every edge of
G_{\mathcal{C}}
is in exactly one of these paths. Each pair of paths intersects in at most one vertex, and this occurs if and only if the corresponding lines intersect in a point of
\mathcal{P}
.

Definition 5.1
.

A configuration
\mathcal{C}
is
forest-like
if the corresponding graph
G_{\mathcal{C}}
is a forest, i.e. it has no cycle.

Note that the graph
G_{\mathcal{C}}
depends on the choice of ordering of the points in
\mathcal{P}
. However, the condition for
G_{\mathcal{C}}
to be a forest is independent of this choice. Essentially, swapping the order of two points on a line corresponds to simply “flipping” an edge of
G_{\mathcal{C}}
. We now make this more precise.

Lemma 5.2
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is forest-like, then any ordering of its points
\mathcal{P}
makes
G_{\mathcal{C}}
a forest.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\}
be an ordering of
\mathcal{P}
such that
G_{\mathcal{C}}
is a forest. Inductively, it suffices to show that the order
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{i-1},p_{i+1},p_{i},p_{i+2},\ldots,p_{n}\}
gives rise to a forest.

If
p_{i}
and
p_{i+1}
do not lie on the same line, then there is no change to
G_{\mathcal{C}}
. So it suffices to consider the case where
p_{i}
and
p_{i+1}
lie on a line
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
. Then
\{p_{i},p_{i+1}\}
is an edge in
G_{\mathcal{C}}
. Let us call these vertices of the graph
v
and
w
. Let
A(v)
be the set of vertices of
G_{\mathcal{C}}
, adjacent to
v
, whose corresponding points in
\mathcal{C}
lie on some line
\ell^{\prime}\neq\ell
, and define
A(w)
similarly. Then swapping the order of
i
and
i+1
corresponds to replacing the edges in
\{\{x,v\}:x\in A(v)\}\cup\{\{x,w\}:x\in A(w)\}
with the edges in
\{\{x,v\}:x\in A(w)\}\cup\{\{x,w\}:x\in A(v)\}
. One can see that this resulting graph is still a forest if
G_{\mathcal{C}}
was a forest.
∎

Because of Lemma
5.2
,
Definition
5.1
is well-defined for point and line configurations with unordered sets of vertices. So, we say that
\mathcal{C}
is forest-like if there exists an ordering of its vertices such that the resulting configuration is forest-like.

The next result will also be helpful.

Lemma 5.3
.

If
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{I})
is a forest-like configuration with
\mathcal{L}\neq\emptyset
, then there exists a line
\ell
which intersects
\mathcal{C}\setminus\ell
in at most one point of
\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

Because two lines intersect in at most one point, it will suffice to show that there exists a line
\ell
which intersects
\mathcal{C}\setminus\ell
(at a point of
\mathcal{P}
) in at most one line. Suppose otherwise for a contradiction. Then every line intersects with at least two other lines. Let
\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ldots
be a sequence of lines in
\mathcal{C}
such that
\ell_{i}
intersects both
\ell_{i-1}
and
\ell_{i+1}
. Since there are only finitely many lines, eventually there will be a repeated element
\ell_{k}
of the sequence.

Since these lines correspond to paths in
G_{\mathcal{C}}
, there is a cycle in
G_{\mathcal{C}}
whose set of edges includes at least one edge from each path corresponding to the lines in the sequence
\ell_{k},\ell_{k+1},\ldots,\ell_{k}
. This cycle contradicts the assumption that
G_{\mathcal{C}}
is a forest.
∎

It is perhaps worth noting that forest-likeness is a rather restrictive condition for a matroid of rank
3
. However, forest-like configurations are not regular (realizable over every field) matroids. For example, no line with four or more points is a regular matroid. Each forest-like configuration is realizable over all infinite fields. To show this, it will be useful to recall the matroid-theoretic notion of
freely adding
an element to a flat of a matroid. Intuitively, this operation takes a new element of the ground set and adds it to a flat as “freely” as possible, that is, we keep as many sets independent as possible; see
[
Cra65
]
and
[
Oxl11
, Section 7.2]
. We will also use this notion in Section
6.3
.

Definition 5.4
.

Let
F_{0}
be a flat of a matroid
M
with rank function
r_{M}
. We say that
M^{\prime}
is the single-element extension obtained by
freely adding

e
to
F_{0}
if the flats of
M^{\prime}
fall into the following disjoint classes:

•

flats
F
of
M
that do not contain
F_{0}
,

•

sets
F\cup e
where
F
is a flat of
M
that contains
F_{0}
, and

•

sets
F\cup e
where
F
is a flat of
M
that does not contain
F_{0}
, and there is no flat
F^{\prime}
of
M
of rank
r_{M}(F)+1
such that
F\subseteq F^{\prime}
and
F_{0}\subseteq F^{\prime}
.

The following results are fairly well-known. In particular, Lemma
5.5
follows from a result of Piff and Welsh
[
PW70
]
; see also
[
Oxl11
, Proposition 11.2.16]
. We provide a more direct proof to keep the paper self-contained.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be an infinite field, and let
M^{\prime}
be a matroid obtained by freely adding an element to a flat
F
of a matroid
M
. Then
M^{\prime}
is
\mathbb{F}
-realizable if and only if
M
is
\mathbb{F}
-realizable.

Proof.

Realizability over a field is closed under deletion. Therefore, if
M^{\prime}
is
\mathbb{F}
-realizable, then so is
M
.

For the converse, let
M
be
\mathbb{F}
-realizable, and let
r
be the rank function of
M
. A flat
F
of
M
corresponds to a linear subspace of
\mathbb{F}^{r(M)}
of dimension
r(F)
. To construct a representation of
M^{\prime}
we must find a point
p
in the subspace corresponding to
F
that is not contained in any subspace corresponding to a flat
F^{\prime}
of
M
that does not contain
F
. For any such flat
F^{\prime}
, the rank of
F\cap F^{\prime}
is strictly less than
r(F)
. Therefore, since
\mathbb{F}
is an infinite field, there are infinitely many such points
p
.
∎

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
M^{\prime}
be obtained by adding a coloop to an
\mathbb{F}
-realizable matroid
M
. Then
M^{\prime}
is
\mathbb{F}
-realizable.

Proof.

Let
A
be a matrix whose columns form a realization of
M
. We construct a matrix realizing
M^{\prime}
by adding a row and column to
A
such that all entries of this row and column are
0
except for the entry contained in both the row and column.
∎

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{I})
be a forest-like point and line configuration, and let
M
be the simple matroid of rank at most
3
associated with it. Then
M
is realizable over all infinite fields.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the number of lines in
\mathcal{L}
. If
|\mathcal{L}|=0
, then
M
is the uniform matroid
U_{r,|\mathcal{P}|}
, where
r=\min\{3,|\mathcal{P}|\}
. For all positive integers
n
, it is clear that
U_{n,n}
is realizable over all fields. If
n>3
, then
U_{3,n}
is obtained from
U_{3,3}
by repeatedly freely adding elements to the rank-
3
flat of the matroid. Therefore, by Lemma
5.5
,
U_{r,|\mathcal{P}|}
is realizable over all infinite fields for all values of
n
.

Now, suppose that we have shown that the result holds for all forest-like configurations with at most
k-1
lines and we wish to prove the result for
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{I})
, where
|\mathcal{L}|=k
. By Lemma
5.3
, there is a line
\ell
in
\mathcal{L}
that intersects with at most one of the points in
\mathcal{C}\setminus\ell
. By the induction hypothesis, the matroid associated with
\mathcal{C}\setminus\ell
is realizable over all infinite fields.

First, we consider the case where
\ell
intersects with a point
v
in
\mathcal{C}\setminus\ell
. Let
w
be an additional point in
\ell
, and let
T=\ell\setminus\{v,w\}
. The matroid
M\backslash T
is obtained from
\mathcal{C}\setminus\ell
either by adding
w
as a coloop or by freely adding
w
to the unique rank-
3
flat (depending on the rank of
\mathcal{C}\setminus\ell
). Then
M
is obtained from
M\backslash T
by freely adding the points in
T
to the rank-
2
flat defined by
v
and
w
.

Now, we consider the case where
\ell
and
\mathcal{C}\setminus\ell
have no points in common. Let
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
be points in
\ell
, and let
T=\ell\setminus\{w_{1},w_{2}\}
. The matroid
M\backslash(T\cup\{w_{2}\})
is obtained from
\mathcal{C}\setminus\ell
either by adding
w_{1}
as a coloop or by freely adding
w_{1}
to the unique rank-
3
flat (depending on the rank of
\mathcal{C}\setminus\ell
). Then
M\backslash T
is obtained from
M\backslash(T\cup\{w_{2}\})
by freely adding
w_{2}
to the unique rank-
3
flat. Then
M
is obtained from
M\backslash T
by freely adding the points in
T
to the rank-
2
flat defined by
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
.

In either case, Lemmas
5.5
and
5.6
imply that
M
is realizable over all infinte fields.
∎

One can see that the last line
\ell
to be added to a forest-like configuration in the inductive process described in the proof of Proposition
5.7
has the property that
|\{p\in\ell:|\mathcal{L}_{p}|\geq 3\}|\leq 1
. In particular, the only point
p\in\ell
that might have the property that
|\mathcal{L}_{p}|\geq 3
is the one point that was on at least one of the other
|\mathcal{L}|-1
lines in
\mathcal{L}
.

So, we have the following straightforward corollary of Theorem
4.5
.

Corollary 5.8
.

The realization space of a forest-like configuration is irreducible with respect to Zariski topology.

The following proof introduces the
perturbation procedure
which we will use throughout this section.

Lemma 5.9

(Perturbation procedure)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a forest-like configuration with realization space
\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Assume that
|\mathcal{L}_{p}|\leq 2
for every
p\in\mathcal{P}
. For every
\epsilon>0
and for any
A\in V^{\mathrm{comb}}_{\mathcal{C}}\backslash\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}
there exists
A^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}
such that
||A-A^{\prime}||<\epsilon
, where
||\cdot||
is the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
.

Proof.

We think of
A
as a realization of a configuration
\mathcal{C}_{A}
. As
A\in V^{\mathrm{comb}}_{\mathcal{C}}\backslash\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}
and the rank of the corresponding matroid is at most
3
, the dependencies satisfied by the configuration
\mathcal{C}_{A}
that are not in
\mathcal{C}
are the following:

•

A point of
\mathcal{C}
may be a loop in
\mathcal{C}_{A}
,

•

Two distinct points of
\mathcal{C}
may coincide in
\mathcal{C}_{A}
,

•

Two distinct lines in
\mathcal{C}
may coincide in
\mathcal{C}_{A}
,

•

A triple of non-collinear points in
\mathcal{C}
may lie on a common line in
\mathcal{C}_{A}
.

We now construct the realization
A^{\prime}
by induction on the number of lines. For the base case, assume that the configuration
\mathcal{C}_{A}
has no lines. Let us form
A^{\prime}
by going through the points in order
p_{1},\dots,p_{r}

and perturbing the corresponding vector in
A
by at most
\epsilon/r
. For each
i\in[r]
, the vector corresponding to point
p_{i}
is perturbed such that it forms no dependencies, listed above, with subsets of points from
\{p_{j}:1\leq j\leq i\}
.

For the induction step, let us assume that for any forest-like configuration
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=(\mathcal{P}^{\prime},\mathcal{L}^{\prime},\mathcal{I}^{\prime})
with at most
n-1
lines, we have that for all
\epsilon>0
and any point
x\in V^{\mathrm{comb}}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}
there exists
x^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}
such that
||x-x^{\prime}||<\epsilon
. Since the graph
G_{\mathcal{C}}
is a forest, we may take
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
to be a line which intersects
\mathcal{C}\backslash\ell
in at most one point. By the inductive hypothesis, we can perturb vectors in
A
corresponding to the configuration
\mathcal{C}\backslash\ell
so that no additional dependencies are satisfied. It remains to show that the points on the line
\ell
may be perturbed to remove all additional dependencies. We proceed by applying some or all of the following steps in order. In particular, after each step we ensure that no new dependencies are introduced which would be removed in a previous step.

(a)

Suppose that the line
\ell
intersects a point
p
in
\mathcal{C}_{A}
which does not belong to
\ell
in
\mathcal{C}
. We may perturb the line
\ell
in
A
to produce
A^{\prime}
so that
\ell
does not pass through the point
p
. In particular, if
\ell
contains
k
points which are not
p
, then we perturb each point by at most
\epsilon/k
.

(b)

Suppose that the line
\ell
coincides with another line
\ell^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{C}_{A}
. Since
\ell
intersects
\mathcal{C}\backslash\ell
in at most one point, we may rotate the line
\ell
in
A
(i.e. perturb each point along the line) by small amount to obtain
A^{\prime}
so that
\ell
does not coincide with any other line.
In particular, if
\ell
contains
k
points which do not lie on the intersection of
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{C}
, then we perturb each point by at most
\epsilon/k
.

(c)

Suppose that two points in
\ell
coincide or some of the points are loops. We may perturb these points away from each other so that they remain on the line
\ell
. Similarly if any of the points are loops, then we can perturb these points away from zero by the same method. In particular, if a point
p
in
\mathcal{C}_{A}
is a loop then we recall that
p
is incident to at most two lines. If it is incident to exactly two lines
\ell,\ell^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{C}
, then we may perturb
p
along
\ell\cap\ell^{\prime}
. Otherwise if
p
is incident only to
\ell
in
\mathcal{C}
then we may perturb it to some non-zero point on
\ell
.

(d)

Suppose that for some point
p_{i}
in
\ell
, there are two distinct points
p_{j},p_{k}
in
\mathcal{C}\backslash\ell
such that
p_{i},p_{j}
and
p_{k}
lie on a line
\ell^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{C}_{A}
but do not lie on a line in
\mathcal{C}
. By the step (a) of the procedure, we have that
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
are distinct lines. For this case, it is useful to consider all lines of
\mathcal{C}_{A}
, including those which contain only two points. The points of intersection between
\ell
and all other distinct lines of
\mathcal{C}_{A}
is a finite set. Since we work over the infinite field
\mathbb{C}
and the line is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{C}^{1}
with respect to Euclidean topology, therefore we may perturb
p_{i}
along
\ell
so that it lies only on
\ell
and no other line.

As a result of the above procedure, we have constructed a realization
A^{\prime}
of

\mathcal{C}
with
||A-A^{\prime}||<\epsilon
.
∎

Remark 5.10
.

By perturbation procedure, the corresponding
configurations are realizable over any subfield of
\mathbb{C}
.

Let

Theorem 5.11
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{I})
be a forest-like configuration with

. Then the combinatorial closure

coincides with the matroid variety

Proof.

Recall that

where

Definition 5.12
.

Let
\mathcal{C}

Before proving an analogous result for a general forest-like point and line configuration, we consider the following simple configuration which has at most one point that lies on more than two lines.

Lemma 5.13
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{I})
be a forest-like configuration which has a unique point

such that

and for all other points

we have
|\mathcal{L}_{p_{j}}|\leq 2
.
Then the combinatorial closure

has exactly two irreducible components: one is the central component and the other arises by setting the intersection point

to be a loop.

Proof.

For each element
A\in V^{\mathrm{comb}}_{\mathcal{C}}
, we write
A_{p}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
for the column vector corresponding to the intersection point
p
. We have that
V_{\mathcal{C}}
is the central component of

and we write

for the collection of elements of the combinatorial closure where the intersection point is zero. Let
\mathcal{C}_{0}
be the configuration obtained from

by setting the intersection point to be a loop. Clearly, we have

, the lemma follows from showing that

by proving the opposite inclusion.

Take any element

and fix
\epsilon>0
. We will show that there exists

such that

by applying the perturbation procedure. Let us take cases on whether

is zero.

Assume that

Since

is a configuration that contains only points

Theorem 5.14
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{I})
be a forest-like
configuration. Let

be the collection of points contained in at least

has at most

irredundant irreducible components. Moreover, these components can be obtained
from
\mathcal{C}
by setting a subset of

Proof.

For each element
A\in V^{\mathrm{comb}}_{\mathcal{C}}
and point

, we write
A_{p}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
for the column vector corresponding to the point

, we write
\mathcal{C}_{J}
for the configuration obtained from
\mathcal{C}

. Note that by Corollary

, each variety

is irreducible. By construction, it is immediately clear that

V^{\mathrm{comb}}_{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq\bigcup_{J\subseteq S}V_{\mathcal{C}_{J}}

Fix

and
\epsilon>0
. We will construct

for some

such that
||A-A^{\prime}||<\epsilon
.

We proceed by induction on
s:=|S|
.
If
s=1
, then the result follows by Lemma
5.13
. So let us assume that
s>1
.
Since
G_{\mathcal{C}}
is a forest, we can find a point

such that the path (if it exists) between any other pair of points in

does not pass through
p
. Let

be the collection of points

lying in the same connected component of

as

. We have

. Clearly,

\mathcal{P}^{\prime}

, where

is the set of vectors obtained from

is zero.

Case 1.
Assume that

. Let

, we construct

6
Consecutive forest hypergraphs
\Delta_{G}

We fix a
d\times n
matrix
X=(x_{i,j})
of indeterminates and the polynomial ring
R=\mathbb{C}[X]
. Throughout this section, we fix a forest
G
(acyclic graph) with vertices labeled
1,\dots,n
.

Definition 6.1
.

We define the
consecutive forest hypergraph of
G
as

\displaystyle\Delta_{G}=\min\left(P_{2}(G)\cup\binom{[n]}{4}\right)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where}\hskip 10.00002ptP_{2}(G)=\{P\subseteq V(G):\ P\text{ is a $2$-path in $G$}\}.

We recall that a
k
-path
P
in
G
is a subgraph of
G
whose vertex set is a non-repeating sequence
p_{1},\dots,p_{k+1}\subseteq V(G)
and whose edge set is
\{\{p_{i},p_{i+1}\}:1\leq i\leq k\}
. We identify
P
with its vertex set, and simply write
C\subseteq P
instead of
C\subseteq V(P)
. If
Q
is another path, then
P\cup Q
is the set of all vertices of
G
lying in
P
and
Q
.

Remark 6.2
.

The hypergraph ideal
I_{\Delta_{G}}
of the
n
-path
G
is exactly the ideal
I_{3n}(3)
of adjacent minors studied in
[
HS04
]
. We note that our construction generalises this particular family by allowing
G
to be an arbitrary graph. In
[
HS04
]
, the ideals
I_{mn}(m)
are studied for general
m\geq 3
which can be thought of as the consecutive forest hypergraph of the
n
-path except with higher order minors.
These ideals can be studied using positroid varieties as described in
[
KLS13
]
. It is not too difficult to show that each of the prime components of
I_{mn}(m)
is a positroid variety. For instance, one can use the characterisation of positroids via their excluded minors
[
Oh09
, Theorem 16]
or the decomposition of the Grassmannian into positroid varieties
[
KLS13
, Section 5.2]
. In particular, we note that the defining ideals of positroid varieties are generated by minors
[
KLS13
, Theorem 5.15]
. However, this is no longer true for the consecutive forest hypergraph varieties. More precisely, the varieties arising in the irreducible decomposition of
V_{\Delta_{G}}
are, in general, not positroid varieties; for instance see Example
6.21
.

6.1 Minimal matroids.

In this section, we will define so-called prime collections which are collections of subsets of vertices of
G
. To each prime collection
\mathcal{S}
we will associate a unique matroid
M_{\mathcal{S}}
.
We will then see that such matroids are realizable over the real numbers and have irreducible realization spaces. Moreover, we prove that such matroids appear as minimal matroids for consecutive forest hypergraphs.

Definition 6.3
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a collection of singleton subsets of
[n]
. We say
\mathcal{S}
is a
prime collection of singletons
for
G
if
\mathcal{S}
satisfies the following inductive definition.

•

The empty set
\mathcal{S}=\emptyset
is a prime collection of singletons.

•

If
|\mathcal{S}|\geq 1
, then
\mathcal{S}=\{\{s_{1}\},\dots,\{s_{t}\}\}
is a prime collection of singletons if, for each natural number
i
with
1\leq i\leq t
,

\mathcal{S}\backslash\{s_{i}\}
is a prime collection of singletons and

satisfies the following two rules:

1.

s_{i}
is not a leaf or isolated vertex of
G^{\prime}
,

2.

If
s_{i}
is adjacent to a leaf of
G^{\prime}
then it has degree at least
3
in
G^{\prime}
,

where
G^{\prime}
is the induced subgraph of
G
obtained by deleting the vertices in the set
\{s_{1},\dots,s_{i-1},s_{i+1},\dots,s_{t}\}
.

For ease of notation and when it is clear, we denote a collection of singletons
\mathcal{S}=\{\{s_{1}\},\dots,\{s_{t}\}\}
as
\{s_{1},\dots,s_{t}\}
.

Definition 6.4
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a collection of singleton subsets
{\rm sing}(\mathcal{S})
and
2
-subsets of

be the induced subgraph of

•

We say
\mathcal{S}
is a

for

if:

–

The
{\rm sing}(\mathcal{S})
is a prime collection of singletons for
G
.

–

The
2
-subsets in
\mathcal{S}
are a subset of edges of

•

Proposition 6.8

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a valid collection. Let
\mathcal{C}
be the collection of subsets of

consisting of the

1.

singletons in
\mathcal{S}
,

2.

2
-subsets of clouds of
\mathcal{S}
,

3.

3
-subsets of
[n]
which are not blocked and do not contain any set in
\mathcal{S}
, and

4.

4
-subsets of
[n]
containing none of the sets listed in 1, 2, or 3 above.

Then
\mathcal{C}
is the collection of circuits of a matroid.

Proof.

We need to check that
\mathcal{C}
satisfies the circuit elimination axiom. That is, if
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}
and
x\in C_{1}\cap C_{2}
, we must check that there is a set in
\mathcal{C}
contained in
(C_{1}\cup C_{2})\backslash\{x\}
. This is clear if either
C_{1}
or
C_{2}
is a set of size
1
or
4
. If
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
both have size
2
, then
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are contained in the same cloud. Thus,
(C_{1}\cup C_{2})\backslash\{x\}
is contained in the same cloud, implying that
(C_{1}\cup C_{2})\backslash\{x\}\in\mathcal{C}
.

Suppose
|C_{1}|=2
and
|C_{2}|=3
. Let
C_{1}=\{v,x\}
and
C_{2}=\{x,y,z\}
.

Since
C_{2}
is not blocked, it is contained in a connected component of
G
.

Let
P_{1}
be the path from
x
to
y
; let
P_{2}
be the path from
v
to
y
; and let
P_{3}
be the path from
v
to
x
. Since
\{x,y,z\}\in\mathcal{C}
, we have
\{x,y\}\notin\mathcal{C}
. Therefore,
y
is not contained in the same cloud that contains
v
and
x
. Since a cloud contains every vertex on the path between two vertices in the cloud, we see then that either
P_{3}\subseteq P_{1}
, or
P_{3}\subseteq P_{2}
, or
P_{3}=P_{1}\cap P_{2}
. In either case, the fact that
\{x,y\}
is not blocked implies that
\{v,y\}
is not blocked. Similarly,
\{v,z\}
is not blocked. We already know that
\{y,z\}
is not blocked because
\{y,z\}\subseteq\{x,y,z\}
. Therefore, we conclude that
\{v,y,z\}
is not blocked and contains a member of
\mathcal{C}
.

Finally, we consider the case where
|C_{1}|=|C_{2}|=3
. If
|C_{1}\cap C_{2}|=1
, then
(C_{1}\cup C_{2})\backslash\{x\}
has size
4
and therefore contains a member of
\mathcal{C}
. If
|C_{1}\cap C_{2}|=2
, let

and

. We will show that

contains a member of
\mathcal{C}
. Since
\{v,x,y\}
and

Lemma 6.16

(Constrained minimal matroids)
.

Let

be a matroid with
\Delta_{G}\subseteq\mathcal{D}(M)
whose circuits of size one and two are the members of

. Then

.

Proof.

By assumption, all of the circuits of

of size one and two are also circuits of
M
. All
4
-subsets of
[n]
are dependent in both
M
and
M_{\mathcal{S}}
. Therefore, to show that
M_{\mathcal{S}}\leq M
, it suffices to show that all
3
-circuits of
M_{\mathcal{S}}
are dependent in
M
. Let

be such a circuit of

Let

be the induced subgraph of
G
obtained by removing those vertices

for which
\{v\}\in\mathcal{S}
. Now since

is a circuit in
M_{\mathcal{S}}
, we have that for any pair of elements

from

to

and it is not blocked by

. We denote by

By abuse of notation, we identify
\textrm{Conv}(A)
with the induced subgraph of

whose vertices are

To complete the proof of the theorem, it suffices to prove the following claim. Since, as a result
we have that
\{a,b,c\}
is a dependent set in
M
. Therefore, every dependent set in
M_{\mathcal{S}}
is also dependent in

All

{\rm Conv}(a,b,c)

.

We must show that every

\{a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime}\}\subseteq\textrm{Conv}(a,b,c)

. We proceed by induction on
|\textrm{Conv}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})|

For the base case, suppose that
|\textrm{Conv}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})|=3
. Then
\textrm{Conv}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})
is a path in

with three vertices. By definition of

, we have that
\{a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime}\}\in\Delta_{G}
. Since
\Delta_{G}\subseteq\mathcal{D}(M)
, we have that
\{a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime}\}
is dependent in

.
For the inductive step, assume that all
3
-subsets of
{\rm Conv}(a,b,c)
whose convex hull has size at most

are dependent and
|\textrm{Conv}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})|=k+1
. Since
|\textrm{Conv}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})|\geq 4
, there is a vertex

. Note that neither

,

,

, nor

because then it would be a loop of

. This implies either that

is blocked or that an element of

is a loop of

. This is impossible since

is a circuit of

.

By induction,

, and

is to show
that
M_{\mathcal{S}}
is minimal for any prime collection
\mathcal{S}
.

Lemma 6.18
.

If
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
are prime collections with
M_{\mathcal{S}}\geq M_{\mathcal{T}}
, then
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{T}
.

Proof.

Let us begin by considering the loops of
M_{\mathcal{S}}
and
M_{\mathcal{T}}
. Since
M_{\mathcal{S}}\geq M_{\mathcal{T}}
, we have that every loop of
M_{\mathcal{T}}
is a loop of
M_{\mathcal{S}}
. Suppose for a contradiction that
M_{\mathcal{S}}
has strictly more loops than
M_{\mathcal{T}}
. Let
G_{\mathcal{S}}
be the induced subgraph of
G
obtained by removing the loops of
M_{\mathcal{S}}
. We define
G_{\mathcal{T}}
similarly. Pick any loop
v
in
M_{\mathcal{S}}
that is not a loop in
M_{\mathcal{T}}
.
We now show that
\deg(v)\geq 2
in
G_{\mathcal{T}}
. Suppose otherwise. Then all but at most one of the neighbors of
v
are loops in
M_{\mathcal{T}}
. Since
M_{\mathcal{S}}\geq M_{\mathcal{T}}
, all but at most one of the neighbors of
v
are loops in
M_{\mathcal{S}}
also. But this fact, with the assumption that
v
is a loop in
M_{\mathcal{S}}
, contradicts the assumption that
\mathcal{S}
is a prime collection. Therefore,
\deg(v)\geq 2
in
G_{\mathcal{T}}
. Similar reasoning shows that each vertex adjacent to
v
in
G_{\mathcal{T}}
must be adjacent to a vertex in
G_{\mathcal{T}}
that is not a loop in
M_{\mathcal{S}}
.

Since
\mathcal{T}
is a prime collection, there is at least one vertex
x
adjacent to
v
in
G_{\mathcal{T}}
such that
\{x,v\}\notin\mathcal{T}
. Since
\deg(v)\geq 2
in
G_{\mathcal{T}}
, let
y\neq x
be another neighbor of
v
in
G_{\mathcal{T}}
. We have already observed that there must be a vertex adjacent to
x
in
G_{\mathcal{T}}
that is not a loop in
M_{\mathcal{S}}
. Call this vertex
x^{\prime}
. We may choose
x^{\prime}
so that
\{x,x^{\prime}\}\notin\mathcal{S}
because, if there is no such vertex, then
x
has no neighbor outside the cloud containing
x
in
G_{\mathcal{S}}
, which implies that
\mathcal{S}
is not a prime collection. Similarly, we let
y^{\prime}
be any vertex adjacent to
y
such that
y
is not a loop in
M_{\mathcal{S}}
and such that
\{y,y^{\prime}\}\notin\mathcal{S}
.
For
X\subseteq[n]
, let
\text{cl}(X)
denote the closure of
X
in
M_{\mathcal{T}}
. Since
\Delta_{G}\subseteq\mathcal{D}(M_{\mathcal{T}})
, we have that
\{x,x^{\prime},v\}
and
\{x,y,v\}
are both dependent sets in
M_{\mathcal{T}}
. By assumption,
\{x,x^{\prime}\}
is independent in
M_{\mathcal{S}}
, implying that it is independent in
M_{\mathcal{T}}
also. Since
\{x,x^{\prime},v\}
is dependent, we have
v\in\text{cl}(\{x,x^{\prime}\})
. Thus
\text{cl}(\{x,x^{\prime}\})=\text{cl}(\{x,x^{\prime},v\})
. Similarly, since
\{x,v\}
is independent in
M_{\mathcal{T}}
but
\{x,y,v\}
is dependent, we have that
y\in\text{cl}(\{x,v\})\subseteq\text{cl}(\{x,x^{\prime},v\})=\text{cl}(\{x,x^{\prime}\})
. Since
y\in\text{cl}(\{x,x^{\prime}\})
, we have that
\{x,x^{\prime},y\}
is a dependent set in
M_{\mathcal{T}}
.
However, by assumption, we have that
\{x,x^{\prime}\}
is independent in
M_{\mathcal{S}}
. Hence
\{x,x^{\prime},y\}
is independent in
M_{\mathcal{S}}
because it is blocked by
v
. So we have found a dependent set in
M_{\mathcal{T}}
which is independent in
M_{\mathcal{S}}
, a contradiction. Therefore, we deduce that
M_{\mathcal{S}}
and
M_{\mathcal{T}}
have the same loops.

To show that
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{T}
, it remains to show that the
2
-subsets in
\mathcal{S}
are the same as the
2
-subsets in
\mathcal{T}
. Since
M_{\mathcal{S}}
and
M_{\mathcal{T}}
have the same loops and
M_{\mathcal{S}}\geq M_{\mathcal{T}}
, it follows that every
2
-subset in
\mathcal{T}
is also in
\mathcal{S}
. Suppose for a contradiction that
\mathcal{S}
has strictly more
2
-subsets than
\mathcal{T}
. Therefore, either
\mathcal{S}
has more clouds than
\mathcal{T}
, or there is a cloud of
\mathcal{T}
that is properly contained in a cloud of
\mathcal{S}
. In either case, since every vertex in the cloud is adjacent to a vertex not in the cloud, there is a
3
-element set that is blocked by
\mathcal{S}
but not by
\mathcal{T}
. This contradicts the assumption that
M_{\mathcal{S}}\geq M_{\mathcal{T}}
.
∎

6.2 Irreducible matroid varieties.

In this subsection, we combine the results of §
6.1

to obtain a classification of the irreducible components of
V_{\Delta_{G}}
.
In particular, we will prove Theorem
6.20
.
This gives a straightforward description of the irreducible components.

Proposition 6.19
.

Let
G
be a forest
and
\mathcal{S}
be a prime collection for
G
as in Definition
6.4
.
Then for any non-central component
V_{N}
of
V_{M_{\mathcal{S}}}^{\mathrm{comb}}
there exists another prime collection
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
for
G
, such that
V_{N}\subseteq V_{M_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}}
.

Proof.

Let
J
be the ideal associated to the matroid
N
and for notation we write
N
as
M_{J}
. By Proposition
6.10
, the simplification of
M_{\mathcal{S}}
is forest-like. So, by Theorem
5.14
,

the matroid
M_{J}
is obtained from
M_{\mathcal{S}}
by setting all elements inside certain clouds of
\mathcal{S}
to be loops, see Definition
5.12
. Denote these clouds by
C_{1},\dots,C_{s}
. More precisely, the circuits of
M_{J}
are the singleton subsets of

C_{1}\cup\cdots\cup C_{s}
along with the
collection of circuits of
M_{\mathcal{S}}\backslash(C_{1}\cup\cdots\cup C_{s})
. Note that
M_{\mathcal{S}}<M_{J}
.

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{I})
be the point and line configuration whose matroid is the simplification of
M_{\mathcal{S}}
. By Theorem
5.11
, if a non-central component
J
of
I_{M_{\mathcal{S}}}^{\mathrm{comb}}
exists, there is a point in
\mathcal{P}
contained in at least three lines in
\mathcal{L}
. This implies that a cloud of
\mathcal{S}
contains at least three elements.

Let
C
be a cloud of
\mathcal{S}
that is not one of
C_{1},\dots C_{s}
. Let
p_{i}\in\mathcal{P}
be the corresponding point in the simplification of
M_{\mathcal{S}}
.
We define the
star of
p_{i}
to be
S(p_{i})=\bigcup_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{i}}\ell
, where
\ell
is identified with the set of points it passes through.
By Lemma
5.13
,
we see that all generators of
I_{M_{\mathcal{S}}}
constructed using points in
S(p_{i})
are also generators of
J
. However, we also see
that
I_{M_{\mathcal{S}}}\nsubseteq J
because the elements of
C_{1}\cup\cdots\cup C_{s}
are loops of
M_{J}
. Therefore, it is enough to show that there exists a prime collection
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
such that:

•

M_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}<M_{J}
and

•

every cloud of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
contained in any of
C_{1},\dots,C_{s}
has size at most two.

We will construct
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
from
\mathcal{S}
by an inductive procedure modifying the clouds
C_{1},\dots,C_{s}
. Let
\mathcal{S}_{0}=\mathcal{S}
. At each step of the procedure, we construct
\mathcal{S}_{i+1}
from
\mathcal{S}_{i}
by modifying a cloud
C
of
\mathcal{S}_{i}
with
|C|\geq 3
such that
C\subseteq C_{1}\cup\cdots\cup C_{s}
. By induction, we assume
\mathcal{S}_{i}
is a prime collection. At each step of this procedure, we construct a prime collection
\mathcal{S}_{i+1}
such that every set that is dependent in
M_{\mathcal{S}_{i+1}}
but not in
M_{\mathcal{S}_{i}}
is dependent in
M_{J}
. Since
M_{\mathcal{S}}<M_{J}
, this implies that
M_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}<M_{J}
.

Let
G^{\prime}
be the induced subgraph of
G
obtained by deleting the singletons in
\mathcal{S}_{i}
. Let
G[C]
be the induced subgraph of
G
with vertex set
C
, and let
\ell
be a leaf of
G[C]
. Since
|C|\geq 3
, the neighbor
x
of
\ell
in
G[C]
is not a leaf of
G[C]
. In particular,
\deg(x)\geq 3
in
G^{\prime}
, so we can add
x
to the collection of singletons of
\mathcal{S}_{i}
to result in a new prime collection of singletons. Therefore, we modify
\mathcal{S}_{i}
as follows. Make
x
a singleton set in
\mathcal{S}_{i+1}
and remove all
2
-subsets in
\mathcal{S}_{i}
which contain
x
. We now consider the neighbors of
x
which lie outside
C
. For each such neighbor
y
of
x
, if
y
belongs to a cloud
C^{\prime}\neq C
and
x
is the only neighbor of
y
not contained in
C^{\prime}
, then we remove
y
from the cloud
C^{\prime}
. This ensures that the new collection is a prime collection. Let
\mathcal{S}_{i+1}
be this new prime collection. Note that this procedure does not add any new
3
-circuits because all paths crossing
C^{\prime}
that contain
y
necessarily pass through
x
.

As a result of this procedure, the cloud
C
no longer contains
x
or
\ell
and might also be split into multiple smaller clouds. Note that all other clouds affected by this procedure have decreased in size. Thus, the only subset of
[n]
that is dependent in
\mathcal{S}_{i+1}
but not in
\mathcal{S}_{i}
is the singleton set
\{x\}
, which is a loop of
M_{J}
. Therefore,
M_{\mathcal{S}_{i+1}}<M_{J}
as desired. We apply this procedure inductively until we obtain a prime collection
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
such that every cloud of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
contained in
C_{1}\cup\cdots\cup C_{s}
has size
2
.
∎

Theorem 6.20
.

Let
G
be a forest and let
\Delta_{G}
be the corresponding consecutive forest hypergraph. Then,

V_{\Delta_{G}}=\bigcup_{\mathcal{S}}V_{M_{\mathcal{S}}},

is an irredundant irreducible decomposition
of
V_{\Delta_{G}}
, where the union is taken over all prime collections
\mathcal{S}
of
G
. In particular, a minimal prime decomposition of
\sqrt{I_{\Delta_{G}}}
is given as

\sqrt{I_{\Delta_{G}}}=\bigcap_{\mathcal{S}}I_{M_{\mathcal{S}}}.

Proof.

By Theorem
6.11
, the set of all minimal matroids of
\Delta_{G}
is
\{M_{\mathcal{S}}:\mathcal{S}\text{ is a prime collection}\}
. It follows, by Proposition
3.10
, that

V_{\Delta_{G}}=\bigcup_{\mathcal{S}}V^{\mathrm{comb}}_{M_{\mathcal{S}}}.

It is easy to see that, the simplification of the matroid
M_{\mathcal{S}}
is a forest-like
configuration. So, the irreducible components of
V^{\mathrm{comb}}_{M_{\mathcal{S}}}
are in one-to-one correspondence with the irreducible components of the combinatorial closure of the matroid variety of this configuration, which are given by Theorem
5.14
.
By Proposition
6.19
, for every non-central component
V_{M_{J}}
of
V^{\mathrm{comb}}_{M_{\mathcal{S}}}
, there exists a prime collection
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
such that
V_{M_{J}}\subseteq V_{M_{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}}
. Moreover, by Theorem
6.22
, each of the matroids
M_{\mathcal{S}}
is realizable. Hence, the irredundant irreducible decomposition of
V_{\Delta_{G}}
is given by

V_{\Delta_{G}}=\bigcup_{\mathcal{S}}V_{M_{\mathcal{S}}}
,
as desired.
∎

In the example below we show that the irreducible components of
V_{\Delta_{G}}
are not necessarily positroid varieties.

.

Let

be the graph on the vertex set

and edge set

, the matroid

6.3 Realizability.

We are now ready to consider the minimal matroids for
\Delta_{G}
. Recall that, if
\mathcal{S}
is a valid collection for
G
, then the matroid
M_{\mathcal{S}}
arising from Proposition
6.8
is minimal with respect to
\Delta_{G}
. Here, we show that
M_{\mathcal{S}}
is realizable over the real numbers hence realizable over
\mathbb{C}
.

Theorem 6.22
.

If
\mathcal{S}
is a valid collection, then
M_{\mathcal{S}}
is
\mathbb{R}
-realizable.

Proof.

Let
r
be the rank function of
M_{\mathcal{S}}
. The elements of
M_{\mathcal{S}}
are the vertices of the forest
G
. Let
L
be the set of loops of
M_{\mathcal{S}}
, so
L
consists of the singletons in
\mathcal{S}
. We will proceed inductively by considering subgraphs of
G
with increasing numbers of vertices. For the base case, we begin with the set
L
of loops of
M
. The restriction
M_{\mathcal{S}}|L
can be represented by the
3\times|L|
zero matrix.

For the inductive step, suppose that we have shown that
M_{\mathcal{S}}|V(G_{0})
is realizable over
\mathbb{R}
for some subgraph
G_{0}
of
G
containing the vertices in
L
. Let
v\in V(G)\backslash V(G_{0})
. We will show that the restriction
M_{\mathcal{S}}|(V(G_{0})\cup\{v\})
is obtained from
M_{\mathcal{S}}|(V(G_{0}))
either by adding
v
as a coloop or by freely adding
v
to a flat. Thus, Lemma
5.5
or Lemma
5.6
implies that
M_{\mathcal{S}}|(V(G_{0})\cup\{v\})
is realizable over the reals. We consider the following cases.

(1)

There is a vertex
w\in V(G_{0})
such that
v
and
w
are in the same cloud.

(2)

Case (1) does not hold, but there are vertices
w,x\in V(G_{0})\backslash L
, not in the same cloud, such that
\{v,w,x\}
is an unblocked set.

(3)

Neither Case (1) nor Case (2) holds.

For Case (1), note that
C\cup L
is a flat of rank
1
for every cloud
C
. Therefore, if
C
is the cloud containing
v
and
w
, then we obtain
M_{\mathcal{S}}|(V(G_{0})\cup\{v\})
by freely adding
v
to the flat
C\cup L
.

In Case (2), the fact that
w
and
x
are not in the same cloud and the fact that neither
w
nor
x
is in
L
imply that
r(\{w,x\})=2
. The fact that
\{v,w,x\}
is an unblocked set implies that
r(\{v,w,x\})=2
also. Thus,
v
is in the closure of
\{w,x\}
in the matroid
M_{\mathcal{S}}
. Since Case (1) does not hold,
M_{\mathcal{S}}|(V(G_{0})\cup\{v\})
is obtained by adding
v
freely to the closure of
\{w,x\}
.

Now we consider Case (3). Since Case (1) does not hold, Proposition
6.8
implies that
M_{\mathcal{S}}
has no circuit of size
2
consisting of
v
and some vertex of
G_{0}
. Since Case (2) does not hold, Proposition
6.8
implies that
M_{\mathcal{S}}
has no circuit of size
3
consisting of
v
and two vertices of
G_{0}
. Therefore, the only circuits of
M_{\mathcal{S}}
containing
v
and some vertex of
G_{0}
are sets of size
4
. Thus, the matroid
M_{\mathcal{S}}|(V(G_{0})\cup\{v\})
is obtained either by freely adding
v
to the ground set
V(G_{0})
(if
r(V(G_{0}))=3
) or by adding
v
as a coloop (if
r(V(G_{0}))<3
).
∎

Definition 7.4

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field, and let
S=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}
be a collection of (not necessarily distinct) vectors in
\mathbb{F}^{d-1}
. Let
v_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}^{d}
be the vector whose first coordinate is
1
and whose other coordinates are those of
v_{i}
.

•

The collection
S
is
affinely dependent
if
k>0
and there are elements
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in\mathbb{F}
that are not all
0
with
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}v_{i}={\bf 0}

and
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}=0
.
This is equivalent to the condition that
\{v_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,v_{k}^{\prime}\}
is linearly dependent.

•

A matroid
M
on the set
[n]
is
affine
over the field
\mathbb{F}
if there is a function
\phi:[n]\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{d-1}
such that
X\subseteq[n]
is an independent set in
M
if and only if
\phi(X)
is affinely independent. In this case,
M
has rank at most
d
and can be realized by a
d\times n
matrix whose columns are
v_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,v_{n}^{\prime}
. An affine matroid must be loopless, and it is simple if and only if
\phi
is injective. If
F
is a flat of
M
of rank
t
, then there is a
(t-1)
-dimensional affine subspace of
\mathbb{F}^{d-1}
whose intersection with
[n]
is
\phi(F)
.

Theorem 7.5
.

Let
s,t,k,\ell,d
be positive integers such that
3\leq s\leq t\leq\ell
,
s\leq k
, and
t\leq d\leq s+t-3
. Then
\mathcal{C}=\min(\Delta^{s,t}\cup\binom{[k\ell]}{d+1})
is the collection of circuits of an
\mathbb{R}
-realizable matroid on
[k\ell]
of rank
d
. This is the unique minimal matroid for
\Delta^{s,t}
in this case.

Proof.

One can check fairly easily that
\mathcal{C}
satisfies circuit elimination and therefore is the collection of circuits of a matroid. However, this will be unnecessary because we will prove this theorem by constructing a realization for an affine matroid over
\mathbb{R}
whose circuits are
\mathcal{C}
. Because
\mathcal{C}
is itself the collection of circuits of a matroid
M
, there can be no matroid
N\neq M
with rank at most
d
and ground set
[k\ell]
such that
\Delta^{s,t}\subseteq\mathcal{D}(N)\subseteq\mathcal{D}(M)
. Thus,
M
is the unique minimal matroid for
\Delta^{s,t}
with
d\leq s+t-3
.

To construct a realization for an affine matroid over
\mathbb{R}
whose circuits are
\mathcal{C}
, we must define a function
\phi:[k\ell]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
.
In such a matroid, each
R_{i}
must be a flat of rank
t-1
and each
C_{j}
must be a flat of rank
s-1
.
A flat of rank
d-m
in an affine matroid over
\mathbb{R}
corresponds to an affine subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
of dimension
d-m-1
. For each such subspace, there are
m
distinct affine hyperplanes in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
whose intersection is the subspace. Therefore, each
\phi(R_{i})
must be defined by the intersection of a collection
\mathcal{H}_{R_{i}}
of
d-t+1
affine hyperplanes. These hyperplanes are defined by the following equations, with the matrix
A_{i}=[a_{p,q}]
having full row rank.

\begin{array}[]{lccclcl}a_{1,1}x_{1}&+&\cdots&+&a_{1,d-1}x_{d-1}&=&c_{1}\\
&&&&&\vdots&\\
a_{d-t+1,1}x_{1}&+&\cdots&+&a_{d-t+1,d-t+1}x_{d-1}&=&c_{d-t+1}\\
\end{array}

We choose
k
such collections of hyperplanes
\mathcal{H}_{R_{1}},\mathcal{H}_{R_{2}},\ldots,\mathcal{H}_{R_{k}}
such that the rank-
(t-1)
subspaces they define are in “general position”. That is, for every
n<t
, the intersection of every collection of
n
such subspaces is a subspace of rank
t-n
.
Similarly, each
\phi(C_{j})
must be defined by the intersection of a collection
\mathcal{H}_{C_{j}}
of
d-s+1
hyperplanes defined by the following equations, with the matrix
B_{j}=[b_{p,q}]
having full row rank.

\begin{array}[]{lccclcl}b_{1,1}x_{1}&+&\cdots&+&b_{1,d-1}x_{d-1}&=&c_{d-t+2}\\
&&&&&\vdots&\\
b_{d-s+1,1}x_{1}&+&\cdots&+&b_{d-s+1,r-1}x_{d-1}&=&c_{2d-s-t+2}\\
\end{array}

Choosing the subspaces defined by
\mathcal{H}_{R_{1}},\mathcal{H}_{R_{2}},\ldots,\mathcal{H}_{R_{k}},\mathcal{H}_{C_{1}},\mathcal{H}_{C_{2}},\ldots,\mathcal{H}_{C_{\ell}}
will ensure that every subset of
\phi([k\ell])
that should be affinely dependent is indeed so. However, we must also ensure that every subset of
\phi([k\ell])
that should be affinely independent is so. That is, for every subset
X
with
|X|\leq d
such that no
t
-subset of any
R_{i}
or
s
-subset of any
C_{j}
is contained in
X
,
\phi(X)
must be affinely independent. To do this, we will choose the collections
\mathcal{H}_{C_{1}},\mathcal{H}_{C_{2}},\ldots,\mathcal{H}_{C_{\ell}}
successively. Suppose we have chosen the collections
\mathcal{H}_{C_{1}},\ldots,\mathcal{H}_{C_{j-1}}
and now must choose
\mathcal{H}_{C_{j}}
. If
X
is a subset of
C_{1}\cup\dots\cup C_{j}
such that
\phi(X)
is to be affinely independent, then
\phi(X-C_{j})
is a basis for a subspace
S
of
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
. We must choose
\mathcal{H}_{C_{j}}
so that, for each
i\leq k
, the intersection of all hyperplanes in
\mathcal{H}_{R_{i}}\cup\mathcal{H}_{C_{j}}
is a subspace of rank
d-(d-s+1)-(d-t+1)=d-(2d-s-t+2)=s+t-d-2
that avoids all such subspaces
S
.

The requirement that this subspace has rank
s+t-d-2
is equivalent to the matrix
\left[\begin{array}[]{c}A_{i}\\
\hline\cr B_{j}\\
\end{array}\right]
having full row rank. This subspace must be nonempty since
R_{i}\cap C_{j}\neq\emptyset
. Thus, we must have
s+t-d-2\geq 1
, which is true because
d\leq s+t-3
. The requirement of avoiding all subspaces defined by independent sets can be achieved as there are only finitely many such subspaces to avoid but infinitely many collections to choose to be
\mathcal{H}_{C_{j}}
.
∎

7.2 General grid matroids.

Given a collection
\mathcal{D}
of subsets of a ground set
E
, we wish to find the matroids that are minimally dependent for
\mathcal{D}
. However, for our purposes here, it is necessary to find the matroids minimally dependent for
\mathcal{D}
among the class of realizable matroids.

Some work toward these ideas has been done by Martí-Farré
[
MF14
]
. Below, we recall the algorithmic procedure used in
[
MF14
]
to obtain minimally dependent matroids. This algorithm is not quite completely satisfactory from our perspective for two reasons. First, the algorithm does not take realizability into account at all. Martí-Farré and de Mier worked with realizability in
[
MFdM15
]
, but as they said there, “The problems under consideration are far from being solved.” Second, although the algorithm is guaranteed to give all of the minimally dependent matroids, it may also give additional matroids that are not minimally dependent. One must compare the matroids given by the algorithm to determine which ones are minimally dependent.

In fact, we will use the algorithm of Martí-Farré to prove a result that illustrates the difficulties of determining the minimal matroids for
\Delta^{s,t}
in general. First, we recall some terminology and notation, much of it coming from
[
MF14
]
. A
clutter
on a set
\Omega
is a collection of subsets of
\Omega
such that no set is contained in another. (The term
clutter
is another word for what we have been calling a
simple hypergraph
.) If
\Lambda
is a clutter on
\Omega
, let
\Lambda^{+}=\{A\subseteq\Omega:A_{0}\subseteq A\textnormal{ for some }A_{0}\in\Lambda\}
. If
\Upsilon
is any collection of subsets of
\Omega
, then recall from Section
2
that
\min(\Upsilon)
denotes the clutter of inclusion-wise minimal sets in
\Upsilon
. For
B\subseteq\Omega
, define

I_{\Lambda}(B)=\bigcap_{A\in\Lambda,A\subseteq B}A.

It follows from the circuit elimination axiom that
\Lambda
is the collection of circuits of a matroid if and only if
I_{\Lambda}(A_{1}\cup A_{2})=\emptyset
for all
A_{1},A_{2}\in\Lambda
with
A_{1}\neq A_{2}
.
To describe the algorithm, we use the notation of Martí-Farré and de Mier in
[
MFdM17
]
. Let
\Lambda
be a clutter with
A_{1},A_{2}\in\Lambda
. An
\alpha_{1}
-transformation of
\Lambda
is

\alpha_{1}(\Lambda;A_{1},A_{2})=\begin{cases}\min(\Lambda\cup\{A_{1}\cap A_{2}\})&\textnormal{if }I_{\Lambda}(A_{1}\cup A_{2})\neq\emptyset,\\
\Lambda&\textnormal{otherwise.}\\
\end{cases}

The
\alpha_{2}
- and
\alpha_{3}
-transformations of
\Lambda
are

\alpha_{2}(\Lambda)=\min(\Lambda\cup\{(A_{1}\cup A_{2})\backslash\{x\}:A_{1},A_{2}\in\Lambda,A_{1}\neq A_{2},x\in A_{1}\cap A_{2}\})\textnormal{ and}

\alpha_{3}(\Lambda)=\min(\Lambda\cup\{(A_{1}\cup A_{2})\backslash I_{\Lambda}(A_{1}\cup A_{2}):A_{1},A_{2}\in\Lambda,A_{1}\neq A_{2}\}).

The following result is proved in
[
MF14
, Theorem 13]
.

Theorem 7.6
.

Let
\Lambda\neq\{\emptyset\}
be a clutter on a finite set
\Omega
and let
M
be a minimally dependent matroid for
\Lambda
whose collection of circuits is
\mathcal{C}(M)
. There is a sequence of clutters
\Lambda=\Lambda_{0},\Lambda_{1},\dots,\Lambda_{r}=\mathcal{C}(M)
such that, for each
i\geq 1
, we have
\Lambda_{i-1}^{+}\subsetneq\Lambda_{i}^{+}
and such that
\Lambda_{i}
is either an
\alpha_{1}
-,
\alpha_{2}
-, or
\alpha_{3}
-transformation of
\Lambda_{i-1}
.

So, in order to obtain all minimally dependent matroids for a clutter, it suffices to perform every possible combination of
\alpha_{1}
-,
\alpha_{2}
-, and
\alpha_{3}
-transformations on the clutter. Then, one must compare the resulting matroids and discard any matroid whose collection of dependent sets strictly contains the collection of dependent sets of another matroid obtained from the algorithm.
Although the elements of the ground set of
\Delta^{s,t}
are the integers
1,2,\dots,\ell k
, it will be convenient to think of each element as an ordered pair
(i,j)
, where
(i,j)
is the unique element of
R_{i}\cap C_{j}
. In the remainder of this section, we show that every matroid can be obtained as a restriction of a matroid obtained from some
\Delta^{s,t}
by
\alpha_{1}
- and
\alpha_{2}
-transformations. We prove the following result.

Theorem 7.7
.

Let

be a matroid on ground set

and a sequence of clutters

on the set

is either an
\alpha_{1}
- or an
\alpha_{2}
-transformation of

and

after giving some definitions and proving some lemmas below. We remark that these lemmas and Theorem

-transformations.
We will use
\alpha_{1}
- and
\alpha_{2}
-transformations to obtain a matroid whose restriction to

is isomorphic to

. That isomorphism will be

for each

. Thus, to simplify notation, we identify the element

Definition 7.8

, and let

n^{\prime}+1\leq j\leq\ell
. (So
j
is not a loop of
M^{+}
.) We will construct a sequence of clutters
\Lambda^{\prime\prime}=\Lambda^{\prime\prime}_{0},\Lambda^{\prime\prime}_{1},\dots,\Lambda^{\prime\prime}_{z}=\min(\Lambda^{\prime\prime}\cup\Psi)
such that
\Lambda^{\prime\prime}_{i}
is an
\alpha_{1}
-transformation of
\Lambda^{\prime\prime}_{i-1}
. By induction, assume
\Lambda^{\prime\prime}_{i-1}=\min(\Lambda^{\prime\prime}\cup\Psi^{\prime})
, where
\Psi^{\prime}\subseteq\Psi
. Let
\{(i,j)\}\in\Psi\backslash\Psi^{\prime}
. Let
A_{1}=\{(i,j)\}\cup\{(i,m):n-c+1\leq m\leq n-c+t-1\}
and let
A_{2}=\{(i,j),(1,j)\}
. Since
n^{\prime}\geq n-c+t-1
, no element of
A_{1}\cup A_{2}
is a singleton set in
\Psi
. Thus,
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are the only members of
\Lambda^{\prime\prime}_{i-1}
contained in
A_{1}\cup A_{2}
and
\alpha_{1}(\Lambda^{\prime\prime}_{i-1};A_{1},A_{2})=\min(\Lambda^{\prime\prime}\cup\{(i,j)\})
. By iterating this process, we obtain
\min(\Lambda^{\prime\prime}\cup\Psi)
.

Let
\Lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}=\min(\Lambda^{\prime\prime}\cup\Psi)
. The members of
\Lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}
are

•

singleton sets
\{(i,j)\}
where
2\leq i\leq 5
and either
1\leq j\leq n-c
or
n^{\prime}+1\leq j\leq\ell
,

•

2
-subsets of
C_{j}\backslash\{(1,j)\}
, where
n-c+1\leq j\leq n^{\prime}
,

•

t
-subsets of
\{(i,j):n-c+1\leq j\leq n^{\prime}\}
for some fixed
i
with
2\leq i\leq 5
, and

•

subsets of
R_{1}
that are circuits of
M^{+}
.

Note that each member of
\Lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}
is either contained in
R_{1}
or disjoint from
R_{1}
.
For every clutter, a process of repeated
\alpha_{2}
-transformations on the clutter must eventually terminate in a clutter that is the collection of circuits of a matroid. Therefore, repeated
\alpha_{2}
-transformations on
\Lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}
will result in a clutter that is the collection of circuits of a matroid
N
whose circuits are either contained in
R_{1}
or disjoint from
R_{1}
. Since the members of
\Lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}
contained in
R_{1}
are the circuits of
M^{+}
, circuit elimination implies that the circuits of
N
contained in
R_{1}
are precisely the circuits of
M^{+}
. In particular, the restriction of
N
to
\{(1,j):1\leq j\leq n\}
is isomorphic to
M
.
∎

Theorem
7.7
applies to all matroids, regardless of rank. However, in the case where the matroid is simple and has rank at most
3
, the result can be illustrated more clearly using a different procedure from the one used in the proof of the theorem. We use point and line configurations.
Consider the Fano matroid whose point and line configuration is the Fano plane given in Figure
3
.
It is well-known that the Fano matroid is not realizable over any field of characteristic other than
2
; therefore, it is not
\mathbb{C}
-realizable. In the case where
n=7
and

\mathcal{D}=\left\{L_{1}=\{1,2,4\},L_{2}=\{1,3,6\},L_{3}=\{1,5,7\},L_{4}=\{2,3,5\},L_{5}=\{2,6,7\},L_{6}=\{3,4,7\},L_{7}=\{4,5,6\}\right\}\cup\binom{[n]}{4},

the only minimally dependent matroid with respect to
\min(\mathcal{D})
is the Fano matroid, which is not relevant for our purposes because it is not
\mathbb{C}
-realizable. However, this can arise as the simplification of a matroid coming from a point and line configuration with
s=2
and
t=3
.

Indeed, consider the case
k=\ell=7
. Let
(i,j)
be the unique element contained in
R_{i}\cap C_{j}
. Iteratively performing
\alpha_{1}
-transformations (defined earlier in this subsection), we can obtain from the clutter
\Delta^{2,3}
a clutter that contains all of the singletons in
R_{i}
except for the elements
(i,j)
such that
j\in L_{i}
. In particular, the members of this clutter are the following:

•

all singletons except for the elements
(i,j)
such that
j\in L_{i}
,

•

3
-subsets of the form
\{(i,j)\in R_{i}:j\in L_{i}\}
, and

•

2
-subsets of
C_{j}
of the form
\{(i_{i},j),(i_{2},j)\}
, where
j\in L_{i_{1}}\cap L_{i_{2}}
.

Then, by performing two transformations (both
\alpha_{2}
-transformations, both
\alpha_{3}
-transformations, or one of each), we obtain a clutter that is the collection of circuits of a matroid whose simplification is the Fano matroid and whose rank-
1
flats each have three non-loop elements. The second transformation is needed to include the Fano matroid’s circuits of size
4
. (This works because, for each
4
-element circuit
C
of the Fano matroid, there is a fifth element
x
such that
C\cup\{x\}
is the union of two
3
-element circuits whose intersection is
x
.)

8
Conditional independence models

Conditional independence (CI) models play an important role in algebraic statistics
[
Stu05
]
. Given a collection of random variables and knowledge of the conditional dependencies, or independencies, among them, we can ask what are the distributions that satisfy them. In a more general setting, some of the random variables appearing in a CI model can be prescribed as unobserved (or hidden). Our goal is to determine when certain constraints on the observed variables arise from conditions on the hidden variables
[
SA15
]
. This problem can be restated algebraically by noting that probability distributions satisfying CI statements are the solutions of certain polynomial equations
[
DSS09
,
Sul18
]
which generate the so-called
CI ideal
. The distributions satisfying a given collection of CI statements can be recovered by intersecting the CI ideal with the probability simplex. When there are no hidden variables, these polynomials are binomials and their associated ideals are well-studied; see e.g.
[
Fin11
,
HHH+10
,
Rau13
,
ST13
]
. However, in the presence of hidden variables, the polynomials become far more complicated of arbitrarily high degrees and very difficult to calculate; see e.g.
[
PS19
,
CMR20
]
.

Let
X,Y_{1},Y_{2}
be observed and
H_{1},H_{2}
be hidden random variables taking values in the finite sets
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}_{1},\mathcal{Y}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}
of cardinalities
|\mathcal{X}|=d
,
|\mathcal{Y}_{1}|=k
,
|\mathcal{Y}_{2}|=\ell
,
|\mathcal{H}_{1}|=s-1
,
|\mathcal{H}_{2}|=t-1
.
Consider the CI model given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}:\ \ {X}\perp\!\!\!\perp{Y_{1}}\mid\{Y_{2},H_{1}\}\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{and}\hskip 10.00002pt{X}\perp\!\!\!\perp{Y_{2}}\mid\{Y_{1},H_{2}\}.

(4)

Then the CI ideal associated to
\mathcal{C}
is precisely the hypergraph ideal
I_{\Delta^{s,t}}
in §
7
; see
[
CMM21
,
CMR20
]
.

Example 8.1
.

Let
d=k=s=t=3
and
\ell=4
.
The joint distribution of
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
has state space
\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{Y}_{1}\times\mathcal{Y}_{2}
which is identified with the
3\times 4
matrix
\mathcal{Y}
with values in the set
[12]
as in (
3
).
In this case, the CI ideal
I_{\Delta}\subseteq\mathbb{C}[P]:=\mathbb{C}[p_{x,y}:x\in\mathcal{X},y\in\mathcal{Y}]
is the hypergraph ideal from Example
3.12
.
The ideal
I_{\Delta}
has two prime components. One component, associated to the matroid
M_{0}
from Example
3.12
, is generated by all
3
-minors of the matrix of variables
P
. This is the ideal associated to the CI statements
{X}\perp\!\!\!\perp{\{Y_{1},Y_{2}\}}\mid H_{1}
and
{X}\perp\!\!\!\perp{\{Y_{1},Y_{2}\}}\mid H_{2}
. Note that
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are hidden random variables taking the same number of values, and so the ideals do not distinguish them. The other prime component
I_{M}
is the ideal of the configuration described in Example
3.12
consisting of
12
points and
7
lines. Besides the rank constraints given by the original CI model, the ideal
I_{M}
contains geometric constraints which are satisfied by all distributions which do not lie in
V_{M_{0}}
.

We note that for large values of
d
, understanding the algebraic properties of the ideal
I_{\Delta^{s,t}}
and its (primary) decomposition is hard. In particular, Theorem
7.7
shows that, up to simplification, any matroid can appear among the dependent matroids for
\Delta^{s,t}
. And, in the example above, some high-degree polynomials may appear in the generating sets of the primary components of
I_{\Delta^{s,t}}
. However, for
d\leq s+t-3
we have shown in Theorem
7.5
that
\Delta^{s,t}
has a unique minimally dependent matroid
M
. So, for a generic point
p
in the variety
V_{\Delta^{s,t}}
, the additional polynomial constraints on
p
arise from geometric constraints on realizations of
M
.

Remark 8.2
.

Suppose that
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are both constant. The
intersection axiom
states that any distribution satisfying
\mathcal{C}
in (
4
)
generically
satisfies
{X}\perp\!\!\!\perp{\{Y_{1},Y_{2}\}}
. Here, genericity means that the distributions have non-zero probabilities.
The intersection axiom has been studied for various CI models; see e.g.
[
HHH+10
,
Rau13
,
CMR20
,
CMM21
,
PS19
]
. We note that our family of hypergraph varieties include all these cases as examples. In particular, the corresponding ideal
I_{\Delta^{s,t}}
has a distinguished prime component with a particular statistical significance, since the distributions which lie inside do not contain any structural zeros
[
Stu02
]
. Importantly, if we assume, without loss of generality, that
s\leq t
then this prime ideal can be realized as the CI ideal of
{X}\perp\!\!\!\perp{\{Y_{1},Y_{2}\}}\mid H_{2}
. We may therefore deduce a hidden variable version of the intersection axiom as follows:

\mathcal{C}=\{{X}\perp\!\!\!\perp{Y_{1}}\mid\{H_{1},Y_{2}\},\ {X}\perp\!\!\!\perp{Y_{2}}\mid\{Y_{1},H_{2}\}\}\implies{X}\perp\!\!\!\perp{\{Y_{1},Y_{2}\}\mid H_{2}}.

Finally, we give connections of our work to an interesting conjecture by Matúš; see
[
Mat99
]
.

Conjecture 8.3

(
[
Mat99
]
)
.

For any discrete conditional independence model
\mathcal{C}
, there exists a distribution
p\in V(J_{\mathcal{C}})
such that all joint probabilities of
p
are rational.

In Theorem
7.7
, we have seen that for large enough
s,t,k,\ell
, any matroid may appear among the dependent matroids for
\Delta^{s,t}
. A natural approach is to carefully choose additional conditional independence and dependence statements for the model (
4
), in order to guarantee that any distribution
p
satisfying
\mathcal{C}
is a realization of a given, realizable, matroid.
Note that there exist matroids that are not realizable over the rationals but are realizable over a real field extension. Hence, this might lead to a characterization of CI models with hidden variables for which Conjecture
8.3
does not hold.
