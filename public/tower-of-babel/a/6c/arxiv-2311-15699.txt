Introduction

Parking assortments were introduced recently in
[
4
]
as a generalization of parking functions, classic combinatorial objects that arose in the 1960s from the study of hash functions and linear probing
[
11
]
.
There are two well-known equivalent definitions of parking functions.
The first can be described via a “parking experiment.”

Definition 1.1
.

Consider a one-way street with
n\in\mathbb{N}\coloneqq\{1,2,3,\dots\}
parking spots.
There are
n\in\mathbb{N}
cars of unit length waiting to enter the street.
For each
i\in[n]\coloneqq\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, car
i
prefers a spot
x_{i}\in[n]
of the parking lot, so it drives up to
x_{i}
and parks if
x_{i}
is unoccupied; otherwise, it parks in the next available spot (if it exists).
We say that
\mathbf{x}\coloneqq(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\in[n]^{n}
is a
parking function of length
n
if every car can park following their respective preference in
\mathbf{x}
.

The second is via a property of
(x_{(1)},x_{(2)},\dots,x_{(n)})
, which is the nondecreasing rearrangement of the entries of
\mathbf{x}
.

Definition 1.2
.

We say that
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\in[n]^{n}
is a
parking function of length
n
if
x_{(i)}\leq i
for all
i\in[n]
.

Perhaps the most celebrated result concerning parking functions is the fact that

|\mathsf{PF}_{n}|=(n+1)^{n-1},

where
\mathsf{PF}_{n}
denotes the set of parking functions of length
n
(cf. Lemma 1 in
[
11
]
).
Such a count allows one to see that parking functions are in bijection with several notable combinatorial objects including labeled trees and the Shi hyperplane arrangement
[
14
]
.
Explicitly constructing and investigating these bijections reveals many illuminating combinatorial properties of parking functions.
As an example, the sequence

(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{n-1}),\quad\text{where}\quad c_{i}\coloneqq(x_{i+1}-x_{i})\pmod{n+1}\quad\forall i\in[n-1],

of successive differences modulo
n+1
of an
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{PF}_{n}
yields the Prüfer code of a unique labeled tree on
n+1
vertices
[
14
]
.
Moreover, let
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
be nondecreasing, and consider the
Pitman-Stanley polytope

\Pi_{n}(\mathbf{u})\coloneqq\left\{(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{j}p_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{j}u_{i}\quad\forall j\in[n]\right\}.

With
\mathbf{u}=(1,2,\dots,n)
, we have
n!V_{n}(\mathbf{u})=|\mathsf{PF}_{n}|
, where
V_{n}(\mathbf{u})
denotes the
n
-dimensional volume of
\Pi_{n}(\mathbf{u})
.
More generally,
n!V_{n}(\mathbf{u})
is the number of
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
such that
x_{(i)}\leq u_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
(cf. Theorem 11 in
[
12
]
); such
\mathbf{x}
generalize Definition
1.2
and are aptly known as
\mathbf{u}
-parking functions of length
n
.
For a detailed treatment on the combinatorial theory of parking functions, see Yan
[
14
]
.

Given these connections and results, parking functions have been an active research topic, appearing in a diverse array of contexts such as pattern avoidance in permutations
[
8
]
, convex geometry
[
1
]
, polyhedral combinatorics
[
3
]
, partially ordered sets
[

]

[

Now, we define the combinatorial object of interest in this work: parking assortments.
This will involve a natural extension to the parking experiment described in Definition
1.1

Definition 1.3
.

Consider a one-way street with
m\in\mathbb{N}
parking spots.
There are
n\in\mathbb{N}
cars waiting to enter the street, and they have lengths
\mathbf{y}\coloneqq(y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, where
m=\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}

For each
i\in[n]
, car
i
(with length
y_{i}
) prefers a spot
x_{i}\in[m]
of the parking lot, so it drives up to
x_{i}
and parks if spots
x_{i},x_{i}+1,\dots,x_{i}+y_{i}-1
are unoccupied; otherwise, it parks in the next
y_{i}
contiguously available spots (if they exist).
We say that
\mathbf{x}\coloneqq(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\in[m]^{n}
is a
parking assortment
for
\mathbf{y}
if every car can park following their respective preference in
\mathbf{x}
.

From Definition

Definition 1.5
.

Let
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
.
We say
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{PA}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
is a
(permutation) invariant parking assortment for

\mathbf{y}
if any permutation of its entries is also in
\mathsf{PA}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
.

In this work, we are interested in the set
\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
of invariant parking assortments for
\mathbf{y}
.
Alternatively, we may study the set
\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv},\uparrow}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
of nondecreasing invariant parking assortments for
\mathbf{y}
, as it is equivalent to
\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
up to permutation.

Example 1.6
.

Let
\mathbf{y}=(7,4,6)\in\mathbb{N}^{3}
.
Then
\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv},\uparrow}_{3}(\mathbf{y})=\{(1^{3}),(1^{2},5)\}
.
Indeed,
(1^{3})\in\mathsf{PA}_{3}(\mathbf{y})
, and
(1^{2},5),(1,5,1),(5,1^{2})\in\mathsf{PA}_{3}(\mathbf{y})
; in other words, all permutations of the entries of
(1^{3})
and
(1^{2},5)
are also parking assortments, so
\{(1^{3}),(1^{2},5)\}\subseteq\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv},\uparrow}_{3}(\mathbf{y})
.
One can also check that no other elements of
[7+4+6]^{3}=[17]^{3}
have this property, which yields the reverse inclusion.

Determining
\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
is generally an arduous task due to the problem of checking each of the elements of
[m]^{n}
for invariance.
The main motivator behind the notions introduced in this work is to attempt to reduce this search space considerably.

A basic approach to narrowing the possibilities is to “decrease the exponent” by noticing that by definition, parking assortments have at least one entry that is equal to
1
; otherwise, parking spot
1
is never occupied by any car.
This reduces the search space to a size of
m^{n-1}
.
We can take this idea a step further and specifically study the number of entries of invariant parking assortments that are not equal to
1
, a fundamental property we will refer to as their
degree
.
Heuristically, one can expect that most of the entries of an
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
are
1
, and so many of the results presented here will concern and analyze the maximum degree across all
\mathbf{z}\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
, which we call the
characteristic
of
\mathbf{y}
.
If there is a straightforward way to bound the characteristic, then this can reduce our search space by more factors of
m
.

Definition 1.7
.

Let
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
.
For any
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
, the
degree
of
\mathbf{x}
is given by

\deg\mathbf{x}\coloneqq|\{i\in[n]:x_{i}\neq 1\}|.

Moreover, the
characteristic
of
\mathbf{y}
is given by

\chi(\mathbf{y})\coloneqq\max_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})}\deg\mathbf{z}.

As a special case, note that
\chi(\mathbf{y})=0
is equivalent to
\mathbf{y}
being
minimally invariant
, which is when
\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})=\{(1^{n})\}

[
4
]
.
Furthermore, we always have
\chi(\mathbf{y})\in[n-1]_{0}\coloneqq[n-1]\cup\{0\}
.

\mathbf{y}
to be of minimal characteristic, thereby establishing Theorem
A
.
In contrast, §
2.2
proves a simple equivalent condition for
\mathbf{y}
to be of maximal characteristic and provides a closed form of
\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
for all such
\mathbf{y}
.
By establishing a bijection between certain
\mathbf{u}
-parking functions and elements of
\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
, we utilize a result of Pitman and Stanley to enumerate
|\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})|
; we deduce a recursive formula to enumerate
|\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv},\uparrow}_{n}(\mathbf{y})|
.
Altogether, §
2.2
proves Theorems
B
and
C
.

2.1.
Minimal Characteristic

Recall the following result for minimally invariant
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{3}
.

Theorem 2.1

(Theorem 5.3 in
[
4
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})\in\mathbb{N}^{3}
.
Then,
\mathbf{y}
is minimally invariant if and only if
y_{1}<\min(y_{2},y_{3})
and
y_{2}\neq y_{1}+y_{3}
.

Thus, one might ask whether this can be generalized for
n>3
.
It turns out that the natural extension of this result is necessary for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
but not sufficient.
We will first prove the necessity, which is Theorem
A
.
The failure of the sufficiency will be easily established after we prove Theorem
D
(a)
in Section
3
.

Proof of Theorem
A
.

We consider the contrapositive.

Assume first that
y_{1}\geq\min(y_{2},y_{3},\dots,y_{n})
.
We claim
(1^{n-1},1+\min\mathbf{y})\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
.
As
y_{1}\geq\min\mathbf{y}
, we have
1+\min\mathbf{y}\leq 1+\sum_{j=1}^{i}y_{j}
for all
i\in[n-1]
, so
(1^{i},1+\min\mathbf{y})\in\mathsf{PA}_{n}(\mathbf{y}_{|_{i+1}})
by Lemma
3.1
.
It follows that
(1^{i},1+\min\mathbf{y},1^{n-i-1})\in\mathsf{PA}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
.
Indeed, under its parking experiment, the last
n-i-1
cars, all with preference
1
, are forced to successively fill
[1+\sum_{j=1}^{i+1}y_{j},m]
, as the first
i+1
cars already occupy
[1,\sum_{j=1}^{i+1}y_{j}]
since
(1^{i},1+\min\mathbf{y})\in\mathsf{PA}_{n}(\mathbf{y}_{|_{i+1}})
.
Lastly, to show that
(1+\min\mathbf{y},1^{n-1})\in\mathsf{PA}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
, note that under its parking experiment, the first car leaves
[1,\min\mathbf{y}]
unoccupied, and these spots can only be filled by one car.
The remaining cars, all with preference
1
, either park starting at the first empty spot after
[1,\min\mathbf{y}]
(if its length exceeds
\min\mathbf{y}
) or fill
[1,\min\mathbf{y}]
(if its length is precisely
\min\mathbf{y}
and no other cars with length
\min\mathbf{y}
have attempted to park).
For the latter possibility, there must exist a smallest index
j>1
such that
j=\argmin\mathbf{y}
by assumption.
Thus,
[1,\min\mathbf{y}]
will be filled by car
j
, after which point
[1,s]
is occupied for some
s\in\mathbb{N}
, so cars
j+1,j+2,\dots,n
will successively fill
[s+1,m]
.
Hence,
(1^{n-1},1+\min\mathbf{y})\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
, as needed.

Now, assume
y_{2}=\sum_{j\in[n]\setminus\{2\}}y_{j}
.
We claim that
\mathbf{x}=(1^{n-1},1+y_{2})\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
.
To see this, note first that
(1+y_{2},1^{n-1})\in\mathsf{PA}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
, as under its parking experiment, the first two cars will occupy
[1,y_{1}+y_{2}]
, and the remaining cars all have preference
1
, so they will park successively in
[1+y_{1}+y_{2},m]
.
Moreover,
(1,1+y_{2},1^{n-2})\in\mathsf{PA}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
because under its parking experiment, the first car parks in
[1,y_{1}]
, while the second car parks in
[1+y_{2},2y_{2}]=[m-y_{2}+1,m]
since
m=\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}=2y_{2}
.
Since
\sum_{j=3}^{n}y_{j}=m-y_{1}-y_{2}=y_{2}-y_{1}
and the remaining cars all have preference
1
, they will park successively in
[1+y_{1},m-y_{2}]
.
Otherwise, let
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime},\dots,x_{n}^{\prime})
be a permutation of
\mathbf{x}
such that
x_{j}^{\prime}=1+y_{2}
, where
j>2
, and consider the parking experiment for
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
.
Cars
1,2,\dots,j-1
all have preference
1
, so they will occupy
[1,\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}y_{i}]
, where
|[1,\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}y_{i}]|>y_{2}
.
As car
j
has preference
1+y_{2}
, it will occupy
[1+\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}y_{i},\sum_{i=1}^{j}y_{i}]
.
Lastly, any remaining cars again all have preference
1
, so they will park successively in
[1+\sum_{i=1}^{j}y_{i},m]
.
Hence,
(1^{n-1},1+y_{2})\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
, as needed.

Therefore, if
y_{1}\geq\min(y_{2},y_{3},\dots,y_{n})
or
y_{2}=\sum_{j\in[n]\setminus\{2\}}y_{j}
, then
\chi(\mathbf{y})\geq 1
, as desired.
∎

2.2.
Maximal Characteristic

Recall the following theorem on
\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
for constant
\mathbf{y}
.

Theorem 2.2

(Theorem 3.1 in
[
4
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{y}=(a^{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
and
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in[m]^{n}=[\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}]^{n}
.
We have
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
if and only if

x_{(i)}\in\{1+(k-1)a:k\in[i]\}\quad\forall i\in[n].

(4)

We now perturb
\mathbf{y}
and study length vectors of the form
(b,a^{n-1})
, where
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
and
n\geq 2
.
We will refer to such
\mathbf{y}
as “almost constant” (note that constant
\mathbf{y}
also fall into this classification).

As seen in the statement of Theorem
C
, it turns out that even when
a\neq b
, under certain conditions, this perturbation of
\mathbf{y}
does not affect
\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
.

First, we prove two intermediate steps for the proof of the closed forms of
\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
as described in Theorem
C
.
For brevity, for parking spots
S,E\in\mathbb{N}
, let the interval
[S,E]\coloneqq\{S,S+1,\dots,E-1,E\}
denote the set of parking spots numbered between
S
and
E
, inclusive; define its length to be
|[S,E]|\coloneqq|\{S,S+1,\dots,E-1,E\}|=E-S+1
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\mathbf{y}=(b,a^{n-1})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, where
n\geq 2
, and
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\in[m]^{n}
.
If
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
, then

x_{i}\equiv 1\pmod{a}\quad\forall i\in[n].

Proof.

We will prove the contrapositive.
Assume that
x_{j}\not\equiv 1\pmod{a}
for some
j\in[n]
.

By Euclidean division, we may write
x_{j}=aq+r
, where
q\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\coloneqq\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, and
r\neq 1
is a remainder (i.e.
r\in[a-1]_{0}\setminus\{1\}
).
Let
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime},\dots,x_{n}^{\prime})
be any permutation of
\mathbf{x}
such that
x_{1}^{\prime}=x_{j}
.
Under the parking experiment for
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
, the first car will occupy the
b
spots
[aq+r,aq+r+b-1]
, which leaves
[1,aq+r-1]
empty.
As
r\neq 1
,
|[1,aq+r-1]|
is not an integral multiple of
a
.
In particular, none of the remaining cars, which all have length
a
, can precisely fill
[1,aq+r-1]
. Thus, we have
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\notin\mathsf{PA}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
and
\mathbf{x}\notin\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\mathbf{y}=(b,a^{n-1})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, where
n\geq 2
, and
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\in[m]^{n}
.
If
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
, then

x_{(i)}\leq 1+(i-1)a\quad\forall i\in[n].

Proof.

Again, we argue using the contrapositive.
Assume that
x_{(j)}>1+(j-1)a
for some
j\in[n]
.
Then
x_{(j)}=1+qa
for some
q\in[n-1]
by Lemma
2.3
.
Let
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime},\dots,x_{n}^{\prime})
be any permutation of
\mathbf{x}
such that
x_{1}^{\prime}=x_{(j)}
.
Under the parking experiment for
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
, since
x_{1}^{\prime}=x_{(j)}\leq x_{(j+1)}\leq\cdots\leq x_{(n)}
, there are at least
n-j+1
cars, including the first car with length
b
, preferring to park in
[1+qa,m]
.
The total length of these cars is then at least
b+(n-j)a
, so to ensure that they all can park,
|[1+qa,m]|\geq b+(n-j)a
must hold.
However,

|[1+qa,m]|=|[1+qa,b+(n-1)a]|=b+(n-q-1)a<b+(n-j)a.

Hence,
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\notin\mathsf{PA}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
, so
\mathbf{x}\notin\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
, as desired.
∎

We are now ready to prove the closed form of
\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
\mathbf{y}=(b,a^{n-1})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, where
n\geq 2
, and
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in[m]^{n}
.
If
a\mid b
, then
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
if and only if

x_{(i)}\in\{1+(k-1)a:k\in[i]\}\quad\forall i\in[n].

(5)

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
.
Combining Lemmas
2.3
and
2.4
, it follows that
\mathbf{x}
satisfies (
5
).

We now prove the converse.
Assume that (
5
) holds.
In other words, for all
i\in[n]
,
x_{(i)}\leq 1+(i-1)a
and
x_{i}=1+t_{i}a
for some
t_{i}\in[n-1]_{0}
.
We will show inductively that parking succeeds under
\mathbf{x}
.

Under the parking experiment for
\mathbf{x}
, the first car, with length
b=ha
and preference
x_{1}
, will park in spots
[x_{1},x_{1}+ha-1]
, where
x_{1}=1+t_{1}a
for some
t_{1}\in[n-1]_{0}
; that is,
[1+t_{1}a,(t_{1}+h)a]
is occupied by car 1.
Next, the second car, with length
a
and preference
x_{2}
, will attempt to park in
[x_{2},x_{2}+a-1]
, where
x_{2}=1+t_{2}a
for some
t_{2}\in[n-1]_{0}
.
Note that (
5
) stipulates that at most one entry of
\mathbf{x}
can be
1+(n-1)a
, so if
t_{1}=n-1
, then
t_{2}<t_{1}
, so that car 2 successfully parks in
[x_{2},x_{2}+a-1]=[1+t_{2}a,(t_{2}+1)a]
.
Now, assume
t_{1}<n-1
.
If
t_{1}+h\leq n-1
, then we have

t_{2}\in[t_{1}-1]_{0}\cup\{t_{1}+h,t_{1}+h+1,\dots,n-1\}\quad\text{or}\quad t_{2}\in\{t_{1},t_{1}+1,\dots,t_{1}+h-1\}.

In the former, car 2 successfully parks in
[x_{2},x_{2}+a-1]=[1+t_{2}a,(t_{2}+1)a]
.
In the latter, car 2 fails to park in
[x_{2},x_{2}+a-1]=[1+t_{2}a,(t_{2}+1)a]
since car 1 occupies
[1+t_{1}a,(t_{1}+h)a]\supseteq[1+t_{2}a,(t_{2}+1)a]
.
Thus, car 2 must park in
[1+(t_{1}+h)a,(t_{1}+h+1)a]
.
If
t_{1}+h>n-1
, then we have

t_{2}\in[t_{1}-1]_{0}\quad\text{or}\quad t_{2}\in\{t_{1},t_{1}+1,\dots,n-1\}.

In the former, car 2 successfully parks in
[x_{2},x_{2}+a-1]=[1+t_{2}a,(t_{2}+1)a]
.
In the latter, car 2 again fails to park in
[x_{2},x_{2}+a-1]
for the same reason, so it must park in
[1+(t_{1}+h)a,(t_{1}+h+1)a]
.
This proves that car
2
will occupy
[S_{2},S_{2}+a-1]
, where
S_{2}\equiv 1\pmod{a}
, which will serve as our base case.

Assume now that the first
k\in[n-1]
cars are parked, and each car
j
, where
j\in[k]\setminus\{1\}
, occupies
[S_{j},S_{j}+a-1]
for some
S_{j}\equiv 1\pmod{a}
.
Moreover, for
S_{1}=1+t_{1}a
, car 1 occupies
[S_{1},S_{1}+ha-1]
.
Thus, all the terms of the sequence
(S_{1},S_{2},\dots,S_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
are distinct, so that its nondecreasing rearrangement
(S_{(1)},S_{(2)},\dots,S_{(k)})
is strictly increasing.
Now, for any
j\in[k]
, define

\ell(j)\coloneqq\begin{cases}ha&\text{if }S_{(j)}=S_{1}\\
a&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then at this stage of the parking experiment, the set of unoccupied parking spots is

\mathcal{U}\coloneqq[m]\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}[S_{(j)},S_{(j)}+\ell(j)-1]=\bigcup_{j=0}^{k}\mathcal{U}_{j},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{U}_{j}\coloneqq\begin{cases}[1,S_{(1)}-1]&\text{if }j=0\\
[S_{(j)}+\ell(j),S_{(j+1)}-1]&\text{if }j\in[k-1]\\
[S_{(k)}+\ell(k),m]&\text{if }j=k.\end{cases}

(6)

We note that the
\mathcal{U}_{j}
are pairwise disjoint and possibly empty, and there should be a total of
(n+h-1)a-(h+k-1)a=(n-k)a
unoccupied spots, so that
|\mathcal{U}|\coloneqq\sum_{j=0}^{k}|\mathcal{U}_{j}|=(n-k)a
.
In particular, as the inductive hypothesis gives
S_{(j)}\equiv 1\pmod{a}
, (
6
) implies that any
\mathcal{U}_{j}
has a left endpoint congruent to
1\pmod{a}
, and
|\mathcal{U}_{j}|
is an integral multiple of
a
.

We now show that the
(k+1)
th car can park.
If
x_{k+1}=1
, then (
6
) implies that car
k+1
can find some interval of
a
unoccupied spots to park in.
If
x_{k+1}=1+t_{k+1}a
for some
t_{k+1}\in[n-1]
, then we have the following three distinct possibilities.

Case 2.5.1
.

t_{1}=n-1
and
x_{k+1}=1+t_{k+1}a<1+t_{1}a
.

Proof.

Here, the first car occupies
[1+(n-1)a,(n+h-1)a]
.
If spot
1+t_{k+1}a
is empty, then car
k+1
will park in
\mathcal{P}\coloneqq[1+t_{k+1}a,(t_{k+1}+1)a]
by (
6
).
Otherwise, we claim that car
k+1
must be able to find and park in an interval of
a
unoccupied spots in
\mathcal{A}\coloneqq[1+(t_{k+1}+1)a,(n-1)a]
.

Figure 2.

Illustration of Case
2.5.1
.
Car 1 takes the last
b
spots in blue.
Next, car
k+1
either parks according to its preference in the red, or it must be able to find an alternative spot in the green, which is located between the red and blue regions.

Assume the contrary, so that
\mathcal{A}
is completely occupied.
Let

S_{\text{max}}=\max\{s\in[m]:s\text{ is unoccupied when car $k+1$ attempts to park}\}.

(7)

Since
S_{\text{max}}
is the right endpoint of an interval of unoccupied spots, by (
6
),
S_{\text{max}}=ta
for some
t\in[t_{k+1}]
.
Then
[1+S_{\text{max}},(n+h-1)a]
, where
|[1+S_{\text{max}},(n+h-1)a]|=(n+h-t-1)a
, is occupied by
n-t
cars, one of which is car 1 (with length
ha
) and
n-t-1
of which are cars with length
a
.
Moreover, by assumption,
[1+S_{\text{max}},(n+h-1)a]\supseteq\mathcal{P}
.
Thus, including car
k+1
, at least
n-t+1
cars have preference at least
1+S_{\text{max}}
since
S_{\text{max}}
is unoccupied.
In other words, at most
t-1
cars have preference strictly less than
1+S_{\text{max}}
, forcing
x_{(t)}\geq 1+S_{\text{max}}=1+ta
.
This contradicts
x_{(t)}\leq 1+(t-1)a
.
∎

Case 2.5.2
.

t_{1}\neq n-1
and
x_{k+1}=1+t_{k+1}a<1+t_{1}a
.

Proof.

Now, the first car no longer occupies spot
m
.
Note again by (
6
) that if spot
1+t_{k+1}a
is empty, then car
k+1
will park in
\mathcal{P}\coloneqq[1+t_{k+1}a,(t_{k+1}+1)a]
.
Otherwise, car
k+1
searches for an interval of
a
unoccupied spots in
\mathcal{A}_{1}\coloneqq[1+(t_{k+1}+1)a,t_{1}a]
.
If it finds one, then it will occupy it.
If it does not, then we claim that it must be able to find and park in such an interval in
\mathcal{A}_{2}\coloneqq[1+(h+t_{1})a,(n+h-1)a]
.

Figure 3.

Illustration of Case
2.5.2
.
Car 1 takes the
b
spots in blue.
Next, car
k+1
either parks according to its preference in the red, or it must be able to find an alternative spot in one of the green regions, which surround car 1.

Assume the contrary, so that
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
are completely occupied.
Let
S_{\text{max}}
be as in (
7
), so that again,
S_{\text{max}}=ta
for some
t\in[t_{k+1}]
.
Repeating the argument for Case
2.5.1
allows us to arrive at the same contradiction.

∎

Case 2.5.3
.

x_{k+1}=1+t_{k+1}a\geq 1+t_{1}a
.

Proof.

Note first that
t_{1}<n-1
due to (
5
). Here, we claim that car
k+1
will find and park in an interval of
a
unoccupied spots in
\mathcal{I}\coloneqq[1+(h+t_{1})a,(n+h-1)a]
.

Figure 4.

Illustration of Case
2.5.3
.
Car 1 takes the
b
spots in blue.
Next, car
k+1
will find parking in the yellow, which is located to the right of car 1.

Assume now that car
k+1
cannot find such an interval, and let
S_{\text{max}}
be as in (
7
).

Note that we may take
h+t_{1}<n-1
.
Otherwise,
h+t_{1}\geq n-1
, and since car 1 occupies
[1+t_{1}a,(h+t_{1})a]
, any car with preference at least
1+t_{1}a
is forced to park in an interval of
a
unoccupied spots in
\mathcal{I}
.
Then our assumption implies that
\mathcal{I}
is completely occupied when car
k+1
attempts to park, so that
S_{\text{max}}=ta
for some
t\in[t_{k+1}]
.
Repeating the argument for Case
2.5.1
then completes the proof.
For similar reasons, we may assume
S_{\text{max}}\geq 1+(h+t_{1})a
.

Given these additional assumptions, we have

S_{\text{max}}=ta\quad\text{for some}\quad t\in\{h+t_{1}+1,h+t_{1}+2,\dots,t_{k+1}\}.

Thus,
[1+S_{\text{max}},(n+h-1)a]
, where
|[1+S_{\text{max}},(n+h-1)a]|=(n+h-t-1)a
, is occupied by
n+h-t-1
cars, all of which must have length
a
since
S_{\text{max}}\geq 1+(h+t_{1})a
.
Thus, including car
k+1
, at least
n+h-t
cars have preference at least
1+S_{\text{max}}
since
S_{\text{max}}
is unoccupied, so at most
t-h
cars have preference strictly less than
1+S_{\text{max}}
.
Thus,
x_{(t-h+1)}\geq 1+S_{\text{max}}=1+ta
.
Since
h
is a positive integer, this contradicts
x_{(t-h+1)}\leq 1+(t-h)a
.
∎

This covers all cases and completes the induction.
Hence,
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{PA}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
.
Note that (
5
) does not depend on how the entries of
\mathbf{x}
are permuted, so parking succeeds under any permutation of
\mathbf{x}
.
Therefore, if
\mathbf{x}
satisfies (
5
), then
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
, concluding the proof.
∎

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
\mathbf{y}=(b,a^{n-1})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, where
n\geq 2
, and
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in[m]^{n}
.
If
b>(n-1)a
, then
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
if and only if

x_{(i)}\in\{1+(k-1)a:k\in[i]\}\quad\forall i\in[n].

(8)

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
.
Lemmas
2.3
and
2.4
again show that
\mathbf{x}
satisfies (
8
).

We now prove the converse.
Assume that (
8
) holds.
In other words, for all
i\in[n]
,
x_{(i)}\leq 1+(i-1)a
and
x_{i}=1+t_{i}a
for some
t_{i}\in[n-1]_{0}
.
We will show inductively that parking succeeds under
\mathbf{x}
.

Under the parking experiment for
\mathbf{x}
, the first car, with length
b
and preference
x_{1}
, will park in
[x_{1},x_{1}+b-1]
, where
x_{1}=1+t_{1}a
for some
t_{1}\in[n-1]_{0}
.
Thus,
[1+t_{1}a,b+t_{1}a]
is now occupied.
Next, the second car, with length

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
\mathbf{y}=(b,a^{n-1})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, where
n\geq 2
, and
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in[m]^{n}
.
If
a\nmid b
and
b<(n-1)a
, then
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
if and only if

x_{(i)}\in\begin{cases}\{1+(k-1)a:k\in[i]\}&\forall i\in\left[\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor+1\right]\\
\left\{1+(k-1)a:k\in\left[\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor+1\right]\right\}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(10)

Proof.

First, assume that
\mathbf{x}
does not satisfy (
10
).
That is, there exists
j\in[n]
for which at least one of the following is true:

\displaystyle x_{(j)}

\displaystyle\not\equiv 1\pmod{a}

\displaystyle x_{(j)}

\displaystyle>1+(j-1)a\text{ if }j\leq\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor+1

\displaystyle x_{(j)}

\displaystyle>1+\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor a\text{ if }j>\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor+1.

Lemma
2.3
allows us to assume that any entry of
\mathbf{x}
is congruent to
1\pmod{a}
.
Similarly, Lemma
2.4
allows us to assume that
x_{(j)}\leq 1+(j-1)a
if
j\leq\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor+1
.

If
x_{(j)}>1+\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor a
, where
j>\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor+1
, then write
x_{(j)}=1+qa
, where
q\geq\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor+1
.
Let
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime},\dots,x_{n}^{\prime})
be any permutation of
\mathbf{x}
such that
x_{1}^{\prime}=x_{(1)}
and
x_{2}^{\prime}=x_{(j)}
.
Under the parking experiment for
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
, the first car will occupy the
b
spots
[1,b]
, and the second car will occupy the
a
spots
[x_{(j)},x_{(j)}+a-1]=[1+qa,(q+1)a]
.
This leaves the
qa-b
spots
[b+1,qa]
empty.
Note that as
a\nmid b
, we have
\frac{b}{a}-1<\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor<\frac{b}{a}
, so

qa-b\geq\left(\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor+1\right)a-b>\frac{ab}{a}-b=0;

thus,
[b+1,qa]\neq\emptyset
.
Similarly,
\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor<\frac{b}{a}<\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor+1
, which yields

\left(q-\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor-1\right)a<qa-b<\left(q-\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor\right)a,

so
qa-b
is strictly bounded between consecutive multiples of
a
.
Hence, none of the remaining cars, which all have length
a
, can fill
[b+1,qa]
.
Thus, we have
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{PA}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
and
\mathbf{x}\notin\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
.

Thus, if
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
, then
\mathbf{x}
satisfies (
10
).

We now prove the converse.
Assume that (
10
) holds.
In other words, for all
i\in\left[\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor+1\right]
,
x_{(i)}\leq 1+(i-1)a
, for all
\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor+1<i\leq n
,
x_{(i)}\leq 1+\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor a
, and
x_{i}=1+t_{i}a
for some
t_{i}\in\left[\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor\right]_{0}
.
We will show inductively that parking succeeds under
\mathbf{x}
.

Under the parking experiment for
\mathbf{x}
, the first car, with length
b
and preference
x_{1}
, will park in
[x_{1},x_{1}+b-1]
, where
x_{1}=1+t_{1}a
for some
t_{1}\in\left[\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor\right]_{0}
.
Then, the second car, with length
a
and preference
x_{2}
, will attempt to park in
[x_{2},x_{2}+a-1]
, where
x_{2}=1+t_{2}a
for some
t_{2}\in\left[\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor\right]_{0}
.
We have

t_{2}\in[t_{1}-1]_{0}\quad\text{or}\quad t_{2}\in\left\{t_{1},t_{1}+1,\dots,\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor\right\}.

In the former, car 2 successfully parks in
[x_{2},x_{2}+a-1]=[1+t_{2}a,(t_{2}+1)a]
.
In the latter, car 2 fails to park in
[x_{2},x_{2}+a-1]=[1+t_{2}a,(t_{2}+1)a]

[1+t_{1}a,b+t_{1}a]
, where

, so

and
b+t_{1}a\geq 1+t_{2}a
.
Thus, car 2 must park in
[1+b+t_{1}a,b+(t_{1}+1)a]
.
This proves that car 2 will occupy
[S_{2},S_{2}+a-1]
, where

S_{2}\equiv\begin{cases}1\pmod{a}&\text{if }S_{2}<1+t_{1}a\\
1+b\pmod{a}&\text{if }S_{2}>b+t_{1}a,\end{cases}

which will serve as our base case.
Assume now that the first
k\in[n-1]
cars are parked, and each car
j
, where
j\in[k]\setminus\{1\}
, occupies
[S_{j},S_{j}+a-1]

S_{j}\equiv\begin{cases}1\pmod{a}&\text{if }S_{j}<1+t_{1}a\\
1+b\pmod{a}&\text{if }S_{j}>b+t_{1}a.\end{cases}

S_{1}=1+t_{1}a
, car 1 occupies
[S_{1},S_{1}+b-1]
.

, where the

) with

as in (

).
Furthermore, from (

), any
\mathcal{U}_{j}
has a left endpoint congruent to either
1\pmod{a}
or
1+b\pmod{a}
, and
|\mathcal{U}_{j}|
is an integral multiple of
a
.

We now show that the
(k+1)
th car can park. If
x_{k+1}=1
, then (
6
) implies that car
k+1
can find some interval of
a
unoccupied spots to park in.
If
x_{k+1}=1+t_{k+1}a
for

, then we have the following two distinct possibilities.

Case 2.7.1
.

x_{k+1}=1+t_{k+1}a<1+t_{1}a
.

Proof.

Note first that car 1 cannot occupy
[1+(n-1)a,b+(n-1)a]

Otherwise, it searches for an interval of

Case 2.7.2
.

x_{k+1}=1+t_{k+1}a\geq 1+t_{1}a
.

Here, we claim that car

will be able to find and park in an interval of

unoccupied spots in

.
Assume not, and let
S_{\text{max}}

).

),
S_{\text{max}}=ta+b
for some

.

\left(t+\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor\right)a<S_{\text{max}}=ta+b<\left(t+\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor+1\right)a.

.

Theorems

illustrate that for almost constant

,

is maximal when

and minimal otherwise.
We now prove a surprising partial converse: the almost constant condition on length vectors is necessary for their characteristic to be maximal.
Recall the following results.

Lemma 2.8

(Lemma 2.3 in
[

]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
and
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
.
If
k=\argmax_{i\in[n]}x_{i}
, then
\mathbf{x}_{\widehat{k}}\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n-1}(\mathbf{y}_{|_{n-1}})
.
In particular, if
\chi(\mathbf{y})=\alpha
, then
\chi(\mathbf{y}_{|_{n-1}})\geq\alpha-1
.

Lemma 2.9

(Theorem 5.1 in
[
4
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},y_{2})\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
.
Then
\chi(\mathbf{y})=1
if and only if
y_{1}\geq y_{2}
.

Lemma 2.10

(Theorem 5.4 in
[
4
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})\in\mathbb{N}^{3}
.
If
\chi(\mathbf{y})=2
, then
y_{2}=y_{3}
.

Now, we will present the partial converse, which implies one direction of Theorem
B
.
Its proof will be via induction on
n
, so the above results will be instrumental in setting up the base cases and proving the inductive step.

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, where
n\geq 2
.
If
\chi(\mathbf{y})=n-1
, then

y_{2}=y_{3}=\cdots=y_{n}.

Proof.

We will proceed via induction on
n
.
Note that
\chi(\mathbf{y})=n-1
implies that there exists
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{N}_{>1}^{n-1}
such that
(1,\mathbf{w})\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
.
For
n=2
, if
(1,w)\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{2}(\mathbf{y})
, where
w>1
, then the conclusion follows vacuously by Lemma
2.9
.
For
n=3
, the conclusion follows by Lemma
2.10
.

Thus, we have verified the base cases, so assume that the statement is true up to some
n=k\geq 3
.
That is, for any
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
such that
\chi(\mathbf{y})=k-1
,
we have
y_{2}=y_{3}=\cdots=y_{k}
.
We now show that this is true for
n=k+1
.
Let
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}=(y_{1}^{\prime},y_{2}^{\prime},\dots,y_{k+1}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{N}^{k+1}
such that
\chi(\mathbf{y}^{\prime})=k
.
Then there exists
\mathbf{x}=(1,\mathbf{w}^{\prime})\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{k+1}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime})
, where
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}_{>1}^{k}
.
Assume without loss of generality that
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
is nondecreasing.
As
w^{\prime}_{k}=\max\mathbf{x}
, by Lemma
2.8
, we have
(1,\mathbf{w}^{\prime}_{|{k-1}})\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{k}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{|_{k}})
.
Thus,
\chi(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{|_{k}})\leq k-1
, and we have equality since
k-1
is the maximal characteristic.
Hence, by the inductive hypothesis,
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{|_{k}}
is of the form
(b,a^{k-1})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
, which shows that
y^{\prime}_{2}=y^{\prime}_{3}=\cdots=y^{\prime}_{k}
.
Consequently,
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
is of the form
(b,a^{k-1},c)
for some
c\in\mathbb{N}
, and it remains to show that
c=a
.

To do this, we prove the contrapositive: if
c\neq a
, then
\mathbf{x}=(1,\mathbf{w}^{\prime})\not\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{k+1}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime})
.
We claim that parking fails for the permutation
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(\mathbf{w}^{\prime},1)
.
This can be rewritten as

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(w^{\prime}_{1},w^{\prime}_{2},\dots,w^{\prime}_{k-1},w^{\prime}_{k},1)=(\mathbf{w}^{\prime}_{|_{k-1}},w^{\prime}_{k},1).

Since
(1,\mathbf{w}^{\prime}_{|{k-1}})\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{k}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{|_{k}})
, we have
(\mathbf{w}^{\prime}_{|_{k-1}},1)\in\mathsf{PA}_{k}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{|_{k}})
.
Thus, consider the parking experiment for
(\mathbf{w}^{\prime}_{|_{k-1}},1)
with length vector
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{|_{k}}
.
As all cars park successfully and
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}_{>1}^{k}
, car
k
, with preference
1
, must occupy
[1,a]
, while the remaining cars completely occupy
[1+a,m-c]
.
This means that in the parking experiment for
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(\mathbf{w}^{\prime}_{|_{k-1}},w^{\prime}_{k},1)
(with length vector
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
),
the first
k-1
cars will completely occupy
[1+a,m-c]
, so they leave
[1,a]
and
[m-c+1,m]
unoccupied.
By assumption,
w^{\prime}_{k}=\max\mathbf{x}^{\prime}>1
, so car
k
, with length
a
, must occupy a subset of
[m-c+1,m]
(otherwise, parking fails, and the proof is complete).

We now consider the following two distinct cases.

Lemma 3.2

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}

Then there is a permutation
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{n})
of the entries of

such that

.

(ii)

b_{i}\geq a_{i}
for all

b_{i}=a_{i}
for all
i\notin S\cup\{1\}
.

(iii)

b_{i}=\min(b_{i},b_{i+1},\dots,b_{n})
for all

.

Proof.

We will describe an algorithm that sequentially swaps certain entries of
\mathbf{a}

\mathbf{b}

\mathbf{a}^{(0)}=\mathbf{a}
and

.
For

\mathbf{a}^{(k)}=(a_{1}^{(k)},a_{2}^{(k)},\dots,a_{n}^{(k)})
satisfies
a_{1}^{(k)}=\min\mathbf{a}^{(k)}
.
If so, we claim
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{a}^{(k)}
and stop.
Otherwise, construct the index set
S^{(k)}=\{i\in[n]:a_{1}^{(k)}>a_{i}^{(k)}\}
, and let
j_{k}=\max S^{(k)}
.
Then, swap the positions of
a_{1}^{(k)}
and

to obtain the next iterate

\mathbf{a}^{(k+1)}=(a_{1}^{(k+1)},a_{2}^{(k+1)},\dots,a_{n}^{(k+1)})\coloneqq(a_{j_{k}}^{(k)},a_{2}^{(k)},\dots,a_{j_{k}-1}^{(k)},a_{1}^{(k)},a_{j_{k}+1}^{(k)},\dots,a_{n}^{(k)}),

and repeat the process.

To prove the correctness of this algorithm, we will first show that it terminates; this gives property
(i)
in the process.

Claim 3.4.1
.

The sequence of index sets
\{S^{(k)}\}
is nested and strictly decreasing.

Proof.

Let
k
be any nonnegative integer, and assume
S^{(k)}\neq\emptyset
.
Because
a_{1}^{(k+1)}=a_{j_{k}}^{(k)}
, where

a_{1}^{(k)}>a_{1}^{(k+1)}
(i.e. the sequence consisting of the first entries
a_{1}^{(k)}

Moreover, by construction,
a_{i}^{(k)}=a_{i}^{(k+1)}
if and only if
i\neq 1,j_{k}
.
It is clear that

We also have
a_{j_{k}}^{(k+1)}=a_{1}^{(k)}>a_{1}^{(k+1)}
, so
j_{k}\notin S^{(k+1)}

∎

We automatically have
\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{y})\subseteq 1+\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{y})
due to Lemma
4.4
, so we show the reverse inclusion.
Let
s=\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{y})
and
\mathbf{x}=(1^{n-1},1+s)
.
Consider any permutation
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2},\dots,x^{\prime}_{n})
of
\mathbf{x}
such that
x^{\prime}_{1}\neq 1+s
.
Under the parking experiment for
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
, the first car occupies
[1,y_{1}]
, and
|[1,y_{1}]|=y_{1}>\sum_{j=2}^{n}y_{j}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{j}y_{j}=s
.
Moreover, there exists
i\in[n]
such that
x^{\prime}_{i}=1+s
, and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{|_{i-1}}=(1^{i-1})\in\mathsf{PA}_{i-1}(\mathbf{y}_{|_{i-1}})
.
Thus, by Lemma
3.2
,
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{|_{i}}\in\mathsf{PA}_{i}(\mathbf{y}_{|_{i}})
. The remaining cars all have preference 1, so parking succeeds and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{PA}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
.
Now, consider the remaining permutation
(1+s,1^{n-1})
.
Under its parking experiment, the first car leaves
[1,s]
empty.
Claim
4.5.1
guarantees that a unique subset of the remaining
n-1
cars, say cars
i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{q}
, can precisely fill
[1,s]
, so
\sum_{j=1}^{q}y_{i_{j}}=s
.

Claim 4.5.2
.

Cars
i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{q}
are the only ones that will park in
[1,s]
under the parking experiment for
(1+s,1^{n-1})
.

Proof.

Due to (
2
),

y_{2}>y_{3}>\dots>y_{i_{1}-1}>\sum_{j=i_{1}}^{n}y_{j}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{q}y_{i_{j}}=s,

so cars
2,3,\dots,i_{1}-1
, all with preference
1
, will drive past
[1,s]
and successively park immediately after.
Thus, car
i_{1}
must park in
[1,s]
, establishing the base case of our claim.
Now, inductively, assume that cars
i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{p}
are the only ones that parked in
[1,s]
among the first
i_{p}
cars, where
p\in[q-1]
.
Note that the unoccupied spots of
[1,s]
are
[\sum_{j=1}^{p}y_{i_{j}},s]
, which has length
\sum_{j=p+1}^{q}y_{i_{j}}
.
Then by (
2
), we have

y_{i_{p}+1}>y_{i_{p}+2}>\dots>y_{i_{p+1}-1}>\sum_{j=i_{p+1}}^{n}y_{j}\geq\sum_{j=p+1}^{q}y_{i_{j}},

so cars
i_{p}+1,i_{p}+2,\dots,i_{p+1}-1
will drive past
[\sum_{j=1}^{p}y_{i_{j}},s]
(and successively park immediately after).
Thus, car
i_{p+1}
is the next car to park in
[1,s]
, completing the induction.
∎

From Claim
4.5.2
, it follows that parking succeeds since each car
j
, where
j\in[n]\setminus\{1,i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{q}\}
, has preference
1
and drives past
[1,s]
, so they will successively fill
[1+s+y_{1},m]
.
Hence, we have
(1^{n-1},1+s)\in\mathsf{PA}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
, so
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
and
\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{y})=1+\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{y})
.

To conclude, we will prove the following.

Claim 4.5.3
.

If
\mathbf{y}
satisfies (
2
), then
\chi(\mathbf{y})=1
.

Proof.

We first prove that there cannot exist
1<w_{1}\leq w_{2}
such that
(1^{n-2},w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
.
Assume otherwise.
Then Lemma
4.2
, Lemma
4.4
, and our result above that
\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{y})=1+\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{y})
guarantee
w_{1}-1=\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{y}
for some
\mathbf{b}\in\{0\}\times\{0,1\}^{n-1}
.
Now, let

k=\min\{i\in[n]:b_{i}=1\}

(we note that
k>1
since
b_{1}=0
).
Consider the preferences
(w_{1},1^{k-2},w_{2},1^{n-k})
.
Under its parking experiment, the first car leaves
[1,\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{y}]
empty, where
|[1,\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{y}]|=\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{y}
.
By Claim
4.5.1
,
\mathbf{b}
is unique, so to ensure
[1,\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{y}]
is completely filled, car
k
must park here.
However, by construction, this is not the case since car
k
has preference
w_{2}\geq w_{1}=1+\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{y}
.
Hence,
[1,\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{y}]
will have unoccupied spaces by the end of the parking experiment, so parking fails, and it follows that if
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
, then
\deg\mathbf{x}\neq 2
.
Thus,
\chi(\mathbf{y})<2
by Theorem
D
(b)
, and so
\chi(\mathbf{y})=1
since
\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{y})\supsetneq\{1\}
.
∎

Therefore,
\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv},\uparrow}_{n}(\mathbf{y})=\{(1^{n-1},1+s):s\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{y})\}
, as desired.

For our count, note that the only nondecreasing invariant parking assortments of
\mathbf{y}
are of the form
(1^{n-1},1+s)
, where
s=\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{y}
for some
\mathbf{b}\in\{0\}\times\{0,1\}^{n-1}
.
Claim
4.5.1
ensures that
\mathbf{b}
is unique, so each choice of
\mathbf{b}
gives a distinct value of
s
.
Thus, by definition,
|\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv},\uparrow}_{n}(\mathbf{y})|=|\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{y})|=2^{n-1}
.

Lastly, note that each of the
2^{n-1}-1
nontrivial elements of
\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv},\uparrow}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
have
n
distinct permutations of their entries due to their unique non-
1
entry.
Therefore,
|\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})|=n(2^{n-1}-1)+1=2^{n-1}n-n+1
, as claimed.
∎

We are now ready to prove the rest of Theorem
E
, which gives a necessary condition for equality to be achieved in Theorem
E
(b)
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
.
If
1+\sum_{j=2}^{n}y_{j}\in\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{y})
, then
y_{1}=\max\mathbf{y}
.

Proof.

If
y_{1}<\max\mathbf{y}
, let
k\in[n]\setminus\{1\}
satisfy
y_{k}=\max\mathbf{y}
, and consider the preferences
(1^{k-1},1+\sum_{j=2}^{n}y_{j},1^{n-k})
.
Then under its parking experiment, the first
k-1
cars park successively to fill
[1,\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}y_{j}]
.
Car
k
occupies
[s,s+y_{k}-1]
, where
s\geq 1+\sum_{j=2}^{n}y_{j}
, so
s+y_{k}-1\geq y_{k}+\sum_{j=2}^{n}y_{j}>m
and hence parking fails.
Thus,
(1^{n-1},1+\sum_{j=2}^{n}y_{j})\notin\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
, so we have the contrapositive of the desired result.
∎

Proof of Theorem
E
(d)
.

If
|\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{y})|=2^{n-1}
, then
|\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{y})|=|\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{y})|=2^{n-1}
.
Thus, there cannot exist
\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}\in\{0\}\times\{0,1\}^{n-1}
such that
\mathbf{b}\neq\mathbf{c}
and
\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{y}
; otherwise,
|\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{y})|\leq 2^{n-1}-1
.
Then
1+\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{y})
for any
\mathbf{b}\in\{0\}\times\{0,1\}^{n-1}
.
In particular, choosing
\mathbf{b}=(0,1^{n-1})
gives
1+\sum_{j=2}^{n}y_{j}\in\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{y})
.
Lemma
4.6
then ensures
y_{1}=\max\mathbf{y}
, so that
y_{1}\geq y_{2}
.

We now show that the remaining inequality detailed in (
3
) holds via induction.
For the base case, suppose
y_{2}\leq\sum_{i=3}^{n}y_{i}
.
For
\mathbf{b}=(0^{2},1^{n-2})
, we have
1+\sum_{i=3}^{n}y_{i}\in\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{y})
.
Hence,
(1+\sum_{i=3}^{n}y_{i},1^{n-1})\in\mathsf{PA}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
, so under its parking experiment, the first car leaves
[1,\sum_{i=3}^{n}y_{i}]
empty for other cars to fill.
As
y_{2}\leq\sum_{i=3}^{n}y_{i}
, car 2 parks in
[1,\sum_{i=3}^{n}y_{i}]
.
Thus, there exists
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{n})\in\{0\}\times\{0,1\}^{n-1}
with
c_{2}=1
such that
\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{y}=\sum_{i=3}^{n}y_{i}=\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{y}
, a contradiction since
\mathbf{b}\neq\mathbf{c}
.
This shows that
y_{2}>\sum_{i=3}^{n}y_{i}
.

Now, assume that for some
p\in[n]\setminus\{1\}
, we have
y_{j}>\sum_{i=j+1}^{n}y_{i}
for all
j\in[p-1]\setminus\{1\}
.
We seek to prove that
y_{p}>\sum_{i=p+1}^{n}y_{i}
.
To do so, we will employ the same argument from above.
Assume
y_{p}\leq\sum_{i=p+1}^{n}y_{i}
.
Choosing
\mathbf{b}=(0^{p},1^{n-p})

yields
(1+\sum_{i=p+1}^{n}y_{i},1^{n-1})\in\mathsf{PA}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
, so under its parking experiment,
[1,\sum_{i=p+1}^{n}y_{i}]
is filled by a subset of cars
2,3,\dots,n
.
By the inductive hypothesis, for any
j\in[p-1]\setminus\{1\}
,

y_{j}>\sum_{i=j+1}^{n}y_{i}>\sum_{i=p+1}^{n}y_{i}.

Since
y_{p}\leq\sum_{i=3}^{n}y_{i}
, car
p
must be the first to park in
[1,\sum_{i=p+1}^{n}y_{i}]
.
Thus, there exists
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{n})\in\{0\}\times\{0,1\}^{n-1}
with
c_{p}=1
such that
\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{y}=\sum_{i=p+1}^{n}y_{i}=\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{y}
, so we arrive at another contradiction.
Therefore,
y_{p}>\sum_{i=p+1}^{n}y_{i}
, completing the induction.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

The condition (
3
) is not sufficient for
|\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{y})|=2^{n-1}
.
For
\mathbf{y}=(7,5,3,1)
, one can check that
\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{y})=\{1,2,4,5,6,7,9\}
, so
|\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{y})|=7<2^{3}
.

Given what was discussed above, we conclude by proving Theorem
F
, which gives an upper bound on
|\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv},\uparrow}_{n}(\mathbf{y})|
independent of
m
.

Proof of Theorem
F
.

The set inclusion
\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv},\uparrow}_{n}(\mathbf{y})\subseteq\{1\}^{n-\chi(\mathbf{y})}\times\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{y})^{\chi(\mathbf{y})}
follows by Definition
1.7
and Lemma
4.2
.
To prove the bound, note that the nondecreasing elements of
\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{y})^{\chi(\mathbf{y})}
are precisely multisets of cardinality
\chi(\mathbf{y})
taken from
\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{y})
.
Letting
|\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{y})|=W
and
\chi(\mathbf{y})=C
, by stars and bars, the number of such multisets is
B(W,C)\coloneqq\binom{W+C-1}{C}
.
By Pascal’s identity,
B(W+1,C)=\binom{(W+1)+C-1}{C}=\binom{(W+1)+C-2}{C-1}+\binom{(W+1)+C-2}{C}=\binom{W+C-1}{C}+\binom{(W+1)+C-2}{C-1}\geq B(W,C)
, so
B(W,C)
is nondecreasing with respect to
W
.
Similarly,
B(W,C+1)=\binom{W+(C+1)-1}{C+1}=\binom{W+C-1}{C}+\binom{W+C-1}{C+1}\geq B(W,C)
, so
B(W,C)
is nondecreasing with respect to
C
.
Therefore, since
W\leq 2^{n-1}
by Theorem
E
(b)
and
C\leq n-1
, we have
|\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv},\uparrow}_{n}(\mathbf{y})|\leq B(W,C)\leq B(2^{n-1},n-1)=\binom{2^{n-1}+n-2}{n-1}
, as claimed.
∎

5.
Open Problems

We now suggest various problems for future research.

5.1.
Length Vectors of Non-Maximal Characteristic

In Section
2
, we found a simple closed form for the set
\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}:\chi(\mathbf{y})=n-1\}
.
A natural follow-up to this is to consider finding closed forms for other characteristics.

Open Problem 1
.

Give a direct characterization of the preimage
\chi^{-1}_{n}(\alpha)\coloneqq\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}:\chi(\mathbf{y})=\alpha\}
for any
\alpha\in[n-1]_{0}
.

We note that Theorem
A
gives a containment of
\chi^{-1}_{n}(0)
in a relatively simple set.
However, refining this containment is the main difficulty.
For any
\alpha
, we suspect that an effective way to approach the problem is to look at repeated entries of
\mathbf{y}
or any matching partial sums of the entries of
\mathbf{y}
and study how these factors affect
\chi(\mathbf{y})
.

5.2.
Preserving the Characteristic

As discussed, Theorem
D
(d)
gives an easy way to bound
\chi(\mathbf{y})
given that we know
\chi(\mathbf{y}_{|_{k}})
for some
k<n
.
This can simplify the process for computing
\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
given
\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y}_{|_{k}})
, but there are still a great deal of possibilities to check especially if
k\ll n
.
In light of this case, we ask the following.

Open Problem 2
.

Let
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, where
\chi(\mathbf{y})=\alpha
, and
\mathbf{y}^{+}=(\mathbf{y},y_{n+1})\in\mathbb{N}^{n+1}
.
What must be true about
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{y}^{+}
to guarantee that
\chi(\mathbf{y}^{+})=\alpha
?

Note that if
\alpha=0
, then an answer to the above can help one construct
\chi(0,n)
using a recursive-like technique.
Moreover, given a condition on
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{y}^{+}
, one tool that may be useful in showing that
\chi(\mathbf{y}^{+})\neq n+1
is Theorem
E
(a)
.

5.3.
Sharper Bounds

Recall that in Theorem
F
, we had an upper bound for the size of
\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv},\uparrow}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
depending only on
n
.
One might ask if this is the best such bound or if there is a similar bound for the size of
\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})
.
For our bound, we posit that the answer is no, and our computational experiments suggest that there is a familiar connection between the best upper bound for
|\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})|
and the best upper bound for
|\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv},\uparrow}_{n}(\mathbf{y})|
, which is described below.

Open Problem 3
.

Let
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
.
Do
|\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{n}(\mathbf{y})|\leq(n+1)^{n-1}
and
|\mathsf{PA}^{\mathrm{inv},\uparrow}_{n}(\mathbf{y})|\leq C_{n}
hold for any
n
?

If this is true, then the upper bounds are automatically sharp since constant
\mathbf{y}
are examples of the equality case.
A potential way to approach this problem is to examine and understand which elements of
1+\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{y})
also are in
\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{y})
, as well as apply any results for Open Problems
1
and
2
.
