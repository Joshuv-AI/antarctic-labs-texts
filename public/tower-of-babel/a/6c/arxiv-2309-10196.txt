Abstract
. We give an alternative proof of the formula for the minimum distance of a projective Reed-Muller code of an arbitrary order. It leads to a complete characterization of the minimum weight codewords of a projective Reed-Muller code. This is then used to determine the number of minimum weight codewords of a projective Reed-Muller code. Various formulas for the dimension of a projective Reed-Muller code, and their equivalences are also discussed.



1.
Introduction

Reed-Muller codes constitute a widely studied and fairly well-understood class of linear codes. These codes were introduced, in the binary case, by Muller
[
21
]
, and further studied by Reed
[
22
]
in 1954. (See
[
23
]
for an account of the early history.) Generalizations of Reed-Muller codes to the
q
-ary case were considered and extensively studied in the following decade by Kasami, Lin, and Peterson
[
16
]
and by Delsarte, Goethals, and MacWilliams
[
10
]
. As a result of this, and also subsequent work, notably by Berger and Charpin
[
7
]
and by Heijnen and Pellikaan
[
14
]
, we know a great deal about
q
-ary Reed-Muller codes of an arbitrary order.
For instance, the length, dimension, minimum distance, characterization and enumeration of minimum weight codewords, duals, automorphisms, and generalized Hamming weights, are completely known. For convenience of reader and ease of reference, a brief summary of several of the known results is given in §
2.1
.

Projective Reed-Muller (PRM) codes in the general
q
-ary case, but of order
d<q
, were introduced by Lachaud
[
17
]
in 1986. (See Section 3 of
[
13
]
for an account of the history.) The length and dimension as well as bounds for the minimum distance of PRM codes of order
d<q
, and the exact minimum distance in the case
d=2
and
q=2^{e}
,
e\geq 2
, were obtained by Lachaud
[
17
]
. Some of these ideas and results go back to Manin’s course of lectures during 1981-82 at Moscow State University, and are sketched in Chapter I, Section 3, Example 2 b) of
[
28
]
. Tsfasman conjectured a sharp bound for the number of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-rational points of a projective hypersurface of a given degree
d\leq q
. This bound was proved in the affirmative by Serre
[
26
]
, and it was then shown by Lachaud
[
18
]
how the minimum distance of PRM codes of order
d\leq q
can be obtained as a consequence of Serre’s inequality. Independently, Sørensen
[
27
]
gave explicit formulas for the length, dimension, and the minimum distance of PRM codes not just of order
d\leq q
, but of an arbitrary order. Further, he determined the duals of PRM codes as well. Sørensen’s work has had a considerable impact as is indicated by the number of citations to
[
27
]
. Moreover, PRM codes continue to be of considerable current interest, and while the automorphism group is known (cf.
[
6
]
), the question about the determination of their generalized Hamming weights is still open in general even though there has been significant progress lately (see, e.g.,
[
5
]
and references therein for the current state of the art).

Recently, while revisiting Sørensen’s proof for the minimum distance of PRM codes of an arbitrary order, we noticed that there was a gap in the argument. While we realized later that the gap can be fixed and the result is correct as stated, we were able to obtain an alternative proof of Sørensen’s result by suitably adapting the arguments of Serre
[
26
]
.
An additional dividend of this alternative proof is that it leads to a characterization of the minimum weight codewords of a projective Reed-Muller code. Since such a characterization is not given in Sørensen’s paper
[
27
]
and it did not seem to be readily available in the literature, we initially thought that it was new. However, we later found that Rolland
[
25
, Lem.
2.3
]
has already given a characterization of the minimum weight codewords of a PRM code using different methods; see also Ballet and Rolland
[
2
, Thm. 8]
for a detailed proof of Rolland’s result. Nonetheless, we believe that the alternative proof given here and the resulting characterization is of some interest. Another noteworthy feature of our proof is the use of the notion of projective reduction that was developed and systematically used in
[
4
]
, and this seems quite natural in the context of PRM codes. We also give an explicit formula for the number of minimum weight codewords of a PRM code of an arbitrary order. As far as we know, this appears to be new and somewhat nontrivial, even after knowing a characterization of the
minimum weight codewords. Finally, we review various formulas for the dimension of a PRM code of an arbitrary order and make some remarks on their equivalence.
We also include a direct proof of one of the dimension formulas using the notion of projective reduction.
An appendix reproduces a hitherto unpublished proof of M. Quercia about the equivalence of two classical formulas for the dimension of a PRM code.

2.
Basics of Reed-Muller and Projective Reed-Muller Codes

Fix, throughout this paper, a prime power
q
and a positive integer
m
.
As usual,
\mathbb{Z}
denotes the set of integers and
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
the finite field with
q
elements. Given any
b\in\mathbb{Z}
and
a
in an integral domain
\mathsf{R}
containing
\mathbb{Z}
as a subring, the corresponding binomial coefficient is defined by

{a\choose b}:=\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\frac{a(a-1)\cdots(a-b+1)}{b!}}&\text{ if }b\geq 0,\\[7.5pt]
0&\text{ if }b<0.\end{cases}

This is, in general, an element of the quotient field of
\mathsf{R}
. In case
\mathsf{R}=\mathbb{Z}
, it is in
\mathbb{Z}
.

2.1.
Reed-Muller Codes

Let
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}
with
\nu\geq 0
, and let
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}]_{\leq\nu}
denote the space of all polynomials in
m
variables with coefficients in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
of (total) degree
\leq\nu
.
Write
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{m}=\{{{P}}_{1},\ldots,{{P}}_{q^{m}}\}
. Consider
the evaluation map

\displaystyle\text{ev}:{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}]_{\leq\nu}\to{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{q^{m}}\quad{\rm defined\;by\;}\quad f\mapsto c_{f}:=\left(f({{P}}_{1}),\ldots,f({{P}}_{q^{m}})\right).

Clearly
ev
is a linear map and
its image is a nondegenerate linear code of length
q^{m}
; this code
is called the
(generalized) Reed-Muller code
of order
\nu
, and it is denoted by
\mathrm{RM}_{q}(\nu,m)
.

It is easy to see that if
\nu<q
, then the map
ev
is injective and so
\dim\ \mathrm{RM}_{q}(\nu,m)={{m+\nu}\choose{\nu}}
; see, e.g.,
[
11
, Lem. 2.1]
.
Also, if
\nu<q
, then it follows from Ore’s inequality (see, e.g.,
[
11
, Cor. 2.7]
) that the minimum distance of
\mathrm{RM}_{q}(\nu,m)
is

(q-\nu)q^{m-1}
.
On the other hand, if
\nu\geq m(q-1)
, then it is not difficult to see (using, for instance, a slight modification of the argument in
[
12
, Rem. 2]
) that the map
\mathrm{ev}
is surjective and so
\mathrm{RM}_{q}(\nu,m)={\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{q^{m}}
. With this in view, we shall assume that
0\leq\nu\leq m(q-1)
. In this general case, the restriction of
\mathrm{ev}
to the space of all
reduced

f=\omega_{0}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{t}(1-\ell_{i}^{q-1})\right)\prod_{j=1}^{s}(\ell_{t+1}-\omega_{j})

(2)

[

[

(q-1)q^{t}{{m}\brack{t}}_{q}M_{s},\quad\text{where}\quad M_{s}:=\begin{cases}\displaystyle{{q\choose s}{{m-t}\brack{1}}_{q}}&\text{ if }s>0,\\[7.5pt]
1&\text{ if }s=0.\end{cases}

(3)

Here, and hereafter, for any
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
, we denote, as usual, by
{{a}\brack{b}}_{q}

{{a}\brack{b}}_{q}:=\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\frac{(q^{a}-1)(q^{a}-q)\cdots(q^{a}-q^{b-1})}{(q^{b}-1)(q^{b}-q)\cdots(q^{b}-q^{b-1})}}&\text{ if }0\leq b\leq a,\\[7.5pt]
0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Delsarte-Goethals-MacWilliams
[

also showed that the dual of

\mathrm{RM}_{q}(\nu,m)

\mathrm{RM}_{q}(m(q-1)-\nu-1,m)

by convention,
\mathrm{RM}_{q}(-1,m)

.
As indicated in the Introduction, the automorphism group as well as the generalized Hamming weights of

2.2.
Projective Reed-Muller Codes

Fix, in the remainder of this paper, a positive integer
d
. Given any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, let

p_{k}:=\begin{cases}1+q+q^{2}+\cdots+q^{k}&\text{ if }k\geq 0,\\
0&\text{ if }k<0.\end{cases}

Each point of the
m
-dimensional projective space
{\mathbb{P}}^{m}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
admits a unique representative in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{m+1}
in which the last
nonzero coordinate is
1
. Let
\mathsf{P}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{P}_{p_{m}}
be an ordered listing of such representatives
in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{m+1}
of points of
{\mathbb{P}}^{m}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
. Let
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}]_{d}
denote the space of homogeneous polynomials
in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\dots,X_{m}]
of degree
d
together with the zero polynomial.
Consider the evaluation map

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ev}:{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}]_{d}\to{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{{p_{m}}}\quad{\rm defined\;by}\quad F\mapsto c_{F}:=(F({\mathsf{P}}_{1}),\ldots,F({\mathsf{P}}_{p_{m}})).

(4)

Note that
\operatorname{Ev}
is a linear map and its image is a nondegenerate linear code of length
p_{m}
. This code is said to be
the
projective Reed-Muller code
of order
d
and length
p_{m}
, and it is denoted by
\mathsf{PRM}_{q}(d,m)
.

A convenient way to study the projective Reed-Muller code is to use the notion of projective reduction, which was introduced in
[
4
]
and employed in
[
11
]
to determine the vanishing ideal of
{\mathbb{P}}^{m}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
. Let us recall that the projective reduction
\overline{\mu}
of a monomial
\mu\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}]

of positive degree is defined as follows. Write

\mu=X_{0}^{a_{0}}\cdots X_{\ell}^{a_{\ell}}
for unique nonnegative integers
a_{0},\dots,a_{\ell}
with
a_{\ell}>0
so that
X_{\ell}
is the last variable in
\mu
.
For
0\leq i\leq\ell-1
, let
\overline{a}_{i}=a_{i}
if
0\leq a_{i}\leq q-1
, whereas if
a_{i}\geq q
, then let

\overline{a}_{i}
be the unique integer such that

\overline{a}_{i}\equiv a_{i}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q-1)
and

1\leq\overline{a}_{i}\leq q-1
. We then define

\overline{\mu}:=X_{0}^{\overline{a}_{0}}\cdots X_{\ell-1}^{\overline{a}_{\ell-1}}X_{\ell}^{a_{\ell}+\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}(a_{i}-\overline{a}_{i})}.

The monomial
\overline{\mu}
is uniquely determined by
\mu
and it has the same degree as
\mu
. In case
\mu=1
, we set
\overline{\mu}:=1
.
Observe that
\overline{\mu}({\mathsf{P}})=\mu({\mathsf{P}})
for all
{\mathsf{P}}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{m+1}
.
We say that a monomial
\mu
is
projectively reduced
if
\overline{\mu}=\mu
.
These notions extend by
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-linearity to arbitrary polynomials in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}]
.
Now let

C_{q}(d,m):=\{f\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}]_{d}:f\text{ is projectively reduced}\}.

Note that
C_{q}(d,m)
is a
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-linear subspace of the polynomial ring
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

The restriction
{\rm Ev}|_{C_{q}(d,m)}
of the evaluation map defined in (
4
), to
C_{q}(d,m)
, is injective. Moreover,
\mathsf{PRM}_{q}(d,m)={\rm Ev}(C_{q}(d,m))
.

Proof.

The first assertion follows from Lemma 3.1 of
[
11
]
, and the second assertion follows from Lemma 3.2 and Theorem 3.3 of
[
11
]
.
∎

Corollary 2.2
.

If
F
is a nonzero polynomial in
C_{q}(d,m)
, then
|V(F)|\leq p_{m}-1
.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of Lemma
2.1
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

If
d\geq m(q-1)+1
, then
\mathsf{PRM}_{q}(d,m)=\mathsf{PRM}_{q}(m(q-1)+1,m)={\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{{p_{m}}}
.

Proof.

Let
\nu\in\{1,\dots,p_{m}\}
. Write
{\mathsf{P}}_{\nu}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{{{m+1}}}
as

{\mathsf{P}}_{\nu}=(a_{0},\dots,a_{j_{\nu}-1},1,0,\ldots,0)
for unique

j_{\nu}\in\{0,1,\dots,m\}
and
a_{0},\ldots,a_{j_{\nu}-1}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
.
Now assume that
d\geq m(q-1)+1
.
Define the polynomial
F_{\nu}=F_{\nu}(X_{0},\dots,X_{m})
by

\displaystyle F_{\nu}:=X^{d-m(q-1)}_{j_{\nu}}\prod\limits_{i=0}^{j_{\nu}-1}\left(X_{j_{\nu}}^{q-1}-(X_{i}-a_{i}X_{j_{\nu}})^{q-1}\right)\prod\limits_{k=j_{\nu}+1}^{m}(X_{j_{\nu}}^{q-1}-X_{k}^{q-1}).

(5)

Clearly,
F_{\nu}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\dots,X_{m}]_{d}
.
Observe that

F_{\nu}({\mathsf{P}}_{\nu})=1
and
F_{\nu}({\mathsf{P}}_{\mu})=0
for any
\mu\in\{1,\dots,p_{m}\}
with
\mu\neq\nu
. Hence
any
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{p_{m}})\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{p_{m}}
can be written as
\lambda=\operatorname{Ev}(F)
, where
F=\lambda_{1}F_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{p_{m}}F_{p_{m}}
. It follows that
\mathsf{PRM}_{q}(d,m)={\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{{p_{m}}}
.
∎

We remark that the polynomial (
5
) was used by Sørensen in
[
27
, Lem. 3]
.

3.
Minimum Distance of Projective Reed-Muller Codes

In view of the results of the previous section, every codeword of
\mathsf{PRM}_{q}(d,m)
is of the form
\text{Ev}(F)
for a unique
F\in{C_{q}(d,m)}
, and determining the Hamming weight of this codeword corresponds to finding the number of zeros in
{\mathbb{P}}^{m}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
of
F
.

For any
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}]_{\leq d}
, we denote by
Z(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r})
the set of common zeros of
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
in
\mathbb{A}^{m}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
, whereas for
F_{1},\ldots,F_{r}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}]_{d}
, we denote by
V(F_{1},\ldots,F_{r})
the set of common zeros of
F_{1},\ldots,F_{r}
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{m}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
. By a
hyperplane
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{m}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
we shall mean a subset of the form
V(L)
, where
L
is a nonzero homogeneous linear polynomial in

{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}]
. We shall denote by
\widehat{{\mathbb{P}}^{m}}
the set of all hyperplanes in
{\mathbb{P}}^{m}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
. Note that the cardinality of
\widehat{{\mathbb{P}}^{m}}
is
p_{m}
. Also note that if
P,P^{\prime}
are distinct points of
{\mathbb{P}}^{m}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
, then
|\{H\in\widehat{{\mathbb{P}}^{m}}:P\in H\}|=p_{m-1}

and
|\{H\in\widehat{{\mathbb{P}}^{m}}:P,P^{\prime}\in H\}|=p_{m-2}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
F
be a nonzero projectively reduced polynomial in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}]_{d}
.
Suppose
L
is a nonzero homogeneous linear polynomial in

{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}]
such that the hyperplane
V(L)
is contained in
V(F)
. Then
L
divides
F
.

Proof.

By a linear change of coordinates, we may assume that
L=X_{m}
. Separating the monomials in
F
that are divisible by
X_{m}
, we can write

F=X_{m}G+R
for some homogeneous and projectively reduced polynomials
G\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\dots,X_{m}]_{d-1}
and
R\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\dots,X_{m-1}]_{d}
. Since
V(X_{m})\subseteq V(F)
, we see that
R(\mathsf{P})=0
for all
\mathsf{P}\in{\mathbb{P}}^{m-1}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
.
But
R
is projectively reduced, and so it follows from Lemma
2.1
that
R
is the zero polynomial. Thus,
X_{m}
divides
F
.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
F
be a nonzero polynomial in
C_{q}(d,m)
. Then

|V(F)|\leq p_{m}-\lceil{(q-s)q^{m-t-1}\rceil},

(6)

where
t,s
are unique integers such that
d-1=t(q-1)+s
with
t\geq 0
and
0\leq s<q-1
. Moreover, if
1\leq d\leq m(q-1)
and if the equality holds in (
6
), then
V(F)
is a union of at most
d
distinct hyperplanes in
{\mathbb{P}}^{m}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
.

Proof.

In case
d\geq m(q-1)+1
, or equivalently,
t>m-1
, then
\lceil{(q-s)q^{m-t-1}\rceil}=1
, and so (
6
) is a consequence of Corollary
2.2
.

Now assume that
1\leq d\leq m(q-1)
. Then
t\leq m-1
and therefore

p_{m}-\lceil{(q-s)q^{m-t-1}\rceil}\geq p_{m}-q^{m}>0.

In particular, (
6
) holds trivially if
V(F)
is empty. Thus we shall assume that
V(F)
is nonempty.

We will use induction on
m
.
First, suppose
m=1
. Then,
d\leq q-1
, and hence
t=0
. Consequently,

p_{m}-\lceil{(q-s)q^{m-t-1}\rceil}=p_{1}-(q-s)q^{1-0-1}=s+1=d
.
Thus (
6
) is a consequence of the elementary fact that a nonzero homogeneous polynomial of degree
d
in two variables over a field
F
has at most
d
non-proportional zeros in
F^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
. Moreover since hyperplanes in
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
are given by points, we also see that if
m=1
and if the equality holds in (
6
), then
V(F)
is a union of exactly
d
distinct hyperplanes in
{\mathbb{P}}^{m}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
.

Next, suppose
m>1
and the result holds for smaller values of
m
.
Define

\displaystyle\Gamma:=\{H\in\widehat{{\mathbb{P}}^{m}}:H\subseteq V(F)\}.

In view of Lemma
3.1
, we see that
\Gamma
can contain at most
d
distinct hyperplanes in
{\mathbb{P}}^{m}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
. Write

\Gamma=\{H_{1},\ldots,H_{\ell}\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{L}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}H_{i}

for some nonnegative integer
\ell\leq d
.
We shall now divide the proof in two mutually exclusive and exhaustive cases.

Case 1.

\mathcal{L}=V(F)
.

In this case, we must have
\ell\geq 1
since
V(F)
is nonempty. By a linear change of
coordinates, we may assume that
H_{1}=V(X_{m})
.
In view of Lemma
3.1
, we see that
F(X_{0},\ldots,X_{m-1},0)=0
and
f(X_{0},\ldots,X_{m-1}):=F(X_{0},\ldots,X_{m-1},1)
is a nonzero reduced polynomial in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m-1}]_{\leq d-1}
. Thus

\displaystyle|V(F)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|V(F(X_{0},\ldots,X_{m-1},0))|+|Z(f)|

(7)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle p_{m-1}+q^{m}-\lceil{(q-s)q^{m-t-1}\rceil}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{m}-\lceil{(q-s)q^{m-t-1}\rceil},

where the second step uses the formula (recalled in §
2.1
) for the minimum distance of Reed-Muller code

\mathrm{RM}_{q}(d-1,m)
and the fact that

\mathrm{ev}(f)
is a nonzero codeword of this code since
f
is nonzero reduced polynomial. Thus (
6
) is proved in Case 1.

Case 2.

\mathcal{L}\neq V(F)
.

In this case, there exists some
P\in V(F)\setminus\mathcal{L}
. In particular, a hyperplane
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{m}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
passing through
P
can not be contained in
V(F)
. For sections of

V(F)
by such hyperplanes, we make the following

Claim:
If
H
is any hyperplane
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{m}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
such that
H\not\subseteq V(F)
, then

|V(F)\cap H|\leq p_{m-1}-\lceil{(q-s)q^{m-t-2}\rceil}.

To prove this, fix a hyperplane
H

in
{\mathbb{P}}^{m}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
such that
H\not\subseteq V(F)
. By a linear change of coordinates, we
may assume that
H=V(X_{m})
. As in the proof of Lemma
3.1
, we can write

F=X_{m}G+R
for some homogeneous and projectively reduced polynomials
G\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\dots,X_{m}]_{d-1}
and
R\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\dots,X_{m-1}]_{d}
. Since
V(X_{m})\not\subseteq V(F)
, we see that
R
is a nonzero polynomial in
C_{q}(d,m-1)
. Also,

V(F)\cap H=V(R)\cap H
is the zero set of
R
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{m-1}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
. Hence by the induction hypothesis,
|V(F)\cap H|\leq p_{m-1}-\lceil{(q-s)q^{m-t-2}\rceil}
.
This proves the chaim.

Recall that we had chosen
P\in V(F)\setminus\mathcal{L}
.
Following Serre
[
26
]
, we consider the incidence set

\mathcal{I}:=\{(P^{\prime},H):P^{\prime}\in V(F)\setminus\{P\}\text{ and }H\in\widehat{{\mathbb{P}}^{m}}\text{ with }P,P^{\prime}\in H\}.

This set can be counted in two different ways as follows. On the one hand,

|\mathcal{I}|\;=\sum_{P^{\prime}\in V(F)\setminus\{P\}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}H\in\widehat{{\mathbb{P}}^{m}}\\
P,P^{\prime}\in H\end{subarray}}1\;=\sum_{P^{\prime}\in V(F)\setminus\{P\}}p_{m-2}\;=\;p_{m-2}(|V(F)|-1).

On the other hand,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{I}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}H\in\widehat{{\mathbb{P}}^{m}}\\
P\in H\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P^{\prime}\in V(F)\setminus\{P\}\\
P^{\prime}\in H\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}H\in\widehat{{\mathbb{P}}^{m}}\\
P\in H\end{subarray}}\left(|V(F)\cap H|-1\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}H\in\widehat{{\mathbb{P}}^{m}}\\
P\in H\end{subarray}}\left(p_{m-1}-\lceil{(q-s)q^{m-t-2}\rceil}-1\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{m-1}\left(p_{m-1}-\lceil{(q-s)q^{m-t-2}\rceil}-1\right)

where the inequality in Step 3 follows from the claim above.
On comparing both these values of
|\mathcal{I}|
, we obtain

\displaystyle|V(F)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p_{m-1}(p_{m-1}-\lceil{(q-s)q^{m-t-2}\rceil}-1)}{p_{m-2}}+1

\displaystyle=\frac{(p_{m-1})^{2}-p_{m-1}+p_{m-2}}{p_{m-2}}-\frac{p_{m-1}\lceil{(q-s)q^{m-t-2}\rceil}}{p_{m-2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(p_{m-1})^{2}-q^{m-1}}{p_{m-2}}-\frac{p_{m-1}}{p_{m-2}}\lceil{(q-s)q^{m-t-2}\rceil}

\displaystyle=\frac{(q^{m}-1)^{2}-q^{m-1}(q-1)^{2}}{(q^{m-1}-1)(q-1)}-\left(\frac{q^{m}-1}{q^{m-1}-1}\right)\lceil{(q-s)q^{m-t-2}\rceil}

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{m+1}-1}{q-1}-\left(\frac{q^{m}-1}{q^{m-1}-1}\right)\lceil{(q-s)q^{m-t-2}\rceil}

\displaystyle<p_{m}-q\lceil{(q-s)q^{m-t-2}\rceil},

where the last inequality follows since
q^{m}-1>q^{m}-q
. Thus,

\displaystyle|V(F)|

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\begin{cases}p_{m}-(q-s)q^{m-t-1}&\text{ if }t<m-1,\\
p_{m}-q&\text{ if }t=m-1\end{cases}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\begin{cases}p_{m}-(q-s)q^{m-t-1}&\text{ if }t<m-1,\\
p_{m}-(q-s)&\text{ if }t=m-1\end{cases}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{m}-\lceil{(q-s)q^{m-t-1}\rceil}.

This yields (
6
). Moreover, since the ineequality in (
6
) is strict in Case 2, the second assertion is proved as well. ∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Assume that
1\leq d\leq m(q-1)+1
. Then the minimum distance of projective Reed-Muller code

is

\displaystyle{(q-s)q^{m-t-1}},

where
t,s
are unique integers such that

and

{\mathbb{F}}_{q}

.

Proof.

Since

, we see that

, then

. Thus,

\lceil{(q-s)q^{m-t-1}\rceil}={(q-s)q^{m-t-1}}

(9)

Next, suppose

are distinct elements of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
and
G

). Clearly,

.
∎

4.
Minimum Weight Codewords of Projective Reed-Muller Codes

In this section, we characterize the minimum weight codewords of projective Reed-Muller codes in terms of homogeneous polynomials whose images under the evaluation map (
4
) are these codewords. We begin with a simple observation.

Lemma 4.1
.

Given any
A,B\in{\mathbb{P}}^{m}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
with
A\neq B
, there is a homogeneous linear polynomial

L\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}]
such that
L(A)=0
and
L(B)\neq 0
.

Proof.

Let
A=(a_{0}:a_{1}:\cdots:a_{m})
and
B=(b_{0}:b_{1}:\cdots:b_{m})
be distinct elements of

{\mathbb{P}}^{m}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
. Since
A\neq B
, there are integers
i,j
with
0\leq i<j\leq m
such that
a_{i}b_{j}-a_{j}b_{i}\neq 0
. Now
L=a_{i}X_{j}-a_{j}X_{i}
has the desired properties.
∎

Let us recall that the codewords of
\mathsf{PRM}_{q}(d,m)
are of the form
c_{F}
, defined as in
(
4
), where
F
varies over the elements of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}]_{d}
. The
support
of such a codeword is given by the set

\mathrm{Supp}\left(c_{F}\right)=\left\{\mathsf{P}\in\{\mathsf{P}_{1},,\ldots,\mathsf{P}_{p_{m}}\}:F(\mathsf{P})\neq 0\right\},

where
\mathsf{P}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{P}_{p_{m}}
are as in §
2.2
.
For any
F\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}]_{d}
, we can
clearly identify
\mathrm{Supp}\left(c_{F}\right)
with
{\mathbb{P}}^{m}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})\setminus V(F)
. In case
c_{F}
is a minimum weight codeword, i.e., a nonzero codeword of least Hamming weight,
we can say a little more.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
F\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}]_{d}
be such that
c_{F}
is a minimum weight codeword of
\mathsf{PRM}_{q}(d,m)
. Then for any
G\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}]_{d}
,

V(F)=V(G)\Longleftrightarrow\mathrm{Supp}\left(c_{F}\right)=\mathrm{Supp}\left(c_{G}\right)\Longleftrightarrow c_{F}=c_{\lambda G}\text{ for some nonzero }\lambda\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}.

Proof.

The first equivalence as well as the reverse implication in the second equivalence is obvious. Suppose
\mathrm{Supp}\left(c_{F}\right)=\mathrm{Supp}\left(c_{G}\right)
. Pick any
{\mathsf{P}}_{\nu}\in\mathrm{Supp}\left(c_{F}\right)
. Then

F({\mathsf{P}}_{\nu})\neq 0\neq G({\mathsf{P}}_{\nu})
, and so
there is
\lambda\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
with
\lambda\neq 0
such that

F({\mathsf{P}}_{\nu})=\lambda\,G({\mathsf{P}}_{\nu})
. Consequently,

\mathrm{Supp}(c_{F-\lambda G})\subseteq\mathrm{Supp}(c_{F})\setminus\{{\mathsf{P}}_{\nu}\}
.
Since
c_{F}
is a minimum weight codeword, it follows that
c_{F-\lambda G}=0
, that is,
c_{F}=c_{\lambda G}
.
∎

We are now ready to prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume that
1\leq d\leq m(q-1)+1
and let
\,t,s
be unique integers such that

d-1=t(q-1)+s
with
t\geq 0
and
0\leq s<q-1
.Then
c\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{{p_{m}}}
is a minimum weight codeword of
\mathsf{PRM}_{q}(d,m)
if and only if
c=c_{Q}
for some

Q\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}]_{d}
of the form

\displaystyle Q=L_{t}\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}\left(L_{t}^{q-1}-L_{i}^{q-1}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{s}(L_{t+1}-\omega_{j}L_{t})

(10)

where
L_{0},\ldots,L_{t+1}
are linearly independent homogeneous linear polynomials in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}]
and
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{s}
are distinct elements of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. [Here it is understood that if
s=0
, then the second product in (
10
) is empty (and hence equal to
1
) and in this case only the existence and linear independence of
L_{0},\ldots,L_{t}
is asserted.]

Proof.

If
Q
is as in (
10
), then it is clear that

Q\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}]_{d}
and moreover, by a linear change of coordinates,
we see that
|V(Q)|=|V(G)|
, where
G
is as in (
8
). Thus Corollary
3.3
implies that
c_{Q}
is a minimum weight codeword of
\mathsf{PRM}_{q}(d,m)
.

To prove the converse, let
F\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}]_{d}
be a nonzero polynomial such that
|V(F)|=p_{m}-(q-s)q^{m-t-1}
. We need to show that there exists some
Q\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}]_{d}
of the form
(
10
) such that
c_{F}=c_{Q}
.
To this end, let us first consider the extreme case when
d=m(q-1)+1
so that
t=m
and
s=0
.
In this case
|V(F)|=p_{m}-1
and so there is unique
\nu\in\{1,\dots,p_{m}\}
such that

F({\mathsf{P}}_{\nu})\neq 0
and
F(\mathsf{P}_{\mu})=0
for all
\mu\in\{1,\dots,p_{m}\}
with
\mu\neq\nu
. Thus if
F_{\nu}
is as in (
5
), then by Lemma
4.2
,
c_{F}=c_{\omega_{0}F_{\nu}}

for some
\omega_{0}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
with
\omega_{0}\neq 0
. Now, if as in
the proof of Lemma
2.1
, we write
{\mathsf{P}}_{\nu}=(a_{0},\dots,a_{j_{\nu}-1},1,0,\ldots,0)
, and we let

L_{t}=L_{m}=\omega_{0}X_{j_{\nu}}\quad\text{and}\quad L_{i}=\begin{cases}(X_{i}-a_{i}X_{j_{\nu}})&\text{if }0\leq i<j_{\nu},\\
X_{i+1}&\text{if }j_{\nu}\leq i\leq m-1,\end{cases}

then
L_{0},\dots,L_{t}
are linearly independent and it is clear from (
5
) that
F
is as in (
10
).

Thus, we will now assume that
1\leq d\leq m(q-1)
. Then
0\leq t\leq m-1
and
0\leq s\leq q-2
.
This implies that

p_{m}-|V(F)|=(q-s)q^{m-t-1}\geq 2.

(11)

Further, since
|V(F)|
attains the bound in (
6
), we see from Theorem
3.2
that there is a positive integer
r\leq d
and homogeneous linear polynomials

G_{1},\dots,G_{r}
in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}]
such that the corresponding hyperplanes

are distinct (i.e.,
G_{1},\dots,G_{r}
are pairwise linearly independent) and

V(F)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}V(G_{i}).

We claim that
r=d
. To see this, assume the contrary, i.e., suppose
1\leq r<d
.
In view of (
11
), there are
A,B\in{\mathbb{P}}^{m}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})\setminus V(F)
such that
A\neq B
. Hence by Lemma
4.1
, there is a homogeneous linear polynomial
L
in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\dots,X_{m}]
such that
L(A)=0
and
L(B)\neq 0
. Now consider

. Then

G\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}]_{d}
and
G(P)=0
for all

. Also
c_{G}

is a nonzero codeword of
\mathsf{PRM}_{q}(d,m)
since
G(B)\neq 0
. But the Hamming weight of
c_{G}
is smaller than that of
c_{F}
since
|V(G)|>|V(F)|
. This contradicts the assumption that
c_{F}
is a minimum weight codeword. Hence the claim that

is proved.

Now let
G:=G_{1}\cdots G_{d}
. Then
V(F)=V(G)
. Hence by Lemma
4.2
,
there is
\lambda\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
with
\lambda\neq 0
such that

c_{F}=c_{\lambda G}
. We can thus assume without loss of generality that
F
is a product of
d
pairwise linearly independent homogeneous linear factors, and moreover by a linear change of coordinates, we may assume that one of the linear factors is

. Consequently, we can write

for some

such that
F_{1}
is not divisible by

.
Thus, if we let
f_{1}(X_{0},\dots,X_{m-1})=F_{1}(X_{0},\dots,X_{m-1},1)
, then

is a polynomial in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m-1}]
of degree
{d-1}
. Moreover, by intersecting

with the hyperplane

and its complement in

, we see that

p_{m}-(q-s)q^{m-t-1}=|V(F)|=p_{m-1}+|Z(f_{1})|\ \text{ and so }\ |Z(f_{1})|=q^{m}-(q-s)q^{m-t-1}.

It follows that
\mathrm{ev}(f_{1})
can be viewed as a minimum weight codeword of the Reed-Muller code

. Hence by

\mathrm{ev}(f_{1})=\mathrm{ev}(f)
for some

for some linearly independent polynomials

of degree

, and some

and

then we see that
Q\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}]_{d}
and
F({\mathsf{P}}_{\nu})=Q({\mathsf{P}}_{\nu})
for each
\nu=1,\dots,p_{m}
, and hence
c_{F}=c_{Q}
. Moreover if
we let

L_{i}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}):=X_{m}\left(\ell_{i+1}\left(\frac{X_{0}}{X_{m}},\ldots,\frac{X_{m-1}}{X_{m}}\right)\right)\quad\text{for $i=0,1,\dots,t-1$,}

and also let
L_{t}:=\omega_{0}X_{m}
and in case
s>0
, we further let

L_{t+1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}):=X_{m}\left(\ell_{t+1}\left(\frac{X_{0}}{X_{m}},\ldots,\frac{X_{m-1}}{X_{m}}\right)\right),

then we see that
L_{1},\dots,L_{t+1}
are nonzero homogeneous linear polynomials in

{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}]
and moreover

Q=L_{t}\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}\left(L_{t}^{q-1}-L_{i}^{q-1}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{s}(L_{t+1}-\omega_{j}L_{t})\quad\text{where}\quad\omega_{j}:=\frac{\omega^{\prime}_{j}}{\omega_{0}}\text{ for }j=1,\dots,s.

It remains to show that
L_{0},\dots,L_{t+1}
are linearly independent, or more precisely,
if
s=0
, then
L_{0},\dots,L_{t}
are linearly independent,
whereas if
s>0
, then
L_{0},\dots,L_{t+1}
are linearly independent.
To this end, first suppose

s=0
. Since

are linearly independent,

L_{0},\ldots,L_{t-1}
are linearly independent. Now if
L_{0},\dots,L_{t}
were linearly dependent, then
X_{m}
would be a
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-linear combination of
L_{0},\dots,L_{t-1}
.
On the other hand, since

, there is some

such that

. In particular,

, but

for

X_{m}
cannot be a
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-linear combination of
L_{0},\dots,L_{t-1}
.

Next, suppose
s>0
. As before, the linear independence of
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{t+1}
shows that

are linearly independent. Fix an arbitrary element
A
of

and

is at most

|\{\mathbf{a}=(a_{0},\dots,a_{m-1})\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{m}:\ell_{i}(\mathbf{a})=0\text{ for }i=1,\dots,t\text{ and }\ell_{t+1}(\mathbf{a})=\lambda\}|=q^{m-t-1},

The following lemma will prove Theorem

Lemma 5.3

-b_{t}/b_{t+1}\not\in S
. On the other hand, our choice of
P
implies that

L^{\prime}_{t}(P)=b_{t}L_{t}(P)+b_{t+1}L_{t+1}(P)=0

and therefore
P\not\in\Psi(E^{\prime},L^{\prime}_{t},L^{\prime}_{t+1},S^{\prime})=\sigma
, which is a contradiction.
Thus the claim is proved. We shall now show that for any fixed choice of
b_{0},\dots,b_{t+1}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
with
b_{t+1}\neq 0
and
-b_{t}/b_{t+1}\in S
, if we take
L^{\prime}_{t}=b_{0}L_{0}+\dots+b_{t+1}L_{t+1}
, and
L^{*}_{t+1}:=L_{t}
,
then there is a unique
S^{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{s}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
such that
(E,L^{\prime}_{t},L^{*}_{t+1},S^{*})\in\mathcal{J}
and

\Psi(E,L^{\prime}_{t},L^{*}_{t+1},S^{*})=\sigma
. To see this, first note that
\{L_{0},\dots,L_{t-1},L^{\prime}_{t},L^{*}_{t+1}\}
is linearly independent.
Further, if we take

S^{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{s}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
such that

{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\setminus S^{*}=\left\{\frac{1}{b_{t}+\mu b_{t+1}}:\mu\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\setminus S\right\}.

then
S^{*}
is well-defined since
-b_{t}/b_{t+1}\in S
. Moreover, if for any
\mu^{*}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\setminus S^{*}
,
we write
\mu^{*}=1/(b_{t}+\mu b_{t+1})
for some
\mu\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\setminus S
, then on points of
E
, we have

L^{*}_{t+1}-\mu^{*}L^{\prime}_{t}=L_{t}-\frac{1}{b_{t}+\mu b_{t+1}}(b_{t}L_{t}+b_{t+1}L_{t+1})=\frac{-b_{t+1}}{b_{t}+\mu b_{t+1}}\left(L_{t+1}-\mu L_{t}\right).

Consequently,

(E\setminus V(L^{\prime}_{t}))\cap\Big(\!\bigcup_{\mu^{*}\in{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\setminus S^{*}}}\!V(L^{*}_{t+1}-\mu^{*}L^{\prime}_{t})\Big)=(E\setminus V(L_{t}))\cap\Big(\!\bigcup_{\mu\in{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\setminus S}}\!V(L_{t+1}-\mu L_{t})\Big)=\sigma.

This shows the existence of a polynomial
L^{*}_{t+1}\in R_{1}
and a set
S^{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{s}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
such that
(E,L^{\prime}_{t},L^{*}_{t+1},S^{*})\in\mathcal{J}
and

\Psi(E,L^{\prime}_{t},L^{*}_{t+1},S^{*})=\sigma
. The uniqueness of
S^{*}
for the given choice of
L^{*}_{t+1}
is clear from
Lemma
5.5
. Moreover, by Lemma
5.6
, there are exactly
q^{t+1}(q-1)
choices of
L^{\prime}_{t+1}
and
corresponding unique choice of
S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{s}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
for which
\Psi(E^{\prime},L^{\prime}_{t},L^{*}_{t+1},S^{*})=\Psi(E^{\prime},L^{\prime}_{t},L^{\prime}_{t+1},S^{\prime})
.
Since
b_{0},\dots,b_{t+1}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
such that
b_{t+1}\neq 0
and
-b_{t}/b_{t+1}\in S
can be chosen in
s(q-1)q^{t}
ways, we see that there are exactly
q^{t+1}(q-1)\times s(q-1)q^{t}=sq^{2t+1}(q-1)^{2}
elements
(E^{\prime},L^{\prime}_{t},L^{\prime}_{t+1},S^{\prime})
in
\Psi^{-1}(\sigma)
for which
L^{\prime}_{t}
is as in Case 2.

By combining the counts in Case 1 and Case 2, we obtain the desired result.
∎

We are now ready to prove the main result of this section.

Proof of Theorem

.
If

It follows that

\displaystyle|\Phi(\mathcal{M})|

Remark 5.8

The formula (

6.
Dimension Formulas for Projective Reed-Muller Codes

Fix throughout this section
m,d\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
m\geq 1
and
1\leq d\leq m(q-1)+1
.
As before, let
\,t,s
be unique integers such that

d-1=t(q-1)+s
with
t\geq 0
and
0\leq s<q-1
.
An explicit formula for the dimension of a PRM code of an arbitrary order
d
was first given by Sørensen
[
27
]
. It is as follows.

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\alpha_{q}(d,m):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e=1\\
e\equiv d\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q-1)\end{subarray}}^{d}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}(-1)^{j}\binom{m+1}{j}\binom{e-jq+m}{e-jq}\right).}

(18)

Later Mercier-Rolland
[
20
]
gave another formula for the dimension of PRM codes of order
d
using the dimension of vanishing ideal of
{\mathbb{P}}^{m}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
. This one is as follows.

\displaystyle\beta_{q}(d,m):=\binom{m+d}{d}-\sum_{j=2}^{m+1}(-1)^{j}{{m+1}\choose{j}}\sum_{i=0}^{j-2}{{d+(i+1)(q-1)-jq+m}\choose{d+(i+1)(q-1)-jq}}.

(19)

Around the same time
4
4

4

A comment on the dates of publication of
[
20
]
and
[
24
]
can be found in
[
11
, Remark 3.4]
.
, Rentería and Tapia-Recillas
[
24
, Prop. 12]

gave the following nice formula for the dimension of
\mathsf{PRM}_{q}(d,m)
:

\gamma_{q}(d,m):=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\sum_{j=0}^{i}(-1)^{j}\binom{i}{j}\binom{i+d-1-jq}{i}.

(20)

More recently, Can, Joshua and Ravindra
[
8
, Prop. 3.5]
have proposed another formula for the dimension of PRM codes of order
d
, which is as follows.

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\delta_{q}(d,m):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e=1\\
e\equiv d\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q-1)\end{subarray}}^{d}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor e/q\rfloor}(-1)^{j}\binom{m+1}{j}\binom{e-jq+m}{m}\right).}

(21)

Can, Joshua and Ravindra
[
8
]
further alleged that the formula of
Sørensen
[
27
]
is wrong and wrote that the expression on the right in (
18
) is equal to
0
. This is, in fact, not correct. To clear the confusion, let us first note that the binomial coefficients are (and always should be!) defined as in the beginning of Section
2
. In particular,
{a\choose b}
is zero when
b<0
. In fact, if
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
, then

{a\choose b}=0\Longleftrightarrow\text{either }b<0\text{ or }b>a\geq 0.

(22)

Furthermore, the identity
{a\choose b}={a\choose{a-b}}
is not absolute, but conditional. In the first place it requires that
a\in\mathbb{Z}
and in fact, for any
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}

{a\choose b}={a\choose{a-b}}\Longleftrightarrow\text{either }a\geq 0\text{ or }a<b<0.

(23)

We remark in passing that on the other hand, the Pascal triangle identity, viz.,

{a\choose{b-1}}+{a\choose{b}}={{a+1}\choose{b}}

(24)

is an absolute identity in the sense that it is valid for all
b\in\mathbb{Z}
and all
a
in any integral domain containing
\mathbb{Z}
as a subring. At any rate, in view of (
22
), the summation over
j
in (
18
)
is, in effect, over
0\leq j\leq\min\{m+1,\lfloor e/q\rfloor\}=\lfloor e/q\rfloor
for
any positive integer
e\leq d
, where the last equality follows since

e\leq d\leq m(q-1)+1\leq mq
. Moreover, for
0\leq j\leq\lfloor e/q\rfloor
, by (
23
), we obtain

\binom{e-jq+m}{e-jq}=\binom{e-jq+m}{m}
. Thus it is clear that
\alpha_{q}(d,m)=\delta_{q}(d,m)
. In other words, the formulas of Sørensen and of Can, Joshua and Ravindra are, in fact, the same! The argument in Section 3 of
[
8
]
to conclude that
\alpha_{q}(d,m)=0
uses their Lemma 3.3, which is perfectly fine, but then it uses the “identity”
\binom{e-jq+m}{e-jq}=\binom{e-jq+m}{m}
for arbitrary nonnegative integer
j
, which as we have noted in (
23
), is not always true. [There is also a typo in the definition given in
[
8
, p. 6]
of the polynomial
P(x)
; namely,
t
should be replaced throughout by
t+m
in the expression on the right, but this is minor].

The equivalence of (
18
) and (
19
) is rather nontrivial. As mentioned in
[
20
]
, a direct proof of this was given by Michel Quercia, but to the best of our knowledge, this has not been published. The argument of Michel Quercia was made available to us by Robert Rolland, and we have reproduced it in the appendix to this paper.
We give below an alternative argument to prove the equivalence of these formulas. This alternative argument uses the following simple combinatorial lemma, which has been given, for instance, by Sørensen
[
27
]
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
n,a,b
be any nonnegative integers and let
N(a,n,b)
denote the number of ways in which one can place
a
objects in
n
blocks such that no block contains more than
b
objects. Then

N(a,n,b)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}{{n}\choose{j}}{{a-j(b+1)+n-1}\choose{a-j(b+1)}}.

(25)

Proof.

See
[
27
, Lem. 5]
. Or use an argument as in the proof of
[
12
, Lem. 1]
.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

Given any nonnegative integers
n,a,b
, it is clear from the description of
N(a,n,b)
that it is the number of monomials in
n
variables of degree
a
such that the degree in each variable is at most
b
. In particular, if
0\leq\nu\leq n(q-1)
, then
N(\nu,n,q-1)
is the number of reduced monomials of degree
\nu
in
n
variables, and so we see that

N(\nu,n,q-1)=\rho_{q}(\nu,n)-\rho_{q}(\nu-1,n)
, where
\rho_{q}(\nu,n)
is given by (
1
) if
n\geq 1
and
where by convention,
\rho_{q}(0,0):=1
and
\rho_{q}(k,n):=0
if
k\in\mathbb{Z}
with
k<0
.
This can also be seen directly by comparing (
1
) with the formula in Lemma
6.1
and using the Pascal triangle identity (
24
). Note also that the degree of a reduced monomial in
n
variables is always
\leq n(q-1)
and so

\rho_{q}(\nu,n)=\rho_{q}\left(\min\{\nu,\,n(q-1)\},\;n\right)\quad\text{for any nonnegative integers }\nu,\,n.

(26)

This is clear from the description of
\rho_{q}(\nu,n)
as the number of reduced monomials in
n
variables of degree
\leq\nu
, and also from the formula (
1
) because if
\nu>n(q-1)
, then using (
1
), (
25
), and successive applications of
(
24
), we see that

\rho_{q}(\nu,n)=\rho_{q}(n(q-1),\,n)+\sum_{a=n(q-1)+1}^{\nu}N(a,\,n,\,q-1)\;=\;\rho_{q}(n(q-1),\,n),

where the last equality follows since any distribution of
n(q-1)+1
or more objects in
n
blocks will result in at least one block containing
q
or more objects.

We are now ready to prove that the two dimension formulas
\alpha_{q}(d,m)
and
\beta_{q}(d,m)
given by (
18
) and (
19
) are equal.

Proposition 6.3
.

\alpha_{q}(d,m)=\beta_{q}(d,m)
.

Proof.

First note that we may rewrite (
19
) as

\displaystyle\beta_{q}(d,m)=\binom{m+d}{d}-\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}(-1)^{j}{{m+1}\choose{j}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e=d+(q-1)\\
e\equiv d\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q-1)\end{subarray}}^{d+(j-1)(q-1)}{{e-jq+m}\choose{e-jq}}.

Also note that by separating terms in (
18
) corresponding to
e=d
and
j=0
as well as

e=d
and
j\geq 1
, we can write

\displaystyle\alpha_{q}(d,m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq e<d\\
e\equiv d\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q-1)\end{subarray}}\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}(-1)^{j}{{m+1}\choose{j}}{{e-jq+m}\choose{e-jq}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\binom{m+d}{d}+\;\sum_{j=1}^{m+1}(-1)^{j}{{m+1}\choose{j}}{{d-jq+m}\choose{d-jq}}

Thus
\alpha_{q}(d,m)=\beta_{q}(d,m)
is equivalent to the equality

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}(-1)^{j}{{m+1}\choose{j}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e=1\\
e\equiv d\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q-1)\end{subarray}}^{d+(j-1)(q-1)}{{e-jq+m}\choose{e-jq}}=0,

(27)

Define

\displaystyle c:=\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}(-1)^{j}{{m+1}\choose{j}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\equiv d\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q-1)\\
-\infty<e\leq d+(j-1)(q-1)\end{subarray}}{{e-jq+m}\choose{e-jq}}.

Then the expression on the left in (
27
) is equal to
c-1
if
d
is a multiple of
q-1
, and is equal to
c
otherwise. Thus it is enough to show that
c=1
if
d
is a multiple of
q-1
and
c=0
if
d
is not a multiple of
q-1
. Now

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}{{m+1}\choose{j}}{{d+(j-1)(q-1)-k(q-1)-jq+m}\choose{d+(j-1)(q-1)-k(q-1)-jq}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}(-1)^{j}{{m+1}\choose{j}}{{d+(j-1)(q-1)-k(q-1)-jq+m}\choose{d+(j-1)(q-1)-k(q-1)-jq}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}(-1)^{j}{{m+1}\choose{j}}{{d-(k+1)(q-1)-j+m}\choose{d-(k+1)(q-1)-j}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}N(d-(k+1)(q-1),\;m+1,\;0)

where the last equality uses Lemma
6.1
and the notation therein. But evidently,

\displaystyle N(d-(k+1)(q-1),\;m+1,\;0)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }d=(k+1)(q-1),\\
0&\text{if }d\neq(k+1)(q-1).\end{cases}

This implies that
c=1
if
d
is a multiple of
q-1
, and
0
otherwise. ∎

We will now consider the formula
\gamma_{q}(d,m)
of Rentería and Tapia-Recillas
[
24
, Prop. 12]
. They proved it
using an ideal-theoretic approach and the notion of Hilbert functions.
(See also
[
9
, Cor. 3.9 (b)]
, where Carvalho, Neumann, and Lopez derive the same formula from a more general result on projective nested cartesian codes.)
Moreover,
[
24
, Lem. 9]
implies that
\gamma_{q}(d,m)
can be related to the dimensions of certain Reed-Muller codes. We show below that the notion of projective reduction can also be used to give a short direct proof of the formula of Rentería and Tapia-Recillas for the dimension of
\mathsf{PRM}_{q}(d,m)
.

Recall that we had defined the Reed-Muller code
\mathrm{RM}_{q}(\nu,m)
for any
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}
with
0\leq\nu\leq m(q-1)
. We shall find it convenient to extend this definition a bit so as to set

\mathrm{RM}_{q}(0,0)
to be the
1
-dimensional code of length
1
, namely,
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
.
This is consistent with the formula (
1
)
given earlier, namely,

\rho_{q}(\nu,m):=\dim\mathrm{RM}_{q}(\nu,m)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}(-1)^{j}\binom{m}{j}\binom{m+\nu-jq}{m}

and also with convention
\rho_{q}(0,0):=1
, which was stated earlier.

Theorem 6.4
.

The dimension of the code
\mathsf{PRM}_{q}(d,m)
is given by

\sum_{i=0}^{m}\rho_{q}(d-1,\,i)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\sum_{j=0}^{i}(-1)^{j}\binom{i}{j}\binom{i+d-1-jq}{i}=\gamma_{q}(d,m).

(28)

Proof.

Let
E
be the set of all
(m+1)
-tuples of nonnegative integers whose sum is equal to
d
.
For
\mathbf{a}=(a_{0},\ldots,a_{m})\in E
, let

\ell(\mathbf{a}):=\max\{j\in\{0,1,\dots,m\}:a_{j}>0\}
.
Note that this is well-defined since
d\geq 1
.
Define

B:=\{\mathbf{a}=(a_{0},\ldots,a_{m})\in E:a_{j}<q\text{ for }0\leq j<\ell(\mathbf{a})\}.

It is clear from Lemma
2.1
that
\dim\mathsf{PRM}_{q}(d,m)=|B|
. Now observe that

B=\coprod_{i=0}^{m}B_{i}\quad\text{where for }0\leq i\leq m,\quad B_{i}:=\{\mathbf{a}\in B:\ell(\mathbf{a})=i\}.

For
i\in\{0,1,\dots,m\}
, the map
\mathbf{a}\mapsto(a_{0},\dots,a_{i-1})
is readily seen to be a bijection of
B_{i}
onto the set of exponent vectors of reduced monomials in
i
variables of degree
\leq d-1
; consequently,
|B_{i}|=\rho_{q}(d-1,\,i)
.
This yields the desired formula.
∎

Finally, we give a direct proof of the equivalence of the dimension formulas of Sørensen and
Rentería–Tapia-Recillas.

Proposition 6.5
.

\alpha_{q}(d,m)=\gamma_{q}(d,m)
.

Proof.

First, note that (
18
) can be written in the notation of Lemma
6.1
as

\alpha_{q}(d,m)=\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e=1\\
e\equiv d\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q-1)\end{subarray}}^{d}\!N(e,m+1,q-1).

(29)

Fix any
e\in\{1,\dots,d\}
. Then
N(e,m+1,q-1)
is the number of reduced monomials of degree
e
in
m+1
variables

x_{0},\dots,x_{m}
.
Given such a reduced monomial, if
x_{i}
is the last variable appearing in it, then it must be of the form
\mu\,x_{i}^{j_{i}}
, where

1\leq j_{i}\leq q-1
and
\mu
is a reduced monomial in
x_{0},\dots,x_{i-1}
of degree
e-j_{i}
. Note that the degree
e-j_{i}
of
\mu

satisfies
e-q+1\leq e-j_{i}\leq e-1
. Conversely, any reduced monomial in
x_{0},\dots,x_{i-1}
of degree
a
, where
e-q+1\leq a\leq e-1
,
would give rise (upon multiplication by
x_{i}^{e-a}
) to a unique reduced monomial of degree
e
in
x_{0},\dots,x_{i}
with a positive exponent for
x_{i}
. Thus partitioning reduced monomials of degree
e
in
x_{0},\dots,x_{m}
by the last variable appearing in them, we see that

N(e,m+1,q-1)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\big(\rho_{q}(e-1,\,i)-\rho_{q}(e-q,\,i)\big).

On the other hand, since
d=t(q-1)+s+1
with
0\leq t\leq m
and
0\leq s<q-1
, the parameter
e
in the summation in (
29
) takes values of the form
r(q-1)+s+1
, where
r
ranges from
0
to
t
. Thus,
we see that

\alpha_{q}(d,m)=\sum_{r=0}^{t}N(r(q-1)+s+1,\,m+1,\,q-1)=\sum_{r=0}^{t}\sum_{i=0}^{m}\big(A(r,i)-A(r-1,i)\big),

where
A(k,i):=\rho_{q}(k(q-1)+s,\,i)
for
-1\leq k\leq t
and
0\leq i\leq m
. Now, by interchanging the summations over
i
and
r
, and then observing that the inner sum is telescoping, and moreover,

A(-1,i)=0
, we obtain

\alpha_{q}(d,m)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}A(t,i)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\rho_{q}(d-1,\,i).

This proves that
\alpha_{q}(d,m)=\gamma_{q}(d,m)
.
∎

Appendix: Quercia’s Proof of Equivalence of Dimension Formulas

Here we reproduce Michel Quercia’s original proof of the equivalence of the formulas, due to
Sørensen and Mercier-Rolland, for the dimension of projective Reed-Muller codes. We use notations consistent with this article.

Proposition 6.6

(Quercia)
.

Let
q
be a prime power and let
d,m
be positive integers such that
d\leq m(q-1)+1
.
Also, let

\alpha_{q}(d,m)
and
\beta_{q}(d,m)
be given by (
18
) and (
19
), respectively. Then
\alpha_{q}(d,m)=\beta_{q}(d,m)
.

Proof.

As in the proof of Proposition
6.3
, the assertion

\alpha_{q}(d,m)=\beta_{q}(d,m)
is equivalent to the equality

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}(-1)^{j}{{m+1}\choose{j}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\equiv d\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q-1)\\
0<e\leq d+(j-1)(q-1)\end{subarray}}{{e-jq+m}\choose{e-jq}}=0.

(30)

Define

\displaystyle c:=\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}(-1)^{j}{{m+1}\choose{j}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\equiv d\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q-1)\\
-\infty<e\leq d+(j-1)(q-1)\end{subarray}}{{e-jq+m}\choose{e-jq}}.

(31)

Observe that the expression on the left in (
30
) is equal to
c-1
if
d
is a multiple of
q-1
, and is equal to
c
otherwise. Thus it is enough to show that
c=1
if
d
is a multiple of
q-1
and
c=0
if
d
is not a multiple of
q-1
. We use
a functional approach to prove this.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be the
\mathbb{Z}
-module of the sequences
u=(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
which take integer values and vanish in the vicinity of
