Two Criteria for Quasihomogeneity
Sarasij Maitra, Vivek Mukundan

Abstract
. Let ( R , 𝔪 R , 𝕜 ) (R,\mathfrak{m}_{R},\mathbb{k}) be a one-dimensional complete local reduced 𝕜 \mathbb{k} -algebra over a field of characteristic zero. The ring R R is said to be quasihomogeneous if there exists a surjection Ω R ↠ 𝔪 \Omega_{R}\twoheadrightarrow\mathfrak{m} where Ω R \Omega_{R} denotes the module of differentials. We present two characterizations of quasihomogeniety of R R . The first one on the valuation semigroup of R R and the other on the trace ideal of the module Ω R \Omega_{R} .

Primary: 13A15. Secondary: 13H05

1.
Introduction

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{k})
be a one dimensional complete local domain where
\mathbb{k}
is algebraically closed field of characteristic zero. We can identify
R
with
\mathbb{k}\llbracket{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}}\rrbracket/I
where
I
is called the defining ideal of
R
. Scheja in
[
11
]
defines
R
to be
quasihomogeneous
if there exists a surjection
\Omega_{R}\twoheadrightarrow\mathfrak{m}
where
\Omega_{R}
is the module of differentials. When
I
is generated by polynomials, this definition coincides with the standard definition of quasihomogeneity found in literature: i.e.,
I
is generated by quasihomogeneous polynomials (
[
6
, Satz 2.1]
). A polynomial
f
is said to be quasihomogeneous of degree
d
, if there exists weights
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f(\lambda^{w_{1}}x_{1},\dots,\lambda^{w_{n}}x_{n})=\lambda^{d}f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{k}
. Scheja proved Berger’s conjecture (
[
1
]
) for quasihomogeneous rings in
[
11
]
. A more general version of this result appeared recently in
[
3
, Theorem 4.11]
. Thus identifying when the ring
R
is
quasihomogeneous
is indeed important and it is desirable to get easy methods to recognize the quasihomogeneous property for
R
. One of the first results in this direction is due to Zariski (
[
14
]
) who showed that for irreducible plane algebroid curves,
R
is quasihomogeneous when the module of differentials
\Omega_{R}
has maximal torsion. Saito (in
[
10
]
) showed that if
R
has an isolated hypersurface singularity (
I=(f)
) then
R
is quasihomogeneous when
f
is in the ideal generated by the partial derivatives
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}},\dots,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{n}}
. The most often used characterization of quasihomogeneous rings defined by polynomials, is due to Kunz and Ruppert
[
6
, Satz 3.1]
. They showed that
R
is quasihomogeneous if and only if
R
is isomorphic to a numerical semigroup ring
\mathbb{k}\llbracket{H}\rrbracket
where
H
is a numerical semigroup.

In this article, we present two new characterizations of quasihomogeneity of
R
. For the first characterization we make use of the conductor ideal
\mathfrak{C}_{R}=\overline{R}:_{\operatorname{Quot}(R)}R
where
\operatorname{Quot}(R)
is the quotient field of
R
and
\overline{R}
is the integral closure of
R
in
\operatorname{Quot}(R)
. Since
\overline{R}=\mathbb{k}\llbracket{t}\rrbracket
we can identify
R
with
\mathbb{k}\llbracket{t^{a_{1}},\dots,t^{a_{n}}}\rrbracket
. We can define an order valuation
v(\sum\alpha_{i}t^{i})=\min\{j~|~a_{j}\neq 0\}
. A subsequent valuation can be defined as
o(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}t^{i})=v\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}t^{i}-\alpha_{0}\right)
. We use this valuation as a check for quasihomogeneity.

Theorem A
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{k})
be a non-regular, complete, local one dimensional domain which is a
\mathbb{k}
-algebra with
\mathbb{k}
algebraically closed of characteristic
0
. Let
\overline{R}=\mathbb{k}\llbracket{t}\rrbracket
with the conductor of
R
given by
\mathfrak{C}_{R}=(t^{c_{R}})\overline{R}
. Writing
R=\mathbb{k}\llbracket{\alpha_{1}t^{a_{1}},\ldots,\alpha_{n}t^{a_{n}}}\rrbracket
with
a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots<a_{n}
, set

\displaystyle o(\alpha_{r})=\min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\{o(\alpha_{i})\},\qquad a=\min_{j\neq r}\{a_{j}\}.

Then
R
is quasihomogeneous if
o(\alpha_{r})+a\geqslant c_{R}
.

This provides a quick way to check quasihomogeneity of
R
. Of course, the downside is that we need to have information on the valuation
c_{R}
which, in general can be hard to compute. But this result gives a quick numerical characterization of quasihomogeneity and easily deployable in computational packages such as Macaulay2.

For the second characterization, we use the invariant defined in
[
8
]
. We use the description of the trace ideal,
\text{tr}_{R}(\Omega_{R})=\sum\alpha(\Omega_{R})
where
\alpha\in\Omega_{R}^{*}=\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(\Omega_{R},R)
. Since the valuation function
v(\cdot)
can be extended to all fractional ideal in
\overline{R}
, we get another characterization of the quasihomogeneity using the valuation of the trace ideal
\text{tr}_{R}(\Omega_{R})
. Since
R
is quasihomogeneous, we have
v(\mathfrak{m})\geqslant v(\text{tr}_{R}(\Omega_{R}))
, by definition. In fact since
R
is non-regular, this is an equality. However, the converse is unclear (see
[
8
, Conclusion 3]
). We settle this completely in the following result.

Theorem B
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{k})
be a non-regular complete local one dimensional domain with
\mathbb{k}
algebraically closed of characteristic
0
. Let
\Omega_{R}
be the module of differentials and
\overline{R}=\mathbb{k}\llbracket{t}\rrbracket
with valuation function
v
. Then
R
is quasihomogeneous if and only if
v(\text{tr}_{R}(\Omega_{R}))=v(\mathfrak{m}).

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout this article,
(R,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{k})
will denote a non-regular complete one dimensional local domain which is a
\mathbb{k}
-algebra with
\mathbb{k}
algebraically closed of characteristic
0
. Hence
R={P}/{I}
where
P=\mathbb{k}\llbracket{X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}}\rrbracket,n\geqslant 2
and
I\subseteq\mathfrak{m}_{P}^{2}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})^{2}
, a prime ideal. We denote
x_{i}
to be the image of
X_{i}
.

Notice that in this situation, the integral closure
\overline{R}
of
R
in its fraction field,
Q=\operatorname{Frac}(R)
, is a Discrete Valuation Ring (
DVR
) by
[
12
, Theorem 4.3.4]
. Thus, henceforth we fix a uniformizing parameter
t
and denote
\overline{R}=\mathbb{k}\llbracket{t}\rrbracket
. Using the inclusion
R\subseteq\overline{R}
, we can write every element of
R
as a power series in
t
. Thus,
R=\llbracket{\alpha_{1}t^{a_{1}},\ldots,\alpha_{n}t^{a_{n}}}\rrbracket
(i.e.,
x_{i}=\alpha_{i}t^{a_{i}}
) where each
\alpha_{i}
is a unit in
\mathbb{k}\llbracket{t}\rrbracket
. We also arrange
a_{1}<a_{2}<\dots<a_{n}
.

For each power series
p(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}c_{i}t^{i}
, we define the order valuation
v(p(t)):=\min\{i\mid c_{i}\neq 0\}
. When
p(t)=0
, define
v(0)=\infty
. Thus,
v(x_{i})=a_{i}
for each
i
. We denote
v(R)
to be the valuation semigroup of
R
. Notice that this extends to a discrete valuation on
Q
by setting
v(p/q)=v(p)-v(q)
, where
p,q\in R
,
q\neq 0
.

For each fractional ideal
I
, i.e., a finitely generated
R
-submodule of
Q
, we denote

v(I):=\min\{v(y)\mid y\in I\}.

Also, for each fractional ideal
I
, we denote
I^{-1}:=R:_{Q}I=\{y\in Q\mid yI\subseteq R\}
.

One of the ideals which is important for our purposes is the conductor
\mathfrak{C}_{R}
. It is defined to be
\mathfrak{C}_{R}=R:_{Q}\overline{R}=\{\alpha\in Q\mid\alpha\overline{R}\subseteq R\}
. One can show that it is the largest common ideal of
R
and
\overline{R}
. Since
\displaystyle\overline{R}=k\llbracket{t}\rrbracket
, we have that
\mathfrak{C}_{R}=(t^{i})_{i\geqslant c_{R}}\overline{R}
. Here
c_{R}
is the smallest integer such that
t^{c_{R}-1}\not\in R
, and
t^{c_{R}+i}\in R
for all
i\geqslant 0
. It is clear from this discussion that there cannot be any element
r\in R
, such that
v(r)=c_{R}-1
. Since
\displaystyle\overline{R}
is finitely generated over
R
(
[
12
, Theorem 4.3.4]
),
\mathfrak{C}_{R}\neq 0
, and it is never equal to
R
unless
R
is regular.

2.1.
Universally Finite Module of Differentials

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{k}),P,I
be as above. Let
I=(f_{1},\dots,f_{m})
where
f_{j}\in P=\mathbb{k}\llbracket{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}}\rrbracket,n\geqslant 2
. Then the
universally finite module of differentials over
\mathbb{k}
, denoted by
\Omega_{R}
, has a (minimal) presentation given as follows:

R^{m}\xrightarrow{\left[\frac{\partial f_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}\right]}R^{n}\to\Omega_{R}\to 0

where
\left[\frac{\partial f_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}\right]
is the Jacobian matrix of
I
, with entries in
R
.

For further details regarding the module of differentials, we refer the reader to the excellent book by E. Kunz
[
5
]
.

2.2.
Quasihomogeneous Rings

Definition 2.2
.

(
[
11
, Satz 9.8]
,
[
1
, Definition 3]
)
Let
(R,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{k})
be a complete local one dimensional non-regular domain which is a
\mathbb{k}
-algebra where
\mathbb{k}
is algebraically closed of characteristic
0
. Let
\Omega_{R}
denote the module of differentials as in
Definition

2.1
. Then
R
is called
quasihomogeneous
if there exists an exact sequence

\Omega_{R}\to\mathfrak{m}\to 0.

Notice that the hypotheses on
R
in
Definition

2.2
can be relaxed to quite an extent and the surjection from
\Omega_{R}
to
\mathfrak{m}
is the essential part (for instance, one may only require
R
to be reduced with its
\mathfrak{m}
-adic completion being a domain). However, for the purposes of this article, we stick to the above definition.

Example 2.3
.

Let
R=\mathbb{C}\llbracket{X,Y,Z}\rrbracket/(XZ-Y^{2},X^{3}-Z^{2})\cong\mathbb{C}\llbracket{t^{4},t^{5},t^{6}}\rrbracket
is quasihomogeneous. First notice that
\Omega_{R}=RdX\oplus RdY\oplus RdZ/U
where
U
is the submodule spanned by
zdX-2ydY+xdZ,3x^{2}dX-2zdZ
. The map

dX\to 4x,dY\to 5y,dZ\to 6z

defines an
R
-linear surjection to the maximal ideal
(x,y,z)
of
R
.

Remark 2.4
.

For rings in the above situation, the map
R
to
R
which is given by
x\mapsto\text{deg}(x)x
for any homogeneous element
x
(known as the Euler derivation map) lifts to a surjection from
\Omega_{R}
to
\mathfrak{m}
(known as the Euler homomorphism) (see the discussion following
[
2
, Theorem 2.3]
and also the proof of
[
2
, Theorem 3.7]
).

Rings of the form
\mathbb{k}\llbracket{t^{b_{1}},\dots,t^{b_{n}}}\rrbracket
are all quasihomogeneous. To see this, first fix a presentation
\mathbb{k}\llbracket{t^{b_{1}},\dots,t^{b_{n}}}\rrbracket\cong\mathbb{k}\llbracket{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}}\rrbracket/J
. Now we can define the Euler homomorphism
\Omega_{R}\rightarrow R
as
dX_{i}\rightarrow b_{i}x_{i}
which leads to the surjection
\Omega_{R}\twoheadrightarrow\mathfrak{m}
.

3.
Quasihomogeneity via order valuation on units

In this section, we prove our first main result. we recall the following notion of order valuation on the units which was utilised to solve partial cases of a long standing conjecture, in a recent work, see
[
9
, Notation 4.1, Theorem 4.2]
.

3.1.
Order Valuation of Units

The units in
\overline{R}
are of the form
\alpha=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}c_{i}t^{i}
with
c_{0}\neq 0,c_{i}\in\mathbb{k}
. Let
\alpha_{j}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}c_{ji}t^{i}
be such a unit. Then the order valuation of
\alpha_{j}
is given by

\displaystyle o(\alpha_{j})=v(\alpha_{j}-c_{j0})=\min_{i\geqslant 1}\{i~|~c_{ji}\neq 0\}.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{k})
be a non-regular, complete, local one dimensional domain which is a
\mathbb{k}
-algebra with
\mathbb{k}
algebraically closed of characteristic
0
. Let
\overline{R}=\mathbb{k}\llbracket{t}\rrbracket
with the conductor of
R
given by
\mathfrak{C}_{R}=(t^{c_{R}})\overline{R}
. Writing
R=\mathbb{k}\llbracket{\alpha_{1}t^{a_{1}},\ldots,\alpha_{n}t^{a_{n}}}\rrbracket
with
a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots<a_{n}
, set

\displaystyle o(\alpha_{r})=\min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\{o(\alpha_{i})\},\qquad a=\min_{j\neq r}\{a_{j}\}.

If
o(\alpha_{r})+a\geqslant c_{R}
, then
R
is quasihomogeneous.

Proof.

As before, we write
x_{i}=\alpha_{i}t^{a_{i}}
. Now assume that
r\geqslant 2
. Then
a=a_{1}
. Choose any
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
. Then
o(\alpha_{j})+a_{j}\geqslant o(\alpha_{r})+a\geqslant c_{R}
. Now

x_{j}=\alpha_{j}t^{a_{j}}=\alpha_{j0}t^{a_{j}}+t^{o(\alpha_{j})+a_{j}}h

where
h\in\overline{R}
. Since
o(\alpha_{j})+a_{j}\geqslant c_{R}
,
t^{o(\alpha_{j})+a_{j}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R}\subseteq R
. Thus,
t^{a_{j}}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{j0}}(x_{j}-t^{o(\alpha_{j})+a_{j}}h)\in R
. Thus,
R=\mathbb{k}\llbracket{t^{a_{1}},\ldots,t^{a_{n}}}\rrbracket
and the quasihomogeneity of
R
follows from
Remark

2.4
.

Finally suppose that
r=1
. Then
a=a_{2}
. Thus, we get that
o(\alpha_{j})+a_{j}\geqslant o(\alpha_{1})+a_{2}\geqslant c_{R}
for all
j\geqslant 2
. Also, we may assume that
\alpha_{1}=1+\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\alpha_{1k}t^{k}
where
\alpha_{1k}\in\mathbb{k}
. Now let
\beta=\alpha_{1}^{1/a_{1}}=1+\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\beta_{k}t^{o(\alpha_{1})+k-1},\beta_{k}\in\mathbb{k}
, a unit in
\overline{R}
(such a
\beta
exists due to Hensel’s Lemma; see for example
[
9
, Section 2]
). We change the parameter to

\displaystyle s=\beta t=t+\beta_{1}t^{o(\alpha_{1})+1}+\beta_{2}t^{o(\alpha_{1})+2}+\cdots

We now identify
R
inside
\mathbb{k}\llbracket{s}\rrbracket
. Notice that
s^{c_{R}-1}\not\in R
but
s^{c_{R}+i}\in R
for all
i\geqslant 0
using the relation
s=\beta t
. In other words, the conductor valuation stays the same. We denote the ring
R
as
\mathbb{k}\llbracket{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}}\rrbracket
where each
x_{i}
has a representation both in terms of
s
and
t
, depending on the choice of the parameter. Notice that both
s^{c_{R}+i}
and
t^{c_{R}+i}
can be written purely in terms of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
and consequently
t^{c_{R}+i}
can be written purely in terms of
s
(with valuation equal to
c_{R}+i
).

Our goal is to write
t=\gamma s
for some
\gamma\in\mathbb{k}\llbracket{s}\rrbracket
which establishes that
\mathbb{k}\llbracket{t}\rrbracket=\mathbb{k}\llbracket{s}\rrbracket
. Notice that

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=\beta t=t+\beta_{1}t^{o(\alpha_{1})+1}+\beta_{2}t^{o(\alpha_{1})+2}+\cdots

\displaystyle\beta_{1}s^{o(\alpha_{1})+1}

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}t^{o(\alpha_{1})+1}+\beta_{1}((o(\alpha_{1})+1)\beta_{1})t^{2o(\alpha_{1})+1}+\cdots.

Thus

(3.1)

\displaystyle s-\beta_{1}s^{o(\alpha_{1})+1}

\displaystyle=(t+\beta_{2}t^{o(\alpha_{1})+2}+\cdots)-(\beta_{1}((o(\alpha_{1})+1)\beta_{1})t^{2o(\alpha_{1})+1}+\cdots)

\displaystyle=\beta^{\prime}t\text{ for }\beta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{k}\llbracket{t}\rrbracket.

Notice that
o(\beta^{\prime})
is at least
o(\alpha_{1})+1
or
2o(\alpha_{1})
. In either case, we see that
o(\beta^{\prime})\geqslant o(\alpha_{1})+1
. We keep repeating this process of subtracting terms involving higher powers of
t
in the expression (
3.1
) to arrive at

\displaystyle s-f(s)=t+\sum_{k\geqslant 0}\eta_{k}t^{c_{R}+k}\text{ where }f(s)\in(s)\mathbb{k}\llbracket{s}\rrbracket,\eta_{k}\in\mathbb{k}.

Now as explained in the paragraphs above,
t^{c_{R}+k}=g_{k}(s)\in(s)\mathbb{k}\llbracket{s}\rrbracket
(i.e., they can be written purely in terms of
s
). Thus we arrive at

\displaystyle s-f(s)-\sum_{k\geqslant 0}\eta_{k}g_{k}(s)=t.

Thus
t=\gamma s,\gamma\in\mathbb{k}\llbracket{s}\rrbracket
. Now notice that
o(\gamma)=o(\alpha_{1})=o(\beta)
.

Now
s^{a_{1}}=(\beta t)^{a_{1}}=\alpha_{1}t^{a_{1}}=x_{1}
. Also

\displaystyle x_{i}=\alpha_{i}t^{a_{i}}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{i0}t^{a_{i}}+\alpha_{i1}t^{o(\alpha_{i})+a_{i}}+\alpha_{i2}t^{o(\alpha_{i})+a_{i}+1}+\cdots

\displaystyle=\alpha_{i0}(\gamma s)^{a_{i}}+\alpha_{i1}(\gamma s)^{o(\alpha_{i})+a_{i}}+\alpha_{i2}(\gamma s)^{o(\alpha_{i})+a_{i}+1}+\cdots

\displaystyle=\alpha^{\prime}_{i0}s^{a_{i}}+\alpha^{\prime}_{i1}s^{o(\alpha_{1})+a_{i}}+\cdots

\displaystyle=\alpha_{i}^{\prime}s^{a_{i}}.

Notice that for all
i\geqslant 2
,
o(\alpha_{1})+a_{i}\geqslant o(\alpha_{1})+a_{2}\geqslant c_{R}
. Thus following the same method as in the beginning of this proof, we can rewrite
x_{i}=s^{a_{i}}
. Since this can be performed for all
x_{i}
, we have
R=\mathbb{k}\llbracket{s^{a_{1}},\dots,s^{a_{n}}}\rrbracket
. Thus quasihomogeneity of
R
now follows from
Remark

2.4
.
∎

Example 3.2
.

Let
R=\mathbb{k}\llbracket{t^{4}+t^{5},t^{7},t^{8},t^{9}}\rrbracket
. Then the conductor
\mathfrak{C}_{R}=(t^{7})\overline{R}
. Here
o(\alpha_{1})=1
and
a=7
. Since
o(\alpha_{1})+a\geqslant c=7
,
R
is a quasihomogeneous ring. Here
R=\mathbb{k}\llbracket{s^{4},s^{7},s^{8},s^{9}}\rrbracket
.

Example 3.3
.

Let
R=\mathbb{k}\llbracket{t^{5},t^{6},t^{8}+t^{9}}\rrbracket
. Then
[
13
, Page 207,(1)]
shows that
R
is not quasihomogeneous. Notice that
o(\alpha_{3})+a_{1}=1+5=6<10=c_{R}
.

4.
Quasihomogeneity via trace ideal of
\Omega_{R}

We recall the following notions and results which will be crucial in establishing our second main result.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
R
be a local domain with fraction field
Q
. Then the
rank
of any module
M
is defined to be
\operatorname{rank}(M):=\dim_{Q}(M\otimes_{R}Q)
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
M
be a finitely generated
R
-module. Then the
trace ideal
of
M
, denoted
\text{tr}_{R}(M)
, is the ideal
\sum\alpha(M)
where
\alpha
ranges over
M^{*}:=\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(M,R)
. An ideal
I
is said to be a trace ideal if
I=\text{tr}_{R}(M)
for some module
M
.

Clearly,
I\subseteq\text{tr}_{R}(I)
. For further details on trace ideals and their properties, we refer the reader to resources such as
[
4
, Proposition 2.4]
and
[
7
]
. we shall need the following properties in this work.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{k})
be a one dimensional non-regular complete local domain which is a
\mathbb{k}
-algebra, where
\mathbb{k}
is algebraically closed of characteristic
0
. Let
\overline{R}=\mathbb{k}\llbracket{t}\rrbracket
with
v
the order valuation. Let
I
be a fractional ideal of
R
, i.e., an
R
-submodule of
Q
. Then the following statements hold.

(1)

For any
R
-module
N
, we have that
\text{tr}_{R}(\text{tr}_{R}(N))=\text{tr}_{R}(N)
.

(2)

Suppose
J
is another fractional ideal that is isomorphic to
I
. Then
\text{tr}_{R}(I)=\text{tr}_{R}(J)

(3)

\text{tr}_{R}(I)=II^{-1}
.

(4)

v(\text{tr}_{R}(I))=v(I)+v(I^{-1})
.

(5)

Suppose
M
is a finitely generated
R
-module of rank one. Suppose
f:M\to R
is any non-zero map and let
I=f(M)
. Then
\text{tr}_{R}(M)=\text{tr}_{R}(I)
.

Proof.

Suppose
\phi:I\to J
be the isomorphism. Then by composing any map
\alpha:J\to R
with
\phi
we get a map from
I\to R
. Thus,
\text{tr}_{R}(J)\subseteq\text{tr}_{R}(I)
. Now a symmetric argument finishes the proof of
(2)
. Statement
(1)
also follows a similar reasoning.

Statement
(3)
appears as
[
4
, Proposition 2.4(2)]
whereas statement
(5)
is
[
8
, Proposition 3.5]
.

Statement
(4)
is immediate from
(3)
.
∎

4.1.
Fractional Ideal of Derivatives

Let
R=\mathbb{k}\llbracket{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}}\rrbracket,n\geqslant 2
where each
x_{i}=\alpha_{i}t^{a_{i}}
where
a_{1}<a_{2}<\ldots<a_{n}
as in our original setup. Let
\Omega_{R}
be the module of differentials. From
[
3
, Section 2.3]
and ignoring
dt
, we see that
\Omega_{R}
surjects to the
R
-span of
x_{i}^{\prime}(t):=\frac{dx_{i}}{dt}
. This is clearly a fractional ideal and we call it the fractional ideal of derivatives. Henceforth we shall use the following notation –

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}:=Rx_{1}^{\prime}(t)+Rx_{2}^{\prime}(t)+\cdots+Rx_{n}^{\prime}(t).

Notice that some power series of high enough valuation multiplies
\mathcal{D}
into
R
and hence we get an ideal of
R
to which
\Omega_{R}
surjects. This ideal of course is isomorphic to
\mathcal{D}
as the map is simply by multiplication by a non-zero element of
Q
. For instance,
t^{c_{R}}\mathcal{D}
is such an ideal, where
c_{R}=v(\mathfrak{C}_{R})
.

4.2.
The
\operatorname{h}(\cdot)
Invariant

Definition 4.4
.

[
8
, Definition 2.1]

Let
R
be a one dimensional local domain and
M
be a finitely generated
R
-module. Then

\operatorname{h}(M)=\min\{\ell(R/I)\mid\text{there exists a homomorphism}~\phi:M\to R~\text{with~}\phi(M)=I\}

where
\ell(\cdot)
denotes length.

Notice that
\operatorname{h}(\cdot)
is a non-negative integer or takes the value
\infty
.

Lemma 4.5
.

[
8
, Theorem 5.2]

Let
R,\Omega_{R},\mathcal{D}
be as discussed above. Then

\operatorname{h}(\Omega_{R})=\ell(\overline{R}/\mathcal{D})-\ell(\overline{R}/R)+v(\mathcal{D}^{-1})\leqslant v(\mathcal{D}^{-1}).

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{k})
be a non-regular complete local one dimensional domain with
\mathbb{k}
algebraically closed of characteristic
0
. Let
\Omega_{R}
be the module of differentials and
\overline{R}=\mathbb{k}\llbracket{t}\rrbracket
with valuation function
v
. Then
R
is quasihomogeneous if and only if

v(\text{tr}_{R}(\Omega_{R}))=v(\mathfrak{m}).

Proof.

Throughout the proof, we assume that
R=\mathbb{k}\llbracket{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}}\rrbracket,n\geqslant 2
with
x_{i}=\alpha_{i}t^{a_{i}}
,
a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots<a_{n}
.

Assume that
R
is quasihomogeneous. So there exists a surjection from
\Omega_{R}
to
\mathfrak{m}
. Since
\operatorname{rank}(\Omega_{R})=1
, we have that
\text{tr}_{R}(\Omega_{R})=\text{tr}_{R}(\mathfrak{m})
by
Lemma

4.3
(5)
. Now
\mathfrak{m}\subseteq\text{tr}_{R}(\mathfrak{m})
and if the latter is equal to
R
, then
\mathfrak{m}
has a free direct summand by
[
7
, Proposition 2.8(iii)]
, contradicting the non-regularity assumption of
R
. Thus
\text{tr}_{R}(\mathfrak{m})=\mathfrak{m}
and hence
v(\text{tr}_{R}(\Omega_{R}))=v(\mathfrak{m})
.

Conversely, suppose that
v(\text{tr}_{R}(\Omega_{R}))=v(\mathfrak{m})=v(x_{1})
. In order to prove that
R
is quasihomogeneous, it is enough to show that
\displaystyle\operatorname{h}(\Omega_{R})=1
: for suppose
1=\displaystyle\operatorname{h}(\Omega_{R})=\ell(R/I)
for some ideal
I
which is a surjective image of
\Omega_{R}
. Now
I\subseteq\mathfrak{m}\subseteq R
and hence
1=\ell(R/I)\geqslant\ell(R/\mathfrak{m})=1
which implies that
I=\mathfrak{m}
.

By
Lemma

4.3
, we get that
\text{tr}_{R}(\Omega_{R})=\text{tr}_{R}(\mathcal{D})=\mathcal{D}\mathcal{D}^{-1}
and hence
v(\text{tr}_{R}(\Omega_{R}))=v(\mathcal{D})+v(\mathcal{D}^{-1})
. Since
\mathbb{k}
is of characteristic
0
, we get that
v(\mathcal{D})=v(x_{1}^{\prime}(t))=v(x_{1})-1=v(\mathfrak{m})-1
. Using this and the hypothesis, we get that
v(\mathcal{D}^{-1})=v(\text{tr}_{R}(\Omega_{R}))-v(\mathcal{D})=v(\mathfrak{m})-v(\mathfrak{m})+1=1
.

Thus, using
Lemma

4.5
, we get that

\displaystyle\operatorname{h}(\Omega_{R})=\ell(\overline{R}/\mathcal{D})-\ell(\overline{R}/R)+1\leqslant 1.

If
\displaystyle\operatorname{h}(\Omega_{R})=0
, then
R
is regular (for instance, see
[
8
, Remark 4.2]
), a contradiction. Thus
\displaystyle\operatorname{h}(\Omega_{R})=1
, thereby finishing the proof.
∎
