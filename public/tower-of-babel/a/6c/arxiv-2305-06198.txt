1.1.
Overview of techniques

We conclude with a brief overview of our techniques.

Spectral independence for
\mu_{k}

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices with maximum degree
\Delta\geq 3
. Since
\alpha_{c}(\Delta)<\frac{1}{\Delta+1}
, it follows that the condition
k\leq(1-\delta)\alpha_{c}(\Delta)n
is closed under pinning vertices of
G
to belong to the independent set. Hence, one might hope to prove rapid mixing of the down-up walk by establishing
O_{\delta,\Delta}(1)
-spectral independence for
\mu_{k}
(viewed as a distribution on
\binom{[n]}{k}
). For proving spectral independence of a distribution for the purposes of establishing rapid mixing (as opposed to optimal mixing) of the down-up walk, there are primarily three techniques: Oppenheim’s trickle down theorem
[
29
]
, zero-free regions of multivariate generating polynomial
[
9
]
, and tree recursions using a suitable potential function (e.g.
[
10
]
). The first of these was used by Alev and Lau
[
1
]
and works until
k\leq n/(2\Delta)
. While the latter two techniques have been successfully used in the case of the hard-core model in the uniqueness region, it is unclear how to adapt them to our cardinality-constrained model. A salient challenge here is that any potential approach must be able to “witness” the threshold
\alpha_{c}(\Delta)n
, which is most naturally interpreted in terms of the uniqueness threshold
\lambda_{c}(\Delta)
of the hard-core model, thereby ruling out several purely “slice-based” approaches.

In order to be able to witness
\alpha_{c}(\Delta)n
, we view
\mu_{k}
as being obtained by rejection sampling from a hard-core model at a suitable activity
\lambda
in the uniqueness region; we note that this is precisely the efficient sampling algorithm for
\mu_{k}
,
k\leq(1-\delta)\alpha_{c}(\Delta)n
provided by Davies and Perkins
[
11
]
. Adopting this viewpoint, spectral independence of
\mu_{k}
(in the strengthened form, known as
\ell_{\infty}
-independence) follows from the known
\ell_{\infty}
-independence of the hard-core model in the uniqueness region, provided we have a fine understanding of
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda,G}(|I|=k)
– the probability that an independent set drawn from the hard-core model at activity
\lambda
has size
k
. Specifically, we need to show that
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda,G}(|I|=k)=\Omega_{\delta,\Delta}(n^{-1/2})
(this follows immediately from a local central limit theorem (LCLT) for the hard-core model established in
[
19
]
) and critically, that if
G^{\prime}
is a graph obtained from
G
by adding or removing
O_{\Delta}(1)
vertices, then
|\mathbb{P}_{\lambda,G^{\prime}}(|I|=k)-\mathbb{P}_{\lambda,G}(|I|=k)|=O_{\delta,\Delta}(n^{-3/2})
; in contrast to the lower bound on the probability, which is a consequence of the Gaussian behavior of
|I|
(as encapsulated by the LCLT), the second point requires us to show that the deviations from Gaussianity are optimally stable under perturbations of the underlying graph
G
. We accomplish this by establishing an Edgeworth expansion for the probability to within arbitrarily small polynomial error (this requires estimates on high Fourier coefficients of
|I|
from
[
19
]
) and then showing that the coefficients of the higher order terms in the expansion—which are polynomials in the cumulants of
|I|
—are stable under perturbations of
G
, by combining the presence of the zero-free region for the (complex) multivariate independence polynomial from
[
30
]
with an application of Cauchy’s integral formula to bound the magnitude of various derivatives.
The use of the (univariate) zero-free region is a key ingredient in the local central limit theorem in
[
19
]
,
and follows a line of literature proving central limit theorems for spin systems from zero-free regions
[
18
,
22
]
as well as more general central limit theorems from zero-free regions
[
17
,
22
,
26
,
27
]
. Our contribution shows that for a spin system in the presence of a
multivariate
zero-free region, one can in fact write an asymptotic expansion for probabilities such as
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda,G}(|I|=k)
for
k
near the mean.

Log-Sobolev inequality

Chen, Liu, and Vigoda
[
8
]
showed how to leverage spectral independence to prove log-Sobolev inequalities for Markov random fields on bounded degree graphs, provided that all marginals of the distribution are uniformly lower bounded under arbitrary pinnings. While our distribution may be viewed as a spin system on a bounded degree graph, the other properties fail to hold:
\mu_{k}
is neither a Markov random field nor has the bounded marginal property; indeed, conditioning on
k-1
vertices to be in the independent set makes all the remaining marginals
O(1/n)
. Overcoming these challenges requires a number of innovations.

First, noting that the distributions obtained by pinning at most
k-\Omega_{\Delta}(k)
vertices are marginally bounded, we can use the local-to-global/annealing machinery from the spectral independence/localization-schemes literature to reduce our task to proving a log-Sobolev inequality for
\mu_{k}
,
k\leq n/\Delta^{8}
(say). Compared to the hard-core model at activity
\lambda\sim 1/\Delta^{8}
, this turns out to be a much harder task; indeed, even proving an asymptotically optimal log-Sobolev inequality for the uniform distribution on the Boolean slice
\binom{[n]}{k}
is non-trivial and was only determined, after much work, by Lee and Yau
[
23
]
(the uniform distribution on
\binom{[n]}{k}
is the same as the uniform distribution over independent sets of size
k
in the empty graph, hence a special case of our setup). In our setting, since there is no symmetry, the situation is much more complicated. The main part of our argument here is
Theorem

28
, which proves an optimal log-Sobolev inequality assuming that the graph has linearly many connected components, each of which has size at most logarithmic in the number of vertices.
Finally, to reduce to the setting of
Theorem

28
, we use an average case “annealing” argument (cf.
[
7
]
) building on a technique in
[
8
]
.

For controlling the log-Sobolev constant in a graph with linearly many, at most logarithmically-sized components (
Theorem

28
), we follow the overall inductive approach of Lee and Yau
[
23
]
for bounding the log-Sobolev inequality on the slice. This proceeds through the
a priori
control of the log-Sobolev constant of the “projected” distribution on each small connected component combined with an inductive control of the log-Sobolev constant of the distribution restricted to the complement of the component, averaged out over all components. In our case, both the control of the projected distribution and the inductive control of the distribution restricted to the complement are highly non-trivial.

In order to control the contribution from the projected distribution, we observe that using the LCLT from
[
19
]
, the log-Sobolev constant of the projected distribution on each component can be bounded by direct comparison with the hardcore model on the component at the corresponding activity. Given this, it remains to upper bound the Dirichlet form of this projected distribution by appropriate terms in the Dirichlet form of the original distribution. In special cases, such as when the graph is empty, such a bound can be obtained in a straightforward manner using the convexity of the functionals appearing in the Dirichlet forms. However, convexity arguments are not available in general. In fact, proving a bound of this form is one of the key steps in Markov chain decomposition results (e.g.
[
20
]
); existing results of this form lose a factor depending on the spectral gap of the distribution, which in our case is far too large for the induction to work. Instead, we provide a novel, sharp decomposition result, which allows us to give an optimal comparison (up to constant factors) between the Dirichlet form of the projected chain and that of the original distribution. Our analysis makes use of Stein’s method for Markov chains
[
5
,
31
]
and leverages the existence of a contractive coupling in the regime we operate in.

The other main part of the argument is the inductive control of the log-Sobolev constant on the entire graph, which is again much more involved than the case of the uniform distribution on the slice. Instead of directly inducting on the bound, we simulate the inductive bound via a stochastic process involving one effective parameter which is the analog of the occupancy ratio of the independent set along the process. This effective parameter is closely captured by a martingale with bounded differences and we control it by the martingale Bernstein inequality.

3
). Adopting this point of view, we can rewrite

(2)

|\mathbb{P}_{k}(v\,|\,\overline{u})-\mathbb{P}_{k}(v\,|\,u)|=\left|\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}(v\,|\,\overline{u})\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}(|I|=k\,|\,v\cap\overline{u})}{\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}(|I|=k\,|\,\overline{u})}-\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}(v\,|\,u)\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}(|I|=k\,|\,v\cap u)}{\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}(|I|=k\,|\,u)}\right|\,.

In light of
Theorem

9
, it therefore suffices to show that

\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}(|I|=k)=\Theta_{\Delta,\delta,\gamma}(n^{-1/2})

and

(3)

\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}(|I|=k\,|\,\bullet)=\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}(|I|=k)+O_{\Delta,\delta,\gamma}(n^{-3/2})\,

for each of the four quantities of this form appearing in (
2
). The first of these equations was established in
[
19
]
, as a consequence of a local central limit theorem for
|I|
and the fact that
\operatorname{Var}_{\lambda}(|I|)=\Theta_{\Delta,\delta,\gamma}(n)
(see
[
19
, Lemma 3.2]
). The heart of the matter is (
3
). Since the variance of
|I|
is only
\Theta_{\Delta,\delta,\gamma}(n)
, a local central limit theorem by itself is too crude to establish a statement of this sort.

There are two main ingredients in the proof of (
3
): an Edgeworth expansion – to an arbitrary polynomial error – for
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda,G}(|I|=k)
; and the property that the cumulants of the random variable
|I|
under the hard-core model at activity
\lambda
are stable under small perturbations of the underlying graph
G
.

Recall that the
j^{th}
cumulant of a random variable
X
is defined in terms of the coefficients of the cumulant generating function
K_{X}(t):=\log\mathbb{E}e^{tX}
(when this expectation exists in a neighborhood of 0). In
particular, the
j^{th}
cumulant is

\kappa_{j}(X)=\frac{d^{j}}{dt^{j}}K_{X}(t)|_{t=0}.

The first and second cumulants are the mean and variance respectively.

3.1.
An Edgeworth expansion: the probability of hitting the mean

To state the Edgeworth expansion, we first recall the Hermite polynomials
H_{k}(x)
, which are defined via

H_{k}(x)=(-1)^{k}e^{x^{2}/2}\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}e^{-x^{2}/2}

and satisfy

(4)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-t^{2}/2}e^{-itx}(it)^{k}\,dt=H_{k}(x)e^{-x^{2}/2}\,.

We now prove the Edgeworth expansion for the size of an independent set sampled from the hard-core model.

Proposition 10
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices of maximum degree
\Delta
and fix
\delta,\gamma>0
. Let
X
be the random variable giving the size of an independent set in
G
sampled from the hard-core model at activity
\gamma\leq\lambda\leq(1-\delta)\lambda_{c}(\Delta)
. Set
\mu=\mathbb{E}X
,
\sigma^{2}=\operatorname{Var}(X)
and
\beta_{j}=\kappa_{j}(X)/(j!\sigma^{j})
. Then, for each fixed
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
a\in\mathbb{R}
so that
\mu+a\in\mathbb{Z}
we have

\mathbb{P}(X=\mu+a)=\frac{e^{-a^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\left(1+\sum_{r\geq 3}H_{r}(a/\sigma)\sum_{j_{3},\ldots,j_{\ell}}\frac{\beta_{3}^{j_{3}}}{j_{3}!}\cdots\frac{\beta_{\ell}^{j_{\ell}}}{j_{\ell}!}\right)+O_{\Delta,\delta,\gamma,d}(n^{-d})

where the inner sum is over sequences of non-negative integers
j_{3},j_{4},\ldots,j_{\ell}
so that
\sum_{a}aj_{a}=r
and
j_{3}/2+j_{4}+j_{5}(3/2)+\cdots+j_{\ell}(\ell-2)/2\leq d
.

Proof.

By Fourier inversion, we have

\mathbb{P}(X=\mu+a)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma}\int_{-\pi\sigma}^{\pi\sigma}e^{-ita/\sigma}\mathbb{E}e^{it(X-\mu)/\sigma}\,dt\,.

By
[
19
, Lemma 3.5]
, there is a constant
c=c(\Delta,\delta,\gamma)
so that

|\mathbb{E}e^{itX/\sigma}|\leq\exp(-cnt^{2}/\sigma^{2})

for all
|t|\leq\pi\sigma
. Since
\sigma^{2}=\Theta_{\Delta,\delta,\gamma}(n)
(see
[
19
, Lemma 3.2]
), it follows that we can take
C=C(\Delta,\delta,\gamma,d)
large enough so that we have

(5)

\mathbb{P}(X=\mu+a)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma}\int_{|t|\leq C\log n}e^{-ita/\sigma}\mathbb{E}e^{it(X-\mu)/\sigma}\,dt+O_{\Delta,\delta,\gamma,d}(n^{-d})\,.

To proceed, we will need the following lemma, which will be proved in the next subsection.

Lemma 11
.

There exists a sufficiently small positive constant
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{PR}}>0
, depending only on
\Delta,\delta
, such that for each
d\in\mathbb{N}

|\kappa_{d}(X)|=O_{\Delta,\delta}(d!2^{d}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{PR}}^{-d}n)\,

and moreover, for all
|t|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{PR}}/4
, we have

\left|\log\frac{Z_{G}(\lambda e^{t})}{Z_{G}(\lambda)}-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\kappa_{j}(X)\frac{t^{j}}{j!}\right|=O_{\Delta,\delta}(|2t/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{PR}}|^{d+1}n)\,.

From
Lemma

11
and
\sigma^{2}=\Theta_{\Delta,\delta,\gamma}(n)
, we can easily deduce the following.

Lemma 12
.

For
t\leq C\log n
we have

\mathbb{E}e^{it(X-\mu)/\sigma}=e^{-t^{2}/2}\left(1+\sum_{r\geq 3}(it)^{r}\sum_{j_{3},\ldots,j_{\ell}}\frac{\beta_{3}^{j_{3}}}{j_{3}!}\cdots\frac{\beta_{\ell}^{j_{\ell}}}{j_{\ell}!}\right)(1+O_{\Delta,\delta,\gamma,d}(n^{-d}\log^{2d+2}n))

where the inner sum is over sequences of non-negative integers
j_{3},j_{4},\ldots,j_{\ell}
so that
\sum_{a\geq 3}aj_{a}=r
and
j_{3}/2+j_{4}+j_{5}(3/2)+\cdots+j_{\ell}(\ell-2)/2\leq d
.

Proof of Lemma.

Apply
Lemma

11
to bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}e^{it(X-\mu)/\sigma}

\displaystyle=e^{-it\mu/\sigma}\frac{Z_{G}(\lambda e^{it/\sigma})}{Z_{G}(\lambda)}=e^{-it\mu/\sigma}\exp\left(\log\frac{Z_{G}(\lambda e^{it/\sigma})}{Z_{G}(\lambda)}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{-it\mu/\sigma}\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{2d+1}\kappa_{j}(X)\frac{(it)^{j}}{\sigma^{j}j!}+O_{\Delta,\delta,\gamma,d}\left(\frac{\log^{2d+2}n}{n^{d}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=e^{-it\mu/\sigma}\exp\left(it\mu/\sigma-t^{2}/2+\sum_{j=3}^{2d+1}\beta_{j}(it)^{j}+O_{\Delta,\delta,\gamma,d}(n^{-d}\log^{2d+2}n)\right)

\displaystyle=e^{-t^{2}/2}\prod_{j=3}^{2d+1}\exp\left(\beta_{j}(it)^{j}\right)\left(1+O_{\Delta,\delta,\gamma,d}(n^{-d}\log^{2d+2}n)\right)\,.

By
[
19
, Lemma 3.2]
, we have
\sigma^{2}=\Theta_{\Delta,\delta,\gamma}(n)
; combining this with
Lemma

11
, we have that

(6)

|\beta_{j}|=O_{\Delta,\delta,\gamma,j}(n^{1-j/2})\,.

Finally, expanding the exponential terms in the above product and collecting terms completes the proof.
∎

Combining the preceding lemma with (
5
) shows

\mathbb{P}(X=\mu+a)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma}\int_{|t|\leq C\log n}e^{-ita/\sigma}e^{-t^{2}/2}\left(1+\sum_{r\geq 3}(it)^{r}\sum_{j_{3},\ldots,j_{\ell}}\frac{\beta_{3}^{j_{3}}}{j_{3}!}\cdots\frac{\beta_{\ell}^{j_{\ell}}}{j_{\ell}!}\right)\,dt+O_{\Delta,\delta,d,\gamma}(n^{-d})

with the inner sum as above and we used the bound
\sigma=\Omega_{\Delta,\delta,\gamma}(n^{-1/2})
to eliminate the logarithms in the error. Applying the identity (
4
) and integrating completes the proof.
∎

For later use, we isolate the
d=2
case of
10
.

Corollary 13
.

In the setting of
10
, if we also have
|a|\leq L
then

\mathbb{P}(X=\mu+a)=\frac{e^{-a^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}+O_{\Delta,\delta,\gamma,L}(n^{-3/2})

Proof.

Apply
10
with
d=2
. Note that all sequences appearing in the sum have
j_{5}=j_{6}=\ldots=0
, and the only choices for
(j_{3},j_{4})
are
(1,0),(0,1)
and
(2,0)
implying

\mathbb{P}(X=\mu+a)=\frac{e^{-a^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\left(1+H_{3}\left(\frac{a}{\sigma}\right)\beta_{3}+H_{4}\left(\frac{a}{\sigma}\right)\beta_{4}+H_{6}\left(\frac{a}{\sigma}\right)\frac{\beta_{3}^{2}}{2}\right)+O_{\Delta,\delta,\gamma}(n^{-2}\log^{6}n)\,.

Recall that
\sigma^{2}=\Omega_{\Delta,\delta,\gamma}(n)
and apply (
6
) along with the bound
|H_{3}(x)|\leq 3|x|
for
|x|\leq 1
to see

\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(1+H_{3}\left(\frac{a}{\sigma}\right)\beta_{3}+H_{4}\left(\frac{a}{\sigma}\right)\beta_{4}+H_{6}\left(\frac{a}{\sigma}\right)\frac{\beta_{3}^{2}}{2}\right)=O_{\Delta,\delta,\gamma,L}(n^{-3/2})\,.\qed

3.2.
Properties of cumulants associated to the hard-core model via zero-free regions

In this subsection, we prove
Lemma

11
as well as the following crucial stability result.

Proposition 14
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices of maximum degree
\Delta
and
u\in V(G)
and fix
\delta>0
. Let
\lambda\leq(1-\delta)\lambda_{c}(\Delta)
. Let
X
be the random variable giving the size of an independent set in the hardcore model on
G
at activity
\lambda
with
u
conditioned on being in the independent set, and let
X^{\prime}
be the size conditioned on
u
not being in the independent set. Then for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
we have

|\kappa_{j}(X)-\kappa_{j}(X^{\prime})|=O_{j,\Delta,\delta}(1)\,.

The engine used to prove these results is a zero-free region for the multivariate partition function for the hard-core model. Given a function
\boldsymbol{\lambda}:V(G)\to\mathbb{C}
define
Z_{G}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
via

Z_{G}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}(G)}\prod_{v\in I}\boldsymbol{\lambda}(v).

Peters and Regts
[
30
]
prove that
Z_{G}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
satisfies a certain zero-free property.

Theorem 15

(Peters-Regts,
[
30
]
)
.

Let
\Delta\geq 3
and
\delta\in(0,1)
. There is an open set
U\subset\mathbb{C}
with
[0,(1-\delta)\lambda_{c}(\Delta)]\subset U
so that for any graph
G
on
n
vertices with maximum degree at most
\Delta
, we have
Z_{G}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\neq 0
provided
\boldsymbol{\lambda}(v)\in U
for all
v\in G
.

In particular, since
U
is an open set, we may find a sufficiently small
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{PR}}>0
, depending only on
\Delta,\delta
, so that
z\in U
for all
|z|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{PR}}
and
\lambda e^{w}\in U
for all
\lambda\in[0,(1-\delta)\lambda_{c}(\Delta)]
and
|w|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{PR}}
.

Our main use of the zero-free region provided by
Theorem

15
is to show analyticity of the logarithm of the partition function and control its derivatives.

Fact 16
.

In the setting of
Theorem

15
, the function
t\mapsto\log\frac{Z_{G}(\lambda e^{t})}{Z_{G}(\lambda)}
is analytic for
|t|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{PR}}
. Further, we have

(7)

\max_{|t|=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{PR}}/2}\left|\log\frac{Z_{G}(\lambda e^{t})}{Z_{G}(\lambda)}\right|=O_{\Delta,\delta}(n)\,.

Proof.

The function
t\mapsto\frac{Z_{G}(\lambda e^{t})}{Z_{G}(\lambda)}
is an analytic function since
Z_{G}
is a polynomial. By
Theorem

15
, we have that
Z_{G}(\lambda e^{t})/Z_{G}(\lambda)\neq 0
for
|t|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{PR}}
. This shows analyticity of the function
t\mapsto\log\frac{Z_{G}(\lambda e^{t})}{Z_{G}(\lambda)}
for
|t|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{PR}}
. To prove (
7
), let
\{\zeta\}
be the set of roots of
Z_{G}
and note that for
|t|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{PR}}
we have that
|\zeta-\lambda e^{t}|
is uniformly bounded below by
Theorem

15
.
This shows

\max_{|t|=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{PR}}/2}\left|\log\frac{Z_{G}(\lambda e^{t})}{Z_{G}(\lambda)}\right|=\max_{|t|=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{PR}}/2}\left|\log\left(\prod_{\zeta}\frac{\zeta-\lambda e^{t}}{\zeta-\lambda}\right)\right|=O_{\Delta,\delta}(n)\,.\qed

In a sample of the hard-core model from
G
at activity
\lambda
, let
X
be the random variable giving the size of the independent set sampled. The use of
16
is that it provides control of the cumulants of
X
, since we have

\kappa_{j}(X)=\frac{d^{j}}{dt^{j}}\log\mathbb{E}e^{tX}|_{t=0}=\frac{d^{j}}{dt^{j}}\log Z_{G}(\lambda e^{t})\bigg|_{t=0}\,.

First, we prove
Lemma

11
.

Proof of
Lemma

11
.

The bound on
\kappa_{j}(X)
follows immediately from
16
together with Cauchy’s integral formula. The second bound follows from noting that

\left|\log\frac{Z_{G}(\lambda e^{t})}{Z_{G}(\lambda)}-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\kappa_{j}(X)\frac{t^{j}}{j!}\right|\leq\sum_{\ell>d}\frac{|\kappa_{\ell}(X)|}{\ell!}|t|^{\ell}

and applying the bound on
|\kappa_{j}(X)|
.
∎

With
Lemma

11
in place, we now shift to the proof of
14
. Let
G^{u}
denote the graph obtained by removing
u
and all of its neighbors from
G
, and let
G^{\overline{u}}
denote the graph obtained by removing
u
from
G
. With
X
and
X^{\prime}
as in the statement of
14
, note that the moment generating function for
X
is
\lambda e^{t}Z_{G^{u}}(\lambda e^{t})/\lambda Z_{G^{u}}(\lambda)
and the moment generating function
X^{\prime}
is
Z_{G^{\overline{u}}}(\lambda e^{t})/Z_{G^{\overline{u}}}(\lambda).
This means that for
j\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\displaystyle\kappa_{j}(X)=\frac{d^{j}}{dt^{j}}\log\lambda e^{t}Z_{G^{u}}(\lambda e^{t})\Big|_{t=0}

\displaystyle\kappa_{j}(X^{\prime})=\frac{d^{j}}{dt^{j}}\log Z_{G^{\overline{u}}}(\lambda e^{t})\Big|_{t=0}

implying that

(8)

\kappa_{j}(X^{\prime})-\kappa_{j}(X)=\frac{d^{j}}{dt^{j}}\log\left(\frac{\lambda e^{t}Z_{G^{u}}(\lambda e^{t})}{Z_{G^{\overline{u}}}(\lambda e^{t})}\right)\Big|_{t=0}\,.

Let

(9)

R_{u}(\lambda,t)=\frac{\lambda e^{t}Z_{G^{u}}(\lambda e^{t})}{Z_{G^{\overline{u}}}(\lambda e^{t})}\,.

This is exactly the so-called occupancy ratio, extended to complex activities. Analyticity of
R_{u}(\lambda,t)
is immediate:

Fact 17
.

The function
t\mapsto R_{u}(\lambda,t)
is analytic for
|t|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{PR}}
.

Proof.

The function
R_{u}(\lambda,t)
is a rational function in
e^{t}
and by
Theorem

15
the denominator is zero-free for
|t|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{PR}}
.
∎

By
17
and Cauchy’s integral formula,
14
follows immediately, provided we can deduce a uniform upper bound on the magnitude of
R
. We will do this by applying
Theorem

15
to a well-chosen multivariate
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
.

Lemma 18
.

There exist
C>0
, depending only on
\Delta,\delta
, such that for
|t|<\varepsilon_{\mathrm{PR}}
and
\lambda\in[0,(1-\delta)\lambda_{c}(\Delta)]
we have
|R_{u}(\lambda,t)|\leq C
.

Proof.

For
\lambda\in[0,(1-\delta)\lambda_{c}(\Delta)]
and
|t|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{PR}}
define
z
via

R_{u}(\lambda,t)=z

and rewrite
Equation

9
as

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda e^{t}}{z}Z_{G^{u}}(\lambda e^{t})+Z_{G^{\overline{u}}}(\lambda e^{t})\,.

Note that if we define the activity
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
via

\boldsymbol{\lambda}(v)=\begin{cases}\lambda e^{t}&\text{ if }v\neq u\\
-\frac{\lambda e^{t}}{z}&\text{ if }v=u\end{cases}

then we can write this equality as
Z_{G}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=0\,.
By
Theorem

15
, since
\boldsymbol{\lambda}(v)\in U
for all
v\neq u
, it must be the case that
\boldsymbol{\lambda}(u)\notin U
. In particular, we must have
|\boldsymbol{\lambda}(u)|=\left|\lambda e^{t}/z\right|\geq\varepsilon_{PR}
, implying that

|z|\leq\lambda e^{|t|}/\varepsilon_{PR}=O_{\Delta,\delta}(1)\,.\qed

Proof of
14
.

Apply equation (
8
) to write

\left|\kappa_{j}(X^{\prime})-\kappa_{j}(X)\right|=\left|\frac{d^{j}}{dt^{j}}\log R_{u}(\lambda,t)\Big|_{t=0}\right|\,.

By
17
and
Lemma

18
, the function
t\mapsto R_{u}(\lambda,t)
is analytic and bounded by
C=O_{\Delta,\delta}(1)
for
|t|<\varepsilon_{\mathrm{PR}}
. Applying Cauchy’s integral theorem completes the proof.
∎

3.3.
Proof of
Theorem

8

By the discussion at the start of this section, it suffices to show (
3
). This follows from at most two repeated applications of the following.

Proposition 19
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices of maximum degree
\Delta

. Then for

Proof of Proposition

.

. We prove (

\left|\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}(|I|=k\,|\,u)-\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}(|I|=k\,|\,\overline{u})\right|=O_{\Delta,\delta,\gamma,L}(n^{-3/2})

\left|\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[|I|]-\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[|I|\,|\,u]\right|=\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}(\overline{u})||\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[|I|\,|\,\overline{u}]-\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[|I|\,|\,u]|=O_{\Delta,\delta}(1),

14
with

. Let

, let

and

the random variable

3.4.
Spectral gap of the down-up walk

We now combine
Theorem

8
with standard machinery from the theory of spectral independence to deduce that the down-up walk has optimal spectral gap
\Omega_{\delta,\Delta}(1/k)
for all
k\leq(1-\delta)\alpha_{c}(\Delta)n
.

Theorem 20
.

Let
\Delta\geq 3
and
\delta\in(0,1)
. For a graph
G=(V,E)
on
n
vertices of maximum degree at most
\Delta
and an integer
1\leq k\leq(1-\delta)\alpha_{c}(\Delta)n
, the down-up walk on independent sets of size
k
satisfies a Poincaré inequality with constant
\Omega_{\delta,\Delta}(1/k)
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the statement only for
k\geq n/(3\Delta)
, since for
k\leq n/(3\Delta)
, the statement follows from the work of Bubley and Dyer
[
6
]
along with a standard inequality relating contractive couplings and spectral gaps (see
[
24
, Theorem 13.1]
): concretely,
[
6
]
shows that for any graph
H
on
m
vertices of maximum degree
\Delta
, for any
s\leq m/(3\Delta)
, and for
\mu_{s}^{H}
the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{I}_{s}(H)
, the spectral gap of the down-up walk on
\mathcal{I}_{s}(H)
is
\Omega_{\Delta}(1/k)
.

We will use
Theorem

8
to lift this to the entire range
k\leq(1-\delta)\alpha_{c}(\Delta)n
. To this end, let
G
be as in the statement of the theorem and let
n/(3\Delta)\leq k\leq(1-\delta)\alpha_{c}(\Delta)n
. Note that for any independent set
J=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell}\}\subset V(G)
of size
\ell\leq k-k\cdot c_{\Delta}
(for a function
c_{\Delta}>0
of
\Delta
, to be specified later) we can view the conditional distribution
\mu_{k}(\cdot\,|\,I\supseteq J)
as
\mu_{k-\ell}^{H}
, where
H
is a graph with maximum degree at most
\Delta
and with at least
n-\ell(\Delta+1)
vertices. Since
k\leq n/(\Delta+1)
, we have

c_{\Delta}\cdot\frac{k}{n}\leq\frac{k-\ell}{n-\ell(\Delta+1)}\leq\frac{k}{n}\leq(1-\delta)\alpha_{c}(\Delta).

Therefore, by
Theorem

8
,
\mu_{k-\ell}^{H}
is
O_{\Delta,\delta}(1)
-spectrally independent.

Let
\nu=\mu_{k}
and consider the measures
\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{k}
defined as follows: let
I\sim\nu
and let
\{j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\}
be a random permutation of
[k]
, independent of
I
. Write
I=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\}
, where
v_{1}<\dots<v_{k}
. For
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, define
\nu_{i}
to be the law of
I
conditioned on
v_{j_{1}},\ldots,v_{j_{i}}\in I
. Let
\nu_{t}=\nu_{\max(t,k)}
. By the
O_{\delta,\Delta}(1)
-spectral independence of
\mu^{H}_{k-\ell}
established in the previous paragraph and
[
7
, Proposition 21, Fact 23]
, it follows that for any
f:\mathcal{I}_{k}(G)\to\mathbb{R}
and any
\ell\leq k-k\cdot c_{\Delta}

(15)

\frac{\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Var}_{\nu_{k-\ell}}[f]]}{\operatorname{Var}_{\nu}[f]}=\Omega_{\delta,\Delta}(1).

Moreover, by the discussion above, it follows that almost surely, the measure
\nu_{k-\ell}
can be represented as

5.
Log-Sobolev inequality: Proof of
Theorem

2

It remains to prove
26
. We will find it more convenient to consider a slightly different Markov chain, where we only perform the down-up walk between different connected components. To this end, we record the following definition.

Definition 27
.

The modified down-up walk on
\mathcal{I}_{k}(G)
is defined as follows: given the current state
I_{t}
at time
t
, we choose independent and uniform vertices
u,v\in V(G)
. If
v
and
u
are in different connected components of
G
and
I^{\prime}=(I\setminus u)\cup v\in\mathcal{I}_{k}(G)
, then
I_{t+1}=I^{\prime}
; else
I_{t+1}=I_{t}
.

Note that the modified down-up walk is reversible with respect to
\mu_{k}(G)
.
26
is a consequence of the following.

Theorem 28
.

There exists a constant
C_{\ref{thm:lee-yau}}=O(1)
for which the following holds: for all graphs
G
on
n
vertices which are
\Delta
-good and for all
n/(C_{\ref{thm:lee-yau}}\Delta^{1000})\leq k\leq n/(C_{\ref{thm:lee-yau}}\Delta^{8})
, the modified down-up walk on
\mathcal{I}_{k}(G)
satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality with constant
\Omega_{\Delta}(1/n)
.

Before proving
Theorem

28
, let us quickly show that it implies
26
.

Proof of
26
given
Theorem

28
.

Let
P
denote the transition matrix of the down-up walk and let
Q
denote the transition matrix of the modified down-up walk. It suffices to show that for all non-negative functions
f
on
\mathcal{I}_{k}(G)
,

\mathcal{E}_{Q}(f,f)\lesssim_{\Delta}\mathcal{E}_{P}(f,f).

For this, we simply note that for any
I\sim J\in\mathcal{I}_{k}(G)
, with
I\neq J

Q(I,J)\lesssim_{\Delta}P(I,J);

indeed, if
J=(I\setminus u)\cup v
with
u,v
in the same connected component, then
Q(I,J)=0
, whereas if
u,v
are in different connected components, then
Q(I,J)=\Theta_{\Delta}(1/n^{2})=\Theta_{\Delta}(P(I,J)).
∎

We prove
Theorem

28
in three stages: first we prove an optimal log-Sobolev inequality for the chain induced by the modified down-up walk on a single connected component of the graph, where we recall that such components are all fairly small (
Lemma

29
); in the spirit of Lee-Yau
[
23
]
, we show a recurrence for the inverse LSI constant of a graph in terms of the graph with a single component removed (
30
); a key ingredient in establishing this recurrence is an optimal Markov chain decomposition-type result in the presence of contractive couplings, based on Stein’s method for Markov chains (
31
); finally, we analyze this recurrence via a martingale argument.

5.1.
The induced chain

Given a graph
\mathcal{G}
with different connected components
\{G\}
, let
I
be a uniformly random independent set of size
k
on
\mathcal{G}
. We denote
I_{G}
the projection of
I
on the component
G
, and
I_{-G}
the projection on
\mathcal{G}-G
. We denote
P_{u,v}
the operator that swaps the status of
u
and
v
in the independent set if
u
and
v
are in different connected components and if the swapping yields a valid independent set. Concretely, by swapping, we mean that if
u
and
v
are both occupied or both unoccupied, we do nothing; if
u
is unoccupied and
v
is occupied, then
u
becomes occupied and
v
becomes unoccupied (and vice versa). We define the induced distribution on
G
as the distribution of
I_{G}
on
2^{G}
and define the following Markov chain on independent sets of
G
:

(18)

P_{G}(I_{G},J_{G})=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{u\in G}\mathbb{P}_{I_{-G}|I_{G},v\sim\mathcal{G}}[J_{G}=(P_{u,v}(I))_{G}].

The importance of the above Markov chain is that its stationary distribution is the distribution of
I_{G}
where
I
is sampled uniformly at random from
\mathcal{I}_{k}(\mathcal{G})
. Indeed, given
I_{G},J_{G}
independent sets on
G
differing in exactly one vertex
v
with
v\in I_{G}
and
v\notin J_{G}
, then
I_{-G}
(respectively
J_{-G}
) is uniformly distributed among independent sets of size
k-|I_{G}|
(respectively
k-|J_{G}|
) in
\mathcal{G}-G
. The above chain satisfies

\frac{{P}_{G}(I_{G},J_{G})}{{P}_{G}(J_{G},I_{G})}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[M(K)]}{(k-|J_{G}|)}.

Here,
K
is uniformly random in
\mathcal{I}_{k-|I_{G}|}(\mathcal{G}-G)
, and
M(K)
is the number of vertices not in
K
that form an independent set when included with
K
. On the other hand, in the bipartite graph with two sides being
\mathcal{I}_{k-|J_{G}|}(\mathcal{G}-G)
and
\mathcal{I}_{k-|I_{G}|}(\mathcal{G}-G)
and two sets adjacent if and only if they differ in exactly one vertex, the average degree of the first side is
(k-|J_{G}|)
and of the second side is
\mathbb{E}[M(K)]
. Hence,

(19)

\frac{\mathbb{E}[M(K)]}{k-|J_{G}|}=\frac{|\mathcal{I}_{k-|J_{G}|}(\mathcal{G}-G)|}{|\mathcal{I}_{k-|I_{G}|}(\mathcal{G}-G)|}.

We will need control over the log-Sobolev constant of the above chain, which we denote by
\rho_{G}^{\rm{induced}}
. This is the content of the next lemma.

Lemma 29
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a graph of maximum degree at most
\Delta
and let
G
be a connected component of
\mathcal{G}
. Assume that
|G|\leq|\mathcal{G}|^{1/4}
. Suppose that
\alpha=k/|\mathcal{G}|\in[\Delta^{-1000},\Delta^{-2}]
. Then,
\rho_{G}^{\textrm{induced}}=\Omega_{\Delta}(|G|^{-1})
.

Proof.

We will show that the induced chain has stationary distribution and transition probabilities within
O_{\Delta}(1)
of the hardcore model on
G
with activity
\lambda=\alpha
. By the Local Central Limit Theorem for the hardcore model in
[
19
]
(or
10
), for integers
a,b\leq|G|\leq|\mathcal{G}|^{1/4}
and
\alpha\in[\Delta^{-1000},\Delta^{-2}]
, the ratio

(20)

\frac{\lambda^{-a}|\mathcal{I}_{k-a}(\mathcal{G}-G)|}{\lambda^{-b}|\mathcal{I}_{k-b}(\mathcal{G}-G)|}=O_{\Delta}(1).

This implies that for all
I_{G}

\frac{\mu_{k}(I_{G})}{\mu_{\lambda}(I_{G})}=O_{\Delta}(1).

Moreover, the transition probability of the induced chain from
I_{G}
to
J_{G}
, where
v\in I_{G}
and
v\notin J_{G}
and
I_{G},J_{G}
differ only at
v
, satisfies

\frac{P_{G}(I_{G},J_{G})}{P_{G}(I_{G},I_{G})}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[M(K)]}{|\mathcal{G}-G|-\mathbb{E}[M(K)]},

with notation as above. Similarly,

\frac{P_{G}(J_{G},I_{G})}{P_{G}(J_{G},J_{G})}=\frac{k-|J_{G}|}{|\mathcal{G}-G|-k+|J_{G}|}.

From (
19
) and (
20
), we have that

\frac{P_{G}(I_{G},J_{G})}{P_{G}(I_{G},I_{G})}=\Theta_{\Delta}(k/n),

and

\frac{P_{G}(J_{G},I_{G})}{P_{G}(J_{G},J_{G})}=\Theta_{\Delta}(k/n).

Hence, the transition probabilities of the induced chain are within
O_{\Delta}(1)
of those of the hardcore model of
G
with activity
\lambda
.

Under the assumption
\lambda\in[\Delta^{-1000},\Delta^{-2}]
and
G
has maximum degree at most
\Delta
, we have that the hardcore model
\mu_{\lambda}
on
G
has LSI with constant
\Omega(1/|G|)
(see, e.g.,
[
8
, Fact 3.5]
). Thus, by standard comparison results (
[
28
, Theorem 2.14]
), we also have the
\rho^{\textrm{induced}}_{G}\geq\Omega_{\Delta}(|G|^{-1})
.
∎

5.2.
Setting up the reduction

We denote a
configuration

\mathcal{C}
to be a graph
\mathcal{G}
together with an integer
k
. We denote the occupancy ratio
\alpha=k/|\mathcal{G}|
.

Given a configuration
\mathcal{C}
, we denote by

the inverse LSI constant associated to

, and
\mu_{\mathcal{C}}
the distribution of independent sets of size

\mathcal{G}

f

\mathcal{G}
of size

\operatorname{Ent}(f)\leq|\mathcal{G}|\rho_{\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{C}}(\sqrt{f},\sqrt{f})

Here we choose this normalization for

because our goal for

\rho_{\mathcal{C}}=O_{\Delta}(1)

For an independent set

and set of vertices

The main goal of this subsection is to prove the following recurrence for

Proposition 30

There exists an absolute constant

be a graph on

\Delta

Our starting point is the following basic fact about entropy: for any non-negative function

on independent sets and any set of vertices

\operatorname{Ent}(f)=\mathbb{E}_{I_{G}}[\operatorname{Ent}f|_{I_{G}}]+\operatorname{Ent}(\mathbb{E}[f|_{I_{G}}])=\mathbb{E}_{I_{G}}[\operatorname{Ent}f|_{I_{G}}]+\operatorname{Ent}(f_{G})

Here we denote

over independent sets on
\mathcal{G}-G
conditional on

and let

A key ingredient in our proof is the following comparison, whose (non-trivial) proof is deferred to

Proposition 31

Under the assumptions of

\mathcal{G}

an independent set of

\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{f_{G}(I_{G})}-\sqrt{f_{G}(I_{G}\setminus u)}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\Delta}\mathbb{E}_{v}\mathbb{E}_{I_{-G}|I_{G}}\left[\left(\sqrt{f(I)}-\sqrt{f(P_{u,v}(I))}\right)^{2}\right],

31
readily implies the following.

Lemma 32

Under the assumptions of

Proof.

Observe that

\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{C}}(\sqrt{f},\sqrt{f})=\mathbb{E}_{I,u,v}\left[\left(\sqrt{f(I)}-\sqrt{f(P_{u,v}(I))}\right)^{2}\right]\,,

We now let

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{I,u,v}\left[\left(\sqrt{f(I)}-\sqrt{f(P_{u,v}(I))}\right)^{2}\right]

Combining this with (

Under the assumptions of

\operatorname{Ent}(f|_{I_{G}})\leq({|\mathcal{G}|-|G|})\rho_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}(\sqrt{f|_{I_{G}}},\sqrt{f|_{I_{G}}}).

.

5.3.
Analyzing the reduction via a martingale approach

In order to obtain a bound on
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}
we will use two bounds: the recurrence obtained in
30
along with the sub-optimal bound

\rho_{\mathcal{C}}\lesssim_{\Delta}|\mathcal{G}|

which holds by
Lemma

23
and standard comparison arguments provided
1/\Delta^{1000}\leq\alpha\leq 1/\Delta^{2}
, for instance.

We will interpret the bound in
30
via the following stochastic process: Initialize
\mathcal{G}_{0}=\mathcal{G}
and
k_{0}=k
. Assume that at step
i
, we have a configuration on
\mathcal{G}_{i}
with parameter
k_{i}
and
\alpha_{i}=k_{i}/|\mathcal{G}_{i}|
. In every step, an adversary selects a pair
I\sim J
, then we pick uniformly at random a component
G=G_{i}
of
\mathcal{G}_{i}
with
I_{G}=J_{G}
, and set the subsequent configuration to be

(28)

\mathcal{G}_{i+1}=\mathcal{G}_{i}-G_{i}\,,\qquad k_{i+1}=k_{i}-|I_{G}|(=k_{i}-|J_{G}|)\,.

We will keep track of
\alpha=\alpha_{i}=k_{i}/|\mathcal{G}_{i}|
along the process. We denote
\mathcal{C}_{i}=(\mathcal{G}_{i},k_{i})
the configuration at time
i
and
\rho_{i}=\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}
. We note that this is equivalent to iterating
30
since if
I\sim J
and
I\neq J
then then
I
and
J
differ in exactly two components, and
I
and
J
are the same in the remaining
m-2
components.

Our goal will be to run the process until time
T=m-\log^{4}n
, and we hope that
\alpha_{j}
does not move too much. With this in mind, define the stopping time

\tau=\min_{t}\{|\alpha_{t}-\alpha_{0}|\geq 1/\sqrt{\log{n}}\}\,.

Via a martingale argument, we will prove

Lemma 34
.

In the above notation, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\tau<T\right)\lesssim_{\Delta}n^{-100}\,.

We now record the iterative bound that follows on
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}
provided that the occupancy ratio always satisfies the assumptions of
30
. In particular this is satisfied if
s<\tau
.

Lemma 35
.

For all
s<\tau
we have

\rho_{\mathcal{C}}\lesssim_{\Delta}\left(\frac{m-s}{m}\right)^{2}\frac{|\mathcal{G}_{0}|}{|\mathcal{G}_{s}|}\rho_{s}+\sum_{i\leq s}\left(\frac{m-i}{m}\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{|\mathcal{G}_{0}|}{|\mathcal{G}_{i}|}\cdot\frac{1}{m-i}\,.

Proof.

This follows from iterating the bound of
30
, using the fact that

\prod_{i=1}^{s}\frac{m-i-2}{m-i}=\Omega_{\Delta}\left(\left(\frac{m-s}{m}\right)^{2}\right)\,.\qed

Assuming
Lemma

34
,
Theorem

28
follows quickly.

Proof of Theorem
28
.

Apply the recursive bound in
30
up to time
R=\min\{\tau,T\}
. In the event that
\tau\geq T
we apply
Lemma

35
to see

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathcal{C}}\lesssim_{\Delta}\left(\frac{m-T}{m}\right)^{2}\frac{|\mathcal{G}_{0}|}{|\mathcal{G}_{T}|}\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{T}}+\sum_{i\leq T}\left(\frac{m-i}{m}\right)^{2}\frac{|\mathcal{G}_{0}|}{|\mathcal{G}_{i}|}\frac{1}{m-i}\,.

The second term is
O_{\Delta}(1)
, noting that under the assumption that
G
is
\Delta
-good,
{|\mathcal{G}_{0}|}/{|\mathcal{G}_{i}|}\leq n/(m-i)=O_{\Delta}(m/(m-i))
. For the former, recall that

\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{T}}\lesssim_{\Delta}|\mathcal{G}_{T}|

and so we have

\rho_{\mathcal{C}}\lesssim_{\Delta}\frac{\log^{8}n}{m^{2}}|\mathcal{G}_{0}|+1\,.

Noting that
m=\Omega_{\Delta}(n)
by
\Delta
-goodness completes the bound on the event that
\tau>T
. To bound
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}
on the event that
\tau<T
we simply bound

\mathbb{E}\rho_{\mathcal{C}}1\{\tau<T\}\lesssim n\mathbb{P}(\tau<T)=o_{n}(1),

where we used
Lemma

23
for the first bound and
Lemma

35
for the second.
∎

All that remains to prove is
Lemma

34
. This will follow from tracking the random sequence
\alpha_{j}
via a martingale argument.

Proof of
Lemma

34
.

Denote
\mathcal{F}_{i}
the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the process up to time
i
. We want to control the increments
\alpha_{i+1}-\alpha_{i}
; for simplicity of notation, in the following argument we denote
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}_{i}
and
G=G_{i}
.
Observe that

\alpha_{i+1}-\alpha_{i}=\frac{k_{i}-|I_{G}|}{|\mathcal{G}|-|G|}-\frac{k_{i}}{|\mathcal{G}|}=\frac{k_{i}|G|-|I_{G}||\mathcal{G}|}{(|\mathcal{G}|-|G|)|\mathcal{G}|}\,.

Since
\sum_{G}|G|=|\mathcal{G}|
and
\sum_{G}|I_{G}|=k_{i}
we have that
\sum_{G:I_{G}=J_{G}}|G|=|\mathcal{G}|+O_{\Delta}(\max_{G}|G|)
, and
\sum_{G:I_{G}=J_{G}}|I_{G}|=k_{i}+O_{\Delta}(\max_{G}|G|)
, and hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{i+1}-\alpha_{i}|\mathcal{F}_{i}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m_{i}-2}\sum_{G:I_{G}=J_{G}}\left(\frac{k_{i}|G|-|I_{G}||\mathcal{G}|}{(|\mathcal{G}|-|G|)|\mathcal{G}|}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m_{i}-2}\sum_{G:I_{G}=J_{G}}\frac{k_{i}|G||\mathcal{G}|-k_{i}|G|(|\mathcal{G}|-|G|)-|I_{G}||\mathcal{G}|^{2}+|I_{G}||\mathcal{G}|(|\mathcal{G}|-|G|)}{|\mathcal{G}|^{2}(|\mathcal{G}|-|G|)}+O_{\Delta}\left(\frac{\max_{G}|G|}{m_{i}|\mathcal{G}|}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m_{i}-2}\sum_{G:I_{G}=J_{G}}\frac{k_{i}|G|^{2}}{|\mathcal{G}|^{2}(|\mathcal{G}|-|G|)}-\frac{1}{m_{i}-2}\sum_{G:I_{G}=J_{G}}\frac{|I_{G}||G|}{|\mathcal{G}|(|\mathcal{G}|-|G|)}+O_{\Delta}\left(\frac{\max_{G}|G|}{m_{i}|\mathcal{G}|}\right).

Thus for
i\leq m-\log^{4}n
we have

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{i+1}-\alpha_{i}|\mathcal{F}_{i}]|\leq\frac{1}{m_{i}-2}\frac{k}{|\mathcal{G}|^{3}}\sum_{G}|G|^{2}+\frac{1}{m_{i}-2}\cdot\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|^{2}}\sum_{G}|G|^{2}+O_{\Delta}\left(\frac{\max_{G}|G|}{m|\mathcal{G}|}\right)\leq O_{\Delta}\left(\frac{\log n}{m|\mathcal{G}|}\right)\,.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle|\alpha_{i+1}-\alpha_{i}|\leq\left|\frac{k_{i}-|I_{G}|}{|\mathcal{G}|-|G|}-\frac{k_{i}}{|\mathcal{G}|}\right|\leq 2\frac{||\mathcal{G}||I_{G}|-k_{i}|G||}{|\mathcal{G}|^{2}}\leq 4\frac{|G|}{|\mathcal{G}|}\,.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\alpha_{i+1}-\alpha_{i})^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{i}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m_{i}-2}\sum_{G:I_{G}=J_{G}}\left(\frac{k_{i}|G|-|I_{G}||\mathcal{G}|}{(|\mathcal{G}|-|G|)|\mathcal{G}|}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{m_{i}-2}\sum_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\frac{|G|^{2}}{|\mathcal{G}|^{2}}

We now construct the martingale
\bar{\alpha}_{i}
by

\bar{\alpha}_{i+1}=\bar{\alpha}_{i}+(\alpha_{i+1}-\alpha_{i})-\mathbb{E}[(\alpha_{i+1}-\alpha_{i})|\mathcal{F}_{i}].

The quadratic variation of
\bar{\alpha}
satisfies

\langle\bar{\alpha}\rangle_{i}=\sum_{j\leq i}\mathbb{E}((\bar{\alpha}_{j}-\bar{\alpha}_{j-1})^{2}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{j-1})\lesssim\sum_{j\leq i}\left(\frac{1}{m_{i}}\sum_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}|G|^{2}/|\mathcal{G}_{i}|^{2}\right)+\sum_{j\leq i}\log^{2}{n}/(m_{i}^{2}|\mathcal{G}_{i}|^{2}).

Consider
s\leq m-\log^{4}n
. Note that since
|G|=O_{\Delta}(\log n)
and
|\mathcal{G}_{i}|\geq\log^{4}n
we have that

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i\leq s}\mathbb{E}[(\alpha_{i+1}-\alpha_{i})|\mathcal{F}_{i}]\right|\lesssim_{\Delta}\sum_{i\leq s}\frac{1}{m-i}\frac{\log n}{|\mathcal{G}_{i}|}\lesssim\log n\sum_{i\leq s}\frac{1}{(m-i)^{2}}\leq\frac{\log n}{(m-s)}\lesssim\frac{1}{\log^{3}n}\,.

The same argument provides the bounds

\displaystyle\langle\bar{\alpha}\rangle_{s}\lesssim_{\Delta}\frac{1}{\log^{3}n}\quad\text{ and }\quad\sup|\bar{\alpha}_{i+1}-\bar{\alpha}_{i}|\lesssim_{\Delta}\frac{1}{\log^{3}n}\,.

We will use the martingale Bernstein inequality by Freedman
[
15
]
. For
s\leq m-\log^{4}n
we have the bound

\mathbb{P}\left(|\bar{\alpha}_{s}-\bar{\alpha}_{0}|>x\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\Omega_{\Delta}\left(\frac{x^{2}\log^{3}n}{(1+x)}\right)\right)\,.

Applying this with
x=1/\sqrt{\log{n}}
, we may sum over
s\leq m-\log^{4}n
to bound
\mathbb{P}(\tau<T)\leq n^{-100}
for
n
sufficiently large in
\Delta
.
∎

5.4.

Throughout, let
\mathcal{G}
be as in the statement of

denote the connected components of

of

of

. We also fix a non-negative function

.

We now establish some notation. Let
\mu_{k}
denote the uniform distribution on independent sets of
\mathcal{G}
of size

. Let
\mu_{1}
denote the distribution of

conditioned on the restriction of

to

. Let

denote the distribution defined similarly, except we want agreement with

(and hence, also with

denote the set consisting of

and all neighbors of

and we let

denote an absolute constant, which is sufficiently large to make an inequality at the end of this subsection hold.

Note that
\mu
is a convex combination of
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
and so we can write
\mu=\theta\mu_{1}+(1-\theta)\mu_{2}

As such we have

\frac{d\mu_{j}}{d\mu}=O_{\Delta}(1)

for

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{j}}[f]=O_{\Delta}(\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[f])

(30)

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{1}}f=f_{G}(I_{G}),\qquad\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{2}}f=f_{G}(I_{G}\setminus u)\,.

Thus, in order to prove

\left(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{1}}f}-\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{2}}f}\right)^{2}

Lemma 36

For
1/(C\Delta^{1000})\leq k/n\leq 1/(C\Delta^{6})

\left(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{1}}f}-\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{2}}f}\right)^{2}\lesssim_{\Delta}\left(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{1}}f}-\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}f}\right)^{2}\,.

Proof.

Bound

\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{1}}f}-\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{2}}f}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{1}}f-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{2}}f)^{2}}{(\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{1}}f+\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{2}}f)}\,.

And similarly bound

\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{1}}f}-\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}f}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{(\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{1}}f-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}f)^{2}}{(\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{1}}f+\mathbb{E}_{\mu}f)}\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{(1-\theta)^{2}(\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{1}}f-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{2}}f)^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{1}}f+\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{2}}f}\,.

Using
Equation

29
completes the proof.
∎

From now on, for notational simplicity, we will denote
\mu_{1}
by
\nu
. We introduce two versions of the modified down-up walk with slight variations so that they are reversible with respect to
\mu
and
\nu
. The transition
P_{\mu}
takes an independent set that on

agrees with
I_{G}
; it then picks two vertices

uniformly at random and moves to

provided that
a,b
are in different connected components and
P_{a,b}(I)
agrees with
I_{G}
on
G\setminus u
. The transition
P_{\nu}
is defined similarly, the only change being that the independent must agree with
I_{G}
on
G
throughout.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
evolve according to the transition
P_{\mu}
. Then we may define

h(x)=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}[f(X_{t})-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}f|X_{0}=x].

As shown in
[
5
,
31
]
, this is well-defined and satisfies the following Poisson equation:
f=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}f+h-P_{\mu}h
. In particular, using that
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[P_{\nu}(h)-h]=0
, we have that

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}f=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\mathbb{E}_{\mu}f+h-P_{\mu}h]=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}f+\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[(P_{\nu}h-h)-(P_{\mu}h-h)].

Hence,

(31)

\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}f}-\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\nu}f}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{|\mathbb{E}_{\mu}f-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}f|^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}f+\mathbb{E}_{\nu}f}\leq\frac{\left|\mathbb{E}_{\nu}(P_{\mu}h-h)-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}(P_{\nu}h-h)\right|^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}f}

Consider a sample
X_{0}\sim\nu
and couple
(Y_{0},Y_{0}^{\prime})
so that
(X_{0},Y_{0})
is a transition from
P_{\mu}
and
(X_{0},Y_{0}^{\prime})
is a transition from
P_{\nu}
. Concretely, pick two vertices
a,b\in\mathcal{G}
uniformly at random. If either
a
or
b
is equal to
u
, then we set
Y_{0}^{\prime}=X_{0}
and set
Y_{0}=P_{a,b}(X_{0})
. If neither
a
nor
b
is equal to
u
, we have two cases: if at least one of
a
or
b
is in
G
, then
Y_{0}=Y_{0}^{\prime}=X_{0}
; otherwise we set
Y_{0}=Y_{0}^{\prime}=P_{a,b}(X_{0})
. In sum, the only case in which
Y_{0}\neq Y_{0}^{\prime}
is in the event that either
a
or
b
is equal to
u
, which occurs with probability at most
2/n
. Moreover, observe that the marginal distributions of
Y_{0}
and
Y_{0}^{\prime}
in
(Y_{0},Y_{0}^{\prime})
conditioned on the event
Y_{0}\neq Y_{0}^{\prime}
coincide with
\mu_{1}(=\nu)
and
\mu_{2}
.

By construction,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}(P_{\mu}h-h)-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}(P_{\nu}h-h)=\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}_{(Y_{0},Y_{0}^{\prime})}\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}[f(Y_{t})-f(Y_{t}^{\prime})],

where
\{(Y_{t},Y_{t}^{\prime})\}_{t\geq 0}
is a contractive coupling of two trajectories evolving according to
P_{\mu}
, started from
(Y_{0},Y_{0}^{\prime})
, and where
(Y_{0},Y_{0}^{\prime})
is distributed as in the previous paragraph. The outer expectation is taken over the randomness of the trajectory.
We will later describe a single step of the contractive coupling in the proof of
Lemma

37
, but for now we only need that once the two walks couple, they remain equal thereafter, and that the coupling time has exponential tails.
Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{\left|\mathbb{E}_{\nu}(P_{\mu}h-h)-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}(P_{\nu}h-h)\right|^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}f}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t}(f(Y_{t})-f(Y_{t}^{\prime}))\right]\right|^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}f}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left|\mathbb{P}(Y_{0}\neq Y_{0}^{\prime})\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t}(f(Y_{t})-f(Y_{t}^{\prime}))\,|\,Y_{0}\neq Y_{0}^{\prime}]\right|^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}f}

(32)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{n^{2}}\frac{\left|\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}_{(Y_{0},Y_{0}^{\prime})\sim\gamma}[\sum_{t}(f(Y_{t})-f(Y_{t}^{\prime}))]\right|^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}f},

where
\gamma
denotes the following distribution: first choose
v\notin G
uniformly at random, then choose
I^{\prime}
from the uniform distribution on independent sets of size
(k-I_{G})
in
\mathcal{G}\setminus(G\cup N_{+}(v))
, and finally, let
Y_{0}=I_{G}\cup I^{\prime}
,
Y_{0}^{\prime}=(I_{G}\setminus u)\cup I^{\prime}\cup v
.

Let
\tau
be the coupling time of
(Y_{t},Y_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
and let
c>0
be a sufficiently small constant to be chosen later. Let

\zeta=\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}_{(Y_{0},Y_{0}^{\prime})\sim\gamma}[f(Y_{t})+f(Y_{t}^{\prime})].

From
Equation

29
, it follows that
\zeta=O_{\Delta}(\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[f])
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\left|\mathbb{E}_{\nu}(P_{\mu}h-h)-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}(P_{\nu}h-h)\right|^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}f}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{n^{2}}\frac{\left|\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}_{(Y_{0},Y_{0}^{\prime})\sim\gamma}[\sum_{t}(f(Y_{t})-f(Y_{t}^{\prime}))]\right|^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}f}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}f}\left(\sum_{t}e^{-ct/n}\zeta\right)\cdot\sum_{t}e^{ct/n}\frac{(\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}_{(Y_{0},Y_{0}^{\prime})\sim\gamma}[f(Y_{t})-f(Y_{t}^{\prime})])^{2}}{\zeta}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\Delta}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t\leq\tau}\exp(ct/n)\frac{(\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}_{(Y_{0},Y_{0}^{\prime})\sim\gamma}[f(Y_{t})-f(Y_{t}^{\prime})])^{2}}{\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}_{(Y_{0},Y_{0}^{\prime})\sim\gamma}[f(Y_{t})+f(Y_{t}^{\prime})]}

(33)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}_{(Y_{0},Y_{0}^{\prime})\sim\gamma}\sum_{t\leq\tau}\exp(ct/n)\left|\sqrt{f(Y_{t})}-\sqrt{f(Y_{t}^{\prime})}\right|^{2},

where the second and last inequalities are Cauchy-Schwarz.
For a distribution
\Gamma
on
(Y_{0},Y_{0}^{\prime})
, let

\displaystyle H(\Gamma):=\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}_{(Y_{0},Y_{0}^{\prime})\sim\Gamma}\sum_{t\leq\tau}\exp(ct/n)|\sqrt{f(Y_{t})}-\sqrt{f(Y_{t}^{\prime})}|^{2}.

Our last step will be to bound
H(\gamma)
.

Lemma 37
.

There are absolute constants
c
and
C
such that for
1/(C\Delta^{1000})\leq k/n\leq 1/(C\Delta^{8})
,

H(\gamma)\leq 2n\cdot\mathbb{E}_{(Y_{0},Y_{0}^{\prime})\sim\gamma}\left[\sqrt{f(Y_{0})}-\sqrt{f(Y_{0}^{\prime})}\right]^{2}

Proof.

We will bound
H(\gamma)
using a recursion derived via first-step analysis. To this end, we describe the first step of the coupling of the trajectories, started from independent sets
(Y_{0},Y_{0}^{\prime})
in the support of
\gamma
. By definition,
Y_{0}\neq Y_{0}^{\prime}
with
u\in Y_{0}
and
Y_{0}^{\prime}=P_{u,v}(Y_{0})
for some
v\notin G
. We pick two vertices
a,b\in\mathcal{G}
uniformly at random. If
a
is equal to
u
or
v
, then we set
Y_{1}=P_{u,b}(Y_{0}),Y_{1}^{\prime}=P_{v,b}(Y_{0}^{\prime})
. Else, if
b
is equal to
u
or
v
, then we set
Y_{1}=P_{a,u}(Y_{0}),Y_{1}^{\prime}=P_{a,v}(Y_{0}^{\prime})
. If neither
a
nor
b
equals
u
or
v
, we have two cases: if at least one of
a
or
b
is in
G
, we set
Y_{1}=Y_{0},Y_{1}^{\prime}=Y_{0}^{\prime}
, else we set
Y_{1}=P_{a,b}(Y_{0})
,
Y_{1}^{\prime}=P_{a,b}(Y_{0}^{\prime})
.

Note that there are three possibilities: (i)
Y_{1}=Y_{1}^{\prime}
, (ii)
Y_{1}^{\prime}=P_{u,v}(Y_{1})
, (iii)
Y_{1}=P_{w,w^{\prime}}(Y_{0}),Y_{1}^{\prime}=Y_{0}^{\prime}
for some
w\in Y_{0}\setminus(G\cup v)
and
w^{\prime}\in N_{-}(v)
. Let
\rho
denote the probability of the third case. The probability of the first case is at least
1/n
, and hence, the probability of the second case is at most
(1-\rho-1/n)
. Note that
\rho\leq 2k/n\cdot\Delta/n
. Also, observe that the distribution of
(Y_{1},Y_{1}^{\prime})
conditioned on the event that
Y_{1}^{\prime}=P_{u,v}(Y_{1})
coincides with
\gamma
. Therefore, we may write:

\displaystyle H(\gamma)\leq\mathbb{E}_{(Y_{0},Y_{0}^{\prime})\sim\gamma}[\sqrt{f(Y_{0})}-\sqrt{f(Y_{0}^{\prime})}]^{2}+e^{c/n}(1-\rho-1/n)H(\gamma)+e^{c/n}\rho\cdot H((Y_{1},Y_{1}^{\prime})|\text{(iii)}).

We now bound the third term. With notation as above, we consider the following distribution on ‘paths’ between
Y_{1}
and
Y_{1}^{\prime}(=Y_{0}^{\prime})
. Let
\delta
be a uniformly random vertex outside the distance two neighborhood of
Y_{0}\cup Y_{0}^{\prime}
and consider the path
Y_{1}\to Z_{0}\to Z_{1}\to Z_{2}\to Y_{0}\to Y_{1}^{\prime}
where
Z_{0}=P_{u,\delta}(Y_{1})
,
Z_{1}=P_{u,w^{\prime}}(Z_{0})
,
Z_{2}=P_{u,w}(Z_{1})
(so that
Y_{0}=P_{u,\delta}(Z_{2})
), and as before,
Y_{1}^{\prime}=Y_{0}^{\prime}=P_{u,v}(Y_{0})
. Observe that, over the randomness of
\gamma
,
w^{\prime}\in N_{-}(v)
has relative density
O(\Delta)
with respect to the uniform distribution on vertices in
\mathcal{G}\setminus G
. Moreover, by
Equation

3
, over the randomness of
\gamma
, the distribution of
w^{\prime}
has relative density
O(1)
with respect to the uniform distribution on vertices in
\mathcal{G}\setminus G
. It follows that each of the five distributions
(Y_{1},Z_{0}),(Z_{1},Z_{0}),(Z_{1},Z_{2}),(Y_{0},Z_{2}),(Y_{0},Y_{1}^{\prime})
has density
O(\Delta)
with respect to
\gamma
. Therefore, by interpolating along this path and using Cauchy-Schwarz, we may bound

\displaystyle H((Y_{1},Y_{1}^{\prime})|\text{(iii)})\lesssim\Delta\cdot H(\gamma);

thus

\displaystyle H(\gamma)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{(Y_{0},Y_{0}^{\prime})\sim\gamma}[\sqrt{f(Y_{0})}-\sqrt{f(Y_{0}^{\prime})}]^{2}+e^{c/n}(1-1/n+O(\Delta\rho))H(\gamma)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{(Y_{0},Y_{0}^{\prime})\sim\gamma}[\sqrt{f(Y_{0})}-\sqrt{f(Y_{0}^{\prime})}]^{2}+(1-1/2n)H(\gamma),

provided we pick
c=1/1000
and
k/n\leq\Delta^{-8}/C
for a sufficiently large absolute constant
C
. From this, we immediately get the required estimate

H(\gamma)\leq 2n\cdot\mathbb{E}_{(Y_{0},Y_{0}^{\prime})\sim\gamma}\left[\sqrt{f(Y_{0})}-\sqrt{f(Y_{0}^{\prime})}\right]^{2}.\qed
