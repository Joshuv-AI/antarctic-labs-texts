Fast Forward-Backward splitting for monotone inclusions with a convergence rate of the tangent residual of o ⁡ ( 1 / k ) o(1/k)
Radu Ioan Boţ, Dang-Khoa Nguyen, Chunxiang Zong

Abstract
We address the problem of finding the zeros of the sum of a maximally monotone operator and a cocoercive operator. Our approach introduces a modification to the forward-backward method by integrating an inertial/momentum term alongside a correction term. We demonstrate that the sequence of iterations thus generated converges weakly towards a solution for the monotone inclusion problem. Furthermore, our analysis reveals an outstanding attribute of our algorithm: it displays rates of convergence of the order o ⁡ ( 1 / k ) o(1/k) for the discrete velocity and the tangent residual approaching zero. These rates for tangent residuals can be extended to fixed-point residuals frequently discussed in the existing literature. Specifically, when applied to minimize a nonsmooth convex function subject to linear constraints, our method evolves into a primal-dual full splitting algorithm. Notably, alongside the convergence of iterates, this algorithm possesses a remarkable characteristic of nonergodic/last iterate o ⁡ ( 1 / k ) o(1/k) convergence rates for both the function value and the feasibility measure. Our algorithm showcases the most advanced convergence and convergence rate outcomes among primal-dual full splitting algorithms when minimizing nonsmooth convex functions with linear constraints.

Introduction and related works

1.1
Forward-Backward splitting

A multitude of problems in optimization, variational inequalities, partial differential equations, mechanics, economics, game theory, and signal and image processing can be streamlined by solving a monotone inclusion. Monotone inclusions seeking to determine a zero of the sum of two maximally monotone operators are efficiently solved using the splitting paradigm. This approach involves evaluating the two operators separately from each other
[
10
,
40
]
. Depending on the nature of the operators, different splitting approaches have been considered. These include
Douglas-Rachford splitting

[
28
,
20
]
,
Forward-Backward splitting

[
28
,
38
]
,
Tseng’s Forward-Backward-Forward splitting

[
45
]
, and
Forward-Reflected-Backward splitting

[
31
]
.

In this paper, our focal point is the
Forward-Backward splitting
approach, with our aim being to notably improve its convergence rate while maintaining its inherent convergence properties.

To introduce the problem, let
\mathcal{H}
be a real Hilbert space,
M\colon\mathcal{H}\to 2^{\mathcal{H}}
a maximally monotone operator and
C\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
a
\beta
-cocoercive operator with parameter
\beta>0
, that is

\left\langle C\left(z\right)-C\left(y\right),z-y\right\rangle\geq\beta\left\lVert C\left(z\right)-C\left(y\right)\right\rVert^{2}\quad\forall z,y\in\mathcal{H}.

We aim to solve the monotone inclusion problem

0\in M\left(z\right)+C\left(z\right),

(1.1)

and assume that it has a solution, namely,

{\cal Z}:=\textrm{zer}\left(M+C\right)=\left\{z\in\mathcal{H}\colon M\left(z\right)+C\left(z\right)\right\}\neq\emptyset.

The
Forward-Backward splitting
(FBS) algorithm, introduced by Passty
[
38
]
, stands out as the most successful and widely used method for solving (
1.1
). In its classical formulation, given an initial point
z_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
, the algorithm generates a sequence
(z_{k})_{k\geq 0}
using the following iteration

(\forall k\geq 0)\quad z_{k+1}:=J_{\gamma M}\left(z_{k}-\gamma C\left(z_{k}\right)\right),

(1.2)

where
\gamma\in(0,2\beta)
represents a fixed step size. Here, we define

J_{\gamma M}\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H},\quad J_{\gamma M}:=\left(\mathrm{Id}+\gamma M\right)^{-1},

as the
resolvent
of the operator
M
with parameter
\gamma>0
, where
\mathrm{Id}\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
represents the
identity operator
.

The FBS algorithm leverages the specific characteristics of each operator and treats them accordingly. As
C
is single-valued, it is naturally addressed through a
forward step
. Conversely, the maximal monotonicity of
M
ensures that its resolvent
J_{\gamma M}
is a single-valued operator, universally defined, and
1
-cocoercive and is therefore addresses through a
backward step
. However, closed-form expressions for the resolvents of certain operator classes arising in applications are readily available. Regarding its convergence properties, the sequence
(z_{k})_{k\geq 0}
weakly converges to a solution of (
1.1
) as
k\rightarrow+\infty
. The FBS algorithm (
1.2
) and its variations have been extensively explored in
[
22
,
19
,
21
,
23
]
.

The rate of convergence of the FBS algorithm is typically assessed using quantities such as

\left\lVert z_{k}-z_{k-1}\right\rVert\quad\textrm{ and }\quad r_{\mathrm{fix},\gamma}\left(z_{k}\right):=\left\lVert z_{k}-J_{\gamma M}\left(z_{k}-\gamma C\left(z_{k}\right)\right)\right\rVert.

These represent the
discrete velocity
and the
fixed-point residual
, respectively. While these formulas coincide in the context of the classical FBS algorithm (
1.2
), they may differ for its various variants. However, the notable significance attributed to the fixed-point residual is motivated by the fact that the monotone inclusion problem (
1.1
) can be expressed equivalently as a fixed-point problem. Precisely, for any
\gamma>0
, the equivalence holds

0\in M\left(z\right)+C\left(z\right)\Leftrightarrow z-\gamma C\left(z\right)\in z+\gamma M\left(z\right)\Leftrightarrow z=J_{\gamma M}\left(z-\gamma C\left(z\right)\right).

(1.3)

This demonstrates that the FBS algorithm can be viewed as an application of the Krasnosel’skiĭ-Mann method
[
10
,
40
]
to a specific fixed-point problem. Consequently, the convergence rate of the FBS method can be derived from the more general Krasnosel’skiĭ-Mann iteration
[
9
,
16
,
24
,
32
]
, and therefore it can be expressed as

r_{\mathrm{fix},\gamma}\left(z_{k}\right)=\left\lVert z_{k}-J_{\gamma M}\left(z_{k}-\gamma C\left(z_{k}\right)\right)\right\rVert=o\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\right)\textrm{ as }k\to+\infty.

1.2
Related works

Over the past two decades, there has been a considerable surge of interest in the optimization community and related fields, focusing on accelerating the convergence speed of first-order methods. It was early recognized that inertial/momentum effects possess the capability to expedite convergence without significantly increasing computational effort
[
34
,
35
,
36
,
11
,
18
,
7
,
4
]
.

In the context of minimizing a differentiable function
\phi\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
, Polyak introduced the
Heavy Ball Method
in
[
39
]
, given by

(\forall k\geq 1)\quad z_{k+1}:=z_{k}+\alpha\left(z_{k}-z_{k-1}\right)-\gamma\nabla\phi\left(z_{k}\right),

(1.4)

which originates from the discretization of the second-order dynamical system

\ddot{z}\left(t\right)+\mu\dot{z}\left(t\right)+\nabla\phi\left(z\left(t\right)\right)=0.

Subsequent extensive research on this dynamical system was conducted by Attouch, Goudou, and Redont in
[
6
]
.

In
[
1
]
, Alvarez examined the implicit counterpart of (
1.4
) to address situations where
\phi
might lack differentiability. Subsequently, Alvarez, in collaboration with Attouch
[
3
,
2
]
, extended this approach to solve monotone inclusions
0\in M(z)
, where
M\colon\mathcal{H}\to 2^{\mathcal{H}}
represents a maximally monotone operator. Given the initial points
z_{0},z_{1}\in\mathcal{H}
and a step size
\gamma>0
, they proposed the iterative process

(\forall k\geq 1)\quad z_{k+1}-z_{k}-\alpha_{k}\left(z_{k}-z_{k-1}\right)+\gamma M\left(z_{k+1}\right)\ni 0,

called
inertial Proximal Point Method
which, in terms of the resolvent, can be equivalently written as

(\forall k\geq 1)\quad z_{k+1}:=J_{\gamma M}\left(z_{k}+\alpha_{k}\left(z_{k}-z_{k-1}\right)\right).

(1.5)

The authors demonstrated that, subject to suitable conditions on
\left(\alpha_{k}\right)_{k\geq 1}\in[0,1)
, the sequence
\left(z_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
converges weakly to a zero of
M
as
k\to+\infty
.

Over the past decade, various approaches have emerged in the literature focusing on integrating inertial effects into the resolution of monotone inclusions with additive structure, as represented in (
1.1
). In
[
33
]
, Moudafi and Oliny introduced the following iterative scheme

(\forall k\geq 1)\quad z_{k+1}:=J_{\gamma M}\left(z_{k}+\alpha_{k}\left(z_{k}-z_{k-1}\right)-\gamma C\left(z_{k}\right)\right).

Inspired by algorithms designed for optimization problems involving inertial effects
(see
[
34
,
35
,
36
,
11
]
), Lorenz and Pock intoduced the
inertial Forward-Backward splitting
algorithm in
[
29
]
, expressed by

(\forall k\geq 1)\quad\left\{\begin{aligned} y_{k}&:=z_{k}+\alpha_{k}(z_{k}-z_{k-1});\\
z_{k+1}&:=J_{\gamma M}\left(y_{k}-\gamma C\left(y_{k}\right)\right).\end{aligned}\right.

(1.6)

Although convergence of the iterates was demonstrated, no convergence rate results could be provided. Moreover, the convergence of iterates was achieved under an assumption regarding the sequence
\left(\alpha_{k}\right)_{k\geq 1}
, necessitating either an adaptive selection dependent on the previously generated iterates or an upper bound constraint of
1/3
.

The
Relaxed Inertial Forward-Backward splitting
algorithm, introduced by Attouch and Cabot in
[
5
]
to address (
1.1
), is defined as follows:

(\forall k\geq 1)\quad\left\{\begin{aligned} y_{k}&:=z_{k}+\alpha_{k}(z_{k}-z_{k-1});\\
z_{k+1}&:=(1-\rho_{k})y_{k}+\rho_{k}J_{\mu_{k}M}\left(y_{k}-\mu_{k}C(y_{k})\right),\end{aligned}\right.

(1.7)

where
\left(\alpha_{k}\right)_{k\geq 1},\left(\rho_{k}\right)_{k\geq 1}
, and
\left(\mu_{k}\right)_{k\geq 1}
are positive sequences. In
[
8
]
, Attouch and Peypouquet demonstrated that the incorporation of relaxation parameters
\left(\rho_{k}\right)_{k\geq 1}
in the iterative scheme allows for a more flexible allows for a more flexible selection of the inertial parameters
\left(\alpha_{k}\right)_{k\geq 1}
than the previously described.

Driven by their remarkable success in optimization, methods aimed at accelerating the convergence speed of algorithms for monotone inclusions have awakened the interest of the scientific community in recent years. Using the performance estimate problem technique, Kim derived in
[
27
]
an
Accelerated Proximal Point Method
(APPM) tailored for finding a zero of a maximally monotone operator within a finite-dimensional setting. Given the initial points
y_{-1}=y_{0}=z_{0}=z_{1}\in\mathcal{H}
, the algorithm is expressed as

(\forall k\geq 1)\quad\left\{\begin{aligned} y_{k}&:=z_{k}+\alpha_{k}(z_{k}-z_{k-1})+\alpha_{k}\left(y_{k-2}-z_{k-1}\right);\\
z_{k+1}&:=J_{\gamma M}\left(y_{k}\right).\end{aligned}\right.

(1.8)

This algorithm enhances the first update rule of (
1.5
) with the
correction term
“
y_{k-2}-z_{k-1}
”. For
\alpha_{k}=1-\frac{2}{k+1}
for every
k\geq 1
, (
1.8
) displays a convergence rate for the fixed-point residual

\left\lVert y_{k}-J_{\gamma M}\left(y_{k}\right)\right\rVert=\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{1}{k}\right)\textrm{ as }k\to+\infty.

The convergence of the iterates generated by (
1.5
) was not addressed in this work.

Recently, in
[
30
]
, Maingé introduced the
Corrected Relaxed Inertial Forward-Backward Algorithm
(CRIFBA), achieved by integrating in (
1.2
) a constant relaxation factor, an inertial/momentum term and a correction term. With initial points
y_{0},z_{0},z_{1}\in\mathcal{H}
, the iterative scheme is expressed as

(\forall k\geq 1)\quad\left\{\begin{aligned} y_{k}&:=z_{k}+\alpha_{k}(z_{k}-z_{k-1})+\delta_{k}(y_{k-1}-z_{k});\\
z_{k+1}&:=(1-\rho)y_{k}+\rho J_{\gamma M}\left(y_{k}-\gamma C(y_{k})\right),\end{aligned}\right.

(1.9)

where
\left(\alpha_{k}\right)_{k\geq 1}\subseteq[0,1)
,
\left(\delta_{k}\right)_{k\geq 1}\subseteq[0,1)
,
\rho\in(0,1)
, and
\gamma>0
. Maingé’s algorithm incorporates a correction term, denoted as “
y_{k-1}-z_{k}
”, which carries a coefficient that may be distinct from that of the momentum term. Under conditions where
\alpha_{k}\sim 1-\frac{1}{k}
and
\delta_{k}\sim 1-\frac{1}{k}
, the weak convergence of the iterate sequence to a zero of
M+C
is demonstrated. Moreover, convergence rates of
o(1/k)
are established for both the discrete velocity and the fixed-point residual. The selection of the sequence of inertial parameters
\left(\alpha_{k}\right)_{k\geq 1}
aligns with those used in fast numerical methods for convex optimization (see
[
18
,
7
,
4
]
).

As (
1.1
) can be reformulated as a fixed-point problem (see (
1.3
)), accelerated Forward-Backward splitting methods can be directly derived from techniques designed for fixed-point problems. One such technique, aiming to expedite the convergence of fixed-point problems, traces its origins to the Halpern iteration
[
25
]
and involves incorporating an anchoring point
[
47
,
44
,
37
,
17
]
. In
[
43
]
, Tran-Dinh highlighted connections between this approach and methods utilizing an inertial/momentum term, particularly for specific parameter choices.

Recently, in
[
15
]
, we derived an iterative scheme for solving (
1.1
) from the general
Fast Krasnosel’skiĭ-Mann algorithm
(fKM) introduced for fixed-point problems (see also
[
13
]
). With initial points
z_{0},z_{1}\in\mathcal{H}
,
\alpha>2
, step size
0<\gamma<2\beta
and
0<s<2-\frac{\gamma}{2\beta}
, the iterative scheme is defined as

\displaystyle(\forall k\geq 1)\quad z_{k+1}:=

\displaystyle\ \left(1-\dfrac{s\alpha}{2\left(k+\alpha\right)}\right)z_{k}+\dfrac{(1-s)k}{k+\alpha}(z_{k}-z_{k-1})+\dfrac{s(\alpha+2k)}{2(k+\alpha)}J_{\gamma M}\left(z_{k}-\gamma C\left(z_{k}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\ -\dfrac{sk}{k+\alpha}J_{\gamma M}\left(z_{k-1}-\gamma C\left(z_{k-1}\right)\right).

(1.10)

The resulting iterates converge weakly to a solution of (
1.1
). Furthermore, the algorithm exhibits a convergence rate of
o\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)
for both the discrete velocity
\left\lVert z_{k}-z_{k-1}\right\rVert
and the fixed-point residual
r_{\mathrm{fix},\gamma}\left(z_{k}\right)
as
k\rightarrow+\infty
.

1.3
Our contributions

We propose a
Fast Forward-Backward algorithm
to solve (
1.1
), incorporating an inertial/momentum term alongside a correction term. For the sequence
(z_{k})_{k\geq 0}
generated by our method, we demonstrate that both the discrete velocity
\left\lVert z_{k}-z_{k-1}\right\rVert
and the tangent residual
r_{\mathrm{tan}}\left(z_{k}\right)
converge to zero with a rate of
o\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)
as
k\rightarrow+\infty
. The
tangent residual
is defined as

r_{\mathrm{tan}}\left(z\right):={\rm dist}(0,M(z)+C(z))=\inf_{\xi\in M(z)}\left\lVert\xi+C\left(z\right)\right\rVert,

and it holds (see Lemma
A.5
in the Appendix) that

r_{\mathrm{fix},\gamma}\left(z\right)=\left\lVert z-J_{\gamma M}\left(z-\gamma C\left(z\right)\right)\right\rVert\leq\gamma{\rm dist}(0,M(z)+C(z))=\gamma r_{\mathrm{tan}}\left(z\right)\quad\forall z\in\mathcal{H}.

For discussions concerning these convegence measures in the context of
variational inequality
problems, we refer to
[
42
]
.

Hence, the convergence rate of
o\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)
as
k\rightarrow+\infty
for the fixed-point residual immediately follows. Moreover, we will establish the weak convergence of the sequence
(z_{k})_{k\geq 0}
to a solution of (
1.1
) as
k\rightarrow+\infty
.

As a significant application case, we derive a primal-dual full splitting algorithm for solving optimization problems of the form

\displaystyle\min\limits_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f\left(x\right)+h\left(x\right),

\displaystyle\textnormal{subject to }Ax=b.

(1.11)

Here,
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
represent real Hilbert spaces, where
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is a proper, convex and lower semicontinuous function,
h\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is a convex and differentiable function with a gradient that is
\beta^{-1}
-Lipschitz continuous (
\beta>0
), and
A\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
is a linear continuous operator. The primal-dual algorithm is derived from the general iterative scheme via the product space approach by Vũ
[
46
]
.

Given the generated sequence of primal-dual iterates
\left(x_{k},\lambda_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
, we observe convergence rates for the velocities

\left\lVert x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\rVert=o\left(\dfrac{1}{k}\right)\qquad\textrm{ and }\qquad\left\lVert\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{k-1}\right\rVert=o\left(\dfrac{1}{k}\right)\qquad\textrm{ and }\qquad k\to+\infty.

Moreover, we derive convergence rates for the
function values
and the
feasibility measure
:

\left\lvert\left(f+h\right)\left(x_{k}\right)-\left(f+h\right)\left(x_{*}\right)\right\rvert=o\left(\dfrac{1}{k}\right)\qquad\textrm{ and }\qquad\left\lVert Ax_{k}-b\right\rVert=o\left(\dfrac{1}{k}\right)\qquad\textrm{ and }\qquad k\to+\infty,

respectively. Here,
x_{*}
denotes an optimal solution of (
1.3
) and
\left(f+h\right)\left(x_{*}\right)
its optimal objective value. The convergence results are derived from the rates of convergence for the tangent residual in the monotone inclusion problem. It is important to highlight that deriving such statements from the convergence rate results based on fixed-point residual seems unattainable. Additionally, we establish the weak convergence of
\left(x_{k},\lambda_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
to a primal-dual optimal solution.

Finally, we verify the theoretical findings by assessing the performance of the proposed algorithm through numerical experiments.

2
The proposed algorithm

In this section, we formulate the algorithm and conduct a thorough analysis of its convergence.

Algorithm 2.1
.

Let
\alpha>2,\gamma>0
,
z_{0},y_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
, and
z_{1}:=J_{\gamma M}\left(y_{0}-\gamma C\left(z_{0}\right)\right)
. We set

(\forall k\geq 1)\quad\begin{cases}y_{k}&:=z_{k}+\left(1-\dfrac{\alpha}{k+\alpha}\right)\left(z_{k}-z_{k-1}\right)+\left(1-\dfrac{\alpha}{2\left(k+\alpha\right)}\right)\left(y_{k-1}-z_{k}\right);\\
z_{k+1}&:=J_{\gamma M}\left(y_{k}-\gamma C\left(z_{k}\right)\right).\end{cases}

(2.1)

In the following, we provide an equivalent formulation for
2.1
, which will play a central role in the convergence analysis.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
z_{0},y_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
,
z_{1}:=J_{\gamma M}\left(y_{0}-\gamma C\left(z_{0}\right)\right)
, and
\xi_{1}:=\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(y_{0}-z_{1}\right)-C\left(z_{0}\right)\in M\left(z_{1}\right)
. Then the sequence
(z_{k})_{k\geq 0}
generated in
2.1
can also be generated equivalently by the following iterative scheme

(\forall k\geq 1)\quad\!\!\!\begin{cases}z_{k+1}\!\!&:=J_{\gamma M}\left(z_{k}-\gamma C\left(z_{k}\right)+\left(1-\dfrac{\alpha}{k+\alpha}\right)\left(z_{k}-z_{k-1}\right)+\frac{2k+\alpha}{2(k+\alpha)}\gamma\left(\xi_{k}+C\left(z_{k-1}\right)\right)\right);\\
\xi_{k+1}\!\!&:=\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(z_{k}-z_{k+1}+\left(1-\dfrac{\alpha}{k+\alpha}\right)(z_{k}-z_{k-1})\right)+\frac{2k+\alpha}{2(k+\alpha)}\left(\xi_{k}+C\left(z_{k-1}\right)\right)-C\left(z_{k}\right).\end{cases}

(2.2)

In addition, it holds

\xi_{k}\in M\left(z_{k}\right)\qquad\quad\forall k\geq 1.

(2.3)

Proof.

Given
z_{0},y_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
and
z_{1}:=J_{\gamma M}\left(y_{0}-\gamma C\left(z_{0}\right)\right)
,
it follows that

\Leftrightarrow\xi_{1}=\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(y_{0}-z_{1}\right)-C\left(z_{0}\right)\in M\left(z_{1}\right).

In the same way, by invoking also the second update block of
2.1
, we obtain

\xi_{k+1}:=\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(y_{k}-z_{k+1}\right)-C\left(z_{k}\right)\in M(z_{k+1})\quad\forall k\geq 0.

Therefore, the iterative scheme in
2.1
can be equivalently written as

\ (\forall k\geq 1)\quad\left\{\begin{aligned} y_{k}&:=z_{k}+\left(1-\dfrac{\alpha}{k+\alpha}\right)\left(z_{k}-z_{k-1}\right)+\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2(k+\alpha)}\right)\gamma\left(\xi_{k}+C\left(z_{k-1}\right)\right);\\
z_{k+1}&:=J_{\gamma M}\left(y_{k}-\gamma C\left(z_{k}\right)\right);\\
\xi_{k+1}&:=\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(y_{k}-z_{k+1}\right)-C\left(z_{k}\right),\end{aligned}\right.

(2.4)

which, after some simplifications, it transforms into (
2.2
).

Conversely, starting from (
2.2
), we can define a sequence
\left(y_{k}\right)_{k\geq 1}
following the first update block in (
2.4
). Consequently, for every
k\geq 1
, the relation

\gamma\left(\xi_{k+1}+C\left(z_{k}\right)\right)=y_{k}-z_{k+1},

(2.5)

holds, leading to the transformation of (
2.2
) into (
2.1
).
∎

Remark 2.3
.

\rm{(i)}

Remarkably, within the first update block of (
2.1
), we incorporate both a
momentum term
denoted as “
z_{k}-z_{k-1}
” and a
correction term
denoted as “
y_{k-1}-z_{k}
”. Nevertheless, in the forward-backward step, our method evaluates
C
at
z_{k}
, thereby it can be seen as an extension of
[
33
]
. Notably, it differes significantly from other forward-backward schemes in the existing literature.

\rm{(ii)}

The apparent similarity between
2.1
and the CRIFBA method is misleading. While the initial update block may appear similar, a significant conceptual distinction arises due to our scheme evaluating
C
at
z_{k}
, contrasting with the CRIFBA method’s evaluation of
C
at
y_{k}
. This disparity leads to a substantial difference in how the correction terms are defined, even when opting for
\rho=1
in the CRFIBA method. Notably, this falls outside the scope of the convergence evidence established in
[
30
]
.

The reformulation (
2.2
) reveals some connection with other Nesterov’s acceleration-type methods in the literature. This inspires us to consider, for
z_{*}\in{\cal Z}
, the following discrete energy function
\mathcal{E}_{\eta,k}
for
0\leq\eta\leq\alpha-1
and
k\geq 1
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\eta,k}:=

\displaystyle\ \dfrac{1}{2}\left\lVert 2\eta\left(z_{k}-z_{*}\right)+2k\left(z_{k}-z_{k-1}\right)+\frac{5\alpha-2}{4(\alpha-1)}\gamma k\left(\xi_{k}+C\left(z_{k-1}\right)\right)\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle+2\eta\left(\alpha-1-\eta\right)\left\lVert z_{k}-z_{*}\right\rVert^{2}+2\eta\gamma\left(\frac{3(\alpha-2)}{4(\alpha-1)}k+\alpha\right)\big\langle z_{k}-z_{*},\xi_{k}+C\left(z_{k-1}\right)\big\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}\left(\frac{3(\alpha-2)}{4(\alpha-1)}k+\alpha\right)\left(\frac{5\alpha-2}{4(\alpha-1)}k+\alpha\right)\left\lVert\xi_{k}+C\left(z_{k-1}\right)\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ 2\eta\left(\alpha-1\right)\left\lVert z_{k}-z_{*}\right\rVert^{2}+4\eta k\big\langle z_{k}-z_{*},z_{k}-z_{k-1}+\gamma(\xi_{k}+C\left(z_{k-1}\right))\big\rangle

\displaystyle+2\eta\gamma\alpha\langle z_{k}-z_{*},\xi_{k}+C\left(z_{k-1}\right)\rangle+\dfrac{1}{2}k^{2}\left\lVert 2\left(z_{k}-z_{k-1}\right)+\frac{5\alpha-2}{4(\alpha-1)}\gamma(\xi_{k}+C\left(z_{k-1}\right))\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}\left(\frac{3(\alpha-2)}{4(\alpha-1)}k+\alpha\right)\left(\frac{5\alpha-2}{4(\alpha-1)}k+\alpha\right)\left\lVert\xi_{k}+C\left(z_{k-1}\right)\right\rVert^{2}.

(2.6)

\displaystyle\|\xi_{k+1}+C\left(z_{k+1}\right)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|C\left(z_{k+1}\right)-C\left(z_{k}\right)\|+\|\xi_{k+1}+C\left(z_{k}\right)\|\leq\frac{\nu_{15}}{k},

where
\nu_{15}:=\nu_{13}+\nu_{14}>0
. Since
(z_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is bounded, we deduce from here that for every
k\geq K_{5}(\eta)

0\leq\langle z_{k+1}-z_{*},\xi_{k+1}+C\left(z_{k+1}\right)\rangle\leq\left\lVert z_{k+1}-z_{*}\right\rVert\|\xi_{k+1}+C\left(z_{k+1}\right)\|\leq\frac{\nu_{12}\nu_{15}}{k}.

In conclusion,

\displaystyle\left\lVert z_{k}-z_{k-1}\right\rVert=\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{1}{k}\right),\,\quad\left\lVert\xi_{k}+C\left(z_{k-1}\right)\right\rVert=\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{1}{k}\right)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\xi_{k}+C\left(z_{k}\right)\right\rVert=\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{1}{k}\right),\,\quad\left\langle z_{k}-z_{*},\xi_{k}+C\left(z_{k}\right)\right\rangle=\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{1}{k}\right)

as
k\rightarrow+\infty
.

In the remainder of the proof we will show that the sequence
(\mathcal{E}_{\eta,k})_{k\geq 1}
is convergent and that it has the same limit as
(\mathcal{F}_{\eta,k})_{k\geq 1}
. On the one hand, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality yields

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}k^{2}\big|\langle z_{k}-z_{k-1},C\left(z_{k}\right)-C\left(z_{k-1}\right)\rangle\big|\leq\nu_{11}\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}k\big\|C\left(z_{k}\right)-C\left(z_{k-1}\right)\big\|=0,

therefore

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}k^{2}\langle z_{k}-z_{k-1},C\left(z_{k}\right)-C\left(z_{k-1}\right)\rangle=0.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle 0\leq\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}\sqrt{k}\left\lVert C\left(z_{k}\right)-C\left(z_{k-1}\right)\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}k\sqrt{k}\left\lVert C\left(z_{k}\right)-C\left(z_{k-1}\right)\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}k^{2}\left\lVert C\left(z_{k}\right)-C\left(z_{k-1}\right)\right\rVert^{2}=0,

which, by using the definitions of the two discrete energy functions, allow us to conclude that

\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}\mathcal{E}_{\eta,k}=\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}\mathcal{F}_{\eta,k}\in\mathbb{R}.

∎

3
A fast primal-dual full splitting algorithm

The aim of this section is to use the Fast Forward-Backward algorithm to construct a primal-dual full splitting algorithm for nonsmooth convex optimization problems with linear constraints that exhibits fast convergence rates as well as convergence of the iterates.

The problem under consideration reads

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rl}\min\limits_{x\in\mathcal{X}}&f\left(x\right)+h\left(x\right),\\
\textrm{subject to}&Ax=b,\end{array}

where
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are real Hilbert spaces,
f\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
is a proper, lower semicontinuous, and convex function,
h\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
convex differentiable function such that
\nabla h
is
\frac{1}{\beta}
-Lipschitz continuous with
\beta>0
, and
A\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
is a linear continuous operator.
Consider the saddle point problem associated to problem (
3
)

\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{\lambda\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{L}\left(x,\lambda\right),

where
\mathcal{L}\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the associated
Lagrangian function

\mathcal{L}\left(x,\lambda\right):=f\left(x\right)+h\left(x\right)+\left\langle\lambda,Ax-b\right\rangle.

A pair
\left(x_{*},\lambda_{*}\right)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
is said to be a
saddle point
of the Lagrangian function
\mathcal{L}
if for every
\left(x,\lambda\right)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}

\mathcal{L}\left(x_{*},\lambda\right)\leq\mathcal{L}\left(x_{*},\lambda_{*}\right)\leq\mathcal{L}\left(x,\lambda_{*}\right).

If
\left(x_{*},\lambda_{*}\right)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
is a saddle point of
\mathcal{L}
, then
x_{*}\in\mathcal{X}
is an optimal solution of (
3
) and
\lambda_{*}\in\mathcal{Y}
is an optimal solution of its Lagrange dual problem. If
x_{*}\in\mathcal{X}
is an optimal solution of (
3
) and a suitable constraint qualification is fulfilled (see, for instance,
[
12
,
10
]
), then there exists an optimal solution
\lambda_{*}\in\mathcal{Y}
of the Lagrange dual problem of (
3
) such that
\left(x_{*},\lambda_{*}\right)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
is a saddle point of
\mathcal{L}
. The set of saddle points of
\mathcal{L}
, called the
set of primal-dual optimal solutions
of (
3
), will be denoted by
\mathcal{S}
.

The system of primal-dual optimality conditions for (
3
) reads

\left(x_{*},\lambda_{*}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\Leftrightarrow\begin{cases}0\in\partial_{x}\mathcal{L}\left(x_{*},\lambda_{*}\right)\\
0=\nabla_{\lambda}\mathcal{L}\left(x_{*},\lambda_{*}\right)\end{cases}\Leftrightarrow\begin{cases}0\in\partial f\left(x_{*}\right)+\nabla h\left(x_{*}\right)+A^{*}\lambda_{*}\\
0=Ax_{*}-b\end{cases},

(3.3)

where
A^{*}:\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
denotes the adjoint operator of
A
.

For
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}=\mathcal{X}\bigoplus\mathcal{Y}
, the problem of finding a primal-dual solution of (
3
) can be equivalently written as the monotone inclusion

\textrm{find}\quad\boldsymbol{z}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}\quad\textrm{such}\quad\textrm{that}\quad\boldsymbol{0}\in\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{z})+\boldsymbol{Q}(\boldsymbol{z}),

(3.4)

where

\ \left\{\begin{aligned} &\boldsymbol{P}\colon\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}\rightarrow 2^{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}}\colon\left(x,\lambda\right)\mapsto\big(\partial f(x)+A^{*}\lambda\big)\times\big(-Ax+b\big),\\
&\boldsymbol{Q}\colon\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}\colon\left(x,\lambda\right)\mapsto(\nabla h(x),0).\end{aligned}\right.

(3.5)

The operator
\boldsymbol{P}
as the sum of the maximally monotone operators
\left(x,\lambda\right)\mapsto\partial f(x)\times\left\{b\right\}
and
\left(x,\lambda\right)\mapsto(A^{*}\lambda,-Ax)
is also a maximally monotone operator. In view of the Baillon-Haddad theorem,
\nabla h
is
\beta
-cocoercive, which means that
\boldsymbol{Q}
is also
\beta
-cocoercive.

Inspired by
[
46
]
, we define the self-adjoint operator

\displaystyle\qquad\boldsymbol{N}\colon\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}},\quad(x,\lambda)\mapsto\left(\frac{1}{\tau}x-A^{*}\lambda,-Ax+\frac{1}{\sigma}\lambda\right),

(3.6)

which is
\rho
-strongly positive with modulus

\rho:=\min\left\{\frac{1}{\tau},\frac{1}{\sigma}\right\}\left(1-\sqrt{\tau\sigma\|A\|^{2}}\right).

Therefore,
its inverse
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
exists and
\|\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\|\leq\frac{1}{\rho}
. This means that
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{P}
is a maximally monotone operator and
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{Q}
is a
\beta\rho
-cocoercive operator. The algorithm below is the Fast Forward-Backward algorithm applied to the problem of finding a zero of
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{P}+\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{Q}
.

Algorithm 3.1
.

Let
\alpha>2
,
\tau,\sigma>0
such that

1<\min\left\{\frac{1}{\tau},\frac{1}{\sigma}\right\}\left(1-\sqrt{\tau\sigma\|A\|^{2}}\right)\frac{8(\alpha-1)}{(5\alpha-2)}\beta,

x_{0},v_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
,
\lambda_{0},\eta_{0}\in\mathcal{Y}
, and
x_{1}:=\textnormal{prox}_{\tau f}\left(v_{0}-\tau A^{*}\eta_{0}-\tau\nabla h\left(x_{0}\right)\right)
and
\lambda_{1}:=\eta_{0}+\sigma\left(Ax_{1}-b\right)+\sigma A\left(x_{1}-v_{0}\right)
. We set

(\forall k\geq 1)\quad\begin{cases}v_{k}&:=x_{k}+\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{k+\alpha}\right)\left(x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right)+\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2(k+\alpha)}\right)\left(v_{k-1}-x_{k}\right);\\
\eta_{k}&:=\lambda_{k}+\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{k+\alpha}\right)(\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{k-1})+\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2(k+\alpha)}\right)\left(\eta_{k-1}-\lambda_{k}\right);\\
x_{k+1}&:=\textnormal{prox}_{\tau f}\left(v_{k}-\tau A^{*}\eta_{k}-\tau\nabla h\left(x_{k}\right)\right);\\
\lambda_{k+1}&:=\eta_{k}+\sigma\left(Ax_{k+1}-b\right)+\sigma A\left(x_{k+1}-v_{k}\right).\end{cases}

Based on
2.9
, we obtain the following convergence and convergence rate results.

Theorem 3.2

as

, where, for every

Proof.

2.1
with step size

to the problem

(3.7)

For the initial points

and

, this gives rise to the following iterative scheme

\end{aligned}\right.

(3.8)

The second equation (

) can be equivalently written for every

as

and further as

\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{z}_{k+1}+\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k+1})\ni\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{y}_{k}-\boldsymbol{Q}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k}).

(3.9)

By denoting
\boldsymbol{z}_{k}:=(x_{k},\lambda_{k})
and

for every

, one can see that the first equation in (

.

Sabach and Teboulle proposed in
[
41
]
in finite-dimensional spaces a class of
faster Lagrangian-based methods
(FLAG), which, when applied to the convex optimization problem (
3

3

Big-O
type. Specifically, this applies to the primal and dual discrete velocities, the primal-dual gap, the feasibility violation measure, and function values. For the latter, we also present a convergence rate from below. Furthermore, we have successfully proven the convergence of the entire primal-dual sequence of iterates to a primal-dual optimal solution.

Our algorithm demonstrates the most advanced convergence and convergence rate results among primal-dual full splitting algorithms in the context of minimizing a nonsmooth convex function under linear constraints. Moreover, the iterative scheme and its convergence results can be readily extended to minimize separable nonsmooth convex optimization functions with linearly coupled block variables (see
[
14
]

be sequences of real numbers. If
(a_{k})_{k\geq 1}

then the following statements are true:

\rm{(i)}

the sequence

is summable, i.e.,
\sum_{k\geq 1}b_{k}<+\infty
;

\rm{(ii)}

the sequence

The following result is stated and proven in

Lemma A.3
.

Let
a\geq 1
and
\left(q_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
be a bounded sequence in
\mathcal{H}
such that

\lim\limits_{k\to+\infty}\left(q_{k+1}+\dfrac{k}{a}\left(q_{k+1}-q_{k}\right)\right)=l\in\mathcal{H}.

Then it holds
\lim\limits_{k\to+\infty}q_{k}=l
.

For the convergence proof of the iterates we use the discrete version of Opial’s Lemma.

Lemma A.4
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a nonempty subset of
\mathcal{H}
and
\left(z_{k}\right)_{k\geq 1}
be a sequence in
\mathcal{H}
.
Assume that

\rm{(i)}

for every

,
\lim\limits_{k\to+\infty}\left\lVert z_{k}-z_{*}\right\rVert
exists;

\rm{(ii)}

every weak sequential cluster point of the sequence
\left(z_{k}\right)_{k\geq 1}
as
k\to+\infty
belongs to
\mathcal{S}
.

Then
\left(z_{k}\right)_{k\geq 1}
converges weakly to a point in
\mathcal{S}
as

.

The following lemma illustrates the relationship between the two convergence measures utilized in this paper (see [Fact 1,
[

]).

Lemma A.5
.

For every
z\in\mathcal{H}
and every
\gamma>0
, it holds

r_{\mathrm{fix},\gamma}(z)=\|z-J_{\gamma M}\big(z-\gamma C(z)\big)\|\leq\gamma\inf_{\xi\in M(z)}\|\xi+C(z)\|=\gamma{\rm dist}(0,M(z)+C(z))=\gamma r_{\mathrm{tan}}(z).

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{fix}}(z)

This proves the statement.
