A hierarchy of eigencomputations for polynomial optimization on the sphere
Benjamin Lovitz, Nathaniel Johnston

Abstract
We introduce a convergent hierarchy of lower bounds on the minimum value of a real form over the unit sphere. The main practical advantage of our hierarchy over the real sum-of-squares (RSOS) hierarchy is that the lower bound at each level of our hierarchy is obtained by a minimum eigenvalue computation, as opposed to the full semidefinite program (SDP) required at each level of RSOS. In practice, this allows us to compute bounds on much larger forms than are computationally feasible for RSOS. Our hierarchy outperforms previous alternatives to RSOS, both asymptotically and in numerical experiments. We obtain our hierarchy by proving a reduction from real optimization on the sphere to Hermitian optimization on the sphere, and invoking the Hermitian sum-of-squares (HSOS) hierarchy. This opens the door to using other Hermitian optimization techniques for real optimization, and gives a path towards developing spectral hierarchies for more general constrained real optimization problems. To this end, we use our techniques to develop a hierarchy of eigencomputations for computing the real tensor spectral norm.

The

in the level

of RSOS when

,

,

]

1.1
The real sum-of-squares hierarchy (RSOS)

In this section we review the RSOS hierarchy for the optimization problem (
1
). By
[
DW12
, Lemma B.2]
, we can (and will) assume
D
is even, and let
d=D/2
.
The RSOS hierarchy is a hierarchy of lower bounds on
p_{\min}
which can be computed by semidefinite programming. Let
\Sigma_{n,k}\subseteq S^{2k}(\mathcal{V}^{*})
(or simply
\Sigma_{k}
when
n
is understood) be the set of forms which are sums of squares of degree-
k
forms.

Theorem 1.1

(RSOS hierarchy)
.

Let
p\in S^{2d}(\mathcal{V}^{*})
be a real form of degree
2d
. For each
k\geq d
, let
\gamma_{k}
be the optimum value of the SDP

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textup{maximize:}\quad&\ \gamma\\
\textup{subject to:}\quad&\ p(x)\cdot\lVert\mspace{1mu}x\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}-\gamma\cdot\lVert\mspace{1mu}x\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2k}\in\Sigma_{k}.\end{split}

(2)

Then
\gamma_{1}\leq\gamma_{2}\leq\dots
and
\lim_{k}\gamma_{k}=p_{\min}
. Furthermore,
p_{\min}-\gamma_{k}=O(1/k)
when
d>n
, and
p_{\min}-\gamma_{k}=O(1/k^{2})
when
d\leq n
.

The limiting statement was proven in
[
Rez95
]
(see also
[
Las01
]
). The convergence rates were proven in
[
Rez95
,
DW12
,
FF21
]
. To see equivalences between this formulation and other, perhaps more familiar, forms of the RSOS hierarchy, see e.g.
[
dKLP05
, Proposition 2]
or
[
Lau19
, Lemma 1.3]
.

1.2
The Hermitian sum-of-squares hierarchy (HSOS)

Let
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathbb{C}^{n}
. A
Hermitian form
of bidegree
(d,d)
is an element
H(z,w^{*})\in S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}^{*})\otimes S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}})
for which
H(z,w^{*})=\overline{H(w,z^{*})}
. Let
\mathrm{Herm}(S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))
be the set of Hermitian forms of bidegree
(d,d)
. By standard polarization arguments,
\mathrm{Herm}(S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))
can be identified with the set of Hermitian
operators
on the vector space
S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}})
(see e.g.
[
D’A11
,
D’A19
]
or Section
3
). A Hermitian analogue to (
1
) is given by

\displaystyle H_{\min}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}\\
\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert=1\end{subarray}}H(z,z^{*}).

(3)

The
Hermitian square
of a form
q(z)\in S^{k}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}^{*})
is given by
|q(z)|^{2}\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{k}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))
. Let
\mathrm{Pos}(S^{k}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{k}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))
be the set of sums of Hermitian squares of degree-
k
forms. This can be identified with the set of positive semidefinite
operators
on the vector space
S^{k}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}})
.

Theorem 1.2

(HSOS hierarchy)
.

Let
H\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))
be a Hermitian form. For each
k\geq d
, let
\mu_{k}
be the optimum value of the SDP

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textup{maximize:}\quad&\ \mu\\
\textup{subject to:}\quad&\ H(z,z^{*})\cdot\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}-\mu\cdot\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2k}\in\mathrm{Pos}(S^{k}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}})).\end{split}

(4)

Then
\mu_{d}\leq\mu_{d+1}\leq\dots
and
\lim_{k}\mu_{k}=H_{\min}
. Furthermore,
H_{\min}-\mu_{k}=O(1/k)
.

The limiting statement was proven in
[
Qui68
,
CD95
]
, and the
O(1/k)
convergence rate was proven in
[
TY06
]
. These results can also be proven using so-called
quantum de Finetti theorems

[
HM76
,
CKMR07
]
, and we will do so in this work.

Remark 1.3
.

Let
H_{k}(z,z^{*}):=H(z,z^{*})\cdot\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{k}(\mathcal{V}))
. The optimum value
\mu_{k}
of the SDP (
4
) is precisely
\lambda_{\min}(H_{k})
, where
\lambda_{\min}(\cdot)
denotes the minimum eigenvalue of the input viewed as a Hermitian operator. So the HSOS hierarchy is in fact a hierarchy of eigencomputations for optimizing a Hermitian form over the sphere.

While RSOS is a hierarchy of full semidefinite programs, HSOS is a hierarchy of mere eigencomputations. This leads us to ask, can we use the HSOS hierarchy for real optimization? The answer is yes.

1.3
Our polynomial optimization hierarchy (HRSOS)

We prove a reduction from the real optimization problem (
1
) to the Hermitian optimization problem (
3
), and then use the HSOS hierarchy to obtain a hierarchy of eigencomputations for (
1
).

Let
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathbb{C}^{n}
be the complexification of
\mathcal{V}
. For a form
p\in S^{2d}(\mathcal{V}^{*})
, let
P\in\mathrm{End}(S^{d}(\mathcal{V}))
be the
maximally symmetric Gram operator
of
p
(see Section
2
,
[
DW12
, Section 3]
, or
[
Lau19
, Lemma 1.2]
). Since
P
is real-symmetric, we can view it as a Hermitian operator. Recall the definitions of
p_{\min}
and
P_{\min}
from (
1
) and (
3
), respectively.

Theorem 1.4
.

For any form
p\in S^{2d}(\mathcal{V}^{*})
, it holds that

\displaystyle P_{\min}\leq p_{\min}\leq\frac{P_{\min}}{\delta(d)},

(5)

where
\delta(d):=2^{-d}\sum_{j=0}^{d}\binom{d}{j}^{2}\binom{2d}{2j}^{-1}
.

In particular, this theorem shows that (
p_{\min}>0\iff P_{\min}>0
). By Theorem
1.2
, we immediately obtain the following hierarchy of eigencomputations to determine if
p_{\min}>0
. For a positive integer
k\geq d
, let
P_{k}(z,z^{*}):=P(z,z^{*})\cdot\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{k}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
p\in S^{2d}(\mathcal{V}^{*})
. If
p_{\min}>0
, then
P_{k}\in\mathrm{Pos}(S^{k}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))
for
k
large enough.

Remark 1.6
.

Viewed as as real operator,
P_{k}
is a
Gram operator
for
p(x)\lVert\mspace{1mu}x\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}
, meaning that

\displaystyle\langle x^{\otimes k},P_{k}x^{\otimes k}\rangle=p(x)\lVert\mspace{1mu}x\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}

(6)

for all
x\in\mathcal{V}
. The RSOS hierarchy says that if
p_{\min}>0
, then there exists a positive semidefinite Gram operator for
p(x)\lVert\mspace{1mu}x\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}
for
k
large enough. By contrast, Theorem
1.5
says that the
single
Gram operator
P_{k}
is positive semidefinite for
k
large enough. We note that Theorem
1.5
does not hold for other choices of Gram operators for
p
; see Remark
4.2
.

More generally, we obtain a hierarchy of eigencomputations for
p_{\min}
with
O(1/k)
convergence.
Let
N^{(d)}
be the canonical Gram operator of
\lVert\mspace{1mu}x\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2d}
, viewed as a Hermitian form, and let

\displaystyle N_{k}^{(d)}(z,z^{*}):=N^{(d)}(z,z^{*})\cdot\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{k}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))

(7)

when
k\geq d
. We obtain the following hierarchy of eigencomputations for
p_{\min}
:

Theorem 1.7

(HRSOS hierarchy)
.

Let
p\in S^{2d}(\mathcal{V}^{*})
. For each
k\geq d
, let

\displaystyle\eta_{k}=\lambda_{\min}((N_{k}^{(d)})^{-1/2}P_{k}(N_{k}^{(d)})^{-1/2}).

(8)

Then
\eta_{d}\leq\eta_{d+1}\leq\dots
and
\lim_{k}\eta_{k}=p_{\min}
. Furthermore,
p_{\min}-\eta_{k}=O(1/k)
.

We prove this using Theorems
1.2
and
1.4
.

1.4
Our tensor optimization hierarchy (m-HRSOS)

More generally, our techniques can be used to minimize the inner product between a real tensor and an element of the spherical Segre-Veronese variety. Let
n_{1},\dots,n_{m}
and
D_{1},\dots,D_{m}
be positive integers, let
\mathcal{V}_{i}=\real^{n_{i}}
for each
i\in[m]
, let
{p}\in S^{D_{1}}(\mathcal{V}_{1}^{*})\otimes\cdots\otimes S^{D_{m}}(\mathcal{V}_{m}^{*})
be a tensor, and consider the minimization problem

\displaystyle p_{\min}:=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}_{j}\\
\lVert\mspace{1mu}v_{j}\mspace{1mu}\rVert=1\end{subarray}}p(v_{1}^{\otimes D_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{m}^{\otimes D_{m}}).

(9)

Tensors of the form
v_{1}^{\otimes D_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes v_{m}^{\otimes D_{m}}
are by definition elements of the
(real) Segre-Veronese variety
.
Our hierarchy extends naturally to this setting, with similar convergence guarantees. We can assume without loss of generality that each
D_{j}=2d_{j}
is even (see Proposition
6.1
). Similarly to before, we associate to
p
a multi-homogeneous Hermitian form
P\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{d_{1}}(\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\mathbb{C}})\otimes\cdots\otimes S^{d_{m}}(\mathcal{V}_{m}^{\mathbb{C}}))
, where
\mathcal{V}_{i}^{\mathbb{C}}:=\mathbb{C}^{n_{i}}
is the complexification of
\mathcal{V}_{i}
. Letting

\displaystyle P_{\min}:=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{j}\in\mathcal{V}_{j}^{\mathbb{C}}\\
\lVert\mspace{1mu}z_{j}\mspace{1mu}\rVert=1\end{subarray}}\langle z_{1}^{\otimes d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes z_{m}^{\otimes d_{m}},Pz_{1}^{\otimes d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes z_{m}^{\otimes d_{m}}\rangle

(10)

be the Hermitian analogue of (
9
), we prove the following reduction from the real optimization problem (
9
) to the Hermitian optimization problem (
10
):

Theorem 1.8
.

For any tensor
p\in S^{2d_{1}}(\mathcal{V}_{1}^{*})\otimes\cdots\otimes S^{2d_{m}}(\mathcal{V}_{m}^{*})
, it holds that

\displaystyle P_{\min}\leq p_{\min}\leq\frac{P_{\min}}{\delta({\bf d})},

(11)

where
\delta({\bf d})=\delta(d_{1})\cdots\delta(d_{m})
, and
\delta(d_{i})
is defined as in Theorem
1.4
.

The quantum de Finetti theorem implies that the optimization problem (
10
) can be solved by a hierarchy of eigencomputations converging as
O(1/k)
in the level
k
. We combine this with Theorem
11
to prove analogous statements to Theorems
1.5
and
1.7
; see Section
6
.

1.5
Related work

Due to the limited capacity of current SDP solvers, there has been much work on developing practical alternatives to the sum-of-squares hierarchy for constrained polynomial optimization problems
[
HR00
,
CS16
,
LTY17
,
DIDW17
,
AM19
,
WM20
,
MLMW22
,
MLM23
,
CV24
]
. In particular, several techniques have been developed for optimization over the real sphere
[
DW12
,
Lau19
,
FF21
,
CV24
]
.

Notably, an optimization-free
harmonic hierarchy
is developed in
[
CV24
]
. This is closely related to harmonic analysis on spheres, and builds on previous work of
[
Ble04
,
FF21
,
Slo22
,
SL23
]
. This hierarchy depends on a choice of cubature rule and kernel, and the authors give an explicit choice for which the harmonic hierarchy converges (multiplicatively) as
O(1/k^{2})
in the level
k
, with the
k
-th level minimizing a certain degree-
2k
polynomial obtained from
p
over a cubature formula of size
2(k+1)^{n-1}
. While the provable convergence in the level of the harmonic hierarchy is faster than ours, the discrete optimization required at each level is prohibitively expensive when
n
is large. It is possible that a smaller cubature formula could lead to computational savings, although the problem of explicitly constructing small cubature formulas is largely unresolved (see
[
CV24
]
and the references therein). Numerical experiments in Section
5
show that even for small examples such as the Motzkin polynomial, the harmonic hierarchy is significantly slower than ours in terms of time budget needed to achieve a given bound. We note that our hierarchy has the additional advantages of being completely canonical, and generalizing easily to optimization over the Segre-Veronese variety.

Our hierarchy uses Hermitian optimization to solve real optimization problems. In the reverse direction, one can view a Hermitian form in
\mathrm{Herm}(S^{d}(\mathbb{C}^{n}))
as a real form in
S^{2d}(\real^{2n})
by the map

. (The natural extension of this map to the space of Hermitian polynomials is invertible, but does not preserve homogeneity in the reverse direction.) In particular, the real sum-of-squares hierarchy can be used for Hermitian optimization. This observation has been used in several works, e.g.
[
DP09
,
HNW17
,
FF21
,
WM23
]
.

Refs.
[

,

]

]

]
. This hierarchy was proven to converge additively as

in
[

]
. By the duality between separable quantum states and non-negative Hermitian forms elucidated in e.g.
[

,

]
, some results known as

can be used for Hermitian optimization. In particular, the result of
[

]
can be used to obtain the HSOS hierarchy, and the result of
[
CKMR07
]
can be used to prove an effective version similar to that of
[
TY06
]
. See Theorem
3.5
. See
[
DZ13
]
and the references therein for further effective versions of Quillen’s result.

Hierarchies for

,

]
and an effective version of

M^{\dagger}\in\mathrm{Hom}(\mathcal{U}_{\mathbb{C}},\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}})

\lVert\mspace{1mu}M\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}=\max_{\lVert\mspace{1mu}v\mspace{1mu}\rVert=1}\langle v,Mv\rangle

The

]
.

The permutation group

acts on

by permuting tensor factors

\displaystyle\sigma\cdot(v_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes v_{d})=v_{\sigma^{-1}(1)}\otimes\dots\otimes v_{\sigma^{-1}(d)},

extended linearly. Define the

2.1
The maximally symmetric Gram operator

For a real form

[

For

be the partial transpose (or

Proposition 2.1

For a real form

In coordinates,

We will also require the following proposition.

Proposition 2.2

It holds that

Proof.

We need to prove that

\displaystyle=\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{V}\\

(19)

\displaystyle>0,

(20)

where

The third line is
[

, Proposition 6.6]

∎

More generally, for real finite dimensional Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{V}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{V}_{m}
,
and

\displaystyle p\in S^{2d_{1}}(\mathcal{V}_{1}^{*})\otimes\cdots\otimes S^{2d_{m}}(\mathcal{V}_{m}^{*}),

3
Hermitian optimization

In this section we review some background material on Hermitian forms, describe the maximally symmetric Gram operator as a Hermitian form, and state the HSOS hierarchy in more details. We then extend all of this to
multi-homogeneous Hermitian optimization
, which we will use in Section
6
to obtain a spectral hierarchy for the tensor spectral norm.

3.1
Hermitian forms

This section follows closely
[
DJL24
, Section 7]
. For an endomorphism
R\in\mathrm{End}(S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))
, let
R(z,w^{*}):=\langle w^{\otimes d},Rz^{\otimes d}\rangle
. The following proposition is standard, and shows that
R
is uniquely determined by
R(z,z^{*})
. See also
[
D’A11
,
D’A19
]
and
[
DJL24
, Proposition 7.1]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
R\in\mathrm{End}(S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))
. If
R(z,z^{*})=0
for all
z\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}
, then
R=0
.

A
Hermitian form
of bidegree
(d,d)
is an element
H\in\mathrm{End}(S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))
for which
H(z,w^{*})=\overline{H(w,z^{*})}
for all
z,w\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}
. Let
\mathrm{Herm}(S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))\subseteq\mathrm{End}(S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))
be the set of Hermitian forms of bidegree
(d,d)
. An immediate consequence of Proposition
3.1
is that
H(z,z^{*})=\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=|\boldsymbol{\beta}|=d}C_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}z^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}z^{*\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is Hermitian if and only if it is Hermitian as an endomorphism, i.e. the matrix
(C_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}})_{(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}
is Hermitian (see also
[
D’A11
, Proposition 1.1]
). Let
\mathrm{Pos}(S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))
be the set of
Hermitian sums-of-squares
: Hermitian forms of the form
P(z,z^{*})=\sum_{i}|q_{i}(z)|^{2}
for some
q_{i}\in S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}^{*})
. These are precisely the forms that are positive semidefinite as endomorphisms, i.e. the matrix
(C_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}})_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is positive semidefinite.

Let
H\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))
be a Hermitian form, and let
H_{k}(z,z^{*})=H(z,z^{*})\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}
when
k\geq d
. As an operator,
H_{k}=\Pi_{k}(H\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}}^{\otimes k-d})\Pi_{k}
, where
\Pi_{k}
is the orthogonal projection onto
S^{k}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}})
. Indeed,
\langle z^{\otimes k},H_{k}z^{\otimes k}\rangle=H(z,z^{*})\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}
under this choice.

3.2
Hermitian forms associated to a real form

In this section we describe the maximally symmetric Gram operator as a Hermitian form. We first elucidate the notion of “viewing a real operator as a complex operator.”

Remark 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a real Hilbert space with complexification
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}
. For an operator
M\in\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{V})
, we frequently abuse notation and also denote the operator
M\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{C}}\in\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}})
by
M
. For example, for
p\in S^{2d}(\mathcal{V}^{*})
and
P\in\mathrm{Sym}(S^{d}(\mathcal{V}))
the maximally symmetric Gram matrix of
p
, we often regard
P
as an element of
\mathrm{Herm}(S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))
. Similarly, since
\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}}=\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{V}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{C}}
and
\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}},d}=\Pi_{\mathcal{V},d}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{C}}
, we often identify
\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}}
with
\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{V}}
and
\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}},d}
with
\Pi_{\mathcal{V},d}
without comment.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a real Hilbert space with complexification
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}
, let
p\in S^{2d}(\mathcal{V}^{*})
be a real form, and let
P
be the maximally symmetric Gram operator of
p
. Regarding
P
as a Hermitian form
P\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{k}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))
, let
P_{k}\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{k}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))
be defined by
P_{k}(z,z^{*})=P(z,z^{*})\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}
, as above. Then

\displaystyle P_{k}=\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}},k}(P\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}}^{\otimes k-d})\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}},k}\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{k}(\mathcal{V})),

(24)

which can be verified by noting that
\langle z^{\otimes k},P_{k}z^{\otimes k}\rangle=P(z,z^{*})\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}
under this choice. As a real operator,

\displaystyle P_{k}=\Pi_{\mathcal{V},k}(P\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{V}}^{\otimes k-d})\Pi_{\mathcal{V},k}\in\mathrm{Sym}(S^{k}(\mathcal{V})).

(25)

Note that
P_{k}
is a
Gram operator
for
p(x)\lVert\mspace{1mu}x\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}\in S^{k}(\mathcal{V}^{*})
, since

\displaystyle\langle x^{\otimes k},P_{k}x^{\otimes k}\rangle=p(x)\lVert\mspace{1mu}x\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}

(26)

for all
x\in\mathcal{V}
. However,
P_{k}
is not in general maximally symmetric. In Appendix
A
we give a formula for
P_{k}
in coordinates, which we found useful for our numerical implementation.

Remark 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a real Hilbert space with complexification
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}
, and let
N^{(d)}:=M(\lVert\mspace{1mu}x\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2d})
be the maximally symmetric Gram operator of
\lVert\mspace{1mu}x\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2d}
. Note that
N^{(1)}(z,z^{*})=\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2}
, but
N^{(d)}(z,z^{*})\neq\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2d}
in general. Indeed, as a Hermitian operator
\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2d}=\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}},d}=\Pi_{\mathcal{V},d}
is the projection onto the symmetric space, which is not maximally symmetric for
d>1
.

3.3
Hermitian optimization

In this section, we present the HSOS hierarchy in more details.

Theorem 3.5

(HSOS hierarchy)
.

Let
H\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))
be a Hermitian form, and let
H_{k}(z,z^{*})=H(z,z^{*})\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}
when
k\geq d
. For each
k\geq d
, let
\mu_{k}=\lambda_{\min}(H_{k})
. Then
\mu_{d}\leq\mu_{d+1}\leq\dots
and
\lim_{k}\mu_{k}=H_{\min}
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle H_{\min}-\mu_{k}\leq\lVert\mspace{1mu}H\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}\frac{4d(n-1)}{k+1}=O(1/k).

(27)

Similar results are proven in
[
Qui68
,
CD95
,
TY06
]
. We prove this theorem using the quantum de Finetti theorem
[
CKMR07
]
. We use the form of this theorem stated and proven in
[
Wat18
, Theorem 7.26]
.

Theorem 3.6

(Quantum de Finetti theorem)
.

Let
k\geq d
be integers. For any unit vector
\psi\in S^{k}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}})
, there exists an operator

\displaystyle\tau\in\mathrm{conv}\{(uu^{*})^{\otimes d}:u\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}},\lVert\mspace{1mu}u\mspace{1mu}\rVert=1\}\subseteq\mathrm{End}(S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))

(28)

for which

\displaystyle\lVert\mspace{1mu}\operatorname{Tr}_{[d+1\cdot\cdot k]}(\psi\psi^{*})-\tau\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{1}\leq\frac{4d(n-1)}{k+1}.

(29)

In the proposition statement,
\mathrm{conv}
denotes the convex hull, and
\operatorname{Tr}_{[d+1\cdot\cdot k]}(\psi\psi^{*})\in\mathrm{Hom}(S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))
is the partial trace of
\psi\psi^{*}
over
k-d
subsystems (see Section
2
). Now we can prove Theorem
3.5
.

Proof of Theorem
3.5
.

Let
\psi_{k}\in S^{k}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}})
be a unit eigenvector for
H_{k}
with minimum eigenvalue, and let
\tau_{k}\in\mathrm{End}(S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))
be an operator of the form (
28
) for which

\displaystyle\lVert\mspace{1mu}\operatorname{Tr}_{[d+1\cdot\cdot k]}(\psi_{k}\psi_{k}^{*})-\tau_{k}\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{1}\leq\frac{4d(n-1)}{k+1}.

(30)

Then

\displaystyle H_{\min}-\mu_{k}

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{Tr}(H\tau_{k})-\mu_{k}

(31)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}(H(\tau_{k}-\operatorname{Tr}_{[d+1\cdot\cdot k]}(\psi_{k}\psi_{k}^{*})))

(32)

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\mspace{1mu}H\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}\lVert\mspace{1mu}\operatorname{Tr}_{[d+1\cdot\cdot k]}(\psi_{k}\psi_{k}^{*})-\tau_{k}\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{1}

(33)

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\mspace{1mu}H\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}\frac{4d(n-1)}{k+1},

(34)

where the first line follows from the form of
\tau
and convexity, the second line is straightforward, the third line follows from the inequality
\operatorname{Tr}(AB)\leq\lVert\mspace{1mu}A\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}\lVert\mspace{1mu}B\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{1}
, and the fourth line follows from the quantum de Finetti theorem. This completes the proof.
∎

Multi-homogeneous Hermitian optimization

Let
\mathcal{V}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{V}_{m}
be finite-dimensional complex Hilbert spaces of dimensions
n_{1},\dots,n_{m}
, let
\mathcal{U}=S^{d_{1}}(\mathcal{V}_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes S^{d_{m}}(\mathcal{V}_{m})

be a Hermitian operator. For

and

\displaystyle H(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w}^{*}):=\langle w_{1}^{\otimes d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes w_{m}^{\otimes d_{m}},H\;\;z_{1}^{\otimes d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes z_{m}^{\otimes d_{m}}\rangle.

By similar arguments as in Proposition
3.1
,

is uniquely determined by

. We call

a

of degree

\displaystyle H_{\min}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{i}\in\mathcal{V}_{i}\\

(36)

For

\displaystyle H_{k}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{*})=H(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{*})\cdot(\lVert\mspace{1mu}z_{1}\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d_{1})}\otimes\cdots\otimes\lVert\mspace{1mu}z_{n}\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d_{m})})\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{k}(\mathcal{V}_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes S^{k}(\mathcal{V}_{m})).

\displaystyle H_{k}=(\Pi_{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\Pi_{d_{m}})(H\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{V}_{1}}^{\otimes k-d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{V}_{m}}^{\otimes k-d_{m}})(\Pi_{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\Pi_{d_{m}}),

where

\displaystyle\langle z_{1}^{\otimes d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes z_{m}^{\otimes d_{m}},H_{k}\;\;z_{1}^{\otimes d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes z_{m}^{\otimes d_{m}}\rangle=H_{k}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{*})

under this choice.

The following theorem shows that the minimum eigenvalue of

converges to

Theorem 3.7

(m-HSOS hierarchy)
.

Let

when

. Then
\mu_{d}\leq\mu_{d+1}\leq\dots
and

. Furthermore,

\displaystyle H_{\min}-\mu_{k}\leq\lVert\mspace{1mu}H\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}\frac{4|\mathbf{d}|(\max_{j}n_{j}-1)}{k+1}=O(1/k),

4
Proof of convergence

In this section we prove our reduction from real to Hermitian optimization on the sphere (Theorem
1.4
from the introduction). We then use this result along with the HSOS hierarchy (Theorem

4.1
Reduction from real to Hermitian optimization on the sphere

In this section we prove Theorem
1.4
. We first restate the theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}
be the complexification of
\mathcal{V}
, let
p\in S^{2d}(\mathcal{V}^{*})
, and let
P=M(p)\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))
be the maximally symmetric Gram operator of
p
. Then

\displaystyle P_{\min}\leq p_{\min}\leq\frac{P_{\min}}{\delta(d)},

(41)

where
\delta(d)=2^{-d}\sum_{j=0}^{d}\binom{d}{j}^{2}\binom{2d}{2j}^{-1}
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Let
z\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}
be a unit vector for which
P_{\min}=P(z,z^{*})
. By definition,
P(z,z^{*})=\operatorname{Tr}((zz^{*})^{\otimes d}P)
. Let
x,y\in\mathcal{V}
be such that
z=x+iy
. Recall that
P
is invariant under partial transposition along any of the
d
factors of
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}
. In particular,
P=\frac{1}{2}(P+P^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}_{1}})
, where
(\cdot)^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}_{1}}
denotes the partial transpose on the first factor. Thus,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}((zz^{*})^{\otimes d}P)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}((zz^{*})^{\otimes d}P)+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}((zz^{*})^{\otimes d}P^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}_{1}})

(42)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}((zz^{*})^{\otimes d}P)+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}((zz^{*})^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}}\otimes(zz^{*})^{\otimes d-1}P)

(43)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\bigg((xx^{*}+yy^{*})\otimes(zz^{*})^{\otimes d-1}P\bigg).

(44)

Continuing in this way for the other factors, we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}((zz^{*})^{\otimes d}M(p))

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left((xx^{*}+yy^{*})^{\otimes d}\;P\right)

(45)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}(M((x^{*2}+y^{*2})^{d})P)

(46)

Let
Q=M((x^{*2}+y^{*2})^{d})
, and let
\delta=\operatorname{Tr}(Q).
By
[
Rez13
, Corollary 5.6]
there exist real unit vectors
{v_{1}},\dots,{v_{d+1}}\in\mathcal{V}
for which

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta}Q\in\mathrm{conv}\{(v_{1}v_{1}^{*})^{\otimes d},\dots,(v_{d+1}v_{d+1}^{*})^{\otimes d}\}.

(47)

Multiplying both sides by
P
and taking the trace, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{P_{\min}}{\delta}\in\mathrm{conv}\{\operatorname{Tr}(({v_{i}}{v_{i}}^{*})^{\otimes d}P):i\in[d+1]\}.

(48)

Thus, there exists
i\in[d+1]
for which
\operatorname{Tr}(({v_{i}}{v_{i}}^{*})^{\otimes d}P)\leq\frac{P_{\min}}{\delta}
.

To complete the proof, it suffices to show that
\delta\geq\delta(d)
. Let
e_{1},e_{2}\in\mathcal{V}
be orthonormal, and let
U\in\mathrm{O}(\mathcal{V})
be an orthogonal operator for which
U(xx^{*}+yy^{*})U^{*}=te_{1}e_{1}^{*}+(1-t)e_{2}e_{2}^{*}
for some
t\in[0,1]
. Redefine
Q\mapsto U^{\otimes d}QU^{*\otimes d}
, which has the same trace and is given by

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=M((te_{1}^{*2}+(1-t)e_{2}^{*2})^{d})

(49)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{d}\binom{d}{j}t^{2j}(1-t)^{2(d-j)}M(e_{1}^{*2j}e_{2}^{*2(d-j)}).

(50)

Note that
\operatorname{Tr}(M(e_{1}^{*2j}e_{2}^{*2(d-j)}))=\binom{d}{j}\binom{2d}{2j}^{-1}
by Remark
A.2
. Hence,

\displaystyle\delta=\sum_{j=0}^{d}\binom{d}{j}^{2}\binom{2d}{2j}^{-1}\;\;t^{j}(1-t)^{d-j}.

(51)

To complete the proof, we prove that this quantity is minimized when
t=1/2
. If we make the change of variables
x=t-1/2
then we see that

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{d}\frac{\binom{d}{j}^{2}}{\binom{2d}{2j}}\;\;\left(\frac{1}{2}+x\right)^{j}\left(\frac{1}{2}-x\right)^{d-j}

(52)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{d}\frac{\binom{d}{j}^{2}}{\binom{2d}{2j}}\;\;\left(\sum_{k=0}^{j}\binom{j}{k}\frac{x^{k}}{2^{j-k}}\right)\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{d-j}\binom{d-j}{\ell}\frac{(-x)^{\ell}}{2^{d-j-\ell}}\right)

(53)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{d}\frac{\binom{d}{j}^{2}}{\binom{2d}{2j}}\;\;\sum_{k=0}^{j}\sum_{\ell=0}^{d-j}(-1)^{\ell}\binom{j}{k}\binom{d-j}{\ell}\frac{x^{k+\ell}}{2^{d-k-\ell}}.

(54)

If we define
s=k+\ell
then the above expression can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{d}}\sum_{j,s=0}^{d}\frac{\binom{d}{j}^{2}}{\binom{2d}{2j}}(-2)^{s}\sum_{k=0}^{s}(-1)^{k}\binom{j}{k}\binom{d-j}{s-k}x^{s}.

(55)

If
s
is odd then the coefficient of
x^{s}
above equals

\displaystyle-2^{s-d}\sum_{j=0}^{d}\sum_{k=0}^{s}(-1)^{k}\frac{\binom{d}{j}^{2}\binom{j}{k}\binom{d-j}{s-k}}{\binom{2d}{2j}},

(56)

which equals
0
since replacing
j
by
d-j
and
k
by
s-k
in the term

(-1)^{k}\frac{\binom{d}{j}^{2}\binom{j}{k}\binom{d-j}{s-k}}{\binom{2d}{2j}}

results in another term with the same absolute value but its sign flipped. It follows that
\delta
is a sum of even powers of
x
, so it is minimized when
x=0
(i.e., when
t=1/2
), completing the proof.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Theorem
4.1
does not hold if we replace
P
by an arbitrary Gram operator of
p
. For example, let
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}\subseteq\real^{2}
be an orthonormal basis, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}=\spn\{e_{1}\otimes e_{2}+e_{2}\otimes e_{1},\;\;e_{1}\otimes e_{1}-e_{2}\otimes e_{2}\}\subseteq S^{2}(\real^{2}),

(57)

and let
\Pi_{\mathcal{U}}\in\mathrm{End}(S^{2}(\real^{2}))
be the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{U}
, which is a Gram operator for
\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mspace{1mu}x\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{4}
. Note that

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\\
\lVert\mspace{1mu}v\mspace{1mu}\rVert=1\end{subarray}}\langle v\otimes v,(\Pi_{\mathcal{U}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{C}})v\otimes v\rangle=1

(58)

because
\frac{1}{2}(e_{1}+ie_{2})^{\otimes 2}\in\mathcal{U}\otimes\mathbb{C}
. Let
Q=\frac{3}{4}\Pi_{S^{2}(\real^{2})}-\Pi_{\mathcal{U}}
, which is a Gram operator for
p(x):=\frac{1}{4}\lVert\mspace{1mu}x\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{4}
. Then
p_{\min}=1/4
, but when
Q
is viewed as a Hermitian operator,
Q_{\min}=-1/4
.

4.2
HRSOS hierarchy

For a real form
p\in S^{2d}(\mathcal{V}^{*})
recall that we define
P=M(p)\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))
to be the maximally symmetric Gram operator of
P
, and

\displaystyle P_{k}(z,z^{*})=P(z,z^{*})\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{k}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}})),

(59)

when
k\geq d
. Similarly, we let
N^{(d)}=M(\lVert\mspace{1mu}x\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2d})\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))
, and let

\displaystyle N^{(d)}_{k}(z,z^{*})=N^{(d)}(z,z^{*})\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{k}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))

(60)

when
k\geq d
. As operators, these are given by
P_{k}=\Pi_{k}(P\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}}^{\otimes k-d})\Pi_{k}
and
{N_{k}^{(d)}=\Pi_{k}(N^{(d)}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}}^{\otimes k-d})\Pi_{k}}
. We also recall the definition

\displaystyle\delta(d)=2^{-d}\sum_{j=0}^{d}\binom{d}{j}^{2}\binom{2d}{2j}^{-1}.

(61)

Theorem 4.3

(HRSOS hierarchy)
.

Let
p\in S^{2d}(\mathcal{V}^{*})
be a real form of even degree, let
P=M(p)
, and for each
k\geq d
let

\displaystyle\eta_{k}=\lambda_{\min}((N_{k}^{(d)})^{-1/2}P_{k}(N_{k}^{(d)})^{-1/2}).

(62)

Then
\eta_{d}\leq\eta_{d+1}\leq\dots
and
\lim_{k}\eta_{k}=p_{\min}
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle p_{\min}-\eta_{k}\leq\lVert\mspace{1mu}P\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}\left(1+\kappa(N^{(d)})\right)\frac{4d(n-1)}{\delta(d)(k+1)}=O\left(\frac{1}{k}\right).

(63)

Numerics suggest that

\displaystyle\kappa(N^{(d)})=\binom{n/2+d-1}{\lfloor d/2\rfloor},

(64)

where the binomial function is extended to non-integer inputs via the Gamma function.

Proof of Theorem
4.3
.

First note that

\displaystyle\eta_{k}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\min}((N_{k}^{(d)})^{-1/2}P_{k}(N_{k}^{(d)})^{-1/2})

(65)

\displaystyle=\max\{\eta:(N_{k}^{(d)})^{-1/2}P_{k}(N_{k}^{(d)})^{-1/2}-\eta\;\mathds{1}_{S^{k}(\mathcal{V})}\succeq 0\}

(66)

\displaystyle=\max\{\eta:P_{k}-\eta\;N_{k}^{(d)}\succeq 0\}

(67)

\displaystyle=\max\{\eta:M(p-\eta\cdot\lVert\mspace{1mu}x\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2d})_{k}\succeq 0\}.

(68)

Let

\displaystyle q^{(k)}(x)=p(x)-\eta_{k}\lVert\mspace{1mu}x\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2d}\in S^{2d}(\mathcal{V}^{*}),

(69)

and let
Q^{(k)}=M(q^{(k)}).
For the inequality
\eta_{k}\leq p_{\min}
, note that since
Q^{(k)}_{k}\succeq 0
, we have

\displaystyle p(x)-\eta_{k}

\displaystyle=\langle x^{\otimes k},Q_{k}^{(k)}x^{\otimes k}\rangle\geq 0

(70)

for all
x\in\mathcal{V}
, so
p_{\min}-\eta_{k}\geq 0
. For the inequality
\eta_{k+1}\geq\eta_{k}
, let
\psi\in S^{k+1}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}})
be a unit eigenvector for
P_{k+1}
with eigenvalue
\eta_{k+1}
. Then

\displaystyle\eta_{k+1}=\operatorname{Tr}(P_{k+1}\psi\psi^{*})=\operatorname{Tr}(P_{k}\operatorname{Tr}_{1}(\psi\psi^{*}))\geq\eta_{k},

(71)

where the second equality follows from
\operatorname{Tr}_{1}(P_{k+1})=P_{k}
, and the inequality follows by convexity.

For the bound, note that

\displaystyle Q_{\min}^{(k)}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\mspace{1mu}Q^{(k)}\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}\frac{4d(n-1)}{k+1}

(72)

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\mspace{1mu}P\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}\left(1+\kappa(N^{(d)})\right)\frac{4d(n-1)}{k+1}.

(73)

The first line follows from Theorem
3.5
and the fact that
\lambda_{\min}(Q_{k}^{(k)})=0
. The second line follows from

\displaystyle\lVert\mspace{1mu}Q^{(k)}\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\mspace{1mu}P\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}+\lvert\eta_{k}\rvert\lVert\mspace{1mu}N^{(d)}\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}

(74)

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\mspace{1mu}P\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}\left(1+\frac{\lambda_{\max}(N^{(d)})}{\lambda_{\min}(N^{(d)})}\right)

(75)

Here, the first line is the triangle inequality. The second line follows from
\lVert\mspace{1mu}N^{(d)}\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}=\lambda_{\max}(N^{(d)})
by Proposition
2.2
, and
\lvert\eta_{k}\rvert\leq\lVert\mspace{1mu}P\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}\;\lambda_{\min}(N^{(d)})^{-1}
since choosing
\eta
equal to minus the righthand side would guarantee
M(p-\eta\;\lVert\mspace{1mu}x\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2d})\succeq 0
.

It follows from Theorem
4.3
and (
72
) that

\displaystyle p_{\min}-\eta_{k}

\displaystyle=q_{\min}^{(k)}

(76)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{Q_{\min}^{(k)}}{\delta(d)}

(77)

\displaystyle=\lVert\mspace{1mu}P\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}\left(1+\kappa(N^{(d)})\right)\frac{4d(n-1)}{\delta(d)(k+1)}.

(78)

This completes the proof.
∎

5
Numerical implementation and examples

Let
\mathcal{V}=\real^{n}
. In this section we implement our polynomial optimization hierarchy described in Theorem
4.3
. While the quantity
\eta_{k}=\lambda_{\min}((N_{k}^{(d)})^{-1/2}P_{k}(N_{k}^{(d)})^{-1/2})
that defines the
k
-th level of our hierarchy is the minimum eigenvalue of a matrix, it should not be computed as such, since doing so would require computation of the inverse of the matrix
N_{k}^{(d)}=M(\lVert\mspace{1mu}x\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2d})_{k}
. An alternate way of computing
\eta_{k}
is to instead solve the generalized eigenvalue/eigenvector problem

\displaystyle P_{k}\;\psi=\lambda\;N_{k}^{(d)}\;\psi

(79)

in
\psi\in S^{k}(\mathcal{V})
and
\lambda\in\real
.

It is straightforward to show that the minimum generalized eigenvalue
\lambda
(i.e., the minimal
\lambda
for which there is a vector
\psi
solving Equation (
79
)) is equal to
\eta_{k}
. However, this generalized eigenvalue can be found without inverting or multiplying any matrices. Furthermore, there are extremely fast numerical algorithms for solving this problem that can exploit the extreme sparsity of
P_{k}
and
N_{k}^{(d)}

[
Ste02
,
MW23
]
;
N_{k}^{(d)}
is sparse because the form
\lVert\mspace{1mu}x\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2d}
itself is sparse (i.e., most of its coefficients are equal to
0
), and
P_{k}
is sparse when
k
is large even if
p
is dense (since

\displaystyle P_{k}=\Pi_{k}(P\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{V}}^{\otimes{k-d}})\Pi_{k},

(80)

and
\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{V}}^{\otimes k-d}

is sparse). These generalized eigenvalue algorithms have been implemented in ARPACK
[
LSY98
]
, making them available out-of-the-box in SciPy, Mathematica, MATLAB, and many other popular computational software packages. We have implemented the computation of
\eta_{k}
in the QETLAB package for MATLAB
[
Joh16
]
.

We will now present several examples to illustrate how well our hierarchy performs numerically compared to other hierarchies. These examples all support the following conclusions:

•

If the polynomial is small enough to be bounded by the RSOS hiearchy, then the RSOS hierarchy should be used, since it typically provides better bounds per unit of computation time. However, since it relies on semidefinite programming, it can only be applied to comparably small problems, e.g. degree-4 polynomials with
25
or fewer variables.

•

If the polynomial is
even
(i.e., in each term, each variable is raised to an even power), specialized optimization techniques have been developed
[
Pól84
,
dKLP05
,
DKLS15
,
AM19
,
AS19
,
AS21
,
VL23
]
. For example, the diagonally-dominant sum-of-squares (DSOS) hierarchy of
[
AM19
]
performs quite well when applied to even polynomials, and has a small enough memory footprint that it can be applied to larger problems, e.g. degree-4 polynomials with
70
or fewer variables.

•

For larger problems (e.g., a degree-4 polynomial with more than
25
variables, or a degree-4 even polynomial with more than
70
variables), our hierarchy outperforms existing hierarchies.
For example, our hierarchy provides better bounds per unit of computation time than the optimization-free hierarchies of
[
CV24
]
for all problem sizes. Furthermore, our hierarchy provides significantly better bounds (at the expense of somewhat higher computation time) than the DSOS hierarchy, and can be applied to much larger problems, e.g. degree-4 polynomials with
90
variables.

6
Tensor optimization hierarchy

Let
\mathcal{V}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{V}_{m}
be finite dimensional real Hilbert spaces of dimensions
n_{1},\dots,n_{m}
, let
D_{1},\dots,D_{m}
be positive integers, let
\mathcal{U}=S^{D_{1}}(\mathcal{V}_{1}^{*})\otimes\cdots\otimes S^{D_{m}}(\mathcal{V}_{m}^{*})
, and let
p\in\mathcal{U}
. In this section we present a hierarchy of eigencomputations for computing

\displaystyle p_{\min}:=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{j}\in\mathcal{V}_{j}\\
\lVert\mspace{1mu}x_{j}\mspace{1mu}\rVert=1\end{subarray}}p(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}),

(82)

where we use the shorthand
p(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}):=p(x_{1}^{\otimes D_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{m}^{\otimes D_{m}})
. The analysis is similar to that of Section
4
. We then apply this hierarchy to compute the real tensor spectral norm and to optimize real biquadratic forms.

The following proposition establishes that it suffices to assume
D_{1},\dots,D_{m}
are all even, at the expense of adding up to
m
new variables.

Proposition 6.1
.

If
D_{1}
is odd, then

\displaystyle p_{\min}

\displaystyle=\frac{(D_{1}+1)^{(D_{1}+1)/2}}{D_{1}^{D_{1}/2}}(f\cdot p)_{\min}

(83)

\displaystyle=\frac{(D_{1}+1)^{(D_{1}+1)/2}}{D_{1}^{D_{1}/2}}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{j}\in\mathcal{V}_{j}\\
\lVert\mspace{1mu}x_{j}\mspace{1mu}\rVert=1\\
1\neq j\in[m]\end{subarray}}\;\;\;\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1}\in\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}\\
\lVert\mspace{1mu}x_{1}\mspace{1mu}\rVert=1\end{subarray}}(f\cdot p)(x_{1}^{\otimes D_{1}+1}\otimes x_{2}^{\otimes D_{2}}\otimes\dots\otimes x_{m}^{\otimes D_{m}}),

(84)

where
\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}=\mathcal{V}_{1}\oplus\real^{1}
and
f=(\vec{0},1)\in(\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime})^{*}
.

Proof of Proposition
6.1
.

For
v:=(v_{2},\dots,v_{m})
fixed, define a polynomial
p_{v}\in S^{D_{1}}(\mathcal{V}_{1}^{*})
by

\displaystyle p_{v}(x_{1})=p(x_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{m}).

(85)

By
[
DW12
, Lemma B.2]
, we have

\displaystyle p_{\min}

\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}_{j}\\
\lVert\mspace{1mu}v_{j}\mspace{1mu}\rVert=1\\
1\neq j\in[m]\end{subarray}}\;\;\;\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1}\in\mathcal{V}_{1}\\
\lVert\mspace{1mu}x_{1}\mspace{1mu}\rVert=1\end{subarray}}p_{v}(x)

(86)

\displaystyle=\frac{(D_{1}+1)^{(D_{1}+1)/2}}{D_{1}^{D_{1}/2}}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}_{j}\\
\lVert\mspace{1mu}v_{j}\mspace{1mu}\rVert=1\\
1\neq j\in[m]\end{subarray}}\;\;\;\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1}\in\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}\\
\lVert\mspace{1mu}x_{1}\mspace{1mu}\rVert=1\end{subarray}}(f\cdot p_{v})(x_{1})

(87)

\displaystyle=\frac{(D_{1}+1)^{(D_{1}+1)/2}}{D_{1}^{D_{1}/2}}(f\cdot p)_{\min}.

(88)

This completes the proof.
∎

In the remainder of this section, we assume
D_{1},\dots,D_{m}
are even without loss of generality. Let
d_{j}=D_{j}/2
for all
j\in[m]
, and let
{\bf d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{m})
.

6.1
Reduction for tensor optimization

In this section, we generalize the reduction proven in Theorem
4.1
to the setting of tensor optimization.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
p\in S^{2d_{1}}(\mathcal{V}_{1}^{*})\otimes\cdots\otimes S^{2d_{m}}(\mathcal{V}_{m}^{*})
be a real tensor, let
\mathcal{V}_{i}^{\mathbb{C}}
be the complexification of
\mathcal{V}_{i}
, and let
P=M(p)\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\mathbb{C}})\otimes\cdots\otimes S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{m}^{\mathbb{C}}))
. Then

\displaystyle P_{\min}\leq p_{\min}\leq\frac{P_{\min}}{\delta({\bf d})},

(89)

where
\delta({\bf d})=\delta(d_{1})\cdots\delta(d_{m})
, and
\delta(d_{i})
is defined in (
61
).

Proof.

Let
z_{i}\in\mathcal{V}_{i}^{\mathbb{C}}
be unit vectors for which

\displaystyle P_{\min}=P(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{*}),

(90)

where
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{m})
. By definition,

\displaystyle P(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{*})=\operatorname{Tr}((z_{1}z_{1}^{*})^{\otimes d_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes(z_{m}z_{m}^{*})^{\otimes d_{m}}P).

(91)

Let
x_{j},y_{j}\in\mathcal{V}
be defined by
x_{j}+iy_{j}=z_{j}
for each
j\in[m]
. Note that
P
is invariant under partial transposition along any of the
d_{1}
factors of
\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\mathbb{C}}
. In particular,
P=\frac{1}{2}(P+P^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}_{1}})
, where
(\cdot)^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}_{1}}
denotes the partial transpose on the first factor of
\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\mathbb{C}}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}((z_{1}z_{1}^{*})^{\otimes d_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes(z_{m}z_{m}^{*})^{\otimes d_{m}}P)

(92)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}((z_{1}z_{1}^{*})^{\otimes d_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes(z_{m}z_{m}^{*})^{\otimes d_{m}}P)+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}((z_{1}z_{1}^{*})^{\otimes d_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes(z_{m}z_{m}^{*})^{\otimes d_{m}}P^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}_{1}})

(93)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}((z_{1}z_{1}^{*})^{\otimes d_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes(z_{m}z_{m}^{*})^{\otimes d_{m}}P)+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}((z_{1}z_{1}^{*})^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}}\otimes(z_{1}z_{1}^{*})^{\otimes d_{1}-1}\otimes\dots\otimes(z_{m}z_{m}^{*})^{\otimes d_{m}}P)

(94)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\bigg((x_{1}x_{1}^{*}+y_{1}y_{1}^{*})\otimes(z_{1}z_{1}^{*})^{\otimes d_{1}-1}\otimes\dots\otimes(z_{m}z_{m}^{*})^{\otimes d_{m}}M(p)\bigg).

(95)

Continuing in this way for the other factors, we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}((z_{1}z_{1}^{*})

{}^{\otimes d_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes(z_{m}z_{m}^{*})^{\otimes d_{m}}\;\;P)

(96)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}((x_{1}x_{1}^{*}+y_{1}y_{1}^{*})^{\otimes d_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes(x_{m}x_{m}^{*}+y_{m}y_{m}^{*})^{\otimes d_{m}}\;\;P)

(97)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}(M((x_{1}^{*2}+y_{1}^{*2})^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes(x_{m}^{*2}+y_{m}^{*2})^{d_{m}})\;\;P)

(98)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}(M((x_{1}^{*2}+y_{1}^{*2})^{d_{1}})\otimes\cdots\otimes M((x_{m}^{*2}+y_{m}^{*2})^{d_{m}})\;\;P).

(99)

For each
j\in[m]
, let

\displaystyle\delta^{(j)}=\operatorname{Tr}(M((x_{j}^{*2}+y_{j}^{*2})^{d_{j}})).

(100)

By
[
Rez13
, Corollary 5.6]
for each
j\in[m]
there exist real unit vectors
{v_{j,1}},\dots,{v_{j,d_{j}+1}}\in\mathcal{V}_{j}
for which

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta^{(j)}}M((x_{j}^{*2}+y_{j}^{*2})^{d_{j}})\in\mathrm{conv}\{(v_{j,1}v_{j,1}^{*})^{\otimes d_{1}},\dots,(v_{m,d_{m}+1}v_{m,d_{m}+1}^{*})^{\otimes d_{m}}\}.

(101)

Taking the tensor product over all
j\in[m]
, multiplying by
P
and taking the trace, this gives

\displaystyle\frac{P_{\min}}{\delta^{(1)}\cdots\delta^{(m)}}\in\mathrm{conv}\{\operatorname{Tr}((v_{1,i_{1}}v_{1,i_{1}}^{*})^{\otimes d_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes(v_{m,i_{m}}v_{m,i_{m}}^{*})^{\otimes d_{m}}\;\;P):i_{j}\in[d_{j}+1]\}.

(102)

Thus, there exist
i_{1}\in[d_{1}+1],\dots,i_{m}\in[d_{m}+1]
for which

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}((v_{1,i_{1}}v_{1,i_{1}}^{*})^{\otimes d_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes(v_{m,i_{m}}v_{m,i_{m}}^{*})^{\otimes d_{m}}\;\;P)\leq\frac{P_{\min}}{\delta^{(1)}\cdots\delta^{(m)}}.

(103)

Note that
\delta^{(j)}\geq\delta({d_{j}})
for each
j\in[m]
, by the proof of Theorem
4.1
. This completes the proof.

6.2
m-HRSOS hierarchy

For
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{m})
, let

\displaystyle N^{(\mathbf{d})}=M(\lVert\mspace{1mu}x_{1}\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\lVert\mspace{1mu}x_{m}\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2d_{m}})\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{d_{1}}(\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\mathbb{C}})\otimes\cdots\otimes S^{d_{m}}(\mathcal{V}_{m}^{\mathbb{C}})).

(104)

Recall that for a Hermitian operator
H\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{d_{1}}(\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\mathbb{C}})\otimes\cdots\otimes S^{d_{m}}(\mathcal{V}_{m}^{\mathbb{C}}))
and an integer
k\geq d:=\max_{j}d_{j}
we define

\displaystyle H_{k}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{*})=H(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{*})\cdot(\lVert\mspace{1mu}z_{1}\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d_{1})}\otimes\cdots\otimes\lVert\mspace{1mu}z_{m}\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d_{m})})\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{k}(\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\mathbb{C}})\otimes\cdots\otimes S^{k}(\mathcal{V}_{m}^{\mathbb{C}})).

(105)

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
p\in S^{2d_{1}}(\mathcal{V}_{1}^{*})\otimes\cdots\otimes S^{2d_{m}}(\mathcal{V}_{m}^{*})
be a real tensor, and let
P=M(p)
. Furthermore, let

\displaystyle\eta_{k}=\lambda_{\min}((N_{k}^{(\mathbf{d})})^{-1/2}P_{k}(N_{k}^{(\mathbf{d})})^{-1/2}).

(106)

Then
\eta_{d}\leq\eta_{d+1}\leq\dots
and
\lim_{k}\eta_{k}=p_{\min}
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle p_{\min}-\eta_{k}\leq\lVert\mspace{1mu}P\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}\left(1+\kappa(N^{(\mathbf{d})})\right)\frac{4|\mathbf{d}|(\max_{j}n_{j}-1)}{\delta(\mathbf{d})(k+1)}=O\left(\frac{1}{k}\right),

(107)

where
|\mathbf{d}|=d_{1}+\dots+d_{m}
.

Numerics indicate that

\displaystyle\kappa(N^{(\mathbf{d})})=\binom{n_{1}/2+d_{1}-1}{\lfloor d_{1}/2\rfloor}\cdots\binom{n_{m}/2+d_{m}-1}{\lfloor d_{m}/2\rfloor},

(108)

where the binomial function is extended to non-integer inputs via the Gamma function.

Now we can prove Theorem
6.3
.

Proof of Theorem
6.3
.

By a similar argument as in Theorem
4.3
, we have

\displaystyle\eta_{k}=\max\{\eta:P_{k}-\eta N^{(\mathbf{d})}_{k}\succeq 0\}.

(109)

Let

\displaystyle q^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})=p(\mathbf{x})-\eta_{k}(\lVert\mspace{1mu}x_{1}\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\lVert\mspace{1mu}x_{m}\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2d_{m}})\in S^{2d_{1}}(\mathcal{V}_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes S^{2d_{m}}(\mathcal{V}_{m}),

(110)

and let
Q^{(k)}=M(q^{(k)}).
For the inequality
\eta_{k}\leq p_{\min}
, note that since
Q^{(k)}_{k}\succeq 0
, we have

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{x})-\eta_{k}

\displaystyle=Q_{k}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{*})\geq 0

(111)

for all
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
with
x_{i}\in\mathcal{V}_{i}
, so
p_{\min}-\eta_{k}\geq 0
. For the inequality
\eta_{k+1}\geq\eta_{k}
, let
r
be a unit eigenvector for
P_{k+1}
with eigenvalue
\eta_{k+1}
. Then

\displaystyle\eta_{k+1}=\operatorname{Tr}(P_{k+1}\psi\psi^{*})=\operatorname{Tr}(P_{k}\operatorname{Tr}_{1,\dots,1}(\psi\psi^{*}))\geq\eta_{k},

(112)

where
\operatorname{Tr}_{1,\dots,1}
denotes the partial trace over one copy of
\mathcal{V}_{j}^{\mathbb{C}}
for each
j\in[m]
, the second equality follows from
\operatorname{Tr}_{1,\dots,1}(P_{k+1})=P_{k}
, and the inequality follows by convexity.

For the bound, note that

\displaystyle Q_{\min}^{(k)}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\mspace{1mu}Q^{(k)}\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}\frac{4d(\max_{j}n_{j}-1)}{k+1}

(113)

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\mspace{1mu}P\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}\left(1+\kappa(N^{(\mathbf{d})})\right)\frac{4d(\max_{j}n_{j}-1)}{k+1}.

(114)

The first line follows from Theorem
3.7
and the fact that
\lambda_{\min}(Q_{k}^{(k)})=0
. The second line follows from

\displaystyle\lVert\mspace{1mu}Q^{(k)}\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\mspace{1mu}P\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}+\lvert\eta_{k}\rvert\lVert\mspace{1mu}N^{(\mathbf{d})}\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}

(115)

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\mspace{1mu}P\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}\left(1+\frac{\lambda_{\max}(N^{(\mathbf{d})})}{\lambda_{\min}(N^{(\mathbf{d})})}\right)

(116)

Here, the first line is the triangle inequality. The second line follows from
\lVert\mspace{1mu}N^{(\mathbf{d})}\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}=\lambda_{\max}(N^{(\mathbf{d})})
by Proposition
2.2
, and
\lvert\eta_{k}\rvert\leq\lVert\mspace{1mu}P\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}\;\lambda_{\min}(N^{(\mathbf{d})})^{-1}
since choosing
\eta
equal to minus the righthand side would guarantee
M(p-\eta\;\lVert\mspace{1mu}x\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2d})\succeq 0
.

It follows from Theorem
6.3
and (
113
) that

\displaystyle p_{\min}-\eta_{k}

\displaystyle=q_{\min}^{(k)}

(117)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{Q_{\min}^{(k)}}{\delta(\mathbf{d})}

(118)

\displaystyle=\lVert\mspace{1mu}P\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}\left(1+\kappa(N^{(\mathbf{d})})\right)\frac{4d(\max_{j}n_{j}-1)}{\delta(\mathbf{d})(k+1)}.

(119)

This completes the proof.
∎

6.3
Examples: Biquadratic forms and the tensor spectral norm

In this section we use our tensor optimization hierarchy and Theorem
6.3
to give hierarchies of eigencomputations for two tasks: minimizing a biquadratic form over the unit sphere, and computing the real spectral norm of a real tensor.

Example 6.4

(Biquadratic forms)
.

Let
\mathcal{V}_{1},\mathcal{V}_{2}
be finite dimensional real Hilbert spaces with complexifications
\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\mathbb{C}},\mathcal{V}_{2}^{\mathbb{C}}
. A
biquadratic form
is an element
p\in S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{1}^{*})\otimes S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{2}^{*})
. Let
P=M(p)
be the maximally symmetric Gram operator of
p
(see Section
2
), and let

\displaystyle P_{k}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{*})=P(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{*})\cdot(\lVert\mspace{1mu}z_{1}\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}\otimes\lVert\mspace{1mu}z_{2}\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)})\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{d}((\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\mathbb{C}})\otimes S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{2}^{\mathbb{C}}))

(120)

for
k\geq d
and
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},z_{2})
with
z_{i}\in\mathcal{V}_{i}^{\mathbb{C}}
. Since
P_{k}
is real-valued, we can regard it as the real symmetric operator

\displaystyle P_{k}=(\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{1},k}\otimes\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{2},k})(P\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{V}_{1}}^{\otimes k-1}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{V}_{2}}^{\otimes k-1})(\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{1},k}\otimes\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{2},k})\in\mathrm{Sym}(S^{k}(\mathcal{V}_{1})\otimes S^{k}(\mathcal{V}_{2})),

(121)

where
\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{i},k}
is the projection onto
S^{k}(\mathcal{V}_{i})
. Let
\eta_{k}:=\lambda_{\min}(P_{k})
. By Theorem
6.3
, the
\eta_{k}
converge to
p_{\min}
from below at a rate of
O(1/k)
.

In particular, suppose we wish to determine whether
p(x,y)>0
for all
x,y\neq 0
. Our hierarchy shows that this holds if and only if
P_{k}
is positive definite for some
k
.

Example 6.5

(Tensor spectral norm)
.

Let
\mathcal{V}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{V}_{m}
be finite dimensional real Hilbert spaces with complexifications
\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\mathbb{C}}\cdots\mathcal{V}_{m}^{\mathbb{C}}
, and let
p\in\mathcal{V}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{V}_{m}
. Assume without loss of generality that
\lVert\mspace{1mu}p\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{2}=1
. The
(real) spectral norm
of
p
is defined as

\displaystyle\lVert\mspace{1mu}p\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\sigma,\real}:=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}_{j}\\
\lVert\mspace{1mu}v_{j}\mspace{1mu}\rVert=1\end{subarray}}\lvert\langle p,v_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes v_{m}\rangle\rvert.

(122)

We can use our hierarchy to compute the spectral norm of
p
as follows. First note that

\displaystyle-\lVert\mspace{1mu}p\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\sigma,\real}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}_{j}\\
\lVert\mspace{1mu}v_{j}\mspace{1mu}\rVert=1\end{subarray}}\langle p,v_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes v_{m}\rangle,

(123)

so it suffices to compute the righthand side of this expression. For each
j
let
\mathcal{U}_{j}=\mathcal{V}_{j}\oplus\real^{1}
, let
u_{j}=(\vec{0},1)\in\mathcal{U}_{j}
, and let

\displaystyle r=p\cdot(u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m})\in S^{2}(\mathcal{U}_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes S^{2}(\mathcal{U}_{m}).

(124)

This product is defined in Section
2
, and we give more details here by way of example. Let

\displaystyle q=p\otimes u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m}\in\bigg(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{U}_{j}\bigg)^{\otimes 2},

(125)

and let

\displaystyle r=(\Pi_{\mathcal{U}_{1},k}\otimes\dots\otimes\Pi_{\mathcal{U}_{m},k})(\sigma\cdot q)\in S^{2}(\mathcal{U}_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes S^{2}(\mathcal{U}_{m}),

(126)

where
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{2m}
is the permutation that sends
(1,2,\dots,2m)
to
(1,m+1,2,m+2,\dots,m,2m)
. Direct calculation shows that
\lVert\mspace{1mu}r\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{2}=2^{-m/2}
. By Proposition
6.1
it holds that

\displaystyle 2^{-m}\;\cdot\;\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}_{j}\\
\lVert\mspace{1mu}v_{j}\mspace{1mu}\rVert=1\end{subarray}}\langle p,v_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes v_{m}\rangle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v_{j}\in\mathcal{U}_{j}\\
\lVert\mspace{1mu}v_{j}\mspace{1mu}\rVert=1\end{subarray}}\langle r,v_{1}^{\otimes 2}\otimes\dots\otimes v_{m}^{\otimes 2}\rangle.

(127)

Our hierarchy can be used to compute the righthand side of (
127
), as follows. Let

\displaystyle R=M(r)\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{U}_{1}^{\mathbb{C}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{U}_{m}^{\mathbb{C}})

(128)

be the maximally symmetric Gram operator of
r
(see Section
2
). For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let

\displaystyle R_{k}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{*})=R(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{*})\cdot(\lVert\mspace{1mu}z_{1}\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\lVert\mspace{1mu}z_{m}\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-1)})\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{k}(\mathcal{U}_{1}^{\mathbb{C}})\otimes\cdots\otimes S^{k}(\mathcal{U}_{m}^{\mathbb{C}})),

(129)

where
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{m})
and
z_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{i}
. Since
R_{k}
is real-valued, we can regard it as the real symmetric operator

\displaystyle R_{k}=(\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{1},k}\otimes\dots\otimes\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{m},k})(R\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{V}_{1}}^{\otimes k-1}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{V}_{m}}^{\otimes k-1})(\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{1},k}\otimes

\displaystyle\dots\otimes\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{m},k})

(130)

\displaystyle\in\mathrm{Sym}(S^{k}(\mathcal{U}_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes S^{k}(\mathcal{U}_{m})).

(131)

Then by our Theorem
6.3
, the minimum eigenvalues
\eta_{k}:=\lambda_{\min}(R_{k})
converge to the righthand side of (
127
) from below at a rate of
O(1/k)
. More precisely, we have the following convergence guarantee:

Theorem 6.6

(Hierarchy for tensor spectral norm)
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
let
\mu_{k}=-2^{m}\lambda_{\min}(R_{k})
. Then
\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{2}\geq\dots
and
\lim_{k}\mu_{k}=\lVert\mspace{1mu}p\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\sigma,\real}
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\mu_{k}-\lVert\mspace{1mu}p\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\sigma,\real}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{m/2+3}m(\max_{j}n_{j}-1)}{k+1}=O\left(\frac{1}{k}\right).

(132)

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\mu_{k}-\lVert\mspace{1mu}p\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\sigma,\real}

\displaystyle=-2^{m}\eta_{k}+\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}_{j}\\
\lVert\mspace{1mu}v_{j}\mspace{1mu}\rVert=1\end{subarray}}\langle p,v_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes v_{m}\rangle

(133)

\displaystyle=-2^{m}\eta_{k}+2^{m}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}_{j}\\
\lVert\mspace{1mu}v_{j}\mspace{1mu}\rVert=1\end{subarray}}\langle r,v_{1}^{\otimes 2}\otimes\dots\otimes v_{m}^{\otimes 2}\rangle

(134)

\displaystyle=2^{m}(r_{\min}-\eta_{k}).

(135)

The bound follows from Theorem
6.3
,
\delta(1,\dots,1)=1
,

\displaystyle{\kappa(N^{(1,\dots,1)})=\kappa(\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{U}_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{U}_{m}})=1},

(136)

and
\lVert\mspace{1mu}M(r)\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\lVert\mspace{1mu}r\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{2}=2^{-m/2}
. This completes the proof.
∎

7
Constrained polynomial optimization

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a finite dimensional real Hilbert space, let
p\in S^{2d}(\mathcal{V}^{*})
, and let
q_{i}\in S^{c_{i}}(\mathcal{V}^{*})
,
i=1,\dots,\ell
be forms. We consider the constrained polynomial optimization problem

\displaystyle p_{q,\min}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{V}\\
\lVert\mspace{1mu}x\mspace{1mu}\rVert=1\\
q_{1}(x)=\dots=q_{\ell}(x)=0\end{subarray}}p(x).

(137)

Analogously, for a finite dimensional complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}
, a Hermitian form
H\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))
, and forms
q_{i}\in S^{c_{i}}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}^{*})
, let

\displaystyle H_{q,\min}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}\\
\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert=1\\
q_{1}(z)=\dots=q_{\ell}(z)=0\end{subarray}}H(z,z^{*}).

(138)

For a finite dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{V}
over
\mathbb{F}\in\{\real,\mathbb{C}\}
, let
{S^{\bullet}(\mathcal{V}^{*})=\bigoplus_{d=0}^{\infty}S^{d}(\mathcal{V}^{*})}
be the symmetric algebra. For forms
q_{i}\in S^{c_{i}}(\mathcal{V}^{*})
,
i=1,\dots,\ell
, let

\displaystyle V_{\mathbb{F}}(q_{1},\dots,q_{\ell})=\{v:q_{1}(v)=\cdots=q_{\ell}(v)=0\}\subseteq\mathcal{V}

(139)

be the
variety
they cut out, and let

\displaystyle I=\langle q_{1},\dots,q_{\ell}\rangle:=\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}q_{i}f_{i}:f_{i}\in S^{\bullet}(\mathcal{V}^{*})\bigg\}\subseteq S^{\bullet}(\mathcal{V}^{*})

(140)

be the ideal they generate. For each positive integer
k
, let
I_{k}\subseteq S^{k}(\mathcal{V}^{*})
be the degree-
k
part of
I
, let
I_{k}^{\perp}\subseteq S^{k}(\mathcal{V})
be the orthogonal complement to
I
with respect to the bilinear pairing
(\cdot,\cdot):\mathcal{V}^{*}\times\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}
, and let
\Pi_{I,k}\in\mathrm{End}(S^{k}(\mathcal{V}))
be the orthogonal projection onto
I_{k}^{\perp}
.

The following hierarchy of eigencomputations for
H_{q,\min}
was recently presented in
[
DJL24
, Theorem 7.2]
. See also
[
CD99
,
DP09
]
.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
H\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))
be a Hermitian form, let
q_{i}\in S^{c_{i}}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}^{*})
be forms for
i=1,\dots,\ell
, and let
{I=\langle q_{1},\dots,q_{\ell}\rangle}.
For each
k\geq d
, let
H_{k}=H(z,z^{*})\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}
and let
\mu_{k}=\lambda_{\min}(\Pi_{I,k}H_{k}\Pi_{I,k})
. Then
\mu_{d}\leq\mu_{d+1}\leq\dots
and
\lim_{k}\mu_{k}=H_{q,\min}
.

In the unconstrained setting, we obtained our hierarchy of eigencomputations for
p_{\min}
by relating it to
P_{\min}
up to a positive constant, where
P=M(p)\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{d}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}))
is the maximally symmetric Gram operator of
p
. In more details, we proved that
P_{\min}\leq p_{\min}\leq\frac{P_{\min}}{\delta(d)}
, where
\delta(d)
is defined in (
61
); in particular,

\displaystyle p_{\min}=0\iff P_{\min}=0.

(141)

It is natural to ask: Can we do the same in the constrained setting? If (
p_{q,\min}=0\iff P_{q,\min}=0
), then we can again get a hierarchy of eigencomputations for
p_{q,\min}
using Theorem
7.1
and similar arguments as in Theorem
4.3
. Note that
P_{q,\min}\leq p_{q,\min}
always holds, since
P(x,x^{*})=p(x)
for any
x\in\mathcal{V}
. The following proposition shows that the equivalence (
p_{q,\min}=0\iff P_{q,\min}=0
) does not always hold.

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
\mathcal{V}=\real^{3}
, and let

\displaystyle p(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=xz\in S^{2}(\mathcal{V}^{*}),

(142)

\displaystyle q(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=x^{2}+y^{2}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}xz\in S^{2}(\mathcal{V}^{*}).

(143)

Then

\displaystyle p_{q,\min}=0>-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}\geq P_{q,\min}.

(144)

The example provided by this proposition is non-trivial in two ways:

1.

Note that
p_{q,\min}\geq P_{r,\min}
for any collection of complex forms
r_{1},\dots,r_{t}
for which
V_{\mathbb{C}}(r_{1},\dots,r_{t})\cap\mathcal{V}=V(q_{1},\dots,q_{\ell})
. In the above example,
V_{\mathbb{C}}(q)=\mathrm{Cl}_{\mathbb{C}}(V(q))
, where
\mathrm{Cl}_{\mathbb{C}}(\cdot)
denotes the complex Zariski closure
[
Har13
]
, so there is no such collection of forms
r_{1},\dots,r_{t}
for which (
p_{q,\min}=0\iff P_{r,\min}=0
) holds. Indeed, it is easily checked that
q
is irreducible over
\mathbb{C}
and that
V_{\mathbb{C}}(q)
contains at least one real smooth point (the only singular point of
V_{\mathbb{C}}(q)
is
(0,0,0)
). Hence
\mathrm{Cl}_{\mathbb{C}}(V(q))=V_{\mathbb{C}}(q)
by
[
Man20
, Theorem 2.2.9(2)]
.

2.

It is also natural to ask if equality holds under a different choice of Hermitian Gram operator for
p
. This is not the case because when
p
is quadratic,
P
is the unique Hermitian Gram operator for
p
.

Proof of Proposition
7.2
.

Let us first verify that
p_{q,\min}=0
. It is clear that
p_{q,\min}\geq 0
because any solution must satisfy
xz=\sqrt{3}(x^{2}+y^{2})\geq 0
. For the equality to zero, note that
q(0,0,1)=p(0,0,1)=0
. For the last inequality, consider the unit vector
v=(\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}},\frac{i}{\sqrt{3}},-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}})\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
, which is a zero of
q
. Note that

\displaystyle M(p)=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&1/2\\
0&0&0\\
1/2&0&0\end{bmatrix},

(145)

and
P(v,v^{*})=-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}
. This completes the proof.
∎

We conclude this section by noting that there are two other natural ways to adapt our hierarchy to handle constraints, neither of which produce hierarchies of eigencomputations: First, one can use the method described in
[
AH19
