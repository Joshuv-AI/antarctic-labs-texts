AN EFFICIENT ALGORITHM FOR CLUSTERED MULTI-TASK compressive SENSING
Alexander Lin, Demba Ba

Abstract
This paper considers clustered multi-task compressive sensing, a hierarchical model that solves multiple compressive sensing tasks by finding clusters of tasks that leverage shared information to mutually improve signal reconstruction. The existing inference algorithm for this model is computationally expensive and does not scale well in high dimensions. The main bottleneck involves repeated matrix inversion and log-determinant computation for multiple large covariance matrices. We propose a new algorithm that substantially accelerates model inference by avoiding the need to explicitly compute these covariance matrices. Our approach combines Monte Carlo sampling with iterative linear solvers. Our experiments reveal that compared to the existing baseline, our algorithm can be up to thousands of times faster and an order of magnitude more memory-efficient.

Introduction

Compressive sensing
(CS) is a fundamental problem with applications in many areas of signal processing, such as medical imaging
[
1
]
, astronomy
[
2
]
, microscopy
[
3
]
, and photography
[
4
]
. Given measurements
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and a sensing matrix
\mathbf{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}
with
N<D
, the goal is to find a sparse signal
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
that satisfies
\boldsymbol{y}=\mathbf{\Phi}\boldsymbol{z}+\text{noise}
.
Certain applications (e.g. multi-contrast MRI
[
5
]
) encounter multiple CS problems
(\boldsymbol{y}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(1)}),\ldots,(\boldsymbol{y}^{(T)},\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(T)})
, which require corresponding solutions
\boldsymbol{z}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{z}^{(T)}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
.
Multi-task compressive sensing

[
6
]
is a popular approach for settings in which
\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(t)}\}_{t=1}^{T}
are known to have the same non-zero support. This model leverages shared information to jointly solve the
T
tasks, outperforming methods that solve each task separately.

In many situations, it is unreasonable to assume that all tasks have the same sparsity structure. Using multi-task compressive sensing for unrelated tasks with differing supports among
\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(t)}\}_{t=1}^{T}
can lead to worse performance than solving each task separately
[
7
]
. Thus, given multiple CS problems, we would like to ideally learn the structure of task relationships and only share information between related tasks; this allows for improvement in overall performance for all tasks.

We consider
clustered multi-task compressive sensing
, a hierarchical model that solves multiple CS problems in which the inter-relatedness of the solutions are apriori unknown. The model automatically determines how to divide the
T
tasks into
C
clusters, only sharing information between related tasks within a cluster. Although variations of this model have been studied before
[
7
,
8
]
, the standard inference algorithm is too computationally demanding, making it impractical for high dimensions
D
and large number of tasks
T
. The main bottleneck is repeated matrix inversion and log-determinant calculation for many
D\times D
covariance matrices, requiring
O(TCD^{2})
-space and
O(TCD^{3})
-time per iteration.

We propose a new algorithm that is more efficient, reducing the space complexity to
O(TCD)
and the time complexity to
O(TC\tau_{D})
, where
\tau_{D}\leq O(D^{2})
is the time needed to multiply a vector by the sensing matrix
\mathbb{\Phi}
. The key idea is to use techniques from Monte Carlo sampling and numerical linear algebra to circumvent the need to form the large covariance matrices. Our algorithm extends and generalizes the state-of-the-art method that we previously developed for Bayesian compressive sensing
[
9
]
to a more challenging setting involving mixture models
[
10
]
. In experiments, we show that our algorithm can be up to thousands of times faster than EM, reducing hours of computation to a few seconds.

MODEL AND BACKGROUND

The
clustered multi-task compressive sensing
model for
T
tasks and
C
clusters has the following generative structure:

\displaystyle{a}^{(t)}

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{Categorical}({\pi}^{(1)},\ldots,{\pi}^{(C)}),

(1)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}^{(t)}\>|\>{a}^{(t)}=c,\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(c)}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(c)})^{-1}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}^{(t)}\>|\>\boldsymbol{z}^{(t)}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(t)}\boldsymbol{z}^{(t)},\tfrac{1}{\beta}\mathbb{I}),\quad t=1,\ldots,T.

This model posits that each CS task
t
has an unknown cluster assignment
{a}^{(t)}
that is one of
C
options. The
C
clusters have prior probabilities
{\pi}^{(1)},\ldots,{\pi}^{(C)}\in[0,1]
, which sum to one. For simplicity, we assume that
\{{\pi}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}
are known, but they can also be learned by the model
[
10
]
. All tasks within a particular cluster
c
are interrelated and share a common non-negative
regularization parameter

\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(c)}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, which the model will learn through an inference algorithm. For a particular dimension
d
, if
{\alpha}^{(c)}_{d}
is large, then latent vectors
\boldsymbol{z}^{(t)}
within cluster
c
have small variance
1/{\alpha}^{(c)}_{d}
around zero for that dimension; in the limiting case where
{\alpha}^{(c)}_{d}\to\infty
, we have exact sparsity, i.e.
{z}^{(t)}_{d}\to 0
for all
t
such that
{a}^{(t)}=c
. Finally, given each
\boldsymbol{z}^{(t)}
, the model lets the measurements be
\boldsymbol{y}^{(t)}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(t)}\boldsymbol{z}^{(t)}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{(t)}
for Gaussian noise
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{(t)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1/\beta)
.

Eq. (
1
) is a combination of Bayesian compressive sensing
[
11
]
and mixture modeling
[
10
]
. For
C=1
, it reduces to the well-known multi-task CS model of
[
6
]
, in which every task has the same parameter. However, when
C>1
, it allows for the more general case in which not all
T
tasks have the same sparsity structure.
This model can be fit to data
\{\boldsymbol{y}^{(t)}\}_{t=1}^{T}
by maximizing the log-likelihood
L(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=1/T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p(\boldsymbol{y}^{(t)}\>|\>\boldsymbol{\theta})
of the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}:=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(c)}\}_{c=1}^{C}
. The objective
L
is known to encourage some components of each
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(c)}
to diverge to
\infty
, sparsifying
\boldsymbol{z}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{z}^{(T)}\>|\>\boldsymbol{\theta}

[
12
]
.

The standard algorithm for optimizing
L
is
expectation-maximization
(EM)
[
13
,
8
]
, which cycles between two steps. Given estimates
\boldsymbol{\widehat{\theta}}:=\{\boldsymbol{\widehat{\alpha}}^{(c)}\}_{c=1}^{C}
from the previous iteration, the E-step creates a surrogate
Q(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{\widehat{\theta}})
that lowerbounds
L(\boldsymbol{\theta})
.
The M-step maximizes this surrogate to find new parameters
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\theta}}:=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}Q(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{\widehat{\theta}})
.
EM theory guarantees that
L(\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\theta}})\geq L(\boldsymbol{\widehat{\theta}})

[
13
]
, which means that we can optimize
L
by repeating these two steps. For the model in Eq. (
1
), the
Q
-function is

\displaystyle Q(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{\widehat{\theta}})\hskip-1.99997pt:=\hskip-1.99997pt\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{{a}^{(t)},\boldsymbol{z}^{(t)}|\boldsymbol{y}^{(t)},\boldsymbol{\widehat{\theta}}}[\log p({a}^{(t)},\boldsymbol{z}^{(t)},\boldsymbol{y}^{(t)}|\boldsymbol{\theta})]\hskip-8.00003pt

(2)

\displaystyle\cong\frac{1}{TC}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=1,\\
c=1\end{subarray}}^{T,C}q^{(t,c)}\hskip-3.00003pt\left[\sum_{d=1}^{D}\frac{\log{\alpha}^{(c)}_{d}}{2}-\frac{{\alpha}^{(c)}_{d}}{2}\hskip-1.99997pt\left[({\mu}^{(t,c)}_{d})^{2}+{\Sigma}^{(t,c)}_{d,d}\right]\right]

where

is the posterior probability of assigning task

to cluster
c
;
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(t,c)}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(t,c)}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
are the mean and covariance of the Gaussian conditional posterior density
p(\boldsymbol{z}^{(t)}\>|\>{a}^{(t)}=c,\boldsymbol{y}^{(t)},\boldsymbol{\widehat{\theta}})
; and
\cong
denotes equality up to additive constants with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. The analytic forms of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(t,c)},\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(t,c)},q^{(t,c)}
are

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(t,c)}:=\left(\beta({\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(t)}})^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(t)}}+\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\widehat{\alpha}}^{(c)})\right)^{-1},\quad\forall t,c

(3)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(t,c)}:=\beta\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(t,c)}({\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(t)}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}^{(t)},\quad\forall t,c

\displaystyle\hskip-3.99994pt\begin{bmatrix}{q}^{(t,1)}\\
\vdots\\
{q}^{(t,C)}\end{bmatrix}:=\mathrm{softmax}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2}{\ell}^{(t,1)}+\log{\pi}^{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
\frac{1}{2}{\ell}^{(t,C)}+\log{\pi}^{(C)}\end{bmatrix}\right),\quad\forall t

\displaystyle{\ell}^{(t,c)}:=\log\det\mathbb{\Sigma}^{(t,c)}+\sum_{d=1}^{D}\log{\widehat{\alpha}}^{(c)}_{d}+\beta(\boldsymbol{y}^{(t)})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(t)}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(t,c)}

By differentiating Eq. (
2
) with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, we derive the M-step update for new parameters
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\theta}}:=\{\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\alpha}}^{(c)}\}_{c=1}^{C}
as

\displaystyle{\widetilde{\alpha}}^{(c)}_{d}:=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}q^{(t,c)}}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}q^{(t,c)}\cdot[({\mu}^{(t,c)}_{d})^{2}+{\Sigma}^{(t,c)}_{d,d}]},\quad\forall c,d.

(4)

EM repeats Eq. (
3
) and (
4
) until convergence. Eq. (
3
) is expensive because it involves matrix inversion and log-determinant calculation for the large covariance matrix
\mathbb{\Sigma}^{(t,c)}
, which requires
O(D^{3})
-time and
O(D^{2})
-space. For
T
tasks and
C
clusters,
TC
different covariances must be computed. Thus, EM does not scale well for large
D
. In the next section, we introduce a new method that makes EM more efficient.

3
Algorithm

Our proposed algorithm is called
covariance-free expectation-maximization
(CoFEM). It accelerates EM by avoiding explicit formation of the large covariance matrices
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(t,c)}
. This algorithm extends our previously developed method for single-task CS
[
9
,
14
]
to the more complicated multi-task mixture model of Eq. (
1
). Our key observation is that Eq. (
4
) only requires three types of quantities that depend on
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(t,c)}
: the posterior means
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(t,c)}
, the posterior variances (i.e. diagonal elements of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(t,c)}
), and the posterior cluster probabilities
{q}^{(t,c)}
. We will show how to estimate these quantities while avoiding the
TC
matrix inversions and log-determinant calculations of standard EM. Our approach combines advances in Monte Carlo sampling and numerical linear algebra.

3.1

We begin by describing how to estimate
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and the diagonal elements of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}

(for notational simplicity we omit the superscripts

+1

[

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}^{(k)}:=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{p}^{(k)},\forall k,\quad\quad\boldsymbol{s}:=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{p}^{(k)}\odot\boldsymbol{x}^{(k)},

(5)

where

denotes element-wise product. It follows that

is an unbiased Monte Carlo estimator of the diagonal of
\mathbb{\Sigma}
(i.e.
\mathbb{E}[s_{d}]=\Sigma_{d,d}
for all

)
[
15
]

\boldsymbol{\mu}
(Eq. (

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{(k)}=\boldsymbol{p}^{(k)},\forall k,\quad\quad\mathbb{A}\boldsymbol{\mu}=\beta\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y},

(6)

for

. We use

(Alg.
1
)
[
16
]
to solve these systems without forming the matrix
\mathbb{\Sigma}
.
Thus, CoFEM efficiently estimates
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and the diagonal elements of

while avoiding costly matrix inversions. As shown by

, typically small values of

3.2
Estimating Posterior Cluster Probabilities

Unlike the single-task setting of
[
9
]
, clustered multi-task CS also requires posterior cluster probabilities
{q}^{(t,c)}
. As shown by Eq. (
3
), most of the quantities in the definition of
{q}^{(t,c)}
are straightforward to compute; the main remaining bottleneck is the
\log\det\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(t,c)}
term. Directly computing a log-determinant is an expensive operation costing
O(D^{3})
-time. Instead, we show how to leverage the probe vectors and CG outputs from diagonal estimation (Eq. (
6
)) to efficiently estimate
\log\det\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(t,c)}
in
O(KU^{3})
-time, where
K,U\ll D
. Let
\mathbb{A}:=\mathbb{\Sigma}^{-1}
and
\mathbb{L}:=\log\mathbb{A}
be its matrix logarithm. Then, we have
\log\det\mathbb{\Sigma}=-\log\det\mathbb{A}=-\mathrm{tr}(\mathbb{L})

[
17
, Th. 2.12]
. We propose to estimate
-\mathrm{tr}(\mathbb{L})
using the
Hutchinson trace estimator

\nu:=-1/K\sum_{q=1}^{K}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(k)})^{\top}\mathbb{L}\boldsymbol{p}^{(k)}
, where
\{\boldsymbol{p}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}
are Rademacher probe vectors
[
18
]
. This estimator is unbiased, meaning that
\mathbb{E}[\nu]=-\mathrm{tr}(\mathbb{L})=\log\det\mathbb{\Sigma}
.

The final question is how to obtain
\boldsymbol{p}^{\top}\mathbb{L}\boldsymbol{p}
, where
\boldsymbol{p}
is a Rademacher probe (we drop the index
(k)
for simplicity). We adapt the methods of Sec. 3.1 to compute a
Lanczos quadrature

[
19
,
20
]
: Recall that in Eq. (
6
), we use CG to solve the system
\mathbb{A}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{p}
. CG theory shows that
D
steps of Alg.
1
implicitly performs a Lancozs tridiagonalization
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{R}^{\top}\mathbb{\Gamma}\mathbb{R}

[
21
, Sec. 6.7.3]
, where
\mathbb{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
is an orthonormal matrix whose rows are normalized CG residuals
\{\boldsymbol{r}_{u}/\lVert\boldsymbol{r}_{u}\rVert\}_{u=1}^{D}
(line 5) and
\mathbb{\Gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
is a symmetric tridiagonal matrix assembled from CG step sizes
\{\gamma_{u},\xi_{u}\}_{u=1}^{D}
(lines 3 & 6), i.e.

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1,1}:=\tfrac{1}{\gamma_{1}},\;\;\Gamma_{u,u}:=\tfrac{1}{\gamma_{u}}+\tfrac{\xi_{u-1}}{\gamma_{u-1}},\;\;\Gamma_{u,u-1}:=\tfrac{\sqrt{\xi_{u-1}}}{\gamma_{u-1}}

(7)

for
1<u\leq D
. Let
\mathbb{\Gamma}:=\mathbb{S}^{\top}\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\mathbb{S}
be the eigendecomposition of
\mathbb{\Gamma}
for eigenvalues
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
and eigenvectors stored as rows of
\mathbb{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
. Since
\mathbb{A}=(\mathbb{S}\mathbb{R})^{\top}\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\mathbb{S}\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{L}=\log\mathbb{A}
, we have
\mathbb{L}=(\mathbb{S}\mathbb{R})^{\top}\mathrm{diag}(\log\boldsymbol{\lambda})\mathbb{S}\mathbb{R}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\hskip-5.50003pt\boldsymbol{p}^{\top}\mathbb{L}\boldsymbol{p}=(\mathbb{S}\hskip-3.99994pt{\underbrace{\mathbb{R}\boldsymbol{p}}_{\sqrt{D}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{1}}}\hskip-3.00003pt)\hskip-1.00006pt^{\top}\hskip-1.49994pt\mathrm{diag}(\log\boldsymbol{\lambda})\mathbb{S}\hskip-3.99994pt{\underbrace{\mathbb{R}\boldsymbol{p}}_{\sqrt{D}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{1}}}\hskip-3.99994pt=D\sum_{u=1}^{D}S_{u,1}^{2}\hskip-1.00006pt\log\lambda_{u}

where
\boldsymbol{e}_{1}:=[1\;0\;\cdots\;0]^{\top}
and
\mathbb{R}\boldsymbol{p}=\sqrt{D}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{1}
because using CG to solve
\mathbb{A}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{p}
with initial condition
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}=\boldsymbol{0}
means that the first scaled residual (i.e. first row of
\mathbb{R}
) is
\boldsymbol{p}/\lVert\boldsymbol{p}\rVert
and all subsequent residuals/rows of
\mathbb{R}
are orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{p}

[
20
,
21
]
.

Though we can use Eq. (
8
) to compute
\boldsymbol{p}^{\top}\mathbb{L}\boldsymbol{p}
, it requires eigendecomposition of the full
D\times D
matrix
\mathbb{\Gamma}
, which is an expensive
O(D^{3})
-time operation. As shown by
[

]
, typically
U\ll D
steps of CG suffice for accurate diagonal estimation. To save computation, we would like to only use these

steps to estimate
\boldsymbol{p}^{\top}\mathbb{L}\boldsymbol{p}
, yet this means that only
\mathbb{\bar{\Gamma}}\in\mathbb{R}^{U\times U}
, the upper-left submatrix of
\mathbb{\Gamma}
, can be generated by Eq. (

). Let
\mathbb{\bar{S}}^{\top}\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\bar{\lambda}})\mathbb{\bar{S}}
be the eigendecomposition of
\mathbb{\bar{\Gamma}}
, for
\mathbb{\bar{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{U\times U}
and
\boldsymbol{\bar{\lambda}}\in\mathbb{R}^{U}
. The extremal values of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, which dominate Eq. (
8
), are well-approximated by those of
\boldsymbol{\bar{\lambda}}

[

]
. This justifies the following estimator for

, which we compute using the byproducts of

\displaystyle\bar{\nu}(\mathbb{\Sigma}):=-\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{u=1}^{U}D\cdot({\bar{S}}^{(k)}_{u,1})^{2}\cdot\log{\bar{\lambda}}^{(k)}_{u}

3.3
Full Algorithm and Complexity Analysis

Alg.
2
summarizes the full CoFEM algorithm. The main cost is solving
K+1
systems for each task
t
and cluster
c
. We can parallelize CG to solve all
TC(K+1)
systems simultaneously on multi-core hardware, such as GPUs
[
9
,
15
]
. In addition, when solving a system with
\mathbb{A}:=\beta\mathbb{\Phi}^{\top}\mathbb{\Phi}+\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
, the matrix
\mathbb{A}
never needs to be explicitly computed; CG (Alg.
1
) only requires a method to compute the matrix-vector product
\mathbb{A}\boldsymbol{v}
for any
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
. Thus, each CG step costs
\tau_{D}
, the time complexity of applying the sensing matrix
\mathbb{\Phi}
(and its transpose) to a vector
\boldsymbol{v}
. Though
\tau_{D}
is upperbounded by
O(D^{2})
, it can be
O(D\log D)
or
O(D)
in many signal processing applications in which
\mathbb{\Phi}
has special structure (e.g. Fourier operators, wavelets, convolutions, low-rank structure, sparsity).

In summary, CoFEM reduces EM’s time complexity from
O(TCD^{3})
to
O(TCK(\tau_{D}U+U^{3}))
, where for each task, cluster, and probe,
\tau_{D}U
is the cost of CG and
U^{3}
is the cost of eigendecomposition for the log-determinant. Additionally, CoFEM reduces EM’s space complexity from
O(TCD^{2})
to
O(TCK(D+U))
. As shown in both
[
9
]
and Sec.
4
,
K
and
U
can be held as small, constant values even as
D
becomes very large, allowing CoFEM to scale much better than EM.

4
Experiments

We perform multi-task CS experiments to validate our new algorithm. For each task
t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
, we simulate a true sparse signal
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
with
5\%
of its components sampled from
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and the rest equal to zero. Each sensing matrix is
\mathbb{\Phi}^{(t)}:=\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{(t)}\mathbb{F}
, where
\mathbb{F}\in\mathbb{C}^{D\times D}
is the discrete Fourier transform and
\mathbb{\Omega}^{(t)}\in[0,1]^{N\times D}
is an undersampling mask (i.e. randomly-chosen row-wise subset of the identity matrix) with
N=D/4
. This form of sensing matrix is common to many CS applications
[
5
]
. We generate the measurements
\boldsymbol{y}^{(t)}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(t)}\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}^{(t)}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{(t)}
, where
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{(t)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
for
\sigma=0.05
.

Our first experiment demonstrates the utility of multi-task CS when there exists subsets of interrelated tasks. We simulate two groups of four tasks (i.e.
