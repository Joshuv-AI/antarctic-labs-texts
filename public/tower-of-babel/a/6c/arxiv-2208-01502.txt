A Multi-body Tracking Framework - From Rigid Objects to Kinematic Structures
Manuel Stoiber, Martin Sundermeyer, Wout Boerdijk, and Rudolph Triebel

Abstract
Kinematic structures are very common in the real world. They range from simple articulated objects to complex mechanical systems. However, despite their relevance, most model-based 3D tracking methods only consider rigid objects. To overcome this limitation, we propose a flexible framework that allows the extension of existing 6DoF algorithms to kinematic structures. Our approach focuses on methods that employ Newton-like optimization techniques, which are widely used in object tracking. The framework considers both tree-like and closed kinematic structures and allows a flexible configuration of joints and constraints. To project equations from individual rigid bodies to a multi-body system, Jacobians are used. For closed kinematic chains, a novel formulation that features Lagrange multipliers is developed. In a detailed mathematical proof, we show that our constraint formulation leads to an exact kinematic solution and converges in a single iteration. Based on the proposed framework, we extend ICG , which is a state-of-the-art rigid object tracking algorithm, to multi-body tracking. For the evaluation, we create a highly-realistic synthetic dataset that features a large number of sequences and various robots. Based on this dataset, we conduct a wide variety of experiments that demonstrate the excellent performance of the developed framework and our multi-body tracker.

I
Introduction

Tracking objects in 3D space and estimating their
6DoF
(
6DoF
) pose is an essential task in computer vision.
The goal is to provide the position and orientation with respect to the camera at high frequency, given consecutive images and the 3D model.
For kinematic structures, the object’s configuration is also required.
It directly corresponds to the
6DoF
pose of all individual rigid bodies in the system.
Typical applications of 3D object tracking range from augmented reality to robotics.
For augmented reality, the pose and configuration are used to superimpose digital information on real-world objects.
In robotics, continuous estimates are needed to facilitate complex manipulation tasks and allow robots to react to unpredicted changes in the environment.
In many such applications, objects are not rigid.
Instead, they consist of multiple connected bodies that form kinematic structures.
Typical examples range from various tools, furniture, and appliances to machinery and robots.

For 3D object tracking, multiple challenges exist, including occlusions, cluttered environments, motion blur, textureless surfaces, object symmetries, computational complexity, and many more.
As a consequence, a wide variety of approaches have been developed

Methods are typically differentiated by their use of keypoints

Depending on the application, all techniques have their own advantages and disadvantages, showing different properties with respect to accuracy, robustness, and runtime.
However, most state-of-the-art methods only implement the tracking of rigid objects without considering multi-body systems.
While, in theory, such algorithms could track bodies independently, in most cases, the task is unfeasible without kinematic information.
As a consequence, many methods with desirable properties can not be used in applications that feature kinematic structures.

To overcome this limitation, we propose a flexible framework that allows the extension of rigid object tracking methods to kinematic structures.
Our approach focuses on Newton-like optimization, which is widely used in various tracking techniques.
The resulting framework allows a flexible configuration of multi-body systems with various joints and constraints.
To the best of our knowledge, it is the first that is able to accurately model closed kinematic chains.
Based on the developed framework, we extend our previous work

•

A unified framework that combines the projection to a minimal parameterization and the application of pose constraints in a single formulation.

•

•

In the following, we first discuss related work on multi-body object tracking.
Subsequently, our multi-body tracking framework is introduced, which allows to extend tracking algorithms that use Newton-like optimization techniques.
The framework is formulated without considering a particular parameterization.

III
Multi-body Tracking

In the following, we derive a flexible framework that allows to extend tracking algorithms from rigid objects to kinematic structures.
For this, the general architecture of methods that are compatible with our approach is discussed.
This is followed by an extension to tree-like structures and, finally, to closed kinematic structures.
The provided formulation is very general and allows different parameterizations of poses, joints, and constraints.

III-A

Rigid Objects

For the task of 3D object tracking, a wide variety of algorithms that use different sources of information have been developed.
Based on the considered data, most methods derive an energy function
E(\boldsymbol{\theta})
or
PDF
(
PDF
)
p(\boldsymbol{\theta})
that depends on the pose variation vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
Given such a function, the pose that best explains the considered data is found by minimizing the energy or maximizing the probability.
While different techniques have been employed for this optimization task, the following framework focuses on Newton-like methods.

In general, Newton-like optimization iteratively estimates the variation vector and updates the object pose.
The estimated pose variation
\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}
is thereby calculated as follows

\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}=-\boldsymbol{H}^{-1}\boldsymbol{g},

(1)

where the gradient vector
\boldsymbol{g}
and the Hessian matrix
\boldsymbol{H}
are the first- and second-order derivatives of the energy function
E(\boldsymbol{\theta})
or the negative logarithmic probability
-\ln(p(\boldsymbol{\theta}))
.
In the following, we derive our approach for the energy function
E(\boldsymbol{\theta})
.
Note, however, that with the relation
E(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\ln(p(\boldsymbol{\theta}))
, exactly the same equations can be used for
PDF
.
Finally, (
1
) is not only limited to classical Newton optimization with an exact Hessian matrix but also allows the use of approximations from Gauss-Newton or quasi-Newton methods.
As a consequence, many popular algorithms, such as
RAPID

[
5
]
,
PWP3D

[
11
]
, or the object tracker employed in
Slam++

[
53
]
, are compatible with the developed framework.

III-B

Tree-like Structures

In general, tree-like kinematic structures consist of individual bodies that are connected by joints.
All bodies, except for the root, have a single parent.
Based on those connections, the
6DoF
pose variation
\boldsymbol{\theta}
of each body is described by joint variations
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{j}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textrm{j}}}
along the kinematic chain from the root.
The number
n_{\textrm{j}}\in[0..6]
describes how many degrees of freedom a particular joint allows for a body relative to its parent.
Finally, the full variation of a kinematic structure with
n
bodies is described by a combination of all joint variations
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{j}0}^{\top}\dots\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{j}n}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}
.
Note that, for the root body, the joint variation
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{j}0}
directly describes the pose variation
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
.

.

Starting from the energy functions
E_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})
of individual bodies, the combined energy of the kinematic structure can be calculated as the sum of the energy of all bodies

E_{\textrm{k}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}E_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}),

(2)

where variation vectors
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}
of individual bodies are functions of the combined variation
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}
.
Given the definitions of gradient vectors and Hessian matrices, we can now write

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}_{\textrm{k}}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial E_{\textrm{k}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}}\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}=\boldsymbol{0}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial E_{i}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}}\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}=\boldsymbol{0}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{g}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{J}_{i},

(3)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathllap{\boldsymbol{H}_{\textrm{k}}}=\frac{\partial^{2}E_{\textrm{k}}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}}^{\!2}}\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}=\boldsymbol{0}}&\approx\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}}^{\!\top}\frac{\partial^{2}E_{i}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}^{\!2}}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}}\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}=\boldsymbol{0}}\\
&\approx\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{J}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{H}_{i}\,\boldsymbol{J}_{i},\end{aligned}

(4)

with
\boldsymbol{g}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{H}_{i}
the gradient vector and the Hessian matrix of the rigid body
i
.
Note that second-order derivatives of pose variations
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}
with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}
were neglected.
The Jacobian matrices
\boldsymbol{J}_{i}
describe how the variation of the entire kinematic structure affects the pose of individual bodies.
A detailed derivation of the required body Jacobians is given in Section
IV-B
.
One major difference of our formulation in comparison to the approach of Lowe
[
18
]
is that we do not calculate the change of each measurement with respect to the combined variation
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}
.
Instead, the change of a body’s measurements is first computed for the minimal
6DoF
pose variation
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}
and then projected to the full kinematic structure.
This has the advantage that the same gradient vectors and Hessian matrices as for single object tracking can be used.
In addition, for kinematic structures with large numbers of bodies and measurements, it is more efficient.

III-C

Closed Kinematic Structures

To model closed kinematic structures, we start from the previously defined tree-like structures and include additional constraints.
A constraint describes any relation between bodies that can be expressed by a constraint equation
\boldsymbol{b}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}})=\boldsymbol{0}
.
A simple example is a rotational joint, where translational differences normal to the rotation axis have to be zero.
An illustration of the topology of a tree-like kinematic structure with constraints between individual bodies is shown in Fig.
2
.
Given
n
constraints, the full constraint equation for the kinematic structure is written as
\boldsymbol{b}_{\textrm{k}}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{b}_{0}^{\top}\dots\boldsymbol{b}_{n}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}
.
To integrate those constraints into the combined energy function
E_{\textrm{k}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}})
, we write the following Lagrangian function

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}},\boldsymbol{\lambda})=E_{\textrm{k}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}})+\boldsymbol{b}_{\textrm{k}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\lambda},

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
is the vector of Lagrange multipliers.
To find a solution for
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}
that satisfies the imposed constraints and minimizes the energy function, the following equation has to be solved

\nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}},\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{g}_{\textrm{k}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}})+\boldsymbol{B}_{\textrm{k}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\lambda}\\
\boldsymbol{b}_{\textrm{k}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}})\end{bmatrix}\overset{!}{=}\boldsymbol{0},

(6)

where the constraint Jacobian
\boldsymbol{B}_{\textrm{k}}
is the first-order derivative of
\boldsymbol{b}_{\textrm{k}}
with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}
.
Detailed derivations of the constraint function and Jacobian are given in Section
IV-C
.

Analogous to the minimization of the nonlinear energy function
E(\boldsymbol{\theta})
of a single object, we want to linearize (
6
) around
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}=\boldsymbol{0}
and iteratively find a solution.
For this, we use the first-order approximations
\boldsymbol{g}_{\textrm{k}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}})\approx\boldsymbol{g}_{\textrm{k}}+\boldsymbol{H}_{\textrm{k}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}
and
\boldsymbol{b}_{\textrm{k}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}})\approx\boldsymbol{b}_{\textrm{k}}+\boldsymbol{B}_{\textrm{k}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}
to derive the following linear equation

\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{\textrm{k}}\\
\boldsymbol{\hat{\lambda}}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{H}_{\textrm{k}}&\boldsymbol{B}_{\textrm{k}}^{\top}\\
\boldsymbol{B}_{\textrm{k}}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{g}_{\textrm{k}}\\
\boldsymbol{b}_{\textrm{k}}\end{bmatrix}.

(7)

Similar to (
1
), the derived linear equation is used to estimate
\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{\textrm{k}}
and to iteratively minimize
E_{\textrm{k}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}})
while, at the same time, considering the constraints
\boldsymbol{b}_{\textrm{k}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}})=\boldsymbol{0}
.
Note that, in general, given a positive definite Hessian
\boldsymbol{H}_{\textrm{k}}
and unique and non-contradicting constraint equations that lead to non-zero and linearly independent row vectors in the constraint Jacobian
\boldsymbol{B}_{\textrm{k}}
, the matrix in (
7
) is invertible and a unique solution exists for the estimated variation vector
\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{\textrm{k}}
.

Finally, in contrast to the
postimposed
method of Drummond and Cipolla
[
6
]
, which first predicts unconstrained poses and applies constraints later, our formulation directly estimates a variation vector that is compatible with constraints.
In addition, the approach is not limited to velocity constraints.
Together with our formulation for tree-like kinematic structures, the developed multi-body tracking framework supports an efficient minimal parameterization while simultaneously providing the flexibility to add constraints for closed chains and temporary limitations.
It, therefore, combines the best of both worlds.

IV
Parameterization

In this section, we derive all equations that are required to define a kinematic structure.
For the pose parameterization, we thereby focus on the axis-angle representation.
Note that while tracking algorithms based on Euler angles, twist coordinates, or quaternions have also been developed, in our experience, this parameterization is the most popular.
After an introduction to general mathematical concepts, Jacobian matrices and constraint equations are derived.
In both cases, we allow to lock or release the rotation and translation along individual coordinate axes, facilitating various different joints and constraints.
Finally, we describe how to update the pose of individual bodies.

IV-A

Preliminaries

To define the pose between two reference frames
A
and
B
, the following transformation matrix
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{B}}\in\mathbb{SE}(3)
is used

{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{B}}=\begin{bmatrix}{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}&{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{B}}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&1\end{bmatrix},

(8)

where
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}\in\mathbb{SO}(3)
is a rotation matrix and
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a translation vector.
They together describe the transformation from
B
to
A
.
For the variation of poses with a minimal set of parameters, we adopt the axis-angle representation for the rotation and write

\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\begin{bmatrix}\exp([\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}}]_{\times})&\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{t}}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&1\end{bmatrix},

(9)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{t}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
are the rotational and translational components of the variation vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{t}}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}
, and
[\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}}]_{\times}
is the skew-symmetric cross-product matrix of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}}
.
Finally, to project the variation vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}
from reference frame
B
to
A
, the following adjoint representation is used

\Ad({}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{B}})=\begin{bmatrix}{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
[{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{B}}]_{\times}\,{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}&{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}\end{bmatrix}.

(10)

Based on those general mathematical concepts, we are able to describe kinematic structures and derive the required body Jacobians and constraint equations.

IV-B

Body Jacobians

Each body has a defined location relative to its joint and corresponding parent.
In the following, we introduce a general class of joints that allow motion along a user-defined set of rotational and translational axes.
Based on the joint reference frame
J
, motion along these directions is modeled by the variation vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{j}}
.
The full
6DoF
pose variation
\boldsymbol{\theta}
of a body’s model frame
M
given the variation of its parent and joint is then defined as follows

\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{\theta})={}_{\textrm{M}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{P}}\,\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{p}})\,{}_{\textrm{P}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{J}}\,\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{\bar{\theta}}_{\textrm{j}})\,{}_{\textrm{J}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{M}},

(11)

where
P
is the reference frame of the parent and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{p}}
the corresponding
6DoF
variation vector.
An illustration of the transformations is shown in Fig.
3
.

For a typical joint that only allows movement along specified axes of the joint frame, we simply set fixed directions to zero.

To project equations of individual bodies to the multi-body system, Jacobians are used.
Given a body’s joint and parent, we are able to formulate a recursive strategy to calculate the Jacobian.
Based on the Jacobian of the parent body
\boldsymbol{J}_{\textrm{p}}
, we calculate

\boldsymbol{J}=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}}\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}=\boldsymbol{0}}=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{p}}}\boldsymbol{J}_{\textrm{p}}+\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}&\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{j}}}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix}\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}=\boldsymbol{0}},

(12)

where the partial derivative
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{j}}}
has to be added at the correct location that corresponds to the position of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{j}}
in the vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}
.
The advantage of this recursive strategy is that it is not necessary to assemble the derivatives of all individual joints along the kinematic chain.
Instead, we simply consider the kinematic chain using the Jacobian of the parent.
For the root body, the parent Jacobian
\boldsymbol{J}_{\textrm{p}}
is simply zero.
To calculate the Jacobian, we now only require the partial derivatives of the body variation with respect to the variations of the joint and parent.

Starting from (
11
) and knowing that the adjoint representation in (
10
) can be used to project variation vectors between reference frames, we write the following relations

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{j}}=\boldsymbol{0}}

\displaystyle=\Ad({}_{\textrm{M}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{P}})\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{p}},

(13)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{p}}=\boldsymbol{0}}

\displaystyle=\Ad({}_{\textrm{M}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{J}})\,\boldsymbol{\bar{\theta}}_{\textrm{j}}.

(14)

As can be seen from those equations, the required first-order derivatives are

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{p}}}\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}=\boldsymbol{0}}

\displaystyle=\Ad({}_{\textrm{M}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{P}}),

(15)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\bar{\theta}}_{\textrm{j}}}\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}=\boldsymbol{0}}

\displaystyle=\Ad({}_{\textrm{M}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{J}}).

(16)

Note that (
16
) considers the derivative with respect to the extended variation vector
\boldsymbol{\bar{\theta}}_{\textrm{j}}
.
To get the first-order derivative
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{j}}}\big|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}=\boldsymbol{0}}
with respect to the variation vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{j}}
, one simply assembles the columns from the partial derivative in (
16
) that are considered by
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{j}}
.
With the derived formulation, we are able to model various joints, including revolute, prismatic, or spherical joints.
For more specific joints, such as screw connections, it is typically also straightforward to derive the necessary first-order derivatives as a combination of the motion along individual axes.
Instead of directly assembling columns from (
16
), one then simply multiplies with the joint’s partial derivative
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\bar{\theta}}_{\textrm{j}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{j}}}
to get the derivative
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{j}}}=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\bar{\theta}}_{\textrm{j}}}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\bar{\theta}}_{\textrm{j}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{j}}}
required for the body Jacobian.

IV-C

Constraint Equations

In the following, we derive constraints that consider rotational and translational differences between two bodies along individual axes.
Differences are calculated with respect to the constraint reference frames
A
and
B
, which are defined for the corresponding bodies
a
and
b
.
Constrained differences between those two frames are enforced to be zero.
The relative pose between frames
A
and
B
, given the pose variation vectors
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{b}
, is written as

{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{B}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{b})={}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}\,\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a})^{-1}\,{}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{M}_{b}}\,\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{b})\,{}_{\textrm{M}_{b}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{B}},

(17)

where
\textrm{M}_{a}
and
\textrm{M}_{b}
are the model frames of bodies
a
and
b
.
An illustration of the transformation is shown in Fig.
4
.

Fig. 4:

Illustration of a constraint between two bodies
a
and
b
.
The model coordinate frames
\textrm{M}_{a}
and
\textrm{M}_{b}
, as well as the variation vectors
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{b}
are illustrated in yellow and blue, respectively.
The constraint coordinate frames
A
and
B
associated with bodies
a
and
b
are colored in red.
The relative transformation
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{B}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{b})
is calculated from
{}_{\textrm{M}_{b}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{B}}
,
{}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{M}_{b}}
, and
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}
and is considered in the constraint equation.

Based on the relative pose
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{B}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{b})
, we define the extended
6DoF
constraint equation for all rotational and translational axes as follows

\boldsymbol{\bar{b}}_{ab}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{b})=\begin{bmatrix}{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{b})\\
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{B}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{b})\end{bmatrix},

(18)

where
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}
is the rotation vector, and
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{B}}
is the translation vector of the transformation matrix
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{B}}
.
The rotation vector and the rotation matrix are related by the exponential map
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}=\exp([{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}]_{\times})
.
Note that, depending on the directions that should be constrained, only individual elements of the extended constraint equation
\boldsymbol{\bar{b}}_{ab}
are used.
The resulting reduced constraint equation is denoted
\boldsymbol{b}_{ab}
.

Given a constraint equation, the corresponding constraint Jacobian can be calculated using body Jacobians
\boldsymbol{J}
that were derived in the previous section as follows

\boldsymbol{B}_{ab}=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{b}_{ab}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}}\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}=\boldsymbol{0}}=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{b}_{ab}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a}}\boldsymbol{J}_{a}+\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{b}_{ab}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{b}}\boldsymbol{J}_{b}\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}=\boldsymbol{0}}.

(19)

For the required partial derivatives of the extended constraint equation
\boldsymbol{\bar{b}}_{ab}
with respect to the pose variations
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{b}
, the calculation is quite lengthy.
This is especially the case for the rotational constraint, which considers the nontrivial change of the rotation vector
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}
for the variation of the two bodies.
Consequently, we provide the full derivation in Appendix
A
and only state the following final results

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\bar{b}}_{ab}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a}}\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}=\boldsymbol{0}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-\boldsymbol{C}\,{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}\,[{}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{B}}]_{\times}&-{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}\end{bmatrix},

(20)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\bar{b}}_{ab}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{b}}\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}=\boldsymbol{0}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{C}\,{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{b}}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
-{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{b}}\,[{}_{\textrm{M}_{b}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{B}}]_{\times}&{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{b}}\end{bmatrix}.

(21)

To obtain the derivatives for the reduced constraint equation
\boldsymbol{b}_{ab}
, which are required in (
19
), one simply assembles the rows from (
20
) and (
21
) that correspond to the constrained axes of coordinate frame
A
.
Finally, given the Jacobians
\boldsymbol{B}_{ab}
of individual constraints, the full constraint Jacobian
\boldsymbol{B}_{\textrm{k}}
can be assembled by row-wise concatenation of those matrices.

The matrix
\boldsymbol{C}
, which is used in the calculated first-order derivatives and which we call variation matrix, is defined as

\boldsymbol{C}=\frac{\alpha}{2}\cot\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\boldsymbol{I}-\frac{\alpha}{2}[\boldsymbol{e}]_{\times}+\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\cot\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\Big)\boldsymbol{e}\boldsymbol{e}^{\top},

(22)

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is the rotation angle and
\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
\lVert\boldsymbol{e}\rVert_{2}=1
is the rotation axis of the rotation vector
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}=\alpha\boldsymbol{e}
evaluated at
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}=\boldsymbol{0}
.
The matrix describes how the rotation vector
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}
changes with the variation of a subsequent infinitesimal rotation.
It is derived in Appendix
A
as part of the calculation of (
20
) and (
21
).
In Appendix
B
, we show that the variation matrix
\boldsymbol{C}
is a normal, non-symmetric, and non-orthogonal matrix that is directly related to the rotation matrix by

{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}=\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{C}^{-1}\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}.

(23)

Also, for the rotation vector
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}
, we are able to prove that

{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}=\boldsymbol{C}\,{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}=\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}.

(24)

This shows that the rotation vector
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}
is an eigenvector of both the original and transposed variation matrix
\boldsymbol{C}
.

For the approach in Section
III-B
, which uses body Jacobians to project equations to a minimal parameterization, only exact solutions are possible.
At the same time, for the constraint approach in Section
III-C
and the equations proposed in this section, kinematic compliance is not apparent.
Especially for the rotation, which is highly non-linear in Euclidean space, one could expect that multiple iterations of the Newton optimization are required to converge to a kinematically accurate result.
However, in Appendix
C
, we are able to prove mathematically that this is not the case.
In our proof, the developed constraint equations and Jacobians are introduced into the linear equations of the Newton optimization.
Also, the previously developed property that
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}=\boldsymbol{C}{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}
is used.
Based on this, we calculate the relative pose change per iteration.
The results demonstrate that both rotational and translational constraints converge to a kinematically compliant result in a single iteration.
While the proof in Appendix
C
assumes that constraint coordinate frames are equal to model frames, experiments in Section
VI-D
demonstrate that results also hold for more general cases.

If constraint equations are fulfilled and
\boldsymbol{\bar{b}}_{ab}=\boldsymbol{0}
, the coordinate frames
A
and
B
are equal, and the variation matrix
\boldsymbol{C}
reduces to the identity matrix.
As a consequence, the derivatives in (
20
) and (
21
) turn into the adjoint representations
-\Ad({}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{M}_{a}})
and
\Ad({}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{M}_{b}})
.
A short proof of this is given in Appendix
D
.
The derived expressions directly correspond to the formulation of Drummond and Cipolla
[
6
]
.
This equivalence demonstrates that our approach is an extension of their method.
However, the big advantage of our generalization is that we directly operate on pose differences instead of velocities.
This ensures that even if the pose constraint is not fulfilled initially, our method will automatically converge to a consistent result.
As a consequence, it is possible to model closed chains or add temporary constraints that are not currently fulfilled.
Both cases are not viable using velocity constraints.
In addition, we would like to highlight the benefits of directly using the rotation vector in the constraint equations.
Compared to other options, such as using orthogonal axes, our formulation has the advantage that it provides consistent results over the entire space of possible rotations.
We are, therefore, able to successfully recover even from maximum rotational differences of
\alpha=\pi
in a single iteration.

IV-D

Pose Update

Given the derived Jacobians and constraint equations, we are able to estimate the variation vector of the kinematic structure
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{j}0}^{\top}\dots\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{j}n}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}
using the linear relation of the Newton optimization in (
7
).
Similar to the calculation of body Jacobians, we formulate a recursive strategy for the pose update.
With the estimated joint variation
\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{\textrm{j}}
and the pose of the parent, the body pose is updated as follows

{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{M}}^{+}={}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{P}}\,{}_{\textrm{P}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{J}}\,\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{\hat{\bar{\theta}}}_{\textrm{j}})\,{}_{\textrm{J}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{M}},

(25)

where
A
is some arbitrary frame of reference such as the camera frame.
Like in (
11
), the full
6DoF
variation vector
\boldsymbol{\hat{\bar{\theta}}}_{\textrm{j}}
has to be used for the calculation of the transformation matrix.
Again, it is simply created by adding zeros to the fixed axes of our joint variation.
Note that for the pose of the joint coordinate frame
J
, either the transformation with respect to the body
{}_{\textrm{J}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{M}}
or the parent
{}_{\textrm{P}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{J}}
is fixed.
The other is inferred from the previous pose estimates of the body and parent, as well as the fixed transformation.
While most of the time, both options are valid, for some joints, which allow, for example, rotation around two axes, it makes a difference if
{}_{\textrm{J}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{M}}
or
{}_{\textrm{P}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{J}}
is fixed.

Finally, to update the root body, which does not have a parent, the following strategy is adopted

{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{M}_{0}}^{+}={}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{M}_{0}}\,{}_{\textrm{M}_{0}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{J}_{0}}\,\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{\hat{\bar{\theta}}}_{\textrm{j}0})\,{}_{\textrm{J}_{0}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{M}_{0}}.

(26)

It considers the variation with respect to the previous pose estimate in the joint’s frame of reference.
In summary, the developed approach allows us to estimate all body poses in the kinematic structure, ensuring that variation vectors comply with constraints and poses are only updated along free joint directions.

V
Implementation

The following section considers the extension of our work
ICG

[
15
]
to multi-body tracking.

ICG
is a highly-efficient probabilistic tracker that considers sparse region and depth information for the tracking of multiple independent objects.
For an efficient representation of geometry that avoids online rendering, it utilizes a so-called sparse viewpoint model that stores randomly sampled contour and surface points, as well as other information, for views all around the object.
For the region modality, color histograms are used to differentiate between the object and the background.
During tracking, contour points are projected into the image to define the location of so-called correspondence lines.
For each line, a discrete probability distribution for the contour location is computed based on the probability that pixels belong to the foreground or background.
For the depth modality, an
ICP
-like approach is implemented that computes the distance between surface model points and closest correspondence points from the depth image.
It uses the point-to-plane error metric and assumes a normal distribution.
For the maximization of the joint
PDF
,
ICG
employs Newton optimization with Tikhonov regularization.
To parameterize the pose variation, it adopts the axis-angle representation.

ICG
, therefore, fits perfectly into the developed multi-body tracking framework.
For the implementation, we directly build on the available source code
3
3

3

https://github.com/DLR-RM/3DObjectTracking
.
If not stated otherwise, parameter values from the corresponding publication
[
15
]
are used.
In the following, we describe modifications that are required for the extension to multi-body tracking.

For kinematic structures, it is very common that individual bodies intersect or that they are very close together.
Also, different bodies often belong to the same region.
As a consequence, one of the main modifications of
ICG
considers the validation of contour and surface points.
To validate surface points from depth modalities, a silhouette image is rendered that contains all bodies.
Based on this image, surface points of a body are considered valid if they fall on the corresponding silhouette.
Note that the same rendering can be employed for multiple modalities that use the same camera.
For contour points from region modalities, we also render a silhouette image.
However, instead of using a unique ID for each body, a region ID is assigned to each body, which is rendered to the respective silhouette.
Bodies with the same region ID belong to the same region and consider similar color statistics.
As described in
[
15
]
, it is essential that, for each correspondence line, the body region on the inside and the non-body region on the outside of the contour are not interrupted for a minimum continuous distance along the line, which was defined as
3
segments.
Consequently, for each contour point, we check if rendered pixels along the normal vector fulfill this rule.
If it is violated, the contour point is rejected.
An illustration of the described validation strategies is shown in Fig.
5
.

Fig. 5:

Validation strategies for the region and depth modality.
Valid points are visualized with a circle, while invalid points are marked by a cross.
For the region modality, the body in the center and lower right corner are rendered with the same region ID.
To check if a contour point is valid, pixels on a line along the normal vector are evaluated.
If the distance to the inside is interrupted by another silhouette or the distance to the outside is interrupted by the same silhouette, the corresponding contour point is rejected. For the depth modality, all bodies are rendered with unique values.
Any surface point that is not on the body’s own silhouette is invalid.

In addition to contour point validation, the silhouette image is also used to check whether pixel values that should be assigned to color histograms are on the correct silhouette.
If they are on the wrong silhouette, they are ignored.
Both the rendering and validation are repeated for each correspondence search.

In addition to validation strategies, we allow individual region modalities to share color histograms.
This is especially beneficial if individual bodies are occluded while other bodies that model the same region are still visible.
Also, color histograms consider more information and, therefore, perform slightly better.
Another modification required for multi-body tracking considers the number of correspondence lines and points each modality deploys.
Since the size of bodies varies widely, the number has to be dynamically adapted to uniformly cover the contour and surface of all bodies.
We, therefore, scale the number of lines and points according to the surface area and contour length of a body’s current silhouette relative to a reference area and length.
In the evaluation, we use the maximum contour length and surface area of all views and bodies within an object as reference.
Also, except for the
Medical Robot
, which is shown in Fig.
6
, a maximum of
300
correspondence lines and points is deployed per body.
For the
Medical Robot
, which consists of equally sized bodies, we only use
100
lines and points to improve computational efficiency.

The last modification considers the maximization of the joint probability using the Newton method.
Similar to
ICG
, the required variation vector
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\textrm{k}}
is estimated using the linear relation in (
7
).
However, in contrast to the original implementation, we do not add regularization to each body separately.
Instead, we directly add the diagonal regularization matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
to the Hessian of the kinematic structure
\boldsymbol{H}_{\textrm{k}}
.
The diagonal elements of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
consist of the rotational and translational parameters
\lambda_{\textrm{r}}=100
and
\lambda_{\textrm{t}}=1000
.
They are applied to the respective rotational and translational components of the full variation vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}
.
Finally, the constraint Jacobian
\boldsymbol{B}_{\textrm{k}}
and the Hessian matrix
\boldsymbol{H}_{\textrm{k}}
have very different orders of magnitude.
To ensure stability when solving the linear equation of the optimization, we use a robust Cholesky decomposition with pivoting.

Since, in the evaluation, our tracker deals with large frame-to-frame pose differences, we conduct
6
iterations in which correspondences are established.
For the scale parameter of the region modality, we use
s=\{9,7,5,2\}
.
Also, the correspondence point threshold of the depth modality, which is given in millimeters, is defined as
r_{\textrm{t}}=\{100,80,50\}
, and is sampled with a stride of
8\,\mathrm{mm}
.
Note that values for the thresholds
r_{\textrm{t}}
and the stride are scaled according to the depth of model points and are defined at a reference distance of
1\,\mathrm{m}
.
In addition to those parameters, we use the standard deviations
\sigma_{\textrm{r}}=\{25,15,10\}
and
\sigma_{\textrm{d}}=\{50,30,20\}
, which are given in pixels and millimeters, respectively.
The provided sets define parameters for each iteration, with the last value employed in all remaining iterations.
Finally, with the developed framework and the described modifications, our
Multi-body ICG
algorithm can be used for the tracking of a wide variety of open and closed kinematic structures.

Metrics

For the evaluation on the

and

[

The
ADD
(
ADD
) error
e_{\textrm{ADD}}
and
ADD-S
(
ADD-S
) error
e_{\textrm{ADD-S}}
are thereby computed for each body and frame as follows

\displaystyle e_{\text{ADD}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{\textrm{v}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\textrm{v}}}\big\lVert\big({}_{\text{M}}\boldsymbol{\widetilde{X}}_{i}-{}_{\text{M}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\text{M}_{\text{GT}}}\,{}_{\text{M}}\boldsymbol{\widetilde{X}}_{i}\big)_{3\times 1}\big\rVert_{2},

(27)

\displaystyle e_{\text{ADD{-}S}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{\textrm{v}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\textrm{v}}}\min_{j\in[n_{\textrm{v}}]}\big\lVert\big({}_{\text{M}}\boldsymbol{\widetilde{X}}_{i}-{}_{\text{M}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\text{M}_{\text{GT}}}\,{}_{\text{M}}\boldsymbol{\widetilde{X}}_{j}\big)_{3\times 1}\big\rVert_{2},

(28)

where
{}_{\textrm{M}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{M}_{\textrm{GT}}}
is the transformation between the ground truth and estimated model pose,
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{X}}_{i}
is a vertex from the 3D mesh of the body written in homogeneous coordinates,
n_{\textrm{v}}
is the number of vertices, and
()_{3\times 1}
denotes the first three elements of a vector.
Note that, in the case that a rigid body is composed of multiple 3D meshes, one simply averages the conventional and shortest distance error over all meshes.
Based on the errors of individual bodies and frames
e_{ij}
, we compute the area-under-curve score for an entire kinematic structure and sequence as follows

s=\frac{1}{n_{\textrm{b}}n_{\textrm{f}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\textrm{b}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\textrm{f}}}\max\Big(1-\frac{e_{ij}}{e_{\textrm{t}}},0\Big),

(29)

with
n_{\textrm{b}}
the number of bodies,
n_{\textrm{f}}
the number of frames, and
e_{\textrm{t}}
an error threshold.
For the computation of
ADD
and
ADD-S
area-under-curve scores
s_{\textrm{ADD}}
and
s_{\textrm{ADD-S}}
, we use the error metrics
e_{\textrm{ADD}}
and
e_{\textrm{ADD-S}}
, respectively.
Error thresholds
e_{\textrm{t}}
that are used for the considered multi-body objects are given in Tab.

.
They are defined to be approximately ten percent of each object’s diameter.
The resulting score considers each body equally important, takes into account the size of the kinematic structure, and rewards accuracy while limiting the impact of bad predictions.

VI-F

Limitations

While our framework allows to model various kinematic structures and is compatible with a wide variety of algorithms, some limitations remain.
First, it focuses on approaches that employ Newton-like optimization techniques such as Gauss-Newton, Levenberg-Marquardt, or quasi-Newton methods.
The framework is, therefore, not compatible with particle-based methods or existing deep learning-based techniques.
Also, in this work, we only derived equations based on the axis-angle representation.
Note, however, that it is relatively easy to derive equations required for other parameterizations.
Another limitation comes from the Newton method itself.
Because each iteration considers the energy function only at a single point, the algorithm can get stuck in local minima.
Like for most tracking methods, this limits maximum configuration changes and pose differences between frames.
Also, algorithms that should be extended with our framework have to deal with multiple bodies at the same time.
Typical challenges thereby include bodies that are very close together or even intersect.
Like in our extension of
ICG
, it might be necessary to modify the original algorithm to successfully deal with such cases.

Finally, if our framework is applied to a specific approach, strengths and weaknesses of the original method remain mostly unchanged.
For example, the original
ICG
algorithm requires objects to be distinguishable from the background and needs reasonable depth measurements for the object’s surface.
In addition, the algorithm might struggle in situations where the object geometry is not conclusive, such as, for example, in the case of a rotationally symmetric cylinder.
Naturally, the same characteristics also apply to our extension.
Fortunately, strengths of the original algorithm are also preserved.
Like
ICG
, the developed
Multi-body ICG
tracker is therefore highly efficient, accurate, and robust, without requiring object texture.

VII
Conclusion

In this work, we developed a framework that allows to extend existing
6DoF
algorithms to multi-body object tracking.
It combines projection to a minimal parameterization and the application of pose constraints into a single formulation.
With the efficiency and realistic regularization of projection and the ability to model temporary connections and additional constraints, it combines the best of both worlds.
The resulting formulation allows to model a wide variety of kinematic structures and, to the best of our knowledge, is the first that accurately considers closed kinematic chains.
In a detailed mathematical proof, as well as in experiments, we were able to show that the developed constraints enforce an exact kinematic solution and converge in a single iteration of the Newton optimization.
In contrast to previous work, our constraints directly operate on pose differences instead of velocities.
This ensures that kinematic errors are minimized and can not accumulate over time.

Based on the developed framework, we extended the
6DoF
object tracker
ICG

[
15
]
to multi-body tracking.
For a thorough, quantitative evaluation, we created the
RTB
dataset.
It features highly-realistic sequences and multiple robots, both with open and closed kinematic chains.
The dataset also provides numerous sequences in various settings with distinct difficulty levels and depth qualities.
In a detailed comparison, we demonstrated that our
Multi-body ICG
approach significantly outperforms
DART

[
13
]
, which is a state-of-the-art articulated object tracker.
With the obtained results and new possibilities for quantitative evaluations, we are confident that both our tracker and dataset will find many applications in robotics and augmented reality.
Also, given the versatility of our framework and its compatibility with a wide variety of existing and potential future methods, we hope that more approaches will move from rigid objects to kinematic structures.

Appendix A
Derivatives of Constraint Equations

In the following, we derive the first-order derivatives of the extended constraint equation
\boldsymbol{\bar{b}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{b})
with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{b}
.
We thereby consider the rotational constraint
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{b})
and the translational constraint
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{B}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{b})
separately.
Constraint equations are introduced in Section
IV-C
.

A-A

Rotational Constraint

Starting from (
17
), the variated rotation between the coordinate frames
A
and
B
can be expressed using the exponential map as follows

\begin{split}\exp\big([{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}a},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}b})]_{\times}\big)=\,&\exp\big([-{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}a}]_{\times}\big)\\
&\exp\big([{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{b}}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}b}]_{\times}\big)\\
&\exp\big([{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}]_{\times}\big),\end{split}

(31)

The vectors
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}a}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}b}
are the rotational components of the pose variation vectors
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{b}
.
The constant rotation vector
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}
is evaluated at
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}a}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}b}=\boldsymbol{0}
.
Note that for the calculation of (
31
), the following relation was used

\boldsymbol{R}\exp([\boldsymbol{x}]_{\times})\boldsymbol{R}^{-1}=\exp([\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{x}]_{\times}).

(32)

Knowing that we only need to calculate the derivative with respect to either
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}a}
or
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}b}
while the other is zero, we reduce (
31
) to take a single variation vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and write

\exp\big([{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]_{\times}\big)=\exp\big([\boldsymbol{\theta}]_{\times}\big)\exp\big([{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}]_{\times}\big).

(33)

For the vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, one can then simply substitute one of the rotated variation vectors
\boldsymbol{\theta}=-{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}a}
or
\boldsymbol{\theta}={}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{b}}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}b}
.

While the relation in (
33
) looks relatively simple, it is not trivial to calculate the rotation vector
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
from
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}
.
Fortunately, a closed-form solution exists that dates back to Olinde Rodrigues.
For this, each rotation vector has to be split into an axis and an angle.
Using the angles
\gamma,\theta,\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and the axes
\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
\lVert\boldsymbol{n}\rVert_{2}=\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{2}=\lVert\boldsymbol{e}\rVert_{2}=1
, we define
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\gamma\boldsymbol{n}
,
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\theta\boldsymbol{v}
, and
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}=\alpha\boldsymbol{e}
.
Based on those values, one can then define the following equations

\cos\Big(\frac{\gamma}{2}\Big)=\cos\Big(\frac{\theta}{2}\Big)\cos\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)-\sin\Big(\frac{\theta}{2}\Big)\sin\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{e},

(34)

\begin{split}\sin\Big(\frac{\gamma}{2}\Big)\boldsymbol{n}&=\sin\Big(\frac{\theta}{2}\Big)\cos\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\boldsymbol{v}+\cos\Big(\frac{\theta}{2}\Big)\sin\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\boldsymbol{e}\\
&\quad\quad\quad+\sin\Big(\frac{\theta}{2}\Big)\sin\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{e}.\end{split}

(35)

For a detailed derivation and geometrical explanation of this relation, we point interested readers to
[
59
]
.
To express the required vector
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, we define two variables
x=\cos(\frac{\gamma}{2})
and
\boldsymbol{y}=\sin(\frac{\gamma}{2})\boldsymbol{n}
.
They are the right-hand sides of (
34
) and (
35
).
Based on their reformulation
\gamma=2\cos^{-1}(x)
and
\boldsymbol{n}=\sin(\frac{\gamma}{2})^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}
, one can write

{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\gamma\boldsymbol{n}=\frac{2\cos^{-1}(x)}{\sin(\cos^{-1}(x))}\,\boldsymbol{y}=\frac{2\cos^{-1}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,\boldsymbol{y}.

(36)

Finally, starting from this definition, we calculate the first-order derivative with respect to the scalar parameter
\theta
, which is the magnitude of the variation vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}

\begin{split}\frac{\partial{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}}{\partial\theta}&=\frac{\frac{-2}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}-2\cos^{-1}(x)\frac{-x}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}}{1-x^{2}}\frac{\partial x}{\partial\theta}\boldsymbol{y}\\
&\quad\quad\quad+\frac{2\cos^{-1}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{y}}{\partial{\theta}},\end{split}

(37)

Also, based on the right-hand sides of (
34
) and (
35
), we calculate

\displaystyle\mathllap{\frac{\partial x}{\partial\theta}}=-\frac{\sin(\frac{\theta}{2})\cos(\frac{\alpha}{2})}{2}-\frac{\cos(\frac{\theta}{2})\sin(\frac{\alpha}{2})}{2}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{e},

(38)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathllap{\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{y}}{\partial{\theta}}}&=\frac{\cos(\frac{\theta}{2})\cos(\frac{\alpha}{2})}{2}\boldsymbol{v}-\frac{\sin(\frac{\theta}{2})\sin(\frac{\alpha}{2})}{2}\boldsymbol{e}\\
&\quad\quad\quad+\frac{\cos(\frac{\theta}{2})\sin(\frac{\alpha}{2})}{2}\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{e}.\end{aligned}

(39)

By evaluating the derivatives at
\theta=0
, for which, according to (
34
) and (
35
),
x=\cos(\frac{\alpha}{2})
and
\boldsymbol{y}=\sin(\frac{\alpha}{2})\boldsymbol{e}
, the following equations are obtained

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}}{\partial\theta}\bigg|_{\theta=0}=\,

\displaystyle\frac{-2+\alpha\cot(\frac{\alpha}{2})}{\sin(\frac{\alpha}{2})}\boldsymbol{e}\frac{\partial x}{\partial\theta}+\frac{\alpha}{\sin(\frac{\alpha}{2})}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{y}}{\partial{\theta}}\bigg|_{\theta=0},

(40)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial x}{\partial\theta}\bigg|_{\theta=0}=\,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sin\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{e},

(41)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{y}}{\partial\theta}\bigg|_{\theta=0}=\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\cos\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\boldsymbol{v}+\frac{1}{2}\sin\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{e}.

(42)

Introducing (
41
) and (
42
) in (
40
) and extracting the pose variation’s rotation axis
\boldsymbol{v}
results in the final expression for the first-order derivative

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathllap{\frac{\partial{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}}{\partial\theta}\bigg|_{\theta=0}}&=\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\cot\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\Big)\boldsymbol{e}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{e}\\
&\quad\quad\quad+\frac{\alpha}{2}\cot\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\boldsymbol{v}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{e}\end{aligned}

(43)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &=\bigg(\frac{\alpha}{2}\cot\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\boldsymbol{I}-\frac{\alpha}{2}[\boldsymbol{e}]_{\times}\\
&\quad\quad\quad+\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\cot\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\Big)\boldsymbol{e}\boldsymbol{e}^{\top}\bigg)\boldsymbol{v}.\end{aligned}

(44)

Equation (
44
) shows that the derivative with respect to the rotation angle
\theta
consists of a linear combination of column vectors with the rotation axis
\boldsymbol{v}
.
For the rotation vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\theta\boldsymbol{v}
, the rotation axis
\boldsymbol{v}
projects the scalar angle
\theta
in a similar fashion.
Knowing that

\frac{\partial{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}}{\partial\theta}=\frac{\partial{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\partial\theta}=\frac{\partial{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,\boldsymbol{v},

(45)

we are finally able to extract the first-order derivative with respect to the rotation vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}
from (
44
) and write

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2}\cot\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\boldsymbol{I}-\frac{\alpha}{2}[\boldsymbol{e}]_{\times}

(46)

\displaystyle+\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\cot\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\Big)\boldsymbol{e}\boldsymbol{e}^{\top}.

For the originally required derivatives of the rotational constraint
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{b})
with respect to the rotational pose variation vectors
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}a}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}b}
, one simply substitutes
\boldsymbol{\theta}=-{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}a}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}={}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{b}}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}b}
.
Considering the partial derivatives
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}a}}=-{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}
and
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}b}}={}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{b}}
, we then obtain the following final results for the first-order derivatives of the rotational constraint

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a}}\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}=\boldsymbol{0}}=\,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}-\boldsymbol{C}\,{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix},

(47)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{b}}\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}=\boldsymbol{0}}=\,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{C}\,{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{b}}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix}.

(48)

The matrix
\boldsymbol{C}
, which we call variation matrix, is thereby defined as

\boldsymbol{C}=\frac{\alpha}{2}\cot\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\boldsymbol{I}-\frac{\alpha}{2}[\boldsymbol{e}]_{\times}+\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\cot\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\Big)\boldsymbol{e}\boldsymbol{e}^{\top}.

(49)

It is the partial derivative of
\frac{\partial{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{0}}
and is computed
using the angle and axis of the current rotation vector
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}=\alpha\boldsymbol{e}
.
The variation matrix
\boldsymbol{C}
describes how the rotation vector
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}
changes for the variation with a subsequent infinitesimal rotation.
Properties of the matrix are derived in Appendix
B
.

A-B

Translational Constraint

For the translational constraint, we again start from (
17
)
to formulate the variated translation between the coordinate frames
A
and
B
.
Using the knowledge that derivatives are either calculated with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{a}}
or
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{b}}
, one can write

\displaystyle{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{B}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a})

\displaystyle={}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}\big(\boldsymbol{R}(-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}a})({}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{B}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{t}a})\big)+{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{M}_{a}},

(50)

\displaystyle{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{B}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{b})

\displaystyle={}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{b}}\big(\boldsymbol{R}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}b})\,{}_{\textrm{M}_{b}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{B}}+\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{t}b}\big)+{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{M}_{b}},

(51)

where the vectors
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{t}a}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{t}b}
are the translational components of the variation vectors
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{b}
.

Using the Taylor series expansion of the exponential map and the knowledge that
[\boldsymbol{x}]_{\times}\boldsymbol{y}=-[\boldsymbol{y}]_{\times}\boldsymbol{x}
, we define the following general relation

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{R}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{t}

\displaystyle=\exp(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{t}

(52)

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{I}+[\boldsymbol{\theta}]_{\times}+\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{2}))\boldsymbol{t}

(53)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{t}-[\boldsymbol{t}]_{\times}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{2})\boldsymbol{t},

(54)

where the expression
\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{2})
models higher-order terms.
Using this relation in (
50
) and (
51
) and neglecting higher-order terms, it is straightforward to calculate the final results for the first-order derivatives of the translational constraint

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{B}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a}}\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}=\boldsymbol{0}}=\,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}\,[{}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{B}}]_{\times}&-{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}\end{bmatrix},

(55)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{B}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{b}}\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}=\boldsymbol{0}}=\,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}-{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{b}}\,[{}_{\textrm{M}_{b}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{B}}]_{\times}&{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{b}}\end{bmatrix}.

(56)

Appendix B
Properties of the Variation Matrix

The variation matrix
\boldsymbol{C}
, which was derived in Appendix
A-A
describes how the rotation vector
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}
changes with the variation of a
subsequent
infinitesimal rotation.
It is computed according to (
49
) and depends on the rotational difference between the coordinate frames
A
and
B
.
In the following, we investigate its relation to the rotation matrix
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}
and the rotation vector
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}
.
For brevity, we drop the subscripts
A
and
B
in the notation and simply write
\boldsymbol{R}
and
\boldsymbol{r}
.

Given the rotation vector
\boldsymbol{r}=\alpha\boldsymbol{e}
with the angle
\alpha
and the normalized axis
\boldsymbol{e}
, the rotation matrix can be computed using the Rodrigues formula

\boldsymbol{R}=\boldsymbol{I}+\sin(\alpha)[\boldsymbol{e}]_{\times}+2\sin\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)^{\hskip-1.0pt2}[\boldsymbol{e}]_{\times}^{2}.

(57)

With the knowledge that
[\boldsymbol{e}]_{\times}\boldsymbol{e}=\boldsymbol{0}
, we are able to calculate the following product for the rotation and variation matrix

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{C}=\,&\frac{\alpha}{2}\cot\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\boldsymbol{I}-\frac{\alpha}{2}[\boldsymbol{e}]_{\times}+\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\cot\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\Big)\boldsymbol{e}\boldsymbol{e}^{\top}\\
&\quad+\frac{\alpha}{2}\sin(\alpha)\cot\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)[\boldsymbol{e}]_{\times}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\sin(\alpha)[\boldsymbol{e}]_{\times}^{2}\\
&\quad+\alpha\sin\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)^{\hskip-1.0pt2}\cot\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)[\boldsymbol{e}]_{\times}^{2}-\alpha\sin\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)^{\hskip-1.0pt2}[\boldsymbol{e}]_{\times}^{3}.\end{split}

(58)

Using the relations

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{2}\sin(\alpha)\cot\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\alpha-\alpha\sin\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)^{\hskip-1.0pt2},

(59)

\displaystyle\alpha\sin\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)^{\hskip-1.0pt2}\cot\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2}\sin(\alpha),

(60)

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{e}]_{\times}^{3}

\displaystyle=-[\boldsymbol{e}]_{\times},

(61)

one can simplify (
58
) and write

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{C}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2}\cot\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\boldsymbol{I}+\frac{\alpha}{2}[\boldsymbol{e}]_{\times}+\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\cot\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\Big)\boldsymbol{e}\boldsymbol{e}^{\top}

(62)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{C}^{\top},

(63)

which shows that the product of the rotation and variation matrix is simply the transposed variation matrix.
Looking at (
34
) and (
35
), one notices that changing the order of the rotations
\boldsymbol{r}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
only changes the sign in the cross product term in (
35
).
Deriving the corresponding variation matrix then leads to the same expression as in (
62
), which is simply the transposed of the original variation matrix
\boldsymbol{C}
.
The transposed variation matrix
\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}
therefore describes how the rotation vector
\boldsymbol{r}
changes with the variation of a
preceding
infinitesimal rotation.
Based on this interpretation, (
63
) shows that rotating the variation matrix
\boldsymbol{C}
with the rotation matrix
\boldsymbol{R}
changes the order in which the infinitesimal rotation
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is applied to the rotation
\boldsymbol{r}
.

Similarly, transposing the relation in (
63
) and using the knowledge that for valid rotation matrices
\boldsymbol{R}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{R}^{-1}
, one is able to write

\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{C}=\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}\;\Leftrightarrow\;\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{R}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{C}\;\Leftrightarrow\;\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{R}.

(64)

This shows that
\boldsymbol{R}
and
\boldsymbol{C}
are commutative.
Hence, applying the rotation matrix
\boldsymbol{R}
on either side of the variation matrix
\boldsymbol{C}
changes the location of the infinitesimal rotation from a
subsequent
to a
preceding
rotation.
Finally, based on the relations in (
64
), we are able to express the rotation matrix with respect to the variation matrix as follows

\boldsymbol{R}=\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{C}^{-1}\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}.

(65)

The equation shows that because
\boldsymbol{C}
and
\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}
consider the variation with respect to the frames before and after the rotation, the matrix
\boldsymbol{R}
is implicitly encoded in the variation matrix
\boldsymbol{C}
.
Also, (
65
) shows that the variation matrix is normal with
\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}
.
However, at the same time, experiments show that, with
\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}\neq\boldsymbol{C}
and
\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}\neq\boldsymbol{C}^{-1}
, it is neither symmetric nor orthogonal.

Finally, we also want to analyze how the rotation vector
\boldsymbol{r}
is connected to the variation matrix
\boldsymbol{C}
.
For this, the product
\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{r}
is computed.
With the relations
[\boldsymbol{e}]_{\times}\boldsymbol{e}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{e}^{\top}\boldsymbol{e}=1
, we calculate

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{r}

\displaystyle=\alpha\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\cot\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\Big)\boldsymbol{e}+\alpha\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\cot\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\Big)\boldsymbol{e},

(66)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{r}.

(67)

Given the knowledge that the rotation vector
\boldsymbol{r}
is the eigenvector of the rotation matrix
\boldsymbol{R}
for the eigenvalue
\lambda=1
, one is able to define the relation
\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{r}
.
Together with the results from (
63
), we can therefore show that

\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{r}\;\Leftrightarrow\;\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{r}\;\Leftrightarrow\;\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{r}.

(68)

This demonstrates that the rotation vector
\boldsymbol{r}
is an eigenvector of both the original and transposed variation matrix
\boldsymbol{C}
.
Hence, it is an eigenvector of both the variation matrix for a
subsequent
and
preceding
infinitesimal rotation.

Appendix C
Constraint Convergence

Given the developed constraint equations and first-order derivatives, in the following, we analyze how fast the Newton method converges to a compliant kinematic result.
For this, two bodies with an initial rotational or translational difference of
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}
or
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{B}}
are considered.
Based on this initial error, the relative pose update for a full rotational or translational constraint is analyzed.
Experiments, which demonstrate that the obtained mathematical results also hold for more general cases, are provided in Section
VI-D
.

C-A

Rotational Constraint

Without loss of generality, we assume that constraint coordinate frames are equal to model frames with
\textrm{A}=\textrm{M}_{a}
and
\textrm{B}=\textrm{M}_{b}
.
Also, the translation of both bodies is fixed and the combined variation
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}a}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}b}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}
only considers rotation.
Based on those assumptions, the constraint equation and constraint Jacobian can be written as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{b}_{\textrm{k}}

\displaystyle={}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}},

(69)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B}_{\textrm{k}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-\boldsymbol{C}&\boldsymbol{C}\,{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}\end{bmatrix}.

(70)

Introducing both statements in (
7
) and given some arbitrary Hessian matrices and gradient vectors for body
a
and
b
, we obtain the linear equation for a single Newton step

\begin{bmatrix}-\boldsymbol{H}_{a}&\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&-\boldsymbol{H}_{b}&-{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}^{\top}\,\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}\\
\boldsymbol{C}&-\boldsymbol{C}\,{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}a}\\
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}b}\\
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{g}_{a}\\
\boldsymbol{g}_{b}\\
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}\end{bmatrix}.

(71)

Based on the first two lines of the equation, one can directly write

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}a}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{H}_{a}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{g}_{a}),

(72)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}b}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{H}_{b}^{-1}(-{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}^{\top}\,\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{g}_{b}),

(73)

where the only unknown is
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
.
Subsequently, introducing (
72
) and (
73
) into the last line of (
71
) leads to the following expression

\boldsymbol{C}(\boldsymbol{X}+\boldsymbol{Y})\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\lambda}+\boldsymbol{C}(-\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{y})={}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}},

(74)

where the matrices
\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{H}_{a}^{-1}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}={}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}\,\boldsymbol{H}_{b}^{-1}{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}^{\top}
, as well as the vectors
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{H}_{a}^{-1}\boldsymbol{g}_{a}
and
\boldsymbol{y}={}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}\,\boldsymbol{H}_{b}^{-1}\boldsymbol{g}_{b}
are used for conciseness.
Rewriting this equation leads to an expression for
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
that only depends on gradient vectors, Hessian matrices, and the current rotational difference

\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\boldsymbol{C}^{-\top}(\boldsymbol{X}+\boldsymbol{Y})^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{C}^{-1}{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}).

(75)

Finally, using the proof from Appendix
B
, which shows that
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}
is an eigenvector of the variation matrix
\boldsymbol{C}
and that
\boldsymbol{C}^{-1}{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}={}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}
, we can further simplify and write

\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\boldsymbol{C}^{-\top}(\boldsymbol{X}+\boldsymbol{Y})^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}+{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}).

(76)

To assess the convergence of our method, we compute how the rotational difference changes for a single Newton step.
Using the axis-angle representation, the change in rotation is thereby calculated as

\Delta\boldsymbol{R}=\exp\big([-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}a}]_{\times}\big)\exp\big([{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}b}]_{\times}\big).

(77)

By introducing (
72
) and (
73
), one can then write

\Delta\boldsymbol{R}=\exp\big([-\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\lambda}+\boldsymbol{x}]_{\times}\big)\exp\big([-\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{y}]_{\times}\big).

(78)

Extending this equation with
\boldsymbol{Y}-\boldsymbol{Y}
and
\boldsymbol{X}-\boldsymbol{X}
leads to

\begin{split}\Delta\boldsymbol{R}&=\exp\Big(\mbox{\large$\frac{1}{2}$}\big[(\boldsymbol{Y}-\boldsymbol{X}-\boldsymbol{X}-\boldsymbol{Y})\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\lambda}+2\boldsymbol{x}\big]_{\times}\Big)\\
&\quad\quad\exp\Big(\mbox{\large$\frac{1}{2}$}\big[(\boldsymbol{X}-\boldsymbol{Y}-\boldsymbol{X}-\boldsymbol{Y})\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\lambda}-2\boldsymbol{y}\big]_{\times}\Big).\end{split}

(79)

Subsequently, we introduce
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
from (
76
) and multiply it with the expression
(-\boldsymbol{X}-\boldsymbol{Y})\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}
in (
79
) to obtain

\begin{split}\Delta\boldsymbol{R}&=\exp\Big(\mbox{\large$\frac{1}{2}$}\big[(\boldsymbol{Y}-\boldsymbol{X})\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\lambda}+\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{y}-{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}\big]_{\times}\Big)\\
&\quad\quad\exp\Big(\mbox{\large$\frac{1}{2}$}\big[(\boldsymbol{X}-\boldsymbol{Y})\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}-{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}\big]_{\times}\Big),\end{split}

(80)

which has the following highly symmetric structure

\Delta\boldsymbol{R}=\exp\Big(\mbox{\large$\frac{1}{2}$}[\boldsymbol{z}-{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}]_{\times}\Big)\exp\Big(\mbox{\large$\frac{1}{2}$}[-\boldsymbol{z}-{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}]_{\times}\Big).

(81)

Finally, observing that the two exponents are commutative and knowing that commutative exponents can be summed, we get the following result for the rotational difference

\Delta\boldsymbol{R}=\exp\big([-{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\textrm{B}}]_{\times}\big)={}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}^{-1}

(82)

This shows that a single iteration of the Newton method is enough to minimize an initial rotational error of
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}
to zero and obtain an exact kinematic solution.

C-B

Translational Constraint

Without loss of generality, we again assume that constraint coordinate frames are equal to model frames with
\textrm{A}=\textrm{M}_{a}
and
\textrm{B}=\textrm{M}_{b}
.
Furthermore, the rotation of both bodies is fixed and the combined variation vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{t}a}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{t}b}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}
only considers translation.
The constraint equation and constraint Jacobian can then be written as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{b}_{\textrm{k}}

\displaystyle={}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{B}},

(83)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B}_{\textrm{k}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-\boldsymbol{I}&{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}\end{bmatrix}.

(84)

Introducing both statements in (
7
), we obtain the following linear equation for a single Newton step

\begin{bmatrix}-\boldsymbol{H}_{a}&\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{I}^{\top}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&-\boldsymbol{H}_{b}&-{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}^{\top}\\
\boldsymbol{I}&-{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{t}a}\\
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{t}b}\\
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{g}_{a}\\
\boldsymbol{g}_{b}\\
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{B}}\end{bmatrix}.

(85)

Based on the first two rows of this relation, variation vectors are computed as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{t}a}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{H}_{a}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{g}_{a}),

(86)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{t}b}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{H}_{b}^{-1}(-{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}^{\top}\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{g}_{b}).

(87)

For conciseness, we again define the matrices
\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{H}_{a}^{-1}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}={}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}\,\boldsymbol{H}_{b}^{-1}{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}^{\top}
, as well as the vectors
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{H}_{a}^{-1}\boldsymbol{g}_{a}
and
\boldsymbol{y}={}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}\,\boldsymbol{H}_{b}^{-1}\boldsymbol{g}_{b}
.
We then introduce (
86
) and (
87
) into the last row of (
85
) to compute the following relation for
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
that includes only known variables

\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\boldsymbol{X}+\boldsymbol{Y})^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}+{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{B}}).

(88)

To assess the convergence, we consider the change of the translational difference between frame
A
and
B
and write

\Delta\boldsymbol{t}=-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{t}a}+{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{t}b}.

(89)

Introducing the variation vectors from (
86
) and (
87
) then leads to the relation

\Delta\boldsymbol{t}=-(\boldsymbol{X}+\boldsymbol{Y})\boldsymbol{\lambda}+\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}.

(90)

Finally, with the definition of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
from (
88
), we are able to write the following result

\Delta\boldsymbol{t}=-{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{B}}.

(91)

Similar to the rotational case, this demonstrates that a single iteration of the Newton method is enough to overcome the translational error
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{B}}
and obtain an exact kinematic solution.

Appendix D
Adjoint Equivalence

In the following, we demonstrate that if the constraint coordinate frames
A
and
B
are equal, the first-order derivatives of the constraint equation in (
20
) and (
21
) reduce to an adjoint representation.
Given that in such a case the variation matrix
\boldsymbol{C}
is equal to the identity matrix, only the partial derivatives of the translational difference
{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{B}}
with respect to the rotational variation vectors
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}a}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}b}
differ from the adjoint representation defined in (
10
).
However, we can show that

\displaystyle{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}\,[{}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{B}}]_{\times}

\displaystyle={}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}\,[{}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{A}}]_{\times}\,{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}^{-1}\,{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}

(92)

\displaystyle=[{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}\,{}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{A}}]_{\times}\,{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}

(93)

\displaystyle=-[{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}]_{\times}\,{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{a}},

(94)

and similarly that

-{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{b}}\,[{}_{\textrm{M}_{b}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{B}}]_{\times}=[{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\textrm{M}_{b}}]_{\times}\,{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{b}}.

(95)

This demonstrates that if
A
and
B
are equal, the derivatives in (
20
) and (
21
) turn into
-\Ad({}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{M}_{a}})
and
\Ad({}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textrm{M}_{b}})
.

Appendix E
Derivatives of Orthogonality Constraints

In the following, we calculate the first-order derivatives of the rotational orthogonality constraint equation.
They are required for the calculation of the constraint Jacobian in (
19
).
Starting from (
30
), the constraint equation is defined as

\begin{split}b_{\textrm{r}ijab}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{b})={}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{\top}&\exp\big([-{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{a}}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}a}]_{\times}\big)\\
&\exp\big([{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{b}}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}b}]_{\times}\big)\\
&{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}\,{}_{\textrm{B}}\boldsymbol{e}_{j},\end{split}

(96)

with the tuple
(i,j)\in\{(\textrm{x},\textrm{y}),(\textrm{y},\textrm{z}),(\textrm{z},\textrm{x})\}
.
Similar to (
53
) and (
54
), we first linearize using the Taylor series expansion

\exp([\boldsymbol{\theta}]_{\times})\approx\boldsymbol{I}+[\boldsymbol{\theta}]_{\times}
and then use
[\boldsymbol{x}]_{\times}\boldsymbol{y}=-[\boldsymbol{y}]_{\times}\boldsymbol{x}
.
With this, the following first-order derivatives can be calculated

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{}b_{\textrm{r}ijab}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}a}}\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}=\boldsymbol{0}}

\displaystyle={}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{\top}[{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}\,{}_{\textrm{B}}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}]_{\times}\,{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{a}},

(97)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{}b_{\textrm{r}ijab}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{r}b}}\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{k}}=\boldsymbol{0}}

\displaystyle={}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{\top}[{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{B}}\,{}_{\textrm{B}}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}]_{\times}\,{}_{\textrm{A}}\boldsymbol{R}_{\textrm{M}_{b}}.

(98)

Knowing that first-order derivatives with respect to the translational variations
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{t}a}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{t}b}
are zero, we are able to compute the constraint Jacobians
\boldsymbol{B}_{ab}

He also performed research at the University of Tokyo and NVIDIA, received the Best Paper Award at ECCV 2018, is a core developer of BlenderProc and organizer of 6D pose estimation workshops and challenges. His research interests include computer vision methods for spatial robotic tasks with known and unknown objects, self-supervised learning from demonstrations and sim2real transfer.

Wout Boerdijk
is a PhD student at the Technical University of Munich and a research scientist at the German Aerospace Center, where he is part of the Perception and Cognition department in the Institute of Robotics and Mechatronics. His research interests include computer vision methods for learning of and interacting with objects.

Rudolph
Triebel
leads the department of Perception and Cognition at the
DLR Institute of Robotics and Mechatronics. He received his PhD in
computer science in 2007 from the University of Freiburg, Germany
and the habilitation in 2015 from the Technical University of Munich. Before working at
DLR, he was a postdoctoral researcher at
ETH Zurich and at the University of Oxford, UK. From 2013 to 2021,
he was also appointed as a lecturer in computer science at TU
Munich. Since the beginning of 2022, he is appointed as a guest
professor in the TUM School of Engineering and Design.
