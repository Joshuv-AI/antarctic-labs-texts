Abstract
. We classify nontrivial, nonnegative, positively homogeneous solutions of the equation Δ ​ u = γ ​ u γ − 1 \Delta u=\gamma u^{\gamma-1} in the plane. The problem is motivated by the analysis of the classical Alt-Phillips free boundary problem, but considered here with negative exponents γ \gamma . The proof relies on several bespoke results for ordinary differential equations.

The classification of homogeneous solutions

\displaystyle\frac{a^{a-1}\,r^{2-a}\,y^{2-a}}{2^{\frac{a}{2}}}\big(\Delta u-\gamma u^{\gamma-1}\big)=\big(ay^{2}+(a-1)(y^{\prime})^{2}+yy^{\prime\prime}\big)-(a-1)=0,

showing that (
3.3
) holds true.
∎

4.
Two special functions
\psi(y)
and
\Psi(y)

In this section, we study a special function built from an improper integral.

Let
m\in\mathbb{R}
and
a\in(0,1)\cup(1,+\infty)
.
If
a>1
assume additionally that

(4.1)

m\geqslant 0.

For all
y>0
we let

(4.2)

\psi(y):=1+m\,y^{2(1-a)}-y^{2}.

We observe that, by (
4.1
),

\lim_{y\searrow 0}\psi(y)=\begin{cases}1&{\mbox{ if }}a\in(0,1),\\
1&{\mbox{ if $a>1$ and\penalty\ $m=0$,}}\\
+\infty&{\mbox{ if $a>1$ and\penalty\ $m>0$.}}\end{cases}

As a result,

\lim_{y\searrow 0}\psi(y)\geqslant 1>-\infty=\lim_{y\to+\infty}\psi(y)

and therefore there exists a unique
y_{*}>0
such that

(4.3)

\psi(y)>0
for all
y\in(0,y_{*})
and
\psi(y_{*})=0
.

Lemma 4.1
.

We have that

(4.4)

\psi^{\prime}(y_{*})<0.

Proof.

By (
4.3
), for all small
\varepsilon>0
,

0\leqslant\frac{\psi(y_{*}-\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}=\frac{\psi(y_{*})-\psi^{\prime}(y_{*})\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2})}{\varepsilon}=-\psi^{\prime}(y_{*})+O(\varepsilon),

hence, sending
\varepsilon\searrow 0
, we find that
\psi^{\prime}(y_{*})\leqslant 0
.

Consequently, to establish (
4.4
), it suffices to
check that

(4.5)

\psi^{\prime}(y_{*})\neq 0.

To this end, suppose, by contradiction, that
\psi^{\prime}(y_{*})=0
. Then,

0=\psi^{\prime}(y_{*})=2(1-a)m\,y_{*}^{1-2a}-2y_{*},

whence
y^{2a}_{*}=(1-a)m
.

This gives that

(4.6)

(1-a)m>0

and

y_{*}=\big((1-a)m\big)^{\frac{1}{2a}}.

Accordingly, if
\psi^{\prime}(y_{*})=0
, we deduce that

(4.7)

\begin{split}&0=\psi(y_{*})=\psi\left(\big((1-a)m\big)^{\frac{1}{2a}}\right)=1+m\,\big((1-a)m\big)^{\frac{1-a}{a}}-\big((1-a)m\big)^{\frac{1}{a}}\\
&\qquad=1+\big((1-a)m\big)^{\frac{1}{a}}\left(\frac{1}{1-a}-1\right)=1+\big((1-a)m\big)^{\frac{1}{a}}\frac{a}{1-a}.\end{split}

Necessarily, this gives that
\frac{a}{1-a}<0
and so
a>1
.
Combined with (
4.6
), this establishes that
m<0
, but this is
against our assumption in (
4.1
).
The proof of (
4.5
) is thereby complete.
∎

Now, for all
y\in[0,y_{*})
we define

(4.8)

\Psi(y):=\int_{0}^{y}\frac{dY}{\sqrt{\psi(Y)}}=\int_{0}^{y}\frac{dY}{\sqrt{1+m\,Y^{2(1-a)}-Y^{2}}}.

Note that
\Psi(0)=0
. Moreover,

(4.9)

\Psi
is a strictly increasing function of
y\in[0,y_{*})

and we may define

(4.10)

t_{*}:=\lim_{y\nearrow y_{*}}\Psi(y)\in(0,+\infty].

Now we show that
t_{*}
is always finite, according to the next observation:

Lemma 4.2
.

We have that
t_{*}<+\infty
.

Proof.

We let
\varepsilon>0
, to be taken suitably small here below.
Then,

(4.11)

\psi(y_{*}-\varepsilon)=-\psi^{\prime}(y_{*})\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2})=|\psi^{\prime}(y_{*})|\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2})

and therefore, for all
\delta>\eta>0
suitably small,

\int_{y_{*}-\delta}^{y_{*}-\eta}\frac{dY}{\sqrt{\psi(Y)}}=\int^{\delta}_{\eta}\frac{d\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\psi(y_{*}-\varepsilon)}}=\int^{\delta}_{\eta}\frac{d\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\sqrt{|\psi^{\prime}(y_{*})|+O(\varepsilon)}}.

As a result, for small
\delta
, using (
4.4
) we find that

\int_{y_{*}-\delta}^{y_{*}-\eta}\frac{dY}{\sqrt{\psi(Y)}}\leqslant\int^{\delta}_{\eta}\frac{d\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\sqrt{|\psi^{\prime}(y_{*})|/2}}\leqslant 2\sqrt{\frac{2\delta}{|\psi^{\prime}(y_{*})|}}.

Therefore, for all
y\in[y_{*}-\delta,y_{*})
,

\Psi(y)=\Psi(y_{*}-\delta)+\int_{y^{*}-\delta}^{y}\frac{dY}{\sqrt{\psi(Y)}}\leqslant\Psi(y_{*}-\delta)+2\sqrt{\frac{2\delta}{|\psi^{\prime}(y_{*})|}},

and therefore we can send
y\nearrow y_{*}
and obtain that
t_{*}\leqslant\Psi(y_{*}-\delta)+2\sqrt{\frac{2\delta}{|\psi^{\prime}(y_{*})|}}<+\infty
.
∎

5.
The inverse function
\Upsilon
of
\Psi

Now we aim at inverting the special function constructed in the previous section.
This method of implicitly inverting an integral equation is somewhat inspired to that used in the study of
cnoidal wave solutions to the Korteweg-de Vries equation, see e.g.
[
6
]
.

The special function that we obtain reconstructs all the solutions of the ODE
presented in Section
3
and therefore, in light of Lemma
3.1
,
all the suitable powers of the angular components of the solutions of the PDE in (
1.4
).

Let us now present the analytical details of this construction.
In view of Lemma
4.2
, we can extend continuously
\Psi
at the point
y_{*}
by setting

(5.1)

\Psi(y_{*}):=t_{*}\in(0,+\infty).

Thus, by (
4.9
), we can define
the inverse function of
\Psi:[0,y_{*}]\to[0,t_{*}]
, that is we denote by
\Upsilon:[0,t_{*}]\to[0,y_{*}]
the unique function such that
\Psi(\Upsilon(t))=t
(that is,
\Upsilon(t)
is the unique solution
y
of the equation
t=\Psi(y)

and note that
\Upsilon>0
in
(0,t_{*}]
).
We then evenly extend
\Upsilon
across
t=t_{*}
by setting, for all
t\in(t_{*},2t_{*}]
,

(5.2)

\Upsilon(t):=\Upsilon(2t_{*}-t).

In this way,
\Upsilon\in C([0,2t_{*}])
.

See Figures
8
and
9
for some numerical plots of
\Upsilon
for
t\in[0,t_{*}]
.
The basic properties of this function are listed below:

Proposition 5.1
.

We have that
\Upsilon\in C^{2}((0,2t_{*}))
. Moreover,
\Upsilon(0)=0=\Upsilon(2t_{*})
and, for all
t\in(0,2t_{*})
, we have that

(5.3)

\Upsilon^{2}(t)+\Upsilon(t)\,\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(t)+(a-1)\big(\Upsilon^{2}(t)+(\Upsilon^{\prime}(t))^{2}-1\big)=0.

Proof.

Since
\Psi(\Upsilon(0))=0=\Psi(0)
, we obtain that
\Upsilon(0)=0
.
As a result,
\Upsilon(2t_{*})=\Upsilon(2t_{*}-2t_{*})=\Upsilon(0)=0
.

We also observe that
\Upsilon\in C^{2}((0,t_{*}))
and, for all
t\in(0,t_{*})
,

\displaystyle 1=\frac{d}{dt}(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\big(\Psi(\Upsilon(t))\big)=\Psi^{\prime}(\Upsilon(t))\,\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)=\frac{\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)}{\sqrt{1+m\,\Upsilon^{2(1-a)}(t)-\Upsilon^{2}(t)}},

that is

(5.4)

\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)=\sqrt{1+m\,\Upsilon^{2(1-a)}(t)-\Upsilon^{2}(t)},

and thus

(5.5)

\begin{split}&\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\sqrt{1+m\,\Upsilon^{2(1-a)}(t)-\Upsilon^{2}(t)}\right)=\frac{\Big(m(1-a)\,\Upsilon^{1-2a}(t)-\Upsilon(t)\Big)\,\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)}{\sqrt{1+m\,\Upsilon^{2(1-a)}(t)-\Upsilon^{2}(t)}}\\
&\qquad\qquad=m(1-a)\,\Upsilon^{1-2a}(t)-\Upsilon(t).\end{split}

By even symmetry, this also gives that
\Upsilon\in C^{2}((0,t_{*})\cup(t_{*},2t_{*}))
.

Additionally, again by even symmetry,

\Upsilon^{\prime}(2t_{*}-t)=-\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)\qquad{\mbox{and}}\qquad\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(2t_{*}-t)=\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(t).

We also observe that
\Psi(\Upsilon(t_{*}))=t_{*}=\Psi(y_{*})
, whence
\Upsilon(t_{*})=y_{*}
and therefore

1+m\,\Upsilon^{2(1-a)}(t_{*})-\Upsilon^{2}(t_{*})=1+m\,y_{*}^{2(1-a)}-y_{*}^{2}=\psi(y_{*})=0.

From the observations above, we infer that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\nearrow t_{*}}\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)-\lim_{t\searrow t_{*}}\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)=\lim_{t\nearrow t_{*}}\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)+\lim_{t\searrow t_{*}}\Upsilon^{\prime}(2t_{*}-t)=2\lim_{t\nearrow t_{*}}\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle\qquad=2\sqrt{1+m\,\Upsilon^{2(1-a)}(t_{*})-\Upsilon^{2}(t_{*})}=0

and therefore
\Upsilon\in C^{1}((0,2t_{*}))
.

Also,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\nearrow t_{*}}\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(t)-\lim_{t\searrow t_{*}}\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(t)=\lim_{t\nearrow t_{*}}\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(t)-\lim_{t\searrow t_{*}}\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(2t_{*}-t)=\lim_{t\nearrow t_{*}}\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(t)-\lim_{t\nearrow t_{*}}\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(t)=0

and therefore
\Upsilon\in C^{2}((0,2t_{*}))
, as desired.

It remains to check (
5.3
). For this, we observe that, if
t\in(t_{*},2t_{*})
,

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{2}(2t_{*}-t)+\Upsilon(2t_{*}-t)\,\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(2t_{*}-t)+(a-1)\big(\Upsilon^{2}(2t_{*}-t)+(\Upsilon^{\prime}(2t_{*}-t))^{2}-1\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{2}(t)+\Upsilon(t)\,\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(t)+(a-1)\big(\Upsilon^{2}(t)+(-\Upsilon^{\prime}(t))^{2}-1\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{2}(t)+\Upsilon(t)\,\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(t)+(a-1)\big(\Upsilon^{2}(t)+(\Upsilon^{\prime}(t))^{2}-1\big),

hence it suffices to check (
5.3
) for
t\in(0,t_{*}]
(or, actually, for
t\in(0,t_{*})

since the values at
t_{*}
can be reached by continuity).

To this end, in
(0,t_{*})
, we recall (
5.4
) and (
5.5
) and we compute that

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{2}+\Upsilon\,\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}+(a-1)\big(\Upsilon^{2}+(\Upsilon^{\prime})^{2}-1\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=a\Upsilon^{2}+\Upsilon\,\big(m(1-a)\,\Upsilon^{1-2a}-\Upsilon\big)+(a-1)\big(1+m\,\Upsilon^{2(1-a)}-\Upsilon^{2}-1\big)=0,

completing the proof of the desired result.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

We have that

(5.6)

y_{*}(0)=1

and

(5.7)

t_{*}(0)=\frac{\pi}{2}.

Proof.

By (
4.2
) and (
4.3
),

0=\psi(y_{*}(0),0)=1-(y_{*}(0))^{2},

leading to (
5.6
).

Moreover, by (
4.8
), (
4.10
) and (
5.6
),

\displaystyle t_{*}(0)=\lim_{y\nearrow y_{*}(0)}\Psi(y,0)=\lim_{y\nearrow 1}\int_{0}^{y}\frac{dY}{\sqrt{1-Y^{2}}}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dY}{\sqrt{1-Y^{2}}}=\frac{\pi}{2},

).
∎

Corollary 5.3
.

There exists
{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
such that
{\mathcal{M}}\neq\varnothing

for which if

the following holds true.

There exist
t_{*}\in(0,\pi]
and a function
\Upsilon\in C^{2}((0,2t_{*}))\cap C([0,2t_{*}])

such that
\Upsilon(0)=0=\Upsilon(2t_{*})
and, for all

, we have that
\Upsilon(t)>0
and

(5.8)

\Upsilon^{2}(t)+\Upsilon(t)\,\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(t)+(a-1)\big(\Upsilon^{2}(t)+(\Upsilon^{\prime}(t))^{2}-1\big)=0.

Also, for all

, we have that
\Upsilon(2t_{*}-t)=\Upsilon(t)
and

t=\int_{0}^{\Upsilon(t)}\frac{dY}{\sqrt{1+m\,Y^{2(1-a)}-Y^{2}}}.

Proof.

The existence and basic properties of
\Upsilon

.
The additional ingredient here is that we can find
{\mathcal{M}}\neq\varnothing

such that when

it holds that
t_{*}(m)\in(0,\pi]
,
which is warranted by Lemma

.
∎

6.
Behavior of
\Upsilon
near boundary point

Now we address the boundary regularity properties of the function
\Upsilon
introduced in Section
5
:

Proposition 6.1
.

If
m=0
, then
\Upsilon(t)=\sin t
.

If instead
m\neq 0
, the following claims hold true.

•

If
a=\frac{1}{2}
then

(6.1)

\Upsilon(t)=\sin t+\frac{m}{2}(1-\cos t).

•

If
a\in(0,1)
then
\Upsilon\in C^{1}([0,2t_{*}])
,

(6.2)

\Upsilon^{\prime}(0)=1=-\Upsilon^{\prime}(2t_{*})

and

(6.3)

\lim_{t\searrow 0}\frac{\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)-\Upsilon^{\prime}(0)}{t^{2(1-a)}}=\frac{m}{2}.

•

More precisely, if
a\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
then

(6.4)

\begin{split}&\Upsilon\in C^{2,1-2a}([0,2t_{*}])\qquad{\mbox{with }}\quad\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(0)=0\quad{\mbox{and }}\quad\lim_{t\searrow 0}\frac{\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(t)-\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(0)}{t^{1-2a}}=(1-a)m,\\
&{\mbox{ but
$\Upsilon\not\in C^{2,\xi}([0,2t_{*}])$ if\penalty\ $\xi>1-2a$,}}\end{split}

while if
a\in\left(\frac{1}{2},1\right)
then

(6.5)

\Upsilon\in C^{1,2(1-a)}([0,2t_{*}])
, but

\Upsilon\not\in C^{1,\xi}([0,2t_{*}])
if
\xi>2(1-a)
.

•

If
a>1
then

(6.6)

\begin{split}&\lim_{t\searrow 0}\frac{\Upsilon(t)-\Upsilon(0)}{t^{\frac{1}{a}}}=a^{\frac{1}{a}}{m}^{\frac{1}{2a}},\\
&{\mbox{$\Upsilon\in C^{\frac{1}{a}}([0,2t_{*}])$,
but $\Upsilon\not\in C^{\xi}([0,2t_{*}])$ when\penalty\ $\xi>\frac{1}{a}$.}}\end{split}

Proof.

We focus on the regularity theory at
t=0
, since the one at
t=2t_{*}
can be
inferred from that by symmetry, owing to (
5.2
).

To this end, when
m=0
we have that
t_{*}=\frac{\pi}{2}
, thanks to (
5.7
),
whence, for all
t\in\left(0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right)
,

t=\Psi(\Upsilon(t))=\int_{0}^{\Upsilon(t)}\frac{dY}{\sqrt{1-Y^{2}}}=\arcsin\Upsilon(t),

therefore
\Upsilon(t)=\sin t
, and this holds for all
t\in(0,\pi)
, using the parity of
\Psi

across
t=t_{*}=\frac{\pi}{2}
, as claimed.

Hence, we now suppose that
m\neq 0
. When
a=\frac{1}{2}
, we have that

0=\psi(y_{*})=1+my_{*}-y_{*}^{2}

and therefore

y_{*}=\frac{m+\sqrt{m^{2}+4}}{2}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle t_{*}=\Psi(y_{*})=\int_{0}^{y_{*}}\frac{dY}{\sqrt{1+mY-Y^{2}}}=\arctan\frac{m}{2}-\arctan\frac{m-2y_{*}}{2\sqrt{1+my_{*}-y_{*}^{2}}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\arctan\frac{m}{2}+\arctan\frac{\sqrt{m^{2}+4}}{0^{+}}=\arctan\frac{m}{2}+\arctan(+\infty)=\arctan\frac{m}{2}+\frac{\pi}{2}\in(0,\pi).

Furthermore, for all
t\in(0,t_{*})
,

t=\Psi(\Upsilon(t))=\int_{0}^{\Upsilon(t)}\frac{dY}{\sqrt{1+mY-Y^{2}}}=\arctan\frac{m}{2}-\arctan\frac{m-2\Upsilon(t)}{2\sqrt{1+m\Upsilon(t)-\Upsilon^{2}(t)}}

and therefore

\frac{m-2\Upsilon(t)}{2\sqrt{1+m\Upsilon(t)-\Upsilon^{2}(t)}}=\tan T,

where

T:=\arctan\frac{m}{2}-t\in\left(\arctan\frac{m}{2}-t_{*},\arctan\frac{m}{2}\right)=\left(-\frac{\pi}{2},\arctan\frac{m}{2}\right)\subseteq\left(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right).

This gives that

\frac{m^{2}-4m\Upsilon(t)+4\Upsilon^{2}(t)}{4\tan^{2}T}=1+m\Upsilon(t)-\Upsilon^{2}(t),

and, as a result,

(6.7)

\begin{split}0\,&=\,\Upsilon^{2}(t)-m\Upsilon(t)+\frac{m^{2}}{4}\cos^{2}T-\sin^{2}T\\
&=\,\left(\Upsilon(t)-\frac{m}{2}\right)^{2}-\frac{m^{2}+4}{4}\sin^{2}T\\
&=\,\left(\Upsilon(t)-\frac{m}{2}\right)^{2}-\frac{(m\cos t-2\sin t)^{2}}{4}\\
&=\,\left(\Upsilon(t)-\frac{m}{2}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{m}{2}\cos t-\sin t\right)^{2}\\
&=\,\left(\Upsilon(t)-\frac{m}{2}-\frac{m}{2}\cos t+\sin t\right)\left(\Upsilon(t)-\frac{m}{2}+\frac{m}{2}\cos t-\sin t\right)\\
&=\,\left(\Upsilon(t)-\frac{m}{2}(1+\cos t)+\sin t\right)\left(\Upsilon(t)-\frac{m}{2}(1-\cos t)-\sin t\right).\end{split}

This yields that
\Upsilon
has the form stated in (
6.1
).
To check this, let us argue by contradiction and suppose that
\Upsilon
does
not agree with the form in (
6.1
) in some interval
(t_{1},t_{2})
,
with
0\leqslant t_{1}<t_{2}\leqslant t_{*}
, and let us suppose that such interval is as large as possible.
Then, in view of (
6.7
), we know that, for every
t\in(t_{1},t_{2})
,

\Upsilon(t)=\frac{m}{2}(1+\cos t)-\sin t

and in particular
\Upsilon(t_{1})=\frac{m}{2}(1+\cos t_{1})-\sin t_{1}
.

Hence, necessarily
t_{1}>0
, otherwise we would have that

0=\Upsilon(0)=\frac{m}{2}(1+\cos 0)-\sin 0=m,

against our assumptions.

This observation and (
6.7
) give that

\Upsilon(t)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{m}{2}(1-\cos t)+\sin t&{\mbox{ if }}t\in(0,t_{1}),\\
\\
\displaystyle\frac{m}{2}(1+\cos t)-\sin t&{\mbox{ if }}t\in(t_{1},t_{2}).\end{cases}

Since, by (
5.8
), we know that
\Upsilon
is
C^{1}
in a neighborhood of
t_{1}
, we thereby find that

\displaystyle\frac{m}{2}\sin t_{1}+\cos t_{1}=\lim_{t\nearrow t_{1}}\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)=\lim_{t\searrow t_{1}}\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{m}{2}\sin t_{1}-\cos t_{1},

leading to

0=\frac{m}{2}\sin t_{1}+\cos t_{1}=\frac{m}{2}\cos\left(t_{1}-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)-\sin\left(t_{1}-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)

and thus
t_{1}=\frac{\pi}{2}+\arctan\frac{m}{2}=t_{*}
, once again in contradiction with our assumptions.
This gives (
6.1
), as desired.

Now we point out that, for
t\in(0,t_{*})
,

1=\frac{d}{dt}t=\frac{d}{dt}\Big(\Psi(\Upsilon(t))\Big)=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\int_{0}^{\Upsilon(t)}\frac{dY}{\sqrt{1+m\,Y^{2(1-a)}-Y^{2}}}\right)=\frac{\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)}{\sqrt{1+m\,\Upsilon^{2(1-a)}(t)-\Upsilon^{2}(t)}},

whence

(6.8)

\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)=\sqrt{1+m\,\Upsilon^{2(1-a)}(t)-\Upsilon^{2}(t)}.

Therefore, if
a\in(0,1)
, it follows that
\Upsilon^{\prime}
is continuous at
t=0

and
\Upsilon^{\prime}(0)=1
, giving (
6.2
).

As a result,
\Upsilon(t)=t+o(t)
as
t\searrow 0
, whence, by (
6.8
),

(6.9)

\begin{split}\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)\,&=\,\sqrt{1+m\,\big(t+o(t)\big)^{2(1-a)}-\big(t+o(t)\big)^{2}}\\
&=\,1+\frac{m\,t^{2(1-a)}\big(1+o(1)\big)^{2(1-a)}-t^{2}\big(1+o(1)\big)^{2}}{2},\end{split}

leading to (
6.3
).

We also deduce from (
6.9
) that,
as
t\searrow 0
,

\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)=1+\frac{m\,t^{2(1-a)}}{2}+o(t^{2(1-a)})

and thus

\big(\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)\big)^{2}=1+m\,t^{2(1-a)}+o(t^{2(1-a)}).

As a consequence, by (
5.3
),

(6.10)

\begin{split}0\,&=\frac{\Upsilon^{2}(t)+\Upsilon(t)\,\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(t)+(a-1)\big(\Upsilon^{2}(t)+(\Upsilon^{\prime}(t))^{2}-1\big)}{t}\\
&=\big(1+o(1)\big)\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(t)+(a-1)\big(m\,t^{1-2a}+o(t^{1-2a})\big)+O(t)\\
&=\big(1+o(1)\big)\,\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(t)+(a-1)m\,t^{1-2a}+o(t^{1-2a}).\end{split}

Let us now assume that
a\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
. Then, the asymptotics in (
6.10
)
shows that
\Upsilon\in C^{2}([0,2t_{*}])
, with
\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(0)=0
and

(6.11)

\lim_{t\searrow 0}\frac{\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(t)-\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(0)}{t^{1-2a}}=(1-a)m.

Furthermore, employing (
5.3
) for taking one more derivative, for small
t>0
we have that

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\big(\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(t)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{d}{dt}\left(a\Upsilon(t)+\frac{(a-1)\big((\Upsilon^{\prime}(t))^{2}-1\big)}{\Upsilon(t)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-a\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)+\frac{2(1-a)\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(t)}{\Upsilon(t)}+\frac{(a-1)\big((\Upsilon^{\prime}(t))^{2}-1\big)\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)}{\Upsilon^{2}(t)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-a\big(1+o(1)\big)+\frac{2(1-a)\big(1+o(1)\big)\big((1-a)m\,t^{1-2a}+o(t^{1-2a})\big)}{t+o(t)}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{(a-1)\big(m\,t^{2(1-a)}+o(t^{2(1-a)})\big)\big(1+o(t)\big)}{t^{2}+o(t^{2})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-a+(a-1)(2a-1)mt^{-2a}+o(t^{-2a}).

For this reason,
if
\hat{t}>0
is sufficiently small and
0<t_{1}<t_{2}<\hat{t}
, we infer that

\displaystyle|\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(t_{2})-\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(t_{1})|\leqslant\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}|\Upsilon^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)|\,dt\leqslant a\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}dt+2(1-a)(1-2a)m\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}t^{-2a}\,dt

\displaystyle\qquad=a(t_{2}-t_{1})+\frac{2(1-a)(1-2a)m}{1-2a}\big(t_{2}^{1-2a}-t_{1}^{1-2a}\big)\leqslant C(t_{2}-t_{1})^{1-2a},

for some
C>0
depending only on
a
and
m
, which shows that
\Upsilon\in C^{2,1-2a}([0,2t_{*}])
.

Additionally, if
\xi>1-2a

\lim_{t\searrow 0}\frac{\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(t)-\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(0)}{t^{\xi}}=(1-a)m\lim_{t\searrow 0}t^{1-2a-\xi}=+\infty,

due to (
6.11
),
hence
\Upsilon\not\in C^{2,\xi}([0,2t_{*}])
.
The proof of (
6.4
) is thereby complete.

Let us now deal with the case
a\in\left(\frac{1}{2},1\right)
. In this situation, we deduce from (
6.10
) that

\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(t)=(1-a)m\,t^{1-2a}+o(t^{1-2a})

and consequently,
if
\hat{t}>0
is sufficiently small and
0<t_{1}<t_{2}<\hat{t}
,

\displaystyle|\Upsilon^{\prime}(t_{2})-\Upsilon^{\prime}(t_{1})|\leqslant\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}|\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(t)|\,dt\leqslant 2(1-a)m\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}t^{1-2a}\,dt

\displaystyle\qquad=2(1-a)m\big(t^{2(1-a)}_{2}-t^{2(1-a)}_{1}\big)\leqslant C(t_{2}-t_{1})^{2(1-a)},

showing that
\Upsilon\in C^{1,2(1-a)}([0,2t_{*}])
.

However, if
\xi>2(1-a)
,

\lim_{t\searrow 0}\frac{\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)-\Upsilon^{\prime}(0)}{t^{\xi}}=+\infty,

due to (
6.3
), hence
\Upsilon\not\in C^{1,\xi}([0,2t_{*}])
.
We have therefore completed the proof of (
6.5
).

Now, we assume that
a>1
. In this case, we have that

\lim_{t\searrow 0}\Upsilon^{1-a}(t)=+\infty,

therefore, for small
t>0
, it is convenient to write (
6.8
) in the form

(6.12)

\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)=\Upsilon^{1-a}(t)\sqrt{m+\Upsilon^{2(a-1)}(t)-\Upsilon^{2a}(t)}=\big(\sqrt{m}+o(1)\big)\Upsilon^{1-a}(t)

and accordingly

\frac{d}{dt}\big(\Upsilon^{a}(t)\big)=a\Upsilon^{a-1}(t)\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)=a\sqrt{m}+o(1).

This entails that

\Upsilon^{a}(t)=a\sqrt{m}\,t+o(t)

and therefore

(6.13)

\Upsilon(t)=\big(a\sqrt{m}\,t+o(t)\big)^{\frac{1}{a}}=\big(a^{\frac{1}{a}}{m}^{\frac{1}{2a}}+o(1)\big)\,t^{\frac{1}{a}}.

This shows that
\Upsilon\not\in C^{\xi}([0,2t_{*}])
when
\xi>\frac{1}{a}
.

In addition, in light of (
6.12
) and (
6.13
),

\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)=\big(\sqrt{m}+o(1)\big)\big(a^{\frac{1}{a}}{m}^{\frac{1}{2a}}+o(1)\big)^{1-a}\,t^{\frac{1-a}{a}}=\big(a^{\frac{1-a}{a}}{m}^{\frac{1}{2a}}+o(1)\big)\,t^{\frac{1-a}{a}}.

Owing to this, if
\hat{t}>0
is sufficiently small and
0<t_{1}<t_{2}<\hat{t}
,

\displaystyle|\Upsilon(t_{2})-\Upsilon(t_{1})|\leqslant\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}|\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)|\,dt\leqslant 2a^{\frac{1-a}{a}}{m}^{\frac{1}{2a}}\,\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}t^{\frac{1-a}{a}}\,dt

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=2a^{\frac{1}{a}}{m}^{\frac{1}{2a}}\big(t^{\frac{1}{a}}_{2}-t^{\frac{1}{a}}_{1}\big)\leqslant C(t_{2}-t_{1})^{\frac{1}{a}},

for some
C>0
, which demonstrates that
\Upsilon\in C^{\frac{1}{a}}([0,2t_{*}])
.
This ends the proof of (
6.6
).
∎

This section contains some bespoke results on solutions of ordinary differential equations
which rely on the preliminary work done in the previous sections and

to establish Theorems

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
a>0
, with
a\neq 1
.
Let
T_{0}>0
and
y\in C([0,T_{0}])\cap C^{2}((0,T_{0}))
be a solution of

(7.1)

\begin{cases}y^{2}+yy^{\prime\prime}+(a-1)(y^{2}+(y^{\prime})^{2}-1)=0,\\
y(0)=0,\\
y(t)>0{\mbox{ for all }}t\in(0,T_{0}).\end{cases}

Then, either

(7.2)

y(t)=\begin{cases}\sin t&{\mbox{ if }}a\neq 1/2,\\
\sin t+c\,\big(1-\cos t\big)&{\mbox{ if }}a=1/2,\end{cases}

with
c\in\mathbb{R}
,
or
y(t)
is implicitly defined by the relation

(7.3)

t=\int_{0}^{y(t)}\frac{dY}{\sqrt{1+m\,Y^{2(1-a)}-Y^{2}}},

for some
m\in\mathbb{R}
, with
m\geqslant 0
if
a>1
.

Proof.

If
y

has the form claimed in (
7.2
),
then it solves (
7.1
) by a direct computation. Furthermore, if
y

is as in (
7.3
), then it solves (
7.1
),
due to Proposition
5.1
.

Hence, it remains to prove that if

y
solves (
7.1
), then it is of the form claimed in either (
7.2
) or (
7.3
).
To establish this, we first observe that if
\widetilde{y}\in C([0,T_{0}])\cap C^{2}((0,T_{0}))
is a solution of (
7.1
),
then, by the uniqueness result for regular Cauchy problems, we deduce that

(7.4)

\begin{split}&{\mbox{if\penalty\ $y(t)=\widetilde{y}(t)$
for all\penalty\ $t$ in an interval\penalty\ $I\subsetneqq[0,T_{0}]$,}}\\
&{\mbox{then\penalty\ $y(t)=\widetilde{y}(t)$
for all\penalty\ $t\in[0,T_{0}]$.}}\end{split}

As a consequence, in light of (
7.4
), it is sufficient
to prove that if

y
solves (
7.1
), then it is of the form claimed in either (
7.2
) or (
7.3
)
for all
t
in a suitable interval.

To this end, we observe that

(7.5)

y
cannot be constant in an open interval.

Indeed, suppose by contradiction that
y(t)=c_{0}
for all
t

in an open interval
I
. Then, by (
7.1
), for all
t\in I
,

0=c_{0}^{2}+0+(a-1)(c_{0}^{2}+0-1)=ac_{0}^{2}-a+1.

In particular, necessarily
a\neq 0
, and then
c_{0}^{2}=\frac{a-1}{a}\neq 0
.
This says that
y
is equal to
c_{0}\neq 0
in an open interval and we can therefore divide by
y
in the ordinary differential
equation in (
7.1
) and extend the solution. But then, using the initial value in (
7.1
), we see that
0=y(0)=c_{0}\neq 0
,
which is a contradiction and (
7.5
) is proved.

Now, we define

(7.6)

w(t):=y^{2}(t)+(y^{\prime}(t))^{2}-1.

We first suppose that
w
vanishes identically in an open interval
I
.
In this case, for all
t\in I
,

(y^{\prime}(t))^{2}=1-y^{2}(t).

Also, by (
7.5
), we can find an interval
I^{\prime}\subseteq I
in which
y^{\prime}\neq 0
.
Thus we conclude that, for every
t\in I^{\prime}
,

by^{\prime}(t)=\sqrt{1-y^{2}(t)},

with
b\in\{-1,1\}
and accordingly

\frac{d}{dt}\Big(b\arcsin y(t)-t\Big)=\frac{by^{\prime}(t)}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}(t)}}-1=0.

From this we arrive at

(7.7)

{\mbox{$y(t)=\sin\frac{t}{b}+\bar{c}=b\sin t+\bar{c}$ for all\penalty\ $t\in I^{\prime}$}},

where
\bar{c}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Consequently, by (
7.1
), for all
t\in I^{\prime}
,

(7.8)

\begin{split}0\,&=(b\sin t+\bar{c})^{2}-b(b\sin t+\bar{c})\sin t+(a-1)\big((b\sin t+\bar{c})^{2}+\cos^{2}t-1\big)\\
&=\bar{c}(a\bar{c}+b(2a-1)\sin t).\end{split}

This gives that

(7.9)

\bar{c}=0.

Because, if not, we deduce from (
7.8
)
that the real analytic function
a\bar{c}+b(2a-1)\sin t
vanishes for all
t\in I^{\prime}

and so, by analytic continuation, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence, taking
t\in\left\{0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right\}
,

a\bar{c}=0\qquad{\mbox{and}}\qquad a\bar{c}+b(2a-1)=0,

yielding that
a=\frac{1}{2}
and then (
7.9
), as desired.

In light of (
7.9
), we deduce that (
7.7
)
boils down to
y(t)=b\sin t
for all
t\in I^{\prime}
. Actually, by (
7.4
),
we have that
y(t)=b\sin t
for all
t\in[0,T_{0}]
.
Now, if
b=-1
, then we obtain a
contradiction with the assumption that
y(t)>0
. Therefore, we conclude that
b=1
and
accordingly
y(t)=\sin t
for all
t\in[0,T_{0}]
, which is of the form claimed in (
7.2
).

Thus, from now on, we can assume that

(7.10)

w
cannot be identically zero in an open interval.

In this setting, we recall (
7.5
) and we deduce that
y^{\prime}
cannot be identically zero
in an open interval.
Furthermore, since
y
is analytic in
(0,T_{0})
(being the solution of an
analytic Cauchy problem, see e.g. page 124 in
[
4
]
), we have that
y^{\prime}
is
analytic in
(0,T_{0})
as well, and therefore the set
\{y^{\prime}=0\}
cannot
have accumulation points in
(0,T_{0})
.

As a consequence of this observation, we have that
there exists
{\mathcal{I}}\subset\mathbb{N}
such that

(7.11)

\{y^{\prime}\neq 0\}=\bigcup_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}(\theta_{i+1},\theta_{i}),

where
\theta_{0}=T_{0}
and
\theta_{i+1}\in[0,\theta_{i})
.

We now claim that

(7.12)

(y^{\prime}(t))^{2}=1+m\,y^{2(1-a)}(t)-y^{2}(t)\quad{\mbox{for all }}t\in(0,T_{0}),

for some
m\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

To prove it, we observe that

(7.13)

w^{\prime}=2yy^{\prime}+2y^{\prime}y^{\prime\prime}=2y^{\prime}(y+y^{\prime\prime}).

Hence, in every interval of the form
(\theta_{i+1},\theta_{i})
,
we can divide by
2y^{\prime}
and find that

\frac{w^{\prime}}{2y^{\prime}}=y+y^{\prime\prime}.

This and (
7.1
)
give that

0=y^{2}+yy^{\prime\prime}+(a-1)w=y(y+y^{\prime\prime})+(a-1)w=\frac{w^{\prime}y}{2y^{\prime}}+(a-1)w,

which produces

\frac{d}{dt}(\log|w|)=\frac{w^{\prime}}{w}=-2(a-1)\frac{y^{\prime}}{y}=2(1-a)\frac{d}{dt}(\log|y|).

As a result, for every
\varepsilon_{i}\in(\theta_{i+1},\theta_{i})
such that
w(\varepsilon_{i})\neq 0
(whose existence is warranted by (
7.10
)) we have that

\log\frac{|w(t)|}{|w(\varepsilon_{i})|}=2(1-a)\log\frac{|y(t)|}{|y(\varepsilon_{i})|}.

Hence, since
y(t)>0
,

(7.14)

|w(t)|=|w(\varepsilon_{i})|\,\left(\frac{|y(t)|}{|y(\varepsilon_{i})|}\right)^{2(1-a)}=\frac{|w(\varepsilon_{i})|}{y^{2(1-a)}(\varepsilon_{i})}\,y^{2(1-a)}(t).

Without loss of generality, we can assume that

(7.15)

w
has a strict sign in the interval
(\theta_{i+1},\theta_{i})
,

otherwise we can pick a sequence of points
\varepsilon_{k}\in(\theta_{i+1},\theta_{i})

such that
w(\varepsilon_{k})\neq 0
,

\varepsilon_{k}\to\overline{\varepsilon}_{i}\in(\theta_{i+1},\theta_{i})
as
k\to+\infty

and
w(\overline{\varepsilon}_{i})=0
. This and (
7.14
) give that, for every
t\in(\theta_{i+1},\theta_{i})
,

|w(t)|=\lim_{k\to+\infty}\frac{|w(\varepsilon_{k})|}{y^{2(1-a)}(\varepsilon_{k})}\,y^{2(1-a)}(t)=\frac{|w(\overline{\varepsilon}_{i})|}{y^{2(1-a)}(\overline{\varepsilon}_{i})}\,y^{2(1-a)}(t)=0,

in contradiction with our statement in (
7.10
).
This proves (
7.15
).

Also, by (
7.6
), (
7.14
) and (
7.15
), for every
t\in(\theta_{i+1},\theta_{i})
,

(7.16)

y^{2}(t)+(y^{\prime}(t))^{2}-1=w(t)=\frac{w(\varepsilon_{i})}{y^{2(1-a)}(\varepsilon_{i})}\,y^{2(1-a)}(t)=m_{i}\,y^{2(1-a)}(t),

where

m_{i}:=\frac{w(\varepsilon_{i})}{y^{2(1-a)}(\varepsilon_{i})}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}.

We claim that

(7.17)

m_{i+1}=m_{i}\quad{\mbox{for all }}i\in{\mathcal{I}}.

Indeed, since (
7.16
) holds true in
(\theta_{i+1},\theta_{i})

with coefficient
m_{i}
and in
(\theta_{i+2},\theta_{i+1})
with coefficient
m_{i+1}
,
we have that

\displaystyle y^{2}(t)+(y^{\prime}(t))^{2}-1-m_{i}\,y^{2(1-a)}(t)=0\qquad{\mbox{ for all }}\,t\in(\theta_{i+1},\theta_{i})

and

\displaystyle y^{2}(t)+(y^{\prime}(t))^{2}-1-m_{i+1}\,y^{2(1-a)}(t)=0\qquad{\mbox{ for all }}\,t\in(\theta_{i+2},\theta_{i+1}).

For this reason,

\displaystyle y^{2}(\theta_{i+1})+(y^{\prime}(\theta_{i+1}))^{2}-1-m_{i}\,y^{2(1-a)}(\theta_{i+1})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=0=y^{2}(\theta_{i+1})+(y^{\prime}(\theta_{i+1}))^{2}-1-m_{i+1}\,y^{2(1-a)}(\theta_{i+1}),

which gives (
7.17
).

As a consequence of (
7.17
), we can set
m:=m_{i}
,
recall (
7.11
)
and obtain that the equation in (
7.12
)
is satisfied in
\{y^{\prime}\neq 0\}
, and thus in
\overline{\{y^{\prime}\neq 0\}}\cap(0,T_{0})=(0,T_{0})
.
This completes the proof of (

).

Now we claim that

(7.18)

\begin{split}&{\mbox{if $a\in(0,1)$ then }}\lim_{t\searrow 0}y^{\prime}(t)=1,\\
&{\mbox{and if\penalty\ $a>1$ then\penalty\ $m>0$ and }}\lim_{t\searrow 0}y^{\prime}(t)=+\infty.\end{split}

To check this, let us first suppose that
a\in(0,1)
. Then, by (

),

\lim_{t\searrow 0}(y^{\prime}(t))^{2}=\lim_{t\searrow 0}\Big(1+m\,y^{2(1-a)}(t)-y^{2}(t)\Big)=1.

Since

is positive for small

, this gives (
7.18
)
in this case.

Let us now suppose that
a>1
. Thus,
using (
7.12
) we obtain that

(7.19)

\lim_{t\searrow 0}(y^{\prime}(t))^{2}=\lim_{t\searrow 0}\Big(1+m\,y^{2(1-a)}(t)-y^{2}(t)\Big)=1+m\;\infty,

therefore, in this case, since the left hand side is nonnegative,

.
Hence, we obtain from (

) that

\lim_{t\searrow 0}(y^{\prime}(t))^{2}=+\infty

and the claim in (

)
follows since

is positive for small

.

As a consequence of (

) we obtain that,

y^{\prime}(t)\in(0,+\infty].

Thus, exploiting (

\Psi(y(t))=t.

Indeed,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\searrow 0}\Big(\Psi(y(t))-t\Big)=\lim_{r\searrow 0}\int_{0}^{r}\frac{dY}{\sqrt{1+m\,Y^{2(1-a)}-Y^{2}}}=0

Remark 7.2
.

We stress that some explicit solutions
can be found among those presented in (
7.3
). Namely, if
a:=2

and
m>0
then (
7.3
) reads

\displaystyle t=\int_{0}^{y(t)}\frac{Y\,dY}{\sqrt{Y^{2}+m-Y^{4}}}.

Since a primitive of
\frac{Y}{\sqrt{Y^{2}+m-Y^{4}}}
is given by
-\frac{1}{2}\arctan\frac{1-2Y^{2}}{2\sqrt{m+Y^{2}-Y^{4}}}
, we find that

\displaystyle t=\frac{1}{2}\left(\arctan\frac{1}{2\sqrt{m}}-\arctan\frac{1-2y^{2}(t)}{2\sqrt{m+y^{2}(t)-y^{4}(t)}}\right)

and therefore

\displaystyle\frac{1-2y^{2}(t)}{2\sqrt{m+y^{2}(t)-y^{4}(t)}}=\tan\left(\arctan\frac{1}{2\sqrt{m}}-2t\right).

Hence, using the trigonometric formula

\tan(\alpha-\beta)=\frac{\tan\alpha-\tan\beta}{1+\tan\alpha\tan\beta},

we obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{1-2y^{2}(t)}{2\sqrt{m+y^{2}(t)-y^{4}(t)}}=\frac{\frac{1}{2\sqrt{m}}-\tan(2t)}{1+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{m}}\tan(2t)},

which gives that

y^{2}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\Big(1\pm\big(2\sqrt{m}\sin(2t)-\cos(2t)\big)\Big).

Noticing that

\lim_{t\searrow 0}\frac{d}{dt}\big(y^{2}(t)\big)=\lim_{t\searrow 0}\pm\big(2\sqrt{m}\cos(2t)+\sin(2t)\big)=\pm 2\sqrt{m}

we find that
0\leqslant y^{2}(t)=\pm 2\sqrt{m}t+o(t)
for small
t
. This solves the sign ambiguity, leading to

y^{2}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\Big(1+\big(2\sqrt{m}\sin(2t)-\cos(2t)\big)\Big)=\frac{1-\cos(2t)}{2}+\sqrt{m}\sin(2t)

and therefore

(7.23)

y(t)=\sqrt{\frac{1-\cos(2t)}{2}+\sqrt{m}\sin(2t)},

see Figure
10
for a diagram of this function when
m:=1
.

Recalling Lemma
3.1
, we infer from this example that the function

u=\frac{r^{2}\big(1-\cos(2\theta)+2\sqrt{m}\sin(2\theta)\big)}{4}=\frac{x_{2}^{2}+2\sqrt{m}\,x_{1}x_{2}}{2}

is a solution of
\Delta u=1
(which can also be checked by a direct calculation). This observation is related to (

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
a>0
.
Assume that there exists a periodic
solution
y\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
of the problem

(7.24)

\begin{cases}y^{2}+yy^{\prime\prime}+(a-1)(y^{2}+(y^{\prime})^{2}-1)=0,\\
\displaystyle\min_{\mathbb{R}}y>0.\end{cases}

Then,

(7.25)

a>1

and

(7.26)

{\mbox{$y$ is constantly equal to }}\sqrt{\frac{a-1}{a}}.

Proof.

Let
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
be such that

y(t_{0})=\min_{[0,2\pi]}y>0.

Then, we have that
y^{\prime}(t_{0})=0
and
y^{\prime\prime}(t_{0})\geqslant 0
.
This and the equation in (
7.24
) give that

(7.27)

\begin{split}&0=y^{2}(t_{0})+y(t_{0})y^{\prime\prime}(t_{0})+(a-1)(y^{2}(t_{0})-1)\\
&\qquad=ay^{2}(t_{0})+y(t_{0})y^{\prime\prime}(t_{0})-a+1>-a+1,\end{split}

which yields (
7.25
),
as desired.

It is also useful to remark that, in view of (
7.27
),

0=ay^{2}(t_{0})+y(t_{0})y^{\prime\prime}(t_{0})-a+1\geqslant ay^{2}(t_{0})-a+1,

and therefore

(7.28)

y(t_{0})\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{a-1}{a}}.

Similarly, if
t_{1}
is such that

(7.29)

y(t_{1})=\max_{[0,2\pi]}y>0,

we have that
y^{\prime}(t_{1})=0
and
y^{\prime\prime}(t_{1})\leqslant 0
,
whence the equation in (
7.24
) gives that

0=y^{2}(t_{1})+y(t_{1})y^{\prime\prime}(t_{1})+(a-1)(y^{2}(t_{1})-1)\leqslant ay^{2}(t_{1})-a+1,

and accordingly

y(t_{1})\geqslant\sqrt{\frac{a-1}{a}}.

We claim that

(7.30)

y(t_{1})=\sqrt{\frac{a-1}{a}}.

For this, we argue by contradiction, supposing that

(7.31)

y(t_{1})>\sqrt{\frac{a-1}{a}}.

We define

W(t):=1-y^{2}(t)-(y^{\prime}(t))^{2}

and we observe that, in light of (
7.28
),

(7.32)

W(t_{0})=1-y^{2}(t_{0})\geqslant 1-\frac{a-1}{a}=\frac{1}{a}>0.

Therefore,
W
is strictly positive in
some interval
I:=(t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}+\delta)
,
for a suitable
\delta>0
. As a consequence, we can consider the logarithm of
W

in
I
and exploit
the equation in (
7.24
) to see that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\log W=\frac{W^{\prime}}{W}=\frac{-2yy^{\prime}-2y^{\prime}y^{\prime\prime}}{1-y^{2}-(y^{\prime})^{2}}=\frac{-2y^{\prime}(y+y^{\prime\prime})}{1-y^{2}-(y^{\prime})^{2}}=\frac{-2y^{\prime}(y^{2}+yy^{\prime\prime})}{y(1-y^{2}-(y^{\prime})^{2})}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{2(a-1)y^{\prime}(y^{2}+(y^{\prime})^{2}-1)}{y(1-y^{2}-(y^{\prime})^{2})}=\frac{-2(a-1)y^{\prime}}{y}=-2(a-1)\frac{d}{dt}\log y,

and, as a result, for all
t\in I
,

\log\frac{W(t)}{W(t_{0})}=-2(a-1)\log\frac{y(t)}{y(t_{0})}=\log\left(\frac{y(t)}{y(t_{0})}\right)^{2(1-a)}.

Therefore, setting

(7.33)

\kappa:=\frac{W(t_{0})}{(y(t_{0}))^{2(1-a)}},

we find that, for all
t\in I
,

(7.34)

1-y^{2}(t)-(y^{\prime}(t))^{2}=W(t)=\kappa(y(t))^{2(1-a)}.

We also remark that
y
is an analytic function, since it is a solution
of an analytic Cauchy problem (the sign condition in (
7.24
)
ensuring that the source term of the differential equation is non-singular,
after a division by
y
), see e.g. page 124 in
[
4
]
.
Consequently, the relation in (
7.34
)
is globally valid, namely

(7.35)

(y^{\prime}(t))^{2}=1-y^{2}(t)-\kappa(y(t))^{2(1-a)}\qquad{\mbox{for all }}t\in\mathbb{R}.

Moreover, recalling (
7.28
), (
7.32
)
and (
7.33
),

\kappa\geqslant\frac{1/a}{((a-1)/a)^{1-a}}=\frac{1}{a}\,\left(\frac{a}{a-1}\right)^{1-a}.

For this reason and (
7.35
), we have that

0\leqslant 1-y^{2}(t)-\kappa(y(t))^{2(1-a)}\leqslant 1-y^{2}(t)-\frac{1}{a}\,\left(\frac{a}{a-1}\right)^{1-a}\,(y(t))^{2(1-a)}\qquad{\mbox{for all }}t\in\mathbb{R}.

From this and (
7.31
), we find that

0<1-\frac{a-1}{a}-\frac{1}{a}\,\left(\frac{a}{a-1}\right)^{1-a}\,\left(\frac{a-1}{a}\right)^{1-a}=0.

This is a contradiction, and thus (
7.30
)
is established.

As a consequence of (
7.29
)
and (
7.30
), we have that

y(t_{1})=\sqrt{\frac{a-1}{a}}\qquad{\mbox{and}}\qquad y^{\prime}(t_{1})=0.

Since, by inspection, the function
y_{\star}
constantly equal to
\sqrt{\frac{a-1}{a}}

is also a solution of (
7.24
), by the uniqueness result
of the standard Cauchy problem we infer that
y(t)=y_{\star}(t)

for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and this proves the desired claim in (
7.26
).
∎

9.
A comment about weak solutions

We point out that none of the implicit solutions presented in Theorem
1.2
, when extended
by zero outside their positivity cone,
is a weak solution of
\Delta u=\gamma u^{\gamma-1}\chi_{\{u>0\}}
.
Indeed, suppose that one of these functions is a weak solution
and that its positivity cone is given by the set
\{(r,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,\varphi)\}
, for some
\varphi\in(0,2\pi)
.

Consider a test function
\phi
supported in a small ball
B
around
e_{1}=(1,0)
.
Then,

\int_{B}\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla\phi(x)\,dx=-\gamma\int_{B}u^{\gamma-1}(x)\chi_{\{u>0\}}(x)\phi(x)\,dx=-\gamma\int_{B^{+}}u^{\gamma-1}(x)\phi(x)\,dx,

where
B^{+}:=B\cap\{x_{2}>0\}
.

But

\displaystyle\int_{B}\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla\phi(x)\,dx=\int_{B\cap\{u>0\}}\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla\phi(x)\,dx=\int_{B^{+}}\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla\phi(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{B^{+}}{\rm div}\big(\phi(x)\,\nabla u(x)\big)\,dx-\int_{B^{+}}\Delta u(x)\phi(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad=-\int_{H}\phi(x_{1},0^{+})\partial_{2}u(x_{1},0^{+})\,dx_{1}-\gamma\int_{B^{+}}u^{\gamma-1}(x)\phi(x)\,dx,

where
H:=B\cap\{x_{2}=0\}
.

Therefore
\partial_{2}u(x_{1},0^{+})=0
along the
x_{1}
-axis.

But the implicit solutions, constructed in Theorem
1.2

do not satisfy this condition, since (up to multiplicative constants that we omit for simplicity):

•

if
a\in(0,1)
, then
y(\theta)=\theta\,(1+o(1))
, due to (
6.2
),

•

if
a>1
, then
y(\theta)=\theta^{\frac{1}{a}}(1+o(1))
, due to (
6.6
).

Therefore

g(\theta)=\begin{cases}y^{a}(\theta)=\theta^{a}(1+o(1))&{\mbox{ when }}a\in(0,1),\\
\theta(1+o(1))&{\mbox{ when }}a>1,\end{cases}

whence

\partial_{2}u(1,0^{+})=g^{\prime}(0^{+})=\begin{cases}+\infty&{\mbox{ when }}a\in(0,1),\\
1&{\mbox{ when }}a>1.\end{cases}
