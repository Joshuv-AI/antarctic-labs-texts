Abstract
. This paper is concerned with portfolio selection for an investor with power utility in multi-asset financial markets in a rough stochastic environment. We investigate Merton’s portfolio problem for different multivariate Volterra models, covering the rough Heston model. First we consider a class of multivariate affine Volterra models introduced in [E. Abi Jaber et al., SIAM J. Financial Math., 12, 369–409, (2021)]. Based on the classical Wishart model described in [N. Bäuerle and Li, Z., J. Appl. Probab., 50, 1025–1043 (2013)], we then introduce a new matrix-valued stochastic volatility model, where the volatility is driven by a Volterra-Wishart process. Due to the non-Markovianity of the underlying processes, the classical stochastic control approach cannot be applied in these settings. To overcome this issue, we provide a verification argument using calculus of convolutions and resolvents. The resulting optimal strategy can then be expressed explicitly in terms of the solution of a multivariate Riccati-Volterra equation. We thus extend the results obtained by Han and Wong to the multivariate case, avoiding restrictions on the correlation structure linked to the martingale distortion transformation used in [B. Han and Wong, H. Y., Finance Res. Lett., 39 (2021)]. We also provide existence and uniqueness theorems for the occurring Volterra processes and illustrate our results with a numerical study.

1.
Introduction

Since the observation was made that the paths of realized volatilities are rougher than established volatility models would suggest, cf.
[
17
]
, there is a growing research interest in developing new models that better fit empirical data. In
[
14
]
, the popular Heston model
[
24
]
was adapted to the rough volatility framework by using a fractional process with Hurst index
H<\frac{1}{2}
as driver of the volatility process. A more general class of volatility models covering the rough Heston model in
[
14
]
is obtained by modelling the volatility process as a stochastic Volterra equation of convolution type
[
4
,
28
,
5
]
. Although most of the literature about rough volatility is concerned with option pricing, there are some recent works dealing with Merton portfolio optimization in such models. While
[
23
]
and
[
5
]
are dealing with the Markowitz portfolio problem, the Merton portfolio problem is studied in
[
15
,
6
,
22
]
.

Merton’s portfolio problem aims at maximizing an investor’s utility from terminal wealth with respect to his utility function. The problem for the classical Heston stochastic volatility model was explicitly solved in
[
29
]
, based on the represenation result of
[
34
]
, and solutions for affine stochastic volatility models were derived in
[
27
]
. In
[
7
]
, the Merton problem was studied for a multi-asset financial market where the volatility is modeled by a matrix-valued Wishart process, using stochastic control theory.
In the rough framework it is no longer possible to apply the classical stochastic control approach deriving the corresponding Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation, due to the non-Markovianity of the rough volatility processes. In order to circumvent this problem, in
[
6
]
, Bäuerle and Desmettre use a finite dimensional approximation of the volatility process in order to cast the problem back into the classical framework. However, this only yields explicit solutions in case that there is no correlation between stock and volatility. Han and Wong
[
22
]
overcome this difficulty using a martingale distortion Ansatz and applying the martingale optimality principle to obtain an explicit solution for the optimal investment strategy in a mono-asset Volterra Heston model.
In order to take into account several important stylized facts about real financial markets such as choice among multiple assets, roughness of the volatility, correlation between different stocks and leverage effects, i.e. correlation between a stock and its volatility, multivariate rough volatility models have recently been developed (cf.
[
10
]
,
[
32
]
,
[
4
]
).
In
[
5
]
, Abi Jaber et al. study the Markowitz portfolio problem for a class of multivariate affine Volterra models, that features correlation between the stocks and between a stock and its volatility.

In this paper we solve the Merton portfolio problem for an investor with a power-utility function for different multivariate Volterra models including the rough Heston model.
The outline of the paper is as follows:
Section
2
gives an overview of the calculus of convolutions and resolvents which is needed throughout the paper.
In Section
3
we introduce a class of multivariate affine Volterra models studied in
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
. For such a market model we consider two different approaches to solve the Merton portfolio problem. We first adapt the martingale distortion transformation used in
[
22
]
to the multivariate case. However, as it is pointed out in
[
5
]
, this only works if the correlation structure is highly degenerate.
Inspired by the techniques used in
[
7
]
, we then provide a solution for the Merton portfolio problem for a more general correlation structure using calculus of convolutions and resolvents.
In Section
4
we introduce a more general market model where the volatility is a matrix-valued stochastic process. In our model we adapt the Wishart stochastic volatility model studied in
[
20
]
and
[
7
]
to the Volterra framework, defining the variance-covariance matrix as the solution of a matrix-valued Volterra-Wishart equation, thus extending the Heston model. Considering a matrix-valued volatility process allows us to take into account correlation between different assets.
Despite the non-Markovianity of these settings, the optimal strategy can be expressed explicitly in terms of the solution of a multivariate Riccati-Volterra equation. In Section
5
we illustrate our results with a numerical example. Section
6
provides existence and uniqueness results for the appearing Volterra equations even in the matrix-valued case.
Auxiliary results and longer proofs can be found in the Appendix.

2.
Convolutions and resolvents

In this section we give a short overview of some important definitions and results from the calculus of convolutions and resolvents, that we are going to use frequently throughout the paper. We start by defining three different types of convolutions:

Definition 2.1

(Convolution of two functions)
.

[
4
, Chapter 2]

Let
K
and
F
be functions defined on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Then the convolution
K\ast F
of
K
and
F
is defined as

(K\ast F)(t)=\int_{0}^{t}K(t-s)F(s)ds,

(2.1)

whenever the above expressions are well-defined.

This definition can of course be extended to matrix-valued functions. In this case it is important that the dimensions of the matrices are compatible.

Definition 2.2

(Convolution of a measurable function and a measure)
.

[
4
, Chapter 2]

Let
K
be a measurable function on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
L
be a measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
of locally bounded variation. Then the convolutions
K\ast L
and
L\ast K
are defined as

(K\ast L)(t)=\int_{[0,t]}K(t-s)L(ds);\quad(L\ast K)(t)=\int_{[0,t]}L(ds)K(t-s),

(2.2)

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
for which the above integrals exist.

Definition 2.3

(Convolution of a measurable function and a local martingale)
.

[
4
, Chapter 2]

Let
M
be a
d
-dimensional continuous local martingale and
K:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
be a function. Then the convolution
K\ast dM
is defined as

(K\ast dM)_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}K(t-s)dM_{s}.

(2.3)

Remark 2.4
.

The above convolution is well-defined as an Itô-integral for any
t\geq 0
satisfying

\int_{0}^{t}\left|K(t-s)\right|^{2}d\operatorname{tr}(M_{s})<\infty.

If
K\in L_{\operatorname{loc}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and there is a locally bounded process
a
such that
\langle M\rangle_{s}=\int_{0}^{s}a_{u}du
then the convolution is well defined for all
t\geq 0
.

The following lemma shows that also this type of convolution is associative.

Lemma 2.5
.

[
4
, Lemma 2.1]

Let
K\in L_{\operatorname{loc}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}^{m\times d})
and let
L
be an
\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
-valued measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
of locally bounded variation. Let
M
be a
d
-dimensional continuous martingale with
\langle M\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}a_{s}ds
,
t\geq 0
, for some locally bounded adapted process
a
. Then

(L\ast(K\ast dM))_{t}=((L\ast K)\ast dM)_{t}

(2.4)

for every
t\geq 0
. In particular, taking
F\in L_{\operatorname{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
we may apply (
2.4
) with
L(dt)=Fdt
to obtain
(F\ast(K\ast dM))_{t}=((F\ast K)\ast dM)_{t}
.

Another useful concept related to the integral kernel
K
are so called resolvents. We distinguish between resolvents of the first and the second kind.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
K\in L_{\operatorname{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
and
L
be a
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
-valued measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Then
L
is called the
resolvent of the first kind
of
K
if

K\ast L=L\ast K\equiv I

(2.5)

where

is the

For a kernel

3.
A class of multivariate affine Volterra models

To start our investigation, we use the affine Volterra model introduced in
[
5
, Chapter 4]
.
Let
K=\operatorname{diag}(K_{1},\dots,K_{d})
with scalar kernels
K_{i}\in L^{2}([0,T],\mathbb{R})
on the diagonal.
In our model we consider
d
stocks and we assume that the price of the
i
th stock has dynamics

dS_{t}^{i}=S_{t}^{i}(r_{t}+\theta_{i}V_{t}^{i})dt+S_{t}^{i}\sqrt{V_{t}^{i}}dW_{1t}^{i},

(3.1)

where
W_{1t}
is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion and
\theta_{i}\geq 0
.
For
N=\operatorname{diag}(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{d})
and
D\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
such that
D_{ij}\geq 0
if
i\neq j
, the volatility
V=(V^{1},\dots,V^{d})^{\top}
is defined as a Volterra square-root process

V_{t}=v_{0}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}K(t-s)DV_{s}ds+\int_{0}^{t}K(t-s)N{\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}(V_{s})}}dB_{s}.

(3.2)

Here
v_{0}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
is a deterministic function and
B
is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion for which the correlation structure with
W_{1}
is given by

dB_{s}^{i}=\rho_{i}dW_{1s}^{i}+\sqrt{1-\rho_{i}^{2}}dW_{2s}^{i},\,i=1,\dots,d,

(3.3)

where
W_{2}
is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion independent of
W_{1}
and
(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{d})\in[-1,1]^{d}
. In accordance with
[
5
]
, we assume that there exists a continuous
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2d}
-valued weak solution
(V,S)
to (
3.1
)-(
3.2
) on some filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
, satisfying the usual conditions.
A function
f
is completely monotone on
(0,\infty)
if it is infinitely differentiable on
(0,\infty)
and
(-1)^{n}f^{n}(t)\geq 0
for all
n\geq 1
and
t>0
.
Under the assumption that for each
i=1,\dots,d
,
K_{i}
is completely monotone on
(0,\infty)
and that there exists
\kappa_{i}\in(0,2]
and
k_{i}>0
such that

\int_{0}^{h}K_{i}^{2}(t)dt+\int_{0}^{T}(K_{i}(t+h)-K_{i}(t))^{2}dt\leq k_{i}h^{\kappa_{i}},\,h>0,

(3.4)

the existence of a unique in law
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
-valued continuous weak solution
V
of equation (
3.2
) is ensured by
[
4
, Theorem 6.1]
in case that
v_{0}(t)=V_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}K(t-s)b^{0}ds
for some
V_{0},b^{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
(cf.
[
5
, Remark 4.1]
). For a discussion about existence of a solution for more general input curves
v_{0}(t)
, see
[
3
]
. Note that condition (
3.4
) is fulfilled for constant, non-negative kernels, fractional kernels of the form
\frac{t^{H-\frac{1}{2}}}{\Gamma(H+\frac{1}{2})}
with
H\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
, and exponentially decaying kernels
e^{-\beta t}
with
\beta>0
. The existence of
S
defined via equation (
3.1
) follows from that of
V
.

3.1.
The optimization problem

A portfolio strategy
\pi_{t}=(\pi_{t,1},\dots,\pi_{t,d})
is an
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}
valued, progressively measurable process, where
\pi_{t,k}
represents the proportion of wealth invested into stock
k
at time
t
. Under a fixed portfolio strategy, the wealth process
(X_{t}^{\pi})
has dynamic

dX_{t}^{\pi}=X_{t}^{\pi}(r_{t}+\pi_{t}{\operatorname{diag}(V_{t})}{\theta}^{\top})dt+X_{t}\pi_{t}\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}(V_{t})}dW_{1t},

(3.5)

where
{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{d})
.
By
\mathcal{A}
we denote the set of admissible portfolio strategies. The conditions under which we consider a strategy to be admissible will be specified later.
We want to solve the Merton portfolio optimization problem for power utility, i.e. our aim is to find the value function
\mathcal{V}(x_{0},v_{0})
such that

\mathcal{V}(x_{0},v_{0})=\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}}\EX_{x_{0},v_{0}}[\frac{1}{\gamma}(X_{T}^{\pi})^{\gamma}];\quad 0<\gamma<1,

(3.6)

where
\EX_{x_{0},v_{0}}
is the conditional expectation given
X_{0}=x_{0},V_{0}=v_{0}
. The parameter
\gamma
represents the relative
risk aversion of the investor. Smaller
\gamma
correspond to higher risk aversion.
A portfolio strategy
\pi^{*}
for which the supremum is attained is called an optimal strategy. Seen as an optimization problem with state process
(X_{t})
this problem is non-Markovian and the standard stochastic control approach cannot be applied.

3.2.
The martingale distortion transformation

Consider a one-dimensional market model where the risky asset
S_{t}
is given by

dS_{t}=(r_{t}+\mu(Y_{t}))S_{t}dt+\sigma(Y_{t})S_{t}dW_{t}

and
Y_{t}
is a Markovian process defined via the SDE

dY_{t}=k(Y_{t})dt+h(Y_{t})dW_{t}^{Y},

where
W
and
W^{Y}
have correlation
\rho
. In this setup, a candidate for the value function

\mathcal{V}(t,x,y):=\sup_{\pi}\EX[\frac{(X_{T}^{\pi})^{\gamma}}{\gamma}|X_{t}=x,Y_{t}=y]

can be obtained by solving the corresponding Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation. The distortion transformation introduced in
[
34
]
uses the Ansatz

\mathcal{V}(t,x,y)=\frac{x^{\gamma}}{\gamma}\Phi(t,y)^{c},

where the constant
c
is defined as
c:=\frac{1-\gamma}{1-\gamma+\gamma\rho^{2}}
.
With this choice of
c
, the quadratic terms
(\Phi_{y})^{2}
in the HJB equation cancel out, leading to a linear PDE for
\Phi

\Phi_{t}+\Big(\frac{1}{2}h^{2}\partial_{yy}+k(y)\partial_{y}+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\lambda(y)ch(y)\partial_{y}\Big)\Phi+\frac{\gamma}{c}\Big(r_{t}+\frac{\lambda^{2}(y)}{2(1-\gamma)}\Big)\Phi=0,\,\Phi(T,y)=1,

where the Sharpe ratio
\lambda
is defined as
\lambda(y):=\mu(y)/\sigma(y)
. By the Feynman-Kac Theorem,
\Phi
can be written as

\Phi(t,y)=\tilde{\EX}[\operatorname{exp}\Big\{\int_{t}^{T}\frac{\gamma}{c}\Big(r_{s}+\frac{\lambda^{2}(Y_{s})}{2(1-\gamma)}\Big)ds\Big\}|Y_{t}=y],

where under the probability measure
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
with Radon-Nikodym density

\frac{d\tilde{\mathbb{P}}}{d\mathbb{P}}|_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}=\operatorname{exp}\Big(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{c\gamma}{1-\gamma}\lambda(Y_{s})dW_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{c^{2}\gamma^{2}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\lambda^{2}(Y_{s})ds\Big),

\tilde{W}_{t}^{Y}=W_{t}^{Y}-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{c\gamma}{1-\gamma}\lambda(Y_{s})ds
is a standard Brownian motion.

In
[
15
]
Fouque and Hu showed that if the Sharpe-ratio
\lambda
is bounded and has bounded derivative, then the value process
\mathcal{V}_{t}
can be expressed as
\mathcal{V}_{t}(x,y)=J_{t}(X_{t}=x,Y_{t}=y)
, where

J_{t}(X_{t}^{\pi},Y_{t}):=\frac{(X_{t}^{\pi})^{\gamma}}{\gamma}\Big(\tilde{\EX}\Big[\operatorname{exp}\Big\{\int_{t}^{T}\frac{\gamma}{c}\Big(r_{s}+\frac{\lambda^{2}(Y_{s})}{2(1-\gamma)}\Big)ds\Big\}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\Big]\Big)^{c}

even if the volatility process
Y_{t}
is non-Markovian.
This approach is called the martingale distortion transformation and was first introduced in the seminal paper
[
34
]
and later transferred to a non-Markovian setting in
[
31
]
. The extension to the multi-asset case is straight forward in the case of a bounded risk premium (cf.
[
15
]
, Remark 2.5.).

3.3.
The degenerate correlation case

In this section we present an extension of the proof of
[
22
]
to the multivariate case for a degenerate correlation structure, i.e. we assume that the correlation in (
3.3
) is of the form
(\rho,\dots,\rho)
for
\rho\in[-1,1]
. Note that since in our model the risk premium is unbounded, we can not apply the results of
[
15
]
. As in the one dimensional case, the Ansatz

J_{t}^{\pi}=\frac{(X_{t}^{\pi})^{\gamma}}{\gamma}\Big(\tilde{\EX}\Big[\operatorname{exp}\Big\{\int_{t}^{T}\frac{\gamma}{c}\Big(r_{s}+\frac{\theta\operatorname{diag}(V_{s})\theta^{\top}}{2(1-\gamma)}\Big)ds\Big\}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\Big]\Big)^{c}.

Here we use the short notation

Under the new probability measure

\tilde{W}_{1t}=W_{1t}-\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\int_{0}^{t}\theta\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}(V_{s})}ds,

an application of the exponential-affine transform formula in

\displaystyle\tilde{\EX}\Big[\operatorname{exp}\Big\{\int_{t}^{T}\Big(\frac{\gamma\theta\Theta V_{s}}{2c(1-\gamma)}\Big)ds\Big\}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\Big]=

Thus we obtain

In order to find the value function and the optimal strategy, we show that the family

(cf.

Note that

Solving the stochastic differential equation for

\displaystyle=\operatorname{exp}\{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}(\lVert cNP_{1}\operatorname{diag}{(V_{t})}\psi^{\top}(T-t)+\gamma\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}(V_{t})}\pi_{t}^{\top}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+\lVert cNP_{2}\operatorname{diag}{(V_{t})}\psi^{\top}(T-t)\rVert_{2}^{2})ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}[c\psi(T-s)NP_{1}\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}(V_{s})}+\gamma\pi_{s}\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}(V_{s})}]dW_{1s}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}c\psi(T-s)NP_{2}\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}(V_{s})}dW_{2s}\}.

Now, since

and thus the stochastic exponential

\EX[J_{t\wedge\tau_{n}}^{\pi}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]\leq J_{s\wedge\tau_{n}}^{\pi},\,s\leq t.

Since

is a supermartingale for every arbitrary admissible strategy
\pi
.
It remains to show that

is a true martingale for the optimal strategy
\pi^{*}
. In this case

and hence

In order to show that

is admissible, we have to show that (a), (b), (c) of Definition

as

For part (b) it suffices to show that

Inserting the explicit solution of the wealth process

Let

First we show that

\displaystyle cF_{1}(\psi){}

4.
The Volterra-Wishart volatility model

In this section we present the Volterra-Wishart model which is a generalization of the Wishart volatility model described in
[
20
]
and
[
7
]
to the Volterra framework. In contrast to the class of models presented in the previous section, the volatility is now modeled as a matrix-valued stochastic process.
The main advantage of using a matrix-valued volatility model is that this allows us to take into account the correlation between different stocks in our market and therefore extending the vector-valued model presented in the previous section. In contrast to the quadratic volatility models described in
[
5
, Chapter 5]
and
[
2
]
, generalizing the Stein-Stein and Schöbel-Zhu model respectively, our model is an extension of the Heston model to the multivariate Volterra framework. The reason why we in particular investigate a Wishart model is that, besides being a straight forward generalisation of the popular Heston model, the additional degrees of freedom with regard to the stochastic correlation enable a better fit of market data while being still efficiently tractable, compare e.g.
[
8
]
and
[
30
]
.

4.1.
Market model and optimization problem

In our model the market consists of one riskfree asset with time-dependent, deterministic risk free rate
r_{t}
and
d
risky assets.
The asset return vector process
(S_{t})_{t\geq 0}=(S_{t,1},\dots,S_{t,d})_{t\geq 0}
is defined via the stochastic differential system

dS_{t}=\operatorname{diag}(S_{t})((\underline{r}_{t}+\Sigma_{t}v)dt+\Sigma_{t}^{1/2}dW_{t}^{S}),

(4.1)

where
(W_{t}^{S})_{t\geq 0}
is a
d
-dimensional Brownian Motion vector ,
\underline{r}_{t}=(r_{t},\dots,r_{t})
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The stochastic volatility process
(\Sigma_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is given by the solution of the matrix-valued Volterra equation

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t}=\Sigma_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}K(t-s)(NN^{\top}+M\Sigma_{s}+\Sigma_{s}M^{\top})ds

(4.2)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}K(t-s)\Sigma_{s}^{1/2}dW_{s}^{\sigma}Q+\int_{0}^{t}K(t-s)Q^{\top}(dW_{s}^{\sigma})^{\top}\Sigma_{s}^{1/2}.

The integral kernel in the above equation
K=\operatorname{diag}(K_{1},\dots,K_{d})
,
K_{1}=\dots=K_{d}
is diagonal with scalar kernels
K_{i}\in L^{2}([0,T],\mathbb{R})
. The deterministic initial value
\Sigma_{0}
is assumed to be positive definite. The
d\times d
matrices
N
and
M
are responsible for the mean reversion, while the matrix
Q
governs the volatility of the process which is driven by a
d\times d
Brownian motion matrix
(W_{t}^{\sigma})_{t\geq 0}
. In order to incorporate the leverage effect in our model, we allow the Brownian motions
(W_{t}^{S})_{t\geq 0}
and
(W_{t}^{\sigma})_{t\geq 0}
to be correlated and we assume that
d\langle W_{t,k}^{S},W_{t,ij}^{\sigma}\rangle=\rho_{k,ij}
with
\rho_{k,ij}=0
for
k\neq i
and
\rho_{k,kj}=:\rho_{j}
independent of
k
. Thus for
\rho=(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{d})^{\top}
and another
d
-dimensional Brownian motion vector
B_{t}
independent of
(W_{t}^{\sigma})
, we have

W_{t}^{S}=\sqrt{1-\rho^{\top}\rho}B_{t}+W_{t}^{\sigma}\rho.

Under the assumption that the components of the kernel
K
fulfill the condition

\displaystyle K_{i}\in L_{\operatorname{loc}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R})\text{ and there is }\kappa_{i}\in(0,2]\text{ such that }\int_{0}^{h}K(t)^{2}dt=O(h^{\kappa_{i}})

(4.3)

\displaystyle\text{ and }\int_{0}^{T}(K(t+h)-K(t))^{2}dt=O(h^{\kappa_{i}})\text{ for every }T<\infty,

Theorem
6.1
ensures the existence of a continuous, symmetric and positive definite
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
-valued local weak solution
\Sigma
to (
4.1
)-(
4.2
) on some filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
, satisfying the usual conditions.
For such a symmetric, positive definite matrix
\Sigma
, by
\Sigma^{1/2}
we denote the unique symmetric, positive definite matrix
M
for which
M^{2}=\Sigma
.
Note that for
K=I
we recover the classical Wishart model described in
[
7
]
.

A portfolio strategy
\pi_{t}=(\pi_{t,1},\dots,\pi_{t,d})^{{\top}}
is an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued, progressively measurable process, where
\pi_{t,k}
represents the proportion of wealth invested into stock
k
at time
t
. Under a fixed portfolio strategy, the wealth process
(X_{t}^{\pi})
has dynamics

dX_{t}^{\pi}=X_{t}^{\pi}[(r_{t}+\pi_{t}^{\top}\Sigma_{t}v)dt+\pi_{t}^{\top}\Sigma_{t}^{1/2}dW_{t}^{S}],\quad X_{0}=x_{0}.

(4.4)

By
\mathcal{A}
we denote the set of admissible portfolio strategies.

Definition 4.1
.

In our setting we say that a portfolio strategy
\pi
is admissible if

(a)

the SDE (
4.4
) for the wealth process
(X_{t}^{\pi})
has a unique solution in terms of
(S,\Sigma,W^{S})
;

(b)

\EX[\frac{1}{\gamma}(X_{T}^{\pi})^{\gamma}]<\infty
for all
0<\gamma<1
;

(c)

\pi
is bounded a.s.

We want to solve the Merton portfolio optimization problem for power utility, i.e. our aim is to find the value function
\mathcal{V}(x_{0},\Sigma_{0})
such that

\mathcal{V}(x_{0},\Sigma_{0})=\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}}\EX_{x_{0},\Sigma_{0}}[\frac{1}{\gamma}(X_{T}^{\pi})^{\gamma}],\quad 0<\gamma<1,

(4.5)

where
\EX_{x_{0},\Sigma_{0}}
is the conditional expectation. Again, the parameter
\gamma
represents the relative risk aversion of the investor. A portfolio strategy
\pi^{*}
for which the supremum is attained is called an optimal strategy. As stated before, seen as an optimization problem with state process
(X_{t})
this problem is non-Markovian and the standard stochastic control approach cannot be applied.

4.2.
The main result

We solve the Merton portfolio problem for the Volterra-Wishart model using a verification argument inspired by
[
7
]
. As it was pointed out in Section
3.3
, the martingale distortion approach, used by
[
22
]
in the one dimensional case, can only be applied to the multivariate setting if the correlation structure is highly degenerate, i.e.
\rho_{1}=\dots=\rho_{d}
. Since in our model we allow arbitrary correlation vectors
\rho
, we have to use different techniques.
The proof builds on ideas presented in
[
7
]
for the classical Wishart volatility model, yet we are facing serious technical challenges by doing that. In particular, in our case the stochastic volatility process is of convolution type and hence non-Markovian, and therefore we cannot use Itô’s formula in order to show optimality of the candidate for the optimal strategy. In order to overcome this, we have to resort to the calculus of convolutions and resolvents. Note that the techniques we apply in our proof rely on the affine structure of the Volterra-Wishart convolution equation. Since the resulting calculations are rather involved, we present a condensed version of the proof here and the full proof can be found in Appendix
A
. Note that in contrast to the vector-valued cased, we cannot prove global existence of a week solution for the Volterra-Wishart process (
4.2
) and also the Riccati equation (
4.6
) can only be solved locally. Therefore we can only solve the Merton problem for a time horizon
T
within the interval
[0;T_{\operatorname{max}}\wedge T_{\operatorname{pos}}]
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume that equation (
4.2
) has a positive definite, continuous weak solution on the interval
[0,T_{\operatorname{pos}})
.
Let
\psi
be the solution of the matrix Riccati-Volterra equation

\psi(t)=\int_{0}^{t}f(\psi)(t-s)K(s)ds

(4.6)

with

f(\psi)=\psi\tilde{M}+\tilde{M}^{\top}\psi+2\psi\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}\psi+\tilde{\Gamma},

(4.7)

\tilde{M}=M+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}Q^{\top}\rho v^{\top},\quad\tilde{Q}^{\top}Q=Q^{\top}Q+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}Q^{\top}\rho\rho^{\top}Q,\quad\tilde{\Gamma}=\frac{\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}vv^{\top}

on the interval
[0,T_{\operatorname{max}})
, given by Theorem
6.4
. Then for
t\in[0,T]
,
T<T_{\operatorname{max}}\wedge T_{\operatorname{pos}}
, an optimal investment strategy
\pi_{t}^{*}
for the Merton portfolio problem (
4.5
) is given by

\pi_{t}^{*}=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}(v+2\psi(T-t)Q^{\top}\rho)

(4.8)

and the value function can be written as

\mathcal{V}(x_{0},\Sigma_{0})=\frac{x_{0}^{\gamma}}{\gamma}\operatorname{exp}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\gamma r_{s}+\operatorname{Tr}[f(\psi)(T-s)\Sigma_{0}+\psi(T-s)NN^{\top}]ds\Big).

Sketch of Proof:

In order to prove that
\pi^{*}
is indeed the optimal portfolio strategy, we show that for

G(x_{0},\Sigma_{0}):=\frac{x_{0}^{\gamma}}{\gamma}\operatorname{exp}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\gamma r_{s}+\operatorname{Tr}[f(\psi)(T-s)\Sigma_{0}+\psi(T-s)NN^{\top}]ds\Big),

we have

(a)

\EX^{x_{0},\Sigma_{0}}{\big[\frac{1}{\gamma}(X_{T}^{\pi^{*}})^{\gamma}\big]}=G(x_{0},\Sigma_{0})
for
\pi_{t}^{*}=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}(v+2\psi(T-t)Q^{\top}\rho)
,

(b)

\EX^{x_{0},\Sigma_{0}}{\big[\frac{1}{\gamma}(X_{T}^{\pi})^{\gamma}\big]}\leq G(x_{0},\Sigma_{0})
for every other admissible strategy.

Proof of the equality (a)
:
The SDE for the wealth process can be solved explicitly:

X_{T}^{\pi}=X_{0}^{\pi}\operatorname{exp}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}(r_{s}+\pi_{s}^{\top}\Sigma_{s}v-\frac{1}{2}\lVert\pi_{s}^{\top}\Sigma_{s}^{1/2}\rVert_{2}^{2})ds+\int_{0}^{T}\pi_{s}^{\top}\Sigma_{s}^{1/2}dW_{s}^{S}\Big);\quad X_{0}^{\pi}=x_{0}.

Introducing a new probability measure
{{\mathbb{Q}}}
with Radon-Nikodym density

{Z}_{0}:=\frac{d{{\mathbb{Q}}}}{d\mathbb{P}}|_{\mathcal{F}_{0}}=\operatorname{exp}\Big(\gamma\int_{0}^{T}(\pi_{s}^{*})^{\top}\Sigma_{s}^{1/2}dW_{s}^{S}-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\lVert(\pi_{s}^{*})^{\top}\Sigma_{s}^{1/2}\rVert_{2}^{2}ds\Big),

we obtain

\displaystyle x_{0}^{-\gamma}\EX_{x_{0},\Sigma_{0}}{(X_{T}^{\pi^{*}})^{\gamma}}{}

\displaystyle=\EX_{x_{0},\Sigma_{0}}^{{\mathbb{Q}}}\Big[\operatorname{exp}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\gamma r_{s}ds+\int_{0}^{T}\operatorname{Tr}\big[F_{s}\Sigma_{s}\big]ds\Big)\Big],

where the matrix-valued, deterministic process
F_{s}

Inserting the candidate for the optimal strategy
\pi_{t}^{*}=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}(v+2\psi(T-t)Q^{\top}\rho)
,

can be written in terms of
f(\psi)

\displaystyle F_{s}

\displaystyle=f(\psi)(T-s)-\psi(T-s)M-M^{\top}\psi(T-s)-2\psi(T-s)Q^{\top}Q\psi(t-s)

\displaystyle+\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}(2\psi(T-s)Q^{\top}\rho v^{\top}+4\psi(T-s)Q^{\top}\rho\rho^{\top}Q\psi(T-s)).

Let
L
be the resolvent of the first kind of the integral kernel
K
.
Then by the associativity of the convolution and applying the fundamental theorem of calculus we obtain

\displaystyle f(\psi)(T-s){}

\displaystyle=(\psi\ast L)^{\prime}(T-s).

Thus we can write
F_{s}
as

\displaystyle F_{s}{}

\displaystyle=(\psi\ast L)^{\prime}(T-s)-\psi(T-s)M-M^{\top}\psi(T-s)-2\psi(T-s)Q^{\top}Q\psi(t-s)

\displaystyle+\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}(2\psi(T-s)Q^{\top}\rho v^{\top}+4\psi(T-s)Q^{\top}\rho\rho^{\top}Q\psi(T-s)),

We are interested in the expression
\int_{0}^{T}\operatorname{Tr}\big[F_{s}\Sigma_{s}\big]ds
.
Under the probability measure
{{\mathbb{Q}}}
, the process

\tilde{W}_{t}^{\sigma}=W_{t}^{\sigma}-\gamma\Sigma_{t}^{1/2}\pi_{t}^{*}\rho^{\top}

is a

-dimensional brownian motion by Girsanov’s theorem.
The dynamics of the volatility process
\Sigma
under

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}K(t-s)(NN^{\top}+M\Sigma_{s}+\Sigma_{s}M^{\top}+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\Sigma_{s}v\rho^{\top}Q

(4.10)

\displaystyle+\frac{2\gamma}{1-\gamma}\Sigma_{s}\psi(T-s)Q^{\top}\rho\rho^{\top}Q+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}Q^{\top}\rho v^{\top}\Sigma_{s}+\frac{2\gamma}{1-\gamma}Q^{\top}\rho\rho^{\top}Q\psi(T-s)\Sigma_{s})ds

\Sigma
into the expression

Hence we obtain

\displaystyle x_{0}^{-\gamma}\EX_{x_{0},\Sigma_{0}}[{(X_{T}^{\pi^{*}})^{\gamma}}]=\EX_{x_{0},\Sigma_{0}}^{{\mathbb{Q}}}\Big[\operatorname{exp}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\gamma r_{s}ds+\int_{0}^{T}\operatorname{Tr}[F_{s}V_{s}]ds\Big)\Big]

Since

B.1
. Thus we get

\EX_{x_{0},\Sigma_{0}}\Big[{\frac{1}{\gamma}(X_{T}^{\pi^{*}})^{\gamma}}\Big]=\frac{x_{0}^{\gamma}}{\gamma}\operatorname{exp}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\gamma r_{s}+\operatorname{Tr}[f(\psi)(T-s)\Sigma_{0}+\psi(T-s)NN^{\top}]ds\Big).

Analogously to the first part we obtain

\displaystyle x_{0}^{-\gamma}\EX_{x_{0},\Sigma_{0}}{(X_{T}^{\pi})^{\gamma}}=\EX_{x_{0},\Sigma_{0}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{Q}}}}\Big[\operatorname{exp}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\gamma r_{s}ds+\int_{0}^{T}\operatorname{Tr}\big[\hat{F}_{s}\Sigma_{s}\big]ds\Big)\Big].

\displaystyle=F_{s}+\gamma v\hat{\pi}_{s}^{\top}+\gamma(\gamma-1)\pi_{s}^{*}\hat{\pi}_{s}^{\top}+\frac{\gamma(\gamma-1)}{2}\hat{\pi}_{s}\hat{\pi}_{s}^{\top},

6.
Existence and uniqueness results for the Volterra equations

In this section we provide existence and uniqueness results for the Riccati-Volterra equations (
4.6
) and the stochastic Volterra equation (
4.2
).

6.1.
Solution of the Volterra Wishart process

Previous research on stochastic Volterra equations has been carried out in the vector framework (cf.
[
5
]
,
[
4
]
).
For proving existence of a weak solution for the matrix-valued Volterra-Wishart equation (
4.2
), we use the vectorization operator in order to be able to resort to existing literature.

Theorem 6.1
.

Assume that
K
admits a resolvent of the first kind and that the components of
K
satisfy (
4.3
). Then the stochastic Volterra equation (
4.2
) has a unique in law
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
-valued continuous weak solution on the interval
[0,T_{\operatorname{pos}})
,
where
T_{\operatorname{pos}}:={\operatorname{sup}}\{t\geq 0:\tau\geq t\text{ almost sure}\}

\tau:=\operatorname{inf}\{t\geq 0:\Sigma_{t}\text{ is not positive definite}\}
.

Proof:

We cast the problem into the vector framework using the vectorization operator
\operatorname{vec}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\cdot d}
stacking the columns of a matrix on top of one another. This leads to the
\mathbb{R}^{d\cdot d}
-valued stochastic Volterra equation

\displaystyle\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma){}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{vec}(K\ast(NN^{\top}+M\Sigma+\Sigma M^{\top}+\Sigma^{1/2}dW^{\sigma}Q+Q^{\top}(dW^{\sigma})^{\top}\Sigma^{1/2}))

\displaystyle=(I\otimes K)\ast\operatorname{vec}(NN^{\top}+M\Sigma+\Sigma M^{\top}+\Sigma^{1/2}dW^{\sigma}Q+Q^{\top}(dW^{\sigma})^{\top}\Sigma^{1/2})

\displaystyle=(I\otimes K)\ast[\operatorname{vec}(NN^{\top})+(I\otimes M+M\otimes I)\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle+Q^{\top}\otimes\Sigma^{1/2}\operatorname{vec}(dW^{\sigma})+\Sigma^{1/2}\otimes Q^{\top}\operatorname{vec}((dW^{\sigma})^{\top})]

\displaystyle=(I\otimes K)\ast[b(\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma))dt+c(\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma))d\operatorname{vec}(W^{\sigma})]

where

b(\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma)):=\operatorname{vec}(NN^{\top})+(I\otimes M+M\otimes I)\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma)

and

c(\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma)):=Q^{\top}\otimes\Sigma^{1/2}+f(\Sigma^{1/2}\otimes Q^{\top}),

and
f:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
swaps entries such that
f(\Sigma^{1/2}\otimes Q^{\top})d\operatorname{vec}(W^{\sigma})=\Sigma^{1/2}\otimes Q^{\top}d\operatorname{vec}((W^{\sigma})^{\top})
. We now show that
b
and
c
fulfill a linear growth condition.

\displaystyle\left|b(\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma))\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\operatorname{vec}(NN^{\top})\right|+\lVert I\otimes M+M\otimes I\rVert_{F}\left|\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\operatorname{vec}(NN^{\top})\right|+2\sqrt{d}\lVert M\rVert_{F}\left|\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma)\right|\leq C_{1}(1+\left|\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma)\right|)

with
C_{1}=\operatorname{max}(\left|\operatorname{vec}(NN^{\top})\right|,2\sqrt{d}\lVert M\rVert_{F})
.

\displaystyle\left|c(\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma))\right|

\displaystyle\leq\lVert Q^{\top}\otimes\Sigma^{1/2}\rVert_{F}+\lVert f(\Sigma^{1/2}\otimes Q^{\top})\rVert_{F}=2\lVert Q^{\top}\rVert_{F}\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}\rVert_{F}

Using the fact that
\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}\rVert_{F}=\sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma^{1/2}\Sigma^{1/2})}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|c(\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma))\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\lVert Q^{\top}\rVert_{F}^{2}\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}\rVert_{F}^{2}=4\lVert Q^{\top}\rVert_{F}^{2}\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma^{1/2}\Sigma^{1/2})=4\lVert Q^{\top}\rVert_{F}^{2}\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle\leq 4\lVert Q^{\top}\rVert_{F}^{2}\sqrt{d}(1+\lVert\Sigma\rVert_{F})\leq 4\lVert Q^{\top}\rVert_{F}^{2}\sqrt{d}(1+\lVert\Sigma\rVert_{F})^{2}=C_{2}^{2}(1+\left|\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma)\right|)^{2}.

Here we have used the fact that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{ii}\leq\sqrt{d}\sqrt{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{ii}^{2}}\leq\sqrt{d}(1+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{ii}^{2})\leq\sqrt{d}(1+\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}\sigma_{ij}^{2})=\sqrt{d}(1+\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma^{2})).

Hence for
C_{2}=2d^{1/4}\lVert Q\rVert_{F}
, we get

\left|c(\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma))\right|\leq C_{2}(1+\left|\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma)\right|).

Thus
b
and
c
fulfill the linear growth condition
[
4
, Condition 3.1]
and hence we can apply
[
4
, Theorem 3.4]
to get the desired result. ∎

6.2.
Solution of the Riccati Volterra equation

We use the concept of non-continuable solutions described in
[
4
]
.
For an integral kernel
K_{i}\in L_{\operatorname{loc}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
and a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
consider the Volterra integral equation

\psi=K\ast f(\psi).

(6.1)

For
0<T_{\operatorname{max}}<\infty
, by a non-continuable solution of (
6.1
) we denote a pair
(\psi,T_{\operatorname{max}})
with
\lVert\psi\rVert_{L_{2}[0,T_{\operatorname{max}})}<\infty
, such that
\psi
satisfies (
6.1
) on
[0,T_{\operatorname{max}})
and
\lVert\psi\rVert_{L_{2}[0,T_{\operatorname{max}}]}=\infty
. A non-continuable solution
(\psi,T_{\operatorname{max}})
is unique if for any
T>0
and
\tilde{\psi}
with
\lVert\tilde{\psi}\rVert_{L_{2}[0,T]}<\infty

and

Theorem 6.2
.

For each of the Riccati-Volterra equations (
3.8
) and (
3.11
) there exists a unique non-continuable continuous solution
(\psi,T_{\operatorname{max}})
.

Proof:

The Riccati-Volterra equations (
3.8
) and (
3.11
) are both of the form

\chi=F(\chi)\ast K

where
F
is given by

F(\chi)=a+B\chi+(c_{1}\chi_{1}^{2},\dots,c_{d}\chi_{d}^{2})^{\top}

with
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Thus for
x
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we get

\displaystyle\left|F(x)-F(y)\right|{}

\displaystyle=\left|Bx+(c_{1}x_{1}^{2},\dots,c_{d}x_{d}^{2})^{\top}-By-(c_{1}y_{1}^{2},\dots,c_{d}y_{d}^{2})^{\top}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|B(x-y)\right|+\left|(c_{1}(x_{1}^{2}-y_{1}^{2}),\dots,c_{d}(x_{d}^{2}-y_{d}^{2}))^{\top}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|x-y\right|\lVert B\rVert_{F}+\max_{1\leq i\leq d}(\left|c_{i}\right|)\left|((x_{1}+y_{1})(x_{1}-y_{1}),\dots,(x_{d}+y_{d})(x_{d}-y_{d}))^{\top}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|x-y\right|\lVert B\rVert_{F}+\max_{1\leq i\leq d}(\left|c_{i}\right|)\left|x+y\right|\cdot\left|x-y\right|

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\left|x-y\right|+C_{2}\left|x-y\right|(\left|x\right|+\left|y\right|)

with positive constants
C_{1}=\lVert B\rVert_{F}
and
C_{2}=\max_{1\leq i\leq d}(\left|c_{i}\right|)
. Here we have used the triangle inequality, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the fact that for
\alpha
,
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}}
we get
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{j}
. Now existence and uniqueness of a non-continuable solution
(\chi,T_{\operatorname{max}})
follows directly from
[
4
, Theorem B1]
. ∎

In case that the matrix

A vectorization argument allows us to prove existence of a local solution for the matrix Riccati-Volterra equation (

).

Theorem 6.4
.

The matrix Riccati-Volterra equation (
4.6
) has a unique non-continuable continuous solution
(\psi,T_{\text{max}})
.

Proof:

We cast the problem into the vector framework using the vectorization operator
\operatorname{vec}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\cdot d}
stacking the columns of a matrix on top of one another. This leads to the
\mathbb{R}^{d\cdot d}
-valued Riccati-Volterra equation

\displaystyle\operatorname{vec}(\psi){}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{vec}(K\ast[\psi\tilde{M}+\tilde{M}^{\top}\psi+2\psi\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}\psi+\tilde{\Gamma}])

\displaystyle=(I\otimes K)\ast\operatorname{vec}(\psi\tilde{M}+\tilde{M}^{\top}\psi+2\psi\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}\psi+\tilde{\Gamma})=:(I\otimes K)\ast p(\operatorname{vec}(\psi)),

where
\otimes:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\cdot d\times d\cdot d}
denotes the Kronecker product. We now show that
p
fulfills the growth condition

\left|p(\operatorname{vec}(X))-p(\operatorname{vec}(Y))\right|\leq C_{1}\left|\operatorname{vec}(X)-\operatorname{vec}(Y)\right|+C_{2}\left|\operatorname{vec}(X)-\operatorname{vec}(Y)\right|(\left|\operatorname{vec}(X)\right|+\left|\operatorname{vec}(Y)\right|)

with positive constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
.

\displaystyle\left|p(\operatorname{vec}(X))-p(\operatorname{vec}(Y))\right|{}

\displaystyle=\left|\operatorname{vec}(X\tilde{M}+\tilde{M}^{\top}X+2X\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}X+\tilde{\Gamma})-\operatorname{vec}(Y\tilde{M}+\tilde{M}^{\top}Y+2Y\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}Y+\tilde{\Gamma})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\operatorname{vec}((X-Y)\tilde{M})+\operatorname{vec}(\tilde{M}^{\top}(X-Y))+\operatorname{vec}(2X\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}X-2Y\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}Y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\operatorname{vec}((X-Y)\tilde{M})\right|+\left|\operatorname{vec}(\tilde{M}^{\top}(X-Y))\right|+2\left|\operatorname{vec}(X\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}X-Y\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}Y)\right|

For the first and the second term of this sum, we have

\left|\operatorname{vec}((X-Y)\tilde{M})\right|=\left|(\tilde{M}^{\top}\otimes I)\operatorname{vec}(X-Y)\right|\leq\lVert\tilde{M}^{\top}\otimes I\rVert_{F}\left|\operatorname{vec}(X-Y)\right|

and

\left|\operatorname{vec}(\tilde{M}^{\top}(X-Y))\right|=\left|(I\otimes\tilde{M}^{\top})\operatorname{vec}(X-Y)\right|\leq\lVert I\otimes\tilde{M}^{\top}\rVert_{F}\left|\operatorname{vec}(X-Y)\right|.

Since
\lVert\tilde{M}^{\top}\otimes I\rVert_{F}=\lVert I\otimes\tilde{M}^{\top}\rVert_{F}=\sqrt{d}\lVert\tilde{M}\rVert_{F}
, we choose
C_{1}=2\sqrt{d}\lVert\tilde{M}\rVert_{F}
.
For the third term, it holds that

\left|\operatorname{vec}(X\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}X-Y\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}Y)\right|=\lVert X\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}X-Y\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}Y\rVert_{F}.

One of the following statements must be true:

\lVert X\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}X-Y\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}Y\rVert_{F}\leq\lVert X\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}X+X\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}Y-Y\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}X-Y\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}Y\rVert_{F}

or

\lVert X\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}X-Y\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}Y\rVert_{F}\leq\lVert X\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}X-X\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}Y+Y\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}X-Y\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}Y\rVert_{F}.

Without loss of generality we only treat the first case. Thus we obtain

\lVert X\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}X-Y\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}Y\rVert_{F}\leq\lVert(X-Y)\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}(X+Y)\rVert_{F}.

The matrix
\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}(\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q})^{\top}
is symmetric and thus its spectral decomposition can be written as
O\Lambda O^{\top}
for an orthogonal matrix
O
. Let
\lambda_{\text{max}}
be the largest eigenvalue of
\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}(\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q})^{\top}
.
Since
\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}(\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q})^{\top}
has only non-negative eigenvalues,
\lambda_{\text{max}}I-\Lambda
is positive definite and so is
(X-Y)(X+Y)O(\lambda_{\text{max}}I-\Lambda)O^{\top}(X+Y)^{\top}(X-Y)^{\top}
. Since the trace of a matrix is the sum of its eigenvalues we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[(X-Y)(X+Y)O(\lambda_{\text{max}}I-\Lambda)O^{\top}(X+Y)^{\top}(X-Y)^{\top}]\geq 0

and thus

\lambda_{\text{max}}\operatorname{Tr}[(X-Y)(X+Y)(X+Y)^{\top}(X-Y)^{\top}]\geq\operatorname{Tr}[(X-Y)(X+Y)O\Lambda O^{\top}(X+Y)^{\top}(X-Y)^{\top}]

Using the above facts, we obtain

\displaystyle\lVert(X-Y)\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}(X+Y)\rVert_{F}^{2}{}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[(X-Y)\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}(X+Y)(X+Y)^{\top}(\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q})^{\top}(X-Y)^{\top}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[(X-Y)(X+Y)\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}(\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q})^{\top}(X+Y)^{\top}(X-Y)^{\top}]

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{\text{max}}\operatorname{Tr}[(X-Y)(X+Y)(X+Y)^{\top}(X-Y)^{\top}]

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\text{max}}\lVert(X-Y)(X+Y)\rVert_{F}^{2}.

Hence we have

\displaystyle\left|\operatorname{vec}(X\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}X-Y\tilde{Q}^{\top}\tilde{Q}Y)\right|{}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\lambda_{\text{max}}}\left|\operatorname{vec}((X-Y)(X+Y))\right|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\lambda_{\text{max}}}\left|(I\otimes(X-Y)\operatorname{vec}(X+Y))\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\lambda_{\text{max}}}\lVert I\otimes(X-Y)\rVert_{F}\left|\operatorname{vec}(X+Y)\right|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\lambda_{\text{max}}}\sqrt{d}\lVert(X-Y)\rVert_{F}\left|\operatorname{vec}(X+Y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\lambda_{\text{max}}}\sqrt{d}\left|\operatorname{vec}(X)-\operatorname{vec}(Y)\right|(\left|\operatorname{vec}(X)\right|+\left|\operatorname{vec}(Y)\right|)

leading to
C_{2}=2\sqrt{d}\sqrt{\lambda_{\text{max}}}
.
Now an application of
[
4
, Theorem B2]
yields the desired result. ∎

Lemma B.1
.

Let us denote

M_{t}:=\operatorname{exp}\Big(\int_{0}^{t}\operatorname{Tr}(A_{s}\Sigma_{s}^{1/2}dW_{s}^{\sigma})-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\lVert A_{s}\Sigma_{s}^{1/2}\rVert^{2}ds\Big)

where
(A_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is a deterministic process with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and bounded by
A^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Then
(M_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is a martingale.

Proof:

The process
M_{t}
is a stochastic exponential of the form
M_{t}=\mathcal{E}(\int_{0}^{t}\operatorname{Tr}(A_{s}\Sigma_{s}^{1/2}dW_{s}^{\sigma}))
.
Since
M
is a non-negative local martingale,
M
is a supermartingale by Fatou’s lemma. Thus, in order to show that
M
is a true martingale, it suffices to show that
\EX[M_{T}]=1
for any
T>0
.
For a fixed
T>0
we define the stopping times

{\tau_{n}:=\inf\{{t\geq 0}:\exists 1\leq i,j\leq d:\left|\Sigma_{t}^{ij}\right|>n\}.}

The process
M^{\tau_{n}}=M_{\tau_{n}\wedge.}
is a uniformly integrable martingale for each
n
, since the Novikov condition is fulfilled due to the boundedness of
A
. Thus we get

1=M_{0}^{\tau_{n}}=\EX_{\mathbb{P}}[M_{T}^{\tau_{n}}]=\EX_{\mathbb{P}}[M_{T}\bm{1}_{\tau_{n}\geq T}]+\EX_{\mathbb{P}}[{M_{\tau_{n}}}\bm{1}_{\tau_{n}<T}].

By the theorem of dominated convergence,
\EX_{\mathbb{P}}[M_{T}\bm{1}_{\tau_{n}\geq T}]\rightarrow\EX_{\mathbb{P}}[M_{T}]
and thus, in order to show that
\EX_{\mathcal{P}}[M_{T}]=1
, it is sufficient to prove that

\EX_{\mathbb{P}}[{M_{\tau_{n}}}\bm{1}_{\tau_{n}<T}]\rightarrow 0,\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty.

Since
M^{\tau_{n}}
is a martingale, we can define probability measures
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
with Radon-Nikodym densities

\frac{d\mathbb{Q}^{n}}{d\mathbb{P}}=M_{\tau_{n}}.

By Girsanov’s theorem the process
W^{\sigma,n}
defined by

dW_{t}^{\sigma,n}=dW_{t}^{\sigma}+\bm{1}_{t\leq\tau_{n}}\Sigma_{t}^{1/2}A_{t}^{\top}ds

is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motions under the measure
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
. Furthermore, under
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
we have
with

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t}^{n}{}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}K(t-s)(NN^{\top}+M\Sigma_{s}+\Sigma_{s}M^{\top}+\bm{1}_{s\leq\tau_{n}}\Sigma_{s}A_{s}^{\top}Q+\bm{1}_{s\leq\tau_{n}}Q^{\top}A_{s}\Sigma_{s})ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}K(t-s)(\Sigma_{s}^{1/2}dW_{s}^{\sigma,n}Q+Q^{\top}(dW_{s}^{\sigma,n})^{\top}\Sigma_{s}^{1/2}).

Using the vectorization operator from Section
6
, the above equation can be written as

\displaystyle\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma^{n}){}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma_{0})+(I\otimes K)\ast[\operatorname{vec}(NN^{\top})

\displaystyle+(I\otimes(M+\bm{1}_{\leq\tau_{n}}Q^{\top}A)+(M+\bm{1}_{\leq\tau_{n}}Q^{\top}A)\otimes I)\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma)]

\displaystyle+(I\otimes K)\ast[Q^{\top}\otimes\Sigma^{1/2}\operatorname{vec}(dW^{\sigma,n})+\Sigma^{1/2}\otimes Q\operatorname{vec}((dW^{\sigma,n})^{\top})]

\displaystyle=(I\otimes K)\ast[b(\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma))dt+c(\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma))d\operatorname{vec}(W^{\sigma,n})].

where

b(\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma)):=\operatorname{vec}(NN^{\top})+(I\otimes(M+\bm{1}_{\leq\tau_{n}}Q^{\top}A)+(M+\bm{1}_{\leq\tau_{n}}Q^{\top}A)\otimes I)\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma)

and

c(\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma)):=Q^{\top}\otimes\Sigma^{1/2}+f(\Sigma^{1/2}\otimes Q^{\top})

and
f:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
swaps entries such that
f(\Sigma^{1/2}\otimes Q^{\top})d\operatorname{vec}(W^{\sigma})=\Sigma^{1/2}\otimes Q^{\top}d\operatorname{vec}((W^{\sigma})^{\top})
.
Using similar arguments as in the proof of Theorem
6.1
, one can show that the drift and the diffusion term of the above equation fulfill the linear growth condition
[
4
, condition (3.1)]
, i.e. we have

\left|b(\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma))\right|\vee\left|c(\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma))\right|\leq c_{LG}(1+\left|\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma\right|)).

Note that the argument for the drift only works if the matrix
A_{t}
is bounded. Choose
p>2
sufficiently large that
\kappa_{i}/2-1/p>0
for
\kappa_{i}
defined in (
4.3
). An application of
[
4
, Lemma 3.1]
yields the moment bound

\sup_{t\leq T}\EX[\left|\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma_{t})\right|^{p}]\leq c

for some constant
c
independent of
n
. The
0
-Hölder seminorm of a function
f
is defined as

\left|f\right|_{C^{0,0}(0,T)}=\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}\left|f(t)-f(s)\right|.

Claim:
For some constant
C
independent of
n
the following inequality holds:

\sum\limits_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\left|\Sigma^{ij}\right|_{C^{0,0}}^{p}\leq C\left|\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma)\right|_{C^{0,0}}^{p}.

Proof of Claim:
We have to show that

\sum\limits_{1\leq i,j\leq d}(\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}\left|\Sigma_{t}^{ij}-\Sigma_{s}^{ij}\right|)^{p}\leq C(\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}(\sum\limits_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\left|\Sigma_{t}^{ij}-\Sigma_{s}^{ij}\right|^{2})^{1/2})^{p}

or equivalently

(\sum\limits_{1\leq i,j\leq d}(\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}\left|\Sigma_{t}^{ij}-\Sigma_{s}^{ij}\right|)^{p})^{1/p}\leq C\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}(\sum\limits_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\left|\Sigma_{t}^{ij}-\Sigma_{s}^{ij}\right|^{2})^{1/2}.

Since for a vector
x
we have
\lVert x\rVert_{l^{q}}\leq\lVert x\rVert_{l^{p}}
for
1\leq p<q\leq\infty
, it holds that the left-hand side is bounded by

(\sum\limits_{1\leq i,j\leq d}(\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}\left|\Sigma_{t}^{ij}-\Sigma_{s}^{ij}\right|)^{p})^{1/p}\leq(\sum\limits_{1\leq i,j\leq d}(\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}\left|\Sigma_{t}^{ij}-\Sigma_{s}^{ij}\right|)^{2})^{1/2}.

Using the fact that the square of the supremum of a set of non-negative numbers equals the supremum of the squares, it remains to show that

\sum\limits_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}\left|\Sigma_{t}^{ij}-\Sigma_{s}^{ij}\right|^{2}\leq C\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}\sum\limits_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\left|\Sigma_{t}^{ij}-\Sigma_{s}^{ij}\right|^{2}.

Clearly

\sum\limits_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}\left|\Sigma_{t}^{ij}-\Sigma_{s}^{ij}\right|^{2}\leq d^{2}\max_{1\leq i,j\leq d}(\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}\left|\Sigma_{t}^{ij}-\Sigma_{s}^{ij}\right|^{2}).

and since

\forall 1\leq i,j\leq d,\,\forall 0\leq s<t\leq T:\left|\Sigma_{t}^{ij}-\Sigma_{s}^{ij}\right|^{2}\leq\sum\limits_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\left|\Sigma_{t}^{ij}-\Sigma_{s}^{ij}\right|^{2}

we get

\max_{1\leq i,j\leq d}(\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}\left|\Sigma_{t}^{ij}-\Sigma_{s}^{ij}\right|^{2})\leq\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}\sum\limits_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\left|\Sigma_{t}^{ij}-\Sigma_{s}^{ij}\right|^{2}.

This completes the proof of the claim. ∎

We now show that
\EX_{\mathbb{P}}[M_{T}\bm{1}_{\tau_{n}<T}]\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
:

\displaystyle\EX_{\mathbb{P}}[{M_{\tau_{n}}}\bm{1}_{\tau_{n}<T}]{}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{Q}}^{n}(\tau_{n}<T)={\mathbb{Q}}^{n}(\exists 1\leq i,j\leq d:\sup_{t\leq T}\left|\Sigma_{t}^{ij}\right|>n)

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{1\leq i,j\leq d}{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}(\sup_{t\leq T}\left|\Sigma_{t}^{ij}\right|>n)\leq\sum\limits_{1\leq i,j\leq d}{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}(\left|\Sigma_{0}^{ij}\right|+\left|\Sigma^{ij}\right|_{C^{0,0}(0,T)}>n)

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{1\leq i,j\leq d}(\frac{1}{n-\left|\Sigma_{0}^{ij}\right|})^{p}\EX_{{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}}[\left|\Sigma^{ij}\right|_{C^{0,0}(0,T)}^{p}]\text{ (Markov inequality)}

\displaystyle\leq(\frac{1}{n-\left|\Sigma_{0}^{\operatorname{max}}\right|})^{p}\EX_{{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}}[\sum\limits_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\left|\Sigma^{ij}\right|_{C^{0,0}(0,T)}^{p}]

\displaystyle\leq C(\frac{1}{n-\left|\Sigma_{0}^{\operatorname{max}}\right|})^{p}\EX_{{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}}[\left|\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma)\right|_{C^{0,0}(0,T)}^{p}]\text{ (Claim)}

\displaystyle\leq C(\frac{1}{n-\left|\Sigma_{0}^{\operatorname{max}}\right|})^{p}\sup_{t\leq T}\EX_{{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}}[\left|b(\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma_{t}))\right|^{p}+\left|c(\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma_{t}))\right|^{p}]\text{ (\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{AJ19}{}{}, Lemma 2.4]})}

\displaystyle\leq C(\frac{1}{n-\left|\Sigma_{0}^{\operatorname{max}}\right|})^{p}\sup_{t\leq T}\EX_{{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}}[(1+\left|\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma_{t})\right|)^{p}]\text{ (Growth condition)}

\displaystyle\leq C(\frac{1}{n-\left|\Sigma_{0}^{\operatorname{max}}\right|})^{p}\sup_{t\leq T}\EX_{{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}}[1+\left|\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma_{t})\right|^{p}]

\displaystyle\leq C(\frac{1}{n-\left|\Sigma_{0}^{\operatorname{max}}\right|})^{p}\text{ (\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{AJ19}{}{}, Lemma 3.1]})}.

Since the constant
C
is independent of
n
we finally obtain

\EX_{\mathbb{P}}[{M_{\tau_{n}}}\bm{1}_{\tau_{n}<T}]\leq C(\frac{1}{n-\left|\Sigma_{0}^{\operatorname{max}}\right|})^{p}\xrightarrow{n\rightarrow\infty}0,

which completes the proof. ∎

. Then the local martingale

Z_{t}=\mathcal{E}(\int_{0}^{t}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}g_{1i}(s)\sqrt{V_{s}^{i}}dW_{1s}^{i}+\int_{0}^{t}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}g_{2i}(s)\sqrt{V_{s}^{i}}dW_{2s}^{i})

is a true martingale.

Proof:

Set
U=\int_{0}^{.}V_{s}ds
. Then by the stochastic Fubini theorem we get

U_{t}^{i}=\int_{0}^{t}v_{0}^{i}(s)ds+\int_{0}^{t}K_{i}(t-s)Z_{s}^{i}ds

with

Z_{t}^{i}=\int_{0}^{t}(DV_{s})_{i}ds+\int_{0}^{t}\nu_{i}\sqrt{V_{s}^{i}}dB_{s}^{i}.

Since
M
is a non-negative local martingale,
M
is a supermartingale by Fatou’s lemma. Thus, in order to show that
M
is a true martingale, it suffices to show that
\EX[M_{T}]=1
for any
T>0
.
For a fixed
T>0
we define the stopping times

\tau_{n}:=\inf\{{t\geq 0}:\exists 1\leq i\leq d:\int_{0}^{t}V_{s}^{i}ds>n\}.

The stopped process
M^{\tau_{n}}=M_{\tau_{n}\wedge.}
is a uniformly integrable martingale for each
n
, since the Novikov condition is fulfilled due to the boundedness of
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
. Thus we get

1=M_{0}^{\tau_{n}}=\EX_{\mathbb{P}}[M_{T}^{\tau_{n}}]=\EX_{\mathbb{P}}[M_{T}\bm{1}_{\tau_{n}\geq T}]+\EX_{\mathbb{P}}[M_{T}\bm{1}_{\tau_{n}<T}].

By the theorem of dominated convergence,
\EX_{\mathbb{P}}[M_{T}\bm{1}_{\tau_{n}\geq T}]\rightarrow\EX_{\mathbb{P}}[M_{T}]
and thus, in order to show that
\EX_{\mathcal{P}}[M_{T}]=1
, it is sufficient to prove that

\EX_{\mathbb{P}}[M_{T}\bm{1}_{\tau_{n}<T}]\rightarrow 0,\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty.

(B.1)

Since
M^{\tau_{n}}
is a martingale, we can define probability measures
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
with Radon-Nikodym densities

\frac{d\mathbb{Q}^{n}}{d\mathbb{P}}=M_{\tau_{n}}.

By Girsanov’s theorem the processes
W_{1}^{n}
and
W_{2}^{n}
defined by

W_{1}^{n,i}=W_{1}^{i}+\int_{0}^{.}\bm{1}_{s\leq\tau_{n}}g_{1,i}(s)\sqrt{V_{s}^{i}}ds,\,1\leq i\leq d,

W_{2}^{n,i}=W_{2}^{i}+\int_{0}^{.}\bm{1}_{s\leq\tau_{n}}g_{2,i}(s)\sqrt{V_{s}^{i}}ds,\,1\leq i\leq d,

are
d
-dimensional Brownian motions under the measure
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
. Furthermore, under
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
we have

U_{t}^{i}=\int_{0}^{t}v_{0}^{i}(s)ds+\int_{0}^{t}K_{i}(t-s)Z_{s}^{n,i}ds

\displaystyle Z_{t}^{n,i}{}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}((DV_{s})_{i}-\bm{1}_{s\leq\tau_{n}}\rho_{i}\nu_{i}g_{1,i}(s)V_{s}^{i}-\bm{1}_{s\leq\tau_{n}})\sqrt{1-\rho_{i}^{2}}\nu_{i}g_{2,i}(s)V_{s}^{i})ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\nu_{i}\sqrt{V_{s}^{i}}(\rho_{i}dW_{1s}^{n,i}+\sqrt{1-\rho_{i}^{2}}dW_{2s}^{n,i})

and under
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
, the drift of
Z^{n}
satisfies a linear growth condition in
U
for some constant
\kappa_{L}
independent of
n
. Therefore an application of the generalized Grönwall inequality (cf.
[
1
, Lemma 3.1]
) yields the moment bound

\EX_{\mathbb{Q}^{n}}[\left|U_{T}\right|^{2}]\leq\eta(\kappa_{L},T,K,v_{0}),

where
\eta(\kappa_{L},T,K,v_{0})
does not depend on
n
. An application of Chebyshev’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\EX_{\mathbb{P}}[M_{T}\bm{1}_{\tau_{n}<T}]{}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{Q}}^{n}(\tau_{n}<T)

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}(U_{T}^{i}>n)

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\EX_{{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}}[\left|U_{T}^{i}\right|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\EX_{{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}}[\left|U_{T}\right|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\eta(\kappa_{L},T,K,v_{0})\xrightarrow{n\rightarrow\infty}0.

This completes the proof. ∎

Appendix C
Dynamics of the process
M

We derive the dynamics of the process
M
appearing in the martingale distortion approach using Itô’s formula (cf.
[
22
, Theorem 3.2]
).

Lemma C.1
.

The process
M_{t}
defined in (
3.7
) has dynamics

\displaystyle dM_{t}{}

\displaystyle=M_{t}[-\gamma r_{t}-\frac{\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}\theta\Theta V_{t}]dt

\displaystyle+M_{t}c\psi(T-t)N\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}{(V_{t})}}P_{1}d{W}_{1t}+M_{t}c\psi(T-t)N\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}{(V_{t})}}P_{2}d{W}_{2t}

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}M_{t}c\psi(T-t)NP_{1}\operatorname{diag}(V_{t})\theta^{\top}-\frac{\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}M_{t}\lVert c\psi(T-t)N\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}{(V_{t})}}P_{1}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Proof:

Let
Z_{t}=\int_{t}^{T}[\gamma r_{s}+\frac{\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}\theta\Theta\xi_{t}(s)+\frac{c}{2}A(\psi)(T-s)\xi_{t}(s)]ds
. Then
M_{t}=e^{Z_{t}}
. Applying Itô’s lemma to
\xi_{t}(s)
yields

d\xi_{t}(s)=R_{\Lambda}(s-t)\Lambda^{-1}N\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}(V_{t})}d\tilde{B}_{t}

by
[
4
, Lemma 4.2]
. Define
A(\psi):=\psi N^{2}\Psi
.
Then

\displaystyle dZ_{t}{}

\displaystyle=[-\gamma r_{t}-\frac{\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}\theta\Theta V_{t}-\frac{c}{2}A(\psi)(T-t)V_{t}]dt

\displaystyle+\int_{t}^{T}\frac{\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}\theta\Theta R_{\Lambda}(s-t)\Lambda^{-1}N\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}{(V_{t})}}d\tilde{B}_{t}ds

\displaystyle+\int_{t}^{T}\frac{c}{2}A(\psi)(T-s)R_{\Lambda}(s-t)\Lambda^{-1}N\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}{(V_{t})}}d\tilde{B}_{t}ds

\displaystyle=[-\gamma r_{t}-\frac{\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}\theta\Theta V_{t}-\frac{c}{2}A(\psi)(T-t)V_{t}]dt

\displaystyle+\int_{t}^{T}\frac{\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}\theta\Theta R_{\Lambda}(s-t)\Lambda^{-1}Nds\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}{(V_{t})}}d\tilde{B}_{t}

\displaystyle+\int_{t}^{T}\frac{c}{2}A(\psi)(T-s)R_{\Lambda}(s-t)\Lambda^{-1}Nds\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}{(V_{t})}}d\tilde{B}_{t}

\displaystyle=[-\gamma r_{t}-\frac{\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}\theta\Theta V_{t}-\frac{c}{2}A(\psi)(T-t)V_{t}]dt

\displaystyle+\int_{t}^{T}[\frac{c}{2}A(\psi)(T-s)+\frac{\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}\theta\Theta]R_{\Lambda}(s-t)\Lambda^{-1}dsN\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}{(V_{t})}}d\tilde{B}_{t}.

Here, for the second equality, we used the stochastic Fubini theorem from
[
33
]
.
Next, we show that

\int_{t}^{T}[\frac{c}{2}A(\psi)(T-s)+\frac{\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}\theta\Theta]R_{\Lambda}(s-t)\Lambda^{-1}ds=c\psi(T-t).

We have

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}[\frac{c}{2}A(\psi)(T-s)+\frac{\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}\theta\Theta]R_{\Lambda}(s-t)\Lambda^{-1}ds-c\psi(T-t)

\displaystyle=[\frac{c}{2}A(\psi)+\frac{\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}\theta\Theta]\ast(R_{\Lambda}\Lambda^{-1})(T-t)

\displaystyle-[\frac{c}{2}A(\psi)-\psi\Lambda+\frac{\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}\theta\Theta]\ast(cK)(T-t)

\displaystyle=[\frac{c}{2}A(\psi)+\frac{\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}\theta\Theta]\ast(R_{\Lambda}\Lambda^{-1}-K)(T-t)+c(\psi\Lambda)\ast K(T-t)

\displaystyle=[\frac{c}{2}A(\psi)-c\psi\Lambda+c\psi\Lambda+\frac{\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}\theta\Theta]\ast(R_{\Lambda}\Lambda^{-1}-K)(T-t)+c(\psi\Lambda)\ast K(T-t)

\displaystyle=[\frac{c}{2}A(\psi)-c\psi\Lambda+\frac{\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}\theta\Theta]\ast((-K\Lambda\ast R_{\Lambda})\Lambda^{-1})

\displaystyle+c(\psi\Lambda)\ast(-R_{\Lambda}\ast K)(T-t)+c(\psi\Lambda)\ast K(T-t)

\displaystyle=(-c(\psi\Lambda)\ast R_{\Lambda}\Lambda^{-1})(T-t)-c(\psi\Lambda)\ast(R_{\Lambda}\ast K)(T-t)+c(\psi\Lambda)\ast K(T-t)

\displaystyle=c(\psi\Lambda)\ast[K-R_{\Lambda}\ast K-R_{\Lambda}\Lambda^{-1}](T-t)=0.

Here the last equality holds, since

\displaystyle K-R_{\Lambda}\ast K{}

\displaystyle=(K-R_{\Lambda}\ast K)\Lambda\Lambda^{-1}=(K\Lambda-(R_{\Lambda}\ast K)\Lambda)\Lambda^{-1}

\displaystyle=(K\Lambda-R_{\Lambda}\ast(K\Lambda))\Lambda^{-1}=(K\Lambda-(K\Lambda-R_{\Lambda}))\Lambda^{-1}=R_{\Lambda}\Lambda^{-1}.

Thus we get

\displaystyle dZ_{t}{}

\displaystyle=[-\gamma r_{t}-\frac{\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}\theta\Theta V_{t}-\frac{c}{2}A(\psi)(T-t)V_{t}]dt+c\psi(T-t)N\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}{(V_{t})}}d\tilde{B}_{t}

\displaystyle=[-\gamma r_{t}-\frac{\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}\theta\Theta V_{t}-\frac{c}{2}A(\psi)(T-t)V_{t}]dt

\displaystyle+c\psi(T-t)N\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}{(V_{t})}}P_{1}d\tilde{W}_{1t}+c\psi(T-t)N\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}{(V_{t})}}P_{2}d{W}_{2t}

where
P_{1}=\operatorname{diag}{(\rho)}
and
P_{2}=\operatorname{diag}{(\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}})}
.

Since
M_{t}=e^{Z_{t}}
, by Itô’s formula we obtain
dM_{t}=M_{t}dZ_{t}+\frac{1}{2}M_{t}d\langle Z_{t},Z_{t}\rangle
, i.e

\displaystyle dM_{t}{}

\displaystyle=M_{t}[-\gamma r_{t}-\frac{\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}\theta\Theta V_{t}-\frac{c}{2}A(\psi)(T-t)V_{t}]dt

\displaystyle+M_{t}c\psi(T-t)N\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}{(V_{t})}}P_{1}d\tilde{W}_{1t}+M_{t}c\psi(T-t)N\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}{(V_{t})}}P_{2}d{W}_{2t}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}M_{t}\lVert c\psi(T-t)N\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}{(V_{t})}}P_{1}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}M_{t}\lVert c\psi(T-t)N\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}{(V_{t})}}P_{2}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=M_{t}[-\gamma r_{t}-\frac{\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}\theta\Theta V_{t}]dt

\displaystyle+M_{t}c\psi(T-t)N\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}{(V_{t})}}P_{1}d{W}_{1t}+M_{t}c\psi(T-t)N\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}{(V_{t})}}P_{2}d{W}_{2t}

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}M_{t}c\psi(T-t)NP_{1}\operatorname{diag}(V_{t})\theta^{\top}-\frac{\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}M_{t}\lVert c\psi(T-t)N\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}{(V_{t})}}P_{1}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

The last equality holds, since in the degenerate correlation case we have the following identity:

\displaystyle-M_{t}\frac{c}{2}A(\psi)(T-t)V_{t}dt+\frac{1}{2}M_{t}\lVert c\psi(T-t)N\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}{(V_{t})}}P_{2}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{c}{2}M_{t}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\psi_{i}^{2}(T-t)\nu_{i}^{2}V_{i}dt+\frac{1}{2}M_{t}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}c^{2}\psi_{i}^{2}(T-t)\nu_{i}^{2}V_{i}(1-\rho^{2})dt

\displaystyle=(-\frac{1}{c}+(1-\rho^{2}))\frac{1}{2}M_{t}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}c^{2}\psi_{i}^{2}(T-t)\nu_{i}^{2}V_{i}dt

\displaystyle=-\frac{\rho^{2}}{1-\gamma}\frac{1}{2}M_{t}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}c^{2}\psi_{i}^{2}(T-t)\nu_{i}^{2}V_{i}dt=-\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\frac{1}{2}M_{t}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}c^{2}\psi_{i}^{2}(T-t)\nu_{i}^{2}V_{i}\rho^{2}dt

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\frac{1}{2}M_{t}\lVert c\psi(T-t)N\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}{(V_{t})}}P_{1}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

∎
