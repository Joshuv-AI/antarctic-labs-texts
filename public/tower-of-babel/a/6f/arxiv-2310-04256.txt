Wardrop equilibrium and Braess’s paradox for varying demand
Jasper Verbree, Ashish Cherukuri

Abstract
This work explores the relationship between the set of Wardrop equilibria (WE) of a routing game, the total demand of that game, and the occurrence of Braess’s paradox (BP). The BP formalizes the counter-intuitive fact that for some networks, removing a path from the network decreases congestion at WE. For a single origin-destination routing games with affine cost functions, the first part of this work provides tools for analyzing the evolution of the WE as the demand varies. It characterizes the piece-wise affine nature of this dependence by showing that the set of directions in which the WE can vary in each piece is the solution of a variational inequality problem. In the process we establish various properties of changes in the set of used and minimal-cost paths as demand varies. As a consequence of these characterizations, we derive a procedure to obtain the WE for all demands above a certain threshold. The second part of the paper deals with detecting the presence of BP in a network. We supply a number of sufficient conditions that reveal the presence of BP and that are computationally tractable. We also discuss a different perspective on BP, where we establish that a path causing BP at a particular demand must be strictly beneficial to the network at a lower demand. Several examples throughout this work illustrate and elaborate our findings.



1
Introduction

With the ever increasing amount of traffic that finds its way to the road, the challenges of designing efficient traffic networks becomes more and more prevalent. Unfortunately, when drivers make routing choices in a selfish manner, that is, when traffic routes according to Wardrop equilibrium (WE)
[
1
]
, road networks are susceptible to innefficiencies which are hard to predict in advance, and hard to detect when they occur. For instance, in some cases travel cost of all drivers could be decreased by removing some roads in the network; a phenomenon known as Braess’s paradox (BP). Constructing a road that subsequently increases travel cost of all drivers using a network is of course the epitome of innefficiency. However, detecting whether or not a traffic network is or will be susceptible to BP, even when using a simple model in which travel costs on roads are affine functions of the traffic on that road, is in general NP-hard
[
2
]
.

In addition to being computationally expensive to detect, BP is also known to be highly dependent on the amount of traffic, or demand, that traverses a network. Though the removal of a road can decrease travel cost for one level of demand, it can increase travel cost at other levels of demand. Removal of a road responsible for BP therefore requires careful consideration of the effect of that removal for the whole range of demands which the network can experience.

Motivated by the above listed observations, we study the relation between these two challenges. To be specific, we study non-atomic, single origin-destination routing games with affine cost functions on the edges of the network. For these type of games we analyze the effects that changes in the demand have on the WE of these games, and then use the obtained observations to gain insight in how the effects of BP change as the demand varies. On the basis of these insights we can give various easy-to-check sufficient conditions revealing the presence of BP.

Literature review

Wardrop equilibria(WE), first introduced in
[
3
]
, have long been an important part of traffic modeling and control. The underlying idea of this concept is that when the flow over a traffic network is a WE, no driver can achieve a lower travel cost by switching its routing choices. In this way it captures the behavior of drivers minimizing their individual travel cost in an intuitive way. The concept has practical use since the set of WE is equivalently given by the set of solutions of a Variational Inequality (VI), which can be computed efficiently. We refer to
[
1
]
for a survey on the subject of WE in general.

Research on routing games with changing demand, and the effects thereof on WE and the associated costs, often analyzes a more general framework then we consider here. In
[
4
]
, WE are studied in a dynamic setting, where demand is time-dependent. Work in
[
5
]
is focused on studying the relation between the total cost under WE and the minimally attainable total cost, the difference between which is known as the price of anarchy. A thorough investigation of the continuity of WE of routing games and monotonicity of the associated costs with respect to changes in the demand is done in
[
6
]
. When multiple Origin-Destination (OD) pairs are considered,
[
7
]
shows how costs under WE can decrease as demand increases. In
[
8
,
9
]
the sensitivity of WE to changes in a broader class of parameters than only the demand is investigated.
Most closely related to our work in this context is
[
8
]
. The conclusion obtained in the first part of this work are similar to results obtained in
[
8
]
, the difference being that we consider a more limited set of cost functions (namely affine cost functions), only consider a single OD-pair, and crucially, do not constrain the WE to be unique. In addition, several intermediate results obtained in our analysis turn out to be useful in the second part of this paper, concerning the Braess’s paradox.

Similar to the subject of Wardrop equilibria, the related concept of Braess’s paradox (BP), has been the subject of a lot of research since its introduction in
[
10
]
(see
[
11
]
for an English translation). Various approaches to detecting the presence of BP have been studied, of which we mention a few examples here. In
[
12
]
, a necessary and sufficient condition for the occurrence of BP are given based on properties of the solutions of a minimization problem. Similarly, in
[
13
]
, a comparison between flows that minimize specific objectives, such as WE, and the flow that minimizes the average cost are used to identify links that potentially cause BP. In
[
14
]
, a heuristic method is employed first identifying single links which cause BP, and subsequently finding the subset of all these links that when removed lead the lowest cost under WE. Rather than focusing on a network with given demand and cost functions, the work
[
15
]
investigates the relation between network topology and BP, showing that any network that is not series-parallel is subject to BP for some assignment of cost functions. However, despite these investigations, tractable options for detecting BP remain limited, and the general problem of detecting BP has in fact been shown to be NP-hard
[
2
]
. In light of this computational intractability, the likelihood of a network being subject to Braess’s paradox has been studied. In
[
16
]
, it is shown that for randomly generated Erdös-Renyi graphs, the likelihood of there existing a set of edges which cause BP at some demand tends to one as the number of vertices tends to infinity. In
[
17
]
, necessary and sufficient conditions for the occurrence of BP are given, and it is shown, under the limiting assumption that paths that were used under WE before removal of an edge are still used under WE after removal as well, that BP is as likely to occur as not. Closer to the subject of our work, the relation between demand and BP has also been studied in several papers. For the archetypical example of the Wheatstone network with affine costs, BP has been shown to only appear within a finite range of demands
[
18
,
19
]
. It has also been shown that under somewhat restrictive assumptions an edge responsible for BP will for higher levels of demand remain unused
[
20
]
.

Statement of contributions

The aim of this paper is to study the relationship between changing demand, Wardrop equilibrium (WE) and Braess’s paradox (BP), in the context of non-atomic, single-origin destination routing games with affine, non-decreasing cost functions on the edges. The paper can be broadly divided into two parts, the first characterizes the change in WE as demand shifts and the second considers Braess’s paradox, its identification and the effect of demand on it.

When the WE is unique for all demand values, the map from the demand to the WE and the cost incurred by each path at WE (loosely termed as the WE cost-vector) is piece-wise affine. These pieces are defined by a set of points that we refer to as breakpoints. That is, the WE and the WE cost-vector vary linearly with demand between any two consecutive breakpoints. These breakpoints are finite in number. When the WE is not unique, the situation is similar, the only change being that the map from the demand to WE is set-valued. In the first part of our work, our contributions consist of the following results concerning the evolution of the set of WE:

1.

In (Corollary
3.6
and Lemma
4.3
), we analyze how the used sets and active sets evolve between and at breakpoints. The used set is the set of paths that carry non-zero flow for at least on WE. The active set is the set of paths that incur minimum cost at WE among all paths.

2.

The main result of the first part (Proposition
4.10
) is the characterization of the directions in which the WE can vary as the demand increases. We call these directions the directions of increase. We show that these can be computed by solving a VI problem. Essentially this is a generalization of results in
[
8
]
, where a similar property was derived under the assumption that WE are unique.

3.

In the process of establishing the main result, we also characterize the map from demand to the WE-cost (cost incurred by the entire flow at WE) and from demand to the WE cost-vector (cost of each path at a WE) between consecutive breakpoints (Proposition
4.6
and Corollary
4.7
).

4.

The final set of results in this section studies the behavior of WE in the “final interval” (Section
4.2
); that is, the interval that starts at the largest finite breakpoint and extends till positive infinity. Specifically, we give a method to compute the largest finite breakpoint (Corollary
4.19
), the direction in which WE cost and WE cost-vector evolve in the final interval (Proposition
4.15
and Lemma
4.17
), and the active set in the final interval (Lemma
4.18
).

Using the results of the first part, we study Braess’s paradox and give the following main insights that form the second part of our contributions:

1.

We introduce the concept of a
necessary
set of paths, which for a given demand is a set of paths on which the total amount of flow is positive under any WE. We show in Proposition
5.10
that when a set of paths is not necessary at a demand, then this indicates that it is either unnecessary for all lower levels of demand, or this set is responsible for BP on some lower level of demand.

2.

Next we provide a set of affine functions which give a set of sufficient conditions for the presence of BP (Lemma
5.12
). Specifically, if at any demand, the WE-cost of the game is more than the value that any of these affine functions take at this demand, then the network is subject to BP.
In fact we show that the network is subject to BP if and only if its WE-cost is less than one of these affine functions (Proposition
5.15
). However, obtaining all of these affine functions is computationally intractable. Therefore we provide two more sufficient conditions, based on these affine fuctions, that detect BP at a particular demand in a tractable manner. The first one (Corollary
5.13
) shows that if the slope of WE-cost increases at any breakpoint, then the network is subject to BP. Additionally, if at a given demand, the set of directions of increase does not overlap with a simplex, then the routing game is subject to BP at that demand (Proposition
5.14
)

3.

The next set of results views BP in a broader perspective and explores its dependence on the demand. We show that any set of paths responsible for BP at some demand must have been strictly beneficial for some lower level of demand (Corollary
5.19
). Here, beneficial means that removal of that set of paths will strictly increase the WE-cost. Motivated by this observation, we introduce two simple metrics that measure the value of a set of paths over a range of demands. For the first metric, we show that over a range of demand, removing a set of paths does not have a negative impact cost-wise (Proposition
5.20
). When considering the second metric, we show that the over a range of demand, removing a set of paths does not have a positive impact cost-wise (Proposition
5.21
). Thus, these two results give a guideline for the planner to remove paths based on the metric that needs to be improved on.

Several results listed above are supported with illustrative examples that clarify nuances and aid understanding.

Definition 2.1
.

(Wardrop Equilibrium):

Given a routing game

(5)

We will denote the set of all Wardrop equilibria as

.
\bullet

The intuition behind this definition is that when the flow is in WE, none of the participants can decrease their travel-time by choosing another path. Note that this implies that all paths receiving a positive amount of flow incur the same cost.

A flow

[
1
]
.
When

(6)

This set collects all the paths that have minimum cost under any WE for the demand

Note that from (

\displaystyle f_{\mathcal{R}}

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{R}}(f)

That is,

Similarly, we define the map

{D\mapsto\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(D):=C(f^{D})}
, where

is any flow that belongs to the set

. We will refer to

3
Preliminaries

Here we introduce some preliminary material on how the set of WE can be obtained by solving either a variational inequality problem (VI) or alternatively an optimization problem. We then gather some useful results on the evolution of the set of WE and the active and used sets as the demand varies.

Definition 3.1
.

(Variational inequalities (VIs)):

Given a map
G:\real^{n}\rightarrow\real^{n}
and a set
\mathcal{X}\subset\real^{n}
, the associated variational inequality problem, denoted
\operatorname{VI}(\mathcal{X},G)
is to find
x^{*}\in\mathcal{X}
such that the following holds:

G(x^{*})^{\top}(x-x^{*})\geq 0,\enskip\forall x\in\mathcal{X}.

(9)

The set of solutions
x^{*}\in\mathcal{X}
satisfying the above property is denoted as
\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{X},G)
.
\bullet

Proposition 3.2
.

(WE as the solution of a VI
[
23
]
):

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C},D)
be given. A flow
f^{D}
is a Wardrop equilibrium if and only if
f^{D}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{D},C)
.

We note that when

for some matrix

, we use the notation
\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F},A):=\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F},C)
.

Alternatively, WE can be characterized as the solution of a convex optimization problem.
This characterization will give us the tools for analyzing the evolution of the WE cost

as demand changes.

.

]

V(D):=\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{D}}\sum_{e_{k}\in\mathcal{E}}\int_{0}^{f_{e_{k}}}C_{e_{k}}(z)dz.

(10)

The relationship between

]

(Properties of WE cost

]

\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{T}=\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D}

T\in(D^{-},D^{+})
, while
\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{T}\neq\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D}
for all
T\in[D^{-},D^{+}]^{c}
. This observation, together with the fact that there are only a finite number of subsets of
\mathcal{P}
leads to the upcoming result. It states that there is a finite set of disjoint open intervals covering the non-negative real line, excepting a finite number of points, such that the active and used set remain constant in each of these intervals. Furthermore, no two such active sets, associated to different open intervals, are equal, and the same holds for the associated used sets.

Corollary 3.6
.

(Piece-wise constant evolution of the active and used sets):

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C})
be given. There exists a finite set of points
{\mathcal{D}:=\{D_{0},D_{1},\cdots,D_{M},D_{M+1}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\cup\{+\infty\}}
with
D_{0}=0
,
D_{M+1}=\infty
and
D_{j}>D_{j-1}
for all
{j\in[M+1]}
, and corresponding sets of subsets of
\mathcal{P}
denoted
\{\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{0},\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{1},\cdots,\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{M}\}
and
\{\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{0},\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{1},\cdots,\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{M}\}
, such that, for all
i\in[M]_{0}
and
D\in(D_{i},D_{i+1})
, we have

\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D}=\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i},\quad\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D}=\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i}.

Furthermore,
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i}\neq\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{j}
and
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i}\neq\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{j}
for all
i\neq j
.

In the above result, the claim regarding the piece-wise constant evolution of active sets was established in
[
5
, Section 4]
. This property in combination with Lemma
3.5
-2 and (
4
) yields the claim regarding piece-wise evolution of the used sets. For the sake of completeness, a proof of this last part is given in Appendix
A
.

We call the demands in the set
\mathcal{D}

breakpoints
, as these are the points where the active set changes. Note that the above result makes no claims about the relationship between the active set at a breakpoint and the adjacent open intervals. We will shed light on this aspect in Lemma
4.3
in the subsequent section. Finally, we note that the points in the set
\mathcal{D}
are also the points where the WE cost-vector

is non-differentiable, further motivating our choice for the term
breakpoints
. We formally establish this in Proposition

in the next section.

Lemma 4.2
.

(Continuity of the map

):

Let

be given. The (set-valued) map

is continuous; that is, for every

and
\epsilon>0

\delta>0

.

2.

There exists a
\delta>0
such that for all

satisfying
\left\lvert{T-D}\right\rvert<\delta
and all
f^{T}\in\mathcal{W}_{T}
there exists
f^{D}\in\mathcal{W}_{D}
such that
\|f^{D}-f^{T}\|<\epsilon
.

]
, or alternatively as inner- and outer-semicontinuity, respectively
[

]
.
The result follows from

With the above continuity property established, we can start our investigation into the evolution of the set of WE by looking at the evolution of the active and used sets. Our next result shows how these sets evolve as the demand varies in the interval

Lemma 4.3
.

(Relationship between active and used sets over an interval):

For a given
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C})
, let
D_{i},D_{i+1}\in\mathcal{D}
and let
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
be the associated active and used sets on
(D_{i},D_{i+1})
, respectively. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D_{i}}

\displaystyle\subseteq\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D_{i}},

(17a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D_{i+1}}

\displaystyle\subseteq\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D_{i+1}}.

(17b)

Proof.

We will first establish (
17a
). Let
p\in\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D_{i}}
and
r\in(\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D_{i}})^{c}
. It follows that
\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}_{p}(D_{i})<\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}_{r}(D_{i})
. Continuity of the map
D\mapsto\mathcal{W}_{D}
, as proven in Lemma
4.2
, implies continuity of the map
\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(\cdot)
. Therefore, it follows that for small enough
\epsilon>0
we have
\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}_{p}(T)<\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}_{r}(T)
for all
T\in[D_{i},D_{i}+\epsilon)
. It follows that
r\in(\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{T})^{c}
for all
T\in[D_{i},D_{i}+\epsilon)
, which shows that
r\in(\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i})^{c}
. Thus we have
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D_{i}}
. Similarly, it follows from Lemma
4.2
that for small enough
\epsilon>0
there exists
f^{T}\in\mathcal{W}_{T}
such that
f^{T}_{p}>0
for all
p\in\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D_{i}}
and
T\in[D_{i},D_{i}+\epsilon)
. This shows that
\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D_{i}}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{T}
for all
T\in[D_{i},D_{i}+\epsilon)
. Thus we have
\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D_{i}}\subseteq\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i}
. By definition of the active and used sets, we also have
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i}
, proving (
17a
). The result (
17b
) concerning
\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D_{i+1}}
and
\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D_{i+1}}
follows by the same arguments considering the interval
(D_{i+1}-\epsilon,D_{i+1}]
.
∎

The implication of the above is that when the demand

moves from the point

into the interval

, the used set

can only gain elements, while the active set

can only lose elements. When the demand
D
then moves from the interval

to the point

can only lose elements, while

can only gain elements. Also note that since

and
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i}\neq\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{j}
for all

moves from

to

. Turning our attention back to Example

\displaystyle(\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D},\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D})=\begin{cases}(\{p_{3}\},\emptyset)&\text{for }D=0,\\

(Convex combinations of WE in

For a given

and

, let

\operatorname{coco}_{\mu}(f^{D},f^{T})\in\mathcal{W}_{\operatorname{coco}_{\mu}(D,T)}
.

Proof.

The result for the case

is already stated in Lemma
3.5
. Now consider the case
{D=D_{i}}
and
{T=D_{i+1}}
. For given
\mu\in[0,1]
,

, and

, denote
{f^{\mu}:=\operatorname{coco}_{\mu}(f^{D},f^{T})}
. Since

, and it follows in a straigthforward manner that

, where

be a path such that

. It follows that either

or

. Since

and

, we obtain either

or

implies that

\displaystyle C_{p}(f^{D})

\displaystyle\leq C_{r}(f^{D}),\quad\forall r\in\mathcal{P},

\displaystyle C_{p}(f^{T})

for all

. This establishes the WE condition (

. The cases

,

and

The main goal of this section is to characterize the evolution of the set of WE. In Example

, we noted the existence of a (set of)

f^{\delta}
along which the WE moved when the demand increases in the interval between points of

. We call such a direction a

. The collection of all such vectors, denoted

, is referred to as the set of directions of increase. Our goal is thus to characterize this set
\Gamma_{D}
, which is formally defined as follows:

Definition 4.5
.

(Set of directions of increase):

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C},D)
be given. The set of

\Gamma_{D}
is the set of all directions
f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
in which the flow can be increased, starting from some WE in

, such that the new flow is a WE as long as the increase is small enough. That is,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{D}:=\{f^{\delta}

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{H}_{1}\;|\;\exists f^{D}\in\mathcal{W}_{D},\hskip 2.0pt\bar{\epsilon}>0\text{ such that }f^{D}+\epsilon f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{W}_{D+\epsilon}\hskip 2.0pt\forall\epsilon\in[0,\bar{\epsilon}]\}.\hbox{$\bullet$}

This definition of
\Gamma_{D}
puts emphasis on the local properties of this set. However, we note that for any
D\in[D_{i},D_{i+1})
every WE
f^{T}\in\mathcal{W}_{T}
, where
T\in(D,D_{i+1}]
, is of the form
f^{D}+(T-D)f^{\delta}
, where
f^{D}\in\mathcal{W}_{D}
and
f^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D}
. In other words, all WE in the range
[D,D_{i+1}]
are characterized by
\mathcal{W}_{D}
and
\Gamma_{D}
. This fact can be derived from Corollary
4.4
.

4.1
Characterizing
\Gamma_{D}

Before we address the evolution of the WE by deriving an expression for
\Gamma_{D}
, we first shift our attention to the evolution of the WE-cost
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}
and the WE cost-vector
\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}
. We characterize the evolution of the WE cost-vector
\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}
and relate it to the set
\Gamma_{D}
.

Proposition 4.6
.

(The evolution of
\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}
):

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C})
be given. For any
{i\in[M]_{0}}
, there exists a vector
\delta C^{i}
such that the following hold:

1.

for all
D\in[D_{i},D_{i+1})
and
f^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D}

Af^{\delta}=\delta C^{i},

(18)

2.

for all
T\in[D_{i},D_{i+1}]

\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(T)=\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(D_{i})+(T-D_{i})\delta C^{i},

(19)

3.

\delta C^{i}\neq\delta C^{i+1}
.

Proof.

We start with the second claim, which is a consequence of the affine form of
C
, given in (
4
), and the convexity result in Corollary
4.4
. Let
f^{D_{i}}\in\mathcal{W}_{D_{i}}
and
f^{D_{i+1}}\in\mathcal{W}_{D_{i+1}}
and define

f^{\delta_{0}}:=(D_{i+1}-D_{i})^{-1}(f^{D_{i+1}}-f^{D}).

By Corollary
4.4
any convex combination of
f^{D_{i}}
and
f^{D_{i+1}}
is a WE. To be specific, pick some
\mu\in[0,1]
and let
f^{\mu}:=\operatorname{coco}_{\mu}(f^{D_{i}},f^{D_{i+1}})
. Then,
f^{\mu}\in\mathcal{W}_{T_{\mu}}
, where
{T_{\mu}=\operatorname{coco}_{\mu}(D_{i},{D_{i+1}})}
. Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle f^{\mu}

\displaystyle=f^{D_{i}}+(1-\mu)(f^{D_{i+1}}-f^{D_{i}})

\displaystyle=f^{D_{i}}+(T_{\mu}-D_{i})\frac{f^{D_{i+1}}-f^{D_{i}}}{{D_{i+1}}-D_{i}}

\displaystyle=f^{D_{i}}+(T_{\mu}-D_{i})f^{\delta_{0}}.

Using the affine nature of
C
, given in (
4
), and the definition of
\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}
we derive

\displaystyle\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(T_{\mu})

\displaystyle=C(f^{\mu})

(20)

\displaystyle=C\big(f^{D_{i}}+(T_{\mu}-D_{i})f^{\delta_{0}}\big)

\displaystyle=C(f^{D_{i}})+(T_{\mu}-D_{i})Af^{\delta_{0}}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(D_{i})+(T_{\mu}-D_{i})Af^{\delta_{0}}.

Since the above holds for all
\mu\in[0,1]
it follows that, for all
T\in[D_{i},D_{i+1}]
, we have
{\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(T)=\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D_{i})+(T_{\mu}-D_{i})Af^{\delta_{0}}}
. Setting
\delta C^{i}:=Af^{\delta_{0}}
the second statement is proven.

To show the first statement, let
D^{-}\in[D_{i},D_{i+1})
and let
f^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D^{-}}
. It follows that there exists
D^{+}\in(D^{-},D_{i+1}]
and
f^{D^{+}}\in\mathcal{W}_{D^{+}}
such that

f^{D^{+}}=f^{D^{-}}+(D^{+}-D^{-})f^{\delta}.

Using the same derivation as in (
20
) we find

\displaystyle\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(D^{+})=\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(D^{-})+(D^{+}-D^{-})Af^{\delta}.

(21)

However, from (
19
) we have

\displaystyle\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(D^{+})

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(D_{i})+(D^{-}-D_{i})\delta C^{i}+(D^{+}-D^{-})\delta C^{i}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(D^{-})+(D^{+}-D^{-})\delta C^{i},

(22)

where we have again used
\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(D^{-})=\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(D_{i})+(D^{-}-D_{i})\delta C^{i}
. Comparing expressions (
21
) and (
22
), we get
Af^{\delta}=\delta C^{i}
, proving the first statement.

We show the third statement using contradiction. To this end, assume that
{\delta C^{i}=\delta C^{i+1}}
for some
i\in[M]_{0}
and let
{p,r\in\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i}}
. By definition of the active set it follows that
\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}_{p}(D)=\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}_{r}(D)
for all
D\in(D_{i},D_{i+1})
. Combined with (
19
) this implies
\delta C^{i}_{p}=\delta C^{i}_{r}
. Under the assumption that
\delta C^{i}=\delta C^{i+1}
we then have, by (
19
), that
\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}_{p}(D)=\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}_{r}(D)
for all
D\in(D_{i},D_{i+2})
and
p,r\in\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i}
. As a consequence either all of the paths in
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i}
are in the active set on the interval
(D_{i+1},D_{i+2})
or none of them are. That is, one of the following holds:
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i}\cap\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i+1}=\emptyset
or
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i+1}
. However, note that from Lemma
4.3
we obtain
\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D_{i+1}}\subseteq\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i}
and
\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D_{i+1}}\subseteq\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i+1}\subseteq\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i+1}
. Since
D_{i+1}>0
, we have
\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D_{i+1}}\neq\emptyset
and it follows that
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i}\cap\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i+1}\neq\emptyset
. Thus, we must have
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i+1}
.

Now since
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i}\neq\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i+1}
, there must exist some path
r^{\prime}\in(\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i})^{c}\cap\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i+1}
. Since
r^{\prime}
is not in the active set
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i}
, one can find a path
p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i}
and a demand
D\in(D_{i},D_{i+1})
such that
\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}_{p^{\prime}}(D)<\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}_{r^{\prime}}(D)
. On the other hand, by Lemma
4.3
,
r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i+1}
implies
{r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D_{i+1}}}
and so,
\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}_{r^{\prime}}(D_{i+1})\leq\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}_{p^{\prime}}(D_{i+1})
. Combining the above two facts with the affine form (
19
), we deduce that
\delta C^{i}_{p^{\prime}}>\delta C^{i}_{r^{\prime}}
. Since
\delta C^{i+1}=\delta C^{i}
it follows from (
19
) that
\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}_{p^{\prime}}(D)>\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}_{r^{\prime}}(D)
for all
D\in(D_{i+1},D_{i+2})
. However, this contradicts the fact that
p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i+1}
.

We see that we arrive at a contradiction, and therefore the premise must be false. We conclude that
{\delta C^{i+1}\not=\delta C^{i}}
.
∎

Proposition
4.6
shows that directions of increase at any demand

\mathcal{D}
and non-differentiable at the points in
\mathcal{D}
. The latter fact is due to the last conclusion in the result.

Corollary 4.7
.

(Evolution of

be given. For any
{i\in[M]_{0}}

Proof.

The result follows from (

∎

and a value

such that for all

. Recall that

is the matrix related to the flow dependent part of the path-cost function

. We will show that the following holds:

Proposition 4.10
.

(Directions of increase as solutions to a VI):

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C},D)
be given. Then,

\Gamma_{D}=\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}_{D},A).

Working towards a proof of the above, we first show two intermediate statements. The first states that the set of directions of increase is contained in the set of directions of feasibility.

Lemma 4.11
.

(

Let

, and let

satisfy
{D\in[D_{i},D_{i+1})}
. Then there exist

,
{T\in(D,D_{i+1}]}
, and

such that
{f^{T}=f^{D}+(T-D)f^{\delta}}
. Thus we have
{f^{\delta}=(T-D)^{-1}(f^{T}-f^{D})}
. Note that since
{D\in[D_{i},D_{i+1})}
and

that

.
By definition of the active set (

) and the WE condition (

.

(Properties of

Let

,

,

2.

There exists a vector
\delta C\in\real^{n}
such that

.

If

and

{A_{p}f^{\delta}=\min_{r\in\mathcal{Q}}A_{r}f^{\delta}}

Proof.

Part
1
:
The first claim follows by noting that
{f^{\delta}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M},A)}
if and only if it solves

\displaystyle\text{minimize }

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}f^{\top}Af

(24)

\displaystyle f\in\mathcal{M}.

Since

is positive semi-definite and
\mathcal{M}
is closed and convex, this minimization problem has a non-empty solution set, showing that
\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M},A)
is non-empty.

Part
2
:
To prove the “only if” side, let
{f^{\delta_{1}},f^{\delta_{2}}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M},A)}
. By Definition

, we have

\displaystyle(Af^{\delta_{1}})^{\top}(f^{\delta_{2}}-f^{\delta_{1}})

\displaystyle\geq 0,

(25)

\displaystyle(Af^{\delta_{2}})^{\top}(f^{\delta_{1}}-f^{\delta_{2}})

\displaystyle\geq 0.

Since

is symmetric and positive semidefinite we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq(f^{\delta_{1}}-f^{\delta_{2}})^{\top}A(f^{\delta_{1}}-f^{\delta_{2}})

\displaystyle=(Af^{\delta_{1}})^{\top}(f^{\delta_{1}}-f^{\delta_{2}})-(Af^{\delta_{2}})^{\top}(f^{\delta_{1}}-f^{\delta_{2}})\leq 0,

where the last inequality follows from (

). Thus, we have

This shows that there exists a

\displaystyle(Af^{\delta})^{\top}(f-f^{\delta})

\displaystyle=\epsilon(A_{p}f^{\delta}-A_{r}f^{\delta})<0.

This contradicts

. We conclude that if

{A_{p}f^{\delta}=\min_{r\in\mathcal{Q}}A_{r}f^{\delta}}

We note that when the set

is empty, the first three statements of the above proposition are equivalent to well-known results on the existence, essential uniqueness, and properties of the cost of WE, which can be proven in much the same way. To be specific, when

is empty, the set

, we have

, and the third and fourth claim gain a useful intuitive interpretation. Namely, the third claim shows that for any

must have the smallest increase in cost among all active paths. The same holds for any used path, regardless of how the flow on that path changes.

.

implies
{C_{p}(f^{D+\epsilon})=\min_{r\in\mathcal{P}}C_{r}(f^{D+\epsilon})}
which means the the WE condition (
5
) is satisfied, and it follows that
f^{D+\epsilon}\in\mathcal{W}_{D+\epsilon}
. Therefore,
{\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}_{D},A)\subseteq\Gamma_{D}}
.

To show
\Gamma_{D}\subseteq\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}_{D},A)
, let
f^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D}
. Since
\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}_{D},A)\subseteq\Gamma_{D}
, we then know that there exists an
{f^{\delta_{0}}\in\Gamma_{D}\cap\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}_{D},A)}
. By Lemma
4.11
we have
f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{M}_{D}
, and by Proposition
4.6
we have
{Af^{\delta}=Af^{\delta_{0}}}
. Therefore, it follows from Proposition
4.12
that
{f^{\delta}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}_{D},A)}
. In conclusion, we obtain
{\Gamma_{D}=\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}_{D},A)}
.
∎

We next illustrate the equivalence established in Proposition
4.10
for the routing game associated to Figure
3
, as discussed in Example
b
. Consider the demand
D=1
. Using (
14
) and (
15
) we obtain
{\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D}=\{p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},p_{4}\}}
and
{\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D}=\{p_{3}\}}
. Thus, by definition
\mathcal{M}_{D}=\{f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}\;|\;f^{\delta}_{1},f^{\delta}_{2},f^{\delta}_{4}\geq 0\}
. We also have
{\ker(A)=\{f\in\real\;|\;f_{1}=f_{2}=-f_{3}\}}
. Note that
{\ker(A)\cap\mathcal{M}_{D}}
is nonempty and in fact, from (
16
), we have
\Gamma_{D}=\ker(A)\cap\mathcal{M}_{D}
. Therefore, we will show next that
\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}_{D},A)=\ker(A)\cap\mathcal{M}_{D}
, which corroborates the claim of Proposition
4.10
. Select some
f\in\ker(A)\cap\mathcal{M}_{D}
. Since
A
is positive semi-definite we obtain

(Af^{\delta})^{\top}(f-f^{\delta})\leq 0

for any
f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{M}_{D}
. Using (
9
) in the definition of
\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}_{D},A)
it follows that in order for
{f^{\delta}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}_{D},A)}
to hold, we need
f^{\delta}\in\ker(A)\cap\mathcal{M}_{D}
. Furthermore, when
{f^{\delta}\in\ker(A)\cap\mathcal{M}_{D}}
, we have
(Af^{\delta})^{\top}(f-f^{\delta})=0
for any
f\in\mathcal{M}_{D}
. In other words,
\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}_{D},A)=\ker(A)\cap\mathcal{M}_{D}
. From (
16
), we then have
\Gamma_{D}=\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}_{D},A)
, as claimed.

The established results have interesting consequences regarding the evolution of the WE in the final interval
(D_{M},\infty)
. However, before we present these, we finish our analysis of the evolution of the set of WE by establishing some basic properties of the set
\Gamma_{D}
, which are now straightforward consequences of Proposition
4.10
. First, note that
\Gamma_{D}
is closed, which follows from the fact that
\mathcal{M}_{D}
is closed
[
27
, Section 1.1]
. In addition,
\Gamma_{D}
is convex
[
27
, Theorem 2.3.5]
.

Corollary 4.13
.

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C},D)
be given. Then
\Gamma_{D}
is nonempty, closed, and convex.

In Example
4.1
we noted that the non-negative real line was divided into intervals in which the set of directions of increase
\Gamma_{D}
remains constant. Using Propostion
4.10
, we can show that this is not an artifact of the considered example and that it holds for any routing game.

Lemma 4.14
.

(
\Gamma_{D}
is constant between breakpoints in
\mathcal{D}
):

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C})
and
{D_{i},D_{i+1}\in\mathcal{D}}
be given. There exists a set
\Gamma^{i}\subset\mathcal{H}_{1}
such that for all
{D\in(D_{i},D_{i+1})}
we have
\Gamma_{D}=\Gamma^{i}
. Furthermore,
\Gamma_{D_{i}}\subseteq\Gamma^{i}
and
\Gamma^{i+1}\cap\Gamma^{i}=\emptyset
.

Proof.

The existence of
\Gamma^{i}
such that
\Gamma_{D}=\Gamma^{i}
for all
{D\in(D_{i},D_{i+1})}
follows directly from Proposition
4.10
and observing that the set
\mathcal{M}_{D}
is same for all
D\in(D_{i},D_{i+1})
, which follows from Corollary
3.6
. Let
f^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D_{i}}
and pick
T\in(D_{i},D_{i+1}]
,
f^{D_{i}}\in\mathcal{W}_{D_{i}}
and
f^{T}\in\mathcal{W}_{T}
such that

f^{T}=f^{D_{i}}+(T-D_{i})f^{\delta}.

Pick
\mu\in(0,1)
and let
T_{\mu}=\operatorname{coco}_{\mu}(D_{i},T)
and
f^{\mu}=\operatorname{coco}_{\mu}(f^{D_{i}},f^{T})
. Note that
{T_{\mu}\in(D_{i},D_{i+1})}
,
{T\in(T_{\mu},D_{i+1})}
, and from Corollary
4.4
,
f^{\mu}\in\mathcal{W}_{T_{\mu}}
. Moreover,
f^{T}=f^{\mu}+(T-T_{\mu})f^{\delta}
.
Therefore
f^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{T_{\mu}}=\Gamma^{i}
,
showing that
\Gamma_{D_{i}}\subseteq\Gamma^{i}
. For the last statement, note that if there exists
f^{\delta}\in\Gamma^{i}\cap\Gamma^{i+1}
then the first conclusion of Proposition
4.6
implies
\delta C^{i+1}=\delta C^{i}
, contradicting the third conclusion of Proposition
4.6
. Therefore
\Gamma^{i+1}\cap\Gamma^{i}=\emptyset
.
∎

4.2
Obtaining
D_{M}

In this section, we derive several properties pertaining to the interval
(D_{M},\infty)
, where we recall that
D_{M}
is the final finite-valued breakpoint in
\mathcal{D}
. In particular, we give a rigorous way in which the value
D_{M}
can be computed. In the process and to its own merit we find the directions in which
\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}
varies over
(D_{M},\infty)
, as well as associated directions of increase in
\Gamma^{M}
. This then allows us to obtain an expression for
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)
for any
D\geq D_{M}
. Using this we can compute the final active set
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{M}
, after which the value of
D_{M}
can be obtained.

We start with the result that
\Gamma^{M}
overlaps with
\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{1},A)
. This gives us a direct method for identifying
\delta C^{M}
, which is the direction in which the WE cost-vector
\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}
evolves in the interval
(D_{M},\infty)
. This will also prove remarkably useful later on, when investigating Braess’s paradox.

Proposition 4.15
.

(Finding
\delta C_{M}
by solving
\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{1},A)
):

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C})
and
{D\geq D_{M}=\max\big(\mathcal{D}\setminus\{\infty\}\big)}
be given.
Then, we have:

1.

\Gamma_{D}\cap\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{1},A)
is non-empty,

2.

Af^{\delta}=\delta C^{M}
for all
f^{\delta}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{1},A)
,

3.

\delta\lambda^{M}=\min_{r\in\mathcal{P}}\delta C^{M}_{p}
.

Proof.

We note that the second statement follows from the first. To see this, let
{f^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D}\cap\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{1},A)}
. From Proposition
4.6
we have
Af^{\delta}=\delta C^{M}
, and Proposition
4.12
then gives us
A\widetilde{f}^{\delta}=\delta C^{M}
for all
f^{\delta}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{1},A)
.
Thus, to prove the first two claims, it is enough to show that
\Gamma_{D}\cap\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{1},A)
is non-empty, which we now do.

First we show that
\Gamma_{D}\cap\mathcal{F}_{1}
is non-empty. For any
{f^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D}}
we already have
{f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}}
. Therefore to establish that
\Gamma_{D}\cap\mathcal{F}_{1}
is non-empty it is enough to prove the existence of
f^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D}
such that
f^{\delta}\geq 0
. This we will do by constucting a sequence
\{f^{\delta,i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\Gamma_{D}
that converges to an
f^{\delta}
satisfying
f^{\delta}\geq 0
. Then, using the fact that
\Gamma_{D}
is closed (as stated in Corollary
4.13
), we conclude that
f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}\cap\Gamma_{D}
.

Let
f^{D}\in\mathcal{W}_{D}
, and let
\{f^{T_{i}}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of WE satisfying
f^{T_{i}}\in\mathcal{W}_{T_{i}}
, where
D_{M}\leq D<T_{1}
,
T_{i}<T_{i+1}
for all
i
, and
\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}T_{i}=\infty
. We then define the sequence
\{f^{\delta,i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
by setting

f^{\delta,i}:=(T_{i}-D)^{-1}(f^{T_{i}}-f^{D}).

Since
D\geq D_{M}
and
T_{i}>D
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, it follows from Corollary
4.4
that any convex combination of
f^{D}
and
f^{T_{i}}
is a WE; that is, for any
\epsilon\in[0,T_{i}-D)
we find that
f^{D}+\epsilon f^{\delta,i}
is a WE. By Definition
4.5
, we deduce that
f^{\delta,i}\in\Gamma_{D}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. In addition, the sequence
\{f^{\delta,i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded. To show this,
let
t:=\max_{r\in\mathcal{P}}f^{D}_{r}
. Since
f^{T_{i}}\geq 0
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
we obtain
(f^{T_{i}}_{r}-f^{D}_{r})\geq-t
for all
r\in\mathcal{P}
and all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore,
f^{\delta,i}_{r}\geq-t(T_{i}-D)^{-1}
for all
r\in\mathcal{P}
and
i\in\mathbb{N}
.
Letting
\mathcal{R}_{i}:=\{r\in\mathcal{P}\;|\;f^{\delta,i}_{r}<0\}
it follows that

\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_{i}}f^{\delta}_{r}\geq-nt(T_{i}-D)^{-1}.

Since
f^{\delta,i}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
the above implies that
f^{\delta,i}_{r}\leq nt(T_{i}-D)^{-1}+1
for all
r\in\mathcal{P}
. Since
T_{1}\leq T_{i}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
we get
{-t(T_{1}-D)^{-1}\leq f^{\delta,i}_{r}\leq-nt(T_{1}-D)^{-1}+1}
for all
r\in\mathcal{P}
and
i\in\mathbb{N}
. In other words,
\{f^{\delta,i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded.
Therefore, the sequence contains a subsequence, denoted
\{f^{\delta,i_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, that converges. For this subsequence, since
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}T_{i_{k}}=\infty
it follows that
\lim_{k\to\infty}f^{\delta,i_{k}}_{r}\geq 0
for all
r\in\mathcal{P}
. We see that

f^{\delta}:=\lim_{k\to\infty}f^{\delta,i_{k}}\geq 0.

Since
\Gamma_{D}
is closed, it follows that
f^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D}
. Thus, there exists
f^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D}\cap\mathcal{F}_{1}
.

The final step is to show that
f^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D}\cap\mathcal{F}_{1}
implies
{f^{\delta}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{1},A)}
. This we do by showing that
f^{\delta}
is a WE of the routing game with the path cost function
\widetilde{C}(f)=Af
and the feasible set
\mathcal{F}_{1}
.

Given an
f^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D}\cap\mathcal{F}_{1}
, let
p\in\mathcal{P}
satisfy
f^{\delta}_{p}>0
. By definition of the set
\Gamma_{D}
we then know that for some
T>D
there exist WE
f^{T}\in\mathcal{W}_{T}
satisfying
f^{T}_{p}>0
. Since
D\geq D_{M}
this implies that the path
p
is used, and therefore active, in the interval
(D_{M},\infty)
. In other words,
p\in\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{M}
.

For the sake of contradiction assume that
r\in\mathcal{P}
satsifies
A_{r}f^{\delta}<A_{p}f^{\delta}
. Since
f^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D}
we have
Af^{\delta}=\delta C^{M}
, which implies
\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(T)=\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(D)+(T-D)Af^{\delta}
for any
T\in(D,\infty)
. It follows that for large enough
T
we have
\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}_{r}(T)<\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}_{p}(T)
, which implies that
p\notin\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{T}
. Since
{T>D}
, and
D\geq D_{M}
this implies that for some
{T\in(D_{M},\infty)}
, the path
p
is not active. In other words
p\notin\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{M}
. We have arrived at a contradiction, and therefore we conclude that
A_{p}f^{\delta}=\min_{r\in\mathcal{P}}A_{r}f^{\delta}
. In other words,
f^{\delta}
is a WE of the routing game with the path cost function
\widetilde{C}(f)=Af
and the feasible set
\mathcal{F}_{1}
. By Proposition
3.2
this implies
f^{\delta}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{1},A)
.

For the third statement, note that when
\delta C^{M}_{r}<\delta\lambda^{M}
for some
r\in\mathcal{P}
, this implies that in the interval
(D_{M},\infty)
, the cost of path
r
under WE increases at a slower rate than the WE-cost. Since the cost of all paths in this interval increases at a constant rate, this implies that at some demand
T>D_{M}
,
\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}_{r}(T)<\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(T)
. However, the WE-cost is the minimal cost of all paths under WE, and therefore this is not possible. Thus we have
\delta\lambda^{M}=\min_{r\in\mathcal{P}}\delta C^{M}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

The intuition behind the above result is as follows. Consider a demand
D
in the final interval
[D_{M},\infty)
, for which we have
\Gamma_{D}\subseteq\Gamma^{M}
by Lemma
4.14
, and consider the set of directions in which the WE moves as demand increases from
D
; that is, consider the set
\Gamma_{D}
. Since the interval
[D_{M},\infty)
stretches to infinity without encountering any other breakpoint, there must be some direction in
\Gamma_{D}
along which we can move indefinitely. For any direction
f^{\delta}
which takes flow from some path, that is the vector has some negative component, one can only move a finite amount in that direction as sooner or later the flow on that path then becomes zero, and we can no longer move in that direction. Thus, there must be some
f^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D}\cap\mathcal{F}_{1}
. In addition, when moving in such a “nonnegative” direction of increase the cost of all paths receiving flow must remain minimal to satisfy the WE conditions. Therefore, the cost of paths receiving flow must show the minimal increase among all paths. This implies that in fact
f^{\delta}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{1},A)
, which in turn shows the above result.
Note that these observations do not imply
\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}_{D},A)=\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{1},A)
, as for instance demonstrated in Example
b
. There we see that for any
D\geq D_{M}=1
there exist
f^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D}
and
p\in\mathcal{P}
such that
f^{\delta}_{p}<0
.

Having obtained
\delta C^{M}
by solving
\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{1},A)
, we now give a full characterization of
\Gamma^{M}\cap\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{1},A)
in the below result. Note that the result considers
\Gamma_{D}
for some
D\geq D_{M}
instead of
\Gamma^{M}
, but whenever
D>D_{M}
we have
\Gamma_{D}=\Gamma^{M}
.

Proposition 4.16
.

(Obtaining
\Gamma^{M}\cap\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{1},A)
):

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C})
and
D\geq D_{M}
be given. The set
\Gamma_{D}\cap\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{1},A)
is equal to the set of solutions of the following minimization problem:

\displaystyle\textnormal{ minimize }

\displaystyle\beta^{\top}f^{\delta}

(26)

\displaystyle\textnormal{subject to }

\displaystyle Af^{\delta}=\delta C^{M},

\displaystyle f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}.

Proof.

We start with the observation that taken together Propositions
4.12
and
4.15
imply
{f^{\delta}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{1},A)}
if and only if
f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}
and
Af^{\delta}=\delta C^{M}
. In other words, the feasibility set of the problem (
26
) is equal to
\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{1},A)
. Thus, our goal is to show that
f^{\delta}
is an optimal solution of (
26
) if and only if it is feasible and
f^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D}
.

We proceed with the “if” part, that is, if
f^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D}
and is a feasible point of (
26
), then it is an optimal solution of (
26
). To do so, let
f^{D_{M}}\in\mathcal{W}_{D_{M}}
and consider the minimization problem

\displaystyle\textnormal{ minimize }

\displaystyle(f^{\delta})^{\top}(Af^{D_{M}}+\beta)

(27)

\displaystyle\textnormal{subject to }

\displaystyle Af^{\delta}=\delta C^{M},

\displaystyle f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}.

This minimization problem is actually equivalent to (
26
), meaning that
f^{\delta}
is an optimal solution of (
26
) if an only if it is so for (
27
). To see this, note that since
f^{D_{M}}\in\mathcal{W}_{D_{M}}
, we have
Af^{D_{M}}+\beta=\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(D_{M})
. Therefore, if
p\in\mathcal{P}
satisfies
f^{D_{M}}_{p}>0
, then it follows that
p\in\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D_{M}}
, and by Lemma
4.3
we then have
p\in\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{M}
. We see that
p
is in the used set on the interval
[D_{M},\infty)
, and therefore it must maintain minimal cost under WE among all paths as the demand increase from
D
to infinity. Consequently
{\delta C^{M}_{p}=\min_{r\in\mathcal{P}}\delta C^{M}_{r}=\delta\lambda^{M}}
. Since
D\geq D_{M}
we also have, by Proposition
4.6
, that
\delta C^{M}=Af^{\delta}
for any
f^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D}
. Therefore
A_{p}f^{\delta}=\delta\lambda^{M}
whenever
f^{D_{M}}_{p}>0
. Since
f^{D_{M}}\in\mathcal{F}_{D_{M}}
this implies

(f^{\delta})^{\top}Af^{D_{M}}=D_{M}\delta\lambda^{M}\quad\text{ for all }f^{\delta}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{1},A).

(28)

We see that the term
(f^{\delta})^{\top}Af^{D_{M}}
is constant over the feasible set of (
27
). Therefore
f^{\delta}
solves (
27
) if and only if it minimizes the term
\beta^{\top}f^{\delta}
over the feasible set. This is exactly the objective function of (
26
), and since the feasible sets of (
26
) and (
27
) are the same, this shows that (
26
) and (
27
) have the same set of optimal solutions.

Next we obtain a lower bound on the objective function of (
27
). We have
{\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(D_{M})=Af^{D_{M}}+\beta}
, and in addition
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D_{M})=\min_{r\in\mathcal{P}}\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}_{r}(D_{M})
. In other words,
\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(D_{M})\geq\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D_{M})\mymathbf{1}
, where
\mymathbf{1}
is the vector of ones. For any
f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}
all elements of
f^{\delta}
are nonnegative, and sum to one, and therefore it follows that for all
f^{\delta}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{1},A)
we have

\displaystyle(f^{\delta})^{\top}(Af^{D_{M}}+\beta)

\displaystyle=(f^{\delta})^{\top}\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(D_{M})

\displaystyle\geq\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D_{M}).

Next we show that any
f^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D}\cap\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{1},A)
achieves this lower bound. We know from Proposition
4.15
that
\Gamma_{D}\cap\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{1},A)
is non-empty, so we can pick
{\widetilde{f}^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D}\cap\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{1},A)}
. By Proposition
4.10
we then have
\widetilde{f}^{\delta}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}_{D},A)
. Now let
p\in\mathcal{P}
satisfy
\widetilde{f}^{\delta}_{p}>0
. From the definition of
\Gamma_{D}
it follows that
p\in\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{T}=\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{M}
for some
T>D
, and by Lemma
4.3
this implies
p\in\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D_{M}}
. Therefore
\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}_{p}(D_{M})=\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D_{M})
. Since
\widetilde{f}^{\delta}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}
it follows that

(\widetilde{f}^{\delta})^{\top}\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(D_{M})=\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D_{M}),

(29)

showing that
\widetilde{f}^{\delta}
achieves the lower bound we established for (
27
). In other words,
\widetilde{f}^{\delta}
is a solution of (
27
), and is therefore also a solution to (
26
). This shows that
\Gamma_{D}\cap\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{1},A)
is contained within the set of solutions of (
26
).

We now proceed with the “only if” part. Let
f^{\delta}
be a solution of (
26
), and therefore also a solution of (
27
). We know from the derivation of (
29
) that this implies
(f^{\delta})^{\top}\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(D_{M})=\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D_{M})
. Since
{\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(D_{M})\geq\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D_{M})\mymathbf{1}}
and
f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}
this gives us

\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}_{p}(D_{M})=\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D_{M})\quad\text{ for all }p\in\mathcal{P}\text{ satisfying }f^{\delta}_{p}>0.

In other words,
f^{\delta}_{p}>0
implies
p\in\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D_{M}}
. Together with
{f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}}
this shows that
f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{M}_{D_{M}}
. Thus we have
f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{M}_{D_{M}}
and
Af^{\delta}=\delta C^{M}
. From Proposition
4.10
we have
\Gamma_{D_{M}}=\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}_{D_{M}},A)
, from Proposition
4.6
we have
A\widehat{f}^{\delta}=\delta C^{M}
for all
\widehat{f}^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D_{M}}
and from Proposition
4.12
it then follows that
\widehat{f}^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D_{M}}
if and only if
\widehat{f}^{\delta}\in\mathcal{M}_{D_{M}}
and
A\widehat{f}^{\delta}=\delta C^{M}
. Thus we see that
f^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D_{M}}
. From Corollary
4.14
we have
\Gamma_{D_{M}}\subseteq\Gamma^{M}
, and thus we conclude that
f^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D}
for any
D\geq D_{M}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Next, we use the obtained results to express
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)
for any
D\geq D_{M}
.

Lemma 4.17
.

(Obtaining
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)
for
D\geq D_{M}
):

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C})
and
D\geq D_{M}
be given. In addition, let
\bar{\beta}=\beta^{\top}f^{\delta}
, where
f^{\delta}
is a solution of (
26
). Then, we have

\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)=\delta\lambda^{M}D+\bar{\beta}.

Proof.

First pick
D>D_{M}
and let
f^{\delta}
be a solution of (
26
). From Proposition
4.16
, we have
f^{\delta}\in\Gamma^{M}\cap\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{1},A)
. Additionally,
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)=\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D_{M})+(D-D_{M})\delta\lambda^{M}
holds. Now consider the expression

(f^{\delta})^{\top}\Big(A\big(f^{D_{M}}+(D-D_{M})f^{\delta}\big)+\beta\Big),

where
f^{D_{M}}\in\mathcal{W}_{D_{M}}
. Since

\displaystyle=(f^{\delta})^{\top}\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(D)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)

\displaystyle(f^{\delta})^{\top}\Big(A\big(f^{D_{M}}+(D-D_{M})f^{\delta}\big)+\beta\Big)

5
Braess’s paradox for changing demand

Figure 7:
An illustration of BP for a routing game over the Wheatstone network, Figure
2
, with and without edge
e_{5}
present and with costs defined by (
12
).

A well-known phenomenon in the context of traffic networks is
Braess’s paradox
(BP). A routing game is said to be subject to this paradox when removal of a path from the network leads to lower cost for all participants. The classical example is the one discussed in Example
a
, considering the Wheatstone network depicted in Figure
2
, where the cost functions are given by (
11
). Consequently, path-cost functions are given by (
12
), and we have an explicit expression for the WE in (
13
). Using these expressions, we find that
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(1)=2
. Alternatively, we can look at the same network, but with edge
e_{5}
removed. The cost functions remain the same, but the path
p_{3}
is no longer accessible. In this case the WE is given by
\widetilde{f}^{D}=(\frac{1}{2},\enskip\frac{1}{2})^{\top}
, and we have
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(1)=1.5
. Notice that for this level of demand,
D=1
, removing
e_{5}
has improved the situation for all participants.

A natural question now is “What happens for other levels of demand?”. For the Wheatstone network, the answer to this question is known to some extent. It has been shown that for this network, using affine cost functions, the associated routing game is subject to Braess’s paradox on at most a bounded set of demands; i.e., there is an upper bound on the demand above which the routing game is no longer subject to the paradox
[
18
,
19
]
. Still, it is useful to study the given example in a bit more detail to see what happens for different levels of demand. We will therefore analyze this example further. We give explicit expressions for
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)
and
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)
, and show that there exists demand where the presence of path
p_{3}
is strictly beneficial.

Example 5.1
.

(Evolution of BP):

(a)

For the Wheatstone network (Example
a
and Figure
2
)with the edge
e_{5}
present we have

\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)=\begin{cases}2D\quad&\text{if }0\leq D\leq 1,\\
2\quad&\text{if }1\leq D\leq 2,\\
\frac{D}{2}+1\quad&\text{if }2\leq D.\end{cases}

For the network without
e_{5}
present, the WE for any
D\geq 0
is given by

\widetilde{f}^{D}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{D}{2},&\frac{D}{2}\end{array}\right)^{\top},

which gives us

\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)=\frac{D}{2}+1.

The situation is illustrated in Figure
7
.
We see that the presence of path
p_{3}
is beneficial when
{0\leq D<\frac{2}{3}}
, detrimental when
\frac{2}{3}<D<2
, and neutral when
D\in\{\frac{2}{3}\}\cup[2,\infty)
. Clearly, the game being subject to Braess’s paradox at one level of demand does not necessarily imply that adding the responsible path is detrimental overall, and the situation warrants further investigation. The aim of this section is therefore to study how the influence of a set of paths causing BP changes as the demand varies.

An illustration of BP for a routing game over the network in Figure
3
, with and without the path
(e_{1},e_{4})
present, and with costs defined by (

).

(b)

We note that classically, Braess’s paradox refers to the situation where removal of an
edge
(or a set of edges) leads to a lesser cost for all participants, but here we will look at a slightly more generalized form of the paradox, where removal of a
path
, or set of paths, from the network leads to a lesser cost for all participants. The previous example shows an instant where these two cases are the same. However, it is possible that a a routing game is subject to an instance of BP that only emerges as the result of removing a (set of) path(s), but not as the result of removing a (set of) edge(s). To show this, we revisit Example
b
. That is, we consider a routing game over the network in Figure

, with path-cost given by (

). Figure

shows the comparison between the cost of this game and the modified game where path
p_{4}
has been removed. Note that the case is essentially identical to that of Example

. Observe now that the game is subject to BP on the interval

, but in this case there is no edge which is responsible for the inefficiency. Instead it is the presence of the path

5.1
Preliminaries for analyzing BP

To ease the exposition of the upcoming analysis, we introduce definitions and useful facts related to the concepts of
necessary sets
,
modified games
and
directions of decrease
.

5.1.1
Necessary sets

The set of all subsets of
\mathcal{P}
that can not be removed from the game without changing the WE at the demand
D
is called the
necessary set
at
D
. Formally, we have the following definition.

Definition 5.2
.

(Necessary sets):

For a given
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C})
we say that a set
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{nec}}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
is
necessary
at demand
D
if

f^{D}_{\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{nec}}}\neq 0\text{ for all }f^{D}\in\mathcal{W}_{D}.

That is, for every WE
f^{D}
, there exists at least one path in the set
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{nec}}
that takes nonzero flow. We use
\mathcal{N}_{D}
to denote the set of all necessary sets at
D
; that is,

\mathcal{N}_{D}:=\{\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{nec}}\subseteq\mathcal{P}\;|\;f^{D}_{\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{nec}}}\neq 0\text{ for all }f^{D}\in\mathcal{W}_{D}\}.

When
\mathcal{S}\notin\mathcal{N}_{D}
we say that
\mathcal{S}
is
unnecessary
at demand
D
.
\bullet

Although at first glance the necessary set may seem closely related to the used set
\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D}
, we note that unlike
\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D}
and
\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D}
, the necessary set is not guaranteed to stay constant in between the breakpoints in
\mathcal{D}
. For instance, in Example
b
we have
\mathcal{D}=\{0,1,\infty\}
. However, we can deduce from the expression given for
\mathcal{W}_{D}
in (
15
) that the singleton set
\{p_{3}\}
is necessary at all demands in
(0,2)
while
\{p_{3}\}
is unnecessary for
D\geq 2
.

5.1.2
Modified games

To better accomodate our path-based notion of BP, we will introduce the concept of a
modified game
. Given a routing game
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C})
, a modified game is constructed by removing a set of paths
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}\subset\mathcal{P}
from the game, which is achieved by forcing the flow on paths in
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}
to be zero, while keeping all cost functions the same. We use
(\widetilde{\mathcal{P}},\mathcal{C})
to denote such a modified game, where
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}=\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}
. We also use the following notation:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{D}

\displaystyle:=\{\widetilde{f}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}^{n}\;|\;\sum_{p\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}\widetilde{f}_{p}=D,\enskip\widetilde{f}_{\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}}=0\},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{D}

\displaystyle:=\{\widetilde{f}\in\real^{n}\;|\;\sum_{p\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}\widetilde{f}_{p}=D,\enskip\widetilde{f}_{\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}}=0\}.

For notational convenience, the dimension of the flows
\widetilde{f}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{D}
of the modified game is kept equal to
n=\left\lvert{\mathcal{P}}\right\rvert
.
For a modified game, we say that
\widetilde{f}^{D}
is a WE when
\widetilde{f}^{D}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{D}
and for all
p\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}
such that
\widetilde{f}_{p}>0
we have

C_{p}(\widetilde{f}^{D})\leq C_{r}(\widetilde{f}^{D})\quad\text{for all }r\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}.

Analogously, we use the notation
\widetilde{(\cdot)}
for other items related to the modified game. We write

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{D}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{SOL}(\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{D},C),

(40)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{vec}}(D)

\displaystyle:=C(\widetilde{f}^{D})\text{ for any }\widetilde{f}^{D}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{D},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D}

\displaystyle:=\{p\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}\;|\;\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{vec}}_{p}(D)\leq\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{vec}}_{r}(D)\text{ for all }r\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}\},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D}

\displaystyle:=\{p\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}\;|\;\exists\widetilde{f}^{D}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{D}\text{ such that }\widetilde{f}^{D}_{p}>0\},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)

\displaystyle:=\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{vec}}_{p}(D)\text{ for any }p\in\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{D}

\displaystyle:=\{\widetilde{f}^{\delta}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\;|\;\widetilde{f}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D}\setminus\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D}}^{\delta}\geq 0,\enskip\widetilde{f}_{(\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D})^{c}}^{\delta}=0\},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Gamma}_{D}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{SOL}(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{D},A).

Similarly, we use
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}
to denote the set of breakpoints of a modified game and use
\widetilde{D}_{i}
to denote the
i
-th breakpoint of
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}
. We write
\widetilde{M}
for the index of the greatest finite valued breakpoint
\widetilde{D}_{\widetilde{M}}
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}
and use
\delta\widetilde{\lambda}^{i}
and
\delta\widetilde{C}^{i}
to denote the directions in which respectively
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}
and
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{vec}}
evolve on the interval between
\widetilde{D}_{i}
and
\widetilde{D}_{i+1}
.

When we need to discuss multiple modified games simultanuously, as is sometimes the case in a proof, we use a similar notation for concepts related to these modified games, replacing
\widetilde{(\cdot)}
with
\widecheck{(\cdot)}
or with
(\cdot)^{\prime}
,
(\cdot)^{\prime\prime}
, or
(\cdot)^{\prime\prime\prime}
. (e.g. we use
\widecheck{\mathcal{P}}
, and
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
,
\mathcal{P}^{\prime\prime}
, and
\mathcal{P}^{\prime\prime\prime}
to denote the related sets of paths, and similarly for the feasible set, WE-cost, etc.)

We note that a modified game is technically not a routing game as presented in Section
2
. The reason for this is that the feasible set has additional restrictions and the definition of WE for a modified game only takes into account a subset of the paths in
\mathcal{P}
. Of course, instead of setting
\widetilde{f}_{\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}}=0
, we could simply drop this set of paths from consideration, and let
\widetilde{f}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}^{\left\lvert{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}\right\rvert}
. In this case we could define
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{D}
without imposing additional restrictions. However, removing a set of paths from a routing game can result in a situation which can no longer be represented by a graph. That is, in that case there does not exists a graph, an associated origin-destination pair and a set of cost functions such that the resulting routing game over all paths from the origin to the destination has the same cost function as that of the modified game. However, the results and proofs in this text are in no way dependent on the existence of such a graph representation for the given set of paths and associated cost functions. Therefore all results that we established for routing games also hold for modified games. We specifically highlight that
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{D}
and
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{D}
, as defined in (
40
), are therefore indeed the set of WE of the modified game and the set of directions of increase of the modified game respectively, as the notation suggests.

5.1.3
Directions of decrease

The results in Section
4
consider the directions of
increase
in which the WE moves as the demand increases, but for some of the upcoming statements it is useful to instead consider the set of
directions of decrease
:

Definition 5.3
.

(Set of directions of decrease):

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C},D)
be given. The set of
directions of decrease

\Gamma^{-}_{D}

is the set of all directions
f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{H}_{-1}
in which the flow can be decreased, starting from some flow in
\mathcal{W}_{D}
, such that the new flow is a WE as long as the decrease is small enough. That is,

\displaystyle\Gamma^{-}_{D}:=\{f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{H}_{-1}\;|\;\exists f^{D}\in\mathcal{W}_{D},\enskip\bar{\epsilon}>0\text{ such that }f^{D}+\epsilon f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{W}_{D-\epsilon}\enskip\forall\epsilon\in[0,\bar{\epsilon}]\}.

Similar to before, we define the set
\mathcal{M}_{D}^{-}
of feasible descent directions as

\mathcal{M}_{D}^{-}:=\{f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{H}_{-1}\;|\;f_{\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D}\setminus\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D}}^{\delta}\geq 0,\enskip f_{(\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D})^{c}}^{\delta}=0\}.

The arguments made in Section
4
can then be repeated to obtain the following modified version of Proposition
4.10
:

Lemma 5.4
.

(Directions of decrease as solutions to a VI):

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C},D)
be given. Then,

{\Gamma_{D}^{-}=\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}_{D}^{-},A)}.

5.1.4
Properties of
V

We recall from Section
3
the function
V
, and similarly define the function
\widetilde{V}
for a modified game as

\displaystyle V(D)=

\displaystyle\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{D}}\sum_{e_{k}\in\mathcal{E}}\int_{0}^{f_{e_{k}}}C_{e_{k}}(z)dz,

(41)

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}(D):=

\displaystyle\min_{\widetilde{f}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{D}}\sum_{e_{k}\in\mathcal{E}}\int_{0}^{\widetilde{f}_{e_{k}}}C_{e_{k}}(z)dz.

Using the inclusion
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{D}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{D}
in combination with Proposition
3.3
then yields us the following observations on modified games and necessary sets:

Lemma 5.5
.

(Relations between the original and the modified game):

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C},D)
and
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}\subset\mathcal{P}
be given, and let
V
and
\widetilde{V}
be given by (
41
). The following then hold:

•

V(D)\leq\widetilde{V}(D)
,

•

V(D)=\widetilde{V}(D)
if and only if
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}\notin\mathcal{N}_{D}
,

•

if
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}\notin\mathcal{N}_{D}
, then
\widetilde{f}^{D}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{D}
if and only if
{\widetilde{f}^{D}_{\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}}=0}
and
{\widetilde{f}^{D}\in\mathcal{W}_{D}}
. As a consequence, we then have
{\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)=\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)}
.

A comparison between
V
and
\widetilde{V}
for the Wheatstone network in Figure

, with and without the path
p_{3}=(e_{1},e_{5},e_{4})
present and with costs defined by (

).

The above statements are illustrated in Figure

, which shows the functions
V
and
\widetilde{V}
for the routing games considered in Example

. Here, the path removed from

to form the modified game is
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}=\{p_{3}\}
. Notice that for demand in the interval

, the singleton set

is a necessary set and as expected, we have

for all

5.2
Evolution of WE-costs

Our upcoming discussion on BP depends for a large part on analysis of the WE-cost
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}
. We show how knowledge on the evolution of this cost can be leveraged to detect when a game is subject to BP. In particular, we look at how the slope of
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}
changes at the breakpoints in
\mathcal{D}
. Since
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}
is potentially non-differentiable at these points, it is useful to consider both the left-hand and right-hand side derivatives:

\delta\lambda^{+}(T):=\frac{\partial^{+}}{\partial D}\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)\Big|_{D=T},\quad\delta\lambda^{-}(T):=\frac{\partial^{-}}{\partial D}\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)\Big|_{D=T}.

Note that whenever
T\notin\mathcal{D}
, we have
\delta\lambda^{+}(T)=\delta\lambda^{-}(\bar{D})
. Using Proposition
4.12
with
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{D}
and
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}^{-}_{D}
, we then have the following observation:

Corollary 5.6
.

(Relation between
\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}_{D},A)
and slope of
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}
):

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C},D)
be given. We have

\displaystyle\delta\lambda^{+}(D)

\displaystyle=\min_{r\in\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D}}A_{r}f^{\delta}\quad\hskip 10.0pt\text{for all }f^{\delta}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}_{D},A),

\displaystyle\delta\lambda^{-}(D)

\displaystyle=-\min_{r\in\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D}}A_{r}f^{\delta}\quad\text{for all }f^{\delta}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}_{D}^{-},A).

We see that sets of the form
\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}_{D},A)
are important when studying
\delta\lambda^{+}(D)
and
\delta\lambda^{-}(D)
. Our next result therefore concerns sets of this form and derives properties that will used in studying the evolution of cost.

Lemma 5.7
.

(Properties of
\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M},A)
):

Let
\mathcal{P}
,
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{K}
, and sets
\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{R},\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}},\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
satisfying
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}
be given. In addition, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle:=\{f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}\;|\;f^{\delta}_{\mathcal{Q}\setminus\mathcal{R}}\geq 0,\quad f^{\delta}_{\mathcal{Q}^{c}}=0\},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle:=\{f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}\;|\;f^{\delta}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}\setminus\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}\geq 0,\quad f^{\delta}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}^{c}}=0\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{-}

\displaystyle:=\{f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{H}_{-1}\;|\;f^{\delta}_{\mathcal{Q}\setminus\mathcal{R}}\geq 0,\quad f^{\delta}_{\mathcal{Q}^{c}}=0\},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{-}

\displaystyle:=\{f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{H}_{-1}\;|\;f^{\delta}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}\setminus\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}\geq 0,\quad f^{\delta}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}^{c}}=0\}.

For given
f^{\delta}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M},A)
,
\widetilde{f}^{\delta}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}},A)
,
{f^{\delta-}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}^{-},A)}
and
{\widetilde{f}^{\delta-}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{-},A)}
,
if
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
(respectively,
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{-}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{-})
then we have

\displaystyle\min_{r\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}}A_{r}\widetilde{f}^{\delta}

\displaystyle\geq\min_{r\in\mathcal{Q}}A_{r}f^{\delta},

\displaystyle\text{(respectively},\quad\min_{r\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}}A_{r}\widetilde{f}^{\delta-}

\displaystyle\leq\min_{r\in\mathcal{Q}}A_{r}f^{\delta-}).

Proof.

We first prove the result for the case
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
. For
f^{\delta}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M},A)
, let
p\in\mathcal{P}
be a path satisfying
f^{\delta}_{p}\neq 0
. Then, either
f^{\delta}_{p}>0
or
p\in\mathcal{R}
. For both these cases, from Proposition
4.12
, we have
A_{p}f^{\delta}=\min_{r\in\mathcal{Q}}A_{r}f^{\delta}
. Using this and the fact that
{f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}}
and
f^{\delta}_{\mathcal{Q}^{c}}=0
, we obtain
{(f^{\delta})^{\top}Af^{\delta}=\min_{r\in\mathcal{Q}}A_{r}f^{\delta}}
. Similarly we find
(\widetilde{f}^{\delta})^{\top}A\widetilde{f}^{\delta}=\min_{r\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}}A_{r}\widetilde{f}^{\delta}
.
Assume for the sake of contradiction that

\min_{r\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}}A_{r}\widetilde{f}^{\delta}<\min_{r\in\mathcal{Q}}A_{r}f^{\delta}.

(42)

Since
f^{\delta}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M},A)
and
\widetilde{f}^{\delta}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
, we have

(f^{\delta})^{\top}A(\widetilde{f}^{\delta}-f^{\delta})\geq 0,

(43)

We now have the following derivation

\displaystyle(\widetilde{f}^{\delta})^{\top}A\widetilde{f}^{\delta}=\min_{r\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}}A_{r}\widetilde{f}^{\delta}<\min_{r\in\mathcal{Q}}A_{r}f^{\delta}

\displaystyle=(f^{\delta})^{\top}Af^{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq(f^{\delta})^{\top}A\widetilde{f}^{\delta},

where the first inequality is due to (
42
) and the second inequality follows from (
43
).
The above implies

(\widetilde{f}^{\delta})^{\top}A(f^{\delta}-\widetilde{f}^{\delta})>0.

(44)

On the other hand, since
A
is positive semi-definite

(\widetilde{f}^{\delta}-f^{\delta})^{\top}A(f^{\delta}-\widetilde{f}^{\delta})\leq 0.

(45)

Expanding the left-hand side of this inequality and using (
43
), we obtain
{(\widetilde{f}^{\delta})^{\top}A(f^{\delta}-\widetilde{f}^{\delta})\leq 0}
, which contradicts (
44
). Therefore the premise is false and the proof concludes. For the case
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{-}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{-}
, the result can be proven using the same arguments, where the direction of the inequality reverses since
f^{\delta-}
is in
\mathcal{H}_{-1}
rather than
\mathcal{H}_{1}
.
∎

Using the above result, we obtain the following:

Lemma 5.8
.

(Slope of
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}
for constant used and active set):

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C})
,
D_{i}\in\mathcal{D}
and
i\in[M]
be given. Then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i-1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D_{i}}\Rightarrow\delta\lambda^{i-1}>\delta\lambda^{i},\qquad\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i}=\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D_{i}}\Rightarrow\delta\lambda^{i-1}<\delta\lambda^{i},

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i-1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D_{i}}\Rightarrow\delta\lambda^{i-1}<\delta\lambda^{i},\qquad\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i}=\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D_{i}}\Rightarrow\delta\lambda^{i-1}>\delta\lambda^{i}.

Proof.

We start with the claim for the case
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i-1}=\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D_{i}}
. From Corollary
4.7
we have
\delta\lambda^{i-1}=\min_{r\in\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i-1}}\delta C^{i-1}_{r}
. Now let
D\in(D_{i-1},D_{i})
. From Proposition
4.6
we then have
\delta C^{i-1}_{r}=Af^{\delta}
for any
{f^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D}}
. Furthermore, Proposition
4.10
gives
{\Gamma_{D}=\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}_{D},A)}
and it follows from the definition of
\mathcal{M}_{D}
and Corollary
3.6
that

\mathcal{M}_{D}=\{f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}\;|\;f^{\delta}_{\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i-1}\setminus\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i-1}}\geq 0,\enskip f^{\delta}_{(\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i-1})^{c}}=0\}.

In summary, we found that
\delta\lambda^{i-1}=\min_{r\in\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i-1}}A_{r}f^{\delta}
, where
{f^{\delta}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}_{D},A)}
, and
\mathcal{M}_{D}
is given by the above equality.

We can obtain a similar result for
\delta\lambda^{i}
. From Proposition
4.6
we have
{\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(T)=\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(D_{i})+\delta C^{i}}
for any
T\in[D_{i},D_{i+1}]
, and from Corollary
4.7
we have
{\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(T)=\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D_{i})+(T-D_{i})\delta\lambda^{i}}
. Since the WE
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(T)
is the minimum of all
\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(T)
it follows that it is equal to the costs of those paths that have minimum cost under WE at demand
D_{i}
, and that keep minimal cost as
\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}
moves in the direction of
\delta C^{i}
. In other words,
\delta\lambda^{i}=\min_{r\in\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D_{i}}}\delta C^{i}
.
Proposition
4.6
also gives
\delta C^{i}=Af^{\delta}
for any
f^{\delta}\in\Gamma_{D_{i}}
, and Proposition
4.10
gives
\Gamma_{D_{i}}=\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}_{D_{i}},A)
. In summary, we found that
\delta\lambda^{i}=\min_{r\in\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D_{i}}}A_{r}f^{\delta}
, where
{f^{\delta}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}_{D_{i}},A)}
, and
\mathcal{M}_{D_{i}}
is given by

\mathcal{M}_{D_{i}}=\{f^{\delta}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}\;|\;f^{\delta}_{\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D_{i}}\setminus\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D_{i}}}\geq 0,\enskip f^{\delta}_{(\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D_{i}})^{c}}=0\}.

By assumption we have
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i-1}=\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D_{i}}
, and from Lemma
4.3
we have
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i-1}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D_{i}}
. Therefore we have
\mathcal{M}_{D}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{D_{i}}
. The rest of the proof follows in the same manner as the proof of Lemma
5.7
, with
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{D_{i}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}=\mathcal{M}_{D}
.
The only difference is that the inequalities (
42
) and (
44
) are non-strict while the inequality in (
45
) is strict, therefore preserving the contradiction. To see that (
45
) holds strictly, note that it holds with equality only if
Af^{\delta}=A\widetilde{f}^{\delta}
. However, in our case we have
Af^{\delta}=\delta C^{i}
and
A\widetilde{f}^{\delta}=\delta C^{i-1}
. Thus equality of (
45
) would imply
\delta C^{i}=\delta C^{i-1}
, which contradicts Proposition
4.6
.

This completes the proof for the case
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i-1}=\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D_{i}}
. The claim for the case
{\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i-1}=\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D_{i}}}
follows by the same arguments, where we find
\mathcal{M}_{D_{i}}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{D}
instead of
\mathcal{M}_{D}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{D_{i}}
. The other claims can be proven similarly, where we consider
\mathcal{M}^{-}_{D}
and
\mathcal{M}^{-}_{D_{i}}
instead of
\mathcal{M}_{D}
and
\mathcal{M}_{D_{i}}
.
∎

In light of Proposition
4.10
, the above is an intuitive result. For instance, consider the first scenario when
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i-1}=\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D_{i}}
. Pick
D\in(D_{i-1},D_{i})
. Then, we know from Lemma
4.14
that
\Gamma_{D}\neq\Gamma_{D_{i}}
. For this to hold, using Proposition
4.10
, we require
\mathcal{M}_{D}\neq\mathcal{M}_{D_{i}}
. Now focusing on the definition of the direction of feasibility given in (
23
), we obtain
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i-1}=\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D}\neq\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D_{i}}
. However, Lemma
4.3
shows that
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i-1}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D_{i}}
, and so,
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i-1}\subset\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D_{i}}
. Thus, we obtain
\mathcal{M}_{D}\subset\mathcal{M}_{D_{i}}
. This is an important inclusion which shows that the set in which the directions of increase must lie has grown strictly larger as ones moves from the interval
(D_{i-1},D_{i})
to the point
D_{i}
. In other words, there are more paths at
D_{i}
which are feasible for carrying flow. Since the division of flow is a result of the traffic participants trying to minimize their own travel time, it is natural that an increase in options will decrease the “rate” at which the WE-cost grows as the demand increases. A similar reasoning can be deduced for the second case
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{act}}_{i-1}=\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D_{i}}
of Lemma
5.8
, where
there are less options for the evolution of the flow at
D_{i}
than in the interval
(D_{i-1},D_{i})
, and so, the “rate” at which the WE-cost grows as the demand increases becomes larger. We can also see this in Example
a
, when comparing Figures
2
and
7
. At the demand
D_{1}=1
(which is a breakpoint), paths
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
become active, but these paths are not in the active set in the interval
(0,1)
. On the other hand, the used set is same in the interval
(0,1)
and at the point
D_{1}=1
. Thus, from the first implication of Lemma
5.8
, the slope of the cost decreases at
D_{1}=1
. This is depicted in Figure
7
. Conversely, at demand
D_{2}=2
path
p_{3}
leaves the used set and it is therefore no longer possible to decrease the amount of flow on this path. At the same time, we have that the active set is same in interval
(1,2)
and the point
D_{2}=2
. Hence, by the second implication of Lemma
5.8
, the slope of the cost increases, as observed in Figure
7
. Note that this intuition is exactly the one that is defied by Braess’s paradox, where we see that more options increases travel time.

A final observation following Lemma
5.8
is related to Example
b
, in which the slope of
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}
does not change at
D=2
, despite this being a breakpoint. From Lemma
5.8
we see that this can only happen when both the active and used set change simultaneously at the considered breakpoint. That is, there needs to be both a path that loses all flow, and there must be a previously inactive path that becomes active. The slope of
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}
then remains constant when the effects on the evolution of
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}
of these changes in the used and active set are exactly opposite.

As a last step before we finally turn our attention to Braess’s paradox, we look at the implications on the evolution of the WE-cost of a modified game when the set
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}
is
not
necessary. We show that in this case the (left- and right-hand) derivative of
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}
upper bounds the (left- and right-hand) derivative of
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}
.

Lemma 5.9
.

(Necessary sets and the slope of
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}
):

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C},D)
and
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}\subset\mathcal{P}
be given. If
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}\notin\mathcal{N}_{D}
where
D>0
, then the following hold:

\displaystyle\delta\widetilde{\lambda}^{+}(D)

\displaystyle\geq\delta\lambda^{+}(D),

\displaystyle\delta\widetilde{\lambda}^{-}(D)

\displaystyle\geq\delta\lambda^{-}(D).

Proof.

We start by proving the first inequality. From Corollary
5.6
we obtain

\delta\lambda^{+}(D)=\min_{r\in\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D}}A_{r}f^{\delta},\quad\delta\widetilde{\lambda}^{+}(D)=\min_{r\in\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D}}A_{r}\widetilde{f}^{\delta},

(46)

where
f^{\delta}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{M}_{D},A)
and
\widetilde{f}^{\delta}\in\operatorname{SOL}(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{D},A)
. Here,

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{D}=\{\widetilde{f}^{\delta}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\;|\;\widetilde{f}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D}\setminus\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D}}^{\delta}\geq 0,\enskip\widetilde{f}_{(\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D})^{c}}^{\delta}=0\}.

Now, since
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}\notin\mathcal{N}_{D}
it follows from Lemma
5.5
that
\widetilde{f}^{D}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{D}
if and only if
\widetilde{f}^{D}\in\mathcal{W}_{D}\cap\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{D}
. Consequently
{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D}\cap(\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}})^{c}}
and in addition we have
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{vec}}(D)=\lambda^{\operatorname{vec}}(D)
, which implies
{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D}=\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D}\cap(\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}})^{c}}
. These facts collectively imply
{\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{D}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{D}}
and it follows from Lemma
5.7
that

\min_{r\in\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D}}A_{r}\widetilde{f}^{\delta}\geq\min_{r\in\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D}}A_{r}f^{\delta}.

Thus, using (
46
) in the above inequality, we obtain

\delta\widetilde{\lambda}^{+}(D)\geq\delta\lambda^{+}(D).

This establishes the first inequality. The second inequality follows by similar arguments, but considering sets of directions of decrease
\mathcal{M}_{D}^{-}
in Lemma
5.7
.
∎

5.3
Conditions revealing Braess’s paradox

Now we are ready to give our first result concerning Braess’s paradox. It states that if a set
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}\subset\mathcal{P}
is unecessary at
D
, then either this set subjects the game to BP at some lower levels of demand, or it is unnecessary for all lower levels of demand.

Proposition 5.10
.

(Sets not in
\mathcal{N}_{D}
are “non-essential” for lower demands):

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C},D)
and
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}\subset\mathcal{P}
be given. If
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}\notin\mathcal{N}_{D}
then exactly one of the following holds:

•

\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}\notin\mathcal{N}_{T}
for all
T\in[0,D]
.

•

There exists
D^{-},D^{+}
satisfying
0<D^{-}<D^{+}\leq D
and

\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(T)>\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(T)\quad\text{for all }T\in(D^{-},D^{+}).

(47)

Proof.

First we recall from Proposition
3.4
that
V
and
\widetilde{V}
are continuously differentiable, with
{\frac{\partial}{\partial T}V(T)=\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(T)}
and
\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\widetilde{V}(T)=\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(T)
. Furthermore, since
{\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}\notin\mathcal{N}_{D}}
, from Lemma
5.5
we have
V(D)=\widetilde{V}(D)
and
{\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)=\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)}
. Define
D^{+}\leq D
as the smallest value such that
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(T)=\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(T)
for all
T\in[D^{+},D]
.
In other words, the derivatives of
V
and
\widetilde{V}
are equal on the interval
[D^{+},D]
. Consequently, since
V(D)=\widetilde{V}(D)
, this implies
V(T)=\widetilde{V}(T)
for all
T\in[D^{+},D]
. By Lemma
5.5
it follows that
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}\notin\mathcal{N}_{T}
for all
T\in[D^{+},D]
. If
D^{+}=0
this gives us
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}\notin\mathcal{N}_{T}
for all
T\in[0,D]
, which corresponds to the first scenario.

Alternatively, if
D^{+}>0
, then it remains to be shown that there exists a
D^{-}\in(0,D^{+})
such that (
47
) holds. First, we note that using the fact
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}\notin\mathcal{N}_{D^{+}}
in Lemma
5.9
gives us
\delta\widetilde{\lambda}^{-}(D^{+})\geq\delta\lambda^{-}(D^{+})
. That is, either
\delta\widetilde{\lambda}^{-}(D^{+})=\delta\lambda^{-}(D^{+})
or
\delta\widetilde{\lambda}^{-}(D^{+})>\delta\lambda^{-}(D^{+})
. The former of these is not possible. To see this, recall that by definition of
D^{+}
, we have
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D^{+})=\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(D^{+})
. Since
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}
and
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}
are continuous, piece-wise affine functions with only finitely many points in which the functions are not differentiable, it follows that if
\delta\widetilde{\lambda}^{-}(D^{+})=\delta\lambda^{-}(D^{+})
, then there exists some
\epsilon>0
such that
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(T)=\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(T)
for all
T\in(D^{+}-\epsilon,D^{+}]
. This however contradicts the definition of
D^{+}
as the smallest value such that
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(T)=\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(T)
for all
T\in[D^{+},D]
. Therefore,
\delta\widetilde{\lambda}^{-}(D^{+})=\delta\lambda^{-}(D^{+})
is not possible and we have
\delta\widetilde{\lambda}^{-}(D^{+})>\delta\lambda^{-}(D^{+})
. This then implies that there exists some
D^{-}>0
such that
D^{-}<D^{+}
and (
47
) holds, completing the proof.
∎

Proposition
5.10
gives us our first way of detecting sets of paths that are at least candidates for subjecting a game to BP. In Example
a
for instance, Figure
2
shows that the set
\{p_{3}\}
is an unnecessary set for any demand
D\geq 2
, and indeed this path subjects the game to BP at a set of demands lower than
2
. Similarly in Example
b
, we deduce from (
15
) that the set
\{p_{1},p_{2}\}
is unnecessary for all demands, and
\{p_{3}\}
is unnecessary for any demand
D\geq 2
. Again, path
p_{3}
subjects the game to BP at lower levels of demand, but the set
\{p_{1},p_{2}\}
is merely “non-essential” in the sense that removing either or both of the paths in this set does not change the edge flow under WE and the associated WE-cost. Note that the converse of Proposition
5.10
does not hold; that is, a set of paths that subjects the game to BP at some demand does not always become an unnecessary set at some higher level of demand. We discuss this further in Example
5.18
.

We note that as a method for finding BP in a given network, Proposition
5.10
has drawbacks. It does not differentiate between sets of paths that subject the game to BP and sets of paths that are only unnecessary. In addition, it only tells us about a potential BP at some lower level of demand.
It would be preferable if we could find a condition which guarantees BP at the level of demand under consideration. The next result is the essential observation that allows us to find more efficient ways of detecting BP. First, we require the following definition.

Definition 5.11
.

(Affine extension functions):

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C})
and
\vec{\mathcal{P}}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
be given, and consider the game
(\vec{\mathcal{P}},\mathcal{C})
. For
i\in[\vec{\mathcal{M}}]_{0}
, we define the following function:

T\mapsto u_{\vec{\mathcal{P}},i}(T):=\vec{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(\vec{D}_{i})+(T-\vec{D}_{i})\delta\vec{\lambda}^{i},

where
\vec{D}_{i}\in\vec{\mathcal{D}}
. Note that
u_{\vec{\mathcal{P}},i}
is simply the affine function that describes
\vec{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}
on the interval
[\vec{D}_{i},\vec{D}_{i+1})
, but extended to
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
. We call
u_{\vec{\mathcal{P}},i}
an
affine extension
.
\bullet

The next results shows that for any affine extension
u_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}},i}
of any modified game
(\widetilde{\mathcal{P}},\mathcal{C})
, there exists another modified game
(\widecheck{\mathcal{P}},\mathcal{C})
with
\widecheck{\mathcal{P}}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}
that achieves a WE-cost equal to or less than
u_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}},i}(D)
for all
D\leq\widetilde{D}_{i+1}
.

Lemma 5.12
.

(An upper bound on the minimum WE-cost over all modified games):

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C})
be given. For any
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
,
i\in[\widetilde{M}]_{0}
and
D\leq{\widetilde{D}_{i+1}}
, there exists a set
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}_{i,D}\subset\mathcal{P}
such that for the routing game
(\widecheck{\mathcal{P}},\mathcal{C})
, where
\widecheck{\mathcal{P}}:=\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}_{i,D}
, the associated WE-cost, given by
\widecheck{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}_{i,D}
, satisfies

\widecheck{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}_{i,D}(D)\leq u_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}},i}(D).

Proof.

For notational convenience, we proof the result for the case
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}=\mathcal{P}
. The case
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}\subset\mathcal{P}
can be proven using the same arguments.
Let
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i}
be the used-set for the game
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C})
on the interval
(D_{i},D_{i+1})
, and let
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=(\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i})^{c}
. We consider the game
(\mathcal{P}^{\prime},\mathcal{C})
, where
{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}
. Clearly,
(\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i})^{c}
is unnecessary with respect to the game
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C})
on interval
(D_{i},D_{i+1})
; i.e.,
(\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i})^{c}\notin\mathcal{N}_{D}
for all
{D\in(D_{i},D_{i+1})}
. By Lemma
5.5
, the cost under WE for the game
(\mathcal{P}^{\prime},\mathcal{C})
, denoted
\lambda^{\prime\operatorname{WE}}(\cdot)
, then satisfies

\lambda^{\prime\operatorname{WE}}(D)=\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D),\quad\text{for all }D\in[D_{i},D_{i+1}].

(48)

This already shows that

{\lambda^{\prime\operatorname{WE}}(D)=u_{\mathcal{P},i}(D)}
holds on the interval
[D_{i},D_{i+1})
. Therefore, if
D_{i}=0
, the proof would be complete.

Now, assume
D_{i}>0
and note
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\mathcal{R}^{\prime\operatorname{use}}_{D}=\mathcal{R}^{\prime\operatorname{use}}_{T}
for all
{D,T\in(D_{i},D_{i+1})}
. It follows from Corollary
3.6
that there exist
D^{\prime}_{j},D^{\prime}_{j+1}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
such that
{(D_{i},D_{i+1})\subseteq(D^{\prime}_{j},D^{\prime}_{j+1})}
and
\mathcal{J}^{\prime\operatorname{use}}_{j}=\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
. Therefore, using this inclusion and (
48
), we have
\delta\lambda^{\prime j}=\delta\lambda^{i}
. This fact in combination with
\lambda^{\prime\operatorname{WE}}(D_{i})=\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D_{i})
shows that
{\lambda^{\prime\operatorname{WE}}(D)=u_{\mathcal{P},i}(D)}
for all
D\in[D^{\prime}_{j},D^{\prime}_{j+1})
. As before, if
D^{\prime}_{j}=0
, the proof would be complete.

When
D^{\prime}_{j}>0
, note that
\mathcal{J}^{\prime\operatorname{act}}_{j}=\mathcal{J}^{\prime\operatorname{use}}_{j}=\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
. From Lemma
4.3
we then have
\mathcal{R}^{\prime\operatorname{act}}_{D^{\prime}_{j}}=\mathcal{J}^{\prime\operatorname{act}}_{j}
, and it follows from Lemma
5.8
that
\delta\lambda^{\prime j-1}>\delta\lambda^{\prime j}
. This in combination with
\delta\lambda^{\prime j}=\delta\lambda^{i}
and
\lambda^{\prime\operatorname{WE}}(D^{\prime}_{j})=u_{\mathcal{P},i}(D^{\prime}_{j})
shows that
\lambda^{\prime\operatorname{WE}}(D)<u_{\mathcal{P},i}(D)
for all
D\in[D^{\prime}_{j-1},D^{\prime}_{j})
. As before, if
D^{\prime}_{j-1}=0
, the proof would be complete. Note that the conclusions up until this point also give
{u_{\mathcal{P}^{\prime},j-1}(D)<u_{\mathcal{P},i}(D)}
for all
D<D^{\prime}_{j}
.

If
D^{\prime}_{j-1}>0
, we can define
\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}=(\mathcal{J}^{\prime\operatorname{use}}_{j-1})^{c}
and consider the game
(\mathcal{P}^{\prime\prime},\mathcal{C})
, where
\mathcal{P}^{\prime\prime}:=\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}
. First we show that
\mathcal{P}^{\prime\prime}
is nonempty. To see this, note that
\mathcal{P}^{\prime\prime}=\emptyset
implies
\mathcal{J}^{\prime\operatorname{use}}_{j-1}=\emptyset
, which in turn implies that for all demands
D\in(D^{\prime}_{j-1},D^{\prime}_{j})
and all WE
f^{\prime D}\in\mathcal{W}_{D}
,
f^{\prime D}_{p}=0
for all
p\in PP^{\prime}
. This however means that the
f^{D}\in\mathcal{F}_{0}
, which contradicts the assumption that
D^{\prime}_{j-1}>0
. We conclude that
\mathcal{P}^{\prime\prime}
is nonempty.

We also have
\mathcal{J}^{\prime\operatorname{use}}_{j-1}\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
, and Corollary
3.6
gives
\mathcal{J}^{\prime\operatorname{use}}_{j-1}\neq\mathcal{J}^{\prime\operatorname{use}}_{j}
. Since
\mathcal{J}^{\prime\operatorname{use}}_{j}=\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
we see that
\mathcal{J}^{\prime\operatorname{use}}_{j-1}\subset\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
. Therefore
\mathcal{P}^{\prime\prime}\subset\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
.

We can now apply the arguments made for comparing
\lambda^{\prime\operatorname{WE}}
with
u_{\mathcal{P},i}
to compare
\lambda^{\prime\prime\operatorname{WE}}
with
u_{\mathcal{P}^{\prime},j-1}
, which gives

\lambda^{\prime\prime\operatorname{WE}}(D)=u_{\mathcal{P}^{\prime},j-1}\quad\text{for all }D\in[D^{\prime\prime}_{k},D^{\prime\prime}_{k+1}),

(49)

where
k
is such that
{[D^{\prime}_{j-1},D^{\prime}_{j})\subseteq[D^{\prime\prime}_{k},D^{\prime\prime}_{k+1})}
for some
D^{\prime\prime}_{k},D^{\prime\prime}_{k+1}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime\prime}
. If
D^{\prime\prime}_{k}=0
, the proof is complete, and if
D^{\prime\prime}_{k}>0
, the same arguments as before give

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime\prime\operatorname{WE}}(D)

\displaystyle<u_{\mathcal{P}^{\prime},j-1}\quad\text{for all }D\in[D^{\prime\prime}_{k-1},D^{\prime\prime}_{k}),

\displaystyle u_{\mathcal{P}^{\prime\prime},k-1}

\displaystyle<u_{\mathcal{P}^{\prime},j-1}\quad\text{for all }D\leq D^{\prime\prime}_{k},

where
D^{\prime\prime}_{k-1}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime\prime}
satisfies
D^{\prime\prime}_{k-1}<D^{\prime\prime}_{k}\leq D^{\prime}_{j-1}
. This then shows that the result holds on the interval
[D^{\prime\prime}_{k-1},D_{i+1})
. If
D^{\prime\prime}_{k-1}=0
, the proof is complete. If
D^{\prime\prime}_{k-1}>0
we can again repeat our arguments, to extend the interval on which the statement is shown to hold. Each time we repeat the arguments the set of paths under consideration is a strict subset of the previous considered set (e.g.
\mathcal{P}^{\prime\prime}\subset\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{P}
). Since there are only finitely many paths, we can only repeat the arguments finitely many times, but we can always repeat the argument as long as the used set at the lowest value in the interval where the statement is shown to hold is non-empty. We conclude that after a finite number of repititions the used set at lowest value of the interval where the statement is shown to hold is empty, which implies that demand at this point is zero. Therefore, the proof is complete.
∎

We outline how the above result can help detect BP. Recall from the start of this section that the routing game discussed in Example
4.1
and associated to Figure

is subject to BP when
D\in(\frac{2}{3},2)
. This instance of BP is caused by the set of paths
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}=\{p_{3}\}
. We wish to derive that the game is subect to BP on this interval using Lemma
5.12
, without knowing apriori that we need to construct a modified game by removing the path
p_{3}
. For this, imagine that the WE-cost
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}
of the original game is availabe to us. Observe from the figure that the affine extension
u_{\mathcal{P},2}
is the extension to
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
of the linear map describing
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}
on the interval
(2,\infty)
(in fact,
u_{\mathcal{P},2}
is the same as
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}
).
From Lemma
5.12
we then have that, for any
D\in[0,2)
, there exists some set of paths
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}
which when removed from
\mathcal{P}
yield a routing game with
\widehat{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)\leq u_{\mathcal{P},i}(D)
. However, the figure show that the WE-cost
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}
lies above this line
u_{\mathcal{P},2}
for any

. Thus, we have
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)>\widehat{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)
on the interval
D\in(\frac{2}{3},2)
, which shows that the network is subject to BP on this interval.

Note that we cannot always count on a BP to be revealed by comparing
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}
with functions of the form

. For instance, the BP present in Example

, shown in Figure

, is not revealed in this way, but instead by comparing

with

, where
\vec{\mathcal{P}}=\mathcal{P}\setminus\{(e_{1},e_{4})\}
. The full potential of Lemma

for detecting BP is revealed later in Proposition

, any increase in the slope of
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}
at a breakpoint implies the game is subject to BP at lower levels of demand.

Corollary 5.13
.

(BP revealed by increase in slope of
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}
):

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C})
,
D_{i}\in\mathcal{D}
and
i\in[M]
be given. If
\delta\lambda^{i-1}<\delta\lambda^{i}
, then there exists, for all
{T\in[D_{i-1},D_{i})}
, a set
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}_{T}\subset\mathcal{P}
such that
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(T)<\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(T)
, where
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(T)
is the WE-cost at demand
T
for the modified game formed by removing paths in
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}_{T}
.

Proof.

The result follows from Lemma
5.12
after noting that
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D_{i})=u_{\emptyset,i}(D_{i})
and that for
D\in(D_{i-1},D_{i})
we have

\displaystyle\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D_{i})+(D-D_{i})\delta\lambda^{i-1},

\displaystyle u_{\mathcal{P},i}(D)

\displaystyle=u_{\emptyset,i}(D_{i})+(D-D_{i})\delta\lambda^{i}.

∎

Lemma
5.12
and the above reveal potentially useful ways of detecting BP, however, they rely on investigating the WE-cost for multiple levels of demand. The following result finally gives us a sufficient condition for a game to be subject to BP at one level of demand
D
, which requires no investigation of modified games, or of the same game at multiple levels of demand.

Proposition 5.14
.

(Paths losing flow reveals BP):

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C},D)
be given. If
{\Gamma_{D}\cap\mathcal{F}_{1}=\emptyset}
, then there exists a set
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}
such that
{\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)<\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)}
.

Proof.

Let
D
be such that
\Gamma_{D}\cap\mathcal{F}_{1}=\emptyset
. In addition, let
D_{i},D_{i+1}\in\mathcal{D}
be such that
D\in[D_{i},D_{i+1})
and let
{\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}:=(\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i})^{c}}
. We consider the modified game over the set of paths
{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}:=\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}=\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i}}
. Note that by definition
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}\notin\mathcal{N}_{T}
for all
T\in[D_{i},D_{i+1})
and so, by Lemma
5.5
, we therefore have

\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(T)=\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(T)
for all
T\in[D_{i},D_{i+1}]
. Consequently, for proving the result, it suffices to show that there exists a set
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\subset\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}
such that
\widehat{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)<\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)
.

Our first aim is to show that for the game defined over the set of paths
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}:=\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i}
we have
D<\widetilde{D}_{\widetilde{M}}
; that is,
D
does not lie in the “final” interval of the modified game. This we do by proving that
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{D}\cap\mathcal{F}_{1}=\emptyset
. Indeed this is enough as from Proposition
4.15
, if
D\geq\widetilde{D}_{\widetilde{M}}
, then
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{D}\cap\operatorname{SOL}(\mathcal{F}_{1},A)
is nonempty.

Note that we have
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}\notin\mathcal{N}_{D}
from above. Consequently, by Lemma
5.5
, we have
\widetilde{f}^{D}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{D}
if and only if
\widetilde{f}^{D}\in\mathcal{W}_{D}
and
\widetilde{f}^{D}_{\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}}=0
. Now pick
\widetilde{f}^{D}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{D}
and
\widetilde{f}^{\delta}\in\widetilde{\Gamma}_{D}
, such that
\widetilde{f}^{D}+\epsilon\widetilde{f}^{\delta}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{D+\epsilon}
as long as
\epsilon
is small enough. For any
\epsilon>0
that then also satisfies
D+\epsilon\in(D,D_{i+1})
we thus obtain a WE of the modified game over the set
\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i}
at demand
D+\epsilon
. Note that we have
(\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i})^{c}\notin\mathcal{N}_{D}
and
(\mathcal{J}^{\operatorname{use}}_{i})^{c}\notin\mathcal{N}_{D+\epsilon}
. It follows from Lemma
5.5
that

5.4
Implications for Braess’s paradox

In the final part of this section, we discuss how the results we have obtained on BP show that even when a game is known to be subject to BP, one should be careful in drawing the conclusion that the responsible set of paths is better removed. We start from Proposition

The arguments for proving this are similar to those for Proposition

\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}
and

are the derivatives of

and

for all

The above result lends us a different perspective on Braess’ paradox. We have already seen that the phenomenon is highly dependent on the demand, but this corollary shows that even though addition of a set of paths may increase the travel-time of all participants at one level of demand, looking at a more complete picture, we see that the same set of paths must have decreased travel time for some lower level of demand. When making the decision to keep or remove a set of paths from a network it would thus be helpful to consider the effect of those paths on the network for the entire range of demands in which the network is planned to function.

This leads naturally to the question of how to quantify the value of a path to the network while considering a range of demands. We first consider the following function

J(D)=\int_{0}^{D}\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(z)-\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(z)dz

\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}
to the network on the range of demands from zero to
D

Proposition 5.20
.

(Benefits of a path using a simple measure):

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C},D)
and
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}\subset\mathcal{P}
be given. We have

J(D)\geq 0\text{ for all }D\in[0,\infty),

with
J(D)=0
if and only if
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}\notin\mathcal{N}_{D}
.

Proof.

Since
\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)=V^{\prime}(D)
and
{\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(D)=\widetilde{V}^{\prime}(D)}
we get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{D}\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(z)-\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(z)dz

\displaystyle=\widetilde{V}(z)|_{0}^{D}-V(z)|_{0}^{D}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{V}(D)-V(D)

\displaystyle\geq 0,

with equality holding if and only if
\widetilde{V}(D)=V(D)
. Using Lemma
5.5
the proof is finished.
∎

Using this measure, we note that in the worst-case scenario, the set
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}
is “neutral” to the network, which only occurs when
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}
is unnecessary at demand
D
. If this is not the case, then the presence of
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}
is strictly beneficial to some degree.

As mentioned, this may be a fairly naive measure. Even when not knowing anything about the levels of demand that a network is likely to carry, we may weigh certain levels more than others. A reasonable approach may be to weigh each level of demand by that amount of demand, as it is more important for the system to perform well when demand is high than when demand is low. In this case we have the measure

W(D)=\int_{0}^{D}z\big(\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(z)-\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(z)\big)dz.

For which we have the following result:

Proposition 5.21
.

(Detriments of a path using a measure weighed by demand):

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C},D)
and
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}\subset\mathcal{P}
be given, where
D>0
. If
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}\notin\mathcal{N}_{D}
then

with equality holding if and only if
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}\notin\mathcal{N}_{T}
for all
T\in(0,D)
.

Proof.

The result can be obtained by integration by parts:

\displaystyle W(D)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{D}z\big(\widetilde{\lambda}^{\operatorname{WE}}(z)-\lambda^{\operatorname{WE}}(z)\big)dz

\displaystyle=z\big(\widetilde{V}(z)-V(z)\big)|_{0}^{D}-\int_{0}^{D}\widetilde{V}(z)-V(z)dz

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{D}V(z)-\widetilde{V}(z)dz.

Here the last equality follows since
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}\notin\mathcal{N}_{D}
and therefore we have, by Lemma
5.5
, that
V(D)=\widetilde{V}(D)
. From the same lemma we know that
V(T)\leq\widetilde{V}(T)
for all
{T\in[0,D]}
and it follows that we end up with an integral over a nonpositive function. This is only equal to the zero function if
{\widetilde{V}(T)=V(T)}
for all
T\in(0,D)
, which happens, by Lemma
5.5
, if and only if
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}
is unnecessary for all
T\in(0,D)
. This completes the proof.
∎

Thus, if we use this measure, and we look at a range of demands from zero to a demand at which the set
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}
is unnecessary, then it follows that
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}
is at best “neutral” to the network performance, and this best case scenario only occurs when
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}
is not used, that is, the set is unnecessary for the whole range of considered demands.

6
Conclusion

We have studied non-atomic, single origin-destination routing games with affine, non-decreasing cost functions on the edges, and how changes in the demand of such a game affect the set of Wardrop equilibria. We have characterized the set of directions in which the set of WE varies as the set of solutions of a variational inequality problem. Subsequently, we have used this characterization and related results to obtain various easy-to-check sufficient conditions for the presence of Braess’s paradox in a network, as well as a necessary and sufficient condition that can be usefully employed, but is computationally intractable to check in full. We have also shown that any set of paths responsible for BP at some level of demand must at other levels of demand strictly reduce the WE-cost. Using two measures on the value of a set of paths we have shown that even when a set of paths is observed to cause BP at some demand, removal of that set from the network could still be detrimental to the performance of the network overall.

For future work, we aim to investigate to what extend these results can be generalized to routing games with more general cost functions, and to networks with multiple origin-destination pairs. There are also several open questions whose answers could help with the efficient implementations of methods for detecting BP on the basis of our presented results. Among those is the question if and to what extent there exist efficient ways of selecting subsets
\mathcal{S}^{\texttt{rem}}
that when used in Proposition
5.15
supply the tighest possible upper bounds on the WE-cost of the original routing game. Finding such tightest upper bounds would equate to fully revealing the presence of BP, so this may be challenging but worthwhile line of inquiry.

T\in(D_{i},D_{i+1})
and
D^{\prime}
can be written as a convex combination of
T
and
D
. That is,
D^{\prime}=\mu D+(1-\mu)T
for some
\mu\in(0,1)
. Since
p\in\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D}
, there exists
f^{D}\in\mathcal{W}_{D}
such that
f^{D}_{p}>0
. Furthermore,
f^{T}_{p}\geq 0
for any
f^{T}\in\mathcal{W}_{T}
. Since
\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D}=\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{T}
, from Lemma
3.5
-2, there exists
f^{D^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{W}_{D^{\prime}}
such that
f^{D^{\prime}}=\mu f^{D}+(1-\mu)f^{T}
and so
f^{D^{\prime}}_{p}>0
. Thus,
p\in\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D^{\prime}}
. Since
D
and
D^{\prime}
were selected arbitrarily, we conclude that the used set remains the same in the interval
(D_{i},D_{i+1})
.

Now, for
D_{i},D_{i+1},D_{j},D_{j+1}\in\mathcal{D}
with
i\neq j
let
D^{-}\in(D_{i},D_{i+1})
and
{D^{+}\in(D_{j},D_{j+1})}
. Note that therefore
\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D^{-}}\neq\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D^{+}}
. For the sake of contradiction assume that
\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D^{-}}=\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D^{+}}
. For any
p\in\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D^{-}}
we know that
{C_{p}(f^{D^{-}})\leq C_{r}(f^{D^{-}})}
for all
r\in\mathcal{P}
and
f^{D^{-}}\in\mathcal{W}_{D^{-}}
. Similarly
C_{p}(f^{D^{+}})\leq C_{r}(f^{D^{+}})
for all
r\in\mathcal{P}
and
f^{D^{+}}\in\mathcal{W}_{D^{+}}
. Since
C(\cdot)
is an affine function (see (
4
)) it follows that for
f^{T}=\operatorname{coco}_{\mu}(f^{D^{-}},f^{D^{+}})
with
f^{D^{-}}\in\mathcal{W}_{D^{-}}
,
f^{D^{+}}\in\mathcal{W}_{D^{+}}
,
\mu\in[0,1]
and
T=\operatorname{coco}_{\mu}(D^{-},D^{+})
we have
C_{p}(f^{T})\leq C_{r}(f^{T})
for all
p\in\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D^{-}}
. Also note that
f^{T}_{p}>0
implies
f^{D^{-}}_{p}>0
or
f^{D^{+}}_{p}>0
, which in turn gives us
p\in\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D^{-}}
.
In other words
f^{T}
is a WE. Since
\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D^{-}}\neq\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D^{+}}
we can without loss of generality pick
r\in\mathcal{P}
such that
r\in\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D^{-}}
and
r\notin\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D^{+}}
. In other words, the cost of path
p
is minimal at the demand
D^{-}
, but not minimal at demand
D^{+}
. Using (
4
) and the fact that
f^{T}
is a WE it follows that the cost of
r
is not minimal under WE at demand
T=\operatorname{coco}_{\mu}(D^{-},D^{+})
. Since
D^{-}\in(D_{i},D_{i+1})
we can set
\mu
such that
T\in(D_{i},D_{i+1})
. This would then imply that
\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{T}\neq\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{act}}_{D^{-}}
, which contradicts Corollary
3.6
. Therefore our premise is false, which shows that
\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D^{-}}\neq\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{use}}_{D^{+}}
, finishing the proof.
