Consumer Welfare under Individual Heterogeneity
Charles Gauthier, Sebastiaan Maes, Raghav Malhotra

Abstract
Welfare effects of price changes are often estimated with cross-sections; however, such data do not identify demand models with heterogeneous consumers. We develop a method that leverages moments of demand to construct a local approximation to the curvature of the expenditure function, which is essential for welfare analysis. Our method applies to arbitrary cross-sections and delivers a nonparametric approximation to the entire distribution of consumer welfare, robust to unobserved preference heterogeneity. We provide moment- and quantile-based estimation strategies that are simple to implement.

Introduction

Evaluating the impacts of price changes on consumer welfare is essential across a wide range of applications, including tax reforms, trade liberalization, market interventions, and competition policies. Moreover, the large and sustained inflationary pressures caused by the pandemic have renewed economists’ interest in well-established measures of consumer welfare, particularly cost-of-living indices (e.g., see
Fajgelbaum and Khandelwal, 2016
,
Fajgelbaum and Khandelwal, 2016
;
Baqaee et al., 2022
,
Baqaee et al., 2022
;
Atkin et al., 2023
,
Atkin et al., 2023
;
Jaravel and Lashkari, 2023
,
Jaravel and Lashkari, 2023
). While contemporary methodological advances emphasize the importance of accounting for non-homothetic preferences, they largely overlook unobserved preference heterogeneity. Yet, preference heterogeneity is a key driver of differences in inflation rates across income groups
(
Kiss and Strasser, 2024
)
.
1
1

1

Empirical evidence showing that inflation rates are lower for higher-income groups include
Kaplan and Schulhofer-Wohl, 2017
,
Jaravel, 2019
, and
Argente and Lee, 2021
.
Consequently, incorporating preference heterogeneity in cost-of-living indices is essential for accurately assessing changes in between-group inequalities.

In this paper, we introduce a nonparametric approach to estimating cost-of-living indices that imposes no assumptions on individual preferences. By accounting for unrestricted unobserved heterogeneity, specifically non-homotheticity, our approach provides a flexible alternative to traditional parametric models.
2
2

2

Accounting for heterogeneous preferences is crucial in empirical applications, as traditional microeconometric models typically explain only a small fraction of the variation in consumer demand. Moreover, interpreting average demand as that of a representative consumer is valid only under restrictive assumptions
(
Jerison, 1994
;
Lewbel, 2001
)
.

Our approach builds on fundamental concepts from microeconomic theory. More precisely, we first leverage the Slutsky equation and Shephard’s lemma to establish a relation between moments of demand and the curvature of the expenditure function. Then, we use information on this curvature to construct and identify local approximations to welfare changes that remain robust to unrestricted preference heterogeneity. Furthermore, we show that higher-order approximations are not identified. As such, our method provides the best welfare point estimates that can be obtained in cross-sectional data.

The identification challenge arises because higher-order approximations depend on higher moments of the marginal propensity to consume across demands, which cannot be inferred from cross-sectional data. Nevertheless—unlike recent advances that account for unrestricted heterogeneity—our approach not only recovers the entire distribution of welfare effects but also remains computationally feasible in high-dimensional settings with many goods.
3
3

3

For instance, in a setup similar to ours,
Hausman and Newey, 2016
derive bounds on average welfare effects in the case of two goods, while
Chernozhukov et al., 2025
estimate welfare effects in a multi-good setting but rely on panel data.
Hence, our methodology provides a more comprehensive assessment of the impact of price changes across different consumer segments.

To illustrate the simplicity of our argument, consider a population of heterogeneous consumers indexed by
\omega
. Further, let
q^{\omega}
and
h^{\omega}
denote the uncompensated and compensated demand for a good, respectively. By the Slutsky equation, at every price
p
and income
y
, we have

\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[D_{p}h^{\omega}(p,v^{\omega})\mid p,y\right]}_{\text{average substitution effect}}=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[D_{p}q^{\omega}(p,y)\mid p,y\right]}_{\text{average price effect}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[q^{\omega}(p,y)D_{y}q^{\omega}(p,y)\mid p,y\right]}_{\text{average income effect}}.

This shows that the average slope of the uncompensated demand makes it possible to identify average compensated price derivatives. However, because average income effects are nonlinear functions of demand, they are not directly identified. Our key insight is that average income effects can be inferred from conditional heteroscedasticity in demand with respect to income:

\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[q^{\omega}(p,y)D_{y}q^{\omega}(p,y)\mid p,y\right]}_{\text{average income effect}}=\frac{1}{2}\underbrace{D_{y}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[q^{\omega}(p,y)^{2}\mid p,y\right]}_{\text{heteroscedasticity w.r.t. income}}.

This relationship enables the identification of the average substitution effect and, by Shephard’s lemma, the curvature of the expenditure function.
4
4

4

The role of the covariance between demand and the marginal propensity to consume in welfare analysis has been highlighted by
Fajgelbaum and Khandelwal, 2016
;
Baqaee et al., 2022
;
Atkin et al., 2023
;
Jaravel and Lashkari, 2023
. This covariance also appears in
Lewbel, 2001
, where it determines whether the average demand of rational consumers satisfies the integrability conditions. Under standard assumptions, we show that it can be estimated from the variance of demand, which, in turn, can be identified nonparametrically from cross-sectional data.
Since our argument naturally extends to higher moments, we can identify approximations for the entire distribution of welfare effects. Moreover, our argument generalizes to multiple goods, where linear combinations can be leveraged to maintain tractability while fully utilizing cross-sectional information. Besides moment-based identification and estimation, we also provide a convenient quantile-based estimator.

Our findings therefore nuance the non-identification result by
Hausman and Newey, 2016
, who show that even average welfare is not identified from cross-sectional data. We demonstrate that, under small price changes, the entire distribution of welfare is point-identified. In this sense, our contribution lies between envelope-theorem-type approaches—where behavioral responses do not affect consumer welfare—and fully global identification approaches.

Moreover, our methodology enables the decomposition of estimation bias in commonly used homogeneous and homothetic indices into components arising from heterogeneity and homotheticity, providing a more nuanced understanding of the limitations inherent in standard cost-of-living measures.

The core theoretical insights beyond the analysis of consumer welfare extend naturally to other applications. We illustrate the versatility of our approach by allowing for unrestricted heterogeneous preferences in the estimation of elasticities of taxable income (e.g., see
Gruber and Saez, 2002
,
Gruber and Saez, 2002
;
Burns and Ziliak, 2016
,
Burns and Ziliak, 2016
). We overcome the implicit quasi-linear utility assumptions common in existing methods and enable the incorporation of income effects under general forms of preference heterogeneity. Our insights readily extend to other settings where sufficient statistics are used to calculate efficiency or welfare costs
(
Kleven, 2021
)
.

Related literature.

There is a longstanding tradition of estimating consumer welfare nonparametrically from cross sections.
5
5

5

See
Deaton and Muellbauer, 1980
and
Lewbel and Pendakur, 2009
for earlier work, and
Bhattacharya, 2024
for a recent review.
The representative agent approach developed by
Hausman, 1981
and
Vartia, 1983
assumes that all observations come from the same individual. Following this direction,
Hausman and Newey, 1995
provide point estimates for a representative consumer using nonparametric regression, while
Foster and Hahn, 2000
and
Blundell et al., 2003
establish conditions under which these point estimates provide first-order approximations to the true welfare impact. Likewise,
Schlee, 2007
gives conditions on preferences such that welfare estimates from a representative agent approach serve as upper bounds.

Closer to us,
Banks et al., 1996
consider second-order approximations to improve on first-order approaches. However, they do not link moments of demand to the curvature of the expenditure function. Under the assumption that demand is monotone in unobserved scalar heterogeneity,
Hoderlein and Vanhems, 2018
derive point estimates of quantile demands for the two-good case.
6
6

6

This identification assumption is restrictive, as it implies that an individual’s relative position in the conditional distribution of demand remains unchanged when prices or income change—a strong condition when income effects vary widely across individuals.
Under general forms of heterogeneity,
Hausman and Newey, 2016
prove that average consumer welfare is not point-identified in cross-sectional data and provide worst-case bounds when income effects are bounded in a setup with two goods. Although robust, their method may yield wide bounds in cases of uncertain income effect magnitude. Instead, our method enhances the precision of point estimates and applies to setups with an arbitrary number of goods.

Another approach employs revealed preference inequalities to derive bounds on welfare changes in a random utility framework. For instance,
Cosaert and Demuynck, 2018
exploit the weak axiom of stochastic revealed preference to derive bounds for a repeated cross-section of consumers.
Allen and Rehbeck, 2019
;
Allen and Rehbeck, 2020
use the law of demand to establish bounds on welfare changes. Similarly,
Deb et al., 2022
introduce revealed preferences over prices to bound the welfare impacts of price changes.

Finally, we contribute to the recent literature on price indices
(
Fajgelbaum and Khandelwal, 2016
;
Baqaee et al., 2022
;
Jaravel and Lashkari, 2023
)
.

Framework

Our framework allows for unrestricted unobserved heterogeneity in preferences. For ease of exposition, we suppress observed individual characteristics from the notation in the rest of the paper; our results can be thought of as conditional on such characteristics.

Setup and notation.

Let
\Omega
be the universe of preference types. Each preference type
\omega\in\Omega
can be viewed as an individual with preferences over bundles of
(k+1)
goods
\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{++}^{k+1}
, where
\mathcal{Q}
is compact and convex. We assume that preferences are represented by smooth and strictly quasi-concave utility function
u^{\omega}:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathbb{R}
that is infinitely differentiable everywhere.
7
7

7

This ensures that demand functions are infinitely differentiable in prices and income.
Furthermore, we denote prices by
\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{R}_{++}^{k+1}
and income by
y\in\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}_{++}
.

We use Euler’s notation for differentiation such that
D_{x_{1},x_{2}}^{m,n}f(x_{1},x_{2}):=\frac{\partial^{m+n}f(x_{1},x_{2})}{\partial x_{1}^{m}\partial x_{2}^{n}}
. The derivative of a vector function with respect to another vector is expressed in numerator layout such that
(D_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}))_{ij}=D_{x_{j}}f_{i}(\mathbf{x})
. Variables in logarithms are denoted with tildes: e.g.,
\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}:=\log(\mathbf{p})
and
\widetilde{y}:=\log(y)
. The symbol
\cdot
denotes the inner product and
\odot
the element-wise product. Throughout, we use
O
to denote Landau’s big O.
8
8

8

Formally,
f(x)=O(g(x))
means there exists constants
C>0
and
x_{0}
such that
|f(x)|\leq C|g(x)|
for all
x\geq x_{0}
.

Consumer demand.

We consider the standard model of utility maximization under a linear budget constraint. Accordingly, an individual demand function
\mathbf{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y):\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Q}
is defined as

\mathbf{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y):=\argmax_{\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{q}\leq y:\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{Q}}u^{\omega}(\mathbf{q}),

where
\cdot
denotes the standard inner product. For every uncompensated (or Marshallian) demand function
\mathbf{q}^{\omega}
, there exists a compensated (or Hicksian) demand function
\mathbf{h}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},u):\mathcal{P}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{Q}
defined as

\mathbf{h}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},u):=\argmin_{\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{Q}}\{\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{\mathbf{q}}|u^{\omega}(\mathbf{q})\geq u\}.

Observe that both demand functions are linked through the Slutsky equation.
9
9

9

The Slutsky equation is defined as
D_{\mathbf{p}}\mathbf{h}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},u^{\omega}(\mathbf{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y)))=D_{\mathbf{p}}\mathbf{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y)+D_{y}\mathbf{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y)\mathbf{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y)^{\intercal}
.
Also note that, without loss of generality, one can omit the demand and price for the
(k+1)
st good due to Walras’ law. Next, the indirect utility function
v^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y):\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as

v^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y):=\max_{\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{q}\leq y:\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{Q}}u^{\omega}(\mathbf{q}),

and gives the maximum utility level obtained for the budget set defined by
(\mathbf{p},y)
. Likewise, the expenditure function
e^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},u):\mathcal{P}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{Y}
is defined as

e^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},u):=\min_{u\leq u^{\omega}(\mathbf{q}):\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{q},

and gives the minimum amount of income needed to achieve utility level
u
at prices
\mathbf{p}
.

Moments of consumer demand.

To avoid complex tensor notation, it will be convenient to introduce scalar-valued
composite demands
. For any
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, let

\begin{split}q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})&:=\mathbf{t}\cdot\mathbf{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y),\\
h^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},u;\mathbf{t})&:=\mathbf{t}\cdot\mathbf{h}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},u),\\
\end{split}

denote the composite uncompensated and compensated demand, respectively. These composite demands can be interpreted as the projection of demand bundles on the line through the origin defined by
\mathbf{t}
. For any
n\in\mathbb{N}_{++}
, we define the
n
th (non-central) conditional moment of composite (uncompensated) demand as

\begin{split}M_{n}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})&:=\mathbb{E}\left[q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})^{n}\mid\mathbf{p},y\right]=\int q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})^{n}dF(\omega),\\
\end{split}

(1)

where
F(\omega)
denotes the distribution of preference types. In particular, note that we recover moments of (non-composite) demand by setting
\mathbf{t}=(0,\dots,0,1,0,\dots,0)^{\intercal}
.

These moments generalize the concept of the average structural function
(
Blundell and Powell, 2003
)
. Under budget set exogeneity, they are nonparametrically identified from cross-sectional data, as they are conditional expectation functions. Specifically, budget set exogeneity implies that
F(\omega\mid\mathbf{p},y)=F(\omega)
.
While the assumption of exogenous budget sets is strong, it is also standard in the literature on nonparametric identification (e.g., see
Hausman and Newey, 2016
,
Hausman and Newey, 2016
;
Blomquist et al., 2021
,
Blomquist et al., 2021
). To our knowledge, existing theoretical results do not establish identification under unrestricted preference heterogeneity when budget sets are endogenous in cross-sectional settings. However, certain forms of endogeneity can be addressed using a control function approach.

In the remainder of the paper, we assume that every moment exists and is finite. Unless stated otherwise, expectations and moments are always conditional on prices and income. Nevertheless, we refer to such conditional moments as ‘‘moments” for the sake of brevity. Finally, we assume that conditions for the dominated convergence theorem hold such that derivative and integral operators can be interchanged.
10
10

10

That is, we assume that there exists a function
g:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that for all
(\mathbf{p},y)\in\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{Y}
and
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
it holds that
\Big\|\text{vec}\left(D_{\mathbf{p},y}^{m,n}\mathbf{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y)\right)\Big\|\leq g(\omega)
with
\int g(\omega)dF(\omega)<\infty
.

(Moments of) budget shares.

Some of our results are more conveniently expressed in terms of budget shares. Therefore, define the uncompensated and compensated budget shares as

\begin{split}\mathbf{w}_{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y)&:=\frac{\mathbf{p}\odot\mathbf{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y)}{y},\\
\mathbf{w}_{h}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},u)&:=\frac{\mathbf{p}\odot\mathbf{h}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},u)}{y},\end{split}

and their associated composite shares as

\begin{split}w_{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})&:=\mathbf{t}\cdot\mathbf{w}_{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y),\\
w_{h}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},u;\mathbf{t})&:=\mathbf{t}\cdot\mathbf{w}_{h}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},u).\end{split}

For instance, notice that
w_{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\widetilde{\mathbf{p}})
corresponds to Stone’s price index
(
Stone, 1954
)
. Similar to levels of demand, the
n
th moment of the composite compensated budget share is denoted with
W_{n}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t}):=\mathbb{E}[w_{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})^{n}]
.

3.1
Curvature of the expenditure function

Before proceeding to our first main result, we establish a fundamental connection between price and income effects and moments of uncompensated demand.

Lemma 1
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{++}
and
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, it holds that

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})^{n-1}D_{y}q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})\right]&=\frac{1}{n}D_{y}M_{n}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t}),\\
\mathbb{E}\left[q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})^{n-1}D_{\mathbf{p}}q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})\right]&=\frac{1}{n}D_{\mathbf{p}}M_{n}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t}),\end{split}

at all
(\mathbf{p},y)\in\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{Y}
.

Proof.

From the definition of moments of demand, we have

\begin{split}D_{y}M_{n}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})&=D_{y}\left(\int q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})^{n}dF(\omega)\right)\\
&=\int D_{y}q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})^{n}dF(\omega)\\
&=n\penalty\ \mathbb{E}\left[q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})^{n-1}D_{y}q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})\right],\end{split}

where the second equality follows from the dominated convergence theorem and the third follows from the chain rule. The proof for the price effects is analogous, mutatis mutandis.
∎

While straightforward, Lemma
1
serves as the key insight underlying all subsequent results. Indeed, Lemma
1
establishes that cross-sectional data are informative about the (non-centered) covariance between powers of composite demand and the marginal propensity to consume. Information on these covariances will be crucial in reconstructing compensated price responses and conducting welfare analysis. For instance, it implies that the expected income effect is identified from the derivative of the second moment of demand with respect to income:

\mathbb{E}\left[q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})D_{y}q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})\right]=\frac{1}{2}D_{y}M_{2}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t}).

The variance of demand being informative about average income effects has been observed before by
Hildenbrand, 1983
and
Hoderlein, 2011
. Lemma
1
generalizes this key insight to higher-order income effects.

Using Lemma
1
and leveraging the Slutsky equation, we can now identify features of the curvature of the expenditure function. Define

\begin{split}c^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})&:=\mathbf{t}^{\intercal}D_{\mathbf{p}}^{2}e^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},v^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y))\mathbf{t},\\
\widetilde{c}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})&:=\mathbf{t}^{\intercal}D_{\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}}^{2}\widetilde{e}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},v^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y))\mathbf{t},\end{split}

as the curvature of the (log) expenditure function in the direction of
\mathbf{t}
. This curvature captures the sensitivity of the consumer’s cost of maintaining her current utility to price changes in the direction
\mathbf{t}
. Its magnitude plays a central role in approximating money-metric welfare measures such as the compensating variation and the cost-of-living index (see Section
3.2
).

Proposition 1
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{++}
and
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, it holds that

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})^{n-1}c^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})\right]&=\frac{1}{n}D_{\mathbf{p}}M_{n}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})\mathbf{t}+\frac{1}{n+1}D_{y}M_{n+1}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t}),\\
\mathbb{E}\left[w^{\omega}_{q}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})^{n-1}\widetilde{c}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})\right]&=\frac{1}{n}D_{\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}}W_{n}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})\mathbf{t}+\frac{1}{n+1}D_{\widetilde{y}}W_{n+1}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})+W_{n+1}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t}),\end{split}

at all
(\mathbf{p},y)\in\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{Y}
.

Proof.

For clarity, we focus on curvature of the expenditure function; see Appendix
A
for the proof for the log expenditure function. For any
(\mathbf{p},y)\in\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{Y}
, we have

\begin{split}c^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})&=\mathbf{t}^{\intercal}D_{\mathbf{p}}^{2}e^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},v^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y))\mathbf{t}\\
&=\mathbf{t}^{\intercal}D_{\mathbf{p}}\mathbf{h}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},v^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y))\mathbf{t}\\
&=\mathbf{t}^{\intercal}\left[D_{\mathbf{p}}\mathbf{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y)+D_{y}\mathbf{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y)\mathbf{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y)^{\intercal}\right]\mathbf{t}\\
&=D_{\mathbf{p}}(\mathbf{t}\cdot\mathbf{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y))\mathbf{t}+(\mathbf{t}\cdot\mathbf{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y))D_{y}(\mathbf{t}\cdot\mathbf{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y))\\
&=D_{\mathbf{p}}q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})\mathbf{t}+q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})D_{y}q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t}),\end{split}

where the second equality follows from Shephard’s lemma and the third from the Slutsky equation. Therefore,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})^{n-1}c^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})\right]&=\mathbb{E}[q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})^{n-1}[D_{\mathbf{p}}q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})\mathbf{t}+q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})D_{y}q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})]]\\
&=\frac{1}{n}D_{\mathbf{p}}M_{n}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})\mathbf{t}+\frac{1}{n+1}D_{y}M_{n+1}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t}),\end{split}

where the last equality follows from Lemma
1
.
∎

Remark 1
.

Proposition
1
implies that the moments of demand contain information about compensated price responses, as by Shephard’s lemma,

\begin{split}c^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})=\mathbf{t}^{\intercal}D_{\mathbf{p}}\mathbf{h}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},v^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y))\mathbf{t}.\end{split}

For instance, Proposition
1
implies that the average substitution matrix
\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathbf{p}}\mathbf{h}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},v^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y)]
is identified from cross-sectional data, since a symmetric matrix is uniquely determined by its quadratic forms. Indeed, using arguments analogous to those above, one can show that

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathbf{p}}\mathbf{h}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},v^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y)]&=\frac{1}{2}\left[D_{\mathbf{p}}\mathbf{M}_{1}(\mathbf{p},y)+\left(D_{\mathbf{p}}\mathbf{M}_{1}(\mathbf{p},y)\right)^{\intercal}+D_{y}\mathbf{M}_{2}(\mathbf{p},y)\right],\end{split}

where
\mathbf{M}_{1}(\mathbf{p},y)
is the vector of first moments and
\mathbf{M}_{2}(\mathbf{p},y)
the matrix of second moments.
12
12

12

This result follows, for example, by adding the Slutsky equation to its transpose and taking expectations.
All terms on the right-hand side are nonparametrically identified from cross-sectional data.

Remark 2
.

In the often studied two-good case, one can obtain a first-order approximation to
n
th moment of compensated demand at a counterfactual price
p^{\prime}
through the
n
th and
(n+1)
st moments of demand. Using a first-order Taylor approximation around
p^{\prime}\approx p
, we have that

\begin{split}h^{\omega}(p^{\prime},v^{\omega}(p,y))^{n}&=h^{\omega}(p,v^{\omega}(p,y))^{n}+\left[h^{\omega}(p,v^{\omega}(p,y))^{n-1}D_{p}h^{\omega}(p,v^{\omega}(p,y))\right](p^{\prime}-p)+O((p^{\prime}-p)^{2})\\
&=q^{\omega}(p,y)^{n}+\left[q^{\omega}(p,y)^{n-1}D_{p}h^{\omega}(p,v^{\omega}(p,y))\right](p^{\prime}-p)+O((p^{\prime}-p)^{2})\\
\end{split}

Taking expectations on both sides and using Lemma
1
gives

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[h^{\omega}(p^{\prime},v^{\omega}(p,y))^{n}]=M_{n}(p,y)+\left(\frac{1}{n}D_{p}M_{n}(p,y)+\frac{1}{n+1}D_{y}M_{n+1}(p,y)\right)(p^{\prime}-p)+O((p^{\prime}-p)^{2}).\end{split}

3.2
Consumer welfare

Since individual preferences are unobservable, consumer welfare is inherently stochastic from the perspective of the analyst. In this section, we examine how the distribution of consumer welfare can be identified and recovered from cross-sectional data.

Measures of consumer welfare.

We derive results for two widely used money-metric measures of consumer welfare: the
compensating variation
(CV) and the
cost-of-living index
(CLI). Consider a (potentially counterfactual) price change from
\mathbf{p}_{0}
to
\mathbf{p}_{1}
and define the absolute and logarithmic price differences as
\Delta\mathbf{p}:=\mathbf{p}_{1}-\mathbf{p}_{0}
and
\widetilde{\Delta\mathbf{p}}:=\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}_{1}-\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}_{0}
, respectively. Further define the indirect utilities as
v^{\omega}_{0}:=v^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y)
and
v^{\omega}_{1}:=v^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}_{1},y)
, respectively.

The CV represents the amount of income an individual would require after a price change to restore her initial utility level. Formally, it is defined as
13
13

13

For clarity of exposition, we adopt a sign convention for the CV and CLI that differs from the standard textbook definition (e.g., see
Mas-Colell et al., 1995
,
Mas-Colell et al., 1995
).

\begin{split}CV^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}_{0},\mathbf{p}_{1},y)&:=e^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}_{1},v^{\omega}_{0})-e^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}_{1},v^{\omega}_{1})\\
&=e^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}_{1},v^{\omega}_{0})-y.\end{split}

(2)

Likewise, the CLI represents the proportional change in income an individual would require after a price change to restore her initial utility level. Formally, it is defined as

\begin{split}CLI^{\omega}({\mathbf{p}}_{0},{\mathbf{p}}_{1},y)&:=\log\frac{e^{\omega}({\mathbf{p}}_{1},v^{\omega}_{0})}{e^{\omega}({\mathbf{p}}_{1},v^{\omega}_{1})}\\
&=\widetilde{e}^{\omega}({\mathbf{p}}_{1},v^{\omega}_{0})-\widetilde{y}.\end{split}

(3)

Note that both
CV^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}_{0},\mathbf{p}_{1},y)
and
CLI^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}_{0},\mathbf{p}_{1},y)
are positive whenever
\mathbf{p}_{1}>\mathbf{p}_{0}
, reflecting a loss in purchasing power due to a price increase. Conversely, both measures are negative when
\mathbf{p}_{1}<\mathbf{p}_{0}
. We assume uniformly bounded income effects to ensure that consumer welfare is bounded (see Appendix
B.1
).

Identification of local effects.

Building on our earlier result on the curvature of the expenditure function, we now state the central result. The theorem below demonstrates that moments of consumer welfare can be approximated in terms of moments of demand.
14
14

14

Since consumer welfare is bounded, standard results on the Hausdorff moment problem ensure that the distributions of the CV and CLI are uniquely determined by their moments.
The identification argument is constructive, providing a basis for straightforward plug-in estimators.

Theorem 1
.

The
(n+1)
st-order approximation of the
n
th moment of the CV and CLI depend only on the
n
th and
(n+1)
st moment of demand:

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[CV^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}_{0},\mathbf{p}_{1},y)^{n}]&=\mathfrak{M}_{n}^{CV}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})+\mathfrak{B}_{n}^{CV}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})+O(||\Delta\mathbf{p}||^{n+2}),\\
\mathbb{E}[CLI^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}_{0},\mathbf{p}_{1},y)^{n}]&=\mathfrak{M}_{n}^{CLI}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\widetilde{\Delta\mathbf{p}})+\mathfrak{B}_{n}^{CLI}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\widetilde{\Delta\mathbf{p}})+O(||\widetilde{\Delta\mathbf{p}}||^{n+2}),\end{split}

where

\begin{split}\mathfrak{M}_{n}^{CV}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})&=M_{n}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p}),\\
\mathfrak{B}_{n}^{CV}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})&=\frac{1}{2}\left(D_{\mathbf{p}}M_{n}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})\Delta\mathbf{p}+\frac{n}{n+1}D_{y}M_{n+1}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})\right),\\
\mathfrak{M}_{n}^{CLI}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\widetilde{\Delta\mathbf{p}})&=W_{n}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\widetilde{\Delta\mathbf{p}}),\\
\mathfrak{B}_{n}^{CLI}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\widetilde{\Delta\mathbf{p}})&=\frac{1}{2}\left(D_{\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}}W_{n}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\widetilde{\Delta\mathbf{p}})\widetilde{\Delta\mathbf{p}}+\frac{n}{n+1}D_{\widetilde{y}}W_{n+1}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\widetilde{\Delta\mathbf{p}})+nW_{n+1}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\widetilde{\Delta\mathbf{p}})\right).\end{split}

Proof.

For the sake of brevity, we only present the proof for the CV herein; see Appendix
A
for the proof of the CLI. The second-order expansion of the expenditure function for
\Delta\mathbf{p}\approx\mathbf{0}
can be written as

\begin{split}e^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}_{1},v_{0}^{\omega})&=e^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}_{0},v_{0}^{\omega})+D_{\mathbf{p}}e^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}_{0},v_{0}^{\omega})\Delta\mathbf{p}+\frac{1}{2}(\Delta\mathbf{p})^{\intercal}D_{\mathbf{p}}^{2}e^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}_{0},v_{0}^{\omega})\Delta\mathbf{p}+O(||\Delta\mathbf{p}||^{3})\\
&=y+q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})+\frac{1}{2}c^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})+O(||\Delta\mathbf{p}||^{3}).\end{split}

Therefore, using Equation (
2
), the second-order approximation of the CV becomes

CV^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}_{0},\mathbf{p}_{1},y)=q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})+\frac{1}{2}c^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})+O(||\Delta\mathbf{p}||^{3}),

such that for higher powers we obtain

CV^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}_{0},\mathbf{p}_{1},y)^{n}=q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})^{n}+\frac{n}{2}q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})^{n-1}c^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})+O(||\Delta\mathbf{p}||^{n+2}).

gives

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[CV^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}_{0},\mathbf{p}_{1},y)^{n}]&=\mathfrak{M}_{n}^{CV}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})+\mathfrak{B}_{n}^{CV}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})+O(||\Delta\mathbf{p}||^{n+2}),\end{split}

as desired.
∎

In these expressions, the terms
\mathfrak{M}_{n}^{CV}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})
and
\mathfrak{M}_{n}^{CLI}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\widetilde{\Delta\mathbf{p}})
represent the mechanical contributions of price changes to the

\mathfrak{B}_{n}^{CV}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})
and
\mathfrak{B}_{n}^{CLI}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\widetilde{\Delta\mathbf{p}})
capture behavioral adjustments, reflecting the reoptimization of consumer choices in response to the change in prices.

Remark 3
.

Theorem
1
admits further simplification in two empirically relevant cases. First, in the two-good setting, the approximation for moments of the CV simplifies to

\mathbb{E}[CV^{\omega}(p_{0},p_{1},y)^{n}]\approx(\Delta p)^{n}\left(M_{n}(p_{0},y)+\frac{\Delta p}{2}\left[D_{p}M_{n}(p_{0},y)+\frac{n}{n+1}D_{y}M_{n+1}(p_{0},y)\right]\right).

This case is of practical importance because, when only the price of a single good changes, only the demand for that good must be modeled. This substantially reduces the dimensionality of the commodity space
(
Hausman, 1981
)
.

Second, even in the many-good setting, the second-order approximation to the average CV depends solely on the first two moments of demand:

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[CV^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}_{0},\mathbf{p}_{1},y)]\approx M_{1}(\mathbf{p},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})+\frac{1}{2}\left(D_{\mathbf{p}}M_{1}(\mathbf{p},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})\Delta\mathbf{p}+\frac{1}{2}D_{y}M_{2}(\mathbf{p},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})\right).\end{split}

Thus, average welfare effects can be computed directly from estimates of the mean and variance of demand, offering a tractable approach in applied work. Analogous simplifications hold for the CLI.

Non-identification of global effects.

Our local approximations of consumer welfare represent the most informative estimates obtainable in a cross-sectional setting. The inability to identify higher-order approximations stems from the fact that cross-sectional data lack information about how the variance—and higher moments—of income effects vary across demand bundles. While the issue of non-identification was previously noted by
Hausman and Newey, 2016
, Proposition
2
sharpens this insight by formally characterizing the limits of identification.

Proposition 2
.

The
(n+2)
nd-order approximation of the
n
th moment of the CV or CLI is not identified.

Proof.

For clarity, we focus on the average CV with two goods. Suppose the true series expansion of the average CV at a given budget set
(p_{0},y)
is
\mathbb{E}[CV^{\omega}(p_{0},p_{1},y)]=a_{0}+a_{1}\Delta p+a_{2}(\Delta p)^{2}+a_{3}(\Delta p)^{3}
. Extending the argument from the proof of Theorem
1
, recovering
a_{3}
requires identifying
\mathbb{E}\left[D_{p}^{2}h^{\omega}(p_{0},v^{\omega}_{0})\right]
, i.e., the expected second derivative of compensated demand with respect to price. Differentiating the identity
h^{\omega}(p_{0},v_{0}^{\omega})\equiv q^{\omega}(p_{0},e^{\omega}(p_{0},v_{0}^{\omega}))
twice with respect to price and substituting in the Slutsky equation, we obtain

\begin{split}D_{p}^{2}h^{\omega}(p_{0},v^{\omega}_{0})&=D_{p}^{2}q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)+q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)D_{p,y}q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)\\
&\qquad+D_{p}q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)D_{y}q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)+q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)\left(D_{y}q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)\right)^{2}.\end{split}

Taking expectations and interchanging differentiation and integration yields

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[D^{2}_{p}h^{\omega}(p_{0},v^{\omega}_{0})\right]&=D_{p}^{2}M_{1}(p_{0},y)+\frac{1}{2}D_{p,y}M_{2}(p_{0},y)+\mathbb{E}\left[q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)\left(D_{y}q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)\right)^{2}\right].\end{split}

As a consequence of Lemma
B.1
in the Appendix, the final term on the right-hand side cannot be identified from cross-sectional data: two observationally equivalent models can yield different values for
\mathbb{E}\left[q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)\left(D_{y}q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)\right)^{2}\right]
. Consequently, the third-order approximation of the average CV is also not identified.
∎

The proof of Proposition
2
reveals that the source of non-identification in cross-sectional data stems from terms such as
\mathbb{E}\left[q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)\left(D_{y}q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)\right)^{2}\right]
. To understand the underlying issue, note that by the law of iterated expectations, this term can be rewritten as

\mathbb{E}\left[q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)\left(D_{y}q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)\right)^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)\mathbb{E}\left[\left(D_{y}q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)\right)^{2}\mid q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)\right]\right].

The right-hand side represents the (non-centered) covariance between the demand bundle and the second moment of the income effect at that demand bundle. Consequently, the failure to identify the third-order approximation to the average CV arises because cross-sectional data do not provide information on how the variance of the income effect varies across demand bundles.

This negative finding is closely linked to fundamental results in the literature on nonseparable models. A direct application of Theorem 2.1 in
Hoderlein and Mammen, 2007
establishes that, in nonseparable models, cross-sectional data identify local average structural derivatives (e.g.,
\mathbb{E}\left[D_{y}q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)\mid q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)\right]
), but not transformations of these derivatives (e.g.,
\mathbb{E}\left[\left(D_{y}q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)\right)^{2}\mid q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)\right]
). As a result, while moments of compensated price derivatives are identified, moments of second-order price derivatives are not. This reasoning extends naturally to higher-order approximations and the many-good case, mutatis mutandis.

The results developed in Section

extend beyond the direct analysis of moments of consumer welfare. In this section, we briefly explore extensions and alternative applications, including the decomposition of cost-of-living indices and the estimation of taxable income elasticities.

4.1
A quantile-based estimator of consumer welfare

If the objective is to recover the full distribution of consumer welfare, an alternative strategy based on quantile regression can be employed. This approach leverages information in the quantiles of composite demand.
15
15

15

Dette et al., 2016
and
Gunsilius and Sithole, 2025
leverage quantiles of linear combinations of demand to test for consumer rationality.

Let
F_{q}(z\mid\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t}):=\Pr[q^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})\leq z\mid\mathbf{p},y]
denote the conditional cumulative distribution function (CDF) of composite demand, and let the corresponding conditional quantile function be defined by
K_{q,\tau}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t}):=\inf\{z:\tau\leq F_{q}(z\mid\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})\}
for all
\tau\in(0,1)
. Similarly, for budget shares, define the conditional CDF by
F_{w}(z\mid\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t}):=\Pr[w_{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})\leq z\mid\mathbf{p},y]
, and the associated conditional quantile function by
K_{w,\tau}(\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t}):=\inf\{z:\tau\leq F_{w}(z\mid\mathbf{p},y;\mathbf{t})\}
.

Proposition 3
.

Under standard regularity conditions
(
Hoderlein and Mammen, 2007
)
, the CDFs of the CV and CLI can be approximated as

\begin{split}F_{CV}(z\mid\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})&\approx\Pr_{\tau}\left[K_{q,\tau}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})+S_{\tau}^{CV}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})\leq z\mid\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p}\right],\\
F_{CLI}(z\mid\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\widetilde{\Delta\mathbf{p}})&\approx\Pr_{\tau}\left[K_{w,\tau}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\widetilde{\Delta\mathbf{p}})+S_{\tau}^{CLI}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\widetilde{\Delta\mathbf{p}})\leq z\mid\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\widetilde{\Delta\mathbf{p}}\right],\end{split}

where
\tau\sim U(0,1)
and

\begin{split}S_{\tau}^{CV}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})&=\frac{1}{2}\left(D_{\mathbf{p}}K_{q,\tau}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})\Delta\mathbf{p}+K_{q,\tau}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})D_{y}K_{q,\tau}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\Delta\mathbf{p})\right),\\
S_{\tau}^{CLI}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\widetilde{\Delta\mathbf{p}})&=\frac{1}{2}\left(D_{\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}}K_{w,\tau}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\widetilde{\Delta\mathbf{p}})\widetilde{\Delta\mathbf{p}}+K_{w,\tau}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\widetilde{\Delta\mathbf{p}})D_{\widetilde{y}}K_{w,\tau}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\widetilde{\Delta\mathbf{p}})+K_{w,\tau}(\mathbf{p}_{0},y;\widetilde{\Delta\mathbf{p}})^{2}\right).\\
\end{split}

Proof.

See Appendix

.
∎

Proposition
3
may be viewed as a local extension of the result established by
Hoderlein and Vanhems, 2018
, generalized to accommodate multiple goods and unrestricted unobserved heterogeneity. In the two-good case,
Hoderlein and Vanhems, 2018
show that exact individual-level welfare analysis is feasible using conditional quantile demands, under the restrictive assumption that demand is monotonic in scalar-valued unobserved heterogeneity.
16
16

16

Under this assumption, the conditional quantiles coincide with individual demand functions, and consumer welfare can be computed using the method of
Vartia, 1983
.
Proposition
3
demonstrates that a version of this insight carries over to the more general setting with multiple goods and arbitrary heterogeneity, provided that price changes are sufficiently small. In this local setting, the distribution of consumer welfare can be approximated using the average compensated responses of hypothetical consumers located at different quantiles of the composite demand distribution. In particular, we show that all moments of these approximate welfare distributions match those derived in Theorem
1
, up to the same order of approximation. Whether more accurate quantile-based approximations exist remains an open question, which is left for future research.

4.3
Elasticity of taxable income

A central theme in the public economics literature concerns the measurement of the impact of income tax reforms. If a reform is small, a sufficient statistic approach based on compensated elasticities delivers a good approximation to the welfare cost of taxation
(
Feldstein, 1999
;
Gruber and Saez, 2002
;
Chetty, 2009
)
.
17
17

17

Income effects enter the first-order approximations due to the nonlinear nature of the tax schedule. That is, virtual income (see infra) is affected by price or tax changes.
In practice, however, most studies proceed by means of uncompensated elasticities, implicitly assuming that individuals have quasi-linear utilities (e.g., see
Burns and Ziliak, 2016
and the references therein). When this assumption is unwarranted, this can bias the efficiency estimates. Our method provides a means to account for income effects without increasing data requirements.

Consider a setting with two goods: before tax income
q^{\omega}
and consumption
c^{\omega}
. An individual derives utility from consumption and disutility from before tax income, as the latter requires effort to earn. To allow for non-proportional taxation, the tax schedule is assumed to be piece-wise linear. At a linear part of this schedule, the budget constraint is given by
c^{\omega}=(1-\tau)q^{\omega}+R
, in which
\tau
denotes the (local) marginal tax rate and
R
virtual income.
18
18

18

Following the literature, we abstract away from individuals located at the kinks of the budget set.

Feldstein, 1995
;
Feldstein, 1999
shows that the welfare cost of taxation can be summarized by means of the compensated elasticity of taxable income

\varepsilon^{\omega}=\frac{(1-\tau)}{h^{\omega}(1-\tau,v_{0}^{\omega})}\frac{\partial h^{\omega}(1-\tau,v_{0}^{\omega})}{\partial(1-\tau)},

where
h^{\omega}
denotes compensated demand for before tax income. Similar insights as in Section
3
allow to nonparametrically identify and estimate the average of this compensated price elasticity.
19
19

19

This result is related to the work of
Paluch et al., 2012
, who derive a connection between individual and aggregate income elasticities.

Proposition 5
.

The average compensated elasticity of taxable income can be written as

\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^{\omega}]=(1-\tau)\left(\frac{\partial\mathbb{E}[\log(q^{\omega}(1-\tau,R))]}{\partial(1-\tau)}+\frac{\partial\mathbb{E}[q^{\omega}(1-\tau,R)]}{\partial R}\right).

Proof.

Using similar arguments as before, it holds that

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^{\omega}]&=(1-\tau)\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{h^{\omega}(1-\tau,v_{0}^{\omega})}\frac{\partial h^{\omega}(1-\tau,v_{0}^{\omega})}{\partial(1-\tau)}\right]\\
&=(1-\tau)\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{q^{\omega}(1-\tau,R)}\left(\frac{\partial q^{\omega}(1-\tau,R)}{\partial(1-\tau)}+q^{\omega}(1-\tau,R)\frac{\partial q^{\omega}(1-\tau,R)}{\partial R}\right)\right]\\
&=(1-\tau)\left(\frac{\partial\mathbb{E}[\log(q^{\omega}(1-\tau,R))]}{\partial(1-\tau)}+\frac{\partial\mathbb{E}[q^{\omega}(1-\tau,R)]}{\partial R}\right).\end{split}

∎

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce a novel method to approximate moments of compensated demand through moments of uncompensated demand. In doing so, we show that the latter contain valuable information about the distribution of income effects in the population. Leveraging this insight, we enable more accurate counterfactual exercises in applied welfare analysis. Furthermore, we establish that no better approximations can be derived from cross-sectional data.

Looking ahead, there are several promising directions for future research. First, it would be valuable to investigate the additional identifying power short consumer panels provide for estimating counterfactuals. Initial steps in this direction has been taken by
Crawford, 2019
,
Cooprider et al., 2022
, and
Chernozhukov et al., 2025
. Second, extending our approach to general equilibrium models could improve the measurement of welfare gains and losses from trade, as explored in
Baqaee and Burstein, 2022
. Third, further work is needed to deepen our understanding of welfare analysis in models that depart from standard rationality assumptions. Recent contributions by
Apesteguia and Ballester, 2015
and
Aguiar and Serrano, 2017
provide a foundation for such research. Finally, efficient estimation in high-dimensional settings with many goods remains an open challenge. Advances in high-dimensional statistics offer potential solutions to the curse of dimensionality, a problem further exacerbated by the endogeneity of budget sets. In this regard, building on the results of
Chernozhukov et al., 2019
appears to be a particularly promising avenue for future study.

B.1
Boundedness of consumer welfare

We establish that consumer welfare is bounded when income effects are uniformly bounded based on the argument developed by
Hausman and Newey, 2016
and reviewed in
Hausman and Newey, 2017
. Income effects are said to be uniformly bounded when there exists a finite constant
b
such that

b\geq\max|D_{y}\mathbf{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p},y)|,

where
|\cdot|
denotes the component-wise absolute value and the maximum is taken across all components.

We present the argument for the CV; the case for the CLI proceeds analogously. Building on the insights of
Hausman, 1981
and
Vartia, 1983
, we characterize the CV as the solution to a first-order nonlinear ordinary differential equation. Let
\mathbf{p}(s):[0,1]\rightarrow\mathcal{P}
denote a continuous price path with
\mathbf{p}(0)=\mathbf{p}_{0}
and
\mathbf{p}(1)=\mathbf{p}_{1}
. For simplicity, we adopt the linear path
\mathbf{p}(s)=\mathbf{p}_{0}+s\Delta\mathbf{p}
.
21
21

21

By Slutsky symmetry, the choice of path is immaterial.
Further, define

w^{\omega}(s)=e^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}(s),v^{\omega}_{0})-y,\hskip 9.24994pts\in[0,1],

which measures the CV up to point
s
along the price path. Differentiating with respect to
s
yields

\begin{split}D_{s}w^{\omega}(s)&=D_{\mathbf{p}}e^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}(s),v^{\omega}_{0})D_{s}\mathbf{p}(s),\hskip 9.24994pts\in[0,1],\\
&=D_{\mathbf{p}}e^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}(s),v^{\omega}_{0})\Delta\mathbf{p}.\end{split}

By Shephard’s lemma, the right-hand side simplifies to
\mathbf{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}(s),y+w^{\omega}(s))^{\intercal}\Delta\mathbf{p}
, so the differential equation becomes

\begin{split}D_{s}w^{\omega}(s)=\mathbf{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}(s),y+w^{\omega}(s))^{\intercal}\Delta\mathbf{p},\hskip 9.24994pts\in[0,1],\end{split}

with initial condition
w^{\omega}(0)=0
. Hence, the CV is given by
w^{\omega}(1)
, the solution at
s=1
. Existence and uniqueness of the solution follow from standard conditions, such as Lipschitz continuity of individual demand. The corresponding integral form of the differential equation is

w^{\omega}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\mathbf{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}(r),y+w^{\omega}(r))^{\intercal}\Delta\mathbf{p}dr.

Since income effects are uniformly bounded, it follows that for each
s\in[0,1]

|\mathbf{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}(s),y+w^{\omega}(s))^{\intercal}\Delta\mathbf{p}|\leq|\mathbf{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}(s),y)^{\intercal}\Delta\mathbf{p}|+b|\mathbf{1}^{\intercal}\Delta\mathbf{p}||w^{\omega}(s)|,

where
\mathbf{1}
is a vector of ones of appropriate dimension. Taking absolute values in the integral form and applying this bound yields:

\begin{split}|w^{\omega}(s)|&=\Bigg|\int_{0}^{s}\mathbf{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}(r),y+w^{\omega}(r))^{\intercal}\Delta\mathbf{p}dr\Bigg|\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{s}|\mathbf{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}(r),y+w^{\omega}(r))^{\intercal}\Delta\mathbf{p}|dr\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{s}\Big[|\mathbf{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}(r),y)^{\intercal}\Delta\mathbf{p}|+b|\mathbf{1}^{\intercal}\Delta\mathbf{p}||w^{\omega}(r)|\Big]dr\\
&=\alpha^{\omega}(s)+\int_{0}^{s}\beta^{\omega}(r)|w^{\omega}(r)|dr,\end{split}

where
\alpha^{\omega}(s):=\int_{0}^{s}|\mathbf{q}^{\omega}(\mathbf{p}(r),y)^{\intercal}\Delta\mathbf{p}|dr
and
\beta^{\omega}(s):=b|\mathbf{1}^{\intercal}\Delta\mathbf{p}|
. Since
\alpha^{\omega}(s)
is non-decreasing and
\beta^{\omega}(s)
is non-negative, direct application of Grönwall’s inequality gives

|w^{\omega}(s)|\leq\alpha^{\omega}(s)\exp\left(\int_{0}^{s}\beta^{\omega}(r)dr\right).

Evaluating at
s=1
, we obtain a finite upper bound on
|w^{\omega}(1)|
, establishing that the CV is bounded under the assumption of uniformly bounded income effects.

B.2
Non-identification of higher-order income effects

Lemma B.1
.

\mathbb{E}[q^{\omega}(p,y)(D_{y}q^{\omega}(p,y))^{n}]
is not identified from cross-sectional data, for
n\geq 2
.

Proof.

We establish non-identification by providing a counterexample. Suppose individual demand is linear in price and income:

q^{\omega}(p,y)=\omega_{1}-p+\omega_{2}y,

where
\omega_{1}\sim U(0,1)
, and
\Pr\left[\omega_{2}=1/3\right]=\Pr\left[\omega_{2}=2/3\right]=1/2
.
Hausman and Newey, 2016
show that for
y<3
, an observationally equivalent specification is given by the quantile demand function:

\overline{q}^{\overline{\omega}}(p,y)=\begin{cases}-p+\mathbb{I}[y<6\overline{\omega}](y/2+\overline{\omega})+\mathbb{I}[y\geq 6\overline{\omega}](y/3+2\overline{\omega}),&\overline{\omega}\leq 1/2,\\
-p+\mathbb{I}[y<6(1-\overline{\omega})](y/2+\overline{\omega})+\mathbb{I}[y\geq 6(1-\overline{\omega})](2y/3+2\overline{\omega}-1),&\overline{\omega}>1/2,\\
\end{cases}

where
\overline{\omega}\sim U(0,1)
.

For the budget set
(p,y)=(1,2)
, straightforward calculations yield

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[q^{\omega}(p,y)\left(D_{y}q^{\omega}(p,y)\right)^{n}\right]&=\mathbb{E}[(\omega_{1}-p+\omega_{2}y)\omega_{2}^{n}\mid p=1,y=2]\\
&=(\mathbb{E}\left[\omega_{1}\right]-1)\mathbb{E}\left[\omega_{2}^{n}\right]+2\mathbb{E}\left[\omega_{3}^{n}\right]\\
&=-1/4[(1/3)^{n}+(2/3)^{n}]+[(1/3)^{n+1}+(2/3)^{n+1}]\\
&=1/12(1/3)^{n}+5/12(2/3)^{n}.\end{split}

(4)

However, differentiating the quantile demand function with respect to income yields

\overline{q}^{\overline{\omega}}(p,y)\left(D_{y}\overline{q}^{\overline{\omega}}(p,y)\right)^{n}\mid_{p=1,y=2}=\begin{cases}\begin{split}\mathbb{I}[1/3<\overline{\omega}]\overline{\omega}(1/2)^{n}+\mathbb{I}[1/3\geq\overline{\omega}](-1/3+2\overline{\omega})(1/3)^{n}\end{split},&\overline{\omega}\leq 1/2,\\
\begin{split}\mathbb{I}[2/3>\overline{\omega}]\overline{\omega}(1/2)^{n}+\mathbb{I}[2/3\leq\overline{\omega}](-2/3+2\overline{\omega})(2/3)^{n}\end{split},&\overline{\omega}>1/2.\\
\end{cases}

Evaluating the expectation gives

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\overline{q}^{\overline{\omega}}(p,y)\left(D_{y}\overline{q}^{\overline{\omega}}(p,y)\right)^{n}\right]&=(1/3)^{n}\int_{0}^{1/3}(-1/3+2\overline{\omega})+(1/2)^{n}\int_{1/3}^{1/2}\overline{\omega}\\
&\hskip 9.24994pt+(1/2)^{n}\int_{1/2}^{2/3}\overline{\omega}+(2/3)^{n}\int_{2/3}^{1}(-2/3+2\overline{\omega})\\
&=1/6(1/2)^{n}+1/3(2/3)^{n}.\end{split}

(5)

Expressions (
4
) and (
5
) are equal only for
n=1
. Since two observationally equivalent models generate different expectations for
n\geq 2
,
\mathbb{E}[q^{\omega}(p,y)(D_{y}q^{\omega}(p,y))^{n}])
is not identified for
n\geq 2
.
∎

B.3
Comparison to other approaches

We now compare our approximation to two established methods for estimating consumer welfare. For simplicity, we focus on the average CV in a two-good setting. To streamline notation, we denote our second-order approximation with heterogeneous agents (HA) by

\overline{CV}_{HA}(p_{0},p_{1},y)=\Delta pM_{1}(p_{0},y)+\frac{(\Delta p)^{2}}{2}\left(D_{p}M_{1}(p_{0},y)+\frac{1}{2}D_{y}M_{2}(p_{0},y)\right).

Representative agent approach.

The representative agent (RA) approach assumes that the whole cross section of demand is generated by a single type of consumer. Under this assumption, moments of demand satisfy
M_{n}(p_{0},y)=M_{1}(p_{0},y)^{n}
. Substituting into the second-order expansion yields the RA approximation:

\begin{split}\overline{CV}_{RA}(p_{0},p_{1},y)&=\Delta pM_{1}(p_{0},y)+\frac{(\Delta p)^{2}}{2}\left(D_{p}M_{1}(p_{0},y)+\frac{1}{2}D_{y}M_{1}(p_{0},y)^{2}\right).\end{split}

The difference between the heterogeneous-agent and representative-agent approximations is

\begin{split}\overline{CV}_{HA}(p_{0},p_{1},y)-\overline{CV}_{RA}(p_{0},p_{1},y)&=\frac{(\Delta p)^{2}}{4}\left(D_{y}M_{2}(p_{0},y)-D_{y}M_{1}(p_{0},y)^{2}\right)\\
&=\frac{(\Delta p)^{2}}{2}\text{Cov}\left(q^{\omega}(p_{0},y),D_{y}q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)\right),\end{split}

which depends on the covariance between consumption and the marginal propensity to consume. Intuitively, in the spirit of
Lewbel, 2001
, through conditional heteroscedasticity, our approach corrects for average demand not being compatible with a representative agent.

Bounds-based approach.

An alternative strategy, when information about the magnitude of income effects is available, is to adopt a bounds-based approach.
Hausman and Newey, 2016
pursue this route by bounding welfare effects using only the first moment of demand.

To see how our approximation compares, suppose that individual income effects satisfy the uniform bounds
b^{l}\leq D_{y}q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)\leq b^{u}
for all
\omega
. Define

\begin{split}CV_{B}^{\omega}(p_{0},p_{1},y;b)&=\Delta pq^{\omega}(p_{0},y)+\frac{(\Delta p)^{2}}{2}\left[D_{p}q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)+bq^{\omega}(p_{0},y)\right],\end{split}

such that after taking expectations

\begin{split}\overline{CV}_{B}(p_{0},p_{1},y;b)&=\Delta pM_{1}(p_{0},y)+\frac{(\Delta p)^{2}}{2}\left[D_{p}M_{1}(p_{0},y)+bM_{1}(p_{0},y)\right].\end{split}

(6)

Since
D_{y}q^{\omega}(p_{0},y)\in[b^{l},b^{u}]
by assumption, it follows directly that
\overline{CV}_{B}(p_{0},p_{1},y;b^{l})\leq\overline{CV}_{HA}(p_{0},p_{1},y)\leq\overline{CV}_{B}(p_{0},p_{1},y;b^{u})
.

How informative are these bounds in practice? If the good is assumed to be normal, the worst-case bounds are such that the percentage point gap between the lower and upper bound amounts to approximately half of the price increase.
