Adaptive virtual element methods with equilibrated fluxes
F. Dassi J. Gedicke L. Mascotto

Abstract
We present an h ​ p hp -adaptive virtual element method (VEM) based on the hypercircle method of Prager and Synge for the approximation of solutions to diffusion problems. We introduce a reliable and efficient a posteriori error estimator, which is computed by solving an auxiliary global mixed problem. We show that the mixed VEM satisfies a discrete inf-sup condition with inf-sup constant independent of the discretization parameters. Furthermore, we construct a stabilization for the mixed VEM with explicit bounds in terms of the local degree of accuracy of the method. The theoretical results are supported by several numerical experiments, including a comparison with the residual a posteriori error estimator. The numerics exhibit the p p -robustness of the proposed error estimator. In addition, we provide a first step towards the localized flux reconstruction in the virtual element framework, which leads to an additional reliable a posteriori error estimator that is computed by solving local (cheap-to-solve and parallelizable) mixed problems. We provide theoretical and numerical evidence that the proposed local error estimator suffers from a lack of efficiency. AMS subject classification : 65N12, 65N30, 65N50. Keywords : virtual element method, hypercircle method, equilibrated fluxes, h ​ p hp -adaptivity, polygonal meshes

Introduction

Polygonal/polyhedral methods have several advantages over more standard technologies based on triangular/tetrahedral meshes.
For instance, when refining a mesh adaptively, the use of general shaped elements allows for the presence of hanging nodes and interfaces.
This simplifies the construction of hierarchies of meshes.
Amongst the various polytopal methods, the virtual element method (VEM) has received an increasing attention over the last years; see
[
5
]
.

Adaptivity in the VEM has been applied to several problems: general elliptic problems in primal
[

,

,
11
]

and mixed formulations

]
, Steklov eigenvalue problems
[

]
, the elasticity equations
[

]
, the

[

,

[

In all these references, residual error estimators were analyzed,
whereas equilibrated error estimators have not been investigated so far.

The hypercircle method for the computation of equilibrated error estimators was introduced by Prager and Synge in
[
46
]
; see also
[
3
]
.
The idea behind it consists in constructing an error estimator based on the approximations of the primal
and
the mixed formulations of the problem.
In the finite element framework, a combined error of the primal and mixed formulations is equal to a term involving the gradient of the solution to the primal method
and
the equilibrated flux solution to the mixed method,
up to oscillation terms.

Braess and Schöberl
[
18

, and later Ern and Vohralík
[
34
]
provided a major improvement to the hypercircle method.
They designed an error estimator based on equilibrated fluxes
using the solution to the primal method and a combination of discrete solutions to local (cheap-to-solve and parallelizable) mixed problems.

In the seminal works
[
17
,
18
]
, Braess, Schöberl, and collaborators proved that the equilibrated error estimator is reliable and has an efficiency that is independent of the polynomial degree
p
.
As discussed by Melenk and Wohlmuth
[
40

, this is not the case for the residual error estimator.
The
p
-robustness of the equilibrated error estimator is proven in the discontinuous Galerkin (dG) setting as well; see, e.g.,
[
34
]
and the references therein.
Hence, they are very well suited for
hp
-adaptivity; see, e.g.,
[
32
]
.

Local flux reconstruction techniques have been applied to various problems, e.g., parabolic problems
[
33
]

reaction diffusion problems
[

, the Helmholtz equation
[

28

This paper represents the first attempt to combine the hypercircle method with the VEM.
In particular, we want to dovetail the geometric flexibility of the VEM with the robustness properties of the hypercircle method and the flux reconstruction.
We analyze the hypercircle method for the
h
-,
p
-, and
hp
-versions of the VEM.

The structure and contents of the paper are as follows.
In Section
2

Despite the construction of the two methods is well understood

We show that

•

In Section

Such an error estimator consists of two terms. One is similar to the FEM equilibrated error estimator; the other involves two stabilization terms, typical of the VEM framework.
Due to the several variational crimes of the VEM, we observe a loss of the
p

as well as the loss of the “constant free” nature of the reliability bound.
Numerical experiments are presented in Section

Moreover, we display the performance of the

-hypercircle method based on the Melenk-Wohlmuth’s

-refining strategy presented in
[
40

.

In Section
5

we discuss how to design a reliable error estimator using local VE flux reconstructions.
Amongst the various technical tools needed in the analysis, we provide

•

the design of a high-order virtual element partition of unity, which differs from the standard one introduced in
[
13
,
45
]
;

•

the design of local mixed VEMs, satisfying an equilibration condition on fluxes and a residual-type equation.

Numerical results with this new error estimator are the topic of Section
6

Here, we check that the equilibration condition is fulfilled, which guarantees reliability. However, notably for high-order methods, we have theoretical and numerical evidence that the local error estimator suffers from a lack of efficiency.
This paves the way to other approaches where a deeper analysis on the design of local mixed problems has to be performed.
We draw some conclusions in Section

The model problem.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a polygonal domain with boundary
\partial\Omega
split into
\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{D}\cup\Gamma_{N}
with
\Gamma_{D}\neq\emptyset
and
\Gamma_{D}\cap\Gamma_{N}=\emptyset
.
Denote the outward unit normal vector on
\Gamma_{N}
by
\mathbf{n}
. Let
\kappa
be a smooth scalar function such that there exist two positive constants
k_{*}<k^{*}
satisfying

0<k_{*}\leq\kappa(\mathbf{x})\leq k^{*}<+\infty\quad\quad\text{for almost all }\mathbf{x}\in\Omega.

(3)

The primal formulation.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
g_{D}\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma_{D})
, and
g_{N}\in H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma_{N})
.
We aim to approximate the solution to the problem: find
\widetilde{u}
such that

\begin{cases}-\operatorname*{div}(\kappa\,\nabla\widetilde{u})=f&\text{in }\Omega\\
\widetilde{u}=g_{D}&\text{on }\Gamma_{D}\\
\mathbf{n}\cdot(\kappa\nabla\widetilde{u})=g_{N}&\text{on }\Gamma_{N}.\\
\end{cases}

(4)

Define the spaces

\widetilde{V}_{g_{D}}:=\{\widetilde{v}\in H^{1}(\Omega)\mid\widetilde{v}=g_{D}\text{ on }\Gamma_{D}\},\quad\quad\widetilde{V}_{0}:=\{\widetilde{v}\in H^{1}(\Omega)\mid\widetilde{v}=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{D}\},

and the bilinear form

\widetilde{a}(\widetilde{u},\widetilde{v}):=(\kappa\nabla\widetilde{u},\nabla\widetilde{v})_{0,\Omega}\quad\forall\widetilde{u},\,\widetilde{v}\in H^{1}(\Omega).

(5)

The weak formulation of problem (
4
) reads

\begin{cases}\text{find }\widetilde{u}\in\widetilde{V}_{g_{D}}\text{ such that}\\
\widetilde{a}(\widetilde{u},\widetilde{v})=(f,\widetilde{v})_{0,\Omega}+(g_{N},\widetilde{v})_{0,\Gamma_{N}}\quad\forall v\in\widetilde{V}_{0}.\end{cases}

(6)

The term
(g_{N},\widetilde{v})_{0,\Gamma_{N}}
has to be understood as a duality pairing between
H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma_{N})
and
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma_{N})
.

The mixed formulation.

Define the spaces

\begin{split}&\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{g_{N}}:=\{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in H(\operatorname*{div},\Omega)\mid\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}=g_{N}\text{ on }\Gamma_{N}\},\\
&\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}:=\{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in H(\operatorname*{div},\Omega)\mid\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{N}\},\quad\quad V:=L^{2}(\Omega),\\
\end{split}

and the bilinear forms

\begin{split}&a(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau}):=\int_{\Omega}\kappa^{-1}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\sigma},\,\boldsymbol{\tau}\in H(\operatorname*{div},\Omega),\\
&b(\boldsymbol{\tau},v):=-\int_{\Omega}\operatorname*{div}(\boldsymbol{\tau})v\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}\in H(\operatorname*{div},\Omega),\,\forall v\in V.\end{split}

(7)

We point out that
\widetilde{V}_{g_{D}}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{g_{N}}
are not vector spaces in general.

Consider the mixed formulation of problem (
6
). In strong formulation, it consists in finding
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
and
u
such that

\begin{cases}\kappa^{-1}\boldsymbol{\sigma}=-\nabla u&\text{in }\Omega\\
\operatorname*{div}\boldsymbol{\sigma}=f&\text{in }\Omega\\
u=g_{D}&\text{on }\Gamma_{D}\\
\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}=-g_{N}&\text{on }\Gamma_{N},\\
\end{cases}

whereas, in weak formulation, it reads

\begin{cases}\text{find }(\boldsymbol{\sigma},u)\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{g_{N}}\times V\text{ such that}\\
a(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})+b(\boldsymbol{\tau},u)=-(g_{D},\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau})_{0,\Gamma_{D}}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}\\
b(\boldsymbol{\sigma},v)=(-f,v)_{0,\Omega}\quad\forall v\in V.\end{cases}

(8)

The term
(g_{D},\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau})_{0,\Gamma_{D}}
has to be understood as a duality pairing between
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma_{D})
and
H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma_{D})
.

The well-posedness of (
6
) and (
8
) is a consequence of a lifting argument, and the Lax-Milgram lemma and the standard inf-sup theory, respectively; see, e.g.,
[
16
]
.

\begin{cases}\text{find }\widetilde{u}_{n}\in\widetilde{V}_{n,g_{D}}\text{ such that}\\
\widetilde{a}_{n}(\widetilde{u}_{n},v)=\langle f,\widetilde{v}_{n}\rangle_{n}+(g_{N},\widetilde{v}_{n})_{0,\Gamma_{N}}\quad\forall\widetilde{v}_{n}\in\widetilde{V}_{n,0}.\end{cases}

(13)

Remark 3
.

So far, we have discussed the construction of virtual elements with uniform degree of accuracy over all the elements.
Indeed, the flexibility of the virtual element framework allows for the construction of global spaces with variable degrees of accuracy.
Let
\mathcal{T}_{n}
be a mesh with
N_{\mathcal{T}_{n}}
elements.
Consider
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{N}^{N_{\mathcal{T}_{n}}}
and associate with each

a degree of accuracy

.
To each internal edge, we associate the maximum of the degrees of accuracy of the two neighbouring elements, whereas, to each boundary edge, we associate the degree of accuracy of the only neighbouring element.
The definition and cardinality of the bulk and edge degrees of freedom is performed accordingly.
We refer to
[
8
, Section 3]
for a thorough presentation of the variable degree virtual element spaces case.

Virtual elements for the mixed formulation

In this section, we discuss the virtual element discretization of the mixed formulation (

For the case of piecewise analytic diffusivity tensor

]

We consider a uniform degree of accuracy

over all the elements; see Remark

Virtual element spaces.

We define the virtual element spaces for both the primal and the flux variables.
As for the primal variable space, we define
V_{n}\subset V
as the space of piecewise discontinuous polynomials of degree
p-1
over
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, i.e.,

V_{n}:=\mathcal{S}^{p-1,-1}(\Omega,\mathcal{T}_{n}).

Given the multi-indices
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
as in bijection (
2
),
let
\{m_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{K}\}_{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=0}^{p-1}
be any basis of
\mathbb{P}_{p-1}(K)
.
We assume that the basis elements
m_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{K}
are invariant with respect to dilations and translations.
A set of unisolvent degrees of freedom is provided by scaled moments: given

\frac{1}{|K|}\int_{K}v_{n}m_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{K}\quad\quad\forall|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=0,\dots,p-1.

The construction of the flux spaces is as follows. On each element
K
, we define the local space

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}(K):=\big\{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\in H(\operatorname*{div},K)\cap H(\operatorname{rot},K)\mid&\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}{}_{|e}\in\mathbb{P}_{p}(e)\text{ for all }e\in\mathcal{E}^{K},\\
&\operatorname*{div}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\in\mathbb{P}_{p-1}(K),\;\operatorname{rot}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\in\mathbb{P}_{p-1}(K)\big\}.\\
\end{split}

(14)

Observe that
[\mathbb{P}_{p}(K)]^{2}\subseteq\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}(K)
and that the functions in
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}(K)
are neither known in closed form on
\partial K
nor inside the element
K
.

For all
p\in\mathbb{N}
, define

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{p}(K):=\nabla\mathbb{P}_{p+1}(K).

For all
K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
, let
\{\mathbf{m}_{\alpha}^{K}\}_{\alpha=1}^{\dim(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{p}(K))}
be a basis of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{p}(K)
.
We assume that the basis elements
\mathbf{m}_{\alpha}^{K}
are invariant with respect to dilations and translations.
An explicit construction of the basis for the polynomial space
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{p}(K)
can be found, e.g., in
[
31
, Proposition 2.1]
.
Further, introduce
\{m_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{K}\}_{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=0}^{p}
, a basis of
\mathbb{P}_{p}(K)
.
We assume that the basis elements are invariant with respect to dilations and translations.

Consider the following set of linear functionals: given
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}(K)
,

•

for all edges
e\in\mathcal{E}^{K}
, the evaluation at the
p+1
Gauss nodes
\{\nu_{j}^{e}\}_{j=0}^{p}
of
e

(\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n})(\nu_{j})\quad\quad\forall j=0,\dots,p;

(15)

•

the gradient-like moments

\frac{1}{|K|}\int_{K}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\cdot\mathbf{m}_{\alpha}^{K}\quad\quad\forall\alpha=1,\dots,\dim(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{p-2}(K));

(16)

•

the rotor-like moments

\frac{h_{K}}{|K|}\int_{K}\operatorname{rot}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n})m_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{K}\quad\quad\forall|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=0,\dots,p-1.

(17)

Proposition 2.2
.

The set of linear functionals in (
15
)–(
17
) is a set of unisolvent degrees of freedom for
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}(K)
, for all
K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
.

Proof.

Since the dimension of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}(K)
equals the number of linear functionals (
15
)–(
17
),
it is enough to show that given
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
with functionals equals to zero is zero. In particular, we show that
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}
solves a div-rot problem with zero data.

Clearly, we have
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}{}_{|\partial K}=0
, thanks to the boundary degrees of freedom (
15
).
Moreover,
\operatorname*{div}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}=0
. In fact, thanks to the definition of the degrees of freedom (
15
)–(
16
), an integration by parts yields

\int_{K}\operatorname*{div}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n})q_{p-1}=-\int_{K}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\cdot\nabla q_{p-1}+\int_{\partial K}\boldsymbol{\tau}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,q_{p-1}=0\quad\quad\forall q_{p-1}\in\mathbb{P}_{p-1}(K).

(18)

Eventually, thanks to rotor moments (
17
), we get
\operatorname{rot}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}=0
.
∎

Define the jump operator
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket_{e}
across an edge
e
as follows.
If
e
is an internal edge shared by the elements
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
with outward unit normal vectors
\mathbf{n}_{K_{1}}
and
\mathbf{n}_{K_{2}}
, respectively, and given
\mathbf{n}_{e}
the global unit normal vector associated with

,
set

\llbracket\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}\rrbracket_{e}=(\mathbf{n}_{e}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K_{1}})\mathbf{n}_{e}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{K_{1}}+(\mathbf{n}_{e}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K_{2}})\mathbf{n}_{e}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{K_{2}}.

Instead, if
e
is a boundary edge, set
\llbracket\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}\rrbracket_{e}=\mathbf{n}_{e}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}
.
For all
K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
and
e\in\mathcal{E}^{K}
, we have that
\mathbf{n}_{e}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K}{}=\pm 1
depending on the choice of
\mathbf{n}_{e}
.

We define the global space
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
without boundary conditions by coupling the normal components at the internal interfaces between elements sharing an edge:

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}:=\left\{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}\in H(\operatorname*{div},\Omega)\mid\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}{}_{|K}\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}(K)\;\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{n},\quad\llbracket\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}\rrbracket_{e}=0\;\forall e\in\mathcal{E}_{n}^{I}\right\}.

We incorporate the boundary condition
g_{N}
in the space by imposing the degrees of freedom associated with the edges on the Neumann part of the boundary
\Gamma_{N}
.
We set the discrete trial and test spaces for the fluxes space as

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n,g_{N}}&:=\left\{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}\mid\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}=g_{N}\text{ on }e\text{ if }e\subset\Gamma_{N}\right\},\\
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n,0}&:=\left\{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}\mid\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}=0\text{ on }e\text{ if }e\subset\Gamma_{N}\right\}.\\
\end{split}

With each global space, we associate a set of unisolvent degrees of freedom obtained by coupling the boundary degrees of freedom.

Remark 4
.

Although the functions in
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}(K)
are not available in closed form inside the elements, their divergence is computable from the degrees of freedom (
15
) and (
16
)
explicitly
; see (

).
Thence, we are able to compute exactly the bilinear form

(

) for all
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
and

.

Projector.

We introduce the
L^{2}(K)
vector projector
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}:\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}(K)\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{p}(K)
into the gradient of polynomials on each element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
:

a^{K}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n},\mathbf{q}_{p})=0\quad\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}(K),\,\forall\mathbf{q}_{p}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{p}(K).

(19)

This projector is computable from the degrees of freedom in (
15
)–(
17
).
To see this, we observe that

a^{K}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n},\nabla q_{p+1})=-a^{K}(\operatorname*{div}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n},q_{p+1})+(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\cdot\mathbf{n},q_{p+1})_{0,\partial K}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}(K),\,\forall q_{p+1}\in\mathbb{P}_{p+1}(K).

The terms on the right-hand side are computable using an integration by parts and the degrees of freedom (
15
) and (
16
).
Indeed, both the divergence and the normal components over the mesh skeleton of functions in virtual element spaces are known explicitly; see also (
18
).

The projector
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}
can be generalized to a projector into spaces of gradients of polynomials with arbitrary degree, i.e.,
[\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{p}}(K)]^{2}
for all
\widetilde{p}\in\mathbb{N}
.

Discrete bilinear forms and right-hand side.

As for the bilinear form
a(\cdot,\cdot)
, we proceed similarly to what is done for the primal formulation in Section
2.2
. First, consider the splitting

a(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}\int_{K}\kappa^{-1}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}=:\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}a^{K}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{|K},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{|K})\quad\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\sigma},\,\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma}.

The projector
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}
in (
19
) allows us to construct a computable discrete bilinear form mimicking
a(\cdot,\cdot)
.
For all
K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
, consider any bilinear form
S^{K}:\ker(\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p})\times\ker(\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, computable via the degrees of freedom and satisfying

\alpha_{*}a^{K}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n})\leq S^{K}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n})\leq\alpha^{*}a^{K}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}).

(20)

The constants
0<\alpha_{*}\leq\alpha^{*}<+\infty
depend possibly on the geometry of the polygonal decomposition through the parameter
\gamma
in the assumptions (
G1
) and (
G2
), the degree of accuracy
p
, and
\kappa
, but must be independent of the size
h_{K}
of element
K
.

Introducing the local discrete bilinear forms

a^{K}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}):=a^{K}(\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n},\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n})+S^{K}((\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p})\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n},(\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p})\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n})\quad\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n},\,\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}(K),

we define the global discrete bilinear form

a_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}):=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}a^{K}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}{}_{|K},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}{}_{|K})\quad\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n},\,\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}.

Following
[
20
]
, the global discrete bilinear form
a_{n}(\cdot,\cdot)
is coercive and continuous with constants
\min(k^{*}{}^{-1},\min_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}\alpha_{*})
and
\max(k_{*}{}^{-1},\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}\alpha^{*})
on the discrete kernel

\mathcal{K}_{n}=\left\{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}\mid b(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n},v_{n})=0\text{ for all }v_{n}\in V_{n}\right\}.

(21)

The discrete kernel
\mathcal{K}_{n}
is contained in the continuous kernel
\mathcal{K}
, which is defined as

\mathcal{K}:=\left\{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid b(\boldsymbol{\tau},v)=0\text{ for all }v\in V\right\}.

As for the right-hand side
(f,v_{n})_{0,\Omega}
, we recall that
v_{n}
is a piecewise polynomial.
Hence, the right-hand side can be approximated at any precision with sufficiently accurate quadrature formulas.

The virtual element method for the mixed formulation.

The virtual element method tailored for the approximation of the problem in mixed form (
8
) reads

\begin{cases}\text{find }(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n},u_{n})\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n,g_{N}}\times V_{n}\text{ such that}\\
a_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n})+b(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n},u_{n})=-(g_{D},\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n})_{0,\Gamma_{D}}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n,0}\\
b(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n},v_{n})=(-f,v_{n})_{0,\Omega}\quad\forall v_{n}\in V_{n}.\end{cases}

(22)

The definition of the trial and test spaces, and the second equation in (
22
) entail that

\operatorname*{div}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}=\Pi_{p-1}^{0}f\quad\quad\text{in }\Omega.

(23)

Remark 5
.

So far, we have discussed the construction of virtual elements with a uniform degree of accuracy over all the elements.
As already highlighted in Remark
3

Since the construction of spaces with variable degree of accuracy follows along the same lines as of the primal formulation case, we omit the details of the construction.

Proposition 2.7
.

For all convex

Proof.

Throughout the proof, we assume that

Besides, it suffices to prove the statement for

First, we show the bound on

Given
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}(K)

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{n}
and
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}^{\partial K}

\mathbf{n}_{K}\cdot\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{n}=0,\quad\operatorname*{div}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{n}=\operatorname*{div}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n},\quad\operatorname{rot}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{n}=\operatorname{rot}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}

\begin{split}\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\|_{0,K}&\leq\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{n}\|_{0,K}+\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}^{\partial K}\|_{0,K}\\

Next, we deal with the bound on

\|\mathbf{n}_{K}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\|_{0,\partial K}\lesssim p^{\frac{3}{2}}\,\|\mathbf{n}_{K}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\|_{-\frac{1}{2},\partial K}.

\widetilde{S}^{K}(\widetilde{\varphi}_{j},\widetilde{\varphi}_{\ell})=\max\left(|\kappa|,(\kappa\nabla\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}\widetilde{\varphi}_{j},\nabla\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\ell})_{0,K}\right)\delta_{j,\ell}\quad\quad\forall j,\ell=1,\dots,N^{\widetilde{V}_{n}},

(34)

where
|\cdot|
denotes the evaluation of a given scalar function at the barycenter of
K
.

Here,
\delta_{j,\ell}
denotes the Kronecker delta, whereas the projector
\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}
is defined in (
11
).

As for the D-recipe stabilization for the mixed formulation, we employ the following: given
N^{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}}:=\dim(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}(K))
,
for all
K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
, given
\{\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N^{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}}}
the canonical basis of the local space
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}(K)
,

S^{K}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{j},\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\ell})=\max\left(|\kappa^{-1}|\;h_{K}^{2},(\kappa^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{j},\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\ell})_{0,K}\right)\delta_{j,\ell}\quad\quad\forall j,\ell=1,\dots,N^{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}},

(35)

where the projector
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}
is defined in (
19
).

Remark 6
.

The difficulty in providing

-explicit bounds for the two practical stabilizations is related to the fact that we need to keep track of the dependence in terms of

For the practical stabilization for the primal formulation, this could be done under more assumptions on the polynomial basis used in the definition of the bulk degrees of freedom (

In Section

2.5
Well-posedness of the two virtual element methods

In this section, we prove the well-posedness of the primal and mixed methods (
13
) and (
22
).

The well-posedness of the primal method (
13
) follows from the continuity and coercivity of the discrete bilinear form
\widetilde{a}_{n}(\cdot,\cdot)
,
a lifting argument,
the continuity of the discrete right-hand side, and the Lax-Milgram lemma.

As for the mixed VEM formulation (
22
),
in addition to the usual lifting argument,
we need two ingredients in order to prove the well-posedness of the method.
The first one is the continuity and the coercivity of the bilinear form
a_{n}(\cdot,\cdot)
on the discrete kernel (
21
).
The second one is the validity of the inf-sup condition for the bilinear form
b(\cdot,\cdot)
with explicit bounds on the inf-sup constant in terms of
h
and
p
.
The remainder of the section is devoted to prove such an inf-sup condition.

Theorem 2.8
.

There exists a constant
\beta_{n}>0
independent of the discretization parameters, such that for all
v_{n}\in V_{n}
there exists
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n,0}
satisfying

b(v_{n},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n})\geq\beta_{n}\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\|_{\operatorname*{div},\Omega}\|v_{n}\|_{0,\Omega}.

(36)

The constant
\beta_{n}
is known in closed form:

\beta_{n}=\left(c_{\Omega}\frac{|\Omega|^{\frac{1}{2}}}{|\Gamma_{D}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}+c_{\Omega}+1\right)^{-1},

where
c_{\Omega}
is a constant depending only on the shape of
\Omega
, which will be detailed in the proof.

Proof.

With each
v_{n}\in V_{n}
, we associate a function
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n,0}
as follows:
for each
K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
, introduce a local
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}
satisfying

\begin{cases}\operatorname*{div}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}=v_{n}&\text{in }K\\
\operatorname{rot}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}=0&\text{in }K\\
\mathbf{n}_{K}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}=c_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{e}&\text{on }e\quad\forall e\in\mathcal{E}^{K},\;e\not\subset\Gamma_{D}\cup\Gamma_{N}\\
\mathbf{n}_{K}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}=c_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\Gamma_{D}}&\text{on }e\quad\forall e\subset\Gamma_{D}\\
\mathbf{n}_{K}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}=0&\text{on }e\quad\forall e\subset\Gamma_{N}.\end{cases}

Above,
c_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\Gamma_{D}}
is defined as the following global constant over
\Gamma_{D}
:

c_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}=\frac{1}{|\Gamma_{D}|}\int_{\Omega}v_{n},

(37)

whereas
c_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{e}
are piecewise constant functions over the interior skeleton such that two properties are satisfied:
(
i
) the compatability conditions of the above problems are satisfied;
(
ii
)
c_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{e}
is single valued on each interior edge
e
.
It is possible to fix such constant values as an easy consequence of Gauss’ formula for graphs.

This second property entails that we can define a global
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}
in
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n,0}
as the solution to the global div-rot problem

\begin{cases}\operatorname*{div}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}=v_{n}&\text{in }\Omega\\
\operatorname{rot}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}=0&\text{in }\Omega\\
\mathbf{n}_{K}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}=c_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}&\text{on }\Gamma_{D}\\
\mathbf{n}_{K}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{N}.\\
\end{cases}

(38)

Applying
[
29
, Remark p. 367]
and the Friedrichs’ inequality
[
42
, Corollary 3.51]
yields

\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq c_{\Omega}\left(\|\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\|_{0,\partial\Omega}+\|\operatorname*{div}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\|_{0,\Omega}\right),

where
c_{\Omega}
is a positive constant depending only on
\Omega
.

We deduce

\begin{split}\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\|_{0,\Omega}&\leq c_{\Omega}\left(\|\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\|_{0,\Gamma_{D}}+\|\operatorname*{div}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\|_{0,\Omega}\right)\overset{\eqref{div-rot:problem}}{=}c_{\Omega}\left(|\Gamma_{D}|^{\frac{1}{2}}|c_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}|+\|\operatorname*{div}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\|_{0,\Omega}\right)\\
&\overset{\eqref{ctau}}{=}c_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{|\Gamma_{D}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left|\int_{\Omega}v_{n}\right|+\|\operatorname*{div}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\|_{0,\Omega}\right)\\
&\overset{\eqref{div-rot:problem}}{=}c_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{|\Gamma_{D}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left|\int_{\Omega}\operatorname*{div}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\right|+\|\operatorname*{div}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\|_{0,\Omega}\right)\leq c_{\Omega}\left(\frac{|\Omega|^{\frac{1}{2}}}{|\Gamma_{D}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}+1\right)\|\operatorname*{div}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\|_{0,\Omega}.\\
\end{split}

This implies

\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\|_{\operatorname*{div},\Omega}\leq\left(c_{\Omega}\frac{|\Omega|^{\frac{1}{2}}}{|\Gamma_{D}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}+c_{\Omega}+1\right)\|\operatorname*{div}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\|_{0,\Omega}=:\beta_{n}^{-1}\|\operatorname*{div}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\|_{0,\Omega}.

(39)

Note that

\|v_{n}\|_{0,\Omega}=\frac{(v_{n},v_{n})_{0,\Omega}}{\|v_{n}\|_{0,\Omega}}\overset{\eqref{div-rot:problem}}{=}\frac{(v_{n},\operatorname*{div}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n})_{0,\Omega}}{\|\operatorname*{div}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\|_{0,\Omega}}.

Applying (
39
) to this identity, we deduce the discrete inf-sup condition

\|v_{n}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq\frac{1}{\beta_{n}}\frac{(v_{n},\operatorname*{div}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n})_{0,\Omega}}{\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\|_{\operatorname*{div},\Omega}}.

∎

The discrete inf-sup condition (
36
), together with the definition of the continuity of the discrete bilinear forms
a_{n}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
b(\cdot,\cdot)
,
and the coercivity of
a_{n}(\cdot,\cdot)
on the discrete kernel
\mathcal{K}_{n}
(
21
), is sufficient to prove the well-posedness of method (
22
); see
[
16
]
.
In fact, a lifting argument allows us to seek solutions in the discrete space
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n,0}
, i.e., solutions with zero normal trace on
\Gamma_{N}
.

Remark 7
.

We have proved Theorem
2.8
assuming that
\Gamma_{D}\neq\emptyset
, which is an assumption stipulated in Section
1
.
The case of pure Neumann boundary conditions has to be dealt with slightly differently. The primal virtual element space has to be endowed with a zero average constraint.
In order to prove the inf-sup condition one should proceed as in
[
20
, Theorem 4.2 and Corollary 4.3]
.
Besides, one ought to prove that the best approximant in mixed virtual element spaces converges optimally in terms of
h
and
p
to a target function, so that the inf-sup constant is
p
-robust.
This can be indeed proven by defining the best approximation in virtual element spaces as the degrees of freedom interpolant of the continuous function to approximate.
However, whatever boundary conditions we pick, the discrete inf-sup constant is
p
-robust.
To the best of our knowledge, this is not the case in the discontinuous Galerkin setting for mixed problems; see, e.g.,
[
47
, Section 4.2]
.
Of course, the price to pay is the
p
dependence in the stability estimates; see (
25
) and Proposition
2.7
.

In Section

Next, in Sections

Throughout, we assume that each local space has a fixed degree of accuracy

The case of variable degree of accuracy is dealt with by substituting
p
with a local

on each element
K
of
\mathcal{T}_{n}

This is reflected in estimates (

3.1
The equilibrated a posteriori error estimator

Given
\widetilde{u}
,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
,
\widetilde{u}_{n}
, and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}
the solutions to (
6
), (
8
), (
13
), and (
22
), respectively,
we observe that

\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}(\nabla\widetilde{u}-\nabla\widetilde{u}_{n})\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\|\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}=\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\widetilde{u}_{n}+\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}-2\int_{\Omega}\nabla(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n})\cdot(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}).

(40)

In order to show this identity, we observe that (
9
) entails

\begin{split}&\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\widetilde{u}_{n}+\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}-2\int_{\Omega}\nabla(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n})\cdot(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})\\
&=\int_{\Omega}\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}(\nabla\widetilde{u}_{n}-\nabla\widetilde{u})\cdot\left(\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\widetilde{u}_{n}+\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}\right)+\int_{\Omega}(\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}+\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\widetilde{u})\cdot\left(\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\widetilde{u}_{n}+\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}\right)\\
&\quad-2\int_{\Omega}\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n})\cdot\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})\\
&=\int_{\Omega}\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}(\nabla\widetilde{u}-\nabla\widetilde{u}_{n})\cdot\left(-\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\widetilde{u}_{n}-\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}+\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\widetilde{u}+\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}\right)\\
&\quad+\int_{\Omega}\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})\cdot\left(-\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\widetilde{u}_{n}-\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}+\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}+\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\widetilde{u}_{n}\right)\\
&=\int_{\Omega}\kappa(\nabla\widetilde{u}-\nabla\widetilde{u}_{n})\cdot(\nabla\widetilde{u}-\nabla u_{n})+\int_{\Omega}\kappa^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})\cdot(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}),\end{split}

which is (
40
).

We rewrite the last term on the right-hand side of (
40
) using an elementwise integration by parts: for all
K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
,

-\int_{K}\nabla(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n})\cdot(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})=\int_{K}(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n})\,\operatorname*{div}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})-\int_{\partial K}(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n})\,\mathbf{n}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}).

We reshape the first term on the right-hand side.
Recalling that
\operatorname*{div}\boldsymbol{\sigma}=f
and
\operatorname*{div}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}=\Pi_{p-1}^{0}f
, see (
23
),
we write

\int_{K}(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n})\,(\operatorname*{div}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}))=\int_{K}(u-\widetilde{u}_{n})(f-\Pi_{p-1}^{0}f).

We collect all the contributions and deduce that, for every piecewise discontinuous polynomial
q_{p-1}
of degree
p-1
over
\mathcal{T}_{n}
,

\begin{split}&\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n})\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\|\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}\\
&=\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\widetilde{u}_{n}+\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+2\int_{\Omega}(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n}-q_{p-1})(f-\Pi_{p-1}^{0}f)\\
&\quad-2\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}\int_{\partial K}(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n})\mathbf{n}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}).\end{split}

(41)

The internal interface contributions appearing in the last term on the right-hand side of (
41
) are zero. The virtual element spaces have been tailored so that this property is fulfilled.
Furthermore, the boundary contributions disappear thanks to the assumption (
D
).

Thus, we write

\begin{split}&\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n})\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\|\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}\\
&=\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\widetilde{u}_{n}+\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+2\int_{\Omega}(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n}-q_{p-1})(f-\Pi_{p-1}^{0}f).\\
\end{split}

(42)

In the two forthcoming Sections
3.2
and
3.3
,
we show upper and lower bounds on the right-hand side of (
42
). This will give rise to a natural choice for the equilibrated error estimator.
Henceforth, we refer to the square root of the left-hand side of (
42
) as to the exact error of the method.
In particular, the exact error is the square root of the sum of the square of the error of the primal (
13
) and mixed (
22
) methods.

The error estimator.

Since
\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\widetilde{u}_{n}+\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}\|_{0,\Omega}
is not computable, we propose the local

\begin{split}\eta_{\mathfrak{eq},K}^{2}&:=\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}\widetilde{u}_{n}+\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}\|^{2}_{0,K}\\
&\quad+\left[\widetilde{S}^{K}((I-\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p})\widetilde{u}_{n},(I-\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p})\widetilde{u}_{n})+S^{K}((\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p})\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n},(\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p})\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})\right],\\
\end{split}

(43)

and global error estimators

\eta_{\mathfrak{eq}}^{2}:=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}\eta_{\mathfrak{eq},K}^{2}.

(44)

3.2
Reliability

From (
42
), we deduce the following upper bound:

\begin{split}&\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n})\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\|\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}\\
&\leq 2\big(\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}(\nabla\widetilde{u}_{n}-\nabla\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}\widetilde{u}_{n})\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}\widetilde{u}_{n}+\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\|\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}\big)\\
&\quad+2\int_{\Omega}(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n}-q_{p-1})(f-\Pi_{p-1}^{0}f)\quad\quad\forall q_{p-1}\in\mathcal{S}^{p-1,-1}(\Omega,\mathcal{T}_{n}).\end{split}

Set
q_{p-1}
as the piecewise
L^{2}
projection of
u-\widetilde{u}_{n}
over
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. Using standard
h
- and
p
- approximation estimates, we deduce

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}&(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n}-q_{p-1})(f-\Pi_{p-1}^{0}f)\leq\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}\|\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n}-q_{p-1}\|_{0,K}\|f-\Pi_{p-1}^{0}f\|_{0,K}\\
&\leq\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}c_{B}(K)\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}\frac{h_{K}}{p}|\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n}|_{1,K}\|f-\Pi_{p-1}^{0}f\|_{0,K}\\
&\leq\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}c_{B}(K)|\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n}|_{1,\Omega}\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}\frac{h_{K}^{2}}{p^{2}}\|f-\Pi_{p-1}^{0}f\|_{0,K}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},\\
\end{split}

where
c_{B}(K)
is the positive constant appearing in the
h
- and
p
-approximation estimates of, e.g.,
[
4
, Lemma 4.5]
.

Young’s inequality entails

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n}-q_{p-1})(f-\Pi_{p-1}^{0}f)\\
&\leq 4\varepsilon\left(\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}c_{B}(K)\right)^{2}|\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n}|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}\frac{h_{K}^{2}}{p^{2}}\|f-\Pi_{p-1}^{0}f\|_{0,K}^{2}\\
&\leq 4k_{*}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\varepsilon\left(\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}c_{B}(K)\right)^{2}\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n})\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}\frac{h_{K}^{2}}{p^{2}}\|f-\Pi_{p-1}^{0}f\|_{0,K}^{2}\\
\end{split}

for all
\varepsilon>0
, where
c_{B}(K)
denotes the best
hp
-approximation constant on element
K
.

Recalling (
9
) and setting

\varepsilon=\frac{k_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}}}{16(\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}c_{B}(K))^{2}},

we obtain

\begin{split}&\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n})\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\|\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}\\
&\leq 4\left(\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}(\nabla\widetilde{u}_{n}-\nabla\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}\widetilde{u}_{n})\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}\widetilde{u}_{n}+\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\right.\\
&\quad\quad\left.+\|\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\right)+16\frac{(\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}c_{B}(K))^{2}}{k_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}\frac{h_{K}^{2}}{p^{2}}\|f-\Pi_{p-1}^{0}f\|^{2}_{0,K}\\
&\leq 4\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}\widetilde{u}_{n}+\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\\
&\quad+4\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}\max(\widetilde{\alpha}_{*}^{-1},\alpha_{*}^{-1})\left[\widetilde{S}^{K}((I-\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p})\widetilde{u}_{n},(I-\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p})\widetilde{u}_{n})+S^{K}((\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p})\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n},(\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p})\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})\right]\\
&\quad+16\frac{(\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}c_{B}(K))^{2}}{k_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}\frac{h_{K}^{2}}{p^{2}}\|f-\Pi_{p-1}^{0}f\|^{2}_{0,K}.\\
\end{split}

Eventually, (

\begin{split}&\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n})\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\|\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}\leq 4\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}\widetilde{u}_{n}+\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\\
&\quad+4\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}\max(\widetilde{\alpha}_{*}^{-1},\alpha_{*}^{-1})\left(\widetilde{S}^{K}((I-\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p})\widetilde{u}_{n},(I-\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p})\widetilde{u}_{n})+S^{K}((\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p})\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n},(\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p})\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})\right)\\
&\quad+16\frac{(\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}c_{B}(K))^{2}}{k_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}\frac{h_{K}^{2}}{p^{2}}\|f-\operatorname*{div}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}\|^{2}_{0,K}.\\
\end{split}

(45)

All the terms on the right-hand side are computable with the exception of the oscillation of the right-hand side. This term can be approximated at any precision employing a sufficiently accurate quadrature formula.

3.3
Efficiency

Here, we show an upper bound on the local equilibrated error estimator

For all polygons

) and the stability of orthogonal projectors, we get

\begin{split}\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}\widetilde{u}_{n}+\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}\|_{0,K}^{2}&\leq 2\left(\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\widetilde{u}-\nabla\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}\widetilde{u}_{n}\|_{0,K}^{2}+\|\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})\|^{2}_{0,K}\right)\\
&\leq 4\big(\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}(\nabla\widetilde{u}-\nabla\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}\widetilde{u})\|^{2}_{0,K}+\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n})\|_{0,K}^{2}\\
&\quad\quad+\|\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}\boldsymbol{\sigma})\|^{2}_{0,K}+\|\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})\|^{2}_{0,K}\big)\\
&\leq 4\big(\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}(\nabla\widetilde{u}-\nabla\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}\widetilde{u})\|^{2}_{0,K}+\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n})\|_{0,K}^{2}\\
&\quad\quad+\|\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}\boldsymbol{\sigma})\|^{2}_{0,K}+\|\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})\|^{2}_{0,K}\big).\\
\end{split}

Next, we deal with the stabilization terms, i.e., the second and third terms on the right-hand side of (
43
). We begin with the stabilization term stemming from the discretization of the primal formulation:

\begin{split}\widetilde{S}^{K}((I-\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p})\widetilde{u}_{n},&(I-\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p})\widetilde{u}_{n})\leq\widetilde{\alpha}^{*}\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla(I-\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p})\widetilde{u}_{n}\|^{2}_{0,K}\\
&\leq 2\widetilde{\alpha}^{*}\left[\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n})\|^{2}_{0,K}+\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}\widetilde{u})\|^{2}_{0,K}+\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n})\|^{2}_{0,K}\right]\\
&\leq 4\widetilde{\alpha}^{*}\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n})\|^{2}_{0,K}+2\widetilde{\alpha}^{*}\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}u)\|^{2}_{0,K}.\\
\end{split}

Analogously, we show an upper bound on the stabilization term stemming from the discretization of the mixed formulation:

S^{K}((\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p})\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n},(\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p})\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})\leq 4\alpha^{*}\|\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})\|^{2}_{0,K}+2\alpha^{*}\|\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}\boldsymbol{\sigma})\|^{2}_{0,K}.

Collecting the three estimates, we get

\begin{split}\eta_{\mathfrak{eq},K}^{2}&=\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}\widetilde{u}_{n}+\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}\|^{2}_{0,K}\\
&\quad+\widetilde{S}^{K}((I-\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p})\widetilde{u}_{n},(I-\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p})\widetilde{u}_{n})+S^{K}((\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p})\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n},(\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p})\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})\\
&\leq 4(1+\widetilde{\alpha}^{*})\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{n})\|^{2}_{0,K}+2(2+\widetilde{\alpha}^{*})\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}\widetilde{u}_{n})\|^{2}_{0,K}\\
&\quad+4(1+\alpha^{*})\|\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})\|^{2}_{0,K}+2(2+\alpha^{*})\|\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})\|^{2}_{0,K}.\\
\end{split}

(47)

We have proven the following efficiency result.

The adaptive algorithm and

In this section, we recall the structure of an adaptive algorithm, the meaning of

The standard structure of an adaptive algorithm is

SOLVE

\quad\longrightarrow\quad

\quad\longrightarrow\quad

\quad\longrightarrow\quad

The remainder of this section is devoted to address the

Refining in
p

The design of the global space and its degrees of freedom is performed accordingly to Remarks
3

5

We describe the

-refinement in more details. Firstly, we anticipate that we shall employ Cartesian and triangular meshes, only.
This might seem idiosyncratic, as we claimed that we want an adaptive method working on general meshes.
However, the flexibility in employing polygons is exploited when refining squares or triangles and creating hanging nodes.
In the refining procedure, we define a geometric square as a geometrical entity with four straight edges. For instance, a polygon with five vertices and having two adjacent edges on the same line is a geometric square.
Analogously, we define a geometric triangle as a geometrical entity with three straight edges.
A geometric square is refined into four smaller geometric squares by connecting its centroid to the midpoints of the four straight edges; see Figure
4
(left).
Instead, a geometric triangle is refined into four smaller geometric triangles as in Figure

Marking elements is a rather standard procedure. We mark for refinement all the elements

5
Local flux reconstruction in VEM: a first investigation

In Section
3
, we proved lower and upper bounds of the exact error in terms of an equilibrated error estimator where the dependence on the distribution of the degrees of accuracy is isolated within the stability constants.
This is a major improvement compared to the results achieved in the residual error estimator setting; see
[
12
, Theorem 1]
.
Nonetheless, the linear system associated with the mixed method (
22
) has approximately three times the number of unknowns of the linear system associated with the primal formulation (
13
).
This downside can be overcome via the localization of the mixed VEM.
This has been already investigated in several works within the continuous and discontinuous finite element framework; see
[
17
]
and
[
34
]
, respectively, and the references therein.

In words, the localization technique works as follows.
We construct an error estimator, which can be computed with the degrees of freedom of the solution to primal VEM (
13
)
and
those of a cheap to compute numerical flux.
Whilst in Section
3
, this function was given by the solution to a local mixed VEM (
22
),
here, it is provided by the combination of solutions to local mixed VEM, which are cheap to solve and can be parallelized.

The aim of this section is to provide an initial study towards the local flux reconstruction in VEM.
We shall be able to prove that the error estimator is reliable and satisfies a condition on equilibration of fluxes.
However, we shall not prove the efficiency. This is also reflected in the numerical results of Section
6
.

Notation and assumptions.

To simplify the forthcoming analysis, we henceforth assume

\kappa=1,\quad\Gamma_{D}=\partial\Omega,\quad\Gamma_{N}=\emptyset.

We define the polygonal patch around a vertex
\nu\in\mathcal{V}_{n}
and the elements of the mesh
\mathcal{T}_{n}
belonging to such patch as

\omega_{\nu}:=\bigcup\left\{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}\mid\nu\in\mathcal{V}^{K}\right\},\quad\quad\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\omega_{\nu}}=\{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}\mid K\subset\omega_{\nu}\}.

Define the two following element spaces over the patch
\omega_{\nu}
:

\begin{split}&V_{n}(\omega_{\nu}):=\mathcal{S}^{p-1,-1}(\omega_{\nu},\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\omega_{\nu}}),\\
&\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}(\omega_{\nu}):=\{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\in H(\operatorname*{div},\omega_{\nu})\mid\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}{}_{|K}\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}(K)\quad\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\omega_{\nu}},\;\llbracket\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\rrbracket_{e}=0\quad\forall e\in\mathcal{E}_{n}^{I}\}.\\
\end{split}

We also define

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n,0}(\omega_{\nu}):=\left\{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}(\omega_{\nu})\mid\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}=0\text{ on }\partial\omega_{\nu}\right\},\quad\quad V_{n}^{*}(\omega_{\nu}):=\left\{v_{n}\in V_{n}(\omega_{\nu})\middle|\int_{\omega_{\nu}}v_{n}=0\right\}.

For each patch
\omega_{\nu}
, we introduce localized discrete bilinear form

a_{n}^{\omega_{\nu}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\nu},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}^{\nu}):=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\omega_{\nu}}}a^{K}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\nu},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}^{\nu})\quad\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\nu},\,\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}^{\nu}\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}(\omega_{\nu}).

Construction of a virtual element partition of unity.

We construct a virtual element partition of unity
\{\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}\}_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}_{n}}
associated with the mesh
\mathcal{T}_{n}
by suitably fixing the degrees of freedom of each
\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}
.
To each vertex
\nu\in\mathcal{V}_{n}
, we associate a function
\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}\in\widetilde{V}_{n}
defined through its degrees of freedom as follows.
It is equal to
1
at
\nu
, annihilates at all the other vertices, and is affine on the skeleton of the mesh.

For all
K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
with
\nu\in\mathcal{V}^{K}
, we proceed as follows.
Let
N^{K}
be the number of vertices of
K
. Then, we set

\frac{1}{|K|}\int_{K}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}m_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{K}=\frac{1}{N^{K}|K|}\int_{K}m_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{K}\quad\quad\forall|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=0,\dots,p-2.

On all the elements
K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
such that
\nu
is not a vertex of
K
,
\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}
is extended by
0

Proof.

Fix

We have that

Let

Proof.

5.2
Lack of efficiency

In this section, we give hints on why we are not able to prove the efficiency of the error estimator (
62
) and why it is probably not valid in general.

To this aim, we first recall what happens in the finite element setting.
Given the patch
\omega_{\nu}
consisting of triangles around an internal vertex
\nu
, let
\mathbb{RT}_{n}(\omega_{\nu})
be the associated Raviart-Thomas space of order
p
.
Moreover, let
Q_{n}(\omega_{\nu})
be the space of piecewise polynomials of degree
p
associated with the subtriangulation of
\omega_{\nu}
.
Observe that the partition of unity elements
\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}
are standard linear hat functions.
The FEM counterpart of (
57
) reads

\begin{cases}\text{find }(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\nu},r_{n}^{\nu})\in\mathbb{RT}_{n}(\omega_{\nu})\times Q_{n}(\omega_{\nu})\text{ such that, for all }\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}^{\nu}\in\mathbb{RT}_{n}(\omega_{\nu})\text{ and }q_{n}^{\nu}\in Q_{n}(\omega_{\nu}),\\
(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\nu},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}^{\nu})_{0,\omega_{\nu}}-(\operatorname*{div}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}^{\nu}),r_{n}^{\nu})_{0,\omega_{\nu}}=-(\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}\,\nabla\widetilde{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}^{\nu})_{0,\omega_{\nu}}\\
-(\operatorname*{div}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\nu},q_{n}^{\nu})_{0,\omega_{\nu}}=-(\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}\,f-\nabla\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{u}_{n},q_{n}^{\nu})_{0,\omega_{\nu}}.\\
\end{cases}

(67)

The first step towards the proof of the local efficiency of the error estimator is based on an
H^{1}
representation of the residual. In particular, introduce
r^{\nu}\in H^{1}(\omega_{\nu})/\mathbb{R}
the solution to

(\nabla r^{\nu},\nabla v)_{0,\omega_{\nu}}=-(\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}\nabla\widetilde{u}_{n},\nabla v)+(\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}f-\nabla\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{u}_{n},v)_{0,\omega_{\nu}}\quad\forall v\in H^{1}(\omega_{\nu})/\mathbb{R}.

(68)

This equation is obtained by formally substituting
v
and
\nabla v
to
q_{n}^{\nu}
and
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}^{\nu}
, using twice an integration by parts, and combining the two equations in (
67
).

As in
[
25
,
17
]
, the following bound can be proven:

|r^{\nu}|_{1,\omega_{\nu}}\lesssim|u-\widetilde{u}_{n}|_{1,\omega_{\nu}},

(69)

where the hidden constant depends on a Poincaré constant.

A crucial ingredient in the proof of (
69
) consists in observing that

\begin{split}-(\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}\nabla\widetilde{u}_{n},\nabla v)+(\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}f-\nabla\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{u}_{n},v)_{0,\omega_{\nu}}&=(f,\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}v)_{0,\omega_{\nu}}-(\nabla\widetilde{u}_{n},\nabla(\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}\,v))_{0,\omega_{\nu}}\\
&=(\nabla(u-\widetilde{u}_{n}),\nabla(\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}\,v))_{0,\omega_{\nu}}.\end{split}

(70)

This is the first step towards the proof of the efficiency, as then it suffices to show that the error estimator is smaller than the energy of the
H^{1}
residual.
In what follows, we explain how the VEM counterpart of (
68
) looks like, and what are the issues in deriving estimates of the type in (
69
).

The main difference in the FE and VE approaches is that in the latter we have to deal with projectors, stabilizations, and functions that are not available in closed-form.
We formally substitute
v
and
\nabla v
to
q_{n}^{\nu}
and
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}^{\nu}
. Using the first equation in (
57
), we can write

\begin{split}&(\nabla r^{\nu},\nabla v)_{0,\omega_{\nu}}\\
&=-\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\omega_{\nu}}}\left[(\widetilde{\Pi}^{0}_{p-2}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}\,\nabla\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}\widetilde{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}\nabla v)_{0,K}-a^{K}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\nu},\nabla v)\right]\\
&=-\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\omega_{\nu}}}\left[(\widetilde{\Pi}^{0}_{p-2}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}\,\nabla\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}\widetilde{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}\nabla v)_{0,K}\underbrace{-a^{K}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\nu},\nabla v)+a^{K}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\nu},\nabla v)}_{=:A}-a^{K}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\nu},\nabla v)\right].\\
\end{split}

(71)

The term
A
is a consistency error, which can be approximated with optimal rate.
Thence, we deal with the last term appearing on the right-hand side of (
71
).
Using the definition of orthogonal projectors, an integration by parts, and the definition of the jumps, we get

-a^{\omega_{\nu}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\nu},\nabla v)=(\operatorname*{div}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}^{\nu},v)_{0,\omega_{\nu}}.

Inserting this bound into (
71
) and using formally the second equation in (
57
) yield

\begin{split}&(\nabla r^{\nu},\nabla v)_{0,\omega_{\nu}}\\
&=A+\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\omega_{\nu}}}\left[-(\widetilde{\Pi}^{0}_{p-2}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}\,\nabla\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}\widetilde{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}\nabla v)_{0,K}+(\widetilde{\Pi}^{0}_{p-2}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}\,f-\nabla\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}\widetilde{u}_{n},v)_{0,K}\right].\\
&=A\underbrace{-\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}(\nabla\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}\widetilde{u}_{n},\widetilde{\Pi}^{0}_{p-2}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}\,\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{p}\nabla v+\nabla\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}\,v)_{0,K}}_{=:B}+\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}(f,\widetilde{\Pi}^{0}_{p-2}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}v)_{0,K}.\end{split}

Eventually, we analyze the last term on the right-hand side. Using the continuous problem (
4
) and an integration by parts lead us to

\begin{split}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}(f,\widetilde{\Pi}^{0}_{p-2}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}v)_{0,K}&=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\omega_{\nu}}}(\nabla\widetilde{u},\nabla(\widetilde{\Pi}^{0}_{p-2}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}\,v))_{0,K}-\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\omega_{\nu}}}(u,\llbracket\widetilde{\Pi}^{0}_{p-2}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\nu}\,v\rrbracket)_{0,e}=:C+D.\end{split}

Combining all the above estimates, we arrive at

(\nabla r^{\nu},\nabla v)_{0,\omega_{\nu}}=A+B+C+D.

In the FE setting the terms
B
and
C
are combined together, see (
70
).
Here, the presence of different projectors forbids us to use either the product rule or the continuous formulation as in (
70
),
which renders the simplification resulted by combining the partition of unity impossible.

In Section
6
below, we provide numerical evidence that the efficiency does not take place in the current setting.

7
Conclusion

We presented an a posteriori error analysis for the virtual element method based on equilibrated fluxes.
We introduced an equilibrated reliable and efficient a posteriori error estimator, using the virtual element solutions to the primal and mixed formulations.
Additionally, we showed that the discrete inf-sup constant for the mixed VEM is
p
-independent
and constructed an explicit stabilization for the mixed VEM, characterized by lower and upper bounds with explicit dependence on the (local) degree of accuracy.

Several numerical experiments have been illustrated.
On the one hand, we showed that the effectivity index for the hypercircle method is
p
-independent in practice. This is a major improvement with respect to the residual error estimator case.
On the other hand, we discussed an
hp
-adaptive algorithm and applied it to two test cases.
We observed exponential convergence in terms of the cubic root of the number of degrees of freedom in all the test cases.

Eventually, we began the analysis of the localized flux reconstruction in VEM.
Notably, we introduced a reliable computable error estimator, which can be obtained using the solution to the primal formulation and a combination of solutions to local mixed problems.
Numerics showed that the equilibrium condition is fulfilled but efficiency does not occur with the exception of the low-order case.
We also gave theoretical justifications for this lack of efficiency. It turns out that the culprit has to be sought in the mismatch in the use of the projection operators appearing in the proposed local mixed VEM.
