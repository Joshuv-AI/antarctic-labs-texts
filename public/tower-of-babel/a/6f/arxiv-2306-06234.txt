Using Foundation Models to Detect Policy Violations with Minimal Supervision
Sid Mittal, Vineet Gupta, Frederick Liu, Mukund Sundararajan

Abstract
Foundation models, i.e. large neural networks pre-trained on large text corpora, have revolutionized NLP. They can be instructed directly ( e.g. Brown et al. 2020 )—this is called hard prompting —and they can be tuned using very little data ( e.g. Lester et al. 2021 )—this technique is called soft prompting . We seek to leverage their capabilities to detect policy violations. Our contributions are: • We identify a hard prompt that adapts chain-of-thought prompting to policy violation tasks. This prompt produces policy violation classifications, along with extractive explanations that justify the classification. • We compose the hard-prompts with soft-prompt tuning to produce a classifier that attains high accuracy with very little supervision; the same classifier also produces explanations. Though the supervision only acts on the classifications, we find that the modified explanations remain consistent with the (tuned) model’s response. • Along the way, we identify several unintuitive aspects of foundation models. For instance, adding an example from a specific class can actually reduce predictions of that class, and separately, the effects of tokenization on scoring etc. • Based on our technical results, we identify a simple workflow for product teams to quickly develop effective policy violation detectors.

1.1
Foundation Models, Prompting, Prompt Tuning

Natural language processing has been transformed by large language models
(
Peters et al. 2018
;
Devlin et al. 2018
;
Brown et al. 2020
;
Chowdhery et al. 2022
)
, pre-trained on large corpora; these are also called
foundation models
. Foundation models demonstrate excellent performance on a wide-variety of tasks including translation, answering questions involving factual knowledge, math problems, or common-sense reasoning
Chung et al. 2022
.

Foundation models can be instructed to perform a task; this is called prompting (
e.g.

Brown et al. 2020
). They can also be fine-tuned efficiently using very little data using an approach called soft-prompt tuning (
e.g.

Lester et al. 2021
;
Li and Liang 2021
), discussed further in Section
3
.

1.2
Toxicity Detection

We seek to leverage foundation models to detect policy violations. We will use a toxicity detection task
(
tox 2019
)
as a running-example; the task is to identify if a piece of text is insulting, identity-hating, or contains obscene references. These pieces of text could be comments about some content (news article or video), or discussions in a forum. Currently, this task is performed by a combination of humans and machines; humans label training data, which machines use for supervised classification.

Issuing rater instructions, collecting training data, and building models using supervised learning is a fairly labor intensive process. In contrast, prompting can be used for one-off tasks where the cost of collecting data is prohibitive, or to quickly modify instructions when guidelines change, or to test new instructions for human raters.

We expect foundation models to have some knowledge of toxicity because they are trained on portions of the web, news articles and even social media conversations (see Section 3 in
Chowdhery et al. 2022
). State-of-the-art results have been achieved on the toxicity task by other Transformer based language models such as BERT
(
Duchene et al. 2023
)
, but there is no prior benchmark that uses models as large as this work and with this little supervision.

2
Prompt Design

A hard prompt consists of text instructions and optionally a few examples of the desired input/output. This is prepended to the inputs to the foundation model.
We discuss the major ideas in the design of our hard prompt below.

2.1
The Hard Prompt

Our hard prompt consists of a Guideline describing the task, followed by a few examples, each consisting of a Comment, Answer, Explanation, Citation and Keywords. The structure of the prompt allows the model to switch between generative (for Explanation) and extractive modes (for Keywords and Citation). Here is part of our prompt. (The full prompt is in Appendix
A
.)

Toxic Policy:
A toxic comment is one which is likely to make the conversation
partner leave a discussion or give up on sharing their perspective.
A comment violates the Toxic Policy if the author:
(1) makes obscene references.
...
(4) humiliates their conversation partner
...
Question: Does the comment violate the Toxic Policy?
...
<Comment> @ okay king of the Wikipedia Nazis. </Comment>
<Answer> Yes </Answer>
<Explanation> The comment mentions ’king of the Wikipedia Nazis’ so
it violates ’(4) humiliates their conversation partner’.
</Explanation>
<Citations> (4) humiliates their conversation partner </Citations>
<Keywords> king of the Wikipedia Nazis </Keywords>
---

2.2
Extractive Explanations

Citations and Keywords are examples of extractive explanations applied to prompting. Extractive Explanations (
DeYoung et al. 2019
;
Narang et al. 2020
) are excerpts from the input text that are produced along with the model’s response; the excerpt justifies or explains the response. We produce two types of excerpts: ‘Keywords’ that are drawn from the input text (Comment), and ‘Citations’ that are drawn from the Guideline.

2.3
Grounded Reasoning Chains

The structure of our prompt bears resemblance to Chain-of-Thought Prompts (
Wei et al. 2022
), where models perform tasks in reasoning or mathematics by being prompted using triples of the form <Question, Reasoning, Answer>. In their case, reasoning is an argument or a derivation that helps the model arrive at the right answer.

In contrast, we partition our reasoning into three steps. Two grounded steps extract relevant keywords (e.g. ’king of the Wikipedia Nazis’ in the example above) from the comment and the applicable citation (e.g. ’humiliates their conversation partner’) from the guideline. The reasoning can be intuitively interpreted as: The Answer is Yes/No because the excerpt ’Keywords’ violates the guideline bullet ’Citation’. The third step is the ’Explanation’ block, a natural language statement that articulates this argument. Our requirement to tune a soft-prompt also influences the prompt design — see Section
4.2
.

2.4
XML Tags

Most other works structure their prompts by using section headings, i.e.,

Text: some text
Reasoning: some reasoning
Answer: some response

We found this to be fragile when there were long, multi-line comments.
We found it effective to use XML-style markup to demarcate the blocks.
This eliminates the fragility as we show in Section
4.1
— our models consistently generated the correct XML-like structure. In addition section headings led to tokenization problems (Section
4.5
), which we avoid with XML and proper spacing.

3
Soft Prompt Tuning

3.1
What is soft prompt tuning?

Soft prompt tuning
Lester et al. 2021
trains a model on a few hundred or few thousand examples.
However, unlike classic fine-tuning, the model’s parameters are frozen.
Instead a few artificial tokens are prefixed to each input — the embeddings of these tokens are tuned via gradient descent.
These prefixed tokens are called the ‘soft’ prompt.
The advantage of this approach is that a single (expensive) foundation model can be used for a variety of tasks.

3.2
Training Dataset Size

We choose a small subset of training examples as a dataset for prompt tuning.
This mimics how we intend to apply foundation models. If a lot of training data is available, we could instead use smaller models like BERT; BERT models are cheaper to operate, but need much more supervision. We therefore seek to apply foundation models when a lot of labeled data is not yet available and choose to work with datasets of size 50-5000.

3.3
Composing Prompt Tuning with Extractive Explanations

We assume that supervision is only available for the model’s prediction (toxic or not). No supervision is available for the extractive explanations, because it is harder to produce such supervision. However, we would like the tuned model to produce extractive explanations. We therefore include the hard prompt during tuning and inference.
1
1

1

One approach is to prompt tune the model without the hard prompt, and add the hard prompt at inference time to get the explanations. However this did not work — the resultant model did not produce any explanations; it just returned the ’Yes’ or ’No’ over and over again.

There has been some prior work in combinations of soft and hard prompts, but none to best of our knowledge with a similar setting to ours: using a hard prompt to generate structured explanations, while tuning the soft prompt with supervision from a binary label. The prior works
Yu et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2021
start with a classification task, and use the hard prompts to make the task resemble the pretraining task. For instance, for a sentiment scoring task, the hard prompt appends the review with a template such as "It was X", where the output blank is scored for the probability of [great] or [terrible].
Han et al. 2021
extends this to the multiclass setting using prior knowledge of each class. In contrast, the goal of the hard prompt in our case is to provide guidelines to describe the task and to control the format of the output.

4
Experiments

We perform experiments on the toxicity detection dataset from
tox 2019
. The binary classification task is to predict whether a piece of text is toxic or not,
i.e.
whether it contains abusive or hateful speech that causes the reader to disengage from a conversation.

The dataset contains around 2M examples. We use the provided splits for train and test. We also downsample the negative examples in order to create balanced splits for train, validation (pulling examples from train split), and test. We randomly sample to further create train samples of size 50, 100, 200, 500, 1k, 2k, and 5k in order to show scaling performance. Our test set is of size 5000. Our results varied only slightly with test set re-sampling.

We focus our experiments on 3 models: 62B FLAN-cont-PaLM, 540B FLAN-PaLM, and 540B FLAN-U-PaLM
Chung et al. 2022
. In addition to being pretrained with prior world knowledge, the instruction tuning from FLAN allows these models to achieve superior few shot and zero shot performance on a variety of benchmarks.

4.2
Prompt tuning

We tuned a prompt consisting of 100 tokens, each with an embedding of dimension
8192
for the 62B model. Similarly, we tune a prompt of 40 tokens with an embedding dimension of
18432
for the 540B models. We trained these tokens with a basic Adam optimizer with clipped gradients. We chose a default learning rate and schedule for all our experiments, and did not tune hyperparameters further. We set sampling temperature to 0 and fixed the prompt initialization, to reduce randomness.

We experimented with different sizes of the dataset, to see how few examples we could get away with. We show the results of our experiments in Figures
1
and
2
. We find that the AUC-ROC and accuracy with a dataset of size as small as 50 is significantly higher than with just the hard prompt; adding more examples only produces a gradual increase in accuracy. From the graph it appears that accuracy would increase with even larger datasets (larger than 5000 in size), but the goal of this work (as discussed in Section
3
) is to explore the benefit of this technique when few training examples are available.

We also computed the AUC for soft prompt tuning without the few-shot prompt, and found it to be slightly lower — for the 62B model the AUC without the few-shot prompt is
0.938
, whereas with the few-shot prompt it was
0.951
(with a training set of size 5000 examples). Thus, not only does the presence of the few-shot prompt during fine-tuning help produce extractive explanations (Keywords and Citations), it is also increases performance slightly.

In Appendix
D
, we show several examples where the prediction of the few-shot prompt differed from the soft prompt. We note that whenever the soft-prompt changes the output, it also constructs a coherent explanation. Thus the explanations (and also citations and keywords) always match the prediction, so the tuned prompt continues to produce useful explanations.

Producing the answer section before the explanations, citations and keywords has a couple advantages. It allows for us to use supervision since the labels are the immediate next generation of the hard prompt. It also speeds up training because we only need to generate a single token output.

4.3
Few-shot examples are exemplars

In traditional ML, examples determine where the decision boundary is drawn, and the boundary is drawn to maximize margin between positive and negative examples. Typically, adding more examples of a certain class, increases the number of times that class is predicted. In contrast, with foundation models, the few examples that we supply, mimic ‘exemplars’,
i.e.
typical examples, as it would with humans. Adding examples from a class could reduce the number of times that class is predicted. For instance, if examples of violations are all very severe, only true severe violations will be predicted as violations. A corollary to this is that if you add an example of a severe violation, the model would tend to predict fewer violations. This will cause the model to miss subtle cases nearer the boundary between the two classes.

As an experiment, we added an extreme comment from our training data:
‘‘Hey faggot, where is the citation for M Magazine? Plus, your gay comment has atrocious syntax."

Our original few-shot prompt identified 1441 of the 5000 examples as toxic, but the prompt augmented with the extreme example identified only 1166 examples as toxic, affirming our intuition that as the exemplars became more extreme, the model labeled only the more extreme examples toxic.

4.4
Correlation between scores and ratings

A decoder only language model, such as the one we used, operates as follows:

1.

Parse the input text as a sequence of tokens, which are predefined character strings (determined at the time of training the model).

2.

Compute a probability distribution over all possible next tokens.

3.

Output the most probable next token.

4.

Append the output token to the input, go back to step (2).

Due to the nature of our prompt, the first token the model outputs is either “Yes” or “No” — the probabilities of these two tokens sum to almost 1. We can use the probability of “Yes” as a score for an example — if the score is above 0.5, the model outputs “Yes”, otherwise the model would output “No”.
The score can also be used as an estimate of the certainty of the model — if the score is far from 0.5, the model is more certain.
We studied the correlation between the score and the average (across raters) rating of an example, and found the correlation to be quite high (see Table
2
). We could use this observation to only route less certain examples to human raters, saving human effort.

4.6
Keyword matching vs. Language Understanding

In Appendix
C
, we study a random sample of 10 comments that were labelled toxic but were determined to be non-toxic by our model. 6 out of 10 Comments should have been labeled non-toxic. This demonstrates an application of foundation models to identify mislabeled examples. The examples shown are labeled toxic as they contain words that often occur in toxic contexts (e.g. idiot, Hitler, lazy), but in this case they are clearly not directed at any individual or group. The foundation models seem to detect the nuance of natural language better than raters in these examples.

The examples in Appendix
D
show an interesting side-effect of prompt tuning.
The predictions of the soft-prompt match the rater responses better.
However, sometimes the language nuances that the model was able to pick up with the few-shot prompt are over-ridden by the training process.
There is no free lunch!

4.7
Some More Examples

In Appendix
B
, we list a few example outputs of the model with the few-shot prompt. We show several outputs, some correct, some wrong, some toxic, some not.

Some of these examples show the model’s ability to deal with nuances of language.
The model was correctly able to recognize that ‘muck-head’ is an obscure insult and ‘shocked you can spell’ is a subtle way to humiliate someone.
In contrast, ‘hit the nail on the head’ is not an expression of violence.
It also recognized that ‘darn “Christina” holidays’ really meant Christian holidays, hence is attacking religion.

Example 9 shows a case where the model is able to predict multiple reasons to regard a comment as toxic.

The remaining examples show some errors made by the model. It is sometimes more eager to declare a comment toxic, taking offence at phrases like “what is wrong with this generation".
It occasionally misses idioms and slang, assuming that “beat me, sue
you, retire” is a threat of violence.
Occasionally it gets the answer right for the wrong reason, as in example 10 — the sarcastic phrase was “Did you really need me to explain that to you?", but it chose another phrase that was not referring to the participants in the conversation.

5
Workflows

As we have shown, foundation models can attain excellent performance with very little supervision (see Figures
2
and
1
. This enables a new workflow that relies on fewer labeled examples (rater supervision), and enables a product team to implement new policies with greater velocity. We illustrate this in Figure
3
.

In the first step, we use a description of a policy along with a handful of labeled examples as a few-shot prompt, and then run this on some data and manually inspect the result, tweaking the prompt till it performs adequately. This step is more art than science, but the discussions in Sections
2.1
can serve as a guide.

The next task is creating a training set for soft-prompt tuning. We either take a few dozen random examples and label them. We can use the technique in Appendix
E
to inspect and fix labels.
We then train a soft-prompt with this data (see Section
3
. We again evaluate this on a test sample, and modify the hard prompt, or expand the training set.

After some iterations, we are ready to deploy the prompt.
If the score produced by the model is extreme,
i.e.
the model is more certain, then we can accept the result. (As Section
4.4
shows, the scores are correlated with rater uncertainty.) Otherwise the example is sent off for human evaluation, and these examples can then be added to the soft-prompt training set for future iterations.

6
Limitations

•

We have only evaluated the method for English language text.

•

We have not tested on adversarial behavior, i.e., someone trying to defeat the classifier willfully.

•

The large language models that we use are not yet available widely.

•

Publicly available foundation models do not yet offer prompt tuning interfaces.
