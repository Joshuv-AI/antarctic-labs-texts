Solitons and soliton interactions in repulsive spinor Bose-Einstein condensates with non-zero background
Asela Abeya, Barbara Prinari, Gino Biondini, P.G. Kevrekidis

Abstract




Abstract.

We characterize the soliton solutions and their interactions for a system of coupled evolution equations of nonlinear Schrödinger (NLS) type
that models the dynamics in one-dimensional repulsive Bose-Einstein condensates with spin one, taking advantage of the representation of such model as a special reduction of a
2\times 2
matrix NLS system. Specifically, we study in detail the case in which solutions tend to a non-zero background at space infinities. First we derive a compact representation for the multi-soliton solutions in the system using the Inverse Scattering Transform (IST). We introduce the notion of canonical form of a solution, corresponding to the case when the background as
x\to\infty
is proportional to the identity. We show that solutions for which the asymptotic behavior at infinity is not proportional to the identity, referred to as being in non-canonical form, can be reduced to canonical form by unitary transformations that preserve the symmetric nature of the solution (physically corresponding to complex rotations of the quantization axes). Then we give a complete characterization of the two families of one-soliton solutions arising in this problem, corresponding to ferromagnetic and to polar states of the system, and we discuss how the physical parameters of the solitons for each family are related to the spectral data in the IST. We also show that any ferromagnetic one-soliton solution in canonical form can be reduced to a single dark soliton of the scalar NLS equation, and any polar one-soliton solution in canonical form is unitarily equivalent to a pair of oppositely polarized displaced scalar dark solitons up to a rotation of the quantization axes.
Finally, we discuss two-soliton interactions and we present a complete classification of the possible scenarios that can arise depending on whether either soliton is of ferromagnetic or polar type.

2
The defocusing spinor NLS equation with NZBC and its multi-soliton solutions

In this work we study the solutions of the defocusing spinor system (
1.1
)
with nonzero background, i.e., with nonzero boundary conditions (NZBC) as
x\to\pm\infty
.
To this end, it is convenient to rewrite the corresponding matrix NLS system (
1.2
) as

iQ_{t}+Q_{xx}-2(QQ^{\dagger}-\kappa_{o}^{2}I_{2})Q=0\,.

(2.1)

where

\kappa_{o}
is a real positive constant,

I_{n}
is the
n\times n
identity matrix
and

Q(x,t)
is the
2\times 2
symmetric matrix-valued potential in (
1.2b
), as before.
The term proportional to
\kappa_{o}^{2}I_{2}
in (
2.1
)
can be removed by the simple gauge transformation

Q(x,t)\mapsto Q(x,t)e^{2i\kappa_{o}^{2}t}
,
but it ensures that the background values of the potential are independent of time.
Namely,
Q(x,t)
satisfies
the following constant NZBC:

Q(x,t)\to Q_{\pm}\quad\text{as}\quad x\to\pm\infty\,.

(2.2)

Furthermore, we assume that the boundary conditions
(i.e., the asymptotic values for the potential)

Q_{\pm}
satisfy the constraint

Q^{\dagger}_{\pm}Q_{\pm}=Q_{\pm}Q^{\dagger}_{\pm}=\kappa_{o}^{2}I_{2}\,.

(2.3)

In terms of the individual entries of the matrices
Q_{\pm}
, (
2.3
) corresponds to the following equivalent set of constraints:

|q_{1,\pm}|^{2}=|q_{-1,\pm}|^{2}\,,\qquad|q_{0,\pm}|^{2}=\kappa_{o}^{2}-|q_{1,\pm}|^{2}=\kappa_{o}^{2}-|q_{-1,\pm}|^{2}\,,\qquad q_{1,\pm}q_{0,\pm}^{*}+q_{0,\pm}q_{-1,\pm}^{*}=0\,.

(2.4)

Note that the above conditions imply that
Q_{\pm}
are both normal matrices, and unitary up to normalization. Besides the norm of the background,
\kappa_{o}
, the boundary condition
Q_{+}
is then specified by three additional real parameters: the (common) amplitude of the diagonal entries and their two phases, with the amplitude and phase of the off-diagonal entry being completely determined by the last two conditions in (
2.4
).

2.1
Canonical versus non-canonical solutions and conserved quantities

Recall that the MNLS equation is invariant under unitary transformations.
Namely, if
Q(x,t)
is a solution of (
2.1
), then
\tilde{Q}(x,t)=U\,Q(x,t)V

is also a solution, with
U
and
V
arbitrary unitary matrices.
On the other hand, these transformations are admissible only if they preserve the symmetry of
Q(x,t)
, namely, if
\tilde{Q}(x,t)
is symmetric whenever

Q(x,t)
is,
in which case, as we show in Appendix
A.1
,
the transformations correspond physically to complex rotations of the quantization axes.

We say that
a solution is in canonical form when
Q_{+}=I_{2}
, and in non-canonical form if
Q_{+}\neq I_{2}
.
As we show next, any solution can be reduced to canonical form by rescaling and
a suitable rotation of the quantization axes.
(Note that we singled out the matrix
Q_{+}
, but an equivalent definition could be given using
Q_{-}
.)
Since
the background matrix
Q_{+}
is a normal matrix
(by virtue of (
2.3
)),
it is
unitarily diagonalizable.
Moreover, one can easily show that the eigenvalues of
Q_{+}
are

\kappa_{o}e^{i(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{-1})\pm i\delta}
,
where
\alpha_{\pm 1}
are determined by the phases of the diagonal entries of
Q_{+}
,
namely,

\alpha_{1}=2\mathop{\rm arg}\nolimits q_{1,+}

and
\alpha_{-1}=2\mathop{\rm arg}\nolimits q_{-1,+}
,
and

\sin\delta=\sqrt{1-(|q_{+,1}|^{2}/\kappa_{o}^{2})\,\cos(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{-1})}\,.

(2.5)

Furthermore, the orthogonal eigenvectors of
Q_{+}
can be chosen to be the
real vectors

\displaystyle v_{\pm}=\Big(a_{\pm}\,,\,\sqrt{\kappa_{o}^{2}-|q_{+,1}|^{2}}\,\Big)^{T}\,,\qquad a_{\pm}=|q_{+,1}|\sin(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{-1})\pm\sqrt{\kappa_{o}^{2}-|q_{+,1}|^{2}\cos^{2}(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{-1})}\,.

(2.6)

We can therefore write the background as

Q_{+}=\kappa_{o}V^{T}\Delta_{+}V
, where

\Delta_{+}=e^{i(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{-1})+i\delta\sigma_{3}}
,
with
\sigma_{3}=\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(1,-1)
the third Pauli matrix,
and

V=(v_{+}/\|v_{+}\|\,,\,v_{-}/\|v_{-}\|)
is the real matrix of orthonormal eigenvectors of
Q_{+}
.
Now consider the transformation

\tilde{Q}(x,t)=\Delta_{+}^{-1/2}VQ(x,t)V^{T}\Delta_{+}^{-1/2}\,.

(2.7)

It is easy to show that
\tilde{Q}(x,t)

is symmetric whenever
Q(x,t)
is.
As a consequence, without loss of generality one can take the background to be
Q_{+}=\kappa_{o}I_{2}

up to admissible unitary transformations, i.e.,
complex rotations of the quantization axes.
Finally, recall that the MNLS equation is scale invariant. Namely, if
Q(x,t)
is a solution of (
2.1
), so is

\tilde{Q}(x,t)=c\hat{Q}(cx,c^{2}t)

for any constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
.
Therefore, we can take
\kappa_{o}=1
,
which implies that
\hat{Q}(x,t)
is in canonical form.

The complete integrability of the MNLS equation implies that the system (
2.1
) has an infinite number of conserved quantities in involution. Of particular
importance for describing the physical properties of the condensate are the total number of holes/particles
\overline{N}
, and the total spin
{\mathbf{F}}
, which can be
expressed, respectively, as integrals over the spatial domain of the particle number density
\bar{n}(x,t)
and of the spin densities in the three components,
f_{1},f_{0},f_{-1}
, namely

\displaystyle\displaystyle\overline{N}=\mathop{\textstyle\int}\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{n}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,,\qquad\bar{n}(x,t)=\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(Q_{\pm}^{\dagger}Q_{\pm})-\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(Q^{\dagger}Q)\,,

(2.8a)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{F}}=\mathop{\textstyle\int}\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{f}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,,\qquad\mathbf{f}(x,t)\equiv\left(f_{1},f_{0},f_{-1}\right):=\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(Q^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\sigma}Q)\,,

(2.8b)

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3})
are the Pauli matrices.
For future reference, we note that the particle number density is invariant under arbitrary unitary transformations and remains the same
if a complex rotation of the quantization axes is performed to reduce the background
Q_{+}
to the identity.
The spin density, on the other hand, is not invariant under unitary transformations from the left. Indeed, one can
easily verify that multiplying
Q
by an arbitrary unitary matrix from the right does not change the spin density, while multiplication
from the left results in the spin density changing covariantly.
More specifically, under the transformation (
2.7
),
which, reduces the solution to canonical form up to the rescaling of
\kappa_{o}
,
the spin density becomes

\tilde{{\mathbf{f}}}(x,t)=S_{\theta,\delta}\,{\mathbf{f}}(x,t)

(2.9a)

where,
writing the orthogonal matrix
V
as
V=\cos\theta I_{2}-i\sin\theta\,\sigma_{2}

(
\sigma_{2}
being the second Pauli matrix, see Appendix
A.1
),

\displaystyle S_{\theta,\delta}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\delta\,\cos(2\theta)&-\sin\delta&\cos\delta\,\sin(2\theta)\\
\sin\delta\,\cos(2\theta)&\cos\delta&\sin\delta\,\sin(2\theta)\\
-\sin(2\theta)&0&\cos(2\theta)\end{pmatrix}\,,

(2.9b)

It is worth noticing that
S_{\theta,\delta}
is indeed an orthogonal matrix, and the
transformation to canonical form amounts to a rotation of the quantization axes (again, see Appendix
A.1
).

2.2
Overview of the IST for the defocusing MNLS equation with NZBC

In order to derive an expression for the multi-soliton solutions of the defocusing MNLS equation (
1.2
),
and to fully characterize the physical parameters of the solitons in terms of spectral data,
it is convenient to first briefly review the IST for (
1.2
) with NZBC
that was developed in
[
47
]
.

As originally shown in
[
17
]
, the MNLS equation (
2.1
) for a
2\times 2
potential matrix
Q(x,t)
is equivalent to the compatibility condition (
\phi_{xt}=\phi_{tx}
) of the following
4\times 4
Lax pair:

\phi_{x}={\mathbf{U}}\phi\,,\qquad\phi_{t}={\mathbf{V}}\phi\,,

(2.10)

with

\displaystyle{\mathbf{U}}(x,t,k)=-ik{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{3}+\mathbf{Q},\quad{\mathbf{V}}(x,t,k)=-2ik^{2}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{3}+2k\mathbf{Q}+i{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{3}[\mathbf{Q}_{x}+\kappa_{o}^{2}I_{4}-\mathbf{Q}^{2}],

(2.11a)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{2}&0_{2}\\
0_{2}&-I_{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}0_{2}&Q\\
Q^{\dagger}&0_{2}\end{pmatrix},

(2.11b)

where

0_{n}
is the
n\times n
zero matrix.
As usual,
one refers to the first equation of the Lax pair (
2.10
) as the scattering problem.
The IST for the MNLS (
2.1
) with non-zero boundary conditions was developed in
[
20
,
24
,
47
]
.
Next we give a brief overview of the IST formulation following
[
47
]
,
which we will then use to obtain a formula for the multi-soliton solution.

Importantly, note that the constraint (
2.3
) on the boundary conditions plays the same role as the “equal amplitude” boundary condition in the scalar and vector NLS equations, and it ensures
that the asymptotic scattering problems as
x\to\pm\infty
are equal and only have two branch points.
Indeed, taking into account (
2.3
), the asymptotic scattering problems
(which are obtained by replacing
\mathbf{Q}
with
\mathbf{Q}_{\pm}
in (
2.11a
))
have eigenvalues
\pm i\lambda
with
\lambda=(k^{2}-\kappa_{o}^{2})^{1/2}
, and each eigenvalue has multiplicity 2.
As in the IST for the Manakov system
[
52
]
,
is convenient to introduce uniformization variable
z
defined by the conformal mapping

z=k+\lambda,

(2.12)

whose inverse transformation is

k=\frac{1}{2}(z+\kappa_{o}^{2}/z),\quad\lambda=\frac{1}{2}(z-\kappa_{o}^{2}/z).

(2.13)

Consequently,
\operatorname{Im}\lambda>0
corresponds to the region
\mathbb{C}^{+}
in the
z
-plane, and
\operatorname{Im}\lambda<0
corresponds to the region
\mathbb{C}^{-}
in the
z
-plane.
The Jost solutions are defined as the simultaneous solutions of both parts of the Lax pair identified by the BCs:

\displaystyle{\Phi}(x,t,z)\equiv(\varphi(x,t,z),\bar{\varphi}(x,t,z))=X_{-}(z)e^{-i\theta(x,t,z){\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{3}}(1+o(1)),\qquad x\to-\infty,

(2.14a)

\displaystyle{\Psi}(x,t,z)\equiv(\bar{\psi}(x,t,z),\psi(x,t,z))=X_{+}(z)e^{-i\theta(x,t,z){\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{3}}(1+o(1)),\qquad x\to\infty,

(2.14b)

where
\varphi(x,t,z)
,
\bar{\varphi}(x,t,z)
,
\bar{\psi}(x,t,z)
and
\psi(x,t,z)
are
4\times 2
matrices,

\theta(x,t,z)=\lambda(z)(x+2k(z)t),

(2.15)

and

\displaystyle X_{\pm}(z)=I_{4}-\frac{i}{z}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{3}\mathbf{Q}_{\pm},\qquad X_{\pm}^{-1}(z)=\frac{1}{\gamma(z)}\left(I_{4}+\frac{i}{z}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{3}\mathbf{Q}_{\pm}\right),\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0,\pm\kappa_{o}\},

(2.16a)

\displaystyle\det X_{\pm}(z)=\left(\frac{2\lambda}{\lambda+k}\right)^{2}=(\gamma(z))^{2},\quad\gamma(z)=1-\frac{\kappa_{o}^{2}}{z^{2}}.

(2.16b)

As usual, the continuous spectrum of the scattering problem corresponds to values of
(k,\lambda)
, or, equivalently,
z
, such that
all four eigenfunctions above are bounded for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, which requires
\lambda(k)\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
and hence
k\in(\infty,-\kappa_{o})\cup(\kappa_{o},+\infty)
.
In the
z
-plane, the continuous spectrum is
\Sigma:=\mathbb{R}\setminus\left\{\pm\kappa_{o}\right\}
.
A complete set of modified analytic eigenfunctions with constant limit as
x\to\pm\infty
can be defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}(x,t,z)\equiv(M(x,t,z),\bar{M}(x,t,z))={\Phi}(x,t,z)\,e^{i\theta(x,t,z){\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{3}},

(2.17a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}(x,t,z)\equiv(\bar{N}(x,t,z),N(x,t,z))={\Psi}(x,t,z)\,e^{i\theta(x,t,z){\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{3}},

(2.17b)

One can express the modified eigenfunctions
M
,
\bar{M}
,
N
and
\bar{N}

as solutions of suitable Volterra-type integral equations,
and show that under some mild integrability conditions of
Q(x,t)-Q_{\pm}
for
x\in(x_{o},\pm\infty)
and any fixed
t\geqslant 0
, the modified eigenfunctions
M(x,t,z)
and
N(x,t,z)
can be analytically extended to
\mathbb{C}^{+}
in the
z
-plane. Similarly, the modified eigenfunctions
\bar{M}(x,t,z)
and
\bar{N}(x,t,z)
can be analytically extended to
\mathbb{C}^{-}
in the
z
-plane.

Because

\det{\Phi}(x,t,z)=\det{\Psi}(x,t,z)=\det X_{\pm}=(\gamma(z))^{2}
for all
x,t,z\in\mathbb{R}
,

{\Phi}
and
{\Psi}
are both fundamental solutions of the scattering problem. Hence there exists a proportionality matrix
{S}(z)
between the two fundamental solutions, such that

{\Phi}(x,t,z)={\Psi}(x,t,z){S}(z),\qquad{S}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}a(z)&\bar{b}(z)\\
b(z)&\bar{a}(z)\end{pmatrix},\quad x,t\in\mathbb{R},\quad z\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{\pm\kappa_{o}\},

(2.18)

where
S(z)
is referred to as the scattering coefficient matrix and
a,b,\bar{a},\bar{b}
are
2\times 2
block matrices. Since
\det\Phi=\det\Psi
we have
\det S(z)=1
for
z\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{\pm\kappa_{o}\}
. In turn,
from (
2.18
) it also follows that:

\displaystyle\det a(z)=\text{Wr}(\varphi,\psi)/\text{Wr}(\bar{\psi},\psi)\equiv\det(\varphi,\psi)/\det\Psi=\det(\varphi,\psi)/(\gamma(z))^{2},

(2.19a)

\displaystyle\det\bar{a}(z)=\text{Wr}(\bar{\psi},\bar{\varphi})/\text{Wr}(\bar{\psi},\psi)\equiv\det(\bar{\psi},\bar{\varphi})/\det\Psi=\det(\bar{\psi},\bar{\varphi})/(\gamma(z))^{2},

(2.19b)

where
\text{Wr}(u,v)
denotes the Wronskian determinant of
4\times 2
vector functions
u
and
v
.

The Jost eigenfunctions satisfy the following symmetry relations with respect to the involution
z\mapsto z^{*}
:

\Phi^{\dagger}(x,t,z^{*}){\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{3}\Phi(x,t,z)=\Psi^{\dagger}(x,t,z^{*}){\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{3}\Psi(x,t,z)=\gamma(z){\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{3}\,.

(2.20)

We will use the following notation to denote the
2\times 2
blocks of the eigenfunction matrices
\Phi
and
\Psi
:

\Phi(x,t,z)=\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{\text{up}}&\bar{\varphi}_{\text{up}}\\
\varphi_{\text{dn}}&\bar{\varphi}_{\text{dn}}\end{pmatrix},\quad\Psi(x,t,z)=\begin{pmatrix}\bar{\psi}_{\text{up}}&\psi_{\text{up}}\\
\bar{\psi}_{\text{dn}}&\psi_{\text{dn}}\end{pmatrix},

(2.21)

so (
2.20
) can be written in block-matrix form

\displaystyle\gamma(z)a(z)=\bar{\psi}_{\text{up}}^{\dagger}(z^{*})\varphi_{\text{up}}(z)-\bar{\psi}_{\text{dn}}^{\dagger}(z^{*})\varphi_{\text{dn}}(z),

(2.22a)

\displaystyle\gamma(z)\bar{a}(z)={\psi}_{\text{dn}}^{\dagger}(z^{*})\bar{\varphi}_{\text{dn}}(z)-{\psi}_{\text{up}}^{\dagger}(z^{*})\bar{\varphi}_{\text{up}}(z),

(2.22b)

\displaystyle\gamma(z)b(z)={\psi}_{\text{dn}}^{\dagger}(z^{*})\varphi_{\text{dn}}(z)-{\psi}_{\text{up}}^{\dagger}(z^{*})\varphi_{\text{up}}(z),

(2.22c)

\displaystyle\gamma(z)\bar{b}(z)=\bar{\psi}_{\text{up}}^{\dagger}(z^{*})\bar{\varphi}_{\text{up}}(z)-\bar{\psi}_{\text{dn}}^{\dagger}(z^{*})\bar{\varphi}_{\text{dn}}(z)\,,

(2.22d)

where the
x,t
dependence of the eigenfunctions on the right-hand side has been omitted for shortness.
The above relations show that
a(z)
can be analytically extended to
\mathbb{C}^{+}
, and
\bar{a}(z)
can be analytically extended to
\mathbb{C}^{-}
.
Also, we obtain

S^{-1}(z)={\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{3}S^{\dagger}(z^{*}){\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{3},\quad S^{-1}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}\bar{c}(z)&d(z)\\
\bar{d}(z)&c(z)\end{pmatrix}\,,

(2.23)

which provides symmetries for the scattering coefficients:

\displaystyle a^{\dagger}(z^{*})a(z)-b^{\dagger}(z^{*})b(z)=I_{2},\qquad a^{\dagger}(z^{*})\bar{b}(z)-b^{\dagger}(z^{*})\bar{a}(z)=0_{2},

(2.24a)

\displaystyle\bar{b}^{\dagger}(z^{*})a(z)-\bar{a}^{\dagger}(z^{*})b(z)=0_{2},\qquad\bar{b}^{\dagger}(z^{*})\bar{b}(z)-\bar{a}^{\dagger}(z^{*})\bar{a}(z)=-I_{2}.

(2.24b)

and

\bar{c}(z)=a^{\dagger}(z^{*}),\quad d(z)=-b^{\dagger}(z^{*}),\quad\bar{d}(z)=-\bar{b}^{\dagger}(z^{*}),\quad c(z)=\bar{a}^{\dagger}(z^{*}).

(2.25)

The scattering problem also admits a second involution:
z\mapsto\kappa_{o}^{2}/z
.
The corresponding symmetries for the eigenfunctions are given by:

\Phi(x,t,z)=-\frac{i}{z}\Phi(x,t,\kappa_{o}^{2}/z){\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{3}\mathbf{Q}_{-}\,,\quad\Psi(x,t,z)=-\frac{i}{z}\Psi(x,t,\kappa_{o}^{2}/z){\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{3}\mathbf{Q}_{+},\quad z\in\Sigma\,.

(2.26)

Explicitly, each of the
4\times 2
Jost eigenfunctions satisfies

\displaystyle\varphi(x,t,z)=\frac{i}{z}\bar{\varphi}(x,t,\kappa_{o}^{2}/z)Q_{-}^{\dagger},\quad\bar{\varphi}(x,t,z)=-\frac{i}{z}\varphi(x,t,\kappa_{o}^{2}/z)Q_{-}\,,

(2.27a)

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}(x,t,z)=\frac{i}{z}\psi(x,t,\kappa_{o}^{2}/z)Q_{+}^{\dagger},\quad\psi(x,t,z)=-\frac{i}{z}\bar{\psi}(x,t,\kappa_{o}^{2}/z)Q_{+}\,.

(2.27b)

These symmetries imply the following relations for the scattering data:

\displaystyle a(\kappa_{o}^{2}/z)=\frac{1}{\kappa_{o}^{2}}Q_{+}\bar{a}(z)Q^{\dagger}_{-}\,,\qquad\bar{a}(\kappa_{o}^{2}/z)=\frac{1}{\kappa_{o}^{2}}Q^{\dagger}_{+}a(z)Q_{-}\,,

(2.28a)

\displaystyle b(\kappa_{o}^{2}/z)=-\frac{1}{\kappa_{o}^{2}}Q^{\dagger}_{+}\bar{b}(z)Q^{\dagger}_{-}\,,\qquad\bar{b}(\kappa_{o}^{2}/z)=-\frac{1}{\kappa_{o}^{2}}Q_{+}b(z)Q_{-}\,.

(2.28b)

A third symmetry follows from the fact that we assume the potential
Q(x,t)
to be a symmetric matrix. Correspondingly, the eigenfunctions satisfy the following symmetries:

\Phi^{T}(x,t,z){\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{2}\Phi(x,t,z)=\Psi^{T}(x,t,z){\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{2}\Psi(x,t,z)=\gamma(z){\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{2},\qquad{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0_{2}&-iI_{2}\\
iI_{2}&0_{2}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(2.29)

where the superscript
T
denotes matrix transpose,
which implies that

S^{T}(z){\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{2}S(z)={\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{2},\quad z\in\Sigma\,,

(2.30)

and consequently

c(z)=a^{T}(z),\quad\bar{c}(z)=\bar{a}^{T}(z),\quad d(z)=-\bar{b}^{T}(z),\quad\bar{d}(z)=-b^{T}(z).

(2.31)

The discrete spectrum is the set of all values
z_{j}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
where
\det a(z)=0
or
\det\bar{a}(z)=0
. Since the scattering operator is self-adjoint,
z_{j}\in C_{o}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=\kappa_{o}\}
. Moreover, the symmetries of the scattering data imply that
\det a(z)=0
if and only if
\det\bar{a}(z^{*})=0
. Suppose that
\det a(z)
has a finite number
J
of zeros
z_{1},\ldots,z_{J}
in
C_{0}^{+}=C_{0}\cap\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Im}>0\}
and, by symmetry,
\det\bar{a}(z)
has a finite number
J
of zeros
z_{1}^{*},\ldots,z_{J}^{*}
in
C_{0}^{-}=C_{0}\cap\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Im}<0\}
. Let us define

P(x,t,z)=(\varphi(x,t,z),\psi(x,t,z)),\quad\bar{P}(x,t,z)=(\bar{\psi}(x,t,z),\bar{\varphi}(x,t,z))\,.

(2.32)

As we will discuss next, the nature of the discrete eigenvalue
z_{j}
(or, equivalently,
z_{j}^{*}
) depends on the rank of the matrix
P(x,t,z_{j})
(equivalently,
\bar{P}(x,t,z_{j}^{*})
).

Norming constants and residue conditions: Case 1,
\operatorname{rank}P(x,t,z_{n})=3
.

As shown in
[
47
]
, the Wronskian representation (
2.19
) in this case yields

\varphi(x,t,z_{n})\alpha(z_{n})=\psi(x,t,z_{n})c_{n},\qquad\bar{\varphi}(x,t,z_{n}^{*})\bar{\alpha}(z_{n})=\bar{\psi}(x,t,z_{n}^{*})\bar{c}_{n},

(2.33)

where
c_{n}
and
\bar{c}_{n}
are constant 2
\times
2 rank-1 matrices, and
\alpha(z),\,\bar{\alpha}(z)
denotes the adjugate (or cofactor) matrix of
a(z)
and
\bar{a}(z)
, respectively.
These provide the residue relations

\displaystyle\underset{z=z_{n}}{\operatorname{Res}}[M(x,t,z_{n})a^{-1}(z)]=e^{2i\theta(x,t,z_{n})}N(x,t,z_{n})C_{n},\qquad C_{n}=\frac{c_{n}}{(\det a)^{\prime}(z_{n})}\,\qquad\det C_{n}=0,

(2.34a)

\displaystyle\underset{z=z_{n}^{*}}{\operatorname{Res}}[\bar{M}(x,t,z)\bar{a}^{-1}(z)]=e^{-2i\theta(x,t,z_{n}^{*})}\bar{N}(x,t,z_{n}^{*})\bar{C}_{n},\qquad\bar{C}_{n}=\frac{\bar{c}_{n}}{(\det\bar{a})^{\prime}(z_{n}^{*})}\,,\qquad\det\bar{C}_{n}=0,

(2.34b)

where prime denotes the derivative with respect to
z
.

Norming constants and residue conditions: Case 2,
\operatorname{rank}P(x,t,z_{n})=2
.

It is possible, on the other hand, to have double zeros of
\det a(z)
and
\det\bar{a}(z)
for which the matrices

Ma^{-1}
and
\bar{M}\bar{a}^{-1}
still have a simple pole for such a value of
z
.
When this happens,
a(z_{n})=\bar{a}(z_{n}^{*})=0_{2\times 2}
and
\operatorname{rank}P(x,t,z_{n})=\operatorname{rank}\bar{P}(x,t,z_{n}^{*})=2
.
In this scenario a stronger condition of proportionality between the eigenfunctions holds, namely:

\varphi(x,t,z_{n})=\psi(x,t,z_{n})b_{n},\qquad\bar{\varphi}(x,t,z_{n}^{*})=\bar{\psi}(x,t,z_{n}^{*}){\bar{b}}_{n},

(2.35)

where
b_{n},\bar{b}_{n}
are constant, non-singular
2\times 2
matrices. In this case, the residue conditions read

\displaystyle\underset{z=z_{n}}{\operatorname{Res}}[M(x,t,z)a^{-1}(z)]=e^{2i\theta(x,t,z_{n})}N(x,t,z_{n})C_{n},\qquad C_{n}=\frac{2b_{n}\alpha^{\prime}(z_{n})}{(\det a)^{\prime\prime}(z_{n})},

(2.36a)

\displaystyle\underset{z=z_{n}^{*}}{\operatorname{Res}}[\bar{M}(x,t,z)\bar{a}^{-1}(z)]=e^{-2i\theta(x,t,z_{n}^{*})}\bar{N}(x,t,z_{n}^{*})\bar{C}_{n},\qquad\bar{C}_{n}=\frac{2\bar{b}_{n}\bar{\alpha}^{\prime}(z_{n}^{*})}{(\det\bar{a})^{\prime\prime}(z_{n}^{*})}.

(2.36b)

and although the residue conditions formally have the same expression as in the rank-1 case (cf (
2.36
)), here the norming constants
C_{n},\bar{C}_{n}
need not be rank-1 matrices.

The asymptotic behaviors of the eigenfunctions and the scattering data as
z\to\infty
and
z\to 0
are needed in order to properly formulate the
inverse problem for the eigenfunctions, and subsequently reconstruct the potential matrix. They are given in
[
47
]
as:

\displaystyle M(x,t,z)=\begin{pmatrix}I_{2}+\frac{i}{z}\mathop{\textstyle\int}\limits_{-\infty}^{x}[Q(x^{\prime},t)Q^{\dagger}(x^{\prime},t)-\kappa_{o}^{2}I_{2}]dx^{\prime}+O(1/z^{2})\\
\frac{i}{z}Q^{\dagger}(x,t)+O(1/z^{2})\end{pmatrix}\qquad z\to\infty,\,z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

(2.37a)

\displaystyle\bar{M}(x,t,z)=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{i}{z}Q(x,t)+O(1/z^{2})\\
I_{2}-\frac{i}{z}\mathop{\textstyle\int}\limits_{-\infty}^{x}[Q^{\dagger}(x^{\prime},t)Q(x^{\prime},t)-\kappa_{o}^{2}I_{2}]dx^{\prime}+O(1/z^{2})\end{pmatrix}\qquad z\to\infty,\,z\in\mathbb{C}^{-},

(2.37b)

\displaystyle\bar{N}(x,t,z)=\begin{pmatrix}I_{2}+\frac{i}{z}\mathop{\textstyle\int}\limits_{x}^{\infty}[Q(x^{\prime},t)Q^{\dagger}(x^{\prime},t)-\kappa_{o}^{2}I_{2}]dx^{\prime}+O(1/z^{2})\\
\frac{i}{z}Q^{\dagger}(x,t)+O(1/z^{2})\end{pmatrix}\qquad z\to\infty,\,z\in\mathbb{C}^{-},

(2.37c)

\displaystyle N(x,t,z)=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{i}{z}Q(x,t)+O(1/z^{2})\\
I_{2}-\frac{i}{z}\mathop{\textstyle\int}\limits_{x}^{\infty}[Q^{\dagger}(x^{\prime},t)Q(x^{\prime},t)-\kappa_{o}^{2}I_{2}]dx^{\prime}+O(1/z^{2})\end{pmatrix}\qquad z\to\infty,z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

(2.37d)

\displaystyle M(x,t,z)=\begin{pmatrix}QQ^{\dagger}_{-}/\kappa_{o}^{2}+O(z)\\
iQ^{\dagger}_{-}/z+O(1)\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad N(x,t,z)=\begin{pmatrix}-iQ_{+}/z+O(1)\\
Q^{\dagger}Q_{+}/\kappa_{o}^{2}+O(z)\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{as }z\to 0,\,z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

(2.37e)

\displaystyle\bar{M}(x,t,z)=\begin{pmatrix}-iQ_{-}/z+O(1)\\
Q^{\dagger}Q_{-}/\kappa_{o}^{2}+O(z)\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad\bar{N}(x,t,z)=\begin{pmatrix}QQ^{{\dagger}}_{+}/\kappa_{o}^{2}+O(z)\\
iQ^{{\dagger}}_{+}/z+O(1)\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{as }z\to 0,\,z\in\mathbb{C}^{-},

(2.37f)

implying

S(z)=I_{2}+O(1/z),\quad z\to\infty,\qquad S(z)=\frac{1}{\kappa_{o}^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}Q_{+}Q^{\dagger}_{-}&0_{2}\\
0_{2}&Q^{\dagger}_{+}Q_{-}\end{pmatrix}+O(z),\quad z\to 0,

(2.38)

with both limits taken along the real axis.

The inverse problem can be formulated as a matrix Riemann-Hilbert problem (RHP) in terms of the uniformization variable:

\mu^{-}(x,t,z)=\mu^{+}(x,t,z)(I_{4}-G(x,t,z)),\quad z\in\Sigma,

(2.39)

where the sectionally meromorphic matrices are

\mu(x,t,z)=\begin{cases}(Ma^{-1},N),\qquad\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits z>0\,,\\
(\bar{N},\bar{M}\bar{a}^{-1}),\qquad\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits z<0\,,\end{cases}

(2.40)

with
\mu^{\pm}(x,t,z)
denoting the projection of
\mu(x,t,z)
to the real
z-
axis from above/below, the jump matrix is

G(x,t,z)=\begin{pmatrix}0_{2}&-e^{-2i\theta(x,t,z)}\bar{\rho}(z)\\
e^{2i\theta(x,t,z)}\rho(z)&\rho(z)\bar{\rho}(z)\end{pmatrix},

(2.41)

and the reflection coefficients are
\rho(z)=b(z)a^{-1}(z)
and
\bar{\rho}(z)=\bar{b}(z)\bar{a}^{-1}(z)
. The matrices
\mu^{\pm}(x,t,z)-I_{2}
are
O(1/z)
as
z\to\infty
. After regularization, to account for the pole at
z=0
and at the discrete eigenvalues
\{z_{j},z_{j}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{N}
, the RHP can be solved via Cauchy projectors, and the asymptotic behavior of the upper
2\times 2
block of
N(x,t,z)
as
z\to\infty

yields the reconstruction formula

Q(x,t)=Q_{+}+i\mathop{\textstyle\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{J}e^{-2i\theta(x,t,z_{j}^{*})}\bar{N}_{\text{up}}(x,t,z_{j}^{*})\bar{C}_{j}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathop{\textstyle\int}\limits_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-2i\theta(x,t,\zeta)}\bar{N}_{\text{up}}(x,t,\zeta)\bar{\rho}(\zeta)d\zeta.

(2.42)

Using the reconstruction formula and the second symmetry we obtain the symmetry relations for the norming constant

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{n}=C_{n}^{\dagger},\qquad Q_{+}C_{n}=e^{2i\mathop{\rm arg}\nolimits(z_{n})}\bar{C}_{n}Q_{+}^{\dagger}

(2.43a)

\displaystyle C_{n}^{T}=C_{n},\qquad\bar{C}_{n}^{T}=\bar{C}_{n}

(2.43b)

where
n=1,...,J
.
We are interested in potentials
Q(x,t)
where the reflection coefficient
\rho(z)
is identically zero for
z\in\mathbb{R}
, which implies that
\bar{\rho}(z)
is also zero for
z\in\mathbb{R}
. Under this assumption of reflectionless potentials, we have

Q(x,t)=Q_{+}+i\mathop{\textstyle\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{J}e^{-2i\theta(x,t,z_{j}^{*})}\bar{N}_{\text{up}}(x,t,z_{j}^{*})\bar{C}_{j},

(2.44)

with

\displaystyle\bar{N}_{\text{up}}(x,t,z_{n}^{*})=I_{2}+\mathop{\textstyle\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{J}\frac{e^{2i\theta(x,t,z_{j})}N_{\text{up}}(x,t,z_{j})C_{j}}{z_{n}^{*}-z_{j}},

(2.45a)

\displaystyle N_{\text{up}}(x,t,z_{n})=-\frac{i}{z_{n}}Q_{+}+\mathop{\textstyle\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{J}\frac{e^{-2i\theta(x,t,z_{j}^{*})}\bar{N}_{\text{up}}(x,t,z_{j}^{*})\bar{C}_{j}}{z_{n}-z_{j}^{*}}.

(2.45b)

Solving the linear system (
2.45
) for the eigenfunctions and substituting into the reconstruction formula (
2.42
) yields
the
J
soliton solution for the defocusing MNLS.

2.3
Multi-soliton solutions

Using the IST formalism above, we now derive an explicit formula for the general multi-soliton solution of (
2.1
) with
\nu=1
and NZBC.
First,
substituting (
2.45b
) into (
2.45a
) we have

\bar{N}_{\text{up}}(x,t,z_{n}^{*})=I_{2}-iQ_{+}\mathop{\textstyle\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{J}\frac{e^{2i\theta(x,t,z_{j})}C_{j}}{z_{j}(z_{n}^{*}-z_{j})}+\mathop{\textstyle\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{J}\mathop{\textstyle\sum}\limits_{l=1}^{J}\frac{e^{2i(\theta(x,t,z_{j})-\theta(x,t,z_{l}^{*}))}}{(z_{n}^{*}-z_{j})(z_{j}-z_{l}^{*})}\bar{N}_{\text{up}}(x,t,z_{l}^{*})\bar{C}_{l}C_{j}.

(2.46)

Note that the exponents
i\theta(x,t,z_{j})
and
i\theta(x,t,z_{j}^{*})
appearing in (
2.44
) and (
2.45
)
are all real.
For convenience let us take the transpose of the equation (
2.46
),

\displaystyle\bar{N}_{\text{up}}^{T}(x,t,z_{n}^{*})=I_{2}-i\mathop{\textstyle\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{J}\frac{e^{2i\theta(x,t,z_{j})}C_{j}}{z_{j}(z_{n}^{*}-z_{j})}\,Q_{+}+\mathop{\textstyle\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{J}\mathop{\textstyle\sum}\limits_{l=1}^{J}\frac{e^{2i(\theta(x,t,z_{j})-\theta(x,t,z_{l}^{*}))}}{(z_{n}^{*}-z_{j})(z_{j}-z_{l}^{*})}C_{j}\bar{C}_{l}\bar{N}_{\text{up}}^{T}(x,t,z_{l}^{*}).

(2.47)

Introducing
{\mathbf{X}}=(Y_{1},Y_{2},...,Y_{J})^{T}
and
{\mathbf{B}}=(V_{1},V_{2},...,V_{J})^{T}
where

Y_{n}=\bar{N}_{\text{up}}^{T}(x,t,z_{n}^{*}),\qquad V_{n}=I_{2}-i\mathop{\textstyle\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{J}\frac{e^{2i\theta(x,t,z_{j})}C_{j}}{z_{j}(z_{n}^{*}-z_{j})}\,Q_{+},\quad n=1,..,J,

and defining the
2J\times 2J
matrix
A=(A_{n,l})
, where

A_{n,l}=\mathop{\textstyle\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{J}\frac{e^{2i(\theta(x,t,z_{j})-\theta(x,t,z_{l}^{*}))}}{(z_{n}^{*}-z_{j})(z_{j}-z_{l}^{*})}C_{j}\bar{C}_{l}\,,\qquad n,l=1,2,..,J,

the system (
2.47
) becomes simply
R{\mathbf{X}}={\mathbf{B}}
, where
R=I_{2J}-A=(R_{1},R_{2},...,R_{2J})
and

{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{B}}
, and
A
consists of
2\times 2
block matrices.
We can then rewrite this system as

RX_{1}=B_{1},\qquad RX_{2}=B_{2},

(2.48)

with
{\mathbf{X}}=(X_{1},X_{2})
and
{\mathbf{B}}=(B_{1},B_{2})
, whose solution (
2.48
) is simply:
X_{n,1}=\det\hat{R}^{\text{ext}}_{n}/\det R
and
X_{n,2}=\det\check{R}^{\text{ext}}_{n}/\det R
for
n=1,2,...,2J
, where

\hat{R}^{\text{ext}}_{n}=(R_{1},R_{2},\dots,R_{n-1},B_{1},R_{n+1},\dots,R_{2J})\,,\qquad\check{R}^{\text{ext}}_{n}=(R_{1},R_{2},\dots,R_{n-1},B_{2},R_{n+1},\dots,R_{2J}).

It then follows that

\displaystyle\bar{N}_{\text{up}}^{T}(x,t,z_{n}^{*})=\begin{pmatrix}X_{2n-1,1}&X_{2n-1,2}\\
X_{2n,1}&X_{2n,2}\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad n=1,2,\dots,J,

(2.49)

and Eq. (
2.44
) yields

Q(x,t)=Q_{+}+i\mathop{\textstyle\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{J}e^{-2i\theta(x,t,z_{j}^{*})}\bar{C}_{j}\,\bar{N}_{\text{up}}^{T}(x,t,z_{j}^{*}).

(2.50)

Finally, upon substituting
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{J}
into the above formula, the resulting expression for the potential can be written compactly as

\displaystyle Q(x,t)=\frac{1}{\det R}\begin{pmatrix}\det N_{11}^{\text{aug}}&\det N_{12}^{\text{aug}}\\
\det N_{21}^{\text{aug}}&\det N_{22}^{\text{aug}}\end{pmatrix},

(2.51)

where the augmented
(2J+1)\times(2J+1)
matrices are given by

N_{jk}^{\text{aug}}=\begin{pmatrix}Q_{+,jk}&-iD_{j}^{T}\\
B_{k}&R\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad j,k\in\{1,2\},

(2.52a)

and

Q_{+}=(Q_{+,ij})\,,\quad i,j\in\{1,2\}\,,\quad(D_{1},D_{2})=(E_{1},E_{2},\dots,E_{N})^{T}\,,\quad E_{n}=e^{-2i\theta(x,t,z_{n}^{*})}\bar{C}_{n}\,,\quad n=1,\dots,J\,.

(2.52b)

3
One-soliton solutions

In this section we discuss and classify the one-soliton solutions,
namely the solutions obtained from (
2.51
) with
J=1
.

3.1
Classification of one-soliton solutions

Solving
(
2.44
) when
J=1
we have

Q(x,t)=Q_{+}+\frac{ie^{2i\theta(x,t,z_{1})}}{z_{1}}\Big(I_{2}+\dfrac{ie^{2i\theta(x,t,z_{1})}}{(z_{1}^{*}-z_{1})}\Pi_{1}\Big)^{-1}\Pi_{1}Q_{+},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R},\,t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\,,

(3.1)

where
\theta(x,t,z)
is as in (
2.15
),
z_{1}=\kappa_{o}e^{i\varphi}
with
\varphi\in(0,\pi)
and

\Pi_{1}=\frac{1}{z_{1}}Q_{+}C_{1}\,,

(3.2)

with
\Pi_{1}=\Pi_{1}^{\dagger}
thanks to (
2.43a
).
Since
\Pi_{1}
is Hermitian, there exists a unitary matrix
U
such that
U\,\Pi_{1}\,U^{\dagger}=\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{-1})
where
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{-1}
are
the (real) eigenvalues of
\Pi_{1}
, and (
3.1
) can be written as:

Q(x,t)=U^{\dagger}\Big[I_{2}+\frac{ie^{2i\theta(x,t,z_{1})}}{z_{1}}\Big(I_{2}+\dfrac{ie^{2i\theta(x,t,z_{1})}}{(z_{1}^{*}-z_{1})}\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{-1})\Big)^{-1}\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{-1})\Big]UQ_{+}\,.

(3.3)

This solution is regular for all
x,t\in\mathbb{R}
, if and only if
\gamma_{1}\leq 0
and
\gamma_{-1}\leq 0
.
Further simplification yields,

Q(x,t)=U^{\dagger}\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(q_{\text{dark},1}(x,t),q_{\text{dark},-1}(x,t))\,UQ_{+}\,,

(3.4)

whenever
\det\Pi_{1}\neq 0
, (i.e.
\gamma_{1}<0
and
\gamma_{-1}<0
) and

q_{\text{dark},j}(x,t)=e^{-i\varphi}\{\cos\varphi+i\sin\varphi\tanh[\kappa_{o}\sin\varphi(x-x_{j}+2\kappa_{o}\,t\cos\varphi)]\}

(3.5)

with
x_{j}
such that
e^{-2x_{j}\,\kappa_{o}\,\sin\varphi}=-2\kappa_{o}\,\sin\varphi/\gamma_{j}
, for
j=1,-1
. Furthermore, if
\det\Pi_{1}=0
then without loss of generality one can assume
\gamma_{-1}=0
, which yields,

Q(x,t)=U^{\dagger}\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(q_{\text{dark},1}(x,t),1)\,UQ_{+}\,.

(3.6)

Exploiting the invariance of MNLS under unitary transformations, we can consider

\tilde{Q}(x,t)=U\,Q(x,t)\,U^{\dagger}\equiv\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(q_{\text{dark},1}(x,t),q_{\text{dark},-1}(x,t))\,UQ_{+}U^{\dagger}\,,

(3.7)

and if the quantization axes have been chosen so that
Q_{+}=I_{2}
(cf. Sec.
2.1
), then

\tilde{Q}(x,t)=\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(q_{\text{dark},1}(x,t),q_{\text{dark},-1}(x,t))\,.

(3.8)

(The unitary transformations (
3.7
) are obviously
admissible, since
\tilde{Q}
is diagonal.) The same obviously holds for (
3.6
), in which case

\tilde{Q}(x,t)=\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(q_{\text{dark},1}(x,t),1)\,.

(3.9)

Specifically, to derive (
3.8
) we have to use two subsequent unitary transformations, first reducing
Q_{+}
to identity and secondly, diagonalizing
\Pi_{1}
. Notice that when we reduce (
3.1
) to its canonical form (i.e., when
Q_{+}=I_{2}
),
\Pi_{1}
becomes a real symmetric matrix, therefore we can find
an orthogonal matrix
W
that diagonalizes
\Pi_{1}
. Thus using the discussion in section
2.1
one can show that the
\tilde{Q}(x,t)
defined in (
3.8
) can be written as:

\tilde{Q}(x,t)=\tilde{U}Q(x,t)\tilde{U}^{T}\,,

(3.10)

with the unitary matrix
\tilde{U}:=W\Delta^{-1/2}\,V
, where
\Delta
and
V
are defined in equation (
2.6
) and
W
is the orthogonal matrix which diagonalizes
\Pi_{1}
.

As shown in Appendix
A.1
, the transformation (
3.10
)
is equivalent to a complex rotation of the quantization axes.
Thus, without loss of generality (i.e., up to admissible unitary transformations), any one-soliton solution can be reduced to a superposition
of two oppositely polarized shifted dark solitons of the scalar NLS equation.

It should be noted that, even though any one-soliton solution is unitarily equivalent to the simpler, diagonal solutions (
3.8
) or (
3.9
), when more than one soliton are present in general it is not possible to simultaneously reduce both solitons to diagonal forms via unitary transformations.
In particular,
\Pi_{1}
and
\Pi_{2}
can be simultaneously diagonalized if and only if they commute, which obviously is a very special case.
For this reason, it is important to discuss the form of the one-soliton also in the generic case in which
\Pi_{1}
is an arbitrary Hermitian matrix, not necessarily diagonal.
And for similar reasons, i.e., in order to elucidate soliton interactions, it is also important to characterize one-soliton solutions that are not in canonical form.
This will be done in the next subsections, where we will distinguish between ferromagnetic solitons and polar solitons.
From the mathematical point of view, we will refer to a ferromagnetic soliton when the associated norming constant is such that
\det\Pi_{1}=0
, and to a polar soliton when
\det\Pi_{1}>0
which is the full rank case. As we will explain below, the terminology corresponds to the standard one in the physical literature, where
a ferromagnetic soliton has nonzero total spin, while the total spin of a polar soliton is zero.

Finally, using (
3.1
) and the fact that
Q_{+}=I_{2}
, one can obtain an explicit expression for the
particle number density (
2.8a
) and the spin density (
2.8b
) for a one-soliton solution:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bar{n}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2i\theta(x,t,z_{1})-\varrho}}{D^{2}}\Big(-e^{2(2i\theta(x,t,z_{1})+\varrho)}\det\Pi_{1}\,\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits\Pi_{1}+4\,e^{2i\theta(x,t,z_{1})+\varrho}\det\Pi_{1}-\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits\Pi_{1}\Big)\,,

(3.11a)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{f}}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2i\theta(x,t,z_{1})-\varrho}\,\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\Pi_{1})}{D^{2}}\Big(1-e^{2(2i\theta(x,t,z_{1})+\varrho)}\det\Pi_{1}\Big)\,,

(3.11b)

where
D=e^{2(2i\theta(x,t,z_{1})+\varrho)}\det\Pi_{1}-e^{2i\theta+\varrho}\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits\Pi_{1}+1
, and
e^{-\varrho}=2\sin\varphi
with
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
as in (
2.8
).

One can simplify the spin densities (
3.11b
) further for polar and ferromagnetic states separately.
Specifically, for ferromagnetic solitons (i.e.,
\det\Pi_{1}=0
) one has

{\mathbf{f}}(x,t)=\frac{1}{4}\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\Pi_{1})\,e^{-(\rho+2\alpha_{0}\,\sin\varphi)}\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits^{2}[\sin\varphi(x-\alpha_{0}+2t\cos\varphi)]\,,

(3.12a)

where
\alpha_{0}=(\rho+\ln(-\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits\Pi_{1}))/2\sin\varphi
and we will show in later sections that
\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits\Pi_{1}<0
. This shows that the spin density is an even function of
x
,
and therefore the total spin of a ferromagnetic soliton in canonical form is nonzero.

On the other hand, the spin density of a polar soliton in canonical form can be written as

\displaystyle{\mathbf{f}}(x,t)=-2\,\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\Pi_{1})\,e^{-(\rho+2\beta_{0}\,\sin\varphi)}\dfrac{\sinh[2\sin\varphi(x-\beta_{0}+2t\cos\varphi)]}{\{2\cosh[2\sin\varphi(x-\beta_{0}+2t\cos\varphi)]-\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\Pi_{1})e^{\rho-2\beta_{0}\,\sin\varphi}\}^{2}}\,,

(3.12b)

with
\beta_{0}=(2\rho+\ln(\det\Pi_{1}))/4\sin\varphi
, and one can see that all three components of the spin density are odd functions of
x
. As a consequence,
the total spin of a polar soliton in canonical form is always zero.
Finally, we note that in light of (
2.9a
) and (
2.9b
) the transformation to canonical form, while changing the total spin, does not change
the nature of the soliton as being polar or ferromagnetic.

3.2
Ferromagnetic states

We start by considering the case of a one-soliton solution for which
\Pi_{1}
(i.e., its associated norming constant
C_{1}
)
is rank-1, which corresponds to a ferromagnetic state.
Since in this case
\det\Pi_{1}=0
, the solution (
3.1
) simplifies to:

Q(x,t)=Q_{+}+\dfrac{2i\sin{\varphi}\,e^{-i\varphi}}{2\kappa_{o}^{2}\sin{\varphi}\,e^{-2i\theta(x,t,z_{1})}-\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\Pi_{1})}\Pi_{1}Q_{+}\,,

(3.13)

where
z_{1}=\kappa_{o}\,e^{i\varphi}
, with
\varphi\in(0,\pi)
.
Taking the limit
x\to-\infty
we have

Q_{-}=\mathcal{V}Q_{+},

(3.14)

where
\mathcal{V}=I_{2}-\dfrac{2i\sin{\varphi}\,e^{-i\varphi}}{\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\Pi_{1})}\Pi_{1}
and
\mathcal{V}
is a unitary matrix.
The above equation shows that
Q_{-}\neq Q_{+}
and that in fact, generically, the energy distribution among the components
as
x\to\infty
and
x\to-\infty
is different.
As we will see, this is different from what happens in polar states (i.e., when
\det\Pi_{1}\neq 0
).
When
Q_{+}=I_{2}
, (
3.13
) yields the canonical form for the ferromagnetic state as

Q(x,t)=I_{2}+\dfrac{2i\sin{\varphi}\,e^{-i\varphi}}{2\sin{\varphi}\,e^{-2i\theta(x,t,z_{1})}-\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits\,\Pi_{1}}\,\Pi_{1}\,,

(3.15)

where
\Pi_{1}=(c_{ij})
is now real, symmetric and with zero determinant, and is therefore completely determined by its diagonal entries.
As we said,
\Pi_{1}
can always be reduced to a diagonal form (in this case,
\Pi_{1}=\mathrm{diag}(\gamma_{1},0)
since
\det\Pi_{1}=0
) via rotations of the quantization axes.
And the above solution in this case in simply given by (
3.9
).
However, as we also clarified before, it is important to understand the properties of the solutions when
\Pi_{1}
is not diagonal. Therefore, we will consider below
a general (i.e., non-diagonal) rank-1 matrix
\Pi_{1}
.
In order for (
3.15
) to be regular for all
x,t\in\mathbb{R}
, it is necessary and sufficient that
\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits\,\Pi_{1}<0
. Since
\det\Pi_{1}=c_{11}c_{22}-c_{12}^{2}=0
, one can show that
c_{11}<0
and
c_{22}<0
.
In what follows, it will be convenient to express
\Pi_{1}
in terms of the ratio of its diagonal entries,
\rho=c_{11}/c_{22}
, and the quantity

x_{o}=\ln[-c_{22}/(2\sin\varphi)]/(2\sin\varphi)

(which amounts to expressing
c_{22}
as
c_{22}=-2e^{2x_{o}\sin\varphi}\sin\varphi
).
That is,

\Pi_{1}=-2e^{2x_{o}\sin\varphi}\sin\varphi\,\begin{pmatrix}\rho&-\sqrt{\rho}\\
-\sqrt{\rho}&1\end{pmatrix}\,.

(3.16)

The reason why the above parametrization is convenient is that
the shape of the solution is controlled only by
\rho
, whereas

x_{o}
corresponds to an overall translation of the solution.
Indeed, the above parametrization yields

Q(x,t)=I-2i\sin\varphi\frac{e^{-i\varphi}}{\mathrm{e}^{2\chi}+(1+\rho)}\begin{pmatrix}\rho&-\sqrt{\rho}\\
-\sqrt{\rho}&1\end{pmatrix},

(3.17)

where
\chi(x,t)=-i\theta(x,t,z_{1})-x_{o}\sin\varphi
.
Explicitly,

\chi(x,t)=\sin\varphi\,(x-x_{o}+2t\cos\varphi)\,.

(3.18)

The canonical form (
3.17
) of the ferromagnetic one-soliton solutions allows one to characterize their physical properties,
as we show next.
Specifically, one can show that
|Q_{12}(x,t)|
does not admit minima or maxima, while
exactly one between
|Q_{11}(x,t)|
and
|Q_{22}(x,t)|
has a minimum for any choice of
\rho\neq 1
.
(Note, however, that these properties do not extend to solitons in non-canonical form.)
More precisely,
|Q_{11}(x,t)|
has a minimum when
\rho>1
, while
|Q_{22}(x,t)|
has a minimum when
\rho<1
.
Moreover, the minimum in either case is located on the line

\chi(x,t)=\frac{1}{2}\ln|1-\rho|
, i.e.:

(x-x_{o})\,\sin\varphi+t\,\sin(2\varphi)=\frac{1}{2}\ln{|1-\rho|}\,,

(3.19)

Note that Eq. (
3.18
) gives
v=-2\cos\varphi=-2\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(z_{1})
for the soliton velocity.

Finally, the depth of the minimum in both cases is given by:

1-|Q_{jj,\mathrm{min}}(x,t)|=1-|\cos\varphi|\,,\quad j=1,2\,.

(3.20)

Importantly, note that the depth of the minimum is independent of the norming constant
C_{1}
.
On the other hand, the “soliton center”, i.e., the location of the minimum (as well as and the information about the component of
Q(x,t)
which exhibits the minimum, if it exists), depends on the diagonal entries of the norming constant.

has a minimum (

); only
|Q_{22}(x,t)|
has a minimum (
\rho<1
); none of the components of
Q(x,t)
has a minimum (
\rho=1
).

) have different max/min patterns. Just to provide some

examples of the phenomenology that can arise,
we use the following choices of boundary conditions and norming constants:

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{5}\begin{pmatrix}1+4e^{i\pi/3}&-2+2e^{i\pi/3}\\

2\sqrt{3}+6i&-4\sqrt{3}-12i\end{pmatrix}\,,

2(86\sqrt{3}+57i)&-4(28\sqrt{3}+37i)\end{pmatrix}\,,

(3.21)

However, one can always reduce the solution to canonical form and characterize the solution in that simpler form, as discussed above.
Note that in all the above cases

is not a symmetric matrix.

or more (Fig.

3.3
Polar states

We now consider one-soliton solutions with
\det\Pi_{1}\neq 0
, i.e., whose associated norming constant
C_{1}
is full rank,
which gives rise to a polar state.
In this case, simplifying (
3.1
) we obtain:

Q(x,t)=\dfrac{(e^{2(2i\theta(x,t,z_{1})+\varrho-i\varphi)}\,\det(\Pi_{1})-e^{2i\theta(x,t,z_{1})+\varrho}\,\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\Pi_{1})+1)\kappa_{o}^{2}Q_{+}+ie^{i(2\theta(x,t,z_{1})-\varphi)}\Pi_{1}Q_{+}}{\kappa_{o}^{2}(e^{2(2i\theta(x,t,z_{1})+\varrho)}\,\det(\Pi_{1})-e^{2i\theta(x,t,z_{1})+\varrho}\,\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\Pi_{1})+1)}

(3.22)

where
e^{-\varrho}=2\kappa_{o}^{2}\sin\varphi
,
z_{1}=\kappa_{o}\,e^{i\varphi}
with
\varphi\in(0,\pi)
. When
x\to-\infty
we have

Q_{-}=e^{-2i\varphi}Q_{+}\,,

(3.23)

showing that for polar solitons the asymptotic states
Q_{+}
and
Q_{-}
always coincide up to an overall phase factor, determined by
the phase of the discrete eigenvalue.
Choosing
Q_{+}=I_{2}
and
\kappa_{o}=1
, (
3.22
)
gives the canonical form of the polar state as

Q(x,t)=\dfrac{(e^{2(2i\theta(x,t,z_{1})+\varrho-i\varphi)}\,\det\Pi_{1}-e^{2i\theta(x,t,z_{1})+\varrho}\,\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits\Pi_{1}+1)I_{2}+ie^{i(2\theta(x,t,z_{1})-\varphi)}\Pi_{1}}{e^{2(2i\theta(x,t,z_{1})+\varrho)}\,\det\Pi_{1}-e^{2i\theta(x,t,z_{1})+\varrho}\,\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits\Pi_{1}+1}\,.

(3.24)

Note that this solution is regular for any real, symmetric matrix
\Pi_{1}=(c_{ij})
with
\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits\Pi_{1}<0
and
\det\Pi_{1}>0
. For future convenience, let us express
\Pi_{1}
in terms of
\rho_{1}=c_{11}/c_{22}
,
\rho_{2}=c_{12}/c_{22}
and the quantity

x_{o}=\ln[-c_{22}\sin\varphi/(c_{11}c_{22}-c_{12}^{2})]/(2\sin\varphi)
as

\Pi_{1}=\frac{-e^{2x_{o}\sin\varphi}(c_{11}c_{22}-c_{12}^{2})}{\sin\varphi}\,\begin{pmatrix}\rho_{1}&\rho_{2}\\
\rho_{2}&1\end{pmatrix}\,.

(3.25)

Next, we characterize the physical properties of polar states in canonical form, but with
\Pi_{1}
non-diagonal.
Unlike the ferromagnetic case, from (

) one can see that for any choice of
z_{1}
and
\Pi_{1}
,
|Q_{11}(x,t)|,\,|Q_{22}(x,t)|
both have a minimum, and the minimum of
|Q_{11}(x,t)|
(if

) or
|Q_{22}(x,t)|

(x+x_{0})\sin\varphi+\sin(2\varphi)\,t=-\frac{1}{2}\ln\Big[|\rho_{1}-1|+\sqrt{(1+\rho_{1})^{2}-4\rho_{2}^{2}}\Big]\,.

) implies the velocity

, where

and

, respectively.

4
Soliton interactions

In this section we will discuss the soliton interaction in detail by computing the long-time asymptotics of the two-soliton solutions,
i.e., the solutions obtained from the general expression (
2.51
) with
J=2
.

4.1
General set up

The canonical form of a two-soliton solution is given by (
2.44
) with
J=2,\,\kappa_{o}=1
and
Q_{+}=I_{2}
, namely

5
Concluding remarks

In the present work we have revisited the defocusing version of the integrable spinor model initiated by the work of
[
17
,
18
]
.
We have highlighted the relevance as well as the differences of the present model from the
3-component coupled NLS system, in which
solely density-dependent (i.e., spin-independent)
interactions are accounted for.
Indeed, this opposite yet still integrable limit involves the case of equal spin-dependent
and spin-independent interactions.
The recent experimental manipulation
[
43
]
of the spin-dependent interactions
to achieve the Manakov model holds some promise towards varying the
relevant ratio of interactions. Perhaps even more importantly, the availability of gases
such as the strongly ferromagnetic
F=1

7
Li
[
55
]
creates
a platform where the spin-dependent part of the interaction is nearly
half that of the spin-independent one. In light of this, it becomes
progressively relevant to explore analytically tractable mathematical limits that may
yield novel waveforms that may emerge as being relevant for potential
observation in experiments.

It is in this vein that the present work has explored the possible waveforms
in the defocusing variant of the MNLS equation. We have leveraged
the earlier integrable formulation of
[
47
]
to identify the
prototypical soliton solutions which we classified into two major
categories. Polar solitons correspond to waveforms reminiscent
of dark- and dark-bright solitons, although with some key distinguishing
features regarding the location of their centers or their potential
to elevate above their asymptotic background. On the other hand,
the ferromagnetic waveforms presented structures that had a
fundamentally distinct pattern involving domain walls asymptoting to different
background values between
x\to-\infty
and
x\to\infty
.
Going beyond the single soliton states, we explored also multi-soliton
collisions. These were more straightforward in preserving the nature
of the waveforms when same types of solitons (e.g. polar-polar or
ferromagnetic-ferromagnetic) collided. Yet, the scenario was clearly
richer and could involve an apparent change of the density distribution
of the profile when a polar and a ferromagnetic soliton might collide.

There results are a clear basis for numerous further studies at the level
of numerical computation and theoretical analysis and are even suggestive of novel
physical experiments. It would be especially relevant to continue parametrically
the solutions identified herein to explore their range of persistence
as the spin-dependent interaction is varied. If these states could
be continued even down to a ration of
1/2
, the recent experiments
of
[
55
]
might enable their observation. Another relevant
possibility might be to compare these waveforms with the
magnetic waves recently explored for 3-component spinor systems
in
[
45

46
]
. A comparison of the latter with dark-bright
waves in two-component systems has recently taken place
in

56

. An additional direction of interest concerns
the generalization of the patterns considered herein in higher-dimensional
systems. While identifying integrable generalizations in the higher-dimensional
realm would be a major challenge in its own right, it is certainly
plausible that vortical (i.e., topologically charged) generalizations
of the states presented herein may exist in the two-dimensional
analogue of the present system. Considering such domain-wall
and vortex-bright soliton structures is a numerical and experimental challenge in its own right.

A.1
Unitary transformations, symmetric matrices and rotation of the quantization axes

Recall that the matrix NLS equation (
2.1
) is invariant under unitary transformations from the left or from the right.
That is, if
Q(x,t)
solves (
2.1
), so does

\tilde{Q}(x,t)=UQ(x,t)V\,,

(A.1)

for all constant
U
and
V
such that
U^{\dagger}=U^{-1}
and
V^{\dagger}=V^{-1}
.
On the other hand, in order for
\tilde{Q}(x,t)
to also represent a spinor wave function, the transformation (
A.1
)
must preserve matrix symmetry.
That is, one must have
\tilde{Q}^{T}(x,t)=\tilde{Q}(x,t)
whenever
Q^{T}(x,t)=Q(x,t)
.
In this appendix we characterize the set of unitary transformations that preserve the symmetry constraint.
We also show that all such transformations correspond to a rotation of the quantization axes.

We begin by representing arbitrary unitary matrices
U
and
V

without loss of generality in terms of the Pauli matrices as

\displaystyle U=\exp[iu_{0}I_{2}+i{\mathbf{u}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}]=e^{iu_{0}}\begin{pmatrix}\cos u+i\hat{u}_{3}\,\sin u&i(\hat{u}_{1}-i\hat{u}_{2})\,\sin u\\
i(\hat{u}_{1}+i\hat{u}_{2})\,\sin u&\cos u-i\hat{u}_{3}\sin u\end{pmatrix},

(A.2a)

\displaystyle V=\exp[iv_{0}I_{2}+i{\mathbf{v}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}]=e^{iv_{0}}\begin{pmatrix}\cos v+i\hat{v}_{3}\,\sin v&i(\hat{v}_{1}-i\hat{v}_{2})\,\sin v\\
i(\hat{v}_{1}+i\hat{v}_{2})\,\sin v&\cos v-i\hat{v}_{3}\sin v\end{pmatrix},

(A.2b)

where
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3})^{T}
is the vector of
Pauli matrices, here chosen as

\sigma_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\sigma_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\sigma_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},

(A.3)

where
u_{0}
,
v_{0}
,
{\mathbf{u}}=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})^{T}
and
{\mathbf{v}}=(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})^{T}
are all real,
with

\hat{{\mathbf{u}}}={\mathbf{u}}/u

and

\hat{{\mathbf{v}}}={\mathbf{v}}/v
,
and where

u=\sqrt{{\mathbf{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{u}}}=\sqrt{u_{1}^{2}+u_{2}^{2}+u_{3}^{2}}\,,\qquad v=\sqrt{{\mathbf{v}}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}}=\sqrt{v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}+v_{3}^{2}}\,.

(A.4)

Since
u_{0}
and
v_{0}
just produce overall phase rotations, without loss of generality we can set
u_{0}=v_{0}=0

owing to the phase invariance of the MNLS equation.
Without loss of generality, we can also take
u
and
v
in
[0,2\pi]
.

Inserting (
A.2
)
in (
A.1
) and requiring the equality of the off-diagonal entries of
\tilde{Q}(x,t)
then yields the following three real constraints:

\displaystyle[(\hat{u}_{2}\hat{v}_{3}+\hat{u}_{3}\hat{v}_{2})\sin u+\hat{v}_{1}\cos u]\,\sin v-\hat{u}_{1}\,\sin u\,\cos v=0\,,

(A.5a)

\displaystyle[(\hat{u}_{1}\hat{v}_{3}-\hat{u}_{3}\hat{v}_{1})\sin u+\hat{v}_{2}\cos u]\,\sin v+\hat{u}_{2}\,\sin u\,\cos v=0\,.

(A.5b)

\displaystyle[(\hat{u}_{1}\hat{v}_{2}+\hat{u}_{2}\hat{v}_{1})\sin u-\hat{v}_{3}\cos u]\,\sin v+\hat{u}_{3}\,\sin u\,\cos v=0\,.

(A.5c)

It is relatively straightforward to see that (
A.5
) are solved by

(\hat{v}_{1},\hat{v}_{2},\hat{v}_{3})\,\tan v=(\hat{u}_{1},-\hat{u}_{2},\hat{u}_{3})\,\tan u\,.

(A.6)

In turn, (
A.6
) implies that (
A.5
)
admit the following inequivalent
classes of solutions, obtained respectively when
v=u
and
v=2\pi-u
:

S_{+}:~(\hat{v}_{1},\hat{v}_{2},\hat{v}_{3})=(\hat{u}_{1},-\hat{u}_{2},\hat{u}_{3})\,,\qquad S_{-}:~(\hat{v}_{1},\hat{v}_{2},\hat{v}_{3})=(-\hat{u}_{1},\hat{u}_{2},-\hat{u}_{3})\,.

(A.7)

One can now check that
S_{+}
implies
V=U^{T}
while
S_{-}
implies
V=-U^{T}
.
Since an overall minus sign can always be rescaled using the phase invariance of the MNLS equation, however,
without loss of generality we can limit ourselves to considering only those transformations produced by
S_{+}
.

Next we show that the unitary transformation (
A.1
) is equivalent to
a complex rotation of the quantization axes.
Let

{\mathbf{q}}(x,t)=(q_{1},\sqrt{2}\,q_{0},q_{-1})^{T}

be the vector wave functions associated with
Q(x,t)
,
and let
\tilde{{\mathbf{q}}}(x,t)=(\tilde{q}_{1},\sqrt{2}\,\tilde{q}_{0},\tilde{q}_{-1})^{T}
be the one associated with

\tilde{Q}(x,t)
.
Observe that a sign change of
Q(x,t)
obviously translates into a sign change in
{\mathbf{q}}(x,t)

and recall that, in the quantum-mechanical context, an overall phase of the wave function is immaterial.
Therefore, we can again limit ourselves to considering transformations produced by
S_{+}
.
It is straightforward to show that

\displaystyle\tilde{{\mathbf{q}}}(x,t)=R\,{\mathbf{q}}(x,t)\,,

(A.8a)

\displaystyle R=\begin{pmatrix}c_{+}^{2}&\sqrt{2}i(\hat{u}_{1}-i\hat{u}_{2})c_{+}\sin u&-(\hat{u}_{1}-i\hat{u}_{2})^{2}\sin^{2}u\\
\sqrt{2}i(\hat{u}_{1}+i\hat{u}_{2})c_{+}\sin u&\cos^{2}u-(1-2\hat{u}_{3}^{2})\sin^{2}u&\sqrt{2}i(\hat{u}_{1}-i\hat{u}_{2})c_{-}\sin u\\
-(\hat{u}_{1}+i\hat{u}_{2})^{2}\sin^{2}u&\sqrt{2}i(\hat{u}_{1}+i\hat{u}_{2})c_{-}\sin u&c_{-}^{2}\end{pmatrix},

(A.8b)

\displaystyle c_{\pm}=\cos u\pm i\hat{u}_{3}\sin u\,.

(A.8c)

It is also straightforward to check that
R
is a unitary matrix, i.e.,
RR^{\dagger}=R^{\dagger}R=I_{3}
,
and that
\det R=1
, implying
R\in\mathrm{SU}(3)
.
Finally, it is also important to realize that
R
corresponds to a rotation of the quantization axes.
Consider again the transformation (
A.1
) with
V=U^{T}
, and
again let
u_{0}=0
without loss of generality.
It is straightforward to show that

\displaystyle R=e^{2i{\mathbf{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{f}}}\,,

(A.9)

where
{\mathbf{f}}=(f_{1},f_{2},f_{3})^{T}
, and
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}
are representation of the angular momentum operators in
\mathrm{SU}(3)
, namely:

f_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
1&0&1\\
0&1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad f_{2}=\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\
1&0&-1\\
0&1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad f_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

(A.10)

In closing, we also point out that the above relations are purely local symmetries, and are therefore
completely independent of the boundary conditions
satisfied by
Q(x,t)
as
x\to\pm\infty
.
