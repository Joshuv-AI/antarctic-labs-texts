Tracking the Mean of a Piecewise Stationary Sequence
Ghurumuruhan Ganesan IISER, Bhopal

Abstract
In this paper we study the problem of tracking the mean of a piecewise stationary sequence of independent random variables. First we consider the case where the transition times are known and show that a direct running average performs the tracking in short time and with high accuracy. We then use a single valued weighted running average with a tunable parameter for the case when transition times are unknown and establish deviation bounds for the tracking accuracy. Our result has applications in choosing the optimal rewards for the multiarmed bandit scenario. Key words: Piecewise stationary sequence, mean tracking, weighted running average, multiarmed bandit. Introduction

The multiarmed bandit problem (Auer et al. 2002) is important from both theoretical and application perspectives and has implications in many fields including economics, machine and financial markets, to name a few. The basic setup is that there are multiple arms each equipped with a reward sequence and the average reward in each arm is different. The user (bandit) wants to latch on to the best arm in as short time as possible and therefore must balance exploration together with exploitation to minimize its overall regret. There are many known bounds regarding the exploration exploitation tradeoff (Lai and Robbins (1985), Burnetas and Katehakis (1996)) and for a recent survey of both theoretical and computational aspects, we refer to Slivkins (2019).

Recently (Besbes et al. 2014), there has been interest in the case when the reward sequence of an arm displays nonstationarity. In this case, a direct running average may not be feasible anymore and it is of interest to establish methods for detecting the best arm in a dynamic manner. Auer et al. (2018) propose dynamic tracking for the two armed bandit problem and proceed by comparing the empirical means of both the arms (obtained from sliding window running averages) and declaring that a transition has occurred when the difference is significant. For more details and theoretical bounds on the regret we refer to Lattimore and Szepesvári (2020).

In general, to compute dynamic running averages and invoke deviation estimates, we would have to store at least
O(\log{t})
samples for
t
rounds. In this paper, we describe and analyse a
single
valued recursive estimator that uses only knowledge of the minimum epoch durations and automatically performs tracking when the means change.

The paper is organized as follows: In Section
2
, we describe the problem of tracking the mean of a piecewise stationary sequence and show that an unweighted running average performs well if the transition times are known. We also define a single valued weighted running average for the case when the transition times are known and describe its tracking performance in our main result Theorem
1
. Next in Section
3
, we prove Theorem
1
.

2
Piecewise Stationary Sequence

For integer
t\geq 1,
let
\{X_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq t}
be positive independent random variables with
0\leq X_{j}\leq 1
and suppose there are deterministic integers
M=M(t)
and
1=s_{1}<s_{2}<\ldots<s_{M}=t
such that

\mathbb{E}X_{j}=m(k)\text{ for each }s_{k}\leq j<s_{k+1}\text{ and }1\leq k\leq M-1.

(2.1)

In other words, the random variables
\{X_{j}\}
are not necessarily stationary but enjoy “epochs” of stationarity in the sense of (
2.1
). Throughout this paper, we consider only first order stationarity, i.e., stationarity in the mean and for concreteness, define
s_{k}\leq j<s_{k+1}
to be the
k^{th}
epoch and
\{s_{k}\}
to be
transition times
.

It is of interest to “track” the mean
\{m(k)\}
as it varies from epoch to epoch, using a
randomly
sampled subsequence of
\{X_{j}\}
as follows. Let
\{Z_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq t}
be independent Bernoulli random variables (that are also independent of
\{X_{j}\}
) with

\mathbb{P}(Z_{j}=1)=\epsilon_{j}=1-\mathbb{P}(Z_{j}=0).

(2.2)

For
t\geq 1,
let
{\cal E}(t)=\{1\leq j\leq t:Z_{j}=1\}
be the set of all sampling times and let
T(1)<T(2)<\cdots<T(w)
be the random indices in
{\cal E}(t).
Using
\{X_{T(l)}\}
we are interested in tracking the mean sequence
\{m(k)\}.

In the context of armed bandit sequential learning,
\{X_{j}\}
denotes the reward sequence of an arm and
\{Z_{j}\}
denotes the exploration sequence used to study and gain information regarding the reward sequence. There are usually multiple arms each with its own reward sequence and after every transition, the goal is
track
the arm with the largest reward, i.e., to latch on to the best arm as quickly as possible, using the rewards observed in all arms during the exploration sequence.

If the transition times
\{s_{k}\}
are
known
, then we can track
\{m(k)\}
simply using law of large numbers, provided each epoch in (
2.1
) is of large enough duration. We use the following deviation estimates to demonstrate our argument.

Lemma 1

(a)
Let
\{W_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq r}
be independent Bernoulli random variables with

\mathbb{P}(W_{j}=1)=1-\mathbb{P}(W_{j}=0)>0.

Setting
S_{r}:=\sum_{j=1}^{r}W_{j},
we have for
0<\epsilon\leq\frac{1}{2}
that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|S_{r}-\mathbb{E}S_{r}\right|\geq\epsilon\mathbb{E}S_{r}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4}\mathbb{E}S_{r}\right).

(2.3)

(b)
Let
\{U_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq r}
be positive independent random variables satisfying
0\leq U_{j}\leq 1
and set
V_{r}:=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}U_{j}
where
\lambda_{j}>0
are positive numbers. For any
\epsilon>0
we have that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|V_{r}-\mathbb{E}V_{r}\right|\geq\epsilon\mathbb{E}V_{r}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon^{2}(\mathbb{E}V_{r})^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}^{2}}\right).

(2.4)

For a proof of (
2.3
) and (
2.4
), we refer to Appendix
A
of Alon and Spencer (2008).

Indeed, suppose that the transition times
\{s_{k}\}
are known and satisfy

\min_{k}(s_{k+1}-s_{k})\geq t^{\gamma_{0}}\text{ and }\min_{k}m(k)\geq\mu_{0},

(2.5)

for some constants
0<\gamma_{0}\leq 1
and
\mu_{0}>0.
Set
\epsilon_{j}=\frac{1}{j^{\beta}}
where
0<\beta<1
is a constant. For
0<\gamma<\gamma_{0},
let
{\cal E}_{k,\gamma}(t)\subset{\cal E}(t)
be the set of all sampling indices lying between
s_{k}
and
s_{k}+t^{\gamma}
and
define

R_{l}:=\frac{1}{\#{\cal E}_{k,\gamma}(t)}\sum_{T(j)\in{\cal E}_{k,\gamma}(t)}X_{T(j)}

(2.6)

for each
l
satisfying
s_{k}+t^{\gamma}\leq T(l)<s_{k+1}.
For
s_{k}\leq T(l)<s_{k}+t^{\gamma},
we set
R_{l}=0.
In words, we collect all observations lying between
s_{k}
and
s_{k}+t^{\gamma}
and compute a single average as defined in (
2.6
), whose right hand side does not depend on
l.

Using (
2.3
) and (
2.4
), we obtain the deviation estimate for
R_{l}
as follows. Since
s_{k}+t^{\gamma}<t
for each
k,
the expected size of
{\cal E}_{k,\gamma}(t)
is

\mathbb{E}\left(\#{\cal E}_{k,\gamma}(t)\right)=\sum_{j=s_{k}}^{s_{k}+t^{\gamma}}\frac{1}{j^{\beta}}\geq\sum_{j=t}^{t+t^{\gamma}}\frac{1}{j^{\beta}}\geq\frac{t^{\gamma}}{t^{\beta}}.

(2.7)

where
\#A
denotes the cardinality of
A.
Therefore using (
2.7
) together with (
2.3
) we get that

\mathbb{P}\left(\#{\cal E}_{k,\gamma}(t)\geq C_{1}t^{\gamma-\beta}\right)\geq 1-\exp\left(-C_{2}t^{\gamma-\beta}\right)

(2.8)

for some constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0,
not depending on
k.
Similarly
\mathbb{E}(\#{\cal E}(t))=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\frac{1}{j^{\beta}}\leq\frac{2t^{1-\beta}}{1-\beta}
and so again using (
2.3
), we get that

\mathbb{P}\left(\#{\cal E}(t)\leq\frac{4t^{1-\beta}}{1-\beta}\right)\geq 1-\exp\left(-C_{3}t^{1-\beta}\right)

(2.9)

for some constant
C_{3}>0.
There are in total
M\leq t
epochs and so defining

E_{nice}:=\bigcap_{k=1}^{M-1}\left\{\#{\cal E}_{k,\gamma}(t)\geq C_{1}t^{\gamma-\beta}\right\}\bigcap\left\{\#{\cal E}(t)\leq\frac{4t^{1-\beta}}{1-\beta}\right\}

we get from (
2.8
), (
2.9
) and the union bound that

\mathbb{P}(E_{nice})\geq 1-t\exp\left(-C_{2}t^{\gamma-\beta}\right)-\exp\left(-C_{3}t^{1-\beta}\right).

(2.10)

Given a realization
\omega\in E_{nice},
the expected value
\mathbb{E}_{\omega}(R_{l})=m(k)
and so the Azuma-Hoeffding inequality (
2.4
) together with the second condition in (
2.5
) imply that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\omega}\left(\left|R_{l}-m(k)\right|\geq\epsilon m(k)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-C_{4}\epsilon^{2}\#{\cal E}_{k,\gamma}(t)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-C_{4}C_{1}\epsilon^{2}t^{\gamma-\beta}\right)

(2.11)

for each
s_{k}+t^{\gamma}\leq T(l)<s_{k+1}
and some constant
C_{4}=C_{4}(\mu_{0})>0
not depending on
k.
There are
M\leq t
epochs and so defining
F_{good}
to be the intersection of the event that

\#{\cal E}(t)\leq\frac{4t^{1-\beta}}{1-\beta}

(2.12)

and the event that

|R_{l}-m(k)|\leq\frac{m(k)}{t^{b}}\text{ for each }k\text{ and each }s_{k}+t^{\gamma}\leq T(l)<s_{k+1},

(2.13)

we use (
2.9
) and (
2.11
) with
\epsilon=\frac{1}{t^{b}}
together with the union bound, to get that
F_{good}
occurs with probability at least

1-t\exp\left(-C_{2}t^{\gamma-\beta}\right)-\exp\left(-C_{3}t^{1-\beta}\right)-2t\exp\left(-C_{4}C_{1}t^{\gamma(1-\beta)-2b}\right).

(2.14)

The expression in (
2.14
) converges to one as
t\rightarrow\infty
provided we fix
b<\frac{\gamma(1-\beta)}{2}.

The relations (
2.12
) and (
2.13
) capture the essential requirements we seek in tracking:

(1)
From (
2.12
), we get that the number of samples used for tracking is much smaller than the overall duration
t.

(2)
The condition (
2.13
) states that once our tracking scheme comes close to the mean in an epoch, it stays that way for the rest of the epoch, even when new samples are encountered.

(3)
The term
t^{\gamma}
could be interpreted as an upper bound for the time needed to track the mean with an accuracy of
1-\frac{1}{t^{b}}.
Since we have chosen
\gamma<\gamma_{0}
strictly, the tracking time in any epoch is much smaller than the total epoch duration, by (
2.5
).

The caveat in the above analysis is that we have crucially used the knowledge of the transition times
\{s_{k}\}
for the tracking. This in fact allowed us to construct
R_{l}
by collecting only the samples observed between
s_{k}
and
s_{k}+t^{\gamma}.
In practice,
\{s_{k}\}
are unknown and so we are interested in “universal” schemes that do not depend on
\{s_{k}\}.
In our main result below, we use the following recursive estimator to perform “automatic” tracking. For
l\geq 1
let
\alpha_{l}:=1-\frac{1}{l^{\delta}}
where
0<\delta<1
is a constant so that
\alpha_{1}=0.
Setting
Y_{0}:=0,
we define the
l^{th}
weighted running average for
l\geq 1
as

Y_{l}:=\alpha_{l}Y_{l-1}+(1-\alpha_{l})X_{T(l)}.

(2.15)

The term
\delta
could be interpreted as the importance or weight given to the past observations in deriving the current average.

We have the following result regarding the tracking performance of
Y_{l}.

Theorem 1

Suppose (
2.5
) holds and we set
\epsilon_{j}=\frac{1}{j^{\beta}}.
For every
0<\beta<\gamma<\gamma_{0}
and every
0<\delta<\frac{\gamma-\beta}{1-\beta},
there are constants
b,\theta>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(E_{good}\right)\geq 1-\exp\left(-t^{\theta}\right)

(2.16)

where
E_{good}=E_{good}(b,\delta,\gamma)
is event that(
2.12
) and (
2.13
) hold, with
R_{l}
replaced by
Y_{l}.

In words, knowing only
\gamma_{0}
and fixing the tracking tolerance
\gamma,
we can choose our parameters
\beta
and
\delta
such that
Y_{l}
tracks the mean
\{m(k)\}
with high probability.

3
Proof of Theorem
1

Let
\omega
be a realization of the sequence
(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{t})
so that
\{T(j)\}_{1\leq j\leq L}
are fixed. We let
\mathbb{P}_{\omega}
be the conditional distribution of
\mathbb{P},
given the sequence
\omega.
Also, for
1\leq k\leq M-1
let

s_{k}+t^{\gamma}\leq T(L_{k})<T(L_{k}+1)<\cdots<T(U_{k})\leq s_{k+1}-1

be the set of random indices in
{\cal E}(t)
lying between
s_{k}+t^{\gamma}
and
s_{k+1}-1.

We prove Theorem
1
in three steps. In the first step, we obtain estimates for
L_{k},U_{k}
and their difference. Using these estimates, we show in the second step that the expected value of the running average is close to the current expected value. Finally, we use the Azuma-Hoeffding inequality (
2.4
) in Lemma
1
, to show that the true running averages are close to their respective expected values. Details follow.

Step 1
: If
{\cal E}(r_{1},r_{2})=\{r_{1}\leq j\leq r_{2}:Z_{j}=1\}
then
\#{\cal E}(r_{1},r_{2})=\sum_{j=r_{1}}^{r_{2}}Z_{j}
and so

\mathbb{E}\left(\#{\cal E}(r_{1},r_{2})\right)=\sum_{j=r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\frac{1}{j^{\beta}}:=a(r_{1},r_{2})

where

\frac{r_{2}-r_{1}}{r_{2}^{\beta}}\leq a(r_{1},r_{2})\leq 1+\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\frac{dx}{x^{\beta}}\leq 1+\frac{r_{2}^{1-\beta}-r_{1}^{1-\beta}}{1-\beta}

(3.1)

for all
r
large. In terms of the notation following (
2.2
), we have
{\cal E}(r)={\cal E}(1,r)
and from (
3.1
), we see that
a(1,r)\geq\frac{r-1}{r^{\beta}}\geq\frac{r^{1-\beta}}{2}
and also that
a(1,r)\leq 1+\frac{r^{1-\beta}}{1-\beta}\leq\frac{2r^{1-\beta}}{1-\beta}
for all
r
large. Consequently, setting

E_{samp}(r):=\left\{a(1,r)\left(1-\frac{1}{\log{r}}\right)\leq\#{\cal E}(r)\leq a(1,r)\left(1+\frac{1}{\log{r}}\right)\right\}

and using the deviation estimate (
2.3
) with
\epsilon=\frac{1}{\log{r}},
we have that

\mathbb{P}\left(E_{samp}(r)\right)\geq 1-\exp\left(-2C_{1}\frac{r^{1-\beta}}{(\log{r})^{2}}\right),

for some constant
C_{1}>0.
We now define

E_{exp}:=\bigcap_{r\geq(\log{t})^{2/(1-\beta)}}E_{samp}(r)

and get from the union bound that

\mathbb{P}(E_{exp})\geq 1-\sum_{r\geq(\log{t})^{2/(1-\beta)}}\exp\left(-2C_{1}\frac{r^{1-\beta}}{(\log{r})^{2}}\right)

(3.2)

for all
t
large.

For
0<\epsilon<1-\beta
strictly and all
r\geq(\log{t})^{2/(1-\beta)}=:a
with
t
large, we have

\exp\left(-2C_{1}\frac{r^{1-\beta}}{(\log{r})^{2}}\right)\leq\exp\left(-r^{\epsilon}\right)

and so from (
3.2
), we get

\mathbb{P}(E_{exp})\geq 1-\sum_{r\geq a}\exp\left(-r^{\epsilon}\right)=1-\sum_{l\geq 0}\exp\left(-(l+a)^{\epsilon}\right).

Using the mean value estimate
(l+a)^{\epsilon}-a^{\epsilon}\geq\epsilon la^{\epsilon-1}=:\lambda l
we further get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E_{exp})

\displaystyle\geq 1-\exp\left(-a^{\epsilon}\right)\sum_{l\geq 0}\exp\left(-l\lambda\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\exp\left(-a^{\epsilon}\right)\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\lambda(x-1)\right)dx

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{\lambda}\exp\left(-a^{\epsilon}\left(1-\frac{\epsilon}{a}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\epsilon a^{1-\epsilon}\exp\left(-\frac{a^{\epsilon}}{2}\right)

since
\epsilon<1
and
a>2
for all
t
large. Setting
\epsilon
to be slightly larger than
\frac{3(1-\beta)}{4}
we then get that

\mathbb{P}(E_{exp})\geq 1-\exp\left(-(\log{t})^{3/2}\right)

(3.3)

for all
t
large. We assume henceforth that
E_{exp}
occurs and this obtains upper and lower bounds for each
L_{k}
and
U_{k}.

For a lower bound on the difference
L_{k+1}-U_{k},
we argue as in (
2.7
) to get that

\mathbb{E}(L_{k+1}-U_{k})\geq\sum_{j=s_{k+1}}^{s_{k+1}+t^{\gamma}}\frac{1}{j^{\beta}}\geq t^{\gamma-\beta}.

(3.4)

Choosing
\gamma>\beta
strictly and setting

E_{diff}:=\bigcap_{k=1}^{M-1}\left\{L_{k+1}-U_{k}\geq\frac{t^{\gamma-\beta}}{2}\right\},

we get from the deviation estimate (
2.3
) and union bound that

\mathbb{P}(E_{diff})\geq 1-t\exp\left(-C_{2}t^{\gamma-\beta}\right)

(3.5)

for some constant
C_{2}>0,
since the number of transitions
M\leq t.
Setting
E_{tot}:=E_{exp}\cap E_{diff}
we get from (
3.3
), (
3.5
) and the union bound that

\mathbb{P}(E_{tot})\geq 1-\exp\left(-(\log{t})^{3/2}\right)-t\exp\left(-C_{2}t^{\gamma-\beta}\right).

(3.6)

We assume henceforth that
E_{tot}
occurs so that for all
r\geq(\log{t})^{2/(1-\beta)}
and
t
large, we have

\frac{r^{1-\beta}}{4}\leq\#{\cal E}(r)\leq\frac{4r^{1-\beta}}{1-\beta},

(3.7)

by (
3.1
).

Step 2
: Let
m(k)
be the value of the mean
\mu_{j}
for
s_{k}\leq j<s_{k+1}.
In this step, we show that for each realization
\omega\in E_{tot},
the expected value of the running average
\nu_{l}:=\mathbb{E}_{\omega}Y_{l}
is close to the current mean
m(k)
for all
l
satisfying
L_{k}\leq l\leq U_{k}.

Defining
W_{j}:=X_{T(j)},
we see that each
Y_{l}
is a linear sum of
\{W_{j}\}
since by expanding the recursion (
2.15
) we get

Y_{l}=\sum_{j=1}^{l}W_{j}\theta_{j,l}

(3.8)

where

\theta_{j,l}=(1-\alpha_{j})\prod_{i=j+1}^{l}\alpha_{i}

for
1\leq j\leq l-1
and
\theta_{l,l}=1-\alpha_{l}.
By definition
\alpha_{1}=0
and
\sum_{j=1}^{l}\theta_{j,l}=1
and so for each
L_{1}\leq l\leq U_{1}
we have that
\nu_{l}=m(1).

Next we consider the case
L_{2}\leq l<U_{2}
for which we have

\displaystyle\nu_{l}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m(1)\sum_{j=L_{0}}^{U_{1}}\theta_{j,l}+m(2)\sum_{j=U_{1}+1}^{l}\theta_{j,l}

(3.9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m(1)\prod_{j=U_{1}+1}^{l}\theta_{j,l}+m(2)\left(1-\prod_{j=U_{1}+1}^{l}\theta_{j,l}\right).

Since
l\geq L_{2},
we have that

\displaystyle\prod_{j=U_{1}+1}^{l}\alpha_{j}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\prod_{j=U_{1}+1}^{L_{2}}\alpha_{j}

(3.10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{j=U_{1}+1}^{L_{2}}\left(1-\frac{1}{j^{\delta}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\sum_{j=U_{1}+1}^{L_{2}}\frac{1}{j^{\delta}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{(L_{2}-U_{1})}{L_{2}^{\delta}}\right).

Since
\omega\in E_{exp},
we get that
L_{k}\leq\frac{4t^{1-\beta}}{1-\beta}
for any
k
and since
\omega\in E_{diff}
as well, we also know that
L_{k+1}-U_{k}\geq\frac{t^{\gamma-\beta}}{2}.
Using these estimates in (
3.10
), we get that

\prod_{j=U_{1}+1}^{l}\alpha_{j}\leq\exp\left(-C_{3}t^{\gamma-\beta-\delta(1-\beta)}\right)

(3.11)

for some constant
C_{3}>0.
Choosing
0<\delta<\frac{\gamma-\beta}{1-\beta}
strictly, we then get from (
3.9
) that

\left|\nu_{l}-m(2)\right|\leq(m(1)+m(2))\exp\left(-C_{3}t^{\gamma-\beta-\delta(1-\beta)}\right)

for all
L_{2}\leq l\leq U_{2}.
Continuing this way iteratively, we get that for each
L_{k}\leq l\leq U_{k}
that

\displaystyle\left|\nu_{l}-m(k)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left(-C_{3}t^{\gamma-\beta-\delta(1-\beta)}\right)\sum_{j=1}^{M}m(j)

(3.12)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{4}t\exp\left(-C_{3}t^{\gamma-\beta-\delta(1-\beta)}\right)

for some constant
C_{4}>0,
since
0\leq X_{j}\leq 1
by Theorem statement and so
m(j)\leq 1.

Step 3
: In this step, we use the deviation estimate (
2.4
) to show that
Y_{l}
is close to
\nu_{l}.
We therefore begin with an upper bound for
\sum_{j=1}^{l}\theta_{j,l}^{2}.

By definition
\theta_{l,l}^{2}=(1-\alpha_{l})^{2}=\frac{1}{l^{2\delta}}
and for
1\leq j\leq l-1
we have that

\displaystyle\theta_{j,l}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\alpha_{j})^{2}\prod_{j+1\leq i\leq l}\alpha_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{j^{2\delta}}\prod_{j+1\leq i\leq l}\left(1-\frac{1}{i^{\delta}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{j^{2\delta}}\exp\left(-2\sum_{j+1\leq i\leq l}\frac{1}{i^{\delta}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{j^{2\delta}}\exp\left(-\frac{2(l-j)}{l^{\delta}}\right).

If
j\leq l-l^{\delta}(\log{l})^{2},
then
\theta_{j,l}^{2}\leq e^{-2(\log{l})^{2}}
and if
j\geq l-l^{\delta}(\log{l})^{2}>\frac{l}{2},

then
\theta_{j,l}^{2}\leq\frac{4}{l^{2\delta}}.
Combining we then get that

\sum_{j=1}^{l}\theta_{j,l}^{2}\leq le^{-2(\log{l})^{2}}+l^{\delta}(\log{l})^{2}\cdot\frac{4}{l^{2\delta}}\leq\frac{5(\log{l})^{2}}{l^{\delta}}

(3.13)

for all
l\geq L_{0},
a large constant.

Plugging (
3.13
) into (
2.4
) in Lemma
1
and setting
\epsilon=\frac{1}{t^{b}}
and using the fact that
m(k)\geq\mu_{0}
a constant, we then get that

\mathbb{P}_{\omega}\left(|Y_{l}-m(k)|\geq\frac{m(k)}{t^{b}}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{5\mu_{0}^{2}l^{\delta}}{t^{2b}(\log{l})^{2}}\right).

(3.14)

The estimate (
3.14
) is for a single epoch and there are
M\leq t
epochs. Moreover, recalling that
\omega\in E_{tot},
we get from (
3.7
) that

\frac{t^{\gamma(1-\beta)}}{4}\leq L_{1}\leq l\leq\frac{4t^{1-\beta}}{1-\beta}\leq t.

Therefore from (
3.14
) and the union bound we get that

\mathbb{P}_{\omega}\left(E_{good}\right)\geq 1-2t\exp\left(-\frac{5\mu_{0}^{2}t^{\delta\gamma(1-\beta)-2b}}{(\log{t})^{2}}\right).

(3.15)

We choose
b
small so that
b<\frac{\delta\gamma(1-\beta)}{2}.
Averaging over
\omega\in E_{tot}
and using (
3.6
), we then get (
2.16
). This completes the proof of the Theorem.
