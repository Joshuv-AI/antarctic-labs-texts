Sequence Reconstruction Problem for Deletion Channels: A Complete Asymptotic Solution
Van Long Phuoc Pham, Keshav Goyal, and Han Mao Kiah

Abstract
Transmit a codeword 𝒙 \mathbfsl{x} , that belongs to an ( ℓ − 1 ) (\ell-1) -deletion-correcting code of length n n , over a t t -deletion channel for some 1 ≤ ℓ ≤ t < n 1\leq\ell\leq t<n . Levenshtein, in 2001, proposed the problem of determining N ⁡ ( n , ℓ , t ) + 1 N(n,\ell,t)+1 , the minimum number of distinct channel outputs required to uniquely reconstruct 𝒙 \mathbfsl{x} . Prior to this work, N ⁡ ( n , ℓ , t ) N(n,\ell,t) is known only when ℓ ∈ { 1 , 2 } \ell\in\{1,2\} . Here, we provide an asymptotically exact solution for all values of ℓ \ell and t t . Specifically, we show that N ⁡ ( n , ℓ , t ) = ( 2 ​ ℓ ℓ ) / ( t − ℓ ) ! ​ n t − ℓ − O ⁡ ( n t − ℓ − 1 ) N(n,\ell,t)=\binom{2\ell}{\ell}/(t-\ell)!n^{t-\ell}-O(n^{t-\ell-1}) and in the special instance where ℓ = t \ell=t , we show that N ⁡ ( n , ℓ , ℓ ) = ( 2 ​ ℓ ℓ ) N(n,\ell,\ell)=\binom{2\ell}{\ell} . I
Introduction

The
sequence reconstruction problem

[
2
]
, introduced by Levenshtein in 2001, considers a communication scenario where the sender transmits a codeword
\mathbfsl{x}
from some codebook
\mathcal{C}
over a number of noisy channels.
The receiver then obtains all the noisy channel outputs and attempts to reconstruct the transmitted codeword
\mathbfsl{x}
.
So, for a fixed codebook
\mathcal{C}
and channel, our task is to determine the minimum number of channels that are required for unique reconstruction.
While the sequence reconstruction problem was first motivated by applications in biology and chemistry, the problem has received renewed interest because of certain emerging data storage media. These modern storage media relies on technologies that provide users with multiple cheap, noisy reads and examples include DNA-based data storage
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
and racetrack memories
[
8
,
9
]
.

In this work, we focus on channels that introduce
deletions
.
Formally, when a word of length
n
is sent through a
t
-deletion channel, a subsequence of length
n-t
is received.
A
t
-deletion correcting code
\mathcal{C}
is then a subset of length-
n
binary words such that for any codeword
\mathbfsl{x}\in\mathcal{C}
, we are able to uniquely identify
\mathbfsl{x}
from any length-
(n-t)
subsequence of
\mathbfsl{x}
.
In his seminal work
[
2
,
10
]
, Levenshtein studied the sequence reconstruction problem for the
t
-deletion channel.
For the case where
\mathcal{C}
is the set of all binary sequences, Levenshtein determined the minimum number of channel outputs required for unique reconstruction.
However, when
\mathcal{C}
is a
\ell
-deletion-correcting code where
\ell\geq 1
, little results are known.
Only recently, Gabrys and Yaakobi
[
11
]
solved the sequence reconstruction problem for the
t
-deletion channel when
\mathcal{C}
is a single-deletion-correcting codes and
in the paper, they noted that the problem remains open for the case where
\mathcal{C}
is an
\ell
-deletion correcting codes for
\ell\geq 2
.

However, little progress was made on this open problem. Nevertheless, there was a slew of related results.
The sequence reconstruction problem was solved in the following instances:

•

when the channel involves insertion errors only and the codebook is any
e
-insertion error-correcting
code
[
12
]
;

•

when the channel involves combinations of single substitution and
single insertion error and the codebook comprises of all binary words
[
13
]
.

Recently, in
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
, the authors study the problem of
code design
under such scenarios.
Specifically, they fix the number of channel outputs available to the receiver and
design codes that allow the receiver to uniquely reconstruct the transmitted codeword.

In this work, we revisit the open problem posed by Levenshtein
[
2
]
(and later by Gabrys and Yaakobi
[
11
]
) and provide an
asymptotic solution for all values of
\ell\geq 2
.
Specifically, let
1\leq\ell\leq t
and we transmit codewords from an
(\ell-1)
-deletion-correcting code over a
t
-deletion channel.
In this work, we first show that the number of channels required for unique reconstruction is upper bounded by the quantity
\binom{2\ell}{\ell}n^{t-\ell}/(t-\ell)!
(see Theorem
4
).
Subsequently, we provide a matching lower bound.
That is, we construct a pair of codewords
\mathbfsl{X}
and
\mathbfsl{Y}
with Levenshtein distance at least
\ell
and show that the number of channels required to disambiguate
\mathbfsl{X}
or
\mathbfsl{Y}
is at least
\binom{2\ell}{\ell}n^{t-\ell}/(t-\ell)!-O(n^{t-\ell-1})
.
This therefore implies that our estimate is asymptotically exact (see Theorem
3
).
Furthermore, in the special case where
\ell=t
, we determine that
N(n,\ell,t)=\binom{2\ell}{\ell}
.

Before we formally state our contributions, we briefly remark on our proof techniques.
The main difficulty lies with the proof of the upper bound.
While our arguments bear certain similarities to that of Levenshtein
[
2
]
and Gabrys and Yaakobi
[
11
]
, an analysis that mimics these works is too tedious.
Instead, we turn to techniques in
subsequence combinatorics
.
Of particular interest is
[
17
]
, where Elzinga
et al.
developed certain recursion rules and used dynamic programming to provide quadratic-time algorithms to enumerate certain subsequence problems. In our paper, we modify these recursion rules to provide an inductive proof of the upper bound in Section
III
.
We believe that the recursion rules developed in this work will provide insights for other problems related to sequence reconstruction.

II
Preliminaries

Let
\Sigma
denote the binary alphabet
\{0,1\}
.
We use
\Sigma^{n}
to denote the set of all length-
n
binary sequences.

Let
\mathbfsl{x}\in\Sigma^{n}
. Then the
deletion ball of radius
t
centered at
\mathbfsl{x}
is defined to be the set of all length-
(n-t)
subsequences of
\mathbfsl{x}
and this ball is denoted by
\mathcal{D}_{t}(\mathbfsl{x})
.
Given two binary sequences
\mathbfsl{x}
and
\mathbfsl{y}
with
|\mathbfsl{y}|=|\mathbfsl{x}|+k
, we are interested in the intersection of their deletion balls and
we use
\mathcal{D}(\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y},t,t+k)
to denote the set
\mathcal{D}_{t}(\mathbfsl{x})\cap\mathcal{D}_{t+k}(\mathbfsl{y})
.
Furthermore, we define their
Levenshtein distance
to be
d_{L}(\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y})\triangleq\min\left\{t\geq 0:\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y},t,t+k\right)\neq\varnothing\right\}
.
Equivalently, if
d_{L}(\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y})=\ell
, we have that

\mathcal{D}(\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y},\ell,\ell+k)\neq\varnothing\text{ and }\mathcal{D}(\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y},\ell-1,\ell+k-1)=\varnothing\,.

Hence, a codebook
\mathcal{C}
is an
(\ell-1)
-deletion-correcting code
if
d_{L}(\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y})\geq\ell
for all distinct
\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y}\in\mathcal{C}
.

We now formally define our problem statement.
For
1\leq\ell\leq t<n
, the task of
sequence reconstruction problem
for deletion channels is to determine the following quantity.

N(n,\ell,t)\triangleq\max\{|\mathcal{D}(\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y},t,t+k)|:\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y}\in\Sigma^{n},d_{L}(\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y})\geq\ell\}.

(1)

Suppose we have an
(\ell-1)
-deletion correcting code
\mathcal{C}
of length
n
.
If a codeword from
\mathcal{C}
is transmitted over a
t
-deletion channel,
Levenshtein showed that
N(n,\ell,t)+1
distinct channel outputs are sufficient to allow unique reconstruction of the transmitted word
[
2
]
.

For
\ell\in\{1,2\}
, the exact values
N(n,\ell,t)
have been determined in
[
2
,
11
]
.
To state these results, we require the maximum size of a
t
-deletion ball.
Specifically, for
0\leq t<n
, we use
D(n,t)
to denote the quantity
\max\{|\mathcal{D}_{t}(\mathbfsl{x})|:\mathbfsl{x}\in\Sigma^{n}\}
. We know from
[
19
]
that

D(n,t)=\sum_{i=0}^{t}\binom{n-t}{i}=\frac{1}{t!}n^{t}-O(n^{t-1})\,,

(2)

and the maximum is achieved when the
\mathbfsl{x}
is alternating.
For convenience, we extend the domain of (
1
) to include
\ell=0
and hence,
N(n,0,t)
is given by
D(n,t)
.

When
\ell=1
, we have the following landmark result of Levenshtein.

Theorem 1

(Levenshtein
[
2
]
)
.

For
1\leq t<n
,

N(n,1,t)=2D(n-2,t-1)=\frac{2}{(t-1)!}n^{t-1}-O(n^{t-2}).

(3)

The result for
\ell=2
is obtained more than a decade later by Gabrys and Yaakobi.

Theorem 2

(Gabrys and Yaakobi
[
11
]
)
.

For
2\leq t<n
and
n\geq 8
,

\displaystyle N(n,2,t)

\displaystyle=2D(n-4,t-2)+2D(n-5,t-2)+2D(n-7,t-2)

\displaystyle\hskip 71.13188pt+D(n-6,t-3)+D(n-7,t-3)

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{(t-2)!}n^{t-2}-O(n^{t-3}).

(4)

II-A

Our Contributions

In this paper, we provide asymptotically exact estimates of
N(n,\ell,t)
for
all
values of

Theorem 3

(Main Theorem)
.

For
0\leq\ell\leq t<n
, we have that

N(n,\ell,t)=\frac{\binom{2\ell}{\ell}}{(t-\ell)!}n^{t-\ell}-O(n^{t-\ell-1})\,.

(5)

Remark 1
.

•

Observe that when

•

In (

First, we demonstrate an upper bound for

In particular, we study a general version of the quantity

III
Upper Bound

In this section, we prove Theorem
4
using
induction
.
For convenience, we rewrite the upper bound in the following form that is amenable to an inductive analysis.

Theorem 8
.

Let
{\sf S}(\ell,t,k)
denotes the statement:

For all
n\geq t
, we have that

\Delta(\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y},t,t+k)\begin{cases}\leq\frac{\binom{k+2\ell}{\ell}}{(t-\ell)!}n^{t-\ell},&\mbox{if $t\geq\ell$},\\
=0,&\mbox{if $t<\ell$}.\end{cases}

Then
{\sf S}(\ell,t,k)
is true for all
\ell,t,k\geq 0
.

Note that in Theorem
8
, we extend the domain to include the cases where
t<\ell
.
We justify this in the following lemma where we demonstrate
{\sf S}(\ell,t,k)
for certain bases cases.

Lemma 9

(Base Cases)
.

The following instances are true.

(i)

{\sf S}(0,t,k)
is true for all
t,k\geq 0
.

(ii)

{\sf S}(\ell,t,k)
is true for all
0\leq t<\ell
.

Proof.

For (i), we have
\ell=0
. Since
\mathcal{D}(\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y},t,t+k)\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{t}(\mathbfsl{x})
,
we apply (
2
) to have
\Delta(\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y},t,t+k)\leq n^{t}/t!
.
For (ii), since
t<\ell
, it follows from the definition of Levenshtein distance that
\mathcal{D}(\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y},t,t+k)=\varnothing
.
In other words,
\Delta(\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y},t,t+k)=0
.
∎

Hence, it remains to demonstrates the induction step.
To this end, we define a total order on the set of triples
\{(\ell,t,k):\ell,t,k\geq 0\}
.
Specifically, we use
\prec
to denote the usual
lexicographic order
on the triples.
That is,
(\ell,t,k)\prec(\ell_{0},t_{0},k_{0})
means one of the following:

•

\ell<\ell_{0}
, or

•

\ell=\ell_{0}
and
t<t_{0}
, or

•

\ell=\ell_{0}
,
t=t_{0}
and
k<k_{0}
.

It is well-known that the lexicographic order defines a total order on the set of triples. Hence, in any nonempty subset of triples, there is always a smallest triple with respect to
\prec
.

Now, we are ready to state the induction step.

Lemma 10

(Induction Step)
.

Suppose that
0<\ell_{0}\leq t_{0}
and
k_{0}\geq 0
.
If
{\sf S}(\ell,t,k)
is true for
(0,0,0)\preceq(\ell,t,k)\prec(l_{0},t_{0},k_{0})
, then
{\sf S}(\ell_{0},t_{0},k_{0})
is true.

As the proof of Lemma
10
is fairly technical, we defer the detailed arguments to Subsection
III-B
.
In what follows, we assume that the lemma is true and complete the induction proof of Theorem
8
.

Proof of Theorem
8
.

Suppose otherwise that
{\sf S}(\ell,t,k)
fails to hold for some triple.
We choose the smallest such triple
(\ell_{0},t_{0},k_{0})
with respect to the order
\prec
.
Since the triple is smallest, we have that
{\sf S}(\ell,t,k)
is true for all
(\ell,t,k)\prec(l_{0},t_{0},k_{0})
. Furthermore, Lemma
9
implies that
\ell_{0}>0
and
t\geq\ell_{0}
.

Therefore, the conditions of Lemma
10
are met and so,
{\sf S}(\ell_{0},t_{0},k_{0})
must be true, contradicting our assumption.
∎

For the rest of this section, we prove the induction step, and we adopt for the following convention.
For
\mathbfsl{x}\in\Sigma^{n}
, we write
\mathbfsl{x}
as
x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{n}
.
In other words, for
1\leq i\leq n
, the
i

\mathbfsl{x}
is denoted by
x_{i}
.
Furthermore, the length-

\mathbfsl{x}
is denoted by
\mathbfsl{x}^{(i)}
.
That is,
\mathbfsl{x}^{(i)}=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{i}

Similarly, for

III-A

Recursion Rules

Our induction relies on two recursion rules, Lemmas
12
and
13
.
To state the recursion rules, we use the following notation used extensively in
[
11
]
.
Given a bit
a\in\Sigma
and a set
\mathcal{X}
of nonempty binary sequences, we use
\mathcal{X}_{a}
to denote the set of sequences in
\mathcal{X}
that ends with
a
. Also, we use
\mathcal{X}\circ a
to denote the set of sequences obtained by appending
a
to all sequences in
\mathcal{X}
. Hence,
|\mathcal{X}\circ a|=|\mathcal{X}|
while
|\mathcal{X}_{a}|\leq|\mathcal{X}|
.

Then the following result is folklore.

Lemma 11
.

Given
\mathbfsl{x}\in\Sigma^{n}
and
a\in\Sigma
, let
i
be the largest index integer such that
x_{i}=a
, then we have that
\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}(\mathbfsl{x})\right)_{a}=\mathcal{D}_{t-(n-i)}\left(\mathbfsl{x}^{(i-1)}\right)\circ a
.
Therefore, the deletion ball centered at
\mathbfsl{x}
can be recursively computed using the rule:

\mathcal{D}_{t}(\mathbfsl{x})=\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\left(\mathbfsl{x}^{(n-1)}\right)\circ x_{n}\right)\cup\mathcal{D}_{t-1}(\mathbfsl{x}^{(n-1)})_{\overline{x_{n}}}

Our first recursion rule provides a lower bound on the Levenshtein distance and is simple modification of the usual recursion rules used in dynamic programming (see for example,
[
17
]
).

Lemma 12
.

Suppose that
\mathbfsl{x}\in\Sigma^{n}
and
\mathbfsl{y}\in\Sigma^{n+k}
with
k\geq 0
and

d_{L}(\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y})\geq\ell
.

•

When
x_{n}=y_{n+k}
,

d_{L}(\mathbfsl{x}^{(n-1)},\mathbfsl{y}^{(n+k-1)})\geq\ell\,.

(9)

•

When
x_{n}\neq y_{n+k}
and
k=0
,

d_{L}(\mathbfsl{x}^{(n-1)},\mathbfsl{y})\geq\ell-1,~~~d_{L}(\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y}^{(n+k-1)})\geq\ell-1.

(10)

•

When
x_{n}\neq y_{n+k}
and
k>0
,

d_{L}(\mathbfsl{x}^{(n-1)},\mathbfsl{y})\geq\ell-1,~~~~~d_{L}(\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y}^{(n+k-1)}))\geq\ell.

(11)

Proof.

If
x_{n}=y_{n+k}
, we claim that
\mathcal{D}(\mathbfsl{x}^{(n-1)},\mathbfsl{y}^{(n+k-1)},

\ell-1,\ell+k-1)=\varnothing
. Suppose otherwise that
\mathbfsl{z}
belongs to
\mathcal{D}(\mathbfsl{x}^{(n-1)},\mathbfsl{y}^{(n+k-1)},\ell-1,\ell+k-1)
, then we have
\mathbfsl{z}\circ~x_{n}=\mathbfsl{z}\circ y_{n+k}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y},\ell-1,\ell+k-1)
, which is a contradiction.

If
x_{n}\neq y_{n+k}
, we claim
\mathcal{D}(\mathbfsl{x}^{(n-1)},\mathbfsl{y},\ell-2,\ell{+}k{-}1){=}\varnothing
. Again, suppose otherwise that
\mathbfsl{z}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbfsl{x}^{(n-1)},\mathbfsl{y},\ell{-}2,\ell{+}k{-}1)
. Then we have
\mathbfsl{z}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y},\ell{-}1,\ell{+}k{-}1)
because
\mathcal{D}_{\ell-2}(\mathbfsl{x}^{(n-1)})\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{\ell-1}(\mathbfsl{x})
. This is a contradiction.

•

When
k=0
, we have
\mathcal{D}(\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y}^{(n+k-1)},\ell-1,\ell+k-2)=\varnothing
by symmetry.

•

When
k>0
, we claim
\mathcal{D}(\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y}^{(n+k-1)},\ell{-}1,\ell{+}k{-}2){=}\varnothing
. Again, suppose that
\mathbfsl{z}{\in}\mathcal{D}(\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y}^{(n+k-1)},\ell{-}1,l{+}k{-}2)
. Then
\mathbfsl{z}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y},\ell-1,l+k-1)
because
\mathcal{D}_{l+k-2}(\mathbfsl{y}^{(n+k-1)})\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{l+k-1}(\mathbfsl{y})
. This is a contradiction.
∎

The next recursion rule is crucial to our inductive proof.
In particular, we show that we can bound the size of
\mathcal{D}(\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{y},t,t+k)
using the corresponding values for the prefixes of
\mathbfsl{x}
and
\mathbfsl{y}
.

Lemma 13
.

Let
\mathbfsl{x}\in\Sigma^{n}
,
\mathbfsl{y}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n+k}
. Then the following are true.

•

If
x_{n}=y_{n+k}
, then

Proposition 17

\mathcal{C}

(t-1)

\mathbfsl{y}_{1}
and
\mathbfsl{y}_{2}

\mathbfsl{x}
and
\mathbfsl{x}^{\prime}

\mathbfsl{X}\in\Sigma^{n}

\mathbfsl{Y},\mathbfsl{Z}\in\mathcal{D}_{t}(\mathbfsl{X})

\mathbfsl{Z}

\mathbfsl{X}=\mathbfsl{A}\mathbfsl{V}

\mathbfsl{V}
is the shortest suffix of
\mathbfsl{X}
that contains
\mathbfsl{U}

In other words,
\mathbfsl{B}0
and
\mathbfsl{C}1

\mathcal{D}_{t-k}(\mathbfsl{A})

Since
\mathcal{D}_{t-k-1}(\mathbfsl{A})\circ\mathbfsl{U}=\mathcal{D}_{t-1}(\mathbfsl{X})_{\mathbfsl{U}}
,
we have that
\mathbfsl{C}10\mathbfsl{U}\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1}(\mathbfsl{X})
.

(ii)

Similarly, if
\mathbfsl{A}=\mathbfsl{A}^{\prime}1
, then
\mathcal{D}_{t-k}(\mathbfsl{A})_{0}=\mathcal{D}_{t-k}(\mathbfsl{A}^{\prime}1)_{0}=\mathcal{D}_{t-k-1}(\mathbfsl{A}^{\prime})_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{t-k-1}(\mathbfsl{A}^{\prime})
.
This means
\mathbfsl{B}0\in\mathcal{D}_{t-k-1}(\mathbfsl{A}^{\prime})
.
Hence,
\mathbfsl{B}01\in\mathcal{D}_{t-k-1}(\mathbfsl{A})
.
Since
\mathcal{D}_{t-k-1}(\mathbfsl{A})\circ\mathbfsl{U}=\mathcal{D}_{t-1}(\mathbfsl{X})_{\mathbfsl{U}}
,
we have that
\mathbfsl{B}01\mathbfsl{U}\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1}(\mathbfsl{X})
. ∎

We are now ready to present our algorithm for Proposition
17
.
Recall that
\mathcal{X}
is a subcode of a
(t-1)
-deletion-correcting code with the property that
D(\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{x}^{\prime},t,t)<M
for all pairs of codewords
\mathbfsl{x},\mathbfsl{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
.

Input
:
\mathbfsl{y}_{1},\mathbfsl{y}_{2},\ldots,\mathbfsl{y}_{M}\in\mathcal{D}_{t}(\mathbfsl{x})
for some
\mathbfsl{x}\in\mathcal{X}

Output
:
\mathbfsl{x}\in\mathcal{X}

(1)

We pick two outputs,
\mathbfsl{y}_{1},\mathbfsl{y}_{2}
, and set
\mathbfsl{u}
to be the longest common suffix of
\mathbfsl{y}_{1}
and
\mathbfsl{y}_{2}
. Without loss of generality, assume that
\mathbfsl{y}_{1}=\mathbfsl{a}_{1}0\mathbfsl{u}
and
\mathbfsl{y}_{2}=\mathbfsl{a}_{2}1\mathbfsl{u}
.

(2)

By Lemma
18
, we have two possible scenarios.

•

If
\mathbfsl{a}_{1}01\mathbfsl{u}\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1}(\mathbfsl{x})
, then we can recover
\mathbfsl{x}
by using the
(t-1)
-deletion-correcting decoder. Specifically, we set
\mathbfsl{x}_{1}\leftarrow{\tt DEC}(\mathbfsl{a}_{1}01\mathbfsl{u})
.

•

Similarly, if
\mathbfsl{a}_{2}10\mathbfsl{u}\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1}(\mathbfsl{x})
, we can also recover
\mathbfsl{x}
using
{\tt DEC}
. So, we set
\mathbfsl{x}_{2}\leftarrow{\tt DEC}(\mathbfsl{y}_{2}^{\prime}10\mathbfsl{u})
.

(3)

Finally, to distinguish between the two scenarios, we use the remaining outputs
\mathbfsl{y}_{3},\mathbfsl{y}_{4},\ldots,\mathbfsl{y}_{M}
. Specifically, if
\{\mathbfsl{y}_{3},\mathbfsl{y}_{4},\ldots,\mathbfsl{y}_{M}\}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{t}(\mathbfsl{x}_{1})
, we return the codeword
\mathbfsl{x}_{1}
. Otherwise, we return
\mathbfsl{x}_{2}
.

To complete the proof of Proposition
17
, we analyse the running time.
Clearly, Steps 1 and 2 can be completed in
O(n)
time and
2T(n)
time respectively.
For Step 3, we need to determine if
\mathbfsl{y}_{j}
is a subsequence of
\mathbfsl{x}_{i}
for
3\leq j\leq M
and
i\in\{1,2\}
.
Since each verification can be completed in
O(n)
time, Step 3 can be completed in
O(Mn)
time and the proposition follows.

To conclude this section, we discuss the implication of Proposition
17
for the case where
t=2
, that is, the channel that introduces two deletions.

Consider a VT code
\mathcal{C}
of length
n
.
As mentioned earlier,
\mathcal{C}
is a single-deletion-correcting code that is equipped with a linear-time decoder.
In
[
11
]
(or Theorem
2
), Gabrys and Yaakobi showed that we can uniquely reconstruct any codeword of
\mathcal{C}
using seven distinct reads.
Later, Chrisnata and Kiah considered a subcode
\mathcal{X}
of
\mathcal{C}
with
2\log_{2}n+O(\log_{2}\log_{2}n)
redundant bits and showed that any codeword of
\mathcal{C}
can be reconstructed using five distinct reads
[
16
]
.
In both cases, naively, we can reconstruct the transmitted word in quadratic time by trying all possibilities for the missing two positions.
However, if we apply the algorithms in Propositions
7
and
17
, we are able to recover the transmitted word in linear time.
