Total negativity: characterizations and single-vector tests
Projesh Nath Choudhury

Abstract
. A matrix is called totally negative (totally non-positive) of order k k , if all its minors of size at most k k are negative (non-positive). The objective of this article is to provide several novel characterizations of total negativity via the (a) variation diminishing property, (b) sign non-reversal property, and (c) Linear Complementarity Problem. (The last of these provides a novel connection between total negativity and optimization/game theory.) More strongly, each of these three characterizations uses a single test vector whose coordinates alternate in sign. As an application of the sign non-reversal property, we study the interval hull of two rectangular matrices. In particular, we identify two matrices C ± ​ ( A , B ) C^{\pm}(A,B) in the interval hull of matrices A A and B B that test total negativity of order k k , simultaneously for the entire interval hull. We also show analogous characterizations for totally non-positive matrices and provide a finite set of test matrices to detect the total non-positivity property of an interval hull. These novel characterizations may be considered similar in spirit to fundamental results characterizing totally positive matrices by Brown–Johnstone–MacGibbon [ J. Amer. Statist. Assoc. , 1981] (see also Gantmacher–Krein, 1950), Choudhury–Kannan–Khare [ Bull. London Math. Soc. , 2021] and Choudhury [ Bull. London Math. Soc. , 2022]. Finally, we show that totally negative/non-positive matrices can not be detected by (single) test vectors from orthants other than the open bi-orthant that have coordinates with alternating signs, via the variation diminishing property or the sign non-reversal property.

1.
Introduction and main results

Given integers
m,n\geq k\geq 1
, a real matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}

totally negative of order
k

A
of order at most
k
are negative, and

if

. Similarly, one defines
totally non-positive
matrices (including of order

In prior discussions of totally negative (or totally non-positive)
matrices, their analogy to totally positive/
TP
(or totally
non-negative/
TN
) matrices, which are matrices with all positive (or
non-negative) minors, perpetually comes up first (and which we refer to
henceforth by the standard abbreviations
TP
and
TN
, or
TP_{k}
and

TN_{k}
to distinguish them from the matrices studied in the present
paper). Indeed,
TP
and
TN
are widely studied because of their
numerous applications in a variety of topics in mathematics, including,
analysis, combinatorics, cluster algebras, differential equations, Gabor
analysis, matrix theory, probability, and representation theory

[
2
,
3
,
4
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
20
,
24
,
28
,
33
,
34
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
. In 1937, Gantmacher–Krein
[

]
characterized

matrices using positivity of the spectra of all square submatrices. A similar characterization for totally negative matrices was given by Fallat–van den Driessche in 2000
[
15
]
. They proved that if
A
is a totally negative matrix then all the eigenvalues of each square submatrix of
A
are real, distinct, nonzero and exactly one eigenvalue is negative. (The converse is immediate.) Similar spectral characterizations hold for
TN
and totally non-positive matrices, using the respective density of
TP
and totally negative matrices in these classes and the continuity of roots. Totally negative/non-positive matrices are also studied in the context of matrix factorization and spectral perturbation, see
[

Another interesting property exhibited by
TP
and
TN
matrices is the variation diminishing property. The phrase ‘variation diminishing’ (‘variationsvermindernd’ in German) was formulated by Pólya in connection with proving the following result of Laguerre
[
29

[
14

: if

is a (real) polynomial and
\gamma\geq 0

var(e^{\gamma x}f(x))
in the Maclaurin coefficients of
e^{\gamma x}f(x)
are non-increasing in
0\leq\gamma<\infty
, and are thereby bounded
above by
var(f(x))<\infty
. In 1912, Fekete in correspondence with Pólya reformulated Laguerre’s result in the language of
TP
and proved this using one-sided Pólya frequency sequences and their variation diminishing property. In 1930, Schoenberg
[
37
]
proved that the variation diminishing property holds for
TN
matrices. The characterization of
TP
and
TN
in terms of variation diminution was achieved in 1950 by Gantmacher–Krein
[
20
]
which was later refined by Brown–Johnstone–MacGibbon in 1981
[
5
]
.
The variation diminishing property is also satisfied by other matrices, e.g.
-A
for
A

TP/TN
. This was explored by Motzkin
[
31
]
in his PhD thesis in 1936, and he showed that the class of matrices satisfying the variation diminishing property are precisely the sign-regular matrices – in other words, there exists a sequence of signs
\varepsilon_{k}\in\{\pm 1\}
such that every
k\times k
minor of
A
has sign
\varepsilon_{k}
, for all
k\geq 1
. A systematic treatment of sign-regular matrices and kernels can be found in Karlin’s comprehensive monograph
[
28
]
.

The goal of this note is to provide similar characterization results for the class of totally negative/totally non-positive matrices, i.e. ones for which all minors are negative/non-positive. More precisely, we provide novel characterizations of such matrices using (a) sign non-reversal and (b) Linear Complementarity, to go along with Motzkin’s classical characterization using (c) variation diminution. In addition, we strengthen all three tests (a), (b), and (c) to work with only a single test vector, in parallel to our recent works
[
8
,
9
]
for
TP
matrices.

To state these results, we need some preliminary definitions, which are used below without further reference.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
m,n\geq 1
be integers.

(1)

Given a vector

, let

denote the number of sign changes in

\pm 1

(2)

A square matrix

(3)

(4)

(5)

Let

denote the set of real vectors whose coordinates are nonzero and have alternating signs. Also define
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\rm+,-}
to comprise the vectors in

with at least one positive and one negative coordinate.

(6)

Given a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and

, let

denote the determinant of the submatrix of
A
of size

(7)

If

to be the determinant of the empty matrix. (This, convention will only be used in Theorem
E

5.8

(8)

Let

denote the vector whose

1

(9)

We say that an array

is
\geq 0
(respectively

) if all coordinates of
X
are
\geq 0
(respectively
>0,~\leq 0,~<0
).

(10)

Define the vector
d^{[n]}:=(1,-1,\dots,(-1)^{n-1})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\rm alt}
.

(11)

Given

, define the diagonal matrix

whose

.

(12)

Given two matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}

via:

|A|_{ij}:=|a_{ij}|,\qquad I_{z,\tilde{z}}(A,B):=\frac{A+B}{2}-D_{z}\frac{|A-B|}{2}D_{\tilde{z}},\qquad C^{\pm}(A,B):=I_{d^{[m]},\;\pm d^{[n]}}(A,B).

Now we state our main results – only for totally negative matrices. The penultimate section contains the analogous theorems for totally non-positive matrices. We first look at one of the most well known and widely used properties of
TP

. Recently in
[

Our first main result similarly characterizes total negativity using the variation diminishing property.

Theorem A
.

Let
m,n\geq 2
be integers. Given a real
m\times n
matrix
A
with
A<0
, the following statements are
equivalent:

(1)

A
is totally negative.

(2)

For all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\rm+,-}
,
S^{+}(Ax)\leq S^{-}(x)
. Moreover, if equality holds and
Ax\neq 0
, then the first (last) component of
Ax
(if zero, the unique sign given in determining

) has the same sign as the first (last) nonzero component of

.

(3)

Let
k=\min\{m,n\}
and
r\in[2,k]
. For every
r\times r
contiguous submatrix
A_{r}
of
A
and for any choice of nonzero vector
\alpha:=(\alpha_{1},-\alpha_{2},\ldots,(-1)^{r-1}\alpha_{r})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
with all
\alpha_{i}\geq 0
(or
\leq 0
), define the vector

(1.1)

x^{A_{r}}:=\adj(A_{r})\alpha.

Then
S^{+}(A_{r}x^{A_{r}})\leq S^{-}(x^{A_{r}})
. If equality occurs here, then the first (last)
component of
A_{r}x^{A_{r}}
(if zero, the unique sign given in determining
S^{+}(A_{r}x^{A_{r}})
) has the same sign as the first (last)
nonzero component of
x^{A_{r}}
.

We now turn our attention to a recently developed characterization of

Theorem B
.

Let

(1)

The matrix

(2)

(3)

\mathbb{R}^{r}_{\rm alt}
.

In fact, these conditions are further equivalent to sign non-reversal at a

(4)

For every contiguous square submatrix

of

(or

(1.2)

.

A careful look at the characterization of

As an application of Theorem

Recall that the

(1.3)

\mathbb{I}(A,B)=\{C\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}:c_{ij}=t_{ij}a_{ij}+(1-t_{ij})b_{ij},t_{ij}\in[0,1]\}.

Theorem C

Let

Remark 1.3

Note that

In the last part of our discussion of total negativity, we characterize

Any

(complementarity) solution

\mathrm{SOL}(A,q)
denote

The Linear Complementarity Problem has important applications in diverse
areas in mathematics, including bimatrix games, convex quadratic
programming, economics, fluid mechanics, and variational inequalities

[

12
,
13
,
30

studied in connection with the Linear Complementarity Problem, for

[

]

-matrices
[

]
(more

]

We end by explaining the organization of the paper. The next three sections contain the proofs of our main results above: the variation diminishing property; the sign non-reversal and interval hull results; and the results involving the LCP. Also note that it is natural to ask for ‘totally non-positive’ analogues of the above main results, in the spirit of
TN

TP

We end on a somewhat philosophical note. The rich applications of
TP

The main results in the present paper obtain similar characterization results for totally negative and totally non-positive matrices. We hope that these results will lead to applications of these classes of matrices in other areas.

2.
Theorem
A
: Characterization of total negativity using variation diminution

We begin by proving Theorem
A
– i.e., to provide a characterization of totally negative matrices using variation diminution. This requires a 1968 result of Karlin for totally negative matrices (in fact strictly sign-regular matrices) which was first proved by Fekete in 1912 for
TP
matrices, and subsequently extended by Schoenberg to
TP_{k}
in 1955.

Theorem 2.1

(Karlin
[
28
]
)
.

Let
m,n\geq k\geq 1
be integers. Then
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}

is totally negative of order
k
if and only if every
r\times r
contiguous submatrix of
A
has negative determinant, for
r\in[1,k]
.

Proof of Theorem
A
.

We begin by showing
(1)\implies(2)
. Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
be a totally negative matrix and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\rm+,-}
with
1\leq S^{-}(x)=k\leq n-1
. Then
x
can be partitioned into
k+1
components of contiguous coordinates with like signs:

(2.1)

(x_{1},\ldots,x_{s_{1}}),~~(x_{s_{1}+1},\ldots,x_{s_{2}}),~~\ldots,(x_{s_{k}+1},\ldots,x_{n}),

with at least one coordinate in each component nonzero and all nonzero coordinates in the
i
th component having the same sign
(-1)^{i-1}
, without loss of generality. Moreover, we set
s_{0}=0
and
s_{k+1}=n
. Denote the columns of
A
by
a^{1},\ldots,a^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and define

b^{i}:=\sum\limits_{j=s_{i-1}+1}^{s_{i}}|x_{j}|a^{j},\hbox{~for~}i\in[1,{k+1}].

One can verify that the matrix
B:=[b^{1},\ldots,b^{k+1}]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times(k+1)}
is totally negative and
Bd^{[k+1]}=Ax
.

With this information in hand, we now prove
S^{+}(Ax)\leq S^{-}(x)=k
. For ease of exposition, we split the remainder of the proof into two cases.

Case 1.
m\leq{k+1}.

If
Ax\neq 0
, then
S^{+}(Ax)\leq{m-1}\leq S^{-}(x)
. If
Ax=0
, then
m\leq k
, since
Ax=Bd^{[k+1]}
and
B
is invertible if
m=k+1
. Thus
S^{+}(Ax)=m\leq S^{-}(x)
.

Case 2.
m>{k+1}.

Set
y:=Ax=Bd^{[k+1]}
. If
S^{+}(Ax)>k
, then there exist indices
i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k+2}\in[1,m]
and a sign
\epsilon\in\{+,-\}
such that
(-1)^{r-1}\epsilon y_{i_{r}}\geq 0
for
r\in[1,{k+2}]
. Since
B
is totally negative, at least two of the
y_{i_{r}}
are nonzero. Let
I=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k+2}\}
and define the
(k+2)\times(k+2)
matrix

M:=[y_{I}|B_{I\times[1,{k+1}]}].

Then
\det M=0
, since the first column of
M
is a linear combination of the rest. Moreover, expanding along the first column gives

(2.2)

0=\sum_{r=1}^{k+2}(-1)^{r-1}y_{i_{r}}\det B_{{I\setminus\{i_{r}\}}\times[1,{k+1}]},

a contradiction, since
B
is totally negative, all
(-1)^{r-1}y_{i_{r}}
have the same sign, and at least two
y_{i_{r}}
are nonzero. Thus
S^{+}(Ax)\leq k=S^{-}(x)
.

It remains to prove the second part of the assertion
(2)
. We continue our discussion using the notation in the preceding analysis. We claim that, if
S^{+}(Ax)=S^{-}(x)=k
with
Ax\neq 0
, and
(-1)^{r-1}\epsilon y_{i_{r}}\geq 0
for
r\in[1,{k+1}]
– as opposed to
[1,{k+2}]
, then
\epsilon=1
. Since
B
is totally negative and
Bd^{[k+1]}=Ax
, so the submatrix
B_{I\times[1,{k+1}]}
is invertible and
B_{I\times[1,~k+1]}d^{[k+1]}=y_{I}
, where
I=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k+1}\}
. By Cramer’s rule, the first coordinate of
d^{[k+1]}
is

1=\frac{\det[y_{I}|B_{I\times[2,{k+1}]}]}{\det B_{I\times[1,{k+1}]}}.

Multiplying both sides by
\epsilon\det B_{I\times[1,{k+1}]}
and expanding the numerator along the first column, we have

(2.3)

\epsilon\det B_{I\times[1,{k+1}]}=\sum_{r=1}^{k+1}(-1)^{r-1}\epsilon y_{i_{r}}\det B_{I\setminus\{i_{r}\}\times[2,{k+1}]}.

Since the summation on the right side is negative and
B
is totally negative, we obtain
\epsilon=1
.

We next show that
(3)\implies(1)
. By Theorem
2.1
,
it suffices to show for all
r\times r
(
r\in[1,k]
) contiguous
submatrices
A_{r}
of
A
that
\det A_{r}<0
. The proof is by induction on

r\in[1,k]
. The base case holds by the hypothesis.

Let
A_{r}
be an
r\times r
contiguous submatrix of
A
with
r\in[2,k]
and suppose that all contiguous minors of
A
of size at most
(r-1)
are negative. The same holds for all proper minors of
A_{r}
by Theorem
2.1
. Define the vector
x^{A_{r}}
as in (
1.1
).
Then

(2.4)

x^{A_{r}}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}(-1)^{j-1}\alpha_{j}\cdot(-1)^{(i-1)+(j-1)}A^{ji}_{r}=(-1)^{i-1}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\alpha_{j}A^{ji}_{r}

and if the
\alpha_{i}
’s are non-negative (or non-positive), then the expression on the right is negative (or positive) for odd
i
and positive (or negative) for even
i
. It follows that

(2.5)

x^{A_{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}_{\rm alt},\qquad A_{r}x^{A_{r}}=(\det{A_{r}})\alpha.

We first claim that
A_{r}
is invertible. Suppose instead that
A_{r}
is singular. Then
r=S^{+}(A_{r}x^{A_{r}})>S^{-}(x^{A_{r}})=r-1
, a contradiction by
(3)
, and hence
A_{r}
is invertible as claimed.

Next we show that
\det{A_{r}}<0
. By (
2.5
), we have

r-1=S^{+}(A_{r}x^{A_{r}})=S^{-}(x^{A_{r}}),

since the condition on the
\alpha_{i}
’s implies that

S^{+}(\alpha)=r-1
.
Also, the sign of the first (last) component of

\frac{1}{\det A_{r}}A_{r}x^{A_{r}}
(if zero, the sign that attains
S^{+}(\frac{1}{\det A_{r}}A_{r}x^{A_{r}})
)
is positive or negative if the
\alpha_{i}
’s are non-negative or
non-positive respectively.
According to the two lines following
(
2.4
), we know the sign of the first (last) component of

x^{A_{r}}
, and can use hypothesis (3) to conclude with
(
2.5
) that
\det{A_{r}}<0
. This completes the
induction step and also the proof of
(3)\implies(1)
.

Finally, we show that
(2)\implies(3)
, by induction on

r\in[2,k]
. We know that
A
is totally negative of order

r-1
(by hypothesis if
r=2
, and by the induction hypothesis
combined with
(3)\implies(1)
if
r>2
). Suppose
A_{r}=A_{I\times J}
is an
r\times r
contiguous submatrix of
A
, for
contiguous sets of indices
I\subseteq[1,m]
and
J\subseteq[1,n]
with
\lvert I\rvert=\lvert J\rvert=r
. Define

x^{A_{r}}
as in (
1.1
). As above,
A_{r}
is
totally negative of order
r-1
, so the entries in each row and
column of
\adj(A_{r})
have alternating signs. Since
\alpha\neq 0
, (
1.1
) implies that
x^{A_{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}_{\rm alt}
. Now
define
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\rm+,-}
to have
x^{A_{r}}
in contiguous positions

J\subseteq[1,m]
and
0
elsewhere. Then

(2.6)

S^{+}(A_{r}x^{A_{r}})\leq S^{+}(Ax)\quad\hbox{and}\quad S^{-}(x)=S^{-}(x^{A_{r}}).

By
(2)
, we have
S^{+}(A_{r}x^{A_{r}})\leq S^{-}(x^{A_{r}})
.

Next, suppose that
S^{+}(A_{r}x^{A_{r}})=S^{-}(x^{A_{r}})=r-1
. Then
S^{+}(Ax)=S^{-}(x)
. Since
S^{+}(A_{r}x^{A_{r}})=S^{+}(Ax)
, by (2), it follows that all coordinates of
Ax
or an
S^{+}
-completion of
Ax
in positions
1,2,\ldots,i_{1}
(respectively, in positions
i_{r},\ldots,m
) are nonzero, and with the same sign as that of
x^{A_{r}}_{1}~(\hbox{respectively}~x^{A_{r}}_{r})
. This shows
(2)\implies(3)
.
∎

3.
Theorems
B
and
C
: Sign non-reversal property and interval hulls for totally negative matrices

We next prove Theorems
B
and
C
. To prove
Theorem
B
, we begin with a preliminary result, which
establishes the sign non-reversal property for matrices with negative
principal minors (these are known as
N
-matrices):

Theorem 3.1
.

[
32
]

Let
n\geq 2
be an integer and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that
A<0
. Then
A
has all principal minors
negative if and only if for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\rm+,-}
, there exists

i\in[1,n]
such that
x_{i}(Ax)_{i}>0
.

Proof of Theorem
B
.

That
(1)\implies(2)
follows from Theorem
3.1
; and that

(2)\implies(3)
is immediate. Next, suppose

(4)
holds. To show
(1)
, by Theorem
2.1
it suffices to show that the
determinants of all
r\times r
contiguous submatrices of
A

are negative, for
r\in[1,k]
. We prove this by induction on

r
, where the base case holds by the hypothesis. Fix
r\in[2,k]
and assume that all
(r-1)\times(r-1)
and smaller
contiguous minors of
A
are negative. Let
A_{r}
be an
r\times r
contiguous submatrix of
A
. By the induction hypothesis, all
the proper contiguous minors of
A_{r}
are negative. In fact, all
the proper minors of
A_{r}
are negative by Theorem
2.1
.

For any choice of a nonzero vector
\alpha:=(\alpha_{1},-\alpha_{2},\ldots,(-1)^{r-1}\alpha_{r})^{T}
with all
\alpha_{i}\geq 0
(or
\leq 0
), define the vector
x^{A_{r}}
as in (
1.2
). Then

(3.1)

x^{A_{r}}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}(-1)^{j-1}\alpha_{j}\cdot(-1)^{(i-1)+(j-1)}A^{ji}_{r}=(-1)^{i-1}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\alpha_{j}A^{ji}_{r}

and if the
\alpha_{i}
’s are non-negative (or non-positive), then the expression on the right is negative (or positive) for odd
i
and positive (or negative) for even
i
. Thus
x^{A_{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}_{\rm alt}
, and

(3.2)

A_{r}x^{A_{r}}=(\det A_{r})\alpha.

By the hypothesis, (
3.1
), and (
3.2
), we have
i_{0}\in[1,r]
such that

(3.3)

0<x^{A_{r}}_{i_{0}}(A_{r}x^{A_{r}})_{i_{0}}=(-1)^{i_{0}-1}(\det A_{r})\alpha_{i_{0}}x^{A_{r}}_{i_{0}}.

From this, it follows that
\det A_{r}<0
, which completes the
induction step. Hence
(4)\implies(1)
.

Finally, we show that
(3)\implies(4)
, by induction on
r\in[2,k]
. We know
A
is totally negative of order
r-1
(by
hypothesis if
r=2
, and by the induction hypothesis and
(4)\implies(1)
if
r>2
). As in (
3.1
) and lines following it,
x^{A_{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}_{\rm alt}
since

\alpha\neq 0
. Now
(3)
immediately implies
(4)
.
∎

To prove Theorem
C
, we need two preliminary lemmas; the first is straightforward.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
m,n\geq 1
and
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. Then
I_{z,\tilde{z}}(A,B)\in\mathbb{I}(A,B)
for all
z\in\{\pm 1\}^{m},\tilde{z}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

[
35
]

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let the vector
z\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
be such that
z_{i}=1
if
x_{i}\geq 0
and
z_{i}=-1
if
x_{i}<0
. If
C\in\mathbb{I}(A,B)
, then

x_{i}(Cx)_{i}\geq x_{i}(I_{z,z}(A,B)x)_{i},\quad\forall i\in[1,n].

With these preliminaries at hand, we now prove Theorem
C
.

Proof of Theorem
C
.

The result is immediate for
k=1
, so we suppose henceforth
m,n\geq k\geq 2
. Let
\mathbb{I}(A,B)
be totally negative of order
k
. By Lemma
3.2
,
C^{\pm}(A,B)
are totally negative of order
k
.

Conversely, suppose
C^{\pm}(A,B)
are totally negative of order
k
, and let
M\in\mathbb{I}(A,B)
. Then
M<0
, since
C^{+}(A,B),C^{-}(A,B)<0
. Fix
r\in[2,k]
and a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{r}_{\rm alt}
. Let
M^{\prime}=M_{J\times K}
be an
r\times r
contiguous submatrix of

M
, where
J\subseteq[1,m]
and
K\subset[1,n]
are contiguous sets of indices with
|J|=|K|=r
. By Theorem
B
(3), it suffices to
show that there exists a coordinate
i\in[1,r]
such that
x_{i}(M^{\prime}x)_{i}>0
.

To proceed further, we need some notation. Let
A^{\prime},B^{\prime}
be contiguous
submatrices of
A,B
respectively, with the rows and columns of both indexed by
J
and
K
, respectively. Since
M^{\prime}\in\mathbb{I}(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
,
Lemma
3.3
implies there exists
z^{\prime}\in\{\pm 1\}^{r}\cap\mathbb{R}^{r}_{\rm alt}
such
that

(3.4)

x_{i}(M^{\prime}x)_{i}\geq x_{i}(I_{z^{\prime},z^{\prime}}(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})x)_{i},\qquad\forall i\in[1,r].

Since
M^{\prime}=M_{J\times K}
, we extend
z^{\prime}
to
z\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\rm alt}\cap\{\pm 1\}^{m}
(
\tilde{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\rm alt}\cap\{\pm 1\}^{n}
) by embedding in contiguous positions
J\subseteq[1,m]
(
K\subseteq[1,n]
) and uniquely padding by
\pm 1
elsewhere. Then
I_{z^{\prime},z^{\prime}}(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
is a
r\times r
contiguous submatrix of

I_{z,\;\tilde{z}}(A,B):=\frac{A+B}{2}-D_{z}\frac{|A-B|}{2}D_{\tilde{z}}\in\{C^{+}(A,B),C^{-}(A,B)\}.

By hypothesis, this matrix is totally negative of order
k
. Thus
I_{z^{\prime},z^{\prime}}(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
is totally negative. Using Theorem
B
(3) and (
3.4
), for some
i\in[1,r]
, we have

x_{i}(M^{\prime}x)_{i}\geq x_{i}(I_{z^{\prime},z^{\prime}}(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})x)_{i}>0.

Thus
M^{\prime}
has the sign non-reversal property on
\mathbb{R}^{r}_{\rm alt}
. Since
M<0
, by Theorem
B
,
M
is totally negative of order
k
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

Theorem
C
can be alternately shown using Garloff’s
results for
n\times n
matrices in
[
21
]
. These results
(also see
[
33
, Chapter 3]
) have been stated using the
checkerboard ordering
\leq^{*}
, wherein

A\leq^{*}B\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad D_{d^{[n]}}(B-A)D_{d^{[n]}}\geq 0_{n\times n}.

It is now easily seen that the composition of the hull

\mathbb{I}(A,B)
does not change upon using the entrywise or the
checkerboard ordering:

\mathbb{I}(A,B)=\{C:I_{l}\leq C\leq I_{u}\}=\{C:C^{-}(A,B)\leq^{*}C\leq^{*}C^{+}(A,B)\}.

This implies that the test set
\{C^{+}(A,B),C^{-}(A,B)\}
will
work regardless of the ordering used – and it will work for all
rectangular matrices, as well as in testing the property of being
totally negative of order
k
, for any
k\geq 1
.

4.
Theorems
D
and
E
: Characterization of total negativity via the LCP

In this section, we show Theorems
D
and
E
. To proceed, we recall a 1990 result which establishes a connection between the LCP and matrices with negative principal minors.

Theorem 4.1
.

[
32
]

A matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with
A<0
has all principal minors negative if and only if

\mathrm{LCP}(A,q)
has exactly two solutions for all
q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
q>0
.

Proof of Theorem
D
.

That
(1)\implies(2)
follows from Theorem
4.1
, while
(2)\implies(3)\implies(4)
is immediate. We now show
(4)\implies(1)
. We first claim that
A<0
. Indeed, if
a_{ij}\geq 0
for some
i\in[1,m]
and
j\in[1,n]
, then
(0)
is the
only solution of
\mathrm{LCP}((a_{ij})_{1\times 1},q)
for any scalar
q>0
.

Next, we show that the determinants of all
r\times r
submatrices of
A
are negative, for
r\in[2,k]
. By Theorem
B
, it suffices to show for that every contiguous square submatrix of
A
of size
r\in[2,k]
has the sign non-reversal property on
\mathbb{R}^{r}_{\rm alt}
. Let
r\in[2,k]
and
A_{r}
be an
r\times r
contiguous submatrix of
A
which does not satisfy this property. Then there exists
x\in\mathbb{R}^{r}_{\rm alt}
such that
x_{i}(A_{r}x)_{i}\leq 0
for all
i\in[1,r]
. Let
A_{r}x=v
. Define
x^{\pm}:=\frac{1}{2}(|x|\pm x)
and
v^{\pm}:=\frac{1}{2}(|v|\pm v)
. Then
x^{+}
,
x^{-}
have sign patterns
\begin{pmatrix}+\\
0\\
+\\
0\\
\vdots\end{pmatrix}
,
\begin{pmatrix}0\\
+\\
0\\
+\\
\vdots\end{pmatrix}
respectively (or vice-versa) and
{(x^{+})}^{T}v^{+}={(x^{-})}^{T}v^{-}=0
, since
x_{i}v_{i}\leq 0
for all
i\in[1,r]
. Define

(4.1)

q:=v^{+}-A_{r}x^{+}=v^{-}-A_{r}x^{-}.

Then
q>0
, since
A<0
. Thus
\mathrm{LCP}(A_{r},q)
has solutions with sign patterns
\begin{pmatrix}+\\
0\\
+\\
0\\
\vdots\end{pmatrix}
and
\begin{pmatrix}0\\
+\\
0\\
+\\
\vdots\end{pmatrix}
, a contradiction. Hence every contiguous square submatrix of
A
of size
r\in[2,k]
has the
sign non-reversal property on
\mathbb{R}^{r}_{\rm alt}
and the result follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
E
.

That
(1)\implies(2)
follows from Theorem
D
. To
show
(2)\implies(1)
, by Theorem
2.1
, it suffices to
show, for
r\in[1,k]
, that all contiguous minors of
A
of size

r
are negative. The proof is by induction on
r
. Let
r=1
and

A_{1}=(a_{ij})
for some
i\in[1,m]
and
j\in[1,n]
. Then

x^{A_{1}}=(-1)
by convention, and
q^{A_{1}}=-(a_{ij})
. If

a_{ij}=0
, then
\mathrm{LCP}(A_{1},q^{A_{1}})
has infinitely many
solutions, while if
a_{ij}>0
, then
\mathrm{LCP}(A_{1},q^{A_{1}})
has only one solution, a contradiction. Thus all entries of
A
are negative.

For the induction step, suppose
A_{r}
is an
r\times r
contiguous submatrix of
A
, with
r\in[2,k]
and all contiguous minors of
A
of size at most
r-1
are negative. By Theorem
2.1
, all proper minors of
A_{r}
are negative. Define

(4.2)

x^{A_{r}}:=(A^{11}_{r},0,A^{13}_{r},0,\ldots)^{T},\qquad z^{A_{r}}:=(0,A^{12}_{r},0,A^{14}_{r},\ldots)^{T},\qquad q^{A_{r}}:=A_{r}x^{A_{r}}.

Then
x^{A_{r}},~z^{A_{r}}\leq 0
and
q^{A_{r}}>0
.
Thus
\bf{0}
and
-x^{A_{r}}
are two solutions of

\mathrm{LCP}(A_{r},q^{A_{r}})
.

We next claim that
A_{r}
is invertible. If not, then
A_{r}x^{A_{r}}=A_{r}z^{A_{r}}
.
So
-z^{A_{r}}
is also a solution of
\mathrm{LCP}(A_{r},q^{A_{r}})
, a contradiction. This shows the claim.

Finally we show that
\det{A_{r}}<0
. Indeed, suppose
\det{A_{r}}>0
. Then

y=-A_{r}z^{A_{r}}+q^{A_{r}}=(\det{A_{r}})e^{1}\geq 0\hbox{~~~ and~~~}y^{T}z^{A_{r}}=0
.

Thus
-z^{A_{r}}
is also a solution of
\mathrm{LCP}(A_{r},q^{A_{r}})
, a contradiction by the hypothesis (2). Thus
\det{A_{r}}<0
. This completes the induction step and hence the proof.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

In Theorem
E
, in place of the vector
x^{A_{r}}=(A^{11}_{r},0,A^{13}_{r},0,\ldots)^{T}
, one can take
{x^{i}}^{A_{r}}:=(A^{i1}_{r},0,A^{i3}_{r},0,\ldots)^{T}
for odd

, or a positive linear combination of these vectors. The result still holds with a similar proof, where we define

similarly as in (

).

5.
Characterizations for totally non-positive matrices

This section presents the counterparts of the above main results for totally non-positive matrices as promised in the introduction. We first provide a characterization of totally non-positive matrices in terms of variation diminution. For this, we require a 1950 density result of Gantmacher–Krein for totally negative matrices (in fact strictly sign regular matrices).

Theorem 5.1
.

[
20
]

Given integers
m,n\geq k\geq 1
, the set of
m\times n
totally negative matrices of order
k
is dense in the set of
m\times n
totally non-positive matrices of order
k
.

Apart from Gantmacher–Krein’s density theorem, the proofs require another preliminary lemma on sign changes of limits of vectors; see e.g.
[
33
]
for the proof.

Lemma 5.2
.

Given
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, we
have

S^{+}(x)+S^{-}(\overline{x})=n-1,\qquad\text{where}\qquad\overline{x}:=(x_{1},-x_{2},x_{3},\ldots,(-1)^{n-1}x_{n})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Moreover, if
\lim\limits_{p\to\infty}x_{p}=x
, then

\liminf\limits_{p\to\infty}S^{-}(x_{p})\geq S^{-}(x),\qquad\limsup\limits_{p\to\infty}S^{+}(x_{p})\leq S^{+}(x).

Theorem 5.3
.

Given a real
m\times n
matrix
A\leq 0
and
m,n\geq 2
, the following statements are
equivalent:

(1)

A
is totally non-positive.

(2)

For all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\rm+,-}
,
S^{-}(Ax)\leq S^{-}(x)
. Moreover, if equality occurs and
Ax\neq 0
, then the first (last)
nonzero component of
Ax
has the same sign as the first (last)
nonzero component of
x
.

(3)

Let
k=\min\{m,n\}
. For every square submatrix
A_{r}
of
A
of size
r\in[2,k]
, define the vector

(5.1)

y^{A_{r}}:=\adj(A_{r})\alpha,\hbox{for some}~\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{r}_{\rm alt}.

Then
S^{-}(A_{r}y^{A_{r}})\leq S^{-}(y^{A_{r}})
. If equality occurs here, then the first (last)
nonzero component of
A_{r}y^{A_{r}}
has the same sign as the first (last)
nonzero component of
y^{A_{r}}
.

Proof.

We first show that
(1)\implies(2)
. Since
A
is totally non-positive, by Gantmacher–Krein’s density Theorem
5.1
, there exists a sequence
(A_{p})
of totally negative matrices such that

\lim\limits_{p\to\infty}A_{p}=A.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\rm+,-}
. By Theorem
A
and Lemma
5.2
, we have

S^{-}(Ax)\leq\liminf\limits_{p\to\infty}S^{-}(A_{p}x)\leq\liminf\limits_{p\to\infty}S^{+}(A_{p}x)\leq\liminf\limits_{p\to\infty}S^{-}(x)=S^{-}(x).

Next, assume that
S^{-}(Ax)=S^{-}(x)=k
and
Ax\neq 0
. Then for all sufficiently large
p
, by Theorem
A
and Lemma
5.2
,

S^{-}(Ax)\leq S^{-}(A_{p}x)\leq S^{+}(A_{p}x)\leq S^{-}(x)=k.

Thus
S^{-}(A_{p}x)=S^{+}(A_{p}x)=S^{-}(x)=k
for
p
large enough. This implies the sign patterns in
A_{p}x
(for large
p
) do not depend on the zero entries. Thus the nonzero sign patterns of
A_{p}x
agree with those of
Ax
. Also, by Theorem
A
, the sign patterns of
x
agree with the sign patterns of
A_{p}x
. Hence the same holds for the sign patterns of
Ax
and
x
.

Next, we show that
(3)\implies(1)
. The claim that
\det{A_{r}}\leq 0
for all
r\times r
submatrices
A_{r}
of
A
, is shown by induction on
r\in[1,k]
. The base case
r=1
holds by the hypothesis.

For the induction step,
A_{r}
is an
r\times r
submatrix of
A
and all proper minors of
A_{r}
are non-positive. If
\det{A_{r}}=0
then there is nothing to prove, so suppose
\det{A_{r}}\neq 0
. Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{r})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}_{\rm alt}
and define
y^{A_{r}}
as in (
5.1
). Then no row of
\adj(A_{r})
is zero and
y^{A_{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}_{\rm alt}
. Also,
y^{A_{r}}_{i}
is negative (or positive) if
\alpha_{i}
is positive (or negative) for all
i\in[1,r]
, by (
2.4
). Since
A_{r}y^{A_{r}}=(\det{A_{r}})\alpha
, we have

S^{-}(A_{r}y^{A_{r}})=S^{-}(y^{A_{r}})=r-1.

By the last part of the hypothesis (3),
\det{A_{r}}<0
and so we are done by induction.

Finally, to show
(2)\implies(3)
, repeat the proof of corresponding implication in Theorem
A
, now working with arbitrary invertible submatrices
A_{r}
of
A
of size
r\in[2,k]
and the vector
y^{A_{r}}=\adj(A_{r})\alpha
, where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{r}_{\rm alt}
is arbitrarily chosen.
∎

Analogous to Theorems
B
and
C
, we next derive the sign non-reversal property and interval hull tests for totally non-positive matrices.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
m,n\geq k\geq 2
be integers. Given
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with
A\leq 0
, the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

The matrix
A
is totally non-positive of order
k
.

(2)

Every square submatrix of
A
of size
r\in[2,k]
has the non-strict
sign non-reversal property on
\mathbb{R}^{r}_{\rm+,-}
.

(3)

Every square submatrix of
A
of size
r\in[2,k]
has the non-strict
sign non-reversal property on
\mathbb{R}^{r}_{\rm alt}
.

(4)

For every
r\in[2,k]
and
r\times r
submatrix
A_{r}

of
A
, the matrix
A_{r}
has the non-strict sign non-reversal property for the vector
x^{A_{r}}=\adj(A_{r})\alpha
, for any choice of
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{r}_{\rm alt}
.

Proof.

First suppose
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is totally non-positive of order
k
. By
Theorem
5.1
, there exists a sequence
A^{(p)}
of totally negative matrices of order
k
such that
A^{(p)}\to A
entrywise. Fix an
r\times r
submatrix
A_{r}
of
A
for
r\in[2,k]
, and let

This completes the proof of
(1)\implies(2)
.

Next, that
(2)\implies(3)
is immediate. Now
assume (4) holds. To prove
(1)
, we show by induction on
r\in[1,k]
that the determinant of every

submatrix
of

is non-positive. The base case

follows from the hypothesis. For the induction step, suppose
A_{r}
is a square submatrix of
A
of size
r\in[2,k]
and all the proper minors of

are non-positive. If
\det A_{r}=0
then we are done; else
\det(A_{r})\neq 0
. Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}_{\rm alt}
and define the vector

is zero, by (
