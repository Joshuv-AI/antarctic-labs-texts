Addditivity of Derived Limits in the Cohen model Volume: Volume VOL, Number 0NUM, MONTH YEAR
Nathaniel Bannister

Abstract
. We show that, in the model constructed by adding sufficiently many Cohen reals, derived limits are additive on a large class of systems. This generalizes the work of Jeffrey Bergfalk, Michael Hrušák, and Chris Lambie-Hanson which focuses on the system 𝐀 \mathbf{A} . In the process, we isolate a partition principle responsible for the vanishing of derived limits on collections of Cohen reals and reframe the propagating trivializations results of Bergfalk, Hrušák and Lambie-Hanson as a theorem of ZFC. In light of results of the author, Jeffrey Bergfalk, and Justin Moore, the additivity of derived limits also implies additivity results for strong homology.

The author would also like to thank the anonymous referee for helpful comments and suggestions.

1.
Introduction

Strong homology was originally defined by Mardešić and offers a correction to the theory of Čech homology, which fails to satisfy the exactness axiom.
A comprehensive reference for strong homology is
[
15
]
.
Motivated by questions coming from strong homology, in particular, whether it is
additive
in the sense that it maps disjoint sums of spaces to direct sums of homology groups, Mardešić and Prasolov consider in
[
16
]
the inverse system
\mathbf{A}
of abelian groups.

\mathbf{A}
is indexed over
\omega^{\omega}
(the set of all functions from the natural numbers to the natural numbers) with respect to the ordering
x\leq y
if and only if for all
n
,
x(n)\leq y(n)
; for each index
x
the group
\mathbf{A}_{x}
is defined by

\mathbf{A}_{x}=\bigoplus_{i<\omega}\mathbb{Z}^{x(i)}

and if
x\leq y
the map from
\mathbf{A}_{y}
to
\mathbf{A}_{x}
is the canonical projection map.
The system
\mathbf{A}
appears naturally in the computation of the strong homology of a countable disjoint sum of Hawaiian earrings, and of higher dimensional variants of the Hawaiian earrings.
Mardešić and Prasolov show that strong homology is additive on a countable disjoint sum of Hawaiian earrings if and only if the derived limits
\lim^{n}\mathbf{A}
are
0
for every
n>0
.
Moreover, they connect the vanishing of
\lim^{1}\mathbf{A}
to a set theoretic question of the form “is every coherent family trivial?” and show that, in the presence of the Continuum Hypothesis,
\lim^{1}\mathbf{A}\neq 0
.
Several years later, Dow, Simon, and Vaughan show in
[
8
]
that
\lim^{1}\mathbf{A}=0
in the presence of the Proper Forcing Axiom, a set theoretic assumption widely believed to have the consistency strength of a supercompact cardinal.
In
[
20
, Theorem 8.7]
, Todorčević reduces the hypothesis to the Open Graph Axiom (also referred to as , a consequence of the Proper Forcing Axiom which has no large cardinal strength.

Prasolov proves in
[
17
]
that in ZFC set theory strong homology is not additive on the class of all topological spaces.
The example involves a countable disjoint sum of copies of a Hawaiian earring with uncountably many circles, and nonadditivity is established by showing that the first derived limit of a “tall” version of
\mathbf{A}
is nonzero.
Prasolov’s result still leaves the question of whether there can be a rich class of spaces on which strong homology is additive - the spaces used in the example are not metrizable, nor even first countable.

In recent years, attention has returned to the derived limits of
\mathbf{A}
as well as to the motivating question of the classes of spaces on which strong homology can consistently be additive.

•

Bergfalk shows in
[
4
]
that the Proper Forcing Axiom implies that
\lim^{2}\mathbf{A}\neq 0
; in particular, the Proper Forcing Axiom still implies a failure of additivity for strong homology even for the class of closed subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

•

In a recent paper, Veličković and Vignati (
[
22
]
) prove that for any
n
, it is consistent relative to ZFC that
\lim^{n}\mathbf{A}
is nonzero.

•

In
[
5
]
, Bergfalk and Lambie-Hanson show that consistently from a mild large cardinal hypothesis,
\lim^{n}\mathbf{A}=0
for all
n>0
.
As a corollary, in their model strong homology is additive on a countable sum of Hawaiian earrings.

•

The author, joint with Bergfalk and Moore, shows in
[
2
]
that in the model of
[
5
]
, strong homology is additive on the class of locally compact separable metric spaces.
This result involves generalizing the definition of the system
\mathbf{A}
to a class of inverse systems of abelian groups known as “
\Omega
systems,” showing that additivity of strong homology for locally compact separable metric spaces is equivalent to the additivity of derived limits of
\Omega
systems, and finally showing that additivity of derived limits for
\Omega
systems holds in the model of
[
5
]
.

•

In
[
6
]
, Bergfalk, Hrušák, and Lambie-Hanson remove the large cardinal hypothesis for the vanishing of the derived limits of
\mathbf{A}
.

The main theorem of this paper is Theorem
1.2
that in the same model used by Bergfalk, Hrušák, and Lambie-Hanson, derived limits are additive for the class of all
\Omega
systems; in light of
[
2
, Theorem 6]
, strong homology is additive on the class of locally compact separable metric spaces.
The main corollary is the following:

Corollary 1.1
.

It is consistent relative to ZFC that strong homology is additive on the class of locally compact separable metric spaces.

The key new ingredient in the proof is to analyze the injective objects in the abelian category of
\Omega
systems, which we call the
injective systems
; additional properties we will prove about injective systems will allow us to mimic the proofs in
[
6
]
.
The class of injective systems is rich enough that vanishing of higher derived limits on injective systems implies additivity of derived limits for all
\Omega
systems.

The techniques involved to produce these results naturally apply to a broader class of systems, which we call “
\Omega_{\kappa}
systems.”
For infinite cardinals
\kappa
, the
\Omega_{\kappa}
systems are a “wide” version of
\Omega
systems, where we now allow for
\kappa
many towers.
The associated objects include the system called
\mathbf{A}_{\kappa}
in
[
4
]
, the natural variation of
\mathbf{A}
indexed over
\omega^{\kappa}
.
The main theorem we will prove is the following:

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose that
\chi\geq\beth_{\omega}(\kappa)
.
Then
\operatorname{Add}(\omega,\chi)
, the forcing to add
\chi
many Cohen reals, forces that whenever
\mathcal{G}
is an
\Omega_{\kappa}
system, the canonical map

\bigoplus_{\alpha<\kappa}\lim{}^{n}\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}\to\lim{}^{n}G

is an isomorphism.

Additivity of derived limits for
\Omega_{\kappa}
systems holds implications for strong homology as well.
For example, we show in section
3.1
the following generalization of
[
2
, Theorem 6]
:

Theorem 1.3
.

The following are equivalent:

(1)

whenever
X
is a locally compact metric space of weight at most
\kappa

\overline{H}_{p}(X)\cong\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}K\subseteq X\\
K\text{ compact}\end{subarray}}{\colim}\overline{H}_{p}(K),

is strong homology (recall that the weight of a topological space is the minimum cardinality of a basis).

on the class of locally compact separable metric spaces.

(2)

\kappa
.

(3)

whenever
\langle X_{i}\mid i<\kappa\rangle
are compact metric spaces, the natural map

\bigoplus_{i<\kappa}\overline{H}_{p}(X_{i})\to\overline{H}_{p}\left(\coprod_{i<\kappa}X_{i}\right)

The key corollary of our results is then

Corollary 1.4
.

Suppose that
\chi\geq\beth_{\omega}(\kappa)

The limit of the second tower is

, and the limit of the fourth tower is the additive group of 2-adic integers

but the map from
\mathbb{Z}
to

induced by the maps from the third tower to the fourth tower is not onto, so the inverse limit functor
\lim
does not preserve exact sequences.
However,
\lim
does turn out to be

, meaning that if

is a short exact sequence of inverse systems then

is an exact sequence of abelian groups.
Left exactness of
\lim
follows from the fact that
\lim
is the right adjoint of the functor which assigns to a group
G
the system where all groups are

and all maps are the identity; see for instance,
[

.
In a scenario with a left exact functor (starting with a category with the technical assertion that there are
enough injective objects
), the

are an attempt to measure the failure of exactness and to recover exactness.
The derived functors of
\lim
are a canonical sequence of functors

which result in a long exact sequence of the form

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lim A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lim B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lim C}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{lim}^{1}A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{lim}^{1}B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{lim}^{1}C}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{lim}^{2}A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{lim}^{2}B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{lim}^{2}C}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ldots}

for any short exact sequence as above.

Two categories of inverse systems are of particular interest.
For a fixed poset
\mathbb{P}
, the category
\operatorname{Ab}^{\mathbb{P}}
consists of inverse systems of abelian groups indexed over
\mathbb{P}
and a morphism from
\mathbf{G}
to
\mathbf{H}
consists of a family of group homomorphisms from

to

for each

commuting with the structure maps of
\mathbf{G}
and
\mathbf{H}
.
That is, the objects of
\operatorname{Ab}^{\mathbb{P}}
are functors from
\mathbb{P}^{\operatorname{op}}
to
\operatorname{Ab}
with morphisms given by natural transformations.
The second category of interest is the category pro-Ab, whose objects are inverse systems indexed over directed posets and whose morphisms can be (tersely) given by, for

a system indexed by
\mathbb{P}
and

a system indexed by

The category pro-Ab is abelian and has enough injective objects (see, for instance,
[

, Theorem 15.8 and Theorem 15.15]
).
Moreover, the functor

is a left exact functor on this category and therefore admits derived functors

as defined on pro-Ab.
We note that by
[

, Theorem 15.14]

is directed then the derived functors of

as computed in

\Omega_{\kappa}

The main goal of this section is to outline how to generalize the proof of

We now outline the proof of Theorem

Proof.

The proofs of
(\ref{cs})\implies(\ref{add_lc})
and of
(\ref{add_c})\iff(\ref{add_om})
can be taken directly from directions

and

of the proof of
[

For the direction

is a locally compact metric space of weight at most
\kappa

By Fact

, we may fix locally compact separable metric spaces
\langle X_{i}\mid i<\kappa\rangle
such that

For each

, fix an increasing sequence of open sets

such that

is compact and

Let

Claim 3.4
.

For each

\overline{H}_{p}(X)\cong\underset{j<\omega}{\colim}\overline{H}_{p}(\overline{U}_{j})\cong\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}K\subseteq X\\
K\text{ compact}\end{subarray}}{\colim}\overline{H}_{p}(K).

in the proof of

For the second isomorphism, by

3.2.
Coherence and Triviality

We now describe the general machinery by which we will prove additivity of derived limits.
We will generally make use of the following cochain complex, whose cohomology gives the derived limits of an inverse system indexed over a subset of
\omega^{\kappa}
; an identical characterization applies to inverse systems indexed over any lower semi-lattice.

Definition 3.5
.

Suppose that
X\subseteq\omega^{\kappa}
and
\mathbf{G}
is an inverse system of abelian groups indexed over
\omega^{\kappa}
.
We say
\Phi\in\prod_{(x_{0},...,x_{n})\in X^{n+1}}\mathbf{G}_{\bigwedge x_{i}}
is
alternating
if for every permutation
\sigma\in S_{n+1}
and every
\mathbf{x}\in X^{n+1}
,
\Phi_{\mathbf{x}}=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\Phi_{\sigma(\mathbf{x})}
where
\sigma
acts by permuting indices.
The
alternating cochain complex

K^{\bullet}(\mathbf{G}\upharpoonright X)
is given by

K^{n}(\mathbf{G}\upharpoonright X)=\left\{\Phi\in\prod_{(x_{0},...,x_{n})\in X^{n+1}}\mathbf{G}_{\bigwedge x_{i}}\,\middle|\,\Phi\text{ is alternating}\right\}

with
d\colon K^{n}\to K^{n+1}
given by

d\Phi(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i}(-1)^{i}p_{\bigwedge{\mathbf{x}^{i}},\bigwedge{\mathbf{x}}}\Phi(\mathbf{x}^{i}).

In practice,
\mathbf{G}
can be any of
G,\overline{G}
, or
\overline{G}/G
from an
\Omega_{\kappa}
system as in Definition
3.1
.

Note that we do not require
\bigwedge x_{i}
to be a member of
X
in the above definition.
It is readily verified that
d^{n+1}d^{n}=0
for any
n<\omega
; our main object of interest will be the cohomology groups
H^{n}(K^{\bullet}(\mathbf{G}\upharpoonright X))
.

In general,
H^{n}(K^{\bullet}(\mathbf{G}\upharpoonright X))

does not
correspond to
\lim^{n}(\mathbf{G}\upharpoonright X)
, the derived limits of the system indexed by those elements of
\omega^{\kappa}
which are in
X
.
For an extreme example, if
X
consists of two incompatible points
x,y
then
H^{0}(K^{\bullet}(\mathbf{G}\upharpoonright X))
can be identified with all elements of
G_{x}\times G_{y}
which project to the same element of
G_{x\wedge y}
whereas
\lim^{0}(\mathbf{G}\upharpoonright X)
is all of
G_{x}\times G_{y}
.
Nevertheless,
H^{n}(K^{\bullet}(\mathbf{G}\upharpoonright X))
does have a characterization in terms of derived limits.
We first cite a theorem due to Leray (see, for instance,
[
11
, Exercise III.4.11]
):

Fact 3.6
.

Let
Y
be a topological space,
\mathscr{F}
a sheaf on
Y
and
\mathscr{U}=\left\{U_{i}\right\}_{i\in I}
an open cover of
Y
. If the sheaf cohomology group
\mathrm{H}^{p}(V,\mathscr{F})=0
for all
p>0
and all
V=U_{i_{0}}\cap\ldots U_{i_{n}},i_{j}\in I
then the Čech cohomology group
\check{\mathrm{H}}^{p}(\mathscr{U},\mathscr{F})
coincides with the sheaf cohomology group
\mathrm{H}^{p}(Y,\mathscr{F})
for all
p
.

The characterization of
H^{n}(K^{\bullet}(\mathbf{G}\upharpoonright X))
is the following:

Proposition 3.7
.

H^{n}(K^{\bullet}(\mathbf{G}\upharpoonright X))\cong\lim^{n}(\mathbf{G}\upharpoonright Y)
where
Y
is the downwards closure of
X
with respect to the coordinatewise domination ordering.

Proof.

An observation in
[
12
, page 4]
shows that
\lim^{n}(\mathbf{G}\upharpoonright Y)
is naturally isomorphic to the sheaf cohomology
H^{n}(Y,\mathcal{F})
, where the topology on
Y
is generated by the sets
U_{x}=\{y\mid y\leq x\}
for
x\in Y
and
\mathcal{F}
is the sheaf
U\mapsto\lim(\mathbf{G}\upharpoonright U)
.
We now consider the cover
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{x}\mid x\in X\}
; observe that these sets cover
Y
and that
U_{x_{1}}\cap\ldots\cap U_{x_{n}}=U_{x_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge x_{n}}
.
Since
U_{x_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge x_{n}}
has a maximal element,
\lim^{s}(\mathbf{G}\upharpoonright U_{x_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge x_{n}})=0
for all
s>0
.
By Fact
3.6
, the
n
th Čech cohomology of
\mathcal{U}
is
\lim^{n}(\mathbf{G}\upharpoonright Y)
.
Since Čech cohomology can be computed using alternating chains by
[
18
, I.20 Proposition 2]
, the proposition follows.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

The downwards closure of

in Proposition
3.7
can be replaced with any

•

.

•

4.
Propagating Trivializations for Injective Systems

In this section, we demonstrate how to propagate trivializations of a coherent family for an injective system on a set containing sufficiently many Cohen sequences to a trivialization on the whole domain.
In section 6, we will define a sequence of cardinals
\langle\lambda_{n}\mid n<\omega\rangle
and show that any
n
-coherent family indexed by a set of
\lambda_{n}
many Cohen sequences is trivial once restricted to some set of
\lambda_{n-1}
many Cohen sequences.
The main goal of this section is to demonstrate that in the presence of a trivialization in such a large set of the sequences implies that the original
n

The results of this section are a generalization of the results of
[
6
, Section 4]
, where trivializations of coherent families for the system
\mathbf{A}
propagated in a very similar manner.
The main difference in this section is that the arguments are for the class of injective
\Omega_{\kappa}
systems; Lemma
3.27
will ultimately allow us to connect coherent families being trivial to additivity of derived limits for all systems.
Despite arguing for the larger class of injective
\Omega_{\kappa}
systems, the argument in this section is extremely similar to the arguments of
[
6
, Section 4]
for the system
\mathbf{A}

We also restate the result as a theorem of ZFC, rather than about coherent families indexed over sets of Cohen sequences as was done in

\displaystyle\sum_{i<s+1}(-1)^{i}e\left(\mathbf{x}^{i},F_{\alpha}({\mathbf{x}})\right)-\sum_{i<s+1}(-1)^{i}\left[\mathcal{C}_{s-1}^{\alpha}\left(\mathbf{x}^{i}\right)*F_{\alpha}({\mathbf{x}})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i<s+1}(-1)^{i}e\left(\mathbf{x}^{i},F_{\alpha}({\mathbf{x}})\right)-

\displaystyle\sum_{i<s+1}(-1)^{i}\left[e\left(\mathbf{x}^{i},F_{\alpha}({\mathbf{x}})\right)-\sum_{j<s}(-1)^{j}\mathcal{A}_{s-1}^{\alpha}\left(\left(\mathbf{x}^{i}\right)^{j}\right)*F_{\alpha}({\mathbf{x}})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i<s+1}\sum_{j<s}(-1)^{i+j}\mathcal{A}_{s-1}^{\alpha}\left(\left(\mathbf{x}^{i}\right)^{j}\right)*F_{\alpha}({\mathbf{x}}).

The key observation is that for any term with
i\leq j
, the corresponding term with
i^{\prime}=j+1
and
j^{\prime}=i
is the same but with opposite sign, thus showing that

\sum_{i<s+1}\sum_{j<s}(-1)^{i+j}\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}_{s-1}\left(\left(\mathbf{x}^{i}\right)^{j}\right)*F_{\alpha}({\mathbf{x}})=0.

∎

As stated above, set
\Psi_{\mathbf{x}}(\alpha)=E_{\Phi}^{\alpha}(A_{n}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}))
for
\alpha\not\in S^{*}
and
\mathbf{x}\in A^{n}
.
If
\alpha\not\in S^{*}
then for each
\mathbf{y}\in A^{n+1}
,

\displaystyle d\Psi_{\mathbf{y}}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}\Psi_{\mathbf{y}^{i}}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}E_{\Phi}^{\alpha}(A_{n}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{y}^{i}))

\displaystyle=E^{\alpha}_{\Phi}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}A_{n}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{y}^{i})\right)

\displaystyle=E^{\alpha}_{\Phi}(e(\mathbf{y})-C^{\alpha}_{n+1}(\mathbf{y}))

\displaystyle=E^{\alpha}_{\Phi}(e(\mathbf{y}))-E^{\alpha}_{\Phi}(C^{\alpha}_{n+1}(\mathbf{y}))

\displaystyle=d\Phi_{\mathbf{y}}(\alpha)-E^{\alpha}_{\Phi}(C^{\alpha}_{n+1}(\mathbf{y}))

where the first line is the definition of
d
, the second line is the definition of
\Psi
, the third and fifth are that
E^{\alpha}_{\Phi}
is a homomorphism, the fourth is the definition of
C^{\alpha}_{n+1}
, and the last line is the definition of
E^{\alpha}_{\Phi}
.
By Claim
6.3
,
C^{\alpha}_{n+1}(\mathbf{y})
is of the form
(-1)^{s+1}S_{n+1}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{y})
plus terms of the form
e(F^{*}_{\alpha}(\vec{\sigma}))
for
\vec{\sigma}\in\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{y})^{{}^{[\![n+1]\!]}}
.
Note that
E_{\Phi}^{\alpha}(S_{n+1}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{y}))=d^{2}\Psi_{\mathbf{y}^{\frown}F_{\alpha}(\mathbf{y})}(\alpha)=0
.
Moreover, since
c_{\Phi}\circ F_{\alpha}^{*}
is constant with value
T
and
\alpha\not\in T
, whenever
\vec{\sigma}\in\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{y})^{{}^{[\![n+1]\!]}}
, we have
E^{\alpha}_{\Phi}(e(F^{*}_{\alpha}(\vec{\sigma})))(\alpha)=0
.
In particular, for
\alpha\not\in S^{*}
,
d\Phi_{\mathbf{y}}=d\Psi_{\mathbf{y}}
.

If we can show that each
\Psi_{\mathbf{x}}
is finitely supported, we will complete the proof as

\Psi_{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}(\alpha)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\Psi_{\mathbf{x}}(\alpha)&\alpha\not\in S^{*}\\
\Phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\alpha)&\alpha\in S^{*}\end{array}\right.

will be a type II trivialization of
\Phi
.
It follows from an easy induction that
A_{s}^{\alpha}(\rho)
is always a sum of terms of the form
e(a_{0}\cup\{F_{\alpha}(a_{i})\mid 1\leq i\leq m\})\}
for
\vec{a}
a string on
[\mathbf{x}]^{\leq n}
.
Since there are only finitely many such strings and there are only finitely many values of
c_{\Phi}
for each string by choice of the
F_{\alpha}
,
\Psi_{\mathbf{x}}
will have the desired finite support.
∎

6.2.
Trivialization Everywhere

We will now complete the proof of our main results.
Let
\lambda_{0}=\beth_{1}(\kappa)^{+}
and
\lambda_{n+1}=\sigma(\lambda_{n}^{+},2n+1)
; observe that
\sup_{n}(\lambda_{n})=\beth_{\omega}(\kappa)
and each
\lambda_{n}
is
<\kappa
inaccessible.

Lemma 6.4
.

Suppose that
\chi\geq\beth_{\omega}(\kappa)
and let
\mathbb{P}=\operatorname{Fn}(\chi\times\kappa,\omega)
.
The following holds in
V^{\mathbb{P}}
:
Suppose that
\mathcal{I}
is an injective
\Omega_{\kappa}
system and
\Phi
is an
n
-coherent family corresponding to
\mathcal{I}
indexed by a set
X\subseteq\omega^{\kappa}
containing at least
\lambda_{n}
many of the Cohen sequences added by
\mathbb{P}
.
Then
\Phi
is
n
-trivial.

Proof.

The proof proceeds by induction on
n
.
By Lemma
6.2
, there is an
A\subseteq X
containing at least
\lambda_{n-1}
many Cohen sequences such that
\Phi\upharpoonright A
is
n
-trivial.
By Lemma
4.4
, the inductive hypothesis, and Proposition
4.5
,
\Phi
is
n
-trivial.
∎

We obtain Theorem
1.2
as an immediate corollary.

7.
Conclusion

We conclude by noting several questions related to the work here.
One conspicuous example is the following.

Question 7.1
.

Is it consistent that derived limits of
\Omega_{\kappa}
systems are additive for every
\kappa
?

The answer is yes in the case of
\lim^{1}\mathbf{A}_{\kappa}
, as shown in
[
4
, Theorem 5.1]
; in fact, it is implied by
\lim^{1}\mathbf{A}=0
.
We may ask whether this holds for higher values of
n
.

Question 7.2
.

Does
\lim^{n}\mathbf{A}=0
imply
\lim^{n}\mathbf{A}_{\kappa}=0
for every cardinal
\kappa
?

The author has been informed through personal communication of a forthcoming negative solution by Jeffrey Bergfalk and Matteo Casarosa to Question
7.2
for all
n>0
and consequently to Questions
7.4
and
7.5
.

Worth noting is that the forcings involved in this paper necessarily increase the size of the continuum; in particular, the case of
\kappa=2^{\aleph_{0}}
is unclear.

Question 7.3
.

Is it consistent that
\lim^{n}\mathbf{A}_{2^{\aleph_{0}}}=0
for all
n>0
?

There are two questions about
\Omega_{\kappa}
systems which serve as extension from the questions about
\mathbf{A}_{\kappa}
to all
\Omega_{\kappa}
systems.
The first is on additivity.

Question 7.4
.

Does additivity of
\lim^{n}
for
\Omega_{\omega}
systems imply additivity of
\Omega_{\kappa}
systems for all uncountable
\kappa
?

The second is about coherent families.

Question 7.5
.

Suppose that every
n
-coherent family is
n
-trivial for all
\Omega_{\omega}
systems.
Must the same hold for all uncountable
\kappa
?

At the moment, we believe the proof of
[
4
, Theorem 5.1]
will generalize to show the answer to Question
7.5
is yes if
n=1
.

We may also ask about how large the continuum must be for additivity of derived limits, both for
\mathbf{A}
and for arbitrary
\Omega_{\omega}
systems.

Question 7.6
.

What is the minimum value of the continuum compatible with
\lim^{n}\mathbf{A}=0
for every
n>0
?

Question 7.7
.

What is the minimum value of the continuum compatible with additivity of derived limits for
\Omega_{\omega}
systems?

Note that if
GCH
holds in the ground model, then after forcing with
\operatorname{Add}(\omega,\beth_{\omega})
the continuum will be of size
\aleph_{\omega+1}
, giving an upper bound.
In light of the results of
[
22
]
and
[
7
]
, of particular interest is when
\mathfrak{b}<\mathfrak{d}
and there are no unbounded
\mathfrak{d}
chains, for instance in the Mitchell and Miller models.
A related question, the answer to which is yes when
n=1
, is the following.

Question 7.8
.

Suppose
X
is a
<^{*}
-increasing
\omega_{n}
sequence. Is
\lim^{n}\mathbf{A}\upharpoonright X\neq 0
?

A theorem from the theory of Hausdorff gaps implies the answer is yes when
n=1
; see
[
3
, 96-98]
; a special case when
X
is an
\aleph_{1}
scale appears as
[
8
, Theorem 2.4]
.
Question
7.8
is likely very difficult and would open many doors.
A positive answer would remove the requirement of weak
\diamondsuit
sequences from
[
22
]
since if
\mathfrak{b}=\mathfrak{d}=\kappa
then there is a
<^{*}
-increasing
\omega_{n}
sequence which is
<^{*}
cofinal in
\omega^{\omega}
; a nontrivial
n
-coherent family defined along such a scale induces a nontrivial coherent family on all of
\omega^{\omega}
.
It seems plausible that a positive answer would also remove the requirement of weak
\diamondsuit
from
[
7
]
as well, though the
<^{*}
-increasing
\omega_{n}
sequence defined there is (importantly) not
<^{*}
-cofinal.

Finally, we conclude by asking how far our results on additivity of strong homology can be extended.

Question 7.9
.

On what class of spaces can strong homology be additive and have compact supports?

The classes of Polish spaces and
\sigma
-compact Polish spaces are of particular interest.
