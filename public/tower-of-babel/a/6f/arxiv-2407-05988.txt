Minimum-regret hydrogen supply chain strategies to foster the energy transition of European hard-to-abate industries
Alissa Ganter, Paolo Gabrielli, Hanne Goericke, Giovanni Sansavini

Abstract
Low-carbon hydrogen ( H 2 \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} ) is envisioned to play a central role in decarbonizing European hard-to-abate industries, such as refineries, ammonia, methanol, steel, and cement. To enable its widespread use and support the transition, low-carbon H 2 \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} supply chain (HSC) infrastructure is required. Mature and economically viable low-carbon H 2 \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} production pathways include steam methane reforming of natural gas coupled with carbon dioxide ( CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} ) capture and storage, water-electrolysis from renewable electricity, biomethane reforming, and biomass gasification. However, uncertainties surrounding demand and feedstock availabilities hamper their proliferation. Therefore, this work investigates the impact of uncertainty in future H 2 \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} demands and biomass availability on the HSC design. The HSC is modeled as a network of H 2 \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} production and consumption sites that are interconnected by H 2 \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} and biomass transport technologies. CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} capture, transport, and storage infrastructure is modeled alongside the HSCs. We determine the cost-optimal low-carbon HSC design by solving a linear optimization model, considering a regional spatial resolution and a multi-year time horizon from 2022 to 2050. We adopt a scenario-based uncertainty quantification approach and define discrete scenarios with varying H 2 \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} demands and biomass availability. Applying a minimum-regret strategy, we show that planning for sufficiently large low-carbon H 2 \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} production capacities (about 9.6 Mt/a by 2030) is essential to flexibly scale up HSCs and accommodate larger H 2 \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} demands of up to 35 Mt/a by 2050. Although biomass-based hydrogen production technologies are identified as the most cost-efficient low-carbon hydrogen production technologies, investments are not recommended unless the availability of biomass feedstocks is guaranteed. In this context, investments in SMR-CCS and electrolyzers often offer greater flexibility. Furthermore, we highlight the importance of CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} capture, transport, and storage infrastructure in the transition, which is required across scenarios. In particular the availability of CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} removal technologies determine the ability to realize the 2050 net-zero emissions target.

System description

Feedstocks and energy sources.

The considered feedstocks and energy sources are natural gas, electricity, and biomass. We assume that natural gas and grid electricity are available at each node. To account for the uncertainty in biomass availability, we define three discrete scenarios: a reference scenario, a scenario with reduced biomass availability to account for competition with other sectors, and a scenario where no biomass is available for low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production. In the reference scenario, the estimates from
[
65
]
are used. In the scenario with reduced biomass availability, we assume that the sectoral biomass consumption is proportional to the sectoral primary energy demands. According to the EU reference scenario, 26 % of the total energy consumption can be attributed to industry. Thus, in the reduced biomass scenario, only 26 % of the sustainable biomass potential is available for low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production.

2.4
Optimization model

The optimal design of the HSC is determined via a linear optimization problem following
[
49
]
. In general form, a linear optimization problem can be formulated as follows:

\begin{split}\min_{\mathbf{x}}&\quad\mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{x}\\
\text{s.t.}&\quad\mathbf{Ax}\leq\mathbf{b}\\
&\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\end{split}

(1)

where the objective function is expressed as a linear combination of continuous decision variables
\mathbf{x}
with dimension
N
and coefficients
\mathbf{c}
; and the constraints are expressed as a linear combination of matrix
\mathbf{A}
, decision variables
\mathbf{x}
, and vector
\mathbf{b}
.

The optimization problem is implemented in the optimization framework ZEN-garden (Zero-emissions Energy Networks), developed at the Reliability and Risk Engineering Lab at ETH Zurich. ZEN-garden optimizes the design and operation of energy system models to investigate transition pathways toward decarbonization
[
49
,
68
,
69
]
. The optimization problem is solved using the commercial solver Gurobi
[
70
]
.

Input Data

The input data to the optimization problem includes (i) spatially-resolved
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demands, carrier prices,
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
intensities, and availabilities of biomass, wind, and solar energy, (ii) the techno-economic parameters describing the cost and performance of production, conditioning, and transport technologies, (iii) the existing
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production capacities, (iv) the size and location of the available
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
storage sites, and (v) the target decarbonization pathway. A yearly resolution is used to model the time-dependent variables, namely the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demands, carrier prices,
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
intensity of the electricity grid, and biomass availability. The input data is reported in
Section S1
.

Decision Variables

The optimization problem determines (i) the optimal selection, capacity, and location of the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production, conditioning, and transport technologies, (ii) the energy inputs and outputs of each
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production and conditioning technology, (iii) the carrier flows through each transport technology, (iv) the nodal carrier imports and exports.

Constraints

The constraints of the optimization problem include (i) the nodal mass balances for electricity, natural gas,
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, biomass, and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, (ii) the performance and operating limits of the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production, conditioning, and transport technologies, and (iii) the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emissions constraint limiting the yearly
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emissions to the target emissions values of the selected decarbonization pathway. Here, we assume linearly decreasing
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emissions from today’s values such that the 2050 net-zero emissions (NZE) target is achieved
[
71
]
. To ensure the feasibility of the optimization problem, a slack variable is added to the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emission constraint. This slack variable can be interpreted as a
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emissions overshoot of the emission target and is associated with a large cost (100k€/
t), ensuring that overshooting the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emission constraint is selected as a last resort. The HSC design is considered a feasible solution if the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emissions target is achieved and the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emissions overshoot is zero.

The constraints are detailed in
[
49
]
. Here, we expand the model formulation from
[
49
]
by adding technology expansion constraints when performing the out-of-sample approach (
Fig.

1
). The technology expansion constraints limit the maximum annual growth rate of a technology based on the existing capacity stock and are formulated following
[
47
,
69
]
and described in
Section S3
. The technology expansion is parameterized based on historically observed growth rates.
[
69
]
investigate the historical annual growth rates for low-carbon technologies and observe growth rates between 10 % (wind offshore) to 29 % (solar PV). For our reference case, we select a technology expansion rate of 20 %. A sensitivity analysis is performed for technology expansion rates varying from 10 % to 29 %.

Objective function

The optimization problem minimizes the net present cost of the system, which includes the investment and operating costs of the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
,
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, and biomass supply chains in compliance with the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emission targets.

2.5
Minimum-regret solution

The minimum regret solution is the HSC scenario design that (i) results in the lowest levelized cost of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
(LCOH) for the worst-case out-of-sample scenario (i.e., the min-max across the LCOH of the out-of-sample scenarios), and (ii) is feasible for all scenarios. The LCOH is computed as the net present costs of the supply chains divided by the net present
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production from 2022 to 2050; and feasibility is defined as the ability to meet the annual
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emissions targets, i.e. the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emission overshoot is zero.

Biomass-based

Furthermore, the coupling of biomass-based

\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

demands, which require between 10-56 Mt/a
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

Without biomass feedstock, low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

emissions, but lower cost compared to electrolysis. Electrolyzer capacities are continuously expanded in scenarios where
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demand is expected to increase.

Independently of the biomass availability, electrolyzers do not play a role in min-low
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demands and are only deployed if medium-max demands are expected. Two factors contribute to the increasing deployment of electrolyzers. First, the unit cost of electrolyzers is expected to decrease by 60 % until 2050, making electrolyzers more cost-competitive. Second, the residual emissions of electrolyzers are 4-10 times lower compared to SMR-CCS (0.6-1.6 ton
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
eq.

\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
eq.

\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

The literature report a wide range of electrolyzer cost estimates. However, even when assuming a very optimistic cost evolution for electrolyzers, where the investment costs are 10 % lower in 2022 and 30 % lower in 2050 with respect to the reference case, electrolyzer capacities remain small and are only deployed at a larger scale in high and max
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

. The number of infeasible scenarios is lower for more conservative system designs, i.e., systems designed without biomass and for medium to max
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

S2
). Nevertheless, the investment strategies remain robust for low and high technology expansion rates, and designing HSCs for medium
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

3.4
Minimum-regret solution

The minimum-regret solution is identified based on two criteria: (1) the solution results in the lowest LCOH in the worst scenario (min-max cost criteria), while (2) meeting the annual
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emissions targets (feasibility criteria).
Overall, only three out of 15 design scenario designs meet the feasibility criteria of complying with the annual
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emissions target at all times and across all out-of-sample scenarios; these are the no biomass scenarios for medium to max
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demands (
Fig.

6
). Out of these three scenarios, the designs for medium
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demands and no biomass is identified as the minimum-regret solution as it results in the lowest LCOH in the worst case (
Section

2.5
). The minimum-regret solution remains robust for different technology expansion rates (
Fig.

S2
).
In the minimum-regret scenario, about 63 % of the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demand in 2050 is met via SMR-CCS, and about 34 % via electrolysis from renewable electricity. Depending on the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demand of the out-of-sample scenario, the electrolyzer capacity must be expanded by up to 22 Mt/a.
While planning for medium demands and reference biomass availability leads to similar LCOH across scenarios, the net-zero emission target for medium-max
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demands is likely not achieved by 2050 if less biomass is available than expected. Therefore, designing HSCs for medium
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demands and without the availability of biomass feedstocks is identified as the minimum-regret strategy (
Section

3.4
).

A medium-sized
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
transport infrastructure is built (3 Mt/a and 172 Mt/a, respectively,
Fig.

).
In particular, the regions of Belgium, Netherlands, and the west of Germany are well connected by
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
transport infrastructure. This offers enough flexibility to expand existing capacities without leading to large unused capacities in case smaller
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demands materialize. Compared to other configurations, the mean network utilization rates remain high (

).

4
Discussion

4.1
The role of biomass in decarbonizing
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production

Biomass-based
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production is identified as the most cost-efficient low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production technology due to low
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production cost and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
intensities. Especially in the initial phase of the transition, when the capital cost of electrolyzers is still high, biomass-based
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production dominates the technology mix (
Fig.

4
and
[
75
]
). This finding is consistent with
[
76
]
, who investigate the optimal
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production technology mix under cost uncertainties. In comparison to biomass-based
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production, the LCOH from electrolysis and renewable electricity is 3-5 times higher, hindering the deployment of electrolyzers at large scales
[
77
]
. Even if the projected cost reductions of 2 €/kg
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
can be achieved by 2040
[
13
]
, the LCOH from electrolyzers is expected to remain high. Only SMR-CCS can achieve lower
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production cost than biomass-based technologies (about 1.5 €/kg
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
), at the expense of higher process emissions (+2.5-3 kg
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{eq}\,{\cdot}\,
/kg
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
). Therefore, the cost-competitiveness of electrolytic
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
is viewed as an unrealistic prospect in the medium-term without appropriate policy support
[
78
]
.

However, the biomass availability is uncertain as multiple sectors compete for it. For instance, biomass can be used as a fuel in the transport sector to decarbonize aviation or heavy-duty transport
[
79
,
29
]
, or it can provide dispatchable, flexible energy in the power sector
[
80
]
. While the LCOH is lower in scenarios that rely on biomass in their decarbonization strategy, planning without biomass leads to more robust solutions.
Fig.

S4
visualizes the annual
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emissions for each design scenario. When planning without biomass, most scenarios can eventually achieve a net-zero supply chain design. Only when significantly higher
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demands materialize than planned (see configurations for min-low demand), the annual
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emission targets are overshot during the transition. This observation is robust across the investigated range of technology expansion rates
Section S4
.

If biomass is included in the technology mix, policymakers must ensure that biomass will be dedicated to low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production. Otherwise, it might not be possible to adapt the decarbonization strategy and scale up alternative
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
removal technologies quickly enough to satisfy the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demands in compliance with stricter
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emission limits (
Fig.

6
).

Currently, biomass is predominantly used for heating and cooling (about 75 % in 2018)
[
81
]
. However, existing studies indicate that using biomass to decarbonize transport and industry is more attractive than using biomass as a dispatchable, flexible energy source for electricity production
[
67
]
. The current bioeconomy strategy of the EU focuses on increasing the sustainability and circularity, but does not provide clear guidelines on the use of biomass
[
82
]
. Therefore, we recommend extending existing EU and national bioeconomy strategies to provide clear guidelines on the strategic use of the limited biomass resources; steering the use of biomass to sectors where biomass provides a cost-efficient decarbonization option and which lack alternatives.

4.2
The role of carbon capture, transport, and storage

\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
capture, transport, and storage (CCTS) infrastructure plays a central role in decarbonizing
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production. More specifically, when coupling
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production with CCS, large amounts of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
are captured at the production site, transported, and stored underground. When
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production is based on electrolysis,
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
removal technologies are installed to remove residual and upstream
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emissions. The
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
removal technologies are typically located close to the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
storage sites to reduce transport costs, and thus, require less extensive
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
transport infrastructure.

For scenarios with large
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demands and reduced or no biomass, existing and planned
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
storage sites are fully utilized to achieve net-zero
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production. A reduced
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
storage availability would lead to an increased installation of electrolysers. Nevertheless, a minimum of about 7, 11, and 50 Mt
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
/a
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
removal and storage capacity would be required to offset upstream emissions and realize net-zero
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production for minimum, medium, and maximum demand, respectively.

The need for CCTS infrastructure is widely recognized
[
83
]
, and according to the International Association of Oil & Gas Producers (IOGP)
[
84
]
, Europe requires a minimum of 0.5-1 Gton
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
/a of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
storage by 2050 to reach its targets. However, the capacity of existing and currently planned
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
storage projects in Europe only corresponds to 13-26 % of the storage capacity envisioned by
[
84
]
. For reference, the European hard-to-abate industry alone currently emits between 0.7-1 Gton
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
/a. Similar observations are made on a global scale, where only 10 % of the required
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
storage capacity might be available by 2050, considering the historically observed expansion rates for
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
storage
[
85
]
. This suggests that rapid changes and coordinated efforts are required to enable CCS across sectors and foster the energy transition.

4.3
Implications of a constrained technology expansion

Technology expansion rates are computed based on historically observed annual growth rates, as they do not appear to change significantly over time
[
86
,
47
,
68
]
. Previous studies indicate that energy systems models tend to overestimate the speed at which emerging technologies can penetrate the market
[
47
,
87
]
. Technology expansion constraints can be used to limit the speed at which the capacity of a technology can be expanded (
Section

2.4
).

Results indicate that the feasibility and cost of out-of-sample designs strongly depend on the availability of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
removal technologies. The reduced investments in
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
removal technologies in design scenarios that rely on biomass and the coupling with CCS pose challenges when biomass does not become available as planned, especially when larger
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demands materialize than initially planned for. The results further indicate that planning ahead and investing in sufficient low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production capacities (about 9.6 Mt/a by 2030) is necessary to scale-up low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production and facilitate larger low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demands. Otherwise, the transition might be delayed and the decarbonization targets cannot be met (
Fig.

6
and
Fig.

S4
). The planning horizon of 2022-2030 is especially important. Both, the design scenarios for no biomass and low and mean demands fail to achieve the annual emission targets in 2024-2030 because insufficient low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production capacities delay the scale-up of SMR-CCS and electrolyzer capacities, and instead, larger shares of the existing, natural-gas based SMR have to be deployed to meet the increased
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demands.
[
88
]
show that large demands for low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
(e.g., induced by high carbon taxation) can stimulate the adoption of low-carbon technologies. Hence, developing a stringent European
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
strategy could promote investments in low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
infrastructure and foster its widespread use.
The lack of a dedicated
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
transport infrastructure
[
56
,
89
]
, as well as uncertainties around material selection and process operation
[
83
]
hinder investments in low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and CCTS infrastructure. While the creation of niche markets for low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
could accelerate its adoption, this also necessitates a coordinated scale-up of the required low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and CCTS transport infrastructure
[
56
]
.

5
Conclusion

This work investigates the optimal design and rollout of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
supply chains (HSCs) to achieve a net-zero European hard-to-abate industry. We determine the optimal technology portfolio of net-zero
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production technologies while accounting for uncertainties in the evolution of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demand and biomass availability. We consider a multi-year time horizon from 2022 to 2050 and include several
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production pathways, namely water-electrolysis from renewable electricity, SMR from natural gas and biomethane, and biomass gasification.
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production from biomass and natural gas can be coupled with CCS. In addition,
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
removal technologies can be installed to remove residual
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emissions. Finally,
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and biomass transport infrastructures are designed alongside the HSC.

We follow a scenario-based uncertainty quantification approach and define 15 scenarios, considering five levels of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demand and three levels of biomass availability. We determine the optimal HSC design for the individual scenarios (design scenarios) and evaluate their performance in case alternative scenarios materialize (out-of-sample approach). Additional investments can be made to adapt the supply chain designs to the new operating conditions. However, these additional investments are limited by technology expansion constraints. The solutions are evaluated and compared based on the levelized cost of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, and a minimum-regret design is determined based on two criteria: (i) the ability to meet the annual
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emissions target and achieve net-zero emissions by 2050, and (ii) the lowest levelized cost of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
in case the worst-case scenario materializes.

\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production via SMR-CCS of natural gas and biomethane reforming are the most cost-efficient low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production pathways. Particularly attractive is the coupling of biomass-based
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production with CCS, a process which achieves a
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
removal, and can thereby offset emissions at other stages in the supply chain. Investments in electrolyzers are delayed due to high initial capital costs.

To harness the full potential of regionally-constrained biomass and to decouple
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production and demands,
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
transport infrastructure is required. Furthermore,
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
capture, transport, and storage infrastructure (CCTS) plays a central role in a low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
economy. Especially for larger
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demands, a pan-European CCTS infrastructure is required to mitigate emissions from
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production and enable CCS. A minimum
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
storage capacity of 2.7-50 Mt
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
/a is needed. Hence, a coordinated effort on a European level is required to accelerate the development and deployment of CCTS.

In general, the LCOH is 9 %-13 % lower for configurations that include biomass in their decarbonization strategy. However, a strategy relying on biomass is not robust if biomass availability is reduced. In this case, it is often impossible to adapt the system design and scale up alternative low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production technologies to meet the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demand and the decarbonization targets. In contrast, planning without biomass leads to more robust supply chains.

We also find that planning for medium
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demands and installing a sufficient
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production capacity (about 9.6 Mt/a by 2030) is required to facilitate the scale-up of existing low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production capacities to accommodate larger
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demands. Otherwise, the scale-up of the low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production capacity might be delayed, ultimately resulting in a failure to achieve the annual
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emissions targets, even if technology expansion rates are high.

A comprehensive European
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and bioenergy strategy could help navigate these large uncertainties by delineating clear directives on (i) the envisioned applications of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and (ii) the role of biomass in the energy transition.
Dedicating further research on the role of low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
in decarbonizing the selected hard-to-abate industries and identifying no-regret applications can reduce uncertainty regarding future levels of low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demand and guide policymakers. However, if the large uncertainty surrounding the future demand for low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
pertains, this may further delay the scale-up of low-carbon HSCs.

Overlooking the importance of CCTS infrastructure can hinder the transition and jeopardize the realization of climate goals.
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
transport infrastructure is required independently of the low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demand. Fostering investments in regional
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
transport infrastructure that can be expanded if larger low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demands materialize can support a successful transition to net-zero. Incentivizing investments in low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production capacities can further reduce the risks first-movers face (slow market dynamics, high low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production costs, lack of transport infrastructure) and facilitate the scale-up of low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
markets.

Finally, although biomass-based
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production is lower in cost, including biomass in the decarbonization strategy can impose risks if these biomass feedstocks are not made available. Therefore, planning without biomass feedstocks is identified as the minimum-regret strategy. Extending the EU bioenergy strategy and providing clear directives on the sectors that will get access to the limited biomass potentials can guide investments in the low-carbon
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
production technologies. Here, additional research can support policymakers in identifying the sectors where biomass plays a key role in the transition to net zero.

\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emissions per NUTS2 region in Europe. Assuming that industry-specific emissions are proportional to the production capacity, we can then estimate the regional
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demand.

We analyze the variability in the regional
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demand estimates by comparing their standard deviation.
Fig.

S1

The standard deviation is highest for steel and methanol production, reaching up to 250 kt/a in selected regions in the north-east of Germany and the north-west of France, and up to 280 kt/a in the west of the Netherlands, respectively. The standard deviation for ammonia production is substantially lower, exceeding 100 kt/a only in few select regions in the Netherlands, Germany, Poland and Lithuania. Cement and refineries exhibit the lowest standard deviations. The
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

Technology expansion constraints

The capacity of technology
h\in\mathcal{H}
at position
p\in\mathcal{P}
and time
t
is expressed by
S_{h,p,t}
. Conversion technologies are installed at nodes
p=n\in\mathcal{N}
and transport technologies can be installed at edges
p=e\in\mathcal{E}
. In each year, the technology capacity can be expanded by
\Delta S_{h,p,t}
. Existing technology capacities that are within their lifetime
l_{h}
are expressed by the parameter
s^{\mathrm{ex}}_{h,p,\bar{t}}
:

S_{h,p,t}=\underbrace{\sum^{t}_{\tilde{t}=\mathrm{max}\left(0,t-l_{h}{}+1\right)}\Delta S_{h,p,\tilde{t}}}_{\text{Capacity increase}}+\underbrace{\sum^{0}_{\bar{t}=min(t-l_{h}+1,0)}s^{\mathrm{ex}}_{h,p,\bar{t}}}_{\text{Capacity of existing technologies}},

(2)

Furthermore, the technology capacity
S_{h,p,t}
and the capacity expansion
\Delta S_{h,p,t}
are constrained by
s^{\mathrm{max}}_{h}{}
, and
\Delta s^{\mathrm{max}}_{h}{}
, respectively:

\displaystyle 0\leq S_{h,p,t}\leq s^{\mathrm{max}}_{h}

(3)

\displaystyle 0\leq\Delta S_{h,p,t}\leq\Delta s^{\mathrm{max}}_{h}

(4)

In the out-of-sample approach, the technology capacity expansion is additionally constrained by the technology expansion constraints. These additional constraints are modeled and parameterized following
[
68
,
47
]
. The technology expansion is limited by the technology expansion rate
\vartheta_{h}
multiplied by the existing knowledge
K_{h,p,t}
, which represents the expertise and knowledge of the industry (
Eq.

8
). In addition, spillover effects from one country
m
to another
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}=\mathcal{M}\setminus\{m\}
are considered, assuming a knowledge spillover rate
\omega=0.07
. The unbounded market share
\xi
and the unbounded capacity addition
\zeta_{h}
allow entry into niche markets
[
68
]
:

\displaystyle 0\leq\Delta S_{h,n,t}\leq

\displaystyle\left(1+\vartheta_{h}\right)\left(K_{h,m,t}+\omega\sum_{\tilde{m}\in\tilde{\mathcal{M}}}K_{h,\tilde{m},t}\right)+\xi\sum_{\tilde{h}\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}}S_{\tilde{h},n,t}+\zeta_{h},

(5)

Spillover effects are not included for transport technologies
h\in\mathcal{J}\subset\mathcal{H}
, which connect regions across all nodes:

\displaystyle 0\leq\Delta S_{h,e,y}\leq\vartheta_{h}K_{h,e,t}+\xi\sum_{\tilde{h}\in\tilde{\mathcal{J}}}S_{\tilde{h},e,t}+\zeta_{h}.

(6)

To avoid unrealistically high spillover effects, the cumulative capacity additions are constrained by the cumulative existing knowledge:

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\Delta S_{h,p,y}\leq\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\left(\vartheta_{h}K_{h,p,t}+\xi\sum_{\tilde{h}\in\tilde{\mathcal{H}}}S_{\tilde{h},p,y}+\zeta_{h}\right).

(7)

where the existing knowledge
K_{h,p,t}
is approximated by the previous capacity additions
\Delta S_{h,p,y}
and
\Delta s^{\mathrm{ex}}_{h,p,y}
, and depreciated over time with the knowledge depreciation rate
\delta=0.1
:

\displaystyle K_{h,p,y}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\tilde{t}=t_{0}}^{t-1}\left(1-\delta\right)^{(t-\tilde{t})}\Delta S_{h,p,\tilde{t}}+\sum_{\hat{t}=-\infty}^{\psi(t_{0})}\left(1-\delta\right)^{\left(t+(\psi(t_{0})-\hat{t})\right)}s^{\mathrm{ex}}_{h,p,\bar{t}}.

(8)

Sensitivity of the technology expansion constraint parameters

The technology expansion constraint limits the maximum annual growth rate of a technology, and is determined by the technology expansion rate and the existing capacity of a technology. Here, we investigate the impact of different expansion rates on the levelized cost of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and compare the results for low expansion rates of 10 %, and high expansion rates of 29 % to the reference case of 20 %.
Fig.

S2
visualize the change in the levelized cost of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
(LCOH) for low and high expansion rates with respect to the reference case (Figure 6). A reduction in the technology expansion rate increases the systems inertia making it more difficult to quickly adapt the investment strategy and system cost increase (up to 1.5 €/kg). In contrast, an increase in the technology expansion rate reduces the system inertia, allowing for a quicker implementation of changes in the investment strategy, and a reduction in cost (up to 1.1 €/kg). In general, smaller systems are more sensitive to changes in the expansion rate (e.g., systems designed for min or low
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
demand), whereas the impact of the expansion rate reduces for larger systems, where the maximum annual growth rates remains high due to the larger existing capacities in the system.
