Adaptive Primal-Dual Stochastic Gradient Method for Expectation-constrained Convex Stochastic Programs
Yonggui Yan, Yangyang Xu

Abstract
Stochastic gradient methods (SGMs) have been widely used for solving stochastic optimization problems. A majority of existing works assume no constraints or easy-to-project constraints. In this paper, we consider convex stochastic optimization problems with expectation constraints. For these problems, it is often extremely expensive to perform projection onto the feasible set. Several SGMs in the literature can be applied to solve the expectation-constrained stochastic problems. We propose a novel primal-dual type SGM based on the Lagrangian function. Different from existing methods, our method incorporates an adaptiveness technique to speed up convergence. At each iteration, our method inquires an unbiased stochastic subgradient of the Lagrangian function, and then it renews the primal variables by an adaptive-SGM update and the dual variables by a vanilla-SGM update. We show that the proposed method has a convergence rate of O ⁡ ( 1 / k ) O(1/\sqrt{k}) in terms of the objective error and the constraint violation. Although the convergence rate is the same as those of existing SGMs, we observe its significantly faster convergence than an existing non-adaptive primal-dual SGM and a primal SGM on solving the Neyman-Pearson classification and quadratically constrained quadratic programs. Furthermore, we modify the proposed method to solve convex-concave stochastic minimax problems, for which we perform adaptive-SGM updates to both primal and dual variables. A convergence rate of O ⁡ ( 1 / k ) O(1/\sqrt{k}) is also established to the modified method for solving minimax problems in terms of primal-dual gap. Our code has been released at https://github.com/RPI-OPT/APriD .

Introduction

The stochastic approximation method can trace back to
robbins1951stochastic
, where a root-finding problem is considered. Stochastic gradient methods (SGMs), as first-order stochastic approximation methods, have attracted a lot of research interests from both theory and applications. In the literature, a majority of works on SGMs focus on problems without constraints or with easy-to-project constraints.

In this work, we consider expectation-constrained convex stochastic programs, which are formulated as

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x}\in X}\,

\displaystyle\,f_{0}(\mathbf{x})\equiv\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{0}}[F_{0}(\mathbf{x};\xi_{0})],

s.t.

\displaystyle\,f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\equiv\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{i}}[F_{i}(\mathbf{x};\xi_{i})]\leq 0,\,i\in[M].

(1.1)

Here,
[M]
denotes
\{1,\ldots,M\}
,
X
is a compact convex set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that admits an easy projection,
\xi_{i}
is a random variable and
f_{i}
is a convex function on
X
for each
i=0,1,...,M
, and
\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{i}}
takes the expectation about
\xi_{i}
. When
\xi_{i}
is distributed uniformly on a finite set
\{\xi_{i1},\ldots,\xi_{iN_{i}}\}
for each
i=0,1,\ldots,M
, (
1
) reduces to the finite-sum structured function-constrained problem

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x}\in X}\,

\displaystyle\,f_{0}(\mathbf{x})\equiv\frac{1}{N_{0}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{0}}F_{0}(\mathbf{x};\xi_{0j})

s.t.

\displaystyle\,f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\equiv\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{i}}F_{i}(\mathbf{x};\xi_{ij})\leq 0,\,i\in[M],

(1.2)

where each
N_{i}
can be a big integer number.

Due to the possible nonlinearity of
\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
, the expectation format, and the possibly big numbers
\{N_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
and/or
M
, it can be extremely expensive to perform projection onto the feasible set of (
1
) or (
1
). Toward finding a solution of (
1
) or (
1
) with a desired accuracy, we will design a primal-dual SGM that does not require the expensive projection. Our method will only require unbiased stochastic subgradients of the Lagrangian function of (
1
) or (
1
) and the weighted projection onto
X
. To have fast convergence, we will incorporate into the designed method an adaptiveness technique, which has been extensively used in solving unconstrained problems, such as training deep learning models.

1.1
Motivating applications

Many applications can be formulated into (
1
) or (
1
). We give a few concrete examples below.

Neyman-Pearson classification.
One example of (
1
) is the Neyman-Pearson classification (NPC)
rigollet2011neyman
;
scott2005neyman
.
For a binary classification problem, a classifier
h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{a})
, parameterized by
\mathbf{x}
, is learned by finding an optimal
\mathbf{x}
from a set of training data. Each training data point
\mathbf{a}
has a label
b\in\{+1,-1\}
. The classifier categorizes a new sample
\mathbf{a}
to the “
+1
” class if
h(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a})\geq 0
and “
-1
” otherwise. The prediction incurs two types of errors: type-I error and type-II error. The former is also called false-positive error, defined as

R^{-}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{Prob}(h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{a})\geq 0\mid b=-1)=\mathbb{E}[\iota_{\geq 0}(h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{a}))\mid b=-1]

and the latter is also called false-negative error, defined as

R^{+}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{Prob}(-h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{a})>0\mid b=+1)=\mathbb{E}[\iota_{\geq 0}(-h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{a}))\mid b=+1].

Here,
b
denotes the true label of
\mathbf{a}
, and
\iota_{\geq 0}
denotes the indicator function of nonnegative numbers, i.e.,
\iota_{\geq 0}(z)=1
if
z\geq 0
and
\iota_{\geq 0}(z)=0
if
z<0
.
In certain applications, the cost of making these two types of error could be severely different. For example, in medical diagnosis, missing a malignant tumor is much more concerned than diagnosing a benign tumor to a malignant one. Hence, for these applications, it would be beneficial to minimize the more concerned type of error while controlling another type of error at an acceptable level.
NPC aims at minimizing the type-II error by controlling the type-I error. It searches for the best parameter
\mathbf{x}

in a given space
X

by solving the error-constrained problem:

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in X}\,R^{+}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad R^{-}(\mathbf{x})\leq c,

(1.3)

where
c>0
is a user-specified level of error.
Due to the nonconvexity of the indicator function
\iota_{\geq 0}(z)
, the problem in (
1.3
) is often computationally intractable. Hence, it is common (cf.
rigollet2011neyman
) to relax the indicator function
\iota_{\geq 0}(z)

by a convex non-decreasing surrogate
\varphi(z):\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
This way, if
h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{a})
is affine with respect to
\mathbf{x}
in a convex set
X
, we can obtain a relaxed convex stochastic program in the form of (
1
) by relaxing
\iota_{\geq 0}(z)
to
\varphi(z)
with
z=h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{a})
and
z=-h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{a})
respectively in the formulations of
R^{-}
and
R^{+}
.

Fairness-constrained classification.

The data involved in a classification problem may contain certain sensitive features, such as gender and race. Fairness-constrained classification
zafar2017fairness
;
zafar2019fairness
aims at finding a classifier that is fair to the samples with different sensitive features. For example, consider the binary classification problem.
Let
(\mathbf{a},z)
denote a data point, following a certain distribution and having a label
b\in\{+1,-1\}
.
Here,
z\in\{0,1\}
represents a unidimensional binary sensitive feature.
Suppose
h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{a},z)
is a classifier, parameterized by
\mathbf{x}
, such that a given sample
(\mathbf{a},z)
is predicted to class
\widehat{b}=\mathrm{sign}(h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{a},z))
.
One can learn a fair classifier
h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{a},z)
by solving the problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x}\in X}\,L(\mathbf{x}),\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad\mathbf{Prob}(\widehat{b}\neq b\mid z=0)=\mathbf{Prob}(\widehat{b}\neq b\mid z=1),

(1.4)

where
L(\mathbf{x})
measures a certain expected loss, e.g.,

L(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}\big[\log\big(1+\exp(-b\cdot h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{a},z))\big)\big]
, and
X
is a convex set.
The constraint in (
1.4
) is one kind of fairness constraint. More kinds of fairness constraints can be found in
zafar2019fairness
. Roughly speaking, a fairness constraint enforces that the classifier should not be correlated to the sensitive features.
In general, the constraint in (
1.4
) is nonconvex.
zafar2019fairness
introduces a relaxation to (
1.4
) by using an empirical estimation of the covariance between
z
and
h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{a},z)
, which results in the following problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x}\in X}\,\tilde{L}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad-c\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(z_{i}-\bar{z})h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{a}_{i},z_{i})\leq c.

(1.5)

Here,
\tilde{L}
is an empirical loss,
\bar{z}=\mathbb{E}[z]
,
\{(\mathbf{a}_{i},z_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
is a set of training samples, and
c>0
is a given threshold that trades off accuracy and unfairness. If
h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{a},z)
is affine about
\mathbf{x}
, then the constraint in (
1.5
) will be convex about
\mathbf{x}
, and in this case, (
1.5
) with a convex loss function
\tilde{L}(\mathbf{x})
becomes a convex problem in the form of (
1
).

Chance-constrained problems.

Another example is the chance-constrained problem. It can be modeled as (cf.
shapiro2014lectures
)

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in X}\,f(\mathbf{x}),\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad\mathbf{Prob}\{F(\mathbf{x},\xi)\leq 0\}\geq p,

(1.6)

where
\xi\in\Xi
is a random vector,
X
is a convex set, and
p\in(0,1)
is a user-specified probability value.
Notice
\mathbf{Prob}\{F(\mathbf{x},\xi)\leq 0\}=1-\mathbf{Prob}\{F(\mathbf{x},\xi)>0\}=1-\mathbb{E}[\iota_{>0}(F(\mathbf{x},\xi))]
, where
\iota_{>0}
denotes the indicator function of positive numbers. We can rewrite (
1.6
) as

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in X}\,f(\mathbf{x}),\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad\mathbb{E}[\iota_{>0}(F(\mathbf{x},\xi))]\leq 1-p.

(1.7)

One approach to solve (
1.7
) is the sample average approximation method
luedtke2008sample
;
pagnoncelli2009sample
. It approximates the expectation in the constraint by the empirical mean and forms the approximation problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x}\in X}\,f(\mathbf{x}),\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\iota_{>0}(F(\mathbf{x},\xi_{i}))\leq 1-\widehat{p},

(1.8)

where
\widehat{p}
is carefully selected such that the solution of (
1.8
) can satisfy certain desired property. When
f
is convex,
F
is linear about
\mathbf{x}
, and the indicator function
\iota_{>0}
is relaxed by a convex surrogate, (
1.7
) and (
1.8
) are convex in the form of (
1
) and (
1
) respectively.

Another approach to solve (
1.6
) is the scenario approximation
calafiore2006scenario
;
calafiore2005uncertain
, which takes
M
independent samples
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3},...,\xi_{M}
of
\xi
and forms the problem

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x}\in X}\,f(\mathbf{x}),\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad F(\mathbf{x},\xi_{i})\leq 0,i\in[M].

(1.9)

When
f(\mathbf{x})
and
F(\mathbf{x},\xi)
are convex about
\mathbf{x}\in X
for all
\xi
, (
1.9
) becomes one instance of (
1
) with
f_{i}(\mathbf{x})=F(\mathbf{x},\xi_{i})
for each
i
.

More.
More examples of (
1
) include the risk averse optimization (cf.
(
shapiro2014lectures
, Chapter 6)
), asset-allocation problems (cf.
rockafellar2000optimization
), and the monotonicity constrained machine learning
gupta2016monotonic
.

1.2
Related Work

Several existing methods can be applied to solve (
1
) or (
1
). The method that we will propose is of primal-dual type and adopts an adaptiveness technique to have faster convergence. Below we review the existing works that are closely related to ours.

1.2.1
Existing methods for solving (
1
) or (
1
)

Lan and Zhou
lan2020algorithms
proposes a cooperative stochastic approximation (CSA) method for stochastic programs that have one function constraint. Let
g(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{M}[f_{i}(\mathbf{x})]_{+}
. The constraint
\{\mathbf{x}:f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0,\forall\,i\in[M]\}
can be reformulated to
g(\mathbf{x})\leq 0
. Hence, CSA can be applied to (
1
) with this reformulation.
At the
k
-th iterate
\mathbf{x}^{k}
for all
k\geq 0
, CSA first calculates an unbiased estimator
\widehat{G}_{k}
of
g(\mathbf{x}^{k})
. Then depending on whether
\widehat{G}_{k}\leq\eta_{k}
or not for some tolerance
\eta_{k}>0
, it moves either along the negative direction of an unbiased stochastic approximation of the subgradient
\tilde{\nabla}f_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{k})

or
\tilde{\nabla}g(\mathbf{x}^{k})
. Given a maximum iteration
K
, CSA takes a weighted average of all iterates indexed by
\mathcal{B}^{K}=\{s\leq k\leq K\mid\widehat{G}_{k}\leq\eta_{k}\}
for some
1\leq s\leq K
. An ergodic convergence of
O(1/\sqrt{K})
can be shown for CSA under the convexity assumption.

When
M
is big in (
1
) and
f_{i}
is a deterministic function for each
i\in[M]
, one can apply the primal-dual SGM in
xu2020primal
or its adaptive variant.
The method in
xu2020primal
is derived by using the augmented Lagrangian
function. At the
k
-th iteration, it inquires an unbiased stochastic subgradient
(\mathbf{u}^{k},\mathbf{w}^{k})
of the augmented Lagrangian function and then performs the updates

\mathbf{x}^{k+1}=\mathbf{Proj}_{X,D_{k}}(\mathbf{x}^{k}-D_{k}^{-1}\mathbf{u}^{k}),\quad\mathbf{z}^{k+1}=\mathbf{z}^{k}+\rho_{k}\mathbf{w}^{k},

(1.10)

where
\rho_{k}>0
is a dual step size, and
D_{k}
is a positive definite matrix. Two settings of
D_{k}
are provided and analyzed in
xu2020primal
. One is a non-adaptive setting with
D_{k}=\frac{I}{\alpha_{k}}
, where
I
is the identity matrix and
\alpha_{k}>0
.
The other is an adaptive setting with
D_{k}=\text{diag}(\mathbf{v}^{k})+\frac{I}{\alpha_{k}}
and
\mathbf{v}^{k}=\eta\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{k}\frac{(\mathbf{u}^{t})^{2}}{\gamma_{t}^{2}}}
, where
\alpha_{k}>0,\eta>0
and
\gamma_{t}=\max\{1,\|\mathbf{u}^{t}\|\}
.
Under both settings, the method has an ergodic convergence rate of
O(1/\sqrt{k})
for the convex case. Notice that
for the expectation or finite-sum constrained problems, the augmented Lagrangian function will have the compositional expectation or finite-sum terms, which prohibit an easy access to an unbiased stochastic subgradient
wang2017stochastic
;
xu2019katyusha
. Hence, the method in
xu2020primal
will not apply to (
1
) with expectation constraints or (
1
) with big numbers
\{N_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
.

If an easy projection can be performed onto
X_{i}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0\}
for each
i\in[M]
, one can also apply the random projection methods
wang2016stochastic
;
wang2015random
to (
1
), and in addition, if the exact gradient of
f_{0}
can be obtained, the stochastic proximal-proximal method in
ryu2017proximal
can also be applied. However, if
X_{i},i\in[M]
are not simple sets such as the logistic loss function induced constraint set in NPC
rigollet2011neyman
, these random projection methods will be inefficient.
For the finite-sum constrained problem (
1
), level-set based methods are studied in
lin2018level-fs
. For the special case of (
1
) where exact gradients of all the functions
\{f_{i}\}_{i=0}^{M}
can be accessed, deterministic first-order methods have been studied in several works, e.g.,
xu2017first
;
xu2019iteration
;
yu2016primal
;
lin2018level-SIOPT
;
aybat2013augmented
;
lu2018iteration
. However, for (
1
) with big numbers
\{N_{i}\}_{i=0}^{N}
, these deterministic methods would be inefficient, as computing exact gradients at each iteration is very expensive.

Deterministic or stochastic gradient methods (e.g.,
nemirovski2009robust
;
hien2017inexact
;
hamedani2018iteration
;
hamedani2018primal
;
zhao2019optimal
) have also been studied for saddle-point problems, which can include the function-constrained problems as special cases if certain constraint qualifications hold. For example, one can solve the problem in (
1
) by the saddle-point mirror stochastic approximation (MSA), which is proposed by Nemirovski et al.
nemirovski2009robust
to solve convex-concave stochastic saddle-point problems. Assuming the strong duality, we can reformulate (
1
) as a saddle-point problem by using the Lagrangian function
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})
; see (
2.1
) below. Applying MSA to the reformulation will yield the following iterative update:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{x}^{k+1}\\
\mathbf{z}^{k+1}\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{Proj}_{X\times Z}\left[\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{x}^{k}\\
\mathbf{z}^{k}\end{pmatrix}-\gamma_{k}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{u}^{k}\\
-\mathbf{w}^{k}\end{pmatrix}\right],

(1.11)

where
(\mathbf{u}^{k},\mathbf{w}^{k})
is an unbiased stochastic subgradient of
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}^{k},\mathbf{z}^{k})
,
Z
is an estimated compact set on the dual variable, and
\gamma_{k}>0
is a step size. Given a maximum iteration
K
, MSA with
\gamma_{k}=\gamma/\sqrt{K},\forall\,k
for some
\gamma>0
can achieve an ergodic convergence rate
O(1/\sqrt{K})
in terms of the primal-dual gap.

1.2.2
Adaptive SGMs

A key difference between the existing methods reviewed above and our method for solving (
1
) or (
1
) is the use of an adaptiveness technique, which has been widely used to improve the empirical convergence speed of SGMs for machine learning, especially deep learning.
The vanilla SGM is easy to implement, but its convergence is often slow.
Many adaptive variants have been proposed to improve the empirical convergence speed, such as Adagrad
duchi2011adaptive
, RMSprop
tieleman2012lecture
, Adam
kingma2014adam
, Adadelta
zeiler2012adadelta
, Nadam
dozat2016incorporating
, and Amsgrad
sashank2018convergence
.
In the vanilla SGM, the same learning rate is used to all coordinates of the variable at each update, and this often results in poor performance for solving a problem with sparse training data
duchi2011adaptive
.
In contrast, adaptive methods adaptively scale the learning rate for different coordinates of the variable, based on a certain average of all the stochastic gradients that have been generated. The arithmetic average is used by Adagrad
duchi2011adaptive
and the exponential moving average by others
tieleman2012lecture
;
kingma2014adam
;
zeiler2012adadelta
;
dozat2016incorporating
;
sashank2018convergence
.
Besides the adaptive learning rate, momentum-based search direction is adopted in adaptive SGMs.
These adaptive methods have been used a lot but mainly for
problems without constraints or with easy-to-project constraints.

The adaptiveness technique that will be used in our method is closely related to the technique used by Amsgrad
sashank2018convergence
. For a stochastic problem

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in X}\,f_{0}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{0}}[F_{0}(\mathbf{x};\xi_{0})]
,
Amsgrad iteratively performs the updates:

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}^{k}

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}\mathbf{m}^{k-1}+(1-\beta_{1})\mathbf{u}^{k},

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{k}

\displaystyle=\beta_{2}\mathbf{v}^{k-1}+(1-\beta_{2})(\mathbf{u}^{k})^{2},

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{k}

\displaystyle=\max(\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{k-1},\mathbf{v}^{k}),

(1.12)

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Proj}_{X,(\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{k})^{1/2}}(\mathbf{x}^{k}-\alpha_{k}\mathbf{m}^{k}/(\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{k})^{1/2}),

where
\mathbf{u}^{k}
is an unbiased stochastic gradient of
f_{0}
at
\mathbf{x}^{k}
. Slightly different from Amsgrad, Adam
kingma2014adam
sets
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{k}=\mathbf{v}^{k}
for all
k
. It turns out that the setting in (
1.12
) is important, as otherwise the method is not guaranteed to converge even for convex problems.
The update in (
1.12
) ensures that each component of
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{k}
is nondecreasing with respect to
k
. This addresses the divergence issue of Adam. However, one drawback is that once an unusual
\mathbf{u}^{k}
with “big” components appears in early iterations, the components of
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{k}
will remain “big”, which results small effective learning rates and can slow the convergence. We address this issue by using the clipping technique in
abadi2016deep
to modify the update of
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{k}
.

1.3
Contributions

The main contributions are listed below.

•

We propose a new adaptive primal-dual stochastic gradient method (APriD) for solving expectation-constrained convex stochastic optimization problems. The method is derived based on the ordinary Lagrangian function of the underlying problem. During each iteration, APriD first inquires an unbiased stochastic subgradient of the Lagrangian function about both primal and dual variables, and then it performs a stochastic subgradient descent update to the primal variable and a stochastic gradient ascent update to the dual variable. For the primal update, an adaptive learning rate is applied, in order to have faster convergence.

•

We analyze the convergence rate of APriD. Assuming the existence of a Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) solution, we prove the boundedness of the dual iterate in expectation. Based on that, we then establish the
O(1/\sqrt{k})
ergodic convergence rate, where
k
is the number of subgradient inquiries. The convergence rate results are in terms of primal and dual objective value and primal constraint violation.

•

We also extend APriD to solve a convex-concave minimax problem. The extended method (named as APriAD), during each iteration, first inquires an unbiased estimation of a subgradient of the minimax problem and then updates the primal and dual variables by performing an adaptive update. An ergodic convergence rate of
O(1/\sqrt{k})
is also established in terms of the primal-dual gap.

•

We test APriD on NPC
scott2005neyman
;
rigollet2011neyman
and the quadratically constrained quadratic program (QCQP) in the expectation form and the scenario approximation form. We compare the proposed method to CSA
lan2020algorithms
and MSA
nemirovski2009robust
. Although all three methods have the same-order convergence rate, the numerical results demonstrate that APriD can decrease the objective error and the constraint violations (sometimes significantly) faster than CSA and MSA, in terms of iteration number and the running time.

We use the convection

For any positive integer

For a given symmetric positive semidefinite matrix

Let

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbf{z}\geq\mathbf{0}}\,\big\{d(\mathbf{z})\equiv\min_{\mathbf{x}\in X}\,\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\big\}.

4
Extension to Stochastic Minimax Problem

In this section, we modify the APriD method in Algorithm
1
to solve a stochastic convex-concave minimax problem in the form of

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in X}\,\max_{\mathbf{z}\in Z}\,\{\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z};\xi)]\}.

(4.1)

Similar to existing works (e.g.,
nemirovski2009robust
;
juditsky2011solving
;
chen2014optimal
;
zhao2019optimal
), we assume that
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
Z\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
in (
4.1
) are compact convex sets,
\xi
is a random vector, and
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})
is convex in
\mathbf{x}\in X
and concave in
\mathbf{z}\in Z
.
By the minimax theorem
neumann1928theorie
, the strong duality holds for (
4.1
), namely,

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in X}\max_{\mathbf{z}\in Z}\,\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})=\max_{\mathbf{z}\in Z}\,\min_{\mathbf{x}\in X}\,\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}).

(4.2)

In Algorithm
1
, we perform an adaptive SGM update to the primal variable
\mathbf{x}
in (
2.3
)-(
2.7
), but a vanilla SGM update to the dual variable
\mathbf{z}
. This is because the Lagrangian function of (
1
) has a simple linear dependence on the dual variable. As demonstrated in
xu2020primal
, an adaptive primal-dual SGM performs similarly as well as its non-adaptive counterpart for a linearly constrained problem.
In (
4.1
),
\mathcal{L}
can have complex dependence on both
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{z}
. Hence, we modify Algorithm
1
to solve (
4.1
) by performing adaptive updates to both
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{z}
. The modified algorithm is named as APriAD, and its pseudocode is given in Algorithm
2
. Similar to Algorithm
1
, we assume a stochastic first-order oracle, which can return an unbiased stochastic subgradient of
\mathcal{L}
at any inquiry point
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})
.

Algorithm 2

4.1
)

;

2:

Lemma 10

For any

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}^{j},\mathbf{z})-\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}^{j})\leq

Proof

For any

\displaystyle\big\langle\mathbf{x}^{j}-\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}^{j}\big\rangle

\displaystyle\big\langle\mathbf{x}^{j}-\mathbf{x},\tilde{\nabla}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}^{j},\mathbf{z}^{j})\big\rangle-\big\langle\mathbf{z}^{j}-\mathbf{z},\tilde{\nabla}_{\mathbf{z}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}^{j},\mathbf{z}^{j})\big\rangle\geq\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}^{j},\mathbf{z})-\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}^{j}).

For any

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n\theta B^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{n}(\theta^{2}+M_{\mathbf{x}}^{2})\sum_{j=1}^{t}\alpha_{j}^{2}}{2(1\!-\!\beta_{1})^{2}(1\!-\!\beta_{2})^{1/2}\theta},

6
Conclusions

We have proposed an adaptive primal-dual stochastic gradient method (SGM) for solving expectation-constrained convex stochastic programming. The method is designed based on the Lagrangian function. At each iteration, it first inquires an unbiased stochastic estimation of the subgradient of the Lagrangian function, and then it performs an adaptive SGM update to the primal variables and a vanilla SGM step to the dual variables. The method has also been extended with a modification to solve stochastic convex-concave minimax problems.
For both methods, we have established the convergence rate of
O(1/\sqrt{k})
, where
k
is the number of inquiries of the stochastic subgradient. Numerical experiments on three examples demonstrate its superior practical performance over two state-of-the-art methods.

Appendix A
Proof of Lemma
2

Proof

First, consider the case of non-constant primal step size.
By
\eta_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\alpha_{i}\beta_{1}^{i-1}
and the
\eta
-update in (
2.8
), we have
\eta_{k}=\sum_{i=k}^{K}\alpha_{i}\beta_{1}^{i-k},k\in[K]
, and thus the
\rho
-update becomes

\rho_{k}=\frac{\rho_{k-1}}{\beta_{1}+\frac{\alpha_{k-1}}{\sum_{i=k}^{K}\alpha_{i}\beta_{1}^{i-k}}}.

By the above equation, we have for
2\leq j\leq K
,

\displaystyle\rho_{j}=\frac{\rho_{j-1}}{\beta_{1}+\frac{\alpha_{j-1}}{\sum_{k=j}^{K}\alpha_{k}\beta_{1}^{k-j}}}\leq\frac{\rho_{j-1}}{\beta_{1}+\frac{\alpha_{j-1}}{\alpha_{j-1}\sum_{k=j}^{K}\beta_{1}^{k-j}}}\leq\frac{\rho_{j-1}}{\beta_{1}+\frac{1}{\sum_{k=j}^{\infty}\beta_{1}^{k-j}}}=\rho_{j-1},

where the inequality follows from the non-increasing monotonicity of
\{\alpha_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}
and
\beta_{1}\in(0,1)
. Hence,
\{\rho_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}
is a non-increasing sequence. Using (
2.8
) again, we have for
2\leq j\leq t\leq K
,

\rho_{j}=\frac{\rho_{j-1}}{\beta_{1}+\frac{\alpha_{j-1}}{\sum_{k=j}^{K}\alpha_{k}\beta_{1}^{k-j}}}\geq\frac{\rho_{j-1}}{\beta_{1}+\frac{\alpha_{j-1}}{\sum_{k=j}^{t}\alpha_{k}\beta_{1}^{k-j}}}=\frac{\sum_{k=j}^{t}\alpha_{k}\beta_{1}^{k-j}}{\sum_{k=j-1}^{t}\alpha_{k}\beta_{1}^{k-(j-1)}}\rho_{j-1},

which clearly implies the inequality in (
2.11
).

For
j=1
, (
2.12
) holds because
\beta\in(0,1)
. To show it holds for
2\leq j\leq K
, we rewrite
\rho_{j}
and obtain

\displaystyle\rho_{j}=\frac{\rho_{j-1}}{\beta_{1}+\frac{\alpha_{j-1}}{\sum_{k=j}^{K}\alpha_{k}\beta_{1}^{k-j}}}=\frac{\sum_{k=j}^{K}\alpha_{k}\beta_{1}^{k-j}}{\sum_{k=j-1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\beta_{1}^{k-(j-1)}}\rho_{j-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{k=j}^{K}\alpha_{k}\beta_{1}^{k-j}}{\sum_{k=j-1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\beta_{1}^{k-(j-1)}}\times\frac{\sum_{k=j-1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\beta_{1}^{k-(j-1)}}{\sum_{k=j-2}^{K}\alpha_{k}\beta_{1}^{k-(j-2)}}\times\cdots\times\frac{\sum_{k=2}^{K}\alpha_{k}\beta_{1}^{k-2}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\beta_{1}^{k-1}}\rho_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{k=j}^{K}\alpha_{k}\beta_{1}^{k-j}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\beta_{1}^{k-1}}\rho_{1}\leq\frac{\frac{\alpha_{j}}{(1\!-\!\beta_{1})}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\beta_{1}^{k-1}}\rho_{1}\leq\frac{\frac{\alpha_{j}}{(1\!-\!\beta_{1})}}{\alpha_{1}}\rho_{1}=\frac{\rho_{1}\alpha_{j}}{\alpha_{1}(1\!-\!\beta_{1})},

where the third equation recursively applies the second equation, and the inequalities hold by the two inequalities in (
2.10
).

Now, consider the case of constant primal step size, i.e.,

\alpha_{j}=\alpha_{1}
for all
j\in[K]
. We can prove
\eta_{j}=\frac{\alpha_{1}}{1-\beta_{1}}
for all
j\in[K]
by the induction, and thus

\rho_{j}=\frac{\rho_{j-1}}{\beta_{1}+\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{1}/(1\!-\!\beta_{1})}}=\frac{\rho_{j-1}}{\beta_{1}+(1\!-\!\beta_{1})}=\rho_{j-1},\forall\,j\geq 2,

which completes the proof.

Appendix I
Proof of Corollary
4

Proof

For the step sizes
\{\alpha_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{K}\alpha_{j}=\alpha\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\sqrt{j+1}}\geq\alpha\int_{s=2}^{K+2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}\mbox{d}s=2\alpha(\sqrt{K+2}-\sqrt{2}),

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{K}\alpha_{j}^{2}=\alpha^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{1}{j+1}\leq\alpha^{2}\int_{s=1}^{K+1}\frac{1}{s}\mbox{d}s=\alpha^{2}\log{(K+1)}.

Similarly, for
\{\rho_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}
, it holds

\sum_{j=1}^{K}\rho_{j}^{2}\leq\rho^{2}\log{(K+1)}
.
Plug these bounds to the result of Theorem
4.1
and note
\log(K+1)\geq 1
for
K\geq 2
.
We finish the proof.
