Proximal methods for point source localisation
Tuomo Valkonen

Abstract
Point source localisation is generally modelled as a Lasso-type problem on measures. However, optimisation methods in non-Hilbert spaces, such as the space of Radon measures, are much less developed than in Hilbert spaces. Most numerical algorithms for point source localisation are based on the Frank–Wolfe conditional gradient method, for which ad hoc convergence theory is developed. We develop extensions of proximal-type methods to spaces of measures. This includes forward-backward splitting, its inertial version, and primal-dual proximal splitting. Their convergence proofs follow standard patterns. We demonstrate their numerical efficacy.

Introduction

The point source localisation problem
[
7
,
26
]
reads

\min_{0\leq\mu\in{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)}\,\frac{1}{2}\|A\mu-b\|^{2}+\alpha\|\mu\|_{{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)}

(1)

for a regularisation parameter
\alpha>0
, measurement data
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and a forward operator
A\in\mathbb{L}({\mathscr{M}}(\Omega);\mathbb{R}^{n})
in the space
{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)
of Radon measures on
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Commonly
A
consists of several convolution operators over the sensors of a sensor grid.
Most iterative algorithms in the literature for (
1
) are based on the Frank–Wolfe conditional gradient method
[
21
]
.
Forward-backward splitting and other proximal-type methods are notably absent.
We want to understand
why are true proximal-type methods not amenable to (
1
)—or are they?

The basic scheme of conditional gradient methods
[
5
,
17
,
18
,
29
,
3
,
16
]
for (
1
) is to add a single Dirac measure (or spike) to the discrete measure
\mu
, and then optimise the weights of the spikes so far inserted. Repeat.
The location of the spike is discovered by maximising
|A_{*}(A\mu-b)|
, where
A_{*}\in\mathbb{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n};C_{0}(\Omega))
is a pre-adjoint of
A
.
This is a difficult non-convex optimisation problem.
Some also include spike sliding
[
17
]
.
The approach has been extended to curve discovery
[
4
]
.
Semismooth Newton approaches have also been proposed
[
8
,
9
]
, and recently, an approach based on semi-infinite programming
[
19
]
.
In some cases particle gradient descent can be used after lifting the measures to a higher-dimensional space
[
12
,
14
]
. The number of particles (or point sources) is, however, fixed, although many-particle limits are also analysed. In
[
13
]
standard Bregman-proximal methods are applied to point source reconstruction by working with densities with respect to a fixed reference measure. In practical application, the reference measure is discretised, so the method is not
grid-free
: the sources cannot be located at arbitrary points of the domain
\Omega
.

Forward-backward splitting is commonly used for
\ell_{1}
-regularised regression (Lasso), which is a discrete, finite-dimensional variant of (
1
). With
F(\mu)=\frac{1}{2}\|A\mu-b\|^{2}
, we can also sketch forward-backward splitting for (
1
) as solving on each step
k\in\mathbb{N}
the surrogate problem

\mu^{k+1}\in\argmin_{0\leq\mu\in{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)}\,F(\mu^{k})+\langle F^{\prime}(\mu^{k})|\mu-\mu^{k}\rangle+\alpha\|\mu\|_{{\mathscr{M}}}+\text{proximal penalty}.

(2)

In Hilbert spaces the proximal penalty would be
\frac{1}{2\tau}\|\mu-\mu^{k}\|^{2}
for a step length parameter
\tau>0
satisfying
\tau L<1
for a Lipschitz factor
L
of
F^{\prime}
.
This choice is not practical in non-Hilbert spaces, as a crucial (Pythagoras’) three-point identity does not hold.
Such an identity, however, holds for Bregman divergences
[
6
]
; see
[
34
]
. The Frank–Wolfe method can, moreover, be seen a forward-backward method for a modified but equivalent problem
1
1

1

\|\mu\|_{{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)}
is replaced by
\varphi(\|\mu\|_{{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)})
for
\varphi
that quadratically penalises values greater than
F(0)/\alpha
.
with zero as the proximal penalty. However, as
F^{\prime}
cannot be Lipschitz with respect to such a degenerate proximal penalty, a form of line search is employed to ensure descent. In the case of (
1
), this amounts to the finite-dimensional weight optimisation subproblems.
These considerations raise the question whether we could develop a distance on
{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)
that would give a practical forward-backward method?

As the first stage of our endeavour to understand (
1
), documented in this manuscript, we were able to do so simply by constructing on
{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)
the semi-inner product

\langle\mu,\nu\rangle_{{\mathscr{D}}}:=\langle{\mathscr{D}}\mu|\nu\rangle\quad(\mu,\nu\in{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega))

for a “particle-to-wave” operator
{\mathscr{D}}:\mathbb{L}({\mathscr{M}}(\Omega);C_{0}(\Omega))
, and then basing the proximal penalty in (
2
) on the square of the induced semi-norm
\|\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,\|_{\mathscr{D}}
.
We analyse an exemplifying choice of
{\mathscr{D}}
based on convolution in
Section

2
. In particular we relate it to the weak-
*
convergence of measures. To develop in
Section

4
a forward-backward method for (
1
), we will need
F^{\prime}
to be Lipschitz with respect to
\|\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,\|_{\mathscr{D}}
. For the squared data term, this reduces to
A_{*}A\leq L{\mathscr{D}}
for some factor
L\geq 0
. Based on approximation theory
[
11
]
, Bochner’s theorem and Fourier transforms, we study this relationship in
Section

3
.

Our convergence proofs in
Section

4
are then standard and simple
[
33
,
15
]
.
However, we will need to account for inexact computation of each step of the method. The resulting approach bears more resemblance to the semi-infinite approach of
[
19
]
than to conditional gradient methods in that more than one point may need to be inserted into the support of
\mu
on each iteration of the method.
Inertial extensions, i.e., FISTA
[
2
]
for (
1
), are immediate.
We treat one in
Section

5
.

A forward-backward or conditional gradient method can only handle differentiable data terms with nonsmooth (Radon norm) regularisation.
However, our overall approach readily extends to other proximal-type type methods. We therefore sketch in
Section

6
an extension of the primal dual proximal splitting (PDPS) of
[
10
]
to problems of the form

\min_{0\leq\mu\in{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)}\,F_{0}(A\mu)+\alpha\|\mu\|_{{\mathscr{M}}},

where
F_{0}
may be nonsmooth.
Thus our overall approach is applicable to problems that are (so far) out of the reach of conditional gradient methods. As a further advantage, thanks to standard convergence proofs, our methods can readily be extended to product spaces
{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)\times X
for
X
a Hilbert space.

We finish with numerical demonstrations in
Section

2
Distances of measures

As we have mentioned in the introduction, the norm or the squared norm is not particularly useful for deriving proximal-type methods in non-Hilbert spaces.
Nevertheless, let
M(\mu):=\frac{1}{2}\|\mu\|_{{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)}^{2}
. The
preduality map

\mu\mapsto\partial M(\mu)
,
{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)\rightrightarrows C_{0}(\Omega)
associates with every measure
\mu\in{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega))
a set of corresponding “wave functions”

\omega_{\mu}\in\partial M(\mu)=\{\omega\|\mu\|_{{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)}\mid\omega\in C_{0}(\Omega),\,-1\leq\omega\leq 1,\,\langle\omega|\mu\rangle=\|\mu\|_{{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)}\}.

If this relationship were linear and single-valued, as it is in Hilbert spaces,
M(\mu-\mu^{k})
could be a practical proximal penalty term in
Eq.

2
. Unfortunately, this is not the case in
{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)
.
However, the concept of the preduality map suggests to define a distance on measures using some
particle-to-wave operator

{\mathscr{D}}\in\mathbb{L}({\mathscr{M}}(\Omega);C_{0}(\Omega))
that explicitly and linearly associates every measure, in the manuscript typically a discrete measure, with a corresponding wave function, an element of the predual.
In
Section

2.2
we will study such operators based on convolution.
Before this, in
Section

2.1
we study general properties of seminorms and semi-inner products constructed with linear operators
{\mathscr{D}}
to a predual space.

2.1
Wave-particle duality

Let
X
be a (real) Banach space with a predual space
X_{*}
, i.e.,
X=(X_{*})^{*}
.
Pick
{\mathscr{D}}\in\mathbb{L}(X;X_{*})
, which is
self-adjoint
and
positive semi-definite
, that is,

\displaystyle\langle{\mathscr{D}}x|y\rangle_{X_{*},X}=\langle x|{\mathscr{D}}y\rangle_{X,X_{*}}\quad(x,y\in X)

(3)

and

\displaystyle\langle{\mathscr{D}}x|x\rangle_{X_{*},X}\geq 0\quad(x,x\in X).

(4)

If the latter inequality is strict, we call
{\mathscr{D}}
(strictly)
positive definite
.
Define

\langle x,x\rangle_{{\mathscr{D}}}:=\langle{\mathscr{D}}x|x\rangle_{X_{*},X}\quad\text{and}\quad\|x\|_{{\mathscr{D}}}:=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle_{{\mathscr{D}}}}.

Then it is easy to see that
\langle\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,,\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,\rangle_{{\mathscr{D}}}
is a pseudo-inner product on
X
, and
\|\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,\|_{{\mathscr{D}}}
a seminorm, i.e., non-negative and satisfies the triangle inequality.
Consequently, by a standard argument,
2
2

2

\langle\mu,\nu\rangle_{\mathscr{D}}=\frac{1}{2}\|\mu-\nu\|_{\mathscr{D}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|\mu\|_{\mathscr{D}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|\nu\|_{\mathscr{D}}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\mu\|_{\mathscr{D}}+\|\nu\|_{\mathscr{D}}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|\mu\|_{\mathscr{D}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|\nu\|_{\mathscr{D}}^{2}=\|\mu\|_{\mathscr{D}}\|\nu\|_{\mathscr{D}}
.
it also satisfies the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.
Moreover, the three-point identity holds:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|_{{\mathscr{D}}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\langle{\mathscr{D}}(x-y)|x-y\rangle

(5)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\langle{\mathscr{D}}((x-z)+(z-y))|(x-z)+(z-y)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|x-z\|_{{\mathscr{D}}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|z-y\|_{{\mathscr{D}}}^{2}+\langle x-z,z-y\rangle_{{\mathscr{D}}}.

If
{\mathscr{D}}
is injective, this construction turns
X
into an inner product space. The (semi-)norm
\|\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,\|_{{\mathscr{D}}}
may not, however, be equivalent to the original norm
\|\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,\|
on
X
. Since
{\mathscr{D}}
is assumed bounded, we always have
\|x\|_{{\mathscr{D}}}\leq\|{\mathscr{D}}\|\|x\|
, so the induced topology is weaker than the original (strong) topology. If the opposite inequality also holds, the norms are equivalent. For our constructions, this will, however, not be the case.

2.2
Particle-to-wave operators based on convolution

We now take
{\mathscr{D}}\mu=\rho*\mu
for a convolution kernel
\rho\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
We call
\rho

symmetric
if
\rho(-x)=\rho(x)
for all

, and

if

.
We first show that
{\mathscr{D}}
is well-defined.
Throughout, we take

as our designated predual space of

.
When

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\rho\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be symmetric.
On a closed domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
{\mathscr{D}}\mu:=\rho*\mu
for
\mu\in{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)

Then
{\mathscr{D}}\in\mathbb{L}({\mathscr{M}}(\Omega);C_{0}(\Omega))

Proof 2.2
.

The definition of the convolution readily shows that
{\mathscr{D}}\mu\in C(\Omega)
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
.
Since
\rho
is continuous and vanishes at infinity,
\|\rho\|_{\infty}<\infty
, and there exist

such that
|\rho(x)|\leq\varepsilon
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(0,r)
.
Consequently

|[{\mathscr{D}}\mu](y)|\leq\left|\int_{B(y,r)}\rho(y-x)\,d\mu(x)\right|+\left|\int_{\Omega\setminus B(y,r)}\rho(y-x)\,d\mu(x)\right|\leq\|\rho\|_{\infty}|\mu|(B(y,r))+\varepsilon\|\mu\|_{{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)}.

Since
\|\mu\|_{{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)}<\infty
, for any
\varepsilon>0
, we can find
R>0
such that
\mu(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(0,R))\leq\varepsilon
.
Thus
|[{\mathscr{D}}\mu](y)|\leq\varepsilon(\|\rho\|_{\infty}+\|\mu\|_{{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)})
for
\|y\|>r+R
.
Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, this shows that
{\mathscr{D}}\mu\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Since
\Omega
is closed, it follows that
{\mathscr{D}}\mu\in C_{0}(\Omega)
.
Indeed, by the definition of
C_{0}(\Omega)
as the closure of
C_{c}(\Omega)
, there exist
\varphi_{k}\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\varphi_{k}\to{\mathscr{D}}\mu
uniformly in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
But for a closed
\Omega

\varphi_{k}|\Omega\in C_{c}(\Omega)
with
\varphi_{k}|\Omega\to({\mathscr{D}}\mu)|\Omega
uniformly in
\Omega
.

We then expand and rearrange using Fubini’s theorem for any

that

\displaystyle\langle{\mathscr{D}}\mu|\nu\rangle_{C_{0}(\Omega),{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}[{\mathscr{D}}\mu](x)\,d\nu(x)=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\rho(x-y)\,d\mu(y)\,d\nu(x)

(6)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\rho(x-y)\,d\nu(x)\,d\mu(y)=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\rho(y-x)\,d\nu(x)\,d\mu(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}[\rho*\nu](y)\mu(y)=\langle{\mathscr{D}}\nu|\mu\rangle_{C_{0}(\Omega),{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)}.

Hence
\|\rho*\nu\|_{\infty}\leq\|\rho\|_{\infty}\|\nu\|_{{\mathscr{M}}}
; see, e.g.,
[

Consequently (

\langle{\mathscr{D}}\mu|\nu\rangle_{C_{0}(\Omega),{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)}\leq\|\rho\|_{\infty}\|\nu\|_{{\mathscr{M}}}\|\mu\|_{{\mathscr{M}}}.

This shows that

is bounded. It is evidently also linear so that

as claimed.
From (

is self-adjoint.

(7)

Example 2.3

Since the Fourier transform of a Gaussian

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose
\varphi\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
the domain

,

Proof 2.5

Let

By assumption

Since

\mu^{k}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox to10.00002pt{\hss\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{*\,}$}\hss}\hss}\hbox{$\rightharpoonup$}}\mu
weakly-
*

{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)

Proof 2.7
.

We know from

2.1

{\mathscr{D}}\in\mathbb{L}({\mathscr{M}}(\Omega);C_{0}(\Omega))

Bochner’s theorem proves

(i)

Therefore, by Fubini’s theorem and the Fourier inverse transform, we have

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\rho(x-y)\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\hat{\rho}(\xi)e^{2\pi\mathbb{i}\langle\xi,x-y\rangle}\,d\xi\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y)

is not identically zero and has
\hat{\rho}\geq 0
, necessarily
\int\hat{\rho}\,d\xi>0
. But then there exists
\varepsilon>0
and a Borel set
E
with nonzero Lebesgue measure such that
\hat{\rho}\geq\varepsilon
on
E
. When
\mu\in\mathscr{Z}(\Omega)
, the zero set of the continuous function
\mathscr{F}[\mu]
, a finite sum of complex exponentials, has Lebesgue measure zero by
[
11
, Lemma 13.6]
.
Therefore
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\hat{\rho}(\xi)|\mathscr{F}[\mu](\xi)|^{2}\,d\xi\geq\varepsilon\int_{E}|\mathscr{F}[\mu](\xi)|^{2}\,d\xi>0
.
The claim now follows from (
8
).

For
Item

3
we observe that
{\mathscr{D}}=\mathscr{Q}_{*}\mathscr{Q}
for
\mathscr{Q}\in\mathbb{L}({\mathscr{M}}(\Omega);L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
defined by
\mathscr{Q}\mu:=\rho^{1/2}*\mu
and
\mathscr{Q}_{*}f:=\rho^{1/2}*f
for
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\mu\in{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)
.
We therefore need to prove that
\rho^{1/2}*(\mu^{k}-\mu)\to 0
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Observe that
\rho_{y}^{1/2}(x):=\rho^{1/2}(x-y)
satisfies
\rho_{y}^{1/2}\in C_{0}(\Omega)
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
(For this we need the closedness of
\Omega
, but not yet the boundedness; compare the proof of
Lemma

2.1
.)
Thus
\langle\rho_{x}^{1/2}|\mu^{k}-\mu\rangle\to 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by the assumed weak-
*
convergence.
Since by assumption
\rho^{1/2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we can for each
\varepsilon>0
find a bounded
U_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus U_{\varepsilon}}\rho^{1/2}(x)^{2}\,dx\leq\varepsilon\|\rho^{1/2}\|_{\infty}^{2}
.
Letting
\Theta_{\varepsilon}:=U_{\varepsilon}+\Omega
, for all
y\in\Omega
then

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Theta_{\varepsilon}}\rho_{y}^{1/2}(x)^{2}\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus(\Theta_{\varepsilon}-y)}\rho^{1/2}(x)^{2}\,dx\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus U_{\varepsilon}}\rho^{1/2}(x)^{2}\,dx\leq\varepsilon\|\rho^{1/2}\|_{\infty}^{2}.

Using Jensen’s inequality and Fubini’s theorem, then

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Theta_{\varepsilon}}[\rho^{1/2}*(\mu^{k}-\mu)](x)^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Theta_{\varepsilon}}\left(\int_{\Omega}\rho_{y}^{1/2}(x)\,d|\mu^{k}-\mu|(y)\right)^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\|\mu^{k}-\mu\|_{{\mathscr{M}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Theta_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\Omega}\rho_{y}^{1/2}(x)^{2}\,d|\mu^{k}-\mu|(y)\,dx

\displaystyle=\|\mu^{k}-\mu\|_{{\mathscr{M}}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Theta_{\varepsilon}}\rho_{y}^{1/2}(x)^{2}\,dx\,d|\mu^{k}-\mu|(y)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\|\rho^{1/2}\|_{\infty}^{2}\|\mu^{k}-\mu\|_{{\mathscr{M}}}^{2}.

The set
\Theta_{\varepsilon}
is of finite measure, because
\Omega
is bounded.
By Egorov’s theorem we can thus find a set
E_{\varepsilon}\subset\Theta_{\varepsilon}
such that the Lebesgue measure
{\mathscr{L}}(\Theta_{\varepsilon}\setminus E_{\varepsilon})\leq\varepsilon
and
x\mapsto\langle\rho_{x}^{1/2}|\mu^{k}-\mu\rangle
converges in
E_{\varepsilon}
uniformly to zero.
Then

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}[\rho^{1/2}*(\mu^{k}-\mu)](x)^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{E_{\varepsilon}}\langle\rho_{x}^{1/2}|\mu^{k}-\mu\rangle^{2}\,dx+\int_{\Theta_{\varepsilon}\setminus E_{\varepsilon}}[\rho^{1/2}*(\mu^{k}-\mu)](x)^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Theta_{\varepsilon}}[\rho^{1/2}*(\mu^{k}-\mu)](x)^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{E_{\varepsilon}}\langle\rho_{x}^{1/2}|\mu^{k}-\mu\rangle^{2}\,dx+2\varepsilon\|\rho^{1/2}\|_{\infty}^{2}\|\mu^{k}-\mu\|_{{\mathscr{M}}}^{2}.

Thus
\limsup_{k\to\infty}\|\rho^{1/2}*(\mu^{k}-\mu)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\|\rho^{1/2}\|_{\infty}\sup_{k}\|\mu^{k}-\mu\|_{{\mathscr{M}}}
.
Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, and
\{\mu^{k}-\mu\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded by weak-
*
convergence, it follows that
\rho^{1/2}*(\mu^{k}-\mu)\to 0
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

For
Item

4
we note that since
V_{\rho^{1/2}}
is by assumption fundamental for
C_{0}(\Omega)
, we can for any
\varphi\in C_{0}(\Omega)
and
\varepsilon>0
find a finite set
U_{\varepsilon}\subset\Omega
and coefficients
\{\alpha_{y}\}_{y\in U_{\varepsilon}}\subset\mathbb{R}
such that

\sup_{x\in\Omega}\Biggl|\varphi(x)-\sum_{y\in U_{\varepsilon}}\alpha_{y}\rho^{1/2}_{y}(x)\Biggr|<\varepsilon.

Then, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\langle\varphi|\mu^{k}-\mu\rangle=\sum_{y\in U_{\varepsilon}}\langle\alpha_{y}\rho^{1/2}_{y}|\mu^{k}-\mu\rangle+\Biggl\langle\varphi-\sum_{y\in U_{\varepsilon}}\alpha_{y}\rho^{1/2}_{y}\Biggm|\mu^{k}-\mu\Biggr\rangle.

(9)

The assumption
\|\mu^{k}-\mu\|_{\mathscr{D}}\to 0
readily implies
\rho^{1/2}*(\mu^{k}-\mu)\to 0
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Hence, there exists a subsequence
\{k_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\{k\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\rho^{1/2}*(\mu^{k_{j}}-\mu)\to 0
almost everywhere.
Since, by the Heine–Borel theorem,
\Omega
is bounded, and
\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\|\mu^{k}-\mu\|_{{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)}\leq M
for some
M>0
,

Lemma

2.4
shows that the family
\{\rho^{1/2}*(\mu^{k}-\mu)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly equicontinuous on
\Omega
.
By a compact covering argument, it follows that
\rho^{1/2}*(\mu^{k_{j}}-\mu)\to 0
uniformly on
\Omega
.
In particular,
|\langle\alpha_{y}\rho^{1/2}_{y}|\mu^{k_{j}}-\mu\rangle|<\varepsilon/\#U_{\varepsilon}
for all
y\in U_{\varepsilon}
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
large enough.
It follows from (
9
) that

|\langle\varphi|\mu^{k_{j}}-\mu\rangle|\leq(1+M)\varepsilon\quad\text{for $j$ large enough.}

(10)

Since
\varepsilon>0
and
\varphi\in C_{0}(\Omega)
were arbitrary, we deduce
\mu^{k_{j}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox to10.00002pt{\hss\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{*\,}$}\hss}\hss}\hbox{$\rightharpoonup$}}\mu
.
If there would exist a subsequence
\{\mu^{k_{\ell}}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\{\mu^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
not weakly-
*
convergent to
\mu
, then we could find a further unrelabelled subsequence,
\varepsilon>0
, and
\varphi\in C_{0}(\Omega)
such that
|\langle\varphi|\mu^{k_{\ell}}-\mu\rangle|\geq 2(1+M)\varepsilon
for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. Repeating the above arguments, we would obtain (
10
), hence a contradiction.
Therefore
\mu^{k}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox to10.00002pt{\hss\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{*\,}$}\hss}\hss}\hbox{$\rightharpoonup$}}\mu
.

.

Let all the assumptions of
Theorem

2.6

Item

4
hold.
Then
\|\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,\|_{\mathscr{D}}
is a norm on
{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)
.

Proof 2.9
.

Based on the discussion that in the beginning of

, it only remains to prove that

.
Indeed, setting

, we have

.

.

3
Convolution kernels and sensor grids

We will need the data term
F(\mu)=\frac{1}{2}\|A\mu-b\|^{2}
to satisfy a smoothness property also known as the “descent lemma” with respect to the wave-particle norm
\|\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,\|_{\mathscr{D}}
. As shown in, e.g.,
[
15
, Lemma 7.1]
, this in general follows from
F^{\prime}
being Lipschitz. In our case the Lipschitz property needs to be with respect to the
{\mathscr{D}}
-seminorm.
For quadratic
F
as in (
1
), this requirement reduces to

A_{*}A\leq L{\mathscr{D}}\quad\text{for some}\quad L>0.

This means that we have to find
{\mathscr{D}}
such that
L{\mathscr{D}}-A_{*}A
is positive semi-definite.
We could take
{\mathscr{D}}=A_{*}A
and
L=1
and still obtain a theoretical algorithm with convergent function values, although in general not the weak-
*
convergence of iterates.
However, the proximal step of the method that we develop in the next section consists of finding new points
x
to insert into the support of
\mu^{k+1}
to approximately satisfy first-order optimality conditions for (
2
) with
\frac{1}{2}\|\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,\|_{\mathscr{D}}
as the proximal penalty.
To avoid such an insertion from perturbing optimality conditions globally, it will be practical if
{\mathscr{D}}\delta_{x}
is localised around
x
. This can be achieved when
{\mathscr{D}}
is a simple convolution operator.
However, even for physical processes that can be modelled as convolutions,
A_{*}A
is in general not
exactly
a convolution operator when the range of
A
consists of measurements on a
finite
sensor grid, and its effects are not localised:
A^{*}A\delta_{x}
may have multiple peaks.

We will, therefore, first in
Section

3.1
construct upper approximations by convolution operators
{\mathscr{D}}
for forward operators
A
modelling measurements of convolved signals on a finite sensor grid.
We provide specific examples in
Section

3.2
.
Although we work with convolution operators in this section, in the following sections on our proposed algorithms, there is in general no need for
A
or
{\mathscr{D}}
to be based on convolution.

3.1
Sensor grids

We now construct some example forward operators
A
, based on a simple physical spreading process followed by sensing. More precisely, the
spread

\psi\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a convolution kernel that models the physical spreading process of, e.g., light. If a source of unit intensity is at
x
, then
\psi(y-x)
is the intensity that falls at
y
. This light is sensed by the
sensor

\theta_{z}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
placed at
z
on a finite
grid

\mathscr{G}
with the sensitivity
\theta_{z}(y)
. With this we define the forward operator
A
as

A\mu:=(\mu(\theta_{z}*\psi))_{z\in\mathscr{G}},\quad\text{i.e.,}\quad[A\mu]_{z}=\int(\theta_{z}*\psi)(x)\,d\mu(x)\quad\text{for}\quad z\in\mathscr{G}.

(11)

The sensors or, more precisely, the field-of-views of the sensors, are a technical necessity for our proof. They can, nevertheless, also be physically justified through practical light sensors having positive area instead of just sensing the light falling at a singular point.
For
A
of this form, the next lemma establishes an upper bound on
A_{*}A
in terms of a simple convolution operator.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let the
sensor grid

\mathscr{G}
be an arbitrary finite index set with corresponding
sensors

0\leq\theta_{z}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, (
z\in\mathscr{G}
), satisfying for some
L_{0}\geq 0
the bound

L_{0}\geq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sum_{z\in\mathscr{G}}\theta_{z}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\theta_{z}(w)\,dw.

(12)

Pick a symmetric
spread

\psi\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and on a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define
A\in\mathbb{L}({\mathscr{M}}(\Omega);\mathbb{R}^{\#\mathscr{G}})
by (
11
).
Then the pre-adjoint
A_{*}\in\mathbb{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n};C_{0}(\Omega))
exists and is given by
A_{*}y=\sum_{z\in\mathscr{G}}y_{z}\theta_{z}*\psi
, and we have

A_{*}A\leq L_{0}\langle\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,|\mathscr{A}[\psi]*\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,\rangle.

Proof 3.2
.

We easily verify that
(A_{*})^{*}=A
.
A_{*}
is also bounded since
A^{*}=JA_{*}
where
J
is the canonical injection
C_{0}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)^{*}
.
We have

\langle\mu|A_{*}A\mu\rangle=\|A\mu\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{z\in\mathscr{G}}\left(\int\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\theta_{z}(w)\psi(x-w)\,dw\,d\mu(x)\right)^{2}

Recalling that
\theta_{z}\geq 0
, defining
I_{\theta_{z}}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\theta_{z}(w)\,dw\in[0,\infty)
, and using Hölder’s inequality, we continue

\displaystyle\langle\mu|A_{*}A\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{z\in\mathscr{G}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int\psi(x-w)\,d\mu(x)\theta_{z}(w)\,dw\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{z\in\mathscr{G}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\theta_{z}(w)\,dw\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\int\psi(x-w)\,d\mu(x)\right)^{2}\theta_{z}(w)\,dw.

Using (
12
) and the symmetricity of
\psi
, it follows

\displaystyle\langle\mu|A_{*}A\mu\rangle

\displaystyle\leq L_{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\int\psi(x-w)\,d\mu(x)\right)^{2}\,dw

\displaystyle=L_{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int\psi(x-w)\,d\mu(x)\int\psi(y-w)\,d\mu(y)\,dw

\displaystyle=L_{0}\int\int\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\psi(x-w)\psi(w-y)\,dw\,d\mu(y)\,d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=L_{0}\langle\mu|\mathscr{A}[\psi]*\mu\rangle.

This establishes the claim.

Example 3.3

(Rectangular sensors)
.

Let
\theta_{z}(x)=\chi_{[-b,b]^{n}}(x-z)
for some
0<b<\inf_{z,\tilde{z}\in\mathscr{G}}\|z-\tilde{z}\|_{\infty}
.
Then (
12
) is satisfied by
L_{0}=(2b)^{n}
.

Having bounded
A_{*}A
by
\langle\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,|\mathscr{A}[\psi]*\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,\rangle
, we now need to bound
\langle\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,|\mathscr{A}[\psi]*\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,\rangle
by
{\mathscr{D}}
to establish the overall bound
A_{*}A\leq L{\mathscr{D}}
. This is done in the next theorem.

Theorem 3.4

(Putting it all together)
.

Suppose the “spread” and the “kernel”
\psi,\rho\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfy

\mathscr{F}[\psi]^{2}\leq L_{1}\mathscr{F}[\rho]

(13)

Also assume
0\leq\theta_{z}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
for all
z\in\mathscr{G}
on a finite grid
\mathscr{G}\subset\mathbb{R}
with
L_{0}
satisfying (
12
).
For a closed
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define

A\mu:=(\mu(\theta_{z}*\psi))_{z\in\mathscr{G}}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathscr{D}}\mu:=\rho*\mu\quad\text{for}\quad\mu\in{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega).

(14)

Then a pre-adjoint
A_{*}\in\mathbb{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n};C_{0}(\Omega))
is given by
A_{*}y=\sum_{z\in\mathscr{G}}y_{z}\theta_{z}*\psi
, and we have

A_{*}A\leq L_{0}L_{1}{\mathscr{D}}.

(15)

Proof 3.5
.

Due to
Lemma

3.1
, we only need to show that
\langle\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,|\mathscr{A}[\psi]*\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,\rangle\leq L_{1}{\mathscr{D}}
.
By Bochner’s theorem (see
[
11
, Chapter 12]
or the proof of
Theorem

2.6
), and the Fourier transform convolution-multiplication exchange rule (see, e.g.,
[
31
, Theorem 7.19]
), this amounts to (
13
).

3.2
Examples

One choice satisfying
Theorem

3.4
with
L_{1}=1
would be
\rho=\mathscr{A}[\psi]
.
This could also easily be made to satisfy all the additional conditions of
Theorem

2.6
with
\rho^{1/2}=\psi
, so would be an option if we were able to calculate
\mathscr{A}[\psi]
efficiently.
For numerical reasons, both
\rho
and
\psi
should also have small support, which is not the case for a Gaussian
\psi
.
Therefore, we would need to cut the spread to ensure small compact support.
The next example, on the other hand, avoids even forming the autoconvolution by taking
\rho=\psi
. Why we call the choice “fast” will be apparent once stumble onto Gauss error functions when treating a cut Gaussian spread.

To proceed, we define for
b>0
the hat function

\tri_{b}(t):=\frac{1}{b}\mathscr{A}[\chi_{[-b/2,b/2]}](t)=\begin{cases}1-|t|/b,&t\in(-b,-b),\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(16)

We also recall for
a>0
that (see, e.g.,
[
25
, Appendix 3]
)

\mathscr{F}[\chi_{[-a,a]}]=\mathscr{F}[\rect(\tfrac{1}{2a}\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,)]=2a\sinc(2\pi a\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,).

(17)

for the (unnormalised)
\sinc t:=\sin t/t
.

Example 3.6

(“Fast” spread)
.

For some
\sigma>0
, let
\rho=\psi
and for
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\psi(x)=\frac{4}{\sigma}\mathscr{A}[\tri_{1/2}](x/\sigma)=\frac{4}{\sigma}\begin{cases}\frac{2}{3}(x/\sigma+1)^{3}&-1<x/\sigma\leq-\frac{1}{2},\\
2|x/\sigma|^{3}-2(x/\sigma)^{2}+\frac{1}{3}&-\frac{1}{2}<x/\sigma\leq\frac{1}{2},\\
-\frac{2}{3}(x/\sigma-1)^{3}&\frac{1}{2}\leq x/\sigma<1,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Also let
\theta_{z}(x)=\chi_{[-b,b]}(x-z)
for some
0<b<\inf_{z,\tilde{z}\in\mathscr{G}}|z-\tilde{z}|
on the finite grid
\mathscr{G}\subset\mathbb{R}
, and define
A
and
{\mathscr{D}}
according to (
14
).
Clearly
\supp\psi=\supp\rho=[-\sigma,\sigma]
.
It can also be verified that
\int\psi\,dx=1
.
Writing
\hat{\psi}:=\mathscr{F}[\psi]
, we expand using standard Fourier transform dilation and convolution rules (see, e.g.,
[
25
, Appendix 3]
), (
16
), and (
17
) that

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}[\psi](\xi)

\displaystyle=4\mathscr{F}[\mathscr{A}[\tri_{1/2}]](\sigma\xi)=4\mathscr{F}[\tri_{1/2}](\sigma\xi)^{2}

\displaystyle=16\mathscr{F}[\chi_{[-1/4,1/4]}](\sigma\xi)^{4}=16\left(\frac{1}{2}\sinc(\pi\sigma\xi/2)\right)^{4}=\sinc(\pi\sigma\xi/2)^{4}\quad(\xi\in\mathbb{R}).

Since
\sinc
achieves its maximum at
\xi=0
with value 1, it follows that
\mathscr{F}[\psi]^{2}\leq\mathscr{F}[\rho]
.
Thus we may apply
Theorem

3.4
with
L_{1}=1
and
L_{0}
given by
Example

3.3
. This gives the bound

A_{*}A\leq 2b{\mathscr{D}}.

Obviously
\rho^{1/2}=\frac{2}{\sigma}\tri_{1/2}(\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,/\sigma)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
The above calculations establish that
\int_{U}\mathscr{F}[\rho^{1/2}](\xi)\,d\xi=\int_{U}\sinc(\pi\sigma\xi/2)^{2}\,d\xi>0
for every open set

, so that (
7
) holds for
\rho^{1/2}
.
Therefore
[
11
, Theorem 18.1]
proves that

is fundamental on compact sets
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Now
Theorem

2.6
shows that
\|\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,\|_{\mathscr{D}}
characterises weak-
*
convergence on such sets.

Finally, to apply
A
, we need to be able to calculate
\theta_{z}*\psi=\chi_{[-b,b]}*v
.
This is a piecewise polynomial, so easily implemented in software, although sizeable to write down case-by-case.

To base the spread
\psi
on a Gaussian, we need to cut it to have a compact support.
We therefore next construct
\psi=\chi v
for a cut-off function
\chi
and a base spread
v
.
Since
\rho=\mathscr{A}[\psi]
can be numerically unwieldy, we also take
\rho=\varphi u
for

\varphi=\mathscr{A}[\chi]
and a computationally simple base kernel
u
, in our numerical practise
u=v
.
The condition (
18
) roughly says that the base spread
v
cannot be more localised in space than the base kernel
u
.

In the following, we use the abbreviations
\hat{u}=\mathscr{F}[u]
,
\hat{v}=\mathscr{F}[v]
, etc., without explicit mention.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
u,v,\chi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Then (
13
) holds if
\hat{v}\geq 0
with
\int\hat{v}\,d\xi<\infty
, and, for some
L_{1}\geq 0
we have

I_{\hat{v}}\hat{v}\leq L_{1}\hat{u}.

(18)

Proof 3.8
.

Since
\mathscr{F}[\mathscr{A}(\chi)u](\xi)=[\hat{\chi}^{2}*\hat{u}](\xi)
by standard Fourier transform exchange rules for convolution and multiplication, we need to prove for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
that

\mathscr{F}[\chi v](\xi)^{2}\leq[\hat{\chi}^{2}*\hat{u}](\xi).

If
I_{\hat{v}}:=\int\hat{v}\,d\xi=0
, also
v=0
, so this is immediate by the assumed non-negativity of
\hat{u}
. So suppose
I_{\hat{v}}>0
.
Since, by assumption
I_{\hat{v}}<\infty
and
\hat{v}\geq 0
, we have
\hat{v}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Now Hölder’s inequality shows as claimed that

(\hat{\chi}*\hat{v})(\xi)^{2}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\hat{\chi}(z)\hat{v}(\xi-z)\,dz\right)^{2}\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\hat{v}(\xi-z)\,dz\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\hat{\chi}(z)^{2}\hat{v}(\xi-z)\,dz\right)=I_{\hat{v}}[\hat{\chi}^{2}*\hat{v}](\xi).

If we want
\|\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,\|_{\mathscr{D}}
defined with the kernel
\rho=\varphi u
to characterise weak convergence we need to show for
Theorem

2.6
the existence of
\rho^{1/2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and that
V_{\rho^{1/2}}(\Omega)
is fundamental. Unfortunately, we have no simple characterisation of the requirement
\rho^{1/2}\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, although it would hold by properties of the Fourier transform if
\rho
were a rapidly decreasing function (i.e., a test function for tempered distributions)
[
31
, Chapter 7]
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Suppose
\rho=\varphi u
for symmetric
\varphi,u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
\hat{\varphi},\hat{u}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\hat{\varphi},\hat{u}\geq 0
. Then there exists
\rho^{1/2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
\rho=\mathscr{A}[\rho^{1/2}]
.
If
\hat{\rho}(\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,)^{1/2}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then, moreover,
\rho^{1/2}\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Proof 3.10
.

We have
\hat{\rho}=\hat{\varphi}*\hat{u}\geq 0
with all of the Fourier transforms real-valued and symmetric by the corresponding properties of
\varphi
and
u
.
Since
\hat{\varphi},\hat{u}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, standard properties of convolutions imply
\hat{\rho}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

We choose a self-adjoint and positive semi-definite particle-to-wave operator

Together with the accuracy requirement, the finite-dimensional subproblem amounts to finding non-negative factors
\{\beta_{x}\}_{x\in S_{\ell}}
such that
\mu_{\ell}:=\sum_{x\in S_{\ell}}\beta_{x}\delta_{x}
satisfies for some
1\geq w\in C_{0}(\Omega)
with
w(y)=1
if
\beta_{y}>0
the bounds

-\frac{\kappa\varepsilon_{k+1}}{1+\|\mu_{\ell}\|_{{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)}}\leq[{\mathscr{D}}\mu_{\ell}+\eta_{k}+\lambda w](y)\leq\frac{\kappa\varepsilon_{k+1}}{1+\|\mu_{\ell}\|_{{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)}}\quad\text{for all}\quad y\in S_{\ell}.

(24)

Since
\kappa\in(0,1)
,
w=1
on
\supp\mu_{\ell}
, and in the parametrisation of
insert_and_adjust
,
\lambda=\tau\alpha
and
\eta_{k}=\tau v^{k}-{\mathscr{D}}\breve{\mu}^{k}
, it follows that
\mu^{k+1}=\mu_{\ell}
thus constructed satisfies the upper bound of (
22a
) as well as (
22b
).
The function
insert_and_adjust
only returns when the lower bound of (
22a
) is also satisfied.
Therefore, it remains to prove that the lower bound of (
22a
) is satisfied for large enough
\ell
.

Since
{\mathscr{D}}
is strictly positive definite,
\rho(0)=\langle{\mathscr{D}}\delta_{x}|\delta_{x}\rangle>0
for any
x\in\Omega
. Since
\rho
is continuous, there exists
R>0
such that
\rho\geq\frac{1}{2}\rho(0)\chi_{B(0,R)}
.
The upper bound of (
24
) ensures for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
y\in S_{\ell}^{>}:=\{y\in S_{\ell}\mid\beta_{y}>0\}
that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\rho(0)\|\mu_{\ell}\llcorner B(y,R)\|_{{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)}=\sum_{x\in S_{\ell}}\frac{1}{2}\beta_{x}\rho(0)\chi_{B(y,R)}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x\in S_{\ell}}\beta_{x}\rho(y-x)={\mathscr{D}}\mu_{\ell}(y)\leq-[\eta_{k}+\lambda](y)+\kappa\varepsilon_{k+1}.

Since
\eta_{k}\in C_{0}(\Omega)
, it is bounded on the compact set
\Omega
.
Therefore, by the above, there exists
M>0
such that
\|\mu_{\ell}\llcorner B(y,R)\|_{{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)}\leq M
for all for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
y\in S_{\ell}^{>}
.
By the finite Vitali covering lemma, see, e.g.,
[
27
, Proof of Theorem 2.1]
, there exists a subcollection

such that
S_{\ell}^{>}\subset\bigcup_{y\in S_{\ell}^{>}}B(y,R/3)\subset\bigcup_{y\in\tilde{S}_{\ell}}B(y,R)
, and the latter balls are disjoint.
Thus

\|\mu_{\ell}\|_{{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)}=\bigl\|\mu_{\ell}\llcorner S_{\ell}^{>}\bigr\|_{{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)}=\sum_{y\in\tilde{S}_{\ell}}\|\mu_{\ell}\llcorner B(y,R)\|_{{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)}\leq\#\tilde{S}_{\ell}M.

Since
\Omega
is bounded,
\#\tilde{S}_{\ell}
is bounded by a constant dependent on
R
and
\Omega
alone; see, e.g.,
[
27
, Lemma 2.6]
.
Therefore
\{\|\mu_{\ell}\|_{{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
is bounded.

Lemma

2.4
now shows that the family
\{{\mathscr{D}}\mu_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly equicontinuous.
Since
w(y)\leq 1
, the lower bound in (
24
) holds with

replaced by
1
.
Therefore, there must exist
r>0
such that

[{\mathscr{D}}\mu_{\ell}+\eta_{k}+\lambda](x)\geq-\frac{\kappa+1}{2}\varepsilon_{k+1}>-\varepsilon_{k+1}\quad\text{for all}\quad x\in B(y,r),\quad y\in S_{\ell},\quad\text{and}\quad\ell\geq 1.

(25)

It follows that

for all

, meaning that

4.2
does not insert new points into

in the

-neighbourhoods of existing points.
Since a finite number of such neighbourhoods cover the compact set

4.2
must terminate in a finite number of steps.

Remark 4.5

(Insertion count)
.

The proof of

4.3

-balls with non-intersecting centres, that can be packed into

.

Remark 4.6

.

According to

Remark 4.7

(Unconstrained problem)
.

If

without the positivity constraint, the inclusion

for all

In

4.2
all we have to change is:

On

.

2.

On

4.3

We now embark on proving the convergence

We require some basic regularity and step length conditions.

Assumption 4.8
.

We assume that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is compact, the regularisation parameter

, and that

(i)

The operator

:

(iii)

The step length
\tau>0

(strictly for weak-

Remark 4.9

(Compactness)
.

The compactness assumption on

is only due to

4.3
on the finite termination of

4.2
. It could be removed if (

) is otherwise ensured on

Example 4.10
.

For

with

and

Our convergence proofs are based on the following simplified (scalar-tested, without strong convexity) version of
[

, Theorem 2.3]
with the addition of inexact solutions (

).
It includes inertial parameters

that we need in
Section

5
.
For non-inertial methods we take
\lambda_{k}\equiv 1
and
z^{k}\equiv\breve{x}^{k}\equiv x^{k}

Lemma 4.11
.

On a Banach space
X
with predual
X_{*}
, let
F,G:X\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
with
F
transport-subdifferentiable and
F
3-point smooth.
For an initial
x^{0}=\breve{x}^{0}\in X
, let
\{x^{k+1}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{\breve{x}^{k+1}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be defined for some
\lambda_{k}>0
and
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{k+1}\in X_{*}
through

\displaystyle\tilde{\varepsilon}_{k+1}\in\tau_{k}[F^{\prime}(\breve{x}^{k})+\partial G(x^{k+1})]+{\mathscr{D}}(x^{k+1}-\breve{x}^{k})

and

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(z^{k+1}-z^{k})=x^{k+1}-\breve{x}^{k}\quad\text{as well as}\quad\lambda_{k}(z^{k+1}-x^{k})=x^{k+1}-x^{k}.

(27)

Then for any
{\bar{x}}\in X
we have

\frac{\lambda_{k}^{2}}{2}\|z^{k+1}-{\bar{x}}\|_{{\mathscr{D}}}^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{k}^{2}(1-\tau_{k}L)}{2}\|z^{k+1}-z^{k}\|_{{\mathscr{D}}}^{2}+\tau_{k}([F+G](x^{k+1})-[F+G]({\bar{x}}))\\
-(1-\lambda_{k})\tau_{k}([F+G](x^{k})-[F+G]({\bar{x}}))\leq\frac{\lambda_{k}^{2}}{2}\|z^{k}-{\bar{x}}\|_{{\mathscr{D}}}^{2}+\lambda_{k}\langle\tilde{\varepsilon}_{k+1}|z^{k}-\bar{x}\rangle.

In particular, if
\lambda_{k}\equiv 1
and
z^{k}\equiv\breve{x}^{k}\equiv x^{k}
, we have

\frac{1}{2}\|x^{k+1}-{\bar{x}}\|_{{\mathscr{D}}}^{2}+\frac{1-\tau_{k}L}{2}\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|_{{\mathscr{D}}}^{2}+\tau_{k}([F+G](x^{k+1})-[F+G]({\bar{x}}))\leq\frac{1}{2}\|x^{k}-{\bar{x}}\|_{{\mathscr{D}}}^{2}+\langle\tilde{\varepsilon}_{k+1}|x^{k}-\bar{x}\rangle.

Proof 4.12
.

Let
q^{k+1}\in\partial G(x^{k+1})
satisfy
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{k+1}=\tau_{k}[F^{\prime}(\breve{x}^{k})+q^{k+1}]+{\mathscr{D}}(x^{k+1}-\breve{x}^{k})
.
Then

\lambda_{k}\langle\tilde{\varepsilon}_{k+1}|z^{k+1}-\bar{x}\rangle=\lambda_{k}\langle\tau_{k}[F^{\prime}(\breve{x}^{k})+q^{k+1}]+{\mathscr{D}}(x^{k+1}-\breve{x}^{k})|z^{k+1}-\bar{x}\rangle.

(28)

Due to the three-point identity (
5
) and (
27
), we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}\langle{\mathscr{D}}(x^{k+1}-\breve{x}^{k})|z^{k+1}-\bar{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\lambda_{k}^{2}\langle{\mathscr{D}}(z^{k+1}-z^{k})|z^{k+1}-\bar{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{k}^{2}}{2}\|z^{k+1}-z^{k}\|_{{\mathscr{D}}}^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{k}^{2}}{2}\|z^{k+1}-{\bar{x}}\|_{{\mathscr{D}}}^{2}-\frac{\lambda_{k}^{2}}{2}\|z^{k+1}-{\bar{x}}\|_{{\mathscr{D}}}^{2}.

By the definition of the convex pre-subdifferential and the
L
-smoothness of
F
with respect to
{\mathscr{D}}
, using both relationships of (
27
), we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}\langle F^{\prime}(\breve{x}^{k})+q^{k+1}|z^{k+1}-\bar{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\lambda_{k}\langle F^{\prime}(\breve{x}^{k})+q^{k+1}|x^{k+1}-\bar{x}\rangle+(1-\lambda_{k})\langle F^{\prime}(\breve{x}^{k})+q^{k+1}|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{k}\left([F+G](x^{k+1})-[F+G]({\bar{x}})-\frac{L}{2}\|x^{k+1}-\breve{x}^{k}\|_{\mathscr{D}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+(1-\lambda_{k})\left([F+G](x^{k+1})-[F+G](x^{k})-\frac{L}{2}\|x^{k+1}-\breve{x}^{k}\|_{\mathscr{D}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=([F+G](x^{k+1})-[F+G]({\bar{x}}))

\displaystyle-(1-\lambda_{k})([F+G](x^{k})-[F+G]({\bar{x}}))-\frac{L\lambda_{k}^{2}}{2}\|z^{k+1}-z^{k}\|_{\mathscr{D}}^{2}.

Inserting these two expressions into (
28
), the claim follows.

We now readily obtain ergodic function value convergence:

Theorem 4.13

(Ergodic function value convergence)
.

Suppose
4.8
holds and let
\bar{\mu}\in{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)
satisfy
0\in\partial[F+G](\bar{\mu})
.
Let
\{\mu^{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
be generated by
Algorithm

4.1
for some
\mu^{0}\in\mathscr{Z}(\Omega)
with the tolerance sequence
\{\varepsilon_{k+1}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(0,\infty)
satisfying

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\varepsilon_{k+1}=0.

(29)

Define the
ergodic iterate

\tilde{\mu}^{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\mu^{k}
.
Then
[F+G](\tilde{\mu}^{N})\to[F+G](\bar{\mu})
, more precisely,

[F+G](\tilde{\mu}^{N})\leq[F+G](\bar{\mu})+\frac{1}{N\tau}\left((\kappa+\|\bar{\mu}\|_{{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)})\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\varepsilon_{k+1}+\frac{1}{2}\|\mu^{0}-\bar{\mu}\|_{{\mathscr{D}}}^{2}\right).

Proof 4.14
.

Algorithms

4.1
,
4.3
and
4.8
ensure (
22
) for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Therefore
Lemma

4.1
provides
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{k+1}\in\partial E_{k}(\mu^{k+1})
satisfying (
23
).
The case
\lambda_{k}\equiv 1
of
Lemma

4.11
with
\tau_{k}\equiv\tau
now establishes

\frac{1}{2}\|\mu^{k+1}-\bar{\mu}\|_{\mathscr{D}}^{2}+\frac{1-\tau L}{2}\|\mu^{k+1}-\mu^{k}\|_{\mathscr{D}}^{2}+\tau[F+G](\mu^{k+1})-\tau[F+G](\bar{\mu})\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\mu^{k}-\bar{\mu}\|_{\mathscr{D}}^{2}+\langle\tilde{\varepsilon}_{k+1}|\mu^{k+1}-\bar{\mu}\rangle.

(30)

Summing over

we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\|\mu^{N}-\bar{\mu}\|_{{\mathscr{D}}}^{2}+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\left(\tau[F+G](\mu^{k+1})-\tau[F+G](\bar{\mu})+\frac{1-\tau L}{2}\|\mu^{k+1}-\mu^{k}\|_{\mathscr{D}}^{2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\mu^{0}-\bar{\mu}\|_{{\mathscr{D}}}^{2}+\tilde{C}_{N},

(Tolerance sequence)
.

Take

Example 4.16

(Controlling the insertion count)

Theorem 4.17

(Function value convergence)
.

Suppose

Proof 4.18
.

Algorithms

4.1
,
4.3
and
4.8
ensure (

) for all

.
Therefore

4.1

Remark 4.20

.

If

Proving metric subregularity for the problem (

Lemma 4.21

,

Sketch of convergence

Following
[
33
]
, see also
[
15
]
, we introduce for some testing parameters
\varphi_{k},\psi_{k}>0
the
testing operator

Z_{k}\in\mathbb{L}(C_{0}(\Omega)\times Y;C_{0}(\Omega)\times Y)
defined by

Z_{k}:=\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{k}\mathop{\mathrm{Id}}&0\\
0&\psi_{k+1}\mathop{\mathrm{Id}}\end{pmatrix}.

We need
Z_{k}M_{k}\in\mathbb{L}({\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)\times Y;C_{0}(\Omega)\times Y)
to be self-adjoint and positive semi-definite. By standard arguments
[
33
,
15
]
this holds when
\omega_{k}=\sigma^{-1}_{k+1}\psi^{-1}_{k+1}\varphi_{k}\tau_{k}
and
\psi_{k}\sigma_{k}=\varphi_{k}\tau_{k}
as well as
\tau_{k}\sigma_{k}A_{*}A<{\mathscr{D}}
with
\{\psi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
non-decreasing.

We then test (
39
) by the application of
\langle Z_{k}\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,|u^{k+1}-{\bar{u}}\rangle
for some
{\bar{u}}\in H^{-1}(0)
. This yields

\displaystyle\varphi_{k}\langle\tilde{\varepsilon}_{k+1}|\mu^{k+1}-\bar{\mu}\rangle

\displaystyle\in\varphi_{k}\tau_{k}\langle\partial G(\mu^{k+1})+A_{*}y^{k+1}|\mu^{k+1}-\bar{\mu}\rangle

(41)

\displaystyle+\psi_{k+1}\sigma_{k+1}\langle\partial F_{0}^{*}(y^{k+1})-A\mu^{k+1}|y^{k+1}-{\bar{y}}\rangle+\langle Z_{k}M_{k}(u^{k+1}-u^{k})|u^{k+1}-{\bar{u}}\rangle.

Using the definition of the convex subdifferential and the three-point identity for
Z_{k}M_{k}
, now

\displaystyle\varphi_{k}\langle\tilde{\varepsilon}_{k+1}|\mu^{k+1}-\bar{\mu}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\|u^{k+1}-{\bar{u}}\|_{Z_{k}M_{k}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|u^{k}-{\bar{u}}\|_{Z_{k}M_{k}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|u^{k+1}-u^{k}\|_{Z_{k}M_{k}}^{2}

(42)

\displaystyle+\varphi_{k}\tau_{k}[G(\mu^{k+1})-G(\bar{\mu})]+\psi_{k+1}\sigma_{k+1}[F_{0}^{*}(y^{k+1})-F_{0}^{*}({\bar{y}})]

\displaystyle+\frac{\psi_{k+1}\sigma_{k+1}\gamma_{F_{0}^{*}}}{2}\|y^{k+1}-{\bar{y}}\|_{Y}^{2}

\displaystyle+\varphi_{k}\tau_{k}[\langle A_{*}y^{k+1}|\mu^{k+1}-\bar{\mu}\rangle-\psi_{k+1}\sigma_{k+1}\langle A\mu^{k+1}|y^{k+1}-{\bar{y}}\rangle.

If now
(\tau_{k},\sigma_{k},\omega_{k})\equiv(\tau,\sigma,1)
, i.e., we consider the unaccelerated algorithm, we can take
\varphi_{k}\equiv 1
and
\psi_{k+1}=\sigma^{-1}\tau
while satisfying the non-negativity and self-adjointness of
Z_{k}M_{k}=ZM
, which is now independent of the iteration. Then (
42
) gives

\frac{1}{2}\|u^{k+1}-{\bar{u}}\|_{ZM}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|u^{k+1}-u^{k}\|_{ZM}^{2}+\tau\mathcal{G}(u^{k+1},{\bar{u}})\leq\frac{1}{2}\|u^{k}-{\bar{u}}\|_{ZM}^{2}+\langle\tilde{\varepsilon}_{k+1}|\mu^{k+1}-\bar{\mu}\rangle,

(43)

where the
Lagrangian gap functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(u,{\bar{u}})

\displaystyle:=G(\mu)-G(\bar{\mu})+F_{0}^{*}(y)-F_{0}^{*}({\bar{y}})+\langle A_{*}y|\mu-\bar{\mu}\rangle-\langle A\mu|y-{\bar{y}}\rangle

\displaystyle=[G(\mu)-F_{0}^{*}({\bar{y}})+\langle A\mu|{\bar{y}}\rangle]-[G(\bar{\mu})-F_{0}^{*}(y)+\langle A\bar{\mu}|y\rangle].

We have
\mathcal{G}(u,{\bar{u}})\geq 0
when
0\in H({\bar{u}})
; compare
[
15
]
.
Summing (
43
) over
k=0,\ldots,N-1
, estimating
\langle\tilde{\varepsilon}_{k+1}|\mu^{k+1}-\bar{\mu}\rangle
with
Lemma

4.1
, and using Jensen’s inequality to pass to ergodic variables, we get:

Theorem 6.1

(Ergodic convergence of the Lagrangian gap functional)
.

Let
{\mathscr{D}}\in\mathbb{L}({\mathscr{M}}(\Omega);C_{0}(\Omega))
be defined as
{\mathscr{D}}\mu=\rho*\mu
on the compact set
\Omega
, where
0\not\equiv\rho\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is symmetric and positive definite, i.e.,
\rho(-y)=\rho(y)
for all
y
, and
\mathscr{F}[\rho]\geq 0
.
Let
{\bar{u}}\in{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)\times Y
with
Y
a Hilbert space satisfy
0\in H({\bar{u}})
, and
\{(\mu^{k},y^{k})\}_{k\geq 1}
be generated by
Algorithm

6.1
for some
(\mu^{0},y^{0})\in\mathscr{Z}(\Omega)\times Y
, step lengths
\tau,\sigma>0
satisfying
\tau\sigma A_{*}A\leq{\mathscr{D}}
, and the tolerance sequence
\{\varepsilon_{k+1}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(0,\infty)
satisfying

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\varepsilon_{k+1}=0;

see
Example

4.15
. Then
\mathcal{G}(\tilde{u}^{N};{\bar{u}})\to 0
, more precisely,

0\leq\mathcal{G}(\tilde{u}^{N};{\bar{u}})\leq\frac{1}{N\tau}\left((\kappa+\|\bar{\mu}\|_{{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega)})\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\varepsilon_{k+1}+\frac{1}{2}\|u^{0}-{\bar{u}}\|_{ZM}^{2}\right).

Weak convergence of the iterates can also be obtained following the arguments of
Theorem

4.24
.
When
F_{0}^{*}
is strongly convex, we can with the step length parameter updates (
40
) obtain
O(1/N^{2})
convergence of the gap functionals and
O(1/N)
convergence of the dual iterates
\{y^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, however, the treatment of the gap functional is very technical; we refer to
[
33
,
15
]
. We therefore skip the treatment of the gap functional, and directly use in (
41
) the fact that
0\in H({\bar{u}})
together with the monotonicity of
\partial G
and the
\gamma_{F_{0}^{*}}
-strong monotonicity of
F_{0}^{*}
.
This gives in place of (
41
) the estimate

\displaystyle\varphi_{k}\langle\tilde{\varepsilon}_{k+1}|\mu^{k+1}-\bar{\mu}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\|u^{k+1}-{\bar{u}}\|_{Z_{k}M_{k}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|u^{k}-{\bar{u}}\|_{Z_{k}M_{k}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|u^{k+1}-u^{k}\|_{Z_{k}M_{k}}^{2}

(44)

\displaystyle+\psi_{k+1}\sigma_{k+1}\gamma_{F_{0}^{*}}\|y^{k+1}-{\bar{y}}\|_{Y}^{2}.

Taking

\varphi_{k}\equiv 1

and

\psi_{k+1}=\psi_{k}(1+2\sigma_{k}\gamma_{F_{0}^{*}}),

we have

Z_{k+1}M_{k+1}=Z_{k}M_{k}+\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&\psi_{k+1}\sigma_{k+1}\gamma_{F_{0}^{*}}\mathop{\mathrm{Id}}\end{pmatrix}+\Xi_{k},

for some skew-adjoint
\Xi_{k}
. Since operator-relative norms are invariant with respect skew-adjoint components, summing (
44
) over
k=0,\ldots,N-1
yields

\frac{1}{2}\|u^{N}-{\bar{u}}\|_{Z_{N-1}M_{N-1}}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|u^{0}-{\bar{u}}\|_{Z_{0}M_{0}}^{2}+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\varphi_{k}\langle\tilde{\varepsilon}_{k+1}|\mu^{k+1}-\bar{\mu}\rangle.

(45)

For some
\kappa\in(0,1)
, we have

Z_{N-1}M_{N-1}\geq\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&(1-\kappa)\psi_{N}\mathop{\mathrm{Id}}\end{pmatrix}

Therefore, dividing (
45
) by
\psi_{N}
, which grows at the rate
\Omega(N^{2})

[
15
, Lemma 10.7]
, we obtain
O(1/N)
convergence of the dual iterates
\{y^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
.

We implemented

Finite-dimensional subproblems

Both conditional gradient methods find a maximiser of
-F^{\prime}(\mu^{k})(\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,)
.
3
3

3

The maximisation of
|F^{\prime}(\mu^{k})(\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,)|
in
[
5
]
corresponds to a version of (
1
) without the non-negativity constraint; compare
Remark

4.7
. The more general cone constraints of
[
29
]
readily treat this constraint.

They add the point to the support
S
of
\mu^{k}
, and then adapt weights by solving the finite-dimensional subproblem

\min_{w}J(w):=F(Pw)+G(Pw),

where
P\in\mathbb{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n};{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega))
maps weights
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to measures
\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}
with support
S=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
. In the finite-dimensional subproblems of
Algorithms

4.1
,
5.1
and
6.1
, by contrast,
F
is linearised, and a quadratic penalty based on
{\mathscr{D}}
is added:

\min_{w}J(w):=\tau[F(\mu^{k})+\langle v^{k},Pw-\mu^{k}\rangle+G(Pw)]+\frac{1}{2}\|Pw-\mu^{k}\|_{\mathscr{D}}^{2}.

The FWr takes a single forward-backward step on each finite-dimensional subproblem after a specific initialisation
[
5
]
.
For our methods and the FWf, we solve the subproblems to the accuracy
\inf_{q\in\partial J(w^{\ell})}\|q\|_{\infty}\leq 0.1\varepsilon_{k+1}
, where
\{\varepsilon_{k+1}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is the overall tolerance sequence.
For FWf we use forward-backward splitting capped at 2000 iterations.
For
Algorithms

4.1
,
5.1
and
6.1
we use a semismooth Newton method (SSN) modelled after
[
23
]
. It is based on the proximal optimality condition

w=\prox_{\tau_{\text{sub}}G\circ P}(w-\tau_{\text{sub}}P^{*}v^{k}-\tau_{\text{sub}}P^{*}{\mathscr{D}}Pw).

SSN can only be used with our methods because
A_{*}A
is in general not positive definite, but
{\mathscr{D}}
is subject to conditions in
Theorem

2.6
.
4
4

4

If the spikes of
\mu^{k}
start grouping very close together,
P_{*}{\mathscr{D}}P
may become ill-conditioned.
If observed, this could be avoided by using a merging heuristic.
Alternatively, a first-order method could be used for the subproblem.

We set the subproblem step length
\tau_{\text{sub}}
in both methods as
0.99
times an estimate of the Lipschitz factor of the gradient of the smooth part of
J
. For the conditional gradient methods this is
n
times an estimate of
\|A_{*}A\|
, and for our methods
n
times an estimate of
\|{\mathscr{D}}\|
.

Branch-and-bound subproblem

To maximise
-F^{\prime}(\mu^{k})(\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,)
for the conditional gradient methods, or to do the corresponding step of
Algorithm

4.2
for our methods, we do branch-and-bound optimisation on a geometric bisection tree representation of weighted sums
\zeta(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\rho(x-x_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\beta_{j}(\theta*\psi)(x-z_{j})
of functions with a small support.
Each node of the tree corresponds to a subcube of the domain, and maintains a list of active, non-zero components of the sum, and rough upper and lower bounds on it, within the subcube.
The sum is, of course, computationally easier if the number of active components is small. To achieve this, we construct the component functions to have the small supports that was one of our guiding principles in
Section

3.2
.

The branch-and-bound maximisation starts by putting the domain
\Omega=[0,1]^{n}
in a priority queue (a binary heap).
Based on upper bound estimates for each cube in the priority queue, it picks the most promising one for refinement. If the maximum of a second-order polynomial model of
\zeta
within the cube is
0.1\varepsilon_{k}
of the rough upper bound, the cube is inserted back into the priority queue, marked as a candidate solution. Otherwise its
2^{n}
subcubes are inserted into the priority queue, unmarked.
When a marked cube is taken from the priority queue, the local approximate maximiser is returned as an approximate maximiser of
\zeta
.
Cubes are pruned from the priority queue when it is apparent that they do not contain the solution.
For further details we refer to our Rust implementation and its documentation.

Merging heuristics

In all algorithms we prune unneeded spikes with zero weights to improve computational performance and result visualisation. The conditional gradient methods are, however,
very
weak at achieving completely zero weights. They instead depend on
merging heuristics
to reduce the number of spikes.
We use the following: if two spikes
w_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}
and
w_{j}\delta_{x_{j}}
of
\mu^{k+1}
satisfy
\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|_{\infty}\leq 0.02
, we merge them as
(w_{i}+w_{j})\delta_{(w_{i}x_{i}+w_{j}x_{j})/(w_{i}+w_{j})}
if doing so does not increase the value of the data term
F
.
Then convergence is not affected.

For our
Algorithms

4.1
,
5.1
and
6.1
this is not true:
non-increase of the data term is
not
sufficient to not affect convergence.
Instead, (
22
) should be maintained. This is significantly more costly.
Therefore, we will
not
use a merging in heuristic.
One is not needed: the methods tend to keep the spike count reasonable even without one.
This stems from the conservative insertion strategy in
Algorithm

4.2
:
if weight optimisation is sufficient to satisfy (
22
), a new point will not be inserted into the support of
\mu^{k+1}
.
However, to improve visual quality, we use the same merging heuristic as with FWr and FWf to postprocess the final iterate.
