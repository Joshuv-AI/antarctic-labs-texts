1
Introduction

The Boggio-Hadamard conjecture states, that for any convex domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
any solution to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}(-\Delta)^{2}u=f,&\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
u=\partial_{\nu}u=0,&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\end{array}\right.

satisfies
u\geq 0
, if
f\geq 0
. Here
\nu
is the outer unit normal of
\Omega
.
This differential equation is also called the clamped plate equation (see e.g.
[
11
]
for more context).
In it
\Omega
is a thin plate clamped at the boundary and subject to an orthogonal force
f
.
Then
u
is the displacement of
\Omega
. Hence the conjecture can also be restated as:

Does upward pushing imply upward bending?

This conjecture was substantiated by Boggio by some explicit formulas for the Greens function in
[
4
]
in case
\Omega
is a ball (see also the letters by Hadamard
[
16
]
and
[
17
]
).
First counterexamples to this conjecture were given by Duffin in
[
7
]
and a little later by Garabedian in
[
10
]
. Today we even have counterexamples with constant
f
, see
[
14
]
and
[
15
]
.
On the other hand by e.g. conformal invariance one can use the ball to construct other domains with positivity preserving, see
[
6
]
.
In general it is not known, what the driving force reagrding
\Omega
behind positivity preserving or a lack thereof is, see e.g. the discussion in
[
11
, § 5, § 6]
and the references therein.

For general elliptic differential operators of higher order positivity preserving cannot be expected, see
[
13
]
.

In
[
5
]
a related problem to (
1.1
) has been discussed, i.e. whether for given
f\geq 0
the boundary value problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}(-\Delta)^{2}u_{\gamma}-\gamma\Delta u_{\gamma}=f,&\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
u_{\gamma}=\partial_{\nu}u_{\gamma}=0,&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\end{array}\right.

(1.1)

has a positive solution
u_{\gamma}
, if
\gamma
is big enough.
\gamma
is called tension and this kind of equation was first used by Bickley in
[
2
]
to model the membrane in microphones.
Here the motivation differs: If
\gamma
becomes bigger, the influence of the laplace term
-\Delta u_{\gamma}
should increase.
Since
-\Delta
satisfies a maximum principle, i.e. also positivity preserving, the chances, that
u_{\gamma}
is nonnegative increases if
\gamma
increases.
This statement was proven rigorously in all dimensions in
[
8
]
and is cited in appendix
A
for the readers convenience (see also
[
12
]
for positivity preserving for all
\gamma\geq 0
in dimension one).

Here we like to search for convex domains in which we have the ’most’ positivity preserving, i.e. for the largest range of
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
.
Let us make the notion of our agenda more precise by letting

R>0
,
f\in L^{\infty}(B_{R}(0))
,
c_{1}>0
,
f\geq 0
,
n=2,3
. Now we consider the boundary value problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}(-\Delta)^{2}u_{\gamma}-\gamma\Delta u_{\gamma}=f,&\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
u_{\gamma}=\partial_{\nu}u_{\gamma}=0,&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\end{array}\right.

(1.2)

for any domain
\Omega\subseteq B_{R}(0)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Since we like to solve this boundary value problem even for nonsmooth
\Omega
we define
u_{\gamma}
to be a (weak) solution of (
1.2
), if and only if
u_{\gamma}\in W^{2,2}_{0}(\Omega)
and for all
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
we have

\int_{\Omega}\Delta u_{\gamma}\Delta\varphi+\gamma\nabla u_{\gamma}\cdot\nabla\varphi-f\varphi=0.

(1.3)

By
\mu_{1}(\Omega)
we will denote the buckling load, a kind of first eigenvalue, given by

\mu_{1}(\Omega):=\inf_{u\in W^{2,2}_{0}(\Omega)-\{0\}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\Delta u|^{2}}{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}}.

(1.4)

\mu_{1}(\Omega)
acts as a lower bound for
\gamma
, because upon reaching it, we would have nonuniqueness or nonexistence of the boundary value problem (
1.2
).
See e.g.
[
11
, § 3.2]
for more details regarding the buckling load.
Next we introduce the set in which we will minimise:

M:=\{\Omega\subseteq B_{R}(0)|\ \Omega\mbox{ is a convex domain and }\mathcal{L}^{n}(\Omega)=c_{1}\}.

(1.5)

We define an energy on
M
by

\gamma_{f}(\Omega):=\inf\{\gamma>-\mu_{1}(\Omega)|\ \forall\tilde{\gamma}\geq\gamma\mbox{ the weak solution to \eqref{eq:1_1} satisfies }u_{\tilde{\gamma}}\geq 0\mbox{ a.e.}\}

(1.6)

The result
[
8
, Thm. 1.1]
(see also the appendix
A
) guarantees, that
\gamma_{f}
is finite for bounded domains
\Omega
with boundary in
C^{4}
.
This note is dedicated to proving the following theorem:

Theorem 1.1
.

There exists an
\Omega_{f}\in M
, such that for all
\Omega\in M
we have

\gamma_{f}(\Omega_{f})\leq\gamma_{f}(\Omega).

This
\Omega_{f}
is an optimal domain for positivity preserving, since the range for
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
is as large as possible.
The strategy of the proof is as follows:
We use the direct variational method. Compactness for a sequence of domains will be achieved w.r.t. Hausdorff distance, see section
2
.
Lower-semicontinuity is at the heart of the proof, in which the convexity of the domains will play a crucial role, see Thm.
2.2
.
The proof itself is given in section
3
.
Finally we discuss some open questions in section
4
.

2
Hausdorff distance and convexity

Here we collect some important results concerning the Hausdorff distance of sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Some of them are focused on convex sets. The Hausdorff distance is defined as

d_{H}(A,B):=\inf\{\varepsilon>0|\ A\subseteq B_{\varepsilon}\mbox{ and }B\subseteq A_{\varepsilon}\}.

(2.1)

Here

A_{\varepsilon}:=\bigcup_{x\in A}B_{\varepsilon}(x)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\ d(x,A)<\varepsilon\}.

For compact sets
d_{H}
is a metric. Furthermore for all compact sets
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
the set

M_{K}:=\{\tilde{K}\subseteq K|\ \tilde{K}\mbox{ compact}\}

is compact with respect to
d_{H}
(see e.g.
[
9
, § 2.10.21]
). Furthermore we have the following result for convex sets, which is also part of the famous Blaschke selection Theorem (see also
[
1
]
for a different approach concerning the convergence of Lebesgue measure and again
[
9
, § 2.10.21]
for the convexity of the limit).
We define convergence of convex domains, i.e. for open convex sets
\Omega_{m},\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
as

d_{H}(\overline{\Omega}_{m},\overline{\Omega})\rightarrow 0,\ m\rightarrow\infty.

(2.2)

The result is then as follows:

Lemma 2.1

(
[
3
]
p.61 & p.62)
.

Let
\Omega_{m}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a sequence of convex domains converging w.r.t. to
d_{H}
to
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

\mathcal{L}^{n}(\Omega_{m})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}^{n}(\Omega)

and
\Omega
is convex.

The next Theorem will greatly help in showing that the limit of some sequences considered in the proof of the main result will satisfy a differential equation weakly. The proof is inspired by
[
21
, Prop. 10]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\Omega_{m}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a sequence of convex open domains converging w.r.t. (
2.2
) to an open convex
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
K\subset\Omega
be compact in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then there exists an
m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, such that for all
m\geq m_{0}
we have

K\subset\Omega_{m}.

Proof.

We proceed by contradiction and assume after possibly choosing a subsequence, that

\forall m\in\mathbb{N}\ \exists x_{m}\in K:\ x_{m}\notin\Omega_{m}.

Since
K
is compact, there exists an
r>0
independent of
m
, such that

B_{r}(x_{m})\subseteq\Omega\mbox{ for all }m\in\mathbb{N}.

Since
\Omega_{m}
is convex, the Hahn-Banach seperation theorem, see e.g.
[
20
, Thm 3.4]
, yields
L_{m}\in\partial B_{1}(0)
,
\alpha_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
, such that

\Omega_{m}\subseteq\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\ \langle L_{m},y\rangle\geq\alpha_{m}\}\mbox{ and }\langle L_{m},x_{m}\rangle\leq\alpha_{m}.

Here
\langle\cdot,\cdot,\rangle
denotes the euclidean scalar product. By e.g. setting
y_{m}:=x_{m}-\frac{r}{2}L_{m}
(see Figure
2.1
), we obtain an
y_{m}\in B_{r}(x_{m})\subseteq\Omega
with

d(y_{m},\Omega_{m})\geq\frac{r}{2}.

Figure 1:
Seperating
x_{m}
from
\Omega_{m}
by Hahn-Banach.

Therefore

y_{m}\notin(\Omega_{m})_{\frac{r}{4}}.

Since
y_{m}\in\Omega
, we have by the definition of Hausdorff distance

d_{H}(\Omega,\Omega_{m})\geq\frac{r}{4}.

This implies that
\Omega_{m}\nrightarrow\Omega
w.r.t. Hausdorff distance, which is a contradiction.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

Theorem
2.2
fails without convexity. Let
\Omega
be any open set and let
x\in\Omega
. Then we define the sequence

\Omega_{R}:=\Omega-B_{R}(x).

Then we have (see also Figure
2
)

\Omega_{R}\rightarrow\Omega\mbox{ w.r.t. }d_{H}\mbox{ for }R\downarrow 0.

Figure 2:
Counterexample for Thm.
2.2
without convexity.

If we now choose any compact set
K\subset\Omega
containing
x
, the result of Theorem
2.2
cannot be expected.

Let us also remark, that the boundary of a convex set is Lipschitz and therefore of Lebesgue measure zero:

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be convex. Then
\partial C
is a Lipschitz boundary and therefore

\mathcal{L}^{n}(\partial C)=0.

Proof.

Since
C
is convex,
\partial C
is locally a graph of a convex function
f
.

f
is therefore also locally Lipschitz.
By covering the boundary with at most countable many balls we then obtain

\mathcal{L}^{n}(\partial C)=0
.
∎

3
Proof of the main Theorem

Proof.

Let
\Omega_{m}\in M
be a minimising sequence for
\gamma_{f}
, i.e.

\gamma_{f}(\Omega_{m})\rightarrow\inf_{\Omega\in M}\gamma_{f}(\Omega),\mbox{ for }m\rightarrow\infty.

Then
\overline{\Omega}_{m}\subseteq\overline{B_{R}(0)}
and after relabeling
\overline{\Omega}_{m}
converges w.r.t. the Hausdorff distance
d_{H}
to a compact set
K\subseteq\overline{B_{R}(0)}
. By Lemma
2.1

K
is convex and
\mathcal{L}^{n}(K)=c_{1}>0
. Hence by Lemma
2.4

\mathcal{L}^{n}(\partial K)=0

and we set
\Omega_{f}:=\dot{K}=K-\partial K
, i.e. the topological inner part of
K
. Since the measure of it is positive, it is not empty. Furthermore it is still convex and satisfies

\mathcal{L}^{n}(\Omega_{f})=c_{1},

hence
\Omega_{f}\in M
. Now we need to show a lower semi-continuity estimate. For this let

\gamma>\inf_{\Omega\in M}\gamma_{f}(\Omega)

be fixated. For
m
big enough we have

\gamma>\gamma_{f}(\Omega_{m})\geq-\mu_{1}(\Omega_{m}),

hence we have unique weak solutions
u_{m}\in W^{2,2}_{0}(\Omega_{m})
to (
1.2
) on
\Omega_{m}
. We extend these by
0
to the whole of
B_{R}(0)
. Therefore we have

u_{m}\in W^{2,2}_{0}(B_{R}(0)).

We find an
\alpha>0
such that after choosing a subsequence and relabeling, we obtain

\gamma-\alpha>\gamma_{f}(\Omega_{m})\geq-\mu_{1}(\Omega_{m}).

This yields

\gamma-\alpha+\mu_{1}(\Omega_{m})>0\mbox{ for all }m\in\mathbb{N}.

If we now choose
\delta,\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
small enough, we also have

(1-\delta)\mu_{1}(\Omega_{m})+\gamma-\varepsilon^{\prime}>0.

Since
C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega_{m})
is dense in
W^{2,2}_{0}(\Omega_{m})
, the solution
u_{m}
is an admissible test function. Hence Poincarés and Youngs inequality yield

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{m}}(\Delta u_{m})^{2}+\gamma|\nabla u_{m}|^{2}=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{m}}fu_{m}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(\varepsilon,\operatorname{diam}(\Omega_{m}))\int_{\Omega_{m}}f^{2}+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{m}|^{2}

for every
\varepsilon>0
. Rearranging yields with the above choosen
\delta>0

\delta\int_{\Omega_{m}}(\Delta u_{m})^{2}+(1-\delta)\int_{\Omega_{m}}(\Delta u_{m})^{2}+(\gamma-\varepsilon)\int_{\Omega_{m}}|\nabla u_{m}|^{2}\leq C\int_{\Omega_{m}}f^{2}.

By the definition of
\mu_{1}(\Omega_{m})
, see (
1.4
), we obtain

\delta\int_{\Omega_{m}}(\Delta u_{m})^{2}+((1-\delta)\mu_{1}(\Omega_{m})+(\gamma-\varepsilon))\int_{\Omega_{m}}|\nabla u_{m}|^{2}\leq C(\varepsilon,\operatorname{diam}(\Omega_{m}))\int_{\Omega_{m}}f^{2}.

Choosing
\varepsilon<\varepsilon^{\prime}
yields a constant
C=C(\varepsilon,R,f)>0
such that

\|u_{m}\|_{W^{2,2}_{0}(B_{R}(0))}\leq C.

After choosing a subsequence and relabeling, we can assume there exists a
u_{0}\in W^{2,2}_{0}(B_{R}(0))
, such that

\displaystyle u_{m}\rightarrow u_{0}

\displaystyle\mbox{ weakly in }W^{2,2}_{0}(B_{R}(0))

\displaystyle u_{m}\rightarrow u_{0}

\displaystyle\mbox{ strongly in }W^{1,2}_{0}(B_{R}(0))

\displaystyle u_{m}\rightarrow u_{0}

\displaystyle\mbox{ uniformely on }B_{R}(0).

Here
n=2,3
and the Sobolev embedding has been applied.
Since
u_{m}\geq 0
, the pointwise convergence yields
u_{0}\geq 0
almost everywhere. Now we show, that
u_{0}
satisfies (
1.3
) on
\Omega_{f}
:

Let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega_{f})
be arbitrary. Then

\operatorname{spt}(\varphi)\subset\Omega_{f}

is a compact set. By Theorem
2.2
we find an
m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, such that for all
m\geq m_{0}
we have

\operatorname{spt}(\varphi)\subset\Omega_{m}.

Since the
u_{m}
are solutions to the equation, we therefore have for these
m

0=\int_{B_{R}(0)}\Delta u_{m}\Delta\varphi+\gamma\nabla u_{m}\nabla\varphi-f\varphi.

Then the weak convergence in
W^{2,2}_{0}(B_{R}(0))
yields

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}(0)}\Delta u_{0}\Delta\varphi+\gamma\nabla u_{0}\nabla\varphi-f\varphi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{f}}\Delta u_{0}\Delta\varphi+\gamma\nabla u_{0}\nabla\varphi-f\varphi,

hence
u_{0}
satisfies (
1.3
) on
\Omega_{f}
. Now we have to show that
u_{0}
also satisfies the boundary values, i.e.
u_{0}\in W^{2,2}_{0}(\Omega_{f})
. In a first step we show

\operatorname{spt}(u_{0})\subset\overline{\Omega_{f}}.

Let
x\in B_{R}(0)
such that
u_{0}(x)>0
. Then we find an
m_{0}(x)\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
m\geq m_{0}
we have
u_{m}(x)>0
. In this argument the uniform convergence in the form of pointwise convergence everywhere is central. Hence
x\in\Omega_{m}
. The Hausdorff convergence yields for all
\varepsilon>0
a
z_{m}\in\overline{\Omega_{f}}
such that

x\in B_{\varepsilon}(z_{m})

for
m
big enough. Since
\overline{\Omega_{f}}
is compact, we find a subsequence, such that
z_{m}\rightarrow z_{\varepsilon}\in\overline{\Omega_{f}}
. Furthermore

x\in\overline{B_{\varepsilon}(z_{\varepsilon})}.

If we choose
\varepsilon:=\frac{1}{k}
with
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we can again employ the compactness of
\overline{\Omega_{f}}
, which yields after choosing a subsequence
z_{\frac{1}{k}}\rightarrow z_{0}\in\overline{\Omega_{f}}
. By
x\in\overline{B_{\frac{1}{k}}(z_{\frac{1}{k}})}
we have

x=z_{0}\in\overline{\Omega_{f}}.

Since
x
was choosen arbitrary, we have

\operatorname{spt}(u_{0})\subseteq\overline{\Omega_{f}}.

Now we can show
u_{0}\in W^{2,2}_{0}(\Omega_{f})
. Without loss of generality we assume
0\in\Omega_{f}
. We define

v_{\lambda}(x):=u_{0}(\lambda\cdot x).

Since
\Omega_{f}
is convex, we have for all
\lambda>1

\operatorname{spt}(v_{\lambda})\subsetneq\Omega_{f}.

This yields
v_{\lambda}\in W^{2,2}_{0}(\Omega_{f})
. Furthermore

\|v_{\lambda}\|_{W^{2,2}_{0}(\Omega_{f})}\leq\lambda^{2}\|u_{0}\|_{W^{2,2}_{0}(B_{R}(0))}.

Hence for
\lambda\downarrow 1
we find a weakly convergent subsequence, such that

v_{\lambda_{k}}\rightarrow g\in W^{2,2}_{0}(\Omega_{f}).

Since the dimensions satisfy
n=2,3
, we can employ the Sobolev embedding theorem and by continuity of
u_{0}
obtain

v_{\lambda_{k}}\rightarrow u_{0}\mbox{ pointwise everywhere.}

Without a restriction to the dimension, this step could also be proven by inner regularity theory, see e.g.
[
11
, § 2.5]
.
Either way the limit
g
is always
u_{0}
, irrespective of the choice of subsequence. This yields

v_{\lambda}\rightarrow u_{0}\mbox{ weakly in }W_{0}^{2,2}(\Omega_{f})\mbox{ for }\lambda\downarrow 1\mbox{ and }u_{0}\in W^{2,2}_{0}(\Omega_{f}).

Hence
u_{0}\in W^{2,2}_{0}(\Omega_{f})
is a weak solution of (
1.2
) w.r.t. our choosen
\gamma
and
u_{0}\geq 0
. Therefore

\gamma_{f}(\Omega_{f})\leq\gamma.

Since
\gamma>\inf_{\Omega\in M}\gamma_{f}(\Omega)
arbitrary, we have

\gamma_{f}(\Omega_{f})\leq\inf_{\Omega\in M}\gamma_{f}(\Omega)=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\gamma_{f}(\Omega_{m}).

Hence
\Omega_{f}
is a minimiser in
M
.

As an addendum we can also show, that
\inf_{\Omega\in M}\gamma_{f}(\Omega)\geq-\mu_{1}(\Omega_{f})
. Let
v_{1}\in W^{2,2}_{0}(\Omega_{f})
be such that

\int_{\Omega_{f}}|\nabla v_{1}|\,=1\mbox{ and }\mu_{1}(\Omega_{f})=\int_{\Omega_{f}}|\Delta v_{1}|^{2}\,.

Then there exists a sequence
\varphi_{k}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega_{f})
, such that

\varphi_{k}\rightarrow v_{1}\mbox{ in }W^{2,2}_{0}(\Omega_{f}).

Since the support of
\varphi_{k}
is compact and in
\Omega_{f}
we can apply Theorem
2.2
and find for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
an
m_{0}(k)\in\mathbb{N}
, such that for all
m\geq m_{0}

\varphi_{k}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega_{m}).

Hence

\mu_{1}(\Omega_{f})=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\int_{B_{R}(0)}|\Delta\varphi_{k}|^{2}}{\int_{B_{R}(0)}|\nabla\varphi_{k}|^{2}}\geq\limsup_{m\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{1}(\Omega_{m}).

Therefore

-\mu_{1}(\Omega_{f})\leq-\limsup_{m\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{1}(\Omega_{m})\leq\limsup_{m\rightarrow\infty}\gamma_{f}(\Omega_{m})=\inf_{\Omega\in M}\gamma_{f}(\Omega)

and the result follows.
∎

4
Open problems

In this section we discuss some open problems and possible further lines of inquiry:

•

Numerics: The proof of
[
8
, Thm. 1.1]
is indirect, i.e. it yields no direct control on
\gamma_{f}
. Hence providing a sound numerical ansatz for calculating
\gamma_{f}
seems out of reach at the moment. Finding such an ansatz would be highly beneficial for further research though.

•

Interpolation: One possible way of attacking the numerical problem, would be to show the following interpolation property: Given
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
f>0
and
\gamma_{1}<\gamma_{2}
such that the solution to (
1.2
)
u_{\gamma_{i}}
is nonnegative (
i=1,2
).
Then prove or disprove that for all
\gamma\in]\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}[
the solution
u_{\gamma}
to (
1.2
) is also nonnegative.

•

Regularity: Completely open is the question of higher regularity of the minimiser
\Omega_{f}
of Thm.
1.1
.
Since
\Omega_{f}
is convex, the boundary is rectifiable but it is unclear if it is smooth. The answer will probably depend on properties of
f
.
The hard part is actually finding a good sense of Euler-Lagrange equation for
\Omega_{f}
, if such a thing even exists.
One could possibly try to perform a shape derivative, but since it is unclear how
\Omega\rightarrow\gamma_{f}(\Omega)
behaves under small perturbations of
\Omega
, this is not trivial.
As a first pointer one may want to look at small smooth perturbations of the ball, see e.g.
[
11
, § 6.1.1]
and references therein.

•

Examples: It should be quite useful to be able to actually calculate some explicit values for
\gamma_{f}
for example for an ellipsoid and constant
f
. In dimension one this has been done in
[
12
]
.
In higher dimension there is a useful result for the ball in
[
19
, Thm. 1.4]
, although it is not readily applicable, because the assumptions
c_{i}\leq 0
in
[
19
, Eq. 1.12]
may be a problem.
Similarly
[
18
, Prop. 2.1]
is not directly applicable.

•

Symmetry breaking: The Boggio-Hadamards conjecture was substantiated by the ball. If
f
is radially symmetric, or even constant, one may wonder, if this symmetry transfers over to a minimiser
\Omega_{f}
.
If one chooses
f=0
, then always
\gamma_{f}(\Omega)=-\mu_{1}(\Omega)
. One would therefore look for a maximiser of the buckling load, but it is conjectured, that the minimiser is the ball, see e.g.
[
11
, §3.2]
. Therefore the present author expects a symmetry breaking phenomenon.

•

Convexity: The proof of Theorem
1.1
heavily relies on the domains
\Omega\in M
being convex. If we drop this assumption, it is unclear whether such a minimiser still exists.
On the other hand by
[
8
, Thm 1.1]

\Omega\rightarrow\gamma_{f}(\Omega)
is still well defined, if
\partial\Omega\in C^{4}
, even without
\Omega
being convex.
Here the hard part seems to be to actually obtain a suitable control in some suitable topology for a minimising sequence, which allows for a similar result like Theorem
2.2
.

Appendix A
Positivity for large tension

By the following theorem
\gamma_{f}
is well defined under some regularity conditions.

Theorem A.1

(see Thm. 1.1 in
[
8
]
)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be connected and open with
\partial\Omega\in C^{4}
. Furthermore let
\tau>0
.
Then there exists a
\gamma_{0}=\gamma_{0}(\Omega,\tau)>0
such that for all
f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
with
f\geq 0
and

\int f\,d\mathcal{L}^{n}\geq\tau\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}>0,

we have that the solution
u_{\gamma}
of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}(-\Delta)^{2}u_{\gamma}-\gamma\Delta u_{\gamma}=f,&\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
u=\partial_{\nu}u=0,&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\end{array}\right.

satisfies

u_{\gamma}>0\mbox{ in }\Omega\mbox{ for all }\gamma_{0}\leq\gamma<\infty.
