Modeling of heat conduction through rate equations
Claudio Giorgi, Angelo Morro, Federico Zullo

Abstract
Starting from a classical thermodynamic approach, we derive rate-type equations to describe the behavior of heat flow in deformable media. Constitutive equations are defined in the material (Lagrangian) description where the standard time derivative satisfies the principle of objectivity. The statement of the Second Law is formulated in the classical form and the thermodynamic restrictions are then developed following the Coleman-Noll procedure. However, instead of the Clausius Duhem inequality we consider the corresponding equality where the entropy production rate is prescribed by a non-negative constitutive function. Both the free energy and the entropy production are assumed to depend on a common set of independent variables involving, in addition to temperature, both temperature gradient and heat-flux vector together with their time derivatives. This approach results in rate-type constitutive equations for the heat-flux vector that are intrinsically consistent with the Second Law and easily amenable to analysis. In addition to obtaining already known models (e.g., Cattaneo-Maxwell’s, Jeffreys-like and Green-Naghdi’s heat conductors), this scheme allows us to build new and more complex models of heat transport that may have applications in describing the thermal behavior in nanosystems. Indeed, when higher order time derivatives of the heat flux vector are involved, many different relaxation times occur within the rate equation.



Keywords
: Heat conduction, Rate-type equations, Thermodynamics, Higher-order temperature equations.

Introduction

A huge class of heat conduction models beyond Fourier have been recently developed to account for relaxational and nonlocal effects, fast phenomena or wave propagation, such as being typical for biological systems, nanomaterials or nanosystems. Non-Fourier models mainly differ for their various thermodynamic backgrounds (thermodynamics of irreversible processes, extended irreversible thermodynamics, etc., see for instance
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
). In connection with wave propagation properties many models of heat propagation are presented and discussed in
[
4
]
. Properties concerning their possible practical applications in light of experiments are debated in a recent review
[
5
]
.
Despite the various approaches and procedures developed in the literature (see, e.g.,
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
and references tharein), the topic deserves further attention.
A challenging question is their possible compatibility with the Second Law. This article aims to discuss their deduction in the context of classical Continuum Thermodynamics and possible compatibility with the Second Law stated therein.
The question is of applicative interest, as constitutive equations must not only be consistent with the experimental data but must also be in agreement with the fundamental laws of thermodynamics.

According to
[
10
]
, we develop a new approach to heat conduction theories that is inherently thermodynamic, as it originates directly from the Clausius-Duhem inequality.
The specific production of entropy
\sigma
enters as a non-negative constitutive function, so that the Second Law is automatically satisfied.
The set of independent variables includes only the macroscopically observable fields and their temporal and spatial derivatives, without making any recourse to internal variables or ambiguous state variables (such as thermal displacement). In particular, no constitutive prescription on the heat (or energy) influx
{\bf q}
is made, rather it is treated as an independent variable.
Although other approaches treat both the heat flux and the temperature gradient as independent variables (see, e.g.,
[
11
,
12
]
and references tharein) our scheme has several advantages: a material description is adopted in order to avoid the problem of the objectivity of time derivatives, the rigidity assumption is not necessary and consistency with thermodynamics is much easier to prove than in theories where heat conduction involves histories
[
13
]
, summed histories
[
14
]
or internal variables
[
15
,
16
]
.

The Coleman-Noll procedure
[
17
]
is applied to derive thermodynamic restrictions on the Helmholtz free energy
\psi
.
However, instead of the Clausius Duhem inequality we consider the corresponding equality where the entropy production rate
\sigma
is prescribed by a non-negative constitutive function. Both the free energy and the entropy production are assumed to depend on a common set of independent variables.
This approach is compatible with both rigid and deformable bodies.

In addition to obtaining already known models (e.g., Cattaneo-Maxwell’s, Green-Naghdi’s and Quintanilla’s heat conductors), this strategy, initially proposed in
[
10
]
, allows us to build new and more complex non-Fourier models of heat transport that have applications in describing the thermal behavior of materials where many different relaxation times occur.
In particular in Sect.4 a class of rate-type linear constitutive equations of the second order for the heat flux is discussed in detail. The thermodynamic consistency of the model (originally presented in
[
18
]
and here referred to as Linear Second Order model) is proved here for the first time. In addition, some features of the corresponding temperature equation are highlighted.

2
Balance laws and the thermodynamic principles

We consider a body occupying a three-dimensional time-dependent region
\Omega
.
We let
{\mathcal{R}}
be a chosen reference configuration,
{\bf X}
the vector position in
{\mathcal{R}}
of a material point and
\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}({\bf X},t)
its motion with
t\in\mathbb{R}
the time. Formally,
\Omega=\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}({\mathcal{R}},t)
.
The velocity
{\bf v}
is given by the time derivative
\partial_{t}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}({\bf X},t)
.
A superposed dot denotes the material time derivative,
\nabla
is the gradient operator and then, for any
f({\bf x},t)
, we have
\dot{f}=\partial_{t}f+{\bf v}\cdot\nabla f
. Instead,
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,
is the gradient in the reference configuration.

Let
{\bf F}
be the deformation gradient,
{\bf F}({\bf X},t)=\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}({\bf X},t)
(in suffix notation
F_{i{\scriptscriptstyle K}}=\partial_{X_{\scriptscriptstyle K}}\chi_{i}
), satisfying the constraint
J:=\det{\bf F}>0
, while

{\bf L}:=\nabla{\bf v}
is the velocity gradient,
L_{ij}=\partial_{x_{j}}v_{i}
, which is related to
\dot{\bf F}
as follows

{\bf L}=\dot{\bf F}{\bf F}^{-1}.

(1)

Also,
{\bf D}={\rm sym}{\bf L}
is the stretching where
{\rm sym}
denotes the symmetric part of a tensor and
{\rm tr}\,
denotes the trace, so that
{\rm tr}\,{\bf D}=\nabla\cdot{\bf v}
.

Further,
{\rm Sym}
denotes the set of symmetric second-order tensors and
{\bf 1}
is the identity (or unit) tensor. For any
{\bf A}\in{\rm Sym}
,
{\bf A}>{\bf 0}
or
{\bf A}\geq{\bf 0}
indicate that
{\bf A}
is positive definite or positive semi-definite, while
{\rm Sym}^{+}
denotes the set of symmetric positive-definite tensors.

Let
\varepsilon
be the internal energy density (per unit mass),
{\bf T}
the Cauchy stress,
{\bf q}
the heat flux vector,
\rho
the mass density,
r
the (external) heat supply and
{\bf b}
the mechanical body force per unit mass.
The conservation of mass is expressed in local form by the continuity equation

\dot{\rho}+\rho\nabla\cdot{\bf v}=0.

(2)

The local form of the linear momentum and internal energy balance equations can be written as

\rho\dot{{\bf v}}=\nabla\cdot{\bf T}+\rho{\bf b},

(3)

\rho\dot{\varepsilon}={\bf T}\cdot{\bf L}-\nabla\cdot{\bf q}+\rho r.

(4)

Let
\eta
be the specific entropy function,
{{\bf h}}
the entropy-flux vector and
s
the specific supply of entropy.
All processes which are compatible with equations (
2
)-(
4
) must satisfy the following entropy balance equation,

\rho\,\dot{\eta}+\nabla\cdot{{\bf h}}=\rho s.

The classical local form, usually named after Clausius-Duhem, is obtained by letting

{{\bf h}}=\frac{{\bf q}}{\theta},\qquad s=\frac{r}{\theta}+\gamma,

where
\theta
denotes the (positive) absolute temperature and the quantity
\gamma
is referred to as
specific entropy production

[
17
,
19
,
20
]
;

\rho\dot{\eta}+\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{{\bf q}}{\theta}\right)-\frac{\rho r}{\theta}=\rho\gamma.

(5)

In continuum thermodynamics the local form of the Second Law is established by assuming

\gamma({\bf x},t)\geq 0.

(6)

along any process compatible with all balance equations.
Furthermore, henceforth we assume that the entropy production
\gamma
is given as a constitutive function of the common set of physical variables, as are the internal energy and entropy. Conceptually our contribution follows the same scheme proposed in
[
21
]
, but imposing the fundamental prescription (
6
) on all admissible processes (see also
[
22
, § 2.6]
).

Upon substitution of
\nabla\cdot{\bf q}-\rho r
from the energy equation (
4
) into (
5
) and multiplication by
\theta
we obtain the basic thermodynamic relation

-\rho(\dot{\psi}+\eta\dot{\theta})+{\bf T}\cdot{\bf L}-\frac{1}{\theta}{\bf q}\cdot\nabla\theta=\rho\theta\gamma,

(7)

where
\psi=\varepsilon-\theta\eta
denotes the specific
Helmholtz free energy
.
Due to (
6
), equation (
7
) becomes an inequality that must be satisfied along whatever process (see also
[
17
, eqn.(4.5)]
).
Finally, multiplying (
7
) by
J
and using the identity
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=J\rho
,
we obtain the basic thermodynamic inequality in the material description

\begin{split}-{\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\big(\dot{\psi}+\eta\dot{\theta}\big)+{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\dot{{\bf E}}-\frac{1}{\theta}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta=\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\theta\gamma\geq 0,\end{split}

(8)

where
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,:={\bf F}^{T}\nabla
and

{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}:=J{\bf F}^{-1}{\bf T}{\bf F}^{-T},\qquad{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}:=J{\bf F}^{-1}{\bf q}.

3
A new approach to rate-type constitutive equations

The revised Coleman-Noll procedure for the exploitation of the entropy principle leads to some consequences of the thermodynamic relation (
8
) after specifying three elements;

-

the set
\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
of admissible variables,

-

the free energy density function
\psi=\psi(\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle R})
,

-

the entropy production function
\gamma=\gamma(\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle R})
.

The strategy can be illustrated first in the case of elastic materials with heat conduction and viscosity. First we consider the
set of admissible variables
,
\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=(\theta,{\bf E},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,\dot{{\bf E}})
and assume that both
\psi
and
\gamma
are functions of these variables. Upon evaluation of
\dot{\psi}
and substitution in (
8
) we obtain

\begin{split}\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}(\partial_{\theta}\psi+\eta)\dot{\theta}+(\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\bf E}\psi-{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}})\cdot\dot{{\bf E}}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta}\psi\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}&\\
+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\dot{{\bf E}}}\psi\cdot\ddot{{\bf E}}+\frac{1}{\theta}{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta&=-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\gamma.\end{split}

Neither
\psi
nor
\gamma
depend on
\dot{\theta}
,
\ddot{{\bf E}}
, therefore the linearity and arbitrariness of these variables imply

\psi=\psi(\theta,{\bf E}),\qquad\eta=-\partial_{\theta}\psi.

(9)

As a consequence,
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=(\theta,{\bf E})
can be viewed as the
set of state variables
.
If
\gamma
is independent of
\dot{\bf E}
, that is, if the material is not viscous, then the linearity and arbitrariness of
\dot{\bf E}
also implies

{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}=\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\bf E}\psi.

(10)

Otherwise, the Clausius-Duhem relation for materials with heat conduction and viscosity reads

(\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\bf E}\psi-{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}})\cdot\dot{{\bf E}}+\frac{1}{\theta}{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta=-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\gamma.

Now, we have to specify the functions
\psi(\theta,{\bf E})
and
\gamma(\theta,{\bf E},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,\dot{{\bf E}})
to satisfy this equation and the fundamental requirement
\gamma\geq 0
. For any choice of
\psi
, the simple quadratic function

\gamma=\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{1}(\theta,{\bf E})\dot{\bf E}\cdot\dot{\bf E}+\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{2}(\theta,{\bf E})\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{1},\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{2}
are positive semi-definite tensor-valued functions, satisfies
\gamma\geq 0
and implies

(\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\bf E}\psi+\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{1}\dot{\bf E}-{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}})\cdot\dot{{\bf E}}+\Big(\frac{1}{\theta}{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{2}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta\Big)\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta=0.

This equation holds identically if

{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}=\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\bf E}\psi+\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{1}\dot{\bf E},\qquad{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}=-\theta\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{2}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,

that represent constitutive equations for a Kelvin-Voigt viscoelastic material with Fourier heat conduction.
The choice of
\psi(\theta,{\bf E})
uniquely determines the elastic component of the stress.

To describe this new approach in the case of materials of the rate type, we expand the basic set of Euclidean invariant variables by adding some quantities that are usually
considered as constitutive functions; specifically,
{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}
,
{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
. Hence we let

\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle R}:=(\theta,{\bf E},{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}},{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,\dot{{\bf E}})

(11)

be the set of admissible variables and assume that
\psi
,
\eta
,
\gamma
are scalar-valued functions of
\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
.

In view of the introduction of rate-type constitutive equations, we look for a scheme where
\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
and
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta
, as well as
\dot{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}
and
\dot{{\bf E}}
, are regarded as mutually dependent variables. This actually implies that
{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}
and
{\bf E}
are implicitly dependent, just as happens in anholonomic system described by a set of parameters subject to differential constraints that make their rates mutually dependent.

Following this scheme, a wide range of rate-type models have been derived in recent papers
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
. In particular, nonlinear models for thermo-viscoelastic, viscoplastic and elastic-plastic materials (solids and fluids) subject to large deformations are established.

3.1
Rate-type models of heat conduction

Lately ref.
[
10
]
developed a similar scheme to obtain several models of heat conduction in deformable solids.
The key idea in
[
10
]
is to exploit the formal similarity between scalar products

{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\dot{{\bf E}},\qquad-{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,(\ln\theta),

that appear in (
8
) and represent the mechanical and thermal powers of internal forces, respectively.
By mimicking the procedure adopted in
[
24
,
25
]
with reference to mechanical power, the exchange

{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\ \longleftrightarrow\ {\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\qquad\dot{\bf E}\ \longleftrightarrow\ -\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,(\ln\theta),

allows the construction of heat conduction models of the rate type, both known and new.

For definiteness, upon evaluation of
\dot{\psi}
by virtue of (
11
) and substitution in (
8
) we obtain

\begin{split}\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}(\partial_{\theta}\psi+\eta)\dot{\theta}+(\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\bf E}\psi-{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}})\cdot\dot{{\bf E}}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}\psi\cdot\dot{{\bf T}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}&\\
+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta}\psi\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\dot{{\bf E}}}\psi\cdot\ddot{{\bf E}}+\frac{1}{\theta}{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta&=-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\gamma.\end{split}

The linearity and arbitrariness of
\dot{\theta}
,
\ddot{{\bf E}}
,

\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}
, imply that

\psi=\psi(\theta,{\bf E},{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}},{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}),\qquad\eta=-\partial_{\theta}\psi.

(12)

The functional dependence of
\psi
suggests to define the set of
state variables
as

\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=(\theta,{\bf E},{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}},{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}).

Accordingly, the thermodynamic inequality reduces to

(\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\bf E}\psi-{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}})\cdot\dot{{\bf E}}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}\psi\cdot\dot{{\bf T}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\frac{{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}{\theta}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta=-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\gamma\leq 0,

(13)

where
\dot{{\bf T}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}
,
\dot{{\bf E}}
and
\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
,
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta
, respectively, are assumed to be implicitly dependent.
Since
\psi
is independent of
\dot{{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta
, then letting
\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta={\bf 0}
we can write (
13
) in the form

\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}\psi\cdot\dot{{\bf T}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+(\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\bf E}\psi-{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}})\cdot\dot{{\bf E}}=-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\gamma^{{\scriptscriptstyle E}{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\leq 0,

(14)

where
\gamma^{{\scriptscriptstyle E}{\scriptscriptstyle T}}
is the entropy production density
\gamma
when
\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta={\bf 0}
.
Likewise,

\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+{{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,(\ln\theta)=-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\gamma^{q}\leq 0,

(15)

where
\gamma^{q}
is the entropy production density when
\dot{{\bf T}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}=\dot{{\bf E}}={\bf 0}
.
Furthermore, we let

\gamma=\gamma^{{\scriptscriptstyle E}{\scriptscriptstyle T}}+\gamma^{q}.

The entropy productions
\gamma^{{\scriptscriptstyle E}{\scriptscriptstyle T}}
and
\gamma^{q}
, as well as
\gamma
, are nonnegative constitutive functions to be determined according to the constitutive model.

Disregarding heat conduction and exploiting inequality (
14
), memory properties of viscoelasticity, elastoplasticity and viscoplasticity were modeled with suitable nonlinear rate-type stress-strain relations in
[
23
,
24
]
.
On the contrary, to establish rate-type models of heat conduction we limit our attention to (
15
) and neglect the dependence of constitutive functions on
{{\bf E}}
and
{{\bf T}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}
.

A given rate-type model of heat conduction involving a set of variables
\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
is said to be
consistent with thermodynamics
if there exists at least a pair of functions
\psi(\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle R}),\gamma^{q}(\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle R})
that satisfy the inequality (
15
).
For instance, the Maxwell-Cattaneo-Vernotte (MCV) model (see
[
26
,
27
]
)

\tau{\dot{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+{{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=-\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,\qquad\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\in{\rm Sym},

(16)

is proved to be consistent with thermodynamics by letting (see
[
10
, § 4.2]
)

\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi=\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi_{0}(\theta)+\frac{\tau}{2\theta}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}^{-1}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\qquad\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\gamma^{q}=\frac{1}{\theta^{2}}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}^{-1}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},

where
\kappa
must be positive-definite in order to have
\gamma^{q}\geq 0
.
The sign of
\tau
is not prescribed by thermodynamic arguments. However, the common assumption
\tau>0
implies that
\psi
has a minimum at
{{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}={\bf 0}
;
\tau
is called
relaxation time
and the Fourier law is recovered as
\tau\to 0^{+}
.

3.2
First order rate-type models

Following the scheme devised in
[
10
]
, hereafter we neglect all variables involving stress and strain, but we expand the previously considered set of admissible variables by adding first-order time derivatives of
{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
and
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta
.
Hence we let

\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle R}:=(\theta,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\dot{{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}).

Moreover, let
\psi,\eta,\gamma
be dependent on
\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
. Upon evaluation of
\dot{\psi}
and substitution in (
8
) we obtain

1

1

Hereafter, in agreement with neglecting the dependence of constitutive functions on
{{\bf E}}
and
{{\bf T}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}
, we disregard the mechanical power
{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\dot{\bf E}
. Consequently,
\gamma
stands for
\gamma^{q}
, the entropy production density occurring when
\dot{{\bf T}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}=\dot{{\bf E}}={\bf 0}

The linearity and arbitrariness of
\dot{\theta}
and
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\ddot{\theta}
imply

\psi=\psi(\theta,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta),\qquad\eta=-\partial_{\theta}\psi,

so that the set of state variables turns out to be

and the entropy inequality reduces to

\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\ddot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta}\psi\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}+\frac{{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}{\theta}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta=-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\gamma\leq 0.

(17)

and

Exploiting this procedure in

\psi

.

3.2.1
Green-Naghdi type III (GN III) heat conductors

Derivation with respect to time of the linear constitutive equation of a type III heat conductor according to Green and Naghdi
[
28
]
yields

\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=-\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta-\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta},\qquad\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$},\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\in{\rm Sym}.

(18)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$},\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}
are constant tensors. A differential version of the Fourier law is obtained when
\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}={\bf 0}
.
If we let (see
[
10
, § 5.2]
)

\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\psi=\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\psi_{0}(\theta)+\frac{1}{2\theta}[{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta]\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{-1}[{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta],\qquad\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\gamma=\frac{1}{\theta^{2}}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,

thermodynamic consistency is ensured by
\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\in{\rm Sym}^{+}
. However
\xi
is required to be invertible in order to guarantee the boundedness of
\psi
.

3.2.2
Heat conductors of the Jeffreys type

The constitutive equation of a heat conductor of the Jeffreys type is given by

\tau\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=-\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta-\tau\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta},\qquad\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$},\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}\in{\rm Sym}.

(19)

The Fourier law is recovered as
\tau\to 0^{+}
.
The Jeffreys type conductor can be obtained as a combination of two different models. Let
{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{(1)}
,
{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{(2)}
be heat fluxes governed by the Fourier law and the MCV law, respectively,

{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{(1)}=-\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}_{1}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta\qquad\tau{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{(2)}+{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{(2)}=-\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}_{2}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}_{1},\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}_{2}
are positive-semidefinite second-order tensors. Hence

\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{(1)}=-\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}_{1}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}
.
It follows that

\tau\big(\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{(1)}+\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{(2)}\big)+{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{(1)}+{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{(2)}=-(\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}_{2})\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta-\tau\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}_{1}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta},

Consequently, the flux
{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}={\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{(1)}+{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{(2)}
satisfies (
19
) with
\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}_{2}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}_{1}
.

As proved in
[
10
, § 5.1]
, different choices of
\psi
and
\gamma
allow the model to be consistent with thermodynamics. For instance, either

\begin{split}\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\psi_{1}&=\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\psi_{0}(\theta)+\frac{\tau}{2\theta}[{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta]\cdot(\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}+\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$})^{-1}[{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta],\\
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\gamma_{1}&=\frac{1}{\theta^{2}}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\cdot(\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}+\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$})^{-1}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+\frac{1}{\theta^{2}}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}(\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}+\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$})^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta.\end{split}

or

\begin{split}\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\psi_{2}&=\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\psi_{0}(\theta)+\frac{\tau}{2\theta}[{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta]\cdot(\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$})^{-1}[{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta],\\
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\gamma_{2}&=\frac{1}{\theta^{2}}({\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta\big)\cdot(\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$})^{-1}({\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta\big)+\frac{1}{\theta^{2}}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta.\end{split}

In the former case, the thermodynamic consistency,
\gamma_{1}\geq 0
, is ensured if and only if
\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\in{\rm Sym}^{+}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}=\beta\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}
,
\beta\geq 0
. In the latter,
\gamma_{2}\geq 0
is ensured if and only if
\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}>\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}\geq 0
.

When the dependence of
\psi
on
\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
is allowed, new models of heat conductors consistent with thermodynamics were obtained in
[
10
]
.

3.2.3
Quintanilla’s heat conduction model

A new theory of thermoelasticity phenomena have been proposed by Quintanilla in
[
29
]
by modifying the Green-Naghdi’s type III theory.
Deriving with respect to time the original equation, we obtain the rate-type Quintanilla model

\tau\ddot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=-\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta-\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta},\qquad\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$},\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\in{\rm Sym}.

(20)

It can be verified (see
[
10
, § 5.3]
) that the free energy and entropy production related to the Quintanilla model respectively take the form

\begin{split}\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\psi&=\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\psi_{0}(\theta)+\frac{1}{2\theta}\big[\tau\dot{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta\big]\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}}{(\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}-\tau\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$})^{-1}}\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{-1}\big[\tau\dot{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta\big]\\
&+\frac{1}{2\theta}\Big({\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+2[\tau\dot{{\bf q}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta]\Big)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{-1}{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}},\\
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\gamma&=\frac{1}{\theta^{2}}(\tau\dot{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta)\cdot(\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}-\tau\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$})^{-1}(\tau\dot{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta).\end{split}

Assuming
\tau>0
, thermodynamic consistency is ensured by
\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}>\tau\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}
. However
\xi
is required to be invertible in order to guarantee the boundedness of
\psi
.
Letting
\tau\to 0
equation (
20
) reduces to (
18
), and the free energy and entropy production of the GN III conductor are recovered. Hence, the Quintanilla model represents a proper generalization of the GN III linear theory.

In order to study the wave propagation, we restrict our attention to a unidimensional rigid body.
We then couple equation (
20
) with the internal energy balance law and obtain

\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}c_{v}\dot{\theta}+\partial_{{\scriptscriptstyle X}}q_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}r,\qquad\tau\ddot{q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+\dot{q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=-\xi\partial_{{\scriptscriptstyle X}}\theta-\kappa\partial_{{\scriptscriptstyle X}}\dot{\theta}.

Letting
r=0
, we obtain a linear version of the well-known Moore-Gibson-Thompson equation
[
29
]
,

\tau\dddot{\theta}+\ddot{\theta}=\frac{1}{\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}c_{v}}\left(\xi\partial_{{\scriptscriptstyle X}}^{2}\theta+\kappa\partial_{{\scriptscriptstyle X}}^{2}\dot{\theta}\right).

3.2.4
Heat conductors of the Burgers type

To our knowledge, Burgers-type heat conductors were first proposed in
[
10
]
. They are characterized by the rate-type equation

\lambda\ddot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+\tau\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=-\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta-\tau\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}.

(21)

By analogy with the rheological model of the Burgers fluid, this equation can be obtained by considering a mixture of two components, each characterized by a conduction mechanism described by the MCV equation (
16
).
The Burgers-like model with
\lambda,\tau>0
is thermodynamically consistent if and only if one of the following hypotheses occurs (see
[
10
, § 5.4]
)

i)

\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}=0
,
\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}\in{\rm Sym}^{+}
;

ii)

\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\in{\rm Sym}^{+}
,
\tau^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}\geq\lambda\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}
.

Due to the linearity of the model equation (
21
), both
\psi
and
\gamma
are quadratic functions of the state variables
{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta
(see
[
10
, eqns. (47) and (60)]
). These functions are not unique.

As to the propagation of thermal waves, we consider a rigid unidimensional body. If the specific heat supply
r
vanishes, the resulting system

\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}c_{v}\dot{\theta}+\partial_{{\scriptscriptstyle X}}q_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=0,\qquad\lambda\ddot{q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+\tau\dot{q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+q_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=-\kappa\partial_{{\scriptscriptstyle X}}\theta-\tau\zeta\partial_{{\scriptscriptstyle X}}\dot{\theta}.

leads to the Joseph-Preziosi temperature equation
[
30
]
,

\lambda\dddot{\theta}+\tau\ddot{\theta}+\dot{\theta}=\frac{1}{\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}c_{v}}\left(\kappa\partial_{{\scriptscriptstyle X}}^{2}\theta+\tau\zeta\partial_{{\scriptscriptstyle X}}^{2}\dot{\theta}\right).

(22)

4
Higher order rate-type models of heat conduction

To describe some new rate-type models in heat conduction we consider higher-order time derivatives of the heat flux vector and the temperature gradient in the set of admissible variables, namely

\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle R}:=(\theta,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\dot{{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\ddot{{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\ddot{\theta}).

Letting
\psi,\eta,\gamma
be dependent on
\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
, upon evaluation of
\dot{\psi}
and substitution in (
8
), we obtain

\begin{split}\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}(\partial_{\theta}\psi+\eta)\dot{\theta}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\ddot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\,\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\ddot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\dddot{{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}&\\
+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta}\psi\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}}\psi\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\ddot{\theta}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\ddot{\theta}}\psi\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dddot{\theta}+\frac{1}{\theta}{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta&=-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\gamma.\end{split}

Hence, the linearity and arbitrariness of
\dot{\theta}
,
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dddot{\theta}
imply that

\psi=\psi(\theta,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\ddot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}),\qquad\eta=-\partial_{\theta}\psi,

and the entropy inequality reduces to

\begin{split}\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\ddot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\,\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\ddot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\dddot{{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}&\\
+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta}\psi\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}}\psi\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\ddot{\theta}+\frac{{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}{\theta}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta&=-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\gamma\leq 0.\end{split}

(23)

Let
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=(\theta,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\ddot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta})
be the set of state variables. As previously remarked, for any value assigned to the state variables, the derivatives
\dddot{{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
and
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\ddot{\theta}
must be regarded as mutually dependent. This in turn implies that
\ddot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
and
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}
, as well as
\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
and
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta
, are implicitly dependent. However, constitutive models in which the free energy is independent of some variables of
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
can also be considered.

In particular, we are interested here to some special models where the dependence of
\psi
on
\ddot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
is neglected. If this is the case, inequality (
23
) becomes

\begin{split}\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\ddot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta}\psi\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}&\\
+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}}\psi\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\ddot{\theta}+\frac{{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}{\theta}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta&=-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\gamma\leq 0.\end{split}

(24)

4.1
A linear second-order model (LSO)

A model of heat conductor is considered in the form (see
[
18
]
)

\lambda\ddot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+\tau\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta-\tau\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}-\lambda\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\ddot{\theta}.

(25)

This model represents an extension of the Burgers-type conductor to which it reduces when
\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}={\bf 0}
. On the other hand, when
\lambda=0
it reduces to the Jeffreys model.

The LSO model can be obtained, by considering a mixture of three different substances and assuming that the resulting heat flux vector is given by the sum
{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}={\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{(1)}+{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{(2)}+{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{(3)}
.
In the first component the heat conduction follows the Fourier law, whereas the second and third components are
characterized by a conduction mechanism described by the Maxwell-Cattaneo equation (
16
), namely

\begin{split}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{(1)}=-\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}^{(1)}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,\qquad\tau_{2}\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{(2)}+{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{(2)}=-\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}^{(2)}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,\qquad\tau_{3}\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{(3)}+{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{(3)}=-\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}^{(3)}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta.\end{split}

We recover (
25
) after some manipulations by letting

\tau=\tau_{2}+\tau_{3},\ \ \lambda=\tau_{2}\tau_{3},\\
\ \mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}^{(1)}+\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}^{(2)}+\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}^{(3)},\ \ \mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}^{(1)}+\frac{1}{\tau}\Big(\tau_{3}\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}^{(2)}+\tau_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}^{(3)}\Big),\ \ \mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}^{(1)}.

In order to investigate the consistency of the LSO constitutive equation
(
25
) with inequality (
24
), we restrict our attention to the isotropic case where
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}=\mu{\bf 1}
,
\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}=\nu{\bf 1}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}=\kappa{\bf 1}
.
Moreover, we assume

\lambda,\kappa\neq 0

to exclude the Jeffreys and Burgers conductors. Accordingly we can consider
\ddot{{\bf q}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}
as a linear function of
{{\bf q}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}},\dot{{\bf q}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}
and
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\ddot{\theta}
, namely

\ddot{{\bf q}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}=-\frac{\tau}{\lambda}\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}-\frac{1}{\lambda}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}-\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta-\frac{\tau\nu}{\lambda}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}-\kappa\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\ddot{\theta}.

(26)

Upon substitution for
\ddot{{\bf q}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}
from (
26
) into (
24
), we have

\begin{split}\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\Big(\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi-\frac{\tau}{\lambda}\partial_{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\Big)\cdot{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}-\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\frac{1}{\lambda}\partial_{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+\Big(\frac{{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}{\theta}-\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\partial_{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\Big)\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta&\\
+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\Big(\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta}\psi-\frac{\tau\nu}{\lambda}\partial_{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\Big)\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\Big(\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}}\psi-\kappa\partial_{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\Big)\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\ddot{\theta}&=-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\gamma\leq 0.\end{split}

Since
\psi
is independent of
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\ddot{\theta}
, assuming that
\gamma
is also independent, the linearity and arbitrariness of
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\ddot{\theta}
imply

\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}}\psi=\kappa\partial_{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi.

(27)

Otherwise, we can assume the constraint (
27
) and in turn obtain that
\gamma
is independent of
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\ddot{\theta}
.
Anyway, (
24
) reduces to

\begin{split}\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\Big(\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi-\frac{\tau}{\lambda}\partial_{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\Big)\cdot{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}-\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\frac{1}{\lambda}\partial_{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}&\\
+\Big(\frac{{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}{\theta}-\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\Big)\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\Big(\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta}\psi-\frac{\tau\nu}{\lambda}\partial_{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\Big)\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}&=-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\gamma\leq 0.\end{split}

(28)

As suggested by the linearity of the model, the free energy
\psi
is assumed to have the following quadratic expression,

\begin{split}\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\psi&=\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\psi_{0}(\theta)+\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2}|{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}|^{2}+\frac{\alpha_{2}}{2}|\dot{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}|^{2}+\frac{\alpha_{3}}{2}|\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta|^{2}+\frac{\alpha_{4}}{2}|\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}|^{2}+\beta_{1}{{\bf q}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\dot{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\\
&+\beta_{2}{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+\beta_{3}\dot{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+\beta_{4}{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}+\beta_{5}\dot{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}+\beta_{6}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta},\end{split}

(29)

whence

\begin{split}\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\partial_{{{\bf q}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}\psi&=\alpha_{1}{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\beta_{1}\dot{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\beta_{2}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+\beta_{4}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta},\\
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\partial_{\dot{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}\psi&=\beta_{1}{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\alpha_{2}\dot{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\beta_{3}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+\beta_{5}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta},\\
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta}\psi&=\beta_{2}{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\beta_{3}\dot{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\alpha_{3}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+\beta_{6}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta},\\
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}}\psi&=\beta_{4}{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\beta_{5}\dot{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\beta_{6}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+\alpha_{4}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}.\end{split}

Upon substitution into (
27
) we obtain

\beta_{4}=\kappa\beta_{1},\qquad\beta_{5}=\kappa\alpha_{2},\qquad\beta_{6}=\kappa\beta_{3},\qquad\alpha_{4}=\kappa\beta_{5}.

(30)

Likewise, from (
28
) and (
30
) it follows

\begin{split}A_{11}|{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}|^{2}+A_{22}|\dot{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}|^{2}+A_{33}|\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta|^{2}+A_{44}|\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}|^{2}+2A_{12}{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\dot{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+2A_{13}{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta\\
+2A_{14}{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}+2A_{23}\dot{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+2A_{24}\dot{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}+2A_{34}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}=\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\gamma\geq 0,\end{split}

(31)

where

\begin{split}&A_{11}=\frac{\beta_{1}}{\lambda},\;A_{22}=\frac{\tau\alpha_{2}-\lambda\beta_{1}}{\lambda},\;A_{33}=\frac{\mu\beta_{3}}{\lambda},\;A_{44}=\frac{\tau\nu\kappa\alpha_{2}}{\lambda}-\kappa\beta_{3}\\
&A_{12}=\frac{\alpha_{2}+\tau\beta_{1}-\lambda\alpha_{1}}{2\lambda},\;A_{13}=\frac{\beta_{1}\mu\theta+\beta_{3}\theta-\lambda}{2\lambda\theta},\;A_{14}=\frac{\beta_{1}\nu\tau+\kappa\alpha_{2}-\lambda\beta_{2}}{2\lambda},\\
&A_{23}=\frac{\mu\alpha_{2}+\tau\beta_{3}-\lambda\beta_{2}}{2\lambda},\;A_{24}=\frac{\tau(\kappa+\nu)\alpha_{2}-\lambda(\kappa\beta_{1}+\beta_{3})}{2\lambda},\;A_{34}=\frac{\kappa\mu\alpha_{2}+\nu\tau\beta_{3}-\lambda\alpha_{3}}{2\lambda}.\end{split}

So overall matrix
A
is characterized by 6 unknowns,
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}
,
i=1,2,3
, 5 real material parameters,
\lambda,\tau,\mu,\nu,\kappa
and
\theta
.
To ensure thermodynamic consistency, we look for the conditions on these material parameters so that the symmetric 4-by-4 matrix
A
is positive semidefinite,
i.e. all principal minors of
A
are nonnegative (see, for instance
[
31
, § 7.6]
). Naturally, there may be different values of the unknowns that are compatible with these conditions. This is related to the fact that there can exist different free energy functions that are consistent with thermodynamics.

The discussion of the thermodynamic consistency of the model is too cumbersome to be included in the body of the paper. We therefore postpone it to the Appendix.

4.2
LSO temperature equation

Let us look briefly at properties of the solutions of the temperature equation corresponding to the LSO model. For simplicity, let us consider a rigid body. Without an external energy supply, the energy balance gives

\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}c_{v}\dot{\theta}=-\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}.

(32)

After combining this equation with (
25
) and assuming the body to be isotropic, we get

\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}c_{v}\left(\lambda\dddot{\theta}+\tau\ddot{\theta}+\dot{\theta}\right)=\mu\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{2}\theta+\tau\nu\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{2}\dot{\theta}+\lambda\kappa\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{2}\ddot{\theta}.

(33)

Letting
\theta({\bf x},t)=T(t)Y({\bf X})
, we obtain

\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}c_{v}\left(\lambda\dddot{T}+\tau\ddot{T}+\dot{T}\right)Y=\left(\mu T+\tau\nu\dot{T}+\lambda\kappa\ddot{T}\right)\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{2}Y

(34)

Equation (
34
) can be separated: the spatial variable
Y
solves the Helmholtz equation

\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{2}Y=-\Lambda Y

where
\Lambda
is a constant. Once the domain and boundary conditions have been fixed, the Helmholtz equation possesses non-trivial solutions only if
\Lambda
assumes specific values (the eigenvalues). It is well-known that under the most common boundary conditions the differential operator
-\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{2}
is strictly positive selfadjoint with discrete spectrum. Hence, its eigenvalues are non-negative, countably infinite and not bounded by any constant value. So, let us denote their set with
\{\Lambda_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, ordered in an ascending sequence, i.e.
\Lambda_{n}<\Lambda_{n+1}
. Equation (
34
) for
T(t)
then reads

\lambda\dddot{T}+(\tau+\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\lambda\kappa)\ddot{T}+(1+\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\tau\nu)\dot{T}+\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\mu T=0,

(35)

where we set
\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}:={\Lambda_{n}}/{\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}c_{v}}
. If
T(t)\propto e^{wt}
, then
w
is a root of the cubic equation

\lambda w^{3}+(\tau+\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\lambda\kappa)w^{2}+(1+\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\tau\nu)w+\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\mu=0.

(36)

To avoid solutions diverging at infinity, we consider only decaying or oscillating solutions to equation (
35
). Therefore, we look for necessary and sufficient conditions under which all roots of (
36
) have negative real parts.
According to the Routh-Hurwitz criterion, all the coefficients must have the same sign and the product of the coefficients of
w
and
w^{2}
minus the product of the coefficients of
w^{3}
and
w^{0}
must be positive. For simplicity, we assume the positivity of
\lambda
and
\tau
, a condition that could be deduced from the physical assumptions we made to build the model. The application of the Routh-Hurwitz criterion to (
36
) yields

\lambda,\tau>0,\;\tau+\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\lambda\kappa>0,\;1+\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\tau\nu>0,\;\mu>0,\;(\tau+\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\lambda\kappa)(1+\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\tau\nu)-\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\lambda\mu>0,

(37)

for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
. By exploiting the unboundedness of
\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}
, the second and third inequalities give
\kappa\geq 0
and
\nu\geq 0
, respectively. So all material parameters must be non negative; in particular

\lambda,\tau,\mu>0,\qquad\kappa,\nu\geq 0.

The last inequality is quadratic with respect to
\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}
and reads

\lambda\kappa\tau\nu\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}^{2}+(\tau^{2}\nu+\lambda(\kappa-\mu))\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}+\tau>0.

(38)

If
\kappa,\nu>0
, then the coefficient of
\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}^{2}
is positive. Since all the values of
\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}
are non negative, the previous relation is satisfied either if all the coefficients of the corresponding quadratic equation are positive (in this case any real roots are negative), or if the discriminant of the corresponding quadratic equation is negative (in this case (
38
) is satisfied for any real value of
\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}
). In the first case
\tau^{2}\nu+\lambda(\kappa-\mu)>0
is required, i.e.

\mu<\kappa+\frac{\tau^{2}\nu}{\lambda}.

(39)

In the second case we let
\left(\tau^{2}\nu+\lambda(\kappa-\mu)\right)^{2}-4\tau^{2}\nu\lambda\kappa<0
,
which implies

\mu\in\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\left(\sqrt{\lambda\kappa}-\sqrt{\tau^{2}\nu}\right)^{2},\left(\sqrt{\lambda\kappa}+\sqrt{\tau^{2}\nu}\right)^{2}\right).

(40)

From the combination of (
39
) and (
40
) follows that (
38
) is satisfied when

\mu<\frac{\left(\sqrt{\lambda\kappa}+\sqrt{\tau^{2}\nu}\right)^{2}}{\lambda}.

(41)

If
\kappa=0,\nu>0
, then

(\tau^{2}\nu-\lambda\mu)\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}+\tau>0

(42)

and the unboundedness of
\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}
yields
\tau^{2}\nu\geq\lambda\mu
.

If
\kappa>0,\nu=0
, then

\lambda(\kappa-\mu)\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}+\tau>0.

(43)

and the unboundedness of
\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}
yields
\kappa\geq\mu
.

Let us consider now the limit case
\mu=0
. If
\mu=0
, we see that one of the roots of (
36
) is zero, the other two being given by the equation

\lambda w^{2}+(\tau+\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\lambda\kappa)w+(1+\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\tau\nu)=0.

(44)

The roots of (
44
) must be negative, meaning that all the coefficients in (
44
) must have the same sign. This implies

\lambda>0,\;(\tau+\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\lambda\kappa)>0,\;(1+\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\tau\nu)>0.

(45)

As we have seen, the conditions on the coefficient of
w
and on the coefficient of
w^{0}
imply
\kappa>0
and
\nu>0
. So we have that all the parameters
\lambda
,
\tau
,
\kappa
,
\nu
must be positive and
\mu=0
.

Finally, we notice that a special behavior occurs when (
38
) holds as an equality for some
\bar{n}\in\mathbb{N}
, i.e.

5
Conclusion

In these Conclusions we would like to make a comment about the simultaneous thermodynamic and dynamic consistency of the LSO model, i.e. we are asking under which conditions the LSO model has a nonnegative entropy production (see Appendix
6
) and the evolution of the temperature equation is described by bounded functions eventually approaching the equilibrium steady state. Firstly, let us consider the case
\mu>0
. By comparing the items in table (
1
) with the results given in Sect.
4.2
, we see that items 10 and 12-16 can be excluded, since in all these cases
\lambda<0
, contrary to the hypotheses (
37
). Also, items 4-7 must be excluded if, according to (
37
),
\mu>0
. Moreover, it is worth noting that items 9 and 11 are very specific: the possible values of the parameter
\mu
are described by the intersection of three different intervals and this intersection must satisfy also (
41
): if the intersection of the three intervals is empty and/or the condition (
41
) is not satisfied the model would be inconsistent. Finally, we observe that items 1-3 and 8 are instead all consistent with (
37
) and (
41
); hence we conclude that these choices of the material parameters are consistent both from a thermodynamic and dynamic point of view.

Particular attention should be paid to the case
\mu=0
. This is compatible only with items 4-7 in table (
1
). However, items 6 and 7 must be excluded since
\lambda<0
, contrary to the hypotheses (
45
). On the contrary, the remaining cases 4 and 5 agree with condition (
45
) and are therefore consistent both from a thermodynamic and dynamic point of view.
