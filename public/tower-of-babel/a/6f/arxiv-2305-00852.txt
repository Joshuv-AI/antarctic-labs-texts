Weighted (residual) varentropy and its applications**
Shital Saha and Suchandan Kayal

Abstract




Abstract

In information theory, it is of recent interest to study variability of the uncertainty measures. In this regard, the concept of varentropy has been introduced and studied by several authors in recent past. In this communication, we study the weighted varentropy and weighted residual varentropy. Several theoretical results of these variability measures such as the effect under monotonic transformations and bounds are investigated. Importance of the weighted residual varentropy over the residual varentropy is presented. Further, we study weighted varentropy for coherent systems and weighted residual varentropy for proportional hazard rate models. A kernel-based non-parametric estimator for the weighted residual varentropy is also proposed. The estimation method is illustrated using simulated and two real data sets.

Keywords:
Weighted varentropy; residual lifetime; monotone transformations; bounds; coherent systems; proportional hazard model; non-parametric estimation.

Introduction

The concept of entropy, introduced by
Shannon 1948
has been widely used in computing chaos and uncertainty of a system. For a discrete type random variable
X
with probability mass function (PMF)
P(X=x_{i})=p_{i}
, where
i=1,\ldots,n
, the Shannon entropy (SE) is defined as

\displaystyle S(X)=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\log p_{i},

(1.1)

where ‘log’ denotes the natural logarithm. Equation (
1.1
) quantifies the information contained in the random variable
X
. Clearly,
S(X)
is a function of probabilities of happening of events only. There are some situations, where it is necessary to consider both these probabilities and some qualitative characteristics of the events in order to quantify uncertainty contained in a random experiment. For example, in a two-handed game, a player needs to think about both the probabilities of different random strategies and the wins corresponding to these strategies. To compute the information contained in a probabilistic experiment having elementary events characterized both by their probabilities and by some qualitative weights,
Guiaşu 1971
proposed weighted entropy of a discrete type random variable
X
, which is given by (see also
Guiasu 1986
)

\displaystyle S^{w}(X)=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}p_{i}\log p_{i},

(1.2)

where
w_{i}\geq 0
is known as the weight corresponding to the
i
th elementary event. The weight is usually specified by the analyst depending upon the situation.

For a non-negative absolutely continuous random variable
X
with probability density function (PDF)
f(\cdot)
, the SE and weighted Shannon entropy (WSE) are given by

\displaystyle H(X)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\log f(x)dx~\mbox{and}~H^{x}(X)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}xf(x)\log f(x)dx,

(1.3)

respectively. In the literature,
H(X)
and
H^{x}(X)
are also known as the differential entropy and weighted differential entropy, respectively. The factor
x
in the integrand of
H^{x}(X)
given in (
1.3
) may be treated as a weight linearly emphasizing the occurrence of the event
\{X=x\}
. It is worthwhile to mention that one may assume a general weight function
w(x)\geq 0
instead of
x
inside the integration of
H^{x}(X)
in (
1.3
). Note that the differential entropy is a shift-independent measure, whereas the weighted differential entropy is shift-dependent. This property makes
H^{x}(X)
a more useful tool than
H(X)
in some situations. For details about the weighted differential entropy, we refer to
Di Crescenzo and Longobardi 2006
.

Denote by
IC(X)=-\log f(X)
, the random information content of a non-negative absolutely continuous random variable
X
with PDF
f(\cdot).
Then,
H(X)=E[IC(X)],
that is, the SE in (
1.3
) is the expectation of the information content of
X.
We note that the justification of
IC(X)
can be given in discrete case, where it represents the number of bits essentially required to represent
X
by a coding scheme that minimizes average code length (see
Shannon 1948
). For continuous case, one may still call
IC(X)
as information content, even though, the coding interpretation does not hold (see
Bobkov and Madiman 2011
). The differential entropy takes values from the extended real line, whereas the SE of a discrete random variable is always non-negative. Some other deficiencies of the differential entropy can be found in
Schroeder 2004
.
The notion of differential entropy has been widely applied in stochastic modelling and various applied fields. Due to importance of the information content in information theory, probability and statistics, it is of interest to study its behaviour.
Bobkov and Madiman 2011
studied that the information content concentrates around the entropy in high dimension when the PDF is log-concave. Sometimes, two random variables have the same SE. The Shannon entropies of the uniform distribution in
(0,1)
and the exponential distribution with mean
1/e
are equal, and they are zero. In this case, the concept of concentration of information content around entropy is useful for analysis purposes. More concentration of information content around entropy will yield a better result. The concentration can be calculated with the variance of
IC(X)
, which is called varentropy. Recently, the varentropy has attracted the attention of the computer scientists for measuring the dispersion of sources, and has many applications in data compression, for details reader may refer to
Kontoyiannis 1997
and
Kontoyiannis and Verdú 2014
. Suppose
X
is a non-negative and absolutely continuous random variable with PDF
f(\cdot)
, then the varentropy is given by (see
Fradelizi et al. 2016
)

\displaystyle{\it VE}(X)=Var(-\log f(X))=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)(\log f(x))^{2}dx-\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\log f(x)dx\bigg)^{2}.

(1.4)

In a similar way, the varentropy of a discrete random variable
X,
which assumes values
x_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
can be defined as (see
Di Crescenzo and Paolillo 2021
)

\displaystyle{\it VE}^{*}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}[\log p_{i}]^{2}-\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\log p_{i}\bigg]^{2},

(1.5)

where
P(X=x_{i})=p_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n.
From (
1.4
), it is clear that the varentropy of
X
is always non-negative. For uniform distribution, it is zero. The varentropy indicates how the information content is scattered around the entropy. Several researchers studied varentropy and discussed its properties including some characterization results. In this direction, we refer to
Bobkov and Madiman 2011
,
Arıkan 2016
,
Fradelizi et al. 2016
,
Di Crescenzo and Paolillo 2021
and
Maadani et al. 2022
. In particular,
Bobkov and Madiman 2011
demonstrated a concentration property of the information content
IC(X)
when an
n
-dimensional random vector has log-concave density function.
Arıkan 2016
established that the sum of the varentropies at the output of the polar transform is less than or equal to the sum of the varentropies at the input, with equality if and only if at least one of the inputs has zero varentropy.
Fradelizi et al. 2016
provided bound for the varentropy of random vectors with log-concave densities, which is sharper than that proposed by
Bobkov and Madiman 2011
.
Di Crescenzo and Paolillo 2021
introduced residual varentropy and studied its various properties with some applications related to the proportional hazards model and the first-passage times of an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck jump-diffusion process. Recently,
Maadani et al. 2022
studied varentropy for order statistics. The authors introduced a new stochastic order based on the varentropy and relationships of it with the other stochastic orders. Besides these, one may also refer to
Buono et al. 2022
,
Raqab et al. 2022
and
Sharma and Kundu 2023
for some results on varentropy.

Unlike differential entropy, the weighted differential entropy given in (
1.3
) can be expressed as
H^{x}(X)=E(-X\log f(X)).
Denote by

\displaystyle IC^{w}(X)=-w(X)\log f(X),

(1.6)

for a given choice of weight
w(x)=x.
We call
IC^{x}(X)
as weighted random information content. Thus, the weighted differential entropy is the expectation of the weighted information content of
X.
In statistics, one may think of the weighted information content as the weighted log-likelihood function. In this paper, we introduce weighted varentropy (WVE) of a random variable
X
and study its properties. We will later observe that the WVE is not affine invariant, whereas, the varentropy is affine invariant. That is, the varentropy is unable to distinguish variability in the information content of a random process in different time points. This drawback can be resolved if we use WVE. Note that the use of WVE is also motivated by the necessity, arising in various communication and transmission problems, of expressing the usefulness of events with a variance measure. Further, an important characteristic of the human visual system is that it can recognize objects in a scale and translation invariant manner. We refer to
Wallis 1996
for details. It is a challenging job to achieve this desirable behavior using biologically realistic networks. Indeed, knowing that a device fails to operate, or a neuron fails to release spikes in a given time-interval, yields a relevantly different information from the case when such an event occurs in a different equally wide intervals. In some cases, we are thus led to resort to a shift-dependent information/variance measure that, for instance, assigns different measures to such distributions. The concept of WVE can be employed in such situations for a better result than the usual varentropy.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce WVE and discuss some properties. The closed-form expressions of the WVE of some distributions have been obtained. The WVE of an affine transformed random variable has been provided. Various bounds of the WVE have been presented. In Section
3
, we propose WRVE and present some results. Various bounds of the WRVE have been obtained. The WRVE has been studied under general monotonic transformations. Further, we present a justification of studying WRVE. A comparative study between the WRVE and WRE has been provided for exponential and uniform distributions. Section
4
deals with the WVE of coherent systems. Some bounds are proposed. In Section
5
, we study WRVE for the proportional hazard rate models. Section
6
focuses on a non-parametric estimator of the WRVE. We consider a simulated data set and two real life data sets for the purpose of illustration of the proposed estimation method. Finally, Section
7
concludes the paper.

Throughout the rest of the paper, we assume that random variables are non-negative and absolutely continuous. The derivatives are assumed to exist whenever they are used. The notation ‘
\prime
’ is used to denote derivative, for example
h^{\prime}(x)=\frac{d}{dx}h(x)
.

2
Weighted varentropy

In this section, we introduce WVE and discuss its various properties. Let
X
be a non-negative absolutely continuous random variable with PDF
f(\cdot)
. The variance of the weighted information content of
X,
denoted by
IC^{w}(X)=-w(X)\log f(X)
with weight function
w(\cdot)
is defined as

\displaystyle{\it VE}^{w}(X)=Var[IC^{w}(X)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E[(IC^{w}(X))^{2}]-[E(IC^{w}(X))]^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}w^{2}(x)f(x)[\log f(x)]^{2}dx-\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}w(x)f(x)\log f(x)dx\bigg]^{2}.

From the expressions of
H(X)
in (
1.3
) and
{\it VE}(X)
in (
1.4
), it is clear that the SE and varentropy do not depend on the realizations of the random variable
X
, but depend only on its PDF. This does not hold for the WSE and WVE. From (
1.3
) and (
2
), it is easy to observe that the WSE and WVE depend on both the PDF and realizations of the random variable. For a discrete random variable
X
taking values
x_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, the WVE is given by

\displaystyle{\it VE}^{*^{w}}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}^{2}p_{i}[\log p_{i}]^{2}-\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}p_{i}\log p_{i}\bigg]^{2},

(2.2)

where
w_{i}\geq 0
is the weight corresponding to the event
\{X=x_{i}\}
. The WVE in (
2.2
) measures the variability of the information content supplied by a probabilistic experiment depending both on the probabilities of events and on qualitative (objective or subjective) weights of the possible events. The qualitative weights are usually the weights in physics or as the utilities in decision theory. Even though, assigning a weight to every elementary event is not an easy job to be done, we generally assign larger weight to the events which are more significant or more useful depending on our goal. Indeed, one may assign non-negative numbers (weights) to each probabilistic events, directly proportional to its importance or significance. Based on the values of the weights, the WVE reduces to different variability measures. For example, if we choose the weights to be
1
, then the WVE reduces to the varentropy given in (
1.5
). Further, let us consider the weight as the ratio of the probabilities of the events and the information supplied by the events with a scaling factor
\frac{1}{2}
, given by

\displaystyle w_{i}=-\frac{p_{i}}{2\log p_{i}}.

(2.3)

Then, the WVE in (
2.2
) reduces to the varextropy of a discrete type random variable
X
, given by (see
Vaselabadi et al. 2021
)

\displaystyle{\it VEx}(X)=\frac{1}{4}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{3}-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{2}\right)^{2}\right].

(2.4)

We refer to
Guiaşu 1971
for an elaborate discussion regarding the importance and some choices of the weights for weighted entropy. From (
1.5
), it can be easily established that the varentropy for a discrete random variable is permutation symmetric with respect to the probabilities
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
. This property does not hold for the WVE given in (
2.2
). Now, we consider an example, illustrating the fact that the WVE is not permutation symmetric with respect to the assigned probabilities.

Example 2.1
.

Consider a discrete type random variable
X\in\{1,2,3\}
with

P(X=1)=p,~~P(X=2)=1-p-q,~~\mbox{and}~~P(X=3)=q,

where
0\leq q\leq 1-p\leq 1
. Using (
1.2
) and (
2.2
), and assuming
w_{i}=x_{i},
we obtain

\displaystyle S^{x}(X)=-x_{1}p\log p-x_{2}(1-p-q)\log(1-p-q)-x_{3}q\log q

(2.5)

and

\displaystyle{\it VE}^{*^{x}}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{1}^{2}p(\log p)^{2}+x_{2}^{2}(1-p-q)(\log(1-p-q))^{2}+x_{3}^{2}q(\log q)^{2}-[S^{x}(X)]^{2}

(2.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\it VE}^{*^{x}}(X;p,q),~\mbox{say},

where
x_{1}=1,x_{2}=2
and
x_{3}=3.
Now, consider two different sets of probabilities
\{p=0.5,1-p-q=0.2,q=0.3\}
and
\{p=0.3,1-p-q=0.2,q=0.5\}
for two different distributions of
X.
Here,
{\it VE}^{*}(X;p,q)=0.13296441046={\it VE}^{*}(X;q,p)
, that is the varentropies of two different distributions are same. But, their WVEs are not same. Here,
{\it VE}^{*^{x}}(X;p,q)=1.92508326678
and
{\it VE}^{*^{x}}(X;q,p)=0.48838794637
, implying that WVEs are not permutation symmetric. The surface plots (upward and downward views) of the WVE given in (
2.6
) are presented in Figure

It is not always an easy task to get closed-form expression of the WVE of a random variable of interest. In this case, certain transformation can be used to compute WVE of a transformed random variable (new distribution) in terms of the WVE of the known distribution. The following result is useful in this regard.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose
X
is a random variable with PDF
f(\cdot)
and
Y=aX+b
, with
a>0
and
b\geq 0
. Then, the WVE of
Y
is obtained as

\displaystyle{\it VE}^{y}(Y)={\it VE}^{w_{1}}(X)+(a\log a)^{2}Var(X)+2\log a(H^{w_{1}^{2}}(X)-H^{w_{1}}(X)E[aX+b]),

where
H^{w_{1}}(X)
and
{\it VE}^{w_{1}}(X)
are the WSE and WVE of
X
, respectively for a given choice of
w_{1}(x)=ax+b
.

Proof.

Denote by
g(.)
the PDF of
Y=aX+b
, where
g(y)=\frac{1}{a}f(\frac{y-b}{a}),~y>b.
From (
2
), we have

\displaystyle{\it VE}^{y}(Y)=\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{2}g(y)[\log g(y)]^{2}dy-[H^{y}(Y)]^{2}.

(2.7)

Further,

\displaystyle H^{y}(Y)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}yg(y)\log g(y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{b}^{\infty}y\frac{1}{a}f\left(\frac{y-b}{a}\right)\log\left(\frac{1}{a}f\left(\frac{y-b}{a}\right)\right)dy

(2.8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}(ax+b)f(x)\{\log f(x)-\log a\}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H^{w_{1}}(X)+\log a~E[aX+b]

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{2}g(y)[\log g(y)]^{2}dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}(ax+b)^{2}f(x)\{\log f(x)-\log a\}^{2}dx

(2.9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}(ax+b)^{2}f(x)\{\log f(x)\}^{2}dx+2\log aH^{w_{1}^{2}}(X)

\displaystyle+(\log a)^{2}E[(aX+b)^{2}].

After using (
2.8
) and (
2.9
) in (
2.7
), we get the required result. Thus, the proof is completed.
∎

Sometimes, it is not always possible to obtain the closed-form expression of WVE. In such cases, bounds are useful for some ideas about the variability measure. In the next proposition, we obtain an upper bound of the WVE defined in (
2
) with weight
w(x)=x
. We note that here, the bound depends on the WSE.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
X
be a non-negative and absolutely continuous random variable with PDF
f(\cdot)
satisfying

\displaystyle e^{-(\alpha x+\beta)}\leq f(x)\leq 1,~~~x>0,

(2.10)

with
\alpha>0
and
\beta\geq 0
. Then,

VE^{x}(X)\leq H^{w_{2}}(X),

where
H^{w_{2}}(X)
is the WSE with weight function
w_{2}(x)=\alpha x^{3}+\beta x^{2}.

Proof.

Under the assumptions made, from (
2
), after some simplification we obtain

\displaystyle VE^{w}(X)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2}f(x)(\alpha x+\beta)\log f(x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}(\alpha x^{3}+\beta x^{2})f(x)\log f(x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H^{w_{2}}(X).

Thus, the result follows.
∎

Next, we investigate the WVE under monotonic transformations, and look into its characteristic features. Denote

\eta_{1}(x)=\phi(x)\log\phi^{\prime}(x)~\mbox{and}~\eta_{2}(x)=\phi(x)\log(-\phi^{\prime}(x)),~x>0.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
X
be a non-negative random variable with PDF
f(\cdot)
. Suppose
\phi(\cdot)
is strictly monotone, continuous, and differentiable function; and
Y=\phi(X)
. Then,

{\it VE}^{y}(Y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\it VE}^{\phi}(X)+Var[\eta_{1}(X)]-2E[\phi(X)\eta_{1}(X)\log f(X)]-2H^{\phi}(X)E[\eta_{1}(X)],\\
\text{if $\phi$ is strictly increasing;}\\
\\
-{\it VE}^{\phi}(X)-Var[\eta_{2}(X)\big)]+2E[\phi(X)\eta_{2}(X)\log f(X)]-2\big(H^{\phi}(X)\big)^{2}\\
-2H^{\phi}(X)E[\eta_{2}(X)]-2\{E[\eta_{2}(X)]\}^{2},~\text{if $\phi$ is strictly decreasing,}\end{array}\right.

(2.11)

where

H^{\phi}(X)
is the WSE with weight
w(x)=\phi(x)
and
{\it VE}^{\phi}(X)
is given in (
2
) with
w(x)=\phi(x)
.

Proof.

First, we consider that
\phi(\cdot)
is strictly increasing. Then, from (
2
) the WVE of
Y=\phi(X)
is expressed as

\displaystyle{\it VE}^{y}(Y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{2}f(\phi^{-1}(y))\bigg|\frac{1}{\phi^{\prime}(\phi^{-1}(y))}\bigg|\bigg[\log f(\phi^{-1}(y))-\log\phi^{\prime}(\phi^{-1}(y))\bigg]^{2}dy

\displaystyle-\Bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}yf(\phi^{-1}(y))\bigg|\frac{1}{\phi^{\prime}(\phi^{-1}(y))}\bigg|\bigg[\log f(\phi^{-1}(y))-\log\phi^{\prime}(\phi^{-1}(y))\bigg]dy\Bigg]^{2}.

Now, substituting
y=\phi(x)
in the right hand side of (
2
), and then after some simplification, the desired result follows. The proof when
\phi(\cdot)
is strictly decreasing is similar, and thus it is omitted for brevity. ∎

The following remark which is immediate from Theorem
2.1
, provides the effect of WVE under the scale and location transformations.

Remark 2.1
.

(a)

Let
Y=aX,~a>0
be the scale transformation. Then,

\displaystyle{\it VE}^{ax}(aX)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{2}\big\{{\it VE}^{x}(X)+(\log a)^{2}Var(X)-2\log a\big(H^{x}(X)E(X)

\displaystyle+E[X^{2}\log f(X)]\big)\big\}.

(b)

Let
Y=X+b,~b\geq 0
be the location transformation. Then,

\displaystyle{\it VE}^{x+b}(X+b)={\it VE}^{x}(X)-b{\it VE}(X),

where
{\it VE}(X)
is the varentropy of
X
, given by (
1.4
).

Fradelizi et al. 2016
proved that for the distributions with log-concave PDF, the varentropy is smaller than
1
. The condition of log-concave density means that the PDF belongs to the class of strong unimodal densities. Further,
Di Crescenzo and Paolillo 2021
established a similar result for the residual varentropy when a random lifetime X is ILR, i.e. its PDF is log-concave. Recently,
Di Crescenzo et al. 2021
obtained the similar results if
X_{t}
is IFR (increasing failure rate)
and its PDF is strictly decreasing. Thus, it is natural to investigate if the WVE given by (
2
) is less than
1
for the case of a log-concave density function. Next, we consider an example, which establishes that the WVE does not have such upper bound like varentropy and residual varentropy.

Example 2.4
.

Consider Weibull distribution with PDF

\displaystyle f(x)=cx^{c-1}e^{-x^{c}},~~x>0,~c>1,

(2.13)

which is clearly log-concave with respect to
x
since
\frac{d^{2}\log f(x)}{dx^{2}}=-\frac{c-1}{x^{2}}-c(c-1)x^{c-2}<0
, for
c>1.
For this distribution, we have computed the WVE for

3
Weighted residual varentropy

In this section, we study WVE for the residual lifetime of a system and discuss some properties. The residual lifetime is an important reliability metric of a system, which provides the information related to its remaining lifetime given that it has survived till time
t>0.
It is denoted by
X_{t}=[X-t|X>t].
The PDF of
X_{t}
is given by
f_{t}(x)=\frac{f(x)}{\tilde{F}(t)},~x>t
, where
\tilde{F}(t)=P(X>t).
Then, the weighted residual varentropy (WRVE) of
X
is defined as

\displaystyle{\it VE}^{w}(X;t)=Var[IC^{w}(X_{t})]=\int_{t}^{\infty}\frac{f(x)}{\tilde{F}(t)}\bigg(w(x)\log\frac{f(x)}{\tilde{F}(t)}\bigg)^{2}dx-[H^{w}(X;t)]^{2},

(3.1)

where
IC^{w}(X_{t})=-w(X)\log f_{t}(X)
and
H^{w}(X;t)
is the weighted residual Shannon entropy (WRSE) with weight
w(x)
, given by

\displaystyle H^{w}(X;t)=\int_{t}^{\infty}w(x)\frac{f(x)}{\tilde{F}(t)}\log\left(\frac{f(x)}{\tilde{F}(t)}\right)dx.

The WRVE in (
3.1
) can be further expressed for
w(x)=x
as

\displaystyle{\it VE}^{w}(X;t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tilde{F}(t)}\int_{t}^{\infty}x^{2}f(x)[\log f(x)]^{2}dx-\Lambda^{2}(t)E[X^{2}|X>t]-2\Lambda(t)H^{w^{*}}(X;t)

(3.2)

\displaystyle-[H^{w}(X;t)]^{2},

where
\Lambda(t)=-\log\tilde{F}(t)
is the cumulative hazard rate function, and
H^{w^{*}}(X;t)
is the WRSE with weight
w^{*}(x)=x^{2}
. It can be easily obtained that

\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow 0}{\it VE}^{w}(X;t)={\it VE}^{w}(X),

where
{\it VE}^{w}(X)
is the WVE given in (
2
). Next, we obtain closed-form expressions of the WRVE for some distributions.

The following result provides an explicit expression of the derivative of WRVE with respect to
t
. We recall that the vitality function of an absolutely continuous random variable
X
was proposed by
Kupka and Loo 1989
, which is defined as

\displaystyle v(t)=E[X|X>t]=\int_{t}^{\infty}x\frac{f(x)}{\tilde{F}(t)}dx.

(3.3)

The vitality function is interpreted as the mean life span of a system whose age exceeds
t>0.
The hazard rate is an important concept widely used in survival analysis and reliability theory. It provides the instantaneous failure rate of an item given that the item has survived till time
t.
For a non-negative and absolutely continuous random variable
X
the hazard rate is given by

\displaystyle r(t)=\frac{f(t)}{\tilde{F}(t)},~t>0,

(3.4)

such that
\tilde{F}(t)>0.

Proposition 3.1
.

We have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\it VE}^{w}(X;t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r(t)\{{\it VE}^{w}(X;t)-2(H^{w}(X;t)v(t)+H^{w^{*}}(X;t))^{2}

(3.5)

\displaystyle-(H^{w}(X;t)+t\log r(t))^{2}\},

where
H^{w}(X;t)
and
H^{w^{*}}(X;t)
are the WRSEs with weights
w(x)=x
and
w^{*}(x)=x^{2}
, respectively.

Proof.

Differentiating (
3.2
) with respect to
t
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\it VE}^{w}(X;t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bigg(\frac{1}{\tilde{F}(t)}\int_{t}^{\infty}x^{2}f(x)[\log f(x)]^{2}dx\bigg)-\frac{d}{dt}\big(\Lambda^{2}(t)E[X^{2}|X>t]\big)

(3.6)

\displaystyle-2\frac{d}{dt}\big(\Lambda(t)H^{w^{*}}(X;t)\big)-\frac{d}{dt}\big([H^{w}(X;t)]^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f(t)}{(\tilde{F}(t))^{2}}\int_{t}^{\infty}x^{2}f(x)[\log f(x)]^{2}dx-\frac{1}{\tilde{F}(t)}t^{2}f(t)[\log f(t)]^{2}

\displaystyle-2\Lambda(t)r(t)E[X^{2}|X>t]-\Lambda^{2}(t)\frac{d}{dt}E[X^{2}|X>t]-2r(t)H^{w^{*}}(X;t)

\displaystyle-2\Lambda(t)\frac{d}{dt}H^{w^{*}}(X;t)-2H^{w}(X;t)\frac{d}{dt}H^{w}(X;t).

Further,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\big(H^{w^{*}}(X;t)\big)=r(t)\{H^{w^{*}}(X;t)+t^{2}\log r(t)-E[X^{2}|X>t]\},

(3.7)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\big(E[X^{2}|X>t]\big)=r(t)\{E[X^{2}|X>t]-t^{2}\},

(3.8)

and

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\big(H^{w}(X;t)\big)=r(t)\{H^{w}(X;t)+t\log r(t)-v(t)\}.

(3.9)

Now, using
(\ref{eq3.5})
,
(\ref{eq3.6}),
and
(\ref{eq3.7})
in
(\ref{eq3.4})
, we get the required result. This completes the proof.
∎

Next, we obtain an upper bound of the WRVE.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
X
be a non-negative absolutely continuous random variable with PDF
f(\cdot)
satisfying
the condition in (
2.10
).
Then, for all
t>0
, we have

\displaystyle VE^{x}(X;t)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle H^{w_{2}}(X;t)+\Lambda^{2}(t)E[X^{2}|X>t],

where
H^{w_{2}}(X;t)
is the WRSE with weight
w_{2}(x)=\alpha x^{3}+\beta x^{2}
.

Proof.

From (
3.1
) we obtain

\displaystyle VE^{x}(X;t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\infty}x^{2}\frac{f(x)}{\tilde{F}(t)}[\log f(x)+\Lambda(t)]^{2}dx-[H^{x}(X;t)]^{2}

(3.10)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\infty}x^{2}\frac{f(x)}{\tilde{F}(t)}[\log f(x)+\Lambda(t)]^{2}dx.

Using (
2.10
) in the right hand side integral of (
3.10
), we get

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\infty}x^{2}\frac{f(x)}{\tilde{F}(t)}[\log f(x)+\Lambda(t)]^{2}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\infty}x^{2}\frac{f(x)}{\tilde{F}(t)}(\log f(x))^{2}dx+2\int_{t}^{\infty}x^{2}\frac{f(x)}{\tilde{F}(t)}\Lambda(t)\log f(x)dx

(3.11)

\displaystyle+\frac{\Lambda^{2}(t)}{\tilde{F}(t)}\int_{t}^{\infty}x^{2}f(x)dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\int_{t}^{\infty}x^{2}(\alpha x+\beta)\frac{f(x)}{\tilde{F}(t)}\log f(x)dx+\frac{\Lambda(t)}{\tilde{F}(t)}\int_{t}^{\infty}x^{2}f(x)dx

\displaystyle-2\Lambda(t)\int_{t}^{\infty}x^{2}(\alpha x+\beta)\frac{f(x)}{\tilde{F}(t)}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H^{w_{2}}(X;t)+\Lambda^{2}(t)E[X^{2}|X>t].

Now, using (
3.11
) in (
3.10
), the result readily follows.
∎

Next, we obtain a lower bound of the WRVE in terms of the variance residual life (VRL), which is defined as

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(t)=Var[X-t|X>t]=\frac{2}{\tilde{F}(t)}\int_{t}^{\infty}dv\int_{v}^{\infty}\tilde{F}(u)du-[\mu(t)]^{2},

(3.12)

where
\mu(t)
is known as the mean residual lifetime (MRL), defined as

\displaystyle\mu(t)=E[X-t|X>t]=\int_{t}^{\infty}\frac{\tilde{F}(x)}{\tilde{F}(t)}dx.

(3.13)

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose
X_{t}
is the residual lifetime with finite MRL
\mu(t)
and finite VRL
\sigma^{2}(t)
. Then,

\displaystyle{\it VE}^{x}(X;t)\geq\sigma^{2}(t)\{1+E[-\eta_{t}(X_{t})\log f_{t}(X_{t})]+E[X_{t}\eta^{{}^{\prime}}_{t}(X_{t})]\}^{2},

(3.14)

where
\eta_{t}(x)
is a real-valued function, obtained from the relationship

\sigma^{2}(t)\eta_{t}(x)f_{t}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}(\mu(t)-u)f_{t}(u)du,~x>0.

Proof.

For an absolutely continuous random variable
X
with PDF
f(\cdot)
, mean
\mu,
and variance
\sigma^{2}
, the following inequality can be established (see
Cacoullos and Papathanasiou 1989
)

\displaystyle Var[h(X)]\geq\sigma^{2}(E[\xi(X)h^{{}^{\prime}}(X)])^{2},

(3.15)

where a real-valued function
\xi(\cdot)
is defined by the relationship
\sigma^{2}\xi(x)f(x)=\int_{0}^{x}(\mu(t)-u)f(u)du.
In order to prove the result, we assume
X_{t}
as a reference random variable, with
h(x)=IC^{x}(x)=-x\log f(x)
. Thus,

\displaystyle VE^{x}(X;t)=Var[-X_{t}\log f_{t}(X_{t})]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(t)\bigg\{E\big[\eta_{t}(X_{t})\big(X_{t}\log f_{t}(X_{t})\big)^{\prime}\big]\bigg\}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(t)\bigg\{E[-\eta_{t}(X_{t})\log f_{t}(X_{t})]-E\bigg[\eta_{t}(X_{t})X_{t}\frac{f^{{}^{\prime}}_{t}(X_{t})}{f_{t}(X_{t})}\bigg]\bigg\}^{2}.

Further,

\displaystyle E\bigg[\eta_{t}(X_{t})X_{t}\frac{f^{{}^{\prime}}_{t}(X_{t})}{f_{t}(X_{t})}\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\bigg[X_{t}\bigg(\frac{\mu(t)-X_{t}}{\sigma^{2}(t)}-\eta^{{}^{\prime}}_{t}(X_{t})\bigg)\bigg]

(3.17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-1-E[X_{t}\eta^{{}^{\prime}}_{t}(X_{t})].

Using (
3.17
) in (
3
), the required result readily follows, completing the proof of the theorem.
∎

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\phi(\cdot)
be a strictly monotonic, continuous and differentiable function, and
Y=\phi(X)
, where
X
is a non-negative absolutely continuous random variable. Then,

{\it VE}^{y}(Y;t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\it VE}^{\phi}(X;\phi^{-1}(t))-2H^{\phi}(X;\phi^{-1}(t))E[\eta_{1}(X)|X>\phi^{-1}(t)]\\
+Var[\eta_{1}(X)|X>\phi^{-1}(t)]-2E[\phi(X)\eta_{1}(X)\log\frac{f(X)}{\tilde{F}(\phi^{-1}(t)}|X>\phi^{-1}(t)],\\
~if~\phi(\cdot)~is~strictly~increasing,\\
\\
\overline{{\it VE}}^{\phi}(X;\phi^{-1}(t))-2\bar{H}^{\phi}(X;\phi^{-1}(t))E[\eta_{2}(X)|X\leq\phi^{-1}(t)]\\
+Var[\eta_{2}(X)|X\leq\phi^{-1}(t)]-2E[\phi(X)\eta_{2}(X)\log\frac{f(X)}{F(\phi^{-1}(t))}|X\leq\phi^{-1}(t)],\\
~if~\phi(\cdot)~is~strictly~decreasing,\end{array}\right.

(3.18)

where
\eta_{1}(x)=\phi(x)\log\phi^{\prime}(x)
,
\eta_{2}(x)=\phi(x)\log(-\phi^{\prime}(x))
,
H^{\phi}(X;t)=\int_{t}^{\infty}\phi(x)\frac{f(x)}{\tilde{F}(t)}\log\frac{f(x)}{\tilde{F}(t)}dx

{\it VE}^{\phi}(X;t)=\int_{t}^{\infty}\phi^{2}(x)\frac{f(x)}{\tilde{F}(t)}(\log\frac{f(x)}{\tilde{F}(t)})^{2}dx-[H^{\phi}(X;t)]^{2}
,

\bar{H}^{\phi}(X;t)=\int_{0}^{t}\phi(x)\frac{f(x)}{{F}(t)}\log\frac{f(x)}{{F}(t)}dx
,
and

\overline{{\it VE}}^{\phi}(X;t)=\int_{0}^{t}\phi^{2}(x)\frac{f(x)}{{F}(t)}(\log\frac{f(x)}{{F}(t)})^{2}dx-[\bar{H}^{\phi}(X;t)]^{2}
.

Proof.

Let
\phi(\cdot)
be strictly increasing. Then, after some calculations, we get

\displaystyle{\it VE}^{y}(Y;t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\phi^{-1}(t)}^{\phi^{-1}(\infty)}\phi^{2}(x)\frac{f(x)}{\tilde{F}(\phi^{-1}(t))}\bigg[\log\bigg\{\frac{f(x)}{\tilde{F}(\phi^{-1}(t))}\bigg\}\bigg]^{2}dx

(3.19)

\displaystyle-E[\phi^{2}(X)\{2\log\phi^{{}^{\prime}}(X)\log\frac{f(X)}{\tilde{F}(\phi^{-1}(t)}-(\log\phi^{{}^{\prime}}(X))^{2}\}|X>\phi^{-1}(t)]

\displaystyle-\{H^{\phi}(X;\phi^{-1}(t))+E[\phi(X)\log\phi^{{}^{\prime}}(X)|X>\phi^{-1}(t)]\}^{2}.

Now, the rest of the proof readily follows from (
3.19
). The proof when
\phi(\cdot)
is strictly decreasing is analogous. ∎

4
Weighted varentropy of coherent systems

In this section, we study WVE of coherent systems. The coherent systems are useful in various real life applications such as industrial systems, telecommunications and oil pipeline systems. For details, please refer to
Elsayed 2012
. Consider a coherent system with
n
components. The lifetime of the coherent system is denoted by
T
. The random lifetimes of
n
components of the coherent system are independent and identically distributed with a common CDF
F(\cdot)
. The CDF of
T
is expressed as

\displaystyle F_{T}(x)=q(F(x)),

(4.1)

where
q:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
is the distortion function (see
Navarro et al. 2013
). Various authors have studied well-established information measures for coherent systems. For instance, see the references by
Toomaj et al. 2017
,
Calì et al. 2020
, and
Saha and Kayal 2023
. We recall that the distortion function depends on the structure of a system and the copula of the component lifetimes. Further,
q(\cdot)
is continuous and increasing with
q(0)=0
and
q(1)=1
. For details about the distortion function, we refer to
Burkschat and Navarro 2018
. The WVE of
T
with PDF
f_{T}(\cdot)
is defined as

\displaystyle{\it VE}^{x}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2}f_{T}(x)[\log f_{T}(x)]^{2}dx-\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}xf_{T}(x)\log f_{T}(x)dx\bigg]^{2}

(4.2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi\big(F_{T}(x)\big)dx-\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi\big(F_{T}(x)\big)dx\bigg]^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi\big(q(F(x))\big)dx-\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi\big(q(F(x))\big)dx\bigg]^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\phi\big(q(u)\big)}{f\big(F^{-1}(u)\big)}du-\bigg[\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\psi\big(q(u)\big)}{f\big(F^{-1}(u)\big)}du\bigg]^{2},~\mbox{using~u=F(x)},

where

\displaystyle\phi(u)=f(F^{-1}(u))[F^{-1}(u)\log f(F^{-1}(u))]^{2}

(4.3)

and

\displaystyle\psi(u)=F^{-1}(u)f(F^{-1}(u))[-\log f(F^{-1}(u))],

(4.4)

for
0\leq u\leq 1
.
Next, we consider a coherent system with lifetime
T=\max\{X_{1},X_{2}\}
, where
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
denote component lifetimes. It is assumed that
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are independent and both follow power distribution in the interval
(0,a)
.

Example 4.1
.

Suppose
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are component lifetimes of a coherent system with a common CDF
F(x)=(\frac{x}{a})^{k},~~0<x<a,~k>0
, and consider a parallel system with lifetime
T=X_{2:2}=\max\{X_{1},X_{2}\}
. The distortion function is
q(u)=u^{2}
, where
0\leq u\leq 1
. Using (
4.2
), the WVE of the coherent (parallel) system is obtained as

\displaystyle\it{VE}^{x}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a^{2}}{(2+3/k)}\bigg[\big(\log(k/a)\big)-\frac{2(1-1/k)}{(2+3/k)}\bigg]^{2}+\frac{4a^{2}(1-1/k)^{2}}{(2+3/k)^{3}}

(4.5)

\displaystyle-\bigg(\frac{a}{2+1/k}\bigg)^{2}\bigg[\log(k/a)-\frac{2(1-1/k)}{(2+1/k)}\bigg]^{2}.

The graphs of the WVE with respect to
k
and
a
are plotted in Figures

Now, we obtain relation between the WVEs of the lifetimes of a coherent system and its components.

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose
T
is the lifetime of a coherent system with identically distributed components. The distortion function is denoted by
q(\cdot)
. The component lifetime is denoted by
X
with CDF
F(\cdot)
and PDF
f(\cdot)
. Also assume that
\phi(u)=f(F^{-1}(u))[F^{-1}(u)\log f(F^{-1}(u))]^{2}
and
\psi(u)=F^{-1}(u)f(F^{-1}(u))[-\log f(F^{-1}(u))],
for all
u\in[0,1]
. Then, if
\phi\big(q(u)\big)\geq(resp.\leq)\phi(u),
and
\psi\big(q(u)\big)\leq(resp.\geq)\psi(u),
for
0\leq u\leq 1
, we have

{\it VE}^{x}(T)\geq(resp.\leq){\it VE}^{x}(X).

Proof.

The proof is straightforward, and thus it is omitted.
∎

Next, we obtain bound of the WVE of coherent systems in terms of WSE.

Proposition 4.2
.

Consider a coherent system as in Proposition
4.1
. Denote
\sup_{u\in[0,1]}\frac{\phi(q(u))}{\phi(u)}=\beta_{1,u},
where
\phi(u)=f(F^{-1}(u))[F^{-1}(u)\log f(F^{-1}(u))]^{2}.
Then, under the assumption in (
2.10
), we have

\displaystyle VE^{x}(T)\leq\beta_{1,u}H^{w_{2}}(X),

where
H^{w_{2}}(X)
is the WSE with weight
w_{2}(x)=\alpha x^{3}+\beta x^{2}
.

Proof.

From (
4.2
), we have

\displaystyle VE^{x}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\phi\big(q(u)\big)}{f\big(F^{-1}(u)\big)}du-\bigg[\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\psi\big(q(u)\big)}{f\big(F^{-1}(u)\big)}du\bigg]^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\phi\big(q(u)\big)}{\phi(u)}\frac{\phi(u)}{f\big(F^{-1}(u)\big)}du-\bigg[\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\psi\big(q(u)\big)}{f\big(F^{-1}(u)\big)}du\bigg]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bigg(sup_{u\in[0,1]}\frac{\phi(q(u))}{\phi(u)}\bigg)\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\phi(u)}{f\big(F^{-1}(u)\big)}du

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\beta_{1,u}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2}(-\alpha x-\beta)f(x)\log f(x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{1,u}H^{w_{2}}(X).

Hence, the proof is completed.
∎

Below, we obtain bound of the WVE of a coherent system in terms of the WVE of component lifetime and WSE.

Proposition 4.3
.

Consider a coherent system as in Proposition
4.1
. Denote
\sup_{u\in[0,1]}\frac{\phi(q(u))}{\phi(u)}=\beta_{1,u}
, where
\phi(u)=f(F^{-1}(u))[F^{-1}(u)\log f(F^{-1}(u))]^{2}.
Then,

{\it VE}^{x}(T)\leq\beta_{1,u}[{\it VE}^{x}(X)+(H^{x}(X))^{2}].

Proof.

The proof is easy, and thus it is omitted for brevity.
∎

The following proposition provides an upper bound of
{\it VE}^{x}(T)
when the PDF of the component lifetimes of a coherent system have a lower bound.

Proposition 4.4
.

Consider a coherent system in Proposition
4.1
. Let the components have PDF
f(x)
with support
S
, such that

f(x)\geq L>0
for all
x\in S.
Then,

{\it VE}^{x}(T)\leq\frac{1}{L}\int^{1}_{0}\phi\big(q(u)\big)du,
where
\phi(u)=f(F^{-1}(u))[F^{-1}(u)\log f(F^{-1}(u))]^{2}.

Proof.

Using the assumptions made, the proof readily follows from (
4.2
).
∎

We consider the following example for the illustration of Proposition
4.4
.

Example 4.2
.

Supose
T
indicates the lifetime of a coherent system with independent and identically distributed components following log-uniform distribution with distribution fnction
F(x)=\frac{\log x-\log\alpha}{\log\beta-\log\alpha},~0<\alpha\leq x\leq\beta
. Then,
L=\frac{1}{\beta\log(\beta/\alpha)},
and as a result from Proposition
4.4
, we get

{\it VE}^{x}(T)\leq\beta\log\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)\int^{1}_{0}\phi\big(q(u)\big)du,

where
\phi(u)=f(F^{-1}(u))[F^{-1}(u)\log f(F^{-1}(u))]^{2}.

5
WRVE for proportional hazard rate model

In this section, we study WRVE for the proportional hazard rate (PHR) model.
Suppose
X
and
Y
are two non-negative random variables with survival functions
\tilde{F}(\cdot)
and
\tilde{G}(\cdot),
respectively. Then,
X
and
Y
have PHR model if the survival function of
Y
can be written as

\displaystyle\tilde{G}(x)=[\tilde{F}(x)]^{a},~x>0,~a>0.

(5.1)

The PDF of
Y
is

\displaystyle g(x)=a[\tilde{F}(x)]^{a-1}f(x),~x>0,

(5.2)

where
f(x)=-\frac{d}{dx}\tilde{F}(x)
is the (baseline) PDF of
X
. The PHR model was introduced by
Cox 1959
. Later, several researchers studied the PHR model. Recently,
Parsa et al. 2018
studied a characterization in terms of the Gini type index of PHR model.
Here, we introduce WRVE for the PHR model. The cumulative hazard rate function
\Lambda^{*}(x)
of
Y
is given by

\displaystyle\Lambda^{*}(x)=-\log\tilde{G}(x)=-\log[\tilde{F}(x)]^{a}=a\Lambda(x),~x>0,

(5.3)

where
\Lambda(x)=-\log\tilde{F}(x)
is the cumulative hazard rate function.
The WRSE of
Y
can be expressed as

\displaystyle H^{x}(Y;t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{t}^{\infty}x\frac{g(x)}{\tilde{G}(t)}\log g(x)dx-\int_{t}^{\infty}x\frac{g(x)}{\tilde{G}(t)}\Lambda^{*}(t)dx

(5.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{[\tilde{F}(t)]^{a}}\bigg\{\int_{0}^{[\tilde{F}(t)]^{a}}\sigma(y:a)dy+a\Lambda(t)\int_{0}^{[\tilde{F}(t)]^{a}}\tilde{F}^{-1}(y^{1/a})dy\bigg\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{[\tilde{F}(t)]^{a}}\int_{0}^{[\tilde{F}(t)]^{a}}\gamma(y:a,t)dy,

where
y=[\tilde{F}(x)]^{a}
,
\sigma(y:a)=\tilde{F}^{-1}(y^{1/a})\log\big\{ay^{1-1/a}f[\tilde{F}^{-1}(y^{1/a})]\big\}
and

\displaystyle\gamma(y:a,t)=\tilde{F}^{-1}(y^{1/a})\log\big\{a\frac{y^{1-1/a}}{[\tilde{F}(t)]^{a}}f[\tilde{F}^{-1}(y^{1/a})]\big\}.

(5.5)

Note that
\tilde{F}^{-1}(y^{1/a})=\sup\{x:\tilde{F}(x)\geq y^{1/a}\}
is known as the quantile function of
\tilde{F}(\cdot)
. In the following theorem, we evaluate the WRVE for the PHR model.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
Y
have the survival function given by (
5.1
). The WRVE of
Y
is

\displaystyle{\it VE}^{x}(Y;t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{[\tilde{F}(t)]^{a}}\int_{0}^{[\tilde{F}(t)]^{a}}[\gamma(y:a,t)]^{2}dy-\frac{1}{[\tilde{F}(t)]^{2a}}\bigg\{\int_{0}^{[\tilde{F}(t)]^{a}}\gamma(y:a,t)dy\bigg\}^{2},

(5.6)

where
\gamma(y:a,t)
is defined in (
5.5
).

Proof.

From (
3.1
) and using (
5.1
), the WRVE of
Y
is

\displaystyle{\it VE}^{x}(Y;t)=\int_{t}^{\infty}\frac{g(x)}{\tilde{G}(t)}\bigg(x\log\frac{g(x)}{\tilde{G}(t)}\bigg)^{2}dx-[H^{x}(Y;t)]^{2}.

(5.7)

Now, using
y=[\tilde{F}(x)]^{a}
,

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\infty}\frac{g(x)}{\tilde{G}(t)}\bigg(x\log\frac{g(x)}{\tilde{G}(t)}\bigg)^{2}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{[\tilde{F}(t)]^{a}}\bigg\{\int_{0}^{[\tilde{F}(t)]^{a}}[\sigma(y:a)]^{2}dy

(5.8)

\displaystyle+2\Lambda^{(a)}(t)\int_{0}^{[\tilde{F}(t)]^{a}}\tilde{F}^{-1}(y^{1/a})\sigma(y:a)dy

\displaystyle+(\Lambda^{(a)}(t))^{2}\int_{0}^{[\tilde{F}(t)]^{a}}[\tilde{F}^{-1}(y^{1/a})]^{2}dy\bigg\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{[\tilde{F}(t)]^{a}}\int_{0}^{[\tilde{F}(t)]^{a}}[\gamma(y:a,t)]^{2}dy.

Finally, using (
5.4
) and (
5.8
) in (
5.7
), the theorem is established.
∎

(5.9)

Non-parametric estimation of WRVE

In this section, we propose non-parametric estimation of the WRVE in (
3.1
) based on the kernel density estimator of
f(\cdot)
, given by

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(x_{i})=\frac{1}{nb_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K\left(\frac{x-X_{i}}{b_{n}}\right),

(6.1)

where
K(\cdot)~(\geq 0)
is known as the kernel, satisfying Lipschitz condition with
\int K(x)dx=1
. It is known that the kernel
K(\cdot)
is symmetric with respect to the origin and
\{b_{n}\}
, known as bandwidths is a sequence of positive real numbers such that
b_{n}\rightarrow 0
and
nb_{n}\rightarrow\infty,
for
n\rightarrow\infty
. For more details about kernel density estimator, the readers can see the references by
Rosenblatt 1956
and
Parzen 1962
. The non-parametric kernel estimator of the WRVE with weight
w(x)=x
is

\displaystyle\widehat{\it{VE}^{x}}(X;t)=\int_{t}^{\infty}\widehat{\delta}(x)(x\log\widehat{\delta}(x))^{2}dx-\left[\int_{t}^{\infty}x\widehat{\delta}(x)\log\widehat{\delta}(x)dx\right]^{2};~t\geq 0,

(6.2)

where
\widehat{\delta}(x)=\frac{\widehat{f}(x)}{\widehat{\tilde{F}}(t)}
and
\widehat{\tilde{F}}(t)=\int_{t}^{\infty}\widehat{f}(x)dx
. Next, we consider a simulated data set and two real data sets for illustrating the proposed non-parametric estimator in (
6.2
).

(6.3)

where

7
Conclusions

In this paper, we have introduced the concept of WVE and WRVE. Various properties of these two variability measures have been obtained. The effect under affine transformations and some bounds have been obtained. A justification of the usefullness of WRVE over the RVE has been illustrated. Further, the WVE has been studied for the coherent systems. The sufficient conditions, under which the WVE of a system lifetime is smaller/larger than that of its component lifetimes have been derived. The WRVE has been also studied for PHR models. A non-parametric estimator of the WRVE has been proposed, and then the estimator has been illustrated using a simulated data set and two real-life data sets. Finally, we mention here that the proposed measures could be introduced in a more general context omitting the non-negativity assumption. However, its use and interpretation may need to be examined carefully.
