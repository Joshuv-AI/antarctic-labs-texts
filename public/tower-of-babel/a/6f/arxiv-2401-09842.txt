Solutions to some sign change problems on the functions involving sums of divisors
Yuchen Ding, Hao Pan and Yu–Chen Sun

Abstract
. In this note, we solve some sign change problems on the functions involving sums of divisors posed by Pongsriiam recently.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
11A25, 11B25, 11N64

1.
Introduction

Inspiring by the prior results of Erdős
[
3
,
9
]
, Wang and Chen
[
10
]
, Pongsriiam
[
7
]
investigated the arithmetic functions on generalized sums of divisors functions, i.e.,

\sigma_{s}(n)=\sum_{d|n}d^{s},

where
s
is a real number. He obtained various results in the same spirit of Newman’s theorem
[
6
]
which states that there are infinitely many
n
such that

\varphi(30n+1)<\varphi(30n),

where
\varphi(n)
is the Euler totient function. Newman’s result is of particular interest because Jarden
[
5
, page 65]
observed that

(1.1)

\displaystyle\varphi(30n+1)>\varphi(30n)

for all
n\leq 10^{5}
. The fact that there are infinite many
n
making Eq. (
1.1
) true follows immediately from Dirichlet’s theorem in arithmetic progressions. In other words, we know that

\varphi(30n+1)-\varphi(30n)

changes sign infinitely often, thanks to the theorems of Dirichlet and Newman. It contributes to another famous example of the Chebyshev bias phenomenon
[
1
]
.

From now on, let
\mathbb{N}
and
\mathcal{P}
be the sets of positive integers and primes, respectively. In Pongsriiam’s article
[
7
]
, he posed several open problems for further research.

Problem 1.1

(
[
7
]
, Problem 3.7)
.

Suppose
s>1
and
a,b,c,d
are positive integers. If

\sigma_{s}(an+b)-\sigma_{s}(cn+d)

changes sign infinitely often, is it true that
a=c
? If it is not true, assuming further that
s
is large and
(a,b)=(c,d)=1
, can we conclude that
a=c
?

This problem, as one can imagine, is motivated by Pongsriiam’s result
[
7
, Theorem 2.10]
, which states that if
a,b,c,d
are integers with
a>c>0
and
b\geq d\geq 0
, then there exists
s_{0}>1
such that

\sigma_{s}(an+b)>\sigma_{s}(cn+d)

for all
s\geq s_{0}
and
n\geq 1
.

Problem 1.2

(
[
7
]
, Problem 3.8 (ii))
.

Is it true that

\sum_{n\leq K}\sigma(30n)>\sum_{n\leq K}\sigma(30n+1)

for all
K\in\mathbb{N}
?

Pongsriiam
[
7
, Theorem 2.4]
himself proved that

\sigma(30n)-\sigma(30n+1)

has infinitely many sign changes. Thus, Problem
1.2
, if true, is certainly nontrivial.

Problem 1.3

(
[
7
]
, last two lines of Example 2.13)
.

Are there infinitely many
m\in\mathbb{N}
for which the inequalities

\sigma(2m+5)>\sigma(6m+17)\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\sigma(5m+4)>\sigma(6m+7)

simultaneously hold.

This problem is motivated by Pongsriiam’s result
[
7
, Theorem 2.11]
, where he proved that

\sigma_{s}(2m+5)<\sigma_{s}(6m+17)\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\sigma_{s}(5m+4)<\sigma_{s}(6m+7)

for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
s>3
.

The aim of our note is the following progress on the problems above.

Theorem 1.1
.

The answer to Problem
1.1
is negative.

Theorem 1.2
.

The answer to Problem
1.2
is positive providing that
K
is sufficiently large.

Theorem 1.3
.

The answer to Problem
1.3
is positive.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

For the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we need the following remarkable theorem of Dirichlet on primes in arithmetic progressions (see e.g.
[
2
, Page 34, Chapter 4]
).

Lemma 2.1

(Dirichlet)
.

Let
m
and
q
be integers such that
(m,q)=1
, then there are infinitely many primes
p\equiv m\pmod{q}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
s>1
be any fixed number. It suffices to prove that there exists some positive odd integer
a
(depending on
s
) such that

\sigma_{s}(an+2)-\sigma_{s}((a+1)n+1)

changes sign infinitely often. The proof goes as follows:

Note firstly that
(a,2)=1
. Then by Lemma
2.1
, there are infinitely many positive integers
n>1
such that
an+2
are primes. For these
n
, we have

\sigma_{s}(an+2)=1+(an+2)^{s}

and

\sigma_{s}((a+1)n+1)\geq 1+((a+1)n+1)^{s}>1+(an+2)^{s},

from which it follows clearly that

\sigma_{s}(an+1)-\sigma_{s}((a+1)n+1)<0.

Now, suppose that
n=2k
with
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, then

(a+1)n+1=2(a+1)k+1.

Since
(2(a+1),1)=1
, there are infinitely many
k
such that
(a+1)n+1
are primes. For these
n=2k
, we have

\sigma_{s}(an+2)\geq 1+\left(\frac{an+2}{2}\right)^{s}+(an+2)^{s}

and

\sigma_{s}((a+1)n+1)=1+((a+1)n+1)^{s}.

Thus, for these
n=2k
we can deduce

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(an+2)-\sigma_{s}((a+1)n+1)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{an+2}{2}\right)^{s}+(an+2)^{s}-((a+1)n+1)^{s}

\displaystyle>\left(1+\frac{1}{2^{s}}\right)a^{s}n^{s}-(a+2)^{s}n^{s}.

Now, we are going to show that

(2.1)

\displaystyle(a+2)^{s}<\left(1+\frac{1}{2^{s+1}}\right)a^{s}

provided that
a
is sufficiently large, from which our theorem would follow immediately.
Actually, Eq. (
2.1
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle a>2\left(\left(1+\frac{1}{2^{s+1}}\right)^{1/s}-1\right)^{-1},

which is clearly true for sufficiently large odd integer
a
.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

The proof of Theorem
1.2
follows from the following result
[
8
, III. 8, page 85]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

f(x)
be a polynomial with integer coefficients, of degree
n
, and such that
f(m)>0
for all positive integers
m
. Then we have

\sum_{m\leq x}\sigma(f(m))=\frac{\beta a_{n}}{n+1}x^{n+1}+O\left(x^{n}(\log x)^{n}\right),

where

\beta=\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\frac{N(d)}{d^{2}}

and
a_{n}
is the coefficient of
x^{n}
in
f(x)
. Here
N(m)
denotes the number of solutions, not counting multiplicities, of the congruence
f(n)\equiv 0\pmod{m}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Note that for given
f
the function
N(m)
is multiplicative of
m
by the Chinese reminder theorem. So we get

\beta=\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\frac{N(d)}{d^{2}}=\prod_{p}\left(1+\frac{N(p)}{p^{2}}+\frac{N(p^{2})}{p^{4}}+\cdot\cdot\cdot\right),

where the products extends over all the primes.

Choose firstly
f(x)=30x
in Lemma
3.1
. Then for
p\nmid 30
we clearly have
N\left(p^{\alpha}\right)=1
for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
. It is also easy to see that

N\left(p^{\alpha}\right)=p

for
p=2,3,5
and any
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, we have

\beta=\prod_{p}\left(1+\frac{N(p)}{p^{2}}+\frac{N(p^{2})}{p^{4}}+\cdot\cdot\cdot\right)=\frac{5}{3}\frac{11}{8}\frac{29}{24}\prod_{p~\!\!\nmid~\!\!30}\left(1-p^{-2}\right)^{-1}=\frac{1595}{576}\prod_{p~\!\!\nmid~\!\!30}\left(1-p^{-2}\right)^{-1}.

Hence, we obtain

(3.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq K}\sigma(30n)=\frac{1595}{576}\cdot 15\prod_{p~\!\!\nmid~\!\!30}\left(1-p^{-2}\right)^{-1}x^{2}+O\left(x\log x\right),

via Lemma
3.1
.

Choose secondly
f(x)=30x+1
in Lemma
3.1
. Then for
p\nmid 30
we have
N\left(p^{\alpha}\right)=1
for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
. It is also easy to see that

N\left(p^{\alpha}\right)=0

for
p=2,3,5
and any
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, we have

\beta=\prod_{p}\left(1+\frac{N(p)}{p^{2}}+\frac{N(p^{2})}{p^{4}}+\cdot\cdot\cdot\right)=\prod_{p~\!\!\nmid~\!\!30}\left(1-p^{-2}\right)^{-1}.

Hence, we obtain

(3.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq K}\sigma(30n+1)=15\prod_{p~\!\!\nmid~\!\!30}\left(1-p^{-2}\right)^{-1}x^{2}+O\left(x\log x\right),

again via Lemma
3.1
.

It can be concluded from Eqs. (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) that

\lim_{K\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\sum_{n\leq K}\sigma(30n)}{\sum_{n\leq K}\sigma(30n+1)}=\frac{1595}{576}>1,

from which our theorem follows immediately.
∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

Below, we shall prove a much more stronger result comparing with Theorem
1.3
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
L_{i}(x)=a_{i}x+b_{i},~H_{j}(x)=c_{j}x+d_{j}~(1\leq i,j\leq k)
be linear functions with integer coefficients such that
a_{i},c_{j}>0
and

a_{i}d_{j}\neq b_{i}c_{j}\quad(~\forall~1\leq i,j\leq k).

Suppose that for any prime
p
there is at least one integer
n_{p}
such that
p
divides none of the
L_{i}(n_{p})
, then there are
\gg x(\log x)^{-k}
integers
n\leq x
such that

\sigma(H_{j}(n))>\max_{1\leq i\leq k}\sigma(L_{i}(n))\quad\quad(~\forall~1\leq j\leq k),

where the constant implied by
\gg
depends on the choices of the functions
L_{i}
and
H_{j}
.

Let
\Omega(n)
be the number of all prime factors of
n
.
We need a sieve result of Heath–Brown
[
4
, Theorem 1]
.

Lemma 4.1

(Heath–Brown)
.

Let
L_{i}(x)=a_{i}x+b_{i},~(1\leq i\leq k)
be linear functions with integer coefficients and
a_{i}>0
. Suppose that for any prime
p
there is at least one integer
n_{p}
such that
p
divides none of the
L_{i}(n_{p})
, then there are
\gg x(\log x)^{-k}
integers
n\leq x
such that

\max_{1\leq i\leq k}\Omega(L_{i}(n))\leq G_{k},

where the constant
G_{k}
is given by

G_{k}=\left\lfloor\log_{2}\left\lfloor\frac{3k^{2}+4k+4}{2}\right\rfloor\right\rfloor.

Our idea on the proof of Theorem
4.1
, roughly speaking, is to choose some suitable integers
n
such that
L_{i}(n)
has ‘small’ prime factors whereas
H_{j}(n)
has ‘many’ prime factors. Let’s implement our idea below.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Let
p_{\ell}
be the
\ell^{\text{th}}
prime. For any
h
there exists some integer
n_{\ell}
such that

(4.1)

\displaystyle p_{\ell}\nmid\prod_{1\leq i\leq k}L_{i}(n_{\ell})

by the condition of the theorem. Let

A=\max_{1\leq i\leq k}a_{i},\quad B=\max_{1\leq j\leq k}c_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad C=\max_{1\leq i,j\leq k}|a_{i}d_{j}-b_{i}c_{j}|

Suppose that
p_{\ell_{1}}
is the smallest prime number strictly greater than
A+B+C+k
, then
there are
k
strings of consecutive primes

p_{\ell_{1}+1},p_{\ell_{1}+2},...,p_{\ell_{2}};\quad p_{\ell_{2}+1},p_{\ell_{2}+2},...,p_{\ell_{3}};\quad\cdot\cdot\cdot\cdot\cdot\cdot;\quad p_{\ell_{k}+1},p_{\ell_{k}+2},...,p_{\ell_{k+1}}

such that for any
1\leq s\leq k
we have

(4.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=\ell_{s}+1}^{\ell_{s+1}}\frac{1}{p_{\ell}}>A2^{G_{k}+3}.

By the Chinese reminder theorem, the system of congruences

\begin{cases}n\equiv n_{\ell}\pmod{p_{\ell}},\quad\quad\quad\quad(~\forall~1\leq\ell\leq\ell_{1})\\
c_{1}n+d_{1}\equiv 0\pmod{p_{\ell}},\quad(~\forall~\ell_{1}+1\leq\ell\leq\ell_{2}),\\
c_{2}n+d_{2}\equiv 0\pmod{p_{\ell}},\quad(~\forall~\ell_{2}+1\leq\ell\leq\ell_{3}),\\
\qquad\vdots\\
c_{k}n+d_{k}\equiv 0\pmod{p_{\ell}},\quad(~\forall~\ell_{k}+1\leq\ell\leq\ell_{k+1})\end{cases}

has exactly one solution
n\equiv n_{0}\pmod{P}
, where

P=p_{1}\cdot\cdot\cdot p_{\ell_{1}}\prod_{1\leq s\leq k}\Big(\prod_{\ell_{s}+1\leq\ell\leq\ell_{s}+1}p_{\ell}\Big).

Suppose now that
n=mP+n_{0}\leq x
, then
m\leq(x-n_{0})/P
. We claim that

\widetilde{L_{i}}(m)=L_{i}(n)=L_{i}(mP+n_{0})=a_{i}Pm+(a_{i}n_{0}+b_{i})\quad(1\leq i\leq k)

has no fixed prime divisor, that is to say, for any prime
p
there is at least one integer
\widetilde{m}_{p}
such that
p
divides none of the
\widetilde{L_{i}}(\widetilde{m}_{p})
. In fact, we choose
\widetilde{m}_{p}=0
for
p\leq p_{\ell_{1}}
. Then by the system of congruences and Eq. (
4.1
), we have

\widetilde{L_{i}}(0)\equiv a_{i}n_{0}+b_{i}\equiv a_{i}n_{\ell}+b_{i}\not\equiv 0\pmod{p_{\ell}}

providing that
\ell\leq\ell_{1}
.
For any
p_{\ell_{1}+1}\leq p\leq p_{\ell_{k+1}}
, we choose again
\widetilde{m}_{p}=0
. Note that there is some
1\leq j\leq k
such that

c_{j}n_{0}+d_{j}\equiv 0\pmod{p}

by the system of congruences again. Then

n_{0}\equiv-c_{j}^{-1}d_{j}\pmod{p}

due to the fact that
p>B
.
Thus, for this prime
p
we have

\widetilde{L_{i}}(0)\equiv a_{i}n_{0}+b_{i}\equiv a_{i}(-c_{j}^{-1}d_{j})+b_{i}\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}

since
p>C
and

a_{i}d_{j}\neq b_{i}c_{j}\quad(~\forall~1\leq i,j\leq k).

Finally, for any prime
p>p_{\ell_{k+1}}
, we have
(p,a_{i}P)=1
. Then
there is exactly one solution to the congruence

\widetilde{L_{i}}(m)\equiv 0\pmod{p}

for each
\widetilde{L_{i}}(m)
. Hence, the congruence

\widetilde{L_{1}}(m)\cdot\cdot\cdot\widetilde{L_{k}}(m)\equiv 0\pmod{p}

has at most
k(<p)
solutions, which completes the proof of our claim.

By our claim and Lemma
4.1
there are

\gg\frac{x-n_{0}}{P}\left(\log\frac{x-n_{0}}{P}\right)^{-k}\gg_{k}x(\log x)^{-k}

integers
m\leq(x-n_{0})/P
such that

\max_{1\leq i\leq k}\Omega\left(L_{i}(n)\right)=\max_{1\leq i\leq k}\Omega\left(\widetilde{L}_{i}(m)\right)\leq G_{k}.

For such integers
m
, i.e.,
n=mP+n_{0}
, we have

(4.3)

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i\leq k}\sigma\left(L_{i}(n)\right)\leq 2An\cdot 2^{G_{k}}=2^{G_{k}+1}An,

provided that
n
is sufficiently large.
On the other hand, for such integers
n=mP+n_{0}
we have

(4.4)

\displaystyle\sigma(H_{j}(n))\geq\sum_{\ell=\ell_{j}+1}^{\ell_{j+1}}\frac{c_{j}n+d_{j}}{p_{\ell}}\geq\frac{n}{2}A2^{G_{k}+3}=2^{G_{k}+2}An

via Eq. (
4.2
), provided that
n
is sufficiently large.
Our theorem now follows directly from Eqs. (
4.3
) and (
4.4
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

We take
L_{1}(x)=6x+17
,
L_{2}(x)=6x+7
,
H_{1}(x)=2x+5
and
H_{2}(x)=5x+4
in Theorem
4.1
.
It can be easily checked that
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
have no fixed prime divisor and

6\cdot 5\neq 2\cdot 17,\quad 6\cdot 4\neq 5\cdot 17,\quad 6\cdot 5\neq 2\cdot 7,\quad 6\cdot 4\neq 5\cdot 7.

Thus, we complete the proof of Theorem
1.3
.
∎

Remark 1
.

It is worth mentioning that some corresponding results can be established in the same way if one replaces the function
\sigma(\cdot)
by
\sigma_{s}(\cdot)
in Theorem
4.1
with
s\leq 1
(but not applicable for
s>1
since the corresponding inequality for Eq. (
4.2
) will no longer hold). These variants of Theorem
4.1
, in principle, provide partial answers to some other problems of Pongsriiam (see
[
7
, Problem 3.2]
and
[
7
, Problem 3.3]
). We do not expand details of these variants for the simplicity of our note as far as possible.
