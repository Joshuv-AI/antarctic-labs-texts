Electric conductivity in graphene: Kubo model versus a nonlocal quantum field theory model
Pablo Rodriguez-Lopez, Jian-Sheng Wang, Mauro Antezza

Abstract
We compare three models of graphene electric conductivity: a non-local Kubo model, a local model derived by Falkovsky, and finally, a non-local quantum field theory (QFT) polarization-based model. These models are supposed to provide consistent results since they are derived from the same Hamiltonian. While we confirm that the local model is a proper 𝒒 → 𝟎 \bm{q}\to\bm{0} limit of both the non-local Kubo and the non-local QFT model (once losses are added to this last model), we find hard inconsistencies in the non-local QFT model as derived and currently used in literature. In particular, in the genuine non-local region ( 𝒒 ≠ 𝟎 \bm{q}\neq\bm{0} ), the available QFT model shows an intrinsic non-physical plasma-like behavior for the interband transversal electric conductivity at low frequencies (even after introducing the unavoidable losses). The Kubo model, instead, shows the expected behavior, i.e., an almost constant electric conductivity as a function of frequency ω \omega with a gap for frequencies ℏ ​ ω < ( ℏ ​ v F ​ q ) 2 + 4 ​ m 2 \hbar\omega<\sqrt{(\hbar v_{F}q)^{2}+4m^{2}} . We show that the Kubo and QFT models can be expressed using an identical Polarization operator Π μ ​ ν ​ ( ω , 𝒒 ) \Pi_{\mu\nu}(\omega,\bm{q}) , but they employ different expressions for the electric conductivity σ μ ​ ν ​ ( ω , 𝒒 ) \sigma_{\mu\nu}(\omega,\bm{q}) . In particular, the Kubo model uses a standard regularized expression, a direct consequence of Ohm’s Law and causality, as we rigorously re-derive. We show that, once the standard regularized expression for σ μ ​ ν ​ ( ω , 𝒒 ) \sigma_{\mu\nu}(\omega,\bm{q}) is used in the QFT model, and losses are included, the Kubo and QFT model coincide, and all its anomalies naturally disappear. Our findings show the necessity to appropriately define and regularize the electric conductivity to connect it with the available QFT model. This can be relevant for theory, predictions, and experimental tests in the nanophotonics and Casimir effect communities.

\displaystyle\sum_{s=\pm}\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{k}}\Psi_{s}^{\dagger}(\bm{k}_{\parallel})\left[\hbar\omega\alpha^{0}-\hat{H}_{s}^{D}(\bm{k}_{\parallel})\right]\Psi_{s}(\bm{k}_{\parallel})

(see tab.

\gamma^{0}=\alpha^{4}\alpha^{0}=\alpha^{4}

\gamma^{4}=\alpha^{4}\alpha^{4}=\alpha^{0}

\hline\cr\tau_{i}&0\end{array}\right).

The usual anti-commutation relations are fulfilled

\displaystyle\{\gamma^{\mu},\gamma^{\nu}\}=2g^{\mu\nu}\gamma^{4}\hskip 10.0pt\forall\{\mu,\nu\}\in\{0,1,2,3\},

(70)

where

\displaystyle S_{0}

(73)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{s}^{W}(\bm{k}_{\parallel})=\tilde{k}_{0}\gamma^{0}-\left(\gamma^{1}\tilde{k}_{1}+\gamma^{2}\tilde{k}_{2}\right)-\gamma^{3}m_{z}-\gamma^{4}m,

II.4
Green function

For a general linear Hamiltonian
\hat{\mathcal{H}}(\bm{r})
, we have two different expressions of the same Green function. Defining
\tilde{k}_{0}=\hbar\omega
, the Green function fulfills

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}(\bm{r})\mathcal{G}_{0}(\bm{r},\bm{s})=\delta(\bm{r}-\bm{s}),

(81)

then, in momentum space we have
\hat{\mathcal{H}}(\bm{k})\mathcal{G}_{0}(\bm{k})=\mathbbm{1}
and, therefore
\mathcal{G}_{0}(\bm{k})=\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}(\bm{k})
. For each of the four-spinor Hamiltonians, its inverse operator is

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{0}^{D}(\bm{k})=\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{D}^{-1}(\bm{k})=\frac{1}{\alpha^{\rho}K_{\rho}-\alpha^{4}m}=\frac{\alpha^{\rho}K_{\rho}+\alpha^{4}m}{K^{\rho}K_{\rho}-m^{2}},

(82)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{0}^{W}(\bm{k})=\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{W}^{-1}(\bm{k})=\frac{1}{\gamma^{\rho}K_{\rho}-\gamma^{4}m}=\frac{\gamma^{\rho}K_{\rho}+\gamma^{4}m}{K^{\rho}K_{\rho}-m^{2}},

(83)

where we define
K_{\rho}=(\hbar\omega+\mu,\hbar v_{F}k_{1},\hbar v_{F}k_{2},m_{z})
(see tab.
1
), the Einstein summation convention is assumed and

\displaystyle K^{\rho}K_{\rho}-m^{2}=\prod_{\lambda=\pm}\left[\hbar\omega+\mu-\epsilon^{\lambda}_{\bm{k}}\right]=\prod_{\lambda=\pm}\left[\hbar\omega-\xi^{\lambda}_{\bm{k}}\right],

(84)

where
\xi^{\lambda}_{\bm{k}}=\epsilon^{\lambda}_{\bm{k}}-\mu
,

\displaystyle\epsilon^{\lambda}_{\bm{k}}=\lambda\sqrt{\tilde{k}_{\parallel}^{2}+m_{z}^{2}+m^{2}},

(85)

with
\tilde{k}_{\parallel}=\hbar v_{F}k_{\parallel}
and
k_{\parallel}=\sqrt{k_{1}^{2}+k_{2}^{2}}
(see tab.
1
). There is another equivalent expression for a general linear Hamiltonian of the form
\hbar\omega\psi=\tilde{k}_{0}\psi=\hat{H}\psi
in terms of the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the Hamiltonian, starting again from the equation for the Green function
\left(\tilde{k}_{0}-\hat{H}\right)G_{0}(\bm{r},\bm{s})=\delta(\bm{r}-\bm{s})
, and from the eigenproblem

\displaystyle\hat{H}\Big|{u_{\bm{k}}^{\lambda}}\Big\rangle=\left(\epsilon_{\bm{k}}^{\lambda}-\mu\right)\Big|{u_{\bm{k}}^{\lambda}}\Big\rangle=\xi_{\bm{k}}^{\lambda}\Big|{u_{\bm{k}}^{\lambda}}\Big\rangle,

(86)

where the chemical potential
\mu
has been included. The grand-canonical Green function in momentum space is

\displaystyle G_{0}(k)=\sum_{\lambda}\dfrac{\Big|{u_{\bm{k}}^{\lambda}}\Big\rangle\Big\langle{u_{\bm{k}}^{\lambda}}\Big|}{\tilde{k}_{0}-\xi_{\bm{k}}^{\lambda}},

(87)

which is an eigenvalue expansion of the Green function. In our case, the Hamiltonian is diagonal in spin. Therefore, the Green functions are multiplied by
\delta_{ss^{\prime}}
.

II.5
Presence of an electromagnetic field

We introduce the coupling of the electronic quasiparticles of the lattice to the electromagnetic field via the Peierls substitution
[
44
]
[
61
]

\displaystyle H=-\sum_{\bm{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\sum_{j=1}^{|\bm{\delta}_{j}|<\delta_{M}}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\ell,\bm{R}_{\bm{n}}}t_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}(\bm{\delta}_{j})e^{-\mathrm{i}Q\bm{A}_{\bm{R}_{\bm{n}}}\cdot\bm{\delta}_{j}}\hat{c}_{\ell^{\prime},\bm{R}_{\bm{n}}+\bm{\delta}_{j}},

(88)

where we have approximated

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}Q\int_{\bm{R}_{\bm{n}}}^{\bm{R}_{\bm{n}}+\bm{\delta}_{j}}\bm{A}(\bm{r})\cdot\mathrm{d}\bm{r}\right)\approx e^{-\mathrm{i}Q\bm{A}_{\bm{R}_{\bm{n}}}\cdot\bm{\delta}_{j}},

(89)

being

the electric charge of the quasi-particle described by the Hamiltonian, and where we have

for electronic excitations. At the linear order in
\bm{A}_{\bm{q}}
, the Hamiltonian in reciprocal space is

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\bm{q}}\int_{\bm{p}}\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\hat{c}_{\bm{p}+\bm{q}}^{\ell,\dagger}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{|\delta_{j}|<\delta_{M}}t_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}(\bm{\delta}_{j})e^{\mathrm{i}\left(\bm{p}-Q\bm{A}_{\bm{q}}\right)\cdot\bm{\delta}_{j}}\right]\hat{c}_{\bm{p}}^{\ell^{\prime}}

(90)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\bm{q}}\int_{\bm{p}}\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\hat{c}_{\bm{p}+\bm{q}}^{\ell,\dagger}\hat{H}^{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\left(\bm{p}-Q\bm{A}_{\bm{q}}\right)\hat{c}_{\bm{p}}^{\ell^{\prime}}.

It is clear that, at first order in
\bm{A}_{\bm{q}}
, the inclusion of the Peierls substitution leads to a minimal coupling of the momentum
[
44
]
. To study electric conductivity, we need an expression for the current, understood as the potential vector’s conjugated force. Then, expanding the Hamiltonian at linear order in
\bm{A}
, we obtain

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{0}-\int_{\bm{q}}J_{\mu,-\bm{q}}A^{\mu}_{\bm{q}}=H_{0}-\int_{\bm{q}}J^{*}_{\mu,\bm{q}}A^{\mu}_{\bm{q}}

(91)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle H_{0}+\int_{\bm{q}}\dfrac{\delta\hat{H}}{\delta A^{\mu}_{\bm{q}}}A^{\mu}_{\bm{q}}.

Therefore, the second quantized current is defined as

\displaystyle J_{\mu,\bm{q}}^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\dfrac{\delta\hat{H}}{\delta A^{\mu}_{\bm{q}}}=\int_{\bm{p}}\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\hat{c}_{\bm{p}+\bm{q}}^{\ell,\dagger}\hat{J}_{\mu}^{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\left(\bm{p}\right)\hat{c}_{\bm{p}}^{\ell^{\prime}},

(92)

with the current operator given as

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{\mu}^{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\left(\bm{p}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left.\dfrac{\partial\hat{H}^{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\left(\bm{p}-Q\bm{A}_{\bm{q}}\right)}{\partial A_{\bm{q}}^{\mu}}\right|_{\bm{A}_{\bm{q}}\to\bm{0}}=Q\dfrac{\partial\hat{H}^{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\left(\bm{p}\right)}{\partial p^{\mu}}

(93)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Q}{\hbar}\dfrac{\partial\hat{H}^{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\left(\bm{k}\right)}{\partial k^{\mu}}=Q\hat{v}_{\mu},

where
\bm{p}=\hbar\bm{k}
, and
\hat{v}_{\mu}
is the velocity operator of the electronic quasiparticles. It is worth stressing that while in Eq. (

) we have a perturbation term associated with a generic electromagnetic field, for the definition of the electric conductivity, we have to consider a perturbation generated exclusively by an electric field, as we have done in Sec.
III.1
.

III
Electric response of graphene: the regularized QFT model is identical to the Kubo model

In this section, we derive Ohm’s Law starting from the electric field perturbation (the effect of the magnetic field is neglected in this derivation since we are interested only in the pure electric response part), we hence derive the definition of the electric conductivity including the natural regularization present in the Kubo formalism. We compare this Kubo electric conductivity with the one given usually in QFT. We show that the induced electric current in the case of zero electric field goes to zero when using the Kubo formula for the electric conductivity, while the same is not necessarily true when the QFT non-regularized expression is employed.

III.1
Derivation of Ohm’s Law and the Kubo formula for the electric conductivity

In this section, we are going to review the Kubo formula for the electric conductivity
J_{\mu}=\sigma_{\mu\nu}^{\rm{K}}E^{\nu}
(compare with Eq. (
131
)). We start from the interaction Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H=H_{0}+H_{I}(t),

(94)

with the perturbation due to only an electric field (Eqs. (2.1) and (5.7) of
[
10
]
)

\displaystyle H_{I}(t)=-\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\hat{d}_{\nu}(\bm{x})E^{\nu}(\bm{x},t),

(95)

where we are carrying out the integral over the volume
\Omega
, we have defined the dipolar moment operator as
\hat{d}_{\nu}(\bm{x})=Q\hat{x}_{\nu}
and the electric current as
\hat{J}_{\nu}(\bm{x})=Q\hat{v}_{\nu}=Q\dot{\hat{x}}_{\nu}
, therefore, we have

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{\nu}(\bm{x})=Q\dot{\hat{x}}_{\nu}=\dot{\hat{d}}_{\nu}(\bm{x}).

(96)

It is worth stressing that there is always a magnetic field associated with an oscillating electric field, and this magnetic field can induce current as well. Here, we are not considering these currents because they are not part of electric conductivity, defined as the transport coefficient that relates the induced electric current with the electric field (see Eq. (
2
)), the transport coefficient that relates the induced electric current with the magnetic field will be studied in a future work
[
62
]
.

Then, a direct application of the Kubo formula for linear transport theory (Eqs. (2.10), (2.17) and (2.19) of
[
10
]
),

\displaystyle\big\langle\hat{J}_{\mu}(\bm{x},t)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big\langle\hat{J}_{\mu}(\bm{x},t_{0})\big\rangle-\dfrac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\text{Tr}\left(\hat{\rho}_{\beta}\big[\hat{J}_{\mu}(\bm{x},t),\,H_{I}(\tau)\big]\right),

(97)

where
[\hat{J}_{\mu}(\bm{x},t),H_{I}(\tau)]
is the commutator between
\hat{J}_{\mu}(\bm{x},t)
and
H_{I}(\tau)
. To avoid any transitory to the new (non-equilibrium) steady state
[
52
]
, we make the switching starts at
t_{0}\to-\infty
and impose
\displaystyle\lim_{t_{0}\to-\infty}\big\langle\hat{J}_{\mu}(\bm{x},t_{0})\big\rangle=0
. Using
H_{I}(t)
as defined in Eq. (
90
), we obtain the Kubo expression of Eq. (
1
)

\displaystyle\big\langle\hat{J}_{\mu}(\bm{x},t)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\bm{y}\dfrac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\text{Tr}\left(\hat{\rho}_{\beta}\big[\hat{J}_{\mu}(\bm{x},t),\,\hat{J}_{\nu}(\bm{y},\tau)\big]\right)A^{\nu}(\bm{y},\tau)=-\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\bm{y}\Pi_{\mu\nu}(\bm{x},t;\bm{y},\tau)A^{\nu}(\bm{y},\tau),

(98)

Using
H_{I}(t)
as defined in Eq. (
95

[
10
]
)

\displaystyle\big\langle\hat{J}_{\mu}(\bm{x},t)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\bm{y}\dfrac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\text{Tr}\left(\hat{\rho}_{\beta}\big[\hat{J}_{\mu}(\bm{x},t),\,\hat{d}_{\nu}(\bm{y},\tau)\big]\right)E^{\nu}(\bm{y},\tau)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\bm{y}\sigma_{\mu\nu}(\bm{x},t;\bm{y},\tau)E^{\nu}(\bm{y},\tau).

Now, using Eq. (
96

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\phantom{\tau}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\dfrac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\text{Tr}\left(\hat{\rho}_{\beta}\big[\hat{J}_{\mu}(\bm{x},t),\,\hat{d}_{\nu}(\bm{y},\tau)\big]\right)=\dfrac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\text{Tr}\left(\hat{\rho}_{\beta}\big[\hat{J}_{\mu}(\bm{x},t),\,\hat{J}_{\nu}(\bm{y},\tau)\big]\right).

(100)

Using that the polarization operator in the position’s space is defined (equivalently to the definition of Eq. (

(102)

Imposing
\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\to-\infty}\sigma_{\mu\nu}(\bm{x},t;\bm{y},\tau)=0

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mu\nu}(\bm{x},t;\bm{y},T)=-\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{T}\mathrm{d}\tau\Pi_{\mu\nu}(\bm{x},t;\bm{y},\tau).

(103)

where the electric conductivity tensor is (Eq. (5.14) of

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mu\nu}(\bm{x},t;\bm{y},T)=\dfrac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{T}\mathrm{d}\tau\text{Tr}\left(\hat{\rho}_{\beta}\big[\hat{J}_{\mu}(\bm{x},t),\,\hat{J}_{\nu}(\bm{y},\tau)\big]\right)

(104)

Note that, for the electric conductivity, we only are considering electric currents generated by a non-zero electric field; it is to say, if

\big\langle\bm{J}\big\rangle=\bm{0}

37

\displaystyle\big\langle J_{\mu}(\bm{x},t)\big\rangle=-\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\bm{y}\sigma_{\mu\nu}(\bm{x},t;\bm{y},\tau)\partial_{\tau}A^{\nu}(\bm{y},\tau).

Here, we apply an integration by parts in time

\displaystyle\big\langle J_{\mu}(\bm{x},t)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\bm{y}\Big[\sigma_{\mu\nu}(\bm{x},t;\bm{y},\tau)A^{\nu}(\bm{y},\tau))\Big]_{-\infty}^{t}+(-1)^{2}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\bm{y}A^{\nu}(\bm{y},\tau)\partial_{\tau}\sigma_{\mu\nu}(\bm{x},t;\bm{y},\tau).

Using that the integral of the derivative is the initial function, we obtain

\displaystyle\big\langle J_{\mu}(\bm{x},t)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Big[\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\bm{y}A^{\nu}(\bm{y},\tau)\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\partial_{\tau_{1}}\sigma_{\mu\nu}(\bm{x},t;\bm{y},\tau_{1})\Big]_{-\infty}^{t}+\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\bm{y}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau A^{\nu}(\bm{y},\tau)\partial_{\tau}\sigma_{\mu\nu}(\bm{x},t;\bm{y},\tau).

\displaystyle\big\langle J_{\mu}(\bm{x},t)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left[\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\bm{y}A^{\nu}(\bm{y},t)\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\partial_{\tau}\sigma_{\mu\nu}(\bm{x},t;\bm{y},\tau)-0\right]+\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\bm{y}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau A^{\nu}(\bm{y},\tau)\partial_{\tau}\sigma_{\mu\nu}(\bm{x},t;\bm{y},\tau).

and this result, using Eq. (

\displaystyle\big\langle J_{\mu}(\bm{x},t)\big\rangle

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\bm{y}A^{\nu}(\bm{y},t)\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\Pi_{\mu\nu}(\bm{x},t;\bm{y},\tau)-\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\bm{y}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\Pi_{\mu\nu}(\bm{x},t;\bm{y},\tau)A^{\nu}(\bm{y},\tau).

(109)

\displaystyle\big\langle J_{\mu}(\bm{x},t)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\bm{y}\Pi_{\mu\nu}(\bm{x},t;\bm{y},\tau)\Big[A^{\nu}(\bm{y},\tau)-A^{\nu}(\bm{y},t)\Big].

(110)

Note that this result is already different from the linear relation between the electric conductivity and the potential vector proposed in Eq. (

\displaystyle\big\langle J_{\mu}(\bm{x},t)\big\rangle=-\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\bm{y}\Pi_{\mu\nu}(\bm{x},t;\bm{y},\tau)A^{\nu}(\bm{y},\tau).

(111)

We can write the integral part proportional to

of Eq. (

by using

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\Pi_{\mu\nu}(\bm{x},t;\bm{y},\tau)A^{\nu}(\bm{y},t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\Pi_{\mu\nu}(\bm{x},t;\bm{y},\tau_{1})A^{\nu}(\bm{y},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\Pi_{\mu\nu}(\bm{x},t;\bm{y},\tau_{1})\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\delta(\tau-t)A^{\nu}(\bm{y},t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\Pi_{\mu\nu}(\bm{x},t;\bm{y},\tau_{1})\delta(\tau-t)A^{\nu}(\bm{y},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\delta(\tau-t)\left(\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\Pi_{\mu\nu}(\bm{x},\tau;\bm{y},\tau_{1})\right)A^{\nu}(\bm{y},\tau).

where we have used the prescription

Assuming homogeneity in space and using the homogeneity in time, we have

\displaystyle\big\langle J_{\mu}(\bm{x},t)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\bm{y}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\left[\Pi_{\mu\nu}(\bm{x}-\bm{y},t-\tau)-\delta(\tau-t)\left(\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\Pi_{\mu\nu}(\bm{x}-\bm{y},\tau-\tau_{1})\right)\right]A^{\nu}(\bm{y},\tau).

Applying a Fourier transform in the position’s coordinates, using the convolution theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle-\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\left[\Pi_{\mu\nu}(t-\tau,\bm{q})-\delta(\tau-t)\left(\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\Pi_{\mu\nu}(\tau-\tau_{1},\bm{q})\right)\right]A^{\nu}(\tau,\bm{q}).

(117)

\displaystyle\big\langle J_{\mu}(\omega,\bm{q})\big\rangle=-\Big[\Pi_{\mu\nu}^{R}(\omega,\bm{q})-\displaystyle\lim_{\omega\to 0}\Pi_{\mu\nu}^{R}(\omega,\bm{q})\Big]A^{\nu}(\omega,\bm{q})\neq-\Pi_{\mu\nu}^{R}(\mathrm{q})A^{\nu}(\omega,\bm{q}).

)

\displaystyle\big\langle J_{\mu}(\omega,\bm{q})\big\rangle=\sigma_{\mu\nu}^{R}(\omega,\bm{q})(\mathrm{i}\omega)A^{\nu}(\omega,\bm{q}).

) and Eq. (

Note that the same calculation can be performed with retarded operators from the very beginning, and we obtain the same result. Assuming spatial homogeneity and applying a Fourier transform in the position’s coordinates, using the convolution theorem to the (causal) microscopic Ohm’s Law in the position space Eq. (

where the Polarization operator is defined (in the Matsubara formalism) as

) predicts the existence of permanent electric currents in the absence of an electric field; therefore, by definition provided in Eq. (

can induce electric current even in the absence of an electric field. This pathological prediction of the QFT model as is developed and used in literature, explicitly using (

VII.2
Differences between the Kubo and the non-regularized QFT electric conductivity expressions

As we have just shown, the only difference between the Kubo and polarization-based model is the absence of the regularization term
\displaystyle\lim_{\omega\to 0}\Pi_{\mu\nu}(\omega,\bm{q})
in the latter (once losses are also added to the QFT model). In order to compare the two electric conductivity models we need to explore the values of this term for the longitudinal
\Pi_{L}(\omega,\bm{q})
and transversal
\Pi_{T}(\omega,\bm{q})
components of the Polarization operator, both in the non-local and local regime
\bm{q}\to\bm{0}
.

The longitudinal Polarization
\Pi_{L}(\omega,\bm{q})
is explicitly given in Eq. (
298
), its zero frequency limit is zero
\displaystyle\lim_{\omega\to 0}\Pi_{L}(\omega,\bm{q})=0
in both the local and non-local regions. In particular, it goes to zero as
\mathcal{O}(\omega^{2})
. We can conclude that the longitudinal part of the Kubo and the polarization-based electric conductivities are identical in the local and pure non-local regions.

Concerning the transversal polarization
\Pi_{T}(\omega,\bm{q})
, its explicit expression is given in Eq. (
305
). We can see that in the local regime, its zero frequency limit is zero
\displaystyle\lim_{\omega\to 0}\Pi_{T}(\omega,\bm{q}=\bm{0})=0
, while in the purely non-local regime
\bm{q}\neq\bm{0}
this is not the case and
\displaystyle\lim_{\omega\to 0}\Pi_{T}(\omega,\bm{q}\neq\bm{0})\neq 0
. We can conclude that the transversal part of the Kubo and the polarization-based electric conductivities are identical in the local region. At the same time, they are different in the purely nonlocal region.

Hence, let us investigate the transversal electric conductivity in detail.

Taking into account that
\Pi_{T}^{(0)}(\mathrm{q})
corresponds to the
\mu=k_{B}T=0\text{ eV}

\hbar\Gamma\to 0

\displaystyle\lim_{\hbar\Gamma\to 0}\mathbb{R}\text{e}\left[\sigma_{T}^{\rm{K}}(\mathrm{q})\right]=f(\mathrm{q})\Theta\left(\hbar\omega-\sqrt{4\Delta^{2}+\tilde{q}_{\parallel}^{2}}\right).

(225)

[
21
]

\displaystyle\sigma_{T}^{(0),\rm{NR}}(\omega,\tilde{q}_{\parallel})=\frac{\alpha c}{4\pi}\dfrac{\sqrt{\tilde{q}_{\parallel}^{2}-\hbar^{2}\omega^{2}}}{-\mathrm{i}\hbar\omega}\Psi\left(\frac{2\Delta}{\sqrt{\tilde{q}_{\parallel}^{2}-\hbar^{2}\omega^{2}}}\right)

VIII
Conclusions

In this article, we have shown a detailed derivation of the Polarization
\Pi_{\mu\nu}
and electric conductivity
\sigma_{\mu\nu}
tensor for graphene close to the Dirac point in the continuous limit. We have used the Kubo formula
[
13
]
(
\sigma^{\rm{K}}
), a Quantum Field Theory based (QFT) model (which approaches the electronic quasiparticles as
(2+1)D
Dirac electrons)
[
21
]
(
\sigma^{\rm{NR}}
) and a local model
[
30
]
(
\sigma^{\rm{F}}
).

The more general result is obtained with the Kubo formula
\sigma^{\rm{K}}
. This result is valid for any complex frequency (with positive imaginary part)
\omega\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, constant dissipation rate
\Gamma
, chemical potential
\mu
and Dirac mass
m
as a closed analytical formula at zero temperature
T
. The non-zero temperature results can be obtained after integration using the Maldague formula (Eq. (
187
)).

We obtain that the local limit of
\sigma^{\rm{K}}
coincides with the local limit of
\sigma^{\rm{NR}}
when
\Gamma=0
. In addition to that, when
m=0
,
\sigma^{\rm{K}}
converges to
\sigma^{\rm{F}}
, and when
m=0
and
\Gamma=0
,
\sigma^{\rm{NR}}
also converges to
\sigma^{\rm{F}}
.

We have derived the Polarization (and, therefore, the electric conductivity) as in the QFT model, we obtain again the results published elsewhere, and we find that the longitudinal electric conductivity derived from the Kubo formula and from the QFT model almost coincide (Fig.
2
), with any difference explained by the different regularization strategies used. However, there is no such coincidence with the transversal electric conductivity (Fig.
3
).

There are several differences between the two formalisms; the first one is that in the Kubo formalism, the effect of losses is taken into account. As a result, the intraband conductivity in the Kubo formula is described by a Drude model (in both longitudinal and transversal terms), while, in the QFT model, those intraband conductivities are described by the dissipation-less plasma model. In addition to that, the main difference comes from the regularization of the Polarization operator used. In the case of the Kubo formula, the expression of the Polarization operator is regularized by imposing
\displaystyle\lim_{\omega\to 0}\Pi^{\mu\nu}(\omega,\bm{q}_{\parallel})A_{\nu}(\omega,\bm{q}_{\parallel})=0
for all
A_{\nu}(\omega,\bm{q}_{\parallel})
, as shown in Eq. (
118
) of Sec.
III.1
. On the contrary, in the derivation of the QFT model, as the assumed constitutive relation (Eq. (
136
)) does not impose any regularization term, the Longitudinal electric conductivity derived from the QFT model coincides with the result derived from the Kubo formula, but this is not the case of the Transversal electric conductivity, for which the QFT result fulfills
\displaystyle\lim_{\omega\to 0}\Pi_{T}(\omega,\bm{q}_{\parallel})\neq 0
. As a consequence, a transversal plasma electric conductivity is obtained in the QFT model, which would imply a dissipation-less electric current generated by an electric field that is not corrected by the addition of losses; this is clearly not acceptable in normal materials.

We have shown that the use of Ohm’s Law as the constitutive relation between the electric current
J_{\mu}
and the electric field
E^{\nu}
(Eq. (
131
)) instead of the assumed linear relationship of the electric current with the potential vector
A^{\nu}
(Eq. (
138
)) in the Kubo formula leads to different models for the electric conductivity of 2D materials described by the Dirac Hamiltonian, like graphene. This difference can be traced to a regularization term that must be applied to the electric conductivity tensor. Once this regularization term is considered, the Kubo and QFT model exactly coincide for all parameters of the model.
