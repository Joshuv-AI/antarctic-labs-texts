Analysis and approximation of a vorticity-velocity-pressure formulation for the Oseen equations
Verónica Anaya, Afaf Bouharguane, David Mora, Carlos Reales Ricardo Ruiz-Baier , Nour Seloula , and Hector Torres

Abstract
We introduce a family of mixed methods and discontinuous Galerkin discretisations designed to numerically solve the Oseen equations written in terms of velocity, vorticity, and Bernoulli pressure. The unique solvability of the continuous problem is addressed by invoking a global inf-sup property in an adequate abstract setting for non-symmetric systems. The proposed finite element schemes, which produce exactly divergence-free discrete velocities, are shown to be well-defined and optimal convergence rates are derived in suitable norms. In addition, we establish optimal rates of convergence for a class of discontinuous Galerkin schemes, which employ stabilisation. A set of numerical examples serves to illustrate salient features of these methods.



Key words
: Oseen equations; vorticity-based formulation; mixed finite elements; exactly divergence-free velocity; discontinuous Galerkin schemes; numerical fluxes; a priori error bounds.

: 65N30, 65N12, 76D07, 65N15.

Introduction

The Oseen equations
stem from linearisation of the steady (or alternatively from the backward Euler time-discretisation
of the transient) Navier-Stokes equations. Of particular appeal to us is their formulation in terms of
fluid velocity, vorticity vector, and pressure. A diversity of discretisation methods is available to solve
incompressible flow problems using these three fields as principal unknowns. Some recent examples
include spectral elements
[
3
,
8
]
as well as stabilised and least-squares
schemes
[
2
,
9
]
for Navier-Stokes; also several mixed and augmented methods for
Brinkman
[
4
,
5
,
7
]
, and a number of other discretisations
specifically designed for Stokes
flows
[
6
,
22
,
23
,
25
,
30
]
.

Both the implementation and the analysis of numerical schemes for
Navier-Stokes are typically based on
the Oseen linearisation. A few related contributions (not only restricted to the velocity-pressure
formulation) include for instance

[
10
]
, that presents
a least-squares method
for Navier-Stokes equations with vorticity-based first-order reformulation, and whose
analysis exploits
the elliptic theory of Agmon-Douglas-Nirenberg.
Conforming finite element methods exhibit optimal order of accuracy for
diverse boundary conditions. We also mention the
non-conforming exponentially
accurate least-squares spectral method for Oseen
equations proposed in
[
27
]
, where
a suitable preconditioner is also proposed.
In
[
32
]
the authors introduce a velocity-vorticity-pressure
least-squares finite element method for Oseen and Navier-Stokes equations with velocity
boundary conditions. They derive error estimates and reported a degeneracy of
the convergence for large Reynolds numbers. A div least-squares
minimisation problem based on the stress-velocity-pressure formulation was
introduced in
[
13
]
. The study shows
that the corresponding homogeneous least-squares functional is elliptic and
continuous in suitable norms. Several first-order
Oseen-type systems are analysed in
[
15
]
, also including vorticity and total pressure in the formulation.

Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) methods have also been used to solve the Oseen problem,
as for example, in
[
19
,
18
]
for the case of Dirichlet boundary conditions.
Compared with conforming finite elements, discretisations based on DG methods have a number of
attractive, and well-documented features. These include
high order accuracy, being amenable for
hp
-adaptivity, relatively simple implementation on
highly unstructured meshes, and superior robustness when handling rough coefficients.
We also mention the a priori error analysis of hybridisable DG schemes introduced
in
[
16
]
for the Oseen equations. The family of DG methods we propose here
has resemblance with those schemes, but exploits a three-field formulation described below.

This paper is concerned with mixed non-symmetric variational problems
which will be analysed using a global inf-sup argument.
To do this, we conveniently restrict the set of equations to the space of divergence-free velocities, and apply
results from
[
24
]
in order to prove that the equivalent resulting non-symmetric saddle-point
problem is well-posed. For the numerical approximation, we first consider
Raviart-Thomas elements of order
k\geq 0
for the velocity field, Nédélec elements
or order
k
for the vorticity, and piecewise polynomials of degree
k
without
continuity restrictions, for the Bernoulli pressure.
We prove unique solvability of the discrete problem by adapting the same tools utilised
in the analysis of the continuous problem. In addition, the proposed family of Galerkin
finite element methods
turns out to be optimally convergent, under the common
assumptions of enough regularity of the exact solutions to the continuous
problem. The method produces exactly divergence-free approximations
of the velocity by construction; thus preserving, at the discrete level, an essential constraint of the governing
equations. Next, inspired by the methods presented in
[
20
,
19
]
, we present another scheme involving the discontinuous Galerkin discretisation of the
\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits
-
\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits
and
\mathrm{grad}
-
\mathrm{div}
operators. We prove the well-posedness of the DG scheme and derive error estimates under some solution regularity assumptions.

The finite element discretisation is constructed in Section

2
Statement and solvability of the continuous problem

Oseen problem in terms of velocity-vorticity-pressure.

A standard backward Euler time-discretisation of the classical Navier-Stokes
equations, or a linearisation of the
steady version of the problem combined with standard curl-div identities,
leads to the following set of equations, known as the
Oseen equations (see
[
29
,
26
]
):

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sigma\boldsymbol{u}-\nu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}+\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{\beta}+\nabla p&=\boldsymbol{f}\qquad\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}&=0\qquad\,\mbox{ in }\Omega,\end{split}

(2.1)

where
\nu>0
is the kinematic fluid viscosity,

\sigma>0
is inversely proportional to the time-step,
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is
an adequate approximation of velocity to be made precise below, and
the vector of external forces
\boldsymbol{f}
also absorbs the contributions related to previous time steps, or to fixed states in
the linearisation procedure of the steady Navier-Stokes equations. As usual in this context, in the momentum
equation we have conveniently introduced the Bernoulli (also known as
dynamic) pressure
p:=P+\frac{1}{2}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}
, where
P
is the actual fluid pressure.

The structure of (
2.1
) suggests to introduce the rescaled
vorticity vector
\boldsymbol{\omega}:=\sqrt{\nu}\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}
as a new
unknown. Furthermore,
in this study we focus on the case of zero normal velocities and zero
tangential vorticity trace imposed on a part of the boundary
\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega
,
whereas a non-homogeneous tangential velocity
\boldsymbol{u}_{\Sigma}
and a fixed Bernoulli pressure
p_{\Sigma}
are set
on the remainder of the boundary
\Sigma=\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma
.
Therefore, system (
2.1
) can be recast in the form

\displaystyle\sigma\boldsymbol{u}+\sqrt{\nu}\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\omega}+\nu^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{\beta}+\nabla p=\boldsymbol{f},\hskip 10.00002pt\boldsymbol{\omega}-\sqrt{\nu}\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{0},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mbox{ in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ and }\hskip 10.00002pt\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\mbox{ on }\Gamma,

(2.2)

\displaystyle p=p_{\Sigma}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ and }\hskip 10.00002pt\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{u}_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle\mbox{ on }\Sigma,

where
\boldsymbol{n}
stands for the outward unit normal
on
\partial\Omega
. Should the boundary
\Sigma
have zero measure,
the additional condition
(p,1)_{\Omega,0}=0
is required to
enforce uniqueness of the
Bernoulli pressure.

Defining a weak formulation.

Let us introduce the following functional spaces

\mathrm{H}:=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits;\Omega):\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0\,\,\text{on}\,\,\Gamma\},\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{Z}:=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathrm{H}(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits;\Omega):\gamma_{t}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\boldsymbol{0}\,\,\text{on}\,\,\Gamma\},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and }\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{Q}:=\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega),

where the operator
\gamma_{t}
is the tangential trace
operator on
\Gamma
, defined by:
\gamma_{t}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\boldsymbol{\theta}\times\boldsymbol{n}
.
Let us endow
\mathrm{H}
and
\mathrm{Q}
with their natural norms.
For the space
\mathrm{Z}
however, we consider the following
viscosity-weighted norm:

\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{\mathrm{Z}}:=\left(\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\nu\|\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

From now on, we will assume that the data are regular enough:
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)^{3}
and
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{3}
.
We proceed to test (
2.2
) against adequate
functions and to impose the boundary conditions in such a manner that
we end up with the following formulation:
Find
(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\omega},p)\in\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Z}\times\mathrm{Q}
such that

\displaystyle a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\;b_{1}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\omega})

\displaystyle+b_{2}(\boldsymbol{v},p)+c(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\;F(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H},

\displaystyle b_{1}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\;d(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\;G(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathrm{Z},

(2.3)

\displaystyle b_{2}(\boldsymbol{u},q)

\displaystyle=\;0

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\forall q\in\mathrm{Q},

where the bilinear forms

a:\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{H}\to\mathbb{R}
,

b_{1}:\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
,

d:\mathrm{Z}\times\mathrm{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
,

b_{2}:\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}\to\mathbb{R}
,

c:\mathrm{Z}\times\mathrm{H}\to\mathbb{R}
, and the
linear functionals
F:\mathrm{H}\to\mathbb{R}
, and
G:\mathrm{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
are
specified as follows

\displaystyle a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle:=\sigma\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,d\boldsymbol{x},\hskip 10.00002ptb_{1}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\sqrt{\nu}\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,d\boldsymbol{x},\hskip 10.00002ptb_{2}(\boldsymbol{v},q):=-\int_{\Omega}q\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\,d\boldsymbol{x},

\displaystyle d(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}\,d\boldsymbol{x},\hskip 10.00002ptc(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{v}):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\nu}}\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\times\boldsymbol{\beta})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,d\boldsymbol{x},

\displaystyle F(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,d\boldsymbol{x}-\langle\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{n},p_{\Sigma}\rangle_{\Sigma},\hskip 10.00002ptG(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=-\sqrt{\nu}\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{\theta}\rangle_{\Sigma},

for all
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}
,
\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathrm{Z}
, and
q\in\mathrm{Q}
.

Solvability analysis.

In order to analyse the variational formulation (
2.3
),
let us introduce the Kernel of the bilinear form

and
its classical characterisation

\mathrm{X}:=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}\,:\,b_{2}(\boldsymbol{v},q)=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\,q\in Q\}\,=\,\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}\,:\,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\,=\,0\;\;\text{in}\;\;\Omega\},

\displaystyle+

Theorem 2.1

Let

If there exists

\displaystyle\,\sup_{\scriptstyle y\in{\mathcal{X}}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\mathcal{A}(x,y)}{\|y\|_{\mathcal{X}}}\,\geq\,\alpha\,\|x\|_{\mathcal{X}}\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\,x\in\mathcal{X},

then there exists a unique solution

\mathcal{A}(x,y)=\mathcal{G}(y)\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\,y\in\mathcal{X}.

Lemma 2.2

Let us assume that

\frac{2\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}^{2}}{\nu\sigma}<1,

and let us define the bilinear form
\mathcal{A}(\cdot,\cdot)

\displaystyle\,\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptstyle(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta})\in{\mathcal{X}}}}{{(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta})\neq 0}}}\frac{\mathcal{A}((\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\omega}),(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta}))}{\|(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{\mathcal{X}}}\,\geq\,\alpha_{2}\,\|(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\omega})\|_{\mathcal{X}}\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\,(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\omega})\in\mathcal{X},

, endowed

Proof.

As a consequence of the boundedness of

,

, and

Concerning the satisfaction of the inf-sup condition (

where

is a constant to be chosen later.
We can then immediately assert that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}((\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\omega}),(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}))

\displaystyle=\sigma\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}\,d\boldsymbol{x}+\sqrt{\nu}\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}\,d\boldsymbol{x}+\sqrt{\nu}\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}\,d\boldsymbol{x}

\|p\|_{0,\Omega}\leq C(\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}+\|p_{\Sigma}\|_{1/2,\Sigma}+\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\Sigma}\|_{-1/2,\Sigma}).

Proof.

Combining the inf-sup condition (
2.4
)
with the first equation in (
2.3
) gives the bound

\|p\|_{0,\Omega}\leq\frac{1}{\beta_{2}}\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}}}{{\boldsymbol{v}\neq 0}}}\frac{|b_{2}(\boldsymbol{v},p)|}{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathrm{H}}}=\frac{1}{\beta_{2}}\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}}}{{\boldsymbol{v}\neq 0}}}\frac{|F(\boldsymbol{v})-a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})-b_{1}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\omega})-c(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{v})|}{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathrm{H}}},

which together with (
2.11
), and the boundedness
of
F
,
a
,
b_{1}
and
c
, complete the proof.

\square

Remark 2.1

An alternative analysis for the nonsymmetric variational problem (

The resulting weak form could then be analysed using classical tools for saddle-point problems,
for instance, following the similar treatment in

Remark 2.2

Assumption (

holds provided one chooses

bound for

Finite element discretisation

In this section we introduce a Galerkin scheme
for (

) and analyse its well-posedness
by establishing suitable assumptions on the finite
element subspaces involved. Error estimates are also derived.

Defining the discrete problem.

Let
\{\mathcal{T}_{h}(\Omega)\}_{h>0}
be a shape-regular
family of partitions of the polyhedral region

\bar{\Omega}
, by tetrahedrons
T
of diameter
h_{T}
, with mesh size

h:=\max\{h_{T}:\;T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}(\Omega)\}
.
In what follows, given an integer
k\geq 0
and a subset

of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

will denote the space of polynomial functions
defined locally in

and being of total degree
\leq k
.

Moreover, for any

, we introduce the local Nédélec space

is a subspace of
\mathcal{P}_{k+1}(T)^{3}
composed by
homogeneous polynomials of degree
k+1
, and being orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{x}
. With
these tools, let us
define the following finite element subspaces:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Z}_{h}

\displaystyle:=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}\in\mathrm{Z}:\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}|_{T}\in\mathbb{N}_{k}(T)\hskip 10.00002pt\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}(\Omega)\},

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Q}_{h}

\displaystyle:=\{q_{h}\in\mathrm{Q}:q_{h}|_{T}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(T)\hskip 10.00002pt\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}(\Omega)\},

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}_{h}

\displaystyle:=\{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathrm{H}:\boldsymbol{v}_{h}|_{T}\in RT_{k}(T)\hskip 10.00002pt\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}(\Omega)\},

(3.3)

where

is the Raviart-Thomas space defined locally in
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}(\Omega)
.

The proposed Galerkin scheme approximating (
2.3
) reads as follows:
Find
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h},p_{h})\in\mathrm{H}_{h}\times\mathrm{Z}_{h}\times\mathrm{Q}_{h}
such that

\displaystyle a(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\;b_{1}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h})

\displaystyle+\;b_{2}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},p_{h})+\;c(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})

\displaystyle=\;F(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathrm{H}_{h},

\displaystyle b_{1}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\;d(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})

\displaystyle=\;G(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}\in\mathrm{Z}_{h},

(3.4)

\displaystyle b_{2}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},q_{h})

\displaystyle=\;0

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\forall q_{h}\in\mathrm{Q}_{h}.

Solvability and stability of the discrete problem.

The analysis of the Galerkin formulation will follow
the same arguments exploited in the continuous setting.
Let us then consider the discrete kernel of
b_{2}

\mathrm{X}_{h}:=\{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathrm{H}_{h}\,:\,b_{2}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h})=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\,q\in\mathrm{Q}_{h}\}=\{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathrm{H}_{h}\,:\,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\equiv 0\hskip 10.00002pt{\rm in}\hskip 10.00002pt\Omega\},

where the characterisation is indeed possible
thanks to the inclusion
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\mathrm{H}_{h}\subseteq\mathrm{Q}_{h}
. Moreover,
it is well-known that the following discrete inf-sup condition holds

[
24
]

\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathrm{H}_{h}}}{{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\neq 0}}}\frac{b_{2}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h})}{\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{\mathrm{H}}}\geq\tilde{\beta}_{2}\|q_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\hskip 10.00002pt\forall q_{h}\in\mathrm{Q}_{h}.

We again resort to a reduced version of the problem, now defined on the product space

\displaystyle a(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\;b_{1}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h})+\;c(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\,

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle F(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\,

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\,\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathrm{X}_{h},

\displaystyle b_{1}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\;d(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})\,

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}\in\mathrm{Z}_{h},

Proposition 1

The DG method (
4.9
) with the numerical
fluxes given by (
4.4
)-(
4.5
)
defines a unique approximate solution

(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h},p_{h})\in\widetilde{\mathrm{H}}_{h}\times\widetilde{\mathrm{Z}}_{h}\times\widetilde{\mathrm{Q}}_{h}

provided that

\frac{2\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\nu\sigma}<1.

(4.13)

Proof.

Since the problem is linear and finite dimensional,
it suffices to show that if
\boldsymbol{f}=\textbf{0}
,
p_{\Sigma}=0

and
\boldsymbol{u}_{\Sigma}=\textbf{0}
, then
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h},p_{h})=(\textbf{0},\textbf{0},0)
.
To this end, take
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}=\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
,
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h}

and
q_{h}=p_{h}
in (
4.9
), summing up the three equations, we obtain:

a(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h})+c(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h})+d(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h})+j(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h})+e(p_{h},p_{h})=0.

It follows that

\displaystyle\sigma\|\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\|\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+|\boldsymbol{u}|_{j}^{2}+|p|_{e}^{2}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\nu}}\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h}\times\boldsymbol{\beta})\cdot\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\,d\boldsymbol{x},

Using Young’s inequality, we can assert that

\displaystyle\sigma\|\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\|\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+|\boldsymbol{u}|_{j}^{2}+|p|_{e}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{\nu}}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\nu\sigma}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\frac{\sigma}{2}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}.

Therefore, in particular we have that:

\frac{\sigma}{2}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+(1-\frac{2\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\nu\sigma})\|\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+|p|_{e}^{2}\leq 0,

which, owing to the assumption (

\boldsymbol{u}_{h}=\textbf{0}
,
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h}=\textbf{0}
and
\left[\![p_{h}\right]\!]=0
on
{\mathcal{E}}_{h}
,

\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\int_{T}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\cdot\nabla p_{h}\,d\boldsymbol{x}=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\widetilde{\mathrm{H}}_{h},

and then
\nabla p_{h}=\textbf{0}

Employing this result, together with
\left[\![p_{h}\right]\!]=0

on
{\mathcal{E}}_{h}

p_{h}=0

{\mathcal{F}}_{h}^{\Sigma}

\square

Theorem 4.1

Let the exact solution
(\boldsymbol{u},p)
satisfy the regularity assumption
in (
4.15

(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h},p_{h})

\displaystyle|(\boldsymbol{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{e}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},e_{p})|_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathcal{A}}h^{\min\{s,\,k+1\}}\|(\boldsymbol{u},p)\|_{s},

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{\min\{s,\,k+1\}}\|(\boldsymbol{u},p)\|_{s},

We begin with the estimate (

Concentrating on the projection of the errors, we can exploit the Galerkin orthogonality to obtain

\displaystyle|(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\eta_{p})|_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\eta_{p};\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\eta_{p})-c(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\eta_{p};\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\xi_{p})-c(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}}).

it follows that

\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\eta_{p};\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\xi_{p})=\tilde{b}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}})-\tilde{b}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}})+\tilde{b}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\eta_{p})-\widetilde{b}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\xi_{p})+c(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}})+j(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}})+e(\xi_{p},\eta_{p}).

Note that all these terms can be controlled using Lemma
4.3
. Indeed we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\eta_{p};\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\xi_{p})

\displaystyle\leq\Big(C_{b_{1}}\|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|_{0,\Omega}+(C_{b_{1}}+C_{b_{2}}+C_{j})|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}}|_{j}+(C_{b_{2}}+C_{e})|\eta_{p}|_{e}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+C_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,\Omega}\Big)h^{\min\{s,\,k+1\}}\|(\boldsymbol{u},p)\|_{s},

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}|(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\eta_{p})|_{\mathcal{A}}\,h^{\min\{s,\,k+1\}}\|(\boldsymbol{u},p)\|_{s},

(4.22)

where
C_{1}=C_{b_{1}}+(C_{b_{1}}+C_{b_{2}}+C_{j})+(C_{b_{2}}+C_{e})+\frac{C_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}{\sigma}
.
Next we only need to estimate
c(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}})

in (
4.21
). To do so we use Young’s inequality

c(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}})\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{\nu}}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq\frac{2}{\nu\sigma}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\frac{\sigma}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}.

(4.23)

Substituting (
4
) and (
4.23
) back into (
4.21
), we obtain the bound

\frac{\sigma}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+(1-\frac{2\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\nu\sigma})\|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}}|_{j}^{2}+|\eta_{p}|_{e}^{2}\leq C_{1}|(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\eta_{p})|_{\mathcal{A}}\,h^{\min\{s,\,k+1\}}\|(\boldsymbol{u},p)\|_{s},

and thanks to assumption (
4.13
), we can arrive at

|(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\eta_{p})|_{\mathcal{A}}\leq C^{\prime}\,h^{\min\{s,\,k+1\}}\|(\boldsymbol{u},p)\|_{s},

(4.24)

where
C^{\prime}=C_{1}(\min\{\frac{1}{2},1-\frac{2\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\nu\sigma}\})^{-1}
.
The error estimate in (
4.18
) is then obtained
by combining estimates (
4.20
), (
4.24
) and using triangle inequality.

We now turn to the estimate of the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-norm of error in the pressure
(
4.19
). Since
e_{p}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
,
we can find
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)^{3}
such that (see for instance
[
26
, Chapter I, Corollary 2.4]
)

-\int_{\Omega}e_{p}\,\mathrm{div}\boldsymbol{z}\,d\boldsymbol{x}\geq\kappa\|e_{p}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2},\hskip 20.00003pt\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{1,\Omega}\leq\|e_{p}\|_{0,\Omega}.

(4.25)

Therefore, we infer from (
4.12
) that

\displaystyle\kappa\|e_{p}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{b}_{2}(\boldsymbol{z},e_{p})

\displaystyle=\big(\tilde{b}_{2}(\boldsymbol{z},e_{p})+a(e_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{z})+\tilde{b}_{1}(\boldsymbol{z},e_{\boldsymbol{\omega}})+c(e_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\boldsymbol{z})\big)-a(e_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{z})-\tilde{b}_{1}(\boldsymbol{z},e_{\boldsymbol{\omega}})-c(e_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}(e_{\boldsymbol{u}},e_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},e_{p};\boldsymbol{z},\textbf{0},0)-a(e_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{z})-\tilde{b}_{1}(\boldsymbol{z},e_{\boldsymbol{\omega}})-c(e_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\boldsymbol{z}),

where we have used that
j(e_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{z})=0
for
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)^{3}
.
Using the Galerkin orthogonality, we obtain

\mathcal{A}(e_{\boldsymbol{u}},e_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},e_{p};\boldsymbol{z},\textbf{0},0)=\mathcal{A}(e_{\boldsymbol{u}},e_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},e_{p};\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{z}},\textbf{0},0)=\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\xi_{p};\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{z}},\textbf{0},0)+\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\eta_{p};\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{z}},\textbf{0},0).

Therefore

\kappa\|e_{p}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\leq|\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\xi_{p};\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{z}},\textbf{0},0)|+|\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\eta_{p};\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{z}},\textbf{0},0)|+|a(e_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{z})|+|\tilde{b}_{1}(\boldsymbol{z},e_{\boldsymbol{\omega}})|+|c(e_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\boldsymbol{z})|.

Next, by the definition of the form
\mathcal{A}
, we have the relation

|\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\eta_{p};\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{z}},\textbf{0},0)|\leq|\tilde{b}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{z}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}})|+|\tilde{b}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{z}},\eta_{p})|+|c(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{z}})|+|j(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{z}})|:=T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3}+T_{4},

and then we can write

\kappa\|e_{p}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\leq|\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\xi_{p};\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{z}},\textbf{0},0)|+T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3}+T_{4}+T_{5}+T_{6}+T_{7}.

(4.26)

The first term in the right-hand side of the above inequality
can be easily estimated by using Lemma
4.3
.
Indeed, it follows by choosing
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{z}}
that

|\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\xi_{p};\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{z}},\textbf{0},0)|\leq Ch^{\min\{s,k+1\}}\|(\boldsymbol{u},p)\|_{s}.

Let us now estimate each of the terms
T_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,7
in (
4.26
).
Using the properties of the
\mathrm{L}^{2}-
projection, Cauchy-Schwarz’s inequality and the inverse inequality, we obtain
the bounds

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C\sqrt{\nu}\Big(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{T}^{-1}\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\|^{2}_{0,\partial T}\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{T}\|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|^{2}_{0,\partial T}\Big)^{1/2}\leq C\sqrt{\nu}\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{1,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|_{0,\Omega}.

\displaystyle\leq C\sqrt{\nu}\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{1,\Omega}|(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\eta_{p})|_{\mathcal{A}}

Then, using (
4.24
) and (
4.25
), we can deduce that

T_{1}\leq C\sqrt{\nu}h^{\min\{s,\,k+1\}}\|(\boldsymbol{u},p)\|_{s}\|e_{p}\|_{0,\Omega}.

Furthermore, since
\int_{T}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\cdot\nabla\eta_{p}\,d\boldsymbol{x}=0
, we get from (
4.11
) the following
estimates

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle=\Big|\sum_{e\in{\mathcal{E}}_{h}}\int_{e}\left\{\!\!\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\right\}\!\!\}\cdot\left[\![\eta_{p}\right]\!]\,ds+\sum_{e\in{\mathcal{F}}_{h}^{\Sigma}}\int_{e}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n})\eta_{p}\,ds\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sum_{e\in{\mathcal{E}}_{h}}\int_{e}\frac{1}{D_{11}}\left\{\!\!\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\right\}\!\!\}^{2}\,ds+\sum_{e\in{\mathcal{F}}_{h}^{\Sigma}}\int_{e}\frac{1}{D_{11}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n})^{2}\,ds\Big)^{1/2}|\eta_{p}|_{e}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\frac{1}{\underline{D_{11}}}\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\|_{0,\partial T}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}|\eta_{p}|_{e}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{1,\Omega}|(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\eta_{p})|_{\mathcal{A}}.

Then, using (
4.24
) and (
4.25
), we can infer that

T_{2}\leq Ch^{\min\{s,\,k+1\}}\|(\boldsymbol{u},p)\|_{s}\|e_{p}\|_{0,\Omega}.

Next, using Cauchy-Schwarz’s inequality together with Lemma
4.2

and (
4.24
), we get

T_{3}=|c(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{z}})|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\nu}}Ch\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty}\|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{1,\Omega}\leq C\mu_{h}h^{\min\{s,k+1\}}\|(\boldsymbol{u},p)\|_{s}\|e_{p}\|_{0,\Omega},

where
\mu_{h}=\frac{h\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{\nu}}
.
Similarly, we have:

T_{4}=|j(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{z}})|\leq|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}}|_{j}|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{z}}|_{j}\leq C|(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\eta_{p})|_{\mathcal{A}}\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{1,\Omega}\leq Ch^{\min\{s,\,k+1\}}\|(\boldsymbol{u},0)\|_{s}\|e_{p}\|_{0,\Omega}.

The terms
T_{5}
,
T_{6}
and
T_{7}
can be readily estimated, much in the same way as before,
using the error bound in (
4.18
) and the fact that
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)^{3}
.

Finally, the pressure estimate follows after putting all individual bounds back into (
4.26
).

\square

Test 2: Transient flow in an open cavity.

In this example we illustrate a more complex
problem involving the non-stationary behaviour of the flow in an open 2D cavity. The main compartment
of the domain consists on a rectangle
(0,1.2)\times(0,1)
whereas smaller rectangles
(0.25,0.45)\times(-0.1,0)

and
(1.2,1.3)\times(0.7,0.9)

an unstructured mesh of 35433 triangular elements.
Normal velocities and a compatible
trace vorticity are imposed on the whole boundary
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
according to

\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=\begin{cases}-75(x-0.25)(0.45-x)&\text{on $y=-0.1$},\\
75(y-0.7)(0.9-y)&\text{on $x=1.3$},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\hskip 10.00002pt\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{n}=\begin{cases}75\sqrt{\nu}(0.7-2x)&\text{on $y=-0.1$},\\
-75\sqrt{\nu}(1.6-2y)&\text{on $x=1.3$},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

\partial\Omega
.

\nu=0.001

\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(x,y)=[\sin(\pi/1.3x)^{2}\sin(\pi/1.1(y+0.1))^{2}\cos(\pi/1.1(y+0.1)),-\frac{1}{3}\sin(2/1.3\pi x)\sin(\pi/1.1(y+0.1))^{3}]^{T}

\sigma=10

plus smaller recirculation areas that emerge on the top left and bottom left corners, together with a preferential path joining the inlet and outlet
boundaries.

solved on the unit square
\Omega=(0,1)^{2}
and the bottom and top walls constitute

\Gamma
, where we impose a
free-slip velocity condition and zero vorticity. The left and right walls are regarded as a periodic boundary.
The initial velocity is

\boldsymbol{u}=\begin{pmatrix}u_{\infty}\tanh((2y-1)/\delta_{0})-c_{n}u_{\infty}[\cos(w^{a}_{\infty}x)+\cos(w_{\infty}^{b}x)]\frac{(2y-1)}{\delta_{0}^{2}}\exp(-\frac{(y-1/2)^{2}}{\delta_{0}^{2}})\\
c_{n}u_{\infty}\exp(-\frac{(y-1/2)^{2}}{\delta_{0}^{2}})[w_{\infty}^{a}\sin(w_{\infty}^{a}x)+w_{\infty}^{b}\sin(w_{\infty}^{b}x)]\end{pmatrix},

with perturbation scaling
c_{n}=0.001
, reference velocity
u_{\infty}=1
,
w_{\infty}^{a}=8\pi
,
w_{\infty}^{b}=20\pi
,
\delta_{0}=1/28
. The
characteristic time is
\bar{t}=\delta_{0}/u_{\infty}
, the Reynolds number is Re
=10000
, and the kinematic viscosity is
\nu=\delta_{0}u_{\infty}/\text{Re}
.
We use a structured mesh of 128 segments per side, representing 131072 triangular elements, and we solve the problem
using our first-order DG scheme, setting again the stabilisation constants to
a_{11}=c_{11}=\sigma=1/\Delta t
and
d_{11}=\nu
,
where the timestep is taken as
\Delta t=\bar{t}/20
. The specification of this problem implies
that the solutions will be quite sensitive to the initial
perturbations present in the velocity, which will amplify and consequently vortices will appear. We proceed
to compute numerical solutions until the dimensionless time
t=7
, and present in Figure
4
sample
solutions at three different simulation times. For visualisation purposes we zoom into the region
0.25\leq y\leq 0.75
, where
all flow patterns are concentrated.
In addition, a qualitative comparison against benchmark
data from
[
31
]
is presented in terms of the temporal evolution of the enstrophy
E(t)
(here we rescale
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h}

with
\sqrt{\nu}
to match again the real vorticity). We also record the evolution of the palinstrophy
P(t)
,
a quantity that encodes the dissipation process. These quantities are defined, respectively, as

E(t):=\frac{1}{2\nu}\|\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h}(t)\|^{2}_{0,\Omega},\hskip 20.00003ptP(t):=\frac{1}{2\nu}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h}(t)\|^{2}_{0,\Omega},

and we remark that for the palinstrophy we use the discrete gradient associated with the DG discretisation.

5
, where also include
results from

]
that correspond to coarse and fine mesh solutions of the Navier-Stokes equations
