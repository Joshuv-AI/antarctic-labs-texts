Time-dependent coupled cluster with orthogonal adaptive basis functions: General formalism and application to the vibrational problem
Mads Greisen Højlund, Alberto Zoccante, Ove Christiansen

Abstract
We derive equations of motion for bivariational wave functions with orthogonal adaptive basis sets and specialize the formalism to the coupled cluster ansatz. The equations are related to the biorthogonal case in a transparent way, and similarities and differences are analyzed. We show that the amplitude equations are identical in the orthogonal and biorthogonal formalisms, while the linear equations that determined the basis set time evolution differ by symmetrization. Applying the orthogonal framework to the nuclear dynamics problem, we introduce and implement the orthogonal time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster (oTDMVCC) method and benchmark it against exact reference results for four triatomic molecules as well as a 5D trans -bithiophene model. We confirm numerically that the biorthogonal TDMVCC hierarchy converges to the exact solution, while oTDMVCC does not. The differences between TDMVCC and oTDMVCC are found to be small for three of the five cases, but we also identify one case where the formal deficiency of the oTDMVCC approach results in clear and visible errors relative to the exact result. For the remaining example, oTDMVCC exhibits rather modest but visible errors.



I
Introduction

The coupled cluster method is a highly useful approach
for computing the electronic and vibrational structure of molecules.
Its benefits include polynomial-scaling cost,
size extensivity and fast convergence
of the coupled cluster hierarchy, leading in many cases to
a favorable balance between cost and accuracy. These advantages
ultimately stem from the exponential coupled cluster parameterization.
However, it is also well known that the coupled cluster ansatz only
works well when the amplitudes are sufficiently small and the
reference describes a large part of the wave
function. Conversely, if the overlap between the wave function
and the reference decreases, the amplitudes grow
and the ansatz tends to break down.
This kind of situation is easily encountered
in explicitly time-dependent
or dynamical settings, where large-amplitude motion such as
ionization (in electronic structure) or
dissociation (in vibrational structure) is commonplace.
It is quite obvious that a static reference is ill-suited
for describing such processes. However, much less
violent phenomena, e.g. the intramolecular vibrational energy redistribution of water,
can also lead to the breakdown of the coupled cluster ansatz.
Madsen et al. 2020a

This weakness of the coupled cluster approach can sometimes be alleviated
by choosing a dynamical single-particle basis,
which in turn induces a dynamical reference that adapts
to the wave function at any given time.

Historically, the idea of optimizing the basis set
in a coupled cluster computation emerged in ground state theory
with the so-called
Brueckner coupled cluster
Chiles and Dykstra 1981
;
Handy et al. 1989
;
Raghavachari et al. 1990
;
Hampel, Peterson, and Werner 1992
method.
Here, the basis is optimized such that the singles projections vanish
(in other words, the singles vanish identically in the Brueckner basis).
The Brueckner coupled cluster theory attracted considerable attention in the 1990s,
in part due to a perceived robustness towards symmetry breaking.
Stanton, Gauss, and Bartlett 1992
;
Barnes and Lindh 1994
;
Xie et al. 1996

It was later discovered that this
robustness is not universal
Crawford and Stanton 2000

and that the Brueckner coupled cluster response function contains spurious second-order poles
Aiga, Sasagane, and Itoh 1994
;
Koch, Kobayashi, and Jørgensen 1994
.
Brueckner coupled cluster has since fallen somewhat out of fashion.

A related idea is to optimize the basis such
that the coupled cluster energy is minimized.

Purvis and Bartlett 1982
;
Scuseria and Schaefer 1987
;
Sherrill et al. 1998
;
Krylov et al. 1998
;
Pedersen, Koch, and Hättig 1999

When unitary (orthogonal) basis set transformations are used, we will refer to this
method as orbital-optimized coupled cluster (similar acronyms such as OO-CC are also encountered in the literature).
In orbital-optimized coupled cluster, the single excitations are excluded
from the outset (i.e.
T=T_{2}+T_{3}+\cdots
), since
\exp(T_1)
is redundant
with the basis set rotations.
Although Brueckner coupled cluster and orbital-optimized coupled cluster are conceptually quite similar,
it turns out that the orbital-optimized coupled cluster hierarchy does not converge to the full configuration interaction limit
Köhn and Olsen 2005
,
which is obviously a disadvantage. For the examples studied by Köhn and Olsen
Köhn and Olsen 2005
(ozone and
\mathrm{CH_{2}}
),
this deficiency of orbital-optimized coupled cluster starts to show at the quadruples (OCCDTQ) or quintuples (OCCDTQ5) level. At the doubles (OCCD) and triples
(OCCDT) levels, orbital-optimized coupled cluster and Brueckner coupled cluster appear to be comparable in accuracy.
Pedersen et al.
Pedersen, Fernández, and Koch 2001
later introduced
non-orthogonal orbital-optimized coupled cluster. The purpose of using a non-unitary (non-orthogonal) basis set transformation
was to simplify response equations, but it was later shown by Myhre
Myhre 2018
that
non-orthogonal orbital-optimized coupled cluster does in fact recover the full configuration interaction limit.

The concept of using optimized or adaptive basis functions for simulating real-time dynamics has
a long history in the nuclear dynamics community, where the multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree
Meyer, Manthe, and Cederbaum 1990
;
Beck et al. 2000

method has been very successful. multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree employs a complete expansion inside
an adaptive active space and thus yields the exact solution for the given choice of space.
The analogous electron dynamics method is denoted
multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree-Fock.
Zanghellini et al. 2003
;
Kato and Kono 2004
;
Nest, Klamroth, and Saalfrank 2005
;
Caillat et al. 2005

Both of these methods involve an exponentially scaling computational effort, so it is highly relevant to
investigate lower-scaling alternatives, e.g. based on the coupled cluster ansatz.
Real-time time-dependent coupled cluster with static basis functions has been considered in the literature
for vibrational,
Hansen et al. 2019
;
Hansen, Madsen, and Christiansen 2020
;
Madsen et al. 2020b

electron,
Schönhammer and Gunnarsson 1978
;
Huber and Klamroth 2011
;
Pedersen and Kvaal 2019
;
Skeidsvoll, Balbi, and Koch 2020

and nucleon
Hoodbhoy and Negele 1978
;
Hoodbhoy and Negele 1979
;
Pigg et al. 2012

dynamics (see also Ref.
36
for a recent review), but we will focus specifically
on combining adaptive basis functions with the time-dependent coupled cluster ansatz.
This idea was first taken up in 2012 by Kvaal, who introduced the orbital-adaptive time-dependent coupled cluster
Kvaal 2012
method
for electron dynamics.
Orbital-adaptive time-dependent coupled cluster uses
biorthogonal adaptive orbitals
and also allows the basis to be split
into an active and a secondary part (only the active orbitals are correlated).
This yields a highly flexible ansatz that converges to the exact limit, i.e. multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree-Fock.
In 2018, Sato et al. proposed the time-dependent OCC (TD-OCC)
Sato et al. 2018
method,
which uses orthogonal orbitals and presumably does not converge to the exact limit.
However, TD-OCCDT calculations seem to agree very well with higher-level
calculations,

Sato et al. 2018
;
Pathak, Sato, and Ishikawa 2020
;
Pathak, Sato, and Ishikawa 2021

which indicates that the use of an orthogonal basis does not introduce large errors in practice.

In the context of nuclear or vibrational dynamics, our group has introduced the
time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster
Madsen et al. 2020a
method, which uses an adaptive active space inspired by multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree
(formally speaking, time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster can be considered a vibrational analogue of orbital-adaptive time-dependent coupled cluster).
Again, the use of a biorthogonal basis guarantees the convergence to the exact solution, i.e. multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree.

Splitting a biorthogonal basis into active and secondary parts leads to the
peculiar situation that the active ket and bra bases are allowed
to span different spaces. Although it is consistent with the formalism,
we have found that this feature sometimes leads to numerical instability.
Højlund et al. 2022

In Ref.
41
we proposed
a scheme that effectively locks the active bra and ket spaces together, while still allowing
non-unitary transformations within the active space. Although this scheme was shown to
solve the stability problem without sacrificing accuracy, we certainly feel there is
more to be learned about time-dependent coupled cluster with adaptive basis functions.
In this paper we therefore consider the use of orthogonal, adaptive basis functions
in vibrational coupled cluster. The resulting method (which is
analogous to time-dependent orbital-optimized coupled cluster) is denoted orthogonal time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster, or oTDMVCC for short.

Time-dependent coupled cluster equations are often derived using Arponen’s
time-dependent bivariational principle
Arponen 1983
.
The original version of this principle uses a complex-valued
action, which must be a holomorphic or complex
analytic function of the wave function parameters,
as explained by Kvaal
Kvaal 2012
.
We will see that the use of an orthogonal basis leads to
a non-holomorphic action, which has some important
mathematical consequences that are best explained using the terminology of complex analysis
Stein and Shakarchi 2003
.
For the convenience of the reader and for the clarity of our exposition, we provide
a brief overview of some aspects of complex analysis (see Appendix
A
)
that we will use throughout the paper.

The paper is organized as follows:
Section
II
covers the theory,
including the time-dependent bivariational principle for holomorphic and non-holomorphic
parameterizations and derivations of the equations of motion.
This is followed by a brief description of our
computer implementation in Sec.
III

and a few numerical examples in Sec.
IV
.
Section
V
summarizes our findings
and concludes with an outlook on future work.

II
Theory

II.1
The time-dependent bivariational principle

In Ref.
41
, we considered a
complex bivariational Lagrangian,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\langle\Psi^{\prime}|(i\partial_{t}-H)|\Psi\rangle=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{y},\dot{\mathbf{y}},t),

(1)

where the bra and ket states are formally independent. We showed the
well-known fact that stationary points (
\delta\mathcal{S}=0
) of
the action-like functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\mathcal{L}\differential{t}

(2)

correspond to the solutions of a set of Euler-Lagrange equations,

\displaystyle 0=\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{y_i}-\derivative{t}\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{\dot{y}_i},

(3)

for the wave function parameters
y_{i}
. This formalism requires the Lagrangian to
be a complex analytic or holomorphic
Stein and Shakarchi 2003
function of the complex parameters
y_{i}
, implying
that complex conjugation cannot appear in the wave function parameterization.
Kvaal 2012

This restriction rules out the use of
orthogonal basis sets, since in that case the bra basis functions are simply the complex conjugate of the ket basis functions.
Instead, one should a biorthogonal basis.

One might be tempted to ignore these formal considerations and simply
write down
\mathcal{L}
with an orthogonal basis set, which would then make
\mathcal{L}
a complex-valued, non-holomorphic function.
As explained in Appendix
A
, we can consider such a function
\mathcal{L}
as depending on
y_{i}
and
y_{i}^{*}
, which are treated as independent variables.
Making the corresponding action-like functional stationary again leads to a set of Euler-Lagrange equations,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{y_i}-\derivative{t}\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{\dot{y}_i},

(4a)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{y_i^*}-\derivative{t}\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{\dot{y}_i^*}.

(4b)

The trouble is that these equations are not each other’s complex conjugate since
\mathcal{L}
is complex.
As an example, note that

\displaystyle\left(\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{y_i}\right)^{\!*}=\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}^*}{y_i^*}\neq\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{y_i^*}.

(5)

The consequence is that Eqs. (
4
) do not have a
consistent solution (this kind of situation is also explained in Appendix
A
).
We can attempt to solve Eqs. (
4
) while treating
y_{i}
and
y_{i}^{*}
as truly independent,
but the solution will not
respect the obvious requirement that
(\mathrm{d}y_{i}/\mathrm{d}t)^{*}=(\mathrm{d}y_{i}^{*}/\mathrm{d}t)
, i.e. complex conjugate pairs do not
remain each other’s complex conjugate. Such a formalism
is thus inconsistent, as also noted by Kvaal
Kvaal 2012
.

In this paper we consider instead
a manifestly real Lagrangian,

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{L}}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathcal{L}+\mathcal{L}^{*})=\bar{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{y},\dot{\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{y}^{*},\dot{\mathbf{y}}^{*},t),

(6)

that depends on the wave function parameters, the complex conjugate parameters and the time derivative of both.
As usual, we define an action-like functional,

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{S}}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\bar{\mathcal{L}}\differential{t},

(7)

which is made stationary (
\delta\bar{\mathcal{S}}=0
) with respect
to variations in the parameters
y_{i}
and the complex conjugate parameters

y_{i}^{*}
. The resulting Euler-Lagrange equations are:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{\bar{\mathcal{L}}}{y_i}-\derivative{t}\partialderivative{\bar{\mathcal{L}}}{\dot{y}_i},

(8a)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{\bar{\mathcal{L}}}{y_i^*}-\derivative{t}\partialderivative{\bar{\mathcal{L}}}{\dot{y}_i^*}.

(8b)

Since
\bar{\mathcal{L}}
is real, the two sets of Euler-Lagrange equations are exactly each other’s complex conjugate ensuring
that the equations of motion for
y_{i}
and
y_{i}^{*}
are also each other’s complex conjugate in the sense that

(\mathrm{d}y_{i}/\mathrm{d}t)^{*}=(\mathrm{d}y_{i}^{*}/\mathrm{d}t)
.
Having settled this point, we only need to solve either Eq. (
8a
) or (
8b
).
Real Lagrangians like Eq. (
6
) have previously been considered in the literature.

Pedersen and Koch 1997
;
Pedersen and Koch 1998
;
Sato et al. 2018
;
Kristiansen et al. 2022

It is quite possible some parameters
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbf{y}
appear only as
\alpha_{i}
and never as

\alpha_{i}^{*}
in the bra and ket states, meaning
\mathcal{L}
is a holomorphic function of
\alpha_{i}
.
In that case the Euler-Lagrange equations of Eq. (
8a
) simplify in the following way:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{\bar{\mathcal{L}}}{\alpha_i}-\derivative{t}\partialderivative{\bar{\mathcal{L}}}{\dot{\alpha}_i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{\alpha_i}-\derivative{t}\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{\dot{\alpha}_i}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{\alpha_i^*}-\derivative{t}\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{\dot{\alpha}_i^*}\right)^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{\alpha_i}-\derivative{t}\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{\dot{\alpha}_i}\right)

(9)

We recover a set of Euler-Lagrange equations based on the complex Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}
, which we will refer to as being
complex bivariational
.
Similarly, we will use the term
real bivariational
when referring to
equations based on the real Lagrangian.
Equation (
9
) then shows that the real and complex bivariational principles are
equivalent for the special subset of parameters
\alpha_{i}
. For parameterizations that
are holomorphic in all parameters, e.g. time-dependent coupled cluster with static basis functions,
the two principles yield fully equivalent equations of motion.

II.2
Parameterization

II.2.1
Time-dependent basis sets

The wave function is parameterized using time-dependent orthonormal
basis functions (denoted modals or orbitals), that are in turn expanded in a primitive
orthonormal basis.
Rather than working directly with the concrete basis functions, we employ
a second quantization formalism for vibrational structure
Christiansen 2004

based on creation and annihilation operators (not ladder operators) that obey the following commutator relations:

\displaystyle[a^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}_{\mspace{-1mu}\alpha^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}},a^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}\beta^{m}}]

\displaystyle=\delta_{mm^{\prime}}\delta_{\alpha^{m}\beta^{m}}

(10a)

\displaystyle[a^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}_{\mspace{-1mu}\alpha^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}},a^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}\beta^{m}}]

\displaystyle=0

(10b)

\displaystyle[a^{m^{\smash{\prime}}\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{1.80835pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}\alpha^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}},a^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}\beta^{m}}]

\displaystyle=0.

(10c)

We use indices
m
and
m^{\prime}
to enumerate the vibrational modes (which are distinguishable degrees of freedom), while
the indices
\alpha^{m}
and
\beta^{m}
indicate the modal basis functions for each individual mode.
The well-known electronic structure second quantization formalism
Helgaker, Jørgensen, and Olsen 2000

is defined in terms of anti-commutators rather than commutators, which reflects the very different physical nature
of electronic and nuclear motion. However, the one-mode shift operators,

\displaystyle E^{m}_{{\alpha}^{m}{\beta}^{m}}=a^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}\alpha^{m}}a^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}\beta^{m}},

(11)

satisfy essentially the same commutator as their electronic counterparts:

\displaystyle[E^{m}_{{\alpha}^{m}{\beta}^{m}},E^{m^{\prime}}_{{\gamma}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}\!{\delta}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}}]=\delta_{mm^{\prime}}(\delta_{\beta^{m}\gamma^{m}}E^{m}_{{\alpha}^{m}{\delta}^{m}}-\delta_{\alpha^{m}\delta^{m}}E^{m}_{{\gamma}^{m}{\beta}^{m}}).

(12)

This means that our derivations carry over to electronic structure after removing mode indices as appropriate.

\displaystyle\tilde{a}^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}p^{m}}=\sum_{\alpha}a^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}\alpha^{m}}V^{m}_{\mspace{-2mu}\alpha^{m}p^{m}}.

(13)

The tilde on the left-hand side indicates the time-dependence of the creation operator.
We use
\alpha^{m}
,
\beta^{m}
to denote primitive indices, while
p^{m}
,
q^{m}
,
r^{m}
,
s^{m}

denote generic time-dependent indices. The time-dependent basis is
split into an active basis indexed by
t^{m}
,
u^{m}
,
v^{m}
,
w^{m}
and a secondary basis indexed by
x^{m}
,
y^{m}
.
We use the symbols
N
and
N_{\mspace{-2mu}\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle A}}
for the total number of basis functions per mode and the number
of active basis functions per mode, respectively.
Collecting the basis set coefficients in square matrices
\mathbf{V}^{m}
,
we obtain the following block structure:

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{m}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c | c}{\mathbf{V}^{m}_{\!\!\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle A}}\,}&\,\mathbf{V}^{m}_{\!\!\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle S}}\rule[-4.30554pt]{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\end{array}\right].

Using the same matrix notation, we write the orthonormality
constraint as

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{m\dagger}\,\mathbf{V}^{m}=\mathbf{1}^{m}.

(15)

We also need to consider the corresponding consistency conditions
Dirac 1950
;
Ohta 2000
;
Ohta 2004
,
i.e. the time derivative of the constraints:

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}=\dot{\mathbf{V}}^{m\dagger}\,\mathbf{V}^{m}+\mathbf{V}^{m\dagger}\,\dot{\mathbf{V}}^{m}.

(16)

The consistency conditions are trivially satisfied if

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{V}}^{m\dagger}\,\mathbf{V}^{m}

\displaystyle=+i\mathbf{G}^{m},

(17a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{m\dagger}\,\dot{\mathbf{V}}^{m}

\displaystyle=-i\mathbf{G}^{m},

(17b)

where
\mathbf{G}^{m}
is a Hermitian but otherwise arbitrary time-dependent matrix, which
is typically denoted the constraint matrix. The corresponding constraint operator
is defined as

\displaystyle g=\sum_{m}g^{m}=\sum_{m}\sum_{p^{m}q^{m}}g^{m}_{{p}^{m}{q}^{m}}\tilde{E}^{m}_{{p}^{m}{q}^{m}}.

(18)

Using Eq. (
17b
) and the unitarity of
\mathbf{V}^{m}
now yields the equations of motion for the basis set coefficients,

\displaystyle i\dot{\mathbf{V}}^{m}=\mathbf{V}^{m}\mathbf{G}^{m}.

(19)

This shows that the time evolution of the basis set is generated by the constraint matrices,
which must be determined from the basis set Euler-Lagrange equations.

II.2.2
Wave function expansion

We consider wave function expansions of the form

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi}

\displaystyle=\ket{\Psi(\bm{\alpha},\mathbf{V})},

(20a)

\displaystyle\bra{\Psi^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\bra{\Psi^{\prime}(\bm{\alpha},\mathbf{V}^{*})}

(20b)

where
\bm{\alpha}
denotes a vector of complex configurational parameters
as opposed to the basis set parameters
\mathbf{V}=\{\mathbf{V}^{m}\}
.
We make the restriction from the outset that the bra and ket states depend only on
\bm{\alpha}

and not on
\bm{\alpha}^{*}
.
The ket state depends on the basis set coefficients
\mathbf{V}
themselves,
while the bra state depends only on the complex conjugate basis set coefficients,
as indicated in Eq. (
20b
).
Finally, the wave function is required to
be contained in the active space, i.e. the space spanned by the active basis functions.
Although this means that the secondary basis functions are not explicitly present in the wave function,
we find it instructive to derive equations of motion for the full matrices
\mathbf{V}^{m}
, including the active
and
secondary blocks.

II.3
Equations of motion

II.3.1
Configurational parameters

The Lagrangian is (by ansatz) a holomorphic function of the configurational parameters
\alpha_{i}
,
so the real bivariational Euler-Lagrange equations (based on
\bar{\mathcal{L}}
) simplify to a set of complex bivariational Euler-Lagrange equations (based on
\mathcal{L}
)
as shown in Eq. (
9
). The complex bivariational case was considered in Ref.
41
,
so rather than deriving the equations of motion from scratch, we simply cite the result:

\displaystyle i\dot{\alpha}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{ij}\bigg(h_{j}-\sum_{m}\sum_{p^{m}q^{m}}A_{{j}({m}\,{p}^{m}{q}^{m})}g^{m}_{{p}^{m}{q}^{m}}\bigg)

(21)

Here, we have defined an anti-symmetric matrix
\mathbf{M}
with elements

\displaystyle M_{ij}=\Big\langle\partialderivative{\Psi'}{\alpha_i}\mspace{2mu}\Big|\mspace{1.5mu}\partialderivative{\Psi}{\alpha_j}\Big\rangle-\Big\langle\partialderivative{\Psi'}{\alpha_j}\mspace{2mu}\Big|\mspace{1.5mu}\partialderivative{\Psi}{\alpha_i}\Big\rangle

(22)

and a vector
\mathbf{h}
that contains energy derivatives:

\displaystyle h_{i}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{\mathcal{H}}{\alpha_i},\quad\mathcal{H}=\langle\Psi^{\prime}|H|\Psi\rangle.

(23)

The matrix
\mathbf{A}
holds derivatives of the one-mode density matrices,

\displaystyle A_{{j}({m}\,{p}^{m}{q}^{m})}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{\rrho{m}{q}{p}}{\alpha_j},\quad\rho^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}q^{m}p^{m}}=\langle\Psi^{\prime}|\tilde{E}^{m}_{{p}^{m}{q}^{m}}|\Psi\rangle.

(24)

The notation
(m\,p^{m}q^{m})
is understood to denote a single compound index.

II.3.2
Basis set parameters

Before considering the basis set equations derived from the real Lagrangian
\bar{\mathcal{L}}
,
we recall the analogous equations from Ref.
41
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{F}^{m}-\big(\bm{\rho}^{m}\mathbf{G}^{m}-\mathbf{G}^{m}\bm{\rho}^{m}\big)+i\dot{\bm{\rho}}^{m},

(25)

The matrix
\mathbf{F}^{m}
has elements

\displaystyle F^{m}_{{q}^{m}{p}^{m}}

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi^{\prime}|[H,\tilde{E}^{m}_{{p}^{m}{q}^{m}}]|\Psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi^{\prime}|[H,\tilde{a}^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}p^{m}}]\tilde{a}^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}q^{m}}|\Psi\rangle-\langle\Psi^{\prime}|\tilde{a}^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}p^{m}}[\tilde{a}^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}q^{m}},H]|\Psi\rangle

(26)

and is thus the difference between the generalized mean-field or Fock matrices

\tilde{\mathbf{F}}^{\prime m}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{F}}^{m}
:

\displaystyle\tilde{F}^{\prime m}_{{q}^{m}{p}^{m}}

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi^{\prime}|[H,\tilde{a}^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}p^{m}}]\tilde{a}^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}q^{m}}|\Psi\rangle,

(27a)

\displaystyle\tilde{F}^{m}_{{q}^{m}{p}^{m}}

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi^{\prime}|\tilde{a}^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}p^{m}}[\tilde{a}^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}q^{m}},H]|\Psi\rangle.

(27b)

We showed in Ref.
41
that the element indexed by
(q^{m}p^{m})

of the complex bivariational constraint equations, Eq. (

),
can be written as

\sum_{m^{\prime}}\sum_{r^{m^{\prime}}s^{m^{\prime}}}\bigg[C_{({m}\,{p}^{m}{q}^{m})({m^{\prime}}{r}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}\!{s}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}})}+\sum_{ij}A_{{i}({m}\,{p}^{m}{q}^{m})}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{ij}A_{{j}({m^{\prime}}{r}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}\!{s}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}})}\bigg]g^{m^{\prime}}_{{r}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}\!{s}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}}\\
=\langle\Psi^{\prime}|[H,\tilde{E}^{m}_{{p}^{m}{q}^{m}}]|\Psi\rangle+\sum_{ij}A_{{i}({m}\,{p}^{m}{q}^{m})}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{ij}h_{j}

(28)

with the definition

\displaystyle C_{({m}\,{p}^{m}{q}^{m})({m^{\prime}}{r}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}\!{s}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}})}

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi^{\prime}|[\tilde{E}^{m^{\prime}}_{{r}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}\!{s}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}},\tilde{E}^{m}_{{p}^{m}{q}^{m}}]|\Psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta_{mm^{\prime}}(\delta_{s^{m}p^{m}}\rho^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}q^{m}r^{m}}-\delta_{r^{m}q^{m}}\rho^{m}_{\mspace{-0mu}s^{m}p^{m}}).

(29)

In matrix notation, Eq. (
28
) reads

\displaystyle(\mathbf{C}+\mathbf{A}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{A})\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{f}+\mathbf{A}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{h}

(30)

or, even more compactly,

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}^{\prime}\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{f}^{\prime}\mathbf{h}.

(31)

The vector
\mathbf{g}

The result simply reads

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{A}\Big[\mathbf{F}^{m}-\big(\bm{\rho}^{m}\mathbf{G}^{m}-\mathbf{G}^{m}\bm{\rho}^{m}\big)+i\dot{\bm{\rho}}^{m}\Big],

denotes the anti-Hermitian part of a square matrix

for the Hermitian part).

part of Eq. (
25
) (derived from a complex Lagrangian).
This shows very clearly how the symmetrization of the Lagrangian induces a symmetrization of the constraint equations.

The elementwise representation of Eq. (

Note how the modal indices are exchanged in the complex conjugate terms:

\displaystyle(p^{m}q^{m})

\displaystyle\rightarrow(q^{m}p^{m}),

\displaystyle(r^{m^{\prime}}s^{m^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\rightarrow(s^{m^{\prime}}r^{m^{\prime}}).

(34)

with elements

\displaystyle S_{({m}\,{p}^{m}{q}^{m})({m^{\prime}}{r}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}\!{s}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}})}

\displaystyle=\delta_{mm^{\prime}}S^{m}_{(p^{m}q^{m})(r^{m}s^{m})},

(35a)

\displaystyle S^{m}_{(p^{m}q^{m})(r^{m}s^{m})}

\displaystyle=\delta_{p^{m}s^{m}}\delta_{q^{m}r^{m}}.

(35b)

III
Implementation

The orthogonal time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster method has been implemented in the Molecular Interactions, Dynamics and Simulations Chemistry Program Package
Christiansen et al. 2023
.
At the two-mode excitation level and for Hamiltonians with one- and two-mode couplings
(oTDMVCC[2]/H2), the code uses the efficient TDMVCC[2]/H2 implementation of Ref.
53
,
which allows computations on large systems.
The only modification necessary was the symmetrization of mean-field and density matrices,
which carries negligible cost.

For higher excitation and coupling levels, the implementation is based on the full-space matrix representation
framework introduced in Ref.
27
. This
is essentially a full configuration interaction code, so the computational effort scales exponentially with respect to the number
of modes.
To solve the active-space constraint equations, Eq. (
52a
),
we compute the matrix
{{}^{aa}\bar{\mathbf{C}}^{\prime}}
and perform a singular value decomposition:

\displaystyle{{}^{aa}\bar{\mathbf{C}}^{\prime}}=\bm{\mathcal{U}}\mathbf{\Sigma}\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}.

(89)

The singular value decomposition is regularized to avoid singularities before the inverse is computed:

\displaystyle{{}^{aa}\bar{\mathbf{C}}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{reg}}}=\bm{\mathcal{U}}\big(\mathbf{\Sigma}+\exp(-\mbf{\Sigma} / \epsilon_\mrm{reg})\big)\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}.

(90)

Here,
\epsilon_{\mathrm{reg}}
is a small regularization parameter (typically,
\epsilon_{\mathrm{reg}}=10^{-8}
).
The constraint elements are finally obtained as

\displaystyle{{}^{a}\mathbf{g}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{{}^{u}\mathbf{g}}\\
\hline\cr{{}^{d}\mathbf{g}}\end{array}\right]=\big({{}^{aa}\bar{\mathbf{C}}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{reg}}}\big)^{-1}\;{{}^{a}\bar{\mathbf{f}}^{\prime}}.

The symmetries of
{{}^{aa}\bar{\mathbf{C}}^{\prime}}
ensure that
{{}^{u}\mathbf{g}}={{}^{d}\mathbf{g}^{*}}
to numerical precision
so that the constraint matrices are properly Hermitian.
However, this also means that we are, in a sense, solving twice for the same constraint elements.
It is clear that an efficient, scalable implementation should
(i) utilize the symmetries at hand and
(ii) use iterative solvers. Such refinements are, however, beyond the scope of this paper.

To improve numerical stability, the secondary-space projector in Eq. (
60
) is replaced by a modified
projector

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{mod}}=\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{V}_{\!\!\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle A}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}(\mathbf{V}_{\!\!\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle A}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{V}_{\!\!\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle A}}^{\phantom{\dagger}})^{-1}\,\mathbf{V}_{\!\!\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle A}}^{\dagger}.

(93)

This procedure, which is commonly used in the multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree
Beck et al. 2000
community, ensures that
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{mod}}

remains a proper projector, even if the basis is not strictly orthonormal due to numerical noise and integration error.

Proving the correctness of the implementation is somewhat challenging since the orthogonal time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster method
does not formally converge to the time-dependent full vibrational configuration interaction or multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree limit. Perfect agreement with an exact reference calculation
can therefore not be expected. The time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster hierarchy, on the other hand, does converge to the exact solution,
and we have shown that the time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster and orthogonal time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster
methods differ only by symmetrization of the constraint equations.
We have thus written a new implementation for the time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster constraint equations that
explicitly constructs and inverts the full matrix
{}^{aa}\mathbf{C}^{\prime}
(rather than solving
the equations mode by mode). This code was validated against
a time-dependent full vibrational configuration interaction calculation. The orthogonal time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster code computes
{}^{aa}\mathbf{C}^{\prime}

and explicitly applies the symmetrization to obtain
{}^{aa}\bar{\mathbf{C}}^{\prime}
.

IV.5
Are oTDMVCC[2] and TDMVCC[2] equivalent?

For the triatomic molecules, our results show that oTDMVCC[2] are TDMVCC[2]
are identical up to numerical precision (this is confirmed by inspecting the Hilbert space angles between the oTDMVCC[2] and TDMVCC[2] wave functions; see Fig. S51).
It is not surprising that the two methods should yield
similar predictions in many cases, but we find the agreement rather striking, in particular
for the demanding case of hydrogen sulfide. Here, one might expect to see a clear
manifestation of the difference between the orthogonal and biorthogonal formalisms,
especially since such a difference is visible at the triples level.

We explain this surprising result with the following observations:
First, we note that the oTDMVCC[2] and TDMVCC[2] expansions assume
a very particular form for two- and three-mode systems:

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi}

\displaystyle=e^{T}\ket{\Phi}

\displaystyle=e^{t_{0}}(1+T_{2})\ket{\Phi},

(97a)

\displaystyle\bra{\Psi^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\bra{\Phi^{\prime}}Le^{-T}

\displaystyle=\bra{\Phi^{\prime}}(l_{0}+L_{2})(1+T_{2})e^{-t_{0}}

\displaystyle=\bra{\Phi^{\prime}}\big((l_{0}+L_{2}T_{2})+L_{2}\big)e^{-t_{0}}

\displaystyle\equiv\bra{\Phi^{\prime}}(l_{0}^{\prime}+L_{2})e^{-t_{0}}.

(97b)

This is essentially a CI-type expansion, although with a somewhat peculiar normalization.
The second observation is that the vibrational self-consistent field initial state has an orthogonal
basis and satisfies
\bra{\Psi^{\prime}}=\ket{\Psi}^{\dagger}
.
We thus have an initial state with symmetry between bra and ket, and a parameterization
that allows this symmetry to be maintained. Numerically, we find
that the one-mode density matrices stay Hermitian
and that the mean fields satisfy
\check{\mathbf{F}}=\check{\mathbf{F}}^{\prime\dagger}
for each mode (see Figs. S11, S22, S33 and S44).
In essence, we get variational rather than bivariational time evolution for this special case.
This also has the effect that expectation values like
\langle\Psi^{\prime}|Q|\Psi\rangle
and
\langle\Psi^{\prime}|H|\Psi\rangle

are strictly real, as can be seen in the supplementary material.

For
trans
-bithiophene, oTDMVCC[2] are TDMVCC[2] are very similar, and one must
look closely (e.g. Fig
11(b)
) to see a difference.
It is of course difficult to define precisely what it means for two calculations to be strictly identical
due to the presence of integration error and numerical
noise. However, if we take the agreement between TDMVCC[5] and multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree (Fig
11(b)
) as a benchmark
for perfect agreement, then it is clear that oTDMVCC[2] are TDMVCC[2] are not exactly identical.
We are also not able to see any mathematical reason that the oTDMVCC[2] and TDMVCC[2] equations
should generally be equivalent.

V
Summary and outlook

The equations of motion for bivariational wave functions
with orthogonal, adaptive basis functions have been derived
from a time-dependent bivariational principle. The use of an orthogonal basis
makes the parameterization non-holomorphic (non-analytic in the complex sense),
which necessitates the use of a manifestly real action functional in the derivations.
We relate the orthogonal formalism (real action) to the
corresponding biorthogonal formalism (complex action) in a
transparent way and analyze similarities and differences.
The general equations of motion are then specialized to the coupled cluster ansatz
and implemented for the nuclear dynamics problem.
We denote the resulting method as orthogonal time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster (orthogonal time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster) in order
to distinguish it from time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster, which uses a biorthogonal basis set.
The orthogonal time-dependent modal vibrational coupled cluster amplitude equations are unchanged relative to the biorthogonal case, while the
linear equations that determine the basis set time
evolution (the so-called constraint equations)
are symmetrized in a particular manner.
Although the orthogonal and biorthogonal formalisms thus rely on
the same matrix elements, the symmetrization has some
consequences for the practical implementation of the constraint
equations. In particular, the orthogonal formalism leads
to constraint equations that couple all modes, in contrast
to the biorthogonal constraint equations that can be solved one mode
at a time. At the doubles level, certain simplification occur
so that the oTDMVCC[2] constraint equations can also be solved
mode by mode. The computational cost of oTDMVCC[2] is thus identical
to that of TDMVCC[2]. At higher excitation levels, the orthogonal
formalism generally involves a larger computational and implementation effort.

It is known
Köhn and Olsen 2005
that coupled cluster with orthogonal optimized or adaptive basis functions
does not converge to the exact solution, even when the cluster expansion
is complete. The precise convergence behavior is, however, not well understood, although electron dynamics studies by
Sato and coworkers
Sato et al. 2018
;
Pathak, Sato, and Ishikawa 2020
;
Pathak, Sato, and Ishikawa 2021

indicate that no substantial error is introduced by using an orthogonal basis.
We benchmarked all members of the oTDMVCC and TDMVCC hierarchies against time-dependent full vibrational configuration interaction or multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree
for a number of triatomics (water, ozone, sulfur dioxide and hydrogen sulfide)
and for a 5D model of the
trans
-bithiophene molecule.
It is confirmed very clearly that TDMVCC converges to the exact limit, while
oTDMVCC does not.
For 5D
trans
-bithiophene, the oTDMVCC[5] observables are
visually converged relative to the exact result, and
differences are only revealed on close inspection. Water and sulfur dioxide behave similarly in
the sense that the oTDMVCC[3] results appear fully converged to the unaided eye.
For ozone, small errors are visible at the oTDMVCC[3] level, while
hydrogen sulfide shows very clear differences between oTDMVCC[3] and TDMVCC[3]/TDFVCI (which are equivalent for a three-mode system).
This difference correlates with rather large amplitudes, which indicates
that the coupled cluster expansion is struggling in order to
describe the wave function. In this difficult case, only TDMVCC[3] is able to reproduce TDFVCI
due to the added flexibility of having a biorthogonal basis.
Based on the examples at hand, our conclusion is thus that the orthogonal and biorthogonal formalisms
agree quite closely in many cases, although noticeable differences are certainly possible.

We have emphasized two drawbacks of the oTDMVCC hierarchy, namely the
lack of convergence to the exact limit and the fact that it involves
larger sets of linear equations compared to the TDMVCC method.
However, the TDMVCC method also has a drawback in the sense that it
can sometimes be prone to numerical
instability when the basis is split into and active
and a secondary basis.
Højlund et al. 2022

The so-called restricted polar scheme presented in Ref.
41
solves
the stability problem without deteriorating accuracy, but the scheme is not fully bivariational. It is thus highly
pertinent to investigate new formalisms that combine the following desirable properties
in a fully bivariational way:
(i) Numerical stability;
(ii) convergence to the exact solution; and
(iii) simple linear equations.
Such a formalism is the subject of current research in our group.

Appendix A
Complex analysis

This appendix covers a few basic aspects of complex analysis.
Our description is strongly inspired by Chapter 2.2 of Ref.
43
to which we
refer the reader for further details.
For simplicity of notation, we consider only functions of a single complex variable,
e.g.
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, where the domain
\Omega
is an open set in
\mathbb{C}
.
We are free to write the function
f
and its argument
z
in terms of real and imaginary parts, i.e.

\displaystyle z=x+iy,

(
A
1)

\displaystyle f(z)=f(x,y)=u(x,y)+iv(x,y),

(
A
2)

where
x,y,u,v
are real.
As an entry point to our discussion, let us consider a common kind
of problem, namely the problem of making
f
stationary:

\displaystyle\partialderivative{f}{x}=0,\quad\partialderivative{f}{y}=0.

(
A
3)

Here,
\partial/\partial x
and
\partial/\partial y
denote partial derivatives in the ordinary, real sense.
Separating
f
into real and imaginary parts, this is obviously equivalent to

\displaystyle\partialderivative{u}{x}=0,\quad\partialderivative{v}{x}=0,\quad\partialderivative{u}{y}=0,\quad\partialderivative{v}{y}=0,

(
A
4)

which are four real equations with two real unknowns. Such a system
cannot have a solution unless
f
has some additional structure
that eliminates two of the equations. We will discuss two kinds of
structure that are relevant to our work,
one which is mathematically trivial and one which
has far-reaching consequences. The first case
can be stated as

\displaystyle f(x,y)=\alpha+\beta g(x,y),

(
A
5)

where
g
is a real-valued function and
\alpha,\beta
are complex numbers.
This includes
f
being real, which is of course a common situation, or purely imaginary.
Making
f
stationary with respect to
x
and
y

is now a matter of solving two real equations with two real unknowns:

\displaystyle\partialderivative{g}{x}=0,\quad\partialderivative{g}{y}=0.

(
A
6)

A solution need not exist, of course, but we cannot rule out the possibility without more information.

In the second case,
f
is holomorphic.
The function
f

is holomorphic (or complex differentiable) at the point
z_{0}\in\Omega
if
the quotient

\displaystyle\frac{f(z_{0}+h)-f(z_{0})}{h}

(
A
7)

converges to a limit when
h\rightarrow 0
. In that case,
the limit is denoted by
f^{\prime}(z_{0})
, and is called the derivative of
f
at
z_{0}
:

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(z_{0})=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{f(z_{0}+h)-f(z_{0})}{h}.

(
A
8)

Although this definition looks exactly like the definition of a real derivative,
it should be emphasized that
h
is complex number that may approach
0

from any direction. This turns out to have profound implications.
A holomorphic function is, for example, infinitely differentiable, i.e.
the existence of the first derivative guarantees the existence of all higher derivatives.
Stein and Shakarchi 2003

Holomorphic functions are also analytic in the sense that they are given (locally)
by a convergent power series expansion.
Stein and Shakarchi 2003

One can easily show (by taking
h
to be real and then purely imaginary)
that the existence of
f^{\prime}(z_{0})
implies

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(z_{0})=\partialderivative{f}{x}\/(z_{0})=\frac{1}{i}\partialderivative{f}{y}\/(z_{0})

(
A
9)

or, after separating real and imaginary parts,

\displaystyle\partialderivative{u}{x}=\partialderivative{v}{y},\quad\partialderivative{u}{y}=-\partialderivative{v}{x}.

(
A
10)

These are the Cauchy-Riemann conditions, which connect real
and complex analysis. We note that the Cauchy-Riemann conditions eliminate
two equations in Eq. (
4
), so that
we are left with two real equations with two real unknowns.
Equation (
9
) also implies that

\displaystyle\partialderivative{f}{z}\/(z_{0})

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left(\partialderivative{f}{x}\/(z_{0})+\frac{1}{i}\partialderivative{f}{y}\/(z_{0})\right)

\displaystyle=f^{\prime}(z_{0}),

(
A
11)

\displaystyle\partialderivative{f}{z^*}\/(z_{0})

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left(\partialderivative{f}{x}\/(z_{0})-\frac{1}{i}\partialderivative{f}{y}\/(z_{0})\right)

\displaystyle=0,

(
A
12)

if
f
is holomorphic.
Here, we have
defined
the differential operators
\partial/\partial z
and

\partial/\partial z^{*}
, which are sometimes called Wirtinger derivatives.
These derivatives are, in principle, nothing more that a shorthand
for a certain combination of real derivatives, but they are nonetheless very convenient.
Equation (
11
) means that
\partial/\partial z
agrees
with the complex partial derivative for holomorphic functions, while Eq. (
12
)
states that holomorphic functions have no formal dependence on
z^{*}
.
For non-holomorphic functions
(where the complex derivative does not exist), the Wirtinger derivatives still
have meaning provided the real derivatives
\partial/\partial x
and
\partial/\partial y
exist.
With the definition of Wirtinger derivatives, the optimization problem in Eq. (
3
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\partialderivative{f}{z}=0,\quad\partialderivative{f}{z^*}=0,

(
A
13)

where
z
and
z^{*}
are considered as independent variables.
For a holomorphic function
f
,
the latter equation is identically zero, and we are simply left with

\displaystyle\partialderivative{f}{z}=0.

(
A
14)

For a non-holomorphic function
f
, both equations must generally be considered.

Wirtinger derivatives have a number of pleasant properties that greatly simplify practical calculations:

\displaystyle\left(\partialderivative{f}{z}\right)^{\!*}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{f^*}{z^*}

(
A
15)

\displaystyle\left(\partialderivative{f}{z^*}\right)^{\!*}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{f^*}{z}

(
A
16)

\displaystyle\partialderivative{z}\/(fg)

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{f}{z}g+f\partialderivative{g}{z}\quad

(product rule)

(
A
17)

\displaystyle\partialderivative{z^*}\/(fg)

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{f}{z^*}g+f\partialderivative{g}{z^*}\quad

(product rule)

(
A
18)

\displaystyle\partialderivative{z}\/(f\circ g)

\displaystyle=\left(\partialderivative{f}{z}\circ g\right)\partialderivative{g}{z}+\left(\partialderivative{f}{z^*}\circ g\right)\partialderivative{g^*}{z}\quad

(chain rule)

(
A
19)

\displaystyle\partialderivative{z^*}\/(f\circ g)

\displaystyle=\left(\partialderivative{f}{z}\circ g\right)\partialderivative{g}{z^*}+\left(\partialderivative{f}{z^*}\circ g\right)\partialderivative{g^*}{z^*}\quad

(chain rule)

(
A
20)

\displaystyle\partialderivative{z}{z^*}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{z^*}{z}

\displaystyle=0,

(
A
21)

\displaystyle\partialderivative{z}{z}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{z^*}{z^*}

\displaystyle=1.

(
A
22)

The mechanics of computing Wirtinger derivatives is thus essentially identical
to that of computing real derivatives, provided we think about
z
and
z^{*}
as independent variables.
In addition, we never need to think about real and imaginary parts explicitly.

An important mapping that is not holomorphic is complex conjugation,
f(z)=z^{*}
.
Indeed,

\displaystyle\frac{f(z_{0}+h)-f(z_{0})}{h}=\frac{h^{*}}{h},

(
A
23)

which has no limit as
h\rightarrow 0
. This is easily checked by taking

h
to be real (in which case the quotient equals
1
)
and then purely imaginary (in which case the quotient equals
-1
).
Another common function is the square modulus,
f(z)=\absolutevalue{z}^{2}=zz^{*}
,
which is non-holomorphic due to the presence of
z^{*}
. In spite
of this, we can make
f
stationary without resorting to real and imaginary parts
by making use of the Wirtinger derivatives:

\displaystyle\partialderivative{f}{z}=z^{*}=0,\quad\partialderivative{f}{z^*}=z=0.

(
A
24)

The solution is obviously
z=0
. It is noted that since
f
is real, the two
equations are each other’s complex conjugate, so that one equation
is effectively eliminated.

For non-holomorphic functions like Eq. (
5
)
we find that the optimization problem becomes

\displaystyle\partialderivative{f}{z}=\beta\partialderivative{g}{z}=0,\quad\partialderivative{f}{z^*}=\beta\partialderivative{g}{z^*}=0.

(
A
25)

or, equivalently,

\displaystyle\partialderivative{g}{z}=0,\quad\partialderivative{g}{z^*}=0.

(
A
26)

Since
g
is real, the two equations are
simply each other’s complex conjugate, so we only need to solve one of them.

For generic non-holomorphic functions we cannot
hope for a solution. As an example, consider
f(z)=z^{2}+z^{*}
.
Attempting to make this function stationary yields

\displaystyle\partialderivative{f}{z}=2z=0,\quad\partialderivative{f}{z^*}=1=0,

(
A
27)

which has no solution. This mirrors the discussion after Eq. (
4
).
