Abstract
. It is shown that Birkhoff–James orthogonality knows everything about the smooth norms in reflexive Banach spaces and can also compute the dimensions of the underlying normed spaces.

1.
Introduction and preliminaries

General normed spaces are not equipped with an inner product. Nonetheless, there do exist several nonequivalent
extensions of orthogonality from inner product spaces to general normed ones. Arguably, one of the most well-known in this respect is the Birkhoff–James orthogonality, introduced by Birkhoff
[
8
]
and developed by
James
[
19
,
17
,
18
]
. It is defined in an arbitrary normed space
(\mathscr{X},\|\cdot\|)

over the field
\mathbb{F}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
as follows: the vector
x\in\mathscr{X}

is
Birkhoff–James orthogonal to
y\in\mathscr{X}
(shortly: BJ orthogonal; in symbols:
x\perp y
), if

\|x+\lambda y\|\geq\|x\|,\quad\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{F}.

One easily sees that in inner product spaces BJ orthogonality is equivalent to the usual one. More importantly, several key properties of orthogonality in inner product spaces are inherited within BJ orthogonality. For example, among all the vectors from a subspace
\mathscr{Y}\subseteq\mathscr{X}
a vector
y_{0}\in\mathscr{Y}
is the best approximation to
x
(in the sense that
\|x-y_{0}\|=\min_{y\in\mathscr{Y}}\|x-y\|
)
precisely when
x-y_{0}
is BJ orthogonal to
\mathscr{Y}
. Another trivial implication, valid for every BJ orthogonal (i.e.,
\mathcal{M}\perp\mathcal{N}
)
closed
subspaces
\mathcal{M},\,\mathcal{N}
in a Banach space
\mathscr{X}
is that they have trivial intersection (excuse the pun) and their sum,
\mathcal{M}+\mathcal{N}
is also a closed subspace — this can be seen by noting that
\mathcal{M}\perp\mathcal{N}
implies that the projection from
\mathcal{M}+\mathcal{N}
onto
\mathcal{M}

is norm-one.
In this respect, we mention here a classical result by Anderson
[
4
]
that the range of an inner-derivation, induced by a normal operator
N
on a complex Hilbert space, is always BJ orthogonal to its kernel (this result has been greatly extended in various directions
[
21
]
).
Among other applications of BJ orthogonality we mention Bhatia–Šemrl’s alternative proof that the diameter of the unitary orbit of a given complex matrix
A
equals
2d(A,\mathbb{C}I)
(see
[
7
]
).

It is an exercise that BJ orthogonality is in general nonsymmetric, that is,
x\perp y
does not always
imply
y\perp x
(e.g., in the space
(\mathbb{F}^{2},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
where
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
is the maximum norm we have

(1,1)\perp(1,0)
but
(1,0)\not\perp(1,1)
). In fact, James showed
[
18
]
that in real spaces of dimension at least three BJ orthogonality is symmetric if and only if the norm is induced by an inner product. His proof works also for complex normed spaces almost with no change, except that instead of Kakutani’s
[
20
]
classification of real inner product spaces (being the ones where for each two-dimensional subspace there exists a norm-one projection onto it), one has to use Bohnenblust’s
[
10
]
extension
of Kakutani’s classification to complex spaces. We refer the interested reader to the book by Amir
[
3
]
where several other classifications of inner-product spaces are found, including a self-contained proof of Kakutani’s classification (see
[
3
, §12]
).

Thus, BJ orthogonality alone can detect if the norm comes from an inner product or not. For two-dimensional spaces such a nice characterization does not exist though; the two-dimensional real vector spaces where BJ orthogonality is symmetric are called Radon planes, an example of which can be found in
[
18
, p. 561]
. We refer to
[
23
]
for much more information as well as historical remarks on this topic.

There are several additional characterizations of inner product spaces in
[
18
]
but they use BJ orthogonality relation and additivity operation in unison.
One such, for example, is the following: a real space
\mathscr{X}
of dimension at least three is an inner product space if and only if
x\perp z
and
y\perp z
always imply that
(x+y)\perp z
(see
[
18
, Theorem 2]
). In a similar way, it was shown in
[
17
]
that BJ orthogonality together with additivity (and homogeneity operations)
can characterize smooth (and rotund, that is, strictly convex) norms.

Our principal aim here is to study in more details what additional properties of the norm are possible to discern from BJ orthogonality alone, that is, without using it in unison with the underlying linear structure. Similarly to James’ and Bohnenblust’s result that BJ orthogonality knows when the
space is Euclidean we show (in Section 2) that BJ orthogonality alone knows when the space is finite-dimensional; it can compute its dimension, it knows when the norm is smooth and when it is strictly convex, and actually (see Section 3) knows everything about the smooth norms of reflexive Banach spaces up to (conjugate) linear isometry.

The easiest way to formulate and prove our results is to use the directed graph, denoted by
\hat{\Gamma}=\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})
, induced by the BJ orthogonality relation on a normed space
(\mathscr{X},\|\cdot\|)
. By definition, the vertex set of this (di)graph consists of one-dimensional subspaces of
\mathscr{X}

V(\hat{\Gamma})=\{[x]=\mathbb{F}x;\;\;x\in\mathscr{X}\setminus\{0\}\}

with two vertices
[x],[y]\in V(\hat{\Gamma})
forming a directed edge
[x]\to[y]

if and only if
x\perp y
. Observe that this relation is well-defined because BJ orthogonality is homogeneous in both factors. Observe
also that no nonzero vector is BJ orthogonal to itself, so
\hat{\Gamma}
has no loops.
Alternatively, we could define
\hat{\Gamma}
over
\mathscr{X}\setminus\{0\}
; it seems, however, more natural to work
over projective spaces.

We call this graph
di-orthograph
to amplify the fact that its edges are directed. Now, James’ and Bohnenblust’s result, which we already mentioned above, says that three or more dimensional normed spaces
\mathscr{X}
are inner product spaces if and only if the di-orthograph
\hat{\Gamma}=\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})
is a simple graph, meaning that for every directed edge
([x],[y])\in\hat{\Gamma}
, its opposite,
([y],[x])
, is also an edge in
\hat{\Gamma}
.

The motivation behind our study is two-fold. Firstly, it has become clear recently that graphs of certain
relations defined on objects of a given category carry a lot of information. For example, a commuting graph
whose vertices are all noncentral elements and two commuting elements are connected can distinguish a finite
nonabelian simple group among all groups
[
1
,
15
,
29
]
and can also
distinguish among different Hilbert spaces
[
22
]
. Also, as it was shown recently
in
[
12
]
, the graph related to Jordan orthogonality distinguishes among finite-dimensional
formally real simple Jordan algebras.

As for the second motivation, orthographs were used in the recent paper
[
11
]
where a
complete classification of maps which preserve orthogonality on finite-dimensional projective spaces over
reals, complexes, quaternions or octonions is given without assuming surjectivity. Such a map is nothing but a
(possibly nonsurjective) homomorphism of the corresponding orthograph, and so it maps the maximum clique (i.e., the subset of pairwise connected vertices of maximal possible cardinality) onto
another maximum clique. In this respect, the information about orthograph is useful for the investigations of
maps preserving the orthogonality (cf. with
[
9
,
34
]
where, in addition, linearity or at least additivity was assumed), which we are planning to do in a separate paper.

Before proceeding with our main results, let us remark that it is often easier to verify BJ orthogonality with the help of James’ criteria
[
17
, Theorem 2.1]
by which
x\perp y
if and only if there exists a supporting functional
f_{x}
at
x
(i.e., a norm-one linear functional
f_{x}\colon\mathscr{X}\to\mathbb{F}
with
f_{x}(x)=\|x\|
which annihilates
y
). One should mention here that James in
[
17
, Theorem 2.1]
assumed real normed spaces but the proof works also for the complex ones (the necessity part of the proof only requires the fact that if unit vectors satisfy
x\perp y
, then a linear functional
f
which maps
x
to
1
and annihilates
y
is norm-one on the span of
x,y
; using the Hahn–Banach theorem one then extends the domain of this
f
from
\mathop{\mathrm{span}}_{\mathbb{C}}\{x,y\}
to
\mathscr{X}
). We refer also to Kittaneh
[
21
, Lemma 1]
for a shorter proof in real or complex spaces in case the norm is smooth at
x
.

By James’ result, a line
[x]=\mathbb{F}x\subseteq\mathscr{X}
is BJ orthogonal to a line
[y]=\mathbb{F}y
if and only if there exists a supporting functional
f_{x}
at
some representative
x\in[x]
which annihilates the line
[y]
. Recall that, by our definition, a supporting
functional at a vector
x
must satisfy
f_{x}(x)=\|x\|
and
\|f_{x}\|=1
. We will call any such functional
a supporting functional of a line
[x]
. In particular, if
f_{x}
is a supporting functional at a line
[x]
, then
\mu f_{x}
is also, provided that
|\mu|=1
.

To simplify things we will frequently not
distinguish between a nonzero vector
x\in\mathscr{X}
and the line
[x]=\mathbb{F}x\subseteq\mathscr{X}
passing through it. Thus, we will often denote
vertices of di-orthograph
\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})
simply by
x
instead of
[x]
but we do call them lines.

Just before we finished our paper we learned that Tanaka very recently obtained similar results to ours in
[
31
,
32
]
; in particular he also obtained a version of one of our main results, i.e., Theorem
3.5
below. However, our methods are different and give some additional insights. Moreover, our Lemma
3.4
answers a question posted in
[
32
, section Remarks]
.

2.
Main results – property recognition

The fundamental question which we address here is the following:

Question 2.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}
be a given property on a normed space. Can we decide, using BJ orthogonality alone, if a space has property
{\mathcal{P}}
or not?

Of course, this is too general for any hope of a definite answer and we will restrict ourselves to three very specific properties: the dimension, the smoothness, and the rotundness.

Let us first show that by means of
\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})
one can calculate the dimension of a normed
space
\mathscr{X}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

A normed
space
\mathscr{X}
over
\mathbb{F}
is infinite-dimensional if and only if
\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})
contains an infinite clique.

Proof.

See our recent paper
[
5
, Corollary 3.1 and Theorem 3.5]
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\mathscr{X}
be a normed space over
\mathbb{F}
and
n\geq 2
. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

For any
k\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
and any
k
-tuple of lines
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
satisfying
x_{i}\perp x_{j}
,
1\leq i<j\leq k
, one can always find
x_{k+1}\in\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})
with
x_{i}\perp x_{k+1}
,
1\leq i\leq k
.

(ii)

For any
(n-1)
-tuple of lines
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})
, one can always find
x_{n}\in\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})
with
x_{i}\perp x_{n}
,
1\leq i\leq n-1
.

(iii)

\dim\mathscr{X}\geq n
.

Proof.

We will once again rely on the fact that
x\perp y
if and only if
y\in\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}f_{x}
, where
f_{x}
is a
supporting functional at
x
.

(iii)
\Longrightarrow
(ii). Choose an arbitrary
(n-1)
-tuple of lines
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})
. Let
f_{i}
be any supporting functional at
x_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n-1
. Denote
\mathscr{X}_{n-1}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}f_{i}
.
Then
\mathrm{codim}\,\mathscr{X}_{n-1}\leq n-1
together with
\dim\mathscr{X}\geq n
imply that the subspace
\mathscr{X}_{n-1}\subseteq\mathscr{X}
contains at least one line,
x_{n}
. Clearly,
x_{i}\perp x_{n}
for
i=1,\dots,n-1
.

(ii)
\Longrightarrow
(i) is straightforward.

\neg
(iii)
\Longrightarrow

\neg
(i). Assume
\dim\mathscr{X}=k\leq n-1
. From
[
26
, Theorem 25.5, p. 246]
and the fact that the norm is positively homogeneous and
x\mapsto\lambda x
is a differentiable map it follows that the norm’s unit ball has a
smooth point
\hat{x}_{1}
. Let
f_{1}
be a unique supporting functional at
\hat{x}_{1}
and let

x_{1}:=[\hat{x}_{1}]

be the corresponding line in
\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})
. Then
x_{1}\perp y
for a line
y
is equivalent to
y\subseteq\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}f_{1}=\mathscr{X}_{1}
.
Now, the induced norm in the
(k-1)
-dimensional space
\mathscr{X}_{1}
again has a smooth point, say
\hat{x}_{2}
;
let
f_{2}\colon\mathscr{X}_{1}\to\mathbb{F}
be the corresponding supporting functional which by the Hahn–Banach theorem we
extend to a supporting
functional at a line
x_{2}:=[\hat{x}_{2}]
on the original space
\mathscr{X}_{0}:=\mathscr{X}
. Recursively, one obtains a
k
-tuple of lines

(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
, where
x_{i}\subseteq\mathscr{X}_{i-1}\subseteq\mathscr{X}_{i-2},

2\leq i\leq k,
is
a line spanned by a smooth point
\hat{x}_{i}\in\mathscr{X}_{i-1}:=\mathscr{X}_{i-2}\cap\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}f_{i-1}
and has a unique supporting functional
f_{i}\colon\mathscr{X}_{i-1}\to\mathbb{F}
which we again extend by the Hahn–Banach theorem to a supporting functional defined on
\mathscr{X}
. Clearly,
i<j

implies that
x_{j}\subseteq\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}f_{i}
, so
x_{i}\perp x_{j}
for
1\leq i<j\leq k
. Assume a line
x\subseteq\mathscr{X}
satisfies
x_{i}\perp x
for each
1\leq i\leq k
. Since
x_{1}\perp x
and

\hat{x}_{1}\in\mathscr{X}
is smooth, then
x\subseteq\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}f_{1}=\mathscr{X}_{1}
. Since
\hat{x}_{2}\in\mathscr{X}_{1}
is smooth and
x_{2}\perp x
, we
further have
x\subseteq\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}f_{2}\cap\mathscr{X}_{1}=\mathscr{X}_{2}
. Proceeding recursively, one finds

x\subseteq\mathscr{X}_{k}=0
, a contradiction.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

Note that a normed
space
\mathscr{X}
has dimension
n
if and only if the di-orthograph
\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})
satisfies item
(i) with
k=1,\dots,n-1

and does not satisfy item (i) for larger
k
, that is, there exist
n
-tuples of lines

x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})
with
x_{i}\perp x_{j}
for
i<j
but no further line

x\in\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})
satisfies
x_{i}\perp x
for
1\leq i\leq n
. Hence, the dimension of
\mathscr{X}
can be
calculated from the di-orthograph
\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})
alone.

Next we show that
\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})
can detect the presence of nonsmooth points.
It is known that the norm on
\mathscr{X}
is smooth if and only if BJ orthogonality is right
additive in the sense that
x\perp y_{1}
and
x\perp y_{2}
always implies
x\perp(y_{1}+y_{2})
(see
[
17
, Theorem 4.2]
).
Below we provide another criterion for smoothness which does not rely on underlying additive structure.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\mathscr{X}
be a Banach space over
\mathbb{F}
with
2\leq\dim\mathscr{X}=n<\infty
. Then the norm in
\mathscr{X}
is nonsmooth if and only if
there exist
n-1
lines
x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1}\in\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})
and two additional distinct lines

y_{n},y_{n}^{\prime}

such that
x_{i}\perp x_{j}
for
1\leq i<j\leq n-1
, and
x_{i}\perp y_{n}
,
x_{i}\perp y_{n}^{\prime}
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
.

Proof.

If
\mathscr{X}
has a smooth norm, then each of the lines
x_{i}=[\hat{x}_{i}]
has a unique supporting
functional
f_{i}
. From

x_{i}\perp x_{j}
,
i<j
, it follows, by induction, that
x_{i}\subseteq\bigcap_{k=1}^{i-1}\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}f_{k}
. This implies
that the functionals
f_{k}
are linearly independent, for otherwise pick the smallest integer
i
with

f_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}\alpha_{k}f_{k}
and evaluate at
\hat{x}_{i}
to deduce
1=f_{i}(\hat{x}_{i})=\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}\alpha_{k}f_{k}(\hat{x}_{i})=\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}\alpha_{k}\cdot 0=0
, a contradiction. In particular,
\dim\bigcap_{k=1}^{n-1}\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}f_{k}=1
, and there is only one line
y_{n}=\bigcap_{k=1}^{n-1}\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}f_{k}
which satisfies
x_{i}\perp y_{n}

(i=1,\dots,n-1)
.

Assume now that the norm has a nonsmooth point, say at a vector
x_{n-1},
and let
f_{n-1},f_{n-1}^{\prime}
be
two
linearly independent supporting functionals at
x_{n-1}
. Choose a vector
y_{n}
such that
f_{n-1}(y_{n})=0

and

f_{n-1}^{\prime}(y_{n})\neq 0
and choose a nonzero
y_{n}^{\prime}\in\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{x_{n-1},y_{n}\}
with
f_{n-1}^{\prime}(y_{n}^{\prime})=0
. In
particular,

x_{n-1}\perp y_{n}\quad\hbox{and}\quad x_{n-1}\perp y_{n}^{\prime}.

Recursively, choose
norm-one functionals
f_{k}
and unit vectors
x_{k}
for
k=n-2,\dots,1
such that: (i)
y_{n},x_{n-1},\dots,x_{k+1}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}f_{k},
(ii) the norm of
f_{k}
is attained at
x_{k}
. It follows that

f_{k}
is a supporting functional at
x_{k}
and that
x_{i}\perp x_{j}
and
x_{i}\perp y_{n}
for
1\leq i<j\leq n-1
.
Also,
y_{n}^{\prime}\in\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{x_{n-1},y_{n}\}\subseteq\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}f_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n-2
implies
x_{i}\perp y_{n}^{\prime}
for

1\leq i\leq n-2
.
By a slight abuse of language we next identify a nonzero vector with a line spanned by it. Thus, the lines

x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1},y_{n},y_{n}^{\prime}\in\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})
satisfy the claim.
∎

We proceed to classify rotund (i.e., strictly convex) norms. Given a vertex
z
in di-orthograph

\hat{\Gamma}
, let
N_{z}:=\{v\in\hat{\Gamma};\;\;(z,v)\in E(\hat{\Gamma})\}
denote its neighborhood; here
E(\hat{\Gamma})
is the set of all directed edges of
\hat{\Gamma}
. Note that
N_{z}\neq\varnothing
if the underlying normed space is at least two-dimensional.

Lemma 2.6
.

A normed space
\mathscr{X}
with
\dim\mathscr{X}\geq 2
over
\mathbb{F}
is strictly convex if and only if the function

\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})\to 2^{\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})}
which maps a vertex
z
to its neighborhood
N_{z}
is injective.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathscr{X}
is not strictly convex and let
{\bf L}:=\{\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y;\;\;0\leq\lambda\leq 1\}
be a nondegenerate line on the norm’s unit
sphere
S
. Consider distinct unit vectors
z_{1},z_{2}
from the relative interior of
{\bf L}\subseteq S
.
Then for some
\varepsilon>0
we have

\|(1-\lambda)z_{1}+\lambda z_{2}\|=1;\qquad\lambda\in(-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon).

Let now
f
be an arbitrary supporting functional at
z_{1}
, that is,
{f(z_{1})=\|f\|=1}
.
Then

|1+\lambda(f(z_{2})-1)|=|f((1-\lambda)z_{1}+\lambda z_{2})|\leq\|f\|\cdot\|z_{1}+\lambda(z_{2}-z_{1})\|=1

for any
\lambda\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
, so
f(z_{2})=1
.
Hence
f
is a supporting functional for both
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
, and thus the sets of supporting functionals at
z_{1}

and at
z_{2}
coincide. This implies that the set
\{x\in\mathscr{X};\;\;z_{1}\perp x\}
coincides with the set

\{w\in\mathscr{X};\;\;z_{2}\perp w\}
because, by
[
17
]
,
z\perp x
if and only if
x
is annihilated by some
supporting functional at
z
. As such, also
N_{z_{1}}=N_{z_{2}}
.

To prove the converse we assume
\mathscr{X}
is strictly convex. We need to show that for any two distinct vertices

z_{1},z_{2}\in\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})
we have
N_{z_{1}}\neq N_{z_{2}}
.

Note first that
x\perp y
in
\mathscr{X}
if and only if
x\perp y
in
\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{x,y\}\subseteq\mathscr{X}
, since,
by the definition, BJ orthogonality of two vectors depends only on the norms of their linear combinations. We
now pass to the subspace
\mathscr{Y}=\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{z_{1},z_{2}\}
. Clearly, the norm on
\mathscr{Y}
is also strictly convex. It is
sufficient to show that
N_{z_{1}}\neq N_{z_{2}}
in
\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{Y})
.

Consider some supporting functional
f
at
z_{1}
in
\mathscr{Y}
, and let
x\in\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}f
. Hence
z_{1}\perp x
, so
x\in N_{z_{1}}
. Assume, to reach a contradiction, that
x\in N_{z_{2}}
. Then there exists a supporting functional

g
at
z_{2}
in
\mathscr{Y}
such that
x\in\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}g
.
Since
\dim\mathscr{Y}=2
and
\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}f\cap\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}g
is nontrivial, it follows that
\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}f=\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}g
which implies that
g
is a unimodular scalar multiple of
f
.
By transferring this scalar multiple to
z_{2}
we may assume that
f(z_{1})=f(z_{2})=\|f\|=\|z_{1}\|=\|z_{2}\|=1
, so it follows that

1=f(\lambda z_{1}+(1-\lambda)z_{2})\leq\|f\|\cdot\|\lambda z_{1}+(1-\lambda)z_{2}\|\leq 1

for all
\lambda\in[0,1]
. Thus the line segment
[z_{1},z_{2}]
consists of unit vectors, a contradiction to
the strict convexity. Hence
x\notin N_{z_{2}}
and
N_{z_{1}}\neq N_{z_{2}}
.
∎

3.
Main results – Isomorphism problem

How much information on the norm is encoded in the di-orthograph
\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})
? First we note that, if

\mathscr{X}
and
\mathscr{Y}
are isometrically isomorphic Banach spaces, then
\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})
and
\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{Y})

are isomorphic. More precisely, if
A\colon\mathscr{X}\to\mathscr{Y}
is a linear bijective isometry then a line
x
is BJ
orthogonal to a line
y
, relative to the norm on
\mathscr{X}
, if and only if
Ax
is BJ orthogonal to
Ay
relative to the norm on

\mathscr{Y}.
That is,
x\mapsto Ax
induces an isomorphism of di-orthographs
\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})
and
\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{Y})
. Our main result (see Theorem
3.5
below) will establish the converse of this fact.

Let us start with some preliminary definitions and results. Recall that, according to
[
6
, part 3, ch. 1, §2]
, in a real smooth normed space
{(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|)}
the unique
supporting functional
f_{x}
at a unit vector
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is given by the norm’s Gateaux directional derivative at
x
. In fact, it equals

(1)

f_{x}=\langle\cdot,\partial\|x\|\rangle

where
\langle x,y\rangle:=xy^{t}
is the standard scalar product of row vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\partial\|x\|:=\left(\frac{\partial\|\cdot\|}{\partial x_{1}},\dots,\frac{\partial\|\cdot\|}{\partial x_{n}}\right)(x)
is the norm’s differential evaluated at vector

.

A complex normed space
(\mathbb{C}^{n},\|\cdot\|)
can be regarded as a real normed space
(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\|\cdot\|)
by restricting the scalars. We write
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and consider the real and complex components of
x_{k}=\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}x_{k}+i\,\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}x_{k}
as independent real variables. The
\mathbb{R}
-linear functional
f_{x}
defined above which depends on

is the real part of a

where

is the standard scalar product of row vectors in

. This can be simplified if one introduces complex partial derivatives

\frac{\partial\|x\|}{\partial x_{k}}:=\frac{\partial{\|x\|}}{\partial\mathop{\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}}x_{k}}-i\frac{\partial{\|x\|}}{\partial\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}x_{k}}\quad\hbox{ and }\quad\frac{\partial\|x\|}{\partial\overline{x_{k}}}:=\frac{\partial{\|x\|}}{\partial\mathop{\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}}x_{k}}+i\frac{\partial{\|x\|}}{\partial\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}x_{k}};

then we can identify the

-linear supporting functional

with the norm’s complex

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial\|x\|}{\partial\overline{x_{1}}},\dots,\frac{\partial\|x\|}{\partial\overline{x_{n}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial{\|x\|}}{\partial\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}x_{1}}+i\frac{\partial{\|x\|}}{\partial\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}x_{1}},\dots,\frac{\partial{\|x\|}}{\partial\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}x_{n}}+i\frac{\partial{\|x\|}}{\partial\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}x_{n}}\right).

The words
a curve
and
a path
may have several different meanings, depending on the context and the literature. Throughout the present paper,
a curve
denotes a subset in
\mathbb{C}
, which is the image of
a path
, i.e., the image of a continuous map
{\bf r}\colon[a,b]\to\mathbb{C}
where
[a,b]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is an interval with at least two different points. We say that a function
f\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
is bounded from above on a subset
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{C}
if
\sup_{z\in\Gamma}|f(z)|<\infty
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose a nonzero ring homomorphism
\sigma\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
is bounded from above on a curve
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{C}
with more than one point. Then
\sigma
is continuous, hence either identity or a complex conjugation.

Proof.

We will rely freely on the fact that if an additive and multiplicative map
\sigma\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
is bounded from above on a set
\Gamma
, then it remains bounded from above on a set
\Gamma_{1}
obtained from
\Gamma
by applying any of the following four procedures finitely many times:
(i) taking a subset, i.e.,
\Gamma_{1}\subseteq\Gamma
, (ii) translating by
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
, i.e.,
\Gamma_{1}=\mu+\Gamma
, (iii) rotating/expanding, i.e.,
\Gamma_{1}=\mu\Gamma
, and (iv) taking the union of finitely many sets obtained in previous iterations.

Let
{\bf r}\colon[0,1]\to\Gamma
be the corresponding non-constant path. By restricting and parameterizing the domain we can assume that
{\bf r}(0)\neq{\bf r}(1)
. By translating and rotating/expanding the resulting curve we can further assume that
{\bf r}(0)=0
and
{\bf r}(1)=1
.

If the curve
\Gamma
contains a line segment then we can further translate and rotate/expand it to assume that this line segment equals the interval
[0,1]
. Then
\sigma
is bounded on the closed unit disk because if
a,b\in[0,1]
, then
\sigma(a+ib)=\sigma(a)+\sigma(i)\sigma(b)
. As such,
\sigma
is continuous (see, e.g.,
[
2
, Corollary 5, p. 15]
) and the result follows.

Assume
\Gamma
contains no line segment. Being compact, it is contained in the smallest solid rectangle
Q
whose sides are parallel to real/imaginary axis. Now, the union
\Gamma\cup(1+\Gamma)
is still a curve because the end point of a path
{\bf r}
in
\Gamma
coincides with the initial point of a translated path
t\mapsto 1+{\bf r}(t)
of
(1+\Gamma)
. By induction we see that the finite union

\bigcup_{k=0}^{n-1}(k+\Gamma)
which, by the abuse of notation we again denote by
\Gamma
, is still a curve which connects
0
to
n
; here an integer
n
is chosen to be bigger than four-times the height of a rectangle
Q
. By another abuse of notation we denote the union of translated solid rectangles
\bigcup_{k=0}^{n-1}(k+Q)
again by
Q
.
We now rotate
\Gamma
by
\pi/2
, i.e., we consider
i\Gamma
, and translate
\Gamma
as well as its rotated version. The resulting union (with negative sign in the exponent denoting that the parametrization of
\Gamma
must be reversed),

\hat{\Gamma}:=\Gamma^{-}\cup(i\Gamma)\cup(ni+\Gamma)\cup(n+i\Gamma^{-})

is a closed curve, which connects in clock-wise direction
0
to
ni
, to
n(i+1)
to
n
and back to
0
. Also, this closed curve is contained in the union of four solid rectangles

\hat{Q}:=Q\cup(iQ)\cup(ni+Q)\cup(n+iQ)

each obtained by rotating
Q
by
\pi/2
and/or translating it. Since
n
is at least four times the height of
Q
, we see that the above union properly encircles, but does not contain a point
\alpha:=\frac{n}{2}(1+i)
.

\Gamma
(with dashed lines) and its translates/rotations (with dotted lines) form a closed curve
\hat{\Gamma}
sitting inside the union
\hat{Q}
of four solid rectangles (with border lines drawn).

Notice that a clock-wise oriented rectangle
\hat{R}:=[0,n]\cup i[0,n]\cup(ni+[0,n])\cup(n+i[0,n])\subseteq\hat{Q}
, i.e., the closed polygonal curve which connects

is a deformation of
\hat{Q}
within
\hat{Q}
(that is, the identity map on
\hat{Q}
is homotopic, within
\hat{Q}
, to a map from
\hat{Q}
onto
\hat{R}
). This deformation then also induces a deformation of
\hat{\Gamma}
onto
\hat{R}
within
\hat{Q}
which never passes through
\alpha:=\frac{n}{2}(1+i)
nor through
\infty
. Since
\hat{R}
separates
\mathbb{C}
by the Jordan Curve Theorem, the same holds for
\hat{\Gamma}
by
[
13
, XVII, 4.3]
, and in fact
\alpha
is in a bounded component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\hat{\Gamma}
.

Since
\sigma
is additive, it is clearly bounded from above also on the subset
\hat{\Gamma}-\hat{\Gamma}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
. We claim that this subset has a nonempty interior, so it must also contain a line segment, and hence, as shown above,
\sigma
is continuous. To see that
\hat{\Gamma}-\hat{\Gamma}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
contains interior points, we borrow the idea from a paper by Simon and Taylor
[
28
]
.

Let
C_{\infty}
be the unbounded component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\hat{\Gamma}
and let
C_{0}:=\mathbb{C}\setminus(C_{\infty}\cup\hat{\Gamma})
be the union of its bounded components. Notice that
C_{\infty}
is an open set whose boundary is contained in
\hat{\Gamma}
(see, e.g.,
[
13
, XVII, 1.2]
),
so that
(C_{\infty}\cup\hat{\Gamma})=\overline{C_{\infty}}\cup\hat{\Gamma}
is closed and as such its complement,
C_{0}
is open.

Let

\Omega:=\{x\in\mathbb{C};\;\;\exists\,a_{1},a_{2}\in\hat{\Gamma}:a_{1}\in x+C_{0}\,\hbox{ and }\,a_{2}\in x+C_{\infty}\}.

This is clearly an open subset. It is also nonempty: in fact, let
\alpha\in C_{0}
be a point in the bounded component. Consider the two horizontal rays emanating from
\alpha
, that is,
L^{-}:=(-\infty,0]+\alpha
and
L^{+}:=\alpha+[0,\infty)
. Since
\hat{\Gamma}
separates
\mathbb{C}
, both rays must intersect it. Let
a_{1}\in\hat{\Gamma}\cap L^{-}
be the closest point to
\alpha
on the left ray which also belongs to a compact closed curve
\hat{\Gamma}
and let
a_{2}\in\hat{\Gamma}\cap L^{+}
be the furthest point from
\alpha
on the right ray which still belongs to
\hat{\Gamma}
. By moving
\hat{\Gamma}
slightly to the left, i.e., by adding small enough real
x<0
to
\hat{\Gamma}
we achieve that
a_{1}\in\hat{\Gamma}
belongs to the bounded component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus(x+\hat{\Gamma})
, that is,
a_{1}\in x+C_{0}
while
a_{2}\in\hat{\Gamma}
belongs to its unbounded component, that is,
a_{2}\in x+C_{\infty}
. Indeed,
\Omega
is nonempty.

Assume there is
x\in\Omega
such that
x\notin\hat{\Gamma}-\hat{\Gamma}
. Then
(x+\hat{\Gamma})\cap\hat{\Gamma}=\varnothing
. It follows that a connected set
-x+\hat{\Gamma}
is completely contained in the union of disjoint open subsets
C_{\infty}\cup C_{0}
(namely,
-x+\hat{\Gamma}\subseteq\mathbb{C}=C_{0}\sqcup\hat{\Gamma}\sqcup C_{\infty}
, the disjoint union), so
\hat{\Gamma}\subseteq(x+C_{0})\sqcup(x+C_{\infty})
. Also, by definition of
\Omega
, both open sets
(x+C_{0})
and
(x+C_{\infty})
contain a portion of
\hat{\Gamma}
, so they partition a connected set
\hat{\Gamma}
, a contradiction. Indeed,
\Omega
is an open subset contained in
\hat{\Gamma}-\hat{\Gamma}
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

The proof of the lemma relies on the fact that if a closed curve
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{C}
separates the complex plane, then
\Gamma-\Gamma
has nonempty interior.
With the help of dimension theory for separable metric spaces (see a book by Hurewitz and Wallman
[
16
]
) much more can be said. In fact, Shchepin
[
27
]
showed the following. If
\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{n}
are
n\geq 1
compact connected subsets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that (i)
0\in\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma_{i}
and (ii) there exist
n
linearly independent points
a_{i}\in\Gamma_{i}
, then the sum
\sum\Gamma_{i}:=\{x_{1}+\dots+x_{n};\;\;x_{i}\in\Gamma_{i}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is of dimension at least
n
and therefore it contains an open ball (see
[
16
, Theorem IV.3]
).

Note that Shchepin’s result no longer holds if we drop the condition that
\Gamma_{1},\cdots,\Gamma_{n}
contain linearly independent points. Say, if
\Gamma_{1}=\Gamma_{2}=[0,1]
(this can be made into a closed curve by going from
0
to
1
and then back), then
\Gamma_{1}+\Gamma_{2}=[0,2]\subseteq\mathbb{C}
is of dimension
1
.

Our next lemma is known in the case of real spaces, see Taylor
[
33
, Theorem 2]
. Since we will use its variant for complex, two-dimensional spaces we present the short proof,
valid for real and complex normed spaces, for the sake of convenience. We remark that Taylor used a similar
approach (see
[
33
, Theorem 1]
).

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
n\geq 2
and let
{\mathscr{X}}
be an
n
-dimensional normed space over
{\mathbb{F}}
. Then there exist
at least
n
unit vectors which are pairwise BJ orthogonal.

Proof.

We may assume that
{\mathscr{X}}={\mathbb{F}}^{n}
.
Let
{\bf S}\subseteq{\mathbb{F}}^{n}
denote the norm’s unit sphere. The continuous real-valued function

f\colon(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\mapsto|\det(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})|

attains its maximum on a compact set
\prod_{1}^{n}{\bf S}
, say at a point with coordinates

y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\in{\bf S}
. Clearly,
f(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})>0
because
{\bf S}
contains
n
linearly independent vectors. We now show that
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
are pairwise
mutually BJ orthogonal.

Assume the contrary. Let
k\neq j
be such that
y_{k}\not\perp y_{j}
, that is,

\|y_{k}+\lambda y_{j}\|<\|y_{k}\|=1

for some
\lambda\in{\mathbb{F}}
. By their linear independence we have
y_{k}+\lambda y_{j}\neq 0
, so

0<\|y_{k}+\lambda y_{j}\|<1.

Consider now
y_{k}^{\prime}=\frac{y_{k}+\lambda y_{j}}{\|y_{k}+\lambda y_{j}\|}\in{\bf S}
. Then

\!\!f(y_{1},\dots,y_{k-1},y_{k}^{\prime},y_{k+1},\dots,y_{n})=\frac{|\det(y_{1},\dots,y_{k-1},y_{k}+\lambda y_{j},y_{k+1},\dots,y_{n})|}{\|y_{k}+\lambda y_{j}\|}\\
=\frac{|\det(y_{1},\dots,y_{k},\dots,y_{n})|}{{\|y_{k}+\lambda y_{j}\|}}=\frac{1}{{\|y_{k}+\lambda y_{j}\|}}f(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})>f(y_{1},\dots,y_{n}),

a contradiction.
∎

It was shown by Rätz
[
25
]
(see also Sundaresan
[
30
, Lemma 1]
) that BJ orthogonality in real normed spaces is Thalesian, that is, if
x,y
are BJ orthogonal vectors in a real normed space, then for every
\lambda_{0}>0
there exists a scalar
\alpha
such that
(x+\alpha y)\perp(\lambda_{0}x-\alpha y)
. This fact was used by Wójcik
[
34
, Proof of Theorem 3.1]
to give an alternative proof that linear maps which preserve BJ orthogonality between real normed spaces are scalar multiples of isometries. The main idea of the proof of the next lemma comes from
Wójcik’s paper (see
[
34
, Proof of Theorem 3.1]
) and uses a partial extension of Thalesian property for BJ orthogonality in
complex
normed spaces:
we show that if unit vectors
x,y
are mutually BJ orthogonal, then there exists a curve
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{C}
with more than one point, such that for every
\lambda\in\Gamma
we can find
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
with
(x+\alpha y)\perp(\lambda x-\alpha y)
.

Recall that an additive map
\Phi\colon\mathscr{X}\to\mathscr{Y}
between
\mathbb{F}
-vector spaces
\mathscr{X}
and
\mathscr{Y}
is
\sigma
-quasilinear if
\Phi(\lambda x)=\sigma(\lambda)\Phi(x)
holds
where
\sigma\colon\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}
is a field homomorphism (if
\sigma
is surjective, such maps are semilinear).

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y}
be smooth normed spaces of dimension at least two over
\mathbb{F}
, and let
\sigma\colon\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}
be a field homomorphism.
If a nonzero
\sigma
-quasilinear map
\Phi\colon\mathscr{X}\to\mathscr{Y}
preserves BJ orthogonality, then
\sigma
is identity or a complex conjugation.

Proof.

For real spaces this is clear because identity is the only nonzero field homomorphism of
\mathbb{R}
. In the sequel we assume
\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y}
are complex normed spaces, so
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
.

By Lemma
3.3

there exist two mutually BJ orthogonal unit vectors
x,y\in\mathscr{X}
such that also
\Phi(x)\neq 0
. We identify the two-dimensional normed subspace
\mathop{\mathrm{span}}_{\mathbb{C}}\{x,y\}\subseteq\mathscr{X}
with the space
(\mathbb{C}^{2},\|\cdot\|)
of row vectors in such a way that
x,y
are identified with standard basis vectors, that is,
x=(1,0)
and
y=(0,1)
. Recall that a
\mathbb{C}
-linear supporting functional at a point
(1,\alpha)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
is given by

f_{\alpha}=\left(\frac{\partial\|(1,\alpha)\|}{\partial\overline{z_{1}}}\,,\,\frac{\partial\|(1,\alpha)\|}{\partial\overline{z_{2}}}\right)^{\ast},\quad(z_{k}=x_{k}+iy_{k}).

We have

\overline{\left(\frac{\partial\|(1,\alpha)\|}{\partial\overline{z_{k}}}\right)}=\frac{\partial\overline{\|(1,\alpha)\|}}{\partial z_{k}}=\frac{\partial\|(1,\alpha)\|}{\partial z_{k}}

for
k=1,2
, so the kernel of
f_{\alpha}
contains a row vector

(2)

\left(\frac{\partial\|(1,\alpha)\|}{\partial z_{2}}\,,\,-{\frac{\partial\|(1,\alpha)\|}{\partial z_{1}}}\right).

Therefore, for every scalar
\alpha
we get
(1,\alpha)\perp\left(\frac{\partial\|(1,\alpha)\|}{\partial z_{2}}\,,\,-{\frac{\partial\|(1,\alpha)\|}{\partial z_{1}}}\right)
.

Since
x,y
are mutually BJ orthogonal, their supporting functionals equal

\left(\frac{\partial\|(1,0)\|}{\partial\overline{z_{1}}},\frac{\partial\|(1,0)\|}{\partial\overline{z_{2}}}\right)^{\ast}=\mu_{x}\cdot(1,0)^{\ast}\;\hbox{ and }\left(\frac{\partial\|(0,1)\|}{\partial\overline{z_{1}}},\frac{\partial\|(0,1)\|}{\partial\overline{z_{2}}}\right)^{\ast}=\mu_{y}\cdot(0,1)^{\ast},

for some nonzero
\mu_{x},\mu_{y}\in\mathbb{C}
, respectively. Since partial derivatives of a smooth norm are continuous (see Rockafellar
[
26
, Theorem 25.5]
), the first supporting functional equals the limit of
\left(\frac{\partial\|(1,\alpha)\|}{\partial\overline{z_{1}}},\frac{\partial\|(1,\alpha)\|}{\partial\overline{z_{2}}}\right)^{\ast}
as
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
goes to
0
. Next, positive homogeneity of the norm implies
\frac{\partial\|(1,\alpha)\|}{\partial\overline{z_{k}}}=\frac{\partial\|(1/\alpha,1)\|}{\partial\overline{z_{k}}}
(
\alpha>0
), so the second supporting functional equals the limit of
\left(\frac{\partial\|(1,\alpha)\|}{\partial\overline{z_{1}}},\frac{\partial\|(1,\alpha)\|}{\partial\overline{z_{2}}}\right)^{\ast}
as
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
goes to
\infty
.
Hence, the vector

w_{\alpha}=\frac{\frac{\partial\|(1,\alpha)\|}{\partial z_{2}}}{\frac{\partial\|(1,\alpha)\|}{\partial z_{1}}}\alpha x-\alpha y

is a well-defined function of
\alpha
around
\alpha=0
, and is clearly parallel to (
2
), so
(x+\alpha y)\perp w_{\alpha}
.
Also, its first component, i.e.,

\lambda(\alpha):=\frac{\frac{\partial\|(1,\alpha)\|}{\partial z_{2}}}{\frac{\partial\|(1,\alpha)\|}{\partial z_{1}}}\alpha,

is a continuous function which cannot be constantly zero because the numerator and the denominator are nonconstant functions and cannot vanish simultaneously.
As such, with
\alpha
restricted to a suitable closed interval
I=[0,\varepsilon]
, its range
\Gamma
is a curve with more than one element in
\mathbb{C}
.

Thus, for every
\alpha\in[0,\varepsilon]
we have
(x+\alpha y)\perp(\lambda(\alpha)x-\alpha y)
. Since we assumed that
\sigma
-quasilinear
\Phi
preserves BJ orthogonality, we get that

\Big(\Phi(x)+\sigma(\alpha)\Phi(y)\Big)\perp\Big(\sigma(\lambda(\alpha))\Phi(x)-\sigma(\alpha)\Phi(y)\Big).

From here, and as
x\perp y
imply
\Phi(x)\perp\Phi(y)
, the very definition of BJ orthogonality of
\Phi(x)
and
\Phi(y)
gives

\displaystyle\|\Phi(x)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\Phi(x)+\sigma(\alpha)\Phi(y)\|=\|\Phi(x+\alpha y)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\Phi(x+\alpha y)+\Phi(\lambda(\alpha)x-\alpha y)\|=|1+\sigma(\lambda(\alpha))|\cdot\|\Phi(x)\|

so that
|\sigma(\mu)|
is bounded from below by
1
on the set
\mu=1+\lambda(\alpha)\in 1+\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{C}
. Being multiplicative, it follows that
\sigma
is bounded from above on the reciprocal set
\frac{1}{1+\Gamma}
, which is also a curve with more than one element. The result then follows from Lemma
3.1
.
∎

The main result of this chapter was essentially proven by Blanco and Turnšek in
[

, Corollary 3.4]
(but see also recent papers by Tanaka
[

,

]
). The proof given there assumed an infinite-dimensional reflexive smooth space and also assumed the domain coincides with the range. Only minor adaptations are needed to classify isomorphisms instead of automorphisms between infinite-dimensional spaces. Additional lemmas are required to show continuity in finite-dimensional spaces. We decided to present their arguments, with all the necessary modifications, for the sake of convenience.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
(\mathscr{X},\|\cdot\|_{\mathscr{X}})
and
(\mathscr{Y},\|\cdot\|_{\mathscr{Y}})
be smooth Banach spaces over
\mathbb{F}
with
\mathscr{X}
reflexive and
3\leq\dim\mathscr{X}\leq\infty
. If
T\colon\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})\to\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{Y})
is a di-orthograph isomorphism, then
\dim\mathscr{Y}=\dim\mathscr{X}
and
there exists a linear or conjugate-linear surjective map
U\colon\mathscr{X}\to\mathscr{Y}
such that

\|Ux\|_{\mathscr{Y}}=\|x\|_{\mathscr{X}}.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.2
,

\dim\mathscr{X}<\infty
if and only if
\dim\mathscr{Y}<\infty
. It then follows from Lemma
2.3
and Remark
2.4
that
\dim\mathscr{X}=\dim\mathscr{Y}
.

Define a map
S\colon\mathbb{P}\mathscr{X}^{\ast}\to\mathbb{P}\mathscr{Y}^{\ast}
between projectivizations of dual spaces of
\mathscr{X}
and
\mathscr{Y}
by
[f]\mapsto[f_{T[x]}]
. Here,
x
is (any) unit vector where
f
achieves its norm (it exists because of reflexivity of
\mathscr{X}
)
and
f_{T[x]}
is a unique supporting functional at
some nonzero vector in a line
T[x]\in\mathbb{P}\mathscr{Y}
. Note that
S
is well-defined because if
f
achieves its norm on unit vectors
x,y\in\mathscr{X}
, then
f
is a unique supporting functional for
[x]
and
[y]
, giving that
N_{[x]}=N_{[y]}=\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}f
(notations copied from Lemma
2.6
).
Therefore, also
N_{T[x]}=N_{T[y]}
and since
\mathscr{Y}
is smooth, the supporting functionals at
T[x],T[y]\in\mathbb{P}\mathscr{Y}
coincide.

The last argument shows at once that
S
is not only well defined but also injective. Also, the surjectivity of
T
implies that the range of
S
consists of all lines spanned by norm-attaining functionals.
It is easily seen that, given
f\in\mathscr{X}^{*}
and
z\in\mathscr{X}
, we have
f(z)=0
if and only if,
for every functional
g\in S[f]
and vector
y\in T[z]
, we have
g(y)=0
.

We claim that
T
is a morphism of projective spaces. Namely, assume otherwise that there exist the lines
[x],[y],[z]\in\hat{\Gamma}
which satisfy

(3)

[z]\subseteq[x]+[y]

but their images
T[x],T[y],T[z]
span a three-dimensional space. Then we can choose a norm-attaining unit functional
g\colon\mathscr{Y}\to\mathbb{F}
which annihilates
T[x]
and
T[y]
but not
T[z]
.
Clearly, the line spanned by such
g
belongs to the range of
S
, so
[g]=S[f]
, and
f
annihilates
[x]
and
[y]
but not the line
[z]
contradicting (
3
).

Indeed,
T
is a bijective morphism of projective spaces. By the (nonsurjective version of) Fundamental Theorem of Projective Geometry (see Faure
[
14
]
),
there
exist
a field isomorphism
\sigma\colon\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}
and a
\sigma
-quasilinear map
V\colon\mathscr{X}\to\mathscr{Y}
such that
T[x]=[Vx]
for
x\in\mathscr{X}\setminus\{0\}
. Clearly,
V
must be orthogonality preserving, so, by Lemma

3.4
,
\sigma
is either identity or a complex conjugation.
It follows by the main result of
[
9
]
(but see also
[
34
]
) that the (conjugate) linear orthogonality preserving map
V\colon(\mathscr{X},\|\cdot\|_{\mathscr{X}})\to(\mathscr{Y},\|\cdot\|_{\mathscr{Y}})
is a scalar multiple of an isometry (i.e., there exists a scalar
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
such that
U:=\mu V
satisfies
\|Ux\|_{\mathscr{Y}}=\|x\|_{\mathscr{X}}
).
∎

Remark 3.6
.

If
\mathscr{Y}
is reflexive, then in the proof above the map
S
is surjective and one could use Molnár’s result,
[
24
, Corollary 1]
, to show that
T
is induced by a
\sigma
-quasilinear bijection.

Remark 3.7
.

If
\mathscr{X}
is finite-dimensional and smooth, then in the theorem above the smoothness of
\mathscr{Y}
need not be assumed in advance; it follows by Lemma
2.5
(after Lemma
2.3
and Remark
2.4
establish that
\dim\mathscr{X}=\dim\mathscr{Y}
).

4.
Concluding examples and remarks

In Banach spaces of dimension two the graphs related to two norms can be isomorphic, even if
the two norms are not isomorphic (i.e., Theorem
3.5
may fail).

Example 4.1
.

Consider an odd integer
p\geq 3
and let
\|\cdot\|_{p}

be the
p
-norm on the two-dimensional real sequence
space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. This is clearly a smooth norm with the differential

\partial\|(x,y)\|_{p}=\frac{(|x|^{p-1}{\mathrm{sign}}\,x,|y|^{p-1}{\mathrm{sign}}\,y)}{(|x|^{p}+|y|^{p})^{1-1/p}},\quad(x,y)\neq(0,0).

For every
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2},
the functional
(x,y)\mapsto\langle(x,y),\partial\|(x_{0},y_{0})\|_{p}\rangle

has a kernel spanned by
(|y_{0}|^{p-1}{\mathrm{sign}}\,y_{0},-|x_{0}|^{p-1}{\mathrm{sign}}\,x_{0}).
By
(
1
) it follows that a unique line which is BJ orthogonal to a line
\mathbb{R}(x,y)
equals

\mathbb{R}(|y|^{p-1}{\mathrm{sign}}\,y,-|x|^{p-1}{\mathrm{sign}}\,x).

Since
p
is odd, the function
t\mapsto|t|^{p-1}{\mathrm{sign}}\,t
is bijective on
\mathbb{R}
. Hence, for a given line

{\bf x}=\mathbb{R}(x,y)
there exists a unique line
{\bf a}=\mathbb{R}(a,b)
with
{\bf a}\perp{\bf x}
, it is given by
a unique solution of the two equations
|a|^{p-1}{\mathrm{sign}}\,a=-y
and
|b|^{p-1}{\mathrm{sign}}\,b=x
. Thus, the di-orthograph
\hat{\Gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\|\cdot\|_{p})
consists of copies of a bilateral infinite directed path connecting (not
necessarily disjoint) vertices

\cdots\rightarrow v_{-1}\rightarrow v_{0}\rightarrow v_{1}\rightarrow\cdots.

If
v_{0}=\mathbb{R}(x,y)
then for
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have
v_{k}=\mathbb{R}f_{p}^{k}(x,y)
, where
f_{p}^{k}=f_{p}\circ\dots\circ f_{p}
is a
k
-fold compose of

f_{p}\colon(x,y)\mapsto(|y|^{p-1}{\mathrm{sign}}\,y,-|x|^{p-1}{\mathrm{sign}}\,x).

One computes that

f_{p}^{k}(x,y)=\begin{cases}(|x|^{(p-1)^{k}}{\mathrm{sign}}\,x,\ |y|^{(p-1)^{k}}{\mathrm{sign}}\,y),&k\equiv 0\pmod{4};\cr(|y|^{(p-1)^{k}}{\mathrm{sign}}\,y,\ -|x|^{(p-1)^{k}}{\mathrm{sign}}\,x),&k\equiv 1\pmod{4};\cr(-|x|^{(p-1)^{k}}{\mathrm{sign}}\,x,\ -|y|^{(p-1)^{k}}{\mathrm{sign}}\,y),&k\equiv 2\pmod{4};\cr(-|y|^{(p-1)^{k}}{\mathrm{sign}}\,y,\ |x|^{(p-1)^{k}}{\mathrm{sign}}\,x),&k\equiv 3\pmod{4}.\cr\end{cases}

Assume that
v_{0}=v_{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
in this path.
Notice that
\mathbb{R}(x,y)=\mathbb{R}f_{p}^{k}(x,y)
if and only if the determinant of a matrix with rows
(x,y)
and

f_{p}^{k}(x,y)
vanishes. By using the fact that
p-1
is even, so
|x|^{(p-1)^{k}}=x^{(p-1)^{k}}
, this reduces,
for
k
even into
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}(1,0)
or
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}(0,1)
or
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}(1,1)
or
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}(1,-1)
. For

k
odd it reduces into
|x|^{s}+|y|^{s}=0
for a suitable integer
s
in which case there are no solutions.

Hence, the di-orthograph
\hat{\Gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\|\cdot\|_{p})
consists of copies of bilateral infinite directed paths
with pairwise disjoint vertices and exactly two disjoint undirected edges
\mathbb{R}(1,0)\leftrightarrow\mathbb{R}(0,1)

and
\mathbb{R}(1,1)\leftrightarrow\mathbb{R}(1,-1)
(if the graph contains an edge together with its opposite edge we call
such edge undirected).

Thus, for all odd
p
such graphs are isomorphic but clearly the two-dimensional
\|\cdot\|_{p}
-spaces are
nonisometric for different
p\in(1,\infty)
.

There is a close relation between a Banach space
\mathscr{X}
and its dual
\mathscr{X}^{\ast}
. This reflects also in di-orthographs:

Example 4.2
.

Let
(\mathscr{X},\|\cdot\|)
be a smooth and strictly convex reflexive Banach space and let

(\mathscr{X}^{\ast},\|\cdot\|^{\ast})
be its dual. The procedure which to each line
x\subseteq\mathscr{X}
associates a unique
line containing the supporting functional
f_{x}
at
x
induces an antiisomorphism between di-orthographs

\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})
and
\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X}^{\ast})
.

This follows from the observation that
x\perp y
if and only if
f_{x}(y)=0
where
f_{x}
is the (unique)
supporting functional at
x
. Notice that the point evaluation functional
F_{x}\colon\mathscr{X}^{\ast}\to\mathbb{F}
, given by

f\mapsto f(x)
, is a supporting functional at
f_{x}
. Thus,
x\perp y
implies that
F_{y}(f_{x})=f_{x}(y)=0
giving

f_{y}\perp f_{x}
. The converse implication follows by reflexivity and the fact the dual norm
\|\cdot\|^{\ast}

is also smooth because
\mathscr{X}
is strictly convex.

Remark 4.3
.

Clearly, in
\dim\mathscr{X}\geq 3
there are infinitely many lines in the kernel of a nonzero linear functional and
hence, given
x\in\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})
, there are infinitely many vertices
y\in\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})
with

x\rightarrow y
. The converse is also true: given
x\in\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})
, there exist
infinitely many vertices
y
with
y\rightarrow x
. Indeed, take an arbitrary
z\in\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})
which is not parallel to
x
, and let
\mathscr{Y}=\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{x,z\}
. Consider a unit functional
f:\mathscr{Y}\to\mathbb{F}
which annihilates
x
and choose a unit vector
y\in\mathscr{Y}
where
f
achieves its norm. Then
y\perp x
in
\mathscr{Y}
and, therefore, in
\mathscr{X}
, so we have
y\rightarrow x
. Clearly,
y\not\in\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{x\}
. Since
\dim\mathscr{X}\geq 3
and
z
is arbitrary, we obtain an infinite number of
y
such that
y\rightarrow x
.

In the case when
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
, the proof can be obtained geometrically. One can imagine a hyper-cylindrical surface circumscribed around the norm’s unit ball in
\mathscr{X}
, whose axis is parallel to
x
. This surface has an infinite number of points of contact with the norm’s unit ball, and each point of contact
y
satisfies
y\rightarrow x
in
\hat{\Gamma}(\mathscr{X})
.

Our final example accompanies Lemma
2.6
.

Example 4.4
.

If
N_{x}=N_{y}
for some unit vectors, then
x,y
share the same supporting functional.

Namely, suppose otherwise and consider the two-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{N}:=\mathop{\mathrm{span}}_{\mathbb{F}}\{x,y\}
. By the Hahn–Banach theorem,
x,y
also do not share the same supporting functional inside a two-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{N}
. Note that there does exist a supporting functional
f_{x}\colon\mathcal{N}\to\mathbb{F}
at
x
, and hence there exists some unit
z\in\mathcal{N}
in its kernel. Clearly, then
x\perp z
, so
z\in N_{x}=N_{y}
. Therefore,
y\perp z
, and hence
z
is annihilated by some supporting functional
f_{y}\colon\mathcal{N}\to\mathbb{F}
at
y
.
Since

\mathcal{N}
is two-dimensional and
\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}f_{x}\cap\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}f_{y}
is nontrivial, it follows that
\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}f_{x}=\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}f_{y}
which implies that
f_{y}
is a unimodular scalar multiple of
f_{x}
, a contradiction.

Remark 4.5
.

Note that the converse implication does not hold. Consider, e.g.,
x=(1,1)
and
y=(1,0)
in
(\mathbb{R}^{2},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
, which share the same supporting functional but
N_{x}\neq N_{y}
.
