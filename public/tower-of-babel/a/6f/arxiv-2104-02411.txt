MPC-based Reinforcement Learning for Economic Problems with Application to Battery Storage
Arash Bahari Kordabad, Wenqi Cai, Sebastien Gros

Abstract
In this paper, we are interested in optimal control problems with purely economic costs, which often yield optimal policies having a (nearly) bang-bang structure. We focus on policy approximations based on Model Predictive Control (MPC) and the use of the deterministic policy gradient method to optimize the MPC closed-loop performance in the presence of unmodelled stochasticity or model error. When the policy has a (nearly) bang-bang structure, we observe that the policy gradient method can struggle to produce meaningful steps in the policy parameters. To tackle this issue, we propose a homotopy strategy based on the interior-point method, providing a relaxation of the policy during the learning. We investigate a specific well-known battery storage problem, and show that the proposed method delivers a homogeneous and faster learning than a classical policy gradient approach.



I
Introduction

Making decisions for the energy system in the presence of different forms of uncertainty is the object of recent publications
[
1
,
2
]
. In smart grids, the uncertainty mainly arises from the imperfect forecasts for the prices, demand, and power generation. Finding a policy minimizing the economic cost of operating the grid in the presence of these uncertainties is highly valuable
[
3
]
. Economic costs for smart grids are linear, based on the difference between the profit made by selling electricity to the power grid, and the losses incurred from buying it
[
4
]
.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) offers tools for tackling Markov Decision Processes (MDP) without having an accurate knowledge of the probability distribution underlying the state transition
[
5
,
6
]
. RL seeks to optimize the parameters underlying a given policy in view of minimizing the expected discounted sum of a given baseline stage cost
L(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}})\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}
are the system states and inputs. RL methods are usually either directly based on an approximation of the optimal policy or indirectly based on an approximation of the action-value function. Policy gradient methods directly seek to find the optimal policy parameters
[
7
,
8
]
. Different variants of Temporal Difference (TD) methods are at the core of many RL techniques for estimating the different value functions associated to the MDP. Least-Squares Temporal-Difference (LSTD) techniques are widely used because of their reliability and efficient use of data
[
9
]
.

Model Predictive Control (MPC) is a control strategy that employs a (possibly inaccurate) model of the real system dynamics to produce an input-state sequence over a given finite horizon such that the resulting predicted state trajectory minimizes a given cost function while explicitly enforcing the input-state constraints imposed on the system trajectories
[
10
]
. The problem is solved at each time instant, and only the first input of the input sequence is applied on the real system. By solving the entire problem at each time instant based on the current state of the system in a receding-horizon fashion, MPC delivers a policy for the real system.

For computational reasons, simple models are usually preferred in the MPC scheme. Hence, the MPC model often does not have the structure required to correctly capture the real system dynamics and stochasticity. As a result, MPC usually delivers a reasonable but suboptimal approximation of the optimal policy. Choosing the MPC parameters that maximises the closed-loop performance for the selected MPC formulation is a difficult problem. Indeed, e.g. selecting the MPC model parameters that best fit the model to the real system is not guaranteed to yield the best closed-loop performance that the MPC scheme can achieve
[
11
]
. In
[
12
,
11
]
, it is shown that adjusting the MPC model, cost and constraints can be beneficial to achieve the best closed-loop performances, and RL is proposed as a possible approach to perform that adjustment in practice. Further recent research have focused on MPC-based policy approximation for RL
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
12
]
.

MPC is a promising choice for the management of smart grids
[
2
]
, because it provides a simple way to exploit forecasts on the grid prices, local power demand, and production, while respecting the physical limitations of the system. The stochasticity of the forecasts uncertainty is, however, not straightforward to treat at low computational costs. In this paper, we investigate a simple, well-known battery storage problem having a purely economic cost and stochastic dynamics. This example is has an optimal policy with a nearly bang-bang structure
[
16
]
, in the sense that the optimal policy selects inputs that are either in the bounds or zero for a large subset of the state space. We show that the deterministic policy gradient method is difficult to use for this type of problem because the state trajectories mostly lie in the set where the policy is trivially zero or in the bounds, which impedes the learning.

In this paper we propose a homotopy strategy based on the interior-point method
[
17
]
, which smoothens the MPC policy via the barrier parameter associated to the method, allowing for a more homogeneous and faster learning. The policy smoothing is gradually removed over the learning to recover the optimal policy. The paper is structured as follows. Section
II
presents the battery storage dynamics and provides its optimal policy of an economic cost. Section
III
formulates the LSTD-based deterministic policy gradient method. Section
IV
details the use of MPC-scheme as a function approximator in RL. The difficulties of applying the policy gradient method for (nearly) bang-bang policies is analyzed. And the main contribution of this paper is presented. Section
V
provides the simulation results for the proposed approach and compares with the classical implementation of the policy gradient methods. Finally, section
VI
delivers the conclusions.

II
A simple motivational example

Photovoltaic (PV) battery systems allow households to participate in a more sustainable energy system
[
2
]
. The local electric demand is covered by the PV battery system, or the connection to the public distribution grid. A simple model for the battery storage reads as
[
1
]
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k}+\alpha\left(\Delta_{k}+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k}\right),

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k}\in[0,1]
is the State-of-Charge (SOC) of the battery and the interval
[0,1]
represents the SOC levels considered as non-damaging for the battery (typically 20%-80% range of the physical SOC). Constant

is a positive value that reflects the battery size. Variable

is the difference between the local power production and demand, which–for the sake of simplicity–is considered as a Normal centred random variable here, where
\bar{\delta}^{X}
and
\sigma^{X}
are the mean and variance of the Gaussian distribution. Input
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k}\in[-\bar{U},\bar{U}]
is the power bought from (for
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k}>0
) and sold to (for
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k}<0
) the power grid. The economic stage cost can be written as follows:

\displaystyle L(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k})=\left\{\begin{matrix}\phi_{b}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k}&\mathrm{if}&\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k}\geq 0\\
\phi_{s}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k}&\mathrm{if}&\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k}<0\end{matrix}\right.,

(2)

where
\phi_{b}\geq 0
is the buying price and
\phi_{s}\geq 0
is the selling price, and we assume that
\phi_{b}\geq\phi_{s}
. For the sake of simplicity, we consider the prices
\phi_{b}
and
\phi_{s}
as constants. Appendix
A
provides the model parameters we use in this paper. More complex models will be considered in the future.

In the deterministic policy gradient context, the optimal policy can be defined as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}^{\star}=\mathrm{arg}\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}\tilde{L}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k}\right)\Bigg|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k})\right],

(3)

where
\gamma\in(0,1]
is the discount factor, and for the battery storage dynamics (
1
) with stage cost (
2
), the modified stage cost
\tilde{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k})
is defined as follows
[
8
]
:

\displaystyle\tilde{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k})=L(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k})

\displaystyle+p\max(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k}-1,0)

\displaystyle+p\max(-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k},0),

(4)

where
p
is a large constant. The expected value
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}}
is taken over the Markov Chain distribution resulting from the real system in closed-loop with policy
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}
. Since the state
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k}
ought to stay in the interval
\left[0,1\right]
, a large penalty is introduced in the RL stage cost for
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k}\notin\left[0,1\right]
.

The example is selected such that its optimal policy
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}^{\star}
can be solved via Dynamic Programming (DP), see fig.
1
, and used as a baseline to assess the policies learned via RL. As can be seen in fig.
1
, the optimal policy has a bang-bang-like structure. When the battery is at
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}\approx 0
, maximum buying is the optimal policy. Then for a fairly large subset of the states (
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}\approx[0.05,0.5]
), no exchange with the grid is the optimal policy. For a high SOC (
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}\approx[0.55,1]
), maximum selling is optimum.

Fig. 1:
Optimal policy
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}^{\star}
resulted from DP.

Note that the computational complexity makes DP unrealistic for systems more complex than this example. Instead, most investigations in RL (e.g., policy gradient methods) focus on achieving approximate solutions, which do not require a model of the dynamics. Next section details the RL algorithm that obtains an optimal policy based on the observed data from the (stochastic) real system.

III
Deterministic policy gradient method

In the context of the deterministic policy gradient method
[
8
]
, the policy
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}
is parameterized by parameters
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}
, which are optimized directly according to the closed-loop performance using the gradient of the performance
J
defined as:

\displaystyle J(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}})=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}\tilde{L}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k}\right)\Bigg|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k})\right].

(5)

The gradient of
J
with respect to parameters
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}
is obtained as follows:

\displaystyle{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}}J({\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}})={\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}}\left[{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}}{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}){\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}}}{A_{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}})\big|_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}}\right],

(6)

where
A_{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}})=Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}})-V_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}})
is the advantage function associated to
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}
, and where
Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}}
and
V_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}}
are the action-value and value functions for the policy
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}
, respectively. Under some conditions detailed in
[
8
]
, the action-value function
Q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}}}
in (
6
) can be replaced by an approximation
{Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{w}}}}
without affecting the policy gradient. Such an approximation is labelled
compatible
and can, e.g., take the form:

\displaystyle{Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{w}}}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right)={\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}-{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}\right)\right)^{\top}}{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}}{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}}{\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}\right)^{\top}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{w}}+{V_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}\right),

(7)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{w}}
is a parameters vector estimating the action-value function and
V_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}}\approx V_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}}
is a baseline function approximating the value function, which can, e.g., take a linear form:

\displaystyle{V_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}\right)=\Phi\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}\right)^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}},

(8)

where
\Phi
is a state feature vector and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}
is the corresponding parameters vector. The parameters
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{w}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}
of the action-value function approximation (
7
) ought to be the solution of the Least Squares problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{w}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}})-Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{w}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}})\right)^{2}\right].

(9)

In this paper, problem (
9
) is tackled via Least Squares Temporal Difference (LSTD)
[
9
]
.

Next section details using an MPC scheme to approximate the optimal policy and proposes a smoothing approach based on the interior-point method for the (nearly) bang-bang policies.

IV
MPC-based RL

Using MPC as a way of supporting the approximations of value function, action-value function and policy
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}
has been proposed and justified in
[
11
]
. In this paper, we focus on the approximation of the optimal policy. Consider the following MPC scheme parameterized with
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}
:

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}}\quad

\displaystyle T_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{N}\right)+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\omega}}_{\mathrm{f}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}_{N}

(10a)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\gamma^{i}(\ell_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{i}\right)+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\omega}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}_{i})

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i+1}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{i}),\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}

(10b)

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{h}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{i})\leq\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}_{i},\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{h}}^{\mathrm{f}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{N})\leq\sigma_{N}

(10c)

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{g}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{i})\leq 0,\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}_{i}\geq 0,\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}_{N}\geq 0,

(10d)

where
T_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}
and
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}
are the MPC terminal and stage costs, respectively. Function
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}
is the model dynamics,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{g}}
is the pure input constraint and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{h}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{h}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}^{\mathrm{f}}

Vectors
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}}=\left\{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{0},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{N}\right\}

where

Theoretically, under some assumptions detailed in

IV-C

Smoothing strategy for (nearly) bang-bang policies

The solution of NLP (
12
) can be seen as a function of the NLP parameters
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}
, and can be a non-differentiable or even discontinuous function of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}
when changes of active set occur. In that context, parameter
\tau
acts as a “smoothing” factor in the NLP solution, in the sense that for
\tau>0
, the parametric solution
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}_{\tau}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}\right)
obtained from solving (
16
) becomes a smooth function of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}
. For
\tau\rightarrow 0
,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}_{\tau}
tends asymptotically to the non-smooth solution of NLP (
12
), and the derivatives of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}_{\tau}
can become unbounded for some
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}
. In contrast, for
\tau
larger, all derivatives of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}_{\tau}
remain bounded, and of lower magnitudes.

When the optimal policy has a (nearly) bang-bang structure–such as in the storage example investigated here–it is beneficial to adopt a policy approximation
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}
that approximates that structure well while remaining smooth, such that the policy gradient (
6
) is guaranteed to be valid. If such a policy approximation can be made arbitrarily close to the bang-bang structure, then (
6
) remains asymptotically well defined, and the approximation can approach the optimal policy.

For non-episodic problems, such as the battery storage example considered here, the expected value operator
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}}[.]
in the policy gradient (
6
) is meant to be taken over the steady-state distribution of the Markov Chain resulting from applying the policy
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}
on the real system. If the MPC policy
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}
has a purely bang-bang structure meant to approximate
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}^{\star}
, for
\tau\rightarrow 0
, the interior-point policy approximation is asymptotically bang-bang. Then, the gradient of the policy
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}
, while remaining well-defined everywhere, tends to be nearly zero on large parts of the state space, and take very large (asymptotically infinite) values when the policy switches between the different input levels. Hence, while the policy gradient (
6
) remains formally correct, evaluating it via sampling the distribution of the Markov Chain becomes very difficult, because the set of states where
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}\approx 0
has a measure close to unity, while
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}
is very large on a set of very small measure. As a result, sample-based estimations of (
6
) have a very large variance, which impedes the learning.

For nearly bang-bang policy structures, the difficulties can be less severe than for purely bang-bang structure but they remain an issue. That issue can be observed for the battery storage problem considered in this paper. Figure

for a given
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}
during a fairly long closed-loop trajectory for different values of
\tau

are the MPC parameters that will be introduced in detail in the simulation section. When using

In this paper, we exploit the smoothing effect of the barrier parameter
\tau
to facilitate the use of the policy gradient method on MDPs that are difficult to treat because of the bang-bang-like structure of their optimal policy. More specifically, we propose to set the barrier parameter
\tau

\tau

\bar{\tau}
of the interior-point method, i.e.

\displaystyle\tau\leftarrow\max(\tau-\beta,\bar{\tau})

(19)

where
\beta>0
is the progression step for
\tau

\bar{\tau}
the final barrier parameter targeted for the interior point method. The starting
\tau
and target
\bar{\tau}
are problem dependent. Alternative progression rules to (
19

VI
CONCLUSION

In this paper, we discuss the use of the policy gradient method on policies having (nearly) bang-bang structures supported via MPC schemes. We detail why this kind of policy structure is difficult to treat in the deterministic policy gradient context, and propose a simple approach to alleviate the problem. A homotopy strategy is used to adapt the barrier parameter in the interior-point method that is used to solve the MPC scheme online. The proposed smoothing approach is illustrated on a classic battery storage problem with an economic stage cost. We show that a classical implementation of the policy gradient method results in a slow convergence, occurring through sudden progressions, while the proposed method offers a more homogeneous and faster convergence, resulting in a better closed-loop performance throughout the learning process. In future work, we will consider more sophisticated techniques to adapt the barrier parameter, analysize the convergence more formally, and tackle challenging economic problems with complex models.
