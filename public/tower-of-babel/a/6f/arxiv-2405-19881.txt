(Non)-hyperuniformity of perturbed lattices
David Dereudre, Daniela Flimmel, Martin Huesmann, Thomas Leblé

Abstract
We ask whether a stationary lattice in dimension 𝖽 \mathsf{d} whose points are shifted by identically distributed but possibly dependent perturbations remains hyperuniform. When 𝖽 = 1 \mathsf{d}=1 or 2 2 , we show that it is the case when the perturbations have a finite 𝖽 \mathsf{d} -moment, and that this condition is sharp. When 𝖽 ≥ 3 \mathsf{d}\geq 3 , we construct arbitrarily small perturbations such that the resulting point process is not hyperuniform. As a side remark of independent interest, we exhibit hyperuniform processes with arbitrarily slow decay of their number variance.



1
Introduction

1.1
Setting and main results

Let
\mathsf{d}\geq 1
be the ambient dimension. A
point configuration
is a locally finite collection of points in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
, possibly with multiplicities, which we identify with a purely atomic Radon measure
\mathbf{X}
(see Section
2.3
for technical details). For
r>0
, we let
\mathrm{B}_{r}
be the closed Euclidean ball of radius
r
centered at the origin and if
\mathbf{X}
is a point configuration we denote by
|\mathbf{X}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|
the number of its points in
\mathrm{B}_{r}
. The following definition has received a lot of interest in the past twenty years:

Let
\mathbf{X}
be a
random
point configuration (i.e. a
point process
). We say that
\mathbf{X}
is
hyperuniform
when:

{\sigma(r):=\frac{\mathrm{Var}\left[|\mathbf{X}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|\right]}{|\mathrm{B}_{r}|}\longrightarrow 0}\text{ as }r\to\infty,

(1.1)

where
|\mathrm{B}_{r}|
denotes the volume of a ball of radius
r
. The quantity
\mathrm{Var}[|\mathbf{X}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|]
is called the
number variance
(of the random point configuration
\mathbf{X}
, in
\mathrm{B}_{r}
) and
\sigma
the rescaled number variance.
Of course, the volume
|\mathrm{B}_{r}|
is proportional to
r^{\mathsf{d}}
so when (
1.1
) holds one can wonder how slow the number variance growth actually is compared to
r^{\mathsf{d}}
. In the terminology of
[
24
]
,
\mathbf{X}
is said to be
hyperuniform of class I
when in fact:

\mathrm{Var}\left[|\mathbf{X}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|\right]=\mathcal{O}(r^{\mathsf{d}-1})\text{ as }r\to\infty.

(1.2)

The basic example of a class I-hyperuniform point process is the “stationary lattice”
\mathbf{L}:=\{x+U,\ x\in\mathcal{L}\}
, where
\mathcal{L}
is some lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
(i.e.
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}
or the image of
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}
by an affine transformation), and the random variable
U
is uniformly distributed in a fundamental domain of
\mathcal{L}
. Since (
1.2
) is the slowest possible growth (cf.
[
1
, Thm. 2A]
), stationary lattices are “as hyperuniform as it gets”.

In this paper, we consider
perturbed lattices
of the form
\mathbf{PL}:=\{x+U+\mathrm{p}_{x},\ x\in\mathcal{L}\}
where
U
is as above while
(\mathrm{p}_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{L}}
is a family of random variables independent of
U
, thought of as “perturbations”, with values in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
, and we discuss whether
\mathbf{PL}
remains hyperuniform or not.

When the perturbations are independent and identically distributed, the answer is well-known:
in any dimension
, if
\mathrm{p}_{0}
has a
finite first moment
, then
\mathbf{PL}
remains
hyperuniform of class I
, see
[
11
]
. In fact, even if
\mathrm{p}_{0}
has only a finite moment of order
0<s<1
, then
\mathbf{PL}
remains hyperuniform (but not necessarily of class I).

For dependent perturbations, with (or without) information about the structure of dependence (as discussed e.g. in
[
10
,
7
,
14
]
), the question was mainly open.

Throughout this paper, we make the following assumption: the perturbations
(\mathrm{p}_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{L}}
are identically distributed, and moreover their joint distribution is
\mathcal{L}
-invariant in the sense that for all
x_{\star}
in
\mathcal{L}
,
(\mathrm{p}_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{L}}
and
(\mathrm{p}_{x+x_{\star}})_{x\in\mathcal{L}}
have the same distribution. It implies that the perturbed lattice
\mathbf{PL}
itself is stationary.

We may now state our main results. Let us emphasize that
we allow the perturbations to be dependent
.

Theorem 1

((Non)-hyperuniformity of perturbed lattices)
.

•

For all
\mathsf{d}\geq 1
, if
\mathbb{E}[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|^{\mathsf{d}}]
is finite, then the number variance of
\mathbf{PL}
in
\mathrm{B}_{r}
is finite for every
r>0
.

•

For
\mathsf{d}=1
or
2
, if
\mathbb{E}[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|^{\mathsf{d}}]
is finite, then
\mathbf{PL}
is hyperuniform in the sense of (
1.1
).

•

For all
\mathsf{d}\geq 1
, taking
\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}
, if
X
is a positive random variable such that
\mathbb{E}[X^{\mathsf{d}}]=+\infty
, then there exist perturbations
(\mathrm{p}_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}}
, with
|\mathrm{p}_{0}|\leq X
almost surely, such that the resulting perturbed lattice has infinite number variance in finite balls.

•

For all
\mathsf{d}\geq 3
, taking
\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}
, for all
\varepsilon>0
there exist perturbations
(\mathrm{p}_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}}
with
|\mathrm{p}_{0}|\leq\varepsilon
almost surely, such that the resulting perturbed lattice satisfies
\lim_{r\to+\infty}\frac{\mathrm{Var}\left[|\mathbf{PL}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|\right]}{|\mathrm{B}_{r}|}=+\infty
.

The third item shows that in dimension
1
and
2
the sufficient condition of the second item allowing to retain hyperuniformity is particularly sharp, because anything below that threshold can give rise to processes which not only are
not hyperuniform
, but have in fact
infinite number variance in finite balls
. In higher dimensions (
d\geq 3
) the last item shows that arbitrarily small perturbations can break hyperuniformity.

Theorem 2

(About class-I hyperuniformity)
.

•

For
\mathsf{d}=1
, if
\mathbb{E}[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|^{2}]
is finite, then
\mathbf{PL}
is class I hyperuniform (which, in dimension
1
, means that the number variance stays bounded as the size of the interval goes to
+\infty
). This condition is sharp.

•

For
\mathsf{d}=2
, there are perturbations of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
which are almost surely bounded by an arbitrarily small constant, and for which the perturbed lattice is not class I hyperuniform.

We thus obtain with the first item a sharp sufficient condition for class-I hyperuniformity of perturbed lattices in dimension
1
based only on the size of the perturbations. The sharpness means that we can construct a perturbation such that
\mathbb{E}[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|^{2}]=+\infty
and
\mathbf{PL}
is not class I hyperuniform, see Remark
4.3
. In dimension
2
, the second item indicates that any sufficient condition should presumably involve the correlation structure of the perturbations, see Section
1.5
.

Theorem 3

(Arbitrarily slow decay of the number variance)
.

Let
\tilde{\sigma}:(0,+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)
be a non-increasing positive function with
\lim_{r\to+\infty}\tilde{\sigma}(r)=0
. For all
\mathsf{d}\geq 1
, there exist
c>0
and a perturbed lattice
\mathbf{PL}
such that:

1.

\mathbf{PL}
is hyperuniform.

2.

The rescaled number variance
\sigma
of
\mathbf{PL}
(see (
1.1
)) satisfies:

\sigma(r)\geq c\tilde{\sigma}(r)\text{ for all }r\geq 1.

(1.3)

We emphasize that
\tilde{\sigma}
can really be chosen tending to
0
as slow as one wants.

1.2
About hyperuniformity

General facts

In the statistical physics literature of the 1980’s (see e.g.
[
19
,
18
]
) it has been observed that some classical systems of particles with long-range interactions exhibit
cancellation of charge fluctuations
, namely that the variance of the number of charges in a domain increases proportionally to the surface area of the domain’s boundary, and not to the volume. The notion of
hyperuniformity
, or equivalently of
super-homogeneous
distributions of points was introduced in
[
9
,
23
]
, with applications in theoretical chemistry and physics. We refer to the survey by S. Torquato
[
24
]
for an overview of the considerable literature that has since been devoted to this topic.

One motivation to study hyperuniform processes is that they can exhibit a form of “order within disorder”: the fact that their rescaled number variance goes to
0
can be interpreted as a form of collective order yet their truncated correlation functions may all decay very fast at infinity hence there is no “long-range order” in the usual sense of statistical physics. It is worth mentioning that since the “number of points in big boxes” is a very natural observable to study, some “hyperuniformity” results can predate the coining of the term itself by decades.

Amusingly, the original definition for hyperuniformity was more stringent than (
1.1
) and corresponds to what is now called “class-I hyperuniformity” (see (
1.2
)), as it has been realized that many interesting processes satisfy (
1.1
) but not (
1.2
). The defining condition (
1.1
) should be understood by comparison to the fundamental example of a random point configuration given by the Poisson point process. Recall that for a Poisson point process
\mathbf{X}
(with constant intensity
c
), the number of points
|\mathbf{X}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|
follows a Poisson distribution of parameter
c|\mathrm{B}_{r}|
for all
r>0
, and as a straightforward consequence the number variance is precisely proportional to the volume
|\mathrm{B}_{r}|
. For a random point configuration
\mathbf{X}
to be hyperuniform thus means that its number variance (in large balls) is asymptotically “much smaller than for a Poisson point process”.

Hyperuniformity and structure factor

Assume that the (stationary) point process
\mathbf{X}
has intensity
1
(i.e. one point per unit volume on average) and is nice enough so that the following quantity:

g_{2}(x):=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{|\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}}\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{X}\text{ has at least one point in }\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(0)\text{ and in }\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)]

exists for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
. The map
x\mapsto g_{2}(x)
is usually called the
pair correlation function
of
\mathbf{X}
. The
structure factor

S
is then defined as:
S:=1+\widehat{(g_{2}-1)}
, where
\widehat{\cdot}
represents the Fourier transform (see Section
2.1
for notation and conventions).

Heuristically speaking, there is a correspondence between the small wave-number behavior of the structure factor (i.e.
S(k)
for
|k|
small) and the growth of number variance in large balls (see for instance
[
24
, Sec. 2]
), which can be summarized as follows:

•

S(0)=0

\longleftrightarrow

\mathrm{Var}\left[|\mathbf{X}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|\right]=o(r^{\mathsf{d}})
(
\mathbf{X}
is hyperuniform). This is sometimes phrased as the condition “
\lim_{k\to 0}S(k)=0
”, or as the
sum rule

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}}(g_{2}-1)=-1
.

•

S(k)\sim|k|^{\alpha}
with
\alpha>1

\longleftrightarrow

\mathrm{Var}\left[|\mathbf{X}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|\right]=\mathcal{O}(r^{\mathsf{d}-1})
(
\mathbf{X}
is class-I hyperuniform)

•

S(k)\sim|k|^{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in(0,1)

\longleftrightarrow

\mathrm{Var}\left[|\mathbf{X}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|\right]=\mathcal{O}(r^{\mathsf{d}-\alpha})
(
\mathbf{X}
is hyperuniform, but not Class-I)

On a mathematical level, there are several aspects to care about:

1.

The two-point correlation function is in general not a proper function and has to be defined as a measure (we recall this in Section
2.3
below). The measure
g_{2}-\mathtt{d}x
might have infinite mass, so its Fourier transform (and thus the structure factor itself) has to be defined in a distributional sense.

2.

The value of
S
at
0
might not be well-defined, or
S
might not be continuous near
0
. As a matter of fact, it can happen that the statement
\lim_{|k|\to 0}S(k)=0
is
false
(because the limit does not exist) and yet the point process
is hyperuniform
. This is not a big issue in itself - in actual computations related to hyperuniformity, the structure factor always gets integrated against some “nice” functions.

The mathematical connection between the
geometric
definition (
1.1
) of hyperuniformity and its
spectral
characterization was first fully established in
[
2
]
. In particular, it is proven that:

Proposition 1.1

(
[
2
]
, Thm. 3.6 & 3.7)
.

Let
S
be the structure factor of stationary point process.

1.

If
S(\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon})=o(\varepsilon^{\mathsf{d}})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
(“spectral hyperuniformity”), then
\sigma(r)=o(r^{\mathsf{d}})
(“geometric hyperuniformity”).

2.

If
S(\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon})=\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{\mathsf{d}+\gamma}\right)
then
\sigma(r)=\mathcal{O}\left(r^{\mathsf{d}-\gamma}\right)
.

1.3
Connection with the literature

Positive result

Our main positive result in Theorem
1
is the fact that in dimension
2
, perturbations with finite second moment preserve the hyperuniformity of lattices. To the best of our knowledge, this was not proven in the mathematical literature
even for bounded perturbations
. In the physics literature, this is discussed in several places including
[
9
, Sec. VI.C]
,
[
10
]
,
[
13
, Sec. IV. A]
- all of which do state that hyperuniformity of a two-dimensional lattice cannot be broken if the perturbations have finite variance. Their argument relies on computing a small wave-number approximation for the structure factor of
\mathbf{PL}
, in which the covariance structure of the perturbations plays an important role. This is detailed in
[
10
]
, which includes the case of Gaussian perturbations (note that the paper also considers the perturbations of general hyperuniform processes, not only stationary lattices).

Examples of pathological processes

Our examples (see again Theorem
1
) of non-hyperuniform perturbed lattices in dimension
\mathsf{d}=2
(with perturbations failing to have a finite second moment) and
\mathsf{d}\geq 3
(with arbitrarily small perturbations) seem to be the first mathematical constructions of such objects. Their existence was hinted on in the physics papers cited above (e.g. in
[
13
]

“In contrast to uncorrelated perturbed lattices that are always hyperuniform, correlated ones can be non-hyperuniform.”
), but no explicit construction was given - rather, based on the small wave-number expansion for the structure factor mentioned above, they argue that if the spectral density of the perturbation field has a certain power-law near
0
, then hyperuniformity will be broken. In dimension
2
, this does not suggest how to proceed (because in that case, the perturbations must have infinite variance, hence no properly defined spectral density). In higher dimensions, this suggests that certain Gaussian perturbation fields with well-chosen spectral densities can do the job. We produce counter-examples with perturbations that are
bounded
and not “only” with finite moments of all order like Gaussian variables.

Our constructions are elementary and allow us to prove lower bounds on the number variance directly, without relying on the structure factor.

Slow decay of the rescaled number variance

In Theorem
3
, we give what seems to be the first examples of hyperuniform processes in any dimension with truly arbitrarily slow decay of the rescaled number variance (although this might have been “folklore” knowledge to some experts, the only general result that we are aware of is the fact that in dimension
1
, any power law decay can be realized by a well-chosen determinantal point process, see
[
21
, Sec. 3]
). For his phenomenological classification, Torquato
[
24
]
considers three asymptotic rates: class-I (
\sigma(r)\sim cr^{-1}
), class-II (
\sigma(r)\sim cr^{-1}\log r
) and class-III (
\sigma(r)\sim cr^{-\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
), which does
not
include cases like
\sigma(r)\geq c\log^{-0.01}r
. We thus show that this classification does not quite cover the entire spectrum of possible behaviors. It is however fair to say that our examples are ad hoc constructions with little “physical” reality.

1.4
An optimal transport perspective

In this section, we reformulate our main result from the point of view of optimal transport between stationary point processes recently introduced in
[
6
]
(using their notation).

Let us recall the relevant notions. Denote the set of locally finite point configurations by
\Gamma
. For two distributions of stationary point processes
\mathsf{P}_{0}
and
\mathsf{P}_{1}
and a cost function
c:\Gamma\times\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
we are interested in the transport problem

\inf_{Q\in\mathsf{Cpl}_{s}(\mathsf{P}_{0},\mathsf{P}_{1})}\int c\mathsf{d}Q

(1.4)

where
\mathsf{Cpl}_{s}(\mathsf{P}_{0},\mathsf{P}_{1})
is the set of all couplings between
\mathsf{P}_{0}
and
\mathsf{P}_{1}
that are invariant under the diagonal action of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
on
\Gamma\times\Gamma
given by
\theta_{x}(\xi,\eta)(\cdot,\cdot)=(\xi,\eta)(\cdot-x,\cdot-x)
(for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
).

Note that in (
1.4
),
c
is a cost function
between point configurations
. A particularly relevant choice of
c
is the following: for
p\geq 1
and for any two point configurations
\xi,\eta
on
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
, we put:

c_{p}(\xi,\eta):=\inf_{q\in\mathsf{cpl}(\xi,\eta)}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{d}}\int_{\Lambda_{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x-y|^{p}q(dx,dy),

where
\mathsf{cpl}(\xi,\eta)
denotes the set of all couplings between the point configurations
\xi
and
\eta
, seen as measures on
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
and
\Lambda_{n}
is the box
[-n/2,n/2]^{d}
. The quantity
c_{p}(\xi,\eta)
can be thought of as a “
p
-Wasserstein distance per unit volume” between the measures
\xi
and
\eta
.

Then, if
\mathsf{P}_{0},\mathsf{P}_{1}
are the distributions of two random point configurations, we define their “
p
-Wasserstein distance”
\mathrm{Wass}_{p}
by:

\mathrm{Wass}_{p}^{p}(\mathsf{P}_{0},\mathsf{P}_{1}):=\inf_{Q\in\mathsf{Cpl}_{s}(\mathsf{P}_{0},\mathsf{P}_{1})}\int c_{p}\mathsf{d}Q.

This defines a geodesic metric on the space of stationary point process with unit intensity.

In order to connect this definition of a Wasserstein distance between point processes with the result of the present paper, it is relevant to mention that there is a representation of
\mathrm{Wass}_{p}
in terms of equivariant random matchings. Let
(\xi^{\bullet},\eta^{\bullet})
be two random point configurations whose distribution is jointly invariant under the shifts
\theta_{x}
(for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
), denote their marginal distributions by
\mathsf{P}_{0}
,
\mathsf{P}_{1}
. Assume that
(\xi^{\bullet},\eta^{\bullet})
are constructed on some probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
equipped with a measurable flow
\theta:\Omega\to\Omega
satisfying
\theta_{0}=id,\theta_{x}\circ\theta_{y}=\theta_{x+y}.

Denote by
\mathsf{cpl}_{e}(\xi^{\bullet},\eta^{\bullet})
the set of all equivariant couplings of
\xi^{\bullet}
and
\eta^{\bullet}
, i.e. for each
\omega\in\Omega
the measure
q^{\omega}
is a coupling of
\xi^{\omega}
and
\eta^{\omega}
and
q^{\theta_{x}\omega}(A,B)=q^{\omega}(A-x,B-x)
. Then, we have
[
6
, Prop. 1.2]

\mathrm{Wass}_{p}^{p}(\mathsf{P}_{0},\mathsf{P}_{1})=\inf_{(\xi^{\bullet},\eta^{\bullet})}\inf_{q^{\bullet}\in\mathsf{cpl}_{e}(\xi^{\bullet},\eta^{\bullet})}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{[0,1]^{\mathsf{d}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x-y|^{p}q(dx,dy)\right],

(1.5)

where the first infimum runs over all probability spaces supporting jointly invariant random measures
\xi^{\bullet}\sim\mathsf{P}_{0},\eta^{\bullet}\sim\mathsf{P}_{1}.
Furthermore, the infimum is attained by an invariant matching
\mathrm{m}^{\bullet}
, i.e. a coupling of the form
(id,T^{\bullet})_{\#}\xi^{\bullet}
, where
S_{\#}\mu
denotes the push-forward of the measure
\mu
by the measurable map
S
.

Now comes the important point: Note that
T^{\bullet}(x)-x
is a stationary displacement field and any stationary displacement field induces a stationary matching. Moreover, if
\xi=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}}\delta_{x}
the cost of a matching
m^{\bullet}=(id,T^{\bullet})_{\#}\xi^{\bullet}
is given by
\mathbb{E}[|T(0)-0|^{p}]
, the
L^{p}
norm of the displacement field.

Let us denote here by
\mathsf{P}^{\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}}
the stationary version of the integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}
, i.e.
\mathsf{P}^{\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}}:=\mathrm{Law}(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}}\delta_{x+U})
, with a random variable
U
uniformly distributed in
[0,1]^{\mathsf{d}}
. It is equivalent to find:

•

A
c_{p}
-optimal equivariant matching between
\mathsf{P}^{\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}}
and
\mathsf{P}
.

•

A stationary displacement field
(\mathrm{p}_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}}
with minimal
L^{p}
norm
\mathbb{E}[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|^{{p}}]
such that the law of the resulting perturbed lattice
\mathbf{PL}
coincides with
\mathsf{P}
.

With this language, the main results of Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
can be rephrased as follows:

Theorem
.

Let
\mathsf{P}
be the law of a stationary point process
\mathbf{X}
on
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
. We have:

1.

If
\mathsf{d}=1,2
and if
\mathrm{Wass}_{\mathsf{d}}(\mathsf{P}^{\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}},\mathsf{P})
is finite, then
\mathsf{P}
is hyperuniform.

2.

If
\mathsf{d}=1,2
, for all
\varepsilon>0
, there are examples with
\mathrm{Wass}_{\mathsf{d}-\varepsilon}(\mathsf{P}^{\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}},\mathsf{P})
finite and yet
\mathsf{P}
is not hyperuniform (in fact the number variance in finite balls is not even finite).

3.

If
\mathsf{d}\geq 3
, for all
\varepsilon>0
, there are examples with
\mathrm{Wass}_{\infty}(\mathsf{P}^{\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}},\mathsf{P})\leq\varepsilon
and yet
\mathsf{P}
is not hyperuniform.

4.

If
\mathsf{d}=1
and if
\mathrm{Wass}_{2}(\mathsf{P}^{\mathbb{Z}},\mathsf{P})
is finite, then
\mathsf{P}
is class-I hyperuniform. If
\mathsf{d}=2
, for all
\varepsilon>0
, there are examples with
\mathrm{Wass}_{\infty}(\mathsf{P}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}},\mathsf{P})\leq\varepsilon
and yet
\mathsf{P}
is not class-I hyperuniform.

Since
\mathrm{Wass}_{p}
is a geodesic metric, it follows that in dimensions
\mathsf{d}=1,2
the set of point processes with finite
\mathrm{Wass}_{p}
distance to
\mathsf{P}^{\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}}
is geodesically closed, and thus the geodesic interpolation between two hyperuniform point processes with finite
\mathrm{Wass}_{\mathsf{d}}
distance to
\mathsf{P}^{\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}}
stays hyperuniform (resp. class-I hyperuniform for
\mathsf{d}=1
and
\mathrm{Wass}_{2}
).

Remark 1.2
.

One can more generally define the Wasserstein distance between Radon measures (not necessarily purely atomic ones). In that sense, a stationary lattice is always at finite
\mathrm{Wass}_{p}
distance of the Lebesgue measure (seen as a deterministic Radon measure) for all
p
(in fact for
p=+\infty
). It is thus equivalent to be “at finite distance of a stationary lattice” or “at finite distance of Lebesgue”.

1.5
Open questions and connection with recent results

Perturbations of general hyperuniform processes

Proving or disproving hyperuniformity of stationary perturbations of a general hyperuniform process (not only a stationary lattice) remains open. In fact, as we discuss below in Section
2.4
, even the correct notion of “perturbations” is not completely clear in general: for example, do we allow perturbations to depend on the realization of the underlying process or not? In
[
10
]
, the case of perturbations that are
independent
from the process is considered.

For perturbations that can be correlated with the original point process, one can make the following observation: if
\mathbf{X}
is hyperuniform
and has finite
2
-Wasserstein distance to a lattice
, then any point process
\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
with finite
2
-Wasserstein distance to
\mathbf{X}
is hyperuniform again, by the triangle inequality and our main result. In other words, any
L^{2}
perturbation of
\mathbf{X}
(independent of
\mathbf{X}
or not) will yield an hyperuniform process. Note that this does not completely answer the question because, as observed in
[
12
]
, not every hyperuniform process has finite
2
-Wasserstein distance to a lattice.

Converse statements in dimension
2
: the results of
[
15
,
3
,
12
]

Using the language introduced just above, our main result in the present paper is that in dimension
2
, finite
2
-Wasserstein distance to a (stationary) lattice implies hyperuniformity. Several recent papers are dealing (among other things) with the converse statement.

In
[
15
]
, the converse statement (hyperuniformity
\implies
finite
2
-Wasserstein distance) was shown to be true
under a certain integrability condition on the reduced pair correlation function
of the process which, they argue, proves that “
most hyperuniform planar point processes are
L^{2}
-perturbed lattices
”.

In
[
3
]
, finite
2
-Wasserstein distance is obtained under a quantitative assumption on the decay of the rescaled number variance which is just slightly stronger than hyperuniformity itself.

In a companion
[
12
]
to the present paper, the integrability condition of
[
15
]
is observed to be closely related to some kind of “finiteness of the Coulomb energy” and it is shown that indeed:

\text{Finite Coulomb energy }\implies\text{ Finite $2$-Wasserstein distance to a lattice }\implies\text{ Hyperuniformity},

(the second implication being the one from the present paper). However, simple examples are given in
[
12
]
of stationary point processes which are hyperuniform, but not at finite
2
-Wasserstein distance to a lattice - thus there is no perfect equivalence between those two notions. It is also proven that under a condition which is slightly stronger than hyperuniformity but slightly weaker than the one of
[
3
]
, one obtains finite
2
-Wasserstein distance to a lattice. Since this condition covers by far the vast majority of known examples, it completely agrees with the statement of
[
15
]
cited above.

The decay condition of
[
12
]
, called “
\star
-hyperuniformity”, is shown there to be equivalent to finite Coulomb energy. In the present paper, as a corollary of the proof of Theorem
1
, we obtain quantitative bounds on the decay of the rescaled number variance. In particular, for all
\varepsilon>0
(see Corollary
3.7
):

\text{ Finite $2+\varepsilon$-Wasserstein distance to a lattice }\implies\star-\text{hyperuniformity}\iff\text{ Finite Coulomb energy}.

Sufficient condition for class-I hyperuniformity

In dimension
2
we could not produce examples of perturbed lattices which are a) hyperuniform but not class-I hyperuniform
and
b) such that the covariance structure of the perturbations satisfy a summability condition of the type:

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{L}}\left|\mathrm{Cov}(\mathrm{p}_{x},\mathrm{p}_{0})\right|<+\infty.

(1.6)

It is well-known that (
1.6
) implies that the spectral density of the perturbations not only exists
but is continuous
, which eliminates certain “bad” behaviors, and could be enough to ensure that the resulting perturbed lattice is class-I hyperuniform.

To see why, recall that the basic plan to break hyperuniformity (see e.g.
[
13
, eq. (34)]
) is to choose perturbations
(\mathrm{p}_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}}
whose spectral density
\rho
satisfies
\rho(\omega)\sim C|\omega|^{-2}
as
\omega\to 0
. Indeed, when doing the computations (see e.g. (
3.16
) below), the behavior near
0
of the spectral density of the perturbations turns out to play a major role for the asymptotic decay of the rescaled number variance, and
\rho(\omega)\sim C|\omega|^{-2}
is in some sense the “critical” case separating hyperuniform and non-hyperuniform behaviors. Having (for the perturbations)
\rho(\omega)\sim C|\omega|^{-2+\delta}
with
\delta>0
produces a perturbed lattice which is hyperuniform, but for
\delta
small it ought to be close to the “non-hyperuniform” case and in particular “not very hyperuniform” (type-I).

In dimension
2
, it is not possible to have
\rho(\omega)\sim C|\omega|^{-2}
near
0
because
\rho
would fail to be integrable, but one can certainly produce Gaussian perturbation fields with
\rho(\omega)\sim C|\omega|^{-2+\delta}
for any
\delta>0
, and the resulting perturbed lattice will presumably
not
be class-I hyperuniform if
\delta\leq 1
. However, if the spectral density
\rho
is known to be continuous near
0
, such divergences are of course forbidden (one needs
\delta\geq 2
). We are thus left with the following question: in dimension
2
, if
\mathbb{E}[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|^{2}]<+\infty
and (
1.6
) holds, is
\mathbf{PL}
necessarily class-I hyperuniform?

1.6
Plan of the paper

In Section
2
, we introduce our notation, and our general setting.

Section
3
is devoted to the proof of the main result of the paper: in dimension
\mathsf{d}=2
, a finite second moment of perturbations ensures hyperuniformity.

The analogous positive result in dimension
\mathsf{d}=1
is simpler to prove and is provided in Appendix
A.1
, along with the proof of the first item of Theorem
2
.

The proofs of other results, namely the first, third, and fourth items in Theorem

, the second item in Theorem

.

Preliminaries

2.1
Some notation

We write
A\preceq B
, or equivalently
A=\mathcal{O}(B)
, when there exists a constant
C
depending only on the dimension
\mathsf{d}
, the lattice
\mathcal{L}
and the law of the perturbations
(\mathrm{p}_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{L}}
such that
|A|\leq C|B|
. We write
f(x)=o(g(x))
as
x\to a
if
\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=0
. If the value
a
is clear from context, we do not mention it.

In the proof of Proposition
3.1
, which is our main result, we will work with a large parameter
r
and auxiliary parameters depending on
r
. In that context, we sometimes write
A\ll B
to signify that
\frac{A}{B}\to 0
as
r\to\infty
(
A
,
B
being two quantities depending explicitly or implicitly on
r
).

We use
|\cdot|
to denote the cardinality of a finite set or the Lebesgue measure of a Borel set in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
. We write
\mathsf{1}_{\cdots}
or
\mathsf{1}\{\cdots\}
for indicator functions.
We sometimes write
\left\langle\mu,f\right\rangle=\int fd\mu
for the integral of a function
f
against a (signed) measure
\mu
. We denote the convolution of two functions
f
and
g
by
f\ast g
.

Fourier transforms

We choose the following convention for the Fourier transform: if
f
is in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}})
we let:

\hat{f}(s):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}}e^{-is\cdot t}f(t)\mathtt{d}t.

We also define the characteristic function of a random variable
X
as
\varphi_{X}(s):=\mathbb{E}[e^{-is\cdot X}]
. With this convention we have
\hat{\hat{f}}=(2\pi)^{d}f(-\cdot)
. We also have
\widehat{f\ast g}=\hat{f}\hat{g}
but
\widehat{fg}=(2\pi)^{-d}\hat{f}\ast\hat{g}
.

2.2
Generalities on lattices

Some definitions

We recall some definitions about lattices, which can be found in most textbooks.

The lattices considered here are subgroups of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
generated by linear combinations
with integer coefficients
of the vectors of a certain basis of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
(we only consider “full-rank” lattices). The basic example is the so-called integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}
(or “square lattice” for
\mathsf{d}=2
) which is simply the subgroup of all points in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
with integer coefficients, or equivalently the set of all integer linear combinations of the vectors of the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
. Any other lattice can be obtained as an affine transformation of
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}
.

Remark 2.1
.

If we could show that any lattice
\mathcal{L}
is itself a bounded stationary perturbation of
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}
, it would be enough to treat the case
\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{Z}^{2}
for, say, the positive result of Theorem
1
in dimension
2
. This is indeed true, as they have both finite
\mathrm{Wass}_{2}
distance to Lebesgue (see Remark
1.2
). However, we did not see an “easy” explicit perturbation that one could write down
in a stationary fashion
to connect
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}
in order to a general lattice, and the cost of working with general lattices is not too high, so we chose this option.

To a lattice
\mathcal{L}
and a basis
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{\mathsf{d}})
, we associate a “fundamental domain” of
\mathcal{L}
which is the set:

\mathcal{D}:=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{\mathtt{d}}t_{i}\lambda_{i},\ (t_{1},\dots,t_{\mathsf{d}})\in[0,1)^{\mathsf{d}}\right\}.

The fundamental domain is a compact parallelotope, whose volume does not depend on the choice of the basis and is called the “covolume” of
\mathcal{L}
.
In this paper, we will always assume the covolume to be
1
.
This is of course only for convenience and in no way a loss of generality. An interesting property is that every vector
t
of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
can be uniquely written as:
t=x+u
, where
x\in\mathcal{L}
and
u\in\mathcal{D}
. In other words, translating a fundamental domain by all the vectors in
\mathcal{L}
makes a perfect tiling of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
.

The dual lattice
\mathcal{L}^{\star}
of
\mathcal{L}
is defined as:
\mathcal{L}^{\star}:=\left\{k\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}},\text{ such that }k\cdot x\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ for all }x\in\mathcal{L}\right\}
. The only important fact for us is that
\mathcal{L}^{\star}
is indeed a lattice, and that it appears in Poisson’s summation formula, which we recall next.

Poisson’s summation formula

We refer to
[
22
, Chap. VII]
. If
f:\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}\to\mathbb{C}
is in
L^{1}
(so that
\hat{f}
is well-defined) and if moreover there exists
\delta>0
and a constant
C>0
such that:
|f(x)|\leq\frac{C}{|x|^{\mathsf{d}+\delta}},\quad|\hat{f}(x)|\leq\frac{C}{|x|^{\mathsf{d}+\delta}}\text{ as }|x|\to\infty
, then Poisson’s summation formula holds, namely we have:

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{L}}f(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{L}^{\star}}\hat{f}(2\pi k).

(2.1)

Note that this obviously includes the case of
f
in Schwartz’s space (i.e. when
f
is smooth and
f
and all its derivatives decay faster than every polynomial at infinity).

2.3
Generalities on point processes

Configuration of points

A deterministic configuration of points in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
is defined as a locally finite integer-valued measure on
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
, i.e. a measure of the form
\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}
where
(x_{i})_{i}
is a finite or infinite sequence of points in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
without accumulation points. Note that multiple points are allowed at the same location. The state space of all point configurations is endowed with the
\sigma
-field generated by the counting functions
\mathbf{x}\mapsto\mathbf{x}(B)
for all Borel sets
B
in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
. A random configuration of points, or “point process”, is a random variable on this state space. We refer to
[
17
, Sec. 2]
for details about multiple points, labelling, etc.

Stationarity.

If
\mathbf{X}
is a point configuration and
u
is a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
, we denote by
\mathbf{X}+u
the “shift by u” i.e. the effect of translating all the points of
\mathbf{X}
by
u
. A point process is said to be
stationary
when its distribution is invariant with respect to all the translations i.e. when
\mathbf{X}+u
and
\mathbf{X}
have the same distribution for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
.

When
\mathbf{X}
is stationary, we define its intensity as the average number of points of
\mathbf{X}
per unit volume. Here for convenience we always work with processes of intensity
1
.

Second order measures

When
\mathbf{X}
is a random point configuration, we say that
\mathbf{X}
is
locally square integrable
if
\mathbb{E}[|\mathbf{X}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|^{2}]
is finite for all
r>0
. If
\mathbf{X}
is locally square integrable, we can define its
reduced second factorial moment measure
, denoted by
\alpha_{\mathrm{red}}^{(2)}
. This measure plays a central role in the analysis of hyperuniformity for perturbed lattices because:

1.

The measure
\alpha_{\mathrm{red}}^{(2)}
of a perturbed lattice has a tractable expression, see (
2.3
).

2.

The number variance is conveniently expressed in terms of
\alpha_{\mathrm{red}}^{(2)}
, see (
A.2
).

We refer to
[
17
, Sections 4.4. & 8.2]
for details.

Definition 2.2

(Second factorial moment measure)
.

If
\mathbf{X}
is a point process, we define its
second factorial moment measure
as the Borel measure
\alpha^{(2)}
on
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
such that for all Borel sets
B_{1},B_{2}
:

\alpha^{(2)}(B_{1}\times B_{2}):=\mathbb{E}\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbf{X}}^{\neq}\mathbf{1}\left\{x_{1}\in B_{1},x_{2}\in B_{2}\right\}.

If
\mathbf{X}
is locally square integrable, then
\alpha^{(2)}
is well-defined, and if moreover
\mathbf{X}
is stationary, then we can define its
reduced second factorial moment measure

\alpha^{(2)}_{red}
(sometimes known as
pair correlation measure
), which is given, for
B
a Borel set, by:

\alpha_{red}^{(2)}(B)=\mathbb{E}\sum_{x,y\in\mathbf{X}}^{\neq}\mathbf{1}\{x\in[0,1)^{\mathsf{d}},y-x\in B\}.

(2.2)

Note that in (
2.2
) we can replace
[0,1)^{\mathsf{d}}
by any Borel set of volume
1
(see e.g. the proof
[
17
, Prop. 8.7]
).

2.4
Basic properties of perturbed lattices

Discussion of the notion of “perturbations”

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a lattice and
\mathcal{D}
a fundamental domain of
\mathcal{L}
. Recall that we define the (unperturbed) stationary lattice as
\mathbf{L}:=\{x+U,x\in\mathcal{L}\}
where
U
is uniform in
\mathcal{D}
. One could think of at least two ways to define “perturbations” of
\mathbf{L}
:

1.

Let
\mathrm{p}
be a random field
\mathcal{L}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
- the value of
\mathrm{p}_{x}
for
x\in\mathcal{L}
corresponds to the “perturbation” applied to
x
. Let
U
be uniform in
\mathcal{D}
and independent of
\mathrm{p}
. We define the perturbed stationary lattice
\mathbf{L}
as the random collection of points given by
\mathbf{PL}:=\{(x+\mathrm{p}(x))+U,\ x\in\mathcal{L}\}
.

2.

Let
\bar{\mathrm{p}}
be a random vector field on
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
, thought of as a “displacement field” - a point placed at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
gets displaced by
\bar{\mathrm{p}}(x)
. Let
U
be uniform in
\mathcal{D}
and independent of
\bar{\mathrm{p}}
. We define the perturbed stationary lattice
\mathbf{L}
as the random collection of points given by
\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}:=\{(x+U)+\bar{\mathrm{p}}(x+U),\ x\in\mathcal{L}\}
.

It is straightforward to check that, when the perturbations themselves are stationary, those two notions coincide.

Claim 2.3
.

With
\mathbf{PL}
and
\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}
as defined above:

1.

Assume that
\mathrm{p}
is
\mathcal{L}
-invariant. For
t\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
, write
t=x+U
with
x\in\mathcal{L}
and
U
in
\mathcal{D}
, and set
\bar{\mathrm{p}}:=\mathrm{p}(t-U)
. Then
\bar{\mathrm{p}}
is a
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
-invariant displacement field and
\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}=\mathbf{PL}
.

2.

Assume that
\bar{\mathrm{p}}
is
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
-invariant and set
\mathrm{p}:=\bar{\mathrm{p}}_{|\mathcal{L}}
. Then
\mathrm{p}
is a
\mathcal{L}
-invariant perturbation field and
\mathbf{PL}=\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}
.

In the introduction, we chose the first option, but for our constructions in Section
4
it will be convenient to use the second option.

If one wanted to perturb hyperuniform point processes which are not lattices, the “displacement field” approach seems more tractable as it does not rely on an explicit labelling of the points. In that case however, the assumption of
\bar{\mathrm{p}}
being independent of
\mathbf{L}
could be seen as restrictive (it is not very much so for lattices).

Remark 2.4
.

The properties of
\mathbf{PL}
are of course invariant under a global shift, thus we can and will assume that the perturbations (which are identically distributed) are centered, i.e.
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{p}_{0}]=0
.

Basic properties

Lemma 2.5
.

If the perturbations are
\mathcal{L}
-invariant as assumed in Section
1
, then:

1.

\mathbf{PL}
is almost surely locally finite.

2.

\mathbf{PL}
is a stationary point process.

Proof.

For the first item, take
r\geq 10
and bound
\mathbb{E}[|\mathbf{PL}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|]
by:

\mathbb{E}[|\mathbf{PL}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|]\leq\mathcal{O}(r^{\mathsf{d}})+\sum_{x\in\mathcal{L},|x|\geq 100r}\mathbb{P}\left[\{x+\mathrm{p}_{x}+U\in\mathrm{B}_{r}\}\right].

Let
C
be an upper bound on the diameter of the fundamental domain
\mathcal{D}
. We see that

\mathbb{P}\left[\{x+\mathrm{p}_{x}+U\in\mathrm{B}_{r}\}\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\{\mathrm{p}_{x}\in\mathrm{B}_{r+C}(-x)\}\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[\{\mathrm{p}_{0}\in\mathrm{B}_{r+C}(-x)\}\right],

using the fact that the perturbations are identically distributed. Finally, it remains to observe that:

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{L},|x|\geq 100r}\mathbb{P}\left[\{\mathrm{p}_{0}\in\mathrm{B}_{r+C}(-x)\}\right]\preceq r^{\mathsf{d}}

is finite, thus
\mathbb{E}[|\mathbf{PL}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|]
is finite, hence
\mathbf{PL}
is almost surely locally finite.

For the second item, take
t\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
and decompose
U+t
as
x_{\star}+v
with
x_{\star}\in\mathcal{L}
and
v\in\mathcal{D}
(see Section
2.2
). If
U
is uniformly distributed in
\mathcal{D}
, then so is
v
. We then have, using the
\mathcal{L}
-invariance of
(\mathrm{p}_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{L}}
the following identities in law:

\{x+U+\mathrm{p}_{x}+t\}_{x\in\mathcal{L}}=\{x+\mathrm{p}_{x}+x_{\star}+v\}_{x\in\mathcal{L}}=\{(x+x_{\star})+\mathrm{p}_{x+x_{\star}}+v\}_{x\in\mathcal{L}}=\{x+\mathrm{p}_{x}+v\}_{x\in\mathcal{L}}.

∎

First finiteness result

Lemma 2.6
.

If
\mathbb{E}[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|^{\mathsf{d}}]
is finite, then
\mathbf{PL}
is locally square integrable in the sense of Section
2.3
.

Proof of Lemma
2.6
.

We write, with
U
uniformly distributed over the fundamental domain of
\mathcal{L}
:

\mathbb{E}[|\mathbf{PL}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|^{2}]=\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{L}}\mathbf{1}\{x+\mathrm{p}_{x}+U\in\mathrm{B}_{r}\}\right)^{2}=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}}\mathbf{1}\{x+\mathrm{p}_{x}+U\in\mathrm{B}_{r}\}\times\mathbf{1}\{x^{\prime}+\mathrm{p}_{x^{\prime}}+U\in\mathrm{B}_{r}\}]\\
\leq\sum_{x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}}\min\left(\mathbb{P}\left[\{x+\mathrm{p}_{x}+U\in\mathrm{B}_{r}\}\right],\mathbb{P}\left[\{x^{\prime}+\mathrm{p}_{x^{\prime}}+U\in\mathrm{B}_{r}\}\right]\right).

Let
C
be an upper bound on the diameter of the fundamental domain
\mathcal{D}
. Since the indices
x,x^{\prime}
play symmetrical roles we can write:

\sum_{x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}}\min\left(\mathbb{P}\left[\{x+\mathrm{p}_{x}{+U}\in\mathrm{B}_{r}\}\right],\mathbb{P}\left[\{x^{\prime}+\mathrm{p}_{x^{\prime}}+U\in\mathrm{B}_{r}\}\right]\right)\leq 2\sum_{x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L},|x^{\prime}|\leq|x|}\mathbb{P}\left[\{x+\mathrm{p}_{x}+U\in\mathrm{B}_{r}\}\right]\\
\preceq\sum_{x\in\mathcal{L}}|x|^{\mathsf{d}}\mathbb{P}\left[\{\mathrm{p}_{x}\in\mathrm{B}_{r+C}(-x)\}\right].

Using the symmetry of
\mathcal{L}
under
x\mapsto-x
and the fact that the random variables
(\mathrm{p}_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{L}}
are identically distributed, we also have:

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{L}}|x|^{\mathsf{d}}\mathbb{P}\left[\{\mathrm{p}_{x}\in\mathrm{B}_{r+C}(-x)\}\right]=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{L}}|x|^{\mathsf{d}}\mathbb{P}\left[\{\mathrm{p}_{0}\in\mathrm{B}_{r+C}(x)\}\right].

The contribution to the sum coming from
\{x\in\mathcal{L},|x|\leq 2r\}
is
\mathcal{O}(r^{\mathsf{d}})\times\mathcal{O}(r^{\mathsf{d}})
and the rest of the sum can be compared to an integral (here, without loss of generality, we assume
r
large enough so that
|x|^{\mathsf{d}}\leq 10|x-r|^{\mathsf{d}}
for
|x|\geq 2r
):

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{L},|x|\geq 2r}|x|^{\mathsf{d}}\mathbb{P}\left[\{\mathrm{p}_{0}\in\mathrm{B}_{r+C}(x)\}\right]\preceq\int_{t\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}},|t|\geq r}|t|^{\mathsf{d}}\mathbb{P}\left[\{\mathrm{p}_{0}\in\mathrm{B}_{r+C}(t)\}\right]\mathtt{d}t,

which itself, using Fubini’s theorem, can be compared to an expression involving the law
P_{0}
of
\mathrm{p}_{0}
, namely:

\int_{t\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}},|t|\geq r}|t|^{\mathsf{d}}\mathbb{P}\left[\{\mathrm{p}_{0}\in\mathrm{B}_{r+C}(t)\}\right]\preceq r^{\mathsf{d}}\int_{t\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}}|t|^{\mathsf{d}}\mathtt{d}P_{0}(t),

and the last integral coincides with
\mathbb{E}[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|^{\mathsf{d}}]
, which is finite.
∎

In particular the reduced second factorial moment measure
\alpha_{\mathrm{red}}^{(2)}
of
\mathbf{PL}
is well-defined. We now give its expression.

The reduced covariance function
\alpha_{\mathrm{red}}^{(2)}
of perturbed lattices

Proposition 2.7
.

For all bounded Borel subsets
B
of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
, we have:

\alpha_{\mathrm{red}}^{(2)}(B)=\mathbb{E}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{0\}}\mathbf{1}{\{x+\mathrm{p}_{x}-\mathrm{p}_{0}\in B\}}.

(2.3)

Proof of Proposition
2.7
.

We start from the definition (
2.2
) of
\alpha_{\mathrm{red}}^{(2)}
, using the fundamental domain
\mathcal{D}
of
\mathcal{L}
instead of
[0,1)^{\mathsf{d}}
(this is valid because
\mathcal{D}
has volume
1
):

\alpha_{\mathrm{red}}^{(2)}(B)=\mathbb{E}\sum^{\neq}_{x\in\mathbf{PL},y\in\mathbf{PL}}\mathbf{1}\left\{x\in\mathcal{D},y-x\in B\right\}\\
=\mathbb{E}\sum^{\neq}_{x\in\mathcal{L},y\in\mathcal{L}}\mathbf{1}\left\{x+\mathrm{p}_{x}+U\in\mathcal{D},y+\mathrm{p}_{y}-x-\mathrm{p}_{x}\in B\right\}\\
=\mathbb{E}\sum^{\neq}_{x\in\mathcal{L},y\in\mathcal{L}}\mathbf{1}\left\{\mathrm{p}_{x}+U\in\mathcal{D}-x,y+\mathrm{p}_{y}-x-\mathrm{p}_{x}\in B\right\}\\
=\mathbb{E}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{L}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{x\}}\mathbf{1}\left\{\mathrm{p}_{0}+U\in\mathcal{D}-x,y+\mathrm{p}_{y-x}-x-\mathrm{p}_{0}\in B\right\}\\
=\mathbb{E}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{L}}\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{0\}}\mathbf{1}\left\{\mathrm{p}_{0}+U\in\mathcal{D}-x,y^{\prime}+\mathrm{p}_{y^{\prime}}-\mathrm{p}_{0}\in B\right\}\\
=\mathbb{E}\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{0\}}\mathbf{1}\left\{\mathrm{p}_{0}+U\in\cup_{x\in\mathcal{L}}\{\mathcal{D}-x\},y^{\prime}+\mathrm{p}_{y^{\prime}}-\mathrm{p}_{0}\in B\right\}\\
=\mathbb{E}\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{0\}}\mathbf{1}{\{y^{\prime}+\mathrm{p}_{y^{\prime}}-\mathrm{p}_{0}\in B\}}.

In the first line we use the definition of
\mathbf{PL}
(the symbol
\neq
there refers to points in the perturbed lattice that are obtained as perturbations of two different lattice points (they could exceptionally end up at the same spot after perturbations), the second line is elementary, the third line uses the
\mathcal{L}
-invariance of the perturbations, the fourth line uses the fact that
\mathcal{L}
is a group, the fifth line uses the fact that the translates
\{\mathcal{D}-x\}
of the fundamental domain are disjoint and the sixth line makes use of the fact that they cover
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
(see Section
2.2
).
∎

3
Positive result in dimension 2

This section is devoted to the proof of our main result in dimension
2
, namely:

Proposition 3.1
.

If the perturbations have a finite second moment, then
\mathbf{PL}
is hyperuniform.

Preliminaries

Without loss of generality, we assume that the perturbations are centered, see Remark
2.4
. Since the perturbations have a finite second moment, by Proposition
2.7
, the measure
\alpha_{\mathrm{red}}^{(2)}
of
\mathbf{PL}
exists. We denote by
\widehat{\alpha}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{red},\mathbf{PL}}
its Fourier transform, and by
\widehat{\alpha}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{red},\mathbf{L}}
the corresponding object for the stationary lattice.

In the sequel, we fix a smooth, compactly supported function
\chi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to[0,1]

, if
r
is large enough then we have
\chi_{r}(2\pi k+2\pi\omega)=0
for all
k\in\mathcal{L}^{\star}\setminus\{0\}
and all
\omega\in\Omega
, and in turn:

\sum_{k\in\mathcal{L}^{\star}\setminus\{0\}}\int_{\Omega}\chi_{r}(2\pi k+2\pi\omega)|k+\omega|^{2}\mathtt{d}\rho(\omega)=0.

For
k=0
.

Denote by
C(r)
the quantity:

C(r):=\int_{\Omega}\chi_{r}(2\pi\omega)|\omega|^{2}\mathtt{d}\rho(\omega).

(3.16)

Let us now justify that
C(r)
tends to
0
as
r\to\infty
. The family of functions
\omega\mapsto\chi_{r}(2\pi\omega)|\omega|^{2}
converges pointwise to
0
on
\Omega
as
r\to\infty
because
\chi_{r}
converges pointwise to
0
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
. Moreover, the quantity
\chi_{r}(2\pi\omega)|\omega|^{2}=r^{2}|\omega|^{2}\chi(2\pi r\omega)
is bounded uniformly in
r,\omega
because
\chi
is compactly supported. Thus by the dominated convergence theorem (this is where we use the fact that the measure
\rho
is finite), we have:

\lim_{r\to 0}\int_{\Omega}\chi_{r}(2\pi\omega)|\omega|^{2}\mathtt{d}\rho(\omega)=0.

(3.17)

This is a point where the speed of decay is more difficult to quantify. For example, imposing additional moment conditions on the perturbations does not help. We can however make the following observation:

Claim 3.5
.

We have
\lim_{r\to\infty}C(r)=0
in such a way that:

\sum_{n\geq 0}C(2^{n})<+\infty.

Proof of Claim
3.5
.

We have by definition of
\chi_{r}
as
r^{2}
times a cutoff function supported on
B_{2r^{-1}}
:

\int_{\Omega}\chi_{r}(2\pi\omega)|\omega|^{2}\mathtt{d}\rho(\omega)\preceq r^{2}\int_{|\omega|\leq\frac{1}{\pi r}}|\omega|^{2}\mathtt{d}\rho(\omega).

(3.18)

For
j\geq 0
, let
\mathtt{A}(j)
be the annulus
\{2^{-j-1}\leq|\omega|<2^{-j}\}
. In view of (
3.18
), we can compare
C(r)
to the following sum:

C(r)\preceq\frac{1}{r^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{\log_{2}r-1}2^{-2j}\rho(\mathtt{A}(j)).

In particular, if we take
r=2^{n}
and sum over
n
, we obtain, since
\rho
is a finite measure:

\sum_{n\geq 0}C(2^{n})\preceq\sum_{n\geq 0}2^{-2n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}2^{2j}\rho(\mathtt{A}(j))=\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty}2^{2j}\rho(\mathtt{A}(j))\sum_{n\geq j+1}2^{-2n}\preceq\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty}\rho(\mathtt{A}(j))<+\infty.

∎

Handling
B_{12}

We are left with the term
B_{12}
. Using Poisson’s formula, we get:

B_{12}=(2\pi)^{2}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{L}^{\star}}G^{1}(0){\psi^{11}_{r}}(2\pi k)+G^{2}(0){\psi^{22}_{r}}(2\pi k)+\frac{1}{2}(G^{1,2-}(0)-G^{1,2+}(0)){\psi^{12}_{r}}(2\pi k),

which is
0
for
r
large enough. Indeed, the
\psi_{r}^{ab}
vanish at
0
and are supported on
\mathrm{B}_{2r^{-1}}
.
∎

Conclusion

We have obtained that
\lim_{r\to 0}B_{1}=0
. More precisely, for
r
large enough,
B_{1}
reduces to the sum of two error terms: the one from (
3.12
), which is
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{r}{M_{r}}\right)
, and the one corresponding to
C(r)
as in (
3.16
), which is such that
\sum_{n\geq 0}C(2^{n})<+\infty
by Claim
3.5
.

3.2
The error term
B_{2}

Recall that:

B_{2}:=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{0\},|x|\leq M_{r}}\int_{t\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{-it\cdot x}\chi_{r}(t)o(|t|^{2})\mathtt{d}t.

Lemma 3.6
.

We have
B_{2}\preceq\left(\frac{M_{r}}{r}\right)^{2}g(2r^{-1})
, with
g
as in Lemma
3.3
.

Proof of Lemma
3.6
.

We use a rough triangular inequality and control
B_{2}
by:

B_{2}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{0\},|x|\leq M_{r}}\int_{t\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{-it\cdot x}\chi_{r}(t)o(|t|^{2})\mathtt{d}t\preceq M_{r}^{2}\int_{t\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\chi_{r}(t)|t|^{2}g(t)\mathtt{d}t,

where
g
is as in Lemma
3.3
. We conclude using the fact that
\chi_{r}
is supported on
\mathrm{B}_{2r^{-1}}
, has mass
1
, and that
g
is radially non-decreasing.
∎

3.3
Conclusion

Gathering our estimates and returning to (
3.2
), we find that:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\chi_{r}(s)\left(\mathtt{d}\widehat{\alpha}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{red},\mathbf{PL}}(s)-\mathtt{d}\widehat{\alpha}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{red},\mathbf{L}}(s)\right)\preceq\frac{r}{M_{r}}+\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|^{2}\mathsf{1}_{|\mathrm{p}_{0}|\geq\frac{1}{4}M_{r}}\right]+C(r)+\left(\frac{M_{r}}{r}\right)^{2}g(2r^{-1}),

where:

•

M_{r}\gg r\gg 1

•

\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|^{2}\mathsf{1}_{|\mathrm{p}_{0}|\geq\frac{1}{4}M_{r}}\right]\to 0
as
r\to 0

•

C(r)\to 0
in such a way that
\sum_{n}C(2^{n})<\infty

•

g(h)\to 0
as
h\to 0

To prove Proposition
3.1
, it remains to choose
M_{r}
such that

\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{r}{M_{r}}=0,\quad\lim_{r\to\infty}\left(\frac{M_{r}}{r}\right)^{2}g(2r^{-1})=0,

which can be done by setting e.g.

M_{r}:=r\left(g(2r^{-1})\right)^{-1/4}.

Speed of decay.

We obtain
\widehat{\alpha}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{red},\mathbf{PL}}(\mathrm{B}_{r})=o(r^{-2})
(spectral hyperuniformity). In general, there is no upper bound on the speed of decay - arbitrarily slow decays are possible, see Theorem
3
, but we if we assume that
|\mathrm{p}_{0}|
has a moment of order
2+\alpha
with
\alpha>0
, then we can say the following:

•

The function
g
from Lemma
3.3
can be taken as a
\mathcal{O}(|t|^{\alpha})
.

•

We can then take
M_{r}
as
r^{1+\alpha^{\prime}}
for some
\alpha^{\prime}>0

•

The error term
\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|^{2}\mathsf{1}_{|\mathrm{p}_{0}|\geq M_{r}}\right]
becomes
\mathcal{O}(r^{-\alpha^{\prime\prime}})
for some
\alpha^{\prime\prime}>0
.

In this case, we obtain

\widehat{\alpha}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{red},\mathbf{PL}}(\mathrm{B}_{r})\preceq r^{-2-\alpha^{\prime\prime\prime}}+r^{-2}C(r),\text{ with }\sum_{n}C(2^{n})<+\infty,

which implies the following “spectral condition” (see
[
20
]
)

\int_{0\leq|\omega|\leq 1}\frac{1}{|\omega|^{2}}\mathtt{d}\widehat{\alpha}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{red},\mathbf{PL}}(\omega)<\infty,

which in turn is known to imply (
[
12
, Lemma 4.4]
) that:

\sum_{n\geq 0}\sigma(2^{n})<+\infty.

(3.19)

Hyperuniform point processes whose rescaled number variance satisfies (
3.19
) are called “
\star
-hyperuniform” in
[
12
]
, where it is argued that they form an interesting sub-class (in fact, the overhelming majority). Moreover,
\star
-hyperuniformity is equivalent to finite (regularized) Coulomb energy (see
[
12
, Theorem 4]
for further details).

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
\mathsf{d}=2
and assume that
\mathbb{E}[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|^{2+\alpha}]<\infty
for some
\alpha>0
. Then, the resulting perturbed lattice
\mathbf{PL}
is
\star
-hyperuniform. In particular, it has finite (regularized) Coulomb energy.

4
Constructions

For
N\geq 10
we let
\Lambda_{N}=\Lambda_{N}^{(0)}
be the (half-open) cube
\Lambda_{N}:=[-\frac{1}{2}N,\frac{1}{2}N)^{\mathsf{d}}
, and for all
k\in N\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}
we let
\Lambda^{(k)}_{N}:=k+\Lambda^{(0)}_{N}
, which is a cube of sidelength
N
centered at
k
.

4.1
Infinite number variance without
\mathsf{d}
-th moment

We prove here the third item of Theorem
1
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\mathsf{d}\geq 1
and let
X
be a non-negative random variable with
\mathbb{E}[X^{\mathsf{d}}]=+\infty
. Then there exists a
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}
-invariant perturbation field
(\mathrm{p}_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}}
such that:

1.

|\mathrm{p}_{0}|\leq X
almost surely.

2.

The resulting perturbed lattice
\mathbf{PL}
has infinite number variance in finite balls.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Let
(C^{(k)}_{N})_{k\in N\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}}
be independent random variables such that
C^{(k)}_{N}
is uniformly distributed in
\Lambda^{(k)}_{N}
for all
k\in N\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}
.
We first define a “displacement field” (as in Section
2.4
)
\tilde{\mathrm{p}}_{N}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
by setting, for all
k\in N\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}
:

\tilde{\mathrm{p}}_{N}(x):=C^{(k)}_{N}-x,\text{ for all }x\in\Lambda^{(k)}_{N},

hence applying
\tilde{\mathrm{p}}_{N}
corresponds to “sending all the points of the cube
\Lambda^{(k)}_{N}
to
C^{(k)}_{N}
. This displacement field is clearly
N\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}
-stationary, we now turn it into a
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
-stationary field by choosing a random vector
\tau
uniformly distributed in
\Lambda_{N}
and independent of the
(C^{(k)}_{N})_{k\in N\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}}
, and setting
\bar{\mathrm{p}}_{N}:=\tilde{\mathrm{p}}_{N}(\cdot+\tau)
. Note that by construction,
the size of all displacements is bounded by
\sqrt{\mathsf{d}}N
. Finally, we set for a
[0,1]^{d}
-uniform random variable
U
(independent of everything else):

\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}_{N}:=\{x+U+\bar{\mathrm{p}}_{N}(x+U),\ x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}\}.

Next, we count the number of points of the corresponding perturbed lattice
\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}_{N}
falling in the unit ball.

With probability greater than
\frac{|\mathrm{B}_{1}|}{(2N)^{\mathsf{d}}}
the random variable
C^{(0)}_{N}-\tau
(which is the difference of two independent random vectors both uniformly distributed in
\Lambda_{N}^{(0)}
) is in
\mathrm{B}_{1}
, and under this event if
y
is a point in
\Lambda^{(0)}_{N}-\tau
, then
\bar{\mathrm{p}}_{N}(y)=\tilde{\mathrm{p}}_{N}(y+\tau)=C^{(0)}_{N}-(y+\tau)
, consequently if
x
is a lattice point in
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}
such that
x+U\in\Lambda^{(0)}_{N}-\tau
, then we have:

x+U+\bar{\mathrm{p}}_{N}(x+U)=x+U+\tilde{\mathrm{p}}_{N}(x+U+\tau)=x+U+{C^{(0)}_{N}}-(x+U+\tau)={C^{(0)}_{N}-\tau}\in\mathrm{B}_{1}.

By a simple lattice point counting, and since we take
N\geq 10
, there exists a constant
c
depending only on the dimension such that for all values of
\tau
and
U
there are at least
cN^{\mathsf{d}}
points of
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}+U
in the cube
\Lambda^{(0)}_{N}-\tau
. In conclusion, under the event
\{C^{(0)}_{N}-\tau\in\mathrm{B}_{1}\}
, the number of points of
\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}_{N}
in
\mathrm{B}_{1}
is larger than
cN^{\mathsf{d}}
. The second moment of the number of perturbed points falling in
\mathrm{B}_{1}
is thus bounded below by:

\mathbb{E}[|\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}_{N}\cap\mathrm{B}_{1}|^{2}]\geq(cN^{\mathsf{d}})^{2}\times\mathbb{P}(\{C^{(0)}_{N}-\tau\in\mathrm{B}_{1}\}\})\geq c^{\prime}N^{\mathsf{d}}.

(4.1)

We are now ready to construct our example.

Infinite number variance without
\mathsf{d}
-th moment.

If
X
is a non-negative random variable, we let
\mathcal{N}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathsf{d}}}\lfloor X\rfloor
and we define our perturbed lattice as
\mathbf{PL}:=\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{N}}
, so that
\mathbf{PL}
is a mixture of the perturbed lattices
\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}_{N}
defined above, the parameter
N
being chosen at random according to
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathsf{d}}}\lfloor X\rfloor
.

On the one hand, since the perturbations in
\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}_{N}
are bounded almost surely by
\sqrt{\mathsf{d}}N
for all
N
, the size of a single perturbation
\mathrm{p}_{0}
in
\mathbf{PL}
is bounded by
X
almost surely. On the other hand, using (
4.1
) we see that:

\mathbb{E}[|\mathbf{PL}\cap\mathrm{B}_{1}|^{2}]\geq c^{\prime}\sum_{N\geq 10}N^{\mathsf{d}}\times\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{N}=N],\quad\text{ with }\mathcal{N}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathsf{d}}}\lfloor X\rfloor,

(4.2)

and thus if
X
fails to have a finite moment of order
\mathsf{d}
then certainly the variance of
|\mathbf{PL}\cap\mathrm{B}_{1}|
is infinite.
∎

4.2
Arbitrarily slow decay of the rescaled number variance: proof of Theorem
3

The processes that we just used for the proof of Proposition
4.1
can also be used to construct hyperuniform processes with arbitrarily slow decay of the rescaled number variance.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\tilde{\sigma}:(1,+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)
be a non-increasing function such that
\lim_{r\to\infty}\tilde{\sigma}(r)=0
. There exists a
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}
-invariant perturbation field
(\mathrm{p}_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}}
such that:

1.

The resulting perturbed lattice
\mathbf{PL}
is hyperuniform (i.e.
\lim_{r\to\infty}\sigma(r)=0
).

2.

There exist
c>0,M\geq 1
(depending on
\mathsf{d}
and
\sigma
) such that
\mathbf{PL}
satisfies, for
r\geq 1
:

\sigma(r):=\frac{\mathrm{Var}[|\mathbf{PL}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|]}{|\mathrm{B}_{r}|}\geq c\tilde{\sigma}(Mr).

(4.3)

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

The letters
c,c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime}
… denote small constants that may change from line to line.

We use the same family of processes
\{\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}_{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
as in the proof of Proposition
4.1
. With a similar argument as the one leading to (
4.1
), we find that if
r\geq 1
and
N\geq 10r
, we have:

\mathbb{E}[|\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}_{N}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|^{2}]-|\mathrm{B}_{r}|^{2}\geq cN^{2\mathsf{d}}\times\mathbb{P}(\{C^{(0)}_{N}-\tau\in\mathrm{B}_{r}\}\})-|\mathrm{B}_{r}|^{2}\geq c^{\prime}N^{\mathsf{d}}r^{\mathsf{d}}-|\mathrm{B}_{1}|^{2}r^{2\mathsf{d}},

because
\mathbb{P}(\{{C^{(0)}_{N}-\tau}\in\mathrm{B}_{r}\})
is larger than
c\frac{r^{\mathsf{d}}}{N^{\mathsf{d}}}
and under this event, all the
N^{\mathsf{d}}
points of
\Lambda_{N}-\tau
will “jump” inside
\mathrm{B}_{r}
. Taking
M
large enough (depending only on
\mathsf{d}
) we thus see that for
N\geq Mr
, we have:

\mathrm{Var}[|\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}_{N}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|]\geq c^{\prime\prime}N^{\mathsf{d}}r^{\mathsf{d}}.

(4.4)

Now let us consider a mixture of the
\{\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}_{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
of the form
\mathbf{PL}:=\sum_{N\geq 1}\alpha_{N}\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}_{N}
, with coefficients
\alpha_{N}
chosen as:

\alpha_{N}:=\frac{1}{S}\frac{-\tilde{\sigma}^{\prime}_{N}}{N^{\mathsf{d}}},\quad\tilde{\sigma}^{\prime}_{N}:=\tilde{\sigma}(N+1)-\tilde{\sigma}(N),\quad S:=\sum_{N\geq 1}\frac{-\tilde{\sigma}^{\prime}_{N}}{N^{\mathsf{d}}}.

(Note that thanks to a summation by parts, the series
\sum_{N\geq 1}\frac{-\tilde{\sigma}^{\prime}_{N}}{N^{\mathsf{d}}}
has the same nature as
\sum_{N\geq 1}\frac{\tilde{\sigma}_{N}}{N^{\mathsf{d}+1}}
, which converges for all
\mathsf{d}\geq 1
because
(\tilde{\sigma}_{N})_{N}
is bounded, and thus
S
is well-defined.) Using (
4.4
) and computing a telescopic sum, we obtain:

\frac{\mathrm{Var}[|\mathbf{PL}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|]}{|\mathrm{B}_{r}|}\geq c\sum_{N\geq Mr}\alpha_{N}N^{\mathsf{d}}=\frac{c}{S}\sum_{N\geq Mr}-\tilde{\sigma}^{\prime}_{N}\geq c\tilde{\sigma}(Mr),

(4.5)

for some positive constant
c
, which gives (
4.3
). It remains to prove that
\mathbf{PL}
is hyperuniform.

For
\mathsf{d}=1,2
we can compute, using again a telescopic sum, the
\mathsf{d}
-th moment of the perturbations:

\sum_{N\geq 1}\alpha_{N}\times N^{\mathsf{d}}=\frac{1}{S}\times\sum_{N\geq 1}-\tilde{\sigma}^{\prime}_{N}<+\infty,

and thus (by our main result)
\mathbf{PL}
is indeed hyperuniform. This condition is not sufficient in dimension
\mathsf{d}\geq 3
, but we can instead directly find an upper bound on the number variance. Let us return to the “building block”
\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}_{N}
and observe that:

•

If
N\geq r
, then
\mathrm{Var}[|\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}_{N}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|]=\mathcal{O}(r^{\mathsf{d}}N^{\mathsf{d}})
. Indeed, with probability
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{r^{\mathsf{d}}}{N^{\mathsf{d}}}\right)
the ball
\mathrm{B}_{r}
receives
N^{\mathsf{d}}
points, and it loses all of its
\mathcal{O}(r^{\mathsf{d}})
points with probability
1-\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{r^{\mathsf{d}}}{N^{\mathsf{d}}}\right)
.

•

If
r\geq N
we have:

\mathrm{Var}[|\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}_{N}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|]\preceq\frac{r^{\mathsf{d}-1}}{N^{\mathsf{d}-1}}\times N^{2\mathsf{d}}=\mathcal{O}\left(r^{\mathsf{d}-1}N^{\mathsf{d}+1}\right).

Indeed, there are
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{r^{\mathsf{d}-1}}{N^{\mathsf{d}-1}}\right)
hypercubes of sidelength
N
intersecting the hypersphere
\partial\mathrm{B}_{r}
, and those are the only ones contributing (independently) to the variance, each one with a contribution
\mathcal{O}(N^{2\mathsf{d}})
.

Summing over
N
, we obtain:

\frac{\mathrm{Var}[|\mathbf{PL}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|]}{|\mathrm{B}_{r}|}\preceq\frac{1}{r}\sum_{N\leq r}\alpha_{N}N^{\mathsf{d}+1}+\sum_{N\geq r}\alpha_{N}N^{\mathsf{d}}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{N\leq r}-\tilde{\sigma}^{\prime}_{N}N+\sum_{N\geq r}-\tilde{\sigma}^{\prime}_{N}\preceq\frac{1}{r}\sum_{N\leq r}\tilde{\sigma}_{N}+\tilde{\sigma}(r)\to 0,

so the process that we constructed is indeed hyperuniform.

∎

Remark 4.3
.

For
\mathsf{d}=1
, using the same construction with
\alpha_{N}
of order
N^{-3+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}
for some
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
gives a perturbation such that:

•

\sum_{N\geq 1}\alpha_{N}N^{2-\varepsilon}<+\infty
, hence
\mathbb{E}[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|^{2-\varepsilon}]
is finite.

•

In view of the first inequality in (
4.5
),

\frac{\mathrm{Var}[|\mathbf{PL}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|]}{|\mathrm{B}_{r}|}\geq c\sum_{N\geq Mr}\alpha_{N}N=c^{\prime}\sum_{N\geq Mr}N^{-2+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\geq c^{\prime\prime}r^{-1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}

which means that the resulting perturbed lattice is
not
type-I hyperuniform.

This confirms our “sharpness” claim in the first item of Theorem
2
.

4.3
Arbitrarily small perturbations with large number variance

We now prove the fourth item of Theorem
1
as well as the second item of Theorem
2
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
.

•

If
\mathsf{d}\geq 3
, there are perturbations of
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}
that are almost surely bounded by
\varepsilon
and such that

\lim_{r\to+\infty}\frac{\mathrm{Var}\left[|\mathbf{PL}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|\right]}{|\mathrm{B}_{r}|}=+\infty

•

If
\mathsf{d}=2
, there are perturbations of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
that are almost surely bounded by
\varepsilon
and such that
\mathbf{PL}
is not hyperuniform of class I.

Proof.

Let
N\geq 10
and let
(C^{(k)}_{N})_{k\in N\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}}
be independent random variables such that
C^{(k)}_{N}
is uniformly distributed in
\Lambda^{(k)}_{N}
for all
k\in N\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}
. We define a first “displacement field”
\tilde{\mathrm{p}}_{N}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
by setting:

\tilde{\mathrm{p}}_{N}(x):=\varepsilon\frac{x-C^{(k)}_{N}}{|x-C^{(k)}_{N}|}\text{ for all $k\in N\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}$ and all $x$ in $\Lambda^{(k)}_{N}$. }

Applying
\tilde{\mathrm{p}}_{N}
corresponds to choosing a center
C^{(k)}_{N}
uniformly at random in each cube
\Lambda^{(k)}_{N}
and then push every point in
\Lambda^{(k)}_{N}
by an amount
\varepsilon
away from
C^{(k)}_{N}
. This displacement field is clearly
N\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}
-stationary, and we can turn it into a
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
-stationary field by choosing a random vector
\tau
uniformly distributed in
\Lambda_{N}
and independent of
\tilde{\mathrm{p}}_{N}
and setting
\bar{\mathrm{p}}_{N}:=\tilde{\mathrm{p}}_{N}(\cdot+\tau)
. Perturbations are bounded by
\varepsilon
. Finally, we set for a
[0,1]^{\mathsf{d}}
-uniform random variable
U
(idependent of everything else):

\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}_{N}:=\{x+U+\bar{\mathrm{p}}_{N}(x+U),\ x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}}\}.

Next, let
r
be such that
{N\geq r}
. We want to count the number of points of
\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}_{N}
falling in
\mathrm{B}_{r}
, and we will show that a significant amount of the original lattice points are “leaving” the ball with positive probability, creating a very large number variance if
\mathsf{d}\geq 3
and a critical number variance if
\mathsf{d}=2
. We rely on the following elementary computation.

Claim 4.5
.

Let
r>0,x,u,\tau,C\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
be such that:

\frac{9}{10}r\leq|x+u|\leq\frac{11}{10}r,\quad|\tau-C|\leq\frac{1}{10}r.

Then we have:

\left|x+u+\varepsilon\frac{x+u+\tau-C}{|x+u+\tau-C|}\right|\geq|x+u|+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.

(4.6)

Proof of Claim
4.5
.

We simply compute:

\left|x+u+\varepsilon\frac{x+u+\tau-C}{|x+u+\tau-C|}\right|^{2}=|x+u|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}+2\varepsilon(x+u)\cdot\frac{x+u+\tau-C}{|x+u+\tau-C|}.

With our assumptions, we know that
\frac{8}{9}|x+u|\leq|x+u+\tau-C|\leq\frac{10}{9}|x+u|
, and thus:

(x+u)\cdot\frac{x+u+\tau-C}{|x+u+\tau-C|}=|x+u|^{2}\frac{1}{|x+u+\tau-C|}+(x+u)\cdot(\tau-C)\frac{1}{|x+u+\tau-C|}\\
\geq|x+u|^{2}\times\frac{9}{10|x+u|}-|x+u|\times\frac{1}{10}r\times\frac{9}{8|x+u|}\geq\frac{9}{10}\times\frac{9}{10}r-\frac{9}{80}r\geq\frac{1}{2}r.

As a consequence, we see that (using again
|x+u|\leq\frac{11}{10}r
):

\left|x+u+\varepsilon\frac{x+u+\tau-C}{|x+u+\tau-C|}\right|^{2}\geq|x+u|^{2}+\varepsilon r+\varepsilon^{2}\geq|x+u|^{2}+2\frac{10}{2\times 11}|x+u|\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{2}\geq\left(|x+u|+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\right)^{2},

which proves the claim.
∎

Since
\tau
and
C^{(0)}_{N}
are uniformly distributed in
\Lambda_{N}
and independent, there exists
c>0
depending only on the dimension, such that:

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{|\tau|\leq\frac{r}{10}\right\}\cap\left\{\left|\tau-C^{(0)}_{N}\right|\leq\frac{r}{10}\right\}\right)\geq c{\frac{r^{2\mathsf{d}}}{N^{2\mathsf{d}}}}.

(4.7)

This event is interesting for us due to the following observation:

Claim 4.6
.

On the event
\left\{|\tau|\leq\frac{r}{10}\right\}\cap\left\{\left|\tau-C^{(0)}_{N}\right|\leq\frac{r}{10}\right\}
, for all
u\in[0,1]^{\mathsf{d}}
, if

\frac{9}{10}r\leq|x+u|\leq\frac{11}{10}r
then
|x+u+\bar{\mathrm{p}}(x+u)|\geq|x+u|+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}
.

In particular, if
x
is such that
|x+u|\geq r-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}
then
x+u+\bar{\mathrm{p}}(x+u)\notin\mathrm{B}_{r}
.

Proof of Claim
4.6
.

If
|x+u|\leq\frac{11}{10r}
then
|x+u+\tau|\leq\frac{12}{10}r
, which means that
x+u+\tau\in\Lambda^{(0)}_{N}
(because
N\geq 5r
) and thus, by construction of the displacement field:

\bar{\mathrm{p}}(x+u)=\tilde{\mathrm{p}}_{N}(x+u+\tau)=\varepsilon\frac{x+u+\tau-C^{(0)}_{N}}{|x+u+\tau-C^{(0)}_{N}|}.

We may then apply Claim
4.5
with
C=C^{(0)}_{N}
, which yields
|x+u+\bar{\mathrm{p}}(x+u)|\geq|x+u|+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}
. Next, let
x
be such that
|x+u|\geq r-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}
.

1.

If
|x+u|\geq\frac{11}{10}r
then
x+u+\bar{\mathrm{p}}(x+u)
cannot be in
\mathrm{B}_{r}
because perturbations are bounded by
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{10}r
.

2.

If
|x+u|\leq\frac{11}{10}r
, then since by assumption
|x+u|\geq r-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\geq\frac{9}{10}r
we get from above that:

|x+u+\bar{\mathrm{p}}(x+u)|\geq|x+u|+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\geq r-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}>r

and thus
x+u+\bar{\mathrm{p}}(x+u)
is not in
\mathrm{B}_{r}
.

∎

From Claim
4.6
we deduce that on the event
\left\{|\tau|\leq\frac{r}{10}\right\}\cap\left\{\left|\tau-C^{(0)}_{N}\right|\leq\frac{r}{10}\right\}
we have:

\left|\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}_{N}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}\right|\leq\left|\left\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}},|x+U|\leq r-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right\}\right|\text{ a.s.}

(4.8)

We are thus left with finding an upper bound for the number of integer points in the ball
\mathrm{B}_{r-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}}(-U)
, which we expect from the continuous approximation to be of order
|\mathrm{B}_{r}|-Cr^{\mathsf{d}-1}\varepsilon
, and thus significantly smaller than
|\mathrm{B}_{r}|
. Although the quest for sharp bounds seems to still be an active area of research, “basic” estimates are sufficient for us and have thankfully been known for a long time - as the classical result of Landau
[
16
]
, which gives:

\sup_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}}\left|\left|\left\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}},x\in\mathrm{B}_{r}(a)\right\}\right|-|\mathrm{B}_{r}|\right|\leq C_{\mathsf{d}}r^{\frac{\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{d}-1)}{\mathsf{d}+1}}\ll r^{\mathsf{d}-1},

(4.9)

so in particular it ensures that for all
u\in[0,1]^{d}
we have:

\left|\left\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{d}},|x+u|\leq r-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right\}\right|=|\mathrm{B}_{r-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}}|+o(r^{\mathsf{d}-1})\leq|\mathrm{B}_{r}|-C_{\mathsf{d}}r^{\mathsf{d}-1}\varepsilon+o(r^{\mathsf{d}-1}),

(4.10)

so in particular
|\mathrm{B}_{r}|-\left|\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}_{N}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}\right|\geq C^{\prime}_{\mathsf{d}}r^{\mathsf{d}-1}\varepsilon
.
From this and our lower bound (
4.7
) on the probability of
\left\{|\tau|\leq\frac{r}{10}\right\}\cap\left\{\left|\tau-C^{(0)}_{N}\right|\leq\frac{r}{10}\right\}
we deduce:

\text{For $r,N$ large enough {with $N\geq r$},}\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\left||\mathrm{B}_{r}|-|\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}_{N}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|\right|^{2}\right]\geq c_{\mathsf{d}}r^{2\mathsf{d}-2}{\times\frac{r^{2\mathsf{d}}}{N^{2\mathsf{d}}}},

(4.11)

for some positive constant
c_{\mathsf{d}}
depending on the dimension. We are now ready to construct our examples.

Non-hyperuniformity for
\mathsf{d}\geq 3
.
If
\mathsf{d}\geq 3
, fix some integer-valued random variable
\mathcal{N}
with
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{N}=N]
proportional to
\frac{1}{N^{1+\delta}}
for
N\geq 1
(where
\delta\in(0,1)
is arbitrary), and set
\mathbf{PL}:=\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{N}}
i.e.
\mathbf{PL}
is a mixture of the perturbed lattices
\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}_{N}
defined above, the parameter
N
being chosen at random. For any
r
, a lower bound on the number variance in
\mathrm{B}_{r}
is given by:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left||\mathbf{PL}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|-|\mathrm{B}_{r}|\right|^{2}\right]\geq\sum_{N\geq r}\mathbb{E}\left[\left||\mathbf{PL}^{\prime}_{N}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|-|\mathrm{B}_{r}|\right|^{2}\right]\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{N}=N].

(4.12)

From (
4.11
) we deduce that, for
r
large enough (depending on
\mathsf{d}
):

\frac{\mathrm{Var}[|\mathbf{PL}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|]}{|\mathrm{B}_{r}|}\geq c_{\mathsf{d}}\frac{r^{2\mathsf{d}-2}}{r^{\mathsf{d}}}\times\sum_{5r\geq N\geq r}\frac{1}{N^{1+\delta}},

which after a simple computation is seen to be greater than
r^{2\mathsf{d}-2-\mathsf{d}-\delta}
, which is
\gg 1
for all
\mathsf{d}\geq 3
.

Non-class-I hyperuniformity for
\mathsf{d}=2
.

The same construction for
\mathsf{d}=2
yields
\frac{\mathrm{Var}[|\mathbf{PL}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|]}{|\mathrm{B}_{r}|}\geq r^{-\delta}
where
\delta>0
can be arbitrary small, hence this process is not class I hyperuniform.

Note that one could go further and consider coefficients
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{N}=N]
proportional to
\frac{1}{N\log^{1+\delta}N}
, which would yield a decay of the rescaled number variance slower than any power law.
∎

Appendix A
The case of dimension 1

A.1
Expressing the number variance in balls

An auxiliary function

For
r>0
, a classical computation shows that the Fourier transform of the indicator function of a ball of radius
r
is given by:
\widehat{\mathsf{1}_{\mathrm{B}_{r}}}=\frac{\mathrm{J}_{\mathsf{d}/2}(r|t|)}{|t|^{\frac{\mathsf{d}}{2}}}r^{\frac{\mathsf{d}}{2}}
, where
\mathrm{J}_{\mathsf{d}/2}
is a Bessel function of the first kind (whose definition is not needed here). As a consequence, we have
\frac{\widehat{\mathsf{1}_{\mathrm{B}_{r}}\ast\mathsf{1}_{\mathrm{B}_{r}}}}{|\mathrm{B}_{r}|}=C_{\mathsf{d}}\frac{\mathrm{J}_{\mathsf{d}/2}(r|t|)^{2}}{|t|^{\mathsf{d}}}
for some constant
C_{\mathsf{d}}
. We define the function
j_{r}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
by:
j_{r}:t\mapsto C^{\prime}_{\mathsf{d}}\frac{\mathrm{J}_{\mathsf{d}/2}(r|t|)^{2}}{|t|^{\mathsf{d}}}
with the right constant
C^{\prime}_{\mathsf{d}}
so that its Fourier transform satisfies:

\widehat{j_{r}}=\frac{\\
1_{\mathrm{B}_{r}}\ast\mathsf{1}_{\mathrm{B}_{r}}}{|\mathrm{B}_{r}|}.

(A.1)

For all
r>0
, we have
\widehat{j_{r}}(0)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}}j_{r}(t)\mathtt{d}t=1
and

j_{r}(0)=\frac{|\mathrm{B}_{r}|}{(2\pi)^{d}}

The family of functions
\{j_{r}\}_{r\geq 1}
is an approximation of unity: the mass stays constant equal to
1
and the functions converge pointwise to
0
on
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}\setminus\{0\}
, while the value at
0
tends to
+\infty
.

Expressing the number variance

When
\mathbf{X}
is a random point configuration, the following is well-known (see e.g.
[
4
, Sec. 1.2]
):

Lemma A.1
.

Assume
\mathbf{X}
is stationary, with intensity
1
and locally square integrable. For all
r>0
:

\frac{\mathrm{Var}\left[|\mathbf{X}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|\right]}{|\mathrm{B}_{r}|}=\left\langle\delta_{0}+(\alpha_{\mathrm{red}}^{(2)}-1),\frac{\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{B}_{r}}\ast\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{B}_{r}}}{|\mathrm{B}_{r}|}\right\rangle=\left\langle\alpha_{\mathrm{red}}^{(2)},\frac{\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{B}_{r}}\ast\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{B}_{r}}}{|\mathrm{B}_{r}|}\right\rangle+1-|\mathrm{B}_{r}|.

(A.2)

In view of (
A.1
) one can re-write (
A.2
) as:

\frac{\mathrm{Var}\left[|\mathbf{X}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|\right]}{|\mathrm{B}_{r}|}=\left\langle\alpha_{\mathrm{red}}^{(2)},\widehat{j_{r}}\right\rangle+1-|\mathrm{B}_{r}|,

(A.3)

and the hyperuniformity of
\mathbf{X}
is thus equivalent to having
\left\langle\alpha_{\mathrm{red}}^{(2)},\widehat{j_{r}}\right\rangle=|\mathrm{B}_{r}|-1+o(1)
, while class I hyperuniformity is equivalent to
\left\langle\alpha_{\mathrm{red}}^{(2)},\widehat{j_{r}}\right\rangle=|\mathrm{B}_{r}|-1+\mathcal{O}(r^{-1})
as
r\to\infty
.

Perturbed lattices in dimension 1

Proposition A.2
.

If
\mathbb{E}[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|]
is finite, then
\mathbf{PL}
is hyperuniform. If
\mathbb{E}[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|^{2}]
is finite, then
\mathbf{PL}
is class-I hyperuniform.

Proof.

We start with introducing an auxiliary function.

Preliminary

We record the following elementary lemma.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
be bounded and compactly supported in
\mathrm{B}_{m}
for some
m\geq 1
. Assume that
M\geq 10m
. We have:

\mathbb{E}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{L},|x|\geq M}f(x+\mathrm{p}_{x}-\mathrm{p}_{0})\preceq\|f\|_{\infty}\times\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|^{\mathsf{d}}\times\mathbf{1}\left\{|\mathrm{p}_{0}|\geq\frac{1}{4}M\right\}\right].

(A.4)

Note that if
\mathbb{E}[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|^{\mathsf{d}}]<+\infty
, then the right-hand side of (
A.4
) tends to
0
as
M\to\infty
.

Proof of Lemma
A.3
.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
0\leq f\leq 1
. Let
|x|\geq M
. Since
f
is supported on
\mathrm{B}_{m}
, then for
f(x+\mathrm{p}_{x}-\mathrm{p}_{0})
to not be
0
we need
|\mathrm{p}_{x}-\mathrm{p}_{0}|\geq|x|-m\geq\frac{1}{2}|x|
, so
\max(|\mathrm{p}_{x}|,|\mathrm{p}_{0}|)\geq\frac{1}{4}{|x|}
. We thus have, since
\mathrm{p}_{x}
and
\mathrm{p}_{0}
are identically distributed:

\sum_{|x|\geq M}\mathbb{E}f(x+\mathrm{p}_{x}-\mathrm{p}_{0})\preceq\sum_{|x|\geq M}\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathrm{p}_{0}|\geq\frac{1}{4}|x|\right),

and by elementary lattice counting this discrete sum is comparable to the integral:

\int_{s\geq\frac{M}{4}}s^{\mathsf{d}-1}\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathrm{p}_{0}|\geq s\right)\preceq\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|^{\mathsf{d}}\mathbf{1}\left\{|\mathrm{p}_{0}|\geq\frac{M}{4}\right\}\right].

∎

Main computation

We start by writing, using (
A.3
) and the expression for
\alpha_{\mathrm{red}}^{(2)}
found in (
2.3
):

\frac{\mathrm{Var}\left[|\mathbf{PL}\cap\mathrm{B}_{r}|\right]}{|\mathrm{B}_{r}|}=\mathbb{E}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{0\}}\widehat{j_{r}}(x+\mathrm{p}_{x}-\mathrm{p}_{0})+1-|\mathrm{B}_{r}|=A+B,

where
A
is a deterministic contribution coming from the lattice
\mathbb{Z}
and
B
is the “error term” due to the perturbations:

A:=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\widehat{j_{r}}(x)+1-|\mathrm{B}_{r}|,\quad B:=\mathbb{E}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\widehat{j_{r}}(x+\mathrm{p}_{x}-\mathrm{p}_{0})-\widehat{j_{r}}(x).

The term
A
is
\mathcal{O}(r^{-1})
because a stationary lattice is class-I hyperuniform, and we focus on
B
.

We use Lemma
A.3
and write:

B=\mathbb{E}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},|x|\leq 100r}\widehat{j_{r}}(x+\mathrm{p}_{x}-\mathrm{p}_{0})-\widehat{j_{r}}(x)+\mathcal{O}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|\mathsf{1}_{|\mathrm{p}_{0}|\geq 10r}\right]\right).

(A.5)

Note that if
\mathbb{E}[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|]
is finite then the error term tends to
0
as
r\to\infty
and if
\mathbb{E}[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|^{2}]
is finite, then by Markov’s inequality it is actually
\mathcal{O}(r^{-1})
.

The function
\widehat{j_{r}}
is a “tent” supported on
[-2r,2r]
and whose expression is given by
\widehat{j_{r}}(t)=1-\frac{|t|}{2r}
on that interval. In particular, it is continuous,
\mathcal{O}(r^{-1})
-Lipschitz and piecewise affine, with a lack of differentiability at
0
and
\pm 2r
. For
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, we let
d:=\mathrm{dist}\left(x,\{0,-2r,2r\}\right)
. We introduce some lengthscale
\bar{d}
to be chosen later with
1\leq\bar{d}\ll r
. First, using the Lipschitz bound on
\widehat{j_{r}}
we have:

\mathbb{E}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z},d\leq\bar{d}}\widehat{j_{r}}(x+\mathrm{p}_{x}-\mathrm{p}_{0})-\widehat{j_{r}}(x)\preceq\frac{\bar{d}}{r}\times\mathbb{E}[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|].

(A.6)

Next, if
x
is such that
d\geq\bar{d}
, we write:

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{j_{r}}(x+\mathrm{p}_{x}-\mathrm{p}_{0})-\widehat{j_{r}}(x)]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widehat{j_{r}}(x+\mathrm{p}_{x}-\mathrm{p}_{0})-\widehat{j_{r}}(x)\right)\mathsf{1}_{|\mathrm{p}_{x}-\mathrm{p}_{0}|\leq d}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widehat{j_{r}}(x+\mathrm{p}_{x}-\mathrm{p}_{0})-\widehat{j_{r}}(x)\right)\mathsf{1}_{|\mathrm{p}_{x}-\mathrm{p}_{0}|\geq d}\right].

Since
\widehat{j_{r}}
is affine on
(x-d,x+d)
with slope
\mathcal{O}(r^{-1})
, and
\mathrm{p}_{x}-\mathrm{p}_{0}
is centered, we have:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widehat{j_{r}}(x+\mathrm{p}_{x}-\mathrm{p}_{0})-\widehat{j_{r}}(x)\right)\mathsf{1}_{|\mathrm{p}_{x}-\mathrm{p}_{0}|\leq d}\right]\leq\frac{1}{r}\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathrm{p}_{x}-\mathrm{p}_{0}|\mathsf{1}_{|\mathrm{p}_{x}-\mathrm{p}_{0}|\geq d}\right]\preceq\frac{1}{r}\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|\mathsf{1}_{|\mathrm{p}_{0}|\geq\frac{d}{2}}\right].

It remains to see that, since
\widehat{j_{r}}
is
\mathcal{O}(r^{-1})
-Lipschitz:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widehat{j_{r}}(x+\mathrm{p}_{x}-\mathrm{p}_{0})-\widehat{j_{r}}(x)\right)\mathsf{1}_{|\mathrm{p}_{x}-\mathrm{p}_{0}|\geq d}\right]\preceq\frac{1}{r}\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathrm{p}_{x}-\mathrm{p}_{0}|\mathsf{1}_{|\mathrm{p}_{x}-\mathrm{p}_{0}|\geq d}\right]\preceq\frac{1}{r}\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|\mathsf{1}_{|\mathrm{p}_{0}|\geq\frac{d}{2}}\right],

(A.7)

from which we can derive the following simple bound:

\mathbb{E}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z},d\geq\bar{d},|x|\leq 100r}\widehat{j_{r}}(x+\mathrm{p}_{x}-\mathrm{p}_{0})-\widehat{j_{r}}(x)\preceq\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|\mathsf{1}_{|\mathrm{p}_{0}|\geq\frac{\bar{d}}{2}}\right].

(A.8)

If
\mathbb{E}[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|]
is finite, the right-hand side goes to
0
as soon as
\bar{d}\to\infty
. On the other hand, if
\mathbb{E}[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|^{2}]
is finite, we might prefer to return to (
A.7
) and compute instead the sum:

\frac{1}{r}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z},|x|\leq 100r,d\geq\bar{d}}\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|\mathsf{1}_{|\mathrm{p}_{0}|\geq\frac{d}{2}}\right]\preceq\frac{1}{r}\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|\sum_{d=\bar{d}}^{100r}\mathsf{1}_{|\mathrm{p}_{0}|\geq\frac{d}{2}}\right]\preceq\frac{1}{r}\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}(r^{-1}).

(A.9)

In summary, if
\mathbb{E}[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|]
is finite, we can choose
1\ll\bar{d}\ll r
in (
A.6
), (
A.8
) and see that the term
B
goes to
0
as
r\to\infty
, so
\mathbf{PL}
is hyperuniform. If moreover
\mathbb{E}[|\mathrm{p}_{0}|^{2}]
is finite then we can choose
\bar{d}=1
in (
A.6
), (
A.9
) and we find that
B=\mathcal{O}(r^{-1})
thus
\mathbf{PL}
is class-I hyperuniform.
∎
