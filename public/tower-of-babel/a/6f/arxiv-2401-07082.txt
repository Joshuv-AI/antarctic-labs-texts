Bernstein-Sato theory modulo p m p^{m}
Thomas Bitoun, Eamon Quinlan-Gallego

Abstract
For fixed prime integer p > 0 p>0 we develop a notion of Bernstein-Sato polynomial for polynomials with ℤ / p m \mathbb{Z}/p^{m} -coefficients, compatible with existing theory in the case m = 1 m=1 . We show that the “roots” of such polynomials are rational and we show that the negative roots agree with those of the mod- p p reduction. We give examples to show that, surprisingly, roots may be positive in this context. Moreover, our construction allows us to define a notion of “strength” for roots by measuring p p -torsion, and we show that “strong” roots give rise to roots in characteristic zero through mod- p p reduction.

Introduction

Let
R:=\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial ring over
\mathbb{C}
and let
\mathcal{D}_{R}:=\mathbb{C}\langle x_{i},\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\rangle
be the Weyl algebra. Given a nonzero
f\in R
, its Bernstein-Sato polynomial
b_{f}(s)\in\mathbb{C}[s]
is the monic polynomial of smallest degree satisfying a functional equation of the form

b_{f}(s)f^{s}=P(s)f^{s+1}

for some
P(s)\in\mathcal{D}_{R}[s]
. Such a
b_{f}(s)
always exists, and its roots are rational and negative by a theorem of Kashiwara
[
Kas77
]
.

This Bernstein-Sato polynomial detects important information about the singularities of
f
. For example, Kashiwara and Malgrange showed that one can recover the eigenvalues of the monodromy action on the nearby cycles from the roots of
b_{f}(s)

[
Kas83
]
,
[
Mal83
]
. Kollár also showed that the log-canonical threshold of
f
is the negative of the largest root of the Bernstein-Sato polynomial
[
Kol96
]
, and afterwards Ein, Lazarsfeld, Smith and Varolin proved that whenever
\alpha\in(0,1]
is a jumping number for the multiplier ideals of
f
then this
\alpha
must be a root of
b_{f}(-s)

[
ELSV04
]
.

In the hope of building new invariants of singularities we develop a notion of “Bernstein-Sato polynomial” in the case where one replaces the base
\mathbb{C}
by
\mathbb{Z}/p^{m+1}
, where
p
is a prime number (the theory works over a more general base, see Setup
2.1
, but we focus on this case here for simplicity). This can be seen as a necessary step in developing a genuinely
p
-adic theory. Note that the case
m=0
(i.e., when one works over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
) has already been treated in work of Mustaţă
[
Mus09
]
and the first author
[
Bit18
]
(see also
[
BS16
]

[
QG21b
]

[
QG21a
]

[
JNBQG23
]
for more references).

The replacement of
\mathbb{C}
with the base
\mathbb{Z}/p^{m+1}
in the definition of
b_{f}(s)
has two major components, which we illustrate with the following questions.

(1)

What should play the role of the algebra
\mathbb{C}\langle x_{i},\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\rangle
?

(2)

How does one give a “functional equation” in this setting?

Let us briefly describe how these are dealt with.

We address question (1) by considering the ring of differential operators as defined by Grothendieck
[
Gro65
, Ch. 16]
, which assigns to every commutative algebra
R
over a commutative ring
A
a subring
\mathcal{D}_{R|A}\subseteq\End_{A}(R)
of the ring
\End_{A}(R)
of
A
-linear endomorphisms of
R
. We will write this ring as
\mathcal{D}_{R}
whenever
A
is clear from the context, and we note that it is noncommutative in general.

We refer the reader to Subsection
2.2
for the precise definition. For now, let us mention that in the case where
R:=A[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
is a polynomial ring over
A
, one can give algebra generators for
\mathcal{D}_{R}
as follows:

\mathcal{D}_{R}=A\langle x_{i},\partial_{i}^{[k]}\ |\ i=1,\dots,n;k=1,2,\cdots\rangle,

where through an abuse of notation one thinks of
x_{i}\in\End_{A}(R)
as the operator that multiplies by the variable
x_{i}
, and
\partial_{i}^{[k]}\in\End_{A}(R)
is the operator determined by

\displaystyle\partial_{i}^{[k]}(x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}})

\displaystyle:={a_{i}\choose k}x_{i}^{-k}x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{i}(a_{i}-1)(a_{i}-2)\cdots(a_{i}-k+1)}{k!}x_{i}^{-k}x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}}

(recall that
{a_{i}\choose k}
is always an integer, and hence this is defined regardless of whether one can divide by
k!
in
A
; note also that
{a_{i}\choose k}=0
for
a_{i}<k
and hence the above is indeed an element of
R
). Moreover, when
A=\mathbb{C}
(or any field of characteristic zero), we have
\partial_{i}^{[k]}=\partial_{i}^{k}/k!
, and one recovers
\mathcal{D}_{R}=\mathbb{C}\langle x_{i},\partial_{i}\rangle
. Finally, let us mention that whenever
A=\mathbb{Z}/p^{m+1}
we give an alternative characterization of this algebra in Proposition
2.18
.

For question (2) we use an alternative description of the Bernstein-Sato polynomial that translates naturally to an arbitrary base
A
. Basically, if
R:=A[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
is a polynomial ring over
A
and
f\in R
is a nonzerodivisor, one considers the polynomial ring
R[t]
in one extra variable, to which one gives a
\mathbb{N}
-grading by declaring
\deg x_{i}=0
,
\deg t=1
; this induces a
\mathbb{Z}
-grading on
\mathcal{D}_{R[t]}
. One then assigns to
f
a module
N_{f}
over the ring
(\mathcal{D}_{R[t]})_{0}
of differential operators of degree zero (we refer the reader to Section
3
for a detailed description of
N_{f}
). In general, every differential operator on
R
induces a differential operator on
R[t]
, giving an inclusion
\mathcal{D}_{R}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{R[t]}
.

Let us describe what happens in the classical case
A=\mathbb{C}
. Here we get
(\mathcal{D}_{R[t]})_{0}=\mathcal{D}_{R}[s]
, where
s:=-\partial_{t}t
, and the Bernstein-Sato polynomial is then the minimal polynomial for the action of
s
on
N_{f}
. Note that
N_{f}
is in general infinite dimensional over
\mathbb{C}
, and hence the fact that
b_{f}(s)\neq 0
is far from trivial, but nonetheless true. From this one concludes that
N_{f}
splits as a direct sum
N_{f}=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}}(N_{f})_{\lambda}
of generalized eigenspaces for the action of
s
, and moreover one can recover the roots of
b_{f}(s)
from this decomposition by

\{\text{Roots of $b_{f}(s)$}\}=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\ |\ (N_{f})_{\lambda}\neq 0\}.

One can also think of this fact geometrically: if one thinks of the
\mathbb{C}[s]
-module
N_{f}
as a sheaf on
\Spec\mathbb{C}[s]=\mathbb{A}^{1}_{\mathbb{C}}
then it is supported at a finite number of points, and these points are precisely the roots of
b_{f}(s)
. As we will see, when working over
\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}
one thinks of this finite support property as an analogue of the “existence theorem” for Bernstein-Sato polynomials.

As mentioned, the construction of the
(\mathcal{D}_{R[t]})_{0}
-module
N_{f}
can be carried over an arbitrary base
A
, and in particular we can consider the case where
A=\mathbb{Z}/p^{m+1}
. The most striking difference in this situation is that
(\mathcal{D}_{R[t]})_{0}
has nonredundant higher order differential operators

(\mathcal{D}_{R[t]})_{0}=\mathcal{D}_{R}[-\partial_{t}t,-\partial^{[p]}t^{p},-\partial^{[p^{2}]}t^{p^{2}},\dots],

and in particular the role of
\mathbb{C}[s]
is now played by the algebra

C_{m}:=(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m+1})[-\partial_{t}t,-\partial^{[p]}t^{p},-\partial^{[p^{2}]}t^{p^{2}},\dots].

We refer the reader to Subsection
2.3
for more usable characterizations of this algebra. For now, let us just mention the following fact (note that in the case
m=0
these facts already appear in
[
Bit18
]

[
JNBQG23
]
).

Proposition 1.1

(see Subsection 2.3)
.

The algebra
C_{m}
is commutative, all of its prime ideals are maximal, and
\Spec(C_{m})
is homeomorphic to the space
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
of
p
-adic integers equipped with
p
-adic topology.

In particular, to each
p
-adic integer
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
one can assign a maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}\subseteq C_{m}
, and every prime ideal of
C_{m}
arises this way. From now on let us identify
\Spec(C_{m})=\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, and hence we think of
N_{f}
as a sheaf on
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.

Let us summarize what is known about the case
m=0
. The following theorem of the first author can be seen as an analogue of the existence of Bernstein-Sato polynomials, as an analogue of Kashiwara’s theorem of rationality and negativity of their roots, and as an analogue of the theorem of Ein-Lazarsfeld-Smith-Varolin that connects these roots to jumping numbers.

Theorem/Definition 1.2

(
[
Bit18
]
)
.

Suppose
f\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
is a nonzero polynomial. The module
N_{f}
is supported at a finite number of
p
-adic integers
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. These
p
-adic integers are called the Bernstein-Sato roots of
f
, and we denote this collection by
\BSR(f)\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.

One has

\BSR(f)=-\big(\FJN(f)\cap\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}\cap(0,1]\big),

where
\FJN(f)
denotes the collection of
F
-jumping numbers of
f
. In particular, the Bernstein-Sato roots of
f
are rational, negative, and lie in the interval
[-1,0)
.

This result raises the question of whether one can develop a theory that also includes roots smaller than
-1
, and whether one can assign a multiplicity to each root (in characteristic zero, the roots that are smaller than
-1
carry important information about the singularities of
f
; see for example
[
Sai93
]

[
MP20
]
).

Let us now talk about our results; in summary, we show that passing to arbitrary
m
does not produce new negative Bernstein-Sato roots, but that (surprisingly) Bernstein-Sato roots can be positive in this setting. We also develop a multiplicity-like invariant that, despite not providing a good analogue for multiplicity, does give interesting information.

We begin by proving the finite support statement for the module
N_{f}
, which we use to define Bernstein-Sato roots.

Theorem/Definition 1.3

(Def.
3.3
, Thm.
4.2
)
.

Let
f\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m+1})[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a nonzerodivisor. Then the
C_{m}
-module
N_{f}
is supported at a finite number of
p
-adic integers
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. These
p
-adic integers are called the Bernstein-Sato roots of
f
, and we denote this collection by
\BSR(f)\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.

We are able to show that these Bernstein-Sato roots are rational. From existing theory in characteristic 0 and characteristic
p>0
(
m=0
), one would expect them to be negative. We give examples to show that this need not be the case (Example
4.14
), but we prove that the roots that
are
negative agree with the Bernstein-Sato roots of the mod-
p
reduction of
f
. We also show that every positive root is an integer translate of a negative one. In summary:

Theorem 1.4

(Thm.
4.6
,
4.12
,
4.15
)
.

Let
f\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m+1})[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a nonzerodivisor, and let
f_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be its mod-
p
reduction.

(i)

The Bernstein-Sato roots of
f
are rational, i.e.,
\BSR(f)\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}
.

(ii)

We have
\BSR(f)\cap(\mathbb{Z}_{(p)})_{<0}=\BSR(f_{0}).

(iii)

We have
\BSR(f)+\mathbb{Z}=\BSR(f_{0})+\mathbb{Z}
.

Given a
p
-adic integer
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, the powers of the maximal ideals
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}\subseteq C_{m}
form a decreasing chain, which takes the form:

\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}\supseteq\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}^{2}\supseteq\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}^{3}\supseteq\cdots\supseteq\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}^{m+1}=\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}^{m+2}=\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}^{m+3}=\cdots

(in particular, when
m=0
the ideals
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}
are idempotent; that is,
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}=\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}^{2}
). Given a Bernstein-Sato root
\alpha
for
f
, we define the number

\str(\alpha,f):=\min\{k\geq 0\ |\ \mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}^{k}(N_{f})_{\alpha}=0\},

where
(N_{f})_{\alpha}
is the stalk of
N_{f}
at
\alpha
(note that in characteristic zero this gives the multiplicity of a root
\alpha
). Some quick computations show that this does not give an analogue for multiplicity; for example, for
f=x_{1}
one has
\str(-1,x_{1})=m+1
(Example
4.19
). For this reason we give this invariant another name, and we call
\str(\alpha,f)
the
strength
of
\alpha
in
f
; this is a new feature of the theory that is not available when
m=0
. We show that these invariants detect whether
\alpha
arises as a root of the Bernstein-Sato polynomial in characteristic zero.

Theorem 1.5

(Cor.
4.22
)
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial with integer coefficients, and let
p
be a large enough prime. For every integer
m\geq 0
, let
f_{m}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m+1})[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be the mod-
p^{m+1}
reduction of
f
. Given
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}
, the sequence
\big(\str(\alpha,f_{m})\big)_{m=0}^{\infty}
is nondecreasing, and satisfies

\nu_{p}\big(b_{f}(\alpha)\big)\geq\str(\alpha,f_{m})

for every
m\geq 0
, where
\nu_{p}(-)
denotes
p
-adic valuation. In particular, if
(\str(\alpha),f_{m})_{m=0}^{\infty}
is unbounded then we have
b_{f}(\alpha)=0
.

2
Background, preliminaries, and notation

In this paper we will always work over a very specific class of base rings; we begin by formalizing this in the following.

Setup 2.1
.

Let
(V,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{K})
be a commutative artinian local ring with residue field
\mathbb{K}
of characteristic
p>0
. We assume that there exists a bijective ring homomorphism
F:V\longrightarrow V
such that the induced map
\mathbb{K}\longrightarrow\mathbb{K}
is the Frobenius morphism. We fix such an
F
, which will be referred to as a lift of Frobenius.

Remark 2.2
.

Let
(V,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{K},F)
be as in Setup
2.1
. Since
F:V\longrightarrow V
is bijective by assumption, it must be a bijection on nonunits, and therefore
F(\mathfrak{m})=\mathfrak{m}
. The bijectivity of
F
also implies that the Frobenius on
\mathbb{K}
is surjective, and hence
\mathbb{K}
is forced to be perfect.

Example 2.3
.

We provide some examples of the above setup.

(i)

The simplest example is the following:
V=\mathbb{Z}/p^{m+1}
,
\mathfrak{a}=(p)
, and
F:V\longrightarrow V
being the identity map.

(ii)

More generally, if
k
is a perfect field of characteristic
p>0
, one could take
V=W_{m+1}(k)
to be the ring of Witt vectors of length
m+1
over
k
, with
\mathfrak{m}=(p)
and
F
the Frobenius endomorphism on Witt vectors.

(iii)

If
K
is a finite Galois extension of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
with ring of integers
(\mathcal{O}_{K},\mathfrak{m},k)
, one can take
V=\mathcal{O}_{K}/\mathfrak{m}^{m+1}
. It is well known that the induced map
\Gal(K/\mathbb{Q}_{p})\longrightarrow\Gal(k/\mathbb{F}_{p})
is surjective
[
Ser79
, §7 Prop. 20]
, so there exists a lift of Frobenius.

(iv)

There are interesting examples in equal characteristic
p
which also satisfy the conditions of Setup
2.1
; for example, one could take
V=\mathbb{F}_{p}[t]/(t^{m+1})
with
F(t)=t
. This gives rise to some interesting questions, which we will not tackle.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
(V,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{K},F)
be as in Setup
2.1
, let

\displaystyle g^{p}

To prove the claim, first recall that in

Lemma 2.12

2.2
Rings of differential operators and Frobenius descent

Let
V
be a commutative ring and
R
be a commutative
V
-algebra. A construction of Grothendieck assigns to this data a subring
\mathcal{D}_{R|A}
of the (noncommutative) ring
\End_{A}(R)
of
A
-linear endomorphisms of
R

[
Gro65
, Ch. 16]
. Here we briefly explain this construction, and provide an alternative characterization of this ring in the case where the base ring
V
is as in Setup
2.1
. Note that some of these results have already been obtained by Berthelot
[
Ber12
]
, but in our exposition we will avoid the use of divided powers.

We begin by noting that
\End_{V}(R)
has a natural
R\otimes_{V}R
-module structure, where a pure tensor
r\otimes s\in R\otimes_{V}R
acts on an element
\phi\in\End_{V}(R)
by

\big((r\otimes s)\cdot\phi)(x):=r\phi(sx)

for all
x\in R
. We let
J_{R|V}\subseteq R\otimes_{V}R
denote the kernel of the natural map
R\otimes_{V}R\longrightarrow R
given by multiplication. The ideal
J_{R|V}
defines the diagonal subscheme
\Spec(R)\hookrightarrow\Spec(R)\times_{V}\Spec(R)
, and we have generators
J_{R|V}=(1\otimes r-r\otimes 1\ |\ r\in R)
.

Definition 2.14

(
[
Gro65
, Ch. 16]
)
.

Let
R
be a
V
-algebra. The ring

\mathcal{D}_{R|V}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{\phi\in\End_{V}(R)\ |\ J_{R|V}^{n}\cdot\phi=0\text{ for some }n\gg 0\}.

is called the ring of
V
-linear differential operators on
R
.

We will write this as
\mathcal{D}_{R}
whenever
V
and the
V
-algebra structure of
R
are clear from context. It is clear that
\mathcal{D}_{R|V}
is closed under addition; one can verify that it is also closed under composition, and thus
\mathcal{D}_{R|V}
forms a subring of
\End_{V}(R)
.

Suppose now that
V
is as in Setup
2.1
. We show that whenever a
V
-algebra
R
admits a lift of Frobenius one can describe
\mathcal{D}_{R|V}
in terms of it. Let us begin by introducing the relevant notation.

Definition 2.15
.

Let
(V,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{K},F)
be as in Setup
2.1
and let
R
be a
V
-algebra. Let

be a compatible lift of Frobenius and

be an integer. Then the ring

\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\Hom_{F^{e}(R)}(R,R)

is called the ring of differential operators of level

with respect to

.

Note that one can also think of
\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}
through the following description (see Equation (

)):

\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}=\Hom_{R}(F^{e}_{*}R,F^{e}_{*}R).

Moreover, the lift of Frobenius

induces a compatible lift of Frobenius
F\otimes F:R\otimes_{V}R\longrightarrow R\otimes_{V}R
. One easily verifies that
(F\otimes F)(J_{R|V})\subseteq J_{R|V}
and therefore we get a descending chain of ideals

J_{R|V}\supseteq\big((F\otimes F)(J_{R|V})\big)\supseteq\big((F\otimes F)^{2}(J_{R|V})\big)\supseteq\cdots

We observe that
\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}
can be described by using these ideals as follows.

Lemma 2.16
.

Let
(V,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{K},F)
be as in Setup
2.1
, let

be a
V
-algebra and let

be a compatible lift of Frobenius. For every integer

we have

\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}=\{\phi\in\End_{V}(R)\ |\ \big((F\otimes F)^{e}(J_{R|V})\big)\cdot\phi=0\}.

Proof.

First note that, by our assumption in Setup
2.1
, the morphism
F
is bijective on
V
and therefore
V\subseteq F^{e}(R)
for every

, which shows that
\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}\subseteq\End_{V}(R)
.

The ideal

is generated by elements of the form

, for

. It follows that
(F\otimes F)^{e}(J)
is the ideal generated by elements of the form
1\otimes F^{e}(r)-F^{e}(r)\otimes 1
, where

ranges through all elements of

. Observe that
(1\otimes F^{e}(r)-F^{e}(r)\otimes 1)\cdot\phi=0
if and only if

commutes with multiplication by

. ∎

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
(V,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{K},F)
be as in Setup
2.1
. Let

be a flat

-algebra,

Proof.

Let

be such that
\mathfrak{m}^{m+1}=0
in

for

, and fix an integer

is generated by products of the form

. Since

, and we are trying to show that

for some

Pick

. Since

We claim that

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{m}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces V}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces V/\mathfrak{m}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}

.

-submodule given by
F^{e*}(R[f^{-1}])=R[f^{-1}]
. The first statement then follows from Proposition
2.22
. For the second statement, simply observe that
\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}(J)
gets identified with the image of the composition

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Hom_{R}(F^{e}_{*}R,R)\otimes_{\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}}J}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Hom_{R}(F^{e}_{*}R,R)\otimes_{\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}}R[f^{-1}]\cong R[f^{-1}],}

which is readily checked to agree with the morphism
[\phi\otimes g\mapsto\phi^{\prime}(g)]
.
∎

Remark 2.25
.

is the
\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}

generated by

.

Corollary 2.26
.

Let

be an
R
-submodule and let

\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}(I)=\mathcal{C}^{(F,e+i)}_{R}\big(F^{i*}(I)\big).

Remark 2.27
.

When
V=\mathbb{K}
is a perfect field of positive characteristic, the submodules
\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}(J)
appear in the theory of test ideals, often with the notation
J^{[1/p^{e}]}
. Indeed, if
R
is a smooth
\mathbb{K}
-algebra and
f\in R
be an element, then for all integers
n\geq 0
and
e\geq 0
, the ideal
\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}(f^{n})
is the test ideal of
f
with exponent
n/p^{e}
, i.e.,

\tau(f^{n/p^{e}})=\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}(f^{n}).

More generally, for an arbitrary ideal
J\subseteq R
and an arbitrary real number
\lambda\geq 0
, the test ideal of
J
with exponent
\lambda
can be expressed as

\tau(J^{\lambda})=\bigcup_{e=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}(J^{\lceil\lambda/p^{e}\rceil});

see
[
BMS08
]
for details.

Note that Corollary
2.24
is still meaningful when
f=1
; in this case, it tells us that
[I\mapsto F^{e*}(I)]
and
[J\leftarrow\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}(J)]
give a bijective correspondence

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\begin{Bmatrix}\text{ Ideals of $R$ }\end{Bmatrix}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\begin{Bmatrix}\text{ $\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}$-submodules of $R$ }\end{Bmatrix}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sim}

The following proposition tells us how, in this situation, we can compute the ideals
\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}(J)
from a basis for
F^{e}_{*}R
and a set of generators of
J
. This generalizes the case of positive characteristic as in
[
BMS08
, Prop. 2.5]
(where the ideal
\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}(J)
is called
J^{[1/p^{e}]}
).

Proposition 2.28
.

Let
(V,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{K},F)
be as in Setup
2.1
,
R
be a finite type
V
-algebra,
F:R\longrightarrow R
be a compatible lift of Frobenius, and
J\subseteq R
be an ideal of
R
. Let
e\geq 0
be an integer, assume that
F^{e}_{*}R
is free over
R
with basis
F^{e}_{*}v_{1},\dots,F^{e}_{*}v_{N}
, and fix generators
J=(f_{1},\dots,f_{s})
for the ideal
J
. For each
i=1,\dots,s
and
j=1,\dots,N
let
g_{ij}\in R
be the coefficients of
F^{e}_{*}f_{i}
in the basis
v_{j}
, i.e.,

F^{e}_{*}f_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}g_{ij}F^{e}_{*}v_{j}.

(2)

Then we have

\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}(J)=\big(g_{ij}\ |\ i=1,\dots,n\ \ j=1,\dots,N\big).

Proof.

Given an arbitrary element of
J
, say
h=\sum_{j}a_{j}f_{j}
, and an arbitrary
R
-linear map
\phi:F^{e}_{*}R\longrightarrow R
, we get

\displaystyle\phi(F^{e}_{*}h)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\phi(F^{e}_{*}a_{j}f_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\phi(g_{ij}F^{e}_{*}a_{j}f_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}g_{ij}\phi(F^{e}_{*}a_{j}f_{j}),

which proves the inclusion
(\subseteq)
. For the other inclusion, it suffices to show that every
g_{ij}
belongs to the left-hand side. For this, let
\pi_{j}:F^{e}_{*}R\longrightarrow R
be the projection to the basis element
F^{e}_{*}v_{j}
, and one has

g_{ij}=\pi_{j}(f_{i})\in\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}(J).\qed

Suppose
R\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}V[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
is a polynomial ring over
V
, and fix the compatible lift of Frobenius
F:R\longrightarrow R
with
F(x_{i})=x_{i}^{p}
. For every integer
e\geq 0
, the
R
-module
F^{e}_{*}R
is free, and the monomials
F^{e}_{*}x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}}
with
0\leq a_{i}<p^{e}
form a basis.

We fix the standard grading on
V[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
. Given a (not necessarily homogeneous) polynomial
g\in R
and an integer
d\geq 0
, we will say that
g
has degree
\leq d
whenever all its homogeneous components have degrees
\leq d
; we write this as
\deg g\leq d
. More generally, we say that an ideal
J\subseteq R
is generated in degrees
\leq d
if there are generators
J=(g_{1},\dots,g_{s})
such that each
g_{i}
has degree
\leq d
. Note that this is equivalent to the condition

J=R\cdot\big(J\cap[R]_{\leq d}\big).

In particular, if
J_{1},J_{2}\subseteq R
are ideals generated in degrees
\leq d
and
J_{1}\cap[R]_{\leq d}=J_{2}\cap[R]_{\leq d}
, then
J_{1}=J_{2}
.

We now give a useful bound on the degrees of generators of the ideals
\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}(J)
which will be of use later (once again, this appears in
[
BMS08
]
in the case of positive characteristic).

Proposition 2.29
.

Let
(V,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{K},F)
be as in Setup
2.1
, let
R\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}V[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial ring over
V
, and fix
F:R\longrightarrow R
to be the compatible lift of Frobenius with
F(x_{i})=x_{i}^{p}
. If
J\subseteq R
is an ideal generated in degrees
\leq d
then the ideal
\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}(J)
is generated in degrees
\leq d/p^{e}
.

Proof.

For the given lift of Frobenius, an
R
-basis for
F^{e}_{*}R
is given by the monomials

x^{\alpha}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}},

where
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
is a tuple of integers with
0\leq\alpha_{i}<p^{e}
for every
i
. Pick generators
J=(f_{1},\dots,f_{s})
such that
\deg f_{i}\leq d
, and express each
F^{e}_{*}f_{i}
in this basis as in Equation (
2
). This gives

f_{i}=\sum_{0\leq\alpha_{i}<p^{e}}F^{e}(g_{i,\alpha})x^{\alpha}.

Since the given lift of Frobenius multiplies degrees by
p^{e}
, for every
i
and
\alpha
we get

d\ \geq\ \deg(f_{i})\ \geq\ p^{e}\deg(g_{i\alpha})+\deg(x^{\alpha})\ \geq\ p^{e}\deg(g_{i\alpha}),

from which we deduce that
\deg(g_{i\alpha})\leq d/p^{e}
. Since
\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}(J)
is generated by the
g_{i\alpha}
, the statement follows.
∎

2.3
Certain algebras of continuous functions

Throughout this subsection we fix a prime number
p
, and
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
will denote the ring of
p
-adic integers. Given sets
X
and
Y
we denote by
\Fun(X,Y)
the set of all functions
X\longrightarrow Y
.

Definition 2.30
.

Let
A
be a set. We denote by
C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},A)
the set of continuous functions
\phi:\mathbb{Z}_{p}\longrightarrow A
, where
A
is equipped with the discrete topology.

Recall that

\mathbb{Z}_{p}=\lim_{\leftarrow e}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{e}),

from which we deduce

C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},A)=\lim_{\rightarrow e}\Fun(\mathbb{Z}/p^{e},A).

In other words, a function
\phi:\mathbb{Z}_{p}\longrightarrow A
belongs to
C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},A)
if and only if there exists some integer
e\geq 0
such that
\phi(\alpha)=\phi(\beta)
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
with
\alpha\equiv\beta\mod p^{e}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.

If
A
is a ring (not necessarily commutative) then
C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},A)
inherits a ring structure, given by pointwise addition and multiplication. In this situation, given an integer
e\geq 0
and a
p
-adic integer
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
we denote by
\chi^{e}_{\alpha}\in C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},A)
the operator

\chi^{e}_{\alpha}(\beta)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\begin{cases}1\text{ if }\beta\equiv\alpha\mod p^{e}\mathbb{Z}_{p},\\
0\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note that these operators satisfy the identity:

\chi^{e}_{\alpha}=\sum_{i=0}^{p^{e}-1}\chi^{e+1}_{\alpha+ip^{e}}.

(3)

Observe that if
M
is an
A
-module then
C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},M)
is naturally a
C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},A)
-module.

If
A
is a commutative ring then so is
C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},A)
, and hence we may consider
\Spec C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},A)
. We give a description of this spectrum as a locally ringed space for some special
A
.

Proposition 2.31
.

Suppose
V
is as in Setup
2.1
. Then we have an isomorphism

\Spec\big(C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V)\big)\cong\big(\mathbb{Z}_{p},\underline{V}\big),

of locally ringed spaces, where
\underline{V}
is the locally constant sheaf with value
V
.

Proof.

If
e\geq 0
is an integer then
\Fun(\mathbb{Z}/p^{e},V)
is a product of
p^{e}
copies of
V
, and therefore
\Spec(\Fun(\mathbb{Z}/p^{e},V))
is a disjoint union of
p^{e}
copies of
\Spec(V)
, indexed by
\mathbb{Z}/p^{e}
. We thus have isomorphisms of locally ringed spaces

\Spec\big(\Fun(\mathbb{Z}/p^{e},V)\big)\cong(\Spec V)\times\mathbb{Z}/p^{e}\cong\big(\mathbb{Z}/p^{e},\underline{V}\big),

where the last isomorphism follows because
\Spec(V)
is a single point.

By
[
Sta21
, 01YW]
we thus get

\Spec\big(C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V)\big)\cong\lim_{\leftarrow e}\big(\mathbb{Z}/p^{e},\underline{V}\big),

where the limit takes place in the category of locally ringed spaces. It hence suffices to show that
(\mathbb{Z}_{p},\underline{V})
satisfies the universal property of the right hand side.

First observe that for each integer
e\geq 0
we have natural maps
\pi_{e}:(\mathbb{Z}_{p},\underline{V})\longrightarrow(\mathbb{Z}/p^{e},\underline{V})
. Suppose that
(W,\mathcal{O}_{W})
is a locally ringed space with compatible maps
f_{e}:(W,\mathcal{O}_{W})\longrightarrow(\mathbb{Z}/p^{e},\underline{V})
for every
e\geq 0
. This induces a unique compatible continuous map of topological spaces
f_{\infty}:W\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, and the claim is that there is a unique compatible map of sheaves
\underline{V}\longrightarrow f_{\infty*}(\mathcal{O}_{W})
, and hence there is a unique compatible map
f_{\infty}:(W,\mathcal{O}_{W})\longrightarrow(\mathbb{Z}_{p},\underline{V})
.

To prove the claim, note that a map of sheaves
\underline{V}\longrightarrow f_{\infty,*}\mathcal{O}_{W}
is equivalent to the data of ring homomorphisms
V\longrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{W}(f^{-1}_{\infty}U)
for every open
U\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{p}
(compatible with restriction), and recall that it is enough to specify these on a basis for the topology of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. To conclude the proof, recall that open sets of the form
\pi_{e}^{-1}(x)
for
x\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{e}
form a basis for the topology of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, and note that we have
f^{-1}_{\infty}\pi^{-1}_{e}(x)=f^{-1}_{e}(x)
.
∎

Fix
(V,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{K},F)
as in Setup
2.1
. By Proposition
2.31
every prime ideal of
C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V)
is maximal, and there is a bijective correspondence between
p
-adic integers and maximal ideals of
C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V)
. Tracing through the proof one sees that, given a
p
-adic integer
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, the corresponding maximal ideal is

(\mathfrak{m}:\alpha)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\big(\phi\in C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V)\ |\ \phi(\alpha)\in\mathfrak{m}\big).

This is an instance of a more general construction: given an ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq V
and a
p
-adic integer
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, we can define an ideal
(\mathfrak{a}:\alpha\subseteq C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V)
given by

(\mathfrak{a}:\alpha)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\big(\phi\in C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V)\ |\ \phi(\alpha)\in\mathfrak{a}\big).

For example, the ideal
(0:\alpha)\subseteq C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V)
is the ideal that consists of functions which vanish at
\alpha
. Note that the evaluation morphism
[\phi\mapsto\phi(\alpha)]
defines a surjective ring homomorphism
e_{\alpha}:C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V)\longrightarrow V
whose kernel is precisely
(0:\alpha)
, and therefore
V\cong C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V)/(0:\alpha)
. Given an ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq V
, we have
e_{\alpha}^{-1}(\mathfrak{a})=(\mathfrak{a}:\alpha)
.

This construction is compatible with taking powers, in the sense that
(\mathfrak{a}:\alpha)^{k}=(\mathfrak{a}^{k}:\alpha)
for every ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq V
, every
p
-adic integer
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, and every integer
k\geq 0
. In particular, if
m\geq 0
is chosen so that
\mathfrak{m}^{m+1}=0
in
V
then we have

(\mathfrak{m}:\alpha)\supseteq(\mathfrak{m}:\alpha)^{2}\supseteq\cdots\supseteq(\mathfrak{m}:\alpha)^{m}\supseteq(\mathfrak{m}:\alpha)^{m+1}=(\mathfrak{m}:\alpha)^{m+2}=\cdots=(0:\alpha).

Given a
C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V)
-module
M
, we obtain an associated quasicoherent sheaf on
\Spec(C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V))\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Recall that, given
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, the stalk
M_{\alpha}
of this sheaf at
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is the localization of
M
at the maximal ideal
(\mathfrak{m}:\alpha)
.

Proposition 2.32
.

Fix
(V,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{K},F)
as in Setup
2.1
, let
M
be a
C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V)
-module, and let
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
be a
p
-adic integer. There is a natural
C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V)
-linear isomorphism

M_{\alpha}\cong M/(0:\alpha)M.

Proof.

For ease of notation let
C\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V)
. Since
M_{\alpha}\cong M\otimes_{C}C_{\alpha}
and
M/(0:\alpha)M\cong M\otimes_{C}C/(0:\alpha)
, it suffices to prove the claim for
M=C
. Given an element
\phi\in C
we denote by
[\phi]
its equivalence class in
C/(0:\alpha)
.

Suppose
\psi\in C
is such that
\psi\notin(\mathfrak{m}:\alpha)
; in other words,
\psi(\alpha)\notin\mathfrak{m}
. It follows that
\psi(\alpha)
is invertible in
V
. We have
\psi(\alpha)^{-1}\psi-1\in(0:\alpha)
, and therefore
[\psi(\alpha)^{-1}][\psi(\alpha)]=[\psi(\alpha)^{-1}\psi]=1
. This shows that
\psi
is invertible in
C/(0:\alpha)
. Since
\psi\in C\setminus(\mathfrak{m}:\alpha)
was arbitrary, we get a natural
C
-linear map
C_{\alpha}\longrightarrow C/(0:\alpha)
.

Now let
\phi\in C
be an element such that
\phi\in(0:\alpha)
, i.e.,
\phi(\alpha)=0
. Picking
e\gg 0
be such that
\phi\in\Fun(\mathbb{Z}/p^{e},V)
, we see that
\chi^{e}_{\alpha}\phi=0
. Since
\chi^{e}_{\alpha}\notin(\mathfrak{m}:\alpha)
,
\phi/1=0
in
C_{\alpha}
. This shows that the natural map
C\longrightarrow C_{\alpha}
factors through
C/(0:\alpha)
, giving a natural map
C/(0:\alpha)\longrightarrow C_{\alpha}
.

Since the maps
C_{\alpha}\longrightarrow C/(0:\alpha)
and
C/(0:\alpha)\longrightarrow C_{\alpha}
obtained above commute with the natural maps
C\longrightarrow C_{\alpha}
and
C\longrightarrow C/(0:\alpha)
, we conclude that they are mutual inverses.
∎

Corollary 2.33
.

If
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is another
p
-adic integer, we have

(M_{\alpha})_{\beta}\cong\begin{cases}M_{\alpha}\text{ if }\alpha=\beta,\\
0\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

Proof.

Write
M_{\alpha}\cong M/(0:\alpha)M
. Since
(\mathfrak{m}:\alpha)
is the only maximal ideal containing
(\mathfrak{m}:\alpha)^{m+1}=(0:\alpha)
, we conclude that
(M_{\alpha})_{\beta}=0
whenever
\alpha\neq\beta
. On the other hand, it is clear that
(M_{\alpha})_{\alpha}\cong M_{\alpha}
.
∎

Proposition 2.34
.

Fix
(V,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{K},F)
as in Setup
2.1
and let
M
be a
C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V)
-module. Suppose there are only finitely many
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
such that
M_{\alpha}\neq 0
; call these
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{s}
. Then the natural map

M\longrightarrow M_{\alpha_{1}}\oplus M_{\alpha_{2}}\oplus\cdots\oplus M_{\alpha_{s}}

is an isomorphism, and the annihilator of
M
takes the form

\Ann(M)=(\mathfrak{a}_{1}:\alpha_{1})\cdot(\mathfrak{a}_{2}:\alpha_{2})\cdots(\mathfrak{a}_{s}:\alpha_{s})

for some ideals
\mathfrak{a}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{a}_{s}\subseteq V
.

Proof.

Let
K
(resp.
Q
) be the kernel (resp. cokernel) of the given map, so that we have an exact sequence

0\longrightarrow K\longrightarrow M\longrightarrow M_{\alpha_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus M_{\alpha_{s}}\longrightarrow Q\longrightarrow 0;

the goal is to show that
K=Q=0
. For this, take an arbitrary
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
and take the stalk at
\beta
of this sequence. By virtue of Corollary
2.33
we conclude that, whenever
\beta\in\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{s}\}
, we get

0\longrightarrow K_{\beta}\longrightarrow M_{\beta}\longrightarrow M_{\beta}\longrightarrow Q_{\beta}\longrightarrow 0.

On the other hand, when
\beta\notin\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{s}\}
, we get

0\longrightarrow K_{\beta}\longrightarrow 0\longrightarrow 0\longrightarrow Q_{\beta}\longrightarrow 0.

In either case we get
K_{\beta}=Q_{\beta}=0
, and since this happens for all
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, we conclude that
K=Q=0
as claimed.

For the statement about the annihilator, observe that for every
i=1,\dots,s
the action of
C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V)
on
M_{\alpha_{i}}
factors through the evaluation map
e_{\alpha}:C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V)\longrightarrow C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V)/(0:\alpha_{i})\cong V
, and therefore the annihilator of
M_{\alpha_{i}}
has the form
e_{\alpha_{i}}^{-1}(\mathfrak{a}_{i})=(\mathfrak{a}_{i}:\alpha_{i})
for some ideal
\mathfrak{a}_{i}\subseteq V
. We conclude that the annihilator of
M
is
\bigcap_{i}(\mathfrak{a}_{i}:\alpha_{i})
, and the statement follows from the fact that the ideals
(\mathfrak{a}_{i}:\alpha_{i})
are pairwise coprime.
∎

For us the algebra
C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V)
comes into play through rings of differential operators as follows. We let
(V,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{K},F)
be as in Setup
2.1
, and consider the polynomial ring
V[t]
in the variable
t
. We give
V[t]
the natural
\mathbb{N}
-grading for which
\deg(t)=1
; this induces a
\mathbb{Z}
-grading on the ring
\mathcal{D}_{V[t]}
of
V
-linear differential operators on
V[t]
. We denote by
(\mathcal{D}_{V[t]})_{0}
the subring of
\mathcal{D}_{V[t]}
that consists of degree zero differential operators.

If
\phi\in C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V)
is a continuous function
\phi:\mathbb{Z}_{p}\longrightarrow V
, we let
\tilde{\phi}:V[t]\longrightarrow V[t]
be the unique
V
-linear map given by

\tilde{\phi}(t^{a})=\phi(-1-a)t^{a}

for every integer
a\geq 0
.

We claim that
\tilde{\phi}
is a differential operator. To see this, we fix the compatible lift of Frobenius
F:V[t]\longrightarrow V[t]
given by
F(t)=t^{p}
. If
e\gg 0
is large enough so that
\phi
factors through
\mathbb{Z}/p^{e}
then for every integer
a\geq 0
we get

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}(F^{e}(t)t^{a})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\phi}(t^{p^{e}+a})=\phi(-1-a-p^{e})t^{p^{e}+a}

\displaystyle=\phi(-1-a)t^{p^{e}+a}=t^{p^{e}}\tilde{\phi}(t^{a})=F^{e}(t)\tilde{\phi}(t^{a}),

which shows that
\tilde{\phi}\in\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{V[t]}
. Since clearly
\tilde{\phi}
has degree zero, we conclude that
\tilde{\phi}\in(\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{V[t]})_{0}
.

We therefore get a
V
-algebra homomorphism

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\mathcal{D}_{V[t]})_{0},}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sim}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Delta}

given by
\Delta(\phi)=\tilde{\phi}
, which one immediately checks to be an isomorphism.

Note, for example, that given an integer
e\geq 0
and integers

, the operator associated to the function
\phi=\chi^{e}_{a}
given above acts by

\tilde{\chi}^{e}_{a}(t^{p^{e}-1-b})=\begin{cases}t^{p^{e}-1-a}\text{ if }b=a\\
0\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

We also have the following generalization. Suppose
(V,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{K},F)
are as in Setup
2.1
and
R

3
Definition and characterization of Bernstein-Sato roots

3.1
Motivation from characteristic zero

Let
R\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial ring over
\mathbb{C}
. Recall that, in this case, the ring
\mathcal{D}_{R}
of
\mathbb{C}
-linear differential operators on
R
can be described as the following subalgebra of
\End_{\mathbb{C}}(R)
:

\mathcal{D}_{R}=R\langle\partial_{1},\dots,\partial_{n}\rangle,

where
\partial_{i}
is the operator
\partial_{i}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\partial/\partial x_{i}
.

Let
f\in R
be a nonzero polynomial, let
s
be a new indeterminate, and consider the
\mathcal{D}_{R}[s]
-module
R[f^{-1},s]\boldsymbol{f^{s}}
, where
\boldsymbol{f^{s}}
is a formal symbol. The action of
R
and
s
on
R[f^{-1},s]\boldsymbol{f^{s}}
is as expected; in other words, we have
R[f^{-1},s]\boldsymbol{f^{s}}\cong R[f^{-1},s]
as an
R[s]
-module. However, the action of derivations is determined by

\partial_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{f^{s}}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}s\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}f^{-1}\boldsymbol{f^{s}}.

A theorem of Bernstein and Sato states that there is a nonzero polynomial
b(s)\in\mathbb{C}[s]
such that

b(s)\boldsymbol{f^{s}}=P(s)f\boldsymbol{f^{s}}

for some
P(s)\in\mathcal{D}_{R}[s]
. The monic polynomial of least degree satisfying the above for some
P(s)\in\mathcal{D}_{R}[s]
is called the Bernstein-Sato polynomial of
f
, and is denoted by
b_{f}(s)
. Said differently,
b_{f}(s)
is the monic generator of the ideal

(b_{f}(s))=\Ann_{\mathbb{C}[s]}\big(\mathcal{D}_{R}[s]\cdot\boldsymbol{f^{s}}\ \big/\ \mathcal{D}_{R}[s]\cdot f\boldsymbol{f^{s}}\big)\subseteq\mathbb{C}[s].

As explained in Section
1
, this description is not suitable for translation to positive characteristic, and one uses instead an alternative construction of
b_{f}(s)
on which we now elaborate. We note that the earliest reference to this construction we could find is in work of Malgrange
[
Mal83
]
.

Consider the graph
\gamma_{f}:\mathbb{A}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{A}^{n}\times\mathbb{A}^{1}
of the polynomial
f
, and the
\mathcal{D}_{R}
-module pushforward
\gamma_{f,+}(R[f^{-1}])
of the
\mathcal{D}_{R}
-module
R[f^{-1}]
. This admits a concrete description as:

\gamma_{f,+}(R[f^{-1}])=\frac{R[t,f^{-1},(f-t)^{-1}]}{R[t,f^{-1}]},

where
t
is the coordinate on the new copy of
\mathbb{A}^{1}
, and for each integer
k\geq 0
we let
\delta_{k}
denote the class

\delta_{k}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}[(f-t)^{-1-k}]\in\gamma_{f,+}(R[f^{-1}].

These elements
\delta_{k}
form a basis for
\gamma_{f,+}(R[f^{-1}])
as a
R[f^{-1}]
-module; that is,

\gamma_{f,+}(R[f^{-1}])=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}R[f^{-1}]\ \delta_{k}.

On the other hand, the module
R[f^{-1},s]\boldsymbol{f^{s}}
is also free over
R[f^{-1}]
, with a basis given by

R[f^{-1},s]\boldsymbol{f^{s}}=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{\infty}R[f^{-1}]s^{i}\boldsymbol{f^{s}}.

With this notation, there is an isomorphism

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\gamma_{f,+}(R[f^{-1}])}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R[f^{-1},s]\boldsymbol{f^{s}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sim}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Phi}

which is
\mathcal{D}_{R}[f^{-1}]
-linear and exchanges
\delta_{0}
with the action of
-\partial_{t}t
on the left with
s
on the right. In particular, the change of basis is given by

\Phi(\delta_{k})=(-1)^{k}{s\choose k}f^{-k}\boldsymbol{f^{s}}.

In view of this, the Bernstein-Sato polynomial of
f
can also be viewed as the annihilator

(b_{f}(s))=\Ann_{\mathbb{C}[s]}\big(\mathcal{D}_{R}[-\partial_{t}t]\cdot\delta_{0}\ \big/\ \mathcal{D}_{R}[-\partial_{t}t]\cdot f\delta_{0}\big)\subseteq\mathbb{C}[s],

where
s
acts on the module as the operator
-\partial_{t}t
. Since the module
\gamma_{f,+}(R[f^{-1}])
can be constructed over an arbitrary base this latter description is better for generalization, as long as one uses the correct replacement of
\mathcal{D}_{R}[-\partial_{t}t]
. We refer the reader to
[
Mus09
]
,
[
Bit18
]
,
[
QG21b
]
,
[
JNBQG23
]
for an implementation of these ideas in positive characteristic.

\displaystyle\big(C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},\mathcal{D}_{R})\cdot\boldsymbol{f^{s}}\big)_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\big(\im\big(C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},\mathcal{D}_{R})\xrightarrow{\boldsymbol{f^{s}}}C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},R[f^{-1}])\boldsymbol{f^{s}}\big)\big)_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\im\big(C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},\mathcal{D}_{R})_{\alpha}\xrightarrow{\boldsymbol{f^{s}}}(C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},R[f^{-1}])\boldsymbol{f^{s}})_{\alpha}\big)

\displaystyle=\im\big(\mathcal{D}_{R}\xrightarrow{\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}}R[f^{-1}]\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}.

Similarly, we obtain that
\big(C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},\mathcal{D}_{R})\cdot f\boldsymbol{f^{s}}\big)_{\alpha}=\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot f\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}
, from which we conclude that

(N_{f})_{\alpha}\cong\frac{\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}}{\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot f\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}},

which gives the equivalence between (a) and (b).

Suppose that (b) were false, and fix
e\gg 0
such that
\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}\in\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}
. Fix
a\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\alpha\equiv a\mod p^{e+m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, for example by truncating the
p
-adic expansion of
\alpha
. We then have (see Subsection
3.4
)

\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}\in\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}=f^{-a}(\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{a+1})\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}},

and therefore
1\in f^{-a}(\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{a+1})
or, equivalently,
f^{a}\in\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{a+1}
. But this gives that
\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{a}=\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{a+1}
, and therefore
a\notin\nu^{\bullet}_{f}(F,p^{e})
. This shows that (c) implies (b).

Conversely, suppose (b) holds, fix an integer
e\geq 0
, and let
a\in(\alpha+p^{e+m}\mathbb{Z}_{p})\cap\mathbb{Z}
; in other words,
a\in\mathbb{Z}
be an integer with the property that
a\equiv\alpha\mod p^{e+m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. As before, we have that
\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}=f^{-a}(\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{a+1})\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}
, and from the assumption that
\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}\notin\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}
we conclude that
f^{a}\notin\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{a+1}
. Therefore, we have
\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{a}\neq\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{a+1}
, and hence
a\in\nu^{\bullet}_{f}(F,p^{e})
. We conclude that (b) implies (c).

That (c) implies (d) is straightforward: if we choose
\nu_{e}
to be any integer with
\alpha\equiv\nu_{e}\mod p^{e+m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
then the
p
-adic limit of
(\nu_{e})_{e=0}^{\infty}
is
\alpha
, and (c) gives that
\nu_{e}\in\nu^{\bullet}_{f}(F,p^{e})
.

To finish the proof, assume that a sequence
(\nu_{e})_{e=0}^{\infty}
as in (d) exists. Since the
p
-adic limit of
\nu_{e}
is
\alpha
, we may pass to a subsequence to assume that
\alpha\equiv\nu_{e}\mod p^{e+m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
(note that, by Proposition
3.16
(i), the property
\nu_{e}\in\nu^{\bullet}_{f}(F,p^{e})
is preserved under passage to a subsequence). From the fact that
\alpha\equiv\nu_{e}\mod p^{e+m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
we conclude that
\alpha+p^{e+m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}=\nu_{e}+p^{e+m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
for every
e\geq 0
. On the other hand, since the
\nu_{e}
are integers, we have
(\nu_{e}+p^{e+m}\mathbb{Z}_{p})\cap\mathbb{Z}=\nu_{e}+p^{e+m}\mathbb{Z}
. Putting these together, we obtain

(\alpha+p^{e+m}\mathbb{Z}_{p})\cap\mathbb{Z}=(\nu_{e}+p^{e+m}\mathbb{Z}_{p})\cap\mathbb{Z}=\nu_{e}+p^{e+m}\mathbb{Z}.

Finally, by Proposition
3.16
(ii) we conclude that
\nu_{e}+p^{e+m}\mathbb{Z}\subseteq\nu^{\bullet}_{f}(F,p^{e})
. We conclude that (d) implies (c), which completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 3.18
.

Suppose
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is a Bernstein-Sato root of
f
, and let
\alpha=\alpha_{0}+p\alpha_{1}+p^{2}\alpha_{2}+\cdots
be its
p
-adic expansion. For every
e\geq 0
, let
\alpha_{<e+m}
be the right truncation

\alpha_{<e+m}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\alpha_{0}+p\alpha_{1}+p^{2}\alpha_{2}+\cdots+p^{e+m-1}\alpha_{e+m-1}.

Then we have
\alpha_{<e+m}\in\nu^{\bullet}_{f}(F,p^{e})
for every
e\geq 0
.

Proof.

Clearly we have
\alpha_{<e+m}\equiv\alpha\mod p^{e+m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
or, in other words, we have
\alpha_{<e+m}\in\alpha+p^{e+m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. The statement then follows from (c).
∎

Corollary 3.19
.

The collection of
p
-adic integers
\alpha
satisfying (c) (resp. (d)) is independent of the choice of
F
.

Proof.

This collection is the collection of Bernstein-Sato roots of
f
, and the definition of Bernstein-Sato roots makes no reference to
F
.
∎

Despite what Corollaries
3.18
and
3.19
might suggest, it is not true that the
\nu
-invariants
\nu^{\bullet}_{f}(F,p^{e})
are independent of the choice of lift of Frobenius
F
. As an example, let
p=2
and
R=(\mathbb{Z}/4)[x,y]
with the lifts of Frobenius
F_{i}:R\longrightarrow R
given by
F_{1}(x)=x^{2}
,
F_{1}(y)=y^{2}
,
F_{2}(x)=x^{2}+2y(x+y)
, and
F_{2}(y)=y^{2}
. By Proposition
3.16
(ii), for an arbitrary nonzerodivisor
f\in R
the
\nu
-invariants
\nu^{\bullet}_{f}(F_{i},p^{1})
of level one are uniquely determined by
\nu^{\bullet}_{f}(F_{i},p^{1})\cap[0,3]
, which we give for
f=x
. On the one hand, one has
\nu^{\bullet}_{x}(F_{1},p^{1})\cap[0,3]=\{1,3\}
. On the other hand, note that
F_{2}
is engineered so that the change of coordinates
[(x,y)\mapsto(x+y,y)]
exchanges
F_{2}
with
F_{1}
. One can easily compute
\nu^{\bullet}_{x+y}(F_{1},p^{1})=\{1,2,3\}
and therefore
\nu^{\bullet}_{x}(F_{2},p^{1})=\nu^{\bullet}_{x+y}(F_{1},p^{1})=\{1,2,3\}
.

Corollary 3.20
.

The polynomials
f
and
F(f)
have the same Bernstein-Sato roots.

Proof.

This follows from description (d) together with Proposition
3.16
(iii).
∎

4
Properties of Bernstein-Sato roots

4.1
Finiteness and rationality

For the whole of this subsection, we let
(V,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{K},F)
be as in Setup
2.1
, let
R\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}V[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial ring over
V
, and let
f\in R
be a nonzerodivisor.

The main goal here is to prove that there are only a finite number of Bernstein-Sato roots of
f
, all of which are rational (recall that the ring
\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}
of rational numbers whose denominator is not divisible by
p
is naturally a subring of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, and that a
p
-adic integer is said to be rational whenever it lies in this subring).

Note that we need to restrict to the case where
R
is a polynomial ring over
V
, because the proof crucially uses the degree filtration. Nonetheless, we expect the same result to hold whenever
R
is an arbitrary smooth
V
-algebra.

As usual, we fix an integer
m\geq 0
such that
\mathfrak{m}^{m+1}=0
in
V
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
F:R\longrightarrow R
be the lift of Frobenius given by
F(x_{i})=x_{i}^{p}
for all
i=1,\dots,n
. Then there is a constant
K\geq 0
such that, for every
e\geq 0
, we have

\#\big(\nu^{\bullet}_{f}(F,p^{e})\cap[0,p^{e+m})\big)\leq K.

Proof.

We want to find
K
such that, for every
e\geq 0
, the chain of ideals

\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{0}\supseteq\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{1}\supseteq\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{2}\supseteq\cdots\supseteq\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{p^{e+m}}

has at most
K
proper inclusions.

Suppose
f
has degree
\leq d
(see the discussion above Proposition
2.29
). By Proposition
2.29
, the ideal
\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{k}
is generated in degrees
\leq kd/p^{e}
, and therefore every ideal in the chain is generated in degrees
\leq dp^{m}
. It follows that
\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{k}=\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{k+1}
if and only if
(\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{k})\cap[R]_{\leq dp^{m}}=(\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{k+1})\cap[R]_{\leq dp^{m}}
, and hence the number of proper inclusions in the chain above is the same as the number of proper inclusions in the following chain of
V
-modules:

\big(\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{0}\big)\cap[R]_{\leq dp^{m}}\supseteq\big(\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{1}\big)\cap[R]_{\leq dp^{m}}\supseteq\cdots\supseteq\big(\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{p^{e+m}}\big)\cap[R]_{\leq dp^{m}}.

Now note that this is a chain of
V
submodules of
[R]_{\leq dp^{m}}
. Note that
[R]_{\leq dp^{m}}
has finite length as a
V
-module; letting
K
be this length, we conclude that there can be no more than
K
proper inclusions in this chain.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
(V,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{K},F)
be as in Setup
2.1
, let
R\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}V[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial ring over
V
, and let
f\in R
be a nonzerodivisor. There are only finitely many Bernstein-Sato roots of
f
.

Proof.

Let
K
be the bound given in Lemma
4.1
, and we claim there can be at most
K
Bernstein-Sato roots of
f
.

For a contradiction, suppose that there were at least
K+1
distinct Bernstein-Sato roots. Call them
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{K+1}
, and choose an integer
a\geq 0
such that
(\alpha_{i}+p^{a+m}\mathbb{Z}_{p})\cap(\alpha_{j}+p^{a+m}\mathbb{Z}_{p})=\emptyset
for
i\neq j
(in metric terms, we are choosing a radius so that balls of that radius centered at the
\alpha_{i}
’s are disjoint).

For every
i=1,\dots,K+1
and every
e\geq 0
, let
\nu_{i,e}
be the truncation of the
p
-adic expansion of
\alpha_{i}
at the
(e+m)
-digit, as in Corollary
3.18
. For every
i
and
e
, we clearly have
\nu_{i,e}\in\alpha_{i}+p^{e+m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
and
0\leq\nu_{i,e}<p^{e+m}
and, by Corollary
3.18
, we also know that
\nu_{i,e}\in\nu^{\bullet}_{f}(F,p^{e})
. We conclude that
\nu_{1,a},\dots,\nu_{K+1,a}
are distinct
\nu
-invariants of level
a
, all in the interval
[0,p^{a+m})
, thus giving a contradiction to the bound from Lemma
4.1
.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

The module
N_{f}
described in Subsection
3.2
splits as a direct sum of stalks

N_{f}\cong(N_{f})_{\alpha_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus(N_{f})_{\alpha_{s}},

where
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{s}
are the Bernstein-Sato roots of
f
, and the
b
-function
B_{f}
of
f
takes the form

B_{f}=(\mathfrak{a}_{1}:\alpha_{1})\cdot(\mathfrak{a}_{2}:\alpha_{2})\cdots(\mathfrak{a}_{s}:\alpha_{s})

for some ideals
\mathfrak{a}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{a}_{s}\subseteq V
.

Proof.

The statement follows by combining Theorem
4.2
and Proposition
2.34
.
∎

Having addressed the finiteness issue, we turn to rationality. The proof will proceed by first proving the statement for nonzerodivisors
f\in R
for which there is a lift of Frobenius
F:R\longrightarrow R
with the property that
F(f)=f^{p}
; this assumption allows the characteristic-
p
proof given in
[
JNBQG23
]
to go through. In particular, this proves that the rationality statement holds for
f^{p^{m}}
with
f\in R
arbitrary (see Lemma
2.12
), and the proof is finished by comparing the Bernstein-Sato roots of
f
with those of
f^{p^{m}}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
f\in R
be a nonzerodivisor, and assume that
R
admits a compatible lift of Frobenius
F:R\longrightarrow R
with the property that
F(f)=f^{p}
.

(i)

Let
e\geq 0
be an integer, and let
n\in\mathbb{Z}
be a
\nu
-invariant of level
e
for
f
with respect to
F
. Then for some
0\leq i<p
we have that
pn+i
is a
\nu
-invariant of level
e+1
for
f
with respect to
F
.

(ii)

If
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is a Bernstein-Sato root of
f
, then for some
0\leq i<p
we have the
p\alpha+i
is a Bernstein-Sato root of
f
.

Proof.

We start with (i). By assumption we know that
\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{n}\neq\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{n+1}
. By applying Corollary
2.26
to
I=(f^{n})
, and using that
F^{*}(f^{n})=(F(f^{n}))=f^{pn}
, we conclude that
\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{n}=\mathcal{C}^{(F,e+1)}_{R}\cdot f^{pn}
. Similarly, we obtain that
\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{n+1}=\mathcal{C}^{(F,e+1)}_{R}\cdot f^{p(n+1)}
, and we conclude that
\mathcal{C}^{(F,e+1)}_{R}\cdot f^{pn}\neq\mathcal{C}^{(F,e+1)}_{R}\cdot f^{p(n+1)}
. In other words, in the chain of ideals

\mathcal{C}^{(F,e+1)}_{R}\cdot f^{pn}\supseteq\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{pn+1}\supseteq\cdots\supseteq\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{pn+p}

there must be at least one proper inclusion, which gives the result.

Let us now tackle (ii). By Theorem
3.17
there is an integer sequence
(\nu_{e})_{e=0}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
with
\nu_{e}\in\nu^{\bullet}_{f}(F,p^{e})
whose
p
-adic limit is
\alpha
. By part (i), for every
e
we can find some
0\leq i_{e}<p
with the property that
p\nu_{e}+i_{e}\in\nu^{\bullet}_{f}(F,p^{e+1})
.

Note that
i_{e}
can only take finitely many values, and hence we can find some
0\leq i<p
such that
i_{e}=i
for infinitely many
e
. By replacing
(\nu_{e})
with a subsequence and applying Proposition
3.16
(i), we get
p\nu_{e}+i\in\nu^{\bullet}_{f}(F,p^{e})
. Since the
p
-adic limit of this sequence is
p\alpha+i
, we conclude that
p\alpha+i
is a Bernstein-Sato root of
f
by applying Theorem
3.17
.
∎

We now discuss how
\nu
-invariants and Bernstein-Sato roots behave when taking
p
-power exponents.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
k\geq 0
be an integer.

(i)

If
n\in\nu^{\bullet}_{f}(F,p^{e})
then
\lfloor n/p^{k}\rfloor\in\nu^{\bullet}_{f^{k}}(F,p^{e})
.

(ii)

If
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
be a
p
-adic integer with
p
-adic expansion
\alpha=\alpha_{0}+p\alpha_{1}+p^{2}\alpha_{2}+\cdots
, and consider its left truncation
\alpha_{\geq k}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\alpha_{k}+p\alpha_{k+1}+p^{2}\alpha_{k+2}+\cdots
. If
\alpha
is a Bernstein-Sato root of
f
then
\alpha_{\geq k}
is a Bernstein-Sato root of
f^{k}
.

Proof.

For (i) consider the chain of ideals given by

\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{k\lfloor n/k\rfloor}\supseteq\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{n}\supseteq\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{n+1}\supseteq\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{k(\lfloor n/k\rfloor+1)},

and observe that if the inclusion in the middle is strict, then the outer two ideals cannot be equal.

Let us now move on to (ii). If
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is a Bernstein-Sato root of
f
then, by Corollary
3.18
, we have that
\alpha_{<e+m}\in\nu^{\bullet}_{f}(F,p^{e})
. By part (i) we conclude that
\lfloor\alpha_{<e+m}/p^{k}\rfloor\in\nu^{\bullet}_{f^{k}}(F,p^{e})
. To complete the proof, we observe that
\lfloor\alpha_{<e+m}/p^{k}\rfloor
gives the left truncation of
\alpha_{\geq k}
; that is,

\lfloor\alpha_{<e+m}/p^{k}\rfloor=(\alpha_{\geq k})_{<e+m-k}

for all
e\geq m-k+1
. In particular, the
p
-adic limit of
\lfloor\alpha_{<e+m}/p^{k}\rfloor
is
\alpha_{\geq k}
, and the result follows from Theorem
3.17
.
∎

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
(V,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{K},F)
be as in Setup
2.1
, let
R\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}V[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial ring over
V
, and let
f\in R
be a nonzerodivisor. All the Bernstein-Sato roots of
f
are rational.

Proof.

Fix
m\geq 0
such that
\mathfrak{m}^{m+1}=0
in
V
. Recall that a
p
-adic integer is rational precisely when its
p
-adic expansion is eventually repeating; using the terminology of Lemma
4.5
, this entails that
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is rational if and only if
\alpha_{\geq m}
is rational. By Lemma
4.5
it suffices to show that the Bernstein-Sato roots of
f^{p^{m}}
are rational. Since
F(f^{p^{m}})=f^{p^{m+1}}
(see Lemma
2.12
), we may replace
f
with
f^{p^{m}}
and therefore assume that
F(f)=f^{p}
.

Let
\BSR(f)\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{p}
denote the set of Bernstein-Sato roots of
f
, and let
[\BSR(f)]
denote its image under the quotient map
\mathbb{Z}_{p}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}
, and given a
p
-adic integer
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, we denote by
[\alpha]
its image in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}
.

By Lemma
4.4
(ii), the set
[\BSR(f)]
is stable under multiplication by
p
, and it is finite by Theorem
4.2
. We conclude that, given
\alpha\in\BSR(f)
, there exist integers
a\geq 0
and
b\geq 1
such that
[p^{a}\alpha]=[p^{a+b}\alpha]
, and therefore
p^{a}\alpha=p^{a+b}\alpha+c
, where
c\in\mathbb{Z}
is an integer. We conclude that
\alpha=c/p^{a}(1-p^{b})
, and therefore
\alpha
is rational (since
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, we can also conclude that
p^{a}
must divide
c
).
∎

4.2
Negative Bernstein-Sato roots

Let
(V,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{K},F)
be as in Setup
2.1
, let
R\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}V[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial ring over
V
, and let
f\in R
be a nonzerodivisor. From Theorems
4.2
and
4.6
we know that, just like in characteristic zero, there are a finite number of Bernstein-Sato roots of
f
, all of which are rational. However, in characteristic zero, a result of Kashiwara also tells us that roots of the Bernstein-Sato polynomial are negative
[
Kas77
]
. The analogous result is also known to hold in our setting when
V
is a perfect field of characteristic
p
, by a result of the first author (see
[
Bit18
]
, restated in the Introduction as Theorem
1.2
). One might therefore expect that the same should hold true in our setting, but as shown in Example
4.14
this need not always be the case. Nonetheless, we can show that this holds in certain situations:

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
(V,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{K},F)
be as in Setup
2.1
, let
R\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}V[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial ring over
V
, and let
f\in R
be a nonzerodivisor.

(i)

All Bernstein-Sato roots of
f
which are integers are negative.

(ii)

Assume there exists a lift of Frobenius
F:R\longrightarrow R
such that
F(f)=f^{p}
. Then all the Bernstein-Sato roots of
f
are negative.

Proof.

For part (i), we use the characterization of Bernstein-Sato roots given in Theorem
3.17
(b). We claim that
\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot f^{n}=R
for every integer
n\geq 0
. When
V=\mathbb{K}
is a perfect field of positive characteristic this is true because
R
is strongly
F
-regular: there exists some integer
e\geq 0
such that
\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{n}=R
, and therefore
\mathcal{D}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{n}=F^{e*}(R)=R
(see
[
Smi95
]
for more details). In our generality this tells us that
\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot f^{n}+\mathfrak{m}R=R
(see discussion below), and the statement follows because
\mathfrak{m}R
is a nilpotent ideal.

Recall that, given a nonnegative integer
n\geq 0
, there is a
\mathcal{D}_{R}
-module isomorphism
R[f^{-1}]\boldsymbol{f^{n}}\cong R[f^{-1}]
given by exchanging the symbol
\boldsymbol{f^{n}}
with the element
f^{n}\in R
. By the claim, we get an isomorphism
\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot\boldsymbol{f^{n}}\cong\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot f^{n}=R
and
\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot f\boldsymbol{f^{n}}\cong\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot f^{n+1}=R
, and therefore we have
\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot\boldsymbol{f^{n}}=\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot f\boldsymbol{f^{n}}
, thus proving (i).

For part (ii), suppose
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p),>0}
were a positive Bernstein-Sato root of
f
. Then
p\alpha+i>\alpha
for all
i=0,1,\dots,p-1
. By iterating Lemma
4.4
(ii) we would obtain an infinite number of Bernstein-Sato roots of
f
, which would contradict Theorem
4.2
.
∎

In this subsection our goal is to show that the Bernstein-Sato roots that are negative agree with the Bernstein-Sato root of the mod-
\mathfrak{m}
reduction of
f
(these, in turn, are characterized by the
F
-jumping numbers by work of the first author; see Theorem
1.2
).

To explain this precisely, we set up some notation. Throughout this subsection, given a
V
-module
M
we will use
M_{0}
to denote

M_{0}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}M/\mathfrak{m}M.

(Beware of the inconsistency with the stalk notation from Subsection
2.3
: even when
M
is a
C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V)
-module, this is not the stalk at
0\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.) Similarly, given an element
u\in M
, we denote by
u_{0}\in M_{0}
the class of
u
in the quotient. With this notation, we have that
R_{0}=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
is a polynomial ring over
\mathbb{K}
and, since
f\in R
was assumed to be a nonzerodivisor, we know that
f_{0}\in R_{0}
is nonzero.

Choose a lift of Frobenius
F:R\longrightarrow R
; by an abuse of notation, we will also use
F:R_{0}\longrightarrow R_{0}
to denote the Frobenius on
R_{0}
. Recall that we a description of
\mathcal{D}_{R}
as

\mathcal{D}_{R}=\bigcup_{e=0}^{\infty}\Hom_{R}(F^{e}_{*}R,F^{e}_{*}R).

Since we have
(F^{e}_{*}R)_{0}=F^{e}_{*}(R_{0})
(see proof of Lemma
2.20
), we conclude that

(\mathcal{D}_{R})_{0}=\mathcal{D}_{R_{0}}.

In particular, if
M
is a
\mathcal{D}_{R}
-module then
M_{0}
is naturally a
\mathcal{D}_{R_{0}}
-module.

Given a
p
-adic integer
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, the natural
\mathcal{D}_{R_{0}}
-module isomorphism
R[f^{-1}]_{0}\cong R_{0}[f_{0}^{-1}]
induces a
\mathcal{D}_{R_{0}}
-module isomorphism

(R[f^{-1}]\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}})_{0}\cong R_{0}[f_{0}^{-1}]\boldsymbol{f_{0}^{\alpha}}.

We will use the following result of Blickle, Mustaţă, and Smith; for simplicity, we state it only for polynomial rings.

Theorem 4.8

(
[
BMS09
, Thm. 2.11]
)
.

Let
R_{0}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial ring over a perfect field
\mathbb{K}
, and let
f_{0}\in R_{0}
be a nonzero element. If
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p),<0}
is a negative rational number whose denominator is not divisible by
p
, we have

\mathcal{D}_{R_{0}}\cdot\boldsymbol{f_{0}^{\alpha}}=R_{0}[f_{0}^{-1}]\boldsymbol{f_{0}^{\alpha}}.

Note that the module
R_{0}[f_{0}^{-1}]\boldsymbol{f_{0}^{\alpha}}
is denoted by
M_{-\alpha}
in
[
BMS09
]
, and that the element we denote as
\boldsymbol{f_{0}^{\alpha}}\in R_{0}[f_{0}^{-1}]\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}
is there denoted by
e_{-\alpha}\in M_{\alpha}
. We refer the reader to
[
BMS09
]
for the proof of the above result.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
\phi:M\longrightarrow N
be a homomorphism of
V
-modules. Then
\phi
is surjective if and only if the induced map
\phi_{0}:M_{0}\longrightarrow N_{0}
is surjective.

Proof.

The “only if” direction follows from the right exactness of the functor
(-)_{0}=(-)\otimes_{V}\mathbb{K}
. For the “if” direction, let
Q
be the cokernel of
\phi
, so that
M\longrightarrow N\longrightarrow Q\longrightarrow 0
is exact. By right exactness we get that
M_{0}\longrightarrow N_{0}\longrightarrow Q_{0}\longrightarrow 0
is exact, and hence
Q_{0}=0
. We conclude that
Q=\mathfrak{m}Q
, and hence
Q=\mathfrak{m}Q=\mathfrak{m}^{2}Q=\cdots=\mathfrak{m}^{m+1}Q=0
.
∎

Using Lemma
4.9
, we show that Theorem
4.8
also applies in our setting.

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
(V,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{K},F)
be as in Setup
2.1
, let
R\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}V[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial ring over
V
, let
f\in R
be a nonzerodivisor, and let
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p),<0}
be a negative rational number whose denominator is not divisible by
p
. We then have

\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}=R[f^{-1}]\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}.

Proof.

The goal is to show that the inclusion
\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}\longrightarrow R[f^{-1}]\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}
is surjective; by Lemma
4.9
, this can be checked after applying
(-)_{0}
. The image of the induced morphism
(\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}})_{0}\longrightarrow R_{0}[f^{-1}_{0}]\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}_{0}}
is
\mathcal{D}_{R_{0}}\cdot\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}_{0}}
, and the result then follows from Theorem
4.8
.
∎

Remark 4.11
.

Note that the proof of Corollary
4.10
works more generally to show the following. If
g\in R[f^{-1}]
is an element whose mod-
\mathfrak{m}
reduction
g_{0}\in R_{0}[f_{0}^{-1}]
satisfies
\mathcal{D}_{R_{0}}\cdot g_{0}\boldsymbol{f_{0}^{\alpha}}=R_{0}[f_{0}^{-1}]\boldsymbol{f_{0}^{\alpha}}
then we have
\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot g\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}=R[f^{-1}]\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}
.

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
(V,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{K},F)
be as in Setup
2.1
, let
R\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}V[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial ring over
V
, and let
f\in R
be a nonzerodivisor. Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p),<0}
be a negative rational number whose denominator is not divisible by
p
. Then
\alpha
is a Bernstein-Sato root of
f
if and only if it is a Bernstein-Sato root of
f_{0}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.17
,
\alpha
is a Bernstein-Sato root of
f
if and only if the natural inclusion
i:\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot f\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}
is an equality (i.e., surjective). By Lemma
4.9
this surjectivity is equivalent to the surjectivity of the induced map
i_{0}:(\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot f\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}})_{0}\longrightarrow(\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}})_{0}
. Similarly,
\alpha
is a Bernstein-Sato root of
f_{0}
if and only if the natural inclusion
j:\mathcal{D}_{R_{0}}\cdot f_{0}\boldsymbol{f_{0}^{\alpha}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{D}_{R_{0}}\cdot\boldsymbol{f_{0}^{\alpha}}
is an equality (i.e., surjective).

Note that
\mathcal{D}_{R_{0}}\cdot f_{0}\boldsymbol{f_{0}^{\alpha}}
is the image of
(\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot f\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}})_{0}\longrightarrow R_{0}[f_{0}^{-1}]\boldsymbol{f_{0}^{\alpha}}
, and similarly
\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot\boldsymbol{f_{0}^{\alpha}}
is the image of
(\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}})_{0}\longrightarrow R_{0}[f_{0}^{-1}]\boldsymbol{f_{0}^{\alpha}}
. This means that the relevant maps fit into a diagram as follows:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot f\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}})_{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}})_{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{D}_{R_{0}}\cdot f_{0}\boldsymbol{f_{0}^{\alpha}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{D}_{R_{0}}\cdot\boldsymbol{f_{0}^{\alpha}}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i_{0}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces j}

At this stage, one can already see that
j
is surjective whenever
i_{0}
is surjective; this proves the “if” direction of the statement. To prove the “only if” direction, we want to show that
i_{0}
is surjective whenever
j
is surjective, and for this it suffices to show that the right vertical arrow in the above diagram is an isomorphism. But this follows by Theorem
4.8
and Corollary
4.10
; indeed, we have:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}})_{0}

\displaystyle=(R[f^{-1}]\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}})_{0}

\displaystyle=R_{0}[f_{0}^{-1}]\boldsymbol{f_{0}^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}_{R_{0}}\cdot\boldsymbol{f_{0}^{\alpha}}.\qed

Remark 4.13
.

In the proof of Theorem
4.12
, the hypothesis on the negativity of
\alpha
is only used to ensure that that the natural map
(\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}})_{0}\longrightarrow\mathcal{D}_{R_{0}}\cdot\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}_{0}}
is an isomorphism.

4.3
Positive Bernstein-Sato roots

In this subsection we show through an example that there exist positive Bernstein-Sato roots, and we show that such roots can only arise as integer translates of negative roots. We begin with our example.

Example 4.14
.

Suppose
p\neq 2
, let
R\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{2})[X,Y]
, and consider
f\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}X^{2}+pY\in R
. We let
F:R\longrightarrow R
be the lift of Frobenius for which
F(X)=X^{p}
and
F(Y)=Y^{p}
. We claim that for all
e\geq 2
, we have

\nu^{\bullet}_{f}(F,p^{e})=\{kp^{e}-1\ |\ k\in\mathbb{Z}\}\cup\left\{\frac{kp^{e}-1}{2}\ \bigg|\ k\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ odd }\right\}\cup\left\{\frac{kp^{e}+1}{2}\ \bigg|\ k\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ odd}\right\}.

and therefore

\BSR(f)=\left\{-1,\frac{-1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right\}.

In particular, the polynomial
f
has a positive Bernstein-Sato root.

To prove the claim, recall that by the translation property of
\nu
-invariants (Proposition
3.16
(ii)), it suffices to compute the set
\nu^{\bullet}_{f}(F,p^{e})\cap[0,p^{e+1})
. Note that for all integers
e,n\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle F^{e}_{*}f^{n}

\displaystyle=F^{e}_{*}(X^{2}+pY)^{n}=F^{e}_{*}\big(X^{2n}+npX^{2n-2}Y\big)

\displaystyle=X^{\lfloor 2n/p^{e}\rfloor}F^{e}_{*}(X^{2n-p^{e}\lfloor 2n/p^{e}\rfloor})+npX^{\lfloor(2n-2)/p^{e}\rfloor}F^{e}_{*}(X^{2n-2-p^{e}\lfloor(2n-2)/p^{e}\rfloor}Y),

and from Proposition
2.28
we conclude that

\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{n}=\big(X^{\lfloor 2n/p^{e}\rfloor},\ np\ X^{\lfloor(2n-2)/p^{e}\rfloor}\big).

Therefore, for
n
to be a
\nu
-invariant one of the two generators above must change when
n
is replaced by
n+1
, and hence one must necessarily have
\lfloor 2n/p^{e}\rfloor\neq\lfloor 2n+2/p^{e}\rfloor
or
\lfloor(2n-2)/p^{e}\rfloor\neq\lfloor 2n/p^{e}\rfloor
. This forces
2n=kp^{e}+a
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
a\in\{-2,-1,0,1\}
. We analyze each of these cases separately:

\diamond

If
2n=kp^{e}-2
then
k
must be even, say
k=2k^{\prime}
, and thus
n=k^{\prime}p^{e}-1
. For
e\geq 2
, we get
\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{n}=(X^{k-1})
, while
\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{n+1}=(X^{k})
. Thus,
n=k^{\prime}p^{e}-1\in\nu^{\bullet}_{f}(F,p^{e})
.

\diamond

If
2n=kp^{e}-1
then
k
must be odd. Both
n
and
n+1
are units in
\mathbb{Z}/p^{2}
, and we get
\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{n}=(X^{k-1})
while
\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{n+1}=(X^{k},pX^{k-1})
. Thus,
n=(kp^{e}-1)/2\in\nu^{\bullet}_{f}(F,p^{e})
.

\diamond

If
2n=kp^{e}
then we get
\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{n}=(X^{k})=\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{n+1}
, and hence we don’t get
\nu
-invariants of this form.

\diamond

If
2n=kp^{e}+1
then
k
must be odd, and we get
\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{n}=(X^{k},pX^{k-1})
while
\mathcal{C}^{(F,e)}_{R}\cdot f^{n+1}=(X^{k})
. Therefore
n=(kp^{e}+1)/2\in\nu^{\bullet}_{f}(F,p^{e})
.

In the above example, note that the positive Bernstein-Sato root
1/2
is an integer translate of a negative one; namely:
1/2=-1/2+1
. We show that this is a general phenomenon.

Theorem 4.15
.

Let
(V,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{K},F)
be as in Setup
2.1
, let
R\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}V[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial ring over
V
, and let
f\in R
be a nonzerodivisor. Every Bernstein-Sato root of
f
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-translate of a negative one.

Proof.

We show that if
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}\cap[-1,0)
is not a Bernstein-Sato root of
f
then no integer translate can be a Bernstein-Sato root. Let
R_{0}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}R/\mathfrak{m}R
and
f_{0}\in R_{0}
be the mod-
\mathfrak{m}
reduction of
f
. Since
\alpha
is not a Bernstein-Sato root of
f
, we know that it is not a Bernstein-Sato root of
f_{0}
.

Since the Bernstein-Sato roots of
f_{0}
are contained in
[-1,0)
by
[
Bit18
]
(see Theorem
1.2
), we know that no integer translate of
\alpha
is a Bernstein-Sato root of
f_{0}
. This (combined with Theorem
3.17
and Remark
3.13
) implies that
\mathcal{D}_{R_{0}}\cdot f^{k}\boldsymbol{f_{0}^{\alpha}}=R_{0}[f_{0}^{-1}]\boldsymbol{f_{0}^{\alpha}}
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. By Remark
4.11
we conclude that
\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot f^{k}\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}=R[f^{-1}]\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}
, and therefore no integer translate of
\alpha
can be a Bernstein-Sato root of
f
.
∎

Corollary 4.16
.

Let
R_{0}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}R/\mathfrak{m}R
and let
f_{0}\in R_{0}
be the mod-
\mathfrak{m}
reduction of
f
. We then have

\BSR(f)+\mathbb{Z}=\BSR(f_{0})+\mathbb{Z}.

4.4
Strength of a Bernstein-Sato root and mod-
p
reduction

By considering the notion of multiplicity for a Bernstein-Sato root, we are lead to consider the following quantity (since eventually we observe this does not provide a good analogue of multiplicity, we give it its own name).

Definition 4.17
.

Let
(V,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{K},F)
be as in Setup
2.1
, let
R\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}V[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial ring over
V
, and let
f\in R
be a nonzerodivisor. Given a
p
-adic integer
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, the strength of
\alpha
in
f
is given by

\displaystyle\str(\alpha,f)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\min\{t\geq 0\ |\ (\mathfrak{m}:\alpha)^{t}\cdot(N_{f})_{\alpha}=0\}

If we fix
m\geq 0
such that
\mathfrak{m}^{m+1}=0
in
V
then
\str(\alpha,f)
is an integer with
0\leq\str(\alpha,f)\leq m+1
, and
\alpha
is a Bernstein-Sato root of
f
if and only if
\str(\alpha,f)\geq 1
. We now give a few alternative descriptions of strength.

First recall that we have a natural identification
(N_{f})_{\alpha}\cong N_{f}/(0:\alpha)N_{f}
, that evaluation at
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
defines a surjective map
C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V)\longrightarrow V
, and that given an ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq V
its preimage along this map
C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V)\longrightarrow V
is given by
(\mathfrak{a}:\alpha)
. With this in mind, we observe that
(\mathfrak{m}:\alpha)^{t}\cdot(N_{f})_{\alpha}=\mathfrak{m}^{t}\cdot(N_{f})_{\alpha}
for every
t\geq 0
, and therefore

\str(\alpha,f)=\min\{t\geq 0\ |\ \mathfrak{m}^{t}\cdot(N_{f})_{\alpha}=0\}.

Suppose that
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
are the Bernstein-Sato roots of
f
. Recall that the
b
-function of
f
takes the form

B_{f}=(\mathfrak{a}_{1}:\alpha_{1})\cdot(\mathfrak{a}_{2}:\alpha_{2})\cdots(\mathfrak{a}_{s}:\alpha_{s})\subseteq C(\mathbb{Z}_{p},V),

where
\mathfrak{a}_{i}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\Ann_{V}((N_{f})_{\alpha_{i}})
. We conclude that, for any
i=1,\dots,s
, we have

\str(\alpha_{i},f)=\min\{t\geq 0\ |\ \mathfrak{m}^{t}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}_{i}\}.

Let us describe what this entails in a fairly common situation.

Example 4.18
.

Suppose
(\mathcal{W},\mathfrak{m})
is a discrete valuation ring of residue characteristic
p>0
, and that
V=\mathcal{W}/\mathfrak{m}^{m+1}
. Then all ideals of
V
take the form
\mathfrak{m}^{t}
for some
0\leq t\leq m+1
. If
\Ann_{V}((N_{f})_{\alpha})=\mathfrak{m}^{t}
for some
0\leq t\leq m+1
, then
\str(\alpha,f)=t
.

Finally, by Corollary
3.10
we have an isomorphism

(N_{f})_{\alpha}\cong\frac{\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}}{\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot f\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}}

and, since
V
is central in
\mathcal{D}_{R}
, we get that
\mathfrak{m}^{k}(N_{f})_{\alpha}=0
if and only if
\mathfrak{m}^{k}\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot f\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}
in the module
R[f^{-1}]\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}
. We conclude that

\str(\alpha,f)=\min\{t\geq 0\ |\ \mathfrak{m}^{t}\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot f\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}\}.

Using this description, we can give a simple example.

Example 4.19
.

Suppose that
f
is one of the variables of
R
, say
f=x_{1}
, and that
\alpha=-1
. Let
m\geq 0
be the smallest nonnegative integer for which
\mathfrak{m}^{m+1}=0
in
V
. Recall that we have a natural
\mathcal{D}_{R}
-module isomorphism
R[x_{1}^{-1}]\boldsymbol{x_{1}^{-1}}\cong R[x_{1}^{-1}]
that exchanges
\boldsymbol{x_{1}^{-1}}
with
x_{1}^{-1}
and identifies
\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot x_{1}\boldsymbol{x_{1}^{-1}}\cong R
. Since
\mathfrak{m}^{t}x_{1}^{-1}\in R
if and only if
t\geq m+1
, we conclude that
\str(-1,x_{1})=m+1
.

Finally, we show that replacing
V
with a quotient can only make the strength decrease. More precisely, let
(V,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{K},F)
be as in Setup
2.1
, let
\mathfrak{b}\subseteq V
is a proper ideal with
F(\mathfrak{b})\subseteq\mathfrak{b}
, and let
\overline{V}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}V/\mathfrak{b}
. Then
\overline{V}
inherits the lift of Frobenius from
V
, and therefore still satisfies the conditions of Setup
2.1
.

Let
R\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}V[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial ring over
V
, and let
\overline{R}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\overline{V}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial ring over
\overline{V}
. Let
f\in R
be a nonzerodivisor, and let
\overline{f}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}[f\mod\mathfrak{b}R]\in\overline{R}
denote the image of
f
. Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
be a
p
-adic integer. Recall that there is a surjective ring homomorphism
\mathcal{D}_{R}\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{D}_{\bar{R}}
which identifies
\mathcal{D}_{\bar{R}}\cong\mathcal{D}_{R}/\mathfrak{b}\mathcal{D}_{R}
(since
V
is central in
\mathcal{D}_{R}
,
\mathfrak{b}\mathcal{D}_{R}
is a two-sided ideal). As a consequence, there is a natural correspondence between
\mathcal{D}_{\bar{R}}
-modules and
\mathcal{D}_{R}
-modules annihilated by
\mathfrak{b}
.

Proposition 4.20
.

With the notation as above, we have

\str(f,\alpha)\geq\str(\overline{f},\alpha).

Proof.

The natural isomorphism
\overline{R}[\bar{f}^{-1}]\cong R[f^{-1}]/\mathfrak{b}R[f^{-1}]
gives rise to a
\mathcal{D}_{R}
-module isomorphism

\overline{R}[\bar{f}^{-1}]\boldsymbol{\bar{f}^{\alpha}}\cong R[f^{-1}]\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}\big/\mathfrak{b}R[f^{-1}]\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}

which exchanges
\boldsymbol{\bar{f}^{\alpha}}
with the class of
\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}
. As a consequence, we have surjective homomorphisms
\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{D}_{\bar{R}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\bar{f}^{\alpha}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{R}\cdot f\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}}\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{D}_{\bar{R}}\cdot\bar{f}\boldsymbol{\bar{f}^{\alpha}}
. We conclude that
(N_{\bar{f}})_{\alpha}
is a quotient of
(N_{f})_{\alpha}
, and the result follows.
∎

We discuss how the strengths of a given Bernstein-Sato root can detect whether such a root arises in characteristic zero. We begin by placing ourselves in a mod-
p
reduction situation as follows.

Let
R_{\mathbb{C}}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial ring over
\mathbb{C}
, let
f\in R_{\mathbb{C}}
be a nonzero nonunit polynomial, and let
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{C}}
denote the ring of
\mathbb{C}
-linear differential operators of
R_{\mathbb{C}}
.

We let
b_{f}(s)\in\mathbb{C}[s]
denote the Bernstein-Sato polynomial of
f
; recall that it satisfies a functional equation of the form

b_{f}(s)f^{s}=P(s)\cdot f^{s+1}

(7)

for some differential operator
P(s)\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{C}}[s]
.

Given a finitely generated
\mathbb{Z}
-subalgebra
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, we let
R_{\mathcal{V}}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathcal{V}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be the subring of
R_{\mathbb{C}}
that consists of polynomials whose coefficients are in
\mathcal{V}
, and we let
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
denote the ring of
\mathcal{V}
-linear differential operators on
R_{\mathcal{V}}
. Note that there is a natural inclusion
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{C}}
.

We choose such a finitely generated
\mathbb{Z}
-subalgebra
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
such that
f\in R_{\mathcal{V}}
, such that
b_{f}(s)\in\mathcal{V}[s]
, and such that

Specializing Equation (

b_{f}(a)f^{a}=P(a)\cdot f^{a+1}

Choose a maximal ideal

, and
f_{m}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}[f\mod\mathfrak{m}^{m+1}R_{\mathcal{V}}]\in R_{m}

Theorem 4.21
.

Fix notation as above, let
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}
be a rational number whose denominator is not divisible by
p
, and let
m\geq 0
be an integer. In the
\mathcal{D}_{m}
-module
R_{m}[f_{m}^{-1}]\boldsymbol{f_{m}^{\alpha}}
we have

b_{f}(\alpha)\boldsymbol{f_{m}^{\alpha}}\in\mathcal{D}_{m}\cdot f_{m}\boldsymbol{f_{m}^{\alpha}}.

Proof.

Pick an integer
a\in\mathbb{Z}
for which
a\equiv\alpha\mod p^{m+1}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
(for example, by truncating the
p
-adic expansion of
\alpha
). By Equation (
8
), over
V_{m}
we obtain

\displaystyle b_{f}(\alpha)f_{m}^{a}

\displaystyle=b_{f}(a)f_{m}^{a}=P(a)_{m}\cdot f_{m}^{a+1}=P(\alpha)_{m}\cdot f_{m}^{a+1}.

Choose
e\gg 0
large enough so that
P(\alpha)_{m}\in\mathcal{D}_{m}^{(F,e)}
and, replacing
a
if necessary, assume that
a\equiv\alpha\mod p^{e+m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. We then get (see Subsection
3.4
)

\displaystyle P(\alpha)_{m}\cdot f_{m}\boldsymbol{f_{m}^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=f_{m}^{-a}(P(a)_{m}\cdot f_{m}^{a+1})\boldsymbol{f_{m}^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=f^{-a}_{m}(b_{f}(\alpha)f^{a}_{m})\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}_{m}}

\displaystyle=b_{f}(\alpha)\boldsymbol{f^{\alpha}_{m}}.\qed

Corollary 4.22
.

We have

\nu_{p}\big(b_{f}(\alpha)\big)\geq\str(\alpha,f_{m}),

where
\nu_{p}(-)
denotes
p
-adic valuation. In particular, whenever the sequence
(\str(\alpha,f_{m}))_{m=0}^{\infty}
is unbounded we have
b_{f}(\alpha)=0
.
