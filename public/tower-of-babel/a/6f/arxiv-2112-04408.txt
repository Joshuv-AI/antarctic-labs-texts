Amine Natik, Aaron Smith

Abstract
Consider a random graph G G of size N N constructed according to a graphon w : [ 0 , 1 ] 2 ↦ [ 0 , 1 ] w\,:\,[0,1]^{2}\mapsto[0,1] as follows. First embed N N vertices V = { v 1 , v 2 , … , v N } V=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{N}\} into the interval [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] , then for each i < j i<j add an edge between v i , v j v_{i},v_{j} with probability w ⁡ ( v i , v j ) w(v_{i},v_{j}) . Given only the adjacency matrix of the graph, we might expect to be able to approximately reconstruct the permutation σ \sigma for which v σ ⁡ ( 1 ) < … < v σ ⁡ ( N ) v_{\sigma(1)}<\ldots<v_{\sigma(N)} if w w satisfies the following linear embedding property introduced in [ 25 ] : for each x x , w ⁡ ( x , y ) w(x,y) decreases as y y moves away from x x . Introduction

In this paper, we study a statistical version of the
seriation problem
, first introduced by Robinson in
[
35
]
as a combinatorial problem. In this problem, we get to observe a graph, and our goal is to order the vertices in such a way that more similar items are placed near to each other. The pioneer of seriation is considered to be Flinders Petrie, an Egyptologist, who called it
sequence dating
in
[
31
]
. While the seriation originates from archaeology, it has recent applications to ecology, sociology, biology, and other topics. See
[
27
]
for a historical overview on the seriation problem.

We relate the seriation problem from
[
31
]
to the notation used in this article. We begin with a collection of
N\in\mathbb{N}
objects, with the
i
’th object associated with an unobserved point
v_{i}\in[0,1]
. In
[
31
]
, these objects corresponds to a set of graves, with
v_{i}
the age of the
i
’th grave. We then get to observe a symmetric graph
G=(V,E)
with vertex set
V=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{N}\}
. Informally, we expect that an edge is more likely to appear between vertices
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
that are closer together. Continuing the example of
[
31
]
, the observed graph is constructed by putting an edge between
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
if we find the same type of artifacts in both the
i
’th and
j
’th graves. This co-occurrence is more likely when the two graves have similar ages. The
goal
in seriation is then to find an ordering
\sigma\,:\,V\mapsto\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
for which
v_{\sigma(1)}<v_{\sigma(2)}<\ldots<v_{\sigma(N)}
. In
[
31
]
, this means inferring a chronological ordering of the true ages of the set of graves by observing the different types of archaeological finds on each grave.

Many versions of the seriation problem have been studied. Perhaps the simplest occurs when the observed graph satisfies the following property: for all
v_{i}<v_{j}<v_{k}
,

(v_{i},v_{k})\in E\implies(v_{i},v_{j})\in E\ \text{and}\ (v_{j},v_{k})\in E.

(1.1)

Graphs satisfying this property are called
Robinsonian
, named after Robinson who first introduced this property in
[
35
]
. We call this the
noiseless
seriation problem, as there will never be any “missing" edges and a perfect ordering exists.

Remark 1.1

(Reversals and Recovery)
.

Since we can only observe the graph
G
, there is no way to distinguish between an ordering
\sigma
and its reversal
\mathrm{rev}(\sigma)
, defined by

\mathrm{rev}(\sigma)(i)=N+1-\sigma(i).

(1.2)

This means that, although we say that we wish to find an ordering
\hat{\sigma}
that is close to the ordering
\sigma
that satisfies
v_{\sigma(1)}<v_{\sigma(2)}<\ldots<v_{\sigma(N)}
, the best that we can hope for is an ordering
\hat{\sigma}
that is close to
either

\sigma

or

\mathrm{rev}(\sigma)

\hat{\sigma}
and
\mathrm{rev}(\hat{\sigma})
.

To avoid repeatedly discussing this small non-identifiability issue, we usually hide it by the following small abuse of notation. For any metric

d(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})=\min(d(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}),d(\mathrm{rev}(\sigma_{1}),\sigma_{2}))

(1.3)

unless we explicitly say otherwise. We only need to pay attention to this issue in step 3 of our post-processing algorithm (Algorithm

This noiseless version of the seriation problem has been well-studied in the computer science literature, and there are several algorithms that are known to return a correct

1.1
Previous Work and Main Contributions

Informally, the main results of our paper are that (i) the popular spectral embedding algorithm gives reasonably good results for the seriation problem, and (ii) a simple post-processing step can be added to obtain very good results. We view this as important reassurance for practitioners: spectral embedding algorithms are very popular, but there was little theoretical support for using these algorithms to fit anything but a few small parametric families of models. Our results provide evidence for the folk belief that spectral algorithms, with appropriate coarse post-processing, are quite insensitive to the details of the generating process
w
.

There are many papers in the literature that have either similar-looking results or similar-looking methods, so we give a quick survey and then summarize the most interesting differences.

1.1.1
Previous Work: Similar Algorithms and Proofs, Different Questions

Spectral embedding algorithms are very popular in many contexts, especially in the
spectral clustering
literature (see e.g.
[
16
,
40
]
and variants such as
[
26
,
6
,
3
]
). The idea behind all these algorithms is to calculate the first few eigenvectors of a matrix related to your data matrix, use these to embed your data in a low-dimensional space, then use a popular clustering algorithm such as
k
-means to cluster points in this representation. As with the seriation problem, this is known to give exactly the desired answer in simple “noiseless" situations.

In “noisy" situations, the most common analyses follow the approach of
[
41
]
. In that paper, the authors show that, under appropriate conditions, the eigenvectors of the observed data matrix will converge to the eigenfunctions of a certain limiting operator. When this convergence happens, the associated spectral embedding will also converge to an embedding related to the limiting operator. One can then bound the difference between the clustering associated with the observed data and the limiting operator, obtaining a consistency result.

The proof of our first main bound, Inequality (
1.7
), follows essentially this approach. We emphasize two main differences:

1.

Definition of “correct" answer:
In spectral clustering, it is often straightforward to check that the clustering associated with the limiting operator is “correct," and sometimes this clustering is even taken as the definition of a “correct" clustering. In seriation, the correct ordering has an independent definition in terms of the embedded positions
v_{1},\ldots,v_{N}
. This means that we must check that the seriation obtained from the limiting operator actually agrees with this ordering. Checking this turns out to be non-trivial unless the generating graphon
w
is very special, and relating the limiting operator to the embedded positions takes up most of Section
3
.

2.

Difficulty of problem:
In spectral clustering, the “correct" clustering maps vertices to a finite set
\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
, where
k
does not grow with the size
N
of the observed graph. In seriation, the “correct" ordering is a permutation on
\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
. The fact that the seriation problem attempts to estimate a much higher-dimensional object makes the problem substantially messier in a few ways. For example, it is often possible to perfectly recover the correct clustering with a finite sample size even without precise information about the data-generating process (see
e.g.

[
10
]
), but it is usually not possible to perfectly recover the solution to the seriation problem without having very accurate a priori knowledge of the graphon
w
(see
e.g.

[
25
]
).

We next relate our second main result, Inequality (
1.8
), to the existing literature.

Although analysis of the spectral embedding gives useful information, there are many possible spectral embeddings and it is difficult to find and analyze the “best" one (see e.g. the discussion of various Laplacians in
[
41
]
). An alternative is to use a simple spectral embedding to obtain an initial rough solution, then use a simple post-processing algorithm to refine it. This is known to work well for the stochastic block model, the most popular toy model for the spectral clustering problem
[
1
]
.

We take a similar approach here to prove Inequality (
1.8
). The main novelty is that we need to develop a post-processing algorithm, as the algorithms used for spectral clustering are not appropriate. Again, the fact that exact recovery is impossible adds some complication to our analysis.

1.1.2
Previous Work: Similar Questions

Our work was inspired by
[
36
,
19
]
, which studied random graphs that were very similar, respectively, to the one-parameter graphon

are:

1.

Inequality (

):
We deal with a large nonparametric family of models, while

]

[

2.

Inequality (

Using our post-processing algorithm, we obtain sharper results than previous work (even in the special cases that were focused on).

3.

Technical differences:
Both

The results in this paper are also closely related to those in

Finally, we note that there is a related literature on detecting and recovering embeddings on the

1.1.3
Previous Work: Summary and Further Reading

Our work belongs to a very large literature on spectral embedding methods, and a smaller literature on attempting to order or embed vertices on an interval or cycle. The most important differences are:

1.

In contrast to most previous work on related questions, we consider a large nonparametric family of graphons. In particular, while our assumptions appear more technical than theirs, this is largely because we are considering a much broader class of models - they are often straightforward to check for the most common explicit parametric families.

2.

In contrast to previous work on spectral clustering, we must check that the embeddings associated with our limiting operators actually give good solutions to the original problem.

3.

In contrast to our own previous work
[
25
]
, we consider both a large nonparametric families of graphons
and
a simple, algorithm based on a widely-used approach.

Finally, we mention recent work on several problems that have a similar feel to seriation, but which are not directly comparable to our note. Several papers, such as
[
18
]
and
[
33
]
, have studied what amount to other relaxation of the condition in Equation (
1.1
). These relaxations are also reasonable versions of the “statistical" or “approximate" seriation problem, though the details are quite different. There have also been many articles on the closely-related problem of estimating a permutation from pairwise comparisons (see
e.g.

[
30
]
) or other estimates related to the entries of a random matrix whose expected value is a Robinsonian matrix. See the longer paper
[
25
]
for a longer section relating our statistical seriation question to this work.

\widehat{P}^{(N)}_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{ind}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{B}\text{ernoulli}\left(\rho_{N}w(\frac{i}{N},\frac{j}{N})\right).

\widehat{w}_{N}(x,y):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\widehat{P}_{ij}^{(N)}1_{\mathcal{I}_{i}^{N}}(x)1_{\mathcal{I}_{j}^{N}}(y).

We next need some basic notation related to operators. We denote by

the usual Hilbert space on (equivalence classes of) functions
f\,:\,[0,1]\mapsto\mathbb{R}

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbb{T}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}:=\sup_{f\in L^{2}([0,1])\ \mathrm{s.t.}\ \left\|f\right\|=1}\left\|\mathbb{T}f\right\|.

For a self-adjoint bounded linear operator

such that

is not bijective. We recall some basic facts about the spectrum (see

[

\sigma(\mathbb{T})
which consists of all isolated points with finite algebraic multiplicity, which means that if
\lambda\in\sigma_{d}(\mathbb{T})
then
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\mathbb{T}
, that is there is some non-zero
f\in L^{2}([0,1])
such that
\mathbb{T}f=\lambda f
, moreover
\cup_{r=1}^{\infty}\ker((\mathbb{T}-\lambda\mathbb{I})^{r})
is finite dimensional. We also define the
essential spectrum

\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(\mathbb{T})=\sigma(\mathbb{T})\setminus\sigma_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbb{T})
. According to
[
17
]
the essential spectrum
\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(\mathbb{T})
is the set of all numbers
\lambda
in the spectrum
\sigma(\mathbb{T})
of
\mathbb{T}
such that at least one of the following conditions holds:

(i)

the range
R(\mathbb{T}-\lambda\mathbb{I})
of
\mathbb{T}-\lambda\mathbb{I}
is not closed;

(ii)

\lambda
is a limit point of
\sigma(\mathbb{T})

2.3
Spectral Theory of the Graphon-Laplacian

We define the
graphon-Laplacian operator

\mathbb{L}
for a given graphon

\displaystyle(\mathbb{L}f)(x)

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{1}w(x,y)(f(x)-f(y))dy

\displaystyle=f(x)d(x)-\int_{0}^{1}w(x,y)f(y)dy,

where

is the degree of

. The operator
\mathbb{L}
for the graphon

is the analogous of the Laplacian matrix of a graph. Similarly, let
\mathbb{L}_{N}
and
\widehat{\mathbb{L}}_{N}
denote the Laplacian operators associated to graphons

and
\widehat{w}_{N}
respectively, as defined in Equation (

).

We make some remarks on the spectrum of
\mathbb{L}
. Note that the operator
(\mathbb{W}f)(x):=\int_{0}^{1}w(x,y)f(y)dy
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator (see e.g.
[
34

), thus
\mathbb{W}
is compact. Nevertheless,
\mathbb{L}
is generally not compact since it is the sum of the compact operator
\mathbb{W}
and the multiplication operator
f(x)\mapsto d(x)f(x)
. It is well known that adding a compact operator does not change the essential spectrum (see e.g.,
[
23
]
), so the essential spectrum of
\mathbb{L}
is the same as the essential spectrum of the multiplication operator
f(x)\mapsto d(x)f(x)
. The essential spectrum of this operator is known to be the range of the degree function
d
, i.e.,
\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(\mathbb{L})=rg(d)
(see,
e.g.
,
[
41
]
). Note that the linear operator
\mathbb{L}
is self-adjoint, bounded and positive.

It is easy to see that
\mathbb{L}e=0
, which means that
\lambda_{1}=0
is an eigenvalue of
\mathbb{L}
. In fact it is the smallest eigenvalue, as:

\langle\mathbb{L}f,f\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}w(x,y)(f(x)-f(y))^{2}dxdy\geq 0.

(2.3)

If
\mathbb{L}f=0
and
w(x,y)
is strictly positive for almost all
x,y
, then Inequality (
2.3
) implies that
f\in\mathrm{Vect}(e)
.

We note here that any Robinsonian graphon

2.4
Statement of Main Results

The main results relies on the following conditions on the graphon:

Assumption 1
(Lipschitz graphon).

There exists a constant
K>0
such that

\left|w(x,y)-w(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\right|\leq K(\left|x-x^{\prime}\right|+\left|y-y^{\prime}\right|)

for all pairs
(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2},(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in[0,1]^{2}
.

Assumption 2
(Differentiable graphon).

The partial derivative
\partial w(x,\cdot)/\partial x
both exists and is non-zero almost everywhere.

Remark 2.1
.

We note that Assumption
2
is violated by the popular toy graphon model

, and so Theorem
2
will not apply as-written to these models. This is somewhat unsatisfying, as this model and very similar ones were studied in
[
36
,
5
,
4
]

Fortunately, this problem is easy to fix, and our arguments were written with this in mind. The proof of Theorem
2
only uses Assumption
2
inside of the proofs of the intermediate results Theorems
4
and
5
. Thus, both the conclusion and the proof of Theorem
2
remain correct if we assume the conclusions of Theorems
4
and
5
directly.

More precisely, Theorem
2
remains true as stated if we substitute the following pair of assumptions for Assumption
2
:

1.

There exists a Fiedler function
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1])
and
L>0
such that
\inf_{x\in[0,1]}\phi^{\prime}(x)=L
(this corresponds to Theorem
4
).

2.

The eigenspace corresponding to eigenvalue

is one-dimensional (this corresponds to Theorem

These two assumptions can be checked directly for the toy model

Assumption 3
.

The derivative
d^{\prime}(x)
exists for all

and the set

Assumption 4

Let

be the graphon-Laplacian operator associated with

Note that

Properties of the Fiedler value and the Fiedler function

In this section we establish basic facts about the spectrum of the graphon-Laplacian operator

as in Equation (

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

\mathbb{L}
and

is discrete and

is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
in

.

In particular, if

\mathcal{C}^{1}
in

First we show that under Assumption
3
the set

is discrete. Let
N=\{x\in[0,1]:d^{\prime}(x)=0\}
as in Assumption
3
. Since
N
is discrete, it is enough to show that

is discrete. Let
x\in D^{\prime}
, so that

and

. Moreover,
d(x+h)-\lambda=d(x+h)-d(x)=d^{\prime}(x)h+o(h)
, hence

for all

in some neighborhood

of

. Consequently every point

has a neighborhood

such that

as required.

Now take

and
\Delta>0
such that
x+\delta\notin D_{\lambda}
for all

f(x)d(x)-\int_{0}^{1}f(y)w(x,y)dy=\lambda f(x).

Plugging

\displaystyle\frac{f(x+\delta)-f(x)}{\delta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d(x+\delta)-\lambda}\left(\int_{0}^{1}(f(y)-f(x))\left(\frac{w(x+\delta,y)-w(x,y)}{\delta}\right)dy\right)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\delta\to 0]{}~\frac{1}{d(x)-\lambda}\left(\int_{0}^{1}(f(y)-f(x))\frac{\partial w(x,y)}{\partial x}\right)dy.

For the integral convergence, we apply the dominated convergence theorem. This is justified by the fact that, by Assumption

1
, we have that

and for all

(3.1)

By a similar argument we can show that

, consequently

\mathcal{C}^{1}
on

.

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\lambda f^{\prime}(x)=f^{\prime}(x)d(x)+f(x)d^{\prime}(x)-\int_{0}^{1}f(y)\frac{\partial w(x,y)}{\partial x}dy

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\lambda f^{\prime}(x)=f^{\prime}(x)d(x)-\int_{0}^{1}(f(y)-f(x))\frac{\partial w(x,y)}{\partial x}dy

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\lambda f^{\prime}(x)=f^{\prime}(x)d(x)-\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\left(f^{\prime}(z)\frac{\partial w(x,y)}{\partial x}\mathbf{1}_{\{x\leq z\leq y\}}\right)dzdy

Lemma 3.3

There exists

\phi(x)=\int_{0}^{x}f^{*}(t)\mathrm{d}t
for all
x\in[0,1]
. By Lemma
3.2
we know that
\mathbb{L}\phi=\lambda_{2}\phi
, thus
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1])
is a Fiedler function with
\phi^{\prime}(x)=f^{*}(x)>0
for all
x\in[0,1]
.

Finally, since
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1])
and
\phi^{\prime}(x)>0
for all
x\in[0,1]
, we have
\inf_{x\in[0,1]}\phi^{\prime}(x)>0
.
∎

Finally, we present the proof of Theorem
5
:

Let
\phi
as in the statement of Theorem
4
. We prove Theorem
5
by contradiction. If the eigenspace is not one-dimensional, there exists some other Fiedler function
\varphi\notin\mathrm{Vect}(\phi)
. Since
\lambda_{2}\notin rg(d)
by Corollary
3.4
, we have that
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1])
as well. Let
a
be such that

\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(a)}{\phi^{\prime}(a)}=\min_{x\in[0,1]}\left(\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(x)}{\phi^{\prime}(x)}\right).

Put
\psi=\phi^{\prime}(a)\varphi-\varphi^{\prime}(a)\phi
, so that
\psi
is an other Fiedler function and satisfies
\psi^{\prime}(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in[0,1]
and
\psi^{\prime}(a)=0
. But this implies
\mathbb{M}\psi^{\prime}(a)=\lambda_{2}\psi^{\prime}(a)=0
. Following the argument in the proof of Lemma
3.3
from Equation (
3.3
), this implies that
\psi=0
in
L^{2}([0,1])
, which is a contradiction.
∎

4

We follow the conventions established at the start of Section

w

1
to

\mathbb{L}
as in Equation (

In this section, we will check that the observed graph Laplacian converges to the limiting Laplacian

4.1

The results in this section are very similar to the results in the paper
[

. In this section we prove that two approximations to

converge quickly. But first, we set some notations. Define

and similarly

K
be as in Assumption

and

\displaystyle N_{1}(w,\tau,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\min\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,N\geq n\Rightarrow d_{\mathrm{MAX}}\geq\frac{4\log\left(4N/\varepsilon\right)}{9\rho_{N}N}+\frac{2K}{N}\right\},

(4.1)

\displaystyle N^{\prime}_{1}(w,\tau,\gamma)

\displaystyle=\min\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,N\geq n\Rightarrow d_{\mathrm{MAX}}\geq\frac{4\log\left(4N/\varepsilon_{\gamma}(N)\right)}{9\rho_{N}N}+\frac{2K}{N}\right\}.

(4.2)

Recall that

and

are well defined. In fact the term

In order to prove the convergence of the Laplacian operator we need the following simple technical lemma:

Lemma 4.1
.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
\gamma>1
. Put

and
N^{\prime}_{1}=N^{\prime}_{1}(w,\tau,\gamma)
. Then for all
N\geq N_{1}(w,\tau,\varepsilon)
we have

d_{\mathrm{MAX}}^{(N)}\geq\frac{4\log\left(4N/\varepsilon\right)}{9\rho_{N}}.

Similarly, for all
N\geq N^{\prime}_{1}
we have

d_{\mathrm{MAX}}^{(N)}\geq\frac{4\log\left(4N/\varepsilon_{\gamma}(N)\right)}{9\rho_{N}}.

For
N\geq N_{1}
, let

for some
i\in[N]
. Then

\displaystyle\left|d(x)-d_{N}(x)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{1}\left(w(x,y)-w_{N}(x,y)\right)dy\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{\mathcal{I}_{j}^{N}}\left|w(x,y)-w(\frac{i}{N},\frac{j}{N})\right|dy

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2K}{N},

where the inequality from line two to line three follows from Assumption
1
. Therefore
d_{N}(x)\geq d(x)-\frac{2K}{N}
, so

\displaystyle\max_{x\in[0,1]}d_{N}(x)

\displaystyle\geq d_{\mathrm{MAX}}-\frac{2K}{N}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{4\log(4N/\varepsilon)}{9\rho_{N}N}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\max_{i=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}P_{ij}^{(N)}\right)

\displaystyle=N\max_{x\in[0,1]}\left(d_{N}(x)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{4\log(4N/\varepsilon)}{9\rho_{N}}.

As required. The proof of the second inequality for
N\geq N^{\prime}_{1}(w,\tau,\gamma)
is identical to the previous proof (replace
\varepsilon
by
\varepsilon_{\gamma}(N)
).
∎

Theorem 4.2
(Convergence of graphon Laplacian).

Let
K
be as in Assumption
1
. Then

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbb{L}_{N}-\mathbb{L}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq\frac{4K}{N}.

(4.3)

Moreover, for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and all
N\geq N_{1}(w,\tau,\varepsilon)
as in Equation (
4.1
),

\mathbb{P}\left({\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\rho_{N}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{L}}_{N}-\mathbb{L}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq 4\sqrt{\frac{\log(4N/\varepsilon)}{\rho_{N}N}}+\frac{4K}{N}\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon.

(4.4)

Additionally, for all
\gamma>1
the same inequality holds for all
N\geq N^{\prime}_{1}(w,\tau,\gamma)
if we replace
\varepsilon
by
\varepsilon_{\gamma}(N)
, where
N^{\prime}_{1}
is defined in (
4.2
).

We prove the two inequalities (

) and (
4.4

Let
f\in L^{2}([0,1])
such that
\left\|f\right\|=1
. Then

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{L}_{N}f-\mathbb{L}f\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\mathbb{L}_{N}f-\mathbb{L}f\right)^{2}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{0}^{1}(f(x)-f(y))(w_{N}(x,y)-w(x,y))dy\right)^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{0}^{1}(f(x)-f(y))^{2}dy\right)\left(\int_{0}^{1}(w_{N}(x,y)-w(x,y))^{2}dy\right)dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{\mathcal{I}_{i}^{N}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}(f(x)-f(y))^{2}dy\right)\left(\int_{0}^{1}(w_{N}(x,y)-w(x,y))^{2}dy\right)dx,

(4.5)

where from line two to three we used Cauchy-Schwartz inequality. For fixed
i\in[N]
and
x\in\mathcal{I}_{i}^{N}
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(w_{N}(x,y)-w(x,y))^{2}dy

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{\mathcal{I}_{j}^{N}}(w(\frac{i}{N},\frac{j}{N})-w(x,y))^{2}dy

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{\mathcal{I}_{j}^{N}}K^{2}\left(\left|\frac{i}{N}-x\right|+\left|\frac{j}{N}-y\right|\right)^{2}dy

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{\mathcal{I}_{j}^{N}}\frac{4K^{2}}{N^{2}}dy

\displaystyle=\frac{4K^{2}}{N^{2}}.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(f(x)-f(y))^{2}dy

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}2(f(x)^{2}+f(y)^{2})dy

\displaystyle=2f(x)^{2}+2.

Applying the last two calculations with Inequality (
4.5
),

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{L}_{Nf}-\mathbb{L}f\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8K^{2}}{N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{\mathcal{I}_{i}^{N}}\left(f(x)^{2}+1\right)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{16K^{2}}{N^{2}}.

This immediately implies (
4.3
).
∎

From the triangle inequality and Inequality (
4.3
),

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\rho_{N}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{L}}_{N}-\mathbb{L}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\rho_{N}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{L}}_{N}-\mathbb{L}_{N}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}+{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbb{L}_{N}-\mathbb{L}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\rho_{N}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{L}}_{N}-\mathbb{L}_{N}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}+\frac{4K}{N}.

(4.6)

Since both
\widehat{\mathbb{L}}_{N}
and
\mathbb{L}_{N}
have finite rank, it is not hard to see that

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\rho_{N}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{L}}_{N}-\mathbb{L}_{N}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}\left(\rho_{N}^{-1}\widehat{L}^{(N)}-L^{(N)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{N}^{-1}}{N}\left\|\widehat{L}^{(N)}-\rho_{N}L^{(N)}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\rho_{N}^{-1}}{N}\left(\left\|\widehat{D}^{(N)}-\rho_{N}D^{(N)}\right\|+\left\|\widehat{P}^{(N)}-\rho_{N}P^{(N)}\right\|\right).

(4.7)

Fix
N\geq N_{1}(w,\tau,\varepsilon)
as in (
4.1
), then the maximum expected degree of the random graph
G^{(N)}
is
\rho_{N}d_{\mathrm{MAX}}^{(N)}\geq\frac{4}{9}\log(4N/\varepsilon)
. Applying Theorem 1 of
[
8
]
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\widehat{P}^{(N)}-\rho_{N}P^{(N)}\right\|\leq\sqrt{4\rho_{N}d_{\mathrm{MAX}}^{(N)}\log(4N/\varepsilon)}\right)\geq 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

(4.8)

Now we give a similar bound for
\left\|\widehat{D}^{(N)}-\rho_{N}D^{(N)}\right\|
. Let
\Delta^{(N)}=\widehat{D}^{(N)}-\rho_{N}D^{(N)}
and
X^{(N)}=\widehat{P}^{(N)}-\rho_{N}P^{(N)}
.

We note that

\sum_{j}\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}\left(X_{ij}\right)=\sum_{j}\rho_{N}P_{ij}^{(N)}(1-\rho_{N}P_{ij}^{(N)})\leq\rho_{N}\sum_{j}P_{ij}^{(N)}\leq\rho_{N}d_{\mathrm{MAX}}^{(N)}.

(4.9)

Then by Bernstein’s inequality, for each
t>0

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\Delta_{i}^{(N)}|>t\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N}X_{ij}^{(N)}\right|>t\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp{\left(\frac{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}}{\sum_{j}\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}\left(X_{ij}\right)+\frac{1}{3}t}\right)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Ineq. (}\ref{IneqSimpleIneqForBern}\text{)}}}{{\leq}}2\exp{\left(\frac{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}}{\rho_{N}d_{\mathrm{MAX}}^{(N)}+\frac{1}{3}t}\right)}.

Taking a union bound,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\Delta^{(N)}\right\|>t\right)\leq 2N\exp{\left(\frac{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}}{\rho_{N}d_{\mathrm{MAX}}^{(N)}+\frac{1}{3}t}\right)}.

Choosing
t=\sqrt{4\rho_{N}d_{\mathrm{MAX}}^{(N)}\log\left(4N/\varepsilon\right)}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\Delta^{(N)}\right\|>\sqrt{4\rho_{N}d_{\mathrm{MAX}}^{(N)}\log\left(4N/\varepsilon\right)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2N\exp{\left(-\frac{2\rho_{N}d_{\mathrm{MAX}}^{(N)}\log(4N/\varepsilon)}{\rho_{N}d_{\mathrm{MAX}}^{(N)}+\frac{1}{3}\sqrt{4\rho_{N}d_{\mathrm{MAX}}^{(N)}\log\left(4N/\varepsilon\right)}}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq 2N\exp{\left(-\frac{2\rho_{N}d_{\mathrm{MAX}}^{(N)}\log(4N/\varepsilon)}{\rho_{N}d_{\mathrm{MAX}}^{(N)}+\rho_{N}d_{\mathrm{MAX}}^{(N)}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Recalling that
\left\|\Delta^{(N)}\right\|=\left\|\widehat{D}^{(N)}-\rho_{N}D^{(N)}\right\|
and

, this implies

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\widehat{D}^{(N)}-\rho_{N}D^{(N)}\right\|\leq\sqrt{4\rho_{N}N\log\left(4N/\varepsilon\right)}\right)\geq 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

(4.10)

For
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we substitute (
4.8
) and (
4.10
) in (
4.7
) to get

\mathbb{P}\left({\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\rho_{N}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{L}}_{N}-\mathbb{L}_{N}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq 4\sqrt{\frac{\log(4N/\varepsilon)}{\rho_{N}N}}\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon.

(4.11)

Finally, applying inequality (
4.6
), we get that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and all

in the sense of (

\mathbb{P}\left({\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\rho_{N}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{L}}_{N}-\mathbb{L}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq 4\sqrt{\frac{\log(4N/\varepsilon)}{\rho_{N}N}}+\frac{4K}{N}\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon,

completing the proof. Again, replace in the

4.2
Convergence of the Fiedler function

In order to prove the convergence of the Fiedler function we use a result by Davis and Kahan
[
11
]
known as the
\sin(\theta)
-theorem for self-adjoint operators.

Theorem 6
(Davis-Kahan).

Let
\mathbb{A}
and
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}
be two self-adjoint operators with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\lambda_{3}<\cdots
and

\tilde{\lambda}_{1}<\tilde{\lambda}_{2}<\tilde{\lambda}_{3}<\cdots
respectively. Consider eigenfunctions
f
and
\tilde{f}
of
\mathbb{A}
and
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}
respectively with eigenvalues
\lambda_{2}
and
\tilde{\lambda}_{2}
respectively, and normalized so that
\left\|f\right\|=\|\tilde{f}\|=1
and
\langle f,\tilde{f}\rangle\geq 0
. If
0<\lambda_{2}<\lambda_{3}
(i.e.,
\lambda_{2}
is isolated), then

\left\|\tilde{f}-f\right\|\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}-\mathbb{A}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}}{\min\{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1},\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{2}\}}.

Applying this to
\mathbb{L}
and
\rho_{N}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{L}}_{N}
immediately gives:

Theorem 7
.

Let
\phi
and
\hat{\phi}_{N}
be the Fiedler functions of
\mathbb{L}
and
\rho_{N}\widehat{\mathbb{L}}_{N}
respectively, normalized so that
\|\phi\|=\|\hat{\phi}_{N}\|=1
and
\langle\phi,\hat{\phi}_{N}\rangle\geq 0
. Fix
\eta<\frac{1-\tau}{3}
, then for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
there exists a positive constant
C=C(w)
and an integer
N_{0}=N_{0}(w,\tau,\eta,\varepsilon)
so that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\hat{\phi}_{N}-\phi\right\|\leq C\sqrt{N^{-3\eta}}\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon

for all
N\geq N_{0}
.

Moreover, for any
\gamma>1
there exists a positive constant
C^{\prime}=C^{\prime}(w)
and an integer
N^{\prime}_{0}=N^{\prime}_{0}(w,\tau,\eta,\gamma)
so that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\hat{\phi}_{N}-\phi\right\|\leq C^{\prime}\sqrt{N^{-3\eta}}\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon_{\gamma}(N)

for all
N\geq N^{\prime}_{0}
.

Define
N_{0}=N_{0}(w,\tau,\eta,\varepsilon)
and
N^{\prime}_{0}=N^{\prime}_{0}(w,\tau,\eta,\gamma)
by

\displaystyle N_{0}(w,\tau,\eta,\varepsilon)=\min\left\{n\geq N_{1}(w,\tau,\varepsilon)\,:\,N\geq n\Rightarrow 4\sqrt{\frac{\log(4N/\varepsilon)}{\rho_{N}N}}+\frac{4K}{N}\leq\sqrt{N^{-3\eta}}\right\}

\displaystyle N^{\prime}_{0}(w,\tau,\eta,\gamma)=\min\left\{n\geq N^{\prime}_{1}(w,\tau,\eta)\,:\,N\geq n\Rightarrow 4\sqrt{\frac{\log(4N/\varepsilon_{\gamma}(N))}{\rho_{N}N}}+\frac{4K}{N}\leq\sqrt{N^{-3\eta}}\right\}

where
N_{1}
and
N^{\prime}_{1}
are defined in (
4.1
), (
4.2
) respectively. Note that both
N_{0}
and
N^{\prime}_{0}
are well defined since the left hand sides of the inequalities inside
N_{0}
and
N^{\prime}_{0}
respectively are on the order of
\mathcal{O}(N^{-(1-\tau)/2}\log^{1/2}(N))
and
\mathcal{O}(N^{-(1-\tau)/2}\log^{\gamma/2}(N))
respectively, which converges faster than the right hand sides of order
\mathcal{O}(N^{-3\eta/2})
(recall that
\tfrac{3\eta}{2}<\tfrac{1-\tau}{2}
).

Take
N\geq N_{0}
, by Theorem
4.2
we know that with a probability of at least
1-\varepsilon

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\rho_{N}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{L}}_{N}-\mathbb{L}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{\frac{\log(4N/\varepsilon)}{\rho_{N}N}}+\frac{4K}{N}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{N^{-3\eta}}.

We apply the Davis-Kahan Theorem
6
to the Fiedler functions
\phi
and
\hat{\phi}_{N}
(note that the theorem applies here since under our assumptions
\lambda_{2}
is simple by Theorem
5
). We have with probability at least
1-\varepsilon

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{\phi}_{N}-\phi\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\rho_{N}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{L}}_{N}-\mathbb{L}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}}{\min\{\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{2}\}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\min\{\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{2}\}}\sqrt{N^{-3\eta}}

as required. We get the same result for the case
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{\gamma}(N)
and

.
∎

5
Error bound for the spectral seriation algorithm

In this section, we prove Theorem
2
. The method we use to bound the errors of the permutation are very similar to the method used in the paper
[
36
]
. We follow the notation used in the statement of Theorem
2
, denote by
\phi
the Fiedler eigenfunction of
\mathbb{L}
, and denote by
\hat{\phi}_{N}
the Fiedler vector appearing in step 3 of Algorithm
1
with input
G^{(N)}
. By a small abuse of notation, for
i\in[N]
we define
\hat{\phi}_{i}
to be the
i
’th element of
{\phi}_{N}
. Similarly, for
x\in[0,1]
, we define

\hat{\phi}_{N}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\phi}_{i}1_{\mathcal{I}_{i}^{N}}(x).

It is straightforward to check that
\hat{\phi}_{N}
is exactly the Fiedler vector of
\rho_{N}\widehat{\mathbb{L}}_{N}
. Note that we use the same notation
\hat{\phi}_{N}
to refer to both the Fiedler vector associated with finite graph
G^{(N)}

and
the Fiedler
function
associated with the empirical graphon
\rho_{N}\widehat{\mathbb{L}}_{N}
. This will not cause any ambiguity in the following argument, as it is always clear if the argument for
\hat{\phi}_{N}
is a generic value
x\in[0,1]
or a generic value
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
. We repeat notation in this way because the two objects take exactly the same values in the same order, and this allows us to avoid some repetition of essentially identical calculations.

Let
\sigma=\sigma_{\hat{\phi}_{N}}
as in Equation (
1.5
). Note that
\sigma
is the output of Algorithm
1
with input
G^{(N)}
and that we have
\hat{\phi}_{\sigma(1)}\leq\hat{\phi}_{\sigma(2)}\leq\cdots\leq\hat{\phi}_{\sigma(N)}
. Since
\phi
is monotone and
\hat{\phi}_{N}
is close to
\phi
or
-\phi
(by Theorem
7
), we expect the permutation
\sigma
to be close to the identity permutation (or its reverse).

For any permutation
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{N}
, define the Kendall tau distance to be

D_{N}(\pi)=\sum_{i<j}\mathbf{1}_{\{\pi(i)>\pi(j)\}}.

In
[
13
]
, it was shown that, for any
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{N}
, we have
D_{N}(\pi)\leq\|\pi-id_{N}\|_{1}\leq 2D_{N}(\pi)
. Thus, when
N\to\infty
, these distances are of the same order.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
2
:

Fix any
0<\eta<\frac{1-\tau}{3}
. We assume without loss of generality that
\left\|\hat{\phi}_{N}-\phi\right\|\leq\left\|\hat{\phi}_{N}+\phi\right\|
(if not, simply replace
\phi
with
-\phi
in the following). Define

\displaystyle E_{N}

\displaystyle:=\left\{(i,j)\in[N]^{2}\,:\,i<j\text{ and }\sigma(j)<\sigma(i)\right\}

\displaystyle E_{N}^{\eta}

\displaystyle:=\left\{(i,j)\in[N]^{2}\,:\,i+N^{1-\eta}<j\text{ and }\sigma(j)<\sigma(i)\right\},

We then have

\displaystyle 2\left\|\hat{\phi}_{N}-\phi\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}(\hat{\phi}_{N}(x)-\phi(x))^{2}dx+\int_{0}^{1}(\hat{\phi}_{N}(y)-\phi(y))^{2}dy

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{\mathcal{I}_{i}^{N}}(\hat{\phi}_{i}-\phi(x))^{2}dx+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{\mathcal{I}_{j}^{N}}(\hat{\phi}_{j}-\phi(x))^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\left(\int_{\mathcal{I}_{i}^{N}}(\hat{\phi}_{i}-\phi(x))^{2}dx+\int_{\mathcal{I}_{i}^{N}}(\hat{\phi}_{j}-\phi(x+\frac{j-i}{N}))^{2}dx\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{(i,j)\in E_{N}^{\eta}}\int_{\mathcal{I}_{i}^{N}}\left((\hat{\phi}_{i}-\phi(x))^{2}+(\hat{\phi}_{j}-\phi(x+\frac{j-i}{N}))^{2}\right)dx.

Since
\phi(x+\frac{j-i}{N})>\phi(x)
and
\hat{\phi}_{j}<\hat{\phi}_{i}
for
(i,j)\in E_{N}
, we also have

(\hat{\phi}_{i}-\phi(x))^{2}+(\hat{\phi}_{j}-\phi(x+\frac{j-i}{N}))^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\phi(x+\frac{j-i}{N})-\phi(x)\right)^{2}.

Combining the previous two estimates,

\displaystyle 2\left\|\hat{\phi}_{N}-\phi\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{(i,j)\in E_{N}^{\eta}}\int_{\mathcal{I}_{i}^{N}}\left(\phi(x+\frac{j-i}{N})-\phi(x)\right)^{2}dx

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Theorem }\ref{thm:monotone}}}{{\geq}}\frac{L^{2}}{2N^{3}}\sum_{(i,j)\in E_{N}^{\eta}}\int_{\mathcal{I}_{i}^{N}}(j-i)^{2}dx

\displaystyle\geq\frac{L^{2}}{2N^{1+2\eta}}\sum_{(i,j)\in E_{N}^{\eta}}\int_{\mathcal{I}_{i}^{N}}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{L^{2}}{2N^{2+2\eta}}\left|E_{N}^{\eta}\right|,

where the inequality from line two to three follows from the fact that

for
(i,j)\in E_{N}^{\eta}
. Consequently,

\left|E_{N}^{\eta}\right|\leq\frac{4}{L^{2}}N^{2+2\eta}\left\|\hat{\phi}_{N}-\phi\right\|^{2}.

On the other hand,

E_{N}\setminus E_{N}^{\eta}=\left\{(i,j)\in[N]^{2}:0<j-i\leq N^{1-\eta}\text{ and }\sigma(j)<\sigma(i)\right\},

thus
\left|E_{N}\setminus E_{N}^{\eta}\right|\leq N^{2-\eta}
. By Theorem

there exists a positive constant

so that for all

we have with probability at least

Recall that

and

\displaystyle\|\sigma-id_{N}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\left|E_{N}\setminus E_{N}^{\eta}\right|+\left|E_{N}^{\eta}\right|\right)

Algorithm 2

Initial Post-processing the output of spectral seriation.

We give an informal summary of what this algorithm is doing and why we expect it to work well, eliding many details.

Steps 1-2 get a good estimate of the right-most and left-most vertices in

(\alpha,\beta)
, we can find the pair
(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})
by using Inequality (
6.30
) as a test. Since the bound in Lemma
6.1
is a bound on the
maximum
error, checking
many
pairs
(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})
from a single observed graph does not present any obstacles.

7
Application

We show that Theorem
1
follows from Theorems
2
and
3
. To do so, it is enough to check that any graphon that is “nice" in the sense of Definition
1.2
will also satisfy Assumptions
1
through
5
.

We now check Assumptions
1
through
5
in order.

Assumption
1
:
Define
R_{\max}=\sup_{r\in(0,1]}R^{\prime}(r)<\infty
. We calculate

\displaystyle\left|w(x,y)-w(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|R(|x-y|)-R(|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|)\right|

\displaystyle\leq R_{\max}\left|\,|x-y|-|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|\,\right|

\displaystyle\leq R_{\max}\left|(x-y)-(x^{\prime}-y^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle\leq R_{\max}(|x-y|+|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|).

Assumption
2
:
We have
\frac{\partial w(x,y)}{\partial x}=R^{\prime}(y)
for
y\neq x
, so this follows immediately from Definition
1.2
.

Assumption
3
:
We calculate

\displaystyle d^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dx}\int_{0}^{1}R(|x-y|)dy

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dx}\left(\int_{0}^{x}R(y)dy+\int_{0}^{1-x}R(y)dy\right)

\displaystyle=R(x)-R(1-x).

Thus
d^{\prime}(x)
exists for all
x\in[0,1]
. Since
R
is monotone,
d^{\prime}(x)>0
for
x\in[0,0.5)
and
d^{\prime}(x)<0
for
x\in(0.5,0]
, with
d^{\prime}(x)=0
only for
x=0.5
.

Assumption
4
:
This is given in
[
32
]
, with the function
h
in that reference replacing the function
R
in this paper.

Assumption
5
:
To prove Inequality (
2.7
), we have

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in[0,0.05]}\Psi_{L}(x)

\displaystyle=\inf_{x\in[0,0.05]}\int_{0}^{0.05}w(x,y)dy

\displaystyle=\inf_{x\in[0,0.05]}\int_{0}^{0.05}R(|x-y|)dy.

Since
R
is monotone, this infimum is achieved at
x=0
and
x=0.05
. Continuing the calculation,

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in[0,0.05]}\Psi_{L}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{0.05}R(y)dy

\displaystyle>\int_{0.26}^{0.31}R(y)dy

\displaystyle=\Psi_{L}(0.31),

where the strict inequality in the second line comes from the assumption that
R^{\prime}
is continuous and strictly positive. This proves one of the four inequalities in the first part Assumption
5
; the remainder are essentially the same.

To prove Inequality (
2.8
), recall that
\frac{\partial w(x,y)}{\partial x}=R^{\prime}(y)>0
. Thus, this follows immediately from Gronwall’s inequality.

8
Discussion

In this paper, we have written down a simple seriation algorithm and shown that, for some fairly large class of model graphons, the result of the algorithm is an estimated permutation
\hat{\sigma}
with sup-norm error of roughly
O(\sqrt{N})
. It is natural to ask whether either the rates of convergence or our assumptions about the generating graphon are optimal.

The second question seems to have an easy answer: we are quite sure that our conditions are not optimal, even for the type of analysis we use. In particular, our main assumptions on
w
imply that all vertices have a fairly non-negligible chance of being connected (more precisely, our main assumptions imply that
w(x,y)>0
for all
x,y\in[0,1]
). We have already seen in Remark
2.1
that this property is not satisfied for many natural graphons, and also that it can be substantially weakened.

We hypothesize that this global connection property can be replaced by a much weaker assumption, saying roughly that some
power
of the adjacency matrix should be globally connected. The main difficulties in carrying this out are different for our two main results:

•

The main difficulty in extending Theorem
2
is carrying through the arguments in Section
3
, which show that the Fiedler eigenspace has dimension 1 and that the Fiedler function is monotone.

As discussed in Remark
2.1
, this can sometimes be checked directly for particularly simple graphons. We also expect that certain generic techniques, such as perturbation analysis, can be used to extend this to small nonparametric families of graphons. However, we do not know how to get rid of this assumption in any great generality.

•

The main difficulty in extending Theorem
3
is not merely technical: Algorithm
3
will not generally give a sensible answer without the global connectedness property. This occurs because the functions
\psi_{R}(i),\psi_{L}(i)
may both be 0 for many
i\in[N]
. In
[
25
]
, we introduce a “local" quantity
R(i,j)
that is analogous to the “global" quantity
R
used in the present paper. We suspect that a similar approach would work in the present context.

We don’t know the optimal rate of convergence for this problem in very many metrics, but we give a few related results largely drawn from our companion paper
[
25
]
. Note that this paper studies a slightly different sampling scheme, but very similar arguments apply:

1.

Under slightly different model conditions, the iterated post-processing algorithm of
[
25
]
can obtain error of
O(N^{\epsilon})
for any fixed
\epsilon>0
, so the rate
N^{0.5}
is certainly not optimal for all classes of graphon models.

2.

On the other hand, we might ask to estimate the latent positions
v_{1},\ldots,v_{N}
of vertices in addition to the latent permutation
\sigma
. The argument in Section 5 of
[
25
]
says that any such estimate must suffer an error of at least
\Omega(N^{-0.5})
for any sufficiently large class of graphon models. Since the natural estimate

\hat{v}_{i}=\frac{\hat{\sigma}(i)}{N}

is so similar to
\hat{\sigma}
itself, this suggests that one can’t do much better in general.

Several other papers have recently studied the optimal rate of convergence for “the" noisy seriation problem, including
[
29
]
and
[
18
]
. Their models and versions of the noisy seriation problem are different enough from ours that it is not possible to directly compare the results. We leave the question of comparing these notions of noisy seriation to future work.

Appendix

Lemma A.1
.

Let
V=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{N}\}
with
v_{i}=i/N
for all
i
. Let
S
be a subset of
V
chosen uniformly at random and with deterministic size
m=|S|
such that
\left\lfloor\tfrac{N}{3}\right\rfloor\leq m\leq\left\lfloor\tfrac{N}{3}\right\rfloor+1
. Define
s_{(1)}<s_{(2)}<\ldots<s_{(m)}
to be the order statics of the elements of
S
. Then for each
1\leq k\leq m
and for any
c>0
, there exists a constant
\delta>0
and an integer
N_{0}
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|s_{(k)}-\frac{k}{m}\right|>c\Delta_{N}\right)\leq\varepsilon_{\gamma}^{\delta}(N)

for all
N\geq N_{0}
. In other words for each
1\leq k\leq m
and
c>0
the event
\left|s_{(k)}-\frac{k}{m}\right|\leq c\Delta_{N}
holds w.e.p.

Let
1\leq k\leq N
and
k\leq j\leq N-m+k
. Then
s_{(k)}=v_{j}
if and only if there is exactly
k-1
elements from
S
less than
v_{j}
and exactly
m-k
elements from
S
bigger than
v_{j}
. Therefore

\mathbb{P}\left(s_{(k)}=v_{j}\right)=\frac{\binom{j-1}{k-1}\binom{N-j}{m-k}}{\binom{N}{m}}.

Note that the distribution of
s_{(k)}
looks very similar to a negative hypergeometric distribution. In fact one can easily check that
Ns_{(k)}-k\sim\mathcal{NHG}(N;N-m;k)
.

The paper
[
38
]
provides concentration inequalities for sampling without replacement. According to
[
38
,
22
]
, for any
\lambda>0
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(|s_{(k)}-\mathbb{E}[s_{(k)}]|>\lambda\right)\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-2N^{2}\lambda^{2}}{m(1-f_{m}^{*})}\right)

with
f_{m}^{*}=(m-1)/N
. We can check that
1/4<f_{m}^{*}\leq 1/3
for all
N
sufficiently large. Thus,

\mathbb{P}\left(|s_{(k)}-\mathbb{E}[s_{(k)}]|>\lambda\right)\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-8N^{2}\lambda^{2}}{3m}\right).

Let
c>0
, we have

\displaystyle|s_{(k)}-\frac{k}{m}|>c\Delta_{N}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow|s_{(k)}-\mathbb{E}[s_{(k)}]|>c\Delta_{N}-|\frac{k}{m}-\mathbb{E}[s_{(k)}]|

\displaystyle\Rightarrow|s_{(k)}-\mathbb{E}[s_{(k)}]|>c\Delta_{N}-\frac{(N-m)k}{Nm(m+1)}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow|s_{(k)}-\mathbb{E}[s_{(k)}]|>c\Delta_{N}-\frac{2}{N}.

For
N
large enough so that
4<cN\Delta_{N}
,

|s_{(k)}-\frac{k}{m}|>c\Delta_{N}\Rightarrow|s_{(k)}-\mathbb{E}[s_{(k)}]|>\frac{c}{2}\Delta_{N}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|s_{(k)}-\frac{k}{m}|>c\Delta_{N}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(|s_{(k)}-\mathbb{E}[s_{(k)}]|>\frac{c}{2}\Delta_{N}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-2c^{2}N^{2}\Delta_{N}^{2}}{3m}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-c^{2}N\Delta_{N}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{\gamma}^{\delta}(N)

for some
0<\delta<c^{2}
.
∎
