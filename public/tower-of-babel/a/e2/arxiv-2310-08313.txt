Real phase structures on tropical varieties and patchworks in higher codimension
Johannes Rau, Arthur Renaudineau, Kris Shaw

Abstract
. This paper generalises the homeomorphism theorem behind Viro’s combinatorial patchworking of hypersurfaces in toric varieties to arbitrary codimension using tropical geometry. We first define the patchwork of a polyhedral space equipped with a real phase structure. When the polyhedral subspace is tropically non-singular, we show that the patchwork is a topological manifold. When a non-singular tropical variety appears as a tropical limit of a real analytic family, we show that the real part of a fibre of the family near the tropical limit is homeomorphic to the patchwork. Finally we extend the spectral sequence introduced by the last two authors in the case of hypersurfaces to non-singular tropical varieties with real phase structures. As a corollary, we obtain bounds on the Betti numbers of the patchwork in terms of the dimensions of the tropical homology groups with coefficients modulo two.



1.
Introduction

Viro’s patchworking method is a powerful method to study the topological types
of real algebraic hypersurfaces in toric varieties
[
Vir84
]
.
A particular case of the method is
combinatorial
patchworking which,
from some combinatorial data, produces two objects: a polyhedral space and a patchworking polynomial.
Viro’s theorem provides a homeomorphism between the polyhedral space and the real part of a hypersurface defined by the polynomial.
It was later generalized to complete intersections, see
[
Stu94
]
and
[
Bih02
]
.
This paper aims to generalise the homeomorphism theorem of combinatorial patchworking beyond the case of hypersurfaces and complete intersections.

To generalise the theorem to arbitrary codimension,
we begin by considering rational polyhedral spaces and tropical varieties equipped with real phase structures, which were defined in the case of polyhedral fans in
[
RRS22
]
.
For a rational polyhedral subspace
X
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, a real phase structure is an assignment of an affine subspace of the vector space
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{n}
, where
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}:={\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}

to each facet of
X
.

The assignment of a real phase structure is then used to describe the
patchwork
of a tropical variety, which should be thought of as its real part, see Definition
2.12
.
Our first result is a description of the structure of the patchwork
when we start from a non-singular tropical subvariety.

Theorem 1.1
.

The patchwork of a non-singular tropical subvariety equipped with a real phase structure in a tropical toric variety is a topological manifold.

We then show that a patchwork describes, up to homeomorphism, fibers of real analytic families with non-singular tropical limits. Let us describe our setup:
Let
{\mathbf{X}}\subset({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}\times{\mathcal{D}}^{*}
be a real analytic family of
algebraic varieties over the punctured disc
{\mathcal{D}}^{*}
.
That is, locally
{\mathbf{X}}
can be described by polynomials whose coefficients are Laurent series convergent on
{\mathcal{D}}^{*}
.
Moreover, the family is compatible with complex conjugations on
({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
and
{\mathcal{D}}^{*}
.
To such a family, we can associate a tropical limit
\Trop({\mathbf{X}})=X\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

{\mathbf{X}}
under the valuation map, see Section
4.1
.
Let us assume that
\Trop({\mathbf{X}})
is tropically non-singular, see Section
4.1

{\mathbf{X}}
induces a real phase structure

\Trop({\mathbf{X}})

4.4
.
We relate this to pre-existing notions of tropicalising real varieties in Remark

For any pointed unimodular fan

Next, we adapt
the arguments from
[
RS23
]
to bound the Betti numbers of the patchwork in terms of the dimensions of the tropical homology groups.
For a tropical manifold
X
, we denote the tropical homology groups with
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
coefficients by

H_{q}(X;{\mathcal{F}}_{p})
. We let
H_{q}^{BM}(X;{\mathcal{F}}_{p})
denote the Borel-Moore variants of these homology groups.
See Section
5.2
for the definition of tropical homology.

We define the (Borel-Moore) tropical signature of
X
as

\sigma^{\diamond}(X):=\sum_{p,q}(-1)^{q}H_{q}^{\diamond}(X;{\mathcal{F}}_{p}),

where
\diamond
is either empty, denoting usual homology, or
\diamond
is BM, denoting Borel-Moore homology.
Notice that
\sigma^{\diamond}(X)
is also equal to
\sum_{p,q}(-1)^{q}H_{q}^{\diamond}(X;{\mathcal{F}}_{p}^{{\mathbb{R}}})

where
H_{q}^{\diamond}(X;{\mathcal{F}}_{p}^{{\mathbb{R}}})
denotes tropical homology with real coefficients.

Theorem 1.3
.

If
X
is a
d
-dimensional non-singular tropical subvariety equipped with a real phase structure
{\mathcal{E}}
, then

b_{q}(\mathfrak{R}(X,{\mathcal{E}}))\leq\sum_{p=0}^{d}\dim H_{q}(X;{\mathcal{F}}_{p})

and

b^{BM}_{q}(\mathfrak{R}(X,{\mathcal{E}}))\leq\sum_{p=0}^{d}\dim H^{BM}_{q}(X;{\mathcal{F}}_{p}).

Moreover, the (Borel-Moore) Euler characteristic of
\mathfrak{R}(X,{\mathcal{E}})
is equal to the
(Borel-Moore) tropical signature of
X
, namely,

\displaystyle\chi(\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}}))=\sigma(X),

\displaystyle\chi^{\text{BM}}(\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}}))=\sigma^{\text{BM}}(X).

We then combine Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
to obtain the following two corollaries.

Corollary 1.4
.

Under the same hypothesis as in Theorem
1.2
, for sufficiently small
t\in{\mathcal{D}}^{*}\cap{\mathbb{R}}
we have

b_{q}({\mathbb{R}}\overline{{\mathbf{X}}}_{t})\leq\sum_{p=0}^{d}\dim H_{q}(\overline{X};{\mathcal{F}}_{p})\quad\text{ and }\quad b^{BM}_{q}({\mathbb{R}}\overline{{\mathbf{X}}}_{t})\leq\sum_{p=0}^{d}\dim H^{BM}_{q}(\overline{X};{\mathcal{F}}_{p}).

We define the
signature
of a generic fibre
\overline{{\mathbf{X}}}_{t}
as

\sigma_{c}(\overline{{\mathbf{X}}}_{t}):=\sum_{p,q}(-1)^{p}e_{c}^{p,q}(\overline{{\mathbf{X}}}_{t})

where
e_{c}^{p,q}(\overline{{\mathbf{X}}}_{t})
is defined via the
mixed Hodge structure on
\overline{{\mathbf{X}}}_{t}
by

e_{c}^{p,q}(\overline{{\mathbf{X}}}_{t}):=\sum_{k}(-1)^{k}h^{p,q}(H_{c}^{k}(\overline{{\mathbf{X}}}_{t})).

See for instance
[
DK86
]
.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
{\mathbf{X}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}\Sigma\times{\mathcal{D}}^{*}
be a real meromorphic family
with non-singular tropical limit satisfying the same hypothesis as in Theorem
1.2
.
Then for sufficiently small
t\in{\mathcal{D}}^{*}\cap{\mathbb{R}}
we have

\chi^{\text{BM}}({\mathbb{R}}\overline{{\mathbf{X}}}_{t})=\sigma_{c}(\overline{{\mathbf{X}}}_{t}).

This equality was already proved in the case of codimension one by Bertrand
[
Ber10
]
(see also
[
Arn17
]
), and by Brugallé in the same situation as above by utilising a different point of view
[
Bru21
]
.

In a recent paper
[
AM22
]
, the authors generalized the ideas of
[
RS23
]
beyond the case of tropical varieties. They proved that the same bounds still apply for any totally real semi-stable degeneration satisfying strong homological properties. Essentially what they require is that the open strata of the special fiber are all maximal in the sense of the Smith-Thom inequality, the real parts only have non-trivial homology in degree
0
, and that the cohomology of the complex open strata satisfy a strong condition on their mixed Hodge structures. These properties are satisfied in our context and so, up to a comparison of tropical homology and the complexes obtained by Ambrosi and Manzaroli, this eventually gives another proof of
Corollary

1.4
.

We would also like to mention the work of Brugallé, López de Medrano and the first author
[
BLdMR22
]
.
This work transfers tropical homology, real phase structures, and the Betti number bounds of
1.3

to the original framework of Viro’s patchworking method, namely the framework of (unimodular) triangulations
of lattice polytopes. An overlap with the present work is given if the triangulation is assumed to be convex. Then the objects of study
in
[
BLdMR22
]
correspond to self-intersections of a
tropical hypersurface associated to the triangulation (and the correspondence can supposedly extended to complete intersections
using mixed subdivisions).
So while
[
BLdMR22
]
also covers non-convex triangulations which do not
have a tropical counterpart, the present approach also treats non-complete intersections
which seem to have no counterpart on the triangulation side.

2.
Real phase structures

2.1.
Tropical toric varieties

Given a polyhedron
\sigma\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
we denote by
T(\sigma)
its
tangent space, that is, the vector subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
generated by
all vectors
u-v
,
u,v\in\sigma
. We are often interested
in the quotient space
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}/T(\sigma)
for which we hence use the shorthand

{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)
.
If
\sigma
is a rational polyhedron, i-e the supporting hyperplanes are defined over
{\mathbb{Q}}
,
we denote by
T_{\mathbb{Z}}(\sigma)=T(\sigma)\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
its
tangent lattice
,
and set
{\mathbb{Z}}(\sigma)={\mathbb{Z}}^{n}/T_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(\sigma)
.
Finally, for a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-module
G
, we set
T_{G}(\sigma)=T_{\mathbb{Z}}(\sigma)\otimes G

and
G(\sigma)=G^{n}/T_{G}(\sigma)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a rational polyhedral pointed fan in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Given
\rho\in\Sigma
, we denote the dual cone by

\rho^{\vee}=\{\lambda\in({\mathbb{R}}^{n})^{\vee}:\lambda|_{\rho}\geq 0\}\subset({\mathbb{R}}^{n})^{\vee}.

We denote by
{\mathbb{T}}U_{\rho}=\Hom(\rho^{\vee}\cap({\mathbb{Z}}^{n})^{\vee},{\mathbb{T}})
the space
of semigroup homomorphisms, where
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{R}}\cup\left\{-\infty\right\}
endowed with the addition is the
tropical
semigroup. We equip it with the coarsest topology
such that for all
\lambda\in\rho^{\vee}\cap({\mathbb{Z}}^{n})^{\vee}

the evaluation map
{\mathbb{T}}U_{\rho}\to{\mathbb{T}}
,
\alpha\mapsto\alpha(\lambda)
,
is continuous. If
\rho\subset\eta
, we
have by restriction a continuous map
{\mathbb{T}}U_{\rho}\to{\mathbb{T}}U_{\eta}
.

Definition 2.1
.

[
MR
, Chapter 3]

[
Pay09
, Section 3]

The tropical toric variety associated to the fan
\Sigma

is the direct limit

{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma:=\varinjlim_{\rho\in\Sigma}{\mathbb{T}}U_{\rho}.

We call
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma

non-singular

if
\Sigma
is unimodular (and hence simplicial).

The space
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
is compact if and only if the corresponding fan is complete.
The action of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
on itself extends to a group action on
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma

whose orbits are given by
{\mathbb{T}}{\mathcal{O}}_{\rho}={\mathbb{R}}(\rho)\cong\Hom(\rho^{\perp},{\mathbb{T}})\subset{\mathbb{T}}U_{\rho}
(the inclusion is given by extension by
-\infty
).
Notice that
\dim{\mathbb{T}}{\mathcal{O}}_{\rho}=n-\dim\rho
.
Given a point
y\in{\mathbb{T}}{\mathcal{O}}_{\rho}
, we call
\rho
the
sedentarity
of
y
.
The
order of sedentarity
of
y
, denoted by
\sed(y)
, is set to be
\dim(\rho)
.

Example 2.2
.

Tropical affine space
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
is the tropical toric variety associated
to the fan that consists of the negative orthant in

and all its faces.
Given a subset

, where the

.
Then the torus orbit

, while its closure in

, denoted by

,
can be identified with

.

Similarly, we set

and for any

\sigma_{A}
be the cone generated by

The collection of these cones forms a complete fan defining

We denote the closure of

It can be identified with a projective space

Definition 2.3
.

Given two fans
\Sigma

{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
\Sigma^{\prime}
in

,

morphism of fans

A\colon\Sigma\to\Sigma^{\prime}

-linear map

such that

.

Let

denote the tropical toric varieties associated
to
\Sigma

\Sigma^{\prime}
.
A
(toric) morphism

is
a continuous map that sends
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\subset{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
to

{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\subset{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma^{\prime}
and such that the restriction

is of the form

where

.

We note that given

, there is a unique continuous extension

Example 2.4
.

Let
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
be a tropical toric variety. The open subsets
{\mathbb{T}}U_{\rho}

are toric varieties themselves (their defining fan consists of
\rho
and all its faces).
If
\rho
is a unimodular cone generated by the first

vectors
of the
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
-basis

, then

\rho^{\vee}\cap({\mathbb{Z}}^{n})^{\vee}=\langle v_{1}^{*},\dots,v_{k}^{*},\pm v_{k+1}^{*},\dots,\pm v_{n}^{*}\rangle\cong{\mathbb{N}}^{k}\times{\mathbb{Z}}^{n-k}

Hence if

).

Example 2.5
.

The only automorphisms of
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
are coordinate permutations
composed with translations, so
\text{Aut}({\mathbb{T}}^{n})=\text{Sym}_{n}\ltimes{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
This follows from the fact that (fan) automorphisms of the

Remark 2.6
.

We focused here on tropical toric varieties, but

2.2.
Polyhedral complexes

A rational polyhedron in a tropical toric variety
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
is the
closure in
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
of a rational polyhedron in some stratum
{\mathbb{T}}{\mathcal{O}}_{\rho}
.
For a polyhedron
\sigma
in
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
, the intersection
\sigma\cap{\mathbb{T}}{\mathcal{O}}_{\eta}
,
if non-empty, is a polyhedron in
{\mathbb{T}}{\mathcal{O}}_{\eta}
. A
face

\tau
of
\sigma

is the closure of a face of any of these intersections (hence
\tau
is a polyhedron in
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
).
The
relative interior

\relint(\sigma)
is the set of points in
\sigma
not contained in
a proper face. We set
\sed(\sigma):=\sed(y)
for an arbitrary
y\in\relint(\sigma)
.
Therefore, we have
\sed(\sigma)=\dim(\rho)
referring to
\rho
from above.

A
rational polyhedral complex

\mathcal{X}
in
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
is a collection of rational polyhedra in
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
such that

•

if
\sigma\in{\mathcal{X}}
and
\tau\subset\sigma
is a face, then
\tau\in{\mathcal{X}}
,

•

if
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in{\mathcal{X}}
and
\sigma_{1}\cap\sigma_{2}\neq\emptyset
, then
\sigma_{1}\cap\sigma_{2}
is a face of
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
.

We refer to the elements
\sigma\in{\mathcal{X}}
as
faces
of
{\mathcal{X}}
.
The maximal faces of
{\mathcal{X}}
are called
facets
.
The
support

|{\mathcal{X}}|
of
{\mathcal{X}}
is the union of all the faces of
{\mathcal{X}}
.
Given a polyhedral complex
{\mathcal{X}}
, a
subdivision
of
{\mathcal{X}}
is
a polyhedral complex
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
with the same support
and such that any face of
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
is contained in some face of
{\mathcal{X}}
.
A
rational polyhedral subspace

X
of
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
is a
subspace which is the support of a rational polyhedral complex
\mathcal{X}
.
Note that two polyhedral complexes have the same
support if and only if they have a common subdivision.
Hence, alternatively we can think of a polyhedral
subspace as an equivalence class of polyhedral complexes
under the equivalence relation induced by subdivision.
We allow ourselves to call

\mathcal{X}
a subdivision of the polyhedral space
|{\mathcal{X}}|
.

A rational polyhedral subspace
X\subset{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
is called
of
pure sedentarity
\rho\in\Sigma

if
X=\overline{X\cap{\mathbb{T}}{\mathcal{O}}_{\rho}}
.
A polyhedral complex is of
pure sedentarity
\rho\in\Sigma

if
|{\mathcal{X}}|
is of pure sedentarity
\rho
.
A polyhedral complex
{\mathcal{X}}
is
of pure dimension

if all facets have dimension
d
.
Clearly, the notion is preserved under subdivision and hence
extends to polyhedral spaces
X
.
In the following, polyhedral complexes
{\mathcal{X}}
and polyhedral spaces

are
always
assumed to be rational, of pure dimension,
and of pure sedentarity.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}

We note that

Given a polyhedron
\sigma\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

recession cone

is defined by

\RecCone(\sigma)=\{v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:\lambda v+p\in\sigma\;\forall\;p\in\sigma,\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\}.

If
\sigma\subset{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
is a polyhedron of sedentarity
\rho
, we set

\RecCone(\sigma)=\RecCone(\sigma\cap{\mathbb{T}}{\mathcal{O}}_{\rho})
.
Given a polyhedral complex
{\mathcal{X}}
, we set
\RecCone({\mathcal{X}})=\{\RecCone(\sigma):\sigma\in{\mathcal{X}}\}
.

Let us assume that
{\mathcal{X}}
is a polyhedral complex in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that

\Sigma=\RecCone({\mathcal{X}})
is a fan in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Let us additionally assume that

{\mathcal{X}}
contains a vertex, or equivalently, that
\Sigma
is pointed.
We will now define a polyhedral complex
\overline{{\mathcal{X}}}

in
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
with the property that
|\overline{{\mathcal{X}}}|=\overline{|{\mathcal{X}}|}

and
\overline{{\mathcal{X}}}\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}={\mathcal{X}}
.
The construction is sometimes called the canonical compactification of

{\mathcal{X}}
, cf.
[
KSW20
]
,
[
AP20
, Section 2.4]
.
We set

(2.1)

\overline{{\mathcal{X}}}:=\{\overline{\sigma}\cap{\mathbb{T}}\Star_{\rho}\Sigma:\sigma\in{\mathcal{X}},\rho\in\Sigma\}.

Note that, for example by
[
MR
, Proposition 3.2.4]
,
the intersection
\overline{\sigma}\cap{\mathbb{T}}\Star_{\rho}\Sigma
is non-emtpy
if and only if
\rho\subset\RecCone(\sigma)
,
and moreover, in this case

\overline{\sigma}\cap{\mathbb{T}}\Star_{\rho}\Sigma
is the closure in
{\mathbb{T}}\Star_{\rho}\Sigma

of

\sigma/T(\rho)\subset{\mathbb{R}}(\rho)={\mathbb{T}}{\mathcal{O}}_{\rho}.

For further reference, we note that the labelling of faces of
\overline{{\mathcal{X}}}

by pairs
(\sigma,\rho)
provides
an isomorphism between the poset
\overline{{\mathcal{X}}}

and the subposet of
{\mathcal{X}}\times\Sigma

given by

(2.2)

\{(\sigma,\rho)\in{\mathcal{X}}\times\Sigma:\rho\subset\RecCone(\sigma)\},

equipped with the product order, that is,

(\sigma^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\leq(\sigma,\rho)\Longleftrightarrow\sigma^{\prime}\leq\sigma\text{ in }{\mathcal{X}},\rho\leq\rho^{\prime}\text{ in }\Sigma.

We note at this point that throughout the following, given a polyhedral complex

{\mathcal{X}}
we regard it as a poset whose partial order is given by inclusion.
We will sometimes refer to this as the
face poset
of
{\mathcal{X}}
to emphasize that
we forget all geometric information and only consider the poset structure.

2.3.
Real phase structures on polyhedral subspaces

Given a polyhedron
\sigma
in
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
of sedentarity
\rho
,
we define its
tangent space
by

T(\sigma):=T(\sigma\cap{\mathbb{T}}{\mathcal{O}}_{\rho})\subset{\mathbb{T}}{\mathcal{O}}_{\rho}={\mathbb{R}}(\rho).

Its integer and binary versions are

\displaystyle T_{\mathbb{Z}}(\sigma)

\displaystyle:=T(\sigma)\cap{\mathbb{Z}}(\rho)\subset{\mathbb{Z}}(\rho),

\displaystyle T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}(\sigma)

\displaystyle:=T_{\mathbb{Z}}(\sigma)\otimes{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}(\rho).

For a vector space
V
over
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}:={\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
,
we denote by
\text{Aff}_{d}(V)
the set of all affine subspaces in
V
of dimension
d
.

Definition 2.7
.

A collection of subsets
S_{1},\dots,S_{k}\subset V
is called an
even covering
if every element in
V
is contained in an even number of the sets.
Equivalently,

S_{1}\triangle\dots\triangle S_{k}=\emptyset,

where
S\triangle T:=(S\cup T)\setminus(S\cap T)
is the symmetric difference.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a polyhedral complex of pure dimension
d
and sedentarity
\rho
contained in a tropical toric variety
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
.
A
real phase structure
on
\mathcal{X}
is a map

\mathcal{E}\colon\Facets({\mathcal{X}})\to\text{Aff}_{d}({\mathbb{Z}}_{2}(\rho))

satisfying

(1)

for every facet
\sigma
of
\mathcal{X}
, the set
\mathcal{E}(\sigma)
is an affine subspace of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}(\rho)
parallel to
\sigma
, in formulas,
T({\mathcal{E}}(\sigma))=T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}(\sigma)
;

(2)

for every codimension one face
\tau
of
\mathcal{X}
with facets
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k}
adjacent to it, the sets
{\mathcal{E}}(\sigma_{1}),\dots,{\mathcal{E}}(\sigma_{k})
are an even covering,

A real phase structure on a polyhedral subspace
X\subset{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
is
a real phase structure on some subdivision
\mathcal{X}
of
X
.

Remark 2.9
.

If
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
is a subdivision of
\mathcal{X}
,
there is a bijection between the set of real phase structures on
\mathcal{X}
and those on
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
.
This bijection is given by
{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}(\sigma^{\prime})={\mathcal{E}}(\sigma)
if
\sigma
is the face of
{\mathcal{X}}
containing
\sigma^{\prime}
in its relative interior.
It is obvious that this assignment is injective, and
condition 2) in Definition
2.8
for a real phase structure on
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
ensures that it is surjective. In fact, if a codimension one face
\tau
is adjacent to exactly two facets
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
, then it follows from condition 2) that
{\mathcal{E}}(\sigma_{1})={\mathcal{E}}(\sigma_{2})
.
Therefore, a real phase structure on a polyhedral subspace
X
is a real phase structure on
any polyhedral complex
{\mathcal{X}}
such that
|{\mathcal{X}}|=X
.

2.4.
Tropical patchworking

Following
[
GKZ08
, Chapter 11]
, we now describe how to obtain a space homeomorphic to
the real part of a complex toric variety by glueing together multiple symmetric copies of the tropical toric variety.
Let
\Sigma
be a pointed polyhedral fan in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
defining a tropical toric variety
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
.
For every
\varepsilon\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{n}
, we denote by
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma(\varepsilon)

a copy of
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
indexed by
\varepsilon
. We then define

(2.3)

\mathfrak{R}{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma:=\bigsqcup_{\varepsilon\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{n}}{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma(\varepsilon)/\sim,

where
\sim
identifies strata
{\mathbb{T}}\Star_{\rho}\Sigma(\varepsilon)
and

{\mathbb{T}}\Star_{\rho}\Sigma(\varepsilon^{\prime})
if and only if
\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime}
is in

T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}(\rho)
.

We can also give a more precise description of
\mathfrak{R}{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
as a direct limit of topological spaces.
Consider the poset

Q(\Sigma):=\{(\rho,\varepsilon):\rho\in\Sigma,\varepsilon\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}(\rho)\}

with partial order given by

(\rho,\varepsilon)\leq(\eta,\delta):\Longleftrightarrow\eta\subset\rho,\varepsilon=\pi(\delta),

where
\pi\colon{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}(\eta)\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}(\rho)
denotes
the canonical projection.
We construct a direct system of topological spaces over
Q(\Sigma)
by
assigning to
(\rho,\varepsilon)
a copy of the toric variety
{\mathbb{T}}\Star_{\rho}\Sigma
,
denoted by
{\mathbb{T}}\Star_{\rho}\Sigma(\varepsilon)
.
For
(\rho,\varepsilon)\leq(\eta,\delta)
, the corresponding map is given by the inclusion

{\mathbb{T}}\Star_{\rho}\Sigma\subset{\mathbb{T}}\Star_{\eta}\Sigma
.
Then
\mathfrak{R}{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
is equal to the limit of this direct system.
In particular, we note
that
\mathfrak{R}{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
contains
2^{n-\dim\rho}
copies of
{\mathbb{T}}\Star_{\rho}\Sigma

(and hence of
{\mathbb{T}}{\mathcal{O}}_{\rho}
) labelled by
\varepsilon\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}(\rho)
.

Remark 2.10
.

The construction can also be understood using the formalism of
toric varieties over arbitrary semigroups, cf.
Remark

2.6
.
Consider the semigroup
S={\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
.
The toric variety
S\Sigma
is the disjoint union

\bigsqcup_{\varepsilon\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{n}}{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma(\varepsilon)

appearing in
Equation

2.3
.
The quotient of
S
under
\{-\infty\}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
is called
the semigroup (more often, semifield or hyperfield) of
real

tropical numbers which we denote by
\mathfrak{R}{\mathbb{T}}
, see
[
Vir11
]
. As a set,

\mathfrak{R}{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{T}}\cup_{-\infty}{\mathbb{T}}
is a union of two copies of
{\mathbb{T}}
along
-\infty
.
Then
\mathfrak{R}{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
is
in fact the toric variety associated to the semigroup
\mathfrak{R}{\mathbb{T}}
,
and the glueing described in
Equation

2.3
is coming from
the map
S\Sigma\to\mathfrak{R}{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
induced by the quotient homomorphism

S\to\mathfrak{R}{\mathbb{T}}
.

To explain the connection to the real toric variety
{\mathbb{R}}\Sigma
, first note that

\log\colon({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq},\cdot)\to({\mathbb{T}},+)

(with
\log(0)=-\infty
) is an isomorphism of semigroups.
Moreover,
{\mathbb{R}}
can be described as the semigroup quotient of
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}

modulo
\{0\}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
. Hence we get an extended semigroup homomorphism

\log^{\mathfrak{R}}\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathfrak{R}{\mathbb{T}}
.
These semigroup homomorphisms yield homeomorphisms

\Log\colon{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\Sigma\to{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
and
\Log^{\mathfrak{R}}\colon{\mathbb{R}}\Sigma\to\mathfrak{R}{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
.
We also note that this construction is a tropical reformulation of
for example
[
GKZ08
, Theorem 11.5.4]
.

Example 2.11
.

We consider the tropical affine space
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma={\mathbb{T}}^{n}
.
In continuation of the notation introduced
Example

2.2
,
given

\mathfrak{R}{\mathbb{T}}^{n}

The stratification agrees with the ordinary sign stratification

For

.

Given a polyhedron
\sigma
in
{\mathbb{T}}\Star_{\rho}\Sigma\subset{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
and

\varepsilon\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}(\rho)

\sigma(\varepsilon)

denote the copy of
\sigma
in
{\mathbb{T}}\Star_{\rho}\Sigma(\varepsilon)\subset\mathfrak{R}{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma

be declaring the sets

{\mathcal{X}}

Definition 2.12
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}

Remark 2.13
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
be a subdivision of

.
Then clearly

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a polyhedral complex in
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
together with a real phase structure
\mathcal{E}
.
The polyhedral complex
\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}})\subset\mathfrak{R}{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
represents a
closed cellular chain in the homology of
\mathfrak{R}{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
over
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
.

Note that the associated homology class
[\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}})]\in H^{BM}_{d}(\mathfrak{R}{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma,{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
is invariant under subdivisions of
{\mathcal{X}}
by Remark
2.13
.
In particular, we can associate a well-defined homology class

[\mathfrak{R}(X,{\mathcal{E}})]\in H^{BM}_{d}(\mathfrak{R}{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma,{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
to any polyhedral subspace

X
with real phase structure
{\mathcal{E}}
.

Proof.

To see that
\partial\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}})=0
we need to verify that every codimension one face of
\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}})
is contained in an even number of
facets of
\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}})
.
If
{\mathcal{X}}
is of sedentarity
\rho
, we can replace
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
by

{\mathbb{T}}\Star_{\rho}\Sigma
,
hence it suffices to prove the statement for sedentarity
0
.

Let
\tau(\varepsilon)
be a face of
\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}})
of codimension one.
We first consider the case
\sed(\tau)=0
.
Then the facets of
\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}})
containing
\tau(\varepsilon)
are in
bijection with the
facets
\sigma
of
{\mathcal{X}}
containing
\tau
and such that
\varepsilon\in{\mathcal{E}}(\sigma)
.
By Condition 2) of Definition
2.8
, the number of such facets is even, which proves the claim in this case.

Now suppose
\tau
is of sedentarity
\rho
.
Let
\sigma
be a facet of
{\mathcal{X}}
containing
\tau
.
This implies that
\RecCone(\sigma)
intersects the
relative interior of
\rho
and moreover the intersection

\rho^{\prime}:=\RecCone(\sigma)\cap\rho

is a one-dimensional cone, see

[
MR
, Proposition 3.2.6]
,
[
OR13
, Lemma 3.9]
.
Let
v\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{n}
be the generator of
T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}(\rho^{\prime})\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
.
Since
v\in T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}(\rho)
, we have
\pi(v)=0\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}(\rho)
.
Since
v\in T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}(\sigma)
, by Condition 1) of

Definition

2.8
the vector space parallel to
{\mathcal{E}}(\sigma)
contains
v
.
Hence the assignment
\sigma(\varepsilon^{\prime})\mapsto\sigma(\varepsilon^{\prime}+v)
defines a fixed-point-free involution
on the set of facets of
\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}})
containing
\tau(\varepsilon)
.
This proves that there is an even number of such facets, which proves the claim.
∎

Remark 2.15
.

A tropical variety is commonly defined as a
weighted
n
-dimensional rational polyhedral complex
{\mathcal{X}}

that satisfies the so-called balancing condition.
The balancing condition
can be reformulated in terms of tropical homology.
From a weighted polyhedral complex
{\mathcal{X}}

one can construct its fundamental chain in tropical homology.
The balancing condition on
{\mathcal{X}}
is then equivalent to
this fundamental chain being closed, see
[
MZ14
, Proposition 4.3]
.

In analogy to this picture, we propose to
think of Conditions 1) and 2) in Definition
2.8

as a real balancing condition in tropical geometry.
Indeed, given an arbitrary map

\mathcal{E}\colon\Facets(\mathcal{X})\to\Pow({\mathbb{Z}}_{2}(\rho))
,
we may still construct
\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}})

as a (cellular) Borel-Moore chain in
\mathfrak{R}{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
.
Then Proposition
2.14

states that this chain is closed if
{\mathcal{E}}
is a real phase structure.
Moreover, the inverse is true
at least for certain natural special cases, for example
when
{\mathcal{X}}\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}=\Sigma
.
More precisely, the sedentarity
0
part of the proof
of Proposition
2.14
can
be inverted to show that Condition 2) is equivalent to

\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}})
being closed in sedentarity
0
.
Moreover, assuming
{\mathcal{X}}\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}=\Sigma
, given
\rho\in\Sigma

one can extend the argument in the proof to show that

\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}})
is closed at higher sedentarity faces of
\overline{\rho}

if and only if
{\mathcal{E}}(\sigma)
is a union of affine subspace satisfying Condition 1).
Allowing unions may be interesting when treating tropical varieties with weight functions which are not equal to one. Here we do not treat this generality for the sake of simplicity.

3.
Non-singular tropical subvarieties and their patchworks

3.1.
Tropically non-singular polyhedral subspaces

The local models for tropically non-singular polyhedral spaces are matroid fans.
We recall them for convenience.
Given a loopfree matroid
M
on the base set
E
,
we denote by
\Sigma_{M}
the
affine
matroid fan in

{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
and by
{\mathbb{P}}\Sigma_{M}=\Sigma_{M}/\langle(1,\dots,1)\rangle

the
projective
matroid fan in
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}/\langle(1,\dots,1)\rangle
.
We now describe how to construct both of these fans
following Ardila and Klivans
[
AK06
]
.
Fix the vectors
v_{i}=-e_{i}
where
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}

is the standard basis of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
for

E=\{1,\dots,n\}
and set
v_{I}=\sum_{i\in I}v_{i}

for any subset
I\subset E
.
For a chain of flats

{\mathcal{F}}=\{\emptyset\subsetneq F_{1}\subsetneq F_{2}\subsetneq\dots\subsetneq F_{k}\subsetneq E\}

in the lattice of flats
{\mathcal{L}}
,
define the
k+1
-dimensional cone

\sigma_{{\mathcal{F}}}=\langle v_{F_{1}},\dots,v_{F_{k}},\pm v_{E}\rangle_{\geq 0}.

The affine matroid fan
\Sigma_{M}
is the collection of all such cones ranging over the chains in
{\mathcal{L}}
.
In particular, the top dimensional faces of
\Sigma_{M}
are in one to one correspondence with
the maximal chains in the lattice of flats of
M
.
The projective matroid fan
{\mathbb{P}}\Sigma_{M}
is the image of

\Sigma_{M}
in the quotient
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}/(1,\dots,1)
.

If a matroid
M
has loops
L=\cl(\emptyset)
, then we set
\Sigma_{M}:=\Sigma_{M/L}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{E\setminus L}
and

{\mathbb{P}}\Sigma_{M}:={\mathbb{P}}\Sigma_{M/L}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{E\setminus L}/(1,\dots,1)
.
We will often assume
E=\{0,\dots,n\}
.
Note that by
Example

2.2
we can consider
{\mathbb{R}}^{E\setminus L}={\mathbb{T}}{\mathcal{O}}_{\rho_{L}}

as a torus orbit of
{\mathbb{T}}^{n+1}
, and
{\mathbb{R}}^{E\setminus L}/(1,\dots,1)={\mathbb{T}}{\mathcal{O}}_{\rho_{L}}

as a torus orbit of
{{\mathbb{T}}{\mathbb{P}}}^{n}
(excluding the trivial case
E=L
).
In this sense, in the following we will regard
\Sigma_{M}
and
{\mathbb{P}}\Sigma_{M}
as subsets
of
{\mathbb{T}}^{n+1}
and
{{\mathbb{T}}{\mathbb{P}}}^{n}
, respectively.

Definition 3.1
.

[
MR
, Chapter 6]

Let
\Sigma
be a fan and
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
the associated tropical toric
variety. Let

X\subset{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
be a polyhedral subspace and consider
p\in X

of sedentarity
\rho\in\Sigma
and
\sed(p)=\dim(\rho)=k
.

Then
X
is
non-singular
at
p
if there exist

•

a toric isomorphism
\psi\colon{\mathbb{T}}U_{\rho}\to{\mathbb{T}}^{k}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n-k}={\mathbb{T}}U_{\rho_{\{1,\dots,k\}}}\subset{{\mathbb{T}}{\mathbb{P}}}^{n}
,

•

an open neighbourhood
p\in U\subset{\mathbb{T}}U_{\rho}
,

•

a matroid
M
on
E=\{0,1,\dots,n\}
,

such that

\psi(p)=(-\infty,0)
and

\psi(X\cap U)=\overline{{\mathbb{P}}\Sigma}_{M}\cap\psi(U)
.

The polyhedral subspace
X
is a
non-singular tropical subvariety
of
Y
if it is non-singular at all its points.

3.2.
Patchworks of non-singular subvarieties are topological manifolds

Our next goal is to prove
1.1
from the introduction. We point the reader to
[
RRS22
]
for the background on the connection between oriented matroids and real phase structures.

Theorem
1.1
.

Let
X\subset{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
be a non-singular tropical subvariety
of a tropical toric variety
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
.
Let
{\mathcal{E}}
be a real phase structure on
X
.
Then the patchwork
\mathfrak{R}X
is a topological manifold.

By
[
RRS22
, Theorem 1.1]
, we proved that matroidal fans equipped with real phase structures are equivalent to oriented matroids.
In
Proposition

3.4
, we show that the patchwork of a matroid fan with a real phase structure is a topological manifold with determined topology. This is essentially a corollary of the Folkman-Lawrence topological representation theorem for oriented matroids
[
FL78
]
.
We note that similar results on patchworking of matroids were obtained in
[
AKW06
, Theorem 3.4]
and in
[
CLY22
, Theorem A]
.
We start with two examples describing the faces of the
simplicial complexes
\mathfrak{R}\overline{\Sigma}_{M}
and
\mathfrak{R}\overline{{\mathbb{P}}\Sigma}_{M}
.

Example 3.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}
be an oriented matroid with underlying matroid

and
let
{\mathcal{E}}
be the associated real phase structure on

[

, Definition 7]
. Hence

is also a real phase structure on
\overline{\Sigma}_{M}\subset{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
.
By combining Example
2.11
with the definition of

,
we see that the faces of the polyhedral complex
\mathfrak{R}\overline{\Sigma}_{M}\subset\mathfrak{R}{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
are labelled by the chains
of covectors

from
{\mathcal{M}}
.
The final covector
Z_{l}
determines the stratum of
\mathfrak{R}{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
that contains the face.
The associated chain of flats

determines the corresponding

\overline{\Sigma}_{M}
.
The map
\phi
here denotes the forgetful map from oriented matroids to
matroids sending a covector

4.
Patchworking close to the tropical limit

4.1.
Non-singular tropical limits

We denote by
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{C}}((t))
the field of
formal Laurent series with complex coefficients,
and by
{\mathbb{K}}_{\mathbb{R}}
the subfield of series with real coefficients.
As algebraic closure we fix the field of Puiseux series
\bar{{\mathbb{K}}}={\mathbb{C}}\{t\}
.
Throughout the following, we fix an algebraic variety
{\mathbf{X}}\subset({\mathbb{K}}^{*})^{n}
defined over
the subring in
{\mathbb{K}}
of series that converge for
0<|t|<1
.
We can alternatively regard
{\mathbf{X}}
as a
subset of
({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}\times{\mathcal{D}}^{*}
describing a family
of algebraic varieties in
({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
parametrized by
{\mathcal{D}}^{*}
,
the punctured unit disc in
{\mathbb{C}}
.
From now on, we refer to such
{\mathbf{X}}
as an

analytic family over
{\mathcal{D}}^{*}
.
For
t\in{\mathcal{D}}^{*}
, we denote the fibre by
{\mathbf{X}}_{t}={\mathbf{X}}\cap(({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}\times\{t\})
.
We define the tropical limit
\Trop({\mathbf{X}})\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
as the closure of
-\text{val}({\mathbf{X}}\otimes\bar{{\mathbb{K}}})
, where

\text{val}\colon(\bar{{\mathbb{K}}}^{*})^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

is the coordinate-wise valuation map.
The tropicalization
\Trop({\mathbf{X}})
is a tropical subvariety
(that is, a weighted polyhedral subspace that
satisfies the balancing condition).
We set
X=\Trop({\mathbf{X}})
.

For each
p\in X
, we have an associated initial scheme

\init_{p}{\mathbf{X}}\subset({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
.
We have
d:=\dim_{\mathbb{K}}{\mathbf{X}}=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\init_{p}{\mathbf{X}}
.
For a generic point
p\in X
,
\init_{p}{\mathbf{X}}

is invariant under the action of an
d
-dimensional
subtorus
{\mathbf{T}}_{p}
of
({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
.
We denote the quotient under this action by

{\mathbf{X}}_{p}=\init_{p}{\mathbf{X}}/{\mathbf{T}}_{p}
. Hence
{\mathbf{X}}_{p}
is a
0
-dimensional scheme.
The
weight
of a generic point
p
is defined
as

\omega(p)=\deg({\mathbf{X}}_{p})=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}K[{\mathbf{X}}_{p}]
.
In particular, the weight of
p
is
1

if and only if

{\mathbf{X}}_{p}
is a reduced
point if and only if

\init_{p}{\mathbf{X}}
is reduced and irreducible.
Throughout the following, and by slight abuse of notation,
we say that
{\mathbf{X}}
has non-singular tropical limit
,
or just
X=\Trop({\mathbf{X}})
is non-singular
, if both of the following conditions hold:

•

the scheme
\init_{p}{\mathbf{X}}
is reduced and irreducible for generic
p
(weight
1
),

•

the polyhedral subspace
X
is non-singular
in the sense of Definition
3.1
.

We first collect a few consequences of
{\mathbf{X}}
having non-singular tropical limit.
A subscheme
\textbf{L}\subset({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
is called
linear

if its closure
\overline{\textbf{L}}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n}

under the standard inclusion of
({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
to
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}

is a linear subspace.
In particular, this implies that
L
is reduced and irreducible.
Then
\overline{\textbf{L}}\backslash\textbf{L}
is the complement of an
arrangement of
n+1
(not necessarily distinct) hyperplanes.
We denote the associated matroid by
M_{\textbf{L}}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

[
KP11
, Proposition 4.2]

Let
\textbf{L}\subset({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
be a subscheme
with non-singular tropical limit
\Trop(\textbf{L})={\mathbb{P}}\Sigma_{M}
,
where
M
is a matroid.
Then
L
is linear and
M_{\textbf{L}}=M
.

A polyhedral subdivision
{\mathcal{X}}
of
X=\Trop({\mathbf{X}})
is called
tropical

if it satisfies the following two conditions.

(1)

Fix
\sigma\in{\mathcal{X}}
. Then for any
p,q
in the relative
interior of
\sigma
we have
\init_{p}{\mathbf{X}}=\init_{q}{\mathbf{X}}
.
We set
\init_{\sigma}{\mathbf{X}}=\init_{p}{\mathbf{X}}
for one such
p
.

(2)

The scheme
\init_{\sigma}{\mathbf{X}}
is invariant under the action
of the subtorus
{\mathbf{T}}_{\sigma}=T(\sigma)\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\subset({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
.
We denote the quotient by

{\mathbf{X}}_{\sigma}=\init_{\sigma}{\mathbf{X}}/{\mathbf{T}}_{\sigma}
.
We have
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbf{X}}_{\sigma}=d-\dim\sigma
.

It is shown for example in
[
Spe05
, Theorem 2.2.1]
that tropical subdivisions
exist for any analytic family
{\mathbf{X}}
.
Moreover, we have the following.

Proposition 4.2
.

If
{\mathbf{X}}
has non-singular tropical limit
X=\Trop({\mathbf{X}})
, then any subdivision
{\mathcal{X}}
of
X

is tropical.

Proof.

This is a result known more generally for so-called schön subvarieties,
see
[
Tev07
, Definition 1.3, Theorem 1.4]

for the
?
constant coefficient
?
case and

[
LQ11
, Definition 7.1, Theorem 7.12]
for the general case.
By
[
Hac08
, Lemma 2.7]
and
[
Spe05
, Theorem 2.4.2]
,
the variety
{\mathbf{X}}
is schön if and only of

\init_{p}{\mathbf{X}}
is non-singular for all
p\in X
. To finish the proof we show the
latter property (see also

[
KS12
, Section 2, Propositions 2.6 and 2.7]

for a summary of the previous discussion).
By
[
Spe05
, Proposition 2.2.3]
, the tropical limit of
\init_{p}{\mathbf{X}}
for
p\in X

is
\Trop(\init_{p}{\mathbf{X}})=\Star_{p}X
.
By our assumptions,
\Star_{p}X
is isomorphic to a matroid fan

{\mathbb{P}}\Sigma_{M}
via a change of coordinates in
{\text{GL}}(n,{\mathbb{Z}})
.
Hence by
Lemma

4.1
,
\init_{p}{\mathbf{X}}
is isomorphic to a linear subvariety via

\init_{p}{\mathbf{X}}
is non-singular.

In the situation of Theorem

Theorem

To do so, we follow closely

]

[

Given a polyhedron

obtained as the closure of the cone over
\sigma\times\{1\}
.
Given a polyhedral complex
{\mathcal{X}}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

we denote by

the fan in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}

C(\sigma)
,
\sigma\in{\mathcal{X}}
, and
\rho\times\{0\}
,

.

\sigma
is

is unimodular. In particular, the polyhedron
\sigma

{\mathbb{Q}}
-coordinates.

\displaystyle\kappa=\text{Conv}(x_{0},x_{0}+e_{1},\dots,x_{0}+e_{k}),\text{ and }

-coordinates.

.

In the remaining subsections, we will prove

.
The general strategy will be as follows.
A strictly unimodular subdivision

4.4.
Regular CW complexes and pairs

In this subsection, we present a convenient framework for the description of the homeomorphisms
that we are about to construct.

Let
X
be a Hausdorff topological space. A subset
\sigma\subset X

is called a
(regular)
n
-cell

if there exists an homeomorphism
\alpha\colon D^{n}\to\sigma
.
Here,
D^{n}=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:|x|\leq 1\}
denotes the closed unit
n
-ball.
Note that
\sigma
is compact and hence closed in
X
.
We define the
relative interior
of
\sigma
by

\sigma^{\diamond}=\alpha(({\mathcal{D}}^{n})^{\circ})
and
the
relative boundary
by
d\sigma=\sigma\setminus\sigma^{\diamond}=\alpha(\partial D^{n})
.
These notions are well-defined, since if given a second homeomorphism

\alpha^{\prime}\colon D^{n}\to\sigma
, the composition
\alpha^{\prime}\circ\alpha^{-1}\colon D^{n}\to D^{n}
is a homeomorphism and hence preserves
\partial D^{n}=S^{n-1}
.
Note also that
n
, called the dimension of
\sigma
, is well-defined
since
D^{n}
and
D^{m}
are homeomorphic if and only if
n=m
.

Definition 4.9
.

Let
X
be a Hausdorff topological space.
A
finite regular CW complex
{\mathcal{X}}
with support
X
is a finite collection
of subsets of
X
such that

(1)

each
\sigma\in{\mathcal{X}}
is a
n_{\sigma}
-cell of
X
for some
n_{\sigma}\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

(2)

the relative interiors
\sigma^{\diamond}
,
\sigma\in{\mathcal{X}}
, form a disjoint union of
X
,

(3)

for each
\sigma
, the relative boundary
d\sigma
is a union of cells of smaller dimension.

A few comments: Given
{\mathcal{X}}
according to the definition, we can choose a homeomorphism

\alpha_{\sigma}\colon D^{n_{\sigma}}\to\sigma\subset X
for every
\sigma\in{\mathcal{X}}
.
By
[
Hat02
, Proposition A.2]
(note that condition (iii) in
loc. cit.
is not needed/automatic since
we restrict to the finite case), the
\alpha_{\sigma}
yield characteristic maps
of a CW complex supporting
X
. Since each
\alpha_{\sigma}
is a homeomorphism,
this CW complex is regular according to the standard definition, see
[
LW69
, Chapter 3, Definition 1.1]
.
Moreover, choosing different
\alpha^{\prime}_{\sigma}
, we obtain a strictly equivalent CW complex, see

[
LW69
, p. 7]
. This justifies our simplified definition and the naming.
We also use
|{\mathcal{X}}|=X=\bigcup_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{X}}}\sigma
to denote the support of
{\mathcal{X}}
.
We consider
{\mathcal{X}}
as a poset with the partial ordering given by inclusion of cells.
We will sometimes refer to this a the
cell poset
of
{\mathcal{X}}
to emphasize that
we forget all geometric information and only consider the poset structure.
The previous discussion also implies that regular CW complexes in the sense of our definition
admit barycentric subdivisions. For convenience, we express this in the following statement.

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a regular CW complex. Then there exists a homeomorphism

\beta\colon|O({\mathcal{X}})|\to|{\mathcal{X}}|
from
the simplicial complex
O({\mathcal{X}})
(the order complex of the poset
{\mathcal{X}}
)
to
{\mathcal{X}}
such that the image of the simplex in
O({\mathcal{X}})
labelled by the chain

\sigma_{1}\subsetneq\dots\subsetneq\sigma_{k}
lies in
\sigma_{k}^{\diamond}
.
Such a
\beta\colon|O({\mathcal{X}})|\to|{\mathcal{X}}|
is called a
barycentric subdivision
of
{\mathcal{X}}
.

Proof.

This is essentially
[
LW69
, Theorem 1.7]
, but for later reference
we give a sketch. Fix characteristic maps

\alpha_{\sigma}\colon D^{n_{\sigma}}\to\sigma
for every
\sigma\in{\mathcal{X}}
.
We proceed by induction on the
k
-skeleton
{\mathcal{X}}^{(k)}
of
{\mathcal{X}}
.
The initial case
k=0
is trivial. Assume that a barycentric subdivision
\beta^{(k)}\colon|O({\mathcal{X}}^{(k)})|\to|{\mathcal{X}}^{(k})|
has already been constructed.
Given a
k+1
-cell
\sigma
, pulling back via

\alpha_{\sigma}\colon S^{k}\to\partial\sigma\subset|{\mathcal{X}}^{(k)}|

we obtain a triangulation on
S^{k}
. Coning over
0\in D^{k+1}
produces
a triangulation of
D^{k+1}
, and hence
\sigma
, labelled canonically by chains
of the form
\sigma_{1}\subsetneq\dots\subsetneq\sigma_{l}\subset\sigma
.
Repeating this for every
k+1
-cell
\sigma
, we obtain the desired
\beta_{k+1}
.
∎

A topological pair
\tau\subset\sigma
is called a standard pair of cells of codimension
k

if there exists an homeomorphism
\alpha\colon D^{n}\to\sigma
such
that
\alpha^{-1}(\tau)=\{x\in{\mathcal{D}}^{n}:x_{1}=\dots=x_{k}=0\}
.
In particular,
\sigma
is an
n
-cell,
\tau
is a
n-k
-cell, and

d\tau=\tau\cap d\sigma
as well as
\tau^{\diamond}=\tau\cap\sigma^{\diamond}
.

Definition 4.11
.

Let
X
be a Hausdorff topological space.
A
finite regular CW pair
of codimension
k
is a tuple
({\mathcal{X}},Y)
such that

{\mathcal{X}}
is a finite regular CW complex and
Y\subset|{\mathcal{X}}|
is a subset
such that for each
\sigma\in{\mathcal{X}}
either
\sigma\cap Y=\emptyset
or

\sigma\cap Y\subset\sigma
is a standard pair of cells of
codimension
k
.

It follows easily from the definition that in this case

{\mathcal{Y}}=\{\sigma\cap Y:\sigma\in{\mathcal{X}},\sigma\cap Y\neq\emptyset\}

is a regular CW complex,
\dim{\mathcal{Y}}=\dim{\mathcal{X}}-k

and
\partial(\sigma\cap Y)=\partial\sigma\cap Y
as well as

(\sigma\cap Y)^{\diamond}=\sigma^{\diamond}\cap Y
.
Note that
Y=|{\mathcal{Y}}|
is a closed subset of
|{\mathcal{X}}|
.
It will be convenient to
use the notation

{\mathcal{X}}_{Y}=\{\sigma\in{\mathcal{X}}:\sigma\cap Y\neq\emptyset\}.

By definition, we have a canonical bijection between
{\mathcal{X}}_{Y}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
.
Since we only consider
finite
regular CW complexes and pairs, we will drop the finite from now on.

The notion of barycentric subdivisions extends to regular CW pairs in the following
sense.

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
({\mathcal{X}},Y)
be a regular CW pair with
X=|{\mathcal{X}}|
.
Then there exists a barycentric subdivision

\beta\colon|O({\mathcal{X}})|\to X
of
{\mathcal{X}}

such that the image of the simplicial subcomplex
O({\mathcal{X}}_{Y})\subset O({\mathcal{X}})

(given by the subposet
{\mathcal{X}}_{Y}\subset{\mathcal{X}}
) is equal to
Y
.
Moreover, in this case the restricted map
\beta_{Y}\colon|O({\mathcal{X}}_{Y})|\to Y

is a barycentric subdivision of
{\mathcal{Y}}
using the identification

O({\mathcal{X}}_{Y})=O({\mathcal{Y}})
.

Proof.

We just have to slightly adapt the argument given for
Proposition

4.10
:
We again choose characteristic maps
\alpha_{\sigma}\colon D^{n_{\sigma}}\to\sigma
for every

\sigma\in{\mathcal{X}}
, but additionally assume that

\alpha_{\sigma}^{-1}(Y)=\{x\in{\mathcal{D}}^{n}:x_{1}=\dots=x_{k}=0\}
whenever
\sigma\cap Y\neq\emptyset
. Note that for any
m
the pair
({\mathcal{X}}^{(m)},Y\cap|{\mathcal{X}}^{(m)}|)

is a regular CW pair as well. Hence the same recursive coning procedure as in

Proposition

4.10
produces the desired homeomorphism.
Finally, given a chain
\sigma_{1}\subsetneq\dots\subsetneq\sigma_{l}
in
{\mathcal{X}}_{Y}
,
the image of the corresponding simplex lies in
\sigma_{l}^{\diamond}\cap Y=(\sigma_{l}\cap Y)^{\diamond}
, which shows that the restricted map

\beta_{Y}\colon|O({\mathcal{X}}_{Y})|\to Y

is a barycentric subdivision of
{\mathcal{Y}}
.
∎

The following theorem states that,
up to homeomorphisms, a regular CW complex
{\mathcal{X}}
is determined by the poset structure
on
{\mathcal{X}}
, and similarly, a regular CW pair is determined by the pair of posets

{\mathcal{X}}_{Y}\subset{\mathcal{X}}
. This follows easily from the existence of barycentric subdivisions.

Theorem 4.13
.

Let
({\mathcal{X}},Y)
and
({\mathcal{X}}^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
be two regular CW pairs.
Assume that there exists poset isomorphism
\varphi:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
that sends

{\mathcal{X}}_{Y}
to
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}_{Y^{\prime}}
. Then there exists a homeomorphism

\Phi\colon|{\mathcal{X}}|\to|{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}|
that sends a cell
\sigma
to the cell
\varphi(\sigma)

and sends
Y
to
Y^{\prime}
.

Proof.

The poset isomorphism
\varphi
yields an induced simplicial homeomorphism

O(\varphi)\colon|O({\mathcal{X}})|\to|O({\mathcal{X}}^{\prime})|
that sends the subcomplex

O({\mathcal{X}}_{Y})
to the subcomplex
O({\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}_{Y^{\prime}})
. Pre- and postcomposing

O(\varphi)
with barycentric subdivisions as provided in

Proposition

4.12
yields the desired homeomorphism
\Phi
.
∎

4.5.
The local cases

In this subsection we will deal with the local case of patchworking
which, for non-singular tropical subvarieties means the case of linear spaces:
We fix a real linear subvariety
\textbf{L}\subset({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
of dimension
d
.
Its locus of real points is
{\mathbb{R}}\textbf{L}\subset({\mathbb{R}}^{*})^{n}
.
We denote by
L=\Trop(\textbf{L})
the support of the associated matroid fan
and by
{\mathcal{E}}={\mathcal{E}}_{\textbf{L}}
the associated real phase structure on
L
.
As usual,
\mathfrak{R}L=\mathfrak{R}(L,{\mathcal{E}})\subset\mathfrak{R}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\approx({\mathbb{R}}^{*})^{n}

denotes the associated patchwork.
It suffices to consider a single orthant, and by symmetry, we focus
on the positive orthant labelled by
\varepsilon=(0,\dots,0)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{n}
.
We set
{\mathbb{R}}_{>}=(0,\infty)
and

\displaystyle{\mathbb{R}}_{>}\textbf{L}

\displaystyle:={\mathbb{R}}\textbf{L}\cap{\mathbb{R}}_{>}^{n}

\displaystyle\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{>}^{n},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{>}L

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{R}L\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}((0,\dots,0))

\displaystyle\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

Note that
{\mathbb{R}}_{>}\textbf{L}
is non-empty if and only if
\mathfrak{R}_{>}L
is
non-empty by definition of
{\mathcal{E}}
.

Let
\Sigma
be a complete unimodular fan.
The closure of
\mathfrak{R}_{>}L
in
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
is denoted by

\mathfrak{R}_{\geq}L
. It is equal to

\mathfrak{R}\overline{L}\cap{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma((0,\dots,0))
.
The closure of the positive orthant in
{\mathbb{R}}\Sigma
is denoted
by
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\Sigma
(note that
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\Sigma
can also be described
as the toric variety over the semigroup
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}
, so the notation is consistent).
The closure of
{\mathbb{R}}_{>}\textbf{L}
in
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\Sigma

is denoted by
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\textbf{L}
.
It is equal to
{\mathbb{R}}\overline{\textbf{L}}\cap{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\Sigma
.
The goal of this subsection is to prove that under suitable conditions
the pairs
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\textbf{L}\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\Sigma
and

\mathfrak{R}_{\geq}L\subset{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
are
standard pairs of cells of dimension
(d,n)
.

Note that
L
is a union of a collection of cones in
\Sigma
if and only if

\Sigma_{L}=\{\sigma\in\Sigma:\sigma\subset L\}

is a polyhedral subdivision for
L
. We want to prove
the following two analogous statements, which we state separately for more
convenient referencing.

Theorem 4.14
.

Assume that
{\mathbb{R}}_{>}\textbf{L}
is non-empty.
Assume that
L
is a union of cones in
\Sigma
.
Then
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\textbf{L}\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\Sigma
is a
standard pair of cells of dimension
(d,n)
.

Theorem 4.15
.

Assume that
\mathfrak{R}_{>}L
is non-empty.
Assume that
L
is a union of cones in
\Sigma
.
Then

\mathfrak{R}_{\geq}L\subset{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
is a
standard pair of cells of dimension
(d,n)
.

We proceed in several steps.

Lemma 4.16
.

Assume that
{\mathbb{R}}_{>}\textbf{L}
(and hence
\mathfrak{R}_{>}L
) is non-empty.
Then the pairs

{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\textbf{L}\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}

and
\mathfrak{R}_{\geq}L\subset{\mathbb{T}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}

are standard pairs of cells
of dimension
(d,n)
.

We emphasize that in this lemma we do not require that

L
is a union of cones of
\Sigma
.

Proof.

It is clear that
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\textbf{L}\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}

is a standard pair of cells:
In projective coordinates,
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
can be described as the
standard simplex
x_{0},\dots,x_{n}\geq 0
,
x_{0}+\cdots+x_{n}=1
.
Since
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\textbf{L}
is the intersection
of this simplex with a linear subspace
which intersects its interior, the claim follows.

To finish, we construct a homeomorphism between

{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\textbf{L}\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}

and
\mathfrak{R}_{\geq}L\subset{\mathbb{T}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
.
As explained above, we regard

{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\textbf{L}\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
as
the intersection of the simplex with a
linear space. We consider a barycentric subdivision
of
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
where the barycenter of a face is chosen
in
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\textbf{L}
whenever the intersection
with the relative interior of this face is non-empty.
We obtain a pair of simplicial
complexes with the following characteristics:

•

vertices of
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
: non-empty subsets of
E=\{0,\dots,n\}
.

•

simplices of
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
: chains under inclusion.

•

vertices of
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\textbf{L}
:
flats of
M_{\textbf{L}}
that touch
\varepsilon=(0,\dots,0)
,
that is,
{\mathcal{F}}_{lv}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{\textbf{L}}}((0,\dots,0))
from

Example

3.3
.

•

simplices of
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\textbf{L}
:
chains of
{\mathcal{F}}_{lv}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{\textbf{L}}}((0,\dots,0))

under inclusion.

As explained in
Example

3.3
,
\mathfrak{R}_{\geq}L\subset{\mathbb{T}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}

is a pair of simplicial complexes with exactly the same poset structure,
which induces a simplicial homeomorphism between the pairs.
∎

We now
first deal with the tropical side, that is, with
the pair
\mathfrak{R}_{\geq}L\subset{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
.

Lemma 4.17
.

For any complete unimodular fan
\Sigma
,
the space
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
is a ball.
Moreover, given a subset
X\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
which is a union of
cones in
\Sigma
, the pair
\overline{X}\subset{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
is homeomorphic
to the pair of PL-stars at
0
of
X\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

We use PL-stars only for simplicity, since they are well-defined up to PL-homeomorphism.
We write
\pStar(X)=\pStar_{0}(X)
for the PL-star at the origin
0\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Proof.

Given a cone
\sigma\in\Sigma
of dimension
k
, choosing primitive generators
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}

we obtain an identification with
[0,\infty)^{k}
.
The closure
\overline{\sigma}
in
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
is then canonically homeomorphic
to an infinite cube
[0,\infty]^{k}
.
More generally, suppose
X
is the support of a subset of cones of
\Sigma
and denote this subset by
\Sigma(X)\subset\Sigma
. Then
the closure
\overline{X}
in
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
(or, equivalently, in
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma(X)
)
is obtained by gluing these infinite cubes
\overline{\sigma}
,
\sigma\in\Sigma(X)
,
as a direct limit governed by the poset
\Sigma(X)
.

Fix a homeomorphism
[0,\infty]\to[0,1]
that sends
0
to
0
.
This induces an homeomorphism between
\overline{\sigma}
and the (finite) cube

in

whose vertices are the points

,

.
Moreover, these homeomorphisms glue to a homeomorphism between the direct limit
\overline{X}

and the set

\pStar(X)=\bigcup_{\sigma\in\Sigma(X)}C_{\sigma}.

As the notation suggests, the set
\pStar(X)
is a PL-star for
X
in
0
.
Finally, applying the same reasoning to
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
\Sigma({\mathbb{R}}^{n})=\Sigma
,
we obtain a homeomorphism of pairs between

and
\pStar(X)\subset\pStar({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. Of course,
\pStar({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is a ball.
∎

We can now finish the proof of

Proof of
4.15
.

By

4.16
, we now that

\mathfrak{R}_{\geq}{L}\subset{\mathbb{T}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}

Since

corresponds to a point in the relative interior

By

\mathfrak{R}_{\geq}L\subset{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma

∎

We now turn our attention to the pair

{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\textbf{L}\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\Sigma
.

Proof of
4.14
.

As we mentioned in
Remark

2.10
,

{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\Sigma
is
homeomorphic to
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
via the extended logarithm map
\Log
.
Hence by
Lemma

4.17
,

{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\Sigma
is a ball.
We now proceed by induction on
\dim(\textbf{L})=d
.
For

We now prove the induction step

.

By our first remark, the subsets
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\Star_{\rho}\Sigma
,
\rho\in\Sigma
,
form a regular CW complex (with support
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\Sigma
).
By the induction assumption, we know that

of positive dimension, the

{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\textbf{L}\cap{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\Star_{\rho}\Sigma\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\Star_{\rho}\Sigma

is a standard pair of cells, while for

{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\textbf{L}\cap{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\Star_{\sigma}\Sigma
is empty.
It follows that
\partial{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\textbf{L}\subset\partial{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\Sigma

is a regular CW pair.

The analogous argument shows the corresponding tropical pair

\partial\mathfrak{R}_{\geq}L\subset\partial{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma

is a regular CW pair with the same poset structure
(since
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\textbf{L}\cap{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\Star_{\rho}\Sigma\neq\emptyset

if and only if

if and only if

is a standard pair of cells.

Since

is a union of cones in

.

by any complete unimodular fan supporting

,

is a standard pair of spheres

.
Hence we assume from now on that

is the permutahedral fan in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

The permutahedral variety

is obtained from
{{\mathbb{R}}{\mathbb{P}}}^{n}

from consecutively blowing up
(strict transforms) of coordinate subspaces of

We know from

that

is a standard pair of cells,
so it remains to
prove that the topology does not change under the aforementioned blow-ups.
For clarity, we denote the closures in
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
and
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\Pi^{n}

by
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}^{\mathbb{P}}\textbf{L}
and
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}^{\Pi}\textbf{L}
, respectively.
The local model for all these blow-ups is blowing up

along

\{0\}^{k}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n-k}

(the image of
L
after this change of coordinates is still a linear subvariety
in

which we call

again by abuse of notation).
We may assume that

.
In order to compare
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}^{\mathbb{P}}\textbf{L}
and
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}^{\Pi}\textbf{L}

,
that is, we may further reduce to the situation of the blow-up
\Bl_{0}{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}^{n}

of
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}^{n}
in the origin

.
By abuse of notation, we now denote by
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}^{\mathbb{P}}\textbf{L}
and
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}^{\Pi}\textbf{L}

their restriction to
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}^{n}
and
\Bl_{0}{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}^{n}

The blow-up can be constructed explicitly as

{\mathbb{R}}_{\triangle}^{n}=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}^{n}:\sum_{i}x_{i}\geq 1\}

with blow-down map given by

Under this identification,

We now construct a homeomorphism

such that
\sum_{i}a_{i}=1

For any

\displaystyle S_{I}=\Conv(a,2a,e_{i},i\in I),

\displaystyle T_{I}=\Conv(0,2a,e_{i},i\in I).

is the piecewise linear map that maps

and being identity

Clearly,

∎

5.
A spectral sequence for the homology of a patchwork

In this section, given a non-singular tropical subvariety
X
of a tropical toric variety
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
and a real phase structure
\mathcal{E}
on
X
, we describe following
[
RS23
]
a filtration of
H_{*}(\mathfrak{R}X;{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
and we give a proof of Theorem
1.3
. The case of real phase structures on non-singular tropical hypersurfaces was treated in
[
RS23
]
.
We begin with an overview of tropical homology.

5.1.
Celluar cosheaves

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a polyhedral complex in
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
. A
cellular cosheaf
{\mathcal{F}}
on
{\mathcal{X}}
is a collection of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
-vector spaces
{\mathcal{F}}(\tau)
for every
\tau\in{\mathcal{X}}

together with linear maps
i_{\sigma\tau}\colon{\mathcal{F}}(\sigma)\to{\mathcal{F}}(\tau)
for every inclusion of faces
\tau\subset\sigma
such that

i_{\sigma\sigma}=\text{id}
and
i_{\tau\upsilon}\circ i_{\sigma\tau}
for every chain
\upsilon\subset\tau\subset\sigma
.

Given a cellular cosheaf
{\mathcal{F}}
on
{\mathcal{X}}
, the groups of
cellular (Borel-Moore)
q
-chains with coefficients in
{\mathcal{F}}
are

C_{q}({\mathcal{X}};{\mathcal{F}})=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\dim\sigma=q\\
\sigma\text{ compact }\end{subarray}}{\mathcal{F}}(\sigma)\quad\text{ and }\quad C^{BM}_{q}({\mathcal{X}};{\mathcal{F}})=\bigoplus_{\dim\sigma=q}{\mathcal{F}}(\sigma).

The boundary maps
\partial\colon C_{q}({\mathcal{X}};\mathcal{F})\to C_{q-1}({\mathcal{X}};\mathcal{F})
are
the usual cellular boundary maps combined with the cosheaf maps
i_{\sigma\tau}
.
Note that since we work with vector spaces over
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
, it is not necessary to choose orientations of the faces.
The
q
-th (Borel-Moore) homology groups of
{\mathcal{F}}
on
{\mathcal{X}}
are

H_{q}({\mathcal{X}};\mathcal{F})=H_{q}(C_{\bullet}({\mathcal{X}};\mathcal{F}))\quad\text{ and }\quad H_{q}^{BM}({\mathcal{X}};\mathcal{F})=H_{q}(C_{\bullet}^{BM}({\mathcal{X}};\mathcal{F})).

Let
X
be a polyhedral space in
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
. A
cellular cosheaf
{\mathcal{F}}
on
X
is a family of cellular cosheaves for any
{\mathcal{X}}
such that
X=|{\mathcal{X}}|

(by abuse of notation, we denote all these sheaves by
{\mathcal{F}}
) satisfying the following condition: If
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
is a subdivision of
{\mathcal{X}}
,
\sigma\in{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
,
and
\tau\in{\mathcal{X}}

the unique face such that
\relint(\sigma)\subset\relint(\tau)
, then
{\mathcal{F}}(\sigma)={\mathcal{F}}(\tau)
.
In order to ensure that the homology groups are invariant under subdivision, we have to assume that our polyhedral complexes satisfy the following property.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a polyhedral complex in
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
. We say that
\mathcal{X}
is a regular polyhedral complex if the cellular structure induced by
\mathcal{X}
on the one-point compactification
\hat{X}
of
X
is a regular CW-complex,
see
Section

4.4
.

For example, if
X={\mathbb{R}}
and
\mathcal{X}
consists of a unique one dimensional cell, then it is not regular.
From now on, we will only consider polyhedral spaces
X
for which any
subdivision
{\mathcal{X}}
admits a regular refinement.
For such
X
, we define

H_{q}(X;\mathcal{F})=H_{q}({\mathcal{X}};\mathcal{F})\quad\text{ and }\quad H_{q}^{BM}(X;\mathcal{F})=H_{q}^{BM}({\mathcal{X}};\mathcal{F}),

where
{\mathcal{X}}
is some chosen regular subdivision of
X
.
The definition does not depend on this choice in the following sense:
Any two regular subdivisions
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
have a common regular refinement
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime\prime}
. By

[
Cur14
, Theorem 7.3.9]
, the homology groups with respect to
{\mathcal{X}}
,
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
and
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime\prime}

are canonically isomorphic.

These homology groups of
X
are all well-defined up to canonical isomorphisms.
A
morphism

{\mathcal{F}}\to{\mathcal{G}}
of cellular cosheaves on a polyhedral complex
{\mathcal{X}}
is a collection of morphisms
{\mathcal{F}}(\tau)\to{\mathcal{G}}(\tau)
for every
\tau\in{\mathcal{X}}

which commute with the restriction maps of both
{\mathcal{F}}
and
{\mathcal{G}}
.
We get the induced notions of
kernels, cokernels, and exact sequences
of morphisms of cellular cosheaves on
{\mathcal{X}}
.

5.2.
Tropical homology

Let
X
be a non-singular tropical subvariety of a tropical toric variety
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
defined by a fan
\Sigma
.
If
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma_{\rho}
and
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma_{\eta}
are a pair of strata corresponding to cones
\rho\subset\eta
of
\Sigma
then there is a canonical projection map of tangent spaces producing the map

(5.1)

\pi_{\rho\eta}\colon T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}({\mathbb{T}}\Sigma_{\rho})\to T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}({\mathbb{T}}\Sigma_{\eta}).

If
\sigma
is a polyhedron of sedentarity
\rho
such that
\sigma\cap{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma_{\eta}\neq\emptyset
, then

\pi_{\rho\eta}
maps
T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}(\sigma)
onto
T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}(\sigma\cap{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma_{\eta})
.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a polyhedral complex in
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
.
The
p
-multi-tangent spaces
of
{\mathcal{X}}
are cellular cosheaves
{\mathcal{F}}_{p}
on
{\mathcal{X}}
defined as follows.
For a face
\tau\in{\mathcal{X}}
of sedentarity
\rho
, we set

(5.2)

{\mathcal{F}}_{p}(\tau)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau\subset\sigma\subset{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma_{\rho}\end{subarray}}\bigwedge^{p}T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}(\sigma).

Given
\tau^{\prime}\subset\tau
of sedentarity
\eta
,
the maps of the cellular cosheaf

i_{\tau\tau^{\prime}}\colon{\mathcal{F}}_{p}(\tau)\to{\mathcal{F}}_{p}(\tau^{\prime})

are given by composing
\wedge^{p}\pi_{\rho\eta}:\bigwedge^{p}T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}(\sigma)\to\bigwedge^{p}T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}(\sigma\cap{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma_{\eta})
from (
5.1
)
with the inclusion maps

\bigwedge^{p}T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}(\sigma\cap{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma_{\eta})\to{\mathcal{F}}_{p}(\tau^{\prime})
.

Clearly, the cosheaves
{\mathcal{F}}_{p}
give rise to cellular cosheaves on polyhedral spaces
X
.
The groups

H_{q}(X;\mathcal{F}_{p})\quad\text{ and }\quad H_{q}^{BM}(X;\mathcal{F}_{p})

are called the
(p,q)
-th (Borel-Moore) tropical homology group
.

5.3.
The sign cosheaf

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a polyhedral complex in
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
. Suppose
{\mathcal{X}}
is equipped with a real phase structure
{\mathcal{E}}
.
We now recall the sign cosheaf defined in
[
RS23
]
.

For a face
\tau
of
{\mathcal{X}}
of sedentarity
\rho
, we set

{\mathcal{E}}(\tau):=\{\varepsilon\in T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}({\mathbb{T}}\Sigma_{\rho}):\tau(\varepsilon)\in\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}})\}.

Note that this coincides with the original
{\mathcal{E}}(\sigma)
in the case when
\sigma
is a facet.
We define the abstract vector space
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{E}}(\tau)
with basis given by the elements of
\mathcal{E}(\tau)
,

(5.3)

\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{E}}(\tau)={\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\left\langle w_{\varepsilon}\mid\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}(\tau)\right\rangle.

The vector space
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{E}}(\tau)
is a linear subspace of the abstract vector space

{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\left\langle w_{\varepsilon}\mid\varepsilon\in T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}({\mathbb{T}}\Sigma_{\rho})\right\rangle.

If
\tau^{\prime}\subset\tau
is a face of sedentarity
\eta
, then
\tau(\varepsilon)\in\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}})

implies that
\tau^{\prime}(\pi_{\rho\eta}(\varepsilon))\in\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}})
(with the projection map

\pi_{\sigma\tau}
from (
5.1
).
It follows that

(5.4)

i_{\tau\tau^{\prime}}\colon{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{E}}(\tau)\to\colon{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{E}}(\tau^{\prime}),\quad w_{\varepsilon}\mapsto w_{\pi_{\rho\eta}(\varepsilon)}

is a well-defined linear map for all
\tau^{\prime}\subset\tau
.

Definition 5.3
.

The
sign cosheaf

{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{E}}
on
{\mathcal{X}}
is the cellular cosheaf on
{\mathcal{X}}

given by the spaces in (
5.3
) and the cosheaf maps in (
5.4
).
It gives rise to a cellular sheaf
{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{E}}
on
X=|{\mathcal{X}}|
.

The proof of the next proposition is exactly the same as the proof of Proposition 3.17 in
[
RS23
]
.
We denote by
C_{\bullet}(\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}});{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
and
C^{BM}_{\bullet}(\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}});{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
the ordinary
cellular chain complexes of
\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}})
over
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
X
be a polyhedral subspace with real phase structure
{\mathcal{E}}
and associated sign cosheaf
{\mathcal{S}}
.
Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a polyhedral complex such that
X=|{\mathcal{X}}|
.
Then we have
isomorphisms of chain complexes

C_{\bullet}({\mathcal{X}};{\mathcal{S}})\cong C_{\bullet}(\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}});{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})\quad\text{and}\quad C^{BM}_{\bullet}({\mathcal{X}};{\mathcal{S}})\cong C^{BM}_{\bullet}(\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}});{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})

and therefore also isomorphisms

H_{q}(X;{\mathcal{S}})\cong H_{q}(\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}});{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})\quad\text{and}\quad H^{BM}_{q}(X;{\mathcal{S}})\cong H^{BM}_{q}(\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}});{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}).

5.4.
The filtration of the sign cosheaf

We describe here a filtration of the sign cosheaf
{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{E}}
. This filtration is a direct generalisation of the one described in
[
RS23
]
in the case of hypersurfaces.
Given a subset
H\subset T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}(\rho)
, we denote by
\text{Aff}_{p}(H)
the set of all
p
-dimensional affine subspaces of
T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}(\rho)
contained in
H
.
For a subset
G\subset T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}(\rho)
, set

w_{G}:=\sum_{\epsilon\in G}w_{\epsilon}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\left\langle w_{\varepsilon}\mid\varepsilon\in T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}(\rho)\right\rangle.

Finally, we define

K_{p}(H):=\langle w_{G}\ |\ G\in\text{Aff}_{p}(H)\rangle.

Definition 5.5
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a polyhedral complex of sedentarity
\rho
in
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
with real phase structure
\mathcal{E}
.
For all
p
, we define a collection of cosheaves
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}
on
{\mathcal{X}}
.
On a face
\tau
of
{\mathcal{X}}
of sedentarity
\eta
, we set

(5.5)

{\mathcal{K}}_{p}(\tau)=\sum_{\sigma\supset\tau}K_{p}(\pi_{\rho\eta}({\mathcal{E}}(\sigma)))\subset{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{E}}(\tau),

where the sum runs through facets of
{\mathcal{X}}
.
If
\tau^{\prime}\subset\tau
, the cosheaf maps
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}(\tau)\to{\mathcal{K}}_{p}(\tau^{\prime})

are the restrictions of the maps
i_{\tau\tau^{\prime}}\colon{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{E}}(\tau)\to{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{E}}(\tau^{\prime}).

The cosheaves
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}
give rise to cosheaves on
X=|{\mathcal{X}}|
which we denote by the same letter.

Remark 5.6
.

Note that, with the assumption from above,

{\mathcal{E}}(\tau)=\bigcup_{\sigma\supset\tau}\pi_{\rho\eta}({\mathcal{E}}(\sigma)),

where the union runs through facets of
{\mathcal{X}}
. This explains the inclusion
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}\subset{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{E}}
mentioned in the
above definition. In fact, the cosheaves
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}
form a filtration of
{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{E}}
given by

(5.6)

{\mathcal{K}}_{d}\subset\dots\subset{\mathcal{K}}_{2}\subset{\mathcal{K}}_{1}\subset{\mathcal{K}}_{0}={\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{E}}.

Indeed, any affine subspace
G
of dimension
p
can be written as disjoint union
G=H_{1}\sqcup H_{2}
of two
affine subpaces of dimension
p-1
. Then
w_{G}=w_{H_{1}}+w_{H_{2}}
, which shows
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}\subset{\mathcal{K}}_{p-1}
.

Assume that
H\subset T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}({\mathbb{T}}\Sigma_{\rho})
is a affine subspace.
Then by general linear algebra we have

(5.7)

K_{p}(H)/K_{p+1}(H)\cong\bigwedge^{p}T(H),

induced by mapping
w_{G}
to
v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{p}
.
Here
G\in\text{Aff}_{p}(M)
and
v_{1},\dots,v_{p}
form a basis of
T(G)
.
Furthermore, note that
{\mathcal{F}}_{p}
can be written in the form

{\mathcal{F}}_{p}(\tau)=\sum_{\sigma\supset\tau}\bigwedge^{p}\pi_{\rho\eta}(T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}(\sigma))

analogous to (
5.5
).
Since

\pi_{\rho\eta}(T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}(\sigma))=\pi_{\rho\eta}(T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}({\mathcal{E}}(\sigma)))=T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}(\pi_{\rho\eta}({\mathcal{E}}(\sigma))),

the restriction of the map
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}(T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}({\mathbb{T}}\Sigma_{\rho}))\to\bigwedge^{p}T_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}({\mathbb{T}}\Sigma_{\rho})
from (
5.7
)
to
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}(\tau)
takes image in
{\mathcal{F}}_{p}(\tau)
. Hence, it gives rise to a morphism of cellular cosheaves
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}\to{\mathcal{F}}_{p}
.
Applying (
5.7
) to each summand, we see that this morphism is surjective and
{\mathcal{K}}_{p+1}
lies in the kernel.
Since the sum is not a direct sum, in general, we cannot conclude that
{\mathcal{K}}_{p+1}
is
equal
to the kernel.
Equality holds, however, if
{\mathcal{X}}
represents a non-singular tropical variety. This is the content of the following proposition.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
X
be a non-singular tropical variety in
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
with a real phase structure
{\mathcal{E}}
.
Then for all
p
there is an exact sequence of cosheaves

0\to{\mathcal{K}}_{p+1}\to{\mathcal{K}}_{p}\to{\mathcal{F}}_{p}\to 0.

Proof.

The proof follows the same lines as the proof of Proposition 4.10 in
[
RS23
]
.
As mentioned before, it is true in general that
{\mathcal{K}}_{p+1}\to{\mathcal{K}}_{p}
(the inclusion map) is injective,

{\mathcal{K}}_{p}\to{\mathcal{F}}_{p}
is surjective, and
{\mathcal{K}}_{p+1}\subset\ker({\mathcal{K}}_{p}\to{\mathcal{F}}_{p})
.
We prove equality in the last statement by a comparison of dimensions.
More precisely, let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a polyhedral complex such that
X=|{\mathcal{X}}|
and pick
\tau\in{\mathcal{X}}
.
It suffices to show that

\sum_{p=0}^{\dim X}\dim{\mathcal{F}}_{p}(\tau)=\dim{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{E}}(\tau).

This statement is a local statement and so by
Definition
3.1
we may assume
{\mathcal{X}}=\overline{{\mathbb{P}}\Sigma}_{M}
for some matroid
M
.
By
[
RRS22
, Theorem 1.1]
, there is an oriented matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
representing the real phase structure
on
{\mathcal{X}}
.

The dimension of
S_{\mathcal{E}}(\tau)
is equal to
|\cup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{E}(\sigma)|
and so
2\dim S_{\mathcal{E}}(\tau)
is equal to the number of topes of the oriented matroid of
M
. By

[
Zas75
]
, the number of topes of
\mathcal{M}
is equal to
(-1)^{r}\chi_{M}(-1)
, where
\chi_{M}
is its characteristic polynomial of the underlying matroid
M
of
\mathcal{M}
, see also

[
BLVS+99
, Theorem 4.6.1]
. Thus
\dim S_{\mathcal{E}}(\tau)=(-1)^{r}\tilde{\chi}_{M}(-1)
where
\tilde{\chi}_{M}(t)
is the reduced characteristic polynomial of
M
.
Finally, by
[
Zha13
]
, we obtain

(-1)^{r}\tilde{\chi}_{M}(-1)=\sum_{0\leq p\leq r}\dim{\mathcal{F}}_{p}(\Sigma_{M}),

and the proof follows.
∎

5.5.
The spectral sequence

We now establish the spectral sequence relating the homology of the patchwork of a tropical manifold to the tropical homology groups.

We denote the complex of relative chains by

C_{\bullet}^{\diamond}({\mathcal{X}};{\mathcal{K}}_{p},{\mathcal{K}}_{p+1}):=C_{\bullet}^{\diamond}({\mathcal{X}};{\mathcal{K}}_{p})/C_{\bullet}^{\diamond}({\mathcal{X}};{\mathcal{K}}_{p+1})

where the
\diamond
denotes either normal or Borel-Moore homology.
This is the
0
-th page of the spectral sequence for the chain complex
C^{\diamond}_{\bullet}({\mathcal{X}};{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{E}})
obtained by the filtration by the
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}
cellular cosheaves.
Thanks to Proposition
5.7
, we obtain the following statement,
analogous to
[
RS23
, Proposition 4.12]
.

Corollary 5.8
.

Let
X
be a non-singular tropical variety in
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
with a real phase structure
{\mathcal{E}}
, and
suppose
{\mathcal{X}}
be a polyhedral complex such that
X=|{\mathcal{X}}|
.
The first page of the spectral sequence associated to the filtration of the chain complex
C_{\bullet}^{\diamond}({\mathcal{X}};{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{E}})
by the chain complexes
C_{\bullet}^{\diamond}({\mathcal{X}};{\mathcal{K}}_{p})
has terms

E^{1,\diamond}_{q,p}\cong H_{q}^{\diamond}(X;{\mathcal{F}}_{p}).

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

The pages of a spectral sequence satisfy

\dim E^{\infty}_{q,p}\leq\dim E^{r}_{q,p}
for all
r
.
By
5.8
,
and the convergence of the spectral sequence associated to the filtration we obtain

\dim H_{q}^{\diamond}(X;{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{E}})=\sum_{p=0}^{\dim X}E^{\infty,\diamond}_{q,p}\leq\sum_{p=0}^{\dim X}E^{1,\diamond}_{q,p}=\sum_{p=0}^{\dim X}H_{q}^{\diamond}(X;\mathcal{F}_{p}).

The first part of the theorem now follows since

\dim H_{q}^{\diamond}(\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}});{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})=\dim H_{q}^{\diamond}(X;{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{E}})
by
Proposition

5.4
.

For the relation between the tropical signature and the Euler characteristic of
\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}})
, using again

Proposition

5.4
we have
\chi^{\diamond}(\mathfrak{R}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{E}}))=\chi^{\diamond}({\mathcal{S}}_{{\mathcal{E}}})
, and moreover,

\chi^{\diamond}({\mathcal{S}}_{{\mathcal{E}}})=\sum_{p,q}(-1)^{q}E^{\infty,\diamond}_{q,p}=\sum_{p,q}(-1)^{q}E^{1,\diamond}_{q,p}=\sum_{p}\chi^{\diamond}({\mathcal{F}}_{p})=\sigma^{\diamond}(X).

This completes the proof.
∎

5.6.
Hirzebruch polynomials

We conclude by proving that in the context of
Section

4
, the Hirzebruch genus
can be computed from the tropical limit.
Recall that the Hirzebruch genus of
\overline{{\mathbf{X}}}_{t}
is

(5.8)

\displaystyle\chi_{y}(\overline{{\mathbf{X}}}_{t})=\sum_{p,q}e_{c}^{p,q}(\overline{{\mathbf{X}}}_{t})y^{p},

where
e_{c}^{p,q}(\overline{{\mathbf{X}}}_{t})=\sum_{k}(-1)^{k}h^{p,q}(H_{c}^{k}({\mathbf{X}}_{t}))

is defined as in the introduction.

Given a tropical non-singular subvariety
X\subset{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma

of dimension
d
, we define
its
tropical Hirzebruch genus
as

\chi_{y}(X):=\sum_{p,q=0}^{d}(-1)^{p+q}\dim H_{q}^{\text{BM}}(X,{\mathcal{F}}_{p})y^{p}.

For the reader familiar with the cohomology versions of tropical
(p,q)
-groups, we note that
(using the universal coefficient theorem for the field
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
)

\chi_{y}(X)=\sum_{p=0}^{d}(-1)^{p+q}\dim H_{c}^{q}({\mathcal{X}};{\mathcal{F}}^{p})y^{p},

in accordance with the classical definition (
5.8
).

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
{\mathbf{X}}
be a analytic family over
{\mathcal{D}}^{*}

with tropical limit
X=\Trop({\mathbf{X}})
.
Let
\Sigma
be a pointed unimodular fan
and consider the closures
\overline{{\mathbf{X}}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}\Sigma\times{\mathcal{D}}^{*}
and

\overline{X}\subset{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
. Assume that
\overline{X}
is non-singular.
Then for generic

t\in{\mathcal{D}}^{*}
,
we have

\chi_{y}(\overline{{\mathbf{X}}}_{t})=\chi_{y}(\overline{X}).

If
{\mathbb{C}}\Sigma={{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}}^{n}
, the statement follows from the
stronger equality of classical and tropical Hodge numbers
proven in
[
IKMZ19
]
. Proposition
5.9
was already proven in the case of hypersurfaces, for any
\Sigma
, in
[
ARS19
]
.

Proof.

Given
\rho\in\Sigma
, we set
{\mathbf{X}}^{\rho}:=\overline{{\mathbf{X}}}\cap({\mathbb{C}}{\mathcal{O}}_{\rho}\times{\mathcal{D}}^{*})

and
X^{\rho}:=\overline{X}\cap{\mathbb{T}}{\mathcal{O}}_{\rho}
. Note that
\Trop({\mathbf{X}}^{\rho})=X^{\rho}
.
We first claim that both sides of the equation are motivic in the sense
that

\displaystyle\chi_{y}(\overline{{\mathbf{X}}}_{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\rho\in\Sigma}\chi_{y}({\mathbf{X}}^{\rho}_{t}),

\displaystyle\chi_{y}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\rho\in\Sigma}\chi_{y}(X^{\rho}).

The first equation follows from the fact that the Hirzebruch genus is motivic.
On the tropical side, it is just a simple computation.
If
\overline{{\mathcal{X}}}
is a regular subdivision
of
\overline{X}
we get

\displaystyle\chi_{y}(\overline{X})

\displaystyle=\sum_{p,q}(-1)^{p+q}\dim H_{q}^{\text{BM}}(X,{\mathcal{F}}_{p})y^{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\overline{{\mathcal{X}}}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{p+\dim\sigma}\dim{\mathcal{F}}_{p}(\sigma)y^{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\rho\in\Sigma}\sum_{p}\sum_{\text{Sed}\sigma=\rho}(-1)^{p+\dim\sigma}\dim{\mathcal{F}}_{p}(\sigma)y^{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\rho\in\Sigma}\chi_{y}(X^{\rho}).

It hence suffices to consider the case
\Sigma=\{0\}
.
By Lemma
4.8
, we can choose a strictly unimodular subdivision
{\mathcal{P}}
of

{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that
{\mathcal{X}}=\{\sigma\in{\mathcal{P}}:\sigma\subset X\}

is a subdivision of
X
.
We can now apply
[
KS16
, Corollary 1.4]
, which proves that for generic
t

\chi_{y}({\mathbf{X}}_{t})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in{\mathcal{X}}\\
\sigma\text{ bounded}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\dim\sigma}\chi_{y}(\init_{\sigma}{\mathbf{X}}_{t}).

Again, we show that the analogous statement holds tropically.
To do so, we denote by
S(\sigma)
the
non-reduced
star of

X
at
\sigma
. That is,
S(\sigma)
is the
d
-dimensional fan in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

such that
S(\sigma)/T(\sigma)=\Star_{\sigma}X
. Applying Poincaré duality
[
JRS18
]
we obtain that

\displaystyle\chi_{y}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p,q}(-1)^{p+q}\dim H_{q}^{\text{BM}}(X,{\mathcal{F}}_{p})y^{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p,q}(-1)^{p+q}\dim H_{d-q}(X,{\mathcal{F}}_{d-p})y^{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in{\mathcal{X}}\\
\sigma\text{ bounded}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{p+d-\dim\sigma}\dim{\mathcal{F}}_{d-p}(\sigma)y^{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in{\mathcal{X}}\\
\sigma\text{ bounded}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\dim\sigma}\sum_{p}(-1)^{p+d}\dim H_{0}(S(\sigma),{\mathcal{F}}_{d-p})y^{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in{\mathcal{X}}\\
\sigma\text{ bounded}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\dim\sigma}\sum_{p}(-1)^{p+d}\dim H_{d}^{\text{BM}}(S(\sigma),{\mathcal{F}}_{p})y^{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in{\mathcal{X}}\\
\sigma\text{ bounded}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\dim\sigma}\chi_{y}(S(\sigma)).

Note that
\Trop(\init_{\sigma}{\mathbf{X}})=S(\sigma)
, so we have reduced to the case
where
{\mathbf{X}}=\textbf{L}
is linear. Let
M=M_{\textbf{L}}
be the associated matroid and let

\overline{\mu}_{M}(y)
denote its reduced characteristic polynomial. By
[
KS12
, Lemma 7.5]
, the Hirzebruch genus of
L
is equal to the reduced characteristic polynomial of
M
. Moreover, by
[
Zha13
]
and
[
OT92
]
, the dimension of
{\mathcal{F}}_{p}({\mathbb{P}}\Sigma_{M})
is the
(d-p)
-th coefficient of
\overline{\mu}_{M}(y)
.
Then we get

\displaystyle\chi_{y}(\textbf{L})

\displaystyle=\overline{\mu}_{M}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}(-1)^{d-p}\dim{\mathcal{F}}_{d-p}({\mathbb{P}}\Sigma_{M})y^{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}(-1)^{p+d}\dim H_{d}^{\text{BM}}({\mathbb{P}}\Sigma_{M},{\mathcal{F}}_{p})y^{p}

\displaystyle=\chi_{y}({\mathbb{P}}\Sigma_{M}).

This proves the claim.
∎

Proof of
Corollary

1.5
.

Evaluating the polynomials from
5.9
in
y=-1
, one obtains

\sigma_{c}(\overline{{\mathbf{X}}}_{t})=\sigma^{\text{BM}}(\overline{X}).

The latter is equal to
\chi^{\text{BM}}({\mathbb{R}}\overline{{\mathbf{X}}}_{t})
by combining Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
.
