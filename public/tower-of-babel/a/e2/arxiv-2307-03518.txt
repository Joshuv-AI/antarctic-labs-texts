A Multivariate Complexity Analysis of the Generalized Noah’s Ark Problem
Christian Komusiewicz [0000-0003-0829-7032] and Jannik Schestag [0000-0001-7767-2970] ,

Abstract
In the Generalized Noah’s Ark Problem , one is given a phylogenetic tree on a set of species X X and a set of conservation projects for each species. Each project comes with a cost and raises the survival probability of the corresponding species. The aim is to select for each species a conservation project such that the total cost of the selected projects does not exceed some given threshold and the expected phylogenetic diversity is as large as possible. We study Generalized Noah’s Ark Problem and some of its special cases with respect to several parameters related to the input structure such as the number of different costs, the number of different survival probabilities, or the number of species, | X | \left|X\right| .



1
Introduction

The preservation of biological diversity is one of humanity’s most critical challenges. To help systematically addressing this challenge, it is useful to quantify or predict the effect of interventions. Here, two questions arise: how to measure the biological diversity of ecosystems and how to model the effect of certain actions on the biological diversity of an ecosystem under consideration.

A popular way to measure the biological diversity of an ecosystem, introduced by Faith
[
1
]
, is to consider the
phylogenetic diversity
of the species present in that system. Here, the phylogenetic diversity is the sum of evolutionary distances between the species, when their evolution is modeled by an evolutionary (phylogenetic) tree. The tree then not only gives the phylogenetic diversity of the whole species set but also allows us to infer the phylogenetic diversity of any subset of these species that would remain after some currently present species are extinct. Now to model the effect of certain actions, a first simple model is that one can afford to protect
k
species and that all other species go extinct. Maximizing phylogenetic diversity under this model can be solved in polynomial time with a simple greedy algorithm
[
2
,
3
]
. Later, more realistic models were introduced. One step was to model that protecting some species may be more costly than protecting others
[
4
]
. Subsequent approaches also allowed us to model uncertainty as follows: performing an action to protect some species does not guarantee the survival of that species but only raises the survival probability
[
5
]
. In this model, one now aims to maximize the
expected
phylogenetic diversity. Finally, one may also consider the even more realistic case when for each species, one may choose from a set of different actions or even from combinations of different actions. Each choice is then associated with a cost and with a resulting survival probability. This model was proposed by Billionnet
[
6
,
7
]
as
Generalized Noah’s Ark Problem
(
GNAP
).

Introducing cost differences for species protection makes the problem of maximizing phylogenetic diversity NP-hard
[
4
]
and thus all of the even richer models are NP-hard as well. Apart from the NP-hardness and several pseudopolynomial-time algorithms, there is no work that systematically studies which structural properties of the input make the problems tractable. This work aims to fill this gap. More precisely, we study how different parameters related to the input structure influence the algorithmic complexity of
GNAP
and of several of its special cases.

In a nutshell, we show the following. First,
GNAP
can be solved more efficiently when the number of different project costs and survival probabilities is small. Second, while a constant number
\left|X\right|
of species
X
leads to polynomial-time solvability, algorithms with running time
f(|X|)\cdot|\mathcal{I}|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
are unlikely to exist. Finally, restricted cases where for example the input tree has height 1 or there is exactly one action for each species which guarantees its survival are much easier than the general problem. We also observe close relations to the
Multiple-Choice Knapsack
problem and to a further natural number problem which we call
Penalty-Sum
.

, where

where each vertex of

A vertex

is a

is one.
In a rooted tree, the

r

.
The

T
.
For an edge
(u,v)
, we call

the parent of
v
and

a child of
u
.
For a vertex
v
with parent

, the

rooted at

is the connected component containing
v
in
T-(u,v)
. In the special case that
v
is the root of
T
, we define
T_{v}:=T
.
For a vertex

with children
w_{1},\dots,w_{t}
and

, the

rooted at

.

.

2.2
Phylogenetic Diversity

A phylogenetic
X
-tree
\mathcal{T}=(V,E,\lambda)
(in short,
X
-tree) is a tree
T
with root
r
, where
\lambda:E\to\mathbb{N}
is an edge-weight function and
X
is the set of leaves of
T
. In biological applications, the internal vertices of
T
correspond to hypothetical ancestors of the leaves and
\lambda(e)
describes the evolutionary distance between the endpoints of
e
.
An
X
-tree
\mathcal{T}
is
ultrametric
if there is an integer
p
such that the sum of the weights of the edges from the root
r
to
x_{i}
equals
p
for every leaf
x_{i}
.

A
project

p_{i,j}
is a tuple
(c_{i,j},w_{i,j})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\times\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]
, where
c_{i,j}
is the
cost
and
w_{i,j}
the
survival probability
of
p_{i,j}
.
For a given
X
-tree
\mathcal{T}
and a taxon
x_{i}\in X
, a
project list

P_{i}
is a tuple
(p_{i,1},\dots,p_{i,\ell_{i}})
. As a project with higher cost will only be considered when the survival probability is higher, we assume the costs and survival probabilities to be ordered. That is,
c_{i,j}<c_{i,j+1}
and
w_{i,j}<w_{i,j+1}
for every project list
P_{i}
and every
j<\ell_{i}
.
An
m
-collection of projects

\mathcal{P}
is a set of
m
project lists
\{P_{1},\dots,P_{m}\}
.
For a project set
S
, the
total cost
\Costs(S)
of
S
is
\sum_{p_{i,j}\in S}c_{i,j}
.

For a given
X
-tree
\mathcal{T}
, the
phylogenetic diversity
PD_{\mathcal{T}}(S)
of a set of projects
S=\{p_{1,j_{1}},\dots,p_{|X|,j_{|X|}}\}
is given by

PD_{\mathcal{T}}(S):=\sum_{(u,v)\in E}\lambda((u,v))\cdot\left(1-\prod_{x_{i}\in\off(v)}(1-w_{i,j_{i}})\right).

The formula
\left(1-\prod_{x_{i}\in\off(v)}(1-w_{i,j_{i}})\right)
describes the likelihood that at least one offspring of
v
survives. Thus, the phylogenetic diversity is the sum of the expected values of the edges of
\mathcal{T}
when applying
S
.

To assess the influence of structural properties of the input on the problem complexity, we study the problems in the framework of parameterized complexity.
For a detailed introduction to parameterized complexity refer to the standard monographs
[
8
,
9
]
. We only recall the most important definitions: A parameterized problem with parameter
k
is
fixed-parameter tractable (
\mathrm{FPT}
)
if every instance
(\mathcal{I},k)
can be solved in
f(k)\cdot|\mathcal{I}|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
time. A parameterized problem is
slicewice-polynomial (
\mathrm{XP}
)
if every instance can be solved in
|\mathcal{I}|^{g(k)}
time. It is widely assumed that problems that are hard for
\mathrm{W[1]}
have no FPT-algorithm.

In our running time analyses, we assume a unit-cost RAM model where arithmetic operations have constant running time.

2.3
Problem Definitions, Parameters, and Results Overview

We now define our main problem,
Generalized Noah’s Ark Problem
, and the special case where each species has two projects,
a_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c_{i}}}{{\to}}b_{i}\>[2]
-
Noah’s Ark Problem
.

Generalized Noah’s Ark Problem (GNAP)

Input
: An
X
-tree
\mathcal{T}=(V,E,\lambda)
,
an
\left|X\right|
-collection of projects
\mathcal{P}
,
and numbers
B\in\mathbb{N}_{0},D\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
.

Question
: Is there a set of projects
S=\{p_{1,j_{1}},\dots,p_{|X|,j_{|X|}}\}
, one from each project list of
\mathcal{P}
,
such that
PD_{\mathcal{T}}(S)\geq D
and
\Costs(S)\leq B
?

A project set
S
is called
a solution for the instance
\mathcal{I}=(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{P},B,D)
.

a_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c_{i}}}{{\to}}b_{i}\>[2]
-Noah’s Ark Problem (
a_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c_{i}}}{{\to}}b_{i}\>[2]
-NAP)

Input
: An
X
-tree
\mathcal{T}=(V,E,\lambda)
,
a
\left|X\right|
-collection of projects
\mathcal{P}
in which the project list
P_{i}
contains exactly two projects
(c_{i,1},a_{i})
and
(c_{i,2},b_{i})
for each
i\in[\left|X\right|]
,
and integers
B\in\mathbb{N}_{0},D\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
.

Question
: Is there a set of projects
S=\{p_{1,j_{1}},\dots,p_{|X|,j_{|X|}}\}
, one from each project list of
\mathcal{P}
,
such that
PD_{\mathcal{T}}(S)\geq D
, and
\Costs(S)\leq B
?

In other words, in an instance
a_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c_{i}}}{{\to}}b_{i}\>[2]
-
NAP
we can decide for each taxon
x_{i}
whether we want to spend
c_{i}
to increase the survival probability of
x_{i}
from
a_{i}
to
b_{i}
.
If
P_{i}
contains two projects
(\bar{c},a_{i})
and
(\hat{c},b_{i})
with
\bar{c}>0
for an

, we can reduce the cost of the two projects and the budget by
\bar{c}
each to receive an equivalent instance.
Therefore, we will assume that the project with the lower survival probability has cost 0 and we refer to the cost of the other project as
c_{i}
.

As a special case of this problem, we consider
0\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c}}{{\to}}b_{i}\>[2]
-
NAP
where every project with a positive survival probability has cost
c
.
Observe that for each
c\in\mathbb{N}
, an instance
\mathcal{I}=(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{P},B,D)
of
0\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c}}{{\to}}b_{i}\>[2]
-
NAP
can be reduced to an equivalent instance
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}=(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{P}^{\prime},B^{\prime},D)
of
0\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\to}}b_{i}\>[2]
-
NAP
by replacing each project
(c,b_{i})
with
(1,b_{i})
, and setting

. Thus,
0\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c}}{{\to}}b_{i}\>[2]
-
NAP
can be considered as the special case of

with unit costs for projects.
In the following we refer to this problem as

.

We study
GNAP
with respect to several parameters which we describe in the following; for an overview of the results see Table
1
. If not stated differently, we assume in the following that
i\in[\left|X\right|]
and
j\in[|P_{i}|]
.
The input of
GNAP
directly gives the natural parameters

|X|
,
budget
B
, and required

. Closely related to

is the

. We may assume that no projects have a cost that exceeds the budget, as we can delete them from the input and so

. We may further assume that

and return yes, if it does and no, otherwise.

Further, we consider the

. By definition,

in
a_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c_{i}}}{{\to}}b_{i}\>[2]
-
NAP

.
We denote the

by
\left\lVert\mathcal{P}\right\rVert=\sum_{i}|P_{i}|
. Clearly,
\left|X\right|\leq\left\lVert\mathcal{P}\right\rVert
,

, and
\left\lVert\mathcal{P}\right\rVert\leq\left|X\right|\cdot L

By
\var_{c}
, we denote the
number of different costs
, that is,
\var_{c}:=|\{c_{i,j}:(c_{i,j},w_{i,j})\in P_{i},P_{i}\in\mathcal{P}\}|
. We define the
number of different survival probabilities

analogously. The consideration of this type of parameterization, called

[
10
]
; it is motivated by the idea that in many real-life instances the number of numbers may be small. In fact, for nonnegative numbers, it is never larger than the maximum values which are used in pseudo-polynomial time algorithms.
Also, we consider the
maximum encoding length for survival probabilities
\wcode=\max_{i,j}(\text{binary length of }w_{i,j})
and the
maximum edge weight
\val_{\lambda}=\max_{e\in E}\lambda(e)

2.4
Observations for
GNAP

We first present some basic observations that provide some first complexity classifications.
In the problem with exactly two projects per taxa,
a_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c_{i}}}{{\to}}b_{i}\>[2]
-
NAP
, one can iterate over all subsets
X^{\prime}
of taxa and check if it is a possible solution pay
c_{i}
to increase the survival probability for each
x_{i}\in X^{\prime}
.
To this end, we check if
\sum_{x_{i}\in X^{\prime}}c_{i}\leq B
and compute if the phylogenetic diversity is at least
D
, when the survival probability of every
x_{i}\in X^{\prime}
is
b_{i}
and
a_{i}
otherwise. Thus,
a_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c_{i}}}{{\to}}b_{i}\>[2]
-
NAP
is fixed-parameter tractable with respect to the number of taxa.

Observation 1
.

a_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c_{i}}}{{\to}}b_{i}\>[2]
-
NAP
can be solved in
2^{\left|X\right|}\cdot|\mathcal{I}|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
time.

A
GNAP
solution contains at most
B
projects with positive costs.
Hence, a solution can be found by iterating over all
B
-sized subsets
X^{\prime}
of taxa and checking every combination of projects for
X^{\prime}
. Like before, we have to check that the budget is not exceeded and the phylogenetic diversity of the selected projects is at least
D
.
This brute-force algorithm shows that
GNAP
is
\mathrm{XP}
with respect to the budget.

Observation 2
.

GNAP
can be solved in
(|X|\cdot L)^{B}\cdot|\mathcal{I}|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
time.

In the NP-hard
Knapsack
problem, one is given a set of items
N
, a cost-function
c:N\to\mathbb{N}
, a value-function
d:N\to\mathbb{N}
, and two integers
B
and
D
and asks whether there is an item set
N^{\prime}
such that
c(N^{\prime})\leq B
and
d(N^{\prime})\geq D
.
We describe briefly a known reduction from
Knapsack
to
0\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c_{i}}}{{\to}}1\>[2]
-
NAP

[
4
]
.
Let
\mathcal{I}=(N,c,d,B,D)
be an instance of
Knapsack
.
Define
\mathcal{T}:=(V,E,\lambda)
to be an
N
-tree with
V:=\{w\}\cup N
and
E:=\{(w,x_{i})\mid x_{i}\in N\}
and
\lambda((w,x_{i})):=d(x_{i})
.
For each leaf
x_{i}
we define a project list
P_{i}
that contains two projects
(0,0)
and
(c(x_{i}),1)
.
Then,
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}:=(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{P},B^{\prime}:=B,D^{\prime}:=D)
is a yes-instance of
0\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c_{i}}}{{\to}}1\>[2]
-
NAP
if and only if
\mathcal{I}
is a yes-instance of
Knapsack
.

Observation 3

(
[
4
]
)
.

0\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c_{i}}}{{\to}}1\>[2]
-
NAP
is
\mathrm{NP}
-hard, even if the tree
\mathcal{T}
has height 1.

Because
0\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c_{i}}}{{\to}}1\>[2]
-

is a special case of

in which

, and

, we conclude that

is NP-hard, even if

and

.
In this reduction, one could also set

and set the survival probability of every project with positive cost to
\nicefrac{{1}}{{D}}
.

Observation 4
.

0\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c_{i}}}{{\to}}b\>[2]
-

is
\mathrm{NP}
-hard, even if

,

is a constant, and the given

Thus,

is
\mathrm{NP}
-hard even if

and

. This however is, because the size of the binary encoding of an survival probability became very large.
Thus, one can wonder if

.
In Proposition

is fixed-parameter tractable with respect to

.

3
Multiple-Choice Knapsack

In this section, we consider a variant of
Knapsack
, in which the set of items is divided into classes. From every class, exactly one item can be chosen.

Multiple-Choice Knapsack Problem (MCKP)

Input
: A set of items
N=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
,
a partition
\{N_{1},\dots,N_{m}\}
of
N
,
two functions
c,d:N\to\mathbb{N}
,
and two integers
B,D
.

Question
: Is there a set
S\subseteq N
such that

|S\cap N_{i}|=1
for each
i\in[m]
,

c_{\Sigma}(S)\leq B
, and
d_{\Sigma}(S)\geq D
?

Herein, we write
c_{\Sigma}(A):=\sum_{a_{i}\in A}c(a_{i})
and
d_{\Sigma}(A):=\sum_{a_{i}\in A}d(a_{i})
for a set
A\subseteq N
.
We call
c(a_{i})
the
cost of
a_{i}
and
d(a_{i})
the
value of
a_{i}
.
Further, for a set
A\subseteq N
we define
c(A):=\{c(a)\mid a\in A\}
and
d(A):=\{d(a)\mid a\in A\}
.
A set
S
that fulfills the presented criteria is called a
solution
for the instance
\mathcal{I}
.

For
MCKP
, we consider parameters that are closely related to the parameters described for
GNAP
: The input directly gives the
number of classes
m
, the
budget
B
, and the desired
value
D
.
Closely related to
B
is the
maximum cost for an item
C=\max_{a_{j}\in N}c(a_{j})
. Since projects whose cost exceeds the budget can be removed from the input, we may assume
C\leq B
. Further, we assume that
B\leq C\cdot m
, as otherwise, one can return yes if the total value of the most valuable items per class exceeds
D
, and no otherwise.
By
\var_{c}
, we denote the
number of different costs
, that is,
\var_{c}:=|\{c(a_{j})\penalty\ :\penalty\ a_{j}\in N\}|
. We define the
number of different values

\var_{d}
analogously.
The size of the biggest class is denoted by
L
. If one class
N_{i}
contains two items
a_{p}
and
a_{q}
with the same cost and
d(a_{p})\leq d(a_{p})
, the item
a_{p}
can be removed from the instance. Thus, we may assume that no class contains two items with the same cost and so

We show that an instance

\Rightarrow
)

-respecting set.

For a leaf

with parent

, and

.
Further, we define

.

To compute the value of

, we use the recurrence

\displaystyle G[v,i+1,d]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{d^{\prime}\in[d]_{0}}\{G_{i}[v,d-d^{\prime}]+F[u_{i+1},d^{\prime}]\}.

(40)

Once we have computed

for a vertex

with

children, we set
F[v,d]=G[v,t,d]
.

Finally, we return yes if
F[r,D]\leq B
for the root
r
of
\mathcal{T}
. Otherwise, we return no.

Correctness.

The initialization of

for a leaf
x_{i}
, the computation of
G[v,1,d]
, and the computation of
F[v,d]
for an internal vertex
v
and a value

are correct by definition.
It remains to show that
G[v,i+1,d]
stores the correct value if
G[v,i,d]
and
F[u_{i+1},d]
do. To this end, we first show that if
S
is an
(\{u_{1},\dots,u_{i+1}\},d)
-respecting set, then
G[v,i+1,d]\leq\Costs(S)
. Afterwards, we show that if
G[v,i+1,d]=c
, then there is an
(\{u_{1},\dots,u_{i+1}\},d)
-respecting set
S
with
\Costs(S)=c
.

(
\Rightarrow
)
Let
S
be an
(\{u_{1},\dots,u_{i+1}\},d)
-respecting set.
Let
S_{F}
be the set of projects that are in one of the project lists of the offspring of
u_{i+1}
.
Define
S_{G}:=S\setminus S_{F}
.
Let

.
Then,
PD_{\mathcal{T}_{v}}(S_{G})=PD_{\mathcal{T}_{v}}(S)-PD_{\mathcal{T}_{v}}(S_{F})=d-d^{\prime}
.
We conclude that
S_{F}
is an
(\{u_{i+1}\},d^{\prime})
-respecting set and

is an
(\{u_{1},\dots,u_{i}\},d-d^{\prime})
-respecting set.
Consequently,

\displaystyle G[v,i+1,d]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle G_{i}[v,d-d^{\prime}]+F[u_{i+1},d^{\prime}]

(41)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Costs(S_{G})+\Costs(S_{F})=\Costs(S).

(42)

Here, Inequality (
41
) follows from the recurrence in Equation (

) and Inequality (
42
) follows from the induction hypothesis.

(
\Leftarrow
)
Let
G[v,i+1,d]
store
c
.
Then there is an integer

, such that

.
By the induction hypothesis, there is an
(\{u_{i+1}\},d^{\prime})
-respecting set
S_{F}
and an

-respecting set

Then,

is an

-respecting set and

.

Running time.

The table

has

For each edge

∎

Unit costs for each project

Second, we consider

to

in which the
X
-tree has a height of 2 and
\deg(r)=1
for the root

Theorem 7

is NP-hard if and only if

restricted to
X
-trees with height 2 and a root

of degree 1 is NP-hard.

It directly follows that

6
Discussion

We have provided several tractability and intractability results for
GNAP
and some of its special cases. Naturally, several open questions remain. For example, it is not known whether
GNAP
is weakly or strongly NP-hard. Moreover, it remains open whether
GNAP
is
\mathrm{FPT}
with respect to
\var_{c}+\var_{w}
. Finally, as described above, it is open whether
unit-cost-NAP
and
Penalty-Sum
are NP-hard.
