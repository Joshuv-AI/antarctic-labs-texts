Minimaxity under the half-Cauchy prior
Yuzo Maruyama ??,??, Takeru Matsuda ??,??

Abstract
This is a follow-up paper of Polson and Scott (2012, Bayesian Analysis), which claimed that the half-Cauchy prior is a sensible default prior for a scale parameter in hierarchical models. For estimation of a p p -variate normal mean under the quadratic loss, they demonstrated that the Bayes estimator with respect to the half-Cauchy prior seems to be minimax through numerical experiments. In this paper, we theoretically establish the minimaxity of the corresponding Bayes estimator using the interval arithmetric.

1
Introduction

Consider a normal hierarchical model

y\mid\beta\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}(\beta,I_{p}),\quad\\
\beta\mid\kappa\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}\bigl(0,\frac{1-\kappa}{\kappa}I_{p}\bigr),\quad\kappa\sim\pi(\kappa),

(1.1)

where the hyperparameter
\kappa\in(0,1)
specifies the shrinkage coefficient of
posterior mean of
\beta
:

\hat{\beta}(y)={\rm E}[\beta\mid y]=(1-{\rm E}[\kappa\mid y])y.

(1.2)

Polson and Scott (2012)
claimed that the hyperprior

\pi(\kappa)\propto\kappa^{-1/2}(1-\kappa)^{-1/2},

(1.3)

is a sensible default choice.
Since it has a U-shape with

\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\pi(\kappa)=\lim_{\kappa\to 1}\pi(\kappa)=\infty,

(1.4)

it may be regarded as a continuous spike and slab prior.
See
Carvalho, Polson and
Scott (2010)
for a related discussion in the context of horseshoe priors.
For the parameterization

\lambda=\sqrt{\frac{1-\kappa}{\kappa}}\in(0,\infty),

(1.5)

the prior (
1.3
) is expressed as

\pi(\lambda)\propto\frac{1}{1+\lambda^{2}}I_{(0,\infty)}(\lambda),

(1.6)

which is the reason why the prior (
1.3
) is called the half-Cauchy prior.

For estimation of a
p
-variate normal mean
\beta
under the quadratic loss
\|\hat{\beta}-\beta\|^{2}
,
the posterior mean (
1.2
) is the minimizer of the corresponding Bayes risk and given by

\hat{\beta}=\left(1-\frac{\int_{0}^{1}\kappa^{p/2+1/2}(1-\kappa)^{-1/2}\exp(-\kappa\|y\|^{2}/2)\mathrm{d}\kappa}{\int_{0}^{1}\kappa^{p/2-1/2}(1-\kappa)^{-1/2}\exp(-\kappa\|y\|^{2}/2)\mathrm{d}\kappa}\right)y.

(1.7)

Polson and Scott (2012)
derived
expressions for the risk of the Bayes estimator (
1.7
).
Recall that the usual estimator
\hat{\beta}=y
is inadmissible for
p\geq 3
although it is minimax for any
p

(
Stein, 1956
;
Fourdrinier, Strawderman and
Wells, 2018
)
.
Through numerical experiments,
Polson and Scott (2012)
discussed the minimaxity of
the Bayes estimator (
1.7
) and compared it with the
James and Stein (1961)
estimator.

In this paper, we theoretically establish the minimaxity
of the Bayes estimator (
1.7
) for
p\geq 7
, as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

The Bayes estimator (
1.7
) under the half-Cauchy prior (
1.3
)
is minimax for
p\geq 7
.

In the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we employ the interval arithmetic
(
Moore, Kearfott and Cloud, 2009
)
, which has been used in the field of verified numerical computation.
See Appendix
E
for a brief review of the interval arithmetic.
To our knowledge, the interval arithmetic has not been common in the community of statisticians.

The organization of the paper is as follows.
We start with a more general prior

\pi(\kappa)=\kappa^{a-1}(1-\kappa)^{b-1}\text{ with }0<b<1.

(1.8)

Note, for
b\geq 1
,
the minimaxity of the corresponding (generalized) Bayes estimators has been well investigated
by
Berger (1976)
and
Faith (1978)
.

Maruyama (1998)
treated the case
0<b<1
and showed that
the corresponding Bayes estimators with
(p+2a+2)/(3p/2+a)\leq b<1
are minimax.
However, these results do not cover the case
a=b=1/2
(half-Cauchy prior) for any
p\geq 3
.
In Section
2
, we give the Bayes estimators under (
1.8
)
and their Stein’s unbiased risk estimates.
In Section
3
, we provide a sufficient condition for the Bayes estimators under (
1.8
)
to be minimax, which will be stated as Theorem
3.1
.
Then, in Section
4
, we focus on the half-Cauchy prior, which is a special case of (
1.8
) with
a=b=1/2
, and prove Theorem
1.1
.
Whereas the minimaxity of the half-Cauchy prior for
p\geq 11
directly follows from Theorem
3.1
, the case of
7\leq p\leq 10
requires additional investigation using the interval arithmetic.
Many of the proofs of technical lemmas are given in Appendix.
The python code for the interval arithmetic proof is available at
https://github.com/takeru-matsuda/half_cauchy
.

2
Bayes estimators and Stein’s unbiased risk estimate

By the identity

\|y-\beta\|^{2}+\frac{\kappa}{1-\kappa}\|\beta\|^{2}=\frac{1}{1-\kappa}\left\|\beta-(1-\kappa)y\right\|^{2}+\kappa\|y\|^{2},

(2.1)

the marginal density under the model (
1.1
) is

\displaystyle m(y)

\displaystyle=\iint\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{p/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|y-\beta\|^{2}}{2}\right)\pi(\beta\mid\kappa)\pi(\kappa)\mathrm{d}\beta\mathrm{d}\kappa

(2.2)

\displaystyle=\iint\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{p/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|y-\beta\|^{2}}{2}\right)\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{p/2}}\left(\frac{\kappa}{1-\kappa}\right)^{p/2}

\displaystyle\quad\times\exp\left(-\frac{\kappa}{1-\kappa}\frac{\|\beta\|^{2}}{2}\right)\kappa^{a-1}(1-\kappa)^{b-1}\mathrm{d}\beta\mathrm{d}\kappa

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{p/2}}\int\kappa^{p/2+a-1}(1-\kappa)^{b-1}\exp\left(-\kappa\frac{\|y\|^{2}}{2}\right)\mathrm{d}\kappa.

(2.3)

Note
\exp(-\kappa\|y\|^{2}/2)\leq 1
. Then, for

p/2+a>0\text{ and }b>0,

(2.4)

we have

m(y)\leq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{p/2}}\int_{0}^{1}\kappa^{p/2+a-1}(1-\kappa)^{b-1}\mathrm{d}\kappa=\frac{\mathrm{B}(p/2+a,b)}{(2\pi)^{p/2}}<\infty.

(2.5)

By Tweedie’s formula
(
Efron, 2011
;
Efron, 2023
)
,
the Bayes estimator is

\hat{\beta}=y+\nabla\log m(y)=\left(1-\frac{\int_{0}^{1}\kappa^{p/2+a}(1-\kappa)^{b-1}\exp(-\kappa\|y\|^{2}/2)\mathrm{d}\kappa}{\int_{0}^{1}\kappa^{p/2+a-1}(1-\kappa)^{b-1}\exp(-\kappa\|y\|^{2}/2)\mathrm{d}\kappa}\right)y.

(2.6)

Since the marginal density
m(y)
given by (
2.3
) is spherically symmetric,
let
m_{*}(\|y\|^{2})\coloneqq m(y)
.
Then, the quadratic risk of the Bayes estimator (
2.6
) is

\begin{split}&E\bigl[\|\hat{\beta}-\beta\|^{2}\bigr]=E\biggl[\Bigl\|Y-2\frac{m^{\prime}_{*}(\|Y\|^{2})}{m_{*}(\|Y\|^{2})}Y-\beta\Bigr\|^{2}\biggr]\\
&=E\biggl[\|Y-\beta\|^{2}+4\left(\frac{m^{\prime}_{*}(\|Y\|^{2})}{m_{*}(\|Y\|^{2})}\right)^{2}\|Y\|^{2}-4\sum_{i=1}^{p}(Y_{i}-\beta_{i})Y_{i}\frac{m^{\prime}_{*}(\|Y\|^{2})}{m_{*}(\|Y\|^{2})}\biggr]\\
&=E\left[p+4\frac{m^{\prime}_{*}(\|Y\|^{2})}{m_{*}(\|Y\|^{2})}\Bigl(p-2\|Y\|^{2}\frac{m^{\prime\prime}_{*}(\|Y\|^{2})}{-m^{\prime}_{*}(\|Y\|^{2})}+\|Y\|^{2}\frac{-m^{\prime}_{*}(\|Y\|^{2})}{m_{*}(\|Y\|^{2})}\Bigr)\right],\end{split}

(2.7)

where the third equality follows from
Stein (1974)
identity.
Let

\hat{R}(\|y\|^{2})=p+4\frac{m^{\prime}_{*}(\|y\|^{2})}{m_{*}(\|y\|^{2})}\Bigl(p-2\|y\|^{2}\frac{m^{\prime\prime}_{*}(\|y\|^{2})}{-m^{\prime}_{*}(\|y\|^{2})}+\|y\|^{2}\frac{-m^{\prime}_{*}(\|y\|^{2})}{m_{*}(\|y\|^{2})}\Bigr).

(2.8)

Then we have
E[\|\hat{\beta}-\beta\|^{2}]=E[\hat{R}(\|Y\|^{2})]
and

\hat{R}(\|y\|^{2})
is called the Stein’s unbiased risk estimate of
E[\|\hat{\beta}-\beta\|^{2}]
.
Recall
m(y)
is given by (
2.3
).
Then

\hat{R}(\|y\|^{2})=p-2\frac{\int_{0}^{1}\kappa^{p/2+a}(1-\kappa)^{b-1}\exp(-\kappa\|y\|^{2}/2)\mathrm{d}\kappa}{\int_{0}^{1}\kappa^{p/2+a-1}(1-\kappa)^{b-1}\exp(-\kappa\|y\|^{2}/2)\mathrm{d}\kappa}\Delta(\|y\|^{2}/2;a,b),

(2.9)

where

\begin{split}\Delta(w;a,b)&=p-2w\frac{\int_{0}^{1}\kappa^{p/2+a+1}(1-\kappa)^{b-1}\exp(-w\kappa)\mathrm{d}\kappa}{\int_{0}^{1}\kappa^{p/2+a}(1-\kappa)^{b-1}\exp(-w\kappa)\mathrm{d}\kappa}\\
&\quad+w\frac{\int_{0}^{1}\kappa^{p/2+a}(1-\kappa)^{b-1}\exp(-w\kappa)\mathrm{d}\kappa}{\int_{0}^{1}\kappa^{p/2+a-1}(1-\kappa)^{b-1}\exp(-w\kappa)\mathrm{d}\kappa}.\end{split}

(2.10)

In Lemma
2.1
below,
\Delta(w;a,b)
is represented
through the confluent hypergeometric function defined by

M(b,c,w)=1+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{b\cdots(b+i-1)}{c\cdots(c+i-1)}\frac{w^{i}}{i!}.

(2.11)

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
0<b<1
and
p/2+a>0
. Then

\begin{split}\Delta(w;a,b)&=\frac{p}{2}-a-2+2\Bigl(\frac{p}{2}+a+1\Bigr)\frac{M(b-1,p/2+a+b+1,w)}{M(b,p/2+a+b+1,w)}\\
&\quad-\Bigl(\frac{p}{2}+a\Bigr)\frac{M(b-1,p/2+a+b,w)}{M(b,p/2+a+b,w)}.\end{split}

(2.12)

Proof of Lemma
2.1
is given in Appendix
A
.

\displaystyle\Psi(b,q)=\frac{4}{3}\frac{b}{1-b}\frac{b+q+1}{b+q+2}-2\psi\bigl\{\zeta(b,q)\bigr\}-\Bigl[\psi\bigl\{\zeta(b,q)\bigr\}\Bigr]^{2},

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume
p\geq 3
,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle-\frac{p}{2}<a\leq-\frac{3}{2},&0<b<1,\\[9.0pt]
\displaystyle-\frac{3}{2}<a<\frac{p}{2}-2,&\displaystyle\frac{8a+12}{2a+3p}\leq b<1,\end{cases}

(3.6)

\displaystyle\text{ and }\ \Psi(b,p/2+a)\geq 0.

(3.7)

1.8
) is minimax.

Proof.

Let
q=p/2+a
in (
2.12
). Then

\begin{split}&\Delta(w;a,b)\\
&=\frac{p}{2}-a-2+2(q+1)\frac{M(b-1,b+q+1,w)}{M(b,b+q+1,w)}-q\frac{M(b-1,b+q,w)}{M(b,b+q,w)}.\end{split}

(3.8)

Recall
0<b<1
. Then

\begin{split}M(b-1,b+q+1,w)&\geq M(b-1,b+q,w)\\
\text{and}\quad M(b,b+q,w)&\geq M(b,b+q+1,w)\geq 0,\end{split}

(3.9)

for all
w\geq 0
.
Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{1}

\displaystyle=\{w:M(b-1,b+q,w)\geq 0\},

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{2}

\displaystyle=\{w:M(b-1,b+q+1,w)\geq 0>M(b-1,b+q,w)\},

(3.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{3}

\displaystyle=\{w:M(b-1,b+q,w)<M(b-1,b+q+1,w)<0\},

(3.12)

(3.13)

By (
3.9
), for

, we have

\displaystyle M(b-1,b+q+1,w)\geq 0,

(3.14)

\displaystyle\frac{M(b-1,b+q+1,w)}{M(b,b+q+1,w)}\geq\frac{M(b-1,b+q,w)}{M(b,b+q,w)}\geq 0,

(3.15)

and hence

\begin{split}\Delta(w;a,b)&\geq\frac{p}{2}-a-2+\left\{2(q+1)-q\right\}\frac{M(b-1,b+q,w)}{M(b,b+q,w)}\\
&\geq p/2-a-2>0,\end{split}

(3.16)

where the last inequality follows from (
3.6
).
For
w\in\mathcal{Q}_{2}
,
it is clear that

\Delta(w;a,b)\geq p/2-a-2>0,

(3.17)

where the inequality follows from (
3.6
).
By (
3.16
) and (
3.17
), the theorem follows
provided

\min_{w\in\mathcal{Q}_{3}}\Delta(w;a,b)\geq 0.

(3.18)

By the assumption (
3.7
), it follows from Lemma
3.1
below that

\min_{w\geq 0}\frac{M(b-1,b+q+1,w)}{M(b,b+q+1,w)}\geq-\frac{1-b}{b+2}.

(3.19)

Also note

(3.20)

for

2.12

3.19
)
and (

\begin{split}\min_{w\in\mathcal{Q}_{3}}\Delta(w;a,b)&\geq\frac{p}{2}-a-2+2\bigl(\frac{p}{2}+a+1\bigr)\min_{w\in\mathcal{Q}_{3}}\frac{M(b-1,b+q+1,w)}{M(b,b+q+1,w)}\\
&\geq\frac{p}{2}-a-2-(p+2a+2)\frac{1-b}{b+2}\\
&=\frac{b(3p+2a)-(8a+12)}{2(b+2)},\end{split}

(3.21)

which is nonnegative under (
3.6
). This completes the proof.

Proof of Lemma
2.1

Let
q=p/2+a
. Then the function
\Delta(w;a,b)
given by (

)
is re-expressed as

\begin{split}\Delta(w;a,b)&=p-2w\frac{\int_{0}^{1}\kappa^{q+1}(1-\kappa)^{b-1}\exp(w\{1-\kappa\})\mathrm{d}\kappa}{\int_{0}^{1}\kappa^{q}(1-\kappa)^{b-1}\exp(w\{1-\kappa\})\mathrm{d}\kappa}\\

(A.1)

In the denominator of the second and third terms of the right-hand side of (

),
we have

E
Interval arithmetic

In this paper, we employed the interval arithemetic
(
Moore, Kearfott and Cloud, 2009
)
to rigorously bound the value of Stein’s unbiased risk estimate.
We used the python package pyinterval (
https://github.com/taschini/pyinterval
).
Here, we briefly explain the idea of the interval arithmetic.
See
Moore, Kearfott and Cloud (2009)
for more details.

In the interval arithemetic, each number is represented by an interval that includes it.
For example,
\sqrt{2}
can be represented by
[1.41,1.42]
.
Such a representation enables to obtain a rigorous bound of numerical computation results accounting for the rounding error.
For example, by representing
\sqrt{2}
and
\sqrt{3}
by
[1.41,1.42]
and
[1.73,1.74]
respectively,
\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{3}
is guaranteed to be included in
[1.41+1.73,1.42+1.74]=[3.14,3.16]
.
Functions of intervals are defined in a similar way.
The interval Newton method (Algorithm
1
) outputs an interval that includes the zero point of a given function.

Algorithm 1

Interval Newton method

0:

f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
f(\bar{x})=0
&
(l_{0},u_{0})
such that
\bar{x}\in[l_{0},u_{0}]
& MAX_ITER

0:

(l,u)
such that
\bar{x}\in[l,u]

1:

l\leftarrow l_{0}

2:

u\leftarrow u_{0}

3:

for

i=1,\dots,\mathrm{MAX\_ITER}

do

4:

m\leftarrow(l+u)/2

5:

[a,b]\leftarrow m-f(m)/f^{\prime}([l,u])
% interval division

6:

l\leftarrow\max(l,a)

7:

u\leftarrow\min(u,b)

8:

end

for
