Real-time dynamics of the O ⁡ ( 4 ) O(4) scalar theory within the fRG approach
Yang-yang Tan, Yong-rui Chen, Wei-jie Fu

Abstract
In this paper, the real-time dynamics of the O ⁡ ( 4 ) O(4) scalar theory is studied within the functional renormalization group formulated on the Schwinger-Keldysh closed time path. The flow equations for the effective action and its n n -point correlation functions are derived in terms of the “classical” and “quantum” fields, and a concise diagrammatic representation is presented. An analytic expression for the flow of the four-point vertex is obtained. Spectral functions with different values of temperature and momentum are obtained. Moreover, we calculate the dynamical critical exponent for the phase transition near the critical temperature in the O ⁡ ( 4 ) O(4) scalar theory in 3 + 1 3+1 dimensions, and the value is found to be z ≃ 2.023 z\simeq 2.023 .

Introduction

The past years have seen rapid progress in our understanding of the strongly correlated physics and its in-medium effects in the context of Euclidean field theories at finite temperature and density, e.g., QCD on a discretized lattice of Euclidean space and time
Bazavov et al. 2019
;
Borsanyi et al. 2020
, functional continuum QCD within the functional renormalization group (fRG)
Fu et al. 2020a
;
Braun et al. 2020a
;
Braun et al. 2020b
and Dyson-Schwinger equations (DSE)
Fischer 2019
;
Isserstedt et al. 2019
;
Gao and Pawlowski 2020a
;
Gao and Pawlowski 2020b
;
Gunkel and Fischer 2021
. Relevant studies have provided us with a plethora of properties of QCD at finite temperature and density, such as equation of state, thermodynamics, fluctuations, phase structure and so forth. Exploration of other properties of the same importance, for instance, nonequilibrium time evolution of quantum fields far away from the thermal equilibrium
Berges 2004
, dynamics of critical fluctuations
Bluhm et al. 2020
, spectral functions and transport coefficients
Blaizot and Iancu 2002
, dynamic critical exponents
Hohenberg and Halperin 1977
, etc., is, however, beyond the capability of the Euclidean field theories, and direct computations of field theories in the Minkowski spacetime are indispensable.

The formalism of the functional integral on a closed time path
Schwinger 1961
;
Keldysh 1964
, i.e., the Schwinger-Keldysh path integral, is well suited for investigations of the above-mentioned properties of real-time dynamics, and also see, e.g.,
Chou et al. 1985
;
Blaizot and Iancu 2002
;
Berges 2004
;
Sieberer et al. 2016
for relevant reviews. It has proved to be a powerful tool to deal with both the equilibrium and nonequilibrium thermodynamic systems. Unfortunately, lattice Monte-Carlo simulations in the formalism of Keldysh path integral are hindered by the notorious ‘sign’ problem, and therefore, in order to study observables related to nonperturbative real-time dynamics, e.g., spectral functions in QCD or other strongly correlated system
Horak et al. 2020
;
Horak et al. 2021
, one has to resort to functional continuum methods. In the references above, a spectral DSE approach in terms of Källén-Lehmann representation of correlation functions is put forward, and is applied in the computation of spectral functions in the
\phi^{4}
-theory and the ghost spectral function in Yang-Mills (YM) theory.

The functional renormalization group is a nonperturbative approach of continuum field theories. In fRG, quantum fluctuations of different momentum shells are integrated out successively via running of flow equations, and thus it is very convenient to cope with physical problems involving different degrees of freedom on different scales
Wetterich 1993
, see also e.g.,
Berges et al. 2002
;
Pawlowski 2007
;
Schaefer and Wambach 2008
;
Gies 2012
;
Rosten 2012
;
Braun 2012
;
Pawlowski 2014
;
Dupuis et al. 2021
for QCD related reviews. Remarkably, significant progress have been made over the last several years in the first-principle fRG computation of QCD or YM theory in the vacuum
Mitter et al. 2015
;
Braun et al. 2016
;
Rennecke 2015
;
Cyrol et al. 2016
;
Cyrol et al. 2018a
and at finite temperature and density
Cyrol et al. 2018b
;
Fu et al. 2020a
;
Braun et al. 2020b
, in the formalism of Euclidean path integral. In the meanwhile, relevant studies in the low energy effective field theories also provided us with a wealth of useful information on QCD phase structure
Schaefer and Wambach 2005
;
Herbst et al. 2011
;
Herbst et al. 2013
;
Rennecke and Schaefer 2017
equation of state
Herbst et al. 2014
;
Yin et al. 2019
, baryon number fluctuations
Skokov et al. 2010
;
Skokov et al. 2011
;
Friman et al. 2011
;
Morita et al. 2015
;
Fu and Pawlowski 2015
;
Fu and Pawlowski 2016
;
Fu et al. 2016
;
Almasi et al. 2017
;
Wen et al. 2019
;
Sun et al. 2018
;
Fu 2019
;
Fu et al. 2021
, baryon-strangeness correlations
Fu et al. 2020b
;
Fu et al. 2019
;
Wen and Fu 2021
, critical exponents
Chen et al. 2021
, etc.

A promising and intriguing possibility is to combine fRG with the Keldysh path integral, i.e., formulating flow equations in terms of the functional integral of closed time path. One of the relevant pioneer works has been done in
Berges and Hoffmeister 2009
, where the fRG on a closed time path is employed to study nonthermal fixed points of the
O(N)
scalar theory, see also
Berges and Mesterhazy 2012
. Another conceptually different combination between fRG and the Keldysh path integral is put forward in
Gasenzer and Pawlowski 2008
;
Gasenzer et al. 2010
;
Corell et al. 2019
, where the regulation is implemented on the time rather than the renormalization group (RG) scale, such that a time evolution equation for the non-equilibrium effective action is obtained. Furthermore, the transition from unitary to dissipative dynamics is investigated in the framework of the real-time fRG
Mesterházy et al. 2015
. Very recently, spectral functions for the scalar field theory in
d
=0+1 dimensions are calculated within the fRG formulated on the Keldysh path
Huelsmann et al. 2020
. The fRG with the Keldysh functional integral has also been used in open quantum systems to study e.g., nonequilibrium transport
Jakobs et al. 2007
, dynamical critical behavior
Sieberer et al. 2013
, etc., and see e.g.,
Sieberer et al. 2016
;
Dupuis et al. 2021
for more comprehensive discussions.

In this work we would like to adopt the fRG formulated on the Keldysh path, to investigate the real-time dynamics of the
O(4)
scalar theory. We will calculate the spectral functions in thermal equilibrium. Note that calculations of the spectral functions in thermal equilibrium have attracted lots of attentions in recent years. While ill-defined, construction of the spectral functions from Euclidean data sheds new light on time-like properties of correlation functions

;
Binosi and Tripolt 2020

;
Pawlowski and Strodthoff 2015

Jung et al. 2017
for more details. Furthermore, we will also investigate the dynamical critical exponent near the phase transition in the
O(4)
scalar theory in the formalism of real-time fRG.

II
The
O(N)
scalar theory within the real-time fRG approach

In this section we begin with a RG scale
k
-dependent effective action for the real-time
O(N)
scalar theory as follows

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}[\phi_{c},\phi_{q}]=

\displaystyle\int_{x}\Big[Z_{\phi,k}(\partial_{\mu}\phi_{q})\cdot(\partial^{\mu}\phi_{c})-U_{k}(\phi_{c},\phi_{q})\Big]\,,

(1)

where
\phi_{i,c}
and
\phi_{i,q}
(
i=0,1,...N-1
) are the “classical” and “quantum” scalar fields with
N
components, respectively. In
Equation

1
we have adopted a local potential approximation (LPA) with a
k
-dependent wave function renormalization
Z_{\phi,k}
, and it allows us to introduce the fRG formalism of Keldysh fields conveniently, which can also be easily extended to cases beyond LPA. The effective potential in
Equation

1
is given by

\displaystyle U_{k}(\phi_{c},\phi_{q})=

\displaystyle V_{k}(\rho_{+})-V_{k}(\rho_{-})\,,

(2)

with
\rho_{\pm}=\phi_{\pm}^{2}/2
, which is obviously
O(N)
invariant. Here,
\phi_{\pm}
denote the fields on the forward and backward branches in the formalism of Keldysh field theory, cf.
Appendix

A
for details.

On the equations of motion (EoM) of fields, i.e., the expectation values of fields under vanishing external sources as shown in
Equation

98
, that would be denoted by variables with a bar in what follows, the “quantum” field is obviously vanishing, viz.,

\displaystyle\bar{\phi}_{q}|_{\mathrm{EoM}}=

\displaystyle 0\,.

(3)

Furthermore, we would like to investigate the breaking of
O(N)
symmetry into the
O(N-1)
one. To that end, a nonvanishing value for one component of the “classical” field is introduced as follows

\displaystyle\bar{\phi}_{c}|_{\mathrm{EoM}}=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\bar{\phi}_{0,c}\qquad i=0\\[12.91663pt]
0\quad\qquad i\neq 0\end{array}\right.,

where the direction of zero component is chosen to that of the symmetry breaking. Consequently, one can define the sigma and pion fields as follows

\displaystyle\sigma_{c}=

\displaystyle\phi_{0,c}-\bar{\phi}_{0,c}\,,\qquad\sigma_{q}=\phi_{0,q}\,,

(6)

and

\displaystyle\pi_{i,c}=

\displaystyle\phi_{i,c}\,,\qquad\pi_{i,q}=\phi_{i,q}\,,

(7)

with
i=1,...N-1
. Note that the nonvanishing
\bar{\phi}_{0,c}
has been shifted away in the definition of the “classical” sigma field in
Equation

6
.

The effective action in
Equation

1
is straightforwardly reformulated in terms of the newly defined
\sigma
and
\pi
fields, which reads

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}[\phi_{c},\phi_{q}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{x}\bigg\{\Big[Z_{\phi,k}\partial_{\mu}\sigma_{q}\partial^{\mu}\sigma_{c}-m_{\sigma,k}^{2}\sigma_{q}\sigma_{c}\Big]

\displaystyle+\Big[Z_{\phi,k}(\partial_{\mu}\pi_{q})\cdot(\partial^{\mu}\pi_{c})-m_{\pi,k}^{2}\pi_{q}\cdot\pi_{c}\Big]

\displaystyle-\frac{\lambda_{3\sigma,k}}{6}(3\sigma_{q}\sigma_{c}^{2}+\sigma_{q}^{3})-\frac{\lambda_{1\sigma 2\pi,k}}{2}\Big[2\sigma_{c}(\pi_{q}\cdot\pi_{c})

\displaystyle+\sigma_{q}(\pi_{q}^{2}+\pi_{c}^{2})\Big]-\frac{\lambda_{4\sigma,k}}{6}\sigma_{q}\sigma_{c}(\sigma_{q}^{2}+\sigma_{c}^{2})

\displaystyle-\frac{\lambda_{2\sigma 2\pi,k}}{2}\Big[\sigma_{q}\sigma_{c}(\pi_{q}^{2}+\pi_{c}^{2})+(\pi_{q}\cdot\pi_{c})(\sigma_{c}^{2}+\sigma_{q}^{2})\Big]

\displaystyle-\frac{\lambda_{4\pi,k}}{6}(\pi_{q}\cdot\pi_{c})(\pi_{q}^{2}+\pi_{c}^{2})-\lambda_{1\sigma,k}\sigma_{q}+\cdots\bigg\}\,,

(8)

where the effective potential in
Equation

1
has been expanded up to the fourth order in powers of fields, and the
\sigma
and
\pi
masses are given by

\displaystyle m_{\sigma,k}^{2}\equiv

\displaystyle V^{\prime}_{k}(\bar{\rho}_{c})+2\bar{\rho}_{c}V^{(2)}_{k}(\bar{\rho}_{c})\,,

(9)

\displaystyle m_{\pi,k}^{2}\equiv

\displaystyle V^{\prime}_{k}(\bar{\rho}_{c})\,,

(10)

with
\bar{\rho}_{c}\equiv{\bar{\phi}_{c}}^{2}/4
. The three-meson couplings in
Equation

8
read

\displaystyle\lambda_{3\sigma,k}\equiv

\displaystyle 3{\bar{\rho}_{c}}^{\frac{1}{2}}V^{(2)}_{k}(\bar{\rho}_{c})+2{\bar{\rho}_{c}}^{\frac{3}{2}}V^{(3)}_{k}(\bar{\rho}_{c})\,,

(11)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1\sigma 2\pi,k}\equiv

\displaystyle{\bar{\rho}_{c}}^{\frac{1}{2}}V^{(2)}_{k}(\bar{\rho}_{c})\,,

(12)

and the four-meson couplings

\displaystyle\lambda_{4\sigma,k}\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}V^{(2)}_{k}(\bar{\rho}_{c})+6{\bar{\rho}_{c}}V^{(3)}_{k}(\bar{\rho}_{c})+2{\bar{\rho}_{c}}^{2}V^{(4)}_{k}(\bar{\rho}_{c})\,,

(13)

\displaystyle\lambda_{4\pi,k}\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}V^{(2)}_{k}(\bar{\rho}_{c})\,,

(14)

\displaystyle\lambda_{2\sigma 2\pi,k}\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}V^{(2)}_{k}(\bar{\rho}_{c})+{\bar{\rho}_{c}}V^{(3)}_{k}(\bar{\rho}_{c})\,.

(15)

Note that in the last line of
Equation

8
there is a term linear in
\sigma_{q}
with the relevant coefficient given by

\displaystyle\lambda_{1\sigma,k}\equiv

\displaystyle 2{\bar{\rho}_{c}}^{\frac{1}{2}}V^{\prime}_{k}(\bar{\rho}_{c})\,.

(16)

The infrared (IR) regulator term as shown in
Equation

104
in our case reads

\displaystyle\Delta S_{k}[\phi_{c},\phi_{q}]=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}\Big[\sigma_{q}(-q)R_{\sigma,k}(q)\sigma_{c}(q)

\displaystyle+\pi_{i,q}(-q)\big(R_{\pi,k}\big)_{ij}(q)\pi_{j,c}(q)\Big]\,,

(17)

where we have used a
3d
flat regulator
Litim 2000
;
Litim 2001
in this work, to wit,

\displaystyle R_{\sigma,k}(q)=

\displaystyle R_{\phi,k}(q)=Z_{\phi,k}\Big(-{\bm{q}}^{2}r_{B}\big(\frac{{\bm{q}}^{2}}{k^{2}}\big)\Big)\,,

(18)

and

\displaystyle\big(R_{\pi,k}\big)_{ij}(q)=

\displaystyle R_{\phi,k}(q)\delta_{ij}\,,

(19)

with

\displaystyle r_{B}(x)

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{1}{x}-1\Big)\Theta(1-x)\,,

(20)

and here
\Theta(x)
is the Heaviside step function. Thus, the regulator matrix, cf.
Equation

105
, in the bases of fields
\Phi=(\sigma_{c},\{\pi_{i,c}\},\sigma_{q},\{\pi_{i,q}\})
with
i=1,...N-1
is readily obtained as

\displaystyle R_{k}(q)=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&R_{k}^{A}(q)\\[8.61108pt]
R_{k}^{R}(q)&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(21)

with

\displaystyle R_{k}^{R}(q)

\displaystyle=R_{k}^{A}(q)=\begin{pmatrix}R_{\sigma,k}(q)&0\\[8.61108pt]
0&R_{\pi,k}(q)\end{pmatrix}\equiv\hat{R}_{\phi,k}(q)\,.

(22)

Note that in
Equation

21
we do not include any regulator for the
qq
-component, since only the real parts of two-point functions are regulated in this work. This is adequate for cases in thermal equilibrium, where the Keldysh propagator is related to the retarded and advanced propagators by the fluctuation-dissipation relation as shown in
Equation

37
in the following. But if the regulator in
Equation

18
is extended to the one having a finite imaginary part, as done in some nonequilibrium calculations, e.g.
Duclut and Delamotte 2017
, a nonvanishing
qq
-component of regulators is necessary.

With the regulator in
Equation

22
, one can reformulate the flow equation for the effective action in
Equation

118
as such

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\Gamma_{k}[\Phi]

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2}\mathrm{STr}\Big[\tilde{\partial}_{\tau}\ln\big(\Gamma_{k}^{(2)}[\Phi]+R_{k}\big)\Big]\,.

(23)

where
\tilde{\partial}_{\tau}
indicates that
\partial_{\tau}
hits only the regulator in
Equation

21
, and
\tau\equiv\ln(k/\Lambda)
is the RG time, with an initial evolution scale
\Lambda
, i.e., the ultraviolet (UV) cutoff. In
Equation

23
one has employed the notation as follows

\displaystyle\Big(\Gamma_{k}^{(2)}[\Phi]\Big)_{ab}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\delta^{2}\Gamma_{k}[\Phi]}{\delta\Phi_{a}\delta\Phi_{b}}\,.

(24)

Moreover, it is more convenient to make the reorganization as follows

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}^{(2)}+R_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{k}+\mathcal{F}_{k}\,,

(25)

where
\mathcal{P}_{k}
is the matrix of inverse propagators with regulators, and
\mathcal{F}_{k}
is the interaction sector which encodes the field dependence.

In what follows we consider the
O(N)
scalar theory in thermal equilibrium with a temperature
T
. As a consequence, one arrives at

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{k}=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathcal{P}^{A}_{k}\\[4.30554pt]
\mathcal{P}^{R}_{k}&\mathcal{P}^{K}_{k}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(26)

Here the inverse retarded propagator reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{R}_{k}=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{P}^{R}_{\sigma,k}&0\\[8.61108pt]
0&\mathcal{P}^{R}_{\pi,k}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(27)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{R}_{\sigma,k}=

\displaystyle Z_{\phi,k}\bigg[q_{0}^{2}-{\bm{q}}^{2}\Big(1+r_{B}\big(\frac{{\bm{q}}^{2}}{k^{2}}\big)\Big)\bigg]-m_{\sigma,k}^{2}

\displaystyle+\mathrm{sgn}(q_{0})i\epsilon\,,

(28)

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{P}^{R}_{\pi,k}\big)_{ij}=

\displaystyle\Bigg\{Z_{\phi,k}\bigg[q_{0}^{2}-{\bm{q}}^{2}\Big(1+r_{B}\big(\frac{{\bm{q}}^{2}}{k^{2}}\big)\Big)\bigg]-m_{\pi,k}^{2}

\displaystyle+\mathrm{sgn}(q_{0})i\epsilon\Bigg\}\delta_{ij}\,,

(29)

where the infinitesimal terms with a sign function are used to determine the contour for the retarded propagator in the complex plane of
q_{0}
. The advanced counterpart
\mathcal{P}^{A}_{k}
in
Equation

26
is related to
\mathcal{P}^{R}_{k}
through a complex conjugate, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{A}_{k}=

\displaystyle(\mathcal{P}^{R}_{k})^{*}\,.

(30)

The Keldysh component of the inverse propagator in
Equation

26
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{K}_{k}=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{P}^{K}_{\sigma,k}&0\\[8.61108pt]
0&\mathcal{P}^{K}_{\pi,k}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(31)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{K}_{\sigma,k}=

\displaystyle 2i\epsilon\,\mathrm{sgn}(q_{0})\coth\Big(\frac{q_{0}}{2T}\Big)\,,

(32)

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{P}^{K}_{\pi,k}\big)_{ij}=

\displaystyle\bigg[2i\epsilon\,\mathrm{sgn}(q_{0})\coth\Big(\frac{q_{0}}{2T}\Big)\bigg]\delta_{ij}\,.

(33)

Therefore, the propagator is readily obtained as follows

\displaystyle G_{k}=

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{P}_{k}\big)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}G^{K}_{k}&G^{R}_{k}\\[4.30554pt]
G^{A}_{k}&0\end{pmatrix}\,,

(34)

where the retarded and advanced components read

\displaystyle G^{R}_{k}=

\displaystyle(\mathcal{P}^{R}_{k})^{-1}\,,\qquad G^{A}_{k}=(\mathcal{P}^{A}_{k})^{-1}\,,

(35)

and the Keldysh propagator or the correlation function is given by

\displaystyle G^{K}_{k}=

\displaystyle-(\mathcal{P}^{R}_{k})^{-1}\mathcal{P}^{K}_{k}(\mathcal{P}^{A}_{k})^{-1}=-G^{R}_{k}\mathcal{P}^{K}_{k}G^{A}_{k}\,.

(36)

It is easy to verify a relation among the different components of the propagator as follows

\displaystyle G^{K}_{k}=

\displaystyle\big(G^{R}_{k}-G^{A}_{k}\big)\coth\Big(\frac{q_{0}}{2T}\Big)\,.

(37)

which is the fluctuation-dissipation relation in thermal equilibrium.

To summarize, the retarded, advanced, correlation (Keldysh) two-point connected Green’s functions or propagators are given by

\displaystyle iG^{R}_{\sigma,k}=

\displaystyle\langle T_{p}\sigma_{c}(x)\sigma_{q}(y)\rangle\,,\quad iG^{A}_{\sigma,k}=\langle T_{p}\sigma_{q}(x)\sigma_{c}(y)\rangle\,,

(38)

\displaystyle iG^{K}_{\sigma,k}=

\displaystyle\langle T_{p}\sigma_{c}(x)\sigma_{c}(y)\rangle=\big(iG^{R}_{k}\big)\big(i\mathcal{P}^{K}_{k}\big)\big(iG^{A}_{k}\big)\,,

(39)

for the
\sigma
meson, and

\displaystyle i\big(G^{R}_{\pi,k}\big)_{ij}=

\displaystyle\langle T_{p}\pi_{i,c}(x)\pi_{j,q}(y)\rangle\,,

(40)

\displaystyle i\big(G^{A}_{\pi,k}\big)_{ij}=

\displaystyle\langle T_{p}\pi_{i,q}(x)\pi_{j,c}(y)\rangle\,,

(41)

\displaystyle i\big(G^{K}_{\pi,k}\big)_{ij}=

\displaystyle\langle T_{p}\pi_{i,c}(x)\pi_{j,c}(y)\rangle\,,

(42)

for the
\pi
meson. Here,
T_{p}
is the time ordering operator in the closed time path from the positive branch to the negative one, and
\langle\cdots\rangle
denotes ensemble average. Note that the last equality in
Equation

39
results from
Equation

36
. In
Figure

1
we show the diagrammatic representation for the retarded, advanced, and Keldysh propagators in this work. The retarded propagator is denoted by a dashed line with two points labelled with “
c,q
”, and the advanced propagator with “
q,c
”. Motivated by
Equation

39
, we use a line with an empty circle in its middle to represent the Keldysh propagator. Hence, the Keldysh propagator is essentially composed of the retarded and advanced propagators jointed with an empty circle, which corresponds to
i\mathcal{P}^{K}_{k}
in
Equation

39
.

III
Flow of the effective potential

Inserting
Equation

Equation

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\Gamma_{k}[\Phi]

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2}\mathrm{STr}\Big[\tilde{\partial}_{\tau}\ln G_{k}^{-1}\Big]+\frac{i}{2}\mathrm{STr}\Big[\tilde{\partial}_{\tau}\big(G_{k}\mathcal{F}_{k}\big)\Big]

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{4}\mathrm{STr}\Big[\tilde{\partial}_{\tau}\big(G_{k}\mathcal{F}_{k}\big)^{2}\Big]+\frac{i}{6}\mathrm{STr}\Big[\tilde{\partial}_{\tau}\big(G_{k}\mathcal{F}_{k}\big)^{3}\Big]

stands for the one-particle-irreducible (1PI) vertices, which are defined as

\displaystyle i\Gamma_{k,\Phi_{a_{1}}\cdots\Phi_{a_{n}}}^{(n)}

. Substituting

\displaystyle\lambda_{3\sigma,k}

\displaystyle={\bar{\rho}_{c}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\partial m_{\sigma,k}^{2}}{\partial\bar{\rho}_{c}}\,,\qquad\lambda_{1\sigma 2\pi,k}={\bar{\rho}_{c}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\partial m_{\pi,k}^{2}}{\partial\bar{\rho}_{c}}\,,

one is led to

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}V^{\prime}_{k}(\bar{\rho}_{c})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i}{4}\int\frac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}\tilde{\partial}_{\tau}\Big[\frac{\partial m_{\sigma,k}^{2}}{\partial\bar{\rho}_{c}}G^{K}_{\sigma,k}(q)+\frac{\partial m_{\pi,k}^{2}}{\partial\bar{\rho}_{c}}\big(G^{K}_{\pi,k}\big)_{ii}(q)\Big]

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{\rho}_{c}}\bigg\{-\frac{i}{4}\int\frac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}\big(\partial_{\tau}R_{\phi,k}(q)\big)\Big[G^{K}_{\sigma,k}(q)

\displaystyle+\big(G^{K}_{\pi,k}\big)_{ii}(q)\Big]\bigg\}\,.

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}V_{k}(\bar{\rho}_{c})

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{4}\int\frac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}\big(\partial_{\tau}R_{\phi,k}(q)\big)\Big[G^{K}_{\sigma,k}(q)+\big(G^{K}_{\pi,k}\big)_{ii}(q)\Big]\,,

up to a term independent of
\bar{\rho}_{c}

Equation

28
and
Equation

29
are used, one arrives at

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}V_{k}(\bar{\rho}_{c})=

\displaystyle\frac{k^{4}}{4\pi^{2}}\bigg[l^{(B,4)}_{0}(\tilde{m}^{2}_{\sigma,k},\eta_{\phi,k};T)

\displaystyle+\big(N-1\big)l^{(B,4)}_{0}(\tilde{m}^{2}_{\pi,k},\eta_{\phi,k};T)\bigg]\,,

with the RG invariant dimensionless meson masses

, and the anomalous dimension which is defined as follows

\displaystyle\eta_{\phi,k}

\displaystyle\equiv-\frac{\partial_{\tau}Z_{\phi,k}}{Z_{\phi,k}}\,.

The threshold function in

\displaystyle l^{(B,4)}_{0}(\tilde{m}^{2}_{\phi,k},\eta_{\phi,k};T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{3}\left(1-\frac{\eta_{\phi,k}}{5}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\tilde{m}^{2}_{\phi,k}}}\left(\frac{1}{2}+n_{B}(\tilde{m}^{2}_{\phi,k};T)\right)\,,

\displaystyle n_{B}(\tilde{m}^{2}_{\phi,k};T)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\exp\bigg\{\frac{k}{T}\sqrt{1+\tilde{m}^{2}_{\phi,k}}\bigg\}-1}\,.

Interestingly, this value of the dynamical critical exponent obtained in this work is compatible with a very recent result for Model A in three spatial dimensions,

, obtained from real-time classical-statistical lattice simulations
Schweitzer et al. 2020
. Here we have used the standard classification for the universality of critical dynamics
Hohenberg and Halperin 1977
. However, the critical dynamics of the relativistic

Rajagopal and Wilczek 1993

Schlichting et al. 2020

VI
Conclusions

In this work we have studied the real-time dynamics of the
O(4)
scalar theory within the functional renormalization group formulated on the Schwinger-Keldysh closed time path. The effective action and flow equations are organized in terms of two classes of fields, i.e., the “classical” and “quantum” fields, which in this work are denoted by subscripts
c
and
q
, respectively. A concise diagrammatic representation for the propagators, including the retarded, advanced and Keldysh propagators, and various vertices are introduced and used in the derivation of flow equations. We have demonstrated in detail that this formalism in the real-time fRG produces identical results for the effective potential, meson mass, and four-point vertex, in comparison to the relevant results obtained in the imaginary-time fRG, when the momentum dependence of vertices is suppressed.

We have solved the flow equations for the momentum-dependent two- and four-point correlation functions in the symmetric phase at finite temperature. A simplified self-consistent truncation has been used, which allows us to obtain analytic expressions for the flows of the propagator and vertex. We have investigated in detail roles of two different processes, i.e., the on-shell creation and annihilation of particles and Landau damping, in the imaginary parts of the two- and four-point correlation functions, as well as in the spectral function. We find that Landau damping probably leads to negative spectral functions and imaginary part of correlation functions at high temperature, which is certainly required to be confirmed in future studies with more improved truncation. Spectral functions with different values of temperature and spacial momentum are obtained. Moreover, we have calculated the dynamical critical exponent for the phase transition near the critical temperature in the
O(4)
scalar theory in
3+1
dimensions, and found
z\simeq 2.023
.

We have to point out that the computation in this work is not fully self-consistent, and
Equation

72
is also neglected in the calculation of the inverse retarded propagator, which certainly should be improved in future work. Even for that, we have shown in this work that the combination between the fRG and the Keldysh functional integral is promising, and it is able to provide us with a wealth of insights on the real-time dynamics of nonperturbative field theories. Therefore, it is very interesting to apply this formalism in more realistic theory, e.g., the Yang-Mills theory, which we hope to report in near future.

Appendix A
Formalism of the fRG in the Keldysh field theory

Given a collective notation for all the fields concerned,
\varphi=\{\varphi_{i}\}
, where the subscript
i
not only distinguishes different species of fields, but also denotes the space-time coordinates and other internal degrees of freedom, the classical Keldysh action for a closed system reads

\displaystyle S[\varphi]

\displaystyle=\int_{x}\Big(\mathcal{L}[\varphi_{+}]-\mathcal{L}[\varphi_{-}]\Big)\,,

(88)

with the shorthand notation
\int_{x}\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\int d^{3}x
, where
\mathcal{L}
is a generic Lagrangian density, and
\varphi_{\pm}
stand for the fields on the forward and backward branches, respectively. This also applies for variables with indices
\pm
in what follows. The Keldysh generating functional is given by

\displaystyle Z[J_{+},J_{-}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\big(\mathcal{D}\varphi_{+}\mathcal{D}\varphi_{-}\big)\exp\Big\{i\Big(S[\varphi]+\big(J_{+}^{i}\varphi_{i,+}-J_{-}^{i}\varphi_{i,-}\big)\Big)\Big\}\,,

(89)

where
J_{\pm}^{i}
are the external sources conjugate to
\varphi_{i,\pm}
, respectively. Note that summations and/or integrals are assumed for repeated indices. By the use of the Keldysh rotation as follows

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\varphi_{i,+}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\varphi_{i,c}+\varphi_{i,q}),\\[12.91663pt]
\varphi_{i,-}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\varphi_{i,c}-\varphi_{i,q}),\end{array}\right.

and

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}J_{+}^{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(J_{c}^{i}+J_{q}^{i}),\\[12.91663pt]
J_{-}^{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(J_{c}^{i}-J_{q}^{i}),\end{array}\right.

where quantities with the subscripts
c
and
q
stand for physical “classical” and “quantum” variables, respectively, one is able to reformulate
Equation

89
in terms of physical fields and external sources, to wit,

\displaystyle Z[J_{c},J_{q}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\big(\mathcal{D}\varphi_{c}\mathcal{D}\varphi_{q}\big)\exp\Big\{i\Big(S[\varphi]+\big(J_{q}^{i}\varphi_{i,c}+J_{c}^{i}\varphi_{i,q}\big)\Big)\Big\}\,.

(94)

Then, we employ the two-point regulator term as follows

\displaystyle\Delta S_{k}[\varphi]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\varphi_{i,c},\varphi_{i,q})\begin{pmatrix}0&R_{k}^{ij}\\
(R_{k}^{ij})^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{j,c}\\
\varphi_{j,q}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big(\varphi_{i,c}R_{k}^{ij}\varphi_{j,q}+\varphi_{i,q}(R_{k}^{ij})^{*}\varphi_{j,c}\Big)\,,

(95)

where the IR regulator, viz.,

\displaystyle R_{k}^{ij}=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}R_{k}^{ji}\qquad\mathrm{bosonic}\\[12.91663pt]
-R_{k}^{ji}\qquad\mathrm{fermionic}\end{array}\right.,

is used to suppress quantum fluctuations of momenta less than a RG scale
k
, i.e.,
q\lesssim k
. Inserting
Equation

95
into
Equation

94
, one is left with the scale-dependent generating functional, which reads

\displaystyle Z_{k}[J_{c},J_{q}]=

\displaystyle\int\big(\mathcal{D}\varphi_{c}\mathcal{D}\varphi_{q}\big)\exp\Big\{i\Big(S[\varphi]+\Delta S_{k}[\varphi]

\displaystyle+(J_{q}^{i}\varphi_{i,c}+J_{c}^{i}\varphi_{i,q})\Big)\Big\}\,.

(98)

It follows that the generating functional for the connected Green’s functions, i.e., Schwinger functional, is also
k
-dependent, i.e.,

\displaystyle W_{k}[J_{c},J_{q}]

\displaystyle=-i\ln Z_{k}[J_{c},J_{q}]\,.

(99)

In the following , we would like to absorb the suffixes
c
and
q
into the index
i
, and denote them collectively with a new label
a
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\{\varphi_{a}\}

\displaystyle=\big\{\{\varphi_{i,c}\},\{\varphi_{i,q}\}\big\}\,,

(100)

\displaystyle\{J^{a}\}

\displaystyle=\big\{\{J_{q}^{i}\},\{J_{c}^{i}\}\big\}\,.

(101)

Hence, the expectation value of field
\varphi_{a}
is given by

\displaystyle\Phi_{a}

\displaystyle\equiv\langle\varphi_{a}\rangle=\frac{\delta W_{k}[J]}{\delta J^{a}}\,,

(102)

and the two-point connected Green’s function, i.e., the propagator, reads

\displaystyle G_{k,ab}\equiv

\displaystyle-i\langle\varphi_{a}\varphi_{b}\rangle_{c}=-i\big[\langle\varphi_{a}\varphi_{b}\rangle-\langle\varphi_{a}\rangle\langle\varphi_{b}\rangle\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\delta^{2}W_{k}[J]}{\delta J^{a}J^{b}}\,.

(103)

In the same way, other notations can be simplified by the new label, and for instance, the regulator term in
Equation

95
then reads

\displaystyle\Delta S_{k}[\varphi]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\varphi_{a}R_{k}^{ab}\varphi_{b}\,,

(104)

with

\displaystyle R_{k}^{ab}\equiv

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&R_{k}^{ij}\\
(R_{k}^{ij})^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(105)

The Legendre transformation of Schwinger functional
W_{k}[J]
allows us to obtain the one-particle-irreducible (1PI) effective action, given by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}[\Phi]

\displaystyle=W_{k}[J]-\Delta S_{k}[\Phi]-J^{a}\Phi_{a}\,.

(106)

Introducing a symbol as follows

\displaystyle\gamma^{a}_{\hskip 4.26773ptb}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}-\delta^{a}_{\hskip 4.26773ptb}\qquad\text{a and b are fermionic}\\
\delta^{a}_{\hskip 4.26773ptb}\qquad\text{others}\end{array}\right.,

one thus has

\displaystyle J^{a}\Phi_{a}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{a}_{\hskip 4.26773ptb}\Phi_{a}J^{b}\,.

(109)

Differentiating both sides of
Equation

106
with respect to
\Phi_{a}
and employing
Equation

109
, one is led to

\displaystyle\frac{\delta(\Gamma_{k}[\Phi]+\Delta S_{k}[\Phi])}{\delta\Phi_{a}}

\displaystyle=-\gamma^{a}_{\hskip 4.26773ptb}J^{b}\,,

(110)

i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{\delta^{2}(\Gamma_{k}[\Phi]+\Delta S_{k}[\Phi])}{\delta J^{b}\delta\Phi_{a}}

\displaystyle=-\gamma^{a}_{\hskip 4.26773ptb}\,.

(111)

The l.h.s. of
Equation

111
can be further reformulated as

\displaystyle\frac{\delta^{2}(\Gamma_{k}[\Phi]+\Delta S_{k}[\Phi])}{\delta J^{b}\delta\Phi_{a}}=\frac{\delta\Phi_{c}}{\delta J^{b}}\frac{\delta^{2}(\Gamma_{k}[\Phi]+\Delta S_{k}[\Phi])}{\delta\Phi_{c}\delta\Phi_{a}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\delta^{2}W_{k}[J]}{\delta J^{b}\delta J^{c}}\frac{\delta^{2}(\Gamma_{k}[\Phi]+\Delta S_{k}[\Phi])}{\delta\Phi_{c}\delta\Phi_{a}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-G_{k,bc}\frac{\delta^{2}(\Gamma_{k}[\Phi]+\Delta S_{k}[\Phi])}{\delta\Phi_{c}\delta\Phi_{a}},

(112)

where we have used
Equation

103
. Thus, one arrives at

\displaystyle G_{k,bc}\frac{\delta^{2}(\Gamma_{k}[\Phi]+\Delta S_{k}[\Phi])}{\delta\Phi_{c}\delta\Phi_{a}}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{a}_{\hskip 4.26773ptb}\,.

(113)

Employing the notation as follows

\displaystyle\Big(\Gamma_{k}^{(2)}[\Phi]+\Delta S_{k}^{(2)}[\Phi]\Big)^{ab}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\delta^{2}(\Gamma_{k}[\Phi]+\Delta S_{k}[\Phi])}{\delta\Phi_{a}\delta\Phi_{b}}\,,

(114)

one can obtain the propagator from
Equation

113
, which reads

\displaystyle G_{k,ab}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{c}_{\hskip 4.26773pta}\Big[\big(\Gamma_{k}^{(2)}[\Phi]+\Delta S_{k}^{(2)}[\Phi]\big)^{-1}\Big]_{cb}\,.

(115)

Let us proceed to considering the flow equation for Schwinger functional
W_{k}[J]
, which is readily obtained by differentiating both sides of
Equation

99
with respect to the RG time
\tau\equiv\ln(k/\Lambda)
, with an initial evolution scale
\Lambda
, i.e., the UV cutoff. One arrives at

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}W_{k}[J]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{k}[J]}\int\mathcal{D}\varphi\Big(\partial_{\tau}\Delta S_{k}[\varphi]\Big)\exp\Big\{i\Big(S[\varphi]+\Delta S_{k}[\varphi]

\displaystyle+J^{a}\varphi_{a}\Big)\Big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{Z_{k}[J]}\int\mathcal{D}\varphi\Big(\varphi_{a}\partial_{\tau}R_{k}^{ab}\varphi_{b}\Big)\exp\Big\{i\Big(S[\varphi]

\displaystyle+\Delta S_{k}[\varphi]+J^{a}\varphi_{a}\Big)\Big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\langle\varphi_{a}\varphi_{b}\rangle\partial_{\tau}R^{ab}_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(iG_{k,ab}+\Phi_{a}\Phi_{b})\partial_{\tau}R^{ab}_{k}\,,

(116)

where we have used
Equation

103
for the last equality. Given the property of the interchange of indices for the regulator in
Appendix

A
as well as the matrix form in
Equation

105
, the flow equation of Schwinger functional above can be reformulated slightly such that

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}W_{k}[J]

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2}\mathrm{STr}\Big[\big(\partial_{\tau}R^{*}_{k}\big)G_{k}\Big]+\frac{1}{2}\Phi_{a}\big(\partial_{\tau}R^{ab}_{k}\big)\Phi_{b}\,.

(117)

where the super trace, denoted by
\mathrm{STr}
, provides an additional minus sign for the fermionic degrees of freedom. Finally, by the use of
Equation

106
, one is led to the flow equation for the effective action as follows

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\Gamma_{k}[\Phi]

\displaystyle=\partial_{\tau}W_{k}[J]-\partial_{\tau}\Delta S_{k}[\Phi]

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2}\mathrm{STr}\Big[\big(\partial_{\tau}R^{*}_{k}\big)G_{k}\Big]\,.

(118)
