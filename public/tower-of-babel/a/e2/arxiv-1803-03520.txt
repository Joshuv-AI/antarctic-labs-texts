On new types of fractional operators and applications


Abstract
We introduce two kinds of fractional integral operators; the one is defined via the exponential-integral function E 1 ​ ( x ) = ∫ x ∞ e − t t ​ 𝑑 t , x > 0 , E_{1}(x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-t}}{t}\,dt,\hskip 8.50012ptx>0, and the other is defined via the special function 𝒮 ⁡ ( x ) = e − x ​ ∫ 0 ∞ x s − 1 Γ ⁡ ( s ) ​ 𝑑 s , x > 0 . \mathcal{S}(x)=e^{-x}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{s-1}}{\Gamma(s)}\,ds,\hskip 8.50012ptx>0. We establish different properties of these operators, and we study the relationship between the fractional integrals of first kind and the fractional integrals of second kind. Next, we introduce a new concept of fractional derivative of order α > 0 \alpha>0 , which is defined via the fractional integral of first kind. Using an approximate identity argument, we show that the introduced fractional derivative converges to the standard derivative in L 1 L^{1} space, as α → 0 + \alpha\to 0^{+} . Several other properties are studied, like fractional integration by parts, the relationship between this fractional derivative and the fractional integral of second kind, etc. As an application, we consider a new fractional model of the relaxation equation, we establish an existence and uniqueness result for this model, and provide an iterative algorithm that converges to the solution. 2010 Mathematics Subject Classification. 26A33; 34A08. Key words and phrases: Fractional integrals of first kind; fractional integrals of second kind; fractional derivative; exponential-integral function; approximate identity; convergence; fractional relaxation equation.

2
Fractional integral operators of first kind

{sec2}

In this section, we introduce a new class of fractional operators involving the exponential-integral function, and we study some of their properties.

The exponential-integral function is defined as (see, for example
[
1
]
):

E_{1}(x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-t}}{t}\,dt,\quad x>0.

Using Fubini’s theorem, it can be easily seen that

\int_{0}^{\infty}E_{1}(x)\,dx=1.

(1)

Definition 2.1

(Left-sided fractional integral)
.

Let
f\in L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})
,
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
a<b
, be a given function. The left-sided fractional integral of order
\alpha>0
of
f
is given by

\left(J_{a}^{\alpha}f\right)(x)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{a}^{x}E_{1}\left(\frac{x-t}{\alpha}\right)f(t)\,dt,\quad\mbox{ a.e. }x\in[a,b].

Similarly, we define the right-sided fractional integral of order
\alpha>0
as follows.

Definition 2.2

(Right-sided fractional integral)
.

Let
f\in L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})
,
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
a<b
, be a given function. The right-sided fractional integral of order
\alpha>0
of
f
is given by

\left(J_{b}^{\alpha}f\right)(x)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{x}^{b}E_{1}\left(\frac{t-x}{\alpha}\right)f(t)\,dt,\quad\mbox{ a.e. }x\in[a,b].

We have the following result.

Theorem 2.1
.

{T1}

Let
\alpha>0
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
and
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be such that
a<b
. Then

J_{a}^{\alpha}:L^{p}([a,b];\mathbb{R})\to L^{p}([a,b];\mathbb{R})

is a linear and continuous operator. Moreover, we have

\left\|J_{a}^{\alpha}f\right\|_{L^{p}([a,b];\mathbb{R})}\leq\|f\|_{L^{p}([a,b];\mathbb{R})},\quad f\in L^{p}([a,b];\mathbb{R}).

Proof.

Let
f\in L^{p}([a,b];\mathbb{R})
be fixed. We define the function
F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle F(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}f(x),&&\mbox{a.e. }x\in[a,b],\\
0,&&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Similarly, we define the function
G_{\alpha}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle G_{\alpha}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\frac{1}{\alpha}E_{1}\left(\frac{x}{\alpha}\right),&&\mbox{ if }x>0,\\
0,&&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Observe that
F\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
and

\|F\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})}=\|f\|_{L^{p}([a,b];\mathbb{R})}.

(8)

Moreover, by (
1
), we have
G_{\alpha}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
and

\|G_{\alpha}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})}=1.

(9)

Therefore,
F*G_{\alpha}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
and

\|F*G_{\alpha}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})}\leq\|F\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})}\|G_{\alpha}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})},

where
*
denotes the convolution product. Further, by (
8
) and (
9
), we obtain

\|F*G_{\alpha}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})}\leq\|f\|_{L^{p}([a,b];\mathbb{R})}.

(10)

On the other hand, for a.e.
x\in[a,b]
, we have

\displaystyle(F*G_{\alpha})(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}F(x-y)G_{\alpha}(y)\,dy

(11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{x-b}^{x-a}f(x-y)G_{\alpha}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{x-a}f(x-y)E_{1}\left(\frac{y}{\alpha}\right)\,dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{a}^{x}E_{1}\left(\frac{x-z}{\alpha}\right)f(z)\,dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(J_{a}^{\alpha}f\right)(x),

which yields

\left\|J_{a}^{\alpha}f\right\|_{L^{p}([a,b];\mathbb{R})}\leq\|F*G_{\alpha}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})}.

(12)

Finally, combining (
10
) with (
12
), the desired result follows.
∎

Using a similar argument to that above, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 2.2
.

{T2}

Let
\alpha>0
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
and
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be such that
a<b
. Then

J_{b}^{\alpha}:L^{p}([a,b];\mathbb{R})\to L^{p}([a,b];\mathbb{R})

is a linear and continuous operator. Moreover, we have

\left\|J_{b}^{\alpha}f\right\|_{L^{p}([a,b];\mathbb{R})}\leq\|f\|_{L^{p}([a,b];\mathbb{R})},\quad f\in L^{p}([a,b];\mathbb{R}).

Note that unlike the case of Riemann-Liouville fractional integrals, the semigroup property is not satisfied in our case. More precisely, the following properties

J_{a}^{\alpha}\left(J_{a}^{\beta}f\right)=J_{a}^{\alpha+\beta}f,\quad\alpha>0,\,\beta>0

and

J_{b}^{\alpha}\left(J_{b}^{\beta}f\right)=J_{b}^{\alpha+\beta}f,\quad\alpha>0,\,\beta>0

do not hold in general.

We have the following integration by parts result.

Theorem 2.3
.

{T3}

Let
1\leq p,q\leq\infty
be such that
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
. Given
\alpha>0
and
(f,g)\in L^{p}([a,b];\mathbb{R})\times L^{q}([a,b];\mathbb{R})
,
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
a<b
, we have

\int_{a}^{b}\left(J_{a}^{\alpha}f\right)(x)g(x)\,dx=\int_{a}^{b}\left(J_{b}^{\alpha}g\right)(x)f(x)\,dx.

Proof.

First observe that by Theorem
2.1
and the regularity assumptions imposed on
f
and
g
, we have

\left|\int_{a}^{b}\left(J_{a}^{\alpha}f\right)(x)g(x)\,dx\right|\leq\|f\|_{L^{p}([a,b];\mathbb{R})}\|g\|_{L^{q}([a,b];\mathbb{R})}<\infty.

Next, using Fubini’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\left(J_{a}^{\alpha}f\right)(x)g(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{a}^{x}E_{1}\left(\frac{x-t}{\alpha}\right)f(t)\,dt\right)g(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f(t)\left(\int_{t}^{b}\frac{1}{\alpha}E_{1}\left(\frac{x-t}{\alpha}\right)g(x)\,dx\right)\,dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f(t)\left(J_{b}^{\alpha}g\right)(t)\,dt,

which yields the desired result.
∎

Definition 2.3

(approximate identity)
.

An approximate identity is a family

\{h_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha>0}
of real valued functions such that

(i)

h_{\alpha}\geq 0
, for all
\alpha>0
.

(ii)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}h_{\alpha}(t)\,dt=1
, for all
\alpha>0
.

(iii)

For every
\delta>0
,

\lim_{\alpha\to 0^{+}}\int_{|x|>\delta}h_{\alpha}(t)\,dt=0.

Lemma 2.1
.

{HAAA}

Let
h
be a real valued function such that

(i)

h\geq 0
.

(ii)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(t)\,dt=1
.

Then, the family
\{h_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha>0}
given by

h_{\alpha}(t)=\frac{1}{\alpha}h\left(\frac{t}{\alpha}\right),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\,\alpha>0

is an approximate identity.

Lemma 2.2
.

{HAAA2}

Let
\{h_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha>0}
be an approximate identity. If
F\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
, then

F*h_{\alpha}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}),\quad\alpha>0.

Moreover, we have

\lim_{\alpha\to 0^{+}}\|F*h_{\alpha}-F\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})}=0.

Let
\widetilde{E_{1}}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be the function given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{E_{1}}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}E_{1}(x),&&\mbox{if }x>0,\\
0,&&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Observe that by (
1
), the function
\widetilde{E_{1}}
satisfies the assumptions of Lemma
2.1
. Therefore, the function
\{G_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha>0}
given by (
2
) is an approximate identity. Hence, by Lemma
2.2
, we have the following result.

Lemma 2.3
.

{HAAA3}

For every
F\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
, we have

\lim_{\alpha\to 0^{+}}\|F*G_{\alpha}-F\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})}=0.

Now, we are ready to give the proof of Theorem

Proof.

Let
f\in L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})

g_{\alpha}:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
,
\alpha>0
, be the function given by

g_{\alpha}(x)=E_{1}\left(\frac{b-x}{\alpha}\right),\quad a\leq x<b.

Then, for a.e.
x\in[a,b]
, we have

\displaystyle\left(J_{b}^{\alpha}g_{\alpha}\right)(x)=

\displaystyle 2\left[\gamma+\ln\left(\frac{b-x}{\alpha}\right)\right]e^{\frac{-(b-x)}{\alpha}}+2\left[1-\left(\frac{b-x}{\alpha}\right)\gamma-\left(\frac{b-x}{\alpha}\right)\ln\left(\frac{b-x}{\alpha}\right)\right]E_{1}\left(\frac{b-x}{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{b-x}{\alpha}\right)\left[\zeta(2)+\left(\gamma+\ln\left(\frac{b-x}{\alpha}\right)\right)^{2}\right]-2\left(\frac{b-x}{\alpha}\right)\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}}{m!m^{2}}\left(\frac{b-x}{\alpha}\right)^{m}.

3
Fractional integral operators of second kind

{sec3}

In this section, we present another class of fractional integral operators. First, let us introduce some special functions that will be used later (see, for example
[
1
]
).

We introduce the function

\mathcal{S}(t)=e^{-t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{s-1}}{\Gamma(s)}\,ds,\quad t>0.

We denote by
\gamma(\cdot,\cdot)
the lower incomplete Gamma function, given by

\gamma(s,x)=\int_{0}^{x}t^{s-1}e^{-t}\,dt,\quad s>0.\,x>0.

The regularized lower Gamma function is given by

P(s,x)=\frac{\gamma(s,x)}{\Gamma(s)},\quad s>0,\,x>0.

The derivative of
P(s,x)
with respect to
x
is given by

\frac{d}{dx}P(s,x)=\frac{x^{s-1}e^{-x}}{\Gamma(s)},\quad s>0,\,x>0.

(22)

Definition 3.1

(Left-sided fractional integral)
.

Let
f\in L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})
,
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
a<b
, be a given function. The left-sided fractional integral of order
\alpha>0
of
f
is given by

\left(S_{a}^{\alpha}f\right)(x)=\int_{a}^{x}\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{x-t}{\alpha}\right)f(t)\,dt,\quad\mbox{ a.e. }x\in[a,b].

Similarly, we define the right-sided fractional integral of order
\alpha>0
as follows.

Definition 3.2

(Right-sided fractional integral)
.

Let
f\in L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})
,
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
a<b
, be a given function. The right-sided fractional integral of order
\alpha>0
of
f
is given by

\left(S_{b}^{\alpha}f\right)(x)=\int_{x}^{b}\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{t-x}{\alpha}\right)f(t)\,dt,\quad\mbox{ a.e. }x\in[a,b].

We have the following result.

Theorem 3.1
.

{TS1}

Let
\alpha>0
and
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be such that
a<b
. Then

S_{a}^{\alpha}:L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})\to L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})

is a linear and continuous operator. Moreover, we have

\left\|S_{a}^{\alpha}f\right\|_{L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})}\leq\alpha\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}P\left(s,\frac{b-a}{\alpha}\right)\,ds\right)\|f\|_{L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})},\quad f\in L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R}).

Proof.

Let
f\in L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})
be fixed. Using Fubini’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle\left\|S_{a}^{\alpha}f\right\|_{L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\left|\left(S_{a}^{\alpha}f\right)(x)\right|\,dx

(23)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\left(\int_{a}^{x}\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{x-t}{\alpha}\right)|f(t)|\,dt\right)\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}|f(t)|\left(\int_{t}^{b}\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{x-t}{\alpha}\right)\,dx\right)\,dt.

On the other hand, for a.e.
t\in[a,b]
, using again Fubini’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{b}\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{x-t}{\alpha}\right)\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{b}\left(e^{\frac{-(x-t)}{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\frac{x-t}{\alpha}\right)^{s-1}}{\Gamma(s)}\,ds\right)\,dx

(24)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\left(\int_{t}^{b}\left(\frac{x-t}{\alpha}\right)^{s-1}e^{\frac{-(x-t)}{\alpha}}\,dx\right)\,ds.

Using the change of variable

y=\frac{x-t}{\alpha},

we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{b}\left(\frac{x-t}{\alpha}\right)^{s-1}e^{\frac{-(x-t)}{\alpha}}\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha\int_{0}^{\frac{b-t}{\alpha}}y^{s-1}e^{-y}\,dy

(25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha\gamma\left(s,\frac{b-t}{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\alpha\gamma\left(s,\frac{b-a}{\alpha}\right).

Next, combining (
24
) with (
25
), we get

\int_{t}^{b}\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{x-t}{\alpha}\right)\,dx\leq\alpha\int_{0}^{\infty}P\left(s,\frac{b-a}{\alpha}\right)\,ds.

(26)

Finally, combining (
23
) with (
26
), we get the desired result.
∎

Using a similar argument to that above, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 3.2
.

{TS2}

Let
\alpha>0
and
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be such that
a<b
. Then

S_{b}^{\alpha}:L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})\to L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})

is a linear and continuous operator. Moreover, we have

\left\|S_{b}^{\alpha}f\right\|_{L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})}\leq\alpha\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}P\left(s,\frac{b-a}{\alpha}\right)\,ds\right)\|f\|_{L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})},\quad f\in L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R}).

Note that, as in the case of fractional integral operators of first kind, the following properties

S_{a}^{\alpha}\left(S_{a}^{\beta}f\right)=S_{a}^{\alpha+\beta}f,\quad\alpha>0,\,\beta>0

and

S_{b}^{\alpha}\left(S_{b}^{\beta}f\right)=S_{b}^{\alpha+\beta}f,\quad\alpha>0,\,\beta>0

do not hold in general.

Further, using a similar argument to that used in the proof of Theorem
2.3
, we obtain the following integration by parts result.

Theorem 3.3
.

{TS3}

Given
\alpha>0
and
(f,g)\in L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})\times L^{\infty}([a,b];\mathbb{R})
,
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
a<b
, we have

\int_{a}^{b}\left(S_{a}^{\alpha}f\right)(x)g(x)\,dx=\int_{a}^{b}\left(S_{b}^{\alpha}g\right)(x)f(x)\,dx.

Next, we shall study the relation between the fractional integral operators of first and second kind. First, we need the following result, which can be easily proved.

Lemma 3.1
.

{Laplace}

We have

\left(\mathcal{L}E_{1}\right)(\lambda)=\frac{\ln(1+\lambda)}{\lambda},\quad\lambda>0

and

\left(\mathcal{L}\mathcal{S}\right)(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\ln(1+\lambda)},\quad\lambda>0,

where
\mathcal{L}
is the Laplace transform operator.

We have the following result

Theorem 3.4
.

{THAYA}

Let
\alpha>0
and
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be such that
a<b
. Then, for every
f\in L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})
, we have

J_{a}^{\alpha}\left(S_{a}^{\alpha}f\right)(x)=S_{a}^{\alpha}\left(J_{a}^{\alpha}f\right)(x)=\int_{a}^{x}f(y)\,dy,\quad\mbox{ a.e. }x\in[a,b].

Proof.

Let
f\in L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})
be fixed. Using Fubini’s theorem, for a.e.
x\in[a,b]
, we have

\displaystyle J_{a}^{\alpha}\left(S_{a}^{\alpha}f\right)(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{a}^{x}E_{1}\left(\frac{x-t}{\alpha}\right)\left(S_{a}^{\alpha}f\right)(t)\,dt

(27)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{a}^{x}E_{1}\left(\frac{x-t}{\alpha}\right)\left(\int_{a}^{t}\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{t-y}{\alpha}\right)f(y)\,dy\right)\,dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{x}f(y)\left(\int_{y}^{x}\frac{1}{\alpha}E_{1}\left(\frac{x-t}{\alpha}\right)\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{t-y}{\alpha}\right)\,dt\right)\,dy.

Using the change of variable

z=\frac{t-y}{\alpha},\quad a<y<x,

we obtain

\int_{y}^{x}\frac{1}{\alpha}E_{1}\left(\frac{x-t}{\alpha}\right)\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{t-y}{\alpha}\right)\,dt=\int_{0}^{\frac{x-y}{\alpha}}E_{1}\left(\frac{x-y}{\alpha}-z\right)\mathcal{S}(z)\,dz,

i.e,

\int_{y}^{x}\frac{1}{\alpha}E_{1}\left(\frac{x-t}{\alpha}\right)\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{t-y}{\alpha}\right)\,dt=\left(E_{1}*\mathcal{S}\right)\left(\frac{x-y}{\alpha}\right).

(28)

On the other hand, using Lemma
3.1
, we have

\mathcal{L}\left(E_{1}*\mathcal{S}\right)(\lambda)=\mathcal{L}(E_{1})(\lambda)\,\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{S})(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\lambda}=\mathcal{L}(1)(\lambda),\quad\lambda>0,

which yields

\left(E_{1}*\mathcal{S}\right)(\lambda)=1,\quad\lambda>0.

(29)

Finally, combining (
27
), (
28
) and (
29
), we obtain

J_{a}^{\alpha}\left(S_{a}^{\alpha}f\right)(x)=\int_{a}^{x}f(y)\,dy,\quad\mbox{ a.e. }x\in[a,b].

Note that due to the symmetry of the convolution product, it can be easily seen that

J_{a}^{\alpha}\left(S_{a}^{\alpha}f\right)(x)=S_{a}^{\alpha}\left(J_{a}^{\alpha}f\right)(x),\quad\mbox{ a.e. }x\in[a,b].

This makes end to the proof.
∎

Using a similar argument to that above, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 3.5
.

{THAYA2}

Let
\alpha>0
and
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be such that
a<b
. Then, for every
f\in L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})
, we have

J_{b}^{\alpha}\left(S_{b}^{\alpha}f\right)(x)=S_{b}^{\alpha}\left(J_{b}^{\alpha}f\right)(x)=\int_{x}^{b}f(y)\,dy,\quad\mbox{ a.e. }x\in[a,b].

Further, we shall prove the following approximation result.

Theorem 3.6
.

{TASS}

Let
f\in L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})
,
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
a<b
. Then

\lim_{\alpha\to 0^{+}}\left\|S_{a}^{\alpha}f-I_{a}f\right\|_{L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})}=0,

where

\left(I_{a}f\right)(x)=\int_{a}^{x}f(y)\,dy,\quad a<x\leq b.

In order to prove Theorem
3.6
, we need the following auxiliary result.

Lemma 3.2
.

{aux}

Given
\alpha_{0}>0
, we have

\sup_{0<\alpha<\alpha_{0}}\alpha\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\mathcal{S}(y)\,dy<\infty.

Proof.

First, by (
1
), there exists a certain
\delta>0
such that

\mu(z)>\frac{1}{2},\quad z\geq\delta,

(30)

where

\mu(z)=\int_{0}^{z}E_{1}(y)\,dy,\quad z>0.

Next, let
0<\alpha<\alpha_{0}
be fixed. Taking
\beta>0
such that

\frac{1}{\beta}=\frac{1}{\alpha}+\delta

and using (
29
), we obtain

\int_{0}^{\frac{x}{\beta}}E_{1}\left(\frac{x}{\beta}-t\right)\mathcal{S}(t)\,dt=1,\quad x>0.

Integrating over
[0,1]
, using Fubini’s theorem and the change of variable

y=\frac{x}{\beta}-t,

we obtain

\beta\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{\beta}}\mathcal{S}(t)\mu\left(\frac{1}{\beta}-t\right)\,dt=1,

i.e.,

\beta\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{\beta}}\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{1}{\beta}-t\right)\mu(t)\,dt=1,

which yields

\beta\int_{\delta}^{\frac{1}{\beta}}\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{1}{\beta}-t\right)\mu(t)\,dt\leq 1.

Using the above inequality and (
30
), we get

\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{\delta}^{\frac{1}{\beta}}\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{1}{\beta}-t\right)\,dt\leq 1.

Next, the change of variable

y=\frac{1}{\beta}-t

leads to

\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{\beta}-\delta}\mathcal{S}(y)\,dy\leq 1,

i.e.,

\alpha\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\mathcal{S}(y)\,dy\leq 2(1+\alpha\delta)\leq 2(1+\alpha_{0}\delta),

which proves the desired result.
∎

Now, we are ready to give the proof of Theorem
3.6
.

Proof.

Let
f\in L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})
and
\alpha>0
be small enough. Using Theorem
3.4
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|S_{a}^{\alpha}f-I_{a}f\right\|_{L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|S_{a}^{\alpha}f-S_{a}^{\alpha}\left(J_{a}^{\alpha}f\right)\right\|_{L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})}

(31)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|S_{a}^{\alpha}\left(J_{a}^{\alpha}f-f\right)\right\|_{L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})}.

On the other hand, by Theorem
3.1
, we have

\left\|S_{a}^{\alpha}\left(J_{a}^{\alpha}f-f\right)\right\|_{L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})}\leq\alpha\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}P\left(s,\frac{b-a}{\alpha}\right)\,ds\right)\left\|J_{a}^{\alpha}f-f\right\|_{L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})}.

(32)

Using Fubini’s theorem, it can be easily seen that

\int_{0}^{\infty}P\left(s,\frac{b-a}{\alpha}\right)\,ds=\int_{0}^{\frac{b-a}{\alpha}}\mathcal{S}(t)\,dt.

Therefore, from (
32
), we get

\left\|S_{a}^{\alpha}\left(J_{a}^{\alpha}f-f\right)\right\|_{L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})}\leq\alpha\left(\int_{0}^{\frac{b-a}{\alpha}}\mathcal{S}(t)\,dt\right)\left\|J_{a}^{\alpha}f-f\right\|_{L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})}.

Next, by Lemma
3.2
, there exists a certain constant
C>0
(independent on
\alpha
) such that

\left\|S_{a}^{\alpha}\left(J_{a}^{\alpha}f-f\right)\right\|_{L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})}\leq C\left\|J_{a}^{\alpha}f-f\right\|_{L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})}.

(33)

Combining (
31
) with (
33
), we get

\left\|S_{a}^{\alpha}f-I_{a}f\right\|_{L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})}\leq C\left\|J_{a}^{\alpha}f-f\right\|_{L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})}.

Finally, passing to the limit as
\alpha\to 0^{+}
, and using Theorem
2.4
, the desired result follows.
∎

In the case of a constant function

{exS1}

4
Fractional derivative operators

{sec4}

In this section, we introduce a new class of fractional derivative operators involving the fractional integrals of first kind. As we will show later, these new fractional derivatives are inverse operations for the fractional integrals of second kind.

First, given
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
a<b
, we denote by

AC([a,b];\mathbb{R})
the space of real valued and absolutely continuous functions in
[a,b]
. Recall that
f\in AC([a,b];\mathbb{R})
if and only if there exist
(c,\varphi)\in\mathbb{R}\times L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})
such that

f(x)=c+\int_{a}^{x}\varphi(t)\,dt,\quad x\in[a,b].

In this case, we have

c=f(a)

and

\frac{df}{dx}(x)=\varphi(x),\quad\mbox{ a.e. }x\in[a,b].

Definition 4.1
.

Let
f\in L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})
,
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
a<b
. We say that
f
has an absolutely continuous representative, if there exists a function
\psi\in AC([a,b];\mathbb{R})
such that

f(x)=\psi(x),\quad\mbox{ a.e. }x\in[a,b].

In this case, the function
f
is identified to its absolutely continuous representative
\psi
.

Definition 4.2

(Left-sided fractional derivative)
.

Let
f\in L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})
,
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
a<b
, be a given function such that
J_{a}^{\alpha}f
,
\alpha>0
, has an absolutely continuous representative.
The left-sided fractional derivative of order
\alpha
of
f
is given by

\left(\mathcal{D}_{a}^{\alpha}f\right)(x)=\frac{d}{dx}\left(J_{a}^{\alpha}f\right)(x),\quad\mbox{ a.e. }x\in[a,b].

Similarly, we define the right-sided fractional derivative of order
\alpha>0
as follows.

Definition 4.3

(Right-sided fractional derivative)
.

Let
f\in L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})
,
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
a<b
, be a given function such that
J_{b}^{\alpha}f
,
\alpha>0
, has an absolutely continuous representative. The right-sided fractional derivative of order
\alpha
of
f
is given by

\left(\mathcal{D}_{b}^{\alpha}f\right)(x)=\frac{d}{dx}\left(J_{b}^{\alpha}f\right)(x),\quad\mbox{ a.e. }x\in[a,b].

Next, let us consider the functional space
V([a,b];\mathbb{R})
,
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
a<b
, given by

V([a,b];\mathbb{R})=\left\{u\in C^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R}):\,u(a)=\frac{du}{dx}(a)=0,\,u(b)=\frac{du}{dx}(b)=0\right\}.

We have the following approximation result.

Theorem 4.1
.

{TAD}

Given
f\in V([a,b];\mathbb{R})
,
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
a<b
, we have

\lim_{\alpha\to 0^{+}}\left\|\mathcal{D}_{a}^{\alpha}f-\frac{df}{dx}\right\|_{L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})}=0.

Before giving the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we need the following standard result form Harmonic Analysis (see, for example
[
5
]
).

Lemma 4.1
.

{finj}

Let
\mu\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
and
\nu\in C_{b}^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
, where
C_{b}^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
denotes the set of functions
u\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
such that
u
and
\frac{du}{dx}
are bounded. Then
\mu*\nu\in C_{b}^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
and

\frac{d(\mu*\nu)}{dx}(x)=\frac{d(\nu*\mu)}{dx}(x)=\left(\mu*\frac{d\nu}{dx}\right)(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Now, we are ready to give the proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Proof.

Let
f\in V([a,b];\mathbb{R})
be fixed. We introduce the function
F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\displaystyle F(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}f(x),&&\mbox{if }x\in[a,b],\\
0,&&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

It can be easily seen that
F\in C_{b}^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
. On the other hand, by (
11
), we have

(F*G_{\alpha})(x)=\left(J_{a}^{\alpha}f\right)(x),\quad\mbox{ a.e. }x\in[a,b],

where
G_{\alpha}
is given by (
2
). Next, using Lemma
4.1
, we have

\frac{d(F*G_{\alpha})}{dx}(x)=\left(G_{\alpha}*\frac{dF}{dx}\right)(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

which yields

\frac{d}{dx}\left(J_{a}^{\alpha}f\right)(x)=\left(G_{\alpha}*\frac{dF}{dx}\right)(x),\quad\mbox{ a.e. }x\in[a,b],

i.e.,

\left(\mathcal{D}_{a}^{\alpha}f\right)(x)=\left(G_{\alpha}*\frac{dF}{dx}\right)(x),\quad\mbox{ a.e. }x\in[a,b].

(37)

Moreover, using a similar argument as in the proof of Theorem
2.4
, we have

\left(G_{\alpha}*\frac{dF}{dx}\right)(x)=\left(J_{a}^{\alpha}\frac{df}{dx}\right)(x),\quad\mbox{ a.e. }x\in[a,b].

(38)

Combining (
37
) with (
38
), we obtain

\left(\mathcal{D}_{a}^{\alpha}f\right)(x)=\left(J_{a}^{\alpha}\frac{df}{dx}\right)(x),\quad\mbox{ a.e. }x\in[a,b].

(39)

Finally, since
\frac{df}{dx}\in L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})
, using (
39
) and Theorem
2.4
, the desired result follows.
∎

Using a similar argument to that above, we obtain the following approximation result for
D_{b}^{\alpha}f
.

Theorem 4.2
.

{TAD2}

Given

,
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
a<b

\lim_{\alpha\to 0^{+}}\left\|\mathcal{D}_{b}^{\alpha}f-\frac{df}{dx}\right\|_{L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R})}=0.

Next, we shall discuss the relation between the fractional integral operators of second kind and the fractional derivative operators introduced above.

Theorem 4.3
.

{TINV1}

i.e.,

∎

Theorem 4.4
.

{TINV2}

Let
f\in L^{1}([a,b];\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
a<b
, and
\alpha>0
. Then

D_{b}^{\alpha}\left(S_{b}^{\alpha}f\right)(x)=-f(x),\quad\mbox{ a.e. }x\in[a,b].

Proof.

Using Theorem
3.5
, we have

J_{b}^{\alpha}\left(S_{b}^{\alpha}f\right)(x)=\int_{x}^{b}f(y)\,dy,\quad\mbox{ a.e. }x\in[a,b].

∎

{TTK}

5
A fractional relaxation equation

{sec5}

The standard form of a relaxation equation is given by

\frac{du(t)}{dt}+\lambda u(t)=f(t),

(48)

where
\lambda
is a positive constant and
f
is a given function (see, for example
[
7
,
17
]
). Equation (
48
) models several physical phenomena, such as the Maxwell model, which describes the behavior of a viscoelastic material using a spring and a dashpot in series. In this case,
\lambda=\frac{E}{\eta}
, where
E
is the elastic
modulus,
\eta
is the viscosity coefficient, and
f(t)
denotes
E
multiplying the strain rate. In this section, we are concerned with the existence and uniqueness of solutions to the fractional relaxation equation

\begin{gathered}\left(\mathcal{D}_{0}^{\alpha}u\right)(t)+\lambda u(t)=f(t,u(t)),\quad\mbox{ a.e. }t\in[0,1],\\
\left(J_{0}^{\alpha}u\right)(0)=0,\end{gathered}

(49)

where
\alpha>0
,
\lambda
is a positive constant and
f:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a given function. Moreover, we provide an iterative algorithm that converges to the solution.

Definition 5.1
.

{desol}

We say that
u
is a solution to (
49
) if
u\in C([0,1];\mathbb{R})
is such that
J_{0}^{\alpha}u
admits an absolutely continuous representative and
u
satisfies (
49
).

Equation (
49
) is investigated under the following assumptions:

(A1)

f:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous.

(A2)

For all
(t,x_{1},x_{2})\in[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
, we have

|f(t,x_{1})-f(t,x_{2})|\leq C_{f}|x_{1}-x_{2}|,

where
C_{f}>0
is a certain constant satisfying

\alpha\left(\lambda+C_{f}\right)\int_{0}^{\infty}P\left(s,\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\,ds<1.

(50)

The functional space
C([0,1];\mathbb{R})
is equipped with the Chebyshev norm

\|u\|_{\infty}=\max\{|u(t)|:\,0\leq t\leq 1\},\quad u\in C([0,1];\mathbb{R}).

Further, given
h\in C([0,1];\mathbb{R})
, we define

\displaystyle(Th)(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{t-y}{\alpha}\right)h(y)\,dy,&\mbox{ if }&0<t\leq 1,\\
\\
0&\mbox{ if }&t=0.\end{array}\right.

Lemma 5.1
.

{LLJJ}

We have

T\left(C([0,1];\mathbb{R})\right)\subset C([0,1];\mathbb{R}).

Proof.

Let
h\in C([0,1];\mathbb{R})
be fixed. We have

|(Th)(t)|\leq\|h\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{t-y}{\alpha}\right)\,dy,\quad 0<t\leq 1,

i.e.,

|(Th)(t)|\leq\alpha\|h\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{\frac{t}{\alpha}}\mathcal{S}(z)\,dz,\quad 0<t\leq 1.

Passing to the limit as
t\to 0^{+}
, and using the dominated convergence theorem, we obtain

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}(Th)(t)=0,

which proves that
Th
is continuous at
0
. Further, let
r>0
be small enough. We have

\displaystyle\left|(Th)(1)-(Th)(1-r)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{1-y}{\alpha}\right)h(y)\,dy-\int_{0}^{1-r}\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{1-r-y}{\alpha}\right)h(y)\,dy\right|

\displaystyle=\alpha\left|\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\mathcal{S}(z)h(1-\alpha z)\,dz-\int_{0}^{\frac{1-r}{\alpha}}\mathcal{S}(z)h(1-r-\alpha z)\,dz\right|

\displaystyle=\alpha\left|\int_{0}^{\frac{1-r}{\alpha}}\mathcal{S}(z)\left[h(1-\alpha z)-h(1-r-\alpha z)\right]\,dz+\int_{\frac{1-r}{\alpha}}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\mathcal{S}(z)h(1-\alpha z)\,dz\right|

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\left(\int_{0}^{\frac{1-r}{\alpha}}\mathcal{S}(z)\left|h(1-\alpha z)-h(1-r-\alpha z)\right|\,dz+\int_{\frac{1-r}{\alpha}}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\mathcal{S}(z)|h(1-\alpha z)|\,dz\right)

\displaystyle:=\alpha\left(K_{1}(r)+K_{2}(r)\right).

(55)

On the other hand, we have

K_{1}(r)\leq\omega(r,h)\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\mathcal{S}(z)\,dz,

(56)

where
\omega(\cdot,h)
is the modulus of continuity of
h
, i.e.,

\omega(r,h)=\sup\left\{|h(x)-h(y)|:\,(x,y)\in[0,1]\times[0,1],\,|x-y|\leq r\right\},\quad r>0.

Note that since
h
is uniformly continuous in
[0,1]
, we have

\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\omega(r,h)=0.

Therefore, passing to the limit as
r\to 0^{+}
in (
56
), we obtain

\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}K_{1}(r)=0.

(57)

Further, we have

K_{2}(r)\leq\|h\|_{\infty}\int_{\frac{1-r}{\alpha}}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\mathcal{S}(z)\,dz.

Passing to the limit as
r\to 0^{+}
, and using the dominated convergence theorem, we obtain

\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}K_{2}(r)=0.

(58)

Using (
5
), (
57
) and (
58
), we obtain

\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\left|(Th)(1)-(Th)(1-r)\right|=0,

which proves that
Th
is continuous at
1
. Using a similar argument as above, we can show that
Th
is continuous at any
t\in]0,1[
. Therefore, we have

Th\in C([0,1];\mathbb{R}).

∎

Suppose now that
u\in C([0,1];\mathbb{R})

4.7

By continuity (see Lemma

u(t)=T\left(-\lambda u+f(\cdot,u(\cdot))\right)(t):=(Fu)(t),\quad t\in[0,1].

(59)

Conversely, suppose that

Observe that

{RESLT}

Lemma 5.3

{CNTM}

Under the assumptions (A1) and (A2), for all
(u,v)\in C([0,1];\mathbb{R})\times C([0,1];\mathbb{R})

where

Proof.

Let
(u,v)\in C([0,1];\mathbb{R})\times C([0,1];\mathbb{R})

|(Fu)(t)-(Fv)(t)|=\left|T\left(-\lambda u+f(\cdot,u(\cdot))\right)(t)-T\left(-\lambda v+f(\cdot,v(\cdot))\right)(t)\right|,

where

\displaystyle|(Fu)(t)-(Fv)(t)|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{t-y}{\alpha}\right)\left(-\lambda u(y)+f(y,u(y))\right)\,dy-\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{t-y}{\alpha}\right)\left(-\lambda v(y)+f(y,v(y))\right)\,dy\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\lambda+C_{f}\right)\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{t-y}{\alpha}\right)\,dy\right)\|u-v\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(\lambda+C_{f}\right)\left(\int_{0}^{\frac{t}{\alpha}}\mathcal{S}(z)\,dz\right)\|u-v\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\left(\lambda+C_{f}\right)\left(\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\mathcal{S}(z)\,dz\right)\|u-v\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(\lambda+C_{f}\right)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}P\left(s,\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\,ds\right)\|u-v\|_{\infty},

which proves the desired result.
∎

Now, we are able to state and prove the following existence result.

Theorem 5.1
.

{EXISTENCE}

Under the assumptions (A1) and (A2), Problem (
49
) admits one and only one solution
u^{*}\in C([0,1];\mathbb{R})
in the sense of Definition
5.1
. Moreover, for every
u_{0}\in C([0,1];\mathbb{R})
, the Picard sequence
\{u_{n}\}\subset C([0,1];\mathbb{R})
, given by

\displaystyle u_{n+1}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{t-y}{\alpha}\right)\big[-\lambda u_{n}(y)+f(y,u_{n}(y))\big]\,dy,&\mbox{ if }&0<t\leq 1,\\
\\
0&\mbox{ if }&t=0\end{array}\right.;\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

converges uniformly to
u^{*}
.

Proof.

From Lemma
5.3
and (
50
), the mapping
F:C([0,1];\mathbb{R})\to C([0,1];\mathbb{R})
is a contraction. Therefore, by Banach contraction principle, the mapping
F
has one and only one solution
u^{*}\in C([0,1];\mathbb{R})
. Moreover, for every
u_{0}\in C([0,1];\mathbb{R})
, the Picard sequence
\{u_{n}\}\subset C([0,1];\mathbb{R})
, given by

u_{n+1}=Fu_{n},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

converges to
u^{*}
with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
. Finally, by Lemma
5.2
, the desired result follows.
∎
