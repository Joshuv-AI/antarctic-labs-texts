Univalent Enriched Categories and the Enriched Rezk Completion
Niels van der Weide ORCID:0000-0003-1146-4161

Abstract
. Enriched categories are categories whose sets of morphisms are enriched with extra structure. Such categories play a prominent role in the study of higher categories, homotopy theory, and the semantics of programming languages. In this paper, we study univalent enriched categories. We prove that all essentially surjective and fully faithful functors between univalent enriched categories are equivalences, and we show that every enriched category admits a Rezk completion. Finally, we use the Rezk completion for enriched categories to construct univalent enriched Kleisli categories.

Introduction

Over the years, category theory
[
ML13
]
has established itself as a powerful mathematical framework with a wide variety of applications.
The applications of category theory range from pure mathematics

This study resulted in the development of various notions of categories.

One of these notions is given by
enriched categories

Enriched categories are categories whose morphisms are equipped with additional structure.
Examples of such categories are plentiful.
For instance, in the study of the semantics of effectful programming languages,
one uses categories enriched over

(DCPOs)
[

The type of morphisms in categories enriched over DCPOs is given by a DCPO,
and thus fixpoint equations of morphisms can be solved in such categories

For similar purposes, categories enriched over partial orders have been used
[

Other applications of enriched categories appear in homological algebra
[

where one is interested in categories enriched over abelian groups,

[

]

where one looks at categories enriched over simplicial sets,
and higher category theory
[

]

where one consider categories enriched over categories.

Univalent Foundations.

Throughout this paper, we work in univalent foundations
[
Rij22
,
Uni13
]
.
Univalent foundations is an extension of dependent Martin-Löf Type Theory
[
Mar84
]
with the
univalence axiom
.
This axiom says that the identity of types is the same as equivalences between them.
More specifically, we have a map that sends identities
A=B
to equivalences
A\simeq B
,
and the univalence axiom states that this map is an equivalence.
Concretely, this means that properties of types are invariant under equivalence
and that two types share the same properties whenever we have an equivalence between them.

Univalent foundations is especially interesting for the study of category theory.
In category theory, objects are generally viewed up to isomorphism:
whenever there is an isomorphism between two objects, they share the same categorical properties.
This is known as the
principle of equivalence
,
and this principle is made precise using
univalent categories
.

Univalent Categories.

In univalent foundations, the “correct” notion of category is given by univalent categories.
Given two objects
x
and
y
in a category,
we have a map sending identities
x=y
to isomorphisms
x\cong y
.
In a univalent category, this mapping is required to be an equivalence:
identities between objects are thus the same as isomorphisms.
Hence, whenever two objects are isomorphic, they satisfy the same properties.
Semantically, this is the correct notion of category,
because in the simplicial set model, univalent categories correspond to set-theoretic categories
[
KL21
]
.

There are several consequences of univalence for categories.
For instance, we have a
structure identity principle
for univalent categories.
This principle says that the identity type of two categories is equivalent to the type of adjoint equivalences between them
[
AKS15
]
.
As a consequence, one gets that whenever two categories are equivalent, then they have the same properties.
Another consequence is that every essentially surjective fully faithful functor
is an adjoint equivalence as well.
Usually, one uses the axiom of choice to prove this principle,
but if the domain is univalent, then one can constructively prove this fact.
Finally, every category is weakly equivalent to a univalent one, called its
Rezk completion

[
AKS15
]
.

While most categories that one encounters in practice are univalent (e.g., Eilenberg-Moore categories and functor categories),
some are not.
An example is given by the Kleisli category.
Usually, the Kleisli category
\mathsf{K}(\mathsf{T})
of a monad
\mathsf{T}
on a category
\mathsf{C}
is defined to be the category
whose objects are objects of
\mathsf{C}

and whose morphisms from
x
to
y
are morphisms
x\rightarrow\mathsf{T}\>y
in
\mathsf{C}
.
However, this does not give rise to a univalent category in general.
One can give an alternative presentation of the Kleisli category as a full subcategory
\mathsf{Kleisli}(\mathsf{T})
of the Eilenberg-Moore category to obtain a univalent category
[
ANST21
]
.
To prove the desired theorems about
\mathsf{Kleisli}(\mathsf{T})
, one uses that it is the Rezk completion of
\mathsf{K}(\mathsf{T})

[
Wei23
]
.

Univalent Enriched Categories.

In this paper, we develop enriched category theory in univalent foundations.
More specifically, we define univalent enriched categories,
and we prove analogous theorems for univalent enriched categories as for univalent categories.
We show that univalent enriched categories satisfy a structure identity principle,
that every essentially surjective fully faithful functor is an adjoint equivalence,
and that every enriched category admits a Rezk completion.
We also use these theorems to construct univalent enriched Kleisli categories.

The Bicategory of Enriched Categories

In the remainder of this paper, we study univalent enriched categories,
and in this section, we discuss a structure identity principle for univalent enriched categories,
which says that identity of univalent enriched categories is the same as equivalence.
Before we do so, we briefly recall
monoidal categories
to fix the notation for the remainder of the paper

{defi}

A

consists of a category
\mathsf{V}
together with

•

an object
\mathbb{1}_{\mathsf{V}}:\mathsf{V}
;

•

a bifunctor
-\otimes-:\mathsf{V}\times\mathsf{V}\rightarrow\mathsf{V}

•

\mathsf{r}_{x}:x\otimes\mathbb{1}_{V}\rightarrow x

and
\mathsf{a}_{x,y,z}:(x\otimes y)\otimes z\rightarrow x\otimes(y\otimes z)

such that
\mathsf{l}_{-}
,
\mathsf{r}_{-}
, and
\mathsf{a}_{-,-,-}
are natural and such that the triangle and pentagon laws hold.

A
symmetric monoidal category
is a monoidal category
\mathsf{V}
together with morphisms
\mathsf{s}_{x,y}:x\otimes y\rightarrow y\otimes x
,
such that
\mathsf{s}_{x,y}

A
symmetric monoidal closed category
is a symmetric monoidal category
\mathsf{V}

such that for every
x:\mathsf{V}
the functor

has a right adjoint.

If we have a symmetric monoidal closed category,
then we have internal homs
x\multimap y
.
We also have evaluation morphisms
\epsilon_{x,y}:(x\multimap y)\otimes x\rightarrow y
,
and an internal lambda abstraction operation
\lambda(f):x\rightarrow y\multimap z
for every

.

Usually, a
\mathsf{V}
-enriched category consists of a collection objects together with a hom-object
\mathcal{E}(x,y)
in
\mathsf{V}
for all
x
and

such that we have appropriate identity morphisms and a composition operation
[

Every enriched category
\mathcal{E}
has an underlying category, which has the same collection of objects
and whose morphisms from
x
to

are the same as morphisms

in
\mathsf{V}

However, we take a slightly different approach: we use

.
An enrichment for a category
\mathsf{C}
consists of a hom-object
\mathcal{E}(x,y)
in
\mathsf{V}
for all
x
and
y
,
such that we have the appropriate identity and composition morphisms
and such that
\mathsf{C}
is equivalent to the underlying category of the corresponding enriched category.
As such, we view an enriched category as a category together with extra structure.
This viewpoint also determines our notion of univalence for enriched categories:
a univalent enriched category is an enriched category such that its underlying category is univalent.
Note that our notion of univalence is similar to completeness for enriched
\infty
-categories
[
GH15
]
.
Using enrichments, we can equivalently phrase univalent enriched categories as a univalent category together with an enrichment.

Proposition

For a monoidal category

the type of categories together with a

The reason why we use enrichments over the usual definition,
is because it simplifies the proof of the structure identity principle for enriched categories.
A structure identity principle is already present for univalent categories

which can be reused directly if we define enriched categories as pairs of univalent categories together with an enrichment.
However, if we would use the definition in

then reusing this principle would be more cumbersome

To phrase the structure identity principle for enriched categories,
we use

More specifically, this principle for enriched categories is expressed by saying that the bicategory of enriched categories is univalent.
We define the bicategory of enriched categories using

A displayed bicategory over a bicategory

represents structures and properties to be added to objects, 1-cells, and 2-cells in

In our case, we define a displayed bicategory

and then

The displayed objects over a univalent category

are
\mathsf{V}
-enrichments for
\mathsf{C}

the displayed 1-cells over a functor are enrichments for functors,
and the displayed 2-cells over a natural transformation are proofs that this transformation is enriched.

Note that from the machinery of displayed bicategories,
we get a pseudofunctor

to be an object in the fiber of

The
bicategory of enriched categories
is defined to be the total bicategory of
\mathsf{dEnrichCat}_{\mathsf{V}}
,
and we denote it by
\mathsf{EnrichCat}_{\mathsf{V}}
.
Its objects are
univalent
\mathsf{V}
-enriched categories
, and we call the 1-cells and 2-cells of
\mathsf{EnrichCat}_{\mathsf{V}}

enriched functors
and
enriched transformations
respectively.

Note that by construction a univalent
\mathsf{V}
-enriched category is the same as a univalent category together with a
\mathsf{V}
-enrichment.
In addition, our univalence condition for
\mathsf{V}
-enriched categories has no local variant in contrast to univalence for bicategories
[
AFM+22
]
,
since we only look at enrichments over monoidal 1-categories.

To show that
section

2
actually gives rise to a displayed bicategory,
one also needs to construct enrichments for the identity and composition,
and one needs to prove that the identity transformation is enriched
and that enriched transformations are preserved under composition and whiskering.
Details on this construction are left to the formalization.

Proposition
\thethm
.

Let
\tau:\mathcal{F}_{1}\Rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{2}
be a 2-cell in
\mathsf{EnrichCat}_{\mathsf{V}}
.
Then
\tau
is invertible if the underlying natural transformation of
\tau
is a natural isomorphism.

Proof.

Suppose that we have enriched functors
\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}:\mathcal{E}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}

and an enriched natural transformation
\tau:\mathcal{F}_{1}\Rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{2}

whose underlying natural transformation is a pointwise isomorphism.
We need to construct another enriched natural transformation
\tau^{-1}:\mathcal{F}_{2}\Rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{1}

such that
\tau
and
\tau^{-1}
compose to the identity.
Given
x:\mathcal{E}_{1}
,
we define
\tau^{-1}(x)
to be the inverse of
\tau(x)
.
To prove naturality,
it suffices to show that the following diagram commutes.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{1}(x,y)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{2}(\mathsf{F}_{2}\>x,\mathsf{F}_{2}\>y)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{2}(\mathsf{F}_{1}\>x,\mathsf{F}_{1}\>y)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{2}(\mathsf{F}_{2}\>x,\mathsf{F}_{1}\>y)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{F}_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{F}_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\tau^{-1}\>x)^{\mathsf{pre}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\tau^{-1}\>y)^{\mathsf{post}}}

This holds because
\tau
is an enriched transformation,
and because
(\tau^{-1}\>x)^{\mathsf{pre}}=((\tau\>x)^{\mathsf{pre}})^{-1}
.
∎

Theorem
\thethm
.

If
\mathsf{V}
is a univalent monoidal category,
then the bicategory
\mathsf{EnrichCat}_{\mathsf{V}}
is univalent.

Proof.

It suffices to verify that the displayed bicategory
\mathsf{dEnrichCat}_{\mathsf{V}}
is both locally and globally univalent
[
AFM+22
, Theorem 7.4]
.
To see that
\mathsf{dEnrichCat}_{\mathsf{V}}
is locally univalent,
we need to show that identities of enrichments
\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}:\mathcal{E}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}
for a functor
\mathsf{F}

are the same as invertible 2-cells from
\mathcal{F}_{1}
to
\mathcal{F}_{2}
over the identity natural transformation.
We first note that we have
\mathcal{F}_{1}=\mathcal{F}_{2}
if and only if we have
\mathcal{F}_{1}=\mathcal{F}_{2}
for all
x,y:\mathcal{E}_{1}
,
and that invertible 2-cells over the identity are the same as enrichments for the identity by
Section

2
.
Using
eq.

1
, we see that the desired enrichment exists if and only if
\mathcal{F}_{1}=\mathcal{F}_{2}
for all
x,y:\mathcal{E}_{1}
,
which concludes the proof that
\mathsf{dEnrichCat}_{\mathsf{V}}
is locally univalent.

Next we verify that
\mathsf{dEnrichCat}_{\mathsf{V}}
is globally univalent,
meaning that we need to show that identities between enrichments
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
over a category
\mathsf{C}

are equivalent to adjoint equivalences between
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
over the identity.
Since
\mathsf{V}
is univalent, identities between
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
correspond to isomorphisms

i_{x,y}:\mathcal{E}_{1}(x,y)\cong\mathcal{E}_{2}(x,y)
for all
x,y:\mathsf{C}

making the following diagrams commute.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{1}_{V}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{1}(x,x)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{2}(x,x)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{id}^{\mathsf{e}}_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}(x)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{id}^{\mathsf{e}}_{\mathcal{E}_{2}}(x)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i_{x,x}}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{1}_{V}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{1}(x,y)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{2}(x,y)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overrightarrow{f}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overrightarrow{f}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i_{x,y}}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{1}(y,z)\otimes\mathcal{E}_{1}(x,y)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{2}(y,z)\otimes\mathcal{E}_{2}(x,y)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{1}(x,z)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{2}(x,z)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i_{y,z}\otimes i_{x,y}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{comp}_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}(x,y,z)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{comp}_{\mathcal{E}_{2}}(x,y,z)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i_{x,z}}

In addition,
for every morphism
f:\mathbb{1}_{V}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}(x,y)

we have that
\overleftarrow{f}=\overleftarrow{f\cdot i_{x,y}}
.
The inverse
i_{x,y}^{-1}
satisfies similar equalities.
Identities
\mathcal{E}_{1}=\mathcal{E}_{2}

give rise to enriched functors
\mathcal{F}_{1}:\mathcal{E}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}
and
\mathcal{F}_{2}:\mathcal{E}_{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}

such that
\mathcal{F}_{1}(x,y)=i_{x,y}
and
\mathcal{F}_{2}(x,y)=i_{x,y}^{-1}
.
This allows us to conclude that
\mathsf{dEnrichCat}_{\mathsf{V}}
is globally univalent.
∎

Finally, we look at the change of base operation for enriched categories,
and for this operation, a subtlety arises.
Given a lax monoidal functor
\mathsf{F}:\mathsf{V}_{1}\rightarrow\mathsf{V}_{2}
,
our goal is to define a pseudofunctor
\mathsf{EnrichCat}_{\mathsf{V}_{1}}\rightarrow\mathsf{EnrichCat}_{\mathsf{V}_{2}}
.
On objects, this operation acts as follows:
given a univalent category
\mathsf{C}
together with an enrichment
\mathcal{E}
,
then we get an enriched category
\mathsf{F}^{*}(\mathcal{E})
whose objects are objects in
\mathsf{C}

and such that
\mathsf{F}^{*}(\mathcal{E})(x,y)=\mathsf{F}\>(\mathcal{E}(x,y))
.
However, the underlying category of this enriched category is
not
necessarily univalent.
For instance, if we take
\mathsf{F}
to be the unique monoidal functor from
\mathsf{Set}
to the terminal category,
then the underlying category of
\mathsf{F}^{*}(\mathcal{E})
would have sets as objects,
and inhabitants of the unit type as the morphisms.
For this reason,
we add a restriction to
\mathsf{F}
in order to define the change of base of enriched categories.

{defi}

Let
\mathsf{F}:\mathsf{V}_{1}\rightarrow\mathsf{V}_{2}
by a lax monoidal functor.
We say that
\mathsf{F}

preserves underlying categories

if for all
x:\mathsf{V}_{1}
the function that sends morphisms
f:\mathbb{1}_{\mathsf{V}_{1}}\rightarrow x

to

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{1}_{\mathsf{V}_{2}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{F}\>\mathbb{1}_{\mathsf{V}_{1}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{F}\>x}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\epsilon_{\mathsf{F}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}\>f}

is an equivalence of types.
If we have
f:\mathbb{1}_{\mathsf{V}_{2}}\rightarrow\mathsf{F}\>x
,
then we denote the action of the inverse by
\zeta_{\mathsf{F}}(f)
.

Every strong monoidal functor that is fully faithful also preserves underlying categories,
because in that case
\epsilon_{\mathsf{F}}
is an isomorphism
and the map sending
f
to
\mathsf{F}\>f
is an equivalence of types.
The requirement in
section

3.1
says that the underlying category is preserved by change of base along
\mathsf{F}
.
With this additional assumption, we define the change of base of enriched categories.

{exa}

Let
\mathsf{F}:\mathsf{V}_{1}\rightarrow\mathsf{V}_{2}
by a lax monoidal functor that preserves underlying categories,
and let
\mathsf{C}
be a category together with a
\mathsf{V}
-enrichment
\mathcal{E}
.
We define the
change-of-base enrichment

\mathsf{F}^{*}(\mathcal{E})
for
\mathsf{C}
as follows.
The hom-object
\mathsf{F}^{*}(\mathcal{E})(x,y)
is defined to be
\mathsf{F}\>(\mathcal{E}(x,y))
,
and the enriched identity
\mathsf{id}^{\mathsf{e}}(x)
is defined as the composition

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{1}_{\mathsf{V}_{2}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{F}\>\mathbb{1}_{\mathsf{V}_{1}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{F}\>(\mathcal{E}(x,x))}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\epsilon_{\mathsf{F}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}\>(\mathsf{id}^{\mathsf{e}}(x))}

Composition is defined similarly.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{F}\>(\mathcal{E}(y,z))\otimes\mathsf{F}\>(\mathcal{E}(x,y))}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{F}\>(\mathcal{E}(y,z)\otimes\mathcal{E}(x,y))}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{F}\>(\mathcal{E}(x,y))}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}\>\mathsf{comp}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{\mathsf{F}}}

If we have a morphism
f:x\rightarrow y
, then we define
\overrightarrow{f}
to be

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{1}_{\mathsf{V}_{2}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{F}\>\mathbb{1}_{\mathsf{V}_{1}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{F}\>(\mathcal{E}(x,x)).}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\epsilon_{\mathsf{F}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}\>(\overrightarrow{f})}

Finally, for a morphism
f:\mathbb{1}_{\mathsf{V}_{2}}\rightarrow\mathsf{F}\>(\mathcal{E}(x,x))
,
we define
\overleftarrow{f}
to be
\overleftarrow{\zeta_{\mathsf{F}}(f)}
.
Note that here we use that
\mathsf{F}
preserves underlying categories.
In addition, if we assume that
\mathsf{C}
is univalent,
then we get a univalent enriched category
\mathsf{F}^{*}(\mathcal{E})
.

Finally,
we discuss two classical examples of enriched categories,
namely the unit and the tensor.
Instead of using enrichments, we construct them in the usual way,
from which we still get a category with an enrichment.

{exa}

Given a monoidal category
\mathsf{V}
,
we construct the
unit enriched category
\mathcal{I}
as follows.

•

The objects of
\mathcal{I}
are inhabitants of the unit type.

•

The enriched hom object from
\mathbf{\mathsf{tt}}
to
\mathbf{\mathsf{tt}}
is
\mathbb{1}_{\mathsf{V}}
where
\mathbf{\mathsf{tt}}
is the inhabitant of the unit type.

•

The enriched identity is given by
\operatorname{id}:\mathbb{1}_{\mathsf{V}}\rightarrow\mathbb{1}_{\mathsf{V}}
.

•

The enriched composition is given by
\mathsf{l}:\mathbb{1}_{\mathsf{V}}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{\mathsf{V}}\rightarrow\mathbb{1}_{\mathsf{V}}
.

Note that
\mathcal{I}
is not necessarily univalent.
For instance,
if
\mathsf{V}
is the symmetric monoidal category of abelian groups with the tensor product,
then the morphisms in
\mathcal{I}
from
\mathbf{\mathsf{tt}}
to
\mathbf{\mathsf{tt}}
are the same as integers and composition is given by addition.
As a consequence, there is an isomorphism from
\mathbf{\mathsf{tt}}
to
\mathbf{\mathsf{tt}}
for each integer,
while for
\mathcal{I}
to be univalent, there must be a unique isomorphism.

{exa}

Let
\mathsf{V}
be a symmetric monoidal category, and suppose that we have enriched categories
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
over
\mathsf{V}
.
We construct their
tensor product
\mathcal{E}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{2}
as follows.

•

The objects of
\mathcal{E}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{2}

are pairs
(x,y)
of objects
x:\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
y:\mathcal{E}_{2}
.

•

The enriched hom object from
(x_{1},y_{1})
to
(x_{2},y_{2})
is
\mathcal{E}_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\otimes\mathcal{E}_{2}(y_{1},y_{2})
.

•

The enriched identity is given by the following composition of morphisms.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{1}_{\mathsf{V}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{1}_{\mathsf{V}}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{\mathsf{V}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{1}(x,x)\otimes\mathcal{E}_{2}(y,y)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{l}^{-1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{id}^{\mathsf{e}}(x)\otimes\mathsf{id}^{\mathsf{e}}(y)}

•

The enriched composition is defined as the following composition of morphisms.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(\mathcal{E}_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\otimes\mathcal{E}_{2}(y_{1},y_{2}))\otimes(\mathcal{E}_{1}(x_{2},x_{3})\otimes\mathcal{E}_{2}(y_{2},y_{3}))}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(\mathcal{E}_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\otimes\mathcal{E}_{1}(x_{2},x_{3}))\otimes(\mathcal{E}_{2}(y_{1},y_{2})\otimes\mathcal{E}_{2}(y_{2},y_{3}))}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{1}(x_{1},x_{3})\otimes\mathcal{E}_{2}(y_{1},y_{3})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{comp}\otimes\mathsf{comp}}

Here the morphism
i
is defined using that
\mathsf{V}
is symmetric monoidal.

Let us reflect upon the underlying category of
\mathcal{E}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{2}
.
Morphisms from
(x_{1},y_{1})
to
(x_{2},y_{2})
in the underlying category
are given by morphisms
\mathbb{1}_{\mathsf{V}}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\otimes\mathcal{E}_{2}(y_{1},y_{2})
.
However, this is not necessarily the same as pairs of morphisms
x_{1}\rightarrow x_{2}
and
y_{1}\rightarrow y_{2}
.
For instance, if
\mathsf{V}
is the symmetric monoidal category of abelian groups with the tensor product,
then morphisms in
\mathcal{E}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{2}
are maps
\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\otimes\mathcal{E}_{2}(y_{1},y_{2})
.
Such maps are uniquely determined by an element of
\mathcal{E}_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\otimes\mathcal{E}_{2}(y_{1},y_{2})
,
which are not the same as pairs of some
f:\mathcal{E}_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})
and
g:\mathcal{E}_{2}(y_{1},y_{2})
.
For this reason, the tensor product
\mathcal{E}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{2}
is not necessarily univalent,
even if we assume
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
to be so.

3.2.
Enrichments over Structures

Next we characterize two classes of enrichments.
First, we characterize enrichments for the category
\mathsf{Set}
of sets equipped with its Cartesian monoidal structure.

Proposition
\thethm
.

Let
\mathsf{C}
be a category.
The type of
\mathsf{Set}
-enrichments for
\mathsf{C}
is contractible.

From
section

3.2
, we can conclude that the type of categories is equivalent to the type of
\mathsf{Set}
-enriched categories.
Second, we characterize enrichments for structured sets with a Cartesian monoidal structure.
To do so, we first define a general notion of structured sets.

{defi}

A
Cartesian notion of structure

\mathcal{S}
consists of

•

a set
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{X}
of structures on
\mathsf{X}
for every set
\mathsf{X}
;

•

a proposition
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})}(f)
which represents that
f
is a structure preserving map from
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}
to
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}
, for all functions
f:\mathsf{X}\rightarrow\mathsf{X}
and structures
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}:\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}:\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{Y}
;

•

an inhabitant
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{unit}}:\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{unit}
;

•

a structure
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\times\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}:\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>(\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y})
for all
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}:\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}:\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{Y}
.

This data is required to satisfy the following axioms.

•

For every set
\mathsf{X}
and structure
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}:\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{X}
, we have
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}})}(\operatorname{id}_{\mathsf{X}})
;

•

for all functions
f:\mathsf{X}\rightarrow\mathsf{Y}
and
g:\mathsf{Y}\rightarrow\mathsf{Z}

such that
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{X},\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{Y})}(f)

and
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{Z})}(g)
,
we have
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{X},\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{Z})}(g\circ f)
;

•

given structures
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}^{\prime}:\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{X}

such that
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}^{\prime}})}(\operatorname{id}_{\mathsf{X}})
and
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}^{\prime}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}})}(\operatorname{id}_{\mathsf{X}})
,
we have
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}=\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}^{\prime}
;

•

given a structure
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}:\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{X}
on a set
\mathsf{X}
,
we have
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{unit}})}(\lambda(x:\mathsf{X}).\mathbf{\mathsf{tt}})
where
\mathbf{\mathsf{tt}}
is the unique element of
\mathsf{unit}
;

•

given structures
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}:\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}:\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{Y}

on sets
\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{Y}
respectively,
we have
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\times\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}})}(\pi_{1})

and
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\times\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})}(\pi_{2})
;

•

for all functions
f:\mathsf{X}\rightarrow\mathsf{Y}
and
g:\mathsf{X}\rightarrow\mathsf{Z}

such that
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{X},\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{Y})}(f)

and
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{X},\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{Z})}(g)
,
we have
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{X},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}\times\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Z}})}(\lambda(x:\mathsf{X}).(f\>x,g\>x))
.

A
Cartesian closed notion of structure

\mathcal{S}
is given by a Cartesian notion of structure
\mathcal{S}

together with a structure
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\Rightarrow\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}:\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>(\sum(f:\mathsf{X}\rightarrow\mathsf{Y}),\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})}(f))

for all
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}:\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}:\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{Y}

such that

•

the constant map is structure preserving,
i.e.,
we have
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})}(\lambda x.y)
for all
y:\mathsf{Y}
;

•

the evaluation is structure preserving,
i.e.,
we have
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\times(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\Rightarrow\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}),\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})}(\lambda z.\pi_{2}(\pi_{1}\>z)
;

•

lambda abstraction is structure preserving,
i.e.,
given
f:\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Z}\rightarrow\mathsf{Y}
such that
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\times\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Z}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})}(f)
,
we also have
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Z}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\Rightarrow\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})}(\lambda{z}{\lambda x.f{(x,z)}})
.

Note that for each
z:\mathsf{Z}
the map
\lambda x.f{(x,z)}
is structure preserving,
because the pairing function
\lambda(x:\mathsf{X}).(f\>x,g\>x)
,
the identity,
and constant functions are structure preserving.

Note that
section

3.2
is extension of standard notions of structures defined in
[
Uni13
, Definition 9.8.1]
:
the added data and axioms guarantee that the resulting category has binary products, a terminal object, and exponentials.

Problem
\thethm
.

Given a Cartesian (closed) notion of structure
\mathcal{S}
, to construct a univalent Cartesian (closed) category
\mathsf{Str}(\mathcal{S})
.

Construction 3.0
(for Problem
3.2
)
.

In
[
Uni13
, Section 9.8]
,
it is shown how every standard notion of structure gives rise to a univalent category.
The terminal object is given by
(\mathsf{unit},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{unit}})
,
and the product of
(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}})
and
(\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})
is given by
(\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\times\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})
.
Their internal hom is
((\sum(f:\mathsf{X}\rightarrow\mathsf{Y}),\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})}(f)),\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\Rightarrow\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})
.
∎

Proposition
\thethm
.

Let
\mathsf{C}
be a category and let
\mathcal{S}
be a Cartesian notion of structure.
Then the type of
\mathsf{Str}(\mathcal{S})
-enrichments for
\mathsf{C}
is equivalent to
a structure
\mathsf{hom}_{(x,y)}:\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>(x\rightarrow y)
for all objects
x,y:\mathsf{C}

such that for all
x,y,z:\mathsf{C}
we have
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{hom}_{(y,z)}\times\mathsf{hom}_{(x,y)},\mathsf{hom}_{(x,z)})}(\lambda f.\pi_{2}\>f\cdot\pi_{1}\>f)
.

As such, to give a
\mathsf{Str}(\mathcal{S})
-enrichment for
\mathsf{C}
one needs to endow every hom-set of
\mathsf{C}

with an
\mathcal{S}
-structure
such that the composition operation is a structure preserving map.

{exa}

We have a Cartesian closed notion of structure
\mathsf{DCPO}
of
directed complete partial orders structures
(DCPOs)
such that
\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{DCPO}}\>\mathsf{X}
is the set of DCPOs on
\mathsf{X}

and such that
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})}(f)
expresses that
f
is a Scott continuous map.
As such, a
\mathsf{DCPO}
-enriched category is given by a category whose hom-sets are directed complete partial orders,
and whose composition operation is a Scott-continuous map.

We also have a Cartesian notion of structure
\mathsf{DCPPO}
of
pointed directed complete partial orders structures
(DCPPOs)
such that
\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{DCPPO}}\>\mathsf{X}
is the set of DCPPOs on
\mathsf{X}

and such that
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})}(f)
expresses that
f
is a Scott continuous map.
Hence,
\mathsf{DCPPO}
-enriched categories are categories whose hom-sets are pointed directed complete partial orders,
and whose composition operation is a Scott-continuous map.

3.3.
Smash Products

Finally,
we discuss a general notion of structure
that gives rise to a symmetric monoidal closed category
via the smash product.
Recall that, given two pointed sets
(\mathsf{X},*_{\mathsf{X}})
and
(\mathsf{Y},*_{\mathsf{Y}})
,
their smash product
\mathsf{X}\wedge\mathsf{Y}
is defined to be
(\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y})/{\sim}

where the equivalence relation generated by the following clauses.

(*_{\mathsf{X}},y_{1})\sim(*_{X},y_{2})\quad\quad(x_{1},*_{\mathsf{Y}})\sim(x_{2},*_{\mathsf{Y}})

Before we can define the desired notion of structure,
we first consider a notion of structure that endows every set with a point.

{defi}

A
pointed notion of structure
is given by
a Cartesian notion
\mathcal{S}
of structure
together with a point
*_{\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}}:\mathsf{X}
for each
\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}:\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{X}

such that

•

the constant map
\lambda x.*_{\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}}
is structure preserving,
i.e. we have
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})}(\lambda x.*_{\mathsf{Y}})
;

•

for all functions
f:\mathsf{X}\rightarrow\mathsf{Y}

such that
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})}(f)
,
we have
f\>*_{\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}}=*_{\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}}
.

Every pointed notion
\mathcal{S}
of structure gives rise to an equivalence relation
\sim_{\wedge}
on
\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}

if we have structures
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}:\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}:\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{Y}
.
More specifically, we say that
(x_{1},y_{1})\sim_{\wedge}(x_{2},y_{2})
if
either
x_{1}=x_{2}
and
y_{1}=y_{2}

or if we have

•

either
x_{1}=*_{\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}}
or
y_{1}=*_{\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}}
, and

•

either
x_{2}=*_{\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}}
or
y_{2}=*_{\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}}
.

This gives rise to a set
\mathsf{X}\wedge_{\mathcal{S}}\mathsf{Y}
,
which we define to be
(\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y})/{\sim_{\wedge}}
.
We write
[(x,y)]:\mathsf{X}\wedge_{\mathcal{S}}\mathsf{Y}
for the equivalence class of the pair
(x,y)
.

Note that the type
\mathsf{X}\wedge_{\mathcal{S}}\mathsf{Y}
satisfies an elimination principle,
because it is defined as a quotient type.
Given a set
\mathsf{Z}
and a map
h:\mathsf{X}\rightarrow\mathsf{Y}\rightarrow\mathsf{Z}

such that

h\>*_{\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}}\>y_{1}=h\>*_{\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}}\>y_{2},\quad h\>*_{\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}}\>y=h\>x\>*_{\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}},\quad h\>x_{1}\>*_{\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}}=h\>x_{2}\>*_{\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}},

(2)

we have a unique map
\mathsf{map}_{\wedge}\>h:\mathsf{X}\wedge_{\mathcal{S}}\mathsf{Y}\rightarrow\mathsf{Z}

such that
\mathsf{map}_{\wedge}\>h\>[(x,y)]=h\>x\>y
.

Now we define when a pointed notion of structure supports the smash product.

{defi}

A
notion of structure that supports smash products
is given by a pointed notion
\mathcal{S}
of structure together with

•

a structure
\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{bool}}:\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{bool}
on the type of booleans;

•

a structure
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\wedge\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}:\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>(\mathsf{X}\wedge_{\mathcal{S}}\mathsf{Y})

for all
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}:\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}:\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>\mathsf{Y}

such that

•

we have
*_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{bool}}}=\mathsf{false}
and
*_{\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\wedge\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}}=[(*_{\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}},*_{\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}})]
;

•

we have
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{bool}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})}(\lambda b.\mathsf{if}\>b\>\mathsf{then}\>y\>\mathsf{else}\>*_{\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}})

and
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})}(\lambda x.\mathsf{if}\>f\>x\>\mathsf{then}\>g\>x\>\mathsf{else}\>*_{\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}})
;

•

we have
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\wedge\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})}(\lambda x.[(x,y)])

and
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\wedge\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})}(\lambda y.[(x,y)])
;

•

we have
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\times\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\wedge\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})}(\lambda z.[z])

and
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\wedge\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Z}})}(\mathsf{map}_{\wedge}\>h)
.

To construct the desired monoidal structure,
we also require that our notion of structure is closed.
More specifically,
this means that we require that we have a structure

\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\Rightarrow_{*}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}

on the hom sets
\mathsf{hom}_{*}(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})
,
which are defined to be
\sum(f:\mathsf{X}\rightarrow\mathsf{Y}),\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})}(f)
.
We also require that structure preserving maps from
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Z}}
to
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\Rightarrow_{*}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}

are the same as structure preserving from
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Z}}\wedge\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}
to
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}
.

Before we precisely define the notion of structure that we use,
we first introduce some notation.
For now we assume that we are given the aforementioned structure
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\Rightarrow_{*}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}
.
The point
*_{\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\Rightarrow_{*}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}}
gives us a structure preserving function from
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}
to
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}
.
Given
f:\mathsf{Z}\rightarrow\mathsf{hom}_{*}(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})
,
we define the currying map
\mathsf{curry}\>f:\mathsf{Z}\wedge_{\mathcal{S}}\mathsf{X}\rightarrow\mathsf{Y}

to be
\mathsf{map}_{\wedge}\>(\lambda x.\lambda y.f\>x{y}\>)
.
Note that
\mathsf{curry}\>f
only is a function between sets,
and that it is not necessarily structure preserving.
If we have a function
f:\mathsf{Z}\wedge_{\mathcal{S}}\mathsf{X}\rightarrow\mathsf{Y}

and a point
z:\mathsf{Z}
,
we define the uncurrying map
\mathsf{uncurry}\>f\>z:\mathsf{hom}_{*}(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})

to be the structure preserving map
\lambda x.f\>[(z,x)]
.

{defi}

A
notion of closed structure that supports smash products

is given by a pointed notion
\mathcal{S}
of structure together with
a structure
\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\Rightarrow_{*}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}
on the set
\mathsf{hom}_{*}(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})

such that the following requirements are satisfied

•

for all
x:\mathsf{X}
we have
*_{\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\Rightarrow_{*}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}}\>x=*_{\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}}
;

•

we have

\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Z}}\wedge\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})}(\mathsf{curry}\>f)

and
\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Z}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\Rightarrow_{*}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})}(\mathsf{uncurry}\>f)

for all suitably typed
f
;

•

we have

\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Z}}\Rightarrow_{*}(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\Rightarrow_{*}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}),\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Z}}\wedge\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\Rightarrow_{*}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})}(\mathsf{curry})

and

\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Z}}\wedge\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\Rightarrow_{*}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}},\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Z}}\Rightarrow_{*}(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\Rightarrow_{*}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}))}(\mathsf{uncurry})
.

Let us reflect upon
Section

3.3
,
and especially the final requirements.
The final two requirements says that we have an isomorphism of
structures
between

\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Z}}\Rightarrow_{*}(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\Rightarrow_{*}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})

and

\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Z}}\wedge\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\Rightarrow_{*}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}

rather than a mere isomorphism of sets.
To formulate these two requirements,
we implicitly use that
\mathsf{curry}\>f
and
\mathsf{uncurry}\>f
are structure preserving.
This is to guarantee that
\mathsf{curry}
indeed gives rise to a structure preserving map from

\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Z}}\Rightarrow_{*}(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\Rightarrow_{*}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})

to

\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Z}}\wedge\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\Rightarrow_{*}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}
,
and similarly for
\mathsf{uncurry}
.

Problem
\thethm
.

Given a notion of closed structure
\mathcal{S}
that supports smash products,
to construct a univalent symmetric monoidal closed category
\mathsf{Str}_{\wedge}(\mathcal{S})
.

Construction 3.0
(for Problem
3.3
)
.

The construction is rather technical and is based on a more general construction
[
EM09
, Lemma 4.19 and Construction 4.20]
.
The main idea is to exploit the fact that we have an isomorphism between

\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Z}}\Rightarrow_{*}(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\Rightarrow_{*}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}})

and

\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Z}}\wedge\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{X}}\Rightarrow_{*}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{Y}}

in the category
\mathsf{Str}(\mathcal{S})
.
∎

Following
Section

3.2
, we can analogously characterize enrichments over the symmetric monoidal closed category
\mathsf{Str}_{\wedge}(\mathcal{S})
.

Proposition
\thethm
.

Let
\mathsf{C}
be a category and let
\mathcal{S}
be a notion of closed structure that supports smash products.
Then
\mathsf{Str}(\mathcal{S})
-enrichments for
\mathsf{C}
is equivalent to
a structure
\mathsf{hom}_{(x,y)}:\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\>(x\rightarrow y)
for all objects
x,y:\mathsf{C}

such that for all
x,y,z:\mathsf{C}
we have

\mathsf{H}_{(\mathsf{hom}_{(y,z)}\times\mathsf{hom}_{(x,y)},\mathsf{hom}_{(x,z)})}(\lambda f.\pi_{2}\>f\cdot\pi_{1}\>f)

and such that for all
f:x\rightarrow y
we have

f\cdot*_{\mathsf{hom}_{(y,z)}}=*_{\mathsf{hom}_{(x,z)}},\quad\quad*_{\mathsf{hom}_{(w,x)}}\cdot f=*_{\mathsf{hom}_{(w,y)}}.

Note that in
Section

3.3
,
we require composition to be a structure preserving map whose domain is the product
rather than the smash product.
This is fine,
because we can lift composition to an operation on the smash product
due to the final two requirements.
Let us finish this section with two examples of notions of closed structures that support smash products.

{exa}

We define the
notion
\mathsf{Set}_{*}
of structure given by pointed sets
.
The type
\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{DCPO}}\>\mathsf{X}
is the collection of points in
\mathsf{X}
,
and
\mathsf{H}_{(x,y)}(f)
says that
f:\mathsf{X}\rightarrow\mathsf{Y}
preserves the point, i.e.
f\>x=y
.
One can show that
\mathsf{Set}_{*}
is a notion of closed structure that supports smash products.
Analogously, we define
\mathsf{Poset}_{\bot}
,
which is given by pointed posets (posets with a least element).
The type
\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{DCPO}}\>\mathsf{X}
is the set of partial orders for
\mathsf{X}
that have a least element,
and
\mathsf{H}_{(x,y)}(f)
says that
f:\mathsf{X}\rightarrow\mathsf{Y}
is strictly monotone.
This also gives rise to a notion of closed structure that supports smash products.

Remark 1
.

In
section

3.2
,
we defined a Cartesian notion of structure by
pointed
DCPOs and Scott continuous maps without requiring these maps to be strict.
For pointed DCPOs and strict Scott continuous maps, one can also define such a structure.
However, in applications, one is often interested in a different monoidal structure for pointed DCPOs with strict maps,
namely the one given by the smash product.
The methods in this section do not extend to pointed DCPOs,
because we construct the smash product using quotients.
However,
one can use other methods to construct this symmetric closed monoidal category constructively
[
Tow96
, Theorem 2.9.1]
.

4.
Image Factorization

We continue our study of univalent enriched categories by proving that every essentially surjective and fully faithful (enriched) functor is an adjoint equivalence.
Classically, one would use the axiom of choice to prove this fact:
to define the inverse, one needs to pick preimages and those are only guaranteed to be unique up to isomorphism.
One can give a constructive proof of this fact if one assumes that the domain of the functor in question is univalent.

The way we approach this result, is via
orthogonal factorization systems
in bicategories.
More specifically,
we show that the essentially surjective and the fully faithful enriched functors form an orthogonal factorization system
[
LR20
, Lemma 4.3.5]
.
From this fact, one directly obtains that every essentially surjective and fully faithful functor is an adjoint equivalence.
The proof is similar to how in orthogonal factorization systems in categories the intersection of the left and right class of maps are precisely the isomorphisms.

We start by defining
orthogonal
maps in bicategories.

{defi}

Let
\mathsf{B}
be a bicategory and let
f:x_{1}\rightarrow x_{2}
and
g:y_{1}\rightarrow y_{2}
be 1-cells.
Then we say that
f
is
orthogonal
to
g
, written
f\mathrel{\bot}g
,
if the following diagram of categories is a weak pullback in the bicategory of categories.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\underline{\mathsf{B}}(x_{2},y_{1})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\underline{\mathsf{B}}(x_{1},y_{1})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\underline{\mathsf{B}}(x_{2},y_{2})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\underline{\mathsf{B}}(x_{1},y_{2})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{\mathsf{pre}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g^{\mathsf{post}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g^{\mathsf{post}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{\mathsf{pre}}}

where the functors
f^{\mathsf{pre}}
and
g^{\mathsf{post}}
are given by precomposition with
f
and postcomposition with
g
respectively.

Let us reflect on
section

4
.
Weak pullbacks of categories are given by iso-comma categories.
The objects in the iso-comma category
\mathsf{F}/_{\cong}\mathsf{G}
of functors
\mathsf{F}:\mathsf{C}_{1}\rightarrow\mathsf{C}_{3}
and
\mathsf{G}:\mathsf{C}_{2}\rightarrow\mathsf{C}_{3}
are given by triples
(x,y,f)
of objects
x:\mathsf{C}_{1}
and
y:\mathsf{C}_{2}
together with an isomorphism
f:\mathsf{F}\>x\cong\mathsf{G}\>y
.
Note that we have a functor
\mathsf{O}_{(f,g)}:\underline{\mathsf{B}}(x_{2},y_{1})\rightarrow f^{\mathsf{pre}}/_{\cong}g^{\mathsf{post}}
.
The functor
\mathsf{O}_{(f,g)}
maps 1-cells
h:x_{2}\rightarrow y_{1}
to the triple
(h\cdot g,f\cdot h,\alpha_{f,h,g})
where
\alpha
is the associator of
\mathsf{B}
.
Orthogonality can equivalently be phrased by saying that the functor
\mathsf{O}_{(f,g)}
is an adjoint equivalence.
Essential surjectivity of
\mathsf{O}_{(f,g)}
says that every square has a diagonal filler as follows.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{x_{1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{y_{1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{x_{2}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{y_{2}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces l}

More concretely, given the diagram above, there is a lift
l:x_{2}\rightarrow y_{1}
making the two triangles commute up to invertible 2-cell.
Fully faithfulness of
\mathsf{O}_{(f,g)}
says that whenever we have two lifts
l_{1},l_{2}:x_{2}\rightarrow y_{1}
together with 2-cells
\tau_{1}:l_{1}\cdot g\Rightarrow l_{2}\cdot g
and
\tau_{2}:f\cdot l_{1}\Rightarrow f\cdot l_{2}
,
we have a unique 2-cell
\zeta:l_{1}\Rightarrow l_{2}

such that
\zeta\vartriangleright g=\tau_{1}
and
f\vartriangleleft\zeta=\tau_{2}
.

{defi}

Let
\mathsf{B}
be a bicategory.
An
orthogonal factorization system
on
\mathsf{B}
consists of two classes of maps, which we denote by
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{R}
, such that

•

\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{R}
are closed under invertible 2-cells;

•

for all 1-cells
f
and
g
such that
\mathcal{L}\>f
and
\mathcal{R}\>g
, we have
f\mathrel{\bot}g
;

•

for every 1-cell
f
, we have a factorization
f\cong l\cdot r
such that
\mathcal{L}\>l
and
\mathcal{R}\>r
.

Note that if
\mathsf{B}
is locally univalent,
then all classes of 1-cells are closed under invertible 2-cells.
In this section, we are interested in a particular factorization system on
\mathsf{EnrichCat}_{\mathsf{V}}
,
which is given by the fully faithful and the essentially surjective enriched functors.

{defi}

Let
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{E}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}
be an enriched functor.

•

We say that
\mathcal{F}
is
fully faithful
if for all objects
x,y:\mathcal{E}_{1}
the morphism
\mathcal{F}(x,y)
is an isomorphism.

•

We say that
\mathcal{F}
is
essentially surjective
if its underlying functor is essentially surjective.
That is to say, for all
y:\mathcal{E}_{2}
we have an inhabitant of
\mathopen{}\left\|\sum(x:\mathcal{E}_{1}),\mathcal{F}\>x\cong y\right\|\mathclose{}
.

•

We say that
\mathcal{F}
is a
weak equivalence
if
\mathcal{F}
is both fully faithful and essentially surjective.

Every enriched functor can be factorized as an essentially surjective functor followed by a fully faithful functor by taking the
full image
.

{exa}

Let
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{E}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}
be an enriched functor.
We define a predicate
\mathsf{P}
on the objects of
\mathcal{E}_{2}
such that

\mathsf{P}\>y\coloneqq\mathopen{}\left\|\sum(x:\mathcal{E}_{1}),\mathcal{F}\>x\cong y\right\|\mathclose{}.

The
full image

\mathsf{Im}(\mathcal{F})
of
\mathcal{F}
is defined to be the full subcategory of
\mathcal{E}_{2}
with respect to
\mathsf{P}
.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose that we have univalent enriched categories
\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{E}_{3}
, and
\mathcal{E}_{4}
.
If we have enriched functors
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{E}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}
and
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{E}_{3}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{4}
such that
\mathcal{F}
is essentially surjective and
\mathcal{G}
is fully faithful,
then
\mathcal{F}\mathrel{\bot}\mathcal{G}
.

Problem 4.1
.

To construct an orthogonal factorization system on
\mathsf{EnrichCat}_{\mathsf{V}}
.

Construction 4.3
(for Problem
4.1
)
.

The classes
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{R}
are given by the essentially surjective and the fully faithful enriched functors respectively.
The desired factorization is given by
section

4
,
and the proof of orthogonality is given in
2
.
It remains to show that essentially surjective and fully faithful enriched functors are closed under enriched natural isomorphisms,
and this is so, because
\mathsf{EnrichCat}_{\mathsf{V}}
is locally univalent.
∎

From this factorization system, we directly obtain that weak equivalence are actually adjoint equivalences.

Theorem 4
.

Every fully faithful and essentially surjective enriched functor
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{E}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}
is an adjoint equivalence.

Proof 4.2
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{E}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}
is fully faithful and essentially surjective.
Consider the following diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{2}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{2}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{F}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{F}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{id}{}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{id}{}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces l}

Due to the orthogonality of fully faithful and essentially surjective morphisms,
this diagram has a lift
l
such that both triangles commute up to invertible 2-cell.
From this, we get that
f
is an equivalence,
and since equivalences can be refined to adjoint equivalences,

f
is an adjoint equivalence.

Remark 5
.

For the proof of
4
,
we require both the domain and codomain of
\mathcal{F}
to be univalent.
This restriction is a consequence of the bicategorical machinery,
because we phrase everything in the bicategory
\mathsf{EnrichCat}_{\mathsf{V}}
whose objects are univalent enriched categories.
In the case that only the domain of
\mathcal{F}
is univalent, one could still use the same construction as in
section

4
.

5.
The Rezk Completion

The next aspect in our study of univalent enriched categories, is the
enriched Rezk completion
.
There are two features to a suitable Rezk completion for enriched categories.
The first feature of the Rezk completion that we consider, is that every enriched category is weakly equivalent to a univalent one.

Problem 5.1
.

Given a category
\mathcal{E}
enriched over a univalent complete symmetric monoidal category
\mathsf{V}
,
to construct a univalent enriched category
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{E})
and a weak equivalence
\mathsf{P}:\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{E})
.

We give two constructions for
5.1
.
The first construction (
Section

5.1
) is similar to the Rezk completion of categories
[
AKS15
]
.
More specifically, we can construct the enriched Rezk completion as the image of the Yoneda embedding.
This construction crucially relies on the assumption that
\mathsf{V}
is both complete and monoidal closed,
which we need to construct an enrichment for the enriched functor category
[\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{op}},\mathsf{self}(\mathsf{V})]
.
In addition, this construction increases the universe level in general.

The second construction (
Section

5.2
) uses higher inductive types (HITs),
and we rely on the fact that the Rezk completion of ordinary categories can be desired as a HIT
[
Uni13
]
.
Explicitly, if we have a category
\mathsf{C}
enriched over a univalent monoidal category
\mathsf{V}
,
then we endow its Rezk completion (of categories)
\mathcal{R}(\mathsf{C})
with a
\mathsf{V}
-enrichment.
To do so, we use the elimination rules of higher inductive types.
This allows us to avoid the assumption that
\mathsf{V}
is both complete and symmetric monoidal closed,
and, if we also assume that the universe is closed under HITs,
the universe level remains the same.

We assume that
\mathsf{V}
is univalent in both
Sections

5.1
and
5.2
,
and we can extend both constructions to the case where
\mathsf{V}
is not necessarily univalent.
To do so, we first take the Rezk completion of monoidal categories
[
WMA22
]
of
\mathsf{V}
to obtain a weak equivalence
\mathsf{P}:\mathsf{V}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}(\mathsf{V})
.
Since
\mathsf{P}
is fully faithful, it preserves underlying categories.
Hence, if we have a category
\mathcal{E}
enriched over
\mathsf{V}
,
we obtain a category
\mathsf{P}^{*}(\mathcal{E})
enriched over
\mathcal{R}(\mathsf{V})
using
section

3.1
.
Then we can use either
Section

5.1
or
Section

5.2
to obtain the desired Rezk completion.

The second feature of the Rezk completion that we consider, is the universal property (
6
).
We formulate this universal property in such a way that we can instantiate it directly for both
Sections

5.1
and
5.2
.
More specifically, we say that every enriched functor from an enriched category
\mathcal{E}
to some univalent enriched category
can be extended along weak equivalences.

Theorem 6
.

Suppose that we have a enriched functor
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{E}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}
that is both essentially surjective and fully faithful.
Then the precomposition functor
\mathcal{F}^{\mathsf{pre}}:[\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{E}_{3}]\rightarrow[\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{3}]
is an adjoint equivalence of categories
for each univalent enriched category
\mathcal{E}_{3}
.

To understand
6
,
let us first note that
\mathcal{F}^{\mathsf{pre}}:[\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{E}_{3}]\rightarrow[\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{3}]
is both essentially surjective and fully faithful
if it is an adjoint equivalence.
Essential surjectivity expresses that for each enriched functor
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{E}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{3}
,
we can find an enriched functor
\mathcal{H}:\mathcal{E}_{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{3}
and a natural isomorphism
\tau
as follows.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{3}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{2}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{G}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{F}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{H}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tau}

To understand fully faithfulness we assume that we have diagrams as follows

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{3}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{2}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{G}_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{F}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{H}_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tau_{1}}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{3}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{2}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{G}_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{F}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{H}_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tau_{2}}

where

and

are natural isomorphisms.

Next we prove the universal property of the Rezk completion (

To verify

\mathcal{E}_{3}
is univalent.
This implies that the categories
[\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{E}_{3}]

The proofs of
11

We end our study of univalent enriched categories by looking at enriched monads.

in
\mathsf{C}
.
We denote this category by
\mathsf{K}(\mathsf{T})
.
In general, this category is not univalent (for example the constant monad on the unit set).
This situation can be rectified by defining the Kleisli category in a slightly different way
[

]
,
namely as the image of the free algebra functor from
\mathsf{C}
to the Eilenberg-Moore category
\mathsf{EM}(\mathsf{T})
of
\mathsf{T}
.
The resulting univalent category is denoted by
\mathsf{Kleisli}(\mathsf{T})
.
To derive the usual theorems about Kleisli categories, one can instantiate the formal theory of monads
[
LS02
,
Str72
,
Wei23
]
,
meaning that it suffices to prove the universal property for Kleisli objects.
Proving the desired universal property is a nice exercise using the universal property of the Rezk completion (

).

The key notion of this section, enriched monads, can be defined concisely as monads internal to
\mathsf{EnrichCat}_{\mathsf{V}}

Recall that monads in bicategories are defined as follows.

{defi}

For enriched categories, one can further unfold this definition and phrase it in terms of enrichments.
This results in the notion of

{defi}

Suppose that we have a category
\mathsf{C}
, a monad
\mathsf{T}
on
\mathsf{C}
, and a
\mathsf{V}
-enrichment for

.
Then a
\mathsf{V}
-enrichment
for
\mathsf{T}

\mathsf{V}
-enrichment for the endofunctor

such that the unit
\eta_{\mathsf{T}}
and multiplication
\mu_{\mathsf{T}}
are
\mathsf{V}
-enriched natural transformations.

When we say

In the remainder of this section,
we are concerned with

in the bicategory of enriched categories.
To define Kleisli objects, we first define their cocones.
Note that for these definitions, we talk about arbitrary bicategories
\mathsf{B}

7.
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied univalent enriched categories,
and we discussed several aspects of their study.
Our notion of univalent enriched category was based on enrichments,
and we viewed enriched categories as a category together with an enrichment.
First, we proved a structure identity principle for univalent enriched categories,
which we formulated using univalent bicategories.
The proof used displayed bicategories.
Second, we showed that all weak equivalences between univalent enriched categories
are adjoint equivalences.
Here we made use of orthogonal factorization systems.
Third, we discussed the Rezk completion of enriched categories,
which we constructed using the Yoneda lemma.
We also used the Rezk completion to construct Kleisli objects in the bicategory of univalent enriched categories.

Along the way, we saw a couple of interesting points where univalence interacted with enrichment.
When we defined the change-of-base operation in
section

3.1
,
we restricted ourselves to lax functors that preserve underlying categories.
This was to guarantee that the resulting category would remain univalent.
In addition, we assumed that the monoidal category
\mathsf{V}
has equalizers in the construction of the univalent Kleisli category (
section

6
).

There are several ways to extend the results in this paper.
A wide variety of notions in category theory can be defined internally to a bicategory.
However, for enriched categories, these internal notions are not always the correct ones.
For example, the notion of a fully faithful 1-cell can be defined internally to a bicategory using a representable definition,
but the obtained notion does not correspond to the one given in
section

4
.
To obtain the desired notions, one could use the theory of equipments
[
Woo82
]
,
and one interesting extension of this work would be to develop the equipment of enriched categories.
Such work would build forth upon recent work on univalent double (bi)categories
[
RvdWAN24
,
WRAN24
,
Ver11
]
.
Another interesting extension would be formalizing applications of enriched categories, such as models of the enriched effect calculus
[
EMS14
]
or enriched profunctor optics
[
CEG+24
]
.
