On the geometry of conullity two manifolds
Jacob Van Hook

Abstract
. We consider complete locally irreducible conullity two Riemannian manifolds with constant scalar curvature along nullity geodesics. There exists a naturally defined open dense subset on which we describe the metric in terms of several functions which are uniquely determined up to isometry. In addition, we show that the fundamental group is either trivial or infinite cyclic.



A manifold
M^{n+2}
has
conullity two
if at every point
p\in M
we have a codimension two nullity distribution

\Gamma_{p}\coloneqq\{T\in T_{p}M:R(T,X,Y,Z)=0\text{ for any }X,Y,Z\in T_{p}M\}.

In other words,
\Gamma_{p}
is the kernel of the curvature tensor.
A vector

is called a

and a geodesic

is called a

if
\gamma^{\prime}(t)
is a nullity vector for all

.
It is well known that if
M
is complete then the nullity geodesics are complete as well.

In

1.
Preliminaries

In this section we summarize some properties of our metrics by generalizing and adapting the proofs of
[
Bro21
]
to dimension
n>3
.

One should note that the curvature tensor of a conullity two manifold is determined by the scalar curvature.
Indeed, at each point the curvature tensor is like that of a product
\Sigma\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
where
\Sigma
is a surface.
If
M
is complete we will show that
\mathrm{Scal}<0
.

The nullity distribution
\Gamma
is everywhere
n
-dimensional by assumption.
It is known to be completely integrable with totally geodesic flat leaves which are complete if
M
is (see
[
Mal72
]
).

Recall the
splitting tensor

C\colon\Gamma\times\Gamma^{\perp}\to\Gamma^{\perp}
is defined as

(1.1)

C(T,X)=C_{T}X\coloneqq-\left(\nabla_{X}T\right)^{\Gamma^{\perp}}.

We begin with some facts about this tensor, see
[
FZ20
]
and
[
Bro21
]
.

Fix some nullity geodesic
\gamma(t)
.
If
T=\gamma^{\prime}
, then in some parallel basis along
\gamma
,
C_{T}
satisfies the Riccati equation
{C_{T}}^{\prime}(t)=C_{T}^{2}(t)
whose solutions are
C_{T}(t)=C_{0}(I-tC_{0})^{-1}
for some initial condition
C_{0}=C_{T}(0)
.
Any real eigenvalues of
C_{T}
must therefore be 0 as otherwise the splitting tensor would blow up in finite time.
As
C_{T}
is a linear map between two dimensional vector spaces, it must be nilpotent or it has two complex eigenvalues.

The second Bianchi identity implies that

\mathrm{Scal}^{\prime}=\tr(C_T)\mathrm{Scal}.

along a nullity geodesic.
If the scalar curvature is constant along nullity geodesics we have
\tr(C_T)=0
.
Furthermore,
(\tr C_{T})^{\prime}=\tr C_{T}^{2}=(\tr C_{T})^{2}-2\det C_{T}
and therefore
\det C_{T}=0
as well, so
C_{T}
is nilpotent.
Conversely, if
C_{T}
is nilpotent, then
\mathrm{Scal}
is constant along the nullity geodesic.
From now on we will assume that
C_{T}
is nilpotent.

The space of self-adjoint
2\times 2
matrices is a 3-dimensional subspace of the 4-dimensional space of
2\times 2
matrices.
This subspace intersects the space of nilpotent matrices trivially and so the image of
C:T\mapsto C_{T}
is 1-dimensional.
Therefore if
C\neq 0
then at each point
p\in M
there is a unique (up to sign)
T_{1}\in\Gamma
such that
C_{T_{1}}\neq 0
, and for any nullity vector
T\perp T_{1}
,
C_{T}=0
.
Since
C_{T_{1}}
is nilpotent, there exists an orthonormal basis
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
of
\Gamma^{\perp}
, parallel in the
T_{1}
direction and unique up to sign, such that with respect to that basis
C_{T_{1}}
has the form

C_{T_{1}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&a_{1}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}

for some smooth function
a_{1}
.
That is,

\left(\nabla_{e_{1}}T_{1}\right)^{\Gamma^{\perp}}=0,\quad\left(\nabla_{e_{2}}T_{1}\right)^{\Gamma^{\perp}}=-a_{1}e_{1},\mbox{\quad and\quad}(\nabla_{e_{i}}T)^{\Gamma^{\perp}}=0

for all
T\in\Gamma
orthogonal to
T_{1}
.
Furthermore, since
{C_{T_{1}}}^{\prime}=C_{T_{1}}^{2}=0
, it follows that
T_{1}(a_{1})=0
.

.

Let
M_{C}\subset M
be the set of points on which
C
(equivalently
a_{1}
) is nonzero.

We first investigate properties of the splitting tensor on
M_{C}
, so for the rest of this section suppose that
a_{1}\neq 0
.
There exist smooth functions
\alpha
and
\beta
such that:

\displaystyle\nabla_{e_{1}}e_{1}

\displaystyle=\alpha e_{2};\quad\nabla_{e_{1}}e_{2}=-\alpha e_{1};

\displaystyle\nabla_{e_{2}}e_{1}

\displaystyle=a_{1}T_{1}-\beta e_{2};\quad\nabla_{e_{2}}e_{2}=\beta e_{1},

which follow from the definition of
T_{1}
and the parallel orthonormal frame
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
along the nullity geodesic.

.

Using the above notation, on
M_{C}
we have:

(a)

\alpha=0
, so
\nabla_{e_{1}}e_{1}=0
;

(b)

T(a_{1})=T(\beta)=0
and
\nabla_{T}T_{1}=\nabla_{T}e_{1}=\nabla_{T}e_{2}=0
for any nullity vector
T\in\Gamma

(c)

\nabla_{e_{1}}T_{1}=0
,
e_{1}(a_{1})=a_{1}\beta
, and
e_{1}(\beta)-\beta^{2}=R(e_{1},e_{2},e_{2},e_{1})=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Scal}

Proof.

A computation shows that

R(e_{1},e_{2})e_{2}=\alpha a_{1}T_{1}+(e_{1}(\beta)+e_{2}(\alpha)-\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})e_{1}.

Since
T_{1}\in\Gamma
and
a_{1}\neq 0
, we have that
\alpha=0.
Thus

e_{1}(\beta)-\beta^{2}=R(e_{1},e_{2},e_{2},e_{1}).

and
\nabla_{e_{1}}e_{1}=0.

Furthermore,

R(e_{2},e_{1})e_{1}=(a_{1}\beta-e_{1}(a_{1}))T_{1}-a_{1}\nabla_{e_{1}}T_{1}+(e_{1}(\beta)-\beta^{2})e_{2}.

Notice that
\nabla_{e_{1}}T_{1}\in\Gamma
and is orthogonal to
T_{1}
, so this tells us that

and

To verify the claims involving an arbitrary nullity vector we check separately the cases where

is orthogonal to
T_{1}
and where

which is sufficient by linearity.
Let
T\in\Gamma
be unit-length and orthogonal to
T_{1}
.
Notice that
\Gamma

\nabla_{T}e_{2}\in\Gamma^{\perp}
, so let this be

.
Likewise, let
\nabla_{T}e_{1}=-f_{1}e_{2}

We also know that

A computation shows that

which implies

Next we have

For the case of

Since

3.2.
Completeness of the metric

.

The manifold

with the metric (

Proof.

We use a coordinate change from

that turns our metric into a warped product.
Define the

\vdots&\vdots&&\ddots&0&-f_{n}(x)\\

and then consider the matrix differential equation

Since

is skew symmetric and

Now consider the coordinate change

\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{0}\\

\vdots\\

\vdots\\

\vdots\\
\differential\theta_{n}\end{pmatrix},\,V=\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v_{1}\\
\vdots\\
v_{n}\end{pmatrix},\mbox{\quad and\quad}\differential V=\begin{pmatrix}\differential u\\
\differential v_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\differential v_{n}\end{pmatrix},

we have

\differential\theta=S^{\prime}V\differential x+S\differential V=SAV\differential{x}+S\differential{V}=S\left(AV\differential{x}+\differential{V}\right),

and hence

\displaystyle\differential\theta_{0}^{2}+\dots+\differential\theta_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\differential\theta)^{T}\differential\theta=\left(AV\differential{x}+\differential{V}\right)^{T}S^{T}S\left(AV\differential{x}+\differential{V}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(AV\differential{x}+\differential{V}\right)^{T}\left(AV\differential{x}+\differential{V}\right).

With this coordinate change our metric becomes the warped product

where

can be obtained from the first entry in

Remark 3.5
.

Imposing an upper bound on the scalar curvature of say

is not sufficient for completeness.
An example is

It is critical to bound the geodesic curvature

along the path

Since Lemma

tells us that the scalar curvature is given by

and

so we may write it as

.

.

If

\mathrm{Scal}<0,\quad\absolutevalue{\kgam(x)}\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\absolutevalue{\scal(x,\uu)}},\mbox{\hskip 10.22217ptand\hskip 10.22217pt}\absolutevalue{\kgam(x)}\leq\Lambda

We will prove this using the equivalent assumption that there is some positive function

Consider the geodesic

Since

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{J(t)}=\absolutevalue{\bfun(x,t)}

This gives completeness in terms of an inequality between the geodesic curvature

and the scalar curvature; however, we still require a uniform bound on the geodesic curvature.

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\pdv{\phi}{x}, \pdv{\phi}{\vv{i}}}_{p}

\displaystyle={v_{i-1}}f_{i}(x)-{v_{i+1}}f_{i+1}(x).

Altogether, we have shown that

One should note that we must have
\eta>0

cannot vanish.
If it did so along some path

This would imply that nearby leaves of the foliation

would cross each other which cannot happen.
∎

5.
Foliations of Surfaces and the Smoothness of our Metric

In order to construct complete conullity 2 manifolds and prove Theorem

we start by investigating foliations of surfaces by geodesics.

In
[
Bro21
]
, Brooks showed that if
H\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a locally Lipschitz function and
\Sigma

(p_{0},v_{0})\in T_{1}\Sigma

(5.1)

\sigma_{x}(u)=\sigma(x,u)\coloneqq\exp_{\gamma(x)}^{\perp}(uX).

Fixing

\gamma
.

.

Let
\Sigma

\mathrm{Scal}\leq-2

Here

Proof.

In
[

has Lipschitz constant 1.

To show we have a foliation we show that for every

on
\gamma

Let
\delta(q)\coloneqq d(p,q)

We also have that
\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}}\dot{\gamma}=h(t)(\dot{\gamma})^{\perp}

\displaystyle(L\circ\gamma)^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\geq\cosh(r)\expectationvalue{\partial_r, \dot{\gamma}}^{2}+\cosh(r)\expectationvalue{\partial_r^\perp, \dot{\gamma}}^{2}+h(t)\sinh(r)\expectationvalue{\partial_r, (\dot{\gamma})^\perp}

\displaystyle=\cosh(r)+h(t)\sinh(r)\expectationvalue{\partial_r, (\dot{\gamma})^\perp}\geq e^{-r}>0

To verify the claim about the metric, it suffices to show that

By the Jacobi equation,

which combined with the initial conditions tells us that

∎

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}(\alpha_{i-1}a_{i}-\alpha_{i+1}a_{i+1}+v(\alpha_{i}))T_{i}+(\alpha_{n-1}a_{n}+v(\alpha_{n}))T_{n}.

Since
M
is locally irreducible
\tilde{M}_{F}\subset\tilde{M}
is an open dense set with all

.
Suppose
q\in\tilde{M}_{F}\cap V
, which we may do since
V
spans an interval of leaves, a dense set of which lie in
\tilde{M}_{F}
.
We will prove inductively that each
\alpha_{i}=0
and
v(\alpha_{i})=0
, in which case
T=0
which contradicts our assumption.
Since
V
is totally geodesic,
\nabla_{v}T\in\text{span}\{v,T\}
.
However,
v
has a nonzero
e_{2}
component while
T
and
\nabla_{v}T
do not, so
\nabla_{v}T\in\text{span}\{T\}
.
Then the
e_{1}
component of
\nabla_{v}T
is zero, so
-a_{1}\alpha_{1}=0
.
Since all
a_{i}\neq 0
, we must have
\alpha_{1}=0
.
This is true at every point in
\tilde{M}_{F}
, in particular in a neighborhood around
q
, so
v(\alpha_{1})=0
as well.
This proves the base case.

For the induction step, if we suppose that
\alpha_{1},\,\dots,\,\alpha_{i}
,
v(\alpha_{1}),\,\dots,\,v(\alpha_{i})=0
then the
T_{i}
component of

\nabla_{v}T

We therefore have that
-\alpha_{i+1}a_{i+1}=0
and so
\alpha_{i+1}=0
, and since this is true in an open set we also have that

, completing the induction argument.

We are now ready to prove Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem
C
.

Pick a leaf
\mathcal{F}_{p_{0}}
of the foliation
\mathcal{F}
and define a real-valued function on the set of leaves

B(\mathcal{F}_{p})\coloneqq\pm A(\mathcal{F}_{p_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{p})

where we assign a positive value on one side of
\mathcal{F}_{p_{0}}
and a negative value on the other.
By an argument similar to Lemma
6.1
,
B
is an injection from the set of leaves to
\mathbb{R}
.
Similarly we may define

A^{*}(p,q)\coloneqq\pm A(p,q)

where we assign a positive or negative sign depending on the relative positions of
\mathcal{F}_{p}
and
\mathcal{F}_{q}
and such that it agrees with the choice relative to
\mathcal{F}_{p_{0}}
.
That is,
B(\mathcal{F}_{p})=A^{*}(p_{0},p)
where
p_{0}\in\mathcal{F}_{p_{0}}
.

Now recall that the value of
A(p,q)
is path-independent as long as the path does not cross back over previously traversed leaves.
So if we take points
p_{1}
,
q_{0}
,
q_{1}\in\tilde{M}
with
p_{1}
,
q_{0}
,
q_{1}
all on the same side of
\mathcal{F}_{p_{0}}
, then

\displaystyle A^{*}(p_{0},q_{0})-A^{*}(p_{0},q_{1})

\displaystyle=(A^{*}(p_{0},p_{1})+A^{*}(p_{1},q_{0}))-(A^{*}(p_{0},p_{1})+A^{*}(p_{1},q_{1}))

\displaystyle=A^{*}(p_{1},q_{0})-A^{*}(p_{1},q_{1}).

We may remove the assumption that all points are on the same side of
p_{0}
by applying this multiple times.
It follows that

(6.1)

\displaystyle B(g(\mathcal{F}_{p}))-B(g(\mathcal{F}_{q}))

\displaystyle=A^{*}(\mathcal{F}_{p_{0}},g(\mathcal{F}_{p}))-A^{*}(\mathcal{F}_{p_{0}},g(\mathcal{F}_{q}))

\displaystyle=A^{*}(g(\mathcal{F}_{p_{0}}),g(\mathcal{F}_{p}))-A^{*}(g(\mathcal{F}_{p_{0}}),g(\mathcal{F}_{q}))

\displaystyle=A^{*}(\mathcal{F}_{p_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{p})-A^{*}(\mathcal{F}_{p_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{q})

\displaystyle=B(\mathcal{F}_{p})-B(\mathcal{F}_{q}).

We therefore get an induced action of
G
on the image of
B
given by
g\cdot B(\mathcal{F}_{p}):=B(g(\mathcal{F}_{p}))
.
Using this definition,
G
acts fixed-point freely since
g\in G
cannot fix a leaf without being the identity element, and the action is by isometries in the absolute value metric on
\mathbb{R}
.
So if
G\neq\{e\}
then the image of
B
is all of
\mathbb{R}
since
G
acts on it by nontrivial translations and by the definition of
B
its image is connected.

If
G
acts properly discontinuously on
\mathbb{R}
then it must be trivial or
\mathbb{Z}
, so suppose it does not.
Such an action on
\mathbb{R}
must then have every orbit dense.
Recall that
a_{1}
is a continuous function, so if it ever vanishes on some leaf then it must vanish on a dense set and then be identically zero.
But this contradicts the assumption that
M
is locally irreducible, so without loss of generality
a_{1}>0
everywhere.
Since
a_{1}
never vanishes,
T_{1}
is defined everywhere and so we can assume that
a_{2}
, which was defined in terms of
T_{1}
, is continuous.
If
a_{2}
ever vanishes then it does so on a dense set and so must vanish everywhere.
We repeat this argument, so without loss of generality all
a_{i}>0
everywhere.

We therefore have a smooth foliation with global coordinates
(x,u,v_{i})
as in Theorem
A
.
Construct these coordinates by picking a
p_{0}\in\mathcal{F}_{p_{0}}
and
p_{0}=(0,0,\dots,0)
.
Since the orbit of 0 in the image of
B
is dense there exists a sequence
g_{k}\in G\setminus\{e\}
such that
p_{k}:=g_{k}(p_{0})\in\mathcal{F}_{p_{k}}
and
B(\mathcal{F}_{p_{k}})\to 0
as

.
Since
G
acts properly discontinuously on
\tilde{M}
, there can only be finitely many
p_{k}
in any compact neighborhood of
p_{0}
.
We will now show that the
u
and
v_{i}
coordinates of the
p_{k}

To see this let
x_{k}
be the
x

p_{k}

q_{k}=(x_{k},0,\dots,0)
.
We want
\beta\neq 0
at
q_{k}
.
If taking such a subsequence is impossible then
\beta(q_{k})=\beta(x_{k},0,\dots,0)=0

.
In this case we may pick a different

p_{0}=(0,\varepsilon,0,\dots,0)
for some small
\varepsilon
and constructing new coordinates with this new

as the origin.
This will not change which leaves the
p_{k}
are in but will shift the
q_{k}
such that
\beta(q_{k})\neq 0
for all sufficiently large
k
.

Then as
k\to\infty
,
q_{k}\to p_{0}
since
B(\mathcal{F}_{q_{k}})=B(\mathcal{F}_{p_{k}})\to 0
and hence
x_{k}\to 0
.
Consider also
g_{k}^{-1}(q_{k})
, which lies in
\mathcal{F}_{p_{0}}
because
q_{k}
and
p_{k}
are connected by a geodesic in the leaf
\mathcal{F}_{p_{k}}

g_{k}^{-1}(q_{k})

\mathcal{F}_{p_{0}}

Furthermore, the length of this geodesic is also preserved.
Therefore if we show that the

and

To see that the

a_{1}(g_{k}^{-1}(q_{k}))=\frac{f_{1}(0)}{\eta(0,u_{k})}

T_{i}(e_{2}(a_{j}))=e_{2}(T_{i}(a_{j}))+[T_{i},e_{2}](a_{j})=-(\nabla_{e_{2}}T_{i})(a_{j})=\begin{cases}a_{1}a_{j}\beta&i=1,\\
