Data Flex: On-Platform Organisations
Álvarez-Teleña, Sergio, Díez-Fernández, Marta

Abstract
The natural alignment between business and architecture within big techs has boosted their transformation (crucially, upon API-fication and synergies exploitation) compared to that in the rest of organisations. The efficiency gap is so large that even the latter fear the irruption of big techs in their own arenas. Nevertheless, organisations have lately lost control of their architectures. They have become a mix of services offered by big techs and orchestrated by external consultants. Such a dynamic has naturally led to a large convergence between architectures across industries in spite of their idiosyncratic differences. Hence, there is room for improvement through a transformation governance that optimally weighs both microeconomics and microservices. As neither of the fields is easy to master, such an improvement remains a greenfield. This paper proposes a novel data architecture paradigm, Data Flex, that helps organisations take control of their transformation journey by becoming platforms - i.e. unlocking convergence with big techs’ efficiency levels. Further, it surpasses the theory by having evolved Data Flex’s first instance for the last 7 years. Along that time, the authors gathered real examples that filled out a cube defined by a series of dimensions significant enough to assert the universal validity of their approach.

1.1.1.1  Innovation management

If an organisation wants to become a monopoly upon technology advances it needs to manage its innovation properly. But, there is little information about innovation management to be inspired by. On one hand, companies are largely secretive around their impactful innovation. And, on the other, there is a lot of noise in the shape of marketing around the dubious impact. We wanted to understand, first hand, the pros and cons of each disclosure, filtering out the realistic statements and building up our own.

First, we delved into microeconomic theory. Therein, technology innovation is said to shift the supply. It leads to more quantity of a product at lower prices or, equally, to larger quantities at the same price. This is, it translates into a large competitive advantage towards taking over a market.

But the theory ends there. Economists know the
what
and the
why
behind that change but not exactly the
how
. That is a role for the technologists of the company. But these, in turn, often lose sight of the former two. And, as a result, innovation happens suboptimally. Those companies capable of aligning the economists judgement with that of the technologists will be able to reach optimality and take over their markets.

1.1.1.2  Transformation management

So, what can a company do to survive monopolistic threats through its own algorithmization in a context of lack of senior algorithmic talent and a high rotation of juniors? Within such question there are several worries out of which we will highlight two.

First, the talent. As we have seen, senior talent can’t be asked to know about the three domains: business, science and technology. As such, they need help. In order to be appropriately boosted, they need a tool and a protocol that allows them to orchestrate the junior talent in angles they do not master themselves (e.g. best practices in coding, compliance guarantees, security, privacy, calibration, etc). Further, they can’t lose sight of current traditional talent at the company, whose responsibilities and workflows may need to change as they are combined with the former. As they do so, the productivity can be eroded due to change resistance.

Second, more subtle and a consequence of the former, the intellectual property. If a company is becoming an algorithm it means that its DNA is now crystal clear - including that of its main competitive advantages. If a high-rotating profile has access to it, it can easily be moved from a company to another.

The solution for the two is technology.

By managing the flow of the business through on-platform protocols a company can ensure a homogeneous quality of the required transformation processes. The technology gives a structure to the factory that is thought to provide the aforementioned above average performance. Employees thereon can propose new changes to be approved by the right governance committee but cannot deviate from it - there is no off-platform path. The platform becomes this way the central piece, the core, of a company. And its main competitive advantage.

Furthermore, the design of the very same on-platform protocols shall dissect, in every project, a well-thought protection of the IP. Hence, the protection occurs in the company by design. As we will see, federation as in Data Flex can play a major role here as well.

In our case though, as our core product is the instance of a Data Flex, we had to design and code everything up from scratch. It took us 6 years to complete the whole structure in a secretive manner - even from our employees

12

12

. Meanwhile, we had to prove ourselves our capacity to scale our impact through our employees without compromising our IP. We deployed our platform in full as a driver framework and then let our employees grow it in federated pockets. By having them grow our factory framework in federated pockets we leverage talent that is:

1.

Decentralised: they have independence at developing pieces yet following our governance guidance; their code can be easily audited, tracked and changed; they can not generate large spaghetti code; a peer can review all that a coder has done and take over at ease; etc. In the limit, we used our own natural rotation of junior talent as a proof for this.

2.

But not independent: even though the overall IP was not being disclosed to them they were adding impact to the aforementioned innovation-upon-innovation. No one knows all the IP, really;, just the company as a whole: the domain expert defines the algorithm and protects it, the data scientists calibrate it and provide new models to be incorporated, and the rest of technologists maintain the platform as a whole upon synergies exploitation.

Thereon, as we will see, a company simply needs to decide which pieces of architecture shall be developed in-house and which pieces shall be borrowed from external parties

1.1.1.3  Startup ecosystem gap

One of the things that struck us from a microeconomic point of view was the effect of the funding ecosystem into the competition landscape.

Angel investors and venture capitalists mostly funded laser-focused, fast-delivery projects. Especially the latter, for a reason.

On one hand, very often they have a laser-focus investment scope themselves that they pitch to the LPs (their main investors) hence, they can not deviate from a cut-clean topic. On the other hand, their funds have a typical lifespan of 10 years - the first 3 are used to trade-in and the last 3 to trade-out. Obviously then, they cannot take risks with projects that take time to deliver no matter the ambition.

Being this type of startups an economic stable equilibrium it could lead to a massive competitive advantage to any company able to bootstrap a cross-industries, long-run project
14
14

14

As said, only if it had the capacity to cope with the pace of innovation.
. Had those companies been launched back in the mid 2010s they would have led to a rare agent in the early 2020s. We aimed at being one of those rare agents.

1.1.1.4  Recent learnings from blockchain technology

During the last years we have closely followed the evolution of blockchain technology from a microeconomic perspective. We believe it has unlocked a new transformation context.

Blockchain technology remains a massive experiment. There have been foreseeable cons that could have been avoided but there have also been notorious, unexpected successes. We are especially supporters of the unexpected acceptance by tier one companies that it has triggered in spite of being a disruption. In fact, we often refer to it as a phenomenon rather than a technology. There has been a massive community support by very skillful technologists that, probably, has lacked the above alignment with microeconomic theory.

One of the areas we often highlight the most is its top-down capacity to break silos across industries and companies. It has proved that the global economy was far from being open since legacies (including governances) across industries were rigid, far from each other and thus, from optimal.

As we will see, we believe a different architecture proposition shall be provided to the organisations to take over their algorithmization process, such as the one herein proposed, by breaking silos in a different manner.

1.1.2
Technology: the M in ML

To the light of the aforementioned complexity to design architectures in the intersection between domain, science and technology, data architectures have largely focused on the digitalization process of a company. On becoming data-driven.

We consider digitalization as just an input of a more ambitious target of the companies: their algorithmization. Algorithmization is the process by which a company evolves upon a series of on-platform protocols that seek maximal efficiency in the combination of humans and machines. Whether augmented humans or, more interestingly, augmented machines.

We have been pioneers in the usage of machine learning (ML) algorithmics in finance back in the late 2000s. And by the early 2010s we realised that the competitive advantage could not lie in the L of ML (the learning, the models).

The L is too competitive. Skillful academics are continuously evolving it through the scientific method. This basically ensures that if you come up with a significant proprietary idea, they will sooner than later arrive at the same conclusion. Further, they broadcast their knowledge and even their code through open source libraries. The steady competitive advantage had then to be somewhere else
15
15

15

Some claimed it to be the data. But obviously, data is mostly shared by peers. Hence, it could not be there either.
.

From our experience, we firmly believed that the edge could still be in the L but if, and only if, the M that underpins it is ambitious enough. The priority then became the creation of a proprietary M that allows for optimal combinations of Ls - smartly, timely and compliantly. As said above, a much less known challenge than the models, and very much protected as black-boxes.

Background

In order to understand the reasons behind the current need for algorithmic-driven data architectures we introduce a number of dimensions around theory and practice so that the reader can gain judgement on the differences between Digitalization and Algorithmization.

2.1
State-of-the-art data architectures evolution

This section is aimed at providing the necessary background to understand the current context of data architectures.

2.1.1
Overview of terminology

Data can be accumulated in databases or in file systems. The former are faster to query as they are already partly living in the RAM of a server. But, given the amount of data nowadays and the fact that RAMs are one of the most expensive pieces of the servers, they are expensive to maintain. On the other hand, file systems are cheaper as they live in the hard disk but, for that very reason, they are slower to query.

Parallelization strategies for data queries across servers (e.g. MapReduce) are often used to avoid the non-linear increase in cost of a marginal unit of RAM within a server. These strategies spread data across servers and query data in an orchestrated manner. Their combination with file systems allows companies to store and query large amounts of data - big data. Note that there is no threshold for a dataset to be identified as big. We would argue that in reality the threshold has to be defined appraising its size with respect to the size of the RAM. If the mismatch calls for the need of parallelization strategies, it is big enough to be called a big data challenge.

Data can be communicated between nodes. These either share RAM leading to immediate exchanges of communication or they are coordinated through servers’ ports (sockets). Sockets serve as mere doors to exchange information (messages) whether through cable or Wi-Fi on networks that can be public (e.g. internet) or private (e.g. intranets). The communication shall follow a protocol that includes engagement (hands shake) and rules to convey the message in an understandable way by the stakeholders of the communication. When the communication is highly dynamic it is often managed by queues (a set of libraries upon socket communication). When queues are not enough to provide reliable communication, further management is granted by so-called buses.

The communicated messages are often called events. Hence, we refer to an event-driven process as the one that reacts in a timely manner, as new information arrives.

As the number of communicated processes (nodes) grows across servers we start talking about networks. In a network, not all nodes necessarily have the same relevance. Typically, those with the largest relevance are called orchestrators as they coordinate calls for actions across the rest of the nodes. An advanced orchestration process that has been broadly used (amongst others, by the algorithmic trading industry) is Complex Event Processing (CEP).

A piece of software is a set of machine instructions written in machine-interpretable language with a specific target. In order to make the most of the code synergies (key in terms of maintenance and resilience) a best practice pattern, object orientation (OOP), became popular. Basically, it was based on structuring pieces of code that accounted for data and models/methods towards their isolation in RAM and interaction with other similar pieces.

As software became more sophisticated, it moved from isolated servers to networks of servers that contain networks of nodes - note that the latter can share RAM as they can live in the same server. When the nodes of those networks are designed from inception to deploy minimum viable products (MVPs) upon minimum viable legacies (MVLs) it favours the overall architecture to be lean (it cannot be granted as developers still need to be creative at optimising the network). As such, mixing OOP and MVL is, in our view, the best practice nowadays.

While software leverages both data and methods/models, typically, its breakthroughs start from data-only versions. As we will see, not only has that been the case of the data architectures but also the evolution of the crypto ecosystem - while Bitcoin is a database, Ethereum includes pieces closer to objects through its addition of smart contracts (in reality, mere native methods).

As a result, the transformation of the industry has not been an exception - it has been dealing with its digitalization (data unlocking) before its algorithmization. From our experience, it seems that the industry is ready to move forward and, again, benefit from the unlocking of methods/models across enterprise architectures.

Federation vs decentralization and centralization discussion

It is important to discuss these concepts because their definition is often blurred away by the flexibility of the platforms - as they can often be instantiated as versions of either of the types - and, more subtly, because of the dimension of the platform to which they can be referring to - governance or architecture

24

In our view, this is the origin of many of the misconceptions around blockchain technologies.

We refer to a structure of agents around a platform as decentralized
if there is independence across them. Their inputs and output can differ significantly. The main pro of decentralization is its maximal freedom per agent and maximal IP protection, yet its main con is the risk of not being open enough to grant a timely collaboration across agents - especially, given the fact that the evolution of the industry suggests that we are moving towards open sectors.

A federated structure
is an orchestration of decentralized agents

Overall, we expect the backbone of those structures to be collaboration in a context of maximal IP protection - for both data and algorithms and whether across companies or, more subtly, across departments, as we will see along the document.

The committee’s voting method can be syndicated or weighted. If weighted, the weighting of the inputs can be static or dynamic. We suggest the latter is the best practice since the distribution shall be optimised depending on the nature of each challenge - the agent with most judgement shall have the largest weight. When the committee is a set of processes the weighting distributions lead to actual algorithms that are orchestrated across all parties which, in the case of different companies as stakeholders, we often refer to as super-algorithms.

A centralized structure
is one where there is orchestration yet no voting process. A unique agent rules out the different steps and protocols to be followed. No agent can protect its IP from the orchestrating one. Examples of centralized structures whose agents are teams span from the usual IT department and the regulator of an industry to blockchain non-profit associations that launch and evolve their own versions of the technology. Most of the time, these evolve from POC to MVP without a change of the structure hence, becoming legacies of low flexibility.
(POCs).

Further, we consider a structure of agents around a platform to account for redundancies
when agents are replicated in order to be reinforced (when the nodes are up) or substituted (when they are down). Redundancies are typically devoted to reinforce the resilience of a platform. When the redundancies mimic agents that belong to a third party (e.g. SaaS) we refer to
tactical technology

27

27

We will be disclosing a number of examples of those that we have launched for different clients.
. Last, the redundant agents can be permanent in the structure or ephemeral - built on-the-fly by algorithmic strategies as we will see in our experiments.

These structures can describe a platform on two different yet often confused dimensions:

•

Governance:
herein federation simply means a series of meetings across stakeholders to decide ad-hoc interoperability, documentation minimums, etc; centralization typically means that the stakeholders follow the industry standards set by, say, an association; and decentralisation typically means that the client adopts as legacy any of the different providers’ protocols.

•

Architecture:
herein federation often means that the servers from each stakeholder are communicated towards a collaborative output; centralization means that there is a department with access to every server and builds an output upon them; decentralization would describe a company built upon isolated units.

Confusions occur when partial (uni-dimensional) descriptions of an architecture are provided.

That is the usual case when
blockchain
technology is defined. It is said to be decentralized. However, it is also centralized. Why? Being a distributed ledger technology (DLT) it is an

Further, the distribution of agents leads to a parallelization of computation that, unlike the aforementioned big data strategies, is not thought towards efficiency but towards reliability - in the origin of blockchain technology there is an issue of trust of counterparties. This is, the largest part of a blockchain technology corresponds to redundant nodes that are not ephemeral but permanent. And their number is not optimised in terms of the nature of the challenge but in terms of the market value of the cryptocurrencies they mint to pay for the redundant agents’ maintenance. This leads to over-optimal redundant nodes. An inefficiency in terms of electricity costs (and with a large environmental impact) that prevents the technology to be a candidate as an enterprise software architecture.

That leads to a further dimension of blockchain architectures: public vs private. Above assertions correspond to the original public concept. As a result, private blockchains have surged. They are more strongly centralized (there is a screening of the nodes that can be included in the architecture), there is often not minting, and they aim at enhancing the efficiency of the technology around both costs and service level agreements (SLAs). This way they become more flexible and, at the same time, start converging with architectures like Data Flex
28
28

28

We believe that, a key feature behind blockchain technology, is that it is a top-down, must-follow protocol that eases the combination of microservices across companies downstream. Data Flex, in that sense, also eases it yet upstream - bottom-up and non-mandatory (opt-in based on efficiencies).

As we will see, even though they unlock breakthroughs at numerous companies they are not truly disruptions between them but rather incremental innovation. Mostly, they don’t compete between them - they are largely compatible dimensions of a complete architecture. However, the order in which they are contemplated can have a major impact in the configuration of the final legacy. And legacies, as said, can exponentially drive a company or strangle its efficiency.

Hence, selecting the first dimension,
the main driver

2.2.1
Services-driven architectures

Main driver: maximal architecture flexibility

These are known as service oriented architectures (SOAs). A key here is to define the service. Services are said to be computer logics that fulfil a complete, specific purpose. If that complete, specific purpose is a business unit, we typically talk about business oriented apps. If it is a technology unit, we typically talk about microservices.

Business apps are inputs towards building platforms. Their scope typically is end-to-end within a department need (often by laser-focused startups).

Microservices were first introduced by Gartner in 1996

29

29

“A service-oriented architecture is a style of multi-tier computing that helps organisations share logic and data among multiple applications and usage modes.”
. The scope of their logics is typically smaller than that of business apps - hence, its name - since its users, technologists, are more prone to demand micro pieces of their workstream than the apps’ users.

The key is the optimization of both service layers, business and technology. And this is a major challenge that we aim at tackling along the document.

There is an exception though. When a company’s business is technology they are both aligned. Hence, by nature, they become utterly efficient. Not surprisingly it is the big techs the ones that fall under this exception

Latency is often the price for flexibility. But, still, that is not always the case - the extra latency is minimal if the connection across servers occurs through cables (e.g. cloud architectures and trading exchanges

2.2.3
Event-driven data architectures

Main driver: timely online exploitation

Kappa

4.2.1
Memory management: cheap, green, accurate

Due to Digitalization, data is growing exponentially across organisations. And, as we saw, there are broadly two types of databases to manage it: in-memory and on-disk. The fastest of the two is, without a doubt, the former as it leverages the RAM of a computer. But RAMs are a major cost in infrastructure maintenance - the cost of increasing it within a machine grows non-linearly.

So, exponential data growth would lead to exponential data bills. And, as such, organisations have to weigh the in-memory vs on-disk dilemma.

Most of the advances around in-memory data management leverage networks of servers. Instead of growing the RAM of a machine, big techs evolve
algorithmic strategies
around microservices that allow their clients to reach almost the same speed on data retrieval but, instead, at a linear cost - that of each machine involved.

But what if a company does not want the cost to rise
61
61

61

From our experience this is the usual demand from SMEs and, more interestingly, a growing one by listed companies.
? We aimed at keeping the data bill fairly steady by creating algorithmic strategies that leveraged on-disk databases while using the on-memory dimension to optimise the UX of the employee.

Note that, very crucially, this approach also leads to greener algorithms by design given the way servers are managed.

But there is more to memory management than the bill, the UX or even the environmental consequences. Because beyond the data there is the algorithm. As formerly defined, an algorithm is a combination of heuristics and models that live in a machine. This is, the characteristics of the machine can affect the output of an algorithm (not only its speed but also its output due to the management of, for instance, asynchronous information). A main characteristic is the available RAM. Two algorithms that are demanding in RAM can be very predatory to each other when co-existing in the same machine. As such, the memory has to be monitored and managed. And it is this management that calls for smart actions.

Business

An asset manager wants to construct and manage its portfolios using a mix of alpha-driven views coming from its employees and real-time algorithms searching autonomously for opportunities. Hence, there are human data queries and machine algorithmics co-existing in a given architecture.

Science

One solution can be, on one hand, an offline algorithm that takes into account the usage of the data by each user (recurrency, data quantity, data type, etc) to decide which data is going to be loaded in-memory as a way to optimise the UX of the employees.

And, on the other, another algorithm, this time online, that checks the RAM availability and releases the least probable data to be used by the employees in order not to affect the performance of the running algorithms.

IT

The solution has to be scalable, microserviced across nodes. Further, it has to run stressed scenarios in order to decide which smart actions are going to be taken in those situations. In particular, we consider three:

1.

Gradual alerts: triggered by memory thresholds and unusual demands from employees;

2.

Risks mitigations: list priorities by ranking, case by case, around individuals vs algorithms dependent on a target function that weighs seniority of the former and accuracy of the latter; and

3.

Punctual over-delivery: ephemeral networks (ideally in the cloud) to, for example, move the database away from the algorithms’ servers and ensure both dimensions, humans and machines, are properly served.

Experts need first to understand the platform they can count on in order to keep evolving their business based on algorithmics. Only then, protocols can be re-designed towards a rigorous process around the distribution of the funds across beta, alpha and cash:

1.

Beta: this is the part of a fund that is intended not to take any risk. It is aimed at minimising the tracking error with respect to a benchmark (whether defined as an instrument or as a KPI target around a set of selected peers). Its construction follows a top-down approach: from
tags
optimal distribution to
instruments’
subsequent distributions. The mandate defines the nature of the fund across a number of tags - fixed income, equities, currencies and commodities distributions’ thresholds along with regional ones. Once the tags are optimised, the distribution of their instruments can be allocated through different strategies. As we are in the beta step we recommend being as neutral as possible with regards to the benchmark. This is, if there is a tradable benchmark, we should take it directly; else, we should distribute the instruments within each tag seeking to optimise both liquidity and diversification.

2.

Alpha: this is the part intended to add extra value to the investor. We distinguish two types of alpha.

(a)

First, an alpha correlated with the beta: buy and hold of a similar universe of instruments to that of the benchmark. Once constructed it is managed through rebalance strategies of the pairs trading type (selected portfolio vs benchmark). Those strategies can be originated from a macroeconomic point of view and/or a microeconomic one both upon human sentiment or machine-driven. Further, leveraging them all, it can define new KPIs to be optimised as a mixture of past data and future expectations, weighted by the user. Finally, they are bounded by both: i) whether the expected alpha compensates the new tracking error plus the market impact and transaction costs implied by the proposed rebalance, and ii) the threshold limits ultimately described in the mandate of the fund.

(b)

Second, an orthogonal/uncorrelated alpha. These can be liquidity takers, liquidity providers or market neutral. The former two are tricky as liquidity provision (being short in the market) raises cash that can be further used in liquidity taking strategies
71
71

71

This is only the case for the agents that can go short on instruments such as hedge funds. That said, it is also true that this can be overcome by, for instance, buying ETFs of shorted instruments. Liquidity taking and liquidity providing strategies are typically mixed up in ratios. E.g. a 2:1 ratio means that half of the liquidity taking strategy is funded by the liquidity providing one.
. Market neutral, is usually a 1:1 ratio of the former two and, when the instrument is the same, it is said to be a market making strategy. These are typically allowed to more agents than hedge funds as long as, by the end of the day, they are all with positive or zero inventory. They can be again originated by humans or by machines and they are managed by taking profits and stopping losses just as in proprietary trading.

3.

Cash: from the start, it is just the difference between the total budget and the former two. Then on, it is an instrument to top up strategies that have hit stop-losses so that all bets account for the same notional and the Law of Large Numbers (LLN) can lead to the mean reversion where the competitive advantage lies. Else, if the trades are different, the LLN does not apply in notional - there would be random asymmetries in the outcomes of the trades.

As usual in transformation, the above can lead to a number of confusions and change resistance. It is important to establish mechanisms to track the personas that can have professional interests away from the overall plan and manage those deviations through checkpoints and the right incentives for everyone.

Science

The number of challenges are endless. Here some examples:

1.

The beta part: often leverages the understanding of the peers whose composition is unknown in real time yet published periodically with a lag. Another challenge is to deal with the peers as if they were a neat benchmark. We call that process the
benchmarkization
of the peers and it requires a hybrid function (close to the utility of the portfolio manager around a set of KPIs) to be optimised typically through nonlinear optimization techniques. A further scientific challenge is the translation of the portfolio from mere distribution of tags to actual instruments. Sales agenda optimization

The sales team of a company - from call centres to travelling salesmen - is a key asset that shall start being optimised. Nevertheless, it is the source of revenues of a company.

We are surprised by the usage of recommender systems that are popular in data science forums but belong to companies with a completely different data nature and consequences from wrong recommendations. More interestingly yet, most recommender systems that we have seen treat all data as equal. The thing is that between today, when the company is optimising the agenda of its sales force, and date D there can be a massive difference in context. 6.2.2
Central Risk Book

There is an interesting issue in the trading floors: while traders can be taking positions that are good unilaterally they can be compromising the trading floor overall. For example, in terms of risk they could all be building non-diversified exposure, or in terms of interest they could be building exposure on opportunities that are not aligned with the needs of the overall bank (e.g. mid-to-long run targets not incentivised by the bonus scheme).

In order to manage all that controversy in a lean way, the most dynamic agents have been managing strategies that balance risks across assets through a new unit: the Central Risk Book (CRB). Basically, it is an agent that quotes market prices, internally, that has priority before a strategy is routed to an exchange. As such, the price fluctuation risk is transferred from the trader to the CRB and that allows its interim management by the latter.

However, similarly to the subsection
6.2.1
challenge, since not all of the most dynamic agents accounted for federated technology the implementation of the CRB has been significantly different across the industry. We have seen many tier two banks managing the CRB manually, at very low frequencies and upon basic risk management tools. As such, they are very far from being optimal.

Business

There are many examples of the usage of a CRB. Let’s focus on an eloquent one. Say a trader wants to buy stock X, and the CRB discovers that there is already too much risk built on X or correlated with it. The CRB then can take its quote and synthetically (e.g. in a swap format) provide the exposure to stock X to the trader at a fair price. As a result, the CRB has internally shorted the risk of X and can now hedge it with another stock, more aligned with the overall needs of the whole trading floor.

Note that the target profits of the CRB is typically zero.

Science

The management of such a position relates to pairs trading. More precisely, to statistical arbitrage. Hence, all the models from section
5.10
should be highly needed here.

Since the algorithmic traders follow systematic strategies the trades can be directly matched in large blocks rather than in small quotes.

Further, the CRB can estimate future flows very precisely with the dynamic information coming from the configuration of the different algorithms of the floor’s traders.

IT

The challenge here is the creation of an internal market per synthetic instrument which has priority over the external market. Then, the synthetic match with the trader has to be timely booked.

6.3
Risks algorithmic strategies: a novel mitigation

By federating the different nodes within an algorithmic trading platform there is a significant room for the improvement of risk management.

So far, most of the smart actions proposed by the risk departments were often criticised by traders due to the predatory effect that they have on their traditional PAs.

However, since the EPAs transform those smart actions from servers’ resources predators to mere listeners with their own federated architectures a whole new level of risk management can be granted.

The following example has been introduced at different forums as a major advancement for the risk vertical. In particular, at an industry-led event organised by the main companies of energy in Europe. Therein, the disruption gave rise to a deep discussion between half of the industry that embraced the new set of tools that this approach unlocked, and the other half that felt more comfortable with the status-quo.

Business

One of the main issues in algorithmic trading is that financial agents have algorithms taking actions at below second speed while leaving risk managers taking actions at above human speed - even the human traders get the information and have the capacity to take actions, much faster than them.

In order to reach a healthy risk management equilibrium, the risk department needs to launch smart actions that can affect the configuration of the live algorithms - e.g. their aggressivity levels and their inventory management.

To start, they can follow the next steps.

First, they should stress the algorithms and understand their behaviour under different scenarios. They need to know under which circumstances the algorithms can alter the markets the most and when they can be more internally dangerous in terms of both exposure building and operational risk. There are several ways to set up these scenarios - we will reveal the state-of-the-art in section
6.4
.

Then, they can define a series of KPIs to check, real time, significant waterfalls of thresholds whose continuous trespassing can trigger a number of different actions: from mere alerts to indeed strategies that ultimately take control of an algorithm seeking maximal prudence.

Further, note that accounting for risk algorithmic strategies is of crucial importance in terms of a trading floor’s dynamics. When there is an event that requires the intervention from risks there is significant pressure and tension across employees. By knowing during the discussions between traders and risk managers that there is an algorithm taking temporary control (rather than another one drifting in the markets), part of the stress can be released and better conclusions on next steps can be reached. This is a subtle dimension that, in turn, can have a major impact.

The scientific challenges are, by definition, not far from those in section
5.10

IT

1.

To let a risk manager decide different configurations of an algorithm dependent on the different thresholds, and

2.

To let that configuration be attached on top of that of the trader on-the-fly so that the algorithm changes its behaviour in a timely manner. To the best of our knowledge we are the first ones to have achieved it.

6.4
VR simulation: calibration and black-box auditing

Simulation engines are a major asset in algorithmic-driven businesses. They are typically largely academic in the sense that they leverage models of more or less complexity whose assumptions are key to believe in the realism of their results. For instance, one of the most populars is Agent Based Models (ABMs) which, overall, lies on the assumption that many agents of low complexity can lead to dynamics similar to real agents.

As a number of traders and risk managers across investment banks did complain about the lack of control on such types of simulation processes we started thinking of a new one where their insights could become a main driver instead.

The framework was clear: we could leverage our
M in ML
. If we were able to launch the most advanced investment strategies through our technology then we were able to launch the most basic ones. And, in between, the whole distribution of agents in the market. As such, the user had to decide which distributions she wanted her algorithm to face and let the algorithm interact with the market.

This way, not only the traders can use it for parameter calibration if the ecosystems generated are realistic (easier for large market making players that account for large flows

Fraud across banks and insurance companies

Even though CISOs often gather to share views on their roles at country-level events, there has traditionally been a low cooperation between them. As seen all throughout the paper, the reason seems to be that the different organisations see each other as competitors. On top, given their secretive nature, any improvement therein achieved through proprietary innovation leads to an over protection of its intellectual property - which consequently adds erosion to the collaborative mindset.

As seen, federated architectures allow organisations to fully interact while managing the level of disclosure of their intellectual property. As a result, a new way of collaboration can be unlocked within the next decade.

Business

Fraudsters often remain as such across sectors. This is, a person who commits frauds at a bank not only is likely to be a fraudster at another bank but also at, say, an insurance company.

Science

The problem with digital fraud detection are the false positives. Since there are very few examples of true positives (real fraudsters) the algorithms are not as accurate as we wished they were. And, the problem lies in the fact that, given the relevance of the accusation, a thorough analysis to detect a fraud takes a lot of human effort - this is, it is expensive.

So, the challenge is to find as much information as possible to add significant context to a positive signal so that trues can be discerned from falses.

IT

There are two key first steps to erode fraud through collaboration across companies:

1.

Fine tune the algorithm so that the false negative, the fraudster that wasn’t flagged, becomes a positive one and, more interestingly, share the MAP of such a model as a service. This way, the data of the fraudster is not shared by any of the companies but the fraudster will be flagged across those companies that are capable of feeding the model with the required significant data - i.e. it depends on the model whether the MAP can be used by many or just a few companies.

2.

Allow consulting the flag of an individual across companies. This can have ethical issues and, as such, the whole process shall be transparent and auditable for supervisors to check. We expect the order of the process to be of crucial significance. This is, last checks-only of dubious tags should be strictly preferred to greedy checks across databases in order to avoid mere black lists. Further, data can be anonymized for two reasons. First, so that in case of a hack the information is not distributed. And second, so that the company checking does not get individual information but the distribution of fraudsters within reduced/micro clusters

On-platform kitchens

The food industry is being industrialised in many dimensions.

For many years now we have been witnessing a change from small restaurants and bakeries to large franchises whose major competitive advantage is the capacity to exploit synergies and whose major challenge is the capacity to scale while keeping prices and quality homogeneous.

Caterings and dark kitchens do account for the same challenge: kitchens are factories that have to be operated in an orchestrated manner.

This is, kitchens have to become on-platform organisations.

Business

The whole process involved in the restaurant industry can be overall summarised as follows.

First, there is the anticipated sales. These are made of reservations and estimations. Caterings and hotels can accurately measure them as they work through booked events - i.e. all of their sales are basically reservations. The rest, need to face the challenge of demand estimations - which, even though remains a major challenge, it can be optimised with data and, more especially, with Augmented Machines.
Once the agenda is fixed the purchase of inputs can take place optimally - ensuring volume discounts are maximised and leftovers minimised - crucial also in terms of environmental impact. More interestingly, as many of the inputs are often bought at auctions, it is crucial for the purchase team to understand the costs linked to each of the products throughout the agenda in order to sensibly quote their bids - just as traders do when pricing a product as a margin upon the cost of its hedge.

Thereon, the inputs are gradually processed across a number of tasks that require both machines and human employees interaction. Instead of simply following the steps of a project (a cooking recipe) in line, some tasks can be parallelized within a project and some across projects. Currently, the most efficient agents are good at parallelising within a project. But the largest efficiencies lie in systematising the synergies across them. Hence, there is a major task around distribution of tasks optimization to benefit from.

Beyond that, the relationships with the clients can be significantly enhanced through technology. For example, there can be a more active management of each client through gamification (ad-hoc discounts if they bring more people, etc). That is a usually explored channel by the largest franchises. But it often leads to becoming yet another spam account at the client. As such, they can also interact passively with the client who logs-in and chats with a bot that proposes a number of possibilities to pick - all ad-hoc to the person and tracked and controlled by the machine through a thorough marketing strategy.

Last but not least, new products and services can be easily unlocked by using the massive amount of clients engaged with the largest franchises in order to cross-sell the products and services from other companies (including across-industries) boosted by architectures like Data Flex.

8.6
New markets unlock: energy

Regulation and subsidies are boosting the existence of new energy markets - the so-called, alternative energy. Both are crucial levers of environmental policies. But markets are not only innovation from the supply side. There must also be a demand and, with it, an equilibrium price. Yet both, supply and demand, are still difficult to set up.

Energy companies often need to fix the price for a certain time horizon for a client. But both, the provider and the client, need to be happy with that price. Right now, there are very few crosses in the industry as none of the parties feel comfortable enough to commit.

A fair price is often built by providers after exploring thoroughly the costs of generating the service and the risks embedded in its dynamic hedging. That leads to a minimum price to sell.

On the other hand, the clients use the asset as a production input of their own market. Hence, by looking at the business numbers they can also discover the maximum price they are willing to buy.

In mature markets where there is perfect competition, both would cross at a fair equilibrium for the two. At immature markets, none of the two agents feel comfortable hence, on top of the fact that supply and demand require investment on innovation, the final impact on the environment lags behind it should if we accounted for technology that facilitated the cross.

9.1
Authentication algorithmic strategies

Even though it is not strictly necessary, authentication mostly occurs through an interface.

Following the concept of bootstrapping, once a company’s platform has been deployed with Data Flex technology and to the light of the frontier of knowledge shifts evidenced above, it is easy to understand that each company can (and should) launch different strategies for authenticating their employees and clients.

Multi-channel has become the standard in authentication through a two-step process that usually involves the user’s mobile phone.

This is enough for the median company. But not for the most ambitious ones in terms of security and intellectual property and/or customer protection. For instance, layers of protection that leverage geospatial coordinates and behaviour patterns of usage per employee can be devoted to trigger timely smart actions.

Smart actions can range from mere alerts to more complete strategies that gradually reduce the access to features (e.g. locking the riskiest actions available to an individual) or, in the limit, to the direct sign-out of the user and the removal of its internet protocol number from the white list of the orchestrating servers.

As in the case of cyber security, we won’t be providing more details in this public document for obvious reasons.

9.2
UX-efficiency equilibrium

From our experience, there are two types of enterprise interfaces: those that seek a better UX in the employees’ daily tasks, and those that seek their maximal efficiency at providing the output. Even though they often seem aligned the truth is that they are not, and that depending on the culture and/or the stage of transformation one has more relevance than the other - e.g. at earlier stages in cultures resistant to change the former shall be prioritised.

As such, we always convey the message that both are relevant and shall be carefully optimised - the target is to minimise any loss of efficiency the sooner the better. Nevertheless, on one hand, a company needs to make sure that its employees find their daily tasks easy if it wants them to have time to keep thinking and proposing better protocols towards further innovation. And on the other, it needs them to adapt to new protocols, often compatible with changes in the rest of the organisation in a timely manner.

So, in the end, the interface becomes ad-hoc across clients as an idiosyncratic equilibrium of the former two: UX vs efficiency. As a result, the UX of the enterprise software interfaces are more complex to design than those that face clients instead.

9.2.1
GUI

GUIs are non-code approaches
107
107

107

The most advanced ones add flexibility to boost the pace of innovation stressed throughout the paper upon pseudo-code.
. The user directly triggers actions that subsequently flow within the Data Flex infrastructure by navigating whether an internet site (if as-a-service) or a downloaded software (if on-premise). All the API communications with the company’s user-interaction orchestrating server (sockets, queues or buses) are hidden away. As such it is easier to protect than through libraries.

Finally, note that GUIs have to be revisited from time to time as the protocol adapts to new evolution. As such, we have found that a couple of best practices seem to be: i) the GUI has to be as tactical as possible (lean and homogeneous) and ii) it has to be built using open source software as that is easier to take over by as many developers as possible.

9.2.2
API libraries

Usually this is the preferred interface by technologists (from data scientists and data engineers to IT developers). These are subsets of code devoted to control the communication with the company’s user-interaction orchestrating server, acting as a wrapper that converts user-friendly calls into the ones that the machine understands by design. As such, in a way, they are also a UX feature.

Having them fully exposed to the coder, they become riskier than the GUIs. But, as conveyed along the document, further protection can be granted if the API does not communicate with the different micro structures directly but through both a VR CISO MAP and a VR compliance MAP.

9.2.3
Voice

As a result of the aforementioned UX-efficiency equilibrium we have launched a voice-driven protocol internally towards the reporting from our employees.

Voice not only can save time that would otherwise be lost in complex GUIs navigation (like those involved in inventory management or, say, a grocery) but, more crucially, they also unlock tasks for which there is a natural resistance.

Last but not least, as thoroughly asserted along the document, the governance of the transformation of a company is largely underpinned by the on-platform protocols that surround it.
