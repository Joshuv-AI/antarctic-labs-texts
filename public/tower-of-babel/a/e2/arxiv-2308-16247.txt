Monotonicity conjecture for multi-party entanglement I
Abhijit Gadde a {}^{\,a} , Shraiyance Jain a {}^{\,a} , Vineeth Krishna a {}^{\,a} , Harshal Kulkarni a , b {}^{\,a,b} , Trakshu Sharma a {}^{\,a}

Abstract
In this paper, we conjecture a monotonicity property that we call monotonicity under coarse-graining for a class of multi-partite entanglement measures. We check these properties by computing the measures for various types of states using different methods.



1
Introduction and motivation

What is a good measure of quantum entanglement? This question has been widely addressed for
mixed
states, see
Horodecki:2009zz
for a review and an extensive list of references. It is known that a good entanglement measure
{\cal M}(\rho_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{\mathtt{q}}})
defined for a density matrix over
{\mathtt{q}}
parties
A_{1}
to
A_{\mathtt{q}}
must have the following properties
Bennett:1996gf
;
Vedral:1997qn
;
Vidal:1998re
,

1.

{\cal M}(\rho_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{\mathtt{q}}})
is invariant under local unitary transformations (
lu
) i.e. under,

\displaystyle\rho_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{\mathtt{q}}}\to U^{\dagger}_{{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes U^{\dagger}_{{\mathtt{q}}}\,\rho_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{\mathtt{q}}}\,U_{{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes U_{{\mathtt{q}}}.

(1)

2.

{\cal M}(\rho_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{\mathtt{q}}})
is monotonically non-increasing under local operations and classical communications (
locc
) i.e. under,

\displaystyle\rho_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{\mathtt{q}}}\to\sum_{i}L^{(i)\dagger}_{{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes L^{(i)\dagger}_{{\mathtt{q}}}\,\rho_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{\mathtt{q}}}\,L^{(i)}_{{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes L^{(i)}_{{\mathtt{q}}}.

(2)

where the operators
L^{(i)}_{\mathtt{a}}
obey the trace preserving condition
\sum_{i}L^{\dagger(i)}_{\mathtt{a}}L^{(i)}_{\mathtt{a}}={\mathbb{I}}
and the overall operation is also trace preserving i.e.
\sum_{i}\otimes_{\mathtt{a}}L^{\dagger(i)}_{\mathtt{a}}L^{(i)}_{\mathtt{a}}={\mathbb{I}}
.

The rationale for the first condition is clear. The local unitary operations furnish a change of basis in individual Hilbert spaces and it is expected that the entanglement measure is invariant under such a change of basis. The second condition requires a little more explanation.

The trace preserving operation
\rho\to\sum_{i}L_{\mathtt{a}}^{(i)\dagger}\rho L_{\mathtt{a}}^{(i)}
for a fixed party
A_{{\mathtt{a}}}
can be implemented by a unitary acting on
H_{A_{\mathtt{a}}}\otimes H_{\rm env}
and then tracing over
H_{\rm env}
. This operation is known as a local operation (
lo
) or as a quantum channel. In this context, the operators
A_{{\mathtt{a}}}^{(i)}
are known as the Kraus operators. An example of such an operation is a measurement of some observable.
A more general operation of the kind given in (
2
) comprises of local operations on multiple parties, but not just that, the overall sum over
i
further correlates these operations. This correlation models classical communication i.e. a (classical) phone call to the other party to perform correlated local operations. Together the operation (
2
) is known as local operation and classical communication (
locc
). As the system interacts with the environment during
locc
, some of the entanglement of the original state is now shared with the environment. That is why it is expected that an
\tt locc
operation must decrease quantum correlations. It may lead to new classical correlations however owing to the phone call. The first condition,
lu
invariance, can also be understood in view of the second condition. A local unitary transformation is an invertible
locc
operation and given that the measure must be non-increasing under
locc
, it must be constant under any local unitary transformation.

A corollary of monotonicity under
locc
is that the measure
{\cal M}(\rho_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{\mathtt{q}}})=0
for completely separable mixed states i.e. for the density matrices that take the form

\displaystyle\rho_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{\mathtt{q}}}=\sum_{i}\,p_{i}\,\rho_{A_{1}}^{(i)}\otimes\ldots\otimes\rho_{A_{{\mathtt{q}}}}^{(i)}.

(3)

where
p_{i}
are probabilities and
\rho_{A_{{\mathtt{a}}}}^{(i)}
are some density matrices for party
A_{\mathtt{a}}
. This is understood as follows. The completely separable states have the property that
locc
operations are reversible on them
Vidal:1998re
. So the entanglement measure must be a constant on them. We can subtract this constant from the definition of
{\cal M}
and make the measure have value
0
on the completely separable states. This is interpreted as completely separable states having only classical correlation and no quantum correlations, see section XV.B.2 in
Horodecki:2009zz
.

Pure states

We are interested in understanding the multi-partite entanglement structure of states of quantum field theory and gravity. Naturally, it’s the
pure
states that take center stage. We will only restrict our discussion of the multi-partite entanglement measure to pure states and will not attempt to extend it to mixed states.

1

There is a standard way to extend the measure defined on the pure states to mixed states via the so-called “convex roof construction”
Uhlmann1998
;
Vidal:1998re
. See section
7
.
Because the property of monotonicity under
locc
is defined inherently only for mixed states, it will not play any role in the rest of the paper. In addition to demanding that the measure
{\cal M}
for pure states be invariant under local unitary transformations, we will also require certain other appealing properties.

•

{\cal M}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(|\Psi\rangle_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{\mathtt{q}}})
is
symmetric
in all the parties i.e. it is invariant under permutations of the parties.

\displaystyle{\cal M}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(|\Psi\rangle_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{\mathtt{q}}})={\cal M}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(|\Psi\rangle_{A_{\sigma\cdot{1}},\ldots,A_{\sigma\cdot{\mathtt{q}}}}),\qquad{\rm for}\quad\sigma\in S_{{\mathtt{q}}}.

(4)

Here we have put the superscript
({\mathtt{q}})
to emphasize that the measure is defined on
{\mathtt{q}}
-partite states.

•

{\cal M}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(|\Psi\rangle\otimes|\Phi\rangle)
for direct product states is the sum of their individual measures. More concretely, for the two
{\mathtt{q}}
-partite states
|\Psi\rangle\in{\cal H}^{\Psi}_{A_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes{\cal H}^{\Psi}_{A_{\mathtt{q}}}
and
|\Phi\rangle\in{\cal H}^{\Phi}_{A_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes{\cal H}^{\Phi}_{A_{\mathtt{q}}}
consider the direct product state
|\Psi\rangle\otimes|\Phi\rangle
also as a
{\mathtt{q}}
-partite state valued in
{\cal H}_{A_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes{\cal H}_{A_{\mathtt{q}}}
where
{\cal H}_{A_{\mathtt{a}}}\equiv{\cal H}_{A_{\mathtt{a}}}^{\Psi}\otimes{\cal H}_{A_{\mathtt{a}}}^{\Phi}
. Then we require,

\displaystyle{\cal M}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(|\Psi\rangle\otimes|\Phi\rangle)={\cal M}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(|\Psi\rangle)+{\cal M}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(|\Phi\rangle).

(5)

Given that monotonicity under
locc
is not at our disposal, we need an alternative notion of loss of entanglement and monotonicity under it. Let us assume that we have a family of multi-partite measures
{\cal M}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
. In that case, we would like to propose the following condition that quantifies the loss of entanglement. If we identify two parties and treat them as a single party and compute
{\cal M}^{({\mathtt{q}}-1)}
on the resulting
{\mathtt{q}}-1
partite state then we require,

•

Monotonicity under coarse graining
:

\displaystyle{\cal M}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(|\Psi\rangle)-{\cal M}^{({\mathtt{q}}-1)}(|\Psi_{[A_{\mathtt{a}}A_{\mathtt{b}}]}\rangle)\geq 0\qquad\quad\forall\,{\mathtt{a}},{\mathtt{b}}.

(6)

We use the notation
|\Psi_{[A_{\mathtt{a}}A_{\mathtt{b}}]}\rangle
to denote the
{\mathtt{q}}-1
partite state obtained after identification of parties
A_{\mathtt{a}}
and
A_{{\mathtt{b}}}
.
Because the measure
{\cal M}
is invariant under local unitary transformations,
{\cal M}^{({\mathtt{q}}-1)}(|\Psi_{[A_{\mathtt{a}}A_{\mathtt{b}}]}\rangle)
is effectively a measure obtained after the action of an arbitrary scrambling unitary transformation on
{\cal H}_{\mathtt{a}}\otimes{\cal H}_{\mathtt{b}}
. It is reasonable to expect that such a scrambling unitary reduces the multi-partite entanglement. The condition of monotonicity under coarse graining is not novel. It has already been considered for multi-partite states in
Hein_2004
.
Because the monotonicity property (
6
) is expected for all values of
{\mathtt{q}}
, it implies a broad set of inequalities on a
{\mathtt{q}}
-partite state coming from sequentially identifying parties.
More formally, given a set of parties
\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{\mathtt{q}}\}
, consider a set of all of its partitions. There is a natural partial order on this set coming from splitting or refinement. An example of this partial order for the case of four parties is shown in figure
1
with the help of the so-called “Hasse diagram”.

Figure 1:
Hasse diagram for partial order under set refinement for the set of four elements
wiki
.

Coarse graining is the opposite of refinement where multiple partitions are combined to form a single partition. The monotonicity under coarse graining asserts that the measure
{\cal M}
respects this partial ordering. It means that the multi-partite measure is non-increasing as we follow a downward path in the Hasse diagram.
As
{\cal M}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(|\Psi\rangle)-{\cal M}^{({\mathtt{q}}-1)}(|\Psi_{[A_{\mathtt{a}}A_{\mathtt{b}}]}\rangle)
is non-negative, this difference can also be thought of as a multi-partite analogue of mutual-information between party
A_{\mathtt{a}}
and
A_{\mathtt{b}}
.

1.1
For density matrices

The monotonicity condition (
6
) for
{\mathtt{q}}
-partite pure state
|\Psi\rangle
can be easily reformulated for the density matrix
\rho
on
{\mathtt{q}}-1
parties obtained after tracing out one of the parties, say party
{\mathtt{q}}
. Note that here we are merely talking about the
reformulation
of the pure state measure for density matrices and not its
extension
to mixed states mentioned in footnote
1
. We will denote the measures computed for the density matrix defined on
{\mathtt{q}}-1
parties with a superscript
({\mathtt{q}})
because the superscript stands for the number of parties in the description of the state as a pure state and any mixed state can be purified by introducing an extra party. Even though there are multiple ways to purify a density matrix, the measure
{\cal M}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
does not depend on the purification. This is because all purifications are related to each other by the action of local unitary transformations on the new party
A_{\mathtt{q}}
introduced by the purification and the measure
{\cal M}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
is invariant under it.
For the density matrix, the monotonicity condition takes the form of two different conditions

•

Monotonicity under coarse graining
:

Denoting the
{\mathtt{q}}-2
partite density matrix obtained by identifying parties
A_{\mathtt{a}}
and
A_{\mathtt{a}}
from
\rho
as
\rho_{[A_{\mathtt{a}}A_{\mathtt{b}}]}
,

\displaystyle{\cal M}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(\rho)

\displaystyle\geq{\cal M}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}}-1)}(\rho_{[A_{\mathtt{a}}A_{\mathtt{b}}]}).

(7)

•

Monotonicity under partial tracing
:

Denoting the
{\mathtt{q}}-2
partite density matrix obtained by tracing out party
A_{\mathtt{a}}
from
\rho
as
\rho_{[A_{\mathtt{a}}]}
,

\displaystyle{\cal M}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(\rho)

\displaystyle\geq{\cal M}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}}-1)}(\rho_{[A_{\mathtt{a}}]}).

(8)

For the original
{\mathtt{q}}
-partite pure state, this corresponds to the identification of
A_{\mathtt{a}}
with
A_{\mathtt{q}}
that is traced out to produce the density matrix.

The conjecture

The following conjecture is the main subject of this paper.

•

Renyi multi-entropy
Gadde:2022cqi
;
Gadde:2023zzj

S_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
, for all positive integers
n
, is monotonic under coarse graining.

In the rest of the paper, we will give support for this conjecture by considering various types of multi-partite pure states.

Outline of the paper

In the rest of the section
1
, we define the Renyi multi-entropy after introducing a method to construct general local unitary invariants using permutations and formulate the monotonicity conjecture precisely. We then verify it for certain simple states as a sanity check. In section
2
, we compute the Renyi multi-entropy for states that are in the neighborhood of the factorized state and verify the conjecture for them. We then prove the conjecture for classical probability distributions in section
3
. From the quantum perspective, classical probability distributions are thought of as diagonal density matrices. The conjecture is then proved for a generalized version of the so-called W state on three parties in section
4
. This example is interesting because the computation of
S_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
reduces to the computation of the partition function of a dimer model on a periodic square lattice. This model is integrable and its partition function is computed using the techniques developed in
KASTELEYN19611209
. In section
5
, we consider the monotonicity conjecture for holographic states and verify it using the prescription of
Gadde:2022cqi
;
Gadde:2023zzj
. We provide the details of numerical checks of the conjecture for finite-dimensional multi-partite states chosen randomly in section
6
and end with the summary and outlook. The paper is supplemented by one appendix describing Kasteleyn’s approach to dimer models.

1.2
Renyi multi-entropy: definition

Consider a normalized quantum state
|\Psi\rangle\in\otimes_{{\mathtt{a}}=1}^{\mathtt{q}}{\cal H}_{\mathtt{a}}
of a
{\mathtt{q}}
-party system. Let
d_{\mathtt{a}}
be the dimension of the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathtt{a}}
and
|\alpha_{\mathtt{a}}\rangle,\,\alpha_{\mathtt{a}}=1,\ldots,d_{\mathtt{a}}
be a set of its orthonormal basis vectors. In
|\alpha_{\mathtt{a}}\rangle
basis, the state
|\Psi\rangle
is given as

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle=\sum_{\alpha_{1}=1}^{d_{1}}\ldots\sum_{\alpha_{\mathtt{q}}=1}^{d_{\mathtt{q}}}\,\,\psi_{\alpha_{1}\ldots\alpha_{\mathtt{q}}}\,\,|\alpha_{1}\rangle\otimes\ldots\otimes|\alpha_{\mathtt{q}}\rangle.

(9)

We call
\psi_{\alpha_{1}\ldots\alpha_{\mathtt{q}}}
, the wavefunction.
Local unitary transformations take the form
|\Psi\rangle\to\otimes_{{\mathtt{a}}=1}^{\mathtt{q}}{\cal U}_{\mathtt{a}}|\Psi\rangle
where
{\cal U}_{\mathtt{a}}
is the unitary transformation that acts on
{{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathtt{a}}}
.
Naturally, the local unitary invariants are constructed by taking multiple copies of
\psi
’s and
\bar{\psi}
’s and contracting the fundamental indices
\alpha_{\mathtt{a}}
of
\psi
with anti-fundamental indices
\alpha_{\mathtt{a}}
of
\bar{\psi}
. As a result, the number of
\psi
’s and the number of
\bar{\psi}
’s is the same in any entanglement measure. We call this number the replica number
m
. We index the replicas by the superscript
(i)
. The Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathtt{a}}
of the
i
-th replica is denoted as
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathtt{a}}^{(i)}
and its basis as
|\alpha_{\mathtt{a}}^{(i)}\rangle
. The wavefunction of the
i
-th replica is then
\psi_{\alpha_{1}^{(i)}\ldots\alpha_{\mathtt{q}}^{(i)}}
and its conjugate is
\bar{\psi}^{\alpha_{1}^{(i)}\ldots\alpha_{\mathtt{q}}^{(i)}}
. A general invariant
\cal E
can be written in terms of contractions of fundamental indices of
m
-replicas of
\psi
with anti-fundamental indices of
m
-replicas of
\bar{\psi}
as follows

\displaystyle{\cal E}

\displaystyle=\Big(\psi_{\alpha_{1}^{(1)}\ldots\alpha_{\mathtt{q}}^{(1)}}\ldots\psi_{\alpha_{1}^{(m)}\ldots\alpha_{\mathtt{q}}^{(m)}}\Big)\Big(\bar{\psi}^{\beta_{1}^{(1)}\ldots\beta_{\mathtt{q}}^{(1)}}\ldots\bar{\psi}^{\beta_{1}^{(m)}\ldots\beta_{\mathtt{q}}^{(m)}}\Big)\delta^{\vec{\alpha}_{1}}_{\sigma_{1}\cdot\vec{\beta}_{1}}\ldots\delta^{\vec{\alpha}_{\mathtt{q}}}_{\sigma_{\mathtt{q}}\cdot\vec{\beta}_{\mathtt{q}}}

\displaystyle{\rm where}\quad\delta^{\vec{\alpha}_{\mathtt{a}}}_{\sigma_{\mathtt{a}}\cdot\vec{\beta}_{\mathtt{a}}}

\displaystyle\equiv\delta^{\alpha_{\mathtt{a}}^{(1)}}_{\beta_{\mathtt{a}}^{(\sigma_{\mathtt{a}}\cdot 1)}}\ldots\delta^{\alpha_{\mathtt{a}}^{(m)}}_{\beta_{\mathtt{a}}^{(\sigma_{\mathtt{a}}\cdot m)}}

(10)

If we call the pair of
\psi
and
\bar{\psi}
as a replica then the invariant
{\cal E}
is labeled by
{\mathtt{q}}
permutation elements
\sigma_{\mathtt{a}}
of the permutation group
S_{m}
acting on the replica set. The element
\sigma_{\mathtt{a}}
indicates how the
m
fundamental indices of party
{\mathtt{a}}
are contracted with
m
anti-fundamental indices. See
Gadde:2023zzj
for a more detailed discussion of general
{\mathtt{q}}
-partite invariants.

The Renyi multi-entropy is now defined starting from an invariant
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
labeled by a specific set of permutation elements
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{\mathtt{q}}
. Consider
m=n^{{\mathtt{q}}-1}
replicas, arranged as a
{\mathtt{q}}-1
dimensional regular hyper-cubical lattice. Let us label these directions by
{1},\ldots,{\mathtt{q}}-1
and the
{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}
discrete “translations” acting along each direction as
{\mathbb{Z}}_{n,{\mathtt{a}}}
.
The permutation
\sigma_{\mathtt{q}}
is taken to be identity and the rest of the permutations are taken to be the generators of independent
{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}
symmetries i.e.
\sigma_{{\mathtt{a}}}=g_{{\mathtt{a}}}
for
{\mathtt{a}}={1},\ldots,{\mathtt{q}}-1
, where
g_{{\mathtt{a}}}
generates
{\mathbb{Z}}_{n,{\mathtt{a}}}
. Although it might seem that
{\mathtt{q}}
-th party is being treated differently from the rest of the parties, the invariant
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
is symmetric in all the parties.
The Renyi multi-entropy is now defined as

\displaystyle S_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}:=\frac{1}{1-n}\frac{1}{n^{{\mathtt{q}}-2}}{\rm log}({\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}).

(11)

It is clear that
S_{n}^{(2)}
is the ordinary Renyi-entropy
S_{n}
. It follows from the definition, that the measures
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
and
S_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
enjoy homogeneity in the state respectively i.e.,

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(|\Psi_{1}\rangle\otimes|\Psi_{2}\rangle)

\displaystyle={\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(|\Psi_{1}\rangle)\cdot{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(|\Psi_{2}\rangle)

\displaystyle S_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(|\Psi_{1}\rangle\otimes|\Psi_{2}\rangle)

\displaystyle=S_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(|\Psi_{1}\rangle)+S_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(|\Psi_{2}\rangle)

(12)

for any
{\mathtt{q}}
-partite states
|\Psi_{1}\rangle,|\Psi_{2}\rangle
.

In many cases, the monotonicity conjecture for the Renyi multi-entropy is more conveniently thought of as the inequality for
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
before taking the logarithm,

In order to understand these conjectures better, it is useful to ask when inequality

Even in the simplest non-trivial case of
{\mathtt{q}}=3
, we can not characterize the saturation in complete generality but in certain cases, as we will see in the main text of the paper, its saturation corresponds to the vanishing of mutual information between the parties being identified. We numerically find that the saturation of

and
A_{\mathtt{b}}

Density matrix and lattices

To discuss the measure
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
more concretely, it is convenient to introduce a graphical notation in which the wavefunction
\psi
is denoted as a
{\mathtt{q}}
-valent black vertex as in figure

\psi

\bar{\psi}

The vertex has outgoing colored edges as they stand for fundamental indices

of distinct Hilbert spaces
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathtt{a}}

\bar{\psi}
is denoted as a
{\mathtt{q}}

{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathtt{a}}

{\mathtt{q}}
’th party is denoted as a black-white vertex with
{\mathtt{q}}-1

{\mathtt{q}}-1

{\mathtt{q}}-1

The quantity
({\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}}-1)}(\rho_{[A_{\mathtt{a}}]}))^{n}

A_{\mathtt{a}}
. The quantity
({\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}}-1)}(\rho_{[A_{\mathtt{a}}A_{\mathtt{b}}]}))^{n}
appearing on the right hand side of inequality (

{\mathtt{a}}
direction disconnecting and going instead in the
{\mathtt{b}}
direction forming “double bonds” with the already existing bonds in the
{\mathtt{b}}
direction. As a result, again the lattice disconnects in the
{\mathtt{a}}
direction and instead becomes
n
disconnected “layers” of
{\mathtt{q}}-2
dimensional lattice in the transverse directions.

For future use, we observe the following. Let us pluck out one
\rho
vertex from the lattice corresponding to
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
. The remaining lattice yields a matrix
H_{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}^{\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}(\rho)\equiv H_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(\rho)
on
{\mathtt{q}}-1
parties such that
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}={\rm Tr}\,\rho H_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(\rho)
. Here the trace is taken over all the
{\mathtt{q}}-1
parties. We observe that the matrix
H_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(\rho)
is hermitian. This is because the hermitian conjugate operation on any graph of
\rho
’s reverses all the arrows. If the graph reversed arrows is isomorphic (identical up to relabelling of vertices) to the original one then the corresponding quantity constructed out of
\rho
’s is hermitian. This is the case for
H_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(\rho)
.

1.3
Certain simple states

Let us get some feel for the monotonicity conjecture for certain simple quantum states. It is convenient to keep the hyper-cubic lattice model of
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
in mind for these computations.

1.3.1
Factorized state

Perhaps the simplest quantum state is the one corresponding to a factorized density matrix,

\displaystyle\rho_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}=\rho_{A_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\rho_{A_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}.

(16)

In this case, the
{\mathtt{q}}-1
dimensional lattice breaks up into
n^{{\mathtt{q}}-2}
copies of linear
1
dimensional lattices in each of the
{\mathtt{q}}-1
directions. We get

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\mathtt{a}}{\rm Tr}\rho_{A_{\mathtt{a}}}^{n}.

\displaystyle S_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{{\mathtt{q}}-2}}\sum_{\mathtt{a}}\,S_{n}^{(2)}(\rho_{A_{\mathtt{a}}}).

(17)

It is clear that the Renyi multi-entropy of the
{\mathtt{q}}-2
partite state after tracing out
A_{\mathtt{b}}
,
S_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(\rho_{[A_{\mathtt{b}}]})
, is given by the same expression as above but without the
{\mathtt{a}}={\mathtt{b}}
term. As the Renyi entropy
S_{n}^{(2)}
is positive, the inequality
{\rho\mathtt{PT}}
is obeyed. The measure after identification of some two parties
A_{\mathtt{a}}
and
A_{\mathtt{b}}
,
S_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(\rho_{[A_{\mathtt{a}}A_{\mathtt{b}}]})
, on the other hand is the same as
S_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(\rho)
. So the inequality
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
is saturated.

In fact, the inequality
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
is saturated for a more general type of density matrix,

\displaystyle\rho_{A_{{1}},\ldots,A_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}=(\rho_{A^{\prime}_{{\mathtt{a}}}}\otimes{\mathbb{I}}_{A_{\mathtt{a}}})\otimes(\rho_{A^{\prime}_{{\mathtt{b}}}}\otimes{\mathbb{I}}_{A_{\mathtt{b}}})

(18)

Here the density matrices
\rho_{A^{\prime}_{\mathtt{a}}},\rho_{A^{\prime}_{\mathtt{b}}}
are
{\mathtt{q}}-2
party density matrices that do not act on
A_{\mathtt{a}}
and
A_{\mathtt{b}}
respectively. In this case, thanks to equation (
1.2
), the measure
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
factorizes.

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(\rho)

\displaystyle={\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(\rho_{A^{\prime}_{\mathtt{a}}}\otimes{\mathbb{I}}_{A_{\mathtt{a}}})\,{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(\rho_{A^{\prime}_{\mathtt{b}}}\otimes{\mathbb{I}}_{A_{\mathtt{b}}})={\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}}-1)}(\rho_{A^{\prime}_{\mathtt{a}}})\,{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}}-1)}(\rho_{A^{\prime}_{\mathtt{b}}}).

(19)

From this expression, it is clear that both terms separately saturate the inequality
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
. Interestingly, the density matrix (
18
), for
{\mathtt{q}}=3
becomes the factorized density matrix
\rho_{A_{1},A_{\mathtt{2}}}=\rho_{A_{1}}\otimes\rho_{A_{\mathtt{2}}}
. This is the density matrix for which the mutual information between party
A_{1}
and
A_{\mathtt{2}}
vanishes. As we have seen, such a density matrix also saturates the inequality
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
. However, for a higher number of parties, there may be density matrices apart from the type (
18
) that saturate the inequality
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
as well. So we can not say anything definitive about the relationship between the vanishing of mutual information and the vanishing of “
{\mathtt{q}}
-partite mutual information” i.e. saturation of the inequality
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
.

1.3.2
Bi-partite states

Let us consider a
{\mathtt{q}}
-partite state which only has pairwise bi-partite entanglement. In other words, it is given by the tensor product

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle=\bigotimes_{{\mathtt{a}}={1}}^{\mathtt{q}}\bigotimes_{{\mathtt{b}}=1;{\mathtt{b}}>{\mathtt{a}}}^{\mathtt{q}}|\Psi_{{\mathtt{a}}{\mathtt{b}}}\rangle.

(20)

In writing this state we have assumed that any given party
A_{\mathtt{a}}
consists of the tensor product of
{\mathtt{q}}-1
Hilbert spaces each of which is entangled separately with each of the remaining
{\mathtt{a}}-1
parties.

Using equation (
1.2
), we get

\displaystyle S_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(|\Psi\rangle)=\frac{1}{n^{{\mathtt{q}}-2}}\sum_{{\mathtt{a}},{\mathtt{b}}=1;{\mathtt{b}}>{\mathtt{a}}}S_{n}^{(2)}(|\Psi_{{\mathtt{a}}{\mathtt{b}}}\rangle)=\frac{1}{n^{{\mathtt{q}}-2}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\mathtt{a}}=1}^{\mathtt{q}}\Big(\sum_{{\mathtt{b}}=1}^{{\mathtt{q}}}S_{n}^{(2)}(|\Psi_{{\mathtt{a}}{\mathtt{b}}}\rangle)\Big).

(21)

The term in the bracket on the right-hand side is simply the Renyi entropy of party
A_{\mathtt{a}}
. So we have

\displaystyle S_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(|\Psi\rangle)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{n^{{\mathtt{q}}-2}}\sum_{{\mathtt{a}}=1}^{\mathtt{q}}S_{n}^{(2)}(\rho_{A_{\mathtt{a}}}).

(22)

When we compute the Renyi multi-entropy of the state obtained by identifying, say parties
A_{1}
and
A_{\mathtt{2}}
, we get the same expression as in equation (
21
) except that the term corresponding to the pair
({\mathtt{a}},{\mathtt{b}})=({1},{\mathtt{2}})
is absent. Because the Renyi entropies are positive, we have
{\psi\mathtt{CG}}
obeyed.

1.3.3
Generalized GHZ state

The Greenberger-Horne-Zeilinger (GHZ) state is the following entangled state of three qubits

\displaystyle|{\mathrm{GHZ}}\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|000\rangle+|111\rangle).

(23)

This state has played an important role in establishing the indispensable nature of quantum entanglement and is also used in quantum communication and cryptography. Inspired by the form of this state, we write a
{\mathtt{q}}
-partite generalization of the GHZ state. It is defined on
{\mathtt{q}}
parties each with Hilbert space dimension
d
as

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}|e_{{1},i}\rangle\otimes\ldots\otimes|e_{{\mathtt{q}},i}\rangle,

(24)

where
|e_{{\mathtt{a}},i}\rangle
forms an orthonormal basis of party
A_{\mathtt{a}}
. Normalizing the state sets
\sum_{i}|\lambda_{i}|^{2}=1
. To compute
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
we observe that there are only
d
configurations of bonds in the statistical model that give a non-vanishing contribution. These configurations are the ones where each bond takes the same index
i
. Summing,

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(|\lambda_{i}|^{2})^{n^{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}^{n}.

(25)

Here we have defined
0\leq a_{i}\equiv(|\lambda_{i}|^{2})^{n^{{\mathtt{q}}-2}}\leq 1
for future use.
When we identify party
A_{\mathtt{a}}
and
A_{\mathtt{b}}
as the same party, the state on the
{\mathtt{q}}-1
parties is isomorphic to the generalized GHZ state with the same coefficients
\lambda_{i}
. The lattice breaks up into
n
copies of
{\mathtt{q}}-2
dimensional lattice with the new
{\mathtt{q}}-1
partite generalized GHZ state as the Boltzmann weight. We get

\displaystyle({\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}}-1)}|_{(A_{\mathtt{a}}A_{\mathtt{b}})})^{n}=\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{d}(|\lambda_{i}|^{2})^{n^{{\mathtt{q}}-2}}\Big)^{n}=\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}\Big)^{n}.

(26)

The equations (
25
) and (
26
) have the same homogeneity in
a_{i}
. Let us rescale
a_{i}
such that
\sum_{i}a_{i}=1
. As
0\leq a_{i}\leq 1
even after rescaling, it is clear that the inequality
{\psi\mathtt{CG}}
is obeyed.

2
Neighborhood of factorized states

In this section, we will consider a
{\mathtt{q}}
-partite pure state such that the density matrix after tracing out one of the parties is almost completely factorized. In other words, if we take the traced-out party to be
A_{\mathtt{q}}
, the density matrix takes the form

\displaystyle\rho_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}=\rho_{A_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\rho_{A_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}+\delta\rho_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}.

(27)

Here we have taken the full density matrix
\rho_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}
and each factor
\rho_{\mathtt{a}}
in the leading order factorized density matrix to be trace normalized. So the trace of the perturbation
\delta\rho
is zero.

2.1
Inequality
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}

The inequality
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
is saturated for all factorized density matrices at leading order after identification of any two parties
A_{\mathtt{a}}
and
A_{\mathtt{b}}
as discussed in section
4
. It is then a non-trivial question whether the inequality
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
continues to hold at sub-leading orders. We will also investigate the saturation of
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
at sub-leading orders. For
{\mathtt{q}}=3
, we will be able to completely characterize the subspace of deformations
\delta\rho
that saturates the inequality
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
. In doing so we will show that the inequality
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
holds for state (
27
) to all orders of perturbation theory in
\delta\rho
.

2.2
Tri-partite states

For simplicity, let us first consider the case of tri-partite state
{\mathtt{q}}=3
.
The measure
{\cal E}_{n}^{(3)}
is graphically represented as the partition function on an
n\times n
lattice as described earlier near figure
3
.
At leading order, the rows and columns of the
2d
lattice decouple. The partition function is given by,

\displaystyle{\cal E}^{(3)}_{n}=\Big({\rm Tr}\rho_{A_{1}}^{n}\Big)^{n}\,\Big({\rm Tr}\rho_{A_{2}}^{n}\Big)^{n}.

(28)

Here the first factor comes from the
n
horizontal strands of the lattice and the second factor comes from the
n
vertical strands. A single strand is “necklace” of
n

\rho
’s contributing
{\rm Tr}\rho^{n}
.

The first correction is obtained by replacing
\rho_{A_{1}}\otimes\rho_{A_{\mathtt{2}}}
at one of the vertices by
\delta\rho_{A_{1},A_{\mathtt{2}}}
.

\displaystyle\delta{\cal E}^{(3)}_{n}=n^{2}\,\Big({\rm Tr}\rho_{A_{1}}^{n}\,{\rm Tr}\rho_{A_{2}}^{n}\Big)^{n-1}{\rm Tr}_{A_{2}}\Big(\rho_{A_{\mathtt{2}}}^{n-1}{\rm Tr}_{A_{1}}\Big(\rho_{A_{1}}^{n-1}\delta\rho_{A_{1},A_{\mathtt{2}}}\Big)\Big).

(29)

Here
{\rm Tr}_{A_{\mathtt{a}}}
stands for the trace over only
A_{\mathtt{a}}

n^{2}

\delta\rho
can appear. There are
n-1

and

\delta_{\rho}
. They contribute the factor
({\rm Tr}\rho_{A_{1}}^{n}\,{\rm Tr}\rho_{A_{2}}^{n})^{n-1}

\rho_{A_{1}}

\rho_{A_{\mathtt{2}}}

\rho_{A_{1}}\otimes\rho_{A_{\mathtt{2}}}

\delta\rho_{A_{1},A_{\mathtt{2}}}
.

The quantity
({\cal E}_{n}^{(2)})^{n}
which we want to compare with
{\cal E}_{n}^{(3)}
is also given by the same lattice but with vertical bonds oriented horizontally (and horizontal bonds staying where they are). The contribution of the strands that don’t involve
\delta\rho
is the same as in
{\cal E}_{n}^{(3)}
. In fact, the contribution of two strands that do have
\delta\rho
is also the same as before. The only difference is that the two strands in question are not oriented horizontally and vertically but rather both of them are oriented horizontally. So the right-hand side of equation (
29
) is precisely what we obtain if we compute the first correction to the
({\cal E}_{n}^{(2)})^{n}
also.
This shows that the inequality
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
remains saturated at first order. In fact, for the conjecture to hold,
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
must remain saturated at first order because the inequality sign could have been reversed by changing the sign of
\delta\rho
.

At second order, we have two insertions of
\delta\rho
on the lattice of
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{3}})}
. This can happen in two inequivalent ways. Either the insertions are collinear along a row or column or they are not. When the insertions are not collinear, we simply get the square of the multiplicative correction factor i.e. the last factor on the right-hand side of equation (
29
), one corresponding to each insertion. The contribution to the second-order change due to these configurations is

\displaystyle\frac{n^{2}(n-1)^{2}}{2}\,\Big({\rm Tr}\rho_{A_{1}}^{n}\,{\rm Tr}\rho_{A_{2}}^{n}\Big)^{n-2}\Big({\rm Tr}_{A_{2}}\Big(\rho_{A_{\mathtt{2}}}^{n-1}{\rm Tr}_{A_{1}}\Big(\rho_{A_{1}}^{n-1}\delta\rho_{A_{1},A_{\mathtt{2}}}\Big)\Big)\Big)^{2}.

(30)

As in the case of single insertions, the two insertions when they are not collinear, contribute the same to the change in
({\cal E}_{n}^{(2)})^{n}
. So the sign of the inequality
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
is determined by the configurations of
\delta\rho
that are collinear.

Let us insert

at the vertex

holds separately for the contribution of configuration with a given value of

\displaystyle\rho_{A_{1}}\rho_{A_{2}}+\delta\rho_{A_{1},A_{2}}=(\rho_{A_{1}}+\delta\rho_{A_{1}})\otimes(\rho_{A_{2}}+\delta\rho_{A_{2}})+{\cal O}(\delta\rho^{2}).

(36)

This means that we have to move along the tangent space of the factorized density matrix to continue to saturate the inequality. And because our starting point is an arbitrary factorized density matrix, it implies that in the neighborhood of the locus of factorized density matrices on two parties, the only density matrices that saturate the inequality

are the factorized density matrices. This leads to a natural conjecture that for tri-partite states, the inequality is saturated precisely for factorized density matrices or equivalently when the mutual information between the two parties in question vanishes.

at second order. For the

2.3.1
Broadening the scope

The above discussion suggests that we can broaden our analysis from the neighborhood of the completely factorized state to a more general state of the following form

\displaystyle\rho_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}=\rho_{A_{1}}\otimes\rho_{A_{\mathtt{2}}}\otimes\rho^{\rm rest}_{A_{\mathtt{3}},\ldots,A_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}+\delta\rho_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}.

(39)

Because the density matrix is factorized into party
A_{1}
, party

and the rest, the proof of saturation of the inequality
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
at leading and first order goes through in the same way as before. At second order, again, the nontrivial problem is to show that the insertion matrix

is hermitian. Instead of having decoupled transverse strands attached to the insertions like in the previous case, an entire
{\mathtt{q}}-3
dimensional torus is attached to it. This is the torus corresponding to the measure
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}}-2)}(\rho^{\rm rest})
with one of the
\rho^{\rm rest}
vertex replaced by
\delta\rho
. Concretely, consider the matrix
H_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}}-2)}(\rho^{\rm rest})
obtained by removing a vertex in the lattice corresponding to
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}}-2)}(\rho^{\rm rest})
. Then,

\displaystyle\widehat{\delta\rho}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{\mathtt{2}}}^{\beta_{1}\beta_{\mathtt{2}}}=\delta\rho_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{\mathtt{2}}\ldots\alpha_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}^{\beta_{1}\beta_{\mathtt{2}}\ldots\beta_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}\Big(H_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}}-3)}(\rho^{\rm rest})\Big)^{\alpha_{\mathtt{3}}\ldots\alpha_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}_{\beta_{\mathtt{3}}\ldots\beta_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}.

(40)

Because
H_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(\rho)
is a hermitian matrix for general
\rho
,
\widehat{\delta\rho}
is also hermitian. This shows that even for the more general state of the type (
39
), the inequality
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
holds. The same argument as before shows that it is saturated if and only if
\widehat{\delta\rho}
is the sum of two terms, one proportional to
\rho_{A_{1}}
and the other proportional to
\rho_{A_{\mathtt{2}}}
as in equation (
38
).
What does this condition mean for
\delta\rho
in equation (
39
)? We can’t solve it generally for
\delta\rho
. However, we see that the following is certainly a solution to the condition (
38
).

\displaystyle\delta\rho_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}=\rho_{A_{1}}\otimes\delta\rho^{(1)}_{A_{\mathtt{2}},A_{\mathtt{3}},\ldots,A_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}+\rho_{A_{\mathtt{2}}}\otimes\delta\rho^{(2)}_{A_{1},A_{\mathtt{3}},\ldots,A_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}.

(41)

Interestingly, in this space of solutions, the mutual information between parties
A_{1}
and
A_{\mathtt{2}}
vanishes at first order in perturbation theory. This is seen as follows. Let us construct
\rho_{A_{1},A_{\mathtt{2}}}
from the full
\rho
given in (
39
) with
\delta\rho
given by the special solution (
41
).

\displaystyle\rho_{A_{1},A_{\mathtt{2}}}

\displaystyle=\rho_{A_{1}}\otimes\rho_{A_{\mathtt{2}}}+\rho_{A_{1}}\otimes\delta\rho_{A_{2}}+\delta\rho_{A_{1}}\otimes\rho_{A_{2}}

(42)

\displaystyle\delta\rho_{A_{2}}

\displaystyle\equiv{\rm Tr}_{A_{\mathtt{3}},\ldots,A_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}\delta\rho^{(1)}_{A_{\mathtt{2}},A_{\mathtt{3}},\ldots,A_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}

\displaystyle\delta\rho_{A_{1}}

\displaystyle\equiv{\rm Tr}_{A_{\mathtt{3}},\ldots,A_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}\delta\rho^{(2)}_{A_{\mathtt{2}},A_{\mathtt{3}},\ldots,A_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}.

Up to corrections of
{\cal O}(\delta\rho^{2})
, the right-hand side can be written as a factorized density matrix
(\rho_{A_{1}}+\delta\rho_{A_{1}})\otimes(\rho_{A_{\mathtt{2}}}+\delta\rho_{A_{\mathtt{2}}})
showing that the mutual information between
A_{1}
and
A_{\mathtt{2}}
vanishes at first order. However, in general, the condition of saturation of inequality
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
and the vanishing of mutual information seem unrelated.

2.4
Multi-entropy for almost factorized states

In
Gadde:2022cqi
, authors defined Multi-entropy from Renyi multi-entropy as the limit

\displaystyle S^{({\mathtt{q}})}\equiv\lim_{n\to 1}\,S_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}=\partial_{n}{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}|_{n=1}.

(43)

In the second equality, we have used the fact that
{\cal E}_{1}^{({\mathtt{q}})}=1
.
The definition of multi-entropy requires analytically continuing
S_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
, which is defined for integer
n
, to complex values of
n
. The precise nature of this analytic continuation is a delicate question. We will not deal with it here. In this paper, we will perform the analytic continuation that is most straightforward. We will first expand
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(\rho)
in powers of
\delta\rho
and analytically continue each term in the perturbative expansion. Finally, we will show that the inequality
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
holds for the multi-entropy up to
{\cal O}(\delta\rho^{2})
just like for integer
n
’s.

Leading order

At the leading order, we have already computed the Renyi multi-entropy in (
1.3.1
). Using the fact that the analytic continuation of the Renyi entropy
S^{(2)}_{n}(\rho)
to
n=1
gives Von Neumann entropy
S(\rho)
, we get

\displaystyle S^{({\mathtt{q}})}(\rho)=\sum_{{\mathtt{a}}=1}^{{\mathtt{q}}-1}S(\rho_{A_{\mathtt{a}}})+{\cal O}(\delta\rho).

(44)

To compute the
n\to 1
limit at higher orders, it is useful to diagonalize the leading order factorized density matrix, let’s call it
\rho_{0}
, within each party. Let
v_{\alpha_{\mathtt{a}}}
be the eigenvectors of
\rho_{A_{\mathtt{a}}}
with eigenvalues
\lambda_{\alpha_{\mathtt{a}}}
. Then

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(\rho)|_{\rm leading}=\prod_{{\mathtt{a}}=1}^{{\mathtt{q}}-1}\Big(\sum_{\alpha_{\mathtt{a}}=1}^{d_{\mathtt{a}}}\lambda_{\alpha_{\mathtt{a}}}^{n}\Big)^{n^{{\mathtt{q}}-2}}\equiv(A_{n})^{n^{{\mathtt{q}}-2}}.

(45)

The second equality serves as a definition of
A_{n}
. This expression can be straightforwardly analytically continued in
n
. It has the property that
A_{1}=1
and
A^{\prime}_{1}=\sum_{{\mathtt{a}}}\sum_{\alpha_{\mathtt{a}}=1}^{d_{\mathtt{a}}}\lambda_{\alpha_{\mathtt{a}}}\log(\lambda_{\alpha_{\mathtt{a}}})
. This gives us (
44
) as expected.

First order

The first order correction to
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
is obtained by replacing one vertex of the lattice of
\rho_{0}
’s with
\delta\rho
. This gives

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}|_{1\,{\rm st}}={\rm Tr}\,H_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}(\rho_{0})\,\delta\rho.

(46)

This lattice consists of decoupled
1
-dimensional strands except for the strands that are attached to the
\delta\rho
vertex. We get

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}|_{1\,{\rm st}}

\displaystyle=n^{{\mathtt{q}}-1}(A_{n})^{n^{{\mathtt{q}}-2}-1}B_{n}

(47)

\displaystyle B_{n}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\alpha_{1}=1}^{d_{1}}\ldots\sum_{\alpha_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}=1}^{d_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}(\lambda_{\alpha_{1}}^{n-1}\ldots\lambda_{\alpha_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}^{n-1})\delta\rho_{\alpha_{1}\ldots\alpha_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}^{\alpha_{1}\ldots\alpha_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}.

Here
B_{n}
can also be analytically continued by promoting the exponent of
\lambda
to a complex number. We have
B_{1}=0
, due to the traceless-ness of
\delta\rho
. So
\partial_{n}{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}|_{1\,{\rm st}}
at
n=1
gets nonzero contribution when the derivative hits
B_{n}
. We get
(\partial_{n}{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}|_{1\,{\rm st}})|_{n=1}=B^{\prime}_{1}
. Hence

\displaystyle S^{({\mathtt{q}})}(\rho)|_{1\,{\rm st}}=-B_{1}^{\prime}=-\sum_{{\mathtt{a}}={1}}^{{\mathtt{q}}-1}\sum_{\alpha_{\mathtt{a}}=1}^{d_{\mathtt{a}}}\left(\log\lambda_{\alpha_{{\mathtt{a}}}}\,(\delta\rho_{A_{\mathtt{a}}})_{\alpha_{\mathtt{a}}}^{\alpha_{{\mathtt{a}}}}\right)=-\sum_{{\mathtt{a}}={1}}^{{\mathtt{q}}-1}{\rm Tr}(\delta\rho_{A_{\mathtt{a}}}\log\rho_{A_{\mathtt{a}}}).

(48)

Here
\delta\rho_{A_{\mathtt{a}}}
is obtained from
\delta\rho
by tracing over all the parties except
A_{\mathtt{a}}
. Using the fact that
-\log\rho_{A_{\mathtt{a}}}
is the modular Hamiltonian
K_{\mathtt{a}}
, interestingly the first correction to the multi-entropy is given by
\sum_{{\mathtt{a}}=1}^{{\mathtt{q}}-1}\langle K_{\mathtt{a}}\rangle_{\delta\rho_{A_{\mathtt{a}}}}
as it would be for the sum of entanglement entropies.

Second order

The computation of
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
is a little more involved. It comes from two types of configurations. One where the two insertions of
\delta\rho
are non-collinear and the other where they are collinear. The contribution of the non-collinear configurations

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}|_{2\,{\rm nd,non-coll}}=\frac{1}{2}n^{{\mathtt{q}}-1}(n^{{\mathtt{q}}-1}-(n-1)({\mathtt{q}}-1)-1)(A_{n})^{n^{{\mathtt{q}}-2}-2}B_{n}^{2}.

(49)

The first factor is a combinatorial one that imposes for the second insertion that it is not collinear to the first one. It is easy to see that the value and the derivative at
n=1
of
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}|_{2\,{\rm nd,non-coll}}
is zero. Here we use
B_{1}=0
. We get the non-zero contribution at second order from configurations where the two insertions of
\delta\rho
are collinear. Without loss of generality, let us take them to be separated in
A_{1}
directions with
m
insertions on
\rho_{A_{1}}
between them. The entire lattice is decoupled into
1
-dimensional lattices, except for the sub-lattice that is attached to the two
\delta\rho
insertions. For all parties, except for
A_{1}
, two of the
n^{{\mathtt{q}}-2}
strands are attached to
\delta\rho
and for party
A_{1}
, one of the strands is attached to
\delta\rho
’s. The contribution from the strands that are not attached to
\delta\rho
’s is then

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{\alpha_{1}=1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{\alpha_{1}}^{n}\Big)\prod_{{\mathtt{a}}=1}^{{\mathtt{q}}-1}\Big(\sum_{\alpha_{\mathtt{a}}=1}^{d_{\mathtt{a}}}\lambda_{\alpha_{\mathtt{a}}}^{n}\Big)^{n^{{\mathtt{q}}-2}-2}=\Big(\sum_{\alpha_{1}=1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{\alpha_{1}}^{n}\Big)A_{n}^{n^{{\mathtt{q}}-2}-2}\equiv J_{n}^{(1)}.

(50)

The contribution from the strands that are attached to
\delta\rho
is

\displaystyle L_{n}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\equiv(B^{(1)}_{n})_{\alpha_{1}}^{\beta_{1}}(B^{(1)}_{n})^{\alpha_{1}}_{\beta_{1}}\sum_{m=0}^{n-2}\lambda_{\alpha_{1}}^{m}\lambda_{\beta_{1}}^{n-m-2}=(B^{(1)}_{n})_{\alpha_{1}}^{\beta_{1}}(B^{(1)}_{n})^{\alpha_{1}}_{\beta_{1}}\frac{\lambda_{\alpha_{{1}}}^{n-1}-\lambda_{\beta_{{1}}}^{n-1}}{\lambda_{\alpha_{{1}}}-\lambda_{\beta_{{1}}}}.

\displaystyle(B^{(1)}_{n})_{\alpha_{1}}^{\beta_{1}}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\alpha_{\mathtt{2}}=1}^{d_{\mathtt{2}}}\ldots\sum_{\alpha_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}=1}^{d_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}(\lambda_{\alpha_{\mathtt{2}}}^{n-1}\ldots\lambda_{\alpha_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}^{n-1})\delta\rho_{\alpha_{1}\ldots\alpha_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}^{\beta_{1}\ldots\alpha_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}

(51)

In the first line, we have summed over different values of
m
. Summing over the party along which the
\delta\rho
insertions are separated (which was taken to be
A_{1}
in the above discussion), we get

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}|_{2\,{\rm nd,coll}}=\frac{n^{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}{2}\sum_{{\mathtt{a}}=1}^{{\mathtt{q}}-1}J^{({\mathtt{a}})}_{n}L^{({\mathtt{a}})}_{n}.

(52)

Here the first factor is a combinatorial factor associated with the symmetry of this configuration. The final answer (
52
) admits a straightforward analytic continuation in
n
. The derivative of
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}|_{2\,{\rm nd,coll}}
at
n=1
contributes only when the derivative hits the ratio appearing in
L_{n}^{({\mathtt{a}})}
. All the other values of
n
must be set to
1
. We get the matrix
B_{1}^{{\mathtt{a}}}=\delta\rho_{A_{\mathtt{a}}}
and also
J_{1}^{(1)}=1
. Hence,

\displaystyle S^{({\mathtt{q}})}(\rho)|_{2\,{\rm nd}}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\mathtt{a}}=1}^{{\mathtt{q}}-1}{\rm Tr}(\delta\rho_{A_{\mathtt{a}}}^{2}M_{\mathtt{a}}),\qquad(M_{A_{\mathtt{a}}})_{\alpha_{\mathtt{a}}}^{\beta_{\mathtt{a}}}\equiv\frac{\log\lambda_{\alpha_{{\mathtt{a}}}}-\log\lambda_{\beta_{{\mathtt{a}}}}}{\lambda_{\alpha_{{\mathtt{a}}}}-\lambda_{\beta_{{\mathtt{a}}}}}

(53)

The symmetric and real matrix
M_{A_{\mathtt{a}}}
is defined by specifying its components in the distinguished basis (the basis of eigenvectors of
\rho_{A_{\mathtt{a}}}
)
v_{\alpha_{\mathtt{a}}}
as shown above.
Combining the results (
44
), (
48
) and (
53
), we get

\displaystyle S^{({\mathtt{q}})}(\rho)=\sum_{{\mathtt{a}}=1}^{{\mathtt{q}}-1}\left(S(\rho_{A_{\mathtt{a}}})+\langle K_{{\mathtt{a}}}\rangle_{\delta\rho_{A_{\mathtt{a}}}}-\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}(\delta\rho_{A_{\mathtt{a}}}^{2}M_{A_{\mathtt{a}}})\right)+{\cal O}(\delta\rho^{3}).

(54)

2.5
Monotonicity for multi-entropy

In this section, we will show that the multi-entropy (
54
) obeys the inequality
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
in the neighborhood of the factorized state. At leading order, from equation (
44
), it is easy to see that the inequality is saturated. In fact, it is saturated even for the first correction as in the case of integer
n
. This is seen as follows. We use
\log(\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B})=\log(\rho_{A})\otimes{\mathbb{I}}+{\mathbb{I}}\otimes\log(\rho_{B})
. We need only focus on the terms contributed by the two parties, say
A_{1}
and
A_{\mathtt{2}}
, that are being identified. The other terms are the same on both sides of
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
. For the relevant parties we have,

\displaystyle-{\rm Tr}(\delta\rho_{A_{1},A_{\mathtt{2}}}\log\rho_{A_{1},A_{\mathtt{2}}})=-{\rm Tr}(\delta\rho_{A_{1}}\log\rho_{A_{1}})-{\rm Tr}(\delta\rho_{A_{\mathtt{2}}}\log\rho_{A_{\mathtt{2}}}).

(55)

This shows that
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
continues to be saturated at first order.

Before we look at the multi-entropy at second order.
Let us define a symmetric matrix
(N_{A})_{\alpha}^{\beta}=\sqrt{(M_{A})_{\alpha}^{\beta}}
. Just like
M
we are defining the matrix
N
by specifying its entries in a chosen basis. Because,
(M_{A})_{\alpha}^{\beta}
are positive, the entries of
N
are real. Hence
N
is hermitian. Let us also note a useful property of matrix
M
.

\displaystyle(M_{A_{1},A_{\mathtt{2}}})_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{\mathtt{2}}}^{\beta_{1}\beta_{\mathtt{2}}}=\frac{\log(\lambda_{\alpha_{{1}}}\lambda_{\alpha_{{\mathtt{2}}}})-\log(\lambda_{\beta_{{1}}}\lambda_{\beta_{{\mathtt{2}}}})}{\lambda_{\alpha_{{1}}}\lambda_{\alpha_{{\mathtt{2}}}}-\lambda_{\beta_{{1}}}\lambda_{\beta_{{\mathtt{2}}}}}.

(56)

So,

\displaystyle(M_{A_{1},A_{\mathtt{2}}})_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{\mathtt{2}}}^{\alpha_{1}\beta_{\mathtt{2}}}=\lambda_{\alpha_{1}}^{-1}M_{A_{\mathtt{2}}},\qquad(M_{A_{1},A_{\mathtt{2}}})_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{\mathtt{2}}}^{\beta_{1}\alpha_{\mathtt{2}}}=\lambda_{\alpha_{\mathtt{2}}}^{-1}M_{A_{1}}.

(57)

Motivated by our proof of the inequality
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
for integer
n
,
Consider the two matrices,

\displaystyle(M_{l})_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\alpha^{\prime}_{1}\alpha^{\prime}_{2}}^{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\beta^{\prime}_{1}\beta^{\prime}_{2}}

\displaystyle=(\delta\rho_{A_{1},A_{\mathtt{2}}})_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{\mathtt{2}}}^{\beta_{1}\beta_{\mathtt{2}}}(N_{A_{1},A_{\mathtt{2}}})_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{\mathtt{2}}}^{\beta_{1}\beta_{\mathtt{2}}}\,\Big((\rho_{A_{1}}\otimes\rho_{A_{\mathtt{2}}})^{1/2}\Big)_{\alpha^{\prime}_{1}\alpha^{\prime}_{2}}^{\beta^{\prime}_{1}\beta^{\prime}_{2}}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\alpha^{\prime}_{1}}\lambda_{\alpha^{\prime}_{2}}(\delta\rho_{A_{1},A_{\mathtt{2}}})_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{\mathtt{2}}}^{\beta_{1}\beta_{\mathtt{2}}}(N_{A_{1},A_{\mathtt{2}}})_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{\mathtt{2}}}^{\beta_{1}\beta_{\mathtt{2}}}\delta_{\alpha^{\prime}_{1}}^{\beta^{\prime}_{1}}\delta_{\alpha^{\prime}_{2}}^{\beta^{\prime}_{2}}.

(58)

This is a hermitian matrix. In the second equality, we have used the fact that
\alpha_{\mathtt{a}}
and
\beta_{\mathtt{a}}
are eigenvectors
\rho_{A_{\mathtt{a}}}
and hence diagonalize it. To match with the notation from before, let us also define
M_{r}\equiv M_{l}
. We are now ready to prove the inequality at second order. As before, consider

\displaystyle\langle M_{l}^{\dagger}|(1-P_{1})(1-P_{2})|M_{r}\rangle=\langle M_{r}|(1-P_{1})(1-P_{2})|M_{r}\rangle.

(59)

Here we have used
M_{l}=M_{r}
and
M_{r}^{\dagger}=M_{r}
. The operators
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
act in the same way on indices
(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime})
as defined near equation (
33
). Because the eigenvalues of
P_{i}
are
\pm 1
, the quantity (
59
) is positive. Now we simply note that
\langle M_{l}^{\dagger}|1+P_{1}P_{2}|M_{r}\rangle
gives
-2S^{({\mathtt{q}})}(\rho_{A_{1},A_{\mathtt{2}}})
and
\langle M_{l}^{\dagger}|P_{1}+P_{2}|M_{r}\rangle
gives
-2S^{({\mathtt{q}})}(\rho_{A_{1}})-2S^{({\mathtt{q}})}(\rho_{A_{\mathtt{2}}})
. In showing the latter, we need to use the property (
57
).

As before, for the inequality
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
to be saturated we need
\delta\rho_{A_{1},A_{\mathtt{2}}}
to be sum of two terms, one proportional to
\rho_{A_{1}}
and the other proportional to
\rho_{A_{\mathtt{2}}}
i.e. we need,

\displaystyle\delta\rho_{A_{1},A_{\mathtt{2}}}=\rho_{A_{1}}\delta\rho_{A_{\mathtt{2}}}+\rho_{A_{\mathtt{2}}}\delta\rho_{A_{1}}.

(60)

Recall,
\delta\rho_{A_{\mathtt{a}}}
is obtained from the full
\delta\rho_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}
by tracing out all the parties except
A_{\mathtt{a}}
. We can’t solve this condition in full generality for
\delta\rho_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}
but we can observe that

\displaystyle\delta\rho_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}=\rho_{A_{1}}\otimes\delta\rho^{(1)}_{A_{\mathtt{2}},A_{\mathtt{3}},\ldots,A_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}+\rho_{A_{\mathtt{2}}}\otimes\delta\rho^{(2)}_{A_{1},A_{\mathtt{3}},\ldots,A_{{\mathtt{q}}-1}}.

(61)

is certainly a solution. As argued near equation (
41
), this solution leads to a density matrix that has vanishing mutual information to first order in
\delta\rho
. But more generally, the saturation of the inequality
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
for multi-entropy and the vanishing of mutual information seem unrelated.

2.6
Inequality
{\rho\mathtt{PT}}

As discussed in section
4
, the inequality
{\rho\mathtt{PT}}
after partially tracing over say party
A_{{1}}
of the factorized density matrix is strictly obeyed except when the
n
-th Renyi entropy of
\rho_{A_{1}}
vanishes. In this case, the inequality is
{\rho\mathtt{PT}}
is saturated. So precisely in this case, we can investigate
{\rho\mathtt{PT}}
at the next order.

At leading order the
{\mathtt{q}}-1
dimensional lattice computing
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}}-1)}
breaks up into
n^{{\mathtt{q}}-2}
number of one-dimensional lattices, one set for each party. This gives

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}=\Big({\rm Tr}\rho_{A_{1}}^{n}\Big)^{n^{{\mathtt{q}}-2}}\ldots\Big({\rm Tr}\rho_{A_{\mathtt{q}}}^{n}\Big)^{n^{{\mathtt{q}}-2}}.

(62)

The first correction is obtained by replacing
\rho_{A_{{1}}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\rho_{A_{\mathtt{q}}}
at of the vertices of the lattice by the perturbation
\delta\rho_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{\mathtt{q}}}
. The correction is given by

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}=n^{{\mathtt{q}}-1}\Big({\rm Tr}\rho_{A_{1}}^{n}\Big)^{n^{{\mathtt{q}}-2}-1}\ldots\Big({\rm Tr}\rho_{A_{\mathtt{q}}}^{n}\Big)^{n^{{\mathtt{q}}-2}-1}{\rm Tr}_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{\mathtt{q}}}\Big((\rho_{A_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\rho_{A_{\mathtt{q}}})^{n-1}\cdot\delta\rho_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{\mathtt{q}}}\Big).

Here the first factor
n^{{\mathtt{q}}-1}
is a combinatorial factor corresponding to the number of possible lattice points where the replacement can occur. The next factor is the contribution of
n^{{\mathtt{q}}-2}-1
number of one-dimensional lattices whose contribution remains unaffected. The last factor is the most interesting one. It corresponds to the
{\mathtt{q}}-1
strands of the lattice that are connected to
\delta\rho
vertex. We will rewrite the last term as

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{\mathtt{q}}}\Big((\rho_{A_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\rho_{A_{\mathtt{q}}})^{n-1}\cdot\delta\rho_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{\mathtt{q}}}\Big)={\rm Tr}_{A_{1}}\,\,\rho_{A_{1}}^{n-1}\cdot\widetilde{\delta\rho}_{A_{1}},

(63)

\displaystyle{\rm where}\quad

\displaystyle\widetilde{\delta\rho}_{A_{1}}\equiv{\rm Tr}_{A_{\mathtt{2}},\ldots,A_{\mathtt{q}}}\Big((\rho_{A_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\rho_{A_{\mathtt{q}}})^{n-1}\cdot\delta\rho_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{\mathtt{q}}}\Big).

Let us label the normalized eigenvectors of the combined density matrix
\rho_{A_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\rho_{A_{\mathtt{q}}}
by
|v_{\beta}\rangle
and the corresponding eigenvalues by
\lambda_{\beta}
. Then the explicit form of
\widetilde{\delta\rho}_{A_{1}}
can be written as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\delta\rho}_{A_{1}}=\sum_{\beta}\lambda_{\beta}^{n-1}\langle v_{\beta}|\delta\rho_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{\mathtt{q}}}|v_{\beta}\rangle.

(64)

Now let us compute
({\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}}-1)})^{n}
for the density matrix obtained tracing over party
A_{1}
. The only difference is that instead of the last factor
{\rm Tr}_{A_{1}}\,\rho_{A_{1}}^{n-1}\cdot\widetilde{\delta\rho}_{A_{1}}
, we get the product of traces
({\rm Tr}\,\rho_{A_{1}})^{n-1}({\rm Tr}\,\widetilde{\delta\rho}_{A_{1}})={\rm Tr}\,\widetilde{\delta\rho}_{A_{1}}
here we have replaced
{\rm Tr}\rho_{A_{1}}=1
. So comparison of
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
and
({\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}}-1)})^{n}
amounts to the comparison between

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}_{A_{1}}\,\,\rho_{A_{1}}^{n-1}\cdot\widetilde{\delta\rho}_{A_{1}}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad{\rm Tr}\,\widetilde{\delta\rho}_{A_{1}}.

(65)

Recall that we want to compare the quantities at the first order only for the case when
n
-th Renyi entropy of
\rho_{A_{1}}
vanishes. This condition implies that the rank of
\rho_{A_{1}}
is
1
. Let us choose an arbitrary eigenbasis of
\rho_{A_{1}}
as
|u_{\alpha}\rangle
such that the eigenvalue for
\alpha=1
is
1
and for
\alpha\neq 1
it is
0
.
This means that
(\rho_{A_{1}})_{11}=1
and all other components are
0
. For this case,
{\rm Tr}_{A_{1}}\,\,\rho_{A_{1}}^{n-1}\cdot\widetilde{\delta\rho}_{A_{1}}=(\widetilde{\delta\rho}_{A_{1}})_{11}
.
We will show
{\rm Tr}\,\widetilde{\delta\rho}_{A_{1}}\geq(\widetilde{\delta\rho}_{A_{1}})_{11}
as follows. First notice that

\displaystyle\langle u_{\alpha}|\langle v_{\beta}|\rho_{A_{1}}\otimes\rho_{A_{\mathtt{2}}}\ldots\otimes\rho_{A_{\mathtt{q}}}+\delta\rho|u_{\alpha}\rangle|v_{\beta}\rangle\geq 0,\qquad{\rm for}\quad\alpha\neq 1,

(66)

thanks to the positivity of the perturbed density matrix. Now, the expectation value of the leading order factorized density matrix
\rho_{A_{1}}\otimes\rho_{A_{\mathtt{2}}}\ldots\otimes\rho_{A_{\mathtt{q}}}
vanishes for
\alpha\neq 1
thanks to the property
(\rho_{A_{1}})_{\alpha\alpha}=0
for
\alpha\neq 1
. This provides us the inequality
\langle u_{\alpha}|\langle v_{\beta}|\delta\rho|u_{\alpha}\rangle|v_{\beta}\rangle\geq 0
for
\alpha\neq 1
. Summing this with positive weight
\lambda_{\beta}^{n-1}
, we get

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\delta\rho}_{A_{1}})_{\alpha\alpha}\geq 0\qquad{\rm for}\quad\alpha\neq 1.

(67)

Because
{\rm Tr}\,\widetilde{\delta\rho}_{A_{1}}-(\widetilde{\delta\rho}_{A_{1}})_{11}
simply consists of the sum above diagonal entries, we prove the inequality that we set out to prove.

We now ask, what is the space of perturbations that continues to saturate the inequality
{\rho\mathtt{PT}}
also at first order? This would happen if and only if
(\widetilde{\delta\rho}_{A_{1}})_{\alpha\alpha}=0
for all
\alpha\neq 1
. We can not solve this condition for
\delta\rho
in general but it is easy to check that
\delta\rho
of the following form obeys this condition.

\displaystyle\delta\rho=\delta\rho_{A_{1}}\otimes\delta\rho_{A_{\mathtt{2}},\ldots,A_{\mathtt{q}}},

(68)

such that the only element of
\delta\rho_{A_{1}}
that is non-zero is
(\delta\rho_{A_{1}})_{11}
.

Unlike the case of inequality
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
, the inequality
{\rho\mathtt{PT}}
did not vanish at first order in
\delta\rho
. In the case of
{\rho\mathtt{PT}}
, we could not have changed the sign of
\delta\rho
to flip the sign of the inequality because, the leading order,
\rho_{A_{1}}
that we started with had rank
1
and lived on the boundary of parameter space. Changing the sign of
\delta\rho
would take us outside the domain of valid density matrices.

3
Probabilities

The probability distribution for
{\mathtt{q}}-1
events can be thought of as a completely diagonal density matrix on
{\mathtt{q}}-1
parties. Equivalently, the probability distributions can be thought of as classical states. In this section, we will study the inequalities
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
and
{\rho\mathtt{PT}}
for such states.

Before we consider such classical density matrices let us consider a more general
\rho_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{\mathtt{q}}}
with the following property that the “transfer matrix”
T
in the direction
{\mathtt{a}}
constructed out
\rho

\rho

{\mathtt{a}}
direction. This lattice has

fundamental and anti-fundamental indices of party

. In other words, it is a matrix acting on
{\cal H}_{A_{\mathtt{a}}}^{\otimes n^{{\mathtt{q}}-2}}
. If

is hermitian and positive definite then it can be thought of as a density matrix itself (not normalized to
1
). The inequality
{\rho\mathtt{PT}}
is then understood as the inequality between the ordinary Renyi-entropy and the
n
-th power of the norm. The normalization of
T
does not matter because both sides of
{\rho\mathtt{PT}}
have the same homogeneity in
T
viz.
n
so we might as well normalize
T
. Then
{\rho\mathtt{PT}}
is the statement that the Renyi entropy is less than
1
.
Now, because the classical density matrix is completely diagonal with positive entries, the transfer matrix

constructed from it is also diagonal with positive entries. In particular,
T
is hermitian and positive definite. This proves the inequality
{\rho\mathtt{PT}}
for classical density matrices.

Now we move our attention to proving
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
. This turns out to be more involved. Motivated by the above argument about the transfer matrices. We can again reduce the
{\mathtt{q}}-1
partite problem only to the two parties
A_{\mathtt{a}}
and
A_{\mathtt{b}}
in question. This is done by constructing a
{\mathtt{q}}-3
dimensional lattice that is orthogonal to directions
{\mathtt{a}}
and
{\mathtt{b}}
, the resulting object is a matrix acting on

. Thanks to the positive and diagonal nature of the classical density matrix, this new “codimension-2” transfer matrix can also be thought of as a classical density matrix on two parties
{\cal H}_{A_{\mathtt{a}}}^{\otimes n^{{\mathtt{q}}-3}}
and
{\cal H}_{A_{\mathtt{a}}}^{\otimes n^{{\mathtt{q}}-3}}
. This two-party classical density matrix is not properly normalized but we can normalize it to one because the inequalities that we are interested in checking do not depend on the norm of the state.
In this way, the proof of inequality

for

-party classical density matrix now reduces to that for
2
-party classical density matrix. Before moving to the general case, let us consider the case of two correlated coins for concreteness. As mentioned earlier, this is equivalent to a diagonal density matrix over two parties where each party is
2

3.1
Heads or tails

In this subsection, we will consider a classical density matrix i.e. a completely diagonal density matrix over two parties,
{1}
and
{\mathtt{2}}
. As emphasized earlier, this is simply a two-variable probability distribution. Let us take the density matrix to be

\displaystyle\rho_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{\mathtt{2}}}^{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}}=p_{\alpha_{1}\alpha{\mathtt{2}}}\,\delta_{\alpha_{1}}^{\beta_{1}}\,\delta_{\alpha_{\mathtt{2}}}^{\beta_{\mathtt{2}}}.

(69)

We can think of
p_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{\mathtt{2}}}
as the probability of outcomes
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{\mathtt{2}})
in an experiment. Let us specialize to the case with the two parties being coins i.e. they both have only two states
0
and
1
each. So
\alpha_{1}
as well as
\alpha_{2}
take only two values
0
and
1
. The measure
({\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{2}})})^{n}
is simply
n
-th power of the Renyi entropy for the combined event i.e.

\displaystyle({\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{2}})})^{n}=(p^{n}_{00}+p^{n}_{01}+p^{n}_{10}+p^{n}_{11})^{n}.

(70)

The calculation of
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{3}})}
is a little more involved. Consider the two-dimensional lattice of
\rho
’s as shown in figure
3
. Because the density matrix is diagonal, we will fix
m
rows (corresponding to the first coin) and
m^{\prime}
columns (corresponding to the second coin) to have the state
0
. The states in the remaining rows and columns are taken to be
1
. The contribution of this configuration to

is
p_{00}^{mm^{\prime}}p_{01}^{m(n-m^{\prime})}p_{10}^{(n-m)m^{\prime}}p_{11}^{(n-m)(n-m^{\prime})}
.
There are
\,{}^{n}C_{m}\,^{n}C_{m^{\prime}}
such configurations. Summing over all of them, we get

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{3}})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\sum_{m^{\prime}=0}^{n}\,{}^{n}C_{m}\,^{n}C_{m^{\prime}}(p_{00}^{mm^{\prime}}p_{01}^{m(n-m^{\prime})}p_{10}^{(n-m)m^{\prime}}p_{11}^{(n-m)(n-m^{\prime})})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\,{}^{n}C_{m}(p^{m}_{00}p^{n-m}_{10}+p_{01}^{m}p_{11}^{(n-m)})^{n}.

(71)

In the second line, we have summed over
m^{\prime}
to obtain a more compact expression. On the other hand, the advantage of the form that is given in the first line is that it is manifestly symmetric in both parties. We would like to show that the quantity in equation (
3.1
) is smaller than the one in equation (
70
) for arbitrary positive numbers
p_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}=1
but because both quantities have the same homogeneity
n^{2}
in
p_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}
’s this normalization does not matter.

The equation (
3.1
) is massaged as follows.

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{n}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,k=0}^{n}{}^{n}C_{k}{}^{n}C_{m}p_{10}^{nk}a^{mk}p_{11}^{n(n-k)}b^{m(n-k)}=\sum_{m,k=0}^{n}{}^{n}C_{k}p_{10}^{nk}p_{11}^{n(n-k)}b^{mn}\times{}^{n}C_{m}c^{mk}

(72)

where we have defined,
a=\frac{p_{00}}{p_{10}},b=\frac{p_{01}}{p_{11}}\ \&\ c=\frac{a}{b}
. Similarly, equation (
70
) is massaged as,

\displaystyle({\cal E}_{n}^{(2)})^{n}=\left(p_{10}^{n}(1+a^{n})+p_{11}^{n}(1+b^{n})\right)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{}^{n}C_{k}p_{10}^{nk}p_{11}^{n(n-k)}\sum_{r=0}^{k}\sum_{s=0}^{n-k}{}^{k}C_{r}{}^{n-k}C_{s}a^{nr}b^{ns}

(73)

Changing the variables from
s
to

as

\displaystyle({\cal E}_{n}^{(2)})^{n}=\sum_{k,m=0}^{n}{}^{n}C_{k}p_{10}^{nk}p_{11}^{n(n-k)}b^{mn}\times\sum_{r=0}^{k}{}^{k}C_{r}{}^{n-k}C_{m-r}c^{nr}.

(74)

The range of summation of

is from

to

but we have extended it from

to

because summand contains

which vanishes in the added part of the summation range.
Hence, proving the inequality
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
reduces to proving

\sum_{k,m=0}^{n}{}^{n}C_{k}p_{10}^{nk}p_{11}^{n(n-k)}b^{mn}\Big(\sum_{r=0}^{k}{}^{k}C_{r}{}^{n-k}C_{m-r}c^{nr}-{}^{n}C_{m}c^{mk}\Big)\geq 0.

(75)

As it turns out, the inequality holds for each term in the

sum.
We will now prove the inequality,

\sum_{r=0}^{k}{}^{k}C_{r}{}^{n-k}C_{m-r}c^{nr}\geq{}^{n}C_{m}c^{mk}.

(76)

for all values of
m,k
and

.
As a sanity check, let us notice that the above inequality is saturated for

. This can be seen by considering the equality
(1+x)^{k}(1+x)^{n-k}=(1+x)^{n}
and matching coefficient of
x^{m}
on both sides.
This is as it should be because

corresponds to a factorized density matrix.

We will prove the inequality (

) using the fact that arithmetic mean
\geq
geometric mean (
am-gm
). Consider the left-hand side as the sum of monomials of gamma with coefficient
1
. The

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\,{}^{n}C_{m}}\sum_{r=0}^{k}{}^{k}C_{r}{}^{n-k}C_{m-r}c^{nr}\geq\Big(c^{n\sum_{r=0}^{k}r\,\,{}^{k}C_{r}{}^{n-k}C_{m-r}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{\,{}^{n}C_{m}}}.

(77)

We now need to show that the right-hand side of the above inequality is
c^{mk}
i.e. we need to show

\displaystyle n\sum_{r=0}^{k}r\,\,{}^{k}C_{r}{}^{n-k}C_{m-r}=\,^{n}C_{m}\,m\,k.

(78)

We have shown that the inequality (

) is saturated at

because it corresponds to a factorized density matrix. In section

, we have also shown that inequality

continues to be saturated at first order in perturbation about the factorized density matrix. Then it must be that the inequality (

) should be saturated if we differentiate both sides at

. This gives the equation (
78
) needed to prove the inequality (
76
) for general states.

Alternatively, we can prove the equation (

) directly by following combinatorial manipulations:

\displaystyle n\sum_{r=0}^{k}\,r\,\,{}^{k}C_{r}{}^{n-k}C_{m-r}

\displaystyle=n\,k\,\sum_{r=1}^{k}{}^{k-1}C_{r-1}{}^{(n-1)-(k-1)}C_{(m-1)-(r-1)}

\displaystyle=n\,k\,\sum_{s=0}^{k-1}{}^{k-1}C_{s}{}^{(n-1)-(k-1)}C_{(m-1)-s},

(79)

where

. Now, note that as explained below the equation (
76
), we also have the identity,

\sum_{r=0}^{k}{}^{k}C_{r}{}^{n-k}C_{m-r}={}^{n}C_{m}.

(80)

This finally simplifies the equation (
3.1
) to give,

\displaystyle n\sum_{r=0}^{k}\,r\,\ {}^{k}C_{r}{}^{n-k}C_{m-r}

\displaystyle=n\,k\,\,{}^{n-1}C_{m-1}={}^{n}C_{m}\,m\,k.

(81)

3.2
Generalization to an arbitrary number of states

We have reduced the proof of inequality
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
for the classical density matrix on any number of parties to that for only two parties but with arbitrary dimensions. In the previous section, we proved
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
for the case of two parties, each with dimension
2
. In this section, we will generalize this proof to two parties with arbitrary dimensions
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
.

Let us consider a sector of configurations on the
n\times n
lattice with states
s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}
and
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
on the rows and columns respectively.
Note that these are the
values
the indices
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
take respectively. They are not indices themselves.
We will prove the inequality
{\rho\mathtt{CG}}
by proving it in a given
(s_{i},t_{i})
sector.
To begin with let us assume that
d_{1},d_{2}\geq n
and take
s_{i}
’s as well as
t_{i}
’s to be distinct. Contribution of this sector of configurations to
{\cal E}_{n}^{(3)}
is,

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{n}^{(3)}|_{\rm sector}=n!n!\prod_{i,j}p_{s_{i},t_{j}}.

(82)

Here, the two factors of
n!
up front come from the number of ways of arranging the distinct
s_{i}
or
t_{i}
on the
n\times n
lattice.

Now, we consider
({\cal E}_{n}^{(2)})^{n}
, which has the same number of vertices as
{\cal E}_{n}^{(3)}
. To compute
({\cal E}_{n}^{(2)})^{n}
, we need to orient party
2
edges along party
1
edges. Let the strand of party
2
that is along the strand
s_{i}
of party
1
be
t_{\sigma\cdot i}
for some permutation
\sigma
. We need to compute the contribution for each
\sigma
and then sum over
\sigma
.

\displaystyle({\cal E}_{n}^{(2)})^{n}|_{\rm sector}=\sum_{\sigma}n!\prod_{i}p^{n}_{s_{i},t_{\sigma\cdot i}}.

(83)

The inequality is now proved using
am-gm
. Consider
({\cal E}_{n}^{(2)})^{n}
as sum of
n!
terms, each corresponding to a permutation.

\displaystyle({\cal E}_{n}^{(2)})^{n}|_{\rm sector}\geq n!\prod_{\sigma}(n!\prod_{i}p^{n}_{s_{i},t_{\sigma\cdot i}})^{1/n!}=n!n!\prod_{i,j}\Big((p^{n}_{s_{i},t_{j}})^{(n-1)!}\Big)^{1/n!}={\cal E}_{n}^{(3)}|_{\rm sector}.

(84)

In the first equality, we have realized that the number of permutations that yield a given term with
\sigma\cdot i=j
is
(n-1)!
.

Let us now allow for repetitions among the index values
s_{i}
’s and
t_{i}
’s.
It is useful to have a simple example in mind while doing the counting. Let’s say, as values of
s_{i}
we have
m_{s}
heads and
n-m_{s}
tails and as values of
t_{i}
we have
m_{t}
heads and
n-m_{t}
tails.
Let
n_{s}
and
n_{t}
be the number of elements in the orbit of
s_{i}
’s and
t_{i}
’s under permutations. For the example at hand,

\displaystyle n_{s}=\,^{n}C_{m_{s}},\qquad n_{t}=\,^{n}C_{m_{t}}.

(85)

With this definition, we have

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{n}^{(3)}|_{\rm sector}

\displaystyle=n_{s}n_{t}\prod_{i,j}p_{s_{i},t_{j}},\qquad({\cal E}_{n}^{(2)})^{n}|_{\rm sector}=\sum_{\sigma\in{\rm nontriv}}n_{s}\prod_{i}p^{n}_{s_{i},t_{\sigma\cdot i}}.

(86)

Here
\sigma\in{\rm nontriv}
stands for non-trivial permutations of
t_{i}
.
These are permutations that result in different assignments of heads and tails to the indices
t_{i}
. These are precisely
n_{t}
in number.
Using the
am-gm
inequality as before,

\displaystyle({\cal E}_{n}^{(2)})^{n}|_{\rm sector}\geq n_{t}\prod_{\sigma\in{\rm nontriv}}(n_{s}\prod_{i}p^{n}_{s_{i},t_{\sigma\cdot i}})^{1/n_{t}}.

(87)

We have obtained this inequality starting from the expression of
({\cal E}_{n}^{(2)})^{n}|_{\rm sector}=n_{t}\cdot{\mathtt{am}}
. As there are
n_{t}
terms in the product over
\sigma
and
n_{s}
is independent of
\sigma
, it comes out of the product and the
n_{t}
-th root. We get

\displaystyle({\cal E}_{n}^{(2)})^{n}|_{\rm sector}\geq n_{t}n_{s}\prod_{i}\prod_{\sigma\in{\rm nontriv}}\Big(p^{n}_{s_{i},t_{\sigma\cdot i}}\Big)^{1/n_{t}}.

(88)

In order to prove the desired inequality, we will show

\displaystyle\prod_{j}p_{s_{i},t_{j}}=\prod_{\sigma\in{\rm nontriv}}\Big(p^{n}_{s_{i},t_{\sigma\cdot i}}\Big)^{1/n_{t}}.

(89)

Let us evaluate the left-hand side for our example. In that case,
m_{t}
number of
t_{j}
’s take the values of heads and
n-m_{t}
number of
t_{j}
’s take the values tails. So

\displaystyle\prod_{j}p_{s_{i},t_{j}}=(p_{s_{i},{\rm heads}})^{m_{t}}(p_{s_{i},{\rm tails}})^{n-m_{t}}.

(90)

On the right-hand side, different factors of
p_{s_{i},t_{j}}
are produced as we vary
\sigma
because there will be
\sigma
’s such that
j=\sigma\cdot i
. The number of such
\sigma
’s is the size of the stabilizer of a given
j
. In our example, we fix one of the
t_{j}
to be, say heads. Then the number of permutations among
n_{t}
number of permutations that preserve this
t_{j}
are

\,{}^{n-1}C_{m_{t}-1}=\,^{n}C_{m_{t}}\,\frac{m_{t}}{n}=n_{t}\frac{m_{t}}{n}.

(91)

This set of permutations yields only those values of
j
for which
t_{j}
is fixed to be heads. Using equation (
91
) and the right hand side of (
90
), their contribution is
(p_{s_{i},{\rm heads}})^{m_{t}}
. A similar calculation for the tails gives those values of
j
for which
t_{j}
is fixed to be tails. Their contribution is
(p_{s_{i},{\rm tails}})^{n-m_{t}}
. Multiplying these contributions, we precisely get the contribution of the equation (
90
).

We can generalize this argument to any number of outcomes. The reason behind the presence of binomial coefficients in the expressions above is that we are restricting ourselves to the case where
s_{i}
and
t_{i}
(
i=1,2,...,n
) take either of the two values (heads or tails). When we generalize to the case where
s_{i}
and
t_{i}
can take more than two values, the binomial coefficients will get replaced by appropriate multinomial coefficients. For a set of
l
distinct outcomes with
k
-th outcome appearing
m_{k}
number of times such that
\sum_{k=1}^{l}m_{k}=n
, we get

\displaystyle n_{t}=\frac{n!}{m_{1}!\ldots m_{l}!}.

(92)

The counting argument remains unaffected.

3.3
What is Renyi multi-entropy?

We end the section on classical states by describing a somewhat amusing interpretation of
{\cal E}_{n}^{(3)}
for probability distributions for two correlated coins.

Let us first consider the meaning of ordinary
{\cal E}_{n}^{(2)}
for the probability distribution for a single coin. Let the probability for heads and tails be
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
respectively. The quantity

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{n}^{(2)}=p_{0}^{n}+p_{1}^{n}

(93)

is the probability of observing the same outcome in
n
coin tosses.

To understand the meaning of
{\cal E}_{n}^{(3)}
let us consider two sets of
n
people each
\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}\}
and
\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\}
. Let the group of people
\{r_{i}\}
have a coin
R
and the group of people
\{b_{i}\}
have the coin
B
. The measure
({\cal E}_{n}^{(2)})^{n}
can be thought of as follows. Each person
r_{i}

picks a partner
, say
b_{i}
from the other group. They pair up and toss their coins
n
times. This happens for each
i
. All in all, there is
n^{2}
number of experiments.
The probability of each person observing the same result for their own coin is precisely
({\cal E}_{n}^{(2)})^{n}
.

Now consider a slightly different experiment. Each person
r_{i}
now pairs up with
every person

b_{j}
in the other group, one after the other, to perform the tossing of the
R,B
coins. Again there is
n^{2}
number of experiments. The probability of each person observing the same result for their own coin is now
({\cal E}_{n}^{(3)})
. This viewpoint can be generalized to two-party classical density matrices over higher dimensional spaces i.e. to the case of “multi-sided dice” straightforwardly. However, it seems difficult to generalize this interpretation for
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
with
{\mathtt{q}}\geq 4
.

Generalized W state

In this section, we will compute Renyi multi-entropy for a special type of three-qubit state that is a generalization of the so-called W-state and check
{\psi\mathtt{CG}}
. We call the state with the following form to be the generalized W-state:

\displaystyle|W\rangle=c_{1}|100\rangle+c_{2}|010\rangle+c_{3}|001\rangle.

(94)

When all the
c
’s are taken to be the same, the state is known as the W-state. We will think of Renyi multi-entropy as the partition function of the statistical mechanical system as outlined in section
1.2.1
. However, instead of tracing out one party and thinking of the resulting density matrix as a 4-valent vertex in a square lattice, it helps to think in terms of the 3-valent vertex of the state itself. When we “resolve” the 4-valent density matrix into the state and its complex conjugate, we get a pair of 3-valent vertices, one black and one white. This resolution is shown in figure
8
. When the vertices of the square lattice are resolved in this way, we get a hexagonal lattice with periodic boundary conditions as shown in figure
9
. A nice property of this lattice is that, unlike the square lattice, it is manifestly symmetric in all the parties.

Figure 8:
Resolution of the 4-valent density matrix.

Figure 9:
Hexagonal lattice obtained after resolution for
n=3
.

To compute the Renyi multi-entropy as the partition function on this lattice, the Boltzmann weight at each vertex is taken to be the state itself. We now have to sum over all configurations of the edges. An edge can either take the value
|0\rangle
and
|1\rangle
. If we interpret
|1\rangle
as occupied and
|0\rangle
as vacant, the problem precisely maps to the fully packed dimer model on the hexagonal lattice. The edges of the lattice are distinguished into three types depending on their spatial orientation. The Boltzmann weight at each vertex dictates we weigh the empty edge by
1
and the occupied edge by
z_{\mathtt{a}}\equiv|c_{\mathtt{a}}|^{2}
depending on the orientation. The fully packed dimer model on any planar lattice was solved by Kasteleyn around 60 years ago
KASTELEYN19611209
;
10.1063/1.1703953
. He also figured out how to deal with non-planarity arising from imposing periodic boundary conditions. We review his formalism briefly in appendix
A
. Here we directly start from the result.

The quantity
{\cal E}_{n}^{(3)}
for W-state is given as,

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{n}^{(3)}(|W\rangle)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left({Z}_{+-}+{Z}_{-+}+(-1)^{n}\left({Z}_{--}-{Z}_{++}\right)\right)

(95)

\displaystyle\textrm{where, }\quad{Z}_{s_{1}s_{2}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}((z_{1}+\omega^{i+\frac{1-s_{1}}{4}}z_{2})^{n}-(-\omega^{\frac{1-s_{2}}{4}})^{n}z_{3}^{n}),\quad\quad\omega=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{n}},s_{1,2}=\pm.

Several comments are in order regarding the equation (
95
). First, although it is not manifest,
{\cal E}_{n}^{(3)}
is symmetric under permutations of
z_{1},z_{2}
and
z_{3}
. Let us write
Z_{++}
as sum of monomials,

\displaystyle Z_{++}=\sum_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}c_{r_{1}r_{2}r_{3}}z_{1}^{r_{1}}z_{2}^{r_{2}}z_{3}^{r_{3}}.

(96)

The coefficient
c_{r_{1}r_{2}r_{3}}
is real but does not have a definite sign. In fact, the particular combination of
Z_{s_{1}s_{2}}
that appears in
{\cal E}_{n}^{(3)}
is constructed precisely so that the expansion of
{\cal E}_{n}^{(3)}
in the monomials is the same as (
96
) but with
|c_{r_{1}r_{2}r_{3}}|
replacing
c_{r_{1}r_{2}r_{3}}
. See section
A
for an explanation.

To prove the inequality
{\psi\mathtt{CG}}
we also want to compute
{\cal E}_{n}^{(2)}
after identifying two parties. Without loss of generality, we take those two parties to be qubit
1
and
2
. We get,

{\cal E}_{n}^{(2)}(|W\rangle)|_{[12]}=z_{3}^{n}+(z_{1}+z_{2})^{n}.

(97)

We would now like to show
{\cal E}_{n}^{(3)}(|W\rangle)\leq({\cal E}_{n}^{(2)}(|W\rangle))^{n}
for all values of
z_{\mathtt{a}}\geq 0
. For a normalized state, we have
z_{1}+z_{2}+z_{3}=1
but we can ignore the normalization in proving the inequality because both sides have the same overall homogeneity in
z_{\mathtt{a}}
. Let us compare a single term in the product in

with
{\cal E}_{n}^{(2)}
. It is clear that

\displaystyle|((z_{1}+\omega^{i}z_{2})^{n}+(-)^{n}z_{3}^{n})|_{z_{1}^{r_{1}}z_{2}^{r_{2}}z_{3}^{r_{3}}}|\leq(z_{3}^{n}+(z_{1}+z_{2})^{n})|_{z_{1}^{r_{1}}z_{2}^{r_{2}}z_{3}^{r_{3}}}.

(98)

Here the subscript
|_{z_{1}^{r_{1}}z_{2}^{r_{2}}z_{3}^{r_{3}}}
denotes the coefficient of the monomial
z_{1}^{r_{1}}z_{2}^{r_{2}}z_{3}^{r_{3}}
. The above inequality (

) is true because of the potential destructive interference on the left-hand side. On the right-hand side, all the terms appear with the positive sign so there is no interference. Now taking the product over
i
on both sides (the right-hand side is independent of

) gives us our desired inequality.

ebk01:3460000000134968
;

.

Although the results of

7
Summary and outlook

In this paper, we have studied the Renyi multi-entropy
S_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
for a diverse class of states. These multi-partite measures were introduced in
Gadde:2022cqi
;
Gadde:2023zzj
motivated by their potential application to holography. In this paper, however, we focused on their quantum information-theoretic properties. We conjectured that the
S_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
is monotonic under coarse graining and proposed that this property makes it a good measure of multi-partite entanglement. We provided evidence for this conjecture by explicitly verifying the monotonicity for various classes of states. An obvious line of investigation now is to either prove the monotonicity conjecture or to find a counter-example. We plan to pursue this line in the future.

The definition (
11
) of
S_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
requires that the quantity
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
be positive definite. In this paper, we did not prove or even discuss this and phrased the monotonicity conjecture in terms of
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
instead. Although we found that
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
is positive in the examples we studied, it would be nice to prove this in general and to see if there is any relation between the positivity of
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
and the coarse-graining monotonicity.

In addition to being monotonic under coarse graining, a good measure of multi-partite entanglement must also be monotonic under
locc
. We did not investigate this aspect of the measure at all in this paper. In this context, it is worth mentioning the following. As stated in section
1
, a measure that is monotonic under
locc
must be constant on separable mixed states. The pure state measure discussed in this paper is a smooth function of the state. Such a function can not be constant on a certain continuous family of states namely separable states and nontrivial on others. That is why the definition of an
locc
monotonic entanglement measure for mixed states always involves some sort of optimization.
In
Uhlmann1998
, Uhlmann developed a general way of extending pure state entanglement measures to mixed states via the so-called “convex roof construction”. This construction was used before in
Bennett:1996gf
to extend the von Neumann entropy for bi-partite pure states to the “entanglement of formation” for mixed states. In
Vidal:1998re
, Vidal showed that if a measure on the bi-partite pure state is concave in the single party density matrix obtained by tracing out one of the parties then its convex roof extension to mixed states is an
locc
monotone. It is conceivable that a version of Vidal’s proof holds also for convex roof extensions of multi-partite entanglement measures. If so, the question of whether our measure is concave in the density matrix becomes important. We hope to investigate this question in the future.

On another note, our proof of coarse-graining monotonicity for holographic states used the Schlafli formula but not in its full generality. The Schlafli formula allows for the cone parameters at other segments to be fixed to any value
\in[1,2]
, not just to
2
, as we take the cone parameter of one of the segments to
1
. A trivalent graph of singularities with possibly different values of cone parameters at every segment is precisely what we get when we consider a larger family of entanglement measures called the “special symmetric measures”, introduced in
Gadde:2023zzj
. In section
1.2
, we have labeled general local unitary invariants of a
{\mathtt{q}}
-partite state by a
{\mathtt{q}}
-tuple of permutation elements
\sigma_{\mathtt{a}}
. The special symmetric measures correspond to choosing
\sigma_{\mathtt{a}}
to be the generators of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{m_{\mathtt{a}}}
with replicas taken to be in a
m_{1}\times\ldots m_{\mathtt{q}}
hyper-cubical lattice with
{\mathbb{Z}}_{m_{\mathtt{a}}}
acting as a lattice translation in direction
{\mathtt{a}}
.
The Renyi multi-entropy studied here is a member of this family. When
m_{\mathtt{a}}
are all taken to be equal to
n
, it reduces to (the
n
-th power of)
{\cal E}_{n}^{({\mathtt{q}})}
. See
Gadde:2023zzj
for details. The monotonicity property following from the general form of the Schlafli formula suggests a tantalizing possibility that the entire family of special symmetric measures is monotonic under coarse-graining. It would be nice to check this conjecture, or better yet, to prove it. It would also be good to analyze its concavity property and convex roof extension. If these measures turn out to have monotonicity under coarse-graining as well as under
locc
then it is possible that they form a
complete
set of good multi-partite entanglement measures capable of distinguishing multi-partite states up to local unitary transformations.

It is also instructive to study the saturation of the coarse-graining inequalities. As pointed out towards the end of section
5
, this is particularly important for holographic states as they saturate a number of coarse graining inequalities. This saturation corresponds to the vanishing of a multi-partite version of mutual information. It would be nice to understand the precise sense in which this is true. The vanishing of ordinary mutual information plays an important role in diagnosing correctable errors via the so-called “decoupling principle”
PhysRevA.54.2629
. This diagnosis of quantum error correction has played an important role in understanding bulk reconstruction
Dong:2016eik
;
Almheiri:2014lwa
;
Harlow:2016vwg
. If we can understand how the saturation of the coarse-graining inequalities is related to quantum error correction for multiple parties, it would give a refinement of the local AdS/CFT correspondence as discussed in the last section of
Gadde:2023zzj
.

Appendix A
Kasteleyn’s formalism

Here we briefly review techniques used by Kasteleyn to compute the partition function of the fully packed dimer model on a graph. Let us first define some terminology. Let
L
be a lattice with an even number
2m
of vertices and
R
be the set of its edges. Let us assume that
R
is divided into several classes
C_{1},\ldots,c_{h}
. As an example, we can consider a square lattice in two dimensions, then the set of vertical bonds and horizontal bonds form two different classes. In the case of regular hexagonal or honeycomb lattice, the bonds are naturally divided into three classes by their angle with respect to, say the positive horizontal axis. We can take the bonds to be aligned at angles
0
,
2\pi/3
and
4\pi/3
with respect to this axis. For the generalized
W
state discussed in section
4
, this is the relevant lattice model. A dimer can be placed on any of the bonds but with the constraint that two dimers can not overlap at a vertex. A fully packed configuration of dimers is one where every single vertex is occupied by a dimer. Kasteleyn developed a theory of computing the partition function of the fully packed dimer model. Let the number of fully packed dimer configurations where
N_{i}
bonds of the type
C_{i}
occupied by dimers be
g_{L}(N_{1},\ldots,N_{h})
. Then the partition function of the fully packed dimer model on
L
is

\displaystyle Z_{\rm dimer}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{h})=\sum_{\rm config}g_{L}(N_{1},\ldots,N_{h})z_{1}^{N_{1}}\ldots z_{h}^{N_{h}}

(104)

where the sum is taken over all fully packed dimer configurations. The measure
{\cal E}_{n}^{(3)}
for the generalized W state considered in section
4
, is precisely the partition function
Z(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})
on a hexagonal lattice with periodic boundary conditions where
z_{a}
is the fugacity for each of the three types of bonds. Its relation to the parameters
c_{a}
of the state is
z_{a}=|c_{a}|^{2}
.

Let us assign an orientation on the lattice
L
by putting arrows on all the edges. Corresponding to this orientation we define an
antisymmetric
adjacency matrix
M_{ij}
such that
M_{ij}=z_{a}
if the edge between vertices
i
and

is of class
C_{a}
and is oriented
from

i

to

j
. Because of anti-symmetry,
M_{ji}=-M_{ij}
and

. The Pfaffian of any anti-symmetric matrix

\displaystyle{\rm Pf}(M)

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits^{{}^{\prime}}_{\sigma\in S_{2m}}{\rm sgn}_{\sigma}\,\,M_{\sigma\cdot 1,\sigma\cdot 2}\ldots M_{\sigma\cdot(2m-1),\sigma\cdot(2m)}

(105)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{m}m!}\sum\nolimits_{\sigma\in S_{2m}}{\rm sgn}_{\sigma}\,\,M_{\sigma\cdot 1,\sigma\cdot 2}\ldots M_{\sigma\cdot(2m-1),\sigma\cdot(2m)}.

for any

and
\sigma\cdot 1<\sigma\cdot 3<\sigma\cdot 5\ldots
. The sum in the second line is unconstrained. The two lines are equal because

is an antisymmetric matrix. The Pfaffian has the property that

. It is not difficult to see that when

comes with the same sign? Kasteleyn solved this problem in

;
10.1063/1.1703953

is zero. By genus of the graph, we mean the minimum genus of the Riemann surface on which the graph can be drawn without crossing. He also showed that if the genus is

In the case of the generalized W state, the graph in question is a hexagonal lattice with periodic boundary conditions. The periodic identification implies that the genus of the graph is
1
and so we need to sum over four terms. For our purposes, the precise form of the sum is not needed. We will simply compute the Pfaffian with a natural orientation and use the form of the partition function given by replacing the monomial coefficients with their absolute values. This would be enough for us to prove the inequality

. Our hexagonal lattice has an extra bi-partite structure because the vertices of the lattice either come from state
