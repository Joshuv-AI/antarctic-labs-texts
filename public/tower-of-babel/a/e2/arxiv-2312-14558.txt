Super Weil-Petersson measures on the moduli space of curves
Paul Norbury

Abstract
. The super Weil-Petersson metric defined over the moduli space of smooth super curves produces a natural measure over the moduli space of smooth curves. The construction of the measure uses the extra data of a spin structure on each smooth curve. When we allow marked points, the construction produces a collection of measures indexed by the behaviour of the spin structure at marked points—Neveu-Schwarz or Ramond. In this paper we define these measures, and prove that they are finite. Each total measure gives the super volume of the moduli space of super curves with marked points. The Neveu-Schwarz volumes are polynomials that satisfy a recursion relation discovered by Stanford and Witten, analogous to Mirzakhani’s recursion relations between Weil-Petersson volumes of moduli spaces of hyperbolic surfaces. We prove here that the Ramond boundary behaviour produces deformations of the Neveu-Schwarz volume polynomials, satisfying a variant of the Stanford-Witten recursion relations.

Introduction

The moduli space
\cal M_{g,n}
of genus
g

n
marked points comes equipped with a natural symplectic form

known as the Weil-Petersson form. It is the imaginary part of the natural Hermitian metric on the (co)tangent bundle over
\cal M_{g,n}
due to Weil
[
20

defined by using the complete hyperbolic metric in a conformal class following Petersson
[

(1)

\langle\eta,\xi\rangle:=\int_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{\overline{\eta}\xi}{h},\qquad\eta,\xi\in H^{0}(C,\omega_{\mathcal{C}}^{\otimes 2}(D))

where
D=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
and

produces a finite measure on

with total measure the Weil-Petersson volume, conveniently defined by

V^{WP}_{g,n}:=\int_{\cal M_{g,n}}\exp\omega^{WP}.

Over the moduli space

of genus
g

spin

n

\begin{array}[]{c}E_{g,n}\\
\downarrow\\
\cal M^{\text{spin}}_{g,n}\end{array}

and the Weil-Petersson form

defined over

by pullback under the map that forgets the spin structure. The bundle

can be identified with the normal bundle of

3/2

(2)

\langle\eta,\xi\rangle:=\int_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{\overline{\eta}\xi}{\sqrt{h}},\qquad\eta,\xi\in H^{0}(C,\omega_{C}^{\otimes 3/2}(D)).

The measure on
\cal M^{\text{spin}}_{g,n}

The natural Hermitian metric (

A super generalisation of the hyperbolic metric in super coordinates

After integrating out the fermionic directions

\cal M_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}=\bigsqcup_{\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\cal M_{g,\sigma}^{\text{spin}}

\sigma=(\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}

\widehat{V}^{WP}_{g,\sigma}:=\epsilon_{g,\sigma}\int_{\cal M_{g,\sigma}^{\text{spin}}}e(E_{g,\sigma}^{\vee})\exp\omega^{WP}

]

Given a vector bundle

\text{Sym}^{*}V^{\vee}\cong k[x_{1},...,x_{n}]
, for

. The moduli space of genus

n

can be defined smoothly by the sheaf of smooth sections of
\Lambda^{*}E_{g,n}^{\vee}
, where

is defined in Definition

, with reduced space the moduli space
\cal M_{g,n}^{\rm spin}
of spin curves with
n
marked points. Although the results in this paper will not rely on super constructions, the super geometry viewpoint produces useful heuristic ideas. Most significantly in the Neveu-Schwarz case it led to the work of Stanford and Witten
[
19
]
which produced recursive formulae for the super volumes analogous to those of Mirzakhani.

A powerful method pioneered by Mirzakhani
[
13
]
to study Weil-Petersson volumes is to consider a family of symplectic deformations
\omega(L_{1},...,L_{n})
of the Weil-Petersson form for
(L_{1},...,L_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
, which produces corresponding
volumes

V_{g,n}(L_{1},...,L_{n}):=\int_{\cal M_{g,n}}\exp\omega(L_{1},...,L_{n})
.
Mirzakhani proved that
V_{g,n}(L_{1},...,L_{n})
is polynomial in
L_{i}
and satisfies a recursion that uniquely determines all polynomials from the initial polynomials
V_{0,3}(L_{1},L_{2},L_{3})=1
and
V_{1,1}(L_{1})=\frac{1}{48}(L^{2}+4\pi^{2})
.
We use the same deformation of the Weil-Petersson form to study the measure defined in (
3
).

It will be useful to deform the symplectic form
\omega^{WP}
only at Neveu-Schwarz points as follows:

(4)

\widehat{V}_{g,n}^{(m)}(L_{1},...,L_{n}):=\epsilon_{g,\sigma}\int_{\cal M_{g,\sigma}^{\rm spin}}\hskip-17.07164pte(E^{\vee}_{g,\sigma})\exp\omega(L_{1},...,L_{n},0^{m}),\quad\sigma=(1^{n},0^{m}).

A consequence of Theorem
1
below, in the
(g,n)=(0,1)
case, and for general
(g,n)
using an algebro-geometric construction of the volume proven in Corollary
3.5
, is the property that
\widehat{V}_{g,n}^{(m)}(L_{1},...,L_{n})
is a polynomial in
(L_{1},...,L_{n})
of degree
2g-2+m
.

For a given genus
g
, allow
n
Neveu-Schwarz marked points and any number of Ramond points. Deform
\omega^{WP}
only at the Neveu-Schwarz points and collect these volumes into the following generating function over all possible numbers of Ramond points, which we refer to as a gas of Ramond points.

(5)

\widehat{V}_{g,n}^{WP}\hskip-1.13809pt(s,L_{1},...,L_{n}):=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{s^{m}}{m!}\widehat{V}_{g,n}^{(m)}(L_{1},...,L_{n})

The Ramond points are essentially unordered due to the factor
1/m!
, and the only non-zero terms occur when
m
is even due to a spin parity condition.
We will denote the special case of
(g,n)=(0,1)
by the
disk
function which gives the genus zero volumes with exactly one Neveu-Schwarz marked point and a gas of Ramond points:

\widehat{V}_{0,1}^{WP}\hskip-1.13809pt(s,L):=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{s^{m}}{m!}\widehat{V}_{0,1}^{(m)}(L).

Define the kernel

D(x,y,z):=\frac{1}{4\pi}\left(\frac{1}{\cosh\frac{x-y-z}{4}}-\frac{1}{\cosh\frac{x+y+z}{4}}\right)

which arises in
[
15
,
19
]
via algebraic geometry, respectively supergeometry.

Theorem 1
.

The disk function

is uniquely determined by the recursion

\widehat{V}_{0,1}^{WP}\hskip-1.13809pt(s,L)=\frac{s^{2}}{2!}+\frac{1}{2L}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\hskip-5.69054ptxyD(L,x,y)\widehat{V}_{0,1}^{WP}\hskip-1.13809pt(s,x)\widehat{V}_{0,1}^{WP}\hskip-1.13809pt(s,y)dxdy.

Theorem
1
defines a recursion in increasing powers of

. We obtain

.

The disk recursion generalises to allow any

For any

Theorem 2
.

The following recursion uniquely determines

.

(6)

\displaystyle L_{1}

\displaystyle\widehat{V}_{g,n}^{WP}\hskip-1.13809pt(s,\vec{L})=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\hskip-5.69054pt\int_{0}^{\infty}\hskip-5.69054ptxyD(L_{1},x,y)P_{g,n+1}(x,y,L_{K})dxdy

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=2}^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}\hskip-5.69054ptxR(L_{1},L_{j},x)\widehat{V}_{g,n-1}^{WP}\hskip-1.13809pt(s,x,L_{K\backslash\{j\}})dx+\delta_{1n}(\tfrac{s^{2}\delta_{0g}}{2}+\tfrac{\delta_{1g}}{8})L_{1}+O(s^{4})

Theorem
2
leads to the recursive calculation of
\widehat{V}_{g,n}^{(2)}(L_{1},...,L_{n})
.
The special cases of
(g,n)=(0,1)
and

The

via algebro-geometric techniques and in

via heuristic supergeometry techniques. What is striking is that the same kernels appear in (

Theorems
1
and
2
are stated in terms of differential geometric structures. Their proofs, given in Sections

The algebro-geometric calculations are aided by algebraic properties of partial differential Virasoro operators that naturally act on partition functions storing the intersection numbers—see Section

Despite the success of the algebro-geometric techniques, differential geometry, via heuristic ideas from supergeometry, actually predicts the interesting structure of

[
13

where she used differential geometry to deduce calculations of intersection numbers in algebraic geometry. We discuss this further in Section

which describes an approach to a proof using super differential geometry.

2.
Moduli space of spin curves

A twisted, pointed curve
(\mathcal{C},p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
with group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
is a compact orbifold such that each marked point
p_{i}
has isotropy group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and all other points have trivial isotropy group. It has a canonical sheaf
\omega_{\mathcal{C}}
which defines an orbifold line bundle over
\mathcal{C}
. The moduli space of twisted, pointed curves with group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
is denoted by
\cal M^{(2)}_{g,n}
. A twisted curve comes equipped with a map which forgets the orbifold structure
\rho:\mathcal{C}\to C
where
C
is a pointed curve known as the coarse curve of
\mathcal{C}
, often also denoted by
C=|\mathcal{C}|
. We abuse notation and write
D=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})\subset C
to denote the image in the coarse curve of the points
D\subset\mathcal{C}
with non-trivial isotropy. A spin curve is a twisted curve together with a square root of its log-canonical sheaf. Its moduli space is defined in
[
5
]
as follows:

Definition 2.1
.

The moduli space of spin curves is defined by

\cal M_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}=\{(\mathcal{C},\theta,p_{1},...,p_{n},\phi)\mid\phi:\theta^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}\omega_{\mathcal{C}}^{\text{log}}\}

where
\mathcal{C}
is a twisted curve and
\theta
is a line bundle over
\mathcal{C}
.

There is a natural map

(7)

p:\cal M_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}\to\cal M_{g,n}

obtained by forgetting the spin structure and mapping to the underlying coarse curve via
\rho
, i.e.
p
is a composition of the map induced by
\rho
together with the
2^{2g}
to 1 map to the moduli space of twisted curves
\cal M_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}\to\cal M^{(2)}_{g,n}
. There are
2^{2g+n-1}
choices of
(\theta,\phi)
for each twisted, pointed curve
(\mathcal{C},p_{1},...,p_{n})\in\cal M^{(2)}_{g,n}
, and after fixing representation data at each
p_{i}
, described below, there are
2^{2g}
different spin structures.

A spin structure on a twisted, pointed curve
(\mathcal{C},p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
defines a line bundle
\theta\to\mathcal{C}
that is a square root of the log-canonical bundle
\theta^{2}\cong\omega_{\mathcal{C}}^{\text{log}}
. Both
\theta
and
\omega_{\mathcal{C}}^{\text{log}}
are line bundles, also known as orbifold line bundles, defined over
\mathcal{C}
. An orbifold line bundle has a well-defined degree which may be a half-integer since the points with non-trivial isotropy can contribute
1/2
to the degree. In particular,

\deg\omega_{\mathcal{C}}^{\text{log}}=2g-2+n,\qquad\deg\theta=g-1+\frac{1}{2}n.

The degree of
\theta^{\vee}
is negative,
\deg\theta^{\vee}=1-g-\frac{1}{2}n<0
, hence
\theta^{\vee}
possesses no holomorphic sections and
h^{0}(\mathcal{C},\theta^{\vee})=0
. The index
h^{0}(\mathcal{C},\theta^{\vee})-h^{1}(\mathcal{C},\theta^{\vee})
is constant over any family, thus
H^{1}(\mathcal{C},\theta^{\vee})
has constant dimension and defines a vector bundle
E_{g,n}
. More precisely, denote by
\mathcal{E}
the universal spin structure defined over the universal curve
\mathcal{U}_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi}}{{\longrightarrow}}\cal M_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}
.

Definition 2.2
.

Define the bundle
E_{g,n}:=-R\pi_{*}\mathcal{E}^{\vee}\hskip-2.84526pt\to\cal M_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}
with fibre
H^{1}(\mathcal{C},\theta^{\vee})
.

The behaviour of a spin structure at a marked point
p
is one of two types, known as Neveu-Schwartz and Ramond which we now define.
The orbifold line bundles
\omega_{\mathcal{C}}^{\text{log}}
and
\theta
over
\mathcal{C}
are locally equivariant bundles over the local charts. On each fibre over a point
p
with non-trivial isotropy the equivariant trivialisation defines a representation acting by multiplication by
(-1)^{\sigma}
for
\sigma\in\{0,1\}
. The representation associated to
\omega_{\mathcal{C}}^{\text{log}}
at
p_{i}
is trivial since
\omega_{\mathcal{C}}^{\text{log}}
is generated locally by
dz/z
and the local
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
action
z\mapsto-z
induces

\frac{dz}{z}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle z\mapsto-z}}{{\longrightarrow}}\frac{dz}{z}.

Note that the representation associated to
\omega_{\mathcal{C}}
at
p_{i}
is non-trivial, since
dz\mapsto-dz
, so in particular
\omega_{\mathcal{C}}
cannot have a square root, which justifies considering square roots of the log canonical bundle.
The representations associated to
\theta
at each
p_{i}
define a vector
\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{n}
.A marked point
p_{i}
is known as a
Neveu-Schwarz
point when the associated representation is non-trivial, and a
Ramond
point otherwise. The representations at marked points define a decomposition of the moduli space into connected components

(8)

\cal M_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}=\bigsqcup_{\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\cal M_{g,\sigma}^{\text{spin}}.

The parameters
\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{n}
will also label the natural measures to be constructed on
\cal M_{g,n}
as pushforwards of a measure on
\cal M_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}
restricted to each component. The restriction of the forgetful map which forgets the spin structure and the orbifold structure to a component is denoted by
p^{\sigma}:\cal M_{g,\sigma}^{\text{spin}}\to\cal M_{g,n}
.

For any sheaf
{\cal F}
over
\mathcal{C}
, define the pushforward sheaf
|{\cal F}|:=\rho_{*}{\cal F}
over the coarse curve
C=|\mathcal{C}|
. The sheaf of local sections
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{C}}(L)
of any line bundle
L
on
\mathcal{C}
pushes forward to a sheaf
|L|:=\rho_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{C}}(L)
on
C
which can be identified with the local sections of
L
invariant under the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
action. The pullback of the pushforward is the bundle

\rho^{*}(|\theta|)=\theta\otimes\bigotimes_{i\in I}\mathcal{O}(-\sigma_{i}p_{i})
,
since locally invariant sections must vanish when the representation is non-trivial. In particular,
\deg|\theta|=\deg\theta-\tfrac{1}{2}|\sigma|
where
\deg\theta=g-1+\frac{1}{2}n
is independent of the representations
\sigma
. Let
U\subset\mathcal{C}
be an open set with local coordinate
z
that contains a single marked point
p_{i}
satisfying
z(p_{i})=0
, and suppose that
p_{i}
is Neveu-Schwarz. Then
\rho^{*}(|\theta|)\otimes\rho^{*}(|\theta|)

lies in the image of the natural map
\omega_{\mathcal{C}}(U)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cdot z}}{{\longrightarrow}}\omega_{\mathcal{C}}(p_{i})(U)
since local sections of the pushforward sheaf and its square vanish. In particular, this shows that one can forget a Neveu-Schwarz point
p_{n+1}
, for
\sigma^{\prime}=(\sigma,1)\in\{0,1\}^{n+1}
:

(9)

\pi:\cal M_{g,\sigma^{\prime}}^{\text{spin}}\to\cal M_{g,\sigma}^{\text{spin}}

where
\pi
forgets the orbifold structure at
p_{n+1}
and, as explained above, the square of the spin structure takes its values in the canonical sheaf around
p_{n+1}
in place of the log-canonical sheaf. The map
\pi
and the map
p
defined in (
7
) are both forgetful maps, forgetting different structures on the curve. We also see that

|\theta|^{2}\cong\omega_{C}(R)

where
R\subset D
consists of the Ramond points, which agrees with the convention in
[
3
]
.

The rank of the bundle
E_{g,n}
depends on the component
\cal M_{g,\sigma}^{\rm spin}
of the moduli space. It can be calculated using the Riemann-Roch theorem applied to the pushforward of
\theta
to the underlying coarse curve
C
, since
H^{i}(\mathcal{C},\theta^{\vee})=H^{i}(C,|\theta^{\vee}|)
. Apply Riemann-Roch to the coarse curve
C
to get:

h^{0}(C,|\theta^{\vee}|)-h^{1}(C,|\theta^{\vee}|)=1-g+\deg|\theta^{\vee}|=2-2g-\tfrac{1}{2}(n+|\sigma|).

and
h^{0}(C,|\theta^{\vee}|)=h^{0}(\mathcal{C},\theta^{\vee})=0
, thus the rank of
E_{g,n}
restricted to
\cal M_{g,\sigma}^{\rm spin}
is

(10)

\text{rank\ }E_{g,n}|_{\cal M_{g,\sigma}^{\rm spin}}=2g-2+\tfrac{1}{2}(n+|\sigma|).

A consequence of (
10
) is that the number of Ramond points on any curve is even.

2.1.
Euler form over the moduli space

We construct a canonical Euler form
e(E_{g,n}^{\vee})
associated to the bundle
E_{g,n}\to\cal M_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}
using the Chern connection of a natural Hermitian metric. The canonical Hermitian metric on
E_{g,n}^{\vee}
is defined similarly to the Hermitian metric on the cotangent bundle of
\cal M_{g,n}
, which produces the Weil-Petersson metric on
\cal M_{g,n}
. Both constructions use the natural hyperbolic metric associated to each curve of the moduli space. For a twisted, pointed curve
(\mathcal{C},D)
, where
D=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
, via Serre duality

H^{1}(\mathcal{C},\theta^{\vee})\cong H^{0}(\mathcal{C},\omega_{\mathcal{C}}\otimes\theta)^{\vee}.

Elements of
H^{0}(\mathcal{C},\omega_{\mathcal{C}}\otimes\theta)
give the analogue of meromorphic quadratic differentials used to define the Weil-Petersson metric.

Definition 2.3
.

Define a Hermitian metric on the vector space
H^{0}(\mathcal{C},\omega_{\mathcal{C}}\otimes\theta)
by

(11)

\langle\eta,\xi\rangle:=\int_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{\overline{\eta}\xi}{\sqrt{h}},\qquad\eta,\xi\in H^{0}(\mathcal{C},\omega_{\mathcal{C}}\otimes\theta)

where
h
is the complete hyperbolic metric on
\mathcal{C}-D
in its conformal class.

Lemma 2.4
.

The Hermitian metric (
11
) is well-defined.

Proof.

We need to show that the integrand in (
11
) defines an integrable two-form.
Locally, the transition functions, equivalently the sheaf restriction maps, of
\overline{\eta}\xi
are given by
|z^{\prime}(w)|^{3}
where
z
and
w
are local coordinates on
\mathcal{C}
. The transition functions for
\sqrt{h}
are
|z^{\prime}(w)|
so the transition functions for
\overline{\eta}\xi/\sqrt{h}

are
|z^{\prime}(w)|^{2}
proving that the quotient is a 2-form which can be integrated over
\mathcal{C}
.

To prove that (
11
) is convergent, consider the local situation. Near any marked point
p_{i}
, with respect to a local coordinate
z
satisfying
z(p_{i})=0
, the hyperbolic metric is given by

h=\frac{|dz|^{2}}{|z|^{2}(\log|z|)^{2}}=\frac{|dx|^{2}}{|x|^{2}(\log|x|)^{2}},\quad x=z^{2}

which uses the local formula for a cuspidal hyperbolic metric on the coarse curve with local coordinate
x
.
Since
\omega_{\mathcal{C}}
consists of holomorphic differentials and
\theta^{2}\cong\omega^{\text{log}}_{\mathcal{C}}
then locally

\overline{\eta}\xi=\frac{F(z)}{|z|}|dz|^{3},\quad\eta,\xi\in H^{0}(\mathcal{C},\omega_{\mathcal{C}}\otimes\theta)

for
F(z)
a smooth function, where the square of a local section of
\theta
contributes a pole of order 1 in
z
so that the Hermitian product of two sections produces the pole of order 1 in
|z|
, and local sections of
\omega_{\mathcal{C}}
are smooth at
z=0
. Hence

\int_{|z|<\epsilon}\frac{\overline{\eta}\xi}{\sqrt{h}}=\int_{|z|<\epsilon}F(z)|\log|z|||dz|^{2}\sim M\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\epsilon}r\log(r)drd\theta<\infty

due to the bounded integrand. We conclude that (
11
) is well-defined.
∎

The pushforward of elements of
H^{0}(\mathcal{C},\omega_{\mathcal{C}}\otimes\theta)

from
\mathcal{C}
to the coarse curve
C=|\mathcal{C}|
has a closer comparison to the meromorphic quadratic differentials with simple poles used to define the Weil-Petersson metric. There is a map
|\omega_{\mathcal{C}}\otimes\theta|\to\omega_{C}^{3/2}(D)
, hence the pushforward sections take values in
H^{0}(C,\omega_{C}^{3/2}(D))
which resembles
H^{0}(\mathcal{C},\omega_{\mathcal{C}}^{\otimes 2}(D))
. For
\eta,\xi\in H^{0}(C,\omega_{C}^{3/2}(D))
, near a cusp the
3/2
differentials are given by
\eta=\frac{f(x)dx^{3/2}}{x}
and
\xi=\frac{g(x)dx^{3/2}}{x}
where
f(x)
and
g(x)
are holomorphic at
x=0
. Hence the local contribution to the metric (
11
) is at worst of order

(12)

\int_{|z|<\epsilon}\frac{|\log|x||}{|x|}|dx|^{2}=\int_{0}^{\epsilon}|\log r|drd\theta<\infty

which reduces to the local contribution in the proof of Lemma
2.4
via the subsitution
x=z^{2}
and again we see the convergence of (
11
) .

2.1.1.
Chern form

A holomorphic bundle
E\to M
equipped with a Hermitian metric is naturally a real oriented bundle of even rank with a Riemannian metric. The Hermitian metric induces the Chern connection, a unique natural Hermitian connection compatible with both the holomorphic structure and the Hermitian metric, which is a metric connection with respect to the underlying Riemannian metric on
E
. Thus the Hermitian metric defined in (
11
) defines the Chern connection on
E_{g,n}^{\vee}\to\cal M_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}
.

Any real oriented bundle
E\to M
of rank
N
equipped with a Riemannian metric
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and a metric connection
A
defines an Euler form

e(E):=\left(\frac{1}{4\pi}\right)^{N}\text{pf}(F_{A})\in\Omega^{N}(M)

where
\text{pf}(F_{A})
is the Pfaffian ofhe curvature of the connection
F_{A}\in\Omega^{2}(M,\text{End}(E))
,
[
17
]
.
The Bianchi identity
\nabla^{A}F_{A}=0
implies
e(E)
is closed, hence it represents a cohomology class on
M
. When
M
is compact, the cohomology class of the Euler form is independent of the choice of metric and connection, and represents the
Euler class
of
E
.

Apply this construction to
E_{g,n}^{\vee}
equipped with its natural Chern connection to produce a canonical Euler form
e(E_{g,n}^{\vee})
. This also gives the Chern-Weil construction of the top Chern form
e(E_{g,n}^{\vee})=c_{N/2}(E_{g,n}^{\vee})
.

2.2.
Measures on the moduli space of curves

We use
e(E_{g,n}^{\vee})
to define a measure on
\cal M_{g,n}^{\rm spin}
which pushes forward to define a measure on
\cal M_{g,n}
. In this paper a measure is given by a top degree differential form. Although differential forms naturally pull back rather than push forward, the nowhere vanishing differential
(\omega^{WP})^{D}
on
\cal M_{g,n}
, for
D=3g-3+n
, allows one to replace any top degree differential form
\eta
by a function
f_{\eta}
defined via
\eta=f_{\eta}p^{*}(\omega^{WP})^{D}
. Then
p_{*}\eta:=\bar{f}_{\eta}(\omega^{WP})^{D}
for
\bar{f}(m):=\sum_{m^{\prime}\in p^{-1}(m)}f(m^{\prime})
the natural pushforward of a function. The Euler form
e(E_{g,n}^{\vee})
restricted to
\cal M_{g,\sigma}^{\rm spin}
is a differential form of degree
4g-4+n+|\sigma|
by (
10
),

e(E_{g,n}^{\vee})\in\Omega^{N}(\cal M_{g,\sigma}^{\rm spin}),\qquad N=4g-4+n+|\sigma|.

Combined with the Weil-Petersson form, it defines a measure
e(E_{g,n}^{\vee})\exp(\omega^{WP})
on
\cal M_{g,\sigma}^{\rm spin}
that pushes forward to a measure on
\cal M_{g,n}
,

\mu_{\sigma}:=p^{\sigma}_{*}\big\{e(E_{g,n}^{\vee})\exp(\omega^{WP})\big\}.

Via the forgetful map
\pi^{(m)}:\cal M_{g,n+m}\to\cal M_{g,n}
, define the formal sum of
S_{n}
symmetric measures
on
\cal M_{g,n}
:

\mu(s)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{s^{m}}{m!}\pi^{(m)}_{*}\mu_{(1^{n},0^{m})}

where the measure
\mu_{m}
is defined on
\cal M_{g,n+m}
.
Then
\mu(s)
has total measure

\widehat{V}^{WP}_{g,n}(s,0,...,0)=\int_{\cal M_{g,n}}\mu(s).

which coincides with (
5
) evaluated at
(L_{1},...,L_{n})=(0,...,0)
.

3.
Moduli space of stable curves

The measures
\mu_{\sigma}
are defined over the moduli space of smooth curves which is non-compact. A powerful tool to prove general properties of these measures, particularly properties of the total measures, is via a compactification of the moduli space. This turns the total measure, given by integration over
\cal M_{g,n}
, into an algebraic topological, or cohomological, invariant and and intersection number in algebraic geometry. The compactifications used here are the moduli space of stable curves
\overline{\cal M}_{g,n}
and a corresponding compactification of the moduli space of spin curves
\overline{\cal M}_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}
. To obtain a relationship of the measure to cohomological invariants of the compactification and to calculate, we need the following.

(i)

Extend
E_{g,n}\to\cal M^{\text{spin}}_{g,n}
to a bundle
\bar{E}_{g,n}\to\overline{\cal M}_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}
.

(ii)

Prove the existence of an extension of
e(E_{g,n}^{\vee})
to
\overline{\cal M}_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}
.

(iii)

Organise the algebraic structure of intersection numbers via a cohomological field theory and an associated partition function.

The extension
(i)
is proven in
[
16
]
, and
(ii)
and
(iii)
are proven in
[
15
]
. The papers
[
15
,
16
]
emphasise the Neveu-Schwartz component, where
\sigma=1^{n}
, so we will recall the constructions here for both Neveu-Schwartz and Ramond points. The construction in
(ii)
naturally relates the extension of
e(E_{g,n}^{\vee})
to the Euler (or Chern)
class
of
\bar{E}_{g,n}
enabling us to deduce that the volumes are given via cohomological calculations, or intersection numbers, over
\overline{\cal M}_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}
.

3.1.
Moduli space of stable spin curves

A stable twisted curve, with group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, is a 1-dimensional orbifold, or stack, and underlying coarse curve
\mathcal{C}\to|\mathcal{C}|
given by a stable curve. The marked points
p_{i}\in\mathcal{C}
and nodes of
\mathcal{C}
have isotropy group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, while all other points have trivial isotropy group. A line bundle
L
over
\mathcal{C}
is a locally equivariant bundle over the local charts, such that at each nodal point there is an equivariant isomorphism of fibres. The representations of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
on
L|_{p}
agree on each local irreducible component at a node due to the equivariant isomorphisms at nodes.

Definition 3.1
.

The moduli space of stable spin curves is defined by

\overline{\cal M}_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}=\{(\mathcal{C},\theta,p_{1},...,p_{n},\phi)\mid\phi:\theta^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}\omega_{\mathcal{C}}^{\text{log}}\}

where
\theta
is a line bundle over a stable, twisted curve
\mathcal{C}
with group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, each nodal point and marked point
p_{i}
has isotropy group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, and all other points have trivial isotropy group.

This gives a natural compactification of the moduli space of twisted, stable, spin curves
\cal M_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}
. We denote the extension of the forgetful map
p:\cal M_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}\to\cal M_{g,n}
, which forgets the spin and orbifold structure, with the same name:

p:\overline{\cal M}_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}\to\overline{\cal M}_{g,n}.

The moduli space of stable spin curves decomposes as in (
8
) into components
\overline{\cal M}_{g,\sigma}^{\text{spin}}\subset\overline{\cal M}_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}
for each
\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{n}
.

Denote by
\mathcal{E}
the universal spin structure on the universal curve
\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi}}{{\longrightarrow}}\overline{\cal M}_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}
using the same notation as its restriction to the moduli space of smooth spin curves.

Definition 3.2
.

Define the bundle
\bar{E}_{g,n}:=-R\pi_{*}\mathcal{E}^{\vee}\hskip-2.84526pt\to\overline{\cal M}_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}
with fibre
H^{1}(\mathcal{C},\theta^{\vee})
.

This defines a bundle, i.e. the dimension of
H^{1}(\mathcal{C},\theta^{\vee})
is constant, due to the vanishing
H^{0}(\mathcal{C},\theta^{\vee})=0
. The proof that
H^{0}(\mathcal{C},\theta^{\vee})=0
when
\mathcal{C}
is smooth used the fact that
\deg\theta^{\vee}<0
. When
\mathcal{C}
is nodal, the proof is subtler. For any irreducible component
X\subset\mathcal{C}
,
\omega_{\mathcal{C}}^{\text{log}}|_{X}=\omega_{X}^{\text{log}}
, hence

\deg\theta^{\vee}|_{X}=-\frac{1}{2}\deg\omega_{\mathcal{C}}^{\text{log}}|_{X}=-\frac{1}{2}\deg\omega_{X}^{\text{log}}<0

since the stability of
\mathcal{C}
implies
\deg\omega_{X}^{\text{log}}>0
for any irreducible component. We conclude that
H^{0}(\mathcal{C},\theta^{\vee})=0
since it is trivial on each irreducible component.

By construction,

\bar{E}_{g,n}|_{\cal M_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}}=E_{g,n}

hence
\bar{E}_{g,n}
defines an extension of
E_{g,n}
to
\overline{\cal M}_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}
.
The rank of
\bar{E}_{g,n}
restricted to the component
\overline{\cal M}_{g,\sigma}^{\text{spin}}
is
2g-2+\tfrac{1}{2}(n+|\sigma|)
which is derived in (
10
).

3.1.1.
Extension of Euler class

The following theorem states that the Euler form
e(E_{g,n}^{\vee})
extends to the compactification
\overline{\cal M}_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}
. On each component
\cal M_{g,\sigma}^{\text{spin}}\subset\overline{\cal M}_{g,\sigma}^{\text{spin}}
, and defines a cohomology class in
H^{4g-4+n+|\sigma|}(\overline{\cal M}_{g,\sigma}^{\text{spin}},\mathbb{R})
. This extension is a consequence of the fact that the Hermitian metric that defines
e(E_{g,n}^{\vee})
extends smoothly from
E_{g,n}^{\vee}
to its extension
\bar{E}_{g,n}^{\vee}
to
\overline{\cal M}_{g,\sigma}^{\text{spin}}
. This enables us to conclude that the cohomology class defined by the extension of
e(E_{g,n}^{\vee})
coincides with the Euler class of
\bar{E}_{g,n}^{\vee}
.

Theorem 3.3

(
[
15
]
)
.

The extension of the Euler form
e(E_{g,n}^{\vee})
to
\overline{\cal M}_{g,\sigma}^{\text{spin}}
defines a cohomology class which coincides with the Euler class
e(\bar{E}_{g,n}^{\vee})\in H^{*}(\overline{\cal M}_{g,\sigma}^{\text{spin}},\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

We prove that the Hermitian metric (
11
) on
E_{g,n}^{\vee}
extends to a Hermitian metric on the bundle
\bar{E}_{g,n}^{\vee}\to\overline{\cal M}_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}
by analysing the behaviour of the poles of the
3/2
differentials representing fibres of
E_{g,n}^{\vee}
.
Let
(\mathcal{C},D,\theta,\phi)\in\overline{\cal M}_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}
such that
\mathcal{C}
is a nodal curve. The pullback of
\theta
to the normalisation of
\mathcal{C}
is an orbifold bundle on each component. In particular, points in the fibre of
\bar{E}_{g,n}^{\vee}
given by elements of
H^{0}(\mathcal{C},\omega_{\mathcal{C}}\otimes\theta)
have the same simple pole behaviour at nodes and at marked points. The pole at a node is present if the behaviour at the node is Neveu-Schwarz and removable if the behaviour at the node is Ramond. Thus the estimate (
12
) applies also at nodes to prove that the Hermitian metric on
H^{0}(\mathcal{C},\omega_{\mathcal{C}}\otimes\theta)
is well-defined when
\mathcal{C}
is nodal.
The conclusion is that the Hermitian metric on
E_{g,n}^{\vee}
extends to a Hermitian metric on
\bar{E}^{\vee}_{g,n}
. Furthermore, it extends to a smooth Hermitian metric on
\bar{E}^{\vee}_{g,n}
because the hyperbolic metric
h
varies smoothly outside of nodes and has a canonical form around nodes, and the Hermitian metric is defined via an integral over
1/\sqrt{h}
times smooth sections.

We conclude that the Euler form
e(E_{g,n}^{\vee})
, constructed from the curvature of the natural metric connection
A
, which is determined uniquely from the Hermitian metric and the holomorphic structure on
E_{g,n}^{\vee}
, extends to
\overline{\cal M}_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}
. The Euler class of
\bar{E}^{\vee}_{g,n}
is determined by a choice of any metric connection on
\bar{E}^{\vee}_{g,n}
, in particular the Chern connection of the extension of the Hermitian metric on
\bar{E}^{\vee}_{g,n}
. Thus the cohomology class defined by the extension of
e(E_{g,n}^{\vee})
coincides with the Euler class
e(\bar{E}_{g,n})\in H^{*}(\overline{\cal M}_{g,n}^{\text{spin}},\mathbb{R})
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

The Weil-Petersson Hermitian metric (

3.1.2.
Spin cohomological field theory

To calculate intersection numbers it is useful to use the structure of a cohomological field theory and more generally any collection of cohomology classes with well-behaved restriction properties to the strata:

\phi_{\text{irr}}:\overline{\cal M}_{g-1,n+2}\to\overline{\cal M}_{g,n},\quad\phi_{h,I}:\overline{\cal M}_{h,|I|+1}\times\overline{\cal M}_{g-h,|I^{c}|+1}\to\overline{\cal M}_{g,n},\ I\subset\{1,...,n\}.

We also write

D_{I}:=\overline{\cal M}_{0,|I|+1}\times\overline{\cal M}_{g,|I^{c}|+1}\to\overline{\cal M}_{g,n}.

A
cohomological field theory
(CohFT) is a pair
(H,\eta)
which consists of a finite-dimensional complex vector space
H
equipped with a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form
\eta
and a sequence of
S_{n}
-equivariant maps:

\Omega_{g,n}:H^{\otimes n}\to H^{*}(\overline{\cal M}_{g,n},\mathbb{C})

that satisfy compatibility conditions from inclusion of strata:

(15)

\begin{split}\phi_{\text{irr}}^{*}\Omega_{g,n}(v_{1}\otimes...\otimes v_{n})&=\Omega_{g-1,n+2}(v_{1}\otimes...\otimes v_{n}\otimes\Delta)\\
\phi_{h,I}^{*}\Omega_{g,n}(v_{1}\otimes...\otimes v_{n})&=\Omega_{h,|I|+1}\otimes\Omega_{g-h,|I^{c}|+1}\big(\bigotimes_{i\in I}v_{i}\otimes\Delta\otimes\bigotimes_{j\in I^{c}}v_{j}\big)\end{split}

where
\Delta\in H\otimes H
is dual to
\eta\in H^{*}\otimes H^{*}
. There exists a vector
1\!\!1\in H
satisfying

(16)

\Omega_{0,3}(v_{1}\otimes v_{2}\otimes 1\!\!1)=\eta(v_{1},v_{2}).

Using
\overline{\cal M}_{g,n}^{\text{spin}}
, we define an example of a CohFT.
Identify the set
\{0,1\}
with the set of distinct vectors
\{e_{0},e_{1}\}\subset H\cong\mathbb{C}^{2}
which induces
\{0,1\}^{n}\to H^{\otimes n}
where
\sigma\mapsto e_{\sigma}=e_{\sigma_{1}}\otimes...\otimes e_{\sigma_{n}}
and define
\eta
by
\eta(e_{i},e_{j})=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{ij}
.
Define

\Omega^{\text{spin}}_{g,n}(e_{\sigma_{1}}\otimes...\otimes e_{\sigma_{n}}):=p_{*}c(\bar{E}_{g,\sigma})\in H^{*}(\overline{\cal M}_{g,n},\mathbb{Q})

where recall that
p:\overline{\cal M}_{g,n}^{\rm spin}\to\overline{\cal M}_{g,n}
forgets the spin and orbifold structures.
These classes, known as Chiodo classes
[
3
]
, form a CohFT on
(V,\eta)
. This is proven in
[
11
]
using an expression for
p_{*}c(\bar{E}_{g,\sigma}^{\vee})
in terms of sums over stable graphs proven in
[
8
]
. It is proven directly in
[
16
]
via pullback properties of the bundles
\bar{E}_{g,\sigma}
.

For
e_{\sigma}=e_{\sigma_{1}}\otimes...\otimes e_{\sigma_{n}}
, define

(17)

\Omega_{g,n}(e_{\sigma}):=\epsilon_{g,\sigma}p_{*}c_{\text{top}}(\bar{E}_{g,\sigma}^{\vee})

where
c_{\text{top}}=c_{2g-2+\frac{1}{2}(n+|\sigma|)}
and
\epsilon_{g,\sigma}=2^{g-1+\frac{1}{2}(n+|\sigma|)}
. These classes satisfy (

Theorem 3.8
.

The disk function
G(z):=\mathcal{L}(L\widehat{V}_{0,1}(s,L))

Proof.

Consider the map
\pi^{(2m)}:\overline{\cal M}_{0,2m+3}\to\overline{\cal M}_{0,3}
that forgets all but three marked points. Then

\psi_{1}=(\pi^{(2m)})^{*}\psi_{1}+\sum_{\underset{2,3\in I^{c}}{1\in I}}D_{I}=\sum_{\underset{2,3\in I^{c}}{1\in I}}D_{I}

since

\displaystyle\psi_{1}^{m}=\psi_{1}^{m-1}\sum_{\underset{2,3\in I^{c}}{1\in I}}D_{I}
for

\displaystyle\int_{\overline{\cal M}_{0,2m+3}}\hskip-5.69054pt\psi_{1}^{m}\Omega_{0,2m+3}(e_{1}\otimes e_{0}^{\otimes 2m+2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\underset{2,3\in I^{c}}{1\in I}}\int_{\overline{\cal M}_{0,2m+3}}\hskip-14.22636pt\psi_{1}^{m-1}D_{I}\Omega_{0,2m+3}(e_{1}\otimes e_{0}^{\otimes 2m+2}).

The summand is given by

\displaystyle\int_{\overline{\cal M}_{0,2m+1}}\hskip-14.22636pt

\displaystyle\psi_{1}^{m-1}D_{I}\Omega_{0,2m+3}(e_{1}\otimes e_{0}^{\otimes 2m+2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\overline{\cal M}_{0,|I|+1}}\hskip-14.22636pt\psi_{1}^{m-1}\Omega_{0,|I|+1}(e_{1}\otimes e_{0}^{\otimes(|I|-1)}\otimes e_{1})\int_{\overline{\cal M}_{0,|I^{c}|+1}}\hskip-14.22636pt\Omega_{0,|I^{c}|+1}(e_{1}\otimes e_{0}^{\otimes|I^{c}|}).

For

\deg\Omega_{0,n}(e_{\sigma})=\frac{1}{2}(n+|\sigma|)-2=n-3\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad|\sigma|=n-2

hence the second factor in the summand vanishes unless

. So

and only one summand is non-zero, leaving

\displaystyle\int_{\overline{\cal M}_{0,2m+3}}\hskip-5.69054pt\psi_{1}^{m}\Omega_{0,2m+3}

\displaystyle(e_{1}\otimes e_{0}^{\otimes 2m+2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\overline{\cal M}_{0,2m+2}}\hskip-14.22636pt\psi_{1}^{m-1}\Omega_{0,2m+2}(e_{1}^{\otimes 2}\otimes e_{0}^{\otimes 2m})\int_{\overline{\cal M}_{0,3}}\hskip-14.22636pt\Omega_{0,|I^{c}|+1}(e_{1}\otimes e_{0}^{\otimes 2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\overline{\cal M}_{0,2m+2}}\hskip-14.22636pt\psi_{1}^{m-1}\Omega_{0,2m+2}(e_{1}^{\otimes 2}\otimes e_{0}^{\otimes 2m})

\displaystyle=\int_{\overline{\cal M}_{0,2m+2}}\hskip-14.22636pt\psi_{1}^{m-1}\psi_{2}\pi^{*}\Omega_{0,2m+1}(e_{1}\otimes e_{0}^{\otimes 2m})

\displaystyle=\int_{\overline{\cal M}_{0,2m+2}}\hskip-14.22636pt\psi_{2}\pi^{*}\big(\psi_{1}^{m-1}\Omega_{0,2m+1}(e_{1}\otimes e_{0}^{\otimes 2m})\big)

\displaystyle=(2m-1)!!

where the first equality uses the restriction of

to a boundary divisor, the second equality uses

Hence

4.

The underlying algebraic, or integrable, structures of partition functions storing intersection numbers over the moduli spaces of marked, stable curves began with the conjecture of Witten

. We relate this algebraic structure directly to the geometric structure of the intersection numbers, and in particular use it to prove Theorem
2

\bar{{\cal Z}}(\hbar,s,\vec{t})
.

The following Virasoro operators were defined in
[
7
]

4.2.
Supergeometry

Mirzakhani
[
13
]
proved a recursion similar to (
6
) via an argument which uses the moduli space of hyperbolic surfaces with geodesic boundary. On any given hyperbolic surface with a distinguished boundary component
\beta
she produced a one-to-one correspondence between a collection of disjoint intervals in
\beta
and embedded pairs of pants in the hyperbolic surface that meet
\beta
. The length of each of the intervals is determined by its corresponding pair of pants. Mirzakhani analysed an arbitrary hyperbolic pair of pants to produce an expression for the length of such an interval as a function of the lengths
x
,
y
and
z
of the three boundary components of the pair of pants, which produces kernels similar to
D(x,y,z)
and
R(x,y,z)
defined in the introduction. The moduli spaces of hyperbolic surfaces with geodesic boundary components of lengths
(L_{1},...,L_{n})
have natural symplectic forms, hence also volumes, which correspond to the symplectic deformations
\omega(L_{1},...,L_{n})
used in the definition (
4
). Mirzakhani applied this construction to each point in the moduli space to produce recursive relations between volumes of moduli spaces of hyperbolic surfaces with a given topology and volumes of moduli spaces of hyperbolic surfaces obtained by cutting out a pair of pants from a surface, thus reducing the complexity of the topology of the hyperbolic surface. The recursive relations involve the kernels and the moduli spaces of simpler complexity as in (
6
).

Stanford and Witten
[
19
]
generalised Mirzakhani’s argument to the moduli space of super hyperbolic surfaces with geodesic boundary. They used this to produce a heuristic proof of (
6
) in the
s=0
case, when all boundary components are Neveu-Schwarz. Via an analysis of super hyperbolic pairs of pants with boundary components of (super) length
x
,
y
and
z
, they produced the kernels
D(x,y,z)
and
R(x,y,z)
defined in the introduction, in the Neveu-Schwarz case. They produced similar kernels when two of the boundary components of the super hyperbolic pair of pants are Ramond. If we apply their construction to a hyperbolic surface with
n
marked Neveu-Schwarz geodesic boundary components together with a gas of Ramond punctures we produce a heuristic proof of (
6
). It produces (without proof) a recursion that contains all of (
6
) plus extra terms with new kernels corresponding to pairs of pants with Ramond boundary components. The extra terms should give a zero contribution to the recursion, because we expect the volumes
to vanish in the case of Ramond geodesic boundary components. Stanford and Witten give an argument in
[
19
]
for the vanishing of these volumes. Such a proof of the recursion is so far incomplete, nevertheless it is useful to heuristically explain the form of the recursion. An explanation of the form of the recursion is currently beyond the algebro-geometric methods used in the proofs of Theorems
1
and
2
.
