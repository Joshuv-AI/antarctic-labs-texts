Well-covered Unit Graphs of Finite Rings
Shahin Rahimi, Ashkan Nikseresht Department of Mathematics, College of Sciences, Shiraz University,71457-44776, Shiraz, Iran

Abstract
Let R R be a finite ring with identity. The unit graph (unitary Cayley graph) of R R is the graph with vertex set R R , where two distinct vertices x x and y y are adjacent exactly whenever x + y x+y is a unit in R R ( x − y x-y is a unit in R R ). Here, we study independent sets of unit graphs of matrix rings over finite fields and use them to characterize all finite rings for which the unit graph is well-covered or Cohen-Macaulay. Moreover, we show that the unit graph of R R is well-covered if and only if the unitary Cayley graph of R R is well-covered and the characteristic of R / J ⁡ ( R ) R/J(R) is two.



Keywords: unit graph, unitary Cayley graph, well-covered graph, finite ring, Cohen-Macaulay graph.

Introduction

Throughout this paper, all rings are assumed to be finite, associative, and with a nonzero identity, and
R
always denotes a ring. Additionally, all graphs considered here are simple and undirected. We let

\mathrm{U}(R)
denote the set of all unit elements in
R
,
J(R)
the Jacobson radical of
R
,
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
the ring of integers modulo
n
, and
I_{n}
the identity matrix of size
n
.

Recall that the
unit graph
of
R
, which we denote by
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
, is the (undirected) graph with vertex set
R
,
where two distinct vertices
x
and
y
are adjacent if and only if
x+y
is a unit in
R
. For certain rings
(roughly speaking, for
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
), unit graphs were introduced by Grimaldi in
[
7
]
and later
generalized to arbitrary rings in
[
3
]
. Recently, several researchers have delved into the study of unit
graphs associated with rings; for instance, see

[
3
,
9
,
14
,
15
,
2
]
.

A graph is called
well-covered
if all its maximal independent sets are of the same size. Well-covered
graphs have been extensively studied in graph theory from various perspectives, including algorithmic, algebraic, and structural characterizations (see, for example,
[
4
,
6
,
25
,
24
,
22
,
12
,
16
]
).

In
[
2
]
, well-covered unit graphs of commutative rings are characterized. The main aim of this research
is to extend these results to non-commutative rings by establishing a connection with unitary Cayley graphs. Recall
that the
unitary Cayley graph

\mathcal{G}(R)
of
R
is the (undirected) Cayley graph of the abelian group
(R,+)
with
respect to the set
\mathrm{U}(R)
of unit elements of
R
. In other words, its vertex set is
R
, and two vertices
x
and
y

are adjacent if and only if
x-y
is a unit in
R
. Here, we show that the unit graph of finite ring
R
is
well-covered if and only if the unitary Cayley graph of
R
is well-covered and
\mathrm{char}\,\big(R/J(R)\big)=2
. Subsequently, by
utilizing the results on well-covered unitary Cayley graphs presented in
[
22
]
, we attain the desired result. A
key step in proving these results is using the Wedderburn-Artin Theorem to relate the structure of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
for an
arbitrary finite ring
R
to
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(S)
where
S
is a product of matrix rings over some finite fields and hence
simplifying the problem.

Given a graph
G
with vertex set
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
and edge set
E(G)
, the
edge ideal
of
G
is the ideal
I(G)=\langle x_{i}x_{j}\mid{i,j}\in E(G)\rangle
of
K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
where
K
is a field. A graph

G
is called
Cohen-Macaulay
(
CM
, for short) over
K
when the quotient ring
K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/I(G)
is a Cohen-Macaulay ring (see,
[
5
]
or
[
8
]
for the algebraic background). In the realm of commutative algebra, extensive research has been devoted to the study of edge ideals associated with graphs and CM graphs, see for example
[
2
,
10
,
18
,
11
,
20
,
8
,
19
,
21
,
12
]
and the references therein. Here, as a corollary to our results on well-covered unit graphs, we present a characterization of rings with a CM unit graph.

2
The Main Results

In this section, we aim to characterize finite rings for which the unit graph is well-covered or Cohen-Macaulay.
To that aim, we first recall the concept of semisimple rings.
A
semisimple ring
is an Artinian ring whose Jacobson radical is zero (see
[
1
, Theorem 15.20]
).
Consider a finite ring
R
. Consequently,
R/J(R)
is a semisimple ring. The Wedderburn-Artin Theorem states that a semisimple ring is isomorphic to
M_{n_{1}}(D_{1})\times\cdots\times M_{n_{r}}(D_{r})
for some division rings
D_{i}
and
positive integers
n_{i}
(see
[
1
, Section 13]
). Moreover, by Wedderburn’s Little Theorem, it is well-known that a finite division ring is a field. Hence, for any finite ring
R
, we have
R/J(R)\simeq M_{n_{1}}(F_{1})\times\cdots\times M_{n_{r}}(F_{r})
for some finite fields
F_{i}
and positive integers
n_{i}
.

According to
[
22
, Proposition 3.1]
, for a subset
\mathcal{A}
of finite ring
R
,
\mathcal{A}
is a maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}(R)
if and only if
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
is a maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}\big(R/J(R)\big)
, where
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
denotes the image of
\mathcal{A}
in
R/J(R)
, and
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}+J(R)
. This correspondence served as a crucial step in reducing the problem to semisimple rings for unitary Cayley graphs. It is noteworthy that this correspondence does not hold for unit graphs in general. For instance,
\{1,2\}
forms a maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(\mathbb{Z}_{3})
, but its inverse image in
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(\mathbb{Z}_{9})
, namely
\{1,2,4,5,7,8\}
, is not even an independent set. Nevertheless, this discrepancy does not occur when
2
is a zero-divisor.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose
R
is a finite ring with
2\notin\mathrm{U}(R)
. Then,
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
is a well-covered graph if and only if so is
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\big(R/J(R)\big)
.

Proof.

The proof follows a similar approach to that in
[
22
, Proposition 3.1]
, with some minor adjustments. Let
\mathcal{A}
be a subset of
R
. It suffices to show that
\mathcal{A}
is a maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
if and only if
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
is a maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\big(R/J(R)\big)
and
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}+J(R)
.
First, assume that
\mathcal{A}
is a maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
. Noting that
u+J(R)\in\mathrm{U}\big(R/J(R)\big)
iff
u\in\mathrm{U}(R)
, it is straightforward to show that
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
is a maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\big(R/J(R)\big)
. To establish
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}+J(R)
, consider
a+j\in\mathcal{A}+J(R)
, where
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
j\in J(R)
. If
a+j
is adjacent to some
a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
, then
(a+a^{\prime})+j
is a unit of
R
. Consequently,
a+a^{\prime}
is a unit of
R
. Since
\mathcal{A}
is an independent set, this cannot happen unless
a=a^{\prime}
. Hence,
2a\in\mathrm{U}(R)
which contradicts the assumption that
2
is a zero-divisor. Therefore,
\mathcal{A}\cup\{a+j\}
is an independent set, which, by maximality of
\mathcal{A}
, implies that
a+j\in\mathcal{A}
, and thus
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}+J(R)
.

Now, for the converse, let
a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
. If
a
is adjacent to
a^{\prime}
, then
\bar{a}
is adjacent to
\bar{a^{\prime}}
, unless
\bar{a}=\bar{a^{\prime}}
. Since
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
is an independent set, it follows that
\bar{a}=\bar{a^{\prime}}
. Hence,
a^{\prime}=a+j
for some
j\in J(R)
. However,
a+a^{\prime}=2a+j\in\mathrm{U}(R)
implies that
2a\in\mathrm{U}(R)
, leading to a contradiction. Thus,
\mathcal{A}
is an independent set. For maximality, assume that
x\in R
is not adjacent to any element of
\mathcal{A}
. Since
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
is a maximal independent set, we have
\bar{x}=\bar{a}
for some
a\in\mathcal{A}
. Hence,
x=a+j
for some
j\in J(R)
which implies that
x\in\mathcal{A}+J(R)=\mathcal{A}
, as desired. Thus, the assertion follows.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

In the proof of Lemma
2.1
, we indeed showed that if
2\not\in\mathrm{U}(R)
, then
x
is adjacent to
y
in
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
if and only if
\bar{x}
is adjacent to
\bar{y}
in
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\big(R/J(R)\big)
.

As mentioned, for a finite ring
R
with
2\in\mathrm{U}(R)
, there is no connection between the maximal independent sets of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
and
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\big(R/J(R)\big)
in general. Indeed, we show that
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
is not well-covered whenever
2\in\mathrm{U}(R)
. First, we deal with rings of matrices. Recall that a diagonal matrix whose entries are
+1
or
-1
is called a
signature matrix
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
F
be a finite field with
\mathrm{char}\,(F)\neq 2
and let
n
be a positive integer. Then,

\mathcal{S}_{n}=\{\mathrm{diag}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\mid a_{i}=\pm 1\}
is a maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\big(M_{n}(F)\big)
.

Proof.

Assuming
S,S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
are distinct elements, we can find a row, say the
i
-th row, in which they differ. However, in this case,
S+S^{\prime}
contains a zero row, that is, the
i
-th row. Hence,
\mathcal{S}_{n}
is an independent set. For maximality, suppose
A\in M_{n}(F)\setminus\mathcal{S}_{n}
such that
\mathcal{S}_{n}\cup\{A\}
is an independent set.
We know that if
S\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, then the matrix
S^{\prime}
obtained by negating any row of
S
is also in
\mathcal{S}_{n}
. Thus,
\det(A+S^{\prime})=\det(A+S)=0
, since
A\neq S,S^{\prime}
.
Letting
1\leq m\leq n
, by induction on
m
, we show that for any
S\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, the matrix formed by selecting any
m
rows from
2I_{n}
and the remaining rows from
A+S
is not invertible. Denote the rows of
A
and
S
by
\textbf{a}_{i}
and
\textbf{s}_{i}
, respectively. Assume that
m=1
and without loss of generality, let the first row of the aforementioned matrix be the first row of
2I_{n}
. Note that the first
m
rows of
I_{n}
is the matrix
(I_{m}\mid 0_{m\times(n-m)})
and
2\textbf{s}_{1}=\pm(2I_{1}\mid 0_{1\times(n-1)})
. Using row
linearity of the determinant, it can be said that:

\displaystyle\pm\left|\hskip-7.5pt\begin{array}[]{rcl}2I_{1}&\mid&\hskip-20.00003pt0_{1\times(n-1)}\\
&\textbf{a}_{2}+\textbf{s}_{2}&\\
&\vdots&\\
&\textbf{a}_{n}+\textbf{s}_{n}&\end{array}\hskip-2.5pt\right|

\displaystyle=\begin{vmatrix}2\textbf{s}_{1}\\
\textbf{a}_{2}+\textbf{s}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\textbf{a}_{n}+\textbf{s}_{n}\end{vmatrix}=\begin{vmatrix}\textbf{a}_{1}+\textbf{s}_{1}\\
\textbf{a}_{2}+\textbf{s}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\textbf{a}_{n}+\textbf{s}_{n}\end{vmatrix}-\begin{vmatrix}\textbf{a}_{1}-\textbf{s}_{1}\\
\textbf{a}_{2}+\textbf{s}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\textbf{a}_{n}+\textbf{s}_{n}\end{vmatrix}

\displaystyle=\det(A+S)-\det(A+S^{\prime})=0.

Thus, the hypothesis holds true for
m=1
. Now, for
m>1
, it can be observed that (here in each term,
0
denotes the zero matrix with the appropriate size):

\pm\left|\hskip-10.00002pt\begin{array}[]{rcc}2I_{m}&\mid&\hskip-37.50005pt0\\
&\textbf{a}_{m+1}+\textbf{s}_{m+1}&\\
&\vdots&\\
&\textbf{a}_{n}+\textbf{s}_{n}&\end{array}\hskip-10.00002pt\right|=\left|\hskip-2.5pt\begin{array}[]{ccc}2I_{m-1}&\mid&\hskip-22.50003pt0\\
&\textbf{2}s_{m}&\\
&\vdots&\\
&\textbf{a}_{n}+\textbf{s}_{n}&\end{array}\hskip-5.0pt\right|=\left|\hskip-2.5pt\begin{array}[]{ccc}2I_{m-1}&\mid&\hskip-30.00005pt0\\
&\textbf{a}_{m}+\textbf{s}_{m}&\\
&\vdots&\\
&\textbf{a}_{n}+\textbf{s}_{n}&\end{array}\hskip-10.00002pt\right|-\left|\hskip-2.5pt\begin{array}[]{ccc}2I_{m-1}&\mid&\hskip-30.00005pt0\\
&\textbf{a}_{m}-\textbf{s}_{m}&\\
&\vdots&\\
&\textbf{a}_{n}+\textbf{s}_{n}&\end{array}\hskip-10.00002pt\right|=0,

by induction hypothesis. Therefore, the hypothesis holds for any
m
. As a result, by setting
m=n
, we obtain
\det(2I_{n})=0
, leading to a contradiction since
\mathrm{char}\,(F)\neq 2
. Thus,
\mathcal{S}_{n}
is a maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\big(M_{n}(F)\big)
.
∎

Indeed, in Lemma
2.3
, we constructed a maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\big(M_{n}(F)\big)
, whose size is a power of two. On the other hand, it is not hard to see that the family of matrices in
M_{n}(F)
whose first row is zero forms a maximal independent set of size
|F|^{n^{2}-n}
, which is an odd number. Consequently, we obtain the following result.

Corollary 2.4
.

For any finite field
F
and positive integer
n
, if
\mathrm{char}\,(F)\neq 2
, then
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\big(M_{n}(F)\big)
is not well-covered.

Next, we aim to extend the above corollary to semisimple rings. Initially, we identify certain maximal independent sets of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
R=R_{1}\times R_{2}\times\cdots\times R_{t}
, where each
R_{i}
is a finite ring and each
\mathcal{M}_{i}
is a maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R_{i})
. Then the following statements hold true.

(i)

If
\mathcal{M}_{i}\subseteq R_{i}\setminus\mathrm{U}(R_{i})
, then
R_{1}\times\cdots\times R_{i-1}\times\mathcal{M}_{i}\times R_{i+1}\times\cdots\times R_{t}
is a maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
.

(ii)

If
2\in\mathrm{U}(R)
and for each
i
we have
\mathcal{M}_{i}\subseteq\mathrm{U}(R_{i})
, then
\mathcal{M}_{1}\times\mathcal{M}_{2}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{M}_{t}
is a maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
.

Proof.

(
i
) For the sake of simplicity, we prove that if
R
and
S
are finite rings and
\mathcal{M}\subseteq R\setminus\mathrm{U}(R)
is a maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
, then
\mathcal{M}\times S
is a maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R\times S)
. To this end, consider
(m,s),(m^{\prime},s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{M}\times S
. If
m=m^{\prime}
, then
(m,s)+(m^{\prime},s^{\prime})=(2m,s+s^{\prime})
is not a unit of
R\times S
since
m\in\mathcal{M}\subseteq R\setminus\mathrm{U}(R)
. So, assume that
m\neq m^{\prime}
. As
\mathcal{M}
is an independent set,
m+m^{\prime}
is not a unit of
R
. Hence,
(m,s)+(m^{\prime},s^{\prime})=(m+m^{\prime},s+s^{\prime})
is not a unit of
R\times S
. Therefore,
\mathcal{M}\times S
is an independent set. For maximality, let
(\mathcal{M}\times S)\cup\{(r,s)\}
be an independent set, where
r\in R\setminus\mathcal{M}
. It can be observed that for any
m\in\mathcal{M}
,
(r,s)+(m,1-s)=(r+m,1)
is not a unit of
R\times S
. Consequently, for any
m\in\mathcal{M}
,
r+m
is not a unit. Since
\mathcal{M}
is a maximal independent set, it follows that
r\in\mathcal{M}
, a contradiction from which the result follows.

(
ii
) Let
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{1}\times\mathcal{M}_{2}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{M}_{t}
. It is not difficult to see that
\mathcal{M}
forms an independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
. To establish its maximality, suppose
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{t})\in R\setminus\mathcal{M}
such that
\mathcal{M}\cup\{x\}
is an independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
and
x_{1}\notin\mathcal{M}_{1}
. Consequently, there exists
m_{1}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
such that
x_{1}+m_{1}
is a unit in
R_{1}
. If all the other
x_{i}
’s are in
\mathcal{M}_{i}
, then as
x\in\mathrm{U}(R)
and
\mathcal{M}_{i}\subseteq\mathrm{U}(R_{i})
, we infer that
x
is adjacent to
(m_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{t})
, which is a contradiction. Therefore, we may assume that
x_{2}\notin\mathcal{M}_{2}
. By a similar argument, we obtain that for each
i
we have
x_{i}\notin\mathcal{M}_{i}
, but there is some
m_{i}
such that
x_{i}+m_{i}
is unit. Consequently,
x
is adjacent to
(m_{1},\ldots,m_{t})\in\mathcal{M}
, resulting in a contradiction. Thus, the assertion follows.
∎

Noting that
r\in R
is a unit iff it is not contained in any maximal ideal of
R
, it follows that
r\in\mathrm{U}(R)
iff
\bar{r}\in\mathrm{U}\big(R/J(R)\big)
. We use this in the following without any further mention.

Lemma 2.6
.

Suppose that
R
is a finite ring and
\bar{\mathcal{M}}
is a maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\big(R/J(R)\big)
.

(i)

If
\bar{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\big(R/J(R)\big)\setminus\mathrm{U}\big(R/J(R)\big)
, then
\mathcal{M}+J(R)
is a maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
with size
|\bar{\mathcal{M}}||J(R)|
.

(ii)

If
\bar{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathrm{U}\big(R/J(R)\big)
and
2\in\mathrm{U}(R)
, then by choosing
\mathcal{M}
to be a complete set of representatives of
\bar{\mathcal{M}}
,
\mathcal{M}
forms a maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
with
|\mathcal{M}|=|\bar{\mathcal{M}}|
.

Proof.

(
i
) Assume that
m_{1}+j_{1}
is adjacent to
m_{2}+j_{2}
in
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
for some
m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathcal{M}
and
j_{1},j_{2}\in J(R)
. Consequently,
(m_{1}+j_{1})+(m_{2}+j_{2})=(m_{1}+m_{2})+(j_{1}+j_{2})
is a unit in
R
. By taking images from
R
to
R/J(R)
, it follows that
\bar{m}_{1}+\bar{m}_{2}
is a unit in
R/J(R)
. This contradicts the independence of
\bar{\mathcal{M}}
unless
\bar{m}_{1}=\bar{m}_{2}
. Since
\bar{\mathcal{M}}
consists only of non-unit elements, thus
\bar{m}_{1}+\bar{m}_{2}=2\bar{m}_{1}
is not a unit element of
R/J(R)
, a contradiction. Therefore,
\mathcal{M}+J(R)
is an independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
. To prove that it is maximal, let
r\in R
. If
\bar{r}\in\bar{\mathcal{M}}
, then
r-m\in J(R)
for some
m\in\mathcal{M}
. Hence
r\in\mathcal{M}+J(R)
. So, assume that
\bar{r}\notin\bar{\mathcal{M}}
. Since
\bar{\mathcal{M}}
is maximal, there exists
m\in\mathcal{M}
such that
\bar{r}
is adjacent to
\bar{m}
. Thus,
\bar{r}+\bar{m}
is a unit in
R/J(R)
. Consequently,
r+m
is a unit in
R
, implying that
r
is adjacent to
m\in\mathcal{M}+J(R)
, as desired.

(
ii
) For
\bar{\mathcal{M}}=\{\bar{m}_{1},\ldots,\bar{m}_{t}\}
, we choose
\mathcal{M}
to be
\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{t}\}
such that
\bar{m}_{i}\neq\bar{m}_{j}
for each
i\neq j
. Under this assumption, if
m_{i}
is adjacent to
m_{j}
for
i\neq j
, then
\bar{m}_{i}
is adjacent to
\bar{m}_{j}
, which is absurd. Hence,
\mathcal{M}
is an independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
. To prove its maximality, let
\mathcal{M}\cup\{r\}
be an independent set, where
r\in R\setminus\mathcal{M}
. As a result, for all
m\in M
, we have
m+r\not\in\mathrm{U}(R)
, implying
\bar{m}+\bar{r}\not\in\mathrm{U}\big(R/J(R)\big)
. Since
\bar{\mathcal{M}}
is a maximal independent set, we infer that
\bar{r}=\bar{m}
for some
m\in\mathcal{M}
. Consequently,
2\bar{m}
is not a unit element of
R/J(R)
, leading to a contradiction, since
\bar{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathrm{U}\big(R/J(R)\big)
and
2\in\mathrm{U}(R)
. Hence, the result follows.
∎

Now, we can show that if the unit graph of
R
is well-covered, then
2
must be a zero-divisor in
R
.

Proposition 2.7
.

If
R
is a finite ring and
2\in\mathrm{U}(R)
, then
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
is not well-covered.

Proof.

Given that
R
is a finite ring, the quotient ring
R/J(R)
is semisimple and isomorphic to
M_{n_{1}}(F_{1})\times\cdots\times M_{n_{r}}(F_{r})
for some finite fields
F_{i}
and positive integers
n_{i}
. For each
i
, let
\mathcal{S}_{i}
be the set of signature matrices in
M_{n_{i}}(F_{i})
. By Lemma
2.3
,
\mathcal{S}_{i}
is a maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\big(M_{n_{i}}(F_{i})\big)
with
|\mathcal{S}_{i}|=2^{n_{i}}
. By virtue of Lemma
2.5
,
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{S}_{r}
forms a maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\big(R/J(R)\big)
contained in
\mathrm{U}\big(R/J(R)\big)
with size
2^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times 2^{n_{r}}
. On the other hand, the family
\mathcal{M}_{1}
of matrices in
M_{n_{1}}(F_{1})
, whose first row is zero, is a maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\big(M_{n_{1}}(F_{1})\big)
. Consequently, by Lemma
2.5
,
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{1}\times M_{n_{2}}(F_{2})\times\cdots\times M_{n_{r}}(F_{r})
is a maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\big(R/J(R)\big)
contained in
\big(R/J(R)\big)\setminus\mathrm{U}\big(R/J(R)\big)
. Since
2\in\mathrm{U}(R)
, it follows that
2\in\mathrm{U}\big(R/J(R)\big)
. Hence,
2\nmid|\mathcal{M}_{1}|
and for all
i
we have
2\nmid|M_{n_{i}}(F_{i})|
. Consequently,
2\nmid|\mathcal{M}|
, which implies that
|\mathcal{M}||J(R)|\neq|\mathcal{S}|
. Now, by applying Lemma
2.6
,
we establish the existence of two maximal independent sets of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
with different sizes. This implies that
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
is not well-covered.
∎

Next, we consider the well-coveredness of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
for rings
R
in which
2
is a zero-divisor. First, we need a lemma.

Lemma 2.8
.

If
R
and
S
are finite semisimple rings with
\mathrm{char}\,(R)=2\neq\mathrm{char}\,(S)
, then
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R\times S)
is not well-covered.

Proof.

Assume that
R\simeq M_{n_{1}}(F_{1})\times\cdots\times M_{n_{r}}(F_{r})
and
S\simeq M_{m_{1}}(E_{1})\times\cdots\times M_{m_{s}}(E_{s})
for some finite fields
F_{i}
and
E_{i}
with
\mathrm{char}\,(F_{i})=2\neq\mathrm{char}\,(E_{i})
and positive integers
n_{i}
and
m_{i}
. Let
\mathcal{M}_{1}
be the maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\big(M_{n_{1}}(F_{1})\big)
as in Proposition
2.7
. With the aid of Lemma
2.5
, we see that
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{1}\times M_{n_{2}}(F_{2})\times\cdots\times M_{n_{r}}(F_{r})
is a maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
. Consequently,
\mathcal{M}\times S
is a maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R\times S)
with size
|\mathcal{M}||S|
.

Now, we construct another maximal independent set with a different size. In order to do this, let
\mathcal{X}
be the set of all non-unit elements of
S
, and set
\mathcal{N}=(R\times\{0\})\cup(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{X})
. Since unit elements of

R\times S
are exactly of the form
(a,b)
, such that
a\in\mathrm{U}(R)
and
b\in\mathrm{U}(S)
, it is not hard to see that

\mathcal{N}
is an independent set. If
\mathcal{N}\cup\{(x,y)\}
is an independent set for some
(x,y)\in\big(R\times S\big)\setminus\mathcal{N}
,
then
(x,y)+(1-x,0)=(1,y)
implies that
y
is not a unit element of
S
and hence
x\notin\mathcal{M}
. Now, we prove the following claim.

Claim:

If
y
is a nonzero non-unit element of the finite semisimple ring
S
, then there exists some non-unit
z\in S
such that
y+z
is a unit in
S
.

Proof of the claim:

Since
S\simeq M_{m_{1}}(E_{1})\times\cdots\times M_{m_{s}}(E_{s})
, let
y=(Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{s})
, where
Y_{i}\in M_{m_{i}}(E_{i})
. If
Y_{i}=0
, set
Z_{i}=I_{m_{i}}
and if
Y_{i}
is invertible, set
Z_{i}=0
. Else,
Y_{i}
is a nonzero non-unit element of
M_{m_{i}}(E_{i})
. Using Gaussian elimination method, we can find invertible matrices
U
and
V
such that
Y_{i}=U\left(\begin{smallmatrix}I_{t}&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)V
where
1\leq t<m_{i}
.
Thus
1\leq m_{i}-t<m_{i}
.
Set
Z_{i}=U\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0\\
0&I_{m_{i}-t}\end{smallmatrix}\right)V
. Clearly,
Z_{i}
is not invertible. Moreover,
Y_{i}+Z_{i}=UI_{m_{i}}V=UV
, which is invertible.
Therefore, in all three cases,
Y_{i}+Z_{i}
is invertible. Now, since
y
is not zero, we can find some nonzero
Y_{i}
, for which
Z_{i}
is not invertible. Hence,
z=(Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{s})
is a non-unit element of
S
such that
y+z
is a unit.

Now,
\mathcal{M}
being a maximal independent set of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
and
x\notin\mathcal{M}
implies that there exists some
m\in\mathcal{M}
such that
x+m\in\mathrm{U}(R)
. But then
(m,z)\in\mathcal{N}
and

(x,y)+(m,z)=(x+m,y+z)
is a unit element of
R\times S
, that is,
(x,y)
is adjacent to
(m,z)\in\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{N}
,
contradicting the independence of
\mathcal{N}\cup\{(x,y)\}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{N}
is a maximal independent set.

If
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R\times S\big)
is
well-covered, then the maximal independent sets
\mathcal{M}\times S
and
\mathcal{N}
must have the same size, implying
|S|=|\mathcal{N}|/|\mathcal{M}|
. Since
(R\times\{0\})\cap(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{X})=\mathcal{M}\times\{0\}
, it can be seen that:

|S|=|\mathcal{N}|/|\mathcal{M}|=\big(|R|+|\mathcal{M}||\mathcal{X}|-|\mathcal{M}|\big)/|\mathcal{M}|=|R|/|\mathcal{M}|+|\mathcal{X}|-1.

From
|S|=|\mathcal{X}|+|\mathrm{U}(S)|
, we obtain
|\mathrm{U}(S)|=|R|/|\mathcal{M}|-1
. Now, we show that the two sides of the last equality have different parity.
Since all the
F_{i}
’s are of characteristic
2
,
|R|
is a power of two, making
|R|/|\mathcal{M}|-1
an odd integer. On the other hand, for each
i
, we have
\mathrm{char}\,(E_{i})\neq 2
, hence

|\mathrm{U}\big(M_{m_{i}}(E_{i})\big)|=\big(|E_{i}|^{m_{i}}-1\big)\big(|E_{i}|^{m_{i}}-|E_{i}|\big)\cdots\big(|E_{i}|^{m_{i}}-|E_{i}|^{m_{i}-1}\big)

is an even integer. Consequently,
|\mathrm{U}(S)|=\big|\mathrm{U}\big(M_{m_{1}}(E_{1})\big)\big|\cdots\big|\mathrm{U}\big(M_{m_{s}}(E_{s})\big)\big|
is an even integer, which is a contradiction. Thus, the result follows.
∎

Next, we present a necessary and sufficient condition under which unit graphs and unitary Cayley graphs coincide.

Proposition 2.9
.

If
R
is a finite ring, then
\mathcal{G}(R)=\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
if and only if
\mathrm{char}\,\big(R/J(R)\big)=2
.

Proof.

Using Remark
2.2
and
[
22
, Proposition 3.1]
, the proof of the converse is routine. For the implication,
suppose
\mathcal{G}(R)=\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
. Thus, for distinct elements
x,y\in R
we have
x-y\in\mathrm{U}(R)
if and only if
x+y\in\mathrm{U}(R)
.
Consequently, for any positive integer
k
we have
2k-1\in\mathrm{U}(R)
if and only if
2k+1\in\mathrm{U}(R)
. As
1
is a unit,
it follows that every odd integer is a unit element of
R
, implying that
\mathrm{char}\,\big(R/J(R)\big)=2
.
∎

Finally, we determine when the unit graph
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
is well-covered.

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
R
be a finite ring. Then, the unit graph of
R
is well-covered if and only if the unitary Cayley graph of
R
is well-covered and
\mathrm{char}\,\big(R/J(R)\big)=2
.

Proof.

If the unit graph of
R
is well-covered, Proposition
2.7
implies that
2
is a zero-divisor. Considering
Lemma
2.1
, we conclude that
R/J(R)
is also well-covered. Given that
R/J(R)
is semisimple, we can find
finite fields
F_{i}
and positive integers
n_{i}
such that
R/J(R)\simeq M_{n_{1}}(F_{1})\times\cdots\times M_{n_{r}}(F_{r})
.
Since 2 is a zero-divisor, for at least one
j
we must have
\mathrm{char}\,F_{j}=2
. Now, if for some
i
the characteristic of

F_{i}
is not equal to
2
, a contradiction arises by taking Lemma
2.8
into account. Therefore, for each

i
, we have
\mathrm{char}\,(F_{i})=2
. Consequently,
\mathrm{char}\,\big(R/J(R)\big)=2
. Moreover, according to Proposition
2.9
, the unit
graph and the unitary Cayley graph coincide. Hence, the only if implication follows. Proposition
2.9
establishes
the reverse implication, completing the proof of the theorem.
∎

Corollary 2.11
.

Let
R
be a finite ring. Then, the unit graph
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)
is well-covered if and only if either

(i)

R/J(R)
is isomorphic to one of
F
,
F\times F
, or
M_{2}(F)
for some finite field
F
with characteristic
2
;

(ii)

or
R/J(R)
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

The result follows from Theorem
2.10
and
[
22
, Theorem 3.5]
.
∎

In addition to examples provided in
[
22
, Example 3.6]
, we present several non-commutative rings (satisfying each
condition stated in the above corollary) for which the unit graph is well-covered. The idea is derived from

[
23
, Example 2.10]
, which utilizes the concept of group algebras. Recall that by letting
F
be a finite field
and
G
a finite group, a
group algebra

F[G]
, is defined as the ring of all (formal) linear combinations of
elements of
G
with coefficients in
F
with addition and multiplication defined as

\displaystyle\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g+\sum_{g\in G}b_{g}g

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in G}(a_{g}+b_{g})g

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{g\in G}b_{g}g\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in G}\left(\sum_{x\in G}a_{x}b_{x^{-1}g}\right)g,

where
a_{g},b_{g}\in F
for each
g\in G
.

Example 2.12
.

Let
F
be a finite field with characteristic
2
and
G
a finite non-abelian
2
-group. Consider the group algebra

R=F[G]
, which is a non-commutative ring. According to
[
13
, Proposition 19.11]
, we see that
R
is a local ring
and
R/J(R)\simeq F
. Moreover, by considering
R^{\prime}=R\times R
, we observe that
R^{\prime}/J(R^{\prime})\simeq R/J(R)\times R/J(R)\simeq F\times F
. Furthermore, if we assume that
S=\big(\mathbb{Z}_{2}[G]\big)^{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, then

S/J(S)\simeq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
. Also, by letting
T=M_{2}(R)
, we infer that
T/J(T)\simeq M_{2}\big(R/J(R)\big)\simeq M_{2}(F)
.
Therefore, for all the rings
R
,
R^{\prime}
,
S
and
T
, the unit graph is well-covered. The key step in this example is to
find a finite non-commutative local ring with characteristic two, so any other ring with this property would provide a
new family of examples.

Considering a graph
G
, it is evident that the family of all independent sets
\Delta(G)
of
G
forms a simplicial
complex, that is called the
independence complex
of
G
(for the definition of simplicial complex and its
properties, we refer the reader to
[
8
]
). A graph
G
is called
CM
(resp.
Gorenstein
,

shellable
) when the independence complex
\Delta(G)
is CM (resp. Gorenstein, shellable).

Corollary 2.13
.

Suppose that
R
is a finite ring. Then, the unit graph of
R
is CM if and only if it is shellable if and only if either
R
is a field
with characteristic
2
or
R\simeq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Also, this graph is Gorenstein if and only if
R\simeq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

It is well-known that a CM graph is well-covered (for instance, see
[
8
, Lemma 9.1.10]
).
So, this is an immediate consequence of Theorem
2.10
and
[
22
, Theorem 3.9 & Corollary 3.10]
.
∎

In the end, we present a problem related to our work for further research. Notions of the unit graph and the unitary
Cayley graph of a ring have been extended and generalized in various ways. One of which is as follows and is introduced for
commutative rings in
[
17
]
. Let
R
be a ring. We define the
generalized unit graph
of
R
to be the graph with
vertex set
R
in which two distinct vertices
x
and
y
are adjacent whenever there exists a unit element
u
of
R

such that
x+uy
is a unit of
R
. It is not hard to see that for a finite field
F
, the generalized unit graph of

F
is a complete graph and therefore well-covered.

Problem 2.14
.

Characterize all finite rings for which the generalized unit graph is well-covered or CM.
