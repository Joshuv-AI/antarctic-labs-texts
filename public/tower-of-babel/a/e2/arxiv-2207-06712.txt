On the Divisibility of 7-Elongated Plane Partition Diamonds by Powers of 8
James A. Sellers, Nicolas Allen Smoot

Abstract
. In 2021 da Silva, Hirschhorn, and Sellers studied a wide variety of congruences for the k k -elongated plane partition function d k ​ ( n ) d_{k}(n) by various primes. They also conjectured the existence of an infinite congruence family modulo arbitrarily high powers of 2 for the function d 7 ​ ( n ) d_{7}(n) . We prove that such a congruence family exists—indeed, for powers of 8. The proof utilizes only classical methods, i.e., integer polynomial manipulations in a single function, in contrast to all other known infinite congruence families for d k ​ ( n ) d_{k}(n) which require more modern methods to prove.

1.
Introduction

The study of
k
-elongated plane partition diamonds began as a part of the series of papers on MacMahon’s Partition Analysis by Andrews and Paule. The number of
k
-elongated partition diamonds is denoted
d_{k}(n)
, and is enumerated by the function

(1.1)

\displaystyle D_{k}(q):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{k}(n)q^{n}=\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-q^{2m})^{k}}{(1-q^{m})^{3k+1}}.

This function serves as a generalization of the unrestricted partition function
p(n)=d_{0}(n)
. Moreover,
d_{k}(n)
was developed by Andrews and Paule in
[
1
]
as an application of the techniques of Partition Analysis. In the latest paper in their series
[
2
]
, Andrews and Paule explored various congruence properties of
d_{k}(n)
, of the form of the classical congruences for
p(n)
first studied by Ramanujan
[
11
]
. In particular, they proposed
[
2
, Section 7]
an infinite family of congruences for
d_{2}(n)
modulo powers of 3, which was proved in
[
12
]
.

More recently, da Silva, Hirschhorn, and Sellers
[
6
]
have explored a large variety of congruences by running both
n
and
k
through different arithmetic progressions. Their work has revealed that
d_{k}(n)
contains an enormous diversity of divisibility properties, even by the standards of partition theory.

As a result of this latest work, combined with numerical experimentation, da Silva, Hirschhorn, and Sellers conjectured
[
6
, Section 5]
the existence of at least one infinite family of congruences over
d_{7}(n)
by powers of 2. We have confirmed this conjecture, as shown in the following result, which we prove in this paper:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n,\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
such that
3n\equiv 1\pmod{4^{\alpha}}
. Then
d_{7}(n)\equiv 0\pmod{8^{\alpha}}
.

This congruence family is associated with the classical modular curve
\mathrm{X}_{0}(8)
. The individual cases of the congruence family are enumerated by the coefficients of the modular function sequence
\mathcal{L}:=\left(L_{\alpha}\right)_{\alpha\geq 1}
defined by

(1.2)

\displaystyle L_{\alpha}:=\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-q^{m})^{26}(1-q^{4m})^{2}}{(1-q^{2m})^{13}}\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{k}(4^{\alpha}n+\lambda_{\alpha})q^{n+1},

with
q:=e^{2\pi i\tau}
,
\tau\in\mathbb{H}
, and
y=\lambda_{\alpha}
the minimum positive solution to
3y\equiv 1\pmod{4^{\alpha}}
.

The curve
\mathrm{X}_{0}(8)
has genus 0, and cusp count greater than 2. Ordinarily this would make the classical techniques difficult to apply. Indeed, the congruence family for
d_{2}
modulo powers of 3 proved in
[
12
]
requires more modern techniques, notably the localization method.

However, in the case of Theorem
1.1
we can construct an associated sequence of modular functions
L_{\alpha}
which live at a single cusp. This allows a polynomial representation of each
L_{\alpha}
in terms of a Hauptmodul.

As an example, we consider the case
\alpha=1
:

\displaystyle L_{1}

\displaystyle=\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-q^{m})^{26}(1-q^{4m})^{2}}{(1-q^{2m})^{13}}\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{7}(4n+3)q^{n+1}.

If we express
L_{1}
in terms of a certain Hauptmodul
x
(defined in (
2.5
) below) which expands to an integer-power series (to be defined in the following section), we have the following:

(1.3)

\displaystyle L_{1}

\displaystyle=2376x+2769184x^{2}+753360896x^{3}+87754260480x^{4}+5608324988928x^{5}+224018944753664x^{6}

\displaystyle+6042206699782144x^{7}+115546340691279872x^{8}+1616547968486211584x^{9}

\displaystyle+16870983657286795264x^{10}+132703559201308278784x^{11}+788474037948865576960x^{12}

\displaystyle+3517455424164433231872x^{13}+11593058074386073911296x^{14}+27374968205384974598144x^{15}

\displaystyle+43792570430986475536384x^{16}+42501298345826806923264x^{17}+18889465931478580854784x^{18}.

A quick examination of the coefficients of (
1.3
) demonstrates that

\displaystyle L_{1}

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{8}.

Using (
1.3
) in combination with some techniques in the theory of modular functions, we prove Theorem
1.1
as a corollary of the following theorem:

Theorem 1.2
.

For all
\alpha\geq 1
,

(1.4)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8^{\alpha}}L_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}[x].

The remainder of this paper is as follows: In Section

to

. We then define our Hauptmodul

In Section

In Section

2.
Setup

For the sake of convenience, we define the standard
q
-Pochhammer symbol by

\displaystyle(q^{a};q^{b})_{\infty}:=\prod_{m=0}^{\infty}\left(1-q^{a+bm}\right).

Notice that with this notation, we have

\displaystyle L_{\alpha}:=\frac{(q;q)^{26}_{\infty}(q^{4};q^{4})^{2}_{\infty}}{(q^{2};q^{2})^{13}_{\infty}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{k}(4^{\alpha}n+\lambda_{\alpha})q^{n+1}.

It is a straightforward process to prove that each
L_{\alpha}
as defined in (
1.2
) is a modular function over the congruence subgroup
\Gamma_{0}(8)
, as we will show in Section

. It is only slightly more difficult to form the necessary linear operator that permits us to construct

(2.1)

\displaystyle U\left(f\right):=U_{4}\left(\mathcal{A}\cdot f\right),

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}:=q\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}^{13}(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}^{24}(q^{16};q^{16})_{\infty}^{2}}{(q;q)_{\infty}^{26}(q^{8};q^{8})_{\infty}^{13}}.

We can quickly verify that
U
is a linear operator.

Lemma 2.1
.

For all
\alpha\geq 1
,
U\left(L_{\alpha}\right)=L_{\alpha+1}
.

Proof.

We use the important property that, for any power series
f(q)=\sum_{n\geq N}a(n)q^{n}
,
g(q)=\sum_{n\geq M}b(n)q^{n}

(2.3)

\displaystyle U_{4}\left(f(q^{4})g(q)\right)=f(q)\cdot U_{4}\left(g(q)\right).

Now, applying
U
to
L_{\alpha}
, we have

\displaystyle U\left(L_{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=U_{4}\left(\mathcal{A}\cdot L_{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=U_{4}\left(\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}^{13}(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}^{24}(q^{16};q^{16})_{\infty}^{2}}{(q;q)_{\infty}^{26}(q^{8};q^{8})_{\infty}^{13}}\cdot\frac{(q;q)^{26}_{\infty}(q^{4};q^{4})^{2}_{\infty}}{(q^{2};q^{2})^{13}_{\infty}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{k}\left(4^{\alpha}n+\lambda_{\alpha}\right)q^{n+2}\right)

\displaystyle=U_{4}\left(\frac{(q^{4};q^{4})^{26}_{\infty}(q^{16};q^{16})^{2}_{\infty}}{(q^{8};q^{8})^{13}_{\infty}}\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{k}\left(4^{\alpha}n+\lambda_{\alpha}\right)q^{n+2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(q;q)^{26}_{\infty}(q^{4};q^{4})^{2}_{\infty}}{(q^{2};q^{2})^{13}_{\infty}}U_{4}\left(\sum_{n\geq 2}d_{k}\left(4^{\alpha}(n-2)+\lambda_{\alpha}\right)q^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(q;q)^{26}_{\infty}(q^{4};q^{4})^{2}_{\infty}}{(q^{2};q^{2})^{13}_{\infty}}\sum_{4n\geq 2}d_{k}\left(4^{\alpha}(4n-2)+\lambda_{\alpha}\right)q^{n}

Notice that
4n\geq 2
is equivalent to
n\geq 1
. Moreover, we can show that

(2.4)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\alpha}=\frac{4^{\alpha}\cdot 2+1}{3}.

We therefore have

\displaystyle U\left(L_{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(q;q)^{26}_{\infty}(q^{4};q^{4})^{2}_{\infty}}{(q^{2};q^{2})^{13}_{\infty}}\sum_{n\geq 1}d_{k}\left(4^{\alpha+1}n-2\cdot 4^{\alpha}+\frac{4^{\alpha}\cdot 2+1}{3}\right)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{(q;q)^{26}_{\infty}(q^{4};q^{4})^{2}_{\infty}}{(q^{2};q^{2})^{13}_{\infty}}\sum_{n\geq 1}d_{k}\left(4^{\alpha+1}n+\frac{4^{\alpha}\cdot 2-3\cdot 4^{\alpha}\cdot 2+1}{3}\right)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{(q;q)^{26}_{\infty}(q^{4};q^{4})^{2}_{\infty}}{(q^{2};q^{2})^{13}_{\infty}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{k}\left(4^{\alpha+1}n+4^{\alpha+1}+\frac{4^{\alpha}\cdot 2-3\cdot 4^{\alpha}\cdot 2+1}{3}\right)q^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(q;q)^{26}_{\infty}(q^{4};q^{4})^{2}_{\infty}}{(q^{2};q^{2})^{13}_{\infty}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{k}\left(4^{\alpha+1}n+\frac{4^{\alpha+1}\cdot 2+1}{3}\right)q^{n+1}

\displaystyle=L_{\alpha+1}.

∎

\displaystyle x:=q\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})^{2}_{\infty}(q^{8};q^{8})^{4}_{\infty}}{(q;q)^{4}_{\infty}(q^{4};q^{4})^{2}_{\infty}}.

We can verify that

\displaystyle\mathrm{ord}_{\infty}(x)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\mathrm{ord}_{1/4}(x)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\mathrm{ord}_{0}(x)

We can also compute a lower bound for the orders of

Theorem 2.2

Define

\displaystyle a_{0}(\tau)

\displaystyle=-(x+20x^{2}+128x^{3}+256x^{4})

\displaystyle a_{1}(\tau)

\displaystyle=-(16x+320x^{2}+2048x^{3}+4096x^{4})

\displaystyle a_{2}(\tau)

\displaystyle x^{4}+\sum_{j=0}^{3}a_{j}(4\tau)x^{j}=0.

\displaystyle a_{j}(\tau)=\sum_{k=1}^{4}a(j,k)2^{\phi(j,k)}x^{k},

3.
Main Lemma

We have seen that for each
L_{\alpha}
we can obtain
L_{\alpha+1}
by application of our operator
U
. Because we intend to represent each
L_{\alpha}
in terms of
x
, we next need to study how
U
affects powers of
x
. With this in mind, we define the function:

(3.1)

\displaystyle\pi(n,r)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\left\lfloor\frac{8r-5}{4}\right\rfloor+3,&n=1,\\
\left\lfloor\frac{8r-5}{4}\right\rfloor+1,&n=2,\\
\left\lfloor\frac{4r-n-1}{2}\right\rfloor,&n\geq 3.\end{cases}

This will serve as a lower bound to the 2-adic valuation of the coefficients of
x^{r}
in
U(x^{n})
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For all
n\geq 1
, there exists a discrete array
h(n,r)
such that

(3.2)

\displaystyle U\left(x^{n}\right)=\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil(n+1)/4\right\rceil}h(n,r)\cdot 2^{\pi(n,r)}x^{r}.

Proof.

We compute
U\left(x^{n}\right)
for
0\leq n\leq 3
directly, and give the polynomials in the Appendix below.

Let us take some
n\geq 4
, and suppose that (
3.2
) applies to
U(x^{m})
for
0\leq m\leq n
. We begin by rearranging (
2.6
), and then multiplying by
\mathcal{A}\cdot x^{n-4}
:

\displaystyle x^{4}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=0}^{3}a_{j}(4\tau)x^{j},

\displaystyle x^{n}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=0}^{3}a_{j}(4\tau)x^{n+j-4},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\cdot x^{n}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=0}^{3}a_{j}(4\tau)\mathcal{A}\cdot x^{n+j-4}.

We now apply our
U
operator from (
2.1
) to both sides:

\displaystyle U_{4}\left(\mathcal{A}\cdot x^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=0}^{3}a_{j}(\tau)U_{4}\left(\mathcal{A}\cdot x^{n+j-4}\right),

\displaystyle U\left(x^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=0}^{3}a_{j}(\tau)U\left(x^{n+j-4}\right).

By hypothesis, (
3.2
) applies for
U\left(x^{n+j-4}\right)
for
0\leq j\leq 3
. We therefore have

\displaystyle U\left(x^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=0}^{3}a_{j}(\tau)\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil(n+j-3)/4\right\rceil}h(n+j-4,r)\cdot 2^{\pi(n+j-4,r)}x^{r}.

Remembering (
2.7
)-(
2.8
), we have

\displaystyle U\left(x^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=0}^{3}\sum_{k=1}^{4}a(j,k)2^{\phi(j,k)}x^{k}\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil(n+j-3)/4\right\rceil}h(n+j-4,r)\cdot 2^{\pi(n+j-4,r)}x^{r}

\displaystyle U\left(x^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq j\leq 3\\
1\leq k\leq 4\\
r\geq\left\lceil(n+j-3)/4\right\rceil\end{subarray}}a(j,k)h(n+j-4,r)\cdot 2^{\pi(n+j-4,r)+\phi(j,k)}x^{r+k}.

If we relabel our powers of
x
, we can rewrite our relation as

(3.3)

\displaystyle U\left(x^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq j\leq 3\\
1\leq k\leq 4\\
r-k\geq\left\lceil(n+j-3)/4\right\rceil\end{subarray}}a(j,k)h(n+j-4,r-k)\cdot 2^{\pi(n+j-4,r-k)+\phi(j,k)}x^{r}.

Notice that

(3.4)

\displaystyle r\geq\left\lceil(n+j-3)/4\right\rceil+k\geq\left\lceil(n-3)/4\right\rceil+1=\left\lceil(n+1)/4\right\rceil.

We now verify that
\pi(n+j-4,r-k)+\phi(j,k)\geq\pi(n,r)
. For
j=0
,

\displaystyle\pi(n-4,r-1)+\phi(1,1)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{4r-n-1}{2}\right\rfloor=\pi(n,r),

\displaystyle\pi(n-4,r-2)+\phi(1,2)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{4r-n-5}{2}\right\rfloor+2=\left\lfloor\frac{4r-n-1}{2}\right\rfloor=\pi(n,r),

\displaystyle\pi(n-4,r-3)+\phi(1,3)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{4r-n-9}{2}\right\rfloor+7=\left\lfloor\frac{4r-n+5}{2}\right\rfloor\geq\pi(n,r)

\displaystyle\pi(n-4,r-4)+\phi(1,4)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{4r-n-13}{2}\right\rfloor+8=\left\lfloor\frac{4r-n+3}{2}\right\rfloor\geq\pi(n,r).

For
1\leq j\leq 3
,

\displaystyle\pi(n+j-4,r-k)+\phi(j,k)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{4r-4k-n-j+3}{2}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{4k+2j+1}{2}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{4r-n+j+3}{2}\right\rfloor\geq\pi(n,r).

For a given
r\geq 0
, we can now define the value of our discrete array
h(n,r)
as the coefficient of
x^{r}
in (
3.3
) after dividing out by

. We only need to know that

exists for

. This can be shown by computing the four initial cases

directly for

, confirming that it is a polynomial in

is divisible by

. We provide the explicit forms of these initial cases in the Appendix.

4.
Main Theorem

In order to prove Theorem
1.2
, we will show that each
L_{\alpha}
is a polynomial in
x
in which each coefficient of
x
is divisible by
8^{\alpha}
. We already understand that this is true for
\alpha=1
, by (
1.3
). We therefore need to construct a subspace of integer polynomials in
x
which includes
L_{1}
, and which is stable upon application of the operator
U
. We define a subspace of integer polynomials in
x
with a certain lower bound on the 2-adic valuation of the coefficients of
x
. For

\displaystyle\theta(n)

\displaystyle:=\left\lfloor\frac{8n-5}{4}\right\rfloor,

define the space

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}:=

\displaystyle\left\{\sum_{n\geq 1}s(n)\cdot 2^{\theta(n)}\cdot x^{n}\right\},

in which
s(n)
is any discrete integer-valued function.

Theorem 4.1
.

For all
f\in\mathcal{V}
,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}U\left(f\right)\in\mathcal{V}.

With this result we prove Theorem
1.2
.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{V}
. Then

(4.3)

\displaystyle f=\sum_{n\geq 1}s(n)\cdot 2^{\theta(n)}\cdot x^{n}.

Applying
U
, we have

(4.4)

\displaystyle U\left(f\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 1}s(n)\cdot 2^{\theta(n)}U\left(x^{n}\right)

(4.5)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 1}\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil(n+1)/4\right\rceil}s(n)h(n,r)\cdot 2^{\pi(n,r)+\theta(n)}x^{r}

(4.6)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\geq 1}\sum_{n\geq 1}s(n)h(n,r)\cdot 2^{\pi(n,r)+\theta(n)}x^{r}.

We want to show that

(4.7)

\displaystyle\pi(n,r)+\theta(n)\geq\theta(r)+3

for all
n\geq 1
,
r\geq\left\lceil(n+1)/4\right\rceil
. In that case, it will be demonstrated that
U\left(f\right)
remains in
\mathcal{V}
, and that each factor gains a power of 8, whereupon we have Theorem
4.1
.

First examining the cases for
n=1,2
, we have

(4.8)

\displaystyle\pi(1,r)+\theta(1)

\displaystyle=\left(\left\lfloor\frac{8r-5}{4}\right\rfloor+3\right)+0=\theta(r)+3,

(4.9)

\displaystyle\pi(2,r)+\theta(2)

\displaystyle=\left(\left\lfloor\frac{8r-5}{4}\right\rfloor+1\right)+2=\theta(r)+3.

For
n\geq 3
we have

(4.10)

\displaystyle\pi(n,r)+\theta(n)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{4r-n-1}{2}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{8n-5}{4}\right\rfloor

(4.11)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{8r-2n-2}{4}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{8n-5}{4}\right\rfloor

(4.12)

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{8r+6n-10}{4}\right\rfloor

(4.13)

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{8r-5}{4}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{6n-5}{4}\right\rfloor

(4.14)

\displaystyle\geq\theta(r)+3.

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Notice that by (
1.3
),
L_{1}\equiv 0\pmod{8}
, and
\frac{1}{8}L_{1}\in\mathcal{V}
. Suppose that for some fixed

,

, and that
\frac{1}{8^{\alpha}}L_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{V}
. Then

(4.15)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}\cdot U\left(\frac{1}{8^{\alpha}}L_{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8^{\alpha+1}}\cdot U\left(L_{\alpha}\right)

(4.16)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8^{\alpha+1}}\cdot L_{\alpha+1}\in\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[x],

whence
L_{\alpha+1}\equiv 0\pmod{8^{\alpha+1}}
.
∎

From this, Theorem
1.1
immediately follows.

6.
Additional Congruence Results

The natural question that follows is whether other infinite congruence families modulo powers of 2 exist for
d_{k}
over other values of
k
. In particular, we may use a very similar prefactor setup and
U
operator to examine possible congruences for the more general

(6.1)

\displaystyle D_{8k+7}(q):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{8k+7}(n)q^{n}=\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-q^{2m})^{8k+7}}{(1-q^{m})^{24k+22}}.

It is tempting to believe that more general congruence families exist in this form, and experimental evidence appears at first sight to favor the existence of other congruence families. However, we have thus far carefully searched for such a congruence family in vain. Certainly, individual congruences exist for
d_{k}(n)
over large powers of any prime
\ell
, provided that one varies
k
. For example, Baruah, Das, and Talukdar
[
5
]
have proved that if

(6.2)

\displaystyle d_{k}(2^{M}n+r)\equiv 0\pmod{2^{N}},

then for any
j\geq 0
,

(6.3)

\displaystyle d_{k+2^{M+N-1}j}(2^{M}n+r)\equiv 0\pmod{2^{N}}.

We obviously have an infinite family corresponding to (
6.2
). However, notice that this parallel set of congruences (
6.3
) does not necessarily correspond to an infinite family for a fixed
k
; as the power of 2 increases, so too does the index
k_{0}
of
d_{k_{0}}(n)
.

On the other hand, such a proliferance of individual congruences should tempt one to search for other congruence families for different but fixed
k
that somehow run parallel to those of the family proved in this article. Nevertheless, we have been unable to find such a family, despite a long search.

Indeed, we have reason to believe that such families are actually quite rare, and indeed outright nonexistent, for most values of
k
.

We are in the process of conducting a detailed investigation of when such families are able to occur; however, it certainly demands a much more thorough approach than is appropriate for the remainder of this paper.

We also note that some other congruence families have been found for
d_{k}(n)
modulo powers of 3
[
2
]
,
[
12
]
, and powers of 5
[
4
]
. Congruence families for other primes appear very likely. In both of the latter cases, the classical techniques (that is, manipulating the associated functions
L_{\alpha}
as polynomials in a given Hauptmodul) are inadequate, and the more recent techniques of localization appear necessary. Indeed, part of what makes Theorem
1.1

We expect that a more complete understanding of the circumstances in which an infinite family of congruences modulo powers of any prime
\ell

(and equally important, the circumstances in which such a family does
not
exist) will be possible very soon.
