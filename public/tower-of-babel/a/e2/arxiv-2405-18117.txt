Acoustic wake in an isothermal profile: dynamical friction and gravitational wave emission
Gali Eytan, Vincent Desjacques, Robin Buehler

Abstract
We consider the motion of a circularly-moving perturber in a self-gravitating, collisional system with spherically symmetric density profile. We concentrate on the singular isothermal sphere which, despite its pathological features, admits a simple polarization function in linear response theory. This allows us to solve for the acoustic wake trailing the perturber and the resulting dynamical friction, in the limit where the self-gravity of the response can be ignored. In steady-state and for subsonic velocities v p < c s v_{p}<c_{s} , the dynamical friction torque F φ ∝ v p 3 F_{\varphi}\propto v_{p}^{3} is suppressed for perturbers orbiting in an isothermal sphere relative to the infinite, homogeneous medium expectation F φ ∝ v p F_{\varphi}\propto v_{p} . For highly supersonic motions, both expectations agree and are consistent with a local approximation to the gravitational torque. At fixed resolution (a given Coulomb logarithm), the response of the system is maximal for Mach numbers near the constant circular velocity of the singular isothermal profile. This resonance maximizes the gravitational wave (GW) emission produced by the trailing acoustic wake. For an inspiral around a massive black hole of mass 10 6 ​ M ⊙ 10^{6}M_{\odot} located at the center of a (truncated) isothermal sphere, this GW signal could be comparable to the vacuum GW emission of the black hole binary at sub-nanohertz frequencies when the small black hole enters the Bondi sphere of the massive one. The exact magnitude of this effect depends on departures from hydrostatic equilibrium and on the viscosity present in any realistic astrophysical fluid, which are not included in our simplified description.

II
Theory

Consider a spherically symmetric, inviscid gaseous medium in hydrostatic equilibrium of density
\rho_{0}(r)
and (adiabatic) sound speed
c_{0}(r)
. The equation of state for the pressure
P_{0}(r)
is the polytrope
P_{0}=K\rho_{0}^{\gamma}
where
K
is a constant and
\gamma
is the adiabatic index. The potential of this self-gravitating medium is
\phi_{0}(r)
.

We are interested in the li near response of this gaseous system to an external, pointlike perturbation of mass
M
moving on a fixed circular orbit of radius
a
and angular velocity
\Omega>0
, in the
x-y
plane.

Let
\textbf{r}_{p}=\textbf{r}_{p}(t)
and
{\textbf{r}_{p}}^{\prime}=\textbf{r}_{p}(t^{\prime})
be the position of the perturber at ”present-day” time
t
and ”retarded” time
t^{\prime}
, respectively, relative to the center of the density profile.
We have
\textbf{r}_{p}(t)=a(\cos\Omega t\,\mathrm{\hat{\bf{x}}}+\sin\Omega t\,\mathrm{\hat{\bf{y}}})
. The Mach number is
\mathcal{M}\equiv v_{p}/c_{s}
, where
v_{p}=\Omega a
is the perturber’s circular velocity.
The point mass has a density
\rho_{p}(\textbf{r},t)=Mh(t)\delta^{D}(\textbf{r}-\textbf{r}_{p}(t))
, where the time-dependent function
h(t)
is unity (zero) when the perturber is turned on (off).

II.1
Methodology

II.1.1
Sound wave equation

Adding the perturber to the system leads to linearized continuity and momentum conservation equations involving perturbations in the gas density
\delta\rho
and pressure
\delta P=c_{0}^{2}\delta\rho
as well as the perturber’s gravitational potential
\phi_{p}
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\delta\rho

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\big(\rho_{0}\delta\textbf{v}\big)

(1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\big(\rho_{0}\delta\textbf{v}\big)

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\delta P-\rho_{0}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\delta\phi-\delta\rho\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi_{0}-\rho_{0}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi_{p}\;.

(2)

These equations are supplemented by the perturbed Poisson equations

\nabla^{2}\delta\phi=4\pi G\delta\rho\quad\mbox{and}\quad\nabla^{2}\delta\phi_{P}=4\pi G\delta\rho_{P}\;,

(3)

where
\delta\phi
is the perturbation to the potential of the gaseous sphere. Using the hydrostatic equilibrium condition
(1/\rho_{0})\boldsymbol{\nabla}P_{0}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi_{0}=0
, they can be combined to yield a perturbation equation of the form

\ddot{\delta}\rho-\nabla^{2}\delta P-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\delta\rho-4\pi G\rho_{0}\delta\rho\\
=\boldsymbol{\nabla}\big(\rho_{0}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi_{p}\big)+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\big(\rho_{0}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\delta\phi\big)\;.

(4)

The right-hand side involves the total perturbation in the gravitational potential, which is the sum of the perturber’s potential
\phi_{P}
and the perturbation
\delta\phi
induced by the trailing density wake. For infinite, homogeneous systems, the right-hand side is simply the total density perturbation
\rho_{P}+\delta\rho
. For realistic, finite and inhomogeneous systems, there is an additional ”non-local” term
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\rho_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}(\phi_{p}+\delta\phi)
which significantly complicates the analysis. Note also that the contribution of the unperturbed gravitational field
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi_{0}
is neglected in the treatment of the infinite, homogeneous case following the ”Jeans swindle”. Eq. (
4
) describes a driven harmonic oscillator, but we shall refer to it as the ”sound wave” equation for simplicity.

For the polytropes considered here, the sound wave equation becomes

\ddot{\delta}\rho-\nabla^{2}(c_{0}^{2}\delta\rho)-4\pi G\rho_{0}\delta\rho-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\delta\rho=S(\rho_{0},\phi_{p},\delta\phi)

(5)

where
S(\rho_{0},\phi_{p},\delta\phi)=\boldsymbol{\nabla}\big(\rho_{0}\boldsymbol{\nabla}(\phi_{p}+\delta\phi)\big)
is the source term. We now apply Green’s method and look for the Green’s function
G(\textbf{r},\textbf{r}^{\prime},t,t^{\prime})
which solves

\left(\partial_{t}^{2}-\nabla^{2}c_{0}^{2}-4\pi G\rho_{0}-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\right)G=\delta^{D}(t-t^{\prime})\delta^{D}(\textbf{r}-\textbf{r}^{\prime})

(6)

Here,
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}
involves only the radial part owing to the spherical symmetry of the unperturbed profile, and it is understood that
\nabla^{2}
acts on the product
c_{0}^{2}G
. Physically,
G
is the polarization function
[
8
, usually denoted by
P
as in]
since it relates the wake overdensity
\delta\rho
to the total perturbation.

The polarization function
G
depends on zeroth order quantities and, therefore, inherits the stationarity (invariance under time-translations) and isotropy (invariance under rotations) of the hydrostatic equilibrium. Therefore,
G
must be of the form

G(\textbf{r},\textbf{r}^{\prime},t,t^{\prime})=\sum_{\ell m}\int_{\omega}g_{\omega\ell}(r,r^{\prime})e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}Y_{\ell}^{m}(\mathrm{\hat{\bf{r}}})Y_{\ell}^{m*}(\mathrm{\hat{\bf{r}}}^{\prime})\;.

(7)

Here,
g_{\omega\ell}(r,r^{\prime})
are the radial components of the polarization function,
Y_{\ell}^{m}(\mathrm{\hat{\bf{r}}})
are spherical harmonics,
and the shorthand for the integral over the (circular) frequency of the sound waves
\omega
is
\int_{\omega}\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!d\omega
. Substituting this ansatz into Eq. (
6
) and using the representations of the Dirac distributions
\delta^{D}(t-t^{\prime})
and
\delta^{D}(\textbf{r}-\textbf{r}^{\prime})
in terms of plane waves and spherical harmonics,

\displaystyle\delta^{D}(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{\omega}e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}

(8)

\displaystyle\delta^{D}(\textbf{r}-\textbf{r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\delta^{D}(r-r^{\prime})\sum_{\ell m}Y_{\ell}^{m*}(\mathrm{\hat{\bf{r}}})Y_{\ell}^{m}(\mathrm{\hat{\bf{r}}}^{\prime})\;,

(9)

the radial Green’s equation can be recast into

-r\partial_{r}^{2}\Big(r\,c_{0}^{2}\,g_{\omega\ell}\Big)-r^{2}\,\partial_{r}\phi_{0}\,\partial_{r}g_{\omega\ell}-\Big[\Big(\omega^{2}+c_{0}^{2}k_{J}^{2}\Big)r^{2}\\
-\ell\big(\ell+1\big)c_{0}^{2}\Big]g_{\omega\ell}=\delta^{D}(r-r^{\prime})

(10)

where
k_{J}^{2}(r)=4\pi G\rho_{0}(r)/c_{0}^{2}(r)
is a (position dependent) Jeans wavenumber.

II.1.2
Acoustic wake and DF force

Once the polarization function is known, the (over)density
\delta\rho(\textbf{r},t)
of the acoustic wake is determined by the (integro-differential) equation

\delta\rho(\textbf{r},t)=\int\!\!d^{3}r^{\prime}\,\int\!\!dt^{\prime}\,G(\textbf{r},\textbf{r}^{\prime},t-t^{\prime})\,S(\rho_{0}^{\prime},\phi_{P}^{\prime},\delta\phi^{\prime})

(11)

where the primes signify that the source term is evaluated at the coordinates
(\textbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})
. When the self-gravity of the density wake can be neglected, we have
S\approx\boldsymbol{\nabla}(\rho_{0}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi_{P})
and the polarization function is equal to the response function of the system:

\delta\rho(\textbf{r},t)\approx\int\!\!d^{3}r^{\prime}\,\int\!\!dt^{\prime}\,G(\textbf{r},\textbf{r}^{\prime},t-t^{\prime})\,\boldsymbol{\nabla}\big(\rho_{0}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi_{P}^{\prime}\big)\;.

(12)

For the pointlike perturber considered here, the solution to Eq. (
12
) in terms of the density multipoles
\delta\rho_{\ell}^{m}(r,t)
reads

\delta\rho_{\ell}^{m}(r,t)=4\pi GM\int_{\omega}\int\!\!dt^{\prime}\,e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}\,h(t^{\prime})\\
\times Y_{\ell}^{m*}(\mathrm{\hat{\bf{r}}}_{p}^{\prime})A_{\omega\ell}(r,r_{p}^{\prime})

(13)

where the function
A_{\omega\ell}(r,r_{p}^{\prime})
is given by

\displaystyle A_{\omega\ell}(r,r_{p}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}(r_{p}^{\prime})\,g_{\omega\ell}(r,r_{p}^{\prime})

(14)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\int\!\!dss^{2}\,g_{\omega\ell}(r,s)\partial_{s}\rho_{0}(s)\partial_{s}\!\!\left(\frac{s_{<}^{\ell}}{s_{>}^{\ell+1}}\right)

Here,
r_{p}^{\prime}=|\textbf{r}_{p}^{\prime}|
and
\mathrm{\hat{\bf{r}}}_{p}^{\prime}
is the unit vector along
\textbf{r}_{p}^{\prime}
. Furthermore,
s_{<}={\rm min}(s,r_{p}^{\prime})
and
s_{>}={\rm max}(s,r_{p}^{\prime})
. The acoustic wake pattern contributes a gravitational potential

\delta\phi(\textbf{r},t)=-G\int\!\!d^{3}r^{\prime}\frac{\delta\rho(\textbf{r}^{\prime},t)}{|\textbf{r}-\textbf{r}^{\prime}|}\;,

(15)

the gradient of which gives the DF force experienced by the perturber. Using the multipole expansion of
|\textbf{r}-\textbf{r}^{\prime}|^{-1}
, the multipoles
\delta\phi_{\ell}^{m}(r,t)
of this self-gravity perturbation can be recast into the form

\displaystyle\delta\phi_{\ell}^{m}(r,t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{4\pi G}{2\ell+1}\int\!\!ds\,s^{2}\,\frac{s_{<}^{\ell}}{s_{>}^{\ell+1}}\,\delta\rho_{\ell}^{m}(s,t)

(16)

where
s_{<}={\rm min}(s,r)
and
s_{>}={\rm max}(s,r)
.

The perturbation
\delta\phi
generates a gravitational drag force (DF) on the perturber given by

{\bf F}_{\text{DF}}(t)=F_{r}(t)\,\mathrm{\hat{\bf{r}}}(t)+F_{\varphi}(t)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(t)

(17)

where
\mathrm{\hat{\bf{r}}}(t)=\textbf{r}_{p}(t)/|\textbf{r}_{p}(t)|
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(t)
are unit vectors in the radial and tangential direction. By definition, the azimuthal unit vector
\hat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(t)
is oriented counterclockwise.

The tangential component
F_{\varphi}(t)
of the DF force at time
t
can be obtained from the torque
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(t)
exerted by the acoustic wake on the perturber:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(t)

\displaystyle=-M\textbf{r}_{p}\times\boldsymbol{\nabla}\delta\phi(\textbf{r}_{p},t)

(18)

\displaystyle=-iML_{z}\delta\phi(\textbf{r}_{p},t)\,\mathrm{\hat{\bf{z}}}

\displaystyle\equiv aF_{\varphi}(t)\,\mathrm{\hat{\bf{z}}}\;,

(19)

where
L_{z}\equiv-i\partial/\partial\varphi
is the
z
-component of the angular momentum operator
{\bf L}=-i\,\textbf{r}\times\boldsymbol{\nabla}
. Therefore,

F_{\varphi}(t)=-i\frac{M}{a}L_{z}\delta\phi(\textbf{r}_{p},t)

(20)

which, in essence, is the
[
39
]
formula for the DF torque.
We shall also consider the radial component given by

F_{r}(t)=-M\partial_{r}\delta\phi(\textbf{r}_{p},t)\;,

(21)

although it is dynamically irrelevant for circular orbits.
These expressions are completely general insofar it applies to any spherically symmetric medium and perturber motion.

II.2
Consistency check: the homogeneous medium

The acoustic wake pattern and DF force can be computed easily in the case of steady-state circular motion in an infinite homogeneous background of density
\bar{\rho}
. This case has been explored in e.g.
[
34
,
35
,
19
]

[see
12
,
45
, for eccentric motion]
. Ignoring the self-gravity of the medium (which leads to a frequency shift), the radial Green’s equation simplifies to

-\big(r^{2}g_{\omega\ell}^{\prime}\big)^{\prime}-\left[\left(\frac{\omega}{c_{s}}\right)^{2}r^{2}-l\big(l+1)\right]g_{\omega\ell}\\
=\frac{\delta^{D}(r-r^{\prime})}{c_{s}^{2}}

(22)

which is of the Sturm-Liouville type.
For purely outgoing wave boundary conditions at infinity and a finite response in the limit
r\to 0
, the radial polarization function takes the form

g_{\omega\ell}(r,r^{\prime})=\frac{i}{c_{s}^{2}}\bigg(\frac{\omega}{c_{s}}\bigg)\,j_{\ell}\!\bigg(\frac{\omega}{c_{s}}r_{<}\bigg)\,h_{\ell}^{(1)}\!\bigg(\frac{\omega}{c_{s}}r_{>}\bigg)

(23)

for
\omega\neq 0
.
Here,
r_{<}={\rm min}(r,r^{\prime})
and
r_{>}={\rm max}(r,r^{\prime})
, whereas
j_{\ell}(x)
and
h_{\ell}^{(1)}(x)
are spherical Bessel and Hankel functions, respectively.
When
\omega=0
, the solution is a powerlaw. We find

g_{0\ell}(r,r^{\prime})=\frac{c_{s}^{-2}}{(2\ell+1)}\frac{r_{<}^{\ell}}{r_{>}^{\ell+1}}

(24)

upon requiring that
g_{0\ell}
decays to zero at infinity.

On setting
h(t^{\prime})\equiv~1
and
\rho(\textbf{r}_{p}^{\prime})\equiv\bar{\rho}
in Eq. (
13
), the integral over
t^{\prime}
simplifies to

\int\!\!dt^{\prime}\,e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}e^{-im\Omega t^{\prime}}=2\pi\,\delta^{D}(\omega-m\Omega)\,e^{-im\Omega t}\;,

where the second complex exponential arises from

Y_{\ell}^{m*}(\mathrm{\hat{\bf{r}}}_{p}^{\prime})=Y_{\ell}^{m*}\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2},0\right)e^{-im\Omega t^{\prime}}\;.

(25)

Note that
Y_{\ell}^{0}(\pi/2,0)=0
for
\ell
even, which reflects the planar symmetry of the response, i.e.
\delta\rho
is invariant under a reflexion along the
z
axis.
Inserting this result into the expression of
\delta\rho
and taking advantage of the fact that
r_{p}=r_{p}^{\prime}=a
for circular motion, the multipole expansion of the acoustic wake density leads to

\delta\rho_{\ell}^{m}(r,t)=4\pi GM\,\bar{\rho}\,g_{m\Omega,\ell}(r,a)\,Y_{\ell}^{m*}(\mathrm{\hat{\bf{r}}}_{p})

(26)

Observe the appearance of all the higher harmonics
m\Omega
of the fundamental (orbital) frequency. Furthermore,
g_{m\Omega,\ell}(r,a)^{*}=g_{-m\Omega,\ell}(r,a)
, which ensures that
\delta\rho
is real.

The monopole (
\ell=0
) contribution to
\delta\rho
is independent of the Mach number. It reflects the accretion onto the perturber’s orbit and, in steady-state, leads to an overdensity

\delta\rho_{\ell=0}(\textbf{r},t)\equiv\delta\rho_{0}^{0}(r,t)\,Y_{0}^{0}(\mathrm{\hat{\bf{r}}})=\frac{GM}{c_{s}^{2}\,r_{>}}\bar{\rho}

(27)

where
r_{>}={\rm max}(r,a)
. Furthermore, while it does not contribute to the tangential DF force
F_{\varphi}(t)
, it yields a force in the radial direction which, as we will see shortly, is the dominant contribution to
F_{r}(t)
in the limit
\mathcal{M}\to\infty
.

The multipole coefficients of the perturbation
\delta\phi
to the medium’s gravitational potential follows from Eq. (
15
) and read

\delta\phi_{\ell}^{m}(r,t)=-\big(4\pi G\big)^{2}M\bar{\rho}\,\big(2\ell+1\big)^{-1}\,Y_{\ell}^{m*}(\mathrm{\hat{\bf{r}}}_{p})\\
\times\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!ds\,s^{2}\frac{s_{<}^{\ell}}{s_{>}^{\ell+1}}\,g_{m\Omega,\ell}(s,a)\;,

(28)

where
s_{<}={\rm min}(s,r)
and
s_{>}={\rm max}(s,r)
.

Starting from the relation Eq. (
20
) and using the property
L_{z}Y_{\ell}^{m}=mY_{\ell}^{m}
, and the relations

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\!\!du\,u^{\ell+2}\,j_{\ell}(xu)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x}\,j_{\ell+1}(x)

(29)

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}\!\!du\,u^{-\ell+1}\,h_{\ell}^{(1)}\!(xu)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x}\,h_{\ell-1}^{(1)}\!(x)

which emerge after splitting the integral over
s
into the intervals
[0,a]
and
[a,\infty[
, the tangential DF force can be recast into

F_{\varphi}(t)=-4\pi\left(\frac{GM}{\Omega a}\right)^{2}\bar{\rho}\,I_{\varphi}(\mathcal{M})

(30)

where

\displaystyle I_{\varphi}(\mathcal{M})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m\neq 0}m\,\frac{(\ell-m)!}{(\ell+m)!}\,\big\lvert P_{\ell}^{m}(0)\big\lvert^{2}

(31)

\displaystyle\quad\times\Big[j_{\ell+1}(m\mathcal{M})h_{\ell}^{(1)}\!(m\mathcal{M})+j_{\ell}(m\mathcal{M})h_{\ell-1}^{(1)}\!(m\mathcal{M})\Big]

is the tangential friction coefficient. The first (second) term in the square brackets of Eq. (
31
) is the contribution from the acoustic wake inside (outside) the orbital radius.
The monopole does not contribute to
I_{\varphi}(\mathcal{M})
due to Newton’s shell (or Birkhoff’s) theorem. The dipole (
\ell=1
) term dominates the friction for
\mathcal{M}\ll 1
. In addition, for
\ell\geq 1
, all the contributions with
m=0
vanish because
F_{\varphi}
cannot be sourced by a density perturbation with azimuthal symmetry. Finally, in the limit
\mathcal{M}\to 0
, the tangential friction scales as
I_{\varphi}(\mathcal{M})\sim\mathcal{M}^{3}/3
, which implies that the tangential drag is proportional to the perturber’s velocity for

.

The friction coefficient

is displayed in the left panel of Fig.

as indicated by the triangles. The agreement is excellent as was already demonstrated in

. For supersonic motion,

is affected by a short distance Coulomb divergence, which is regularized by truncating the series expansion Eq. (

\ell_{\text{max}}
. Choosing
\ell_{\text{max}}=12

Similarly, the radial DF force can be expressed as

F_{r}(t)=-4\pi\left(\frac{GM}{\Omega a}\right)^{2}\bar{\rho}\,I_{r}(\mathcal{M})

(32)

where

\displaystyle I_{r}(\mathcal{M})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{M}^{2}}{3}+i\mathcal{M}^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m}\frac{(\ell-m)!}{(\ell+m)!}\,\big\lvert P_{\ell}^{m}(0)\big\lvert^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\times\Big[(\ell+1)\,j_{\ell+1}(m\mathcal{M})\,h_{\ell}^{(1)}\!(m\mathcal{M})

\displaystyle\qquad-\ell\,j_{\ell}(m\mathcal{M})\,h_{\ell-1}^{(1)}\!(m\mathcal{M})\Big]

(33)

is the ”radial friction coefficient”. The term
\mathcal{M}^{2}/3
arises from the monopole Eq. (
27
) of the density perturbation
\delta\rho(\textbf{r},t)
which gives rise to a radial force

F_{r}(t)\supset-\frac{GM}{a^{2}}\left(\frac{4\pi}{3}\frac{GM}{c_{s}^{2}a}\bar{\rho}a^{3}\right)=-4\pi\left(\frac{GM}{\Omega a}\right)^{2}\bar{\rho}\,\frac{\mathcal{M}^{2}}{3}

(34)

as follows immediately from Newton’s shell theorem. Furthermore, when
m=0
, the terms in the square brackets of Eq. (
33
) should be replaced by

\displaystyle(\ell+1)\,j_{\ell+1}\,h_{\ell}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\to\left(-\frac{i}{4}\right)\frac{\big(\ell+1\big)}{\left(\ell+\frac{3}{2}\right)\left(\ell+\frac{1}{2}\right)}

(35)

\displaystyle\ell\,j_{\ell}\,h_{\ell-1}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\to\left(-\frac{i}{4}\right)\frac{\ell}{\left(\ell+\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\ell-\frac{1}{2}\right)}

for practical evaluation.

For a static perturber (
\mathcal{M}=0
), the frequencies are
\omega=m\Omega=0
so that the density multipoles are given by

\delta\rho_{\ell}^{m}(r,t)=4\pi GM\bar{\rho}\,g_{0\ell}(r,a)\,Y_{\ell}^{m*}(\mathrm{\hat{\bf{r}}}_{p})\;,

(36)

which yields an overdensity

\delta\rho(\textbf{r})=\frac{GM}{c_{s}^{2}}\bar{\rho}\,\frac{1}{\big|\textbf{r}-\textbf{r}_{p}\big|}\qquad(\mathcal{M}=0)

(37)

that exceeds unity within the Bondi radius
2GM/c_{s}^{2}
of the perturber (where linear response theory becomes invalid).
This density profile originates from the accretion onto the perturber, which is present regardless of the perturber’s motion. Since it is isotropic around the constant perturber position
\textbf{r}_{p}
, DF vanishes in the limit
\mathcal{M}\to 0
. Therefore, the

III
The singular isothermal sphere

We now turn to the singular isothermal sphere (SIS) with
\gamma=1
and
K=c_{s}^{2}
, for which the unperturbed density, potential and pressure are

\displaystyle\rho_{0}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{s}^{2}}{2\pi G}\,r^{-2}

(38)

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(r)

\displaystyle=2c_{s}^{2}\,{\rm ln}(r)+\mbox{const}

and
P_{0}(r)=c_{s}^{2}\rho_{0}(r)
. This yields a Jeans wavenumber
k_{J}^{2}=2/r^{2}
.
The Green’s equation is more complex in this case but, as we shall see below, the methodology is the same as in the infinite, homogeneous case.

III.1
Polarization function

For the SIS profile, the radial Green’s equation becomes

-r^{2}g_{\omega\ell}^{\prime\prime}-4rg_{\omega\ell}^{\prime}-\bigg[\left(\frac{\omega}{c_{s}}\right)^{2}r^{2}-\ell(\ell+1)+2\bigg]\,g_{\omega\ell}\\
=\frac{\delta^{D}(r-r^{\prime})}{c_{s}^{2}}

(39)

The constancy of the sound speed implies
g_{\omega\ell}(\lambda r,\lambda r^{\prime})=\lambda^{-1}g_{(\lambda\omega)\ell}(r,r^{\prime})
for any
\lambda>0
.
The solution to the homogeneous equation (that is, for
r\neq r^{\prime}
) reads

g_{\omega\ell}=\frac{A}{r}\,j_{\ell}\!\left(\frac{\omega}{c_{s}}r\right)+\frac{B}{r}\,y_{\ell}\!\left(\frac{\omega}{c_{s}}r\right)\;.

(40)

To construct the radial polarization function, we impose the same boundary conditions as in the infinite homogeneous case.
Since
y_{\ell}
diverges at the origin,
B
must be to zero for
r<r^{\prime}
. Furthermore, the outgoing wave boundary condition at infinity implies that the solution is proportional to
h_{\ell}^{(1)}
for
r>r^{\prime}
. Hence, we have

g_{\omega\ell}(r,r^{\prime})=\begin{cases}A(r^{\prime})\,r^{-1}\,j_{\ell}\!\left(\frac{\omega}{c_{s}}r\right)&(r<r^{\prime})\\
B(r^{\prime})\,r^{-1}\,h_{\ell}^{(1)}\!\left(\frac{\omega}{c_{s}}r\right)&(r>r^{\prime})\end{cases}\;.

(41)

The continuity and jump conditions at
r=r^{\prime}
determine
A(r^{\prime})
and
B(r^{\prime})
such that, for
\omega\neq 0
, the radial polarization function is given by

g_{\omega\ell}(r,r^{\prime})=\frac{i}{c_{s}^{2}}\,\left(\frac{\omega}{c_{s}}\right)\frac{r^{\prime}}{r}j_{\ell}\!\bigg(\frac{\omega}{c_{s}}r_{<}\bigg)\,h_{\ell}^{(1)}\!\bigg(\frac{\omega}{c_{s}}r_{>}\bigg)

(42)

Except for the asymmetric term
r^{\prime}/r
arising from the radial dependence of the unperturbed density profile, it is identical to the polarization function Eq. (
23
) of infinite, homogeneous systems. Therefore, we have

g_{0\ell}(r,r^{\prime})=\frac{c_{s}^{-2}}{(2\ell+1)}\frac{r^{\prime}}{r}\frac{r_{<}^{\ell}}{r_{>}^{\ell+1}}

(43)

in the special case
\omega=0
.

III.2
Acoustic wake

In the steady-state limit, the multipole coefficients of the wake (over)density are given by

\delta\rho_{\ell}^{m}(r,t)=4\pi GM\,A_{m\Omega,\ell}(r,a)\,Y_{\ell}^{m*}(\mathrm{\hat{\bf{r}}}_{p})

(44)

where
A_{m\Omega,\ell}(r,a)
is the auxiliary function, Eq. (
14
) with
\omega=m\Omega
and
r_{p}^{\prime}\equiv a
. Introducing

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\ell}^{n}(x;a,b)

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}\!\!du\,u^{n}\,j_{\ell}(xu)

(45)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{n}(x;a,b)

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}\!\!du\,u^{n}\,h_{\ell}^{(1)}\!(xu)

for shorthand convenience,
A_{m\Omega,\ell}(r,a)
is given by

\displaystyle A_{m\Omega,\ell}(r,a)

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}(a)\frac{im\mathcal{M}}{c_{s}^{2}r}\bigg\{j_{\ell}\!\Big(m\mathcal{M}\frac{r}{a}\Big)\,h_{\ell}^{(1)}\!(m\mathcal{M})

(46)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2}{2\ell+1}\bigg[\ell\,h_{\ell}^{(1)}\!\Big(m\mathcal{M}\frac{r}{a}\Big)\,\mathcal{J}_{\ell}^{\ell-1}\!\Big(m\mathcal{M};0,\frac{r}{a}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad+\ell\,j_{\ell}\Big(m\mathcal{M}\frac{r}{a}\Big)\,\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{\ell-1}\!\Big(m\mathcal{M};\frac{r}{a},1\Big)

\displaystyle\quad-\big(\ell+1\big)j_{l}\Big(m\mathcal{M}\frac{r}{a}\Big)\,\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{-\ell-2}\!\big(m\mathcal{M};1,\infty\big)\bigg]\bigg\}

for
r<a
, and

\displaystyle A_{m\Omega,\ell}(r,a)

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}(a)\frac{im\mathcal{M}}{c_{s}^{2}r}\bigg\{j_{\ell}(m\mathcal{M})\,h_{\ell}^{(1)}\!\Big(m\mathcal{M}\frac{r}{a}\Big)

(47)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2}{2\ell+1}\bigg[\ell\,h_{\ell}^{(1)}\!\Big(m\mathcal{M}\frac{r}{a}\Big)\,\mathcal{J}_{\ell}^{\ell-1}\!\big(m\mathcal{M};0,1\big)

\displaystyle\quad-\big(\ell+1\big)h_{\ell}^{(1)}\!\Big(m\mathcal{M}\frac{r}{a}\Big)\,\mathcal{J}_{\ell}^{-\ell-2}\!\Big(m\mathcal{M};1,\frac{r}{a}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad-\big(\ell+1\big)j_{l}\Big(m\mathcal{M}\frac{r}{a}\Big)\,\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{-\ell-2}\!\Big(m\mathcal{M};\frac{r}{a},\infty\Big)\bigg]\bigg\}

for
r>a
. Eqs .(
44

47

[
66
,
33
]
for self-gravitating collisionless systems. For the boundary conditions assumed here (steady-state), the acoustic wake pattern is purely resonant and depends only on harmonics of the fundamental frequency
\Omega

At large separations
r\gg a

h_{\ell}^{(1)}(x)\sim(-i)^{\ell+1}e^{ix}/x\sim a/r

\delta\rho(\textbf{r},t)/\rho_{0}(r)

38

\delta\rho(\textbf{r},t)\sim\epsilon\,\rho_{0}(r)\quad\mbox{for}\quad r\gg a\;,

(48)

where

\epsilon\equiv\frac{GM}{c_{s}^{2}a}\;.

(49)

Notice also that
M/M(<a)=\epsilon/2
, where
M(<a)
is the mass enclosed in the profile within a radius
a
. Therefore, we must have
\epsilon\ll 1
to ensure the validity of linear response theory, at least across a reasonable range of radii. In practice, the medium is not an ideal fluid and sound waves are damped by viscosity before they escape to infinity.

III.3
Friction coefficients

The perturbation
\delta\phi
to the gravitational potential of the isothermal sphere induced by a circularly-moving perturber at radius

16

\delta\phi_{\ell}^{m}

F_{\varphi}

of the DF force upon applying Eqs. (
20

The DF force can eventually be expressed as

{\bf F}_{\text{DF}}(t)=-4\pi\left(\frac{GM}{\Omega a}\right)^{2}\rho_{0}(a)\,\Big(I_{r}\,\mathrm{\hat{\bf{r}}}(t)+I_{\varphi}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(t)\Big)\;.

(54)

In the steady-state regime considered here, the tangential friction coefficient is given by

\displaystyle I_{\varphi}(\mathcal{M})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m\neq 0}m\,\frac{(\ell-m)!}{(\ell+m)!}\,\big\lvert P_{\ell}^{m}(0)\big\lvert^{2}

(55)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\Big[\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{-}(m\mathcal{M})+\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{+}\!(m\mathcal{M})\Big]\;.

The functions
\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\pm}\!(x)
defined in Appendix §
A
are related to the contribution from the inner (
-
) and outer (
+
) region.
Likewise, the radial friction coefficient is

\displaystyle I_{r}(\mathcal{M})

\displaystyle=i\mathcal{M}^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m}\frac{(\ell-m)!}{(\ell+m)!}\,\big\lvert P_{\ell}^{m}(0)\big\lvert^{2}

(56)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\Big[(\ell+1)\,\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{-}\!(m\mathcal{M})-\ell\,\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{+}\!(m\mathcal{M})\Big]\;.

Unlike the infinite, homogeneous case, the monopole contribution vanishes because there is no excess mass within the orbital radius. For practical evaluations, the terms in the square brackets of Eq. (
33
) should be replaced by

\displaystyle(\ell+1)\,\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{-}

\displaystyle\to-\frac{i\,(\ell+1)}{\big(2\ell+1\big)^{2}}

(57)

\displaystyle\ell\,\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{+}

\displaystyle\to-\frac{i\,\ell}{\big(2\ell+1\big)^{2}}

when
m=0
. Appendix §
A
outlines how the limiting behavior of
\mathcal{I}^{\pm}(x)
can be derived. Note also that
I_{\varphi}(\mathcal{M})
(

\mathcal{M}
since a change in the direction of the circular motion leaves

unchanged while it flips the sign of
F_{\varphi}
. Therefore, a series expansion of
I_{\varphi}(\mathcal{M})
(

) around
\mathcal{M}=0

\mathcal{M}
.

IV
Gravitational wave emission

An interesting aspect of our findings is the
r^{-2}
decay of the wake density multipoles
\delta\rho_{\ell}^{m}
in the outer region. In a realistic astrophysical system, such a scaling would, of course, hold only over a limited range of scales. Notwithstanding, interesting effects could arise even for a truncated isothermal profile, as we will illustrate now.

IV.1
Truncated isothermal profile

Consider a circular, binary black hole (BBH) with mass
M_{2}\ll M_{1}
, such that the massive companion
M_{1}
is located at the center of the circular orbit of radius
a
drawn by the perturber
M_{2}
.
This BBH is embedded in an
1/r^{2}
isothermal profile extending from
r_{\text{min}}
out to a finite radial distance
r_{\text{max}}
.
The mass enclosed in a sphere of radius
a
thus is
M(<a)=M_{1}+(2c_{s}^{2}/G)a=M_{1}(1+2/\epsilon_{1}(a))
.
Here and henceforth,
\epsilon_{i}(a)=GM_{i}/c_{s}^{2}a
designates the ratio of (half) the Bondi radius of
M_{i}
to the orbital radius. For a black hole of mass
M_{1}=10^{6}\ {\rm M_{\odot}}
and a sound speed
c_{s}=100\ {\rm km\,s^{-1}}
for example,
\epsilon_{1}=1
for
a\simeq 0.43\ {\rm pc}
. At this orbital radius however, such a system would emit at frequencies much smaller than the nanohertz (nHz) band proved by pulsar timing arrays (PTAs). In general,
\epsilon_{1}\gg 1
is required to get GW emission in the nHz frequency band, but one should relax the assumption of hydrostatic equilibrium to explore this interesting regime. Such an extension is beyond the scope of this paper, and we leave it for future work. Likewise, the validity of linear response theory requires
\epsilon_{2}(a)\ll 1
, which will not be satisfied for small values of
a
.

IV.2
Power radiated in GWs

The Quadrupole formula can be applied to compute the GW emission of the BBH, had it been evolving in vacuum. In this approximation, the power radiated in GW is

P_{\text{gw}}=\frac{G}{5c^{5}}\big\langle\dddot{Q}_{ij}\dddot{Q}_{ij}\big\rangle\;,

(59)

where the temporal average
\langle\dots\rangle
is taken over several periods, and the quadrupole moment is

Q_{ij}=\int\!d^{3}r\,\delta\rho(\textbf{r},t)\left(x_{i}x_{j}-\frac{1}{3}r^{2}\delta_{ij}\right)\;.

(60)

In vacuum, the circular binary would radiate GWs at a frequency
2\Omega
with a power
[
49
]

P_{\text{gw}}^{\text{Vac}}\approx\frac{32}{5c^{5}}\frac{G^{4}M_{2}^{2}M_{1}^{3}}{a^{5}}

(61)

for the small mass ratio assumed here.
The effect of the truncated isothermal profile on the emission of GWs is twofold: (i) it perturbs the emission arising from the two compact bodies through a non-vacuum metric; and (ii) it provides an extra source of GWs through the time-varying quadrupole moment of the acoustic wake. Let us assess the importance of (ii) in what follows.

\omega_{\text{gw}}^{2}\sim GM(<a)/a^{3}
or, equivalently, a (reduced) wavelength
\lambdabar_{\text{gw}}^{2}\sim a^{3}c^{2}/GM(<a)

(62)

where
-2\leq m\leq+2
.
The integration domain of the radial integral will be specified shortly.
Only the
m=\pm 2
multipoles contribute to GW emission. For sake of completeness however, the matrices
\mathcal{Q}^{(m)}
are given by
\mathcal{Q}^{(0)}=(2/3)\sqrt{\pi/5}\,{\rm diag}\big(-1,-1,2\big)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{(+1)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{15}}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&0&-1\\

-1&i&0\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{(+2)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{15}}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&-i&0\\

\displaystyle\langle\dddot{Q}_{ij}\dddot{Q}_{ij}\rangle

\displaystyle=\big(4\pi GM_{2}\big)^{2}\Omega^{6}\sum_{m=\pm 2}m^{6}\big\lvert Y_{2}^{m}(\mathrm{\hat{\bf{r}}}_{p})\big\lvert^{2}

(69)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\bigg\lvert\int_{r_{-}}^{r_{+}}\!\!dr\,r^{4}\,A_{m\Omega,2}(r,a)\bigg\lvert^{2}{\rm tr}\big(\mathcal{Q}^{(m)}\mathcal{Q}^{(m)*}\big)\;,

\Big\langle Y_{2}^{m*}(\mathrm{\hat{\bf{r}}}_{p})Y_{2}^{m^{\prime}}(\mathrm{\hat{\bf{r}}}_{p})\Big\rangle=\delta_{mm^{\prime}}\;.

\mathcal{M}

M_{2}

r_{+}=r_{\text{max}}
and equating the sound-crossing time
r_{+}/c_{s}
to the free-fall time
(G\bar{\rho})^{-1}
. This gives
\bar{\rho}=G^{-1}(c_{s}/r_{+})^{2}
. This way, the unperturbed density is proportional to
c_{s}^{2}
for both the truncated isothermal profile and the uniform sphere.

We have checked that the results do not change noticeably when the inner radius is set to
r_{-}=0
, or a region
[0.9a,1.1a]
around the small companion
M_{2}
is excised. In other words, all the signals originates from the outer region of the profile, which is unsurprising given the
r^{-2}
dependence of
\delta\rho
. The vertical dotted line marks
\mathcal{M}=\sqrt{2}
, which is the (constant) Mach number of a circularly-moving perturber in a truncated isothermal profile so long as
M(<a)\gg M_{1}
. For
\mathcal{M}\approx\sqrt{2}
, the motion of
M_{2}
and the acoustic wake are in resonance and maximize the shape factor
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{M},r_{+}/a)
. At these Mach numbers, the ratio

\frac{P_{\text{gw}}^{\text{wake}}}{P_{\text{gw}}^{\text{Vac}}}=\frac{1}{12}\left(\frac{M(<a)}{M_{1}}\right)^{3}\,\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{M})

(74)

can be as large as
\sim 10^{2}
for
M(<a)\sim M_{1}
and
r_{+}\sim 10a
, regardless of whether the binary is embedded in an isothermal profile or a uniform medium. At fixed
a
and
M_{1}
, the limit
\mathcal{M}\to\infty
is equivalent to
c_{s}\to\infty
, that is, a vanishing profile density
\rho_{0}
. In this regime,
P_{\text{gw}}^{\text{wake}}
becomes negligible as expected. The critical density
\rho_{*}
above which
P_{\text{gw}}^{\text{wake}}>P_{\text{gw}}^{\text{Vac}}
decreases with increasing
r_{+}
. For
r_{+}\lesssim 10a
for instance, we have
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{M})\gtrsim 10
for
\mathcal{M}\lesssim 10
. Therefore, the condition
P_{\text{gw}}^{\text{wake}}>P_{\text{gw}}^{\text{Vac}}
becomes
c_{s}^{2}\gtrsim 10^{-2}\Omega^{2}a^{2}
, which implies
\rho_{0}(a)>\rho_{*}\sim 10^{-2}M_{1}/a^{3}
. This shows that, in this case, it is possible to get a significant effect even if the mass enclosed in the profile within the orbital radius is much smaller than
M_{1}
.

In a real fluid, acoustic wakes will be dissipated by viscosity and thermal conductivity
[
37
,
43
,
21
,
56
, see e.g.]
. Our results are valid in the limit of small viscosity, i.e.
\gamma c_{s}/\omega\ll 1
where
\gamma\sim\omega^{2}/c_{s}^{3}\nu
is the fluid absorption coefficient and
\omega
is the circular frequency of the sound waves
[
37
]
. In this case, the amplitude of the sound waves is damped according to
e^{-\gamma x}
. Since the kinematic viscosity
\nu
approximately scales as
\nu\sim c_{s}\lambda_{\text{mfp}}
, where
\lambda_{\text{mfp}}
is the mean free path of the fluid particles, the condition
\gamma c_{s}/\omega\ll 1
is equivalent to
\lambda_{\text{mfp}}\ll c_{s}/\Omega
or, equivalently,
\lambda_{\text{mfp}}\ll a
. Furthermore, the condition
\lambda_{\text{mfp}}\lesssim GM_{2}/c_{s}^{2}
is required for the ideal fluid approximation
[
31
, see e.g.]
.

For sound waves launched in the intracluster medium (ICM), the typical mean free path of particles due to collisions,
\lambda_{\text{mfp}}\sim 0.1-10\ {\rm Kpc}

[
55
,
1
,
54
]
, are much larger than the orbital sizes of interest. For such systems, one should start from the Chandrasekhar formula appropriate to collisionless systems. In the (warm, diffuse) interstellar medium (ISM) of a galaxy, the typical mean free path is of order
10^{15}-10^{16}{\ {\rm cm}}
, i.e.
\lambda_{\text{mfp}}\sim 10^{-2}-10^{-3}\ {\rm pc}
for densities
n\sim 1\ {\rm cm^{-3}}

[
40
]
. For such densities however, the mass in a gaseous sphere of radius
1\ {\rm pc}
is
\sim 0.1\ {\rm M_{\odot}}
, which is too small to influence the dynamical evolution of compact binaries under consideration. This could drastically change if the compact binary is embedded in a dense gaseous cloud, a gas-rich galaxy at high redshift or, alternatively, in a dark matter core or spike.

V
Conclusions

We have applied linear response theory to solve for the acoustic wake induced by a pointlike perturber in circular motion around the center of an isothermal
r^{-2}
density profile (for simplicity). We consider the limit of an ideal fluid. Our work thus complements the analysis of
[
61
,
66
]
, who focused on collisionless systems.

Our results are valid in the limit where the self-gravity of the acoustic wake can be neglected. Taking into account the wake’s self-gravity leads to a (Fredholm integral) equation of the form
\delta\rho=\delta\tilde{\rho}+{\rm K}\,\delta\rho
, where
\delta\rho
and
\delta\tilde{\rho}
are the solution with and without self-gravity, respectively, whereas
{\rm K}
is a nonlocal operator (due to Poisson’s equation
\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{2}\delta\phi=4\pi G\delta\rho
). Depending on the properties of
{\rm K}
, this equation could be solved iteratively (i.e.
\delta\rho=\sum_{n\geq 0}{\rm K}^{n}\,\delta\tilde{\rho}
) in analogy with the matrix method of
[
29
,
67
]
for collisionless systems. We have not explored this avenue here.

To calculate the acoustic wake pattern, we have expanded the polarization function in multipoles. This allows us to isolate the contribution of each multipole to the dynamical friction experienced by the perturber. The outer region
r>a
(
a
is the orbital radius) dominates the (tangential) drag force
F_{\varphi}
for subsonic motion. For Mach numbers
\mathcal{M}\ll 1
, the dipole of the acoustic wake yields the largest contribution to the drag. In this limit, the (tangential) friction coefficient scales as
I_{\varphi}(\mathcal{M})\sim\mathcal{M}^{5}
for the SIS profile, unlike the
\mathcal{M}^{3}
scaling in the infinite, homogeneous case. For
\mathcal{M}\gg 1
however, the two backgrounds yield similar results, in agreement with the intuition that the local approximation should always be valid deep in the supersonic regime. By contrast, predictions differ sharply for the radial component
F_{r}
of dynamical friction (which is dynamically irrelevant for circular orbits). All our results assume steady-state. Comparison with numerical simulations, which requires different boundary conditions, is deferred to future work.

The circular velocity
\Omega a
of the perturber is determined by the mass
M(<a)
enclosed within the orbit. In the absence of a massive companion at the center, the
r^{-2}
dependence of
\rho_{0}(r)
leads to a flat rotation curve and, thereby, a constant Mach number
\mathcal{M}\sim\sqrt{2}
so long as an adiabatic sequence of quasi-circular orbit is a reasonable approximation to the inspiral of the perturber. At this Mach number, the acoustic wake pattern is in resonance with the orbital motion and maximizes the drag. Therefore, the GW emission produced by the quadrupole of the wake density pattern should also be maximized around
\mathcal{M}\sim 1
. To estimate this effect, we have considered an extreme mass ratio inspiral around a massive black hole residing at the center of a (truncated) isothermal profile. Our calculation suggests that GW emission from the acoustic wake could be large relative to the vacuum predictions when
\mathcal{M}\sim
a few (i.e. in the early phase of an inspiral). These findings must be interpreted with caution as our simplified setting does not take into account neither the viscosity of real astrophysical fluids, nor the radial infall onto the massive companion. Nevertheless, it would be interesting to assess how much of this signal remains for real astrophysical systems, and the extent to which it could leave a signal in the nanohertz frequency range probed by pulsar timing arrays.
