The rate of convergence of Bregman proximal methods: Local geometry vs. regularity vs. sharpness Thanks: The authors are grateful to J. Bolte for many fruitful discussions.
Waïss Azizian c,∗, Franck Iutzeler ♯, Jérôme Malick ∗, Panayotis Mertikopoulos ⋄

Abstract
. We examine the last-iterate convergence rate of BPM – from MD to MP and its optimistic variants – as a function of the local geometry induced by the prox-mapping defining the method. For generality, we focus on local solutions of constrained, non-monotone VI , and we show that the convergence rate of a given method depends sharply on its associated Legendre exponent , a notion that measures the growth rate of the underlying Bregman function (Euclidean, entropic, or other) near a solution. In particular, we show that boundary solutions exhibit a stark separation of regimes between methods with a zero and non-zero Legendre exponent: the former converge at a linear rate, while the latter converge, in general, sublinearly. This dichotomy becomes even more pronounced in linearly constrained problems where methods with entropic regularization achieve a linear convergence rate along sharp directions, compared to convergence in a finite number of steps under Euclidean regularization.

1.
Introduction

\Acp

BPM have a long and rich history in optimization, going back at least to the introduction of
MD
by Nemirovski & Yudin
[
36
]
.
In plain terms,
BPM
are first-order (constrained) optimization algorithms that forego Euclidean projections in favor of a more sophisticated “prox-mapping” that minimizes a certain distance-like functional known as the Bregman divergence
[
36
,
15
,
13
,
25
]
.
When this Bregman divergence is the Euclidean distance squared, one recovers the standard projection-based methods;
other than that, depending on the problem’s feasible region, different Bregman setups lead to a diverse collection of algorithms,
from exponentiated gradient descent on the simplex
[
36
,
10
,
6
]
,
to matrix multiplicative weights on the positive-semidefinite cone
[
47
,
24
]
,
variants of Karmarkar’s affine scaling algorithm for linear programs
[
48
]
,
etc.

One of the most appealing features of
BPM
is that they achieve almost dimension-free convergence rates in problems with a convex structure and a favorable geometry – such as the
L^{1}
ball, the spectraplex, second-order cones, etc.
[
14
,
38
,
10
]
.
This is owed to a delicate interplay between the algorithms’ non-Euclidean update scheme and the global geometry of the problem’s domain.
However, these (almost) dimension-free guarantees also come with some strings attached:
they do not concern the sequence of iterates generated by the method, but only its time average
(or, through the same, “regret-based” analysis, the method’s “best iterate”);
in this way, the best guarantee that can be achieved after
t
iterations is
\bigoh(1/t)
.

In terms of oracle complexity, this is sufficient for problems that are not strongly convex / strongly monotone, but if one targets finer, geometric convergence rates,
the inherent averaging involved in regret-based guarantees is hard to compensate.
And, on the other extreme, if the problem is not convex / monotone to begin with, iterate averaging does not provide any quantifiable benefits whatsoever, so it becomes crucial to study the actual trajectory of the method.

Our contributions

Our paper seeks to quantify the last-iterate convergence rate of
BPM
as a function of the Bregman divergence defining the method and the local geometry that it induces.
To treat this question in as general a manner as possible, we focus on
VI
(
VI
) problems of the form

(VI)

\text{Find $x^{\ast}\in\mathcal{X}$ such that}\;\;\langle g(x^{\ast}),x-x^{\ast}\rangle\geq 0\;\;\text{for all $x\in\mathcal{X}$},

where
\mathcal{X}
is a closed convex subset of a finite-dimensional normed space
\mathcal{V}
, and

is a (possibly non-monotone) single-valued operator on
\mathcal{X}
with values in
\mathcal{V}^{\ast}
, the dual of
\mathcal{V}
.
This problem is a staple of many areas of mathematical programming, game theory and data science, as it provides a template for “optimization beyond minimization” – i.e., for problems where finding an optimal solution does not necessarily involve minimizing a loss function.
In particular, in addition to standard minimization problems – which are recovered when
g=\nabla f
for some smooth function

for an introduction, see
[
18
]
and references therein.

In this broad context, we examine the rate of convergence of a wide class of

to local solutions of (
VI
) that satisfy a

Specifically, the class of algorithms we consider includes as special cases

(1)

the original
MD
(
MD
) algorithm of
[
36
]
;

(2)

the
MP
(
MP
) method of Nemirovski
[
34
]
– which has the same update structure as the Bregman-based algorithm of
[
7
]
and contains as a special case the

(

) algorithm of
[
26
]
;

(3)

the so-called
OMD
(
OMD
) method of
[
42
]
– itself a Bregman analogue of the modified Arrow-Hurwicz algorithm of
[
41
]
;

etc.

Our first finding is a crisp characterization of last-iterate convergence rate of
BPM
in terms of the local geometry induced by the underlying Bregman function near a given solution of (
VI
).
We make this dependence precise via the notion of the
Legendre exponent
, a regularity measure for Bregman methods due to
[
8
]
, which can roughly be described as the logarithmic ratio of the volume of a Euclidean ball to that of a Bregman ball of the same radius.
For example, Euclidean methods have a Legendre exponent of
\beta=0
and they converge at a linear rate;
entropic methods have a Legendre exponent of
\beta=1/2
at boundary points, and they converge at a rate of
\bigoh(t^{-1})
;
more generally,
as we show in
Theorem

1
, methods with a Legendre exponent
\beta>0
converge at a rate of
\bigoh(t^{1-1/\beta})
.

The Euclidean regime (
\beta=0
) is perfectly aligned with existing results for the geometric last-iterate convergence rate of the
EG
algorithm and its variants
[
20
,
28
,
22
,
33
]
.
By contrast, the Legendre regime (
\beta>0
) indicates a significant drop in the algorithm’s last-iterate convergence speed, even though ergodic convergence rates
[
37
]
and results for bilinear games
[
49
]
might suggest otherwise.

Subsequently, motivated by applications to game theory and linear programming, we take a closer look at the convergence rate of
BPM
across the constraints that are active at a solution
x^{\ast}
of (
VI
) depending on the position of
g(x^{\ast})
relative to said constraints.
This analysis reveals that Bregman proximal methods have a particularly fine structure:
along
sharp directions
(i.e., constraints along which
g(x^{\ast})
is strictly inward-pointing),

converge

(1)

at a rate of
\bigoh(1/t^{1/(2\beta-1)})
if
1/2<\beta<1
;

(2)

at a
geometric rate
if
0<\beta\leq 1/2
(e.g., for entropic methods);
and

(3)

in a
finite
number of iterations if
\beta=0

(cf.
Theorem

2
).
Thus, even though the estimates of
Theorem

1
are, in general tight, the actual convergence rate of a Bregman method along different coordinates / constraints could be starkly different – and, in fact, dramatically faster if the solution under study is itself sharp.

The closest antecedent of our work is the conference paper
[
8
]
where the Legendre exponent was introduced to analyze the convergence of
OMD
in
stochastic

VI
problems (without considering sharp directions and/or faster identification rates).
The stochastic and deterministic settings are obviously very different, both in the challenges involved as well as the rates obtained, so there is no overlap in our analysis and results.
Other than that, we are not aware of any comparable results in the literature concerning the radically different convergence landscape of
BPM
along active and inactive constraints.

2.1.
Blanket assumptions

Throughout the sequel, we will make the following assumptions for the defining vector field
g\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
of (
VI

under study:

Assumption 1

(Lipschitz continuity)
.

The vector field

is

, i.e.,

(LC)

\lVert g(x^{\prime})-g(x)\rVert_{\ast}\leq L\lVert x^{\prime}-x\rVert\quad\text{for all $x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}$}.

Assumption 2

(Second-order sufficiency)
.

There exists
a convex neighborhood
\mathcal{B}
of
x^{\ast}
in
\mathcal{X}

and
a positive constant
\mu>0

such that

(SOS)

\langle g(x)-g(x^{\ast}),x-x^{\ast}\rangle\geq\mu\lVert x-x^{\ast}\rVert^{2}\quad\text{for all $x\in\mathcal{B}$}.

In general,

is the unique solution of (
VI
) in
\mathcal{B}
;
we illustrate this in two special cases of interest:

(1)

Minimization problems:

suppose that
g=\nabla f
for some Lipschitz smooth objective function

on
\mathcal{X}
.
Then,
2
implies that
f
grows (at least) quadratically along every ray emanating from

, i.e.,
f(x)-f(x^{\ast})\geq\langle\nabla f(x^{\ast}),x-x^{\ast}\rangle+(\mu/2)\lVert x-x^{\ast}\rVert^{2}=\Omega(\lVert x-x^{\ast}\rVert^{2})
for all
x\in\mathcal{B}
, implying in particular that

is an isolated minimizer of

.

(2)

Min-max problems:

suppose that
\mathcal{X}
factorizes as
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\mathcal{X}_{2}
for suitable factor sets
\mathcal{X}_{1}
, and
\mathcal{X}_{2}
,
let
\mathcal{L}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function on
\mathcal{X}
,
and
write
g=(\nabla_{x_{1}}\mathcal{L},-\nabla_{x_{2}}\mathcal{L})
for the min-max gradient of
\mathcal{L}
(with respect to
x_{1}\in\mathcal{X}_{1}
and
x_{2}\in\mathcal{X}_{2}
respectively).
Then, any solution

of (

) that satisfies

is an isolated, hyperbolic saddle-point of

More examples satisfying (

]

Overall,

2.2.
BP

As we discussed in the introduction, the main algorithmic template that we will examine for solving (

(

The defining ingredient of this class is the notion of

Definition 1

(Bregman regularizers and related notions)
.

A proper,
lsc
, strictly convex function

is a

on

if
the following are true

(1)

is supported on
\mathcal{X}
, i.e.,
\dom h=\mathcal{X}
.

(2)

The subdifferential of

admits a
continuous selection
, i.e., there exists a continuous mapping
\nabla h\colon\dom\partial h\to\mathcal{Y}
such that
\nabla h(x)\in\partial h(x)
for all
x\in\dom\partial h
.

(3)

h
is
locally strongly convex
relative to
\lVert\cdot\rVert
, i.e., for any compact set

(1)

h(x^{\prime})\geq h(x)+\langle\nabla h(x),x^{\prime}-x\rangle+\tfrac{\kappa}{2}\lVert x^{\prime}-x\rVert^{2}

for some
\kappa>0
and for all
x\in\mathcal{K}\cap\dom\partial h
,
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}
.

The set
\mathcal{X}_{h}\coloneqq\dom\partial h
will be referred to as the

of

.
In addition, we also define the

of

as

(2)

\displaystyle D(p,x)

\displaystyle=h(p)-h(x)-\langle\nabla h(x),p-x\rangle

(3)

\displaystyle P_{#1}(y)

\displaystyle=\argmin\nolimits_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\{\langle y,x-x^{\prime}\rangle+D(x^{\prime},x)\}

\displaystyle\text{for all $x\in\mathcal{X}_{h}$, $y\in\mathcal{Y}$}.

Remark 1
.

Examples of Bregman regularizers are given in

Motivating examples

We now proceed to take a closer look at some commonly used Bregman regularizers (and the induced prox-mappings) with the goal of determining the rate of convergence of the associated Bregman method.
For concreteness, we focus on one-dimensional problems where
\mathcal{X}

To streamline our presentation, we will only examine the

In this case, we obtain the scheme

To illustrate the spectrum of different behaviors that arise near the boundary of

Example 1

(Euclidean regularization)
.

We begin with the quadratic regularizer

In this case, noting that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \quad a)\;\;&\text{Prox-domain:}&\qquad&\mathcal{X}_{h}=\mathcal{X}&\\
\quad b)\;\;&\text{Bregman divergence:}&\qquad&D(p,x)=(p-x)^{2}/2&\\

4.
The Legendre exponent and convergence rate analysis

Our goal in this section is to provide a precise link between the geometry induced by a Bregman regularizer near a solution and the convergence rate of the associated Bregman proximal method.
The key notion in this regard is that of the
Legendre exponent
, which we define and discuss in detail below.

4.1.
The Legendre exponent

Our starting point is the observation that, without loss of generality, the local strong convexity requirement for
h
can be expressed as

(18)

D(p,x)\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert p-x\rVert^{2}\quad\text{for all $x\in\mathcal{X}_{h}$ sufficiently close to $p$}.

Qualitatively, this means that the convergence topology induced by the Bregman divergence of
h
on
\mathcal{X}
is
at least as fine
as the ambient norm topology:
if a sequence
x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}_{h}
,
t=1,2,\dotsc
, converges to
p\in\mathcal{X}
in the Bregman sense (
D(p,x_{t})\to 0
), it also converges in the ambient norm topology (
\lVert x_{t}-p\rVert\to 0
).
On the other hand, from a quantitative standpoint, the rate of this convergence could be quite different:
as we saw in the previous section, the reverse inequality
D(p,x)=\bigoh(\lVert p-x\rVert^{2})
may fail to hold, in which case
\sqrt{D(p,x_{t})}
and
\lVert x-x_{t}\rVert
would exhibit a different asymptotic behavior.

To quantify this gap, we use the notion of the
Legendre exponent
, as introduced in
[
8
]
.

Definition 2
.

Let
h
be a Bregman regularizer on
\mathcal{X}
.
The
Legendre exponent
of
h
at
p\in\mathcal{X}
is defined as

(19)

\beta_{h}(p)\coloneqq\inf\left\{\beta\in[0,1]:\limsup_{x\to p}\frac{\sqrt{D(p,x)}}{\lVert x-p\rVert^{1-\beta}}<\infty\right\}

and we say that
h
is
tight
at
p
if the infimum is attained in (
19
), i.e., if
\beta_{h}(p)
is the minimal
\beta\in[0,1]
such that

(20)

D(p,x)=\mathcal{O}\big(\lVert p-x\rVert^{2(1-\beta)}\big)\quad\text{for $x$ near $p$}.

Informally, the Legendre exponent measures the deficit in relative size between ordinary “norm neighborhoods” in
\mathcal{X}
and the corresponding “Bregman neighborhoods” induced by the sublevel sets of the Bregman divergence.
Specifically,

(1)

the case
\beta_{h}(p)=0
corresponds to the “norm-like” behavior
D(p,x)=\Theta(\lVert p-x\rVert^{2})
;

(2)

any other value
\beta_{h}(p)\in(0,1)
indicates a different limiting behavior for
D(p,x)
as
x\to p
;
and, finally,

(3)

when
\beta_{h}(p)=1
we may have
\limsup_{x\to p}D(p,x)>0
.

In this last case, the ambient norm topology is
strictly coarser
than the Bregman topology in the sense that
D(p,x_{t})
may remain bounded away from zero even if
x_{t}\to p
as
t\to\infty
;
we provide an example of such behavior below – and see also
[
8
,
39
]
for further discussion.

Example 5

(Non-compatible topologies)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\lVert x\rVert_{2}\leq 1\}
be the unit Euclidean ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and consider the
n
-dimensional Hellinger regularizer
h(x)=-\sqrt{1-\lVert x\rVert_{2}^{2}}
.
Then, for all
p
on the boundary of
\mathcal{X}
and all

, we readily get

(21)

D(p,x)=\frac{1-\langle p,x\rangle}{\sqrt{1-\lVert x\rVert_{2}^{2}}}.

If
n\geq 2
, the limit
\lim_{x\to p}D(p,x)
may not exist,
a fact which has the following counterintuitive consequences:

(1)

the “Hellinger ball”
\mathcal{B}_{r}^{h}(p)\coloneqq\{x\in\mathcal{X}_{h}:D(p,x)\leq r^{2}/2\}
is
not closed
in the Euclidean topology;
and

(2)

the “Hellinger center”
p
of
\mathcal{B}_{r}^{h}(p)
actually belongs to the Euclidean boundary of
\mathcal{B}_{r}^{h}(p)
.

As a result, for all
n\geq 2
, it is straightforward to construct a sequence
x_{t}
with
\lVert x_{t}-p\rVert_{2}\to 0
, but which remains at
constant
Hellinger divergence relative to
p
.

3
3

3

For instance, if

, the point

converges to

as

, even though
D\left(p,x_{u}\right)=1
for all

.
Crucially, if

.

We are now in a position to state and prove our refinement of
Theorem

1
for linearly constrained problems.
To that end, following
[
1
]
, we will assume in the rest of this section that (

that is adapted to the polyhedral structure of

as per the definition below:

Definition 4
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a polyhedral domain of the form (

Then, a Bregman regularizer

\mathcal{X}
is said to be

h(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta(x_{i})\quad\text{for all $x\in\mathcal{X}$}.

such that

(1)

\theta^{\prime\prime}(z)>0
for all

and

(2)

\theta^{\prime\prime}

(0,\infty)

In addition to facilitating calculations, the notion of decomposability will further allow us to describe the convergence rate of the iterates of (

in finer detail.
In fact, as it turns out, the speed of convergence along a given direction will actually be determined by the behavior of the derivative of the Bregman kernel

near

In this regard, there are two distinct regimes to consider.
First, if
\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}\theta^{\prime}(x)=-\infty

\dom\partial h=\relint\mathcal{X}
so, by
Lemma

A.2
in
Appendix

of (

for all

is essentially smooth – or

– in the sense of
[
43
, Chap. 26]
, and we will refer to it as

.
Otherwise, if
\theta^{\prime}(0)
exists and is finite,

may reach the boundary of
\mathcal{X}
in a finite number of iterations;
we will refer to this case as

The key difference between these two regimes is that, in the non-steep case, the algorithm may achieve convergence in a finite number of steps (at least along certain directions).
On the other hand, even though finite-time convergence is not possible in the steep regime, the algorithm’s rate of convergence may still depend on the boundary behavior of
\theta
.
To illustrate this, we will consider the following concrete cases:

Assumption 4
.

If

is a kernel function as per

,

Euclidean-like:

(2)

Entropy-like:

(3)

Power-like:

\liminf_{x\to 0^{+}}x^{p}\theta^{\prime}(x)>-\infty

Remark
.

Items

Our results indicate that Euclidean regularization leads to faster trajectory convergence rates near

.

We conclude our treatment of the simplex by showing that
