Cayley extensions of maniplexes and polytopes
Gabe Cunningham, Elías Mochán, Antonio Montero

Abstract
. A map on a surface whose automorphism group has a subgroup acting regularly on its vertices is called a Cayley map. Here we generalize that notion to maniplexes and polytopes. We define ℳ \mathcal{M} to be a Cayley extension of 𝒦 \mathcal{K} if the facets of ℳ \mathcal{M} are isomorphic to 𝒦 \mathcal{K} and if some subgroup of the automorphism group of ℳ \mathcal{M} acts regularly on the facets of ℳ \mathcal{M} . We show that many natural extensions in the literature on maniplexes and polytopes are in fact Cayley extensions. We also describe several universal Cayley extensions. Finally, we examine the automorphism group and symmetry type graph of Cayley extensions.

Primary: 52B15 Secondary: 05C25, 05E18.

1.
Introduction

Maniplexes, introduced in
[
30
]
, are combinatorial structures that generalize maps on surfaces and abstract polytopes.
Indeed, maniplexes of rank
3
are maps (and vice-versa), and every maniplex
\mathcal{M}
of rank
n+1
can be constructed by gluing together maniplexes of rank
n
(the facets of
\mathcal{M}
) in an appropriate way.
Whenever all the facets of
\mathcal{M}
are isomorphic to a given maniplex
\mathcal{K}
, then
\mathcal{M}
is an
extension
of
\mathcal{K}
.
Many tools and ideas from the study of maps can be adapted to maniplexes, but often there are additional difficulties to be overcome in higher ranks.

In this paper, we study
Cayley (co)extensions
of maniplexes, inspired by Cayley graphs and maps which are named after A. Cayley.
Given a group
\Gamma
and a set
\Sigma
such that
\sigma^{-1}\in\Sigma
for every
\sigma\in\Sigma
, the
Cayley graph

\cay(\Gamma,\Sigma)
is a graph whose vertices are the elements of
\Gamma
and two vertices
\alpha,\beta\in\Gamma
are connected if and only if
\alpha\beta^{-1}\in\Sigma
.
A classical theorem by Sabidussi
[
25
]
proves that a graph
X
is a Cayley graph if and only if there is a group
\Gamma\leqslant\Aut(X)
acting regularly on the vertices of
X
.
This notion has been generalized to maps
[
24
]
: an orientable map
M
is a
Cayley map
if there exists a group of orientation-preserving automorphisms acting regularly on the vertices of
M
.
There have been generalizations of Cayley maps to include maps on non-orientable surfaces (see
[
12
]
, for example) but the theory is not as developed as that of orientable Cayley maps.

Analogously to Cayley maps, we define a maniplex
\mathcal{M}
to be a
Cayley extension
(resp. coextension) if some subgroup of the automorphism group of
\mathcal{M}
acts regularly on the facets (resp. vertices) of
\mathcal{M}
. This provides one of the first generalizations of the Cayley property to the context of maniplexes and abstract polytopes.
Using voltage graphs, we describe how to create Cayley extensions of a given facet with a given group acting regularly on the facets. We will show that many natural extensions of maniplexes and polytopes can be constructed in this way.

In Section
2
we review the basics of maniplexes and voltage graphs. Then in Section
3
, we define Cayley extensions formally, and we describe some basic properties. We consider the question of when a Cayley extension is an abstract polytope in Section
3.2
. Section
4
demonstrates several examples of known extensions that can be described using our framework, and then defines new classes of universal extensions. Finally, Section
5
determines the symmetry type graphs of these universal extensions, and characterizes when different ways of constructing a universal extension lead to the same result.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Graphs

In this work we use the definition of graph used in
[
14
]
, which is slightly more general than the typical definition.
A
graph

X
is a quadruple
(D,V,I,(\cdot)^{-1})
where
D
and
V
are disjoint sets,
I:D\to V
is a function and
(\cdot)^{-1}:D\to D
is an involutory permutation of
D
.
The set
V
is the set of
vertices
of
X
, and the set
D
is the set of
darts
of
X
.
For a dart
d
, the vertex
I(d)
is the
initial vertex
or
starting point
of
d
and
d^{-1}
is the
inverse
of
d
. The
terminal vertex
or
endpoint
of
d
is the starting point of
d^{-1}
.
The
edges
are the orbits of
D
under the action of
(\cdot)^{-1}
. If an edge consists only of a single dart, that is, a dart
d
that satisfies
d^{-1}=d
, then it is called a
semiedge
.
A
loop
is an edge consisting of two darts whose initial vertex is the same.
A
link
is an edge that is not a loop or a semiedge;
that is, an edge whose two darts are different and have different starting points.

Most classical graph theoretical concepts extend naturally to our definition; we shall just make precise a few of them that are relevant for this manuscript.

If
X=(V(X),D(X),I_{X},(\cdot)^{-1}_{X})
and
Y=(V(Y),D(Y),I_{Y},(\cdot)^{-1}_{Y})
are graphs, a
graph homomorphism

f:X\to Y
is a pair of functions
f=(f_{V},f_{D})
such that
f_{V}:V(X)\to V(Y)
,
f_{D}:D(X)\to D(Y)
, and

\displaystyle I_{Y}\left(df_{D}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(I_{X}(d)\right)f_{V}

\displaystyle\text{and}

\displaystyle\left(df_{D}\right)_{Y}^{-1}

\displaystyle=(d^{-1}_{X})f_{D}

for every dart
d\in D(X)
.
Note that we evaluate graph homomorphisms on the right.

If
v\in V(X)
and
d\in D(X)
, we shall write
vf
and
df
instead of
vf_{V}
and
df_{D}
.
If both
f_{V}
and
f_{D}
are bijective and
f^{-1}:=(f_{V}^{-1},f_{D}^{-1})
is also a graph homomorphism, then we say that
f
(and
f^{-1})
is an
isomorphism
and that
X
and
Y
are
isomorphic
(and write
X\cong Y
).
Naturally, for a graph
X
, an
automorphism
is an isomorphism
f:X\to X
.

A
path
is a finite sequence
W=(d_{1},\dots,d_{k})
of darts such that the endpoint of
d_{i}
is the starting point of
d_{i+1}
for
i\in\{0,\dots,k-1\}
.
We usually omit commas and parentheses and simply write
W=d_{1}\cdots d_{k}
.
The number
k
is the
length
of
W
.
The starting point of
d_{1}
is the
starting point
of
W
; we also say that
W

starts
at this vertex.
Similarly, the
endpoint
of
W
is the endpoint of
d_{k}
and we say that
W

ends
at this vertex.
A single vertex is a path of length
0
.
A path is
closed
if its starting point and its endpoint are the same vertex.

An
elementary graph move
on a path consists of removing or inserting two consecutive inverse darts.
Two paths
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
are
graph-homotopic
if their initial vertex is the same and
W_{1}
can be obtained from
W_{2}
after a (possibly empty) sequence of elementary graph moves.
In this case we write
W_{1}\sim W_{2}
.
Clearly graph-homotopy is an equivalence relation and we often identify a path with its homotopy class.
Observe that if
W
is graph-homotopic to a path of length
0
then
W
must be closed.

Given two paths
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
we say that they are
compatible
if the endpoint of
W_{1}
is the starting point of
W_{2}
.
We can operate on compatible paths by concatenation.
That is, if
W_{1}=d_{1}\cdots d_{k}
and
W_{2}=a_{1}\cdots a_{\ell}
then
W_{1}W_{2}=d_{1}\cdots d_{k}a_{1}\cdots a_{\ell}
.
If
W_{1}\sim W_{1}^{\prime}
and
W_{2}\sim W_{2}^{\prime}
then
W_{1}W_{2}\sim W_{1}^{\prime}W_{2}^{\prime}
, which implies that we can operate not only on compatible paths but also on homotopy classes of compatible paths.

The
fundamental groupoid
of a graph
X
, denoted by
\fg(X)
, is the set of graph-homotopy classes of
X
with the partial operation defined above.
If
u
is a vertex in
X
, then the fundamental group of
X
at
u
, denoted by
\fg^{u}(X)
, is the set of graph-homotopy classes of closed paths at
u
with concatenation as operation.
We also denote by
\fg^{u,v}(X)
the set of homotopy classes of paths from
u
to
v
.
Observe that
\fg^{u}(X)
is actually a group and that if
W\in\fg^{u,v}
then
\fg^{u}(X)=W\fg^{v}(X)W^{-1}
.

2.2.
Maniplexes and premaniplexes

A
properly
n
-edge-colored graph
is an
n
-valent graph
X
with a coloring
c:D(X)\to\left\{0,\dots,n-1\right\}
such that
c(d_{1})\neq c(d_{2})
whenever
I(d_{1})=I(d_{2})
, and
c(d)=c(d^{-1})
for every dart
d
.
Observe that thanks to this last condition
c
is well defined on edges.
If
d
is a dart (resp. an edge) we say that
a
is an
i
-dart (resp.
i
-edge) if
c(d)=i
.
Notice that each vertex
x
of
X
has a unique
i
-dart
d_{i}
such that
I(d_{i})=x
.
The endpoint of
d_{i}
is the
i
-adjacent
vertex of
x
and it is denoted by
x^{i}
.
Observe that a properly
n
-edge-colored graph cannot have loops but it might have semi-edges or parallel edges.
For a sequence
w=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
with
i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\in\left\{0,\dots,n-1\right\}
a
w
-path is a path
(d_{1},\dots,d_{k})
such that
c(d_{j})=i_{j}
for
j\in\left\{1,\dots,k\right\}
.
In this situation we say that
w
is the
color sequence
of the path.
Observe that a path is completely determined by its initial vertex and its color sequence.

A
premaniplex of rank
n
or
n
-premaniplex

\mathcal{X}
is a properly
n
-edge-colored graph such that every
(i,j,i,j)
-path is closed whenever
i
and
j
are non-consecutive.
If
\mathcal{X}
is also connected and the
(i,j,i,j)
-paths are not only closed but actually alternating
4
-cycles then
\mathcal{X}
is a
maniplex
.

Natural examples of maniplexes are the flag-graphs of polytopes.
In fact, maniplexes were introduced by Wilson
[
30
]
as a common generalization of maps and abstract polytopes.
The flag-graph of a map or an
n
-polytope is a
3
-maniplex or an
n
-maniplex respectively
1
1

1

In
[
30
]
, the indexing is is shifted by 1, so that the flag-graph of a map is called a
2
-maniplex, for example. However, by now the established convention is the one we present here.
.
On the other hand, in
[
9
]
Hubard and Garza-Vargas give a characterization of those maniplexes that are flag-graphs of polytopes.
The notion of premaniplexes is slightly less natural but it conveniently arises when considering the symmetry type graphs of abstract polytopes.
For a polytope
\mathcal{P}
and
\Gamma\leqslant\aut(\mathcal{P})
, the
symmetry type graph
of
\mathcal{P}
with respect to
\Gamma
is the edge-colored graph obtained as the quotient of the flag graph of
\mathcal{P}
by
\Gamma
.
When
\Gamma=\aut(\mathcal{P})
we just call it the
symmetry type graph of
\mathcal{P}
(see
[
5
]
for details).
Premaniplexes are the natural candidates to be symmetry type graphs of abstract polytopes, in the sense that every symmetry type graph is a premaniplex.
Connected premaniplexes are precisely what were called
admissible graphs
in
[
7
]
.

Maniplexes are often thought of as a graph theoretical version of abstract polytopes.
To emphasise this fact, we shall call the vertices of a maniplex
flags
.
If
\mathcal{M}
is an
n
-maniplex and
i\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
, the
i
-faces
of
\mathcal{M}
are the connected components of
\mathcal{M}
after removing the edges of color
i
.
The
(n-1)
-faces are called
facets
.
The set of
i
-faces of
\mathcal{M}
is denoted by
(\mathcal{M})_{i}
in particular,
(\mathcal{M})_{n-1}
denotes the set of facets of
\mathcal{M}
.
More generally, if
0\leqslant k<\ell\leqslant n-1
, the
(k,\ell)
-sections
of
\mathcal{M}
are the connected components of
\mathcal{M}
after removing the edges of color
i
for
i<k
and
i>\ell
.
If
\Phi
is a flag of
\mathcal{M}
and
i\in\left\{0,\dots,n-1\right\}
we denote by
\Phi_{i}
the
i
-face of
\Phi
.
The
(1,n-1)
-sections are called
vertex-figures
.
The notions of faces, facets, sections and vertex-figures extend naturally to premaniplexes.

The
universal string Coxeter group of rank
n
is the group
W^{n}
generated by
r_{0},\dots,r_{n-1}
subject to the following defining relations:

\displaystyle r_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\text{for }i\in\left\{0,\dots,n-1\right\},

\displaystyle(r_{i}r_{j})^{2}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\text{if }\left|i-j\right|>1.

If
\mathcal{X}
is an
n
-premaniplex the group
W^{n}
acts on the vertices of
\mathcal{X}
by

r_{i}x=x^{i}.

The induced permutation group
\mon(\mathcal{X})
is the
monodromy group
(also called the
connection group
) of
\mathcal{X}
and the elements of
\mon(\mathcal{X})
are called
monodromies
.
Note that we evaluate monodromies on the left.
Observe that a path with color sequence
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
determines the element
w=r_{i_{k}}\cdots r_{i_{1}}\in W^{n}
such that if the path starts at
x
then the endpoint is
wx
.

The group
\mon(\mathcal{X})
acts transitively on every connected component of
\mathcal{X}
, hence
\mathcal{X}
is connected if and only if
\mon(\mathcal{X})
is transitive and in this case it is a maniplex if and only if the permutations induced by
r_{i}
and by
r_{i}r_{j}
for
i,j\in\left\{0,\dots,n-1\right\}
are fixed-point-free.

Given two
n
-premaniplexes
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, a
homomorphism

\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
of premaniplexes is a color-preserving graph homomorphism.
This is equivalent to

(r_{i}x)\phi=r_{i}(x\phi)

for every
i\in\left\{0,\dots,n-1\right\}
.
A homomorphism
\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
is a
covering
if for every vertex of
y
of
\mathcal{Y}
there exists a vertex
x
in
\mathcal{X}
such that
x\phi=y
.
Almost every homomorphism is a covering in the sense that if
y
is in the image of
\phi
, then the whole connected component of
\mathcal{Y}
containing
y
is in the image of
\phi
.
More precisely, if
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
lie in the same connected component of
\mathcal{Y}
, then there exists
w\in W^{n}
such that
y_{2}=wy_{1}
.
Hence if
x_{1}\phi=y_{1}
, then

(wx_{1})\phi=w(x_{1}\phi)=wy_{1}=y_{2}.

It follows that every homomorphism is determined by the image of a given vertex of each connected component of
\mathcal{X}
, and if
\mathcal{Y}
is connected, every homomorphism
\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
is a covering.
This notion of coverings has been previously treated in the context of abstract polytopes where they are often called
rap-maps
(see
[
18
, Section 2]
and
[
15
, Section 2D]
)

The notions of isomorphism and automorphism of premaniplexes extend naturally.
If
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are
n
-premaniplexes an
isomorphism

\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
is just a color-preserving isomorphism and an automorphism of
\mathcal{X}
is an isomorphism
\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
.

Automorphisms of (pre)maniplexes are the graph theoretical analogue of automorphisms of abstract polytopes, which in turn generalize geometrical symmetries of convex polytopes and similar structures.
We denote by
\aut(\mathcal{X})
the group of automorphisms of
\mathcal{X}
.
It can be easily seen that if
\mathcal{X}
is connected then the action of
\aut(\mathcal{X})
on
\mathcal{X}
is free.

If the action of
\aut(\mathcal{X})
is also transitive we say that
\mathcal{X}
is
regular
.
We point out that the word
reflexible
has been used for flag transitive maniplexes; we use
regular
as an attempt to unify the terminology with that of abstract polytopes.

Regular abstract polytopes are by far the most studied class of highly symmetric polytopes.
Many of the classical results in the theory of abstract regular polytopes extend naturally to regular maniplexes.
In particular, if
\mathcal{M}
is a regular
n
-maniplex and
\Phi
is a fixed base flag, then for each
i\in\left\{0,\dots,n-1\right\}
there exists a (unique) automorphism
\rho_{i}
such that

\Phi\rho_{i}=r_{i}\Phi.

It can be proved easily that
\aut(\mathcal{M})=\left\langle\rho_{0},\dots,\rho_{n-1}\right\rangle
.

A maniplex
\mathcal{M}
is
chiral
if
\aut(\mathcal{M})
has two orbits on flags in such a way that adjacent flags lie in different orbits.
Chirality is the combinatorial version of a geometric polytope-like object admitting full rotational symmetry but no reflections.
Chiral abstract polytopes were introduced in the 90’s by Schulte and Weiss
[
28
]
and since then they have been extensively studied. See
[
22
]
for a nice review of the basic theory and interesting problems on the theory of chiral polytopes.

A natural consequence of
\aut(\mathcal{M})
acting freely on
\mathcal{M}
is that we can use the number of flag-orbits of
\aut(\mathcal{M})
as a degree of symmetry of a maniplex.
A maniplex
\mathcal{M}
is a
k
-orbit
maniplex if
\aut(\mathcal{M})
has
k
flag-orbits.
Chiral maniplexes are just one of
2^{n-1}
symmetry types of
2
-orbit maniplexes.
The mere existence of maniplexes for all such symmetry types was recently established
[
23
]
.
The theory of
k
-orbit polytopes for
k\geqslant 3
is even less explored.
The possible symmetry types for
3
- and
4
-orbit maniplexes were described in
[
5
]
, where the corresponding automorphism groups were also described.
Very recently, Hubard and the second author established the necessary conditions to build
k
-orbit abstract polytopes as coset geometries (see
[
11
]
).
See
[
7
]
for a review of the basic theory and open problems for
k
-orbit polytopes.

If
\mathcal{X}
is an
n
-premaniplex, the dual of
\mathcal{X}
(often denoted by
\mathcal{X}^{\ast}
) is the premaniplex with the same vertex set and such that two vertices are
i
-adjacent in
\mathcal{X}^{\ast}
if and only if they are
(n-i-1)
-adjacent in
\mathcal{X}
.
Notice that up to a permutation of the color set, if
F
is a facet in
\mathcal{X}
, then the graph induced by the vertices of
F
in
\mathcal{X}^{\ast}
is isomorphic to
F^{\ast}
and it is a vertex-figure of
\mathcal{X}^{\ast}
.

The key notion of this paper is that of
extensions
.
If
\mathcal{M}
is an
(n+1)
-maniplex such that all its facets are isomorphic to a given
n
-maniplex
\mathcal{K}
, then we say that
\mathcal{M}
is an extension of
\mathcal{K}
.
Similarly, if all the vertex figures of
\mathcal{M}
are isomorphic to
\mathcal{K}
we say that
\mathcal{M}
is a
coextension
of
\mathcal{K}
.
Observe that
\mathcal{M}
is a coextension of
\mathcal{K}
if and only if
\mathcal{M}^{\ast}
is an extension of
\mathcal{K}^{\ast}
.
Extensions and coextensions have been deeply studied on the context of regular and chiral polytopes (see
[
4
,
6
,
19
,
21
,
26
,
29
]
, for example).

2.3.
Voltage assignments

If
X
is a graph and
G
is a group, a
voltage assignment
is a function
\xi:\fg(X)\to G
that satisfies
\xi(W_{1}W_{2})=\xi(W_{2})\xi(W_{1})
for every pair of compatible paths
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
.
Notice that traditionally a voltage assignment
\xi
is defined such that
\xi(W_{1}W_{2})=\xi(W_{1})\xi(W_{2})
(cf.
[
14
]
). The reason we do it the other way is because we are considering automorphisms acting on the right and monodromies on the left, as is customary in the polytopes and maniplexes literature.
The group
G
is called the
voltage group
of
\xi
and the pair
(X,\xi)
is called a
voltage graph
.

Since every path can be thought as the product of its darts, we can regard a voltage assignment as a function
\xi:D(X)\to G
such that for every dart
d
,
\xi(d^{-1})=\xi(d)^{-1}
. The voltage of a path
W=d_{1},\dots,d_{k}
is simply
\xi(d_{k})\cdots\xi(d_{1})
.

If
(X,\xi)
is a voltage graph, the
derived graph
is the graph
X^{\xi}
whose vertices and darts are the elements in
V(X)\times G
and
D(X)\times G
, respectively.
The initial vertex of a dart
(d,g)
is
(I(d),g)
and its inverse is
(d^{-1},\xi(d)g)
.
The adjacent vertices of a given vertex
(x,g)
are the vertices
(y,\xi(d)g)
for each dart
d
starting at
x
and ending at
y
.
Equivalently, two vertices
(x,g)
and
(y,h)
are connected if there exists a dart
d
starting at
x
and ending at
y
whose voltage is
hg^{-1}
.

An
elementary (maniplex) move
on a path
W
consists of either adding or removing the same color two times at any two consecutive positions
or swapping two non-consecutive colors in consecutive positions in the color sequence.
More precisely, let
W_{0}
be a path with color sequence
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
and starting vertex
x
.
Assume that
i\in\left\{0,\dots,n-1\right\}
and that
j,\ell\in\left\{1,\dots,k\right\}
with
\ell<k
and
|i_{\ell}-i_{\ell+1}|>1
.
Let
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
be paths starting at
x
and with color sequences
(i_{1},\dots,i_{j},i,i,i_{j+1},\dots,i_{k})
and
(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell-1},i_{\ell+1},i_{\ell},i_{\ell+2}\dots i_{k})
, respectively.
Then
W_{0}\mapsto W_{1}
,
W_{1}\mapsto W_{0}

and
W_{0}\mapsto W_{2}
are elementary maniplex moves.

We say that two paths in a premaniplex are
maniplex-homotopic
if we can turn one into the other by a finite sequence of elementary maniplex moves. When it is clear from context, we will drop the modifier ‘maniplex’ and merely call such paths homotopic.
Observe that two paths in a premaniplex are homotopic if and only if they start at the same vertex and determine the same element of
W^{n}
.
For an
n
-premaniplex
\mathcal{X}
and
x,y
in
\mathcal{X}
we will abuse notation and denote by
\fg(\mathcal{X})
,
\fg^{x}(\mathcal{X})
and
\fg^{x,y}(\mathcal{X})
the fundamental groupoid, the fundamental group and the set of homotopy classes of paths starting at
x
and ending at
y
with respect to maniplex homotopy.
Moreover, if
I\subseteq\left\{0,\dots n-1\right\}
we denote by
\fg_{I}(\mathcal{X})
,
\fg^{x}_{I}(\mathcal{X})
and
\fg^{x,y}_{I}(\mathcal{X})
the corresponding subsets of homotopy-classes containing paths using only colors in
I
.
When using this notation we often write
\overline{I}
instead of
\left\{0,\dots,n-1\right\}\setminus I
and if
I
is an interval, meaning there exist
k\leqslant m
such that
I=\left\{i:k\leqslant i\leqslant m\right\}
we write
[k,m]
instead of
I
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a premaniplex and let
\fg(\mathcal{X})
be its fundamental groupoid.
If
\xi:\fg(\mathcal{X})\to G
is a voltage assignment such that
\xi(W)
is the identity in
G
whenever
W
is a path of length 4 alternating between two non-consecutive colors, we say that the pair
(\mathcal{X},\xi)
is a
voltage premaniplex
.
In other words, a voltage premaniplex is a voltage graph where the voltage of the maniplex homotopy class of a path is well defined.

Our main technique in this paper is to build maniplexes as derived graphs of certain voltage premaniplexes.

3.
Cayley extensions and coextensions

3.1.
Definition and examples

Let
\mathcal{K}
be an
n
-maniplex and
\mathcal{M}
an extension of
\mathcal{K}
.
Let
G\leqslant\Gamma(\mathcal{M})
.
We say that
\mathcal{M}
is a
Cayley extension of
\mathcal{K}
by
G
if
G
acts regularly (that is, transitively and freely) on the set of facets of
\mathcal{M}
.
If we say that
\mathcal{M}
is a Cayley extension of
\mathcal{K}
we mean that it is a Cayley extension by some group
G\leqslant\Gamma(\mathcal{M})
.

Note that if
\mathcal{M}
is a Cayley extension of
\mathcal{K}
, the quotient
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}=\mathcal{M}/G
has only one facet, and that facet is isomorphic to
\mathcal{K}
.
In other words,
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}
consists of a copy of
\mathcal{K}
with some extra edges of color
n
, and satisfying that two darts of color
n
starting at flags in a fixed facet
F
of
\mathcal{K}
also end at some fixed facet
F^{\prime}
.
The monodromy
r_{n}
in
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}
can be thought of as a set of isomorphisms between pairs of (not necessarily distinct) facets of
\mathcal{K}
, in such a way that every facet is paired with exactly one other facet.

If
\mathcal{M}
is a Cayley extension of
\mathcal{K}
we can recover
\mathcal{M}
as the derived graph of a voltage graph
(\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}},\xi)
where
\xi:\Pi(\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}})\to G
satisfies the following properties:

(i)

The voltage
\xi(d)
of a dart
d
is trivial whenever the dart has a color smaller than
n
.

(ii)

If
d
and
d^{\prime}
are two darts of color
n
starting at flags in the same facet of
\mathcal{K}
, then
\xi(d)=\xi(d^{\prime})
.
This means in particular that we may alternatively think of
\xi
as a function from the set
(\mathcal{K})_{n-1}

of facets of
\mathcal{K}
to the voltage group
G
.

Definition 3.1
.

A
Cayley extender
is a triple
(\mathcal{K},r_{n},\xi)
where:

(i)

\mathcal{K}
is an
n
-maniplex,

(ii)

r_{n}
is a permutation of the flags of
\mathcal{K}
satisfying that
r_{n}^{2}=1
and that if
F
is a facet of
\mathcal{K}
, then, the restriction
{\left.\kern-1.2ptr_{n}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{F}}
is an isomorphism between
F
and
r_{n}(F)
. (In other words,
r_{n}
commutes with
\langle r_{0},\ldots,r_{n-2}\rangle
),

(iii)

There is a group
G
, called the
voltage group of
(\mathcal{K},r_{n},\xi)
, such that
\xi:(\mathcal{K})_{n-1}\to G
is a function satisfying that
\xi(r_{n}(F))=\xi(F)^{-1}
.

Additionally, a pair
(\mathcal{K},r_{n})
satisfying Items
(i)
and
(ii)
above is called a
pre-extender
of
\mathcal{K}
.

Given a Cayley extender we can construct a voltage premaniplex
(\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}},\xi)

as follows:
the flags of
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}
are the flags of
\mathcal{K}
and for
i<n
the
i
-adjacencies on
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}
are the same as in
\mathcal{K}
.
For a flag
\Phi
in
\mathcal{K}
, its
n
-adjacent flag is
r_{n}\Phi
.
Finally, the voltage
\xi(d)
is
\xi(I(d)_{n-1})
, if
d
has color
n
and
1
otherwise. That is, the voltage of a dart of color
n
is the voltage of the facet at which it starts, and all other darts have trivial voltage.
The premaniplex
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}
is called a
pre-extension
of
\mathcal{K}
.
Note that the automorphism group
\Gamma(\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}})
consists of all the automorphisms of
\mathcal{K}
that commute with the monodromy
r_{n}
and it may be a proper subgroup of
\Gamma(\cal K)
.

Notice that the premaniplex
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}
is regular if and only if
\mathcal{K}
is regular and there exists
w\in W^{n}
such that the
n
-adjacent flag of
\Phi
is
w\Phi
for every
\Phi
in
\mathcal{K}
.
More generally if
\mathcal{K}
is a
k
-orbit maniplex with orbits
O_{1},\dots,O_{k}
, then
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}
has
k
orbits (hence
\Gamma(\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}})
coincides with
\Gamma(\cal K)
) if and only if it is possible to chose
k
elements
w_{1},\dots,w_{k}\in W^{n}
such that
\Phi^{n}=w_{i}\Phi
whenever
\Phi\in O_{i}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

The derived graph
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
will be a maniplex if and only if:

(i)

The set
\xi(\mathcal{K}_{n-1})=\{\xi(F):F\in(\mathcal{K})_{n-1}\}
is a generating set for
G
.

(ii)

If
{\left.\kern-1.2ptr_{n}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{F}}
is the identity for some facet
F
, or if
r_{n}\Phi
is adjacent to
\Phi
for some flag
\Phi
in
F
, then
\xi(F)\neq 1
.

Proof.

The first condition ensures that the graph is connected, and the second condition ensures that
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
has no semi-edges or parallel edges.
∎

From now on, we will assume that our Cayley extenders satisfy the conditions of Proposition
3.2
.

Proposition 3.3
.

A maniplex
\mathcal{M}
is a Cayley extension of
\mathcal{K}
by
G
if and only if
\mathcal{M}\cong\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
for some Cayley extender
(\mathcal{K},r_{n},\xi)
with voltage group
G
.

Proof.

First, suppose that
\mathcal{M}
is a Cayley extension of
\mathcal{K}
by
G
, where
\mathcal{K}
is an
n
-maniplex.
In other words,
G
acts regularly on the facets of
\mathcal{M}
.
Let
\mathcal{K}
be a particular facet of
\mathcal{M}
.
Then the quotient
\mathcal{M}/G
is isomorphic to a pre-maniplex
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}
; a copy of
\mathcal{K}
with added edges of color
n
.
For each facet
F
of
\mathcal{K}
(which is a subfacet of
\mathcal{M}
) let
\xi(F)
be the automorphism in
G
that maps
\mathcal{K}
to the facet that shares
F
with it.
Then
\mathcal{M}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
.

Conversely, suppose that
\mathcal{M}\cong\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
.
Note that the facets of
\mathcal{M}
are of the form
(\mathcal{K},\gamma):=\{(\Phi,\gamma):\Phi\in\mathcal{K}\}
for some
\gamma\in G
, each of which is isomorphic to
\mathcal{K}
. Note also that an element
\alpha\in G
maps the facet
(\mathcal{K},\gamma)
to the facet
(\mathcal{K},\gamma\alpha)
.
From this it follows immediately that
G
acts regularly on the facets of
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
, and so
\mathcal{M}
is a Cayley extension of
\mathcal{K}
by
G
.
∎

A more intuitive way of constructing
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
is the following:
Let
\{(\mathcal{K},\gamma):\gamma\in G\}
be a family of copies of
\mathcal{K}
.
These will be the facets of
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
.
Then, for each facet
F
of
\mathcal{K}
and for each
\gamma\in G
, glue the copy of
F
in
(\mathcal{K},\gamma)
to the copy of
r_{n}(F)
in
(\mathcal{K},\xi(F)\gamma)
using
r_{n}
as gluing instructions.

A special case of Cayley extenders are those in which
r_{n}=Id
. We will call such an extender a
canonical Cayley extender
, and will omit
r_{n}
in the notation. If we say that
(\mathcal{K},\xi)

(\mathcal{K},Id,\xi)
.
If
(\mathcal{K},\xi)

\mathcal{K}^{\xi}
instead of
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
for the corresponding Cayley extension.

Example 3.7
.

For a maniplex
\mathcal{K}
, let
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and let
\xi:(\mathcal{K})_{n-1}\to\mathbb{Z}_{2}
be constant equal to 1. Then
\mathcal{K}^{\xi}
is the ditope
\{K,2\}
.
This is also often called the
trivial extension
of
\mathcal{K}
.

Now let us give an ‘external’ characterization of canonical Cayley extensions.

Theorem 3.8
.

An
(n+1)
-maniplex
\mathcal{M}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{K}^{\xi}
for some canonical Cayley extender
(\mathcal{K},\xi)
if and only if there is a group
G\leqslant\Gamma(\mathcal{M})
acting regularly on the facets of
\mathcal{M}
such that for every flag
\Phi
in
\mathcal{M}
there is an automorphism
\gamma\in G
such that
\Phi^{n}=\Phi\gamma
.

Proof.

Proposition
3.3
establishes that
\mathcal{M}
is a Cayley extension of
\mathcal{K}
if and only if
\mathcal{M}\cong\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
for some Cayley extender
(\mathcal{K},r_{n},\xi)
. If
\mathcal{M}\cong\mathcal{K}^{\xi}
, then the voltage group
G
acts regularly on the facets of
\mathcal{M}
.
Now, a typical flag of
\mathcal{M}
has the form
(\Psi,\alpha)
with
\Psi
a flag of
\mathcal{K}
and
\alpha\in G
.
Furthermore,
(\Psi,\alpha)^{n}=(\Psi,\xi(\Phi_{n})\alpha)=(\Psi,\alpha)(\alpha^{-1}\xi(\Phi_{n})\alpha)
, and so
\gamma=\alpha^{-1}\xi(\Phi_{n})\alpha
has the desired property.

Conversely, suppose that
G
acts regularly on the facets of
\mathcal{M}
and has the given property.
Then the facets of
\mathcal{M}
are all isomorphic to some maniplex
\mathcal{K}
, and by definition,
\mathcal{M}
is a Cayley extension of
\mathcal{K}
by
G
.
Again, a typical flag of
\mathcal{M}
has the form
(\Psi,\alpha)
.
By assumption, there is
\gamma\in G
such that
(\Psi,\alpha)^{n}=(\Psi,\alpha)\gamma=(\Psi,\alpha\gamma)
.
Since the first coordinate of
(\Psi,\alpha)^{n}
is
r_{n}\Psi
, then
r_{n}\Psi=\Psi
, and since
\Psi
was arbitrary, we get
r_{n}=Id
, which is what we wanted to prove.

∎

In light of Theorem
3.8
, we will say that
\mathcal{M}
is a
canonical Cayley extension (of
\mathcal{K}
)
if there is a subgroup
G\leqslant\Gamma(\mathcal{M})
acting regularly on the facets of
\mathcal{M}
such that for every flag
\Phi
in
\mathcal{M}
there is an automorphism
\gamma\in G
such that
\Phi^{n-1}=\Phi\gamma
.

Remark 3.9
.

Since chiral maniplexes do not have any automorphisms that send
\Phi
to
\Phi^{n-1}
, it follows that no chiral maniplex is a canonical Cayley extension.
However, as pointed out before, all chiral toroidal maps of type
\{4,4\}
are (non-canonical) Cayley extensions of the square (see Example
3.6
).

If
\mathcal{M}
is regular, then we know exactly which subgroup of
\Gamma(\mathcal{M})
we need for Theorem
3.8
.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a regular maniplex with base flag
\Phi
and with automorphism group
\langle\rho_{0},\ldots,\rho_{n-1}\rangle
. Let
N^{+}_{n-1}
be the normal closure of
\langle\rho_{n-1}\rangle
in
\Gamma(\mathcal{M})
.
Then
\mathcal{M}
is a canonical Cayley extension if and only if
N^{+}_{n-1}
acts regularly on the facets of
\mathcal{M}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{M}
is regular, for each flag
\Psi
there is an automorphism in
N^{+}_{n-1}
that maps
\Psi
to
\Psi^{n-1}
. So if
N^{+}_{n-1}
acts regularly on the facets, then clearly
\mathcal{M}
is a canonical Cayley extension. Conversely, if
\mathcal{M}
is a canonical Cayley extension with
G
acting regularly on the facets, then
G
must contain
N^{+}_{n-1}
. Now,
N^{+}_{n-1}
contains automorphisms that map each facet to each of its adjacent facets (that is, facets that share a subfacet), and by connectivity,
N^{+}_{n-1}
acts transitively on the facets. It follows that
\Gamma=N^{+}_{n-1}
.
∎

Let us show that the notion of canonical Cayley extensions is a generalization of the Flat Amalgamation Property to general maniplexes
[
15
, Sec. 4E]
.

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a regular polytope with facets isomorphic to
\mathcal{K}
.
Then
\mathcal{P}
satisfies the Flat Amalgamation Property with respect to its facets if and only if it is a canonical Cayley extension.

Proof.

Let
\langle\rho_{0},\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{n-1}\rangle
be the distinguished generators of
\aut(\mathcal{P})
with respect to a base flag
\Phi
.
Then
\mathcal{P}
satisfies the Flat Amalgamation Property with respect to its facets if and only if
\Gamma(\mathcal{P})=N^{+}_{n-1}\rtimes\Gamma_{n-1}
where
N^{+}_{n-1}
is the normal closure of the group generated by
\rho_{n-1}
, and
\Gamma_{n-1}
is the stabilizer of the base facet
F=\Phi_{n-1}
.

Now, note that
\Gamma_{n-1}
is generated by
\rho_{0},\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{n-2}
and that
N^{+}_{n-1}
is generated by the elements of the form
\rho_{n-1}^{\alpha}=\alpha^{-1}\rho_{n-1}\alpha
. Note also that
\rho_{n-1}^{\alpha}
maps
\Phi\alpha
to
(\Phi\alpha)^{n-1}
.
In general
\aut(\mathcal{P})=N^{+}_{n-1}\Gamma_{n-1}
and
N^{+}_{n-1}
is normal in
\aut(\mathcal{P})
. It is also true that
N^{+}_{n-1}
acts transitively on the facets of
\mathcal{P}
. So
\aut(\mathcal{P})=N^{+}_{n-1}\rtimes\Gamma_{n-1}
if and only if
N^{+}_{n-1}
acts freely (and thus regularly) on the facets of
\mathcal{P}
.
The result then follows from Proposition
3.10
.
∎

Recall that a maniplex
\mathcal{M}
is a
coextension
of
\mathcal{K}
if its dual
\mathcal{M}^{*}
is an extension of
\mathcal{K}^{*}
, or in other words, if all vertex figures of
\mathcal{M}
are isomorphic to
\mathcal{K}
.

We can define a
Cayley coextender

(\mathcal{K},r_{-1},\xi)
analogously to a Cayley extender by replacing the facets of
\mathcal{K}
by its
0
-faces (vertices) as the domain of
\xi
, and defining that
r_{-1}
pairs 0-faces of
\mathcal{K}
by isomorphisms between the vertex figures.
If
(\mathcal{K},r_{-1},\xi)
is a Cayley coextender, then the 1-skeleton of
\mathcal{K}_{r_{-1}}^{\xi}
is the Cayley graph of
G
with respect to the set
\xi(\mathcal{K}_{0})
(where
\mathcal{K}_{0}
denotes the set of vertices of
\mathcal{K}
).
In analogy with canonical Cayley extenders, we define a
canonical Cayley coextender
to be the Cayley coextender with
r_{-1}=Id
. As a mild abuse of notation, we write
\mathcal{K}^{\xi}
instead
of writing
\mathcal{K}_{r_{-1}}^{\xi}
.
Then, to know whether
\mathcal{K}^{\xi}
denotes an extension or a coextension of
\mathcal{K}
, one only needs to know whether the domain of
\xi
is the facets or the 0-faces of
\mathcal{K}
.

Example 3.12
.

Let
G
be a group generated by a (multi-)set
S=\{s_{0},s_{1},\ldots,s_{n-1}\}
of
n
involutions and let
\mathcal{K}
be an
n
-simplex. Number the vertices of
\mathcal{K}
using
\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
and let
\xi
assign
s_{i}
to vertex
i
(in this case
(\mathcal{K},\xi)
is a canonical Cayley coextender). Then
\mathcal{K}^{\xi}
is the colorful polytope obtained from the Cayley graph of
\Gamma
with respect to
S
(see
[
1
]
).

3.2.
Polytopality of Cayley extensions

Of course, a natural question when building maniplexes is to determine whether or not they are (flag graphs of) abstract polytopes.
The maniplexes that are polytopes are precisely those that satisfy the
path intersection property
(see
[
9
]
).
The following result is a voltage version of such result.

Theorem 3.13
.

(
[
11
, Theorem 4.2]
)
Let
\mathcal{X}
be an
n
-premaniplex and let
\xi:\fg(\mathcal{X})\to G
be a voltage assignment such that
\mathcal{X}^{\xi}
is a maniplex. Then
\mathcal{X}^{\xi}
is the flag graph of a polytope if and only if

(3.1)

\xi(\fg^{x,y}_{[k,n-1]}(\mathcal{X}))\cap\xi(\fg^{x,y}_{[0,m]}(\mathcal{X}))=\xi(\fg^{x,y}_{[k,m]}(\mathcal{X})),

for all
k,m\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
and all vertices
x,y
in
\mathcal{X}
.

Proposition 3.14
.

Let
(\mathcal{K},r_{n},\xi)
be a Cayley extender. Let
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}
be the premaniplex obtained from adding edges of color
n
according to
r_{n}
. Then
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
is a polytope if and only if the following two conditions hold:

(i)

\mathcal{K}
is a polytope, and

(ii)

if
\{W\in\fg^{\Phi,\Psi}_{[k,n]}(\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}):\xi(W)=1\}
is non-empty, then
\fg^{\Phi,\Psi}_{[k,n-1]}\left(\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}\right)
is also non-empty.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
is a polytope. Obviously
\mathcal{K}
is a polytope since the facets of
\mathcal{K}^{\xi}_{r_{n}}
are isomorphic to
\mathcal{K}
.
The second condition is a consequence of Theorem
3.13
.
Indeed, since
\mathcal{K}
is connected, then there exists a path
V
in
\mathcal{K}
connecting
\Phi
and
\Psi
. The path
V
can be thought as a path in
\fg_{[0,n-1]}^{\Phi,\Psi}\left(\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}\right)
.
Observe that
\xi(V)=1
, hence Equation (
3.1
) implies that

\xi\left(\fg_{[k,n-1]}^{\Phi,\Psi}\left(\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}\right)\right)=\xi\left(\fg_{[k,n]}^{\Phi,\Psi}\left(\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}\right)\right)\cap\xi\left(\fg_{[0,n-1]}^{\Phi,\Psi}\left(\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}\right)\right)\neq\emptyset,

which in turn implies that
\fg_{[k,n-1]}^{\Phi,\Psi}\left(\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}\right)
is non-empty.

Suppose
\mathcal{K}
is a polytope and that the second condition holds.
Let
\Phi,\Psi
be flags in
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}
and let
W\in\Pi^{\Phi,\Psi}_{[0,m]}\left(\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}\right)
and
V\in\Pi^{\Phi,\Psi}_{[k,n]}\left(\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}\right)
.
Suppose
\xi(W)=\xi(V)
.
Without loss of generality we can assume
k>0
and
m<n
, so this means that
\xi(W)=\xi(V)=1
.
Because of our hypothesis, there is a path
W^{\prime}\in\Pi^{\Phi,\Psi}_{[k,n-1]}\left(\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}\right)
.
Since
\mathcal{K}
is a polytope, from
V
and
W^{\prime}
we get a path
Z\in\Pi^{\Phi,\Psi}_{[k,m]}\left(\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}\right)
. Trivially
\xi(Z)=\xi(V)=\xi(W)=1
, so by Theorem
3.13
, we get that
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
is polytopal.
∎

Corollary 3.15
.

Let
(\mathcal{K},\xi)
be a canonical Cayley extender. Then
\mathcal{K}^{\xi}
is a polytope if and only if
\mathcal{K}
is a polytope.

Proof.

Use the previous theorem, considering the fact that if we delete the edges of color
n
of a path from
\Phi
to
\Psi
then we obtain another such path.
∎

Next, let us show that a group epimorphism from the voltage group of a Cayley extender naturally induces a covering of maniplexes.

Proposition 3.16
.

Let
(\mathcal{K},r_{n},\xi)
be a Cayley extender with
\xi:(\mathcal{K})_{n-1}\to G
. Let
\pi:G\to H
be a group epimorphism and let
\xi^{\prime}:(\mathcal{K})_{n-1}\to H
be defined by
\xi^{\prime}(F)=(\xi(F))\pi
.
Then
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
covers
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

Let us abuse notation and define
\pi:\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}\to\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi^{\prime}}
as
(\Phi,\gamma)\pi=(\Phi,\gamma\pi)
. To prove that this is a maniplex homomorphism, we need to show that
\pi
commutes with each
r_{i}
. This is clear, since
\pi
only multiplies the second coordinate on the right by
\pi
, whereas
r_{i}
modifies the first coordinate and, if
i=n
, multiplies the second coordinate on the left.
∎

3.3.
Flat amalgamations

Let us now consider the following problem, related to the ideas in
[
15
, Sec. 4F]
. A maniplex is
flat
if every facet is incident to every vertex. Let
\mathcal{K}
be an
n
-maniplex, and suppose that we want to construct a flat
(n+2)
-maniplex
\mathcal{M}
with medial sections (facets of vertex-figures) isomorphic to
\mathcal{K}
. Then the facets of
\mathcal{M}
will be isomorphic to a coextension
\cal X
of
\mathcal{K}
and the vertex-figures of
\mathcal{M}
will be an extension
\cal Y
of
\mathcal{K}
.
We say that
\cal M
is an
amalgamation of
\cal X
and
\cal Y
.
Given two
n+1
maniplexes
\cal X
and
\cal Y
, such that all the vertex figures of
\cal X
and the facets of
\cal Y
are isomorphic, it is natural to ask if there is an amalgamation of
\cal X
and
\cal Y
. If we consider Cayley coextensions and extensions, then there is a natural sufficient condition for a flat amalgamation to exist:

Theorem 3.17
.

Let
(\cal K,r_{n},\xi)
be a Cayley extender with voltage group
G
and
(\cal K,r_{-1},\xi^{\prime})
be a Cayley co-extender with voltage group
G^{\prime}
.
Suppose that
(r_{n}r_{-1})^{2}
acts trivially on the flags of
\cal K
.
Then, there exists a flat
amalgamation of
\cal K_{r_{-1}}^{\xi^{\prime}}
and
\cal K_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
.
If both
\cal K_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
and
\cal K_{r_{-1}}^{\xi^{\prime}}
(and therefore
\cal K
) are polytopal, then the amalgamation is also polytopal.

Proof.

Consider the premaniplex
\cal K_{r_{-1}\wedge r_{n}}
obtained from shifting the colors of
\cal K
up by 1, adding edges of colors 0 according to
r_{-1}
and edges of color
n+1
according to
r_{n}
. Since
(r_{n}r_{-1})^{2}
is the identity we know that
\cal K_{r_{-1}\wedge r_{n}}
is in fact a premaniplex.
Consider the voltage group
G^{\prime}\times G
and for every flag
\Phi
in
\cal K
assign voltages
(\xi^{\prime}(\Phi_{0}),1)
to the dart of color 0 starting at
\Phi
and
(1,\xi(\Phi_{n-1}))
to the dart of color
n+1
starting at
\Phi
. Assign trivial voltage to every dart with color between 1 and
n
. Let
\cal M
be the derived graph of
\cal K_{r_{-1}\wedge r_{n}}
with this voltage assignment.

It is easy to see that
\cal M
has copies of
\cal K_{r_{-1}}^{\xi^{\prime}}
as facets and copies of
\cal K_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
as vertex figures.

Let us show that
\cal K_{r_{-1}\wedge r_{n}}
is a maniplex, using Proposition
3.2
.
Since we are assuming that
\mathcal{K}_{r_{-1}}^{\xi^{\prime}}
and
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
are maniplexes, Proposition
3.2
implies that the possible semiedges, as well as the edges of color 0 and
n+1
that are parallel to some other edge, cannot have trivial voltage.
Also, since
\{\xi^{\prime}(v):v\in\cal K_{0}\}
is a generating set for
G^{\prime}
and
\{\xi(F):F\in(\cal K)_{n-1}\}
is a generating set for
G
, then
\{(\xi^{\prime}(v),1):v\in\mathcal{K}_{0}\}\cup\{(1,\xi(F)):F\in(\mathcal{K})_{n-1}\}
is a generating set for
G^{\prime}\times G
. These two facts imply that
\mathcal{M}
is in fact a maniplex.

The set of flags of the derived graph can be written as
\cal K\times G^{\prime}\times G
. When we remove the edges of color
n+1
we get one connected component for each element
\gamma\in G
, namely
\cal K\times G^{\prime}\times\{\gamma\}
. Analogously, when we erase the edges of color
0
we get components of the form
\cal K\times\{\gamma^{\prime}\}\times G
. The vertex
\cal K\times G^{\prime}\times\{\gamma\}
and the facet
\cal K\times\{\gamma^{\prime}\}\times G
intersect in the medial section
\cal K\times\{\gamma^{\prime}\}\times\{\gamma\}
, proving that
\cal M
is flat.

Since the vertex figures of
\cal M
are isomorphic to
\cal K_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
and the facets are isomorphic to
\cal K_{r_{1}}^{\xi^{\prime}}
, we get that if
\cal M
is polytopal, then
\cal K_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
and
\cal K_{r_{1}}^{\xi^{\prime}}
are polytopal as well.
Conversely, suppose that both
\cal K_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
and
\cal K_{r_{1}}^{\xi^{\prime}}
are polytopal. Let
W
be a path in
\cal K_{r_{-1}\wedge r_{n}}
with colors in
[0,m]
and
V
be a path with colors in
[k,n+1]
with the same end-points. We may assume without loss of generality that
k>0
and
m<n+1
; this implies that the voltage of
W
lies in
\Gamma^{\prime}\times\{1\}
and the voltage of
V
lies in
\{1\}\times\Gamma
. Therefore, if
W
and
V
have the same voltage, they have voltage
(1,1)
.
If we erase the edges of color
n+1
from
\cal K_{r_{-1}\wedge r_{n}}
we get exactly
\cal K_{r_{-1}}
, so we can apply the dual of Proposition
3.14
to
W
and get a path
W^{\prime}
with the same endpoints as
W
but with colors in
[1,m]
.
If instead we erase the edges of color 0 we get a shifted copy of
\cal K_{r_{n}}
, so by applying Proposition
3.14
we get a path
V^{\prime}
with the same endpoints and colors in
[k,n]
(remember colors are shifted by 1).
The paths
W^{\prime}
and
V^{\prime}
have also the same voltage
(1,1)
, and since they lie in (a shifted)
\cal K
, there is a path
Z
with colors in
[k,m]
with the same endpoints (and also voltage
(1,1)
). Then Theorem
3.13
proves that the derived graph is polytopal.
∎

Corollary 3.18
.

If
\cal M
is a Cayley extension of
\cal K
and
\cal M^{\prime}
is a Cayley coextension of
\cal K
, and one of them is canonical, then there is a flat amalgamation of
\cal M
and
\cal M^{\prime}
.

Proof.

If
\cal M
is canonical, then
\cal M=\cal K_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
where
r_{n}=Id
. Since the identity commutes with any choice for
r_{-1}
, using Theorem
3.17
we are done.
The proof when
r_{-1}=Id
is identical.
∎

Theorem
3.17
gives us a way to know whether there is a flat amalgamation of a Cayley extension and a Cayley coextension of the same polytope. One only has to check if the matching
r_{-1}
induced by the co-extension commutes with the matching
r_{n}
induced by the extension.

In particular, we let both
\cal M
and
\cal M^{\prime}
be regular, canonical Cayley (co)-extensions of
\cal K
then Corollary
3.18
is just

[
27
, Theorem 2]
(see also
[
15
, Corollary 4F11]
).

Classic examples and universal Cayley extensions

We have already seen that the ditope over

In this section we will explore these examples and use them as motivation to construct several Cayley extensions with certain universal properties.

Example 4.1

Suppose that
\mathcal{K}
is facet-bipartite. Let

\xi
as a proper
2
-coloring of the facet graph of
\mathcal{K}
, using colors
x
and

. Then
\mathcal{K}^{\xi}
is the

denoted
\mathcal{K}|2m
in
[
4
]
.

Example 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a
n
-maniplex such that for every flag
\Phi
of
\mathcal{K}
the facets of
\Phi
and
\Phi^{n-1}
are different. (In other words, no facet is “glued to itself”.) Let
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\mathcal{K}_{n-1}}
, and let
\overline{\mathrm{e}}_{F}
denote the vector that has a

in the coordinate corresponding to the facet

Example 4.3
.

More generally, if
c

k

and set
\xi(F)=\overline{\mathrm{e}}_{c(F)}
. Then

is a “color-coded extension” of

.
Note that Example

Example 4.4
.

Now let us describe another way to generalize Example

. Let
\mathcal{K}
be a
n
-maniplex such that for every flag
\Phi
of
\mathcal{K}
the facets of
\Phi
and
\Phi^{n-1}
are different. Let
\{F_{j}:0\leqslant j<m\}
be a labelling of the set of facets of
\mathcal{K}
so that

is the base facet. Choose
s\in\mathbb{N}
such that
s\geqslant 2
and let

A=\bigoplus_{0\leqslant j<m}\mathbb{Z}_{s}=\big\{\overline{\mathrm{x}}=(x_{j})_{0\leqslant j<m}:x_{j}=0\text{ for all but finitely many }j<m\big\}.

Let

Let

for every

.
Consider the vectors
\overline{\mathrm{a}}_{j}=\overline{\mathrm{e}}_{j}-\overline{\mathrm{e}}_{0}\in U
, where
\overline{\mathrm{e}}_{i}
denotes the vector of

with

and every other entry equal to

.
Note that
\overline{\mathrm{a}}_{j}=(-1,\dots,0,1,0\dots)

and that
\overline{\mathrm{a}}_{0}=0
.
Observe that the set
\{(\overline{\mathrm{a}}_{j},\chi):0\leqslant j<m\}
is a generating set of involutions for

\xi(F_{j})=(\overline{\mathrm{a}}_{j},\chi)

).

4.1.
Universal extensions

Given a pre-extender
(\cal K,r_{n})
, we can complete it to make a Cayley extender whose derived graph is the freest extension of that type (meaning the freest extension with that extender as its quotient by the action of the voltage group).

Definition 4.5
.

The
universal Cayley extension of
\mathcal{K}
with respect to
r_{n}
, denoted by

\cal U(\cal K,r_{n})
, is the maniplex
\cal K_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
, where

G(\cal K,r_{n})=\langle\{\alpha_{F}\}_{F\in(\cal K)_{n-1}}:\alpha_{F}\alpha_{r_{n}F}=1\rangle,

and
\xi(F)=\alpha_{F}
.

In other words,
G(\cal K,r_{n})
is the freest group generated by one element of infinite order for each pair
\{F,r_{n}F\}
with
F\neq r_{n}F
and one involution for each facet fixed by
r_{n}
.
Using Proposition
3.16
, we can see that indeed,
\cal U(\cal K,r_{n})
covers all the Cayley extensions of
\cal K
having
(\cal K,r_{n})
as the underlying pre-extender.
Note that despite not being necessarily free,
G(\cal K,r_{n})
has the property that each element can be written uniquely as a reduced word in
\{\alpha_{F}\}_{F\in(\cal K)_{n-1}}
.

A particularly nice case is when
r_{n}=Id
, in which case we get the universal canonical Cayley extension of
\mathcal{K}
, which we denote by
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{K})
.

Proposition 4.6
.

If
\cal K
is polytopal then
\cal U(\cal K,r_{n})
is polytopal.

Proof.

Let
W
be a path in
\cal K_{r_{n}}
with voltage 1 and colors in
[k,n]
.
By Proposition
3.14
, it suffices to show that there is a path (with voltage 1)
with the same starting and end point as
W
and with colors in
[k,n-1]
.
We may write
W
as
W=W_{0}d_{1}W_{1}d_{2}\ldots d_{k}W_{k}
where
W_{i}
uses colors in
[k,n-1]
and
d_{i}
has color
n
for each
i
. Then
1=\xi(W)=\xi(d_{k})\xi(d_{k-1})\ldots\xi(d_{1})
.
Because of the presentation of
G(\mathcal{K},r_{n})
the fact that
\xi(d_{k})\xi(d_{k-1})\ldots\xi(d_{1})=1
implies that there are two consecutive terms that are inverse to one another.
Let
j
be such that
\xi(d_{j})=\xi(d_{j+1})^{-1}
. This means that
d_{j}
starts at the same facet
F
of
\mathcal{K}
as
d_{j+1}^{-1}
. Then, the endpoint
\Phi
of
d_{j}
and the starting point
\Psi
of
d_{j+1}
are connected by the path
W_{j}
with colors in
[k,n-1]
and also share a facet (namely,
r_{n}F
). But, since
\cal K
is polytopal, this means that there is a path
V
with colors in
[k,n-2]
that goes from
\Phi
to
\Psi
.
Then, since
{\left.\kern-1.2ptr_{n}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{F}}
is an isomorphism, the path
r_{n}(V)
(the image of
V
under this isomorphism) is a path with colors in
[k,n-2]
that connects the starting point of
d_{j}
with the endpoint of
d_{j+1}
(see Figure
3
).
Now we consider the path
W^{\prime}=W_{0}d_{1}\ldots d_{j-1}W_{j-1}r_{n}(V)W_{j+1}d_{j+1}\ldots W_{k}
, which has the same end points and voltage as
W
, and it uses colors in
[k,n]
, but it uses 2 darts of color
n
less than
W
.
If we do the same procedure to
W^{\prime}
and repeat the process
k/2
times (any trivial word in
G(\mathcal{K},r_{n})
must have even length), we get a path of trivial voltage with the same end points as
W
that does not use any dart of color
n
, proving the claim.
∎

Now let us see how we can adapt universal extensions to describe other classes of natural extensions. We start by considering the order of
r_{n-1}r_{n}
in
\Mon(\mathcal{K}^{\xi})
(we are slightly abusing notation and identifying
r_{i}\in W^{n}
with the induced permutation of flags of
\mathcal{K}^{\xi}
).

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
(\mathcal{K},\xi)
be a canonical Cayley extender with voltage group

G=\langle\{\alpha_{F}\}_{F\in(\cal K)_{n-1}}\rangle

where
\alpha_{F}=\xi(F)
for each facet
F
. Let
\Mon(\cal K^{\xi})=\langle r_{0},\ldots,r_{n}\rangle
. Then the order of
r_{n-1}r_{n}
is twice the least common multiple of the orders of all
\alpha_{F^{\prime}}\alpha_{F}
such that
F
shares a subfacet with
F^{\prime}
.

Proof.

For each flag
(\Phi,x)
we have that
(r_{n}r_{n-1})^{2}(\Phi,x)=(\Phi,\alpha_{F}\alpha_{F^{\prime}}x)
,
where
F
is the facet of
\Phi
and
F^{\prime}
is the facet of
\Phi^{n-1}
. In particular, since
\Phi
and
\Phi^{n-1}
have the same subfacet,
F
and
F^{\prime}
share a subfacet, and the result follows.
∎

Now, the maniplex
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{K})
has voltage group

G=\langle\{\alpha_{F}\}_{F\in(\cal K)_{n-1}}:\alpha_{F}^{2}=1\rangle.

Then, for each pair of facets
F
and
F^{\prime}
that share a subfacet, the element
\alpha_{F}\alpha_{F^{\prime}}
has infinite order, and so Proposition

implies that

has infinite order. Suppose that we instead wanted

to have order

for some

for each pair of facets

and

that share a subfacet.

that uses this group by
\cal U_{2s}(\cal K)

, if
\cal K
is a polytope, then so is

In other words, the product
\alpha_{F}\alpha_{F^{\prime}}
has order
s
regardless of whether the facets
F
and
F^{\prime}
share a subfacet. We will denote this extension by
\cal U_{2s}^{\prime}(\cal K)

\cal U_{4}^{\prime}(\cal K)\cong\hat{2}^{\cal K}
.

5.
Symmetries of Cayley extensions

Our final goal is to determine the automorphism group and the symmetry type graph of a Cayley extension. We start with the following result which essentially states that if an automorphism projects locally, then it projects completely.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{X}
be connected premaniplexes and let
p:\cal M\to\cal X
be a covering. Let
\widetilde{\tau}
be an automorphism of
\cal M
. If there exists a flag
\Phi
in
\cal M
and an automorphism
\tau
of
\cal X
such that
\Phi\widetilde{\tau}p=\Phi p\tau
, then
\widetilde{\tau}
projects to
\tau
, that is
\widetilde{\tau}p=p\tau
.

Proof.

Let
\Psi
be any flag of
\cal M
. Then there is a monodromy
w
such that
\Psi=w\Phi
. Then,

\displaystyle\Psi\widetilde{\tau}p

\displaystyle=(w\Phi)(\widetilde{\tau}p)

\displaystyle=w(\Phi\widetilde{\tau}p)

\displaystyle=w(\Phi p\tau)

\displaystyle=(w\Phi)(p\tau)

\displaystyle=\Psi p\tau.

∎

Corollary 5.2
.

If
p:\cal M\to\cal X
is a covering and
\cal X
is regular, every automorphism of
\cal M
projects to
\cal X
.

It is clear that in general,
\Gamma(\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}})
is a subgroup of
\Gamma(\mathcal{K})
; the edges that we add with label
n
may disrupt some of the symmetry of
\mathcal{K}
. Let us consider what we can say about the automorphisms of a Cayley extension when
\Gamma(\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}})=\Gamma(\mathcal{K})
.

Proposition 5.3
.

If
\Gamma(\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}})=\Gamma(\mathcal{K})
, then every automorphism of
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
projects to
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}
.

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{\tau}
be an automorphism of
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
and let
p:\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}\to\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}
be the natural projection.
Let
(\Phi,\alpha)
be any flag of
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
and let
(\Psi,\beta)=(\Phi,\alpha)\widetilde{\tau}
. Then
(\Phi,\alpha)\widetilde{\tau}\beta^{-1}\alpha=(\Psi,\alpha)
.
This means that
\widetilde{\tau}\beta^{-1}\alpha
stabilizes the facet
(\mathcal{K},\alpha)
, which is isomorphic to
\mathcal{K}
.
Therefore, there is an automorphism
\tau
of
\mathcal{K}
that maps
\Phi
to
\Psi
, and by hypothesis,
\tau
is also an automorphism of
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}
. Proposition
5.1
finishes the proof.
∎

Proposition
5.3
lets us find the whole automorphism group and symmetry type graph of certain Cayley extensions. To do so, we will use the following Theorem:

Theorem 5.4

(
[
13
, Corollary 5.2]
)
.

Let
X
be a graph,
\xi:\Pi(X)\to G
a voltage assignment,
A
a subgroup of the automorphism group of
X
and
\Omega
a set of vertices of
X
invariant under the action of
A
.
If it is true that for every path
W
with both ends on
\Omega
with trivial voltage and every
\tau\in A
then
\xi(W\tau)=1
, then every element of
A
lifts to
X^{\xi}
and the set of all these lifts is a semidirect product
G\rtimes A
.

Recall that a polytope is
hereditary
if every automorphism of each facet can be extended to an automorphism of the whole polytope that fixes that facet
[
16
]
.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
(\mathcal{K},r_{n},\xi)
be a Cayley extender.
Suppose that
\Gamma(\cal K_{r_{n}})=\Gamma(\cal K)
and that every automorphism of
\mathcal{K}
induces an automorphism of the voltage group
\Gamma
.
Then
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
is hereditary,
\Gamma(\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi})=\Gamma\rtimes\Gamma(\mathcal{K})
, and the symmetry type graph of
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
is
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}/\Gamma(\cal K)
.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma
be the voltage group of
(\mathcal{K},r_{n},\xi)
.
Note that our hypotheses imply that if
W
is a path in
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}
with trivial voltage, then its image under any automorphism of
\cal K_{r_{n}}
also has trivial voltage.
Therefore, we can use Theorem
5.4
taking
\Omega
as the whole set of vertices, and we get that the automorphism group of
\cal K
lifts as a semidirect product
\Gamma\rtimes\Gamma(\cal K)\leqslant\Gamma(\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi})
.
But, because of Proposition
5.3
every automorphism of
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
projects to
\cal K_{r_{n}}
. This proves that
\Gamma\rtimes\Gamma(\cal K)
is the whole automorphism group of
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
.
∎

Note that in Theorem
5.5
, the symmetry type graph that we obtain,
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}/\Gamma(\mathcal{K})
, is just the symmetry type graph of
\mathcal{K}
but with additional edges of label
n
.

Corollary 5.6
.

If
(\mathcal{K},\xi)
is a canonical Cayley extender and every automorphism of
\mathcal{K}
induces an automorphism of the voltage group
\Gamma
, then
\mathcal{K}^{\xi}
is hereditary,
\Gamma(\mathcal{K}^{\xi})=\Gamma\rtimes\Gamma(\mathcal{K})
, and the symmetry type graph of
\mathcal{K}^{\xi}
is obtained from the symmetry type graph of
\mathcal{K}
by adding semi-edges of label
n
to every node.

5.1.
Symmetry type graphs and
r_{n}
-friendly sets

Our goal now is to extend Theorem
5.5
to see what more we can say about the symmetry type graphs of Cayley extensions. In particular, we will calculate the symmetry type graph of the universal extension
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{K},r_{n})
in terms of
\mathcal{K}
and
r_{n}
(see Definition
4.5
).

Note that since
G
acts regularly on the facets of
\cal K_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
we get that
\Gamma(\cal K_{r_{n}}^{\xi})=SG
where
S
is the stabilizer of
(\cal K,1)
.
Indeed, if
\tau\in\Gamma(\cal K_{r_{n}}^{\xi})
maps
(\Phi,1)
to
(\Psi,\alpha)
, then
\tau\alpha^{-1}\in S
.
However, this might not be a semidirect product, as
G
is not necessarily normal.

It is known (see , for example,
[
17
, Theorem 2.3.1]
), that an automorphism of a derived graph projects if and only if it normalizes the voltage group, so
G
is normal if and only if
S
is the subgroup of
\Gamma(\cal K_{r_{n}})
that induces automorphisms of
G
.

In any case, we know that
\cal K_{r_{n}}^{\xi}/G
is isomorphic to
\cal K_{r_{n}}
, but the actual symmetry type graph might be a quotient of this.
However, since every flag is in the same orbit as a flag in the base facet
({\cal K},1)
, we can find out which orbits have to be identified in
\cal K_{r_{n}}
to get the actual symmetry type graph just by looking at the quotient of
\cal K_{r_{n}}
by the stabilizer of
({\cal K},1)
.
This quotient is well-defined despite the fact that the stabilizer of
(\cal K,1)
does not act by automorphisms on
\cal K_{r_{n}}
. We will dig deeper into this fact after we have defined
r_{n}
-friendly sets.
So let us turn to the problem of determining that stabilizer.

First, let us motivate the following technical definition. Consider an automorphism
\tau
of a Cayley extension
\cal K_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
, and fix a flag
\Phi
of
\cal K
. Then
\tau
takes
(\Phi,\gamma)
to some flag
(\Phi^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime})
, and the flag
\Phi^{\prime}
depends not only on
\Phi
but possibly on
\gamma
. Let us define
\tau_{\gamma}
in
\Gamma(\cal K)
by

\Phi\tau_{\gamma}=((\Phi,\gamma)\tau)p,

where
p

is the projection onto the first coordinate.
In other words,

\tau_{\gamma}
is the automorphism of
\cal K
induced by the restriction of
\tau
to the facet
(\mathcal{K},\gamma)

Thus, each automorphism
\tau
may induce several automorphisms of
\cal K
, and we denote the set of such automorphisms by
S_{\tau}
. More generally, we may adapt the preceding process even to homomorphisms between Cayley extensions.

Definition 5.7
.

Let
(\cal K,r_{n},\xi)
and
(\cal K,r_{n}^{\prime},\xi^{\prime})
be Cayley extenders with voltage groups
G
and
G^{\prime}
respectively.
Suppose that
\cal K_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
covers
\cal K_{r_{n}^{\prime}}^{\xi^{\prime}}
, and let
\varphi:\cal K_{r_{n}}^{\xi}\to\cal K_{r_{n}^{\prime}}^{\xi^{\prime}}
be a covering.
For each
\gamma\in G
and each
\Phi\in\cal K
define
\varphi_{\gamma}
by
\Phi\varphi_{\gamma}=((\Phi,\gamma)\varphi)p
, where
p
is the projection on the first coordinate.
Then

S_{\varphi}:=\{\varphi_{\gamma}:\gamma\in G\}.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
\cal K_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
be a Cayley extension. The stabilizer of the facet
(\cal K,1)
under the action of
\Gamma(\cal K_{r_{n}}^{\xi})
is isomorphic to

\bigcup_{\tau\in\Gamma(\cal K_{r_{n}}^{\xi})}S_{\tau}.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma=\Gamma(\cal K_{r_{n}}^{\xi})
, let
\Gamma_{(\cal K,1)}
be the stabilizer of the facet
(\cal K,1)
and
S=\bigcup_{\tau\in\Gamma}S_{\tau}
.
We will define a group homomorphism
\varphi:\Gamma_{(\cal K,1)}\to S
given by
\varphi:\tau\mapsto\tau_{1}
(see Definition
5.7
).
In other words, we consider the action of
\tau
on
\cal K
by looking at its action on the base facet of
\cal K_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
.
Observe that
\varphi
is a homomorphism only because the domain is the stabilizer of the facet
(\cal K,1)
; in general
(\tau\alpha)_{1}\neq\tau_{1}\alpha_{1}
.

Let us show that
\varphi
is surjective. If
\tau_{\gamma}
is an element of
S
, then there is a
\tau\in\Gamma
such that
(\Phi,\gamma)\tau=(\Phi\tau_{\gamma},\sigma)
for every
\Phi\in\cal K
and some fixed
\sigma\in G
.
But
(\Phi,\gamma)
is in the same
\Gamma
-orbit as
(\Phi,1)
, and
(\Phi\tau_{\gamma},\sigma)
is in the same
\Gamma
-orbit as
(\Phi\tau_{\gamma},1)
.
This means that there is an automorphism
\tau^{\prime}\in\Gamma
such that
(\Phi,1)\tau^{\prime}=(\Phi\tau_{\gamma},1)
.
In particular
\tau^{\prime}
stabilizes
(\cal K,1)
.
Now by definition of
\tau^{\prime}_{1}
we have that
(\Phi,1)\tau^{\prime}=(\Phi\tau^{\prime}_{1},1)
, but this was
(\Phi\tau_{\gamma},1)
.
By projecting on the first coordinate and using semi-regularity we get that
\tau^{\prime}_{1}=\tau_{\gamma}
, in other words
\varphi:\tau^{\prime}\mapsto\tau_{\gamma}
.
Note that, by definition, the image of
\varphi
is contained in
S
, so we have proved that
S
is the image of
\varphi
; in particular it is a group.

Note that
\tau_{1}=\tau^{\prime}_{1}
if and only if
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
act the same way on the base facet, which happens if and only if
\tau=\tau^{\prime}
. This proves that
\varphi
is an isomorphism.
∎

Corollary 5.9
.

Let
(\mathcal{K},r_{n},\xi)
be a Cayley extender.
The symmetry type graph of
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
is the quotient of
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}
by

\bigcup_{\tau\in\Gamma(\cal K_{r_{n}}^{\xi})}S_{\tau}.

We now consider the problem of what possible symmetry type graphs we obtain from varying the voltage assignment on a fixed pre-extension
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}
.
To start with, we note that (having fixed a voltage assignment), each set
S_{\alpha}
has the property that, if
\tau\in S_{\alpha}
, then for every flag
\Phi
of
\cal K
, the flags
r_{n}\Phi
and
r_{n}(\Phi\tau)
are in the same orbit of
\Gamma(\cal K)
(moreover, they differ by an element of
S_{\alpha}
itself).
This ends up being the key property of these sets, and we will need a minor generalization in the following subsection, so let us introduce those ideas here.

Definition 5.10
.

Consider two pre-extensions
(\mathcal{K},r_{n})
and
(\mathcal{K},r_{n}^{\prime})
.
We will say that a set
S\subseteq\Gamma(\mathcal{K})
is
(r_{n},r_{n}^{\prime})
-friendly
if for every flag
\Phi
of
\mathcal{K}
and every
\tau\in S
there is some
\widetilde{\tau}
in
S
such that
r_{n}^{\prime}(\Phi\tau)=(r_{n}\Phi)\widetilde{\tau}
. We will say that
\widetilde{\tau}
is an
(r_{n},r_{n}^{\prime})
-friend
of
\tau
, or just a
friend
if
r_{n}
and
r_{n}^{\prime}
are clear from context.
If a set is
(r_{n},r_{n})
-friendly we simply say that it is
r_{n}
-friendly.

Consider two pre-extensions as in the definition. If
\Phi
and
\Psi
are in the same facet of
\cal K
, there is a monodromy
w
that does not use
r_{n-1}
and such that
\Psi=w\Phi
.
Suppose that
r_{n}^{\prime}(\Phi\tau)=(r_{n}\Phi)\widetilde{\tau}
.
Using the fact that
\tau
and
\widetilde{\tau}
are automorphisms of
\cal K
and hence they commute with
w
and that
r_{n}
and
r_{n}^{\prime}
also commute with
w
(since
w
does not use
r_{n-1}
) we get the following calculation:

\displaystyle r_{n}^{\prime}(\Psi\tau)

\displaystyle=r_{n}^{\prime}((w\Phi)\tau)

\displaystyle=r_{n}^{\prime}(w(\Phi\tau))

\displaystyle=w(r_{n}^{\prime}(\Phi\tau))

\displaystyle=w((r_{n}\Phi)\widetilde{\tau})

\displaystyle=(w(r_{n}\Phi))\widetilde{\tau}

\displaystyle=(r_{n}(w\Phi))\widetilde{\tau}

\displaystyle=(r_{n}\Psi)\widetilde{\tau}.

This shows that
\widetilde{\tau}
does not depend on the flag
\Phi
itself, but only on its facet on
\cal K
.
Therefore, we may write
\widetilde{\tau}_{F}
for the element in
S
satisfying that
r_{n}^{\prime}(\Phi\tau)=(r_{n}\Phi)\widetilde{\tau}_{F}
for every flag
\Phi
in the facet
F
.

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
(\cal K,r_{n})
and
(\cal K,r_{n}^{\prime})
be pre-extenders. Then:

(i)

If
S_{1}
is
(r_{n},r_{n}^{\prime})
-friendly and
S_{2}
is any set of isomorphisms from
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}
to
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}^{\prime}}
, then
S_{2}
and
S_{1}\cup S_{2}
are
(r_{n},r_{n}^{\prime})
-friendly.

(ii)

If
S
is
(r_{n},r_{n}^{\prime})
-friendly, then
S^{-1}
is
(r_{n}^{\prime},r_{n})
-friendly.

(iii)

If
S_{1}
is
(r_{n},r_{n}^{\prime})
-friendly and
S_{2}
is
(r_{n}^{\prime},r_{n}^{\prime\prime})
-friendly, then
S_{1}S_{2}
is
(r_{n},r_{n}^{\prime\prime})
-friendly.

Proof.

First notice that if
\tau
is an isomorphism from
\cal K_{r_{n}}
to
\cal K_{r_{n}^{\prime}}
we can define
\widetilde{\tau}_{F}=\tau
for any facet
F
.
This proves that any set of such isomorphisms is
(r_{n},r_{n}^{\prime})
-friendly and that adding isomorphisms to an
(r_{n},r_{n}^{\prime})
-friendly set preserves the
(r_{n},r_{n}^{\prime})
-friendliness.

Let
S
be
(r_{n},r_{n}^{\prime})
-friendly and let
\tau\in S
. Let
\Phi
be a flag in a facet
F
of
\cal K
.

Then

r_{n}^{\prime}\Phi=r_{n}^{\prime}(\Phi\tau^{-1}\tau)=(r_{n}(\Phi\tau^{-1}))\widetilde{\tau}_{F\tau^{-1}},

Which implies that

r_{n}(\Phi\tau^{-1})=(r_{n}^{\prime}\Phi)\widetilde{\tau}_{F\tau^{-1}}^{-1}.

In other words,
S^{-1}
is
(r_{n}^{\prime},r_{n})
-friendly and
\widetilde{(\tau^{-1})}_{F}=\widetilde{\tau}_{F\tau^{-1}}^{-1}
.

Now let
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
be
(r_{n},r_{n}^{\prime})
-friendly and
(r_{n}^{\prime},r_{n}^{\prime\prime})
-friendly, respectively, and let
\sigma\in S_{1}
and
\tau\in S_{2}
.
Let
\Phi
be a flag in a facet
F
.
If we calculate
r_{n}^{\prime\prime}(\Phi\sigma\tau)
we get

r_{n}^{\prime\prime}(\Phi\sigma\tau)=(r_{n}^{\prime}(\Phi\sigma))\widetilde{\tau}_{F\sigma}=(r_{n}\Phi)\widetilde{\sigma}_{F}\widetilde{\tau}_{F\sigma}.

This shows that
S_{1}S_{2}
is also
(r_{n},r_{n}^{\prime\prime})
-friendly (with
\widetilde{(\sigma\tau)}_{F}=\widetilde{\sigma}_{F}\widetilde{\tau}_{F\sigma}
).
∎

By making
r_{n}=r_{n}^{\prime}=r_{n}^{\prime\prime}
we get the following lemma as a corollary.

Lemma 5.12
.

Let
(\cal K,r_{n})
be a pre-extender:

(i)

If
S_{1}
is
r_{n}
-friendly and
S_{2}
is any subset of
\Gamma(\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}})
, then
S_{2}
and
S_{1}\cup S_{2}
are
r_{n}
-friendly.
In particular
\{1\}
is
r_{n}
-friendly.

(ii)

If
S
is
r_{n}
-friendly, then
S^{-1}
is
r_{n}
-friendly.

(iii)

If
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are
r_{n}
-friendly, then
S_{1}S_{2}
is
r_{n}
-friendly.

In particular, the group generated by the union of
r_{n}
-friendly sets is also
r_{n}
-friendly.

Proof.

To prove the last claim, let

S=\bigcup_{i\in I}S_{i}

be an arbitrary union of
r_{n}
-friendly sets. If
\tau\in\langle S\rangle
, then
\tau
has finite length and
\tau\in S_{i_{1}}^{\pm 1}\cdots S_{i_{k}}^{\pm 1}
for some
k
. Each
S_{i_{1}}^{\pm 1}
is
r_{n}
-friendly by Item
(ii)
, and thus this set is
r_{n}
-friendly by Item
(iii)
, meaning that
\tau
has an
r_{n}
-friend in this set for each flag
\Phi
, and thus
\tau
has an
r_{n}
-friend in
S
for each
\Phi
.∎

Corollary 5.13
.

Given
(\cal K,r_{n})
, there is a unique greatest
r_{n}
-friendly set
\heartsuit(\cal K,r_{n})
and it is a subgroup of
\Gamma(\cal K)
containing
\Gamma(\cal K_{r_{n}})
. In particular, if
r_{n}=Id
, then
\heartsuit(\cal K,r_{n})=\Gamma(\mathcal{K})
.

We will call the group
\heartsuit(\cal K,r_{n})
the
r_{n}
-friendly group of
\cal K
. We say that an automorphism
\tau
of
\Gamma(\cal K)
is
r_{n}
-friendly
if it is an element of
\heartsuit(\cal K,r_{n})
.

Remark 5.14
.

It is tempting to start by defining an automorphism
\tau
to be
r_{n}
-friendly if, for every flag
\Phi
there is an automorphism
\widetilde{\tau}
such that
r_{n}(\Phi\tau)=(r_{n}\Phi)\widetilde{\tau}
. However, this misses an important nuance; namely, even if
\tau
satisfies this condition, it need not be the case that all of its
r_{n}
-friends
\widetilde{\tau}
satisfy this condition.

We now describe a more constructive method for finding the
r_{n}
-friendly group of a pre-extension.

We will construct two sequences of partitions of
\cal K
,
\{\mathscr{P}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{\mathscr{P}_{k}^{\prime}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and such that

\mathscr{P}_{k+1}
is a refinement of
\mathscr{P}^{\prime}_{k}
which is itself a refinement of
\mathscr{P}_{k}
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
We will also use these partitions to create a decreasing sequence
\{G_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of groups whose intersection will be
\heartsuit(\cal K,r_{n})
.

Given a partition
\mathscr{P}
we use the expression
\Phi\sim_{\mathscr{P}}\Psi
to say that
\Phi
and
\Psi
are in the same element of
\mathscr{P}
.

Let us start by defining
\mathscr{P}_{0}=\{\cal K\}
.
We will use recursion for the rest of the construction.
If
\mathscr{P}_{k}
is defined, let us define

G_{k}=\{\tau\in\Gamma(\cal K):\Phi\sim_{\mathscr{P}_{k}}\Phi\tau\,\ \text{for all}\ \Phi\in\cal K\}.

Now define

\mathscr{P}_{k}^{\prime}=\{\Phi G_{k}:\Phi\in\cal K\}.

Note that
\mathscr{P}_{k}^{\prime}
refines
\mathscr{P}_{k}
by the definition of
G_{k}
.

Given two sets
Q,R\subseteq\cal K
, we define
A_{QR}=\{\Phi\in Q:r_{n}\Phi\in R\}
. Then we define:

\mathscr{P}_{k+1}=\{A_{QR}:Q,R\in\mathscr{P}^{\prime}_{k}\}.

In other words,
\Phi\sim_{\mathscr{P}_{k+1}}\Psi
if and only if
\Phi\sim_{\mathscr{P}_{k}^{\prime}}\Psi
and
r_{n}\Phi\sim_{\mathscr{P}_{k}^{\prime}}r_{n}\Psi
.
Note that
\mathscr{P}_{k+1}
refines
\mathscr{P}_{k}^{\prime}
, as
A_{QR}
is a subset of
Q
.
Since
\mathscr{P}_{k+1}
refines
\mathscr{P}_{k}
(by transitivity), we also know that
G_{k+1}\leqslant G_{k}
.

Let us illustrate the first few steps of the construction. As mentioned,
\mathscr{P}_{0}=\mathcal{K}
, and so
G_{0}=\Gamma(\mathcal{K})
. Then
\mathscr{P}_{0}^{\prime}
consists of the flag-orbits of
\mathcal{K}
. To obtain
\mathscr{P}_{1}
, we essentially partition each flag-orbit; given a flag
\Phi
in that orbit, we look at what orbit
r_{n}\Phi
is in. So we have effectively labeled each flag
\Phi
of
\mathcal{K}
by a pair consisting of its flag-orbit and the flag-orbit of
r_{n}\Phi
. Now
G_{1}
is the subgroup of
\Gamma(\mathcal{K})
that respects this new partition,
\mathscr{P}_{1}^{\prime}
is the set of orbits under
G_{1}
, and we continue the process.

Proposition 5.15
.

Let
(\cal K,r_{n})
be a pre-extender and let
\mathscr{P}_{k},G_{k}
and
\mathscr{P}^{\prime}_{k}
be constructed as above. Then,

\heartsuit(\cal K,r_{n})=\bigcap_{k=0}^{\infty}G_{k}.

Proof.

First we will prove by induction that
\heartsuit(\cal K,r_{n})\leqslant G_{k}
.
For
k=0
we get that
G_{0}=\Gamma(\cal K)
, so there is nothing to prove.
Now suppose that
G_{k}
contains
\heartsuit(\cal K,r_{n})
.
Let
\Phi
be a flag of
\cal K
and let
\tau\in\heartsuit(\cal K,r_{n})
.
Then
r_{n}(\Phi\tau)=(r_{n}\Phi)\widetilde{\tau}
for some
\widetilde{\tau}\in\heartsuit(\cal K,r_{n})\leqslant G_{k}
, in other words,
r_{n}(\Phi\tau)\sim_{\mathscr{P}_{k}^{\prime}}r_{n}\Phi
.
On the other hand
\Phi\tau\sim_{\mathscr{P}_{k}^{\prime}}\Phi
(with
\tau
as witness).
Therefore,
\Phi\tau\sim_{\mathscr{P}_{k+1}}\Phi
.
But this is precisely the definition of
\tau
being in
G_{k+1}
.
It follows by induction that
\heartsuit(\cal K,r_{n})\leqslant\bigcap_{k=0}^{\infty}G_{k}
.

Now we will prove that
\bigcap_{k=0}^{\infty}G_{k}
is
r_{n}
-friendly.
Let
\tau\in\bigcap_{k=0}^{\infty}G_{k}
and let
\Phi
be any flag of
\cal K
.
For every
k
we know that
\tau\in G_{k+1}
, therefore
\Phi\sim_{\mathscr{P}_{k+1}^{\prime}}\Phi\tau
.
Since
\mathscr{P}_{k+1}^{\prime}
refines
\mathscr{P}_{k+1}
we get that
\Phi\sim_{\mathscr{P}_{k+1}}\Phi\tau
.
By definition of
\mathscr{P}_{k+1}
, in particular we have that
r_{n}\Phi\sim_{\mathscr{P}_{k}^{\prime}}r_{n}(\Phi\tau)
.
This means that there exists some
\widetilde{\tau}_{k}\in G_{k}
such that
r_{n}(\Phi\tau)=(r_{n}\Phi)\widetilde{\tau}_{k}
.
But, since the action of
\Gamma(\cal K)
on
\cal K
is free, we get that
\widetilde{\tau}_{k}
actually does not depend on
k
, so this unique
\widetilde{\tau}_{k}
is in
\bigcap_{k=0}^{\infty}G_{k}
.
This proves that
\bigcap_{k=0}^{\infty}G_{k}
is
r_{n}
-friendly, and therefore contained in
\heartsuit(\cal K,r_{n})
.
∎

Note that in the previous proposition the common limit of the partitions
\mathscr{P}_{k}
and
\mathscr{P}^{\prime}_{k}
as
k
goes to infinity are the orbits of flags of
\cal K
under the action of
\heartsuit(\cal K,r_{n})
.

To prove that each automorphism of
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}
induces an
r_{n}
-friendly set we first prove the following more general proposition:

Proposition 5.16
.

Given a homomorphism
\phi:\cal K_{r_{n}}^{\xi}\to\cal K_{r_{n}^{\prime}}^{\xi^{\prime}}
,
the set

is

-friendly.

Proof.

We want to prove that

for some

.
Let

be the facet of

containing

\displaystyle\phi(r_{n}\Phi,\xi(F)\gamma)

\displaystyle=\phi((\Phi,\gamma)^{n})

Corollary 5.17

Theorem 5.18
.

Let
(\mathcal{K},r_{n},\xi)
be a Cayley extender.
The symmetry type graph of
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}^{\xi}
is a quotient of
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}
by some

-friendly group
H\leqslant\heartsuit(\mathcal{K},r_{n})

The previous theorem tells us that by knowing the subgroups of

Theorem 5.19
.

The symmetry type graph of
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{K},r_{n})
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}/\heartsuit(\cal K,r_{n})
.

Proof.

We know from Theorem
5.18
that the symmetry type graph of
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{K},r_{n})
is
\cal K_{r_{n}}/\bigcup_{\tau\in\Gamma(\cal K_{r_{n}}^{\xi})}S_{\tau}
.
We also know that
\bigcup_{\tau\in\Gamma(\cal K_{r_{n}}^{\xi})}S_{\tau}
is
r_{n}
-friendly, and therefore contained in
\heartsuit(\cal K,r_{n})
.
Therefore, if we prove that
\heartsuit(\cal K,r_{n})\subseteq\bigcup_{\tau\in\Gamma(\cal K_{r_{n}}^{\xi})}S_{\tau}
, we get our result.

To prove this, we will take an element
\tau\in\heartsuit(\cal K,r_{n})
and construct an automorphism
\varphi
of
\cal U(\cal K,r_{n})
that acts as
\tau
in some facet (the base facet, for simplicity). This will prove that
\tau\in S_{\varphi}
and therefore our result.

The basic idea is that we want
(\Psi,1)\varphi=(\Psi\tau,1)
for every flag
\Psi
of
\mathcal{K}
. Now, we need the action of
\varphi
to commute with the action of
r_{n}
. We see that

r_{n}((\Psi,1)\varphi)=r_{n}(\Psi\tau,1)=(r_{n}(\Psi\tau),\xi(\Psi\tau))

whereas

(r_{n}(\Psi,1))\varphi=(r_{n}\Psi,\xi(\Psi))\varphi.

Since
\tau
is
r_{n}
-friendly, that implies that
r_{n}(\Psi\tau)=(r_{n}\Psi)\widetilde{\tau}
for some
r_{n}
-friendly automorphism

. So
we find that

(r_{n}\Psi,\xi(\Psi))\varphi=((r_{n}\Psi)\widetilde{\tau},\xi(\Psi\tau)).

Furthermore, using the fact that
\varphi
commutes with each

with

, we find that the action of
\varphi
does not depend on the first coordinate; i.e., for every flag

(\Lambda,\xi(\Psi))\varphi=(\Lambda\widetilde{\tau},\xi(\Psi\tau)).

Thus, this shows that once we decide how
\varphi
acts on flags of the form
(\Psi,1)
, we can deduce how it must act on flags of the form
(\Psi,\gamma)
, where
\gamma
has ‘length’ 1 (that is, it consists of the voltage of a single flag). Using the same sort of process, we can deduce how
\varphi
acts when
\gamma
has length 2 and so on. Let us describe the process formally.

Let
\Phi
be a flag of
\cal K
and
\Phi^{\prime}=\Phi\tau
. We will construct an automorphism of
\cal U(\cal K,r_{n})
that maps
(\Phi,1)
to
(\Phi^{\prime},1)
.
To do this, we will construct a sequence of functions

such that:

(i)

(\Psi,1)\varphi_{k}=(\Psi\tau,1)
for every flag
\Psi
of
\cal K
. In particular,

,

(ii)

\varphi_{k}
is a permutation of the set

where the

of

is the smallest

such that
\gamma
can be written as a product of
k
elements in
\left\{\alpha_{F}:F\in(\mathcal{K})_{n-1}\right\}
(see Definition
4.5
).

(iii)

For every
\gamma\in G(\cal K,r_{n})
with length
k
or less, there is an
r_{n}
-friendly automorphism
\sigma_{\gamma}
of
\cal K
and an element
\gamma^{\prime}\in G(\cal K,r_{n})
satisfying that
(\Psi,\gamma)\varphi_{k}=(\Psi\sigma_{\gamma},\gamma^{\prime})

(note that this property implies that
\varphi_{k}
preserves
i
-adjacencies for
i<n
).

(iv)

For any two
n
-adjacent flags in the domain of
\varphi_{k}
, their images are also
n
-adjacent.

(v)

The function
\varphi_{k+1}
is an extension of
\varphi_{k}
; that is,
\varphi_{k+1}
coincides with
\varphi_{k}
in the domain of
\varphi_{k}
.

Once we succeed in creating this sequence, we can define
\varphi
as its limit and it will (clearly) be an automorphism of
\cal U(\cal K,r_{n})
that acts like
\tau
on the base facet.

First we define
\varphi_{0}
by
(\Psi,1)\varphi_{0}=(\Psi\tau,1)
for all flags
\Psi
of
\mathcal{K}
.

Now suppose we already have constructed
\varphi_{k}
with the desired properties. In particular, for each
\gamma\in G(\cal K,r_{n})
with length
k
and for every flag
\Psi
we have
(\Psi,\gamma)\varphi_{k}=(\Psi\sigma_{\gamma},\gamma^{\prime})
. For every
\alpha_{F}
that does not cancel the first character of
\gamma
, let us define
(\Psi,\alpha_{F}\gamma)\varphi_{k+1}
as

(\Psi,\alpha_{F}\gamma)\varphi_{k+1}=(\Psi\widetilde{(\sigma_{\gamma}\tau)}_{F}\widetilde{\tau}_{F\sigma_{\gamma}}^{-1},\alpha_{F\sigma_{\gamma}}\gamma^{\prime}).

(Recall that, if
\alpha
is
r_{n}
-friendly and
\Phi
has facet
F
, then
\widetilde{\alpha}_{F}
is the automorphism such that
r_{n}(\Phi\alpha)=(r_{n}\Phi)\widetilde{\alpha}_{F}
.)
For the other flags in the domain of
\varphi_{k+1}
we just make it coincide with
\varphi_{k}
.

We will now prove that
\varphi_{k+1}
satisfies the desired conditions.
We automatically get
Items
(i)
,
(ii)
and
(v)
.
Also, by defining
\sigma_{\alpha_{F}\gamma}:=\widetilde{(\sigma_{\gamma}\tau)}_{F}\widetilde{\tau}_{F\sigma_{\gamma}}^{-1}
and
(\alpha_{F}\gamma)^{\prime}:=\alpha_{F\sigma_{\gamma}}\gamma^{\prime}
we get Item
(iii)
.

Let us verify that Item
(iv)
holds. Suppose that
(\Psi,\gamma)
is in the domain of
\varphi_{k}
and let
F
be the facet of
\cal K
containing
\Psi
.
If
(\Psi,\gamma)^{n}
is also in the domain of
\varphi_{k}
, then there is nothing to prove, since
\varphi_{k}
satisfies Item
(iv)
and
\varphi_{k+1}
is an extension of it.
If
(\Psi,\gamma)^{n}
is not in the domain of
\varphi_{k}
we have that

, and we notice that
\alpha_{F}\gamma
must have length

.
Therefore, our definition tells us that

\displaystyle(\Psi,\gamma)^{n}\varphi_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(r_{n}\Psi,\alpha_{F}\gamma)\varphi_{k+1}

\displaystyle=((r_{n}\Psi)\widetilde{(\sigma_{\gamma}\tau)}_{F}\widetilde{\tau}_{F\sigma_{\gamma}}^{-1},\alpha_{F\sigma_{\gamma}}\gamma^{\prime})

\displaystyle=((r_{n}(\Psi\sigma_{\gamma}\tau)\widetilde{\tau}_{F\sigma_{\gamma}}^{-1},\alpha_{F\sigma_{\gamma}}\gamma^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(((r_{n}(\Psi\sigma_{\gamma}))\widetilde{\tau}_{F\sigma_{\gamma}}\widetilde{\tau}_{F\sigma_{\gamma}}^{-1},\alpha_{F\sigma_{\gamma}}\gamma^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(r_{n}(\Psi\sigma_{\gamma}),\alpha_{F\sigma_{\gamma}}\gamma^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(\Psi\sigma_{\gamma},\gamma^{\prime})^{n}

\displaystyle=((\Psi,\gamma)\varphi_{k+1})^{n}.

.

Now let us suppose that
(\Psi,\gamma)
is in the domain of

but not in the domain of
\varphi_{k}
. If

we must have that

has length

and
\alpha_{F}\gamma
has length

We can then do a change of variable

and we are back to the previous case.
This concludes the proof that
\varphi_{k+1}
satisfies the desired properties.

Then we simply define
\varphi
as the limit of the sequence

. It is clear that

is an automorphism of
\cal U(\mathcal{K},r_{n})
and that it acts as

on the base facet, so

and thus

∎

\cal U(\cal K,r_{n}^{\tau})
is isomorphic to
\cal U(\cal K,r_{n}^{\prime})
, but
\cal U(\cal K,r_{n}^{\tau})
is also isomorphic to
\cal U(\cal K,r_{n})
.
∎

Now that we know when two universal Cayley extensions are isomorphic we can characterize those polytopes and maniplexes that have a unique universal extension.

Theorem 5.25
.

All the universal extensions of
\cal K
are isomorphic to each other, if and only if every isomorphism between two different facets of
\cal K
as well as every involutory automorphism of a facet of
\cal K
can be extended to an automorphism of
\cal K
.

In this case,
\Gamma(\cal K)
is
r_{n}
-friendly for every pre-extender
(\cal K,r_{n})
.

Proof.

Because of Theorem
5.24
, if
\cal U(\cal K,r_{n})
is isomorphic to
\cal U(\cal K,Id)
we get that there is a non-empty
(Id,r_{n})
-friendly set
S
.
This means that given
\sigma
in
S
and
\Phi
in
\cal K
, there exists some
\widetilde{\sigma}\in S
such that
r_{n}(\Phi\sigma)=\Phi\widetilde{\sigma}
.
Moreover, because of statement
(iii)
in Theorem
5.24
, there must be some automorphism
\tau
of
\cal K
such that
\mathcal{K}_{Id^{\tau}}/\heartsuit(\cal K,Id)^{\tau}=\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}/\heartsuit(\cal K,r_{n})
, but
Id^{\tau}=Id
for every possible
\tau
. This shows that
\mathcal{K}_{Id}/\heartsuit(\cal K,Id)=\mathcal{K}_{r_{n}}/\heartsuit(\cal K,r_{n})
, and therefore using Theorem
5.22
we may assume that
1
is in
S
.
By taking
\sigma=1
we get that
\Phi
and
r_{n}\Phi
must be in the same orbit.
In other words,
{\left.\kern-1.2ptr_{n}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{F}}
can be extended to an automorphism of
\cal K
.

Suppose that all the universal extensions of
\cal K
are isomorphic.
Let
F
and
F^{\prime}
be two isomorphic facets of
\cal K
.
Let
\sigma
be an isomorphism between
F
and
F^{\prime}
.
By our previous observation, to prove that
\sigma
can be extended to an automorphism of
\cal K
we only have to find a pre-extender
(\cal K,r_{n})
such that
{\left.\kern-1.2ptr_{n}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{F}}=\sigma
.
If
F\neq F^{\prime}
, we simply let
r_{n}
act as
\sigma
on
F
, as
\sigma^{-1}
on
F^{\prime}
and as the identity in every other facet of
\cal K
.
If
F=F^{\prime}
and
\sigma
has order 2, we define
r_{n}
as
\sigma
on
F
and as the identity anywhere else.

We have proven that if all the universal extensions of
\cal K
are isomorphic, then the isomorphisms between two different facets of
\cal K
and the involutory automorphisms of facets of
\cal K
can be extended to an automorphism of
\cal K
.
Now let us assume that we can extend such automorphisms.
Let
(\cal K,r_{n})
be an arbitrary pre-extender of
\cal K
.
First we will prove that
\Gamma(\cal K)
is
r_{n}
-friendly.

Let
\tau\in\Gamma(\cal K)
,
\Phi
a flag of
\cal K
, and
F
the facet of
\Phi
.
By hypothesis,
\sigma:={\left.\kern-1.2ptr_{n}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{F\tau}}
, which is an isomorphism between the facets
F\tau
and
r_{n}(F\tau)
, can be extended to an automorphism
\gamma\in\Gamma(\cal K)
.
Symbolically,
r_{n}(\Phi\tau)=(\Phi\tau)\sigma=\Phi\tau\gamma
.
On the other hand,
\sigma^{\prime}:={\left.\kern-1.2ptr_{n}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{r_{n}F}}
can also be extended to an automorphism
\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma(\cal K)
, and this means
\Phi=r_{n}(r_{n}\Phi)=(r_{n}\Phi)\sigma^{\prime}=(r_{n}\Phi)\gamma^{\prime}
.
If we now make a simple substitution we get that
r_{n}(\Phi\tau)=(r_{n}\Phi)\gamma^{\prime}\tau\gamma
, and since
\tau
and
\Phi
were arbitrary, we proved that
\Gamma(\cal K)
is
r_{n}
-friendly, or symbolically,
\heartsuit(\mathcal{K},r_{n})=\Gamma(\cal K)
.
This proves the second statement of our theorem.

Finally, we see that
\cal K_{r_{n}}/\heartsuit(\cal K,r_{n})=\cal K_{r_{n}}/\Gamma(\cal K)
, but since the restrictions of
r_{n}
to a facet are isomorphisms or involutory automorphisms, our hypothesis ensures that every flag is in the same
\Gamma(\cal K)
-orbit as its
n
-adjacent flag.
This means that
\cal K_{r_{n}}/\Gamma(\cal K)
has a semi-edge at each vertex, or in other words that it is isomorphic to
\cal K_{Id}/\Gamma(\cal K)
.
Therefore, Theorem
5.24
tells us that
\cal U(\cal K,r_{n})
is isomorphic to the universal canonical Cayley extension of
\cal K
.
This completes our proof.
∎

Corollary 5.26
.

If
\mathcal{K}
is regular, then all the universal extensions of
\cal K
are isomorphic and regular.

Every abstract regular polytope
\cal K
has a regular extension
\{\cal K,\infty\}
, called
the free extension of
\cal K
, which covers all other regular extensions of
\cal K
(
[
15
, Thm. 4D4]
). It follows that
\{\mathcal{K},\infty\}
covers the unique universal Cayley extension of
\cal K
,
\cal U(\cal K)
.
Also, using other results from
[
15
]
(for example, Lemma 4E10), we get that
\{\cal K,\infty\}
has the Flat Amalgamation Property (i.e., it is a canonical Cayley extension of
\cal K
, see
3.11
), which means that it is covered by
\cal U(\cal K)
.
The composition of both covering homomorphisms (in any order) would be a covering homomorphism of a regular polytope to itself, but since homomorphisms are defined by the image of a single flag, this composition would have to be an automorphism.
This proves that for a regular polytope, the free extension is the same as the universal Cayley extension.

Regular maniplexes are not the only family of maniplexes with a unique universal Cayley extension, as we see in the following corollary. A
k
-maniplex with
k\geqslant n
has
symmetry type
2_{\overline{n-1}}
if it has two flag orbits, and for every flag
\Phi
, the flags
\Phi
and
r_{i}\Phi
are in the same orbit if and only if
i\neq n-1
. In particular, such maniplexes have two orbits of facets.

Corollary 5.27
.

If
\mathcal{K}
is an
n
-maniplex with symmetry type
2_{\overline{n-1}}
and it has two isomorphism classes of facets, then all the universal extensions of
\cal K
are isomorphic
(n+1)
-maniplexes with symmetry type
2_{\overline{n-1}}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\cal K
has symmetry type
2_{\overline{n-1}}
and that it has two isomorphism classes of facets.
Let
\sigma:F\to F^{\prime}
be any isomorphism between two facets
F
and
F^{\prime}
of
\cal K
(we may assume that
\sigma
is involutory if
F=F^{\prime}
, but it is not necessary).
Since the two types of facet of
\cal K
are non-isomorphic,
F
and
F^{\prime}
must be in the same
\Gamma(\cal K)
-orbit, and since
\cal K
has symmetry type
2_{\overline{n-1}}
, all the flags in the same type of facet are in the same
\Gamma(\cal K)
-orbit.
Since
\sigma
maps flags in
F
to flags in the same
\Gamma(\cal K)
-orbit, it can be extended to an automorphism of
\cal K
.
Then Theorem
5.25
tells us that all the universal extensions of
\cal K
are isomorphic.
∎

In the previous result, the fact that there are two isomorphism classes of facets is necessary:
Let
\cal K
have symmetry type
2_{\overline{n-1}}
but with all facets isomorphic to each other.
For example, there is a map with symmetry type
2_{\overline{2}}
with automorphism group

\langle x,y,z:x^{2}=y^{2}=z^{2}=(xy)^{4}=(xz)^{4}=(yz)^{3}=xz(xy)^{2}zyxy=1\rangle.

It is clear that
\cal K
must have at least 2 facets
F
and
G
of one type.
Let
F^{\prime}
be a facet of the other type.
Let us define
r_{n}
as some isomorphism between
F
and
F^{\prime}
, and as the identity in any other facet.

Let
\Phi
be a flag in
F
, and let
\tau\in\Gamma(\cal K)
be such that
\Phi\tau
is in
G
.
Then
r_{n}(\Phi\tau)=\Phi\tau
by construction of
r_{n}
.
On the other hand,
(r_{n}\Phi)\tau
is in the same orbit as
r_{n}\Phi
, which is the opposite orbit to
\Phi
, so there is no automorphism
\widetilde{\tau}
such that
(r_{n}\Phi)\widetilde{\tau}=r_{n}(\Phi\tau)
.

Corollary 5.28
.

Let
\cal K
be a map. Then, all the universal extensions of
\cal K
are isomorphic to each other if and only if
\cal K
is either regular or alternating semiregular with non isomorphic facets (i.e. it has type
2_{\overline{2}}
).

Proof.

Let
\cal K
be a map such that all its universal extensions are isomorphic. The facets of
\cal K
are polygons, which are regular.
Therefore Theorem
5.25
tells us that every flag of
\mathcal{K}
is in the same orbit as its 0-adjacent flag and its 1-adjacent flag (note that an automorphism that maps a flag to its 0-adjacent flag is an involution).
Therefore,
\cal K
is either regular or it has symmetry type
2_{\overline{2}}
.
∎

Note that Corollary
5.28
does not generalize to higher ranks.
For example, if
\cal M
has no pair of facets that are isomorphic and no facet has a non-trivial automorphism, then it also has a unique universal Cayley extension.
