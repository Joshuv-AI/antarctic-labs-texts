Superradiant many-qubit absorption refrigerator
Michal Kloc, Kurt Meier, Kimon Hadjikyriakos, Gernot Schaller

Abstract
We show that the lower levels of a large-spin network with a collective anti-ferromagnetic interaction and collective couplings to three reservoirs may function as a quantum absorption refrigerator. In appropriate regimes, the steady-state cooling current of this refrigerator scales quadratically with the size of the working medium, i.e., the number of spins. The same scaling is observed for the noise and the entropy production rate.

Additionally, studies for simple toy models

An alternative scheme encompasses continuous heat engines

For this class of heat engines, stationary heat currents can be directed to accomplish certain tasks, provided that the quantum WFs have been appropriately engineered.

This application is already possible for two grand-canonical reservoirs and with a single quantum dot (two-level) WF

Alternatively, for three-terminal systems one may aim to direct the heat currents such that heat is simultaneously entering from the hottest (work) and the coldest reservoir, while the waste heat is dumped into the intermediate temperature (hot) reservoir.

This device is known as quantum absorbtion refrigerator (QAR), and it requires a WF with at least three levels

A pedagogical introduction to QARs and description of their physical implementations can be found in Chapter 6 of

Additionally, to discuss the reliability of the collective QAR, we also address the noise

Master equation

III.1
Reservoir properties

If the coupling operators are linear
B_{\nu}=\sum_{k}h_{k\nu}b_{k\nu}+{\rm h.c.}
with spontaneous emission amplitudes
h_{k\nu}
, we can write the Fourier transforms of the reservoir correlation functions as

\displaystyle\gamma_{\nu}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int d\tau{\rm Tr}\left\{e^{+\mathrm{i}H_{S}\tau}B_{\nu}e^{-\mathrm{i}H_{S}\tau}B_{\nu}\bar{\rho}_{B}^{\nu}\right\}e^{+\mathrm{i}\omega\tau}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{\nu}(\omega)[1+n_{\nu}(\omega)]

(4)

\Gamma(\omega)=-\Gamma_{\nu}(-\omega)
and with the Bose-Einstein distribution

n_{\nu}(\omega)=[e^{\beta_{\nu}\omega}-1]^{-1}

We assume that the spectral densities of all three reservoirs can be parametrized as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\nu}(\omega)=\frac{4\bar{\Gamma}_{\nu}\varepsilon_{\nu}\delta_{\nu}^{2}\omega}{\left[(\omega-\varepsilon_{\nu})^{2}+\delta_{\nu}^{2}\right]\left[(\omega+\varepsilon_{\nu})^{2}+\delta_{\nu}^{2}\right]}\,,

(5)

We just note here that such peaked spectral densities can for each reservoir be effectively implemented by coupling the large spin via

III.2
Populations in the energy eigenbasis

\displaystyle R_{ab}^{\nu}=\gamma_{\nu}(E_{b}-E_{a})\left|\langle v_{a}^{+}|A_{\nu}|v_{b}^{+}\rangle\right|^{2}\,.

\displaystyle\left\langle v_{a}^{+}\left|J_{x}\right|v_{b}^{+}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\alpha_{b}^{+}\delta_{a,b+1}+\alpha_{b}^{-}\delta_{a,b-1}\right]\,,

\displaystyle\left\langle v_{a}^{+}\left|J_{x}^{2}\right|v_{b}^{+}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\big[\alpha_{b+1}^{+}\alpha_{b}^{+}\delta_{a,b+2}+\alpha_{b-1}^{-}\alpha_{b}^{-}\delta_{a,\left|b-2\right|}\big]\,,

If the coupling of the system to the reservoirs is engineered as described in Sec.

This allows us to construct an absorption refrigerator by driving transitions between these levels such that it pumps energy from the cold reservoir to the hot one, see Fig.

Thus, for a proper initialization in the even parity and maximum angular momentum sector, the time-dependent density matrix is given by the statistical mixture
\rho(t)=\sum_{a=1/2}^{N/2}\rho_{aa}(t)|v_{a}^{+}\rangle\langle v_{a}^{+}|

Despite the simple diagonal form in the proper collective basis, our model features highly coherent states in the local

To see this, consider e.g. the ground state in the maximum angular momentum and even parity sector.
A state like

These local coherences are the essential ingredient to the quantum speedup we investigate below.

III.3
Current and Noise

To obtain the long-term statistics of transferred energy quanta between the system and its reservoirs (in particular, the cold one), we employ the counting field formalism.
We generalize the rate matrix
R(0)=R\to R(\chi)

This can of course be done for all reservoirs but we exemplify it only for energy exchanges with the cold one

\displaystyle R_{ab}(\chi)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{b\neq a}R_{ba}(0)\,.

III.4
Thermodynamic consistency

It can be shown that the approach is thermodynamically consistent.
For example, the first law of thermodynamics at steady state is reflected in the fact that

\displaystyle\bar{I}_{E}^{c}+\bar{I}_{E}^{h}+\bar{I}_{E}^{w}=0\,.

(35)

The existence of a second law can be deduced from the fact that the rates obey local detailed balance

\displaystyle\frac{R_{ab}^{\nu}}{R_{ba}^{\nu}}=e^{-\beta_{\nu}(E_{a}-E_{b})}\,,

(36)

such that the standard thermodynamic framework of Pauli-type master equations
schnakenberg1976a
applies.
From this one can derive many implications, for
example that the individual rate matrices locally thermalize the system

\displaystyle\sum_{b}R_{ab}^{\nu}e^{-\beta_{\nu}\Omega b^{2}}=0\,,

(37)

and that the stationary irreversible entropy production rate is positive

\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}_{\mathrm{i}}=-\beta_{c}\bar{I}_{E}^{c}-\beta_{h}\bar{I}_{E}^{h}-\beta_{w}\bar{I}_{E}^{w}\geq 0\,,

(38)

which – in the regime where the Pauli-type rate equation is valid – constitutes the second law of thermodynamics and bounds the coefficient of performance of our engine by the corresponding Carnot bound, see the discussion in App.
F
.
Related, one finds that a Crooks heat exchange fluctuation theorem
crooks1999a
is obeyed and furthermore, that the standard thermodynamic uncertainty relation (TUR)
barato2015a
,
gingrich2016a
holds, which e.g. for the current and its fluctuations from the cold reservoir reads

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{S}_{E}^{c}\bar{\sigma_{\mathrm{i}}}}{\left(\bar{I}_{E}^{c}\right)^{2}}\geq 2\,.

(39)

The l.h.s. of this equation can be seen as a measure for the reliability of the engine: Its fluctuations cannot be arbitrarily small but follow a universal bound.
Additionally, the above equation can – analogous to Ref.
pietzonka2018a
– be used to bound the coefficient of performance (see App.
F
)
in the regime where
\bar{I}_{E}^{c}>0
,
\bar{I}_{E}^{w}>0
and
\beta_{c}\geq\beta_{h}\geq\beta_{w}

\kappa\leq\bar{\kappa}
where

\displaystyle\bar{\kappa}=\kappa_{\rm Ca}\frac{1}{1+2\frac{\bar{I}_{E}^{c}}{\bar{S}_{E}^{c}(\beta_{c}-\beta_{h})}}\,,

(40)

which can be a significantly tighter restriction than the standard Carnot bound

V
Summary

We have suggested a model for interacting two-level systems that – when dissipatively driven by three independent reservoirs – perform a useful function as a collective quantum absorption refrigerator.
Globally valid conservation laws allowed us to perform a simplified analysis within the standard framework of rate equations.
Despite this fact, we could find a true quadratic quantum speedup in the cooling current, while standard thermodynamic laws were obeyed.
We also remark that the boosted cooling current cannot break the third law of thermodynamics, since the operational cooling window is always finite.

In suitable parameter regimes – narrow spectral densities driving desired transitions exclusively and furthermore not too large temperatures – we could further reduce the state space of our model to an ideal quantum absorption refrigerator, for which analytic results are available.
The full counting statistics formalism allowed us to investigate deviations of our model from this ideal performance.
Here, we found a reduced cooling operational window, which however did not affect the quadratic speedup.

We emphasize that – despite the simple rate equation structure of our underlying equations – our results are not in conflict with Refs.
holubec2019
,
liu2021a
, as coherence is a basis-dependent concept.
Indeed, our proper collective basis features highly coherent states in the local basis of the individual working fluid particles.

We did not consider corrections to the collectivity of our model nor to the parity conservation explicitly.
If the initial preparation prepares a small fraction of the population in other subspaces, we do not expect significant corrections as the subspaces still evolve independently, see App.
A
.
However, imperfect implementations of our Hamiltonian may lead to always-present transitions between the subspaces, for which we expect a quick breakdown of the cooling function – just as it would not work for
N
independently driven two-level systems.
Likewise, we did not consider the effect of stronger system-reservoir coupling strengths.
While one can of course expect corrections
bhandari2021a
for stronger couplings, we do not expect these to directly affect the scaling behaviour of the refrigerator, since for a perfectly implemented Hamiltonian the underlying symmetries are respected to all orders.
To model such imperfections would be a technically more demanding challenge but as our model can be implemented with at least
N=5
two-level systems, corresponding studies are within reach and are an interesting possibility of further research.

Finally, we remark that although we have treated the cold reservoir by non-interacting bosons, the true benefit of a boosted cooling functionality would be revealed if that reservoir is replaced by a quantum system of interest.
This would enable one to explore the various quantum phenomena near ground states of interacting many-body systems, or to find solutions to quantum search problems encoded in such ground states.
We therefore believe that our first-step exposition can be useful to design machines exploiting quantum effects.

Appendix A
Fragility considerations

As discussed in the main text, our model operates at steady state in the maximum angular momentum and positive parity subspace, and thus requires the initial preparation of some representative state of that subspace.
For example, the energetically highest state of that sector
|v_{N/2}^{+}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[|N/2,+N/2\rangle+|N/2,-N/2\rangle\right]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[|0\ldots 0\rangle+|1\ldots 1\rangle\right]
is a Greenberger-Horne-Zeilinger (GHZ) state
greenberger1990a
.
The
N
-qubit GHZ state could be prepared by unitary pulses from a product state via
e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\pi/2\sigma^{1}_{y}\otimes\ldots\otimes\sigma^{N}_{y}}|0\ldots 0\rangle
(where the sign depends on whether
(N+1)/2
is even or odd).
Also measurements of the parity would prepare the GHZ state from a simple product state.
Alternatively, one could relax to the ground state of a finite-size Lipkin-Meshkov-Glick model in the normal phase and then adiabatically deform it
Klo19
into the superradiant phase – where the Hamiltonian corresponds to our Eq. (
1
) and has the even parity ground state
|v_{1/2}^{+}\rangle
.
Such schemes all appear challenging to implement but appear within reach of current experimental abilities, as recent experiments
zhao2021a
have created GHZ states on thousands of qubits.

We consider the consequences of imperfect initial preparation below, which suggest that for odd
N
imperfect initial preparation is not the main error source.

A.1
Imperfect angular momentum preparation

If instead one prepares a fraction of the system’s population in other subspaces with smaller total angular momentum (but even parity), the structure of our system Hamiltonian (
1
) would not change, but
m
could only assume smaller values.
That is, the relevant lower states in Fig.
1
would remain unchanged, but the Clebsch-Gordan coefficients (
9
) (and derived transition rates) would be reduced by inserting smaller
j
values.
If for large
N
the current of the maximum-
j
sector (
j=N/2
) scales as

I_{j}\approx\alpha j^{2}
, the average cooling current (with
P_{j},P_{j-1},\ldots
denoting the probabilities of the respective sectors in the initial state preparation) would be

\displaystyle I\approx P_{j}I_{j}+P_{j-1}I_{j-1}+\ldots\,,

(42)

which is not a drastic modification as long as only the large-
j
sectors are occupied.

A.2
Imperfect parity preparation

If we assume that we have prepared a mixture of even and odd parity sectors, things depend on whether
N
is even or odd.

If
N
is odd, both even and odd parity sectors are completely symmetric (this then also holds for the other angular momentum subspaces).
Therefore, all transitions are the same, and both sectors would contribute equally to the cooling (of course only in the operational window).
The functionality would in this case not be diminished at all.

In contrast, if
N
is even, the even parity sector (with states
\{|v_{0}^{+}\rangle,|v_{1}^{+}\rangle,|v_{2}^{+}\rangle,\ldots\}
) has different energy differences than the odd parity sector
(with states
|v_{1}^{-}\rangle,|v_{2}^{-}\rangle,|v_{3}^{-}\rangle,\ldots\})

2
2
footnotemark:

2

. Thus, if the even sector is tuned to operate as absorbtion refrigerator, the other sector will most certainly be out of its operational window.
In the best case (e.g. if the spectral densities are highly peaked on the
\Omega
,
3\Omega
, and
4\Omega
transitions that exist in the even parity sector for even
N
), it would simply mean that the odd sector does not contribute to steady state cooling, as there only the
3\Omega
-transition of the work reservoir could drive the transitions
|v_{1}^{-}\rangle\leftrightarrow|v_{2}^{-}\rangle
.
In the worst case (e.g. broader spectral densities), the cycle of the odd subspace could also be traversed in the opposite direction, effectively superradiantly heating the cold reservoir, such that this case should be avoided.

Appendix B
Generating peaked spectral densities via an inverted reaction-coordinate mapping

Harmonic reservoirs may be reorganized by Bogoliubov transforms, and if these reservoirs are coupled by spectral density
\Gamma_{\nu}(\omega)
to a system, such transformations will also affect the spectral density.
A particularly important mapping – the reaction-coordinate mapping – reorganizes the Hamiltonian such that the original system is first coupled with coupling strength
\tilde{\lambda}_{\nu}
to a single collective bosonic mode of energy
\tilde{\Omega}_{\nu}
that is then coupled to a residual reservoir via a transformed spectral density
\tilde{\Gamma}_{\nu}(\omega)
.
Specifically, these parameters are fully determined by the original spectral density of the model (see e.g. Eqns (5-7) of Ref.
nazir2019a
).
The squared reaction coordinate energy is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\Omega}^{2}=\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}\omega^{3}\Gamma(\omega)d\omega}{\int_{0}^{\infty}\omega\Gamma(\omega)d\omega}\,,

(43)

and the squared coupling strength between system and reaction coordinate computes as

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi\tilde{\Omega}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\omega\Gamma(\omega)d\omega\,.

(44)

Then, the mapped residual spectral density is obtained via

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}(\omega)=\frac{4\tilde{\lambda}^{2}\Gamma(\omega)}{\left[\frac{1}{\pi}{\cal P}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\omega^{\prime})}{\omega^{\prime}-\omega}\right]^{2}+\left[\Gamma(\omega)\right]^{2}}\,,

(45)

where
{\cal P}
denotes the principal value.
From this we see that scaling the original spectral density by a constant leaves the mapped spectral density invariant, and this argument is typically used to motivate a perturbative treatment for the supersystem composed of original system and reaction-coordinate even in the limit of strong coupling with large
\Gamma(\omega)
.
Unfortunately, the first numerator integral in (
43
) does not converge when we try this for our assumed spectral density (
5
) in the main text.
Therefore, we consider a regularized spectral density

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm reg}(\omega)=\Gamma(\omega)\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\Delta^{2}+\omega^{2}}\,,

(46)

with
\Gamma(\omega)
taken from (
5
) (for brevity we drop the index
\nu
since it is individually done for every reservoir).
Then we get

\displaystyle\tilde{\Omega}^{2}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2}+\delta^{2}+2\Delta\delta\,,\qquad\tilde{\lambda}^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi\tilde{\Omega}}\frac{\Gamma\pi\delta\varepsilon\Delta^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}+(\Delta+\delta)^{2}}

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}(\omega)

\displaystyle=2\frac{\delta}{\tilde{\Omega}}\left[\varepsilon^{2}+(\Delta+\delta)^{2}\right]\frac{\omega}{\omega^{2}+(\Delta+2\delta)^{2}}\,,

(47)

which is a Lorentz-Drude spectral density, see Fig.
6
.

Figure 6:
Mapping procedure for spectral densities. Since the original spectral density (black) does not decay fast enough, we consider the regularized version (red). The mapped spectral density (green) is of Lorentz-Drude type and rather structureless.

Since Bogoliubov transforms are invertible, we can also turn the perspective.
Then, a system that is via coupling
\tilde{\lambda}
, coupled to a harmonic mode of energy
\tilde{\Omega}
that is further coupled to a reservoir with a Lorentz-Drude spectral density, feels the presence of the harmonic mode with its reservoir as an effectively highly peaked spectral density (
46
), which for
\Delta\to\infty
reduces to (
5
) in the main text.
Put more generally, the harmonic mode acts as an energy filter between system and reservoir that generates a sharply peaked spectral density seen by the system.

Appendix C
Superradiant thermalization close to the ground state for one reservoir

Connecting the WF of a type (
1
) to a single heat bath with the dipole operator
J_{x}
gives rise to the population dynamics, i.e. Eq. (
6
), which thermalizes the system in the basis (
3
).
The explicit dynamics of the population in the state
|v_{a}^{+}\rangle
is given as

\begin{split}\dot{\rho}_{a}=&\frac{1}{4}\gamma(E_{\left|a-1\right|}-E_{a})\left|\alpha^{+}_{a-1}\right|^{2}\rho_{\left|a-1\right|}\\
&+\frac{1}{4}\gamma(E_{a+1}-E_{a})\left|\alpha^{-}_{a+1}\right|^{2}\rho_{a+1}\\
&-\frac{1}{4}\gamma(E_{a}-E_{a+1})\left|\alpha^{+}_{a}\right|^{2}\rho_{a}\\
&-\frac{1}{4}\gamma(E_{a}-E_{\left|a-1\right|})\left|\alpha^{-}_{a}\right|^{2}\rho_{a}\,.\end{split}

(48)

Here
\gamma
is the energy-dependent coupling strength from Eq. (
4
).
Note that
\gamma(-\omega)=\Gamma(\omega)n(\omega)
where
\Gamma(\omega)
is a spectral density of the reservoir and
n(\omega)
the Bose-Einstein distribution.
The Clebsch-Gordan coefficients
\alpha_{a}^{\pm}
defined in Eq. (
9
) are responsible for the
N^{2}
boost in the dynamics close to the ground state
a\ll N
.
They fulfill a trivial identity
\alpha^{+}_{a}=\alpha^{-}_{-a}
.
Equation. (
48
) takes the same form for
N
odd and even.

To quantify the thermalization time
t_{\rm th}
we compute the time-dependent relative entropy
S(\rho(t)||\rho_{\beta})={\rm Tr}\left\{\rho(t)\left(\log{\rho(t)}-\log{\rho_{\beta}}\right)\right\}
between the state
\rho(t)
and the thermal state
\rho_{\beta}
given by the inverse temperature
\beta
of the reservoir.
We define
t_{\rm th}
as the time point where
S(\rho(t)||\rho_{\beta})\leq 10^{-6}
. Because of the contractivity of Markovian dynamics such definition of
t_{\rm th}
is unique.

In the following we use the Ohmic spectral density of the heat reservoir
\Gamma(\omega)=\omega e^{-\omega/\omega_{c}}
with the cut-off
\omega_{c}=100\Omega
and we set its (inverse) temperature to
\beta_{\rm f}=4\Omega^{-1}
.
Initiating the WF in the thermal state with the inverse temperature
\beta_{\rm i}=1\Omega^{-1}
we compute the thermalization time

using Eq. (
48
) after bringing the WF into contact with the heat bath, see Fig.
7
.
Apparently, for growing
N
, the fixed temperatures
\beta_{\rm i}
and
\beta_{\rm f}
allow to populate only the low lying levels of the WF.
In our model of the WF these are exactly the levels whose dynamics is boosted by the Clebsch-Gordan.
Thus, we observe

scaling of the thermalization time

.
This is in contrast to the system on
N
mutually non-interacting spins forming the WF which are collectively coupled to the heat reservoir

.
Such a system, described by a collective Hamiltonian

, can also exhibit superradiant scaling in the maximal

m\approx 0

m
labels the

If the dynamics is restricted to the region around the ground state only, the superradiant boost is lost.

Klo19

with the diagonal matrix

containing the no-jump evolution, we can easily construct the waiting-time distribution

\displaystyle\omega(\tau)=-\frac{d}{d\tau}P_{0}(\tau)=-{\rm Tr}\left\{R_{0}e^{R_{0}\tau}\rho_{0}\right\}\,,

(49)

which can be simply evaluated as
R_{0}
is a diagonal matrix.
For example, when
\rho_{0}=|v_{a+1}^{+}\rangle\langle v_{a+1}^{+}|
is also diagonal, the waiting time distribution for the decay of state
|v_{a+1}^{+}\rangle

|v_{a}^{+}\rangle

.
Since at zero temperature, relaxation to the ground state is the only decay channel, we may just add the individual waiting times to get an estimate on the total relaxation time.

Appendix D
Supertransmittance close to the ground state for two reservoirs

If our system is only coupled to work and cold reservoir (
\bar{\Gamma}_{h}=0
) and the spectral densities of the remaining cold and work reservoir are not highly peaked, we generate a collective transport situation similar to Ref.
vogl2011a
, but with the difference that there the system was just a collection of non-interacting spins.
In that system, due to the equidistant spectrum, an analytic calculation of the nonequilibrium steady state was possible in terms of some average temperature.
There, collective transport enhancements were only visible in regimes where both reservoirs were rather hot, essentially because large Clebsch-Gordan coefficients were encountered only in the middle of the spectrum.
In contrast, for our system the nonequilibrium steady state is not so easy to obtain in a closed analytic form as spectrum of
H_{S}=\Omega J_{z}^{2}
is not equidistant.
However, when both reservoirs are at low temperature
\beta_{c/w}2\Omega\gtrapprox 1
, we also find a quadratically scaled current (from work reservoir to cold reservoir
\left|\bar{I}_{E}^{c}\right|\propto N^{2}
), which can be understood analytically.
If there is no hot reservoir and work and cold reservoir act analogously, the red and green transitions in Fig.
1
should be superimposed and drive only transitions between neighbouring states
|v_{a}^{+}\rangle\leftrightarrow|v_{a\pm 1}^{+}\rangle
.
Moreover, if already
\beta_{c/w}2\Omega\gtrapprox 1
, this is even more fulfilled for the higher-energy transitions (shaded in Fig.
1
), and it will be much more likely to relax the system than to excite it.
As a result, the dynamics is dominantly described by the two-lowest states

\{|v_{1/2}^{+}\rangle,|v_{3/2}^{+}\rangle\}
, where we have the rate matrix

R(\chi)=R_{c}(\chi)+R_{w}
with

\displaystyle R_{c}(\chi)

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{c}^{\rm eff}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-n_{c}&+(1+n_{c})e^{-\mathrm{i}\chi 2\Omega}\\
+n_{c}e^{+\mathrm{i}\chi 2\Omega}&-(1+n_{c})\end{array}\right)\,,

\displaystyle R_{w}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{w}^{\rm eff}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-n_{w}&+(1+n_{w})\\
+n_{w}&-(1+n_{w})\end{array}\right)\,,

where
n_{c}=\frac{1}{e^{\beta_{c}2\Omega}-1}
and

n_{w}=\frac{1}{e^{\beta_{w}2\Omega}-1}
denote the Bose distributions evaluated at the lowest energy difference
E_{3/2}-E_{1/2}=2\Omega
and where

\Gamma_{c/w}^{\rm eff}=\Gamma_{c/w}(2\Omega)\left|\langle v_{1/2}^{+}|J_{x}|v_{3/2}^{+}\rangle\right|^{2}=\Gamma_{c/w}(2\Omega)/4\left[\frac{N}{2}\left(\frac{N}{2}+1\right)-\frac{3}{4}\right]

are the effective bare transition rates determined by spectral densities (
5
) and the Clebsch-Gordan coefficients (
9

As both
\Gamma_{c/w}^{\rm eff}\propto N^{2}
, the dominant eigenvalue
\lambda(\chi)
and therefore all cumulants
touchette2009a

in this regime.
From its first derivative with respect to the counting field
\bar{I}_{E}^{c}=-\mathrm{i}\partial_{\chi}\lambda(\chi)|_{\chi=0}
(or, alternatively by computing it via (
11
)), we get the current from the cold reservoir

\displaystyle\bar{I}_{E}^{c}\to-\frac{2\Omega\Gamma_{c}^{\rm eff}\Gamma_{w}^{\rm eff}(n_{w}-n_{c})}{\Gamma_{c}^{\rm eff}[1+2n_{c}]+\Gamma_{w}^{\rm eff}[1+2n_{w}]}\,,

(55)

which is negative when
n_{w}>n_{c}
(as heat flows from the work reservoir through the system to the cold reservoir) and changes sign under reversing the thermal biases
\beta_{c}\leftrightarrow\beta_{w}
.
Analogous to known results for bosonic transport through a two-level system
segal2005b
, the above formula may display some mild rectification effects for asymmetric coupling strengths – with the difference that the effective coupling constants scale quadratically with
N
.

In contrast, considering the limit
\bar{\Gamma}_{w}=0
and
\delta_{h}\to 0
with
\varepsilon_{h}=6\Omega
, the two remaining reservoirs act very differently and one can observe stronger rectification effects under exchanging the thermal bias.
However, the rectification ratio is not further enhanced with increasing
N
.

Appendix E
Current and noise formulas

The moment-generating function for the distribution of energies entering the system from the cold reservoir during the interval
[0,t]
is given by

\displaystyle M(\chi,t)={\rm Tr}\left\{\rho(\chi,t)\right\}={\rm Tr}\left\{e^{R(\chi)t}\rho_{0}\right\}\,.

(56)

From it, the moments of the energies transferred can be obtained by taking derivatives
{\langle E^{k}\rangle}_{t}=(-\mathrm{i}\partial_{\chi})^{k}M(\chi,t)|_{\chi=0}
.

To evaluate the energy current from the cold reservoir, we compute the time-derivative of the first moment

\displaystyle I_{E}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}{\langle E\rangle}_{t}=(-\mathrm{i}\partial_{\chi}){\rm Tr}\left\{R(\chi)e^{R(\chi)t}\rho_{0}\right\}|_{\chi=0}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}{\rm Tr}\left\{R^{\prime}(0)\rho(t)\right\}\,,

(57)

where we have used that
{\rm Tr}\left\{R(0)v\right\}=0
for any vector
v
due to the trace-preservation of the rate matrix
R(0)
and furthermore
\rho(t)=e^{R(0)t}\rho_{0}
.
In the long-term limit this simplifies to (
11
) in the main text, where
\bar{\rho}
is just the – properly normalized – steady state solution of the rate equation that is defined by Eq.
(
III.3
).

The noise is given by the time-derivative of the second cumulant.
Since we cannot analytically obtain the dominant eigenvalue of the resulting
(N+1)/2\times(N+1)/2
-dimensional rate matrix
R(\chi)
, we obtain the noise by adapting the methods developed for the counting-statistics of time-dependent (driven) conductors
benito2016b
,
restrepo2019a
to the simpler undriven case.
The time-dependent noise then becomes

\displaystyle S_{E}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\left[{\langle E^{2}\rangle}_{t}-{\langle E\rangle}_{t}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=(-\mathrm{i}\partial_{\chi})^{2}{\rm Tr}\left\{R(\chi)e^{R(\chi)t}\rho_{0}\right\}-2I_{E}(t){\langle E\rangle}_{t}

\displaystyle=-{\rm Tr}\left\{R^{\prime\prime}(0)\rho(t)\right\}-2{\rm Tr}\left\{R^{\prime}(0)\left(\partial_{\chi}e^{R(\chi)t}|_{\chi=0}\right)\rho_{0}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad+2{\rm Tr}\left\{R^{\prime}(0)\rho(t)\right\}{\rm Tr}\left\{\left(\partial_{\chi}e^{R(\chi)t}|_{\chi=0}\right)\rho_{0}\right\}

\displaystyle=-{\rm Tr}\left\{R^{\prime\prime}(0)\rho(t)\right\}-2{\rm Tr}\left\{R^{\prime}(0)\sigma(t)\right\}\,,

(58)

where we have again used the trace conservation property of the rate matrix
R(0)
and in the last step we have combined the last two terms by introducing
the auxiliary quantity (that we arranged as a vector as well)

\displaystyle\sigma(t)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\chi}\left.\frac{e^{R(\chi)t}\rho_{0}}{{\rm Tr}\left\{e^{R(\chi)t}\rho_{0}\right\}}\right|_{\chi=0}

(59)

\displaystyle=\left(\partial_{\chi}e^{R(\chi)t}|_{\chi=0}\right)\rho_{0}-\rho(t){\rm Tr}\left\{\left(\partial_{\chi}e^{R(\chi)t}|_{\chi=0}\right)\rho_{0}\right\}\,.

By construction, we have
{\rm Tr}\left\{\sigma(t)\right\}=(1,\ldots,1)\cdot\sigma=0
.
Furthermore, the auxiliary obeys the differential equation

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}=R^{\prime}(0)\rho(t)+R(0)\sigma(t)-\rho(t){\rm Tr}\left\{R^{\prime}(0)\rho(t)\right\}\,,

(60)

which – to obtain the full time-dependent noise – would have to be solved with the initial condition
\sigma(0)=(0,\ldots,0)^{\rm T}
.
However, if we are just interested in the steady-state values, we may set the r.h.s. of the above equation to zero and insert
\rho(t)\to\bar{\rho}
to solve for
\bar{\sigma}
, leading to (
III.3
) in the main text.
Equation (
E
) then reduces in the long-term limit to Eq. (
23
) in the main text.

The generalization of such methods to non-Markovian systems
flindt2008a
,
flindt2010a
or time-dependently driven ones
benito2016b
,
restrepo2019a
is also possible.

Appendix F
Bound for the coefficient of performance for three reservoirs

In the regime where
\beta_{c}>\beta_{h}>\beta_{w}
it follows from Eqns. (
35
) and (
38
) that cooling of the coldest reservoir (
\bar{I}_{E}^{c}>0
) is only possible via heat entering from the work reservoir (
\bar{I}_{E}^{w}>0
) and dumping the waste heat into the hot reservoir (
\bar{I}_{E}^{h}<0
).
From the positivity of the entropy production rate (
38
) one can then conclude that the cooling coefficient of performance (we now explicitly assume that
\beta_{c}\geq\beta_{h}\geq\beta_{w}
and
\bar{I}_{E}^{c}\geq 0
and
\bar{I}_{E}^{w}\geq 0
)

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\bar{I}_{E}^{c}}{\bar{I}_{E}^{w}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta_{h}\bar{I}_{E}^{c}}{\left[-(\beta_{c}\!-\!\beta_{w})\bar{I}_{E}^{c}\!-\!(\beta_{h}\!-\!\beta_{w})\bar{I}_{E}^{h}\right]\!+\!\beta_{w}\bar{I}_{E}^{w}\!+\!(\beta_{c}\!-\!\beta_{h})\bar{I}_{E}^{c}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta_{h}\bar{I}_{E}^{c}}{\beta_{w}\bar{I}_{E}^{w}+(\beta_{c}-\beta_{h})\bar{I}_{E}^{c}}\leq\frac{\beta_{h}}{\beta_{c}-\beta_{h}}\equiv\kappa_{\rm Ca}

(61)

is upper-bounded by the usual Carnot bound.
The second line above can be verified by using energy conservation (
35
).
We have written it in a way that all three summands of the denominator are separately positive – positivity of the term in square brackets is seen from combining (
38
) and (
35
) – and the inequalities then follow by discarding parts of the denominator.

Analogously, we can use Eq. (
39
) to obtain Eq. (
40
) (under the same conditions as above) as follows

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta_{h}\bar{I}_{E}^{c}}{\bar{\sigma}_{\mathrm{i}}+\beta_{w}\bar{I}_{E}^{w}+(\beta_{c}-\beta_{h})\bar{I}_{E}^{c}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta_{h}\bar{I}_{E}^{c}}{\bar{\sigma}_{\mathrm{i}}+(\beta_{c}-\beta_{h})\bar{I}_{E}^{c}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta_{h}\bar{I}_{E}^{c}}{\frac{2(\bar{I}_{E}^{c})^{2}}{\bar{S}_{E}^{c}}+(\beta_{c}-\beta_{h})\bar{I}_{E}^{c}}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{\rm Ca}\frac{1}{1+2\frac{\bar{I}_{E}^{c}}{\bar{S}_{E}^{c}(\beta_{c}-\beta_{h})}}\,,

(62)

where in the second inequality we have used the TUR (
39
).

Appendix G
Reduced model

In the ideal limit where our system is described by the lowest states
\{|v_{1/2}^{+}\rangle,|v_{3/2}^{+}\rangle,|v_{5/2}^{+}\rangle\}
only with the three transitions driven exclusively by the reservoirs, the rate matrix assumes the form

R(\chi)=R_{c}(\chi)+R_{h}+R_{w}
with

\displaystyle R_{c}(\chi)

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{c}^{\rm eff}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}-n_{c}&+(1+n_{c})e^{-\mathrm{i}\chi 2\Omega}&0\\
+n_{c}e^{+\mathrm{i}\chi 2\Omega}&-(1+n_{c})&0\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right)\,,

\displaystyle R_{h}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{h}^{\rm eff}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}-n_{h}&0&+(1+n_{h})\\
0&0&0\\
+n_{h}&0&-(1+n_{h})\end{array}\right)\,,

\displaystyle R_{w}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{w}^{\rm eff}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&0&0\\
0&-n_{w}&+(1+n_{w})\\
0&+n_{w}&-(1+n_{w})\end{array}\right)\,,

where
n_{c}=\frac{1}{e^{\beta_{c}2\Omega}-1}
,
n_{h}=\frac{1}{e^{\beta_{h}6\Omega}-1}
, and

n_{w}=\frac{1}{e^{\beta_{w}4\Omega}-1}
denote the Bose distributions evaluated at the respective energy differences.
Technically, this limit is achieved with perfectly tuned
\varepsilon_{c}=2\Omega
,
\varepsilon_{h}=6\Omega
,
\varepsilon_{w}=4\Omega
and highly peaked spectral densities
\Omega\gg\delta_{\nu}\to 0
to drive the transitions exclusively.
A system that is initially prepared in one of the three lowest state will then not be able to leave it.
Within this subspace, the effective bare transition rates become

\displaystyle\Gamma_{c}^{\rm eff}

\displaystyle=\bar{\Gamma}_{c}\left|\left\langle v_{1/2}^{+}\left|J_{x}\right|v_{3/2}^{+}\right\rangle\right|^{2}=\frac{\bar{\Gamma}_{c}}{4}\left(\frac{N}{2}\left(\frac{N}{2}-1\right)-\frac{3}{4}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{w}^{\rm eff}

\displaystyle=\bar{\Gamma}_{w}\left|\left\langle v_{3/2}^{+}\left|J_{x}\right||v_{5/2}^{+}\rangle\right\rangle\right|^{2}=\frac{\bar{\Gamma}_{w}}{4}\left(\frac{N}{2}\left(\frac{N}{2}-1\right)-\frac{15}{4}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{h}^{\rm eff}

\displaystyle=\bar{\Gamma}_{h}\left|\left\langle v_{1/2}^{+}\left|\frac{J_{x}^{2}}{N}\right|v_{5/2}^{+}\right\rangle\right|^{2}

(72)

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{\Gamma}_{h}}{16N^{2}}\left(\frac{N}{2}\left(\frac{N}{2}-1\right)-\frac{3}{4}\right)\left(\frac{N}{2}\left(\frac{N}{2}-1\right)-\frac{15}{4}\right)\,,

which makes their quadratic scaling with
N
very apparent.

With standard methods (see the previous section or Ref
segal2018a
) one can calculate current and noise.

The analysis becomes particularly simple in the limit
n_{w}\to\infty
, where one can coarse-grain the two excited states into one.
For the coarse-grained probabilities
P_{0}=P_{1/2}^{+}
and
P_{12}=P_{3/2}^{+}+P_{5/2}^{+}
we can – by using that the conditional probabilities
P_{3/2}^{+}/P_{12}
and
P_{1/2}^{+}/P_{12}
both approach
1/2
– set up a simpler rate matrix

\displaystyle\tilde{R}(\chi)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-\Gamma_{c}^{\rm eff}n_{c}&\frac{\Gamma_{c}^{\rm eff}}{2}(1+n_{c})e^{-\mathrm{i}\chi 2\Omega}\\
+\Gamma_{c}^{\rm eff}n_{c}e^{+\mathrm{i}\chi 2\Omega}&-\frac{\Gamma_{c}^{\rm eff}}{2}(1+n_{c})\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-\Gamma_{h}^{\rm eff}n_{h}&+\frac{\Gamma_{h}^{\rm eff}}{2}(1+n_{h})\\
+\Gamma_{h}^{\rm eff}n_{h}&-\frac{\Gamma_{h}^{\rm eff}}{2}(1+n_{h})\end{array}\right)\,.

The dominant eigenvalue of this matrix is simpler to compute and yields a particularly simple expression for the current

\displaystyle\bar{I}_{E}^{c}

\displaystyle\to\frac{2\Omega\Gamma_{c}^{\rm eff}\Gamma_{h}^{\rm eff}(n_{c}-n_{h})}{\Gamma_{c}^{\rm eff}(1+3n_{c})+\Gamma_{h}^{\rm eff}(1+3n_{h})}\,.

(77)

One can see that to achieve cooling of the cold reservoir one needs to have
n_{c}>n_{h}
, such that cooling works when
\beta_{c}\in(\beta_{h},3\beta_{h})
, which bounds the range of applicability of our engine.
Furthermore, if
\Gamma_{c/h}^{\rm eff}
scale quadratically (our limit implies that
\Gamma_{w}^{\rm eff}
is not the bottleneck either) with the number of two-level systems, so does the current.
For the noise computation we can proceed similarly and obtain

\displaystyle\bar{S}_{E}^{c}

\displaystyle\to\frac{4\Omega^{2}\Gamma_{c}^{\rm eff}\Gamma_{h}^{\rm eff}\left[A+2\Gamma_{c}^{\rm eff}\Gamma_{h}^{\rm eff}B\right]}{\left[\Gamma_{c}^{\rm eff}(1+3n_{c})+\Gamma_{h}^{\rm eff}(1+3n_{h})\right]^{3}}\,,

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\equiv\left[\left(\Gamma_{c}^{\rm eff}(1+3n_{c})\right)^{2}+\left(\Gamma_{h}^{\rm eff}(1+3n_{h})\right)^{2}\right]\times

\displaystyle\qquad\times(n_{c}+n_{h}+2n_{c}n_{h})\,,

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle\equiv n_{c}(1+n_{c})+n_{h}(1+n_{h})+12n_{c}n_{h}

\displaystyle\qquad+15n_{c}n_{h}(n_{c}+n_{h})+18n_{c}^{2}n_{h}^{2}\,.

(78)

Finally, we remark that similar results arise in the limit where the transitions of the work reservoir are driven by a laser instead, although the proper definition of heat and work for periodically driven master equations is debated
kalaee2021a
.
In the simplest realization of a resonant laser, the work reservoir is described by the rate matrix

\displaystyle R_{w}\to R_{L}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{L}^{\rm eff}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&0&0\\
0&-1&+1\\
0&+1&-1\end{array}\right)\,,

which merely reflects the fact that absorption and emission are equally likely and where
\Gamma_{L}^{\rm eff}
is proportional to the pump intensity.
Considering strong driving with
\Gamma_{L}^{\rm eff}\to\infty
leads to the same effects as an infinite-temperature work reservoir for current and noise.
