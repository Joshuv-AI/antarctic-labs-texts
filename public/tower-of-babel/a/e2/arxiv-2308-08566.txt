The classical Doppler effect revisited by the mathematical description of the phase function
Óscar Alejos and José María Muñoz

Abstract
This paper aims to shed some more light on one of the best known phenomena in the field of physics, the Doppler effect, in particular, on its classical version. Although, as mentioned, it is a phenomenon already described more than 150 years ago, the general case, even considering constant velocities, continues to be the subject of very recent bibliographical works which either show it incompletely or start from a not entirely accurate description of it. Interestingly, general expressions of both frequency shift and aberration are obtained by describing the phase of the wave in the reference frame in which the propagating medium is at rest, which allows for a generalization of the resulting expressions for any reference frame, even those where the propagating medium is not still.

Introduction

Doppler effect (DE) is a very well-known topic in Physics since its discovery by Doppler in 1842
[
1
]
. Many textbooks

]
include amidst their contents references to this effect, both in its classical and relativistic version. Within the classical study, the particular case of sound waves is discussed, where the wave requires a propagating medium. However, only the situation when the observer is within the trajectory of the emitter is considered in most textbooks, i.e., emitter and observer positions are collinear with emitter’s trajectory. The non-collinear case is indeed the subject of recent scientific papers
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
, where even some attempts to unify the classical and relativistic DEs are made. However, some presented results do not consider the nonlinear dependence of phase on time at the observer’s location, and erroneously compare one period of the emitted signal with what it is called a period perceived by the observer, i.e., the interval of time between to identical vibration states of the wave
[
7
]
. In addition, some other bibliographic references
[
10
]
present an incomplete treatment of the noncollinear case.

Accordingly, the present paper gives a clearer view of classical DE by expounding a full description of this phenomenon in terms of the spatial and temporal dependence of the phase of the propagating wave in the medium. The expressions obtained not only can be tested against those provided by the traditional geometrical description, but also allow for describing the noncollinear case on a restless propagating medium.

The work is organized according to the following structure. First, the case of a stationary observer in the reference frame in which the propagating medium is at rest is reviewed, the emitter moving at constant velocity in that reference frame. The problem is addressed by constructing the function that defines the phase at each instant and at each point in space in the observer’s reference frame, in a much more intuitive way than other approaches. The frequency shift appears naturally from the derivative of the phase function with respect to time. Importantly, the aberration effect perceived by an observer which is not in the path of the emitter also appears from the space derivative of the phase function. In the following section, the case of a moving observer in a reference frame where the propagating medium is still and the emitter at rest is briefly considered. Finally, the more general case of moving observer and emitter is addressed. This study provides with a expression of the frequency shift due to the DE in terms of relative positions and velocities, which can be easily extended to any reference frame, particularly, those where the propagating medium is not still and wave fronts drift due to the medium velocity.

2
Moving emitter

Since in the framework of Galilean relativity, the wave equation is not invariant with respect to coordinate transformations, the calculation of the phase of a wave in a reference frame in which the emitter is in motion with respect to the observer cannot be performed by simply considering circular wave fronts in emitter’s reference frame and applying the corresponding coordinate transformation. The procedure is therefore not so straightforward, but must be approached from the wavefronts created by the emitter at each of the points it travels through.

To determine the phase of each point in space, it is then necessary to first estimate the position
\mathbf{x}_{0}
where the emitter was located when the corresponding wavefront that has reached that point was emitted. The time
\tau
that the wavefront requires to travel the distance from
\mathbf{x}_{0}
to the point is given by the quotient between that distance and the phase velocity.
The phase at the point at any time
t
is indeed equal to the phase the emitter had when it was at the position
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, a time
\tau
ago.
Let us call
\varphi_{e}\left(t\right)
to the current phase of the emitter, the phase at any point is then given by the function
\varphi_{e}\left(t-\tau\right)
,
\tau
being a function of
t
determined by the point location.

According to the above discussion, a pair of equations can be proposed in the case of a emitter moving at constant velocity, as shown in fig.
1

\left(x,y\right)
at instant

. The wavefront was emitted when the emitter was at position
\left(x_{0},0\right)
, a time
\tau
ago. In this way,

\displaystyle x_{e}-x_{0}

\displaystyle=v\tau\text{,}

(1a)

\displaystyle\left(x-x_{0}\right)^{2}+y^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(c\tau\right)^{2}\text{.}

(1b)

By combining both equations, the second degree equation

\left(c^{2}-v^{2}\right)\tau^{2}-2\left(x-x_{e}\right)v\tau-\left[\left(x-x_{e}\right)^{2}+y^{2}\right]=0

(2)

holds, that can be solved for positive
\tau

\tau

\tau=\frac{1}{c}\frac{1}{1-\beta^{2}}\left[\beta\left(x-x_{e}\right)+\sqrt{\left(x-x_{e}\right)^{2}+\left(1-\beta^{2}\right)y^{2}}\right]\text{.}

(3)

Once the function that establishes the phase as a function of position and time has been determined, the propagation characteristics, i.e., frequency and wave vector, can be easily derived. To do so, it suffices to consider
f^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi}{dt}
for the former and
\mathbf{k}^{\prime}=-\nabla\varphi
for the latter. Starting with the frequency, it turns out that

f^{\prime}=f\left\{1+\frac{\beta}{1-\beta^{2}}\left[\beta+\frac{x-vt}{\sqrt{\left(x-vt\right)^{2}+\left(1-\beta^{2}\right)y^{2}}}\right]\right\}\text{.}

(5)

which explicitly shows the time dependence of the
instantaneous

f^{\prime}=f\left[1+\frac{\beta}{1-\beta^{2}}\left(\beta+\frac{\cos\theta}{\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}}\right)\right]\text{.}

(6)

Proper handling of the above expression (see
B

f^{\prime}=\frac{f}{1-\beta\left(\beta\sin^{2}\theta+\cos\theta\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\right)}\text{,}

(7)

\theta=\vartheta

f^{\prime}=\frac{f}{1-\beta}\text{,}

(8)

\theta=180\degree

f^{\prime}=\frac{f}{1+\beta}\text{.}

(9)

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{\left(1-\beta^{2}\right)\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}}\times

\displaystyle\times\left[\left(\beta\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}+\cos\theta\right)\hat{\mathbf{x}}+\left(1-\beta^{2}\right)\sin\theta\hat{\mathbf{y}}\right]\text{,}

This expression can be straightforwardly rewritten in terms of the angle

as it can be expected, since the direction of propagation at each point is perpendicular to the wavefronts, then pointing in the direction of the segment joining this point and that were the wavefront was started. This result accounts for the effect known as aberration, i.e., when the observer perceives a certain frequency

Moving observer

Within emitter’s reference frame the medium is still, so that (
4
) is rewritten as

\varphi\left(x,y,t\right)=2\pi ft-k\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}\text{.}

(14)

leading to concentric wave fronts centered at emitter’s location, as shown in fig.
5
. The frequency
f^{\prime}
perceived by the observer is obtained from the derivative of the previous expression with time. The use of the convective derivative
\frac{d}{dt}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla
is now necessary, where
\mathbf{v}
represents observer’s velocity, also plotted in fig.
5
. As a result, the frequency perceived by the observer is written as
f^{\prime}=f\left(1-\beta^{\prime}\cos\theta\right)
, where
\beta^{\prime}
is defined fron the magnitude of velocity
v=\beta^{\prime}c
of the observer. This is indeed the DE expected expression for moving observers, and is exactly equal to (
S8
) obtained from the geometrical construct presented in sec.
S2
of the supplementary text. As for the wave vector, the use of the
\nabla
operator results in
\mathbf{k}=-\nabla\varphi=k\left(\cos\theta\hat{\mathbf{x}}+\sin\theta\hat{\mathbf{y}}\right)
. In this case, no aberration must be considered, since wave vectors are directed radially from the emitter, being then perpendicular to the wave fronts.

4
Moving emitter and observer

This case will be addressed directly from the point of view of the phase function. As in the previous sections, the velocities will be taken with respect to the reference frame in which the propagating medium is at rest, so that, without loss of generality, the emitter will be considered to move with velocity
v\hat{\mathbf{x}}
, with
v=\beta c
, and the motion of the observer is given by the vector
v_{x}\hat{\mathbf{x}}+v_{y}\hat{\mathbf{y}}
. The use of the convective derivative yields the expression for the frequency shift as

f^{\prime}=f\left[1-\frac{\beta\left(\beta_{x}-\beta\right)+\frac{\left(\beta_{x}-\beta\right)\cos\theta+\beta_{y}\left(1-\beta^{2}\right)\sin\theta}{\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}}}{1-\beta^{2}}\right]\text{,}

(15)

where
\beta_{x}=\frac{v_{x}}{c}
and
\beta_{y}=\frac{v_{y}}{c}
, and
\theta
being the angle between emitter’s trajectory and the relative instantaneous position between the observer and the emitter, as shown in fig.
6
. Interestingly, despite being a rather complex expression, it predicts that the DE vanishes when both the observer and emitter move parallel with the same velocity, i.e.,
\beta=\beta_{x}
and
\beta_{y}=0
. More importantly, this expression depends indeed on relative positions, through the
\theta
-angle, and relative velocities, that between the emitter and the reference frame in which the propagating medium is at rest, and that between the observer and the emitter. This means, in particular, that the result is valid for any other reference frame. Hence, by defining
\left(\beta^{\prime}_{x},\beta^{\prime}_{y}\right)
as the components of the relative velocity of the observer with respect to the emitter, previous equation can be rewritten as

f^{\prime}=f\left[1-\frac{\beta\beta^{\prime}_{x}\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}+\beta^{\prime}_{x}\cos\theta+\beta^{\prime}_{y}\left(1-\beta^{2}\right)\sin\theta}{\left(1-\beta^{2}\right)\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}}\right]\text{,}

(16)

where
\beta
will still be used to refer to the relative velocity between the emitter and the reference frame in which the propagating medium is at rest.

Figure 6:
DE for the case when both emitter and observer are moving with respect to the reference frame where the propagating medium is at rest. The velocity of the emitter is given by vector
v\hat{mathbf{x}}
, while the observer moves with velocity
v_{x}\hat{\mathbf{x}}+v_{y}\hat{\mathbf{y}}
. The angle
\theta
defining the relative instantaneous position between observer and emitter is also displayed.

As an example, fig.
7
shows the case where the propagating medium moves with respect to the emitter. The medium velocity is defined as
-v\hat{\mathbf{x}}
, and three different velocities are chosen, corresponding to
\beta=-0.25,-0.5,\text{and}-0.75
. These are indeed the cases shown in fig.
2
but seen from the emitter’s reference frame. Due to the moving medium, a drift of the wave fronts occurs. This drift promotes the gathering of wave fronts to the right of the emitter and its spreading to the left or, equivalently, different propagating velocities of the wave depending on direction. As it has been mentioned above, despite these different propagating velocities, a stationary observer with respect to the emitter will not notice any frequency shift, since the wavelengths in the direction of the observation will enlarge or shorten accordingly.

The emitter moves with velocity

vdt

\varphi+d\varphi

\frac{k^{\prime}_{y}}{k^{\prime}_{x}}=\frac{\left(1-\beta^{2}\right)\sin\theta}{\beta\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}+\cos\theta}\text{.}

(S18)

By multiplying the numerator and the denominator by the factor
\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}-\beta\cos\theta
and simplifying the result, it follows that

\frac{k^{\prime}_{y}}{k^{\prime}_{x}}=\frac{\sin\theta\left(\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}-\beta\cos\theta\right)}{\beta\sin^{2}\theta+\cos\theta\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}}=\frac{\sin\vartheta}{\cos\vartheta}=\tan\vartheta\text{,}

(S19)

which demonstrates that the wave vector
\mathbf{k}^{\prime}
is oriented along the direction given by the angle
\vartheta
.
