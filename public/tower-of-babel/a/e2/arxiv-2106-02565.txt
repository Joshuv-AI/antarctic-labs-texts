The Poisson spectrum of the symmetric algebra of the Virasoro algebra
Alexey V. Petukhov and Susan J. Sierra

Abstract
. Let W = ℂ [ t , t − 1 ] ∂ t W=\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]\partial_{t} be the Witt algebra of algebraic vector fields on ℂ × \mathbb{C}^{\times} and let V ​ i ​ r V\!ir be the Virasoro algebra , the unique nontrivial central extension of W W . In this paper, we study the Poisson ideal structure of the symmetric algebras of V ​ i ​ r V\!ir and W W , as well as several related Lie algebras. We classify prime Poisson ideals and Poisson primitive ideals of S ⁡ ( V ​ i ​ r ) \Sa(V\!ir) and S ⁡ ( W ) \Sa(W) . In particular, we show that the only functions in W ∗ W^{*} which vanish on a nontrivial Poisson ideal (that is, the only maximal ideals of S ⁡ ( W ) \Sa(W) with a nontrivial Poisson core) are given by linear combinations of derivatives at a finite set of points; we call such functions local . Given a local function χ ∈ W ∗ \chi\in W^{*} , we construct the associated Poisson primitive ideal through computing the algebraic symplectic leaf of χ \chi , which gives a notion of coadjoint orbit in our setting. As an application, we prove a structure theorem for subalgebras of V ​ i ​ r V\!ir of finite codimension and show in particular that any such subalgebra of V ​ i ​ r V\!ir contains the central element z z , substantially generalising a result of Ondrus and Wiesner on subalgebras of codimension 1. As a consequence, we deduce that S ⁡ ( V ​ i ​ r ) / ( z − ζ ) \Sa(V\!ir)/(z-\zeta) is Poisson simple if and only if ζ ≠ 0 \zeta\neq 0 .

1.
Introduction

Let
G
be a connected algebraic group over
\mathbb{C}
with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, and consider the coadjoint action of
G
on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
.
This is a beautiful classical topic, with profound connections to areas from geometric representation theory to combinatorics to physics.
Algebraic geometry tells us that coadjoint orbits in
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
correspond to
G
-invariant radical ideals in the symmetric algebra
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
.

These can also be defined using the Kostant-Kirillov
Poisson bracket
on
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
:

\{f,g\}=\sum_{i,j}\frac{\partial f}{\partial e_{i}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial e_{j}}[e_{i},e_{j}]

where
\{e_{i}\}
is a basis of
\mathfrak{g}
.
Recall that an ideal
I
of
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
is a
Poisson ideal
if
I
is also a Lie ideal for the Poisson bracket.
A basic fact is that
I
is
G
-invariant if and only if
I
is Poisson.

Thus to compute the closure of the coadjoint orbit of

\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, let
\mathfrak{m}_{\chi}

be the kernel of the
evaluation morphism

{\rm ev}_{\chi}:{\rm S}(\mathfrak{g})\to\mathbb{{\Bbbk}},

and let
\CoreP(\chi)

be the
Poisson core

of
\mathfrak{m}_{\chi}
:
the maximal Poisson ideal contained in
\mathfrak{m}_{\chi}
.
By definition, an ideal of the form
\CoreP(\chi)
is called
Poisson primitive
;

by a slight abuse of notation, we refer to
\CoreP(\chi)
as the
Poisson core of
\chi
.
The closure of the coadjoint orbit of
\chi
is defined by
\CoreP(\chi)
:

(1.0.1)

\overline{G\cdot\chi}=V(\CoreP(\chi)):=\{\nu\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}\ |\ \ev_{\nu}(\CoreP(\chi))=0\},

and so
\chi,\nu\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
are in the same
G
-orbit if and only if
\CoreP(\chi)=\CoreP(\nu)
.
In the case of algebraic Lie algebras over
\mathbb{C}
or
\mathbb{R}
, coadjoint orbits are symplectic leaves for the respective Poisson structure.

In this paper, we investigate how this theory extends to the
Witt algebra

W=\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]\partial_{t}
of algebraic vector fields on
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, and to its central extension the
Virasoro algebra

V\!ir=\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]\partial_{t}\oplus\mathbb{C}z
, with Lie bracket given by

[f\partial_{t},g\partial_{t}]=(fg^{\prime}-f^{\prime}g)\partial_{t}+\Res_{0}(f^{\prime}g^{\prime\prime}-g^{\prime}f^{\prime\prime})z,\quad\mbox{$z$ is central.}

(We also consider some important Lie subalgebras of
W
.)
These infinite-dimensional Lie algebras, of fundamental importance in representation theory and in physics, have no adjoint group
[
L
]
, but one can still study the Poisson cores of maximal ideals, and more generally the Poisson ideal structure of
\Sa(W)
and
\Sa(V\!ir)
.
Motivated by (
1.0.1
), we will say that functions
\chi,\nu\in V\!ir^{*}
or in
W^{*}
are in the same
pseudo-orbit
if
\CoreP(\chi)=\CoreP(\nu)
.
These (coadjoint) pseudo-orbits can be considered as algebraic symplectic leaves in
V\!ir^{*}
or
W^{*}
.

Taking the discussion above as our guide, we focus on prime Poisson ideals and Poisson primitive ideals of
\Sa(V\!ir)
and
\Sa(W)
.
Important questions here, which for brevity we ask in the introduction only for
V\!ir
, include:

•

Given
\chi\in V\!ir^{*}
, can we compute the Poisson core
\CoreP(\chi)
and the pseudo-orbit of
\chi
? When is
\CoreP(\chi)
nontrivial?

•

How can we understand prime Poisson ideals of
\Sa(V\!ir)
? Can we parameterise them in a reasonable fashion, ideally in a way which gives us further information about the ideal? How does one distinguish Poisson primitive ideals from other prime Poisson ideals?

•

It is known, see
[
LS
, Corollary 5.1]
, that
\Sa(V\!ir)
satisfies the ascending chain condition on prime Poisson ideals. The augmentation ideal of
\Sa(V\!ir)
, that is, the ideal generated by
V\!ir\subset\Sa(V\!ir)
, is clearly a maximal Poisson ideal. What are the others? Conversely, does any nontrivial prime Poisson ideal have finite height?

•

Do prime Poisson ideals induce any reasonable algebraic geometry on the uncountable-dimensional vector space
V\!ir^{*}
?

We answer all of these questions, almost completely working out the structure of the Poisson spectra of
\Sa(V\!ir)
and
\Sa(W)
.

Let us begin by discussing the idea of algebraic geometry on
V\!ir^{*}
.
A priori, this seems completely intractable as
V\!ir^{*}
is an uncountable-dimensional affine space; little interesting can be said about
\Sa(\mathfrak{a})
where
\mathfrak{a}
is a countable-dimensional
abelian
Lie algebra.
However,
V\!ir
and
W
are extremely noncommutative and so Poisson ideals in their symmetric algebras are very large: in particular, by a result of Iyudu and the second author
[
IS
, Theorem 1.3]
, if
I
is a nontrivial Poisson ideal of
\Sa(W)
(respectively, a non-centrally generated Poisson ideal of
\Sa(V\!ir)
), then
\Sa(W)/I
(respectively,
\Sa(V\!ir)/I
) has polynomial growth.
This suggests that we might hope that a Poisson primitive ideal, and more generally a prime Poisson ideal, would correspond to a finite-dimensional algebraic subvariety of
V\!ir^{*}
, which we might be able to investigate using tools from affine algebraic geometry. We will see that this is indeed the case.

From the discussion above, it is important to characterise which functions
\chi\in V\!ir^{*}
have nontrivial Poisson cores.
One striking result, proved in this paper, is that such
\chi
must vanish on the central element
z
.
Further, the induced function
\overline{\chi}\in W^{*}
is given by evaluating
local
behaviour on a proper (that is, finite) subscheme of
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
.
We have:

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem
3.3.1
)
.

Let
\chi\in V\!ir^{*}
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

The Poisson core of
\chi
is nontrivial: that is,
\CoreP(\chi)\supsetneqq(z-\chi(z))
.

(2)

\chi(z)=0
and the induced function
\overline{\chi}\in W^{*}
is a linear combination of functions of the form

f\partial_{t}\mapsto\alpha_{0}f(x)+\ldots+\alpha_{n}f^{(n)}(x)

where
x\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
and
\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
.

(3)

The isotropy subalgebra
V\!ir^{\chi}
of
\chi
has finite codimension in
V\!ir
.

We call functions
\chi\in V\!ir^{*}
satisfying the equivalent conditions of Theorem
1.1

local functions
as by condition
(2)
they are defined by local data.

Motivated by condition
(3)
of Theorem
1.1
, we investigate subalgebras of
V\!ir
of finite codimension.
We prove:

Theorem 1.2

(Proposition
3.3.3
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{k}\subseteq V\!ir
be a subalgebra of finite codimension. Then there is
f\in\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]\setminus\{0\}
so that
\mathfrak{k}\supseteq\mathbb{C}z+f\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]\partial_{t}
. In particular, any finite codimension subalgebra of
V\!ir
contains
z
.

As an immediate corollary of Theorem
1.2
, we show:

Corollary 1.3

(Corollary
3.3.5
)
.

If
0\neq\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
, then
\Sa(V\!ir)/(z-\zeta)
is Poisson simple: it has no nontrivial Poisson ideals.

We then study the pseudo-orbits of local functions on
V\!ir
,
W
, and related Lie algebras; we describe our results for
V\!ir
in the introduction.
If
\chi\in V\!ir^{*}
is local, then by combining Theorem
1.1
and
[
IS
, Theorem 1.3]

\Sa(V\!ir)/\CoreP(\chi)
has polynomial growth and we thus expect the pseudo-orbit of
\chi
to be finite-dimensional.
We show that pseudo-orbits of local functions in
V\!ir^{*}
are in fact orbits of a finite-dimensional solvable algebraic (Lie) group acting on an affine variety which maps injectively to
V\!ir^{*}
, and we describe these orbits explicitly
(Section
4.2
).
This allows us to completely determine the pseudo-orbit of an arbitrary local function in
V\!ir^{*}
(Theorem
4.3.1
) and thus also determine the Poisson primitive ideals of
\Sa(V\!ir)
(Remark
4.3.9
).
We also classify maximal Poisson ideals in
\Sa(V\!ir)
(Corollary
4.3.18
): they are the augmentation ideal, the ideals
(z-\zeta)
for
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, and the defining ideals of all but one of the two-dimensional pseudo-orbits.

Through this analysis, we obtain a nice combinatorial description of pseudo-orbits in
W^{*}
: pseudo-orbits of local functions on
W
, and thus Poisson primitive ideals of
\Sa(W)
, correspond to a choice of a partition
\lambda
and a point in an open subvariety of a finite-dimensional affine space
\mathbb{A}^{k}
, where
k
can be calculated from
\lambda
.
(See Remark
4.3.3
.) In Theorem
5.2.8
and Remark
5.2.13
, we expand this correspondence to obtain a parameterisation of all prime Poisson ideals of
\Sa(W)
and
\Sa(V\!ir)
.
We also study the related Lie algebra
W_{\geq-1}=\mathbb{C}[t]\partial_{t}
, and prove (Corollary
5.2.14
) that Poisson primitive and prime Poisson ideals of
\Sa(W_{\geq-1})
are induced by restriction from
\Sa(W)
.

Our understanding of prime Poisson ideals allows us to determine exactly which prime Poisson ideals of
\Sa(V\!ir)
obey the
Poisson Dixmier-Moeglin equivalence
, which generalises the characterisation of primitive ideals in enveloping algebras of finite-dimensional Lie algebras due to Dixmier and Moeglin.
The central question is when a Poisson primitive ideal of
\Sa(V\!ir)
is
Poisson locally closed
: that is, locally closed in the Zariski topology on Poisson primitive ideals.
(If
\dim\mathfrak{g}<\infty
then a prime Poisson ideal of
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
is Poisson primitive if and only if it is Poisson locally closed
[
LL
, Theorem 2]
.)
We show (Theorem
5.3.1
) that
(z)
is the only Poisson primitive ideal of
\Sa(V\!ir)
which is not Poisson locally closed.
We further prove (Corollary
5.4.2
) that
\Sa(W)
has no nonzero prime Poisson ideals of finite height.

One part of the proof of Theorem
1.2
is to show that, given a subalgebra
\mathfrak{k}
of finite codimension in
W
, there are a finite collection of points
S
(the “support” of
\mathfrak{k}
) and
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that all vector fields in
\mathfrak{k}
vanish at all points of
S
and so that
\mathfrak{k}
contains all vector fields vanishing to order
\geq n
at every point of
S
.
Based on this result, we classify subalgebras of
V\!ir
of codimension
\leq 3
in Section
6
.

Our original motivation for studying Poisson ideals of
\Sa(V\!ir)
was to study two-sided ideals in the universal enveloping algebra
\Ua(V\!ir)
, and we turn to enveloping algebras in Section
7
.
In the finite-dimensional setting, Kirillov’s orbit method gives a correspondence between primitive ideals of
\Ua(\mathfrak{g})
and coadjoint orbits in
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
.
We conjecture that a similar correspondence exists for
V\!ir
and related Lie algebras, and in subsection
7.1
we show that pseudo-orbits of dimension 2 in
V\!ir^{*}
quantise to give a family of primitive ideals, kernels of well-known maps from
\Ua(V\!ir)
through
\Ua(W)
to the localised Weyl algebra.
We end the paper with some conjectures about (two-sided) ideals of the universal enveloping algebra
\Ua(V\!ir)
which are motivated by our work on
\Sa(V\!ir)
; these are the subject of further research.

To end the introduction, let us briefly discuss the classical (continuous) version of the theory of coadjoint orbits of the Virasoro algebra.
If one considers the real Lie algebra of continuous vector fields on the circle and its central extension the real Virasoro algebra
V\!ir_{\mathbb{R}}
, then the group of diffeomorphisms of the circle acts on
V\!ir_{\mathbb{R}}
and its continuous dual. There are of course notions of coadjoint orbits in this context, see
[
K
,
W
]
.
However, it happens that the corresponding orbits do not define an interesting (Poisson) ideal in the symmetric algebra
\Sa(V\!ir_{\mathbb{R}})
; in fact, this can be viewed as a somewhat informal result of our paper.
Note that the local functions which appear in Theorem
1.1
and which we study in this paper can be thought of as a product of a point-based distribution with a vector field — and hence are very far from being continuous.

2.
Poisson ideals and pseudo-orbits

In this section we recall the general notions of Poisson algebra, Poisson ideal, and Poisson primitive ideal. We then consider how these concepts behave for the symmetric algebras of the infinite-dimensional Lie algebras in which we are interested.

Throughout, let
{\Bbbk}
be an uncountable algebraically closed field of characteristic 0.
Let us define the Lie algebras of interest in this paper.
The
Witt algebra

W={\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]\partial

is the Lie algebra of vector fields on
{\Bbbk}^{\times}:={\Bbbk}\setminus\{0\}
; here
\partial=\partial_{t}=\frac{d}{dt}
.
It is graded by setting
\deg t^{n}\partial=n-1
.
Then
W_{\geq-1}

is the subalgebra
{\Bbbk}[t]\partial
of
W
, and
W_{\geq 1}
,

sometimes called the
positive Witt algebra
, is the subalgebra
t^{2}{\Bbbk}[t]\partial
;
W_{\geq 0}
stands for
t{\Bbbk}[t]\partial
.

The
Virasoro algebra

V\!ir

is isomorphic as a vector space to
{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]\partial\oplus{\Bbbk}z
. It is endowed with a Lie algebra structure by the formula

[f\partial+c_{1}z,g\partial+c_{2}z]=(fg^{\prime}-f^{\prime}g)\partial+\Res_{0}(f^{\prime}g^{\prime\prime}-f^{\prime\prime}g^{\prime})z.

(Here
\Res_{0}(f)
stands for the algebraic residue of
f
at 0, i.e. the coefficient of
t^{-1}
in the Laurent expansion of
f
at 0.)
It is well-known that
V\!ir
is the unique nontrivial one-dimensional central extension of
W
. There is a canonical Lie algebra homomorphism
V\!ir\to W
given by factoring out
z
.

Let
V
be a
{\Bbbk}
-vector space. We will use

\Sa(V)
to denote the symmetric algebra of
V
; that is, polynomial functions on
V^{*}
.
For
\chi\in V^{*}
we denote by
\ev_{\chi}

the induced homomorphism
\Sa(V)\to{\Bbbk}
defined by
\ev_{\chi}(f)=f(\chi)
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be one of the Lie algebras
V\!ir
,
W
,
W_{\geq-1}
, or
W_{\geq 1}
. Our assumption on the cardinality of the field
{\Bbbk}
means that the following extended Nullstellensatz applies to
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
; see
[
MR
, Corollary 9.1.8, Lemma 9.1.2]
.

Theorem 2.0.1

(Extended Nullstellensatz)
.

Let
A
be a commutative
{\Bbbk}
-algebra such that
\dim_{\Bbbk}A<|{\Bbbk}|.
Then:

\bullet

A
is a Jacobson ring: every radical ideal is an intersection of a family of maximal ideals;

\bullet
if
\mathfrak{m}\subseteq A
is a maximal ideal then the canonical map
{\Bbbk}\to A/\mathfrak{m}
is an isomorphism;

\bullet
if
A=\Sa(V)
for a vector space
V
with
\dim V<|{\Bbbk}|
, then the maximal ideals of
A
are all of the form
\mathfrak{m}_{\chi}=\Ker{\rm ev}_{\chi}
for some
\chi\in V^{*}
.

Let
A
be a commutative
{\Bbbk}
-algebra. (Note that we make no noetherianity or finiteness assumption on
A
.)
Denote by
\MSpec A

the set of maximal ideals of
A
, which we consider as a (potentially infinite-dimensional) variety.
We denote by
\mathfrak{m}_{x}
the maximal ideal corresponding to a point
x\in{\rm MSpec}A
.

As usual,
\MSpec A
is a topological space under the Zariski topology.
Given an ideal
N
of
A
, we denote the corresponding closed subset of
\MSpec A
by

V(N):=\{\mathfrak{m}\in\MSpec A\ |\ N\subseteq\mathfrak{m}\}.

Given
X\subseteq\MSpec A
, we denote the corresponding radical ideal of
A
by

I(X):=\bigcap_{x\in X}\mathfrak{m}_{x}.

As we have enlarged the class of varieties somewhat beyond the usual, we make the convention that for us an
algebraic variety
is a classical variety: a (nonempty) integral separated scheme of finite type over
{\Bbbk}
, as in
[
H
, p. 105]
.
If
A,B
are commutative
{\Bbbk}
-algebras which are domains, an algebraic map or
morphism of varieties

\phi:\MSpec A\to\MSpec B
has the usual meaning: a function so that the pullback
\phi^{*}
defines an algebra homomorphism
B\to A
.

Let
\{\cdot\ ,\cdot\}:A\times A\to A
be a skew-symmetric
{\Bbbk}
-bilinear map.
We say that
(A,\{\cdot\ ,\cdot\})
is a
Poisson algebra

if
\{\cdot\ ,\cdot\}
satisfies the Leibniz rule on each input and the Jacobi identity.
An ideal
I
of
A
is
Poisson
if
\{I,A\}\subseteq I
.

It is clear that the sum of all Poisson subideals of any ideal
I
of
A
is the maximal Poisson ideal inside
I
; we denote this ideal by
{\rm Core}(I)
and refer to it as the
Poisson core

of
I
. Note that if
I
is radical, respectively prime, then
{\rm Core}(I)
inherits this property; see
[
PS
, Lemmata 2.6 and 2.8]
.

A Poisson ideal
I
is called
Poisson primitive

if
I={\rm Core}(\mathfrak{m})
for a maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
of
A
; Poisson primitive ideals are prime.
We denote the set of Poisson primitive ideals of
A
by
\PSpec_{\rm prim}A
,

and the set of prime Poisson ideals by
\PSpec A
. Both are given the Zariski topology, where the closed subsets are defined by Poisson ideals of
A
.

Consider a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
with
\dim\mathfrak{g}<|{\Bbbk}|
.
It is well known that
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
possesses a canonical Poisson algebra structure, induced by defining
\{u,v\}=[u,v]
for any
u,v\in\mathfrak{g}
.
As
\dim\mathfrak{g}<|{\Bbbk}|

then by the Nullstellensatz
\MSpec(\Sa(\mathfrak{g}))
can be canonically identified with
\mathfrak{g}^{*}:

\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}\leftrightarrow\mathfrak{m}_{\chi}:=\Ker{\rm ev}_{\chi}\in\MSpec(\Sa(\mathfrak{g})).

Thus any Poisson primitive ideal of
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
is equal to
\Core(\mathfrak{m}_{\chi})
for some
\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
.
Set
\CoreP(\chi):=\Core(\mathfrak{m}_{\chi})
.

For any Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, the ideal
\mathfrak{m}_{0}=\Core(\mathfrak{m}_{0})=\CoreP(0)
(the augmentation ideal) is the Poisson core of
0\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
and so is Poisson primitive.

Even in the absence of an adjoint group to
\mathfrak{g}
, the Poisson primitives
\CoreP(\chi)
give analogues of coadjoint orbits.

Definition 2.0.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be any Lie algebra.
The
pseudo-orbit
of
\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is

\mathbb{O}(\chi):=\{\nu\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}|\CoreP(\nu)=\CoreP(\chi)\}.

The
dimension
of
\mathbb{O}(\chi)
is defined to be
\GK\Sa(\mathfrak{g})/\CoreP(\chi)
. (Here if
R
is a
{\Bbbk}
-algebra then
\GK R
denotes the
Gelfand-Kirillov dimension
of
R
; see
[
KL
]
.)

Remark 2.0.3
.

If
\mathfrak{g}={\rm Lie}\ G
is the Lie algebra of a connected algebraic group and
\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, then
\mathbb{O}(\chi)
is the coadjoint orbit of
\chi
.
In our setting,
\mathfrak{g}
is not the Lie algebra of any algebraic group.
However, we will see that we can still define algebraic group actions on pieces of
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
that allow us to recover pseudo-orbits as actual orbits.

Part (b) of the next result is an analogue of (
1.0.1
) for pseudo-orbits.

Lemma 2.0.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a Lie algebra with
\dim\mathfrak{g}<|{\Bbbk}|
.

(a)

Any radical Poisson ideal in
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
is equal to an intersection of Poisson primitive ideals.
Explicitly, given a radical Poisson ideal
I
we have

I=\bigcap\{\CoreP(\chi)\ |\ \chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*},~\ev_{\chi}(I)=0\}.

(b)

Assume now that
\dim\mathfrak{g}
is countable,
and let
\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
be nonzero.
Then

\CoreP(\chi)=\bigcap_{\nu\in\mathbb{O}(\chi)}\mathfrak{m}_{\nu}.

Proof.

(a) is well-known, but we give a proof for completeness.
As
I
is radical and Poisson,

I\subseteq\bigcap\{\CoreP(\chi)\ |\ \chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*},~\ev_{\chi}(I)=0\},

and this is contained by definition in
\bigcap_{\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*},\ev_{\chi}(I)=0}\mathfrak{m}_{\chi}
.
By the Nullstellensatz, this last is equal to
I
.

For (b), set
R:=\Sa(\mathfrak{g})/\CoreP(\chi)
.
Thanks to the proof of
[
LS
, Theorem 6.3]
, which uses only that
\mathfrak{g}
is countably generated (see also
[
BLLM
, Theorem 3.2]
), there is a sequence of non-zero Poisson ideals
L_{1},L_{2},\ldots
of
R
such that if
\mu\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
with
\CoreP(\mu)
strictly containing
\CoreP(\chi)
, then
\CoreP(\mu)/\CoreP(\chi)
contains
L_{i}
for at least one
i
.

This is equivalent to the following statement: there is a sequence
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots\in R\setminus\{0\}

such that if
\CoreP(\mu)
strictly contains
\CoreP(\chi)
then
f_{i}(\mu)=0
for at least one
i
.
Therefore
R^{\prime}=(\Sa(\mathfrak{g})/\CoreP(\chi))[f_{1}^{-1},f_{2}^{-1},\ldots]
is Poisson simple.
On the other hand
R^{\prime}
is clearly at most countable-dimensional and hence
(0)\subset R^{\prime}
is an intersection of a family of maximal ideals of
R^{\prime}
by the Nullstellensatz. The definition of
R^{\prime}
and
f_{i}^{\prime}
guarantees that
\CoreP(\mu)=\CoreP(\chi)
for each
\mu\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
defining such an ideal.
We thus obtain a family of maximal ideals of
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})

contained in the pseudo-orbit of
\chi
whose intersection is
\CoreP(\chi)
.
This implies the desired result.
∎

2.1.
Some results on pseudo-orbits

We now give several results which are well known for finite-dimensional algebraic Lie algebras, but require proof in our setting. Throughout this subsection, let
\mathfrak{g}
be a Lie algebra with
\dim\mathfrak{g}<|{\Bbbk}|
.

For every
\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
define the skew-symmetric bilinear form
B_{\chi}(x,y):=\chi([x,y])
.

The kernel of
B_{\chi}
is a Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
, which we denote by
\mathfrak{g}^{\chi}
.

Observe that
\mathfrak{g}^{\chi}
is precisely
\{v\in\mathfrak{g}\ |\ v\cdot\chi=0\}
; that is
\mathfrak{g}^{\chi}
is the isotropy subalgebra of
\chi
under the (coadjoint) action of
\mathfrak{g}
on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
.
As
B_{\chi}
induces a nondegenerate skew-symmetric form on
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{g}^{\chi}
, we have
\rk B_{\chi}=\dim\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{g}^{\chi}
.

Lemma 2.1.1
.

The dimension of
\mathbb{O}(\chi)
is at least
\rk B_{\chi}
, i.e.

{\rm GKdim}({\rm S}(\mathfrak{g})/\CoreP(\chi))\geq\rk B_{\chi}.

Before proving Lemma
2.1.1
, we establish some notation, which we will need for several results. For
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n},

v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\in\mathfrak{g}
set
D(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n};v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})\in\Sa(\mathfrak{g})

to be the determinant

(2.1.2)

\begin{vmatrix}[u_{1},v_{1}]&\cdots&[u_{1},v_{n}]\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
{[u_{n},v_{1}]}&\cdots&{[u_{n},v_{n}]}\\
\end{vmatrix}.

Proof of Lemma
2.1.1
.

Pick
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that
r\leq\rk B_{\chi}
.
Then there is an
r
-dimensional subspace
V
of
\mathfrak{g}
so that

\rk(B_{\chi}|_{V})=r
;
that is, if
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}
is a basis of
V
then
\ev_{\chi}(D(u_{1},\dots,u_{r},u_{1},\dots,u_{r}))\neq 0
.
We will show that
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}
are algebraically independent in
{\rm S}(\mathfrak{g})/\CoreP(\chi)
; that is, that
\GK(\Sa(\mathfrak{g})/\CoreP(\chi))\geq r
.

Assume to the contrary that
the
u_{i}
are not algebraically independent modulo
\CoreP(\chi)
.
This means that there is some nonzero
P\in{\Bbbk}[u_{1},\dots,u_{r}]\cap\CoreP(\chi)
, and we may assume
P
is of minimal degree among such elements.
As
\CoreP(\chi)
is Poisson,

(2.1.3)

\{u_{i},P\}=\sum_{j}\{u_{i},u_{j}\}\partial_{j}P\in\CoreP(\chi),

where
\partial_{j}P=\frac{\partial P}{\partial u_{j}}
.

Let
Q
be the field of fractions of
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})/\CoreP(\chi)
(recall that
\CoreP(\chi)
is prime).
We may rewrite (
2.1.3
) as the matrix equation

\Bigl(\{u_{i},u_{j}\}\Bigr)_{ij}\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{1}P\\
\vdots\\
\partial_{r}P\end{pmatrix}=\vec{0}

over
Q
. By minimality of
\deg P
, the vector
\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{1}P\\
\vdots\\
\partial_{r}P\end{pmatrix}\in Q^{r}
is nonzero.
Thus

\Bigl(\{u_{i},u_{j}\}\Bigr)_{ij}\in M_{r\times r}(Q)
is singular, so

D(u_{1},\dots,u_{r};u_{1},\dots,u_{r})\in\CoreP(\chi)\subseteq\mathfrak{m}_{\chi}
. This contradicts the first paragraph of the proof.
∎

It is not necessarily easy to calculate
\CoreP(\chi)
, but we can sometimes use GK-dimension to show that a Poisson ideal contained in
\mathfrak{m}_{\chi}
is in fact equal to
\CoreP(\chi)
. This is given by the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a Lie algebra and
A
a commutative
{\Bbbk}
-algebra that is a domain. Let
{\phi}:\Sa(\mathfrak{g})\to A
be an algebra homomorphism so that
\ker{\phi}
is Poisson.
Let
\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
be such that

(2.1.5)

\Ker\phi\subseteq\mathfrak{m}_{\chi}\mathrm{~and~}\rk B_{\chi}\geq\GK A.

Then
\Ker\phi=\CoreP(\chi)
and thus is Poisson primitive; further,
\rk B_{\chi}=\GK A=\dim\mathbb{O}(\chi)
.

Proof.

Certainly
\ker{\phi}\subseteq\CoreP(\chi)
.
If the containment is strict, then as
A
is a domain,

\GK(\Sa(\mathfrak{g})/\CoreP(\chi))<\GK A\leq\rk B_{\chi},

contradicting
Lemma
2.1.1
.
The final equality is direct from Lemma
2.1.1
.
∎

Corollary 2.1.6
.

If both
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}
satisfy (
2.1.5
) for the same
{\phi}
then
\CoreP(\chi_{1})=\CoreP(\chi_{2})
. ∎

We will use Lemma
2.1.4
and Corollary
2.1.6
to describe primitive ideals in
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
and the corresponding pseudo-orbits in
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
explicitly.
In the situation of Lemma
2.1.4
,
A
may not necessarily be a Poisson algebra.
We abuse notation slightly, however, and say that if
\ker{\phi}
is a Poisson ideal,
then
{\phi}
is a
Poisson morphism
.

Further,
given a morphism of varieties

\psi:\MSpec(A)\to\MSpec(\Sa(\mathfrak{g}))=\mathfrak{g}^{*},

we say that
\psi
is
Poisson
if
\psi^{*}:\Sa(\mathfrak{g})\to A
is Poisson.

It is important to know under which conditions maps are Poisson and the answer is given in Proposition
2.1.7
.
To explain the setup we need to use some concepts from (affine) algebraic geometry.
As mentioned, we will, somewhat loosely, refer to infinite-dimensional vector spaces such as
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
as varieties, enlarging the class from standard usage.
For any variety
X
and any point
x\in X
let
{\rm T}_{x}X=(\mathfrak{m}_{x}/\mathfrak{m}_{x}^{2})^{*}

denote the tangent space to
X
at
x
; this definition makes sense for
X=\mathfrak{g}^{*}
as well. We will, without further comment, identify
{\rm T}_{x}X
with elements of
\mathfrak{m}_{x}^{*}
which vanish on
\mathfrak{m}_{x}^{2}
.
If there is a map
\psi:X\to Y
between algebraic varieties
X
and
Y
then, for all
x\in X
, there is an induced map
\psi_{x}:{\rm T}_{x}X\to{\rm T}_{\psi(x)}Y
.

We canonically identify the tangent space to
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
at
\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
with
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
.
For any derivation
D
of
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
and any
\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
we denote by
D_{\chi}

the tangent vector defined by
D
at
\chi
;
that is
D_{\chi}(f)=\chi(Df)
for any
f\in\mathfrak{m}_{\chi}
.
Now,
\mathfrak{g}
acts by derivations on
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
. For
u\in\mathfrak{g}
and
\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
let
u_{\chi}

be the tangent vector defined by
u
at
\chi
.

If
v\in\mathfrak{g}
then
u_{\chi}(v)=\chi([u,v])
, and thus
u_{\chi}\in{\rm T}_{\chi}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})=\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is identified with
u\cdot\chi
.
If
\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
let
\mathfrak{u}_{\chi}=\{u_{\chi}\ |\ u\in\mathfrak{u}\}
.
Thus
\mathfrak{g}_{\chi}\cong\mathfrak{g}\cdot\chi\cong\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{g}^{\chi}
and
\rk B_{\chi}=\dim\mathfrak{g}_{\chi}
.

Proposition 2.1.7
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a Lie algebra with
\dim\mathfrak{g}<|{\Bbbk}|
.
Let
X
be an affine variety and let
\phi:X\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
be a morphism of varieties.
Then
\phi
is Poisson if and only if
\mathfrak{g}_{\phi(x)}\subseteq\phi_{x}({\rm T}_{x}X)
for all
x\in X
.

Proposition
2.1.7
is a direct consequence of the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1.8
.

Let
\mathfrak{g},X,\phi
be as in the statement of Proposition
2.1.7
and let
D
be a derivation of
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
.
Then
\ker\phi^{*}
is
D
-stable if and only if
D_{\phi(x)}\in\phi_{x}({\rm T}_{x}X)
for all
x\in X
.

Proof.

Suppose that
D_{\phi(x)}\in\phi_{x}({\rm T}_{x}X)
for all
x\in X
.
Let
f\in\ker\phi^{*}
.
We show that
\phi^{*}(Df)=0
, or, equivalently, that
Df\in\mathfrak{m}_{\phi(x)}
for all
x\in X
.
Fix
x
, and let
\ell\in{\rm T}_{x}X
be such that
D_{\phi(x)}=\phi_{x}(\ell)
.
Then

(Df)(\phi(x))=\ell(\phi^{*}f)=\ell(0)=0,

as needed.

Conversely, let
x\in X
and let
\mathfrak{n}_{x}:=\mathfrak{m}_{x}\cap\phi^{*}(\Sa(\mathfrak{g}))
.
If
\ker\phi^{*}
is
D
-stable, then
D
induces a derivation
\overline{D}
on
\phi^{*}(\Sa(\mathfrak{g}))
, defined by

\overline{D}(\phi^{*}f)=\phi^{*}(Df),

and thus defines an element
\overline{D}_{x}\in(\mathfrak{n}_{x}/\mathfrak{n}_{x}^{2})^{*}
.
Let
\ell\in{\rm T}_{x}X
be any extension of
\overline{D}_{x}
to
\mathfrak{m}_{x}/\mathfrak{m}_{x}^{2}
.
Then for
f\in\mathfrak{m}_{\phi(x)}
we have

D_{\phi(x)}(f)=\ev_{\phi(x)}(Df)={\phi}^{*}(Df)(x)=\overline{D}_{x}\phi^{*}f=\ell\phi^{*}f,

showing that
D_{\phi(x)}\in\phi_{x}({\rm T}_{x}X)
.
∎

2.2.
Some pseudo-orbits in
W^{*}
and
W_{\geq-1}^{*}

This subsection is effectively an extended example, where we use the methods of the previous subsection, particularly Lemma
2.1.4
, to compute the Poisson cores of some particular functions in
W^{*}
and
W_{\geq-1}^{*}
.
We will see later (Proposition
4.3.14
) that these give all of the prime Poisson ideals of
\Sa(W)
and
\Sa(W_{\geq-1})
of co-GK-dimension 2.

Throughout the subsection fix
x,\alpha,\gamma\in{\Bbbk}
with
x\neq 0
and
\alpha,\gamma
not both 0.
Let
\chi:=\chi_{x;\alpha,\gamma}\in W^{*}
be defined by

f\partial\mapsto\alpha f(x)+\gamma f^{\prime}(x).

Further, given
g\in{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]
let
W(g):={\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]g\partial\subseteq W
.

Both notations will be generalised and used more extensively in Section
3
.

We first compute the isotropy subalgebra of
\chi
.

Lemma 2.2.1
.

We have:

W^{\chi}=\begin{cases}W((t-x)^{2})&\gamma=0\\
\{g\partial\ |\ g(x)=\alpha g^{\prime}(x)+\gamma g^{\prime\prime}(x)=0\}&\gamma\neq 0.\end{cases}

Proof.

Recall that
\chi
defines a bilinear form
B_{\chi}
on
W
by
B_{\chi}(v,w)=\chi([v,w])
,
and that
W^{\chi}=\ker B_{\chi}
. For all
\chi
—that is, for all choices of
x,\alpha,\gamma
—therefore,
W^{\chi}\neq W
.

First assume that
\gamma=0
.
If
g\partial\in W((t-x)^{2})

then

\chi([g\partial,f\partial])=\alpha(g(x)f^{\prime}(x)-f(x)g^{\prime}(x))=0,

so
W((t-x)^{2})\subseteq W^{\chi}
. But
B_{\chi}
defines a nondegenerate bilinear form on
W/W^{\chi}
, so
\dim W/W^{\chi}\geq 2
and thus
W((t-x)^{2})=W^{\chi}
.

Now suppose
\gamma\neq 0
.
Let

V:=\{g\in{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]\ |\ g(x)=\chi(g^{\prime}\partial)=0\},

which is a codimension 2 subspace of
{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]
,
and let
g\in V
,
f\in{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]
.
Then

\chi([f\partial,g\partial])=\alpha(f(x)g^{\prime}(x)-f^{\prime}(x)g(x))+\gamma(f(x)g^{\prime\prime}(x)-f^{\prime\prime}(x)g(x))=f(x)\chi(g^{\prime}\partial)-g(x)\chi(f^{\prime}\partial),

which is 0 by assumption on
g
.
Thus
W^{\chi}\supseteq V\partial
, and as before the two must be equal.
∎

Note that in all cases
W^{\chi}\supseteq W((t-x)^{3})
,
and that if
\lambda\neq 0
then
W^{\chi}=W^{\chi_{x;\lambda\alpha,\lambda\gamma}}
.

We now compute
\CoreP(\chi)
.
Let
B={\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1},y]
, and define a Poisson bracket on
B
induced from
defining
\{y,t\}=1
.

Define

p_{\gamma}:\Sa(W)\to B

as the algebra homomorphism induced by defining

(2.2.2)

p_{\gamma}(f\partial)=fy+\gamma f^{\prime}.

We verify:

\{p_{\gamma}(f\partial),p_{\gamma}(g\partial)\}=\{fy+\gamma f^{\prime},gy+\gamma g^{\prime}\}=y(fg^{\prime}-f^{\prime}g)+\gamma(fg^{\prime\prime}-f^{\prime\prime}g)=p_{\gamma}((fg^{\prime}-f^{\prime}g)\partial).

Thus
p_{\gamma}

respects Poisson brackets, so

\ker p_{\gamma}

is a Poisson ideal of
\Sa(W)
.

Lemma 2.2.3
.

The Poisson core of
\chi
is equal to
\ker p_{\gamma}
, and in particular depends only on
\gamma
as long as
(\alpha,\gamma)\neq(0,0)
.

Proof.

First,
\chi(f\partial)=p_{\gamma}(f\partial)|_{t=x,y=\alpha}
, and it follows immediately that if we extend
\chi
to a homomorphism
\ev_{\chi}:\Sa(W)\to{\Bbbk}
, it factors through
p_{\gamma}
.
As a result,
\ev_{\chi}(\ker p_{\gamma})=0.

Since in all cases
W^{\chi}
has codimension
2=\GK(B)
by Lemma
2.2.1
, the result is a direct consequence of Lemma
2.1.4
.
∎

Remark 2.2.4
.

Let
x,\alpha,\gamma\in{\Bbbk}
with
\alpha,\gamma
not both 0, and define
\nu\in W_{\geq-1}^{*}
analogously to
\chi
: that is,
\nu(f\partial)=\alpha f(x)+\gamma f^{\prime}(x)
.
One may similarly prove that
\CoreP(\nu)=\Sa(W_{\geq-1})\cap\ker p_{\gamma}
, and in particular that

\CoreP(\chi|_{W_{\geq-1}})=\CoreP(\chi)\cap\Sa(W_{\geq-1}).

We will see in Proposition
4.3.6
that this is true for all elements of
W^{*}
.
Likewise,
\CoreP(\chi|_{W_{\geq 1}})=\CoreP(\chi)\cap\Sa(W_{\geq 1})
.

2.3.
Pseudo-orbits versus orbits

We wish to relate the pseudo-orbits from subsection
2.1
to orbits of an algebraic group acting on an appropriate algebraic variety
X
.
The next result gives us a general technique to do this.

Proposition 2.3.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a Lie algebra with
\dim\mathfrak{g}<|{\Bbbk}|
.
Let
X
be an irreducible affine algebraic variety acted on by a connected algebraic group
H
with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}
and let
U\subseteq X
be an open affine subset.
Fix a morphism of varieties
\phi:U\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
.
Assume that
for every
x\in U
we have
\mathfrak{g}_{\phi(x)}\subseteq\phi_{x}(\mathfrak{h}_{x})
.

(a)

For all
x\in U
the pseudo-orbit of
\phi(x)
is contained in
\overline{\phi(Hx\cap U)}
, where recall that the topology on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is the Zariski topology.

(b)

For
x\in U
, let
I^{U}(Hx)
be the defining ideal in
{\Bbbk}[U]
of
\overline{Hx}\cap U
.
Let
x\in U
be such that
\dim\mathfrak{g}_{\phi(x)}=\dim\mathfrak{h}_{x}
.
Then
\CoreP(\phi(x))
is equal to the kernel of the induced homomorphism

(2.3.2)

\Sa(\mathfrak{g})\to{\Bbbk}[U]/I^{U}(Hx)={\Bbbk}[\overline{Hx}\cap U],

and

(2.3.3)

\dim\mathbb{O}(\phi(x))=\GK\Sa(\mathfrak{g})/\CoreP(\phi(x))=\dim\mathfrak{g}_{\phi(x)}.

In particular, if
y\in U
is such that
Hx=Hy
and

\dim\mathfrak{g}_{\phi(y)}=\dim\mathfrak{h}_{y}
then
\CoreP(\phi(x))=\CoreP(\phi(y))
.

(c)

Let
x,y\in U
be such that
\dim\mathfrak{g}_{\phi(x)}=\dim\mathfrak{h}_{x}
and
\dim\mathfrak{g}_{\phi(y)}=\dim\mathfrak{h}_{y}
. Then
\CoreP(\phi(x))=\CoreP(\phi(y))
if and only if there are open subsets
U_{x}\subseteq Hx\cap U
and
U_{y}\subseteq Hy\cap U
such that
\phi(U_{x})=\phi(U_{y})
.

Proof.

(a).
The kernel of (
2.3.2
) is Poisson by Proposition
2.1.7
and is contained in
\mathfrak{m}_{\phi(x)}
by definition.
Thus it is contained in
\CoreP(\phi(x))
, which is what we need.
Note also that this statement is completely trivial if
\mathfrak{g}
is the Lie algebra of a finite-dimensional algebraic group.

(b).
Let
K
be the kernel of (
2.3.2
).
Then
K
is Poisson by (a).
We have

\GK\Sa(\mathfrak{g})/K\leq\dim Hx=\dim\mathfrak{h}_{x}=\rk B_{\phi(x)},

so
K=\CoreP(\phi(x))
by Lemma
2.1.4
.
Certainly

\GK\Sa(\mathfrak{g})/\CoreP(\phi(x))\leq\GK{\Bbbk}[U]/I^{U}(Hx)=\dim\mathfrak{h}_{x}=\dim\mathfrak{g}_{\phi(x)},

and the two are equal by Lemma
2.1.4
.
The final statement follows from Corollary
2.1.6
.

(c).
For any dense subset
U_{x}\subseteq Hx\cap U
, by (b)
\CoreP(\phi(x))
is equal to the kernel of the induced map
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})\to{\Bbbk}[U_{x}]
.
This is determined by
\phi(U_{x})
, so if
\phi(U_{x})=\phi(U_{y})
then
\CoreP(\phi(x))=\CoreP(\phi(y))
.

Suppose now that
\CoreP(\phi(x))=\CoreP(\phi(y))
.
Consider the induced maps
Hx\to\mathfrak{g}^{*},Hy\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
and the respective fibre product
(Hx)\times_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}(Hy)
.
Note that
(Hx)\times_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}(Hy)
is a closed subset of
(Hx)\times(Hy)
and hence the ideal defining
(Hx)\times_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}(Hy)
is generated by a finite collection of elements.
This implies that there is a finite-dimensional subspace
V\subset\mathfrak{g}
such that

(Hx)\times_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}(Hy)=(Hx)\times_{V^{*}}(Hy).

As
\CoreP(\phi(x))=\CoreP(\phi(y))
, the images of
Hx
and
Hy
in
V^{*}
have the same closure in
V^{*}
; call it
Z
.
The image of
Hx
in
V^{*}
may not be open in
Z
, but it is constructible and thus contains an open subset
U(x)
of
Z
.
Likewise the image of
Hy
in
V^{*}
contains an open subset
U(y)
of
Z
.
We pick
U_{x}
to be the preimage of
U(x)\cap U(y)
in
Hx
and let
U_{y}
be the preimage of
U(x)\cap U(y)
in
Hy
; then they satisfy the desired property.
∎

3.
Primitive ideals and local functions

We now specialise to let
\mathfrak{g}
be one of
W
,
W_{\geq 1}
,
W_{\geq-1}
, or
V\!ir
.
The main goal of this section is to determine which functions
\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
have nontrivial Poisson core
\CoreP(\chi)
.
We will see that these
\chi
are precisely those
\chi
which measure the local behavior of
f\partial\in\mathfrak{g}
at a finite collection of points; we call these functions
local
. (For example, the functions
\chi\in W^{*}
of Subsection
2.2
are local.)
We provide several equivalent characterisations of local functions, and apply these to classify subalgebras of
\mathfrak{g}
of finite codimension.
As a consequence, we show that
\Sa(V\!ir)/(z-\zeta)
is Poisson simple for
\zeta\neq 0
.

More formally, consider
\mathfrak{g}=W_{\geq-1}
.
Let
x,\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{n}\in{\Bbbk}
with
(\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{n})\neq\vec{\hskip 2.0pt0}
.
Define a linear function
\chi_{x;\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{n}}\in W_{\geq-1}^{*}
by

(3.0.1)

\chi_{x;\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{n}}:f\partial\mapsto\alpha_{0}f(x)+\alpha_{1}f^{\prime}(x)+\ldots+\alpha_{n}f^{(n)}(x).

The same formula defines elements of
W_{\geq 1}^{*}
and
W^{*}
, although in the last case we need to require that
x\neq 0
.

We now formally define local functions.

Definition 3.0.2
.

(a)

A
local function on
W_{\geq-1}
or
W_{\geq 1}

is a sum of finitely many functions of the form (
3.0.1
) with (possibly) distinct
x
;

(b)

A
local function on
W
is a sum of finitely many functions of the form (
3.0.1
) with (possibly) distinct
x\neq 0
;

(c)

A
local function on
V\!ir
is the pullback of a local function on
W
via the canonical map
V\!ir\to W
.

A local function of the form (
3.0.1
) is called a
one-point local function
.
Let
\chi=\chi_{x;\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{n}}
be a one-point local function.
We say that
\{x\}
is the
support

of
\chi
and that
x
is the
base point
of
\chi
.
If
\alpha_{n}\neq 0
we say that
n
is the
order
of
\chi
.

Let
\chi
be an arbitrary local function. The
support

of
\chi
is the union of the supports of the component one-point local functions.
Further, the orders of the component one-point local functions give rise to a partition
\lambda(\chi)
.
More explicitly,
write
\chi=\chi_{1}+\dots+\chi_{r}
, where the
\chi_{i}
are one-point local functions based at distinct points.
Let
m_{i}
be the order of
\chi_{i}
.
By reordering the
\chi_{i}
if necessary, we may assume that
m_{1}\geq m_{2}\geq\dots\geq m_{r}
.
The partition

\lambda(\chi):=(m_{1}+1,\dots,m_{r}+1)

is called the
order partition
of
\chi
.
(We add 1 here so that the partition
(0)
corresponds to the zero function.)
We call
m_{1}
the
order
of
\chi
.

It follows from the Chinese remainder theorem that a local function is 0 if and only if it is 0 pointwise and thus any local function
\chi
on
W
,
W_{\geq-1}
, or
V\!ir
has a unique presentation as a sum of
nonzero one-point local functions with distinct base points.
This also shows that the partition
\lambda(\chi)
and the order of
\chi
are well-defined.
For
W_{\geq 1}
it is easy to see that
\chi_{0;1}=\chi_{0;0,1}=0
and the presentation is unique under the assumption that the coefficients of
f(0)
and
f^{\prime}(0)
are 0.

Remark 3.0.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be
V\!ir
or
W
or
W_{\geq-1}
or
W_{\geq 1}
.
Then
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
and the subspace of local functions are both uncountable-dimensional vector spaces;
for local functions observe that any set of one-point local functions with distinct base points is linearly independent.
On the other hand, clearly “most” elements of
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
are not local.
In fact, we will see in Remark
5.2.3
that local functions are parameterised by a countable union of algebraic varieties.

For a specific example of a non-local function, let
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\dots\in{\Bbbk}
be algebraically independent over

, and define
\varkappa\in W_{\geq-1}^{*}
by

\varkappa(t^{i}\partial)=\alpha_{i}
.

As local functions are defined similarly for

,
W_{\geq 1}
,
W_{\geq-1}
, and
V\!ir
we sometimes discuss all of them simultaneously.
When we do so, we will assume without comment whenever we talk about a one-point local function on
W
or

x\neq 0
.

Pick
\chi\in W^{*}
or
W_{\geq 1}^{*}
or
W_{\geq-1}^{*}
.
In this section we will show that
\CoreP(\chi)
is nonzero iff
\chi
is local and prove a similar statement for
V\!ir^{*}
.
The starting point here is the following result, due in its strongest form to Iyudu and the second author.

Theorem 3.0.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be
W
or
W_{\geq 1}
or
W_{\geq-1}
and let

be a nonzero Poisson ideal of
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
.
Then

\GK(\Sa(\mathfrak{g}))/I<\infty.

In particular, if
\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is such that
(0)\neq\CoreP(\chi)
, then

\GK(\Sa(\mathfrak{g})/\CoreP(\chi))<\infty
.

Further, if
\chi\in V\!ir^{*}
is such that
\CoreP(\chi)\neq(z-\chi(z))
, then

\GK(\Sa(V\!ir)/\CoreP(\chi))<\infty.

Proof.

See
[
PS
, Theorem 1.4]
for
W_{\geq 1}
and
[
IS
, Theorem 1.3]
for all other
\mathfrak{g}
.∎

Theorem

has the following extremely useful consequence:

Corollary 3.0.5
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be as above and let
\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}

If
\mathfrak{g}=W

or

Combine Theorem

We also recall:

.

3.1.
Local functions on
W_{\geq-1}
and
W_{\geq 1}

In this subsection we set
\mathfrak{g}=W_{\geq-1}={\Bbbk}[t]\partial
.
It will be useful to consider Lie subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
of a particular form.
For any
f\in{\Bbbk}[x]\setminus\{0\}
denote by
W_{\geq-1}(f)

the space of vector fields of the form

\{gf\partial\}_{g\in{\Bbbk}[x]}.

In other words,
W_{\geq-1}(f)=fW_{\geq-1}
under the obvious notation.
It is clear that
W_{\geq-1}(f)
is a Lie subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}=W_{\geq-1}(1)
.

We give five equivalent conditions for local functions. Similar conditions will hold for the other Lie algebras we consider, see Theorems
3.2.1
and
3.3.1
.

Theorem 3.1.1
.

Let
f\in{\Bbbk}[t]\setminus\{0\}
and
\chi\in W_{\geq-1}(f)^{*}
.
Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(0)

\chi
is the restriction of a local function on
W_{\geq-1}
;

(1)

\CoreP(\chi)\neq(0)
;

(2)

\dim W_{\geq-1}(f)/W_{\geq-1}(f)^{\chi}=\dim W_{\geq-1}(f)\cdot\chi<\infty
;

(3)

there exists
h\in{\Bbbk}[t]
such that
\chi|_{W_{\geq-1}(fh)}=0
;

(4)

W_{\geq-1}(f)^{\chi}\neq(0)
.

Remark 3.1.2
.

Fix a basis
\{ft^{i}\partial\ |\ i\geq-1\}
of
W_{\geq-1}(f)
and consider a local function
\chi\in W_{\geq-1}(f)^{*}
.
Then
\chi
can be identified with a sequence

(3.1.3)

\chi_{0}=\chi(f\partial),\chi_{1}=\chi(ft\partial),\chi_{2}=\chi(ft^{2}\partial),\chi_{3}=\chi(ft^{3}\partial),\cdots\in{\Bbbk}.

Condition
(3)
above
can be restated as follows:

a_{n}\chi_{m+n}+...+a_{0}\chi_{m}=0

for all
m\geq 0
, where
h=a_{n}t^{n}+\cdots+a_{0}
.
Therefore, local functions on
W_{\geq-1}(f)
can be identified with sequences (
3.1.3
) obeying a linear recurrence relation.
This shows, in particular, that the function
\varkappa
defined in Remark
3.0.3
is not local.

Part of the proof of Theorem
3.1.1
is a general technique that can allow us to show that Poisson cores of elements of
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
are nontrivial for any Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
.

Lemma 3.1.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be an arbitrary Lie algebra and let
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
There is a Poisson ideal
I(n)
with the property that

(3.1.5)

I(n)\subseteq\mathfrak{m}_{\chi}\iff\dim\mathfrak{g}\cdot\chi\leq n

for any
\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
.

Proof.

Recall the determinant
D(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n};v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
from (
2.1.2
), and note that
\dim\mathfrak{g}\cdot\chi\leq~n-1

if and only if for all
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n},v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\in\mathfrak{g}
, (
2.1.2
) evaluated at
\chi

is degenerate, i.e.

{\rm ev}_{\chi}(D(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n};v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}))=0.

Let
I(n)
be the ideal generated by the
D(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n+1};v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n+1})

for all possible tuples

u_{1},\ldots,u_{n+1},v_{1},\ldots,v_{n+1}\in\mathfrak{g}.

By the previous paragraph,

I(n)\subseteq\mathfrak{m}_{\chi}\iff\dim\mathfrak{g}\cdot\chi\leq~n.

Let
w\in\mathfrak{g}
.
It is easy to check that

\{D(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots;v_{1},v_{2},\ldots),w\}=\\
D([u_{1},w],u_{2},u_{3},\ldots;v_{1},v_{2},\ldots)+D(u_{1},[u_{2},w],u_{3},\ldots;v_{1},v_{2},\ldots)+\ldots\\
+D(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots;[v_{1},w],v_{2},v_{3},\ldots)+D(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots;v_{1},[v_{2},w],v_{3},\ldots)+\ldots\in I(n).

It follows that
I(n)
is Poisson.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1.1
.

That
(0)

\iff

(3)
is a straightforward application of the Chinese remainder theorem.
It is clear that
(2)

\Rightarrow

(4)
;
Corollary
3.0.5
gives that
(1)

\Rightarrow

(2)
.
We will show that
(4)

\Rightarrow

(3)

\Rightarrow

(2)

\Rightarrow

(1)
.
This will complete the proof.

We first show that
(4)
implies
(3)
.
Let
hf\partial\in W_{\geq-1}(f)^{\chi}\setminus\{0\}
with
h\in{\Bbbk}[t]
.
Then

(3.1.6)

0=\chi([hf\partial,hfr\partial])=\chi(h^{2}f^{2}r^{\prime}\partial)

for all
r\in{\Bbbk}[t]
.
This is equivalent to
\chi|_{W_{\geq-1}(h^{2}f^{2})}=0
as needed.

Next, we will show that
(3)
implies
(2)
.
Let
h
satisfy condition
(3)
.
As

[W_{\geq-1}(f^{2}h^{2}),W_{\geq-1}]\subseteq W_{\geq-1}(fh),

we have

W_{\geq-1}(h^{2}f^{2})\subseteq W_{\geq-1}(f)^{\chi}
and thus

\dim W(f)/W(f)^{\chi}<\infty

as needed.

Finally we show that
(2)
implies
(1)
.
Suppose that
\chi\in W_{\geq-1}(f)
satisfies condition
(2)
.
Let
n=\dim W_{\geq-1}(f)/W_{\geq-1}(f)^{\chi}=\dim W_{\geq-1}(f)\cdot\chi
and let

be the ideal defined in Lemma
3.1.4
.
By that lemma,
I(n)
is Poisson and
I(n)\subseteq\mathfrak{m}_{\chi}
, so

I(n)\subseteq\CoreP(\chi)
.

Therefore if

then
\CoreP(\chi)\neq 0
.
To show that

it suffices to find
\omega\in W_{\geq-1}(f)^{*}
with
\dim W_{\geq-1}(f)\cdot\omega>n
.
In fact, we will find
\omega\in W_{\geq-1}^{*}
with

Indeed, as
(2)

\Rightarrow

(3)
, if

then

can be represented by a linearly recurrent sequence by Remark

. On the other hand, the sequence
1,\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{3},\ldots
is clearly not linearly recurrent.
∎

Remark 3.1.7
.

Similarly to Remarks
3.0.3
and
5.2.3
, we should expect that “most” sequences are not linearly recurrent and that the linearly recurrent sequences are parameterised by a countable union of affine varieties, although we do not formalise these notions here.

Note that

gives a complete characterization of local functions on

.

3.2.
Local functions on
W
and applications

In this subsection we set
\mathfrak{g}={\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]\partial
and define
W(f)
similarly to
W_{\geq 1}(f)
.

A partial analogue of Theorem
3.1.1
holds for
W
.

Theorem 3.2.1
.

For any
f\in{\Bbbk}[t]\setminus\{0\}
and
\chi\in W(f)^{*}
the following conditions are equivalent:

(0)

\chi
is the restriction of a local function on
W
;

(1)

\CoreP(\chi)\neq(0)
;

(2)

\dim W(f)/W(f)^{\chi}<\infty
;

(3)

there exists
h\in{\Bbbk}[x]
such that
\chi|_{W(fh)}=0
.

Remark 3.2.2
.

The reason that Theorem
3.2.1
differs slightly from Theorem
3.1.1
is that the function
\Res_{0}(\cdot)\in W^{*}
satisfies condition (4) of Theorem
3.1.1
but does not satisfy the other conditions (0), (1), (2), (3).

Before proving Theorem
3.2.1
we give two lemmata on functions defined by residues.
Denote by
{\Bbbk}((t))
the field of formal Laurent power series in
t
.
Fix
f\in{\Bbbk}((t))
and consider the map

(3.2.3)

(a,b)\mapsto(a,b)_{f}:=\Res_{0}(f(ab^{\prime}-a^{\prime}b))

which defines a skew-symmetric bilinear form on
{\Bbbk}((t))
.

Lemma 3.2.4
.

The kernel of
(a,b)_{f}
is one-dimensional if
f
is a perfect square in
{\Bbbk}((t))
and is trivial otherwise.
In the first case the kernel is generated by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{f}}
.

Proof.

Let
a
be in the kernel of
(\cdot,\cdot)_{f}
.
Then

(3.2.5)

\Res_{0}(fa^{2}r^{\prime})=\Res_{0}(f(a(ar)^{\prime}-a^{\prime}(ar)))=(a,ar)_{f}=0

for all
r\in{\Bbbk}((t))
.
This implies that
fa^{2}
is constant.
Thus
f
is a perfect square in
{\Bbbk}((t))
and
a
is proportional to
\frac{1}{\sqrt{f}}
.
Equation (
3.2.5
) also gives that if
f
is a perfect square, then
\frac{1}{\sqrt{f}}
belongs to the kernel of
(\cdot,\cdot)_{f}
.
∎

The second lemma proves part of (a more general version of) Remark
3.2.2
.

Lemma 3.2.6
.

Let
g\in{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]\setminus\{0\}
and define
\omega\in W(g^{2})^{*}
by

\omega=\Res_{0}(\frac{\cdot}{g^{2}}).

Then
\dim W(g^{2})\cdot\omega=\infty
.

Proof.

For
a,b\in{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]
we have

B_{\omega}(g^{2}a\partial,g^{2}b\partial)=\Res_{0}(g^{2}(ab^{\prime}-a^{\prime}b)).

Suppose that
a\not\in{\Bbbk}\cdot\frac{1}{g}
.
Then by Lemma
3.2.4
there is a formal Laurent series
\hat{b}
so that

(a,b)_{g^{2}}=\Res_{0}(g^{2}(a\hat{b}^{\prime}-a^{\prime}\hat{b}))\neq 0.

However, the computation of
\Res_{0}(g^{2}(a\hat{b}^{\prime}-a^{\prime}\hat{b}))
needs only finitely many terms in the Laurent expansion of
\hat{b}
and so we may replace
\hat{b}
by
b\in{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]
so that

\Res_{0}(g^{2}(ab^{\prime}-a^{\prime}b))\neq 0
.
Thus
ag^{2}\partial\not\in\ker B_{\omega}=W(g^{2})^{\omega}
.
This means that
W(g^{2})^{\omega}\subseteq{\Bbbk}\cdot g\partial
and
\dim W(g^{2})\cdot\omega=\infty
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.2.1
.

The proofs of
(0)

\iff

(3)
,
(1)

\Rightarrow

(2)
and
(3)

\Rightarrow

(2)

\Rightarrow

(1)
are very similar to the corresponding steps of the proof of Theorem
3.1.1
.
The only part which is significantly different is (2)
\Rightarrow(3)
.

Pick
\chi
satisfying
(2)
and
h\in W(f)^{\chi}\setminus\{0\}
.
Then (
3.1.6
) holds for all
r\in{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]
.
Unfortunately, this is not enough to show that
\chi
vanishes on
W(h^{2}f^{2})
, as the map
r\mapsto r^{\prime}
is not surjective on
{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]
.

Consider
\chi|_{W(f^{2}h^{2})}
: we have

\chi(f^{2}h^{2}p\partial)=\chi(\frac{f^{2}h^{2}}{t}\partial)\Res_{0}(p)

for all
p\in{\Bbbk}[t]
. Suppose that
\chi(\frac{f^{2}h^{2}}{t}\partial)\neq 0
.
Then
\dim W(f^{2}h^{2})\cdot\chi|_{W(f^{2}h^{2})}=\infty
by Lemma
3.2.6
.
Thus
\dim W(h^{2}f^{2})\cdot\chi=\infty
, as
\dim W(f)/W(f^{2}h^{2})<\infty
.
This contradicts our assumption that
\chi
satisfies
(2)
and so
\chi(\frac{f^{2}h^{2}}{t}\partial)=0
, i.e.
\chi|_{W(f^{2}h^{2})}=0
.
∎

To end the subsection, we apply Theorem
3.2.1
to obtain a structure result on finite codimension subalgebras of
W
.
For a polynomial
f\in{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]
set

{\rm rad}(f):=\prod\{(t-x)|x\in{\Bbbk}^{\times},f(x)=0\}.

Proposition 3.2.7
.

Let
\mathfrak{k}
be a subalgebra of
W
of finite codimension. Then

(a)

there exists
f\in{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]
so that
W({\rm rad}(f))\supseteq\mathfrak{k}\supseteq W(f)
;

(b)

we can choose
f
satisfying
\mathrm{(a)}
so that
f\in{\Bbbk}[t]
,
f
is monic and
f(0)\neq 0
;

(c)

if we assume that
f
is of minimal degree then such a choice of
f
is unique.

Proof.

The inclusion
\mathfrak{k}\subseteq W
induces the dual map
W^{*}\to\mathfrak{k}^{*}
.
We identify
(W/\mathfrak{k})^{*}
with the kernel of this map; that is, with elements of
W^{*}
which vanish on
\mathfrak{k}
, so
\mathfrak{k}
is the set of common zeroes of
(W/\mathfrak{k})^{*}\subseteq W^{*}
.
Let
\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{s}
be a basis of
(W/\mathfrak{k})^{*}
.
Fix
i\in\{1,\dots,s\}
; by definition we have
B_{\chi_{i}}(\mathfrak{k},\mathfrak{k})=0
, so
\mathfrak{k}
is an isotropic subspace of
W
with respect to
B_{\chi_{i}}
.
Hence the rank of
B_{\chi_{i}}
is at most
2\dim(W/\mathfrak{k})
and thus is finite.
By Theorem
3.2.1
,
\chi_{i}
is local.

Theorem
3.2.1
implies that for all
i
there is
h_{i}\in{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]\setminus\{0\}
with
\chi_{i}(W(h_{i}))=0
.
Therefore
W(h_{1}\cdots h_{s})
is annihilated by all
\chi_{i}
and therefore
W(h_{1}\cdots h_{s})\subseteq\mathfrak{k}
as desired.

Let
f\in{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]\setminus\{0\}
with
\mathfrak{k}\supseteq W(f)
; we may assume without loss of generality that
f\in{\Bbbk}[t]
,
f
is monic and
f(0)\neq 0
as
W(f)
corresponds to an ideal of
{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]
.
Suppose in addition that
f
has minimal degree among all such polynomials with
\mathfrak{k}\supseteq W(f)
.
Thus if
\mathfrak{k}\supseteq W(h)
, then by the Euclidean algorithm
f|h
.
This justifies uniqueness of
f
.

Write
f=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(t-x_{i})^{a_{i}}
with the
x_{i}\neq 0
distinct and
a_{i}>0
;
set
h:={\rm rad}(f)=(t-x_{1})\cdots(t-x_{n}).
It is clear that
h\mid f\mid h^{\max(a_{i})}
.
Let
k\partial\in\mathfrak{k}
.
We wish to show that
h|k
.
Indeed, for all
r\in{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]
the element

[k\partial,fr\partial]=(k(fr^{\prime}+f^{\prime}r)-frk^{\prime})\partial
is in
\mathfrak{k}
.
As
\mathfrak{k}\supseteq W(f)
, thus
kf^{\prime}r\in\mathfrak{k}
for all
r\in{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]
.
Thus
\mathfrak{k}\supseteq W(kf^{\prime})
and so
f|f^{\prime}k
.
This forces
k
to vanish at all roots of
f
, which is equivalent to
h|k
.
∎

3.3.
Local functions on
V\!ir

In this subsection we set
\mathfrak{g}=V\!ir
.

The natural map
V\!ir\to W
extends to the morphism
\Sa(V\!ir)\to\Sa(W)
; the kernel is the Poisson ideal
(z)
of
\Sa(V\!ir)
.
The main goal of the subsection is to prove the following analogue of Theorem
3.2.1
for
V\!ir
.

Theorem 3.3.1
.

For

\chi\in V\!ir^{*}
we have

\CoreP(\chi)\neq(z-\chi(z))\quad\iff\quad\chi\mbox{ is local }\quad\iff\quad\dim V\!ir/V\!ir^{\chi}<\infty.

In particular, if
\CoreP(\chi)\neq(z-\chi(z))
then
\chi(z)=0
.

Remark 3.3.2
.

It follows from Theorems
3.2.1
and
3.3.1
that
\chi\in V\!ir^{*}
is local if and only if there is some
f\in{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]
such that
\chi
vanishes on
W(f)+{\Bbbk}\cdot z\subseteq V\!ir
.

Before proving Theorem
3.3.1
, we consider arbitrary subalgebras of
V\!ir
of finite codimension, and show they are strongly constrained.

Proposition 3.3.3
.

If
\mathfrak{k}
is a subalgebra of
V\!ir
of finite codimension, then
z\in[\mathfrak{k},\mathfrak{k}]
.
Thus
\dim V\!ir/\mathfrak{k}<\infty
if and only if
\mathfrak{k}
contains some
W(f)+{\Bbbk}\cdot z
, where
f\in{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]\setminus\{0\}
.

This result generalises
[
OW
, Proposition 2.3]
, which considered subalgebras of
V\!ir
of codimension 1. We also note that
[
OW
]
refers to subalgebras of
V\!ir
of the form
W(f)+{\Bbbk}\cdot z
as
polynomial subalgebras
.

Proof of Proposition
3.3.3
.

Let
\bar{\mathfrak{k}}
be the image of
\mathfrak{k}
in
W
. By Proposition
3.2.7
there is some
f\in{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]\setminus\{0\}
so that
\bar{\mathfrak{k}}\supseteq W(f)
. Thus for all
p\in\mathbb{Z}
, there is
\zeta_{p}\in{\Bbbk}
so that the element

v_{p}:=ft^{p}\partial+\zeta_{p}z

is in
\mathfrak{k}
.
Therefore,
[\mathfrak{k},\mathfrak{k}]
contains the elements

(3.3.4)

\frac{1}{q-p}[v_{p},v_{q}]=f^{2}t^{p+q-1}\partial+\Res_{0}\Bigl(t^{p+q-3}(2t^{2}(f^{\prime})^{2}+ff^{\prime}t(p+q-1)-t^{2}ff^{\prime\prime}+f^{2}pq)\Bigr)z

for all
p,q\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Fix
d=p+q
and consider
p=d-q
as a function of
q
.
The only part of (
3.3.4
) that varies with
q
is

q(d-q)\Res_{0}(t^{d-3}f^{2})
.

If

\Res_{0}(t^{d-3}f^{2})

is not zero then

\frac{1}{d-2q_{1}}[v_{d-q_{1}},v_{q_{1}}]-\frac{1}{d-2q_{2}}[v_{d-q_{2}},v_{q_{2}}]=(q_{1}-q_{2})(d-q_{1}-q_{2})\Res_{0}(t^{d-3}f^{2})z

is a nonzero scalar multiple of
z
for almost all
q_{1},q_{2}
.
If
z\not\in[\mathfrak{k},\mathfrak{k}]
, we therefore have

\Res_{0}(t^{d}f^{2})=0
for all
d\in\mathbb{Z}
.
This implies that
f^{2}=0
, contradicting our assumption on
f
.

The final sentence is an immediate consequence of Proposition
3.2.7
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.3.1
.

Let
\chi\in V\!ir^{*}
.
If
\chi
is local then by definition
\chi
descends to a local function
\overline{\chi}
on
V\!ir/(z)\cong W
.
By Theorem
3.2.1
,

\CoreP(\chi)\supsetneqq(z)
and
\dim V\!ir/V\!ir^{\chi}=\dim W/W^{\overline{\chi}}<\infty
.

If
\dim V\!ir/V\!ir^{\chi}<\infty
then by Lemma
3.3.3
,
z\in[V\!ir^{\chi},V\!ir^{\chi}]
and so
\chi(z)=0
, as
\chi
vanishes, by definition, on
[V\!ir^{\chi},V\!ir^{\chi}]
.
We may thus factor out
z
and apply Theorem
3.2.1
again to conclude that
\chi
is local.

Finally, suppose that
\CoreP(\chi)\neq(z-\chi(z))
.
Then by Corollary
3.0.5

\dim V\!ir\cdot\chi=\dim V\!ir/V\!ir^{\chi}<\infty
.
∎

As an immediate corollary of Theorem
3.3.1
, we obtain a powerful result on Poisson ideals of
\Sa(V\!ir)
.

Corollary 3.3.5
.

If
\zeta\in{\Bbbk}^{\times}
then
\Sa(V\!ir)/(z-\zeta)
is Poisson simple, i.e. contains no nontrivial Poisson ideals.

Proof.

Let
\zeta\in{\Bbbk}
.
If
\Sa(V\!ir)/(z-\zeta)
is not Poisson simple then
(z-\zeta)
is strictly contained in some proper Poisson ideal
J
of
\Sa(V\!ir)
.
By the Nullstellensatz there is some
\chi\in V\!ir^{*}
with
J\subseteq\mathfrak{m}_{\chi}
; thus
\chi(z)=\zeta
as
z-\zeta\in\mathfrak{m}_{\chi}
.
Further
\CoreP(\chi)\supseteq J\supsetneqq(z-\zeta)
and so by Theorem
3.3.1
we have
\zeta=0
.
∎

We thus show that almost all prime Poisson ideals of
\Sa(V\!ir)
contain
z
.

Corollary 3.3.6
.

Let
Q
be a prime Poisson ideal of
\Sa(V\!ir)
.
Then either:

•

Q=(0)
;

•

Q=(z-\zeta)
for some
\zeta\in{\Bbbk}^{\times}
;

•

Q\supseteq(z)
.

Proof.

By Corollary
3.3.5
and primeness of
Q
, it suffices to prove that if
Q\neq(0)
then
Q
contains a nonzero element of
{\Bbbk}[z]
.
Let
h\in Q\setminus\{0\}
; using primeness of
Q
we may assume that
h
is not a multiple of any element of
{\Bbbk}[z]\setminus{\Bbbk}
.
Let
\chi\in V\!ir^{*}
so that
Q\subseteq\mathfrak{m}_{\chi}
.
As
h\in\CoreP(\chi)
, we see that
\CoreP(\chi)\neq(z-\chi(z))
.
By Theorem
3.3.1

\CoreP(\chi)\supseteq(z)
.
Thus, applying Lemma
2.0.4
(a),

Q=\bigcap\{\CoreP(\chi)\ |\ \ev_{\chi}(Q)=0\}\supseteq(z).

∎

Given Corollary
3.3.5
it is natural to conjecture:

Conjecture 3.3.7
.

If
\zeta\neq 0
then
\Ua(V\!ir)/(z-\zeta)
is simple.

However, we as yet have no proof of Conjecture
3.3.7
.
Note that the obvious strategy of proof by taking the associated graded of an ideal
(z-\zeta)\subsetneqq J\triangleleft\Ua(V\!ir)
does not work; for in this case
\gr J\ni z
and Corollary
3.3.5
is not directly relevant.

4.
Pseudo-orbits and Poisson primitive ideals for the algebras of interest

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be one of
V\!ir,W,W_{\geq-1}
, or
W_{\geq 1}
.
In this section we will describe the pseudo-orbits for
\mathfrak{g}
, using the
results on local functions from the previous section and the general strategy of Proposition
2.3.1
, and derive some consequences for the Poisson primitive spectrum of
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
.

We begin by describing the pseudo-orbits of non-local functions, where the results of Section
3
quickly give the answer.

Proposition 4.0.1
.

For
\mathfrak{g}=W,W_{\geq-1}
, or
W_{\geq 1}
, the non-local functions in
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
form a pseudo-orbit. If
\mathfrak{g}=V\!ir
, then for any
\zeta\in{\Bbbk}
the non-local functions
\chi
with
\chi(z)=\zeta
form a pseudo-orbit.

Proof.

This is immediate from Theorems
3.1.1
(
\mathfrak{g}=W_{\geq-1}
or
W_{\geq 1}
),
3.2.1
(
\mathfrak{g}=W
), or
3.3.1
(
\mathfrak{g}=V\!ir
). For by those results, if
\mathfrak{g}=W,W_{\geq-1},
or
W_{\geq 1}
then
\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is not local if and only if
\CoreP(\chi)=0
; and if
\mathfrak{g}=V\!ir
, then
\chi
is not local if and only if
\CoreP(\chi)=(z-\chi(z))
.
∎

We may thus restrict to considering pseudo-orbits of local functions. By Proposition
4.0.1
if
\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is local and
\omega\in\mathbb{O}(\chi)
then
\omega
is also local.
Since by definition local functions on

vanish on

, the pseudo-orbits for

directly determine those for
V\!ir
.

Thus for the rest of the section we let
\mathfrak{g}
be
W,W_{\geq-1}
, or
W_{\geq 1}
.
In Subsection

we will introduce a finite-dimensional action which determines the pseudo-orbits of one-point local functions on

, in Subsection

we describe the orbits of this action explicitly, and in Subsection

we use this action to describe pseudo-orbits of arbitrary local functions.

4.1.
An algebraic group acting on local functions

Set
\mathfrak{g}
to be
W
,
W_{\geq-1}
, or
W_{\geq 1}
. We fix notation for the subsection.

Definition 4.1.1
.

For

,

For all

If
\chi

\mathfrak{g}

for all

.
We now study the action of
\mathfrak{g}
on
{\it Loc}_{x}

2.3.1
.

We begin by defining a group action.
Fix
x\in{\Bbbk}

\mathfrak{g}=W

\tilde{t}:=t-x

Clearly, (
3.0.1

({\Bbbk}[[\tilde{t}]]\partial)^{*}

For every

with
s(x)=x

we introduce a local change of coordinates endomorphism
\End_{t\to s}(\cdot)

of
{\Bbbk}[[\tilde{t}]]

t\to s
.
Note that
\End_{t\to s}
is invertible if
s^{\prime}(x)\neq 0
.
Let
{\it DLoc}_{x}
,

the group of

x

\End_{t\to s}
with
s(x)=x
and
s^{\prime}(x)\neq 0
.
The group
{\it DLoc}_{x}
has a subgroup of transformations of the form
t\to\zeta t+(1-\zeta)x
, for
\zeta\in{\Bbbk}^{\times}
; we also write this transformation as
\tilde{t}\to\zeta\tilde{t}
and let
{\it Dil}_{x}

denote the group of such transformations, which we term
dilations at
x

Pick
s\in{\Bbbk}[[\tilde{t}]]

s(x)=x
and
s^{\prime}(x)\neq 0
.
We extend
\End_{t\to s}
to an automorphism of
{\Bbbk}[[\tilde{t}]]\partial

(4.1.2)

t\mapsto s,\hskip 10.0pt\partial\mapsto\frac{1}{s^{\prime}}\partial~(=\partial_{s}).

This gives actions of
{\it DLoc}_{x}
on
{\Bbbk}[[\tilde{t}]]\partial
and on
({\Bbbk}[[\tilde{t}]]\partial)^{*}
.
We may consider
{\it Loc}_{x}^{\leq n}
as a subset of
({\Bbbk}[[\tilde{t}]]\partial)^{*}
, and this subset is preserved by the
{\it DLoc}_{x}
-action.

Denote by
{\it DLoc}_{x}^{\leq n}

the image of
{\it DLoc}_{x}
in the group
\Aut({\it Loc}_{x}^{\leq n})
of linear automorphisms of
{\it Loc}_{x}^{\leq n}
.
Although
{\it DLoc}_{x}
is infinite-dimensional, its image in

is a finite-dimensional solvable algebraic group.
Let us consider the action of the corresponding (finite-dimensional) Lie algebra, which we denote by
\lie_{x}^{\leq n}
.

Lemma 4.1.3
.

Identify
\lie_{x}^{\leq n}
with the tangent space to
{\it DLoc}_{x}^{\leq n}
at the identity.
Let
s\in{\Bbbk}[[t]]
with

s(x)=0
and denote by
\xi_{s}
the tangent direction at the identity defined by the line
h\to\End_{t\to t+hs}(\cdot)
for
h\in{\Bbbk}
.
The action of
{\it DLoc}_{x}^{\leq n}
derives to an action of
\xi_{s}
on
{\it Loc}_{x}^{\leq n}
. Then

(a)

\lie_{x}^{\leq n}
consists of vectors of the form
\xi_{s}
with
s(x)=0
;

(b)

\xi_{s}\cdot\chi=s\partial\cdot\chi
for all
\chi\in{\it Loc}_{x}
.

Proof.

Part (a) is clear from the definition of
{\it DLoc}_{x}^{\leq n}
.
For (b), as the action of
\xi_{s}
on
{\it Loc}_{x}
is induced by the action (
4.1.2
) on
{\Bbbk}[[\tilde{t}]]\partial
, it is enough to check that
\xi_{s}\cdot f\partial=[s\partial,f\partial]
for all
f\partial\in\mathfrak{g}
.
But

\displaystyle\xi_{s}\cdot f\partial

\displaystyle=(\xi_{s}\cdot f)\partial+f(\xi_{s}\cdot\partial)

\displaystyle=(\partial_{h}f(t+hs))|_{h=0}\partial+f(\partial_{h}(\End_{t\to t+hs}\partial))|_{h=0}

\displaystyle=(f^{\prime}(t+hs)s)|_{h=0}\partial+f(\partial_{h}(\frac{1}{1+hs^{\prime}}\partial))|_{h=0}

\displaystyle=f^{\prime}s\partial-fs^{\prime}\partial=[s\partial,f\partial],

as needed.
∎

We now let
\mathfrak{g}=W_{\geq-1}
.
For all
x,n
, the coadjoint action of
W_{\geq-1}(t-x)

preserves
{\it Loc}_{x}^{\leq n}
, and Lemma

shows that

(4.1.4)

W_{\geq-1}(t-x)\cdot\chi=\lie_{x}^{\leq n}\cdot\chi

for

For

define

{\it Shift}_{z}(\chi_{x;\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\ldots}):=\chi_{x+z;\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\ldots}.

The set
\{{\it Shift}_{z}|z\in{\Bbbk}\}
forms a one-dimensional algebraic group, which we denote by
{\it Shifts}
;

clearly
{\it Shifts}\cong{\Bbbk}^{+}
.
Deriving the action of
{\it Shifts}

{\it Loc}_{W_{\geq-1}}^{\leq n}
, we have

(4.1.5)

\partial_{h}{\it Shift}_{h}(\chi_{x;\alpha_{0},\cdots,\alpha_{n}}(f\partial))|_{h=0}\\
=\partial_{h}(\alpha_{0}f(x+h)+\alpha_{1}f^{\prime}(x+h)+\cdots+\alpha_{n}f^{(n)}(x+h))|_{h=0}\\
=\alpha_{0}f^{\prime}(x)+\cdots+\alpha_{n}f^{(n+1)}(x)=(\partial\cdot\chi_{x;\alpha_{0},\cdots,\alpha_{n}})(f\partial)

and thus we may without ambiguity identify the Lie algebra of
{\it Shifts}
with
{\Bbbk}\cdot\partial
.

For all

,

gives a continuous homomorphism

In particular, we can take

, and then for

and for all

This allows us to introduce the action of
\widehat{{\it DLoc}}^{\leq n}:={\it Shifts}\times{\it DLoc}_{1}^{\leq n}

and
\widehat{{\it DLoc}}:={\it Shifts}\times{\it DLoc}_{1}

Note that
\widehat{{\it DLoc}}
and
\widehat{{\it DLoc}}^{\leq n}
also act on

is equivariant.

Let
\widehat{\lie}^{\leq n}

denote the Lie algebra of
\widehat{{\it DLoc}}^{\leq n}
.
From Lemma
4.1.3
we have:

Lemma 4.1.6
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=W,W_{\geq-1}
or

and

,

(a)

for

and

.

Proof.

Parts (a), (b), and (d) are straightforward from the above discussion.
For part (c) note that the image of

in

depends only on the Taylor series expansion of

, which is not affected by the behaviour of

∎

It is useful to have a more detailed description of

and

.
The main point here is that

Pick

. Let

(or

Elements of the form

.

Let

4.2.
Explicit description of
{\it DLoc}_{x}^{\leq n}
-orbits

We now compute the
{\it DLoc}_{x}
-orbits of one-point local functions; in the next subsection we will see that this allows us to compute the pseudo-orbit of an arbitrary local function.
If
\mathfrak{g}=W_{\geq-1}
then the action of
{\it DLoc}_{x}
is clearly homogeneous in
x
for all
x\in{\Bbbk}
, and similarly for
W
for
x\neq 0
.
If
x=0
and
\mathfrak{g}=W_{\geq 1}
the story is a bit more delicate.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be
W_{\geq-1}
or
W
or
W_{\geq 1}
, and let
x\in{\Bbbk}
. If
\mathfrak{g}=W
or
W_{\geq-1}
we additionally assume that
x\neq 0
.
For
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
we set
e_{i}(x)=(t-x)^{i+1}\partial
and define
e_{i}(x)^{*}

by the formula

f\partial\mapsto\frac{f^{i+1}(x)}{(i+1)!},

so that
e_{i}(x)^{*}(e_{j}(x))=\delta_{ij}
.
(We view the
e_{i}(x)^{*}
as elements either of
({\Bbbk}[[t-x]]\partial)^{*}
or
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, depending on context.)
The main goal of this subsection is to prove the following theorem, and to consider its consequences.

Theorem 4.2.1
.

Assume
\mathfrak{g}=W_{\geq-1}
or
W
or
W_{\geq 1}
, and let
x\in{\Bbbk}
. If
\mathfrak{g}=W
or
W_{\geq 1}
we additionally assume that
x\neq 0
.
Fix
n,\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{n}
with
\beta_{n}\neq 0
and set
\chi=\chi_{x;\beta_{0},...,\beta_{n}}
.
Let
e_{i}:=e_{i}(x)
and
e_{i}^{*}:=e_{i}(x)^{*}
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.

(a)

If
n
is even then
{\it DLoc}_{x}^{\leq n}\chi={\it DLoc}_{x}^{\leq n}e_{n-1}^{*}
, and
\dim{\it DLoc}_{x}^{\leq n}\chi=n+1
.

(b)

If
n
is odd and
n>1
then there is
\beta
such that
{\it DLoc}_{x}^{\leq n}\chi={\it DLoc}_{x}^{\leq n}(e_{n-1}^{*}+\beta e_{k}^{*})
where
k=\frac{n-1}{2}
. We have

\dim{\it DLoc}_{x}^{\leq n}\chi=n
.

(b
′
)

If
n=1
then

{\it DLoc}_{0}^{\leq n}\chi={\it DLoc}_{x}^{\leq n}(\beta_{1}e_{0}^{*})
, and
\dim{\it DLoc}_{x}^{\leq n}\chi=1
.

(c)

Pick
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in{\Bbbk}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
.
Then
{\it DLoc}_{x}^{\leq n}(e_{2k}^{*}+\beta_{1}e_{k}^{*})={\it DLoc}_{x}^{\leq n}(e_{2k}^{*}+\beta_{2}e_{k}^{*})
if and only if
\beta_{1}=\pm\beta_{2}
.

(c
′
)

Pick
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in{\Bbbk}
.
Then
{\it DLoc}_{x}^{\leq 1}(\beta_{1}e_{0}^{*})={\it DLoc}_{x}^{\leq 1}(\beta_{2}e_{0}^{*})
if and only if
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}
.

Proof.

The proof is the same for any base point; to reduce notation we give the proof for
\mathfrak{g}=W_{\geq-1}
and
x=0
.

First we compute the dimensions of the corresponding orbits.
Note that

\dim{\it DLoc}_{0}^{\leq n}\chi=\dim\lie_{0}^{\leq n}\cdot\chi=\dim W_{\geq 0}\cdot\chi,

see (
4.1.4
).
Moreover, (
4.1.5
) implies that
W_{\geq-1}\cdot\chi\not\subseteq{\it Loc}_{0}^{\leq n}
.
Thus
\dim W_{\geq-1}\cdot\chi=\dim W_{\geq 0}\cdot\chi+1
.
Further
\dim W_{\geq-1}\cdot\chi=\rk B_{\chi}
.
The rank of this form can be evaluated explicitly.
Indeed,

\chi_{0;\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{n}}([e_{i},e_{j}])=\begin{cases}0&{\rm~if~}i+j>n-1\\
(j-i)(i+j+1)!\beta_{i+j+1}&{\rm~if~}i+j\leq n-1\end{cases}.

From this formula it is clear that
B_{\chi}(e_{i},e_{j})=0
if
i\geq n+1
or
j\geq n+1
.
Thus the rank of this form can be evaluated on the first
(n+2)\times(n+2)
entries corresponding to
-1\leq i,j\leq n
.
This block is skew-upper-triangular.
If
n
is even then all values on the skew-diagonal line are nonzero (because
\beta_{n}\neq 0
); hence the rank is
n+2
in this case.
If
n
is odd then this skew-diagonal line contains zero only in the position corresponding to
i=j=\frac{n-1}{2}
; hence the rank is
n+1
in this case.
This provides the desired dimensions.

The idea of the rest of the proof is to use the subnormal series in Lemma
4.1.7
to reduce the number of coefficients which we have to consider step by step.

Let
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. For
j\geq 1
we have

\End_{t\to t+\alpha t^{j+1}}(e_{i})=\frac{(t+\alpha t^{j+1})^{i+1}}{1+\alpha(j+1)t^{j}}\partial=(t^{i+1}+\alpha(i-j)t^{i+j+1}+\mbox{higher})\partial=e_{i}+\alpha(i-j)e_{i+j}+\mbox{higher}.

Thus

(4.2.2)

\End_{t\to t+\alpha t^{j+1}}(e_{i}^{*})=e_{i}^{*}+\alpha(i-2j)e_{i-j}^{*}+\mbox{a linear combination of $e_{<i-j}^{*}$}.

Likewise

(4.2.3)

\End_{t\to\zeta t}(e_{i}^{*})=\zeta^{i}e_{i}^{*}.

We apply these transformations to

(4.2.4)

\chi_{0;\beta_{0},\dots,\beta_{n}}=\beta_{0}e_{-1}^{*}+\dots+\beta_{n}(n!)e_{n-1}^{*},

noting that the coefficient of
e_{n-1}^{*}
in (
4.2.4
) is nonzero.

By applying
\End_{t\to t+\alpha_{1}t^{2}}
with appropriate
\alpha_{1}
we may cancel the coefficient of
e_{n-2}^{*}
, using (
4.2.2
). This does not affect the coefficient of
e_{n-1}^{*}
.
Then by applying the appropriate
\End_{t\to t+\alpha_{2}t^{3}}

We now prove (c

Finally, we prove (c).
Formula
(
4.2.3
) implies
that if

{\it DLoc}_{0}^{\leq n}(e_{2k}^{*}+\beta_{1}e_{k}^{*})={\it DLoc}_{0}^{\leq n}(e_{2k}^{*}+\beta_{2}e_{k}^{*}).

Pick
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in{\Bbbk}
and set

.
Assume that

with

We next describe the “Bruhat order” on
{\it DLoc}_{x}^{\leq n}
orbits (that is, the inclusions of orbit closures): it turns out that it can almost be computed just from the dimension of the orbit.

Corollary 4.2.9
.

Let
\mathfrak{g},x
be as in the statement of Theorem
4.2.1
.
Let
\chi^{-},\chi^{+}
be local functions on
\mathfrak{g}
based at
x
and of order
\leq n
.

Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

{\it DLoc}_{x}^{\leq n}\cdot\chi^{-}
is contained in the closure of
{\it DLoc}_{x}^{\leq n}\cdot\chi^{+}
and
{\it DLoc}_{x}^{\leq n}\cdot\chi^{-}\neq{\it DLoc}_{x}^{\leq n}\cdot\chi^{+}
;

(2)

\CoreP(\chi^{+})\subsetneqq\CoreP(\chi^{-})
;

(3)

\dim{\it DLoc}_{x}^{\leq n}\cdot\chi^{-}<\dim{\it DLoc}_{x}^{\leq n}\cdot\chi^{+}
and
\chi^{+}
is not of order 1.

Proof.

We again give the proof for
\mathfrak{g}=W_{\geq-1}
and
x=0
. We will show that
(2)\Rightarrow(3)\Leftrightarrow(1)\Rightarrow(2)
.
Next, without loss of generality we assume that
n
is the order of
\chi^{+}
.

We first show that
(3)
implies
(1)
.
As in Theorem
4.2.1
, pick the related presentation
\chi_{0;\beta_{0}^{+},\beta_{1}^{+},\ldots,\beta_{n}^{+}}
for
\chi^{+}
, with
\beta_{n}^{+}\neq 0
.
If
n
is even then the closure of
{\it DLoc}_{0}^{\leq n}\cdot\chi^{+}
equals
{\it Loc}_{0}^{\leq n}
.
One the other hand all orbits of strictly smaller dimension belong to
{\it Loc}_{0}^{\leq n}
, so this completes the proof in this case.

If
n=2k+1
is odd then
k\geq 1
by the assumptions of (3).
The closure of
{\it DLoc}_{0}^{\leq n}\cdot\chi^{+}
is an irreducible subvariety of
{\it Loc}_{0}^{\leq n}
of codimension 1, i.e. a hypersurface defined by some function
F_{+}
.
We claim that this function is semiinvariant with respect to
{\it DLoc}_{0}^{\leq n}
.

To prove that
F_{+}
is semiinvariant, we describe it in more detail.
Let
R={\Bbbk}[\beta_{0},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n-1},\beta_{n}^{\pm 1}]
, where
\beta_{0},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}
are free variables. Denote by
(\cdot)_{{\Bbbk}\to R}
the base change from
{\Bbbk}
to
R
.
The arguments of Theorem
4.2.1
(b) imply that there exists a group element
g\in({\it DLoc}_{0}^{\leq n})_{{\Bbbk}\to R}
and
h\in R
such that

g\cdot\chi_{\mbox{$0$};\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{k\text{~times}},\mbox{$h$},\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{k\text{~times}},\mbox{$\beta_{n}$}}=\chi_{0;\beta_{0},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}}.

Recall that
k=\frac{n-1}{2}\geq 1
.
By replacing each
\beta_{i}
by
\frac{e_{i-1}}{i!}
(cf. (
4.2.4
)) we may identify
R
with
{\Bbbk}[{\it Loc}_{0}^{\leq n}][e_{2k}^{-1}]
.
(Here we regard the
e_{i}
as functions on
{\it Loc}_{0}^{\leq n}
in the obvious way.)
Then
h=f/e_{2k}^{\ell}
for some

and
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.

Let
\chi,\chi^{\prime}\in{\it Loc}_{0}^{\leq n}
with
e_{2k}(\chi),e_{2k}(\chi^{\prime})\neq 0

As in the proof of Theorem
4.2.1
(c),
{\it DLoc}_{0}^{\leq n}\cdot\chi={\it DLoc}_{0}^{\leq n}\cdot\chi^{\prime}
if and only if

e_{2k}(\chi)^{-1}\frac{f^{2}(\chi)}{e_{2k}(\chi)^{2\ell}}=e_{2k}(\chi^{\prime})^{-1}\frac{f^{2}(\chi^{\prime})}{e_{2k}(\chi^{\prime})^{2\ell}}.

The rational function

(4.2.10)

F:=f^{2}/e_{2k}^{2\ell+1}

is thus
{\it DLoc}_{0}^{\leq n}
-invariant and separates orbits.
Therefore,
{\it DLoc}_{0}^{\leq n}\cdot\chi^{+}
is the hypersurface in
{\it Loc}_{0}^{\leq n}\setminus{\it Loc}_{0}^{\leq n-1}
defined by

F_{+}:=e_{2k}(\chi^{+})^{2\ell+1}f^{2}-f^{2}(\chi^{+})e_{2k}^{2\ell+1}.

Note that
F_{+}
is semiinvariant, as claimed.

All orbits of dimension
<n
belong to
{\it Loc}_{0}^{\leq n-2}
.
We will check that the closure of
{\it DLoc}_{0}^{\leq n}\cdot\chi^{+}
contains
{\it Loc}_{0}^{\leq n-2}
.
The orbit closure
\overline{{\it DLoc}_{0}^{\leq n}\chi^{+}}
is defined in
{\it Loc}_{0}^{\leq n}
by
F_{+}
, so we must show that
F_{+}|_{{\it Loc}_{0}^{\leq n-2}}=0.

The restriction of
F_{+}
to
{\it Loc}^{\leq n-1}_{0}

{\it DLoc}_{0}^{1+,\leq n}

4.1.7
.
The arguments of Theorem
4.2.1
imply that all
n

e_{n-1}=\beta_{n-1}
for some
\beta_{n-1}\in{\Bbbk}
.
This implies that the restriction of
F_{+}
is a polynomial in
e_{n-1}
.
The fact that
F_{+}
is
{\it DLoc}_{0}^{\leq n}
-semiinvariant implies that

F_{+}|_{{\it Loc}^{\leq n-1}_{0}}=c(e_{n-1})^{m}
for some
m\geq 0
and

.
This implies the desired condition that
F_{+}|_{{\it Loc}^{\leq n-2}_{0}}=0
.

We now show that
(1)
implies
(3)
, so suppose that
(1)
holds.
The dimension inequality in
(3)
is clear.
Thus we are left to deal with the case when
\chi^{+}
has order 1; we want to show this cannot happen.

Suppose now that
\chi^{+}=\chi_{x;\alpha,\beta}
has order 1, so
\beta\neq 0
.
By the dimension inequality,
\chi^{-}=0
.
Now, Theorem
4.2.1
(b
′

{\it DLoc}_{0}^{\leq 1}\cdot\chi^{+}={\it DLoc}_{0}^{\leq 1}\cdot(\beta e_{0}^{*})

We now show that
(1)\Rightarrow(2)
.
This is an application of Proposition
2.3.1
.
Let

(recall that
\mathfrak{g}=W_{\geq-1}

Let

Let

By Lemma

Thus by Proposition
2.3.1

\CoreP(\chi^{+})
is the kernel of the map

\textstyle{\Sa(\mathfrak{g})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

We still must show (2) implies (3).
If

.
By the discussion above and (

Finally, note that if

p_{\gamma}:\Sa(\mathfrak{g})\to{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1},y],\quad f\partial\mapsto fy+\gamma f^{\prime}.

4.3.
Implicit description of pseudo-orbits

Let
\mathfrak{g}=W
,
W_{\geq-1}
, or
W_{\geq 1}
. We now consider arbitrary local functions on
\mathfrak{g}
.
We will provide every pair of local functions
\chi,\nu\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
with an affine variety
X
acted on by an algebraic group
H
, an open subset
U\subseteq X
, a Poisson map
\phi:U\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, and a pair of points
x,y\in X
which will satisfy the conditions of Proposition
2.3.1
.
Thus we will reduce the mysterious infinite-dimensional case to the more easily comprehensible action of an algebraic group on an affine variety. We will use this to determine the pseudo-orbits in
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
.

Theorem 4.3.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=V\!ir
or
\mathfrak{g}=W
or
W_{\geq-1}
. Let
\chi^{{I}}
and
\chi^{{II}}
be local functions in
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
represented by sums

\chi^{{I}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\chi_{i}^{{I}},\hskip 10.0pt\chi^{{II}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\chi_{i}^{{II}},\hskip 10.0pt\chi_{i}^{{I}}\in{\it Loc}_{x_{i}^{{I}}},\chi_{i}^{{II}}\in{\it Loc}_{x_{i}^{{II}}},

such that
x^{{I}}_{i}\neq x^{{I}}_{j}
and
x^{{II}}_{i}\neq x^{{II}}_{j}
for
i\neq j
.
Then
\chi^{{I}}
and
\chi^{{II}}
are in the same pseudo-orbit if and only if it is possible to reorder
x^{{I}}_{i}
and
x^{{II}}_{i}
in such a way that

(4.3.2)

{\it DLoc}_{1}({\it Shift}_{1-x_{i}^{{I}}}\chi^{{I}}_{i})={\it DLoc}_{1}({\it Shift}_{1-x_{i}^{{II}}}\chi^{{II}}_{i})\mbox{ for all $i$;}

that is,

\widehat{DLoc}(\chi^{{I}}_{i})=\widehat{{\it DLoc}}(\chi^{{II}}_{i})\mbox{ for all $i$.}

In particular, if
\mathbb{O}(\chi^{{I}})=\mathbb{O}(\chi^{{II}})
then
\lambda(\chi^{{I}})=\lambda(\chi^{{II}})
and hence the supports of
\chi^{{I}}
and
\chi^{{II}}
have the same cardinality.

Remark 4.3.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=W
or
W_{\geq-1}
. Fix a partition
\lambda=(m_{1},\dots,m_{r})
and consider the set
V^{\lambda}_{\mathfrak{g}}

of all local functions
\chi\in{\mathfrak{g}}^{*}
with
\lambda(\chi)=\lambda
.
Let
\chi=\chi_{1}+\dots+\chi_{r},\nu=\nu_{1}+\dots+\nu_{r}\in V^{\lambda}_{\mathfrak{g}}
, where each
\chi_{i}
(respectively,
\nu_{i}
) is a one-point local function of order
m_{i}-1
.
By Theorem
4.3.1
,
\mathbb{O}(\chi)=\mathbb{O}(\nu)

\iff

\mathbb{O}(\chi_{i})=\mathbb{O}(\nu_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq r
(up to permutation of indices).
Combining Corollary
4.2.11
and Theorem
4.3.1
applied to one-point local functions we have

(a)

if
m_{i}
is odd, then
{\it Loc}^{m_{i}-1}_{\mathfrak{g}}
is a single pseudo-orbit;

(b)

if
m_{i}
is even and
m_{i}\neq 2
then the pseudo-orbits in
{\it Loc}_{\mathfrak{g}}^{m_{i}-1}
are parameterised by
\mathbb{A}^{1}
;

(c)

the pseudo-orbits in
{\it Loc}_{\mathfrak{g}}^{1}
are parameterised by
\mathbb{A}^{1}\setminus\{0\}
.

Thus pseudo-orbits in
V^{\lambda}_{\mathfrak{g}}
(and the corresponding Poisson primitive ideals of
\Sa({\mathfrak{g}})
) are parameterised by a symmetric product
(\mathbb{A}^{1})^{\times(k-k_{2})}\times(\mathbb{A}^{1}\setminus\{0\})^{\times k_{2}}
, where

is the total number of even parts of

and
k_{2}:=|\{i\mid m_{i}=2\}|.

More specifically, let

\mathbb{A}(\lambda):=\begin{cases}\mathbb{A}^{k}&\text{if}~k_{2}=0\\
\mathbb{A}^{k-1}\times(\mathbb{A}^{1}\setminus\{0\})&\text{if}~k_{2}\neq 0\end{cases}.

Let
\mathfrak{S}_{\lambda}=\{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}\ |\ m_{i}=m_{\sigma(i)}\mbox{ for all }i\}
.

The
pseudo-orbits in
V^{\lambda}_{\mathfrak{g}}
are parameterised by

[(\mathbb{A}^{1})^{\times(k-k_{2})}\times(\mathbb{A}^{1}\setminus\{0\})^{\times k_{2}}]/\mathfrak{S}_{\lambda}\cong\mathbb{A}(\lambda).

In Subsection
5.2
we will refine this parameterisation to apply to arbitrary prime Poisson ideals of
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
.

To prove Theorem
4.3.1
we need two preparatory results.

Lemma 4.3.4
.

Let
\chi\in W^{*}
be a local function with
\chi=\chi_{1}+\ldots+\chi_{s}
and
\chi_{i}\in{\it Loc}_{x_{i}}
such that
x_{i}\neq x_{j}
if
i\neq j
. Then
W\cdot\chi=\bigoplus_{i}W\cdot\chi_{i}
.
Likewise, if
\chi\in W_{\geq-1}^{*}

W_{\geq-1}\cdot\chi=\bigoplus_{i}W_{\geq-1}\cdot\chi_{i}
.

Proof.

This statement is basically implied by the Chinese remainder theorem, but we give the details for
W
.
It is easy to verify that
W\cdot\chi=\{B_{\chi}(u,\cdot)\mid u\in W\}

Since

5.
Prime Poisson ideals

In this section we apply our results on Poisson primitive ideals to the structure of arbitrary Poisson prime ideals of
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
, where
\mathfrak{g}
is one of our Lie algebras of interest.
After a preliminary subsection
on birational maps in our context,
we show that the partition data associated to local functions may be used to parameterise Poisson primes of
\Sa(W)
and
\Sa(W_{\geq 1})
.
This will allow us to show that the bijection
\PSpec_{\rm prim}\Sa(W)\to\PSpec_{\rm prim}\Sa(W_{\geq-1})
of Corollary
4.3.11
extends to prime Poisson ideals.
We then investigate when the Poisson analogue of the Dixmier-Moeglin equivalence holds for
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
and, in particular, when Poisson primitive ideals are locally closed in
\PSpec\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
; we will see that this is almost always, but not always, the case.
Finally, we answer a question of León Sánchez and the second author
[
LS
]
on heights of Poisson prime ideals, and in the process show that
\PSpec\Sa(W)
has the somewhat counterintuitive property that every proper radical Poisson ideal contains a proper Poisson primitive ideal.

5.1.
Birational maps

Chevalley’s fundamental result that images of algebraic maps are constructible holds for morphisms of finite presentation
[

, Theorem 10.29.10, Tag 00FE]
and thus applies to any homomorphism from
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
to an affine algebra.
The main result of this subsection is related to Chevalley’s theorem, and allows us to conclude that two domains are birational, even in our non-noetherian context.

Then there is

Without loss of generality

Remark 5.2.7

Let

X^{\prime}=(\psi^{\lambda}_{\mathfrak{g}})^{-1}(Z^{\prime})

By Chevalley’s Theorem

contains a dense subset

As

Let

This is an irreducible closed subvariety of

\widehat{{\it DLoc}}^{\lambda}
-orbits. It therefore follows that

is closed in
\mathbb{A}(\lambda)
. This proves all but the last statement of (a).

We now prove (b).
Fix a partition
\lambda
and let
k:=\len(\lambda^{\rm e})
.
Let
\mathfrak{S}_{\lambda}
acts as in (

).
Let

\mathbb{A}(\lambda)

Let

As the fibres of

Let

be the kernel of
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})\to{\Bbbk}[X]

is prime, and by Proposition
5.2.5

is a Poisson ideal of
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
.
Let

.
By applying Chevalley’s Theorem to the morphism

This shows in particular that

Conversely, given

Given a nonzero proper Poisson prime

\Sa(\mathfrak{g})

For part (c), let

The proof of (b) showed that

∎

Corollary 5.2.14
.

Restriction induces a bijection between prime Poisson

Certainly, if

5.3.
The Poisson Dixmier-Moeglin equivalence

We next consider which Poisson prime ideals satisfy the
Poisson Dixmier-Moeglin equivalence
. This is the Poisson version of the equivalent conditions for primitive ideals in enveloping algebras of finite-dimensional Lie algebras, which are due to Dixmier and Moeglin. We describe the conditions here.

Let
Q
be a Poisson prime ideal in a Poisson algebra
A
, which we assume to be a domain.
Then
Q
is
Poisson locally closed
if it is locally closed in the Zariski topology on
\PSpec(A)
.
We say
Q
is
Poisson rational
if the Poisson centre of the field of fractions of
A/Q
is algebraic over
{\Bbbk}
.
We say the
Poisson Dixmier-Moeglin equivalence (PDME) holds for
A
if for any prime Poisson ideal
Q
of
A
, the conditions

\begin{array}[]{c}\mbox{$Q$ is Poisson locally closed,}\\
\mbox{$Q$ is Poisson primitive,}\\
\mbox{$Q$ is Poisson rational}\end{array}

are equivalent.
If
\mathfrak{g}
is a finite-dimensional Lie algebra, then the PDME holds for
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})

[
LL
, Theorem 2]
.

On the other hand, the next result shows that the PDME fails for
\Sa(V\!ir)
,
\Sa(W)
, and
\Sa(W_{\geq-1})
. However, it
almost
holds: for all these algebras, there is only one Poisson prime for which the PDME fails.

Theorem 5.3.1
.

The Poisson Dixmier-Moeglin equivalence holds for all prime Poisson ideals of
\Sa(V\!ir)
except for
(z)
: that is, if
Q\neq(z)
is a prime Poisson ideal of
\Sa(V\!ir)
then
Q
is locally closed in the Poisson spectrum if and only if
Q
is Poisson primitive, if and only if
Q
is Poisson rational. However,
(z)
is Poisson primitive and Poisson rational but not Poisson locally closed.
Thus the PDME for
\Sa(W)
fails but holds for all prime Poisson ideals except for
(0)
.
Likewise, the PDME for
\Sa(W_{\geq-1})
fails but holds for all prime Poisson ideals except for
(0)
.

Remark 5.3.2
.

By Corollary
3.3.5
, the Poisson spectrum of
\Sa(V\!ir)[z^{-1}]
, which is in natural bijection with the set of prime Poisson ideals of
\Sa(V\!ir)
that do not contain
z
, consists of
(0)
and the ideals
(z-\lambda)
for
\lambda\in{\Bbbk}^{*}
: in other words,
\PSpec(\Sa(V\!ir)[z^{-1}])\cong\mathbb{A}^{1}\setminus\{0\}
.
Therefore, the most interesting structure of
\Sa(V\!ir)
is concentrated above the ideal
(z)
, which Theorem
5.3.1
shows to be pathological in some sense.

To prove Theorem
5.3.1
, we will need notation for the Zariski topology on
\PSpec\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
. If
A
is a Poisson algebra and
N
is a Poisson ideal of
A
, we denote the corresponding closed subset of
\PSpec A

by

V_{P}(N):=\{P\in\PSpec A\ |\ N\subseteq P\}.

We caution the reader that although
V(N)\subseteq\MSpec A
consists of maximal ideals,
V_{P}(N)\subseteq\PSpec A
consists of prime (Poisson) ideals.

Before proving Theorem
5.3.1
, we note that the standard equivalent condition for a Poisson prime to be locally closed also holds in this infinite-dimensional setting.

Lemma 5.3.3
.

Let
A
be a (possibly non-noetherian) Poisson algebra, and let
Q
be a prime Poisson ideal of
A
. Then
Q
is locally closed in
\PSpec A
if and only if there is some
f\in A\setminus Q
so that
(A/Q)[f^{-1}]
is Poisson simple.
∎

We leave the proof to the reader.

Proof of Theorem
5.3.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be any countable-dimensional Lie algebra.
By
[
LS
, Theorem 6.3]
, for prime Poisson ideals of
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
, Poisson locally closed implies Poisson primitive and Poisson primitive is equivalent to Poisson rational. Thus to prove the theorem for
\Sa(V\!ir)
and
\Sa(W)
, it suffices to prove:

(a)

Let
Q
be a prime Poisson ideal of
\Sa(V\!ir)
with
Q\neq(z)
. If
Q
is Poisson primitive, then
Q
is Poisson locally closed.

(b)

The ideal
(z)
is Poisson primitive but not Poisson locally closed.

We first prove (b).
Let
\nu\in V\!ir^{*}
be any non-local function with
\nu(z)=0
(for example, we can take
\nu
to induce the non-local function
\varkappa
on
W
given in Remark
3.0.3
). By Theorem
3.3.1
,
\CoreP(\nu)=(z)
and so
(z)
is Poisson primitive.

To prove that
(z)
is not Poisson locally closed, by Lemma
5.3.3
it is enough to prove that
\Sa(W)[f^{-1}]
is not Poisson simple for any
f\in\Sa(W)\setminus\{0\}
.
Suppose that
\Sa(W)[f^{-1}]
is Poisson simple. Then
f
is contained in all proper Poisson ideals of
\Sa(W)
. Let
\chi\in W^{*}
be a local function. The Poisson core
\CoreP(\chi)
of
\chi
is nontrivial, so
f\in\CoreP(\chi)\subseteq\mathfrak{m}_{\chi}
. In other words, for any local function
\chi\in W^{*}
, we have
\ev_{\chi}(f)=0
, which is ridiculous.

We now prove (a). Let
\chi\in V\!ir^{*}
with
\CoreP(\chi)\neq(z)
. We show that
\CoreP(\chi)
is Poisson locally closed.

The ideals
(z-\zeta)
with
\zeta\neq 0
are maximal in
\PSpec\Sa(V\!ir)
by Corollary
3.3.5
and are thus closed points of
\PSpec\Sa(V\!ir)
.
So we may assume that

\CoreP(\chi)\neq(z-\chi(z))
.
By Theorem
3.3.1

\chi
is therefore local and
d:=\dim V\!ir/V\!ir^{\chi}<\infty
.

Let
I(d-1)
be the Poisson ideal of
\Sa(V\!ir)
defined in Lemma
3.1.4
.
We claim that

V_{P}(\CoreP(\chi))\setminus V_{P}(I(d-1))=\{\CoreP(\chi)\}

so
\CoreP(\chi)
is Poisson locally closed.

First, by (
3.1.5
)
I(d-1)\not\subseteq\mathfrak{m}_{\chi}
so
\CoreP(\chi)\not\in V_{P}(I(d-1))
.
By Proposition
4.3.6

d=\GK\Sa(V\!ir)/\CoreP(\chi)
.
Thus if
\nu\in\overline{\mathbb{O}(\chi)}\setminus\mathbb{O}(\chi)
, we have

d-1\geq\GK\Sa(V\!ir)/\CoreP(\nu)=\dim V\!ir\cdot\nu,

where we have used Proposition
4.3.6
again for the last equality.
Thus
I(d-1)\subseteq\mathfrak{m}_{\nu}
by (
3.1.5
), so
I(d-1)\subseteq\CoreP(\nu)
.
It follows that if
P
is a Poisson prime ideal of
\Sa(V\!ir)
with
P\supsetneqq\CoreP(\chi)
then, writing

P=\bigcap\{\CoreP(\nu)\ |\ \mathfrak{m}_{\nu}\supseteq P\},

we have
P\supseteq I(d-1)
, establishing the claim.

The proof for
\Sa(W_{\geq-1})
is almost identical.
∎

We remark that we have used relatively few pieces of structure theory of
V\!ir
,
W
, and
W_{\geq-1}
in the proof of Theorem
5.3.1
(the inputs are essentially that
\dim\mathbb{O}(\chi)=\dim\mathfrak{g}\cdot\chi
and that
\dim\mathfrak{g}\cdot\chi<\infty
for all
\chi
in which we are interested).
Thus similar results may hold for a wider class of Lie algebras.

5.4.
Radical Poisson ideals contain Poisson primitive ideals

Let
\mathfrak{g}=W
or
W_{\geq-1}
.
From the bijection in Theorem
5.2.8
it is tempting to think of Poisson primitive ideals of
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
as analagous to closed points: by that result, a prime Poisson ideal
Q
is Poisson primitive if and only if
Y(Q)
is a single point.
Note, however, that Theorem
5.3.1
also tells how to distinguish Poisson primitive ideals in
\PSpec\Sa(W)
, and we saw there that in general they are only locally closed.
The next result shows how far most of the Poisson primitive ideals are from being maximal in
\PSpec\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
; see also Corollary
4.3.18
.

Proposition 5.4.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=W
or
W_{\geq-1}
or
W_{\geq 1}
and let
I
be a nonzero radical Poisson ideal of
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
.
Then there is some local function
\nu\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
with
\CoreP(\nu)\subseteq I
.

If
I
is prime and
\mathfrak{g}=W
or
W_{\geq-1}
this may be deduced from the bijection
\Psi
of Subsection
5.2
.
We provide a direct proof, however.

Proof.

Thanks to Theorem
3.0.4
we have that
\GK(\Sa(\mathfrak{g})/I)<\infty
; pick
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
with
2d>\GK(\Sa(\mathfrak{g})/I)
.
By Lemma
2.0.4

I
is an intersection of a family of primitive ideals
\CoreP(\mu)
, and

\GK(\Sa(\mathfrak{g})/\CoreP(\mu))\leq\GK(\Sa(\mathfrak{g})/I)

for each
\mu
with
\mathfrak{m}_{\mu}\supseteq I
.
We will show that there exists
\nu=\nu_{d}\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}

such that if
\mu\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
with

\GK(\Sa(\mathfrak{g})/\CoreP(\mu))<2d,

then
\CoreP(\nu)\subseteq\CoreP(\mu)
.

Pick
\mu\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
with
\dim\mathfrak{g}\cdot\mu=\GK(\Sa(\mathfrak{g})/\CoreP(\mu))<2d
, see Proposition
4.3.6
(b).
Then
\mu
is local and thus is a sum of several (say
\ell
) nonzero one-point
local functions
\mu_{i}\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
with distinct supports
x_{i}
.
Recall that
\dim\mathfrak{g}\cdot\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\dim\mathfrak{g}\cdot\mu_{i}
by Lemma
4.3.4
and therefore
\dim\mathfrak{g}\cdot\mu_{i}<2d
together with
\ell<2d
.
Pick
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that
n\geq 2d
and
\mu_{i}\in{\it Loc}_{x_{i}}^{\leq n}
for all
i
.
Thanks to Lemmata
4.1.3
,
4.1.6
we have
\dim{\it DLoc}_{x_{i}}^{\leq n}\mu_{i}<2d
for all
i
.

Let
\widetilde{\chi}_{i}:=\chi_{\mbox{$x_{i}$};\small{\underbrace{0,0,\ldots,0,}_{2d\text{~times}}}\mbox{1}}
.
Thanks to Theorem
4.2.1
we have
\dim{\it DLoc}_{x_{i}}^{\leq n}\widetilde{\chi}_{i}=2d+1>\dim{\it DLoc}_{x_{i}}^{\leq n}\mu_{i}
.
This together with Corollary
4.2.9
implies that
\CoreP(\widetilde{\chi}_{i})\subseteq\CoreP(\chi_{i})
.

By Corollary
4.3.17
,
\CoreP(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\widetilde{\chi_{i}})\subseteq\CoreP(\mu)
.

Set
\widetilde{\chi_{i}}^{\prime}:=\chi_{\mbox{\it{i}};\underbrace{\scriptsize{0,0,\ldots,0,}}_{2d\text{~times}}\mbox{1}}
.
By Theorem
4.3.1
,
\CoreP(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\widetilde{\chi_{i}})=\CoreP(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\widetilde{\chi_{i}}^{\prime})
and hence, applying Corollary
4.3.17
,
\nu=\sum_{i=1}^{2d}\widetilde{\chi_{i}}^{\prime}
satisfies the desired properties.

By Lemma
2.0.4
,
\CoreP(\nu)\subseteq I
.
∎

The statement of the above corollary can be rephrased as follows: every proper algebraic collection of pseudo-orbits is (strictly) contained in the closure of a single finite-dimensional pseudo-orbit.
If

\mathfrak{g}^{*}
contains a dense coadjoint orbit; such Lie algebras are called Frobenius Lie algebras and there are very few of them.
For infinite-dimensional Lie algebras this statement seems quite counterintuitive.
We expect that only a few Lie algebras satisfy it.

.

Corollary 5.4.2

Let
\mathfrak{g}=W
or

The crucial property of

.
By the proof of Proposition

by induction: let

By (

), each
\CoreP(\nu_{d_{i+1}})\subsetneqq\CoreP(\nu_{d_{i}})
.
∎

By Corollary

, if

then

is the only prime Poisson ideal of

6.
Subalgebras of finite codimension

In this section we sharpen earlier results to classify subalgebras of
V\!ir
of small codimension.
By Proposition
3.3.3
we know any such subalgebra contains
z
, so we may reduce to considering the corresponding subalgebra of
W
; by Proposition
3.2.7
this contains some
W(f)
with
f\neq 0
. We refine these results and provide more precise statements on subalgebras of
V\!ir
and of
W
of codimensions 1, 2, 3.

Throughout this section we assume that
\mathfrak{k}
is a subalgebra of
W
of finite codimension.
Let
f_{\mathfrak{k}}\in{\Bbbk}[t]

be the lowest degree monic polynomial with
\mathfrak{k}\supset W(f)
, which exists by Proposition
3.2.7
.
Proposition
3.2.7
in fact gives us that

(6.0.1)

W(f_{\mathfrak{k}})\subseteq\mathfrak{k}\subseteq W({\rm rad}(f_{\mathfrak{k}})),

where recall that
{\rm rad}(f)=\prod\{(t-x)|f(x)=0\}
.
Thus

(6.0.2)

\codim_{W}\mathfrak{k}\geq\deg{\rm rad}(f_{\mathfrak{k}})=|\{x\in{\Bbbk}^{\times}\mid f_{\mathfrak{k}}(x)=0\}|.

By the Euclidean algorithm,
W(h)\subseteq\mathfrak{k}
if and only if
f_{\mathfrak{k}}\mid h
.

We immediately obtain a classification of subalgebras of codimension 1, a more conceptual proof of a result originally due to Ondrus and Wiesner
[
OW
, Proposition 2.3]
.

Corollary 6.0.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{h}
be a subalgebra of
V\!ir
of codimension 1.
Then there is
x\in{\Bbbk}^{\times}
so that

\mathfrak{h}=(t-x){\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]\partial\oplus{\Bbbk}z.

Proof.

As remarked above, by Proposition
3.3.3

z\in\mathfrak{h}
so it suffices to prove that
\mathfrak{k}:=\mathfrak{h}/(z)
, which is a subalgebra of
W
of codimension 1, is equal to some
W(t-x)
.
By (
6.0.2
)
f_{\mathfrak{k}}
must be equal to some
(t-x)^{a}
and by (
6.0.1
)
\mathfrak{k}\subseteq W(t-x)
.
But
W(t-x)
already has codimension 1.
∎

The problem of classifying general cofinite-dimensional subalgebras of
V\!ir
is equivalent, by Proposition
3.3.3
and (
6.0.1
), to the problem of classifying subalgebras of an arbitrary Lie algebra of the form
W({\rm rad}(f))/W(f)
;
note that
W(f)
is a Lie ideal of
W({\rm rad}(f))
and that
W({\rm rad}(f))/W(f)
is finite-dimensional and nilpotent.
In subsections
6.2
and
6.3
we give a complete classification of Lie subalgebras
\mathfrak{k}\subseteq W
of codimension 2 and 3, illustrating the complexity of the problem.

6.1.
Notation and concepts

We begin by establishing some needed notation.
We call the full list of subalgebras of
W
of codimension 1 the
spectrum
of
W
.
Corollary
6.0.3
implies that every such subalgebra is of the form
W(t-x)
for an appropriate
x\in{\Bbbk}^{\times}
and thus we can identify the spectrum of
W
with
{\Bbbk}^{\times}
.
Pick a subalgebra
\mathfrak{k}
of
W
of finite codimension.
Denote by
\supp(\mathfrak{k})

the list of
x\in{\Bbbk}^{\times}
so that
\mathfrak{k}\subseteq W(t-x)
.
By (
6.0.1
)
\supp(\mathfrak{k})
is nonempty and finite.
The picture is parallel to that of ideals in a commutative
{\Bbbk}
-algebra, where subalgebras of codimension 1 (maximal subalgebras) are the natural analogues of ideals of codimension 1 (maximal ideals).

It will be useful below to carry out a more detailed analysis of subalgebras with
|\supp(\mathfrak{k})|=1
.
We establish notation for various invariants of such
\mathfrak{k}
.

Notation 6.1.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{k}
be a finite codimension subalgebra of
W
with
|\supp(\mathfrak{k})|=1
.
We define the following invariants of
\mathfrak{k}
.

Let
d:=d(\mathfrak{k}):=\codim_{W}\mathfrak{k}
.
As
\supp(\mathfrak{k})=\{x\}
for some
x\in{\Bbbk}^{\times}

we have
f_{\mathfrak{k}}=(t-x)^{a}
for some
a\geq d
.
Let
a(\mathfrak{k}):=a
.
If
a=d
then
\mathfrak{k}=W((t-x)^{d})
.
By definition of
f_{\mathfrak{k}}
, if
a\neq d
then

(6.1.2)

(t-x)^{a-1}\partial\not\in\mathfrak{k}.

Assume that
a\neq d
.
Set
\tilde{t}:=t-x
.
Let
f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{a-d}
be elements of
{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]
so that the images of the
f_{i}\partial
give a basis for
\mathfrak{k}/W(f)
.
We may write each
f_{i}
as
\tilde{t}^{n_{i}}k_{i}
where
k_{i}(x)=1
.
By cancelling leading terms in the Taylor expansion of
f_{i}
around
\tilde{t}=0
we may assume that

1\leq n_{1}<n_{2}<\dots<n_{a-d}<a-1,

where we used (
6.1.2
) for the last inequality.
Write
\{1,\dots,a-1\}\setminus\{n_{1},\dots,n_{a-d}\}=\{g_{1},\dots,g_{d-1}\}
where
g_{1}<g_{2}<\dots
.
We say that

\ldeg(\mathfrak{k}):=\{n_{1}-1,\ldots,n_{a-d}-1\}

are the
leading degrees

of
\mathfrak{k}
. We say that
\sdeg(\mathfrak{k}):=\{g_{1}-1,\ldots,g_{d}-1\}
are
gaps
of
\mathfrak{k}
.

Note that we do not allow
\sdeg(\mathfrak{k})=\{0,\dots,a-2\}
as this would mean that
\ldeg(\mathfrak{k})=\emptyset
, contradicting the assumption that
a(\mathfrak{k})\neq d(\mathfrak{k})
.

Lemma 6.1.3
.

(a)

Let
\mathfrak{k}
satisfy
|\supp(\mathfrak{k})|=1
with
a:=a(\mathfrak{k})>d:=d(\mathfrak{k})
.
Pick
1\leq i\neq j\leq a-d
.
Then either
(n_{i}-1)+(n_{j}-1)\geq(a-1)
or
(n_{i}-1)+(n_{j}-1)\in\ldeg(\mathfrak{k})
.

(b)

Let
S=\{n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell}\}
be a subset of
\{1,\ldots,a-2\}
satisfying the conclusion of (a).
Then there exists a subalgebra
\tilde{\mathfrak{k}}
of
W
with
a(\tilde{\mathfrak{k}})=a
and
\ldeg(\tilde{\mathfrak{k}})=S
.

Proof.

(a). Consider

\tilde{f}_{i,j}\partial:=[f_{i}\partial,f_{j}\partial]=[\tilde{t}^{n_{i}}k_{i}\partial,\tilde{t}^{n_{j}}k_{j}\partial]=(j-i)\tilde{t}^{n_{i}+n_{j}-1}k_{i}k_{j}+\tilde{t}^{n_{i}+n_{j}}(k_{i}k_{j}^{\prime}-k_{i}^{\prime}k_{j})\in\mathfrak{k}.

It is easy to verify that
k_{i,j}=\tilde{f}_{i,j}/\tilde{t}^{n_{i}+n_{j}-1}\in{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]
and
k_{i,j}(x)\neq 0
. This implies (a).

For (b) it is clear that the space
\tilde{\mathfrak{k}}
defined as the span of
W(\tilde{t}^{a})
and
\tilde{t}^{i}\partial
with
i\in S
is a Lie subalgebra of
W
.
∎

The reason for subtracting 1 in the definition of the leading degrees of
\mathfrak{k}
is that
Lemma
6.1.3
shows that
\ldeg(\mathfrak{k})
has the structure of a partial semigroup under addition.

We deduce from Lemma
6.1.3
the following fact.

Lemma 6.1.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{k}
satisfy
|\supp(\mathfrak{k})|=1
with
a:=a(\mathfrak{k})>d:=d(\mathfrak{k})
.
If
g_{1}=1
then
g_{i}\leq 2i-1
for all
i,1\leq i\leq d-1
.
If
g_{1}\neq 1
then
g_{i}\leq 2i+1
for all
i,1\leq i\leq d
.

Proof.

First assume that
g_{1}=1
.
Next, assume to the contrary that
g_{i}=2i-1+\delta
with
\delta>0
.
Consider the list of pairs

(6.1.5)

(2,2i-2+\delta),(3,2i-3+\delta),\ldots,(i,i+\delta).

For each pair
(p,q)
in (
6.1.5
) we have
p+q-1=g_{i}
; further,
Lemma
6.1.3
implies that either
p-1
or
q-1
belongs to
\sdeg(\mathfrak{k})
for each pair
(p,q)
from (
6.1.5
), it must be in
\{g_{2},\dots,g_{i-1}\}
as
g_{1}=1<p,q
.
There are
i-1
pairs in (
6.1.5
) but only
i-2
gaps from
g_{2}
to
g_{i-1}
.
This contradiction completes the case
g_{1}=1
. In the case
g_{1}>1
we have to do the same thing with a minor modification: we have to add
g_{1}
to the list
\{g_{2},\ldots,g_{i-1}\}
.
∎

Remark 6.1.6
.

Note that
g_{d}=a-1\leq 2d+1
and hence one can enumerate all the pairs
(S,a)
satisfying the conclusion of condition (a) of Lemma
6.1.3
for a given codimension
d
.
We believe that such pairs
(S,a)
are in bijection with
irreducible components of the moduli space of subalgebras
\mathfrak{k}
of codimension
d
with
|\supp(\mathfrak{k})|=1
.

6.2.
Subalgebras of codimension 2

The goal of this subsection is to show that all subalgebras of
W
of codimension 2 are listed in the following table.

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr\vrule\lx@intercol\hfil\mbox{Subalgebras of $W$ of codimension 2}\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@intercol\\
\hline\cr\rm{Code}&\sdeg(\mathfrak{k})&f_{\mathfrak{k}}&\begin{tabular}[]{c}Additional\\
generators\end{tabular}\\
\hline\cr W((t-x)(t-y))&-&(t-x)(t-y)&-\\
\hline\cr W_{x;\alpha}^{2;1}&1&(t-x)^{3}&\begin{array}[]{c}(t-x)\partial+\alpha(t-x)^{2}\partial\end{array}\\
\hline\cr W_{x;\alpha}^{2;2}&2&(t-x)^{4}&\begin{array}[]{c}(t-x)\partial+\alpha(t-x)^{3}\partial,\\
(t-x)^{2}\partial\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

Here
x,y,\alpha
are parameters taking values in
{\Bbbk}
with
x,y\neq 0
.

The proof consists of the following two statements.

Proposition 6.2.1
.

(a)

If
\codim_{W}\mathfrak{k}=2
then
|\supp(\mathfrak{k})|\in\{1,2\}
.

(b)

If
|\supp(\mathfrak{k})|=2
then
\mathfrak{k}=W((t-x)(t-y))
and
\supp(\mathfrak{k})=\{x,y\}
for some
x\neq y\in{\Bbbk}^{\times}
.

Proposition 6.2.2
.

(a)

If
\codim_{W}\mathfrak{k}=2
and
|\supp(\mathfrak{k})|=1
(so
\supp(\mathfrak{k})=\{x\}
for some
x\in{\Bbbk}^{\times}
) then either
\mathfrak{k}=W((t-x)^{2})
or
\sdeg(\mathfrak{k})
is
\{1\}
or
\{2\}
.

(b)

If
\sdeg(\mathfrak{k})=\{1\}
then
\mathfrak{k}=W_{x;\alpha}^{2;1}
for a unique
\alpha\in{\Bbbk}
.

(c)

If
\sdeg(\mathfrak{k})=\{2\}
then
\mathfrak{k}=W_{x;\alpha}^{2;2}
for a unique
\alpha\in{\Bbbk}
.

Proof of Proposition
6.2.1
.

Theorem
3.2.7
implies
|\supp(\mathfrak{k})|\geq 1
, and (
6.0.2
) implies that
|\supp(\mathfrak{k})|\leq 2
. This proves (a).

Assume
\supp(\mathfrak{k})=\{x,y\}
for some distinct
x,y\in{\Bbbk}^{\times}
.
We have
\mathfrak{k}\subseteq W((t-x)(t-y))
and

\codim_{W}W((t-x)(t-y))=\codim_{W}\mathfrak{k}=2.

Hence
\mathfrak{k}=W((t-x)(t-y))
and (b) is complete.
∎

Proof of Proposition
6.2.2
.

We may assume that
\mathfrak{k}\neq W((t-x)^{2})
.
Adopt the terminology of Notation
6.1.1
.
We have

|\sdeg(\mathfrak{k})|=\codim(\mathfrak{k})-1
and hence
|\sdeg(\mathfrak{k})|=\{g_{1}-1\}
for a certain positive integer
g_{1}
.
Note that
g_{1}=a(\mathfrak{k})-1
by (
6.1.2
).
Thanks to Lemma
6.1.3
we have
g_{1}\leq 3
.
This proves (a).

Note that
g_{1}\neq 1
as by convention
\sdeg(\mathfrak{k})
cannot be equal to
\{0,\dots,a-2\}
.
Assume
g_{1}=2
, so

a(\mathfrak{k})=3
.
Recall that
\tilde{t}=t-x
.
Let
f_{1}=\tilde{t}k_{1}
be as in Notation
6.1.1
; as
a(\mathfrak{k})-d(\mathfrak{k})=1
,
the image of
f_{1}\partial
in
\mathfrak{k}/W(\tilde{t}^{3})
provides a generator of this one-dimensional vector space.
We can assume that
f_{1}
is monic in
\tilde{t}
and contains no terms in
\tilde{t}
of degree
3
or more.
Thus
f_{1}=\tilde{t}+\alpha\tilde{t}^{2}
for some
\alpha\in{\Bbbk}
and

\mathfrak{k}=W_{x;\alpha}^{2;1}
. Further,
\alpha
is unique as
\dim\mathfrak{k}/W(\tilde{t}^{3})=1
.

Assume
g_{1}=3
, so
a(\mathfrak{k})=4
.
Let
f_{1}=\tilde{t}k_{1},f_{2}=\tilde{t}^{2}k_{2}\in{\Bbbk}[t]={\Bbbk}[\tilde{t}]
be
as in Notation
6.1.1
, so the images of
f_{1}\partial,f_{2}\partial
in
\mathfrak{k}/W((t-x)^{3})
give a basis.
We can assume that
f_{1},f_{2}
contain no terms in
\tilde{t}
of degree
4
or more and
f_{1}
contains no terms in
\tilde{t}
of degree
2
.
Thus
f_{1}=\tilde{t}+\alpha\tilde{t}^{3},f_{2}=\tilde{t}^{2}+\beta\tilde{t}^{3}
for some
\alpha,\beta\in{\Bbbk}
.
We have

\mathfrak{k}\ni[f_{1}\partial,f_{2}\partial]=\tilde{t}^{2}\partial+2\beta\tilde{t}^{3}\partial\mod W(\tilde{t}^{4}).

Hence
[f_{1}\partial,f_{2}\partial]=f_{2}\partial
modulo
W(\tilde{t}^{4})
.
This implies
\beta=0
and therefore
\mathfrak{k}=W_{x;\alpha}^{2;2}
. Again
\alpha
is unique.
∎

Remark 6.2.3
.

Similar proofs give a classification of subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
of codimension 2: these are either of the form

W_{\geq-1}((t-x)(t-y))
or may be written
(W_{\geq-1})^{2;1}_{x;\alpha}
or
(W_{\geq-1})^{2;2}_{x;\alpha}
, where these last two are defined similarly to the analogous subalgebras of
W
.
(Here of course we allow
x,y
to be
0
.)
These subalgebras are all deformations of
W_{\geq-1}(t^{2})=W_{\geq 1}
.

Deformations of
W_{\geq 1}
are classified in
[
F
]
(see also
[
FF
]
).
These papers show that up to isomorphism there are three such deformations, denoted in
[
F
]
by
L_{1}^{(1)}
,
L_{1}^{(2)}
,
L_{1}^{(3)}
.
It can be shown that

L_{1}^{(1)}\cong W(t(t-y)),\quad L_{1}^{(2)}\cong(W_{\geq-1})^{2;1}_{0;\alpha},\quad L_{1}^{(3)}\cong(W_{\geq-1})^{2;2}_{0;\alpha}

for appropriate
y,\alpha
.
We thank Lucas Buzaglo for explaining this to us.

6.3.
Subalgebras of codimension 3

It can also be shown that all subalgebras of
W
of codimension 3 are listed in the following table.
Because the methods are similar to those in Subsection
6.2
we omit the proof.

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr\vrule\lx@intercol\hfil\mbox{Subalgebras of $W$ of codimension 3}\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@intercol\\
\hline\cr{\rm Code}&\sdeg(\mathfrak{k})&f_{\mathfrak{k}}&\begin{tabular}[]{c}Additional generators\\
or description\end{tabular}\\
\hline\cr W(f_{\mathfrak{k}})&-&(t-x)(t-y)(t-z)&-\\
\hline\cr W_{x,y;\alpha,\beta}^{3A}&-&(t-x)^{2}(t-y)^{2}&\begin{array}[]{c}(t-x)(t-y)(\alpha t+\beta)\partial,\\
\alpha x+\beta,\alpha y+\beta\neq 0,x\neq y\end{array}\\
\hline\cr W_{x,y;\alpha}^{3B1}&-&(t-x)^{3}(t-y)&\begin{array}[]{c}W^{2;1}_{x;\alpha}\cap W(t-y),\\
x\neq y\end{array}\\
\hline\cr W_{x,y;\alpha}^{3B2}&-&(t-x)^{4}(t-y)&\begin{array}[]{c}W^{2;2}_{x;\alpha}\cap W(t-y),\\
x\neq y\end{array}\\
\hline\cr W_{x;\alpha}^{3C1}&0,2&(t-x)^{4}&(t-x)^{2}\partial+\alpha(t-x)^{3}\partial\\
\hline\cr W_{x;\alpha,\beta}^{3C2}&1,2&(t-x)^{4}&(t-x)\partial+\alpha(t-x)^{2}\partial+\beta(t-x)^{3}\partial\\
\hline\cr W_{x;\alpha,\beta}^{3C3}&1,3&(t-x)^{5}&\begin{array}[]{c}(t-x)\partial+\alpha(t-x)^{2}\partial+\beta(t-x)^{4}\partial,\\
(t-x)^{3}\partial-\alpha(t-x)^{4}\partial\end{array}\\
\hline\cr W_{x;\alpha,\beta}^{3C4}&1,4&(t-x)^{6}&\begin{array}[]{c}(t-x)\partial+\alpha(t-x)^{2}\partial+\beta(t-x)^{5}\partial,\\
(t-x)^{3}\partial-\alpha^{2}(t-x)^{5}\partial,\\
(t-x)^{4}\partial-2\alpha(t-x)^{5}\partial\\
\end{array}\\
\hline\cr W_{x;\alpha,\beta}^{3C5}&2,3&(t-x)^{5}&\begin{array}[]{c}(t-x)\partial+\alpha(t-x)^{3}\partial+\beta(t-x)^{4}\partial,\\
(t-x)^{2}\partial+\frac{\alpha}{2}(t-x)^{4}\partial\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

The notation here is as follows:

\bullet

\mathfrak{k}
is given in the final column as either an intersection of two explicitly given subalgebras or is spanned by
W(f_{\mathfrak{k}})
and a few more explicit generators;

\bullet

\alpha,\beta
are parameters taking arbitrary values in
{\Bbbk}
for all cases but
W^{3A}_{x,y;\alpha,\beta}
(for this case the required restrictions on
\alpha,\beta
are given in the table);

\bullet

x,y
are parameters taking arbitrary values in
{\Bbbk}^{\times}
except when restrictions are given in the table.

It is easy to verify that a subalgebra
\mathfrak{k}
of
W
of codimension 3 belongs to only one type and moreover the parameters defining
\mathfrak{k}
are unique if
\mathfrak{k}\neq W_{x,y;\alpha,\beta}^{3A}
and unique up to scaling
(\alpha,\beta)\to(\lambda\alpha,\lambda\beta)
in the case
\mathfrak{k}=W_{x,y;\alpha,\beta}^{3A}
.

Remark 6.3.1
.

(1)
Recall the analogy between subalgebras of
W
of codimension 1 and maximal ideals. It is natural to ask whether or not an analogue of the Lasker-Noether primary decomposition theorem holds in this setting. However, this statement fails as we can easily see that
the Lie algebra
W^{3A}_{x,y;\alpha,\beta}
is not an intersection of subalgebras
\mathfrak{k}_{x}
and
\mathfrak{k}_{y}
with
\supp(\mathfrak{k}_{x})=\{x\}
and
\supp(\mathfrak{k}_{y})=\{y\}
.

(2)

Recall that every subalgebra
\mathfrak{k}
of finite codimension in
W
lies between
W(f_{\mathfrak{k}})
and
W({\rm rad}(f_{\mathfrak{k}}))
.
One can construct a sequence of polynomials

h_{0}={\rm rad}f_{\mathfrak{k}},h_{1},h_{2},\ldots,h_{s}=f_{\mathfrak{k}}

with
\deg h_{i+1}=\deg h_{i}+1
and
h_{i}\mid h_{i+1}
and consider the filtration of
W({\rm rad}f_{\mathfrak{k}})
by the Lie ideals
W(h_{i})
.
The associated graded algebra

\oplus_{i\geq 0}([W(h_{i})\cap\mathfrak{k}]/[W(h_{i+1})\cap\mathfrak{k}])

is isomorphic to a graded algebra
\mathord{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\teni$\mathfrak{k}$\cr\tenrm\char 126\relax\cr}}}

with

W(h_{0})\supseteq\mathord{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\teni$\mathfrak{k}$\cr\tenrm\char 126\relax\cr}}}\supseteq W(f_{\mathfrak{k}})
,
\codim_{W}\mathfrak{k}=\codim_{W}\mathord{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\teni$\mathfrak{k}$\cr\tenrm\char 126\relax\cr}}}
and
f_{\mathfrak{k}}=f_{\mathord{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\teni$\mathfrak{k}$\cr\tenrm\char 126\relax\cr}}}}
.

The subalgebras
\mathord{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\teni$\mathfrak{k}$\cr\tenrm\char 126\relax\cr}}}
can be described in purely combinatorial terms and thus they give a collection of discrete invariants for
\mathfrak{k}
.
This generalises the notation of gaps and leading degrees for subalgebras with one-point support.

6.4.
Some general comments on finite codimension subalgebras of
W

The lattice of subalgebras
\mathfrak{k}
with
\supp(\mathfrak{k})=\{x\}
and
a(\mathfrak{k})\leq a
can be naturally identified with the subalgebras of

W(t-x)/W((t-x)^{a}))\cong W(t)/W(t^{a});

in particular the isomorphism class of this lattice is independent of
x
.

A similar result holds true in a greater generality.
Pick
s\geq 0,a\geq 1
and distinct
x_{1},\ldots,x_{s}\in{\Bbbk}^{\times}
; set
h:=(t-x_{1})\ldots(t-x_{s})
.
Consider subalgebras
\mathfrak{k}
satisfying
W(h)\supset\mathfrak{k}\supset W(h^{a})
.
It is clear that
W(h^{a})
is a Lie ideal of
W(h)
and the quotient
W(h)/W(h^{a})
is a finite-dimensional solvable Lie algebra.
A version of the Chinese remainder theorem implies that

(6.4.1)

W(h)/W(h^{a})\cong\oplus_{i}W(t-x_{i})/W((t-x_{i})^{a})\cong[W(t)/W(t^{a})]^{\oplus s}.

In particular, the isomorphism class of
W(h)/W(h^{a})
depends only on
a
and
s
but not on the particular choice of the
x_{i}
.
This immediately gives the following corollaries.

Corollary 6.4.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{k}
be a subalgebra of finite codimension of
\mathfrak{g}=W,W_{\geq-1},W_{\geq 1}
or
V\!ir
. Then there are subalgebras
\mathfrak{k}^{+},\mathfrak{k}^{-}
with

\mathfrak{k}^{-}\subseteq\mathfrak{k}\subseteq\mathfrak{k}^{+}\mathrm{~and~}\codim_{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathfrak{k}^{-})+1=\codim_{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathfrak{k})=\codim_{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathfrak{k}^{+})-1.

Proof.

A similar statement is well-known for subalgebras of solvable Lie algebras so (
6.4.1
) implies the desired result if
\mathfrak{k}\neq W(f_{\mathfrak{k}})
.
If
\mathfrak{k}=W(f_{\mathfrak{k}})
the result follows from a similar fact on ideals in
{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]
.∎

Corollary 6.4.3
.

The lattices of subalgebras of finite codimension of
V\!ir
, of
W
, and of
W_{\geq-1}
are all isomorphic.

Proof.

Lemma
3.3.3
implies that the lattices of subalgebras of finite codimension are isomorphic for
W
and
V\!ir
; hence we left to show that the lattices are isomorphic for
W
and
W_{\geq-1}
.

Theorem
3.2.7
implies that the lattices of subalgebras of finite codimension for both
W
and
W_{\geq-1}
are direct limits of the sublattices of subalgebras
\mathfrak{k}
containing
W((t-x_{1})^{a}\cdots(t-x_{s})^{a})
for all tuples
(a;x_{1},\ldots,x_{s})
; the only difference between
W
and
W_{\geq-1}
here is that in the first case
x_{i}\neq 0
.
These lattices are isomorphic for
W,W_{\geq-1}
and the embeddings between them are the same for
W
and
W_{\geq-1}
(they essentially depend only on the integer-valued parameters
s
and
a
and on the cardinality of
{\Bbbk}
).
∎

7.
Implications of our results for
\Ua(\mathfrak{g})

In this final section, we shift, for the first time in this paper, to considering the universal enveloping algebra
\Ua(\mathfrak{g})
of one of our Lie algebras of interest.
We
apply a version of the orbit method to relate
the Poisson primitive ideals

\ker p_{\gamma}=\CoreP(\chi_{x;\alpha,\gamma})
of
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
to primitive ideals of
\Ua(W)
obtained as kernels of maps to the (localised) Weyl algebra.
We end with some conjectures about ideals in
\Ua(V\!ir)
,
\Ua(W)
, and
\Ua(W_{\geq-1})
.

7.1.
Constructing primitive ideals through the orbit method

Kirillov’s orbit method for nilpotent and solvable Lie algebras attaches to
\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
the annihilator in
\Ua(\mathfrak{g})
of the module induced from a polarization of
\chi
and the induced character.
We apply the same construction to
\mathfrak{g}=W
and
\chi=\chi_{x;\alpha,\gamma}
.
We will denote the corresponding induced
W
-modules by
M_{x;\gamma}
.

A description of the annihilators of
M_{x,\gamma}
is given in Proposition
7.1.5
.
The main result here is that

\Ann_{\Ua(W)}M_{x;\gamma}

depends only on
\gamma
; thanks to Theorem
4.2.1
(or Lemma
2.2.3
) the same holds for
\CoreP(\chi_{x;\alpha;\gamma})
.
This shows that the constructions of Kirillov’s orbit method give rise to a map from a certain class of Poisson primitive ideals of
\Sa(W)
to a certain class of primitive ideals of
\Ua(W)
, which are known in the literature
[
CM
,
SW
]
as kernels of maps to the localised Weyl algebra.
We believe this map extends to a surjection from Poisson primitive ideals of
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
to primitive ideals of
\Ua(\mathfrak{g})
; this is the subject of ongoing research.

Throughout this section, we write the localised Weyl algebra as
A={\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1},\partial]
, with
\partial t=t\partial+1
.

We first describe a polarization for
\chi_{x;\alpha,\gamma}
.
Let
x,\alpha,\gamma\in{\Bbbk}
with
x\neq 0,(\alpha,\gamma)\neq(0,0)
and let
\chi=\chi_{x;\alpha,\gamma}
. Recall the computation of
W^{\chi}
in Lemma
2.2.1
, and consider the Lie subalgebra
W(t-x)
of
W
, which contains
W^{\chi}
.
We have

\dim W/W^{\chi}=2\implies W(t-x)={\Bbbk}(t-x)\partial\oplus W^{\chi}.

As
B_{\chi}((t-x)\partial,(t-x)\partial)=0
, thus
W(t-x)
is a totally isotropic subspace of
(W,B_{\chi})
; by dimension count it is maximal totally isotropic.
Thus
W(t-x)
is a
polarization
of
W
at
\chi
, as in
[
Dix
, 1.12.8]
.
Further,
W(t-x)
is the unique polarization of
W
at
\chi
: since any polarization of
W
at
\chi
must be a codimension 1 subalgebra of
W
, by Corollary
6.0.3
it must be equal to some
W(t-y)
and it is easy to see that we must have
y=x
.

Note that
\chi
is a character of
W(t-x)
; let

{\Bbbk}m_{x;\gamma}
be the corresponding 1-dimensional representation of
W(t-x)
, with basis element
m_{x;\gamma}
.
(The restriction
\chi|_{W(t-x)}
, which sends
p\partial\mapsto\gamma p^{\prime}(x)
, depends only on
x
and
\gamma
— this is the reason to omit
\alpha
in the notation
{\Bbbk}m_{x;\gamma}
. )
Put

M_{x;\gamma}:=\Ua(W)\otimes_{\Ua(W(t-x))}{\Bbbk}m_{x;\gamma}.

Since
e_{-1}=\partial\not\in W(t-x)
, thus
W={\Bbbk}e_{-1}\oplus W(t-x)
and by the Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt theorem the set
\{e_{-1}^{k}m_{x;\gamma}|k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\}
is a basis for
M_{x;\gamma}
.

We now give an alternative construction of
M_{x;\gamma}
. Set

N_{x}:={\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1},(t-x)^{-1}]/{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}].

For every
x
,
N_{x}
is a simple (faithful) left
A
-module.

Remark 7.1.1
.

The space
N_{x}
can be thought of as a space of distributions on
{\Bbbk}^{\times}
; for, setting
\delta_{x}=(t-x)^{-1}\in N_{x}
we have
(t-x)\delta_{x}=0
so
\delta_{x}
behaves like a
\delta
-function at
x
. The elements
\partial^{k}\delta_{x}
form a basis of
N_{x}
.

Recall that, for any
\gamma\in{\Bbbk}
, the map

(7.1.2)

\pi_{\gamma}:W\to A,\quad f\partial\mapsto f\partial+\gamma f^{\prime}

is a Lie algebra homomorphism; see
[
CM
]
.
Thus
\pi_{\gamma}
extends to define a ring homomorphism
\Ua(W)\to A
.
Note that the map
p_{\gamma}
defined in (
2.2.2
) is
not
the associated graded map attached to
\pi_{\gamma}
even though they are clearly analogous.

The images of
\pi_{\gamma}
have been computed in
[
CM
, Lemma 2.1]
, and we give them here.

Lemma 7.1.3
.

We have

(a)

\im(\pi_{0})={\Bbbk}\oplus A\partial
, and
\im\pi_{1}={\Bbbk}\oplus\partial A
.

(b)

\im(\pi_{\gamma})=A
if
\gamma\neq 0,1
.

Remark 7.1.4
.

The restriction of
\pi_{\gamma}
to
\Ua(W_{\geq 1})
was considered, under slightly different notation, in
[
SW
]
; see
[
SW
, Remark 3.14]
. It was shown there that the ideal
\ker\pi_{0}|_{W_{\geq 1}}=\ker\pi_{1}|_{W_{\geq 1}}
is not finitely generated as a left or right ideal of
\Ua(W_{\geq 1})
.

For every
\gamma\in{\Bbbk}
the map
\pi_{\gamma}
from (
7.1.2
) induces the structure of a
W
-module on
N_{x}
; we denote the space
N_{x}
with the corresponding
W
-module structure
\cdot_{\gamma}
by
N_{x}^{\gamma}
.

Proposition 7.1.5
.

Let
x\neq 0,\alpha,\gamma\in{\Bbbk}
.
Then
N_{x}^{\gamma}\cong M_{x;\gamma-1}
.
Moreover,
N_{x}^{\gamma}
is a simple
W
-module if and only if
\gamma\neq 1
.
There is an exact sequence

0\to N_{x}^{0}\to N_{x}^{1}\to{\Bbbk}\to 0.

For all
\gamma
the annihilator of
N_{x}^{\gamma}
is equal to
\ker\pi_{\gamma}
, which is primitive.

Proof.

Let
p\in{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]
with
(t-x)|p
. By taking the Taylor expansion of
p
about
x
one may verify that

(7.1.6)

(p\partial)\cdot_{\gamma}\delta_{x}=(\gamma-1)p^{\prime}(x)\delta_{x}.

This immediately implies that there exists a surjective module homomorphism
M_{x;\gamma-1}\to N_{x}^{\gamma}
which sends
m_{x;\gamma-1}\mapsto\delta_{x}
.
The basis element
e_{-1}^{k}m_{x;\gamma-1}
maps to
\partial^{k}\delta_{x}
, so this map is an isomorphism.

Assume
\gamma\neq 0,1
.
In this case
\pi_{\gamma}
is surjective by Lemma
7.1.3
, so simplicity of
N_{x}^{\gamma}
follows from simplicity of
N_{x}
as an
A
-module.

We claim that
N_{x}^{0}
is simple. To see this, let
0\neq n\in N_{x}
, which we recall is a simple
A
-module.
By construction of
N_{x}
there is some
0\neq f(t)\in{\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]
so that
fn=0
, and thus
\partial n\neq 0
as
A\partial+Af(t)=A
.
Thus
A\partial n=N_{x}
, proving the claim since by Lemma
7.1.3

A\partial\subseteq\pi_{0}(\Ua(W))
.

Finally, we consider
N_{x}^{1}
.
Lemma
7.1.3
implies
\partial A\triangleleft\im\pi_{1}={\Bbbk}\oplus\partial A
, and thus
\tilde{N}:=\partial AN_{x}=\partial N_{x}
is a submodule of
N_{x}^{1}
, with
N_{x}^{1}/\tilde{N}\cong{\Bbbk}
.
We claim that
\tilde{N}
is simple. Let
0\neq n\in\tilde{N}
.
As
N_{x}
is a simple
A
-module,
\partial An=\tilde{N}
and so
\Ua(W)\cdot_{1}n=({\Bbbk}\oplus\partial A)n=\tilde{N}
, as needed. The reader may verify that as in (
7.1.6
)

p\partial\cdot_{1}\partial\delta_{x}=-p^{\prime}(x)\partial\delta_{x},

and so
\tilde{N}\cong M_{x;-1}\cong N_{x}^{0}
.
The claim about annihilators follows from the fact that
N_{x}
is a faithful module over the (simple) ring
A
.
That
\ker\pi_{\gamma}
is primitive is immediate for
\gamma\neq 1
; for
\gamma=1
it follows from the fact that
\ker\pi_{1}=\ker\pi_{0}
.
∎

Remark 7.1.7
.

(a)

Note that the primitive ideal
\ker\pi_{\gamma}
is completely prime.
We do not know of a primitive (or prime) ideal of
\Ua(W)
which is not completely prime.

(b)

We believe that the ideals
\ker\pi_{\gamma}
above are all of the primitive ideals of
\Ua(W)
of Gelfand-Kirillov codimension 2.

(c)

By Remark
7.1.4
,
\ker\pi_{0}=\ker\pi_{1}
. However,
\ker p_{0}\neq\ker p_{1}
.
To see this, note that if
\ker p_{0}=\ker p_{1}
then
\chi_{1;1,0}\in V(\ker p_{1})=\overline{\mathbb{O}(\chi_{1;1,1})}
.
Thus either
\mathbb{O}(\chi_{1;1,0})=\mathbb{O}(\chi_{1;1,1})
or
\dim\mathbb{O}(\chi_{1;1,0})<\dim\mathbb{O}(\chi_{1;1,1})
. Neither is true.

Thus the orbit method does not give a bijection from Poisson primitive ideals of
\Sa(W)
to primitive ideals of
\Ua(W)
.

7.2.
Conjectures for
\Ua(\mathfrak{g})

We have focused almost entirely on the symmetric algebra of
\mathfrak{g}
, where
\mathfrak{g}
is one of
V\!ir
,
W
, or several related Lie algebras.
However, our results are at minimum suggestive for the enveloping algebras of these Lie algebras.
In this final subsection, we make several conjectures for
\Ua(\mathfrak{g})
.
Broadly speaking, these are instances of the meta-conjecture:

The ideal structure of
\Ua(\mathfrak{g})
is closely analogous to the Poisson structure of
\Sa(\mathfrak{g})
.

For each conjecture, we give the Poisson result which suggested it to us.

Conjecture 7.2.1

(cf. Corollary
3.3.5
)
.

If
\zeta\neq 0
then
\Ua(V\!ir)/(z-\zeta)
is simple.

Conjecture 7.2.2

(cf. Corollaries
4.3.11
,
5.2.14
)
.

Restriction gives a bijection between primitive (respectively, prime) ideals of
\Ua(W)
and
\Ua(W_{\geq-1})
, and a homeomorphism
{\rm PSpec}_{\rm prim}\Ua(W)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}{\rm PSpec}_{\rm prim}\Ua(W_{\geq-1})
.

Conjecture 7.2.3

(cf. Proposition
5.4.1
)
.

Every proper prime ideal of
\Ua(W)
contains a proper primitive ideal.

Conjecture 7.2.4

(cf. Proposition
4.3.14
)
.

The
\Ker\pi_{\gamma}
are all of the prime ideals of
\Ua(W)
of co-GK-dimension 2.

Conjecture 7.2.5

(cf. Subsection
7.1
)
.

Kirillov’s orbit method, i.e. the assignment

a local function
\chi\to
a polarization of
\chi\to
the annihilator of the induced module

always produces a primitive ideal, is independent of polarization, and depends only on
\mathbb{O}(\chi)
. There is thus an induced map
{\rm PSpec}_{prim}\Sa(\mathfrak{g})\to\Spec_{\rm prim}\Ua(\mathfrak{g})
for
\mathfrak{g}=V\!ir,W,W_{\geq-1}
.
This map is surjective onto
\Spec_{\rm prim}\Ua(\mathfrak{g})
.

These conjectures are the subject of ongoing research.
