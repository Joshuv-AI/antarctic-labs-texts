The standard lens cluster in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} uniquely minimizes relative perimeter
Stan Alama, Lia Bronsard, Silas Vriend

Abstract
In this article we consider the isoperimetric problem for partitioning the plane into three disjoint domains, one having unit area and the remaining two having infinite area. We show that the only solution, up to rigid motions of the plane, is a lens cluster consisting of circular arcs containing the finite area region, attached to a single axis, with two triple junctions where the arcs meet at 120 degree angles. In particular, we show that such a configuration is a local minimizer of the total perimeter functional, and on the other hand any local minimizer of perimeter among clusters with the given area constraints must coincide with a lens cluster having this geometry. Some known results and conjectures on similar problems with both finite and infinite area constraints are presented at the conclusion.

Introduction

In a previous paper
[
4
]
we have conjectured that the minimal cluster for the planar partitioning problem with one unit area chamber and two improper chambers (i.e. of infinite area) is the standard lens cluster depicted in Figure
1
. The boundary set of this cluster consists of two collinear rays emanating from a pair of standard triple junctions which are joined by a pair of symmetric circular arcs, meeting at 120 degree angles. The center corresponding to each circular arc lies on the midpoint of the opposite circular arc, and these conditions uniquely determine the geometry of the lens, also known as a
vesica piscis
(or fish’s bladder.) In this paper we show that this configuration is the unique solution to the partitioning problem, in the sense that any other configuration which minimizes perimeter locally (i.e. among compactly supported variations preserving the area of the proper chamber) must be congruent to the unit area lens cluster.

Figure 1:
The standard lens cluster in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

All partitioning problems are extensions and generalizations of the classical isoperimetric problem: finding the shape of given area (or Lebesgue measure, in the general case) whose boundary has the smallest possible perimeter. More recently attention has turned to partitioning problems in which the plane (or space) is subdivided into several disjoint but complementary domains, each with given measure. This gave rise to the famous Double-Bubble Conjecture, to determine the optimal shape of a pair of planar regions with given measure whose combined perimeter is minimized. The solution is a double-bubble composed of arcs of circles (spheres, in higher dimensions), meeting at 120 degree angles at the junction points
[
15
]
; this was proven by a group of undergraduate students at Williams in
[
7
]
. An analogous question may be posed in
n
dimensions, and other generalizations include partitions with any number of chambers of (finite) prescribed measure. The proof that double-bubbles, domains composed of spherical caps meeting at 120 angles, minimize surface area in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with two given volumes, was given by Hutchings, Morgan, Ritoré, and Ros
[
8
]
, and generalized to any dimension by Reichardt
[
16
]
.
The case of triple-bubbles, with three prescribed areas in the plane, was handled by Wichiramala
[
20
]
, but the complexity of the method has limited progress on multiple-bubble problems with more than three given areas.

Multiple bubble configurations also arise in certain models of block copolymers: nonlocal variational problems for multiphase materials exhibiting short range attractive forces but longer range repulsion between different phases. In an appropriate limit (described by Gamma-convergence) minimizers of a ternary model exhibit double-bubble configurations
[
17
,
1
]
, while a quaternary model (with four phases) produces triple-bubble minimizers
[
3
]
.

It is in connection with the triblock copolymer models that we encountered the lens cluster problem treated in this paper. If one considers a partitioning problem in the torus, with three phases, two of which occupy nearly all of the total area with the third accounting for only a tiny fraction, we expect minimizers of the nonlocal triblock copolymer energy to form a lamellar pattern with the two majority phases, with tiny droplets of the third phase aligned on each lamellar stripe. By blowing up the droplets in the limit of vanishing area of the third phase we expect to recover the lens shape in the plane, and the characterization of the
vesica piscis
as the unique minimizer (up to symmetries) of this problem will be critical to the analysis of the small-area limit of the triblock problem. (See
[
2
]
.)

Isoperimetric minimizers also arise as stationary or asymptotic solutions to geometrical flows.
In work of Bellettini-Novaga
[
6
]
and Schnürer et al.
[
18
]
, the lens configuration appears as the limiting configuration of a planar network with two triple junctions under curve-shortening flow.

Clusters

In studying the partitioning problem we adopt the notation and vocabulary of
clusters
as developed in Maggi
[
12
]
. Unlike the clusters defined in
[
12
]
, our configurations will have two chambers with infinite measure, and so we introduce a definition which extends the framework of clusters to the more general setting in which there may be several chambers with infinite measure.

Let
\mathbb{N}_{0}
denote the set
\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. For greater generality, we set the definitions to follow in dimension
n\geq 2
. Let
\absolutevalue{E}
denote the Lebesgue measure of a set
E
, let
\Haus^{k}
denote the
k
-dimensional Hausdorff measure, and let
P(E)
denote the perimeter of
E
.

Definition 1.1
.

An
(N,M)
-cluster

\mathcal{X}=(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a pair of finite families of sets of
locally
finite perimeter

\mathcal{E}=\{\mathcal{E}(h)\}_{h=1}^{N},\quad\mathcal{F}=\{\mathcal{F}(i)\}_{i=1}^{M},\quad N,M\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

which satisfies the following list of properties:

(i)

Proper chambers:
the sets
\mathcal{E}(h)
,
1\leq h\leq N
, satisfy
0<\absolutevalue{\E(h)}<\infty
.

(ii)

Improper chambers:
the sets
\mathcal{F}(i)
,
1\leq i\leq M
, satisfy
\absolutevalue{\F(i)}=\infty
.

(iii)

Null overlap:
the sets
\mathcal{E}(h)
and
\mathcal{F}(i)
have pairwise Lebesgue null overlap, that is,

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\E(h) \cap\E(k)}=0,

\displaystyle\quad 1\leq h<k\leq N,

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\E(h) \cap\F(i)}=0,

\displaystyle\quad 1\leq h\leq N,\;1\leq i\leq M,

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\F(i) \cap\F(j)}=0,

\displaystyle\quad 1\leq i<j\leq M.

(iv)

Full measure:
following
[
12
]
Section 30.3], we have

\absolutevalue{\R^n \setminus\left(\bigcup_{h = 1}^{N} \E(h)
\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^M \F(i)\right)}=0.

Remark 1.2
.

We would like to immediately highlight a key difference between this definition and those of
[
12
]
: the proper chambers are
not
assumed to have finite perimeter
a priori
. Nevertheless, we will show in Proposition
3.2
that, for the case of
(1,2)
-clusters which minimize perimeter locally, the proper chamber must have finite total perimeter.

We remark that as a consequence of the full measure axiom, any
(N,M)
-cluster necessarily has
M\geq 1
. Proper
N
-clusters, having
N
disjoint chambers
\mathcal{E}=\{\mathcal{E}(h)\}_{h=1}^{N}
of finite measure and perimeter, are defined in
[
12
]
. Given such an
N
-cluster we may always append the exterior chamber to obtain an
(N,1)
-cluster consistent with this definition. Conversely, any
(N,1)
-cluster whose proper chambers have finite perimeter must be an
N
-cluster as defined in
[
12
]
, together with its exterior chamber (up to a null set). Note that
[
12
]
implicitly includes the complement of an
N
-cluster as an improper chamber by denoting it
\mathcal{E}(0)
. Note also that Definition
1.1
with

extends the notion of an improper

-cluster from
[
12
]
.

It will often be convenient to denote the proper and improper chambers of a cluster with a single index, and so we also write an
(N,M)
-cluster as
\mathcal{X}=\{\mathcal{X}(j)\}_{j=1}^{N+M}
by setting

\mathcal{X}(j)=\begin{cases}\mathcal{E}(j)&\text{if }1\leq j\leq N,\\
\mathcal{F}(j-N)&\text{if }N+1\leq j\leq N+M.\end{cases}

Remark 1.3
.

The chambers of an
(N,M)
-cluster are
not
assumed to be indecomposable (i.e. measure-theoretically connected; see
[
12
]
Exercise 16.9). Thus, a cluster could also have several connected components in any one or more of its chambers.

Definition 1.4
.

Let
\overline{\mathbb{R}}=\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
denote the extended real numbers, and let
\mathcal{X}=(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
be an
(N,M)
-cluster. The
volume vector

\mathbf{m}(\mathcal{X})\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{N+M}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}(\mathcal{X})

\displaystyle=(\absolutevalue{\E(1)},\dots,\absolutevalue{\E(N)},\absolutevalue{\F(1)},\dots,\absolutevalue{F(M)})

\displaystyle=(\absolutevalue{\E(1)},\dots,\absolutevalue{\E(N)},\underbrace{\infty,\dots,\infty}_{M\text{ times}}).

In dimension
n=2
, we call
\mathbf{m}(\mathcal{X})
the
area vector
.

So, for example, a double-bubble cluster in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is described as a
(2,1)
-cluster, with two disjoint chambers
\mathcal{E}(1),\mathcal{E}(2)
of prescribed areas
|\mathcal{E}(1)|=\mathbf{m}_{1}=A_{1}
,
|\mathcal{E}(2)|=\mathbf{m}_{2}=A_{2}
, and the exterior chamber

\mathcal{F}=\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\mathcal{E}(1)\cup\mathcal{E}(2)}
having infinite area. The area vector is thus
\mathbf{m}=(A_{1},A_{2},\infty)
.

To define perimeter, we set the following notation for the interfaces, in other words, the boundary arcs of the chambers of the cluster:

Definition 1.5
.

The
interfaces
of an
(N,M)
-cluster
\mathcal{X}
are the
\Haus^{n-1}
-rectifiable sets

\mathcal{X}(j,k)=\partial^{*}\mathcal{X}(j)\cap\partial^{*}\mathcal{X}(k),\quad 1\leq j<k\leq N+M.

The
boundary set

\partial\mathcal{X}
of an
(N,M)
-cluster
\mathcal{X}
is defined by

\partial\mathcal{X}=\bigcup_{1\leq j\leq N+M}\partial\mathcal{X}(j).

We are interested in configurations which minimize perimeter, but in case the number of improper chambers
M\geq 2
the total perimeter will necessarily be infinite. Hence we must express the isoperimetric problems in terms of a
relative perimeter
:

Definition 1.6
.

The
relative perimeter
of
\mathcal{X}
in
F\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined by

P(\mathcal{X};F)=\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq N+M}\Haus^{n-1}(F\cap\mathcal{X}(j,k)).

The
perimeter
of
\mathcal{X}
is defined by

P(\mathcal{X})=P(\mathcal{X};\mathbb{R}^{n})=\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq N+1}\Haus^{n-1}(\mathcal{X}(j,k)).

Similarly, while it is natural to seek a global minimizer of perimeter among all clusters with given mass vector
\mathbf{m}(\mathcal{E})=\mathbf{m}
, for clusters with
M\geq 2
improper chambers we are limited to studying
local minimizers,
which minimize perimeter only with respect to compactly supported perturbations of
\mathcal{X}
. For any set
E
of locally finite perimeter, let
\mu_{E}
denote the
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued Radon measure (Gauss-Green measure, see
[
12
, Proposition 12.1]
) for which

\int_{E}\text{div}\,T=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}T\cdot d\mu_{E},\quad\text{for all $T\in C_{c}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n}).$}

Then, we define:

Definition 1.7
.

Given an
(N,M)
-cluster
\mathcal{X}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we say that
\mathcal{X}
is a
locally minimizing
(N,M)
-cluster in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
if
\spt\mu_{\mathcal{X}(j)}=\partial X(j)
for every
1\leq j\leq N+M
, and, moreover,

P(\mathcal{X};B_{r})\leq P(\mathcal{X}^{\prime};B_{r})

for each ball
B_{r}
of radius
r>0
centred at the origin, and every
(N,M)
-cluster
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
satisfying
\mathbf{m}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime})=\mathbf{m}(\mathcal{X})
and

\mathcal{X}^{\prime}(j)\Delta\mathcal{X}(j)\subset\subset B_{r}\quad\text{for each }1\leq j\leq N+M.

Definition 1.8
.

By an
(N,M)
-partitioning problem in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
we mean a variational problem where given a volume vector
\mathbf{m}=(\mathbf{m}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{m}_{N+M})\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{N+M}
with

\displaystyle\begin{cases}0<\mathbf{m}_{j}<\infty&\text{if }1\leq j\leq N,\\
\mathbf{m}_{j}=\infty&\text{if }N+1\leq j\leq N+M,\end{cases}

we seek an
(N,M)
-cluster
\mathcal{X}
which is a local minimizer in the sense of Definition
1.7
.

The resolution of the double-bubble conjecture by
[
7
,
8
]
thus proves that for any choices of
A_{1},A_{2}>0
, the
(2,1)
-partitioning problem in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with volume vector
\mathbf{m}=(A_{1},A_{2},\infty)
is a double-bubble, consisting of spherical interfaces meeting at 120 degree angles at the junctions. Such a configuration is both a global and local minimizer of the perimeter.

The Main Result

Definition 1.9
.

The
standard lens cluster
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the

(1,2)
-cluster
\mathcal{X}_{\infty}=(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})

whose chambers are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(1)=\{(x,y):\absolutevalue{x}\leq\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}R,\;\absolutevalue{y}\leq u(x)\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(1)=\{y\geq 0\}\setminus\Int(\mathcal{E}(1)),\quad\mathcal{F}(2)=\{y\leq 0\}\setminus\Int(\mathcal{E}(1)),

where
u:[-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}R,\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}R]\to\mathbb{R}
is determined by

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{R^{2}-x^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}R,

and the radius of curvature
R
is determined by

R^{-1}=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{3}-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}}.

(1)

An elementary computation shows that
\absolutevalue{\E(1)}=1
.

Note that the two interfaces of
\mathcal{E}(1)
are circular arcs of the same radius
R
, and the center of each arc is located on the midpoint
(0,\pm{\tfrac{R}{2}})
of the opposite arc.

In this article, we prove the

Theorem 1.10

(Main Result)
.

The standard lens cluster is (up to translation and rotational symmetry) the unique locally minimizing cluster for the
(1,2)
-partitioning problem in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, with area vector
\mathbf{m}=(1,\infty,\infty)
.

The proof of Theorem
1.10
will be in two parts. First, we show that the standard lens cluster is a local minimizer, in the sense of Definition
1.7
. For this, we use a strategy suggested to us by Frank Morgan, thinking of the lens cluster as a limit of double-bubbles as the area of one chamber diverges to infinity. This limit is described in Section 2. Next, we show that any local minimizer must reduce to the standard lens cluster, by a sequence of constructions which would reduce the local perimeter were the configuration to have a different geometry. The proof of Theorem
1.10
is contained in Section 3. Some conjectures on the geometry of minimizing
(N,M)
-clusters for larger values of
M,N
are included in Section 4.

2
A Convergence Result

In this section we show that the standard lens cluster may be obtained as a local limit of double bubbles with a very large chamber. We first define a weak notion of convergence for clusters.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
N,M,N^{\prime},M^{\prime}
be such that
N+M=N^{\prime}+M^{\prime}
. The
cluster distance
in
F\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
between an
(N,M)
-cluster
\mathcal{X}
and an
(N^{\prime},M^{\prime})

\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined as

d_{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}^{\prime})=\sum_{j=1}^{N+M}\absolutevalue{F \cap(\X(j) \Delta\X'(j))}.

We set
d(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}^{\prime})=d_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}^{\prime})
. Notice that in general we could have
d(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}^{\prime})=\infty
.

Definition 2.2
.

Fix
N,M
and let
\mathcal{X}
be an
(N^{\prime},M^{\prime})
-cluster such that
N^{\prime}+M^{\prime}=N+M
.

•

We say that a sequence of
(N,M)
-clusters
\{\mathcal{X}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

locally converges to
\mathcal{X}
, and write
\mathcal{X}_{k}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{loc}}\mathcal{X}
, if for every compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have
d_{K}(\mathcal{X}_{k},\mathcal{X})\to 0
as
k\to\infty
.

•

We say that
\{\mathcal{X}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

converges to
\mathcal{X}
, and write
\mathcal{X}_{k}\to\mathcal{X}
, if
d(\mathcal{X}_{k},\mathcal{X})\to 0
as
k\to\infty
.

Remark 2.3
.

For clusters with
M\geq 2
, we can in general only talk about local convergence. Note that cluster type need not be preserved under cluster convergence, and cluster distance is indexing-dependent.

The key idea in the existence part of the proof of the main result is that the standard lens cluster is the limit in local cluster convergence of a suitably chosen family of standard double bubbles (with their exteriors). We make this precise below.

Lemma 2.4

If

This follows from a straightforward adaptation of

Uniqueness of the minimizer

Let

(1,2)

We recall the notation
\mathcal{M}(j,k)=\partial^{*}\mathcal{M}(j)\cap\partial^{*}\mathcal{M}(k)

First, we recall Remark

Proposition 3.2

Suppose
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{M}(1),\mathcal{M}(2),\mathcal{M}(3))

(i)

|\mathcal{M}(1)|=1

(ii)

|\mathcal{M}(2)|=\infty=|\mathcal{M}(3)|

(iii)

(iv)

\mathcal{M}
is locally minimizing.

\mathrm{Per}_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(\mathcal{M}(1))<\infty

Assume for a contradiction that
\mathrm{Per}_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(\mathcal{M}(1))=\infty

\mathrm{Per}_{B_{R}}(\mathcal{M}(1))\geq 3\sqrt{\pi}.

by Fubini’s Theorem there exists

\mathcal{M}(1)

\varepsilon:=\int_{B_{R_{0}}}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{M}(1)}(x)\,dx\leq 1.

Inside

\partial\mathcal{M}(1)
must intersect either
\partial\mathcal{M}(2)
or
\partial\mathcal{M}(3)
(or both). Suppose it’s true for

, so
\Haus^{1}(\partial\mathcal{M}(1)\cap\partial\mathcal{M}(2)\cap B_{R_{0}})>0

Now, construct a competitor
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
as follows, by modification of

inside
\overline{B_{R_{0}}}

so that the ball

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{M}}(1):=\left[\mathcal{M}(1)\setminus B_{R_{0}}\right]\cup B_{r}(0),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{M}}(2):=\left(\mathcal{M}(2)\cup[\mathcal{M}(1)\cap B_{R_{0}}]\right)\setminus B_{r}(0),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{M}}(3):=\mathcal{M}(3)\setminus B_{r}(0).

We now prove the uniqueness statement via a series of claims.

1.

Each interface
\mathcal{M}(j,k)
is an analytic arc of constant curvature, and so either a straight line or an arc of a circle.
This follows from Almgren
[
5
]
(see also p. 391 or Remark 30.4 in
[
12
]
, and Taylor
[
19
]
for the three-dimensional case.) Note that Almgren and Maggi only consider the case of a single improper chamber, but the regularity theory holds locally along the interfaces and so the proofs are identical.

Since the chambers of
\mathcal{M}
have piecewise smooth boundary, we may assume that each is an open set, and speak of its connected components (as opposed to indecomposable measurable sets, the proper notion of connectivity for sets of locally finite perimeter.)

2.

The transitions
\mathcal{M}(2,3)
, between phase domains
\mathcal{M}(2)
and
\mathcal{M}(3)
are straight line segments, rays, or full lines.
As there is no mass constraint for
\mathcal{M}(2),\mathcal{M}(3)
, there is no Lagrange multiplier in the Euler-Lagrange equation, and so minimizers must have zero curvature at points of
\mathcal{M}(2,3)
.

3.

The transitions
\mathcal{M}(1,2),\mathcal{M}(1,3)
between the finite area component
\mathcal{M}(1)
and the infinite area components
\mathcal{M}(2),\mathcal{M}(3)
are circular arcs, each of the same constant curvature independent of the connected component of
\mathcal{M}(1)
or the neighboring phase
\mathcal{M}(2),\mathcal{M}(3)
.
This follows from the regularity theory, and the calculation of the Euler-Lagrange equation in the area constrained case, as in
[
12
, Theorem 17.20]
. The choice of Lagrange multiplier should be the same at any regular point in
\partial\mathcal{M}(1)
, and so is independent of the connected component or of the choice of interface
\mathcal{M}(1,3),\mathcal{M}(1,2)
.

4.

Singular points of
\mathcal{M}(j,k)
occur when analytic arcs meet; these can only occur at triple junction points, that is, points at which all three phases meet, and the tangents to the arcs form 120 degree angle junctions.
Again, this follows from the regularity theory, and the classification of planar cone clusters
[
12
, Theorem 30.7]
. As an immediate consequence, we observe that the lines forming the transitions
\mathcal{M}(2,3)
cannot cross, and can only terminate on components of the finite area domain
\mathcal{M}(1)
.

5.

The infinite area components
\mathcal{M}(2),\mathcal{M}(3)
need not be connected, but they cannot have bounded connected components.
Assume that (say)
\mathcal{M}(2)
has a connected component
\Omega
which is bounded. Suppose
\partial\Omega
has nontrivial intersection with
\partial\mathcal{M}(3)
. In that case, we create a new cluster,
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}=(\tilde{\mathcal{M}}(1),\tilde{\mathcal{M}}(2),\tilde{\mathcal{M}}(3))
which is identical to
\mathcal{M}
except that
\Omega
is assigned to
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}(3)
, that is,
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}(3)=\mathcal{M}(3)\cup\Omega
,
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}(2)=\mathcal{M}(2)\setminus\overline{\Omega}
, and
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}(1)=\mathcal{M}(1)
. Then,
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
is an admissible cluster which agrees with
\mathcal{M}
outside of a compact set (containing
\Omega
), but has smaller total perimeter, as a component of
\mathcal{M}(2,3)
is erased by the modification. This contradicts local minimality of
\mathcal{M}
, and so we obtain the desired conclusion. If instead the boundary
\partial\Omega
is disjoint from
\partial\mathcal{M}(3)
, then the component
\Omega
must be contained inside a connected component
\tilde{\Omega}
of
\mathcal{M}(1)
. If there were such an island, by translating it inside
\tilde{\Omega}
until it contacts the outer boundary of

we create a competing configuration with the same area of

outside of a compact set, and with the same perimeter. However, the new configuration has a singular point where the phases are tangent, violating the 120 degree condition at junctions.

Each connected component of
\mathcal{M}(1)
has exactly two triple junction points on its boundary.
That is, for any connected component
\Omega

\mathcal{M}(1)

consists of two disjoint arcs of circles (of the same curvature, for each arc and each connected component), one from
\mathcal{M}(1,2)

\mathcal{M}(1)

Next, any connected component

\mathcal{M}(2)
in the region
B_{R}\setminus\Omega
, saving at least
2n(R-r)
in perimeter. (See Figure
5
for an example.)
The new configuration
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
agrees with
\mathcal{M}
outside of
C_{R}
, and has perimeter,

P(\tilde{\mathcal{M}};B_{R})-P(\mathcal{M};B_{R})\leq\pi R+2\pi r-2n(R-r)=(\pi-2n)R+(2\pi+2n)r<0,

choosing
R
sufficiently large, when
n\geq 2
. This again contradicts the local minimality of
\mathcal{M}
, and so we must have
n=1
, and each connected component
\Omega
of
\mathcal{M}(1)
has exactly one pair of triple junctions on
\partial\Omega
.

7.

Each connected component of
\mathcal{M}(1)
is isomorphic to a single lens shape (each with the same area).
From the previous step, we know that a component
\Omega
of
\mathcal{M}
is simply connected and has exactly one pair of triple junctions,
q_{1},q_{2}
. Each arc in
\partial\Omega
is a circle of the same radius
s
; call
p_{1},p_{2}
the centers of these circles, and draw the rhombus connecting
p_{1},q_{1},p_{2},q_{2}
(each side of length
s
). As the triple points meet with tangents of 120 degrees, the radii (which are normal to the arcs at the triple points) also meet at an angle of 120 degrees. Hence the opposite angles to the rhombus at
p_{1},p_{2}
each measure 60 degrees, and the rhombus decomposes as two equilateral triangles, via the diagonal of length
s
connecting
q_{1},q_{2}
. The circular arcs then describe a lens, with area uniquely determined by the radius
s
, and so each connected component of
\mathcal{M}(1)
is congruent.

8.

There are a finite number of connected components of
\mathcal{M}(1)
, and it is a bounded set.
This follows directly from the finite area constraint and the previous step.

9.

Outside of a sufficiently large disk
B_{R}
,
\mathcal{M}(2)
and
\mathcal{M}(3)
coincide with complementary half-planes, and
\mathcal{M}(1)
is symmetric with respect to reflection in the separating plane.
Fix any one of the connected components
\Omega
of
\mathcal{M}(1)
. By Step
7
we may choose axes for which
\Omega
is centered at the origin, and whose triple points lie on the
x
-axis. By the 120 degree angle condition, a part of the transition set
\mathcal{M}(2,3)
must lie along the
x
-axis outside of
\Omega
. Either these are rays extending out along the
x
-axis to infinity, or else they terminate at some other connected component of
\mathcal{M}(1)
. In the latter case, those other components of
\mathcal{M}(1)
are again congruent to a standard lens shape, and by the 120 degree angle condition those additional lenses must connect to
\mathcal{M}(2,3)
as segments or rays along the
x
-axis. Since there are only finitely many connected components of
\mathcal{M}(1)
this process must terminate, and there is a first and last component of
\mathcal{M}(1)
lying along the
x
-axis, with a component of
\mathcal{E}(2,3)
lying on opposing rays along the axis. We claim that this exhausts
all
connected components of
\mathcal{M}(1)
, and so the whole set
\mathcal{M}(1)
lies along the
x
-axis, and is symmetric with respect to reflection in the axis. Indeed, if there were another connected component
\tilde{\Omega}
of
\mathcal{M}(1)
which did
not
lie on the axis, we would repeat the above procedure to reveal a distinct second line in the plane on which
\tilde{\Omega}
is centered. Other components could lie on this second line, but being finite in number there will be a first and last one along the line, and another component of
\mathcal{M}(2,3)
would exist as a pair of collinear rays, distinct from the
x
-axis. However, this contradicts Step
5
, which states that on any sufficiently large circle there can be only a single pair of transition points between
\mathcal{M}(2)
and
\mathcal{M}(3)
. In conclusion, there can be no other rays of
\mathcal{M}(2,3)
other than along the
x
-axis, and thus outside of a large disk
\mathcal{M}(2)
and
\mathcal{M}(3)
coincide with the upper and lower half-planes, and all connected components of
\mathcal{M}(1)
must be symmetrically placed along the
x
-axis.

10.

\mathcal{M}(1)
consists of a single unit lens, centered on the
x
-axis.
If there were several connected components in
\mathcal{M}(1)
, they would be aligned on the
x
-axis, and so each could be translated horizontally without changing the total area or perimeter, until they were in contact at their junction points. This modified configuration coincides with
\mathcal{M}
outside of a ball, and it therefore also a local minimizer. However, the contact at the junction points would then create singularities which contradict the 120 degree angle condition imposed by regularity theory, and so having multiple components in
\mathcal{M}
is not possible. By Step
7
, the single component is a unit lens.

This proves uniqueness. ∎
