Stochastic non-bipartite matching models and order-independent loss queues Author version of the paper available at https://doi.org/10.1080/15326349.2021.1962352 .
Céline Comte

Abstract
The problem of appropriately matching items subject to compatibility constraints arises in a number of important applications. While most of the literature on matching theory focuses on a static setting with a fixed number of items, several recent works incorporated time by considering a stochastic model in which items of different classes arrive according to independent Poisson processes and assignment constraints are described by an undirected non-bipartite graph on the classes. In this paper, we prove that the Markov process associated with this model has the same transition diagram as in a product-form queueing model called an order-independent loss queue. This allows us to adapt existing results on order-independent (loss) queues to stochastic matching models and, in particular, to derive closed-form expressions for several performance metrics, like the waiting probability or the mean matching time, that can be implemented using dynamic programming. Both these formulas and the numerical results that they allow us to derive are used to gain insight into the impact of parameters on performance. In particular, we characterize performance in a so-called heavy-traffic regime in which the number of items of a subset of the classes goes to infinity while items of other classes become scarce. Keywords: Stochastic matching model, order-independent queue, product-form stationary distribution, dynamic programming, heavy-traffic regime.

Introduction

Matching items with one another
in such a way that an individual
item’s preferences are met
is a well-known problem
in mathematics, computer science, and economics.
Classical applications include
assigning users to servers
in distributed computing systems,
joining components in assembly systems,
matching passengers to drivers
in ride-sharing applications,
and assigning questions to experts
in question-and-answer websites.

While most of the literature on matching theory
focuses on a
static
setting
in which a fixed set of items has to be matched,
applications to modern
large-scale service systems
require that we also consider the time dimension.
In assembly systems for instance,
components of different types
arrive at random instants
as they are manufactured,
and the primary objective is to have
sufficient balance between components
to maximize throughput
while also minimizing the number
of components to be stored.
This observation gave rise to models
that can be seen as stochastic generalizations
of classical matching problems.
In these stochastic matching models,
items of different classes
arrive according to independent Poisson processes.
Compatibility constraints between items
are described by an undirected graph on the classes,
so that there is an edge between two classes
if items of these classes
can be matched with one another.
In the example of Figure
1
,
class-
3
items can be matched
with items of classes
1
,
2
and
4
,
and items of classes
1
and
2

can also be matched with one another.
A
matching policy
is a decision rule
that specifies, whenever an item arrives,
to which compatible waiting unmatched item,
if any, it is matched.
The primary objective of a matching policy
is to make the system
stable

in the sense that
the stochastic process describing
the evolution of the number
of unmatched items over time is ergodic.
The secondary objective
is to optimize performance metrics
like the mean matching time, defined as
the average amount of time
between the arrival of an item
and its matching with another item.

Two variants of the stochastic matching model
have been studied in the literature.
The stochastic
bipartite
matching model
was introduced in
[
11
]

and further studied
in
[
3
,
9
,
1
,
2
]
.
As the name suggests,
this model assumes that
the compatibility graph is bipartite.
One can verify that such a model
is always unstable if items arrive
one by one at random instants
(as the corresponding Markov process is
either transient or null recurrent).
Stability in this model is made possible by imposing
that items always arrive in pairs,
so that the classes of items in a pair
belong to the two parts of the graph.
The stochastic
non-bipartite
matching model,
which we consider in this paper,
was introduced in
[
20
]

and further studied
in
[
10
,
21
,
4
]
.
In this model,
items arrive one at a time,
and stability is made possible
by imposing the presence of odd cycles
in the compatibility graph.
Mairesse and Moyal
[
20
]
consider
matching policies
that are
admissible
in the sense that
the matching decision is
based solely on the class of the incoming item
and the sequence, in order of arrival,
of classes of unmatched items.
This work characterizes
the maximum stability region, that is,
the set of vectors of per-class arrival rates
under which there exists an admissible policy that makes the system stable.
The focus of
[
21
,
10
,
4
]

is on an admissible policy
called
first-come-first-matched
;
this is also our focus.
As the name suggests, each incoming item
is matched with the compatible item
that has been waiting the longest, if any.
In addition to being perceived as fair,
this policy has the advantage of
maximizing the stability region
and leading to tractable results,
as we will develop in this paper.
The rationale behind this policy,
which gives an intuition for its maximum stability,
is as follows:
if an item has been waiting longer than another,
it is likely that this item
is compatible with classes that are relatively scarcer,
so that matching this item if an opportunity arises
is a good heuristic to preserve stability.
It is shown in
[
21
]
that
this matching policy is
indeed maximally stable
and leads to a product-form
stationary distribution.
This work also derives
a closed-form expression
for the normalization constant
of this stationary distribution.
A similar closed-form expression
for the mean matching time
is proposed in
[
10
]
,
which applies this result to study
the impact of the compatibility graph on performance.
The authors observe that adding edges
sometimes has a negative impact on performance,
which is akin to Braess’s paradox in road networks.
Lastly, the recent work
[
4
]

adapts some of the above results
to a variant of the model
in which the items of a given class
can also be matched with one another.

Several recent papers
[
3
,
1
,
2
,
16
]
have
pointed out the similarity between
the stationary distribution
of stochastic matching models
under this first-come-first-matched policy
and that of order-independent queues
[
5
]
,
a product-form queueing model
that recently gained momentum
in the queueing-theory community.
Our first main contribution
consists of formally proving
that a stochastic non-bipartite matching model
is an order-independent
loss
queue,
a loss variant of order-independent queues
introduced in
[
6
]
.
This allows us to adapt
known results on order-independent queues
to stochastic non-bipartite matching models,
and in particular to provide simpler proofs
for the product-form and stability results
derived in
[
21
]
.
The relation with order-independent loss queues
also allows us to extend these results
to several variants of the model,
some of which were already considered in the literature.
Our second main contribution
consists of closed-form expressions
for several performance metrics
like the waiting probability
and the mean matching time.
Some of these expressions
can be seen as recursive variants
of those derived in
[
21
,
10
]
,
and are adapted from the works
[
22
,
14
,
12
]

which focus on order-independent queues
and related queueing models.
The recursive form of these expressions
allows us to avoid duplicate calculations
by using dynamic programming,
which significantly reduces
the complexity of the calculations.
In addition to their numerical applications,
these formulas allow us to gain intuition
on the impact of the parameters on performance,
and in particular to formalize
what it means to have a
balanced
system.
Along the same lines,
our third and last contribution
is a performance analysis
in a scaling regime, called
heavy traffic

by analogy with the scaling regime
of the same name in queueing theory,
in which the vector of per-class arrival rates
tends to a boundary of the stability region.
This result can be seen as
a generalization of the
heavy-traffic result of
[
12
]
,
which focuses on order-independent queues,
although the conclusion is different
and illustrates the subtle difference
between stochastic matching models
and conventional queueing models.

The paper is organized as follows.
Section
2

In Section

This result is applied
in the rest of Section

In particular, Sections
3.2

and

and stability condition of
[

]
,
while in Section

we derive closed-form expressions

These results are applied in Section

to gain insight into the impact
of parameters on performance.
More specifically, we propose several heuristics
to minimize the mean matching time

Numerical results in Section

into the dynamics

2
Stochastic non-bipartite matching model

We first define a continuous-time variant
of the stochastic non-bipartite matching model
introduced in
[
20
]
.

2.1
Continuous-time model

Let
{\mathcal{V}}=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}

denote a finite set of item classes
and consider an undirected
connected non-bipartite
graph without loops
with vertex set
{\mathcal{V}}
.
This graph will be called the

compatibility graph
of the matching model
because it will eventually describe
the matching compatibilities between items.
An example
is shown in Figure
1
.
For each
i,j\in{\mathcal{V}}
,
we write
i\sim j
if there is an edge
between classes
i
and
j

in the compatibility graph
and
i\nsim j
otherwise.
For each
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
,
we also let
{\mathcal{E}}_{i}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}

denote the set of neighbors of class
i

in the compatibility graph.
With a slight abuse of notation,
for each set

{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}
of classes,
we also let

{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{A}})=\bigcup_{i\in{\mathcal{A}}}{\mathcal{E}}_{i}

denote the set of classes
that are neighbors
of at least one class in
{\mathcal{A}}
.
Our assumption
that the compatibility graph is non-bipartite
is not necessary for the soundness of the model,
but we will see in Section
3.3

that it is required for stability.

For each
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
,
class-
i
items enter the system
according to an independent
Poisson process with rate
\alpha_{i}>0
.
As we will see in Section
3.2
,
we can (and will) assume without loss of generality
that the vector
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{N})

is normalized
in the sense that
\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}\alpha_{i}=1
.
For each subset
{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}
of classes,
we also let
\alpha({\mathcal{A}})=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{A}}}\alpha_{i}

denote the overall arrival rate
of the classes in
{\mathcal{A}}
.
All unmatched items are queued
in a buffer in their arrival order.
In Figure
2

for instance,
unmatched items are ordered
from the oldest on the left
to the newest on the right,
and the number written on each item
represents its class.
Each incoming item is matched with
an item of a compatible class
present in the buffer, if any,
in which case both items
disappear immediately;
otherwise, the incoming item
is added at the end of the buffer.
If an incoming item
finds several compatible items
in the buffer,
it is matched with the compatible item
that has been in the buffer the longest.
This matching policy is called

first-come-first-matched

and was studied in
[
20
,
21
,
10
,
4
]
.
Note that this matching policy
can be applied
with a more decentralized approach
in which each item is only aware
of the arrival order
of its
compatible
items.
Such a decentralized approach
leads to the same dynamics and therefore
to the same performance measures.

2.2
Markov process

We now define
a Markov process
that describes the evolution
of the continuous-time stochastic
matching model that we have just introduced.

Markovian state

We describe the system state
with the sequence of unmatched item classes
present in the buffer.
More specifically, we consider the sequence

c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})
,
where
n
is the number of items in the buffer
and
c_{p}
is the class of the
p
-th oldest item,
for each
p\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
In particular,
c_{1}
is the class of the item
that has been in the buffer the longest.
Figure
2

shows a possible buffer state
associated with the compatibility graph
of Figure
1
,
with the newest items on the left.
The corresponding state descriptor
is
c=(4,4,1,4,1)
.
We assume that the state is initially empty,
with
n=0
, in which case we write
c=\varnothing
.
The assumption that each class of items
arrives according to an independent Poisson process
guarantees that the stochastic process
defined by the evolution
of this state descriptor over time
has the Markov property.
When the system is in state
c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})

and a class-
i
item arrives,
the new system state is

(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n},i)

if
c_{p}\nsim i

for each
p\in\{1,\ldots,n\}

and
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{q-1},c_{q+1},\ldots,c_{n})
,
where

q=\min\{p\in\{1,\ldots,n\}:c_{p}\sim i\}
,
otherwise.
The overall departure rate from each state
is
\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}\alpha_{i}=1
.

State space

The system state
c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})

belongs to the set
{\mathcal{V}}^{*}

made of the finite sequences over the alphabet
{\mathcal{V}}
,
but the matching policy imposes
additional restrictions on the form of this sequence.
More specifically, the buffer
cannot contain customers of classes
i,j\in{\mathcal{V}}

such that
i\sim j
,
as the newest of the two items would necessarily
have been matched with the oldest
(or with an older compatible item)
upon arrival.
In Figure
2
for instance,
an incoming class-
2
item would
be matched with the oldest class-
1
item,
so that items of classes
1
and
2

cannot be in the buffer at the same time.
Therefore, in general, the state space
of the Markov process
defined by the system state
is a strict subset of
{\mathcal{V}}^{*}

which we denote by
{\mathcal{C}}
.
To better describe the structure of this state space,
additional notation is introduced in the next paragraph.

A nonempty set
{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}

is called an
independent set

(of the compatibility graph)
if
i\nsim j
for each
i,j\in{\mathcal{I}}
.
In other words,
the set
{\mathcal{I}}
is independent
if the intersection of this set
with the set
{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}})

of its neighbors is empty.
The set of all independent sets
is denoted by
{\mathbb{I}}
.
As an example,
the set of independent sets
of the compatibility graph of Figure
1
is

{\mathbb{I}}=\{\{1\},\{2\},\{3\},\{4\},\{1,4\},\{2,4\}\}
.
In general, if a set
{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}

is an independent set,
then any nonempty subset of
{\mathcal{I}}

is also an independent set.
For convenience,
we also let
{\mathbb{I}}_{0}={\mathbb{I}}\cup\{\emptyset\}

denote the set of independent sets
completed with the empty set.

For each
{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}_{0}
, we let

{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{I}}
denote the set of words in
{\mathcal{V}}^{*}

that contain exactly the classes in
{\mathcal{I}}
.
Equivalently, if
{\mathcal{V}}(c)\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}

denotes the set of item classes
present in the word
c\in{\mathcal{V}}^{*}
,
we have
c\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{I}}

if and only if
{\mathcal{V}}(c)={\mathcal{I}}
.
Observe that
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{I}}
is a strict subset
of
{\mathcal{I}}^{*}
in general,
as words in
{\mathcal{I}}^{*}

need not contain
all
classes in
{\mathcal{I}}
.
In the special case where
{\mathcal{I}}=\emptyset
,
the set
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{I}}
contains only
the empty state
\varnothing
.
With this notation,
the state space of the Markov process
defined in the previous paragraph
can be partitioned as follows:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}=\bigcup_{{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}_{0}}{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{I}}.

In particular, the set of item classes
present in a feasible state
of the matching model
is an independent set.

Remark
.

The stochastic non-bipartite matching model
of
[
20
,
21
,
10
,
4
]

works in the same way
as the one presented above,
except that time is slotted and
there is exactly one arrival during each time slot.
More specifically,
the classes of incoming items
during different time slots are independent
and identically distributed and,
for each
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
,

\alpha_{i}
is the probability
that an incoming item is of class
i
.
One can verify
that the (discrete-time) Markov chain associated with
the state of this
discrete-time stochastic matching model
is the jump chain of
the Markov process defined above.
Since the departure rate
from each state in the Markov process
is
\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}\alpha_{i}=1
,
inspection of the balance equations reveals
that the Markov chain
and the Markov process
have the same stationary measures.
Consequently, the results in

[
21
, Theorem 1]
,
derived for the discrete-time stochastic matching model,
also apply to the continuous-time stochastic matching model
defined above.
These results will be recalled
in Sections
3.2

and
3.3
.
For simplicity,
the continuous-time stochastic non-bipartite matching model
introduced in Section
2.1

will be called the
stochastic matching model

in the remainder.

3
Product-form queueing model

In Section
3.1
,
we prove that
the stochastic matching model
of Section
2.1

is an instance of a queueing model,
called an
order-independent
loss queue

[
6
]
,
that is known to have a
product-form stationary distribution.
We then identify several extensions
of the stochastic matching model
that can also be cast
as order-independent loss queues.
In Sections
3.2

and
3.3
,
we use this observation
to provide simpler proofs for two results
from the literature on stochastic matching models.
The reader who is more interested
in understanding the long-term performance
of the stochastic matching model
can skip Section
3.1
and move directly to Section
3.2
.

3.1
Order-independent loss queue

Our definition of
order-independent loss queues,
given in Definition
1
below,
differs in spirit
from that of
[
6
, Section 1]

in that conditions are given
directly in terms of the associated
Markov process,
rather than in terms of the queueing system itself.
In particular, although
the name
loss
usually entails rejecting customers,
here this manifests itself via the fact that
the state space
{\mathcal{C}}
may be a strict subset of
{\mathcal{V}}^{*}
.
This definition happens
to be more convenient
to show the connection
with the stochastic matching model
of Section
2.1
,
and preserves all the properties
of the order-independent loss queues
defined in
[
6
]
.
We also make a few restrictions
(such as imposing constant arrival rates)
compared to
[
6
]

to avoid introducing superfluous notation.

Definition 1
.

Consider a queueing system
with a set
{\mathcal{V}}=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}

of customer classes.
This queueing system
is called
an
order-independent loss queue

with state space
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}^{*}
,
arrival-rate vector

\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{N})
,
and departure-rate function
\mu:{\mathcal{C}}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}

if the following conditions are satisfied:

(a)

The evolution over time
of the sequence

c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in{\mathcal{V}}

of classes of present customers,
ordered by arrival,
defines a Markov process
such that:

(i)

The state space of this
Markov process is
{\mathcal{C}}
.

(ii)

The transitions from each state

(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in{\mathcal{C}}

are given as follows:

•

For each class
i\in{\mathcal{V}}

such that
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n},i)\in{\mathcal{C}}
,
there is a transition
from state
c

to state
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n},i)

with rate
\lambda_{i}
.

•

For each position
p\in\{1,\ldots,n\}

such that
\mu(c_{1},\ldots,c_{p})>\mu(c_{1},\ldots,c_{p-1})
,
there is a transition
from state
c
to state

(c_{1},\ldots,c_{p-1},c_{p+1},\ldots,c_{n})

with rate

\mu(c_{1},\ldots,c_{p})-\mu(c_{1},\ldots,c_{p-1})

(with the convention that

(c_{1},\ldots,c_{p-1})=\varnothing

if
p=1
).

(b)

The state space
{\mathcal{C}}

satisfies the following conditions:

(i)

if
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in{\mathcal{C}}
,
then
(c_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,c_{\sigma(n)})\in{\mathcal{C}}

for each permutation
\sigma

of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and

(ii)

if
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\{\varnothing\}
,
then
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n-1})\in{\mathcal{C}}
.

(c)

The departure-rate function
\mu

satisfies the following conditions:

(i)

\mu(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})=\mu(c_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,c_{\sigma(n)})

for each state
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in{\mathcal{C}}

and permutation
\sigma
on
\{1,\ldots,n\}
,

(ii)

\mu(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n},i)\geq\mu(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})

for each state
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in{\mathcal{C}}

and class
i\in{\mathcal{V}}

such that
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n},i)\in{\mathcal{C}}
,

(iii)

\mu(\varnothing)=0

and
\mu(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})>0

for each
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\varnothing
.

Remark
.

The versatility of the order-independent framework
allows us to generalize
the stochastic matching model
introduced in Section
2.1

in several directions
while preserving its product-form nature.
For instance,
we again obtain
an order-independent loss queue
if items of a given class can
be matched with one another.
The main difference with the original model
is that the state space is reduced,
as the buffer can
contain at most one customer
of such a class at a time.
We note that this extension was considered
in the recent work
[
4
]
,
and that the proofs of Theorems
3

and
4

in Sections
3.2

and
3.3

can be adapted to provide a simpler proof of

[
4
, Theorem 1]
.
Another extension,
which again leads to
an order-independent loss queue,
consists of
describing compatibilities with a directed graph
(instead of an undirected graph),
so that there is an edge
from class
i
to class
j

if incoming class-
i
items can be matched
with class-
j
items
(which does not imply that incoming class-
j
items
can be matched with class-
i
items).
Yet another extension consists of
allowing for item abandonment
[
23
]
,
meaning that an item may leave
the system without being matched.
The stability region
and product-form stationary distribution
of these extensions
can be characterized by following a similar approach
as in the proofs of Theorems
3

and
4
,
or by applying known results
on order-independent queues
and quasi-reversible queues,
a more general class of queueing models
studied in
[
18
, Chapter 3]

and
[
13
]
.
A last extension consists
of imposing an upper bound on the number
of unmatched items (either of each class or in total),
or any other rejection rule
that again leads to an order-independent loss queue.
In this case, the stationary distribution
is the restriction of that recalled in Theorem
3

to the set of reachable states
(after renormalization),
and no stability condition is needed.

Remark
.

The usefulness of the equivalence
between stochastic matching models
and conventional queueing models
is particularly evident
under the first-come-first-matched policy,
as the corresponding queueing model is known
to have a product-form stationary distribution.
However, this equivalence may also prove useful
to transpose existing scheduling policies
into matching policies,
as was done in
[
9
,
20
,
4
]
,
or to adapt exact or approximate analysis methods.

3.2
Product-form stationary distribution

The Markov process
defined in Section
2.2

is irreducible.
Indeed, for each state

c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in{\mathcal{C}}

and each state
d=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{m})\in{\mathcal{C}}
,
we can first go
from state
c
to state
\varnothing

via
n
transitions
corresponding to arrivals of items
compatible with the items in state
c
,
and then from state
\varnothing
to state
d

via
m
transitions
corresponding to arrivals of the items
present in state
d
.
Note that the first
n
transitions
occur with a positive probability
because the compatibility graph
contains no isolated node;
additionally, the latter
m
transitions
indeed lead to state
d

because the set of classes in
d

is an independent set by definition of
{\mathcal{C}}
.

Theorem
3
below
recalls the form of the stationary measures
of the Markov process
defined in Section
2.2
.
As observed before,
this theorem is analogous
to
[
21
, Theorem 1]
,
which was stated for the discrete-time
variant of the stochastic matching model.
Thanks to the relation with
order-independent loss queues
proved
in Proposition
2
,
we propose a simpler proof
that consists of explicitly writing
the (partial) balance equations
of the Markov process.
This proof, which is a special case
of that of
[
6
, Theorem 1]
,
is sketched below for completeness.

Theorem 3
.

The stationary measures of
the Markov process
associated with the system state
are of the form

\displaystyle\pi(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})=\pi(\varnothing)\prod_{p=1}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{c_{p}}}{\alpha({\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{V}}(c_{1},\ldots,c_{p})))},\quad(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in{\mathcal{C}},

(2)

where
\pi(\varnothing)

is a normalization constant.
The system is stable, in the sense that
this Markov process is ergodic, if and only if

\displaystyle\sum_{(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in{\mathcal{C}}}\prod_{p=1}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{c_{p}}}{\alpha({\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{V}}(c_{1},\ldots,c_{p})))}<\infty,

(3)

in which case the stationary distribution
of the Markov process is given by (
2
)
with the normalization constant

\displaystyle\pi(\varnothing)=\left(\sum_{(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in{\mathcal{C}}}\prod_{p=1}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{c_{p}}}{\alpha({\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{V}}(c_{1},\ldots,c_{p})))}\right)^{-1}.

(4)

Sketch of proof.

We will prove that
any measure
\pi
of the form (
2
)
satisfies the following
partial balance equations
in each state
c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in{\mathcal{C}}
:

•

Equalize the flow out of state
c

due to the arrival of an item
that can be matched with a present item
and the flow into state
c

due to the arrival of an item
that cannot be matched with a present item
(if
c\neq\varnothing
):

\displaystyle\pi(c)\alpha({\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{V}}(c)))=\pi(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n-1})\alpha_{c_{n}}.

(5)

•

Equalize the flow out of state
c

due to the arrival of an item
of a class
i\in{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{V}}(c))

that cannot be matched with a present item
and the flow into state
c

due to the departure of an item of this class:

\displaystyle\pi(c)\alpha_{i}=\sum_{p=1}^{n+1}\pi(c_{1},\ldots,c_{p-1},i,c_{p},\ldots,c_{n})\alpha({\mathcal{E}}_{i}\setminus{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{V}}(c_{1},\ldots,c_{p-1}))),

(6)

with the convention that

(c_{1},\ldots,c_{p-1})=\varnothing

if
p=1

and
(c_{p},\ldots,c_{n})=\varnothing

if
p=n+1
.
Observe that,
for each
i\in{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{V}}(c))

and
p\in\{1,\ldots,n+1\}
,
state

(c_{1},\ldots,c_{p-1},i,c_{p},\ldots,c_{n})

belongs to
{\mathcal{C}}
because
the set of item classes present in this state,

{\mathcal{V}}(c)\cup\{i\}
,
is an independent set.

Proving that a measure
\pi

satisfies the partial balance
equations (
5
)
and (
6
)
is sufficient to prove that

\pi
is a stationary measure of
the Markov process defined by the system state,
as summing these partial balance equations
yields the balance equations
of the Markov process.
We can easily verify that
any measure
\pi
of the form (
2
)
satisfies (
5
).
That this measure also satisfies (
6
)
can be proved by induction
over the buffer length
n
,
in a similar way as in
[
5
, Theorem 1]
.

The stability condition (
3
)
means that there exists a stationary measure
whose sum is finite,
which is indeed necessary and sufficient
for ergodicity of the Markov process.
Lastly, the constant
\pi(\varnothing)

given by (
4
)
is chosen such that the stationary distribution sums to one.
∎

Multiplying all arrival rates

\alpha_{i}
for
i\in{\mathcal{V}}

by the same positive constant
does not modify the form of
the stationary measures (
2
).
Indeed, this amounts to rescaling time
without modifying the relative importance
of the states.
In particular, as already stated
in Section
2.1
, we can assume
without loss of generality
that
\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}\alpha_{i}=1
,
so that
\alpha_{i}
is also
the fraction of arrivals
that are of class
i
.
In the remainder,
it will also be more convenient to define
the stationary measures (
2
)
with the following recursion:

\displaystyle\pi(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})=\frac{\alpha_{c_{n}}}{\alpha({\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{V}}(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})))}\pi(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n-1}),\quad(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\{\varnothing\}.

(7)

3.3
Stability condition

We now recall the stability condition
derived in
[
21
, Theorem 1]

and again provide a simpler proof based on
the relation with order-independent loss queues.

Theorem 4
.

The stochastic matching model
is stable,
in the sense that the
associated Markov process is ergodic,
if and only if

\displaystyle\alpha({\mathcal{I}})<\alpha({\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}})),\quad{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}.

(8)

Sketch of proof.

The proof consists of showing
that (
3
)
is satisfied if and only if
the arrival rates satisfy (
8
).
The key idea is
to observe that the denominators
in (
3
)
only depend on the set of classes
in the sequence
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{p})
.
Guided by this observation,
we can define a new partition
of the state space
{\mathcal{C}}
,
that is a refinement of
the partition
({\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{I}},{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}_{0})
,
such that the restriction of the sum
in (
3
)
to each part of this new partition
can be written
as a finite product of geometric series
that are convergent
if and only if
the conditions (
8
)
are satisfied. The details of the proof follow
along the same lines
as in the proof of Theorem 1
in the appendix of
[
7
]
.
The only difference is that
the set of customer classes
that can be in the queue
at the same time
is an independent set,
so that the stability condition
is also restricted
to independent sets.
∎

The stability
condition (
8
) means that,
for each independent set
{\mathcal{I}}
,
there are enough items
that can be matched with the classes in
{\mathcal{I}}

to prevent items of these classes
from building up in the buffer.
As proved in
[
20
]
,
no matching policy can make the system stable
if the arrival rates do not satisfy
the stability condition (
8
)
(in particular, first-come-first-matched is
maximally stable).
It is also shown in
[
20
]
that
we would not be able to find arrival rates
that satisfy this stability condition
if the compatibility graph were bipartite.
To better understand why
the presence of odd cycles is necessary,
we examine two toy examples.
First consider a stochastic matching model
similar to that of Section
2.1
,
except that the graph is bipartite
and consists of only two classes connected by an edge.
The stability condition (
8
)
becomes

\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}
and
\alpha_{2}<\alpha_{1}
,
and can therefore not be satisfied.
Consistently, if we examine
the associated Markov process,
we obtain a two-sided infinite
birth-and-death process,
with transition rate
\alpha_{1}
in one direction
and
\alpha_{2}
in the other,
that is either transient
(if
\alpha_{1}\neq\alpha_{2}
)
or null recurrent
(if
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}
),
but cannot be positive recurrent.
Now, if we instead consider a stochastic matching model
with three classes that are all compatible,
the stability condition (
8
) becomes

\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}
,
\alpha_{2}<\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{3}
,
and
\alpha_{3}<\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
,
and is satisfied for instance when

\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\alpha_{3}=\frac{1}{3}
.
If we examine the associated Markov process,
we obtain a three-sided infinite birth-and-death process
that is positive recurrent under the above conditions.
In the remainder, we will assume that
the stability condition (
8
)
is satisfied
and let
\pi
denote the stationary distribution
of the Markov process.

4
Performance metrics

Another consequence of Proposition
2

is that we can adapt numerical methods
originally developed for order-independent
queues
[
22
,
14
,
12
]

to stochastic matching models.
Note that calculating
average performance metrics
associated with
loss
product-form
queueing models
is considered
a hard problem in the queueing literature
[
19
]
.
Our task here is simplified
by the fact that
the number of items of a given class
can be arbitrarily large if it is not zero,
so that we can apply methods
developed for
open
queues.
Also, since we established that
the continuous-time stochastic matching model
and its discrete-time counterpart
have the same stationary distribution,
the formulas derived in this section
can be applied without change
to the discrete-time
variant of the model.

4.1
Probability of an empty system

We first propose a new formula
to compute the normalization constant
of the stationary distribution
of the stochastic matching model.
With a slight abuse of notation,
for each
{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}_{0}
,
we let
\pi({\mathcal{I}})
denote the stationary probability
that the set of unmatched item classes is
{\mathcal{I}}
, that is,

\displaystyle\pi({\mathcal{I}})=\sum_{c\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{I}}}\pi(c),\quad{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}_{0}.

(9)

Equation (
1
) guarantees that

\sum_{{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}_{0}}\pi({\mathcal{I}})=1
.
The following proposition
gives a recursive formula
for
\pi({\mathcal{I}})
for each
{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}
.
We will see later that
this recursive formula
also gives a closed-form expression for
the normalization constant

\pi(\emptyset)=\pi(\varnothing)
.
The proof technique is
similar to that of
[
14
, Theorem 5.1]
,
which was itself inspired from
[
22
, Theorem 4]
,
but the proof is more straightforward
because we skip the intermediary state aggregation
considered in these two works.

Proposition 5
.

The stationary distribution of
the set of unmatched item classes
satisfies the recursion

\displaystyle\pi({\mathcal{I}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}\alpha_{i}\pi({\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{i\})}{\alpha({\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}}))-\alpha({\mathcal{I}})},\quad{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}.

(10)

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}
.
The proof of (
10
) relies
on the following partition of
the set
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{I}}
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{I}}=\bigcup_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}\left(({\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{I}}\cdot i)\cup({\mathcal{C}}_{{\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{i\}}\cdot i)\right),

(11)

where the unions are disjoint and,
for each
{\mathcal{D}}\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}

and
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
, we write

{\mathcal{D}}\cdot i=\{(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n},i):(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in{\mathcal{D}}\}
.
We first apply (
7
)
and (
9
)
to rewrite
\pi({\mathcal{I}})
as follows:

\displaystyle\pi({\mathcal{I}})=\frac{1}{\alpha({\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}}))}\sum_{c\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{I}}}\alpha_{c_{n}}\pi(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n-1}).

Then applying (
11
) yields

\displaystyle\alpha({\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}}))\pi({\mathcal{I}})=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}\alpha_{i}\sum_{c\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{I}}}\pi(c)+\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}\alpha_{i}\sum_{c\in{\mathcal{C}}_{{\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{i\}}}\pi(c)=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}\alpha_{i}\pi({\mathcal{I}})+\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}\alpha_{i}\pi({\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{i\}).

Rearranging the terms yields (
10
).
∎

Proposition
5

gives a closed-form expression for
the normalization constant

\pi(\emptyset)=\pi(\varnothing)
.
It suffices to observe that
the unnormalized stationary measure

\psi({\mathcal{I}})=\pi({\mathcal{I}})/\pi(\emptyset)

also satisfies recursion (
10
).
Therefore, we can first compute

\psi({\mathcal{I}})
for each
{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}

by applying recursion (
10
)
with the base case
\psi(\emptyset)=1
,
and then derive
\pi(\emptyset)
by normalization,
that is,
\pi(\emptyset)=(\sum_{{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}_{0}}\psi({\mathcal{I}}))^{-1}
.
Since there is a finite number of independent sets,
this provides us with a closed-form expression
for the normalization constant.
Note that the denominator in (
10
)
is always positive thanks to
the stability condition (
8
).

One can verify that unfolding the above recursion
yields the closed-form expression
derived in
[
21
, Equation (5)]
.
The advantage of the above recursion
is that it avoids duplicate calculations.
More specifically, the overall complexity
to evaluate the normalization constant
using (
10
)
with dynamic programming is
O(MN)
,
where
M=|{\mathbb{I}}|
is the number of independent sets
and
N=|{\mathcal{V}}|
is the number of item classes.
By comparison,
if we naively apply
[
21
, Equation (5)]
,
the complexity is
O(MNP\times P!)
,
where
P
is the independence number
of the compatibility graph,
that is, the cardinality
of a maximum independent set in this graph.
In both cases however, we need to enumerate
all independent sets of the compatibility graph,
which is an NP-hard optimization problem in general.
Consequently, both formulas
can be applied to make calculations only
when there are few classes,
when symmetries between classes
can be exploited to reduce complexity
(see for instance
[
8
]
),
or when the graph structure guarantees that
the number of independent sets is small.
The same remark holds for the formulas derived in
Propositions
6

to
10
below.
However, we will see in Section
5
that,
even when these conditions are not meet,
these formulas can be used to
gain insight into the impact
of parameters on performance.

4.2
Waiting probability

The result of Proposition
5

gives us directly a closed-form expression
for the waiting probability
of the items of each class.
Indeed, it follows from the PASTA property that,
for each
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
, the waiting probability
of class-
i
items is given by

\displaystyle\omega_{i}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}_{0}:\\
i\notin{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}})\end{subarray}}\pi({\mathcal{I}}).

(12)

Upon recalling that
\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}\alpha_{i}=1
,
Proposition
6
below
shows that the average waiting probability
is simply given by
\frac{1}{2}
.
This result is not surprising.
Indeed, each matching occurs between two items,
one of that has just arrived
(and therefore does not wait)
and another that was waiting.
However, this result highlights that
improving the performance of one class
(in terms of the waiting probability)
necessarily comes at the expense of another class.

Proposition 6
.

The average waiting probability is given by

\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}\alpha_{i}\omega_{i}=\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

We will verify that the per-class
waiting probabilities satisfy
the following conservation equation:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}\alpha_{i}\omega_{i}=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}\alpha_{i}(1-\omega_{i}),

(13)

after which the result follows
by rearranging the terms.
We have successively:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}\alpha_{i}(1-\omega_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}\alpha_{i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}:\\
i\in{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}})\end{subarray}}\pi({\mathcal{I}})=\sum_{{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}}\pi({\mathcal{I}})\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}})}\alpha_{i}=\sum_{{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}}\pi({\mathcal{I}})\alpha({\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}})),

where the first equality follows from
the definition of
\omega_{i}
,
the second by exchanging the two sums,
and the third from the definition of
\alpha({\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}}))
.
Applying (
10
)
to the last member of this equality yields

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}\alpha_{i}(1-\omega_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}}\left(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}\alpha_{i}\pi({\mathcal{I}})+\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}\alpha_{i}\pi({\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{i\})\right)=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}\alpha_{i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}:\\
i\in{\mathcal{I}}\end{subarray}}\left(\pi({\mathcal{I}})+\pi({\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{i\})\right),

where the second equality follows
by exchanging the sums.
Equation (
13
) follows
by observing that, for each
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}:\\
i\in{\mathcal{I}}\end{subarray}}\left(\pi({\mathcal{I}})+\pi({\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{i\})\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}_{0}:\\
i\notin{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}})\end{subarray}}\pi({\mathcal{I}})=\omega_{i},

where the first equality follows
from a change of variable
and the second by definition of
\omega_{i}
.
∎

4.3
Mean number of unmatched items
and mean matching time

Consider a random vector
X=(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{N})

distributed as
the vector of numbers of unmatched items of each class
in the stationary regime
in the stochastic matching model
of Section
2.1
.
For now, we focus on the means

L_{i}=\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}\right]
for each
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
,
and we also let
L=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}L_{i}

be the mean number of unmatched items,
all classes included.
Propositions
7

and
8
below
give a closed-form expression
for these quantities
by using a similar aggregation technique
as in the proof of Proposition
5
.
As observed in
[
21
, Section 3.4]
,
these can be used to calculate
the mean matching time of items,
that is, the mean time between
the arrival of an item
and its matching with another item.
Indeed, according to Little’s law,
the mean matching time of class-
i
items
is
L_{i}/\alpha_{i}
, for each
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
,
and the overall mean matching time
is given by
L/(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}\alpha_{i})=L

(since we assumed that
\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}\alpha_{i}=1
).

Proposition 7
.

For each
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
, the mean
number of unmatched class-
i
items is given by

L_{i}=\sum_{{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}:i\in{\mathcal{I}}}L_{i}({\mathcal{I}})\pi({\mathcal{I}}),

where
L_{i}({\mathcal{I}})
is the mean number
of unmatched class-
i
items
given that the set of unmatched items is
{\mathcal{I}}
, and is
given by the recursion

\displaystyle L_{i}({\mathcal{I}})\pi({\mathcal{I}})=\frac{\alpha_{i}\pi({\mathcal{I}})+\alpha_{i}\pi({\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{i\})+\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{i\}}\alpha_{j}L_{i}({\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{j\})\pi({\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{j\})}{\alpha({\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}}))-\alpha({\mathcal{I}})},\quad{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}:i\in{\mathcal{I}},

(14)

with the base case
L_{i}({\mathcal{I}})=0

for each
{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}_{0}
such that
i\notin{\mathcal{I}}
.

Proof.

The proof follows similar steps
as that of Proposition
5
,
with some technical complications.
Let
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
.
We have

L_{i}=\sum_{{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}_{0}}L_{i}({\mathcal{I}})\pi({\mathcal{I}})
where,
for each
{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}_{0}
,

L_{i}({\mathcal{I}})
is the mean number
of unmatched class-
i
items
given that the set of unmatched
item classes is
{\mathcal{I}}
.
We have directly that
L_{i}({\mathcal{I}})=0

if
i\notin{\mathcal{I}}
.
If
i\in{\mathcal{I}}
, we have

\displaystyle L_{i}({\mathcal{I}})\pi({\mathcal{I}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{I}}}|c|_{i}\pi(c)=\sum_{c\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{I}}}|c|_{i}\frac{\alpha_{c_{n}}}{\alpha({\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}}))}\pi(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n-1}),

where
|c|_{i}
is the number of
unmatched class-
i
items
in state
c
, for each
c\in{\mathcal{C}}
,
and the second equality follows from (
7
).
Applying partition (
11
) yields

\displaystyle\alpha({\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}}))L_{i}({\mathcal{I}})\pi({\mathcal{I}})

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} &\alpha_{i}\left[\sum_{c\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{I}}}\left(|c|_{i}+1\right)\pi(c)+\sum_{c\in{\mathcal{C}}_{{\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{i\}}}\left(0+1\right)\pi(c)\right]\\
&+\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{i\}}\alpha_{j}\left[\sum_{c\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{I}}}|c|_{i}\pi(c)+\sum_{c\in{\mathcal{C}}_{{\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{j\}}}|c|_{i}\pi(c)\right].\end{aligned}

This, in turn, can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\alpha({\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}}))L_{i}({\mathcal{I}})\pi({\mathcal{I}})

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} &\alpha_{i}\left[L_{i}({\mathcal{I}})\pi({\mathcal{I}})+\pi({\mathcal{I}})+\pi({\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{i\})\right]+\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{i\}}\alpha_{j}\left[L_{i}({\mathcal{I}})\pi({\mathcal{I}})+L_{i}({\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{j\})\pi({\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{j\})\right],\end{aligned}

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} &\alpha({\mathcal{I}})L_{i}({\mathcal{I}})\pi({\mathcal{I}})+\alpha_{i}\pi({\mathcal{I}})+\alpha_{i}\pi({\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{i\})+\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{i\}}L_{i}({\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{j\})\pi({\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{j\}).\end{aligned}

Rearranging the terms yields (
14
).
∎

To the best of our knowledge,
no previous work
obtained the above formula
for the stochastic matching model
(either in recursive or in non-recursive form).
We now provide an analogous formula
to directly calculate
the mean number of unmatched items,
all classes included,
without needing to calculate
the mean number of unmatched items of each class.

Proposition 8

The mean number

\displaystyle L=\sum_{{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}}L({\mathcal{I}})\pi({\mathcal{I}}),

(15)

Proof.

The result follows by applying (

Unfolding recursion (

As before, let

4.5
Distribution of the matching time

Let
T=(T_{1},T_{2},\ldots,T_{N})
denote
a vector distributed like the vector of
matching times of each class
in the stationary regime
in the stochastic matching model
of Section
2.1
.
For each
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
,
the Laplace-Stieltjes transform
of the stationary matching time
T_{i}

is defined as

\displaystyle\varphi_{T_{i}}(u)=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-uT_{i}}\right],\quad u\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

(19)

One can show that
this expectation is finite
whenever
u\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
but we will be especially interested
in the range
0\leq u\leq\alpha_{i}
.
Proposition
10
below
gives a closed-form expression for
this Laplace-Stieltjes transform
by applying the distributional form
of Little’s law, in a similar way
as in
[
12
, Corollary 3.2]
.

Proposition 10
.

For each
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
,
the Laplace-Stieltjes transform
of the matching time of class
i

is given by

\displaystyle\varphi_{T_{i}}(u)=g_{X}(z),\quad 0\leq u\leq\alpha_{i},

(20)

where the function
g_{X}

is defined on
{\mathcal{Z}}
by (
17
)
and the vector
z\in{\mathcal{Z}}

is given by

\displaystyle z_{j}=\begin{cases}1-\frac{u}{\alpha_{i}}&\text{if $j=i$,}\\
1&\text{if $j\in{\mathcal{V}}\setminus\{i\}$.}\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
.
The main argument consists of observing
that class
i

satisfies the assumptions of
the following theorem,
proved in
[
17
, Theorem 1]
:

Theorem 11

(Distributional form of Little’s law)
.

Let an ergodic queueing system
be such that, for a given class
i
of customers:

(a)

arrivals form
a Poisson process
with rate
\alpha_{i}
;

(b)

all arriving customers
enter the system,
and remain in the system until served,
i.e. there is no blocking,
balking, or reneging;

(c)

the customers leave
the system one at a time
in order of arrival;

(d)

for any time
t
,
the arrival process
after time
t

and the time in the system
of any customer
arriving before
t

are independent.

Then the moment generating function
g_{X_{i}}

of the ergodic number
X_{i}

of customers of this class in the system
and the Laplace-Stieltjes transform
\varphi_{T_{i}}

of the ergodic time
T_{i}

spent in the system
by a customer of this class
satisfy

g_{X_{i}}(z)=\varphi_{T_{i}}(\alpha_{i}(1-z))
.

The distributional form
of Little’s law therefore implies that

\displaystyle\varphi_{T_{i}}(u)=g_{X_{i}}\left(1-\frac{u}{\alpha_{i}}\right),\quad 0\leq u\leq\alpha_{i},

where
g_{X_{i}}
is the probability generating function
of the stationary number
X_{i}

of unmatched class-
i
items.
We conclude by observing that,
for each
z_{i}\in[0,1]
, we have

g_{X_{i}}(z_{i})=g_{X}(z)

where
z\in{\mathcal{Z}}
is the
N
-dimensional vector
with
z_{i}
in component
i

and
1
elsewhere.
∎

5
Impact of load on performance

Performance in a stochastic matching model
is the result of an intricate interaction
between arrival rates and matching compatibilities.
Even finding arrival rates that
stabilize the matching model associated
with a given compatibility graph
is non-trivial
a priori
.
More subtly, it was observed in
[
10
]
that,
given a compatibility graph and a set of arrival rates
that make the system stable,
adding more edges to the compatibility graph
may hurt performance
(even if (
8
) guarantees
that the system will remain stable).
Furthermore, applying the results of
Sections
3

and
4

to derive performance
in a real-world matching problem
will often be impossible
due to the complexity of the formulas.
In this section, we show that
these results are also useful to gain intuition about
the choices of parameters that optimize performance
or at least make the system stable.
By analogy with queueing theory,
we define the
loads

of the independent sets as

\displaystyle\rho({\mathcal{I}})=\frac{\alpha({\mathcal{I}})}{\alpha({\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}}))},\quad{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}.

(21)

These loads will be key to analyze performance,
as for instance
the stability condition (
8
)
can be rewritten as

\rho({\mathcal{I}})<1
for each
{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}
.
Although this definition
is inspired from the analogy with queueing theory,
there is a fundamental difference
between (
21
) and
the regular definition of load in a queue,
namely in (
21
)
the arrival rates appear both
in the numerator and denominator.
This implies in particular that
the loads are not increasing functions
of the arrival rates.

5.1
Heuristics to optimize performance

Given the conclusions of
[
10
]
,
one may wonder,
for a given vector of arrival rates

\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{N})

and a given compatibility graph
that lead to a stable
stochastic matching model,
whether or not removing edges
from the compatibility graph
may improve performance,
and in this case which edge(s) should be removed.
This discrete optimization problem
is difficult in general,
as the number of configurations
is exponential in the number of classes.
To gain insight into this question,
we consider a related problem
that consists of finding arrival rates
that stabilize the system
and minimize the mean matching time
for a given compatibility graph.
Solving this optimization problem exactly
with the formulas of Section
4

is practically unfeasible
when the number of classes is large,
but we propose two heuristics
that yield acceptable performance in practice.

Degree proportional

It was shown in
[
20
, Theorem 1]
that
there exists a vector of arrival rates

\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{N})

that stabilizes the system
if and only if the compatibility graph is non-bipartite
(which we assumed in Section
2.1
),
but exhibiting such arrival rates
is not trivial
a priori
.
Lemma
12
below
gives a simple method to generate
(an infinite number of)
such arrival rates.

Lemma 12
.

Let
A=(a_{i,j})_{i,j\in{\mathcal{V}}}

denote a symmetric matrix
such that
a_{i,j}>0

if and only if nodes
i
and
j

are neighbors in the compatibility graph.
For each
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
,
the weight of class
i

is defined as
d_{i}=\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{E}}_{i}}a_{i,j}
.
The matching model is stable
if the arrival rates are chosen
to be proportional to the weights, that is,

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}=\frac{d_{i}}{\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{V}}}d_{j}},\quad i\in{\mathcal{V}}.

(22)

Proof.

Assume that
the arrival rates are given
by (
22
)
for some matrix
A
that
satisfies the assumptions
of the proposition,
and let
d=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}d_{i}
.
We will prove that these arrival rates
satisfy the stability condition (
8
).
Consider an independent set
{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}
.
We have successively

\displaystyle d\cdot\alpha({\mathcal{I}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}d_{i}=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{E}}_{i}}a_{i,j}=\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}})}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}\cap{\mathcal{E}}_{j}}a_{j,i}<\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}})}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{E}}_{j}}a_{j,i}=\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}})}d_{j}=d\cdot\alpha({\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}})).

To prove that the above inequality is indeed strict,
it suffices to show that we cannot find
an independent set
{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}
such that

{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}}))={\mathcal{I}}
.
We can prove this by contradiction.
Indeed, if there were such
an independent set
{\mathcal{I}}
,
this would imply that

{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}})={\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}}

(because the compatibility graph is connected)
and that
{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}})
is also an independent set,
which would contradict our assumption
that the compatibility graph is non-bipartite.
∎

The matrix
A
can be interpreted
as the adjacency matrix
of a weighted variant
of the compatibility graph.
As a special case,
if
A
is the adjacency matrix
of the compatibility graph,
then
d_{i}
is the degree of class
i
,
in which case the arrival rates
defined by (
22
)
are called
degree proportional
.
For example, in the graph
of Figure
1
,
the degree-proportional arrival rates
are
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\frac{1}{4}
,

\alpha_{3}=\frac{3}{8}
, and
\alpha_{4}=\frac{1}{8}
;
choosing these arrival rates yields approximately
a probability 0.17
that the system is empty
and a mean matching time
of 2.25 time units.
In general, if the number of classes
is sufficiently small so that all independent sets

can

be enumerated,
this degree-proportional solution
can also be used to initialize
an optimization algorithm
that searches for arrival rates
that minimize the mean matching time
by applying Proposition
8
.

Load minimization

The loads defined in (
21
)
also play an instrumental role
in better understanding which arrival rates
yield acceptable performance.
To understand this, let us first focus on
the probability that the system is empty,
which is the inverse of the normalization constant
and is given by Proposition
5
.
First observe that (
10
)
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\pi({\mathcal{I}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho({\mathcal{I}})}{1-\rho({\mathcal{I}})}\left(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\alpha({\mathcal{I}})}\pi({\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{i\})\right),\quad{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}},

(23)

where the loads
\rho({\mathcal{I}})
for
{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}

are given by (
21
).
Expanding the recursion yields

\displaystyle\pi({\mathcal{I}})

\displaystyle=\pi(\emptyset)\sum_{s\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathcal{I}}}\left(\prod_{p=1}^{|{\mathcal{I}}|}\frac{\rho(\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{p}\})}{1-\rho(\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{p}\})}\right)\left(\prod_{p=1}^{|{\mathcal{I}}|}\frac{\alpha_{s_{p}}}{\alpha(\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{p}\})}\right),\quad{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}},

where
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathcal{I}}
is the set
of permutations of the set
{\mathcal{I}}
,
interpreted as the set of all sequences

s=(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{|{\mathcal{I}}|})

in which each element of
{\mathcal{I}}

appears exactly once.
The first product may tend to infinity
if the load
\rho({\mathcal{J}})
of
any set
{\mathcal{J}}\subseteq{\mathcal{I}}
tends to one,
while the second product is always
between zero and one.
By applying the normalization condition,
we obtain that
1/\pi(\emptyset)

is a linear combination of products of the form

\displaystyle\prod_{p=1}^{n}\frac{\rho(\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{p}\})}{1-\rho(\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{p}\})},\quad{\mathcal{I}}=\{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{n}\}\in{\mathbb{I}}.

Similarly, we can rewrite (
15
)
as follows:

\displaystyle L({\mathcal{I}})\pi({\mathcal{I}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi({\mathcal{I}})}{1-\rho({\mathcal{I}})}+\frac{\rho({\mathcal{I}})}{1-\rho({\mathcal{I}})}\left(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\alpha({\mathcal{I}})}L({\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{i\})\pi({\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{i\})\right),\quad{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}.

(24)

In both cases,
the variations of the function

x\mapsto\frac{x}{1-x}
on
(0,1)

suggest that solving
the following optimization problem
is a good heuristic to maximize
the probability that the system is empty
and minimize the mean matching time:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \underset{\alpha}{\text{Minimize}}\quad&\max_{{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}}\left(\rho({\mathcal{I}})\right),\\
\text{subject to}\quad&\alpha_{i}\geq 0,\quad i\in{\mathcal{V}},\\
&\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}\alpha_{i}=1.\end{aligned}

(25)

One can verify that
a solution of this optimization problem satisfies
the stability condition (
8
).
In the example
of Figure
1
,
the objective function of
the optimization problem (
25
)
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\max\left(\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}},\frac{\alpha_{2}}{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{3}},\frac{\alpha_{3}}{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{4}},\frac{\alpha_{4}}{\alpha_{3}},\frac{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{4}}{\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}},\frac{\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{4}}{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{3}}\right),

and the unique solution is

\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\alpha_{3}=\frac{1}{3}
and
\alpha_{4}=0
.
These arrival rates happen to be a global maximum for the probability that the system is empty, with maximum value 0.25, and a local minimum for the mean matching time, with minimum value 1.5 time units
1
1

1

Although numerical evaluations suggest that this is also a global minimum, proving this conjecture is complicated by the structure of (
15
). Better understanding the properties of the mean matching time seen as a function of the arrival rates and/or the graph structure would be an interesting topic for future work.
.

In general,
calculating a solution of
the optimization problem (
25
)
requires enumerating all independent sets,
which is precisely why
we may not be able to apply
the results of Section
4

on the first place.
This issue can be mitigated
by focusing on the independent sets
with cardinality at most
n
for some
n\ll N
,
or on the independent sets
containing the most
central
nodes
(under an appropriate definition
of centrality),
but the obtained solution
may not be stable.
To further stress the importance of
minimizing the loads,
we now consider a scaling regime
in which the load of
a (subset of) class(es) becomes close to one,
thus making the system unstable.

5.2
Heavy-traffic regime

We consider a scaling regime
called
heavy traffic

by analogy with the regime of the same name
studied in queueing theory.
In a nutshell,
the stability condition
associated with a maximal independent set
becomes violated in (
8
),
so that items of these classes
accumulate in the buffer
(while items of other classes become scarce);
our result describes
the corresponding limiting distributions and performance metrics.
The analysis relies on
the closed-form expressions
derived in Section
4
.
The proof technique and the results
generalize those of

[
12
, Theorem 3.1]
but,
as announced in the introduction,
the conclusions are different
and illustrate the fundamental difference
between stochastic matching models
and conventional queueing models.

Scaling regime

Fix
{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{I}}
, an independent set
that is maximal with respect to the inclusion relation.
We have in particular that

{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}})={\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}}

and

\alpha({\mathcal{I}})+\alpha({\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}}))=\alpha({\mathcal{I}})+\alpha({\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}})=1
.
We consider a scaling regime where,
for each
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}=\begin{cases}p_{i}\frac{\rho}{1+\rho},&i\in{\mathcal{I}},\\
q_{i}\frac{1}{1+\rho},&i\in{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}},\end{cases}

(26)

with
\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}p_{i}=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}}}q_{i}=1

and
\rho\in(0,1)
.
In this way,

\rho=\alpha({\mathcal{I}})/\alpha({\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}})=\rho({\mathcal{I}})

is the ratio of the arrival rate of the classes in
{\mathcal{I}}

to the arrival rate of their compatible classes,
that is, the load of set
{\mathcal{I}}
.
We will show that, under some technical assumptions,
as
\rho\uparrow 1
,
the buffer becomes saturated with
items of the classes in
{\mathcal{I}}

while running out of items of the classes
in
{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}}
.
That the classes in
{\mathcal{I}}
become saturated
is a consequence of (
26
),
as we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\rho\uparrow 1}\alpha({\mathcal{I}})=\lim_{\rho\uparrow 1}\alpha({\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}})=\frac{1}{2}.

(27)

The following technical assumption guarantees that
the set
{\mathcal{I}}
is the only one that becomes saturated.

Assumption 13
.

There exists
\rho^{*}\in[0,1)
such that
the stability conditions (
8
)
are satisfied by the measures
\alpha

defined by (
26
)
for each
\rho\in(\rho^{*},1)
, and

\displaystyle\lim_{\rho\uparrow 1}\alpha({\mathcal{J}})<\lim_{\rho\uparrow 1}\alpha({\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{J}})),\quad{\mathcal{J}}\in{\mathbb{I}}\setminus\{{\mathcal{I}}\}.

Asymptotic distribution

Proposition
14
below shows that,
under the above technical assumption,
the mean number of items
of the classes in
{\mathcal{I}}

goes to infinity
like
(1-\rho)^{-1}

as
\rho\uparrow 1
,
while the mean number of items
of the classes in
{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}}

goes to zero.
Furthermore, the distribution of items
of the classes in
{\mathcal{I}}

depends on their respective arrival rates
but not on their compatibility constraints
with the classes in
{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}}
.
Similarly, as intuition suggests,
the waiting probability of
the classes in
{\mathcal{I}}
tends to one
as
\rho\uparrow 1
,
while the waiting probability of
the classes in
{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}}

tends to zero.
In this way,
despite the structural differences
between the stochastic matching model
and a more conventional queueing model,
the classes in
{\mathcal{I}}
experience
the same performance as
in an M/M/1 multi-class queue
in which the load
\rho
tends to one
while, for each
i\in{\mathcal{I}}
,
the relative arrival rate
of class
i
is kept constant equal to
p_{i}
.
The fundamental difference
with an M/M/1 multi-class queue,
discussed in the remark below,
is that the number of items
of the classes in
{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}}

tends to zero.
Given
\rho^{*}\in[0,1)

and two functions
f
and
g
,
defined on
(\rho^{*},1)
, such that

g(\rho)>0
for each
\rho\in(\rho^{*},1)
,
we write
f(\rho)\underset{\rho\uparrow 1}{\sim}g(\rho)

if
f(\rho)/g(\rho)\xrightarrow[\rho\uparrow 1]{}1
.

Proposition 14
.

If Assumption
13

is satisfied, then the following
limiting results hold:

(14-a)

The stationary distribution
of the set of unmatched item classes
satisfies:

\displaystyle\pi({\mathcal{I}})

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\rho\uparrow 1]{}1,

\displaystyle\pi({\mathcal{J}})

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\rho\uparrow 1]{}0,\quad{\mathcal{J}}\in{\mathbb{I}}_{0}\setminus\{{\mathcal{I}}\}.

The waiting probability
of each item class satisfies:

\displaystyle\omega_{i}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\rho\uparrow 1]{}1,\quad i\in{\mathcal{I}},

\displaystyle\omega_{i}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\rho\uparrow 1]{}0,\quad i\in{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}}.

(14-c)

The mean number of

\displaystyle L_{i}=\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}\right]

\displaystyle\underset{\rho\uparrow 1}{\sim}p_{i}\frac{\rho}{1-\rho},\quad i\in{\mathcal{I}},

\displaystyle L_{i}=\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}\right]

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\rho\uparrow 1]{}0,\quad i\in{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}}.

In particular, the limiting mean number of
unmatched items (over all classes) satisfies

L\underset{\rho\uparrow 1}{\sim}\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}
.

(14-d)

The number of unmatched items of each class
has the following limit in distribution:

\displaystyle(1-\rho)(X_{i})_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\rho\uparrow 1]{d}\mathrm{Exp}(1)(p_{i})_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}},

\displaystyle(X_{i})_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}}}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\rho\uparrow 1]{d}0,

where
\mathrm{Exp}(1)
represents a unit-mean
exponentially-distributed random variable.

(14-e)

The mean matching time of
each class satisfies:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[T_{i}\right]

\displaystyle\underset{\rho\uparrow 1}{\sim}\frac{2}{1-\rho},\quad i\in{\mathcal{I}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[T_{i}\right]

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\rho\uparrow 1]{}0,\quad i\in{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}}.

(14-f)

The matching time of each class
has the following limit in distribution:

\displaystyle(1-\rho)T_{i}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\rho\uparrow 1]{d}\mathrm{Exp}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right),\quad i\in{\mathcal{I}},

\displaystyle T_{i}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\rho\uparrow 1]{d}0,\quad i\in{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}},

where
\mathrm{Exp}(\frac{1}{2})

an exponentially-distributed random variable

Sketch of proof.

The proof of each property relies
on the corresponding expression
derived in Section

.
Each proof follows the same pattern:
the expression for which
we want to calculate the limit is a fraction
whose numerator and denominator are
sums of terms given by recursive expressions;
we first use the recursive expressions

The complete proof is given

Remark
.

The analysis is simplified
by our choice of a maximal independent set
and by Assumption
13
.
Indeed, these two assumptions guarantee that

{\mathcal{I}}
is the only independent set
that becomes saturated as
\rho\uparrow 1
,
and that all other classes are absorbed by
the surplus of items in the classes in
{\mathcal{I}}
.
We could also consider a scaling regime
where these assumptions are not satisfied.
For instance, we could choose
an independent set
{\mathcal{I}}

that is not maximal with respect
to the inclusion relation.
Under some technical assumptions,
we would obtain a scaling regime where
the buffer becomes saturated with
items of the classes in
{\mathcal{I}}
and
runs out of items of the classes in
{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}})
,
while containing a positive but finite
number of items of the classes
in
{\mathcal{V}}\setminus({\mathcal{I}}\cup{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{I}}))
.
This intermediary scaling regime
becomes apparent in the numerical results
of Section
6
.

Remark
.

The above-defined heavy-traffic regime
is actually closer to
a
light-traffic
regime
for the classes in
{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}}
,
as Proposition
14

shows that the number of items
of these classes tends to zero.
Yet, one can verify that the loads
of the classes of this set do
not tend to zero.
These two observations may seem paradoxical,
especially with conventional queueing models,
like the M/M/1 queue, in mind.
This apparent paradox
can be explained by recalling that
the arrival of an item
can either increase
or decrease

the number of present items.
This is different from
conventional queueing models,
in which the arrival of a customer
always increases the number of present customers.
Intuitively, in the scaling regime
studied in this section,
the arrival of an item
of a class in
{\mathcal{I}}

will (almost) always
increase the number of unmatched items,
while the arrival of an item
of a class in
{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}}

will (almost) always
decrease the number of unmatched items.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we proved that
a stochastic non-bipartite matching model
is an order-independent loss queue.
This equivalence allowed us
to propose simpler proofs for existing results
and to derive new results
regarding stochastic matching models.
In particular, we used
the order-independent queue framework
to give alternative proofs
for the product-form stationary distribution
and the stability condition
derived in
[
21
, Theorem 1]
.
By adapting numerical methods
developed in
[
22
,
14
,
12
]
,
we also derived new closed-form expressions
for the normalization constant,
the mean matching time,
and other performance metrics.
In turn, these formulas were applied
to gain insight into
the impact of parameters on performance.
By analogy with queueing theory,
we highlighted the importance
of the so-called load parameters
and studied the system behavior
in the heavy-traffic regime,
in which the load of a subset
of classes becomes critical.
The obtained formulas
were also put in practice with numerical results.

For future works,
we would like to derive simpler formulas
for the performance metrics.
Indeed, even though our recursive formulas
allow for a dynamic-programming approach,
complexity remains exponential
in the number of classes in general,
which limits their applicability
to models with only a few classes.
In parallel, we would like to
understand if and how these results
can be adapted to analyze the performance
of stochastic matching models
with a bipartite compatibility graph,
like the one studied
in
[
11
,
3
,
9
,
1
,
2
]
,
in which items arrive in pairs.
Lastly, as mentioned before,
we would like to explore the generalizations
of the stochastic matching model
made possible by the equivalence
with order-independent loss queues.
Yet another avenue for future work,
which goes beyond the scope
of stochastic matching models,
consists of extending the heavy-traffic result
proved for multi-class multi-server queues in
[
12
]

to other order-independent (loss) queues,
like those considered in
[
14
, Section 4.3]
.

have a finite limit,
while its denominator still tends to infinity,
so that
L_{i}
tends to zero
as
\rho\uparrow 1
.
The limiting result for
L
follows by summation,
and that for

\mathbb{E}\left[T_{i}\right]
,
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
,
follows by applying Little’s law
and simplifying the result.
∎

Proof of Property
(14-d)
.

Consider the random vector

S=(S_{1},S_{2},\ldots,S_{N})
defined by

\displaystyle S_{i}=\begin{cases}(1-\rho)X_{i},&i\in{\mathcal{I}},\\
X_{i},&i\in{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}}.\end{cases}

The Laplace-Stieltjes transform
of this random vector is defined,
for each
u\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}
, by

\displaystyle\varphi_{S}(u)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}e^{-u_{i}S_{i}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\prod_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}\mathrm{e}^{-u_{i}(1-\rho)X_{i}}\right)\left(\prod_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}}}\mathrm{e}^{-u_{i}X_{i}}\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}{z_{i}}^{X_{i}}\right]=g_{X}(z),

where the vector

z=(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{N})

is given by

z_{i}=\mathrm{e}^{-u_{i}(1-\rho)}
for
i\in{\mathcal{I}}

and
z_{i}=\mathrm{e}^{-u_{i}}
for
i\in{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}}
.
We will use Proposition
9

to show that
this Laplace-Stieltjes transform satisfies

\displaystyle\varphi_{S}(u)\xrightarrow[\rho\uparrow 1]{}\frac{1}{1+\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}p_{i}u_{i}}.

(31)

One can verify that this is also
the Laplace-Stieltjes transform
of a random vector

\tilde{S}=(\tilde{S}_{1},\tilde{S}_{2},\ldots,\tilde{S}_{N})

such that

\tilde{S}_{i}=\mathrm{Exp}(1)p_{i}

for each
i\in{\mathcal{I}}

and
\tilde{S}_{i}=0

for each
i\in{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}}
.
According to
[
15
, Section 13.1, Theorem 2]
,
this suffices to conclude that

S
weakly converges to
\tilde{S}

as
\rho\uparrow 1
.

By following a similar approach
as in the proof of
Properties
(14-a)

and
(14-c)

and recalling that

z_{i}\uparrow 1
if
i\in{\mathcal{I}}

and
z_{i}=e^{-u_{i}}<1

if
i\in{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}}
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\varphi_{S}(u)\underset{\rho\uparrow 1}{\sim}\frac{\psi_{z\alpha,\alpha}({\mathcal{I}})}{\psi_{\alpha,\alpha}({\mathcal{I}})}.

Using (
18
),
we can rewrite the right-hand side
of this equivalence as

\displaystyle\frac{\psi_{z\alpha,\alpha}({\mathcal{I}})}{\psi_{\alpha,\alpha}({\mathcal{I}})}=\frac{\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}z_{i}\alpha_{i}\psi_{z\alpha,\alpha}({\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{i\})}{\alpha({\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}})-(z\alpha)({\mathcal{I}})}}{\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}\alpha_{i}\psi_{\alpha,\alpha}({\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{i\})}{\alpha({\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}})-\alpha({\mathcal{I}})}}=\frac{\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}z_{i}\alpha_{i}\psi_{z\alpha,\alpha}({\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{i\})}{\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}\alpha_{i}\psi_{\alpha,\alpha}({\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{i\})}\cdot\frac{1-\frac{\alpha({\mathcal{I}})}{\alpha({\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}})}}{1-\frac{(z\alpha)({\mathcal{I}})}{\alpha({\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}})}}.

(32)

As
\rho\uparrow 1
,
the first factor tends to
1

because
z_{i}\uparrow 1
for each
i\in{\mathcal{I}}
,
while we can show with a Taylor series expansion
that the second factor tends to

(1+\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}p_{i}u_{i})^{-1}
.
This proves (
31
)
and completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Property
(14-f)
.

We will show that,
for each
i\in{\mathcal{I}}
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\rho\uparrow 1}\varphi_{(1-\rho)T_{i}}(u)\xrightarrow[\rho\uparrow 1]{}\frac{\frac{1}{2}}{\frac{1}{2}+u},\quad i\in{\mathcal{I}},\quad\qquad\lim_{\rho\uparrow 1}\varphi_{T_{i}}(u)\xrightarrow[\rho\uparrow 1]{}1,\quad i\in{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}}.

(33)

We recognize the Laplace-Stieltjes transform
of an exponentially-distributed random variable
with rate
\frac{1}{2}
for
i\in{\mathcal{I}}

and the degenerate Laplace-Stieltjes transform
of a constant random variable equal to
0

for
i\in{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}}
.
The conclusion of the proposition
then again follows
from
[
15
, Section 13.1, Theorem 2]
.

First let
i\in{\mathcal{I}}
.
According to Proposition
10
, we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{(1-\rho)T_{i}}(u)=\varphi_{T_{i}}((1-\rho)u)=g_{X}(z),

where
z=(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{N})

is given by

z_{i}=1-(1-\rho)\frac{u}{\alpha_{i}}

and
z_{j}=1
for each
j\in{\mathcal{V}}\setminus\{i\}
.
Since
z_{i}\uparrow 1
as
\rho\uparrow 1
,
and by following a similar reasoning
as in the proof of Property
(14-d)
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\varphi_{(1-\rho)T_{i}}(u)\underset{\rho\uparrow 1}{\sim}\frac{\psi_{z\alpha,\alpha}({\mathcal{I}})}{\psi_{\alpha,\alpha}({\mathcal{I}})}.

The right-hand side of this equivalence
is again given by (
32
).
By using the definition of
z
,
we can show that the second factor
in (
32
)
simplifies to

\alpha({\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}})/(\alpha({\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}})+u)
,
which tends to
\frac{1}{2}/(\frac{1}{2}+u)

according to (
33
).
Since we also know that
the first factor in (
32
) tends to
1
,
this proves (
33
).
The proof for
i\in{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}}
is similar,
the main difference being that we have directly

\psi_{z\alpha,\alpha}({\mathcal{I}})=\psi_{\alpha,\alpha}({\mathcal{I}})

because
i\notin{\mathcal{I}}
.
∎
