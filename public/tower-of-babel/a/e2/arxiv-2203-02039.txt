Abstract
. We describe the large-time asymptotics of solutions to the heat equation for the fractional Laplacian with added subcritical or even critical Hardy-type potential. The asymptotics is governed by a self-similar solution of the equation, obtained as a normalized limit at the origin of the kernel of the corresponding Feynman-Kac semigroup.

Introduction

1.1.
Main results and structure of the paper

Let
d\in{\mathbb{N}}:=\{1,2,\ldots\}
,
\alpha\in(0,2)
and
\alpha<d
.
We
consider the semigroup
\tilde{P}_{t}
,

, of the following Hardy operator
on
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}

\Delta^{\alpha/2}+\kappa|x|^{-\alpha}.

We call
\kappa
, and
\Delta^{\alpha/2}+\kappa|x|^{-\alpha}
, subcritical if
\kappa<\kappa^{*}
, critical if
\kappa=\kappa^{*}
and supercritical if
\kappa>\kappa^{*}
.
Here
\Delta^{\alpha/2}:=-(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}
is the fractional Laplacian,

\kappa^{*}:=\frac{2^{\alpha}\Gamma((d+\alpha)/4)^{2}}{\Gamma((d-\alpha)/4)^{2}},

and
\Gamma(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{t-1}e^{-y}\,{\rm d}y
is the Gamma function.
It is well known that
\kappa^{*}
is the best constant in the Hardy inequality for the quadratic form of
\Delta^{\alpha/2}
,
see Herbst
[
20
, Theorem 2.5]
, Beckner
[
2
, Theorem 2]
or Yafaev
[
44
, (1.1)]
; see also Frank and Seiringer
[
15
, Theorem 1.1]
and Bogdan, Dyda and Kim
[
5
, Proposition 5]
.
Following
[
5
, Section 4]
, for
\beta\in[0,d-\alpha]
we let

(1.2)

\kappa_{\beta}=\frac{2^{\alpha}\Gamma((\beta+\alpha)/2)\Gamma((d-\beta)/2)}{\Gamma(\beta/2)\Gamma((d-\beta-\alpha)/2)},

where
\kappa_{0}=\kappa_{d-\alpha}=0
, according to the convention
1/\Gamma(0)=0
. The function
\beta\mapsto\kappa_{\beta}
is increasing on
[0,(d-\alpha)/2]
, decreasing on
[(d-\alpha)/2,d-\alpha]
, and
\kappa_{\beta}=\kappa_{d-\alpha-\beta}
, see
[
5
, Proof of Proposition 5]
. The maximal or
critical
value of
\kappa_{\beta}
is, therefore,
\kappa_{(d-\alpha)/2}=\kappa^{*}
, and
for each
\kappa\in[0,\kappa^{*}]
there is a unique number
\delta
such that

(1.3)

0\leq\delta\leq(d-\alpha)/2\quad\text{ and }\quad\kappa=\kappa_{\delta}=\frac{2^{\alpha}\Gamma((\delta+\alpha)/2)\Gamma((d-\delta)/2)}{\Gamma(\delta/2)\Gamma((d-\delta-\alpha)/2)}.

In what follows,
\delta
and
\kappa

shall satisfy (
1.3
). We let

h(x)=h_{\delta}(x):=|x|^{-\delta}
,
x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
.
By Bogdan, Grzywny, Jakubowski and Pilarczyk
[
6
]
,
the Schrödinger operator (
1.1
) has heat kernel
\tilde{p}
with singularity at the origin in
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
and sharp explicit estimates (given by
(
2.12
) below).
The first main result
of the present paper
is a description of the limiting behavior of
\tilde{p}
, as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

The limit
\Psi_{t}(x):=\lim\limits_{y\to 0}\frac{\tilde{p}(t,x,y)}{h(y)}
exists whenever
0\leq\delta\leq\frac{d-\alpha}{2}
,
t>0
,
x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is given in Section
3
.
The function
\Psi_{t}(x)
is a self-similar semigroup solution of the heat equation for the Hardy operator,
as we assert in (
5.2
) and (
5.3
) below.
It has an important application to large-time asymptotics of the semigroup
\tilde{P}_{t}
, which we now present. To this end we consider Doob–conditioned and weighted

L^{q}
spaces. Let

H=\max\{1,h\}.

As usual,
L^{1}=L^{1}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}},\,{\rm d}x)
,
L^{1}(H)=L^{1}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}},H(x)\,{\rm d}x)
, etc. We have
L^{1}(H)=\{f/H:f\in L^{1}\}=L^{1}(h)\cap L^{1}
.
We then define, for
1\leq q<\infty
,

(1.4)

\|f\|_{q,h}:=\|f/h\|_{L^{q}(h^{2})}=\bigg(\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}|f(x)|^{q}h^{2-q}(x)\,{\rm d}x\bigg)^{\frac{1}{q}}=\|f\|_{L^{q}(h^{2-q})},

Of course,

For

Our second main result is the following large-time asymptotics for

Theorem 1.2

If

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}t^{\frac{d-2\delta}{\alpha}(1-\frac{1}{q})}\|u(t,\cdot)-A\Psi_{t}\|_{q,h}=0.

1.2.
Motivation and methods

The classical result of Baras and Goldstein
[
1
]
asserts the
existence of nontrivial nonnegative solutions of the heat equation
\partial_{t}=\Delta+\kappa|x|^{-2}
in
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
for (subcritical)
\kappa\in[0,(d-2)^{2}/4]
, and non-existence of such solutions for (supercritical)
\kappa>(d-2)^{2}/4
.
Later on, the upper and lower bounds for the heat kernel of the subcritical Hardy operator
\Delta+\kappa|x|^{-2}
were obtained by Liskevich and Sobol
[
31
]
, Milman and Semenov
[
32
,
33
]
, Moschini and Tesei
[
34
]
, Filippas, Moschini and Tertikas
[
13
]
.

The classical Hardy operator
\Delta+\kappa|x|^{-2}
plays a distinctive role in limiting and self-similar phenomena in
probability
[
36
]
and partial differential equations
[
37
]
. This is related to the scaling of the corresponding heat kernel, which is the same as for the Gauss-Weierstrass kernel, and to the asymptotics at the origin in
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
, which is very different. Such applications motivate our work on the Hardy perturbation of the fractional Laplacian. In fact the paper
[
6
]
was a preparation for the present work, which now comes to fruition.

The strategy of the proof of Theorem
1.1
is to
prove and use the existence of a stationary density of a corresponding Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup. Then the large-time asymptotics of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup yields the asymptotics of
\tilde{p}
at the origin. To the best of our knowledge the approach is new
and should apply to other heat kernels with scaling.

Let us also comment on Theorem
1.2
. We start by recalling the initial value problem for the classical heat equation,

(1.7)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u(x,t)=\Delta u(x,t),\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\quad t>0,\\
u(x,0)=f(x).\end{cases}

For

f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
the following is asymptotics is well-known:

(1.8)

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}t^{\frac{d}{2}(1-\frac{1}{p})}\|u(t,\cdot)-Mg_{t}\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}=0,

see, e.g., Giga, Giga and Saal
[
16
, Theorem in Sect. 1.1.4]
or Duoandikoetxea and Zuazua
[
12
]
). Here
p\in[1,\infty]
,
M:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x)\,{\rm d}x
,
u(t,x)=g_{t}\ast f(x)

is the semigroup solution of (
1.7
), and
g_{t}(x)=\big(4\pi t\big)^{-d/2}\exp(-|x|^{2}/(4t))
is the Gauss-Weierstrass kernel. Of course, we have
scaling
:
g_{t}(x)=t^{-d/2}g_{1}(t^{-1/2}x)
, that is, the function is
self-similar
. The function also satisfies the first equation in (
1.7
). We can consider (
1.8
) as a statement about the universality of the self-similar solution
g_{t}(x)
for the large-time behavior of all solutions to (
1.7
).

Theorem
1.2

gives an analogous result for
u(t,x)=\tilde{P}_{t}f(x)
and the initial value problem

(1.9)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u(x,t)=\left(\Delta^{\alpha/2}+\kappa|x|^{-\alpha}\right)u(x,t),\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\quad t>0,\\
u(x,0)=f(x).\end{cases}

for
\kappa
satisfying (
1.3
) and

f\in L^{1}(H)
.
The use of
h
is novel in this setting – this
and connections to the classical literature will be discussed in more detail in Section
5
.

1.3.
General conventions

We tend to use “:=” to indicate definitions, e.g.,
a\land b:=\min\{a,b\}
and
a\vee b:=\max\{a,b\}
,

and

. Throughout, we only consider Borel measurable functions and Borel measures.
As usual, integrals are considered well-defined when the integrands are nonnegative or absolutely integrable with respect to a given measure. In the case of integral kernels, the corresponding integrals
should at least be well-defined pointwise almost everywhere (
a.e.
).
For
x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
and
r>0
we define
B(x,r)=\{y\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}:|y-x|<r\}
, the ball with center at
x
and radius
r
.
We write
f\approx g
, which we call
approximation
or
comparison
, and say
f
and
g
are
comparable
, if
f,g
are nonnegative functions,
c^{-1}g\leq f\leq cg
with some constant
c
, that is a number in
(0,\infty)
. The values of constants may change without notice from line to line in a chain of estimates. Of course, we shall also use constants in inequalities (one-sided comparisons of functions), e.g.,
f\leq cg
. We occasionally write
c=c(a,\ldots,z)
to assert that the constant
c
may be so selected as to depend only on
a,\ldots,z
.
As usual, for
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
L^{p}:=L^{p}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}},\,{\rm d}x)
, with norm
\|\cdot\|_{p}
, and

L^{p}(g):=L^{p}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}},g\,{\rm d}x)
with norm
\|f\|_{L^{p}(g)}
and nonnegative (weight) function
g
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Fractional Laplacian

Let

\nu(y)=\frac{\alpha 2^{\alpha-1}\Gamma\big((d+\alpha)/2\big)}{\pi^{d/2}\Gamma(1-\alpha/2)}|y|^{-d-\alpha}\,,\quad y\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\,.

The
coefficient is so chosen
that

(2.1)

\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\left[1-\cos(\xi\cdot y)\right]\nu(y)\,{\rm d}y=|\xi|^{\alpha}\,,\quad\xi\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\,,

see, e.g., Bogdan, Byczkowski, Kulczycki, Ryznar, Song, and Vondraček
[
4
, (1.28)]
. The fractional Laplacian for (smooth compactly supported)
test functions

\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
is

\Delta^{\alpha/2}\varphi(x)=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{|y|>\varepsilon}\left[\varphi(x+y)-\varphi(x)\right]\nu(y)\,{\rm d}y\,,\quad x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\,.

Many authors use the notation
-(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}
for the operator. In terms of the Fourier transform,

\widehat{\Delta^{\alpha/2}\varphi}(\xi)=-|\xi|^{\alpha}\widehat{\varphi}(\xi)
, see, e.g.,
[
4
, Section 1.1.2]
or
[
26
]
.

2.2.
The semigroup of
\Delta^{\alpha/2}

We consider the convolution semigroup of functions

(2.2)

p_{t}(x):=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}e^{-t|\xi|^{\alpha}}e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\,{\rm d}\xi\,,\quad\ t>0,\ x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\,.

According to
(
2.1
)
and the Lévy-Khinchine formula,
each
p_{t}
is a radial probability density function and
\nu(y)\,{\rm d}y
is the Lévy measure of the semigroup,
see, e.g.,

[
4
]
.
From (
2.2
) we have

(2.3)

p_{t}(x)=t^{-d/\alpha}p_{1}(t^{-{1/\alpha}}x)\,.

It is well-known that

p_{1}(x)\approx 1\land|x|^{-d-\alpha}
(see
[
4
]
, Bogdan, Grzywny and Ryznar
[
7
, remarks after Theorem 21]
or
[
26
]
), so

(2.4)

p_{t}(x)\approx t^{-d/\alpha}\land\frac{t}{|x|^{d+\alpha}}\,,\quad t>0,\ \ x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\,.

Since
\alpha<d
, we get (the Riesz kernel)

(2.5)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{t}(x)\,{\rm d}{t}={\mathcal{A}}_{d,\alpha}|x|^{\alpha-d},\quad x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}},

where

(2.6)

{\mathcal{A}}_{d,\alpha}=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d-\alpha}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{\alpha}{2})2^{\alpha}\pi^{d/2}},

see, e.g.,
[
4
, Section 1.1.2]
.
We denote

p(t,x,y)=p_{t}(y-x),\quad t>0,\ x,y\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}.

Clearly,
p
is symmetric:

p(t,x,y)=p(t,y,x),\quad t>0,\ x,y\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}},

and satisfies the Chapman-Kolmogorov equations:

(2.7)

\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}p(s,x,y)p(t,y,z)\,{\rm d}y=p(t+s,x,z),\quad x,z\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}},\,s,t>0.

We denote, as usual,
P_{t}g(x)=\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}p(t,x,y)g(y)\,{\rm d}y
.
The fractional Laplacian extends to the generator of the semigroup
\{P_{t}\}_{t>0}
on many Banach spaces, see, e.g.,
[
26
]
.

2.3.
Schrödinger perturbation by Hardy potential

We recall
elements of the integral analysis of
[
5
]
and
[
6
]
, which was used to handle the heat kernel
\tilde{p}
of

\Delta^{\alpha/2}+\kappa|x|^{-\alpha}
. Thus,

f_{\beta}(t):=c_{\beta}t_{+}^{(d-\alpha-\beta)/\alpha},\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}},

for
\beta\in(0,d)
. The constant
c_{\beta}
is so chosen that

(2.8)

h_{\beta}(x):=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\beta}(t)p_{t}(x){\rm d}t=|x|^{-\beta},\quad x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}.

The existence of such
c_{\beta}\in(0,\infty)
follows from (
2.3
) and the estimate
p_{1}(x)\approx 1\land|x|^{-d-\alpha}
. Of course,
f_{\beta}^{\prime}(t)=c_{\beta}\frac{d-\alpha-\beta}{{\alpha}}t^{(d-2\alpha-\beta)/\alpha}
. Accordingly, for
\beta\in(0,d-\alpha)
we may define

q_{\beta}(x)=\frac{1}{h_{\beta}(x)}\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\beta}^{\prime}(t)p_{t}(x){\rm d}t,\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{d},

where
{\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{d}:={{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus\{0\}
. By
[
5
, (26)]
1
1

1

The exponent
(d-\alpha-\beta)/\alpha
in the definition of
f
is denoted
\beta
in
[
5
, Corollary 6]
.
,

q_{\beta}(x)=\kappa_{\beta}|x|^{-\alpha},

where
\kappa_{\beta}
is defined by (
1.2
).
In what follows we keep our notation from (
1.3
), that is, we let

(2.9)

\delta\in[0,(d-\alpha)/2],\quad\kappa=\kappa_{\delta},\quad h(x)=h_{\delta}(x)=|x|^{-\delta},\quad q(x)=q_{\delta}(x)=\kappa|x|^{-\alpha}.

To wit, the case of
\delta\in(0,(d-\alpha)/2]
is covered by the discussion of
\beta
above, and
\delta=0
yields the trivial
\kappa=0
,
q=0
and

.
We then define the Schrödinger perturbation of
p
by
q
:

(2.10)

\tilde{p}=\tilde{p}_{\delta}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}.

Here for
t>0
and
x,y\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
we let
p_{0}(t,x,y)=p(t,x,y)
and then proceed by induction:

(2.11)

\displaystyle p_{n}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}p(s,x,z)q(z)p_{n-1}(t-s,z,y)\,{\rm d}z\,{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}p_{n-1}(s,x,z)q(z)p(t-s,z,y)\,{\rm d}z\,{\rm d}s,\quad n\geq 1.

Of course,
\tilde{p}_{0}=p
.
By (

), for
t>0
and
y\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
we have

\displaystyle p_{1}(t,0,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}p(s,0,z)q(z)p(t-s,z,y)\,{\rm d}z\,{\rm d}s

\displaystyle\geq c_{t,y}\int_{0}^{t/2}\int_{|z|<s^{1/{\alpha}}}s^{-d/{\alpha}}|z|^{-{\alpha}}\,{\rm d}z\,{\rm d}s=\infty.

By symmetry,
p_{1}(t,x,0)=\infty
, too, for all

and

\tilde{p}(t,x,y)=\infty
if

or

.
By
[
6

, the above discussion of

and

and the usual notational conventions we have for all

,

(2.12)

\tilde{p}(t,x,y)\approx\left(t^{-d/\alpha}\wedge\frac{t}{|x-y|^{d+\alpha}}\right)\left(1+t^{\delta/\alpha}|x|^{-\delta}\right)\left(1+t^{\delta/\alpha}|y|^{-\delta}\right).

Clearly, if
0<t_{1}<t_{2}<\infty
, then

(2.13)

\tilde{p}(s,x,y)\approx\tilde{p}(t,x,y),\quad x,y\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}},\quad t_{1}\leq s,t\leq t_{2}

(the comparability

,

Recall that

(2.14)

H(x)=1\vee h(x)=1\vee|x|^{-\delta}\approx 1+|x|^{-\delta},\quad x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}.

Thus, we can reformulate (
2.12

(2.15)

\tilde{p}(t,x,y)\approx p(t,x,y)\ H(t^{-1/\alpha}x)\ H(t^{-1/\alpha}y),\qquad t>0,\quad x,y\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}.

By

6

and the above conventions,

\tilde{p}
is a symmetric time-homogeneous transition density on
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
, in particular the Chapman-Kolmogorov equations hold:

(2.16)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\tilde{p}(s,x,z)\tilde{p}(t,z,y)\,{\rm d}z=\tilde{p}(t+s,x,y)\,,\quad{x,y\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}},\ s,t>0.}

The following Duhamel formulae hold for

and

,

(2.17)

\displaystyle\tilde{p}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle=p(t,x,y)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}p(s,x,z)q(z)\tilde{p}(t-s,z,y)\,{\rm d}z\,{\rm d}s

(2.18)

\displaystyle=p(t,x,y)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\tilde{p}(s,x,z)q(z)p(t-s,z,y)\,{\rm d}z\,{\rm d}s,\quad t>0,\ x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

In passing we refer to Bogdan, Hansen and Jakubowski
[
8

and Bogdan, Jakubowski and Sydor
[
9
]
for a general setting of Schrödinger perturbations of transition semigroups and other families of integral kernels.

The function
\tilde{p}
is self-similar, i.e., has the following scaling

:

(2.19)

\tilde{p}(t,x,y)=t^{-d/\alpha}\tilde{p}\big(1,t^{-1/\alpha}x,t^{-1/\alpha}y\big),\qquad t>0,\ x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

This is the same scaling as for
p
.
Furthermore, if

is a (linear) isometry of

, then for all

,

we have

is radial. Of course,

is radial, too.
By the change of variables

and induction,

\displaystyle p_{n}(t,Tx,Ty)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}p(s,Tx,z)q(z)p_{n-1}(t-s,z,Ty)\,{\rm d}z\,{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}p(s,Tx,Tv)q(Tv)p_{n-1}(t-s,Tv,Ty)\,{\rm d}v\,{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}p(s,x,v)q(v)p_{n-1}(t-s,v,y)\,{\rm d}v\,{\rm d}s=p_{n}(t,x,y),\quad n\geq 1.

Therefore,

(2.20)

\tilde{p}(t,Tx,Ty)=\tilde{p}(t,x,y),\qquad t>0,\ x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

2.4.
Doob’s conditioning

Recall that
\delta\in[0,(d-\alpha)/2]
and
\kappa=\kappa_{\delta}
,
h(x)=h_{\delta}(x)=|x|^{-\delta}
,
\tilde{p}
depends on
\delta
.
By
[
6
, Theorem 3.1]
and the preceding discussions, the function
h
is
invariant
in the following sense:

(2.21)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\tilde{p}(t,x,y)h(y)\,{\rm d}y=h(x),\qquad t>0,\;x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\,.

We define the following Doob-conditioned (renormalized) kernel

(2.22)

\rho_{t}(x,y)=\frac{\tilde{p}(t,x,y)}{h(x)h(y)},\quad t>0,\quad x,y\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}

(later on we shall extend
\rho
to
(0,\infty)\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
).
We consider the integral weight
h^{2}(x),x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
.
Doob-type conditioning is also called Davies’ method, see Murugan and Saloff-Coste
[
35
]
, and
h
is sometimes called desingularizing weight, see Milman and Norsemen
[
32
]
.
By (
2.21
),

(2.23)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{t}(x,y)h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y=1,\quad x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}},\quad t>0.

By (
2.7
),

\displaystyle\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\rho_{s}(x,y)\rho_{t}(y,z)h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y=\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\frac{\tilde{p}(s,x,y)}{h(x)h(y)}\frac{\tilde{p}(t,y,z)}{h(y)h(z)}h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y

(2.24)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h(x)h(z)}\tilde{p}(t+s,x,z)=\rho_{t+s}(x,z),\quad x,z\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}},\,s,t>0.

We see that
\rho
is a symmetric time-homogeneous transition probability density on
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}
with the reference measure
h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y
.
For nonnegative
f\in L^{1}(h^{2})
,
by Fubini-Tonelli and (
2.23
),

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(y)\rho_{t}(x,y)h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y\,h^{2}(x)\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(y)h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y,

so the operators

\mathcal{R}_{t}f(x):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(y)\rho_{t}(x,y)\,h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y,\quad t>0,

are contractions on
L^{1}(h^{2})
.
By (
2.19
),
\rho
is self-similar: for
t>0
and
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
we have

(2.25)

\rho_{t}(x,y)=\frac{t^{-d/\alpha}\tilde{p}\big(1,t^{-1/\alpha}x,t^{-1/\alpha}y\big)}{t^{-\delta/\alpha}h(t^{-1/\alpha}x)t^{-\delta/\alpha}h(t^{-1/\alpha}y)}=t^{\frac{2\delta-d}{\alpha}}\rho_{1}\big(t^{-1/\alpha}x,t^{-1/\alpha}y\big),

hence

(2.26)

\rho_{st}(t^{1/\alpha}x,t^{1/\alpha}y)=t^{\frac{2\delta-d}{\alpha}}\rho_{s}(x,y),\quad s>0.

For each (linear) isometry
T
of
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
,

h\circ T=h
, thus by (
2.20
) we get

(2.27)

\rho_{t}(Tx,Ty)=\rho_{t}(t,x,y),\qquad t>0,\ x,y\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}.

By (
2.12
),

(2.28)

\rho_{1}(x,y)\approx\left(1\wedge{|x-y|^{-d-\alpha}}\right)\left(1+|x|^{\delta}\right)\left(1+|y|^{\delta}\right),\quad\ x,y\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}.

Notably,
\rho_{1}
is not bounded (consider large
x=y
). By (
2.25
),

(2.29)

\rho_{t}(x,y)\approx\left(t^{-d/\alpha}\wedge\frac{t}{|x-y|^{d+\alpha}}\right)\left(t^{\delta/\alpha}+|x|^{\delta}\right)\left(t^{\delta/\alpha}+|y|^{\delta}\right),\quad t>0,\ x,y\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}.

We also note that if
0<t_{1}<t_{2}<\infty
, then

(2.30)

\rho_{s}(x,y)\approx\rho_{t}(x,y),\quad x,y\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}},\quad t_{1}\leq s,t\leq t_{2}

(the comparability constant depends on
t_{1}
,
t_{2}
and
d
,
\alpha
,
\delta
).
By (
2.28
), for every
M\in(0,\infty)
,

(2.31)

\rho_{1}(x,y)\approx(1+|y|)^{-d-\alpha+\delta},\quad 0<|x|\leq M,\quad y\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}.

Clearly, the right-hand side is integrable with respect to
h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y
and bounded. Our aim is to prove the continuity of
\rho_{1}
, notably at
x=0
.

3.
Limiting behaviour at the origin

Here is a full-fledged variant of Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 3.1
.

The function
\rho
has a continuous extension to
(0,\infty)\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
and

(3.1)

\rho_{t}(0,y):=\lim_{x\to 0}\rho_{t}(x,y),\quad t>0,\quad y\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}},

satisfies:

(3.2)

\rho_{t}(0,y)=t^{\frac{2\delta-d}{\alpha}}\rho_{1}(0,t^{-1/\alpha}y),\quad t>0,\quad y\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}},

(3.3)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{t}(0,y)\rho_{s}(y,z)h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y=\rho_{t+s}(0,z),\quad s,t>0,\quad z\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}.

The proof of Theorem
3.1
is given below in this section. Let us explain the line of attack.
Given (
3.1
) and taking the limit in (
2.24
) as
x\to 0
,
we should get (
3.3
).
By (
2.25
) we should then obtain (
3.2
)
and

(3.4)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}t^{\frac{2\delta-d}{\alpha}}\rho_{1}(0,t^{-1/\alpha}y)\rho_{s}(y,z)h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y=(t+s)^{\frac{2\delta-d}{\alpha}}\rho_{1}(0,(t+s)^{-1/\alpha}z),\quad s,t>0,z\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}.

Changing variables
u=(t+s)^{-1/\alpha}z
and
x=t^{-1/\alpha}y
, we see that (
3.4
) is equivalent to

(3.5)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{1}(0,x)(t+s)^{\frac{d-2\delta}{\alpha}}\rho_{s}(t^{1/\alpha}x,(t+s)^{1/\alpha}u)h^{2}(x)\,{\rm d}x=\rho_{1}(0,u).

By
(
2.26
), this is the same as

(3.6)

\int\limits_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{\frac{s}{s+t}}\left(\left(t/(s+t)\right)^{1/\alpha}x,u\right)\rho_{1}(0,x)h^{2}(x)\,{\rm d}x=\rho_{1}(0,u).

In what follows we shall
define

\rho_{1}(0,\cdot)
as a solution to the integral equation (
3.6
) and then essentially reverse the above reasoning. Additionally, to simplify the notation and arguments
we introduce an auxiliary Ornstein-Uhlenbeck-type semigroup.

3.1.
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup

For
f\geq 0
we let

(3.7)

L_{t}f(y)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}l_{t}(x,y)f(x)h^{2}(x)\,{\rm d}x,

where

(3.8)

l_{t}(x,y)=\rho_{1-e^{-t}}(e^{-t/\alpha}x,y),\qquad t>0,\quad x,y\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}.

By (
2.24
) and (
2.26
),

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}l_{s}(x,y)l_{t}(y,z)h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{1-e^{-s}}(e^{-s/\alpha}x,y)\rho_{1-e^{-t}}(e^{-t/\alpha}y,z)h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{1-e^{-s}}(e^{-s/\alpha}x,y)(e^{-t})^{\frac{2\delta-d}{\alpha}}\rho_{e^{t}-1}(y,e^{t/\alpha}z)h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=(e^{-t})^{\frac{2\delta-d}{\alpha}}\rho_{e^{t}-e^{-s}}(e^{-s/\alpha}x,e^{t/\alpha}z)=\rho_{1-e^{-s-t}}(e^{-(s+t)/\alpha}x,z)=l_{t+s}(x,z).

Thus
l_{t}(x,y)
is a transition density on
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}

with respect to the measure
h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y
. By Fubini’s theorem,

\{L_{t}\}_{t>0}

is a semigroup of operators on
L^{1}(h^{2})
, an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck-type semigroup, see
[
39
, solution to E 18.17 on p. 462–463]
. It shall be a major technical tool in our development.

3.2.
Stationary density

If
\varphi\geq 0

and
\int\varphi(x)h^{2}(x)\,{\rm d}x=1
, then we say that
\varphi
is a
density
.
By Fubini-Tonelli Theorem and (
2.23
), for
t>0
and
f\geq 0
, we have

(3.9)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}L_{t}f(y)h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x)h^{2}(x)\,{\rm d}x.

Thus, the operators
L_{t}
preserve densities. So they are Markov, see
Komorowski
[
22
]
,
Lasota and Mackey
[
28
]
, Lasota and York
[
29
]
for this setting.

We say that a density
\varphi
is
stationary
for

L_{t}
if

L_{t}\varphi=\varphi.

Theorem 3.2
.

There is a unique stationary density
\varphi
for the operators

L_{t}
,
t>0
.

Proof.

Fix
t>0
and let
P=L_{t}

so that

P^{k}=L_{kt}
,
k=1,2,\ldots
.
By (
3.8
) and (
2.25
), for all
f\geq 0
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}

we have

(3.10)

\displaystyle P^{k}f(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(y)\rho_{1-e^{-kt}}(e^{-kt/\alpha}y,u)h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(y)(e^{-kt})^{\frac{2\delta-d}{\alpha}}\rho_{e^{kt}-1}(y,e^{kt/\alpha}u)h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y,\qquad u\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}.

Let
B=\{x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}:0<|x|\leq 1\}
. We write
f\in F
if

(3.11)

f(y)=\int_{B}\rho_{1}(x,y)\mu(\!\,{\rm d}x)

for some subprobability measure
\mu
concentrated on
B
. Then,
by (
2.24
) and (
2.25
),

\displaystyle P^{k}f(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{B}(e^{-kt})^{\frac{2\delta-d}{\alpha}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{1}(x,y)\rho_{e^{kt}-1}(y,e^{kt/\alpha}u)h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y\mu(\!\,{\rm d}x)

\displaystyle=\int_{B}(e^{-kt})^{\frac{2\delta-d}{\alpha}}\rho_{e^{kt}}(x,e^{kt/\alpha}u)\mu(\!\,{\rm d}x)

\displaystyle=\int_{B}\rho_{1}\left(e^{-kt/\alpha}x,u\right)\mu(\!\,{\rm d}x)

(3.12)

\displaystyle=\int_{B}\rho_{1}(x,u)\tilde{\mu}(\!\,{\rm d}x)\approx(1+|u|)^{-d-\alpha+\delta},\qquad u\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}},

where
\tilde{\mu}
is a subprobability measure concentrated on
e^{-kt/\alpha}B\subset B
. Thus,
P^{k}F\subset F
. We note that the comparison (
3.12
) is independent of
k
, see (
2.31
).
We next argue
that the hypotheses of
[
22
, Theorem 3.1]
hold true for
P
and
F
: (C1) – the lower bound, and (C2) – the uniform absolute continuity. Indeed, by (
3.12
),

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{B}P^{k}f(u)h^{2}(u)\,{\rm d}u\approx\int_{B}(1+|u|)^{-d-\alpha+\delta}h^{2}(u)\,{\rm d}u>0,

which yields (C1) in
[
22
]
.
Also, (C2) therein is satisfied because

\int_{A}P^{k}f(y)h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y\leq C\int_{A}(1+|y|)^{-d-\alpha+\delta}h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y\to 0,

uniformly in
k
as
\int_{A}h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y\to 0
, due to the integrability of
(1+|y|)^{-d-\alpha+2\delta}
.
By
[
22
, Theorem 3.1]
, a density
\varphi
exists satisfying
L_{t}\varphi=P\varphi=\varphi
.
Moreover, the
density
\varphi
is unique. Indeed, if
\psi
is a probability density with respect to
h^{2}(x)\,{\rm d}x
and
P\psi=\psi
, then

r:=\varphi-\psi
satisfies
Pr=r
, too. If
r=0

a.e.
, then we are done. Otherwise,
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}r_{+}(x)h^{2}(x)\,{\rm d}x=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}r_{-}(x)h^{2}(x)\,{\rm d}x>0
. Then, on the one hand
|Pr|=|r|
, on the other hand for
a.e.

x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
,

\displaystyle Pr(x)

\displaystyle=Pr_{+}(x)-Pr_{-}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}r_{+}(y)l_{t}(y,x)h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}r_{-}(y)l_{t}(y,x)h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y

and both terms are nonzero, because

l_{t}
is
positive. Therefore ,

|Pr(x)|<Pr_{+}(x)\vee Pr_{-}(x)\leq P|r|(x).

By this and (
3.9
),

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|r(x)|h^{2}(x)\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|Pr(x)|h^{2}(x)\,{\rm d}x<\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}P|r|(x)h^{2}(x)\,{\rm d}x=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|r(x)|h^{2}(x)\,{\rm d}x.

We obtain a contradiction, so
r=0
, and
\psi=\varphi
in
L^{1}(h^{2})
. In passing we note that the above argument is a part of the proof of Doob’s theorem
[
10
, Theorem 4.2.1]
.

Since the operators
L_{t}
,
t>0
, commute, they have the same stationary density, that is
\varphi
. Indeed, if
s>0
and
Q=L_{s}
, then
P(Q\varphi)=QP\varphi=(Q\varphi)
, and
Q\varphi
is a density, so by the uniqueness,
Q\varphi=\varphi
.

\Box

In what follows,
\varphi
denotes the stationary density for the operators

,

(in Lemma

3.5

below we verify that

can be defined pointwise so as to be continuous).

3.3.
Asymptotic stability

The state space
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}
is a Polish space and
l_{t}
is positive. By Theorem
3.2
and the results
of Kulik and Scheutzov
[
25
, Theorem 1 and Remark 2]
, for every
x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}
we get

(3.13)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left|l_{t}(x,y)-\varphi(y)\right|h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y\to 0\mbox{ as }t\to\infty.

This is the (large-time) asymptotic stability of the semigroup aforementioned in the title of this subsection.
Let
A
be a bounded subset of
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}
and
x,x_{0}\in A
.
By Theorem
3.2
and (
2.28
),

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left|l_{1+t}(x,y)-\varphi(y)\right|h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}l_{1}(x,z)\left(l_{t}(z,y)-\varphi(y)\right)h^{2}(z)\,{\rm d}z\right|h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y

(3.14)

\displaystyle\leq c\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}l_{1}(x_{0},z)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left|l_{t}(z,y)-\varphi(y)\right|h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y\ h^{2}(z)\,{\rm d}z.

We have
I_{t}(z):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left|l_{t}(z,y)-\varphi(y)\right|h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y\to 0
as
t\to\infty
. Furthermore, for every
z\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}
,

I_{t}(z)\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left(l_{t}(z,y)+\varphi(y)\right)h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y=2
.
Of course,
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}2l_{1}(x_{0},z)\ h^{2}(z)\,{\rm d}z=2<\infty
.
By the dominated convergence theorem, the iterated integral in (
3.14
) converges to
0
, therefore the convergence in (
3.13
) is uniform for
x\in A
. In terms of
\rho
, (
3.13
) reads as follows: uniformly in
x\in A
,

(3.15)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left|\rho_{1-e^{-t}}(e^{-t/\alpha}x,y)-\varphi(y)\right|h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y\to 0\mbox{ as }t\to\infty.

As a consequence we obtain the following spatial convergence in
L^{1}(h^{2})
.

Lemma 3.3
.

We have
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left|\rho_{1}(x,y)-\varphi(y)\right|h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y\to 0
as
x\to 0
.

Proof.

It suffices to make cosmetic changes to (
3.15
). Of course,
e^{-t/\alpha}x\to 0
and
1-e^{-t}\to 1
as
t\to\infty
. By scaling,

\rho_{1-e^{-t}}(e^{-t/\alpha}x,y)=(1-e^{-t})^{(2\delta-d)/\alpha}\rho_{1}\big((e^{t}-1)^{-1/\alpha}x,(1-e^{-t})^{-1/\alpha}y\big),

so

(3.16)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left|\rho_{1}\big((e^{t}-1)^{-1/\alpha}x,(1-e^{-t})^{-1/\alpha}y\big)-\varphi(y)\right|h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y\to 0\mbox{ as }t\to\infty.

By the continuity of dilations on
L^{1}(\!\,{\rm d}x)
,

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left|\varphi\big((1-e^{-t})^{-1/\alpha}y\big)h^{2}(y)-\varphi(y)h^{2}(y)\right|\,{\rm d}y\to 0\mbox{ as }t\to\infty.

Using the triangle inequality and changing variables in (
3.16
) we get

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left|\rho_{1}\big((e^{t}-1)^{-1/\alpha}z,y\big)-\varphi(y)\right|h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y\to 0\mbox{ as }t\to\infty

uniformly in
z\in A
for bounded
A\subset{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}
. We take
A
as the unit sphere, for
x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}
we write
x=(e^{t}-1)^{-1/\alpha}z
, where
t=\ln(1+|x|^{-\alpha})
and
z=x/|x|\in A
, and we obtain the result.

\Box

By Lemma
3.3
and (
2.31
)
we obtain the following estimate.

Corollary 3.4
.

\varphi(y)\approx(1+|y|)^{-d-\alpha+\delta}
for almost all
y\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
.

By the next result we may actually consider
{\varphi}
as defined pointwise.

Lemma 3.5
.

After modification on a set of Lebesgue measure zero,
\varphi
is a continuous radial function on
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
and
\varphi(y)\approx(1+|y|)^{-d-\alpha+\delta}
for all
y\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.2
,
{\varphi}=L_{1}{\varphi}
(
a.e.
), so it suffices to prove that

L_{1}{\varphi}
is
a.e.
equal to a continuous function on
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
.
By (
2.30
) and (
2.31
),
c(1+|y|)^{-d-\alpha+\delta}
is an integrable majorant of
l_{1}(x,y)
for bounded
x
. Furthermore,
{\varphi}
is essentially bounded (
a.e.
), by Corollary
3.4
.
The function
l_{1}(x,y)
is continuous in
y\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}
so, by the dominated convergence theorem,
L_{1}{\varphi}(y)
is continuous for
y\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}
.
It remains to prove
the convergence of

L_{1}{\varphi}(y)
to a finite limit as
y\to 0
.
We let
t>0
and
y\to 0
. By scaling, changing variables, the symmetry of
\rho_{1}
and Lemma
3.3
,

\displaystyle L_{t}{\varphi}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}\rho_{1-e^{-t}}\big(e^{-t/\alpha}x,y\big){\varphi}(x)h^{2}(x)\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}e^{t(d-2\delta)/\alpha}\rho_{1}\big(z,(1-e^{-t/\alpha})^{1/\alpha}y\big){\varphi}\big((e^{t}-1)^{1/\alpha}z\big)h^{2}(z)\,{\rm d}z

\displaystyle\to\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}e^{t(d-2\delta)/\alpha}{\varphi}(z){\varphi}\big((e^{t}-1)^{1/\alpha}z\big)h^{2}(z)\,{\rm d}z

(3.17)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}{\varphi}((e^{-t})^{1/\alpha}x){\varphi}\big((1-e^{-t})^{1/\alpha}x\big)h^{2}(x)\,{\rm d}x<\infty.

Since
-2d-2\alpha+2\delta<-d-3\alpha<-d
, the finiteness in (
3.17
) follows from Corollary
3.4
.
This proves the continuity of the extension of
L_{1}{\varphi}
on the whole of
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
. The rest of the lemma follows from the continuity, Lemma
3.3
and (
2.27
), and from Corollary
3.4
.

\Box

Needless to say, the continuous modification of
{\varphi}
is unique and pointwise defined for every
x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
. From now on the extension shall be denoted by
{\varphi}
.
By the equality in (
3.17
),

(3.18)

{\varphi}(0)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}{\varphi}\big(\lambda^{1/\alpha}x\big){\varphi}\big((1-\lambda)^{1/\alpha}x\big)h^{2}(x)\,{\rm d}x

for every
\lambda\in[0,1]
, including the endpoint cases, since
\varphi
is a density.
In particular,

(3.19)

{\varphi}(0)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\varphi}(2^{-1/\alpha}x)^{2}h^{2}(x)\,{\rm d}x.

3.4.
Regularization of
\rho

We are now in a position to prove
convergence of
\rho_{1}(x,y)
as
x\to 0
to a finite limit. By scaling, Chapman-Kolmogorov, Lemma
3.3
and the boundedness of
{\varphi}
, for
y\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}
and
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}\ni x\to 0
we get

\displaystyle\rho_{1}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{\frac{d-2\delta}{\alpha}}\rho_{2}(2^{1/\alpha}x,2^{1/\alpha}y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{\frac{d-2\delta}{\alpha}}\int\rho_{1}(2^{1/\alpha}x,z)\rho_{1}(z,2^{1/\alpha}y)h^{2}(z)\,{\rm d}z

\displaystyle\to

Lemma 4.2

\displaystyle\leq\|P_{t}f-P_{t}f_{n}\|_{L^{1}(h)}+\|P_{t}f_{n}-f_{n}\|_{L^{1}(h)}+\|f_{n}-f\|_{L^{1}(h)}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|f-f_{n}\|_{L^{1}(h)}+\|P_{t}f_{n}-f_{n}\|_{L^{1}(h)}.

Lemma 4.7

\|\tilde{P}_{t}f\|_{\infty,h}\leq\|f\|_{\infty,h}
.

Proof.

For
x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}
we have
|\tilde{P}_{t}f(x)\,{\rm d}x|\leq\|f\|_{\infty,h}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\tilde{P}_{t}(x,y)h(y)\,{\rm d}y\leq h(x)\|f\|_{\infty,h}
.

\Box

5.
Asymptotic behavior for large time

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.2
. For the sake of
comparison let us
discuss the following classical analogue of
(
1.9
),

(5.1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u(x,t)=\left(\Delta+\kappa|x|^{-2}\right)u(x,t),\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,t>0,\\
u(x,0)=f(x),\end{cases}

where
d\geq 3
and
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}
. As we already mentioned
in Section
1.2
, the Cauchy problem (
5.1
) was popularized by Baras and Goldstein
[
1
]
, who discovered that (
5.1
) has no positive local-in-time solutions if

\kappa>(d-2)^{2}/4
, which is called the instantaneous blow up. See also Goldstein and Kombe
[
17
]
for a simple proof via Harnack inequality.
Vázquez and Zuazua
[
42
]
then studied the large time behavior of solutions to (
5.1
) with
0<\kappa\leq(d-2)^{2}/4
. Using a weighted version of the Hardy-Poincaré inequality, they proved in
[
42
, Theorem 10.3]
the stabilization of some solutions toward the following self-similar solution of (
5.1
)

V(x,t)=t^{\sigma-\frac{d}{2}}|x|^{-\sigma}{\text{\rm{e}}}^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{4t}},

where
\sigma=\frac{d-2}{2}-\sqrt{(d-2)^{2}/4-\kappa}
. Then, Pilarczyk
[
37
]
proved that if
u=u(t,x)
is the solution of (
5.1
) with
\kappa\in\big(-\infty,(d-2)^{2}/4\big)
, then for every
1\leq q\leq\infty
,

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}t^{\frac{d}{2}(1-\frac{1}{q})-\frac{\sigma}{2}}\|u(\cdot,t)-AV(\cdot,t)\|_{q,{\varphi}_{\sigma}(t)}=0.

Here
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\cap L^{1}(|x|^{-\sigma}\,{\rm d}x)
,

A=\frac{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{-\sigma}f(x)\,{\rm d}x}{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{-2\sigma}{\text{\rm{e}}}^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{4}}\,{\rm d}x},

{\varphi}_{\sigma}(x,t)=1\vee({\sqrt{t}}/{|x|})^{\sigma}
, and

\displaystyle\|g\|_{q,{\varphi}_{\sigma}(t)}=\begin{cases}\Big(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|g(x)|^{q}{\varphi}_{\sigma}^{2-q}(x,t)\,{\rm d}x\Big)^{1/q}&\textrm{for}\quad 1\leq q<\infty,\\
{\rm ess}\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|g(x)|/{\varphi}_{\sigma}(x,t)&\text{for}\quad q=\infty.\end{cases}

In Theorem
1.2
we extend the results of
Vázquez and Zuazua
[
42
]
and Pilarczyk
[
37
]

to the Cauchy problem (
1.9
). In fact we also propose a novel description of the asymptotics, using simpler, time-independent norms
\|f\|_{q,h}
defined in (
1.4
).

We now return to the setting of (
1.9
) and (
2.9
). The solutions to (
1.9
) will be defined as
u(t,x)=\tilde{P}_{t}f(x)
,
t>0
,
x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
for
f\in L^{1}(H)
. By Theorem
1.1
,

\displaystyle\Psi_{t}(x):=\lim_{y\to 0}\frac{\tilde{p}(t,x,y)}{h(y)}=\rho_{t}(0,x)h(x)=t^{\frac{2\delta-d}{\alpha}}{\varphi}\big(t^{-1/\alpha}x\big)h(x),\quad t>0,\quad x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}.

In particular,
\Psi_{t}(0)=\infty
. Since, for
s>0
,

\displaystyle\rho_{t+s}(0,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{t}(0,y)\rho_{s}(y,x)h^{2}(y)\,{\rm d}y=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{t}(0,y)h(y)\tilde{p}(s,y,x)/h(x)\,{\rm d}y,

we get the following evolution property

(5.2)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\tilde{p}(s,y,x)\Psi_{t}(y)\,{\rm d}y=\Psi_{t+s}(x).

By (
3.28
) we also have

(5.3)

\Psi_{t}(x)=t^{\frac{\delta-d}{\alpha}}\Psi_{1}(t^{-1/\alpha}x),\quad t>0,\quad x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}.

We summarize (
5.2
) and (
5.3
) by saying that
\Psi_{t}(x)
is a self-similar semigroup solution of (
1.9
). By (
3.23
),

(5.4)

\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\Psi_{t}(x)h(x)\,{\rm d}x=\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\Psi_{1}(x)h(x)\,{\rm d}x=1.

Furthermore,
\Psi_{t}(x)
is a mild solution of (
1.9
) since it satisfies the following Duhamel formula.

Lemma 5.1
.

(5.5)

\displaystyle\Psi_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\Psi_{r}(z)q(z)p(t-r,x,z)\,{\rm d}{z}\,{\rm d}{r},\quad t>0,\;x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}.

Proof.

By (
2.12
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{p}(t,x,y)}{h(y)}\approx(|y|^{\delta}+t^{\delta/\alpha})(1+t^{\delta/\alpha}|x|^{-\delta})p(t,x,y),

hence

(5.6)

\displaystyle\Psi_{t}(x)\approx t^{\delta/\alpha}(1+t^{\delta/\alpha}|x|^{-\delta})\big(t^{-d/\alpha}\land\tfrac{t}{|x|^{d+\alpha}}\big),\qquad t>0,\;x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}.

Now, by (
5.2
) and (
2.17
) for
0<s<t
we obtain

\displaystyle\Psi_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tilde{p}(t-s,x,y)\Psi_{s}(y)\,{\rm d}y

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t-s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(r,x,y)q(y)\tilde{p}(t-s-r,y,z)\Psi_{s}(z)\,{\rm d}z\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}r

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(t-s,x,y)\Psi_{s}(y)\,{\rm d}y+\int_{0}^{t-s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(r,x,y)q(y)\Psi_{t-r}(,y)\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}r

(5.7)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(t-s,x,y)\Psi_{s}(y)\,{\rm d}y+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(t-r,x,y)q(y)\Psi_{r}(y)\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}r.

Using (
5.6
) and (
5.3
), we get

\displaystyle 0\leq\limsup_{s\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(t-s,x,z)\Psi_{s}(z)\,{\rm d}{z}

\displaystyle\leq c\limsup_{s\to 0}(t-s)^{-d/\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Psi_{s}(z)\,{\rm d}{z}

\displaystyle=c\limsup_{s\to 0}(t-s)^{-d/\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}s^{(\delta-d)/\alpha}\Psi_{1}(s^{-1/\alpha}z)\,{\rm d}{z}

\displaystyle=ct^{-d/\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Psi_{1}(z)\,{\rm d}{z}\lim_{s\to 0}s^{\delta/\alpha}=0.

Therefore, taking
s\to 0
in (
5.7
), we obtain (
5.5
).

\Box

Remark 5.5
.

By Lemma
4.1

for all

If we multiply (
1.9

h
and integrate, then indeed we get

\frac{\ d}{\ dt}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{-\delta}u(t,x)\,{\rm d}x=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(-\Delta)^{\nicefrac{{\alpha}}{{2}}}u(t,x)|x|^{-\delta}\,{\rm d}x+\kappa_{\delta}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{-\delta-\alpha}u(t,x)\,{\rm d}x.

Using the Fourier symbol of
\Delta^{\alpha/2}
, Parseval’s relation and (
2.9
) we get

\displaystyle\frac{\ d}{\ dt}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{-\delta}u(t,x)\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=-\frac{2^{\alpha}\pi^{\alpha+\delta-\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}}\Gamma\left(\frac{d-\delta}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|\xi|^{\alpha+\delta-d}\hat{u}(t,\xi)\,{\rm d}\xi+\kappa_{\delta}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{-\delta-\alpha}u(t,x)\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\Big(\kappa_{\delta}-\kappa_{\delta}\Big)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{-\delta-\alpha}u(t,x)\,{\rm d}x=0,

see
[
18
, Theorem 2.2.14 p.102]
and
[
40
, Lemma 2 p.117]
.

Recall that
\tilde{P}_{t}
is defined in (
1.5
). The next lemma is crucial for the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Lemma 5.6
.

If
f\in L^{1}(H)
,
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x)h(x)\,{\rm d}x=0
and
1\leq q<\infty
, then

(5.12)

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}t^{\frac{d-2\delta}{\alpha}(1-\frac{1}{q})}\|\tilde{P}_{t}f\|_{q,h}=0.

If, additionally,
f
has compact support, then (
5.12
) is true for
q=\infty
, too.

Proof.

First, we consider a compactly supported function
\psi
such that
\psi\in L^{1}(H)
and

(5.13)

\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\psi(x)h(x)\,{\rm d}x=0,

and we intend to prove (
5.12
) with
f
replaced by
\psi
.

Step 1. Case
q=\infty
.

By the definition of the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty,h}
,

\displaystyle I(t)

\displaystyle:=t^{\frac{d-2\delta}{\alpha}}\|\tilde{P}_{t}\psi\|_{\infty,h}=t^{\frac{d-2\delta}{\alpha}}\sup_{x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\left|\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\rho_{t}(x,y)h(y)\psi(y)\,{\rm d}y\right|.

Using the condition (
5.13
), we obtain

\displaystyle I(t)=t^{\frac{d-2\delta}{\alpha}}\sup_{x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\left|\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\left(\rho_{t}(x,y)-\rho_{t}(x,0)\right)h(y)\psi(y)\,{\rm d}y\right|.

We fix
\omega>0
. Since
\psi
has a compact support, for sufficiently large
t>0
we get

\displaystyle I(t)

\displaystyle=t^{\frac{d-2\delta}{\alpha}}\sup_{x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\left|\int_{|y|\leq t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\omega}\left(\rho_{t}(x,y)-\rho_{t}(x,0)\right)h(y)\psi(y)\,{\rm d}y\right|

\displaystyle\leq t^{\frac{d-2\delta}{\alpha}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\\
|y|\leq\omega t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\end{subarray}}\big|\rho_{t}(x,y)-\rho_{t}(x,0)\big|\int_{|y|\leq\omega t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}h(y)|\psi(y)|\,{\rm d}y,

so by scaling of
\rho
,

\displaystyle I(t)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\\
|y|\leq\omega t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\end{subarray}}\left|\rho_{1}\left(t^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}x,t^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}y\right)-\rho_{1}\left(t^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}x,0\right)\right|\int_{|y|\leq t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\omega}h(y)|\psi(y)|\,{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\\
|y|\leq\omega\end{subarray}}\left|\rho_{1}\left(x,y\right)-\rho_{1}\left(x,0\right)\right|\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}h(y)|\psi(y)|\,{\rm d}y.

By Lemma
3.6
and (
2.31
) we choose
\omega
small enough to have

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\\
|y|\leq\omega\end{subarray}}\left|\rho_{1}\left(x,y\right)-\rho_{1}\left(x,0\right)\right|<{\varepsilon}.

This proves (
5.12
) in the considered setting.

Step 2. Case
q=1
.

By the definition of the norm
\|\cdot\|_{1,h}
,

J(t):=\|\tilde{P}_{t}\psi\|_{1,h}=\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\left|\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\tilde{p}(t,x,y)h(x)\psi(y)\,{\rm d}y\right|\,{\rm d}x=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\Big|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{t}(x,y)h^{2}(x)\psi(y)h(y)\,{\rm d}y\Big|\,{\rm d}x.

Applying (
5.13
), we get

J(t)\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\big|\rho_{t}(x,y)-\rho_{t}(x,0)\big|h^{2}(x)|\psi(y)|h(y)\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}x.

We fix
\omega>0
and notice that

\displaystyle J(t)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\int_{|y|\leq\omega t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\big|\rho_{t}(x,y)-\rho_{t}(x,0)\big|h^{2}(x)|\psi(y)|h(y)\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}x,

for sufficiently large
t
, since the function
\psi
has a compact support. Then

\displaystyle J(t)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{|y|\leq\omega t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\|\rho_{t}(\cdot,y)-\rho_{t}(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{1}(h^{2})}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}h(y)|\psi(y)|\,{\rm d}y.

By scaling of
\rho
, substituting
x=t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}z
we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{|y|\leq\omega t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\|\rho_{t}(\cdot,y)-\rho_{t}(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{1}(h^{2})}

\displaystyle=t^{\frac{2\delta-d}{\alpha}}\sup_{|y|\leq\omega t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\int\left|\rho_{1}\left(t^{-{1}/{\alpha}}x,t^{-{1}/{\alpha}}y\right)-\rho_{1}\left(t^{-{1}/{\alpha}}x,0\right)\right|h^{2}(x)\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\sup_{|y|<\omega}\|\rho_{1}(\cdot,y)-\rho_{1}(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{1}(h^{2})}.

Applying Lemma
3.3
, for
\omega>0
sufficiently small we get

J(t)\leq{\varepsilon}\|\psi\|_{L^{1}(h)}.

Step 3. Case
q\in(1,\infty)
.

By the definition of the norm
\|\cdot\|_{q,h}
and Hölder inequality,

\displaystyle t^{\frac{d-2\delta}{\alpha}(1-\frac{1}{q})}\|\tilde{P}_{t}\psi\|_{q,h}

\displaystyle=t^{\frac{d-2\delta}{\alpha}(1-\frac{1}{q})}\bigg(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\big|\tilde{P}_{t}\psi(x)/h(x)\big|^{q-1}\big|\tilde{P}_{t}\psi(x)h(x)\big|\,{\rm d}x\bigg)^{\frac{1}{q}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(t^{\frac{d-2\delta}{\alpha}}\|\tilde{P}_{t}\psi\|_{\infty,h}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{q}}\left(|\tilde{P}_{t}\psi\|_{1,h}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}.

Both factors converge to zero as
t\rightarrow\infty
by
Step 1.
and
Step 2.

Step 4. General initial datum.

Let
R>0
,
c_{R}=\int_{|x|\leq R}f(x)h(x)\,{\rm d}x/\int_{|x|\leq R}h(x)\,{\rm d}x
and
\psi_{R}(x)=(f(x)-c_{R})\mathbf{1}_{|x|\leq R}
. Of course,
\psi_{R}
is
compactly supported and

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}h(x)\psi_{R}(x)\,{\rm d}x=0.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\|f-\psi_{R}\|_{L^{1}(h)}

\displaystyle=|c_{R}|\int_{|x|\leq R}h(x)\,{\rm d}x+\int_{|x|>R}h(x)|f(x)|\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\Big|\int_{|x|\leq R}h(x)f(x)\,{\rm d}x\Big|+\int_{|x|>R}h(x)|f(x)|\,{\rm d}x\to 0

as
R\rightarrow\infty
, due to the assumption (
5.13
) and the condition
f\in L^{1}(h)
. Let
{\varepsilon}>0
and choose
R>0
so large that

\|f-\psi_{R}\|_{1,h}<{\varepsilon}.

For
q=1
, by using the triangle inequality and Lemma
4.1
, we have

\displaystyle\|\tilde{P}_{t}f\|_{1,h}

\displaystyle\leq\|\tilde{P}_{t}\psi_{R}\|_{1,h}+\|\tilde{P}_{t}(f-\psi_{R})\|_{1,h}

\displaystyle\leq\|\tilde{P}_{t}\psi_{R}\|_{1,h}+\|f-\psi_{R}\|_{1,h}.

Hence, by
Step 2.
of this proof we get

(5.14)

\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|\tilde{P}_{t}f\|_{1,h}\leq{\varepsilon},

which completes the verification of (
5.12
) in this case.

If
1<q<\infty
, then using the triangle inequality and Theorem
4.6
, we obtain

\displaystyle t^{\frac{d-2\delta}{\alpha}(1-\frac{1}{q})}\|\tilde{P}_{t}f\|_{q,h}

\displaystyle\leq t^{\frac{d-2\delta}{\alpha}(1-\frac{1}{q})}\|\tilde{P}_{t}\psi_{R}\|_{q,h}+t^{\frac{d-2\delta}{\alpha}(1-\frac{1}{q})}\|\tilde{P}_{t}(f-\psi_{R})\|_{q,h}

\displaystyle\leq t^{\frac{d-2\delta}{\alpha}(1-\frac{1}{q})}\|\tilde{P}_{t}\psi_{R}\|_{q,h}+C\|f-\psi_{R}\|_{1,h}+Ct^{-\frac{\delta}{\alpha}}\|f-\psi_{R}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}.

By
Step 3.
of this proof,

(5.15)

\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}t^{\frac{d-2\delta}{\alpha}(1-\frac{1}{q})}\|\tilde{P}_{t}f\|_{q,h}\leq 2C{\varepsilon},

which is valid both for
\delta>0
and
\delta=0
.
This completes the proof of (
5.12
) for
q\in(1,\infty)
.

\Box

1.2
immediately after the following discussion of

to (
1.9
).

Theorem
1.2
is optimal, as asserted by the following two observations.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
q\in[1,\infty)
and
\tau:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be increasing with
\lim_{t\to\infty}\tau(t)=\infty
. Then there is
f\in L^{1}(H)
such that
\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}f(x)h(x)\,{\rm d}x=1
and
u:=\tilde{P}_{t}f
satisfies

(5.17)

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\tau(t)t^{\frac{d-2\delta}{\alpha}(1-\frac{1}{q})}\|u(t,\cdot)-\Psi_{t}\|_{q,h}=\infty.

Proof.

The proof builds on the fact that
\|\Psi_{1}-\Psi_{s}\|_{q,h}>0
for
s>1
, which, as we shall see below, is a consequence of the scaling of
\Psi_{t}(x)
.

Without loss of generality we may assume that
\tau
is continuous, strictly increasing and
\tau(0)=0
,
by replacing
\tau(t)
by
t/(t+1)\left[t/(t+1)+\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\tau(s)ds\right]\leq\tau(t)+1
, see also (
1.6
). In particular,
\tau^{-1}
is well defined
:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
.
For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
we let
t_{n}=\tau^{-1}(2^{2n})
.
Let

f=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}\Psi_{t_{n}}
.
According to (
5.10
),

\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}f(x)h(x)\,{\rm d}x=\|f\|_{1,h}=1
. By (
5.2
),

u(t,x):=\tilde{P}_{t}f(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}\Psi_{t+t_{n}}(x)\geq 0,\quad t>0,\ x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}.

We remark that if
\tau(t)\geq 1
, then
t_{n}\geq t
is equivalent to

n\geq\left\lceil{\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}\tau(t)}\right\rceil
. In this case,
by (
5.9
), and triangle inequality, we get

(5.18)

\displaystyle t^{\frac{d-2\delta}{\alpha}(1-\frac{1}{q})}\|u(t,\cdot)-\Psi_{t}\|_{q,h}=\|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}\Psi_{1+t_{n}/t}-\Psi_{1}\|_{q,h}

\displaystyle\geq\|\Psi_{1}\|_{q,h}-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}\|\Psi_{1+t_{n}/t}\|_{q,h}=\|\Psi_{1}\|_{q,h}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}\left(1-(1+t_{n}/t)^{-\frac{d-2\delta}{\alpha}(1-\frac{1}{q})}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\|\Psi_{1}\|_{q,h}\left(1-2^{-\frac{d-2\delta}{\alpha}(1-\frac{1}{q})}\right)\sum_{t_{n}\geq t}^{\infty}2^{-n}=\|\Psi_{1}\|_{q,h}\left(1-2^{-\frac{d-2\delta}{\alpha}(1-\frac{1}{q})}\right)2^{1-\left\lceil{\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}\tau(t)}\right\rceil}

\displaystyle\geq\|\Psi_{1}\|_{q,h}\left(1-2^{-\frac{d-2\delta}{\alpha}(1-\frac{1}{q})}\right)\tau(t)^{-1/2}.

The case of
q\in(1,\infty)
in (
5.17
) is resolved, because
1-2^{-\frac{d-2\delta}{\alpha}(1-\frac{1}{q})}>0
in this case.

We next consider
q=1
. Then, starting from (
5.18
),
we get

\displaystyle\|u(t,\cdot)-\Psi_{t}\|_{1,h}

\displaystyle=\|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}\Psi_{1+t_{n}/t}-\Psi_{1}\|_{1,h}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{B(0,1)}|\Psi_{1}(x)-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}\Psi_{1+t_{n}/t}(x)|h(x)\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\geq\int_{B(0,1)}\left(\Psi_{1}(x)-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}\Psi_{1+t_{n}/t}(x)\right)h(x)\,{\rm d}x

(5.19)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}\left(\int_{B(0,1)}\Psi_{1}(x)h(x)\,{\rm d}x-\int_{B(0,1)}\Psi_{1+t_{n}/t}(x)h(x)\,{\rm d}x\right).

By the same change of variables as in (
5.8
), for all
t>0
and
U\subset{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
we get

(5.20)

\displaystyle\int_{U}\Psi_{t}(x)h(x)\,{\rm d}x=

\displaystyle\int_{t^{-1/\alpha}U}\Psi_{1}(x)h(x)\,{\rm d}x.

Therefore by (
5.19
),

\displaystyle\|u(t,\cdot)-\Psi_{t}\|_{1,h}\geq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}\int_{B(0,1)\setminus B(0,(1+t_{n}/t)^{-1/\alpha})}\Psi_{1}(x)h(x)\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\geq\int_{B(0,1)\setminus B(0,2^{-1/\alpha})}\Psi_{1}(x)h(x)\,{\rm d}x\sum_{t_{n}\geq t}^{\infty}2^{-n}.

By (
5.6
),
\int_{B(0,1)\setminus B(0,2^{-1/\alpha})}\Psi_{1}(x)h(x)\,{\rm d}x>0
, and we conclude as before.
\Box

Remark 5.9
.

We note that (
1.6
) does not hold for
q=\infty
. Indeed, let
f_{n}(x)={\bf 1}_{B(x_{n},1)}(x)
, where
\{x_{n}\}
is a sequence in
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
such that
|x_{n}|=2^{n}
. Then, by (
2.4
), for
t>1
we have

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{t}f_{n}(x_{n})\geq\int_{B(x_{n},1)}p(t,x_{n},y)\,{\rm d}{y}\geq ct^{-d/\alpha}.

Let
f(x)=\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}f_{n}(x)
. Clearly
f\in L^{1}(h)
and
\tilde{P}_{t}f(x_{n})\geq ct^{-d/\alpha}
.
Moreover, by (
5.6
) and (
2.4
),
\Psi_{t}(x_{n})\leq ct^{\delta/\alpha}(1+t^{-\delta/\alpha}|x_{n}|^{-\delta})t|x_{n}|^{-d-\alpha}
. Hence for every
t>1
we actually have,

\displaystyle\|u(t,\cdot)-A\Psi_{t}\|_{\infty,h}=\sup_{x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}|\tilde{P}_{t}f(x)-A\Psi_{t}(x)|/h(x)\geq c_{t}\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(|x_{n}|^{\delta}-|x_{n}|^{\delta-d-\alpha})=\infty.

6.
The potential of the self-similar solution

In this section we assume that
0\leq\kappa\leq\kappa^{*}
, except that on several occasions we explicitly exclude the critical case
\kappa=\kappa^{*}
.
As usual,
\kappa
and
\delta
are related by (
2.9
).

If

\kappa<\kappa^{*}
then, by (
5.3
) and (
5.6
), there is
c\in(0,\infty)
, such that

\displaystyle c\int_{0}^{\infty}\Psi_{s}(x)\,{\rm d}s=|x|^{\delta+\alpha-d},\qquad x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y)|y|^{\delta+\alpha-d}\,{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=c\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\Psi_{s}(y)\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y)\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}s=c\int_{0}^{\infty}\Psi_{t+s}(x)\,{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=c\int_{0}^{\infty}\Psi_{s}(x)\,{\rm d}s-c\int_{0}^{t}\Psi_{s}(x)\,{\rm d}s=|x|^{\delta+\alpha-d}-c\int_{0}^{t}\Psi_{s}(x)\,{\rm d}s.

Hence, for
t>0
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
,

\displaystyle c\int_{0}^{t}\Psi_{s}(x)\,{\rm d}s=|x|^{\delta+\alpha-d}-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y)|y|^{\delta+\alpha-d}\,{\rm d}y.

The main goal of this section is to calculate the constant
c
, and derive a similar formula for
\kappa=\kappa^{*}
(see Corollary
6.3
).

Lemma 6.1
.

For
t>0
and
x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
we have

\displaystyle\Psi_{t}(x)=\lim_{\beta\to 0^{+}}\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{2\pi^{d/2}}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\beta|z|^{\beta+\delta-d}\tilde{p}(t,x,z)\,{\rm d}{z}.

Proof.

Let
x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}
. For
z\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}
and
\beta>0
, put
f_{\beta}(z)=\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{2\pi^{d/2}}\beta|z|^{\beta-d}
. Let
\varepsilon>0
and
\beta\to 0
. We have

\displaystyle\beta\int_{B(0,\varepsilon)}|z|^{\beta-d}\,{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=d|B(0,1)|\beta\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}r^{\beta-d}r^{d-1}\,{\rm d}{r}=d|B(0,1)|\varepsilon^{\beta}\longrightarrow\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}.

Furthermore, by (
2.12
) and the dominated convergence theorem for every
x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}
we get

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\to 0^{+}}\beta\int_{|z|>\varepsilon}\frac{\tilde{p}(t,x,z)}{h(z)}|z|^{\beta-d}\,{\rm d}{z}=0.

Then, since
z\mapsto\tilde{p}(t,x,z)/h(z)
has a continuous extension to
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
with the value
\Psi_{t}(x)
at the origin, we get

\displaystyle\Psi_{t}(x)=\lim_{\beta\to 0^{+}}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\frac{\tilde{p}(t,x,z)}{h(z)}f_{\beta}(z)\,{\rm d}{z}.

The result follows (in the case of
x=0
, the statement is trivial).

\Box

Lemma 6.2
.

For
t>0
and
x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\Psi_{s}(x)\,{\rm d}{s}=\lim_{\beta\to 0^{+}}\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{2\pi^{d/2}}\frac{\beta}{\kappa_{\delta+\beta}-\kappa_{\delta}}\left(|x|^{-(d-\delta-\alpha-\beta)}-\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\tilde{p}(t,x,z)|z|^{-(d-\delta-\alpha-\beta)}\,{\rm d}{z}\right).

Proof.

Fix
x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}
and
t>0
. By (
2.4
), there is a constant
c=c(d,\alpha)
such that

\displaystyle\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}p(s,x,z)|z|^{\gamma-d}\,{\rm d}{z}\leq\int_{|z|>|x|/2}p(s,x,z)(|x|/2)^{\gamma-d}\,{\rm d}{z}+c\int_{|z|\leq|x|/2}\frac{s}{|x|^{d+\alpha}}|z|^{\gamma-d}\,{\rm d}{z}

\displaystyle\leq(|x|/2)^{\gamma-d}+\frac{c}{\gamma}t|x|^{\gamma-d-\alpha}\leq c\frac{t+1}{\gamma}\left(1+|x|^{-d-\alpha}\right),\quad s\in(0,t),\;\gamma\in(0,d).

Then, by (
2.15
), there is
c^{\prime}=c^{\prime}(d,\alpha,t)
such that

\displaystyle\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\beta\tilde{p}(s,x,z)|z|^{\beta+\delta-d}\,{\rm d}{z}

\displaystyle\leq c^{\prime}H(x)\beta\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}p(s,x,z)(|z|^{\beta+\delta-d}+|z|^{\beta-d})\,{\rm d}{z}

\displaystyle\leq c^{\prime}H(x)\left(1+|x|^{-d-\alpha}\right),\quad\beta\in(0,d-\delta).

Therefore, by Lemma
6.1
, the dominated convergence theorem and
[
6
, Theorem 3.1]
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\Psi_{s}(x)\,{\rm d}{s}=\lim_{\beta\to 0^{+}}\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{2\pi^{d/2}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\beta\tilde{p}(s,x,z)|z|^{\beta+\delta-d}\,{\rm d}{z}\,{\rm d}{s}

(6.1)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\beta\to 0^{+}}\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{2\pi^{d/2}}\frac{\beta}{\kappa_{d-\delta-\alpha-\beta}-\kappa_{\delta}}\left(|x|^{-(d-\delta-\alpha-\beta)}-\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\tilde{p}(t,x,z)|z|^{-(d-\delta-\alpha-\beta)}\,{\rm d}{z}\right).

The result follows from the symmetry of
\kappa
.

\Box

Corollary 6.3
.

For
0\leq\delta<\frac{d-\alpha}{2}
, we have

(6.2)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\Psi_{s}(x)\,{\rm d}{s}=\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{2\pi^{d/2}\kappa^{\prime}_{\delta}}\left(|x|^{-(d-\delta-\alpha)}-\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\tilde{p}(t,x,z)|z|^{-(d-\delta-\alpha)}\,{\rm d}{z}\right),\quad t>0,\,x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}.

For
\delta=\frac{d-\alpha}{2}
,

(6.3)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\Psi_{s}(x)\,{\rm d}{s}=\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{\pi^{d/2}\kappa_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}}\left(|x|^{-\delta}\ln|x|-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\tilde{p}(t,z,x)|z|^{-\delta}\ln|z|\,{\rm d}{z}\right),\quad t>0,\,x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}.

Here,
\kappa_{\delta}^{\prime}
and
\kappa_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}
are the first and the second derivatives of
\kappa_{\delta}
, respectively.

Proof.

For
0\leq\delta<\frac{d-\alpha}{2}
the statement follows directly from Lemma
6.2
, (
2.12
) and the dominated convergence theorem. For
\delta=\frac{d-\alpha}{2}
, we have
\kappa_{\delta}^{\prime}=0
and

(6.4)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\tilde{p}(t,x,z)|z|^{\delta+\alpha-d}\,{\rm d}{z}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\tilde{p}(t,x,z)|z|^{-\delta}\,{\rm d}{z}=|x|^{-\delta}=|x|^{\delta+\alpha-d},

see (
2.21
), so the difference in the parentheses on the right hand side of (
6.1
) tends to
0
.
So, as
\beta\to 0
, we use (
6.4
), (
2.12
) and the dominated convergence theorem, obtaining

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\Psi_{s}(x)\,{\rm d}{s}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\beta\to 0^{+}}\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{2\pi^{d/2}}\frac{\beta^{2}}{\kappa_{\delta+\beta}-\kappa^{*}}\left(\frac{|x|^{\beta-\delta}-|x|^{-\delta}}{\beta}-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\tilde{p}(t,x,z)\frac{|z|^{\beta-\delta}-|z|^{-\delta}}{\beta}\,{\rm d}{z}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{\pi^{d/2}\kappa_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}}\left(|x|^{-\delta}\ln|x|-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\tilde{p}(t,x,z)|z|^{-\delta}\ln|z|\,{\rm d}{z}\right).

\Box

Theorem 6.4
.

For
\delta\in[0,\frac{d-\alpha}{2})
and
x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
we have

(6.5)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\Psi_{s}(x)\,{\rm d}{s}=\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{2\pi^{d/2}\kappa_{\delta}^{\prime}}|x|^{\delta+\alpha-d},

and

(6.6)

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\infty}\Psi_{s}(x)\,{\rm d}{s}=\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{2\pi^{d/2}\kappa_{\delta}^{\prime}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\tilde{p}(t,x,z)|z|^{\delta+\alpha-d}\,{\rm d}z,\quad t>0.

For
\delta=(d-\alpha)/2
,

(6.7)

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\infty}\Psi_{s}(x)\,{\rm d}{s}=\infty,\quad x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}},\quad t\geq 0.

Proof.

We shall prove (
6.5
) by letting
t\to\infty
in (
6.2
). To this end let
x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}
and
T>1
. By (
6.2
),
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\tilde{p}(t,x,z)|z|^{\delta+\alpha-d}\,{\rm d}z
is finite and decreases
as
t
increases to
\infty
. Hence, for every
\varepsilon>0
there is
R>0
such that for every
t>T
,

(6.8)

\displaystyle\int_{B(0,R)^{c}}\tilde{p}(t,x,z)|z|^{\delta+\alpha-d}\,{\rm d}z\leq\int_{B(0,R)^{c}}\tilde{p}(T,x,z)|z|^{\delta+\alpha-d}\,{\rm d}z<\varepsilon.

By (
2.12
), for
t>T
we get

\displaystyle|z|^{\delta+\alpha-d}\tilde{p}(t,x,z)

\displaystyle\leq c|z|^{\delta+\alpha-d}(1+t^{\delta/\alpha}|z|^{-\delta})(1+t^{\delta/\alpha}|x|^{-\delta})t^{-d/\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq c|z|^{\delta+\alpha-d}(1+|z|^{-\delta})(1+|x|^{-\delta})T^{-(d-2\delta)/\alpha}.

By the dominated convergence theorem,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{B(0,R)}\tilde{p}(t,x,z)|z|^{\delta+\alpha-d}\,{\rm d}z=0.

This and (
6.8
) yield (
6.5
). Then (
6.6
) follows by (
6.2
) and (
6.5
). If
x=0
, then we trivially have infinity on both sides of (
6.5
) and (
6.6
).
Finally, (
6.7
) follows from (
5.6
).

\Box

Remark 6.5
.

We note that the function

(6.9)

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(x)

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{t}\Psi_{s}(x)\,{\rm d}s

is self-similar, too. Namely, by (
5.3
) and changing variables
s=tu
in (
6.9
), we get

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(x)=t^{(\alpha+\delta-d)/\alpha}\mu_{1}(t^{-1/\alpha}x),\quad t>0,x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}.

Furthermore,
\mu
satisfies the Duhamel formula,

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(x)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\mu_{s}(z)q(z)p(t-s,x,z)\,{\rm d}{z}\,{\rm d}{s}.

Indeed, by (
5.5
) and Fubini-Tonelli,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(t-s,x,z)q(z)\mu_{s}(z)\,{\rm d}{z}\,{\rm d}{s}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(t-s,x,z)q(z)\int_{0}^{s}\Psi_{s-r}(z)\,{\rm d}{r}\,{\rm d}{z}\,{\rm d}{s}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{r}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(t-s,x,z)q(z)\Psi_{s-r}(z)\,{\rm d}{s}\,{\rm d}{z}\,{\rm d}{r}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t-r}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(t-r-s,x,z)q(z)\Psi_{s}(z)\,{\rm d}{s}\,{\rm d}{z}\,{\rm d}{r}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\Psi_{t-r}(z)\,{\rm d}{r}=\mu_{t}(x).

Of course, it is
\Psi_{t}
, not
\mu_{t}
, that captures the large time asymptotics for the solutions of the equation (
1.9
) in Theorem
1.2
. Interestingly, it seems feasible, if not easy, to construct
\mu_{t}
directly as
\lim_{y\to 0}\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{p}(s,x,y)/h(y)\,{\rm d}{s}
and then attempt
to define

\Psi_{t}(x)=\partial\mu_{t}(x)/\partial t
.
