Abstract
. In a continuous-time economy, this paper formulates the Epstein-Zin preference for discounted dividends received by an investor as an Epstein-Zin singular control utility. We introduce a backward stochastic differential equation with an aggregator integrated with respect to a singular control, prove its well-posedness, and show that it coincides with the Epstein-Zin singular control utility. We then establish that this formulation is equivalent to a robust dividend policy chosen by the firm’s executive under the Maenhout’s ambiguity-averse preference. In particular, the robust dividend policy takes the form of a threshold strategy on the firm’s surplus process, where the threshold level is characterized as the free boundary of a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman variational inequality. Therefore, dividend-caring investors can choose firms that match their preferences by examining stock’s dividend policies and financial statements, whereas executives can make use of dividend to signal their confidence, in the form of ambiguity aversion, on realizing the earnings implied by their financial statements.



1.
Introduction

Dividend (or cash payout) policy is an important topic in both corporate finance and asset pricing. The Miller-Modigliani
[
56
]
dividend irrelevance theory suggests that a firm’s dividend policy does not affect its value and stock prices in a perfect market. However, the underlying incentives for stockholders to receive dividends and for the firm’s executives to pay dividends are largely unknown in reality. For instance, a stockholder can liquidate some of his stocks for consumption instead of receiving cash dividend, which is subject to a higher tax rate in the USA. Hence, Black
[
16
]
proposes the dividend puzzle which has generated a great of attentions in the literature.

A school of thought is the dividend signaling theory (DST) (see Bhattacharya
[
15
]
, John and Williams
[
43
]
, Miller and Rock
[
57
]
) that a firm’s executive passes signals about the firm’s information to the public through dividends. In particular, Black
[
17
]
wrote,

“The idea that dividends convey information beyond that conveyed by the firm’s financial
statements and public announcements stretches the imagination…. I think we must assume that investors care about dividends directly. We must put dividends
into the utility functions.”

Although most empirical studies support DST, the
survey in Brav et al.
[
20
]
shows that executives viewed the mechanism behind DST as broadly misguided. However, dividend changes do contain information about future earnings according to Ham et al.
[
35
]
. Additionally, Michaely et al.
[
55
]
shows that payout announcements signal changes in cash flow volatility (in the opposite direction) but not stock volatility, which the authors refer to as “signaling safety”. A firm’s cashflow is related to its earnings so that the cashflow volatility indicates the earnings’ volatility. By modeling the firm’s uncontrolled surplus process as diffusion models with a stochastic drift, there is a mathematical toolkit as in Reppen et al.
[
70
]
to compute the optimal dividend policy under a random profitability.

In this paper, we consider the suggestions by Black
[
17
]
. We assume that the firm’s financial reports contain information on the firm’s expected earnings and risk. Thus, we model the firm’s uncontrolled surplus by an diffusion process and put dividends into the utility functions for investors. Investors who care directly about dividends have a value function that considers the short-term cash inflow from discounted cash dividends and the certainty equivalence of long-term stock holdings subject to bankruptcy risk. This consideration requires us to separate risk aversion from elasticity of intertemporal substitutions (EIS) in the decision making process.

The preference introduced by Epstein and Zin (EZ)
[
30
]
is a natural candidate designed for this purpose.
In our formulation, we increase the degree of risk aversion of intertemporal preference ordering via a parameter
R\in(0,1)
, without affecting “certainty preferences” of a dividend payment policy. We also follow the continuous-time dividend models where the discounted dividend stream is paid in a singular control manner (see Cadenillas et al.
[
22
]
, Jeanblanc-Picqué and Shiryaev
[
41
]
, Reppen et al.
[
70
]
, Shreve et al.
[
74
]
). This formulation turns out to be a singular control problem with recursive utilities for which we have to establish that there exists a unique value function for the EZ investor within the recursion in singular control setting.

Alternatively, the firm’s executive aims to maximize the expected discounted dividend stream subject to the bankruptcy risk. Although the financial reports indicate the firm’s expected earnings and risk, the executive encounters uncertainty in the predicted earnings. We view this uncertainty as
ambiguity
so that the executive’s value function is postulated as the Maenhout’s
[
51
]
robust preference. Specifically, the executive aims to make a robust dividend decision subject to bankruptcy risk and Maenhout’s regularity.

Our first aim is to establish the equivalence between the EZ singular control utility and Maenhout’s counterpart under the setting that the risk-aversion parameter in the EZ utility coincides with the ambiguity-aversion parameter in Maenhout’s preference (see Theorem
2.10
). While the connection between EZ and Maenhout preferences is initially addressed in
[
51
]
and formally established in Skiadas
[
76
]
for consumption models, this paper is, to the best of our knowledge, the first to develop this equivalence within a
singular control setting
, even accounting for
the bankruptcy time
.

To establish the equivalence, a substantial portion of our efforts revolves around showing that the EZ singular control utility is well-defined. In Proposition
2.7
, we prove the existence and uniqueness of the EZ singular control utility via backward stochastic differential equations (BSDEs). The analysis is nontrivial
due to the interaction of a non-decreasing control with a non-Lipschitz aggregator, together with the inclusion of the bankruptcy time. In contrast to the EZ consumption literature (see, e.g., Aurand and Huang
[
4
]
, Xing
[
78
]
), our framework explicitly incorporates the risk of ruin rather than solely considering on cash flow utility, which results in a nonconvex set of dividend strategies. This nonconvexity implies the lack of concavity in our EZ singular control utility. Moreover, we focus on the case
R\in(0,1)
, where standard a priori bounds for BSDEs suitable for the case

R\in(1,\infty)
cannot be directly used (see, e.g.,
[
4
,
78
]
, Popier
[
68
]
).

Our next aim is to develop a shooting method in Proposition
2.16
to show that under a Markovian setting and certain regularities on the reference parameters of the surplus process, the robust dividend policy is a threshold strategy (see Theorem
2.17
). When the firm’s surplus hits the upside threshold, the dividend is paid. When there is no bankruptcy, the shooting method has been used to a Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) variational inequality (VI) (see Cohen et al.
[
25
]
). Our Maenhout’s singular control problem with bankruptcy can be transformed into a HJB-VI. However, the bankruptcy time complicates the analysis because it is involved within the dividend decision. In particular, we have to prove that there is a threshold on the surplus process which shoots two targets on the bankruptcy boundary condition and HJB-VI, simultaneously.

Our theoretical results lead to the following interpretations for the dividend signaling theory:
While investors are informed with the firm’s expected earnings from the firm’s financial statements, executives set dividend threshold to showcase their confidence on realizing the expected earnings. The level of confidence is reflected by the ambiguity aversion parameter in our model.
Therefore, our model predicts that dividend policy conveys information about earnings uncertainty or, equivalently, the executive’s confidence in realizing the expected earnings. We call this notion “signaling confidence”.

Notation and preliminaries

Consider a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
that supports a one-dimensional Brownian motion
W:=(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.
Let
\mathbb{F}:=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the augmented filtration generated by
W
. We assume that
{\mathcal{}F}_{0}
is trivial. We write
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
for the expectation under
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\cdot]:=\mathbb{E}[\cdot|{\mathcal{}F}_{t}]
for its conditional expectation given
{\mathcal{}F}_{t}
.

\mathcal{P}
is the set of all real-valued
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable processes, and
{\mathcal{}P}_{+}\subset\mathcal{P}
is the subset of processes that take values in
\mathbb{R}_{+}:=[0,+\infty)
. Moreover, we denote for every
p\geq 1
and
t\geq 0
by
L^{p}(\mathcal{F}_{t})
the set of all
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable random variable with norm
\|X\|_{L^{p}}^{p}:=\mathbb{E}[|X|^{p}]<\infty
.
We introduce some notations for defining stochastic differential utility under singular dividend flows.

\bullet

{\mathcal{}T}
is the set of all
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely (
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.) finite,
\mathbb{F}
-stopping times
\tau
.

\bullet

\mathcal{A}
is the set of all
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable,

\mathbb{P}
-a.s. continuous, and non-decreasing processes

with

. Here we write
D_{0-}=0
for indicating that
D_{0}>0
can only be achieved by a jump of the process at time zero, followed by a continuous path for all

.

We define the stochastic integral with respect to (w.r.t.)
D\in{\mathcal{}A}
as follows: for any
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable and locally bounded process
(g_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, the stochastic integral,

\int_{0}^{\tau}g_{t}dD_{t}:=g_{0}D_{0}+\int_{(0,\tau]}g_{t}dD_{t},

is well-defined in the Stieltjes sense (see Protter
[
69
, Theorem IV.15]
). Here, the jump of

at time zero is accounted, so that
\int_{0}^{t}dD_{s}=D_{t}
for

.

Finally, we introduce a set of processes that serve as Girsanov kernels, which will be used to define Maenhout’s ambiguity aversion in Section

.

\bullet

is the set of all processes
\theta:=(\theta_{t})_{t\geq 0}\in{\mathcal{}P}
under which a stochastic exponential

defined as

(1.1)

\displaystyle\eta_{t}^{\theta}=\exp\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\theta_{s}dW_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\theta_{s}^{2}ds\bigg),\quad t\geq 0,

is a martingale. We call
\Theta
the Girsanov kernels set.

For each
\theta\in\Theta
, we define a probability measure
\mathbb{Q}^{\theta}
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F})
by

(1.2)

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbb{Q}^{\theta}}{d\mathbb{P}}\bigg|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}=\eta^{\theta}_{T}\quad\mbox{for each $T>0$.}

Then
W_{t}^{\theta}:=W_{t}-\int_{0}^{t}\theta_{s}ds
,
t\in[0,T]
, is a
\mathbb{Q}^{\theta}
-Brownian motion. We write
\mathbb{E}^{\theta}[\cdot]
for the expectation under
\mathbb{Q}^{\theta}
and
\mathbb{E}^{\theta}_{t}[\cdot]
for its conditional expectation given
\mathcal{F}_{t}
.

2.
Main results

2.1.
Epstein-Zin utility under singular dividend flows

Following the suggestion of Black
[
16
]
, we incorporate dividends into the investor’s objective function through recursive utilities.

We start by defining the surplus process and its controlled version under singular dividend flows. We let
\mathcal{X}:=(\ell,+\infty)
,
-\infty\leq\ell<0
, denote the state space. For each
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we denote by
X^{x}:=(X^{x}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
the surplus process governed by the
\mathcal{X}
-valued stochastic differential equation (SDE)

(2.1)

\displaystyle X^{x}_{t}=x+\int_{0}^{t}\mu(X^{x}_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(X^{x}_{s})dW_{t},\quad t\geq 0,

where the drift
\mu:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and volatility
\sigma:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
functions are Borel measurable.

We impose the following condition on the uncontrolled surplus process (
2.1
).

Assumption 2.1
.

The SDE (
2.1
) admits a unique weak solution in law, which is a regular diffusion with unattainable boundaries
\{\ell,+\infty\}
; see, e.g., Karatzas and Shreve
[
44
, Chapter 5.5]
, Revuz and Yor
[
71
, Chapter VII.3]
.

Remark 2.2
.

Assumption
2.1
implies that
X^{x}
in (
2.1
) does not explode at infinity and is recurrent (see
[
44
, Proposition 5.22]
,
[
71
, Chapter VII, Exercise 3.22]
). Consequently, bankruptcy occurs as intended in our model (cf. Definition
2.3
). An example satisfying this assumption is provided in Remark
2.14

We recall the set
{\mathcal{}A}
defined in Section
1.1
.
For each
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\subset{\mathcal{}X}
and
D\in\mathcal{A}
, we let
X^{x,D}:=(X_{t}^{x,D})_{t\geq 0}
denote the controlled surplus process governed by

(2.2)

\displaystyle X_{t}^{x,D}=x+\int_{0}^{t}\mu(X_{s}^{x,D})ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(X_{s}^{x,D})dW_{t}-D_{t},\quad t\geq 0,

with its left limit
X_{0-}^{x,D}=x
at time zero.

We then introduce the bankruptcy risk of the investor under the controlled surplus process and the set of singular controls for admissible dividend flows.

Definition 2.3
.

We define the bankruptcy (ruin) time by

\displaystyle\tau^{x,D}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:X_{t}^{x,D}\leq 0\}.

Under Assumption
2.1
,
\tau^{x,D}
is finite
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. (see Remark
2.2
); hence
\tau^{x,D}\in{\mathcal{}T}
.

Moreover, we let
\xi_{\tau^{x,D}}
denote the bankruptcy payout satisfying

(2.3)

\displaystyle\xi_{\tau^{x,D}}\in L^{2}({\mathcal{}F}_{\tau^{x,D}})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mbox{$\xi_{\tau^{x,D}}>0$ $\mathbb{P}$-a.s..}

Definition 2.4
.

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, let
\mathcal{A}^{x}
denote the set of all
D\in\mathcal{A}
satisfying

\int_{0}^{\tau^{x,D}}e^{-\rho s}dD_{s}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{\tau^{x,D}})
and
D_{t}-D_{t-}\leq X_{t-}^{x,D}
for
t\geq 0
, where
\rho>0
is a constant discount rate.

Having completed the investor’s dividend model, we now introduce her utility under Epstein-Zin (EZ) preferences.

Throughout this section, we fix a parameter
R\in(0,1)
, which corresponds to a
degree of risk-aversion
. Following Epstein and Zin
[
30
]
, we introduce the EZ aggregator
g_{\text{EZ}}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
defined by

(2.4)

\displaystyle g_{\text{EZ}}(s,v):=e^{-\rho s}(1-R)v^{\frac{-R}{1-R}},\quad(s,v)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}.

For each
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
D\in{\mathcal{}A}^{x}
(see Definition
2.4
), we define the investor’s EZ utility
V^{x,D}:=(V^{x,D}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
by

(2.5)

\displaystyle V^{x,D}_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{t}\bigg[\int_{t\wedge\tau^{x,D}}^{\tau^{x,D}}g_{\text{EZ}}(s,V^{x,D}_{s})dD_{s}+\xi_{\tau^{x,D}}^{1-R}\bigg],\quad t\geq 0.

We note that when
R=0
,
V^{x,D}
in (
2.5
) reduces to the classical dividend utility
K^{x,D}:=(K^{x,D}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
(see Alvarez and Virtanen
[
1
]
) given by

(2.6)

\displaystyle K^{x,D}_{t}:=\mathbb{E}_{t}\bigg[\int_{t\wedge\tau^{x,D}}^{\tau^{x,D}}e^{-\rho s}dD_{s}+\xi_{\tau^{x,D}}\bigg],\quad t\geq 0.

In the setting (
2.5
), the degree of risk aversion of intertemporal preference ordering is increased (i.e.,
R>0
) without affecting the “certainty preferences”.

The following definition is inspired by the concept of stochastic differential utility (see Duffie and Epstein
[
28
]
, Epstein and Zin
[
30
]
, Schroder and Skiadas
[
73
]
), while tailored for our singular dividend flow setting.

Definition 2.5
.

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
D\in{\mathcal{}A}^{x}
, we denote by
\mathbb{I}{(g_{\text{EZ}},D,{\tau^{x,D}})}
the set of all
V\in{\mathcal{}P}
for which the stochastic integral
\int g_{\text{EZ}}(t,V_{t})dD_{t}
is well-defined and the integrability condition
\mathbb{E}[\int_{0}^{\tau^{x,D}}|g_{\text{EZ}}(s,V_{s})|dD_{s}]<\infty
holds.

i.

A process
V\in\mathbb{I}{(g_{\text{EZ}},D,{\tau^{x,D}})}
is called a utility with
(g_{\text{EZ}},D,{\tau^{x,D}},\xi^{1-R}_{\tau^{x,D}})
if it satisfies (
2.5
);

ii.

We denote by
\mathbb{UI}(g_{\text{EZ}},D,{\tau^{x,D}},\xi^{1-R}_{\tau^{x,D}})
the set of all uniformly integrable utilities
V
with
(g_{\text{EZ}},D,{\tau^{x,D}},\xi^{1-R}_{\tau^{x,D}})
.

Remark 2.6
.

The utility specification in (
2.5
) is motivated by its connection to the conventional EZ aggregator in the infinite elasticity of intertemporal substitution limit.
While
g_{\text{EZ}}
in (
2.4
) differs from the standard form by a scaling factor
(1-R)
(rather than
(1-R)^{-\frac{R}{1-R}}
), this does not affect preferences for any
R\in(0,1)

(see Herdegen et al.
[
36
, Remark 2.4 (a)]
).
Our scaling choice in (
2.5
) clarifies the equivalence between EZ utility and robust Maenhout utility (see Theorem
2.10
).
For
R\in(1,\infty)
, the sign and domain of the generator become more delicate; see, e.g., Aurand and Huang
[
4
]
, Herdegen et al.
[
36
,
37
]
, Xing
[
78
]
, for further discussion.

We now establish the well-posedness (i.e., existence and uniqueness) of the EZ utility. In particular, for each
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
D\in{\mathcal{}A}^{x}
,
V^{x,D}
in (
2.5
) will be characterized as the unique solution to the following backward stochastic differential equation (BSDE): for
t\geq 0

(2.7)

\displaystyle V^{x,D}_{t}=\xi_{\tau^{x,D}}^{1-R}+\int_{t\wedge\tau^{x,D}}^{\tau^{x,D}}g_{\text{EZ}}(s,V^{x,D}_{s})\,dD_{s}-\int_{t\wedge\tau^{x,D}}^{\tau^{x,D}}Z^{x,D}_{s}\,dW_{s}.

Proposition 2.7
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
holds.
For any
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
D\in{\mathcal{}A}^{x}
, there exists a unique utility
V^{x,D}\in\mathbb{UI}(g_{\text{EZ}},D,{\tau^{x,D}},\xi^{1-R}_{\tau^{x,D}})
, which is strictly positive, has continuous paths, and satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\geq 0}(V^{x,D}_{t})^{2}]<\infty
. Moreover, there exists
Z^{x,D}\in\mathcal{P}
such that
\int_{0}^{\tau^{x,D}}(Z_{t}^{x,D})^{2}\,dt<\infty

\mathbb{P}
-a.s., and
(V^{x,D},Z^{x,D})
is a solution of (
2.7
) in the sense of Definition
3.1
.

The proof of Proposition
2.7
can be found in Section
3.1
. We outline the main steps as follows. First, we establish the existence of a solution to (
2.7
) under a bounded dividend flow and a Lipschitz aggregator. In the second step, we prove a comparison theorem under aggregators
g(\cdot,\cdot)
that are nonincreasing in their second argument, which yields uniqueness, and we derive a priori bounds for solutions to (
2.7
) under
g_{\text{EZ}}(\cdot,\cdot)
which is non-Lipschitz. Using these bounds, in the third step we approximate
g_{\text{EZ}}(\cdot,\cdot)
by a sequence of Lipschitz aggregators and show convergence of the corresponding BSDE solutions. Finally, for unbounded dividend flows
D\in{\mathcal{}A}^{x}
, we follow some arguments in Aurand and Huang
[
4
]
to complete the proof.

By Proposition
2.7
, we are able to define the investor’s dividend optimization problem under EZ preferences by

(2.8)

\displaystyle V_{t}^{x}:=\esssup\{V^{x,D}_{t}:D\in{\mathcal{A}}^{x}\},\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Solving (
2.8
) is demanding due to the recursive nature of EZ preference, the random time horizon and singular controls. In the next section, we introduce a robust dividend formulation under ambiguity, which will later be shown to be equivalent to (
2.8
). The robust formulation not only provides a convenient basis for characterizing the optimal dividend policy under suitable assumptions, but extends the previous findings in Maenout
[
51
]
and Skiadas
[
76
]
.

2.2.
Robust singular dividend criterion under Maenhout preferences

In this section, we take the perspective of the firm’s executive, who aims to maximize expected discounted dividends while considering bankruptcy risk (see Definition
2.3
). At the same time, she suffers from ambiguity regarding the firm’s earnings or the drift of the firm’s surplus given in (
2.1
).

Throughout this section, we fix a parameter
{\mathcal{}R}\in(0,1)
, which corresponds to a
degree of ambiguity-aversion
. In the presence of ambiguity, the executive prefers a family of unspecified alternative models that are in close proximity to the reference model given in (
2.1
). In line with this, we define the executive’s admissible sets of Girsanov kernels as follow.

Definition 2.8
.

For any initial wealth
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and consumption plan
D\in{\mathcal{}A}^{x}
,
we define the set of admissible Girsanov kernels
\Theta^{D}\subseteq\Theta
by

(2.9)

\Theta^{D}:=\left\{\theta\in\Theta\;\middle|\;\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau^{x,D}}\eta_{t}^{\theta}e^{\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\theta^{2}_{s}}{2{\mathcal{}R}}ds-\rho t}dD_{t}\bigg)^{2}\bigg]<\infty,\quad\text{and}\\[15.00002pt]
\mathbb{E}\left[(\eta_{\tau^{x,D}}^{\theta})^{2}e^{\int_{0}^{\tau^{x,D}}\frac{\theta^{2}_{u}}{{\mathcal{}R}}du}\max\{\xi_{\tau^{x,D}}^{2},1\}\right]<\infty\end{array}\right\}.

where
\eta^{\theta}
is the stochastic exponential from (

\displaystyle v^{*}(x):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &v_{b^{*}}(x)\quad&&\mbox{if}\;\;x\in[0,b^{*}];\\
&x-b^{*}+\psi^{+}(b^{*})\quad&&\mbox{if}\;\;x\in(b^{*},\infty).\end{aligned}\right.

We note that
v^{*}(=v_{b^{*}})
on
[0,b^{*}]
satisfies the first and third lines of (
2.17
), and that
(v^{*})^{\prime}=1
on
(b^{*},\infty)
. Thus, it suffices to show that
{\mathcal{}L}v^{*}\leq 0
on
(b^{*},\infty)
.

The function
v_{b^{*}}
is in
\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and satisfies (
3.18
) with
\gamma=0
.
In particular,
v_{b^{*}}^{\prime\prime}(b^{*})=0
by construction. Indeed, substituting the boundary conditions
v_{b^{*}}^{\prime}(b^{*})=1
and
v_{b^{*}}(b^{*})=\psi^{+}(b^{*})
into the ODE at
x=b^{*}
gives

\frac{\sigma^{2}(b^{*})}{2}v_{b^{*}}^{\prime\prime}(b^{*})+\mu(b^{*})-R\frac{\sigma^{2}(b^{*})}{2}\frac{1}{\psi^{+}(b^{*})}-\rho\psi^{+}(b^{*})=0.

Since
\psi^{+}(b^{*})
solves the quadratic equation
Q(\psi;b^{*})=0
, it follows that
v_{b^{*}}^{\prime\prime}(b^{*})=0
.
By the definition given in (
3.39
), the function
v^{*}
is in
\mathcal{C}^{2}({\mathbb{R}_{+}})
and satisfies the free boundary problem (
2.17
) to the left of
b^{*}
. It remains to show that for
x\in(b^{*},\infty)
,
{\mathcal{}L}v^{*}\leq 0
(as
({v^{*}})^{\prime}(x)=1
is obvious).

For
x\in(b^{*},\overline{b})
, note that
\psi^{+}(x)-x
is decreasing, as stated in Assumption
2.12
ii.. Consequently, we have
v^{*}(x)=x-b^{*}+\psi^{+}(b^{*})\geq\psi^{+}(x)
. Hence
\mathcal{L}v^{*}(x)
is given by

\mathcal{L}v^{*}(x)=-\frac{Q(v^{*}(x);x)}{v^{*}(x)}\leq 0,

where
Q(\psi;x)
is defined in Assumption
2.12
ii..

If
\overline{b}=\infty
, the proof concludes here. Otherwise, for
x\in[\overline{b},\infty)
, we have
Q(v^{*}(x);x)<0
, which implies
\mathcal{L}v^{*}(x)<0
. Thus, we conclude that
v^{*}(x)
satisfies
\mathcal{L}v^{*}\leq 0
on
(b^{*},\infty)
. This means it solves (
2.17
) with the free boundary
b^{*}\in(\underline{b},\hat{b})
. This completes the proof.

∎

3.5.
Verification theorem: Proof of Theorem
2.17

To establish the boundary condition
{\mathcal{}V}(0+)=\xi_{0}
for value function
{\mathcal{}V}
(defined in (

X^{x}
.
To that end, we define the following stopping time for each

(3.40)

\displaystyle\zeta_{y|x}=\inf\{t\geq 0,X_{t}^{x}=y\},\quad y\in\mathcal{X}\cup\{\ell,\infty\}.

Assumption
2.1
ensures that for all
x\in\mathcal{X}/\{0\}
,

\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{0|0}=0)=\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{0|x}<\infty)=1
and
\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{\infty|x}<\infty)=0
.

Lemma 3.18
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, for any
\delta>0
, we have

(3.41)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow h}\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{h|x}<\delta)=1,

where stopping time
\zeta_{y|x}
is defined in (
3.40
). In particular, for fixed
y>0
,

(3.42)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\downarrow 0}\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{0|x}<\delta)=1,\quad\lim_{x\downarrow 0}\mathbb{E}\Big[\max_{0\leq s\leq\delta\wedge{\zeta}_{0|x}\wedge{\zeta}_{y|x}}X^{x}_{s}\Big]=0.

Proof.

The proof involves a transformed process
U_{t}^{x}
of
X_{t}^{x}
, which is a time-changed Brownian motion, and we prove that auxiliary versions of (
3.41
), (
3.42
) hold with the process
U_{t}
.
We define the
scale function
of
X^{x}
in (
2.1
) as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}(x):=\int_{0}^{x}\exp\Big(-2\int_{0}^{z}\frac{\mu(y)}{\sigma^{2}(y)}dy\Big)dz,

which is strictly increasing, continuously differentiable bijection of the interval
\mathcal{X}
onto the interval
I=(\iota_{l},\iota_{\infty})
with endpoints
\iota_{l}:=\mathfrak{s}(\ell+)
and
\iota_{\infty}:=\mathfrak{s}(\infty-)
; see, e.g., Borodin and Salminen
[
18
, II.1.4]
.Denote by
\mathfrak{s}^{-1}
the inverse mapping of
\mathfrak{s}
. We define
U_{t}^{x}:=\mathfrak{s}(X^{x}_{t})
, with state space
I
, and its dynamic is given by

(3.43)

\displaystyle U^{x}_{t}=\mathfrak{s}(X^{x}_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\varsigma(U^{x}_{s})dW_{s},

where the dispersion function
\varsigma(u):=(\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)(\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(u))
; see e.g., Karatzas and Shreve
[
44
, Section 5.5B]
.
To further remove the dependency on the function
\varsigma
in (
3.43
), we consider a time change for transformation.
According to the Dambis–Dubins–Schwarz theorem (see Revuz and Yor
[
71
, Theorem V.1.6]
), one can rewrite the stochastic integral
\int_{0}^{\cdot}\varsigma(U^{x}_{s})dW_{s}
as a time-changed Brownian motion on (an extension of) the probability space, i.e.,
\hat{W}_{\Lambda^{U^{x}}(\cdot)}
that we define as follows.
Define a time scale
\Gamma^{U^{x}}(r)
for
r\in{\mathbb{R}_{+}}
as

\displaystyle\Gamma^{U^{x}}(r):=\int_{0}^{r}\frac{dt}{\varsigma^{2}\big(\mathfrak{s}(X_{0}^{x})+\hat{W}_{t}\big)}

in terms of a standard Brownian motion
\hat{W}
, and denote by
\Lambda^{U^{x}}
the inverse mapping of
\Gamma^{U^{x}}
, i.e.
\Lambda^{U^{x}}(t):=\inf\{r\geq 0:\Gamma^{U^{x}}(r)>t\}
. The representation

(3.44)

\displaystyle U_{t}^{x}=\mathfrak{s}(X_{0}^{x})+\hat{W}_{\Lambda^{U^{x}}(t)}

holds. Due to the strict monotonicity of the scale function
\mathfrak{s}
, it is sufficient to prove auxiliary properties of (
3.41
)-(
3.42
) for the process
U^{x}
given by (
3.44
).
For convenience of presentation, we adopt the notation

\displaystyle\zeta^{U}_{y|x}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:U_{t}^{x}=\mathfrak{s}(y)\}.

From the continuous path properties of standard Brownian motion together with the fact that
\Lambda^{U}(t)>0
holds for all
t>0
, it is proved (see e.g.
[
18
, II.1.3]
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\big\{\omega\in\Omega:\exists\epsilon(\omega)>0\text{ s.t. }U_{t}^{x}\leq\mathfrak{s}(x),\forall t\in[0,\epsilon(\omega)]\big\}\Big)=0,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\big\{\omega\in\Omega:\exists\epsilon(\omega)>0\text{ s.t. }U_{t}^{x}\geq\mathfrak{s}(x),\forall t\in[0,\epsilon(\omega)]\big\}\Big)=0.

Given the above statements, we have for any fixed
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\zeta^{U}_{a|x}\geq\delta)=\mathbb{P}\Big(\inf_{t\in[0,\delta]}U_{t}^{x}\geq\mathfrak{s}(a)\Big)\rightarrow\mathbb{P}\Big(\inf_{t\in[0,\delta]}U_{t}^{x}>\mathfrak{s}(x)\Big)=0,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\zeta^{U}_{b|x}\geq\delta)=\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{t\in[0,\delta]}U_{t}^{x}\geq\mathfrak{s}(b)\Big)\rightarrow\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{t\in[0,\delta]}U_{t}^{x}<\mathfrak{s}(x)\Big)=0,

as
a\uparrow x
and
b\downarrow x
respectively.
Hence, we conclude (
3.41
).

To show (
3.42
), we recognize from the time change property that

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq s\leq\delta\wedge{\zeta}^{U}_{0|x}\wedge{\zeta}^{U}_{y|x}}U_{s}^{x}

\displaystyle=\max_{0\leq r\leq\Lambda^{U^{x}}(\delta)\wedge\Lambda^{U^{x}}(\zeta^{U}_{0|x})\wedge\Lambda^{U^{x}}(\zeta^{U}_{y|x})}\mathfrak{s}(x)+\hat{W}_{r}

\displaystyle=\max_{0\leq r\leq\Lambda^{U^{x}}(\delta)\wedge\hat{\zeta}_{0|x}\wedge\hat{\zeta}_{y|x}}\mathfrak{s}(x)+\hat{W}_{r},

where

\hat{\zeta}_{y|x}=\inf\{r\geq 0:B_{x}(r)=\mathfrak{s}(y)\}
, with
B_{x}(r):=\mathfrak{s}(x)+\hat{W}_{r}
.

By the monotonicity of
\mathfrak{s}
,

decreases as
x\downarrow 0
.
By the law of iterated logarithm (see Revuz and Yor
[
71
, Chapt. II, Theorem 19]
), we have
\hat{\zeta}_{0|x}\rightarrow 0
as
x\downarrow 0
. By definition,
\Lambda^{U^{x}}(\delta)
is finite a.s. for
\delta\in[0,\infty)
. Since
B_{x}(r)
has continuous path, considering a decreasing sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
x_{n}<y
and
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}x_{n}=0

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\max_{0\leq r\leq\Lambda^{U^{x}}(\delta)\wedge\hat{\zeta}_{0|x_{n}}\wedge\hat{\zeta}_{y|x_{n}}}B_{x_{n}}(r)=\mathfrak{s}(0),

and
\max_{0\leq r\leq\Lambda^{U^{x}}(\delta)\wedge\hat{\zeta}_{y|x}}B_{x}(r)\leq\mathfrak{s}(y)
; hence (
3.42
) holds.
∎

Remark 3.20
.

In dividend optimization problems under model ambiguity, the regularity of the corresponding value function w.r.t. the state process is typically established only under specific models, such as constant-coefficient Brownian motion (Chakraborty et al.
[
23
]
).
Related works without ambiguity, such as Choulli et al.
[
24
, Lemma 1]
and Alvarez and Virtanen
[
1

2.13
aligns with such conditions, and it enables to extend the regularity results to general processes even under model ambiguity.

Finally, we provide the proof of Theorem

\displaystyle v^{*}(x)\geq

\displaystyle\sup_{D\in{\mathcal{}A}^{x}}\inf_{\theta\in\Theta^{D}}\mathbb{E}^{\theta}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau^{x,D}}e^{-\rho t}\Big(dD_{t}+\frac{\theta_{t}^{2}}{2{\mathcal{}R}}v^{*}(X_{t}^{x,D})dt\Big)+\xi_{\tau^{x,D}}\bigg],

e^{-\rho t+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{({\theta}^{*}_{s})^{2}}{2R}ds}v^{*}(X_{t}^{x,D})
on the random time interval
[0,\tau^{x,D}\wedge\hat{\tau}_{n}]
and take expectation.
Then we obtain that

\displaystyle v^{*}(x)\geq\mathbb{E}^{\theta^{*}}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau^{x,D}}e^{-\rho s+\int_{0}^{s}\frac{(\theta_{u}^{*})^{2}}{2R}du}dD_{s}+e^{-\rho\tau^{x,D}+\int_{0}^{\tau^{x,D}}\frac{(\theta_{u}^{*})^{2}}{2R}du}\xi_{0}\bigg]={\mathcal{}V}^{x,D,\theta^{*}}_{0},

where the equality holds by (
3.11
) and Assumption
2.11
. The last term is greater than
\inf_{\theta\in\Theta^{D}}{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D,\theta}
. Taking supreme over
{D\in\mathcal{A}^{x}}
on both sides of the above equation gives (
3.50
).

Step 2.
Let
D^{*}=D(b^{*})
be the
b^{*}
-threshold strategy. We show that

(3.54)

\displaystyle v^{*}(x)\leq

\displaystyle\inf_{\theta\in\Theta^{D^{*}}}\mathbb{E}^{\theta}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau^{x,D^{*}}}e^{-\rho t}\Big(dD^{*}_{t}+\frac{\theta_{t}^{2}}{2R}v^{*}(X_{t}^{x,D^{*}})dt\Big)+\xi_{\tau^{x,D^{*}}}\bigg],

(3.55)

\displaystyle v^{*}(x)\leq

\displaystyle{\mathcal{}V}(x).

We split the proof into two cases:
x\in(0,b^{*}]
and
x\in(b^{*},\infty)
. By the definition of
b^{*}
-threshold strategy (see Definition
2.15
), for every
s\in(0,\tau^{x,D^{*}}]
, we have

(3.56)

\displaystyle X^{x,D^{*}}_{s}\in[0,b^{*}],\quad dD^{*}_{s}>0\quad\mbox{only when $X_{s}^{x,D^{*}}=b^{*}$}.

Furthermore, if
x\in(0,b^{*}]
, the above properties hold for every
s\in[0,\tau^{x,D^{*}}]
.

Case 1.

x\in(0,b^{*}]
. Let
\theta\in\Theta^{D^{*}}
, and
\mathbb{Q}^{\theta}
be the corresponding probability measure induced by the kernel
\theta
. Furthermore, as in (
3.51
),
we define for every

by
\hat{\tau}_{n}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:X^{x,D^{*}}_{t}\notin[1/n,n]\}\wedge\tau^{x,D^{*}}.

An application of Dynkin formula to the process
e^{-\rho t}v^{*}(X_{t}^{x,D^{*}})
on the random time interval
[0,\hat{\tau}_{n}]
under
\mathbb{Q}^{\theta}
gives that

(3.57)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} v^{*}(x)&=\mathbb{E}^{\theta}\bigg[e^{-\rho\hat{\tau}_{n}}v^{*}(X_{\hat{\tau}_{n}}^{x,D^{*}})-\int_{0}^{\hat{\tau}_{n}}e^{-\rho s}(\hat{\mathcal{}L}^{\theta}v^{*}(X_{s}^{x,D^{*}})ds-dD^{*}_{s})\bigg],\end{aligned}

where
\hat{\mathcal{}L}^{\theta}v^{*}(X_{s}^{x,D^{*}}):=(\sigma^{2}(v^{*})^{\prime\prime}/2+(\mu+\sigma\theta_{s})(v^{*})^{\prime}-\rho v^{*})(X_{s}^{x,D^{*}})

and we use the fact that
\mathbb{E}^{\theta}[\int_{0}^{\tau^{x,D^{*}}\wedge\hat{\tau}_{n}}e^{-\rho s}\sigma(X_{s}^{x,D^{*}})(v^{*})^{\prime}(X_{s}^{x,D^{*}})dW^{\mathbb{Q}}_{s}]=0
and that

dD^{*}_{s}>0
only when

for

From (
3.56

2.17

{\mathcal{}L}v^{*}(X_{s}^{x,D^{*}})=0
for

(3.58)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{}L}^{\theta}v^{*}(X_{s}^{x,D^{*}})\geq{\mathcal{}L}v^{*}(X_{s}^{x,D^{*}})-\frac{(\theta_{s})^{2}}{2R}v^{*}(X_{s}^{x,D^{*}})=-\frac{(\theta_{s})^{2}}{2R}v^{*}(X_{s}^{x,D^{*}}).

3.58

\displaystyle v^{*}(x)\leq\mathbb{E}^{\theta}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\hat{\tau}_{n}}e^{-\rho s}\bigg(dD^{*}_{s}+\frac{(\theta_{s})^{2}}{2R}v^{*}(X_{s}^{x,D^{*}})ds\bigg)+e^{-\rho\hat{\tau}_{n}}v^{*}(X_{\hat{\tau}_{n}}^{x,D^{*}})\bigg].

Case 2.

x\in(b^{*},\infty)

As

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\theta}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau^{x,D^{*}}}e^{-\rho s}\bigg(dD^{*}_{s}+\frac{(\theta_{s})^{2}}{2R}v^{*}(X_{s}^{x,D^{*}})ds\bigg)+e^{-\rho\tau^{x,D^{*}}}\xi_{0}\bigg]\geq v^{*}(x)

holds for every

To show (

\displaystyle v^{*}(x)\leq\inf_{\theta\in{\Theta^{{D}^{*}}}}{\mathcal{}V}_{0}^{x,D^{*},\theta}\leq\sup_{D\in\mathcal{A}^{x}}\inf_{\theta\in{\Theta^{D}}}{\mathcal{}V}_{0}^{x,D,\theta}={\mathcal{}V}(x),\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}_{+}}.

Lemma 3.21

\mathbb{P}

We now provide the proof of Theorem
2.18
.

Proof.

Fix
(x,D)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times{\mathcal{}A}^{x}
. We first prove the theorem under the assumption that
\xi_{\tau^{x,D}}>C

\mathbb{P}
-a.s. with some constant
C>0
(as in Lemma
3.21
). The general case follows by considering the truncated sequence
{\xi}_{\tau^{x,D}}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{n}\vee\xi_{\tau^{x,D}}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and then letting
n\to\infty
.
We present the proof in two steps.

Step 1. Monotonicity.
Let
0<{\mathcal{}R}_{1}<{\mathcal{}R}_{2}<1
. For any
\theta\in\Theta
,

\frac{1}{2{\mathcal{}R}_{1}}\int_{0}^{t}\theta_{s}^{2}\,ds\geq\frac{1}{2{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\theta_{s}^{2}\,ds,\quad\forall t\geq 0.

By the representation of
{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D,\theta}
in (
3.11
) (see Proposition
2.9
), the inequality
{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D,\theta}_{t}({\mathcal{}R}_{1})\geq{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D,\theta}_{t}({\mathcal{}R}_{2}),

t\geq 0,
holds for all
\theta\in\Theta
(even if not in the admissible set
\Theta^{D}({\mathcal{}R}_{1})
of
{\mathcal{}R}_{1}
).
From this, we have for
t\geq 0

\displaystyle{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{t}({\mathcal{}R}_{1})=

\displaystyle\essinf_{\theta\in\Theta^{D}({\mathcal{}R}_{1})}{\mathcal{}V}_{t}^{x,D,\theta}({\mathcal{}R}_{1})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\essinf_{\theta\in\Theta^{D}({\mathcal{}R}_{2})}{\mathcal{}V}_{t}^{x,D,\theta}({\mathcal{}R}_{1})\geq\essinf_{\theta\in\Theta^{D}({\mathcal{}R}_{2})}{\mathcal{}V}_{t}^{x,D,\theta}({\mathcal{}R}_{2})={\mathcal{}V}_{t}^{x,D}({\mathcal{}R}_{2}).

The first inequality holds because
\Theta^{D}({\mathcal{}R}_{1})\subseteq\Theta^{D}({\mathcal{}R}_{2})
; as
{\mathcal{}R}
increases, the integrability conditions in (
2.9
) become less restrictive.
On the other hand, since
\theta^{0}:=(\theta^{0}_{t})_{t\geq 0}\equiv 0\in\Theta^{D}({\mathcal{}R})
for all
{\mathcal{}R}\in(0,1)
, it holds that

{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{t}({\mathcal{}R})=\essinf_{\theta\in\Theta^{D}({\mathcal{}R})}{\mathcal{}V}_{t}^{x,D,\theta}({\mathcal{}R})\leq{\mathcal{}V}_{t}^{x,D,\theta^{0}}({\mathcal{}R})\equiv K_{t}^{x,D},\quad t\geq 0,\quad{\mathcal{}R}\in(0,1).

Step 2. Continuity.

Let
0<{\mathcal{}R}_{1}<{\mathcal{}R}_{2}<1
and let
\theta^{*,2}\in\Theta^{D}({\mathcal{}R}_{2})\subset\Theta
denote the worst-case kernel for
{\mathcal{}R}_{2}
so that
{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}({\mathcal{}R}_{2})={\mathcal{}V}^{x,D,\theta^{*,2}}({\mathcal{}R}_{2})
(see Lemma
3.11
). Specifically,

(3.59)

\displaystyle\theta^{*,2}_{t}:=-{\mathcal{}R}_{2}\frac{{\mathbb{Z}}^{x,D}_{t}({\mathcal{}R}_{2})}{{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{t}({\mathcal{}R}_{2})}=-\frac{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}{1-{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}\frac{Z^{x,D}_{t}({\mathcal{}R}_{2})}{V^{x,D}_{t}({\mathcal{}R}_{2})},\quad t\geq 0.

Let
\eta^{\theta^{*,2}}
be defined as in (
1.1
) with
\theta=\theta^{*,2}
. By Lemma
3.21
i.,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{t}({\mathcal{}R}_{1})\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}^{\theta^{*,2}}\bigg[\int_{t\wedge\tau^{x,D}}^{\tau^{x,D}}e^{-\rho s}dD_{s}+\xi_{\tau^{x,D}}+\frac{1}{2{\mathcal{}R}_{1}}\int_{t\wedge\tau^{x,D}}^{\tau^{x,D}}{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{s}({\mathcal{}R}_{1})(\theta^{*,2}_{s})^{2}ds\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{t}({\mathcal{}R}_{2})+\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{1}{{\mathcal{}R}_{1}}-\frac{1}{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}\Big)\mathbb{E}_{t}^{\theta^{*,2}}\bigg[\int_{t\wedge\tau^{x,D}}^{\tau^{x,D}}(\theta^{*,2}_{s})^{2}{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{s}({\mathcal{}R}_{1})ds\bigg]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{t}^{\theta^{*,2}}\bigg[\int_{t\wedge\tau^{x,D}}^{\tau^{x,D}}\frac{(\theta^{*,2}_{s})^{2}}{2{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}\big({\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{s}({\mathcal{}R}_{1})-{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{s}({\mathcal{}R}_{2})\big)ds\bigg].

Since
{\mathcal{}V}_{t}^{x,D}({\mathcal{}R}_{1})\geq{\mathcal{}V}_{t}^{x,D}({\mathcal{}R}_{2})
(by Step 1), Grönwall’s inequality gives

\displaystyle{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{t}({\mathcal{}R}_{1})-{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{t}({\mathcal{}R}_{2})

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}-{\mathcal{}R}_{1}}{2{\mathcal{}R}_{1}{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}\mathbb{E}_{t}^{\theta^{*,2}}\bigg[\int_{t\wedge\tau^{x,D}}^{\tau^{x,D}}e^{\int_{t\wedge\tau^{x,D}}^{s}\frac{(\theta^{*,2}_{u})^{2}}{2{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}du}(\theta^{*,2}_{s})^{2}{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{s}({\mathcal{}R}_{1})ds\bigg]

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}-{\mathcal{}R}_{1}}{2{\mathcal{}R}_{1}{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}\mathbb{E}_{t}\bigg[\int_{t\wedge\tau^{x,D}}^{\tau^{x,D}}\eta_{s}^{\theta^{*,2}}e^{\int_{t\wedge\tau^{x,D}}^{s}\frac{(\theta^{*,2}_{u})^{2}}{2{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}du}(\theta^{*,2}_{s})^{2}{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{s}({\mathcal{}R}_{1})ds\bigg]:=\mathrm{I}_{t}.

Using Itô’s formula for
\log({\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{s}({\mathcal{}R}_{2}))
, a direct calculation gives

\displaystyle\eta_{s}^{\theta^{*,2}}e^{\int_{t}^{s}\frac{(\theta^{*,2}_{u})^{2}}{2{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}du}

\displaystyle=\exp\bigg\{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}\bigg(\log\Big(\frac{{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{t}({\mathcal{}R}_{2}))}{{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{s}({\mathcal{}R}_{2}))}\Big)-\int_{t}^{s}\frac{e^{-\rho u}}{{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{u}({\mathcal{}R}_{2}))}dD_{u}\bigg)\bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\frac{{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{t}({\mathcal{}R}_{2}))}{{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{s}({\mathcal{}R}_{2}))}\bigg)^{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}.

Hence, using the explicit form of
\theta^{*,2}
in (
3.59
), we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}_{t}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}-{\mathcal{}R}_{1}}{2{\mathcal{}R}_{1}{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}\mathbb{E}_{t}\bigg[\int_{t\wedge\tau^{x,D}}^{\tau^{x,D}}\bigg(\frac{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}^{2}}{(1-{\mathcal{}R}_{2})^{2}}\frac{|Z_{s}^{x,D}({\mathcal{}R}_{2})|^{2}}{V_{s}^{x,D}({\mathcal{}R}_{2})\big({\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{s}({\mathcal{}R}_{2})\big)^{1-{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\times\bigg(\frac{{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{t}({\mathcal{}R}_{2}))}{{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{s}({\mathcal{}R}_{2}))}\bigg)^{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{s}({\mathcal{}R}_{1})\bigg)ds\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{({\mathcal{}R}_{2}-{\mathcal{}R}_{1}){\mathcal{}R}_{2}}{2{\mathcal{}R}_{1}(1-{\mathcal{}R}_{2})^{2}}\frac{\big({\mathcal{}V}_{t}^{x,D}({\mathcal{}R}_{2})\big)^{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}}{C^{\frac{1}{1-{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}}}\mathbb{E}_{t}\bigg[\sup_{t\geq 0}\frac{K^{x,D}_{t}}{V^{x,D}_{t}({\mathcal{}R}_{2})}\int_{t\wedge\tau^{x,D}}^{\tau^{x,D}}|Z^{x,D}_{s}({\mathcal{}R}_{2})|^{2}ds\bigg],

where
C^{\frac{1}{1-{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}}
is a lower bound for
{\mathcal{}V}_{s}^{x,D}({\mathcal{}R}_{2})
, and we have used the fact that
{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{t}({\mathcal{}R}_{1})\leq K^{x,D}_{t}
. To bound the expectation, we use Lemma
3.21
ii to have

\displaystyle\sup_{t\geq 0}\frac{K_{t}^{x,D}}{V^{x,D}_{t}({\mathcal{}R}_{2})}\leq\sup_{t\geq 0}\Big(1+{\mathcal{}R}_{2}\frac{\overline{c}_{t}}{C}\Big)=\sup_{t\geq 0}(1+{\mathcal{}R}_{2}\overline{c}_{t}^{\prime})<\infty,\text{ ${\mathbb{P}}$-a.s.,}

where
\overline{c}_{t}^{\prime}
is the constant from Lemma
3.21
ii, and we use
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\geq 0}\overline{c}_{t}^{\prime}]<\infty
.
Together with
\int_{0}^{\tau^{x,D}}|Z_{t}^{x,D}({\mathcal{}R}_{2})|^{2}dt\in L^{1}({\mathcal{}F}_{\tau})
, there exists
C^{\prime}_{t}>0
such that

\displaystyle 0\leq{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{t}({\mathcal{}R}_{1})-{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{t}({\mathcal{}R}_{2})\leq C^{\prime}_{t}({\mathcal{}R}_{2}-{\mathcal{}R}_{1}),\text{ ${\mathbb{P}}$-a.s..}

This establishes the continuity of
(0,1)\ni{\mathcal{}R}\mapsto{\mathcal{}V}_{t}^{x,D}({\mathcal{}R})
. In addition, the limit
\lim_{{\mathcal{}R}\downarrow 0}{\mathcal{}V}_{t}^{x,D}({\mathcal{}R})=K^{x,D}_{t}
follows from Lemma
3.21
ii, so (
2.18
) holds.

∎

We next provide the proof of Corollary

Proof.

Since
{\mathcal{}V}_{t}^{x}({\mathcal{}R})=\esssup_{D\in{\mathcal{}A}^{x}}{\mathcal{}V}^{x,D}_{t}({\mathcal{}R})

Step 1. Continuity of

J^{\theta^{*,2}}(x;D,f,{\mathcal{}R}):=\mathbb{E}^{\theta^{*,2}}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau^{x,D}}e^{-\rho s}\Big(dD_{s}+\frac{({\theta_{s}^{*,2}})^{2}}{2{\mathcal{}R}}f(X_{s}^{D})ds\Big)+e^{-\rho\tau^{x,D}}\xi_{0}\bigg].

Following similar arguments as in the proof of Theorem
2.18
(Step 2), we obtain

\displaystyle 0\leq v^{*}(x;{\mathcal{}R}_{1})-v^{*}(x;{\mathcal{}R}_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{({\mathcal{}R}_{2}-{\mathcal{}R}_{1}){\mathcal{}R}_{2}}{2{\mathcal{}R}_{1}}v^{*}(x;{\mathcal{}R}_{2})^{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau^{x,D_{1}^{*}}}e^{-\rho s}\tilde{v}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{1},{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}(X_{s}^{D_{1}^{*}})ds\bigg]\leq C_{\mathcal{}I}({\mathcal{}R}_{2}-{\mathcal{}R}_{1}),

with
\tilde{v}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{1},{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}(\cdot):=((v^{*})^{\prime}(\cdot;{\mathcal{}R}_{1})\sigma(\cdot))^{2}\frac{v^{*}(\cdot;{\mathcal{}R}_{1})}{v^{*}(\cdot;{\mathcal{}R}_{2})}(v^{*}(\cdot;{\mathcal{}R}_{2}))^{-(1-{\mathcal{}R}_{2})}

and some constant
C_{\mathcal{}I}>0
(not depending on
{\mathcal{}R}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{}R}_{2}
). This uniform bound holds since
X^{D_{{\mathcal{}R}}^{*}}
is valued in
[0,\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}]
and
\xi_{0}+x\leq v^{*}(x;{\mathcal{}R})\leq\xi_{0}+x+\overline{\mu}/\rho
for all
{\mathcal{}R}\in{\mathcal{}I}
.
Therefore,
v^{*}(x;{\mathcal{}R})
is continuous in
{\mathcal{}R}
.

Step 2. Continuity of
{\mathcal{}I}\ni{\mathcal{}R}\mapsto(v^{*})^{\prime}(0;{\mathcal{}R})
.

Note that
v^{*}(0;{\mathcal{}R})=\xi_{0}
, and by Theorem
2.18
, the mapping
{\mathcal{}R}\mapsto v^{*}(x;{\mathcal{}R})
is decreasing. Thus,
(v^{*})^{\prime}(0;{\mathcal{}R}_{1})\geq(v^{*})^{\prime}(0;{\mathcal{}R}_{2})
.
Suppose, to the contrary, that
{\mathcal{}R}\mapsto(v^{*})^{\prime}(0;{\mathcal{}R})
is not continuous. Then there exists
\tilde{{\mathcal{}R}}\in\operatorname{int}{\mathcal{}I}
such that

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}:=\liminf_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0+}(v^{*})^{\prime}(0;\tilde{{\mathcal{}R}}-\epsilon)>\limsup_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0+}(v^{*})^{\prime}(0;\tilde{{\mathcal{}R}}+\epsilon):=\alpha_{2}\geq 1,

where the inequality
\alpha_{2}\geq 1
follows from the fact that
(v^{*})^{\prime}(0;{\mathcal{}R})\geq 1
, as it satisfies the HJB-VI (
2.16
). For a sufficiently small
\delta_{e}>0
,we have

\displaystyle(v^{*})^{\prime}(0;\tilde{{\mathcal{}R}}-\epsilon)>(v^{*})^{\prime}(0;\tilde{{\mathcal{}R}}+\epsilon)+\frac{\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}}{2},\quad\mbox{for all }\epsilon\in(0,\delta_{e}).

Since
v^{*}(x;{\mathcal{}R})
is twice continuously differentiable w.r.t.
x
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, the derivative
(v^{*})^{\prime}(x;{\mathcal{}R})
is continuous in
x
. Thus, for a sufficiently small
\delta_{x}>0
,

\displaystyle(v^{*})^{\prime}(x;\tilde{{\mathcal{}R}}-\epsilon)>(v^{*})^{\prime}(x;\tilde{{\mathcal{}R}}+\epsilon)+\frac{\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}}{2},\quad\mbox{for all }x\in[0,\delta_{x}],\epsilon\in(0,\delta_{e}).

Integrating both sides from
x=0
to
x=\delta_{x}
yields

\displaystyle v^{*}(\delta_{x};\tilde{{\mathcal{}R}}-\epsilon)>v^{*}(\delta_{x};\tilde{{\mathcal{}R}}+\epsilon)+\frac{\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}}{2}\delta_{x},\quad\mbox{for all }\epsilon\in(0,\delta_{e}).

Taking the limit as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
, we obtain
v^{*}(\delta_{x};\tilde{{\mathcal{}R}}^{-})>v^{*}(\delta_{x};\tilde{{\mathcal{}R}}^{+})
, which contradicts to the continuity of
{\mathcal{}R}\mapsto v^{*}(x;{\mathcal{}R})
in Corollary
2.20
. We conclude that
{\mathcal{}I}\ni{\mathcal{}R}\mapsto(v^{*})^{\prime}(0;{\mathcal{}R})
must be continuous.

Step 3. Continuity of
{\mathcal{}I}\ni{\mathcal{}R}\mapsto g_{b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}}}(x)
.

Recall
g_{b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}}}(x)\equiv g_{b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}},0}(x)
satisfying

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\sigma^{2}(x)}{2}(g_{b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}}})^{\prime}(x)+\mu(x)g_{b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}}}(x)+\frac{(1-{\mathcal{}R})}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)(g_{b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}}})^{2}(x)=\rho,\\
&g_{b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}}}(b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}})=1/\psi^{+}(b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}}),\end{aligned}\right.

as in (
3.22
).
For notational simplicity,
let
g_{i}
denote
g_{b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{i}}}
for
i=1,2
. Then,

\displaystyle g_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=g_{i}(0)-\int_{0}^{x}g^{\prime}_{i}(y)\,dy=\frac{(v^{*})^{\prime}(0;{\mathcal{}R}_{i})}{v^{*}(0;{\mathcal{}R}_{i})}-\int_{0}^{x}g^{\prime}_{i}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=\frac{(v^{*})^{\prime}(0;{\mathcal{}R}_{i})}{\xi_{0}}+\int_{0}^{x}\frac{2\mu(y)}{\sigma^{2}(y)}g_{i}(y)+(1-{\mathcal{}R}_{1})g_{i}^{2}(y)-\rho dy.

Therefore, for
x\in[0,\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}]
,

\displaystyle|g_{1}(x)-g_{2}(x)|\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\xi_{0}}\left|(v^{*})^{\prime}(0;{\mathcal{}R}_{1})-(v^{*})^{\prime}(0;{\mathcal{}R}_{2})\right|+C_{\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}}\int_{0}^{x}|g_{1}(y)-g_{2}(y)|dy

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{x}({\mathcal{}R}_{2}-{\mathcal{}R}_{1})g_{2}^{2}(y)dy,

where
C_{\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}}:=\sup_{0\leq y\leq\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}}\left\{\frac{2\mu(y)}{\sigma^{2}(y)}+(1-{\mathcal{}R}_{1})|g_{1}(y)+g_{2}(y)|\right\}
, for which we recall that
\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}
is a uniform upper bound for
b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}}
by Proposition
2.16
.
By Grönwall’s inequality, there is some constant
c>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq x\leq\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}}|g_{1}(x)-g_{2}(x)|\leq ce^{C_{\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}}}\big(|(v^{*})^{\prime}(0;{\mathcal{}R}_{1})-(v^{*})^{\prime}(0;{\mathcal{}R}_{2})|+|{\mathcal{}R}_{2}-{\mathcal{}R}_{1}|\big).

Since
{\mathcal{}I}\ni{\mathcal{}R}\mapsto(v^{*})^{\prime}(0;{\mathcal{}R})
is continuous (by Step 1), we have

(3.60)

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}|{\mathcal{}R}_{2}-{\mathcal{}R}_{1}|\to 0^{+}\\
{\mathcal{}R}_{1},\,{\mathcal{}R}_{2}\in{\mathcal{}I}\end{subarray}}\sup_{0\leq x\leq\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}}|g_{1}(x)-g_{2}(x)|=0.

Step 4. Continuity of
{\mathcal{}I}\ni{\mathcal{}R}\mapsto b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}}
.

By definition of
b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}}
in Proposition
3.12
,
(
3.21
) and (
3.39
), we have

(3.61)

\displaystyle\frac{\psi^{+}\big(b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{1}};{\mathcal{}R}_{1}\big)e^{-\int_{0}^{b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{1}}}g_{1}(y)dy}}{\psi^{+}\big(b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}};{\mathcal{}R}_{2}\big)e^{-\int_{0}^{b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}}g_{2}(y)dy}}=\frac{v^{*}(0;{\mathcal{}R}_{1})}{v^{*}(0;{\mathcal{}R}_{2})}=1.

To proceed, we recall several properties established:
the mapping
{\mathcal{}R}\mapsto\psi^{+}(x;{\mathcal{}R})
is continuous and uniformly decreasing for
x\geq 0
; since
\psi^{+}(x;{\mathcal{}R})-x
is decreasing for
x>\underline{b}
, it follows that
(\psi^{+})^{\prime}(x;{\mathcal{}R})\leq 1
for
x>\underline{b}
; Lemma
3.15
i. asserts that
g_{i}(x)\geq 1/\psi^{+}(x;{\mathcal{}R}_{i})
for
x>\underline{b}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{i}}
.

Taking logarithms on both sides of (
3.61
), we consider the cases separately.

Case 1:

b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{1}}<b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}
.
We have

\displaystyle\ln\psi^{+}\big(b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{1}};{\mathcal{}R}_{1}\big)-\ln\psi^{+}\big(b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}};{\mathcal{}R}_{1}\big)+\int_{b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{1}}}^{b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}}g_{1}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}}\big(g_{1}(y)-g_{2}(y)\big)\,dy,

\displaystyle\int_{b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{1}}}^{b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}}\left(g_{1}(y)-\frac{(\psi^{+})^{\prime}(y;{\mathcal{}R}_{1})}{\psi^{+}(y;{\mathcal{}R}_{1})}\right)\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}}|g_{1}(y)-g_{2}(y)|\,dy,

\displaystyle 0\leq\int_{b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{1}}}^{b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}}\left(g_{1}(y)-\frac{1}{\psi^{+}(y;{\mathcal{}R}_{1})}\right)\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}}|g_{1}(y)-g_{2}(y)|\,dy,

where the first inequality follows from the monotonicity of
{\mathcal{}R}\mapsto\psi^{+}(x;{\mathcal{}R})
, and in the last line, we use
(\psi^{+})^{\prime}(x;{\mathcal{}R}_{1})\leq 1
and
g_{1}(x)\geq 1/\psi^{+}(x;{\mathcal{}R}_{1})
for
x>b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{1}}>\underline{b}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{1}}
.

Case 2:

b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{1}}>b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}
.
By analogous reasoning,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle<\int_{b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}}^{b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{1}}}g_{2}(y)-\frac{1}{\psi^{+}(y;{\mathcal{}R}_{1})}\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\left|\ln\psi^{+}\big(b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}};{\mathcal{}R}_{1}\big)-\ln\psi^{+}\big(b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}};{\mathcal{}R}_{2}\big)\right|+\int_{0}^{b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{1}}}|g_{2}(y)-g_{1}(y)|\,dy,

where the strict inequality holds because
\psi^{+}(x;{\mathcal{}R}_{2})<\psi^{+}(x;{\mathcal{}R}_{1})
, which implies
g_{2}(x)\geq 1/\psi^{+}(x;{\mathcal{}R}_{2})>1/\psi^{+}(x;{\mathcal{}R}_{1})
for
\underline{b}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}<b^{*}_{{\mathcal{}R}_{2}}<x
.

For both cases, by (
3.60
) and the continuity of
{\mathcal{}R}\mapsto\psi^{+}(x;{\mathcal{}R})
, we can have the desired continuity of
b^{*}_{\mathcal{}R}
.

∎
