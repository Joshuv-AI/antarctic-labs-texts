Another quantum version of Sanov theorem
Masahito Hayashi

Abstract
We study how to extend Sanov theorem to the quantum setting. Although a quantum version of the Sanov theorem was proposed in Bjelakovic et al (Commun. Math. Phys., 260, p.659 (2005)), the classical case of their statement is not the same as Sanov theorem because Sanov theorem discusses the behavior of the empirical distribution when the empirical distribution is different from the true distribution, but they studied a problem related to quantum hypothesis testing, whose classical version can be shown by classical Sanov theorem. We propose another quantum version of Sanov theorem by considering the quantum analog of the empirical distribution.



I
Introduction

Quantum Sanov theorem is known as a theorem for
quantum hypothesis testing with a composite null hypothesis and a simple alternative hypothesis
[
1
,
2
]
,
which is a natural extension of quantum Stein’s lemma
[
3
,
4
]
.
However, the original Sanov theorem has a
slightly different statement in the classical setting
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
.
That is, when
n
data
X^{n}
is subject to
the independent and identical distribution of
Q
,
the original Sanov theorem
evaluates the probability that the empirical distribution of
X
is
P
.
That is, this probability goes to zero exponentially,
and its exponent is given as the relative entropy
D(P\|Q)
.
Using this fact, we can discuss the above type of hypothesis testing in the classical case.
Hence, the authors of the references
[
2
,
1
]

considered that the above statement is a quantum extension of Sanov theorem.
That is, although the original Sanov theorem focuses on the empirical distribution,
the quantum Sanov theorem by
[
2
,
1
]

does not consider any quantum extension of an empirical distribution.

Therefore, it is possible to consider another quantum version of
Sanov theorem by considering a quantum extension of an empirical distribution.
In this paper, we define a quantum analog of an empirical distribution
by using a density matrix
\rho

and discusses the case when
the true state is the
n
-fold tensor product of a density matrix
\sigma
.
However, when we simply measure each system over a given basis repetitively,
we cannot distinguish two different states that
have the same diagonal elements for the basis
{\cal B}
.
In fact, the paper
[
8
]
studied
Sanov theorem based on the empirical distribution
based on this method,
In this paper, to avoid the above problem,
we employ the simultaneous measurement of
the empirical distribution and the decomposition given by Schur duality
because the
n
-fold tensor product of a density matrix

\sigma
satisfies the symmetry given by Schur duality.
That is, the use of the decomposition given by Schur duality is essential
to avoid the above problem.
Then, we evaluate the probability with respect to the outcome
of the above simultaneous measurement.

Indeed, many results in information theory for the classical system
employs empirical distribution.
For example, the strong converse for channel resolvability for fixed input

[
9
]

employs employs empirical distribution.
To exploit such approaches, we need to establish a quantum extension of
Sanov theorem based on a quantum extension of
empirical distribution.
In this way, this kind of result is strongly desired in the area of quantum information theory.

II
Review of existing results

We focus on a
d
-dimensional quantum system
{\cal H}

spanned by the basis
\{|j\rangle\}_{j=1}^{d}
.
We consider the
n
-fold tensor product system
{\cal H}^{\otimes n}
.
To state the existing Sanov theorem,
we consider a set
S
of density matrices on
{\cal H}

and a density matrix
\sigma
on
{\cal H}
.

We consider a
d
-dimensional Hilbert space
{\cal H}
and a state
\rho
on
{\cal H}
.
We assume the
n
-fold independently and identical distributed (iid) condition
and consider the following two hypotheses;

\displaystyle H_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}

\displaystyle\hbox{The state is }\sigma^{\otimes n},

(1)

\displaystyle H_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}

\displaystyle\hbox{The state is }\rho^{\otimes n}\hbox{ with }\rho\in S.

(2)

To address this problem, we focus on the following quantity.

\displaystyle\beta_{\epsilon,n}(S\|\sigma)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\min_{0\leq T\leq I}\Big\{\mathrm{Tr}T\sigma^{\otimes n}\Big|\max_{\rho\in S}\mathrm{Tr}(I-T)\rho^{\otimes n}\leq\epsilon\Big\}.

(3)

Then, we define the quantum relative entropy

\displaystyle D(\rho\|\sigma)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathrm{Tr}\rho(\log\rho-\log\sigma).

(4)

The relation

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\beta_{\epsilon,n}(S\|\sigma)=\inf_{\rho\in S}D(\rho\|\sigma)

(5)

is known as quantum Sanov theorem
[
1
,
2
]
.
The above theorem means that
the choice of the test does not depend on a state
\rho\in S
in the sense of quantum Stein’s lemma.
In fact,
when our measurement is required
to achieve the optimal performance
in the sense of quantum Stein’s lemma,
the measurement does not depend on a state
\rho\in S

[
10
]
.

Next, we review the original Sanov theorem in the classical case, i.e.,
when all possible states are diagonal state with respect to
a basis
{\cal B}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{|v_{j}\rangle\}_{j=1}^{d}
.
We denote the set of diagonal states for
the basis
{\cal B}
by
{\cal S}[{\cal B}]
.
Also, we assume that our observation is based on the measurement based on
the basis
{\cal B}
.
When our system is
{\cal H}^{\otimes n}
,
our observation is given as a basis

|v[x^{n}]\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=|v_{x_{1}},\ldots,v_{x_{n}}\rangle

with a data
x^{n}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.
In this case, we obtain the data
x^{n}
.
The state
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}|v_{x_{j}}\rangle\langle v_{x_{j}}|

is called its empirical state under the basis
{\cal B}
,
and is denoted by
\rho_{x^{n},{\cal B}}
.
In the
n
-fold tensor product case,
we denote the set of empirical states under the basis
{\cal B}

by
{\cal S}_{n}[{\cal B}]
.
Then, for an empirical state
\rho\in{\cal S}_{n}[{\cal B}]
,
we consider the projection

\displaystyle T_{\rho,{\cal B}}^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{x^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\rho_{x^{n},{\cal B}}=\rho}|v[x^{n}]\rangle\langle v[x^{n}]|.

(6)

When
\rho\notin{\cal S}_{n}[{\cal B}]
,

T_{\rho,{\cal B}}^{n}
is defined to be
0
.
For any diagonal state
\sigma\in{\cal S}[{\cal B}]
,
the eigenvalue of

\sigma^{\otimes n}T_{\rho,{\cal B}}^{n}

is calculated by using the operator norm as

\displaystyle\|\sigma^{\otimes n}T_{\rho,{\cal B}}^{n}\|=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\langle v_{x_{j}}|\sigma|v_{x_{j}}\rangle^{n\langle v_{x_{j}}|\rho|v_{x_{j}}\rangle}=e^{n\mathrm{Tr}\rho\log\sigma}.

(7)

Since
\mathrm{Tr}T_{\rho,{\cal B}}^{n}\cong e^{nH(\rho)}
,
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\sigma^{\otimes n}T_{\rho,{\cal B}}^{n}\cong e^{n\mathrm{Tr}\rho\log\sigma}e^{nH(\rho)}=e^{-nD(\rho\|\sigma)},

(8)

which is known as Sanov theorem
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
.

Given a subset
S\subset{\cal S}({\cal B})
,
we denote the interior of
S
by
\mathrm{Int}\,(S)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Int}\,(S)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{\rho\in S|\exists\epsilon>0,U_{\epsilon}(\rho)\subset S\},

(9)

where
U_{\epsilon}(\rho)
is the
\epsilon
-neighborhood of
\rho

in the set
{\cal S}[{\cal B}]
.
Since
a state
\rho\in\mathrm{Int}\,(S)
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\rho^{\otimes n}\sum_{\rho^{\prime}\in S}T_{\rho^{\prime},{\cal B}}^{n}\to 1\hbox{ as }n\to\infty,

(10)

the converse part of Stein’s lemma
[
4
]
implies

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{-1}{n}\log\mathrm{Tr}\sigma^{\otimes n}\sum_{\rho^{\prime}\in S}T_{\rho^{\prime},{\cal B}}^{n}\leq D(\rho\|\sigma).

(11)

Since the number of possible empirical distributions is only a polynomial for
n
,
the combination of (
8
) and (
11
) implies

\displaystyle\inf_{\rho\in S}D(\rho\|\sigma)\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{-1}{n}\log\mathrm{Tr}\sigma^{\otimes n}\sum_{\rho^{\prime}\in S}T_{\rho^{\prime},{\cal B}}^{n}\leq\inf_{\rho\in\mathrm{Int}\,(S)}D(\rho\|\sigma),

(12)

which is also considered as Sanov theorem
[
5
,
6
]
.
When
\inf_{\rho\in\mathrm{Int}\,(S)}D(\rho\|\sigma)=\inf_{\rho\in S}D(\rho\|\sigma)
,
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\sigma^{\otimes n}\sum_{\rho^{\prime}\in S}T_{\rho^{\prime},{\cal B}}^{n}\cong e^{-n\inf_{\rho\in S}D(\rho\|\sigma)}.

(13)

Since the converse part of (
5
), i.e., its part of
\leq
, is guaranteed by
Stein’s lemma,
(
13
) implies (
5
) when
the states in
S
and
\sigma
are commutative with each other.
However, we cannot say that
(
5
) implies (
13
).

In addition, when
we simply measure all subsystems of
{\cal H}^{\otimes n}

with the basis
{\cal B}
,
it is impossible to distinguish the pure state
\sigma
and
the diagonal state
\sigma_{{\cal B}}
for the basis
{\cal B}
with the diagonal element of
\sigma
.
Therefore, it is necessary to study the quantum extension of
(
13
)
separately the existing result (
5
), which
can distinguish the pure state
\sigma
and
the diagonal state
\sigma_{{\cal B}}
.

III
Main result

To formulate another quantum extension of Sanov theorem,
we consider the case when
the state
\sigma
is not necessarily diagonal with respect to the basis
{\cal B}
.
For this aim, we prepare several notations.
Similar to the references
[
18
,
10
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
2
,
19
,
11
,
20
]
,
we employ Schur duality of
{\cal H}^{\otimes n}
.
A sequence of monotone-increasing non-negative integers

\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})
is called Young index.
Although many references
[
20
,
24
,
23
]
define the Young index as
monotone-decreasing non-negative integers,
we define it in the opposite way for notational convenience.
We denote the set of Young indices
\bm{\lambda}
with the condition

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}=n
by
Y_{d}^{n}
.
Also, the set of probability distributions
p=(p_{j})_{j=1}^{d}
with the condition

p_{1}\leq p_{2}\leq\ldots\leq p_{d}
by
{\cal P}_{d}
.
For any density
\rho
on
{\cal H}
,
the eigenvalues of
\rho
forms an element of
{\cal P}_{d}
, which
is denoted by
p(\rho)
.
Let
{\cal P}_{d}^{n}
be the set of elements
p
of
{\cal P}_{d}
such that

p_{j}
is an integer time of
1/n
.
We define the majorization relation
\succ
in two elements of

{\cal P}_{d}
and
Y_{d}^{n}
.
For two element
p,p^{\prime}
of
{\cal P}_{d}
, we say that

p\succ p^{\prime}
when

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}^{\prime}

(14)

for
k=1,\ldots,d-1
.
We define the majorization relation
\succ
for two elements of
Y_{d}^{n}

in the same way.

As explained in
[
20
, Section 6.2]
, we have

\displaystyle{\cal H}^{\otimes n}=\bigoplus_{\bm{\lambda}\in Y_{d}^{n}}{\cal U}_{\bm{\lambda}}\otimes{\cal V}_{\bm{\lambda}}.

(15)

Here,

{\cal U}_{\bm{\lambda}}
expresses the irreducible space of
\mathop{\rm SU}(d)

and
{\cal V}_{\bm{\lambda}}
expresses the irreducible space of
the representation
\pi
of the permutation group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.
We define

d_{\bm{\lambda}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\dim{\cal U}_{\bm{\lambda}}
.
As shown in
[
20
, (6.16)]
, the dimension
d_{\bm{\lambda}}
is upper bounded as

\displaystyle d_{\bm{\lambda}}\leq(n+1)^{\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}.

(16)

We denote the projection to the space
{\cal U}_{\bm{\lambda}}\otimes{\cal V}_{\bm{\lambda}}
by

P_{\bm{\lambda}}
.
For
\bm{\lambda}\in Y_{d}^{n}
and
\rho\in{\cal S}_{n}[{\cal B}]
,
we define the projection

\displaystyle T_{\bm{\lambda},\rho,{\cal B}}^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=P_{\bm{\lambda}}T_{\rho,{\cal B}}^{n}.

(17)

The relation

T_{\bm{\lambda},\rho,{\cal B}}^{n}\neq 0
holds
if and only if

\displaystyle\bm{\lambda}\succ np(\rho).

(18)

This is because the weight vectors in the range of

T_{\rho,{\cal B}}^{n}
are restricted to
vectors whose weight is majorized by the highest weight

\bm{\lambda}
as (
18
)
[
23
,
20
,
24
]
.
We also define the set

\displaystyle{\cal R}[{\cal B}]

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{(p,\rho)\in{\cal P}_{d}\times{\cal S}[{\cal B}]|p\succ p(\rho)\},

(19)

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{n}[{\cal B}]

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}={\cal R}[{\cal B}]\cap({\cal P}_{d}^{n}\times{\cal S}_{n}[{\cal B}]).

(20)

That is, the decomposition
\{T_{\bm{\lambda},\rho,{\cal B}}^{n}\}_{(\frac{\bm{\lambda}}{n},\rho)\in{\cal R}_{n}[{\cal B}]}

is the joint measurement of
the Schur sampling and
the measurement to get the empirical distribution based on the basis
{\cal B}

[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
11
]
.
Notice that

\displaystyle|{\cal R}_{n}[{\cal B}]|\leq(n+1)^{2(d-1)}

(21)

because
|{\cal P}_{d}^{n}|,|{\cal S}_{n}[{\cal B}]|\leq(n+1)^{d-1}
.
The following subset of
{\cal R}[{\cal B}]
plays a key role in our quantum analog of Sanov theorem.
For
\rho\in{\cal S}[{\cal B}]
and
r>0
, we define

\displaystyle S_{\rho,r}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\big\{(p^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\in{\cal R}[{\cal B}]\big|r(p^{\prime},\rho^{\prime}\|\rho)\leq r\big\},

(22)

where
r(p^{\prime},\rho^{\prime}\|\rho)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=D(\rho^{\prime}\|\rho)+H(\rho^{\prime})-H(p^{\prime})
.
The set
S_{\rho,r}
is characterized as follows.

Lemma 1
.

For
\rho\in{\cal S}[{\cal B}]
and
r>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\rho^{\otimes n}\Big(\sum_{(p^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\in S_{\rho,r}^{c}\cap{\cal R}_{n}[{\cal B}]}T_{np^{\prime},\rho^{\prime},{\cal B}}^{n}\Big)\leq(n+1)^{\frac{(d+4)(d-1)}{2}}e^{-nr},

(23)

and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{-1}{n}\log\mathrm{Tr}\rho^{\otimes n}\Big(\sum_{(p^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\in S_{\rho,r}^{c}\cap{\cal R}_{n}[{\cal B}]}T_{np^{\prime},\rho^{\prime},{\cal B}}^{n}\Big)=r.

(24)

This lemma is shown in Section
V
.

To study the case with an arbitrary state
\sigma
,
we recall the sandwich Rényi relative entropy
[
21
,
22
]

\displaystyle D_{1+s}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{\phi(-s|\rho\|\sigma)}{s},

(25)

\displaystyle\phi(s|\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\log\mathrm{Tr}(\sigma^{\frac{s}{2(1-s)}}\rho\sigma^{\frac{s}{2(1-s)}})^{1-s}.

(26)

We have the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

The map
s\mapsto\phi(s|\rho\|\sigma)
is convex.

This lemma is shown in Section
IV
.
The quantum relative entropy
D(\rho\|\sigma)
is characterized as

\displaystyle D(\rho\|\sigma)=\lim_{s\to 0}D_{1+s}(\rho\|\sigma).

(27)

Now, we consider another quantum extension of relative entropy as

\displaystyle\hat{D}(\rho\|\sigma)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{-\phi(1-s|\sigma\|\rho)}{s}.

(28)

Since the map
s\mapsto-\phi(1-s|\sigma\|\rho)
is concave due to
Lemma
2
,
the value
\frac{-\phi(1-s|\sigma\|\rho)}{s}
is monotone decreasing
for
s>0
, which implies

\displaystyle\hat{D}(\rho\|\sigma)\geq\frac{-\phi(1-s|\sigma\|\rho)}{s}.

(29)

In particular, substituting
1/2
into
s
, we have

\displaystyle\hat{D}(\rho\|\sigma)\geq-2\phi(1/2|\sigma\|\rho)=-2\log\mathrm{Tr}|\rho^{1/2}\sigma^{1/2}|.

(30)

When
\rho\neq\sigma
,
the fidelity
\mathrm{Tr}|\rho^{1/2}\sigma^{1/2}|

is strictly smaller than
1
. Then,

\hat{D}(\rho\|\sigma)
is strictly positive due to (
29
).

When
\rho
and
\sigma
are commutative with each other,

\hat{D}(\sigma\|\rho)={D}(\sigma\|\rho)
.
To describe the exponents, we define the function;

\displaystyle\hat{B}_{e}(r|\rho\|\sigma)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sup_{s\in(0,1)}\frac{-(1-s)r-\phi(1-s|\sigma\|\rho)}{s}.

(31)

Its limit is characterized as follows.

Lemma 3
.

We have the relations

\displaystyle D(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle\geq\hat{D}(\rho\|\sigma),

(32)

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to+0}\hat{B}_{e}(r|\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle=\hat{D}(\rho\|\sigma).

(33)

This lemma is shown in Section
VII
.
Then, we have the following versions of quantum Sanov theorem.

Theorem 4
.

We fix a basis
{\cal B}
.
For states
\rho_{n}\in{\cal S}_{n}[{\cal B}]

and Young index
\bm{\lambda}_{n}\in Y_{d}^{n}

with the condition (
18
),
we have

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{n}\log\mathrm{Tr}\sigma^{\otimes n}T_{\bm{\lambda}_{n},\rho_{n},{\cal B}}^{n}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{-(1-s)r_{n}-\phi(1-s|\sigma\|\rho)}{s}-\frac{d(d+3)}{2sn}\log(n+1),

(34)

where
r_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=r(\frac{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}{n},\rho_{n}\|\rho)
.
Therefore, when
r_{n}\to r
,
taking the limit
n\to\infty
and the supremum for
s
,
we have

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{-1}{n}\log\mathrm{Tr}\sigma^{\otimes n}T_{\bm{\lambda}_{n},\rho_{n},{\cal B}}^{n}\geq\hat{B}_{e}(r|\rho\|\sigma).

(35)

In particular, when
r_{n}\to 0
, we have

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{-1}{n}\log\mathrm{Tr}\sigma^{\otimes n}T_{\bm{\lambda}_{n},\rho_{n},{\cal B}}^{n}\geq\hat{D}(\rho\|\sigma).

(36)

This theorem is shown in Section
VIII
.
The projection
T_{\bm{\lambda}_{n},\rho_{n},{\cal B}}^{n}

with
r_{n}\to 0
takes a similar role of the empirical distribution
in the original Sanov theorem (
8
).
To get the same behavior as (
8
), we need the opposite inequality.
For the opposite inequality,
as explained in the following theorem,
we need to focus on the summand in the set

S_{\rho,r}\cap{\cal R}_{n}[{\cal B}]
.

Theorem 5
.

For
\rho\in{\cal S}[{\cal B}]
and
r>0
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{-1}{n}\log\mathrm{Tr}\sigma^{\otimes n}\Big(\sum_{(p^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\in S_{\rho,r}\cap{\cal R}_{n}[{\cal B}]}T_{np^{\prime},\rho^{\prime},{\cal B}}^{n}\Big)=\hat{B}_{e}(r|\rho\|\sigma).

(37)

This theorem is shown in Section
IX
.

Combining Lemma
3
and Theorem
4
, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 6
.

For any
0<R<\hat{D}(\rho\|\sigma)
,
there exists a small
r>0
such that

\hat{B}_{e}(r|\rho\|\sigma)\geq R
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{-1}{n}\log\mathrm{Tr}\sigma^{\otimes n}\Big(\sum_{(p_{n},\rho_{n})\in S_{\rho,r}\cap{\cal R}_{n}[{\cal B}]}T_{np_{n},\rho_{n},{\cal B}}^{n}\Big)\geq R.

(38)

When
\rho\neq\sigma
,

\hat{D}(\rho\|\sigma)
is strictly positive.
Therefore, when
we apply the measurement
\{T_{\bm{\lambda}_{n},\rho_{n},{\cal B}}^{n}\}_{(\frac{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}{n},\rho_{n})\in{\cal R}_{n}[{\cal B}]}
,
we can distinguish the state
\rho_{n}\in{\cal S}[{\cal B}]
from any other states
\sigma
.
Hence, the information
(\frac{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}{n},\rho_{n})\in{\cal R}_{n}[{\cal B}]

can be considered as a quantum version of
an empirical distribution.
In fact, when we employ only the information
\rho_{n}\in{\cal R}_{n}[{\cal B}]
,
it contains the case when the Young index
\bm{\lambda}_{n}\in Y_{d}^{n}
has a small exponent
for the true state
\sigma
.
Therefore, it is important to use the Young index
\bm{\lambda}_{n}
as well.

Example 1
.

As a simple example, we consider the case when

\sigma
is a pure state

|{}_{1}\rangle\langle{}_{1}|
.

\displaystyle\phi(s|\rho\|\sigma)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=

\displaystyle\log\mathrm{Tr}(|{}_{1}\rangle\langle{}_{1}|\rho|{}_{1}\rangle\langle{}_{1}|)^{1-s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-s)\log\langle{}_{1}|\rho|{}_{1}\rangle

(39)

Hence, the equality in (
30
) holds, i.e.,

\displaystyle\hat{D}(\sigma\|\rho)=-\log\langle{}_{1}|\rho|{}_{1}\rangle=-2\log\mathrm{Tr}|\rho^{1/2}\sigma^{1/2}|

(40)

while
D(\sigma\|\rho)=\infty
unless
\sigma=\rho
.
This example shows that the equality in
(
32
)
does not hold in general.

Further, when
\rho
is the diagonal state
\sigma_{\cal B}
,

\hat{D}(\sigma\|\sigma_{\cal B})
is strictly positive.
Hence, even though
\sigma_{\cal B}
has the same diagonal element
under the basis
{\cal B}
as
\sigma
,

\sigma
and
\sigma_{\cal B}
can be distinguished.
When the true state is a pure state
\sigma^{\otimes n}
,
the observed Young index
\lambda_{n}
is always

(n,0,\ldots,0)
.
But,
when the true state is the diagonal state
\sigma_{\cal B}^{\otimes n}
,
the probability that the observed Young index
\lambda_{n}
is

(n,0,\ldots,0)
is quite small.
These two states can be distinguished by the observed Young index
\lambda_{n}
.

IV
Preparation

We prepare several technical tools for our discussion.
Our proof employs
the pinching map
{\cal E}_{{\cal B}}
, which is defined as

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{{\cal B}}(X)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{\rho\in{\cal S}_{n}[{\cal B}]}T_{\rho,{\cal B}}^{n}XT_{\rho,{\cal B}}^{n},

(41)

where
X
is a Hermitian matrices over
{\cal H}^{\otimes n}
.
Then, we introduce two TP-CP maps. For this aim,
we define
{\cal K}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\bigoplus_{\bm{\lambda}\in Y_{d}^{n}}{\cal U}_{\bm{\lambda}}
.
We define
the TP-CP map
\Gamma_{1}
from
{\cal S}({\cal H}^{\otimes n})

to
{\cal S}({\cal K}_{n})

and
the TP-CP map
\Gamma_{2}
from
{\cal S}({\cal K}_{n})

to
{\cal S}({\cal H}^{\otimes n})

as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}(\rho)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{\bm{\lambda}\in Y_{d}^{n}}\mathrm{Tr}_{{\cal V}_{\bm{\lambda}}}P_{\bm{\lambda}}\rho P_{\bm{\lambda}}

(42)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}(\rho^{\prime})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{\bm{\lambda}\in Y_{d}^{n}}\tilde{P}_{\bm{\lambda}}\rho^{\prime}\tilde{P}_{\bm{\lambda}}\otimes\rho_{\bm{\lambda},\mathop{\rm mix}\nolimits},

(43)

where
\tilde{P}_{\bm{\lambda}}
is the projection to

Discussion

We have proposed another quantum version of Sanov theorem
by introducing a quantum extension of an empirical distribution.
In our analysis, a quantum empirical distribution is given as
the pair of an empirical distribution with respect to the given basis
and a Young index.
As given in Corollary
6
, the exponent is given as
\hat{D}(\rho\|\sigma)
, which is smaller than the conventional quantum relative entropy

D(\rho\|\sigma)
, which gives the optimal performance for simple quantum hypothesis testing as quantum Stein’s lemma
[
3
,
4
]
.
Several results in information theory for classical systems
employ empirical distributions, like
[
9
]
.
Hence, it can be expected that our result can be applied to
their quantum extension.
Therefore, it is an interesting future study to derive
various quantum extensions by using our result.
