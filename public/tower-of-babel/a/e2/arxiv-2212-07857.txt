Octonionic Calabi-Yau theorem.
Semyon Alesker, Peter V. Gordon

Abstract
On a certain class of 16-dimensional manifolds a new class of Riemannian metrics, called octonionic Kähler, is introduced and studied. It is an octonionic analogue of Kähler metrics on complex manifolds and of HKT-metrics of hypercomplex manifolds. Then for this class of metrics an octonionic version of the Monge-Ampère equation is introduced and solved under appropriate assumptions. The latter result is an octonionic version of the Calabi-Yau theorem from Kähler geometry.

Introduction.

1

Real and complex Monge-Ampère (MA) equations play a central role in several areas of analysis and geometry.
In particular, the celebrated Calabi-Yau theorem
[
58
]
establishes existence of Ricci flat Kähler metrics on compact Kähler manifolds reducing the problem to solving
a complex MA equation. This result is a powerful analytic tool to study geometry and topology of
compact Kähler manifolds.

2

There were several attempts
to generalize the notion of MA equation and of plurisubharmonic function to a few other contexts.
Namely, the Calabi-Yau theorem was successfully extended to the more general class of compact Hermitian manifolds by Tosatti-Weinkove
[
54
]
in full generality and previously by Cherrier
[
20
]
in special cases.

Furthermore, the first author
[
3
]

introduced and studied the quaternionic MA operator and the class of quaternionic plurisubharmonic (qpsh) functions on the flat quaternionic space
\mathbb{H}^{n}
.
Later he introduced and solved, under appropriate assumptions, the Dirichlet
problem for quaternionic MA equation in
[
4

. The latter problem was solved under weaker assumptions by Zhu
[
59
]
and Kolodziej-Sroka
[
52
]
.

M. Verbitsky and the first author
[
12

extended the definition of the quaternionic MA operator and the class of qpsh functions to the broader class of
hypercomplex manifold; then the first author
[
8

extended them to even broader class of quaternionic manifolds. M. Verbitsky and the first author

[
13

introduced a quaternionic version of the Calabi problem. So far this problem is open in full generality, but it was proven in a few special cases, see
[

,
[

]
,
[

]
.
Some relevant a priori estimates were obtained in
[

]
,
[

,
[

,
[

.

A number of results in quaternionic pluripotential theory were obtained in
[

Let us describe the main results of this paper.

As mentioned above, the MA operator in two octonionic variables was introduced by the first author in
[
7
]
.
Based on this operator, the present paper introduces and studies the octonionic MA equation analogous to the Calabi problem in Kähler geometry.

The MA equation is stated for a special class of 16-dimensional manifolds with a kind of octonionic structure. We call such manifolds
GL_{2}(\mathbb{O})
-affine manifolds, they are introduced in Section
9
. Basic examples of such manifolds
are torii
\mathbb{O}^{2}/\Lambda
, where
\mathbb{O}^{2}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{16}
is the octonionic plane, and
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{O}^{2}
is a lattice.

Furthermore in Section
10
we introduce on any
GL_{2}(\mathbb{O})
-affine manifold a class of Riemannian metrics which are octonionic analogues of Kähler metrics. We call them octonionic Kähler metrics.
In local coordinates, i.e. on
\mathbb{O}^{2}
, such metrics are characterized by two conditions.
First, the restriction of each such metric on any (right) octonionic line is proportional to the standard Euclidean metric on
\mathbb{O}^{2}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{16}
.
Second, such metrics satisfy a system of linear first order PDEs (see (
10.1
)) analogous to the closeness of Kähler form on a complex manifold.
We also prove an analogue of the local
dd^{c}
-lemma from complex analysis (see Theorem
10.2
): locally any such metric can be given by a smooth potential
u
, i.e. the metric can be identified with the octonionic Hessian of
u
.
The converse is also true provided the Hessian of
u
is pointwise positive definite.

Next we introduce an octonionic MA equation on a
GL_{2}(\mathbb{O})
-affine manifold
M
. Let
G_{0}
be an octonionic Kähler (
C^{\infty}
-smooth) metric; in local coordinates it can be identified with a positive definite octonionic Hermitian matrix.
Let
f\colon M\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a
C^{\infty}
-smooth function. Consider the equation which in local coordinates reads

\displaystyle\det(G_{0}+Hess_{\mathbb{O}}(\phi))=e^{f}\det(G_{0}),

(1.1)

where
Hess_{\mathbb{O}}
is the octonionic Hessian (see Section
4
for the precise definition),
\det
is the determinant of octonionic Hermitian
2\times 2
matrices (see (
5
)).
The main result of this paper, Theorem
12.3
, says that on the subclass of compact connected so called
Spin(9)
-affine manifolds
(example: tori
\mathbb{O}^{2}/\Lambda
where
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{O}^{2}
is a lattice) there exists a
C^{\infty}
-smooth solution
\phi
of (
1.1
)
if and only if the function
f
satisfies the normalization condition

\int_{M}(e^{f}-1)\det(G_{0})\cdot\Theta=0,

where
\Theta
is a parallel non-vanishing 3/4-density on
M
(it always exists on
Spin(9)
-affine manifolds and is unique up to multiplication by a constant).

Algebra of octonions.

1

The octonions
\mathbb{O}
form an 8-dimensional algebra over the reals

\mathbb{R}
which is neither associative nor commutative. The product on
\mathbb{O}
can
be described as follows.
\mathbb{O}
has a basis
e_{0},e_{1},\dots,e_{7}
over

\mathbb{R}
where
e_{0}=1
is the unit, and the product of basis elements is given by the
following multiplication table:

\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|}&e_{1}&e_{2}&e_{3}&e_{4}&e_{5}&e_{6}&e_{7}\\
\hline\cr e_{1}&-1&e_{4}&e_{7}&-e_{2}&e_{6}&-e_{5}&-e_{3}\\
\hline\cr e_{2}&-e_{4}&-1&e_{5}&e_{1}&-e_{3}&e_{7}&-e_{6}\\
\hline\cr e_{3}&-e_{7}&-e_{5}&-1&e_{6}&e_{2}&-e_{4}&e_{1}\\
\hline\cr e_{4}&e_{2}&-e_{1}&-e_{6}&-1&e_{7}&e_{3}&-e_{5}\\
\hline\cr e_{5}&-e_{6}&e_{3}&-e_{2}&-e_{7}&-1&e_{1}&e_{4}\\
\hline\cr e_{6}&e_{5}&-e_{7}&e_{4}&-e_{3}&-e_{1}&-1&e_{2}\\
\hline\cr e_{7}&e_{3}&e_{6}&-e_{1}&e_{5}&-e_{4}&-e_{2}&-1\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

2

There is also an easier alternative way to reproduce the product rule using the so
called
Fano plane
(see Figure 1 below). In Figure 1 each
pair of distinct points lies in a unique line (the circle is also
considered to be a line). Each line contains exactly three points,
and these points are cyclically oriented. If
e_{i},e_{j},e_{k}
are
cyclically oriented in this way then

e_{i}e_{j}=-e_{j}e_{i}=e_{k}.

We have to add two more rules:

\bullet

e_{0}=1
is the identity element;

\bullet

e_{i}^{2}=-1
for
i>0
.

All these rules define uniquely the algebra structure of
\mathbb{O}
. The
center of
\mathbb{O}
is equal to
\mathbb{R}
.

Fano plane, the figure is taken from
[
16
]

3

Every octonion
q\in\mathbb{O}
can be written uniquely in the form

q=\sum_{i=0}^{7}x_{i}e_{i}

where
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
. The summand
x_{0}e_{0}=x_{0}
is called the real
part of
q
and is denoted by
Re(q)
.

One defines the octonionic conjugate of
q
by

\bar{q}:=x_{0}-\sum_{i=1}^{7}x_{i}e_{i}.

It is well known that the conjugation is an anti-involution of

\mathbb{O}
:

\overline{\overline{q}}=q,\,\overline{a+b}=\bar{a}+\bar{b},\,\overline{ab}=\bar{b}\bar{a}.

Let us define a norm on
\mathbb{O}
by
|q|:=\sqrt{q\bar{q}}
. Then

|\cdot|
is a multiplicative norm on
\mathbb{O}
:
|ab|=|a||b|
. The
square of the norm
|\cdot|^{2}
is a positive definite quadratic
form. Its polarization is a positive definite scalar product

<\cdot,\cdot>
on
\mathbb{O}
which is given explicitly by

<x,y>=Re(x\bar{y}).

Furthermore
\mathbb{O}
is a division algebra: any
q\neq 0
has a unique
inverse
q^{-1}
such that
qq^{-1}=q^{-1}q=1
. In fact

q^{-1}=|q|^{-2}\bar{q}.

4

We denote by
\mathbb{H}
the usual quaternions. It is associative division
algebra. We will fix once and for all an imbedding of algebras

\mathbb{H}\subset\mathbb{O}
. Let us denote by
i,j\in\mathbb{H}
the usual
quaternionic units, and
k=ij
. Then
i,j,k
are pairwise orthogonal
with respect to the scalar product
<\cdot,\cdot>
. Let us fix once
and for all an octonionic unit
l\in\mathbb{O},l^{2}=-1
which is
orthogonal to
i,j,k
. Then
l
anti-commutes with
i,j,k
. Every
element
q\in\mathbb{O}
can be written uniquely in the form

q=x+yl\mbox{ with }x,y\in\mathbb{H}.

The multiplication of two such octonions is given by the formula

\displaystyle(x+yl)(w+zl)=(xw-\bar{z}y)+(zx+y\bar{w})l

(2.1)

where
x,y,w,z\in\mathbb{H}
.

5

We have the following weak forms of the associativity in
octonions. A proof can be found in
[
7
]
, Lemma 1.1.1.

2.1 Lemma
.

Let
a,b,c\in\mathbb{O}
. Then

(i)
Re((ab)c)=Re(a(bc))
(this real number will be denoted by

Re(abc)
).

(ii)
a(bc)+\bar{b}(\bar{a}c)=(ab+\bar{b}\bar{a})c
.

(iii)
(ca)b+(c\bar{b})\bar{a}=c(ab+\bar{b}\bar{a})
.

(iv) Any subalgebra of
\mathbb{O}
generated by any two elements and
their conjugates is associative. It is always isomorphic either to

\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{C}
, or
\mathbb{H}
.

(v)
Re((\bar{a}b)(ca))=|a|^{2}Re(bc).

The following identities are called Moufang identities (see e.g.
[
48
]
).

2.2 Lemma
.

Let
x,y,z\in\mathbb{O}
. Then

\displaystyle(zx)(yz)=z((xy)z)=(z(xy))z.

Polarizing the Moufang identities we immediately get a corollary.

2.3 Corollary
.

Let
x,y,z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{O}
. Then

\displaystyle(z_{1}x)(yz_{2})+(z_{2}x)(yz_{1})=z_{1}((xy)z_{2})+z_{2}((xy)z_{1})=(z_{1}(xy))z_{2}+(z_{2}(xy))z_{1}.

Octonionic projective line
\mathbb{O}\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

1

The results of this section are well known and elementary, we provide them for completeness of presentation.

3.1 Definition
.

An octonionic (right) line in
\mathbb{O}^{2}
is a real 8-dimensional subspace
L\subset\mathbb{O}^{2}
either of the form

\{(q,aq)|q\in\mathbb{O}\}
with arbitrary
a\in\mathbb{O}
,
or of the form
\{(0,q)|q\in\mathbb{O}\}
.

The latter option is considered as the line at infinity. The set of all octonionic lines is denoted by
\mathbb{O}\mathbb{P}^{1}
. It is easy to see that it is a compact subset of the Grassmannian of 8-dimensional linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{16}
.
Moreover it is a smooth submanifold diffeomorphic to the 8-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{8}
: the line at infinity corresponds to the pole of this sphere, and the rest is parameterized by octonions
a\in\mathbb{O}
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{8}
.

2

Let us summarize a few other basic and well known properties of
\mathbb{O}\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

3.2 Lemma
.

(i) For any
a\in\mathbb{O}
the set
\{(aq,q)|q\in\mathbb{O}\}
is also an octonionic line.

(ii) Any two octonionic lines either intersect trivially or coincide.

(iii) For any non-zero vector
\xi\in\mathbb{O}^{2}
there is a unique octonionic line containing
\xi
. We call it the line spanned by
\xi
.

(iv) Let
\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{O}^{2}
be two vectors of norm one. The lines spanned by these vectors are equal if and only if one has for the
2\times 2
-matrices

\xi\cdot\xi^{*}=\eta\cdot\eta^{*}.

In this case we write
\xi\sim\eta
.

The proof of this lemma is elementary and straightforward.

Octonionic Hessian.

In this section we remind the octonionic Hessian introduced in
[
7
]
. For
i=1,2
write

q_{i}=\sum_{a=0}^{7}x_{i}^{a}e_{a}\mbox{ with }x_{i}^{a}\in\mathbb{R}.

Let
F\colon\mathbb{O}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{O}
be a sufficiently smooth function. Define for
i,j=1,2

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F}{\partial\bar{q}_{i}}:=\sum_{a=0}^{7}e_{a}\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{i}^{a}},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F}{\partial q_{j}}:=\sum_{a=0}^{7}\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{j}^{a}}\bar{e}_{a}.

In general the operators
\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{q}_{i}}
and
\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{j}}
do not commute. However, they do commute when applied to real valued functions.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{O}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a
C^{2}
-smooth real valued function. Define its octonionic Hessian by

\displaystyle Hess_{\mathbb{O}}(f):=\left(\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial\bar{q}_{i}\partial q_{j}}\right)_{i,j=1,2},

(4.1)

where the operators
\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{q}_{i}}
and
\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{j}}
are applied in either order. Then
Hess_{\mathbb{O}}(f)
is an octonionic Hermitian
2\times 2
matrix.

Octonionic linear algebra.

1

Let us denote

\mathbb{O}^{m}:=\{(q_{1},\dots,q_{m})|\,q_{i}\in\mathbb{O}\}.

For
\xi=(q_{1},\dots,q_{m})\in\mathbb{O}^{m}
,
x\in\mathbb{O}
we will denote by

\xi\cdot x
the
m
-tuple
(q_{1}x,\dots,q_{m}x)\in\mathbb{O}^{m}
. Notice that
usually we will write elements of
\mathbb{O}^{m}
as
m
-columns rather than
rows.

Let us denote by
{\cal H}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
the space of real symmetric

(n\times n)
-matrices. The space
{\cal H}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is naturally
identified with the space of real valued quadratic forms on

\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Let us denote by
{\cal H}_{m}(\mathbb{O})
the space of octonionic Hermitian

(m\times m)
-matrices. By definition, an
(m\times m)
-matrix

A=(a_{ij})
with octonionic entries is called Hermitian if

a_{ij}=\overline{a_{ji}}
for any
i,j
. For a matrix
A=(a_{ij})

denote also
A^{*}:=(\overline{a_{ji}})
. In what follows we will be only interested
in
2\times 2
octonionic Hermitian matrices. In this case we have the
following explicit description of such matrices. Namely,

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{2}(\mathbb{O})=\left\{\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&q\\
\bar{q}&b\end{array}\right]\big|a,b\in\mathbb{R},q\in\mathbb{O}\right\}.

2

We have the natural
\mathbb{R}
-linear map

\displaystyle j\colon{\cal H}_{2}(\mathbb{O})\longrightarrow{\cal H}_{16}(\mathbb{R}),

(5.4)

which is defined
as follows: for any
A\in{\cal H}_{2}(\mathbb{O})
the value of the quadratic
form
j(A)
on any octonionic 2-column
\xi\in\mathbb{O}^{2}=\mathbb{R}^{16}
is given by
j(A)(\xi)=Re(\xi^{*}A\xi)
(note that the
bracketing inside the right hand side of the formula is not important due to Lemma

2.1
(i)). It is easy to see that the map
j
is
injective. Via this map
j
we will identify
{\cal H}_{2}(\mathbb{O})
with a
subspace of
{\cal H}_{16}(\mathbb{R})
.

3

Let us construct now a linear map

\theta\colon{\cal H}_{16}(\mathbb{R})\longrightarrow{\cal H}_{2}(\mathbb{O})

in the opposite direction and such that

\theta\circ j=Id
; it will be useful later. For any

B\in{\cal H}_{16}(\mathbb{R})
let us denote by
b
the corresponding
quadratic form on
\mathbb{R}^{16}=\mathbb{O}^{2}
, namely

b(x)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{16}x_{i}B_{ij}x_{j}.

Define

\displaystyle\theta(B):=\frac{1}{16}Hess_{\mathbb{O}}(b)=\frac{1}{16}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}b}{\partial\bar{q}_{i}\partial q_{j}}\right)_{i,j=1}^{2}.

(5.5)

Note that the matrix
in the right hand side of the last formula is independent of a point
in
\mathbb{O}^{2}
.

The next lemma is proved in
[
7
]
, Corollary 1.2.2, but we present a proof for the sake of completeness.

5.1 Lemma
.

The following identity holds.

\theta\circ j=Id.

Proof.
Let
B=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&q\\
\bar{q}&b\end{array}\right]
where
a,b\in\mathbb{R},\,q\in\mathbb{O}
. Below we denote
\Delta_{i}=\sum_{a=0}^{7}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial(x_{i}^{a})^{2}}
. We have

\displaystyle(\theta\circ j)(B)=\frac{1}{16}\left(Hess_{\mathbb{O}}(Re(\xi^{*}B\xi)\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{16}Hess_{\mathbb{O}}\left(a|\xi_{1}|^{2}+b|\xi_{2}|^{2}+2Re(\bar{\xi}_{1}q\xi_{2})\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{16}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a\Delta_{1}(|\xi_{1}|^{2})&2\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bar{\xi}_{1}\partial\xi_{2}}Re(\bar{\xi}_{1}q\xi_{2})\\
2\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bar{\xi}_{2}\partial\xi_{1}}Re(\bar{\xi}_{1}q\xi_{2})&b\Delta_{2}(|\xi_{2}|^{2})\end{array}\right]=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&\frac{1}{8}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bar{\xi}_{1}\partial\xi_{2}}Re(\bar{\xi}_{1}q\xi_{2})\\
\frac{1}{8}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bar{\xi}_{2}\partial\xi_{1}}Re(\bar{\xi}_{1}q\xi_{2})&b\end{array}\right].

We have to show that the term above the diagonal equals
q
(the term under the diagonal is its conjugate). Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bar{\xi}_{1}\partial\xi_{2}}Re(\bar{\xi}_{1}q\xi_{2})=\sum_{a,b=0}^{7}e_{a}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{1}^{a}\partial x_{2}^{b}}Re(\bar{\xi}_{1}\cdot q\cdot\xi_{2})\right)\bar{e}_{b}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}\sum_{a,b,c,d=0}^{7}e_{a}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{1}^{a}\partial x_{2}^{b}}Re(x_{1}^{c}\bar{e}_{c}\cdot q\cdot x_{2}^{d}e_{d})\right)\bar{e}_{b}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}\sum_{a,b,c,d=0}^{7}e_{a}\left(Re(\delta_{ac}\bar{e}_{c}\cdot q\cdot\delta_{bd}e_{d})\right)\bar{e}_{b}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}\sum_{a,b=0}^{7}e_{a}(Re(\bar{e}_{a}qe_{b}))\bar{e}_{b}=\frac{1}{8}\sum_{b}\left(\sum_{a}e_{a}(Re(\bar{e}_{a}(qe_{b})))\right)\bar{e}_{b}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}\sum_{b}(qe_{b})\bar{e}_{b}=\frac{1}{8}\sum_{b}q(e_{b}\bar{e}_{b})=q.

Lemma follows. Q.E.D.

Now we remind the notion of
mixed determinant

of two octonionic Hermitian matrices in analogy to the classical
real case (see e.g.
[
49
]
). First observe that the
determinant
\det{\cal H}_{2}(\mathbb{O})\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a homogeneous polynomial of

unique polarization: a bilinear symmetric map

D\colon{\cal H}_{2}(\mathbb{O})\times{\cal H}_{2}(\mathbb{O})\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

such that
D(A,A)=\det A
for any
A\in{\cal H}_{2}(\mathbb{O})
. This map
D
is
called the mixed determinant. By the abuse of notation it will be
denoted again by
