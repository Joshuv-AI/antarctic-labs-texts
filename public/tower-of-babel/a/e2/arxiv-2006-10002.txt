Lattices over Bass rings and graph agglomerations
Nicholas R. Baeth, Daniel Smertnig

Abstract
. We study direct-sum decompositions of torsion-free, finitely generated modules over a (commutative) Bass ring R R through the factorization theory of the corresponding monoid T ⁡ ( R ) T(R) . Results of Levy–Wiegand and Levy–Odenthal together with a study of the local case yield an explicit description of T ⁡ ( R ) T(R) . The monoid is typically neither factorial nor cancellative. Nevertheless, we construct a transfer homomorphism to a monoid of graph agglomerations—a natural class of monoids serving as combinatorial models for the factorization theory of T ⁡ ( R ) T(R) . As a consequence, the monoid T ⁡ ( R ) T(R) is transfer Krull of finite type and several finiteness results on arithmetical invariants apply. We also establish results on the elasticity of T ⁡ ( R ) T(R) and characterize when T ⁡ ( R ) T(R) is half-factorial. (Factoriality, that is, torsion-free Krull–Remak–Schmidt–Azumaya, is characterized by a theorem of Levy–Odenthal.) The monoids of graph agglomerations introduced here are also of independent interest.

Introduction

A (commutative) ring
R
is a
Bass ring
if it is noetherian, reduced (zero is the only nilpotent element), has module-finite integral closure, and every ideal of
R
is
2
-generated
[
LW85
]
.
Bass rings arise naturally in geometry—as coordinate rings of (not necessarily irreducible) affine algebraic curves whose only singularities are double points, in number theory—for example, as quadratic orders, and in representation theory—in the form of
\mathbb{Z}[G]
with
G
a finite abelian group of square-free order.
Bass rings have Krull dimension at most one and belong to the larger class of stable rings — rings in which every ideal that contains a nonzerodivisor projective over its ring of endomorphisms — see
[
Gab14
,
Olb16
]
.

Let
R
be a Bass ring.
We are interested in direct-sum decompositions of
R
-lattices, that is torsion-free, finitely generated
R
-modules.
In the present setting, these are precisely the maximal Cohen–Macaulay modules.
To understand the representation theory of
R
, one seeks to understand (i) the indecomposable modules, and (ii) the different ways modules decompose as direct sums of indecomposable ones.
Denoting by
T(R)
the monoid of isomorphism classes of
R
-lattices, together with operation induced by the direct sum, this means studying the factorization theory of the monoid
T(R)
.
This monoid theoretical point of view was pioneered in work of Facchini, Herbera, and Wiegand
[
FH00a
,
FH00b
,
Wie01
,
Fac02
,
FW04
]
, with later extensions to countably generated modules by Herbera and Příhoda
[
HP10
]
; see also the surveys
[
WW09
,
Fac12
,
BW13
]
and the book
[
Fac19
]
.
It permits the application of techniques originating in the study of the factorization theory of integral domains.
The monoid
T(R)
is cancellative if the Bass ring
R
is semilocal, or more generally if
\Pic(R)
is trivial, but is otherwise typically noncancellative (
Remark

3.17
).

In his
ubiquity paper
, Bass showed that every
R
-lattice over a Bass ring is a direct sum of ideals
[
Bas63
]
(the rings were later named after him).
Levy–Wiegand
[
LW85
]
described
R
-lattices in terms of a
genus
and a
class
, represented by an element of the ideal class semigroup
\Pic(R\mid\overline{R})
of
R
.
This semigroup is the disjoint union of the Picard groups
\Pic(S)
of the finitely many intermediate rings
R\subseteq S\subseteq\overline{R}
.
If every two
R
-lattices in the same genus are isomorphic, then
R
has
finite representation type
(
FRT
)
and
T(R)
is finitely generated.
This is always the case when
R
is semilocal.
Moreover, Bass rings always have
bounded representation type
(
BRT
)

[
CWW95
]
.

Our focus here will be on problem (ii) mentioned above.
In the best case, the decomposition of a lattice into indecomposables is unique up to order and isomorphism, that is, the
R
-lattices satisfy the Krull–Remak–Schmidt–Azumaya property (KRSA).
Equivalently, the monoid
T(R)
is
factorial
.
The results from the pivotal papers by Levy–Odenthal
[
LO96b
,
LO96a
]
, applied to the special case of Bass rings, show that
T(R)
is factorial if and only if (a) every two
R
-lattices in the same genus are isomorphic (equivalently, the group
\Pic(R)
is trivial), and (b) every connected component of
\Spec(R)
contains at most one singular maximal ideal (see
Proposition

3.13
).

In the vast majority of cases the monoid
T(R)
is therefore
not
factorial.
Even in the semilocal case, where statement (a) in the previous paragraph always holds, the monoid
T(R)
can be far from factorial.
In these cases we seek to understand the decompositions through the study of
arithmetical invariants
of
T(R)
, that measure the deviation from the uniqueness, respectively, describe the non-uniqueness in qualitative and quantitative ways.

These types of questions arose at first in the factorization theory of integral domains and monoids; we mention the recent surveys
[
HK08
,
GZ20
]
, monographs
[
GHK06
,
FHL13
]
, and proceedings
[
And97
,
Cha05
,
CFGO16
]
.
For monoids of modules, this perspective was pursued in
[
FHKW06
,
Dir07
,
BL11
,
BS12
,
BG14
,
BGGS15
]
for some classes of rings; also see the survey
[

]
.
Typically, arithmetical invariants have been studied in cancellative settings, with recent work in some noncancellative settings by Geroldinger, Fan, Kainrath, and Tringali
[
FGKT17
,
FT18
]
.

For instance, to the class
[M]\in T(R)
of an
R
-lattice
M
, we associate the
set of lengths

\mathsf{L}([M])\coloneqq\{\,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:M\cong N_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus N_{k}\text{ with $N_{i}$ indecomposable}\,\}
.
The
system of sets of lengths
of the monoid

is
\mathcal{L}(T(R))\coloneqq\{\,\mathsf{L}([M]):M\text{ an $R$-lattice}\,\}
.
The system of sets of lengths contains a great deal of information about the direct-sum decompositions of
R
-lattices.
For example, it tells us whether
T(R)
is
half-factorial
, that is the sets of lengths are all singletons.
If not, we may ask about the

such that we can find indecomposables

, or even a description of the structure of the sets of lengths.

To study the system of sets of lengths, we construct a
transfer homomorphism
from
T(R)
to a simpler monoid.
To the Bass ring
R
we associate the graph
\mathcal{G}_{R}=(V,E,r)
of
prime ideal intersections
(our graphs are finite, possibly with multiple edges, but no loops).
The graph
\mathcal{G}_{R}
has as its set of vertices the minimal prime ideals of
R
, and has an edge between two minimal prime ideals
\mathfrak{p}
and
\mathfrak{q}
for every maximal ideal containing both
\mathfrak{p}
and
\mathfrak{q}
.
An
agglomeration
on
\mathcal{G}_{R}
is a map
a\colon V\cup E\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
assigning nonnegative numbers to every edge and every vertex in such a way that
a(v)\geq a(e)
whenever a vertex
v
is incident with an edge
e
.
The
monoid of agglomerations on
\mathcal{G}_{R}
, denoted
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}_{R})
, is the additive submonoid of
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{V\cup E}
consisting of all agglomerations on
\mathcal{G}_{R}
.

The central result of the present paper is the following (for missing definitions see
Section

2
).

Theorem 1.1
.

For every Bass ring
R
, there exists a transfer homomorphism
\theta\colon T(R)\to\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}_{R})
.
In particular, the monoid
T(R)
is
transfer Krull of finite type
, we have
\mathsf{L}([M])=\mathsf{L}(\theta([M]))
for all
R
-lattices
M
, and
\mathcal{L}(T(R))=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}_{R}))
.

Thus, monoids of graph agglomerations serve as combinatorial models for the factorizations in
T(R)
, and consequently direct-sum decompositions of
R
-lattices; many questions about the arithmetic in
T(R)
can be answered in
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}_{R})
instead, and in particular, this applies to all questions about sets of lengths.
The algebraic structure of
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}_{R})
is very easy; it is a Diophantine monoid, and therefore a finitely generated reduced Krull monoid.
Krull monoids are one of the central objects in factorization theory.
There, factorizations are typically studied by means of
monoids of zero-sum sequences
and techniques from combinatorial and additive number theory; see
[
GR09

and
[

]
.
However, for monoids of graph agglomerations it turns out to be more fruitful to study the factorization theory directly.

In

we therefore initiate a study of the arithmetic of monoids of graph agglomerations.
Because agglomerations with values in

may be identified with subgraphs of

, an agglomeration can be viewed as a sum of subgraphs of
\mathcal{G}_{R}
.
It is therefore not surprising that the factorization theory is strongly linked to the structure of the underlying graph;

It seems that this class of monoids has not been considered before (certainly not from the point of factorization theory) and we hope that the link between arithmetical properties and graph-theoretical properties may be of independent interest to the factorization theory community.
We have therefore ensured that
Section

is largely self-contained, making reference only to

2
for notation and background.

2.
Background and Notation

Our goal is to describe the degree to which direct-sum decompositions of lattices (finitely generated torsion-free modules) over Bass rings are nonunique. We take the approach of instead studying the arithmetic of the monoid
T(R)=\{\,[M]:M\text{ an $R$-lattice}\,\}
of isomorphism classes of
R
-lattices, with operation induced by the direct sum:
[M]+[N]=[M\oplus N]
. Since the set of
R
-lattices is closed under isomorphisms, direct summands, and finite direct sums, we can glean a great deal of information from the monoid
T(R)
. For example, the atoms of
T(R)
are the classes of indecomposable
R
-lattices and the ring
R
has
finite representation type
(
FRT
)
(up to isomorphism, there exist only finitely many indecomposable
R
-lattices) if and only if the monoid
T(R)
is finitely generated.

Therefore, in the remainder of this section we introduce the necessary tools from factorization theory of commutative monoids and the arithmetical invariants that we will consider as well as the means of which to transfer information from the (usually) noncancellative monoid
T(R)
to a cancellative, in fact Krull, monoid.

Monoids and factorization theory

By a
monoid
, we always mean a commutative monoid; that is, a commutative semigroup with identity. If the identity is the only invertible element, the monoid is
reduced
.

1
1

1

A ring is reduced if and only if
0
is the only nilpotent element; this corresponds neither to the monoid
(R,\cdot)
nor to the monoid
(R,+)
being reduced, but unfortunately both terminologies are standard. We leave it to the reader to divine the correct meaning from context.

Since we are concerned only with monoids of modules and monoids of graph agglomerations, we will stick to additive notation throughout. A monoid
(H,0,+)
is
cancellative
if an equation of the form
a+b=a+c
implies
b=c
. A reduced monoid
H
satisfies the weaker property of being
unit-cancellative
if, whenever
a+u=a
with
a
,
u\in H
, then
u=0
.

A nonzero element
a
of a reduced monoid
H
is an
atom
(or
irreducible
) if
a=b+c
with
b
,
c\in H
implies
b=0
or
c=0
.
It is
prime
provided whenever
a
is a summand of
b+c
for some
b
,
c\in H
, then
a
is a summand of
b
or of
c
.
A weaker property, an atom
a\in H
is
absolutely irreducible
if every multiple
na
has a unique factorization, namely
na=a+\cdots+a
. The monoid
H
is
atomic
if every nonzero element
a\in H
can be expressed as a sum of atoms. Assume from now on that
H
is atomic.
In this case it makes sense to study the different factorizations of
a
and this may be accomplished by means of studying suitable arithmetical invariants. We introduce those most important to this work. For additional invariants and further details we refer to the survey by Geroldinger–Zhong
[
GZ20
]
and the references cited therein, as well as the monograph
[
GHK06
]
. For the rest of this section we assume the following standing hypotheses.

The monoid
H
is nonzero, reduced, and atomic.

Throughout, the set of nonnegative integers is denoted by
\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and
[a,b]=\{\,x\in\mathbb{Z}:a\leq x\leq b\,\}
denotes a discrete interval. The
set of lengths
of a non-unit
a
is

\mathsf{L}(a)\coloneqq\{\,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:\text{there exist atoms $a_{1}$, $\ldots\,$,\penalty\ $a_{k}\in H$ with $a=a_{1}+\dots+a_{k}$}\,\},

and
\mathsf{L}(u)\coloneqq\{0\}
for any unit
u
. The
system of sets of lengths

\mathcal{L}(H)\coloneqq\{\,\mathsf{L}(a):a\in H\,\}
is one of the basic arithmetical invariants of
H
.

As an exact description of
\mathcal{L}(H)
is usually not possible for interesting classes of monoids
H
, simpler invariants are used to describe the structure of sets of lengths. The
elasticity
of a nonzero
a\in H
is
\rho(a)\coloneqq\sup\mathsf{L}(a)/\min\mathsf{L}(a)\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 1}\cup\{\infty\}
. We set
\rho(0)\coloneqq 1
and define the
elasticity of
H
to be
\rho(H)\coloneqq\sup\{\,\rho(a):a\in H\,\}
.
The elasticity is
accepted
if there exists
a\in H
with
\rho(a)=\rho(H)
.
For each
k\geq 2
, the
refined elasticity
is
\rho_{k}(H)\coloneqq\sup\{\,\sup\mathsf{L}(a):a\in H\text{ with }k\in\mathsf{L}(a)\,\}\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}\cup\{\infty\}
. For
k\geq 2
, one has
\rho_{k}(H)=\sup\,\mathcal{U}_{k}(H)
where

\mathcal{U}_{k}(H)=\{\,n\colon a_{1}+\cdots+a_{k}=b_{1}+\cdots+b_{n}\text{ for atoms $a_{1}$, $\ldots\,$,\penalty\ $a_{k}$, $b_{1}$, $\ldots\,$,\penalty\ $b_{n}$}\,\}

is the
union of sets of lengths containing
k
. One can show

\rho(H)=\sup_{k\geq 2}\rho_{k}(H)/k=\lim_{k\to\infty}\rho_{k}(H)/k.

If
\mathsf{L}(a)=\{\,l_{1}<l_{2}<\cdots\,\}
, we set
\Delta(a)\coloneqq\{\,l_{i+1}-l_{i}:i\geq 1\,\}
to be the
set of distances
of
a
.
Let
\Delta(H)\coloneqq\bigcup_{a\in H}\Delta(a)
.

As a general rule, larger elasticities, and more wildly behaved length sets are indicative of highly nonunique factorization. The monoid
H
is
half-factorial
if it is atomic and
\lvert\mathsf{L}(a)\rvert=1
for every
a\in H
.
For an atomic monoid, this is equivalent to
\Delta(H)=\emptyset
, respectively,
\rho(H)=1
. Even when factorization in
H
is not unique, length sets and systems of sets of lengths can be very well structured (see
Theorem

2.3
below).

Other invariants provide finer measures of how nonunique factorization can be. Here we briefly discuss two such invariants that are mentioned in subsequent sections.
Given a reduced cancellative monoid
H
and two factorizations
z:=a_{1}+\cdots+a_{l}+b_{1}+\cdots+b_{m}
and
z^{\prime}:=a_{1}+\cdots+a_{l}+c_{1}+\cdots+c_{n}
with each
a_{i}
,
b_{j}
,
c_{k}
an atom of
H
and with
b_{j}\not=c_{k}
for any pair
j
,
k
. The
distance
between
z
and
z^{\prime}
is
\mathsf{d}(z,z^{\prime})=\max\{m,n\}
. Though
\mathsf{d}(z,z^{\prime})=0
whenever
H
is factorial and
z
,
z^{\prime}
are factorizations of the same element, even when
H
is half-factorial, the distance
d(z,z^{\prime})
can be arbitrarily large. The catenary degree provides a refinement and is defined as follows. For
a\in H
, the
catenary degree

\mathsf{c}(a)
of
a
is the smallest nonnegative integer
N
so that given any two factorizations
z
and
z^{\prime}
of
a
, there is a chain of factorizations
z=z_{0}
,
z_{1}
,
\ldots\,
,
z_{n}=z^{\prime}
such that
\mathsf{d}(z_{i-1},z_{i})\leq N
for all
i\in[1,n]
. Then the catenary degree of
H
is
\mathsf{c}(H)=\sup\{\,\mathsf{c}(a)\colon a\in H\,\}
. It can be shown that
\mathsf{c}(H)=0
if and only if
H
is factorial. If
H
is cancellative and not factorial, then
\mathsf{c}(H)\geq 2
.

For a reduced monoid
H
, and elements
a
,
b\in H
, we set
\omega(a,b)
to be the smallest
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}
with the following property: For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
a_{1}
,
\ldots\,
,
a_{n}\in H
and
a=a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}
, if
a=b+c
for some
c\in H
, there exists some

and

with
b+d=\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}
. Then
\omega(H,b)\coloneqq\sup\{\,\omega(a,b):a\in H\,\}
and
\omega(H)\coloneqq\sup\{\,\omega(H,b):b\text{ is an atom}\,\}
. Put more simply, if
\omega(H,b)=N
, then whenever
b
is a summand of a sum of elements, it must be a summand of some subsum of no more than
N
elements. It is clear that
\omega(H,b)=0
if and only if
b
is a unit and
\omega(H,b)=1
if and only if
b
is prime. Thus the
\omega
-invariant provides a measure for how far an element is from being prime and
\omega(H)
measures how far, even a half-factorial monoid is from being factorial.

A submonoid
S\subseteq H
is
divisor-closed
, provided that for every
s\in S
also all the summands of
s
, as considered in
H
, are already contained in
S
.
It is clear that all questions about factorizations of an element

may be studied in a divisor-closed submonoid containing
a
.

Transfer homomorphisms and Krull monoids

Transfer homomorphisms are a key tool in studying invariants as they allow one to transfer many arithmetic results from simpler objects to the objects of interest.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(H,0_{H},+)
and
(T,0_{T},+)
be reduced atomic monoids with neutral elements
0_{H}
and
0_{T}
, respectively.
A surjective monoid homomorphism
\theta\colon H\to T
is a
transfer homomorphism
if it satisfies the following properties.

(1)

\theta^{-1}(0_{T})=0_{H}
.

(2)

If
\theta(a)=s+t
for some
a\in H
and
s
,
t\in T
, then there exist
b
,
c\in H
with
a=b+c
and such that
\theta(b)=s
and
\theta(c)=t
.

Since transfer homomorphism allow the lifting of factorizations from
T
to
H
, they transfer arithmetical information from
T
to
H
.
Specifically, we recall the following. A proof in the cancellative setting, that carries over to the more general setting, can be found in
[
GHK06
, Proposition 3.2.3]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\theta\colon H\to T
be a transfer homomorphism between reduced atomic monoids.
Then
\mathsf{L}(a)=\mathsf{L}(\theta(a))
for every
a\in H
.
In particular, we have
\mathcal{L}(H)=\mathcal{L}(T)
and therefore
\rho(H)=\rho(T)
, for all
k\geq 2
also
\rho_{k}(H)=\rho_{k}(T)
and
\Delta(H)=\Delta(T)
.

From a factorization-theoretic standpoint, the most widely studied cancellative monoids are Krull monoids.
Krull monoids have numerous equivalent characterizations (see
[
GHK06
, Theorems 2.3.11 and 2.4.8]
), we recall two.
A monoid homomorphism
\varphi\colon H\to D
is a
divisor homomorphism
if, whenever
\varphi(a)
is a summand of
\varphi(b)
in
D
for
a
,
b\in H
, then
a
is a summand of
b
in
H
.
A submonoid
H\subseteq D
is
saturated
if the inclusion is a divisor homomorphism; for cancellative monoids this is equivalent to
\mathbf{q}(H)\cap D=H
.
Here
\mathbf{q}(H)\subseteq\mathbf{q}(D)
denote the quotient groups of the respective monoids.
A
divisor theory
is a divisor homomorphism
\varphi\colon H\to\mathbb{N}_{0}^{(I)}
such that every standard basis vector
\mathbf{e}_{i}
of
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{(I)}
can be expressed as

with
a_{1}
,
\ldots\,
,

.
A cancellative monoid
H
is a
Krull monoid
if there exists a divisor homomorphism from
H
into some free monoid
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{(I)}
.
Equivalently, the monoid

has a divisor theory.

A Krull monoid

is finitely generated if and only if this divisor theory can be taken into a finitely generated free monoid

.
In this case

is the

.
Finitely generated reduced Krull monoids can be characterized as Diophantine monoids,
[

.
To be more specific, if

is some integer-valued matrix.

If

is a divisor theory, the
divisor class group
of

Because of the uniqueness of divisor theories (

The class group

The monoid

Krull monoids can also be characterized in terms of monoids of zero-sum sequences over subsets of abelian groups, but for our purposes we recall only that a not necessarily cancellative, or even commutative, monoid

transfer Krull of finite type
provided that there is a (weak) transfer homomorphism

There has been a great deal of research devoted to the arithmetic of transfer Krull monoids (cf.
[

]

Consequently, we can measure the degree to which direct-sum decompositions over a Bass ring are not unique by instead studying the arithmetic of certain finitely generated Krull monoids and, in particular, certain Diophantine monoids introduced in

Length sets are almost arithmetical multiprogressions (AAMP) and all unions of sets of lengths are almost arithmetic progressions (AAP). AAPs (resp. AAMPs) are essentially (unions of) arithmetic progressions, possibly with some gaps at the beginning and/or end of the sequence.

Graphs

By a
graph
we mean a triple
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,r)
where
V
is a finite set of vertices, where
E
is a finite set of edges (disjoint from
V
) and where
r\colon E\to\{\,\{v,w\}:v\neq w\in V\,\}
is a map associating to an edge its two incident vertices.
We write
e\sim v
if the edge
e
is incident with the vertex
v
(that is,
v\in r(e)
).
Observe that our definition allows for multiple edges but not for loops.
The graph is
simple
if there are no multiple edges; that is, for each pair
v
,
w\in V
, there is at most one
e\in E
with
r(e)=\{v,w\}
.

A subgraph consists of a subset of the edges and vertices, satisfying the condition that for every edge of the subgraph we must also take its two incident vertices. Formally, a graph
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime},r)
is a
subgraph
of
\mathcal{G}
if
V^{\prime}\subseteq V
, if
E^{\prime}\subseteq E
, if
r(E^{\prime})\subseteq\{\,\{v,w\}:v\neq w\in V^{\prime}\,\}
, and if
r^{\prime}
is the restriction of
r
to
E^{\prime}
.

For a vertex
v
of a graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,r)
, its
degree
, written
\deg_{\mathcal{G}}(v)=|\{e\in E\colon v\in r(e)\}|
is the number of distinct edges incident with
v
. A simple graph
\mathcal{G}
with vertex set
V
is
k
-regular
if
\deg(v)=k
for all
v\in V
. We write
\mathcal{C}_{n}
for a cycle (
2
-regular) graph on
n
vertices and
\mathcal{K}_{n}
for the complete (
(n-1)
-regular) graph on
n
-vertices.

3.
Module theory

Throughout this section, let
R
be a reduced noetherian ring with total quotient ring
K
and set of minimal prime ideals
\minspec(R)=\{\mathfrak{p}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{p}_{k}\}
.
For convenience, we shall always assume that
R
is nonzero and
R\neq K
(our results generally hold, but trivially so, in these cases).
Since
R
is reduced, the set of zerodivisors is
\mathfrak{p}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathfrak{p}_{k}
. Then
K\cong R_{\mathfrak{p}_{1}}\times\cdots\times R_{\mathfrak{p}_{k}}
, and we write
e_{i}\in K
to denote the idempotent which has
1
in the
i
-th coordinate and
0
everywhere else. We view
R
as embedded in
K
, and denote the integral closure of
R
in
K
by
\overline{R}
. For an
R
-module
M
, we set
\rank_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}(M)=\dim(M\otimes_{R}R_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}})
. Then
\rank(M)=(\rank_{\mathfrak{p}_{1}}(M),\ldots,\rank_{\mathfrak{p}_{k}}(M))
.

A module
M_{R}
is
torsion-free
if the canonical map
M\to K\otimes_{R}M
is injective. Equivalently,
M
is torsion-free if whenever
0\neq m\in M
and
r\in R
with
rm=0
, then
r
is a zerodivisor of
R
. An
R
-lattice

M
is a finitely generated torsion-free
R
-module. Such a lattice
M
can always be viewed as a submodule of
K\otimes_{R}M
, and it makes sense to define the
coefficient ring

\mathcal{O}(M)=\{\,x\in\overline{R}\mid xM\subseteq M\,\}
as the largest subring of
\overline{R}
acting on
M
. Letting
\varepsilon(M)=\sum\{\,e_{i}:e_{i}M=0\,\}
, we observe that
M
is a faithful
R(1-\varepsilon(M))
-module.

A
Bass ring
is a noetherian reduced ring with module-finite integral closure such that every ideal is generated by two elements.
Bass rings always have Krull dimension at most
1
and they admit the following characterization.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
LW85
, Theorem 2.1]
)
.

Let
R
be a one-dimensional reduced noetherian ring and assume that
\overline{R}
is finitely generated as an
R
-module.
The following conditions are equivalent.

(a)

R
is a Bass ring.
(
Every ideal is generated by
2
elements.
)

(b)

\overline{R}/R
is a cyclic
R
-module.

(c)

Every faithful
R
-lattice
M
has a direct summand isomorphic to an invertible ideal of
\mathcal{O}(M)
.

(d)

Every ring between
R
and
\overline{R}
is Gorenstein.

(e)

R_{\mathfrak{m}}
has multiplicity at most
2
for every height-one maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
.

As Bass showed, every
R
-lattice over a Bass ring decomposes as a direct sum of ideals. This follows from
(c)
together with the observation that
Re
is a Bass ring for every idempotent
e\in K
.
In particular, every indecomposable
R
-lattice is isomorphic to an ideal of
R
.
This readily yields a description of the indecomposable
R
-lattices for local Bass rings.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a local Bass ring.
Then
R
has at most two minimal primes and
R
has finite representation type.

(1)

If
\lvert\minspec{R}\rvert=1
, that is,
R
is a domain, then all indecomposable
R
-lattices have rank
1
.

(2)

If
\lvert\minspec{R}\rvert=2
, then the ranks of indecomposable
R
-lattices are
(1,0)
,
(0,1)
, and
(1,1)
. Moreover, up to isomorphism, there are unique
R
-lattices of ranks
(1,0)
and
(0,1)
.

Proof.

We have
\dim(R)=1
.
The ring
R
has finite representation type since it satisfies the Drozd–Roiter conditions; see Chapter 4 of
[
LW12
]
. If
\dim(R/\mathfrak{p}_{i})=0
for some
i\in[1,k]
, then
\mathfrak{p}_{i}=\mathfrak{m}
is the unique maximal ideal of
R
, and hence
\dim(R)=0
.
Thus we have
\dim(R/\mathfrak{p}_{i})=1
for all
i\in[1,k]
.
Now Theorem 14.7 of
[
Mat89
]
implies
\lvert\minspec(R)\rvert\leq\mu(R)\leq 2
, where
\mu(R)
is the multiplicity of
R
.

(1)
This is clear, since every indecomposable
R
-lattice is isomorphic to an ideal of
R
.

(2)
Since every indecomposable
R
-lattice is isomorphic to an ideal,
(1,1)
,
(1,0)
, and
(0,1)
are the only possible ranks. They are realized by
R
,
R/\mathfrak{p}_{1}
, and
R/\mathfrak{p}_{2}
, respectively.

As before, Theorem 14.7 of
[
Mat89
]
implies
\mu(R/\mathfrak{p}_{i})\leq 1
for
i\in\{1,2\}
.
Thus each
R/\mathfrak{p}_{i}
is a discrete valuation ring, and
R/\mathfrak{p}_{i}
is the unique indecomposable
R/\mathfrak{p}_{i}
-lattice.
If
M
and
N
are
R
-lattices of rank
(1,0)
, then
M_{\mathfrak{p}_{2}}=0=N_{\mathfrak{p}_{2}}
, and therefore
\mathfrak{p}_{1}
annihilates
M_{\mathfrak{p}_{2}}
and
N_{\mathfrak{p}_{2}}
.
Since
\mathfrak{p}_{1}R_{\mathfrak{p}_{1}}=0
as well, we have
(\mathfrak{p}_{1}M)_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}=0=(\mathfrak{p}_{1}N)_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
.
Since
M
and
N
are torsion-free, this implies
\mathfrak{p}_{1}M=0=\mathfrak{p}_{1}N
.
Thus
M
and
N
are
R/\mathfrak{p}_{1}
-modules of rank
1
, and hence
M\cong N
as
R/\mathfrak{p}_{1}
-modules.
Consequently,
M\cong N
as
R
-modules.

Analogously,
R
has at most one
R
-lattice of rank
(0,1)
.
∎

Example 3.3
.

While
Proposition

3.2
guarantees that each local Bass ring
R
with two minimal primes has exactly one lattice of rank
(1,0)
and one of rank
(0,1)
, the ring
R
can be taken to have arbitrarily many indecomposable lattices with rank
(1,1)
. For example, fix
n\geq 1
and take
R=k[x,y]_{(x,y)}/(x^{2}-y^{2n+2})
for any perfect field of characteristic not
2
,
3
, or
5
. Then
R
is a local Bass ring with completion
\widehat{R}\cong k\llbracket x,y\rrbracket/(x^{2}-y^{2n+2})
. From
[
Bae07
, Theorem 4.2]
we know that
\widehat{R}
has exactly
n+1
indecomposable lattices of rank
(1,1)
. Since
\lvert\minspec{R}\rvert=\lvert\minspec{\widehat{R}}\rvert

[
LO96b
, Theorem 6.2]
implies that
R
does as well. In fact, the completion
\widehat{R}
is a ring with Dynkin type (
A_{2n+1}
) and the constant rank indecomposable lattices are precisely the overrings between
\widehat{R}
and its integral closure.

Wiegand, in
[
Wie01
]
, showed that for a local noetherian ring, the homomorphism
T(R)\to T(\widehat{R})
induced by completion is a divisor homomorphism. This provides a standard strategy for studying
T(R)
in the local case. We use this approach in
Theorem

3.4
and refer the reader to
[
BG14
,
BW13
]
for additional background.

Theorem 3.4
.

If
R
is a local Bass ring, then
T(R)
is factorial.
More specifically, there is an isomorphism
T(R)\cong\mathbb{N}_{0}^{t}
for some
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

We first show that the
\mathfrak{m}
-adic completion
\widehat{R}
of
R
is a local Bass ring. By standard results, the ring
\widehat{R}
is a one-dimensional local noetherian ring of multiplicity at most
2
. Since
R
is a one-dimensional reduced local noetherian ring and
\overline{R}
is a finitely generated
R
-module, the ring
R
is
analytically unramified
, that is, the completion
\widehat{R}
is again reduced (see
[
Mat73
, Chapter 10]
for this non-trivial fact). By Theorem 4.3.4 of
[
HS06
]
, the integral closure of
\widehat{R}
is module-finite over
\widehat{R}
.

Since
\widehat{R}
is a complete local noetherian ring, the monoid
T(\widehat{R})
is factorial by the Krull–Remak–Schmidt–Azumaya Theorem
[
LW12
, Corollary 1.10]
. Moreover, by
[
Wie01
]
, the map
T(R)\to T(\widehat{R})
,
[M]\mapsto[\widehat{M}]
is a divisor homomorphism. A result of Levy and Odenthal
[
LO96b
, Theorem 6.2]
describes the image of this map: an
\widehat{R}
-lattice
M
is
extended
if there exists an
R
-lattice
M_{0}
with
M=\widehat{M_{0}}
. By the Levy–Odenthal result, an
\widehat{R}
-module
M
is extended if
\rank_{\mathfrak{P}}(M)=\rank_{\mathfrak{Q}}(M)
whenever
\mathfrak{P}
,
\mathfrak{Q}\in\minspec(\widehat{R})
with
\mathfrak{P}\cap R=\mathfrak{Q}\cap R
. If
\lvert\minspec R\rvert=\lvert\minspec\widehat{R}\rvert
, then this implies
T(R)\cong T(\widehat{R})
and the claim follows. The only way for the equality
\lvert\minspec R\rvert=\lvert\minspec\widehat{R}\rvert

to fail is if
\lvert\minspec(R)\rvert=1
and
\lvert\minspec\widehat{R}\rvert=2
. Let
\minspec\widehat{R}=\{\mathfrak{P}_{1},\mathfrak{P}_{2}\}
. Then
\rank\widehat{R}/\mathfrak{P}_{1}=(1,0)
,
\rank\widehat{R}/\mathfrak{P}_{2}=(0,1)
, and by
Proposition

3.2
these are the only indecomposable
\widehat{R}
-lattices of rank not equal to
(1,1)
. Let
M
be an
R
-lattice with completion
\widehat{M}\cong\widehat{R}/\mathfrak{P}_{1}\oplus\widehat{R}/\mathfrak{P}_{2}
, and let
N_{1}
,
\ldots\,
,
N_{n}
be
R
-lattices representing the indecomposable
\widehat{R}
-lattices of rank
(1,1)
. Note that
M
is indecomposable as an
R
-module.

If
L
is any
R
-lattice, then

\widehat{L}\cong(\widehat{R}/\mathfrak{P}_{1})^{e_{1}}\oplus(\widehat{R}/\mathfrak{P}_{2})^{e_{2}}\oplus\widehat{N}_{1}^{f_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\widehat{N}_{n}^{f_{n}}.

with
e_{1}=e_{2}
.
We conclude
L\cong M^{e_{1}}\oplus N_{1}^{f_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus N_{n}^{f_{n}}
.
Thus
T(R)
is factorial.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

A similar analysis (see
[
BL11
]
) has been completed for all one-dimensional local rings with finite representation type. In this more general setting, the ranks (at each minimal prime) of indecomposable lattices are still always
0
or
1
, but there can be up to three minimal primes and there can be multiple non-isomorphic indecomposable modules with the same non-constant rank. Consequently, the monoid
T(R)
is often not half-factorial. However, the elasticity of the monoid
T(R)
in this setting never exceeds
3/2
. As we will see, this is in stark contrast to what can happen for
non-local
Bass rings, where elasticities can be arbitrarily large.

The localization
R_{\mathfrak{m}}
of
R
at a maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
is regular if and only if
R_{\mathfrak{m}}
is a discrete valuation ring.
If
\mathfrak{p}_{i}
is a minimal prime, then
R_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}
is a field and hence regular. Thus the singular locus of
R
is precisely

\begin{split}\Sing(R)&=\{\,\mathfrak{p}\in\Spec(R)\mid R_{\mathfrak{p}}\text{ not regular}\,\}\\
&=\{\,\mathfrak{m}\in\maxspec(R)\mid R_{\mathfrak{m}}\text{ is not a DVR}\,\}.\end{split}

We recall the following basic result.

Lemma 3.6
.

For a Bass ring
R
, the singular locus
\Sing(R)
is finite.

Proof.

Since the integral closure
\overline{R}
is finitely generated as an
R
-module, there exists a non-zerodivisor
x\in R
in the conductor
(R:\overline{R})
.
If
x\not\in\mathfrak{m}
, then
x
is a unit in
R_{\mathfrak{m}}
and hence
\overline{R}_{\mathfrak{m}}=R_{\mathfrak{m}}
.
In this case,
R_{\mathfrak{m}}
is a local noetherian integrally closed ring of dimension
1
, and thus a discrete valuation ring.
This implies that
\widehat{R}_{\mathfrak{m}}
is a discrete valuation ring as well. Since
R
is noetherian and one-dimensional, there exist only finitely many
\mathfrak{m}\in\maxspec(R)
with
x\in\mathfrak{m}
.
∎

We now work towards an explicit description of
T(R)
for a not necessarily local Bass ring. This description follows from the combination of a Package Deal Theorem by Levy and Odenthal (Theorem 2.9 of
[
LO96b
]
), that describes the possible genera of
R
-lattices, with a theorem of Levy and R. Wiegand describing the isomorphism classes in a genus. Recall that if
M
is an
R
-lattice, then the
genus
of
M
is the class of all
R
-lattices
N
such that
M_{\mathfrak{m}}\cong N_{\mathfrak{m}}
for all
\mathfrak{m}\in\maxspec(R)
.

Levy and Wiegand, in Equation (3.2.4) of
[
LW85
]
, define the
class

\cl(M)
of an
R
-lattice. Formally, this is the isomorphism class of a faithful
R
-ideal, namely the
R
-isomorphism class of the
R
-module

\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}\Big(\bar{\bigwedge}^{n}M\Big)\left(\varepsilon_{n}(M)\right)

where
\bar{\bigwedge}^{n}(M)
denotes the
n
th torsion-free exterior power of
M
— the canonical image of
\bigwedge_{R}^{n}(M)
in
\bigwedge_{K}^{n}(KM)
— and where
\varepsilon_{n}(M)
denotes the idempotent
\sum\big\{\,e_{i}\colon\rank_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}(M)=n\,\big\}
.
As in the Levy–Wiegand paper, by slight abuse of notation, we also use
\cl_{R}(M)
to denote a representative of said class.
We rely on the reader to deduce the correct meaning from context.

By
(d)
of
Theorem

3.1
, if
M
and
N
are faithful
R
-lattices, then
\cl(M)=I
and
\cl(N)=J
with

and

invertible ideals of their respective coefficient rings. With operation given by
\cl(I)\cl(J)=\cl(IJ)
, the set of
R
-isomorphism classes of faithful lattices contained in
K
forms the
ideal class semigroup

\pic(R\mid\overline{R})
(
[
LW85
, Section 4.3]
). The semigroup
\Pic(R\mid\overline{R})
is the disjoint union of the Picard group
\pic(S)
, where
S
ranges over all overrings of
R
(of which there are only finitely many). As such it is an
inverse semigroup
. Note, also, that
IJ
is a fractional ideal in

and that
IJ
is the product in
\pic(\mathcal{O}(IJ))
of
IS
and
JS
.
The multiplication of two elements from the ideal class semigroup
\Pic(R\mid\overline{R})
may therefore always be carried out in a suitable Picard group of an overring.
The genus of
\cl(M)
depends only on the genus of
M
. As
\mathcal{O}(\cl(M))
is determined locally, the coefficient ring
\mathcal{O}(\cl(M))
also depends only on the genus of

.

The following result of Levy and Wiegand describes faithful
R
-lattices in a given genus.

Proposition 3.7

(
[

, Theorem 5.2]
)
.

Let
M
be a faithful
R
-lattice.
Then the map
M\mapsto\cl(M)
induces a bijection between the genus of
M

\pic(\mathcal{O}(\cl(M)))
.

If

is unfaithful, the result can be applied over the Bass ring

with

. We now explicitly state how classes and Picard groups behave under this reduction.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
M
be an
R
-lattice and
e=\varepsilon(M)
.
Then

\cl_{R}(M)\cong\cl_{R(1-e)}(M)\oplus Re.

In particular,

(1)

\cl_{R(1-e)}(M)=\cl_{R}(M)(1-e)
.

(2)

\mathcal{O}(\cl_{R(1-e)}(M))=\mathcal{O}(\cl_{R}(M))(1-e)
.

(3)

\pic(\mathcal{O}(\cl_{R(1-e)}(M)))\cong\pic(\mathcal{O}(\cl_{R}(M))(1-e))
.

Proof.

The first isomorphism can be deduced from the definition of the class as follows.
Let

denote the torsion submodule, that is, the submodule of all
m\in M
that are annihilated by a non-zerodivisor of
R
.
Then
\overline{\bigwedge}_{R}^{n}M\cong\bigwedge^{n}_{R}M/t_{R}(\bigwedge^{n}M)
.
The canonical map
\pi\colon R\to R(1-e)
is a ring epimorphism with kernel
R\cap Re=\{\,r\in R:r=er\,\}
.
Since
\ker(\pi)\subseteq\operatorname{ann}(M)
, the module
M
is an
R(1-e)
-module with
\bigwedge_{R}^{n}M=\bigwedge_{R(1-e)}^{n}M
for every
n\geq 1
.
One easily checks
t_{R}(\bigwedge_{R}^{n}M)=t_{R(1-e)}(\bigwedge_{R(1-e)}^{n}M)
, and thus also
\overline{\bigwedge}_{R}^{n}M=\overline{\bigwedge}_{R(1-e)}^{n}M
for
n\geq 1
.
For

\overline{\bigwedge}_{R}^{0}M=R
and
\overline{\bigwedge}_{R(1-e)}^{0}M=R(1-e)
.
Observing

if and only if
\rank_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}M_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}=0
yields
\varepsilon^{R}_{0}(M)=e
and
\varepsilon^{R(1-e)}_{0}(M)=0
.
Hence
\cl_{R}(M)\cong\cl_{R(1-e)}(M)\oplus Re
.

The other statements are consequences of the first isomorphism.
∎

Let
S=\mathcal{O}(\cl_{R}(M))
. Since

, the group
\pic(\mathcal{O}(\cl_{R(1-e)}(M)))\cong\pic(S(1-e))
is an epimorphic image of
\pic(S)
.

Lemma 3.9
.

(1)

The genus of an
R
-lattice completely determines its isomorphism class if and only if the Picard group
\Pic(R)
is trivial.

(2)

If
R
is semilocal, then the genus of an
R
-lattice completely determines its isomorphism class.

Proof.

(1)

If
R\subseteq S\subseteq\overline{R}
is an overring, then there is an epimorphism
\Pic(R)\to\Pic(S)
, and hence also an epimorphism
\Pic(R)\to\Pic(Se)
for every idempotent
e\in K
.

If
M
,
N
are nonisomorphic

-lattices in the same genus, then

is non-trivial by

3.7
. Thus also
\Pic(R)
is non-trivial.

Conversely, if the Picard group
\Pic(R)
is trivial, then so are all groups
\Pic(Se)
.
If
M
,
N
are two
R
-lattices in the same genus, then
M
and
N
are faithful
Se
-lattices for
S=\mathcal{O}(M)
and an idempotent

.
The claim follows again from
Proposition

3.7
.

(2)
This is well-known.
∎

3.1.
A transfer homomorphism

We are now prepared to piece together the results of
[

]
and
[
LW85
]
to describe

as a subsemigroup of
\prod_{\mathfrak{m}\in\maxspec(R)}T(R_{\mathfrak{m}})\times\pic(R\mid\overline{R})

an easier-to-understand object.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
R
be a Bass ring.
Define

\varphi\colon T(R)\to\mkern-30.0mu\prod_{\mathfrak{m}\in\maxspec(R)}\mkern-30.0muT(R_{\mathfrak{m}})\times\pic(R\mid\overline{R})

by
\varphi([M])=(([M_{\mathfrak{m}}])_{\mathfrak{m}},\cl(M))
.
Then
\varphi
is injective and an element
(([M(\mathfrak{m})])_{\mathfrak{m}},g)
is in the image of
\varphi
if and only if

(1)

M(\mathfrak{m})\otimes_{R_{\mathfrak{m}}}K\cong M(\mathfrak{n})\otimes_{R_{\mathfrak{n}}}K
for all
\mathfrak{m}
,
\mathfrak{n}\in\maxspec(R)
, and

(2)

g=[I\oplus Re]
with
I
an invertible ideal of
\mathcal{O}(\cl(M))(1-e)
, where
M
is any module with
M_{\mathfrak{m}}\cong M(\mathfrak{m})
for all
\mathfrak{m}\in\maxspec(R)
and
e=\varepsilon(M)
.

The first condition is equivalent to
\rank_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}M(\mathfrak{m})=\rank_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}M(\mathfrak{n})
whenever
\mathfrak{p}_{i}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}\cap\mathfrak{n}
.

Proof.

We first show that
\varphi(T(R))
is contained in the described set.
If
M
is an
R
-lattice then clearly
M_{\mathfrak{m}}\otimes_{R_{\mathfrak{m}}}K\cong M_{\mathfrak{n}}\otimes_{R_{\mathfrak{n}}}K
for
\mathfrak{m}
,
\mathfrak{n}\in\maxspec(R)
.
Moreover, the faithful
R
-lattice
\cl(M)
is invertible in
\mathcal{O}(\cl(M))
by
(c)
of
Theorem

3.1
and has the described form by
Lemma

3.8
.

Suppose that
(M_{\mathfrak{m}})_{\mathfrak{m}}
is a family of
R_{\mathfrak{m}}
-lattices satisfying the stated conditions.
For
i\in[1,k]
and a maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
with
\mathfrak{p}_{i}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}
, let
r_{i}=\rank_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}M_{\mathfrak{m}}
.
Because of the stated hypotheses, the value of

does not depend on the choice of
\mathfrak{m}
.
Set
F=(Re_{1})^{r_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus(Re_{k})^{r_{k}}
.
Then

is an
R
-lattice with
\rank_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}F=r_{i}
for all
i\in[1,k]
.
If
\mathfrak{m}\in\maxspec(R)
is such that

is a discrete valuation ring and

is the unique minimal prime ideal contained in
\mathfrak{m}
, then
F_{\mathfrak{m}}\cong R_{\mathfrak{m}}^{r_{i}}\cong M(\mathfrak{m})
.
By
Lemma

3.6
, the singular locus is finite.
By Levy and Odenthal’s Package Deal Theorem 2.9 of
[
LO96b
]
, there exists an
R
-lattice

such that
N_{\mathfrak{m}}\cong M(\mathfrak{m})
for all
\mathfrak{m}\in\maxspec(R)
.

Letting

, the module

is faithful over the Bass ring
R(1-e)
.
By
Proposition

3.7
, the genus of

is in bijection with
\pic(\mathcal{O}(\cl_{R(1-e)}(N)))
via the correspondence
N^{\prime}\mapsto\cl_{R(1-e)}(N^{\prime})
.
The claim now follows using
Lemma

3.8
by choosing a suitable
R
-lattice

in the genus of
N
.
∎

If
R_{\mathfrak{m}}
is a discrete valuation ring, then

is fully determined by its rank at the unique minimal prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}_{i}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}
.
Thus, we may replace all but finitely many of the

factors in the codomain of

by a rank vector.
This immediately yields the following description of

.

Corollary 3.11
.

The homomorphism

\varphi(T(R))\to\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k}\times\mkern-15.0mu\prod_{\mathfrak{m}\in\Sing(R)}\mkern-15.0muT(R_{\mathfrak{m}})\times\pic(R\mid\overline{R}).

given by

\varphi([M])=(\rank_{\mathfrak{p}_{1}}(M),\ldots,\rank_{\mathfrak{p}_{k}}(M),([M_{\mathfrak{m}}])_{\mathfrak{m}\in\Sing(R)},\cl(M)),

is injective.
Its image is the submonoid defined by

•

\rank_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}(M)=\rank_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}(M_{\mathfrak{m}})
for all
\mathfrak{m}\in\maxspec(R)
and
\mathfrak{p}_{i}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}
, and

•

\cl(M)=[I\oplus Re]\in\pic(\mathcal{O}(\cl(M)))
with
e=\varepsilon(M)
, and
[I]\in\pic(\mathcal{O}(\cl(M))(1-e))
.

Dropping the
\pic(R\mid\overline{R})
-factor, we can still obtain a transfer homomorphism.
Unless
\Pic(R)
is trivial (for instance, when

is semilocal), we obviously lose some information. Nevertheless, this vantage point is still sufficient to study many factorization theoretical invariants, in particular sets of lengths.
The main advantage is that, since the monoids

are factorial, the resulting monoid is a Diophantine monoid, and hence a Krull monoid.

Theorem 3.12
.

For a Bass ring
R
, the homomorphism

\psi\colon T(R)\to\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k}\times\prod_{\mathfrak{m}\in\Sing(R)}T(R_{\mathfrak{m}})

given by
\psi([M])=(\rank_{\mathfrak{p}_{1}}(M),\ldots,\rank_{\mathfrak{p}_{k}}(M),([M_{\mathfrak{m}}])_{\mathfrak{m}\in\Sing(R)})
induces a transfer homomorphism
\psi\colon T(R)\to\psi(T(R))
.
Its image is defined by
\rank_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}(M)=\rank_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}(M_{\mathfrak{m}})
for all
\mathfrak{m}\in\Sing(R)
and
\mathfrak{p}_{i}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}
.

Moreover, the map
\psi
is injective if and only if
\Pic(R)
is trivial.

Proof.

From
Corollary

3.11
we know that
\psi
is an epimorphism to the described monoid.
If
\psi([M])=0
, then
M=0
.
The only thing remaining to show is that, whenever
\psi([M])=g+h
with
g
,
h\in\psi(T(R))
, then there exist
R
-lattices
N
,
L
with
\psi([N])=g
and
\psi([L])=h
such that
M\cong N\oplus L
.
Passing to
R(1-\varepsilon(M))
, we may assume that
M
is faithful.
Let
N^{\prime}
and
L^{\prime}
be
R
-lattices with
\psi([N^{\prime}])=g
and
\psi([L^{\prime}])=h
.
Then
M_{\mathfrak{m}}\cong N^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{m}}\oplus L^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{m}}
for all
\mathfrak{m}\in\maxspec(R)
.

The genus, the class, and the coefficient rings are determined locally.
Moreover, by
[
LW85
, Proposition 3.4]
, we have
\cl(A\oplus B)=\cl(A)\cl(B)
, and so the isomorphisms
M_{\mathfrak{m}}\cong N^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{m}}\oplus L^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{m}}
imply
\mathcal{O}(\cl(N^{\prime}))\subseteq\mathcal{O}(\cl(M))
and
\mathcal{O}(\cl(L^{\prime}))\subseteq\mathcal{O}(\cl(M))
.

Let
e=\varepsilon(N^{\prime})
.
Using
Proposition

3.7
, together with the epimorphism
\pic(\mathcal{O}(\cl(N^{\prime})))\to\pic(\mathcal{O}(\cl(M)))
, we can find
N
in the genus of
N^{\prime}
with

\cl(M)\mathcal{O}(\cl(M))(1-e)=\cl(N)\cl(L^{\prime})\mathcal{O}(\cl(M))(1-e).

With
f=\varepsilon(L)
, we can similarly choose
L
in the genus of
L^{\prime}
with

\cl(M)\mathcal{O}(\cl(M))(1-f)=\cl(N)\cl(L)\mathcal{O}(\cl(M))(1-f).

Since
M
is faithful, we must have
ef=0
.
Moreover,
\cl(L)f=Rf=\cl(L^{\prime})f
.
Writing
1=(1-e)f+(1-f)
, the equalities above therefore imply

\cl(M)\mathcal{O}(\cl(M))=\cl(N)\cl(L)\mathcal{O}(\cl(M))=\cl(N\oplus L)\mathcal{O}(\cl(M)).

Hence
M\cong N\oplus L
by
Proposition

3.7
.

By
Lemma

3.9
the Picard group
\Pic(R)
is trivial if and only if the genus completely determines the isomorphism class of an
R
-lattice.
Hence, in this case, the factor
\pic(R\mid\overline{R})
can be removed in
Corollary

3.11
.
Conversely, if
\Pic(R)
is non-trivial, then there exist two non-isomorphic
R
-lattices in the same genus and
\psi
is not injective.
∎

Levy–Odenthal gave necessary and sufficient criteria for
R
to satisfy [torsion-free] Krull–Remak–Schmidt–Azumaya.
Their results hold for semiprime, module-finite algebras over a commutative noetherian ring of Krull dimension
1
.
We recall the special case for Bass rings.

Proposition 3.13

(Levy–Odenthal
[
LO96a
]
)
.

Let
R
be a Bass ring.
The monoid
T(R)
is factorial if and only if
\Pic(R)
is trivial and every connected component of
\Spec(R)
contains at most one singular maximal ideal.

Proof.

Decomposing
R
as a product of subrings, we may assume that
\Spec(R)
is connected in the Zariski topology, that is, that
R
is indecomposable as a ring.

[
LO96a
, Theorem 1.3]
gives three conditions that are, in conjunction, equivalent to
T(R)
being factorial.
Condition (a) is that the genus of every
R
-lattice contains a single isomorphism class; this is equivalent to the triviality of
\Pic(R)
;
(b) is that there is at most one non-singular maximal ideal.
The final condition (c) is a local condition.
It is satisfied for Bass rings because
T(R_{\mathfrak{m}})
is factorial by
Theorem

3.4
.
∎

Remark 3.14
.

The previous result can also be proved without making use of the—much more general—theorem of Levy–Odenthal (but still by using their Package Deal Theorem).
We sketch the argument, omitting details.
First the necessity.
It is easy to see that
\Pic(R)
being trivial is necessary.
Then the transfer homomorphism
\psi
from
Theorem

3.12
induces an isomorphism

and it suffices to study
\psi(T(R))
.
With a bit of work, from the connectedness of
\Spec(R)
, one can deduce that there is at most one maximal ideal containing more than one minimal prime ideal.
(In fact, this follows from the existence of the transfer homomorphism, together with
Theorems

4.20
and
5.3
below.)
Each singular

has at least two non-isomorphic indecomposable
R
-lattices of constant rank

: namely
R_{\mathfrak{m}}
and
\overline{R_{\mathfrak{m}}}
.
From this, it is easy to see

.
This proves the necessity.

For the sufficiency, it is again easy to see that if

, then
\psi(T(R))
is factorial (recall that
T(R_{\mathfrak{m}})\cong\mathbb{N}_{0}^{t}
by
Theorem

3.4
).

3.2.
Construction of
\psi(T(R))
as a Diophantine monoid

In this section we further flesh out the structure of the monoid
T(R)
when
R
is a Bass ring. More specifically, we completely describe the image
\psi(T(R))
under the transfer homomorphism given in
Theorem

3.12
, showing that
T(R)
is transfer Krull. Then we state arithmetical results about
T(R)
that are proved later in
Section

5
.

We will describe
\psi(T(R))
as a Diophantine monoid, and will make use of the following basic lemma showing that duplicate columns can essentially be ignored when considering a Diophantine monoid. That is, there is a natural transfer homomorphism between two Diophantine monoids; one with all duplicate columns removed. For simplicity, we state the lemma when the second column is a duplicate of the first. Induction and a permutation of columns yields the more general result.

Lemma 3.15
.

Let
B=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{a}_{1}&\cdots&\mathbf{a}_{n}\end{bmatrix}
be an
m\times n
matrix with integer entries such that
\mathbf{a}_{1}=\mathbf{a}_{2}
and take
H=\ker(B)\cap\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
. Set
B^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{a}_{1}&\mathbf{a}_{3}&\cdots&\mathbf{a}_{n}\end{bmatrix}
and
H^{\prime}=\ker(B^{\prime})\cap\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n-1}
. Then there is a transfer homomorphism
\theta:H\rightarrow H^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Define
\theta:H\rightarrow H^{\prime}
by

\theta(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\ldots,x_{n})=(x_{1}+x_{2},x_{3},\ldots,x_{n}).

If
(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n-1})\in H^{\prime}
, then
y_{1}\mathbf{a}_{1}+\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}y_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}=\mathbf{0}
, and so
(y_{1},0,y_{3},\ldots,y_{n})\in H
, whence
\theta
is surjective. Suppose
\theta(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{z}
. Then
x_{1}+x_{2}=y_{1}+z_{1}
and
y_{i-1}+z_{i-1}=x_{i}
for all
i\in[3,n]
. Since either
x_{1}\leq y_{1}
or
x_{2}\leq z_{1}
, we assume without loss of generality that
y_{1}\leq x_{1}
. Set
\mathbf{w}=(x_{1}-y_{1},x_{2},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n-1})
and
\mathbf{v}=(y_{1},0,y_{2},y_{3},\ldots,y_{n-1})
. Then
\theta(\mathbf{v})=\mathbf{y}
,
\theta(\mathbf{w})=\mathbf{z}
, and
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}.
Thus
\theta
is a transfer homomorphism.
∎

The transfer homomorphism

\psi\colon T(R)\to\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k}\times\prod_{\mathfrak{m}\in\Sing(R)}T(R_{\mathfrak{m}})

of
Theorem

3.12
is given by
\psi([M])=(\rank_{\mathfrak{p}_{1}}(M),\ldots,\rank_{\mathfrak{p}_{k}}(M),([M_{\mathfrak{m}}])_{\mathfrak{m}\in\Sing(R)})
and has image defined by
\rank_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}(M)=\rank_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}(M_{\mathfrak{m}})
for all
\mathfrak{m}\in\Sing(R)
and
\mathfrak{p}_{i}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}
. Since each
T(R_{\mathfrak{m}})
is free (
Theorem

3.4
), the codomain of
\psi
is free. We now describe its image as a Diophantine monoid having defining matrix with columns indexed by
\minspec{R}
and by the isomorphism classes of indecomposable lattices over the localizations of
R
.

Let
\Sing(R)=\{\mathfrak{m}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{m}_{b_{1}},\ldots,\mathfrak{m}_{b}\}
with
b=b_{1}+b_{2}
so that
\mathfrak{m}_{1}
,
\ldots\,
,
\mathfrak{m}_{b_{1}}
are the maximal ideals in
\Sing(R)
containing exactly one minimal prime ideal and
\mathfrak{m}_{b_{1}+1}
,
\ldots\,
,
\mathfrak{m}_{b}
are the maximal ideals in
\Sing(R)
containing exactly two minimal prime ideals. For each
i\in[1,b]
, fix
A_{i1},\ldots,A_{it_{i}}
representatives of the finitely many indecomposable
R_{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}
-lattices and for an
R
-lattice
M
, write
M_{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}\cong A_{i1}^{e_{i1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus A_{it_{i}}^{e_{it_{i}}}.

If
R_{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}
is a domain containing only the minimal prime
\mathfrak{p}
, then
\sum_{j=1}^{t_{i}}e_{ij}=\rank_{\mathfrak{p}}(M)
. If
R_{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}
has two minimal primes
\mathfrak{p}R_{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}
and
\mathfrak{q}R_{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}
, then assume
\rank(A_{i1})=(1,0)
,
\rank(A_{i2})=(0,1)
, and
\rank(A_{ij})=(1,1)
for all

. Then
\rank_{\mathfrak{p}}(M)=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{t_{i}}e_{ij}\right)-e_{i2}
and
\rank_{\mathfrak{q}}(M)=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{t_{i}}e_{ij}\right)-e_{i1}
.

We now define a
(b_{1}+2b_{2})\times(k+\sum_{i=1}^{b}t_{i})
matrix

. The first
k
columns record which minimal primes of
R
are contained in which maximal ideals. If
1\leq i\leq b_{1}
and
1\leq j\leq k
, the

th entry of

is
1
if
\mathfrak{p}_{i}\in\mathfrak{m}_{j}
and
0
otherwise. If
b_{1}<i\leq b
, there are two rows of
B
associated to
\mathfrak{m}_{i}

\mathfrak{p}_{u}
and
\mathfrak{p}_{v}
are distinct minimal primes contained in

and so we place a

in the

st row and

th column and in the

st row and

th column. The other columns record the ranks of the indecomposable modules over the various localizations of

. If

is an indecomposable

-lattice with

, then there is a
-1
in the

th row and

th column. If

, then the

Observe that the entries of the first

are all in

while the entries of the remaining

columns are all in

. With this setup, and with the description of

given in

, we have the following result.

Proposition 3.16
.

Let

be a Bass ring and let

be as defined above. The map

given in

gives a transfer homomorphism from

to

Remark 3.17
.

Although
\psi(T(R))
is always Krull when

is a Bass ring, the monoid

itself is rarely Krull since it is not, in general, cancellative. In fact, by
[

, the monoid

is cancellative if and only if the natural map

is injective, and this rarely happens. However, as is observed in

, Theorem 6.2]
, an isomorphism

implies
M\cong N
whenever
M
,
N
and
X
are lattices with
X
projective. In fact, the monoid
T(R)
is unit-cancellative since we consider only finitely generated modules over a commutative ring.

Example 3.18
.

If
R
is a domain, by
[

]
, we already know that

is half-factorial. However, we explicitly build

to illustrate the construction. Every maximal ideal of

contains the unique minimal ideal of

are in the first column, consisting of all

-lattice

. Thus

B=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1&\cdots&-1&0&\cdots&0&\cdots&0&\cdots&0\\

&&\vdots&&&&&\\

4.
Monoids of graph agglomerations

In this section we depart from the theme of modules over Bass rings.
Instead we introduce the new, to our knowledge, class of monoids of graph agglomerations and study their factorization theory.
This will be justified by the final section of the paper, in which we identify the images of the transfer homomorphisms in
Section

3
as monoids of graph agglomerations.
Thus, results on the factorization theory of monoids of graph agglomerations yield corresponding results on the factorization theory of monoids of modules over Bass rings.

As the monoids introduced here may be of independent interest to the factorization theory community, we have ensured that present section is readable independently from the rest of the paper (excluding, perhaps, the occasional glance at the background material presented in
Section

2
).
Keep in mind that our graphs are finite and permit multiple edges but no loops.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,r)
be a graph.

(1)

An
agglomeration on
\mathcal{G}
is a function
a\colon V\cup E\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
a(v)\geq a(e)
whenever
v\in V
and
e\in E
are incident.

(2)

The
monoid of (graph) agglomerations on
\mathcal{G}
, denoted by
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
is the set of all agglomerations on the graph
\mathcal{G}
together with pointwise addition.

Observe that a sum of two agglomerations is an agglomeration, and that the function that is identically zero is an agglomeration, so that
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
is indeed a monoid.
As a submonoid of
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{V\cup E}
, the monoid
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
is commutative, cancellative, and reduced.
If
\mathcal{G}
is the null graph, then
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})=0
is the trivial group.
We tacitly ignore this trivial case when convenient.
If
\mathcal{G}
is a trivial graph, then
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})=(\mathbb{N}_{0},+)
is a factorial monoid with the unique prime element
1
; we sometimes also exclude this trivial case for convenience.

The following two basic properties are immediate from the definition.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a graph.

(1)

If
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
is a subgraph of
\mathcal{G}
, then
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})
embeds canonically as a divisor-closed submonoid into
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
.

(2)

If
\mathcal{G}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{G}_{2}
is a disjoint union of two graphs, then

\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{G}_{2})\cong\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}_{1})\times\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}_{2}).

Despite this lemma being trivial, it has two significant consequences for the study of factorizations in agglomeration monoids.
First, it generally suffices to consider connected graphs.
Second, it is possible to obtain lower bounds on arithmetical invariants by working with suitable subgraphs.

The definition of an agglomeration ensures that whenever
a(e)>0
for an edge
e
, then also
a(v)>0
for the vertices
v
incident with
e
; this allows us to define the support of an agglomeration.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,r)
be a graph.

(1)

If
a\in\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
then the support of
a
, denoted by
\supp(a)
, is the subgraph of
\mathcal{G}
consisting of the vertices
v\in V
with
a(v)>0
and the edges
e\in E
with
a(e)>0
.

(2)

If
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime},r^{\prime})
is a subgraph of
\mathcal{G}
, then the
indicator

\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}\colon V\cup E\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
is the agglomeration defined by

\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $x\in V^{\prime}\cup E^{\prime}$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

It is clear that the indicator and the support furnish a bijection between subgraphs of
\mathcal{G}
and agglomerations on
\mathcal{G}
taking values in
\{0,1\}
.
We shall tacitly identify subgraphs of
\mathcal{G}
with their indicators when convenient.
If
v\in V
, we write
\mathbf{1}_{v}
for the indicator which is
1
on
v
and
0
everywhere else.
If
e\in E
, we write
\mathbf{1}_{(r(e),e)}
for the indicator which is
1
on
e
and its two incident vertices, but
0
everywhere else.

Lemma 4.4

(Splitting Lemma)
.

Let
a
be an agglomeration on a non-null graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,r)
.

(1)

Let
m=\max\{\,a(x):x\in V\cup E\,\}
.
Let
b\colon V\cup E\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
be defined by

b(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $a(x)=m$,}\\
0&\text{if $a(x)<m$.}\end{cases}

Then
b
and
a-b
are agglomerations, and therefore the agglomeration
a
factors as
a=b+(a-b)
in
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
.

(2)

The indicator
\mathbf{1}_{\supp(a)}
and
a-\mathbf{1}_{\supp(a)}
are agglomerations, and therefore the agglomeration
a
factors as
a=\mathbf{1}_{\supp(a)}+(a-\mathbf{1}_{\supp(a)})
in
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
.

Proof.

(1)

If
e
is an edge with
a(e)=m
, then
b(v)\geq m
for any incident vertex
v
.
Since
m
is the maximum of the function
a
, we must have
b(v)=m
.
Thus
b
is an agglomeration.

Consider now
a-b
.
We only have to verify the inequality for vertices
v
with
a(v)=m
.
Then
(a-b)(v)=m-1
.
Let
e
be an edge incident with
v
.
If
a(e)<m
, then
(a-b)(e)=a(e)\leq m-1=(a-b)(v)
.
Otherwise, we have
a(e)=m
, but then
(a-b)(e)=a(e)-1=m-1=(a-b)(v)
.

(2)

It is easy to see that in
a-\mathbf{1}_{\supp(a)}
all positive values are decreased by
1
, and therefore the inequalities for an agglomeration are satisfied.
∎

Observe that in
(1)
of the previous lemma, the agglomeration
b
is simply the indicator of the subgraph of
\mathcal{G}
at which
a
is maximal.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a graph.
An agglomeration
a\in\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
is an atom in
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
if and only if
a=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}
for a non-null, connected subgraph of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

If
a
is an atom, the splitting lemma implies
a(x)\in\{0,1\}
for all
x\in V\cup E
.
Thus necessarily
a=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}
for a subgraph
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{G}
.
Now
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{G}_{1}^{\prime}}+\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{G}_{2}^{\prime}}
if and only if
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
is the disjoint union of
\mathcal{G}_{1}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}^{\prime}
.
The claim follows.
∎

The splitting lemma implies that every nonzero
a\in\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
is a sum of atoms, that is, the monoid
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
is atomic (this is also obvious from more general facts).
Having in mind the bijection between subgraphs and indicator functions, the monoid
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
is generated by the connected subgraphs of
\mathcal{G}
.
Hence, an arbitrary element of
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
may be viewed as an
agglomeration
of subgraphs of
\mathcal{G}
, explaining our terminology.

The next two results place
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
in the much larger class of Krull monoids.
For Krull monoids there is an well-established machinery to study their factorization theory via transfer homomorphisms to monoids of zero-sum sequences.
Surprisingly, this does
not
seem to be the best approach to study agglomeration monoids on graphs; after a short interlude we will therefore return to study the factorization theory of agglomeration monoids directly.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,r)
be a graph.
The monoid of agglomerations
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
is a finitely generated reduced Krull monoid and its divisor theory has
3\lvert E\rvert+\iota
prime divisors.
Here,
\iota
is the number of isolated vertices in
\mathcal{G}
.

Proof.

It suffices to consider the case where
\mathcal{G}
is connected.
If
\mathcal{G}
has no edges, then it is either the null graph, in which case
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})=0
, or it consists of a single vertex, in which case
\mathbf{A}(G)=\mathbb{N}_{0}
, which is a Krull monoid with a single prime divisor.
From now on we may assume that
\mathcal{G}
has at least one edge.

By definition
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
is the submonoid of
\mathbb{N}^{V\cup E}
defined by the inequalities
a(e)\leq a(v)
whenever
e\in E
is incident with
v\in V
.
We shall explicitly determine a divisor theory for
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
; we do so through the standard technique of introducing slack variables to turn the defining inequalities into equations and then eliminate redundant variables.

Let
I=\{\,(e,v)\in E\times V:e\sim v\,\}
. Note
\lvert I\rvert=2\lvert E\rvert
; we may assume without restriction
E\cap(E\times V)=\emptyset
.
For
a\in\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
we define
f_{a}\colon E\cup I\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
by

f_{a}(e)=a(e)\text{ for $e\in E$}\quad\text{and}\quad f_{a}(e,v)=a(v)-a(e)\text{ for $(e,v)\in I$}.

Let
\varphi\colon\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathbb{N}_{0}^{E\cup I}
be defined by
\varphi(a)=f_{a}
.
Then
\varphi
is a monoid homomorphism.
We show that
\varphi
is injective.
Let
a
,
b\in\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
with
f_{a}=f_{b}
.
Then
a(e)=b(e)
for all
e\in E
.
Let
v\in V
and let
e
be an edge incident with
v
.
Then
a(v)=f_{a}(e,v)+f_{a}(e)=f_{b}(e,v)+f_{b}(v)=b(v)
.

It is similarly easy to see that
\varphi(\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}))
is the submonoid of all
f\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{E\cup I}
satisfying the equations
f(e,v)+f(e)=f(e^{\prime},v)+f(e^{\prime})
whenever
e
,
e^{\prime}\in E
are both incident with
v
.
As a Diophantine monoid, therefore
\varphi(\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}))\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{0}^{E\cup I}
is saturated.
Hence
\varphi\colon\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathbb{N}_{0}^{E\cup I}
is a divisor homomorphism.

To show that
\varphi
is a divisor theory, it suffices to show that for every standard basis vector
f
of
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{E\cup I}
there exist
a
,
b\in\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
with
f=\min\{\varphi(a),\varphi(b)\}
, where the minimum is taken pointwise.

We distinguish two cases for
f\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{E\cup I}
.
Let first
e\in E
and let
f(e)=1
and
f(x)=0
for all
x\in E\setminus\{e\}\cup I
.
Let
v
,
w
be the two vertices incident with
e
.
Define
a\in\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
by
a(e)=a(v)=a(w)=1
and
a(x)=0
if
x\not\in\{v,w,e\}
.
Then
f_{a}(e)=1
and
f_{a}(e^{\prime})=0
for
e^{\prime}\in E\setminus\{e\}
.
Now define
b\in\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
by
b(v^{\prime})=b(e^{\prime})=1
for all
v^{\prime}\in V
and
e^{\prime}\in E
.
Then
f_{b}(e^{\prime})=1
for all
e^{\prime}\in E
and
f_{b}(e^{\prime},v^{\prime})=0
for all
(e^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in I
.
We conclude
f=\min\{f_{a},f_{b}\}
.

Let now
(e,v)\in I
and let
f(e,v)=1
and
f(x)=0
for all
x\in E\cup I\setminus(e,v)
.
Define
a\in\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
by
a(v)=1
and
a(x)=0
for all
x\neq v
.
Then
f_{a}(e)=0
for all
e\in E
.
For the incidences, we have
f_{a}(e^{\prime},v)=1
if
e^{\prime}\sim v
and
f_{a}(e^{\prime},v^{\prime})=0
for all
(e^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in I
with
v^{\prime}\neq v
.
Now define
b\in\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
by
b(v^{\prime})=1
for all
v^{\prime}\in V
, by
b(e)=0
, and
b(e^{\prime})=1
if
e^{\prime}\in E\setminus\{e\}
.
Then
f_{b}(e,v^{\prime})=1
for both vertices
v^{\prime}
with
v^{\prime}\sim e
.
Moreover
f_{b}(e^{\prime},v^{\prime})=0
for all
(e^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in I
with
e^{\prime}\neq e
.
We again conclude
f=\min\{f_{a},f_{b}\}
.
∎

Corollary 4.7
.

The divisor class group of
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
is free abelian of rank
2\lvert E\rvert-\lvert V\rvert+\iota
, where
\iota
is the number of isolated vertices in
\mathcal{G}
.

Proof.

The divisor class group of a finite product of Krull monoid is isomorphic to the product of the individual class groups.
Without restriction, let
\mathcal{G}
be connected and non-trivial.

We continue in the notation of the proof of
Theorem

4.6
.
The image of the divisor theory
\varphi\colon\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathbb{N}_{0}^{E\cup I}
is defined by the equations
f(e,v)+f(e)=f(e^{\prime},v)+f(e^{\prime})
whenever
e
,
e^{\prime}
are edges incident with a vertex
v
.
Fixing for each vertex
v
an edge
e_{v}
incident with
v
, we observe that the equations

f(e_{v},v)+f(e_{v})=f(e^{\prime},v)+f(e^{\prime}),\quad\text{whenever $e^{\prime}\in E\setminus\{e_{v}\}$ is incident with $v$,}

are sufficient to define
\im\varphi
.
This yields
\lvert I\rvert-\lvert V\rvert+\iota=2\lvert E\rvert-\lvert V\rvert+\iota
linear equations defining
\im\varphi
as a submonoid of
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{E\cup I}
.
These equations are all linearly independent because
f(e^{\prime},v)
appears in a unique equation.
We may write the corresponding system matrix as an integer matrix
M
with columns indexed by
E\cup I
, and having
2\lvert E\rvert-\lvert V\rvert+\iota
linearly independent rows.

Denote by
\mathbf{q}(\im\varphi)
the quotient group of
\im\varphi
.
Then the divisor class group
\Cl(\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}))
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{E\cup I}/\mathbf{q}(\im\varphi)
.
We claim
\mathbf{q}(\im\varphi)=\ker M
.
Indeed the inclusion
\mathbf{q}(\im\varphi)\subseteq\ker M
is obvious.
For the converse, note that the agglomeration

with

for all

for all

all of whose entries are positive.
Now let

.
Let

be sufficiently large so that

.
Then
nf_{b}
,
x+nf_{b}\in\im\varphi
and

.

We conclude

.

is free abelian of rank

.

Definition 4.9
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(E,V,r)
be a non-null graph and
a\in\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
.

(1)

The
sequence-length
of
a
is

\ell(a)\coloneqq\sum_{v\in V}\deg_{\mathcal{G}}(v)a(v)-\sum_{e\in E}a(e)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(2)

The
Davenport constant
of
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
is

\mathsf{D}(\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}))\coloneqq\max\{\,\ell(a):a\text{ an atom of $\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})$}\,\}.

Using the defining property of an agglomeration, it is easy to verify that
\ell(a)
is indeed nonnegative.

Remark 4.10
.

A divisor theory of a Krull monoid gives rise to a transfer homomorphism to a monoid of zero-sum sequences
\mathcal{B}(G_{0})
, where
G_{0}
is a subset of the divisor class group
G
(see the surveys
[
BW13
,
Ger16
,
GZ20
]
).
In this way, to every
a\in\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
, we can associate a zero-sum sequence
\theta(a)\in\mathcal{B}(G_{0})
.
The sequence-length
\ell
is defined in such a way that
\ell(a)
is indeed the length of the zero-sum sequence
\theta(a)
.
This can be seen from the divisor theory as constructed in the proof of
Theorem

4.6
.
By the uniqueness properties of a divisor theory, this length does not depend on the particular choice of divisor theory.

The Davenport constant is a classical invariant in factorization theory through which many arithmetical invariants can be estimated, or even expressed exactly.
In many cases, e.g., when
G=G_{0}
is a finite abelian group, it is notoriously difficult to compute the Davenport constant.
The case of agglomeration monoids is remarkable in that it is very easy to compute
\mathsf{D}(\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}))
.
However, due to the set of prime divisors not being symmetric in our setting (
G_{0}\neq-G_{0}
) this is of limited use.
For instance,
\mathsf{D}(\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}))
gives bounds on invariants such as the refined elasticities
\rho_{k}(\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}))
, but in the case
G_{0}\neq-G_{0}
it does not give precise values.

Proposition 4.11
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(E,V,r)
be a non-trivial, connected graph.

(1)

We have

\mathsf{D}(\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}))=2\lvert E\rvert-\lvert V\rvert+1.

(2)

An atom
a
of
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
satisfies
\ell(a)=\mathsf{D}(\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}))
if and only if
\supp(a)
is a tree containing every vertex of degree at least
2
.
In particular, every spanning tree of
\mathcal{G}
yields an atom of maximal sequence-length.
If
\mathcal{G}
has minimum vertex degree at least
2
, then the atoms of maximal sequence-length are in bijection with the spanning trees of
\mathcal{G}
.

Proof.

(1)

Let
a\in\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
be an atom.
Then
a
is the indicator of the connected subgraph
\supp(a)=(V_{a},E_{a},r_{a})
of
\mathcal{G}
.
Therefore we get

(1)

\ell(a)=\sum_{v\in V_{a}}\deg_{\mathcal{G}}(v)-\lvert E_{a}\rvert.

Note that the degree of
v
is considered as a vertex of
\mathcal{G}
,
not
as a vertex of
\supp(a)
.

If there is a vertex
v\in V\setminus V_{a}
, then
v
can be chosen in such a way that it can be connected to
\supp(a)
by a single edge.
Adding
v
, and a connecting edge, to
\supp(a)
produces an atom
a^{\prime}
with
\ell(a^{\prime})\geq\ell(a)
.
To maximize
Eq.

1
, we may therefore assume
V_{a}=V
.
Then

\ell(a)=\sum_{v\in V}\deg(v)-\lvert E_{a}\rvert=2\lvert E\rvert-\lvert E_{a}\rvert.

This value is maximized by taking
\lvert E_{a}\rvert
minimal, which happens if and only if
\supp(a)
is a spanning tree.
In this case
\lvert E_{a}\rvert=\lvert V\rvert-1
.

(2)

Let again
a
be an atom with
\supp(a)=(V_{a},E_{a},r_{a})
.
If there exists
v\in V\setminus V_{a}
and
\deg(v)>1
, then the extension process just described produces an atom
a^{\prime}
with
\ell(a^{\prime})>\ell(a)
and
\ell(a)
cannot be maximal.
Similarly, if
\supp(a)
is not a tree we may remove an edge, without breaking the connectivity, to find an atom
a^{\prime}
with
\ell(a^{\prime})>\ell(a)
.
Thus, if
\ell(a)=\mathsf{D}(\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}))
, then
\supp(a)
is a tree and contains every vertex of
\mathcal{G}
of degree at least
2
.

Conversely, if
\supp(a)
is of this form, we may extend
a
to
a^{\prime}
with
\supp(a^{\prime})
a spanning tree and
\ell(a)=\ell(a^{\prime})
.
By the argument in
(1)
, this sequence-length is maximal.
∎

(2)

By

4.1.
Elasticities

We now turn our attention to specific bounds for the elasticity

and the refined elasticities

.
After that, we characterize factorial and half-factorial monoids of graph agglomerations in
Theorem

4.20
.

A
semi-length function
is a monoid homomorphism
\sigma\colon\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with
\sigma(a)=0
if and only if
a=0
.
By
[
AA92
]
we have
\rho(\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}))\leq M^{*}/m^{*}

with

\displaystyle M^{*}

\displaystyle=\max\{\,\sigma(a):\text{$a$ is an atom of $\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})$}\,\}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle m^{*}

\displaystyle=\min\{\,\sigma(a):\text{$a$ is an atom of $\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})$}\,\}.

Theorem 4.13
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a non-null graph.
Let
m
be the maximal order of a connected component of
\mathcal{G}
, and
D
the maximal degree of
\mathcal{G}
.

(1)

For every
r>D/2
, the function
\sigma_{r}\colon\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
defined by

\sigma_{r}(a)=r\sum_{v\in V}a(v)-\sum_{e\in E}a(e)

is a semi-length function.

(2)

We have
\rho(\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}))\leq m-\frac{m-1}{D}
.

(3)

If
\mathcal{G}
is simple, then
\rho(\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}))\leq m-2+\frac{2}{m}
.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{G}_{1}
,
\ldots\,
,
\mathcal{G}_{s}
are the connected components of
\mathcal{G}
, it is easily shown that
\rho(\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}))=\max\{\,\rho(\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}_{i})):i\in[1,s]\,\}
, see
[
GHK06
, Proposition 1.4.5.2]
.
Since it also suffices to establish the semi-length property on each connected component, we assume without restriction that
\mathcal{G}
is connected and of order
m
.
The claims are trivial for
m=1
, so assume
m\geq 2
.

(1)

Clearly
\sigma_{r}
is additive on
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
.
Since
r>\frac{D}{2}
and
\mathcal{G}
has at most
Dm/2
edges, we have
\sigma(r)\geq 0
with equality only when
a=0
.

We now show
(2)
and
(3)
through a suitable choice of
r
.
Suppose
r\geq 1
.
From the definition of
\sigma_{r}
and the fact that an atom has values in
\{0,1\}
, it is then clear that
\sigma_{r}(a)
is maximized by taking
a
an atom with
\supp(a)
a spanning tree. Thus
M^{*}=rm-(m-1)
.

(2)
Set
r=D\geq 1
. Let
a
be an atom with
\supp(a)
of order
m^{\prime}
.
If
m^{\prime}=1
, then clearly
\sigma_{r}(a)=D
.
If
m^{\prime}>1
, then
\sigma_{r}(a)\geq m^{\prime}D-m^{\prime}D/2=m^{\prime}D/2\geq D
.
Thus
m^{*}=D
and
\rho(\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}))\leq m-(m-1)/D
.

(3)

Set
r=m/2\geq 1
and let
a
be an atom with
\supp(a)
of order
m^{\prime}\leq m
.
Since
\mathcal{G}
is simple, the support of
a
has at most
m^{\prime}(m^{\prime}-1)/2
edges.
Thus

\sigma_{r}(a)\geq rm^{\prime}-\frac{m^{\prime}(m^{\prime}-1)}{2}=r+(m^{\prime}-1)\Big(r-\frac{m^{\prime}}{2}\Big)\geq r.

Thus

and
\rho(\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}))\leq m-2+2/m
.
∎

Theorem 4.15

,

Thus

\begin{split}l&=\sum_{i=1}^{s}l_{i}\leq s+\sum_{i=1}^{s}(k_{i}-1)\lvert V_{i}\rvert\leq s+\sum_{i=1}^{s}(k_{i}-1)(\lvert V\rvert-2)\leq s+(k-s)(\lvert V\rvert-2)\\
&\leq s+(k-1)(\lvert V\rvert-2)=s+(k-1)\lvert V\rvert-2(k-1)\leq(k-1)\lvert V\rvert,\end{split}

where the last inequality follows from
2\leq s\leq k
.
Note that this bound is strictly smaller than the one we claim for
\rho_{k}(\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}))
.
This implies that any
a
achieving the upper bound
\rho_{k}(a)=(k-1)\lvert V\rvert+1
must have connected support with
\supp(a)=\mathcal{G}
.

(2)

Let
k\geq 2
.
Suppose there exists
a\in\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G})
and atoms
c_{1}
,
\ldots\,
,
c_{k}
,
b_{1}
,
\ldots\,
,
b_{l}
with
l=(k-1)\lvert V\rvert+1
such that

a=c_{1}+\cdots+c_{k}=b_{1}+\cdots+b_{l}.

By what we just showed
\supp(a)=\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{G}
is connected.
Without restriction we may replace each
c_{i}
with the indicator of a spanning tree of
\supp(c_{i})
.
This may change
a
and the
b_{i}
, but cannot make
l
smaller.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a spanning tree of
\mathcal{G}
, and as before, denote by
n_{i}(\mathcal{T})
the number of edges of
\mathcal{T}
that appear in
\supp(b_{i})
.
Then in
Eq.

2
we must have equality throughout, so that

\sum_{i=1}^{l}(n_{i}(\mathcal{T})+1)=k\lvert V\rvert=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{v\in V}c_{i}(v).

The first thing we observe from this is that necessarily each
c_{i}
must have
\lvert V\rvert
vertices, so that
\supp(c_{i})
is a spanning tree.
Secondly, suppose that distinct
c_{i}
and
c_{j}
share an edge.
Taking
\mathcal{T}=\supp(c_{i})
, then
\sum_{i=1}^{l}(n_{i}(\mathcal{T})+1)\geq l+\lvert V\rvert
, and thus

, a contradiction.
We have thus shown that
\supp(c_{1})
,
\ldots\,
,
\supp(c_{k})
are pairwise edge-disjoint spanning trees of
\mathcal{G}
.
∎

Remark 4.16
.

The
spanning tree packing number
\tau(\mathcal{G})
of a graph
\mathcal{G}
is the largest number
k
such that
\mathcal{G}
contains
k
edge-disjoint spanning trees.
By the previous theorem, the number
\tau(\mathcal{G})
is the precise

at which the value of
\rho_{k}(\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}))
starts to differ from the upper bound.
The survey
[
Pal01
]
discusses several results about the spanning tree packing number.
We also mention the more recent papers
[
LHGL14
,
GPGS18
,
LLT19
]
.

Example 4.17
.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{k}
be the graph consisting of two vertices and
k\geq 2
edges between them.
Since
\mathcal{B}_{k}
has

for

We now concern ourselves with the question when

is half-factorial and when it is factorial.

Lemma 4.18
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,r)
be a graph.
If

and
\supp(a)
is acyclic, then
\mathsf{L}(a)=\{l\}
with

In particular

.

Proof.

Using
Lemma

4.2
it suffices to consider the case where
\mathcal{G}
is a tree.
Suppose

is a summand of

\supp(b)
is a subgraph of

and therefore acyclic.
In particular,
\supp(b)
is a tree for every atom

dividing

.
For a tree

we have

.
Thus, if

with

,
\ldots\,
,

Remark 4.19

The converse of the previous lemma is false: there exist agglomerations

5.
From lattices to graph agglomerations

In this final section we bridge the studies of lattices of modules over Bass rings and monoids of graph agglomerations.
By establishing a transfer homomorphism, we are able to prove
Theorems

1.1
and
3.22
.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
R
be a Bass ring.
The
graph of prime ideal intersections
of
R
, denoted by
\mathcal{G}_{R}
, is the graph with

•

set of vertices equal to
\minspec(R)
,

•

set of edges consisting of all maximal ideals of
R
that contain more than one minimal prime ideal, and

•

each edge being incident with the two minimal prime ideals contained in it.

Since every maximal ideal in a Bass ring
R
contains at most two minimal prime ideals, this is a well defined graph (possibly with multiple edges, but with no loops).
Because
R
has finitely many minimal prime ideals and the set of singular maximal ideals is finite, the graph is finite.
We discuss two examples below (
Example

5.6
).

Remark 5.2
.

Let
R
be a Bass ring with minimal prime ideals
\mathfrak{p}_{1}
,
\ldots\,
,
\mathfrak{p}_{k}
.
The Zariski closed set
V_{i}\coloneqq\overline{\{\mathfrak{p}_{i}\}}=\{\,\mathfrak{q}\in\Spec(R):\mathfrak{p}_{i}\subseteq\mathfrak{q}\,\}
is open, as it is the complement of the closed set
\bigcup_{j\neq i}V_{j}
.
Two distinct such sets
V_{i}
and
V_{j}
have non-trivial intersection if and only if there exists a maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
containing both
\mathfrak{p}_{i}
and
\mathfrak{p}_{j}
.
Therefore
\mathcal{G}_{R}
is the intersection (multi)graph of the sets
V_{1}
,
\ldots\,
,
V_{k}
in the graph-theoretical sense.

We also see that
R
is indecomposable as a ring if and only if
\mathcal{G}_{R}
is connected.
If
R=R_{1}\times\cdots\times R_{l}
with indecomposable rings
R_{1}
,
\ldots\,
,
R_{l}
, then
\mathcal{G}_{R}\cong\mathcal{G}_{R_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{G}_{R_{l}}
, with
\mathcal{G}_{R_{i}}
the connected components of the graph
\mathcal{G}_{R}
.

We now recognize the codomain of the transfer homomorphism of
Proposition

3.21
as a monoid of graph agglomerations.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
R
be a Bass ring and let
\mathcal{G}_{R}
be its prime ideal intersection graph.
Then there exists a transfer homomorphism
\theta\colon T(R)\to\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}_{R})
from the monoid of isomorphism classes of
R
-lattices to the monoid of agglomerations on
\mathcal{G}_{R}
.

Proof.

If
R=0
, then
\mathcal{G}_{R}
is the null graph, and
T(R)=\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}_{R})=0
.
If
\dim(R)=0
, then
R=K_{1}\times\cdots\times K_{k}
is a product of fields and
\mathcal{G}_{R}
is a disjoint union of
k
trivial graphs.
Hence
T(R)\cong\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k}\cong\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}_{R})
and there is nothing to show.
From now on
\dim(R)=1
.

Let
H\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{0}^{c}
be the Diophantine monoid from
Proposition

3.21
.
With
E\subseteq\maxspec(R)
the set of maximal ideals of
R
containing two minimal prime ideals, with
V\coloneqq\minspec(R)
, and with indexing as in
Proposition

3.21
, we have

H=\{\,\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{c}:x_{\mathfrak{p}}=x_{\mathfrak{m}}+x_{\mathfrak{m}_{\mathfrak{p}}}\text{ for all $(\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{p})\in E\times V$ with $\mathfrak{p}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}$ }\,\}.

Since there exists a transfer homomorphism
\varphi\colon T(R)\to H
, it is sufficient to show
H\cong\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}_{R})
.
Note that
x_{\mathfrak{p}}\geq x_{\mathfrak{m}}
for every
(\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{p})\in E
with
\mathfrak{p}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}
, and that
x_{\mathfrak{m}_{\mathfrak{p}}}
can then be computed as
x_{\mathfrak{m}_{\mathfrak{p}}}=x_{\mathfrak{p}}-x_{\mathfrak{m}}
.
Dropping the coordinates corresponding to columns of the type
\mathfrak{m}_{\mathfrak{p}}
, we have that

H\cong\{\,\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{V\cup E}:x_{\mathfrak{p}}\geq x_{\mathfrak{m}}\text{ for all $(\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{m})\in E$ with $\mathfrak{p}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}$}\,\}.

Then
H\cong\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{G}_{R})
by definition of the monoid of graph agglomerations,
Definition

4.1
.
∎

Not only can the arithmetic of
T(R)
be studied in terms of a monoid of graph agglomerations whenever
R
is a Bass ring, but every graph can be realized as the graph of prime ideal intersections of some Bass ring. In the following realization result, keep in mind that all our graphs are finite and that we permit multiple edges but no loops.

Proposition 5.4
.

(1)

Let
R
be a Bass ring and let
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
be a subgraph of
\mathcal{G}_{R}
.
Then there exists a multiplicative set
S\subseteq R
such that the localization
R^{\prime}=(S^{-1})R
satisfies
\mathcal{G}_{R^{\prime}}\cong\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
.

(2)

For every finite graph
\mathcal{G}
, there exists a semilocal Bass ring
R
with
\mathcal{G}_{R}\cong\mathcal{G}
.

Proof.

(1)

Let
\mathfrak{m}_{1}
,
\ldots\,
,
\mathfrak{m}_{k}
be the maximal ideals of
R
that are the edges of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
, and let
\mathfrak{p}_{1}
,
\ldots\,
,
\mathfrak{p}_{l}
denote the minimal prime ideals of
R
that are the vertices of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
.
Then
S\coloneqq R\setminus(\mathfrak{p}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathfrak{p}_{l}\cup\mathfrak{m}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathfrak{m}_{k})
is a multiplicative set.
The localization
R^{\prime}=S^{-1}R
is a Bass ring with minimal prime ideals
R^{\prime}\mathfrak{p}_{1},\ldots,R^{\prime}\mathfrak{p}_{l}
and maximal ideals
R^{\prime}\mathfrak{m}_{1},\ldots,R^{\prime}\mathfrak{m}_{k}
.
(In the degenerate case
l=0
, the ring
R^{\prime}
is the zero ring.)
Since the inclusions between these ideals are preserved, we have
\mathcal{G}_{R}^{\prime}\cong\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
.

(2)

The null graph is realized by the zero ring.
Assume
\mathcal{G}
is non-null.
By
(1)
it suffices to prove: for all
n\geq 1
and
d\geq 1
, there exists a semilocal Bass ring
R
with
\mathcal{G}_{R}
having
n
vertices, and at least
d
edges between any two vertices.
Let
K
be an algebraically closed field and let
C_{1}
,
\ldots\,
,
C_{n}
be pairwise distinct, smooth affine plane curves over
K
such that any two of them have at least
d
intersection points.
We further assume that all these intersection points are pairwise distinct.
Smoothness guarantees that every
C_{i}
has multiplicity
1
at each of its points.
Thus
C=C_{1}\cup\cdots\cup C_{n}
has multiplicity
2
at each point where two curves intersect, and multiplicity
1
at every other point.
It follows that the affine coordinate ring
R=K[C]
is a Bass ring, and that
\mathcal{G}_{R}
has
n
vertices and at least
d
edges between any two vertices.

Let
\mathfrak{m}_{1}
,
\ldots\,
,
\mathfrak{m}_{k}
denote the maximal ideals of
R
corresponding to the intersection points of the curves
C_{1}
,
\ldots\,
,
C_{n}
.
Let
S\coloneqq R\setminus(\mathfrak{m}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathfrak{m}_{k})
.
Then

is a semilocal Bass ring with
\mathcal{G}_{S^{-1}R}\cong\mathcal{G}_{R}
.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

If

is a simple graph, then we can take the curves

,

in the proof of

For higher multiplicity of the edges, it is similarly easy to construct higher degree curves with enough intersection points.

.
For any

Choosing among the infinitely many curves of this family

Remark 5.7
.

(1)

The transfer homomorphism
\psi
in
Section

3.2
maps the isomorphism class of a module
M
to its genus; for each singular maximal ideal we record the multiplicity of the indecomposable modules in the direct-sum decomposition
M_{\mathfrak{m}}
.
If
\mathfrak{m}
contains two minimal prime ideals, each of these modules
M_{\mathfrak{m}}
has rank
(1,0)
,
(0,1)
, or
(1,1)
.
In passing to the monoid of graph agglomerations we lose some additional information: only the ranks of the indecomposable summands of
M_{\mathfrak{m}}
are recorded (this corresponds to the elimination of duplicate columns in the defining matrix of the Diophantine monoid, as in
Lemma

3.15
).
Put another way, if there are multiple modules of rank
(1,1)
, we do not distinguish between them.

To correct for this loss of information, it would be possible to refine the monoid of graph agglomerations as follows:
put an edge for each
\mathfrak{m}
containing two minimal prime ideals and each indecomposable
N_{\mathfrak{m}}
and group these edges by
\mathfrak{m}
.
Then the function associating values to the edges and vertices of the graphs must be defined so that the value of each vertex is larger than or equal to the sum of weights of all the edges corresponding to
\mathfrak{m}
.
Although this definition would provide a more complete translation of the genus (it still does not account for the different indecomposables at a singular maximal ideal containing a unique minimal prime), such a monoid would be more complicated to study.

Since there is already a transfer homomorphism from
T(R)
to a monoid of graph agglomerations, this additional complexity does not provide more information as far as sets of lengths are concerned.

(2)

More refined arithmetical invariants, such as the tame degree, catenary degree, or
\omega
-invariant, are in general not preserved by transfer homomorphisms.
To pull back information about these invariants along a transfer homomorphism, one needs to study the fibers of the transfer homomorphism.
In the present paper, we have chosen to forego the additional technical complexity this entails.
