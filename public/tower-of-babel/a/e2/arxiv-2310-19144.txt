Exchange-only virial relation from the adiabatic connection
Andre Laestadius, Mihály A. Csirik, Markus Penz, Nicolas Tancogne-Dejean, Michael Ruggenthaler, Angel Rubio, Trygve Helgaker

Abstract
The exchange-only virial relation due to Levy and Perdew is revisited. Invoking the adiabatic connection, we introduce the exchange energy in terms of the right-derivative of the universal density functional w.r.t. the coupling strength λ \lambda at λ = 0 \lambda=0 . This agrees with the Levy–Perdew definition of the exchange energy as a high-density limit of the full exchange-correlation energy. By relying on v v -representability for a fixed density at varying coupling strength, we prove an exchange-only virial relation without an explicit local-exchange potential. Instead, the relation is in terms of a limit ( λ ↘ 0 \lambda\searrow 0 ) involving the exchange-correlation potential v xc λ v_{\mathrm{xc}}^{\lambda} , which exists by assumption of v v -representability. On the other hand, a local-exchange potential v x v_{\mathrm{x}} is not warranted to exist as such a limit.

Exact constraints play an important role in the development and testing of density-functional approximations,

\displaystyle E_{\rm x}[\rho]=-\int\rho(\mathbf{r})\,\mathbf{r}\cdot\nabla v_{\mathrm{x}}(\mathbf{r})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}.

(1)

Relying on a different work of
Levy and Perdew 1994
, we
define
the exchange energy as the high-density limit of the exchange–correlation energy in the manner

E_{\rm x}[\rho]=\lim_{\gamma\to\infty}\frac{1}{\gamma}E_{\mathrm{xc}}[\rho_{\gamma}],

(2)

where the scaled density is given by
\rho_{\gamma}(\mathbf{r})=\gamma^{3}\rho(\gamma\mathbf{r})
.
The exchange–correlation energy
E_{\mathrm{xc}}[\rho]
is defined in terms of the universal density functional
F[\rho]
in the usual manner; see Eq. (
33
). Noting that
(\rho_{\gamma})_{\mu}=\rho_{\gamma\mu}
, it follows directly from Eq. (
2

;

E_{\rm x}[\rho_{\gamma}]=\gamma E_{\rm x}[\rho]
.

There exists an alternative, equivalent definition for
E_{\rm x}[\rho]
that employs the adiabatic connection in DFT. This extremely useful concept relates not only the non-interacting system (with interaction strength
\lambda=0
) with the fully interacting one (
\lambda=1
) for a fixed, given density
\rho
, as it is customary in Kohn–Sham DFT, but also reveals the properties of the functionals for all intermediate values of the coupling strength
\lambda
. In the following,
we study
F[\rho]
as a function the interaction strength for a fixed, given density
\rho
,
using the notation
\lambda\mapsto F(\lambda)
; see Eq. (
8
). The theory of convex analysis then provides multiple tools to study the adiabatic connection. Already from the definition of the universal density functional, many practical properties can be derived when considering it as a function of
\lambda
. The intuitive understanding of the exchange energy in this setting is then as the linear component of
\lambda E_{\mathrm{xc}}(\lambda)=F(\lambda)-F(0)-\lambda E_{\rm H}
, where
E_{\rm H}
is the Hartree energy (see Eq. (
32
)).

Here, all derivations will be performed for general, mixed
N
-electron states, thus allowing for degeneracy at all coupling strengths, with the overall assumption of a
v
-representable density
\rho
at all coupling strengths
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
(a property henceforth called
v^{\lambda}
-representability). This comes as a form of minimal assumption, since, without
v^{\lambda}
-representability, the selected density cannot be assigned to a valid ground state of the system under consideration.
On the other hand, it cannot be assumed that the universal density functional is functionally differentiable with respect to the density, nor does this need to hold for its individual parts such as the exchange functional. Equation (
1
) would then be the direct consequence of a functionally differentiable
E_{\rm x}[\rho]
(see App. B of
Tancogne-Dejean et al. 2024
in the same special issue of this journal), but,
unfortunately, the universal density functional is everywhere discontinuous.
Lammert 2007

The principal aim of this paper to find a version of the exchange virial relation that holds without assuming functional differentiability.
We shall find that this is indeed possible using a limit procedure
and the main result, derived in Section
VII
, is

E_{\rm x}=-\lim_{\lambda\searrow 0}\int\rho(\mathbf{r})\,\mathbf{r}\cdot\nabla v_{\mathrm{xc}}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{r})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r},

(3)

as opposed to Eq. (
1
). Here,
v_{\mathrm{xc}}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{r})
is the corresponding exchange-correlation potential for interaction strength
\lambda
.
However, this procedure does not provide us with a well-defined local-exchange potential
v_{\rm x}(\mathbf{r})=\lim_{\lambda\searrow 0}v_{\mathrm{xc}}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{r})
, as one would perhaps expect. Indeed, a general local-exchange potential need not exist, a finding that agrees with the force-based approach to DFT, where an additional vector potential is introduced to fulfill the exchange-only virial relation.
Tancogne-Dejean et al. 2024
This also opens the possibility to a
non-local
limit potential.

We begin by giving the basic setting and some useful relations for the expectation values of the main components of the Hamiltonian. In atomic units, we consider a nonrelativistic system of
N
spinless particles in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, for which the Hamiltonian at coupling strength
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is given by

\hat{H}^{\lambda}[v]=\hat{T}+\lambda\hat{W}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}v(\mathbf{r}_{j}),

(4)

where
\hat{T}
and
\hat{W}
denote the kinetic and two-particle interaction operators, respectively:

\hat{T}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla_{\mathbf{r}_{j}}^{2},\quad\hat{W}=\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq N}\frac{1}{|\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{r}_{k}|}.

(5)

Both operators are positive definite.
We can bound the expectation value of
\hat{W}
in terms of
\hat{T}
, for instance, by using the Hardy-, and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequalities, we have

\trace\hat{W}\Gamma\leq C_{N}\sqrt{\trace\hat{T}\Gamma}\leq\frac{C_{N}}{2}(1+\trace\hat{T}\Gamma)

(6)

for some
N
-dependent constant
C_{N}>0
.

If
v(\mathbf{r})
is such that it admits a ground state with density
\rho(\mathbf{r})
then the full virial relation for such a system can be derived from an application of the Ehrenfest theorem for mixed states. For a ground state
\Gamma
it states that
\mathrm{i}\trace[\hat{H},\hat{A}]\Gamma=\partial_{t}\trace\hat{A}\Gamma=0
and we can apply this to the operator
\hat{A}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{r}_{j}\cdot\nabla_{j}
. This gives
Hirschfelder 1960
;
Theophilou et al. 2020

2\trace\hat{T}\Gamma+\lambda\trace\hat{W}\Gamma=\int\rho(\mathbf{r})\,\mathbf{r}\cdot\nabla v(\mathbf{r})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r},

(7)

where
\rho\mapsfrom\Gamma
is the ground-state density associated
with the mixed state
\Gamma
.
This expression forms the basis for our discussion of the virial relation in DFT.

II
Coupling-strength density functionals

Central to DFT is the universal density functional, which, for a given coupling strength
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and density
\rho
is defined as the constrained search over all density matrices that yield a given one-particle density
\rho
,

F(\lambda)=\inf_{\Gamma\mapsto\rho}\trace(\hat{T}+\lambda\hat{W})\Gamma.

(8)

Since the density
\rho
is kept fixed in our discussion, we omit it
as an argument to the functional. The only exception is when the scaled density
\rho_{\gamma}
rather than
\rho
is considered, always at
\lambda=1
, in which case we write
F[\rho_{\gamma}]
.
By Corollary 4.5 in
Lieb 1983
, for all ‘physical’,
N
-representable densities
\rho
(non-negative, normalized, and of finite kinetic energy), there exists a minimizing (possibly non-unique) density matrix
\Gamma(\lambda)
in Eq. (
8
) such that
\Gamma(\lambda)\mapsto\rho
and

F(\lambda)=\trace(\hat{T}+\lambda\hat{W})\Gamma(\lambda).

(9)

Although this corollary is formulated with
\lambda\geq 0
, it can easily be extended to all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
1
1

1

In the proof of Theorem 4.4 in
Lieb 1983
, we replace
h^{2}=\hat{T}+\lambda\hat{W}+1
by
h^{2}=\hat{T}+\lambda\hat{W}+1+M_{\lambda}
, where
-M_{\lambda}
is the lower bound of the combined operator
\hat{T}+\lambda\hat{W}
given by Kato–Rellich theorem
Reed and Simon 1975
.

Existence of a minimizer in Eq. (
8
) ensures that
F(\lambda)
is finite for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.

Since the minimizing density matrix
\Gamma(\lambda)\mapsto\rho
in Eq. (
9
) is in general not unique, it is
a priori
unclear how to decompose the function
\lambda\mapsto F(\lambda)
uniquely into a kinetic and an interaction contribution. For every choice of minimizing
\Gamma(\lambda)\mapsto\rho
, however, we
do
have the decomposition

F(\lambda)=T(\Gamma(\lambda))+\lambda W(\Gamma(\lambda))

(10)

with

\displaystyle T(\Gamma(\lambda))=\trace{\hat{T}\Gamma(\lambda)},

(11)

\displaystyle W(\Gamma(\lambda))=\trace{\hat{W}\Gamma(\lambda)}.

(12)

As we shall now show, this decomposition becomes unique under the assumption of
v
-representability of the density
\rho
at interaction strength
\lambda
. Hence, instead of
T(\Gamma(\lambda))
and
W(\Gamma(\lambda))
we may write
T(\lambda)
and
W(\lambda)
and so

F(\lambda)=T(\lambda)+\lambda W(\lambda)

(13)

holds.

To demonstrate uniqueness, we first note that
v
-representability for
\rho
means that we can find a potential
v^{\lambda}(\mathbf{r})
such that there is a ground state
\Gamma(\lambda)\mapsto\rho
for the Hamiltonian
H^{\lambda}[v^{\lambda}]
.
By the virial theorem, Eq. (
7
), we then have

2T(\Gamma(\lambda))+\lambda W(\Gamma(\lambda))=\int\rho(\mathbf{r})\,\mathbf{r}\cdot\nabla v^{\lambda}(\mathbf{r})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}.

(14)

Subtracting
F(\rho)
as given in Eq. (
9
) from both sides of this equation and using Eq. (
13
), we obtain

T(\Gamma(\lambda))=\int\rho(\mathbf{r})\,\mathbf{r}\cdot\nabla v^{\lambda}(\mathbf{r})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}-F(\lambda),

(15)

whose right-hand side is uniquely determined by the density, i.e., it is independent of the choice of
\Gamma(\lambda)
.
For
F(\lambda)
this is clear while for
\nabla v^{\lambda}(\mathbf{r})
it follows from the Hohenberg–Kohn theorem
Hohenberg and Kohn 1964
, which states that
v^{\lambda}(\mathbf{r})
is determined uniquely up to a scalar by the ground-state density. Hence
T(\lambda):=T(\Gamma(\lambda))
is well-defined (for all
\lambda)
and
W(\lambda):=W(\Gamma(\lambda))
is also well-defined (for
\lambda\neq 0
) via
\lambda W(\lambda)=F(\lambda)-T(\lambda)
implying that

the universal density functional has a unique decomposition into kinetic-, and interaction contributions at each interaction strength where the density is
v
-representable.

Another possibility to achieve well-defined functions
T(\lambda)
and
W(\lambda)
is discussed in Section
V
.
We will consider the case
W(0)
later.

In the pure-state formulation, the functionals are defined as expectation values with
\Psi(\lambda)\mapsto\rho
as the minimizer in the constrained-search functional. It is possible that this minimizing state is then uniquely given, and so the functionals are already defined unambiguously, but this does not seem to be guaranteed. Consequently, by the same argument as above, we rely on
pure-state

v^{\lambda}
-representability to make sure that
\langle\Psi(\lambda)|\hat{T}|\Psi(\lambda)\rangle
and
\langle\Psi(\lambda)|\hat{W}|\Psi(\lambda)\rangle
have values that only depend on
\rho
for
\lambda>0
. Only if
\Psi(0)
is determined uniquely – for example, if
\rho
is noninteracting pure-state
v
-representable by a nondegenerate ground state – can also
W(0)=\langle\Psi(0)|\hat{W}|\Psi(0)\rangle
be given, as it is the case in most presentations on the subject. Here, we aim to avoid such restrictions, only demanding
ensemble

v
-representability of
\rho
, defining
W(0)
by a limit procedure that is laid out in Section
IV
.

III
Basic properties of the adiabatic connection

Recall that by our assumption of
v^{\lambda}
-representability of the considered density
\rho
and following the discussion in Sec.
II
, the individual parts
T(\lambda)
and
W(\lambda)
of the universal density functional
F(\lambda)
are uniquely defined.
We proceed by collecting important properties of the function
F(\lambda)
that stem directly from its definition by Eq. (
8
). Firstly, we relate
F(\lambda)
with
\lambda=1/\gamma>0
to the scaled density as
Levy and Perdew 1994

\displaystyle\gamma^{2}F(1/\gamma)=

\displaystyle\inf_{\Gamma\mapsto\rho}\trace{(\gamma^{2}\hat{T}+\gamma\hat{W})\Gamma}

(16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{\Gamma\mapsto\rho}\trace{(\hat{T}+\hat{W})\Gamma_{\gamma}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{\Gamma_{\gamma}\mapsto\rho_{\gamma}}\trace{(\hat{T}+\hat{W})\Gamma_{\gamma}}=F[\rho_{\gamma}],

where
\Gamma_{\gamma}=\gamma^{3N}\Gamma(\gamma\cdot)
is the coordinate-scaled density matrix that yields precisely
\rho_{\gamma}
.

Importantly,
F(\lambda)
is concave, as follows easily from the superadditivity of the infimum – namely, for each
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
and
s\in[0,1]
, we have

\displaystyle F(s\lambda+(1-s)\lambda^{\prime})=\inf_{\Gamma\mapsto\rho}\trace(\hat{T}+(s\lambda+(1-s)\lambda^{\prime})\hat{W})\Gamma

(17)

\displaystyle\geq s\inf_{\Gamma\mapsto\rho}\trace(\hat{T}+\lambda\hat{W})\Gamma+(1-s)\inf_{\Gamma\mapsto\rho}\trace(\hat{T}+\lambda^{\prime}\hat{W})\Gamma

\displaystyle=sF(\lambda)+(1-s)F(\lambda^{\prime}).

Moreover, since the operator
\hat{W}
is positive definite, we have that
W(\lambda)
is strictly positive and thus
F(\lambda)
is strictly monotonically increasing.
Further, it holds that

\displaystyle F(\lambda^{\prime})

\displaystyle=T(\lambda^{\prime})+\lambda^{\prime}W(\lambda^{\prime})

(18)

\displaystyle\leq T(\lambda)+\lambda^{\prime}W(\lambda)=F(\lambda)+(\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda)W(\lambda),

where the inequality stems from the fact that on the right-hand side
T(\lambda)
and
W(\lambda)
are constructed from
\Gamma(\lambda)
, which in general is not a minimizer for
F(\lambda^{\prime})
. From

F(\lambda^{\prime})\leq F(\lambda)+(\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda)W(\lambda),

(19)

we see that
W(\lambda)
,
\lambda\neq 0
, is an element of the superdifferential of
F(\lambda)
. The individual elements of the set-valued superdifferential are called supergradients and they form tangents lying entirely on or above the respective concave function.
Any choice of supergradients for different
\lambda
must be monotonically decreasing since
F(\lambda)
is concave
Niculescu and Persson 2004
, so this also holds for
W(\lambda)
. Then, by a generalization of the fundamental theorem of integral calculus
Niculescu and Persson 2004
, we can write
F(\lambda)=F(0)+\int_{0}^{\lambda}f(\mu)\,\mathrm{d}\mu
for any choice of supergradient
f(\mu)
of
F(\mu)
. By choosing
f(\mu)=W(\mu)
we get, in particular,

F(\lambda)=F(0)+\int_{0}^{\lambda}W(\mu)\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

(20)

This result also follows as a direct consequence of the Hellmann–Feynman theorem
Perdew, Ernzerhof, and Burke 1996
if one considers pure-state
v^{\lambda}
-representable densities. But the Hellmann–Feynman theorem has to be at least handled with care in the case of degeneracies
Zhang and George 2002
;
Balawender, Holas, and March 2004
;
Fernández 2004
.

IV
Differential properties of the adiabatic connection

Note that in Eq. (
20
) the choice of the integrand is not limited to
W(\lambda)
, but it can be any supergradient of
F(\lambda)
.
A different choice would be the right-derivative

\partial^{+}F(\lambda)=\lim_{\mu\searrow 0}\frac{F(\lambda+\mu)-F(\lambda)}{\mu},

(21)

where the limit always exists as
F(\lambda)
is concave.
The superdifferential of a concave function is always given as the closed interval from the right-derivative to the left-derivative, so we have for
\lambda\neq 0

W(\lambda)\in[\partial^{+}F(\lambda),\partial^{-}F(\lambda)].

(22)

Next, we will show how the right-derivative, that will be substantial for our later definition of the exchange energy, relates to the interaction energy
W(\lambda)
.
From
F(\lambda)=T(\lambda)+\lambda W(\lambda)
and
F(0)=T(0)
we also have that

\frac{F(\lambda)-F(0)}{\lambda}-\frac{T(\lambda)-T(0)}{\lambda}=W(\lambda).

(23)

The limit
\lambda\searrow 0
is well-defined for the first term in Eq. (
23
) since it equals the right-derivative of
F(\lambda)
. Since
W(\lambda)
is monotone decreasing and finite as the subgradient of the concave and finite
F(\lambda)
, also here the limit exists and we
define

W(0)=\lim_{\lambda\searrow 0}W(\lambda)
. An alternative way to see that
W(\lambda)
is finite is through the bound of Eq. (
6
) that can be easily made into an estimate in terms of
F(\lambda)
. So far, we have defined
W(\lambda)
only for
\lambda\neq 0
, see Eq. (
12
) and the discussion related to it. The limit
\lambda\searrow 0
of Eq. (
23
) thus gives

\partial^{+}F(0)-\partial^{+}T(0)=W(0).

(24)

.

It is worth noting that for a
\rho
that is
v
-representable and has a certain regularity, the treatment in Sec. 4 of
Lammert 2007
shows that
F[\rho_{\gamma}]
is differentiable with respect to
\gamma
and thus also
F(\lambda)
for that
\rho
is differentiable with respect to
\lambda
for
\lambda>0
. This would be beneficial, since then the supergradient would always be unique for
\lambda>0
, but we will continue here without any such assumption.

In the case when
F(\lambda)
is differentiable on at least a small interval
(0,\lambda_{0})
, the supergradient is unique and it thus holds that
\partial^{+}F(\lambda)=W(\lambda)
for all
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{0})
since
W(\lambda)
was found to be a supergradient. Consequently, in this case it holds that
\lim_{\lambda\searrow 0}\partial^{+}F(\lambda)=\partial^{+}F(0)=W(0)
since the right-derivative is right-continuous (§1.8 in
van Tiel 1984
). If
F(\lambda)
is not differentiable, then as a concave function it only has a countable number of points
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i}
,
0<\lambda_{i+1}<\lambda_{i}<\varepsilon
, where it is non-differentiable (again §1.8 in
van Tiel 1984
). If this is a finite number of points then we will just be back to the previous case, since the smallest
\lambda_{i}
will then fill in the role for
\lambda_{0}
from before. So we are left with an infinite sequence of points
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i}
that has
\lim_{i\to\infty}\lambda_{i}=0
and where it holds (§1.6–7 in
van Tiel 1984
but for a concave function, and using that
F(\lambda)
is non-differentiable at
\lambda_{i}
and
\lambda_{i+1}
and thus right-derivative and left-derivative cannot agree)

\partial^{+}F(\lambda_{i})<\partial^{-}F(\lambda_{i})\leq\partial^{+}F(\lambda_{i+1})<\partial^{-}F(\lambda_{i+1}).

(25)

The
\partial^{+}F(\lambda_{i})
, as a convergent sequence with
\partial^{+}F(\lambda_{i})\to\partial^{+}F(0)<\infty
, must have
\partial^{+}F(\lambda_{i+1})-\partial^{+}F(\lambda_{i})\to 0
. But from Eq. (
25
) it then follows that also
\partial^{-}F(\lambda_{i})-\partial^{+}F(\lambda_{i})\to 0
, and so no matter how
W(\lambda_{i})
is chosen from the superdifferential of

at
\lambda=\lambda_{i}
(cf. Eq. (
22
)), in the limit only the possibility
W(0)=\lim_{i\to\infty}W(\lambda_{i})=\lim_{i\to\infty}\partial^{+}F(\lambda_{i})=\partial^{+}F(0)
remains. This is the main result in this section and helps to unambiguously define
W(0)
in the given setting. From Eq. (
24
) it then follows that

\partial^{+}T(0)=0

(26)

and the above derived expression

W(0)=\lim_{\lambda\searrow 0}W(\lambda)=\partial^{+}F(0)

(27)

Additional remarks on
T(\lambda)
and
W(\lambda)

In this section—which can be safely skipped at first reading as the rest of the paper is independent from it—we first want to mention an alternative route for the definition of the functions
T(\lambda)
and
W(\lambda)
that was suggested by the Anonymous Referee that avoids any assumption of
v
-representability.
Let
\mathfrak{G}(\lambda)
be the set of all minimizers
\Gamma(\lambda)\mapsto\rho
for a fixed
\rho
in Eq. (
8
). This set is non-empty by the result quoted before Eq. (
9
).
Then, for example, we can make the following choice in order to define

,

W(\lambda)=\inf_{\Gamma\in\mathfrak{G}(\lambda)}\trace{\hat{W}\Gamma}.

(28)

This then fixes

for all
\lambda\neq 0
by the relation of Eq. (

) as

T(\lambda)=\sup_{\Gamma\in\mathfrak{G}(\lambda)}\trace{\hat{T}\Gamma}.

(29)

If
\lambda=0
then

anyway.
Note that any other choice of the form

W(\lambda)\in\left[\inf_{\Gamma\in\mathfrak{G}(\lambda)}\trace{\hat{W}\Gamma},\sup_{\Gamma\in\mathfrak{G}(\lambda)}\trace{\hat{W}\Gamma}\right]

(30)

in the interval between minimum and maximum is possible and would again fix
T(\lambda)
.

A second remark concerns Remark 3.1 in
Lewin, Lieb, and Seiringer 2023
which states that not only
W(\lambda)
, but also the right-derivative and the left-derivative of
F(\lambda)
, and thus by convex combination all values in-between, come from
\trace\hat{W}\Gamma(\lambda)
with different minimizers
\Gamma(\lambda)
from Eq. (
8
). This is equivalent to saying that the interval in Eq. (
22
) agrees with the one from Eq. (
30
). Since for
v^{\lambda}
-representable densities
\trace\hat{W}\Gamma(\lambda)
has a fixed value for all minimizers, as was noted in Sec.

, this would imply that the right-derivative and left-derivative agree and thus
F(\lambda)
would be differentiable at
\lambda
.
Nevertheless, we have not been able to provide a proof for this statement at this point so we do not treat

as differentiable here.

VI
Zero-coupling limit and exchange energy

We now continue with our main discussion and first establish a relation between the interaction term and the exchange energy.
In the Kohn–Sham (KS) scheme an interacting system (
\lambda>0
) and a non-interacting system (
\lambda=0
) are related by demanding that their ground-state densities are equal. This defines the Hartree-exchange-correlation energy

\lambda E_{\mathrm{Hxc}}(\lambda)=F(\lambda)-F(0).

(31)

It is customary to separate out the Hartree mean-field term, defined as

E_{\rm H}=\frac{1}{2}\iint\frac{\rho(\mathbf{r})\rho(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime},

(32)

so that one remains with just the exchange-correlation energy

\lambda E_{\mathrm{xc}}(\lambda)=F(\lambda)-F(0)-\lambda E_{\rm H}.

(33)

Using the integral formula from Eq. (
20
), we readily obtain

\lambda E_{\mathrm{xc}}(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\lambda}W(\mu)\,\mathrm{d}\mu-\lambda E_{\rm H}.

(34)

By substituting
\lambda=1/\gamma>0
and by using the scaling relation of Eq. (
16
) we have

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{xc}}(1/\gamma)

\displaystyle=\frac{F(1/\gamma)-F(0)}{1/\gamma}-E_{\rm H}

(35)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(\gamma^{2}\left(F(1/\gamma)-F(0)\right)-\gamma E_{\rm H}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(F^{\lambda=1}[\rho_{\gamma}]-T^{\lambda=0}[\rho_{\gamma}]-E_{\rm H}[\rho_{\gamma}]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma}E_{\mathrm{xc}}[\rho_{\gamma}]

and thus in the limit using Eq. (
27
),

\displaystyle E_{\rm x}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\gamma\to\infty}\frac{1}{\gamma}E_{\mathrm{xc}}[\rho_{\gamma}]=\lim_{\lambda\searrow 0}E_{\mathrm{xc}}(\lambda)

(36)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\lambda\searrow 0}\frac{F(\lambda)-F(0)}{\lambda}-E_{\rm H}

\displaystyle=\partial^{+}F(0)-E_{\rm H}=W(0)-E_{\rm H}.

This coincides exactly with definition in
Levy and Perdew 1985
and beautifully relates the definition of the exchange energy as the high-density limit through coordinate scaling with its alternative definition as a zero-coupling limit. It further yields a third definition of the exchange energy as the right-derivative of the Lieb functional at zero coupling strength minus the Hartree energy. This also shows that if we split the exchange-correlation energy in the usual way,

E_{\mathrm{xc}}(\lambda)=E_{\rm x}+E_{\rm c}(\lambda),

(37)

then
E_{\rm c}(\lambda)
carries all
\lambda
dependence of
E_{\mathrm{xc}}(\lambda)
, or, said differently, the linear (in
\lambda
) contribution to
\lambda E_{\mathrm{xc}}(\lambda)
is exactly equal to
E_{\rm x}
and at zero coupling it holds
E_{\rm c}(0)=0
. This fact can be taken as a motivation to define
E_{\rm x}
by Eq. (
36
) instead.

Note that in the presented setting, the exchange-correlation potential for different coupling strengths
\lambda
can be defined through
(by assumption of
v^{\lambda}
-representability)

v^{0}(\mathbf{r})=v^{\lambda}(\mathbf{r})+\lambda v_{\mathrm{Hxc}}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{r})=v^{\lambda}(\mathbf{r})+\lambda v_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathbf{r})+\lambda v_{\mathrm{xc}}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{r}),

(38)

where

v_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathbf{r})=\int\frac{\rho(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}

(39)

is the usual Hartree potential. In analogy to Eq. (
36
) it is then tempting to define the local-exchange potential as the zero-coupling limit
v_{\mathrm{x}}(\mathbf{r})=\lim_{\lambda\searrow 0}v_{\mathrm{xc}}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{r})
(since the usual definition as the functional derivative of
E_{\rm x}
needs to be avoided) and introduce the split of the exchange-correlation potential
v_{\mathrm{xc}}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{r})=v_{\mathrm{x}}(\mathbf{r})+v_{\mathrm{c}}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{r})
. However, we cannot be sure about the existence of the zero-coupling limit for the xc potential. In the next section, we are only able to establish the limits for the energy expressions in the context of the virial relation.

VII
Exchange-only virial relation

In this section, we present our main result about the exchange-only virial relation (Eq. (
3
) from Sec.
I
, or Eq. (
45
) below).
By using the usual virial relation from Eq. (
7
) we arrive easily at the form given by
Levy and Perdew 1985
for
\lambda>0
and
\lambda=0
,

\displaystyle F(\lambda)+T(\lambda)=\int\rho(\mathbf{r})\,\mathbf{r}\cdot\nabla v^{\lambda}(\mathbf{r})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r},

(40)

\displaystyle F(0)+T(0)=\int\rho(\mathbf{r})\,\mathbf{r}\cdot\nabla v^{0}(\mathbf{r})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}.

(41)

We subtract those and divide by
\lambda\neq 0
to get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda}(F(\lambda)

\displaystyle-F(0))+\frac{1}{\lambda}(T(\lambda)-T(0))

(42)

\displaystyle=-\int\rho(\mathbf{r})\,\mathbf{r}\cdot\nabla v_{\mathrm{Hxc}}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{r})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}.

Here, we introduced the (
\lambda
- and
\rho
-dependent) Hartree-exchange-correlation potential
\lambda v_{\mathrm{Hxc}}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{r})=v^{0}(\mathbf{r})-v^{\lambda}(\mathbf{r})
, which of course relies on
v^{\lambda}
-representability again. In the limit
\lambda\searrow 0
, we then obtain that

\partial^{+}F(0)+\partial^{+}T(0)=-\lim_{\lambda\searrow 0}\int\rho(\mathbf{r})\,\mathbf{r}\cdot\nabla v_{\mathrm{Hxc}}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{r})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}.

(43)

Using Eq. (
36
), Eq. (
26
), and additionally the virial relation for the Hartree term that follows from direct computation,

E_{\rm H}=-\int\rho(\mathbf{r})\,\mathbf{r}\cdot\nabla v_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathbf{r})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r},

(44)

that we have with
v_{\mathrm{Hxc}}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{r})=v_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathbf{r})+v_{\mathrm{xc}}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{r})
the exchange-only virial relation

E_{\rm x}=-\lim_{\lambda\searrow 0}\int\rho(\mathbf{r})\,\mathbf{r}\cdot\nabla v_{\mathrm{xc}}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{r})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}.

(45)

It is interesting to contrast this with the result from the force-based treatment in
Tancogne-Dejean et al. 2024
, where the virial relation includes an additional transversal term next to the gradient of the force-based local exchange potential
v_{\rm fx}
,

E_{\rm x}=\int\rho(\mathbf{r})\,\mathbf{r}\cdot(-\nabla v_{\rm fx}(\mathbf{r})+\nabla\times\bm{\alpha}_{\rm fx}(\mathbf{r}))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}.

(46)

The necessary appearance of this additional term (within the integrand), which will be non-zero in all non-trivial situations, already makes it clear that we cannot expect
v_{\rm fx}(\mathbf{r})=\lim_{\lambda\searrow 0}v_{\mathrm{xc}}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{r})
.
We can, however, note that
\int\rho(\mathbf{r})\,\mathbf{r}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{\alpha}_{\rm fx}(\mathbf{r})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}=0
for spherically-symmetric systems
Tancogne-Dejean et al. 2024
, which implies for such systems an equality between
\lim_{\lambda\searrow 0}\int\rho(\mathbf{r})\,\mathbf{r}\cdot\nabla v_{\mathrm{xc}}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{r})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}
and
\int\rho(\mathbf{r})\,\mathbf{r}\cdot\nabla v_{\rm fx}(\mathbf{r})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}
.
Further results connecting to the force-based approach beyond this obvious observation is relegated to future work.

VIII
Summary and Discussion

The adiabatic connection relates the different energy contributions of a quantum many-body system in its ground state with variable coupling strength
\lambda
and fixed one-particle density
\rho
.
Central to our work is the assumption of
v^{\lambda}
-representability of
\rho
, which means that
\rho
is
v
-representable at each coupling strength
\lambda\geq 0
.
This allowed us assign unique values to the kinetic-energy function
T(\lambda)
and the interaction-energy function
W(\lambda)
, see Sec.
II
. Various convex-analytic properties of the adiabatic connection were studied in Sec.
III
and Sec.
IV
. We found that
W(\lambda)
is exactly a supergradient of
F(\lambda)
at
\lambda\neq 0
and if defined as
W(0)=\lim_{\lambda\searrow 0}W(\lambda)
at
\lambda=0
then
W(0)
is given by the right-derivative
\partial^{+}F(0)
of
F(\lambda)
at
\lambda=0
.
We then confirmed the usual relation
E_{\rm x}=W(0)-E_{\rm H}
, which now alternatively allows a definition of the exchange energy through the adiabatic connection as
\partial^{+}F(0)-E_{\rm H}=E_{\rm x}
.
The last equation connects the zero-coupling limit to the high-density limit from coordinate scaling, since
E_{\rm x}
is here defined through the latter.

In this context, it is natural to also ask for the virial relation (
1
) relating the exchange energy with an exchange potential and we examined this possibility in Sec.
VII
.
While the zero-coupling limits in Eq. (
43
) can indeed be guaranteed to exist, this is unlikely to be the case for the associated exchange-correlation potential
v_{\mathrm{xc}}^{\lambda}
without further assumptions.
Although one would usually like to set
v_{\mathrm{x}}=\lim_{\lambda\searrow 0}v_{\mathrm{xc}}^{\lambda}
,
only the limit of the corresponding virial integral in Eq. (
45
) is proven to exist to the exchange energy. This means that while we have a well-defined exchange energy
E_{\rm x}[\rho]
for a
v^{\lambda}
-representable density
\rho
from Eq. (
36
), there might not be a general well-defined local-exchange potential from a similar limit or a functional derivative. This realization is in line with the results from
Tancogne-Dejean et al. 2024
in the same special issue of this journal.
In that work, following a definition of the exchange contribution in terms of force densities, one needs an additional vector potential to fulfill the exchange-only virial relation (
1
). That the exchange-only virial relation does not hold in general can also be seen from numerical evidence
Tancogne-Dejean et al. 2024
. Of course, techniques like the optimized-effective potential (OEP) method
Sharp and Horton 1953
;
Talman and Shadwick 1976
;
Yang and Wu 2002
can still be used to get an approximate local-exchange potential.
