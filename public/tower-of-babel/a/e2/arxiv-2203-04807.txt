1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background and known results

In the present paper, we consider the Cauchy problem of the following family of 1D quasilinear wave equations parameterized by
\lambda
:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u_{tt}=c(u)^{2}u_{xx}+\lambda c(u)c^{\prime}(u)(u_{x})^{2},\ \ (t,x)\in(0,T]\times\mathbb{R},\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\ \ x\in\mathbb{R},\\
\partial_{t}u(0,x)=u_{1}(x),\ \ x\in\mathbb{R},\end{array}\right.

where
u(t,x)
is an unknown real-valued function, and
c^{\prime}(\theta)=dc(\theta)/d\theta
.
Assume that the function
c(\cdot)
is smooth in a neighborhood of
0
and satisfies the following:

(1.5)

\displaystyle c(0)=c_{0}>0\ \mbox{and}\ c^{\prime}(0)=c_{1}>0.

The parameterized nonlinear wave equation in (
1.1
) was introduced by Glassey, Hunter, and Zheng
[
15
]
(see also Chen and Shen
[
12
]
).
This equation can be rewritten as

\displaystyle u_{tt}-c^{2-\lambda}[c^{\lambda}u_{x}]_{x}=0,

which has different mathematical and physical backgrounds depending on
\lambda
.
If
\lambda=2
, then the parameterized nonlinear wave equation in (
1.1
) is formally equivalent to the following conservation system:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}U\\
V\\
\end{array}\right)-\partial_{x}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}V\\
p(U)\\
\end{array}\right)=0,

where
U(t,x)=u(t,x)
,
V(t,x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\partial_{t}u(t,y)dy
, and
p(\theta)=\int c(\theta)^{2}d\theta
.
This conservation system is referred to as a p-system and describes several phenomena of the wave propagation in nonlinear media, including the electromagnetic wave in a transmission line, shearing-motion in elastic-plastic rods and one-dimensional gas dynamics (see Ames and Lohner
[
2
]
, Cristecu
[
13
]
, Zabusky
[
34
]
and Landau and Lifshitz
[
24
]
).
Many authors has investigated the necessary and sufficient conditions for the occurrence of the blow-up solutions and their properties (e.g. Lax
[
23
]
, Klainerman and Majda
[
21
]
, Manfrin
[
26
]
, Zabusky
[
34
]
, Kong
[
22
]
, Chen
[
6
]
and Chen, Pan, and Zhu
[
9
]
).
In a study of the p-system, the following unknown invariant variables are important:

\displaystyle R=u_{t}+c(u)u_{x}

\displaystyle S=u_{t}-c(u)u_{x}.

Note that these variables are
x
-derivatives of the Riemann invariant.
Roughly speaking, it is known that solutions can blow up if
R(0,x)
or
S(0,x)
has a positive value.
Conversely, the negativity of
R(0,\cdot)
and
S(0,\cdot)
leads to the absence of singularities.

When
\lambda=1
, the equation in (
1.1
) is called the variational wave equation:

u_{tt}-c(u)[c(u)u_{x}]_{x}=0.

As its name suggests, the equation with
\lambda=1
has a variational structure.
Namely, this equation is derived by the least action principle

\frac{\delta}{\delta u}\int\{u_{t}^{2}-c^{2}(u)u_{x}^{2}\}dxdt=0.

The variational wave equation has physical backgrounds, including nematic liquid crystals and long waves on a dipole chain in the continuum limit (see
[
15
]
).
Zhang and Zheng
[
35
]
showed the existence of the global classical solution under the assumption that
R(0,x),S(0,x)\leq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
Glassey, Hunter, and Zheng
[
14
,
15
]
showed that solutions can blow up in finite time, if this assumption is not satisfied.
A number of papers have examined the global existence of weak solutions to variational wave equations without the assumption that
R(0,x),S(0,x)\leq 0
(e.g. Bressan and Zheng
[
3
]
and Zhang and Zheng
[
35
,
36
,
37
]
).
In the results for the construction of the blow-up solution and the existence of the global weak solution, the following energy conservation law is essentially used:

E(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{t}^{2}+c^{2}(u)u_{x}^{2}dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{1}^{2}+c(u_{0})^{2}{u_{0}}_{x}^{2}dx.

When
\lambda=0
, the equation in (
1.1
) describes the second sound wave of entropy in superfluids (e.g. Landau and Lifshitz
[
24
]
). This equation is the one-dimensional version of

\partial^{2}_{t}u=c(u)^{2}\Delta u,\ \ (t,x)\in(0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{3},

which was studied by Lindbald
[
27
]
.
In
[
27
]
, Lindblad showed that solutions exist globally in time with small initial data.
If
c(\cdot)
is uniformly positive, then it seems possible that (
1.1
) has a global smooth solution for any smooth initial data, although a complete proof or a counterexample for this problem is also open.

When
0\leq\lambda\leq 2
, the author in
[
29
]
has shown the existence of a global classical solution under the assumptions of the non-positivity of
R(0,x)
,
S(0,x)
and the uniform positivity of
c(\cdot)
.
It remains open as to whether a global weak solution exists without the non-positivity of
R(0,x)
and
S(0,x)
, except for the case that
\lambda=1
.
Chen and Shen
[
12
]
presented numerical results that indicate the existence of a global weak solution.
They also expected that the global weak solution is Hölder continuous with the Hölder exponent
1-\lambda/2
.
This expectation was solved by Bressan and Zheng
[
3
]
, only in the case with
\lambda=1
.

In the present paper, we treat the blow-up problem in the case of
0<\lambda\leq 1
.
More precisely, we extend the blow-up result of Glassey, Hunter, and Zheng
[
14
]
to the case with
0<\lambda\leq 1
.
Moreover, we show that the blow-up solution is Hölder continuous with the Hölder exponent
1-\lambda/2
.

1.2.
Assumptions on initial data

Before stating the main theorem of the present paper, we illustrate the assumption on the initial data and the notation of the lifespan.
In the main theorem, initial data are chosen as follows:

(1.7)

\displaystyle u_{1}(x)=-c(u_{0}(x))\partial_{x}u_{0}(x)\ \ \mbox{and}\ \ u_{0}(x)=\varepsilon\phi(x/\varepsilon)

where
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\partial_{x}\phi(0)<0
and
\varepsilon>0
.
Note that
R(0,x)=0
and
S(0,x)=-2c(\varepsilon\phi(x/\varepsilon))\phi^{\prime}(x/\varepsilon)
for these initial data.
From the standard local-existence theorem for quasilinear wave equations (e.g. Hughes, Kato, and Marsden
[
16
]
, Kato
[
20
]
and Majda
[
25
]
),
the lifespan
T^{*}
of the solution is defined for suitable initial data as

(1.8)

T^{*}=\sup\{T>0\ |\ \sup_{[0,T]}\|\partial_{x}u(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\partial_{t}u(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}<\infty\ \mbox{and}\ \inf_{[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}}c(u(t,x))>0\}.

This choice of initial data is same as that of Glassey, Hunter, and Zheng
[
14
]
.

1.3.
Main theorems

We are now in a position to state the first main theorem of the present paper.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
0<\lambda\leq 1
and
\varepsilon>0
.
Suppose that initial data
u_{0},u_{1}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies the conditions of (
1.7
).
Then, there exists a positive number
\varepsilon_{0}
such that
if
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, then
T^{*}
is bounded independently
\varepsilon
. In particular, the gradient blow-up occurs in finite time.
Namely, it holds that

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\rightarrow T^{*}}\|u_{t}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|u_{x}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}=\infty.

Furthermore, the solution satisfies the following:

(1.9)

\displaystyle\sup_{[0,T^{*}]\times\mathbb{R}}|u_{t}+c(u)u_{x}|\leq C_{1}\varepsilon^{\lambda},

(1.10)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|u_{t}|^{2/\lambda}+|u_{x}|^{2/\lambda}(t,x)dx\leq C_{2}\varepsilon

(1.11)

\displaystyle\frac{{c_{0}}}{2}\leq c(u(t,x))\leq 2{c_{0}},

where the constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
do not depend on
\varepsilon
.

The second main theorem asserts the more precise nature of the blow-up solution constructed in Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 1.2
.

The blow-up solution constructed in Theorem
1.1
satisfies the following:

(1.12)

\displaystyle|u(t,x)-u(s,y)|\leq C(|t-s|^{1-\frac{\lambda}{2}}+|x-y|^{1-\frac{\lambda}{2}})

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
and
t,s\in[0,T^{*})
.
Furthermore, there exists the limit
u(T^{*},x)=\lim_{t\rightarrow T^{*}}u(t,x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
the solution satisfies that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}

(1.13)

|u(T^{*},x)-u(T^{*},y)|\leq C|x-y|^{1-\frac{\lambda}{2}}.

Remark 1.3
.

The assertion on the gradient blow-up in Theorem
1.1
can be replaced by

(1.14)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow T^{*}}\|u_{t}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|u_{x}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}=\infty.

In other words,
\limsup
can be improved by
\lim
, which means that
\|u_{t}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|u_{x}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}
does not oscillate near
T^{*}
.
Furthermore, near the blow-up point
(T^{*},x^{*})
, the solution satisfies the following:

(1.15)

\displaystyle u_{t}(t,x)\rightarrow\infty\ \mbox{and}\ u_{x}(t,x)\rightarrow-\infty.

(
1.15
) is shown by (
1.9
) and the fact that
S(t,x)
grows up near blow-up point
(T^{*},x^{*})
.

1.4.
Strategy and ideas of the proof

The main theorem of the present paper is an analogy of the result of Glassey, Hunter, and Zheng
[
14
]
.
However, the conservation law of the energy plays an important role in their proof.
From the equation in (
1.1
), the variables
R
and
S
satisfy the following:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}R-c(u)\partial_{x}R=\dfrac{c^{\prime}(u)}{4c(u)}(\lambda R^{2}+2(1-\lambda)RS-(2-\lambda)S^{2}),\\
\partial_{t}S+c(u)\partial_{x}S=\dfrac{c^{\prime}(u)}{4c(u)}(\lambda S^{2}+2(1-\lambda)RS-(2-\lambda)R^{2}).\end{array}\right.

In the equation for
S
, the term
-(2-\lambda)R^{2}
may prevent
S
from blowing up.
Hence, we desire the estimate ensuring the smallness of
\|R\|_{L^{\infty}}
, which enables us to reduce the
equation for
S
to a Riccati-type differential equation.
Broadly speaking, the authors in
[
14
]
derive the estimate of
\|R\|_{L^{\infty}}
from the energy conservation law.
Specifically, they use another energy estimate on the characteristic curves.
In (
1.7
), the choice of initial data enhances the growth of
S
and makes
|R|
smaller as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.
Thus, we can obtain upper and lower estimates of the blow-up time of solutions to the Riccati differential equation, independent of
\varepsilon
.
Since the energy is not conserved in the case that
\lambda\not=1
, we show a
L^{2/\lambda}
inequality as a substitute for the energy via the bootstrap argument.
To obtain the
L^{2/\lambda}
inequality, we use the following identity

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2}(\partial_{t}(|R|^{2/\lambda}+|S|^{2/\lambda})-\partial_{x}(c(u)(|R|^{2/\lambda}-|S|^{2/\lambda})))

(1.19)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\lambda}{4}\right)\frac{c^{\prime}(u)}{c(u)}RS(R-S)(|R|^{2(1-\lambda)/\lambda}-|S|^{2(1-\lambda)/\lambda}).

We remark that the right-hand side of this identity vanishes if
\lambda=1
, which implies the energy conservation law.
Under the bootstrap argument ensuring the smallness
\|R\|_{L^{\infty}}
and the boundedness of
\|c^{\prime}/c\|_{L^{\infty}}
, we can find that the above identity yields the
smallness of
\|R\|^{2/\lambda}_{L^{2/\lambda}}+\|S\|^{2/\lambda}_{L^{2/\lambda}}
from the Gronwall inequality (see Lemma
2.2
),
since the right-hand side of the identity is estimated as
C|R|(|R|^{2/\lambda}+|S|^{2/\lambda})
.

The Hölder regularity with
\lambda=1
is established for the time-global weak solution proposed by Bressan and Zheng
[
3
]
by the method of
energy coordinate. Our proof of Theorem
1.2
is somewhat simple, as we only use the
L^{2/\lambda}
inequality for
R
and
S
, although the theorem treats only our blow-up solutions.
The Hölder estimates in Theorem
1.2
are expected to hold for global weak solutions (if they exist).
The proof of Theorem
1.2
is valid with global weak solutions for which unknown variable
R
or
S
is bounded.

For the case that
\lambda=2
, the negative quadratic term does not appear in (
1.4
).
Therefore, the condition on the initial data is much weaker than that for the case in which
\lambda=1
(e.g.,
[
9
]
).
Difficulties in dealing with this case are focused in the control of
u
(see Subsection 5.2).

The remainder of the present paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we recall some formulas on the characteristic curves and properties of solutions of (
1.1
).
The proofs of Theorem
1.1
in Section 3 and Theorem
1.2
are presented in Sections 4 and 5, respectively.
Concluding remarks are given in Section 5.

Notation

We denote the Lebesgue space for
1\leq p\leq\infty
and the
L^{2}
Sobolev space with the order
m\in\mathbb{N}
on
\mathbb{R}
by
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
and
H^{m}(\mathbb{R})
, respectively.
For a Banach space
X
,
C^{j}([0,T];X)

2.
Preliminary

2.1.
Basic formulation for unknown variables
R
and
S

We set
R(t,x)
and
S(t,x)
as follows:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}R=\partial_{t}u+c(u)\partial_{x}u,\\
S=\partial_{t}u-c(u)\partial_{x}u.\end{array}\right.

The functions
R
and
S
were used in Glassey, Hunter, and Zheng
[
14
,
15
]
and Zhang and Zheng
[
35
]
.
We recall some properties of
R
and
S
proven in
[
29
]
.

By (
1.1
),
R
and
S
are solutions to the system of the following first-order equations:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}R-c(u)\partial_{x}R=\dfrac{c^{\prime}(u)}{2c(u)}(RS-S^{2})+\lambda\dfrac{c^{\prime}(u)}{4c(u)}(R-S)^{2},\\
\partial_{x}u=\dfrac{1}{2c(u)}(R-S),\\
\partial_{t}S+c(u)\partial_{x}S=\dfrac{c^{\prime}(u)}{2c(u)}(SR-R^{2})+\lambda\dfrac{c^{\prime}(u)}{4c(u)}(S-R)^{2}.\end{array}\right.

Let
x_{\pm}(t)
be the characteristic curves of the first and third equations of (
2.1
). In other words,
x_{\pm}(t)
are solutions to the following differential equations:

(2.8)

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dt}x_{\pm}(t)=\pm c(u(t,x_{\pm}(t))).

Whereas the characteristic curve with spatial variable
t_{\pm}(x)
is defined by solutions to the differential equations, as follows:

(2.9)

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dx}t_{\pm}(x)=\pm\dfrac{1}{c(u(t_{\pm}(x),x))}.

Based on their definitions,
R
and
S
can be expressed on the characteristic curves
x_{\pm}(t)
by

\displaystyle R(t,x_{+}(t))=\dfrac{d}{dt}u(t,x_{+}(t)),

\displaystyle S(t,x_{-}(t))=\dfrac{d}{dt}u(t,x_{-}(t)).

Next, we rewrite (
2.1
) on the characteristic curves using this equality.
Multiplying
c^{(\lambda-1)/2}=(c(u))^{(\lambda-1)/2}
and using the method of characteristics, the first equation of (
2.1
) is reduced to

(2.10)

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(c^{(\lambda-1)/2}R(t,x_{-}(t))\right)=\dfrac{c^{\prime}c^{(\lambda-3)/2}}{4}\left(\lambda R^{2}-(2-\lambda)S^{2}\right).

Similarly, it holds for
S
that

(2.11)

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(c^{(\lambda-1)/2}S(t,x_{+}(t))\right)=\dfrac{c^{\prime}c^{(\lambda-3)/2}}{4}\left(\lambda S^{2}-(2-\lambda)R^{2}\right).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
0\leq\lambda\leq 2
,
c(u)>0
, and
c^{\prime}(u)\geq 0
for the
C^{1}
-solution
u
of (
1.1
) on
[0,T^{*})
. Suppose that
R(0,x)\leq 0
(
S(0,x)\leq 0
). Then, it holds that

(2.12)

\displaystyle R(t,x)\leq 0\ (S(t,x)\leq 0\ \mbox{resp.})\ \mbox{for}\ (t,x)\in[0,T^{*})\times\mathbb{R},

where
R
and
S
are the functions in
(\ref{ri})
.

Proof.

The proof is the same as in the case for which
\lambda=1
and
\lambda=2
. We give a slightly simple proof here for the self-containedness of the present paper.
We only show that
R(0,x)\leq 0
implies that
R(t,x)\leq 0
.
We set
\tilde{R}=c^{(\lambda-1)/2}R
. From (
2.10
) and the assumption that
\lambda\in[0,2]
, it follows that

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dt}\tilde{R}(t,x_{-}(t))\leq\dfrac{c^{\prime}c^{-(\lambda+1)/2}}{4}\tilde{R}^{2}.

Solving this differential inequality, we have
\tilde{R}(t,x)\leq 0
.
∎

2.2.
Key inequality

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
0<\lambda\leq 1
.
Suppose that
\sup_{[0,T^{*})}\|R(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}+\sup_{[0,T^{*})}\|\frac{c^{\prime}}{c}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}<\infty
. Then, it holds that for
C^{1}
-solution of (
1.1
)

(2.13)

\displaystyle\|R(t)\|^{\frac{2}{\lambda}}_{L^{2/\lambda}}+\|S(t)\|^{\frac{2}{\lambda}}_{L^{2/\lambda}}\leq(\|R(0)\|^{\frac{2}{\lambda}}_{L^{2/\lambda}}+\|S(0)\|^{\frac{2}{\lambda}}_{L^{2/\lambda}})e^{C^{*}t},

where
t\in[0,T^{*})
and
C^{*}=\frac{2}{\lambda}\left(2-\lambda\right)\sup_{[0,T^{*})}\|R(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\sup_{[0,T^{*})}\|\frac{c^{\prime}}{c}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}
.

Proof.

We denote
p=2/\lambda
and note that
p\geq 2
for
0<\lambda\leq 2
.
Multiplying both sides of the first equation in (
2.1
) by
|R|^{p-2}R
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\{\partial_{t}|R|^{p}-c\partial_{x}|R|^{p}\}=

\displaystyle\dfrac{c^{\prime}(u)}{2c(u)}(RS-S^{2})|R|^{p-2}R

(2.14)

\displaystyle+\lambda\dfrac{c^{\prime}(u)}{4c(u)}(R-S)^{2}|R|^{p-2}R.

From the equation
\displaystyle\partial_{x}u=\frac{1}{2c}(R-S)
, we have

(2.15)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}c(u)\partial_{x}|R|^{p}=\frac{1}{p}\partial_{x}(c(u)|R|^{p})-\frac{1}{p}\frac{c^{\prime}}{2c}(R-S)|R|^{p},

from which,
(\ref{zz2es1})
yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\{\partial_{t}|R|^{p}-\partial_{x}(c(u)|R|^{p})\}=

\displaystyle-\frac{c^{\prime}(u)}{2pc(u)}(R-S)|R|^{p}

\displaystyle+\frac{c^{\prime}(u)}{2c(u)}(RS-S^{2})|R|^{p-2}R

(2.16)

\displaystyle+\lambda\dfrac{c^{\prime}(u)}{4c(u)}(R-S)^{2}|R|^{p-2}R.

By a similar computation, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\{\partial_{t}|S|^{p}+\partial_{x}(c(u)|S|^{p})\}=

\displaystyle-\frac{c^{\prime}(u)}{2pc(u)}(S-R)|S|^{p}

\displaystyle+\frac{c^{\prime}(u)}{2c(u)}(SR-R^{2})|S|^{p-2}S

(2.17)

\displaystyle+\lambda\dfrac{c^{\prime}(u)}{4c(u)}(S-R)^{2}|S|^{p-2}S.

Summing (
2.2
) and (
2.2
)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}(\partial_{t}(|R|^{p}+|S|^{p})-\partial_{x}(c(u)(|R|^{p}-|S|^{p})))=

\displaystyle\frac{c^{\prime}(u)}{c(u)}\left(-\frac{1}{2p}(R-S)(|R|^{p}-|S|^{p})\right.

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}RS(R-S)(|R|^{p-2}-|S|^{p-2})

\displaystyle+\left.\frac{\lambda}{4}(R-S)^{2}(|R|^{p-2}R+|S|^{p-2}S)\right)

and adopting the following fundamental identity under the assumption that
p=\frac{2}{\lambda}

\displaystyle(R-S)(|R|^{p}-|S|^{p})-(R-S)^{2}(|R|^{p-2}R+|S|^{p-2}S)

\displaystyle=RS(R-S)(|R|^{p-2}-|S|^{p-2}),

we obtain the identity

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}(\partial_{t}(|R|^{p}+|S|^{p})-\partial_{x}(c(u)(|R|^{p}-|S|^{p})))

(2.18)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\lambda}{4}\right)\frac{c^{\prime}(u)}{c(u)}RS(R-S)(|R|^{p-2}-|S|^{p-2}).

Integrating this identity over
\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|R|^{p}+|S|^{p}dx=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\lambda}{4}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{c^{\prime}(u)}{c(u)}RS(R-S)(|R|^{p-2}-|S|^{p-2})dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(2-\lambda\right)\sup_{[0,T^{*})}\|R(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\sup_{[0,T^{*})}\|\frac{c^{\prime}}{c}\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|R|^{p}+|S|^{p}dx,

where we used the fundamental inequality that
|S|(R-S)(|R|^{p-2}-|S|^{p-2})\leq 4(|R|^{p}+|S|^{p})
.
Integrating both sides of the above inequality over
[0,t]
and applying the Gronwall inequality, we complete the proof of Lemma
2.2
.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

The proof of Lemma (
2.2
) does not work for the case in which
\lambda\in(1,2]
, because
|S|(R-S)(|R|^{p-2}-|S|^{p-2})\leq 4(|R|^{p}+|S|^{p})
does not hold if
p<2
. Even if the boundedness of
\|R(t)\|_{L^{2/\lambda}}
is obtained, some
L^{r}
estimates with
r\geq 2
for
R
are still desired in order to obtain the boundedness of
\|R(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is divided in two steps.
First, we show several a priori bounds of
u
,
R
, and
\|R(t)\|^{2/\lambda}_{L^{2/\lambda}}+\|S(t)\|^{2/\lambda}_{L^{2/\lambda}}
with small
\varepsilon
via the bootstrap argument in order to control
R^{2}
,
c
, and
c^{\prime}
in (
2.11
).
In particular, we prove these estimates hold until a fixed finite time
T_{b}
, if
\varepsilon

3.1.
Some estimates via the bootstrap argument

We fix

Using the bootstrap argument, we show the following assertion: there exists a positive number

and

(3.6)

and

Here, we take

Applying the above two estimates to (

\displaystyle\leq(2^{4/\lambda}{c_{0}}^{2/\lambda}\|\phi_{x}\|^{p}_{L^{p}}+1)\varepsilon.

Next, we prove (

\displaystyle 0\geq R(t,x)\geq

\displaystyle-c^{-(\lambda-1)/2}\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{(2-\lambda)c^{\prime}c^{(\lambda-3)/2}}{4}S^{2}(s,x_{-}(s))ds

3.2.
Formation of the singularity via the ODE argument

This part is similar to the argument in Glassey, Hunter, and Zheng
[

We present in detail for the self-containedness of the present paper.
We set

\displaystyle T_{b}=\dfrac{-2^{(3-\lambda)/2}}{C^{*}_{3}{c_{0}}^{(\lambda+1)/2}\varphi_{x}(0)}.

We suppose that

.
The estimates (

Setting

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dt}s(t)=

\displaystyle c^{\prime}\left(\lambda\dfrac{c^{-(\lambda+1)/2}}{4}s^{2}-(2-\lambda)\dfrac{c^{(\lambda-3)/2}}{4}R^{2}(t,x_{+}(t))\right).

We take the characteristic curve as

.
Applying (

), (

), and (

), we have the differential inequality on

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dt}s(t)\geq

\displaystyle C^{*}_{3}s^{2}-C^{*}_{4}T_{b}^{2(1-\lambda)}\varepsilon^{2\lambda},

where the constants
C^{*}_{3}
and
C^{*}_{4}
are defined as

(3.12)

\displaystyle C^{*}_{3}=\lambda{c_{1}}{c_{0}}^{-(\lambda+1)/2}

\displaystyle C^{*}_{4}=2(2-\lambda){c_{1}}{c_{0}}^{(\lambda-3)/2}(C^{*}_{1})^{2}.

Setting

and

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\dfrac{d}{dt}\bar{S}(t)=a^{2}\bar{S}^{2}-b^{2}T_{b}^{2(1-\lambda)}\varepsilon^{2\lambda},\\

\displaystyle S(t)=\dfrac{(1+k(t))b\varepsilon^{\lambda}}{(1-k(t))a},

Note that

5.
Concluding remarks

5.1.
Generalized Carlemann model

Here, we present remarks on the following generalized Carlemann model:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}R-\partial_{x}R=a_{1}R^{2}+b_{1}RS+c_{1}S^{2},\\
\partial_{t}S+\partial_{x}S=a_{2}S^{2}+b_{2}RS+c_{2}R^{2}.\end{array}\right.

The original Carlemann model, appearing in the discrete kinetic theory, is the case that
a_{1}=a_{2}=1
,
b_{1}=b_{2}
, and
c_{1}=c_{2}=-1
.
The blow-up existence and the blow-up problems of this system have been studied by many authors (e.g. Tartar
[
32
]
, Balabane
[
4
]
, Aregba-Driollet and Hanouzet
[
1
]
, Rauch
[
28
]
and Bianchini and Staffilani
[
5
]
).
Blow-up solutions can be constructed if
a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}\not=0
(e.g. Aregba-Driollet and Hanouzet
[
1
]
and Rauch
[
28
]
).
However, in the blow-up results, their method relies heavily on the discontinuity of solutions and initial data. (Note that this model is locally well posed for
L^{\infty}
initial data). To the best of our knowledge, it would be still unknown as to whether there exists a blow-up solution with smooth initial data, even for the original Carlemann model.

5.2.
Blow-up results for the case in which
\lambda=2

As reviewed in the introduction, when
\lambda=2
, necessary and sufficient conditions have been established by several authors.
For simplicity, we only consider the case with
c(u)=(1+u)^{a}
, which appears in the modeling of the isentropic fluid if
a\leq-1
and in the modeling of the elastic or plastic materials if
a>0
(see
[
13
]
).
In this case, the following variables (called Riemann invariants) are very useful to obtain point-wise estimates of
u

\displaystyle w_{+}(t,x)=\frac{(1+u)^{a+1}}{a+1}+\int_{-\infty}^{x}u_{t}dx

\displaystyle w_{-}(t,x)=\frac{(1+u)^{a+1}}{a+1}-\int_{-\infty}^{x}u_{t}dx,

since
w_{\pm}
are invariant on the minus and plus characteristic curves (
w_{\pm}(t,x_{\mp}(t))=w_{\pm}(0,x_{\mp}(0))
), respectively.
From this invariance property, one can easily obtain

\displaystyle\frac{2(1+u(t,x))^{a+1}}{a+1}=

\displaystyle w_{+}(t,x)-w_{-}(t,x)=w_{+}(0,x_{-}(0))-w_{-}(0,x_{0}(0))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1+u_{0}(x_{-}(0)))^{a+1}}{a+1}-\frac{(1+u_{0}(x_{+}(0)))^{a+1}}{a+1}

(5.4)

\displaystyle+\int_{x+(0)}^{x_{-}(0)}u_{1}(x)dx,

which implies a uniform lower estimate of
u
if
a<-1
. We note that the above identity is the d’ Alembert’s formula for the one-dimensional linear wave equation. Using this lower estimate, we can show that the positivity of
R(0,\cdot)
and
S(0,\cdot)
is equivalent to the global existence with
a\leq-2
(e.g., Lax
[
23
]
).
While, when
-2<a<-1
, Chen, Pan, and Zhu
[
9
]
established new upper estimates of
u
and proved that the same equivalence holds (see also
[
7
]
and
[
8
]
).

5.3.
Results on the degeneracy

When
c(u(t,x))
approaches
0
, the equation becomes non-strictly hyperbolic, which would not satisfy the persistence of regularity in general. This phenomena, which is referred to as degeneracy, is a breakdown of solutions and has been investigated in a previous paper
[
29
,
30
,
31
]

The author has studied the sufficient condition for the occurrence of the degeneracy.
We restrict
c(u)=(1+u)^{a}
with
a\in\mathbb{R}
.
If it is assumed that
a>0
,
2/a+1>-2\int_{-1}^{0}c(\theta)d\theta
, and the negativity of
R(0,x)
and
S(0,x)
holds, then (
1.1
) with
\lambda=2
has
a global smooth solution such that the equation does not degenerate (e.g. Johnson
[
18
]
and Yamaguchi and Nishida
[
33
]
).
In fact, using (
5.4
) and the negativity of
R(0,x)
and
S(0,x)
(which means the decreasing property of
w
and
z
) and assuming
\lim_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}u_{0}(x)=0
, we can obtain

\displaystyle\frac{2(1+u(t,x))^{a+1}}{a+1}\geq\frac{2}{a+1}-\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{1}dx,

which implies the positivity of
1+u
(non-degeneracy) for
a>-1
, if
2/a+1>-2\int_{-1}^{0}c(\theta)d\theta
.
On the other hand, in
[
30
]
, the author has shown that degeneracy occurs in finite time, if
\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{1}(x)dx<-2/a+1>-2\int_{-1}^{0}c(\theta)d\theta
,
a>0
, and
R(0,x),S(0,x)\leq 0
.
Namely, these results indicate that
-2\int_{-1}^{0}c(\theta)d\theta
is a threshold of
\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{1}(x)dx
separating the global existence of solutions (such that the equation does not degenerate) and the degeneracy of the equation under the assumption that
R(0,x)
and
S(0,x)
are negative.
If
R(0,x)
or
S(0,x)
has a positive value and
a>0
, then solutions can blow up in finite time, as mentioned in the introduction.
It seems still open as to whether the function
c(u)=(1+u(t,x))^{a}
diverges when gradient blow-up occurs for
-1<a<0
.
For the case in which
a>0
and
0\leq\lambda<2
, it is proven in
[
31
]
that the degeneracy in finite time of the equation in (
1.1
) can occur regardless of
\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{1}(x)dx
(see also
[
19
]
). In other words, the degeneracy occurs in finite time, if
R(0,x),S(0,x)\leq 0
with non-trivial data.

5.4.
Blow-up results for the multi-dimensional case with radially symmetric initial data

Duan, Hu, and Wang
[
17
]
considered the multi-dimensional variational wave equation:

\displaystyle u_{tt}=c(u)\nabla\cdot(c(u)\nabla u)

and extended the one-dimensional blow-up result of
[
14
]
to the multi-dimensional case with radially symmetric initial data.
The key idea for the proof is to introduce new variables
\bar{R}=r^{(n-1)/2}(u_{t}-c(u)u_{x})
and
\bar{R}=r^{(n-1)/2}(u_{t}-c(u)u_{x})
with
r=|r|
and to use the energy inequality
E(t):=\int_{0}^{\infty}R^{2}(t,r)+S^{2}(t,r)dr=E(0)
. Using this concept presented, we can extend Theorem
1.1
to the multi-dimensional case.
Cai, Chen, and Wang
[
10
]
investigated the multi-dimensional hyperbolic conservation law (the compressible Euler equation) and showed that the gradient blow-up occurs in finite time with radially symmetric initial data.

5.5.
Traveling wave solution

Glassey, Hunter, and Zheng
[
14
]
investigated the traveling wave solution of (
1.1
) with
\lambda=1
and
c(u)=\alpha\cos^{2}u+\beta\sin^{2}u
. Namely, they looked for solutions of the form

u(t,x)=\psi(x-st),

where
s
is a constant. They constructed the unbounded and continuous traveling wave solution and showed that the solution has isolated singularities at
x-st=\xi_{0}+2\pi n
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\xi\in[0,2\pi]
.
Furthermore, they proved that the traveling wave solution is Hölder continuous at
\xi_{0}+2\pi n
and that their Hölder exponent is
2/3
if
c^{\prime}(\xi)\not=0
, and is
1/2
if
c^{\prime}(\xi)\not=0
, which is different from our Hölder estimate in Theorem
1.2
. We believe that this difference is caused by the non-smoothness of the traveling solution at initial time
t=0
and/or its unboundedness and the Hölder estimate in Theorem
1.2
as indicated by numerical experiments by Chen and Shen
[
12
]
.
