Intrinsic Lipschitz sections of no-linear quotient maps
Daniela Di Donato

Abstract
. Le Donne and the author introduced the so-called intrinsically Lipschitz sections of a fixed quotient map π \pi in the context of metric spaces. Moreover, the author introduced the concept of intrinsic Cheeger energy when the quotient map is also linear. In this note we investigate about the no-linearity of π \pi . In particular, we find a Leibniz formula for the intrinsic slope when π \pi satisfies a weaker condition. After that, we focus our attention on Carnot groups and using the properties of intrinsic dilations we show that the dilation of a Lipschitz section is so too. Finally, in Carnot groups of step 2, we give a suitable additional condition in order to get the sum of two intrinsically Lipschitz sections is so too.



1.
Introduction

Find out a good notion of rectifiability in subRiemannian Carnot groups
[
ABB19
,
BLU07
,
CDPT07
]
is unanswered questions which have continued to perplex many mathematicians for decades. Starting from a negative result by Ambrosio and Kirchheim
[
AK00
]
, Franchi, Serapioni and Serra Cassano introduced the notion of intrinsically Lipschitz maps in
[
FSSC01
,
FSSC03b
,
FSSC03a
]
(see also
[
SC16
,
FS16
]
) in order to answer this question. Recently, Le Donne and the author
[
DDLD22
]
generalize this concept in metric spaces and prove some relevant properties like Ahlfors-David regularity, Ascoli-Arzelá Theorem, Extension theorem, etc. for the so-called intrinsically Lipschitz sections.

Our setting is the following. We have a metric space
X
, a topological space
Y
, and a
quotient map
\pi:X\to Y
, meaning
continuous, open, and surjective.
The standard example for us is when
X
is a metric Lie group
G
(meaning that the Lie group
G
is equipped with a left-invariant distance that induces the manifold topology), for example a subRiemannian Carnot group, and
Y
if the space of left cosets
G/H
, where

H<G
is a closed subgroup and
\pi:G\to G/H
is the projection modulo
H
,
g\mapsto gH
.

Definition 1.1
.

We say that a map
\varphi:Y\to X
is a section of
\pi
if

(1)

\pi\circ\varphi=\mbox{id}_{Y}.

Moreover, we say that a map
\varphi:Y\to X
is an
intrinsically Lipschitz section of
\pi
with constant
L
, with
L\in[1,\infty)
, if in addition

(2)

d(\varphi(y_{1}),\varphi(y_{2}))\leq Ld(\varphi(y_{1}),\pi^{-1}(y_{2})),\quad\mbox{for all }y_{1},y_{2}\in Y.

Here
d
denotes the distance on
X
, and, as usual, for a subset
A\subset X
and a point
x\in X
, we have

d(x,A):=\inf\{d(x,a):a\in A\}
.

A first observation is that this class is contained in the class of continuous maps (see
[
DDLD22
, Section 2.4]
) and in the case
\pi
is a Lipschitz quotient or submetry
[
BJL+99
,
VN88
]
, being intrinsically Lipschitz is equivalent to biLipschitz embedding, see Proposition 2.4 in
[
DDLD22
]
. Moreover, since
\varphi
is injective by (
1
), the class of Lipschitz sections not include the constant maps.

Conversely to Lipschitz classical notion, the intrinsically Lipschitz sections cannot be uniformly continuous; an easy example is as follows.

Example 1.2

(
\pi
is not linear)
.

Let
X=(0,1),Y=(1,+\infty)
and
\pi:(0,1)\to(1,+\infty)
defined as
\pi(x)=\frac{1}{x},
for every
x\in(0,1).
It is easy to see that
\pi
is a quotient map and
\varphi:(1,+\infty)\to(0,1)
defined as

\varphi(y)=\frac{1}{y},\quad\forall y\in(1,+\infty),

is an intrinsic
1
-Lipschitz section of
\pi
. On the other hand, it is well-known that
\varphi
is not uniformly continuous.

The long-term objective is to obtain
a Rademacher type theorem á la Cheeger

[
Che99
]
(see also
[
KM16
,
Kei04
]
) for the intrinsically Lipschitz sections.

This project fits to an active line of research
[
Pan89
,
Vit20
,
FMS14
]
on Rademacher type theorems, but it also studies connections to different notions and mathematical areas like Cheeger energy, Sobolev spaces, Optimal transport theory. The reader can see
[
AGS14a
,
AGS14b
,
Stu06
,
LV09
,
Vil09
,
Ha96
,
Sha00
,
JPNJ15
]
.

A first step of this research is given in
[
DD22b
, Proposition 3.1-3.2]
and
[
DD22a
]
as follows.

Proposition 1.3
.

Let
\pi:X\to Y
be a linear and quotient map between a normed space
X
and a topological space
Y.

(1)

the set of all sections is a vector space over
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}.

(2)

If
\varphi:Y\to X
is an intrinsically Lipschitz section of
\pi,
then for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}-\{0\}
the section
\lambda\varphi
is also intrinsic Lipschitz for
1/\lambda\pi
with the same Lipschitz constant.

(3)

If
X=\mathbb{R}^{s}
,
Y
is a metric space and each fiber is contained in a straight line and (
3
) holds, then the sum of two intrinsically Lipschitz sections is so too.

Here it is fundamental that
\pi
is a linear map. The aim of this paper is to prove the last result when
\pi
does not satisfy this property. We focus our attention on that property because it is not true in the context of Carnot groups. More precisely, the main results are

(1)

Proposition
2.4
: we give a Leibniz formula for the intrinsic slope when
\pi
satisfies a weaker linearity condition.

(2)

Proposition
3
and Corollary
3
: we focus our attention on the well-known properties of intrinsic dilations in Carnot groups and we show that the dilation of a Lipschitz section is so too.

(3)

Proposition
4
:
an open question is whether the sum of two Lipschitz maps in the Franchi, Serapioni and Serra Cassano sense is so too.
Our idea of proof in
[
DD22a
]
does not work to solve this open question in general case; on the other hand, if we just consider the linearity of
\pi
valued in the points of the two sections, then it is possible to get a positive answer in the context of Carnot groups of step 2
[
BLU07
,
DD20
]
.

2.
A weaker linearity condition for
\pi

In this section, we study the properties proved in
[
DD22a
]
when
\pi:X\to Y
is not linear. Regarding the definition of the intrinsic Lipschitz constants the reader can see
[
DD22a
, Section 3]
. Here, we ask that there exists
\lambda>0
such that

(3)

\pi(\alpha x_{1}+\beta x_{2})=\alpha^{\lambda}\pi(x_{1})+\beta^{\lambda}\pi(x_{2}),\quad\mbox{ for any }x_{1},x_{2}\in X,

and for any
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
with
(\alpha,\beta)\neq(0,0).

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\varphi,\psi:Y\to X
be sections of
\pi.
Then, for any
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
with
(a,b)\neq(0,0)
the map
\eta:=\alpha\varphi+\beta\psi:Y\to X
is a section of
(\frac{1}{\alpha^{\lambda}+\beta^{\lambda}})\pi.

Proof.

Fix
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
with
(\alpha,\beta)\neq(0,0).
The thesis follows because
\pi(\eta(y))=\pi(\alpha\varphi(y)+\beta\psi(y))=(\alpha^{\lambda}+\beta^{\lambda})y
for any
y\in Y.

∎

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
X
be a normed space and
\varphi:Y\to X
be an intrinsically
L
-Lipschitz section of
\pi.
Then, for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}-\{0\}
the map
\alpha\varphi:Y\to X
is an intrinsically Lipschitz section of
(\frac{1}{\alpha^{\lambda}})\pi
with the same Lipschitz constant.

Proof.

Fix
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}-\{0\}
and
y,z\in Y.
By Proposition
2
, we have that
\alpha\varphi
is a section of
(\frac{1}{\alpha^{\lambda}})\pi.
On the other hand, noting that if
x\in\pi^{-1}(z),
then

(4)

\alpha x\in\pi^{-1}(\alpha^{\lambda}z)=\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{\lambda}}\pi\right)^{-1}(z);

indeed,

\pi(\alpha x)=\alpha^{\lambda}\pi(x)=\alpha^{\lambda}z,

and so
\frac{1}{\alpha^{\lambda}}\pi(\alpha x)=z.
Now we choose
x\in\pi^{-1}(z)
such that

d\left(\alpha\varphi(y),\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{\lambda}}\pi\right)^{-1}(z)\right)=d(\alpha\varphi(y),\alpha x).

Consequently,

\displaystyle d(\alpha\varphi(y),\alpha\varphi(z))

\displaystyle=|\alpha|d(\varphi(y),\varphi(z))\leq L|\alpha|d(\varphi(y),\pi^{-1}(z))

\displaystyle\leq L|\alpha|d(\varphi(y),x)=Ld\left(\alpha\varphi(y),\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{\lambda}}\pi\right)^{-1}(z)\right).

∎

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
X=\mathbb{R}^{s}
,
Y
be a metric space and
\pi:X\to Y
be a quotient map such that each fiber is contained in a straight line. If
\pi
satisfies (
3
), then the sum of two intrinsically Lipschitz sections is so too.

Proof.

The proof is exactly as in
[
DD22a
, Theorem 4.1]
, noting that the fiber of the sum is
(1/2\pi)^{-1}
thanks to Proposition
2
.
∎

Now we want to obtain the Leibniz formula for the intrinsic Lipschitz constants using an additional suitable condition on the fibers as in
[
DD22a
]
.

Theorem 2.4

(Leibniz formula)
.

Let
Y
be a metric space and
\pi:X\to Y
be a quotient map such that either
X=\mathbb{R}
or
X=\mathbb{R}^{s}
and each fiber is contained in a straight line. Assume also that
\pi
satisfies (
3
) and
\varphi
and
\psi
are intrinsically
L
-Lipschitz sections of
\pi
such that there is
c\geq 1
satisfying

(5)

d(\pi^{-1}(y),\pi^{-1}(z))\geq\frac{1}{c}d(f(y),\pi^{-1}(z)),\quad\,\forall y,z\in Y,

\mbox{for }f=\varphi,\psi.
Then, denoting
\eta=\alpha\varphi+\beta\psi
the map
Y\to X
with
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}-\{0\},
we have that

(6)

Ils(\eta)(y)\leq\frac{c}{2^{1/\lambda}}(Ils(\varphi)(y)+Ils(\psi)(y)),\quad\forall y\in Y.

We need the following lemma which is true in more general setting.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
X
be a normed space,
Y
be a topological space and
\pi:X\to Y
be a quotient map satisfies (
3
). Then

(7)

|\alpha|d(\pi^{-1}(y_{1}),\pi^{-1}(y_{2}))=d\left(\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{\lambda}}\pi\right)^{-1}(y_{1}),\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{\lambda}}\pi\right)^{-1}(y_{2})\right),\quad\forall y_{1},y_{2}\in Y,\forall\alpha\in\mathbb{R}-\{0\}.

Proof.

Fix
y_{1},y_{2}\in Y.
If
d(\pi^{-1}(y_{1}),\pi^{-1}(y_{2}))=d(x_{1},x_{2})
for some
x_{1}\in\pi^{-1}(y_{1})
and
x_{2}\in\pi^{-1}(y_{2}),
then as above (see (
4
)) we deduce that

\alpha x_{i}\in\pi^{-1}(\alpha^{\lambda}y_{i})=\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{\lambda}}\pi\right)^{-1}(y_{i}),

for
i=1,2
and, consequently,

(8)

\displaystyle d(\pi^{-1}(y_{1}),\pi^{-1}(y_{2}))

\displaystyle=\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|=\frac{1}{|\alpha|}\|\alpha x_{1}-\alpha x_{2}\|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{|\alpha|}d\left(\left(1/{\alpha^{\lambda}}\pi\right)^{-1}(y_{1}),\left(1/{\alpha^{\lambda}}\pi\right)^{-1}(y_{2})\right).

On the other hand, if
d((1/\alpha^{\lambda}\pi)^{-1}(y_{1}),(1/\alpha^{\lambda}\pi)^{-1}(y_{2}))=d(h_{1},h_{2})
for some
h_{i}\in(1/\alpha^{\lambda}\pi)^{-1}(y_{i})
for
i=1,2
then

\frac{1}{\alpha}h_{i}\in\pi^{-1}(y_{i});

indeed,

\pi\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}h_{i}\right)=\frac{1}{\alpha^{\lambda}}\pi(h_{i})=y_{i},\quad\mbox{ for }i=1,2.

Hence,

(9)

\displaystyle d((1/\alpha^{\lambda}\pi)^{-1}(y_{1}),(1/\alpha^{\lambda}\pi)^{-1}(y_{2}))

\displaystyle=\|h_{1}-h_{2}\|=|\alpha|\left\|\frac{1}{\alpha}h_{1}-\frac{1}{\alpha}h_{2}\right\|

\displaystyle\geq|\alpha|d(\pi^{-1}(y_{1}),\pi^{-1}(y_{2})).

Hence, putting together (
8
) and (
9
), we get (
7
).
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.4
.

Notice that since
Ils(\varphi)=Ils(\alpha\varphi)
for any
\alpha\neq 0
(see Proposition
2
), it is sufficient to prove (
6
) for
\eta=\varphi+\psi.

We split the proof in two steps. In the first step we prove that
\eta
is intrinsic Lipschitz and in the second one we show (
6
) following a similar computation in
[
KM16
, Lemma 3.2]
.

(1). The thesis follows from Theorem
2.3
noting that for
s=1
it is trivial that each fiber is contained in a straight line.

(2). Fix
\bar{y}\in Y
and
r>0.
Suppose we are given
\varepsilon>0.
For any
y\in B(\bar{y},r)
we have

(10)

\displaystyle\frac{d(\varphi(\bar{y}),\varphi(y))}{d(\varphi(\bar{y}),\pi^{-1}(y))}\leq Ils(\varphi)(\bar{y})+\varepsilon\qquad{and}\mbox{\qquad}\frac{d(\psi(\bar{y}),\psi(y))}{d(\psi(\bar{y}),\pi^{-1}(y))}\leq Ils(\psi)(\bar{y})+\varepsilon.

On the other hand, thanks to point (1), we can find
z\in B(\bar{y},r)
so that

\displaystyle Ils(\varphi+\psi)(\bar{y})\leq\frac{d(\varphi(\bar{y})+\psi(\bar{y}),\varphi(z)+\psi(z))}{d(\varphi(\bar{y})+\psi(\bar{y}),(1/2\pi)^{-1}(z))}+\varepsilon,

and so

\displaystyle Ils(\varphi+\psi)(\bar{y})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d(\varphi(\bar{y}),\varphi(z))+d(\psi(\bar{y}),\psi(z))}{d(\varphi(\bar{y})+\psi(\bar{y}),(1/2\pi)^{-1}(z))}+\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d(\varphi(\bar{y}),\varphi(z))+d(\psi(\bar{y}),\psi(z))}{d((1/2\pi)^{-1}(\bar{y}),(1/2\pi)^{-1}(z))}+\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\frac{d(\varphi(\bar{y}),\varphi(z))+d(\psi(\bar{y}),\psi(z))}{2^{1/\lambda}d(\pi^{-1}(\bar{y}),\pi^{-1}(z))}+\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c}{2^{1/\lambda}}\frac{d(\varphi(\bar{y}),\varphi(z))}{d(\varphi(\bar{y}),\pi^{-1}(z))}+\frac{c}{2^{1/\lambda}}\frac{d(\psi(\bar{y}),\psi(z))}{d(\psi(\bar{y}),\pi^{-1}(z))}+\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c}{2^{1/\lambda}}(Ils(\varphi)(\bar{y})+\varepsilon)+\frac{c}{2^{1/\lambda}}(Ils(\psi)(\bar{y})+\varepsilon)+\varepsilon

where in the first equality we used the triangle inequality, and in the second one we used the simple fact
d((1/2\pi)^{-1}(\bar{y}),(1/2\pi)^{-1}(z))\leq d(\varphi(\bar{y})+\psi(\bar{y}),(1/2\pi)^{-1}(z)),
noting that
\varphi(\bar{y})+\psi(\bar{y})\in(1/2\pi)^{-1}(\bar{y})
. In the first equality we used Lemma
2
for
\alpha^{\lambda}=2
and in the last two inequalities we used (
5
) and (
10
).

Hence, by the arbitrariness of
\varepsilon,
the proof is complete.
∎

3.
The intrinsic dilations vs quotient map

In this section the general setting is a group
G
together with a closed subgroup
H
of
G
in such a way that the quotient space
G/H:=\{gH:g\in G\}
naturally is a topological space for which the map
\pi:g\mapsto gH
is continuous, open, and surjective: it is a quotient map.

A section for the map
\pi:G\to G/H
is just a map
\varphi:G/H\to G
such that
\varphi(gH)\in gH
, since we point out the trivial identity
\pi^{-1}(gH)=gH
. To have the notion of intrinsic Lipschitz section we need the group
G
to be equipped with a distance which we assume left-invariant. We refer to such a
G
as a
metric group
. The reader can see
[
DDLD22
, Section 6]
.

Following the Carnot structure, we present a result where the additional hypothesis are compatibility conditions between the distance on
G
, the quotient map and another suitable maps which corresponds to so-called intrinsic dilations in Carnot groups.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
G=H_{1}\cdot H_{2}
be a metric group and, for any fixed
\lambda>0,

\delta_{\lambda}:G\to G
such that

(1)

\pi_{H_{1}}
is
k
-Lipschitz at
1;

(2)

d(\delta_{\lambda}g,\delta_{\lambda}q)=\lambda d(g,q)
for any
g,q\in G;

(3)

\delta_{\lambda}(\delta_{1/\lambda}(g))=g
for any
g\in G;

(4)

\pi_{H_{1}}(\delta_{\lambda}g)=\delta_{\lambda}\pi_{H_{1}}(g)
for any
g\in G.

Then
\delta_{\lambda}\circ\varphi:G/H_{2}\to G
is an intrinsically
L\lambda
-Lipschitz section of
\delta_{1/\lambda}\circ\pi_{H_{1}}
, if
\varphi:G/H_{2}\to G
is so too for
\pi_{H_{1}}
with Lipschitz constant
L
.

Proof.

The fact that
\varphi
is a section of
\delta_{1/\lambda}\pi_{H_{1}},
follows from
(3)
and

\pi_{H_{1}}(\delta_{\lambda}\varphi(g))=\delta_{\lambda}\pi_{H_{1}}(\varphi(g))=\delta_{\lambda}g,\quad\forall g\in G.

Moreover, by
(4)
we have that for every
a,b\in G/H_{2}

d(\delta_{\lambda}\varphi(a),\delta_{\lambda}\varphi(b))=\lambda d(\varphi(a),\varphi(b))\leq L\lambda d(\varphi(a),\pi_{H_{1}}^{-1}(b)).

∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
G=N\rtimes H
be a Carnot group and we consider
\delta_{\lambda}:G\to G
the usual intrinsic dilations
(
see, for instance,
[
SC16
]
)
.
Then
\delta_{\lambda}\varphi
is an intrinsically
L\lambda
-Lipschitz section of
\delta_{1/\lambda}\pi_{N}
, if
\varphi
is so too for
\pi_{N}
with Lipschitz constant
L
.

Proof.

The only non trivial fact is
(4)
in Proposition
3
but it is true for the homogeneity of the polynomials that appear in the group operation.

∎

Example 3.3

(Heisenberg groups)
.

The
n
-th Heisenberg group

\mathbb{H}^{n}
is the model case of Carnot groups. It is the set
\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
with the group
product
\cdot
given by

(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2n},t)\cdot(x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{2n}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\left(x_{1}+x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{2n}+x_{2n}^{\prime},t+t^{\prime}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}x_{n+i}^{\prime}-x_{i}^{\prime}x_{n+i}\right)

for

(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2n},t),(x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{2n}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
.
We equip
\mathbb{H}^{n}
with the left-invariant metric

(11)

d(p,q):=\|q^{-1}\cdot p\|,\quad p,q\in\mathbb{H}^{n},

where
\|(x,t)\|:=\max\{|x|,\sqrt{|t|}\}
and
|\cdot|
denotes
the usual Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
and on
\mathbb{R}
. Here, for any fixed
\lambda>0,
the intrinsic dilation
\delta_{\lambda}:\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\mathbb{H}^{n}
is an automorphism defined as

\delta_{\lambda}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2n},t)=(\lambda x_{1},\ldots,\lambda x_{2n},\lambda^{2}t).

Hence, it is easy to see that
\delta_{\lambda}(\delta_{1/\lambda}(g))=g
and
\|\delta_{\lambda}g\|=\lambda\|g\|
for any
g\in\mathbb{H}^{n}.

Moreover, if we consider the splitting
\mathbb{H}^{n}=H\ltimes N
given by

H=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},0,\ldots,0)\colon(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\}

and

N=\{(0,\ldots,0,x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{2n},t)\colon(x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{2n},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n+1-k}\},

then

\displaystyle\pi_{N}(\delta_{\lambda}(p))

\displaystyle=\pi_{N}(\lambda x_{1},\ldots,\lambda x_{2n},\lambda^{2}t)

\displaystyle=\left(0,\dots,0,\lambda x_{k+1},\ldots,\lambda x_{2n},\lambda^{2}t-\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}x_{n+i}\right).

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\delta_{\lambda}(\pi_{N}(p))

\displaystyle=\delta_{\lambda}\left(0,\dots,0,x_{k+1},\dots,x_{2n},t-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}x_{n+i}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(0,\dots,0,\lambda x_{k+1},\ldots,\lambda x_{2n},\lambda^{2}t-\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}x_{n+i}\right),

and so we get

\pi_{N}(\delta_{\lambda}(p))=\delta_{\lambda}(\pi_{N}(p)).

Unfortunately, the non-linearity of
\pi
can already be seen in this case.

4.
The sum of intrinsic Lipschitz sections is so too in Carnot groups of step 2

In this section we consider Carnot groups of step 2 as follow.

Definition 4.1
.

We say that
\mathbb{G}:=(\mathbb{R}^{m+n},\cdot,\delta_{\lambda})
is a Carnot group of step 2 if
there are
n
linearly independent, skew-symmetric
m\times m
real matrices
\mathcal{B}^{(1)},\dots,\mathcal{B}^{(n)}
such that
for all
p=(p^{1},p^{2})
and
q=(q^{1},q^{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
and for all
\lambda>0

(12)

p\cdot q=(p^{1}+q^{1},p^{2}+q^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{B}p^{1},q^{1}\rangle)

where
\langle\mathcal{B}p^{1},q^{1}\rangle:=(\langle\mathcal{B}^{(1)}p^{1},q^{1}\rangle,\dots,\langle\mathcal{B}^{(n)}p^{1},q^{1}\rangle)

and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and

\delta_{\lambda}p:=(\lambda p^{1},\lambda^{2}p^{2}).

When
H
is one dimensional, without loss of generality (see
[
BLU07
, Chapter 3.4]
), we consider the following splitting

H:=\{(x_{1},0\dots,0)\,:\,x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}\},\qquad N:=\{(0,x_{2},\dots,x_{m+n})\,:\,x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Unfortunately, in the context of Carnot groups of step two
\pi_{N}
is non linear (see the model case as in Example
3
). However, with the additional hypothesis which is a sort of a compatibility condition between two Lipschitz sections it is possible to deduce the following proposition in Carnot groups of step 2.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
G=N\rtimes H
be a Carnot group of step 2 with
H
1-dimensional and
\varphi,\psi:G/H\to G
be intrinsically Lipschitz sections of
\pi_{G/H}
such that if we put

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1,\ell}:=\langle\mathcal{B}^{(\ell)}p^{1},q^{1}\rangle-\langle\mathcal{B}^{(\ell)}(0,p_{2}+q_{2},\dots,p_{m}+q_{m}),(p_{1}+q_{1},0\dots,0)\rangle,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{2,\ell}:=\langle\mathcal{B}^{(\ell)}(0,p_{2},\dots,p_{m}),(0,q_{2},\dots,q_{m})\rangle+\langle\mathcal{B}^{(\ell)}(0,p_{2},\dots,p_{m}),(p_{1},0)\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle\mathcal{B}^{(\ell)}(0,q_{2},\dots,q_{m}),(q_{1},0)\rangle,

\forall\ell=1,\dots,n
where
p_{i}=\varphi_{i}(a)
and
q_{i}=\psi_{i}(b)
for any
i=1,\dots,m+n,
we want that

(13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1,\ell}=\mathcal{A}_{2,\ell},\quad\forall\ell=1,\dots,n.

Then the sum of
\varphi
and
\psi
is intrinsic Lipschitz.

Proof.

We want to apply Corollary 4.6 in
[
DD22a
]
. The only hypothesis that we need is the linearity of
\pi
which we don’t have in general. However, it is sufficient to show that

\pi(\varphi(y_{1})+\psi(y_{2}))=\pi(\varphi(y_{1}))+\pi(\psi(y_{2})),\quad\forall y_{1},y_{2}\in G/H,

where
\varphi
and
\psi
satisfy (
13
).
For simplicity, we identify
G/H
with
N,
and
\pi_{G/H}
with
\pi_{N}
and we just write
\pi.
We prove

(14)

\displaystyle\pi(p\cdot q)=\pi(p)\cdot\pi(q),

for

and

By an easy computation, we get

\displaystyle\pi(p)=\left(0,p_{2},\dots,p_{m},[\pi(p)]_{m+1},\dots,[\pi(p)]_{m+n}\right)

where

\displaystyle{}_{m+\ell}

\displaystyle=p_{m+\ell}+\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{B}^{(\ell)}(0,p_{2},\dots,p_{m}),(p_{1},0\dots,0)\rangle,

for

Consequently,

\pi(p)\cdot\pi(q)=(0,p_{2}+q_{2},\dots,p_{m}+q_{m},[\pi(p)\cdot\pi(q)]_{m+1},\dots,[\pi(p)\cdot\pi(q)]_{m+n}),

\displaystyle{}_{m+\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{B}^{(\ell)}(0,p_{2},\dots,p_{m}),(0,q_{2},\dots,q_{m})\rangle+[\pi(p)]_{m+\ell}+[\pi(q)]_{m+\ell}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\pi(p\cdot q)=\left(0,p_{2}+q_{2},\dots,p_{m}+q_{m},[\pi(p\cdot q)]_{m+1},\dots,[\pi(p\cdot q)]_{m+n}\right),

\displaystyle{}_{m+\ell}

\displaystyle=p_{m+\ell}+q_{m+\ell}+\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{B}^{(\ell)}p^{1},q^{1}\rangle

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{B}^{(\ell)}(0,p_{2}+q_{2},\dots,p_{m}+q_{m}),(p_{1}+q_{1},0\dots,0)\rangle,

Hence we get the thesis using the hypothesis
