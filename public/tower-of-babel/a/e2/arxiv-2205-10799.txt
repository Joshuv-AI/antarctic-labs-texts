On point estimators for Gamma and Beta distributions
Nickos Papadatos

Abstract

1
Introduction

Let
\Gamma(\alpha)=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-x}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x
,
\alpha>0
,
be the Gamma function.
The Gamma distribution,
\mathcal{G}(\alpha,\lambda)
, with density

f(x)=\frac{\lambda^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\lambda x},\ \ \ \ x>0,

and parameters
\alpha>0
(the shape) and
\lambda>0
(the reciprocal scale),
generates one of the most useful statistical models, especially when the data
are nonnegative. This distribution belongs to the Pearson family, and in particular,
to the Integrated Pearson family of distributions, see, e.g.,
Afendras and Papadatos (2015).
Therefore, estimating procedures for the parameters
\alpha
,
\lambda
, and/or

1/\alpha
and
1/\lambda
, are of fundamental importance in applications.
It is well-known that the maximum likelihood estimators (MLEs) are not tractable and, consequently, one has to apply numerical procedures for their evaluation. Moreover,
to the best of our knowledge, it is not even known whether
an unbiased estimator for
\alpha
(and
\lambda
) exists, for some
sample size
n
. In the contrary, unbiased estimators
for the reciprocal parameters
1/\alpha
and
1/\lambda
do exist;
recently, Ye and Chen (2017) obtained closed-form unbiased estimators
for the reciprocal parameters with very high asymptotic efficiency.
It should be noted that an augmented-sample (simulation)
technique for obtaining exact confidence intervals
for
\alpha
is introduced by Iliopoulos (2016).

In the present work we show that an unbiased estimator for
\alpha

exists if and only if the sample size
n
is at least
4
, and the same
is true for
\lambda
(provided that
\alpha
is not too small).
More precisely, in Section
2

we obtain the uniformly minimum variance unbiased estimators
(UMVUEs) of
\alpha
,
\lambda
,
1/\alpha
,
1/\lambda
. In particular,
the UMVUE of
1/\lambda
, based on two observations, can be written as a positive
symmetric kernel, whence, the corresponding
U
-statistic
generates the Ye-Chen (2017) estimator; see Remark
2.2
.

Let
B(\alpha,\beta)=\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)/\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)
,
\alpha>0
,

\beta>0
, be the Beta function. The Beta distribution has density

f(x)=\frac{1}{B(\alpha,\beta)}x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1},\ \ \ \ 0<x<1.

(1.1)

This distribution belongs to the Integrated Pearson family,
and, at least to our knowledge,
it is not known whether the parameters can be unbiasedly estimated.
The MLEs do not have closed forms, and one has to overcome similar difficulties
as for the Gamma case.
In Section
3
we obtain closed-form, Ye-Chen-type, estimators
for the parameters.
Our method is different than that of Ye and Chen (2017), since it is based on
a Stein-type covariance identity for the Beta distribution, followed
by an application of the theory of
U
-statistics. The covariance identity
generates an unbiased bivariate symmetric kernel for the parameter

\theta=1/(\alpha+\beta)
.
Then, an application of the classical theory of
U
-statistics
produces an unbiased estimator of
\theta
,
which, in turn, yields the desired estimators of
\alpha
and
\beta
.
The asymptotic
efficiency of the proposed estimators
is studied in some detail, and it is shown to be very high
(see Table 1). The Beta model is useful not only for analyzing data
with bounded support, but also when positive data are
mapped to the interval
(0,1)
by means of a transformation,
e.g.,
x\mapsto x/(1+x)
.

2
Unbiased estimation of the parameters of Gamma distribution

Let
{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
(
n\geq 2
)
be a random sample from
{\it\Gamma}(\alpha,\lambda)
,
where the unknown parameter
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}=(\alpha,\lambda)\in{\mbox{\boldmath$\Theta$}}=(0,\infty)^{2}
.
It is well-known that the complete, sufficient statistic
can be written in the form

{\mbox{\boldmath$T$}}(\mbox{\boldmath$X$})=(X,Y)
, where
X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}

and
Y^{1/n}
is the ratio of the geometric to the arithmetic
mean of the data. The random variables
X,Y
are independent, as follows, e.g.,
from Basu’s theorem, because the distribution of
Y
is free of
\lambda
,
while
X
is complete for
\lambda
when
\alpha>0
is known.
The random variable
X
follows a

\mathcal{G}(n\alpha,\lambda)
distribution, while
Y

is distributed like
\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}B_{\alpha}(i/n)
,
where the random variables
B_{\alpha}(i/n)
,
i=1,\ldots,n-1
, are
independent, and
B_{\alpha}(i/n)

has a Beta distribution (
1.1
) with parameters
\alpha
and

\beta=i/n
.

By using the Beta product representation,
it follows inductively that the random variable
Y
has density

f_{Y}(y;\alpha)=K(\alpha)y^{\alpha-1}(1-y)^{(n-3)/2}G(y),\ \ \ 0<y<1,

(2.1)

where

K(\alpha)=\Gamma(\alpha)^{-n}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\Gamma(\alpha+i/n)/\Gamma(i/n)
,
and the (power-series) function
G

is given by

G(y)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}d_{m}(1-y)^{m},\ \ 0<y<1.

(2.2)

Notice that our notation suspends the dependence of
K(\alpha)

and
G(y)
on
n
.

In (
2.2
) the coefficients
(d_{m})_{m\geq 0}
are defined
recurrently as follows:

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}(m)=I(m=0)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}1,&\mbox{if}&m=0,\\
0,&\mbox{if}&m=1,2,\ldots,\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\gamma_{i+1}(m)=\frac{\Gamma(m+i(i+1)/(2n))}{\Gamma(m+(i+1)(i+2)/(2n))}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{\Gamma(m-k+(i+1)/n)}{(m-k)!}\gamma_{i}(k),\ \ \ \ i=1,\ldots,n-2,

\displaystyle d_{m}:=\gamma_{n-1}(m),\ \ \ \ m=0,1,\ldots\ .\Bigg.

(2.6)

Most of the preceding results can be found in Glaser (1976), Nandi (1980),
and Tang and Gupta (1984). Obviously,

G(y)\equiv 1
when
n=2
.
Moreover, for
n\geq 3
,

G
is strictly decreasing and positive; more precisely,

d_{m}>0
for all
m
,
G^{\prime}(y)<0
and
G(y)>G(1-)=d_{0}>0
,
0<y<1
.
It should be noted at this point that, when
n\geq 3
,
the auxiliary random variable

Y_{1}=\prod_{i=1}^{n-2}B_{\alpha}(i/n)
has density
(cf. Tang and Gupta, 1984)

f_{Y_{1}}(y;\alpha)=K_{1}(\alpha)y^{\alpha-1}(1-y)^{(n-1)(n-2)/(2n)-1}G_{1}(y),\ \ \ 0<y<1,

(2.7)

where
K_{1}(\alpha)=\Gamma(\alpha)^{-(n-1)}\prod_{i=1}^{n-2}\Gamma(\alpha+i/n)/\Gamma(i/n)
and

G_{1}(y)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{n-2}(k)(1-y)^{k},\ \ \ 0<y<1.

(2.8)

The random variable
Y_{1}

will be used in the proof of Theorem
2.1
, below.

Definition 2.1
.

An estimator
T(\mbox{\boldmath$X$})
will be called
uniformly minimum variance
unbiased estimator
(UMVUE) for the parametric function

h(\alpha,\lambda)
if
T(\mbox{\boldmath$X$})=u(X,Y)
is a (Borel)
function of the complete, sufficient statistic
(X,Y)
, and

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}u(X,Y)=h(\alpha,\lambda)
, for all

\alpha>0
,
\lambda>0
, where the subscript
(\alpha,\lambda)

denotes expectation w.r.t. the joint density of

(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
,

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\frac{\lambda^{n\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)^{n}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right)^{\alpha-1}\exp\left(-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right),\ \ \ \ (x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in(0,\infty)^{n}.

Accordingly, an UMVUE may, or may not have
finite variance. For completeness of the presentation,
the term UMVUE will be also used
in a wider sense, namely, in the case where
the equality

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}u(X,Y)=h(\alpha,\lambda)

is merely satisfied for all
(\alpha,\lambda)
in
a subset of
(0,\infty)^{2}
with nonempty interior.

In the sequel we shall make use of the following simple,
but useful, observation, saying that if two functions have
identical Laplace transforms in an arbitrarily small interval
then they coincide.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
I=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
where
0\leq\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\leq\infty
.
Suppose that for the Borel functions
g_{i}:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,2
, it is true
that
\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda x}|g_{i}(x)|\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x<\infty
for all
\lambda\in I
.
If

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda x}g_{1}(x)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda x}g_{2}(x)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x,\ \ \ \lambda\in I,

then
g_{1}(x)=g_{2}(x)
for almost all
x\in(0,\infty)
.

Proof:
For
i=1,2
write

g_{i}=g_{i}^{+}-g_{i}^{-}
where
g_{i}^{+}(x)=\max\{g_{i}(x),0\}
,

g_{i}^{-}(x)=\max\{-g_{i}(x),0\}
. By assumption,

0\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda x}w_{1}(x)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda x}w_{2}(x)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x<\infty,\ \ \ \lambda\in I,

where
w_{1}=g_{1}^{+}+g_{2}^{-}\geq 0
,
w_{2}=g_{1}^{-}+g_{2}^{+}\geq 0
.
Fix
\lambda_{0}\in I

and set
\nu_{0}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda_{0}x}w_{1}(x)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda_{0}x}w_{2}(x)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x\geq 0
. Since
w_{i}\geq 0
, it is clear that
\nu_{0}=0
implies that

w_{1}=0
and
w_{2}=0
a.e.; then,
w_{1}=w_{2}
a.e. and, thus,

g_{1}=g_{2}
a.e.
If
\nu_{0}>0
, we may define the probability densities

f_{i}(x)=e^{-\lambda_{0}x}w_{i}(x)/\nu_{0}
,
i=1,2
; then, for
\lambda\in I
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{(\lambda_{0}-\lambda)x}f_{1}(x)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x=\frac{1}{\nu_{0}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda x}w_{1}(x)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x=\frac{1}{\nu_{0}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda x}w_{2}(x)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{(\lambda_{0}-\lambda)x}f_{2}(x)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x.

If
M_{i}
is the moment generating function of
f_{i}
, it follows that

M_{1}(t)=M_{2}(t)<\infty
for all
t\in J:=(\lambda_{0}-\lambda_{2},\lambda_{0}-\lambda_{1})
,
and since
J
contains the origin,
f_{1}=f_{2}
a.e.; this implies that
w_{1}=w_{2}
a.e.,
and consequently,
g_{1}=g_{2}
a.e.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
s(x,y):(0,\infty)^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be a Borel measurable function
satisfying

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}|s(x,y)|\exp(-\lambda x-\alpha y)x^{n\alpha-1}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x<\infty\ \ \mbox{for all $\alpha>0$, $\lambda>0$.}

Then, the function

H(\alpha,\lambda):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}s(x,y)\exp(-\lambda x-\alpha y)x^{n\alpha-1}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x

is continuous in
(0,\infty)^{2}
.

Proof:

Consider an arbitrary sequence

(\alpha_{m},\lambda_{m})\to(\alpha,\lambda)\in(0,\infty)^{2}
, as

m\to\infty
.
Then, we can find
m_{0}
such that

\frac{\alpha}{2}<\alpha_{m}<2\alpha
and
\frac{\lambda}{2}<\lambda_{m}<2\lambda

for
m\geq m_{0}
,
The function
\delta_{1}(\lambda)=e^{-\lambda x}
is obviously decreasing
(for each fixed
x>0
), so that
e^{-\lambda_{m}x}<e^{-\frac{\lambda}{2}x}
,

m\geq m_{0}
.
Consider also the function
\delta_{2}(\alpha)=e^{-\alpha y}x^{n\alpha-1}

(for fixed
x>0
,
y>0
).
Then,
\log\delta_{2}(\alpha)=(n\log x-y)\alpha-\log x
is a linear
function of
\alpha
, and thus,

\delta_{2}(\alpha_{m})<\max\{\delta_{2}(\alpha/2),\delta_{2}(2\alpha)\}<\delta_{2}(\alpha/2)+\delta_{2}(2\alpha)
,
m\geq m_{0}
.
Combining the above we obtain the inequality

e^{-\lambda_{m}x-\alpha_{m}y}x^{n\alpha_{m}-1}<e^{-\frac{\lambda}{2}x-\frac{\alpha}{2}y}x^{n\frac{\alpha}{2}-1}+e^{-\frac{\lambda}{2}x-2\alpha y}x^{2n\alpha-1},\ \ \ (x,y)\in(0,\infty)^{2},\ \ m\geq m_{0}.

It follows that the sequence

w_{m}(x,y):=s(x,y)e^{-\lambda_{m}x-\alpha_{m}y}x^{n\alpha_{m}-1},\ \ \ (x,y)\in(0,\infty)^{2},\ \ m=m_{0},m_{0}+1,\ldots,

is dominated by
W(x,y):=|s(x,y)|e^{-\frac{\lambda}{2}x-\frac{\alpha}{2}y}x^{n\frac{\alpha}{2}-1}+|s(x,y)|e^{-\frac{\lambda}{2}x-2\alpha y}x^{2n\alpha-1}
,
and, by assumption,
W
is integrable on
(0,\infty)^{2}
.
Clearly,
w_{m}(x,y)\to w(x,y):=s(x,y)e^{-\lambda x-\alpha y}x^{n\alpha-1}
as
m\to\infty
.
Thus, by dominated convergence,

H(\alpha_{m},\lambda_{m})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}w_{m}(x,y)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x\to\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}w(x,y)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x=H(\alpha,\lambda),\ \ \ \mbox{as}\ \ m\to\infty,

completing the proof.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
h(\alpha):(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be a parametric function
of the shape parameter. If there exists an unbiased estimator
U_{1}=U_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath$X$})

for
h
, then the UMVUE of
h(\alpha)
is a function of
Y
, alone.

Proof:

By assumption,
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}|U_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath$X$})|<\infty
for all

\alpha>0
,
\lambda>0
.
The function
U_{1}:(0,\infty)^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
can always be taken
to be Borel measurable. From the RB/LS Theorem,
the UMVUE is given by
U(X,Y)=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}(U_{1}|X,Y)
,
where
U:(0,\infty)^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
can also be taken to be Borel measurable
with no loss of generality. Unbiasedness of the estimator

U(X,Y)
means that (recall that
X,Y
are independent)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\left\{\frac{\lambda^{n\alpha}}{\Gamma(n\alpha)}x^{n\alpha-1}e^{-\lambda x}\right\}\left\{K(\alpha)y^{\alpha-1}(1-y)^{(n-3)/2}G(y)\right\}U(x,y)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x=h(\alpha),

for all
\alpha>0
,
\lambda>0
. This relation can be rewritten in the form

H(\alpha,\lambda):=\frac{h(\alpha)\Gamma(n\alpha)}{K(\alpha)\lambda^{n\alpha}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-e^{-y})^{(n-3)/2}G(e^{-y})U(x,e^{-y})\exp(-\lambda x-\alpha y)x^{n\alpha-1}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x.

Since
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}|U(X,Y)|<\infty
for all
\alpha>0
and
\lambda>0
, we
may apply Proposition
2.1
with

s(x,y)=(1-e^{-y})^{(n-3)/2}G(e^{-y})U(x,e^{-y})

to conclude that the function

H(\alpha,\lambda)

is continuous, and hence,
h(\alpha)
is a continuous function
of
\alpha>0
; notice that all functions
K(\alpha)
,
\Gamma(n\alpha)
,
and
\lambda^{n\alpha}
, are positive and continuous.

Write now the relation
h(\alpha)=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}U(X,Y)

in the form

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda x}h(\alpha)x^{n\alpha-1}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda x}K(\alpha)x^{n\alpha-1}\left\{\int_{0}^{1}y^{\alpha-1}(1-y)^{(n-3)/2}G(y)U(x,y)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y\right\}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x.

It follows that the Laplace
transforms of the functions
f_{1}(x):=h(\alpha)x^{n\alpha-1}

and
f_{2}(x):=K(\alpha)x^{n\alpha-1}\int_{0}^{1}y^{\alpha-1}(1-y)^{(n-3)/2}G(y)U(x,y)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y

are identical,
and Lemma
2.1
implies that for every
\alpha>0
,

\frac{h(\alpha)}{K(\alpha)}=\int_{0}^{1}y^{\alpha-1}(1-y)^{(n-3)/2}G(y)U(x,y)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y,\ \ \ \mbox{for almost all}\ x\in(0,\infty).

(2.9)

Write
\mathbb{Q}_{+}=\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,\infty)

and set
A_{\alpha}=\{x>0:\mbox{(\ref{a.e.}) holds}\}
,

N_{\alpha}=(0,\infty)\setminus A_{\alpha}=\{x>0:\mbox{(\ref{a.e.}) does not hold}\}
,

A=\cap_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}}A_{\alpha}
,

N=\cup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}}N_{\alpha}=(0,\infty)\setminus A
.
By construction, the Lebesgue measure of
N
is zero.
We chose an arbitrary
x_{0}\in A
and define
\widetilde{u}(y):=U(x_{0},y)
.
Notice that
\widetilde{u}:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is not necessarily
Borel measurable;
it is, however, Lebesgue measurable and, hence, it is equal
a.e. to a Borel function
u:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
. Obviously,
substituting
u
in place of
\widetilde{u}
, the values
of the above integrals are not affected.
From (
2.9
)
we have

\frac{h(\alpha)}{K(\alpha)}=\int_{0}^{1}y^{\alpha-1}(1-y)^{(n-3)/2}G(y)u(y)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y,\ \ \ \ \alpha\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}.

(2.10)

The right-hand side of (
2.10
) defines a continuous function
of
\alpha>0
, because

\int_{0}^{1}y^{\alpha-1}(1-y)^{(n-3)/2}G(y)u(y)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha y}(1-e^{-y})^{(n-3)/2}G(e^{-y})u(e^{-y})\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y

is the Laplace transform of the function

y\mapsto(1-e^{-y})^{(n-3)/2}G(e^{-y})u(e^{-y})
.
Since
h(\alpha)
and
K(\alpha)
are both continuous,
it follows from (
2.10
) that

h(\alpha)=\int_{0}^{1}K(\alpha)y^{\alpha-1}(1-y)^{(n-3)/2}G(y)u(y)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y=\int_{0}^{1}f_{Y}(y;\alpha)u(y)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y

for all

\alpha>0
. Hence,
u(Y)
is an unbiased
estimator of
h(\alpha)
, and also, it is a function
of the complete, sufficient statistic
(X,Y)
; thus,
it is the (unique) UMVUE.

Remark 2.1
.

(A consequence to the Cramér-Rao bound).
The Fisher information matrix based on a single observation
from
\mathcal{G}(\alpha,\lambda)
is given by

J=J(\alpha,\lambda)=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda^{2}\psi_{1}(\alpha)&-\lambda\\
-\lambda&\alpha\end{array}\right),\mbox{ with inverse }J^{-1}=\frac{1}{\alpha\psi_{1}(\alpha)-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\alpha&\lambda\\
\lambda&\lambda^{2}\psi_{1}(\alpha)\end{array}\right),

where
\psi_{1}(\alpha)=(\log\Gamma(\alpha))^{\prime\prime}>1/\alpha
.
The Cramér-Rao (CR) bound says that for any unbiased estimator

T=T(\mbox{\boldmath$X$})
of the parametric function
h(\alpha,\lambda)
,

n\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}T\geq(\nabla h(\alpha,\lambda))^{T}J^{-1}\nabla h(\alpha,\lambda)
.
Hence, in the particular case where
h=h(\alpha)
is a parametric function
of the shape parameter alone
(i.e.,
h
does not depend on
\lambda
),
the CR bound takes
the form

n\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}T(\mbox{\boldmath$X$})\geq\frac{h^{\prime}(\alpha)^{2}}{\psi_{1}(\alpha)-1/\alpha},\ \ \ \mbox{whenever }\ \mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}T(\mbox{\boldmath$X$})=h(\alpha)\ \mbox{ for all }\alpha>0,\ \lambda>0.

(2.11)

We shall now show that Proposition
2.2
yields a
better lower bound. Indeed, since the UMVUE of
h(\alpha)

is a Borel function of
Y
, say
u=u(Y)
,
we can apply the CR bound,
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}u(Y)\geq h^{\prime}(\alpha)^{2}/J_{Y}
,
where
J_{Y}
is the Fisher information of
Y
.
From (
2.1
), the density of
Y
is given by

f_{Y}(y;\alpha)=\exp\Big(a\log y-A(\alpha)\Big)k(y),

where
A(\alpha)=-\log K(\alpha)
and

k(y)=y^{-1}(1-y)^{(n-3)/2}G(y)I_{(0,1)}(y)
,
with
I_{B}
denoting the indicator function of
B
. This shows that

\{f_{Y}(y;\alpha),\alpha>0\}
defines
a natural exponential family of Lebesgue densities, and thus,
the regularity conditions
are fulfilled.
We calculate
-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha^{2}}\log f_{Y}(y;\alpha)=A^{\prime\prime}(\alpha)

and, therefore,

\displaystyle J_{Y}\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt(-\log K(\alpha))^{\prime\prime}=\left(n\log\Gamma(\alpha)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\log\Gamma(i/n)-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\log\Gamma(\alpha+i/n)\right)^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554ptn\psi_{1}(\alpha)-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\psi_{1}(\alpha+i/n).

It follows that for any unbiased estimator
T(\mbox{\boldmath$X$})
of
h(\alpha)
,

n\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}T(\mbox{\boldmath$X$})\geq n\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}u(Y)\geq\frac{h^{\prime}(\alpha)^{2}}{\psi_{1}(\alpha)-n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\psi_{1}(\alpha+i/n)}.

(2.12)

Since the function
\psi_{1}(\alpha)
is positive (the
\Gamma
function
is log-convex) and decreasing, we have

n^{-1}\psi_{1}(\alpha+i/n)<\int_{(i-1)/n}^{i/n}\psi_{1}(\alpha+t)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}t
,

i=1,\ldots,n
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\psi_{1}(\alpha+i/n)<\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{1}(\alpha+i/n)<\int_{0}^{1}\psi_{1}(\alpha+t)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}t=\int_{\alpha}^{\alpha+1}\psi_{1}(t)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}t

\displaystyle=(\log\Gamma(\alpha+1))^{\prime}-(\log\Gamma(\alpha))^{\prime}=\left(\log\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\right)^{\prime}=(\log\alpha)^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\alpha}.

Thus, excluding the trivial case where

h(\alpha)
is the constant function, the lower bound in (
2.12
)
is strictly larger than the CR bound of (
2.11
).
This means that,
for any given non-constant parametric function
h(\alpha)
,
no efficient estimator exists, when efficiency is defined
in the traditional way, i.e., in terms of attainability
of the bound in (
2.11
). In the contrary,
there are functions of the shape parameter that can be efficiently
estimated in the sense of (
2.12
). More precisely,
it can be checked that for any constants
c_{1},c_{2}
,
the parametric function

h(\alpha)=c_{1}\Big(\psi(\alpha)-n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\psi(\alpha+i/n)\Big)+c_{2}
,
where
\psi(\alpha)=\Gamma^{\prime}(\alpha)/\Gamma(\alpha)
,
is efficiently estimated by
T(\mbox{\boldmath$X$})=u(Y)=(c_{1}/n)\log Y+c_{2}
,
in the sense that
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}T(\mbox{\boldmath$X$})=h(\alpha)

for all
\alpha,\lambda
, and

n\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}T(\mbox{\boldmath$X$})=c_{1}^{2}\Big(\psi_{1}(\alpha)-n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\psi_{1}(\alpha+i/n)\Big)
,
which is equal to the lower bound given by (
2.12
).
Finally, it can be easily verified that all efficiently estimated
functions are of the above form. Notice, however, that
asymptotically the lower bounds do coincide, since,
by the Riemann integral,

\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\psi_{1}(\alpha+i/n)=\int_{\alpha}^{\alpha+1}\psi_{1}(t)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}t=1/\alpha
.

We now state the main result of this section. To the best
of our knowledge, this result, as well as the existence
of
u_{0}
, below, is new.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
,
n\geq 2
, be a random sample
from
\mathcal{G}(\alpha,\lambda)
.
For every
n\geq 4
, the UMVUE of the shape parameter
\alpha
is
given by

u_{0}(Y):=\frac{1}{2}(n-3)\frac{Y}{1-Y}-\frac{YG^{\prime}(Y)}{G(Y)},

(2.13)

where
G
is as in (
2.2
) and
Y^{1/n}

is the ratio of the geometric to the arithmetic mean
of
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
. The estimator
u_{0}(Y)

has finite variance if and only if
n\geq 6
.
Moreover, for
n=2,3
, no unbiased estimator of
\alpha
exists.

Proof:

According to Proposition
2.2
, if an unbiased estimator
(of
\alpha
) exists, the UMVUE must be a function of
Y
, say
u(Y)
.
Then, the relation
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{\alpha}u(Y)=\alpha
can be written as

\frac{1}{K(\alpha)}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{1}y^{\alpha-1}(1-y)^{(n-3)/2}G(y)u(y)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y,\ \ \alpha>0.

Observing that
1/K(\alpha)=\int_{0}^{1}y^{\alpha-1}(1-y)^{(n-3)/2}G(y)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y
and

1/\alpha=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha x}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x
,
the substitution
y=e^{-x}
yields

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha x}(1-e^{-x})^{(n-3)/2}G(e^{-x})\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x=\left\{\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha x}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x\right\}\left\{\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha x}(1-e^{-x})^{(n-3)/2}G(e^{-x})u(e^{-x})\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x\right\},

for all
\alpha>0
. Since we have assumed that

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{\alpha}|u(Y)|<\infty
for all
\alpha>0
, we may apply Fubini’s theorem
to the right-hand side of the above equation, obtaining

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha x}(1-e^{-x})^{(n-3)/2}G(e^{-x})\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha x}\left\{\int_{0}^{x}(1-e^{-t})^{(n-3)/2}G(e^{-t})u(e^{-t})\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}t\right\}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x,\ \ \ \alpha>0.

This relation shows that the Laplace transforms of

f_{1}(x):=(1-e^{-x})^{(n-3)/2}G(e^{-x})
and

f_{2}(x):=\int_{0}^{x}(1-e^{-t})^{(n-3)/2}G(e^{-t})u(e^{-t})\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}t
are identical.
Hence,
f_{1}\equiv f_{2}
(notice that both functions are continuous).
Setting
x=-\log y
we get

(1-y)^{(n-3)/2}G(y)=\int_{0}^{-\log y}(1-e^{-t})^{(n-3)/2}G(e^{-t})u(e^{-t})\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}t=\int_{y}^{1}\frac{1}{x}(1-x)^{(n-3)/2}G(x)u(x)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x,

(2.14)

0<y<1
, and a differentiation of (
2.14
) leads to

-\frac{n-3}{2}(1-y)^{(n-5)/2}G(y)+(1-y)^{(n-3)/2}G^{\prime}(y)=\frac{-u(y)}{y}(1-y)^{(n-3)/2}G(y),\ \ \ \mbox{for almost all}\ y\in(0,1).

Hence, solving for
u
we see that
u(y)=\big((n-3)/2\big)y/(1-y)-yG^{\prime}(y)/G(y)=u_{0}(y)
,
a.e., with
u_{0}
as in (
2.13
). The preceding argument shows that
if an unbiased estimator
T(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
exists then
u_{0}(Y)
must be unbiased,
but

it does not prove
that an unbiased estimator exists
. To see this, let
n=2
.
Then, since
G(y)\equiv 1
when
n=2
,
(
2.14
) reads as

\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-y}}=\int_{y}^{1}\frac{u(x)}{x\sqrt{1-x}}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x.

(2.15)

The assumption
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{\alpha}|u(Y)|<\infty
is equivalent
to
\int_{0}^{1}x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{-1/2}|u(x)|\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x<\infty
for all
\alpha>0
.
Hence,

\left|\int_{y}^{1}\frac{u(x)}{x\sqrt{1-x}}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x\right|\leq\int_{y}^{1}\frac{|u(x)|}{x\sqrt{1-x}}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x=\int_{y}^{1}\frac{x^{\alpha-1}|u(x)|}{\sqrt{1-x}}\frac{1}{x^{\alpha}}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x\leq\frac{1}{y^{\alpha}}\int_{y}^{1}\frac{x^{\alpha-1}|u(x)|}{\sqrt{1-x}}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x\to 0,

as

y\to 1-
, and this contradicts (
2.15
).
When
n=3
, it is easy to see that the function
G
of (
2.2
)
is given by

G(y)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(3m)!}{(3^{m}m!)^{3}}(1-y)^{m},\ \ \ 0<y<1.

Hence,
G(1-)=1
. Since
(1-y)^{(n-3)/2}\equiv 1
, assuming

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{\alpha}|u(Y)|<\infty
we get

\int_{0}^{1}x^{\alpha-1}G(x)|u(x)|\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x<\infty
,

\alpha>0
. Therefore,

\left|\int_{y}^{1}\frac{G(x)}{x}u(x)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x\right|\leq\int_{y}^{1}\frac{G(x)}{x}|u(x)|\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x=\int_{y}^{1}\frac{x^{\alpha-1}G(x)|u(x)|}{x^{\alpha}}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x\leq\frac{1}{y^{\alpha}}\int_{y}^{1}x^{\alpha-1}G(x)|u(x)|\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x\to 0,

as

y\to 1-
.
This contradicts (
2.14
), i.e.,

G(y)=\int_{y}^{1}\frac{1}{x}G(x)u(x)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x,

since the left-hand side of the above equation approaches
1
as
y\to 1-
.

The preceding argument shows that there is no unbiased estimator when

n\leq 3
. For
n\geq 4
, however, the situation is completely
different: The (positive)
estimator
u_{0}(Y)
of (
2.13
) has finite expectation for all
\alpha>0
and,
indeed,
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{\alpha}u_{0}(Y)=\alpha
. We now proceed to verify this claim.
First observe that

-G^{\prime}(y)>0
so that
u_{0}(y)>0
for
0<y<1
. Noting that

G^{\prime}(y)=-\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}md_{m}(1-y)^{m-1}
,
we calculate

\displaystyle\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{\alpha}u_{0}(Y)

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt=\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle K(\alpha)\int_{0}^{1}y^{\alpha}(1-y)^{(n-5)/2}\left(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{n-3}{2}+m\right)d_{m}(1-y)^{m}\right)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt=\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle K(\alpha)\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{n-3}{2}+m\right)d_{m}\int_{0}^{1}y^{\alpha}(1-y)^{m+(n-5)/2}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt=\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)K(\alpha)\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(m+(n-1)/2)}{\Gamma(\alpha+m+(n-1)/2)}d_{m},

where, since
d_{m}>0
, the
interchanging of summation and integration is justified by
Beppo Levi’s theorem. It remains to verify that

S:=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(m+(n-1)/2)}{\Gamma(\alpha+m+(n-1)/2)}d_{m}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)K(\alpha)},\ \ \ \alpha>0.

(2.16)

From the definition of
d_{m}=\gamma_{n-1}(m)
we have (set
i=n-2
in (
2.6
))

d_{m}=\gamma_{n-1}(m)=\frac{\Gamma(m+(n-1)(n-2)/(2n))}{\Gamma(m+(n-1)/2)}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{\Gamma(m-k+(n-1)/n)}{(m-k)!}\gamma_{n-2}(k).

Substituting this expression to the series in (
2.16
)
and changing the order of summation according to Tonelli’s theorem,
we obtain

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt=\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{n-2}(k)\sum_{m=k}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(m+(n-1)(n-2)/(2n))\ \Gamma(m-k+(n-1)/n)}{(m-k)!\ \Gamma(\alpha+m+(n-1)/2)}

(2.17)

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt=\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{n-2}(k)\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(m+k+(n-1)(n-2)/(2n))\ \Gamma(m+(n-1)/n)}{m!\ \Gamma(m+\alpha+k+(n-1)/2)}

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt=\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\ \Gamma((n-1)/n)}{\Gamma(\alpha+(n-1)/n)}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(k+(n-1)(n-2)/(2n))}{\Gamma(\alpha+k+(n-1)(n-2)/(2n))}\gamma_{n-2}(k),

where for the last equality we applied the identity

\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(m+\rho_{1})\ \Gamma(m+\rho_{2})}{m!\ \Gamma(m+\rho+\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})}=\frac{\Gamma(\rho)\ \Gamma(\rho_{1})\ \Gamma(\rho_{2})}{\Gamma(\rho+\rho_{1})\ \Gamma(\rho+\rho_{2})},\ \ \ \ \ \rho,\ \rho_{1},\ \rho_{2}>0,

with
\rho=\alpha
,
\rho_{1}=k+(n-1)(n-2)/(2n)
,
\rho_{2}=(n-1)/n
.
Since the function
f_{Y_{1}}(y;\alpha)
in (
2.7
) is a density, we have
(see (
2.8
))

\frac{1}{K_{1}(\alpha)}=\int_{0}^{1}y^{\alpha-1}(1-y)^{(n-1)(n-2)/(2n)-1}G_{1}(y)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{n-2}(k)\int_{0}^{1}y^{\alpha-1}(1-y)^{(n-1)(n-2)/(2n)+k-1}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y,

and hence,

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(k+(n-1)(n-2)/(2n))}{\Gamma(\alpha+k+(n-1)(n-2)/(2n))}\gamma_{n-2}(k)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)K_{1}(\alpha)}.

Using the fact that

K_{1}(\alpha)=K(\alpha)\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma((n-1)/n)/\Gamma(\alpha+(n-1)/n)
and substituting the above equality in (
2.17
) we conclude that

S=\frac{\Gamma((n-1)/n)}{\Gamma(\alpha+(n-1)/n)\ K_{1}(\alpha)}=\frac{\Gamma((n-1)/n)\ \Gamma(\alpha+(n-1)/n)}{K(\alpha)\ \Gamma(\alpha)\ \Gamma((n-1)/n)\ \Gamma(\alpha+(n-1)/n)}=\frac{1}{K(\alpha)\Gamma(\alpha)};

this verifies (
2.16
) and the claim is proved.
Thus, for
n\geq 4
,

u_{0}(Y)
is the UMVUE of the parametric function
h(\alpha)=\alpha
.

We shall now investigate the finiteness of the variance of the UMVUE.
Assume first that
n\geq 6
.
From the inequality

u_{0}(y)^{2}\leq 2c^{2}y^{2}/(1-y)^{2}+2y^{2}|G^{\prime}(y)|^{2}/G(y)^{2}
, where
c=(n-3)/2
,
we see that
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{\alpha}u_{0}(Y)^{2}<\infty
if both integrals

\int_{0}^{1}y^{\alpha+1}(1-y)^{(n-7)/2}G(y)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y\ \ \ \mbox{and}\ \ \ \int_{0}^{1}y^{\alpha+1}(1-y)^{(n-3)/2}\ \frac{|G^{\prime}(y)|^{2}}{G(y)}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y

are finite. The integrands are continuous functions in
(0,1)
, hence,
it suffices to verify their integrability in a neighborhood
of the endpoints. Obviously,

\int_{1-\epsilon}^{1}y^{\alpha+1}(1-y)^{(n-7)/2}G(y)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y<\infty\ \ \ \mbox{and}\ \ \ \int_{1-\epsilon}^{1}y^{\alpha+1}(1-y)^{(n-3)/2}\ \frac{|G^{\prime}(y)|^{2}}{G(y)}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y<\infty,

since
n\geq 6
,
\lim_{y\to 1-}y^{\alpha+1}G(y)=d_{0}

and
\lim_{y\to 1-}y^{\alpha+1}|G^{\prime}(y)|^{2}/G(y)=d_{1}^{2}/d_{0}
,
so that, close to
y=1
, the first integrand behaves like
(1-y)^{(n-7)/2}

and the second one like
(1-y)^{(n-3)/2}
.
Regarding the behavior of the first integral close to
y=0

it suffices to observe that

G(y)
is positive
and
\int_{0}^{1}y^{\alpha-1}(1-y)^{(n-3)/2}G(y)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y=1/K(\alpha)<\infty
;
see (
2.1
).
Then, we trivially have

\int_{0}^{\delta}y^{\alpha+1}(1-y)^{(n-7)/2}G(y)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y<(1-\delta)^{-2}\int_{0}^{\delta}y^{\alpha-1}(1-y)^{(n-3)/2}G(y)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y<\infty
.
It remains to verify that

\int_{0}^{\delta}y^{\alpha+1}(1-y)^{(n-3)/2}\ |G^{\prime}(y)|^{2}/G(y)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y<\infty
, and since
1/G(y)<1/d_{0}
and

(1-y)^{(n-3)/2}<1
,
it suffices to show that for some
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\int_{0}^{\delta}y^{\alpha+1}|G^{\prime}(y)|^{2}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y<\infty.

(2.18)

Recall that
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{\alpha}u_{0}(Y)<\infty
; since

u_{0}(y)>y|G^{\prime}(y)|/G(y)
, it follows that

\int_{0}^{1}y^{\alpha}(1-y)^{(n-3)/2}|G^{\prime}(y)|\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y<\infty,

and thus,

\int_{0}^{1/2}y^{\alpha}|G^{\prime}(y)|\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y<\infty
.
The function
|G^{\prime}(y)|=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}md_{m}(1-y)^{m-1}

is strictly decreasing in
(0,1)

and therefore, with
c_{\alpha}=(\alpha+1)/(1-2^{-\alpha-1})>0
,
we get

y^{\alpha+1}|G^{\prime}(y)|=c_{\alpha}\ |G^{\prime}(y)|\int_{y/2}^{y}x^{\alpha}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x<c_{\alpha}\int_{y/2}^{y}x^{\alpha}|G^{\prime}(x)|\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x<c_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{y}x^{\alpha}|G^{\prime}(x)|\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x\to 0,

as
y\to 0+
, by dominated convergence.
Hence,
\lim_{y\to 0+}y^{\alpha+1}|G^{\prime}(y)|=0
(for all
\alpha>0
).
Thus, for every
\epsilon>0
we can find
\delta=\delta(\alpha,\epsilon)>0

such that
\big(y^{\alpha/4+1}|G^{\prime}(y)|\big)^{2}<\epsilon^{2}
for

y\in(0,\delta)
. Then, for this value of
\delta
,

\int_{0}^{\delta}y^{\alpha+1}|G^{\prime}(y)|^{2}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y=\int_{0}^{\delta}y^{\alpha/2-1}\Big(y^{\alpha/4+1}|G^{\prime}(y)|\Big)^{2}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y<\epsilon^{2}\int_{0}^{\delta}y^{\alpha/2-1}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y<\infty,

and (
2.18
) is proved. We thus conclude that the variance of
u_{0}(Y)

is finite for all
n\geq 6
and
\alpha>0
.

Finally, assume that
n=4
or
5
.
Since
u_{0}(y)=cy/(1-y)+y|G^{\prime}(y)|/G(y)>cy/(1-y)
,
c=(n-3)/2>0
,
and
G(y)>d_{0}>0
, the variance of the UMVUE is infinite:

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{\alpha}u_{0}(Y)^{2}>c^{2}K(\alpha)\int_{0}^{1}y^{\alpha+1}(1-y)^{(n-7)/2}G(y)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y>c^{2}K(\alpha)d_{0}\int_{0}^{1}y^{\alpha+1}(1-y)^{(n-7)/2}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y=\infty

for all
\alpha>0
; this completes the proof of the theorem.

Corollary 2.1
.

For
n\geq 4
, the UMVUE of the rate parameter (reciprocal scale parameter)
\lambda
is given by

u_{1}(X,Y)=\frac{u_{0}(Y)-1/n}{\overline{X}}=\frac{n\ u_{0}(Y)-1}{X},\ \ \ \ n>1/\alpha,

where
u_{0}(Y)
is as in (
2.13
) and

\overline{X}=X/n=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
.
For
n\geq 6
, the estimator
u_{1}(X,Y)

has finite variance if and only if
\alpha>2/n
and
\lambda>0
,
while for
n=4
or
5
its variance is infinite. Finally,
if either
n\in\{2,3\}
or
n\geq 4
and
0<\alpha\leq n^{-1}
,
no unbiased estimator
for
\lambda
exists.

Proof:

By definition,
u_{1}
is a function of the complete, sufficient
statistic
(X,Y)
. Since
X,Y
are independent and

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}X^{-1}=\lambda/(n\alpha-1)
,
n>\alpha^{-1}
,
we obtain

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}u_{1}(X,Y)=\Big(n\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}u_{0}(Y)-1\Big)\left(\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}X^{-1}\right)=\lambda
,
and
u_{1}(X,Y)
is, indeed, the UMVUE, according to Definition

2.1
. Let
n=4
or
5
.
Since
u_{1}(x,y)^{2}=x^{-2}(n^{2}u_{0}(y)^{2}-2nu_{0}(y)+1)

and
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}u_{0}(Y)^{2}=\infty
, it follows
that

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}\Big(n^{2}u_{0}(Y)^{2}-2nu_{0}(Y)+1\Big)=\infty

and, thus, by the independence of
X,Y
,

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}u_{1}(X,Y)^{2}=\left(\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}X^{-2}\right)\left(\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}\Big(n^{2}u_{0}(Y)^{2}-2nu_{0}(Y)+1\Big)\right)=\infty
.
Next, assume
n\geq 6
. In this case,

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}u_{1}(X,Y)^{2}\leq\left(\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}X^{-2}\right)\left(1+n^{2}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}u_{0}(Y)^{2}\right)
,
and since
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}u_{0}(Y)^{2}<\infty
, we obtain

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}u_{1}(X,Y)^{2}<\infty
if and only if

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}X^{-2}<\infty
; equivalently,
\alpha>2n^{-1}
.

Assume now that
n\geq 2
,
0<\alpha\leq n^{-1}
,
and set
\theta:=n\alpha\in(0,1]

for fixed
\alpha
. If for this particular value
of
\alpha
the estimator
w(X,Y)

were unbiased for
\lambda>0
, then we could find
an interval

I=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
,
0\leq\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\leq\infty
, such
that

\lambda=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{\theta}}{\Gamma(\theta)}x^{\theta-1}e^{-\lambda x}\delta(x)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x,\ \ \ \ \lambda\in I,

(2.19)

where

\delta(x):=K(\theta/n)\int_{0}^{1}y^{\theta/n-1}(1-y)^{(n-3)/2}g(y)w(x,y)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y
. Certainly, the function
w
is assumed integrable,
that is,
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\theta,\lambda)}|w(X,Y)|<\infty
,
\lambda\in I
. Hence,

\delta(x)
is a.e. finite, and we can redefine it, if necessary, to
be finite for all
x>0
. Notice that the assumption

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\theta,\lambda)}|w(X,Y)|<\infty

for
\lambda\in I
shows that

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda x}x^{\theta-1}|\delta(x)|\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\lambda x}x^{\theta-1}y^{\theta/n-1}(1-y)^{(n-3)/2}g(y)|w(x,y)|\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x<\infty,\ \ \ \lambda\in I.

We now rewrite (
2.19
)
as

\frac{\Gamma(\theta)}{\lambda^{\theta}}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda x}x^{\theta-1}\delta(x)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x,\ \ \ \lambda\in I.

The left-hand side of the above equation equals to

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda x}x^{\theta-1}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x
, while
its right-hand side can be written as

\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda x_{2}}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x_{2}\right)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda x_{1}}x_{1}^{\theta-1}\delta(x_{1})\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x_{1}\right)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda x}\left\{\int_{0}^{x}t^{\theta-1}\delta(t)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}t\right\}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x,

from Fubini’s theorem. Therefore, we conclude that

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda x}x^{\theta-1}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda x}\left\{\int_{0}^{x}t^{\theta-1}\delta(t)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}t\right\}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x,\ \ \ \lambda\in I,

and Lemma
2.1
shows that

x^{\theta-1}=\int_{0}^{x}t^{\theta-1}\delta(t)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}t
, a.e. in
(0,\infty)
.
Hence, the only possibility is
\delta(x)=(\theta-1)/x
, a.e., and
since
\delta(x)\leq 0
, this contradicts (
2.19
).
Given
n=3
observations from
\mathcal{G}(\alpha,\lambda)
,
it can be seen (we omit the details) that the relation

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}w(X,Y)=\lambda
for all
\alpha\in(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
,

\lambda\in(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
[with
\alpha_{1}\geq 1/3
,
\lambda_{1}\geq 0
]
implies
w(x,y)=x^{-1}(y|G^{\prime}(y)|/G(y)-1)
, a.e. in
(0,\infty)^{2}
.
Since
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}X^{-1}=\lambda/(3\alpha-1)
, this estimator
would be unbiased for
\lambda
if and only if

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{\alpha}Y|G^{\prime}(Y)|/G(Y)=3\alpha
;
however, we showed in Theorem
2.1
that this is impossible.
Hence, there is no unbiased estimator for
\lambda
when
n=3
, and therefore,
for
n\leq 3
, too.

Several authors use the scale parameter
\beta=1/\lambda
in defining
the Gamma density, that is,

f(x;\alpha,\beta)=\frac{x^{\alpha-1}}{\beta^{\alpha}\Gamma(\alpha)}e^{-x/\beta},\ \ \ x>0.

We now proceed to verify that the function
G
in (
2.2
)
also appears
in the form of the UMVUE of both
1/\alpha
and
1/\lambda
.

Theorem 2.2
.

For
n\geq 2
, the UMVUE of
1/\alpha
is given by

u_{2}(Y):=\frac{(1-Y)^{-(n-3)/2}}{G(Y)}\int_{Y}^{1}\frac{1}{x}(1-x)^{(n-3)/2}G(x)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x,

with
Y
and
G
as in Theorem
2.1
; the UMVUE
of
1/\lambda
is given by

u_{3}(X,Y):=\overline{X}u_{2}(Y)=Xu_{2}(Y)/n.

Proof:
It suffices to show that
u_{2}
and
u_{3}
are unbiased.
For
u_{2}
we have

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{\alpha}u_{2}(Y)=K(\alpha)\int_{0}^{1}y^{\alpha-1}\left\{\int_{y}^{1}\frac{1}{x}(1-x)^{(n-3)/2}G(x)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x\right\}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}y=\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{1}f_{Y}(x;\alpha)\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x=\frac{1}{\alpha},

where the change in the order of integration is justified by Tonelli’s theorem.
The result for
u_{3}
is obvious, since

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}\overline{X}=\alpha/\lambda
and the random
variables
\overline{X}
,
Y
are independent.

Remark 2.2
.

(Alternative derivation of the closed-form Ye-Chen-type
estimators, and their asymptotic efficiency).
For
n=2
,
u_{2}
and
u_{3}
admit closed forms because
G(y)\equiv 1
.
More specifically, let
X_{1},X_{2}
be independently distributed
\mathcal{G}(\alpha,\lambda)

random variables, so that
\overline{X}=(X_{1}+X_{2})/2
and
Y=4X_{1}X_{2}/(X_{1}+X_{2})^{2}
.
Then,

u_{2}(Y)=\sqrt{1-Y}\int_{Y}^{1}\frac{1}{x\sqrt{1-x}}\hskip 0.43057pt\textrm{$\rm{d}$}x=\sqrt{1-Y}\left(2\log\left(1+\sqrt{1-Y}\right)-\log Y\right).

Therefore, after some algebra, one can verify that the unbiased estimators
u_{2}

and
u_{3}
(when
n=2
) can be written as

\displaystyle u_{2}(Y)=T_{2}(X_{1},X_{2})=\frac{(X_{2}-X_{1})(\log X_{2}-\log X_{1})}{X_{1}+X_{2}},

\displaystyle u_{3}(X,Y)=T_{3}(X_{1},X_{2})=\frac{1}{2}(X_{2}-X_{1})(\log X_{2}-\log X_{1}).

From the above form of
u_{3}=T_{3}
it follows that

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}T_{3}(X_{1},X_{2})=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptCov\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}(X_{1},\log X_{1})
.
The well-known Stein-type identity for the Gamma distribution –
see, e.g., Afendras
et al.
(2011) – states
that for any absolutely continuous function
w:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
,

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptCov\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}(X_{1},w(X_{1}))=\frac{1}{\lambda}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha,\lambda)}X_{1}w^{\prime}(X_{1}),

provided
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}_{(\alpha.\lambda)}X_{1}|w^{\prime}(X_{1})|<\infty
. It is now clear that,
if we apply Stein’s identity to
w(x)=\log x
, we conclude
the unbiasedness of
u_{3}
. Consider a random sample

X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
of size
n\geq 2
from any distribution; then,
as is well-known, under suitable moment conditions,
an unbiased estimator of
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptCov\hskip 0.86108pt}(X_{1},w(X_{1}))
is given by the
corresponding
U
-statistic with kernel

K(X_{1},X_{2})=(X_{2}-X_{1})(w(X_{2})-w(X_{1}))/2
, that is,

U_{n}:=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i<j}K(X_{i},X_{j})=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\ w(X_{i})-\frac{n}{n-1}\overline{X}\ \overline{w}(X),

where
\overline{X}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
,

\overline{w}(X)=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w(X_{i})
.
With
w(x)=\log x

we obtain the highly efficient unbiased estimator of the scale parameter

1/\lambda
,

\widehat{\lambda^{-1}}(n)=U_{n}=\frac{1}{n-1}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\log X_{i}-\overline{X}\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\log X_{i}\right\},

introduced by Ye and Chen (2017) (see their Theorem 4.1),
using generalized
likelihood equations. From the well-known asymptotic theory
of
U
-statistics, Hoeffding (1948),
we have
n\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}U_{n}\to 4\zeta_{1}^{2}
and

\sqrt{n}(U_{n}-\mu)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny d}}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,4\zeta_{1}^{2})
, as
n\to\infty
, where

\mu=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}K(X_{1},X_{2})
,
\zeta_{1}^{2}=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}(K(X_{1},X_{2})|X_{2})
.
Therefore, with
K=T_{3}

(equivalently,
w(x)=\log x
) we obtain

\sqrt{n}\left(\widehat{\lambda^{-1}}(n)-\lambda^{-1}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny d}}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\Big(0,\big(1+\alpha\psi_{1}(\alpha)\big)/\lambda^{2}\Big),\ \ \ \mbox{as }\ n\to\infty,

in accordance to Theorem 3.2 in Ye and Chen (2017), since
1/\lambda=\beta
.
Hence, the asymptotic
efficiency of the estimator
\widehat{\lambda^{-1}}(n)

(compared to the MLE, see Remark
2.1
) is given by
\operatorname{ARE}_{\widehat{\lambda^{-1}}(n)}(\alpha,\lambda)=\psi_{1}(\alpha)/(\alpha^{2}\psi_{1}(\alpha)^{2}-1)
, and it is independent
of
\lambda
. Numerical calculations show that the asymptotic efficiency is greater
than
96.4\%
for all values of
\alpha>0
(the minimum of the function

\psi_{1}(\alpha)/(\alpha^{2}\psi_{1}(\alpha)^{2}-1)
is attained
at
\alpha=\alpha^{*}=.920835\ldots
). Applying the delta-method (using
the mapping
x\mapsto x^{-1}
) we immediately obtain
the corresponding result for the rate parameter

\lambda
, namely,

\sqrt{n}\left(1/\widehat{\lambda^{-1}}(n)-\lambda\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny d}}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,(1+\alpha\psi_{1}(\alpha))\lambda^{2})
,
n\to\infty
.
Certainly, the asymptotic efficiency is the same:

\operatorname{ARE}_{1/\widehat{\lambda^{-1}}(n)}(\alpha,\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{2}\psi_{1}(\alpha)/(\alpha\psi_{1}(\alpha)-1)}{(1+\alpha\psi_{1}(\alpha))\lambda^{2}}=\frac{\psi_{1}(\alpha)}{\alpha^{2}\psi_{1}(\alpha)^{2}-1}>0.964.

In a similar fashion, a closed-form unbiased estimator for
1/\alpha
can
be constructed as an
U
-statistic with kernel
K(X_{1},X_{2})=T_{2}(X_{1},X_{2})
.
However, it is difficult to evaluate the asymptotic variance of the
resulting estimator, because
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}(T_{2}(X_{1},X_{2})|X_{2})
is intractable.
Ye and Chen proposed a different unbiased estimator for
\alpha^{-1}
,
which is equal to the UMVUE if and only if
n=2
, namely

\widehat{\alpha^{-1}}(n)=\frac{1}{n-1}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\log X_{i}-\overline{X}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log X_{i}}{\overline{X}}.

From Theorem 3.2
in Ye and Chen (2017) it follows that

\sqrt{n}\left(1/\widehat{\alpha^{-1}}(n)-\alpha\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny d}}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\alpha v(\alpha)\right)

and

\sqrt{n}\left(\widehat{\alpha^{-1}}(n)-\alpha^{-1}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny d}}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\alpha^{-3}v(\alpha)\right)
,
as
n\to\infty
,
with
v(\alpha)=\alpha^{2}\psi_{1}(\alpha)+\alpha-1
.
Therefore, both closed-form estimators
\widehat{\alpha^{-1}}(n)
(for
1/\alpha
)
and
1/\widehat{\alpha^{-1}}(n)
(for
\alpha
) have asymptotic efficiency
(compared to the MLE – see Remark
2.1
) given by

\rho(\alpha)=\big[(\alpha\psi_{1}(\alpha)-1)(\alpha^{2}\psi_{1}(\alpha)+\alpha-1)\big]^{-1}
.
Numerical computations show that
\rho(\alpha)\geq\rho(\alpha^{*})=.9281\ldots

for all
\alpha>0
(where
\alpha^{*}=.41541\ldots
), hence,
the asymptotic efficiency is greater then
92.8\%
.
It should be noted, however, that, due to Jensen’s inequality,
both estimators

1/\widehat{\lambda^{-1}}(n)
(for
\lambda
) and

1/\widehat{\alpha^{-1}}(n)
(for
\alpha
) are
positively biased.

The present powerful estimation procedure of Ye and Chen was further analyzed
by Louzada
et al.
(2019),
showing that the bias-corrected estimator

\widehat{\alpha}_{n}=\frac{n-3}{(n-1)\widehat{\alpha^{-1}}(n)}+\frac{2}{5\left(n+(n-1)\widehat{\alpha^{-1}}(n)\right)}\ \Bigg(\frac{11}{3}-\frac{2n}{n+(n-1)\widehat{\alpha^{-1}}(n)}\Bigg)

improves upon the estimator
1/\widehat{\alpha^{-1}}(n)
of
\alpha

for small values of
n
.

3
Closed form estimators for the two-parameter Beta distribution

Through the present section we consider a random sample
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}

(
n\geq 2
) from the
Beta
distribution with density as in (
1.1
),
where the parameter
{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}=(\alpha,\beta)\in(0,\infty)^{2}
.
The Fisher information matrix (based on a single observation)
is given by

\displaystyle J=J(\alpha,\beta)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\psi_{1}(\alpha)-\psi_{1}(\alpha+\beta)&-\psi_{1}(\alpha+\beta)\\
-\psi_{1}(\alpha+\beta)&\psi_{1}(\beta)-\psi_{1}(\alpha+\beta)\end{array}\right),\mbox{ with inverse }

\displaystyle J^{-1}=\frac{1}{\psi_{1}(\alpha)\psi_{1}(\beta)-\psi_{1}(\alpha+\beta)(\psi_{1}(\alpha)+\psi_{1}(\beta))}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\psi_{1}(\beta)-\psi_{1}(\alpha+\beta)&\psi_{1}(\alpha+\beta)\\
\psi_{1}(\alpha+\beta)&\psi_{1}(\alpha)-\psi_{1}(\alpha+\beta)\end{array}\right),

with
\psi_{1}(x)=\left(\log\Gamma(x)\right)^{\prime\prime}
, as in Section
2
.
The complete sufficient statistic has now the form
(X,Y)=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-X_{i})\right)
, and an extra difficulty,
compared to the Gamma-case,
is due to the lack of independence of
X
and
Y
.

The proposed Ye-Chen-type (biased) estimators for
\alpha
and
\beta

are as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{n}=\frac{n\overline{X}_{n}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\log(X_{i}/(1-X_{i}))-\overline{X}_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(X_{i}/(1-X_{i}))},

(3.3)

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}_{n}=\frac{n(1-\overline{X}_{n})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\log(X_{i}/(1-X_{i}))-\overline{X}_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(X_{i}/(1-X_{i}))},

(3.4)

where
\overline{X}_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
.
In order to make clear how these estimators have been derived, we recall
the Stein-type covariance identity for the Beta distribution,

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptCov\hskip 0.86108pt}(X,w(X))=\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}X(1-X)w^{\prime}(X),

which holds true for sufficiently smooth functions, namely, when
w:(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}

is absolutely continuous and its derivative,
w^{\prime}
, satisfies
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}X(1-X)|w^{\prime}(X)|<\infty
;
see, e.g., Afendras
et al.
(2011). The special choice

w(x)=\log(x/(1-x))
leads to
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptCov\hskip 0.86108pt}(X,w(X))=1/(\alpha+\beta)
. Therefore,
the
U
-statistic
U_{n}
with kernel

K(X_{1},X_{2})=\frac{1}{2}(X_{2}-X_{1})\log\frac{X_{2}(1-X_{1})}{X_{1}(1-X_{2})}

is an unbiased estimator of the parameter
1/(\alpha+\beta)
. One finds

U_{n}=\frac{2\sum_{i<j}K(X_{i},X_{j})}{n(n-1)}=\frac{1}{n-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\log(X_{i}/(1-X_{i}))-\overline{X}_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(X_{i}/(1-X_{i}))\right)=\frac{nV_{n}}{n-1},

say. From the SLLN,

V_{n}\to\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptCov\hskip 0.86108pt}(X,\log(X/(1-X)))=1/(\alpha+\beta)
, a.s., as
n\to\infty
; thus,

1/V_{n}\to\alpha+\beta
, a.s. Since, obviously,

\overline{X}_{n}\to\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)
and
1-\overline{X}_{n}\to\beta/(\alpha+\beta)
,
we conclude that
\widehat{\alpha}_{n}=\overline{X}_{n}/V_{n}\to\alpha

and
\widehat{\beta}_{n}=(1-\overline{X}_{n})/V_{n}\to\beta
, a.s., so both estimators
are strongly consistent.
In order to investigate their asymptotic efficiency, we have to calculate
explicitly the variance of their asymptotic normal distribution.
This is the subject of the following

Theorem 3.1
.

The joint limiting distribution of
\widehat{\alpha}_{n}
,
\widehat{\beta}_{n}

(see (
3.3
), (
3.4
)) is normal. More precisely, as
n\to\infty
,

\sqrt{n}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\widehat{\alpha}_{n}-\alpha\\
\widehat{\beta}_{n}-\beta\end{array}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny d}}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{N}({\mbox{\boldmath$0$}},\Delta),\ \ \ \mbox{where}\ \ \ \Delta=\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta+1}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}v_{11}(\alpha,\beta)&v_{12}(\alpha,\beta)\\
v_{12}(\alpha,\beta)&v_{22}(\alpha,\beta)\end{array}\right)

(3.5)

with

\displaystyle v_{11}(\alpha,\beta)\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt\alpha\left(\alpha(\alpha+\beta+1)+\alpha^{2}\beta\ \Big[\psi_{1}(\alpha)+\psi_{1}(\beta)\Big]-\beta\right),

\displaystyle v_{12}(\alpha,\beta)\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt\alpha^{2}\beta+\alpha\beta^{2}+\alpha^{2}\beta^{2}\ \Big[\psi_{1}(\alpha)+\psi_{1}(\beta)\Big]-\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2},

\displaystyle v_{22}(\alpha,\beta)\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt\beta\left(\beta(\alpha+\beta+1)+\alpha\beta^{2}\ \Big[\psi_{1}(\alpha)+\psi_{1}(\beta)\Big]-\alpha\right).

Proof:
The result is quite simple,
since it is a by-product of the CLT and the delta-method.
However, the calculations are somewhat involved.
We define the random variables
Y_{i}=\log(X_{i}/(1-X_{i}))

and
Z_{i}=X_{i}\log(X_{i}/(1-X_{i}))
. It follows after some
algebra that the mean vector and the dispersion matrix
of
(X_{i},Y_{i},Z_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, are given by

{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mu_{1}\\
\mu_{2}\\
\mu_{3}\end{array}\right)=\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha\\
(\alpha+\beta)(\psi(\alpha)-\psi(\beta))\\
1+\alpha(\psi(\alpha)-\psi(\beta))\end{array}\right),\ \ \ \ \ \ \Sigma=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\sigma_{11}&\sigma_{12}&\sigma_{13}\\
\sigma_{12}&\sigma_{22}&\sigma_{23}\\
\sigma_{13}&\sigma_{23}&\sigma_{33}\end{array}\right),

where, with
\Psi=\psi(\alpha)-\psi(\beta)
(recall
\psi(x)=\left(\log\Gamma(x)\right)^{\prime}
)
and
\Psi_{1}=\psi_{1}(\alpha)+\psi_{1}(\beta)
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{11}\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt\frac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha+\beta)^{2}(\alpha+\beta+1)},

\displaystyle\sigma_{12}\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta},

\displaystyle\sigma_{13}\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt\frac{\beta+\alpha(\alpha+\beta)+\alpha\beta\ \Psi}{(\alpha+\beta)^{2}(\alpha+\beta+1)},

\displaystyle\sigma_{22}\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt\Psi_{1},\begin{array}[]{c}\\
\frac{}{}\end{array}

\displaystyle\sigma_{23}\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt\frac{\Psi+\alpha\Psi_{1}}{\alpha+\beta},

\displaystyle\sigma_{33}\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt\frac{\alpha\beta\ \Psi^{2}+2(\alpha^{2}+\alpha\beta+\beta)\Psi+\alpha(\alpha+1)(\alpha+\beta)\Psi_{1}+\alpha+\beta-1}{(\alpha+\beta)^{2}(\alpha+\beta+1)}.

Hence, from the classical CLT,

\sqrt{n}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\overline{X}_{n}-\mu_{1}\\
\overline{Y}_{n}-\mu_{2}\\
\overline{Z}_{n}-\mu_{3}\end{array}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny d}}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{N}({\mbox{\boldmath$0$}},\Sigma),\ \ \ \mbox{as }\ n\to\infty,

where
\overline{X}_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
,
\overline{Y}_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}
,

\overline{Z}_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}
.
Consider the functions
k_{1}(x,y,z)=x/(z-xy)
,
k_{2}(x,y,z)=(1-x)/(z-xy)
,
so that
k_{1}(\overline{X}_{n},\overline{Y}_{n},\overline{Z}_{n})=\widehat{\alpha}_{n}
and

k_{2}(\overline{X}_{n},\overline{Y}_{n},\overline{Z}_{n})=\widehat{\beta}_{n}
.
The Jacobian matrix is

A(x,y,z):=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}k_{1}(x,y,z)&\frac{\partial}{\partial y}k_{1}(x,y,z)&\frac{\partial}{\partial z}k_{1}(x,y,z)\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}k_{2}(x,y,z)&\frac{\partial}{\partial y}k_{2}(x,y,z)&\frac{\partial}{\partial z}k_{2}(x,y,z)\end{array}\right)=\frac{1}{(xy-z)^{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}z&x^{2}&-x\\
y-z&x-x^{2}&x-1\end{array}\right).

Hence, with
A_{0}:=A(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3})
, a straightforward application
of the delta-method yields

\sqrt{n}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}k_{1}(\overline{X}_{n},\overline{Y}_{n},\overline{Z}_{n})-k_{1}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3})\\
k_{2}(\overline{X}_{n},\overline{Y}_{n},\overline{Z}_{n})-k_{2}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3})\end{array}\right)=\sqrt{n}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\widehat{\alpha}_{n}-\alpha\\
\widehat{\beta}_{n}-\beta\end{array}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny d}}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{N}({\mbox{\boldmath$0$}},A_{0}\Sigma A_{0}^{T}),\ \ \ \mbox{as }\ \ n\to\infty,

because, obviously,

k_{1}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3})=\alpha
and
k_{2}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3})=\beta
.
Finally, a lengthy computation reveals that

A_{0}\Sigma A_{0}^{T}=\Delta
as in (
3.5
),
completing the proof.

For any unbiased estimator
U
of
h(\alpha,\beta)
(provided that
such an estimator exists),
the CR-bound yields the inequality

n\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}U\geq\nabla h(\alpha,\beta)^{T}J^{-1}\nabla h(\alpha,\beta)
; in particular, with
h(\alpha,\beta)=\alpha

we get

n\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}U\geq\frac{\psi_{1}(\beta)-\psi_{1}(\alpha+\beta)}{\psi_{1}(\alpha)\psi_{1}(\beta)-\psi_{1}(\alpha+\beta)(\psi_{1}(\alpha)+\psi_{1}(\beta))},

and the right-hand side is the asymptotic variance of the MLE of
\alpha
,
which, as is well known, does not admit a closed form.
For
h(\alpha,\beta)=\beta
we obtain a similar inequality,
interchanging the roles of
\alpha
and
\beta
. Hence, from
Theorem
3.1
,
the asymptotic efficiency
\rho_{1}=\rho_{1}(\alpha,\beta)

of
\widehat{\alpha}_{n}
is given by

\rho_{1}=\frac{(\alpha+\beta+1)(\psi_{1}(\beta)-\psi_{1}(\alpha+\beta))/\alpha}{\Big(\alpha(\alpha+\beta+1)+\alpha^{2}\beta\ \big[\psi_{1}(\alpha)+\psi_{1}(\beta)\big]-\beta\Big)\Big(\psi_{1}(\alpha)\psi_{1}(\beta)-\psi_{1}(\alpha+\beta)(\psi_{1}(\alpha)+\psi_{1}(\beta))\Big)},

while the asymptotic efficiency of
\widehat{\beta}_{n}
equals to

\rho_{2}(\alpha,\beta)=\rho_{1}(\beta,\alpha)
.
Despite the fact that the function
\rho_{1}
is too complicated to enable
a complete study, we can report some properties supported by
numerical evidence. For fixed
\beta>0
, define
\phi(\alpha)=\rho_{1}(\alpha,\beta)
.
The function
\phi
has limits
1
, as
\alpha\to 0+
, and

\psi_{1}(\beta)/(\beta^{2}\psi_{1}(\beta)^{2}-1)>.964
, as
\alpha\to+\infty
;
see Remark
2.2
. Hence, either the infimum
equals to the limit at
+\infty
, or it is attained at some point

\alpha^{*}=\alpha^{*}(\beta)
such that
\phi^{\prime}(\alpha^{*})=0
.
Several plots showed that the graph of
\phi
(for any fixed

\beta
) first decreases, then increases, and finally decreases.
We calculated numerically the smallest stationary point,
\alpha^{*}
, of
\phi

(always lying between
0
and
1
), resulting
to the inequality
\phi(\alpha)\geq\min\{\phi(\alpha^{*}),.964\}
.
Numerical values of
\rho_{1}^{*}(\beta)=\rho_{1}(\alpha^{*}(\beta),\beta)
, as well as
minimization points
\alpha^{*}(\beta)
,
are presented in Table 1. These values indicate
that
\rho_{1}(\alpha^{*}(\beta),\beta)\to\min_{x>0}\big[(x\psi_{1}(x)-1)(x^{2}\psi_{1}(x)+x-1)\big]^{-1}=.9281\ldots
,
and
\alpha^{*}(\beta)\to\alpha^{*}=.41541\ldots
(
\alpha^{*}
is the same
as in Remark

2.2
), as
\beta\to\infty
;
however, we was not able to provide a proof.

The values of Table 1
indicate that for almost the entire parametric range,
the asymptotic efficiency of
\widehat{\alpha}_{n}
is no less that
90\%
.
The same is clearly true for
\widehat{\beta}_{n}
, since

\rho_{2}(\alpha,\beta)=\rho_{1}(\beta,\alpha)
. This verifies
that the proposed estimating procedure is quite powerful and, at the
same time, it produces simple, closed-form, estimators.

Remark 3.1
.

(a)
The estimators
\widehat{\alpha}_{n}
and
\widehat{\beta}_{n}
are positive (w.p. 1)
for all
n\geq 2
. This is so because the denominator is a positive multiple
of an
U
-statistic with symmetric kernel
K(X_{1},X_{2})>0
, w.p.
1
.

(b)
The asymptotic covariance of
\widehat{\alpha}_{n}
and
\widehat{\beta}_{n}

is positive. This follows from the inequality
\psi_{1}(x)>x^{-2}>x^{-2}-x^{-1}
,
x>0
,
which implies that
p(x):=\psi_{1}(x)-(x^{-2}-x^{-1})>0
.
Then, from (
3.5
),

v_{12}(\alpha,\beta)=\alpha^{2}\beta^{2}\ \big[p(\alpha)+p(\beta)\big]>0
.
This fact is somewhat counterintuitive, since
\widehat{\alpha}_{n}=\overline{X}_{n}/V_{n}
,

\widehat{\beta}_{n}=(1-\overline{X}_{n})/V_{n}
,
V_{n}>0
, and the random variables

\overline{X}_{n}
and
1-\overline{X}_{n}
are, obviously, negatively correlated.

(c) The limiting distribution of the estimator
U_{n}

(which is unbiased for
1/(\alpha+\beta)
) can be derived from
Theorem
3.1
, since
U_{n}=(n/(n-1))V_{n}
and thus,

\sqrt{n}(U_{n}-V_{n})=o_{p}(1)
and
\sqrt{n}(1/U_{n}-1/V_{n})=o_{p}(1)
,
n\to\infty
.
Applying the delta-method to (
3.5
) with
k(x,y)=1/(x+y)

we obtain

\sqrt{n}\left(U_{n}-(\alpha+\beta)^{-1}\right)=\sqrt{n}\left(V_{n}-(\alpha+\beta)^{-1}\right)+o_{p}(1)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny d}}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,v^{2}\right)
, as
n\to\infty
,
where

v^{2}=\frac{v_{11}(\alpha,\beta)+v_{22}(\alpha,\beta)+2v_{12}(\alpha,\beta)}{(\alpha+\beta)^{4}(\alpha+\beta+1)}=\frac{\alpha+\beta+\alpha\beta\ \big[\psi_{1}(\alpha)+\psi_{1}(\beta)\big]-1}{(\alpha+\beta)^{2}(\alpha+\beta+1)}.

Consequently,

\sqrt{n}\ \Big(1/V_{n}-(\alpha+\beta)\Big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny d}}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,(\alpha+\beta)^{4}v^{2}\right)
, as
n\to\infty
.
Using these formulas one can verify that the estimators
U_{n}
and
1/V_{n}

exhibit high asymptotic efficiency.

(d) For small sample sizes, bias-corrected estimators
in the sense of Louzada
et al.
may reduce the mean squared error; this, however,
is beyond the scope of the present work.
