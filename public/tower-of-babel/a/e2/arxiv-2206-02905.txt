Adjoint-based Adaptive Multi-Level Monte Carlo for Differential Equations
Jehanzeb Chaudhry, Zachary Stevens

Abstract
We present a multi-level Monte Carlo (MLMC) algorithm with adaptively refined meshes and accurately computed stopping-criteria utilizing adjoint-based a posteriori error analysis for differential equations. This is in contrast to classical MLMC algorithms that use either a hierarchy of uniform meshes or adaptively refined meshes based on Richardson extrapolation, and employ a stopping criteria that relies on assumptions on the convergence rate of the MLMC levels. This work develops two adaptive refinement strategies for the MLMC algorithm. These strategies are based on a decomposition of an error estimate of the MLMC bias and utilize variational analysis, adjoint problems and computable residuals.



keywords

Multi-level Monte Carlo, Adjoint operators, Adaptive refinement, Error estimation, Uncertainty quantification, Differential equations

Introduction

Uncertainty quantification (UQ) plays a vital role in predictive computational science and numerical analysis
[
3
,
47
]
. Many physical phenomena are modeled by differential equations, often with uncertain inputs such as parameter values, boundary/initial conditions or the geometry of the domain. UQ is used to characterize, quantify, or control the uncertainty in the outputs of the model, based on information about the uncertainty of the input data
[

The uncertainty of the output is quantified by approximating the probability distribution or some statistic (e.g. expectation, standard deviation, mean squared error) of the random variable corresponding to the solution of the model. The UQ of many complex applications presents a challenge due to high-dimensional parameter spaces, non-smooth model responses, and non-linearities.

Monte Carlo (MC) methods form a broad approach for UQ and have been used as a classical tool in predictive computational sciences
[
40
,
37
,

. MC methods are simple to implement and are used across a wide range of disciplines such as high-energy physics, finance, particle transport, inter-particle collision, statistical mechanics and numerous other engineering applications
[
46
,
41
]
. However, obtaining an accurate estimate is often computationally expensive in the context of partial differential equation (PDE) models
[
39
]
. Several adaptations and expansions have been made to the MC method to improve its cost effectiveness including the Quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC), Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), multi-fidelity Monte Carlo (MFMC), and multi-level Monte Carlo (MLMC) methods
[

,

,
27
,
2

. Adaptive MLMC methods
[

, which employ adaptive refinement strategies for pathwise problems, have also been investigated.

Many widely used MLMC algorithms rely on a number of assumptions
[
39
,
40
]
, hence the reliability of these methods in predictive science requires quantifying the error in the output of these algorithms. This article develops an adaptive MLMC algorithm that takes advantage of
a posteriori
analysis of differential equations. In contrast to the adaptive MLMC method in
[
43
]
, the algorithm here uses adjoint-based
a posteriori
error approximations to adaptively refine meshes for new levels of the MLMC estimator and also accurately determine when the estimator has achieved a specified stopping criteria. The bias of the MLMC estimator contains a sum of the errors in samples of a quantity of interest (QoI). As such, an adjoint-based error analysis is employed to accurately estimate these errors, and thus the bias of the MLMC estimator. This analysis provides a decomposition of the bias in order to adaptively refine meshes as new levels of the estimator are created. By adaptively refining meshes, this robust MLMC algorithm yields a more cost effective estimate than MLMC with uniformly refined meshes.

MLMC for Differential Equations

This section reviews the multi-level Monte Carlo estimator for the expected value of a random variable
[
38
,
40
,
39
]
. In particular, the random variable in this discussion is a QoI related to the solution of a differential equation that depends on some random parameter(s). The particular differential equations and QoIs investigated in this article are described in more detail in §
3
. For now, the notation is left more general to emphasize that the MLMC estimator is applicable to any QoI that is dependent on a random parameter.

Let
Q=Q(u;w)
be a QoI related to the solution
u
of a differential equation that depends on a random variable
w
belonging to the probability space
\left(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}\right)
. Also let
Q(w^{(n)})=Q(u;w^{(n)})
, for
n=1,\dots,N
, be a large number
N
of samples of
Q
, where
w^{(n)}
is sampled from the same probability space
\left(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}\right)
. Given an approximation (e.g. a numerical solution)
U
to
u
(i.e.
U\approx u
), let
\widehat{Q}(w^{(n)})=\widehat{Q}(U;w^{(n)})
be an approximation of
Q(w^{(n)})
. The standard Monte Carlo method uses the
N
samples
\widehat{Q}(w^{(n)})
to estimate the expected value,
\mathbb{E}[Q]
, of
Q
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Q]\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\widehat{Q}(w^{(n)}).

(1)

There are two sources of error for the estimator (
1
); error from using a finite number of samples, and discretization error from the approximation
\widehat{Q}\approx Q
.

The multi-level Monte Carlo method is an extension of the MC method that utilizes several different approximations
\{\widehat{Q}_{\ell}(w)=\widehat{Q}(U_{\ell};w)\}
in order to obtain a more cost-efficient approximation of the expected value.
More rigorously,
let
\{\widehat{Q}_{\ell}(w)=\widehat{Q}(U_{\ell};w)\}_{\ell=0}^{L-1}
be a collection of approximate QoI where the accuracy of the approximations
U_{\ell}
increases with
\ell
. At each level
\ell
,
N_{\ell}
samples of the random variable
w
are taken from the probability space
\left(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}\right)
and denoted as
\{w_{\ell}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N_{\ell}}
. The MLMC estimator using
L
levels is constructed by expanding the expected value of the most accurate estimator as
[
38
]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Q]\approx\mathbb{E}[\widehat{Q}_{L-1}]=\mathbb{E}[\widehat{Q}_{0}]+\sum_{\ell=1}^{L-1}\mathbb{E}[\widehat{Q}_{\ell}-\widehat{Q}_{\ell-1}].

(2)

Using the standard MC method to obtain the expected values on the right side of (
2
) gives the L-level MLMC estimator for the expected value of
Q
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Q]\approx\widehat{Q}_{L,\{N_{\ell}\}}^{ML}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{0}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{0}}\widehat{Q}_{0}(w_{0}^{(n)})+\sum_{\ell=1}^{L-1}\left\{\frac{1}{N_{\ell}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\ell}}\left(\widehat{Q}_{\ell}(w_{\ell}^{(n)})-\widehat{Q}_{\ell-1}(w_{\ell}^{(n)})\right)\right\},

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\left\{\frac{1}{N_{\ell}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\ell}}\left(\widehat{Q}_{\ell}(w_{\ell}^{(n)})-\widehat{Q}_{\ell-1}(w_{\ell}^{(n)})\right)\right\},

(3)

where
\widehat{Q}_{-1}\equiv 0
. One sample on the
\ell-th
level is

Y_{\ell}(w_{\ell}^{n})=\left(\widehat{Q}_{\ell}(w_{\ell}^{(n)})-\widehat{Q}_{\ell-1}(w_{\ell}^{(n)})\right),

(4)

and requires two different approximations of the QoI. Both come with the same sample of the random parameter
w
, but they are obtained using the different approximations
\widehat{Q}_{\ell}
and
\widehat{Q}_{\ell-1}
.

2.1
Mean Squared Error for MLMC method

The Mean Squared Error (MSE) of the MLMC estimator is
[
39
]

\displaystyle MSE

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widehat{Q}^{ML}_{\{N_{\ell}\},L}-Q\right)^{2}\right]=\mathbb{V}\left[\widehat{Q}^{ML}_{\{N_{\ell}\},L}\right]+\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{Q}^{ML}_{\{N_{\ell}\},L}-Q\right]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\frac{1}{N_{\ell}}\mathbb{V}\left[\widehat{Q}_{\ell}-\widehat{Q}_{\ell-1}\right]+\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{Q}_{L-1}-Q\right]\right)^{2},

(5)

where the variance
\mathbb{V}
of a random variable
X
is

\mathbb{V}[X]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(X-\mathbb{E}[X]\right)^{2}\right].

(6)

The first term in (
2.1
) is the variance, which is decomposed into contributions from each level of the multi-level estimator. The second term is the squared bias, which only depends on the highest level. If a tolerance
\epsilon
is desired for the MSE, it is sufficient that

\mathbb{V}\left[\widehat{Q}^{ML}_{\{N_{\ell}\},L}\right]<\epsilon/2\quad\text{ and }\quad\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{Q}^{ML}_{\{N_{\ell}\},L}-Q\right]\right)^{2}<\epsilon/2.

(7)

The variance at level
\ell
and the bias can be approximated using a finite number of samples
[
39
]

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\left[\widehat{Q}_{\ell}-\widehat{Q}_{\ell-1}\right]

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{N_{\ell}-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\ell}}\left(\widehat{Q}_{\ell}(w_{\ell}^{(n)})-\widehat{Q}_{\ell-1}(w_{\ell}^{(n)})-\frac{1}{N_{\ell}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\ell}}\left(\widehat{Q}_{\ell}(w_{\ell}^{(k)})-\widehat{Q}_{\ell-1}(w_{\ell}^{(k)})\right)\right)^{2},

(8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{Q}_{L-1}-Q\right]

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{N_{L-1}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{L-1}}\left(\widehat{Q}_{L-1}(w_{L-1}^{(n)})-Q(w_{L-1}^{(n)})\right).

(9)

With the approximations (
8
) and (
9
), the MSE is approximated as

\displaystyle MSE

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\frac{1}{N_{\ell}}\left[\frac{1}{N_{\ell}-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\ell}}\left(\widehat{Q}_{\ell}(w_{\ell}^{(n)})-\widehat{Q}_{\ell-1}(w_{\ell}^{(n)})-\frac{1}{N_{\ell}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\ell}}\left(\widehat{Q}_{\ell}(w_{\ell}^{(k)})-\widehat{Q}_{\ell-1}(w_{\ell}^{(k)})\right)\right)^{2}\right]+

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{1}{N_{L-1}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{L-1}}\left(\widehat{Q}_{L-1}(w_{L-1}^{(n)})-Q(w_{L-1}^{(n)})\right)\right)^{2}.

(10)

The squared-bias term in (
2.1
) is not directly computable as it requires a true sample of the QoI,
Q(w_{L-1}^{(n)})
; formulas to estimate the bias for different QoIs are presented in §
4
. This estimate for the MSE shows that the bias depends on the error of the highest level of the MLMC estimator. In order to effectively lower the bias, the estimator
\widehat{Q}_{L-1}
would need to become more accurate, i.e. a new level would have to be introduced. This new highest level generally does not require a large number of samples
[
39
,
40
]
.

The MSE estimate (
2.1
) also shows that taking more samples on a given level (increasing some
N_{\ell}
) decreases the overall variance of the MLMC estimator. However, taking more samples increases the cost of the estimator so a balance must be met in order to decrease the variance without increasing the cost significantly.
Let the variance on level
\ell
be denoted as
V_{\ell}=\mathbb{V}\left[\widehat{Q}_{\ell}-\widehat{Q}_{\ell-1}\right]
and let the cost of taking one sample on the
\ell
-th level be
C_{\ell}
. Using the method of Lagrange multipliers to minimize overall cost,
\sum N_{\ell}C_{\ell}
, under the constraint for the total variance
\sum\frac{V_{\ell}}{N_{\ell}}<\frac{1}{2}\epsilon
gives the optimal number of samples to take on level
\ell
to be
[
39
]

N_{\ell,opt}=\left\lceil\frac{2}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\frac{V_{\ell}}{C_{\ell}}}\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\sqrt{\frac{V_{k}}{C_{k}}}\right\rceil.

(11)

Note that in practice only an approximation of the variances
V_{\ell}
are available and thus only an approximate
N_{\ell,opt}
can be computed. If this yields an under-approximation of
N_{\ell,opt}
, the MSE of the MLMC estimator will be larger than the desired tolerance.

Applications and Quantities of Interest

The MLMC method as described in §
2
is applicable to any QoI that depends on a random parameter. This section describes different differential equations and three specific QoIs analyzed in this article and on which numerical experiments are carried out in §
6

L^{2}(\Omega)
inner-product is denoted as the inner-product pairing
\left(\alpha,\beta\right)
, while the Euclidean inner-product is denoted by the dot-product
\alpha\cdot\beta

3.1
Stationary PDEs

Consider a partial differential equation of the form

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathcal{D}u&=f,\hskip 14.22636ptx\in\Omega,\\
u&=g,\hskip 14.22636ptx\in\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

(12)

over a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with differential operator
\mathcal{D}

=\mathcal{D}(w)
,
and continuous functions
f=f(u,x;w)
and
g=g(x;w)

w

u=u(x;w)
of (
12

w

For example, in the steady-state advection-diffusion equation

\nabla^{2}u+b\cdot{\nabla u}=f,

(13)

the operator is
\mathcal{D}u=\nabla^{2}u+b\cdot{\nabla u}

i

1\leq i\leq d

3.1.1
QoIs for Stationary PDEs

The form of QoI used in this article for the stationary equations is considered to be a ‘standard’ QoI because it can be expressed as a linear functional of the solution:

\displaystyle Q_{S}(u)=\left(u,\psi\right)=\int_{\Omega}u\cdot\psi{\rm d}\Omega,

(14)

for some function
\psi\in L^{2}(\Omega)
with
\psi(x)=0
for
x\in\partial\Omega
. This QoI is used to represent a weighted average of
u
over a smaller area inside
\Omega
. The adjoint-based error representation for this QoI is described in Theorem
1
.

3.2

Adjoint-based Error Analysis

The computation of the bias in MLMC, given in (
9
), requires estimation of the error in the QoI for each sampled parameter
w_{L-1}^{(n)}
. This section presents a posteriori analysis for estimating this error for a given (or fixed) parameter
w
and derives error formulation of the different QoIs discussed in §
3
. Since the sampled parameter
w
is fixed throughout this section, we omit mentioning the dependence of solutions on the parameter for simplicity of notation.
The error representations are derived using variational analysis and rely on solutions to certain adjoint problems. First, the variational forms of the two differential equations (
12
) and (
15
) are presented. Then, the error representations and the associated adjoint problems are discussed for the three QoIs (
14
), (
17
), and (
20
).

4.1
Variational Forms of Differential Equations

Let
W
be a Hilbert space over
\Omega
with inner-product
\left(\alpha,\beta\right)_{W}
and the induced W-norm
\|\alpha\|_{W}
, for
\alpha,\beta\in W
.

Stationary PDEs
: The variational form of (
12
) can be expressed as: Find
u\in W
such that

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{D}_{1}u,\mathcal{D}_{2}v\right)=\left(f,v\right),\hskip 28.45274pt\forall v\in W,

(21)

with
L^{2}(\Omega)
inner-product
\left(a,b\right)=\int_{\Omega}a\cdot b\ {\rm d}\Omega
. For example, for the PDE (
13
),
W=H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
. The operators
\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathcal{D}_{2}
are linear differential operators in
x
, such that
\mathcal{D}_{2}^{*}\mathcal{D}_{1}u=\mathcal{D}u
. The operator
\mathcal{D}_{2}^{*}
is the formal adjoint of
\mathcal{D}_{2}
which satisfies the property that

\left(u,\mathcal{D}_{2}v\right)=\left(\mathcal{D}_{2}^{*}u,v\right),\hskip 14.22636pt\text{ for all }u\in W,v\in W.

(22)

The differential operator
\mathcal{D}_{1}
may be of lower order than
\mathcal{D}
. Although, if a solution
u
to (
21
) is sufficiently differentiable, it will also satisfy (
12
).

ODEs
: The variational form of (
15
) is: Find
u\in\left(H^{1}((0,T))\right)^{d}
such that

\int_{0}^{T}\frac{du}{dt}\cdot v{\rm d}t=\int_{0}^{T}f\cdot v{\rm d}t,\quad\forall v\in\left(L^{2}(0,T)\right)^{d}.

(23)

The variational equations (
21

4.2.1
Error for Standard QoIs

The error representations for two standard QoIs are presented. Theorem
1
applies to QoIs of form (

) related to the solution of a time-independent PDE (

). Theorem

Stationary PDEs:

Theorem 1
.

Given a numerical solution
U(x)
to (
12
), let
e(x)=u(x)-U(x)
. The error,
\left(e,\psi\right)
, in the quantity (
14
) is given by

\left(e,\psi\right)=\left(\mathcal{D}_{1}U,\mathcal{D}_{2}\phi\right)-\left(f,\phi\right)

(24)

where
\phi(x)
is the solution of the adjoint problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathcal{D}^{*}(\phi(x))+\psi&=0,\hskip 14.22636ptx\in\Omega,\\
\phi(x)&=0,\hskip 14.22636ptx\in\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

(25)

The linear differential operator
\mathcal{D}^{*}
is the corresponding adjoint operator of
\mathcal{D}
in (
12
) with property (
22
). The proof of Theorem
1
is standard; see
[
29
]
.

The error representation (
24
) provides a way to compute the terms in the bias (
9
). Moreover, taking advantage of the linearity of the integral with respect to the domain produces a decomposition of the error into contributions from different regions of the domain. Let
\mathcal{T}
be a partition of the domain
\Omega
. Then

\displaystyle\left(e,\psi\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathcal{D}_{1}U,\mathcal{D}_{2}\phi\right)-\left(f,\phi\right)=\int_{\Omega}\left(\left[\mathcal{D}_{1}U\cdot\mathcal{D}_{2}\phi\right]-f\cdot\phi\right){\rm d}\Omega,

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\int_{\tau}\left(\left[\mathcal{D}_{1}U\cdot\mathcal{D}_{2}\phi\right]-f\cdot\phi\right){\rm d}\Omega=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}e_{\tau},

(26)

where the error contribution,
e_{\tau}
, from a particular simplex
\tau
is given by the integral

e_{\tau}=\int_{\tau}\left(\left[\mathcal{D}_{1}U\cdot\mathcal{D}_{2}\phi\right]-f\cdot\phi\right){\rm d}\Omega.

(27)

ODEs
:

Theorem 2
.

Given a numerical solution
U(t)
to (
15
), let
e(t)=u(t)-U(t)
. The error,
e(t^{*})\cdot\psi
, in the quantity (
17
) at a time
t^{*}\in(0,T]
is given by

Q_{S}(u)-Q_{S}(U)=e(t^{*})\cdot\psi=\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\left[f\cdot\phi-\frac{dU}{dt}\cdot\phi\right]{\rm d}t,

(28)

where
\phi(t)
is the solution of the adjoint problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\frac{d\phi}{dt}&=\overline{A_{u,U}(t)}^{T}\phi,\hskip 14.22636pt,t\in[0,t^{*}),\\
\phi(t^{*})&=\psi.\end{cases}

(29)

where

\overline{A_{u,U}(t)}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{df}{dz}(z,t){\rm d}s

(30)

with
z=su+(1-s)U
.

The proof of Theorem
2
is standard; see
[
32
]
. Note that the initial condition for the adjoint problem is at time
t=t^{*}
and the equation is solved backwards in time.
Since
\overline{A_{u,U}}
requires the true solution

of (
15

\overline{A_{u,U}}\approx\nabla_{u}f(U,x,t)
. With this, the right side of the adjoint equation (
29
) is approximated by

\displaystyle\overline{A_{u,U}(t)}^{T}\phi\approx\left(\nabla_{u}f(U,x,t)\right)^{\top}\phi(x,t).

(31)

Again, taking advantage of linearity in the integral in (
28
) provides a decomposition of the error. If the domain
(0,t^{*})
is partitioned into
N^{*}
sub-intervals with endpoints
\{t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{N^{*}}\}
, a decomposition of the error (
28
) is

\displaystyle e(t^{*})\cdot\psi=\sum_{i=0}^{N^{*}-1}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}\left[f\cdot\phi-\frac{dU}{dt}\cdot\phi\right]{\rm d}t=\sum_{i=0}^{N^{*}-1}e_{(t_{i},t_{i+1})},

(32)

with error contributions,
e_{(t_{i},t_{i+1})}
, given by the integrals

e_{(t_{i},t_{i+1})}=\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}\left[f\cdot\phi-\frac{dU}{dt}\cdot\phi\right]{\rm d}t.

(33)

4.2.2
Nonstandard QoI

For a chosen threshold
R
and fixed value of
\hat{t}
in (

), denote the true value of the QoI as

t_{t}:=Q(u).

(34)

Let
U(x,t)
be an approximation of the solution satisfying (

) and let

t_{c}:=Q(U)

(35)

be the corresponding computed (or approximated) QoI. Denote the error in the approximate solution as

. The error representation for the nonstandard QoI (

) is given in Theorem

Theorem 3
.

Let

be the true solution to (

) and
t_{t}
be the true NSQoI (

). Also let

and

be a numerically computed solution and its corresponding computed NSQoI, respectively. For a chosen functional

) is given as

\displaystyle e_{Q}

\displaystyle=t_{t}-t_{c}=\frac{e(t_{c})\cdot\psi+\mathcal{R}_{1}}{f(t_{c})\cdot\psi+e(t_{c})\cdot\nabla_{u}[\psi\cdot f]+\mathcal{R}_{2}}.

(36)

Where
e(t)=u(t)-U(t)
and the remainders satisfy
\mathcal{R}_{1}=\mathcal{O}\left((t_{t}-t_{c})^{2}\right)
and
\mathcal{R}_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(|u-U|^{2}\right)
.

The proof of Theorem

relies on Taylor’s Theorem to expand around known quantities; see
[

]
.

In the error representation (

\mathcal{R}_{1}\approx 0
and
\mathcal{R}_{2}\approx 0
gives the approximation

t_{t}-t_{c}\approx\frac{e(t_{c})\cdot\psi}{f(t_{c})\cdot\psi+e(t_{c})\cdot\nabla_{u}[\psi\cdot f]}.

(37)

The terms containing the unknown error

are estimated using Theorem

The numerator of (

\displaystyle t_{t}-t_{c}\approx\frac{1}{\mathcal{C}}\sum_{i=0}^{N^{*}-1}e_{(t_{i},t_{i+1})}

where

The three error decompositions, (

5
Apaptive Multi-level Monte Carlo Algorithm

This section develops an MLMC algorithm that uses the error decompositions from §
4
to adaptively create meshes as new levels are added to the estimator. First, two grid refinement methods are presented.

5.1
Creating New Levels for MLMC

There are many refinement methods to choose from when creating the grid for a new level. MLMC algorithms usually employ standard uniform refinement
[
39
]
. We take advantage of the form of the error decompositions (
26
), (
32
), and (
38
) in order to adaptively create new grids and in turn create a more efficient MLMC algorithm. The rest of this section describes the two adaptive refinement methods used in this article. Since these refinement methods are applied to individual samples, we also discuss how each refinement method is adapted to deal with multiple samples.

5.1.1
Dual Weighted Residual Refinement

For the dual-weighted-residual (DWR) refinement method, the regions,
\tau
(used in the error decompositions; see §
4.2
), corresponding to the largest contributions of error are refined by a given factor
[
6
,
4
]
. This type of grid refinement relies on the decomposition of the error (
26
) and some criteria to determine whether a region should be refined or not. Possible criteria include refining any region that corresponds to an error larger than some tolerance, or refining a certain number of regions that contribute the most error.

When using this refinement method in an MLMC algorithm, we want to combine data from multiple samples to determine which regions to refine. We do this by determining which regions should be refined for each individual sample, and then refining
all
of these regions.

More precisely, let
\mathcal{T}_{h}=\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},...,\tau_{M}\}
be a simplicial decomposition of a domain
\Omega
, where
h
denotes the maximum diameter of the elements of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
. Let
U(w^{(1)})=U(x;w^{(1)})
and
U(w^{(2)})=U(x;w^{(2)})
be two numerical solutions corresponding to different samples of the random parameter
w
. Let
\mathcal{J}=\{\tau_{J_{1}},\tau_{J_{2}},...,\tau_{J_{\widehat{M}}}\}
be the set of
\widehat{M}<M
regions to be refined based on the error decomposition corresponding to the numerical solution
U(w^{(1)})
. Also let
\mathcal{K}=\{\tau_{K_{1}},\tau_{K_{2}},...,\tau_{K_{\widetilde{M}}}\}
be the similar set corresponding to
U(w^{(2)})
. We then refine all of the regions in the union of both sets,
\mathcal{J}\cup\mathcal{K}
.

5.1.2
Meso-scale Refinement

In time-dependent problems, a meso-scale refinement of the time domain may be used in order to preserve cancellations of the error over large sections of the domain
[
16
]
. This refinement method considers the accumulation of the error over sub-intervals and forms “meso-scale regions”, i.e. regions of maximal cancellation in the error. The meso-scale regions are each uniformly refined, using different scaling factors on different regions. Since uniform refinement usually preserves cancellations, the locations of these minima are preserved but the error decreases. Examples presented in this article follow the “Allocation of fixed resources” algorithm from
[
16
]
, which is described below.

Given a numerical solution
U
of an ODE (
12
), computed over a temporal grid
\mathcal{T}=\{I_{1},I_{2},\dots,I_{\widetilde{N}}\}
, define the accumulated contributions to the error as

E_{k}=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}e_{I_{i}}\right|,

(39)

where the
e_{I_{i}}
are define in (
33
). Meso-scale regions, or meso-regions, are determined by finding the global minimum of the accumulated error after the initial increase of accumulated error. This is then repeated, starting at the minimum, until the end of the temporal domain is reached. See Figure
1b
for an example of an
E_{k}
and the corresponding meso-regions.

Let
P
be the number of meso-scale regions and
{\widetilde{N}}_{i}
be the number of time-steps in the
i
-th meso-scale region. If the
i
-th meso-scale region starts at interval
I_{p}
and ends at interval
I_{r}
, then
\widetilde{N}_{i}=r-p+1
. Also let
\mathcal{E}_{i}=(E_{r}-E_{p})
denote the error accumulated over the
i
-th meso-scale region. Assume that this error accumulated over the
i
-th meso-scale region satisfies

\mathcal{E}_{i}=\frac{c_{i}}{{\widetilde{N}}_{i}^{q}},

(40)

where
c_{i}
is some positive constant and
q
is determined by the order of the numerical method. The goal of the meso-scale refinement is to create a new grid with
\widehat{N}
sub-intervals that minimizes the total error. If the total number of intervals
\widehat{N}
is fixed, the total error is minimized if

\frac{c_{i}}{{\widehat{N}}_{i}^{q+1}}=K,\ \ \ \ \forall i,

(41)

for some constant
K
. The
\widehat{N}_{i}s
are obtained as follows:
First obtain all
c_{i}
from (
40
). Then, rearranging (
41
) to get an expression for
\widehat{N}_{i}
and using the fact that

\sum_{i=1}^{P}\widehat{N}_{i}=\widehat{N},

(42)

compute the value of
K
as

K=\left[\frac{1}{\widehat{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{P}\left(c_{i}^{1/(q+1)}\right)\right]^{q+1}.

(43)

Finally, obtain the
\widehat{N}_{i}
s from (
41
).

It is not clear how to modify the meso-scale refinement to combine information from multiple samples. Instead of combining the samples, we use the refinement that corresponds to the single sample that had the largest computed absolute error in the QoI,
with the added condition that regions are never unrefined.

This is accomplished as follows. First, a tentative level
\ell
grid is created with the meso-regions and the
\widehat{N}_{i}
s as described above. Then, a common refinement of the meso-regions from this tentative and the level
\ell-1
is taken, see Figure
2a
for an illustration. For each of the regions in this common refinement, determine whichever grid (
\ell-1
or tentative) has more nodes in that region. Finally, using that number of nodes, make a uniform sub-grid over that region. This process is explained in Figure
2b
. Here the grid at level
\ell-1
has two meso-scale regions, with the first meso-region having a single interval while the second meso-region has five intervals. The tentative grid (shown in the middle) has three meso-regions having one, six and one intervals respectively. The refinement, which forms the level
\ell
grid is shown at the bottom. This grid has four meso-regions having one, four, three and three intervals respectively. We observe that all regions of level
\ell

\ell-1

Next, the bias is compared to the desired tolerance. If the tolerance has not been achieved, a new level is added. The mesh for the new level is constructed using an adaptive refinement method as discussed in §

Remark 4
.

When creating new levels, the initial number of samples taken does not have to be the same number
N
as for the lowest level. If the same large
N
is used for every level, starting new levels will be much more expensive than starting previous levels and it becomes more likely that unnecessary samples are taken on high levels. In our examples below, we keep the cost of starting a level relatively fixed for the first three levels, after which we keep the number of initial samples constant. This maintains a reasonable computational cost while also taking enough samples for the variance estimates to be meaningful.

6
Numerical Results

This section begins by discussing the finite element method (FEM) used in the numerical examples. For each problem, we present the adjoint equation needed for the error analysis of §
4
. Examples include standard QoIs for a harmonic oscillator and a stationary advection-diffusion equation, as well as non-standard QoIs for a harmonic oscillator, the Lorenz equations, and the two-body problem. The MLMC algorithm is applied to each problem using different refinement methods with results provided for comparison.

6.1
Numerical Methods: Continuous Galerkin FEM

The error analysis presented in §
4
is well-suited for numerical solutions obtained from variational methods. The analysis also applies to other numerical methods, provided they be cast to an equivalent variational form. The examples in this section use the first order, continuous Galerkin finite element method.

Continuous Galerkin finite element method are constructed using the standard continuous Lagrangian finite element spaces. Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be a simplicial decomposition of
\Omega
, where h is the
maximum diameter of the elements of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, such that the union of the elements of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is
\Omega
, and the intersection of any two elements is either a common edge, a node, or is empty. The degree
q
continuous Lagrange finite element space is then defined as

\mathcal{P}_{h}^{q}=\left\{v\in C(\Omega):\forall\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{h},\left.v\right|_{\tau}\in\mathcal{P}^{q}(\tau)\right\},

(44)

where
\mathcal{P}^{q}(\tau)
is the space of polynomials of degree at most
q
defined on the element
\tau
.

Stationary PDEs
: The degree
q
continuous Galerkin finite element method, with respect to
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, for the PDE (
12
) is: Find
U\in\mathcal{P}_{h}^{q}
such that

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{D}_{1}U,\mathcal{D}_{2}v\right)=\left(f,v\right),\hskip 28.45274pt\forall v\in\mathcal{P}^{q}_{h}.

(45)

ODEs
: For the differential equation (
15
), the domain is the interval
(0,T]
and
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is a set of sub-intervals
\{(t_{n},t_{n+1})\}_{n=0}^{N^{*}-1}
with
t_{n+1}-t_{n}\leq h
. The continuous Galerkin method is: Find
U\in\mathcal{P}_{h}^{q}
such that the restriction of
U
to any
(t_{n},t_{n+1})\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\frac{dU}{dt}\cdot v{\rm d}t=\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}f\cdot v{\rm d}t,\hskip 28.45274pt\forall v\in\mathcal{P}^{q-1}(t_{n},t_{n+1}).

(46)

For example, for the ODE (
16
) with a domain decomposed into sub-intervals
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, the continuous Galerkin method with
q=1
is: Find
U\in\mathcal{P}_{h}^{1}
such that the restriction of
U
to any
(t_{n},t_{n+1})\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\frac{dU}{dt}\cdot v{\rm d}t=\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}(\tilde{f}-AU)\cdot v{\rm d}t,\hskip 28.45274pt\forall v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

(47)

Here, the solution

is continuous and piece-wise linear with respect to the partition
\mathcal{T}_{h}
.

Two-Body Problem

Stationary Advection-Diffusion Equation

Consider the equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\nabla^{2}u(x)+b\cdot\nabla u(x)=f(x),\hskip 28.45274pt&x\in(0,3)\times(0,1),\\
u(x)=0,\hskip 28.45274pt&x\in\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

has, as its first component, the random parameter

f
is non-zero only over an interior region of the domain, and is given as

f=\begin{cases}10000(x-1)(x-2.5)(y-1/6)(y-5/6)\ \ \ \ 1\leq x\leq 2.5,\ 1/6\leq y\leq 5/6,\\
0\hskip 227.62204pt\text{else}.\end{cases}

The weak form of (

such that

-\Big(\nabla u,\nabla v\Big)+\Big(b\cdot\nabla u,v\Big)=\Big(f,v\Big),\hskip 14.22636pt\forall v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

Q_{S}(u)=\int_{\Omega}\psi\cdot u{\rm d}\Omega,\ \ \ \text{ where }\ \ \ \psi=\begin{cases}1,\ \ \ \ \ \ (x,y)\in(1,1.5)\times(1/3,2/3)\\

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\nabla^{2}\phi(x)-b\cdot\nabla\phi(x)+\psi(x)=0,\hskip 28.45274pt&x\in\Omega,\\

(67)

7
Conclusion

We develop an MLMC algorithm using adjoint-based error analysis to obtain an accurate approximation of the bias and adaptively refine meshes when creating new levels. It is shown that using an adaptive refinement method, either meso-scale or refining regions of largest error, leads to a more cost-effective method than uniform refinement. The advantages of adaptive refinement become more prominent in higher-dimensional problems and in problems where error accumulation is localized. Further improvements can be made to the meso-scale refinement method to better incorporate data from all samples. Other future work will also focus on parabolic PDEs.
