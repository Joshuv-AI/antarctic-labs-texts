k k -Median clustering under discrete Fréchet and Hausdorff distances Thanks: The authors would like to thank Pankaj Agarwal, Kamesh Munagala and anonymous reviewers for helpful discussions and feedback. A shorter version of this paper will appear in SoCG 2020.
Abhinandan Nath, Erin Taylor

Abstract
We give the first near-linear time ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -approximation algorithm for k k -median clustering of polygonal trajectories under the discrete Fréchet distance, and the first polynomial time ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -approximation algorithm for k k -median clustering of finite point sets under the Hausdorff distance, provided the cluster centers, ambient dimension, and k k are bounded by a constant. The main technique is a general framework for solving clustering problems where the cluster centers are restricted to come from a simpler metric space. We precisely characterize conditions on the simpler metric space of the cluster centers that allow faster ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -approximations for the k k -median problem. We also show that the k k -median problem under Hausdorff distance is NP-Hard .

The
k
-median problem has been very widely studied. We are given a set
P
of
n
elements from a metric space. The goal is now to select
k

centers
so that the sum of the distances of each point to the nearest cluster center is minimized.
In the simplest setting,
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
under the Euclidean metric. In this paper, each individual element of
P
is itself a collection of points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, e.g., a curve traced by a moving object, or a point cloud. Since the objective of clustering is to group
similar
objects into the same cluster and to summarize each cluster using its cluster center, it is important that a meaningful distance function is used to compare two input elements. In our work, we look at the widely used discrete Fréchet and Hausdorff distances for trajectories and point sets respectively.

Trajectories can model a variety of systems that change with time. As such, trajectory data is being collected at enormous scales. As a first step, clustering is hugely important in understanding and summarizing the data. It involves partitioning a set of trajectories into clusters of similar trajectories, and computing a representative trajectory (a center) per cluster. It can be viewed as a compression scheme for large trajectory datasets, effectively performing non-linear dimension reduction. If the centers have low complexity, this representation can reduce uncertainty and noise found in individual trajectories. Information provided by the set of centers is useful for trajectory analysis applications such as similarity search and anomaly detection
[
31
]
. The Hausdorff distance is another widely used shape-based distance
[
8
,
23
]
. In shape matching applications, we may want to cluster similar shapes into one group (where a shape is represented by a point cloud).

However if the center is unrestricted, it will contain a lot of vertices inside the four noticeable noisy
clumps

The
k
-median problem is hard to solve exactly, even in Euclidean space
[
16
,
29
]
. There is a long line of work on both constant factor and
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximations, with varying running time dependence on
k
and the ambient dimension (if applicable). Many of these algorithms require the underlying metric space to have bounded doubling dimension (e.g., see
[
1
]
). However, it can be shown that both the discrete Fréchet and Hausdorff distances do not have doubling dimension bounded by a constant (Appendix
A
).
We circumvent this problem by considering cluster centers from a somewhat
simpler
metric space compared to the input metric space. For trajectories and point sets, we restrict the centers to have low complexity, i.e., a bounded number of points. This approach has been used before (
[
9
,
10
,
13
]
). It has the added benefit of preventing the cluster center from overfitting to the elements of its cluster. This is crucial, since real-life measurements are noisy and error-prone, and without any restrictions the cluster center can inherit noise and high complexity from the input (see Fig.
1
). As another example, in clustering financial time-series data using Hausdorff distance
[
6
]
, frequent intra-day fluctuations may not be useful in capturing long-term trends, and we want to avoid them by retricting the cluster centers’ complexity. However, our work differs from previous approaches in that we precisely characterize general conditions on the simpler metric space for the cluster centers, which leads to faster
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithms for the
k
-median problem for the discrete Fréchet and Hausdorff distances.

Problem definition.

A
metric space

{\mathcal{X}}=(X,\mathtt{d})
consists of a set
X
and a distance function
\mathtt{d}:X\times X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
that satisfies the following properties : (i)
\mathtt{d}(x,x)=0
for all
x\in X
; (ii)
\mathtt{d}(x,y)=\mathtt{d}(y,x)
for all
x,y\in X
; and (iii)
\mathtt{d}(x,z)\leq\mathtt{d}(x,y)+\mathtt{d}(y,z)
for all
x,y,z\in X
.

Given subsets
P,C\subseteq X
, the
(k,C)
-median
problem is to compute a set
C^{\prime}\subseteq C
of
k

center
points that minimizes

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in P}\mathtt{d}(p,C^{\prime}),

where
\mathtt{d}(p,C^{\prime})=\min_{c\in C^{\prime}}\mathtt{d}(p,c)
. Here
P
is finite, but
C
need not be.

Let
T^{l}
(resp.
U^{l}
) be the set of all trajectories (resp. point sets) in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where each trajectory (resp. point set) has at most
l
points. Thus,
T=\bigcup_{l>0}T^{l}
and
U=\bigcup_{l>0}U^{l}
are the set of all trajectories and finite point sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
respectively.
As special cases of the
(k,C)
-median problem, we discuss the
(k,T^{l})
-median and the
(k,U^{l})
-median problems for the metric spaces
{\mathcal{T}}=(T,\mathtt{d}_{F})
and
{\mathcal{U}}=(U,\mathtt{d}_{H})
respectively, i.e., each center trajectory or point set can have at most
l
points. Here,
\mathtt{d}_{F}
and
\mathtt{d}_{H}
denote the discrete Fréchet and Hausdorff distances respectively.

Challenges and ideas.

As mentioned before, both the discrete Fréchet and Hausdorff metrics do not have low doubling dimension, so a number of previous techniques do not directly apply to our setting to yield efficient algorithms. One approach would be to embed these metrics into other metric spaces. Backurs and Sidiropoulos
[
4
]
give an embedding of the Hausdorff metric over point sets of size
s
in
d
-dimensional Euclidean space, into
l_{\infty}^{s^{O(s+d)}}
with distortion
s^{O(s+d)}
. However, both the distortion and the resultant dimension are too high for many applications. It is not known if the Fréchet distance can be embedded into an
l_{p}
space using finite dimension.

We circumvent the problem by restricting the cluster centers to come from a subset
C
of the original space
X
, namely trajectories and point sets defined by a bounded number of points each. We show that if every metric ball in
C
can be
covered
by a small number of metric balls of a fixed smaller radius, then we can use a sampling-based algorithm similar to the one in Ackermann
et al.

[
1
]
; we make this precise by introducing the notion of
coverability
of
C
. We crucially show that the centers of the balls in the
cover
can be arbitrary, and need not come from
C
. This is more general than
C
having bounded doubling dimension. This allows us to approximate the optimal
(1,C)
-median of
P
using the
(1,C)
-median of a constant sized random sample of
P
, allowing us to use the framework of
[
1
]
.

It is not known how to efficiently compute the optimal
(1,C)
-median under the discrete Fréchet and Hausdorff distances. However, we show that all we need is to compute a constant number of candidate centers in time independent of the size of the input, at least one of which is a good approximation to the optimal
(1,C)
-median of the input. We show how to compute these candidates for a
coverable
set
C
. Then, we can apply the sampling technique of
[
1
]
and recursively use this property to find
k
centers that approximate the cost of the
(k,C)
-median optimal solution.
Although our work heavily relies on the framework of Ackermann et al.
[
1
]
, it is a significant improvement from existing work on clustering under the discrete Fréchet distance, and the first such result for clustering under the Hausdorff distance.

2
Preliminaries, definitions and an overview

We formally define the discrete Fréchet and Hausdorff distances. We also define two properties on
C
and
\mathtt{d}
which allow us to design efficient clustering algorithms. Finally we give an overview of our algorithm.

Discrete Fréchet and Hausdorff distance.

Consider two finite sets
\zeta_{1}
and
\zeta_{2}
. A
correspondence

{\mathcal{C}}
between
\zeta_{1}
and
\zeta_{2}
is a subset of
\zeta_{1}\times\zeta_{2}
such that every element of
\zeta_{1}
and
\zeta_{2}
appears in at least one pair in
{\mathcal{C}}
. For
\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the
Hausdorff distance

[
21
]
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathtt{d}_{H}(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})=\min_{{\mathcal{C}}\in\Xi(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})}\max_{(p,q)\in{\mathcal{C}}}\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert,

where
\left\lVert.\right\rVert
denotes the
l_{2}
norm, and
\Xi(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})
is the set of all possible correspondences between
\zeta_{1}
and
\zeta_{2}
.

A trajectory
\gamma
is a finite sequence of points
\langle p_{1},p_{2}\ldots\rangle
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A correspodence can be defined for a pair of trajectories
\gamma_{1}=\langle p_{1},p_{2},\ldots\rangle
and
\gamma_{2}=\langle q_{1},q_{2},\ldots\rangle
by treating each trajectory as a point sequence; such a correspondence is said to be
monotonic
if it also respects the ordering of points in the trajectories, i.e., if
(p_{i_{1}},q_{j_{1}}),(p_{i_{2}},q_{j_{2}})\in{\mathcal{C}}
, then
i_{2}\geq i_{1}\Rightarrow j_{2}\geq j_{1}
. The
discrete Fréchet distance

[
14
]
between
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathtt{d}_{F}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=\min_{{\mathcal{C}}\in\Xi_{M}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})}\max_{(p,q)\in{\mathcal{C}}}\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert,

where
\Xi_{M}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
is the set of all monotone correspondences between
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
. Our algorithms cluster in the metric space defined by the discrete Fréchet and Hausdorff distance.

Strong and weak sampling properties.

We define two properties that make efficient clustering algorithms possible. These are generalizations of the
strong
and
weak
sampling properties defined by Ackermann
et al.
(see Theorem 1.1 and Property 4.1 in
[
1
]
). The major difference is that the cluster centers are restricted to a subset
C
of the metric space
X
. These properties allow fast approximation of the
(1,C)
-median using only a constant sized random sample of the input. We later show how to get an efficient
(k,C)
-median algorithm using the fast
(1,C)
-median algorithm as a subroutine. We denote the optimal
(1,C)
median of any set
P\subseteq X
by
c_{P}
.

Definition 1

(Strong sampling property)
.

Let
0<\varepsilon,\delta<1
be arbitrary.
(X,C,\mathtt{d})
is said to satisfy the strong sampling property for
\varepsilon,\delta
iff

(i) For any finite
P\subseteq X
,
c_{P}
can be computed in time depending only on
|P|
.

(ii) There exists a positive integer
m_{\delta,\varepsilon}
depending on
\delta,\varepsilon
such that for any
P\subseteq X
, the optimal
(1,C)
-median
c_{S}
of a uniform random multiset
S\subseteq P
of size
m_{\delta,\varepsilon}
satisfies

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\sum_{p\in P}\mathtt{d}(p,c_{S})\leq(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{p\in P}\mathtt{d}(p,c_{P})\right]\geq 1-\delta.

The strong sampling property characterizes those instances in which the optimal
(1,C)
-median of a constant-sized random sample is a good approximation to the optimal
(1,C)
-median of the whole set. However, in many cases it is impossible to efficiently solve the
(1,C)
-median exactly (e.g., when
C,X=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathtt{d}
is the Euclidean metric). This is also true for the discrete Fréchet and Hausdorff distances for polygonal trajectories and finite point sets respectively. The following definition becomes helpful then.

Definition 2

(Weak sampling property)
.

Let
0<\varepsilon,\delta<1
be arbitrary.
(X,C,\mathtt{d})
is said to satisfy the weak sampling property for
\varepsilon,\delta
iff there exist positive integers
m_{\delta,\varepsilon}
and
t_{\delta,\varepsilon}
depending on
\delta,\varepsilon
such that for any
P\subseteq X
and a uniform random multiset
S\subseteq X
of size
m_{\delta,\varepsilon}
, there exists a set
\Gamma(S)\subseteq C
of size
t_{\delta,\varepsilon}
that satisfies

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\exists c\in\Gamma(S)\mid\sum_{p\in P}\mathtt{d}(p,c)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{p\in P}\mathtt{d}(p,c_{P})\right]\geq 1-\delta.

Furthermore,
\Gamma(S)
can be computed in time depending on
\delta,\varepsilon,|S|
but independent of
|P|
.

The weak sampling property characterizes those instances in which it is possible to generate a constant number of candidate cluster centers in time independent of the size of the input set, and at least one of which is guaranteed to be a good approximation to the optimal
1
-center of the input set. We later show that the discrete Fréchet and Hausdorff distances satisfy the weak sampling property. We use
m_{\delta,\varepsilon}
to denote the size of the random sample for both the strong and weak sampling properties.

Algorithm overview.

We give an overview of our algorithm, denoted
Cluster
, in Fig.
2
. It is similar to the algorithm
Cluster
from
[
1
]
, with the small but crucial difference being that the set of candidate cluster centers
\overline{C}_{S}
comes from
C
; this also changes how the candidates are generated. We show that with a careful choice of
C
, our instance satisfies one of the sampling properties (Definitions
1
,
2
), and we can apply the framework of Ackermann et al.
[
1
]
.

The algorithm takes as input the set of points
\overline{P}\subseteq P
that are yet to be assigned cluster centers, the number of cluster centers
\overline{k}
still to be computed, and the centers
\overline{C}\subseteq C
already computed. It returns the final set of cluster centers. To solve the
(k,C)
-median problem for
P
, we call
Cluster
(P,k,\{\})
with values of
\alpha,m_{\delta,\varepsilon},F
that we will specify later.

Briefly, the algorithm has two phases. In the pruning phase no new centers are added. Rather, the set
N
containing half of the points of
\overline{P}
closest to
\overline{C}
are removed from
\overline{P}
, and the algorithm is called recursively on
\overline{P}\setminus N
. In the sampling phase, new centers are added. The algorithm first samples a uniformly random multiset
S
of
\overline{P}
of size
\frac{2}{\alpha}m_{\delta,\varepsilon}
for some constant
\alpha
and
m_{\delta,\varepsilon}
to be defined later. Then for each subset
S^{\prime}\subset S
of size
m_{\delta,\varepsilon}
, a set of candidate centers
F(S^{\prime})
is generated; the function
F
varies depending on certain conditions satisfied by
(X,C,\mathtt{d})
; in particular
F(S^{\prime})=\{c_{S^{\prime}}\}
for the strong sampling property, and
F(S^{\prime})=\{\Gamma(S^{\prime})\}
for the weak sampling property. Each candidate center is in turn added to
\overline{C}
, and the algorithm is run recursively. Finally, the solution with the lowest cost is returned.

3
Clustering via sampling

We show that the
Cluster
algorithm (Fig.
2
) computes an approximate
(k,C)
-median for instances satisfying the sampling properties and for appropriate
\alpha,m_{\delta,\varepsilon}
and
F
, i.e., if we use
F(S^{\prime})=\{c_{S^{\prime}}\}
for the strong sampling property and
F(S^{\prime})=\Gamma(S^{\prime})
for the weak sampling property. The analysis closely follows that of Ackermann
et al.

[
1
]
, so we leave detailed proofs to Appendix
B
.

The
superset sampling lemma
(Appendix
B.1
, Lemma
17
) shows how to draw a uniform random multiset from
P^{\prime}\subseteq P
while only knowing
P
(without explicitly knowing
P^{\prime}
), provided
P^{\prime}
contains a constant fraction of the points of
P
. Using this lemma and the strong and weak sampling properties, we have the following. We leave the proof of the
superset sampling lemma
and the following lemma in Appendices
B.1
and
B.2
respectively.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\alpha<\frac{1}{4k}
be an arbitrary positive constant. Suppose
(X,C,\mathtt{d})
satisfies the strong or weak sampling property (Definitions
1
,
2
) for some
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
. Given
P\subseteq X
, algorithm
Cluster
run with input
(P,k,\{\})
and appropriate
F
computes a set
\tilde{C}\subseteq C
of size
k
such that

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\sum_{p\in P}\mathtt{d}(p,\tilde{C})\leq(1+8\alpha k^{2})(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{p\in P}\mathtt{d}(p,C^{*})\right]\geq\left(\frac{1-\delta}{5}\right)^{k},

where
C^{*}
is an optimal solution to the
(k,C)
-median problem for
P
.

Running time.

We characterize the running time of
Cluster
in terms of
\mathtt{d}
,
C
and
F
. Let
h(C)
denote the maximum time required to compute
\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,min}}_{y\in C}\mathtt{d}(x,y)
for any
x\in X
, and let
t(C)
denote the maximum time required to compute
\mathtt{d}(x,y)
for any
x\in X,y\in C
. Finally, let
w(m)=\max_{S\subseteq X,|S|=m}|F(S)|
, and let
f(m)
be the maximum number of operations needed to compute
F(S)
for any
S\subseteq X
of size
m
, where computing
\mathtt{d}
between points in
X
and
C
, and computing the closest point in
C
to any point in
X
count as one operation each. The proof is similar to the running time analysis from
[
1
]
, and is given in Appendix
B.3
.

Lemma 4
.

Suppose
(X,C,\mathtt{d})
satisfies the strong or weak sampling properties (Definitions
1
and
2
) for some
\epsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
. Given
P\subseteq X
containing
n

runs in time

\displaystyle n\cdot 2^{O\left(km_{\delta,\varepsilon}\log\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}m_{\delta,\varepsilon}\right)\right)}\cdot\left(w(m_{\delta,\varepsilon})\cdot f(m_{\delta,\varepsilon})\right)^{O(k)}\cdot(h(C)+t(C)).

By setting
\alpha=\frac{\varepsilon}{8k^{2}}
, the approximation factor in Lemma
3

(1+3\varepsilon)
. Moreover, the error probability can be made arbitrarily small by running the

2^{\Theta(k)}
times and taking the minimum cost solution, without changing the asymptotic running time. We thus get the following. Note that
w(m_{\delta,\varepsilon})
takes on values
1
and

for the strong and weak sampling properties respectively.

Theorem 5
.

Suppose
(X,C,\mathtt{d})
satisfies the strong sampling property (Definition

) for some

. Further, suppose

-approximate solution to the

can be computed in time

\displaystyle n\cdot 2^{O\left(km_{\delta,\varepsilon}\log\left(\frac{k}{\varepsilon}m_{\delta,\varepsilon}\right)\right)}\cdot a(m_{\delta,\varepsilon})^{O(k)}\cdot(h(C)+t(C)).

.

Suppose
(X,C,\mathtt{d})
satisfies the weak sampling property (Definition

) for some

. Further, suppose

can be computed in

operations, where computing

between points in

, and computing the closest point in

4
Covering metric spaces

We specify sufficient conditions on
C
for the strong and weak sampling properties to hold. These conditions characterize how well can certain subsets of
C
be
covered
using a small number of sets.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}=(X,\mathtt{d})
be a metric space. Given
x\in X
, let
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathtt{d}}(x,r)=\{x^{\prime}\in X\mid\mathtt{d}(x,x^{\prime})\leq r\}
denote the ball of radius
r
(under
\mathtt{d}
) centered at
x
; we will drop the subscript
\mathtt{d}
if it is clear from the context. An
r-cover
of a subset
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
for some
r>0
is a set
Y\subseteq X
such that

X^{\prime}\subseteq\bigcup_{y\in Y}{\mathcal{B}}(y,r).

Note that the elements of
Y
need not be in
X^{\prime}
. Also note that if
Y
is an
r
-cover for
X^{\prime}
, it is also an
r
-cover for any subset of
X^{\prime}
.

A subset
Y\subseteq X
is said to be
g-coverable
for some non-decreasing function
g:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
iff for all
y\in Y
and
r>r^{\prime}>0
, there exists an
r^{\prime}
-cover of
{\mathcal{B}}(y,r)\cap Y
of size at most
g(r/r^{\prime})
. Note that if
Y
is
g
-coverable then any subset
Y^{\prime}\subseteq Y
is also
g
-coverable.

Intuitively, if
C
has a small cover, then for any metric ball in
C
there exists a small set of points (not necessarily from
C
), termed the cover, such that the distance from any point in the ball to a point in the cover is smaller than the radius of the ball.

Sufficient conditions for the strong sampling property.

The following theorem gives sufficient conditions for the strong sampling property to hold in terms of coverability of
C
. The proof is similar to Lemma 3.4 of
[
1
]
but has been adapted to our setting, see Appendix
C
for more details.

Theorem 7
.

If
C
is
g
-coverable, and for any
P\subseteq X
,
c_{P}
can be computed in time depending only on
|P|
, then
(X,C,\mathtt{d})
satisfies the strong sampling property (Definition
1
) for any
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
. Here, the constant
m_{\delta,\varepsilon}=m_{\delta,\varepsilon,g}
also depends on
g
.

From Theorems
5
and
7
, we get the following.

Corollary 8
.

Suppose
C
is
g
-coverable and the optimal
(1,C)
-median of any subset of
X
can be computed in time depending on the size of the subset. Let
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
. Given
P\subseteq X
having
n
points and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, with probability
\geq 1-\delta
, a
(1+3\varepsilon)
-approximate solution to the
(k,C)
-median problem for
P
can be computed in time

\displaystyle n\cdot 2^{O\left(km_{\delta,\varepsilon,g}\log\left(\frac{k}{\varepsilon}m_{\delta,\varepsilon}\right)\right)}\cdot a(m_{\delta,\varepsilon,g})^{O(k)}\cdot(h(C)+t(C)),

where
m_{\delta,\varepsilon,g}
is a constant depending only on
\varepsilon,\delta,g
, and
a(m)
is the number of operations needed to compute the optimal
(1,C)
-median of
m
points in
X
, where computing
\mathtt{d}
between points in
X
and
C
, and computing the closest point in
C
to any point in
X
count as one operation each.

Sufficient conditions for the weak sampling property.

We give sufficient conditions for the weak sampling property to hold in terms of coverability of
C
.

For
Y\subseteq X
, let
\theta_{Y}\left(\frac{r}{r^{\prime}}\right)
be the number of operations required to compute an
r^{\prime}
-cover of
{\mathcal{B}}(y,r)\cap Y
for any
y\in Y
(if such a cover exists) where computing
\mathtt{d}
between points in
X
and
C
, and computing the closest point in
C
to any point in
X
count as one operation each (we assume that the number of operations can be expressed in terms of
\frac{r}{r^{\prime}}
).

The following lemma will be helpful, and states that if
C
has a small cover and if we have a good estimate of the
cost
of the optimal
(1,C)
-median, then we can construct a small set of points in
C
such that at least one of them is a good approximation to the optimal
(1,C)
-median. Further, this can be done in time independent of
|P|
. Both of these properties are necessary for the weak sampling property (Definition
2
).

Lemma 9
.

Let
P\subseteq X
. Suppose
C
is
g
-coverable. Then given
a,b
such that
a\leq\frac{1}{|P|}\sum_{p\in P}\mathtt{d}(c_{P},p)\leq b
, we can compute a set
Q\subseteq C
of size
O(g\left(\frac{4b}{\varepsilon\delta a}\right))
such that

\displaystyle Pr\left[\exists q\in Q\mid\sum_{p\in P}\mathtt{d}(p,q)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{p\in P}\mathtt{d}(p,c_{P})\right]\geq 1-\delta.

Further,
Q
can be computed in
O\left(\theta_{C}\left(\frac{4b}{\varepsilon\delta a}\right)+g\left(\frac{4b}{\varepsilon\delta a}\right)\right)
operations, where computing
\mathtt{d}
between points in
X
and
C
, and computing the closest point in
C
to any point in
X
count as one operation each.

Proof.

For any
x\in X
, we define
x^{\prime}=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,min}}_{y\in C}\mathtt{d}(x,y)
.
Consider a point
q\in P
chosen uniformly at random. By Markov’s inequality,
\mathtt{d}(q,c_{P})\leq\frac{1}{\delta|P|}\sum_{p\in P}\mathtt{d}(p,c_{P})
with probability
\geq 1-\delta
. In such a case,

\displaystyle\mathtt{d}(q^{\prime},c_{P})\leq\mathtt{d}(q^{\prime},q)+\mathtt{d}(q,c_{p})\leq 2\mathtt{d}(q,c_{P})\leq\frac{2}{\delta|P|}\sum_{p\in P}\mathtt{d}(p,c_{P}).

Thus,
c_{P}\in{\mathcal{B}}(q^{\prime},\frac{2b}{\delta})
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Let
C^{\prime}
be an
\left(\frac{\varepsilon a}{2}\right)
-cover of
{\mathcal{B}}(q^{\prime},\frac{2b}{\delta})\cap C
. Since
C
is
g
-coverable,
|C^{\prime}|=g\left(\frac{4b}{\varepsilon\delta a}\right)
. We will argue that
Q=\{q^{\prime}\}\cup\{c^{\prime}\mid c\in C^{\prime}\}
is the required solution. Let
x=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,min}}_{y\in C^{\prime}}\mathtt{d}(y,c_{P})
. Since
C^{\prime}
is an
\left(\frac{\varepsilon a}{2}\right)
-cover,
\mathtt{d}(x,c_{P})\leq\frac{\varepsilon a}{2}
. Also,
\mathtt{d}(x,x^{\prime})\leq\mathtt{d}(x,c_{P})\leq\frac{\varepsilon a}{2}
. Thus,
\mathtt{d}(x^{\prime},c_{P})\leq\mathtt{d}(x,x^{\prime})+\mathtt{d}(x,c_{P})\leq\varepsilon a
. We then have

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in P}\mathtt{d}(p,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p\in P}\left(\mathtt{d}(p,c_{P})+\mathtt{d}(x^{\prime},c_{P})\right)\leq\left(\sum_{p\in P}\mathtt{d}(p,c_{P})\right)+\varepsilon a|P|

\displaystyle\leq(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{p\in P}\mathtt{d}(p,c_{P}).

Computing
C^{\prime}
takes
\theta_{C}\left(\frac{4b}{\varepsilon\delta a}\right)
operations. Computing the output set takes
|C^{\prime}|+1=O\left(g\left(\frac{4b}{\varepsilon\delta a}\right)\right)
operations.
∎

The next theorem shows that with a small random sample of
P
, one of two things can happen. Either one of the samples is close to an approximate
(1,C)
-median, or we can approximate the cost of the optimal
(1,C)
-median in time independent of
|P|
. This along with Lemma
9
shows that the weak sampling property holds if
C
is
g
-coverable. The proof is inspired by the proof of Theorem 1 in
[
26
]
, and it also shows how to compute
\Gamma
for the weak sampling property (Definition
2
).

Theorem 10
.

If
C
is
g
-coverable, then
(X,C,\mathtt{d})
satisfies the weak sampling property (Definition
2
) for
0<\varepsilon<\frac{4}{9}
and
\left(1-\frac{5}{18}\varepsilon\right)<\delta<1
. Further, the constants
m_{\delta,\varepsilon}=1+\frac{4}{\varepsilon}
and
t_{\delta,\varepsilon}=O\left(g\left(\frac{2048}{\delta_{1}\varepsilon^{5}}\right)\right)
, and the number of operations needed to compute
\Gamma(S)
is
O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\theta_{C}\left(\frac{2048}{\delta_{1}\varepsilon^{5}}\right)+g\left(\frac{2048}{\delta_{1}\varepsilon^{5}}\right)\right)
, where
\delta_{1}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}-\frac{9}{5}(1-\delta)
; and computing
\mathtt{d}
between points in
X
and
C
, and computing the closest point in
C
to any point in
X
count as one operation each.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon_{1}=\frac{\varepsilon}{4}
and
\bar{r}=\frac{1}{|P|}\sum_{p\in P}\mathtt{d}(p,c_{P})
. Also, let
x^{\prime}=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,min}}_{y\in C}\mathtt{d}(x,y)
for any
x\in X
.

Let
Q\subseteq P
be a uniform random multiset of size
\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{1}}
, and
q\in P
be another point chosen uniformly at random. We will show that
Q\cup\{q\}
plays the role of
S
in Definition
2
.

Using Markov’s inequality and union bound, we have

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\mathtt{d}(q,c_{P})>\frac{\bar{r}}{2\varepsilon_{1}^{2}}\right]<2\varepsilon_{1}^{2}\text{ and }\Pr\left[\exists p\in Q\mid\mathtt{d}(p,c_{P})>\frac{\bar{r}}{2\varepsilon_{1}^{2}}\right]<\left(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon_{1}}\right)2\varepsilon_{1}^{2}=2\varepsilon_{1}.

Thus with probability
\geq 1-2\varepsilon_{1}-2\varepsilon_{1}^{2}
,
q
and
Q
are in
{\mathcal{B}}\left(c_{P},\frac{\bar{r}}{2\varepsilon_{1}^{2}}\right)
. We assume that this event happens. Now, by definition of
q^{\prime}
, we have
\mathtt{d}(q,q^{\prime})\leq\mathtt{d}(q,c_{P})
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathtt{d}(q^{\prime},c_{P})\leq\mathtt{d}(q,q^{\prime})+\mathtt{d}(q,c_{P})\leq 2\mathtt{d}(q,c_{P})\leq\frac{\bar{r}}{\varepsilon_{1}^{2}}.

Let
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}={\mathcal{B}}\left(c_{P},\frac{\bar{r}}{\varepsilon_{1}^{2}}\right)
,
{\mathcal{B}}_{2}={\mathcal{B}}\left(q^{\prime},\varepsilon_{1}\bar{r}\right)
and
P^{\prime}=P\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{1}
. Then,
q^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}
and
Q\subseteq P^{\prime}
. We consider two cases now.

Case 1:

P^{\prime}
has at least
2\varepsilon_{1}|P^{\prime}|
points outside
{\mathcal{B}}_{2}
. For any
p\in Q
, the probability
p
is outside
{\mathcal{B}}_{2}
is
2\varepsilon_{1}
. Thus, with probability at least
2\varepsilon_{1}
, there exists
p\in Q
such that
\mathtt{d}(p,q^{\prime})\geq\varepsilon_{1}\bar{r}
and hence
\sum_{p\in Q}\mathtt{d}(p,q^{\prime})\geq\varepsilon_{1}\bar{r}
.
Also,
\mathtt{d}(p,q^{\prime})\leq\frac{2\bar{r}}{\varepsilon_{1}^{2}}
for any
p\in Q
. Hence,
\sum_{p\in Q}\mathtt{d}(p,q^{\prime})\leq\frac{2\bar{r}}{\varepsilon_{1}^{3}}
.

Let
\delta_{1}=2\varepsilon_{1}-\frac{9}{5}(1-\delta)
. We can now use Lemma
9
with
a=\frac{\varepsilon_{1}^{3}}{2}\sum_{p\in Q}\mathtt{d}(p,q^{\prime})
and
b=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{1}}\sum_{p\in Q}\mathtt{d}(p,q^{\prime})
to compute a set
Q_{1}\subseteq C
of
O\left(g\left(\frac{4b}{\varepsilon\delta_{1}a}\right)\right)=O\left(g\left(\frac{2048}{\delta_{1}\varepsilon^{5}}\right)\right)
candidate centers, one of which is a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate center with probability at least
1-\delta_{1}
. The total probability of getting a good set of candidate centers is
(2\varepsilon_{1}-\delta_{1})(1-2\varepsilon_{1}-2\varepsilon_{1}^{2})>1-\delta
.

Case 2:

P^{\prime}
has at most
2\varepsilon_{1}|P^{\prime}|
points putside
{\mathcal{B}}_{2}
. We further consider two cases.

Case 2(a):

\mathtt{d}(q^{\prime},c_{P})\leq 4\varepsilon_{1}\bar{r}.
Then

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in P}\mathtt{d}(p,q^{\prime})\leq\sum_{p\in P}\left(\mathtt{d}(p,c_{p})+\mathtt{d}(q^{\prime},c_{p})\right)\leq(1+4\varepsilon_{1})\sum_{p\in P}\mathtt{d}(p,c_{P})\leq(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{p\in P}\mathtt{d}(p,c_{P}).

Case 2(b):

\mathtt{d}(q^{\prime},c_{P})>4\varepsilon_{1}\bar{r}.
Suppose we assign all points from
c_{P}
to
q^{\prime}
. By an averaging argument, we have
|P^{\prime}|\geq(1-\varepsilon_{1}^{2})|P|
. Then, the number of points of
P
that are outside
{\mathcal{B}}_{2}
is at most

\displaystyle|P\setminus P^{\prime}|+2\varepsilon_{1}|P^{\prime}|=

\displaystyle|P|-(1-2\varepsilon_{1})|P^{\prime}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|P|-(1-2\varepsilon_{1})(1-\varepsilon_{1}^{2})|P|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\varepsilon_{1}^{2}+2\varepsilon_{1}(1-\varepsilon_{1}^{2}))|P|.

Thus,
|P\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{2}|\geq(1-\varepsilon_{1}^{2}-2\varepsilon_{1}(1-\varepsilon_{1}^{2}))|P|
.
Now, for
p\in P\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{2}
, the decrease in cost on switching from
c_{P}
to
q^{\prime}
is at least

\displaystyle\mathtt{d}(p,c_{P})-\mathtt{d}(p,q^{\prime})\geq\mathtt{d}(p,c_{P})-\varepsilon_{1}\bar{r}\geq\mathtt{d}(q^{\prime},c_{P})-2\varepsilon_{1}\bar{r}.

For
p\in P\setminus{\mathcal{B}}_{2}
, the increase in cost on switching from
c_{P}
to
q^{\prime}
is at most

\displaystyle\mathtt{d}(p,q^{\prime})-\mathtt{d}(p,c_{P})\leq\mathtt{d}(q^{\prime},c_{P}).

The overall decrease in cost is

\displaystyle|P\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{2}|(\mathtt{d}(q^{\prime},c_{P})-2\varepsilon_{1}\bar{r})-|P\setminus{\mathcal{B}}_{2}|\mathtt{d}(q^{\prime},c_{P})>0

for our choice of
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\mathtt{d}(q^{\prime},c_{P})>4\varepsilon_{1}\bar{r}
. But
c_{P}
is the optimal
(1,C)
-median of
P
, a contradiction. Hence case 2(b) cannot occur.

From the two cases above, we can see that
Q_{1}\cup\{q^{\prime}\}
plays the role of
\Gamma(S)
in Definition
2
.

Sampling
Q,q
takes time
O(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
. Computing
\sum_{p\in P}\mathtt{d}(p,q^{\prime})
involves
O(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
computations of
\mathtt{d}
between a pair of points from
X
, at least one of which comes from
C
. Computing the set of candidates takes
O\left(\theta_{C}\left(\frac{2048}{\delta_{1}\varepsilon^{5}}\right)+g\left(\frac{2048}{\delta_{1}\varepsilon^{5}}\right)\right)
operations. Thus, total number of operations needed is
O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\theta_{C}\left(\frac{2048}{\delta_{1}\varepsilon^{5}}\right)+g\left(\frac{2048}{\delta_{1}\varepsilon^{5}}\right)\right)
. Moreover,
m_{\delta,\varepsilon}=|Q\cup\{q\}|=1+\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{1}}=1+\frac{4}{\varepsilon}
, and
t_{\delta,\varepsilon}=|Q_{1}\cup\{q^{\prime}\}|=O\left(g\left(\frac{2048}{\delta_{1}\varepsilon^{5}}\right)\right)
.
∎

From Theorems
6
and
10
, we get the following.

Corollary 11
.

Suppose
C
is
g
-coverable. Let
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{4}{9}),\delta\in(1-\frac{5}{18}\varepsilon,1)
. Given
P\subseteq X
of size
n
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, with probability
\geq 1-\delta
, a
(1+3\varepsilon)
-approximate solution to the
(k,C)
-median problem for
P
can be computed in time

\displaystyle n\cdot 2^{O\left(\frac{k}{\varepsilon}\log\left(\frac{k}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)}\cdot\left(b(m_{\delta,\varepsilon})\cdot t_{\delta,\varepsilon}\right)^{O(k)}\cdot(h(C)+t(C)),

where
b(m_{\delta,\varepsilon})=O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\theta_{C}\left(\frac{2048}{\delta_{1}\varepsilon^{5}}\right)+g\left(\frac{2048}{\delta_{1}\varepsilon^{5}}\right)\right)
,
t_{\delta,\varepsilon}=O\left(g\left(\frac{2048}{\delta_{1}\varepsilon^{5}}\right)\right)
, and
\delta_{1}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}-\frac{9}{5}(1-\delta)
.

5
Clustering discrete Fréchet and Hausdorff distances

In this section, we show how the results from the previous section can be used to cluster trajectories and point sets under the discrete Fréchet and Hausdorff distances respectively.

Clustering under discrete Fréchet distance.

Recall that
T^{l}
is the set of all trajectories in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
having at most
l
points each; thus
T=\bigcup_{l>0}T^{l}
is the set of all trajectories in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Given
{\mathcal{T}}=(T,\mathtt{d}_{F})
and trajectories
P\subseteq T
, the
(k,l)
-median problem
[
13
,
10
]
is equivalent to the
(k,T^{l})
-median problem in our setting, i.e., the center trajectories contain at most
l
points. We show that
T^{l}
is
g
-coverable for some
g
that depends on
l
.

Lemma 12
.

T^{l}
is
g
-coverable under
\mathtt{d}_{F}
for
g(x)=l^{2l}\cdot x^{O(dl)}
. Further, an
r^{\prime}
-cover of
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathtt{d}_{F}}(\gamma,r)\cap T^{l}
for
r>r^{\prime}>0
and
\gamma\in T^{l}
can be computed in
l^{2l}\cdot\left(\frac{r}{r^{\prime}}\right)^{O(dl)}
time.

Proof.

Let
r>r^{\prime}>0
be arbitrary. Let
\gamma=\langle p_{1},\ldots,p_{l^{\prime}}\rangle\in T^{l}
for some
l^{\prime}\leq l
. Since the Euclidean metric in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
has doubling dimension
O(d)
, for any
p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
there exist
\left(\frac{r}{r^{\prime}}\right)^{O(d)}
points in the Euclidean ball
{\mathcal{B}}_{E}(p,r)
centered at
p
such that any point in
{\mathcal{B}}_{E}(p,r)
is at most
r^{\prime}
distance away from one of these points; denote these points by
B_{p}(r,r^{\prime})
. Consider the set of points
\bigcup_{i=1}^{l^{\prime}}B_{p_{i}}(r,r^{\prime})
; this set has cardinality
l^{\prime}\cdot\left(\frac{r}{r^{\prime}}\right)^{O(d)}
.

Next, consider the set of all trajectories
T^{\prime}
defined by at most
2l
points from
\bigcup_{i=1}^{l^{\prime}}B_{p_{i}}(r,r^{\prime})
and containing at least one point from
B_{p_{i}}(r,r^{\prime})
for every
i
; further these points respect the ordering of the sets that they belong to, i.e., if
p\in B_{p_{i}}(r,r^{\prime})
,
q\in B_{p_{j}}(r,r^{\prime})
, and
i<j
, then
p
appears before
q
in the trajectory (for points coming from the same set
B_{p_{i}}(r,r^{\prime})
all possible orderings are considered). Note that
|T^{\prime}|\leq\left(l^{\prime}\cdot\left(\frac{r}{r^{\prime}}\right)^{O(d)}\right)^{2l}
. Further,
\mathtt{d}_{F}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})\leq r
for all
\gamma^{\prime}\in T^{\prime}
.

We will show that for any
\gamma^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathtt{d}_{F}}(\gamma,r)\cap T^{l}
, there exists
\gamma^{\prime}\in T^{\prime}
such that
\mathtt{d}_{F}(\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime})\leq r^{\prime}
. Thus
T^{\prime}
is the desired cover, completing the first part of our proof. Let
\gamma^{\prime\prime}=\langle q_{1},\ldots,q_{l^{\prime\prime}}\rangle
for some
l^{\prime\prime}\leq l
. By definition of
\mathtt{d}_{F}
and the fact that
\mathtt{d}_{F}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime\prime})\leq r
, each
q_{i}
has a corresponding sequence of points
\langle p_{j_{i}},p_{j_{i}+1},\ldots,p_{j^{\prime}_{i}}\rangle
each of which is at most
r
distance away from
q_{i}
. Moreover, for all
1\leq i<l^{\prime\prime}
we have
j^{\prime}_{i}\leq j_{i+1}\leq j^{\prime}_{i}+1
, and
j_{1}=1,j^{\prime}_{l^{\prime\prime}}=l^{\prime}
.

For each
q_{i}
and
j\in\{j_{i},j_{i}+1,\ldots,j^{\prime}_{i}\}
, let
u_{j}
denote the point in
B_{p_{j}}(r,r^{\prime})
that is closest to
q_{i}
. Note that
q_{i}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{E}(p_{j},r)
and
\left\lVert q_{i}-u_{j}\right\rVert\leq r^{\prime}
. Consider the sequence of points
\gamma(q_{i})=\langle u_{j_{i}},u_{j_{i}+1},\ldots,u_{j^{\prime}_{i}}\rangle
. Then,
\mathtt{d}_{F}(\langle q_{i}\rangle,\gamma(q_{i}))\leq r^{\prime}
. Let
\gamma^{\prime}
be the trajectory obtained by concatenating
\gamma(q_{1}),\ldots,\gamma(q_{l^{\prime\prime}})
in order. Then we get
\mathtt{d}_{F}(\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime})\leq r^{\prime}
. Further, by construction
\gamma^{\prime\prime}\in T^{\prime}
.

As far as running time is concerned, computing the set
\bigcup_{i=1}^{l^{\prime}}B_{p_{i}}(r,r^{\prime})
takes time
l^{\prime}\cdot\left(\frac{r}{r^{\prime}}\right)^{O(d)}
. From this set, computing
T^{\prime}
takes time
l^{2l}\cdot\left(\frac{r}{r^{\prime}}\right)^{O(dl)}
.
∎

For a trajectory having
m
points, computing
\mathtt{d}_{F}
to any trajectory in
T^{l}
takes time
O(ml)

6
Hardness of
k
-median clustering under Hausdorff distance

We prove the following hardness result for
k
-median clustering under the Hausdorff distance.

Theorem 16
.

The
k
-median clustering problem for finite point sets under the Hausdorff distance is
NP-Hard
.

Proof.

We reduce the Euclidean
k
-median problem, which is known to be
NP-Hard

[
29
]
. The reduction is fairly straightforward – for each input point
p
of an instance of the Euclidean
k
-median problem, we have a singleton set
\{p\}
as input to the Haudorff
k
-median problem. Any solution to the Euclidean
k
-median problem is also a solution to the Hausdorff
k
-median problem of the same cost – we replace cluster center
c
in the Euclidean version by the cluster center
\{c\}
for the Haudorff version, and for each
p
assigned to
c
, we assign
\{p\}
to
\{c\}
.

On the other hand, consider a solution to the instance of Hausdorff
k
-median problem. In particular, let
S
be a cluster center that is assigned the sets
\{\{p_{1}\},\ldots,\{p_{n}\}\}
. The cost of this single cluster is

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathtt{d}_{H}(\{p_{i}\},S)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max_{s\in S}\left\lVert s-p_{i}\right\rVert,

by the definition of
\mathtt{d}_{H}
. Thus, replacing
S
by a singleton set
\{s\}
for any
s\in S
does not increase the cost of clustering. Hence we can assume that all cluster centers are singleton sets. We can then construct a solution for the Euclidean
k
-median problem by assigning
p
to
s
, where
\{s\}
is the cluster center that
\{p\}
was assigned to in the Hausdorff clustering solution. This does not increase the cost of the clustering as well.
∎

7
Conclusion

We have given a framework for clustering where the cluster centers are restricted to belong to a simpler metric space. We characterized general conditions on this simpler space that allow us to obtain efficient
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithms for the
k
-median problem. As special cases, we gave efficient algorithms for clustering trajectories and point sets under the discrete Fréchet and Hausdorff distances respectively.

We believe the general framework can be extended to other metric spaces as well. The next step would be to see if it can be applied to the continuous Fréchet distance, and to non-metric distance measures such as dynamic time warping. It would be interesting to provide other characterizations on the metric space for the cluster centers (as alternatives to the notion of covering discussed in this paper) that are amenable to efficient clustering algorithms.

Appendix A
Doubling dimension of Fréchet and Hausdorff distance

We show that the discrete Fréchet distance does not have a doubling dimension bounded by a constant. The Hausdorff case can be shown similarly.

Suppose
{\mathcal{T}}=(T,\mathtt{d}_{F})
has a constant doubling dimension
D
. Then by definition, for any trajectory
\gamma\in T
and any
r>0
, there exist
2^{D}
trajectories
T^{\prime}\subset T
such that any trajectory
\gamma^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathtt{d}_{F}}(\gamma,r)
is at most at distance
\frac{r}{2}
from a trajectory in
T^{\prime}
. By the pigeon hole principle, any set of
2^{D}+1
or more trajectories in
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathtt{d}_{F}}(\gamma,r)
will have at least two trajectories that have the same closest trajectory in
T^{\prime}
, and are therefore at most
r
distance apart (by the triangle inequality).

Consider a trajectory
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
consisting of a sequence of points
\langle p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{m}\rangle
in a straight line in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with a distance of at least
3r
between every consecutive pair of points, for some
m,r>0
. For each
p_{i}
, let
P_{i}=\{a_{i},b_{i}\}
be such that
\left\lVert a_{i}-b_{i}\right\rVert>r
and
\left\lVert a_{i}-p_{i}\right\rVert,\left\lVert b_{i}-p_{i}\right\rVert\leq r
(e.g.,
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
can be the diametrically opposite points of the Euclidean ball of radius
r
centered at
p_{i}
). Consider the set of trajectories
T^{\prime\prime}=\{\langle q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{m}\rangle\mid q_{i}\in P_{i}\}
. Then by construction,
T^{\prime\prime}\subset{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathtt{d}_{F}}(\gamma^{\prime\prime},r)
, and
\mathtt{d}_{F}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})>r
for any
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in T^{\prime\prime}
. Further,
|T^{\prime\prime}|=2^{m}\geq 2^{D}+1
for sufficiently large
m
, contradicting the fact that
{\mathcal{T}}=(T,\mathtt{d}_{F})
has doubling dimension
D
.
