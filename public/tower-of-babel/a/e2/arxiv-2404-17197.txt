Review of martingale basics

Random variables will be usually defined on a filtered probability space
(\Omega,(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\mu)
.
We denote by
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\cup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{F}_{n}
.
The following examples are useful to keep in mind.

Example 0.1

(Dyadic filtration)
.

\Omega=[0,1]
,
\mu
Lebesgue measure,
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the dyadic intervals of length
2^{-n}
, that is, intervals of the form
[2^{-n}k,2^{-n}(k+1)]
with
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

A higher dimensional version involves dyadic cubes.
One can also construct similar filtrations on more general manifolds, or even metric measure spaces.

Example 0.2

(Atomic filtrations)
.

\Omega=[0,1]
,
\mu
Lebesgue measure,
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is a
\sigma
-algebra generated by finitely many intervals.

We recall that an
atom
for a measure
\mu
on a
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}
is a set
A\in\mathcal{F}
such that
\mu(A)>0
and, for every
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
with
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
, we have
\mu(A^{\prime})\in\{0,\mu(A)\}
.
In an atomic
\sigma
-algebra, every measurable set is a finite union of atoms.

One can view martingale analysis as analysis of atomic filtrations, if we do not allow any constants to depend on the filtration.
All results that we are interested in can be transferred from atomic filtrations to general filtrations.
However, it is technically convenient to always use general filtrations, since they can appear in applications.

Example 0.3
.

\Omega=[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}}
,
\mu
the product of Lebesgue measures,

\mathcal{F}_{n}=\{B\times[0,1]^{\{n,n+1,\dotsc\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}B\subseteq[0,1]^{\{0,\dotsc,n-1\}}\text{ Borel}\}.

(0.1)

This filtration appears in the analysis of independent random variables, and gives a good idea of how a general filtration looks like.

An
adapted process
is a sequence of functions
(f_{n})
such that, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the function
f_{n}
is
\mathcal{F}_{n}
-measurable.

An adapted process
f
is called
predictable
if, for every
n>0
, the function
f_{n}
is
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
-measurable.

For nested
\sigma
-algebras
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
on
\Omega
, the
conditional expectation
is the orthogonal projection

\mathbb{E}(\cdot|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}):L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mu)\to L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}^{\prime},\mu).

The conditional expectation has the following properties.
Here and later, all identities and inequalities are meant to hold almost surely, unless mentioned otherwise.

1.

\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{1}|\mathcal{F}^{\prime})=\mathbf{1}
.

2.

For every
p\in[1,\infty]
,
\mathbb{E}
extends to a contraction
L^{p}(\mathcal{F})\to L^{p}(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})

3.

\int\mathbb{E}(f|\mathcal{F}^{\prime})=\int f
for every
f\in L^{1}(\mathcal{F})
.

4.

Positivity:
f\geq 0\implies\mathbb{E}(f|\mathcal{F}^{\prime})\geq 0
.

5.

Assume that
f\in L^{1}(\mathcal{F})
,
g\in L^{0}(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
, and either
fg\in L^{1}(\mathcal{F})
or
f\geq 0,\mathbb{E}(f|\mathcal{F}^{\prime})g\in L^{1}(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
. Then

\mathbb{E}(fg|\mathcal{F}^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}(f|\mathcal{F}^{\prime})g\text{ in }L^{1}(\mathcal{F}^{\prime}).

Suppose that
(X,\mathcal{F},\mu)
is a regular measure space and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
is a sub-
\sigma
-algebra.
Then there exists an essentially unique measurable map
(X,\mathcal{F}^{\prime})\to\mathcal{M}(X)
,
y\mapsto\mu_{y}
such that, for every
f\in L^{1}(\mathcal{F})
, for
\mu
-a.e.
y\in X
, we have
f\in L^{1}(X,\mu_{y})
, and
\int f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{y}=\mathbb{E}(f|\mathcal{F}^{\prime})(y)
.
This map is called a
measure disintegration
.
A measure disintegration satisfies
\mu_{x}=\mu_{y}
for
\mu
-a.e.
y
and
\mu_{y}
-a.e.
x
.

Example 0.4
.

If
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is an atomic filtration, then we can choose a collection of disjoint atoms
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
with
\mu(\cup_{A\in\mathcal{A}}A)=1
.
Then, for each
x\in A\in\mathcal{A}
, we can set
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{x}:=\mu(A)^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{A}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu
, and

\mathbb{E}(f|\mathcal{F}^{\prime})(x)=\mu(A)^{-1}\int_{A}f(x^{\prime})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x^{\prime}).

Example 0.5
.

Let
\Omega=[0,1]^{2}
with the Lebesgue measure,
\mathcal{F}
the Borel
\sigma
-algebra, and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
the Borel
\sigma
-algebra in the first variable, like in (
7.5
).
Then, we can choose
\mu_{(x,y)}
to be the Lebesgue measure on
\{x\}\times[0,1]
, and

\mathbb{E}(f|\mathcal{F}^{\prime})(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}f(x,y^{\prime})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y^{\prime}.

A
martingale
is an adapted process with values in
\mathbb{C}
(later also in a Banach space) such that, for every
m\leq n
, the function
f_{n}
is integrable, and we have

f_{m}=\mathbb{E}(f_{n}|\mathcal{F}_{m}).

Example 0.6
.

Sums of independent random variables

Example 0.7

(Dyadic martingale)
.

Let
f
be an integrable function on
[0,1]
with the Lebesgue measure and
f_{n}=\mathbb{E}(f|\mathcal{F}_{n})
(the same definition works for any filtration).

This construction can often be used to transfer results from martingales to a real analysis setting.
The main difference between the analysis of dyadic martingales and general martingales is that the Calderón–Zygmund decomposition does not work for general martingales.
A substitute that does work for general martingales is the Gundy decomposition.

The main reason why this construction does not produce all possible martingales is that it may happen that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\lVert f_{n}\rVert=\infty
, in which case there might be no function
f
with
f_{n}=\mathbb{E}(f|\mathcal{F}_{n})
.
A well-known example involves a doubling betting strategy.

Example 0.8
.

Any integrable process can be written as the sum of a process with predictable jumps and a martingale.

A
stopping time
is a function
\tau:\Omega\to\bar{\mathbb{N}}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
such that, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\{\tau\leq n\}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
.

Example 0.9
.

Any constant function is a stopping time.

Example 0.10

(Hitting time)
.

If
f
is an adapted process with values in a metric space
X
and
B\subseteq X
is a Borel set, then

\tau:=\inf\{t\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}f_{t}\in B\}

is a stopping time, called the
first hitting time
of
B
.

If
\sigma,\tau
are stopping times, then
\sigma\wedge\tau
and
\sigma\vee\tau
are also stopping times.

For a stopping time
\tau
and a process
f
, the
stopped process

f^{\tau}
is defined by

f^{\tau}_{t}:=f_{\tau\wedge t}.

If
f
is a martingale, then the stopped process
f^{\tau}
is again a martingale.

If
\tau
is a stopping time, the corresponding
\sigma
-algebra is defined by

\mathcal{F}_{\tau}:=\{A\in\mathcal{F}_{\infty}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}(\forall n)A\cap\{\tau\leq n\}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}\}.

The function
\tau
is
\mathcal{F}_{\tau}
-measurable.
We abbreviate
\mathbb{E}_{\tau}f:=\mathbb{E}(f|\mathcal{F}_{\tau})
.

The
optional sampling
theorem says that, for every discrete time martingale
f
, bounded stopping time
\tau
, and another stopping time
\sigma
, we have

f_{\sigma\wedge\tau}=\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}f_{\tau}.

Maximal and square functions

You are probably already familiar with the Lebesgue differentialtion theorem.
The best proof of that theorem uses the maximal operator to absorb error terms.
This idea is used in many places in analysis, and the study of martingales is one of these places.

Definition 1.1

(Maximal operator)
.

For an adapted process
f
with values in a normed space, we write

Mf_{n}=(Mf)_{n}:=\sup_{k\leq n}\lvert f_{k}\rvert.

Remark
.

Since our martingales are indexed by a countable set, we can use a pointwise supremum here.
In continuous time, we would need a lattice supremum.

A
submartingale
is an adapted process with values in
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that, for every
m\leq n
, we have

f_{m}\leq\mathbb{E}_{m}f_{n}.

Example 1.2
.

If
(f_{n})
is a martingale, then
(\lvert f_{n}\rvert)
is a submartingale.

Example 1.3
.

If
f
is any adapted process, then
Mf
is a submartingale.

If
f
is a submartingale, then, for any
p\in[1,\infty]
and
m\leq n
, we have

\lVert f_{m}\rVert_{p}\leq\lVert f_{n}\rVert_{p}.

We define the
L^{p}
norm of a (sub-)martingale by

\lVert f\rVert_{p}:=\sup_{n}\lVert f_{n}\rVert_{p}.

With this definition, the maximal operator is clearly bounded on
L^{\infty}
.
The next result looks a lot like an
L^{1}\to L^{1,\infty}
bound for
M
, but is in fact stronger and more convenient to use.

Lemma 1.4
.

Let
f
be a submartingale with values in
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
Then, for every
\lambda>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\lambda\lvert\{Mf_{n}>\lambda\}\rvert\leq\int_{\{Mf_{n}>\lambda\}}f_{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu.

Proof.

For a fixed
\lambda
, define the stopping time

\tau:=\inf\{k\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}f_{k}>\lambda\}.

Then,
\{Mf_{n}>\lambda\}=\{\tau\leq n\}
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\lambda\lvert\{Mf_{n}>\lambda\}\rvert

\displaystyle=\lambda\sum_{k\leq n}\lvert\{\tau=k\}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\leq n}\int_{\{\tau=k\}}f_{k}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu

(submartingale property)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\leq n}\int_{\{\tau=k\}}f_{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=\int_{\{Mf_{n}>\lambda\}}f_{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu.\qed

Remark

(
L^{1,\infty}
norm)
.

By Chebychev’s inequality, for any measurable function
g
, we have

\sup_{\lambda>0}\lambda^{-1}\lvert\{g>\lambda\}\rvert\leq\lVert g\rVert_{1}.

The converse inequality is false with any constant, as shown by the example
g(x)=1/x
on the measure space
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
with the Lebesgue measure.
The left-hand side of the above inequality is the so-called
L^{1,\infty}
seminorm of
g
.
As suggested by the notation,
L^{1,\infty}
is part of a larger family of Lorentz spaces, which is in turn contained in Orlicz spaces, but we will not talk about such generalities.

Theorem 1.5

(Doob’s maximal inequality)
.

Let
f
be a submartingale with values in
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
Then, for every
p\in(1,\infty]
, we have

\lVert Mf\rVert_{p}\leq p^{\prime}\lVert f\rVert_{p}.

Here and later,
p^{\prime}
denotes the Hölder conjugate:
1/p+1/p^{\prime}=1
.

Proof.

By the layer cake formula and Lemma
1.4
, we write

\displaystyle\lVert Mf_{n}\rVert_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}p\lambda^{p-1}\mu(Mf_{n}>\lambda)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}p\lambda^{p-2}\int_{\{Mf_{n}>\lambda\}}f_{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{Mf_{n}}p\lambda^{p-2}f_{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{p-1}\int_{\Omega}(Mf_{n})^{p-1}f_{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p}{p-1}(\int_{\Omega}f_{n}^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu)^{1/p}(\int_{\Omega}(Mf_{n})^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu)^{1-1/p}.

Since
f_{n}\in L^{p}
implies
f_{k}\in L^{p}
for all
k\leq n
by
L^{p}
contractivity of conditional expectations, we have
Mf_{n}\in L^{p}
.
Hence, we can cancel suitable powers of
\lVert Mf_{n}\rVert_{p}
on both sides and obtain the claim.
∎

There are other ways to deduce Theorem
1.5
from Lemma
1.4
, such as real interpolation with the
L^{\infty}
estimate.
But the above proof does not rely on the
L^{\infty}
estimate, only on the
L^{1}
-like estimate in Lemma
1.4
.

It is often the case in martingale theory that inequalities near
L^{1}
are the most powerful ones.
One can justify this by the observation that
L^{1}
is the minimal assumption required to even define a martingale.

If
f
is a martingale, then the increments
df_{k}
are orthogonal in the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Indeed, more generally, for any
n
and
g\in L^{0}(\mathcal{F}_{n-1})
, we have

\mathbb{E}(gdf_{n})=\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{E}(gdf_{n}|\mathcal{F}_{n-1}))=\mathbb{E}(g\mathbb{E}(df_{n}|\mathcal{F}_{n-1}))=\mathbb{E}(g\cdot 0)=0.

(1.1)

Definition 1.6

(Square function)
.

For a martingale
f
, we write

Sf_{n}:=\bigl(\sum_{k\leq n}\lvert df_{k}\rvert^{2}\bigr)^{1/2}.

In continuous time, the analog of
Sf
is denoted by
[f]
and called the
quadratic variation
.
We will construct it in (
6.12
).

Here and later, for notational simplicity, we will consider martingales with
f_{0}=0
.
Since martingale increments
df_{k}
are orthogonal, we have

\lVert f_{n}\rVert_{2}=\lVert Sf_{n}\rVert_{2}.

Next, we will see that
S
is bounded from
L^{1}
to
L^{1,\infty}
.
The proof that we present for this fact uses a summation by parts identity of a kind that also appears e.g. in the Itô formula.

Lemma 1.7
.

Let
f
be a martingale,
\lambda>0
, and
\tau:=\inf\{n\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\lvert f_{n}\rvert>\lambda\}
.
Then,

\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}(\lvert df_{k}\rvert^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\tau>k})\leq 2\lambda\lVert f\rVert_{1}.

On the left-hand side of the above estimate, we are taking the expectation of
S_{\tau-1}
.
Note that
\tau-1
is not a stopping time.
Such predictability emulation will also be important in the BDG inequality below.

Proof.

By the monotone convergence theorem, it suffices to consider the case
f_{N}=f_{N+1}=\dotsb
for an arbitrarily large
N
.
Then, we can replace
\tau
by
\tau\wedge N+1
without changing the inequality.
This is necessary to apply the optional sampling theorem.

We use the identity

\sum_{k<\tau}\lvert df_{k}\rvert^{2}+\lvert f_{\tau-1}\rvert^{2}=2f_{\tau}f_{\tau-1}-2\sum_{k\leq\tau}f_{k-1}df_{k}.

Note that

\{k\leq\tau\}=\Omega\setminus\{\tau<k\}=\Omega\setminus\{\tau\leq k-1\}\in\mathcal{F}_{k-1}.

Therefore, by (
1.1
), we have

\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{1}_{k\leq\tau}f_{k-1}df_{k})=0.

Hence,

\mathbb{E}\sum_{k<\tau}\lvert df_{k}\rvert^{2}+\mathbb{E}\lvert f_{\tau-1}\rvert^{2}=2\mathbb{E}(f_{\tau}f_{\tau-1}).

Therefore,

\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}(\lvert df_{k}\rvert^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\tau>k})\leq 2\mathbb{E}\lvert f_{\tau}f_{\tau-1}\rvert\leq 2\mathbb{E}\lvert f_{\tau}\lambda\rvert\leq 2\lambda\lVert f\rVert_{1},

where the last step follows from the optional sampling theorem, which gives in particular
f_{\tau}=\mathbb{E}_{\tau}f_{N+1}
.
∎

Corollary 1.8
.

Let
f
be a martingale and
\lambda>0
.
Then,

\lvert\{Sf>\lambda\}\rvert\leq 3\lambda^{-1}\lVert f\rVert_{1}.

Proof.

Let
\tau:=\inf\{n\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\lvert f_{n}\rvert>\lambda\}
.
Then

\lvert\{Sf>\lambda\}\rvert\leq\lvert\{Sf_{\tau-1}>\lambda\}\rvert+\lvert\{\tau<\infty\}\rvert\leq\lambda^{-2}\lVert Sf_{\tau-1}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda^{-1}\lVert f\rVert_{1}.

By Lemma
1.7
, the first summand is
\leq 2\lambda^{-1}\lVert f\rVert_{1}
.
∎

Lemma 1.9

(Davis decomposition)
.

For every martingale
(f_{n})
, there exists a decomposition
f=f^{\mathrm{pred}}+f^{\mathrm{bv}}
as a sum of two martingales such that
f^{\mathrm{pred}}
has a predictable bound on jumps:

\lvert df^{\mathrm{pred}}_{n}\rvert\leq 2Mdf_{n-1},

(1.2)

and
f^{\mathrm{bv}}
has bounded variation:

\mathbb{E}\sum_{n}\lvert df^{\mathrm{bv}}_{n}\rvert\leq 2\mathbb{E}Mdf.

(1.3)

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle dg_{n}

\displaystyle:=\min(1,\frac{Mdf_{n-1}}{\lvert df_{n}\rvert})df_{n},

\displaystyle df^{\mathrm{pred}}_{n}

\displaystyle:=dg_{n}-\mathbb{E}_{n-1}(dg_{n}),

\displaystyle dh_{n}

\displaystyle:=df_{n}-dg_{n}=\max(0,1-\frac{Mdf_{n-1}}{\lvert df_{n}\rvert})df_{n},

\displaystyle df^{\mathrm{bv}}_{n}

\displaystyle:=dh_{n}-\mathbb{E}_{n-1}(dh_{n}).

Then, by definition,

\lvert dg_{n}\rvert\leq Mdf_{n-1},

and, by positivity of conditional expectation, also

\lvert\mathbb{E}_{n-1}(dg_{n})\rvert\leq\mathbb{E}_{n-1}(\lvert dg_{n}\rvert)\leq\mathbb{E}_{n-1}(Mdf_{n-1})=Mdf_{n-1}.

This implies (
1.2
).

On the other hand, we have the telescoping bound

\lvert dh_{n}\rvert=\max(0,\lvert df_{n}\rvert-Mdf_{n-1})=Mdf_{n}-Mdf_{n-1},

which implies (
1.3
).
∎

Theorem 1.10

(Davis inequalities)
.

Let
f
be a martingale with
f_{0}=0
.
Then,

\mathbb{E}Sf\sim\mathbb{E}Mf.

Here and later,
A\lesssim B
means that
A\leq CB
with an absolute constant
C
, and
A\sim B
means that
A\lesssim B
and
B\lesssim A
.

Proof.

It suffices to consider martingales with
f_{N}=f_{N+1}=\dotsb=:f_{\infty}
for some
N
, as long as we show bounds independent of
N
.

Let
f=f^{\mathrm{pred}}+f^{\mathrm{bv}}
be a Davis decomposition as in Lemma
1.9
.
Then, for every
n>0
, we have the predictable bound

\lvert f^{\mathrm{pred}}_{n}\rvert\leq\lvert f^{\mathrm{pred}}_{n-1}\rvert+\lvert df^{\mathrm{pred}}_{n}\rvert\leq\lvert f^{\mathrm{pred}}_{n-1}\rvert+2\lvert Mdf_{n-1}\rvert=:\rho_{n-1}.

Let
\lambda>0
and
\tau:=\inf\{n\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\rho_{n}>\lambda\}
.
Then,

\displaystyle\lvert\{Sf^{\mathrm{pred}}>\lambda\}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lvert\{Sf^{\mathrm{pred}}>\lambda,\tau=\infty\}\rvert+\lvert\{\tau<\infty\}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lvert\{Sf^{\mathrm{pred},(\tau)}>\lambda\}\rvert+\lvert\{\tau<\infty\}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lambda^{-2}\lVert Sf^{\mathrm{pred},(\tau)}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lvert\{\tau<\infty\}\rvert

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-2}\lVert f^{\mathrm{pred}}_{\tau}\rVert^{2}+\lvert\{\tau<\infty\}\rvert.

On the set
\{\tau=\infty\}
, we use the bound
Mf^{\mathrm{pred}}\leq M\rho\leq\lambda
, while on the set
\{\tau<\infty\}
, we use the bound
\lvert f^{\mathrm{pred}}_{\tau}\rvert\leq\rho_{\tau-1}\leq\lambda
(at this point, predictability is essential).
This gives

\displaystyle\lvert\{Sf^{\mathrm{pred}}>\lambda\}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lambda^{-2}\int_{\{\tau=\infty\}}\lvert f^{\mathrm{pred}}_{\tau}\rvert^{2}+\lambda^{-2}\int_{\{\tau<\infty\}}\lvert f^{\mathrm{pred}}_{\tau}\rvert^{2}+\lvert\{\tau<\infty\}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lambda^{-2}\int_{\{Mf^{\mathrm{pred}}\leq\lambda\}}\lvert Mf^{\mathrm{pred}}\rvert^{2}+\lvert\{\tau<\infty\}\rvert+\lvert\{\tau<\infty\}\rvert.

Note that
\{\tau<\infty\}=\{M\rho>\lambda\}
.
Inserting this in the above inequality and integrating in
\lambda
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Sf^{\mathrm{pred}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\lvert\{Sf^{\mathrm{pred}}>\lambda\}\rvert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda^{-2}\int_{\{Mf^{\mathrm{pred}}\leq\lambda\}}\lvert Mf^{\mathrm{pred}}\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda+2\int_{0}^{\infty}\lvert\{M\rho>\lambda\}\rvert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\lvert Mf^{\mathrm{pred}}\rvert^{2}\int_{Mf^{\mathrm{pred}}}^{\infty}\lambda^{-2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda+2\mathbb{E}M\rho

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}Mf^{\mathrm{pred}}+2\mathbb{E}M\rho

\displaystyle\leq 3\mathbb{E}Mf^{\mathrm{pred}}+4\mathbb{E}Mdf

\displaystyle\leq 3\mathbb{E}Mf+3\mathbb{E}\sum_{n}\lvert df^{\mathrm{bv}}_{n}\rvert+4\mathbb{E}Mdf

\displaystyle\leq 3\mathbb{E}Mf+10\mathbb{E}Mdf.

This, together with the simple bound

\mathbb{E}Sf^{\mathrm{bv}}\leq\mathbb{E}\sum_{n}\lvert df^{\mathrm{bv}}_{n}\rvert\leq 2\mathbb{E}Mdf,

implies
\mathbb{E}Sf\lesssim\mathbb{E}Mf
.

The proof of the converse inequality is similar and uses

Sf^{\mathrm{pred}}_{n}=\bigl((Sf^{\mathrm{pred}}_{n-1})^{2}+(df^{\mathrm{pred}}_{n})^{2})^{1/2}\leq\lvert Sf^{\mathrm{pred}}_{n-1}\rvert+\lvert df^{\mathrm{pred}}_{n}\rvert\leq\lvert Sf^{\mathrm{pred}}_{n-1}\rvert+2\lvert Mdf_{n-1}\rvert=:\rho_{n-1}.

∎

Lemma 1.11

(Garsia–Neveu)
.

Let
W,Z
be positive random variables such that, for every
\lambda>0
, we have

\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{1}_{W>\lambda}(W-\lambda))\leq\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{1}_{W>\lambda}Z).

(1.4)

Then, for every
p\geq 1
, we have

\lVert W\rVert_{p}\leq p\lVert Z\rVert_{p}.

Proof.

For
p=1
, it suffices to take
\lambda\to 0
, so assume
p>1
.
Suppose first that
W
is bounded by
\Lambda
and (
1.4
) holds for all
\lambda\in(0,\Lambda)
.
We will use the formula

t^{p}=p(1-p)\int_{0}^{t}(t-\lambda)\lambda^{p-2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda.

It yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}W^{p}

\displaystyle=p(p-1)\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{W}(W-\lambda)\lambda^{p-2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda

\displaystyle=p(p-1)\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\mathbb{E}((W-\lambda)\mathbf{1}_{W>\lambda})\lambda^{p-2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda

\displaystyle\leq p(p-1)\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\mathbb{E}(Z\mathbf{1}_{W>\lambda})\lambda^{p-2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda

\displaystyle=p(p-1)\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{W}Z\lambda^{p-2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda

\displaystyle=p\mathbb{E}ZW^{p-1}

\displaystyle\leq p(\mathbb{E}Z^{p})^{1/p}(\mathbb{E}W^{p})^{1-1/p}.

Since
W
is bounded, we can cancel a suitable power of
\mathbb{E}W^{p}
on both sides and obtain the claim.

For general
W
, we apply the bounded case to
\min(W,\Lambda)
and let
\Lambda\to\infty
.
∎

.

2
Lépingle inequality

For
0<r<\infty
and a sequence of random variables
f=(f_{n})_{n}
, the
r
-variation of
f
on the interval
[t^{\prime},t]
is defined by

V^{r}f_{t^{\prime},t}:=\sup_{J,t^{\prime}\leq u_{0}<\dotsb<u_{J}\leq t}\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lvert f_{u_{j-1}}-f_{u_{j}}\rvert^{r}\Bigr)^{1/r},

(2.1)

where the supremum is taken over arbitrary increasing sequences.
Analogously,
V^{\infty}f_{t^{\prime},t}:=\sup_{t^{\prime}\leq u^{\prime}<u\leq t}\lvert f_{u^{\prime}}-f_{u}\rvert
.

We abbreviate
V^{r}f_{t}:=V^{r}f_{0,t}
and
V^{r}f:=V^{r}f_{0,\infty}
.

The following result is a quantitative version of the martingale convergence theorem.

Theorem 2.1

(Lépingle inequality)
.

For every
p\in[1,\infty)
, there exists a constant
C_{p}<\infty
such that, for every
r>2
, and every martingale
f
, we have

\lVert V^{r}f\rVert_{L^{p}}\leq C_{p}\frac{r}{r-2}\lVert Mf\rVert_{L^{p}}.

(2.2)

The main difficulty here is that the supremum in the definition of
V^{r}
is taken over not necessarily adapted partitions.
We will remedy this by a greedy selection algorithm, stopping as soon as we see a large jump.
We have to use stopping rules depending on a parameter
m
in order to capture jumps of all possible sizes.

Proof.

By the monotone convergence theorem, we may assume that
f
becomes constant after some time
N
.

Let
M_{t}:=\sup_{t^{\prime\prime}\leq t^{\prime}\leq t}\lvert f_{t^{\prime}}-f_{t^{\prime\prime}}\rvert
.
For each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, define an adapted partition by

\tau^{(m)}_{0}:=0,\quad\tau^{(m)}_{j+1}:=\inf\{t\geq\tau^{(m)}_{j}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\lvert f_{t}-f_{\tau_{j}}\rvert\geq 2^{-m}M_{t},M_{t}>0\}.

(2.3)

Claim
.

Let
0\leq t^{\prime}<t<\infty
and
m\geq 2
.
Suppose that

2<\lvert f_{t^{\prime}}-f_{t}\rvert/(2^{-m}M_{t})\leq 4.

(2.4)

Then there exists
j
with
t^{\prime}<\tau^{(m)}_{j}\leq t
and

\lvert f_{t^{\prime}}-f_{t}\rvert\leq 8\lvert f_{\tau^{(m)}_{j-1}}-f_{\tau^{(m)}_{j}}\rvert.

(2.5)

Proof of the claim.

Let
j
be the largest integer with
\tau^{\prime}:=\tau^{(m)}_{j}\leq t
.
We claim that
\tau^{\prime}>t^{\prime}
.
Suppose for a contradiction that
\tau^{\prime}<t^{\prime}
(the case
\tau^{\prime}=t^{\prime}
is similar but easier).
By the hypothesis (
2.4
) and the assumption that
t,t^{\prime}
are not stopping times, we obtain

2\cdot 2^{-m}M_{t}<\lvert f_{t^{\prime}}-f_{t}\rvert\leq\lvert f_{\tau^{\prime}}-f_{t^{\prime}}\rvert+\lvert f_{\tau^{\prime}}-f_{t}\rvert<2^{-m}M_{t^{\prime}}+2^{-m}M_{t}\leq 2\cdot 2^{-m}M_{t},

a contradiction.
This shows
\tau^{\prime}>t^{\prime}
.

It remains to verify (
2.5
).
Assume that
M_{\tau^{\prime}}<M_{t}/2
.
Then, for some
\tau^{\prime\prime}\in(\tau,t]
, we have
\lvert f_{\tau^{\prime}}-f_{\tau^{\prime\prime}}\rvert\geq M_{t}/2\geq 2^{-m}M_{\tau^{\prime\prime}}
, contradicting maximality of
\tau^{\prime}
.
It follows that

\lvert f_{\tau^{(m)}_{j-1}}-f_{\tau^{(m)}_{j}}\rvert\geq 2^{-m}M_{\tau^{\prime}}\geq 2^{-m}M_{t}/2\geq\lvert f_{t^{\prime}}-f_{t}\rvert/8.\qed

Next, for any
0<\rho<r<\infty
, we will show the pathwise inequality

V^{r}_{t}(f_{t})^{r}\leq 8^{\rho}\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}\bigl(2^{-(m-2)}M_{\infty}\bigr)^{r-\rho}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lvert f_{\tau^{(m)}_{j-1}}-f_{\tau^{(m)}_{j}}\rvert^{\rho}.

(2.6)

Let
(u_{l})
be any increasing sequence.
For each
l
with
\lvert f_{u_{l}}-f_{u_{l+1}}\rvert\neq 0
, let
m=m(l)\geq 2
be such that

2<\lvert f_{u_{l}}-f_{u_{l+1}}\rvert/(2^{-m}M_{u_{l+1}})\leq 4.

Such
m
exists because the distance is bounded by
M_{u_{l+1}}
.

Let
j=j(l)
be given by the Claim above with
t^{\prime}=u_{l}
and
t=u_{l+1}
.
Then

\lvert f_{u_{l}}-f_{u_{l+1}}\rvert^{r}\leq 8^{\rho}\lvert f_{\tau^{(m)}_{j-1}}-f_{\tau^{(m)}_{j}}\rvert^{\rho}\cdot(4\cdot 2^{-m}M_{u_{l+1}})^{r-\rho}.

Since each pair
(m,j)
occurs for at most one
l
, this implies

\sum_{l}\lvert f_{u_{l}}-f_{u_{l+1}}\rvert^{r}\leq 8^{\rho}\sum_{m,j}\lvert f_{\tau^{(m)}_{j-1}}-f_{\tau^{(m)}_{j}}\rvert^{\rho}\cdot(2^{-(m-2)}M_{\infty})^{r-\rho}.

Taking the supremum over all increasing sequences
(u_{l})
, we obtain (
2.6
).

Since we assumed that
f_{n}
becomes independent of
n
for sufficiently large
n
, we have

M_{\infty}\leq V^{r}_{t}(f_{t})<\infty.

Substituting this inequality in (
2.6
) and canceling
V^{r}_{t}(f_{t})^{r-2}
on both sides, we obtain

V^{r}_{t}(f_{t})^{\rho}\leq 8^{\rho}\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}2^{-(m-2)(r-\rho)}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lvert f_{\tau^{(m)}_{j-1}}-f_{\tau^{(m)}_{j}}\rvert^{\rho}.

(2.7)

By the optional sampling theorem, for each
m
, the sequence
(f_{\tau^{(m)}_{j}})_{j}
is a martingale with respect to the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{\tau^{(m)}_{j}})_{j}
.
Let

S_{(m)}:=\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lvert f_{\tau^{(m)}_{j-1}}-f_{\tau^{(m)}_{j}}\rvert^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}

denote the square function of this martingale.
Then (
2.7
) implies

V^{r}f\leq 8\Bigl(\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}2^{-(m-2)(r-2)}S_{(m)}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\leq 8\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}2^{-(m-2)(r-2)/2}S_{(m)}

By Minkowski’s inequality, this implies

\lVert V^{r}f\rVert_{L^{p}}\leq 8\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}2^{-(m-2)(r-2)/2}\lVert S_{(m)}\rVert_{L^{p}}.

Applying the BDG inequality for the martingales
(f_{\tau^{(m)}_{j}})_{j}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lVert V^{r}f\rVert_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}2^{-(m-2)(r-2)/2}\lVert\sup_{j}\lvert f_{\tau^{(m)}_{j}}\rvert\rVert_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{r}{r-2}\lVert Mf\rVert_{L^{p}}.\qed

Remark
.

The dependence of the constant in Theorem
2.1
on the variation exponent
r
can be improved to
\sqrt{\frac{r}{r-2}}
using a vector-valued BDG inequality.
In fact, a slightly more careful version of the above argument already shows this for
p\in[2,\infty)
.

2.1
Martingale convergence

Corollary 2.2

(Martingale convergence)
.

Let
f
be a martingale with
\lVert f\rVert_{1}<\infty
.
Then, for every
r>2
,
V^{r}f
is finite a.s.
In particular, the sequence
(f_{n})_{n}
converges a.s.

Proof.

Fixing
\lambda<\infty
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the stopping time

\tau:=\inf\{n\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\lvert f_{n}\rvert>\lambda\}.

Then
Mf^{\tau\wedge N}\leq\lambda\vee\lvert f_{\tau\wedge N}\rvert
.
Since
\tau\wedge N
is a bounded stopping time, by the optional sampling theorem, we have

\lVert Mf^{\tau\wedge N}\rVert_{1}\leq\lambda+\lVert f_{\tau\wedge N}\rVert_{1}\leq\lambda+\lVert f\rVert_{1}.

By Lépingle’s inequality, for
r>2
, we obtain

\lVert V^{r}f^{\tau\wedge N}\rVert_{1}\lesssim\lambda+\lVert f\rVert_{1}.

Since the right-hand side does not depend on
N
and by the monotone convergence theorem, we obtain

\lVert V^{r}f^{\tau}\rVert_{1}\lesssim\lambda+\lVert f\rVert_{1}.

In particular, the function
V^{r}f^{\tau}
is finite a.s.
On the set
\{Mf<\lambda\}
, we have
f=f^{\tau}
, so that
V^{r}f
is finite a.s. on this set.

Taking the union over
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
, we find that
V^{r}f
is finite a.s. on the set
\{Mf<\infty\}
.
By Doob’s maximal inequality, this set has full probability.
∎

We have shown that, for an
L^{1}
martingale
f
, the limit
f_{\infty}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}
exists a.s.
We recall the condition under which this convergence also holds in
L^{1}
.
We start with an example that shows that this is not always the case.

Example 2.3
.

Consider
\Omega=[0,1]
with the dyadic filtration.
The sequence

f_{n}=2^{n}\mathbf{1}_{[0,2^{-n}]}

is a martingale and converges pointwise a.s. to
0
.
On the other hand,
\mathbb{E}f_{n}=1\neq 0=\mathbb{E}0
.

Definition 2.4
.

A set
F
of real random variables is called
uniformly integrable
if

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\sup_{f\in F}\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{1}_{\lvert f\rvert>\lambda}\lvert f\rvert)=0.

Example 2.5
.

If
f\in L^{1}
, then the singleton
\{f\}
is uniformly integrable.

Uniform integrability is useful because it is the minimal hypothesis under which an analogue of the dominated convergence theorem holds.
We recall this analogue.

Proposition 2.6

(Dominated convergence)
.

For every sequence of random variables
f=(f_{n})
, the following are equivalent:

1.

f
is Cauchy in
L^{1}
,

2.

f
is uniformly integrable and Cauchy in probability, that is,

(\forall\varepsilon>0)(\exists N\in\mathbb{N})(\forall m,n\geq N)\mu\{\lvert f_{n}-f_{m}\rvert>\varepsilon\}<\varepsilon.

In relation with martingales, it is important that uniform integrability is preserved under conditional expectation.
This can be seen using the following characterization.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
F
be a set of random variables.
The following are equivalent.

1.

F
is uniformly integrable.

2.

\sup_{f\in F}\mathbb{E}\lvert f\rvert<\infty
and

\lim_{\mu(A)\to 0}\sup_{f\in F}\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{1}_{A}\lvert f\rvert)=0.

(2.8)

Proof.

Without loss of generality, all r.v. in
F
are positive.

\implies
:
For every
\lambda>0
, we have

\mathbb{E}f\leq\lambda+\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{1}_{f>\lambda}f).

Taking the supremum over
f\in F
on both sides and choosing
\lambda
such that

\sup_{f\in F}\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{1}_{f>\lambda}f)<\infty,

we see that
\sup_{f\in F}\mathbb{E}\lvert f\rvert<\infty
.

Moreover,

\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{1}_{A}f)\leq\mu(A)\lambda+\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{1}_{f>\lambda}f).

Taking first the supremum over
f\in F
, and then
\lim_{\mu(A)\to 0}
, we obtain

\lim_{\mu(A)\to 0}\sup_{f\in F}\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{1}_{A}f)\leq\sup_{f\in F}\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{1}_{f>\lambda}f).

Taking
\lim_{\lambda\to 0}
, we obtain (
2.8
).

\impliedby
:
We have
\mu\{f>\lambda\}\leq\lambda^{-1}\mathbb{E}f
.
Taking the supremum over
f\in F
, we obtain

\sup_{f\in F}\mu\{f>\lambda\}\leq\lambda^{-1}\sup_{f\in F}\mathbb{E}f.

The RHS converges to
0
as
\lambda\to\infty
.
The second hypothesis now implies that
F
is uniformly integrable.
∎

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
F\subset L^{1}(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
be uniformly integrable.
Then the set

\{\mathbb{E}(f|\mathcal{F}^{\prime})\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}f\in F,\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{F}\text{ sub-$\sigma$-algebra}\}

is uniformly integrable.

Proof.

We verify the characterization above.
We have

\mathbb{E}\lvert\mathbb{E}(f|\mathcal{F}^{\prime})\rvert\leq\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}(\lvert f\rvert|\mathcal{F}^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}\lvert f\rvert.

This verifies the first condition.

Fixing
\lambda>0
, we also have

\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{1}_{A}\lvert\mathbb{E}(f|\mathcal{F}^{\prime})\rvert)\leq\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{1}_{A}\mathbb{E}(\lvert f\rvert|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}))=\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{1}_{A}|\mathcal{F}^{\prime})\lvert f\rvert)\leq\lambda\mathbb{E}\lvert f\rvert+\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{1}_{A}|\mathcal{F}^{\prime})>\lambda}\lvert f\rvert)

Since
\mu\{\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{1}_{A}|\mathcal{F}^{\prime})>\lambda\}\leq\lambda^{-1}\mu(A)
and using (
2.8
) for the set
F
, we obtain

\lim_{\mu(A)\to 0}\sup_{f\in F,\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{1}_{A}\lvert\mathbb{E}(f|\mathcal{F}^{\prime})\rvert)\leq\lambda\mathbb{E}\lvert f\rvert.

Since
\lambda>0
was arbitrary, this implies (
2.8
) for the set of conditional expectations.
∎

The above results can now be summarized as follows.

Proposition 2.9

(Martingale closure)
.

For every martingale
f=(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, the following are equivalent.

1.

The set
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly integrable.

2.

The sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in
L^{1}
.

3.

There exists
f_{\infty}\in L^{1}
such that, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
f_{n}=\mathbb{E}_{n}f_{\infty}
.

If the above conditions hold, then we can take
f_{\infty}=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}
a.s. and in
L^{1}
.

Remark
.

We have seen that, for a martingale
f
, we have

Mf\in L^{1}\implies f\text{ is uniformly integrable}\implies f\in L^{1}.

The second implication cannot be reversed by Example
2.3
.
The following example shows that the first implication also cannot be reversed, and also that
M
is not bounded on
L^{1}
.

Example 2.10
.

Let
\Omega=[0,1]
with the dyadic filtration and

f_{\infty}:=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(m+1)^{-2}2^{m}\mathbf{1}_{[2^{-m-1},2^{-m}]}

and
f_{n}:=\mathbb{E}_{n}f_{\infty}
.
Then, for
x\in(2^{-l-1},2^{-l})
, we have

Mf(x)=f_{l}(x)=2^{l}\sum_{m=l}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{2^{-l}}(m+1)^{-2}2^{m}\mathbf{1}_{[2^{-m-1},2^{-m}]}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\\
=2^{l-1}\sum_{m=l}^{\infty}(m+1)^{-2}\sim(l+1)^{-1}2^{l}.

We see that
Mf\not\in L^{1}
.

Remark
.

The norm on a Banach space
X
will be sometimes denoted by
Xf:=\lVert f\rVert_{X}
.
This is consistent with operator notation, in that every norm is also a sublinear operator.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
X
be a Banach space.
Let
T
be a subadditive operator that maps
X
-valued martingales starting at
0
to
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
-valued increasing adapted processes starting at
0
such that, for every stopping time
\tau
, we have

Tf_{\tau}=T(f^{\tau})_{\tau},

and for every time
t
we have

Tf_{t}\leq Tf_{t-1}+Xdf_{t}.

Let
U
be another operator satisfying the same properties as
T
.

Assume that, for some
r\in(1,\infty)
and every
X
-valued martingale
f
, we have

\mathbb{P}\{Tf>\lambda\}\lesssim\lambda^{-r}(L^{r}Uf)^{r}.

(3.1)

Then, for every
q\in[1,\infty)
and every
X
-valued martingale
f
, we have

L^{q}Tf\lesssim_{q}L^{q}Uf+L^{q}MXdf.

(3.2)

The formulation is flexible enough to apply to any combination
\{T,U\}=\{XM,XS\}
, and also some other operators, such as martingale transforms or
r
-variation norms.

Proof.

Consider first the case
q=1
.
We use the Davis decomposition
f=f^{\mathrm{pred}}+f^{\mathrm{bv}}
.
Of the two possible generalizations of the Davis decomposition to
X
-valued martingales, we use the one in which the absolute value is replaced by the
X
-norm (the alternative would be to make a decomposition for each
k
separately).
Specifically, we have

Xdf^{\mathrm{pred}}_{n}\leq 2MXdf_{n-1},\quad\mathbb{E}\sum_{n}Xdf^{\mathrm{bv}}_{n}\leq 2\mathbb{E}MXdf.

For
\lambda>0
, define the stopping time

\tau:=\inf\{t\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}Uf^{\mathrm{pred}}_{t}>\lambda\text{ or }MXdf_{t}>\lambda\}.

We claim that

Uf^{\mathrm{pred}}_{\tau}\leq Uf^{\mathrm{pred}}\wedge 3\lambda.

(3.3)

Indeed, the first bound is trivial.
The second bound is trivial if
\tau=\infty
, so assume
\tau\in(0,\infty)
.
Then, by properties of the Davis decomposition, we have

Uf^{\mathrm{pred}}_{\tau}\leq Uf^{\mathrm{pred}}_{\tau-1}+Xdf^{\mathrm{pred}}_{\tau}\leq\lambda+2MXdf_{\tau-1}\leq 3\lambda.

Also,

\displaystyle\{Tf^{\mathrm{pred}}>\lambda\}

\displaystyle\subseteq\{Tf^{\mathrm{pred}}_{\tau}>\lambda\}\cup\{\tau<\infty\}

\displaystyle\subseteq\{Tf^{\mathrm{pred}}_{\tau}>\lambda\}\cup\{Uf^{\mathrm{pred}}>\lambda\}\cup\{MXdf_{\tau}>\lambda\}.

By the layer cake formula,

\displaystyle\lVert Tf^{\mathrm{pred}}\rVert_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\{Tf^{\mathrm{pred}}>\lambda\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\{Tf^{\mathrm{pred}}_{\tau}>\lambda\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda+\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\{Uf^{\mathrm{pred}}>\lambda\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\{MXdf>\lambda\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda=:I+II+III.

The term
III
is easy to bound by the layer cake formula.

In the term
II
, we use the properties of the Davis decomposition to estimate

\displaystyle II

\displaystyle=\lVert Uf^{\mathrm{pred}}\rVert_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert Uf\rVert_{L^{1}}+\lVert Uf^{\mathrm{bv}}\rVert_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert Uf\rVert_{L^{1}}+\lVert\sum_{n}Xdf^{\mathrm{bv}}_{n}\rVert_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert Uf\rVert_{L^{1}}+2\lVert MXdf\rVert_{L^{1}}.

Using the hypothesis (
3.1
) and (
3.3
), we bound the first term by

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda^{-r}\lVert Uf^{\mathrm{pred}}_{\tau}\rVert_{L^{r}}^{r}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda^{-r}\lVert Uf^{\mathrm{pred}}\wedge 3\lambda\rVert_{L^{r}}^{r}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{\infty}\min\bigl(\lambda^{-r}(Uf^{\mathrm{pred}})^{r},3^{r}\bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}Uf^{\mathrm{pred}}=II,

and we reuse the previously established estimate for
II
.

We have shown (
3.2
) for
q=1
, and we will now extend this claim to
q>1
.
In doing so, we may replace
Uf
by
(Uf+MXdf)/2
.
This has the effect that we may omit the second summand on the RHS of (
3.2
).

For
q>1
, we use the Garsia-Neveu lemma.
For a martingale
f
, let
\prescript{\tau}{}{\!}f:=f-f^{\tau}
be the martingale
f
started at (stopping) time
\tau
.

For any
t\in\mathbb{N}
and
B\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
, we can apply the case
q=1
to the martingale
\mathbf{1}_{B}\prescript{t}{}{\!}f
, and obtain

\mathbb{E}_{t}T\prescript{t}{}{\!}f\lesssim\mathbb{E}_{t}U\prescript{t}{}{\!}f.

Note that

Tf_{\infty}-Tf_{t}=Tf_{\infty}-T(f^{t})_{\infty}\leq T(f-f^{t})_{\infty}=T\prescript{t}{}{\!}f_{\infty},

U\prescript{t}{}{\!}f_{\infty}\leq(Uf+Uf^{t})_{\infty}\leq 2Uf_{\infty},

so the conditions of Corollary
1.12
hold for
A_{t}=Tf_{(t-1)\vee 0}
and
\xi\sim Uf_{\infty}
.
Corollary
1.12
now shows the claim.
∎

Monotonicity of
Uf
is used in estimates for both
I
and
II
above.

3.1
Weighted and vector-valued Doob inequalities

Let
w\in L^{1}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{\infty})
be a positive function, that we will call a
weight
.
We write
w_{k}:=\mathbb{E}_{k}w
,
w^{*}:=\sup_{k}w_{k}
, and
wB:=\int_{B}w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu
for
B\in\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
f
be an
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
-valued submartingale (recall that this means
f_{k}\leq E_{k}f_{n}
for
k\leq n
).
Then, for every
\lambda>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\lambda w\{Mf_{n}>\lambda\}\leq\int_{\{Mf_{n}>\lambda\}}f_{n}Mw_{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu.

Proof.

For a fixed
\lambda
, define the stopping time

\tau:=\inf\{k\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}f_{k}>\lambda\}.

Then,
\{Mf_{n}>\lambda\}=\{\tau\leq n\}
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\lambda w\{Mf_{n}>\lambda\}

\displaystyle=\lambda\sum_{k\leq n}w\{\tau=k\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\leq n}\int_{\{\tau=k\}}f_{k}w_{k}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu

(submartingale property)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\leq n}\int_{\{\tau=k\}}f_{n}w_{k}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\{Mf_{n}>\lambda\}}f_{n}w^{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu.\qed

We recall that, for a function
f
on a measure space
(\Omega,\mu)
and
p\in[1,\infty)
, the
L^{p,\infty}
quasinorm is defined by

\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p,\infty}}:=\sup_{\lambda>0}\lambda\mu\{\lvert f\rvert>\lambda\}^{1/p}.

For
p=\infty
, we have
\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p,\infty}}=\lVert f\rVert_{L^{\infty}}
.

Theorem 3.4

(Marcinkiewicz/real interpolation, see e.g.
[
Gra14
]
)
.

Let
(\Omega,\mu)
and
(\tilde{\Omega},\tilde{\mu})
be
\sigma
-finite measure spaces.
Let
0<p_{0}<p_{1}\leq\infty
.
Let

T:L^{p_{0}}(\Omega)+L^{p_{1}}(\Omega)\to L^{0}(\tilde{\Omega})

be a subadditive operator such that, for every function
f
, we have

\lVert Tf\rVert_{L^{p_{j},\infty}(\tilde{\Omega})}\leq A_{j}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p_{j}}(\Omega)},\quad j=0,1.

Then, for every
p\in(p_{0},p_{1})
, we have

\lVert Tf\rVert_{L^{p}(\tilde{\Omega})}\lesssim_{p}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
,
f
be a submartingale, and
w
a weight.
Then,

\lVert Mf\rVert_{L^{p}(w)}\lesssim_{p}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p}(w^{*})}

Proof.

We will show the following more precise estimate:

\lVert Mf_{N}\rVert_{L^{p}(w)}\lesssim_{p}\lVert f_{N}\rVert_{L^{p}(Mw_{N})},

(3.4)

from which the claim follows by letting
N\to\infty
.
The main advantage of this formulation is that the right-hand side increases in
N
, which would not be the case if we would use the weight
w^{*}
instead of
Mw_{N}
.

Lemma
3.3
implies in particular

\lVert Mf_{N}\rVert_{L^{1,\infty}(w)}\lesssim\lVert f_{N}\rVert_{L^{1}(Mw_{N})}.

The estimate

\lVert Mf_{N}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(w)}\lesssim\lVert f_{N}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(Mw_{N})}

is easy to see.
By real interpolation, these two bounds imply (
3.4
).
∎

Proposition 3.6

(Vector-valued maximal inequality)
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
and
r\in(1,\infty]
.
Then, for any sequence of martingales
f_{k}=(f_{k,n})_{n}
, we have

L^{p}\ell^{r}_{k}Mf_{k}\lesssim_{q,r}L^{p}\ell^{r}_{k}f_{k}.

Proof.

The case
r=\infty
follows from the scalar case, because
\ell^{\infty}f_{k}
is a submartingale.

The case
p=r
also follows from the scalar case and Fubini.

For
1<r<p<\infty
, let
\rho:=(p/r)^{\prime}=p/(p-r)
.
Then, for some weight
w\in L^{\rho}
with
\lVert w\rVert_{\rho}\leq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\ell^{r}_{k}Mf_{k}\rVert_{p}^{r}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Bigl(\mathbb{E}\bigl(\sum_{k}(Mf_{k})^{r}\bigr)^{p/r}\Bigr)^{r/p}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\sum_{k}(Mf_{k})^{r}w

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}\sum_{k}\lvert f_{k}\rvert^{r}w^{*}

and we conclude using Hölder’s inequality and boundedness of the maximal operator on
L^{\rho}
.

For
1<p<r<\infty
, let
s\in(1,p)
.
Then, for some sequence of weights with

\lVert\ell^{(r/s)^{\prime}}_{k}w_{k}\rVert_{(p/s)^{\prime}}\sim 1,

we have

\displaystyle\lVert\ell^{r}_{k}Mf_{k}\rVert_{p}^{s}

\displaystyle=\lVert\ell^{r/s}_{k}(Mf_{k})^{s}\rVert_{p/s}

\displaystyle\sim\int\sum_{k}w_{k}(Mf_{k})^{s}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int\sum_{k}w_{k}^{*}\lvert f_{k}\rvert^{s}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\ell^{(r/s)^{\prime}}_{k}w_{k}^{*}\rVert_{(p/s)^{\prime}}\lVert\ell^{r/s}_{k}\lvert f_{k}\rvert^{s}\rVert_{p/s}.

Since
(r/s)^{\prime}<(p/s)^{\prime}
, from the previously shown case, we obtain

\lVert\ell^{(r/s)^{\prime}}_{k}w_{k}^{*}\rVert_{(p/s)^{\prime}}\lesssim\lVert\ell^{(r/s)^{\prime}}_{k}w_{k}\rVert_{(p/s)^{\prime}}\sim 1.\qed

4
Rough paths

In this section, we discuss how to define
\int a\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}g
for not very regular functions
a,g
.
The intended application is that
g
is a sample path of a martingale, which only has bounded
r
-variation for
r>2
, so that we cannot use Stieltjes integration.

The function
a
is assumed to have the same regularity as
g
, because we want the theory to be suitable for solving equations like
f(t)=\int_{0}^{t}a(s,f(s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}g(s)
.

4.1
Young integration with Hölder functions

We begin with a criterion for convergence of Riemann-like sums.
Let
\Delta:=\{(s,t)\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}0\leq s\leq t\leq T\}
.
For any
\Xi:\Delta\to\mathbb{R}
and a partition
0=\pi_{0}<\dotsc<\pi_{J}=T
, write

\mathcal{I}^{\pi}\Xi_{0,T}:=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\Xi_{\pi_{j-1},\pi_{j}}.

We will discuss sufficient conditions for the convergence of these sums along the directed (by set inclusion) set of partitions.

For a mapping
\Xi:\Delta\to E
(
E
a vector space), we write

\delta\Xi_{s,u,t}:=\Xi_{s,t}-\Xi_{s,u}-\Xi_{u,t}.

A
control
is a function
\omega:\Delta\to[0,\infty)
that is superadditive in the sense that

\omega(s,t)+\omega(t,u)\leq\omega(s,u)

for all
s\leq t\leq u
.
This implies in particular that
\omega(t,t)=0
for all
t
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\omega
be a control and
\theta>1
.
Let
(E,\lvert\cdot\rvert)
be a Banach space and
\Xi:\Delta\to E
such that, for every
0\leq s\leq t\leq u\leq T
, we have

\lvert\delta\Xi_{s,t,u}\rvert\leq\omega(s,u)^{\theta}.

(4.1)

Then, for every partition
0=\pi_{0}\leq\dotsc\leq\pi_{J}=T
, we have

\lvert\Xi_{0,T}-\mathcal{I}^{\pi}\Xi_{0,T}\rvert\lesssim(\theta-1)^{-1}\omega(0,T)^{\theta}.

Proof.

By induction on the partition size
J
, we will show the bound

\lvert\Xi_{0,T}-\mathcal{I}^{\pi}\Xi_{0,T}\rvert\leq\sum_{k=1}^{J-1}(2/k)^{\theta}\omega(0,T)^{\theta}.

For
J=1
, we have
\mathcal{I}^{\pi}\Xi_{0,T}=\Xi_{0,T}
, which serves as the induction base.

Suppose that the claim is known for all partitions of size
J
and let
\pi
be a partition of size
J+1
.
By superadditivity of
\omega
, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{J-1}\omega(\pi_{k},\pi_{k+2})=\sum_{k\leq J-1\text{ even}}\omega(\pi_{k},\pi_{k+2})+\sum_{k\leq J-1\text{ odd}}\omega(\pi_{k},\pi_{k+2})\leq 2\omega(0,T).

Hence, there exists
k
with
\omega(\pi_{k},\pi_{k+2})\leq 2\omega(0,T)/J
.
Let
\pi^{\prime}:=\pi\setminus\{\pi_{k+1}\}
.
Then,

\lvert\mathcal{I}^{\pi}\Xi_{0,T}-\mathcal{I}^{\pi^{\prime}}\Xi_{0,T}\rvert=\lvert\delta\Xi_{\pi_{k},\pi_{k+1},\pi_{k+2}}\rvert\leq\omega(\pi_{k},\pi_{k+2})^{\theta}\leq(2/J)^{\theta}\omega(0,T).

Applying the inductive hypothesis to
\pi^{\prime}
, we obtain the claim.
∎

Proposition 4.2

(Sewing)
.

Let
\omega
be a control such that

\lim_{\pi}\sup_{j}\omega(\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1})=0.

(4.2)

Let
\Xi:\Delta\to E
be such that (
4.1
) holds.
Then, the limit

\mathcal{I}\Xi_{0,T}:=\lim_{\pi}\mathcal{I}^{\pi}\Xi_{0,T}

exists and satisfies

\lvert\mathcal{I}\Xi_{0,T}-\Xi_{0,T}\rvert\lesssim(\theta-1)^{-1}\omega(0,T)^{\theta}.

Proof.

For any partitions
\pi\subseteq\pi^{\prime}
, by Lemma
4.1
, we have

\displaystyle\lvert\mathcal{I}^{\pi}\Xi_{s,t}-\mathcal{I}^{\pi^{\prime}}\Xi_{s,t}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j}\lvert\Xi_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}}-\mathcal{I}^{\pi^{\prime}}\Xi_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}}\rvert

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j}\omega(\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1})^{\theta}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sup_{j}\omega(\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1})^{\theta-1}\sum_{j}\omega(\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1})

\displaystyle\lesssim\omega(s,t)\sup_{j}\omega(\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1})^{\theta-1}.

The hypothesis (
4.2
) implies that
\pi\mapsto\mathcal{I}^{\pi}\Xi_{s,t}
is a Cauchy net.
∎

Example 4.3

(Young integral)
.

Let
a,g:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
be
\alpha
-Hölder functions and

\Xi_{s,t}:=a_{s}(g_{t}-g_{s}).

Then,

\displaystyle\delta\Xi_{s,t,u}

\displaystyle=\Xi_{s,u}-\Xi_{s,t}-\Xi_{t,u}

\displaystyle=a_{s}(g_{u}-g_{s})-a_{s}(g_{t}-g_{s})-a_{t}(g_{u}-g_{t})

\displaystyle=a_{s}(g_{u}-g_{t})+a_{s}(g_{t}-g_{s})-a_{s}(g_{t}-g_{s})-a_{t}(g_{u}-g_{t})

\displaystyle=(a_{s}-a_{t})(g_{u}-g_{t}).

In particular,
\Xi_{s,t,u}\lesssim\lvert s-t\rvert^{\alpha}\lvert u-t\rvert^{\alpha}\leq\lvert u-s\rvert^{2\alpha}
.
Hence, provided that
2\alpha>1
, the hypothesis of Proposition
4.2
holds with
\theta=2\alpha
and
\omega(s,t)\sim\lvert t-s\rvert
.

4.2
Young integration with
V^{r}
functions

If the function
g:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
has bounded
r
-variation, then

\omega_{g,r}(s,t):=(V^{r}g_{s,t})^{r}=\sup_{s\leq\pi_{0}\leq\dotsb\leq\pi_{J}\leq t}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lvert g_{\pi_{j}}-g_{\pi_{j-1}}\rvert^{r}

is a control.
If both
a,g\in V^{r}
, then, in the situation of Example
4.3
, we have

\lvert\delta\Xi_{s,t,u}\rvert\leq\omega_{a,r}(s,t)^{1/r}\omega_{g,r}(t,u)^{1/r}.

(4.3)

This implies in particular (
4.1
) with
\omega=\omega_{a,r}+\omega_{g,r}
and
\theta=2/r
.
However, (
4.2
) only holds for this control if
a,g
are also continuous.
In order to integrate functions with jumps, we will show a version of the sewing lemma under the condition (
4.3
).

Definition 4.4
.

If
f
is a function defined on a suitable interval, we write

f(t+):=\lim_{t^{\prime}\to t,t^{\prime}>t}f(t^{\prime}),\quad f(t-):=\lim_{t^{\prime}\to t,t^{\prime}<t}f(t^{\prime}),

provided that the respective limits exist.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\omega
be a control.
Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a partition
\pi
such that

\max_{j}\bigl(\omega(\pi_{j-1}+,\pi_{j})\wedge\omega(\pi_{j-1},\pi_{j}-)\bigr)\leq\varepsilon.

(4.4)

Proof.

We select the partition greedily starting with
\pi_{0}=0
.
It will be clear from the construction that the claim (
4.4
) holds.
If
\pi_{j}
has been already selected and
\pi_{j}<T
, we distinguish two cases.

Case 1: if
\omega(\pi_{j},\pi_{j}+)<\varepsilon
, then we let

\pi_{j+1}:=\sup\{t>\pi_{j}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\omega(\pi_{j},t)<\varepsilon\}.

Then
\omega(\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}-)\leq\varepsilon
.

Case 2: if
\omega(\pi_{j},\pi_{j}+)\geq\varepsilon
, then we choose any
\pi_{j+1}\in(\pi_{j},T]
with
\omega(\pi_{j}+,\pi_{j+1})\leq\varepsilon
.

To see that such
\pi_{j+1}
exists, suppose for a contradiction that, for every
t\in(\pi_{j},T]
, we have
\omega(\pi_{j}+,t)>\varepsilon
.
Choose recursively
t_{0}=T
and, given
t_{k}
, let
t_{k+1}\in(\pi_{j},t_{k})
be such that
\omega(t_{k+1},t_{k})>\varepsilon
.
By superadditivity, we obtain

\omega(\pi_{j},T)\geq\sum_{k}\omega(t_{k+1},t_{k})=+\infty,

a contradiction.

The selection of
\pi_{j}
’s ends after finitely many steps, because otherwise we would have
\omega(\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}+)\geq\varepsilon
for every
j
, which in turn implies
\omega(\pi_{j},\pi_{j+2})\geq\varepsilon
, and summing over even
j
we obtain a contradiction with the superadditivity of
\omega
.
∎

Theorem 4.6

(Sewing with jumps)
.

Let
\omega_{1,n},\omega_{2,n}
be controls and
\alpha_{1,n},\alpha_{2,n}\geq 0
with
\alpha_{1,n}+\alpha_{2,n}>1
for all
n\in\{1,\dotsc,N\}
.

Let
\Xi:\Delta\to E
.
Assume

\lvert\delta\Xi_{s,u,t}\rvert\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{1,n}^{\alpha_{1,n}}(s,u)\omega_{2,n}^{\alpha_{2,n}}(u,t),

Then the following net limit exists,

\mathcal{I}\Xi_{0,T}:=\lim_{\pi}\mathcal{I}^{\pi}\Xi_{0,T},

and one has the following estimate:

\lvert\mathcal{I}\Xi_{0,T}-\Xi_{0,T}\rvert\lesssim\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{1,n}^{\alpha_{1,n}}(0,T-)\omega_{2,n}^{\alpha_{2,n}}(0+,T),

with
C
depending only on
\min_{n}\alpha_{1,n}+\alpha_{2,n}
.

Proof.

Let

\theta:=\min_{n}\alpha_{1,n}+\alpha_{2,n}>1

and

\omega(s,t):=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{1,n}^{\alpha_{1,n}/\theta}(s,t-)\omega_{2,n}^{\alpha_{2,n}/\theta}(s+,t).

Note that the functions
(s,t)\mapsto\omega_{1,n}(s,t-)
and
(s,t)\mapsto\omega_{2,n}(s+,t)
are also controls.
The function
\omega
is a control, because, by Hölder’s inequality,

\omega_{1,n}^{\alpha_{1,n}/\theta}(s,u)\omega_{2,n}^{\alpha_{2,n}/\theta}(s,u)\geq(\omega_{1,n}(s,t)+\omega_{1,n}(t,u))^{\alpha_{1,n}/\theta}(\omega_{2,n}(s,t)+\omega_{2,n}(t,u))^{\alpha_{2,n}/\theta}\\
\geq\omega_{1,n}(s,t)^{\alpha_{1,n}/\theta}\omega_{2,n}(s,t)^{\alpha_{2,n}/\theta}+\omega_{1,n}(t,u)^{\alpha_{1,n}/\theta}\omega_{2,n}(t,u)^{\alpha_{2,n}/\theta}.

It follows from Lemma
4.5
applied to the control
\sum_{n}(\omega_{1,n}+\omega_{2,n})
that (
4.2
) holds.
Since
\lvert\delta\Xi_{s,t,u}\rvert\leq\omega(s,u)^{\theta}
, we can apply Proposition
4.2
.
∎

Theorem
4.6
allows us to integrate under the hypothesis (
4.3
), but only for
r<2
.
By the law of the iterated logarithm, paths of the Brownian motion are not in
V^{r}
for any
r\leq 2
.

There are two options to refine the above reasoning in order to obtain an integration theory suitable for martingales.

•

The classical stochastic integration uses orthogonality between martingale increments.
We will return to that.

•

Rough integration theory replaces Riemann–Stieltjes sums by higher order quadrature schemes.
Higher order quadrature improves the order of convergence (under suitable regularity assumptions).
In the rough setting, it improves from having no convergence to having convergence.

Another motivation for us to look at the rough integration theory is that it informs us about the estimates that we will want when we return to stochastic integration.

4.3
Rough paths

Throughout this section, we fix some
r\in[2,3)
.

An
r
-rough path (with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
) consists of

X:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad\mathbb{X}:\Delta\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

such that
X\in V^{r}
,
\mathbb{X}\in V^{r/2}
, where

V^{r}\mathbb{X}_{s,t}=\sup_{s\leq u_{0}\leq\dotsb\leq u_{J}\leq t}\Bigl(\sum_{j}\lvert\mathbb{X}_{u_{j},u_{j+1}}\rvert^{r}\Bigr)^{1/r}.

and
Chen’s relation
holds for
s<t<u
:

\mathbb{X}_{s,u}=\mathbb{X}_{s,t}+\mathbb{X}_{t,u}+(X_{t}-X_{s})\otimes(X_{u}-X_{t}).

(4.5)

Remark
.

If
X
is sufficiently regular for the integral to be defined, one can take

\mathbb{X}_{s,t}:=\int_{s}^{t}(X_{u-}-X_{s})\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X_{u}.

Next, we introduce the spaces in which one can state and solve differential equations driven by rough paths.

Definition 4.7

(Controlled path)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(X,\mathbb{X})
be an
r
-rough path in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
An
\mathbf{X}
-controlled
r
-rough path
in a vector space
E
consists of
Y:[0,T]\to E
,
Y^{\prime}:[0,T]\to L(\mathbb{R}^{d},E)
such that
Y^{\prime}\in V^{r}
and

R^{\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{X}}_{s,t}:=\delta Y_{s,t}-Y^{\prime}_{s}\delta X_{s,t}\in V^{r/2}.

Although
R^{\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{X}}
depends on all of
Y,Y^{\prime},X
, it is commonly abbreviated to
R^{Y}
, since the other dependencies are usually unambiguous.

It may be helpful to think about the scalar case
d=d^{\prime}=1
, and we will use the scalar notation.
All arguments will be, however, formulated in such a way that they work in the vector-valued case, which is important e.g. because one may wish to incorporate time as an additional coordinate in
\mathbf{X}
.

Example 4.8
.

(X,1)
is an
X
-controlled path.

Lemma 4.9

(Rough integral)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(X,\mathbb{X})
be an
r
-rough path and
\mathbf{Y}=(Y,Y^{\prime})
an
\mathbf{X}
-controlled path in
L(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}})
.
Let
\Xi_{s,t}:=Y_{s}\delta X_{s,t}+Y_{s}^{\prime}\mathbb{X}_{s,t}
.
Then,

\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{Y}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}:=\lim_{\pi}\mathcal{I}^{\pi}\Xi_{0,T}

exists and satisfies

\Big\lvert\int_{s}^{t}\mathbf{Y}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}-\Xi_{s,t}\Big\rvert\lesssim V^{r/2}R_{s,t-}V^{r}X_{s+,t}+V^{r}Y^{\prime}_{s,t-}V^{r/2}\mathbb{X}_{s+,t}.

Proof.

\begin{split}\delta\Xi_{s,t,u}&=Y_{s}\delta X_{s,u}+Y_{s}^{\prime}\mathbb{X}_{s,u}-(Y_{s}\delta X_{s,t}+Y_{s}^{\prime}\mathbb{X}_{s,t})-(Y_{t}\delta X_{t,u}+Y_{t}^{\prime}\mathbb{X}_{t,u})\\
&=Y_{s}\delta X_{t,u}+Y_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbb{X}_{s,u}-\mathbb{X}_{s,t})-(Y_{t}\delta X_{t,u}+Y_{t}^{\prime}\mathbb{X}_{t,u})\\
&=(Y_{s}-Y_{t})\delta X_{t,u}+Y_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbb{X}_{t,u}+\delta X_{s,t}\otimes\delta X_{t,u})-Y_{t}^{\prime}\mathbb{X}_{t,u}\\
&=(Y_{s}-Y_{t})\delta X_{t,u}+(Y_{s}^{\prime}-Y_{t}^{\prime})\mathbb{X}_{t,u}+Y_{s}^{\prime}(\delta X_{s,t}\otimes\delta X_{t,u})\\
&=-R^{\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{X}}_{s,t}\delta X_{t,u}+(Y_{s}^{\prime}-Y_{t}^{\prime})\mathbb{X}_{t,u}.\end{split}

(4.6)

Hence, we can apply Theorem
4.6
.
∎

Example 4.10
.

\int_{0}^{T}(X-X_{0},1)\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}=\mathbb{X}_{0,T}

Indeed, in this case

This gives even a

4.3.1
Rough differential equations

We consider an initial value problem that is informally described by
Y_{0}=y_{0}
,
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Y=\varphi(Y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X
.
A rigorous formulation is the following: given a rough path
\mathbf{X}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}\to L(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}})
, we are looking for an
\mathbf{X}
-controlled path
\mathbf{Y}
such that

Y_{T}=y_{0}+\int_{0}^{T}\varphi(\mathbf{Y})\mathbf{X}.

(4.7)

We still have to define what we mean by
\varphi(\mathbf{Y})
.

We will find that this problem has a unique solution if
\varphi\in C^{2,1}_{b}
, the space of twice differentiable functions with
\varphi,D\varphi,D^{2}\varphi
bounded and
D^{2}\varphi
Lipschitz.

The proof proceeds by a fixed point argument.
First, we need to identify a metric space in which this argument will be run.
We abbreviate
\lVert X\rVert_{r}:=V^{r}X_{0,T}
.

4.3.2
Operations on controlled paths

We have deliberately omitted the condition

from the definition of the controlled path, because it is implicit in the other conditions.

Lemma 4.11

(Implicit bound)

Let
\mathbf{X}
be a rough path and
\mathbf{Y}
an
\mathbf{X}
-controlled path.

\lVert Y\rVert_{r}\leq\lVert Y^{\prime}\rVert_{\sup}\lVert X\rVert_{r}+\lVert R^{Y}\rVert_{r/2}.

Lemma 4.12

(Integration)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}
be a rough path and
\mathbf{Y}

\mathbf{X}
-controlled path.

Proof.

\displaystyle R^{Z}_{s,t}

\displaystyle=\delta Z_{s,t}-Z^{\prime}_{s}\delta X_{s,t}

Lemma 4.13

(Composition with a smooth function)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}
be a rough path and
\mathbf{Y}
an
\mathbf{X}
-controlled path.
Suppose
\varphi\in C^{1,1}_{b}

Then
\varphi(\mathbf{Y}):=(\varphi(Y),D\varphi(Y)Y^{\prime})

\mathbf{X}
-controlled path, and we have

\lVert\varphi(\mathbf{Y})^{\prime}\rVert_{r}=\lVert D\varphi(Y)Y^{\prime}\rVert_{r}\leq\lVert D\varphi\rVert_{\sup}\lVert Y^{\prime}\rVert_{r}+\lVert D\varphi\rVert_{Lip}\lVert Y\rVert_{r}\lVert Y^{\prime}\rVert_{\sup},

(4.9)

\lVert R^{\varphi(Y)}\rVert_{r/2}\leq\lVert D\varphi\rVert_{\sup}\lVert R^{Y}\rVert_{r/2}+\frac{1}{2}\lVert D\varphi\rVert_{Lip}\lVert Y\rVert_{r}^{2}.

(4.10)

Interestingly, this estimate does not depend on
\mathbf{X}

Proof.

Adding and subtracting

\displaystyle\lvert R^{\varphi(Y)}_{s,t}\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert\varphi(Y_{t})-\varphi(Y_{s})-D\varphi(Y_{s})Y_{s}^{\prime}X_{s,t}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lvert\varphi(Y_{t})-\varphi(Y_{s})-D\varphi(Y_{s})Y_{s,t}\rvert+\lvert D\varphi(Y_{s})R^{Y}_{s,t}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\lVert D\varphi\rVert_{Lip}\lvert Y_{s,t}\rvert^{2}+\lVert D\varphi\rVert_{\sup}\lvert R^{Y}_{s,t}\rvert,

which implies second estimate.
∎

Lemma 4.14

(Solution space)
.

For any

and any

for any

Proof.

Implicit constants in this proof are allowed to depend on the

\varphi

Suppose
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathcal{Y}

4.13

(4.14)

By Lemma

+\lVert\Delta Y\rVert_{\sup}\lVert\tilde{Y}^{\prime}\rVert_{r}+\lVert\Delta Y^{\prime}\rVert_{r}\lVert\tilde{Y}^{\prime}\rVert_{\sup}+\lVert\Delta Y\rVert_{\sup}\lVert\tilde{Y}\rVert_{r}\lVert\tilde{Y}^{\prime}\rVert_{\sup}\lesssim\alpha(1+A),

\lVert R^{\varphi(Y)}-R^{\varphi(\tilde{Y})}\rVert_{r/2}\lesssim\lVert\Delta R^{Y}\rVert_{r/2}+\lVert\Delta Y\rVert_{\sup}\lVert R^{\tilde{Y}}\rVert_{r/2}\\
+(\lVert Y\rVert_{r}+\lVert\tilde{Y}\rVert_{r})\lVert\Delta Y\rVert_{r}+\lVert\Delta Y\rVert_{\sup}\lVert\tilde{Y}\rVert_{r}^{2}\lesssim\alpha(1+A^{2}).

Let
\mathbf{Z}=\operatorname{Step}(\mathbf{Y})
,
\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}=\operatorname{Step}(\tilde{\mathbf{Y}})
.
Then

\lVert\Delta Z^{\prime}\rVert_{r}=\lVert\varphi(Y)-\varphi(\tilde{Y})\rVert_{r}\leq\alpha/2.

By Lemma
4.16
,

\lVert\Delta R^{Z}\rVert_{r/2}\lesssim\varepsilon(\lVert\Delta\varphi(Y)^{\prime}\rVert_{\sup}+\lVert\Delta\varphi(Y)^{\prime}\rVert_{r}+\lVert\Delta R^{\varphi(Y)}\rVert_{r/2})\lesssim\alpha\varepsilon.

By Lemma
4.17

\lVert\Delta Z\rVert_{r}\lesssim\lVert\Delta Z^{\prime}\rVert_{\sup}\lVert X\rVert_{r}+\lVert\Delta R^{Z}\rVert_{r/2}\leq\lVert\Delta Z^{\prime}\rVert_{r}\varepsilon+C\varepsilon\alpha\lesssim\varepsilon\alpha.

Choosing

small enough, we obtain the claim.
∎

Lemma 4.20

(Local Lipschitz dependence of RDE solution on the data)
.

For every
\varphi\in C^{2,1}_{b}
and every

, there exists

Let

\lVert\Delta R^{Y}\rVert_{r/2}\lesssim\varepsilon(\lVert\Delta\varphi(Y)^{\prime}\rVert_{\sup}+\lVert\Delta\varphi(Y)^{\prime}\rVert_{r}+\lVert\Delta R^{\varphi(Y)}\rVert_{r/2})\\
+\beta(\lVert\varphi(\tilde{Y})^{\prime}\rVert_{\sup}+\lVert\varphi(\tilde{Y})\rVert_{r}+\lVert R^{\varphi(\tilde{Y})}\rVert_{r/2})\\
\lesssim\varepsilon\alpha(1+A^{2})+\beta(1+A^{2}).

\lVert\Delta Y^{\prime}\rVert_{r}=\lVert\varphi(Y)-\varphi(\tilde{Y})\rVert_{r}\leq\lVert D\varphi\rVert_{\sup}\lVert\Delta Y\rVert_{r}+\lVert D\varphi\rVert_{Lip}\lVert\Delta Y\rVert_{\sup}\lVert\tilde{Y}\rVert_{r}\\

5.1
Davis decomposition

We will use the following

bound for the Davis decomposition (constructed in Lemma

).

Lemma 5.1

(Davis decomposition in
L^{q}
)
.

For every martingale
(f_{n})
with values in a Banach space
X
, there exists a decomposition
f=f^{\mathrm{pred}}+f^{\mathrm{bv}}
as a sum of two martingales adapted to the same filtration with
f^{\mathrm{pred}}_{0}=0
such that the differences of
f^{\mathrm{pred}}
have predictable majorants:

Xdf^{\mathrm{pred}}_{n}\leq 2MXdf_{n-1}

(5.1)

and
f^{\mathrm{bv}}
has bounded variation, in an integral sense for every
q\in[1,\infty)
:

L^{q}\sum_{k\leq n}Xdf^{\mathrm{bv}}_{k}\leq(q+1)L^{q}MXdf_{n}.

(5.2)

Proof.

Recall from the construction in Lemma
1.9
that

df^{\mathrm{bv}}_{n}=d\tilde{h}_{n}-\mathbb{E}_{n-1}(d\tilde{h}_{n}),

where
\tilde{h}_{n}
is a process such that

Xd\tilde{h}_{n}=MXdf_{n}-MXdf_{n-1}.

Therefore,

L^{q}\sum_{k\leq n}Xdf^{\mathrm{bv}}_{k}\leq L^{q}\sum_{k\leq n}Xd\tilde{h}_{k}+L^{q}\sum_{k\leq n}\mathbb{E}_{k-1}Xd\tilde{h}_{k}.

Using Lemma
1.15
in the second summand, we obtain the claim.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
1\leq q<\infty
,
X
be a Banach function space, elements of which are
\mathbb{R}
-valued maps
x(\cdot)
, and
(f_{n})
a martingale with values in
X
.
Then for
f^{\mathrm{pred}}
given by Lemma
5.1
we have

\lVert\lVert Sf^{\mathrm{pred}}\rVert_{X}\rVert_{L^{q}}\leq(q+2)\lVert\lVert Sf\rVert_{X}\rVert_{L^{q}},

where the square function is given by

\lVert Sf\rVert_{X}:=\lVert\ell^{2}_{n}(df_{n}(\cdot))\rVert_{X}

Remark
.

We will apply this with

, i.e.

-summable series, viewed as maps from

, with the usual Banach structure.

Proof.

Using (
5.2
) we estimate

\displaystyle\lVert\lVert Sf^{\mathrm{pred}}\rVert_{X}\rVert_{L^{q}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\lVert Sf\rVert_{X}\rVert_{L^{q}}+\lVert\lVert Sf^{\mathrm{bv}}\rVert_{X}\rVert_{L^{q}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\lVert Sf\rVert_{X}\rVert_{L^{q}}+\lVert\lVert\sum_{n}\lvert d_{n}f^{\mathrm{bv}}\rvert\rVert_{X}\rVert_{L^{q}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\lVert Sf\rVert_{X}\rVert_{L^{q}}+\lVert\sum_{n}\lVert d_{n}f^{\mathrm{bv}}\rVert_{X}\rVert_{L^{q}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\lVert Sf\rVert_{X}\rVert_{L^{q}}+(q+1)\lVert\sup_{n}\lVert d_{n}f\rVert_{X}\rVert_{L^{q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lVert\lVert Sf\rVert_{X}\rVert_{L^{q}}.\qed

5.2
Vector-valued maximal paraproduct estimate

We call a process
(F_{s,t})_{s\leq t}
depending on two time variables

is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable for every
s\leq t
.

For an adapted process
(F_{s,t})
and a martingale

, we define

\Pi(F,g)_{s,t}:=\sum_{s<j\leq t}F_{s,j-1}dg_{j}

(5.3)

Note that
\Pi(F,g)_{s,\cdot}
only depends on
(F_{s,\cdot})
.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
0<q,q_{1}\leq\infty
,
1\leq q_{0},r,r_{0}<\infty
,
1\leq r_{1}\leq\infty
.
Assume
1/q=1/q_{0}+1/q_{1}
and
1/r=1/r_{0}+1/r_{1}
.
Then, for any martingales
(g^{(k)}_{n})_{n}
, any adapted sequences
(F^{(k)}_{s,t})_{s\leq t}
, and any stopping times
\tau_{k}^{\prime}\leq\tau_{k}
with
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

\big\lVert\ell^{r}_{k}\sup_{\tau_{k}^{\prime}\leq t\leq\tau_{k}}\lvert\Pi(F^{(k)},g^{(k)})_{\tau_{k}^{\prime},t}\rvert\big\rVert_{q}\leq C_{q_{0},q_{1},r_{0},r_{1}}\big\lVert\ell^{r_{1}}_{k}\sup_{\tau_{k}^{\prime}\leq t<\tau_{k}}\lvert F^{(k)}_{\tau_{k}^{\prime},t}\rvert\big\rVert_{q_{1}}\lVert\ell^{r_{0}}_{k}Sg^{(k)}_{\tau_{k}^{\prime},\tau_{k}}\rVert_{q_{0}},

(5.4)

where
Sg_{s,t}:=(\delta(Sg)^{2})^{1/2}_{s,t}=\bigl(\sum_{j=s+1}^{t}\lvert dg_{j}\rvert^{2}\bigr)^{1/2}
.

Proof of Proposition
5.3
.

We may replace each
g^{(k)}
by the martingale

\tilde{g}^{(k)}_{n}:=g^{(k)}_{n\wedge\tau_{k}}-g^{(k)}_{n\wedge\tau_{k}^{\prime}}

(5.5)

without changing the value of either side of (
5.4
).

Consider first
q\geq 1
.
For each
k
, the sequence

h^{(k)}_{t}:=\begin{cases}0,&t<\tau_{k}^{\prime},\\
\Pi(F^{(k)},g^{(k)})_{\tau_{k}^{\prime},t},&t\geq\tau_{k}^{\prime},\end{cases}

is a martingale.
We may also assume
F_{\tau_{k}^{\prime},t}=0
if
t\not\in[\tau_{k}^{\prime},\tau_{k})
.
By the
\ell^{r}
valued BDG inequality (Corollary
3.2
), we can estimate

\displaystyle LHS\penalty\ \eqref{eq:vv-pprod}

\displaystyle\lesssim\big\lVert\ell^{r}_{k}\lvert Sh^{(k)}\rvert\big\rVert_{q}

\displaystyle=\big\lVert\ell^{r}_{k}\ell^{2}_{j}\lvert F_{\tau_{k}^{\prime},j-1}^{(k)}dg^{(k)}_{j}\rvert\big\rVert_{q}

\displaystyle\leq\big\lVert\ell^{r}_{k}MF^{(k)}\ell^{2}_{j}\lvert dg^{(k)}_{j}\rvert\big\rVert_{q}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\ell^{r_{1}}_{k}MF^{(k)}\rVert_{q_{1}}\big\lVert\ell^{r_{0}}_{k}Sg^{(k)}\big\rVert_{q_{0}}.

Here and later, we abbreviate
MF^{(k}:=\sup_{j}\lvert F_{\tau_{k}^{\prime},j}^{(k}\rvert
.

Consider now
q<1
.
By homogeneity, we may assume

\big\lVert\ell^{r_{1}}_{k}MF^{(k)}\big\rVert_{q_{1}}=\big\lVert\ell^{r_{0}}_{k}Sg^{(k)}\big\rVert_{q_{0}}=1,

(5.6)

and we have to show

\big\lVert\ell^{r}_{k}\sup_{\tau_{k}^{\prime}\leq t\leq\tau_{k}}\lvert\Pi(F^{(k)},g^{(k)})_{\tau_{k}^{\prime},t}\rvert\big\rVert_{q}\lesssim 1.

We use the Davis decomposition
g=g^{\mathrm{pred}}+g^{\mathrm{bv}}
(Lemma
5.1

The contribution of the bounded variation part is estimated as follows:

\displaystyle\lVert\ell^{r}_{k}\sup_{\tau_{k}^{\prime}\leq t\leq\tau_{k}}\lvert\Pi(F^{(k)},g^{(k),\mathrm{bv}})_{\tau_{k}^{\prime},t}\rvert\rVert_{q}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\ell^{r}_{k}\sum_{j}\lvert F^{(k)}_{\tau_{k}^{\prime},j-1}\rvert\cdot\lvert dg^{(k),\mathrm{bv}}_{j}\rvert\rVert_{q}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\ell^{r_{1}}_{k}MF^{(k)}\rVert_{q_{1}}\lVert\ell^{r_{0}}_{k}\Bigl(\sum_{j}\lvert dg^{(k),\mathrm{bv}}_{j}\rvert\Bigr)\rVert_{q_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\ell^{r_{1}}_{k}MF^{(k)}\rVert_{q_{1}}\lVert\sum_{j}\ell^{r_{0}}_{k}\lvert dg^{(k),\mathrm{bv}}_{j}\rvert\rVert_{q_{0}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lVert\ell^{r_{1}}_{k}MF^{(k)}\rVert_{q_{1}}\lVert\sup_{j}\ell^{r_{0}}_{k}\lvert dg^{(k)}_{j}\rvert\rVert_{q_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\ell^{r_{1}}_{k}MF^{(k)}\rVert_{q_{1}}\lVert\ell^{r_{0}}_{k}Sg^{(k)}\rVert_{q_{0}},

where we used (
5.2
) in the penultimate step.

It remains to consider the part
g^{\mathrm{pred}}
with predictable bounds for jumps.
We use the layer cake formula in the form

\int f^{q}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\{f^{q}>\lambda\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\{f>\lambda^{1/q}\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda.

By the layer cake formula, we have

\big\lVert\ell^{r}_{k}\sup_{\tau_{k}^{\prime}\leq t\leq\tau_{k}}\lvert\Pi(F^{(k)},g^{(k),\mathrm{pred}})_{\tau_{k}^{\prime},t}\rvert\big\rVert_{q}^{q}\\
=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\{\ell^{r}_{k}\sup_{\tau_{k}^{\prime}\leq t\leq\tau_{k}}\lvert\Pi(F^{(k)},g^{(k),\mathrm{pred}})_{\tau_{k}^{\prime},t}\rvert>\lambda^{1/q}\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda.

(5.7)

Fix some
\lambda>0
and define a stopping time

\tau:=\inf\Big\{t\nonscript\>\Big|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}M(\ell^{r_{0}}_{k}dg^{(k)})_{t}>c\lambda^{1/q_{0}}\text{ or }\ell^{r_{0}}_{k}Sg^{(k),\mathrm{pred}}_{t}>c\lambda^{1/q_{0}}\text{ or }\ell^{r_{1}}_{k}\sup_{0<j\leq t}\lvert F_{\tau_{k}^{\prime},j}^{(k)}\rvert>\lambda^{1/q_{1}}\Big\}.

(5.8)

Define stopped martingales
\tilde{g}^{(k)}_{t}:=g^{(k),\mathrm{pred}}_{t\wedge\tau}
and adapted processes

\tilde{F}^{(k)}_{t^{\prime},t}:=F^{(k)}_{t^{\prime},t\wedge\tau-1}.

Then, on the set
\{\tau=\infty\}
, we have

\Pi(F^{(k)},g^{(k),\mathrm{pred}})_{\tau_{k}^{\prime},t}=\Pi(\tilde{F}^{(k)},\tilde{g}^{(k)})_{\tau_{k}^{\prime},t}\quad\text{for all }k,t.

Hence,

\begin{split}&\{\ell^{r}_{k}\sup_{\tau_{k}^{\prime}\leq t\leq\tau_{k}}\lvert\Pi(F^{(k)},g^{(k),\mathrm{pred}})_{\tau_{k}^{\prime},t}\rvert>\lambda^{1/q}\}\\

&\cup\{\ell^{r_{1}}_{k}MF^{(k)}>\lambda^{1/q_{1}}\}\end{split}

(5.9)

The contributions of the latter three terms to (

It remains to handle the first term.

By construction, we have
\ell^{r_{1}}_{k}M\tilde{F}^{(k)}\leq\lambda^{1/q_{1}}

\ell^{r_{0}}_{k}S\tilde{g}^{(k)}\leq\lambda^{1/q_{0}}

Choose an arbitrary exponent
\tilde{q}
with

By the already known case of the Proposition with

replaced by
(\tilde{q},\infty)

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\{\ell^{r}_{k}\sup_{\tau_{k}^{\prime}\leq t\leq\tau_{k}}\lvert\Pi(\tilde{F}^{(k)},\tilde{g}^{(k)})_{\tau_{k}^{\prime},t}\rvert>\lambda^{1/q}\}\\
&\leq\lambda^{-\tilde{q}/q}\lVert\ell^{r}_{k}\sup_{\tau_{k}^{\prime}\leq t\leq\tau_{k}}\lvert\Pi(\tilde{F}^{(k)},\tilde{g}^{(k)})_{\tau_{k}^{\prime},t}\rvert\rVert_{\tilde{q}}^{\tilde{q}}\\

(5.10)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda^{-\tilde{q}/q_{0}}\lVert\ell^{r_{0}}_{k}Sg^{(k),\mathrm{pred}}\wedge\lambda^{1/q_{0}}\rVert_{\tilde{q}}^{\tilde{q}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda

.

Lemma 5.5

For any discrete time adapted process

\tau^{(m)}_{j}
such that, for every

, we have

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}l_{\max},\\
u_{0}<\dotsb<u_{l_{\max}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{l=1}^{l_{\max}}\lvert\Pi_{u_{l-1},u_{l}}\rvert^{r}\leq\frac{(\Pi^{*})^{r}}{1-2^{-r}}+2^{\rho}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(2^{-m}\Pi^{*})^{r-\rho}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(\sup_{\tau^{(m)}_{j-1}\leq t<\tau^{(m)}_{j}}\lvert\Pi_{t,\tau^{(m)}_{j}}\rvert\Bigr)^{\rho}.

(5.12)

Proof of Lemma

.

For

, define stopping times

\tau^{(m)}_{0}:=0,

and then, for

\tau^{(m)}_{j+1}:=\inf\Big\{t>\tau^{(m)}_{j}\nonscript\>\Big|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\sup_{\tau^{(m)}_{j}\leq t^{\prime}<t}\lvert\Pi_{t^{\prime},t}\rvert>2^{-m-1}\Pi^{*}_{t}\Big\}.

(5.13)

Fix

and let

be a finite strictly increasing sequence.
Consider

and split

.

Fix

6
Itô integration

In this section, we discuss integration with respect to a càdlàg martingale in continuous time, generalizing the paraproduct
\Pi
from the previous section.
We consider stochastic processes adapted to some filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
indexed by positive real times.
We begin with some convenient regularity assumptions.
These assumptions are not restrictive, in the sense that, for any martingale, one can reparametrize time and change the filtration in such a way that they are satisfied, but we will not discuss this, since our focus is on more quantitative issues.

A filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
is called
right-continuous
if, for every
t\geq 0
, we have

\mathcal{F}_{t}=\bigcap_{t^{\prime}>t}\mathcal{F}_{t^{\prime}}.

For a function
f:R_{\geq 0}\to E
with values in a metric space
E
, the left and right limits at a point
t
are denoted by

f_{t-}:=\lim_{s\to t,s<t}f_{s},\quad f_{t+}:=\lim_{s\to t,s>t}f_{s},

if they exist.
A function
f:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to E
is called
càdlàg
(for “continue à droite, limite à gauche”, “right continuous with left limits”; some authors use the English abbreviation “rcll”) if
f_{t-}
exists for every
t>0
, and
f_{t}=f_{t+}
for every
t\geq 0
.
A stochastic process
g:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to E
is called càdlàg if every path
g(\omega,\cdot)
is a càdlàg function.

Theorem 6.1

(Regularization, see e.g.
[
Kal21
, Theorem 9.28]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
be a right-continuous filtration.
If
g
is a martingale with respect to
\mathcal{F}
, then there exists a càdlàg martingale
\tilde{g}
with respect to
\mathcal{F}
such that, for every
t\geq 0
, we have

g(\cdot,t)=\tilde{g}(\cdot,t)\quad\text{a.e.}

For an adapted partition
\pi
, we write

\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor:=\max\{s\in\pi\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}s\leq t\},\quad 0\leq t<\infty.

(6.1)

For a càdlàg adapted process
f=(f_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, a càdlàg martingale
g=(g_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, and an adapted partition
\pi
, we consider the following Riemann–Stieltjes sums:

\Pi^{\pi}(f,g)_{t,t^{\prime}}:=\sum_{t<\pi_{j}<t^{\prime}}\delta f_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor,\pi_{j}}\delta g_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime}},\quad 0\leq t\leq t^{\prime}<\infty.

(6.2)

In each summand, the integrand
f
is evaluated at the left endpoint of the interval
[\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}]
on which we consider the increment of the integrator
g
(and also at
\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor
, which is even further to the left).
This is the distinguishing feature of the Itô integral that makes it a martingale in the
t^{\prime}
variable.

Unlike in Riemann(–Stieltjes) integration with a bounded variation integrator, evaluating
t
at other points in general produces different results (e.g. Stratonovich integral, where
f
is averaged over
\pi_{j}
and
\pi_{j+1}
).

Most classical treatments involve sums

\sum_{\pi_{j}<t^{\prime}}f_{\pi_{j}}\delta g_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime}},

which corerspond to fixing
t=0
, but this obscures the observation of the natural regularity of the Itô integral that fits nicely with rough path theory.

For an adpated process
f
and an adapted partition
\pi
, we write

f^{(\pi)}_{t}:=f_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor}.

(6.3)

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
0<q_{1}\leq\infty
,
1\leq q_{0}<\infty
, and
0<r,p_{1}\leq\infty
.
Suppose

1/r<1/p_{1}+1/2,\quad 1/q=1/q_{0}+1/q_{1}.

(6.4)

Let
(f_{t})
be a càdlàg adapted process and
(g_{t})
a càdlàg martingale.

Then, for every adapted partition
\pi
, we have the estimate

\big\lVert V^{r}\Pi^{\pi}(f,g)\big\rVert_{L^{q}}\lesssim\lVert V^{p_{1}}f^{(\pi)}\rVert_{L^{q_{1}}}\lVert V^{\infty}g\rVert_{L^{q_{0}}}.

(6.5)

Proof of Proposition
6.2
.

Since
\Pi^{\pi}(F,g)_{t,t^{\prime}}
is càdlàg in both
t
and
t^{\prime}
, we have

V^{r}\Pi^{\pi}(F,g)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{l_{\max},u_{0}<\dotsb<u_{l_{\max}},u_{l}\in\pi^{(n)}}\Bigl(\sum_{l=1}^{l_{\max}}\lvert\Pi^{\pi}(F,g)_{u_{l-1},u_{l}}\rvert^{r}\Bigr)^{1/r},

where
\pi^{(n)}=\pi\cup 2^{-n}\mathbb{N}
.
By the monotone convergence theorem, it suffices to consider a fixed
\pi^{(n)}
, as long as the bound does not depend on
n
.

For any adapted partitions
\pi\subseteq\tau
, we have

\begin{split}\Pi^{\pi}(f,g)_{t,t^{\prime}}&=\sum_{k:t<\pi_{k}<t^{\prime}}\delta f_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor,\pi_{k}}\delta g_{\pi_{k},\pi_{k+1}\wedge t^{\prime}}\\
&=\sum_{k:t<\pi_{k}<t^{\prime}}\delta f_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor,\pi_{k}}\sum_{l:\pi_{k}\leq\tau_{l}<\pi_{k+1}\wedge t^{\prime}}\delta g_{\tau_{l},\tau_{l+1}\wedge t^{\prime}}\\
&=\sum_{k:t<\pi_{k}<t^{\prime}}\sum_{l:\pi_{k}\leq\tau_{l}<\pi_{k+1}\wedge t^{\prime}}\delta f_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor,\lfloor\tau_{l},\pi\rfloor}\delta g_{\tau_{l},\tau_{l+1}\wedge t^{\prime}}\\
&=\sum_{l:t<\tau_{l}<t^{\prime}}\delta f^{(\pi)}_{\lfloor t,\tau\rfloor,\tau_{l}}\delta g_{\tau_{l},\tau_{l+1}\wedge t^{\prime}}\\
&=\Pi^{\tau}(f^{(\pi)},g)_{t,t^{\prime}},\end{split}

(6.6)

where
f^{(\pi)}
is given by (
6.3
).
Define discrete time processes
f^{(\pi)}_{\tau},g_{\tau}
by

(f^{(\pi)}_{\tau})_{j}=f^{(\pi)}_{\tau_{j}},\quad(g_{\tau})_{j}=g_{\tau_{j}}.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\Pi^{\pi}(f,g)_{\tau_{j},\tau_{j^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=\Pi^{\tau}(f^{(\pi)},g)_{\tau_{j},\tau_{j^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l:\tau_{j}<\tau_{l}<\tau_{j^{\prime}}}\delta f^{(\pi)}_{\lfloor\tau_{j},\tau\rfloor,\tau_{l}}\delta g_{\tau_{l},\tau_{l+1}\wedge\tau_{j^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l:j<l<j^{\prime}}\delta f^{(\pi)}_{\tau_{j},\tau_{l}}\delta g_{\tau_{l},\tau_{l+1}}

\displaystyle=\Pi(f^{(\pi)}_{\tau},g_{\tau})_{j,j^{\prime}},

where the last line is the discrete time paraproduct defined in (
5.3
).
By Theorem
5.7
and the BDG inequality (Corollary
1.13
) for the discrete time martingale
g_{\tau}
, we obtain

\lVert V^{r}\Pi(f^{(\pi)}_{\tau},g_{\tau})\rVert_{q}\lesssim\lVert V^{r_{1}}f^{(\pi)}_{\tau}\rVert_{q_{1}}\lVert Sg_{\tau}\rVert_{q_{0}}\lesssim\lVert V^{r_{1}}f^{(\pi)}_{\tau}\rVert_{q_{1}}\lVert V^{\infty}g_{\tau}\rVert_{q_{0}}\leq\lVert V^{r_{1}}f\rVert_{q_{1}}\lVert V^{\infty}g\rVert_{q_{0}}.\qed

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
(f_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a càdlàg adapted process.
Suppose that
V^{p_{1}}f\in L^{q_{1}}
for some
p_{1},q_{1}\in(0,\infty]
.
Then, for every
\tilde{p}_{1}\in(p_{1},\infty)\cup\{\infty\}
, we have

\lim_{\pi}\lVert V^{\tilde{p}_{1}}(f-f^{(\pi)})\rVert_{L^{q_{1}}}=0.

Proof.

We have
V^{p_{1}}f^{(\pi)}\leq V^{p_{1}}f
and, by Hölder’s inequality,

V^{\tilde{p}_{1}}(f-f^{(\pi)})\leq V^{p_{1}}(f-f^{(\pi)})^{1-\theta}V^{\infty}(f-f^{(\pi)})^{\theta}

with some
\theta\in(0,1]
, so it suffices to consider
\tilde{p}_{1}=\infty
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and define a sequence of stopping times recursively, starting with
\pi_{0}:=0
, by

\pi_{j+1}:=\inf\Big\{t>\pi_{j}\nonscript\>\Big|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\lvert\delta f_{\pi_{j},t}\rvert\geq\varepsilon\Big\}.

Since
f
is càdlàg, the infimum is either
+\infty
or a minimum, so that this is indeed a stopping time.
Also by the càdlàg assumption, this sequence of stopping times is strictly monotonically increasing in the sense that
\pi_{j}<\infty\implies\pi_{j}<\pi_{j+1}
.
Moreover, if
T:=\sup_{j}\pi_{j}<\infty
, then the left limit
f_{T-}
does not exist, contradicting the càdlàg hypothesis.
Therefore,
\pi_{j}\to\infty
, so that
\pi
is an adapted partition.

Then, by (
4.18
), for any adapted partition
\pi^{\prime}\supseteq\pi
and
s\leq t
, we have

\displaystyle\lvert\delta f_{s,t}-\delta f^{(\pi^{\prime})}_{s,t}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lvert f_{t}-f_{\lfloor t,\pi^{\prime}\rfloor}\rvert+\lvert f_{s}-f_{\lfloor s,\pi^{\prime}\rfloor}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon+2\varepsilon.\qed

Theorem 6.4

(Itô integral)
.

In the situation of Proposition
6.2
, suppose that the right-hand side of (
6.8
) is finite.
Then

\Pi(f,g):=\lim_{\pi}\Pi^{\pi}(f,g)

(6.7)

exists in
L^{q}(\Omega,V^{r})
, satisfies the bound

\big\lVert V^{r}\Pi(f,g)\big\rVert_{L^{q}}\lesssim\lVert V^{p_{1}}f\rVert_{L^{q_{1}}}\lVert V^{\infty}g\rVert_{L^{q_{0}}},

(6.8)

and, for any
0\leq t\leq t^{\prime}\leq t^{\prime\prime}<\infty
,
Chen’s relation

\Pi(f,g)_{t,t^{\prime\prime}}-\Pi(f,g)_{t,t^{\prime}}-\Pi(f,g)_{t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime}}=\delta f_{t,t^{\prime}}\delta g_{t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime}}.

(6.9)

The limit (
6.7
) is called the
Itô integral
(with integrand
f
and integrator
g
).

Proof of Theorem
6.4
.

By the Cauchy criterion for net convergence, the existence of the limit (
6.7
) will follow if we can show that

\lim_{\pi}\sup_{\tau\supseteq\pi}\big\lVert V^{r}(\Pi^{\pi}(f,g)-\Pi^{\tau}(f,g))\big\rVert_{L^{q}}=0.

(6.10)

To this end, we use that, by (
6.6
), we have

\Pi^{\pi}(f,g)-\Pi^{\tau}(f,g)=\Pi^{\tau}(f^{(\pi)}-f^{(\tau)},g).

Let
\tilde{p}_{1}\in(p_{1},\infty]\cup\{\infty\}
be such that
1/r<1/\tilde{p}_{1}+1/2
.
By Proposition
6.2
with
f
replaced by
f^{(\pi)}-f^{(\tau)}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\big\lVert V^{r}\Pi^{\tau}(f^{(\pi)}-f^{(\tau)},g)\big\rVert_{L^{q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lVert V^{\tilde{p}_{1}}(f^{(\pi)}-f^{(\tau)})^{(\tau)}\rVert_{L^{q_{1}}}\lVert V^{\infty}g\rVert_{L^{q_{0}}}

This converges to
0
by Lemma
6.3
.

In order to show the Chen relation (
6.9
), we first show that the corresponding relation holds pointwise for the discretized paraproducts
\Pi^{\pi}
.
Indeed, by definition (
6.2
), for
t\leq t^{\prime}\leq t^{\prime\prime}
, we have

\Pi^{\pi}(f,g)_{t,t^{\prime\prime}}-\Pi^{\pi}(f,g)_{t,t^{\prime}}-\Pi^{\pi}(f,g)_{t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime}}

(6.11)

=\sum_{t<\pi_{j}<t^{\prime\prime}}\delta f_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor,\pi_{j}}\delta g_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime\prime}}-\sum_{t<\pi_{j}<t^{\prime}}\delta f_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor,\pi_{j}}\delta g_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime}}\\
-\sum_{t^{\prime}<\pi_{j}<t^{\prime\prime}}\delta f_{\lfloor t^{\prime},\pi\rfloor,\pi_{j}}\delta g_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime\prime}}

=\sum_{t<\pi_{j}<t^{\prime}}f_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor,\pi_{j}}(\delta g_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime\prime}}-\delta g_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime}})+\sum_{t<\pi_{j}=t^{\prime}<t^{\prime\prime}}\delta f_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor,\pi_{j}}\delta g_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime\prime}}\\
+\sum_{t^{\prime}<\pi_{j}<t^{\prime\prime}}(\delta f_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor,\pi_{j}}-\delta f_{\lfloor t^{\prime},\pi\rfloor,\pi_{j}})\delta g_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime\prime}}

=\sum_{t<\pi_{j}<t^{\prime}}f_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor,\pi_{j}}\delta g_{\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime},\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime\prime}}+\sum_{t<\pi_{j}=t^{\prime}<t^{\prime\prime}}\delta f_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor,\pi_{j}}\delta g_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime\prime}}\\
+\delta f_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor,\lfloor t^{\prime},\pi\rfloor}\sum_{t^{\prime}<\pi_{j}<t^{\prime\prime}}\delta g_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime\prime}}

All summands except possibly the one with
\pi_{j}<t^{\prime}<\pi_{j+1}
in the first sum vanish, and it follows that

\dots=\delta f_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor,\lfloor t^{\prime},\pi\rfloor}\Bigl(\sum_{t<\pi_{j}<t^{\prime}<\pi_{j+1}}\delta g_{\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime},\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime\prime}}\\
+\sum_{t<\pi_{j}=t^{\prime}<t^{\prime\prime}}\delta g_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime\prime}}+\sum_{t^{\prime}<\pi_{j}<t^{\prime\prime}}\delta g_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime\prime}}\Bigr)

=\delta f_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor,\lfloor t^{\prime},\pi\rfloor}\Bigl(\sum_{t<\pi_{j}\leq t^{\prime}<\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime\prime}}\delta g_{t^{\prime},\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime\prime}}+\sum_{t^{\prime}<\pi_{j}<t^{\prime\prime}}\delta g_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime\prime}}\Bigr)=\delta f^{(\pi)}_{t,t^{\prime}}\delta g_{t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime}}.

By the already known conclusion (
6.7
), the process (
6.11
) converges to the left-hand side of (

).
By Lemma
6.3

).

6.1
Quadratic covariation

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
q_{0},q_{1}\in[1,\infty)
,
1/q=1/q_{0}+1/q_{1}
, and
r>1
.
For càdlàg martingales
f,g
with
V^{\infty}f\in L^{q_{0}}
,
V^{\infty}g\in L^{q_{1}}
, let

[f,g]^{\pi}_{t,t^{\prime}}:=\sum_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor\leq\pi_{j}<\lfloor t^{\prime},\pi\rfloor}\delta f_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}}\delta g_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}}.

Then,

[f,g]_{t,t^{\prime}}:=\lim_{\pi}[f,g]^{\pi}_{t,t^{\prime}}

(6.12)

exists in
L^{q}(V^{r})
.
Moreover, we have the integration by parts formula

\delta f_{t,t^{\prime}}\delta g_{t,t^{\prime}}=\Pi(f,g)_{t,t^{\prime}}+\Pi(g,f)_{t,t^{\prime}}+[f,g]_{t,t^{\prime}}.

(6.13)

The process (
6.12
) is called the
quadratic covariation
of
f
and
g
.
Here are a few facts about it that easily follow from the definition:

\forall t\leq t^{\prime}\leq t^{\prime\prime},\quad[f,g]_{t,t^{\prime\prime}}=[f,g]_{t,t^{\prime}}+[f,g]_{t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime}}\quad\text{a.s.}

To see this, note that the same identity holds for
[\cdot,\cdot]^{\pi}
for any adapted partition
\pi
that contains the times
t,t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime}
.

\forall t\leq t^{\prime},\quad\mathbb{E}_{t}[f,f]_{t,t^{\prime}}=\mathbb{E}_{t}\lvert\delta f_{t,t^{\prime}}\rvert^{2}.

To see this, note that the same identity holds for
[\cdot,\cdot]^{\pi}
for any adapted partition
\pi
that contains the times
t,t^{\prime}
, because, by the optinal sampling theorem,
\delta f_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}}
are martingale increments, and therefore orthogonal in
L^{2}
.

Proof of Proposition
6.5
.

First, we compute

\displaystyle\delta f_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor,t^{\prime}}\delta g_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor,t^{\prime}}-\Pi^{\pi}(f,g)_{t,t^{\prime}}-\Pi^{\pi}(g,f)_{t,t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\sum_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor\leq\pi_{k}<t^{\prime}}\delta f_{\pi_{k},\pi_{k+1}\wedge t^{\prime}}\bigr)\bigl(\sum_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor\leq\pi_{j}<t^{\prime}}\delta g_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime}}\bigr)

\displaystyle-\sum_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor<\pi_{j}<t^{\prime}}\delta f_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor,\pi_{j}}\delta g_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime}}-\sum_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor<\pi_{k}<t^{\prime}}\delta g_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor,\pi_{k}}\delta f_{\pi_{k},\pi_{k+1}\wedge t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor\leq\pi_{j}<t^{\prime}}\delta f_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime}}\delta g_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor\leq\pi_{k}<\pi_{j}<t^{\prime}}\delta f_{\pi_{k},\pi_{k+1}\wedge t^{\prime}}\delta g_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime}}-\sum_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor<\pi_{j}<t^{\prime}}\delta f_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor,\pi_{j}}\delta g_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor\leq\pi_{j}<\pi_{k}<t^{\prime}}\delta f_{\pi_{k},\pi_{k+1}\wedge t^{\prime}}\delta g_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime}}-\sum_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor<\pi_{k}<t^{\prime}}\delta g_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor,\pi_{k}}\delta f_{\pi_{k},\pi_{k+1}\wedge t^{\prime}}.

Each of the last two lines vanishes identically.

In particular, replacing
t^{\prime}
by
\lfloor t^{\prime},\pi\rfloor
, we obtain

\delta f_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor,\lfloor t^{\prime},\pi\rfloor}\delta g_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor,\lfloor t^{\prime},\pi\rfloor}-\Pi^{\pi}(f,g)_{t,\lfloor t^{\prime},\pi\rfloor}-\Pi^{\pi}(g,f)_{t,\lfloor t^{\prime},\pi\rfloor}=\sum_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor\leq\pi_{j}<\lfloor t^{\prime},\pi\rfloor}\delta f_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}}\delta g_{\pi_{j},\pi_{j+1}}.

(6.14)

Note that

\Pi^{\pi}(f,g)_{t,\lfloor t^{\prime},\pi\rfloor}=\Pi^{\pi}(f,g)_{t,t^{\prime}}-\sum_{\pi_{j}<t^{\prime}<\pi_{j+1}}\delta f_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor,\pi_{j}}\delta g_{\pi_{j},t^{\prime}},

(6.15)

where the sum consists of either
0
or
1
summands.
For any inrcreasing sequence
u_{0}\leq\dotsc\leq u_{K}
, we have
In particular, with any
\alpha\in(0,r-1)
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\Big\lvert\sum_{\pi_{j}<u_{k+1}<\pi_{j+1}}\delta f_{\lfloor u_{k},\pi\rfloor,\pi_{j}}\delta g_{\pi_{j},u_{k+1}}\Big\rvert^{r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k:\lfloor u_{k},\pi\rfloor<\lfloor u_{k+1},\pi\rfloor}\lvert\delta f_{\lfloor u_{k},\pi\rfloor,\lfloor u_{k+1},\pi\rfloor}\delta g_{\lfloor u_{k+1},\pi\rfloor,u_{k+1}}\rvert^{r}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\sum_{k:\lfloor u_{k},\pi\rfloor<\lfloor u_{k+1},\pi\rfloor}\lvert\delta f_{\lfloor u_{k},\pi\rfloor,\lfloor u_{k+1},\pi\rfloor}\rvert^{2r}\Bigr)^{1/2}\Bigl(\sum_{k:\lfloor u_{k},\pi\rfloor<\lfloor u_{k+1},\pi\rfloor}\lvert\delta g_{\lfloor u_{k+1},\pi\rfloor,u_{k+1}}\rvert^{2r}\Bigr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq(V^{2r}f)^{r}\sup_{t}\lvert\delta g_{\lfloor t,\pi\rfloor,t}\rvert^{\alpha}(V^{2(r-\alpha)}g)^{r-\alpha}.

This expression no longer depends on the sequence
(u_{k})
.
By Lépingle’s and Hölder’s inequalities, this is bounded in
L^{q}
, and, taking into account Lemma
6.3
, this converges to
0
in
L^{q}
.
Hence, the second term on the right-hand side of (
6.15
) converges to
0
in
L^{q}V^{r}
.
Theorem
6.4
and Lépingle’s inequality for
f
now imply

\lim_{\pi}\Pi^{\pi}(f,g)_{t,\lfloor t^{\prime},\pi\rfloor}=\Pi(f,g)_{t,t^{\prime}}\quad\text{in }L^{q}V^{r}.

Writing (
6.14
) as

[f,g]^{\pi}_{t,t^{\prime}}=\delta f^{(\pi)}_{t,t^{\prime}}\delta g^{(\pi)}_{t,t^{\prime}}-\Pi^{\pi}(f,g)_{t,\lfloor t^{\prime},\pi\rfloor}-\Pi^{\pi}(g,f)_{t,\lfloor t^{\prime}\rfloor},

(6.16)

we see that the first summand on the right-hand side converges to
\delta f\delta g
in
L^{q}V^{r}
by Lemma
6.3
, and the remaining summands to Itô integrals by the above discussion.
Hence, the left hand side converges, as was claimed in (
6.12
).
The identity (
6.13
) is the limit of the above equality.
∎

7
A sharp inequality for the square function

The proof of the BDG inequality that we have seen in this notes was quite indirect: we started with some
L^{2}
identities and used the Davis decomposition to lower the
L^{p}
exponent.
In this section, we will take a look at a more direct method for proving martingale inequalities.
In this special case, it will yield an inequality with an optimal constant.

This section follows
[
Bur02
]
.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a real-valued martingale.
Then, for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\Bigl(3\lvert f_{0}\rvert+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\lvert df_{n}\rvert^{2}}{f^{*}_{n}}\Bigr)\leq\mathbb{E}\Bigl(2f^{*}_{N}+\frac{\lvert f_{N}\rvert^{2}}{f^{*}_{N}}\Bigr).

(7.1)

We will see later that the inequality (
7.1
) implies the Davis inequality for the martingale square function with the sharp constant.
In applications, one replaces (
7.1
) by the slightly weaker inequality

\mathbb{E}\Bigl(3\lvert f_{0}\rvert+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\lvert df_{n}\rvert^{2}}{f^{*}_{n}}\Bigr)\leq 3\mathbb{E}f^{*}_{N}.

However, the preculiar form of the right-hand side of (
7.1
) permits to show this result
by induction on
N
.

7.1
A Bellman function

The inductive step in the proof of Theorem
7.1
is usually stated as a concavity property of a special function.
Functions used in such arguments are called “Bellman functions”; many more examples can be found in the books
[
Osę12
,
VV20
]
.

Thoroughout this section,

\mathcal{D}:=\{(x,t,z)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\lvert x\rvert\leq z\}.

We define
U:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
by

U(x,y,m):=y-\frac{\lvert x\rvert^{2}+(\gamma-1)m^{2}}{m},

where
\gamma=3
.
The main feature of this function is the following concavity property.

Proposition 7.2
.

For any
x,h\in\mathbb{R}
and
y,m\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with
\lvert x\rvert\leq m
, we have

U(x+h,y+\frac{\lvert h\rvert^{2}}{(\lvert x+h\rvert\vee m)},\lvert x+h\rvert\vee m)\leq U(x,y,m)-\frac{2(x)h}{m}.

(7.2)

Proof of Theorem
7.1
assuming Proposition
7.2
.

Using (
7.2
) with

x=f_{n},\quad y=\tilde{S}_{n}:=\gamma\lvert f_{0}\rvert+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\lvert df_{j}\rvert^{2}}{f^{*}_{j}},\quad m=f^{*}_{n},\quad h=df_{n+1},

we obtain

U(f_{n+1},\tilde{S}_{n+1},f^{*}_{n+1})\leq U(f_{n},\tilde{S}_{n},f^{*}_{n})-\frac{2f_{n}df_{n+1}}{f^{*}_{n}}.

(7.3)

By conditional independence, we have

\mathbb{E}(\frac{2f_{n}df_{n+1}}{f^{*}_{n}}\lvert\mathcal{F}_{n})=\frac{2f_{n}}{f^{*}_{n}}\mathbb{E}(df_{n+1}\rvert\mathcal{F}_{n})=0.

Taking expectations, we obtain

\mathbb{E}U(f_{n+1},\tilde{S}_{n+1},f^{*}_{n+1})\leq\mathbb{E}U(f_{n},\tilde{S}_{n},f^{*}_{n}).

Iterating this inequality, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\Bigl(3\lvert f_{0}\rvert+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\lvert df_{n}\rvert^{2}}{(f^{*}_{n})}-\frac{\lvert f_{N}\rvert^{2}}{f^{*}_{N}}-2f^{*}_{N}\Bigr)=\mathbb{E}U(f_{N},\tilde{S}_{N},f^{*}_{N})\leq\mathbb{E}U(f_{0},\tilde{S}_{0},f^{*}_{0})=0.\qed

Remark
.

The above proof in fact shows the pathwise inequality

3\lvert f_{0}\rvert+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\lvert df_{n}\rvert^{2}}{(f^{*}_{n})}\leq 2f^{*}_{N}+\frac{\lvert f_{N}\rvert^{2}}{f^{*}_{N}}-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{2(f_{n})df_{n+1}}{(f^{*}_{n})}.

Proof of Proposition
7.2
.

If
\lvert x+h\rvert\leq m
, then

\displaystyle U(x+h,y+\frac{\lvert h\rvert^{2}}{(\lvert x+h\rvert\vee m)},\lvert x+h\rvert\vee m)

\displaystyle=(y+\frac{\lvert h\rvert^{2}}{m})-\frac{\lvert x+h\rvert^{2}+(\gamma-1)m^{2}}{m}

\displaystyle=y+\frac{\lvert h\rvert^{2}}{m}-\frac{\lvert x\rvert^{2}+2xh+\lvert h\rvert^{2}+(\gamma-1)m^{2}}{m}

\displaystyle=y-\frac{\lvert x\rvert^{2}+(\gamma-1)m^{2}}{m}-\frac{2xh}{m}

\displaystyle=U(x,y,m)-\frac{2(x)h}{m}.

If
\lvert x+h\rvert>m
, then we need to show

(y+\frac{\lvert h\rvert^{2}}{\lvert x+h\rvert})-\frac{\lvert x+h\rvert^{2}+(\gamma-1)\lvert x+h\rvert^{2}}{\lvert x+h\rvert}\leq y-\frac{\lvert x\rvert^{2}+(\gamma-1)m^{2}}{m}-\frac{2xh}{m}.

(7.4)

This is equivalent to

\frac{\lvert h\rvert^{2}-\gamma\lvert x+h\rvert^{2}}{\lvert x+h\rvert}\leq\frac{-\lvert x\rvert^{2}-(\gamma-1)m^{2}}{m}-\frac{2xh}{m}.

(7.5)

The inequality (
7.5
) is equivalent to

\frac{\lvert h\rvert^{2}m}{m^{2}\lvert x+h\rvert}-\gamma\frac{\lvert x+h\rvert}{m}\leq\frac{-\lvert x+h\rvert^{2}+\lvert h\rvert^{2}}{m^{2}}-(\gamma-1).

Let
t:=\lvert x+h\rvert/m>1
and
\tilde{t}:=\lvert h\rvert/m
.
Note that
\lvert t-\tilde{t}\rvert=\lvert\lvert x+h\rvert-\lvert h\rvert\rvert/m\leq\lvert x\rvert/m\leq 1
.
With this notation, (
7.5
) is equivalent to

\tilde{t}^{2}/t-\gamma t\leq-t^{2}+\tilde{t}^{2}-(\gamma-1),

or

\gamma\geq\frac{1}{t-1}\bigl(t^{2}-1-\tilde{t}^{2}(1-1/t)\bigr)=(t+1)-\tilde{t}^{2}/t.

Hence, it suffices to ensure

\gamma\geq\sup_{t>1,\lvert t-\tilde{t}\rvert\leq 1}(t+1)-\tilde{t}^{2}/t.

The supremum in
\tilde{t}
is assumed for
\tilde{t}=(t-1)
, so this condition becomes

\gamma\geq\sup_{t>1}t+1-(t-1)^{2}/t=\sup_{t>1}3-1/t=3.

∎

7.2
Sharp constant in the Davis inequality for the square function

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
f
be a real-valued martingale.
Then

\mathbb{E}Sf\leq\sqrt{3}\mathbb{E}f^{*}.

Proof.

By Hölder’s inequality and (
7.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Sf

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl((f^{*})^{1/2}\bigl(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\lvert df_{n}\rvert^{2}}{f^{*}_{n}}\bigr)^{1/2}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\bigl(\mathbb{E}f^{*}\bigr)^{1/2}\bigl(\mathbb{E}\bigl(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\lvert df_{n}\rvert^{2}}{f^{*}_{n}}\bigr)\bigr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{3}\mathbb{E}f^{*}.\qed

The next result shows that
\sqrt{3}
is the best possible constant in Proposition
7.3
.

Proposition 7.4
.

Let
\gamma\geq 0
.
Suppose that the inequality

\mathbb{E}Sf\leq\gamma\mathbb{E}f^{*}

(7.6)

holds for all real-valued simple martingales
f
with
f_{0}=0
.
Then
\gamma\geq\sqrt{3}
.

Proof.

Let
V:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
be given by

V(x,t,z):=\sqrt{t}-\gamma z.

Define
U:\mathcal{D}\to(-\infty,\infty]
by

U(x,t,z):=\sup\Big\{\mathbb{E}V(f_{\infty},t+S^{2}(f),f^{*}\vee z)\nonscript\>\Big|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}f_{0}=x\Big\},

(7.7)

where the supremum is taken over all simple martingales (that is, martingales
f
that take only finitely many values).
This function will play the role of the “best” Bellman function for the inequality (
7.6
).

Substituting the constant martingale into the definition (
7.7
), we see that, for any
(x,t,z)\in\cal D
, we have

V(x,t,z)\leq U(x,t,z),

(7.8)

We claim that, for any simple measurable function
d:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
with
\mathbb{E}d=0
, we have

\mathbb{E}U(x+d,t+\lvert d\rvert^{2},\lvert x+d\rvert\vee z)\leq U(x,t,z).

(7.9)

Proof of (
7.9
).

Fix
x\in\mathbb{R}
with
\lvert x\rvert\leq z
.
Let
d:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be a simple function with
\mathbb{E}d=0
and
\mathbb{P}(d=s_{j})=p_{j}\in(0,1]
for
1\leq j\leq m
, where
\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}=1
.
Choose
b_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
so that

U\left(x+s_{j},t+\lvert s_{j}\rvert^{2},\lvert x+s_{j}\rvert\vee z\right)>b_{j}.

Then, by the definition of
U
, there exists a martingale
f^{j}
with
f^{j}_{0}=x+s_{j}
satisfying

\mathbb{E}V\bigl(f_{\infty}^{j},t+\lvert s_{j}\rvert^{2}+S^{2}\left(f^{j}\right),(f^{j})^{*}\vee z\bigr)>b_{j}.

Let
f
be a martingale with
f_{1}=x+d
which continues with the same distribution as
f^{j}
(rescaled by
p_{j}
in measure) on the set
\{d=s_{j}\}
.
Because
\lvert x\rvert\leq z
, we have
f^{*}\vee z=(f^{j})^{*}\vee z
on
\{d=s_{j}\}
.
By (
7.7
), we have

\displaystyle U(x,t,z)

\displaystyle\geq EV\left(f_{\infty},t+S^{2}(f),f^{*}\vee z\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{\{d=s_{j}\}}V\left(f_{\infty},t+\lvert s_{j}\rvert^{2}+\lvert df_{2}\rvert^{2}+\cdots,f^{*}\vee z\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}\mathbb{E}V\left(f_{\infty}^{j},t+\lvert s_{j}\rvert^{2}+S^{2}(f^{j}),(f^{j})^{*}\vee z\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}b_{j}.

Using the freedom in the choice of
b_{j}
’s, this implies (
7.9
).
∎

For every
\lambda>0
, we have

V(x,t,z)=\lambda V(x/\lambda,t/\lambda^{2},z/\lambda),\quad U(x,t,z)=\lambda U(x/\lambda,t/\lambda^{2},z/\lambda).

Define
u,v:[-1,1]\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to(-\infty,\infty]
by

v(x,t):=V(x,t,1),\quad u(x,t)=U(x,t,1).

Since
t\mapsto V(x,t,z)
is nondecreasing, the same holds for
t\mapsto u(x,t)
, so left limits exist.
We claim that

u(1,1-)\geq u(0,2-)+u(1,1-).

(7.10)

To see this, let
0<s<1<r
.
Let
d
be a random variable such that
\mathbb{P}(d=-1)=r/(r+1)
and
\mathbb{P}(d=r)=1/(r+1)
.
Then, (
7.9
) and scaling imply that

\displaystyle u(1,s)

\displaystyle=U(1,s,1)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{r}{r+1}U(0,s+1,1)+\frac{1}{1+r}U(1+r,s+r^{2},1+r)

\displaystyle=\frac{r}{r+1}u(0,s+1)+u(1,(s+r^{2})/(1+r)^{2})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{r}{r+1}u(0,s+1)+u(1,r^{2}/(1+r)^{2}),

where we used monotonicity of
u
in the second variable in the last step.
Both summands on the RHS are increasing in both variables
s,r
.
Taking
r\to\infty
and
s\to 1
, we obtain (
7.10
).

If
f
is a simple martingale with
f_{0}=0
, then, by the hypothesis (
7.6
), we have

\mathbb{E}V(f_{\infty},0+S^{2}f,f^{*}\vee 1)=\mathbb{E}Sf-\gamma\mathbb{E}(f^{*}\vee 1)\leq\mathbb{E}Sf-\gamma\mathbb{E}f^{*}\leq 0.

By the definition (
7.7
), this implies that
u(0,0)=U(0,0,1)\leq 0
.

Let
d
be a random variable with
\mathbb{P}(d=1)=\mathbb{P}(d=-1)=1/2
.
Using (
7.9
) and the fact that
u(x,t)=u(-x,t)
, we obtain

0\geq u(0,0)\geq\frac{1}{2}[u(1,1)+u(-1,1)]=u(1,1)\geq u(1,1-)\geq v(1,1-)=1-\gamma.

This implies that
u(1,1-)
is finite.
So (
7.10
) yields
u(0,2-)\leq 0
.
Let
d
be a random variable with
\mathbb{P}(d=1)=\mathbb{P}(d=-1)=1/2
.
By (
7.9
), for every
\varepsilon>0
, we obtain

0\geq u(0,2-)\geq u(0,2-\varepsilon)\geq\frac{1}{2}[u(1,3-\varepsilon)+u(-1,3-\varepsilon)]\\
=u(1,3-\varepsilon)\geq v(1,3-\varepsilon)=\sqrt{3-\varepsilon}-\gamma

Therefore,
\gamma\geq\sqrt{3-\varepsilon}
.
Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, this implies
\gamma\geq\sqrt{3}
.
∎
