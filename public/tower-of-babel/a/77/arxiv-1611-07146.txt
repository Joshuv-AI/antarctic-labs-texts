Discrepancy of general symplectic lattices
Jayadev S. Athreya and Ioannis Konstantoulas

Abstract
. We consider random lattices taken from the general symplectic ensemble and count the number of lattice points of a typical lattice in nested families B t B_{t} of certain Borel sets. Our main result is that for almost every general symplectic lattice, the discrepancy D ⁡ ( Λ , B t ) D(\Lambda,B_{t}) of the lattice point count with respect to the volumes is O ⁡ ( vol ​ ( B t ) − δ ) O(\textrm{vol}(B_{t})^{-\delta}) . This extends work of W. Schmidt who gave similar discrepancy bounds for the space of all lattices in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} .

For the case

[
11

Rogers’s formulas seem to be specific to the full space of lattices and the Eisenstein series approach, although promising for

In this work, we prove a Rogers type formula for the symplectic group and apply it to give estimates for the corresponding variance

2.
Analysis of the second moment

In order to extract the main and error terms from the second moment of the (primitive) Siegel transform

(2.1)

\widehat{f}(\Lambda)=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\textrm{prim}}}f(\lambda)

we will regroup the sum into orbits of the diagonal action of
\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{Z})
on primitive pairs of integral vectors.
We will use the straightforward steps of the regrouping as an opportunity to introduce notation and make our conventions clear.

Let
F
be a (fixed once and for all) fundamental domain of
\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})
for the action of
\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{Z})
,
d\mu
the Haar measure that gives total measure
1
to
F
.
We will occasionally be sloppy and write
\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})/\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{Z})
for
F
; any homogeneous space that we will write down will be either a discrete tiling by
F
with the obvious counting measure on the quotient, or an embedded subdomain in
F
with the normalized Haar measure induced by restricting the Lie algebra directions to the homogeneous space defining the subdomain.

Now we can proceed to expand and regroup the square of the Siegel transform:

\displaystyle\left(\widehat{f}(g\mathbb{Z}^{2n})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{2n}_{\textrm{{prim}}}}f(gz)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{(u,v)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2n}_{\textrm{{prim}}}\times\mathbb{Z}^{2n}_{\textrm{{prim}}}}f(gu)f(gv)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{o}\in\mathcal{O}}\sum_{(u,v)\in\mathfrak{o}}f(gu)f(gv)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{o}\in\mathcal{O}}\sum_{\gamma\in\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{Z})/\textrm{Stab}_{\Gamma}(\mathfrak{o})}f(g\gamma u_{0})f(g\gamma v_{0}).

Here
\mathcal{O}
denotes the set of orbits of
\Gamma=\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{Z})
acting diagonally on
\mathbb{Z}^{2n}_{\textrm{prim}}\times\mathbb{Z}^{2n}_{\textrm{prim}}
and
\textrm{Stab}_{\Gamma}(\mathfrak{o})
is the stabilizer in
\Gamma
of an arbitrary representative
(u_{0},v_{0})
of the orbit
\mathfrak{o}
(we will pick convenient representatives later).

Now we pass the integration over the fundamental domain
F
down to the level of individual orbits to get

\displaystyle\int_{F}\left(\widehat{f}(g\mathbb{Z}^{2n})\right)^{2}\,d\mu(g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{o}\in\mathcal{O}}\int_{F}\sum_{\gamma\in\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{Z})/\textrm{Stab}_{\Gamma}(\mathfrak{o})}f(g\gamma u_{0})f(g\gamma v_{0})\,d\mu(g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{o}\in\mathcal{O}}\int_{U}f(gu_{0})f(gv_{0})\,d\mu(g),

where

U=\bigcup_{\gamma\in\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{Z})/\textrm{Stab}_{\Gamma}(\mathfrak{o})}F\gamma

is the fundamental domain for the infinite measure homogeneous space
Sp(2n,\mathbb{R})/\textrm{Stab}_{\Gamma}(\mathfrak{o})
given by tiling the translates of
F
.

Thus the problem of estimating this second moment breaks down to understanding the set
\mathcal{O}
of diagonal orbits of
\Gamma
on primitive pairs of vectors and the individual contributions of each term.
The first part is dealt with in the next section, while the second part is treated in the subsequent sections where it splits further into “intrinsically” arithmetic contributions and geometric contributions.
The former is reflected in the indices of conjugates of
\textrm{Stab}_{\Gamma}(\mathfrak{o})
in
\textrm{Stab}_{\Gamma}(\mathfrak{o}_{1})
for a specific orbit
\mathfrak{o}_{1}
and the latter in the fiber measure of an embedded copy of
\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})/\textrm{Sp}(2n-2,\mathbb{R})
in
U
.
Happily, the two conspire to give exactly the expected behavior as we average over orbits.

denote the pair
\langle e^{1},sf^{1}\rangle
.
It is clear that the stabilizer of
(s)
in
\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})
is the embedded
\textrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{R})=:S(s)
fixing the symplectic plane
\mathbb{R}e^{1}+\mathbb{R}f^{1}
, and this describes the stabilizer in
G
of any point of an orbit
(s,d,a)
as a suitable
G
-congugate of
S(s)
(in fact, as a conjugate over
\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1}{s}])
).

Explicitly, the transformation in
\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1}{s}])
given by

(4.1)

T_{(s,d,a)}=\left(e^{1},e^{2},\cdots,\frac{-d}{s}e^{1}+e^{n},\frac{a}{s}e^{1}+f^{1}+f^{n}\frac{d}{s},f^{2},\cdots,f^{n}\right)

fixes
e^{1}
and takes
(a,0,\cdots,s,\cdots,d)^{\intercal}
to
sf^{1}
.
Thus we have

\textrm{Stab}_{G}(s,d,a)=T_{(s,d,a)}^{-1}S(s)T_{(s,d,a)}.

The crucial thing about this stabilizer is that its
\mathbb{Z}
-points come from the subgroup of
\textrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{Z})
of matrices with the constraints (
i
below ranges from
1
to
m-1
for
n\geq 3
):

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A&B\\
C&D\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle A_{mi}\equiv D_{im}\equiv 0\pmod{q}

\displaystyle A_{mm}\equiv D_{mm}\equiv 1\pmod{q},

\displaystyle B_{mi}\equiv B_{im}\equiv 0\pmod{q}

\displaystyle B_{mm}\equiv 0\pmod{q^{2}}.

where
q=\frac{s}{d}
.

For instance, when
n=2
the stabilizer is conjugate to the preimage of

(4.5)

\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1+qk&0\\
l&1-qk\end{array}\right):k\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z},\,l\in\mathbb{Z}/q^{2}\mathbb{Z}\right\}<\textrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}/q^{2}\mathbb{Z})

of cardinality
q^{3}
, so the stabilizer corresponds to a subgroup of
\textrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
of index

(4.6)

[\textrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}):S_{\Gamma}(s,d,a)]=q^{3}\prod_{p|q^{2}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right).

In general, we have

Lemma 4.1
.

The index of
S_{\Gamma}(s,d,a)
in the copy of
\textrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{Z})
in
S(s)
is

(4.7)

q^{2m+1}\prod_{p|q}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2m}}\right)

where the outer product is over primes
p
dividing
q
.

Proof.

First of all we have the general formula for
q\geq 2

(4.8)

\left|\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})\right|=q^{2n^{2}+n}\prod_{p|q}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2i}}\right).

Let’s abbreviate
S_{\Gamma}(s,d,a)=S
.
The index in
\textrm{Sp}(2n-2,\mathbb{Z})
of
S
is the same as the index of
S\pmod{q^{2}}=:S_{q^{2}}
in
\textrm{Sp}(2n-2,\mathbb{Z}/q^{2}\mathbb{Z})=:\Gamma_{q^{2}}
by the surjectivity of projections of arithmetic groups onto their
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})
-points and the fact that
S
contains the principal congruence subgroup modulo
q^{2}
.
Next we want to simplify the counting by noting that

|S_{q^{2}}|=|S_{q}||\ker(S_{q^{2}}\to S_{q})|

so

(4.9)

|\Gamma_{q^{2}}:S_{q^{2}}|=\frac{|\Gamma_{q^{2}}|}{|S_{q}||\ker(S_{q^{2}}\to S_{q})|}.

First we treat the cardinality
|S_{q}|
.
The image
S_{q}
consists of matrices of the form

(4.10)

\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}a&x&b&0\\
0&1&0&0\\
c&y&d&0\\
t&z&s&1\\
\end{array}\right)

where
(a,b,c,d)\in\textrm{Sp}(2m-2,\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})
arbitrary,
(x,y,z)\in(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{2(m-1)+1}
arbitrary and
(s,t)\in(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{2m-2}
completely determined by the previous data.
In fact, we can recognize this subgroup as

(4.11)

S_{q}\simeq\textrm{Sp}(2m-2,\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})\ltimes H_{2m-1}(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})

where
H_{k}
is the
k
-dimensional Heisenberg group given by any symplectic form defined over
\mathbb{Z}
.

Using induction and the obvious cardinality of the Heisenberg group, we have

(4.12)

|S_{q}|=q^{2m-1}q^{2(m-1)^{2}+(m-1)}\prod_{p|q}\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2i}}\right)

which we simplify to

|S_{q}|=q^{2m^{2}-m}\prod_{p|q}\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2i}}\right).

Now the kernel of the projection morphism consists of symplectic matrices as in (
4
) supplanted by the rest of the congruences giving
A\equiv 1\pmod{q}
,
B\equiv 0\pmod{q}
,
C\equiv 0\pmod{q}
and
D\equiv 1\pmod{q}
with the extra condition
B_{mm}=0
in
\mathbb{Z}/q^{2}\mathbb{Z}
.

Writing each such matrix
M
as
M=I+qX
where

X=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}X_{1}&X_{2}\\
X_{3}&X_{4}\end{array}\right)

and each of the block matrices with entries in
\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
, the symplectic restrictions in
\mathbb{Z}/q^{2}\mathbb{Z}
become the equations
X_{3}=X_{3}^{\intercal}
,
X_{2}=X_{2}^{\intercal}
,
X_{1}=-X_{4}^{\intercal}
.
From the congruences in (
4
) the only one not appearing above is
B_{mm}\equiv 0\pmod{q^{2}}
which becomes the extra restriction
(X_{2})_{mm}=0
.

With this description of the kernel, we can now easily count:
q^{m^{2}}q^{\frac{m(m+1)}{2}}q^{\frac{m(m+1)}{2}-1}=q^{2m^{2}+m-1}
is the cardinality of the kernel. Now that we have the cardinality of the kernel and the image, we can use (
4.9
) and the fact that
p|q\iff p|q^{2}
for a prime
p
to get the result.
∎

5.
Orbital spaces and their measures

Using the transformation
T_{(s,d,a)}
from the previous section, we can translate the space (we emphasize again that these spaces are identified with
F
-tilings inside
\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})
)

\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})/\textrm{Stab}_{\Gamma}(s,d,a)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{(s,d,a)}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})/\Gamma_{1}

where
\Gamma_{1}
is the congruence subgroup we obtained there. This transformation takes the representative
(s,d,a)
to
(s,0,0)=(e^{1},sf^{1})
which is fixed by all
\gamma\in\textrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{Z})
, for the embedded
\textrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{Z})=S_{\Gamma}((s,0,0))=S_{\Gamma}((1,0,0))
in the stabilizer of the plane
\mathbb{R}e^{1}+\mathbb{R}f^{1}
.
This way we obtain the identity (since
T
is measure-preserving)

\displaystyle\int_{\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})/S_{\Gamma}(s,d,a)}f(gu_{0})f(gv_{0})\,d\mu(g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})/\Gamma_{1}}f(ge^{1})f(sgf^{1})\,d\mu(g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[\textrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{Z}):\Gamma_{1}]\int_{\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})/\textrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{Z})}f(ge^{1})f(sgf^{1})\,d\mu(g).

Now we want to decompose

\displaystyle\int_{\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})/\textrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{Z})}f(ge^{1})f(sgf^{1})\,d\mu(g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})/\textrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{R})}\int_{\textrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{R})/\textrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{Z})}f(ghe^{1})f(sghf^{1})\,d\mu_{2}(h)\,d\mu_{1}(g)

where
\mu_{i}
come from Haar measures on the corresponding group normalized so their product gives
d\mu
.
The most convenient way of doing this is to use the Lie algebra perspective taken in
[
6
]
, which is equivalent to the approach of
[
3
]
.
In both cases, for the embedded
\textrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{R})/\textrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{Z})
(which happens to be the exact same space we are considering here) we get the recurrence relation

\textrm{vol}(\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})/\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{Z}))=\zeta(2n)\textrm{vol}(\textrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{R})/\textrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{Z}))

so with our normalization
\textrm{vol}(F)=1
, the latter has total volume
\frac{1}{\zeta(2n)}
.
Since the pair
(e^{1},f^{1})
is fixed by this copy of
\textrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{R})
, we finally get the expression for the orbital integral

(5.1)

\int_{\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})/S_{\Gamma}(s,d,a)}f(gu_{0})f(gv_{0})\,d\mu(g)=\frac{[\textrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{Z}):\Gamma_{1}]}{\zeta(2n)}\int_{\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})/\textrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{R})}f(ge^{1})f(sgf^{1})\,d\mu_{1}(g).

The choice of the normalizations made in
[
6
]
for the
\mu
and
\mu_{2}
of course forces
\mu_{1}
to have a specific normalization, namely the one coming from taking the orthonormal basis vectors of the Lie algebra of
\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})
orthogonal to those of
\textrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{R})
and tensoring to get the volume form on the quotient.
To understand this space, let
h(x,y)=f(x)f(sy)
, and write the integral as

\int_{\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})/\textrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{R})}h(ge^{1},gf^{1})\,d\mu_{1}(g).

Since
\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})
is transitive on pairs of the symplectic value
1
and
\textrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{R})
is the stabilizer of one such point, the homogeneous space can be identified with the hypersurface of pairs
(x,y)\in(\mathbb{R}^{2n})^{2}
with
\langle x,y\rangle=1
(in fact, it is clear that the pair is the first and
(n+1)
-th columns of
g
).
Now we will give convenient coordinates and write down the measure explicitly.

First, fix
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}\setminus 0
.
Select
y^{*}(x)
so that
\langle x,y^{*}(x)\rangle=1
and extend
\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})
-equivariantly
by defining
y^{*}(gx)=gy^{*}(x)
for all
g\in\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})
.
Since
\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})
is transitive on non-zero vectors, this defines
y^{*}
for all
x
.

Note that if two vectors
y,y^{\prime}
satisfy
\langle x,y\rangle=\langle x,y^{\prime}\rangle=s
, their difference lies on the hyperplane
\langle x,z\rangle=0
. On each hyperplane we give coordinates as follows: given
x
as before, pick a basis
y_{1}(x),\cdots,y_{2n-1}(x)
for the hyperplane so that
y_{i}(gx)=gy_{i}(x)
.

With this definition, it is trivial to check that we get an equivariant assignment of bases for each non-zero
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.
Then every element in the hypersurface
\langle x,y\rangle=s
can be written as

(5.2)

(x,y^{*}(x)+t_{1}y_{1}(x)+\cdots+t_{2n-1}y_{2n-1}(x))=(x,sy^{*}(x)+t\cdot\tilde{y}(x))

and this coordinate system is compatible with the diagonal action of
\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})
.

Lemma 5.1
.

The measure
dx_{1}\cdots dx_{2n}\,dt_{1}\cdots dt_{2n-1}
coincides with the Haar measure on
\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})/\textrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{R})
with the normalization we chose.

Proof.

This follows directly from the
G
-equivariance of the assignments
y^{*}
and
y_{i}
and the fact that this is precisely the measure coming from tensoring the Lie algebra directions orthogonal to
\textrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{R})
.
∎

Proposition 5.2
.

We have

(5.3)

\displaystyle\int_{\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})/S_{\Gamma}(s,d,a)}f(gu_{0})f(gv_{0})\,d\mu(g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{[\textrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{Z}):\Gamma_{1}]}{s^{2n-1}\zeta(2n)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n-1}}f(x)f\left(sy^{*}+\sum_{i}t_{i}y_{i}(x)\right)\,dt\,dx.

Proof.

From the previous lemma,

\int_{\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})/\textrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{R})}h(ge^{1},gf^{1})\,d\mu_{1}(g)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n-1}}h(x,y^{*}(x)+\sum t_{i}y_{i})\,dt\,dx

and now substitute the definition and make the change of variables
(x,y)\to(x,sy)
.
∎

This concludes our analysis of the individual orbital integrals.

6.
Orbital sums in a given symplectic class and effective Ikehara-Wiener

We now aggregate orbital integrals corresponding to a given
s
and estimate their contribution.
From the results of the previous sections we have

(6.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{d|s,a\in(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{*}}\int_{\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})/\textrm{Stab}_{\Gamma}(s,d,a)}f(gu_{0},gv_{0})\,d\mu(g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\zeta(2n)s^{2n-1}}\sum_{d|s,a\in(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{*}}q^{2m+1}\prod_{p|q}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2m}}\right)\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n-1}}f(x)f\left(sy^{*}+\sum_{i}t_{i}y_{i}(x)\right)\,dt\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\zeta(2n)s^{2n-1}}\sum_{d|s}\phi(d)q^{2m+1}\prod_{p|q}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2m}}\right)\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n-1}}f(x)f\left(sy^{*}+\sum_{i}t_{i}y_{i}(x)\right)\,dt\,dx.

The goal in this section is to estimate the contribution of the arithmetic coefficients.
To that end, note that the divisor sum is the Dirichlet convolution of
\phi(s)
and
X(s)=s^{2m+1}\prod_{p|s}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2m}}\right)
.

Definition 6.1
.

Let

(6.2)

a(s)=\frac{1}{\zeta(2n)s^{2n-1}}\sum_{d|s}\phi(d)q^{2m+1}\prod_{p|q}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2m}}\right)=\frac{(\phi*X)(s)}{s^{2n-1}\zeta(2n)}

denote the
s
-th arithmetic coefficient in the integral above, and

A(M)=\sum_{s\leq M}a(S)

its summatory function.

Lemma 6.2
.

The
L
-function corresponding to
\phi*X
is
L(s)=\frac{\zeta(s-2n+1)}{\zeta(s)}
.

Proof.

This follows from a direct computation of the Euler product of
\phi*X
.
Since the
L
-series of
\phi
is
\frac{\zeta(s-1)}{\zeta(s)}
, we need to show the corresponding one for
X
is
\frac{\zeta(s-2n+1)}{\zeta(s-1)}
.
We summarize the computation of the Euler product for
X
:
For a fixed prime power
p^{k}
, we have
X(p^{k})=X(p)=p^{2n-1}\left(1-p^{-2n+2}\right)
.
Thus the Euler factor at
p
is

\left(1+X(p)(p^{-s}+p^{-2s}+\cdots)\right)=\left(1+p^{2n-1-s}\left(1-p^{-2n+2}\right)\left(1+p^{-s}+\cdots\right)\right)

and summing the geometric series we get

\left(1+p^{2n-1-s}\left(1-p^{-2n+2}\right)\left(1-p^{-s}\right)^{-1}\right)

which after simplifying gives

1+\frac{1-p^{-2n+2}}{1-p^{-s+2n-1}}p^{-s+2n-1}=\frac{1-p^{-s+1}}{1-p^{-s+2n-1}}

which we recognize as the
p
-th Euler factor of

\displaystyle L(X,s)=\prod_{p}\frac{1-p^{-s+1}}{1-p^{-s+2n-1}}=\frac{\zeta(s-2n+1)}{\zeta(s-1)}.

∎

The next observation, although trivial, is very important in handling the terms
\frac{\phi*X}{s^{2n-1}}
.

Lemma 6.3
.

The convolution
\phi*X
is always bounded by
s^{2n-1}
.

Proof.

The function is multiplicative, and for prime powers the claim is clear.
∎

We now need an estimate on the summatory function of
\phi*X
; since its
L
-function is explicitly given in terms of the Riemann zeta function, we can use classical methods to give an Ikehara-Wiener type estimate with error term.
Tauberian theorems for Dirichlet series are usually either given with no explicit error estimate or in such generality that it is easier to argue directly from the effective Perron formulae, which we do next.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
S(x)
be the summatory function of
s_{m}=(\phi*X)(m)
.
We have for every
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small

(6.3)

S(x)=\frac{x^{2n}}{2n\zeta(2n)}+O_{\epsilon}\left(\frac{x^{2n}\ln(x)}{x^{\frac{3}{13}-\epsilon}}\right).

Proof.

First, we use the effective Perron formula
[
10
, Section II.2.1, Theorem 2]
to write, for
T
large enough and
k>2n
,

(6.4)

S(x)=\int_{k-iT}^{k+iT}L(s)\frac{x^{s}}{s}\,ds+O\left(x^{k}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{s_{m}}{m^{k}(1+T|\log(x/m)|}\right).

Using Lemma
6.3
and making the choice
k=2n+\epsilon
, we get

S(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{k-iT}^{k+iT}L(s)\frac{x^{s}}{s}\,ds+O\left(x^{2n+\epsilon}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m^{1+\epsilon}(1+{T|\log(x/m))|})}\right).

Now by elementary calculus

\sum_{m=\sqrt{x}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m^{1+\epsilon}}=O_{\epsilon}(x^{-\epsilon/2})

and for
1\leq m\leq\sqrt{x}
we have
1+T|\log(x/m)|>\frac{1}{2}T\log(x)

giving overall

S(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{k-iT}^{k+iT}L(s)\frac{x^{s}}{s}\,ds+O\left(\frac{x^{2n+\epsilon}}{T\log(x)}\right).

Next, we shift the integration from the line segment
\Re(s)=2n+\epsilon,\,|\Im(s)|\leq T
to
\Re(s)=2n-\frac{1}{2}
passing through
s=2n
where the numerator of
L(s)
has a simple pole of residue
\frac{1}{\zeta(2n)}
(the denominator has no zeros in this rectangle so there are no poles contributing from it).
Therefore, from Cauchy’s theorem we get

\displaystyle S(x)=\frac{x^{2n}}{2n\zeta(2n)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{2n-\frac{1}{2}-iT}^{2n-\frac{1}{2}+iT}L(s)\frac{x^{s}}{s}\,ds+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{2n-\frac{1}{2}-iT}^{2n+\epsilon-iT}L(s)\frac{x^{s}}{s}\,ds+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{2n-\frac{1}{2}+iT}^{2n+\epsilon+iT}L(s)\frac{x^{s}}{s}\,ds

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{x^{2n+\epsilon}}{T\log(x)}\right).

Since
n\geq 1
,
\frac{1}{\zeta(s)}
is bounded on the half-plane
\Re(s)\geq 2n-\frac{1}{2}
by
\prod_{p}(1+p^{-3/2})
and
|s|\geq|\Re(s)|\geq 3/2
.
Thus
|L(s)|
is determined on this half-plane by the behavior of
\zeta(s-2n+1)
which is known:
on the critical line we have the subconvexity bound
|\zeta(\frac{1}{2}+i\tau)|\leq C\tau^{1/6}\log(\tau)
(
[
10
, Section II.3.4, Corollary 5.2]
) with obvious conventions for small
\tau
, so

\left|\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{2n-\frac{1}{2}-iT}^{2n-\frac{1}{2}+iT}L(s)\frac{x^{s}}{s}\,ds\right|=O\left(x^{2n-\frac{1}{2}}T^{\frac{7}{6}}\log(T)\right).

For the horizontal integrals, at levels
\pm iT
we have
|\zeta(s)|\leq T^{\frac{1-\sigma}{3}}
for
\frac{1}{2}\leq\sigma\leq 1
and beyond
1

\zeta
is
O(\log(T))
so taking the worse bound over the entire line segment and using
|s|\geq T
, with the change of variables
s^{\prime}=2n-1+\sigma+iT
we get

\int_{2n-\frac{1}{2}+iT}^{2n+\epsilon_{i}T}L(s)\frac{x^{s}}{s}\,ds\leq C\frac{x^{2n-1}}{T}T^{1/3}\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{1+\epsilon}T^{-\sigma/3}x^{\sigma}\,d\sigma

\leq C\frac{x^{2n+\epsilon}}{T^{-\epsilon/3-1}}\log(x/T^{1/3}).

Finally, looking at the contributions of the various segments we let
T=x^{a}
for an
a
to be optimized: we have to balance
2n+\epsilon-a
from the horizontal segments and the Perron error with
2n-\frac{1}{2}+7/6a
from the critical line so we choose
2n-a=2n-\frac{1}{2}+7/6a
to get
a=\frac{3}{13}
.
∎

Remark 6.5
.

The above result is not optimal and the exponent
3/13
can be improved with a more careful analysis.
Below we will use a softer bound implied by the lemma to make computations easier.

The next proposition gives the asymptotics of the summatory function of the arithmetic coefficient in the expression we got for the second moment

Proposition 6.6
.

We have the asymptotic

(6.5)

A(M)=\frac{M}{\zeta(2n)^{2}}(1+E(M))

with error term
E=O(M^{-\delta})
for some
\delta>0
.

Proof.

By the previous lemma, the summatory function
S(M)=\sum_{s=1}^{M}\phi*X
has asymptotic

S(M)=\frac{M^{2n}}{2n\zeta(2n)}(1+O(M^{-\delta})).

Then a summation by parts gives

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{M}\frac{\phi*X(s)}{s^{2n-1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{M}\frac{dS(s)}{s^{2n-1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{M}S(s)d(s^{-2n+1})+\frac{S(M)}{M^{-2n+1}}+O(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{M}\frac{1}{\zeta(2n)}\frac{s^{2n}}{2n}(2n-1)s^{-2n}+\frac{M}{\zeta(2n)}+O(M^{1-\delta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\zeta(2n)}(M+O(\int_{1}^{M}s^{-\delta}\,ds))=\frac{M}{\zeta(2n)}(1+E(M)).

∎

7.
Stieltjes integration and discrete Fubini

In this section we will give an explicit formula for the mean square error averaged over
\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})/\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{Z})
; we will not use it verbatim in the sequel but consider it important because it clarifies what precisely needs to be bounded and in what way. It should be noted that this formula could in principle be used to give discrepancy bounds over
\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})/\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{Z})
rather than
GSp
, but he could not control the terms in sufficient generality.

To begin, observe that the integral

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n-1}}f(x)f\left(sy^{*}+\sum_{i}t_{i}y_{i}(x)\right)\,dt\,dx

has the property that if we integrate over
s
and use Fubini’s theorem, we get
\textrm{vol}(B)^{2}
.
This is because the inner integration will be over
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
of the characteristic function
f
with respect to Lebesgue measure:
y^{*}
and the
y_{i}
together form a basis for
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
which comes from the standard basis
e^{i},f^{i}
by an element
g\in\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})
which has determinant
1
.

Using equation (
6.1
), the second moment of
\widehat{f}
becomes

(7.1)

\sum_{\textrm{lin. dep.}}+\sum_{s=-\infty}^{\infty}a(|s|)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n-1}}f(x)f\left(sy^{*}+\sum_{i}t_{i}y_{i}(x)\right)\,dt\,dx.

The first term gathers the orbits of linearly dependent pairs of primitive vectors, which will clearly have contribution asymptotic to
\frac{\textrm{vol}(B)}{\zeta(2n)}
.
Thus we focus on the second term over primitive pairs, as it emerges after the reductions in the previous sections.
Let’s give a convenient name to the geometric term in the sum:

Definition 7.1
.

Let

(7.2)

G(s)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n-1}}f(x)f\left(sy^{*}+\sum_{i}t_{i}y_{i}(x)\right)\,dt\,dx

be the integral over the symplectic hypersurface, and

(7.3)

C(f)=\sum_{s=-\infty}^{\infty}a(|s|)G(s)

the total contribution of the primitive pairs.

We will think of
C(f)
as a twisted integral by the arithmetic terms
a(s)
and our goal is to decouple the two quantities.
In order to isolate what we expect to be the main term, write
C(f)
as a Stieltjes integral

(7.4)

C(f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}a(|s|)G(s)d[s]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}a(|s|)G(s)\,ds+\int_{\mathbb{R}}a(|s|)G(s)\,d\{s\}

and isolate the contribution from
G
:

C(f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}G(s)\,ds-\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-a(|s|))G(s)\,ds+\int_{\mathbb{R}}a(|s|)G(s)\,d\{s\}

giving

C(f)=\frac{\textrm{vol}(B)^{2}}{\zeta(2n)^{2}}-\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-a(|s|))G(s)\,ds+\int_{\mathbb{R}}a(|s|)G(s)\,d\{s\}.

Here it is crucial that
a(|s|)
and
1-a(|s|)
lie in
[0,1]
.
We summarize the result in the following

Proposition 7.2
.

We have the explicit formula for the error term

(7.5)

\left|C(f)-\frac{\textrm{vol}(B)^{2}}{\zeta(2n)^{2}}\right|=\int_{\mathbb{R}}a(|s|)G(s)\,d\{s\}-\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-a(|s|))G(s)\,ds.

We conclude this section by noting that

(7.6)

G(s)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}f(x)\tilde{f}(r\omega,s)\,dx

where
x=r\omega
is the polar decomposition of
x
and
\tilde{f}
is the Radon transform of
f
.
We expect that the realization of
G(s)
as an averaged Radon transform will be instrumental in giving bounds for
C(f)
.

8.
Discrepancy in the symplectic cone

At the heart of
[
8
]
is a mean square error estimate for the lattice points taken from a random lattice of determinant
0<\det(\Lambda)\leq 1
.
That estimate was used to make a claim about almost everywhere bounds with respect to Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
, by giving explicit bounds for an equivalent (mutually absolutely continuous) measure.
Here, we will prove similar bounds averaging over a much thinner set, namely the subgroup
\textrm{GSp}^{+}(2n,\mathbb{R})<\textrm{GL}(2n,\mathbb{R})
given by

\textrm{GSp}^{+}(2n,\mathbb{R})=\{g\in\textrm{GL}(2n,\mathbb{R}):gJg^{\intercal}=c_{g}J\textrm{ for some }c_{g}>0\}

where
J
is the matrix in the definition of
Sp
.
We can show that
c_{g}=\det(g)^{\frac{1}{n}}
and observe that
c_{g}^{-1/2}g\in\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})
.

The method is nearly identical to that of the aforementioned work once we have the expression (
7.3
) at our disposal.
For this reason, we will be brief in our arguments, and only highlight the main ideas and points of departure from
[
8
, Section 4]
.
One important difference concerns the right scaling factors; the determinant scaling needs to be replaced by more intrinsic factor in higher symplectic spaces.

First of all, note
\textrm{GSp}^{+}(2n,\mathbb{R})=\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{*}
where we write
\mathbb{R}^{*}=\mathbb{R}_{>0}
viewed as the connected component of the identity in the multiplicative group of
\mathbb{R}
.
Under the map
g\mapsto(c_{g}^{-\frac{1}{2}}g,c_{g})
, the Haar measure of
\textrm{GSp}^{+}
decomposes as
dh(g)=\nu\,d\mu(\tilde{g})\,dm(\nu)
which we normalize so that
d\mu
is the probability measure on
\textrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})
and
dm
the usual Lebesgue measure giving measure
1
to
(0,1]
; in particular the measure of the unit cone
C=\{g\in\textrm{GSp}(2n,\mathbb{R}):c_{g}\in(0,1]\}
is
\frac{1}{2}
.
Therefore, integrating against Haar measure on
C
will not give an average of
\frac{1}{\zeta(2n)}\textrm{vol}(B)
lattice points in a Borel set
B
.
Schmidt’s idea in the
n=1
case was then to change the measure so that the average over
\mathbb{S}
will be the same as the average over
C
for an appropriately weighted version of
\chi_{B}
.
To that end, define
du
on
\textrm{GSp}(2n,\mathbb{R})/\textrm{GSp}(2n,\mathbb{Z})
by

\int_{C}f(g)du(g)=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{S}}h(\nu^{\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{(}g))d\mu(\tilde{g})d\nu

for a Borel function
h
on
C
.

The function to which we will apply this integration (in the Cartesian coordinates mentioned above) is
h(\tilde{g},\nu)=\nu^{n}\widehat{f\circ L_{\nu^{\frac{1}{2}}}}(\tilde{g})
where
L_{t}
is left multiplication by
t
.
Note that
h(\tilde{g},\nu)
is simply

\det(g)\sum_{\lambda\in g\mathbb{Z}^{2n}_{\textrm{prim}}}f(g)\quad\textrm{ for }g\in C

in the coordinates introduced above.

This way

\int_{C}hdu=\int_{0}^{1}\nu^{n}\int\widehat{f\circ L_{\nu^{\frac{1}{2}}}}(g)d\mu(\tilde{g})\,d\nu

=\frac{1}{\zeta(2n)}\int_{0}^{1}\nu^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}f\circ L_{\nu^{\frac{1}{2}}}(x)dx\,d\nu

=\frac{1}{\zeta(2n)}\int_{0}^{1}\nu^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\nu^{-n}f(x)dx\,d\nu

=\frac{1}{\zeta(2n)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}f(x)\,dx

recovering the correct statistics when
f=\chi_{B}
.

From now on we will drop the tilde from a unimodular
g
since we will always use the coordinates introduced above for
C
and
\textrm{GSp}(2n,\mathbb{R})
in general.
For the second moment of
h
, we have

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
f
be the characteristic function of a Borel set
B
of volume
V
such that there exists
\epsilon>0
such that

(8.1)

\int_{B}\int_{B}|\langle x,y\rangle|_{+}^{-\delta}\,dx\,dy\leq CV^{2-\epsilon}.

We have the mean square bound

(8.2)

\int_{C}\left|h(g,\nu)-\frac{\int f}{\zeta(2n)}\right|^{2}\,dg\,d\nu\ll\left(\int f\right)^{2-\epsilon}.

Proof.

Following the unwrapping procedure as before, the second moment over
C
becomes

\sum_{s=-\infty}^{\infty}a(|s|)\tilde{G}(s)

where

\tilde{G}(s)=\int_{0}^{1}\nu^{2n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}f(\nu^{1/2}x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n-1}}f\left(s\nu^{1/2}y^{*}(x)+\sum_{i}t_{i}\nu^{1/2}y_{i}(x)\right)\,dt\,dx\,d\nu.

Now make the change of variables
x^{\prime}=\nu^{1/2}x
and let
y^{\prime}_{i}=\nu^{1/2}y_{i}
; note
y^{\prime*}=\nu^{-1/2}y^{*}
modulo the hyperplane
\langle x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\rangle=0
.
This way we get

\tilde{G}(s)=\int_{0}^{1}\nu^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}f(x^{\prime})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n-1}}f\left(s\nu y^{\prime*}(x^{\prime})+\sum_{i}t_{i}y^{\prime}_{i}(x^{\prime})\right)\,dt\,dx^{\prime}\,d\nu.

Taking the summation over
s
inside the integral, making the substitution
u=s\nu
and using Fubini’s theorem, we get

(8.3)

\sum_{s}a(|s|)\tilde{G}(s)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}f(x^{\prime})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\left(uy^{\prime*}(x^{\prime})+\sum_{i}t_{i}y^{\prime}_{i}(x^{\prime})\right)u^{n-1}\left(\sum_{s>|u|}\frac{a(s)}{s^{n}}\right)\,du\,dt\,dx.

An argument similar to that of Proposition
6.6
shows that

u^{n-1}\sum_{s>|u|}\frac{a(s)}{s^{n}}=\frac{1}{\zeta(2n)^{2}}+O(\min\{1,u^{-\delta}\})

and so

\displaystyle\sum_{s}a(|s|)\tilde{G}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\zeta(2n)^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}f(x^{\prime})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\left(uy^{\prime*}(x^{\prime})+\sum_{i}t_{i}y^{\prime}_{i}(x^{\prime})\right)\,du\,dt\,dx

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle O\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}f(x^{\prime})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{-\delta}f\left(uy^{\prime*}(x^{\prime})+\sum_{i}t_{i}y^{\prime}_{i}(x^{\prime})\right)\,du\,dt\,dx\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\int f}{\zeta(2n)}\right)^{2}+O\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}f(x^{\prime})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{-\delta}f\left(uy^{\prime*}(x^{\prime})+\sum_{i}t_{i}y^{\prime}_{i}(x^{\prime})\right)\,du\,dt\,dx\right).

The error term can be written

E=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|\langle x,y\rangle|_{+}^{-\delta}f(x)f(y)\,dx\,dy

which completes the proof.

∎

.

Let

Proof.

This is a straightforward consequence of the following general lemma:

Let

9.
From mean square error to metrical bounds

In this section we summarize the transition from Proposition
8.1
to the discrepancy bound of (
1.3
); the method is identical to Schmidt’s arguments in
[
8
, Lemmata 12 and 13, Theorem 1]
apart from different exponents, but since we do not aim for optimality in the bounds, our exposition will be much simpler. We will abuse notation and write
\delta,\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime},\cdots
for several different constants, all linearly related to the original ones to avoid proliferation of symbols. Finally, we assume at the onset that
B_{t}
is a continuous family of Borel sets with the hypotheses in Proposition
8.1
and such that the volumes
\textrm{vol}(B_{t})=v_{t}
take all large real values.

Theorem 9.1
.

Let
B_{t}
be a nested family of Borel sets satisfying the properties of Proposition
8.1
. Then there exists a
\delta>0
depending only on the dimension and the exponent
\epsilon
in
8.1
so that for almost all
\Lambda\in\textrm{GSp}^{+}(2n,\mathbb{R})/\textrm{GSp}^{+}(2n,\mathbb{Z})
we have

(9.1)

D(B_{t},\Lambda)\leq C(\Lambda,n,\delta)v_{t}^{-\delta}.

From the bound

\int_{C}\left(\det(g)\widehat{\chi_{B}}(g\mathbb{Z}^{2})-\frac{\textrm{vol}(B)}{\zeta(2n)}\right)^{2}d\mu(g)\leq C\textrm{vol}(B)^{2-2\delta}

we get via Chebychev’s inequality

(9.2)

\mu\left(\Lambda\in C:\left|\det(\Lambda)\cdot(\#\Lambda_{\textrm{prim}}\cap B)-\frac{\textrm{vol}(B)}{\zeta(2n)}\right|>k\textrm{vol}(B)^{1-\delta}\right)\leq\frac{1}{k^{2}}

which we rearrange as

(9.3)

\mu\left(\Lambda\in C:\left|\frac{(\#\Lambda_{\textrm{prim}}\cap B)}{\textrm{vol}(B)}-\frac{1}{\det(\Lambda)\zeta(2n)}\right|>k\textrm{vol}(B)^{-\delta}\right)\leq\det(\Lambda)^{2}\frac{1}{k^{2}};

with
k=\textrm{vol}(B)^{\frac{\delta}{2}}
and the definition of the discrepancy function, we get

(9.4)

\mu\left(\Lambda\in C:D(B,\Lambda)>\textrm{vol}(B)^{-\delta/2}\right)\leq\det(\Lambda)^{2}\frac{1}{\textrm{vol}(B)^{\delta}}.

Now consider the family
B_{t}
and let
l\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
large. Choose a subsequence
B_{m(l)}=B^{\prime}_{l}
of volumes
v(l)=l^{\frac{1+\rho}{\delta}}
for any fixed
\rho
with
0<\rho<1
.

Then by Borel-Cantelli we see that for almost all lattices
\Lambda\in C
, we have

(9.5)

D(B^{\prime}_{l},\Lambda)\leq C(\Lambda,n)\textrm{vol}(B^{\prime}_{l})^{-\delta/2}

for some constant
C(\Lambda,n)
depending only on the (covolume of) the lattice and the dimension.

For the remaining elements
B_{t}
in the sequence, choose
l
such that

l^{\frac{1+\rho}{\delta}}\leq v_{t}\leq(l+1)^{\frac{1+\rho}{\delta}}

and use the inclusions to interpolate the discrepancy of
B_{t}
between the two discrepancies
D(B^{\prime}_{l},\Lambda)
and
D(B^{\prime}_{l+1},\Lambda)
:

\displaystyle D(B_{t},\Lambda)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max(\left(\frac{l+1}{l}\right)^{\frac{1+\rho}{\delta}}\left[D(B^{\prime}_{l},\Lambda)+\left(\left(\frac{l}{1+l}\right)^{\frac{1+\rho}{\delta}}-1\right)\right],

\displaystyle\left(\frac{l+1}{l}\right)^{\frac{1+\rho}{\delta}}\left[D(B^{\prime}_{l+1},\Lambda)+\left(\left(\frac{l+1}{l}\right)^{\frac{1+\rho}{\delta}}-1\right)\right]).

Note that the coefficients are bounded by
2^{\frac{1+\rho}{\delta}}
and the additive error is of the order
l^{-1}
; since
\rho<1
, we see
l^{\frac{2}{\delta}}>l^{\frac{1+\rho}{\delta}}=\textrm{vol}(B^{\prime}_{l})
so

l^{-1}=(l^{\frac{2}{\delta}})^{-\delta/2}<\textrm{vol}(B^{\prime}_{l})^{\frac{-\delta}{2}}

giving

(9.6)

D(B_{t},\Lambda)\leq 2^{\frac{1+\rho}{\delta}+1}\textrm{vol}(B^{\prime}_{l})^{-\delta/2}\leq C(\Lambda,n,\delta)v_{t}^{-\delta/2}.

as
t\to\infty
.

Finally, consider the cone
C_{L}
of lattices
\Lambda\in\textrm{GSp}(2n,\mathbb{R})/\textrm{GSp}(2n,\mathbb{Z})
with
0<\det(\Lambda)\leq L
for
L\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
\frac{1}{L^{1/n}}\Lambda
lies in the unit cone; applying the results above to the dilated family
L^{-\frac{1}{n}}B_{t}
which again satisfies all the hypotheses as the original
B_{t}
we get that for almost every
\Lambda\in C_{L}
we have the same discrepancy bounds as before (the
\delta
does not change); taking the intersection of these countable co-null sets in
C_{L}
for
L=1,2,\cdots
we see that for almost every lattice in
\textrm{GSp}^{+}(2n,\mathbb{R})/\textrm{GSp}(2n,\mathbb{Z})
we have
D(B_{t},\Lambda)=O(\textrm{vol}(B_{t})^{-\delta})
for some
\delta>0
.
