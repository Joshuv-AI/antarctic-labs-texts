The Fisher Geometry and Geodesics of the Multivariate Normals, without Differential Geometry
Brodie A. J. Lawson, Kevin Burrage, Kerrie Mengersen, Rodrigo Weber dos Santos

Abstract
Choosing the Fisher information as the metric tensor for a Riemannian manifold provides a powerful yet fundamental way to understand statistical distribution families. Distances along this manifold become a compelling measure of statistical distance, and paths of shorter distance improve sampling techniques that leverage a sequence of distributions in their operation. Unfortunately, even for a distribution as generally tractable as the multivariate normal distribution, this information geometry proves unwieldy enough that closed-form solutions for shortest-distance paths or their lengths remain unavailable outside of limited special cases. In this review we present for general statisticians the most practical aspects of the Fisher geometry for this fundamental distribution family. Rather than a differential geometric treatment, we use an intuitive understanding of the covariance-induced curvature of this manifold to unify the special cases with known closed-form solution and review approximate solutions for the general case. We also use the multivariate normal information geometry to better understand the paths or distances commonly used in statistics (annealing, Wasserstein). Given the unavailability of a general solution, we also discuss the methods used for numerically obtaining geodesics in the space of multivariate normals, identifying remaining challenges and suggesting methodological improvements.



Keywords:
Information geometry, Information theory, Statistical distance

1
Introduction

The concept of
statistical distance
, some measure of discrepancy between two distributions, is ubiquitous in statistics. Classical tests such as the Cramér–von Mises criterion or Anderson–Darling test use a distance between (cumulative) density functions to identify whether given data is expected to have come from a given distribution
[
1
]
. The expectation maximisation algorithm, and variational approaches to Bayesian inference, operate by minimising an information theoretic discrepancy measure — the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence
[
2
]
. The Wasserstein distance (also known as the Kantrovich–Rubenstein metric or earth-mover’s distance) describes in a sense the amount of work required to physically reshape one distribution into another, a concept that brings with it both appealing mathematics
[
3
]
, and demonstrated potential in statistics and machine learning
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
. These are only a few examples of the many statistical discrepancy measures that see use (for example see also
[
7
,
8
]
).

Spoiled for choice in such a manner, it is often desirable to select a discrepancy that is mathematically specified by a distance metric, such that we may understand our measure of statistical distance through analogs to the familiar Euclidean distance (satisfying key properties such as symmetry, positivity, and the triangle inequality). If we also choose this metric such that our notion of distance is tied to statistical information, then lengths of paths through distribution space become measures of the information required to traverse that path, and shortest-distance paths become “paths of least statistical resistance”. This is of practical concern, as can be seen within sampling approaches using distribution sequences such as sequential Monte Carlo
[
9
]
or parallel tempering
[
10
]
, as well as normalising constant (ratio) estimators such as annealed importance sampling
[
11
,
12
]
.

The Fisher information matrix serves as just such a (Riemannian) metric. Use of this metric began with Rao in 1945, who wile presenting the well-known Cramér–Rao bound in statistical estimation also discussed the idea of members of a distribution family sitting on what is now known as a “statistical manifold”
[
13
]
. The geometry of this Riemannian manifold is warped by the information already present in the distribution, in a fashion similar to the way Riemannian manifolds in astrophysics describe the gravitational warping of space-time
[
14
, Ch. 10]
. Especially after their modernisation by
Shun’ichi 1985
, these ideas of
information geometry
have often concentrated on the theoretical properties of the manifolds associated with statistical models or distribution families
[
16
]
, but have also seen use in generating new statistical algorithms
[
17
]
, explaining convergence behaviour
[
18
]
and making learning algorithms parameterisation-invariant
[
19
]
. As a means of quantifying statistical distance, information geometry has a natural application to hypothesis testing
[
20
]
, and is also intrinsically linked to the question of identifiability of parameters in models of complex systems
[
21
,
22
]
.

For a given family of distributions, information geometry provides an information-aware measure of distance termed the Fisher–Rao distance (or sometimes the Rao distance) that behaves like a distance, unlike say the KL divergence
[
8
]
. However, owing to the way Riemannian geometry defines distance in an infinitesimal fashion, finding the Fisher–Rao distance between two points on the statistical manifold requires first finding the shortest-distance path along the manifold between those two points. Known as geodesics, such paths generalise straight lines to a non-Euclidean geometry, but are challenging to generate. This, together with the general requirement of familiarity with differential geometry, means that information geometry remains somewhat unwieldy.

In this work, we provide an introduction to, and practical understanding of, information geometry for general statisticians. We do so by avoiding as many differential geometric concepts and as much of the corresponding notation as possible, and by concentrating on a fundamental distribution family, the multivariate normals (MVNs). What may at first appear to be a simplistic choice reveals a rich topic area of ongoing research that we review, with applications for example to segmentation of diffusion tensor imaging data
[
23
]
and wireless signal processing
[
24
,
25
]
. We provide an intuitive way of understanding this statistical manifold’s geometry, and the form taken by its geodesic paths. This approach allows us to unify the existing cases for which a closed-form expression for the geodesic is known, and informs approximations for use when a closed-form solution is not available. We also discuss how other commonly-used paths through distribution space (annealing/geometric, Wasserstein-optimal transport) compare to these information-optimal paths in the space of MVNs, providing a deeper understanding of their function and expected performance.

Given the need to numerically find the geodesic in the majority of cases where a closed-form solution is unavailable, we also thoroughly discuss the shooting methods used for this purpose. As part of our review, we link recent developments in approximately-geodesic curves to shooting method performance, by demonstrating the potential of the former as an initialisation. We conclude with a discussion of how the concepts we discuss extend beyond the MVN family, and the unsolved problem of a closed form for the geodesic and Fisher–Rao distance between arbitrary MVNs.

2
The Fisher Geometry of the Normal Distribution

2.1
The Riemannian Metric

Consider a family of distributions parameterised by
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. For the MVN family we consider here, the typical choice of parameters is the mean vector,
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, and covariance matrix,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
. As such, the parameter space for
d
-variate MVNs could be taken as
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+d(d+1)/2}
, with the vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}
housing the
d
components of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and the
d(d+1)/2
unique elements of the symmetric
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
. Owing to the requirement that
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
be a valid (positive definite) covariance matrix, the parameter space for the family of
d
-variate normals in fact occupies some subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d+d(d+1)/2}
.

Asked for the distance between two points in the parameter space for
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, we could say calculate the Euclidean norm of the difference in their associated vectors,
\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}\|_{2}
. This would completely ignore the statistical information represented within
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, through its constituent components
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
and what they imply about the MVN-distributed random variables. Information geometry rectifies this, by treating the parameter space instead as a curved manifold that explicitly acknowledges this statistical context.

As stated, our goal here is to avoid a differential geometric treatment of the statistical manifold associated with MVNs, which has already been comprehensively provided
[
20
,
26
,
27
]
. However, in describing the (Riemannian) geometry of this manifold, the metric tensor is an inescapable concept. Commonly denoted
g_{ij}
, the metric tensor defines the warping of space by defining the distance
ds
that is traversed when the co-ordinates are infinitesimally incremented by some adjustment
d\boldsymbol{\theta}
,

ds^{2}=\sum_{i}\sum_{j}g_{ij}\,d\theta_{i}d\theta_{j}=d\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}\mathbf{G}d\boldsymbol{\theta},

(1)

with
\theta_{i}
denoting the
i
-th co-ordinate of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, and
\mathbf{G}
the matrix with elements
g_{ij}
. When
\mathbf{G}
is the identity matrix, we have
ds^{2}=d\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}d\boldsymbol{\theta}=\|d\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}
and so recover the familiar Euclidean distance for a non-warped space. On the other hand, where
\mathbf{G}
is itself a function of the parameters, as it is for the MVN statistical manifold, the distance associated with
d\boldsymbol{\theta}
depends also on the current position on this manifold.

Information geometry typically imposes this curvature on the parameters of a family of distributions by choosing
\mathbf{G}
to be the Fisher information matrix associated with that distribution family
[
15
]
. For a family with density functions
f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})
, the Fisher information matrix
{\cal I}
has elements defined by

{\cal I}_{ij}=E_{\mathbf{X}}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{i}}\log f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}\log f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})\right].

For
d
-variate MVNs with log-density

\log f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=-\frac{d}{2}\log 2\pi-\frac{1}{2}\log\det\boldsymbol{\Sigma}-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}),

we may show that the distance metric takes the form
[
28
]

ds^{2}=d\boldsymbol{\mu}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}d\boldsymbol{\mu}+\frac{1}{2}\tr\Bigl[(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}d\boldsymbol{\Sigma})^{2}\Bigr]=\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}d\boldsymbol{\mu}\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}d\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\right\|_{F}^{2},

(2)

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm. Equation (
2
) completely defines the geometry of the Fisher manifold for MVNs, but only for two points infinitesimally close together (separated by
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
), and it is not immediately obvious what minimum distance paths between finitely-separated points should look like. Before we discuss such paths, however, it is beneficial to gain a high-level understanding of how the warping of space defined by equation (
2
) acts.

The first thing we may observe is that for any point on the manifold
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, the distance is affected only by
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
(which appears only as the precision,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}
). This makes good statistical sense, in that different values of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
do not inherently correspond to differing amounts of information, only to information pointing to different locations in the sample space for the associated random variable. The precision matrix, however, describes how well
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is specified, and thus has direct effect on how the distribution will respond to additional information. That is, the greater the precision, the more information that is required to obtain a shift in the distribution’s parameters. This information cost is composed of wholly separate contributions due to the movement in
\boldsymbol{\mu}
-spaec and the movement in
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
-space, owing to the block-diagonal structure of the Fisher information matrix in this case
[
29
]
.

The second piece of intuition comes from the norm-based definition for
ds^{2}
in equation (
2
). Considering the property for an MVN-distributed random variable
\mathbf{x}\sim{\cal N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
that the affine-transformed variable
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{r}
is distributed
\mathbf{y}\sim{\cal N}(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbf{r},\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{P}^{T})
, we describe affine transformations of an MVN’s parameters as those of form

\boldsymbol{\mu}\mapsto\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbf{r},\qquad\qquad\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mapsto\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{P}^{T},\qquad\qquad\det(\mathbf{P})>0.

(3)

We then see through the second expression in equation (
2
) that the elements of
d\boldsymbol{\theta}=(d\boldsymbol{\mu},d\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
do in fact add as a sum of squares reminiscent of Euclidean distance, but only after the effect of the current precision is removed by applying the affine transformation
\mathbf{P}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}
,
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{0}
that achieves an identity covariance (known as a sphering or whitening transformation). Indeed, the well-known Mahalanobis distance
[
30
]
may be thought of as the Euclidean distance after application of a whitening transformation, and the first term in (
2
) is precisely the (squared) Mahalanobis distance between two means separated by
d\boldsymbol{\mu}
. This crystallises the property of the MVN statistical manifold that shifts in the mean in strongly-specified directions cost more information than shifts in weakly-specified directions.

It can also be shown via direct substitution that the metric tensor for MVNs (
2
) is invariant to further affine transformations of the form (
3
). Interpreted through the lens of the norm-based definition, this invariance may be intuitively understood as pure translations of the mean (through
\mathbf{r}
) not affecting
d\boldsymbol{\mu}
, and the sphering transformation applied in (
3
) removing any scaling effects imparted by
\mathbf{P}
(rotation may still occur). This invariance of the metric is an important property, as for a given pair of points, we may first transform these points so that one lies at the origin,
(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
, without affecting the lengths or intrinsic shapes of these paths
[
31
]
between them.

2.2
Geodesics in Multivariate Normal Distribution Space

The distance metric (
2
) directly specifies infinitesimal distances on the statistical manifold associated with MVNs. To define
finite
distances on this manifold, these infinitesimal increments must be integrated along the path of minimal distance that connects them — the geodesic. In Euclidean space, the geodesics are straight lines, and thus the Euclidean metric functions for both infinitesimal and finite distances. For MVNs, however, the geodesics are nontrivial and unavailable in closed form. When found, however, their length gives the Fisher–Rao distance between a pair of MVNs, a statistically-informed measure of their separation. For very close members of a distribution family (MVN or otherwise), half of the square of the Fisher–Rao distance approximates the KL divergence
[
32
]
. For more distant distributions, the two become distinct.

Before describing the geodesics for the space of MVNs mathematically, we present in Fig.
1
some example geodesics in the space of bivariate and trivariate normal distributions that illustrate how the metric (
2
) influences their shapes. As discussed above, only the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
contributes to the warping of space, and so we may interpret
\boldsymbol{\mu}
-space as functioning in a Euclidean fashion outside of this “external” influence. As such, where we discuss “straight” or “curved” paths in
\boldsymbol{\mu}
-space we use these terms in the Euclidean sense (in the Riemannian sense, the geodesics function as the analog of straight lines by definition). Following others
[
23
,
33
]
, we also plot bivariate and trivariate MVNs and their geodesics in this sense, as points and paths through
\boldsymbol{\mu}
-space with covariance indicated by ellipses/ellipsoids with axis lengths given by (a scaling of) the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, and orientations given by its eigenvectors.

Geodesic paths are seen to curve in
\boldsymbol{\mu}
-space in order to strike the balance between moving towards the target
\boldsymbol{\mu}
value in the Euclidean sense, and travelling along higher-variance directions (which as per equation (
2
) incur less distance). In general, the geodesics initially increase the variance in the direction of travel, easing their movement through
\boldsymbol{\mu}
-space before restoring the covariance to that of the curve’s endpoint. The endpoint’s covariance can also bias the direction in
\boldsymbol{\mu}
-space from which the geodesic approaches, providing a motivation to leave the starting point from a direction that does not immediately point at the target. Notably however, when a principal direction of variance (as defined by the covariance matrix’s eigenvectors) is aligned with the Euclidean connection between the two points in
\boldsymbol{\mu}
-space, the geodesic travels along this path. We return to this point in more detail in Section
2.3
.

Figure 1:
Example geodesics starting from the origin for bivariate (left) and trivariate (right) MVNs. Drawn paths indicate movement through
\boldsymbol{\mu}
-space, while positions in
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
-space at regular intervals along this path are indicated by (scaled) ellipses/ellipsoids. Geodesic paths curve through
\boldsymbol{\mu}
-space to optimise movement in higher-variance directions and often increase variance in the direction of travel.

Geodesics on Riemannian manifolds are found by solving the geodesic equations, which are ordinary differential equations (ODEs) defined wholly by the metric tensor. For the space of MVN distributions, these equations take the neat form
[
31
]

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}}{dt^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{d\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{dt}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\,\frac{d\boldsymbol{\mu}}{dt}

(4)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{dt^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{d\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{dt}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\frac{d\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{dt}-\left(\frac{d\boldsymbol{\mu}}{dt}\right)\left(\frac{d\boldsymbol{\mu}}{dt}\right)^{T}\!\!.

In order to simplify the geodesic equations, it is beneficial to consider geodesics originating from the origin,
(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
. Making use of the freedom to transform according to (
3
) without changing the metric, we may choose

\mathbf{P}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\mathbf{R},\qquad\mathbf{r}=\boldsymbol{\mu}

(5)

to map the origin,
(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
, to any given point
(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
. Here,
\mathbf{R}
is an orthogonal matrix
\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{T}=\mathbf{I}
that reflects the freedom to choose
\mathbf{P}
as any decomposition
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{P}^{T}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
. This is equivalent to the freedom to choose from a variety of transformation matrices that all carry out the process of data whitening
[
34
]
.

Considering (
5
), we see that the geodesic between a starting point,
(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0})
, and a target point,
(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1})
, can instead be treated as a connection between the origin and a new transformed target,

(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t})=\Bigl(\mathbf{R}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1/2}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}),\,\,\mathbf{R}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1/2}\mathbf{R}\Bigr).

(6)

The desired geodesic can then be obtained by applying the transform (
5
) to this connection according to (
3
). For a geodesic beginning at the origin, the geodesic equations (
4
) can be integrated once to become

\displaystyle\frac{d\boldsymbol{\mu}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=\left.\frac{d\boldsymbol{\mu}}{dt}\right|_{t=0}

(7)

\displaystyle\frac{d\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{x}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{T})

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}=\left.\frac{d\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{dt}\right|_{t=0}.

Several authors
[
20
,
31
,
35
]
have shown that these equations permit a closed-form solution when expressed in terms of the canonical co-ordinates
(\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\Delta})=(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1})
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}(t)

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{B}\Bigl(\cosh(t\mathbf{G})-\mathbf{I}\Bigr)(\mathbf{G}^{-})^{2}\mathbf{x}+\sinh(t\mathbf{G})\mathbf{G}^{-}\mathbf{x},

(8)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Delta}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I}+\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\cosh(t\mathbf{G})-\mathbf{I}\Bigr)+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{B}\Bigl(\cosh(t\mathbf{G})-\mathbf{I}\Bigr)(\mathbf{G}^{-})^{2}\mathbf{B}-\frac{1}{2}\sinh(t\mathbf{G})\mathbf{G}^{-}\mathbf{B}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{B}\sinh(t\mathbf{G})\mathbf{G}^{-}.

Here
\mathbf{G}^{2}=\mathbf{B}^{2}+2\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{T}
, and
\mathbf{G}^{-}
denotes the generalised inverse of
\mathbf{G}
, satisfying
\mathbf{B}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{-}=\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{-}\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{-}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{G}^{-}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}
(see
[
31
]
for details). Notably, owing to the forms of the (globally convergent) Taylor series for the hyperbolic sine and cosine functions, it can be seen that equations (
8
) calls for only even powers of
\mathbf{G}
, and so there is no degeneracy associated with knowing only
\mathbf{G}^{2}
, and not
\mathbf{G}
.

Although equations (
8
) can be evaluated directly, in our experience points along the geodesic path are best numerically evaluated using the form of the solution presented in
[
35
]
. In this form,
\boldsymbol{\delta}(t)
and
\boldsymbol{\Delta}(t)
are found by reading off a portion of the result of exponentiating a matrix of augmented dimension,

\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Delta}&\boldsymbol{\delta}&\cdot\,\,\\
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{T}&\cdot&\cdot\,\,\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\,\,\end{pmatrix}=\exp\left(t\begin{pmatrix}-\mathbf{B}&\mathbf{x}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{x}^{T}&0&-\mathbf{x}^{T}\\
\mathbf{0}&-\mathbf{x}&\mathbf{B}\end{pmatrix}\right).

(9)

Here elements of the matrix marked with
\cdot
are unused.

By observing that the velocity along geodesic curves is constant, the length of these curves over the segment
t\in[0,1]
(the Fisher–Rao distance between the distributions at their start and endpoints) can be calculated by evaluating the velocity at the origin according to the metric (
2
), giving
[
33
]

d_{F}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\tr(\mathbf{B}^{2})+\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{x}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\tr(\mathbf{G}^{2})}\,.

(10)

Given a geodesic’s initial velocity
\mathbf{v}=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{B})
, we thereby have closed-form expressions for both the path the geodesic takes (
8
), and the Fisher–Rao distance to the point at
t=1
(
10
). However, as yet there is no known analytic solution to the problem of choosing
\mathbf{v}
so as to produce a geodesic passing through a given arbitrary target point. Outside of the special cases discussed subsequently, geodesics between arbitrary points in the MVN space can be found using a specially-tailored shooting method
[
23
]
, as we discuss in Section
4
. If only the Fisher–Rao distance between a pair of MVNs is required, closed-form bounds may suffice
[
27
,
36
]
.

2.3
Special Cases with Known Solution

2.3.1
Regions in
\boldsymbol{\mu}
-space Admitting Closed-Form Solution

For several special cases, the velocity of the geodesic connecting a pair of MVNs is known, as summarised by
Pinele et al. 2020
and also
Nielsen 2023
using the lens of totally geodesic submanifolds. Also relevant is the work of
Herntier and Peter 2022
, who consider solutions for closest points on submanifolds (and geodesic paths to them), given some starting point on the full statistical manifold for bivariate MVNs. Here, we present the cases with known closed-form solution without any further invocation of differential geometry.

As equation (
8
) gives the form of geodesics that begin at the origin, we discuss the cases with known solution in the context of having already been affine transformed so that they begin at the origin. That is, rather than the problem of finding the geodesic connecting two MVNs
(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0})
and
(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1})
we consider the analogous problem of finding the geodesic connecting the pair
(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
and
(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t})
, with the latter given by equation (
6
). The desired initial velocity in this transformed space is
\mathbf{v}=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{B})
, and when it is obtained, the velocity of the geodesic connecting the actual targets is given by undoing the transform,

\left.\dot{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\right|_{t=0}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{1/2}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x},\qquad\qquad\left.\dot{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\right|_{t=0}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{1/2}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{R}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{1/2}.

Owing to the invariance of the metric to these affine transforms, the Fisher–Rao distance between the original pair and the transformed pair remains the same.

As the metric (

Aligning the covariance eigenvectors with the direction of movement thus becomes a way to potentially decrease overall information cost, by providing the ability to increase variance only in the direction where this will be useful. \boldsymbol{\mu}

3
Non-Geodesic Paths

3.1
Commonly-Used Paths

Given the observation that paths with low information cost improve samplers using distribution sequences
[
9
,
10
]
, we summarise here how commonly-used paths in such settings function in terms of information cost, using our distribution family of MVNs as an accessible way to understand how these different choices of paths behave. Some of these paths along the MVN manifold were discussed in a very recent publication
[
27
]
, however in keeping with the character of this review we concentrate on practical performance and an interpretation external to information geometry. For notational consistency with the geodesic paths already discussed, we parameterise these paths using
t\in[0,1]
, with
t=0
corresponding to the starting point
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}=(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0})
and
t=1
to the endpoint,
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}=(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1})
.

A predominant choice in a variety of contexts is the annealing path (e.g.
[
37
,
38
]
), which takes the form

p(t;\mathbf{x})=\bigl[p_{0}(\mathbf{x})\bigr]^{1-t}\,\bigl[p_{1}(\mathbf{x})\bigr]^{t},\qquad\quad\mbox{equivalently}\quad\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{\delta}(t)&=(1-t)\boldsymbol{\delta}_{0}+t\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1}\\
\boldsymbol{\Delta}(t)&=(1-t)\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{0}+t\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{1}.\end{aligned}

This path represents a linear interpolation between the log densities associated with the two distributions, and as MVNs are a member of the exponential family, the annealing path also corresponds to a linear interpolation in terms of their natural parameters
[
12
]
, and equivalently,
\boldsymbol{\delta}
and
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
. Observing that the geodesic paths (
8
) are non-linear in these canonical co-ordinates immediately demonstrates that annealing paths are non-geodesic.

A second choice of path for distributions in a single exponential family is the moment-averaged path, which can offer significantly improved performance in annealed importance sampling
[
12
]
. For MVNs, this path takes the form

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}(t)

\displaystyle=(1-t)\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}+t\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}

(12)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(t)

\displaystyle=(1-t)\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}+t\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}+\frac{1}{4}t(1-t)(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0})(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0})^{T}.

These paths move in Euclidean fashion through
\boldsymbol{\mu}
-space, which immediately renders them non-geodesic. However, these paths hold the attractive property that this movement in
\boldsymbol{\mu}
-space is eased by the temporary injection of variance in that direction, mimicking one of the key behaviours of geodesic paths.

A somewhat separate type of path are those defined by the Wasserstein distance. The Wasserstein distance is formally a distance with an associated metric
[
6
]
, albeit one of different form to that of the Fisher metric. Informally, the Wasserstein distance measures the amount of physical work required to move the probability mass of one density function, in the fashion that minimises the work performed, so that it becomes the density function of another. Paths that are geodesic with respect to the Wasserstein metric are not in general geodesic with respect to the Fisher metric, but are interesting to consider given the use of Wasserstein distance as a statistical distance measure
[
4
]
and in averaging distributions via the Fréchet mean
[
39
]
.

In the space of MVNs, the 2-Wasserstein distance is typically used as it provides a closed form for the geodesic path (and distance) between two MVNs
[
40
]
. This path takes the form

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}(t)

\displaystyle=(1-t)\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}+t\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}

(13)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(t)

\displaystyle=\Bigl((1-t)\mathbf{I}+t\mathbf{C}\Bigr)\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}\Bigl((1-t)\mathbf{I}+t\mathbf{C}\Bigr),\qquad\mathbf{C}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}^{1/2}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}^{1/2}\right)^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}^{1/2}.

Paths minimising the 2-Wasserstein distance also move in Euclidean fashion through
\boldsymbol{\mu}
-space, but without any explicit injection of variance — equations (
13
) may be interpreted as applying a linearly-varying affine transformation to the starting point of the path, starting with the identity transformation and ending with an affine transformation that maps
(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0})
to
(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1})
. As such, the Wasserstein metric does not directly acknowledge the information about the random variable contained in the distribution
[
41
]
, and indeed for the univariate case equations (
13
) reduce to simply a linear interpolation of mean and standard deviation,
\sqrt{\Sigma(t)}=(1-t)\sqrt{\Sigma_{0}}+t\sqrt{\Sigma_{1}}

[
3
]
. Despite this information-unaware property, the Wasserstein distance has numerous applications in statistics, many of which are reviewed by
Panaretos and Zemel 2019
. It is also well suited to comparing distributions with disjoint support, in contrast to information theoretic approaches
[
41
]
.

In Fig.
4
a, we visualise these different choices of closed-form path for a few examples in the space of bivariate MVNs. We immediately see that 2-Wasserstein-optimal paths move in direct fashion through the space, and are perhaps best interpreted as optimal
absent
the informational context. Annealing paths strongly favour travelling the directions of high variance at the start and end point, as they do not inject additional variance. These paths curve too much in
\boldsymbol{\mu}
-space relative to the geodesic path and hence become information-inefficient. Conversely, moment-averaged paths inject additional variance to ease movement through
\boldsymbol{\mu}
-space, but do not curve this movement to take advantage of the variance at the path’s endpoints.

As observed by
Grosse et al. 2013
, the behaviours of annealing and moment-averaged paths can be understood through a variational lens, as the former can be shown to be reverse-KL optimal (mode-seeking, avoiding additional variance) and the latter forwards-KL optimal (mean-seeking, adding additional variance).
Nielsen 2023
demonstrates the potential of paths formed by combining these two paths, trading off their respective strengths and weaknesses.

3.2
MVN-Specific Approximations

Ideas that approximate the geodesic path, either by approximating the correct choice of
\mathbf{v}
to reach a given target or by generating paths that reach the target and are close to geodesic, are useful both for approximating or bounding the Fisher–Rao distance

(14)

4
Shooting to Find Geodesic Paths

4.1
Fundamental Concepts

Outside the cases listed in Table
1
, the choice of
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{B}
in equation (
8
) that produce the geodesic that reaches a given target point remains unknown. Instead, geodesics between arbitrary points in MVN space are produced numerically, via shooting
[
23
]
. Shooting is the process of taking an initial guess for the velocity
\mathbf{v}=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{B})
, firing out the geodesic with this velocity, and then adjusting this velocity according to where the geodesic ray ends up relative to the target point. These adjustments are made leveraging the known geometric properties of the space. Convergence of shooting is nontrivial, and
Han and Park 2014
suggest that for manifold distances greater than about seven the algorithm demonstrates issues with both stability and convergence rate. For these more challenging problems, they propose an alternative shooting via path refinement (Section
4.3
).

Here, we first review the fundamental pieces of the algorithm of
Han and Park 2014
to complete this work’s differential geometry-free treatment of these statistical manifolds. Then, in Section
4.2
, we demonstrate the use of MVN-specific approximations (Section
3.2
) inside the shooting process.

After shifting a problem to the origin using equation (
5
), we can obtain the geodesic associated with a given initial velocity using equation (
9
). To formulate some correction to this velocity, we require a “residual” vector that compares the geodesic’s endpoint and the target point. This vector becomes an update to the geodesic’s initial velocity via the differential geometric process known as parallel transport, which moves a vector along a Riemannian manifold whilst transforming it so as to keep it pointing in the same direction relative to the manifold.

The parallel transport equations for the case of MVNs are given in
Han and Park 2014
, and for geodesics beginning at the origin they take the form

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\left(\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{x}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{T}\right)\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\,\mathbf{x}

(19)

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\left(\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{x}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{T}\right)\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\left(\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{x}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{T}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{x}\,\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\,\mathbf{x}^{T}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}.

Integrating equations (
19
) backwards along a geodesic (that is, from
t=1
to
t=0
), we can thus shift the residual vector back to the start of the geodesic, where it can be used to update our initial velocity guess. Integration of these equations is achieved numerically, with the values of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
at each timepoint given by (
8
).

The issue is, for a non-Euclidean manifold the traditional concept of a residual vector is unavailable.
Han and Park 2014
use the Euclidean residual as a “good enough” choice in their shooting approach, reducing the magnitude of resulting velocity updates when the magnitude of this vector grows too large and the resulting approximation unsatisfactory. Observing that the residual vector in Euclidean space is also the velocity of the geodesic connecting the two points (a straight line), here we also consider the approximate velocities in Section
3.2
as alternative choices of residual vector (Section
4.2
). Note, however, that even if the exact geodesic velocity is available, this does not produce a perfect update.

In order to decide an appropriate step length for a velocity update, shooting makes use of the Riemannian geometry concept known as the Jacobi field. The Jacobi field describes the difference between infinitesimally close geodesics, and hence in a fashion analogous to the derivative or Jacobian in other contexts, can be used to obtain the linearised sensitivity of a geodesic to some perturbation. To explicitly define the Jacobi field and the numerical shooting algorithms, we first introduce the compact notation
{\cal G}(t\mathbf{v})
to denote the action of tracing out a geodesic through MVN space from the origin
(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
with initial velocity
\mathbf{v}=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{B})
out to distance
t
. This function
{\cal G}(t\mathbf{v})
is given by either of equations (
8
) or (
9
). The problem of finding the geodesic between two points in MVN space may thus be expressed as seeking an initial velocity
\mathbf{v}
such that

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}={\cal G}(\mathbf{v}),\qquad\qquad\mbox{or equivalently,}\quad\mathbf{v}={\cal G}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}),

where again
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}=(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t})
is the target point after shifting to the origin. We note that in differential geometry,
{\cal G}(\mathbf{v})
and
{\cal G}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
are often described as the exponential map,
\exp_{(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})}(\mathbf{v})
, and the logarithmic map,
\log_{(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, respectively.

Using this notation, the Jacobi field associated with a given velocity update
\Delta\mathbf{v}
is given by

{\cal J}(t)=\left.\frac{d}{d\tau}{\cal G}\Bigl(t(\mathbf{v}+\tau\Delta\mathbf{v})\Bigr)\right|_{\tau=0}=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{{\cal G}\Bigl(t(\mathbf{v}+\epsilon\Delta\mathbf{v})\Bigr)-{\cal G}(t\mathbf{v})}{\epsilon}.

(20)

Estimating
{\cal J}(1)
using finite differencing, we obtain the linearised effect of a velocity update,
\Delta\mathbf{v}
, on the end point of the geodesic,

{\cal J}(1)\approx\frac{{\cal G}(\mathbf{v}+s\Delta\mathbf{v})-{\cal G}(\mathbf{v})}{s}.

(21)

Seeking a scalar stepsize
s
such that
{\cal G}(\mathbf{v}+s\Delta v)\approx\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
, we can substitute this relation into equation (
21
) and then scalarise it by taking an inner product of both sides,

s\approx\frac{\Bigl\langle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}-{\cal G}(\mathbf{v}),{\cal J}(1)\Bigr\rangle_{{\cal G}(\mathbf{v})}}{\Bigl\langle{\cal J}(1),{\cal J}(1)\Bigr\rangle_{{\cal G}(\mathbf{v})}}.

(22)

Equation (
22
) mirrors the choice of
Han and Park 2014
to use the inner product with the Jacobi field (projecting both sides of the equation onto one side). The inner product used is the one that induces the metric (
2
), evaluated at the geodesic endpoint. Just like the metric, this inner product depends also on the location on the manifold
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, and for two vectors
\mathbf{u}=(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
and
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}=(\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
takes the form

\Bigl\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\Bigr\rangle_{\mathbf{p}}=\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}+\frac{1}{2}\tr\Bigl(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\Bigr).

(23)

With all of these pieces in place, shooting can proceed by repeatedly iterating the process of choosing a vector connecting the current geodesic endpoint to the actual target, parallel transporting this vector to the origin via backwards integration of equations (
19
), and then choosing a stepsize for this velocity update via equation (
22
) to obtain a new geodesic. Termination can be triggered using the norm of residual vectors, or the symmeterised Kullback–Leibler divergence as an easily-calculated proxy for squared manifold distance,

D_{\mathrm{sKL}}=\frac{1}{4}\Bigl(\tr\bigl(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}-2\mathbf{I}\bigr)+(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0})^{T}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1}\bigr)(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0})\Bigr)

Two iterations of the algorithm are demonstrated graphically in Fig.
5
, and algorithm pseudocode is given in Algorithm
S1
in the supplement.

Initialisation using the projection-based approximation remains beneficial for the target distances considered here (

4.3
Shooting via Path Refinement

Addressing the issues with convergence of shooting for pairs of MVNs separated by larger Fisher–Rao distances,
Han and Park 2014
also proposed a method in which a set of intermediate points between the start point and target are refined until all lie along the desired geodesic path. This is achieved by using the shooting described in Section
4
to find geodesic connections between all of the even-numbered points and all of the odd-numbered points, and using the midpoints of these geodesics to update the locations of the opposite set of points. Iterating this process causes the path defined by these points to settle onto the geodesic (see Fig.
7
, and Algorithm
S2
in the supplementary material), and each individual set (odd or even) may be run in parallel. Once the locations of the set of points have settled onto the geodesic, the stored initial velocity for the geodesic between the first and third points can be scaled according to the number of points to obtain the desired velocity. The Fisher–Rao distance is given simply by totalling the lengths of each individual geodesic segment.

per iteration

5
Conclusions

Although they are among the most mathematically tractable of distributional families, MVNs have continued to defy a complete information geometric treatment. Here we have presented a more pragmatic understanding of how the paths of shortest distance along this manifold behave, and how this may inform efforts to determine such a path between two given MVNs. Finding these paths, or their length (the Fisher–Rao distance), is the primary way in which such information geometry is practically applied
[
9
,
23
,
24
,
25
]
.
Despite the non-trivial Riemannian geometry, we have demonstrated how these geodesics permit a more intuitive, traditional geometric understanding that explains when geodesics do or not travel straight lines in
\boldsymbol{\mu}
-space (and hence, when a closed-form solution is available). This geometric understanding highlights the importance of repeated eigenvalues of the transformed target covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1/2}
, which correspond to more expansive subspaces of points in
\boldsymbol{\mu}
-space that can be reached via such geodesics.

Treating the distance along the statistical manifold associated with MVNs as an information distance also provides a powerful means of interpreting commonly-used paths in sequential or tempered sampling methods. Annealing paths select intelligent, variance-informed paths along which to move the location of a distribution, but fail to incorporate additional variance to reduce the cost of this movement. Moment-averaged paths incorporate additional variance to compensate for the naive path of movement for their location, mimicking a feature of geodesic paths and hence explaining their generally lower information cost. Wasserstein-optimal paths completely ignore the informational context.

Without a closed-form solution available, geodesics on the MVN statistical manifold are found via a numerical shooting process. Here we have thoroughly reviewed this process and examined the concerns associated with its practical usage. In particular, we have highlighted the use of geodesic velocity approximations to accelerate shooting, as well as issues with convergence inherent to the shooting via path refinement proposed by
Han and Park 2014
and the use of path point culling to resolve them. Considering path information costs also provides good initialisations for the path refinement process.

This work has concentrated on the information geometry of MVNs, acknowledging their ubiquity in statistics and position as a comparatively tractable family that still demonstrates the additional geometric complexities that come with vector-valued random variables. However, many of the ideas we have summarised and discussed here are extensible to other distributional families and contexts. The intuition of temporarily increasing distribution scale in order to lower the information cost associated with changes in location should be applicable to many other distributions, and indeed (univariate) Cauchy and Student’s
t
distributions possess a Fisher geometry very similar to univariate normal distributions
[
42
]
. The moment-averaged paths that also capture this desirable property are well-defined (though not always in closed form) for any exponential family distribution
[
12
]
. The MVN-manifold geodesic connecting the Laplace approximations
[
43
]
for a pair of distributions might also be used to inform information-efficient paths between the distributions themselves.

A closed-form solution for the geodesic connecting two members of the MVN family has continued to elude researchers in information geometry despite sophisticated treatments of the topic via differential geometry and Lie theory
[
20
,
31
]
. By presenting here the information geometry of MVNs with only minimal use of differential geometry, our hope is that these fascinating concepts might become more broadly considered, and that this might increase the opportunity for these remaining questions in the space of MVNs to be solved. Until a closed-form solution for the geodesic (and Fisher–Rao distance) between an arbitrary pair of MVNs is found, the numerical approaches we have reviewed and extended here remain necessary.

For a geodesic beginning at the origin, the geodesic equations for multivariate normals take the form (

S.3
A Velocity Approximation via Higher-dimensional Manifold Embedding

The approximately geodesic paths found by embedding the MVN statistical manifold in the space of symmetric positive definite matrices also produce a velocity approximation, simply by taking the initial velocity of these paths. As discussed in the main document, these paths take the form

\mathbf{p}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}^{T}&\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}^{T}&1\end{bmatrix}^{t}.

We first seek

\frac{d}{dt}\log\mathbf{p}(t)=\log\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}^{T}&\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}\\

\mathbf{p}^{\prime}(0)=\log\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}^{T}&\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}^{T}&1\end{bmatrix}.

Now we take the derivative of the embedding of an MVN path
