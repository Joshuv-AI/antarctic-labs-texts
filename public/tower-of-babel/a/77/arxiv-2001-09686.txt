Solving Maxwell’s Eigenvalue Problem via Isogeometric Boundary Elements and a Contour Integral Method Journal: arXiv
Stefan Kurz, Sebastian Schöps, Gerhard Unger, Felix Wolf

Abstract
We solve Maxwell’s eigenvalue problem via isogeometric boundary elements and a contour integral method. We discuss the analytic properties of the discretisation, outline the implementation, and showcase numerical examples.



2010 MSC

34L16, 35P30, 65N38, 65D07

1
Motivation

The development and construction of particle accelerators is arguably one of the most time and money consuming research projects in modern experimental physics.
A cause for this is that essential components are not available off the shelf and must be manufactured uniquely tailored to the design specification of the planned accelerator.

One of the most performance critical components are so-called
cavities
, resonators often made out of superconducting materials in which electromagnetic fields oscillate at radio frequencies. The resonant fields are then used to accelerate bunches of particles up to speeds close to the speed of light.
The geometry, and consequently the resonance behaviour of these structures is vital to the overall performance of the accelerator as a whole.

Due to the expensive (e.g. superconducting) materials and vast amounts of manual labor that are needed in the manufacturing of these devices, the design of cavities has become its own area of research, cf.
[
1
]
and the sources cited therein.
Consequently, the development of simulation tools specifically for this purpose became an important part of the related scientific advancement, see e.g.
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
.

Figure 1:
For a finite element computation a mesh is generated from the boundary data available from the design framework. Afterwards, a volume mesh is created. This introduces geometrical errors and limits the obtainable order of convergence to that of the geometry representation. Graphics from
[
5
]
.

A bottleneck with these classical approaches has always been the representation of the geometry, which often limits the achievable accuracies, cf. Figure
1
. However, high accuracies are desired such that the initial design and its simulation are not the weakest link within the manufacturing pipeline.
As an example, manufacturers alone are interested in the simulation of deformation effects, the so-called
Lorentz detuning
, which are dependent on a relative error margin of roughly
10^{-7}
,
[
1
, Tab. II]
. There are also other, more advanced applications which have such high demands on accuracy. One is presented by Georg et al.
[
6
]
, who show that even higher accuracies than those already achievable are required to simulate eccentricities.

Introduction

Nowadays, isogeometric analysis
[
7
]
has been established as the method of choice when dealing with such high demands on accuracy w.r.t. geometry representation. Isogeometric methods are well understood for the case of electromagnetism
[
8
,
9
]
and a corresponding finite element approach has already been applied to Maxwell’s eigenvalue problem
[
10
]
.

Figure 2:
Isogeometric boundary element methods enable the computation directly on the CAD representation. Graphics from
[
5
]
.

The boundary-only representations in modern CAD frameworks, as well as the demand for highly accurate simulation techniques, suggests the transition to isogeometric boundary element methods for these types of problems, cf. Figure
2
.
While the use of boundary element methods promises to both reduce the number of degrees of freedom w.r.t. the element size
h
drastically, and at the same time double the rate of convergence for the point evaluation of the solution in the domain
[
11
, Cor. 3.11 & Rem. 3.12]
,
the system matrices become densely pouplated and the corresponding eigenvalue problem becomes non-linear. However, through the recent introduction of a new family of eigenvalue solvers known as
contour integral methods

[
12
,
13
]
, this problem was mitigated.

A first approach to a boundary element eigenvalue problem via the contour integral method
was investigated in
[
14
]
, however, neither with a higher-order approach, a discussion of the related convergence theory, or within the isogeometric setting. Moreover, a comparison of volume-based and boundary element methods has not been discussed within the literature.

This article is build on top of the recent mathematical results of
[
11
,
12
,
15
,
16
]
and aims at advancing the solution of eigenvalue problems via boundary elements by discussing the convergence analysis of the isogeometric discretisation of the eigenvalue problem. In this, we prove that a convergence order
{\mathcal{O}}(h^{2p+1})
for the eigenvalue aproximation can be achieved for discretisations with mesh-size
h
and ansatz functions of order
p
. For a corresponding volume-based IGA only a convergence order
{\mathcal{O}}(h^{2p})
can be expected
[
17
]
.

The organisation of this document is straight forward. Section
3
will introduce the cavity problem based on Maxwell’s equations. Afterwards, Section
4
will show how this problem can be rephrased as a boundary integral equation, the well-known
electric field integral equation
, and we will discuss the equivalence of both formulations.
We will then discuss our discretisation scheme, where we will review our isogeometric discretisation of the boundary integral equation in Section
5
, making theoretical predictions regarding the convergence behaviour. Our approach to the arising non-linear eigenvalue problem will then be discussed in Section
6
.
In Section
7
a selection of numerical examples will be presented, and finally we will briefly conclude our findings in Section
8
.

3
The Eigenvalue Problem

For a compact and simply connected Lipschitz domain
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
the electromagnetic fields in the source-free case are governed by the equations

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{curl}}}{}{(\mathbf{E})}

\displaystyle=-i\omega\mu_{0}\mathbf{H}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega

(1)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{curl}}}{}{(\mathbf{H})}

\displaystyle=i\omega\varepsilon_{0}\mathbf{E}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}{}{(\varepsilon_{0}\mathbf{E})}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}{}{(\mu_{0}\mathbf{H})}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

assuming the time-harmonic case.
As usual,
\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{H}
denote the electric and magnetic field
[
18
]
, respectively.
In the case of the cavity problem, the electric permittivity
\varepsilon_{0}

and the magnetic permeability
\mu_{0}
are those of vacuum.
Moreover, since superconducting alloys can be modelled as perfect electric conducting, we assume the boundary conditions on
\Gamma\coloneqq\partial\Omega
to be given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega

(2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}\cdot\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega.

By eliminating
\mathbf{H}
from (
1
) one can then derive the classical cavity problem
[
19
]
.

Problem 1

(Cavity problem)
.

Find the wave number
k:=\omega\sqrt{\mu_{0}\epsilon_{0}}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbf{E}\neq 0
such that

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{curl}}}{}{\bigl({\boldsymbol{\operatorname{curl}}}{}{(\mathbf{E})}\bigr)}

\displaystyle=k^{2}\mathbf{E}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega

(3)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}{}{(\mathbf{E})}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma.

4
The Electric Field Integral Equation

Before we introduce the variational formulation of Maxwell’s eigenvalue problem, we need to introduce some notation.

4.1
Function Spaces Related to Maxwell’s Equations and the EFIE

Let
\Omega
be a Lipschitz domain.
By
H^{0}(\Omega)
we denote the usual square integral functions
L^{2}(\Omega)
, and we write
H^{s}(\Omega)
for the usual Sobolev spaces of higher regularity
s>0
, cf.
[
20
]
. Their vector-valued counterparts
\boldsymbol{H}^{s}(\Omega)=\big(H^{s}(\Omega)\big){}^{3}
are denoted by bold letters. We now define the spaces

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}^{s}({\boldsymbol{\operatorname{curl}}}{},\Omega){}

\displaystyle{}=\{\boldsymbol{f}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{s}\colon{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{curl}}}{}\boldsymbol{f}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{s}(\Omega)\},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}^{s}({\boldsymbol{\operatorname{curl}}}{}^{2},\Omega){}

\displaystyle{}=\{\boldsymbol{f}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{s}({\boldsymbol{\operatorname{curl}}}{},\Omega)\colon{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{curl}}}{}\,{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{curl}}}{}\boldsymbol{f}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{s}(\Omega)\},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}^{s}(\operatorname{div}{},\Omega){}

\displaystyle{}=\{\boldsymbol{f}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{s}\colon\operatorname{div}{}\boldsymbol{f}\in H^{s}(\Omega)\},

dropping the index
s
in the case of
s=0
for convenience.
For
\mathbf{n}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}
denoting the outwards directed unit normal at
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\Gamma
, the
rotated tangential trace

\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\mathrm{t}}(\boldsymbol{f})\coloneqq\lim_{\boldsymbol{x}\to\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\times\mathbf{n}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}

is well defined for smooth
\boldsymbol{f}.
Note that
\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}
is well-defined for almost all
\boldsymbol{x}_{0},
cf.
[
20
, p. 96]
. We will equip the rotated tangential trace with the superscript
int
if the limit is taken from within
\Omega
, and with the superscipt
ext
if taken from
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega},
omitting the notation if the mapping properties are clearly stated.
The operator
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\mathrm{t}}
is extended to a weak setting through density arguments.
We can now define

\boldsymbol{H}_{\times}^{-1/2}(\operatorname{div}{}_{\Gamma},\Gamma)=\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\mathrm{t}}\big(\boldsymbol{H}({\boldsymbol{\operatorname{curl}}}{},\Omega)\big).

By definition this renders the rotated tangential trace
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\mathrm{t}}\colon\boldsymbol{H}({\boldsymbol{\operatorname{curl}}}{},\Omega)\to\boldsymbol{H}_{\times}^{-1/2}(\operatorname{div}{}_{\Gamma},\Gamma)
surjective, and one can prove that it is continuous, compare
[
21
]
.

We can now reformulate Maxwell’s eigenvalue problem as a variational problem exclusively on the boundary
\Gamma
by using the anti-symmetric pairing

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\mu}\rangle_{\times}\coloneqq\int_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\mathbf{n})\boldsymbol{\mu}\mathrm{d}\Gamma,

(4)

cf.
[
21
, Def. 1]
.

4.2
Recasting the Eigenvalue Problem

Any solution of the electric wave equation can be derived via a boundary integral formulation, which we will review within this section.
The version reviewed here resembles the one presented in
[
21
, Thm. 6]
.

We define the
Maxwell single layer potential

{\boldsymbol{V}_{\kappa}}{}\colon\mathbf{H}^{-1/2}_{\times}(\operatorname{div}{}_{\Gamma},\Gamma)\to\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{curl}}}{}}^{2},\Omega)

via the Helmholtz fundamental solution

\displaystyle u^{*}_{\kappa}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{e^{-i\kappa\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|}}{4\pi\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|}

(5)

as

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{V}_{\kappa}}{}(\boldsymbol{\mu})(\boldsymbol{x})=\int_{\Gamma}u^{*}_{\kappa}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{y})\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{y}}+\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}\boldsymbol{\operatorname{grad}{}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\int_{\Gamma}u^{*}_{\kappa}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})(\operatorname{div}{}_{\Gamma}\circ\boldsymbol{u})(\boldsymbol{y})\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{y}}.

The mapping properties are known, see
[
21
, Thm. 5]
, where it is also shown that the image of
{\boldsymbol{V}_{\kappa}}{}
is divergence free.
With the help of this operator, one can show the following.

Lemma 2

(Single Layer Representation,
[
21
, Thm. 6]
)
.

If
\mathbf{E}\in\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{curl}}}{}}^{2},\Omega)
is solution to the electric wave equation (
3
) on
\Omega
, then it can be represented via

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}={\boldsymbol{V}_{\kappa}}{}\big({\mathbf{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{t}}^{\mathrm{int}}\mathbf{(}{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{curl}}}{}\;\mathbf{E})\big).

We now define the

Maxwell single layer operator

{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\kappa}}{}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\mathrm{t}}^{\mathrm{int}}\circ{\boldsymbol{V}_{\kappa}}{}
.
Applying the rotated tangential trace to the identity in Lemma
2
allows us to recast the eigenvalue problem (
3
) as a variational problem w.r.t. the duality pairing (
4
). The underlying boundary integral equation
reads as follows:

Problem 3

(Variational Eigenvalue Problem)
.

Find a non-zero
\boldsymbol{j}\in\boldsymbol{H}_{\times}^{{-1/2}}(\operatorname{div}{}_{\Gamma},\Gamma)
and
\kappa>0
such that

\displaystyle\langle{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\kappa}}{}\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{\mu}\rangle_{\times}=0

(6)

holds for all
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\boldsymbol{H}_{\times}^{{-1/2}}(\operatorname{div}{}_{\Gamma},\Gamma)
.

The problem in (
6
) is a nonlinear eigenvalue problem with respect to the eigenvalue parameter
\kappa
, since
\kappa
occurs nonlinearly in the fundamental solution (
5
) which builds the kernel of the single layer boundary integral operator
{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\kappa}}{}
.
The eigenvalue problem formulations (
3
) and (
6
) are equivalent in the following sense:

Lemma 4

(Equivalence of eigenvalue problems)
.

Let
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
and
\kappa>0
.

1.

If
(\kappa,\mathbf{E})
is an eigenpair of (
3
), then
(\kappa,({\mathbf{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{t}}^{\mathrm{int}}\mathbf{(}{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{curl}}}{}\;\mathbf{E}))
is an eigenpair of (
6
).

2.

If
(\kappa,\boldsymbol{j})
is an eigenpair of (
6
), then
(\kappa,{\boldsymbol{V}_{\kappa}}{}\boldsymbol{j})
is an eigenpair of (
3
).

Proof.

Assertion a) has been already shown by the derivation of the boundary integral formulation (
6
). For assertion b) note that
{\boldsymbol{V}_{\kappa}}{}\boldsymbol{j}
is a solution of Maxwell’s equation in
\Omega

[
21
, Sect. 4]
and that
0={\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\kappa}}{}\boldsymbol{j}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\mathrm{t}}^{\mathrm{int}}{\boldsymbol{V}_{\kappa}}{}\boldsymbol{j}
. It remains to show that
{\boldsymbol{V}_{\kappa}}{}\boldsymbol{j}\neq 0
in
\Omega
which follows form the unique solvability of the exterior problem and the jump relations of the single layer potential on the boundary
\Gamma
, see
[
16
, Prop. 2.1(ii)]
.
∎

We want to mention that the eigenvalue problem (
6
) has in addition to the real eigenvalues also non-real eigenvalues which correspond to the eigenvalues of the exterior eigenvalue problem. We refer to
[
16
]
for an analysis of this kind of eigenvalues.

5
A Brief Review of Isogeometric Analysis

This section is devoted to a brief review of isogeometric analysis as required for its utilisation in the context of boundary element methods for electromagnetism.
We follow the lines of
[
22
,
11
]
, which in their turn are based on the works of Buffa et al.
[
23
]
aimed at finite element discretisations.

Let
p\geq 0
and
m>0
be integers. While all of the results reviewed in this article are applicable to so-called
p
-open locally quasi-uniform knot vectors
, cf.
[
24
, Assum. 2.1]
, we assume all
knot vectors
to be of the form

\Xi^{p}_{m}=\big[\underbrace{0,\cdots,0}_{p+1\text{ times}},1/2^{m},\cdots,(2^{m}-1)/2^{m},\underbrace{1,\cdots,1}_{p+1\text{ times}}\big].

This is introduced only for notational convenience and in agreement with our numerical examples.
We will then refer to
m
as the
refinement level
and define the
mesh size

h\coloneqq 2^{-m}.

We proceed to define B-spline bases via the well-known recursion formula

\displaystyle b_{i}^{p}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{x-\xi_{i}}{\xi_{i+p}-\xi_{i}}b_{i}^{p-1}(x)+\frac{\xi_{i+p+1}-x}{\xi_{i+p+1}-\xi_{i+1}}b_{i+1}^{p-1}(x),

anchored for
p=0
by the locally constant functions
b_{i}^{0}(x)=\chi_{[\xi_{i},\xi_{i}+1)},
cf.
[
25
, Sec. 2.2]
. We define the spline space
S^{p}_{m}
as the span of the B-spline basis defined on
\Xi^{p}_{m}.

In reference domain, i.e., on
\square\coloneqq(0,1)^{2}
, we can now define the divergence conforming spline space
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{S}}^{p}_{m}(\square)
as done in
[
24
, Sec. 5.5]
via

\boldsymbol{\mathbb{S}}^{p}_{m}(\square)\coloneqq(S^{p}_{m}\otimes S^{p-1}_{m})\times(S^{p-1}_{m}\otimes S^{p}_{m}),

for
\otimes
denoting the tensor product and
\times
denoting the Cartesian product.

5.1
Geometry and Discretisation in the Physical Domain

Let a
patch

\Gamma
be given by the image of
\square
under an invertible diffeomorphism
\boldsymbol{F}\colon\square\to\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
For
\Omega
being a Lipschitz domain, define a
multipatch geometry
to be a compact, orientable two-dimensional manifold
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
invoked via
\bigcup_{0\leq j<N}\Gamma_{j}
by a family of patches
\{\Gamma_{j}\}_{0\leq j<N},

N\in\mathbb{N}
.
The family of diffeomorphisms
\{\boldsymbol{F}_{j}\colon\square\hookrightarrow\Gamma_{j}\}_{0\leq j<N}
will be called
parametrisation
.

We assume the
\Gamma_{j}
to be disjoint and that for any
patch interface

D
of the form
D=\partial\Gamma_{j_{0}}\cap\partial\Gamma_{j_{1}}\neq\emptyset
we require the continuous extensions of the parametrisations
\boldsymbol{F}_{j_{0}}
and
\boldsymbol{F}_{j_{1}}
onto
\overline{\square}=[0,1]^{2}
to coincide.

Within the framework of isogeometric analysis parametrisations will usually be given by tensor products of non-uniform rational B-splines (NURBS), i.e., functions of the form

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{F}_{j}(x,y)\coloneqq\sum_{0\leq j_{1}<k_{1}}\sum_{0\leq j_{2}<k_{2}}\frac{\boldsymbol{c}_{j_{1},j_{2}}b_{j_{1}}^{p_{1}}(x)b_{j_{2}}^{p_{2}}(y)w_{j_{1},j_{2}}}{\sum_{i_{1}=0}^{k_{1}-1}\sum_{i_{2}=0}^{k_{2}-1}b_{i_{1}}^{p_{1}}(x)b_{i_{2}}^{p_{2}}(y)w_{i_{1},i_{2}}}.

To extenf the definition of
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{S}}^{p}_{m}
to the physical domain, we resort to an application of the so-called
Piola transformation

[
26
, Chap. 6]
.

For the case of geometry mappings
\boldsymbol{F}_{j}\colon\square\to\Gamma_{j}
and
\boldsymbol{f}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{-1/2}_{\times}(\operatorname{div}{}_{\Gamma},\Gamma)
that allow for point evaluations, its explicit form is given by

\displaystyle\iota_{\boldsymbol{F}_{j}}(\boldsymbol{f})(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\eta(\boldsymbol{x})(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}\boldsymbol{F}_{j})^{-1}(\boldsymbol{f}\circ\boldsymbol{F}_{j})\big)(\boldsymbol{x}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}\in\square.

Therein,
\eta
denotes the
surface measure

\eta(\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq\|{\partial_{x_{1}}\boldsymbol{F}_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}})\times\partial_{x_{2}}\boldsymbol{F}_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}})}\|,

and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}\boldsymbol{F}_{j}
denotes the Jacobian of
\boldsymbol{F}_{j}.
Although it is not readily invertible, an inverse exists in the sense of a mapping from the two-dimensional tangent space of
\Gamma_{j}
to vector fields on
\square,
as has already been discussed in
[
11
, Sec. 3.2]
.
We remark that it needs not be computed, since all computations can be reduced to computations in the reference domain. Explicit formulae are easily derived, see e.g.
[
26
, Sec. 6.3]
.

We can now introduce the globally divergence conforming space

\boldsymbol{\mathbb{S}}^{p}_{m}(\Gamma)\coloneqq\left\{\boldsymbol{f}\in\boldsymbol{H}_{\times}^{{-1/2}}(\operatorname{div}{}_{\Gamma},\Gamma)\colon\iota_{\boldsymbol{F}_{j}}(\boldsymbol{f}|_{\Gamma_{j}})\in\boldsymbol{\mathbb{S}}^{p}_{m}(\square)\text{ for all }0\leq j<N\right\}.

It has been analysed in
[
15
, Def. 10]
, where it has been shown that it enjoys quasi-optimal approximation properties w.r.t. energy norm of the EFIE. Specifically, the spline space satisfies estimates of the kind

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{f}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{\mathbb{S}}^{p}_{m}(\Gamma)}\|{\boldsymbol{f}-\boldsymbol{f}_{h}}\|_{\boldsymbol{H}_{\times}^{{-1/2}}(\operatorname{div}{}_{\Gamma},\Gamma)}\leq Ch^{s+1/2}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{\boldsymbol{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{pw}}(\operatorname{div}{}_{\Gamma},\Gamma)},\qquad 0\leq s\leq p,

(7)

for all
\boldsymbol{f}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{pw}}(\operatorname{div}{}_{\Gamma},\Gamma)
, cf.
[
15
, Thm. 3]
, where the space
{\boldsymbol{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{pw}}(\operatorname{div}{}_{\Gamma},\Gamma)}

is equipped with the norm

\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{\boldsymbol{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{pw}}(\operatorname{div}{}_{\Gamma},\Gamma)}\coloneqq\sum_{0\leq j<N}\big\|{{\iota_{\boldsymbol{F}_{j}}(\boldsymbol{f}|_{\Gamma_{j}})\big\|_{\boldsymbol{H}^{s}(\operatorname{div}{},\square)}}}.

We remind of the relation
h=2^{-m}
due to the assumption of uniform refinement on the knot vectors.
These discrete spaces give rise to the discrete analogue of Problem
3
.

Problem 5

(Discrete Eigenvalue Problem)
.

Find a non-zero
\boldsymbol{j}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{\mathbb{S}}^{p}_{m}(\Gamma)
and
\kappa_{h}
such that

\displaystyle\langle{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\kappa}}{}\boldsymbol{j}_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}\rangle_{\times}=0

(8)

holds for all
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\boldsymbol{\mathbb{S}}^{p}_{m}(\Gamma)
.

In its discrete form, this problem induces a linear system

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{V}}_{\kappa}}{}\mathbf{j}_{h}=0,

(9)

which we choose to assemble as in
[
11
,
27
]
.
Therein,
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{V}}_{\kappa}}{}
can be interpreted as a matrix valued function dependent on
\kappa.

The well-posedness of this discrete problem is closely related to the following criteria, cf.
[
28
, Sec. 3]
.

(C1)

There exists a continuous splitting
\boldsymbol{H}^{-1/2}_{\times}(\operatorname{div}{}_{\Gamma},\Gamma)=\boldsymbol{W}\oplus\boldsymbol{V}
such that the bilinear form
\langle{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\kappa}}{}\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\times}
is stable and coercive on
\boldsymbol{V}\times\boldsymbol{V}
and
\boldsymbol{W}\times\boldsymbol{W}
, and compact on
\boldsymbol{V}\times\boldsymbol{W}
and
\boldsymbol{W}\times\boldsymbol{V}
.

(C2)

\boldsymbol{\mathbb{S}}^{p}_{m}(\Gamma)
can be decomposed into a sum
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{S}}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{m}}(\Gamma)=\boldsymbol{W}_{h}\oplus\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
of closed subspaces of
\boldsymbol{H}^{-1/2}_{\times}(\operatorname{div}{}_{\Gamma},\Gamma)
.

(C3)

\boldsymbol{W}_{h}
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
are stable under complex conjugation.

(C4)

Both
\boldsymbol{W}_{h}\subseteq\boldsymbol{W}
, as well as the so-called
gap-property

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}}\inf_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V}}\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h}}\|_{\boldsymbol{H}^{-1/2}_{\times}(\operatorname{div}{}_{\Gamma},\Gamma)}}{\|{\mathbf{v}_{h}}\|_{\boldsymbol{H}^{-1/2}_{\times}(\operatorname{div}{}_{\Gamma},\Gamma)}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle h\rightarrow 0}}{{\longrightarrow}}0

(10)

hold.

These properties have been proven for the isogeometric discretisation
[
11
, Thm. 3.9]
.

5.2
Numerical Analysis

The convergence of the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the Galerkin eigenvalue problem (
8
) can be shown using abstract results of
[
29
,
16
]
and
[
30
,
31
]
. Crucial for the convergence analysis are the above listed criteria (C1)-(C4), that
{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\kappa}}{}
satisfies a
T
-Gårding’s inequality with respect to the splitting in (C1)
[
11
]
, and that the mapping
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}\ni\kappa\mapsto{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\kappa}}{}
is holomorphic
[
16
, Lem. 2]
. In
[
29
,
16
]
sufficient conditions for the convergence of conforming Galerkin methods are specified for eigenvalue problems for holomorphic
T
-Gårding operator-valued functions. According to
[
16
, Lem. 5]
and
[
29
, Lem. 2.7]
the criteria (C1)-(C4) allow the application of the convergence theory established in
[
30
,
31
]
to the Galerkin eigenvalue problem (
8
). From the comprehensive convergence results presented in
[
30
,
31
,
29
,
16
]
we only want to state the error estimate for semi-simple eigenvalues
\kappa
:

|\kappa-\kappa_{h}|=\mathcal{O}(\delta_{m,p}^{2}),

(11)

where

\delta_{m,p}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{j}\in\mathrm{ker}{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\kappa}}{}\\
\|\boldsymbol{j}\|_{\boldsymbol{H}^{-1/2}_{\times}(\operatorname{div}{}_{\Gamma},\Gamma)}=1\end{subarray}}\inf_{\boldsymbol{j}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{\mathbb{S}}^{p}_{m}(\Gamma)}\|\boldsymbol{j}-\boldsymbol{j}_{h}\|_{\boldsymbol{H}^{-1/2}_{\times}(\operatorname{div}{}_{\Gamma},\Gamma)}.

(12)

The quadratic convergence order with respect to
\delta_{h,p}
follows from the fact that for any eigenfunction
\boldsymbol{j}_{\text{adj}}
of the adjoint eigenvalue problem there exists an eigenfunction
\boldsymbol{j}
of the eigenvalue problem
{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\kappa}}{}\boldsymbol{j}=0
such that
\overline{\boldsymbol{j}}=\boldsymbol{j}_{\text{adj}}
, see
[
16
, Lem. 3]
.
The estimate (
7
) together with (
11
) and (
12
) yields the final estimate

\displaystyle|\kappa-\kappa_{h}|=\mathcal{O}(h^{2(p+1/2)}),

(13)

for sufficiently smooth surface current densities
\boldsymbol{j}.

Remark 6

(Convergence on non-smooth geometries)
.

In this sense sufficiently smooth needs to be understood in terms of patchwise regularity as explained in
[
15
]
. In general, for surface current densities on non-smooth geometries this smoothness assumption will not be fulfilled, since
\boldsymbol{j}
may admit singularities at corners and edges.

However, for the specific densities of resonant modes within interior cavities this smoothness assumption will often be fulfilled. For the cube an analytical representation of
\boldsymbol{j}
is known to be smooth
[
14
]
, and even for other, non-trivial geometries this can be argued.

6
The Contour Integral Method

This chapter will give a short summary of the contour integral method as introduced by Beyn
[
12
]
, without any non-trivial modifications. Note that there are alternative approaches by other authors, where the first publication
[
13
]
seems to go back to 2009, compare also
[
32
]
. After this short review, we will discuss three numerical examples that utilise this method to solve Problem
8
.

Let
\mathbf{T}\colon M\to\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
be holomorphic on some domain
M\subset\mathbb{C}
. Let
\mathbf{T}^{h}
denote the Hermitian transpose of
\mathbf{T}
and
\mathbf{T}^{\prime}
denote its usual transpose.
We want to solve the nonlinear eigenvalue problem

\mathbf{T}(z)\mathbf{v}=0,\quad\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{m},\quad\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0},\quad z\in M.

If
z
is no eigenvalue of
\mathbf{T}
, we find by the condition above that the kernel of
\mathbf{T}(z)
is trivial, thus
\mathbf{T}(z)
has full rank and is invertible.

Fix some domain
D\subseteq M
with boundary
\partial D,
and assume there to be
k
eigenvalues
(\lambda_{j})_{j\leq k}
in the interior of
D
, such that all eigenvalues are simple. We remark that the entire approach was also generalised to that case of non simple eigenvalues
[
12
]
.
As a consequence of the version of the Keldysh Theorem stated in
[
33
]
one can proof the following result, on which this method is based.

Theorem 7

(Contour Integral Theorem,
[
12
, Thm. 2.9.]
)
.

For
\mathbf{T}
as above and holomorphic
f\colon M\to\mathbb{C}
it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial D}f(z)\mathbf{T}(z)^{-1}\mathrm{d}z=\sum^{k}_{j=1}f(\lambda_{j})\mathbf{v}_{h}\mathbf{w}_{h}^{h},

where
\mathbf{v}_{j}
and
\mathbf{w}_{j}
are left and right eigenvectors corresponding to
\lambda_{n}
that are normalized according to

\mathbf{w}_{j}^{h}\mathbf{T}^{\prime}(\lambda_{j})\mathbf{v}_{j}=1

for all
j\leq k
.

For our purposes, where we set
\mathbf{T}(z)=\mathbf{V}_{z}
as a matrix-valued function, cf. (
9
), the contour integral method can be reduced to the following theorem.

Theorem 8

(Linearisation of Eigenvalue Problems,
[
12
, Thm. 3.1]
)
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{T}\colon M\to\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
is holomorphic and has only simple eigenvalues
(\lambda_{j})_{j\leq k}
in some connected subdomain
D\subset M
.
Then there exists a diagonalizable matrix
\mathbf{B}
which can be computed from evaluations of
\mathbf{T}
such that
\mathbf{B}
that has the same eigenvalues as the eigenvalue problem under consideration within
D
.

The proof of this theorem is constructive and corresponds to the following algorithm, on which our implementation is based.

At first the algortihm might seem prohibitively expensive due to the application of a singular value decomposition. However, since the number of eigenvalues
k
will be small and often a reasonable upper estimate
\ell_{0}
of the number of eigenvalues is known such that one can choose
\ell=\ell_{0}
.
Then the complexity of the singular value decomposition becomes negligible in comparison to solving the linear system in lines 5 and 15 of Algorithm
9
.

Moreover, in an actual implementation
\mathbf{A}_{1}
and
\mathbf{A}_{0}
are assembled simultaneously, since the most expensive operation of the algorithm is evaluating
\mathbf{T}(t_{j})^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{V}}
. In general,
k
and
\ell
will be small, so storing both
\mathbf{A}_{j}
poses no issues. Since smooth contours should be used, exclusively, one can expect the trapezoidal rules for the assembly of the
\mathbf{A}_{j}
to converge exponentially with respect to
N
.
Thus, the bottleneck in terms of accuracy of the entire scheme is the accuracy in which
\mathbf{X}
represents the bilinear form of the EFIE.

Remark 10

(Obtaining the Eigenvectors)
.

Note that through this algorithm we not only obtain the eigenvalues of Problem
3
, but also the coefficients of the corresponding eigenfunctions in the form of the matrices
\mathbf{V}
and
\mathbf{W}.
Note moreover that the number of non-zero singular values reflects the number of solutions within
D
and can be used for verification of an implementation if the number of solutions is known from analytical representations or measurements.

As a third example we discuss an eigenvalue computation of the one-cell TESLA geometry as shown in Figures

p=5

\lambda_{\mathrm{cim}}=26.75690023

is most likely due to the reduced regularity of the corresponding eigenfunction.
Either way, one can clearly see an increased accuracy in higher-order approaches.

8
Conclusion

Overall, the contour integral method yields exceptional accuracies in conjunction with our isogeometric boundary element method.
Judging from the asymptotic behaviour predicted in (
13
) and observed in the numerical examples, the accuracy of the combination of isogeometric boundary element method and the contour integral method promises higher orders of convergence to the correct solution compared to currently implemented volume based approaches, since these will not benefit from the convergence order of
{\mathcal{O}}(h^{2p+1})
.
This means that, for the same computational resources, the maximum reachable accuracy of the IGA-BEM with contour integration is higher compared to many volume-based methods. However, due to the long time spend solving the systems, this means that the IGA-BEM with contour integral offers this higher accuracy only in exchange for runtime, until efficient preconditioning strategies are available, thus making the utilisation of fast methods and iterative solvers viable for this type of application.
