Saparya Suresh a and Sudheesh K. Kattumannil b a Indian Institute of Management, Kozhikode, India, b Indian Statistical Institute, Chennai, India

Abstract
. The categorical Gini covariance is a dependence measure between a numerical variable and a categorical variable. The Gini covariance measures dependence by quantifying the difference between the conditional and unconditional distributional functions. The categorical Gini covariance equals zero if and only if the numerical variable and the categorical variable are independent. We propose a non-parametric test for testing the independence between a numerical and categorical variable using a modified categorical Gini covariance. We used the theory of U-statistics to find the test statistics and study the properties. The test has an asymptotic normal distribution. Since the implementation of a normal-based test is difficult, we develop a jackknife empirical likelihood (JEL) ratio test for testing independence. Extensive Monte Carlo simulation studies are carried out to validate the performance of the proposed JEL ratio test. We illustrate the test procedure using Iris flower data set. Key words: Categorical Gini covariance; Jackknife empirical likelihood; U-statistics; Test for independence.



1.
Introduction

Measuring the strength of associations or correlations between two variables is important in many scenarios. Many studies look at various methods to capture the correlations for two numerical variables (Gibbons, 1993, Mari and Kotz, 2001). While the popular Pearson correlation measures the linear relationship, Spearman’s
\rho
and Kendall’s
\tau
explore monotonic relationships between two numerical variables. Similarly, there are different measures that capture the associations between numerical or ordinal random variables (Shevlyakov and Smirno, 2001). However, these correlation measures cannot be directly applied to a categorical variable. Moreover, for most of these proposed measures, though
X
and
Y
being independent implied zero, the converse was not true. Cramer’s V can be used to measure the association between two nominal variables. Many approaches were suggested by various researchers like Beknazaryan, Dang and Sand, (2019), and Dang et al. (2019) for measuring the dependence between a categorical and numerical variable, however, they lacked computational flexibility.

The categorical Gini correlation is a dependence measure between a numerical variable
X
and a categorical variable
Y
that was proposed by Dang et al. (2021). Consider a continuous random variable
X
with a distribution
F
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
Y
be a categorical random variable taking values
L_{1},\cdots,L_{K}
and its distribution given by
P_{y}
such that
P(Y=L_{k})=p_{k}>0
for
k=1,2,\cdots,K
. Let
F_{k}
be the conditional distribution of
X
given
Y=L_{k}
. When the conditional distribution of
X
given
Y
is the same as the marginal distribution of
X
, then
X
and
Y
are independent. Otherwise, they are dependent. The categorical Gini covariance and the correlation measure the dependence based on the weighted distance between the marginal and the conditional distributions.

Let
\Psi_{k}
and
\Psi
be the characteristic function of
F_{k}
and
F
respectively. We define the
L_{2}
distance between
\Psi_{k}
and
\Psi
as

T(F_{k},F)=c(d)\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\frac{|\Psi_{k}(t)-\Psi(t)|^{2}}{||t||^{d+1}}dt,

where
c(d)=\Gamma((d+1)/2)/\pi^{\frac{(d+1)}{2}}
. Then the Gini covariance between
X
and
Y
is defined as

gCov(X,Y)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}T(F_{k},F).

The Gini covariance measures the dependence of
X
and
Y
by quantifying the difference between the conditional and unconditional characteristic functions. The corresponding Gini correlations standardize the Gini covariance to have a range in
[0,1]
. When
d=1
, the categorical Gini covariance and correlation between
X
and
Y
can be defined by

gCov(X,Y)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(F_{k}(x)-F(x))^{2}dx

(1)

\rho_{g}(X,Y)=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(F_{k}(x)-F(x))^{2}dx}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}(F(x)-F(x))^{2}dx}.

Note that the Gini covariance is the weighted squared distance between the marginal distribution and conditional distribution. It has been shown that
\rho_{g}
has a lower computation cost. It is more straightforward to perform statistical inference and it is a more robust measure even with unbalanced data than the popular distance correlation measures (Dang et al., 2021). Hewage and Sang (2024) developed the jackknife empirical likelihood (JEL) method to find the confidence interval of the categorical Gini correlation. Sang et al. (2021) illustrated the use of the categorical Gini correlation for developing a test for equality of K-distributions.

In this paper, we aim to propose a non-parametric test of independence between a numerical and categorical variable using a modified categorical Gini covariance and the theory of U-statistics. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In section 2, we develop a U-statistics-based test and study the asymptotic properties of the same. We also develop a jackknife empirical likelihood (JEL) ratio test for testing independence. Section 3 details the Monte Carlo simulations carried out to validate the performance of the proposed JEL ratio test. In section 4, we illustrate the test procedure using real data sets and finally, we conclude in section 5.

2.
Test Statistic

Consider a continuous random variable
X
with a distribution function
F
in
\mathbb{R}
. Denote
\bar{F}(x)=1-F(x)
as the survival function of
X.
Let
Y
be a categorical random variable taking values
L_{1},\cdots,L_{K}
with probability mass function
p_{k}=P(Y=L_{k}),\,k=1,2,\cdots K
. Further, let
F_{k}
be the conditional distribution of
X
given
Y=L_{k}
, that is
F_{k}(x)=P(X\leq x|Y=L_{k})
. Denote
\bar{F}_{k}(x)=P(X>x|Y=L_{k})
. Based on the observed data, we are interested in testing the null hypothesis

H_{0}:X\text{ and }Y\text{ are independent}

against the alternative hypothesis

H_{0}:X\text{ and }Y\text{ are dependent}.

Note that the alternative hypothesis we consider here is very general in the sense
H_{1}
only specifies the lack of independence and not assume any specific dependence structure between
X
and
Y
.

We define a departure measure
\Delta
, from the null hypothesis
H_{0}
towards alternative hypothesis
H_{1}
as follows:

\Delta=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(F_{k}(x)-F(x))^{2}dF(x).

(2)

We can see that
\Delta
is a weighted average of the squared distance between the marginal distribution and conditional distribution. We call
\Delta
defined in (
2
) as modified Gini Covariance. We can easily verify that
\Delta=0
if and only if
X
and
Y
are independent.

The
\Delta
can be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(F_{k}(x)-F(x))^{2}dF(x)

(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(-\bar{F}_{k}(x)+\bar{F}(x)\right)^{2}dF(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\bar{F}^{2}_{k}(x)+\bar{F}(x)^{2}-2\bar{F}_{k}(x)\bar{F}(x)\right)dF(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{F}^{2}_{k}(x)dF(x)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{F}^{2}(x)dF(x)

\displaystyle-2\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{F}_{k}(x)\bar{F}(x)dF(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{F}^{2}_{k}(x)dF(x)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{F}^{2}(x)dF(x)

\displaystyle-2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\bar{F}_{k}(x)\right)\bar{F}(x)dF(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{F}^{2}_{k}(x)dF(x)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{F}^{2}(x)dF(x)

\displaystyle-2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}P(X>x,Y=k)\right)\bar{F}(x)dF(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{F}^{2}_{k}(x)dF(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{F}(x)^{2}dF(x)-2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{F}^{2}(x)dF(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{p_{k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{k}^{2}\bar{F}^{2}_{k}(x)dF(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{F}^{2}(x)dF(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{p_{k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}P(X>x,Y=k)^{2}dF(x)-\frac{1}{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{p_{k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}P(X_{1}>x,Y_{1}=k)P(X_{2}>x,Y_{2}=k)dF(x)-\frac{1}{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{p_{k}}P(\min(X_{1},X_{2})>X_{3},Y_{1}=Y_{2}=k)-\frac{1}{3}.

Note that if
X
and
Y
are independent, then equation (
3
) becomes

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{p_{k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}P(X>x,Y=k)^{2}dF(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{F}^{2}(x)dF(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{p_{k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(p_{k}P(X>x)\right)^{2}dF(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{F}^{2}(x)dF(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(P(X>x)\right)^{2}dF(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{F}^{2}(x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(P(X>x)\right)^{2}dF(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{F}^{2}(x)dF(x)=0.

Thus, under
H_{0}
,
\Delta
is zero and it is positive under the alternative
H_{1}
.

In (
3
), we represent
\Delta
as a sum of probabilities. This enables us to use the theory of U-statistics to develop the test.

Let
(X_{1},Y_{1}),(X_{2},Y_{2})
and
(X_{3},Y_{3})
be independent and identical copies of
(X,Y)
.
Define,

h_{k}^{*}((X_{1},Y_{1}),(X_{2},Y_{2}),(X_{3},Y_{3}))=I(\\
min(X_{1},X_{2})>X_{3},Y_{1}=Y_{2}=k),

where
I
denotes the indicator function. Then
\Delta
can be written

\Delta=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{p_{k}}E(h_{k}^{*}((X_{1},Y_{1}),(X_{2},Y_{2}),(X_{3},Y_{3})))-\frac{1}{3}.

Let
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\dots,(X_{n},Y_{n})
be the observed data. An estimator of
\Delta
is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{6}{n(n-1)(n-2)}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\widehat{p}_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n-1}\sum_{l=j+1}^{n}h_{k}((X_{i},Y_{i}),(X_{j},Y_{j}),(X_{l},Y_{l}))-\frac{1}{3},

where
h_{k}(.)
is the symmetric version of the kernel
h_{k}^{*}(.)
and
\widehat{p}_{k}
is estimated by the following equation

\widehat{p}_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}I(Y_{i}=k).

For convenience, we represent
\widehat{\Delta}
as

\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\widehat{p}_{k}}\frac{6}{n(n-1)(n-2)}\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n-1}\sum_{l=j+1}^{n}h_{k}((X_{i},Y_{i}),(X_{j},Y_{j}),(X_{l},Y_{l}))-\frac{1}{3}

(4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\widehat{p}_{k}}\widehat{\Delta}_{k}-\frac{1}{3}.

The test procedure is to reject the null hypothesis
H_{0}
against the alternative
H_{1}
for a large value of
\widehat{\Delta}
.

Next, we study the asymptotic properties of the test statistic. By the law of large numbers,
\widehat{p}_{k}
is a consistent estimator of
p_{k}
. Using the asymptotic theory of U-statistics,
\widehat{\Delta}_{k}
is a consistent estimator of
E(\widehat{\Delta}_{k})
(Sen, 1960). Hence, it is easy to verify that
\widehat{\Delta}
is a consistent estimator of
\Delta
.
Next, we find the asymptotic distribution of
\widehat{\Delta}
.

Theorem 1
.

As
n\rightarrow\infty
,
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\Delta}-\Delta)
converges in distribution to a normal random variable with mean zero and variance
\sigma^{2}
given by

\sigma^{2}=\mathbf{a^{T}}\Sigma\mathbf{a},

where
\mathbf{a}^{T}=(\frac{1}{p_{1}},\frac{1}{p_{2}},\cdots,\frac{1}{p_{K}})
and
\Sigma=((\sigma_{kl}))_{K\times K}
with

\sigma_{kk}=9Var(E(h_{k}((X_{1},Y_{1}),(X_{2},Y_{2}),(X_{3},Y_{3})))|X_{1},Y_{1})),\,k=1,2,\cdots,K

and

\displaystyle\sigma_{kl}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 9Cov(h_{k}((X_{1},Y_{1}),(X_{2},Y_{2}),(X_{3},Y_{3})),h_{l}((X_{1},Y_{1}),(X_{4},Y_{4}),(X_{5},Y_{5}))),

\displaystyle\quad\,\text{ if }\,k\neq l,\,k,l=1,2,\cdots,K.

Proof.

Denote

\widehat{\Delta}^{*}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{{p}_{k}}\widehat{\Delta}_{k}-\frac{1}{3},

then

. Since
\widehat{p}_{k}
is consistent estimator of
p_{k}
, the asymptotic distributions of
\widehat{\Delta}
and
\widehat{\Delta}^{*}
are same.
Observe that
\widehat{\Delta}_{k}
,
k=1,2,\ldots,K
are U-statistics with symmetric kernel
h_{k}
of degree 3. Also, we have
E(\widehat{\Delta}_{k})=P(\\
min(X_{1},X_{2})>X_{3},Y_{1}=Y_{2}=k)=\Delta_{k}.
Using the central limit theorem for U-statistics (Lee, 2019), it follows that as
n\rightarrow\infty
,
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\Delta}_{k}-\Delta_{k})
converges in distribution to normal random variables with mean
0
and variance
\sigma_{kk}
, where

\sigma_{kk}=9Var(E(h_{k}((X_{1},Y_{1}),(X_{2},Y_{2}),(X_{3},Y_{3})))|X_{1},Y_{1})).

(5)

Hence, the distribution of
\widehat{\mathbf{\Delta}}=(\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\Delta}_{1}-{\Delta}_{1}),\ldots,\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\Delta}_{K}-{\Delta}_{K}))^{T}
is multivariate normal with mean vector zero and variance-covariance matrix
\Sigma=((\sigma_{kl}))_{K\times K}

\sigma_{kk}
is given as in equation (

\sigma_{kl}
is given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{kl}=9Cov(h_{k}((X_{1},Y_{1}),(X_{2},Y_{2}),(X_{3},Y_{3})),h_{l}((X_{1},Y_{1}),(X_{4},Y_{4}),(X_{5},Y_{5}))).

(6)

If a random vector
\mathbf{Z}

\Sigma

the distribution of
\mathbf{a}^{T}\mathbf{Z}

\mathbf{a}^{T}\mathbf{\mu}
and variance
\mathbf{a}^{T}\Sigma\mathbf{a}
. Hence, for the choice of
\mathbf{a}=(\frac{1}{p_{1}},\frac{1}{p_{2}},\cdots,\frac{1}{p_{K}})^{T}
, we have the required result.

Corollary 1
.

Under
H_{0}
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,
\sqrt{n}\widehat{\Delta}
converges in distribution to a normal random variable with mean zero and variance
\sigma_{0}^{2}=\mathbf{a^{T}}\Sigma\mathbf{a},

where
\mathbf{a}^{T}=(\frac{1}{p_{1}},\frac{1}{p_{2}},\cdots,\frac{1}{p_{K}})
and
\Sigma=((\sigma_{kl}))_{K\times K}
with
\sigma_{kk}
and

\sigma_{kl}
are given in equation (
7
) and (
8
), respectively.

Using Corollary 1, we can obtain a normal-based critical region for the proposed test. Let
\widehat{\sigma}_{0}^{2}
be a consistent estimator of
\sigma_{0}^{2}
. Then, we reject
H_{0}
in favor of
H_{1}
at significance level
\alpha
if

\frac{\sqrt{n}\widehat{\Delta}}{\widehat{\sigma}}>Z_{\alpha},

where
Z_{\alpha}
is the upper
\alpha
percentile point of a standard normal distribution.

Finding a consistent estimator for the null variance
\sigma_{0}^{2}
is difficult, which in turn makes it difficult to determine a critical region of the proposed test based on the asymptotic distribution of the test statistic. Thus a normal-based test is not recommended. Therefore, we develop a jackknife empirical likelihood ratio for testing the proposed hypothesis.

2.1.
JEL ratio test

The concept of empirical likelihood was first proposed by Thoman and Grunkemier (1975) to determine the confidence range for survival probabilities under right censoring. The work by Owen (1988, 1990) extended this concept of empirical likelihood to a general methodology. Computationally, empirical likelihood involves maximizing non-parametric likelihood supported by the data subject to some constraints. If the constraints become non-linear in these cases, it poses a computational difficulty to evaluate. Further, it becomes increasingly difficult as
n
gets large. To overcome this problem, Jing et al. (2009) introduced the jackknife empirical likelihood method for finding the confidence interval of the desired parametric function. Because it combines the advantages of the likelihood approach and the jackknife technique, this method is popular among researchers.

Let
(X_{1},Y_{1}),(X_{2},Y_{2}),\cdots,(X_{n},Y_{n})
be the random sample from a joint distribution function
F(x,y)
of
X
and
Y
. Recall that the U-statistics based estimator for the test statistics
\widehat{\Delta}
is given by

\widehat{\Delta}=\frac{6}{n(n-1)(n-2)}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\widehat{p}_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n-1}\sum_{l=j+1}^{n}h_{k}(X_{i},X_{j},X_{l})-\frac{1}{3}.

Let
\nu_{i}
be the jackknife pseudo-values defined as follows:

\nu_{i}=n\widehat{\Delta}-(n-1)\widehat{\Delta}_{i}\,i=1,\cdots,n,

where
\widehat{\Delta}_{i}
is the value of
\widehat{\Delta}
obtained using
(n-1)
observations
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\cdots,

(X_{i-1},Y_{i-1}),(X_{i+1},Y_{i+1}),\cdots,(X_{n},Y_{n})
. It can be observed that

\widehat{\Delta}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}.

It can be shown that
\nu_{i},i=1,\cdots,n
are asymptotically independent random variables. Hence, Jackknife estimators
\widehat{\Delta}
is the average of asymptotically independent random variables
\nu_{i}
.

Let
w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})
be a probability vector assigning probability
w_{i}
to each
v_{i}
. Note that the objective function
\prod_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}
subject to the constraints
\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}=1
attain its maximum value
n^{-n}
when
w_{i}=1/n
.

Thus, the jackknife empirical likelihood ratio for testing the independence between a categorical variable and continuous variable based on the departure measure
{\Delta}
is given by

R(\Delta)=\max\Big\{\prod_{i=1}^{n}nw_{i},\,\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}=1,\,\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\nu_{i}=0\Big\},

Using the Lagrange multiplier method, we obtain

w_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\frac{1}{1+\lambda\nu_{i}}

where
\lambda
satisfies

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\nu_{i}}{1+\lambda\nu_{i}}=0.

Thus the jackknife empirical log-likelihood ratio is given by

\log R(\Delta)=-\sum\log(1+\lambda\nu_{i}).

From Theorem 1 of Jing et al. (2009), we have the following result as an analog of Wilk’s theorem on the limiting distribution of the jackknife empirical log-likelihood ratio.

Theorem 2
.

Let
g(x)=E(h(X_{1},X_{2})|X_{1}=x)
and
\sigma^{2}_{g}=Var(g(X_{1})).
Assume that
E(h^{2}(X_{1},X_{2})<\infty
and
\sigma^{2}_{g}>0
. Then, under
H_{0}
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,
-2\log R(\Delta)
converges in distribution to a
\chi^{2}
random variable with one degree of freedom.

In the view of Theorem
2
we can obtain a critical region based on JEL ratio. We can reject the null hypothesis
H_{0}
against the alternatives hypothesis
H_{1}
at a significance level
\alpha
, if

-2\log R(\Delta)>\chi^{2}_{1,\alpha},

(9)

where
\chi^{2}_{1,\alpha}
is the upper
\alpha
-percentile point of the
\chi^{2}
distribution with one degree of freedom.

. We can also see that the proposed test performs better that IPC and MV test when sample size is smaller.

4.
Data Analysis

For the purpose of illustration, we demonstrate the application of the proposed JEL-based test on a real data set. The data set used for illustration is the famous Iris built-in data set in R which includes the measurement in centimeters of Sepal Length, Sepal Width, Petal Length and Petal Width of different species. The data set contains 3 different species (Setosa, Veriscolour and Virginica) of 50 instances each. In this study, we employed separate lengths of species to illustrate the use of the proposed test.
It is natural to assume that species of the flower has an effect on determining the dimension of the Sepal of the flower. Hence, we are employing the test to check whether the Sepal Length of the flower has dependence on the species (categorical variable in this scenario). To have a clarity, Figure
1
shows the density plots of the Sepal Length for each species.

Density plot of Sepal Length

From Figure
1
, it is clear that different species have different distributions of Sepal Length, suggesting that there might be dependence of sepal length on the species. To confirm the same inference, we conduct the proposed hypothesis test on the data set. The calculated value of the JEL ratio
-2\log R(\Delta)=78.55
. The result suggests that we can reject the null hypothesis at 5% as well as 1% significance level. Thus, as per the test, we can conclude that the Sepal length of the Iris flower is dependent on species.

5.
Conclusion

Testing the independence between a continuous variable
X
and a discrete variable
Y
has many practical applications in various fields such as economics, social sciences, environmental science and medical science, etc. Testing independence between continuous and discrete variables helps simplify models, improve accuracy, and ensure better interpretability in machine learning applications. For example, when a continuous variable is distributed differently among different categories, one can use a Gaussian mixture model to model the data. Therefore, it is desirable to develop a test for independence when
X
is continuous and
Y
is discrete.

Motivated by this, we developed a U-statistics based test for testing the independence between a categorical and a continuous variable. As the implementation of the U-statistics test is difficult, we developed a JEL ratio test to test the same. The simulation study revealed that the proposed JEL ratio test has a well-controlled type I error and has good power for various choices of alternatives. We also compare our test procedure with recently developed tests for independence. We illustrated the test procedure using the famous Iris data set. In the data analysis, we demonstrated that the test shows dependence between the continuous variable representing Sepal length and the categorical variable representing the flower species thus exploring the possibility of using the proposed test in classification problems. This proposed test can be used for variable selection in scenarios of discriminant analysis or generalized linear modeling (See Cui et al., 2015).

In lifetime data analysis, various types of censoring and truncation may occur. If a continuous variable is subjected to right censoring, one can consider developing a test for independence between a categorical and a continuous variable. We can modify the proposed test procedure using the U-statistics for censored data (Datta et al., 2010). Testing independence under left-truncated and right-censored cases can also be considered (Sudheesh et al. 2023).
