Frozen Gaussian Sampling: A Mesh-free Monte Carlo Method For Approximating Semiclassical Schrödinger Equations
Yantong Xie Zhennan Zhou

Abstract
In this paper, we develop a Monte Carlo algorithm named the Frozen Gaussian Sampling (FGS) to solve the semiclassical Schrödinger equation based on the frozen Gaussian approximation. Due to the highly oscillatory structure of the wave function, traditional mesh-based algorithms suffer from ”the curse of dimensionality”, which gives rise to more severe computational burden when the semiclassical parameter ε \varepsilon is small. The Frozen Gaussian sampling outperforms the existing algorithms in that it is mesh-free in computing the physical observables and is suitable for high dimensional problems. In this work, we provide detailed procedures to implement the FGS for both Gaussian and WKB initial data cases, where the sampling strategies on the phase space balance the need of variance reduction and sampling convenience. Moreover, we rigorously prove that, to reach a certain accuracy, the number of samples needed for the FGS is independent of the scaling parameter ε \varepsilon . Furthermore, the complexity of the FGS algorithm is of a sublinear scaling with respect to the microscopic degrees of freedom and, in particular, is insensitive to the dimension number. The performance of the FGS is validated through several typical numerical experiments, including simulating scattering by the barrier potential, formation of the caustics and computing the high-dimensional physical observables without mesh.



Key words:
Semiclassical Schrödinger equation, Frozen Gaussian approximation, Frozen Gaussian sampling, Monte Carlo method, variance reduction

65C05, 65M75, 81Q05, 81Q20

Introduction

The Schrödinger equation, which depicts the evolution of the wave function of a quantum system, is given as follows:

\mathrm{i}\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}u(t,x)=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m_{\text{o}}}\Delta u(t,x)+E(x)u(t,x),

(1.1)

where
m_{\text{o}}
denotes the mass of the particles and
\hbar
denotes the reduced Planck constant. By adopting the nondimensionalisation techniques (see Appendix A), one obtains the following semiclassical Schrödinger equation in the dimensionless form:

\begin{cases}\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}u(t,x)=-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\Delta u(t,x)+E(x)u(t,x),&\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{m},\\
u(0,x)=u_{\mathrm{in}}(x),&\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m},\end{cases}

(1.2)

where the parameter
\varepsilon
, named the semiclassical parameter, describes the microscopic macroscopic scale ratio and is small within the semiclassical regime (i.e.
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
). The quantum mechanical wave function
u(t,x)
, valued in
{\mathbb{C}}
, often serves as an auxiliary quantity to compute the physical quantities of interest. The physical quantities, including position, momentum and energy, etc., are referred to as ”observables”, which are of the form of quantum averages with respect to the wave functions of certain self-adjoint operators:

O=\langle u|\hat{O}|u\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\overline{u(t,x)}\,\hat{O}\,u(t,x)\mathrm{d}x.

(1.3)

Here,
\hat{O}
denotes the operator related to a physical observable. For examples,
\hat{O}^{q}=x
and
\hat{O}^{p}=-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\nabla
are the operators corresponding to the position and momentum observables, respectively.

In the semiclassical regime, namely
\varepsilon\ll 1
, the solution to equation (
1.2
) is highly
oscillatory both in space and time on the scale
{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon)
and doesn’t converge in strong sense as
\varepsilon\to 0
, which gives rise to significant computational burdens
[
13
,
17
]
. Nevertheless, various effective numerical algorithms have been proposed so far, succeeding in capturing the highly oscillatory wave functions accurately with affordable computational complexity. The existing algorithms can be divided into two classes: direct discretization methods and asymptotic methods. Among the direct discretization methods, one of the most prevailing choices is the time splitting spectral method proposed in
[
1
]
, where the meshing strategy
\Delta t={\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon)
and
\Delta x={\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon)
is practically sufficient for accurate approximation of the wave function. The time step size of the time splitting spectral method can be even relaxed to
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
, in other words, independently of
\varepsilon
, if one is only interested in capturing the correct physical observables, whereas one still needs to resolve the spatial oscillations. The asymptotic methods, including the WKB methods
[
5
,
6
]
and various wave packets based methods
[
29
,
14
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
27
,
12
,
24
]
, provide accurate numerical approximation to the wave function with a relatively less computational cost with respect to the parameter
\varepsilon
compared to the direct discretization methods. The wave packets based methods have been extended to more complicated quantum dynamics, such as the vector potential cases and systems with random inputs
[
30
,
27
,
12
,
24
]
, providing broad application prospects in many applications.

Although the existing algorithms have made great progress in efficiently simulating the wave functions and the physical observables in the semiclassical regime, they still suffer from the ”the curse of dimensionality” when considering high dimensional cases, since those methods necessarily reply on an Eulerian mesh either in the initial decomposition or in the time evolution. When the dimension number
m\gg 1
, the computation and memory amounts of the both classes of methods increase exponentially as
m
increases, which often leads to more infeasible computational cost when
\varepsilon\ll 1
. For example, for the time splitting spectral method, the degree of freedom to approximate the wave function on an
m-
dimensional mesh is
{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{-m})
, not to mention the increased computational complexity to evolve the wave function on this high dimensional mesh. Therefore, a novel numerical method for the Schrödinger equation in semiclassical regime, whose cost is insensitive to
\varepsilon
and the dimension number
m
, is highly desirable.

To the best of our knowledge, there has been very few existing methods, which are feasible or have the potential to be suitable for high dimensional simulations. From this perspective, the path integral sampling method proposed recently in
[
22
,
21
]
can be viewed as a pioneering work, which is constructed on the so-called Frozen Gaussian approximation (FGA). The FGA represents the wave function with a superposition of Gaussian functions in the phase space with fixed widths and is often wrote in the form of an integral on the phase space:

u_{\mathrm{FGA}}(t,x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\varepsilon)^{\frac{3m}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}A(t,q,p)e^{\frac{i\Theta(t,x,q,p)}{\varepsilon}}\mathrm{d}q\mathrm{d}p,

(1.4)

where the variables
A
and
\Theta
are can be approximately obtained through evolving a system of Lagrangian ODEs, thus the full quantum dynamics is reduced to an ensemble of wave packets dynamics. There have been quite a few works related to the FGA, including the theoretical studies in the chemistry community
[
8
,
9
,
15
,
16
]
, the rigorous convergence analysis
[
28
]
and the FGA based algorithms for solving hyperbolic systems which reply on the direct phase space discretization
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
. However, it is not until
[
22
,
21
]
that people find another distinct prospective, giving the FGA ansatz (
1.4
) a probabilistic interpretation, and show that the Monte-Carlo sampling method based on the FGA gives a feasible algorithm which is mesh-free in computing the physical observables. The main spirit of
[
22
,
21
]
can be summarized as follows: firstly, as an integral on the phase space, the FGA ansatz can be viewed as an expectation of random variables on the phase space, which can be calculated by solving the parameter ODEs; in addition, when the initial data is Gaussian, the module function
|A(0,q,p)|
is also Gaussian and naturally serves as an ideal choice of a sampling density function on the phase space. However, what remains to be done is that there have been no rigorous numerical analysis on the sampling error nor discussion on how to extend the sampling method to general cases when the initial data is not Gaussian. Our current work aims to fill the vacancy on these issues.

In this work, we develop a Monte Carlo method which relies on the probabilistic interpretation on the FGA ansatz, and we shall name it the Frozen Gaussian Sampling (FGS). The essence of the FGS is to properly choose a reference density function on the phase space, and reformulate the FGA representation (
1.4
) as an expectation of a complex-valued functional with respect to the Frozen Gaussian trajectories. In this view, the FGS is a path-integral approximation of the wave function, and in the dynamical picture, the evolution of the quantum state is replaced by propagating an ensemble of Frozen Gaussian wave packets, where the initial phase space coordinates are sampled from the reference density function. The design of an efficient FGS algorithm boils down to proposing a suitable reference sampling measure, which is closely related the form of the initial condition of the wave function, and the quality of an FGS algorithm is measured by the variance of the functional to be sampled. Hence, the FGS provides a subtle shift in the computation paradigm and the analysis framework, which, as we shall elaborate, turns to show great promises as a simulation tool for high dimensional problems.

We focus on two of the most important types of initial quantum states: the Gaussian wave packet and the WKB data, and study the respective strategies in designing the density functions that balance the need of variance reduction and sampling convenience.
For the Gaussian initial condition scenario, we rigorously prove that, with the feasible choice of the sampling density, the variance of the functional to be sampled is independent of the scaling parameter
\varepsilon
. Furthermore, the number of samples to reach a certain sampling error
\delta
is in the order
{\mathcal{O}}\left(c^{m}\delta^{-2}\right)
, where
c
is a
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
constant and
m
is the dimension number. In particular, if one aims to halve the sampling error, only four times of the samples are needed, which is independent of the dimension
m
or the semiclassical parameter
\varepsilon
. Different from all the existing methods, this result shows that the number of wave packets does not to ”resolve” any spatial oscillations, and is of a sublinear scaling of the microscopic degrees of freedom. Besides, the computation cost grows rather gently as
m
increases, and the whole FGS algorithm is parallelizable, which makes high dimensional simulations rather affordable.
For WKB initial data cases, we propose a sequential sampling strategy leveraging the mono-kinetic structure of the highly oscillatory wave function.
We numerically verify that the sample size to reach a certain error is also insensitive to
\varepsilon
, which still outperforms the existing mesh-based algorithms whose computational cost grows rapidly as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

It is worthy emphasizing that, we only require that the initial condition to be in certain forms to carry out efficient initial sampling, but we do not require that the solutions remain in such ansatz forms. This is a natural consequence of the construction of the FGS algorithm, while most asymptotic methods break down when the solution ansatz is no longer valid. And we have carried a set of 2D numerical tests to illustrate such a property, where the initial Gaussian wave packet is scattered into various profiles by different potential barriers, and the FGS method is able to capture the correct evolution behavior with a reasonably small sample size.

Another main highlight of the FGS lies in that it is mesh-free in computing the physical observables. By taking advantage of path integral representation, the FGS succeeds in computing a wide class of physical observables without reconstructing the wave functions.
Therefore, if one is only interested in physical observables rather than the detained behavior of the wave function, the FGS serves as an efficient algorithm to simulate high dimensional Schrödinger equation, since it is a mesh-free algorithm of a sublinear scaling complexity and is naturally compatible with parallel computing. To demonstrate the performance of the FGS in computing observables, we implement the FGS to compute the physical observables in high dimensional quantum systems with a harmonic potential and it is shown numerically that the computational cost to reach a certain error is rather affordable in various dimensions. With the sample size quadrupling when the dimension number increases by one, we observe that the errors in the observables is uniformly bounded in all dimensions tested.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows: in Section 2, we review the basic setup the FGA and give the detailed procedures to compute the wave functions and physical observables with the FGS. We also present the error analysis framework of the FGS and elaborate the difficulties to be overcome when choosing the density function for the FGS. In Section 3, we focus on Equation (
1.2
) with Gaussian initial data and prove that the quantitative estimates of the FGS sampling error . In Section 4, we propose a feasible sampling strategy to apply the FGS on Equation (
1.2
) with WKB initial data. In Section 5, the numerical performance of the FGS is validated through five carefully designed numerical experiments, including simulating the Gaussian barrier potential and the caustics and computing the high-dimensional position and momentum observables without mesh.

2
The frozen Gaussian approximation and the frozen Gaussian sampling

In this section, we briefly review the forzen Gaussian approximation (FGA) to the semiclassical Schrödinger equation. The frozen Gaussian approximation is in the form of an integral on the phase space, which can be given a probabilistic interpretation. Then we present the frozen Gaussian sampling (FGS) algorithm as a general computation methodology for semiclassical quantum dynamics and discuss the practical implementation of computing physical observables based on the FGS algorithm.

2.1
The frozen Gaussian representation for the semiclassical Schrödinger equation

The frozen Gaussian approximation is an asymptotic approximation to the solution of Equation (
1.2
) with
{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon)
error
[
28
]
. It is based on an integral representation on the phase space:

u_{\mathrm{FGA}}(t,x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\varepsilon)^{\frac{3m}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}A(t,q,p)\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\Theta(t,x,q,p)}{\varepsilon}}\mathrm{d}q\mathrm{d}p.

(2.1)

Here the phase function
\Theta
is given by

\displaystyle\Theta(t,x,q,p)=S(t,q,p)+P(t,q,p)\cdot(x-Q(t,q,p))+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}|x-Q(t,q,p)|^{2}.

(2.2)

For a given initial condition
q
and
p
, the evolution of
Q
and
P
is governed by the classical Hamitonian
H(q,p)=E(q)+\frac{1}{2}|p|^{2}
and defines a trajectory on phase space
{\mathbb{R}}^{2m}
called an
FGA tracjectory
, which also determines the value of the amplitude
A
and action
S
. We name the variables
Q,P,S,A
as
FGA variables
. The evolution of the FGA variables is determined by the ODE system as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}Q(t,q,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}P(t,q,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\nabla E(Q),

(2.4)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}S(t,q,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|P|^{2}-E(Q),

(2.5)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}A(t,q,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{A}{2}\mathrm{tr}\left(Z^{-1}\left(\partial_{z}P-\mathrm{i}\partial_{z}Q\nabla_{Q}^{2}E(Q)\right)\right),

(2.6)

where we use the short hand notations

\partial_{z}=\partial_{q}-\mathrm{i}\partial_{p}\quad\text{and}\quad Z=\partial_{z}(Q+\mathrm{i}P).

Moreover, the initial condition is given by

\displaystyle Q(0,q,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q,

(2.7)

\displaystyle P(0,q,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p,

(2.8)

\displaystyle S(0,q,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(2.9)

\displaystyle A(0,q,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{\frac{m}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}u_{\mathrm{in}}(y)e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon}(-p\cdot(y-q)+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}|y-q|^{2})}\mathrm{d}y.

(2.10)

The derivation of the ODE system (
2.3
)-(
2.6
) is obtained through asymptotic matching. See
[
28
]
for detailed discussion on this issue. To summarize, with the help of the FGA, one can calculate the asymptotic solution to semiclassical Schrödinger equation (
1.2
) through solving an ensemble of ODE systems of the FGA variables instead of a high dimensional PDE.

In addition, we remark that the phase space integral (
2.1
) can also be viewed as an integral of the FGA trajectory. Denote the FGA tracjectory by
z_{t}=(Q(t),P(t))
, where we hide the dependence of the FGA variables on
(q,p)
for simplicity (we use the similar short hand notation in the rest of the paper). Then
z_{t}
satisfies the Hamiltonian flow and defines a one-to-one map
z_{0}\mapsto z_{t}
on the phase space. In other words, the FGA trajectory
z_{t}
is specified once the initial condition
(q,p)
is fixed and the FGA variables
S
and
A
are also specified with a given trajectory
z_{t}
. The idea of viewing the FGA ansatz (
2.1
) through FGA trajectory is important in the introduction of the frozen Gaussian sampling.

To conclude this subsection, we directly quote two theorems from
[
18
,
28
]
. The first theorem shows the FGA ansatz integration (
2.1
) reproduces the initial condition:

Theorem 2.1
.

For
u_{\mathrm{in}}\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
, we have

u_{\mathrm{in}}(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\varepsilon)^{\frac{3m}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}A(0,q,p)\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\Theta(0,x,q,p)}{\varepsilon}}\mathrm{d}q\mathrm{d}p,

(2.11)

where the initial amplitude
A(0,q,p)
given in Equation (
2.10
) is determined by
u_{\mathrm{in}}
.

The second theorem shows that the
L^{2}
error of
u_{\mathrm{FGA}}
with respect to the exact solution is
{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon)
:

Theorem 2.2
.

Consider the semiclassical Schrödinger equation (
1.2
) with the exact solution
u(t,x)
. Assume that the potential function
E(x)\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
. For any
t>0
, we have the following
L^{2}
error estimate result for the FGA ansatz (
2.1
):

\left\|u_{\mathrm{FGA}}(t,\cdot)-u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{A}(t,m)\varepsilon\left\|u_{\mathrm{in}}(\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}},

(2.12)

where
C_{A}(t,m)
is a parameter independent of the scaling parameter
\varepsilon
.

2.2
The frozen Gaussian sampling algorithm

We have seen in Section 2.1 that the FGA ansatz (
2.1
) is an integral on the phase space. With the FGA, the task of solving a PDE is converted into solving an ensemble of ODEs (
2.3
)-(
2.6
). However, difficulties still remain in computing the integral (
2.1
), which can be of high dimensions. Direct mesh method of numerical integration may suffer from ”the curse of dimensionality” and does not lead to a practical algorithm. To deal with the high-dimensional integral (
2.1
), we seek a stochastic method for calculation. To this end, we write the FGA ansatz in the form of an expectation and approximate the integral (
2.1
) numerically with a Monte Carlo method. The resulting stochastic algorithm is called the
frozen Gaussian sampling
(FGS), which also gives a mesh-free implement to compute physical observables. In this subsection, we elaborate the FGS algorithm and discuss its advantages and difficulties to be further investigated.

To write the FGA ansatz (
2.1
) into an expectation, we introduce a real-valued probability density function
\pi(\cdot)
on the phase space
{\mathbb{R}}^{2m}
. The density
\pi
then defines a probability measure
\mathbf{P}_{0}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2m}
:

\mathbf{P}_{0}(\Omega)=\int_{\Omega}\pi(z_{0})\mathrm{d}z_{0},\quad\forall\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2m},

(2.13)

where
\mathbf{P}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{2m})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2m}}\pi(z_{0})\mathrm{d}z_{0}=1
. With the probability measure, we can rewrite the FGA ansatz (
2.1
) as

\displaystyle u_{\mathrm{FGA}}(t,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi\varepsilon)^{\frac{3m}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}\pi(z_{0})\frac{A(t,z_{0})}{\pi(z_{0})}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\Theta(t,x,z_{0})}{\varepsilon}}\mathrm{d}z_{0}

(2.14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{z_{0}\sim\pi}\left[\frac{1}{(2\pi\varepsilon)^{\frac{3m}{2}}}\frac{A(t,z_{0})}{\pi(z_{0})}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\Theta(t,x,z_{0})}{\varepsilon}}\right].

Thus, we may use a Monte Carlo sampling for
u_{\mathrm{FGA}}(t,x)
:

u_{\mathrm{FGA}}(t,x)\approx u_{\mathrm{FGS}}\left(t,x;M,\left\{z_{0}^{(j)}\right\}_{j=1}^{M}\right)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\Lambda\left(t,x,z_{0}^{(j)}\right),

(2.15)

where

\Lambda\left(t,x,z_{0}\right)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\varepsilon)^{\frac{3m}{2}}}\frac{A\left(t,z_{0}\right)}{\pi\left(z_{0}\right)}\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}\Theta\left(t,x,z_{0}\right)}{\varepsilon}\right).

(2.16)

Here
\left\{z_{0}^{(j)}\right\}_{j=1}^{M}
are
M
independent identically distributed samples with the probability density function
\pi
. We refer to
u_{\mathrm{FGS}}
as the frozen Gaussian sampling (FGS) wave function. We also note that
\Lambda\left(t,x,z_{0}\right)
can be treated as a random variable related to
z_{0}
with the expectation

\mathbf{E}_{z_{0}\sim\pi}\Lambda\left(t,x,z_{0}\right)=u_{\mathrm{FGA}}(t,x).

(2.17)

Algorithmically, once we have sampled
z_{0}
, the FGA trajectory and FGA variables are available through evolving the ODE system Equation (
2.3
)-(
2.6
) up to time
t
. With enough samples of the FGA trajectories, we approximates the FGS wave function
u_{\mathrm{FGS}}
numerically by Equation (
2.15
). From the discussion above, we give the pseudo-code of the frozen Gaussian sampling (FGS) in Algorithm
1
. For simplicity, in the rest of this paper, we use the short hand notations to the FGA trajectories and FGA variables:

\displaystyle Q_{j}(t)=Q\left(t,z_{0}^{(j)}\right),\quad P_{j}(t)=P\left(t,z_{0}^{(j)}\right),

\displaystyle S_{j}(t)=S\left(t,z_{0}^{(j)}\right),\quad A_{j}(t)=A\left(t,z_{0}^{(j)}\right),

\displaystyle\quad\forall j=1,\cdots,M.

We note that analogous short hand notation may be also applied to other variables without confusion.

Algorithm 1

The frozen Gaussian sampling

1:

Initial sampling:
sample
M
independent identically distributed initial conditions
\left\{z_{0}^{(j)}\right\}_{j=1}^{M}
that obey the density function
\pi
on the phase space
{\mathbb{R}}^{2m}
.

2:

Initial amplitude computing:
for
j=1:M
, compute the initial amplitude
A_{j}(0)
by Equation (
2.10
).

3:

Trajectories evolution:
for
j=1:M
, evolve the ODE system Equation (
2.3
)-(
2.6
) up to time
t
with the initial condition given by Equation (
2.7
)-(
2.10
) to obtain the FGA variables
Q_{j}(t),P_{j}(t),S_{j}(t),A_{j}(t)
.

4:

Reconstruction:
compute the wave function or physical observable based on Equation (
2.15
) or Equation (
2.21
) respectively.

However, there are certain difficulties when actually executing the FGS algorithm, especially in high dimensional cases. First, to compute the initial amplitude, one needs to calculate the integral (
2.10
). For a Gaussian wave packet initial condition
u_{\mathrm{in}}
, the initial amplitude
A(0,q,p)
can be calculated explicitly in Equation (
3.2
), which has been pointed out in
[
22
]
. However, for a more complex initial condition
u_{\mathrm{in}}
such as a WKB initial condition, the explicit expression of
A(0,q,p)
is unavailable. To compute the initial amplitude (
2.10
), the computational cost of numerical integration is huge due to the high dimension and high frequency oscillation structure of the integrand.

Next, let us discuss the error analysis framework of the FGS wave function
u_{\mathrm{FGS}}
. Obviously, the
L^{2}
error function
E_{0}=\left\|u_{\mathrm{FGS}}-u\right\|_{L^{2}}
of the FGS wave function with respect to the exact solution can be divided it into two parts:

\left\|u_{\mathrm{FGS}}-u\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq\left\|u_{\mathrm{FGA}}-u\right\|_{L^{2}}+\left\|u_{\mathrm{FGS}}-u_{\mathrm{FGA}}\right\|_{L^{2}}=E_{\mathrm{FGA}}+E_{S},

(2.18)

Here
E_{\mathrm{FGA}}=\left\|u_{\mathrm{FGA}}-u\right\|_{L^{2}}
denotes the asymptotic error of the FGA ansatz and
E_{S}=\left\|u_{\mathrm{FGS}}-u_{\mathrm{FGA}}\right\|_{L^{2}}
denotes the Monte Carlo sampling error. Thanks to Throrem
2.2
, we arrive at

E_{\mathrm{FGA}}=\left\|u_{\mathrm{FGA}}-u\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{A}(t,m)\varepsilon,

(2.19)

where
C_{A}(t,m)
is a constant depending on time
t
and dimension
m
and independent of the rescaled parameter
\varepsilon
. However, with unreasonable choice of density function
\pi
, the Monte Carlo sampling error
E_{S}
may increase dramatically as the dimension number
m
increases, thus formidably many samples are needed. Therefore, to apply the FGS in high dimensional cases, variance reduction techniques such as importance sampling are necessary to reduce the number of samples. Ideally speaking, we aim to choose a reference density
\pi
which not only leads to a minimized sampling variance, but also easy to sample numerically. If we solely consider the variance reduction prospective, an obvious choice of sampling density can be given as follows:

\pi(z_{0})=\frac{1}{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2m}}|A(0,z)|\mathrm{d}z}|A(0,z_{0})|.

(2.20)

For a Gaussian wave packet initial condition, the density function given by Equation (
2.20
) actually defines a multivariate normal distribution and the resulting FGS algorithm is proven numerically practical and effective
[
22
,
21
]
. For a more complex WKB initial condition when the explicit expression of
|A(0,z_{0})|
in Equation (
2.20
) is unavailable, how to choose
\pi(z_{0})
is still unclear. As far as we know, no rigorous numerical analysis on sampling error
E_{S}
has been carried out prior to this work.

The aim of the rest of this paper is to investigate and overcome the above difficulties of the FGS. In Section 3, we prove rigorously that the sampling error
E_{S}
of FGS with sampling density (
2.20
) is independent of
\varepsilon
with a Gaussian initial condition. In Section 4, we introduce an asymptotic expression approximating the initial amplitude (
2.10
) when the initial data is of a WKB form and present a new initial sampling method of the FGS algorithm based on this approximation. In Section 5, we show various numerical examples to test the practical performance of the FGS with these new techniques, especially in high dimensional cases.

2.3
Computing observables without mesh

For many applications, the goal is not to approximate
the wave function in semiclassical Schrödinger equation (
1.2
) itself, but rather computing certain observables. To calculate an observable, reconstructing the wave function on a mesh is often neither feasible nor necessary, especially for high dimensional cases. In this subsection, we introduce a mesh-free method to compute a wide class of observables, which only use the FGA trajectories sampled by the FGS and doesn’t require the reconstruction of the FGS wave function. The method exhibits great advantages on computing high dimensional problems.

Let
O=\langle u|\hat{O}|u\rangle
where
\hat{O}
denotes a self-adjoint operator asscoiated with a physical observable. Then we compute
O
based on the FGS wave function
u_{\mathrm{FGS}}
as follows:

\displaystyle O_{\mathrm{FGA}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle u_{\mathrm{FGS}}\left(t;M,\left\{z_{0}^{(j)}\right\}_{j=1}^{M}\right)\right|\hat{O}\left|u_{\mathrm{FGS}}\left(t;M,\left\{z_{0}^{(j)}\right\}_{j=1}^{M}\right)\right\rangle

(2.21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(2\pi\varepsilon)^{-3m}}{M^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{M}\frac{\overline{A_{j}(t)}}{\left(\pi\left(z_{0}^{(j)}\right)\right)}\cdot\frac{A_{k}(t)}{\left(\pi\left(z_{0}^{(k)}\right)\right)}g_{j,k}\left(t,x\right),

where

g_{j,k}\left(t,x\right)=\left\langle\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\Theta_{j}(t,x)}{\varepsilon}}\right|\hat{O}\left|\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\Theta_{k}(t,x)}{\varepsilon}}\right\rangle.

(2.22)

We observe that the only dependence on
x
in Equation (
2.21
) is in the term
g_{j,k}
, which is an integration over the Gaussian wave packets. For a wide class of observables
\hat{O}
, the term
g_{j,k}\left(t,x\right)
can be carried out analytically through Gaussian integration formula, hence the observables
O=\langle u|\hat{O}|u\rangle
can be computed without mesh through computing the double summation in Equation (
2.21
). This class of observables include functions of the position variable and the momentum variable, which is in a polynomial form or can be approximated by a polynomial.

Now we take the position observable and the momentum observable as examples to show the analytical expression of
g_{j,k}
. For the position observable
\hat{O}=x
, we obtain

\displaystyle g_{j,k}\left(t,x\right)=(\pi\varepsilon)^{\frac{m}{2}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(Q_{k}(t)+Q_{j}(t)\right)+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\left(P_{k}(t)-P_{j}(t)\right)\right]\times\mathrm{e}^{\frac{i}{\varepsilon}\left(S_{k}(t)-S_{j}(t)\right)}

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{i}{2\varepsilon}\left(Q_{k}(t)-Q_{j}(t)\right)\cdot\left(P_{k}(t)+P_{j}(t)\right)}\times\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}\left|Q_{k}(t)-Q_{j}(t)\right|^{2}-\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}\left|P_{k}(t)-P_{j}(t)\right|^{2}}.

(2.23)

For the momentum observable
\hat{O}=-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\nabla_{x}
, we obtain

\displaystyle g_{j,k}\left(t,x\right)=(\pi\varepsilon)^{\frac{m}{2}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\left(Q_{j}(t)-Q_{k}(t)\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(P_{k}(t)+P_{j}(t)\right)\right]\times\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon}\left(S_{k}(t)-S_{j}(t)\right)}

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\varepsilon}\left(Q_{k}(t)-Q_{j}(t)\right)\cdot\left(P_{k}(t)+P_{j}(t)\right)}\times\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}\left|Q_{k}(t)-Q_{j}(t)\right|^{2}-\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}\left|P_{k}(t)-P_{j}(t)\right|^{2}}.

(2.24)

To sum up, given the FGA trajectories and FGA variables, we can reconstruct the observables in a mesh-free fashion without computing the FGS wave function. Clearly, the complexity for the physical observable evaluation (
2.21
) is
{\mathcal{O}}(M^{2})
. In the following sections, we shall further explore the dependence of the ensemble size
M
on the scaling parameter
\varepsilon
and the dimension number
m
and show great potential of the FGS in computing physical observables in high-dimensional cases.

3
Frozen Gaussian sampling with Gaussian initial conditions

In Section 3, we consider a
m-
dimension normalized semiclassical Gaussian wave packet centering at
\tilde{q}
with momentum
\tilde{p}
:

u_{\mathrm{in}}(x)=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}(\pi\varepsilon)^{-\frac{m}{4}}\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon}\tilde{p}\cdot(x-\tilde{q})\right)\exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{a_{j}}{2\varepsilon}\left(x_{j}-\tilde{q}_{j}\right)^{2}\right),

(3.1)

where
\tilde{p},\tilde{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
a_{j}(1\leq j\leq m)
are positive.

The semiclassical Gaussian wave packet is one of the simplest yet most important wave function in quantum dynamics, whose semiclassical limit arrives at a delta function on the phase space. With the Gaussian integration formula, we are able to calculate the initial amplitude
A(0,q,p)
(see Equation (
2.10
)) explicitly and the resulting density function
\pi
as shown in Equation (
2.20
) is Gaussian. Finally, we rigorously prove the sampling error
E_{S}
of the FGS with density function is independent of
\varepsilon
and discuss its dependence on the dimension number
m
.

3.1
Initial sampling and initial amplitude for Gaussian initial condition

Plug the Gaussian initial condition (
3.1
) into Equation (
2.10
), we calculate
A(0,q,p)
analytically with the Gaussian integration formula:

\displaystyle A(0,q,p)=2^{m}(\pi\varepsilon)^{\frac{m}{4}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left[\left(\frac{\sqrt{a_{j}}}{1+a_{j}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left(\tilde{p}_{j}-p_{j}\right)^{2}+a_{j}\left(\tilde{q}_{j}-q_{j}\right)^{2}}{2\left(1+a_{j}\right)\varepsilon}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}\left(a_{j}\tilde{q}_{j}+q_{j}\right)\left(\tilde{p}_{j}-p_{j}\right)}{\left(1+a_{j}\right)\varepsilon}+\frac{\mathrm{i}\left(p_{j}q_{j}-\tilde{p}_{j}\tilde{q}_{j}\right)}{\varepsilon}\right)\right],

(3.2)

and

\left|A(0,q,p)\right|=2^{m}(\pi\varepsilon)^{\frac{m}{4}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(\frac{\sqrt{a_{j}}}{1+a_{j}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\left(\tilde{p}_{j}-p_{j}\right)^{2}+a_{j}\left(\tilde{q}_{j}-q_{j}\right)^{2}}{2\left(1+a_{j}\right)\varepsilon}\right).

(3.3)

As we have stated, the probability density function
\pi(z_{0})
given by Equation (
2.20
) is a feasible choice of initial sampling. For Gaussian initial condition (
3.1
), the resulting probability distribution for the initial position and momentum
z_{0}=(q,p)
is a multivariate normal distribution centered at
q=\tilde{q}
and
p=\tilde{p}
with probability density function:

\pi(q,p)=(2\pi\varepsilon)^{-m}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(\frac{\sqrt{a_{j}}}{1+a_{j}}\right)\exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\left(\tilde{p}_{j}-p_{j}\right)^{2}+a_{j}\left(\tilde{q}_{j}-q_{j}\right)^{2}}{2\left(1+a_{j}\right)\varepsilon}\right).

(3.4)

Algorithmically, we can directly sample
z_{0}=(q,p)
from Gaussian density function in Equation (
3.4
), then the initial amplitude
A(0,q,p)
can be computed analytically.

3.2
Main result of sampling error

In this subsection, we aim to establish a rigorous error estimate for the FGS algorithm. We justify that the Monte Carlo sampling error
E_{S}=\left\|u_{\mathrm{FGS}}-u_{\mathrm{FGA}}\right\|_{L^{2}}
is independent of the scaling parameter
\varepsilon
and study the relationship between
E_{S}
and the dimension number
m
. With the error estimate, we are able to discuss the proper sample size for the FGS for given
\varepsilon
and
m
and illustrates the advantages of the FGS algorithm, especially in high-dimensional cases.

Recall that
u_{\mathrm{FGA}}
denotes the FGA ansatz defined in Equation (
2.1
) and
u_{\mathrm{FGS}}
denotes the FGS wave function given in Equation (
2.15
). Now that
u_{\mathrm{FGS}}
is determined by a series of random variables
\left\{z_{0}^{(j)}\right\}
on the phase space, we define the quadratic expectation of sampling error
E_{S}
and total error
E_{0}
as follows:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{S}(t,M)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{z_{0}^{(j)}\sim\pi}\left\|u_{\mathrm{FGA}}(t,\cdot)-u_{\mathrm{FGS}}\left(t,\cdot;M,\left\{z_{0}^{(j)}\right\}_{j=1}^{M}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

(3.5)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{0}(t,M)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{z_{0}^{(j)}\sim\pi}\left\|u_{\mathrm{FGS}}\left(t,\cdot;M,\left\{z_{0}^{(j)}\right\}_{j=1}^{M}\right)-u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(3.6)

Then plug Equation (
2.15
) into the expression of
{\mathcal{E}}_{S}
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{S}(t,M)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left[\mathbf{E}_{z_{0}^{(j)}\sim\pi}\left|u_{\mathrm{FGS}}(t,x)-u_{\mathrm{FGA}}(t,x)\right|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}x

(3.7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left[\mathbf{E}_{z_{0}^{(j)}\sim\pi}\left|\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\left(\Lambda\left(t,x,z_{0}^{(j)}\right)-u_{\mathrm{FGA}}(t,x)\right)\right|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left(\mathbf{E}_{z_{0}\sim\pi}\left|\Lambda\left(t,x,z_{0}\right)-u_{\mathrm{FGA}}(t,x)\right|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x=:\frac{I_{\mathrm{FGA}}}{M},

where

I_{\mathrm{FGA}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left(\mathbf{E}_{z_{0}\sim\pi}\left|\Lambda\left(t,x,z_{0}\right)-u_{\mathrm{FGA}}(t,x)\right|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x,

(3.8)

is regarded as the sampling variance of the FGS wave function. The derivation of Equation (
3.7
) uses the fact that
z_{0}^{(j)}
are independent identically distributed with density
\pi
. We aim to estimate an upper bound for the integral
I_{\mathrm{FGA}}
.

Before we present the main result of error estimate, we introduce the definition of the subquadratic condition. We say the potential function
E(x)
satisfies the subquadratic condition if there exists a constant
C_{E}
such that

\sup_{q\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left|\partial_{\alpha}E_{k}(q)\right|\leq C_{E},\quad\forall|\alpha|=2.

(3.9)

The subquadratic condition is commonly used in analysis of hyperbolic systems
[
28
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
and we also assume the subquadratic condition in our theorem. With all of the preparation, let us show the main result of this section:

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider the semiclassical Schrödinger equation (
1.2
) up to time
t
with the normalized Gaussian initial condition (
3.1
). Suppose that the potential function
E(x)\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
and satisfies the subquadratic condition (
3.9
). When applying the frozen Gaussian sampling (FGS) as in Equation (
2.15
) with the initial sampling density
\pi
as in Equation (
2.20
), there exists a constant
C_{0}(m,t)
independent of the scaling parameter
\varepsilon
and the initial data
u_{\mathrm{in}}
such that the sampling variance given in Equation (
3.8
) satisfies

I_{\mathrm{FGA}}\leq C_{0}(t,m)\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(\frac{1+a_{j}}{\sqrt{a_{j}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad\forall\varepsilon\in(0,1].

(3.10)

Moreover, if the assumptions in Lemma
3.6
are valid, the constant
C_{0}(t,m)
in Equation (
3.10
) follows

C_{0}(t,m)\leq\left(\gamma_{0}(t)\right)^{m},

(3.11)

where
\gamma_{0}(t)>1
is a constant independent of
\varepsilon
and
m
.

We leave the proof of Theorem
3.1
to Section 3.3 and discuss on the theorem first. As a corollary, the following estimate on the quadratic expectation
{\mathcal{E}}_{S}
and
{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
defined in Equation (
3.12
) and (
3.6
) is obvious:

Corollary 3.2
.

Under the same assumptions in Theorem
3.1
, we have the following estimates to
{\mathcal{E}}_{S}
and
{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
:

{\mathcal{E}}_{S}\leq\frac{\left((\gamma_{0}(t)\right)^{m}}{M}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(\frac{1+a_{j}}{\sqrt{a_{j}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(3.12)

and

{\mathcal{E}}_{0}\leq\left(C_{A}(t,m)\varepsilon\right)^{2}+\frac{\left((\gamma_{0}(t)\right)^{m}}{M}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(\frac{1+a_{j}}{\sqrt{a_{j}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(3.13)

To conclude this subsection, let us demonstrate the advantages of the FGS over the time-splitting spectral methods (see
[
1
]
) through the error estimate in Corollary
3.2
. For simplicity, we assume
a_{j}=1
for
j=1,2,\cdots,m
and ignore the asymptotic error
E_{\mathrm{FGA}}=\left\|u_{\mathrm{FGA}}-u_{\mathrm{FGS}}\right\|_{L^{2}}
of the FGA ansatz, then the
L^{2}
error
E_{0}=\left\|u-u_{\mathrm{FGS}}\right\|_{L^{2}}
can be approximated by the quadratic expectation
{\mathcal{E}}_{S}
, i.e.

E_{0}=\left\|u-u_{\mathrm{FGS}}\right\|\approx\left({\mathcal{E}}_{S}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2^{\frac{m}{2}}\left((\gamma_{0}(t)\right)^{m}}{M}}=\frac{\left(\gamma_{1}\right)^{m}}{\sqrt{M}}.

(3.14)

where
\gamma_{1}=2^{\frac{1}{4}}\left(\gamma_{0}(t)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
is another constant independent of
\varepsilon
and
m
. In order to guarantee that
E_{0}\leq\delta
, the sample size of the FGS should be more than

{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathrm{FGS}}=\frac{\left(\gamma_{1}\right)^{2m}}{\delta^{2}},\quad\delta>0.

(3.15)

In contrast, if we use a mesh-based algorithm such as time-splitting spectral methods in
[
1
]
to numerically approximate the semiclassical Schrödinger equation (
1.2
), the spatial step length of the mesh should be at least of order
O(\varepsilon)
to capture the highly oscillatory wave function. For an
m-
dimensional semiclassical Schrödinger equation, the spatial degree of freedom of the mesh-based method should be at least

{\mathcal{N}}_{\textrm{TSP}}={\mathcal{O}}\left(\varepsilon^{-m}\right).

(3.16)

Recall that the sample size
{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathrm{FGA}}
is the
m-
th power of an
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
parameter. Therefore, when scaling parameter
\varepsilon\ll 1
or the dimension number
m
is large, the computational cost to solve a given semiclassical Schrödinger equation with the FGS is much smaller than those with the grid based methods, i.e.
{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathrm{FGA}}\ll{\mathcal{N}}_{\textrm{TSP}}
.

Remark 3.3
.

We remark that though the sampling size
{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathrm{FGS}}
increases exponentially as
m
increases, we examine from numerical examples that the increasing rate of
{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathrm{FGS}}
is much smaller compared to
\varepsilon^{-1}
. For more information of this issue, see Example 5 in Section 5.3.

3.3
Proof of Theorem
3.1

Before we prove Theorem
3.1
, let us first collect some properties of the Hamiltonian flow on the phase space. In this subsection, we assume the potential function
E(x)\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
and satisfies the subquadratic condition (
3.9
) without special instructions.

For simplicity, we denote the map on the phase space from initial time 0 to time
t
as:

\displaystyle\kappa_{t}:

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2m}

(3.17)

\displaystyle z_{0}=(q,p)\longmapsto z_{t}=\left(Q(t,q,p),P(t,q,p)\right)=:\left(Q^{\kappa_{t}}(q,p),P^{\kappa_{t}}(q,p)\right).

For the transformation
\kappa
on the phase space, we denote its Jacobian matrix as

J^{\kappa_{t}}(q,p)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}\left(\partial_{q}Q^{\kappa_{t}}\right)^{T}(q,p)&\left(\partial_{p}Q^{\kappa_{t}}\right)^{T}(q,p)\\
\left(\partial_{q}P^{\kappa_{t}}\right)^{T}(q,p)&\left(\partial_{p}P^{\kappa_{t}}\right)^{T}(q,p)\end{array}\right).

(3.18)

Note that the transformation
\kappa
is canonical, i.e. its Jacobian matrix
J^{\kappa_{t}}
satisfies

\left(J^{\kappa_{t}}\right)^{T}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&I_{m}\\
-I_{m}&0\end{array}\right)J^{\kappa_{t}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&I_{m}\\
-I_{m}&0\end{array}\right),

(3.19)

for any
(q,p)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2m}
. Here
I_{m}
denotes
m\times m
identity matrix.

The following propositions give uniform bounds of the Jacobian matrix
J^{\kappa_{t}}(q,p)
and its derivatives.

Proposition 3.4
.

For given time
t>0
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there exists a constant
K_{0}(t,k)
such that

\sup_{(q,p)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2m}}\max_{\left|\alpha_{p}\right|+\left|\alpha_{q}\right|\leqslant k}\left|\partial_{q}^{\alpha_{q}}\partial_{p}^{\alpha_{p}}\left[J^{\kappa_{t}}(q,p)\right]\right|\leqslant K_{0}(t,k),

(3.20)

where
|\cdot|
denotes the matrix norm.

Note that Proposition
3.4
was proven in Proposition 1 of
[
28
]
.

For the canonical transformation
\kappa
, we define

Z^{\kappa_{t}}(q,p)=\partial_{z}\left(Q^{\kappa_{t}}(q,p)+\mathrm{i}P^{\kappa_{t}}(q,p)\right),

(3.21)

where
\partial_{z}=\partial_{q}-\mathrm{i}\partial_{p}
. The invertibility and boundness estimate of
Z^{\kappa_{t}}
can be proven by mimicking the proof of Lemma 5.1 in
[
20
]
. We summarize them into the following proposition.

Proposition 3.5
.

For given time
t>0
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there exists a constant
K_{0}(t,k)
such that

\sup_{(q,p)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2m}}\max_{\left|\alpha_{p}\right|+\left|\alpha_{q}\right|\leqslant k}\left|\partial_{q}^{\alpha_{q}}\partial_{p}^{\alpha_{p}}\left[\left(Z^{\kappa_{t}}(q,p)\right)^{-1}\right]\right|\leqslant K_{0}(t,k),

(3.22)

where
|\cdot|
denotes the matrix norm.

With the properties of the matrix
J^{\kappa_{t}}
and
\left(Z^{\kappa_{t}}\right)^{-1}
, we finally establish the estimate of the FGA variable
A(t,q,p)
:

Lemma 3.6
.

Consider the FGA variable
A(t,q,p)
that solves Equation (
2.6
) on the phase space. For given time
t>0
, there exists a constant
K_{1}(t,m)
such that

\sup_{(q,p)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2m}}\left|\frac{A(t,q,p)}{A(0,q,p)}\right|\leq K_{1}(t,m).

(3.23)

Moreover, if we assume the constant
K_{0}(t,1)
in Proposition
3.4
and
3.5
is independent of the dimension number
m
, there exists a parameter
\gamma^{*}(t)>1
such that

K_{1}(t,m)\leq\left(\gamma^{*}(t)\right)^{m}.

(3.24)

The proof is straightforward through Gronwall’s inequality.

Proof.

Based on Equation (
2.6
), we arrive at

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left|A(t)\right|\leq\frac{\left|A(t)\right|}{2}\left|\text{tr}\left[Z^{-1}\left(\partial_{z}P-\mathrm{i}\partial_{z}Q\nabla_{Q}^{2}E(Q)\right)\right]\right|.

(3.25)

Note that the constants in Proposition
3.4
and
3.5
are independent of the dimension number
m
, we use the constant
K^{*}(t)=K_{0}(t,1)
to estimate Equation (
3.25
):

\frac{1}{2}\left|\text{tr}\left[Z^{-1}\left(\partial_{z}P-\mathrm{i}\partial_{z}Q\nabla_{Q}^{2}E(Q)\right)\right]\right|\leq 2m\left(K^{*}(t)\right)^{2}.

(3.26)

Then apply Gronwall’s inequality to Equation (
3.25
):

|A(t)|\leq\mathrm{e}^{2m\left(K^{*}(t)\right)^{2}}=\left(\gamma(t)\right)^{m},

(3.27)

where
\gamma^{*}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{2\left(K^{*}(t)\right)^{2}}
is independent of
\varepsilon
and
m
.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

We remark that the importance of the three propositions and lemmas lie in the fact that the estimate to the FGA trajectories and FGA variables is uniform with respect to the initial phase
z_{0}=(q,p)
. Hence we can make global estimate on the integration

. Validity of the propositions and lemmas rely on the subquadratic condition of the potential function

.

After the preparation above, we finally move to the proof of Theorem

:

Proof.

(3.29)

2.16
) into Equation (

\displaystyle M_{(2)}(x)

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{FGA}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}M_{(2)}(x)\mathrm{d}x

4
Frozen Gaussian sampling with WKB initial conditions

In this section, we study the semiclassical Schrödinger equation (
1.2
) with the following normalized WKB initial condition:

u_{\mathrm{in}}(x)=a_{\mathrm{in}}(x)\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon}S_{\mathrm{in}}(x)\right),

(4.1)

where
a_{\mathrm{in}}
and
S_{\mathrm{in}}
are two real-valued functions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, which are both independent of
\varepsilon
. The initial amplitude
a_{\mathrm{in}}
follows

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}a_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}(x)\mathrm{d}x=1.

(4.2)

With the celebrated WKB approximation (see Appendix B for a brief introduction), the exact solution to Equation (
1.2
) has an asymptotic description of WKB type before caustics onset, provided that
a_{\mathrm{in}}
is smooth and decays rapidly
[
13
]
. Practically, we mainly consider the cases with the following Gaussian initial amplitude:

a_{\text{in}}(x)=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\pi^{-\frac{m}{4}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}(x_{j}-\tilde{x}_{j})^{2}\right),

(4.3)

where
a_{j}>0
are sufficiently big and
\tilde{x}_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}
(
j=1,2,\cdot,m
) are given parameters. We denote the mean of
a_{\mathrm{in}}
as

\tilde{x}=\left(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n}\right)^{T}.

(4.4)

The cases with general initial amplitude
a_{\mathrm{in}}
may be treated in a similar fashion and we refer to Remark
4.4
for discussion on this issue.

Compared to Gaussian wave packet initial data cases where the explicit expression of
A(0,q,p)
is available, the difficulty of applying the FGS in WKB initial data cases lies in that there is no natural choice for the reference density function
\pi(z_{0})
which achieves the balance between variance reduction and convenience of sampling. Moreover, the WKB approximation to Equation (
1.2
) may break down after caustics onset, which brings additional difficulties to the numerical approximation of Equation (
1.2
). In this section, we put forward a new initial sampling algorithm based on the stationary phase approximation to the initial amplitude (
2.10
). Then we validate the numerical performance of the FGS associating with the new initial sampling strategy through various typical examples in Section 5.

4.1
Description of the algorithm

As we have stated, one of the difficulties of the FGS algorithm with a WKB initial condition is that
A(0,q,p)
(given in Equation (
2.10
)) can not be calculated explicitly, hence there is no obvious way to do initial sampling. In this section, we apply the stationary phase method to approximate the initial amplitude
A(0,q,p)
in Equation (
2.10
) and give a practical sampling method based on the analytical approximation to
A(0,q,p)
. Before we start, let us summarize the stationary phase method into the following lemma:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\alpha(x)
and
\Psi(x)
be two smooth functions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. Suppose that
\alpha(x)\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
and
\Psi(x)\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
. Assume that
\nabla\Psi(x)
has a finite number of zero points
\{y_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
on the support of
\alpha
and
\det\left(\nabla^{2}\Psi(y_{k})\right)\neq 0
for each zero point
y_{k}
. We consider the highly-oscillatory integration

I_{\varepsilon}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\alpha(x)\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}\Psi(x)}{\varepsilon}\right)\mathrm{d}x.

(4.5)

As
\varepsilon\to 0
,
I_{\varepsilon}
has the following asymptotic behavior:

I_{\varepsilon}=(2\pi\varepsilon)^{\frac{m}{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}\Psi(x)}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{4}\mathrm{sgn}\left(\nabla^{2}\Psi\left(y_{k}\right)\right)\right)}{\left|\det\nabla^{2}\Psi\left(y_{k}\right)\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left(\alpha\left(y_{k}\right)+{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon)\right),

(4.6)

where the term
\mathrm{sgn}\left(\nabla^{2}\Psi\left(y_{k}\right)\right)
denotes the number of positive eigenvalues of the matrix
\nabla^{2}\Psi\left(y_{k}\right)
minus the number of negative eigenvalues.

A proof to Lemma
4.1
can be found in
[
3
]
.

According to Equation (
4.6
), the initial amplitude
A(0,q,p)
in Equation (
2.10
) can be calculated asymptotically as follows:

\displaystyle A(0,q,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{\frac{m}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}a_{\text{in}}(y)\mathrm{e}^{\frac{i}{\varepsilon}S_{\text{in}}(y)}e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon}(-p\cdot(y-q)+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}|y-q|^{2})}\mathrm{d}y

(4.7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{\frac{m}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left(a_{\text{in}}(y)e^{-\frac{|y-q|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}}\right)\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon}(S_{\text{in}}(y)-p\cdot(y-q))}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 2^{m}(\pi\varepsilon)^{\frac{m}{2}}\sum_{\nabla S_{\text{in}}(y_{k})=p}\frac{\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon}(S_{\mathrm{in}}(y_{k})-p\cdot(y_{k}-q))+\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{4}\operatorname{sgn}\left(\nabla^{2}\phi\left(y_{k}\right)\right)\right)}{\left|\mathrm{det}\nabla^{2}S_{\mathrm{in}}\left(y_{k}\right)\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\left(a_{\mathrm{in}}(y_{k})\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{|y_{k}-q|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}}+{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon)\right).

In order to illustrate the spirit of sampling strategy, we make some reasonable assumptions which simplify the asymptotic description of
A(0,q,p)
in Equation (
4.7
). The cases when the assumptions break down will be discussed in Remark 4.3. We will show the

Assumption 1
.

For any
p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, there exists at most one
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
such that
\nabla S_{\mathrm{in}}(y)=p
, i.e.
\nabla S_{\mathrm{in}}
is an injective mapping. The inverse mapping of
\nabla S_{\mathrm{in}}
is given by
T:p\mapsto y
. The definition domain of
T
may not be
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and we denote it by
{\mathcal{D}}(T)
, i.e.

T:{\mathcal{D}}(T)\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m},\quad p\mapsto y.

(4.8)

Recall that we assume the amplitude of the WKB initial data
a_{\mathrm{in}}(x)
decays exponentially as
x\to\infty
, the support of
a_{\mathrm{in}}
can be viewed as a small interval. Hence it is reasonable to assume that for any
p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, there exists at most one
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
such that
\nabla S_{\text{in}}(y)=p
and
a_{\mathrm{in}}(y)\neq 0
. With this assumption, the summation in Equation (
4.7
) can be reduced to only one term. Besides, we will show later in Remark 4.3 that the difficulties caused by multivalueness of the mapping
T
are not essential.

For simplicity, we use the variable
y
to denote the unique image of the mapping
T
defined in Equation (
4.8
) at
p\in{\mathcal{D}}(T)
in the rest of this section without special instructions.

The next assumption is a technique assumption without lose of generality:

Assumption 2
.

The inverse mapping
T
defined in Equation (
4.8
) is smooth and satisfies

\mathrm{det}\left(\nabla^{2}S_{\mathrm{in}}(y)\right)\neq 0,\quad\forall p\in{\mathcal{D}}(T).

(4.9)

Based on the two assumptions, the asymptotic approximation (
4.7
) of the initial amplitude
A(0,q,p)
can be simplified as follows:

\displaystyle A_{\mathrm{sp}}(q,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{m}(\pi\varepsilon)^{\frac{m}{2}}\chi_{{\mathcal{D}}(T)}(p)a_{\text{in}}(y)\exp\left(-\frac{|y-q|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\right)

(4.10)

\displaystyle\times\frac{\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon}(S_{\text{in}}(y)-p\cdot(y-q))\right)+\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{4}\operatorname{sgn}\left(\nabla^{2}\phi\left(y\right)\right)}{\left|\operatorname{det}\nabla^{2}S_{\text{in}}\left(y\right)\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}},

where
\chi_{{\mathcal{D}}(T)}(p)
stands for the indicative function on
{\mathcal{D}}(T)
. Then the modulus of Equation (
4.10
) is given by

|A_{\mathrm{sp}}(q,p)|=2^{m}(\pi\varepsilon)^{\frac{m}{2}}\chi_{{\mathcal{D}}(T)}(p)a_{\text{in}}(y)\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{|y-q|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\right)}{\left|\operatorname{det}\nabla^{2}S_{\text{in}}\left(y\right)\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

(4.11)

For Gaussian initial condition cases, we directly use the real-valued Gaussian function
|A(0,q,p)|
on the phase space as the sampling density
\pi
in Equation (
2.14
), which results in the minimum sampling variance. However, for the WKB initial condition cases, it is impossible to sample towards
|A_{\mathrm{sp}}(q,p)|
given by Equation (
4.11
) directly since the non-Gaussian function
|A_{\mathrm{sp}}(q,p)|
is hard to sample. To balance between variance reduction and convenience of sampling, we choose the following sampling density function:

\pi(q,p)=\frac{1}{{\mathcal{Z}}}2^{m}(\pi\varepsilon)^{\frac{m}{2}}\chi_{{\mathcal{D}}(T)}(p)a_{\text{in}}(y)\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{|y-q|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\right)}{\left|\operatorname{det}\nabla^{2}S_{\text{in}}\left(y\right)\right|},

(4.12)

where
{\mathcal{Z}}
is a normalization parameter to ensure
\pi
a probability density, i.e.

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2m}}\pi(q,p)\mathrm{d}q\mathrm{d}p=1.

We note that the only difference between
|A_{\mathrm{sp}}(q,p)|
in Equation (
4.11
) and the probability density
\pi
in Equation (
4.12
) lies in the exponent of the term
\left|\det\nabla^{2}S_{\mathrm{in}}\left(y\right)\right|
in the denominator. Hence the choice of
\pi
in Equation (
4.12
) is rather acceptable from the prospective of variance reduction. The following proposition shows that the probability density
\pi
in Equation (
4.12
) is also easy to sample, provided that the the amplitude
a_{\mathrm{in}}
is given by Equation (
4.3
):

Proposition 4.2
.

Consider the WKB initial data (
4.1
) where the amplitude function
a_{\mathrm{in}}
of is given by Equation (
4.3
). Assume that Assumption 1 and Assumption 2 are valid. Suppose that the random variables
{\mathcal{Q}}
and
{\mathcal{P}}
are sampled with respect to the probability density
\pi(q,p)
defined in Equation (
4.12
), then
{\mathcal{Q}}
and
{\mathcal{P}}
follows

1.

The marginal distribution of the random variable
{\mathcal{Q}}
is a normal distribution with expectation
{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}q=\tilde{x}
, where
\tilde{x}
as in Equation (
4.4
) denotes the mean of
a_{\mathrm{in}}
.

2.

Denote
{\mathcal{Y}}=T({\mathcal{P}})
, then the conditional distribution of the random variable
{\mathcal{Y}}
with respect to
{\mathcal{Q}}
is a normal distribution.

3.

The normalization parameter
{\mathcal{Z}}
in Equation (
4.12
) is given as follows:

{\mathcal{Z}}=2^{2m}\pi^{\frac{5m}{4}}\varepsilon^{\frac{m}{2}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\sqrt{a_{j}}\left(\frac{1}{a_{j}}+\varepsilon\right)}{a_{j}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(4.13)

Proof.

Recall that the mapping
T
as in Equation (
4.8
) is the inverse mapping of the gradient function
\nabla S_{\mathrm{in}}
. We refer to
{\mathcal{J}}_{T}(p)
as the Jacobian matrix of the mapping
T
at
p
. Based on Assumption 2, the determinant of
{\mathcal{J}}_{T}(p)
follows:

\left|\mathrm{det}{\mathcal{J}}_{T}(p)\right|=\frac{1}{\left|\mathrm{det}\nabla^{2}S_{\text{in}}\left(y\right)\right|},\quad\forall p\in{\mathcal{D}}(T).

(4.14)

Then we apply Equation (
4.14
) and the integral substitution rule to compute the marginal density
\pi_{\mathcal{Q}}(q)
of the random variable
{\mathcal{Q}}
:

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathcal{Q}}(q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\mathcal{Z}}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}2^{m}\pi^{\frac{m}{4}}\varepsilon^{\frac{m}{2}}\int_{{\mathcal{D}}(T)}\frac{\exp\left[-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{a_{j}}{2}(y_{j}-\tilde{x}_{j})^{2}-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}|y-q|^{2}\right]}{\left|\mathrm{det}\nabla^{2}S_{\mathrm{in}}\left(y\right)\right|}\mathrm{d}p

(4.15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\mathcal{Z}}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}2^{m}\pi^{\frac{m}{4}}\varepsilon^{\frac{m}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\exp\left[-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{a_{j}}{2}(y_{j}-\tilde{x}_{j})^{2}-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}|y-q|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\mathcal{Z}}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\sqrt{a_{j}}}{a_{j}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}2^{\frac{3m}{2}}\pi^{\frac{3m}{4}}\varepsilon^{\frac{m}{2}}\exp\left[-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{(q_{j}-\tilde{x}_{j})^{2}}{2\left(\varepsilon+\frac{1}{a_{j}}\right)}\right],

Then the marginal distribution of the random variable
{\mathcal{Q}}
is a normal distribution
{\mathcal{N}}(\tilde{x},\Sigma_{1})
, where the covariance matrix
\Sigma
is a diagonal matrix as follows:

\Sigma_{1}=\mathrm{diag}\left(\varepsilon+\frac{1}{a_{1}},\varepsilon+\frac{1}{a_{2}},\cdots,\varepsilon+\frac{1}{a_{n}}\right).

(4.16)

To calculate the expression of the normalization parameter
{\mathcal{Z}}
given in Equation (
4.13
), we just need to integrate
\pi_{\mathcal{Q}}(q)
by
q
to ensure that
\int\pi_{\mathcal{Q}}(q)\mathrm{d}q=1
, i.e.

{\mathcal{Z}}=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\sqrt{a_{j}}}{a_{j}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}2^{\frac{3m}{2}}\pi^{\frac{3m}{4}}\varepsilon^{\frac{m}{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\exp\left[-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{(q_{j}-\tilde{x}_{j})^{2}}{2\left(\varepsilon+\frac{1}{a_{j}}\right)}\right]\mathrm{d}q.

(4.17)

For fixed
{\mathcal{Q}}=q
, we denote the conditional density of the random variables
{\mathcal{P}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
with respect to
{\mathcal{Q}}
as
\pi_{{\mathcal{P}}|{\mathcal{Q}}}(p)
and
\pi_{{\mathcal{Y}}|{\mathcal{Q}}}(y)
. According to Equation (
4.15
), we compute
\pi_{{\mathcal{P}}|{\mathcal{Q}}}(p)
as follows:

\pi_{{\mathcal{P}}|{\mathcal{Q}}}(p)=\frac{\pi(q,p)}{\pi_{\mathcal{Q}}(q)}=\frac{2^{m}(\pi\varepsilon)^{\frac{m}{2}}}{{\mathcal{Z}}\pi_{\mathcal{Q}}(q)}a_{\mathrm{in}}(y)\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{|y-q|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\right)}{\left|\mathrm{det}\nabla^{2}S_{\mathrm{in}}\left(y\right)\right|}.

(4.18)

Then we can compute
\pi_{{\mathcal{Y}}|{\mathcal{Q}}}(y)
:

\displaystyle\pi_{{\mathcal{Y}}|{\mathcal{Q}}}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi_{{\mathcal{P}}|{\mathcal{Q}}}(p)\left|\mathrm{det}{\mathcal{J}}_{T}(p)\right|^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{m}(\pi\varepsilon)^{\frac{m}{2}}}{{\mathcal{Z}}\pi_{\mathcal{Q}}(q)}a_{\mathrm{in}}(y)\exp\left(-\frac{|y-q|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\frac{2^{m}\pi^{\frac{m}{4}}\varepsilon^{\frac{m}{2}}}{{\mathcal{Z}}\pi_{\mathcal{Q}}(q)}\exp\left[-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{a_{j}}{2}(y_{j}-\tilde{x}_{j})^{2}-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}|y-q|^{2}\right].

Note that the exponent term of the conditional density function
\pi_{{\mathcal{Y}}|{\mathcal{Q}}}(y)
is quadratic of
y
, therefore the conditional distribution of
{\mathcal{Y}}
with fixed
{\mathcal{Q}}
is a normal distribution. For fixed
{\mathcal{Q}}=q
, the conditional distribution of
{\mathcal{Y}}
follows
{\mathcal{N}}\left(\mu_{2},\Sigma_{2}\right)
, where the mean vector and covariance matrix is given by

\mu_{2}=\left(\frac{\varepsilon a_{1}\tilde{x}_{1}+q_{1}}{\varepsilon a_{1}+1},\frac{\varepsilon a_{2}\tilde{x}_{2}+q_{2}}{\varepsilon a_{2}+1},\cdots,\frac{\varepsilon a_{n}\tilde{x}_{n}+q_{n}}{\varepsilon a_{n}+1}\right)^{T},

(4.20)

and

\Sigma_{2}=\mathrm{diag}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon a_{1}+1},\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon a_{2}+1},\cdots,\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon a_{n}+1}\right).

(4.21)

∎

Motivated by Proposition
4.2
, one can sample the random variables
(q,p)
obeying the probability density
\pi
defined in Equation (
4.12
) on the phase space by sampling two normally distributed random variable
q
and
y
, where
p=\nabla S_{\mathrm{in}}(y)
. The initial sampling method bases on Proposition
4.2
can be summarized into the following algorithm:

Algorithm 2

Initial sampling of
(q,p)
on the phase space for WKB initial data (
4.1
) with amplitude (
4.3
)

1:

Initial position sampling:
Sample
q\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(\tilde{x},\Sigma_{1}\right)
, where
\tilde{x}
and
\Sigma_{1}
is given by Equation (
4.4
) and (
4.16
).

2:

Initial momentum sampling:
For specified
q
, sample
y\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(\mu_{2},\Sigma_{2}\right)
and compute
p=\nabla S_{\mathrm{in}}(y)
. Here
\mu_{2}
and
\Sigma_{2}
are given in Equation (
4.20
) and (
4.21
).

To conclude this subsection, we make two remarks on the initial sampling algorithm
2
. The first remark elaborate what happens when Assumption 1 is not satisfied. The second remark discuss the cases of WKB initial data (
4.1
) with general amplitude
a_{\mathrm{in}}
and generalize Proposition
4.2
to these cases.

Remark 4.3
.

Now We discuss the possible treatments when Assumption 1 is violated. If there exists more than one
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that
\nabla S_{\mathrm{in}}(y)=p
for some
p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the asymptotic approximation
A_{\mathrm{sp}}(q,p)
of the initial amplitude
A(0,q,p)
(see Equation (
4.7
)) can’t be reduced to only one term as in Equation (
4.11
), i.e.

\displaystyle A_{\mathrm{sp}}(q,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{m}(\pi\varepsilon)^{\frac{m}{2}}\chi_{{\mathcal{D}}(T)}(p)\sum_{\nabla S_{\text{in}}\left(y_{k}\right)=p}\left[a_{\text{in}}\left(y_{k}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{|y-q|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\right)\right.

(4.22)

\displaystyle\left.\times\frac{\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon}(S_{\text{in}}\left(y_{k}\right)-p\cdot\left(y_{k}-q\right))\right)+\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{4}\operatorname{sgn}\left(\nabla^{2}\phi\left(y_{k}\right)\right)}{\left|\operatorname{det}\nabla^{2}S_{\text{in}}\left(y_{k}\right)\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right].

The sampling density associating with
A_{\mathrm{sp}}(q,p)
in Equation (
4.22
) can be given through mimicking the density function (
4.12
):

\pi_{\mathrm{sp}}(q,p)=2^{m}(\pi\varepsilon)^{\frac{m}{2}}\chi_{{\mathcal{D}}(T)}(p)\sum_{\nabla S_{\text{in}}\left(y_{k}\right)=p}a_{\text{in}}\left(y_{k}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{|y-q|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\right).

(4.23)

Note that the density function
\pi_{\mathrm{sp}}
determines a mixture distribution and each component of the summation in the density function
\pi_{\mathrm{sp}}
can be sampled through algorithm
2
. Then it is natural to adopt the method of sampling the mixture model (see Section 4.2 in
[
4
]
) to sample
\pi_{\mathrm{sp}}
. Therefore, the difficulties resulted from the invalidity of Assumption 1 is not essential.

Remark 4.4
.

We discuss the possible treatments when
a_{\mathrm{in}}
is not Gaussian. Since
a_{\mathrm{in}}
is of a general profile, Proposition
4.2
no longer holds and there is no direct way to sample the probability density
\pi
given in Equation (
4.12
). To overcome these problems, we may approximate
a_{\mathrm{in}}
with a Gaussian amplitude
a_{\mathrm{in}}^{S}
and sample the following probability with Algorithm
2
:

\pi^{S}(q,p)=\frac{1}{{\mathcal{Z}}}2^{m}(\pi\varepsilon)^{\frac{m}{2}}\chi_{{\mathcal{D}}(T)}(p)a^{S}_{\text{in}}(y)\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{|y-q|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\right)}{\left|\operatorname{det}\nabla^{2}S_{\text{in}}\left(y\right)\right|},

(4.24)

where we have replaced
a_{\mathrm{in}}
in Equation (
4.12
) with the approximated Gaussian amplitude
a_{\mathrm{in}}^{S}
. Ideally, the density
\pi^{S}
balances the need of variance reduction and convenience of sampling such that it serves as the sampling density of the FGS when
a_{\mathrm{in}}
is not Gaussian.

5
Numerical examples

In the previous sections, we have elaborated the FGS algorithm with Gaussian initial data and WKB initial data and studied the sampling error with respect to the sampling size
M
, the scaled Planck parameter
\varepsilon
and the dimension number
m
. In this section, we present five typical examples using the FGS to numerically approximate the semiclassical Schrödinger equation (
1.2
) to illustrate the theoretical results on the sampling error and manifest the practical potential of the FGS in solving Equation (
1.2
), especially in high-dimensional cases with small
\varepsilon
. The numerical examples include exploring the relationship between the sampling error and parameter
\varepsilon
, applying the FGS in challenging cases such as caustics and computing the physical observable with the FGS in high-dimensional cases. The rest of this section is organized as follows. In Section 5.1 and 5.2, we investigate the cases with Gaussian initial data and WKB initial data respectively. In section 5.3, we compute the physical observables for a high-dimensional problem with the FGS and compare the numerical results with the exact value obtained through the Hagedorn wave packet method.

5.1
Examples with Gaussian initial conditions

Example 1: 1D Gaussian initial data examples

In this example, we aim to justify the theoretical results on the sampling error
E_{S}
in Section 3 through numerical examples. We implement the FGS to approximate Equation (
1.2
) at time
t=0.5
and study the relationship among the sampling error
E_{S}
, the scaled Planck parameter
\varepsilon
and ensemble size
M
.

In the Gaussian initial condition (
3.1
), let
m=1
,
a_{1}=2
,
\tilde{p}=-\frac{1}{2}
and
\tilde{q}=\frac{1}{2}
. Then we obtain the following Gaussian wave packet initial data:

u_{\mathrm{in}}(x)=\left(\frac{\pi\varepsilon}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\exp\left[-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\varepsilon}\left(x-\frac{1}{2}\right)-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\left(x-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\right].

(5.1)

Here we consider two different potential functions respectively: the harmonic potential
E_{1}(x)=\frac{x^{2}}{2}
and the torsion potential
E_{2}(x)=1-\cos x
.

Recall that the
L^{2}
sampling error
E_{S}
is defined as follows:

E_{S}\left(M,\varepsilon\right)=\left\|u_{\mathrm{FGS}}\left(t,x;M,\left\{z_{0}^{(j)}\right\}\right)-u_{\mathrm{FGA}}(t,x)\right\|_{L^{2}},

(5.2)

where
u_{\mathrm{FGA}}
denotes the FGA ansatz (
2.1
), which deviates from the exact solution to Equation (
1.2
) by the asymptotic error of the FGA ansatz. Note that
u_{\mathrm{FGA}}
is numerically unavailable, we choose the FGS wave function with a sufficiently large ensemble size
M_{0}
as an accurate approximation of the FGA ansatz

u_{\mathrm{FGA}}(t,x)\approx u_{\mathrm{FGS}}\left(t,x;M_{0},\left\{z_{0}^{(j)}\right\}_{j=1}^{M_{0}}\right)=\frac{1}{M_{0}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{0}}\Lambda\left(t,x,z_{0}^{(j)}\right),

(5.3)

where
M_{0}=1.5\times 10^{5}
is chosen large enough.

To compute the FGS wave function
u_{\mathrm{FGS}}
and
u_{\mathrm{FGA}}
, we use the fourth order Runge-Kutta method to solve the ODE system (
2.3
)-(
2.6
) in the time interval
[0,0.5]
with time step length
\Delta t=0.01
and reconstruct the wave function
u_{\mathrm{FGS}}
at
t=0.5
. We emphasize that
\Delta t
is chosen sufficiently small, hence the error due to the approximation of the ODE system is negligible. The wave function reconstruction is carried on the spatial interval
[-\pi,\pi]
. Also note that the FGS wave function
u_{\mathrm{FGS}}
with a fixed ensemble size
M
may vary with different initial samples
\left\{z_{0}^{(j)}\right\}_{j=1}^{M}
, the sampling error
E_{S}
is actually a random variable. Therefore, when approximating the FGS wave function
u_{\mathrm{FGS}}
, we average over numerical results obtained through 30 sets of initial samples for each simulation of
E_{S}(M,\varepsilon)
.

E_{S}\left(M,\varepsilon\right)

.

Computing physical observables in high dimensions

Example 5: High dimensional example

In this subsection, we aim to compute the position observable and momentum observable as follows:

\displaystyle O^{q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle u|\hat{O}^{q}|u\rangle,\quad\hat{O}^{q}=x,

(5.7)

\displaystyle O^{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle u|\hat{O}^{p}|u\rangle,\quad\hat{O}^{p}=-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\nabla_{x}.

(5.8)

where
u
denotes the exact solution to semiclassical Schrödinger equation (
1.2
). According to Section 2.3, the FGS provides a mesh-free algorithm to compute the position observable and momentum observable without reconstructing the FGS wave function
u_{\mathrm{FGS}}
. The physical observables computed via the FGS are denoted as follows:

\displaystyle O^{q}_{\mathrm{FGA}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle u_{\mathrm{FGS}}|\hat{O}^{q}|u_{\mathrm{FGS}}\rangle,\quad\hat{O}^{q}=x,

(5.9)

\displaystyle O^{p}_{\mathrm{FGA}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle u_{\mathrm{FGS}}|\hat{O}^{p}|u_{\mathrm{FGS}}\rangle,\quad\hat{O}^{p}=-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\nabla_{x},

(5.10)

where we refer to Equation (
2.21
) for the computing expressions of
O^{q}_{\mathrm{FGA}}
and
O^{p}_{\mathrm{FGA}}
. We compute the mean-square error of the quantities of the physical observables:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}^{q}(M,m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left|O^{q}-O^{q}_{\mathrm{FGS}}\right|^{2},

(5.11)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}^{p}(M,m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left|O^{p}-O^{p}_{\mathrm{FGS}}\right|^{2},

(5.12)

where we view the observables
O^{q}_{\mathrm{FGS}}
and
O^{p}_{\mathrm{FGS}}
computed via the FGS as a random variables, whose probabilistic distribution is determined by the FGS random samples
\left\{z_{0}^{(j)}\right\}_{j=1}^{m}
on the phase space. We aim to discuss the mean-square errors
{\mathcal{E}}^{q}
and
{\mathcal{E}}^{p}
with respect to the semiclassical parameter
\varepsilon
and the ensemble size
M
.

To approximate the mean-square errors, we compute

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}^{q}(M,m)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\mathcal{M}}}\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{M}}\left|O^{q}-O^{q(j)}_{\mathrm{FGS}}\right|^{2},

(5.13)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}^{p}(M,m)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\mathcal{M}}}\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{M}}\left|O^{p}-O^{p(j)}_{\mathrm{FGS}}\right|^{2},

(5.14)

where
O^{q(j)}_{\mathrm{FGS}}
and
O^{p(j)}_{\mathrm{FGS}}
are sampled by independent phase space samples. We choose the ensemble size
{\mathcal{M}}=60
in our tests. To compute the exact quantities
O^{q}
and
O^{p}
of the position and momentum observables which are analytically unavailable, we consider a special case when the quantities of
O^{q}
and
O^{p}
can be given explicitly by a semiclassical Gaussian wave packet named the Hagedorn wave packet
[
10
,
11
,
30
,
17
]
, which can be approximated numerically through evolving ODEs. For this special case, the position and momentum observables involved to the wave packet can be derived explicitly through the wave packet parameters. We directly quote Proposition 3.18 in
[
17
]
as the theoretical foundation of the Hagedorn wave packet method:

Theorem 5.1
.

Consider the semiclassical Schrödinger equation (
1.2
) with a quadratic potential
E(x)=\frac{|x|^{2}}{2}
. Let vectors
q_{H}(t),p_{H}(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and matrices
Q_{H}(t),P_{H}(t)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{m\times m}
be the solution to the following ODE system:

\displaystyle\dot{q_{H}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{H},

(5.15)

\displaystyle\dot{p_{H}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\nabla E(q_{H}),

(5.16)

\displaystyle\dot{Q_{H}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{H},

(5.17)

\displaystyle\dot{P_{H}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\nabla^{2}E(q_{H})Q_{H}.

(5.18)

Let
S_{H}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{1}{2}|p_{H}(s)|^{2}-E(q_{H}(s))\right)\mathrm{d}s
be the corresponding action function. Then the following complex Gaussian wave packet

\displaystyle u_{H}(t,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}S_{H}(t)}{\varepsilon}}(\pi\varepsilon)^{-\frac{m}{4}}(\mathrm{det}Q_{H}(t))^{-\frac{1}{2}}\times

\displaystyle\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\varepsilon}(x-q_{H}(t))^{T}P_{H}(t)Q_{H}(t)^{-1}(x-q_{H}(t))+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon}p_{H}(t)^{T}(x-q_{H}(t))\right),

is an exact solution to equation (
1.2
) of unit
L^{2}
norm for any
t\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
provided that:

1.

the initial matrices
P(0)
and
Q(0)
satisfies the symplecticity relationship, i.e.

\displaystyle Q_{H}(0)^{T}P_{H}(0)-P_{H}(0)^{T}Q_{H}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(5.20)

\displaystyle Q_{H}(0)^{*}P_{H}(0)-P_{H}(0)^{*}Q_{H}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{i}\mathrm{I}_{m},

(5.21)

where
\mathrm{I}_{m}
denotes the
m
-th order identity matrix.

2.

The initial condition of
u_{H}(t,x)
is exact, i.e.
u_{H}(0,\cdot)=u_{\mathrm{in}}
.

To apply the Hagedorn wave packet method to compute the exact values of the observables, we only consider the semiclassical Gaussian wave packet initial data (
3.1
) and fix the potential function as
E(x)=\frac{|x|^{2}}{2}
in this example. Moreover, in order to ensure
\left(q_{H}(0),p_{H}(0),Q_{H}(0),P_{H}(0)\right)
reproduce the Gaussian initial condition (
3.1
) exactly and satisfies the symplecticity relationship (
5.20
) and (
5.21
), the initial condition is chosen to be

q_{H}(0)=\tilde{q},\quad p_{H}(0)=\tilde{p},\quad Q_{H}(0)=\mathrm{i}A_{m}^{-\frac{1}{2}}U,\quad P_{H}(0)=A_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}U,

(5.22)

where
A_{m}=\mathrm{diag}\{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{m}\}
and
U
denotes any unitary matrix. With the preparations above, the exact solution to Equation (
1.2
) is of the form
u_{H}(t,x)
given in Equation (
5.1
), whose parameters are numerically available through evolving the ODE system (
5.15
)-(
5.18
). Now recall that the Hagedorn wave packet
u_{H}(x,t)
is a semiclassical Gaussian wave packet, hence the quantities of the position and momentum observables are analytically available:

\displaystyle O^{q}_{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle u_{H}|\hat{O}^{q}|u_{H}\rangle=\frac{\left[\det\left(\mathrm{Im}\left(P_{H}Q_{H}^{-1}\right)\right)\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}}}{\det\left(Q_{H}\right)}q_{H},

(5.23)

\displaystyle O^{p}_{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle u_{H}|\hat{O}^{p}|u_{H}\rangle=\frac{\left[\det\left(\mathrm{Im}\left(P_{H}Q_{H}^{-1}\right)\right)\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}}}{\det\left(Q_{H}\right)}p_{H},

(5.24)

where
\mathrm{Im}
denotes the imaginary part of the matrix. As a result, the observables
O^{q}_{H}
and
O^{p}_{H}
computed via the Hagedorn wave packet method serves as an accurate and efficient reference to the exact quantities of the physical observables in high-dimensional cases.

Now we move to design our numerical experiments. In the Gaussian initial condition (
3.1
), let the initial position and momentum to be

\tilde{q}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\left(1,1,\cdots,1\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m},

(5.25)

and

\tilde{p}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\left(1,1,\cdots,1\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}.

(5.26)

Moreover, the initial amplitude
\left(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{m}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is given as follows

a_{j}=1+\{0.2j\},\quad j=1,2,\cdots,m,

(5.27)

where
\{x\}
denotes the fractional part of a rel number
x
.

.

As shown in Equation (
3.12
), the sampling error of the FGS is relevant to the term
\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(\frac{1+a_{j}}{\sqrt{a_{j}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
, which is related to the initial amplitude. To avoid the influence resulting from the initial conditions, we choose
a_{j}
to be restricted in the interval
[1,2)
.

We aim to compute the mean-square error
{\mathcal{E}}^{q}
and
{\mathcal{E}}^{p}
given by Equation (
5.13
) (
5.14
) at
t=0.5
, where the exact quantities of the physical observables
O^{q},O^{p}
are approximate via Equation (
5.23
) and (
5.24
) associated with the Hagedorn wave packets. We use the fourth order Runge-Kutta method to solve Equation (

)-(

) associated with the Hagedorn wave packets and Equation (

)-(

) associated with the FGS with a sufficiently small step length
\Delta t=0.01
on time interval

. The computation is carried out on spatial interval

and the scaling parameter is fixed to
\varepsilon=0.0256
.

In figure

{\mathcal{E}}^{q}
and

with different ensemble sizes

and dimension number

6
Conclusion and discussion

In this work, we have developed a novel stochastic simulation methodology for the semiclassical Schrödinger equation that shows great promises for high dimensional simulations. Its numerical cost is insensitive to the semiclassical parameter, grows gently with respect to the dimension number and it provides an efficient way to compute the physical observables without reconstructing the wave function. The core component of the algorithm is to properly choose a density function, that achieves a balance between variance reduction and sampling convenience, and should be specified for different types of initial wave functions. Although we have already obtained satisfactory results for the Gaussian initial conditions, and the WKB initial conditions, more advanced sampling techniques, in theory, can be utilized in the framework of the FGS methodology, and hence further improve its performance. Besides, the idea of turning computing highly oscillatory waves into sampling and propagating wave packets may also be applied to other wave equations in the high frequency regime. Thus, this work initiates many directions that we shall explore in the future.

Appendix A:
The nondimensionalisation of the Schrödinger equation

When considering equations with dimensional physical quantities, one can scale all the variables by dimensional constant to yield dimensionless equations, named the process of nondimensionalisation, effectively leaving a ”clean” mathematical system with dimensionless magnitudes and dimensionless parameters.

In the case of Schrödinger equation (
1.1
), we choose the spatial and temporary characteristic scales
x_{c}
and
t_{c}
in order to make the scaled variable
\bar{x}=\frac{x}{x_{c}}
and
\bar{t}=\frac{t}{t_{c}}
dimensionless. By taking
\bar{u}(\bar{t},\bar{x})=u(t,x)
, Equation (
1.1
) can be rewritten as

\mathrm{i}\hbar\frac{1}{t_{c}}\partial_{\bar{t}}\bar{u}=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m_{o}x_{c}^{2}}\partial_{\bar{x}\bar{x}}\bar{u}+E(x)\bar{u}.

(6.1)

Then we multiply
\frac{\left(t_{c}\right)^{2}}{m_{o}\left(x_{c}\right)^{2}}
on both sides of Equation (
6.1
):

\mathrm{i}\left(\frac{\hbar t_{c}}{m_{o}x_{c}^{2}}\right)\partial_{\bar{t}}\bar{u}=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\hbar t_{c}}{mx_{c}^{2}}\right)^{2}\partial_{\bar{x}\bar{x}}\bar{u}+\frac{t_{c}^{2}}{mx_{c}^{2}}E\left(\bar{x}x_{c}\right)\bar{u}.

(6.2)

Note that the parameter
\varepsilon=\frac{\hbar t_{c}}{mx_{c}^{2}}
and potential
\bar{E}(\bar{x})=\frac{t_{c}^{2}}{mx_{c}^{2}}E\left(\bar{x}x_{c}\right)
are dimensionless, we obtain the dimensionless Schrödinger equation:

\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\partial_{\bar{t}}\bar{u}=-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\bar{u}+\bar{E}\left(\bar{x}\right)\bar{u}.

(6.3)

Finally, Equation (
6.3
) is actually identical with the semiclassical Schrödinger equation (
1.2
) if we remove the bars in Equation (
6.3
).

Appendix B:
The WKB approximation and the caustics

The WKB approximation is an asymptotic description of the exact solution to the semiclassical Schrödinger equation (
1.2
). To match the
{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon)
frequency oscillation structure of the wave function, we give the following WKB ansatz:

u(t,x)\sim u_{W}(t,x)=a_{W}(t,x)\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon}S_{W}\right),\quad\varepsilon\to 0,

(6.4)

where
a_{W}(t,x)
and
S_{W}(t,x)
are two real-valued functions. Through asymptotic matching at order
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
and
{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon)
, we derive the eikonal equation:

\partial_{t}S_{W}+\frac{1}{2}\left|\nabla S_{W}\right|^{2}+E(x)=0,\quad S_{W}(0,x)=S_{\mathrm{in}}(x),

(6.5)

and the transport equation:

\partial_{t}a_{W}+\nabla S_{W}\cdot\nabla a_{W}+\frac{a_{W}}{2}\Delta S_{W}=0,\quad a_{W}(0,x)=a_{\mathrm{in}}(x),

(6.6)

which solve the amplitude function
a_{w}(t,x)
and the phase function
S_{W}(t,x)
.

Note that the eikonal equation (
6.5
) is a nonlinear Hamiltonian-Jacobi equation and can be solved through the method of characteristic. The characteristic flow is given by the following Hamiltonian system of ODEs:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{x_{W}}(t,y)=\xi_{W}(t,y),\quad x_{W}(0,x)=y,\\
\dot{\xi_{W}}(t,y)=-\nabla_{x}E(x_{W}(t,y)),\quad\xi_{W}(0,y)=\nabla S_{\mathrm{in}}(y).\end{array}\right.

(6.7)

With the characteristic flow
X_{t}:y\mapsto x_{W}(r,y)
, the ODE system (
6.7
) yields a phase function as follows:

S_{W}(t,x)=S_{\mathrm{in}}(x)-\int_{0}^{t}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left|\nabla S_{W}\left(\tau,X_{\tau}^{-1}(x)\right)\right|^{2}+E\left(X_{\tau}^{-1}(x)\right)\right]\mathrm{d}\tau.

(6.8)

Note that the characteristic flow
X_{t}:y\mapsto x_{W}(r,y)
may not be one-by-one, hence the phase function
S_{W}
given in Equation (
6.8
) may be smooth only in a small time interval when characteristic flow
X_{t}:y\mapsto x_{W}(r,y)
doesn’t cross. The cases when
X_{t}
ceases to be a diffeomorphism are called caustics phenomena. See Figure 2 in
[
7
]
for an illustration of the caustics characteristic.

Before caustics onset, the WKB ansatz in Equation (
6.4
) is an asymptotic approximation to the exact solution to Equation (
1.2
) of order
{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon
), provided that the initial considition is exact and the amplitude
a_{\mathrm{in}}
decays rapidly (see Theorem 2.3 in
[
13
]
). After caustics forms, the exact solution may no longer be approximated by the WKB ansatz (
6.4
), hence the WKB approximation breaks down. The readers may refer to
[
2
]
for detailed discussion on the WKB approximation and analytic techniques after caustics onset.
