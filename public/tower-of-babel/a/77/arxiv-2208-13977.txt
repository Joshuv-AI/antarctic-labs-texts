𝒯 \mathcal{T} -matrix method for computation of second-harmonic generation upon optical wave scattering from clusters of arbitrary particles
Ivan Sekulic, Jian Wei You, Nicolae C. Panoiu

Abstract
We derive the 𝒯 \mathcal{T} -matrix formalism tailored for the numerical analysis of second-harmonic (SH) generation from arbitrarily-shaped particles made of centrosymmetric optical materials. First, the transfer matrix of a single particle is computed via the extended boundary condition method, in which the electromagnetic fields both at fundamental frequency and SH are expanded in vector spherical wave functions and the integral formulation is satisfied away from the surface of the scatterer. We allow for the accurate physical description of the SH sources by taking into account both local surface and nonlocal bulk polarization contributions to the nonlinear polarization density source responsible for the generation of the SH signal in a particle. This single-particle formalism is then extended to arbitrary distributions of particles by incorporating into the formalism linear and nonlinear electromagnetic wave scattering from the particles in the cluster. Importantly from a practical point of view, our method can be applied to particles of arbitrary shape made of optical materials characterized by general frequency-dispersion relations, so that it can describe the linear and nonlinear optical response of clusters of metallic, semiconductor, or polaritonic particles, as well as mixtures of such particles. The approach proposed here is faster and more memory-efficient than well-established numerical techniques, especially in the analysis of spheroidal particles, due to the favourable symmetries of spherical wave basis functions used in the wave scattering analysis.



I
Introduction

Fast and accurate full-wave computational analysis of resonant electromagnetic response of nanoparticles is of great interest to the scientific community, chiefly due to their widespread use in many areas of science and technology, including plasmonic nanoantennas, remote sensing, optical metamaterials, biology, nonlinear optics, imaging, and colloidal chemistry
Homola 2008
;
Pendry 1999
;
Liu 2008
;
Ziolkowski 2004
;
Hatab 2010
;
Butet 2015
;
Panoiu 2018
;
Boyd 1992
;
Ray 2003
, just to name a few. Modern nanofabrication tools, such as electron beam lithography, have made possible to produce particles of virtually any shape, thus the ability to accurately analyze the electromagnetic properties of nanoparticles with different morphologies is a prerequisite in the design process of certain modern optical systems. These nanoparticles are often used as building blocks of metamaterials, so-called
meta-atoms
, and can be shaped as crosses, split-rings, cuboids, spheroids, ellipsoids,
etc
.
Canfield 2006
;
Valev 2011
. Combined with deep-neural networks, fast numerical solvers have dramatically sped up the computer-aided design phase of the final structures
Malkiel 2018
;
Molesky 2018
;
Kudyshev 2020
.

Second-harmonic generation (SHG) is perhaps the most ubiquitous nonlinear optical process encountered in modern photonics. It consists of the generation of waves with frequency twice as large as that of an excitation (pump) optical wave that interacts with a nonlinear optical medium
Boyd 1992
;
Shen 2003
. The full-wave analytical solutions of this electromagnetic problem are restricted to a very few canonical geometries, such as infinite cylinders and spheres
Biris 2010
;
Pavlyukh 2004
;
Beer1 2007
;
Forestiere1 2014
;
Xu 2012
;
Sekulic 2021
. These solutions rely on the expansion of the fundamental-frequency (FF) and second-harmonic (SH) fields into Fourier series of cylindrical or spherical harmonic functions and the application of the tangential field boundary conditions at the surface of the particle. In this way, the Fourier series coefficients can be efficiently computed.

Numerical methods become indispensable when trying to go beyond these canonical geometries. The well-known finite-element method (FEM)
Silvester 1996
;
Jin 2002
and finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) method
Yee 1966
;
Taflove 2005
have been successfully applied to analyze the optical response at the SH of systems containing a small number of particles. Their drawbacks pertaining to the SHG analysis are inherited from the linear regime and can be traced to the discretization of the computational domain and application of absorbing boundary conditions in order to artificially truncate it. On the other hand, electromagnetic integral equations implicitly incorporate the radiation boundary condition at infinity making them particularly appealing candidates for tackling complex scattering problems
Mayergoyz 2005
;
Hohenester1 2005
.

The Poggio–Miller–Chang–Harrington–Wu–Tsai (PMCHWT) surface integral equation (SIE)
Poggio 1973
;
Wu 1977
is one of the most popular descriptions of the optical wave scattering from particles in the linear regime, chiefly due to its accuracy and resonance-free behaviour. It has recently been extended to the analysis of SH processes in centrosymmetric media
Makitalo 2011
;
Forestiere2 2013
. The PMCHWT integral formulation is defined on the surface of the scatterer, mapping the unknown equivalent surface electric and magnetic currents to the tangential field components. In its classical conforming Galerkin method of moments (MoM) numerical discretization
Harrington 1968
, the lowest order Rao-Wilton-Glisson (RWG) functions
Rao 1982
are used as current expansion and field testing set. These are sub-sectional basis functions defined on the tessellation of the particle surface (mesh), linked to the internal edges and straddling two adjacent triangles. This restriction of the RWG expansion functions to the mesh covering the particle surface implies that a large number of such functions are needed to adequately interpolate the unknown surface currents and consequently leads to MoM impedance matrices of particularly large size. Therefore, intense efforts have been invested into developing new approaches to accelerate execution of MoM-based codes by combining highly parallel and performance-oriented implementations with proficient algorithms aimed at reducing the computational complexity and execution time of the code
Song 1997
;
Bebendorf 2000
. However, even if one employs state-of-the-art acceleration tools, an efficient scattering analysis of clusters containing more than just a few nanoparticles is seriously impeded by the prohibitively large computational resources that are required.

In many instances of practical interest the particles under investigation exhibit axial symmetry,
e.g.
ellipsoids and cylinders, or the response of a cluster of particles with different shapes can be well approximated with one of an assembly of spherical scatterers. In these cases certain symmetries can be exploited by adopting vector spherical wave functions (VSWFs) as the basis functions for the expansion of fields and currents
Varshalovich 1988
;
Jackson 1999
. This approach is usually coupled to the EBCM
Waterman1 1965
;
Waterman2 1969
;
Barber 1975
, whereby the surface integral equations are imposed at some distance away from the boundary of the particle, in the null-field region. In this process, a
\mathcal{T}
-matrix of the system of particles relating the Fourier coefficients of the incident and scattered fields is introduced
Mishchenko1 1996
;
Mishchenko1 2007
.

A key strength of the TMM consists in the fact that the
\mathcal{T}
-matrices of particles in a cluster depend solely on the intrinsic physical properties of the particles. Therefore, they only need to be computed once for a particular frequency, as they do not depend on the angle and polarization of the incident field, nor on the arrangement of particles in the cluster. This feature makes the TMM uniquely suitable for calculations of certain physical quantities, such as radar cross sections. Furthermore, in the calculation of the cluster response, the multiple scattering (electromagnetic coupling) between nanoparticles is conveniently described using the translation-addition theorem for VSWFs
Cruzan 1962
;
Mackowski 1996
;
Stout1 2002
, and the
scattering matrix
of the whole nanoparticle aggregate is readily obtained from the single-particle
\mathcal{T}
-matrices and translation-addition matrices. Due to this key characteristic property of the TMM, it is alternatively called the multiple-scattering matrix (MSM) method. The TMM has been extensively developed and used in the linear scattering regime, including for particles of different shapes, sizes, and electric permittivity (see
Mishchenko1 2007
and references therein). It has been extended to the analysis of the SHG in centrosymmetric media, but so far, only the particular cases of spheres and infinite cylinders have been considered
Biris 2010
;
Xu 2012
;
Sekulic 2021
.

In this article, we present a rigorous computational analysis based on the TMM of optical wave scattering from particles made of centrosymmetric materials and of arbitrary shape. Several examples of axially symmetric particles, where the TMM performs the best, are also presented. In particular, we compare our computational predictions with results derived using analytical methods, where they are applicable, or otherwise with results obtained by employing commercially available software based on the FEM. Our study is structured as follows: in the next section, we describe the physics pertaining to the generation of the optical waves at the SH upon the interaction of optical fields applied at the FF with systems of nanoparticles made of centrosymmetric materials. Then, in section
III
, we introduce the TMM formalism describing the linear and nonlinear optical wave scattering from a single nanoparticle. We extend this concept to the multiparticle case in section
IV
, and illustrate how our method performs by considering a few relevant examples in section
V
. In the last section we summarise the main ideas of this work.

II
System geometry and the origin of SHG in centrosymmetric media

The central goal of this study is to develop a framework in which to analyze the SHG upon electromagnetic wave scattering from an arbitrary distribution of
N
particles made of centrosymmetric, non-magnetic and isotropic optical material, occupying region
V_{i}
and with electrical properties described by a frequency-dependent complex permittivity function,
\epsilon_{i}
, with
i=1,\ldots,N
. The particles are embedded in a lossless non-dispersive background medium
V_{e}
(usually vacuum) characterized by permittivity
\epsilon_{e}
(see Fig.
1
). The system is excited by a time-harmonic electromagnetic plane wave with unity amplitude,
\{\mathbf{E}_{inc}^{\omega},\mathbf{H}_{inc}^{\omega}\}
, oscillating at FF,
\omega
. In this study, we assume an
e^{-i\omega t}
time-harmonic variation of all fields and sources, but throughout the mathematical derivation of our results this multiplicative harmonic factor is omitted. The interior (
V_{i}
) and the exterior (
V_{e}
) regions are separated by an orientable surface
\mathcal{S}
equipped at each surface point with an outwardly oriented unit normal vector,
\hat{\mathbf{n}}
.

The nonlinear waves generated at the SH oscillate at frequency,
\Omega=2\omega
, and originate from two types of polarization sources. The centrosymmetric crystal lattice of the material forbids the generation of a local dipole polarization source inside the bulk of constituent medium. However, due to the inversion symmetry breaking in the vicinity of the surface of the particle, a dipole surface polarization density source is induced onto the surface. Although in general this interfacial component of the nonlinear polarization describes accurately enough many of the properties of the experimentally measured SHG signal from metallic particles, for an accurate analysis of nonlinear wave scattering from dielectric particles, a nonlocal polarization source distribution generated inside the bulk of the particle and originating from the induced nonlinear electric quadrupoles and nonlinear magnetic dipoles has to be properly taken into account
Timbrell 2018
.

The nonlinear surface polarization density is induced within a few atomic layers just underneath the surface of the particle and is related to the electric field applied at the FF
via
a third-rank surface susceptibility tensor,
\hat{\bm{\chi}}_{s}^{(2)}

Boyd 1992
;
Shen 2003
:

\mathbf{P}_{s}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})=\epsilon_{0}\hat{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{s}^{(2)}:\mathbf{E}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r})\mathbf{E}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r})\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{s}),

(1)

where
\epsilon_{0}
is the vacuum electric permittivity and the Dirac function
\delta(\mathbf{r})
ensures that the applied electric field is evaluated onto the surface,
\mathbf{r}_{s}\in\mathcal{S}
(more precisely, the field must be evaluated at a point located on the inner side of the particle surface). The susceptibility tensor
\hat{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{s}^{(2)}
is defined at boundary points
\mathbf{r}_{s}
, and is expressed in a coordinate system defined by a local right-handed orthonormal triplet of vectors
(\hat{\mathbf{n}},\hat{\mathbf{t}}_{1},\hat{\mathbf{t}}_{2})
, where
\hat{\mathbf{t}}_{1}
and
\hat{\mathbf{t}}_{2}
are unit vectors tangential to the surface at
\mathbf{r}_{s}
. Assuming isotropic surface symmetry, only three tensor components are nonzero and independent:
\chi_{s,\perp\perp\perp}^{(2)}
,
\chi_{s,\perp\parallel\parallel}^{(2)}
, and
\chi_{s,\parallel\perp\parallel}^{(2)}=\chi_{s,\parallel\parallel\perp}^{(2)}

Shen 2003
, where the symbols
\perp
and
\parallel
refer to the directions perpendicular and parallel to the boundary, respectively. Taking this into account,
\mathbf{P}_{s}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}_{s})
can be expressed as
Dadap 2004
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{s}^{\Omega}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{r}_{s})=\epsilon_{0}\left\{\hat{\mathbf{n}}\left[\chi_{s,\perp\perp\perp}^{(2)}E_{n}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r}_{s})E_{n}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r}_{s})\right.\right.

(2)

\displaystyle+\left.\left.\chi_{s,\perp\parallel\parallel}^{(2)}E_{t}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r}_{s})E_{t}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r}_{s})\right]+2\chi_{s,\parallel\perp\parallel}^{(2)}E_{n}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r}_{s})\mathbf{E}_{t}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r}_{s})\right\},

where
\mathbf{E}_{n}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r}_{s})
and
\mathbf{E}_{t}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r}_{s})
are normal and tangential components of the linear field
\mathbf{E}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r})
taken at the inner side of the surface
\mathcal{S}
, at point
\mathbf{r}_{s}
,
i.e.

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{n}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r}_{s})

\displaystyle=\left[\hat{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\mathbf{E}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r})|_{\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}_{s}}\right]\hat{\mathbf{n}},

(3a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{t}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r}_{s})

\displaystyle=-\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\mathbf{E}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r})|_{\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}_{s}}.

(3b)

The nonlocal bulk polarization is induced in the interior of the particle by the fundamental field and its spatial derivatives, and is described by the following expression:

\mathbf{P}_{b}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})=\epsilon_{0}\hat{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{b}^{(2)}\vdots\mathbf{E}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r})\nabla\mathbf{E}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r}),\quad\mathbf{r}\in V_{i},

(4)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{b}^{(2)}
is a fourth-rank bulk susceptibility tensor. This polarization can be alternatively expressed as
Bloembergen 1968
:

\mathbf{P}_{b}^{\Omega}=\epsilon_{0}\left[\beta\mathbf{E}^{\omega}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}^{\omega}+\gamma\nabla(\mathbf{E}^{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{E}^{\omega})+\delta^{\prime}(\mathbf{E}^{\omega}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{E}^{\omega}\right],

(5)

with
\beta,\gamma
, and
\delta^{\prime}
being material material parameters. The first term in this equation vanishes because we assume that for all the particles there are no free charges, so that
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}^{\omega}=0
. The relative magnitude of the third term as compared to the second one is a question that is still debated. Thus, the contribution of the third term increases when the field at the FF inside the particle becomes strongly inhomogeneous, as it is the case around steep variations of the morphology of the scatterer or nearby sharp corners where so-called
hot spots
can form. Since here we are mostly concerned with axially symmetric and smooth particles, rapid variations of the linear field are not expected and therefore we set
\delta^{\prime}=0
as in fact most authors do
Sipe1 1980
. It should be noted, however, that the term proportional to
\delta^{\prime}
can be accounted for in a rigorous description of generation of SH upon optical wave scattering from particles, but the mathematical descrition can become quite intricate even in the simple case of a single particle
Forestiere1 2014
. Finally, the total nonlinear polarization density source is equal to the sum of the surface and bulk components:

\mathbf{P}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})=\mathbf{P}_{b}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})+\mathbf{P}_{s}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}),\quad\mathbf{r}\in V_{i}.

(6)

The numerical and analytical methods used in the treatment of the SH signal are almost exclusively developed within the so-calledundepleted-pump approximation. In this framework it is assumed that no back-coupling between the nonlinear and linear fields occurs,
i.e.
the down-conversion process is disregarded. This assumption is valid in most situations of practical interest due to the very low energy conversion efficiency of the SHG process.

III
The transfer-matrix method for single particle scattering

In this section, we introduce the mathematical apparatus characteristic to the EBCM, both at the FF and SH. More specifically, we discretize the associated integral operators in a basis consisting of VSWFs and establish a relation between the Fourier coefficients of the series expansions in this basis of the scattered and excitation fields. Importantly, this relation defines the
\mathcal{T}
-matrix of the particle, which is at the heart of the TMM.

III.1
Linear scattering regime

The linear part of our TMM, which is used to determine the FF distribution around an arbitrarily shaped particle, is constructed in a well-known manner described in detail in the literature (see, for example, Ref.
Tsang 1985
). For the sake of completeness and to facilitate the presentation of the nonlinear part of the algorithm, here we will briefly outline the main steps of the derivation, whereas the explicit formulae for the
\mathcal{T}
-matrix elements are given in Appendix A.

We start by invoking the surface equivalence principle, which divides the original physical problem into two problems equivalent to the initial one. The first problem is concerned with the exterior domain
V_{e}
and states that the fields in this region are uniquely determined by the rotated tangential traces of the total fields located at the boundary
\mathcal{S}=\partial V_{i}
, the equivalent electric and magnetic currents defined as
\mathbf{J}_{e}^{\omega}=\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}\times\mathbf{H}_{e}^{\omega}
and
\mathbf{M}_{e}^{\omega}=-\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}\times\mathbf{E}_{e}^{\omega}
, and the incident fields
\left\{\mathbf{E}_{inc}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r}),\mathbf{H}_{inc}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r})\right\}
. These (non-physical) equivalent currents radiate outwardly in
V_{e}
the scattered fields
\left\{\mathbf{E}_{s}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r}),\mathbf{H}_{s}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r})\right\}
and exactly cancel the incident fields inside
V_{i}
, so as the equalities
\mathbf{E}_{e}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r})=\mathbf{E}_{s}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r})+\mathbf{E}_{inc}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r})
and
\mathbf{H}_{e}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r})=\mathbf{H}_{s}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r})+\mathbf{H}_{inc}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r})
hold throughout the domain
V_{e}
. These conditions are mathematically expressed by the Stratton-Chu integral field representations
Stratton 1939
:

\displaystyle\oiint_{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle\left\{i\omega\mu_{0}\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{e}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\cdot\left[\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}\times\mathbf{H}_{e}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\right]+\left[\nabla\times\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{e}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.\cdot\left[\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}\times\mathbf{E}_{e}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\right]\right\}dS+\mathbf{E}_{inc}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r})=0,\quad\mathbf{r}\in V_{i},

(7a)

\displaystyle\oiint_{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle\left\{i\omega\mu_{0}\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{e}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\cdot\left[\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}\times\mathbf{H}_{e}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\right]+\left[\nabla\times\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{e}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.\cdot\left[\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}\times\mathbf{E}_{e}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\right]\right\}dS=\mathbf{E}_{s}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r}),\quad\mathbf{r}\in V_{e},

(7b)

where the primed coordinates refer to the integration points on
\mathcal{S}
. According to the first equivalent problem, the electromagnetic medium filling
V_{i}
is irrelevant for the field solution in
V_{e}
, so that we can in principle replace it, to our convenience, with the same background material occupying
V_{e}
. This way we can use the dyadic Green’s function of the infinite space,
\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{e}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})

\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{e}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\left[\hat{\mathbf{I}}+\frac{\nabla\nabla}{(k_{e}^{\omega})^{2}}\right]\frac{e^{ik_{e}^{\omega}|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|}}{4\pi|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|},

(8)

where
\hat{\mathbf{I}}
is the unit dyad and
k_{e}^{\omega}=\omega\sqrt{\epsilon_{e}\mu_{0}}
is the wave number in the background medium at the FF. The integral field representations (
7
) form the basis of EBCM. Since the equivalent currents radiate in
V_{i}
fields that exactly cancel the incident fields, these equations are sometimes called the
null-field
or
extinction

Chew 2009

The electric fields incident, scattered, and internal to the particle are expanded in VSWFs

;
Jackson 1999

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{inc}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{\nu\geq 1}\left[q_{\nu}^{\omega}\mathbf{M}_{\nu}^{(1)}(k_{e}^{\omega}\mathbf{r})+p_{\nu}^{\omega}\mathbf{N}_{\nu}^{(1)}(k_{e}^{\omega}\mathbf{r})\right],\quad\mathbf{r}\in V_{e},

(9a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{s}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{\nu\geq 1}\left[b_{\nu}^{\omega}\mathbf{M}_{\nu}^{(3)}(k_{e}^{\omega}\mathbf{r})+a_{\nu}^{\omega}\mathbf{N}_{\nu}^{(3)}(k_{e}^{\omega}\mathbf{r})\right],\quad\mathbf{r}\in V_{e},

(9b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{i}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{\nu\geq 1}\left[c_{\nu}^{\omega}\mathbf{M}_{\nu}^{(1)}(k_{i}^{\omega}\mathbf{r})+d_{\nu}^{\omega}\mathbf{N}_{\nu}^{(1)}(k_{i}^{\omega}\mathbf{r})\right],\quad\mathbf{r}\in V_{i},

(9c)

where
k_{i}^{\omega}=\omega\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}^{\omega}\mu_{0}}
is the wave number at the FF of the medium filling the particle. The incident and the internal fields are expanded in a basis of regular spherical functions
\{\mathbf{M}_{\nu}^{(1)},\mathbf{N}_{\nu}^{(1)}\}
, which are finite at the origin, the corresponding expansion coefficients being
\{q_{\nu}^{\omega},p_{\nu}^{\omega}\}
and

In particular, they are given in Appendix B for the specific case of an incident linearly polarized plane wave. On the other hand, the expansion coefficients of the internal and scattered fields are unknown and their calculation is the main goal of the TMM.

Combining Eqs. (
13
) and (
14
), we arrive to the
\mathcal{T}
-matrix that maps the Fourier coefficients of the incident field to those of the scattering field,

\mathbf{T}^{\omega}\mathbf{g}^{\omega}=\mathbf{f}^{\omega},

(15)

with the
\mathcal{T}
-matrix of the particle given by the product of
Q
-matrices
Mishchenko1 1996
:

\mathbf{T}^{\omega}=-\mathbf{Q}^{\omega(1,1)}\left[\mathbf{Q}^{\omega(1,3)}\right]^{-1}.

(16)

Although in principle this formalism can be applied to scatterers of practically any shape, because of the spherical nature of the underlying expansion functions, the TMM is especially suitable for the analysis of optical wave scattering from particles with axial symmetry, such as ellipsoids and cylinders. In these cases, the numerical method converges relatively fast. More precisely, both the near- and far-field physical quantities can be accurately calculated using a fairly small number of VSWFs in the field expansions. Moreover, the VSWFs can be viewed as
entire-domain
basis functions,
i.e.
they are defined irrespective of the surface discretization mesh, so that the constraints pertaining to the mesh generation process can be more relaxed when compared to the MoM. Specifically, the RWG basis functions used in the MoM are edge-based sub-sectional based, so that the underlying surface discretization mesh has to be conformal with all pairs of adjacent facets sharing a certain edge. On the other hand, the surface discretization mesh needed for the evaluation of the elements of the
\mathcal{T}
-matrix can be nonconformal as well, where adjacent triangles may not share single edges. This is a truly significant alleviation on the mesh generation constraints. However, if the shape of the particle is highly irregular, with sharp corners, tips, and deep corrugations, strongly inhomogeneous near-fields are induced. In such cases, MoM could be preferable to TMM since the flexibility provided by the RWG basis functions allows one to accurately interpolate the rapidly varying surface current densities
Sekulic 2019
.

III.2
Wave scattering at the second-harmonic

Unlike the linear electromagnetic field, whose sources are not contained in the physical domain of interest, the nonlinear fields are generated by sources (nonlinear polarization) distributed inside the particle or at its surface. Hence, the electromagnetic fields oscillating at the SH frequency,
\Omega
, must satisfy the following
inhomogeneous
Maxwell equations valid inside the particle:

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{H}_{i}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})+i\Omega\epsilon_{i}^{\Omega}\mathbf{E}_{i}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})=-i\Omega\mathbf{P}_{b}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}),\quad\mathbf{r}\in V_{i},

(17a)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{E}_{i}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})-i\Omega\mu_{0}\mathbf{H}_{i}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})=0,\quad\mathbf{r}\in V_{i},

(17b)

and the homogeneous system of equations defined in the surrounding medium:

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{H}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})+i\Omega\epsilon_{e}\mathbf{E}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})=0,\quad\mathbf{r}\in V_{e},

(18a)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{E}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})-i\Omega\mu_{0}\mathbf{H}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})=0,\quad\mathbf{r}\in V_{e}.

(18b)

The external field solutions
\left\{\mathbf{E}_{e}^{\Omega},\mathbf{H}_{e}^{\Omega}\right\}
at the SH must satisfy in the far-field region the Silver-Müller radiation condition, whereas on the boundary of the particle the system of Maxwell equations is supplemented with a set of
nonlinear
tangential field boundary conditions:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\left[\mathbf{E}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})-\mathbf{E}_{i}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})\right]=-\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{\Omega},\quad\mathbf{r}\in\mathcal{S},

(19a)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\left[\mathbf{H}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})-\mathbf{H}_{i}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})\right]=\bm{\mathcal{J}}^{\Omega},\quad\mathbf{r}\in\mathcal{S}.

(19b)

It can be seen from these equations that, unlike the linear case described by Eqs. (
12
), the tangent fields at the SH are discontinuous. These field discontinuities are induced by the surface nonlinear polarization, and can be described by introducing nonlinear surface electric and magnetic currents,
\bm{\mathcal{J}}^{\Omega}
and
\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{\Omega}
, respectively. Specifically, these nonlinear surface currents are related to the surface nonlinear polarization density as
Heinz 1991
:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{J}}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}_{s})=i\Omega\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\left[\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\bm{\mathbf{P}}_{s}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}_{s})\right],

(20a)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}_{s})=\frac{1}{\epsilon_{0}}\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\nabla_{S}\left[\hat{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{P}}_{s}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}_{s})\right],

(20b)

where the operator
\nabla_{S}
is defined on a plane tangent to the surface at the field evaluation point.

We follow the well-known technique of solving inhomogeneous systems of partial differential equations by seeking the solution as the sum of the general solution of the associated homogeneous system satisfying the homogeneous boundary conditions (
12
) and a particular solution of the inhomogeneous system obeying the nonlinear boundary conditions (
19
). Applying this procedure to the Maxwell equations at the the SH (
17
), we look for the electromagnetic fields
\left\{\bar{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\Omega},\bar{\mathbf{H}}_{i}^{\Omega}\right\}
satisfying the homogeneous system of equations obtained from Eqs. (
17
) by setting
\mathbf{P}_{b}^{\Omega}=0
, and a particular solution
\left\{\mathbf{E}_{i,p}^{\Omega},\mathbf{H}_{i,p}^{\Omega}\right\}
that satisfies the inhomogeneous system (
17
).

As can be easily verified by direct substitution into Eqs. (
17
), one particular solution to Eqs. (
17
) is:

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{i,p}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})=0,\quad\mathbf{r}\in V_{i},

(21a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{i,p}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})=-\frac{1}{\epsilon_{i}^{\Omega}}\mathbf{P}_{b}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}),\quad\mathbf{r}\in V_{i},

(21b)

so that the full solution to the system (
17
) is:

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{i}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})=\bar{\mathbf{H}}_{i}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})+\mathbf{H}_{i,p}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}),\quad\mathbf{r}\in V_{i},

(22a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{i}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})=\bar{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})+\mathbf{E}_{i,p}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}),\quad\mathbf{r}\in V_{i}.

(22b)

Solutions
\left\{\bar{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\Omega},\bar{\mathbf{H}}_{i}^{\Omega}\right\}
and
\left\{\mathbf{E}_{e}^{\Omega},\mathbf{H}_{e}^{\Omega}\right\}
of the corresponding homogeneous systems of equations valid inside and outside the particle, respectively, must obey the following boundary conditions on the particle surface,
\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times[\mathbf{E}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})-\bar{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})]=-\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})+\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\mathbf{E}_{i,p}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}),\quad\mathbf{r}\in\mathcal{S},

(23a)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times[\mathbf{H}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})-\bar{\mathbf{H}}_{i}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})]=\bm{\mathcal{J}}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}),\quad\mathbf{r}\in\mathcal{S}.

(23b)

Analogously to the integral field representations (
7

\displaystyle\oiint_{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle\left\{i\Omega\mu_{0}\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\cdot\left[\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}\times\mathbf{H}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\right]+\left[\nabla\times\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.\cdot\left[\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}\times\mathbf{E}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\right]\right\}dS=0,\quad\mathbf{r}\in V_{i},

(24a)

\displaystyle\oiint_{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle\left\{i\Omega\mu_{0}\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\cdot\left[\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}\times\mathbf{H}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\right]+\left[\nabla\times\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.\cdot\left[\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}\times\mathbf{E}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\right]\right\}dS=\mathbf{E}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}),\quad\mathbf{r}\in V_{e},

(24b)

where
\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
is the unbounded dyadic Green function at the SH. It is given by Eq. (
8
), in which we replace
k_{e}^{\omega}\rightarrow k_{e}^{\Omega}
. In a similar way to that described by Eqs. (
11

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=

\displaystyle ik_{e}^{\Omega}\sum_{\nu\geq 1}\left[\mathbf{M}_{\bar{\nu}}^{(3)}(k_{e}^{\Omega}\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\mathbf{M}_{\nu}^{(1)}(k_{e}^{\Omega}\mathbf{r})\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\mathbf{N}_{\bar{\nu}}^{(3)}(k_{e}^{\Omega}\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\mathbf{N}_{\nu}^{(1)}(k_{e}^{\Omega}\mathbf{r})\right],\quad|\mathbf{r}|<|\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|,

(25a)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=

\displaystyle ik_{e}^{\Omega}\sum_{\nu\geq 1}\left[\mathbf{M}_{\bar{\nu}}^{(1)}(k_{e}^{\Omega}\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\mathbf{M}_{\nu}^{(3)}(k_{e}^{\Omega}\mathbf{r})\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\mathbf{N}_{\bar{\nu}}^{(1)}(k_{e}^{\Omega}\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\mathbf{N}_{\nu}^{(3)}(k_{e}^{\Omega}\mathbf{r})\right],\quad|\mathbf{r}|>|\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|.

(25b)

The relations expressed in Eqs. (
24

\{\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}\times\mathbf{E}_{e}^{\Omega},\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}\times\mathbf{H}_{e}^{\Omega}\}
to the SH electric field,

, outside the particle [as per Eq. (
24b
)], and to the zero field inside it – see Eq. (
24a
). It is important to notice that there are no SH excitation field sources in the exterior region, thus the total fields outside the particle are equal to the
scattered

via
boundary conditions and the particular solution given by Eqs. (
21
).

We now proceed with the expansion of SH electric fields, which are solutions of the homogeneous Maxwell equations at the SH, into Fourier series of VSWFs:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{\nu\geq 1}\Big[b_{\nu}^{\Omega}\mathbf{M}_{\nu}^{(3)}(k_{e}^{\Omega}\mathbf{r})+a_{\nu}^{\Omega}\mathbf{N}_{\nu}^{(3)}(k_{e}^{\Omega}\mathbf{r})\Big],\quad\mathbf{r}\in V_{e},

(26a)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{\nu\geq 1}\Big[c_{\nu}^{\Omega}\mathbf{M}_{\nu}^{(1)}(k_{i}^{\Omega}\mathbf{r})+d_{\nu}^{\Omega}\mathbf{N}_{\nu}^{(1)}(k_{i}^{\Omega}\mathbf{r})\Big],\quad\mathbf{r}\in V_{i},

(26b)

with
k_{e}^{\Omega}
and
k_{i}^{\Omega}
representing wave numbers associated to the exterior and the interior regions calculated at the SH frequency, respectively. The corresponding Fourier series expansions of the SH magnetic fields are derived from Eqs. (
26

17b
) and (
18b
), and can be written as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{iZ_{e}}\sum\limits_{\nu\geq 1}\Big[a_{\nu}^{\Omega}\mathbf{M}_{\nu}^{(3)}(k_{e}^{\Omega}\mathbf{r})+b_{\nu}^{\Omega}\mathbf{N}_{\nu}^{(3)}(k_{e}^{\Omega}\mathbf{r})\Big],~\mathbf{r}\in V_{e},

(27a)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{H}}_{i}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{iZ_{i}^{\Omega}}\sum\limits_{\nu\geq 1}\Big[d_{\nu}^{\Omega}\mathbf{M}_{\nu}^{(1)}(k_{i}^{\Omega}\mathbf{r})+c_{\nu}^{\Omega}\mathbf{N}_{\nu}^{(1)}(k_{i}^{\Omega}\mathbf{r})\Big],~\mathbf{r}\in V_{i}.

(27b)

Regular spherical harmonics are used in the expansion of the internal SH fields
\left\{\bar{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\Omega},\bar{\mathbf{H}}_{i}^{\Omega}\right\}
, as they must be finite at the origin, while the external SH fields
\left\{\mathbf{E}_{e}^{\Omega},\mathbf{H}_{e}^{\Omega}\right\}
are expanded in the basis of outgoing vector spherical harmonics. The Fourier expansion coefficients
\{c_{\nu}^{\Omega},d_{\nu}^{\Omega}\}
for the internal fields and
\{b_{\nu}^{\Omega},a_{\nu}^{\Omega}\}
for the external ones are unknown physical quantities and will be determined by solving a certain linear system of equations that we will construct in what follows.

In the construction of the
\mathcal{T}
-matrix at the SH, which relates the expansion coefficients of the excitation and external (scattering) fields, we employ the SH integral relation (
24a
) valid in the interior of the particle in which we replace the external field traces
\left\{\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}\times\mathbf{E}_{e}^{\Omega},\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}\times\mathbf{H}_{e}^{\Omega}\right\}
with the homogeneous internal field traces
\left\{\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}\times\bar{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\Omega},\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}\times\bar{\mathbf{H}}_{i}^{\Omega}\right\}
. This latter step is performed by taking into account the boundary conditions (
23
). After these mathematical manipulations, we arrive to the following integral equation:

\displaystyle\oiint_{\mathcal{S}}\left\{i\Omega\mu_{0}\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\cdot\left[\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}\times\bar{\mathbf{H}}_{i}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\right]\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\left[\nabla\times\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\right]\cdot\left[\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}\times\bar{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\right]\right\}dS=-i\Omega\mu_{0}\oiint_{\mathcal{S}}\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\cdot\bm{\mathcal{J}}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})dS

\displaystyle+\oiint_{\mathcal{S}}\left[\nabla\times\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\right]\cdot\left[\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})-\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}\times\mathbf{E}_{i,p}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\right]dS,\quad\mathbf{r}\in V_{i},

(28)

where the r.h.s. of this equation represents the contribution of the SH polarization sources. Substituting in the equation above the VSWF-expansions of the internal homogeneous fields at the SH, described by Eqs. (
26b
) and (
27b
), as well as the expansion in VSWFs of the dyadic Green function at the SH and valid for the points inside the target, given by Eq. (
25a
), and equating the coefficients multiplying regular spherical harmonics with the same multi-index, we are left with a linear system of equations represented in the matrix form as:

\mathbf{Q}^{\Omega(1,3)}\mathbf{h}^{\Omega}=\mathbf{g}^{\Omega(3)}.

(29)

The matrix elements
\mathbf{Q}^{\Omega(1,3)}
are of a form similar to that of the FF matrix elements
\mathbf{Q}^{\omega(1,3)}
, but computed at the frequency
\Omega
. Their specific expressions are given in Appendix A. Moreover, the unknown Fourier coefficients of the internal homogeneous fields,
\{c_{\nu}^{\Omega},d_{\nu}^{\Omega}\}
, are stacked in the vector
\mathbf{h}^{\Omega}
. The nonlinear excitation source components are collected in the vector
\mathbf{g}^{\Omega(3)}
and are defined by surface integrals of products of tangential components of the nonlinear current sources and outgoing VSWFs. The specific formulae of the components of
\mathbf{g}^{\Omega(3)}
are provided in Appendix A, too. Note that
\mathbf{g}^{\Omega(3)}
can be computed once the fields at the FF are determined, so that as far as the calculation of the nonlinear fields is concerned the vector
\mathbf{g}^{\Omega(3)}
should be viewed as a known physical quantity.

In order to evaluate the Fourier coefficients
\{b_{\nu}^{\Omega},a_{\nu}^{\Omega}\}
of the external fields at the SH, we use Eq. (
24b
) from which again the external fields are eliminated with the help of the boundary conditions (
23
). The resulting equation can be cast in the form:

\displaystyle\oiint_{\mathcal{S}}\left\{i\Omega\mu_{0}\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\cdot\left[\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}\times\bar{\mathbf{H}}_{i}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\right]\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\left[\nabla\times\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\right]\cdot\left[\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}\times\bar{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\right]\right\}dS=\mathbf{E}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})-i\Omega\mu_{0}\oiint_{\mathcal{S}}\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\cdot\bm{\mathcal{J}}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})dS

\displaystyle+\oiint_{\mathcal{S}}\left[\nabla\times\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{e}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\right]\cdot\left[\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})-\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}\times\mathbf{E}_{i}^{\Omega,p}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\right]dS,\quad\mathbf{r}\in V_{e}.

(30)

After replacing the external and the internal fields with their corresponding spherical harmonic expansions, then substituting Eq. (
25b
) for the dyadic Green function
\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{e}^{\Omega}
in the outer region, and finally collecting the Fourier coefficients multiplying radiating spherical harmonics
\mathbf{M}_{\nu}^{(3)}
and
\mathbf{N}_{\nu}^{(3)}
of the same multi-index
\nu
, we are left with the following matrix equation:

\mathbf{f}^{\Omega}+\mathbf{g}^{\Omega(1)}=\mathbf{Q}^{\Omega(1,1)}\mathbf{h}^{\Omega}.

(31)

Here, the vector
\mathbf{f}^{\Omega}
contains the unknown coefficients of the SH external fields,
\{b_{\nu}^{\Omega},a_{\nu}^{\Omega}\}
, and
\mathbf{g}^{\Omega(1)}
is the source vector with components expressed as surface integrals of products involving tangential components of the nonlinear current sources and regular VSWFs.

Finally, we eliminate the vector
\mathbf{h}^{\Omega}
between Eqs. (
29
) and (
31
), and so arrive to the following matrix equation relating the vector containing the expansion coefficients of the external (scattering) field,
\mathbf{f}^{\Omega}
, and the vectors
\mathbf{g}^{\Omega(3)}
and
\mathbf{g}^{\Omega(1)}
holding the expansion coefficients of the nonlinear excitation sources:

\mathbf{f}^{\Omega}=-\mathbf{g}^{\Omega(1)}+\mathbf{Q}^{\Omega(1,1)}[\mathbf{Q}^{\Omega(1,3)}]^{-1}\mathbf{g}^{\Omega(3)}.

(32)

This formula can be used to define the
\mathcal{T}
-matrix of the particle that characterizes the nonlinear scattering wave interaction at the SH:

\mathbf{T}^{\Omega}=\mathbf{Q}^{\Omega(1,1)}[\mathbf{Q}^{\Omega(1,3)}]^{-1}.

(33)

Similarly to the TMM formulation at the FF, the SH TMM developed here can in principle be used to analyze nonlinear wave scattering from particles with arbitrary morphologies. One important idea related to the nonlinear field calculations, which we want to highlight here, is that in this two-step procedure the accurate evaluation of the linear near-field on the inner side of the particle surface is of paramount importance, as these near-fields fully determine the polarization sources at the SH. This aspect of the TMM should be considered carefully especially when studying metallic particles or particles with surface that deviates significantly from a sphere, as it is well known that in these cases the TMM calculation of the linear near-fields converges slowly.

IV
Extension to the multiparticle systems

In this section, we generalize the formalism introduced in the previous section, valid for a single particle, to an arbitrary distribution of particles. This extension is conveniently achieved by dint of translation-addition matrices
Cruzan 1962
;
Mackowski 1996
;
Stout1 2002
, which are used to capture the multiple wave scattering nature of the physical process investigated.

IV.1
Linear wave scattering

The physical system under investigation is schematically depicted in Fig.
2
. It consists of a general distribution of
N
particles whose shape and material parameters are given but arbitrary otherwise. We associate to each particle a (local) coordinate system with origin at
\mathcal{O}_{n},n=1,\ldots,N
, and describe the entire cluster of particles in a (global) coordinate system with origin at
\mathcal{O}
, chosen in such a manner that the particles are arranged around it in some loosely defined balanced way.

Schematic illustration of a distribution of particles, the global coordinate system with origin at
\mathcal{O}
, and local coordinate systems attached to each of the particles.

We can express the field exciting the
n
th particle,
n=1,\ldots,N
, as the sum of the incident field at the location of the particle and the fields scattered from all the other particles (after possibly being scattered multiple times among the particles):

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{n,ex}^{\omega}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{r})=\left[\mathbf{M}_{\nu}^{(1)}(k_{e}^{\omega}\mathbf{r}),\mathbf{N}_{\nu}^{(1)}(k_{e}^{\omega}\mathbf{r})\right]\mathbf{g}^{\omega}

(34)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq n\end{subarray}}^{N}\left[\mathbf{M}_{\nu}^{(3)}(k_{e}^{\omega}\mathbf{r}_{k}),\mathbf{N}_{\nu}^{(3)}(k_{e}^{\omega}\mathbf{r}_{k})\right]\mathbf{f}_{k}^{\omega},\quad n=1,\ldots,N.

To simplify the notation, in this equation and in what follows the summation over the multi-index
\nu
is omitted. This field can be expanded in the local coordinate system associated to the
n
th particle as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}

{}_{n,ex}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r}_{n})=\left[\mathbf{M}_{\nu}^{(1)}(k_{e}^{\omega}\mathbf{r}_{n}),\mathbf{N}_{\nu}^{(1)}(k_{e}^{\omega}\mathbf{r}_{n})\right]\bm{\beta}_{n,0}^{\omega}\mathbf{g}^{\omega}

(35)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq n\end{subarray}}^{N}\left[\mathbf{M}_{\nu}^{(1)}(k_{e}^{\omega}\mathbf{r}_{n}),\mathbf{N}_{\nu}^{(1)}(k_{e}^{\omega}\mathbf{r}_{n})\right]\bm{\alpha}_{n,k}^{\omega}\mathbf{f}_{k}^{\omega},~n=1,\ldots,N.

Here, the matrix
\bm{\alpha}_{n,k}^{\omega}
is the FF irregular translation-addition operator mapping the radiative VSWFs defined in the local coordinate system with origin at
\mathcal{O}_{k}
to the regular VSWFs expressed in the local coordinate system with origin at
\mathcal{O}_{n}
(see Fig.
2
). Similarly,
\bm{\beta}_{n,0}^{\omega}
is the regular translation-addition matrix computed at the FF
\omega

\mathcal{O}
to their expansion in the local coordinate system with origin at
\mathcal{O}_{n}
, associated to the
n
th particle. The total field incident on the

th particle,
\mathbf{E}_{n,ex}^{\omega}(\mathbf{r}_{n})
, can itself be expanded into a series of regular VSWFs expressed in the local coordinate system with origin at
\mathcal{O}_{n}
and weighted with coefficients stacked in a vector denoted with
\mathbf{e}_{n}^{\omega}
. Therefore, we are left with the following equation containing only Fourier expansion coefficients:

\mathbf{e}_{n}^{\omega}=\bm{\beta}_{n,0}^{\omega}\mathbf{g}^{\omega}+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq n\end{subarray}}^{N}\bm{\alpha}_{n,k}^{\omega}\mathbf{f}_{k}^{\omega},\quad n=1,\ldots,N.

(36)

Note that
\mathbf{e}_{n}^{\omega}
describes the field incident onto the

th particle of the cluster, so that it plays the rôle of
\mathbf{g}_{n}^{\omega}
for an isolated individual particle labeled by

\mathbf{e}_{n}^{\omega}
consists of both the field directly incident onto the cluster, which reaches the particle

n

\mathbf{g}_{n}^{\omega}

\mathbf{f}_{n}^{\omega}=\mathbf{T}_{n}^{\omega}\mathbf{e}_{n}^{\omega}

\mathbf{T}_{n}^{\omega}

\mathcal{T}
-matrix of the

th particle.

Multiplying Eqs. (

\mathcal{T}
-matrices of individual scatterers
\left\{\mathbf{T}_{1}^{\omega},\ldots,\mathbf{T}_{N}^{\omega}\right\}
, respectively, which are precomputed and stored beforehand, we are left with a linear system of equations whose solution gives us the scattering field Fourier coefficients:

\mathbf{f}_{n}^{\omega}=\mathbf{T}_{n}^{\omega}\bm{\beta}_{n,0}^{\omega}\mathbf{g}^{\omega}+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq n\end{subarray}}^{N}\mathbf{T}_{n}^{\omega}\bm{\alpha}_{n,k}^{\omega}\mathbf{f}_{k}^{\omega},\quad n=1,\ldots,N.

(37)

This linear system of equations can be cast in the following matrix form

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{I}&-\textbf{T}_{1}^{\omega}\bm{\alpha}_{1,2}^{\omega}&\cdots&-\textbf{T}_{1}^{\omega}\bm{\alpha}_{1,N}^{\omega}\\

\textbf{f}_{2}^{\omega}\\
\vdots\\

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{T}_{1}^{\omega}\bm{\beta}_{1,0}^{\omega}\textbf{g}^{\omega}\\
\textbf{T}_{2}^{\omega}\bm{\beta}_{2,0}^{\omega}\textbf{g}^{\omega}\\
\vdots\\
\textbf{T}_{N}^{\omega}\bm{\beta}_{N,0}^{\omega}\textbf{g}^{\omega}\\
\end{bmatrix},

(38)

or in the more condensed form,

\mathbf{S}^{\omega}\mathbf{F}^{\omega}=\mathbf{G}^{\omega}.

(39)

The matrix
\mathbf{S}^{\omega}

of the system of particles. It contains all the information regarding the geometry and material parameters of the cluster. The unknown scattering field coefficients corresponding to all of the particles are collected in the vector
\mathbf{F}^{\omega}
and are retrieved by solving the linear system (

(40)

where the symbol “
†
” denotes Hermitian conjugation and
\mathfrak{Re}\{z\}
is the real part of the complex number
z

IV.2
Wave scattering at the second-harmonic

The analysis of the wave scattering at the SH follows the same main steps as in the case of the linear wave scattering we just discussed. The main difference between the linear and nonlinear wave scattering amounts to the fact that the sources of the SH field are not located at an infinite distance from the cluster of particles but inside the particles and at their surface. To account for this key difference, we add to the right hand side of the integral equation (
24a
) the excitation field at the SH, representing the source field for the
n
th particle. This excitation field consists of the sum of the fields scattered from all the other particles.

This excitation field at the SH can be expanded into series of regular VSWFs around the origin
\mathcal{O}_{n}
associated to the
n
th particle, so that we can establish a relation between the expansion coefficients of the SH internal homogeneous fields corresponding to the
n
th particle and the expansion coefficients of the total nonlinear field impinging onto the particle:

\mathbf{Q}_{n}^{\Omega(1,3)}\mathbf{h}_{n}^{\Omega}=\mathbf{g}_{n}^{\Omega(3)}-\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq n\end{subarray}}^{N}\bm{\alpha}_{n,k}^{\Omega}\mathbf{f}_{k}^{\Omega},\quad n=1,\ldots,N,

(41)

In these relations, the translation-addition matrices
\bm{\alpha}_{n,k}^{\Omega}
are evaluated at the SH frequency,
\Omega
. Substituting the internal SH field expansion coefficients
\mathbf{h}_{n}^{\Omega}
to Eq. (
31
) governing the Fourier coefficients of the scattered field at the SH, and iterating through all of the particles, we obtain:

\mathbf{f}^{\Omega}_{n}=-\mathbf{g}^{\Omega(1)}_{n}+\mathbf{T}^{\Omega}_{n}\mathbf{g}^{\Omega(3)}_{n}-\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq n\end{subarray}}^{N}\mathbf{T}_{n}^{\Omega}\bm{\alpha}_{n,k}^{\Omega}\mathbf{f}_{k}^{\Omega},\quad n=1,\ldots,N,

(42)

These relations can be expressed in matrix form as:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{I}&\textbf{T}_{1}^{\Omega}\bm{\alpha}_{1,2}^{\Omega}&\cdots&\textbf{T}_{1}^{\Omega}\bm{\alpha}_{1,N}^{\Omega}\\
\textbf{T}_{2}^{\Omega}\bm{\alpha}_{2,1}^{\Omega}&\textbf{I}&\cdots&\textbf{T}_{2}^{\Omega}\bm{\alpha}_{2,N}^{\Omega}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\textbf{T}_{N}^{\Omega}\bm{\alpha}_{N,1}^{\Omega}&\textbf{T}_{N}^{\Omega}\bm{\alpha}_{N,2}^{\Omega}&\cdots&\textbf{I}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{f}_{1}^{\Omega}\\
\textbf{f}_{2}^{\Omega}\\
\vdots\\
\textbf{f}_{N}^{\Omega}\end{bmatrix}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}-\textbf{g}_{1}^{\Omega(1)}+\textbf{T}_{1}^{\Omega}\textbf{g}_{1}^{\Omega(3)}\\
-\textbf{g}_{2}^{\Omega(1)}+\textbf{T}_{2}^{\Omega}\textbf{g}_{2}^{\Omega(3)}\\
\vdots\\
-\textbf{g}_{N}^{\Omega(1)}+\textbf{T}_{N}^{\Omega}\textbf{g}_{N}^{\Omega(3)}\\
\end{bmatrix},

(43)

or, more concisely,

\mathbf{S}^{\Omega}\mathbf{F}^{\Omega}=\mathbf{G}^{\Omega}.

(44)

Similarly to the wave scattering at the FF,
\mathbf{S}^{\Omega}
represents the system scattering matrix at the SH, the vector
\mathbf{F}^{\Omega}
holds the Fourier coefficients of the scattering field corresponding to all of the particles, and the vector
\mathbf{G}^{\Omega}
contains the information on the nonlinear polarization sources responsible for the generation of the optical field at the SH. After the SH scattering field expansion coefficients corresponding to all of the particles are computed by solving Eq. (
44
), for each particle one can compute
via
Eq. (
31
) the expansion coefficients corresponding to the internal
homogeneous
SH field. The associated scattering and internal homogeneous fields are computed, respectively, from Eqs. (
26a
) and (
26b
) (electric fields) and Eqs. (
27a
) and (
27b
) (magnetic fields). Finally, in order to compute the total fields inside each particle, the particular (inhomogeneous) solution defined by Eqs. (
21
) must be added to the homogeneous one. This is conveniently done by expanding the product of linear electric fields inside the particle into Fourier series of VSWFs
Varshalovich 1988
. The SH scattering cross-section spectra are then calculated in an analogous fashion to the procedure used at the FF, as per (
40
), with all the physical quantities being evaluated at SH frequency.

Applications and discussion

In this section we will illustrate how the numerical method developed in the preceding section can be used to compute the scattering cross-section spectra of of systems of particles with different morphology, the calculations being performed both at the fundamental and second-harmonic frequencies. In the case in which the particles are spherical, we compare our results with analytical solutions
Forestiere1 2014
(for a single sphere), or predictions of the TMM with analytically computed
\mathcal{T}
-matrices (for a multiple sphere system). Because in this latter case the analytic calculation of the matrix elements is based on the Mie theory
Mie1 1908

Mie-TMM

Sekulic 2021

CST Studio

Our choice was guided by the fact that both these optical materials present challenging testing conditions for any numerical method, namely silicon particles have large electric permittivity and hence scatter significantly the incident field, whereas (plasmonic) particles made of Au possess plasmon resonances that induce strongly inhomogeneous, enhanced near-fields.

The cluster of particles is assumed to be embedded in vacuum, whereas the excitation field is a
\hat{\bm{\theta}}

\theta_{inc}=\pi/4

VI
Conclusion

To summarize, in this manuscript we have extended the
T
-matrix numerical formalism to the second-harmonic scattering analysis of arbitrarily shaped targets made of centrosymmetric optical materials. The mathematical apparatus developed in this text is general in purpose and can be in principle applied to the SH treatment of targets with arbitrary shapes. However, in traditional TMM fashion, we restrict ourselves to the analysis of particles with axial symmetry, where TMM performs the best due to the underlying spherical expansion functions employed. The numerical technique presented in this text is developed from the integral representations of the fundamental and second-harmonic fields and the imposition of the null-field conditions via EBCM. The integral operators are then discretized with vector spherical harmonic basis functions and the matrix equation is established relating the unknown field and the known source expansion coefficients. The SH polarization sources, induced in the bulk and on the surface of the target under analysis, are conveniently introduced into TMM formalism via nonlinear tangential boundary conditions thus allowing the accurate physical description of the second-harmonic signal.

The method presented in this study is particularly useful for the analysis of different kinds of ellipsoids, where certain symmetries can be exploited through the spherical expansion of the fields and sources generating substantially lower memory imprint when compared to the standard methods based on finite element discretizations and subsectional bases. Furthermore, this method is convenient for the analysis of particle clusters since the individual scatterer
T
-matrices can be precomputed and connected with translation-addition operators to form a scattering matrix of a whole particle assembly, a process which is easily parallelized and independent of the underlying excitation field. Thus, we expect this method will be useful in the design process of finite parts of metamaterials or photonic crystals where the edge effects can not be disregarded.

In this Appendix we introduce the definition and main properties of vector spherical harmonics

Definition of vector spherical wave functions

Given the unit vector
\hat{\mathbf{r}}=\mathbf{r}/r

\mathbf{r}
is the position vector of a
point,
P

and

Varshalovich 1988

Stout2 2001

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}_{lm}(\theta,\phi)=Y_{lm}(\theta,\phi)\hat{\mathbf{r}},

(B.1a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{lm}(\theta,\phi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{l(l+1)}}\hat{\mathbf{r}}\times\nabla_{\hat{\mathbf{r}}}Y_{lm}(\theta,\phi),

(B.1b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}_{lm}(\theta,\phi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{l(l+1)}}\nabla_{\hat{\mathbf{r}}}Y_{lm}(\theta,\phi).

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}_{lm}^{(1)}(kr,\theta,\phi)=\frac{1}{k}\nabla\times\mathbf{M}_{lm}^{(1)},

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{lm}^{(1)}(kr,\theta,\phi)=\frac{1}{k}\nabla\times\mathbf{N}_{lm}^{(1)},
