Abstract
. Let 𝑿 \bm{X} be a d d -dimensional random array on [ n ] [n] whose entries take values in a finite set 𝒳 \mathcal{X} , that is, 𝑿 = ⟨ X s : s ∈ ( [ n ] d ) ⟩ \bm{X}=\langle X_{s}:s\in\binom{[n]}{d}\rangle is an 𝒳 \mathcal{X} -valued stochastic process indexed by the set ( [ n ] d ) \binom{[n]}{d} of all d d -element subsets of [ n ] ≔ { 1 , … , n } [n]\coloneqq\{1,\dots,n\} . We give easily checked conditions on 𝑿 \bm{X} that ensure, for instance, that for every function f : 𝒳 ( [ n ] d ) → ℝ f\colon\mathcal{X}^{\binom{[n]}{d}}\to\mathbb{R} that satisfies 𝔼 ⁡ [ f ⁡ ( 𝑿 ) ] = 0 \mathbb{E}[f(\bm{X})]=0 and ‖ f ⁡ ( 𝑿 ) ‖ L p = 1 \|f(\bm{X})\|_{L_{p}}=1 for some p > 1 p>1 , the random variable f ⁡ ( 𝑿 ) f(\bm{X}) becomes concentrated after conditioning it on a large subarray of 𝑿 \bm{X} . These conditions cover several classes of random arrays with not necessarily independent entries. Applications are given in combinatorics, and examples are also presented that show the optimality of various aspects of the results.

Introduction

1.1.
Motivation

The concentration of measure refers to the powerful phenomenon asserting that a function
that depends smoothly on its variables is essentially constant, as long as the number of the variables is large enough.
There are various ways to quantify this “smooth dependence” (
e.g.

It is easy to see that this phenomenon is no longer valid if we drop the smoothness assumption.
Nevertheless, one can still obtain some form of concentration under a much milder integrability

1.4.

The first step of the proof of Theorem

The proof of this step is based on estimates for martingale difference sequences in

2.1.
Main result

Let

9

Definition 2.1

Theorem 2.2

Let

, let

(2.2)

\displaystyle\beta=\beta(p,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{10}\Big)^{\frac{10}{p-1}},

2.3.
Martingale difference sequences

It is an elementary, though important, fact that martingale difference sequences

\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|d_{i}\|^{2}_{L_{p}}\bigg)^{1/2}\leqslant\big(p-1\big)^{-1/2}\,\bigg\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{i}\bigg\|_{L_{p}}.

In particular,

\min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m}\|d_{i}\|_{L_{p}}\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{m(p-1)}}\,\bigg\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{i}\bigg\|_{L_{p}}.

2.4.
Mixing and orthogonality

In what follows, it is convenient to introduce the following terminology.
Let
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mathbb{P})
be a probability space, and let
0\leqslant\beta\leqslant 1
; given two sub-
\sigma
-algebras

\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
of
\Sigma
, we say that
\mathcal{A}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
are
\beta
-mixing

provided that for every
A\in\mathcal{A}
and every
B\in\mathcal{B}
we have

(2.11)

\big|\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)-\mathbb{P}(A)\,\mathbb{P}(B)\big|\leqslant\beta.

Notice that in the extreme case “
\beta=0
” the estimate (
2.11
) is equivalent to saying that the
\sigma
-algebras

\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are independent, which in turn implies for every random variable
X
with
\mathbb{E}[X]=0

we have
\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\,|\,\mathcal{A}]\,|\,\mathcal{B}]=0
. The main result in this
subsection (Proposition
2.7
below) is an approximate version of this fact.

We start with the following lemma.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mathbb{P})
be a probability space, let
0\leqslant\beta\leqslant 1
, and let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
be
two sub-
\sigma
-algebras of
\Sigma
that are
\beta
-mixing. Then for every real-valued, bounded, random variable
X

and every
1\leqslant p\leqslant\infty
we have

(2.12)

\big\|\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\,|\,\mathcal{A}]\,|\,\mathcal{B}\big]-\mathbb{E}[X]\big\|_{L_{p}}\leqslant(4\beta)^{1/p}\,\|X-\mathbb{E}[X]\|_{L_{\infty}}.

For the proof of Lemma
2.5
we need the following simple fact.

Fact 2.6
.

Let
(X,\Sigma,\mu)
be a measure space, and let
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
be an integrable function. Then we have

(2.13)

\|f\|_{L_{1}(\mu)}\leqslant 2\sup_{A\in\Sigma}\bigg|\int_{A}f\,d\mu\bigg|.

In particular, if
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
, then

(2.14)

\sum_{i=1}^{m}|x_{i}|\leqslant 2\max_{\emptyset\neq I\subseteq[m]}\bigg|\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\bigg|.

Proof.

Since
[f\geqslant 0],[f<0]\in\Sigma
, we have

\|f\|_{L_{1}(\mu)}=\bigg|\int_{[f\geqslant 0]}f\,d\mu\bigg|+\bigg|\int_{[f<0]}f\,d\mu\bigg|\leqslant 2\sup_{A\in\Sigma}\bigg|\int_{A}f\,d\mu\bigg|.\qed

We proceed to the proof of Lemma
2.5
.

Proof of Lemma
2.5
.

We prove the
L_{1}
-estimate; the
L_{p}
-estimate for
p>1
follows from the
L_{1}-L_{\infty}
bound,
and the fact that the conditional expectation is a linear contraction on
L_{\infty}
. Without loss
of generality we may assume that
\mathbb{E}[X]=0
. (If not, then we work with the random variable

X^{\prime}\coloneqq X-\mathbb{E}[X]
instead of
X
). Set
Z\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[X\,|\,\mathcal{A}]
, and observe
that
\mathbb{E}[Z]=\mathbb{E}[X]=0
. Hence, by Fact
2.6
, it suffices to obtain an upper bound for

\big|\mathbb{E}[Z\mathbf{1}_{B}]\big|
for arbitrary
B\in\mathcal{B}
. To this end, note that

\|Z\|_{L_{\infty}}\leqslant\|X\|_{L_{\infty}}
; therefore, by a standard approximation, we may assume
that
Z
is of the form
\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}}
, where
N
is a positive integer,

|a_{i}|\leqslant\|Z\|_{L_{\infty}}
for every
i\in[N]
, and the family
\{A_{1},\dots,A_{N}\}
forms a partition
of
\Omega
into measurable events. Let
B\in\mathcal{B}
be arbitrary. Using the fact that

\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\,\mathbb{P}(A_{i})=\mathbb{E}[Z]=0
and the triangle inequality, we have

(2.15)

\big|\mathbb{E}[Z\mathbf{1}_{B}]\big|=\bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\,\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap B)\bigg|\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{N}|a_{i}|\cdot|\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap B)-\mathbb{P}(A_{i})\,\mathbb{P}(B)|.

If we set
x_{i}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap B)-\mathbb{P}(A_{i})\,\mathbb{P}(B)
, we obtain that

(2.16)

\big|\mathbb{E}[Z\mathbf{1}_{B}]\big|\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{N}|a_{i}|\cdot|x_{i}|\leqslant 2\|Z\|_{L_{\infty}}\,\max_{\emptyset\neq I\subseteq[N]}\bigg|\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\bigg|,

where we have also used the pointwise bound
|a_{i}|\leqslant\|Z\|_{L_{\infty}}
and Fact
2.6
.
Finally, setting
A_{I}\coloneqq\bigcup_{i\in I}A_{i}
for every nonempty
I\subseteq[N]
, then we have

(2.17)

\bigg|\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\bigg|=\big|\mathbb{P}(A_{I}\cap B)-\mathbb{P}(A_{I})\,\mathbb{P}(B)\big|\leqslant\beta

since the sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{N}
are pairwise disjoint and
A_{I}\in\mathcal{A}
. We conclude that

(2.18)

\big|\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[Z\,|\,\mathcal{B}]\mathbf{1}_{B}\big]\big|=\big|\mathbb{E}[Z\mathbf{1}_{B}]\big|\leqslant 2\beta\|X\|_{L_{\infty}}.

Since
B\in\mathcal{B}
was arbitrary, the result follows.
∎

We are now ready to state the main result in this subsection.

Proposition 2.7

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mathbb{P})
be a probability space, let
0\leqslant\beta\leqslant 1
, and let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
be
two sub-
\sigma
-algebras of
\Sigma
that are
\beta
-mixing. Let
1\leqslant r<p\leqslant\infty

Then,

(2.19)

\big\|\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\,|\,\mathcal{A}]\,|\,\mathcal{B}\big]-\mathbb{E}[X]\big\|_{L_{r}}\leqslant 10\beta^{\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{p}}\,\|X-\mathbb{E}[X]\|_{L_{p}}.

Proof.

Notice that (
2.19

\beta=0
; thus, we may assume that
\beta>0
. In this case,
we will obtain the estimate by truncating

and employing Lemma

, we may assume that

. Let

(to be chosen later) be the truncation level,
and set

. Markov’s inequality yields that

\mathbb{P}(|X|>t)\leqslant t^{-p}\|X\|_{L_{p}}^{p}
, thus applying Hölder’s inequality we obtain that

(2.20)

\|X_{t}-X\|_{L_{r}}^{r}=\mathbb{E}\big[|X|^{r}\,\mathbf{1}_{[|X|>t]}\big]\leqslant\|X\|_{L_{p}}^{r}\,\mathbb{P}(|X|>t)^{1-\frac{r}{p}}\leqslant\frac{\|X\|_{L_{p}}^{p}}{t^{p-r}}

for any

(2.21)

\displaystyle\big\|\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\,|\,\mathcal{A}]\,|\,\mathcal{B}\big]\big\|_{L_{r}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\big\|\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X-X_{t}\,|\,\mathcal{A}]\,|\,\mathcal{B}\big]\big\|_{L_{r}}+

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;+\big\|\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X_{t}\,|\,\mathcal{A}]\,|\,\mathcal{B}\big]-\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]\big\|_{L_{r}}+\big|\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]\big|

\displaystyle\leqslant\|X-X_{t}\|_{L_{r}}+(4\beta)^{1/r}\cdot 2t+\|X-X_{t}\|_{L_{1}},

where we have used the contraction property of the conditional expectation, Lemma

and the fact

, respectively. Taking into account (

(2.22)

\big\|\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\,|\,\mathcal{A}]\mid\mathcal{B}\big]\big\|_{L_{r}}\leqslant 2\frac{\|X\|_{L_{p}}^{p/r}}{t^{\frac{p}{r}-1}}+8\beta^{1/r}t.

It remains to optimize the latter with respect to

∎

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem

In a nutshell, the argument is based on repeated averaging and an appropriate version of the weak law of large

Lemma 4.1

Let

\bm{X}
, the estimate (
4.35
) follows.
∎

The second lemma shows that the box independence condition (
3.3
) is inherited by the two-dimensional faces
of
d
-dimensional boxes.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
n
be an integer with
n\geqslant 2d
, let
0<\eta<1
, let
\vartheta>0
, let
\mathcal{X}
be a nonempty finite set,
let
\mathcal{S}
be a nonempty subset of
\mathcal{X}
, and let
\bm{X}=\langle X_{s}:s\in\binom{[n]}{d}\rangle

be an
\mathcal{X}
-valued,
\eta
-spreadable,
d
-dimensional random array on
[n]
that is

(\vartheta,\mathcal{S})\text{-box}
independent. Then for every
t\in\binom{[n-2]}{d-1}
and
every
a\in\mathcal{S}
we have

(4.37)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big([X_{t\cup\{n-1\}}=a]

\displaystyle\cap[X_{t\cup\{n\}}=a]\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{P}\big([X_{t\cup\{n-1\}}=a]\big)\,\mathbb{P}\big([X_{t\cup\{n\}}=a]\big)+\vartheta_{3}(\eta,\vartheta,d,n),

where
\vartheta_{3}(\eta,\vartheta,d,n)=\frac{d-1}{(n-2d+2)^{1/2^{d-1}}}+(2^{d}+5)\eta^{1/2^{d-1}}+\vartheta^{1/2^{d-1}}+3\eta
is as in (
4.12
).

Proof.

Fix
a\in\mathcal{S}
. We define, recursively, a finite sequence
(\vartheta_{i})_{i=0}^{d-1}

by setting
\vartheta_{0}=\vartheta
and

(4.38)

\vartheta_{r+1}=\bigg(\frac{1}{n-2d+r+2}+(2^{d-r}+5)\eta+\vartheta_{r}\bigg)^{1/2}.

By induction on
r\in\{0,\dots,d-1\}
, we will show that

(4.39)

\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcap_{v\in B_{r}}[X_{t_{r}\cup v}=a]\Big)\leqslant\prod_{v\in B_{r}}\mathbb{P}\big([X_{t_{r}\cup v}=a]\big)+\vartheta_{r},

where
t_{r}\coloneqq[r]
(and, by convention,
[0]=\emptyset
) and

B_{r}\coloneqq\mathrm{Box}\big((H_{r+1},\dots,H_{d})\big)
is the
(d-r)
-dimensional box—see (
3.1
)—determined
by the sequence
(H_{r+1},\dots,H_{d})
with
H_{i}\coloneqq\{n-2d+2i-1,n-2d+2i\}
for each
i\in\{r+1,\dots,d\}
.
The case “
r=0
” follows from the fact that the random array
\bm{X}
is
(\vartheta,\mathcal{S})
-box independent.
Next, let
r\in\{0,\dots,d-2\}
and assume that (
4.39
) has been proved up to
r
. For every
j\in[n-2d+r+2]
set

(4.40)

A_{j}\coloneqq\bigcap_{v\in B_{r+1}}[X_{t_{r}\cup\{r+j\}\cup v}=a].

Since
\bm{X}
is
\eta
-spreadable, the sequence
(A_{1},\dots,A_{n-2d+r+2})
is
\eta
-spreadable.
Using this observation and the inductive assumptions, we see that

(4.41)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A_{1}\cap A_{2})

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{P}(A_{n-2d+r+1}\cap A_{n-2d+r+2})+\eta

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcap_{v\in B_{r}}[X_{t_{r}\cup v}=a]\Big)+\eta\leqslant\prod_{v\in B_{r}}\mathbb{P}\big([X_{t_{r}\cup v}=a]\big)+\eta+\vartheta_{r}.

On the other hand, since
\bm{X}
is
\eta
-spreadable, we have

(4.42)

\prod_{v\in B_{r}}\mathbb{P}\big([X_{t_{r}\cup v}=a]\big)\leqslant\bigg(\prod_{v\in B_{r+1}}\mathbb{P}\big([X_{t_{r+1}\cup v}=a]\big)\bigg)^{2}+2^{d-r}\eta.

Moreover, by Lemma
4.3
applied to the
\eta
-spreadable sequence
(A_{j})_{j=1}^{n-2d+r+2}
,

(4.43)

\mathbb{P}(A_{1}\cap A_{2})\geqslant\mathbb{P}(A_{1})\,\mathbb{P}(A_{2})-\frac{1}{n-2d+r+2}-3\eta.

By (
4.41
)–(
4.43
) and using the
\eta
-spreadability of the sequence

(A_{j})_{j=1}^{n-2d+r+2}
once again, we obtain that

(4.44)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcap_{v\in B_{r+1}}[X_{t_{r+1}\cup v}=a]\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(A_{1})^{2}\leqslant\mathbb{P}(A_{1})\,\mathbb{P}(A_{2})+\eta

\displaystyle\leqslant\bigg(\prod_{v\in B_{r+1}}\mathbb{P}\big([X_{t_{r+1}\cup v}=a]\big)\bigg)^{2}+\vartheta_{r+1}^{2}.

Taking square-roots, this estimate completes the inductive proof of (
4.39
).

Now notice that

(4.45)

\displaystyle\vartheta_{d-1}

\displaystyle\leqslant\vartheta^{\frac{1}{2^{d-1}}}+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\Big((n-2d+2)^{-1/2^{j}}+((2^{d}+5)\eta)^{1/2^{j}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{d-1}{(n-2d+2)^{1/2^{d-1}}}+(d-1)(2^{d}+5)^{1/2}\eta^{1/2^{d-1}}+\vartheta^{1/2^{d-1}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{d-1}{(n-2d+2)^{1/2^{d-1}}}+(2^{d}+5)\eta^{1/2^{d-1}}+\vartheta^{1/2^{d-1}}.

Setting
s_{1}\coloneqq[d-1]\cup\{n-1\}
and
s_{2}\coloneqq[d-1]\cup\{n\}
,
by (
4.39
) and (
4.45
), we have

(4.46)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big([X_{s_{1}}=a]\cap[X_{s_{2}}=a]\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{P}\big([X_{s_{1}}=a]\big)\,\mathbb{P}\big([X_{s_{2}}=a]\big)+

\displaystyle\,+\frac{d-1}{(n-2d+2)^{1/2^{d-1}}}+(2^{d}+5)\eta^{1/2^{d-1}}\!\!+\vartheta^{1/2^{d-1}}.

Taking into account the
\eta
-spreadability of
\bm{X}
and the definition of
\vartheta_{3}(\eta,\vartheta,d,n)
,
the estimate (
4.37
) follows from (
4.46
).
∎

4.5.2.
Step 2: rewriting the inductive assumptions

We proceed with the following lemma, which will enable us to use
\mathrm{P}(d-1)

in a more convenient form.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
n,\eta,\vartheta,\mathcal{X},\mathcal{S}
be as in
\mathrm{P}(d)
,
and let
\bm{X}=\langle X_{s}:s\in\binom{[n]}{d}\rangle
be an
\mathcal{X}\text{-valued}
,
\eta
-spreadable,

d
-dimensional random array on
[n]
that is
(\vartheta,\mathcal{S})
-box independent.
We define
\widetilde{\bm{X}}=\langle\widetilde{X}_{t}:t\in\binom{[n-1]}{d-1}\rangle
by setting

(4.47)

\widetilde{X}_{t}\coloneqq X_{t\cup\{n\}}.

Then the random array
\widetilde{\bm{X}}
is
\mathcal{X}
-valued,
\eta
-spreadable
and
(\vartheta_{1}(\eta,\vartheta,d,n),\mathcal{S})
-box independent, where

\vartheta_{1}(\eta,\vartheta,d,n)=(n-2d+2)^{-1/2}+(2^{d}+5)\sqrt{\eta}+\sqrt{\vartheta}
is as (
4.10
).

Proof.

Since
\bm{X}
is
\mathcal{X}
-valued and
\eta
-spreadable, by (
4.47
),
we see that these properties are inherited to
\widetilde{\bm{X}}
.
Thus, we only need to check that
\widetilde{\bm{X}}
is
(\vartheta_{1}(\eta,\vartheta,d,n),\mathcal{S})
-box independent.

To this end, fix
a\in\mathcal{S}
and a finite sequence
\mathcal{H}=(H_{1},\dots,H_{d-1})

of
2\text{-element}
subsets of
[n-1]
with
\max(H_{i})<\min(H_{i+1})
for all
i\in[d-2]
;
let
B\coloneqq\mathrm{Box}(\mathcal{H})
denote the
(d-1)
-dimensional box determined
by the sequence
\mathcal{H}
. Moreover, set

B_{0}\coloneqq\mathrm{Box}\big((\{1,2\},\dots,\{2d-3,2d-2\})\big)

and
A_{r}\coloneqq\bigcap_{t\in B_{0}}[X_{t\cup\{2d-2+r\}}=a]
for every

Notice that the sequence
(A_{1},\dots,A_{n-2d+2})
is
\eta
-spreadable. Therefore, by Lemma
4.3
,

(4.48)

\mathbb{P}(A_{1})^{2}\leqslant\mathbb{P}(A_{1})\,\mathbb{P}(A_{2})+\eta\leqslant\mathbb{P}(A_{1}\cap A_{2})+\frac{1}{n-2d+2}+4\eta.

Next, set
B^{\prime}\coloneqq\mathrm{Box}\big((\{1,2\},\dots,\{2d-3,2d-2\},\{2d-1,2d\})\big)
,
and observe that
B^{\prime}
is a
d\text{-dimensional}
box and
A_{1}\cap A_{2}=\bigcap_{s\in B^{\prime}}[X_{s}=a]
.
Since
\bm{X}
is
(\vartheta,\mathcal{S})
-box independent and
a\in\mathcal{S}
, we see that

(4.49)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A_{1}\cap A_{2})

\displaystyle\leqslant\prod_{s\in B^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}\big([X_{s}=a]\big)+\vartheta

\displaystyle=\Big(\prod_{t\in B_{0}}\mathbb{P}\big([X_{t\cup\{2d-1\}}=a]\big)\Big)\Big(\prod_{t\in B_{0}}\mathbb{P}\big([X_{t\cup\{2d\}}=a]\big)\Big)+\vartheta

\displaystyle\leqslant\bigg(\prod_{t\in B_{0}}\mathbb{P}\big([X_{t\cup\{2d-1\}}=a]\big)\bigg)^{2}+2^{d-1}\eta+\vartheta,

where the last inequality follows from the
\eta
-spreadability of
\bm{X}
.
By (
4.48
), (
4.49
) and the definition of
A_{1}
, we obtain

(4.50)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcap_{t\in B_{0}}[X_{t\cup\{2d-1\}}=a]\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\prod_{t\in B_{0}}\mathbb{P}\big([X_{t\cup\{2d-1\}}=a]\big)+

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;+\bigg(\frac{1}{n-2d+2}+(2^{d-1}+4)\eta+\vartheta\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\prod_{t\in B_{0}}\mathbb{P}\big([X_{t\cup\{2d-1\}}=a]\big)+

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;+(n-2d+2)^{-1/2}+(2^{d-1}+4)\sqrt{\eta}+\sqrt{\vartheta}.

On the other hand, using the
\eta
-spreadability of
\bm{X}
, we have

(4.51)

\displaystyle\bigg|\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcap_{t\in B_{0}}[X_{t\cup\{2d-1\}}=a]\Big)-\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcap_{t\in B}[\widetilde{X}_{t}=a]\Big)\bigg|\leqslant\eta\leqslant\sqrt{\eta},

(4.52)

\displaystyle\bigg|\prod_{t\in B_{0}}\mathbb{P}\big([X_{t\cup\{2d-1\}}=a]\big)-\prod_{t\in B}\mathbb{P}\big([\widetilde{X}_{t}=a]\big)\bigg|\leqslant 2^{d-1}\eta\leqslant 2^{d-1}\sqrt{\eta}.

Combining (
4.50
)–(
4.52
) and invoking the definition
of
\vartheta_{1}(\eta,\vartheta,d,n)
in (
4.10
), we conclude that

(4.53)

\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcap_{t\in B}[\tilde{X}_{t}=\alpha]\Big)\leqslant\prod_{t\in B}\mathbb{P}\big([\tilde{X}_{t}=a]\big)+\vartheta_{1}(\eta,\vartheta,d,n).

Since
a
and
B
were arbitrary, the result follows.
∎

By Lemma
4.8
and
\mathrm{P}(d-1)
, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 4.9
.

be as in Lemma

.

).

The following lemma complements Lemma

. It is also based on the inductive hypothesis

, but it will enable to use it in a rather different form.

Lemma 4.10

(Doubling)
.

Let
n,\eta,\vartheta,\mathcal{X},\mathcal{S}
be as in the statement of
\mathrm{P}(d)
,
and assume that
\bm{X}=\langle X_{s}:s\in\binom{[n]}{d}\rangle
is an
\mathcal{X}
-valued,
\eta
-spreadable,

d
-dimensional random array on
[n]
that is
(\vartheta,\mathcal{S})
-box independent.
We define a
(d-1)
-dimensional random array

\widetilde{\bm{X}}^{\prime}=\langle\widetilde{X}^{\prime}_{t}:t\in\binom{[n-2]}{d-1}\rangle

(4.55)

\widetilde{X}^{\prime}_{t}\coloneqq(X_{t\cup\{n-1\}},X_{t\cup\{n\}}).

Then
\widetilde{\bm{X}}^{\prime}
is
(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X})
-valued,

\widetilde{\bm{X}}^{\prime}
is

-valued and
\eta
-spreadable. So, we only need
to show that
\widetilde{\bm{X}}^{\prime}
is
(\vartheta_{2}(\eta,\vartheta,d,n),\{(a,a):a\in\mathcal{S}\})
-box independent.

Let
H_{1},\dots,H_{d-1}
be
2
-element subsets of
[n-2]
with
\max(H_{i})<\min(H_{i+1})
for all
i\in[d-2]
.
Also let

\bm{X}

is

-box independent, we see that

(4.56)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcap_{t\in\widetilde{B}}[\widetilde{X}^{\prime}_{t}=(a,a)]\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcap_{s\in B}[X_{s}=a]\Big)\leqslant\prod_{s\in B}\mathbb{P}\big([X_{s}=a]\big)+\vartheta.

By Lemma
4.6
, we have

\displaystyle\prod_{s\in B}\mathbb{P}\big([X_{s}

\displaystyle=a]\big)=\prod_{t\in\widetilde{B}}\mathbb{P}\big([X_{t\cup\{n-1\}}=a]\big)\,\mathbb{P}\big([X_{t\cup\{n\}}=a]\big)

\displaystyle=\prod_{t\in\widetilde{B}}\mathbb{P}\big([\widetilde{X}^{\prime}_{t}=(a,a)]\big)+\frac{2^{d-1}}{n-d+1}+2^{d}3\eta.

By (
4.56
) and (
4.57
) and the definition of
\vartheta_{2}(\eta,\vartheta,d,n)

4.5.4.

This is the main step of the proof. Specifically, our goal is to prove the following proposition.

(Gluing)
.

n\geqslant 2d+2
be an integer, let
\eta,\vartheta,\mathcal{X},\mathcal{S}

\mathrm{P}(d)

\displaystyle\,\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcap_{t\in\mathcal{G}}[X_{t\cup\{n-1\}}=a_{t}]\Big)\,\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcap_{t\in\mathcal{G}}[X_{t\cup\{n\}}=a_{t}]\Big)+\gamma^{(3)}_{|\mathcal{G}|}(\eta,\vartheta,d,n),

\displaystyle=a_{t}]\cap\bigcap_{t\in\mathcal{G}}[X_{t\cup\{n\}}=a_{t}]\Big)

(4.61)

\displaystyle\prod_{t\in\mathcal{G}}

\displaystyle\,\mathbb{P}\big([X_{t\cup\{n-1\}}=a_{t}]\cap[X_{t\cup\{n\}}=a_{t}]\big)

\displaystyle\prod_{t\in\mathcal{G}}\mathbb{P}\big([X_{t\cup\{n-1\}}=a_{t}]\big)

The estimate (

) follows by combining (

)–(

) and invoking the definition

in (
4.15
).

We are now ready to give the proof of Proposition

.

Proof of Proposition
4.12
.

Set
E\coloneqq\bigcap_{s\in\mathcal{F}}[X_{s}=b_{s}]
and
A_{i}\coloneqq\bigcap_{t\in\mathcal{G}}[X_{t\cup\{r-1+i\}}]

for every
i\in\{1,\dots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor\}
. Since
\bm{X}
is
\eta
-spreadable,
for every
i,j\in\{1,\dots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor\}
with
i\neq j
we have

(i)

|\mathbb{P}(A_{i})-\mathbb{P}(A_{j})|\leqslant\eta
, and

(ii)

|\mathbb{P}(E\cap A_{i})-\mathbb{P}(E\cap A_{j})|\leqslant\eta
.

Moreover, applying Lemma
4.13
and using the
\eta
-spreadability of
\bm{X}
again,
for every
i,j\in\{1,\dots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor\}
with
i\neq j
we have

(iii)

\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap A_{j})\leqslant\mathbb{P}(A_{i})\,\mathbb{P}(A_{j})+\gamma^{(3)}_{|\mathcal{G}|}(\eta,\vartheta,d,n)+(2|\mathcal{G}|+1)\eta
.

By Lemma

Step 5: completion of the proof

This is the last step of the proof. Recall that we need to prove that the statement

We proceed by induction on

. Let

\mathcal{F}

\mathcal{S}

First observe that

Extensions/Refinements

6.1.
Dissociated random arrays

The following theorem is a version of Theorem
5.1
for the case of dissociated random arrays.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
1<p\leqslant 2
, let
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
, and set

(6.1)

\displaystyle c=c(\varepsilon,p)\coloneqq\frac{1}{4}\,\varepsilon^{\frac{2(p+1)}{p}}(p-1).

of
[n]

(6.2)

\mathbb{P}\big(\big|\mathbb{E}[f(\bm{X})\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{J}]\big|\leqslant\varepsilon\big)\geqslant 1-\varepsilon.

Proof.

Set

and

, and note that

.
Using the continuity of the

-dissociated

\big\|\mathbb{E}[f(\bm{X})\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{J}]\big\|_{L_{p}}\leqslant\varepsilon^{\frac{p+1}{p}}.

∎

Note that Theorem

improves upon Theorem

in two ways.

no restriction is imposed on

becomes concentrated by conditioning it on a subarray whose size is proportional to

Remark 6.2
.

Observe that the lower bound on the cardinality of the set

obtained by Theorem

depends polynomially
on the parameter
\varepsilon

of dependence is actually necessary. This can be seen by considering (appropriately normalized) linear
functions of i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables and invoking the Berry–Esseen theorem.

7.1.1.
Quasirandom graphs and hypergraphs

Quasirandom objects are deterministic discrete structures that behave like random ones for most practical
purposes. The phenomenon was first discovered in the context of graphs by Chung, Graham and Wilson
[
CGW88
,
CGW89
]

who build upon previous work of Thomason
[
Tho87
]
. In the last twenty years the theory was also extended
to hypergraphs, and it has found numerous significant applications in number theory and theoretical computer
science (see,
e.g.
,
[
Rő15
]
).

7.1.1.1.

Much of the modern theory of quasirandomness is developed using the box norms introduced by Gowers
[
Go07
]
.
Specifically, let
d\geqslant 2
be an integer, let
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
be a probability space,
and let
\Omega^{d}
be equipped with the product measure. For every integrable random variable

f\colon\Omega^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
we define its
box norm

\|f\|_{\square}
by the rule

(7.5)

\|f\|_{\square}\coloneqq\bigg(\int\prod_{\bm{\epsilon}\in\{0,1\}^{d}}f(\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\epsilon}})\,d\bm{\mu}(\bm{\omega})\bigg)^{1/2^{d}},

where
\bm{\mu}
denotes the product measure on
\Omega^{2d}
and, for every

\bm{\omega}=(\omega^{0}_{1},\omega^{1}_{1},\dots,\omega^{0}_{d},\omega^{1}_{d})\in\Omega^{2d}

and every
\bm{\epsilon}=(\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{d})\in\{0,1\}^{d}
we have

\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\epsilon}}\coloneqq(\omega_{1}^{\epsilon_{1}},\dots,\omega_{d}^{\epsilon_{d}})\in\Omega^{d}
;
by convention, we set
\|f\|_{\square}\coloneqq+\infty
if the integral in (
7.5
) does not exist.
The quantity
\|\cdot\|_{\square}
is a norm on the vector space
\{f\in L_{1}:\|f\|_{\square}<+\infty\}
,
and it satisfies the following inequality, known as the
Gowers–Cauchy–Schwarz inequality
:
for every collection
\langle f_{\bm{\epsilon}}:\epsilon\in\{0,1\}^{d}\rangle
of integrable
random variables on
\Omega^{d}
we have

(7.6)

\bigg|\int\prod_{\bm{\epsilon}\in\{0,1\}^{d}}f_{\bm{\epsilon}}(\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\epsilon}})\,d\bm{\mu}(\bm{\omega})\bigg|\leqslant\prod_{\bm{\epsilon}\in\{0,1\}^{d}}\|f_{\bm{\epsilon}}\|_{\square}.

For proofs of these basic facts, as well as for a more complete presentation of related material, we refer to
[
GT10
, Appendix B]
.

7.1.1.2.

The link between the box norms and quasirandomness is given in the following definition.

Definition 7.1

(Box uniformity)
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
, let

be a finite set with
|V|\geqslant d
, and let

. We say that a

-uniform

-box uniform

(
or, simply,

provided that

(7.7)

\big\|\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{G}}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{G}}]\big\|_{\square}\leqslant\varrho,

where
\mathcal{G}
is as in (
7.2
).
(
Here, we view

as a discrete probability space equipped
with the uniform probability measure.
)

Of course, Definition

is interesting when the parameter

is much smaller
than
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{G}}]
. We also note that although box uniformity is defined analytically,
it has a number of equivalent combinatorial formulations. For instance, it is easy to see that a graph

is box uniform if and only if it has roughly the expected number of

-cycles; see,
e.g.
,

[

,

,

,

,

7.2.
Mixtures

An important property of the class of boolean, spreadable random arrays is that it is closed under mixtures.
More precisely, let
n,d,J
be positive integers with
n\geqslant d\geqslant 2
and let

\bm{X}_{1}=\langle X^{1}_{s}:s\in\binom{[n]}{d}\rangle,\dots,\bm{X}_{J}=\langle X^{J}_{s}:s\in\binom{[n]}{d}\rangle

be boolean, spreadable,
d
-dimensional random arrays on
[n]
. Then, for any choice
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{J}

of convex coefficients, there exists a boolean, spreadable,
d
-dimensional random array

\bm{X}=\langle X_{s}:s\in\binom{[n]}{d}\rangle
on
[n]
that satisfies

(7.13)

\mathbb{E}\Big[\prod_{s\in\mathcal{F}}X_{s}\Big]=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j}\mathbb{E}\Big[\prod_{s\in\mathcal{F}}X^{j}_{s}\Big]

for every nonempty finite subset
\mathcal{F}
of
\binom{[n]}{d}
.

It turns out that boolean, spreadable random arrays that satisfy the box independence condition
are also closed under mixtures under suitable conditions. In particular, we have the following proposition
(its proof follows from a direct computation).

Proposition 7.4
.

Let
n,d,J
be positive integers with
n\geqslant d\geqslant 2
, and let
\delta,\vartheta>0
. For every
j\in[J]
let

\bm{X}_{j}=\langle X^{j}_{s}:s\in\binom{[n]}{d}\rangle
be a boolean, spreadable,
d
-dimensional random array on
[n]

that is
(\vartheta,\{1\})
-box independent and satisfies
|\mathbb{E}[X^{j}_{s}]-\delta|\leqslant\vartheta
for all

. If
\bm{X}
is any mixture of
\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{J}
, then
\bm{X}
is

(2^{d+2}\vartheta,\{1\})
-box independent.

Observe that, by Propositions
7.2
and
7.4
, if
G_{1},\dots,G_{J}
are quasirandom,
d\text{-uniform}

hypergraphs with the same edge density, then any mixture of the finite subarrays of
\bm{X}_{G_{1}},\dots,\bm{X}_{G_{J}}

satisfies the box independent condition. We note that this fact essentially characterizes the box independence condition.
Specifically, it follows from
[

, Propositions 8.3 and 3.1]
that for every boolean, spreadable,
d
-dimensional
random array

that satisfies the box independence condition, there exist quasirandom,

hypergraphs

with the same edge density, such that the law of

is close, in the total variation distance,

\bm{X}_{G_{1}},\dots,\bm{X}_{G_{J}}
.

8.2.1.
Preliminary tools

We start with a more precise, quantitative, version of Proposition
1.3
for boolean
two-dimensional random arrays. Specifically, let

be integers with

is the least integer

It is a classical result due to Erdős and Rado

Fact 8.3

Let

on

\eta
-spreadable

(

1.1
)

\bm{X}
and, for notational convenience, set

of

Notice, in particular, that for every
