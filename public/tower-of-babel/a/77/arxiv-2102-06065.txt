Slow and fast minimal speed traveling waves of the FKPP equation with chemotaxis
Christopher Henderson

Abstract
We examine a general model for the Fisher-KPP (FKPP) equation with nonlocal advection. The main interpretation of this model is as describing a diffusing and logistically growing population that is also influenced by intraspecific attraction or repulsion. For a particular choice of parameters, this specializes to the Keller-Segel-Fisher equation for chemotaxis. Our interest is in the effect of chemotaxis on the speed of traveling waves. We prove that there is a threshold such that, when interactions are weaker and more localized than this, chemotaxis, despite being non-trivial, does not influence the speed of traveling waves; that is, the minimal speed traveling wave has speed 2 as in the FKPP case. On the other hand, when the interaction is repulsive, we show that the minimal traveling wave speed is arbitrarily large in a certain asymptotic regime in which the interaction strength and length scale tend to infinity.

Introduction and main results

The general setting and the main goal

The model we consider is

u_{t}+(vu)_{x}=u_{xx}+u(1-u),\qquad\text{ in }(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R},

(1.1)

where, for
\chi\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma>0
, and
K\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, we define

v=\chi\,K_{\sigma}*u\qquad\text{ and }\qquad K_{\sigma}=\frac{1}{\sigma}K\left(\frac{\cdot}{\sigma}\right).

(1.2)

The unknown,
u
, typically represents the population density of a species that is experiencing an intraspecific aggregation or dispersion effect such as chemotaxis. For simplicity, we always refer to this nonlocal effect as chemotaxis, although the model could be used in other settings. The parameters
\chi
and
\sigma
encode the strength and length-scale of the chemotaxis, respectively, while the sign of
\chi
determines if the effect is attractive (
\chi>0
) or repulsive (
\chi<0
).

Before making our assumptions precise, we discuss the main goal of this article. The equation (
1.1
) and similar reaction-diffusion equations model the invasion of species into a new environment. When
\chi\equiv 0
, it is well-known that this invasion occurs with speed two. More precisely, the minimal speed traveling wave has speed two (we define traveling waves below). We aim to understand the effect of chemotaxis on this invasion speed; that is, we aim to answer the question: does the localized, nonlinear chemotaxis term alter speed of the minimal speed traveling wave, and, if it does, when and how is the speed changed? While some results exist, they are either partial or require very specific structure of
K
. We discuss this at the conclusion of this section.

Assumptions on
K
,
\chi
, and
\sigma

Throughout the paper,
K
is a bounded odd function that is increasing except at
x=0
; that is, it is increasing in
(-\infty,0)
and in
(0,+\infty)
. We also assume that
K
does not change sign in
(-\infty,0)
and in
(0,+\infty)
. Without loss of generality, we assume that

\|K\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}=1\quad\text{ and }\quad 2|K(0^{+})|=-2K(0^{+})=1,

(1.3)

where
K(0^{+})=\lim_{x\searrow 0}K(x)
. Defining
K(0^{-})
analogously, notice that
2|K(0^{+})|=|K(0^{+})-K(0^{-})|
; that is,
2|K(0^{+})|
is the magnitude of the “jump” of
K
across the origin.

Finally, we assume that

K=\overline{K}^{\prime}\qquad\text{ for some }\overline{K}\text{ such that }(1+|x|)\overline{K}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}).

(1.4)

We make this assumption, but note that this is not necessary for all results below.

Remark 1.1
.

We give some examples of admissible choices of
K
here.

(i)

K(x)=-\frac{\sign(x)}{2}e^{-|x|}
. This is the kernel related to the Keller-Segel model for chemotaxis; that is,
v=(\chi/\sqrt{\sigma})\bar{v}_{x}
for
\bar{v}

-\sigma^{2}\bar{v}_{xx}+\bar{v}=u
. This has been studied extensively, see the discussion below.

(ii)

K(x)=-\frac{\sign(x)}{2}\mathds{1}_{[-1,1]}(x)
.

(iii)

K(x)=-\frac{\sign(x)}{2}\left(1+\frac{|x|}{k-1}\right)^{-k}
for
k>2
.

(iv)

K(x)=-\frac{\sign(x)}{2}e^{-\frac{|x|^{\alpha}}{D_{\alpha}}}
for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, where
D_{\alpha}=\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-|x|^{\alpha}}\,dx\right)^{-\alpha}

The choice of

1.1

1.1

\chi
, either repel or attract one another. One example of this phenomenon is herding, although many examples exist. To further illustrate this, we discuss the choice
Remark

1.1
.(ii). This corresponds to individuals that interact only when they are distance
\sigma
or closer and could be due to, e.g., limited vision. Roughly, when
\chi<0
, they “feel a pull” towards one another with strength
O(|\chi|)
with a multiplicative factor related to the fraction of the population within distance
\sigma
. When
\chi>0
, they experience a “push” instead.
In general, aggregation-diffusion models arise both as gradient flows of a free energy involving an interaction kernel and as limits of microscopic models; see, e.g., the results, discussions, and references in
[
9
,
27
]
.

Our interest in this article is, roughy, in the cases
|\chi|,|\chi|/\sigma\ll 1
and
-\chi\gg 1
. As such, we make the standing assumption that

\chi,\frac{\chi}{\sigma}<\frac{1}{2}.

(1.5)

This simplifies some estimates but does not play a fundamental role in our analysis.

Traveling waves and preliminary observations

Our main objects of study are the traveling wave solutions of (
1.1
), which we define below. In particular, we are interested in the effect of the chemotaxis term
v
on the speed of the traveling wave.

Definition 1.2
.

A traveling wave solution is a pair
(c,u)
such that (i)
0<u\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, (ii)
c\geq 0
, (iii)
\widetilde{u}(t,x):=u(x-ct)
solves (
1.1
), and (iv)

\lim_{x\to-\infty}u(x)=1,\quad\text{ and }\quad\lim_{x\to\infty}u(x)=0.

(1.6)

We refer to

as the speed and

as the profile. We say that a traveling wave
(c^{*},u^{*})
is a minimal speed traveling wave if
c^{*}\leq c
for all traveling waves
(c,u)
.

For similar reaction-diffusion equations, one sometimes assumes that

is simply uniformly positive as

. In fact, the convergence to one of

-\infty

For any traveling wave

, notice that

-cu_{x}+(vu)_{x}=u_{xx}+u(1-u).

(1.7)

This equation will be the basis for our analysis.

Using standard techniques, we can show that traveling waves exist.

Proposition 1.3
.

Under the assumptions above, there exists a wave with speed

c\leq 2\sqrt{1+\frac{|\chi|}{\sigma}}+\frac{|\chi|}{2}\leq 2+\frac{|\chi|}{\sigma}+\frac{|\chi|}{2}.

Moreover, if
(c,u)
is any traveling wave solution of (
1.7
), then

.

We note that the lower bound on the traveling wave speed, that is, that traveling waves cannot have a
slower

2

[
22
, Theorem 1.1]
, but we provide a slightly different proof here.

Slow traveling waves

The first main result of the article is that, under a smallness condition on
\chi\sigma^{2}
and
\chi/\sigma
, the speed of the minimal speed traveling wave is exactly
2
, which is the same as in the case where chemotaxis is not present (
\chi=0
).

Theorem 1.4
.

Under the assumptions above, there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that, if
|\chi|(\sigma^{-1}+\sigma^{2})<\varepsilon_{0}
, then there exists a traveling wave solution
(c,u)
of (
1.1
) such that
c=2
.

In view of the discussion of
Proposition

1.3
, the thrust of
Theorem

1.4
is to establish the upper bound on
c
. The standard method of establishing an upper bound on the speed is to build supersolutions of
u
. There are two main obstructions to this, both stemming from the fact that
v
depends nonlocally on
u
. First, (
1.7
) does not enjoy the maximum principle. Second, the standard arguments use strongly imposed structure on the advection, such as periodicity, which
v
does not have.

In some cases, such difficulties can be sidestepped by ad hoc methods; however, to our knowledge, such proofs use strongly the structure of the advection
[
38
,
10
]
. These are discussed below in the discussion of the history of this problem, and, in that discussion, we describe why their methods cannot generalize to our setting.

We now describe the main idea of the proof of
Theorem

1.4
. We work on the level of the “slab problem” on
[-a,a]
for
a\gg 1
, as is typical in the construction of traveling waves. As described above, we need only establish an upper bound. To this end, we treat
v
as a given function and construct a supersolution to the linear equation

-c\overline{u}_{x}+(v\overline{u})_{x}=\overline{u}_{xx}+\overline{u}(1-u).

By removing an integrating factor involving
v
, we reduce this problem to studying the principal eigenfunction and eigenvalue of

-\varphi^{a}_{xx}-V\varphi^{a}=\lambda\varphi^{a},\qquad\text{where }V=-u-(c-2)\left(1+\frac{c-2}{4}\right)+v\left(\frac{2c-v}{4}\right)+\frac{v_{x}}{2},

(1.8)

on
[-a,a]
with periodic boundary conditions. A significant advantage of (
1.8
) is that it is self-adjoint and, thus, one may analyze
\lambda
through the Rayleigh quotient. There are two main steps in the analysis.

First, we establish the nonnegativity of
\lambda
. Heuristically, this occurs because, due to the
u
term,
V
is negative everywhere except far to the right, where it can take, at most, a small positive value (and that can only occur if
c>2
); hence, a potential eigenfunction trying to “minimize” its eigenvalue, must be large only on the far right. On the other hand, it must be periodic, meaning that it will be large on the far left where
V
is very negative. We are unable to make this intuition rigorous. Instead, in order to establish this rigorously, we develop a pair of functional inequalities (
3.17
)-(
3.18
) allowing us to show that, if
c>2
,

\int(\varphi_{x}^{a})^{2}-\int V(\varphi^{a})^{2}dx\geq 0,

when
a
is sufficiently large. This, after multiplying (
1.8
) by
\varphi^{a}
and integrating by parts, yields the nonnegativity of
\lambda
.

This brings us to the second step. The fact that
\lambda\geq 0
allows us to construct a supersolution of (
1.7
) via
\varphi^{a}
. This supersolution is only useful if we have good pointwise bounds on
\varphi^{a}
that hold for all
a
. We control the size of
\varphi^{a}
by constructing barriers and using the regularity imparted by (
1.8
). We use this to show that, if
c>2
,
u(0)
must be exponentially small in the parameter
a
, which contradicts the fact that we have constructed
u
to take a fixed positive value at
x=0
.

Theorem

1.4
is proved in
Section

3.3
.

Fast traveling waves

In view of
Proposition

1.3
and
Theorem

1.4
, it is natural to wonder if chemotaxis can
speed up
traveling waves. Our second main result establishes this.

Theorem 1.5
.

Fix any traveling wave solution
(c,u)
of (
1.1
) in the sense of
Definition

1.2
. For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, there exists
A_{\varepsilon}
such that

c\geq(1-\varepsilon)\frac{|\chi|}{2}

whenever

\sigma>A_{\varepsilon}\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{\sigma}{-\chi}>A_{\varepsilon}.

The constant
A_{\varepsilon}
depends only on
\varepsilon
and
K
.

Theorem

1.5
is essentially sharp when
1\ll-\chi\ll\sigma
. Indeed, the lower bound in
Theorem

1.5
and the upper bound in
Proposition

1.3
match up to
o(\chi)
. Roughly, this shows that the minimal speed traveling wave has speed
|\chi|/2+o(\chi)
.

We now discuss the idea of the proof. Roughly, the wider the front
1
1

1

Here, we loosely think of the front as the region in which
u\not\approx 1,0
.
is in a reaction-diffusion problem, the faster the wave should propagate. Intuitively, this is due to a wider region in which the “reaction” (in this case, the population reproducing) occurs. Mathematically, one can see this by integrating (
1.7
) to obtain

c=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u(1-u)\,dx,

and noticing that
u(1-u)
is
\approx 0
away from the front. On the other hand, if the front is narrow, one can show that
v\approx|\chi|/2
everywhere “near” the front. To see this, notice that, as
\sigma\gg 1
, roughly half the convolution occurs where
u\approx 1
while the other half the convolution occurs where
u\approx 0
. In this case, the wave is “pushed” by
v
at the speed
\approx|\chi|/2
. The proof proceeds by quantifying this intuition in a careful way.

In contrast to the usual FKPP equation, in which fronts are pulled (that is, the speed is determined by the behavior far to the right where
u\approx 0
), the intuition above shows that fronts are pushed when
-\chi>2
and
\sigma
is sufficiently large. Thus, the combination of
Theorems

1.4
and
1.5
shows a transition from pulled to pushed. To our knowledge the only similar setting in which this is observed is the Burgers-FKPP equation considered in
[
5
]
, discussed below. In both cases, a “threshold” exists for certain parameters under which the advection has no effect on the speed (although the front profile is changed) and over which speed-up occurs.

We collect here some preliminary results on the size of

Lemma 2.1

(Upper bound on

For any

\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\max\left\{1,\left(1-\chi/\sigma\right)^{-1}\right\}

3

3

We observe some preliminary bounds on

. We omit the proof.

A useful tool is that
u
is monotonic when it is small.

Lemma 2.3

(Monotonicity of
u
)
.

Suppose that
u
is a traveling wave solution of (
1.1
) in the sense of
Definition

1.2
. Then
u_{x}\leq 0
whenever:

\begin{split}&u<\frac{1}{1-\frac{\chi}{2\sigma}}=\frac{1}{1+\frac{|\chi|}{2\sigma}}\quad\text{if}\quad\chi\leq 0\qquad\text{ or }\qquad u<\frac{1-\frac{2\chi}{\sigma}}{\left(1-\frac{\chi}{\sigma}\right)^{2}}\quad\text{if}\quad\chi>0.\end{split}

Proof.

Fix any
\varepsilon>0
. Let
\overline{u}_{\rm mon}
be
(1-\chi/2\sigma)^{-1}
if
\chi\leq 0
and
(1-2\chi/\sigma)/(1-\chi/\sigma)^{2}
if
\chi>0
. We argue by contradiction, assuming that there exists
x_{0}
such that
u(x_{0})\leq\overline{u}_{\rm mon}-\varepsilon
and
u_{x}(x_{0})>0
. Define

y_{0}=\min\left\{y:u(y)\text{ is a local minimum and }u(y)\leq\overline{u}_{\rm mon}-\varepsilon\right\}.

Since
u\to 1
as
x\to-\infty
and due to the existence of
x_{0}
, it follows that
y_{0}
is well-defined. We point out that
u(x)\geq u(y_{0})
for all
x<y_{0}
(if this were not true, we could find another point in the set defining
y_{0}
that is smaller than
y_{0}
).

We re-write (
1.7
) as

u_{xx}-\left(v-c\right)u_{x}=-u\left(1-u-v_{x}\right).

We claim that
1-u-v_{x}>0
at
y_{0}
. If this were true, then, since
y_{0}
is the location of a local minimum, we find

0\leq u_{xx}-\left(v-c\right)u_{x}=-u\left(1-u-v_{x}\right)<0,

which is a contradiction. Hence, due to this and the arbitrariness of
\varepsilon
, the proof is finished if we can establish the positivity of
1-u-v_{x}
.

We now establish this inequality. Using (
2.1
), we notice that, at
y_{0}

\begin{split}1-u-v_{x}&=1-u\left(1-\frac{\chi}{\sigma}\right)-\chi\int_{0}^{\infty}(K_{\sigma})_{y}(y)(u(y_{0}-y)+u(y_{0}+y))\,dy.\end{split}

(2.2)

When
\chi\leq 0
,
-\chi(K_{\sigma})_{x}\geq 0
. Hence, using the choice of
y_{0}
, we find

\begin{split}-\chi\int_{0}^{\infty}(K_{\sigma})_{y}(y)(u(y_{0}-y)+u(y_{0}+y))\,dy&\geq-\chi\int_{0}^{\infty}(K_{\sigma})_{y}(y)u(y_{0}-y)\,dy\\
&\geq-\chi u(y_{0})\int_{0}^{\infty}(K_{\sigma})_{y}(y)\,dy=\frac{|\chi|}{2\sigma}u(y_{0}).\end{split}

Using this along with the definition of
\overline{u}_{\rm mon}
in (
2.2
), we find, at
y_{0}
,

1-u-v_{x}\geq 1-u\Big(1+\frac{|\chi|}{2\sigma}\Big)\geq 1-(\overline{u}_{\rm mon}-\varepsilon)\Big(1+\frac{|\chi|}{2\sigma}\Big)>0.

The proof is thus finished in this case.

When
\chi>0
,
-\chi(K_{\sigma})_{y}\leq 0
. Hence, by
2.1
,

-\chi\int_{0}^{\infty}(K_{\sigma})_{y}(y)(u(x-y)+u(x+y))\,dy\geq-2\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\chi\int_{0}^{\infty}(K_{\sigma})_{y}(y)\,dy\geq-\frac{\chi}{\sigma}\frac{1}{1-\frac{\chi}{\sigma}}.

Including this inequality in (
2.2
) and using the definition of
\overline{u}_{\rm mon}
, we find

1-u(x)-v_{x}(x)>1-(\overline{u}_{\rm mon}-\varepsilon)\left(1-\frac{\chi}{\sigma}\right)-\frac{\chi}{\sigma}\frac{1}{1-\frac{\chi}{\sigma}}>0.

In both cases, we have established that
1-u-v_{x}>0
, which completes the proof.
∎

Finally we recall that

on the left when

is appropriately bounded above. We cite

Lemma 2.4

[

Suppose that

3
Speed 2 traveling waves:

In this section, we construct traveling waves whose speed is unperturbed off the case

when

After, we return to this slab problem to analyze the bounds on

3.1
The problem on a finite slab

Let

,
\tau\in[0,1]
, and
\theta\in(0,\theta_{0})
for

\theta_{0}<\min\Big\{\frac{1}{100},\frac{1-\frac{2|\chi|}{\sigma}}{1+\frac{|\chi|}{\sigma}}\Big\}

that is chosen in the sequel.
Throughout this section

is a constant decreased in order to encode the various statements “for

\theta_{0}
of

Consider

\begin{cases}-cu_{x}+\tau(\widetilde{v}u)_{x}=u_{xx}+u(1-u),\qquad&\text{ in }(-a,a),\\

(3.2)

where we define

with

\widetilde{u}(x)=\begin{cases}1,\quad&\text{ if }x\leq-a,\\
u(x),&\text{ if }x\in(-a,a),\\
0,&\text{ if }x\geq a.\end{cases}

(3.3)

It is apparent that were
u
and

to exist, both would depend on

and
\theta
. We suppress that dependence in the notation in this subsection. In the sequel, however, we denote

and

in order to clarify the presentation of the limit

.

Our goal is to produce a solution to
(c,u)
to (

) when

is sufficiently large and
\theta
is sufficiently small. The first step is obtaining

(c,u)
of (

3.1.1
Bounds on

It is clear that the maximum principle yields the same upper bound of

in
2.1
and the same monotonicity properties as
Lemma

2.3
. We collect this in the following (recall (
3.1
)):

Lemma 3.1

(Upper bound on and monotonicity of
u
)
.

For all
a>0
and
\theta\in(0,\theta_{0})
, we have

\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\max\left\{1,\left(1-\chi/\sigma\right)^{-1}\right\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad u_{x}\leq 0\ \text{ on }[0,a].

In addition, a simple computation using the definition of
\widetilde{v}
yields

\|\widetilde{v}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{|\chi|}{2}\max\{1,(1-\chi/\sigma)^{-1}\}\quad\text{ and }\quad\|\widetilde{v}_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{|\chi|}{\sigma}\max\{1,(1-\chi/\sigma)^{-1}\}.

(3.4)

See
Lemma

2.2
for similar bounds.

Next we get a preliminary, suboptimal bound on the front speed that is sufficient for the fixed point argument.

Lemma 3.2

(Upper bound on the speed)
.

There exists
\theta_{0}>0
and
C>0
such that if
\theta\in(0,\theta_{0})
and
a
is sufficiently large, depending only on
\theta
, then

c\leq 2\sqrt{1+\frac{\tau|\chi|}{\sigma}}+\frac{\tau|\chi|}{2}\leq 2+\frac{\tau|\chi|}{\sigma}+\frac{\tau|\chi|}{2}.

Proof.

Let
\psi_{A}(x)=Ae^{-\lambda x}
for
\lambda>0
to be chosen. Let
A_{0}=\min\{A\in\mathbb{R}:\psi_{A}\geq u\}.

First, notice that this is well-defined since
A=e^{\lambda a}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}
is in the set, while no negative
A
are in the set due to the nonnegativity of
u
. In fact,
A_{0}\geq\theta>0
since

u(0)=\theta\quad\text{ and }\quad\psi_{A}(0)=A.

Finally, by continuity, there exists a point
x_{0}\in[-a,a]
such that
\psi_{A_{0}}(x_{0})=u(x_{0})
.

Since
\psi_{A_{0}}>0
and
u(a)=0
, then
x_{0}\in[-a,a)
. In addition, if
x_{0}=-a
, then
1=u(-a)=\psi_{A_{0}}(-a)=A_{0}e^{\lambda a}
. Hence,

\theta=u(0)\leq\psi_{A_{0}}(0)=A_{0}=e^{-\lambda a}.

This cannot happen if
a>\lambda^{-1}\log(1/\theta)
.

Thus, we have that
x_{0}\in(-a,a)
. Then
\psi_{A_{0}}-u
has an interior minimum at
x_{0}
of zero, which yields

u(x_{0})=\psi_{A_{0}}(x_{0}),\quad\psi_{A_{0},x}(x_{0})=u_{x}(x_{0}),\quad\text{ and }\quad\psi_{A_{0},xx}(x_{0})\geq u_{xx}(x_{0}).

Using (
3.2
), these identities, (
3.4
), and (
1.5
), we find, at
x_{0}
,

\begin{split}0&=u_{xx}+u(1-u)+cu_{x}-\tau(\widetilde{v}u)_{x}\leq\psi_{A_{0}}\left(\lambda^{2}+1-c\lambda+\lambda\tau\widetilde{v}-\tau\widetilde{v}_{x}\right)\\
&\leq\psi_{A_{0}}\left(\lambda^{2}+1-c\lambda+\frac{\tau|\chi|\lambda}{2}+\tau\frac{|\chi|}{\sigma}\right).\end{split}

We now choose
\lambda=(c-\tau|\chi|/2)/2
to find, using the positivity of
\psi_{A_{0}}
,

\begin{split}0&\leq-\frac{(c-\tau|\chi|/2)^{2}}{4}+1+\tau\frac{|\chi|}{\sigma}.\end{split}

This implies that
c\leq\tau|\chi|/2+2\sqrt{1+\tau|\chi|/\sigma}
, which, after an application of Taylor’s theorem, concludes the proof.
∎

We now know that all coefficients in our equation are bounded in
L^{\infty}
, which allows us the use of the Harnack inequality. We use this to derive an estimate quantifying the fact that if
u
is “small” somewhere then it is small “nearby.”

Lemma 3.3

(Upper bound on spatial growth)
.

There exists a constant
C_{\chi,\chi/\sigma}
such that

u(x)\leq C_{\chi,\chi/\sigma}u(y)e^{C_{\chi,\chi/\sigma}|x-y|}\qquad\text{ for all }x,y\in[-a+1,a-1].

Proof.

This follows by a simple iteration of the Harnack inequality
n\approx|x-y|
times.
∎

Lemma 3.4

(Lower bound on the speed)
.

For all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\theta_{\varepsilon}
and
a_{\varepsilon}
such that if
\theta\leq\theta_{\varepsilon}
and
a>a_{\varepsilon}
then
c\geq 2-\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Here we simply construct a subsolution that “pushes”
u
to speed
2
. We argue by contradiction. Suppose that
\varepsilon>0
and
c<2-\varepsilon
.

For any
A,\lambda>0
and
R\in(0,a-1]
to be chosen, let

\underline{u}_{A}(x)=\frac{1}{A}e^{-\lambda x}\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{2x-R}{R}\right)\qquad\text{ for }x\in\left[0,R\right].

Since
u>0
on
[0,R]
,
\underline{u}_{A}<u
on
[0,R]
if
A
is sufficiently large. Let

A_{0}=\inf\left\{A>0:\underline{u}_{A}<u\text{ on }\left[0,R\right]\right\}.

It is clear that
A_{0}
is well-defined and that, by continuity, there exists
x_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}}\in[0,R]
such that
\underline{u}_{A_{0}}(x_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}})=u(x_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}})
. Since
u(0),u(R)>0
and
\underline{u}_{A_{0}}(0)=\underline{u}_{A_{0}}(R)=0
, we find
x_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}}\in(0,R)
. Finally, by construction
u-\underline{u}_{A_{0}}\geq 0
on
[0,R]
and has a minimum over
[0,R]
of
0
at
x_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}}
.

For notational ease, we drop the
A_{0}
notation and denote
\underline{u}_{A_{0}}=\underline{u}
. Since
u-\underline{u}
has a minimum of
0
at
x_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}}
, we find

u_{x}(x_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}})=\underline{u}_{x}(x_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad u_{xx}(x_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}})\geq\underline{u}_{xx}(x_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}}).

Using these along with (
3.2
), we find, at
x_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}}
,

\begin{split}0&\leq(u-\underline{u})_{xx}=-cu_{x}+\tau\left(\widetilde{v}u\right)_{x}-u(1-u)-\underline{u}_{xx}\\
&=-c\underline{u}_{x}+\tau\widetilde{v}_{x}\underline{u}+\tau\widetilde{v}\underline{u}_{x}-\underline{u}(1-u)-\underline{u}_{xx}.\end{split}

(3.5)

Recall that
u
is decreasing on
[0,a]
, and, hence,
\underline{u}_{x}(x_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}})=u_{x}(x_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}})\leq 0
. In addition,
u(x_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}})\leq u(0)=\theta
.

We first estimate
\widetilde{v}
and
\widetilde{v}_{x}
. Fix
L>0
to be chosen. We claim that there is a constant
C_{\chi,\sigma}
such that

|\widetilde{v}_{x}(x_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}})|,|\widetilde{v}(x_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}})|\leq C_{\chi,\sigma}K_{\sigma}(L)+C_{\chi,\sigma}\int_{L}^{\infty}|K_{\sigma}(x)|dx+C_{\chi,\sigma}\theta e^{C_{\chi,\sigma}L}.

(3.6)

We show the argument only for
\widetilde{v}
. The argument is similar for
\widetilde{v}_{x}
, though additionally using (
2.1
). Indeed, decomposing the integral and then using
3.3
on the close-to-
x_{\rm touch}
portion, we find

\begin{split}|\widetilde{v}(x_{\rm touch})|&\leq\int_{-L}^{L}|K_{\sigma}(y)|u(x_{\rm touch}-y)dy+\left(\int_{-\infty}^{-L}+\int_{L}^{\infty}\right)|K_{\sigma}(y)|u(x_{\rm touch}-y)dy\\
&\leq C_{\chi,\chi/\sigma}\int_{-L}^{L}|K_{\sigma}(y)|u(x_{\rm touch})e^{C_{\chi,\chi/\sigma}|y|}dy+\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{-L}+\int_{L}^{\infty}\right)|K_{\sigma}(y)|dy.\end{split}

Then (
3.6
) is obtained by using the following:
K_{\sigma}
is bounded and odd,
u(x_{\rm touch})\leq\theta
, and
\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}
is bounded due to
3.1
.

Returning to (
3.6
), choosing
L
sufficiently large and
\theta
sufficiently small, we have
|\widetilde{v}_{x}(x_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}})|,|\widetilde{v}(x_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}})|<\varepsilon^{4}
. Using this, as well as the facts that
\tau\leq 1
,
c<2-\varepsilon
,
\underline{u}_{x}\leq 0
, and
u\leq\theta
, we find

0\leq-\left(2-\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{4}\right)\underline{u}_{x}-\underline{u}(1-\theta-\varepsilon^{4})-\underline{u}_{xx}.

Let
y_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}}=\pi(2x_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}}-R)/(2R)
. Using the definition of
\underline{u}
to compute
\underline{u}_{x}
and
\underline{u}_{xx}
and dividing through by
e^{-\lambda x_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}}}/A
, we find

\begin{split}&0\leq\left(2-\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{4}\right)\left(\frac{\pi}{R}\sin\left(y_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}}\right)+\lambda\cos\left(y_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}}\right)\right)-\cos(y_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}})(1-\theta-\varepsilon^{4})\\
&\qquad-\left(-\frac{\pi^{2}}{R^{2}}\cos(y_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}})+2\lambda\frac{\pi}{R}\sin(y_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}})+\lambda^{2}\cos(y_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}})\right)\\
&=\cos(y_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}})\left(\left(2-\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{4}\right)\lambda-1+\theta+\varepsilon^{4}-\lambda^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{R^{2}}\right)+\sin(y_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}})\left(\left(2-\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{4}-2\lambda\right)\frac{\pi}{R}\right).\end{split}

To eliminate the
\sin
term, whose sign is unknown, let
\lambda=1-\varepsilon/2+\varepsilon^{4}/2
. This yields

0\leq\cos(y_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}})\left(\frac{1}{4}\left(2-\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{4}\right)^{2}-1+\theta+\varepsilon^{4}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{R^{2}}\right).

Further increasing
R
(and, thus,
a
) and decreasing
\theta
and
\varepsilon
, if necessary, the coefficient of
\cos
is negative. In addition, as
x_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}}\in(0,R)
,
y_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}}\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
, and, hence,
\cos(y_{\text{\rm\tiny touch}})>0
. We conclude that the the right hand side above is negative, which is a contradiction. The proof is finished.
∎

3.1.2
Existence of a solution on the slab

We now use Leray-Schauder degree theory to establish the following proposition. See
[
33
]
for a review of standard results in this theory that are used below.

Proposition 3.5
.

There exists
\theta_{0}>0
such that, for any
\theta\in(0,\theta_{0})
and any
a
sufficiently large, there exists a solution
(c,u)
of (
3.2
) with
\tau=1
. Further,
u\geq\theta
on
[-a,0]
.

Proof.

We first point out the following bound on any solution
(c,u)
of (
3.2
). By
3.1
,
3.4
and
3.2
and elliptic regularity theory, we have that, for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, if
\theta
is sufficiently small and
a
sufficiently large (depending only on
\theta
), there exists a constant
C_{0}>0
, depending on
\chi
and
\sigma
but not on
\tau
, such that

\frac{1}{C_{0}}<c\quad\text{ and }\quad c+\|u\|_{C^{2,\alpha}}\leq C_{0}.

(3.7)

Let
\mathcal{B}=\{(c,u)\in[0,2C_{0}]\times C^{1,\alpha}:\|u\|_{C^{1,\alpha}}\leq 2C_{0},u\geq 0\}
. Consider the operator:

S_{\tau}:\mathcal{B}\to C^{1,\alpha}\times\mathbb{R}

such that
S_{\tau}(c,u)=(c+\theta-\max_{x\geq 0}u(x),\bar{u})
, where
\bar{u}
is the unique solution of the linear problem

\begin{cases}-c\bar{u}_{x}+\tau(\chi(K_{\sigma}*\widetilde{u})\bar{u})_{x}=\bar{u}_{xx}+u(1-u)\quad&\text{ for all }x\in(-a,a),\\
\bar{u}(-a)=1\quad\bar{u}(a)=0\end{cases}

(recall the definition of
\widetilde{u}
from (
3.3
)). It is important to note that
\widetilde{u}
is defined in terms of
u
, not
\bar{u}
, so that the above equation is linear in
\bar{u}
.

At this point we note that the existence of a solution of (
3.2
) is equivalent to the existence of a fixed point of
S_{1}
. We now establish the existence of such a fixed point.

Standard elliptic regularity theory provides bounds on the
C^{2,\alpha}
norm of
\bar{u}
depending only on the
C^{1,\alpha}
norm of
u
and
a
. Hence, we have that
S_{\tau}
is a compact operator. Moreover, if
a
is sufficiently large, any fixed point of
S_{\tau}
is an element of the interior of
\mathcal{B}
, by (
3.7
). Hence, we have that

\deg(\id-S_{1},\mathcal{B},0)=\deg(\id-S_{0},\mathcal{B},0).

On the other hand, any fixed point of
S_{0}
is a solution to the “slab” problem for the Fisher-KPP equation. The uniqueness of such solutions is well-known (and easy to establish). Hence,
\deg(\id-S_{0},\mathcal{B},0)=1
or
-1
. In either case, we see that
\deg(\id-S_{1},\mathcal{B},0)\neq 0
, which establishes the existence of a fixed point.

The last step is to establish the lower bound of
u
on
[-a,0]
. To this end, if
u(x_{0})<\theta
for some
x_{0}\in[-a,0]
, then we may apply
Lemma

2.3
(which can clearly be extended to the slab problem) to conclude that
u
is decreasing on
[x_{0},a]
. It follows that
u(0)\leq u(x_{0})<\theta
, which contradicts the fact that, by construction,
u(0)=\theta
. We conclude that
u\geq\theta
on
[0,a]
, as claimed. Thus, the proof is complete.
∎

3.2
Taking
a\to\infty
: the proof of
Proposition

1.3

Proposition 3.6
.

There exists a traveling wave solution
(c,u)
, in the sense of
Definition

1.2
with

c\leq 2\sqrt{1+\frac{|\chi|}{\sigma}}+\frac{|\chi|}{2}\leq 2+\frac{|\chi|}{\sigma}+\frac{|\chi|}{2}.

Furthermore,
u(-\infty)=1
.

Proof.

For clarity, we denote the solution constructed in (
3.5
) as
(c^{a},u^{a})
. The bounds in (
3.7
) are uniform in
a
. Hence, for any
\theta\in(0,\theta_{0})
, there exists a sequence
a_{n}\to\infty
and
(c,u)\in[0,2+|\chi|/2+|\chi|/\sigma]\times C^{2,\alpha}
such that, as
n\to\infty
,
c^{a_{n}}\to c
and
u^{a_{n}}\to u
. Using (
3.2
), it is easy to check that

-cu_{x}+(vu)_{x}=u_{xx}+u(1-u)\qquad\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R},

(3.8)

where

is as in (
1.2
).

Next, we establish the behavior of

as

. By

, let

. Let
u_{n}(x)=u(x+n)
. Since

satisfies (

), we may take a subsequence

locally uniformly in

. Using this, as well as the fact that

satisfies (

) with

, we find

0=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(-c(u_{n})_{x}+(v_{n}u_{n})_{x}-(u_{n})_{xx}-u_{n}(1-u_{n})\right)=\delta(1-\delta).

Note that we used that, since

locally uniformly then

locally uniformly. As

, it follows that

. Thus

It follows immediately from

2.3
and the fact that

that

. The fact that this limit is equal to one is due to

. This concludes the proof.
∎

The proof of this claim follows exactly as in the

Clearly the combination of

3.3
Minimal speed traveling waves with speed
2

For notational ease, we fix a sequence
a_{n}\to\infty
along which
(c_{n},u_{n})=(c^{a_{n}},u^{a_{n}})
converges to
(c_{U},U)
, where we use

to denote the traveling wave constructed in the previous section. Since we mainly work with

in this section, we suppress the dependence on

always refers to the approximate traveling wave on the slab

and

refers to its locally uniform limit as

\chi
and
\sigma

We now improve the bounds of

to show that

for all

(see

1.4

Proposition 3.8
.

Suppose
\theta<\theta_{0}
. There exists
\delta_{\theta}>0
, depending only on
\theta
and
K
such that if
|\chi|(\sigma^{2}+\sigma^{-1})\in(0,\delta_{\theta})
and

, then

Proof.

We proceed by contradiction, writing

c=2+\varepsilon\quad\text{ for some }\varepsilon\in(0,2|\chi|(1+\sigma^{-1})],

(3.9)

where the upper bound follows from
3.2
. Define
w:[-a,a]\to\mathbb{R}
by

u(x)=\exp\left\{-\frac{c}{2}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{x}\widetilde{v}(y)\,dy\right\}w(x).

Let
V(x)=1-(u+(c-\widetilde{v})^{2}/4-\widetilde{v}_{x}/2)
. From (
3.2
), we find

\begin{cases}-w_{xx}-wV=0\qquad\text{ on }(-a,a),\\
w(-a)=\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}ca-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{-a}\widetilde{v}(y)\,dy\right\},\quad w(0)=\theta,\quad w(a)=0.\end{cases}

(3.10)

At this point it is useful to notice a few key properties of
V
. First,

V=-u-\varepsilon\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)+\widetilde{v}\left(\frac{c}{2}-\frac{\widetilde{v}}{4}\right)+\frac{\widetilde{v}_{x}}{2}.

(3.11)

The important thing is that, when
|\chi|(1+\sigma^{-1})
is small enough,
V<0
except (perhaps) far to the right where it is small and decays to zero. This heuristic should imply that the principal eigenvalue
\lambda
of
-\partial_{xx}-V
should be positive. The corresponding principal eigenfunction is then a supersolution of (
3.10
), which we use to derive a contradiction.

We now make this argument precise. Let
(\lambda,\varphi)
solve

\begin{cases}-\varphi_{xx}-\varphi V=\lambda\varphi\qquad\text{ in }(-a,a),\\
1=\varphi(-a)=\varphi(a),\quad\varphi>0.\end{cases}

(3.12)

One way to construct a solution of (
3.12
) is via the Rayleigh quotient representation of
\lambda
; that is,

\lambda=\min_{\psi\in H_{\rm per}^{1}([-a,a])}\frac{\int_{-a}^{a}(\psi_{x}^{2}-V\psi^{2})\,dx}{\int_{-a}^{a}\psi^{2}dx},

(3.13)

where
H^{1}_{\rm per}([-a,a])
is the closure of all
2a
periodic
C^{1}
functions under the
H^{1}([-a,a])
norm. The existence and positivity of a minimizer and the fact that a minimizer is, up to normalization, a solution of (
3.12
) is classical.

We postpone the analysis of (
3.12
) momentarily, though we record the important properties of
(\lambda,\varphi)
in

, below, which is proved in
Section

3.3.1
.

Lemma 3.9
.

For
|\chi|(\sigma^{2}+\sigma^{-1})
and
\theta_{0}
small enough,
\lambda\geq 0
and
\varphi(0)\leq\exp\{a/2\}
for all
a
sufficiently large when
c=2+\varepsilon
with
\varepsilon\geq 0
.

We now use
3.9
to conclude. Let
\varphi_{A}(x)=A\varphi(x)
. If
A
is sufficiently large, then
\varphi_{A}>w
because
w
is bounded and
\varphi
is uniformly positive. Let
A_{0}=\inf\{A>0:\varphi_{A}>w\}
. Then we find
x_{0}\in[-a,a]
such that
\varphi_{A_{0}}(x_{0})=w(x_{0})
. Since
w>0
on
[-a,a)
and
\varphi>0
, it follows that
A_{0}>0
.
There are three cases.

Case one:
x_{0}=a
.
This, clearly, cannot occur since
\varphi_{A_{0}}(a)=A_{0}\varphi(a)>0=w(a)
.

Case two:
x_{0}\in(-a,a)
.
First, note that, due to
3.9
,

-\varphi_{xx}-V\varphi=\lambda\varphi\geq 0.

Hence
\varphi_{A_{0}}
is a supersolution of (
3.12
). Recall that
\varphi_{A_{0}}>w(a)
and
\varphi_{A_{0}}\geq w
on
[-a,a)
, by construction. Applying the strong maximum principle, we conclude that
\varphi_{A_{0}}>w
on
(-a,a)
, which contradicts the fact that
x_{0}\in(-a,a)
. Thus this case cannot occur.

Case three:
x_{0}=-a
.
Then we have that

A_{0}=\varphi_{A_{0}}(-a)=w(-a)=\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left(ca-\int_{0}^{-a}\widetilde{v}(y)\,dy\right)\right\}.

In addition, using this identity for
A_{0}
, we find

\theta=w(0)\leq\varphi_{A_{0}}(0)\leq A_{0}\varphi(0)\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left(ca-\int_{0}^{-a}\widetilde{v}(y)\,dy\right)\right\}\exp\left\{\frac{a}{2}\right\}.

(3.14)

By assumption
c\geq 2
. In addition, if
|\chi|(1+\sigma^{-1})
is sufficiently small, then, by (
3.4
),

\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{-a}|\widetilde{v}(y)|\,dy\leq\frac{a}{4}.

Hence, the right hand side of (
3.14
) clearly tends to zero as
a\to\infty
. This contradicts the fact that
\theta
is positive and independent of
a
.

We have reached a contradiction in all cases, which implies that

. This concludes the proof.
∎

3.3.1
Nonnegativity of the eigenvalue and asymptotics of its eigenfunction

The first step is establishing the exponential decay of
u
for all
a
sufficiently large and all
\chi
and
\sigma
such that
|\chi|(1+\sigma^{-1})
is sufficiently small.

Lemma 3.10

(Exponential decay of
u
)
.

There exists
\mu,\delta_{\theta}>0
such that if
|\chi|(1+\sigma^{-1})\leq\delta_{\theta}
and
a>1/\delta_{\theta}
and
\theta<1/4
, then

u(x)\leq\theta e^{-\mu x}\qquad\text{ for all }x\geq 0.

The constant
\delta_{\theta}
depends on
\theta
.

Proof.

Let
\rho=u_{x}/u
, and fix
\mu\in(0,1/4]
a constant to be chosen. We prove this lemma in two steps. First, we show that
\rho
cannot have a maximum greater than
-\mu
on
(0,a)
. Since
\rho(a)=-\infty
, then
\rho(x)\leq\max\{-\mu,\rho(0)\}
. Second, we show that
\rho(0)\leq-\mu
. We conclude that
\rho(x)\leq-\mu
, which finishes the proof.

Step one:
\rho
cannot have an interior max larger than
-\mu
.
Suppose that
x_{0}
is the location of an interior maximum
M\geq-\mu
. Using that
u\rho=u_{x}
and
u\rho_{x}+u\rho^{2}=u_{xx}
in (
3.2
) (with
\tau=1
) and that
\rho=M
and
\rho_{x}=0
at
x_{0}
, we obtain

0=(c-\widetilde{v})u_{x}+u_{xx}+u(1-u-\widetilde{v}_{x})=u\left((c-\widetilde{v})M+M^{2}+(1-u-\widetilde{v}_{x})\right).

Hence,

M=-\frac{c-\widetilde{v}\pm\sqrt{(c-\widetilde{v})^{2}-4(1-u-\widetilde{v}_{x})}}{2}\leq\frac{-\left(c-\widetilde{v}\right)+\sqrt{(c-\widetilde{v})^{2}-4(1-u-\widetilde{v}_{x})}}{2}.

(3.15)

Recall from
Proposition

1.3
that

2\leq c\leq 2+\frac{|\chi|}{2}+\frac{|\chi|}{\sigma}.

(3.16)

Hence, taking
|\chi|(1+\sigma^{-1})
to zero, the right hand side above tends to

\frac{-2+\sqrt{4-4(1-u)}}{2}=-1+\sqrt{u(x_{0})}\leq-1+\sqrt{\theta}.

As
\theta<1/4
by assumption, this yields
M<-1/2<-1/4\leq-\mu
. Hence, if
|\chi|(1+\sigma^{-1})
is sufficiently small, (
3.15
) cannot hold. This contradiction concludes the proof of this step.

Step two:
\rho(0)\leq-\mu
.
We argue by contradiction: if the claim were not true for all
a
sufficiently large and
|\chi|(1+\sigma^{-1})
sufficiently small, then there would exist sequences
a_{n}
,
\chi_{n}
, and
\sigma_{n}
, such that
a_{n}\to\infty
and
|\chi_{n}|(1+\sigma_{n}^{-1})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
such that
\rho^{\chi_{n},\sigma_{n}}(0)>-\mu
.

Recall (
3.16
). By compactness,
(c^{\chi_{n},\sigma_{n}},u^{\chi_{n},\sigma_{n}})
converges to the minimal speed traveling wave solution
(2,U)
of the Fisher-KPP equation

-2U_{x}=U_{xx}+U(1-U),

with the normalization
U(0)=\theta
, and
\rho^{\chi_{n},\sigma_{n}}(0)
converges to
U_{x}(0)/U(0)
.

Recall, from, e.g.,
[
24
, Theorem 1.3]
, the asymptotics of the Fisher-KPP equation: if
\overline{U}
is the solution to the Fisher-KPP equation with the normalization
\overline{U}(0)=1/2
, then there exists
\kappa
such that
\overline{U}(x)/(\kappa xe^{-x})\to 1
as
x\to\infty
. By the uniqueness of traveling wave solutions for the Fisher-KPP equation, there exists
x_{\theta}
, tending to
\infty
as
\theta
tends to zero, such that
U(x)=\overline{U}(x+x_{\theta})
. Further, by the monotonicity of the traveling wave for the Fisher-KPP equation,
x_{\theta}
increases as
\theta
decreases. Hence,

\sup_{[0,\infty)}\frac{U_{x}}{U}=\sup_{[x_{\theta},\infty)}\frac{\overline{U}_{x}}{\overline{U}}\leq\sup_{[0,\infty)}\frac{\overline{U}_{x}}{\overline{U}}.

The first inequality follows since
\theta<\theta_{0}<1/2
.

Thus, let

\mu=-\frac{1}{4}\sup_{[0,\infty)}\frac{\overline{U}_{x}}{\overline{U}}.

First, notice that
\overline{U}_{x}/\overline{U}\to-1
as
x\to\infty
, so
\mu\leq 1/4
, which is consistent with our choice in the previous step. On the other hand, since
\overline{U}_{x}/\overline{U}
is a continuous, negative function on
[0,\infty)
such that
\overline{U}_{x}(0)<0
and
\overline{U}_{x}/\overline{U}\to-1
as

, we find that
\mu>0
.

Choosing
|\chi|(1+\sigma^{-1})
sufficiently small, depending on
\theta
, we find

-\mu<\rho(0)\leq\frac{1}{2}U(0)=\frac{1}{2}\sup_{[0,\infty)}\frac{U_{x}}{U}\leq-\mu.

This is a contradiction, which concludes the proof.
∎

\theta
and

\varphi_{x}(x)\leq\frac{\varphi_{x}(a)}{2}\quad\text{ for all }x\in[-a,\bar{x}].

. This concludes the proof.

Proof of

3.17
) to (
3.18
). Indeed, integrating by parts, we obtain

\int_{-a}^{a}\widetilde{v}_{x}f^{2}\,dx=f(-a)^{2}\left[\widetilde{v}(a)-\widetilde{v}(-a)\right]-2\int_{-a}^{a}\widetilde{v}ff_{x}\,dx.

(3.28)

Using the Sobolev inequality, we see that, for some
C>0
that changes line-by-line in the sequel but is independent of all parameters,

f(-a)^{2}[\widetilde{v}(a)-\widetilde{v}(-a)]\leq C\|\widetilde{v}\|_{L^{\infty}}\left(\int_{-a}^{-a+1}(f^{2}+f_{x}^{2})\,dx\right)

and using Young’s inequality, we find

-2\int_{-a}^{a}\widetilde{v}ff_{x}dx\leq\int_{-a}^{a}|\widetilde{v}|f^{2}dx+\|\widetilde{v}\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{-a}^{a}f_{x}^{2}dx.

Hence, (
3.28
) becomes

\int_{-a}^{a}\widetilde{v}_{x}f^{2}\,dx\leq C\|\widetilde{v}\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{-a}^{-a+1}f^{2}\,dx+\|\widetilde{v}\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{-a}^{a}|f_{x}|^{2}dx+\int_{-a}^{a}|\widetilde{v}|f^{2}dx.

Recalling the bounds on
\widetilde{v}
in (
3.4
) and that
u\geq\theta
on
[-a,0]
due to
3.5
, we find

\begin{split}\int_{-a}^{a}\widetilde{v}_{x}f^{2}\,dx&\leq\frac{C|\chi|}{\theta}\int_{-a}^{-a+1}uf^{2}\,dx+C|\chi|\int_{-a}^{a}f_{x}^{2}dx+\int_{-a}^{a}|\widetilde{v}|f^{2}dx\\
&\leq\frac{C|\chi|}{\theta}\left(\int_{-a}^{a}uf^{2}\,dx+\int_{-a}^{a}|f_{x}|^{2}\,dx\right)+\int_{-a}^{a}|\widetilde{v}|f^{2}\,dx.\end{split}

(3.29)

It is now clear from (
3.29
) that (
3.17
) holds if (
3.18
) does. We now establish (
3.18
). First, by
3.5
,
u(x)\geq\theta
for all
x\in[-a,0]
so that
|\widetilde{v}(x)|\leq C|\chi|u(x)/\theta
for all
x\in[-a,0]
. Thus,

\int_{-a}^{0}|\widetilde{v}|f^{2}\,dx\leq\frac{C|\chi|}{\theta}\int_{-a}^{0}uf^{2}\,dx.

(3.30)

Next, we bound the integral on
[0,a]
. Indeed,

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{a}|\widetilde{v}|f^{2}\,dx&=\int_{0}^{a}|\widetilde{v}|\left(\int_{0}^{x}f_{x}dy+f(0)\right)^{2}dx\leq 2\int_{0}^{a}|\widetilde{v}|\left(x\int_{0}^{x}f_{x}^{2}dy+f(0)^{2}\right)\,dx.\end{split}

(3.31)

As above, we bound
f(0)
using the Sobolev inequality and the fact that
u\geq\theta
on
[-1,0]
. Indeed:

\begin{split}|f(0)|^{2}&\leq C\int_{-1}^{0}f_{x}^{2}\,dx+C\int_{-1}^{0}f^{2}\,dx\leq C\int_{-1}^{0}f_{x}^{2}\,dx+\frac{C}{\theta}\int_{-1}^{0}uf^{2}\,dx\\
&\leq C\int_{-a}^{a}f_{x}^{2}\,dx+\frac{C}{\theta}\int_{-a}^{a}uf^{2}\,dx.\end{split}

Hence, (
3.31
) becomes

\int_{0}^{a}|\widetilde{v}|f^{2}\,dx\leq C\left(\int_{-a}^{a}|f_{x}|^{2}\,dx+\frac{C}{\theta}\int_{-a}^{a}uf^{2}\,dx\right)\int_{0}^{a}(1+x)|\widetilde{v}|\,dx.

Clearly we have that

\int_{0}^{a}(1+x)|\widetilde{v}|\,dx\leq 2\int_{0}^{a}x|\widetilde{v}|\,dx+\int_{0}^{1}|\widetilde{v}|\,dx\leq 2\int_{0}^{a}x|\widetilde{v}|\,dx+C|\chi|.

As a result, the proof is finished if

\int_{0}^{a}x|\widetilde{v}|\,dx\leq C|\chi|\sigma^{2}.

(3.32)

We now establish this inequality. We begin by using the exact form of
\widetilde{v}
along with the decay of
u
given in
3.10
. Indeed,

\begin{split}\frac{1}{|\chi|}\int_{0}^{a}x|\widetilde{v}|\,dx&\leq\int_{0}^{a}x\int_{0}^{\infty}|K_{\sigma}(y)||\widetilde{u}(x-y)-\widetilde{u}(x+y)|\,dydx\\
&=\int_{0}^{a}x\left(\int_{0}^{x/2}+\int_{x/2}^{\infty}\right)|K_{\sigma}(y)||\widetilde{u}(x-y)-\widetilde{u}(x+y)|\,dydx\\
&\leq\theta\int_{0}^{a}x\int_{0}^{x/2}|K_{\sigma}(y)|e^{-\mu(x-y)}\,dydx+2\int_{0}^{a}x\int_{x/2}^{\infty}|K_{\sigma}(y)|\,dydx.\end{split}

(3.33)

In the last inequality, we used that
u
is bounded above by
2
(recall
3.1
and that
|\chi|/\sigma<1/2
, by assumption).

We bound the first term on the last line of (
3.33
). Re-writing the integral, we find

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{a}x\int_{0}^{x/2}|K_{\sigma}(y)|e^{-\mu(x-y)}\,dydx&=\int_{0}^{a/2}|K_{\sigma}(y)|\int_{2y}^{a}xe^{-\mu(x-y)}\,dxdy\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{a/2}|K_{\sigma}(y)|\left(\frac{e^{-\mu y}(1+2\mu y)}{\mu^{2}}\right)\,dy\leq C,\end{split}

(3.34)

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
\|K_{\sigma}\|_{L^{1}}=\|K\|_{L^{1}}=1
(see (
1.3
)).

Next we bound the second term on the last line of (
3.33
). Using the fact that
|K|=-K=-\overline{K}^{\prime}
on
[0,\infty)
with
(1+|x|)\overline{K}\in L^{1}
(see (
1.4
)), we find

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{a}x&\int_{x/2}^{\infty}|K_{\sigma}(y)|\,dydx=-\int_{0}^{a}x\int_{x/2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sigma}\overline{K}^{\prime}\left(\frac{y}{\sigma}\right)\,dydx=-\int_{0}^{a}x\int_{x/2}^{\infty}\left(\overline{K}\left(\frac{y}{\sigma}\right)\right)_{y}dydx\\
&=\int_{0}^{a}x\overline{K}\left(\frac{x}{2\sigma}\right)\,dx\leq C\sigma^{2}.\end{split}

(3.35)

The combination of (
3.33
), (
3.34
), and (
3.35
) establishes (
3.32
). Thus, the proof is complete.
∎

4
Fast traveling waves:
Theorem

1.5

We now establish large lower bounds on the traveling wave speed
c
whenever
\sigma
is large, in absolute terms and relative to
-\chi
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.5
.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
and suppose that

c<\frac{(1-\varepsilon)|\chi|}{2}.

(4.1)

Recall that

\overline{K}(x)=-\int_{x}^{\infty}K(y)\,dy=-\int_{x\sigma}^{\infty}K_{\sigma}(y)\,dy\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{K}(0)=\frac{1}{2}.

The last equality follows from the symmetry of
K
and (
1.3
).
Now we define the constants

\theta=\frac{|\chi|}{2R}\quad\text{ and }\quad R=\frac{1}{2}\overline{K}^{-1}\left(\frac{1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}}{2}\right).

(4.2)

Without loss of generality, we may restrict to the case where
\varepsilon<1/10
and is sufficiently small that
R\leq 1
. Next, choose
A_{\varepsilon}
such that, if
\sigma,\sigma/|\chi|\geq A_{\varepsilon}
, then

\frac{\theta}{\sigma}=\frac{|\chi|}{2\sigma R}<\varepsilon/4\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\varepsilon>\frac{1}{R\sigma}.

(4.3)

The proof proceeds by showing that either the front is “stretched” by the advective term, which results in a large speed, or the advection is large on a large interval. To this end, we define “ends” of the front

\begin{split}&x_{1}=\max\{y\in\mathbb{R}:u(y)=1-\theta\sigma^{-1}\}\quad\text{ and}\\
&x_{2}=\max\{y\in\mathbb{R}:u(y)=\theta\sigma^{-1}\},\end{split}

which are well-defined due to
Lemma

2.4
. Due to
Lemma

2.3
, we see that
u
is decreasing on

[x_{1},\infty)

because, by the choice of
\theta
,
1-\theta/\sigma<(1-\chi/2\sigma)^{-1}
(recall that
R\leq 1
). We conclude that

u(x)\in\begin{cases}\left[1-\frac{\theta}{\sigma},1\right)\qquad&\text{ if }x\leq x_{1}\\
\left[\frac{\theta}{\sigma},1-\frac{\theta}{\sigma}\right]\qquad&\text{ if }x\in[x_{1},x_{2}]\\
\left(0,\frac{\theta}{\sigma}\right]&\text{ if }x\geq x_{2}.\end{cases}

(4.4)

We now separate into two cases.

Case one:
x_{2}-x_{1}>R\sigma
.
For any
L>0
, by integrating (
1.7
) from
-L
to
L
, we find

c[u(-L)-u(L)]+[v(L)u(L)-v(-L)u(-L)]=u_{x}(L)-u_{x}(-L)+\int_{-L}^{L}u(1-u)\,dx.

The first term on the left and the last term on the right are the crucial terms. Our goal is to show that all other terms tend to zero. First, since
u(-L)\to 1
and
u(L)\to 0
as
L\to\infty
, it follows that

c[u(-L)-u(L)]\to c\quad\text{ and }\quad v(\pm L)=\chi(K_{\sigma}*u)(\pm L)\to 0\qquad\text{ as }L\to\infty.

Using elliptic regularity theory, since
u
tends to constants at
\pm\infty
, then
u_{x}(\pm L)\to 0
as
L\to\infty
.
Putting all of these together, we find

\begin{split}c&=\lim_{L\to\infty}c[u(-L)-u(L)]\\
&=\lim_{L\to\infty}\left(-[v(L)u(L)-v(-L)u(-L)]+u_{x}(L)-u_{x}(-L)+\int_{-L}^{L}u(1-u)\right)\\
&=\lim_{L\to\infty}\int_{-L}^{L}u(1-u)\,dx=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u(1-u)\,dx.\end{split}

By
2.1
, we observe that
u(1-u)\geq 0
. Hence,

c\geq\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}u(1-u)\,dx.

We now obtain a lower bound on the integral term. By (
4.4
) and basic calculus,

u(1-u)\geq(\theta/\sigma)(1-\theta/\sigma)
on
[x_{1},x_{2}]
. Thus, we find

\begin{split}c&\geq\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}u(1-u)\,dx\geq\left(1-\frac{\theta}{\sigma}\right)\frac{\theta}{\sigma}|x_{2}-x_{1}|\geq\left(1-\frac{\theta}{\sigma}\right)\theta R\geq\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)\frac{|\chi|}{2},\end{split}

which contradicts (
4.1
). Hence, case one cannot occur.

Case two:
x_{2}-x_{1}\leq R\sigma
.
In this case, we build an explicit sub-solution in order to “push” the wave at a fast speed. The key step in doing so is in estimating the effect of the advective term
v
on an interval near the front.

To obtain this estimate, we obtain a lower bound on
v
near the front for any
x\in[x_{2},x_{2}+R\sigma]
.
First, we note that
u(x+y)\leq u(x-y)
for any
x\geq x_{2}
and
y\geq 0
. Indeed, if
x-y>x_{2}
, this follows because
u
is decreasing on
[x_{2},\infty]
. On the other hand, if
x-y\leq x_{2}
then
u(x-y)\geq\theta/\sigma
(see (
4.4
)) and
u(x+y)\leq\theta/\sigma
since
x+y\geq x_{2}
.

As a consequence of this inequality, we find

\begin{split}v(x)=\chi(K_{\sigma}*u)(x)&=\chi\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{\sigma}(y)[u(x-y)-u(x+y)]\,dy\\
&\geq\chi\int_{x-x_{1}}^{\infty}K_{\sigma}(y)[u(x-y)-u(x+y)]\,dy\quad\text{ for }x\geq x_{2}.\end{split}

Above we used that
\chi K_{\sigma}\geq 0
on the domain of integration.
Then, using (
4.4
) yields

v(x)\geq\chi\int_{x-x_{1}}^{\infty}K_{\sigma}(y)\left(1-\frac{2\theta}{\sigma}\right)\,dy.

Finally, observe that
x-x_{1}=(x-x_{2})+(x_{2}-x_{1})\leq 2R\sigma
and, thus,

\begin{split}v(x)&\geq\chi\int_{2R\sigma}^{\infty}K_{\sigma}(y)\left(1-\frac{2\theta}{\sigma}\right)\,dy=|\chi|\left(1-\frac{2\theta}{\sigma}\right)\overline{K}(2R).\end{split}

(4.5)

We now use the choice of constants
\theta
and
R
, (
4.2
) and (
4.3
). We conclude that, for all
x\in[x_{2},x_{2}+R\sigma]
,

v(x)\geq\frac{|\chi|}{2}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)^{2}\geq c.

(4.6)

The second inequality above follows from (
4.1
).

Using (
4.6
), we define our sub-solution. For any
A>0
, let

\underline{u}_{A}(x)=A\sin\left(\frac{\pi(x-x_{2})}{R\sigma}\right).

(4.7)

Notice that
\underline{u}_{A}(x_{2})=\underline{u}_{A}(x_{2}+R\sigma)=0
.

By
Definition

1.2
,
u>0
. Then, by the compactness of
[x_{2},x_{2}+R\sigma]
and the continuity of
u
(due to elliptic regularity),
\inf_{[x_{2},x_{2}+R\sigma]}u>0
. Hence,
\underline{u}_{A}<u
on
[x_{2},x_{2}+R\sigma]
if
A
is sufficiently small. We then “raise”
\underline{u}_{A}
up until it touches
u
; i.e., let

A_{0}=\sup\{A:\underline{u}_{A}(x)<u(x)\text{ for }x\in[x_{2},x_{2}+R\sigma]\}.

Thus, there exists
x_{0}\in(x_{2},x_{2}+R\sigma)
such that
\underline{u}_{A_{0}}(x_{0})=u(x_{0})
. Define

\varphi(x)=u(x)-\underline{u}_{A_{0}}(x).

Note that
\varphi\geq 0
on
[x_{2},x_{2}+R\sigma]
,
\varphi(x_{2}),\varphi(x_{2}+L\sigma)>0
, and that
x_{0}
is the location of a zero minimum of
\varphi
, which yields

\varphi(x_{0})=0,\quad\varphi_{x}(x_{0})=0,\quad\text{ and }\quad\varphi_{xx}(x_{0})\geq 0.

(4.8)

Also, notice that, since
x_{0}>x_{2}
, then
u(x_{0})<\theta/\sigma<(1-\chi/\sigma)^{-1}
, which yields
\underline{u}_{A_{0},x}(x_{0})=u_{x}(x_{0})<0
.

We now derive a differential inequality for
\varphi
. Indeed, using (
1.7
), (
4.8
), and that
\varphi(x_{0})=0
, we find, at
x_{0}
,

\begin{split}0\geq(v-c)\varphi_{x}-&\varphi_{xx}-(1-v_{x})\varphi=-u^{2}-(v-c)\underline{u}_{A_{0},x}+\underline{u}_{A_{0},xx}+\underline{u}_{A_{0}}(1-v_{x}).\end{split}

(4.9)

By (
4.6
),
v-c\geq 0
at
x_{0}
. In addition, by
Lemma

2.3
and the choice of
x_{2}
,
\underline{u}_{A_{0},x}(x_{0})=u_{x}(x_{0})<0
. Thus, (
4.9
) becomes, at
x_{0}
,

0\geq-u^{2}+\underline{u}_{A_{0},xx}+\underline{u}_{A_{0}}(1-v_{x}).

(4.10)

Since
x_{0}>x_{2}
,
u(x_{0})<\theta/\sigma
. Also, recall that
u(x_{0})=\underline{u}_{A_{0}}(x_{0})
and, by (
2.1
),

v_{x}(x_{0})=\frac{|\chi|}{\sigma}u(x_{0})-|\chi|\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{\sigma,x}(y)(u(x_{0}-y)+u(x_{0}+y))\,dy.

Hence,
-u(x_{0})^{2}>-\underline{u}_{A_{0}}(x_{0})\theta/\sigma
and
-v_{x}(x_{0})>-|\chi|\theta/\sigma^{2}
.
Using these two inequalities along with (
4.10
) yields, at
x_{0}
,

0>\underline{u}_{A_{0},xx}+\underline{u}_{A_{0}}\left(1-\frac{\theta}{\sigma}-\frac{\theta|\chi|}{\sigma^{2}}\right).

(4.11)

Using the explicit form of
\underline{u}_{A_{0}}
above,
\underline{u}_{A_{0},xx}=-(\pi/R\sigma)^{2}\underline{u}_{A_{0}}
; hence,

0>\underline{u}_{A_{0}}(x_{0})\left(1-\frac{\theta}{\sigma}-\frac{|\chi|\theta}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{R^{2}\sigma^{2}}\right).

Recalling (
4.2
) and (
4.3
), we obtain

0>\underline{u}_{A_{0}}(x_{0})\left(1-\frac{\theta}{\sigma}-\frac{|\chi|\theta}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{R^{2}\sigma^{2}}\right)\geq\underline{u}_{A_{0}}(x_{0})\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}R}{8}-\pi^{2}\varepsilon^{2}\right)\geq 0,

which is a contradiction (recall that
\varepsilon<1/10
and
R\leq 1
). This completes the proof.
∎
