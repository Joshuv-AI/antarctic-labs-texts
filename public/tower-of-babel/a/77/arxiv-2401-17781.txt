Vision-Assisted Digital Twin Creation for mmWave Beam Management
Maximilian Arnold 1 , Bence Major 1 , Fabio Valerio Massoli 1 , Joseph B. Soriaga 2 , Arash Behboodi 1

Abstract
In the context of communication networks, digital twin technology provides a means to replicate the RF ( RF ) propagation environment as well as the system behaviour, allowing for a way to optimize the performance of a deployed system based on simulations. One of the key challenges in the application of Digital Twin technology to mmWave systems is the prevalent channel simulators’ stringent requirements on the accuracy of the 3D Digital Twin, reducing the feasibility of the technology in real applications. We propose a practical Digital Twin creation pipeline and a channel simulator, that relies only on a single mounted camera and position information. We demonstrate the performance benefits compared to methods that do not explicitly model the 3D environment, on downstream sub-tasks in beam acquisition, using the real-world dataset of the DeepSense6G challenge.

I
Introduction

In recent years,
DT
(
DT
) technology has emerged as a revolutionary approach to enhance the design, operation, and optimization in various disciplines
[
1
]
. By creating virtual replicas of real-world assets,
DT
provide an interactive and dynamic platform for analyzing performance, predicting behavior, and facilitating decision-making processes.

In the context of communication networks,
DT
s are envisioned to enhance network efficiency by enabling more accurate and efficient optimization of various network parameters both during deployment (e.g. base stations positions/rotations
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
) and also during system operation. One of the main beneficiary use-cases of supplementary
DT
information is
mmWave
(
mmWave
) beam management, due to the challenges of establishing and maintaining directional links.

Due to the wave propagation characteristics at
mmWave
frequencies, (specular behavior, strong attenuation and short coherence length) it is essential to simulate the propagation of electromagnetic waves by tracing the trajectories of individual rays and accounting for various phenomena, including reflection, diffraction, and scattering
[
5
]
. This necessitates the modeling of real-world complexities of the environment with high degree of fidelity. The most prominent representation used for this purpose are 3D meshes and assigned material properties for the given deployment frequency.

While there is prior art for
mmWave
beam management using 3D representations
[
6
,
7
]
, these approaches rely on the existence of 3D meshes with high fidelity, which greatly increases the cost of deployment, as they require precise
digitalisation
of the environment. Moreover, for a practical solution, the same information needs to be acquired also for dynamic objects in real-time.

A possible solution to acquire real-time 3D information is Deep Learning based Computer Vision, using, for example, an
RGB
(
RGB
)-camera co-located with the base-station. These techniques can reach high reconstruction quality in a multi-view setting
[
8
]
, which is not practical due to large deployment/maintaining effort. Single-view scene reconstruction solutions, on the other hand, lack accuracy of the estimated surface normals
[
9
]
.

An alternative approach is proposed by
[
10
]
, who maps a camera image (using a neural network)
directly
to the selected beam index, without explicit modeling of the 3D environment in an intermediate step. We refer to such approaches that omit this intermediate step as
end-to-end approaches
. These solutions, being black-box and hard to interpret, do not leverage
RF
domain knowledge and can base their decisions on irrelevant data patterns, and thereby harm generalizability.

This poses the question: Is there a practical
3D reconstruction
technique, in conjunction with a robust
channel simulator
, that together enable enhanced communication?

Pipeline to automatically create a 3D environment, simulate the channel state and improve the communication network.

Summarizing our contributions:

•

We propose a
DT
framework that automatically (without
manual
intervention) reconstructs the 3D environment using a stationary camera, and then estimates the channel state (see Fig.
1
).

•

To demonstrate the use of
DT
for beam management, we propose and implement solutions for downstream sub-problems in beam acquisition.

•

On a real-world dataset, we demonstrate that our
DT
-based solution outperforms the state-of-the-art end-to-end methods
[
11
,
6
]
, and we analyze the differences in explainability.

II-A

Reconstruction of angular power profile

As the dataset provides power measurements using analogue beamforming, it prevents us to test different downstream tasks on the data. To overcome this limitation, we reconstruct an angular power profile and use it as a proxy for the full channel-state-information.

The reconstruction is done as follows:

\mathbf{r}_{\alpha}=\left|\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}y_{k}\mathbf{b}_{k}\right|\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N_{\text{angles}}}

(1)

where
\mathcal{K}
is the set of the top-
K

y_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
the magnitude of the measured received power for beam
k
,
\mathbf{b}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N_{\text{angles}}}
is the beam profile of beam
k
, measured in an anechoic chamber. To compensate for noise, we use a larger number of measurements (
K=16

1\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
with coverage of
N_{\text{angles}}=180
.

Using the reconstructed angular power profile, we can simulate the received power of any beam given by its beam profile
\mathbf{\tilde{b}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N_{\text{angles}}}

\hat{y}_{k}=\mathbf{\tilde{b}}_{k}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{r}_{\alpha},

(2)

to estimate its received power
\hat{y}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}

III
Digital Twin

In this section we describe our digital twin pipeline that consists of three primary components: 3D reconstruction, channel simulation and an optional adaptation stage.

III-A

3D reconstruction

Fig. 4:
Proposed pipeline: Leveraging advanced computer vision techniques to create a
DT
that estimates the angular power profile.

To create our 3D model in an automated fashion without manual intervention, we leverage a combination of prominent techniques from the field of computer vision:

•

Monocular depth estimation via PixelFormer
[
13
]
allows us to estimate depth information per pixel from a single image,

•

Pinhole transformation with known camera parameters to estimate a 3D direction vector per pixel location,

•

Semantic segmentation via Segment Anything
[
14
]
provides us with 2D pixel masks for each object in the scene,

•

Object detection via YOLO (You Only Look Once)
[
15
]
provides a class label and a 2D bounding box for each object in the scene.

First, we compute the 3D position of each pixel based on the monocular depth estimation (range) and pinhole coordinate transformation (azimuth, elevation). Then, we use semantic segmentation to identify the pixels of each object in the scene. The
3D position
of the object is the mean 3D position of its corresponding pixels.

To estimate the
reflectance
of the object, we use the object detection system. We find the 2D bounding box with the largest
IoU
(
IoU
) with the object’s 2D pixel mask. Then, we map its class label to a scalar reflectance value (tree:
0.3
, car:
1.0
, pole:
0.6
).

Our
3D representation
models each reflector as a
point reflector
with a scalar reflectance. Fig.
4
provides an overview of the algorithm.

All of the techniques used in this pipeline are off-the-shelf solutions, requiring no further training.

3
shows the four different scenarios reconstructed from the camera view.

We identify the
UE
object by selecting the object closest to the
UE

DGPS

Adaptation

Due to the simplified channel simulation, we could exclude some critical components, e.g. scattering and diffraction, causing a significant gap between simulation and real measurements.

In order to close this gap, we use a linear angular mapping
\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{R}^{180\times 180}

The values of the matrix

IV
Downstream tasks

To demonstrate the benefits of a
DT
-based solution over end-to-end approaches, we will consider the task of beam acquisition. We simplify the problem statement as follows.

In a regular setting, the
BS
has no information about the whereabouts of the
UE
or any other environmental information, so it has to sweep its
L1
beams. The
UE
then reports the received power values to the
BS
. Based on these values, the
BS
selects an L1 beam to further refine. This is then done by sweeping the corresponding
L2
beams with a finer beam pattern.

To minimize the beam management overhead, we evaluate two tasks. First, we have
L1
prediction
where we can skip the sweeping of the
L1
beams by predicting which one to refine further. If this is done accurately, only the corresponding
L2
beams need to be swept next.

Next, we have
L2
prediction
where we can skip the sweeping of both the
L1
and
L2
beams by predicting a single or a few
L2
beams to measure the receive power for. In this case, the requirement is to minimize the energy loss compared to the beam with maximum received power.

In our solution, for both tasks, we determine the predicted beams by using Eq.
2
to estimate the received power for all beams in the given
L1
/
L2
codebook, and selecting the top-
K
with the highest value. The number of selected beams (
K
) is set differently in each of the benchmarks to adjust for task difficulty.

We compare this solution with a
GPS
-only
LoS
baseline, and a learned end-to-end solution, both described in detail in the experiments section.

V-A

Metrics

For the experiments comparing to state-of-the-art, we use the
DBA
(
DBA
) score suggested by the DeepSense6G beam prediction challenge, and described in detail in
[
11
]
. We note that the
DBA
score accounts only for the
proximity
of the predicted indices to the best ones, not accounting for received power, for example from reflectors. Since we wanted to analyze the impact on the RF communication system, we propose different metrics for our downstream tasks.

For the task of
L1 prediction
we predict the L1 beam that will be refined by L2 beam sweep in the next stage, so we need to maximise L1 prediction accuracy, where accuracy is defined as:

L_{1,\text{acc}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }c_{n}\in\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{n}\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(4)

where
c_{n}
is the best beam index of sample
n
and
\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{n}
the predicted beam indices.

For the task of
L2 prediction
, the primary objective is to find the beam that maximises the received power. In other words, the used metric should proportionally reflect the energy loss, not just the accuracy of the system. For this reason, the main metric is the received power loss:

P_{\text{loss}}=20\log_{10}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left(\frac{y_{c,n}}{\max\mathbf{y}_{\hat{c},n}}\right)},

(5)

where
\mathbf{y}_{\hat{c},n}
is the received power of the top-
K
predicted beams and
y_{c,n}
is the received power of the best beam.

As analysing the system behavior in
LoS
and
NLoS
(
NLoS
) cases separately can lead to additional insights, we use 50th and 95th percentile of the power loss values to represent
LoS
and
NLoS
cases, respectively.

While we analyze all scenarios, it is important to note the difference between them. Scenario 32, 33 and 34 are considered
seen
scenarios, ie., they are also part of the training set. On the other hand, Scenario 31 is
unseen
, i.e., not part of the training set, closer reflecting a real-world deployment setting. The overall DBA score used in the challenge reflects this difference, by assigning
1/2
weight for the unseen, and
1/6
to each of the seen scenarios.

V-B

Baselines

In order to analyse the behavior of the proposed system, we utilize both a
GPS
-only system that is only expected to work in
LoS
conditions and a more flexible neural network based end-to-end approach.

Our
GPS
solution (referred to as
GPS-LoS
) simply chooses the beam with the maximum beam profile value at the
UE
azimuth, assuming
LoS
conditions.

As the state-of-the-art approach
[
11
]
has the downstream task built into its design, we needed to introduce an alternative baseline that estimates the angular power profile directly, allowing for a flexible application to new downstream tasks. Such a system can serve as substitute for
DT
-creation and channel simulation stages jointly, allowing us to have direct comparison with a
DT
-based approach.

The proposed end-to-end

architecture, relying on standard off-the-shelf feature extractor (ResNet-18) and a projection head.

For this purpose, we utilize a multi-modal
NN
, shown in Fig.

.
To extract features from images, we employ the ResNet-18 architecturefor which we substitute the final projection layer with a linear projection to a 180-dimensional space. Regarding the
GPS

MSE

MSE

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{\hat{r}}_{\alpha},\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})=\frac{1}{N_{\text{batch}}}\sum^{N_{\text{batch}}-1}_{n=0}\left\|\mathbf{\hat{r}}_{\alpha,n}-\mathbf{r}_{\alpha,n}\right\|^{2}.

(6)

The training is done with a learning rate of
0.001
using the ADAM optimizer. We will refer to our neural network based solution as the

Improved explainability

Fig. 6:
Example for a reconstructed angular power profile comparing to the camera image and identifying useful reflectors. Dashed paths are correctly removed during the adaptation phase, while the dominant paths can be seen in the reconstructed profile. (Scenario 32, Sample 50)

Fig.
6
demonstrates the angular power profiles reconstructed by the different approaches.

The profile reconstructed by the end-to-end solution demonstrates no clear visible correspondence to the ground truth (GT). This is a strong example for the lack of explainability of standard
NN
s.

Our
DT
-reconstructed profile, however, shows clear correspondence with the objects visible on the camera image (marked with green circles). It is worth noting that the
DT
identified reflectors that should not be able provide a reflecting path to the
UE
, as the
UE
vehicle’s frame is expected to block those paths (see paths reflected from poles on the right), or the normal vector of the surface was not appropriate (see path reflected from column on left). These phenomena are not modeled in our proposed 3D representation.

A solution to this problem is provided by our proposed adaptation technique, as it keeps the
LoS
path, and suppresses the paths introduced by our simplified 3D representation.

We note that the dataset sample used for this analysis was not cherry-picked, we used the first example with multiple reflections in the scene.

VI
Conclusion

In conclusion, we propose a pipeline of
DT
creation and channel simulation including a learnable adapatation stage, which is versatile for tackling mmWave beam management downstream tasks. The described pipeline is practical and relies only on a single camera co-located with the
BS
, and UE position information. We demonstrate the performance benefits compared to end-to-end approaches on the downstream task of beam acquisition, using the real-world dataset of the DeepSense6G challenge.

Due to the chosen 3D representation, an added benefit of our approach over end-to-end solutions is that it is extendable, for example to blockage prediction through the trajectory prediction of objects in the scene. Since our channel simulator is simple and differentiable, it allows for further extensions, for example to codebook learning. We leave the exploration of such approaches to future work.
