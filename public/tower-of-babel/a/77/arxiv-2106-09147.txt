Efficient recurrence for the enumeration of permutations with fixed pinnacle set
Wenjie Fang

Abstract
Initiated by Davis, Nelson, Petersen and Tenner (2018), the enumerative study of pinnacle sets of permutations has attracted a fair amount of attention recently. In this article, we provide a recurrence that can be used to compute efficiently the number | 𝔖 n ​ ( P ) | |\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)| of permutations of size n n with a given pinnacle set P P , with arithmetic complexity O ⁡ ( k 4 + k ​ log ⁡ n ) O(k^{4}+k\log n) for P P of size k k . A symbolic expression can also be computed in this way for pinnacle sets of fixed size. A weighted sum q n ​ ( P ) q_{n}(P) of | 𝔖 n ​ ( P ) | |\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)| proposed in Davis, Nelson, Petersen and Tenner (2018) seems to have a simple form, and a conjectural form is given recently by Flaque, Novelli and Thibon (2021+). We settle the problem by providing and proving an alternative form of q n ​ ( P ) q_{n}(P) , which has a strong combinatorial flavor. We also study admissible orderings of a given pinnacle set, first considered by Rusu (2020) and characterized by Rusu and Tenner (2021), and we give an efficient algorithm for their counting.

Introduction

Given a permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
in one-line notation
\pi_{1}\pi_{2}\cdots\pi_{n}
, we consider its local maxima,
i.e.
, elements
\pi_{i}
for
2\leq i\leq n-1
such that
\pi_{i-1}<\pi_{i}>\pi_{i+1}
. In this case, the index
i
is called a
peak
of
\pi
, and the element
\pi_{i}
is called a
pinnacle
. We denote by
[n]
the set
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. The
pinnacle set
of a permutation
\pi
, denoted by
\operatorname{Pin}(\pi)
, is the set of pinnacles of
\pi
. For
P\subseteq[n]
, we denote by
\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)
the set of permutations with pinnacle set
P
. Peaks of permutations have already been much studied, partly due to its link to the algebraic aspect of the symmetric group through the peak algebra. Inspired by the studies on peaks, pinnacle sets of permutations were first explored in
Davis et al. 2018
, and various results were given there.

One of the main questions asked in
Davis et al. 2018
is how to compute
|\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)|
efficiently. Two recurrences were proposed there, but both need super-exponential time in the number of pinnacles to compute. In
Diaz-Lopez et al. 2021
, an improved formula was given, but it is still a sum of super-exponentially many terms. A formula with only exponentially many terms was given recently in
Domagalski et al. 2021
using the inclusion-exclusion principle. Then, a new and simpler recurrence was proposed in
Falque et al. 2021
that drastically improves the arithmetic complexity,
i.e.
, the number of arithmetic operations, to
O(\max(P)|P|^{2})
, but there is still a dependency on the values in
P
, thus not polynomial in the bit-length of input. In this article, we again improve on the arithmetic complexity, giving a recurrence (see Theorem
4.1
) that can be used to compute
|\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)|
with polynomial arithmetic complexity in the bit-length of input.

Proposition 1.1
.

There is an algorithm that computes
|\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)|
with
O(|P|^{2}\log n+|P|^{4})
arithmetic operations.

Using the same recurrence in Theorem
4.1
, through a computer algebra system, we also obtain general symbolic expressions of
|\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)|
for
P
of arbitrary fixed size, extending formulas given in
Davis et al. 2018
for
|P|=1,2
. However, these expressions are messy in general. In
Davis et al. 2018
, it was suggested that the following weighted sum may have a simpler form:

q_{n}(P)=\sum_{Q\subseteq P}2^{|Q|}|\mathfrak{S}_{n}(Q)|.

(1)

It is also observed in
Davis et al. 2018
that we may use
q_{n}(P)
to recover
|\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)|
with the inclusion-exclusion principle. In
Falque et al. 2021
, a general process of generating an expression of
q_{n}(P)
was conjectured, with a few examples of such expressions for small
|P|
, which are indeed simpler. We settle this problem by providing an alternative form of
q_{n}(P)
as a summation over some combinatorial objects. Our formula is equivalent to the one conjectured in
Falque et al. 2021
(see Remark
5.3
).

For
s\leq k
, let
\mathcal{R}_{k,s}
be the set of sequences
r=(r_{0},r_{1},\ldots,r_{k})
with the conditions

•

r_{0}=0
,
r_{k}=s
,
r_{i}\geq 0
for all
0\leq i\leq k
;

•

r_{i}=r_{i-1}\pm 1
for all
1\leq i\leq k
.

We can interpret
\mathcal{R}_{k,s}
as the set of
y
-coordinate sequences of Dyck meanders of length
k
terminating on
y=s
. We define
\mathcal{R}_{k}=\cup_{s=0}^{k}\mathcal{R}_{k,s}
.

Theorem 1.2

(See also Conjecture 5.1 in
Falque et al. 2021
)
.

For
n\geq 1
and
P=\{p_{1}>p_{2}>\cdots>p_{k}\}\subseteq[n]
, we take the convention that
p_{0}=n+1
and
p_{k+1}=1
. Given
r\in\mathcal{R}_{k}
, we define its weight
w_{\mathcal{R}}(r)
by

w_{\mathcal{R}}(r)=\prod_{m=0}^{k}(r_{m}+1)^{p_{m}-p_{m+1}}.

Then we have

q_{n}(P)=2^{n-k-1}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_{k}}w_{\mathcal{R}}(r).

Although the statement of Theorem
1.2
is purely combinatorial, our proof uses heavy computations. We thus ask naturally for a more satisfying combinatorial proof.

For permutations with the same pinnacle set, it is possible that their pinnacles appear in different orders, and not all orders are possible. A pinnacle order is called
admissible
if there is a permutation in which these pinnacles appear in that order. Given a pinnacle set
P
, we denote by
\mathcal{O}(P)
the set of all admissible pinnacle orders of
P
(its formal definition is postponed to Section
6
). Admissible pinnacle order was first considered in
(
Rusu 2020
, Question 3)
. A characterization of
\mathcal{O}(P)
was given in Theorem 3.6 of
Rusu and Tenner 2021
using the language of interruptions. In the same paper, the authors asked for a function that computes
|\mathcal{O}(P)|
. As a response, a formula of
|\mathcal{O}(P)|
was given in
Domagalski et al. 2021
, but it involves exponentially many terms in the size of
P
. In the article, we provide an efficient way to compute
|\mathcal{O}(P)|
using a recurrence.

Proposition 1.3
.

There is an algorithm that computes
|\mathcal{O}(P)|
with
O(|P|^{2})
arithmetic operations.

This article is organized as follows. Section
2
reduces the counting of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)
to that of some weighted Motzkin paths, using a variant of the Françon-Viennot bijection (Proposition
2.3
). It is then further reduced in Section
3
to the counting of appropriately weighted Dyck paths by compressing horizontal steps in the weighted Motzkin paths (Theorem
3.5
). Given the formulation in Dyck paths, we provide in Section
4
the main result of this article (Theorem
4.1
), a recurrence that allows us to compute
|\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)|
efficiently, as in Proposition
1.1
. Using a variant of this recurrence, we provide in Section
5
a proof of Theorem
1.2
. We then deal with the enumeration of admissible pinnacle orderings in Section
6
.

2
Construction of permutations with a fixed pinnacle set

In the following, we consider
cyclic permutations
, which are equivalent classes of permutations under the action of position-shifting
\pi\mapsto\pi^{\prime}
with
\pi^{\prime}_{i}=\pi_{(i\mod n)+1}
for
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Cyclic permutations are marked with a bar, and we write its one-line notation ending with the largest element. For instance, the cyclic permutation
\overline{\pi}=\overline{42135}
is the equivalent class
\{42135,21354,13542,35421,54213\}
. We denote by
\overline{\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}}
the set of cyclic permutations with
n+1
elements.

The pinnacles of a cyclic permutation
\overline{\pi}
is defined in the same way as for normal permutations, up to cyclic index, and we also define
\overline{\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}(P)}
analogously. We observe that
(n+1)
is always a pinnacle of
\overline{\pi}\in\overline{\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}}
. We denote by
\operatorname{cyc}(\pi)
the
cyclic completion
of
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, which is in
\overline{\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}}
and whose one-line notation is obtained by adding
(n+1)
at the end of that of
\pi
. For instance,
\operatorname{cyc}(2413)=\overline{24135}
. It is clear that
\operatorname{cyc}
is a bijection between
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and
\overline{\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}}
. Furthermore, it also preserves
\operatorname{Pin}(\pi)
in the following sense.

Lemma 2.1

(Lemma 4.2 in
Domagalski et al. 2021
)
.

Given
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, we have
\operatorname{Pin}(\operatorname{cyc}(\pi))=\operatorname{Pin}(\pi)\cup\{n+1\}
. In other words,
\operatorname{cyc}
is a bijection from
\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)
to
\overline{\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}(P\cup\{n+1\})}
.

Given
\overline{\pi}\in\overline{\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}}
and
2\leq\ell\leq n+1
, we define the
segment set

S_{\ell}(\overline{\pi})
of level
\ell
as the list of maximal consecutive segments (in the cyclic sense) in
\overline{\pi}
whose elements are at least
\ell
. We denote by
s_{\ell}(\overline{\pi})=|S_{\ell}(\overline{\pi})|
. For instance,
S_{4}(\overline{435281679})
consists of the segments
5,8,6794
with all elements at least
4
, and we thus have
s_{4}(\overline{435281679})=3
. It is clear that
s_{n+1}(\overline{\pi})=s_{2}(\overline{\pi})=1
.

We now define the
Motzkin type
of
\overline{\pi}
, denoted by
\mathfrak{M}(\overline{\pi})
, as the lattice path of length
n-1
whose
i
-th step is
(1,s_{n+1-i}(\overline{\pi})-s_{n+2-i}(\overline{\pi}))
, for
i\in[n-1]
. In other words,
\mathfrak{M}(\overline{\pi})
is the lattice path through the points
(i,s_{n+1-i}(\overline{\pi})-1)
for
0\leq i\leq n-1
. Figure
1
shows a cyclic permutation and its Motzkin type, along with all
S_{\ell}(\overline{\pi})
.

Figure 1:
Example of a cyclic permutation, its Motzkin type and segment sets

We recall that a
Motzkin path
of length
n
is a lattice path starting at
(0,0)
, ending at
(n,0)
, composed by up steps
(1,1)
, horizontal steps
(1,0)
and down steps
(1,-1)
, while always staying weakly above the
x
-axis. We denote by
\mathcal{M}_{n-1}
the set of Motzkin paths of length
n-1
. The name “Motzkin type” comes from the following lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

For
\overline{\pi}\in\overline{\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}}
, the lattice path
\mathfrak{M}(\overline{\pi})
is a Motzkin path. Furthermore, the
i
-th step of
\mathfrak{M}(\overline{\pi})
is an up step if and only if
n+1-i
is a pinnacle of
\overline{\pi}
.

Proof.

We only need to show that
s_{j}(\overline{\pi})-s_{j+1}(\overline{\pi})\in\{-1,0,1\}
for
j\geq 2
. The element
j
in
\overline{\pi}
can only be in three cases:

•

j
is not next to any element at least
j+1
, in this case
s_{j}(\overline{\pi})-s_{j+1}(\overline{\pi})=1
, as we have the new segment
j
;

•

j
is next to only one element
e
at least
j+1
, in this case
s_{j}(\overline{\pi})-s_{j+1}(\overline{\pi})=0
, as
j
joins the segment of
e
;

•

j
is next to two elements
e_{1},e_{2}
at least
j+1
, in this case
s_{j}(\overline{\pi})-s_{j+1}(\overline{\pi})=-1
. It is because
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
come from two different segments, as
j\geq 2
, and then
j
fusions these two segments.

We thus conclude that
\mathfrak{M}(\overline{\pi})
is a Motzkin path. For the second part, we observe that an up step means the creation of a new segment, which is equivalent to the new element being a pinnacle.
∎

Given a Motzkin path
M
, we define a weight on its steps. Up steps have weight
1
, horizontal steps on height
\ell
have weight
2(\ell+1)
, and down steps from height
\ell
to
\ell-1
have weight
\ell(\ell+1)
. The weight of
M
, denoted by
w_{\mathcal{M}}(M)
, is the product of the weights of its steps.

Proposition 2.3
.

Given a Motzkin path
M
of length
n-1
, the number of cyclic permutations
\overline{\pi}\in\overline{\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}}
with
\mathfrak{M}(\overline{\pi})=M
is
w_{\mathcal{M}}(M)
.

Proof.

We construct
\overline{\pi}
with
\mathfrak{M}(\overline{\pi})=M
step by step. We start from
S_{n+1}=\{(n+1)\}
, then we follow
M
step by step, and construct the segment set
S_{n+1-i}
by adding the element
n+1-i
to
S_{n+2-i}
. There are three possible cases.

•

The
i
-th step of
M
is an up step. Thus,
S_{n+1-i}
has one more segment than
S_{n+2-i}
. The only way to do is to add
n+1-i
as a new segment, that is,
S_{n+1-i}=S_{n+2-i}\cup\{(n+1-i)\}
.

•

The
i
-th step of
M
is a horizontal step of height
h
, meaning that
n+1-i
joins one of the
h+1
segments. There are
2(h+1)
such possibilities, as
n+1-i
can be attached to both ends of a segment.

•

The
i
-th step of
M
is a down step from height
h
to
h-1
, meaning that
n+1-i
joins two of the
h+1
segments. There are
h(h+1)
possibilities,
(h+1)
for the left segment and
h
for the right one.

To see that each sequence of possible choices gives a unique permutation
\pi
, we observe that
\pi
is totally determined by all the pairs
(\pi(j),\pi(j+1))
. In the
i
-th step, we see that each choice determines uniquely the pairs containing
n+1-i
as the smaller element, and the sequence of choices thus determines
\pi
uniquely. Furthermore, each such permutation can be constructed in this way by looking at its segment sets. The number of choices for each step is exactly its weight. Thus, the number of permutations with Motzkin type
M
is
w_{\mathcal{M}}(M)
.
∎

Given a set
P\subseteq[n]
, we denote by
\mathcal{M}_{n-1,P}
the set of Motzkin paths
M
of length
n-1
such that, for each
p\in P
, the
(n+1-p)
-th step of
M
is an up step, and these are the only up steps of
M
. We have the following corollary.

Corollary 2.4
.

For a set
P\subseteq[n]
, we have

|\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)|=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{n-1,P}}w_{\mathcal{M}}(M).

Proof.

Let
P^{\prime}=P\cup\{n+1\}
. From Lemma
2.1
, we have
|\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)|=|\overline{\mathfrak{S}_{n+1,P^{\prime}}}|
. We then conclude by Lemma
2.2
and the definition of
\mathcal{M}_{n-1,P}
.
∎

As a corollary, we have a simple proof of the lower bound of
|\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)|
for
|P|=k
.

Corollary 2.5

(Proposition 3.13 in
Davis et al. 2018
)
.

Given
n\geq 2k+1
, for every
P\subseteq[n]
with
|P|=k
and
\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)\neq\varnothing
, we have

|\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)|\geq 2^{n-k-1}.

The lower bound is reached by
P_{\min}=\{3,5,7,\ldots,2k+1\}
.

Proof.

As
\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)\neq\varnothing
, by Corollary
2.4
, there is at least one Motzkin path
M\in\mathcal{M}_{n-1,P}
. Now, there are
n-2k-1
horizontal step and
k
down steps in
M
, each contributing a factor at least
2
. We thus have
|\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)|\geq w_{\mathcal{M}}(M)\geq 2^{n-k-1}
. For
P_{\min}
, from the definition of Motzkin type, it is clear that the only possible Motzkin type for
\overline{\pi}\in\overline{\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}}
with
\operatorname{Pin}(\overline{\pi})=P_{\min}\cup\{n+1\}
is the one starting with
2^{n-2k-1}
horizontal steps, then ending with
k
pairs of up-and-down steps. This Motzkin path has exactly the minimal weight.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

The construction in Proposition
2.3
can be seen as a variant of the Françon-Viennot bijection between permutations and weighted Motzkin paths, but we use a slightly different set of weights that is more adapted to our analysis afterwards. It was also formulated in terms of increasing binary trees in
Flajolet 1980
, which also provides a general theory to express the generating function of weighted Motzkin paths by continued fractions. In the original bijection, reformulated in our terms here, segments are already positioned in the permutation upon its creation, instead of free-floating. This leads to a weight
\ell+1
on up steps starting at height
\ell
, while the weight of down steps starting at height
\ell
is reduced to
\ell+1
.

3
Motzkin path compression

A
Dyck path
is a Motzkin path without horizontal steps. It is clear that it has the same number of up steps and down steps. We denote by
\mathcal{D}_{n}
the set of Dyck paths of length
2n
. Given a Motzkin path
M
, we define its
Dyck compression
, denoted by
\operatorname{dc}(M)
, the Dyck path obtained by removing all horizontal steps in
M
. We define the
Dyck type

\mathfrak{D}(\overline{\pi})
of a cyclic permutation
\overline{\pi}
to be
\operatorname{dc}(\mathfrak{M}(\overline{\pi}))
. The following is a direct consequence of Lemma
2.2
.

Proposition 3.1
.

For
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, the length of
\mathfrak{D}(\operatorname{cyc}(\pi))
is
2|\operatorname{Pin}(\pi)|
.

Proof.

We know from Lemma
2.1
and
2.2
that pinnacles in
\operatorname{Pin}(\pi)
are in bijection with up steps in
\mathfrak{M}(\operatorname{cyc}(\pi))
, which are kept in
\mathfrak{D}(\operatorname{cyc}(\pi))
.
∎

Given
n
and
P=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}\}\subseteq[n]
, we take the convention
p_{0}=n+1
and
p_{k+1}=1
. We define the
gap sequence

(g_{0},g_{1},\ldots,g_{k})
of
(n,P)
by taking
g_{i}=p_{i}-p_{i+1}-1
for all
i
. The name comes from the fact that
g_{i}
is the number of steps between the
i
-th and the
(i+1)
-st up step of Motzkin paths in
\mathcal{M}_{n-1,P}
.

We start by a computational lemma. For
k\geq 1
, we denote by
h_{m}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k})
the homogeneous symmetric function of order
m
with
k
variables:

h_{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=\sum_{m_{1}+m_{2}+\cdots+m_{k}=m,m_{i}\geq 0}x_{1}^{m_{1}}x_{2}^{m_{2}}\cdots x_{k}^{m_{k}}.

(2)

Readers are referred to
(
Stanley 1999
, Chapter 7)
for more on symmetric functions. We have the following computational lemma for
h_{m}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
m\in\mathbf{N}
, we have

h_{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}\frac{x_{i}^{m+k-1}}{\prod_{j\neq i}(x_{i}-x_{j})}.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
k
. The equality holds trivially for
k=1
. Now, to pass from
k
to
k+1
, first write
h_{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k+1})
as a polynomial in
x_{k+1}
with coefficients
h_{m^{\prime}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
, then apply the induction hypothesis on these coefficients. Comparing the resulting expression of
h_{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k+1})
with the claimed one, the induction step is reduced to the proof of the following equality:

\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}x_{i}^{k-1}\cdot\frac{\displaystyle\prod_{1\leq j\leq k,j\neq i}(x_{k+1}-x_{j})}{\displaystyle\prod_{1\leq j\leq k,j\neq i}(x_{i}-x_{j})}=x_{k+1}^{k-1}.

This equality holds by Lagrange interpolation.
∎

Now, given Proposition
3.1
, for each pair
(P,D)
with
P\subseteq[n]
with
|P|=k
, and
D
a Dyck path of length
2k
, we associate a weight
w_{\mathcal{D}}(n,P,D)
defined as follows. For
1\leq i\leq k-1
, let
d_{i}
be the number of down steps between the
i
-th and the
(i+1)
-st up steps of
D
, and we also take
d_{0}=0
and
d_{k}
the number of down steps at the end of
D
. We also suppose that, for
1\leq i\leq k
, the
i
-th up step of
D
goes from height
\ell_{i}-1
to
\ell_{i}
, and we take
\ell_{0}=0
. We thus have two tuples
(d_{0},\ldots,d_{k})
and
(\ell_{0},\ldots,\ell_{k})
depending only on
D
. Let
(g_{0},g_{1},\ldots,g_{k})
be the gap sequence of
(n,P)
. We then define
w_{\mathcal{D}}(n,P,D)
by

w_{\mathcal{D}}(n,P,D)=\prod_{0\leq i\leq k}f(d_{i},\ell_{i},g_{i}),

(3)

where
f(d,\ell,g)
is defined as follows:
f(d,\ell,g)=0
when
d>g
, otherwise

\displaystyle\begin{split}f(d,\ell,g)&=([\ell=0]+\ell(\ell+1))h_{g-d}(\ell+1,\ell,\ldots,\ell-d+1)\\
&=([\ell=0]+\ell(\ell+1))\sum_{m=0}^{d}(-1)^{m}\frac{(\ell+1-m)^{g}}{m!(d-m)!}.\end{split}

(4)

Here,
[h=0]
is the Iverson bracket for the condition
h=0
, taking the value
1
when
h=0
, and
0
otherwise. We remark that
d_{i}=\ell_{i}-\ell_{i+1}+1
, but we choose the current notation for simplicity. The last equality of (
4
) is from Lemma
3.2
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Given
P=\{p_{1}>p_{2}>\cdots>p_{k}\}\in[n]
and a Dyck path
D
of length
2k
, the number of permutations
\pi
in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)
with Dyck type
D
is
w_{\mathcal{D}}(n,P,D)
.

Proof.

Let
(d_{0},\ldots,d_{k})
and
(\ell_{0},\ldots,\ell_{k})
be the two tuples used in (
3
), and
(g_{0},\ldots,g_{k})
the gap sequence of
P
. The length of
D
given
P
is imposed by Proposition
3.1
. By Lemma
2.1
and Proposition
2.3
, we only need to show that

w_{\mathcal{D}}(n,P,D)=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{n-1,P},\;\operatorname{dc}(M)=D}w_{\mathcal{M}}(M).

By the definition of
\mathcal{M}_{n-1,P}
, for each
M\in\mathcal{M}_{n-1,P}
, the
(n+1-p_{i})
-th step is an up step for each
1\leq i\leq k
. We now consider the steps between the
i
-th and the
(i+1)
-st up step in
M
. There are
g_{i}
such steps,
d_{i}
of them are down steps, so
g_{i}-d_{i}
of them are horizontal. These horizontal steps come with heights from
\ell_{i}
to
\ell_{i}-d_{i}
. The contribution of all possibilities is

\sum_{m_{1}+\cdots+m_{d_{i}+1}=g_{i}-d_{i}}2^{g_{i}-d_{i}}\prod_{j=1}^{d_{i}+1}(\ell_{i}-j+1)^{m_{j}}=2^{g_{i}-d_{i}}h_{g_{i}-d_{i}}(\ell_{i}+1,\ell_{i},\ldots,\ell_{i}-d_{i}+1).

For steps after the last up step in
M
, the same formula applies. It also holds for the
n-p_{1}=g_{0}
horizontal steps that comes before the first up step in
M
by taking

Remark 4.2
.

Another use of Theorem

is the exhaustive generation of all permutations in

Remark 5.3
.

Conjecture 5.1 in
Falque et al. 2021
involves a relatively simple but non-trivial iterative procedure to generate an expression of

. However, it is equivalent to Theorem
1.2
. We only briefly describe the reason here, without a detailed proof. In the procedure in
Falque et al. 2021
, there are two operators

and
f_{o}
, one used for even number of pinnacles, the other for the odd case. In our model of lattice paths, both operators “extend” the path by one step, up or down. The apparent difference between

and
f_{o}
is due to the fact that, when paths are extended to odd height (so even weight for horizontal steps), a power of
2
is collected somewhere else.

Theorem
1.2
also explains the phenomenon observed in
Falque et al. 2021
that, in the computation of

, we may have

in
P
, although it cannot be a pinnacle. For

, let

. It is clear that

, as

for any

containing

. We check that, for each

, the contribution of

and

to

is exactly twice that of

to

, which is compensated by the increment of number of pinnacles.

In
Davis et al. 2018
, it was proposed that, as

may have a simpler form, it may be easier to compute

using

and the principle of inclusion-exclusion. However, the arithmetic complexity of such computation is exponential in

, in contrast to Proposition
1.1
here.

Although the statement of Theorem
1.2
has a strong combinatorial flavor, its proof here is mainly computational, which is not quite satisfying. We thus have the following natural question:

Open question 1
.

Is there a combinatorial or even bijective proof of Theorem
1.2
?

If such a proof exists, it might lead us to a better understanding why the form of
q_{n}(P)
is much simpler than that of
|\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)|
.

6
Counting pinnacle orders

Given
P=\{p_{1}>p_{2}>\cdots>p_{k}\}
, for some
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)
for
n\geq p_{1}
, the
pinnacle ordering
of
\pi
, denoted by
\operatorname{ord}(\pi)
, is a permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
such that
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}
appear in
\pi
in the order
p_{\sigma(1)},p_{\sigma(2)},\ldots,p_{\sigma(k)}
. For instance, for the permutation
\pi=46352817
, its pinnacle set is
\operatorname{Pin}(\pi)=\{8,6,5\}
, and we have
\operatorname{ord}(\pi)=231
, as the pinnacles in
\operatorname{Pin}(\pi)
appear in
\pi
in the order
6,5,8
.

A permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
is an
admissible pinnacle ordering
(or simply
admissible ordering
) of
P
if there is
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)
with
\operatorname{ord}(\pi)=\sigma
. We denote by
\mathcal{O}(P)
the set of admissible pinnacle ordering of
P
. Note that
\mathcal{O}(P)
does not depend on
n
, as in every
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)
, any element greater than
p_{1}
cannot be a peak, and they must thus be located at the two ends of
\pi
. Hence, taking any
n>p_{1}
will not lead to more admissible orderings than taking
n=p_{1}
.

Given
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
, we want to know whether
\sigma
is in
\mathcal{O}(P)
. We have the following characterization of possible pinnacle orders
\sigma
for
\operatorname{cyc}(\pi)
of a given Dyck type, using the Motzkin type of
\operatorname{cyc}(\sigma)
. To simplify the proof, for
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
, we define its
complement
, denoted by
\hat{\sigma}
, by
\hat{\sigma}(i)=k+1-\sigma(i)
. We recall that, for a cyclic permutation
\overline{\pi}
, its segment set of level
\ell
, denoted by
S_{\ell}(\overline{\pi})
, is the set of maximal consecutive segments formed by elements at least
\ell
in
\overline{\pi}
, and we define
s_{\ell}(\overline{\pi})=|S_{\ell}(\overline{\pi})|
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Given
P\subseteq[n]
with
|P|=k
, we fix
D
a Dyck path of length
2k
such that there is some
\pi_{*}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)
with
\mathfrak{D}(\operatorname{cyc}(\pi_{*}))=D
. For a permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
, we let
M
be the Motzkin type of
\operatorname{cyc}(\sigma)
, that is,
M=\mathfrak{M}(\operatorname{cyc}(\sigma))
. We have the following equivalence:

(i)

There exists
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)
with
\mathfrak{D}(\operatorname{cyc}(\pi))=D
and
\operatorname{ord}(\pi)=\hat{\sigma}
;

(ii)

The Motzkin path
M
is
compatible
with the Dyck path
D
, that is, for all
1\leq i\leq k-1
, the starting height of the
i
-th step of
M
never exceeds that of the
i
-th
up step
of
D
.

Proof.

For simplicity, we take
\overline{\pi}=\operatorname{cyc}(\pi)
and
\overline{\sigma}=\operatorname{cyc}(\sigma)
, and we suppose that
P=\{p_{1}>p_{2}>\cdots>p_{k}\}
. By construction, the pinnacles of
\overline{\pi}
are exactly
n+1,p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{i-1}
, corresponding to elements
k+1,k,\ldots,k-i+2
in
\overline{\sigma}
, as
\operatorname{ord}(\pi)=\hat{\sigma}
and
n+1
is always a pinnacle in
\overline{\pi}
. By the definition of
\mathfrak{M}
, the starting height of the
i
-th step of
M
is
s_{k+2-i}(\overline{\sigma})-1
. Similarly, from Lemma
2.2
and the definition of
\mathfrak{D}
, we know that the
i
-th up step of
D
corresponds to the pinnacle
p_{i}
, and its starting height is
s_{p_{i}+1}(\overline{\pi})-1
. Hence, in the following, we may replace (ii) by

(ii)’

For all
1\leq i\leq k-1
, we have
s_{k+2-i}(\overline{\sigma})\leq s_{p_{i}+1}(\overline{\pi})
.

We start by proving (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii)’. The elements in each segment in
S_{k+2-i}(\overline{\sigma})
correspond to pinnacles in
\overline{\pi}
. However, if two elements
j,j^{\prime}
are not in the same segment of
S_{k+2-i}(\overline{\sigma})
, then there are two elements
m,m^{\prime}<k+2-i
that separate
j
and
j^{\prime}
in
\overline{\sigma}
, which transposes to two pinnacles
p_{k-m+1},p_{k-m^{\prime}+1}\leq p_{i}
that separate
p_{k-j+1}
and
p_{k-j^{\prime}+1}
in
\overline{\pi}
. It means that
p_{k-j+1}
and
p_{k-j^{\prime}+1}
are not in the same segment in
S_{p_{i}+1}(\overline{\pi})
. The restriction of segments in
S_{p_{i}+1}(\overline{\pi})
to pinnacles is thus a refinement of
S_{k+2-i}(\overline{\sigma})
as set partition, and we have
s_{k+2-i}(\overline{\sigma})\leq s_{p_{i}+1}(\overline{\pi})
to conclude (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii)’.

To show (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i), we only need to show a way to construct
\pi
satisfying (i). Knowing that there is
\pi_{*}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)
such that
\mathfrak{D}(\operatorname{cyc}(\pi_{*}))=D
, as in the proof of Proposition
2.3
, we may alter any choices in the procedure to construct
\operatorname{cyc}(\pi_{*})
from its Motzkin type
\mathfrak{M}(\operatorname{cyc}(\pi_{*}))
to construct
\overline{\pi}
of the same Motzkin type, thus also with the same Dyck type. We leave choices for horizontal steps unchanged, focusing only on down steps. We now read each step of
M
and its choice in the construction of
\overline{\sigma}
from
M
, and translate them to choices of down steps in
\mathfrak{M}(\operatorname{cyc}(\pi_{*}))
, which are just down steps in
D
. With an abuse of language that will be justified later, given an element or a segment in
\overline{\sigma}
, we say that its corresponding segment in
\overline{\pi}
is the one containing the corresponding pinnacles. There are three possible steps in
M
:

•

Up step: do nothing;

•

Horizontal step: suppose that it joins a new element
i
to an existing segment
w
in
S_{i+1}(\overline{\sigma})
, then the next down step in
D
should join the corresponding segments of
i
and of
w
in the same way;

•

Down steps: suppose that it joins a new element
i
with two segments
w
and
w^{\prime}
, forming
wiw^{\prime}
, then we take the next
two
down steps in
D
to join the corresponding segments of
i
,
w
and
w^{\prime}
in the same way, with arbitrary order.

The procedure above is well-defined, as we mimic in
\overline{\pi}
how segments join in
\overline{\sigma}
, meaning that the corresponding pinnacles of elements in the same segment of
\overline{\sigma}
, once it comes to existence during the reading of
M
, are also in the same segment of
\overline{\pi}
after the translated steps. The only way that the process above fails is when it is about to deal with a new element in
\overline{\sigma}
for the
i
-th step of
M
, but the corresponding pinnacle
p_{i}
is not yet introduced by the
i
-th up step of
\mathfrak{M}(\overline{\pi})
(thus also that of
D
). Suppose that the starting height of the
i
-th step of
M
is
\ell_{i}
and that of the
i
-th up step of
D
is
\ell_{i}^{\prime}
. Steps in
M
for elements larger than
i
correspond by the process above to
i-1-\ell_{i}
down steps in
D
, as a step in
M
with height increment
1
,
0
,
-1
consumes
0
,
1
,
2
down steps in
D
respectively. Then, for the
i
-th up step of
D
, there are
i-1-\ell_{i}^{\prime}
down steps before it. Therefore, our process fails for the
i
-th step of
M
when the first
i-1
steps in
M
produces strictly less down steps than those before the
i
-th up step in
D
, that is, when
i-1-\ell_{i}<i-1-\ell_{i}^{\prime}
. But this is impossible due to (ii). Therefore, our process never fails, and it produces a permutation
\pi
with
\operatorname{ord}(\pi)=\hat{\sigma}
.
∎

Given a pinnacle set
P
, a Dyck path
D
is a
admissible Dyck type
of
P
if there is some
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)
such that
\mathfrak{D}(\operatorname{cyc}(\pi))=D
. By Proposition
6.1
, to obtain
|\mathcal{O}(P)|
, we only need to compute all Motzkin paths of length
k-1
that is compatible with any of the admissible Dyck types. The following characterization of admissible Dyck types of
P
allows us to focus on only one of them, instead of all, when computing
|\mathcal{O}(P)|
.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
P=\{p_{1}>p_{2}>\cdots>p_{k}\}\subseteq[n]
and
D
an admissible Dyck type of
P
. Let
\ell_{i}
be the starting height of the
i
-th up step of
D
. Then for all
2\leq i\leq k
, we have

\ell_{i}\leq\min\left(\ell_{i-1}+1,p_{i}-3-2(k-i)\right).

Proof.

It is clear that
\ell_{i}\leq\ell_{i-1}+1
, as there is no up step between the
(i-1)
-st and the
i
-th up step. Now for the other inequality, as
D
is admissible, there is some
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)
such that
\mathfrak{D}(\operatorname{cyc}(\pi))=D
. Consider all the steps of
D
after the
i
-th up step (itself included), among them
k-i+1
are up steps, and thus
\ell_{i}+k-i+1
are down steps, as
D
is a Dyck path. By the definition of
\mathfrak{D}
and
\mathfrak{M}
, these steps are from distinct elements from
p_{i}
down to
2
. Hence, we must have more elements than down steps, meaning that
\ell_{i}+2(k-i+1)\leq p_{i}-1
, thus
\ell_{i}\leq p_{i}-3-2(k-i)
.
∎

As a simple corollary, we recover the characterization of admissible pinnacle sets (
i.e.
, pinnacle sets
P
such that
\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)
is not empty) in
Davis et al. 2018
, here stated and strengthened for our need.

Corollary 6.3

(See Proposition 2.3 in
Davis et al. 2018
)
.

Let
P=\{p_{1}>p_{2}>\cdots>p_{k}\}\subseteq[n]
. Then
\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)
is not empty if and only if
p_{i}\geq 3+2(k-i)
for all
2\leq i\leq k
.

Furthermore, when this condition is satisfied, there is an admissible Dyck type
D_{P}
for
P
, with all
\ell_{i}
reaching the maximum.
D_{P}
can be constructed as follows: for each
2\leq i\leq k
, we put
\max(0,\ell_{i-1}-p_{i}+2+2(k-i))
down steps between the
(i-1)
-st up step and the
i
-th one, and we add the correct number of down steps at the end to make
D_{P}
a Dyck path. For
D_{P}
, we have
\ell_{i}=\min\left(\ell_{i-1}+1,p_{i}-3-2(k-i)\right)
.

Proof.

For the “only if” part, suppose that there is an index
i
with
p_{i}<3+2(k-i)
. If
\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)
contains some
\pi
, we take
D=\mathfrak{D}(\operatorname{cyc}(\pi))
, and by Proposition
6.2
, the starting height
\ell_{i}
of the
i
-th step of
D
satisfies
\ell_{i}\leq p_{i}-3-2(k-i)<0
, which is impossible. Therefore, such an index
i
cannot exist.

For the “if” part, we first show that
D_{P}
constructed above is an admissible Dyck type. As
p_{i}\geq 3+2(k-i)
, the number of down steps between the
(i-1)
-st and the
i
-th up steps is at most
\ell_{i-1}+1
, and accounting for the
(i-1)
-st up step, we have
\ell_{i}\geq 0
, showing that
D_{P}
is indeed a Dyck path. To show that
D_{P}
is admissible, we expand
D_{P}
into a Motzkin path
M
that satisfies the condition in Lemma
2.2
. This is always possible, as
p_{i}\geq 3+2(k-i)
implies that there are enough elements for down steps at each time, as in the proof of Proposition
6.2
. With
M
a valid Motzkin type, by Proposition
2.3
, we see that
\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)
is not empty.

The equality for
\ell_{i}
holds by construction. Now, let
D^{\prime}
be an admissible Dyck type of
P
, with
\ell^{\prime}_{i}
the starting height of its
i
-th step. We show by induction that
\ell^{\prime}_{i}\leq\ell_{i}
. This holds for
i=1
as
\ell^{\prime}_{i}=\ell_{i}=0
. Suppose that
\ell^{\prime}_{i-1}\leq\ell_{i-1}
, then by Proposition
6.2
,

\ell^{\prime}_{i}\leq\min\left(\ell^{\prime}_{i-1}+1,p_{i}-3-2(k-i)\right)\leq\min\left(\ell_{i-1}+1,p_{i}-3-2(k-i)\right)=\ell_{i}.

We thus conclude the maximality of
D_{P}
.
∎

Given
P\subseteq[n]
with
\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)
not empty, we call the Dyck path
D_{P}
defined in Corollary
6.3
to be the
maximal Dyck type
of
P
. We can now characterize permutations in
\mathcal{O}(P)
in a simple way.

Theorem 6.4
.

Given
P\subseteq[n]
with
|P|=k
. A permutation
\sigma
is in
\mathcal{O}(P)
if and only if the Motzkin type of
\operatorname{cyc}(\hat{\sigma})
is compatible (as defined in Proposition
6.1
) with the maximal Dyck type
D_{P}
of
P
. We thus have

|\mathcal{O}(P)|=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in\mathcal{M}_{k-1}\\
M\text{ compatible with }D_{P}\end{subarray}}w_{\mathcal{M}}(M).

Proof.

The “if” part follows directly from Proposition
6.1
by taking
D=D_{P}
therein, as
\sigma\mapsto\hat{\sigma}
is involutive. For the “only if” part, as
\sigma\in\mathcal{O}(P)
, there exists
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}(P)
such that
\operatorname{ord}(\pi)=\sigma
. By Proposition
6.1
, the Motzkin type of
\operatorname{cyc}(\hat{\sigma})
is compatible with the Dyck type of
\operatorname{cyc}(\pi)
, meaning that it is also compatible with
D_{P}
, according to Corollary
6.3
and the definition of compatibility. By accounting for all possible
M
, using Proposition
2.3
, we have the formula of
|\mathcal{O}(P)|
.
∎

Following
Rusu and Tenner 2021
, we say that a pinnacle set
P
with
k=|P|
is
maximally admissible
if
\mathcal{O}(P)=\mathfrak{S}_{k}
. The following corollary recovers a result in
Rusu and Tenner 2021
.

Corollary 6.5

(Corollary 4.6 in
Rusu and Tenner 2021
)
.

A pinnacle set
P=\{p_{1}>p_{2}>\cdots>p_{k}\}\subseteq[n]
is maximally admissible if and only if
p_{i}\geq\min(2k-i+2,3(k+1-i))
for all
2\leq i\leq k-1
.

Proof.

From Theorem
6.4
, we know that
P
is maximally admissible if and only if
D_{P}
is compatible with all possible Motzkin paths of length
k-1
. By definition, it is equivalent to
D_{P}
being compatible with the Motzkin path that starts with
\lfloor\frac{k-1}{2}\rfloor
up steps and ends with
\lfloor\frac{k-1}{2}\rfloor
down steps. This is equivalent to say that
\ell_{i}\geq\min(i-1,k-i)
for
\ell_{i}
for
D_{P}
defined in Corollary
6.3
.

For the “only if” part, it is clear that, for
2\leq i\leq k-1
, we have
p_{i}-3-2(k-i)\geq\min(i-1,k-i)
, which implies what we want. For the “if” part, we proceed by induction on
i
. The case
i=1
is trivial, as we have
\ell_{1}=0\geq\min(0,k)
. Suppose that
\ell_{i-1}\geq\min(i-2,k-i+1)
, then by Corollary
6.3
and the condition on
p_{i}
, we have

\ell_{i}=\min(\ell_{i-1}+1,p_{i}-3-2(k-i))\geq\min(\min(i-1,k-i+2),\min(i-1,k-i)).

We thus have
\ell_{i}\geq\min(i-1,k-i)
to conclude the induction.
∎

Using Theorem
6.4
, we propose the following recurrence for
|\mathcal{O}(P)|
that avoids the enumeration of the exponentially many Motzkin paths.

Proposition 6.6
.

Given
P=\{p_{1}>p_{2}>\cdots>p_{k}\}\subseteq[n]
, we define
\ell_{1}=0
and
\ell_{i}=\min(\ell_{i-1}+1,p_{i}-3-2(k-i))
for
2\leq i\leq k
. We then define
b_{P}(i,j)
for
-1\leq i,j\leq k
as follows. When
j>\ell_{i-1}
or
j<0
, we have
b_{P}(i,j)=0
. Otherwise, we have

\displaystyle b_{P}(0,0)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle b_{P}(i+1,j)

\displaystyle=b_{P}(i,j-1)+2(j+1)b_{P}(i,j)+(j+1)(j+2)b_{P}(i,j+1).

Then we have
|\mathcal{O}(P)|=b_{P}(k-1,0)
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Theorem
4.1
and Proposition
5.1
. We first extend the weight
w_{\mathcal{M}}
to prefixes of Motzkin paths. Then, the quantity
b_{P}(i,j)
stands for the total weight of prefixes of Motzkin paths compatible with
D_{P}
ending at
(i,j)
, where
D_{P}
is defined in Corollary
6.3
. At
b_{P}(k-1,0)
, we thus have the total weights of Motzkin paths compatible with
D_{P}
, and we conclude by Theorem
6.4
.
∎

The recurrence in Proposition
6.6
gives a concrete algorithm for Proposition
1.3
.

of Proposition
1.3
.

Let
k=|P|
. Computing
|\mathcal{O}(P)|
using the recurrence in Proposition
6.6
needs two steps: computing
\ell_{i}
for
i\in[k]
, which takes
O(k)
operations; computing
b_{P}(i,j)
for
i,j\in[k]

O(k^{2})

Using the algorithm above, we are able to compute
|\mathcal{O}(P)|
for most practical purposes. We observe that, for fixed
k=|P|
, there is a finite number of possible non-zero values for
|\mathcal{O}(P)|
. We denote this number by
\alpha_{k}
. A natural bound of
\alpha_{k}
is the
(k-1)
-st Motzkin number, as the restriction imposed by
D_{P}
essentially means summing over the weights of all Motzkin paths of length
k-1
below a given “ceiling”, which is also a Motzkin path, when computing
|\mathcal{O}(P)|
. However, this bound is not tight. For instance, a Motzkin path and its mirror, as ceilings, lead to the same weighted sum. An exhaustive computation shows that, for
k
from
1
to
19
, the values of
\alpha_{k}
are:

1,1,2,3,6,10,21,38,86,173,412,926,2331,5713,14981,38750,104907,279344,769429

At the time of writing, this sequence is not yet on the
Online Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences

OEIS Foundation Inc.
. It may thus be interesting to study
(\alpha_{k})_{k\geq 1}
. A precise description of
\alpha_{k}
may be complicated, as it involves various symmetries and coincidences due to explicit values of the weights. However, we may expect
(\alpha_{k})_{k\geq 1}
to have the same asymptotic behavior as Motzkin numbers.

Open question 2

(\alpha_{k})_{k\geq 1}
grow exponentially with the growth constant
