A high-order finite volume method for Maxwell’s equations in heterogeneous and time-varying media
Damian P. San Roman Alerigi, David I. Ketcheson, Boon S. Ooi

Abstract
We develop a finite volume method for Maxwell’s equations in materials whose electromagnetic properties vary in space and time. We investigate both conservative and non-conservative numerical formulations. High-order methods are employed to accurately resolve fine structures that develop due to the varying material properties. Numerical examples demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method in handling temporal variation and its efficiency relative to traditional 2nd-order FDTD.

Introduction

Maxwell’s equations in a charge- and current-free space are defined as:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\vec{D}-\nabla\times\vec{H}

\displaystyle=0,

(1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\vec{B}+\nabla\times\vec{E}

\displaystyle=0;

(1b)

where
\vec{E},\vec{B}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
are the electric and magnetic fields, respectively. They are related to the material fields
\vec{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\vec{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
via the constitutive relations:

\displaystyle\vec{D}

\displaystyle=\vec{D}\left(\bar{\varepsilon}(\vec{r},t),\vec{E}\right),

(2a)

\displaystyle\vec{B}

\displaystyle=\vec{B}\left(\bar{\mu}(\vec{r},t),\vec{H}\right).

(2b)

where
\bar{\varepsilon},\bar{\mu}:\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
are symmetrical and second-rank tensors with non-zero off-diagonal entries, and describe the electric and magnetic response of the material; they are known as permittivity and permeability.

In this work, we are interested in the general case in which
\bar{\varepsilon},\bar{\mu}
vary in space and time, and
\vec{D}
,
\vec{B}
may be nonlinear.

We are motivated by recent interest in materials whose properties can change at a pace comparable to that of electromagnetic wave oscillation;
e.g.
[
5
,
6
,
23
]
. The effect of spatially heterogeneous materials on wave propagation has received much attention, but the effect of temporal material variations is much less studied. Novel effects arising from wave propagation in space-time-varying media include trapping, confinement, ultra-short pulse coupling, beam transformation, negative refraction, eigen–polarization, and the optical Bohm–Aharonov effect
[
11
,
20
,
19
,
22
,
15
,
16
,
21
,
2
,
3
,

]
. This has opened the theoretical possibility to control light in new ways,
e.g.
Alcubierre’s warp drives
[
25
]
.

A numerical scheme for electromagnetic wave propagation in time-dependent linear and heterogeneous media is developed in
[
8
]
. The algorithm is based on the finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) method presented in
[
10
]
, with an extension to account for the temporal variation of the medium. This is attained by applying an operator splitting method and Magnus’ series expansion
[
8
]
. Due to the splitting, the scheme requires that certain iteration matrices be recomputed at every time step.

We apply the high-order wave propagation method developed in
[
14
]
. This method is based on weighted essentially non-oscillatory (WENO) reconstruction, Runge-Kutta time integration, and wave-propagation Riemann solvers. We expect these high-order algorithms to be more efficient than traditional second-order finite differences (i.e., the FDTD method) for high-frequency waves in rapidly-varying media. Furthermore, they can handle nonlinear media, do not require operator splitting, and avoid the costly computation of iteration matrices required in
[
8
]
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we consider two forms of Maxwell’s equations with explicit time-dependence: first as a homogeneous and conservative hyperbolic system in which the conserved quantities appear implicitly, and second as a non-conservative system of balance laws. Section
3
we review the high-order wave propagation method introduced in
[
14
]
and present the general algorithm to solve the Riemann problem. In Section
4
, we derive the order of convergence for the algorithm using some fundamental 1D and 2D examples. Finally, in Section
5
, we discuss future work, advantages, and opportunities.

2
Maxwell’s equations in nonlinear, time-varying media

To illustrate the difficulties involved in discretizing this system, let us consider the one-dimensional case. Then
D,B,H,E
are scalar fields, and the system may be written in the form:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{D}+\partial_{x}{H}

\displaystyle=0,,

(3a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{B}+\partial_{x}{E}

\displaystyle=0.,

(3b)

In general, the constitutive relations in (
2
) can take any shape. For the purpose of the following example, we follow a customary electromagnetic approach and expand them in a power series
[
9
]
:

\displaystyle D\left(\varepsilon(x,t),E\right)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{o}\left(E+\sum_{i}\chi_{e}^{(i)}(x,t)E^{i}\right),

(4a)

\displaystyle B\left(\mu(x,t),H\right)

\displaystyle=\mu_{o}\left(H+\sum_{i}\chi_{h}^{(i)}(x,t)H^{i}\right),.

(4b)

where
\varepsilon_{o},\mu_{o}\in\mathbb{R}
are the vacuum permittivity and permeability, and
\chi^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}
are the electric (e) and magnetic (h) susceptibility scalar functions.

Equation
4
allows us to consider two types of constitutive relations. For linear media, we have

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=\varepsilon E,

(5a)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\mu H;

(5b)

whereas for non-linear meadia we can consider a system with cubic nonlinearity,
i.e.
:

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=\varepsilon E+\chi^{3}E^{3},

(6a)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\mu H+\chi^{3}H^{3}.

(6b)

Finally, we observe that equation
3
can be rewritten in conservative and non-conservative form.

2.1
Conservative form

Let us define the conserved vector
\vec{q}
and flux
\vec{f}\left(\vec{q}\right)
as:

\displaystyle\vec{q}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}D\\
B\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\vec{f}\left(\vec{q}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}H(\mu(x,t),B)\\
E(\varepsilon(x,t),D)\end{pmatrix};

(7)

hence we can summarize (
3
) as an homogeneous first-order hyperbolic system:

\partial_{t}\vec{q}+\partial_{x}\vec{f}(\vec{q};x,t)=0,

(8)

with

\displaystyle\partial_{q}\vec{f}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&\partial_{B}H\\
\partial_{D}E&0\end{pmatrix},

(9)

and wave speeds (
s
) given by:

s=\pm\sqrt{\partial_{D}E\partial_{B}H}.

(10)

2.1.1
Linear media

In the linear example we substitute (
5
) into (
7
), and obtain:

\displaystyle\vec{f}(\vec{q};x,t)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}B/\mu(x,t)\\
D/\varepsilon(x,t)\end{pmatrix}.

(11)

Consequently, the wave speeds are:

s=\pm\sqrt{1/(\varepsilon\mu)}.

(12)

2.1.2
Nonlinear media

In the nonlinear case, we must determine derivatives of the inverse functions
H(\mu,B)
and
E(\varepsilon,D)
to compute the speed of the waves in (
10
) and other relevant quantities. We can approximate the terms
\partial_{B}H
and
\partial_{D}E
by means of an iterative method. For example, consider the nonlinear constitutive relation for the electric field given in (
6
), and solve for
E(D)
as:

E(D)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(D-\chi^{3}(E(D))^{3}\right).

(13)

Substituting the latter into itself yields

E(D)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(D-\chi^{3}D^{3}+{\mathcal{O}}(D^{5})\right).

(14)

Thus, finally we obtain the partial derivative of
E
with respect to
D
as:

\partial_{D}E\approx\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(1-3\chi^{3}_{e}D^{2}\right).

(15)

Similarly, we can follow the same algorithm to calculate the derivative of
H
with respect to
B
and obtain:

\partial_{B}H\approx\frac{1}{\mu}\left(1-3\chi^{3}_{h}B^{2}\right).

(16)

So the wave speeds are:

s\approx\pm\sqrt{\frac{1}{\varepsilon\mu}\left(1-3\chi^{3}_{e}D^{2}\right)\left(1-3\chi^{3}_{h}B^{2}\right)}+{\mathcal{O}}(D^{3})

(17)

2.2
Non-conservative form

As we can see from the previous treatment, the conservative form can be intricate to solve complex nonlinear materials. An alternative is to derive a non-conservative form to (
3
). In this case, we proceed by applying the chain rule to the time derivatives in (
3
), obtaining:

\displaystyle\partial_{E}D\partial_{t}E+\partial_{x}H

\displaystyle=-\partial_{\varepsilon}D\partial_{t}\varepsilon,

(18a)

\displaystyle\partial_{H}B\partial_{t}H+\partial_{x}E

\displaystyle=-\partial_{\mu}B\partial_{t}\mu.

(18b)

Let us re-define the conserved quantity vector and flux as:

\displaystyle\vec{q}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}E\\
H\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\vec{f}\left(\vec{q}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}H\\
E\end{pmatrix};

(19)

and introduce a second-rank tensor known as the capacity,
\bar{\kappa}
, and vector term named source,
\vec{\psi}
term:

\displaystyle\bar{\kappa}(\vec{q};x,t)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{E}D\\
\partial_{H}B\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\vec{\psi}(\vec{q};x,t)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-E\partial_{t}\varepsilon\\
-H\partial_{t}\mu\end{pmatrix}.

(20)

Then, we can rewrite equation
18
as a balance law of the form:

\bar{\kappa}(\vec{q};x,t)\cdot\partial_{t}\vec{q}(x,t)+\partial_{x}\vec{f}\left(\vec{q}\right)=\vec{\psi}(\vec{q};x,t),

(21)

This balance law results in a simpler form of linear and nonlinear cases. For linear media, substitute (
5
) into (
18
) to produce:

\displaystyle\varepsilon(x,t)E_{t}+\partial_{x}H

\displaystyle=-E\partial_{t}\varepsilon,

(22a)

\displaystyle\mu(x,t)H_{t}+\partial_{x}E

\displaystyle=-H\partial_{t}\mu.

(22b)

Whereas for nonlinear media we can substitute (
6
) instead and obtain:

\displaystyle\left(\varepsilon+2\chi^{3}_{e}E^{2}\right)\partial_{t}E+\partial_{x}H

\displaystyle=-E\partial_{t}\varepsilon,

(23a)

\displaystyle\left(\mu+2\chi^{3}_{h}H^{2}\right)\partial_{t}H+\partial_{x}E

\displaystyle=-H\partial_{t}\mu.

(23b)

Note that in either case the speed of the waves is given

s=\pm 1

(24)

Semi-discrete wave propagation

Numerical wave propagation methods for systems similar to (
21
) have been developed previously
[
18
,
17
,
14
]
. However, in those methods, the capacity function
\kappa
was assumed to depend only on
x
; whereas here, it may depend also on
q
and
t
. In this Section, we review those methods and discuss their extension to the case of (

) and (

). Specifically, we extend the scheme presented in
[

]
to handle time-varying fluxes and capacity functions that depend on
q,x,t
. Since that scheme is based on the method of lines, time-varying coefficients can be handled in a straightforward way. We focus first on the one-dimensional method and then briefly describe the extension to more spatial dimensions.

3.1
Basic scheme

We consider a grid of cells with centers
x_{i}
and interfaces
x_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}
(see figure
1
), and define the average of
q
over cell
i
:

Q_{i}=\int_{x_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}^{x_{i+\frac{1}{2}}}q(x,t)dx.

(25)

From the cell averages we construct a piecewise-polynomial approximation to
q
:

\hat{\vec{q}}(x)=\hat{\vec{q}}_{i}\left(x\right),\qquad\text{for}\quad x\in\left(x_{i-\frac{1}{2}},x_{i+\frac{1}{2}}\right),

(26)

where

\hat{\vec{q}}_{i}\left(x\right)=\vec{q}\left(x,t\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\Delta x^{r+1}\right).

(27)

At each interface
x_{i-\frac{1}{2}}
, the approximation is discontinuous; hence, we define the interface values as:

\displaystyle q^{L}_{i}-\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle=\hat{q}_{i-1}(x_{i}-\frac{1}{2})

\displaystyle q^{R}_{i}-\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle=\hat{q}_{i}(x_{i}-\frac{1}{2}).

(28)

3.1.1
Conservative scheme

When applied to (
8
), the semi-discrete scheme presented in
[
14
]
takes the form

\frac{\partial Q_{i}}{\partial t}=-\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(\mathcal{A}^{+}\Delta q_{i}-\frac{1}{2}+\mathcal{A}^{-}\Delta q_{i}+\frac{1}{2}+f(q^{L}_{i}+\frac{1}{2})-f(q^{R}_{i}-\frac{1}{2})\right).

(29)

The flux difference arises from integrating the flux term over the cell. The terms
\mathcal{A}^{-}\Delta q,\mathcal{A}^{+}\Delta q
are referred to as fluctuations and incorporate the effects of waves emanating from the cell interfaces, where we must solve a Riemann problem.

At each cell interface, we approximate the solution of a Riemann problem with initial states given by the reconstructed values at the interface:

q(x)=\left\{\begin{matrix}q^{L}_{i}-\frac{1}{2}&x<x_{i-\frac{1}{2}},\\
q^{R}_{i}-\frac{1}{2}&x>x_{i-\frac{1}{2}}.\end{matrix}\right.

(30)

The solution is approximated by a sequence of jump discontinuities; this amounts to decomposing the jump as:

q^{R}_{i}-\frac{1}{2}-q^{L}_{i}-\frac{1}{2}=\sum_{p=1}^{m}\alpha^{p}_{i}-\frac{1}{2}r^{p}_{i}-\frac{1}{2}=\sum_{p}\mathcal{W}^{p}_{i}-\frac{1}{2}.

(31)

Here the vectors
r^{p}
are the eigenvalues of some approximate flux Jacobian at the interface. The vectors
\mathcal{W}^{p}
are known as
waves
, and the collective effect of the right- and left-going waves yields the
fluctuations
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{-}\Delta q_{i}-\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{p}\left(s^{p}_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{-}\mathcal{W}^{p}_{i}-\frac{1}{2},

(32a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{+}\Delta q_{i}-\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{p}\left(s^{p}_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{+}\mathcal{W}^{p}_{i}-\frac{1}{2},

(32b)

Here
s^{p}
is the speed of the wave
\mathcal{W}^{p}
(often given by an eigenvalue of an approximate flux Jacobian), and

\left(s^{p}\right)^{-}=\min\left(s^{p},0\right)\qquad\left(s^{p}\right)^{+}=\max\left(s^{p},0\right).

3.1.2
Non-conservative scheme

Following a similar derivation to its conservative counterpart, the non-conservative form (
21
) becomes

\frac{\partial Q_{i}}{\partial t}=-\frac{1}{\bar{K}_{i}\Delta x}\left(\mathcal{A}^{+}\Delta q_{i-\frac{1}{2}}+\mathcal{A}^{-}\Delta q_{i+\frac{1}{2}}+f(q^{L}_{i}+\frac{1}{2})-f(q^{R}_{i}-\frac{1}{2})\right)+\Psi_{i}.

(33)

Here
K_{i}
and
\Psi_{i}
are averages of
\kappa
and
\psi
over cell
i
. For
\Psi
we use the trapezoidal rule:

\displaystyle\Psi^{1}_{i}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\dot{\epsilon}^{R}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}E^{R}_{i}-\frac{1}{2}+\dot{\epsilon}^{L}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}E^{L}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}}{2}

(34)

\displaystyle\Psi^{2}_{i}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\dot{\mu}^{R}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}H^{R}_{i}-\frac{1}{2}+\dot{\mu}^{L}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}H^{L}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}}{2}

(35)

where for readability
\dot{\eta}=\eta_{t}
. For
\kappa
, we also use a trapezoidal average.
Let
\tilde{\eta}_{i}
be a suitable average of
\eta
across the
i
th cell at some time
t^{n}
. Then, at the interface
x_{i-\frac{1}{2}}
we can approximate the average value
K_{i-\frac{1}{2}}
using the trapezoidal rule,

K_{k,i-\frac{1}{2}}^{n}\approx\frac{\tilde{\eta}^{R}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}+\tilde{\eta}^{L}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}{2}+\frac{p}{2^{p}}\>\chi^{(p)}\left(q^{R}_{k,i-\frac{1}{2}}+q^{L}_{k,i-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{p-1},\quad p\geq 2,

(36)

3.2
Riemann solver

Following (LeVeque, 2002), we can design a Riemann solver for this system as follows. We assume the values of
\epsilon,\mu
are constant in each cell so that we can write
\epsilon_{i},\mu_{i}
. The eigenvector matrix for
f_{q}
is

\displaystyle R_{i}-\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-Z_{i-1}&Z_{i}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},

(37)

where
Z_{i}=\sqrt{H_{B}/E_{D}}
. We can use an
f
-wave solver by solving the system

R\beta=f_{i}(Q_{i})-f_{i-1}(Q_{i-1}).

(38)

3.3
Reconstruction and time integration

It remains to specify the method of time integration and spatial reconstruction. These ingredients determine the order of accuracy of the scheme.

We use the ten-stage fourth-order strong-stability-preserving Runge-Kutta scheme described in
[
12
]
for time integration. Although it uses many stages, this method has a large region of absolute stability, allowing the use of large CFL numbers.

We use fifth-order component-wise WENO reconstruction in space as described in
[
24
]
. Traditionally, methods based on finite differences use several points per wavelength to correctly model the high-frequency waves that occur in some physical problems,
e.g.
electromagnetism. We use WENO or any high-degree polynomial because it allows us to keep a high level of accuracy with relatively coarser grids.

All of this is implemented in Clawpack’s PyClaw module
[
13
,
4
]
. The Riemann solvers for Maxwell’s equations are implemented in a separate package called EMClaw, available at
[
7
]
.

3.4
Algorithm

At every Runge-Kutta stage, the numerical implementation of (
33
) follows the steps:

1.

For the non-conservative system only: update cell averages of the time-dependent capacity
\bar{K}_{i}^{n}
, and source
\Psi_{i}^{n}
functions;

2.

Reconstruct the interface values
q_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{R},q_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{L}
using fifth-order WENO reconstruction;

3.

Solve the Riemann problem with initial states
(q_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{L},q_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{R})
to compute the fluctuations,
\mathcal{A}^{\pm}\Delta q_{i-\frac{1}{2}}
;

4.

Calculate the flux difference
f(q^{L}_{i}+\frac{1}{2})-f(q^{R}_{i}-\frac{1}{2})
;

5.

Compute
\partial Q_{i}/\partial t
using the semi-discrete scheme (
33
).

3.5
Extension to two dimensions

Using a dimension-by-dimension approach, we extend the numerical wave propagation method to two dimensions where the main equation is (on the Cartesian grid):

\bar{\kappa}\cdot\vec{q}_{t}+\vec{f}(\vec{q})_{x}+\vec{g}(\vec{q})_{y}=\vec{\psi}(\vec{q},x,y,t).

(39)

In two dimensions, the semi-discrete scheme (
33
) takes the form:

\begin{split}\frac{\partial Q_{ij}}{\partial t}=-\frac{1}{\bar{K}_{ij}\Delta x\Delta y}\Bigg(&\mathcal{A}^{+}\Delta q_{i-\frac{1}{2},j}+\mathcal{A}^{-}\Delta q_{i+\frac{1}{2},j}+f(q^{L}_{i+\frac{1}{2},j})-f(q^{R}_{i-\frac{1}{2},j})\\
&+\mathcal{B}^{+}\Delta q_{i,j-\frac{1}{2}}+\mathcal{B}^{-}\Delta q_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}+g(q^{L}_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}})-g(q^{R}_{i,j-\frac{1}{2}})\Bigg)\\
&+\Psi_{ij}.\end{split}

(40)

The fluctuation terms are determined by solving the Riemann problem in the corresponding direction and initial data; for example,
\mathcal{B}^{+}\Delta q_{i,j-\frac{1}{2}}
is calculated by solving the Riemann problem in the
y
-direction with initial states
(q^{L}_{i,j-\frac{1}{2}},q^{R}_{i,j-\frac{1}{2}})
. As noted in
[
14
]
, for the method to be high-order accurate, the fluctuations and flux differences must be computed based on a multidimensional reconstruction of
q
. In the present work, we instead use a much cheaper dimension-by-dimension reconstruction, described in
[
14
]
. Although this leads to a formally 2nd-order accurate scheme, it is still much less dissipative than traditional second-order schemes and allows high-frequency waves to be resolved on much coarser grids.

4
Numerical applications and convergence

We test the above-described techniques numerically by studying the propagation of electromagnetic waves in media with spacetime variations. We choose examples where the exact solution can be calculated with great accuracy.

To obtain the results in this section, we developed EMClaw
[
7
]
, a multi-dimensional numerical solver for Maxwell’s equations in spacetime-varying and nonlinear media, based on the scheme (
33
). EMClaw was designed as an extension of the Clawpack package
[
4
]
.

Miscellaneous definitions

We use the numerical examples to measure the
L_{1}
errors,
\vec{E}
, and rate of convergence,

E_{L_{1}}=\Delta x\sum_{i}|Q_{i}-\hat{Q_{i}}|,

where
\hat{Q_{i}}
is a highly accurate solution cell average computed by characteristics (exact solution) or by using grid refinement.

For the subsequent numerical studies, let

h

E_{s}(h)
the error of the numerical solution on a grid with spacing
h

h/2

Let

I(\vec{r},t)=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}q_{i}^{2}(\vec{r},t)}.

Finally let

5
Summary

We have shown an approach to extend the highly accurate wave propagation algorithm of SharpClaw to model wave propagation in nonlinear and spacetime-varying media in one and two spatial dimensions. The scheme is second-order accurate, as demonstrated by the test results.

Using WENO and strong-stability-preserving time integration, high-order accurate results are obtained in one dimension even when the average value of the coefficients in each cell is second-order-accurate.

A drawback of our implementation is that to achieve the high-order convergence in two dimensions, as observed in SharpClaw, the spacetime-varying coefficients need to be resolved with high accuracy, for example, using high-degree polynomial interpolation and adequate quadrature.
