Using Malliavin calculus to solve a chemical diffusion master equation
Alberto Lanconelli

Abstract
We propose a novel method to solve a chemical diffusion master equation of birth and death type. This is an infinite system of Fokker-Planck equations where the different components are coupled by reaction dynamics similar in form to a chemical master equation. This system was proposed in [ 3 ] for modelling the probabilistic evolution of chemical reaction kinetics associated with spatial diffusion of individual particles. Using some basic tools and ideas from infinite dimensional Gaussian analysis we are able to reformulate the aforementioned infinite system of Fokker-Planck equations as a single evolution equation solved by a generalized stochastic process and written in terms of Malliavin derivatives and differential second quantization operators. Via this alternative representation we link certain finite dimensional projections of the solution of the original problem to the solution of a single partial differential equations of Ornstein-Uhlenbeck type containing as many variables as the dimension of the aforementioned projection space.



Key words and phrases: Particle-based reaction-diffusion models, Fock space, Malliavin calculus.

AMS 2000 classification: 60H07; 60H30; 92E20.

Introduction and statement of the main result

Suppose we want to model the probabilistic evolution of a system that is initially constituted by a single particle of a chemical species

where

denotes the propensity for reaction (I) to occur for a particle located at position
x\in[0,1]
(i.e., the probability per unit of time for this particle to disappear) while
\lambda_{c}(x)
is the propensity for a new particle to be created at position
x\in[0,1]
by reaction (II);

•

drift-less isotropic
diffusion
in space.

While reactions (

[

This is due to the hybrid nature of the considered reaction-diffusion process, i.e. discrete in the evolution of the number of particles (and hence of the spatial dimension of the problem) and continuous in the random movement of those particles. In the recent paper
[
3
]
, the authors proposed a set of equations for the functions

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n}):=p_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})\mathbb{P}(N(t)=n),\quad n\geq 0,

that aims to model the reaction-diffusion process described above. Here

represents the joint probability density function, conditioned to the event

of birth and death type and it takes the form:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\rho_{0}(t)=&\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{d}(y)\rho_{1}(t,y)dy-\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{c}(y)dy\cdot\rho_{0}(t),\quad t>0;\\
\partial_{t}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})=&\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial^{2}_{x_{i}}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})\\
&+(n+1)\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{d}(y)\rho_{n+1}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n},y)dy\\
&-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{d}(x_{i})\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})\\
&+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{c}(x_{i})\rho_{n-1}(t,x_{1},...,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},...,x_{n})\\
&-\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{c}(y)dy\cdot\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n}),\quad n\geq 1,t>0,(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in]0,1[^{n},\end{split}

(1.2)

with initial and Neumann boundary conditions

\displaystyle\begin{split}\rho_{0}(0)&=0;\\
\rho_{1}(0,x_{1})&=\zeta(x_{1}),\quad x_{1}\in[0,1];\\
\rho_{n}(0,x_{1},...,x_{n})&=0,\quad n>1,(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in[0,1]^{n};\\
\partial_{\nu}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})&=0,\quad n\geq 1,t\geq 0,(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in\partial[0,1]^{n}.\end{split}

(1.3)

This is an infinite system of Fokker-Planck equations where the components have an increasing number of degrees of freedom (to account for all the possible numbers of particles in the system) and are coupled through the reaction mechanism (
1.1
). The term

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial^{2}_{x_{i}}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})

\displaystyle(n+1)\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{d}(y)\rho_{n+1}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n},y)dy-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{d}(x_{i})\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})

formalize gain and loss, respectively, due to reaction (I), while

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{c}(x_{i})\rho_{n-1}(t,x_{1},...,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},...,x_{n})-\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{c}(y)dy\cdot\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})

relate to reaction (II). The functions
\lambda_{d},\lambda_{c},\zeta:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
in (
1.2
)-(
1.3

\zeta
is smooth with

in (

[0,1]^{n}
. The particles are assumed to be indistinguishable thus entailing the symmetry of

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{n}}p_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})dx_{1}\cdot\cdot\cdot dx_{n}=1,

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{n}}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})dx_{1}\cdot\cdot\cdot dx_{n}=\mathbb{P}(N(t)=n).

(1.4)

Therefore, since
\sum_{n\geq 0}\mathbb{P}(N(t)=n)=1

1.2

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\int_{[0,1]^{n}}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})dx_{1}\cdot\cdot\cdot dx_{n}=1.

&+\lambda_{c}\mathbb{P}(N(t)=n-1)-\lambda_{c}\mathbb{P}(N(t)=n).\end{split}

To state our main result we introduce a few notations and a couple of standing assumptions.

Assumption 1.1
.

There exists an orthonormal basis
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
of
L^{2}([0,1])
that diagonalizes the operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}:=-\partial^{2}_{x}+\lambda_{d}(x),\quad x\in[0,1],

(1.7)

with homogenous Neumann boundary conditions. This means that for all
j,k\geq 1
we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\xi_{k}(y)\xi_{j}(y)dy=\delta_{kj},\quad\xi_{k}^{\prime}(0)=\xi_{k}^{\prime}(1)=0,

and there exists a sequence of non negative real numbers
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\xi_{k}=\alpha_{k}\xi_{k},\quad\mbox{ for all $k\geq 1$}.

Remark 1.2
.

We note that according to the classical Sturm-Liouville boundary value problem the continuity of
\lambda_{d}
is sufficient for Assumption
1.1
to hold true.

We now denote by
\Pi_{N}:L^{2}([0,1])\to L^{2}([0,1])

, i.e.

\displaystyle\Pi_{N}f(x):=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\langle f,\xi_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1])}\xi_{k}(x),\quad x\in[0,1];

we also set

\displaystyle d_{k}:=\langle\lambda_{d},\xi_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1])},\quad c_{k}:=\langle\lambda_{c},\xi_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1])},\quad\gamma:=\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{c}(y)dy,\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\zeta_{k}:=\langle\zeta,\xi_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1])},

where the functions

,

and

)-(

).

Assumption 1.3
.

There exists

such that
\Pi_{N_{0}}\lambda_{d}=\lambda_{d}
; this is equivalent to say
\Pi_{N}\lambda_{d}=\lambda_{d}
for all

.

Remark 1.4
.

Assumption (
1.3
) is readily fulfilled in the case of a constant function

This gives

\displaystyle\xi_{1}(x)=1(x)\quad\mbox{ and hence }\quad(\Pi_{1}\lambda_{d})(x)=\lambda_{d}(x),

.

It is useful to recall that the solution to the Cauchy problem

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}u(t,z)&=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\partial^{2}_{z_{i}}u(t,z)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(d_{i}-c_{i}-\alpha_{i}z_{i}\right)\partial_{z_{i}}u(t,z)+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}z_{i}-\gamma\right)u(t,z);\\
u(0,z)&=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\zeta_{i}z_{i},\end{split}

(2.5)

which is the key ingredient of formulas (
1.9
)-(
1.10
), admits the following Feynman-Kac representation (see for instance
[
7
]
):

\displaystyle u(t,z)=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\zeta_{i}Z^{z_{i}}_{i}(t)\right)\exp\left\{\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}Z^{z_{i}}_{i}(s)-\gamma\right)ds\right\}\right],\quad t\geq 0,z=(z_{1},...,z_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

(2.6)

Here, for
i\in\{1,...,N\}
, the stochastic process
\{Z_{i}^{z_{i}}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is the unique strong solution of the mean-reverting Ornstein-Uhlenbeck stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle dZ_{i}^{z_{i}}(t)=\left(d_{i}-c_{i}-\alpha_{i}Z^{z_{i}}_{i}(t)\right)dt+\sqrt{2\alpha_{i}}dW_{i}(t),\quad Z_{i}^{z_{i}}(0)=z_{i},

(2.7)

with
\{W_{1}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
,…,
\{W_{N}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
being independent one dimensional Brownian motions. It is well known that the solution to (
2.7
) can be explicitly written for
\alpha_{i}>0
as

\displaystyle Z^{z_{i}}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=z_{i}e^{-\alpha_{i}t}+\frac{d_{i}-c_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}\left(1-e^{-\alpha_{i}t}\right)+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\alpha_{i}(t-s)}\sqrt{2\alpha_{i}}dW_{i}(s),

and simply

\displaystyle Z^{z_{i}}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=z_{i}+(d_{i}-c_{1})t,

when
\alpha_{i}=0
. This shows that the function
z_{i}\mapsto Z^{z_{i}}_{i}(t)
is almost surely affine and hence that, according to equation (
2.6
), for any
\tau>0
there exist positive constant
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
such that

\displaystyle|u(t,z)|\leq m_{1}e^{m_{2}|z|},\quad\mbox{for all $t\in[0,\tau]$ and $z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$}.

(2.8)

This bound entails the finiteness of the expectation in (
1.9
); since the same reasoning applies to the partial spatial derivatives of
u
(they also satisfy an equation of the form (
2.5
)), we conclude that the expectations in (
1.10
) are well defined and finite as well.

We are now going to verify that the right hand sides of (
1.9
)-(
1.10
) solve equation (
2.2
) for
n=0,1,2
; to ease the reference to formula (
1.9
)-(
1.10
), we write down explicitly the first four terms:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\rho_{0}(t)&=\mathbb{E}[u(t,Z)];\\
\Pi_{N}\rho_{1}(t,x_{1})&=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[(\partial_{z_{j}}u)(t,Z)\right]\xi_{j}(x_{1});\\
\Pi_{N}^{\otimes 2}\rho_{2}(t,x_{1},x_{2})&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[(\partial_{z_{j_{2}}}\partial_{z_{j_{1}}}u)(t,Z)\right]\xi_{j_{1}}(x_{1})\xi_{j_{2}}(x_{2});\\
\Pi_{N}^{\otimes 3}\rho_{3}(t,x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})&=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[(\partial_{z_{j_{3}}}\partial_{z_{j_{2}}}\partial_{z_{j_{1}}}u)(t,Z)\right]\xi_{j_{1}}(x_{1})\xi_{j_{2}}(x_{2})\xi_{j_{3}}(x_{3}).\end{split}

(2.9)

2.1
Equation for
\rho_{0}

We have:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}[u(t,Z)]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\partial_{t}u)(t,Z)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{k}\partial^{2}_{z_{k}}u(t,Z)+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(d_{k}-c_{k}-\alpha_{k}Z_{k}\right)\partial_{z_{k}}u(t,Z)+\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}Z_{k}-\gamma\right)u(t,Z)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{k}\mathbb{E}[\partial^{2}_{z_{k}}u(t,Z)]+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(d_{k}-c_{k}-\alpha_{k}Z_{k}\right)\partial_{z_{k}}u(t,Z)\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}Z_{k}-\gamma\right)u(t,Z)\right].

Observe that an integration by parts gives (the boundary term vanishes thanks to the bound (
2.8
) applied to
\partial_{z_{k}}u(t,z)
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\partial^{2}_{z_{k}}u(t,Z)]=\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}\partial_{z_{k}}u(t,Z)];

therefore,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}[u(t,Z)]=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{k}\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}\partial_{z_{k}}u(t,Z)]+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(d_{k}-c_{k}-\alpha_{k}Z_{k}\right)\partial_{z_{k}}u(t,Z)\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}Z_{k}-\gamma\right)u(t,Z)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(d_{k}-c_{k}\right)\partial_{z_{k}}u(t,Z)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}Z_{k}-\gamma\right)u(t,Z)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}d_{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{z_{k}}u(t,Z)\right]-\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{z_{k}}u(t,Z)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}Z_{k}-\gamma\right)u(t,Z)\right].

An additional integration by parts yields

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{z_{k}}u(t,Z)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}Z_{k}\right)u(t,Z)\right],

and hence

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}[u(t,Z)]=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}d_{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{z_{k}}u(t,Z)\right]-\gamma\mathbb{E}\left[u(t,Z)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{d}(y)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[(\partial_{z_{j}}u)(t,Z)\right]\xi_{j}(y)\right)dy-\gamma\mathbb{E}[u(t,Z)].

This corresponds to equation (
2.2
) for
n=0
, with
\rho_{0}(t)=\mathbb{E}[u(t,Z)]
and

\displaystyle\Pi_{N}\rho_{1}(t,x_{1})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[(\partial_{z_{j}}u)(t,Z)\right]\xi_{j}(x_{1}).

2.2
Equation for
\Pi_{N}\rho_{1}

We have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[(\partial_{z_{j}}u)(t,Z)\right]\xi_{j}(x_{1})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[(\partial_{z_{j}}\partial_{t}u)(t,Z)\right]\xi_{j}(x_{1}).

(2.10)

Now,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(\partial_{z_{j}}\partial_{t}u)(t,Z)\right]=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(\partial_{z_{j}}\partial_{t}u)(t,z)(2\pi)^{-N/2}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}}dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\partial_{z_{j}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{k}\partial^{2}_{z_{k}}u(t,z)+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(d_{k}-c_{k}-\alpha_{k}z_{k}\right)\partial_{z_{k}}u(t,z)\right)(2\pi)^{-N/2}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}}dz

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\partial_{z_{j}}\left(\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}z_{k}-\gamma\right)u(t,z)\right)(2\pi)^{-N/2}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}}dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{k}\partial_{z_{j}}\partial^{2}_{z_{k}}u(t,z)-\alpha_{j}\partial_{z_{j}}u(t,z)\right)(2\pi)^{-N/2}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}}dz

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(d_{k}-c_{k}-\alpha_{k}z_{k}\right)\partial_{z_{j}}\partial_{z_{k}}u(t,z)\right)(2\pi)^{-N/2}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}}dz

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(c_{j}u(t,z)+\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}z_{k}-\gamma\right)\partial_{z_{j}}u(t,z)\right)(2\pi)^{-N/2}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}}dz.

An integration by parts with respect to
\partial_{z_{k}}
in the first term of the fourth line above will produce the term

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{k}z_{k}\partial_{z_{j}}\partial_{z_{k}}u(t,z)\right)(2\pi)^{-N/2}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}}dz,

which is identical to one of the terms from the fifth line but opposite in sign. We can therefore write

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[(\partial_{z_{j}}\partial_{t}u)(t,Z)\right]=&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(-\alpha_{j}\partial_{z_{j}}u(t,z)+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(d_{k}-c_{k}\right)\partial_{z_{j}}\partial_{z_{k}}u(t,z)\right)(2\pi)^{-N/2}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}}dz\\
&+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(c_{j}u(t,z)+\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}z_{k}-\gamma\right)\partial_{z_{j}}u(t,z)\right)(2\pi)^{-N/2}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}}dz.\end{split}

(2.11)

Similarly, an integration by parts with respect to
\partial_{z_{k}}
in the term

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(-\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\partial_{z_{j}}\partial_{z_{k}}u(t,z)\right)(2\pi)^{-N/2}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}}dz

from the first line of (
2.11
) will give

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}z_{k}\right)\partial_{z_{j}}u(t,z)(2\pi)^{-N/2}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}}dz.

hence cancelling the corresponding term from the second line in (
2.11
). Summing up,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(\partial_{z_{j}}\partial_{t}u)(t,Z)\right]=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(-\alpha_{j}\partial_{z_{j}}u(t,z)+\sum_{k=1}^{N}d_{k}\partial_{z_{j}}\partial_{z_{k}}u(t,z)\right)(2\pi)^{-N/2}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}}dz

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(c_{j}u(t,z)-\gamma\partial_{z_{j}}u(t,z)\right)(2\pi)^{-N/2}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}}dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\alpha_{j}\mathbb{E}[(\partial_{z_{j}}u)(t,Z)]+\sum_{k=1}^{N}d_{k}\mathbb{E}\left[(\partial_{z_{j}}\partial_{z_{k}}u)(t,Z)\right]

\displaystyle+c_{j}\mathbb{E}[u(t,Z)]-\gamma\mathbb{E}[(\partial_{z_{j}}u)(t,Z)].

We can now plug the last expression in (
2.10
) to get

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[(\partial_{z_{j}}u)(t,Z)\right]\xi_{j}(x_{1})=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[(\partial_{z_{j}}\partial_{t}u)(t,Z)\right]\xi_{j}(x_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}\mathbb{E}[(\partial_{z_{j}}u)(t,Z)]\xi_{j}(x_{1})+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}d_{k}\mathbb{E}\left[(\partial_{z_{j}}\partial_{z_{k}}u)(t,Z)\right]\xi_{j}(x_{1})

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}\mathbb{E}[u(t,Z)]\xi_{j}(x_{1})-\gamma\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[(\partial_{z_{j}}u)(t,Z)]\xi_{j}(x_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}\mathbb{E}[(\partial_{z_{j}}u)(t,Z)]\xi_{j}(x_{1})+2\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{d}(y)\Pi_{N}^{\otimes 2}\rho_{2}(t,x_{1},y)dy

\displaystyle+\Pi_{N}\lambda_{c}(x_{1})\rho_{0}(t)-\gamma\Pi_{N}\rho_{1}(t,x_{1}).

This corresponds to equation (
2.2
) for
n=1
with the prescriptions (
2.9
).

Equation for

We start as before with

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\partial_{t}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[(\partial_{z_{j_{2}}}\partial_{z_{j_{1}}}u)(t,Z)\right]\xi_{j_{1}}(x_{1})\xi_{j_{2}}(x_{2})\right)\\
&=\quad\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[(\partial_{z_{j_{2}}}\partial_{z_{j_{1}}}\partial_{t}u)(t,Z)\right]\xi_{j_{1}}(x_{1})\xi_{j_{2}}(x_{2}).\end{split}

(2.12)

Now,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(\partial_{z_{j_{2}}}\partial_{z_{j_{1}}}\partial_{t}u)(t,Z)\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\partial_{z_{j_{2}}}\partial_{z_{j_{1}}}\partial_{t}u(t,z)(2\pi)^{-\frac{N}{2}}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}}dz

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\partial_{z_{j_{2}}}\partial_{z_{j_{1}}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{k}\partial^{2}_{z_{k}}u(t,z)+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(d_{k}-c_{k}-\alpha_{k}z_{k}\right)\partial_{z_{k}}u(t,z)\right)(2\pi)^{-\frac{N}{2}}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}}dz

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\partial_{z_{j_{2}}}\partial_{z_{j_{1}}}\left(\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}z_{k}-\gamma\right)u(t,z)\right)(2\pi)^{-\frac{N}{2}}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}}dz

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\partial_{z_{j_{2}}}\partial_{z_{j_{1}}}\partial^{2}_{z_{k}}u(t,z)(2\pi)^{-\frac{N}{2}}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}}dz

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(-\alpha_{j_{1}}\partial_{z_{j_{2}}}\partial_{z_{j_{1}}}u(t,z)-\alpha_{j_{2}}\partial_{z_{j_{1}}}\partial_{z_{j_{2}}}u(t,z)\right)(2\pi)^{-\frac{N}{2}}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}}dz

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(c_{j_{1}}\partial_{z_{j_{2}}}u(t,z)+c_{j_{2}}\partial_{z_{j_{1}}}u(t,z)+\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}z_{k}-\gamma\right)\partial_{z_{j_{2}}}\partial_{z_{j_{1}}}u(t,z)\right)(2\pi)^{-\frac{N}{2}}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}}dz.

in

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\partial_{z_{j_{2}}}\partial_{z_{j_{1}}}\partial^{2}_{z_{k}}u(t,z)(2\pi)^{-\frac{N}{2}}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}}dz

3.1
Wiener-Itô chaos expansion

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{B},\mathbb{P})
be the classical Wiener space over the interval
[0,1]
, i.e.
\Omega
is the space of continuous functions defined on the interval
[0,1]
and null at zero,
\mathcal{B}
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
\Omega
induced by the supremum norm and
\mathbb{P}
the Wiener measure on
(\Omega,\mathcal{B})
. We denote by

\displaystyle\begin{split}B_{x}&:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\\
&\quad\omega\mapsto B_{x}(\omega):=\omega(x),\quad x\in[0,1],\end{split}

the coordinate process which by construction is a one dimensional Brownian motion under
\mathbb{P}
. According to the Wiener-Itô chaos expansion theorem, any random variable
\Phi
in
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)
can be uniquely represented as

\displaystyle\Phi=\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}(h_{n}),

(3.1)

where

•

h_{0}:=\mathbb{E}[\Phi]
;

•

for
n\geq 1
,
h_{n}\in L_{s}^{2}([0,1]^{n})
, the space of square integrable symmetric functions;

•

I_{0}(h_{0}):=h_{0}=\mathbb{E}[\Phi]
;

•

for
n\geq 1
,
I_{n}(h_{n})
stands for the
n
-th order multiple Itô integral defined as

\displaystyle I_{n}(h_{n}):=n!\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{x_{1}}\cdot\cdot\cdot\int_{0}^{x_{n-1}}h_{n}(x_{1},...,x_{n})dB_{x_{n}}\cdot\cdot\cdot dB_{x_{2}}dB_{x_{1}}.

The series in (
3.1
) provides an orthogonal decomposition of
\Phi
that converges in
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)
; in fact, multiple Itô integrals possess the following general properties:

•

for all
n\geq 1
,
\mathbb{E}[I_{n}(h_{n})]=0
;

•

if
n\neq m
, then
\mathbb{E}[I_{n}(h_{n})I_{m}(h_{m})]=0
;

•

for
n\geq 1
,
\mathbb{E}[I_{n}(h_{n})^{2}]=n!|h_{n}|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,1]^{n})}
.

From the last two identities we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Phi^{2}]=\sum_{n\geq 0}n!|h_{n}|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,1]^{n})},

and, for
\Psi\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)
with

\displaystyle\Psi=\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}(g_{n}),

(3.2)

that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Phi\Psi]=\sum_{n\geq 0}n!\langle h_{n},g_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1]^{n})}.

(3.3)

Two notable subsets of
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)
are

\displaystyle\mathtt{F}:=\left\{\sum_{n=0}^{M}I_{n}(h_{n}),\mbox{ for some $M\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}$, $h_{0}\in\mathbb{R}$ and $h_{n}\in L_{s}^{2}([0,1]^{n})$, $n=1,...,M$}\right\},

which collects the random variables with a finite order chaos expansion, and

\displaystyle\mathtt{E}:=\left\{\mathcal{E}(f):=\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}\left(\frac{f^{\otimes n}}{n!}\right),\mbox{ for some $f\in L^{2}([0,1])$}\right\},

which is the family of the so-called
stochastic exponentials
. It is well known that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(f)=\exp\left\{I_{1}(f)-\frac{1}{2}|f|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,1])}\right\}

and that
\mathtt{F}
and the linear span of
\mathtt{E}
are both dense in
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)
. In particular,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Phi Z]=\mathbb{E}[\Psi Z],\quad\mbox{for all $Z\in\mathtt{F}$},

or

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Phi\mathcal{E}(f)]=\mathbb{E}[\Psi\mathcal{E}(f)],\quad\mbox{for all $f\in L^{2}([0,1])$ (or some dense subset of $L^{2}([0,1])$)},

implies
\Phi=\Psi
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.. Note also that, according to (
3.3
), we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Phi\mathcal{E}(f)]=\sum_{n\geq 0}\langle h_{n},f^{\otimes n}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1]^{n})},

whenever
\Phi=\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}(h_{n})
. We recall in addition that, by virtue of the
Hu-Meyer formula

\displaystyle I_{n}(h_{n})\cdot I_{m}(h_{m})=\sum_{r=0}^{n\wedge n}r!{n\choose r}{m\choose r}I_{n+m-2r}(h_{n}\hat{\otimes}_{r}h_{m}),

(3.4)

the linear space
\mathtt{F}
is an algebra with respect to the point-wise multiplication. Here,
h_{n}\otimes_{r}h_{m}
stands for the
r
-th order contraction
of
h_{n}
and
h_{m}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\begin{split}&(h_{n}\otimes_{r}h_{m})(x_{1},....,x_{n+m-2r})\\
&:=\int_{[0,1]^{r}}h_{n}(x_{1},...,x_{n-r},y_{1},...,y_{r})h_{m}(y_{1},...,y_{r},x_{n-r+1},...,x_{n+m-2r})dy_{1}\cdot\cdot\cdot dy_{r},\end{split}

(3.5)

while
h_{n}\hat{\otimes}_{r}h_{m}
denotes the
symmetrization
of
h_{n}\otimes_{r}h_{m}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\begin{split}&(h_{n}\hat{\otimes}_{r}h_{m})(x_{1},....,x_{n+m-2r})\\
&:=\frac{1}{(n+m-2r)!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n+m-2r}}(h_{n}\otimes_{r}h_{m})(x_{\sigma(1)},...,x_{\sigma(n+m-2r)}),\end{split}

(3.6)

with
S_{n+m-2r}
being the group of permutations on
\{1,...,n+m-2r\}
.

3.2
Malliavin derivative

The
Malliavin derivative
of
\Phi=\sum_{n=0}^{M}I_{n}(h_{n})\in\mathtt{F}
, denoted
\{D_{x}\Phi\}_{x\in[0,1]}
, is the element of
L^{2}([0,1];\mathtt{F})
defined by

\displaystyle D_{x}\Phi:=\sum_{n=0}^{M-1}(n+1)I_{n}(h_{n+1}(\cdot,x)),\quad x\in[0,1].

For
l\in L^{2}([0,1])
and
\Phi=\sum_{n=0}^{M}I_{n}(h_{n})\in\mathtt{F}
, we also write

\displaystyle\begin{split}D_{l}\Phi:=\langle D\Phi,l\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1])}&=\sum_{n=0}^{M-1}(n+1)I_{n}\left(\int_{0}^{1}h_{n+1}(\cdot,y)l(y)dy\right)\\
&=\sum_{n=0}^{M-1}(n+1)I_{n}\left(h_{n+1}\otimes_{1}l\right)\end{split}

(3.7)

for the
directional Malliavin derivative
of
\Phi
along
l
(in the last member above we utilized the notation (
3.5
)). We remark that
D_{l}\Phi
is also a member of
\mathtt{F}
.

If we now take
l\in L^{2}([0,1])
,
\Phi=\sum_{n=0}^{M}I_{n}(h_{n})\in\mathtt{F}
and
\Psi=\sum_{n=0}^{K}I_{n}(g_{n})\in\mathtt{F}
, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[D_{l}\Phi\cdot\Psi]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{(M-1)\wedge K}n!(n+1)\langle h_{n+1}\otimes_{1}l,g_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1]^{n})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{(M-1)\wedge K}(n+1)!\langle h_{n+1},l\otimes g_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1]^{n+1})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{(M-1)\wedge K}(n+1)!\langle h_{n+1},l\hat{\otimes}g_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1]^{n+1})}

(3.8)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{M\wedge(K+1)}n!\langle h_{n},l\hat{\otimes}g_{n-1}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1]^{n})}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\Phi\cdot D_{l}^{\star}\Psi]

,

where

\displaystyle D^{\star}_{l}\Psi:=\sum_{n=1}^{K+1}I_{n}(l\hat{\otimes}g_{n-1})

and

\displaystyle(l\hat{\otimes}g_{n-1})(x_{1},...,x_{n}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})g_{n-1}(x_{1},...,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},...,x_{n})

(compare with definitions (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) for
r=0
). We remark that in (
3.2
) we utilized the symmetry of
h_{n}
and the fact that the symmetrization operator is idempotent and self-adjoint in
L^{2}([0,1]^{n})
.

It is clear that
D^{\star}_{l}\Psi
also belongs to
\mathtt{F}
; moreover,
\mathbb{E}[D^{\star}_{l}\Psi]=0
, for all
l\in L^{2}([0,1])
and
\Psi\in\mathtt{F}
.

If in the Hu-Meyer formula (
3.4
) we take
m=1
and set
g_{1}=l\in L^{2}([0,1])
, we get

\displaystyle I_{n}(h_{n})\cdot I_{1}(l)

\displaystyle=I_{n+1}(h_{n}\hat{\otimes}l)+I_{n-1}(h_{n}\otimes_{1}l)

\displaystyle=D_{l}^{\star}I_{n}(h_{n})+D_{l}I_{n}(h_{n}).

Summing over
n
and using the linearity of the operators
D^{\star}_{l}
and
D_{l}
, we deduce the identity

\displaystyle D_{l}^{\star}\Psi+D_{l}\Psi=\Psi\cdot I_{1}(l),

(3.9)

which is valid for
\Psi\in\mathtt{F}
and
l\in L^{2}([0,1])
. One can also introduce the adjoint of
D_{x}
, denoted
\delta
: if
\Phi(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{M}I_{n}(h_{n}(\cdot;x))
is a stochastic process in
\mathtt{F}
, then

\displaystyle\delta(\Phi(\cdot)):=\sum_{n=0}^{M}I_{n+1}(\tilde{h}_{n})\in\mathtt{F},

where
\tilde{h}_{n}
stands for the symmetrization of
h_{n}
with respect to the
n+1
variables
x_{1},...,x_{n},x
.

It is useful to mention that the definition of Malliavin derivative can also be extended to the members of the family
\mathtt{E}
: for any
f,l\in L^{2}([0,1])
, we have

\displaystyle D_{x}\mathcal{E}(f)=f(x)\mathcal{E}(f),x\in[0,1]\quad\mbox{ and }\quad D_{l}\mathcal{E}(f)=\langle f,l\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1])}\mathcal{E}(f).

(3.10)

3.3
Second quantization operators

Let
A:L^{2}([0,1])\to L^{2}([0,1])
be a bounded linear operator; for
\Phi=\sum_{n=0}^{M}I_{n}(h_{n})\in\mathtt{F}
we define the
second quantization operator
of
A
as

\displaystyle\Gamma(A)\Phi:=\sum_{n=0}^{M}I_{n}\left(A^{\otimes n}h_{n}\right),

and the
differential second quantization operator
of
A
as

\displaystyle d\Gamma(A)\Phi:=\sum_{n=1}^{M}I_{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}h_{n}\right),

where
A_{i}
stands for the operator
A
acting on the
i
-th variable of
h_{n}
. The boundedness of
A
implies that both
\Gamma(A)\Phi
and
d\Gamma(A)\Psi
also belong to
\mathtt{F}
; note in addition that for
A
being the identity, we recover from
d\Gamma(A)
the well known
number operator
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\Phi=\sum_{n=1}^{M}nI_{n}\left(h_{n}\right).

Via a simple verification on can see that for all
\Phi
and
\Psi
in
\mathtt{F}
the following identities hold true:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}[\Gamma(A)\Phi]=\mathbb{E}[\Phi];\quad\mathbb{E}[d\Gamma(A)\Phi]=0;\\
&\mathbb{E}[\Gamma(A)\Phi\cdot\Psi]=\mathbb{E}[\Phi\cdot\Gamma(A^{\star})\Psi];\quad\mathbb{E}[d\Gamma(A)\Phi\cdot\Psi]=\mathbb{E}[\Phi\cdot d\Gamma(A^{\star})\Psi].\end{split}

Here,
A^{\star}
denotes the adjoint of
A
in
L^{2}([0,1])
. As for the Malliavin derivative, the actions of second quantization and differential second quantization operators can be extended to the class
\mathtt{E}
of stochastic exponentials:

\displaystyle\Gamma(A)\mathcal{E}(f)=\mathcal{E}(Af)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad d\Gamma(A)\mathcal{E}(f)=D^{\star}_{Af}\mathcal{E}(f).

The differential second quantization operator can also be represented as a composition of the Malliavin derivative and its adjoint; more precisely,

\displaystyle d\Gamma(A)\Phi=\delta\left(AD_{\cdot}\Phi\right).

(3.11)

3.4
Space of generalized random variables

We remark that the convergence of the series in (
3.3
) is implied via the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality by the conditions

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}n!|h_{n}|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,1]^{n})}<+\infty\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\sum_{n\geq 0}n!|g_{n}|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,1]^{n})}<+\infty.

(3.12)

However, if
\Psi
has a finite order expansion, i.e.

\displaystyle\Psi=\sum_{n=0}^{M}I_{n}(g_{n}),\mbox{ for some $M\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}$},

(3.13)

then one can drop the first condition in (
3.12
) and get a still well-defined pairing between the
generalized
random variable
\Phi
, represented by the formal series
\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}(h_{n})
(which in general will not convergence in
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)
), and the
regular
or
test
random variable
\Psi
with finite order expansion (
3.13
). Let

\displaystyle\mathtt{F}^{\star}:=\left\{\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}(h_{n}),\mbox{ for some $h_{0}\in\mathbb{R}$ and $h_{n}\in L_{s}^{2}([0,1]^{n})$, $n\geq 1$}\right\}

be a family of
generalized
random variables. The action of
T=\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}(h_{n})\in\mathtt{F}^{\star}
on
\varphi=\sum_{n=0}^{M}I_{n}(g_{n})\in\mathtt{F}
is defined as

\displaystyle\langle\langle T,\varphi\rangle\rangle:=\sum_{n=0}^{M}n!\langle h_{n},g_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1]^{n})}.

By construction, we have the inclusions

\displaystyle\mathtt{F}\subset\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)\subset\mathtt{F}^{\star}

with

\displaystyle\langle\langle T,\varphi\rangle\rangle=\mathbb{E}[T\varphi],

whenever
T\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)
. We will say that
T=U
in
\mathtt{F}^{\star}
if

\displaystyle\langle\langle T,\varphi\rangle\rangle=\langle\langle U,\varphi\rangle\rangle,\quad\mbox{for all }\varphi\in\mathtt{F}.

The
generalized expectation
of
T=\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}(h_{n})\in\mathtt{F}^{\star}
is
\mathbb{E}[T]:=\langle\langle T,1\rangle\rangle=h_{0}
. It is also important to observe that, according to the Hu-Meyer formula (
3.4
), the vector space
\mathtt{F}
is closed with respect to the point-wise product between random variables. Therefore, if
T\in\mathtt{F}^{\star}
and
\psi\in\mathtt{F}
, the product
T\cdot\psi
is well defined and corresponds to the element of
\mathtt{F}^{\star}
given by the prescription

\displaystyle\langle\langle T\cdot\psi,\varphi\rangle\rangle:=\langle\langle T,\psi\cdot\varphi\rangle\rangle,\quad\varphi\in\mathtt{F}.

The definitions of Malliavin derivative, its adjoint and (differential) second quantization operators can be lifted from
\mathtt{F}
to
\mathtt{F}^{\star}
by duality:

\displaystyle\langle\langle D_{l}T,\varphi\rangle\rangle:=\langle\langle T,D^{\star}_{l}\varphi\rangle\rangle,\quad\langle\langle D^{\star}_{l}T,\varphi\rangle\rangle:=\langle\langle T,D_{l}\varphi\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle\langle\langle\Gamma(A)T,\varphi\rangle\rangle:=\langle\langle T,\Gamma(A^{\star})\varphi\rangle\rangle,\quad\langle\langle d\Gamma(A)T,\varphi\rangle\rangle:=\langle\langle T,d\Gamma(A^{\star})\varphi\rangle\rangle.

Lastly, we recall a generalized version of the so-called Stroock-Taylor formula: if
T=\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}(h_{n})\in\mathtt{F}^{\star}
, then

\displaystyle h_{n}(x_{1},...,x_{n})=\frac{1}{n!}\mathbb{E}[D_{x_{1},...,x_{n}}T],\quad(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in[0,1]^{n}.

(3.14)

Here,
\mathbb{E}[D_{x_{1},...,x_{n}}T]
stands for the the generalized expectation of the
n
-th order Malliavin derivative of
T
.

Remark 3.1
.

The space
\mathtt{F}^{\star}
has been already utilized for solving some stochastic partial differential equations which admits only generalized solutions. See for instance the paper
[
9
]
where the authors investigate an unbiased version of the stochastic Navier-Stokes equations.

4
Proof of Theorem
1.5

In this section we present the rigorous derivation of formula (
1.9
)-(
1.10
). Firstly, we devote our attention to the original system, i.e. the one without projection operators
\Pi_{N}^{\otimes n}
, that we report here for easiness of reference:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\rho_{0}(t)=&\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{d}(y)\rho_{1}(t,y)dy-\gamma\rho_{0}(t);\\
\partial_{t}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})=&-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{A}_{i}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})\\
&+(n+1)\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{d}(y)\rho_{n+1}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n},y)dy\\
&+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{c}(x_{i})\rho_{n-1}(t,x_{1},...,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},...,x_{n})\\
&-\gamma\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n}),\end{split}

(4.1)

with initial and boundary conditions

\displaystyle\begin{split}\rho_{0}(0)&=0;\\
\rho_{1}(0,x_{1})&=\zeta(x_{1}),\quad x_{1}\in[0,1];\\
\rho_{n}(0,x_{1},...,x_{n})&=0,\quad n>1,(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in[0,1]^{n};\\
\partial_{\nu}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})&=0,\quad n\geq 1,t\geq 0,(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in\partial[0,1]^{n}.\end{split}

(4.2)

The first fundamental step of our analysis consists in integrating all the spatial variables of
\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})
with respect to the one dimensional Brownian motion
\{B_{x}\}_{x\in[0,1]}
; this procedure will produce a sequence of time-dependent multiple Itô integrals which satisfies a stochastic counterpart of equation (
4.1
)-(
4.2
).

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be a classical solution to (
4.1
)-(
4.2
). Then, the sequence of random variables
\{I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\}_{n\geq 0}
satisfies the equations

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))=&d\Gamma(-\mathcal{A})I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))+D_{\lambda_{d}}I_{n+1}\left(\rho_{n+1}(t,\cdot)\right)\\
&+D^{\star}_{\lambda_{c}}I_{n-1}\left(\rho_{n-1}(t,\cdot)\right)-\gamma I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot)),\quad t>0,n\geq 0;\\
I_{1}(\rho_{1}(0,\cdot))=&I_{1}(\zeta);\\
I_{n}(\rho_{n}(0,\cdot))=&0,\mbox{ for all $n\neq 1$},\end{split}

(4.3)

with probability one. Here, we agree on setting
I_{-1}(\cdot)\equiv 0
.

Proof.

Using the first order contraction, see (
3.5
), and symmetrized tensor product, see (
3.6
), we can reformulate (
4.1
) as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\rho_{0}(t)=&\lambda_{d}\otimes_{1}\rho_{1}(t,\cdot)-\gamma\rho_{0}(t);\\
\partial_{t}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})=&-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{A}_{i}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})+(n+1)(\lambda_{d}\otimes_{1}\rho_{n+1}(t,\cdot))(x_{1},...,x_{n})\\
&+(\lambda_{c}\hat{\otimes}\rho(t,\cdot))(x_{1},...,x_{n})-\gamma\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n}).\end{split}

(4.4)

The continuity and symmetry of the functions
\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})
,
\partial_{t}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{A}_{i}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})
entail their membership to
L^{2}_{s}([0,1]^{n})
; this allows us to perform, for any
n\geq 1
, an
n
-th order multiple Itô integral on both sides of the equation (
4.4
) to get

\displaystyle\begin{split}I_{n}(\partial_{t}\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))=&-I_{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{A}_{i}\rho_{n}(t,\cdot)\right)+(n+1)I_{n}\left(\lambda_{d}\otimes_{1}\rho_{n+1}(t,\cdot)\right)\\
&+I_{n}\left(\lambda_{c}\hat{\otimes}\rho_{n-1}(t,\cdot)\right)-\gamma I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot)),\end{split}

or equivalently,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))=&d\Gamma(-\mathcal{A})I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))+D_{\lambda_{d}}I_{n+1}\left(\rho_{n+1}(t,\cdot)\right)\\
&+D^{\star}_{\lambda_{c}}I_{n-1}\left(\rho_{n-1}(t,\cdot)\right)-\gamma I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot)).\end{split}

The last identity holds for all
n\geq 1
,
t>0
,
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely. The initial conditions in (
4.3
) are readily checked.
∎

Our next step is to construct a generalized stochastic process
\{\Phi(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
out of the sequence
\{I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\}_{n\geq 0}
in the spirit of the Wiener-Itô chaos expansion.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be a classical solution to (
4.1
)-(
4.2
). Then, the stochastic process

\displaystyle\Phi(t):=\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot)),\quad t\geq 0,

(4.5)

belongs to
\mathtt{F}^{\star}
and solves the differential equation

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\Phi(t)=&d\Gamma(-\mathcal{A})\Phi(t)+D_{\lambda_{d}}\Phi(t)+D^{\star}_{\lambda_{c}}\Phi(t)-\gamma\Phi(t),\quad t>0,\\
\Phi(0)=&I_{1}(\zeta).\end{split}

(4.6)

Proof.

The continuity of
\rho_{n}(t,\cdot)
together with its partial derivatives up to the second order ensure that the dual pairing
\langle\langle\Phi(t),\varphi\rangle\rangle
between
\Phi(t)
from (
4.5
) and any element
\varphi=\sum_{n=0}^{M}I_{n}(h_{n})
of
\mathtt{F}

\mathtt{F}^{\star}
, for all
t\geq 0

\{\Phi(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
solves (
4.6

4.3
) over
n\geq 0
with the convention that
I_{-1}(\cdot):=0
(recall also that
D_{\lambda_{d}}I_{0}(\cdot)=0
): this will lead to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Phi(t)=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))=\sum_{n\geq 0}\partial_{t}I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}d\Gamma(-\mathcal{A})I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))+\sum_{n\geq 0}D_{\lambda_{d}}I_{n+1}\left(\rho_{n+1}(t,\cdot)\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{n\geq 0}D^{\star}_{\lambda_{c}}I_{n-1}\left(\rho_{n-1}(t,\cdot)\right)-\gamma\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\Gamma(-\mathcal{A})\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))+D_{\lambda_{d}}\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n+1}\left(\rho_{n+1}(t,\cdot)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\Gamma(-\mathcal{A})\Phi(t)+D_{\lambda_{d}}\Phi(t)+D^{\star}_{\lambda_{c}}\Phi(t)-\gamma\Phi(t).

Observe that the smoothness of the functions
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}

,

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be a classical solution to (
4.1
)-(

the sequence of random variables
\{I_{n}(\Pi_{N}^{\otimes n}\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\}_{n\geq 0}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}I_{n}(\Pi_{N}^{\otimes n}\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))=&d\Gamma(-\mathcal{A})I_{n}(\Pi_{N}^{\otimes n}\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))+D_{\lambda_{d}}I_{n+1}\left(\Pi_{N}^{\otimes(n+1)}\rho_{n+1}(t,\cdot)\right)\\

(4.7)

with probability one.

Proof.

We know from Proposition
4.1
that the sequence of random variables
\{I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\}_{n\geq 0}
satisfies equation (
4.3

\displaystyle I_{n}(\Pi_{N}^{\otimes n}\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))=\Gamma(\Pi_{N})I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot)).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}I_{n}(\Pi_{N}^{\otimes n}\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))=&\partial_{t}\Gamma(\Pi_{N})I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\\
=&\Gamma(\Pi_{N})\partial_{t}I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\\
=&\Gamma(\Pi_{N})\left[d\Gamma(-\mathcal{A})I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))+D_{\lambda_{d}}I_{n+1}\left(\rho_{n+1}(t,\cdot)\right)\right]\\
&+\Gamma(\Pi_{N})\left[D^{\star}_{\lambda_{c}}I_{n-1}\left(\rho_{n-1}(t,\cdot)\right)-\gamma I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\right]\\
=&\Gamma(\Pi_{N})d\Gamma(-\mathcal{A})I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))+\Gamma(\Pi_{N})D_{\lambda_{d}}I_{n+1}\left(\rho_{n+1}(t,\cdot)\right)\\
&+\Gamma(\Pi_{N})D^{\star}_{\lambda_{c}}I_{n-1}\left(\rho_{n-1}(t,\cdot)\right)-\gamma\Gamma(\Pi_{N})I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot)).\end{split}

(4.8)

We now have to investigate the commutation relations of
\Gamma(\Pi_{N})
with
d\Gamma(-\mathcal{A})
,
D_{\lambda_{d}}
and
D^{\star}_{\lambda_{c}}
. Let
h\in C^{2}([0,1])
; then,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[\Gamma(\Pi_{N})d\Gamma(-\mathcal{A})I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\cdot\mathcal{E}(h)]&=\mathbb{E}[d\Gamma(-\mathcal{A})I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\cdot\mathcal{E}(\Pi_{N}h)]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\cdot d\Gamma(-\mathcal{A})\mathcal{E}(\Pi_{N}h)]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\cdot D^{\star}_{-\mathcal{A}\Pi_{N}h}\mathcal{E}(\Pi_{N}h)]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\cdot D^{\star}_{-\Pi_{N}\mathcal{A}h}\mathcal{E}(\Pi_{N}h)].\end{split}

(4.9)

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[d\Gamma(-\mathcal{A})\Gamma(\Pi_{N})I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\cdot\mathcal{E}(h)]&=\mathbb{E}[\Gamma(\Pi_{N})I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\cdot d\Gamma(-\mathcal{A})\mathcal{E}(h)]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[\Gamma(\Pi_{N})I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\cdot D^{\star}_{-\mathcal{A}h}\mathcal{E}(h)]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\cdot\Gamma(\Pi_{N})D^{\star}_{-\mathcal{A}h}\mathcal{E}(h)]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\cdot D^{\star}_{-\Pi_{N}\mathcal{A}h}\mathcal{E}(\Pi_{N}h)].\end{split}

(4.10)

Comparing the first and last members of (
4.9
) and (
4.10
) we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Gamma(\Pi_{N})d\Gamma(-\mathcal{A})I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\cdot\mathcal{E}(h)]=\mathbb{E}[d\Gamma(-\mathcal{A})\Gamma(\Pi_{N})I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\cdot\mathcal{E}(h)],

for all
h\in C^{2}([0,1])
and hence that

\displaystyle\Gamma(\Pi_{N})d\Gamma(-\mathcal{A})I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))=d\Gamma(-\mathcal{A})\Gamma(\Pi_{N})I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot)),\quad\mathbb{P}-\mbox{a.s}.

(4.11)

We now consider the commutation between
\Gamma(\Pi_{N})
and
D_{\lambda_{d}}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Gamma(\Pi_{N})D_{\lambda_{d}}I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\cdot\mathcal{E}(h)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[D_{\lambda_{d}}I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\cdot\mathcal{E}(\Pi_{N}h)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[nI_{n-1}(\lambda_{d}\otimes_{1}\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\cdot\mathcal{E}(\Pi_{N}h)]

\displaystyle=n\langle\lambda_{d}\otimes_{1}\rho_{n}(t,\cdot),(\Pi_{N}h)^{\otimes{n-1}}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1]^{n-1})}

\displaystyle=n\langle\lambda_{d}\otimes_{1}\Pi_{N}^{\otimes(n-1)}\rho_{n}(t,\cdot),h^{\otimes{n-1}}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1]^{n-1})},

while

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[D_{\lambda_{d}}\Gamma(\Pi_{N})I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\cdot\mathcal{E}(h)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[D_{\lambda_{d}}I_{n}(\Pi_{N}^{\otimes n}\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\cdot\mathcal{E}(h)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[nI_{n-1}(\lambda_{d}\otimes_{1}\Pi_{N}^{\otimes n}\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\cdot\mathcal{E}(h)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[nI_{n-1}(\Pi_{N}\lambda_{d}\otimes_{1}\Pi_{N}^{\otimes(n-1)}\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\cdot\mathcal{E}(h)]

\displaystyle=n\langle\Pi_{N}\lambda_{d}\otimes_{1}\Pi_{N}^{\otimes(n-1)}\rho_{n}(t,\cdot),h^{\otimes{n-1}}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1]^{n-1})}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\Gamma(\Pi_{N})D_{\lambda_{d}}I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))=D_{\lambda_{d}}\Gamma(\Pi_{N})I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot)),

(4.12)

if
\lambda_{d}=\Pi_{N}\lambda_{d}
; but this is the case since we are assuming
N\geq N_{0}
(recall Assumption
1.3
). Lastly, we investigate the commutation between
\Gamma(\Pi_{N})
and
D^{\star}_{\lambda_{c}}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Gamma(\Pi_{N})D^{\star}_{\lambda_{c}}I_{n-1}\left(\rho_{n-1}(t,\cdot)\right)\cdot\mathcal{E}(h)]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[D^{\star}_{\lambda_{c}}I_{n-1}\left(\rho_{n-1}(t,\cdot)\right)\cdot\mathcal{E}(\Pi_{N}h)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[I_{n-1}\left(\rho_{n-1}(t,\cdot)\right)\cdot D_{\lambda_{c}}\mathcal{E}(\Pi_{N}h)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[I_{n-1}\left(\rho_{n-1}(t,\cdot)\right)\cdot\langle\lambda_{c},\Pi_{N}h\rangle\mathcal{E}(\Pi_{N}h)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[I_{n-1}\left(\rho_{n-1}(t,\cdot)\right)\cdot\langle\Pi_{N}\lambda_{c},h\rangle\mathcal{E}(\Pi_{N}h)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Gamma(\Pi_{N})I_{n-1}\left(\rho_{n-1}(t,\cdot)\right)\cdot\langle\Pi_{N}\lambda_{c},h\rangle\mathcal{E}(h)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[D^{\star}_{\Pi_{N}\lambda_{c}}\Gamma(\Pi_{N})I_{n-1}\left(\rho_{n-1}(t,\cdot)\right)\cdot\mathcal{E}(h)];

comparing the first and last members we can conclude that

\displaystyle\Gamma(\Pi_{N})D^{\star}_{\lambda_{c}}I_{n-1}\left(\rho_{n-1}(t,\cdot)\right)=D^{\star}_{\Pi_{N}\lambda_{c}}\Gamma(\Pi_{N})I_{n-1}\left(\rho_{n-1}(t,\cdot)\right),

(4.13)

almost surely. Now, using (
4.11
)-(
4.12
)-(
4.13
) in (
4.8
) we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}I_{n}(\Pi_{N}^{\otimes n}\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))=

\displaystyle\Gamma(\Pi_{N})d\Gamma(-\mathcal{A})I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))+\Gamma(\Pi_{N})D_{\lambda_{d}}I_{n+1}\left(\rho_{n+1}(t,\cdot)\right)

\displaystyle+\Gamma(\Pi_{N})D^{\star}_{\lambda_{c}}I_{n-1}\left(\rho_{n-1}(t,\cdot)\right)-\gamma\Gamma(\Pi_{N})I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\Gamma(-\mathcal{A})I_{n}(\Pi_{N}^{\otimes n}\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))+D_{\lambda_{d}}I_{n+1}\left(\Pi_{N}^{\otimes(n+1)}\rho_{n+1}(t,\cdot)\right)

\displaystyle+D^{\star}_{\Pi_{N}\lambda_{c}}I_{n-1}\left(\Pi_{N}^{\otimes(n-1)}\rho_{n-1}(t,\cdot)\right)-\gamma I_{n}(\Pi_{N}^{\otimes n}\rho_{n}(t,\cdot)),

which corresponds to (
4.7
).
∎

The last step of our construction consists in rewriting equations (
4.7
) from a standard partial differential equation’s perspective.

Proposition 4.5
.

For any
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle I_{n}(\Pi_{N}^{\otimes n}\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))=\varphi_{n,N}\left(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\right),

(4.14)

where
\varphi_{n,N}:[0,+\infty[\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is a polynomial of degree
n
in the variables
I_{1}(\xi_{1})
,…,
I_{1}(\xi_{N})
. With this representation, equations (
4.7
) read

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\partial_{t}\varphi_{n,N})\left(t,z\right)=&\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{k}\partial^{2}_{z_{k}}\varphi_{n,N}\left(t,z\right)-\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{k}z_{k}\partial_{z_{k}}\varphi_{n,N}\left(t,z\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{N}d_{k}\partial_{z_{k}}\varphi_{n+1,N}\left(t,z\right)\\
&+\varphi_{n-1,N}\left(t,z\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}z_{k}\right)-\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\partial_{z_{k}}\varphi_{n-1,N}\left(t,z\right)-\gamma\varphi_{n,N}(t,z),\end{split}

(4.15)

for
n\geq 1
,
t\geq 0
and
z=(z_{1},...,z_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Here we set
\varphi_{-1,N}\equiv 0
.

Proof.

We start observing (see for instance
[
6
]
) that

\displaystyle I_{n}(\Pi_{N}^{\otimes n}\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))=\Gamma(\Pi_{N})I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))=\mathbb{E}[I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))|\sigma(I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N}))],

where the right hand side above stands for the conditional expectation of the random variable
I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))
with respect to the sigma-algebra generated by the random variables
\{I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\}
. It is also well known from the theory of multiple Itô integrals that
I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))
can be written as an infinite linear combination of polynomials of degree
n
in the variables
I_{1}(\xi_{1}),I_{1}(\xi_{2}),...
; the action of the conditional expectation above reduce that linear combination to a finite number of terms in the variables
\{I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\}
. This proves identity (
4.14
). Let us now derive equation (
4.15
); according to formula (
3.11
) we have

\displaystyle d\Gamma(-\mathcal{A})I_{n}(\Pi_{N}^{\otimes n}\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))=

\displaystyle d\Gamma(-\mathcal{A})\varphi_{n,N}\left(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\delta\left(\mathcal{A}D_{\cdot}\varphi_{n,N}\left(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\delta\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\partial_{z_{k}}\varphi_{n,N})\left(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\right)\mathcal{A}\xi_{k}(\cdot)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{k}\delta\left((\partial_{z_{k}}\varphi_{n,N})\left(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\right)\xi_{k}(\cdot)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{k}\delta(\xi_{k})\cdot(\partial_{z_{k}}\varphi_{n,N})\left(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{k}\partial^{2}_{z_{k}}\varphi_{n,N}\left(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\right);

moreover, using the chain rule for Malliavin derivatives we get

\displaystyle D_{\lambda_{d}}I_{n+1}\left(\Pi_{N}^{\otimes(n+1)}\rho_{n+1}(t,\cdot)\right)=

\displaystyle D_{\lambda_{d}}\varphi_{n+1,N}\left(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\partial_{z_{k}}\varphi_{n+1,N})\left(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\right)\langle\lambda_{d},\xi_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1])}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}d_{k}(\partial_{z_{k}}\varphi_{n+1,N})\left(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\right),

and

\displaystyle D^{\star}_{\Pi_{N}\lambda_{c}}I_{n-1}\left(\Pi_{N}^{\otimes(n-1)}\rho_{n-1}(t,\cdot)\right)=

\displaystyle D^{\star}_{\Pi_{N}\lambda_{c}}\varphi_{n-1,N}\left(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi_{n-1,N}\left(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\right)\cdot I_{1}(\Pi_{N}\lambda_{c})

\displaystyle-D_{\Pi_{N}\lambda_{c}}\varphi_{n-1,N}\left(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi_{n-1,N}\left(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}I_{1}(\xi_{k})\right)

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\partial_{z_{k}}\varphi_{n-1,N})\left(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\right)c_{k}.

Note that in the second equality above we made use of identity (
3.9
). Combining all the previous identities we can rewrite equations (
4.7
) as

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}\varphi_{n,N})\left(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\right)=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{k}\partial^{2}_{z_{k}}\varphi_{n,N}\left(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\right)

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{k}\delta(\xi_{k})\cdot(\partial_{z_{k}}\varphi_{n,N})\left(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{N}d_{k}(\partial_{z_{k}}\varphi_{n+1,N})\left(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\right)

\displaystyle+\varphi_{n-1,N}\left(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}I_{1}(\xi_{k})\right)

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\partial_{z_{k}}\varphi_{n-1,N})\left(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\right)c_{k}

\displaystyle-\gamma\varphi_{n,N}\left(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\right).

This corresponds to (
4.15
) upon replacing
I_{1}(\xi_{k})
with
z_{k}
, for
k=1,...,N
.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.5
. Let

\displaystyle u(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})):=\sum_{n\geq 0}\varphi_{n,N}\left(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\right).

(4.16)

According to the last proposition, summing over
n\geq 0
equations (
4.15
) we see that the function
u
in (
4.16
) solves

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}u(t,z)&=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{k}\partial^{2}_{z_{k}}u(t,z)+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(d_{k}-c_{k}-\alpha_{k}z_{k}\right)\partial_{z_{k}}u(t,z)+\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}z_{k}-\gamma\right)u(t,z)\\
u(0,z)&=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\zeta_{k}z_{k}.\end{split}

On the other hand, by construction

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\varphi_{n,N}\left(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})\right)=\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}\left(\Pi_{N}^{\otimes n}\rho_{n}(t,\cdot)\right);

this gives

\displaystyle u(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N}))=\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}\left(\Pi_{N}^{\otimes n}\rho_{n}(t,\cdot)\right).

Thus, the kernels in the Wiener-Itô chaos expansion of
u(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N}))
, which in general can be represented via the Stroock-Taylor formula (
3.14
), coincide with the sequence
\{\Pi_{N}^{\otimes n}\rho_{n}(t,\cdot)\}_{n\geq 0}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\Pi_{N}^{\otimes n}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\mathbb{E}\left[D_{x_{1},...,x_{n}}u(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N}))\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{j_{1},...j_{n}=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\partial_{z_{j_{1}}}\cdot\cdot\cdot\partial_{z_{j_{n}}}u\right)(t,Z)\right]\xi_{j_{1}}(x_{1})\cdot\cdot\cdot\xi_{j_{n}}(x_{n}),

where

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\partial_{z_{j_{1}}}\cdot\cdot\cdot\partial_{z_{j_{n}}}u\right)(t,Z)\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\partial_{z_{j_{1}}}\cdot\cdot\cdot\partial_{z_{j_{n}}}u\right)(t,z)(2\pi)^{-N/2}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}}dz.

This completes the proof of our main result (recall that the orthonormality of the functions
\xi_{1},...,\xi_{N}
implies that
I_{1}(\xi_{1}),...,I_{1}(\xi_{N})
are independent standard Gaussian random variables).

Remark 4.6
.

Following the previous construction, we could formally associate equation (
4.6
) with

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}u(t,z)&=\sum_{k\geq 1}\alpha_{k}\partial^{2}_{z_{k}}u(t,z)+\sum_{k\geq 1}\left(d_{k}-c_{k}-\alpha_{k}z_{k}\right)\partial_{z_{k}}u(t,z)+\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}z_{k}-\gamma\right)u(t,z)\\
u(0,z)&=\zeta(z),\end{split}

(4.17)

where now
u(t,z)=u(t,z_{1},z_{2},....)
is a function of infinitely many variables, and obtain the identity

\displaystyle\Phi(t)=u\left(t,I_{1}(\xi_{1}),I_{1}(\xi_{2}),...\right).

From this point of view, the use of the projection operator
\Pi_{N}
is needed for reducing (
4.17
) to a standard partial differential equation with a finite number of spatial variables.
