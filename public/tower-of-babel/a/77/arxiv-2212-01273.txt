On the non-existence of sympathetic Lie algebras with dimension less than 25
A. L. García-Pulido †, G. Salgado ‡

Abstract
. In this article we investigate the question of the lowest possible dimension that a sympathetic Lie algebra 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} can attain, when its Levi subalgebra 𝔤 L \mathfrak{g}_{L} is simple. We establish the structure of the nilradical of a perfect Lie algebra 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} , as a 𝔤 L \mathfrak{g}_{L} -module, and determine the possible Lie algebra structures that one such 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} admits. We prove that, as a 𝔤 L \mathfrak{g}_{L} -module, the nilradical must decompose into at least 4 4 simple modules. We explicitly calculate the semisimple derivations of a perfect Lie algebra 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} with Levi subalgebra 𝔤 L = 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 2 ​ ( ℂ ) \mathfrak{g}_{L}=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C}) and give necessary conditions for 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} to be a sympathetic Lie algebra in terms of these semisimple derivations. We show that there is no sympathetic Lie algebra of dimension lower than 15 15 , independently of the nilradical’s decomposition. If the nilradical has 4 4 simple modules, we show that a sympathetic Lie algebra has dimension greater or equal than 25 25 .

1.
Introduction

A Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is
sympathetic
if its centre is trivial, if
\mathfrak{g}
is perfect and if
\mathfrak{g}

has no outer derivations; that is,

(1)

Z(\mathfrak{g})=0
,

(2)

\mathfrak{g}=[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]

(3)

\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{g})=\operatorname{ad}(\mathfrak{g})
.

It is well known that any semisimple Lie algebra satisfies properties (
1
),
(
2
) and (
3
). Sympathetic Lie algebras were introduced to determine if properties (
1
), (
2
) and (
3
) completely
characterise semisimple Lie algebras.
In
[
1
]
the first non semisimple Lie algebra satisfying the above properties appears; it has dimension 35. This was followed by an example in dimension 48
[
2
]
,
where the term
sympathetic
was first used. The lowest known dimension of a sympathetic
Lie algebra is 25 and can be found in
[
3
]
. This algebra has much smaller dimension that the previous examples due to the careful construction with Levi subalgebra equal to
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, which is the lowest dimensional simple Lie algebra.

In
[
3
]
and
[
2
]
, Benayadi develops theory describing structural properties of sympathetic Lie algebras.
However, the existing results do not lead to a classification, or even showing existence,
of these algebras in low dimensions. Indeed, the non-existence of sympathetic Lie algebras
of dimensions less than 10 is attributed to J. Simon in
[
3
]
and it remains an open
problem to determine if 25 is the smallest dimension possible in a sympathetic Lie algebra.
The lack of progress could be due to two factors. Little was known of the classification of
Lie algebras at that time and the available computational resources and power were very limited.

In this paper we give a solution to the problem of dimension minimality of sympathetic
Lie algebras
which are not semisimple. To do this, we restrict our attention to the class of algebras
whose Levi factor is a simple Lie algebra.

By taking full advantage of the representation theory of simple Lie algebras, we find
very precise necessary conditions that a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{L}\ltimes\operatorname{Rad}(\mathfrak{g})
must satisfy
in order to be sympathetic.
In
3.10
,
3.12
, we show that if
\mathfrak{g}

is perfect and
\operatorname{Rad}(\mathfrak{g})
decomposes into 1, 2 or 3 simple
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-modules, then
\mathfrak{g}
is not sympathetic.
Then
\operatorname{Rad}(\mathfrak{g})
must decompose into at least
4
simple
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-modules.

This condition completely characterises sympathetic Lie algebras where
\operatorname{Rad}(\mathfrak{g})

decomposes into 4 representations. In
3.14
we show that, up to isomorphism, there are only
6
possible algebra structures. We are therefore able
to determine that the smallest possible dimension such a sympathetic Lie algebra can have
is 25, see
3.32
. Moreover, we show there is only one such algebra in dimension 25 and it is the example given in
[
3
]
. The proof of the non-existence in dimensions smaller than 10
follows easily from our results. These results are independent of the choice of simple Levi subalgebra.

We now describe the techniques used to prove our main theorems.
We make systematic use of each of the properties that define a sympathetic Lie algebra

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{L}\ltimes\operatorname{Rad}(\mathfrak{g})
to derive necessary conditions on its algebra structure.
Recall that
\operatorname{Rad}(\mathfrak{g})
admits a
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-module structure induced by the Lie bracket and
therefore can be decomposed into a direct sum of simple
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-modules.

A crucial point of our argument is to use the fact that the product between any
two simple
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-modules
V,W
is completely determined by bilinear

\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-equivariant morphisms
V\otimes W\to U
, where
U
is one of the simple
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-modules
appearing in the decomposition of
\operatorname{Rad}(\mathfrak{g})
.
In other words, the multiplication is completely determined by the spaces

\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{g}_{L}}(V\otimes W,U)
, where
V,W,U
are simple
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-modules appearing in the
decomposition of
\operatorname{Rad}(\mathfrak{g})
.
In addition, the nilpotency of
\operatorname{Rad}(\mathfrak{g})
enables us to derive a general form of the
multiplicative structure of
\operatorname{Rad}(\mathfrak{g})
. This process further highlights how, when working
under the assumption that
\mathfrak{g}
is sympathetic, one would construct such an algebra.

Combining the above multiplicative structure and Clebsh-Gordan-type theorems for simple
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-modules, we obtain explicit forms of the possible multiplication tables in terms of the number of simple
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-modules in the decomposition of
\operatorname{Rad}(\mathfrak{g})
.
The final step is to verify the (non) existence of sympathetic Lie algebras with this explicit multiplication tables in the case of at most 4 modules.

The outline of the paper is as follows. For completeness, we begin Section 2 with preliminary notions of classical Lie algebras. In Section 3, we prove our main theorem
3.1
: if
\mathfrak{g}
is a sympathetic Lie algebra such that its Levi subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
is simple and its nilradical decomposes into four simple
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-modules, then
\operatorname{dim}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathfrak{g})\geq 25
. From this point, we work with Lie algebras which Levi subalgebra is simple. In
Section

3.1
, we show that the nilradical of any sympathetic Lie algebra decomposes into at least four simple modules and determine the possible multiplicative structures for this case
3.14
. In
Section

3.2
and
Section

3.3
we investigate whether any of these possible algebras are sympathetic Lie algebras.
For part of this analysis, we designed algorithms that enable us to do explicit calculations to this end. In Section 4, we include these algorithms and show in a concrete example how to put to practice our theoretical results and use our algorithms. An implementation of our algorithms can be found in
https://github.com/winsy17/Sympathetic_Lie_Algebras
.

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout this article we only consider finite dimensional vector spaces over
\mathbb{C}
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
Lie algebra

\mathfrak{g}
, is a vector space together with a bilinear map
[,]:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
satisfying:

(1)

[x,y]=-[y,x]
for every
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}
,

(2)

[x,[y,z]]+[y,[z,x]]+[z,[x,y]]=0
for every
x,y,z\in\mathfrak{g}
.

Equation (
2
) is known as the
Jacobi identity
. Notice that (
1
)
is equivalent to
[x,x]=0
for every
x\in\mathfrak{g}
.

One of the typical examples of a Lie algebra is the following. Consider a vector space
V
and the
associative algebra of endomorphims of
V
denoted by
\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)
. Define the
commutator
on

\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)
, by

[T,S]:=T\circ S-S\circ T

for every
T,S\in\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)
. The resulting Lie algebra is denoted by
\mathfrak{gl}(V)
and called the

general linear algebra associated to
V
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{h}
be two Lie algebras. A Lie algebra
homomorphism
, is a linear transformation
\rho\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{h}
such that

\rho([x,y]_{\mathfrak{g}})=[\rho(x),\rho(y)]_{\mathfrak{h}}
.
An
isomorphism
of Lie algebras is a bijective homomorphism.
A
linear representation
of a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
on a vector space
V
is a Lie algebra homomorphism

\rho\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(V)
.

Let
\alpha\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(U)
and
\beta\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(W)
be two linear representations of
\mathfrak{g}
on
U
and
W
, respectively. The space of
equivariant homomorphisms
from
U
to
W
is

\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{g}}(U,W):=\{T\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(U,W)\ |\ T\circ\alpha(x)=\beta(x)\circ T,\ x\in\mathfrak{g}\}.

We can induce a new representation
\alpha\otimes\beta
from

\mathfrak{g}
on
U\otimes W
defined by

(\alpha\otimes\beta)(x)(u\otimes w):=\alpha(x)(u)\otimes w+u\otimes\beta(x)(w)

for every
x\in\mathfrak{g}
,
u\in U
and
w\in W
. This representation is the
tensor product
of
\alpha

and
\beta
.

Given any Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
the Lie bracket induces a natural linear representation

\operatorname{ad}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})
defined by
\operatorname{ad}_{x}(y):=[x,y]
for any
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}
. We call this representation
the
adjoint representation
. Notice that

\operatorname{Ker}(\operatorname{ad})=Z(\mathfrak{g})=\{x\in\mathfrak{g}\mid[x,y]=0,\forall y\in\mathfrak{g}\}
.

A
derivation
on
\mathfrak{g}
is a linear transformation
D\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
which satisfies the Leibniz rule
with respect to the Lie bracket,

D([x,y])=[Dx,y]+[x,Dy],

for every
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a Lie algebra. The following statements are equivalent.

(1)

D
is a derivation in
\mathfrak{g}
,

(2)

D([e_{i},e_{j}])=[De_{i},e_{j}]+[e_{i},De_{j}]
for every
e_{i},e_{j}\in\beta
,

(3)

[D,\operatorname{ad}(e_{i})]-\operatorname{ad}(De_{i})=0
for every
e_{i}\in\beta
.

Where
\beta=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
is a basis of
\mathfrak{g}
.

Remark 2.3
.

Item

3
shows that obtaining a basis for the vector space

\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{g})=\{D\in\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{g})\mid D([x,y])=[Dx,y]+[x,Dy]\}
is equivalent to finding the solutions of a homogeneous system of linear equations.

Given a linear representation of
\mathfrak{g}
on
V

\rho:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(V)
, we say that a subspace

W\subset V
is
invariant
if
\rho(x)W\subset W
for any
x\in\mathfrak{g}
. The trivial subspaces
\{0\}

and
V
are invariant subspaces.
We call a representation
\rho\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(V)

irreducible
if the only invariant
subspaces of
V
are the trivial ones.
We say that the representation is
completely reducible
if every invariant subspace

W
admits an invariant complement
U
such that

V=W\oplus U
.

From now on, we say that
V
is a
\mathfrak{g}
-
module
if there exists a linear representation
\rho:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(V)
.
We will say that the
\mathfrak{g}
-module
V
is
simple
if
\rho
is an irreducible representation and,

semisimple
, if
\rho
is completely reducible.

The very well known classification of simple Lie algebras over
\mathbb{C}
is given as follows.

(See
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
)
.

If
\mathfrak{g}
is a finite dimensional simple Lie algebra over
\mathbb{C}
, then
\mathfrak{g}
is isomorphic to one of the following Lie algebras.

(1)

A_{n}:=\mathfrak{sl}(n+1)
,
n\geq 1
,

(2)

B_{n}:=\mathfrak{so}(2n+1)
,
n\geq 2
,

(3)

C_{n}:=\mathfrak{sp}(2n)
,
n\geq 3
,

(4)

D_{n}:=\mathfrak{so}(2n)
,
n\geq 4
,

(5)

E_{6}
,
E_{7}
,
E_{8}
,

(6)

F_{4}
,

(7)

G_{2}
.

The corresponding dimensions in each case are given by

(1)

\operatorname{dim}(\mathfrak{sl}(n+1))=n^{2}+2n
,

(2)

\operatorname{dim}(\mathfrak{so}(2n+1))=2n^{2}+n
,

(3)

\operatorname{dim}(\mathfrak{sp}(2n))=2n^{2}+n
,

(4)

\operatorname{dim}(\mathfrak{so}(2n))=2n^{2}-n
,

Theorem 2.5

(Weyl)
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a semisimple Lie algebra. Then,
any finitely dimensional linear representation of
\mathfrak{g}
is completely reducible.

Theorem 2.6

(Schur’s Lemma)
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a Lie algebra and let

\rho_{V}\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(V)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\rho_{W}\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(W)

be two irreducible representations. Let
T\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(V,W)
be such that
T\circ\rho_{V}=\rho_{W}\circ T
.

(1)

If
\rho_{V}\not\sim\rho_{W}
then
T=0
,

(2)

If
\rho_{V}=\rho_{W}
then
T=\lambda\operatorname{Id}_{V}
.

3.
Main Theorem

We divide this section in two parts. In the first part we assume that

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{L}\ltimes\operatorname{Rad}(\mathfrak{g})
is a Lie algebra such that its Levi subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
is simple and its radical
\operatorname{Rad}(\mathfrak{g})
is nilpotent. We show that, if
\mathfrak{g}
is sympathetic, the decomposition of
\operatorname{Rad}(\mathfrak{g})
into simple
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-modules must contain at least four non-trivial modules.

In the second part, we fix a simple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
and determine all the possible
multiplicative structures that a sympathetic Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
can have assuming that
\operatorname{Rad}(\mathfrak{g})

decomposes into four simple
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-modules. These possible Lie brackets significantly reduce
the candidate sympathetic Lie algebras. We verify if any of the remaining candidates is a sympathetic Lie algebra.

The main result of this work is the following.

Theorem 3.1

(Main Theorem)
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a sympathetic Lie algebra with simple Levi subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
, then
\operatorname{dim}_{\mathbb{C}}\mathfrak{g}\geq 25
.

From now on, we will only consider Lie algebras
\mathfrak{g}
with a simple Levi subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
and we will denote the radical
\operatorname{Rad}(\mathfrak{g})
by
\mathfrak{h}
.
By
2.5
,
\mathfrak{h}
can be decomposed, with respect to the
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-action on
\mathfrak{h}
induced by the Lie bracket, into a sum of simple
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-modules:

\mathfrak{h}=V_{n_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{n_{k}}.

Denote by
s(\mathfrak{g}):=k
to the number of simple
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-modules in the decomposition of
\mathfrak{h}
.
Throughout this work we denote by
V_{n_{i}}
to the irreducible representation of
\mathfrak{g}_{L}

with highest weight
n_{i}
.

Now assume that
\mathfrak{g}
is perfect. It is well known that if
\mathfrak{g}
is perfect then
\operatorname{Rad}(\mathfrak{g})
is nilpotent (see
[
6
]
(Chapter 3, Section 9 Corollary 2)) and therefore
\operatorname{Rad}(\mathfrak{g})=\operatorname{NilRad}(\mathfrak{g})
. Since
\mathfrak{h}
is a nilpotent subalgebra,
0\neq Z(\mathfrak{h})\subset\mathfrak{h}
and
Z(\mathfrak{h})
inherits a decomposition as a
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-submodule of
\mathfrak{h}
:

Z(\mathfrak{h})=V_{z_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{z_{r}},

where
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{r}\}\subseteq\{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{L}\ltimes\mathfrak{h}
be a Lie algebra with simple Levi subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
. Given the decomposition
\mathfrak{h}=V_{n_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{n_{k}}
into simple
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-modules, then

(1)

\operatorname{dim}(V_{n_{i}})=1
if, and only if,
[\mathfrak{g}_{L},V_{n_{i}}]=0
,

(2)

if
\operatorname{dim}(V_{n_{i}})>1
, then
[\mathfrak{g}_{L},V_{n_{i}}]=V_{n_{i}}
,

(3)

if
[V_{n_{i}},V_{n_{j}}]\neq 0
, then there exists a
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-module

V_{n_{p}}
,
1\leq p\leq k
, such that

V_{n_{p}}\subset V_{n_{i}}\otimes V_{n_{j}}
; and a unique
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-equivariant morphism

\Gamma_{i,j}^{p}\colon V_{n_{i}}\otimes V_{n_{j}}\to V_{n_{p}}
such that

\pi_{p}([V_{n_{i}},V_{n_{j}}])=\Gamma_{i,j}^{p}(V_{n_{i}},V_{n_{j}})
, where
\pi_{p}\colon V_{n_{i}}\otimes V_{n_{j}}\to V_{n_{p}}

is the natural projection to
V_{n_{p}}
. If
i=j
, then
V_{n_{p}}\subset\Lambda^{2}(V_{n_{i}})
and

\Gamma_{i,i}^{p}
is skew-symmetric.

Proof.

The proof of
Item

1
and
Item

2
is straightforward.

The fact that any triple
x\in V_{n_{i}}
,
y\in V_{n_{j}}
,
z\in\mathfrak{g}_{L}
satisfies the Jacobi identity is equivalent to the existence of a
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-equivariant morphism
[,]\colon V_{n_{i}}\otimes V_{n_{j}}\to\mathfrak{h}
.
As a
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-module, there is a decomposition

V_{n_{i}}\otimes V_{n_{j}}=V_{m_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{m_{l}}.

By Schur’s lemma,
\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{l}\}\cap\{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\}=\emptyset
implies
[V_{n_{i}},V_{n_{j}}]=0
.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{L}\ltimes\mathfrak{h}
be a Lie algebra with simple Levi subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
. Given the decomposition
\mathfrak{h}=V_{n_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{n_{k}}
into simple
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-modules, if
[V_{n_{i}},V_{n_{j}}]\neq 0
,
then there exist simple
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-modules
V_{m_{1}},\ldots,V_{m_{r}}\subset V_{n_{i}}\otimes V_{n_{j}}
and unique

\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-equivariant morphisms
\Gamma_{i,j}^{s}:V_{n_{i}}\otimes V_{n_{j}}\to V_{m_{s}}
such that

[V_{n_{i}},V_{n_{j}}]=V_{m_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{m_{r}},\quad\text{and}\quad[x,y]=\sum\limits_{s=1}^{r}\Gamma_{i,j}^{s}(x,y),

for every
x\in V_{n_{i}}
,
y\in V_{n_{j}}
.
In the case when
i=j
,
V_{m_{1}},\ldots,V_{m_{r}}\subset\Lambda^{2}(V_{n_{i}})
and we have a skew-symmetric
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-equivariant morphism
\Gamma_{i}^{s}:V_{n_{i}}\otimes V_{n_{i}}\to V_{m_{s}}
.

Remark 3.4
.

Given
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{L}\ltimes\mathfrak{h}
, the Lie algebra structure of
\mathfrak{h}
is uniquely determined by a
collection of
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-equivariant morphisms, when considering
\mathfrak{h}
as a
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-module.
Conversely, in order to construct a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}
from a
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-module,
one first needs to determine the possible equivariant morphisms which define an algebra structure on
\mathfrak{h}
. In fact, any scalar multiple of such morphisms defines a multiplicative structure
on
\mathfrak{h}
. Therefore,
\mathfrak{h}
is a Lie algebra when there exist an appropriate choice of multiples for which the Jacobi identity holds.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{L}\ltimes\mathfrak{h}
be a finite dimensional sympathetic Lie algebra with simple Levi subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
.
Suppose
D\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
is a derivation such that
D|_{\mathfrak{g}_{L}}=0
and

D|_{V_{n_{i}}}=\lambda_{n_{i}}\operatorname{Id}_{V_{n_{i}}}
for any simple
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
-module in the decomposition
of
\mathfrak{h}
. Then
D
is an inner derivation of
\mathfrak{g}
if, and only if,
D|_{\mathfrak{h}}=0
.

Proof.

Let
D
be an inner derivation. Write
D
as the sum of its semisimple and nilpotent parts
D=\operatorname{ad}_{\mathfrak{g}}(s+h)
for some
s\in\mathfrak{g}_{L}
and
h\in\mathfrak{h}
. By definition of
D
, for any
y\in\mathfrak{g}_{L}
,

0=D(y)=[s+h,y]=[s,y]+[h,y].

Since
[s,y]\in\mathfrak{g}_{L}
and
[h,y]\in\mathfrak{h}
, this implies
[s,y]=0
and
[h,y]=0
for every
y\in\mathfrak{g}_{L}
.
Thus
s\in Z(\mathfrak{g}_{L})
and hence
s=0
because
\mathfrak{g}_{L}
is a simple Lie algebra.
Consequently,
D
is nilpotent and this implies
\lambda_{n_{i}}=0
for every
i
.
The converse is straightforward.

∎

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\ltimes\mathfrak{h}
be a finite dimensional Lie algebra and
V_{m},V_{p},V_{k}
simple
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
-modules in the decomposition of
\mathfrak{h}
such that
[V_{m},V_{p}]_{\mathfrak{g}}=V_{k}
, or

[V_{p},V_{p}]_{\mathfrak{g}}=V_{k}
. For any semisimple derivation
D\in\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{g})
there exist
\lambda,\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{F}
such that

\displaystyle\alpha+\beta-\gamma

\displaystyle=\lambda(m+p-k),\quad\text{or}

\displaystyle 2\beta-\gamma

\displaystyle=\lambda(2p-k).

Proof.

Since
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
is a subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
, there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}
such that

D|_{\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})}=\lambda\operatorname{ad}_{\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})}(H).

Suppose that
V_{m}=\langle e_{i}\rangle
,
V_{p}=\langle f_{i}\rangle
and

V_{k}=\langle g_{i}\rangle
, where the basis for
V_{m},V_{p}
and
V_{k}
are as in
3.19
Since
D
is a semisimple derivation, we assume without loss of generality that
D
is of the form

D(e_{i})=\alpha_{i}e_{i},\quad D(f_{i})=\beta_{i}f_{i},\quad D(g_{i})=\gamma_{i}g_{i}.

Given any element
e_{i}\in V_{m}
, we have

D([E,e_{i}])=\alpha_{i-1}[E,e_{i}],

and

\displaystyle\hbox{}[DE,e_{i}]+[E,De_{i}]

\displaystyle=2\lambda[E,e_{i}]+\alpha_{i}[E,e_{i}]

\displaystyle=(2\lambda+\alpha_{i})[E,e_{i}].

Using the fact that
D
is a derivation we obtain

\alpha_{i-1}=2\lambda+\alpha_{i}.

Equivalently,

[D|_{V_{m}}]=\operatorname{Diag}(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{0}-2\lambda,\cdots,\alpha_{0}-m(2\lambda)).

4.3.
Algorithm to calculate
\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{g})

The input for this algorithm is the set of adjoint matrices of the basis of
\mathfrak{g}
and its output is a matrix containing a basis of
\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{g})
.

Let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
be a basis for
\mathfrak{g}
and
\operatorname{ad}_{e_{i}}
the associated adjoint to
e_{i}
. A linear map
D\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
is a derivation if and only if
D=(d_{r,s})_{1\leq r,s\leq n}
satisfies

\displaystyle D\circ\operatorname{ad}_{e_{i}}(e_{j})

\displaystyle=[D(e_{i}),e_{j}]+\operatorname{ad}_{e_{i}}\circ D(e_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}d_{k,i}[e_{k},e_{j}]+\operatorname{ad}_{e_{i}}\circ D(e_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}d_{k,i}\operatorname{ad}_{e_{k}}(e_{j})+\operatorname{ad}_{e_{i}}\circ D(e_{j})

for every
1\leq i,j\leq n
.
This identity is equivalent to equality of linear operators

D\circ\operatorname{ad}_{e_{i}}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}d_{k,i}\operatorname{ad}_{e_{k}}+\operatorname{ad}_{e_{i}}\circ D,

for all
1\leq i\leq n
. Using the adjoint matrices as an input, the above identities allow us to define a system of linear equations, where
D
is the unknown. We then use the isomorphism
\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}}
to obtain a basis for the space of solutions of
D
. That is, we obtain a set of generators of the vector space
\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{g})
and as a consequence, its dimension.

4.4.
Algorithm to verify if a vector space is a Lie algebra.

Given candidate structure constants as input, this algorithm verifies if these are the structure constants of a Lie algebra. That is, it checks the Jacobi identity.

Using candidate structure constants
[e_{i},e_{j}]=\sum_{k}{c^{i}_{jk}}e_{k}
for
1\leq i<j\leq n
, we first construct associated adjoint matrices
\operatorname{ad}_{e_{i}}
using the antisymmetry of a Lie bracket.
In our code we verify if the identity

\operatorname{ad}_{[e_{i},e_{j}]}=\operatorname{ad}_{e_{i}}\circ\operatorname{ad}_{e_{j}}-\operatorname{ad}_{e_{j}}\circ\operatorname{ad}_{e_{i}}

holds for every
1\leq i<j\leq n
.
