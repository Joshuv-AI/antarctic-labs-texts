Superradiant Production of Heavy Dark Matter from Primordial Black Holes
and Yong Xu

Abstract
Rotating black holes (BHs) can efficiently transfer energy to the surrounding environment via superradiance. In particular, when the Compton length of a particle is comparable to the gravitational radius of a BH, the particle’s occupation number can be exponentially amplified. In this work, we investigate the effect of the primordial-black-hole (PBH) superradiant instabilities on the generation of heavy bosonic dark matter (DM) with mass above ∼ \sim 1 TeV. Additionally, we analyze its interplay with other purely gravitational and therefore unavoidable DM production mechanisms such as Hawking emission and the ultraviolet freeze-in. We find that superradiance can significantly increase the DM density produced by PBHs with respect to the case that only considers Hawking emission, and hence lower initial PBH densities are required.

Introduction

Superradiance is a radiation enhancement phenomena, ubiquitous in many physical systems. In general relativity, a spinning black hole (BH) can feature superradiant instabilities, allowing efficient transfer of energy and angular momentum to the surrounding environment (for a review, see e.g. Ref.
[
1
]
).
In particular, the frequency of a wave that scatters with a Kerr BH can be enhanced if it is smaller than the BH angular velocity
[
2
]
, similar to the Penrose process in the ergoregion of a Kerr BH
[
3
]
.
Additionally, Press and Teukolsky proposed the BH bomb Gedankenexperiment
[
4

: if one surrounds the BH with a reflecting mirror, the frequency can be further amplified when the wave bounces back and forth.
In reality, the gravitational interaction with the massive field that scatters can naturally act as a mirror
[
5

For boson fields, the occupation number can be exponentially enhanced because of superradiant instabilities, leading to the formation of gravitational clouds around BHs, which could leave observable astrophysical signals. For example, these clouds could source gravitational waves (GWs) via

direct emission due to a dipole or quadrupolar configuration,

Primordial Black Holes

Different mechanisms could lead to the formation of PBHs in the early Universe
[
32
,
50
]
.
For example, if above a threshold, density fluctuations can collapse into a BH when reentering the Hubble horizon
[
51
]
.
Other scenarios for PBH formation include the collapse of cosmic string loops, the collapse of domain walls, or the collision of bubbles
[
52

Here, we remain agnostic about the formation mechanism, and assume that an initial PBH density was formed in a radiation-dominated era.
For a plasma temperature

(2.1)

with numerical efficiency factor
\kappa\simeq 0.2

and

denote the radiation energy density and the Hubble rate, respectively.
Moreover, we adopt a monochromatic mass spectrum such that all PBHs were generated with same mass.

\beta\equiv\frac{\rho_{\text{BH}}(T_{\text{in}})}{\rho_{R}(T_{\text{in}})}\,.

(2.2)

A phase dominated by PBH with

and corresponds to the SM temperature at which the PBHs have completely evaporated. Here
g_{\star}(T)
represents the number of relativistic degrees of freedom contributing to the SM radiation energy density.
Note that if
\beta>\beta_{c}

\rho_{R}(T_{\text{eq}})=\rho_{\text{BH}}(T_{\text{eq}})
occurred.
Such a radiation-PBH equality temperature is given by

T_{\text{eq}}=\beta\,T_{\text{in}}\left(\frac{g_{\star s}(T_{\text{in}})}{g_{\star s}(T_{\text{eq}})}\right)^{1/3},

(2.5)

where
g_{\star s}(T)
is the number of relativistic degrees of freedom that contribute to the SM entropy density.

If such evaporation occurred during or after BBN, the large particle injection could spoil the standard prediction of the abundance of light elements.
To avoid this, we consider PBHs that have fully evaporated before the onset of BBN, that is,
T_{\text{ev}}>T_{\text{BBN}}\simeq 4

as implied by Eq. (

On the other hand, one can obtain a lower limit on

taking into account the upper bound on inflationary scale

H_{I}\leq 2.5\times 10^{-5}~M_{P}

[

Since superradiant amplification takes place only for such BHs, we will focus on Kerr PBHs in what follows.

Superradiance and Hawking Radiation

In this section, we first have a quick overview of Hawking radiation, then focus on the particle number growth by BHs due superradiant instabilities. After that we put things together, and describe the interplay between superradiance and Hawking evaporation.

3.1
Hawking Radiation from Kerr Black Holes

PBHs could have been produced with a large initial angular momentum
[
26
,
27
,
25
,
28
,
29
,
30
,
31

.
For simplicity, we consider a monochromatic spin distribution, where all the PBHs had the same initial spin, without specifying the initial mechanism that generated it.
A Kerr BH is characterized by its instantaneous mass
M_{\text{BH}}
and dimensionless spin parameter
a_{\star}\equiv 8\pi\,J\,M_{P}^{2}/M_{\text{BH}}^{2}\in[0,1]
, with
J
denoting the total angular momentum of the BH.

The line element of the Kerr metric in Boyer-Lindquist coordinates is given by
[
8
]

ds^{2}=-dt^{2}+\frac{2\,r_{g}\,r}{\Sigma}\left(dt-a_{\star}\,r_{g}\,\sin^{2}\theta\,d\varphi\right)^{2}+\frac{\Sigma}{\Delta}\,dr^{2}+\Sigma\,d\theta^{2}+\left(r^{2}+a_{\star}^{2}\,r_{g}^{2}\right)\sin\theta^{2}\,d\varphi^{2},

(3.1)

where
t
denotes cosmic time and
(r,\,\theta,\,\varphi)
are spherical coordinates,
\Sigma\equiv r^{2}+a_{\star}^{2}\,r_{g}^{2}\,\cos^{2}\theta
, and
\Delta\equiv r^{2}-2\,r_{g}\,r+a_{\star}^{2}\,r_{g}^{2}
, with
r_{g}\equiv G\,M_{\text{BH}}
denoting the gravitational radius.
The event horizon
r_{+}
and the Cauchy horizon
r_{-}
correspond to the coordinate singularities
\Delta=0
, and are given by

r_{\pm}=r_{g}\left(1\pm\sqrt{1-a_{\star}^{2}}\right).

(3.2)

The region
r_{+}\leq r\leq r_{t}
, with

r_{t}\equiv r_{g}\,\left(1+\sqrt{1-a_{\star}^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}\right),

(3.3)

denotes the so-called ergosphere, in which particles can gain energy from spinning BHs, and corresponds to the surface where the argument of
dt
vanishes.
Meanwhile, the horizon temperature for spinning BHs is given by

T_{\text{BH}}=\frac{2M_{P}^{2}}{M_{\text{BH}}}\,\frac{\sqrt{1-a_{\star}^{2}}}{1+\sqrt{1-a_{\star}^{2}}}\,.

(3.4)

As mentioned above, during the Hawking evaporation process, all particles with masses smaller than
T_{\text{BH}}
can be generated. Within a time interval
[t,\,t+dt]
and a momentum interval
[p,\,p+dp]
, the production rate for particle species
i
with spin
s_{i}
, mass
\mu_{i}
and internal degrees of freedom
g_{i}
is
[
32
,
47
]

\frac{d^{2}\mathcal{N}_{i}}{dp\,dt}=\frac{g_{i}}{2\pi^{2}}\sum_{l=s_{i}}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}\frac{\sigma_{s_{i}}^{lm}\left(M_{\text{BH}},p,a_{\star}\right)}{\exp\left[\left(E_{i}-m\,\Omega\right)/T_{\text{BH}}\right]-(-1)^{2s_{i}}}\,\frac{p^{3}}{E_{i}}\,,

(3.5)

where
E_{i}(p)=\sqrt{p^{2}+\mu_{i}^{2}}
denotes the energy,
\sigma_{s_{i}}
describes the absorption cross-section, and
l
and
m
correspond to the total and magnetic quantum numbers, respectively.
Finally, in Eq. (
3.5
),
\Omega
is the BH horizon angular velocity, a crucial quantity for superradiance, which is given by

\Omega\equiv\frac{4\pi\,a_{\star}}{1+\sqrt{1-a_{\star}^{2}}}\,\frac{M_{P}^{2}}{M_{\text{BH}}}\,.

(3.6)

Summing over all possible particle species and integrating over the phase space the rate in Eq. (
3.5
), one has the following set of equations describing the time evolution of the BH mass and spin, without including the effect of superradiance
[
78
]

\displaystyle\frac{dM_{\text{BH}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\left(M_{\text{BH}},a_{\star}\right)\frac{M_{P}^{4}}{M_{\text{BH}}^{2}}\,,

(3.7a)

\displaystyle\frac{da_{\star}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-a_{\star}\left[\gamma\left(M_{\text{BH}},a_{\star}\right)-2\varepsilon\left(M_{\text{BH}},a_{\star}\right)\right]\frac{M_{P}^{4}}{M_{\text{BH}}^{3}}\,,

(3.7b)

where the evaporation functions
\varepsilon\left(M_{\text{BH}},a_{\star}\right)\equiv\sum_{i}g_{i}\,\varepsilon_{i}(z_{i})
and
\gamma\left(M_{\text{BH}}\right)\equiv\sum_{i}g_{i}\,\gamma_{i}(z_{i})
are given by the dimensionless quantities

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{i}\left(z_{i},a_{\star}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{27}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{z_{i}}^{\infty}\sum_{l,m}\frac{\psi_{s_{i}}^{lm}\left(x,a_{\star}\right)\left(x^{2}-z_{i}^{2}\right)}{\exp\left(x^{\prime}/2f\left(a_{\star}\right)\right)-(-1)^{2s_{i}}}\,xdx\,,

(3.8a)

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}\left(z_{i},a_{\star}\right)

\displaystyle=108\int_{z_{i}}^{\infty}\sum_{l,m}\frac{m\,\psi_{s_{i}}^{lm}\left(x,a_{\star}\right)\left(x^{2}-z_{i}^{2}\right)}{\exp\left(x^{\prime}/2f\left(a_{\star}\right)\right)-(-1)^{2s_{i}}}\,dx\,,

(3.8b)

with
z_{i}=\mu_{i}/T_{\text{BH}}
,
\psi_{s_{i}}(E,\mu)\equiv 64\pi M_{P}^{2}\sigma_{s_{i}}(E,\mu)/(27M_{\text{BH}}^{2})
,
x^{\prime}=x-m\,\Omega^{\prime}
,
x=M_{\text{BH}}\,E_{i}/M_{P}^{2}
,
\Omega^{\prime}=M_{\text{BH}}\,\Omega/M_{P}^{2}
, and
f(a_{\star})=\sqrt{1-a_{\star}}/(1+\sqrt{1-a_{\star}})
.
We refer the reader to Refs.

3.2
Superradiance in a Nutshell

When the Compton length
\lambda_{C}=1/m_{\text{dm}}
of a boson with mass
m_{\text{dm}}
is comparable to the BH gravitational radius, a superradiant instability can occur, leading to an enhancement of the particle occupation number and a formation of bound states.
Such bound states are generally referred to as
gravitational atoms
, characterized by a “fine-structure constant”
[
11
]

\alpha\equiv\frac{r_{g}}{\lambda_{C}}=G\,M_{\text{BH}}\,m_{\text{dm}}\simeq 0.38\left(\frac{M_{\text{BH}}}{10^{7}~{\rm g}}\right)\left(\frac{m_{\text{dm}}}{10^{7}~{\rm GeV}}\right).

(3.9)

Similarly to the hydrogen atom, the energy spectrum is controlled by three quantum numbers, namely the principal, orbital, and magnetic quantum numbers
(n,\,l,\,m)
, and at leading order is given by
[
11
,
34
]

\omega\simeq m_{\text{dm}}\left(1-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2n^{2}}\right).

(3.10)

Generically, in order to allow superradiance to occur, the energy or frequency
\omega
has to be smaller than the BH angular velocity. In other words, if the latter is smaller than the former, PBHs would be unable to further amplify
\omega
.
Let us now state the superradiance condition in a more mathematical way.
At leading order
\omega\sim m_{\text{dm}}
(cf. Eq. (
3.10
)), and
\Omega\sim(2\,r_{g})^{-1}
for
a_{\star}\simeq 1
(cf. Eq. (
3.6
)).
To allow superradiance to occur,
\omega<m\,\Omega
, which translates into

\alpha<\frac{1}{2}\,,

(3.11)

for
m=l=1
. As mentioned above, due to superradiance, the frequencies can be enhanced, which could also be understood as a growth of the particle occupation number. The growth rate is defined to be the imaginary part of the bound state frequency
\mathfrak{Im}(\omega)
.

where

represents the event horizon as defined in Eq. (

, and therefore the growth rate

For completeness, we next describe the numerical method that we have used to compute the growth rates, following Ref.
[

To determine the frequencies of the bound states, it is required to solve the Klein-Gordon equation on a Kerr background
[

After a standard variable separation, two power series solutions are proposed to solve the radial and angular equations, which lead to three-term recurrence relations between the different expansion coefficients
[
79
,
9
]

Implicit conditions for continued fractions, which are only satisfied by the bound-state frequencies, are obtained after performing algebraic manipulations.
Thus, one needs to employ numerical method to solve such implicit conditions and obtain the growth rate.
We use a
scipy
standard root finder that applies the
HYBR
method
[
80
]
to simultaneously solve the radial and angular implicit conditions.
We have checked that our code gives the same rates as those presented in Ref.
[

]

Contributions to the DM abundance coming from other

Superradiant instabilities exponentially amplify the number of DM particles.
Defining
N_{\text{dm}}^{\text{sr}}
to be the total number of DM particles gravitationally bounded to a PBH and considering for the moment only superradiance, the dynamics of the system is governed by the system of coupled equations
[

,
1
,
82
]

\displaystyle\frac{dN_{\text{dm}}^{\text{sr}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{\text{sr}}\,N_{\text{dm}}^{\text{sr}}\,,

(3.13a)

\displaystyle\frac{dM_{\text{BH}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-m_{\text{dm}}\,\frac{dN_{\text{dm}}^{\text{sr}}}{dt}\,,

(3.13b)

\displaystyle\frac{dJ}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{2}\,\frac{dN_{\text{dm}}^{\text{sr}}}{dt}\,.

(3.13c)

Apart from having
\alpha\sim 1
, superradiant exponential growth requires a nonvanishing initial population of gravitationally bounded particles.
However, it is interesting to realize that the exact number of initial particles has a small impact on its final number.
Noticing that

\frac{dN_{\text{dm}}^{\text{sr}}}{da_{\star}}=\frac{dN_{\text{dm}}^{\text{sr}}}{dt}\times\frac{dt}{da_{\star}}=\left[2\,\frac{m_{\text{dm}}}{M_{\text{BH}}}\,a_{\star}-8\pi\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{M_{P}}{M_{\text{BH}}}\right)^{2}\right]^{-1}\simeq-\frac{1}{8\pi\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{M_{\text{BH}}}{M_{P}}\right)^{2},

(3.14)

it can be seen that the final amount of DM particles
N_{\text{dm}}^{\text{sr}}(\text{end})
after the superradiant instability is

N_{\text{dm}}^{\text{sr}}(\text{end})\simeq N_{\text{dm}}^{\text{sr}}(\text{in})+1.5\times 10^{19}\left(\frac{M_{\text{BH}}}{10^{5}~\text{g}}\right)^{2}\left(a^{\text{ini}}_{\star}-a^{\text{end}}_{\star}\right)\simeq\mathcal{O}\left(10^{18}\right)\left(\frac{M_{\text{BH}}}{10^{5}~\text{g}}\right)^{2},

(3.15)

featuring a small additive dependence on the initial number of DM particles
N_{\text{dm}}^{\text{sr}}(\text{in})
.

3.3
Interplay of Superradiance and Hawking Radiation

As mentioned in Sect.
3.1
, on top of the contribution from superradiance, DM particles are also unavoidably sourced by Hawking evaporation. In this section we investigate the interplay between the two mechanisms. The evolution of the system is governed by the set of coupled equations:

\displaystyle\frac{dN_{\text{dm}}^{\text{sr}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{\text{sr}}\,N_{\text{dm}}^{\text{sr}}\,,

(3.16a)

\displaystyle\frac{dM_{\text{BH}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\,\frac{M_{P}^{4}}{M_{\text{BH}}^{2}}-m_{\text{dm}}\,\Gamma_{\text{sr}}\,N_{\text{dm}}^{\text{sr}}\,,

(3.16b)

\displaystyle\frac{da_{\star}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-a_{\star}\left[\gamma-2\varepsilon\right]\frac{M_{P}^{4}}{M_{\text{BH}}^{3}}-8\pi\left[\sqrt{2}-2\alpha\,a_{\star}\right]\Gamma_{\text{sr}}\,N_{\text{dm}}^{\text{sr}}\,\frac{M_{P}^{2}}{M_{\text{BH}}^{2}}\,,

(3.16c)

\displaystyle\frac{dn_{\text{dm}}}{dt}+3H\,n_{\text{dm}}

\displaystyle=n_{\text{BH}}\left[\Gamma_{\text{BH$\to$DM}}+\Gamma_{\text{sr}}\,N_{\text{dm}}^{\text{sr}}\right],

(3.16d)

with
n_{\text{dm}}
and
n_{\text{BH}}
denoting the DM and PBH number density, respectively. The first line describes the usual evolution of total bounded DM numbers due to superradiant instability. The second and third lines are a combination of Eqs. (
3.7
) and (
3.13
), and describe the evolution of PBH mass and spin. Finally, the last line denotes the modified Boltzmann equation for the DM number density with contributions from both the superradiant instability and the Hawking evaporation.
In the first term of the right-hand side (RHS),
\Gamma_{\text{BH$\to$DM}}
denotes the DM production rate from BH through evaporation, which can be obtained by integrating Eq. (
3.5
) over the phase space for a scalar DM field
[
47
]
.
In the second term,
\Gamma_{\text{sr}}\,N_{\text{dm}}^{\text{sr}}
corresponds to the DM production rate per BH. Note that we are assuming that after the evaporation of the PBHs, DM behaves as free particles.
This allows us to explore the maximum reach of superradiant DM production.
The details the fate of the DM clouds will be explored in a future work.
Finally, Eqs. (
3.16
) reduce to Eqs. (
3.7
) or (
3.13
) in the limit where superradiance or Hawking emission are artificially switched off.

To allow superradiance to take place, the PBH should not evaporate before the instability becomes significant.
Thus, we can compare the typical timescale for superradiance to the lifetime of PBHs.
The PBH lifetime is roughly
[
44
]

\tau=\frac{160}{\pi g_{\star}}\frac{M_{\text{in}}^{3}}{M_{P}^{4}}\,,

(3.17)

whereas the typical timescale for superradiance
t_{\text{sr}}
corresponds to the inverse of the superradiance rate in Eq. (
3.12
),

t_{\text{sr}}\sim\frac{1}{\Gamma_{\text{sr}}}\simeq\frac{24}{m_{\text{dm}}}\left(\frac{8\pi\,M_{P}^{2}}{M_{\text{in}}\,m_{\text{dm}}}\right)^{8}\frac{1}{a_{\star}}\,.

(3.18)

To develop superradiance,
t_{\text{sr}}<\tau
is required, so we can estimate a lower bound on the initial PBH mass, depending on the DM mass and initial PBH spin

M_{\text{in}}\gtrsim 70\left(\frac{1}{a_{\star}}\right)^{1/11}\left(\frac{10^{11}~\text{GeV}}{m_{\text{dm}}}\right)^{9/11}\text{g}\,.

(3.19)

However, we stress that Kerr BHs lose angular momentum much faster than their mass
[
78
]
, so they effectively become Schwarzschild BHs before completely evaporating.
As the BH should have enough angular momentum for the superradiant instability to rise, the estimate in Eq. (
3.19
) is conservative, since it does not consider the time when the BH would have shed their spin.
Still, we have verified that this characteristic of Kerr BHs only affects the estimated lower bound for light PBHs
M_{\text{in}}\lesssim 10
g.

4
Gravitational UV Freeze-in

Independently from the PBH evaporation, there is an irreducible DM production channel which is particularly efficient in the gray region in Fig.
3
(i.e. large values for the DM mass and the reheating temperature), and corresponds to the gravitational UV freeze-in
[
84
,
85
,
86
,
35
]
.
DM can be generated by 2-to-2 annihilations of SM particles, mediated by the exchange of massless gravitons in the
s
channel.
We emphasize that this gravitational production mechanism is unavoidable due to universal couplings between the metric and the energy-momentum tensor
\sim g_{\mu\nu}\,h^{\mu\nu}
.

The evolution of the DM number density
n
from gravitational freeze-in is given by the Boltzmann equation

\frac{dn}{dt}+3\,H\,n=\gamma\,,

(4.1)

where
\gamma
corresponds to the DM production rate density, given by
[
84
,
85
,
35
]

\gamma(T)=\delta\,\frac{T^{8}}{M_{P}^{4}}\,,

(4.2)

where
\delta\simeq 1.1\times 10^{-3}
for scalar DM.
Light DM particles (lighter than the reheating temperature) are mainly produced after the end of reheating. The DM yield from gravitational freeze-in
Y\equiv n(T)/s(T)
, with entropy density
s(T)=\frac{2\pi^{2}}{45}g_{\star s}T^{3}
, is obtained by integrating Eq. (
4.1
),

Y_{0}=\frac{45\,\delta}{2\pi^{3}g_{\star s}}\sqrt{\frac{10}{g_{\star}}}\left(\frac{T_{\text{rh}}}{M_{P}}\right)^{3},\qquad\text{ for }m_{\text{dm}}\ll T_{\text{rh}}\,.

(4.3)

It is interesting to note that away from the instantaneous decay approximation, the DM yield is typically only boosted by a small factor of order
\mathcal{O}(1)

[
87
,
88
,
89
]
.

Alternatively, in the case where DM is heavier than the reheating temperature (but still lighter than the highest temperature reached by the SM thermal bath
[
90
]
), it could not be produced after but during the reheating era.
Equation (
4.1
) therefore yields

Y_{0}=\frac{45\,\delta}{2\pi^{3}g_{\star s}}\sqrt{\frac{10}{g_{\star}}}\frac{T_{\text{rh}}^{7}}{M_{P}^{3}\,m_{\text{dm}}^{4}}\,,\qquad\text{ for }m_{\text{dm}}\gg T_{\text{rh}}\,.

(4.4)

We would like to stress that once produced by the UV freeze-in gravitational mechanism, the DM could suffer from a dilution due to the PBH evaporation, in the case where they manage to dominate the total energy density of the universe (i.e., if
\beta>\beta_{c}
).
The total entropy dilution is given by
[
67
]

\frac{S(T_{\text{in}})}{S(T_{\text{ev}})}\simeq\begin{cases}1&\text{ for }\beta<\beta_{c}\,,\\
\frac{T_{\text{ev}}}{T_{\text{eq}}}\simeq\left(\frac{g_{\star}}{640}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\left(\frac{M_{P}}{M_{\text{in}}}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\frac{M_{P}}{\beta\,T_{\text{in}}}&\text{ for }\beta>\beta_{c}\,,\end{cases}

(4.5)

with the entropy defined as
S(T)=a^{3}s(T)
.
To reproduce the observed DM relic density, one requires

m_{\text{dm}}\,Y_{0}\,\frac{S(T_{\text{in}})}{S(T_{\text{ev}})}=\Omega_{\text{dm}}h^{2}\,\frac{1}{s_{0}}\,\frac{\rho_{c}}{h^{2}}\simeq 4.3\times 10^{-10}~\text{GeV},

(4.6)

with
\rho_{c}\simeq 1.05\times 10^{-5}\,h^{2}
GeV/cm
3
being the critical energy density,
s_{0}\simeq 2.69\times 10^{3}
cm
-3
the entropy density at present
[
91
]
, and
\Omega_{\text{dm}}h^{2}\simeq 0.12
the observed DM relic abundance
[
92
]
.

The regions where the gravitational UV freeze-in is effective and can reproduce and even overshoot the whole observed DM abundance are overlaid in gray in Fig.
3
.
Here, two assumptions were made:
i)
as PBHs are assumed to be formed in a radiation-dominated universe,
T_{\text{rh}}\geq T_{\text{in}}
.
Here we take
T_{\text{rh}}\geq T_{\text{in}}
.
And
ii)
no entropy dilution was considered, which can be achieved if
\beta<\beta_{c}

6
Conclusions

When the Compton wave length of a particle is comparable to the size of a spinning black hole (BH), energy can be efficiently transferred to the particle, leading to an amplification of occupation number in the ergoregion. For astrophysical BHs with mass
M_{\text{BH}}\geq M_{\odot}
, it is possible to form clouds around BHs, which could give rise to some observational signatures; this has been utilized as a potential new avenue to constrain ultralight particles beyond the standard model of particle physics
[
1
]
.

In this work, complementary to the astrophysical BHs as widely discussed in literature, we pay particular attention to the phenomenology of the superradiant instability with
primordial black holes
(PBHs) lighter than
\sim 10^{9}
g. In particular, we investigate the impact of superradiance on the generation of
heavy
(
m_{\text{dm}}\gtrsim 1
TeV)
dark matter
(DM) particles in the early Universe. To this end we minimally set up a framework with the inevitable scenario where DM is generated pure gravitationally via either UV freeze-in or Hawking emission. Due to the exponential enhancement effect, we find that in the regime where superradiance manifests, the required BH abundance
\beta
to yield the correct DM relics is reduced.

5
summarizes our main conclusions, where we show the parameter space that reproduces the whole observed DM abundance, taking into account all the gravitational production channels described previously (Hawking radiation, superradiance and gravitational freeze-in). The thickness of the bands brackets all possible values of the PBH spin:
0\leq a_{\star}<1
.
The color code is the same used before, i.e.
m_{\text{dm}}=10^{3}
GeV (orange),
10^{6}
GeV (black),
10^{9}
GeV (blue),
10^{12}
GeV (red) and
10^{15}
GeV (green).
We find that for a close-to-maximally rotating PBHs (
a_{\star}=0.999
) the relic abundance is increased by a factor of
\sim 20
, when the gravitational coupling between the DM and the PBH is
\sim{\cal O}(1)
.
For lower values of
a_{\star}
, the superradiant effect decreases and the observed relic abundance can be explained by purely Hawking evaporation.

Massive Scalar Fields around Kerr Black Holes

We revisit the solution of a massive scalar
\phi
in the vicinity of a Kerr BH and further present the approximation of the superradiance rate
\Gamma_{\text{sr}}
. We closely follow Ref.
[
34
]
where
G=\hbar=c=1
is used, however we will restore the units at the end. The Klein-Gordon equation for a massive scalar
\phi
with mass
\mu
in a Kerr metric, defined in Eq. (
3.1
), is given by

\left(g_{\mu\nu}\nabla^{\mu}\nabla^{\nu}-\mu^{2}\right)\phi=0\,.

(A.1)

Separating variables in a standard manner,

\phi(t,r,\theta,\varphi)=\sum_{\omega,n,l.m}e^{-i\omega t+im\varphi}S_{lm}(\theta)R_{nlm}(r)\,,

(A.2)

then one has

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\theta}\left(\sin\theta\,\partial_{\theta}S\right)+\left[a^{2}_{\star}r_{g}^{2}\left(\omega^{2}-\mu^{2}\right)\cos^{2}\theta-\frac{m^{2}}{\sin^{2}\theta}+\lambda\right]S=0\,,

(A.3)

\displaystyle\Delta\partial_{r}\left(\partial_{r}R\right)-\Delta\left[\mu^{2}r^{2}+a^{2}_{\star}r_{g}^{2}\omega^{2}-2\omega\,m\,a_{\star}r_{g}\,r+\left(\omega\left(r^{2}+a^{2}_{\star}r_{g}^{2}\right)-m\,a_{\star}r_{g}\right)^{2}+\lambda\right]R=0\,,

(A.4)

where
\lambda
denotes a separation constant, which is the eigenvalue of Eq. (
A.3
).
Here our main interest is the solution of the radial component
R
, Eq. (
A.4
), with a particular focus on the behavior of the frequency
\omega
therein. Note that if the imaginary part
\mathfrak{Im}(\omega)<0
, the wave would decay exponentially. However, the amplitude of the field grows exponentially if
\mathfrak{Im}(\omega)>0
.

Generically, there are no analytical solutions for Eq. (
A.4
).
However, we can consider the limit
\mu M\ll 1
, where
\lambda\simeq l(l+1)

[
8
]
.
With a dimensionless coordinate
x=(r-r_{+})/r_{+}

x\ll l/\bar{\omega}
and far region
x\gg 1
, where we have defined
\bar{\omega}=r_{+}\,\omega
.
One can then match the two solutions to obtain the field behavior in the overlapping region,
1\ll x\ll l/\bar{\omega}
, which includes the ergosphere.
There, an exponential growing mode appears if
\mathfrak{Im}(\omega)
is positive. This matching procedure leads to a constraint among the frequency
\omega
, BH mass
M

\mathfrak{Im}(\omega)

[

\mathfrak{Im}(\omega M)=-\frac{1}{2}A_{nl}\left(\frac{\omega^{\prime}M}{\tau}\right)(\mu M)^{4l+5}\left(\frac{r_{+}-r_{-}}{r_{+}+r_{-}}\right)^{2l+1},

(A.5)

where
\omega^{\prime}=(2-\tau)(\omega-m\Omega)

\tau=(r_{+}-r_{-})/r_{+}
and

A_{nl}=\left(\frac{l!}{(2l+1)!(2l)!}\right)^{2}\frac{(l+n)!}{(n-l-1)!}\frac{4^{2l+2}}{n^{2l+4}}\prod_{k=1}^{l}\left[k^{2}+16\left(\frac{\omega^{\prime}M}{\tau}\right)^{2}\right].

(A.6)

For the mode of interest with

(A.7)

and

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Im}(\omega M)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}A_{21}\left(\frac{\omega^{\prime}M}{\tau}\right)(\mu M)^{9}\left(\frac{r_{+}-r_{-}}{r_{+}+r_{-}}\right)^{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{24}(\mu M)^{9}(a_{\star}-2\omega\,r_{+})\frac{(1-a_{\star}^{2})}{\left(1+\sqrt{1-a_{\star}^{2}}\right)}+\mathcal{O}\left(\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{3}.

(A.8)

Note that in the Schwarzschild limit with
a_{\star}\to 0

At leading order

(A.9)

where we have considered

3.6

Due to the superradiance condition,

(A.10)

where
