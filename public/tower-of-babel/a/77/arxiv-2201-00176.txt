J. E. Björnberg, B. Lees

Abstract
. We elucidate connections between four models in statistical physics and probability theory: (1) the toric code model of Kitaev, (2) the uniform eight-vertex model, (3) random walk on a hypercube, and (4) a classical Ising model with four-body interaction. As a consequence of our analysis (and of the GKS-inequalities for the Ising model) we obtain correlation inequalities for the toric code model and the uniform eight-vertex model.



1.
Introduction

Recent years has seen a rapid development of research on vertex
models, primarily the six-vertex model.
The six-vertex model was originally introduced by Pauling as a simple
model of hydrogen bonding in water ice. Lieb

[
17
,
18
,
19
]
carried out pioneering work on the
model using the Bethe Ansatz originally developed for the Heisenberg
spin chain. Recently, the model has received growing attention from
the probability community, with several rigorous results as a
consequence, e.g.
[
7
,
8
,
12
]
.

The eight-vertex model is a generalisation of the six-vertex model
that allows two extra types of vertices:

sources
and
sinks
.
Both the six- and eight-vertex models are
integrable lattice models
[
11
,
23
]
.
Using methods coming from integrability, Baxter
[
4
]

computed the free energy per site.
For information on integrable models we
direct the reader to the book by Baxter
[
5
]
.

In this work we consider the eight-vertex model from a different
perspective than solvability/integrability. We consider some simple
dynamics which preserve eight-vertex configurations: select a

plaquette
(face) of the square lattice
at random and reverse the
direction of its four bounding edges. Because every vertex has an even
number (either zero or two) of its incident edges reversed, these
dynamics preserve the set of allowed configurations.
(A variant of the dynamics preserves the six-vertex model and is in
fact in detailed balance with the
six-vertex model’s probability distribution
[
1
]
.)

There is a close connection between these dynamics
and Kitaev’s
toric code model
.
Kitaev’s model (at zero temperature) is an important example of a

quantum code
,
which are motivated by problems in quantum computing.
While quantum computers would allow certain
computations to be performed
much faster than a conventional computer
(e.g. factoring large numbers

[
21
]
or searching unstructured databases

[
13
,
14
]
),
a major barrier in realising this potential
is the proclivity for the
quantum bits (qubits) of the computer to to have errors. One way to try
to overcome
this problem is to use multiple physical qubits to encode a single

logical
qubit that performs better than its individual physical
qubit components, primarily due to the ability to recover the
intended state of the logical qubit
even after one or more of the component physical qubits has
experienced an error. We direct the reader to
[
20
]
for an accessible
treatment of major topics in quantum information and quantum computation.
Surface codes, of which Kitaev’s toric code is an
example, are examples of such a scheme that
have enjoyed huge amounts of attention, in part due to their
relatively high tolerance for local errors
[
22
]
.
For an introduction to the various aspects of the toric/surface code
and how it operates we direct the reader to the review
[
10
]
.

Using the dynamics indicated above facilitates explicit
computations of certain thermodynamic quantities for the
toric code model at positive temperature.
Guided by these calculations and their
consequences, we establish a connection between certain
correlations in the toric code model and expectations in a
many-body classical Ising model. The ground state
of the latter Ising model also gives the
eight-vertex model with uniform vertex weights. Using well-known
correlation inequalities for the Ising model (GKS) we obtain
correlation inequalities for the uniform eight-vertex model and the
toric code model. The following is an example of our correlation
inequality for the uniform eight-vertex model: given any two sets of
vertices, consider the events that they are all sources or sinks.
Then these events are positively correlated, i.e. the probability of
both occurring is at least as large as the product of the
probabilities of the two individual events.
The full statement appears in Theorem
1.1
.

1.1.
Setting and results

For
m,n\geq 1
, let
V=\{1,\dotsc,m\}\times\{1,\dotsc,n\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
We view
V
as the vertex set of the
m\times n
torus
\Lambda
.
(It is possible to work with
\Lambda
as a subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with a
boundary; we do this in Section
4
.)
Let
E
denote the set of edges of
\Lambda
and
F
the set of faces.
In keeping with common terminology in the area, faces will
often be referred to as
plaquettes
and denoted
p\in F
,
and vertices referred to as
stars
and denoted
s\in V
.

The first model we consider is the
uniform eight-vertex model
,
defined as follows.
Let
\Delta
denote the set of assignments
of directions to the edges
E
, meaning that each horizontal edge is
directed either
\rightarrow
or
\leftarrow
,
and each vertical edge either
\uparrow
or
\downarrow
.
Elements of
\Delta
will sometimes be referred to as

arrow-configurations
.
Let
\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}\subseteq\Delta
denote the set
of arrow-configurations such
that the number of arrows pointing towards any vertex

s\in V
is even. At each vertex
s\in V
,
there are eight
possible configurations of arrows, depicted in
Figure
1
.
We refer to these eight local configurations by the roman numerals

\texttt{I},\texttt{II},\dotsc,\texttt{VIII}
.
The first six of these are the allowed configurations for the
six-vertex model;
VII
is called a
sink

and
VIII
a
source
.
We let
\mu(\cdot)
denote the uniform probability measure on

\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}
; this is the probability measure governing the uniform
8-vertex model.

Figure 1.
The eight vertex types

\texttt{I},\texttt{II},\dotsc,\texttt{VIII}

for arrow-configurations in
\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}
.

The next model we consider is Kitaev’s
toric code model

[
15
]
,
defined as follows. For
E
the edge-set of the torus
\Lambda
as above,
let
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes E}
be the tensor product of one copy of
\mathbb{C}^{2}

for each edge
e\in E
.
Using the standard basis

|+\rangle=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\big)
,

|-\rangle=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
1\end{smallmatrix}\big)

for
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, one obtains a (product) basis for
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes E}

with elements
|\omega\rangle
for
\omega\in\Omega=\{-1,+1\}^{E}
.
Consider the Pauli matrices

(1)

\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\big),\qquad\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}0&i\\
-i&0\end{smallmatrix}\big),\qquad\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\big),

and write
\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(j)}_{e}
for the linear
operator on

(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes E}
which acts as
\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(j)}

on the
e
factor and the identity elsewhere.
Since each
\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}_{e}
acts diagonally on the basis elements

|\omega\rangle
, we refer to this basis as the
\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}
product basis.

Introduce the operators

(2)

Z_{s}=\prod_{e\sim s}\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}_{e},\qquad X_{p}=\prod_{e\sim p}\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}_{e},\qquad s\in V,\quad p\in F,

where
e\sim s
and
e\sim p
mean that the edge
e
is adjacent to
the vertex
s
or the face
p
, respectively.
(The operators
Z_{s}
and
X_{p}
are more commonly denoted

A_{s}
and
B_{p}
, respectively.)
For
\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{x}}=(J^{\mathrm{x}}_{p}:p\in F)
and

\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{z}}=(J^{\mathrm{z}}_{s}:s\in V)
vectors of constants satisfying

J^{\mathrm{x}}_{p},J^{\mathrm{z}}_{s}\geq 0
, the
Hamiltonian operator

is defined as

(3)

H=H(\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{x}},\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{z}})=-\sum_{p\in F}J^{\mathrm{x}}_{p}X_{p}-\sum_{s\in V}J^{\mathrm{z}}_{s}Z_{s}.

For all
J^{\mathrm{x}}_{p}=J^{\mathrm{z}}_{s}=1
this is Kitaev’s
H_{0}
, see
[
15
]
.
All terms in
H
commute, since
for any star and plaquette the number of edges belonging to both
of them is even.

The Hamiltonian (
3
) governs the toric code model, for
instance through the equilibrium states defined as follows:
for an operator
A
on
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes E}
,

(4)

\langle A\rangle=\langle A\rangle_{\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{x}},\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{z}}}=\frac{\mathrm{tr}[A\,e^{-H}]}{\mathrm{tr}[e^{-H}]}.

If we take
J^{\mathrm{x}}_{p}=J^{\mathrm{z}}_{s}=\beta\to\infty
we obtain the ground state
which is important in the theory of quantum codes; see
Appendix
A
.
We will see below that the limit of all
J^{\mathrm{x}}_{p}\to\infty
,
with all
J^{\mathrm{z}}_{s}=\beta
fixed, essentially gives
the uniform 8-vertex model.

By further relating the uniform 8-vertex model and the toric code
model to a certain classical Ising model,
defined in the next subsection, we will prove certain
correlation inequalities. The setting is as follows. First, for the
8-vertex model,
let
\eta\in\{\texttt{I},\dotsc,\texttt{VIII}\}

be any 8-vertex configuration.
Let
\mathcal{A}^{\eta}_{C}
be the
event that, for each
s\in C
, the arrows around
s
are either all
equal to those of
\eta
or all the opposite to those of
\eta
. Additionally,
for
\nu\in\{\texttt{I},\dotsc,\texttt{VIII}\}
with
\nu\neq\eta
let
\mathcal{A}^{\eta,\nu}_{C}

be the event that, for each
s\in C
, the arrows around
s
are either all
equal to those of
\eta
or
\nu
or all the opposite
to those of
\eta
or
\nu
(this trivially
includes the even
\mathcal{A}^{\eta}_{C}
when
\nu
has all arrows opposite to
those of
\eta
).
For the toric code the setting is similar but more complex to state.
For
s\in V
, consider the four edges
e_{1}
,
e_{2}
,
e_{3}
and
e_{4}

adjacent to
s
and write

\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}_{i}(s)
for
\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}_{e_{i}}
. Let

\varepsilon\in\{-1,+1\}^{4}
, satisfying

\prod_{i=1}^{4}\varepsilon_{i}=+1
,
be a local sign-configuration.
Define the operators

(5)

P_{s}^{\varepsilon}=\tfrac{1}{16}\prod_{i=1}^{4}\big(1+\varepsilon_{i}\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}_{i}(s)\big),\qquad\bar{P}_{s}^{\varepsilon}=\tfrac{1}{16}\prod_{i=1}^{4}\big(1-\varepsilon_{i}\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}_{i}(s)\big),

and for
C\subseteq V
let

(6)

Q^{\varepsilon}_{C}=\prod_{s\in C}(P^{\varepsilon}_{s}+\bar{P}^{\varepsilon}_{s}).

For
\delta\in\{-1,+1\}^{4}
with
\delta\neq\pm\varepsilon
define

(7)

Q^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{C}=\prod_{s\in C}(P^{\varepsilon}_{s}+\bar{P}^{\varepsilon}_{s}+P^{\delta}_{s}+\bar{P}^{\delta}_{s}).

Finally,
we say that
C\subseteq V
is
contractible
if
C
does not contain a
set of nearest-neighbour vertices that forms a non-contractible loop
on the torus
\Lambda
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\eta,\nu
,
\varepsilon,\delta
, with
\nu\neq\eta
and
\delta\neq\pm\varepsilon
, be as above and let

C_{1},C_{2}\subseteq V
be sets of vertices such that

•

either
C_{1}\cup C_{2}
is contractible,

•

or any non-contractible loop in
C_{1}\cup C_{2}

has even length.

Then

(1)

For the uniform 8-vertex model:

\mu(\mathcal{A}^{\eta}_{C_{1}}\cap\mathcal{A}^{\eta}_{C_{2}})\geq\mu(\mathcal{A}^{\eta}_{C_{2}})\mu(\mathcal{A}^{\eta}_{C_{2}})

and

\mu(\mathcal{A}^{\eta,\nu}_{C_{1}}\cap\mathcal{A}^{\eta,\nu}_{C_{2}})\geq\mu(\mathcal{A}^{\eta,\nu}_{C_{2}})\mu(\mathcal{A}^{\eta,\nu}_{C_{2}}).

(2)

For the toric code model:

\langle Q^{\varepsilon}_{C_{1}}Q^{\varepsilon}_{C_{2}}\rangle\geq\langle Q^{\varepsilon}_{C_{1}}\rangle\langle Q^{\varepsilon}_{C_{2}}\rangle,

and

\langle Q^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{C_{1}}Q^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{C_{2}}\rangle\geq\langle Q^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{C_{1}}\rangle\langle Q^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{C_{2}}\rangle.

As an example of the correlation inequality for the 8-vertex model, we
can take
\eta
to be the constant vector of all
VII
’s, i.e. only
sinks
. Then
\mathcal{A}^{\eta}_{C}
is the event that every vertex
in
C
is either a source or a sink, and the Theorem says that

\mathcal{A}^{\eta}_{C_{1}}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\eta}_{C_{2}}
are positively correlated. This
is primarily interesting when
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are not too far apart:
we will see in Section
4.1

that if
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are not adjacent
to any common faces, then the states of the vertices in
C_{1}

and
C_{2}
are in fact independent under
\mu(\cdot)
.

1.2.
Relations between the models

As mentioned above, Theorem
1.1
builds on relating the two
models (uniform 8-vertex and toric code) to a classical Ising model.
We now define the latter.
Let
\mathbf{J}=(J_{s}:s\in V)
be a vector with all
J_{s}\geq 0
.
Recalling that
\Omega=\{-1,+1\}^{E}
,
define the probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{J}}(\cdot)

on
\Omega
by

(8)

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{J}}(\sigma)=\frac{\exp\big(\textstyle{\sum_{s\in V}J_{s}\prod_{e\sim s}\sigma_{e}}\big)}{\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\exp\big(\textstyle{\sum_{s\in V}J_{s}\prod_{e\sim s}\omega_{e}}\big)}.

This is a ferromagnetic Ising model with four-body interaction,
and the restriction of the
toric code model to a certain class of observables is equivalent to
this model:

Proposition 1.2
.

Let
Q
be any observable diagonal in the
\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}
product basis,
say
Q|\sigma\rangle=q(\sigma)|\sigma\rangle
for any
\sigma\in\Omega
.
Then

(9)

\langle Q\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{z}}}[q(\sigma)],

where
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{J}}[\cdot]
denotes expectation with respect to

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{J}}(\cdot)
.
In particular,
\langle Q\rangle
does not depend on
\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{x}}
.

Note also that if
J_{s}\to\infty
for all
s\in V
then

\lim\mathbb{P}_{J}(\cdot)
is supported on the set

\Omega_{\mathrm{8vx}}\subseteq\Omega
of configurations

\sigma
satisfying
\prod_{e\sim s}\sigma_{1}=+1
for all
s\in V
,
since
\sum_{s\in V}\prod_{e\sim s}\sigma_{e}

attains its maximum value
|V|
for such
\sigma
.
This observation will allow us to (essentially) identify the uniform
8-vertex measure
\mu(\cdot)
with

\lim\mathbb{P}_{J}(\cdot)
.

The key fact we use about the Ising model
(
8
) is that
it satisfies GKS-inequalities (see e.g.
[
9
, Theorem 3.49]
):
for any sets
A,B\subseteq E
we have

(10)

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{J}}\big[\prod_{e\in A}\sigma_{e}\prod_{e\in B}\sigma_{e}\big]\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{J}}\big[\prod_{e\in A}\sigma_{e}\big]\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{J}}\big[\prod_{e\in A}\sigma_{e}\big],\qquad\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{J}}\big[\prod_{e\in A}\sigma_{e}\big]\geq 0.

The fourth model which we use in our analysis is

simple random walk on the hypercube
.
To define this, let
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{+}
denote the two-element group with
elements
\{0,1\}
satisfying
1+1=0
, and consider

G=(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{+})^{F}
with generators given by

(11)

g^{p}_{j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\mbox{if }j=p,\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Then
G
is a hypercube of dimension
|F|
.
A random walk on
G
is given by
selecting, independently at random, faces
p_{1},p_{2},\dotsc,p_{k}

and letting
X(k)=X(0)+\sum_{i=1}^{k}g^{p_{i}}

where
X(0)
is a starting position.
Here at each step we choose face
p
with probability

J_{p}/\sum_{q\in F}J_{q}
.

The relation to the previous models comes by letting
G

act on the sets
\Omega
and
\Delta
. The intuition is simple:
g^{p}
acts
on elements of

\Delta
by reversing all the arrows around the face
p
, and on

\Omega
by negating the
\pm
signs on the edges surrounding
p
. It
is easy to see that this action leaves
\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}\subseteq\Delta
invariant,
and also leaves the measures
\mu(\cdot)
and
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{J}}(\cdot)

invariant. The connection to the toric code model is slightly more
subtle: we will see in Section
2.1
that the term

\sum_{p\in F}J^{\mathrm{x}}_{p}X_{p}
in the Hamiltonian (
3
)
is essentially the generator matrix of (the continuous-time version
of) simple random walk on
G
. This allows us to express
the equilibrium states (
4
) for
H

using transition probabilities for the random walk,
which will be instrumental in understanding some basic features of the
toric code model, including the relation to the Ising model,
Proposition
1.2
.

2.
Properties of the toric code model

2.1.
Dynamic description

Recall that (below (
11
))
we defined random walk on the hypercube
G=(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{+})^{F}
as
the process obtained by sampling independently at random
faces
p_{1},p_{2},\dotsc
and successively adding them to an initial
element of
G
. We make this into a continuous-time random walk using
a Poisson process
N(t)
of rate
|F|
and defining

(12)

X(t)=X(0)+\sum_{i=1}^{N(t)}g^{p_{i}}.

An equivalent description is that each face
p\in F
is assigned an
independent exponentially distributed random ‘clock’
of rate
J_{p}
and that we add 1
in the postion of
p
when the corresponding clock rings.

We next map the random walk (
12
) onto a process in

\Omega=\{-1,+1\}^{E}
as well as onto a process in the set
\Delta
of
arrow-configurations. As already described for the discrete-time
case, the process in
\Omega
proceeds by ‘flipping’ (negating) all signs
on the edges around the selected face
p
, and the process in
\Delta

similarly reverses the arrows around
p
.
We may describe this more formally as follows. The group
G
acts on
the set
\Omega
by

(13)

g^{p}:\omega\mapsto x^{p}\cdot\omega,\qquad\mbox{where}\quad x^{p}_{e}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-1,&\mbox{if }e\sim p,\\
+1,&\mbox{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

and
\cdot
denotes component-wise multiplication.
(We are implicitly presenting
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{+}
as the multiplicative group
with elements
\{-1,+1\}
.)
Then we define
\omega(t)
as the result of applying (
13
) for
the randomly selected faces
p_{i}
in (
12
):

(14)

\omega(t)=\big(\prod_{i=1}^{N(t)}x^{p_{i}}\big)\cdot\omega(0).

Here
\omega(0)\in\Omega
is an initial configuration, and since the group

G
is commutative we do not need to specify an order of
multiplication in (
14
).

It will be useful to record here the generator-matrix for the random
walk (
14
). Indeed, regarding the
x^{p}
of (
13
)
as a matrix with rows and columns indexed by
\Omega
, the generator
matrix can be written as

(15)

\sum_{p\in F}J_{p}(x^{p}-1),

where
1
denotes the identity matrix. It follows that the
transition-probabilities at time
t
are given by the matrix

(16)

\exp\big(t\textstyle\sum_{p\in F}J_{p}(x^{p}-1)\big).

To formally define the process in
\Delta
, we note that
we may think of
\omega\in\Omega
as
encoding an arrow-configuration in
\Delta

by indicating which edges are reversed
(or not) with respect to some a-priori
configuration of arrows. More precisely, fix a
reference-configuration
\rho\in\Delta
and define, for
\omega\in\Omega
, the
arrow-configuration
\omega\cdot\rho
such that the arrow at
e
has the

same
orientation as in
\rho
if
\omega_{e}=+1
, respectively the

opposite
orientation if
\omega_{e}=-1
. We then obtain a random walk

\delta(t)\in\Delta
by

(17)

\delta(t)=\omega(t)\cdot\rho.

Note that
\delta(t)
depends both on the reference-configuration

\rho\in\Delta
and the initial sign-configuration
\omega(0)\in\Omega
.

We next describe the relevance of the random walk
\omega(t)
for the
toric code model. Recall that the elements
\omega\in\Omega
are in
one-to-one correspondence with basis-vectors
|\omega\rangle
for

(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes E}
, given as the product-basis obtained from the
basis

|+\rangle=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\big)
,

|-\rangle=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
1\end{smallmatrix}\big)

for
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Thus the action of
G
on
\Omega
carries over to a
representation of
G
on
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes E}
. Moreover,
from the explicit form of the Pauli-matrices (
1
) we see
that
\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}|\pm\rangle=|\mp\rangle
. Thus the operator
X_{p}
in (
2
)
acts precisely as
x^{p}
in (
13
). It follows from
(
15
) that the term

\sum_{p\in F}J^{\mathrm{x}}_{p}X_{p}
in the Hamiltonian
(
3
) is, up to adding a multiple of the identity, the
generator for a
random walk
|\omega(t)\rangle
on the set of basis-vectors.

We note here that the random walk on
\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}
is
not irreducible, i.e. the set decomposes into several disjoint
communicating classes. See Proposition
4.1
.

2.2.
Duality

There are two useful notions of duality for the toric code model:
that of the lattice
\Lambda
as a planar graph, as well as a duality
between the operators
Z_{s}
and
X_{p}
. In this subsection we aim to
make precise these dualities as well as the connection between them.

We start by looking at the operators
Z_{s}
and
X_{p}
. Recall that we
have been using a basis
for
\mathbb{C}^{2}
in which
\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}
is diagonal and the other Pauli
matrices are given as in (
1
).
For the rest of this subsection, we denote this basis by

|+\rangle_{\mathrm{z}}=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\big)
,

|-\rangle_{\mathrm{z}}=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
1\end{smallmatrix}\big)

where the subscript z serves to indicate that the third Pauli matrix is
diagonal. The corresponding product basis for
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes E}

will be denoted
|\omega\rangle_{\mathrm{z}}
for
\omega\in\Omega
.
Recall that, in this basis, the operator
X_{p}
negates the signs on
all edges
e
surrounding
p\in F
, while
Z_{s}
is diagonal.

We can also consider a basis for
\mathbb{C}^{2}
in which
\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}
is
diagonal: define

(18)

|+\rangle_{\mathrm{x}}=\frac{|+\rangle_{\mathrm{z}}+|-\rangle_{\mathrm{z}}}{\sqrt{2}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad|-\rangle_{\mathrm{x}}=\frac{|+\rangle_{\mathrm{z}}-|-\rangle_{\mathrm{z}}}{\sqrt{2}}.

The corresponding product basis for
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes E}

will be denoted
|\omega\rangle_{\mathrm{x}}
for
\omega\in\Omega
.
The basis-change matrix for going between

|\pm\rangle_{\mathrm{z}}
and
|\pm\rangle_{\mathrm{x}}
is the symmetric, orthogonal
matrix (the Hadamard matrix)

(19)

U=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{smallmatrix}\big),\qquad\mbox{for which}\quad U|\pm\rangle_{\mathrm{z}}=|\pm\rangle_{\mathrm{x}}.

To go between the bases for
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes E}
one uses the

E
-fold tensor product
U^{\otimes E}
.
This change of basis maps
\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}
to
U\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}U=\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}
and
\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}

to
U\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}U=\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}
, meaning that in the
|\cdot\rangle_{\mathrm{x}}
-basis,

Z_{s}
negates all the signs on the edges
adjacent
s\in V
, while
X_{p}
is diagonal.

What we have described so far dovetails with the planar duality of

\Lambda
, as follows. Define the dual
\Lambda^{\ast}
to be the graph with
vertex set
V^{\ast}=F(\Lambda)
, edge-set
E^{\ast}=E(\Lambda)
, and faces

F^{\ast}=V(\Lambda)
. One obtains
\Lambda^{\ast}
by placing a vertex
s^{\ast}(p)

in the middle of each face
p
of
\Lambda
and drawing edges
perpendicularly across those of
\Lambda
. In this way, any vertex
s
of
the orignal lattice
\Lambda
lies in the middle of a unique face

p^{\ast}(s)
of the dual
\Lambda^{\ast}
. We can then write, for
s\in V

and
p\in F
,

(20)

\begin{split}U^{\otimes E}X_{p}U^{\otimes E}&=\prod_{e\sim p}U\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}_{e}U=\prod_{e\sim s^{\ast}(p)}\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}_{e}=:Z_{s^{\ast}},\mbox{ and}\\
\ U^{\otimes E}Z_{s}U^{\otimes E}&=\prod_{e\sim s}U\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}_{e}U=\prod_{e\sim p^{\ast}(s)}\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}_{e}=:X_{p^{\ast}}.\end{split}

As noted above, the operators
Z_{s^{\ast}}
and
X_{p^{\ast}}
act on the

\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}
-basis

|\omega\rangle_{\mathrm{x}}
in the exact same way as

Z_{s}
and
X_{p}
act on the
\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}
-basis
|\omega\rangle_{\mathrm{z}}
.
Namely,

Z_{s^{\ast}}
is diagonal while
X_{p^{\ast}}
negates the signs around

p^{\ast}
. In this sense, going between the lattice
\Lambda
and its dual

\Lambda^{\ast}
is equivalent to changing basis between
|\cdot\rangle_{\mathrm{z}}

and
|\cdot\rangle_{\mathrm{x}}
. Some particular instances of this are the
identities:

(21)

\begin{split}&\prescript{}{\mathrm{z}}{\langle\tau|}\exp\big(t{\textstyle\sum_{p\in F}}J^{\mathrm{x}}_{p}X_{p}\big)|\sigma\rangle_{\mathrm{z}}=\prescript{}{\mathrm{x}}{\langle\tau|}\exp\big(t{\textstyle\sum_{s^{\ast}\in V^{\ast}}}J^{\mathrm{x}}_{s^{\ast}}Z_{s^{\ast}}\big)|\sigma\rangle_{\mathrm{x}},\\
&\prescript{}{\mathrm{z}}{\langle\tau|}\exp\big(t{\textstyle\sum_{s\in V}}J^{\mathrm{z}}_{s}Z_{s}\big)|\sigma\rangle_{\mathrm{z}}=\prescript{}{\mathrm{x}}{\langle\tau|}\exp\big(t{\textstyle\sum_{p^{\ast}\in F^{\ast}}}J^{\mathrm{z}}_{p^{\ast}}X_{p^{\ast}}\big)|\sigma\rangle_{\mathrm{x}},\end{split}

and for any
A\subseteq V(\Lambda)
,
B\subseteq F(\Lambda)
with corresponding dual sets

A^{\ast}\subseteq F(\Lambda^{\ast})
,
B^{\ast}\subseteq V(\Lambda^{\ast})
,

(22)

\prescript{}{\mathrm{z}}{\langle\tau|}\textstyle\prod_{s\in A}Z_{s}\prod_{p\in B}X_{p}|\sigma\rangle_{\mathrm{z}}=\prescript{}{\mathrm{x}}{\langle\tau|}\textstyle\prod_{p^{\ast}\in A^{\ast}}X_{p^{\ast}}\prod_{s^{\ast}\in B^{\ast}}Z_{s^{\ast}}|\sigma\rangle_{\mathrm{x}}.

These identities may all be obtained by conjugating with

U^{\otimes E}
.

Lemma 2.1

For any

Proof.

The first identity in (

For (

∎

Lemma

Proposition 2.2
.

The partition function is given explicitly as

(25)

\mathrm{tr}[e^{-H}]=2^{|E|}\big(\prod_{s\in V}\cosh(J^{\mathrm{z}}_{s})+\prod_{s\in V}\sinh(J^{\mathrm{z}}_{s})\big)\big(\prod_{p\in F}\cosh(J^{\mathrm{x}}_{p})+\prod_{p\in F}\sinh(J^{\mathrm{x}}_{p})\big).

In particular, (
26
) and (

Proof.

For (
25

(28)

\begin{split}\mathrm{tr}[e^{-H}]&=\mathrm{tr}\big[\exp\big({\textstyle\sum_{s\in V}}J^{\mathrm{z}}_{s}Z_{s}\big)\exp\big({\textstyle\sum_{p\in F}}J^{\mathrm{x}}_{p}X_{p}\big)\big]\\

The first equality follows from the fact that the terms commute, the

2.1
and the elementary fact that

are

is constant on the

(29)

\mathrm{tr}\big[\exp\big({\textstyle\sum_{p\in F}}J^{\mathrm{x}}_{p}X_{p}\big)\big]=2^{|E|}\Big(\prod_{p\in F}\cosh(tJ^{\mathrm{x}}_{p})+\prod_{p\in F}\sinh(tJ^{\mathrm{x}}_{p})\Big).

Also, from the duality-relations (

(30)

\mathrm{tr}\big[\exp\big({\textstyle\sum_{s\in F}}J^{\mathrm{z}}_{s}Z_{s}\big)\big]=2^{|E|}\Big(\prod_{s\in V}\cosh(tJ^{\mathrm{z}}_{s})+\prod_{s\in V}\sinh(tJ^{\mathrm{z}}_{s})\Big)

-basis.

For (

(31)

\begin{split}\mathrm{tr}\big[\big(\textstyle{\prod_{p\in C}X_{p}}\big)&e^{-H}\big]=\mathrm{tr}\big[\exp\big(\textstyle{\sum_{s\in V}J^{\mathrm{z}}_{s}Z_{s}}\big)\big(\textstyle{\prod_{p\in C}X_{p}}\big)\exp\big(\textstyle{\sum_{p\in F}J^{\mathrm{x}}_{p}X_{p}}\big)\big]\\

\prod_{s\in D_{1}}\sigma_{i(s)}\sigma_{j(s)}\prod_{t\in D_{2}\triangle D_{3}}A_{t}=1.

Recall that we represent each factor of the product over

as an element of the set of shapes

\{\lefthalfcup,-\hskip-4.0pt-,\righthalfcup,\lefthalfcap,\mid\hskip-1.0pt,\righthalfcap\}
,
indicating the two selected edges.
Similarly, each factor of the product over

may be represented as a

+

We think of the latter as consisting of two
superimposed shapes, such as

\lefthalfcup
and
\righthalfcap

We can assume that
D_{1}\cap(D_{2}\triangle D_{3})=\varnothing

appears in the product either exactly twice, or not at all. Consider
now the subset of edges which appear exactly twice (counting each such
edge once). Due to the possible choices of ‘shapes’ at each vertex,
this subset forms
an even subgraph (each vertex has even degree)
and thus decomposes as a collection of closed loops.
We claim that
each such loop has the following property:
the number of vertices where it exhibits a shape from

\{\lefthalfcup,\righthalfcap\}
is even, similarly
the number of vertices where it exhibits a shape from

\{\righthalfcup,\lefthalfcap\}
is even,
and finally the number of vertices where it exhibits a shape from

\{-\hskip-4.0pt-,\mid\hskip-1.0pt\}
is even. The latter claim can
be seen by induction, as follows.
We may take the loops to be non-crossing.
For any non-crossing contractible
loop enclosing at least two squares,

area one square by removing a corner. As we do so, the number of
shapes from each of the four sets changes by an even amount.
Eventually the loop reduces to a single square, for which the claim
holds by inspection. If the loop is not contractible, then a similar
reduction can be used to reduce it to a ‘straight’ loop, which by our
assumption on
C_{1}\cup C_{2}
has even length, meaning again that the
claim holds by inspection.

Finally, the sign on the left-hand-side of (

can be written as

(51)

\prod_{s\in D_{1}}\varepsilon_{i(s)}\varepsilon_{j(s)}=\prod_{s\in D_{1}}\varepsilon_{i(s)}\varepsilon_{j(s)}\prod_{t\in D_{2}\triangle D_{3}}\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}\varepsilon_{3}\varepsilon_{4}.

Here we used that
\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}\varepsilon_{3}\varepsilon_{4}=+1
.
For any choice of an even number of shapes from each of the sets

\{\lefthalfcup,\righthalfcap\}
,

\{\righthalfcup,\lefthalfcap\}
and

\{-\hskip-4.0pt-,\mid\hskip-1.0pt\}
,
the product of the corresponding
\varepsilon

in (
51
) is
=+1
, again using

\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}\varepsilon_{3}\varepsilon_{4}=+1
.
The result follows.

4.
Further results for the uniform 8-vertex model

In this section we discuss some further properties of the uniform
eight-vertex model that are natural to consider
from the viewpoint of ‘plaquette-flipping’.

4.1.
Communicating classes

We start by considering the communicating classes of the
dynamics (
17
) in the case when all

J_{p}>0
. Recall that
\Lambda
is an

m\times n
torus.

Proposition 4.1
.

We have that

|\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}|=2^{|V|+1}
, and there are four
communicating classes for the dynamics (
17
),
each of size
2^{|V|-1}
. We may move between them
by reversing arrows along a non-contractible path in
\Lambda
.

Proof.

We start by showing that

|\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}|=2^{|V|+1}
.
First, clearly
|\Delta|=2^{|E|}=2^{2|V|}
.
By fixing a reference-configuration
\rho

as in (
17
) we can encode each element of
\Delta

using an element
v\in(\mathbb{F}_{2})^{E}
where
\mathbb{F}_{2}=\{0,1\}

is the two-element field. Then, for each
s\in V
,
the constraint that
s

has an even number of incoming arrows becomes a linear constraint over

\mathbb{F}_{2}
, namely
g^{s}\cdot v=0
where (similarly to (
11
))

(57)

g^{s}_{e}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\mbox{if }e\sim s,\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

and
\cdot
is the scalar product.
These constraints are
not
linearly independent,
since
(\sum_{s\in V}g^{s})_{e}=0
for each
e\in E
. However, this is
the only linear relation: any other linear relation would have to be
of the form
\sum_{s\in A}g^{s}\equiv 0
for some proper subset
A
of

V
. But then there must be some
edge
e\in E
with precisely one end point in
A
and then

(\sum_{s\in A}g^{s})_{e}=1\neq 0
.
From this and the rank-nullity theorem we get that

|\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}|=2^{2|V|-(|V|-1)}=2^{|V|+1}
, as claimed.

Now it is simple to see that there are four communicating classes for
the dynamics. There are
|V|
plaquettes in
F
and hence
2^{|V|}

possible sums
\sum_{p\in A}g^{p}
for
A\subseteq V
. However, we have
that
\sum_{p\in A}g^{p}=\sum_{p\in V\setminus A}g^{p}
(since

\sum_{p\in V}g^{p}\equiv 0
). Hence,
starting from any reference configuration
\rho\in\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}
,
there are
2^{|V|-1}
configurations reachable by flipping plaquettes.
This shows that there are four communicating classes.

Lastly, let
P\subset E
be a non-contractible path of edges in

\Lambda
, it is clear that such a path is not the boundary of a
collection of plaquettes
A\subset F
. This means that the
configuration obtained by reversing arrows on
P
is not reachable
from the reference configuration via the dynamics. There are two
homotopy classes of non-contractible paths on the torus and reversing
arrows along a path of edges in one or two of the classes moves us
between the four communicating classes.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

hej

(1)

Because our dynamics for the uniform

(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{+})^{F}

For example, the mixing time

4.2.
Emptiness formation probability

In this subsection we allow to view
\Lambda

not only as a torus, but also
as a subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with a boundary.
In that case we replace each of the edges of the form

\{(m,y),(1,y)\}
with two boundary-edges

\{(m,y),(m+1,y)\}
and
\{(0,y),(1,y)\}

and similarly replace each

\{(x,n),(x,1)\}
with two boundary-edges

\{(x,n),(x,n+1)\}
and
\{(x,0),(x,1)\}

First we consider the
domain wall boundary
condition

edges (of the form
\{(0,y),(1,y)\}
or
\{(m,y),(m+1,y)\}

receive the fixed orientation pointing
in
towards

\Lambda

\{(x,0),(x,1)\}

\{(x,m),(x,m+1)\}

out
of
\Lambda
.

We emphasise

and

using the notation

We denote

\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}^{\textsc{dw}}(\Lambda_{m,n})

Proposition 4.3
.

We have that

(58)

|\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}^{\textsc{dw}}(\Lambda_{m,n})|=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\mbox{if $m+n$ is odd,}\\
2^{(m-1)(n-1)},&\mbox{if $m+n$ is even}.\end{array}\right.

When non-empty,
\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}^{\textsc{dw}}(\Lambda_{m,n})

consists of a single communicating class for the

Proof.

We begin by showing that
\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}^{\textsc{dw}}(\Lambda_{m,n})\neq\varnothing

if and only if
m+n
is even. This is done in three steps.

Step 1: if
\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}^{\textsc{dw}}(\Lambda_{m-1,n-1})\neq\varnothing

then
\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}^{\textsc{dw}}(\Lambda_{m,n})\neq\varnothing
. Indeed,
Figure
3
illustrates how an element of

\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}^{\textsc{dw}}(\Lambda_{m-1,n-1})

\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}^{\textsc{dw}}(\Lambda_{m,n})

Left: extending

Right:

\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}^{\textsc{dw}}(\Lambda_{m-1,n-1})\neq\varnothing

\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}^{\textsc{dw}}(\Lambda_{m,n})\neq\varnothing

Step 2: conversely, if
\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}^{\textsc{dw}}(\Lambda_{m,n})\neq\varnothing

\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}^{\textsc{dw}}(\Lambda_{m-1,n-1})\neq\varnothing

Indeed, fix an element

\Lambda_{m,n}

\delta

to
\Lambda_{m-1,n-1}
is an element of

\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}^{\textsc{dw}}(\Lambda_{m-1,n-1})

the edges pointing the ‘wrong way’ (i.e. out for vertical edges on

. It is not hard to check that the number of faults must
be even. Then, pair up the successive

\delta
to a configuration whose restriction to

\Lambda_{m-1,n-1}

\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}^{\textsc{dw}}(\Lambda_{m-1,n-1})

Step 3: from the previous two steps we see that

\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}^{\textsc{dw}}(\Lambda_{m,n})\neq\varnothing

\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}^{\textsc{dw}}(\Lambda_{m-n+1,1})\neq\varnothing

it holds if and only if

\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}^{\textsc{dw}}(\Lambda_{1,n-m+1})\neq\varnothing
.
It is straightforward to check that the latter sets are non-empty if
and only if
m-n
is even, which is equivalent to
m+n
being even.

To determine the number of configurations when
m+n
is even,
we use a simple counting argument.
First, let us count the number of choices at each vertex if we start
at the top left corner
(1,n)
and proceed
left to right and then top to bottom.
As we proceed, each time we ‘arrive’ at a new vertex
except
the

(59)

|\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}^{\textsc{dw}}(\Lambda_{m,n})|\leq 2^{(m-1)(n-1)}.

On the other hand, if

\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}^{\textsc{dw}}(\Lambda_{m,n})\neq\emptyset

(60)

|\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}^{\textsc{dw}}(\Lambda_{m,n})|\geq 2^{(m-1)(n-1)}.

Combining (

)

Assume that

.

Consider

Let us fix the column that is to be empty as the rightmost column.

.

A boundary condition

\Lambda

|\Delta^{\eta}_{\mathrm{8vx}}(\Lambda)|=2^{(m-1)(n-1)}

If
\Delta^{\eta}_{\mathrm{8vx}}\neq\emptyset

The next result can be interpreted as saying that

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
\eta

\Lambda
. Then

\frac{|\Delta_{\mathrm{8vx}}|}{|\Delta^{\eta}_{\mathrm{8vx}}|}=2^{m+n-1}=2^{O(\partial\Lambda)}.

Appendix A
The toric code

In this appendix we summarise some of the original motivation
for the toric code model, starting with basic properties of quantum
codes.

Recall
that classical codes store information in sequences of numbers 0 or 1,
each of which is called a bit. For quantum codes, the bits 0 or 1 are
replaced by so-called
qubits
which are more elaborate objects.
A qubit may be defined as
an irreducible two-dimensional
representation of
\mathfrak{su}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
and a quantum code of
length
n
as
an
n
-fold tensor product of qubits
.
Let us now unpack these definitions.

Consider as before the two-dimensional
vector space
\mathbb{C}^{2}
and with
standard basis

|+\rangle=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\big)

and

|-\rangle=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
1\end{smallmatrix}\big)

(these are often denoted
|1\rangle
and
|0\rangle
instead,
but
|+\rangle
and
|-\rangle
is a more natural choice for us).
The Pauli matrices (
1
), under addition and
multiplication, generate an
algebra of two-by-two
Hermitian matrices with trace 0. Together with the
underlying vector space
\mathbb{C}^{2}
on which the matrices act, this algebra
of matrices is a
representation
of (the universal
enveloping algebra of)
\mathfrak{su}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. That is, they form a
single qubit by our definition.

It is a crucial point that there are other ways to represent a qubit
than the specific construction above. That is, one may find other
two-dimensional vector spaces
\mathbb{V}
(over
\mathbb{C}
),
together with matrices
X,Y,Z

acting on
\mathbb{V}
, that have “all relevant properties” of
\mathbb{C}^{2}

together with the
\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)},\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)},\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}
.
More precisely, there are other representations of

\mathfrak{su}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
which are
isomorphic
to the one above
and therefore also form a single qubit.
To check that
\mathbb{V}
together with
X,Y,Z
form a qubit, one needs to
check that
X,Y,Z
satisfy the following
relations:

(65)

[X,Z]:=XZ-ZX=-2iY=-2ZX,\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad X^{2}+Y^{2}+Z^{2}=3\hbox{\rm 1\kern-2.70004ptI}.

For classical codes, one obtains protection from errors by using

redundance
; that is, a single bit is encoded in a sequence of
several bits of length
n>1
, where the extra bits are used to detect
possible errors of transmission. For quantum codes, the analogous
setting is obtained using the
n
-fold tensor product

(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
.
For each
k\in\{1,\dotsc,n\}
one then has a

physical qubit
consisting of copies

\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}_{k},\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}_{k},\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}_{k}
of the Pauli matrices which act only
on the
k
entry.
To obtain a quantum code with desirable properties, one sets this up
in such a way that
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
has at least one
two-dimensional
subspace carrying a representation of

\mathfrak{su}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, that is, forms a qubit
(other than the ones obtained using the factors

\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}_{k},\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}_{k},\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}_{k}
). Such a
sub-representation is then
called a
logical qubit
.

For the toric code, we use the setting described above with

n=|E|
, that is the physical qubits are indexed by the edge-set
E

of the torus.
To identify logical qubits we use the operators

(66)

A_{s}=\prod_{e\sim s}\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}_{e},\qquad B_{p}=\prod_{e\sim p}\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}_{e},\qquad s\in V,\quad p\in F.

(These are the same as
X_{s}
and
Z_{p}
in (
2
) but here we
use the more common notation (
66
).)
The relevant subspace of
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes E}
is

(67)

\mathcal{L}=\big\{|\xi\rangle\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes E}:A_{s}|\xi\rangle=B_{p}|\xi\rangle=|\xi\rangle,\mbox{ for all }s\in V,p\in F\big\},

i.e. the subspace stabilised by all
A_{s}
and
B_{p}
.
We will see that
\mathcal{L}
carries
two
logical qubits.

Let us look more closely at the condition
A_{s}|\xi\rangle=|\xi\rangle
.
Note that

(68)

A_{s}|\xi\rangle=\big(\prod_{e\sim s}\xi_{e}\big)|\xi\rangle.

Thus, the condition
A_{s}|\xi\rangle=|\xi\rangle
is identical to the
constraint defining
\Omega_{\mathrm{8vx}}\subseteq\Omega
as the set
of configurations

\sigma
satisfying
\prod_{e\sim s}\sigma_{1}=+1
for all
s\in V
,
In particular, we may
identify
\mathcal{L}
with a subset of
\Omega_{\mathrm{8vx}}
.

Translating the multiplicative constraints
defining
\mathcal{L}
into linear constraints, one may use the
same reasoning (rank-nullity) as for
Proposition
4.1

to conclude that
\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{L})=4
. Thus
\mathcal{L}
has the correct
dimension for carrying two qubits. To see that it indeed does, we need
to define operators
X_{1},Z_{1}
and
X_{2},Z_{2}
on
\mathcal{L}
which commute
(for differing indices) and satisfy (
65
)
(for matching indices). Let
L_{1}\subseteq E
be the set of edges of the
form
\{(x,1),(x+1,1)\}
for
1\leq x\leq m
. Thus
L_{1}
is a
horizontal path that wraps around the torus. Also let
L_{1}^{\prime}
be the
set of edges of the form
\{(1,y),(2,y)\}
for
1\leq y\leq n
forming
a vertical ‘ladder’ around the torus. Define

(69)

X_{1}=\prod_{e\in L_{1}}\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}_{e},\qquad Z_{1}=\prod_{e\in L_{1}^{\prime}}\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}_{e}.

Similarly let
L_{2}
be the set of edges of the
form
\{(1,y),(1,y+1)\}
for
1\leq y\leq n

and let
L_{2}^{\prime}
be the set of edges of the form

\{(x,1),(x,2)\}
for
1\leq x\leq m
, respectively forming
a vertical path and a horizontal ‘ladder’, and define

(70)

X_{2}=\prod_{e\in L_{2}}\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}_{e},\qquad Z_{2}=\prod_{e\in L_{2}^{\prime}}\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}_{e}.

One may check that these operators indeed have the desired
properties, the key observation being that the paths share either zero
or exactly one edge.

Let us briefly describe the error-correction properties of the code

\mathcal{L}
on an intuitive level.
Imagine that a message is encoded as an element of the space

\mathcal{L}
, but an error occurs in transmission so that the received
message is no longer an element of
\mathcal{L}
. The most
likely cause for this is that a
minimal
(non-zero) number of
the constraints
A_{s}|\xi\rangle=|\xi\rangle
and
B_{p}|\xi\rangle=|\xi\rangle

is broken. By parity constraints, this minimal number is two
(since
\prod_{s\in V}A_{s}=1
and
\prod_{p\in F}B_{p}=1
).
Suppose that
A_{s_{1}}|\xi\rangle\neq|\xi\rangle

and
A_{s_{2}}|\xi\rangle\neq|\xi\rangle
. In terms of arrow configurations

\omega\in\Omega
, this means that
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
are
faults
, i.e. the total number of in- (or out-) pointing arrows is odd. Then,
flipping all the qubits along a path between
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
gives an
element of
\mathcal{L}
again. It is plausible that the element we obtain in
this way is the message that was sent.
