Data-Driven Mechanism Design via Multi-Agent Revealed Preferences
Luke Snow, Vikram Krishnamurthy

Abstract
. We study a sequence of independent one-shot non-cooperative games in which agents generate decisions according to equilibria parameterized by a mechanism. Given only these observed decisions, and without parametric knowledge of the agents’ utility functions, our goal is to achieve mechanism design by steering equilibria toward social optimality. When this is infeasible due to the intrinsic structure of the underlying game, we seek to identify such unachievability. This paper constructs a reinforcement learning (RL) framework for adaptively achieving this mechanism design objective. We first derive a novel multi-agent revealed preference test for Pareto optimality – this yields necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of utility functions under which empirically observed mixed-strategy Nash equilibria are socially optimal. These conditions take the form of a testable linear program, and this result is of independent interest. We utilize this result to construct an inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) step to determine the Pareto gap , i.e., the distance of observed strategies from Pareto optimality. We pair this IRL step with an RL policy gradient algorithm and prove convergence to a mechanism which globally minimizes the Pareto gap. This enables a principled test for achievability of social optimality: if social optimality is achievable for the game setup and observational equilibria, Algorithm 1 will achieve it; else, we can identify this unachievability, while still converging to a mechanism which is closest to social optimality . We also reveal an intimate connection between our constructed loss function and several robust revealed preference metrics; this allows us to reason about algorithmic suboptimality through the lens of these well-established microeconomic principles. Finally, in the case when only finitely many i.i.d. samples from mixed-strategies (partial strategy specifications) are available, we derive concentration bounds for our algorithm’s convergence, and we construct a distributionally robust RL procedure which achieves the mechanism design objective for the fully specified strategies. 1 1 1 A preliminary version of this work appears in the 2024 IEEE Conference on Decision and Control. 1.
Introduction

We consider a sequence of independent one-shot non-cooperative games. Multiple agents act in their own self-interest, aiming to maximize their individual utility functions which are dependent on the actions of the entire set of agents. Furthermore, agents are allowed to randomize over actions in order to play from mixed-strategies. It is well known that mixed-strategy Nash equilibria represent the stable joint-strategy states of the entire group, consisting of those states in which no agent has any incentive to unilaterally deviate from its mixed-strategy given the strategies of all other agents.

The goal of
mechanism design
is to fashion the game structure, specifically the mapping from joint-action space to agent utilities ("mechanism"), such that
non-cooperative behavior gives rise to socially optimal solutions
.
The
mechanism
specifies how agents’ chosen actions determine the resulting outcomes and thus individual utilities—it is the rule that translates strategic behavior into payoffs. Thus, by modifying the mechanism, the strategic agent outputs will adjust accordingly, and a mechanism designer can aim to indirectly adjust these outputs through the mechanism, such that they become socially optimal.

Existing mechanism design approaches exploit observations
or reports
of the agent utility functions to construct mechanisms which induce socially optimal outcomes from non-cooperative interaction. In this work we develop a data-driven reinforcement learning (RL) approach to achieve mechanism design with
no analytical, parametric, or distributional knowledge of
agent utilities (Our terminology of RL is consistent with the machine learning literature, where the aim is to achieve data-driven optimization without direct knowledge or estimation of the system parameters.)

Classical mechanism design approaches such as Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG)

Examples include bidding behavior in ad auctions

Operational Mixed-Strategy Equilibria

We distinguish the technical content of the algorithm from its mechanism-design interpretation. Theorems
4
and
3
, together with Algorithm 1, take as input a dataset of observed mixed strategies and return a mechanism minimizing the associated Pareto gap; neither the revealed-preference test of Theorem
4
nor the policy-gradient recursion guarantees of Theorem
3
depend on Nash structure. The mechanism-design conclusion, that a vanishing Pareto gap certifies socially optimal
non-cooperative
play, requires, in addition, that those strategies constitute equilibria of the game induced by
\phi
. Mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium is therefore adopted as an
operational assumption
: it is the classical setting for the design problem, it renders the objective well-posed by guaranteeing (under Assumptions
2
–
4
) the existence of a mechanism that admits a socially optimal mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium, and the exposition is developed in that language. If the observations are instead generated by another behavioral protocol, the same procedure remains well-defined and continues to minimize the Pareto gap of the observed play. What is then obtained is a welfare improvement of that protocol, rather than implementation of social optimality at equilibrium. In all cases the mechanism is fitted to the particular strategies present in the data; we do not attempt to control unobserved alternative equilibria (e.g., in the sense of the price of anarchy).
6
6

6

The price of anarchy measures the discrepancy between socially optimal welfare and that of the
worst-case
Nash equilibrium. Controlling this quantity is a strictly stronger requirement than the one considered here: we optimize the mechanism for the strategies that are observed, and have no access to other (in particular, worst-case) equilibria of the game.

2.3.
Mixed Strategy Mechanism Design

This section discusses the important concept of social optimality in mechanism design, which will form the objective of our RL approach. The goal of mechanism design is broadly to choose the mechanism
o_{\phi}
such that Nash equilibria solutions are simultaneously
globally optimal
, i.e., benefit the entire group. This section motivates the definition of ’globally optimal’ through the social optimality criteria.

2.3.1.
Design Objective. Social Optimality

The standard global welfare condition, also known as the utilitarian condition
(
57
)
, is to maximize the ’social welfare’

(5)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}f^{i}_{\phi}(\mu)

which is simply the sum of agent utility functions. This is referred to as social optimality.

Definition 0 (Mixed-Strategy Social Optimality).

A product measure
\mu
is socially optimal for the game
G_{\text{mixed}}
and mechanism
o_{\phi}
if it maximizes the sum of agent utility expectations:

(6)

\mu\in\arg\max_{\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal{A})}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f^{i}_{\phi}(\mu)=\arg\max_{\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal{A})}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}\sim\mu}\left[f^{i}_{\phi}(\textbf{a})\right]

Notice that social optimality (
6
) is a special case of Pareto-optimality, which maximizes the convex combination
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}f_{\phi}^{i}(\mu)
,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=1,\lambda_{i}\geq 0
. In fact, the results presented in this work can easily be extended to the case where general Pareto-optimal solutions are considered. We exclude presentation and discussion of this generalization for brevity.

Remarks.

-

Rank optimality.
In Appendix
A
we discuss "rank optimality" in which the game outcome maximizes its rank among the agent’s ordinal preference relations. This ordinal preference setting is a useful restriction
(
22
)
,
(
21
)
that arises naturally from human preference relations. We prove that our criteria of social optimality implies the satisfaction of rank optimality. Thus, approaching mechanism design with the social optimality objective (
6
) automatically guarantees that rank optimality will be met in restricted game conditions, enhancing the "optimality" of social optimality beyond aggregate utility maximization. In what follows we focus solely on social optimality as the mechanism design objective since it is more general.

-

Strategic participant generality.
Our formulation is intentionally general: all strategic participants are represented within the common agent set
n
. In auction environments this means that the seller (or auctioneer) is modeled as an additional agent whose utility may encode expected revenue. This differs from much of the auction-theoretic literature, where the seller defines the mechanism but is not treated as a strategic participant. Our representation enables a treatment of mechanism design in which any entity influencing the outcome—seller or buyer—may be incorporated symmetrically within the same multi-agent social welfare metric.

-

Extension to correlated equilibria.

While our current formalism focuses on independent mixed strategies, it can be extended to the more general notion of correlated equilibrium
(
6
)
.
In that case, the agents would play from a joint distribution

\boldsymbol{\mu}(a^{1},\dots,a^{M})
satisfying joint incentive–compatibility conditions

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}[f^{i}(a^{i},a^{-i})]\geq\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}[f^{i}(a_{i}^{\prime},a^{-i})]
for all

Our approach would remain applicable by simply determining a mechanism inducing social optimality

In revealed preference theory, the generalized axiom of revealed preference (GARP) is a key definition that serves as a necessary and sufficient condition for a dataset to be consistent with utility maximization. 3.2.
Modeling Considerations

3.2.1.
Motivation for repeated one-shot dataset collection.

Each data pair
(\mathcal{A}_{\phi,t},\mu_{t})
corresponds to the action space and resultant equilibria strategy of an independent one-shot game. Our formulation should not be interpreted as a repeated–game setting. Specifically, agents best-respond within each stage game without updating across stages. Any learning occurs either prior to the designer initialization, or
within
the stage-game itself through an iterative learning dynamics. At best, then, the relevant information incorporated into agent play in each stage is a complete characterization of opponent’s utilities, inducing a complete-information equilibrium within each stage; our framework readily handles this.
Also, we emphasize that repeated-game equilibria frequently support socially-optimal outcomes
automatically
through reputation and cooperation
(
35
)
, in which case these “good" outcomes are attributable to the repeated structure rather than the mechanism. Thus, by targeting one-shot equilibria, our Pareto-gap objective isolates the
mechanism’s own
causal contribution to welfare. Furthermore, in our model formulation the inter-stage incentive structure of repeated games cannot be expressed since we
isolate incentives (constrained utilities) within each distinct stage game
.

This sequence of independent one-shot games is a standard structural specification in adaptive/online mechanism design settings
(
62
)
, repeated posted-price auctions with myopic buyers
(
17
)
, or repeated Stackelberg security games
(
9
)
. Furthermore, it models observational setups of pertinent multi–agent settings in electronic sensing and communication, e.g., radar–UAV interactions
(
72
)
or resource–allocation networks, where
the environment issues a time–varying constraint
\mathcal{A}_{\phi,t}
(power, bandwidth, detectability, etc.)
while the agents’ internal decision rules (leading to equilibria strategies) remain fixed.
Each interaction can therefore be viewed as an independent realization of a stage game
under a new constraint. This abstraction allows the designer to infer or optimize
the underlying utilities or mechanism parameters from diverse but independent samples,
without needing to model long–horizon learning or adaptation.

3.2.2.
Rationale for equilibrium achievement.

While Nash equilibria are well-motivated in several applications, constructing multi-agent interacting systems from which these equilibria emerge can be difficult. Classical works
(
34
,
40
)
and more recent work
(
13
,
12
)
on learning in Bayesian auction games and on markets with learning agents characterizes conditions under which convergence does or does not occur. The framework of this paper is compatible with these learning dynamics, if they occur as intra-stage procedures which produce a fixed joint strategy in each stage-game, regardless of whether this joint strategy is an equilibrium. Recall the discussion of this applicability to non-equilibrium behavior in Section
1.2
.

3.3.
Modeling Mechanism-Dependent Action Space Structure

In revealed preference theory, it is customary to have a dataset of endogenous inputs (constraints) and resultant outputs (equilibria distributions, in our case). This input-output dataset pairing is essential to the mathematical characterization of a dataset’s consistency with optimality. In this section we provide an assumption on the structure of the imposed constraint functions which determine these actions spaces; this serves as a sufficient specification for "probing" the system which allows
exact
determination of consistency with social optimality (through necessary
and
sufficient conditions), derived in Section
3.5
. Furthermore, this modeling structure allows the constraint specifications to be dependent on the mechanism
\phi

We introduce the following assumption on the structure of constraint functions
\{g_{\phi,t}^{i}(\cdot),t\in[T]\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

Assumption 1 (Constraint Function Structure).

Assume that

a) The constraint functions
\{g_{\phi,t}^{i}:\mathbb{R}^{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}k}\to\mathbb{R}\}
are each concave and increasing in each dimension.

b) For each
i\in[n]
there is some vector
\beta^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that for any
a\in\mathbb{R},t\in[T]
,

x,y\in\{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}k}:g_{\phi,t}^{i}(\gamma-a\beta^{i})=0\}\Rightarrow g_{\phi,t}^{i}(x)=g_{\phi,t}^{i}(y)

i.e., the equivalence class of points in the zero level-set is invariant under shifts of
g_{\phi,t}^{i}(\cdot)
by
\beta^{i}
.

Remark. Modeling
. Assumption
1
will be necessary for the proof of our extended result in Theorem
4
, and is easily satisfied when we, the analyst, have control over the sequential constraint functions
g_{\phi,t}^{i}
. If, so we may generate the constraint functions

as follows:

i)

\{g_{\phi}^{i}:\mathbb{R}^{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}k}\to\mathbb{R}\}

there is some vector
\beta^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that for any

x,y\in\{\gamma:g_{\phi}^{i}(\gamma-a\beta^{i})=0\}\Rightarrow g_{\phi}^{i}(x)=g_{\phi}^{i}(y)

Comparison with alternative optimization methods.

While the SPSA recursion provides the theoretical convergence guarantees necessary for our setting, it is worth noting that other optimizers, such as Evolutionary Strategies (ES), Bayesian Optimization (BO), or stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics (SGLD), could in principle be employed to minimize the Pareto gap. Each offers different trade-offs in terms of sample efficiency and computational cost. ES methods
(
37
)
approximate the gradient via finite-population perturbations and are often effective in low-noise continuous control problems, but their population-based sampling requires

O(p)
, where
\phi\in\Phi\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
, or more function evaluations per iteration. In our mechanism-design setting, each function evaluation entails an equilibria strategy observation, making such methods substantially more expensive than SPSA’s constant-two-query structure. Bayesian optimization approaches
(
68
)
can be highly sample-efficient in low-dimensional problems but scale poorly with

p
due to Gaussian-process kernel inversion and model-update costs. SGLD global minimization guarantees have been analyzed in
(
61
)

4.4.
Robust RL through Relaxed Revealed Preferences

Theorem
3
states that Algorithm
1
globally minimizes the Pareto gap
L(\phi)
; achieving social optimality if the level set
L(\phi)=0
is non-empty. If this level set is empty, Algorithm

Specifically, we provide an equivalence between the Pareto gap value
L(\phi)
and several well-established revealed preference goodness-of-fit metrics, namely "relaxed revealed preferences". Thus, if a mechanism
\phi
induces a positive Pareto gap value

(such that the dataset

is
not

Robust revealed preference metrics are well-established in the microeconomic literature. Afriat
(
2

and Varian
(

argued that full consistency with optimality may be too restrictive hypothesis in empirical applications, and thus introduced several metrics to capture "nearly optimal" behavior. These notions are typically quantified through relaxations of GARP. Thus, our equivalence result allows for an understanding of our algorithmic suboptimality through the lens of these developed frameworks.

4.4.1.
Relaxed Revealed Preference Metrics

In order to capture these metrics in the regime of our nonlinear budget constraint, we may here assume that we work with budget constraints

4.5.
Individual Rationality Guarantees and Repeated-Game Generalizations

Here we present discussions for extending the present framework in two pertinent directions: to guarantee individual rationality conditions, and to generalize beyond the current iterated stage-game structure to repeated games by removing stage-stationarity of the Pareto gap.

4.5.1.
Participation and Individual Rationality

In classical mechanism design,
individual rationality
(IR) ensures that under equilibrium, each agent weakly prefers
participation over abstention, i.e.

(22)

\mathbb{E}_{(a_{i},a_{-i})\sim\mu(\phi)}[f_{\phi}^{i}(a_{i},a_{-i})]\geq c_{i},\quad\forall i,

where
c_{i}
is agent
i
’s utility of non-participation. This notion of individual rationality is readily incorporated into our framework, and can be imposed through the following structural condition:

Assumption 5 (Non-Participation Invariance).

For every agent
i
, there exists a feasible action
\bar{a}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{\phi,t}^{i}
for all
t\in[T]
, such that

(23)

f_{\phi}^{i}(\bar{a}_{i},a_{-i})=c_{i}\,\,\forall\phi\in\Phi,a_{-i}\in\mathcal{A}_{\phi,t}^{-i}

where
c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is the utility of non-participation.

Thus, we require that there exists
at least one
feasible action which induces the non-participation utility, for any mechanism
\phi
and any opponent joint-action
a_{-i}
. Consequently, the utility
f_{\phi}^{i}(\cdot,a_{-i})
under
\bar{a}_{i}
is
invariant
to the mechanism
\phi
and opponent joint-action
a_{-i}
. This assumption is naturally motivated in engineering and economics applications, imposes the individual rationality condition, and does not alter the technical results of this paper:

(1)

Applicability.
Assumption
5
holds when
f_{\phi}^{i}(a_{i},a_{-i})=u_{i}(a_{i},a_{-i};\phi)-k_{i}(a_{i},a_{-i};\phi)
with an interaction benefit
u_{i}
and cost
k_{i}
that vanish at some feasible
\bar{a}_{i}
independently of
a_{-i}
or
\phi
. This is standard for mechanisms governing resource allocation or coordination, where "non-participation" has an unambiguous physical or economic meaning. For instance, the wireless spectrum sharing example in Section
3.4
and the river pollution game in Section
6.2
, fall into this category and thus satisfy Assumption
5
, by choosing non-participation as
\bar{a}_{i}=0
. Then
c_{i}=0
, which is naturally interpreted as the utility of non-participation.

(2)

Individual rationality implication.
(
22
) holds at equilibrium, since if agent
i
’s equilibrium strategy has expected payoff below
c_{i}
, deviating to
\bar{a}_{i}
would strictly increase expected utility, contradicting equilibrium. Hence,
\mathbb{E}_{a_{i}\sim\mu^{i}(\phi),a_{-i}\sim\mu^{-i}(\phi)}[f_{\phi}^{i}(a_{i},a_{-i})]\geq c_{i}
in the equilibria mixed strategy
\mu^{i}(\phi)
, recovering (
22
) in expectation for every
\phi\in\Phi
.

(3)

Compatibility with technical results.
Allowing
\bar{a}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{\phi,t}^{i}
, leaves the action space regularity of Assumptions
1

3
unchanged, and thus Theorem
4
is unaffected. Compatibility with Theorem

follows from the smoothness properties required by Assumption

; the differentiability arguments underlying Theorem

then apply unmodified.

4.5.2.
Beyond stage-stationarity of the Pareto gap

The convergence guarantee of Algorithm 1 (Theorem 2) relies on the fact that,
for a fixed mechanism
\phi
, the induced Pareto gap
L(\phi)
is independent of the stage index
m
and independent of the history of past play. This holds because the stage-wise equilibrium map
\phi_{m}\mapsto\mu_{t}(\phi_{m})
is
stage-stationary
: the distribution of
\hat{\mu}_{t}(\phi_{m,\zeta})
conditional on
\phi_{m,\zeta}=\phi
does not depend on
m
or on the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{m-1}
generated by prior mechanisms and observed equilibria. This is due to the isolation of incentives (constrained utilities) to the structure of each distinct stage-game (
14
) in our model, and is precisely what allows
L(\phi)
to be treated as a fixed, time-invariant objective landscape that Algorithm 1’s stochastic approximation recursion can be shown to converge within.

If agents were permitted to strategize with respect to the trajectory of mechanisms
(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{m})
rather than
\phi_{m}
alone (e.g., to build reputation or anticipate future updates
(
35
)
), the equilibrium map would
become history-dependent and anticipative. The Pareto gap then inherits this dependence and becomes a stochastic,
\mathcal{F}_{m-1}
-measurable object
L(\phi,\mathcal{F}_{m-1})
rather than a fixed function of
\phi
. Theorem
3
does not cover optimization of such a history-dependent non-stationary objective; establishing convergence in that setting would require substantially different tools; we view this as a distinct, promising direction for future work.

5.
Finite Sample Consistency via Distributionally Robust RL

Thus far, our approach achieves
the mechanism design objective
in the asymptotic limit as the number of i.i.d. samples tends to infinity (Theorem
3
). This section considers the case when only a
finite-sample
empirical distribution is available. We employ distributionally robust RL as a principled approach to handle this case, in order to provide guarantees for the full strategies from only finite empirical distributions.
Distributionally robust optimization is an approach that seeks optimal decision-making under uncertainty by minimizing the worst-case scenario across a set of possible probability distributions, ensuring solutions are resilient to variations in data. We utilize this to minimize the Pareto gap over the worst-case distribution in proximity of the empirical distribution, thereby minimizing the Pareto gap of the true fully specified distributions. Our contributions are:

i)

We formulate a distributionally robust objective function which allows us to achieve mechanism design for the
full
(unobserved) mixed strategies from a finite set of
i.i.d.
samples.

ii)

We prove the equivalence between this objective function and a reformulated semi-infinite program, allowing us to employ exchange methods to compute solutions.

iii)

We develop an algorithmic solution, provide convergence rate guarantees, and discuss its interface with Algorithm
1
. In Section
6.2
we provide a numerical example demonstrating this algorithm’s rapid convergence.

5.1.

We formulate a distributionally robust optimization (DRO) problem which will be used to achieve finite-sample consistency.

7.
Conclusion

We discussed a novel reinforcement learning (RL) methodology for achieving the mechanism design objective when the agent utility functions are unknown
and unreported
to the designer. The paper comprised four main results:

(i) We generalized the result of
(
23
)
to incorporate nonlinear budget constraints and mixed strategies
(Theorem
4
)
. This gives necessary and sufficient conditions for observed mixed-strategy Nash equilibria behavior to be consistent with social (Pareto) optimality.

(ii) We constructed a loss function which quantifies the Pareto gap of a set of mixed strategies, i.e., their distance from social optimality. We proved that an RL SPSA algorithm will converge in probability to the set of global minima of this loss function.
This enables a principled test for achievability of social optimality: if social optimality is achievable for the game setup and observational equilibria, Algorithm
1
will achieve it; else, we can
identify
this unachievability, while still converging to a mechanism which is closest to social optimality
. The key distinguishing feature of this RL approach is that we achieve mechanism design
without observations or reports of agent utilities
; this is enabled by our IRL Pareto gap evaluation step.

(iii) We provided an equivalence between the Pareto gap loss function and several robust revealed preference metrics, allowing for algorithmic sub-optimality guarantees in the case when
social optimality is unachievable
.

(iv) We described a distributionally robust RL procedure which achieves
the mechanism design objective
for the full mixed-strategies from only a finite number of i.i.d. samples.

We illustrated the performance of Algorithm
1
and Algorithm
2
numerically in standard non-cooperative game settings.
It would be an interesting future endeavor to modify this algorithmic approach to not only induce Pareto optimality in the empirical dataset, but to steer the dataset towards specific regions in the Pareto frontier.

Appendix A
Design Objective. Rank Optimality

Here we discuss the conceptual motivation for extending the notion of optimality from
cardinal
social optimality, specified by (
10
) through utility functions
f_{\phi}^{i}
, to
ordinal
optimality, as studied in
[
22
]
. We will show that this objective captures a powerful new quantification of optimality, and under reasonable conditions is
subsumed under the condition of social optimality (
6
)
.

Each utility function
f_{\phi}^{i}
corresponds to a partial ordering ("preference profile")
\succ_{i}
over outcomes
o_{\phi}(\cdot)\in O
: for any two joint-actions
\textbf{a}_{1},\textbf{a}_{2}\in\mathcal{A}
, outcome
o_{\phi}(\textbf{a}_{1})
is
preferred
to outcome
o_{\phi}(\textbf{a}_{2})
by agent
i
, denoted
o_{\phi}(\textbf{a}_{1})\succ o_{\phi}(\textbf{a}_{2})
, if and only if
f_{\phi}^{i}(\textbf{a}_{1})>f_{\phi}^{i}(\textbf{a}_{2})
. Now, using these preference orderings, we may construct the following notion of social welfare known as
rank optimality
. Let
\succ=(\succ_{1},\dots,\succ_{n})
be the collection of preference orderings specified by utility functions
f_{\phi}^{i},\,i\in[n]
. Then, for
k\in[D]
, we denote the
k
-rank
of an outcome
o\in O
in
\succ
,
\rnk_{k}(o;\succ)
, as the number of agents having
o\in O
in their top
k
choices:

\rnk_{k}(o;\succ)=|\{j:\pos(\succ_{j},o)\leq k\}|

where
\pos(\succ_{j},o)
denotes the rank of outcome
o
in preference profile
\succ_{j}
. Then we may define the
max-rank
metric:

\maxrnk_{k}(\succ):=\max_{o\in O}\rnk_{k}(o;\succ)

as the maximum possible
k
-
\rnk
over all outcomes for the preference profile
\succ
. Thus, we may define a
rank-optimal
strategy as follows.

Definition 0 (Rank Optimal Strategy).

For a preference profile
\succ=(\succ_{1},\dots,\succ_{n})
and mechanism
\phi
, a joint-action
a
is rank-optimal if

\rnk_{k}(o_{\phi}(\textbf{a});\succ)=\max\rnk_{k}(\succ)\,\,\,\forall\,k\in[D]

Similarly, a mixed-strategy
\mu
is rank-optimal if

\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}\sim\mu}[\rnk_{k}(o_{\phi}(\textbf{a});\succ)]=\max\rnk_{k}(\succ)\,\,\,\forall\,k\in[D]

This notion of rank-optimality is well-motivated as a social welfare design principle for mechanism design
[
22
]
, and may replace the social optimality criterion (
6
) in certain settings. One such setting is when the agents have
ordinal
preferences, i.e., can only compare finitely many outcomes against one another, without quantifying their utility explicitly. In this case, the utility functions
\{f_{\phi}^{i},i\in[n]\}
are constructed synthetically from the preference profile
\succ
, and are regarded as
homogeneous
:

Definition 0 (Homogeneous Utility Functions).

Let
|\mathcal{A}|=D<\infty
, and denote
f_{\phi}^{i}(j),j\in[D]
the utility of agent
i
’s
j
’th preferred outcome, under preference profile
\succ
. Utility functions
\{f_{\phi}^{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
are said to be homogeneous if for every
\phi\in\Phi,i,i^{\prime}\in[n]
, we have
f_{\phi}^{i}(j)=f_{\phi}^{i^{\prime}}(j)
.

Utility functions are homogeneous if each agent assigns the same value to their
j
’th preference, for every
j\in[D]
. This arises out of straightforward construction of utility functions
f_{\phi}^{i}(\cdot)
from a finite preference profile
\succ
.

The setting of ordinal preference relations and homogeneous utility functions is an important practical setting, and can easily be recovered as a special case of the general game form (
2
). In this special case, the mechanism design goal should be to find a mechanism inducing
max-rank
strategies. We now show that this is an immediate consequence of our general social optimality objective (
6
).

Lemma 0 (Social Optimality Induces Rank Optimality).

Assume a finite joint-action space
\mathcal{A}
:
|\mathcal{A}|=D<\infty
and homogeneous utility functions
\{f_{\phi}^{i}(\cdot)\}_{i\in[n]}
inducing preference profile
\succ=(\succ_{1},\dots,\succ_{n})
. Assume the relation (
6
) holds. Then, we have

(34)

\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}\sim\mu}[\rnk_{i}(\textbf{a};\succ)]=\max\rnk_{i}(\succ)\,\,\forall i\in[D]

Thus, the goal of rank-optimality for the setting of finite action space and ordinal preferences is subsumed under our general mechanism design goal of social optimality (
7
).

, and the aggregate group consumption is given by
\beta_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{t}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}
, subject to price vector (probe)
\alpha_{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}
. It is assumed that the preferences of each member
i
can be represented by a non-satiated and non-decreasing utility function
U^{i}(\beta),\ \beta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}
.

For each
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
, suppose an analyst observes probe signals
\alpha_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}
, aggregate consumption
\beta_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\beta_{t}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and "assignable quantities"
\hat{\beta}_{t}^{i}\leq\beta_{t}^{i}\ \forall i\in\{1,\dots,M\}
. The assignable quantity
\hat{\beta}_{t}^{i}
represents observed consumption of some subset of the quantities consumed by individual
i
at time
t
, and hence it is elementwise no greater than the total consumption vector
\beta_{t}^{i}
. The apparatus by which these assignable quantities are observed, and the amount observed, varies by application. We denote this dataset as

(44)

\mathcal{D}=\{\alpha_{t},\beta_{t},\{\hat{\beta}_{t}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{M},\ t\in\{1,\dots,T\}\}

We emphasize that the true individual consumption vectors
\beta_{t}^{i}
are hidden. Given this dataset,
[
23
]
provides necessary and sufficient conditions for consistency with Pareto-optimality. To formulate these conditions, we need one more definition:
For each observation
t
,
feasible personalized quantities

q_{t}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N},\ i=1,\dots,M
satisfy
q_{t}^{i}\geq\hat{\beta}_{t}^{i}\ \forall i
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{t}^{i}=\beta_{t}
.

Now we provide the main result of
[
23
]
which states the equivalence between a consistency of dataset
\mathcal{D}
with coordination and existence of a non-empty feasible region of a set of inequalities.

Theorem 1.

[
23
]

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a set of observations. The following are equivalent:

(1)

there exist a set of
M
concave and continuous utility functions
U^{1},\dots,U^{m}
such that
\mathcal{D}
is consistent with Pareto-optimality.

(2)

there exist feasible personalized quantities
q_{t}^{i}
and numbers
u_{j}^{i}>0,\lambda_{j}^{i}>0
such that for all
s,t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
:

(45)

u_{s}^{i}-u_{t}^{i}\leq\lambda_{t}^{i}[\alpha_{t}^{\prime}q_{s}^{i}-\alpha_{t}^{\prime}q_{t}^{i}]

for each member
i=1,\dots,M
.

Proof.

See Appendix A.1 of
[
23
]
.
∎

Lemma 0.

Suppose the conditions of Theorem
1
hold. Explicit monotone and concave utility functions that rationalize the dataset by satisfying Pareto optimality are given by

(46)

\hat{U}^{i}(\beta)=\min_{t\in\{1,\dots,T\}}\{u_{t}^{i}+\lambda_{t}^{i}\alpha_{t}^{\prime}(\beta-q_{t}^{i})\}

Proof.

This follows immediately by application of Afriat’s Theorem
[
1
]
.
∎

Thus if the feasible region of (
45
) is nonempty, then monotone concave utility functions which rationalize the dataset can be reconstructed using (
46
).

Implementing this feasibility test (
45
) in practice presents a problem however, because the set
\{q_{t}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
is
unobserved
. To overcome this,
[
23
]
present an equivalent mixed-integer linear program (MILP) formulation which allows for practical computation of this coordination test.

Theorem 3.

[
23
]

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a set of observations. There exists a set of
n
concave and continuous utility functions
U^{1},\dots,U^{M}
such that
\mathcal{D}
is consistent with coordination if and only if there exist
q_{t}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}
,
\eta_{t}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and
x_{st}^{i}\in\{0,1\},i=1,\dots,M
that satisfy

i)

\sum_{i=1}^{M}q_{t}^{i}=\beta_{t}
and
q_{t}^{i}\geq\hat{\beta}_{t}^{i}

ii)

\eta_{t}^{i}=\alpha_{t}^{\prime}q_{t}^{i}

iii)

\eta_{s}^{i}-\alpha_{t}^{\prime}q_{t}^{i}<y_{s}x_{st}^{i}

iv)

x_{su}^{i}+x_{ut}^{i}\leq 1+x_{st}^{i}

v)

\eta_{t}^{i}-\alpha_{t}^{\prime}q_{t}^{i}\leq y_{t}(1-x_{st}^{i})

where
y_{t}=\alpha_{t}^{\prime}\beta_{t}
is the group consumption cost at time
t
.

Proof.

See Proposition 2 of
[
23
]
.
∎
