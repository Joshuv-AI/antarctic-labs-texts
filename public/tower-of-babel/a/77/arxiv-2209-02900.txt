Cross-calibration of atomic pressure sensors and deviation from quantum diffractive collision universality for light particles
Pinrui Shen 1 , Erik Frieling 1 , Katherine R. Herperger 1 , Denis Uhland 1 , Riley A. Stewart 1 , Avinash Deshmukh 1 , Roman V. Krems 2 , James L. Booth 3 , and Kirk W. Madison 1

Abstract
The total room-temperature, velocity-averaged cross section for atom-atom and atom-molecule collisions is well approximated by a universal function depending only on the magnitude of the leading order dispersion coefficient, C 6 C_{6} . This feature of the total cross section together with the universal function for the energy distribution transferred by glancing angle collisions ( p QDU6 p_{\rm{QDU6}} ) can be used to empirically determine the total collision cross section and realize a self-calibrating, vacuum pressure standard. This was previously validated for Rb+N 2 and Rb+Rb collisions. However, the post-collision energy distribution is expected to deviate from p QDU6 p_{\rm{QDU6}} in the limit of small C 6 C_{6} and small reduced mass. Here we observe this deviation experimentally by performing a direct cross-species loss rate comparison between Rb+H 2 and Li+H 2 and using the ab initio value of ⟨ σ tot ​ v ⟩ Li + H 2 \langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle_{\rm{Li+H}_{2}} . We find a velocity averaged total collision cross section ratio, R = ⟨ σ tot ​ v ⟩ Li + H 2 : ⟨ σ tot ​ v ⟩ Rb + H 2 = 0.83 ​ ( 5 ) R=\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle_{\rm{Li+H}_{2}}:\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle_{\rm{Rb+H}_{2}}=0.83(5) . Based on an ab initio computation of ⟨ σ tot ​ v ⟩ Li + H 2 = 3.13 ​ ( 6 ) × 10 − 15 \langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle_{\rm{Li+H}_{2}}=3.13(6)\times 10^{-15} m 3 /s, we deduce ⟨ σ tot ​ v ⟩ Rb + H 2 = 3.8 ​ ( 2 ) × 10 − 15 \langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle_{\rm{Rb+H}_{2}}=3.8(2)\times 10^{-15} m 3 /s, in agreement with a Rb+H 2 ab initio value of ⟨ σ tot ​ v ⟩ Rb + H 2 = 3.57 × 10 − 15 ​ m 3 / s \langle\sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}v\rangle_{\mathrm{Rb+H_{2}}}=3.57\times 10^{-15}\mathrm{m}^{3}/\mathrm{s} . By contrast, fitting the Rb+H 2 loss rate as a function of trap depth to the universal function we find ⟨ σ tot ​ v ⟩ Rb + H 2 = 5.52 ​ ( 9 ) × 10 − 15 \langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle_{\rm{Rb+H}_{2}}=5.52(9)\times 10^{-15} m 3 /s. Finally, this work demonstrates how to perform a cross-calibration of sensor atoms to extend and enhance the cold atom based pressure sensor.

For example, the background gas density or pressure can be determined by observing loss of trapped atoms induced by collisions with ambient atoms or molecules.
In general, the quantitative determination of the background gas density or pressure requires the knowledge of the thermally averaged total collision cross section between the trapped atoms and all atoms or molecules in the background gas.

The total collision cross sections can be obtained from quantum scattering calculations
Makrides et al. 2019
;
Makrides et al. 2020
;
Makrides et al. 2022a
;
Makrides et al. 2022b
. However, such calculations are affected by the uncertainty in the underlying potential energy surfaces (PESs) and are limited to molecular species with a small number of active degrees of freedom. As an alternative, the total collision cross sections can be obtained from measurements with a background gas of known density. However, this requires an independent calibration of gas density.

We have recently demonstrated a different approach to determine the collision cross section by measuring the dependence of loss of trapped atoms on the trap depth.
Such measurements rely on the universal function (
p_{\rm{QDU6}}
) for the energy distribution imparted to the sensor atom by quantum diffractive collisions mediated by van der Waals interactions
Booth et al. 2019
.
This was used to demonstrate a cold-atom based, self-calibrating, primary vacuum pressure standard
Booth et al. 2019
;
Madison et al. 2018
;
Shen et al. 2020
;
Shen et al. 2021
. The validity of this pressure standard was proven for Rb+N
2
collisions by comparison with an orifice flow pressure standard using an ion gauge calibrated for N
2
using both standards. The underlying principle, the universality of quantum diffractive collisions as manifested in the universality of the
p_{\rm{QDU6}}
function, has also been independently validated for Rb+Rb collisions
Stewart et al. 2022
.

This collision universality relies on the collision cross section being dominated by scattering determined by the long range part of PES, parameterized by
C_{6}
, and on the velocity averaging of the cross-section inherent in the trap loss measurement process. The velocity averaging removes the oscillatory variation of the cross section with collision energy. These “glory” oscillations arise from interference between the scattering amplitudes due to the short- and long-range parts of the interaction potential
Child 1974
. Therefore, deviations from the universal prediction are expected in the limit of small reduced mass and small
C_{6}

Booth et al. 2019
.
For collisions with a small reduced mass, the glory oscillation amplitude and period can become so large that the total cross section does not average to the underlying trend set by the long-range interaction.
This effect is compounded by the relatively small atomic polarizabilities of low mass collision partners yielding relatively low
C_{6}
values, enhancing the effects of the core repulsion and compromising the universal description.

The main goal of this work is to obtain experimental evidence for the deviation of the total collision cross section for light collision partners from the values derived from the universal
p_{\rm{QDU6}}
function. In order to do this, we require an independent measurement of the total collision cross section.
We consider Rb+H
2
collisions and develop an experiment to obtain the collision cross section independently of
p_{\rm{QDU6}}
by cross-calibration with measurements for another trapped sensor atom. Specifically, we present measurements utilizing Rb and Li sensor atoms, both exposed to the same H
2
gas. We then use known information on Li+H
2
collision cross sections to obtain the total collision cross section for Rb+H
2
collisions. The results obtained experimentally are confirmed by rigorous quantum scattering calculations. We show that the results thus obtained are inconsistent with the value of the cross section obtained from the
p_{\rm{QDU6}}
function, thus demonstrating the departure of Rb+H
2
diffractive collisions from universality.

The trap loss rate measurements are inherently averaged over the Maxwell-Boltzmann (MB) speed distribution of the room temperature background gas (the dotted traces in Figure

When this average encompasses many oscillation periods and the oscillation amplitudes are small, their effect is minimized and the information about the short range PES carried by the glory oscillations is removed.

Since the velocity-averaged loss cross section asymptotically approaches the velocity-averaged total cross section as the trap depth goes to zero,

Because universality results from the velocity averaging, the parameter regime in which this universality is expected to break down is small
C_{6}

Booth et al. 2019
explored the robustness of this universality for the case of Rb-Ar collisions by varying the PES at short range (thus changing the relative magnitudes of the short and long range scattering contributions) and by varying the test gas temperature, the regime of low reduced mass was only considered analytically and only briefly. Therefore, this work focuses on the low reduced mass complexes Rb-H
2
and Li-H
2
. A full study of the parameter regimes where universality holds is needed; however, such a study is beyond the scope of this paper.

Device (B)

This apparatus was used to perform loss rate measurements of both Rb and Li atoms exposed to the same H

In device (B), effusive ovens emit thermal, parallel atomic beams of the two species – Rb and Li – which are subsequently decelerated and cooled in a Zeeman slower region by a counter-propagating bi-chromatic optical beam.

IV.1
Trap loss measurements of Rb exposed to H
2

Using apparatus A, we performed an extensive study of the trap loss rate versus trap depth of
87
Rb atoms exposed to a H
2
background gas over a much wider range of depths than previously studied
Shen et al. 2020

In particular, we made loss rate measurements from 0.5 mK to 6 mK, in an effort to define the shape of the loss rate versus trap depth curve more precisely and facilitate a clearer comparison with the universal prediction. However, as the trap depth increases, the collision-induced heating of the trapped ensemble becomes more pronounced, confounding the loss rate measurements. Here we performed additional measurements and data analysis to properly account for this effect.

Collisions that fail to eject sensor atoms from the trap change the sensor ensemble energy distribution over time
Beijerinck 2000a
;
Beijerinck 2000b
. Because the effective trap depth depends on the sensor-atom energy distribution in the trap, this results in a time-dependent and monotonically decreasing effective trap depth. If not accounted for, this leads to a systematic overestimate of the measured loss rate with trap depth and, consequently, a systematic underestimate in the deduced
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle
value.

Although the fitting uncertainty for
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle
decreases as the range of trap depths increases, the systematic error caused by unaccounted sensor-ensemble heating will increase with trap depth. We have found that the ensemble heating error becomes dominant once the scaled trap depth,
U/U_{\rm{d}}
, exceeds 0.3
Shen et al. 2020
. Therefore, the uncertainty in determining
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle
is limited by ensemble heating. To resolve this problem and eliminate the resulting systematic uncertainty, we apply a correction to the trap depth by measuring the energy distribution of the ensemble as a function of the hold time. This allows us to infer the correct effective trap depth as a function of the hold time (details are provided in Appendix
A
).
After properly accounting for ensemble heating, we obtained corrected values of
\langle\sigma_{\rm{loss}}(U)\ v\rangle/\rm{i_{\rm{g}}}
as a function of the trap depth. For each trap depth, we repeated the measurements for three times and we repeated the whole measurement sequence on three different calendar dates. The data are shown in Fig.

V
Cross-Species Calibration

The application of cold atom trap losses to pressure measurements is an exciting realization of a fully quantum pressure sensor. The key ingredient for this sensor is the determination of the velocity-averaged total collision cross-section between the trapped species and the background gas,
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle
. There are three methods for determining these quantities: calibration of the loss rate against a known pressure,
ab initio
quantum scattering computation of
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle
, or relying on the universality description of the quantum diffractive collisions. No single method can span the entire range of collision partners. Calibration against a known background pressure can be implemented for a limited number of inert gas species where the pressure can be determined independently using an orifice flow standard. The
ab initio
QS computation technique may be limited to collisions between light species by the computation complexity associated with accurately finding the PES for many-electron systems. The universality approach, as shown here, is inaccurate for collisions between low reduced mass and low polarizability collision partners. To overcome its limitations, as we have demonstrated in this study, we leveraged a cross-species calibration for the loss rate coefficients. While we have calibrated
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle_{\rm{Rb+H}_{2}}
to the
ab initio
value of
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle_{\rm{Li+H}_{2}}
, the inverse method can readily be applied. Namely, when low mass species such as Li are used as sensor atoms, it is unlikely that the quantum diffractive universality description will be applicable to any collision induced loss measurements, regardless of the collision partner. The strength of using trapped Li sensor atoms, the ability to perform precise QS computations of
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle
, is realized for only a handful of particles. However, the Li cross sections can be calibrated against those for a heavy sensor atom such as Rb, where the QDU technique is available for a wide range of background gas collisions, including other significant vacuum constituents such as N
2
and Ar. In short, cross-species calibration allows us to exploit the strengths of each sensor atom to effect an optimum quantum pressure sensor/standard.

VI
Conclusions

The main goal of this work has been to demonstrate, experimentally, an anticipated deviation of the total collision cross section for light collision partners from the predictions of the universal
p_{\rm{QDU6}}
function
Booth et al. 2019
;
Shen et al. 2020
;
Shen et al. 2021
. We have carefully characterized the loss rate coefficient,
\left<\sigma_{\rm{loss}}\,v\right>
, as a function of trap depth for Rb-H
2
collisions and fit these data to the universal prediction. This yielded a value of
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle_{\rm{Rb+H}_{2}}=5.52(9)\times 10^{-15}
m
3
/s. Next, we implemented a ratiometric technique which compared the loss rates of Li atoms and Rb atoms, confined in very shallow magnetic traps, simultaneously exposed to background H
2
. The choice of Li for cross-species calibration is motivated by the simplicity of Li - H
2
interactions, which makes highly accurate predictions of Li + H
2
scattering cross sections feasible. The
ab initio
predictions of cross sections for Li + H
2
collisions at room temperature were previously published by other authors
Makrides et al. 2019
;
Makrides et al. 2022a
. To ensure the accuracy of our results, in the present work, we have recomputed the rate coefficients for Li + H
2
collisions,
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle_{\rm{Li+H}_{2}}=3.101(6)\times 10^{-15}
m
3
/s, in good agreement with the value of
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle_{\rm{Li+H}_{2}}=3.13(6)\times 10^{-15}
m
3
/s from Refs.
Makrides et al. 2019
;
Makrides et al. 2022a
. Under our experimental conditions, the loss rate ratio is
R=\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle_{\rm{Li+H}_{2}}:\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle_{\rm{Rb+H}_{2}}=0.83(5)
, yielding
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle_{\rm{Rb+H}_{2}}=3.8(2)\times 10^{-15}
m
3
/s when calibrated to the Li-H
2
total collision cross section value. We have also computed the cross sections and rate coefficients for Rb + H
2
collisions using the PES from
Upadhyay et al. 2019
, yielding
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle_{\rm{Rb+H}_{2}}=3.60(7)\times 10^{-15}
m
3
/s. Thus, the
ab initio
value of
R=0.869
agrees with the experimentally determined value and verifies that the experimental value for
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle_{\rm{Rb+H}_{2}}
deviates from the value derived from the universal function, which would predict
R=0.567(3)
.

The universality of quantum diffractive collisions was previously exploited to demonstrate self-calibrating, primary vacuum pressure standard based on trapped Rb atoms
Booth et al. 2019
;
Madison et al. 2018
;
Shen et al. 2020
;
Shen et al. 2021
.
The energy distribution function
p_{\rm{QDU6}}
was shown to be universal for collisions of neutral species because diffractive collisions are predominantly determined by the long-range part of PES and because the dependence of the total scattering cross section on short-range interactions is removed by Maxwell-Boltzmann averaging. However, both the relative contribution of long-range glancing collisions and the effect of Maxwell-Boltzmann averaging are expected to diminish for species with small mass and, consequently, small polarizability.

The present work demonstrates this expected departure of Rb + H
2
diffractive collisions from universality. We have shown that this limitation can be overcome by performing a cross-calibration of
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle_{\rm{Rb+H}_{2}}
to
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle_{\rm{Li+H}_{2}}
. However, we recognize that there is a limited number of collision partners which are amenable to this
ab initio
approach. This has several important implications:
First, we believe that heavy polarizable atoms may be a preferred choice of pressure sensors. These can be calibrated for a wide range of collision partners via the universality method while light collision partners, such as H
2
and He, can readily be calibrated against other sensor species, such as Li, for which
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle
can be computed accurately. Second, the inverse method, namely a lower mass, lower polarizability sensor atom, like Li, can be calibrated to a heavier mass sensor atom (such as Rb) for a wide range of collision partners accessible to the universal quantum diffractive collision formalism.
Finally, our work brings to light the necessity of assessing the limitations of universality of quantum diffractive collisions for light background species as well as light sensor atoms.

Appendix A
Heating rate corrections

In this section, we describe how ensemble heating is accounted for in our determination of
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle
.
For clarity, we repeat here the expression in Eq.
10
describing the fraction of
sensor atoms remaining in the MT at times
t_{j}
and
t_{k}
.

\displaystyle\ln\Big[\frac{f_{j}(t_{j})}{f_{k}(t_{k})}\Big]=\Gamma_{\rm{loss}}(t_{k}-t_{j})+\Gamma_{0}(t_{k}-t_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{\overline{\langle\sigma_{\rm{loss}}(U)\ v\rangle}\Big[(P_{k}-P_{0})t_{k}-(P_{j}-P_{0})t_{j}\Big]}{\rm{i_{\rm{g}}}k_{\rm{B}}T}+\Gamma_{0}(t_{k}-t_{j}).

(16)

Here, we have replaced
\langle\sigma_{\rm{loss}}(U)\ v\rangle
with
\overline{\langle\sigma_{\rm{loss}}(U)\ v\rangle}
, indicating that the rate coefficient, modeled by the universal function in Eq.
4
, is averaged over the energy distribution of the ensemble,
\rho(E)

Shen et al. 2021
. This accounts for the trap depth of each atom in the ensemble with total energy
E\neq 0
being different than the maximum trap depth,
E_{\mathrm{max}}
.

\displaystyle\overline{\langle\sigma_{\rm{loss}}(U)\ v\rangle}=\frac{\int_{0}^{E_{\rm{max}}}\left<\sigma_{\rm{loss}}(E_{\rm{max}}-E)\ v\right>\rho(E)dE}{\int_{0}^{E_{\rm{max}}}\rho(E)dE}.

(17)

We can now include collision-induced ensemble heating by explicitly writing the ensemble energy distribution as a time dependent function,
\rho(E,t)
, and evaluating the integrated loss rate coefficient from zero time to the measurement time
t
. In this case, the ratio of the remaining atoms satisfies

\displaystyle\ln\Bigg[\frac{f_{j}(t_{j})}{f_{k}(t_{k})}\Bigg]-\Gamma_{0}(t_{k}-t_{j})

\displaystyle\hskip-120.0pt=\frac{(P_{k}-P_{0})}{\rm{i_{\rm{g}}}k_{\rm{B}}T}\int_{0}^{t_{k}}\frac{\int_{0}^{E_{\rm{max}}}\left<\sigma_{\rm{loss}}(E_{\rm{max}}-E)\ v\right>\rho(E,t)dE}{\int_{0}^{E_{\rm{max}}}\rho(E,t)dE}dt

\displaystyle\hskip-115.0pt-\frac{(P_{j}-P_{0})}{\rm{i_{\rm{g}}}k_{\rm{B}}T}\int_{0}^{t_{j}}\frac{\int_{0}^{E_{\rm{max}}}\left<\sigma_{\rm{loss}}(E_{\rm{max}}-E)\ v\right>\rho(E,t)dE}{\int_{0}^{E_{\rm{max}}}\rho(E,t)dE}dt.

To simplify the expression, we can factor out the loss rate coefficient at zero time,
\overline{\langle\sigma_{\rm{loss}}(U)\ v\rangle}_{t=0}
, which gives the following final expression,

\displaystyle\ln\Bigg[\frac{f_{j}(t_{j})}{f_{k}(t_{k})}\Bigg]-\Gamma_{0}(t_{k}-t_{j})

\displaystyle\hskip-110.0pt=\frac{\overline{\langle\sigma_{\rm{loss}}(U)\ v\rangle}_{t=0}}{\rm{i_{\rm{g}}}k_{\rm{B}}T}\Big[(P_{k}-P_{0})H(t_{k})-(P_{j}-P_{0})H(t_{j})\Big],

where we define the heating factor as,

\displaystyle H(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}\frac{\int_{0}^{E_{\rm{max}}}\left<\sigma_{\rm{loss}}(E_{\rm{max}}-E)\ v\right>\rho(E,t)dE}{\int_{0}^{E_{\rm{max}}}\rho(E,t)dE}dt}{\frac{\int_{0}^{E_{\rm{max}}}\left<\sigma_{\rm{loss}}(E_{\rm{max}}-E)\ v\right>\rho(E,t=0)dE}{\int_{0}^{E_{\rm{max}}}\rho(E,t=0)dE}}

(20)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}\frac{\int_{0}^{E_{\rm{max}}}\left<\sigma_{\rm{loss}}(E_{\rm{max}}-E)\ v\right>\rho(E,t)dE}{\int_{0}^{E_{\rm{max}}}\rho(E,t)dE}dt}{\overline{\langle\sigma_{\rm{loss}}(U)\ v\rangle}_{t=0}}.

In order to evaluate these expressions, we measured the sensor ensemble energy distribution as a function of the hold time. Specifically, we determined
\rho(E,t)
from the measured cumulative energy distribution of the atoms in the trap. This was done by measuring the number of atoms remaining in the trap after ejecting atoms above an energy cutoff using a radio-frequency field as described in Ref.
Shen et al. 2020
. We found that the distribution is well approximated by a Maxwell Boltzmann distribution with an energy offset,

\displaystyle\rho(E,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-40.0pt\Theta(E-E_{\rm{min}})\cdot 2\left(\frac{E-E_{\rm{min}}}{\pi}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{1}{k_{B}T(t)}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}e^{-\frac{E-E_{\rm{min}}}{k_{B}T(t)}}.

We measured the evolution of
\rho(E,t)
, specifically the variation of
T
and
E_{\mathrm{min}}
with hold time, at the two different trapping currents used in this study,
I=60
A and
I=200
A. We observed that
E_{\mathrm{min}}
was constant and that the temperature varied linearly in time as shown in figure
6
. The ensemble temperature is modeled by
T(t)=T_{0}+mt
.

The experimentally determined ensemble energy distribution can then be used to evaluate the integrals in Eq.
20
to determine the heating factor.
Using this, we can solve for the value of
\overline{\langle\sigma_{\rm{loss}}(U)\ v\rangle}_{t=0}/\rm{i_{\rm{g}}}
, by plugging the computed heating factors into Eq.
,

\displaystyle\frac{\overline{\langle\sigma_{\rm{loss}}(U)\ v\rangle}_{t=0}}{\rm{i_{\rm{g}}}k_{\rm{B}}T}=\frac{\ln\Big[\frac{f_{j}(t_{j})}{f_{k}(t_{k})}\Big]-\Gamma_{0}(t_{k}-t_{j})}{\Big[(P_{k}-P_{0})H(t_{k})-(P_{j}-P_{0})H(t_{j})\Big]}.

(22)

Figure 6
:
The measured temperature of the sensor ensemble versus the holding time for atoms trapped in
F=2
state at the magnetic trapping current,
I=60
A (open blue squares) and
I=200
A (blue filled squares). The ensemble temperature is observed to increase linearly with the holding time. The dashed lines are linear fits to the data,
T(t)=T_{0}+mt
, with
T_{0}=377(26),944(69)\;\mu

K/s respectively.

However, the right-hand side of Eq.
22
also depends on the unknown parameter,
\langle\sigma_{\rm{loss}}(U)\ v\rangle
(included in the heating factor). This necessitates a self-consistent method to find the solution of Eq.
22
. To achieve this, we used the following iteration fitting method to determine the final
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle
and
\rm{i_{\rm{g}}}
values:

1.

We pick an initial guess value for
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle
to determine a guess for the
\langle\sigma_{\rm{loss}}(U)\ v\rangle
function and feed this
into the expression for
H
, Eq.
20
, to compute the values of
H(t_{j})
and
H(t_{k})
for the right-hand side of Eq.
22
.

2.

Using the computed
H
values, we solve for the left-hand side,
\frac{\overline{\langle\sigma_{\rm{loss}}(U)\ v\rangle}_{t=0}}{\rm{i_{\rm{g}}}}
, of Eq.
22
.

3.

We then fit the computed results,
\frac{\overline{\langle\sigma_{\rm{loss}}(U)\ v\rangle}_{t=0}}{\rm{i_{\rm{g}}}}
, in step 2 to the universal fitting function to obtain fitted values
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle
and
\rm{i_{\rm{g}}}
.

4.

We replace the initial guessed value for
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle
with the newly fitted result and repeat the steps from 2 to 4 until the fitted
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle
converges.

We have found this iteration method works well and observed a convergence of the
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle
value after 3 steps even when the initial guess was off by a factor of 6, as illustrated by the simulated results shown in figure
7
. Convergence happens because if the initial guess of
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle
is overestimated (underestimated), the heating rate will also be overestimated (underestimated) which leads to a smaller (larger) fitted
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle
result. Therefore, the guessed value at each iteration step will be closer to the true value. Using this method we could determine the
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle
value with systematic errors from heating removed.

Figure 7
:
Fitted
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle
results as a function of the iteration step for atoms trapped in
F=2
state. The
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle
value at step zero represents the value of the initial guess. Here the initial guesses are varied by more than a factor of 6 and yet they all converge to the same
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle
value,
5.49\times 10^{-15}\rm{m}^{3}/\rm{s}
.

Appendix B
Quantum Scattering Calculations

To describe the quantum scattering dynamics for Li+
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and Rb+
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
collisions, we solve the time-independent Schrödinger equation using the coupled channel approach of Arthurs and Dalgarno
Arthurs and Dalgarno 1960
.

We represent the interaction potential energy of both Li+
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and Rb+
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
as a Legendre polynomial expansion,

U(R,\theta)=\sum_{\lambda}U_{\lambda}(R)P_{\lambda}(\mathrm{cos}\theta),

(23)

where
R
is the magnitude of the centre of mass (COM) of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
to the COM of the atom, and
\theta
is the angle between
\vec{R}
and the molecular axis.
For Li+
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, the coefficients
U_{\lambda}(R)
are provided by Makrides
et al.
for
\lambda=0,2,4
and
R<20
a.u.
Makrides et al. 2019
;
Makrides et al. 2022a

The Rb+
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
interaction potential
{U}(R,\theta)
from Ref.
Upadhyay et al. 2019
is used with permission from Upadhyay
et al.

For both systems, we employed a Degli-Esposti-Werner function,

\begin{split}{f(x)}&{=(g_{0}+g_{1}x+g_{2}x^{2}+g_{3}x^{3}+g_{4}x^{4}+g_{5}x^{5})}\\
&{\times\mathrm{exp}(-a_{1}x-a_{2})-\frac{1}{2}(1+\mathrm{tanh}(1+tx))}\\
&{\times(C_{6}x^{-6}+C_{8}x^{-8}+C_{10}x^{-10}),}\end{split}

(24)

in order to interpolate and extrapolate the data
Esposti and Werner 1990
.

The collision cross sections are computed for energies from 0.1 cm
-1
to 2000 cm
-1
.
Calculations for a given initial rotational state
N_{i}
were performed seperately, for
N_{i}=0-6
.
Additionally, to account for the two spin isomers of molecular Hydrogen, calculations with orthohydrogen can only have odd values of the
N
(1,3,…) and parahydrogen can only have even values of
N
(0,2,…). This is because protons are fermions which require an antisymmetric wavefunction; since orthohydrogen has symmetric nuclear spin, it must have antisymmetric rotational wavefunctions, and since parahydrogen has antisymmetric nuclear spin, it must have symmetric rotational wavefunctions.

For Li+
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, a basis of
J=0-125
is employed. A calculation with a given initial state
N_{i}
, employs a basis with the maximum rotational quantum number
N_{\mathrm{max}}
=12. For the
R
-propagaton procedure, we employ a grid from 2 a.u. to 50 a.u. with 0.0025 a.u. spacing.

For Rb+
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, we use a basis with
J_{\mathrm{max}}=300
and
N_{\mathrm{max}}
=12. The
R
-propagation grid goes from 4 a.u. to 120 a.u. for collision energies greater than 1 cm
-1
, and 4 a.u. to 250 a.u. for energies less than 1 cm
-1
.

:
Total cross-section in log-log format plotted as a function of velocity for Li+

\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

N_{i}

Given the total cross-section
\sigma_{N_{i}}(E_{\mathrm{col}})
from the scattering calculations (see Figure
8
), our goal is to determine the averaged thermalized rate coefficient

K_{\mathrm{all}}(T)=\frac{\Gamma}{n_{\mathrm{BG}}},

(25)

where
\Gamma
is the trap loss rate and
n_{\mathrm{BG}}
is the background gas density. We first calculate the total collision rate coefficient
\langle\sigma v\rangle_{N_{i}}
for a given initial state by integrating the product of the cross-section and the velocity
v
of the molecule over the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution for collision energy
p(E_{\mathrm{col}})
,

\langle\sigma v\rangle_{N_{i}}\;=\int\sigma(E_{\mathrm{col}})v_{N_{i}}p(E_{\mathrm{col}})dE_{\mathrm{col}}

(26)

where

p(E_{\mathrm{col}})=2\sqrt{\frac{E_{\mathrm{col}}}{\pi}}\left(\frac{1}{k_{B}T}\right)^{3/2}\mathrm{exp}\left(-\frac{E_{\mathrm{col}}}{k_{B}T}\right).

(27)

Next, we let
L_{N_{i}}(T)
(see Figure
9
) denote the thermally-averaged rate coefficient for a given initial state,

\begin{split}L_{N_{i}}(T)=\frac{\mathrm{exp}(-E_{N_{i}}/k_{B}T)}{Z}\langle\sigma v\rangle_{N_{i}},\end{split}

(28)

which includes the partition function

Z=\sum_{N}(2N+1)(2-(-1)^{N})\mathrm{exp}(-E_{N}/k_{B}T)

(29)

for all possible initial states
N=0\rightarrow 6
. In the above equation,
(2-(-1)^{N})
accounts for the ratio 3:1 of orthohydrogen to parahydrogen. Then, the averaged thermalized rate coefficient is

\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle=\sum_{N_{i}}(2N_{i}+1)(2-(-1)^{N_{i}})\times L_{N_{i}}(T).

(30)

For room temperature
T=300
K, we calculated
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle\approx 3.570\times 10^{-15}\;\mathrm{m}^{3}/\mathrm{s}
for Rb+
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle\approx 3.101\times 10^{-15}
m
3
/s for Li+
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, converged to four significant figures. Then, the ratio

\begin{split}R&=\frac{\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle^{\mathrm{Li}+{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}}(T=300~\mathrm{K})}{\langle\sigma_{\rm{tot}}\,v\rangle^{\mathrm{Rb}+{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}}(T=300~\mathrm{K})}\\
&=\frac{3.101\times 10^{-15}\;\mathrm{m}^{3}/\mathrm{s}}{3.570\times 10^{-15}\;\mathrm{m}^{3}/\mathrm{s}}\\
&=0.8686\end{split}

(31)

is within the error bars of the experiment.
