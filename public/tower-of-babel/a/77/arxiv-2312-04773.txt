Rational discrete analytic functions on a rhombic lattice
Daniel Alpay, Zubayir H. Kazi, Mariana Tecalero, Dan Volok

Abstract
. A novel basis of discrete analytic polynomials on a rhombic lattice is introduced and the associated convolution product is studied. A class of discrete analytic functions that are rational with respect to this product is also described.



AMS Classification: 30G25

Keywords:
Discrete analytic functions, rational function

1.
Introduction

Discrete complex analysis has drawn much attention in the recent years; see for instance
[
14
]
and
[
15
]
. The first advances in this field are associated with the works of J. Ferrand and R. J. Duffin (
[
9
]
,
[
7
]
) who studied certain discretizations of complex analytic functions on the integer lattice in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}.
From the beginning, one of the basic issues that arose in the investigation of discrete analytic (DA for short) functions was that the usual point-wise product of functions does not preserve discrete analyticity. In spite of this, DA polynomials are well enough understood and several different bases of DA polynomials on the integer lattice can be found in the literature. Here, in addition to the above-cited works, one should mention the work of D. Zeilberger
[
16
]
who first constructed a basis suitable for ”power” series expansions.

The situation with rational DA functions is more complicated. In
[
11
]
R. Isaacs (who used a slightly different notion of discrete analyticity) formulated a conjecture that every rational DA function is a polynomial. Isaacs’ conjecture was ultimately proved false by Harman
[
10
]
. However, these works indicate that the class of DA functions, that are rational in the sense of the point-wise product, is not rich enough for applications.

Another notion of DA rationality for functions on the integer lattice in
\mathbb{C}
is based on the Cauchy - Kovalevskaya extension of the point-wise product from the real axis. It was introduced and studied in
[
2
]
. This approach was inspired by
[
3
]
, which dealt with another case of analyticity being incompatible with the point-wise product – hyperholomorphic functions of the quaternionic variable.

In more recent works
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
, the convolution product
\odot
of DA polynomials on the integer lattice with respect to a certain ”standard” basis
z^{(n)}
was considered instead. Although this setup proved to be more fruitful, it is heavily dependent on the properties of the elementary difference operators

\delta_{x}f(z)=f(z+1)-f(z),\quad\delta_{y}f(z)=f(z+i)-f(z).

The main goal of the present paper is to extend the notion of rationality to the case of a rhombic lattice, where the difference operators do not seem to be very helpful.
As it turns out, the forward shift operator on the space of DA polynomials can be adapted to the rhombic lattice case much more easily.

One of the main ingredients
is that every rational matrix-valued function
f(z),
analytic in a neighborhood of the origin, can be represented as

f(z)=D+zC(I-zA)^{-1}B,

where
A,B,C,D
are matrices of suitable dimensions. Such representations, called
realizations
, arose in linear system theory (see
[
12
]
) and proved quite useful in the study of rational matrix-functions (e.g.
[
6
]
).

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
provides the background on discrete analyticity on rhombic lattices, most of which goes back to
[
8
]
and

[
13
]
. In Section
3
the forward and backward shift operators are introduced. Investigation of their properties (Theorem
3.2
, Proposition
3.4
) justifies the terminology: they do resemble the shift operators in the complex analytic case; see Remark
3.5
. Section
4
contains the construction of the eigenfunction of the backward shift operator (Theorem
4.1
). A power series expansion of this eigenfunction in terms of a continuous parameter generates ”pseudo-powers” of
z
that serve as a DA polynomial basis. Finally, in Section
5
the main definitions and results are presented. The convolution product

\odot
of DA polynomials is set up in Definition
5.1
, and Theorem
5.11
states that
\odot
-quotients of DA polynomials are precisely those DA functions that admit realizations, thus justifying the notion of a ”rational DA function.” The relationship between rational DA functions and rational functions of the complex variable is formulated in Theorem
5.6
. This relationship leads to explicit formulas for products and inverses of rational DA functions in terms of realizations. A characterization of rational DA functions in terms of shift-invariance is stated as Theorem
5.12
. Finally, an example of a rational DA function
K_{w}(z)
is given (Example
5.13
).
This rational function is a positive reproducing kernel that gives rise to a Hilbert space of DA functions, to be investigated in a forthcoming publication.

2.
Preliminaries

In what follows,
\Lambda
is a rhombic lattice in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
– a monohedral tessellation of
\mathbb{C}
with unit rhombi. The sets of vertices, edges and faces of
\Lambda
are denoted by
\mathbf{V}(\Lambda),

\mathbf{E}(\Lambda)
and
\mathbf{F}(\Lambda),
respectively. A
track
in
\Lambda
is a sequence
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
in
\mathbf{F}(\Lambda),
such that for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
faces
F_{n}
and
F_{n+1}
are adjacent and, furthermore, the edges shared by
F_{n}
with its neighbors
F_{n+1}
and
F_{n-1}
form two opposite sides of the rhombus
F_{n}.
These shared edges are referred to as
ties
of the track, and the rest are called
rails
of the track. Note that any two ties of a track are parallel. The same cannot be said, in general, about rails of a track.

The set of directions of the edges of
\Lambda
is denoted by
\hat{\mathbf{E}}(\Lambda):

\hat{\mathbf{E}}(\Lambda)=\{b-a:(a,b)\in\mathbf{E}(\Lambda)\}.

Proposition 2.1
.

\hat{\mathbf{E}}(\Lambda)
is a finite set.

Proof.

The case of a square lattice is trivial. If the faces of
\Lambda
are not squares, denote by
\alpha
the radian measure of the acute angle in each of the rhombic faces of
\Lambda.
If
\alpha/\pi
is a rational number, say

\dfrac{\alpha}{\pi}=\dfrac{m}{n},\quad\text{where}\quad m,n\in\mathbb{N},

then connectedness of
\Lambda
implies that for a suitable
\alpha_{0}\in\mathbb{R}

\hat{\mathbf{E}}(\Lambda)\subset\{e^{i\pi(\alpha_{0}+k)/n}:k=0,1,\dots,2n-1\}.

If
\alpha/\pi
is irrational, then every vertex of
\Lambda
necessarily has degree
4,
and lattice
\Lambda
is formed by parallel translations of a single track in the direction of its ties, much like in the square lattice case.
∎

Let
a,b\in\mathbf{V}(\Lambda).
A path in
\Lambda
from

a
to
b
is a finite sequence
(z_{0},z_{1},\dots,z_{N})
of vertices of
\Lambda,
where
z_{0}=a,

z_{N}=b,
and for
n=1,\dots,N

the vertices
z_{n-1}
and
z_{n}
are adjacent in
\Lambda.
A path from
a
to
b
is closed if
a=b.

Given a function
f:\mathbf{V}(\Lambda)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C},
the discrete integral of
f
over a path

\gamma=(z_{0},\dots,z_{N})
is defined by

\int_{\gamma}f\delta z=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\dfrac{f(z_{n-1})+f(z_{n})}{2}(z_{n}-z_{n-1}).

Definition 2.2
.

Function
f:\mathbf{V}(\Lambda)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
is said to be discrete analytic (DA) if for every closed path
\gamma
in
\Lambda

\int_{\gamma}f\delta z=0.

Note that a closed path of the form
(a,b,c,d,a),

where
a\not=c
and
b\not=d
forms a face of
\Lambda;
it is denoted simply by
abcd.

Theorem 2.3
.

Function
f:\mathbf{V}(\Lambda)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
is DA if, and only if, on every face
abcd
of
\Lambda

f(z)
satisfies the discrete Cauchy - Riemann equation

\dfrac{f(a)-f(c)}{a-c}=\dfrac{f(b)-f(d)}{b-d}.

Proof.

Since the integral over any closed path in
\Lambda
can be written as a sum of integrals over faces,
f(z)
is DA if, and only if, for every face
abcd
of
\Lambda

\int_{abcd}f\delta z=0,

which is equivalent to (
2.3
).
∎

As is clear from Definition
2.2
, the integral of DA function
f(z)
over a path
\gamma
from
a
to
b
is independent of the choice of
\gamma,
hence a simplified notation can be used:

\int_{a}^{b}f\delta z=\int_{\gamma}f\delta z,

where
\gamma
is any path in
\Lambda
from
a
to
b.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
z_{0}\in V(\Lambda)
be fixed. If
f(z)
is a DA function, then so is

F(z)=\int_{z_{0}}^{z}f\delta z.

Proof.

Let
abcd
be a face of
\Lambda.
One has

\dfrac{F(a)-F(c)}{a-c}-\dfrac{F(b)-F(d)}{b-d}\\
=\dfrac{1}{a-c}\int_{c}^{a}f\delta z-\dfrac{1}{b-d}\int_{d}^{b}f\delta z\\
=\dfrac{f(c)(b-c)+f(b)(a-c)+f(a)(a-b)}{2(a-c)}-\\
-\dfrac{f(d)(c-d)+f(c)(b-d)+f(b)(b-c)}{2(b-d)}\\
=\dfrac{(f(a)-f(c))(a-b)}{2(a-c)}+\dfrac{(f(b)-f(d))(c-d)}{2(b-d)}\\
=\dfrac{f(a)-f(c)}{2(a-c)}(a-b+c-d)+\\
+\dfrac{c-d}{2}\left(\dfrac{f(b)-f(d)}{b-d}-\dfrac{f(a)-f(c)}{a-c}\right)=0

in view of Theorem
2.3
and the fact that
abcd
is a rhombus.
∎

In what follows, two additional assumptions about the lattice
\Lambda
are made. Firstly,
0\in\mathbf{V}(\Lambda).

Secondly, for every
z\in\mathbf{V}(\Lambda)
there is
N\in\mathbb{N}
and a path
(z_{0},\dots,z_{N}),
such that:

(2.1)

\displaystyle z_{0}=z;

(2.2)

\displaystyle z_{n}-z_{n-1}\not=\pm 1,\quad n=1,\dots,N;

(2.3)

\displaystyle z_{N}-z_{N-1}=1.

A path with the properties (
2.1
) – (
2.3
) is said to be a
leash
of
z
of length
N.

Remark 2.5
.

The above assumptions can be satisfied with a suitable affine linear transformation. Indeed,
in view of Proposition
2.1
, one can find an infinite family of tracks in
\Lambda
that that have parallel ties. With a suitable rotation, one can ensure that these ties are parallel to the real axis and, moreover, that every vertex in
\Lambda
lies to the left of infinitely many tracks in the family.

3.
Shift operators on the space of DA functions

Denote by
\mathcal{H}(\lambda)
the space of DA functions on
\Lambda.

Definition 3.1
.

The forward shift operator
Z_{+}:\mathcal{H}(\Lambda)\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}(\Lambda)
is defined by

(Z_{+}f)(z)=\dfrac{f(0)-f(z)}{2}+\int_{0}^{z}f\delta z.

Theorem 3.2
.

The kernel and range of
Z_{+}
can be characterized as follows:

\ker(Z_{+})=\{0\},\quad\ran(Z_{+})=\{f\in\mathcal{H}(\Lambda):f(0)=0\}.

Proof.

Suppose first that
f\in\mathcal{H}(\Lambda)
is such that
Z_{+}f=0.
Then

\dfrac{f(z)-f(0)}{2}=\int_{0}^{z}f\delta z,\quad z\in\mathbf{V}(\Lambda).

Therefore, if
a,b\in\mathbf{V}(\Lambda),
then

f(b)-f(a)=2\int_{a}^{b}f\delta z.

In particular, if the vertices
a
and
b
are adjacent, then

(3.1)

\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}=f(a)+f(b).

Now fix an arbitrary
z\in\mathbf{V}(\Lambda),
and let
(z_{0},\dots,z_{N})
be a leash of
z.
Then (
3.1
) implies that

f(z)=f(z_{1})\dfrac{1+z_{0}-z_{1}}{1+z_{1}-z_{0}}=\dots=f(z_{N})\prod_{n=1}^{N}\dfrac{1+z_{n-1}-z_{n}}{1+z_{n}-z_{n-1}}=0,

since
1+z_{N-1}-z_{N}=0.
Thus
\ker(Z_{+})=\{0\}.

As to the range of
Z_{+},
the inclusion

\ran(Z_{+})\subset\{f\in\mathcal{H}(\Lambda):f(0)=0\}

follows immediately from Definition
3.1
. To prove the opposite inclusion,
assume that
g(z)
is a DA function, such that
g(0)=0.
One needs to show that there exists a DA function
f(z),

such that
Z_{+}f=g.
This can be done in a number of steps.

Step 1.

Let
f:\mathbf{V}(\Lambda)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
be given. Then
f(z)
is the pre-image of
g(z)
under
Z_{+}
if, and only if,
the relation

(3.2)

f(v)(1+u-v)-f(u)(1+v-u)=2(g(u)-g(v))

holds on every edge
(u,v)
of
\Lambda.
Indeed, if
f\in\mathcal{H}(\Lambda)
is such that
Z_{+}f=g,
then for every pair
u,v\in\mathbf{V}(\Lambda)
it holds that

g(u)-g(v)=Z_{+}f(u)-Z_{+}f(v)=\dfrac{f(v)-f(u)}{2}+\int_{v}^{u}f\delta z.

In particular, if the vertices
u
and
v
are adjacent, then

g(u)-g(v)=\dfrac{f(v)-f(u)}{2}+\dfrac{f(v)+f(u)}{2}(u-v),

which is equivalent to (
3.2
). Conversely, if (
3.2
) holds on every edge of
\Lambda
, then on every face
a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}a_{4}
of
\Lambda

one has (with
a_{0}:=a_{4}
):

\int_{a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}a_{4}}f\delta z=\sum_{k=1}^{4}\dfrac{f(a_{k-1})+f(a_{k})}{2}(a_{k}-a_{k-1})\\
=\sum_{k=1}^{4}\left(g(a_{k})-g(a_{k-1})-\dfrac{f(a_{k-1})-f(a_{k})}{2}\right)=0,

hence
f(z)
is a DA function. Now fix an arbitrary
z\in\mathbf{V}(\Lambda)
and choose a path
(z_{0},\dots z_{N})
from
z_{0}=0
to
z_{N}=z.

Applying (
3.2
) to every edge of the path, one gets

(Zf)(z)=\dfrac{f(0)-f(z)}{2}+\int_{0}^{z}f\delta z\\
=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\dfrac{f(z_{n-1})-f(z_{n})}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\dfrac{f(z_{n-1}+f(z_{n})}{2}(z_{n}-z_{n-1})\\
=\sum_{n=1}^{N}(g(z_{n})-g(z_{n-1}))=g(z)-g(0)=g(z).

Step 2.

Whenever
z\in\mathbf{V}(\Lambda)
has a leash of length 1,
set

f(z)=g(z+1)-g(z).

Then (
3.2
) holds on every tie and every left rail of every track with horizontal ties. To see this, note that the above equality is a special case of (
3.2
) with

u=z,

v=z+1.

Furthermore, if
abcd
is a face of
\Lambda,
such that
c=b+1
and
d=a+1,
then

(1+b-a)f(a)-(1+a-b)f(b)\\
=(g(a+1)-g(a))(1+b-a)-(g(b+1)-g(b))(1+a-b)\\
=(g(a+1)-g(b))(b+1-a)-(g(b+1)-g(a))(a+1-b)+\\
+2(g(b)-g(a)).

By Theorem
2.3
,

\dfrac{g(a+1)-g(b)}{a+1-b}=\dfrac{g(b+1)-g(a)}{b+1-a},

hence (
3.2
) holds on the edge
(a,b),
as well.

Step 3.

If
abcd
is a face without horizontal edges, and if the value
f(a)
is given, then there is a unique way to assign the values
f(b),f(c),f(d)
so that (
3.2
) holds on all the edges of the face
abcd.
Indeed, (
3.2
) holds on the edges
(a,b),

(b,c)
and
(c,d)
if, and only if,

\displaystyle f(b)=\dfrac{2(g(b)-g(a))-(b-a+1)f(a)}{b-a-1},

\displaystyle f(c)=\dfrac{2(g(c)-g(b))-(c-b+1)f(b)}{c-b-1},

\displaystyle f(d)=\dfrac{2(g(d)-g(c))-(d-c+1)f(c)}{d-c-1}.

In this case one has

2(g(c)-g(a))=2(g(c)-g(b))+2(g(b)-g(a))\\
=(b-a+1)f(a)+(c-a)f(b)+(c-b-1)f(c)

and, similarly,

2(g(d)-g(b))=(c-b+1)f(b)+(d-b)f(c)+(d-c-1)f(d).

Since
g(z)
is a DA function, it follows from Theorem
2.3
that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dfrac{b-a+1}{c-a}f(a)&+f(b)+\dfrac{c-b-1}{c-a}f(c)\\
&=\dfrac{c-b+1}{d-b}f(b)+f(c)+\dfrac{d-c-1}{d-b}f(d),\end{split}

\displaystyle(a-b-1)\dfrac{f(c)-f(a)}{c-a}-(d-c-1)\dfrac{f(d)-f(b)}{d-b}=0.

Since
a-b=d-c\not=1,
one has

\dfrac{f(c)-f(a)}{c-a}=\dfrac{f(d)-f(b)}{d-b}

and

\int_{abcd}f\delta z=0.

But then

g(d)-g(a)=g(d)-g(c)+g(c)-g(b)+g(b)-g(a)\\
=\dfrac{f(a)-f(d)}{2}+\int_{abcd}f\delta z-\dfrac{f(a)+f(d)}{2}(a-d)\\
=\dfrac{f(a)-f(d)}{2}+\dfrac{f(a)+f(d}{2}(d-a),

and equality (
3.2
) on the edge
(a,d)
follows.

Step 4.

Proceeding as at Step 3, one can define
f(z)
on the vertices of any face without horizontal edges that has a vertex with a leash of length 1. This set includes, in particular, all vertices of
\Lambda
that have a leash of length at most
2.
By induction on the length of the shortest leash, one can extend the definition of
f(z)
to the whole of
V(\Lambda)
in such a way that (
3.2
) holds on every edge of
\Lambda.

∎

Definition 3.3
.

The backward shift operator
Z_{-}:H(\Lambda)\longrightarrow H(\Lambda)
is defined as follows:
given
f\in\mathcal{H}(\Lambda),

Z_{-}f
is the unique element of
\mathcal{H}(\Lambda),
such that

Z_{+}Z_{-}f(z)=f(z)-f(0).

Proposition 3.4
.

Operator
Z_{-}
is a left inverse of
Z_{+},
and the kernel of
Z_{-}
consists of constant DA functions.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{H}(\Lambda).
In view of Theorem
3.2
and Definition
3.3
, one has

\displaystyle Z_{+}Z_{-}Z_{+}f(z)=Z_{+}f(z)-Z_{+}f(0)=Z_{+}f(z),

\displaystyle Z_{-}Z_{+}f(z)=f(z).

As to the kernel of
Z_{-},
it follows immediately from Definition
3.3

that

Z_{-}f(z)=0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad f(z)-f(0)=0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad f(z)=f(0).

∎

Remark 3.5
.

The shift operators
Z_{+}
and
Z_{-}
are ”discrete counterparts” of the classical shift operators

f(z)\mapsto zf(z)\quad\text{and}\quad f(z)\mapsto\dfrac{f(z)-f(0)}{z}

on the space of continuous analytic functions in the open unit disk.

4.
Discrete analytic polynomials

Denote

\mathbf{S}(\Lambda)=\{t\in\mathbb{C}:t(1+b-a)=-2\text{ for some }(a,b)\in\mathbf{E}(\Lambda)\}.

In view of Proposition
2.1
,
\mathbf{S}(\Lambda)
is a finite set. It is contained in the complement of the open unit disk.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
t\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbf{S}(\Lambda).

Then
t
is an eigenvalue of the operator
Z_{-}.
The corresponding eigenspace is one-dimensional; it is spanned by the DA function
e_{t}(z),
which is determined as follows:
e_{t}(0)=1,
and

(4.1)

e_{t}(z)=\prod_{k=1}^{N}\dfrac{2+t(1+z_{k}-z_{k-1})}{2+t(1+z_{k-1}-z_{k})}

for
z\not=0,
where
(z_{0},z_{1},\dots z_{N})
is any path from
z_{0}=0
to
z_{N}=z.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Theorem
3.2
. It can be broken down into a number of steps.

Step 1.

Let
t\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbf{S}(\Lambda),
and let
e_{t}:V(\Lambda)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}.

Then
e_{t}\in\mathcal{H}(\Lambda)
and
Z_{-}e_{t}=te_{t}
if, and only if,
the relation

(4.2)

e_{t}(u)(2+t(1+v-u))=e_{t}(v)(2+t(1+u-v))

holds on every edge
(u,v)
of
\Lambda.
Indeed, if
e_{t}(z)
is a DA function, then, by Definition
3.3
and Proposition
3.4
, equality

Z_{-}e_{t}(z)=te_{t}(z),

is equivalent to

e_{t}(z)-e_{t}(0)=tZ_{+}e_{t}(z).

This last equality holds if, and only if,
for every pair of adjacent vertices
u
and
v
one has

e_{t}(u)-e_{t}(v)=t(Z_{+}e_{t}(u)-Z_{+}e_{t}(v))=t\left(\dfrac{e_{t}(v)-e_{t}(u)}{2}+\int_{v}^{u}e_{t}\delta z\right)\\
=t\left(\dfrac{e_{t}(v)-e_{t}(u)}{2}+\dfrac{e_{t}(v)+e_{t}(u)}{2}(u-v)\right),

which is equivalent to (
4.2
). Note, however, that if a function
e_{t}(z)
satisfies (
4.2
) on every edge of
\Lambda,
then it is automatically DA, since on every face
abcd

one then has

\dfrac{e_{t}(b)-e_{t}(d)}{b-d}=\dfrac{e_{t}(a)}{b-d}\left(\dfrac{2+t(1+b-a)}{2+t(1+a-b)}-\dfrac{2+t(1+d-a)}{2+t(1+a-d)}\right)\\
=\dfrac{e_{t}(a)(4t+2t^{2})}{(2+t(1+a-b))(2+t(1+a-d))}\\
=\dfrac{e_{t}(a)(4t+2t^{2})}{(2+t(1+a-b))(2+t(1+b-c))}\\
=\dfrac{e_{t}(a)}{c-a}\left(\dfrac{(2+t(1+b-a))(2+t(1+c-b))}{(2+t(1+a-b))(2+t(1+b-c))}-1\right)=\dfrac{e_{t}(c)-e_{t}(a)}{c-a}.

Step 2.

Since
t\not\in\mathbf{S}(\Lambda),
one can define a function
e_{t}(z)
by
e_{t}(0)=1
and (
4.1
) for
z\not=0,
where
(z_{0},z_{1},\dots z_{N})
is some path from
z_{0}=0
to
z_{N}=z.
Note that definition (
4.1
) is actually path-independent because on every face
abcd
of
\Lambda

one has:

\displaystyle b-a=c-d,\quad c-b=d-a,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dfrac{(2+t(1+b-a))(2+t(1+c-b))}{(2+t(1+a-b))(2+t(1+b-c))}\times\qquad\ \\
\times\dfrac{(2+t(1+d-c))(2+t(1+a-d))}{(2+t(1+c-d))(2+t(1+d-a))}&=1.\end{split}

Therefore,
e_{t}(z)
satisfies (
4.2
) on every edge of
\Lambda
and is an eigenfunction of
Z_{-}
associated with the eigenvalue
t.
It is also clear from (
4.2
) that such a function is unique up to a multiplicative constant.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Denote by
\mathbf{S}_{0}(\Lambda)
the complement of the set of eigenvalues of the operator
Z_{-}.
Using an approach that is quite similar to the proof of Theorem
3.2
, one can show that
t=-1
is not an eigenvalue of
Z_{-},
and hence
\mathbf{S}_{0}(\Lambda)\not=\emptyset.
On the other hand, according to Theorem
4.1
,
\mathbf{S}_{0}(\Lambda)\subset\mathbf{S}(\Lambda).
Depending on the lattice
\Lambda,
the inclusion can be proper, although in some important cases, including the case of the square lattice, it is not. It is assumed from now on that
\mathbf{S}_{0}(\Lambda)=\mathbf{S}(\Lambda).

In view of (
4.1
),
e_{t}(z)
is a rational function in
t
(of degree depending on
z,
in general), analytic in the complement of
\mathbf{S}(\Lambda)
and, in particular, in the open unit disk
\mathbb{D}.
Consider the Taylor expansion

(4.3)

e_{t}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^{n}z^{(n)},\quad t\in\mathbb{D},z\in\mathbf{V}(\Lambda).

Proposition 4.3
.

Taylor coefficients
z^{(n)}
in the expansion (
4.3
) are DA functions of
z
with the following properties:

(4.4)

z^{(0)}=1\quad\text{and}\quad z^{(n)}=Z_{+}z^{(n-1)}\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N},

in particular,
z^{(1)}=z,

(4.5)

\displaystyle Z_{-}z^{(0)}=0\quad\text{and}\quad Z_{-}z^{(n)}=z^{(n-1)}\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N},

(4.6)

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt[n]{|z^{(n)}|}\leq 1.

Moreover,
z^{(0)},z^{(1)},z^{(2)},\dots
form a linearly independent vector family in
\mathcal{H}(\Lambda).

Proof.

Discrete analyticity of
z^{(n)}
can be seen by differentiating the discrete Cauchy - Riemann equation for
e_{t}(z)
with respect to
t
. Substituting the expansion (
4.3
) into the equality

e_{t}(z)-1=tZ_{+}e_{t}(z),

one obtains (
4.4
). In particular,

z^{(1)}=Z_{+}1=\int_{0}^{z}\delta z=z.

In view of Proposition
3.4
, (
4.5
) holds, as well, and hence for a linear combination
a_{0}z^{(0)}+a_{1}z^{(1)}+\dots+a_{N}z^{(N)}
one has

(Z_{-}^{n}(a_{0}z^{(0)}+a_{1}z^{(1)}+\dots+a_{N}z^{(N)}))(0)=a_{n},\quad n=0,1,\dots,N,

implying linear independence of the family
z^{(0)},z^{(1)},z^{(2)},\dots
Finally, formula (
4.6
) is a consequence of the Cauchy - Hadamard theorem.∎

Definition 4.4
.

Elements of the subspace of
\mathcal{H}(\Lambda)
spanned by

z^{(0)},z^{(1)},z^{(2)},\dots

are called DA polynomials.

It should be mentioned here that the space of DA polynomials defined above was originally introduced by Duffin in
[
8
]
using a different basis:

\rho_{0}=1,\quad\rho_{n}(z)=n\int_{0}^{z}\rho_{n-1}\delta z.

5.
Convolution product and rational DA functions

There is little difficulty involved in adapting the results of previous sections to the case of DA functions with matricial values. Such functions can be viewed as matrices with DA entries, with the discrete integral and the shift operators being applied entry-wise. The space of
\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
-valued DA functions on
\mathbf{V}(\Lambda)
is denoted by
\mathcal{H}(\Lambda)^{m\times n}.
Matrix-valued DA polynomials are introduced similarly.

Definition 5.1
.

The convolution product
\odot
of a
\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
-valued DA polynomial

p(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}A_{n}z^{(n)}

with a
\mathbb{C}^{n\times k}
-valued DA function
f(z)
is given by

(p\odot f)(z):=\sum_{n=0}^{N}A_{n}(Z_{+}^{n}f)(z).

Similarly, if
g(z)
is a
\mathbb{C}^{k\times m}
-valued DA function, then

(g\odot p)(z):=\sum_{n=0}^{N}(Z_{+}^{n}g)(z)A_{n}.

Note that the space of
\mathbb{C}
-valued DA polynomials equipped with the convolution product
\odot

is a commutative ring, where

z^{(m)}\odot z^{(n)}=z^{(m+n)}.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}.
Then the DA polynomial
I_{m}-zA
is
\odot
-invertible in
\mathcal{H}(\Lambda)^{m\times m}

if, and only if,

(5.1)

\mathbf{S}(\Lambda)\cap\sigma(A)=\emptyset.

In this case the
\odot
-inverse of
I_{m}-zA
is given by

(5.2)

(I_{m}-zA)^{-\odot}:=\prod_{k=1}^{N}\big((2I_{m}+(1+z_{k}-z_{k-1})A)(2I_{m}+(1+z_{k-1}-z_{k})A)^{-1}\big),

where
(z_{0},z_{1},\dots z_{N})
is any path from
z_{0}=0
to
z_{N}=z.

Proof.

Let
r(z)=I_{m}-zA
and
suppose that
f
is a left
\odot
-inverse of
r(z)
in
\mathcal{H}(\Lambda)^{m\times m}:

(f\odot r)(z)=I_{m},

or, equivalently,

(5.3)

f(z)=I_{m}+Z_{+}f(z)A.

Then

f(0)=I_{m}\quad\text{and}\quad Z_{-}f(z)=f(z)A.

Let
t
be an eigenvalue of
A
with the associated eigenvector
w\not=0.

Then

Z_{-}f(z)w=tf(z)w.

If
t\in\mathbf{S},
then (see Remark
4.2
)
f(z)w=0,
which leads to a contradiction for
z=0.

Thus the necessity of the condition (
5.1
) is established.

Conversely, suppose that (
5.1
) is in force and observe that (
5.3
) is equivalent to
f(0)=I_{m}
and

f(u)(2I_{m}+(1+v-u)A)=f(v)(2I_{m}+(1+u-v)A)

holding on every edge of
\Lambda
(compare with (
4.2
)). Exactly as in the proof of Theorem
4.1
, one may deduce
that there exists a unique DA function
f(z)
satisfying (
5.3
). Moreover,
f(z)=(I_{m}-zA)^{-\odot}
is given by (
5.2
) (in particular, this formula is path-independent). Since
(I_{m}-zA)^{-\odot}
commutes with
A,
it is a right
\odot
-inverse of
r(z),
as well.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

If the spectral radius
\rho(A)<1,
then

(I-zA)^{-\odot}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{(n)}A^{n}.

Definition 5.4
.

A matrix-valued DA function
f(z)
is said to be rational if it can be represented as

f(z)=D+C(I-zA)^{-\odot}\odot(zB),

where
A,B,C,D
are complex matrices of suitable dimensions, and

\mathbf{S}(\Lambda)\cap\sigma(A)=\emptyset.

Such a representation is called a realization of
f(z).

It may not be obvious from the above definition that a sum of matrix-valued rational DA functions of suitable dimensions is itself rational.
This is settled in the proposition below.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
m,n\in\mathbb{N}.

Let
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathcal{H}(\Lambda)^{m\times n}
be rational DA functions with given realizations:

f_{j}(z)=D_{j}+C_{j}(I-zA_{j})^{-\odot}\odot(zB_{j}),\quad j=1,2.

Then
f_{1}(z)+f_{2}(z)
is a rational DA function, as well, which admits the realization

(f_{1}+f_{2})(z)=D_{1}+D_{2}+\begin{pmatrix}C_{1}&C_{2}\end{pmatrix}\left(I-z\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&0\\
0&A_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right)^{-\odot}\odot\left(z\begin{pmatrix}B_{1}\\
B_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right).

Proof.

It suffices to observe that

\left(I-z\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&0\\
0&A_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right)^{-\odot}=\begin{pmatrix}(I-zA_{1})^{-\odot}&0\\
0&(I-zA_{2})^{-\odot}\end{pmatrix}.

∎

Definition
5.4
suggests that rational DA functions are closely related to rational functions of the complex variable. One must treat this relationship with caution, since
a given rational function admits infinitely many distinct realizations. A precise formulation of the relationship is stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
m,n\in\mathbb{N}.

The mapping

f(z)\mapsto\tau f(t):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}Z_{-}^{k}f(0)t^{k}

is a linear bijection from the space of
\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
-valued rational DA functions to the space of

\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
-valued rational functions of the complex variable
t
that have no poles in the set

\mathbf{P}(\Lambda)=\left\{t\in\mathbb{C}:t=0\text{ or }\dfrac{1}{t}\in\mathbf{S}(\Lambda)\right\}.

For any realization

(5.4)

f(z)=D+C(I-zA)^{-\odot}\odot(zB)

of a rational DA function
f(z)
it holds that

(5.5)

\tau f(t)=D+tC(I-tA)^{-1}B.

Proof.

From the identity

(I-zA)\odot(I-zA)^{-\odot}=I

and
Proposition
3.4
it follows that

Z_{-}(I-zA)^{-\odot}=(I-zA)^{-\odot}A.

Therefore,
for a rational DA function
f(z)
of the form (
5.4
) one has

D=f(0),\quad\text{and}\quad Z_{-}^{k}f(0)=CA^{k-1}B\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N},

from which (
5.5
) and the surjectivity of the mapping
\tau
follow.

To verify the injectivity of
\tau,
consider a rational function

D+C(I-zA)^{-\odot}\odot(zB)

in the kernel of
\tau,
where
A\in\mathbb{C}^{\ell\times\ell}
satisfies (
5.1
). Then

D=0\text{ and }CA^{k-1}B=0\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

Consider

\mathcal{M}=\ran(B)+\ran(AB)+\ran(A^{2}B)+\dots+\ran(A^{\ell-1}B)

– an
A
-invariant subspace of the finite-dimensional
\ker(C)
. Since

A
satisfies (
5.1
), for every edge
(u,v)
of
\Lambda
the matrix
2I_{\ell}+(1+u-v)A
is non-degenerate, and the associated linear transformation of
\mathbb{C}^{\ell}
maps
\mathcal{M}
bijectively to itself. Therefore, In view of (
5.2
),

C(I-zA)^{-\odot}B=0

and

C(I-zA)^{-\odot}\odot(zB)=Z(C(I-zA)^{-\odot}B)=0.

∎

Theorem
5.6
allows to extend the convolution product
\odot
to the case where the factors are rational DA functions.

Definition 5.7
.

Let
m,n,k\in\mathbb{N}.
Let
f_{1}\in\mathcal{H}(\Lambda)^{m\times n}
and
f_{2}\in\mathcal{H}(\Lambda)^{n\times k}
be rational DA functions. Define the convolution product
f_{1}\odot f_{2}
by

f_{1}\odot f_{2}=\tau^{-1}(\tau f_{1}\cdot\tau f_{2}).

Remark 5.8
.

Note that in the case where
f_{1}
or
f_{2}
is a DA polynomial, Definition
5.7
agrees with Definition
5.1
, since

\tau z^{(n)}=t^{n}.

In terms of realizations, the convolution product of rational DA functions can be described more explicitly, as the following proposition shows.

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
f_{1}\in\mathcal{H}(\Lambda)^{m\times n}
and
f_{2}\in\mathcal{H}(\Lambda)^{n\times k}
be rational DA functions with given realizations:

f_{j}(z)=D_{j}+C_{j}(I-zA_{j})^{-\odot}\odot(zB_{j}),\quad j=1,2,

then

(f_{1}\odot f_{2})(z)=D+C(I-zA)^{-\odot}\odot(zB),

where

\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}A_{2}&0\\
B_{1}C_{2}&A_{1}\end{pmatrix},\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}B_{2}\\
B_{1}D_{2}\end{pmatrix};

\displaystyle C=\begin{pmatrix}D_{1}C_{2}&C_{1}\end{pmatrix},\quad D=D_{1}D_{2}.

Proof.

In view of Theorem
5.6
, it suffices to check that the rational matrix-valued function
\tau f_{1}(t)\tau f_{2}(t)
admits the realization

\tau f_{1}(t)\tau f_{2}(t)=D+tC(I-tA)^{-1}B,

where
A,B,C,D
are given above. This is straightforward,
since

(I-tA)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}I-tA_{2}&0\\
-tB_{1}C_{2}&I-tA_{1}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\\[4.30554pt]
=\begin{pmatrix}(I-tA_{2})^{-1}&0\\
t(I-tA_{1})^{-1}B_{1}C_{2}(I-tA_{2})^{-1}&(I-tA_{1})^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.

∎

The question of
\odot
-invertibility is to be addressed next. As follows from Definition
5.7
, a matrix-valued rational DA function
f(z)
has a rational
\odot
-inverse if, and only if, the rational matrix-function
\tau f(t)
is invertible and
\tau f(t)^{-1}
has no poles in the set
\mathbf{P}(\Lambda)
of Theorem
5.6
. In this case such a
\odot
-inverse is unique:

f^{-\odot}(z)=\tau^{-1}((\tau f)(t)^{-1}).

A more explicit expression for the
\odot
-inverse in terms of realizations is given in the next proposition.

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}.
Let
f\in\mathcal{H}(\Lambda)^{n\times n}
be a rational DA function
with a realization

f(z)=D+C(I-zA)^{-\odot}\odot(zB).

Denote

A^{\times}=A-BD^{-1}C.

If

\det(D)\not=0\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{S}(\Lambda)\cap\sigma(A^{\times})=\emptyset,

then
f(z)
has a unique rational
\odot
-inverse:

f^{-\odot}(z)=D^{-1}-D^{-1}C(I-zA^{\times})^{-\odot}\odot(zBD^{-1}).

Proof.

The statement follows from Proposition
5.9
by straightforward computation.
∎

The following theorem is the ”discrete counterpart” of the classical definition of rationality in terms of quotients of polynomials.

Theorem 5.11
.

Let
f(z)
be a matrix-valued DA function. Function
f(z)
is rational if, and only if, there exists a
\mathbb{C}
-valued DA polynomial
p(z)\not\equiv 0,
such that

(pI\odot f)(z)
is a DA polynomial, as well.

Proof.

The ”only if” part of the statement follows immediately from Theorem
5.6
, Definition
5.7
and the fact that
\tau
maps DA polynomials to ordinary polynomials of one complex variable. As to the ”if” part, let
p(z)\not\equiv 0
be a
\mathbb{C}
-valued DA polynomial, of the minimal possible degree, such that
(pI\odot f)(z)

is a DA polynomial. It suffices to show that
p(z)
has a rational
\odot
-inverse.

Suppose, to obtain a contradiction, that there exists
\lambda\in\mathbf{P}(\Lambda),
such that
\tau p(\lambda)=0.

If
\lambda=0,
then

p(z)=z\odot p_{1}(z),
where

p_{1}(z)=\tau^{-1}\left(\dfrac{\tau p(t)}{t}\right)=Z_{-}p(z)

is a DA polynomial of degree
\deg(p_{1})=\deg(p)-1.

Hence

p_{1}I\odot f=Z_{-}(pI\odot f)

is also a DA polynomial, in contradiction to the minimality of
\deg(p).

If
\lambda\not=0,
then
\frac{1}{\lambda}\in\mathbf{S}(\Lambda)
and
p(z)=(z-\lambda)\odot p_{1}(z),
where

p_{1}(z)=\tau^{-1}\left(\dfrac{\tau p(t)}{t-\lambda}\right)

is a DA polynomial of degree
\deg(p_{1})=\deg(p)-1.

Hence

pI\odot f=(Z_{+}-\lambda I)(p_{1}I\odot f)

and

\left(\dfrac{1}{\lambda}I-Z_{-}\right)(p_{1}I\odot f)=\dfrac{1}{\lambda}Z_{-}(pI\odot f)

is also a DA polynomial. Note that for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
the finite-dimensional
space
\mathcal{M}_{N}
of DA polynomials of degree not exceeding
N
is invariant under
\frac{1}{\lambda}I-Z_{-}.
Moreover, since
\frac{1}{\lambda}
is not an eigenvalue of
Z_{-}
(see Remark
4.2
), the operator
\frac{1}{\lambda}I-Z_{-}
maps
\mathcal{M}_{N}
onto itself. Therefore,
p_{1}I\odot f
is a DA polynomial, yet again in contradiction to the minimality of
\deg(p).

Thus
\tau p(t)
does not vanish on the set
\mathbf{P}(\Lambda).
But then the rational function
\frac{1}{\tau p(t)}
has no poles in
\mathbf{P}(\Lambda),
the polynomial
p(z)
has a rational
\odot
-inverse, and, finally,

f=(p^{-\odot}I)\odot(pI\odot f)

is a rational DA function.
∎

Another useful characterization of DA rationality is in terms of shift invariance. For a rational DA function
f(z),

Z_{+}f(z)=z\odot f(z),

hence the space of matrix-valued rational DA functions (of given dimensions) is forward shift invariant.
It is also backward shift invariant, since for

f(z)=D+C(I-zA)^{-\odot}\odot(zB)

one has

Z_{-}f(z)=C(I-zA)^{-\odot}B=CB+C(Z_{+}Z_{-}(I-zA)^{-\odot})B\\
=CB+C(I-zA)^{-\odot}\odot(zAB).

Theorem 5.12
.

Let
m,n\in\mathbb{N},
and let
f\in\mathcal{H}^{m\times n}.
DA function
f(z)
is rational if, and only if,
the column space of
f(z),

\{f(z)w:w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\},
is contained in a finite-dimensional space of
\mathbb{C}^{m}
-valued DA functions that is
Z_{-}
-invariant.

Proof.

The ”only if” part of the statement follows from the Caley - Hamilton theorem, since for

f(z)=D+C(I-zA)^{-\odot}\odot(zB)

one has

Z_{-}^{k}f(z)=C(I-zA)^{-\odot}A^{k-1}B\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}

and, therefore,

\dim\spa_{\mathbb{C}}\{Z_{-}^{k}f(z)w:k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\}<\infty.

As to the ”if” part,
let
\mathcal{M}
be a finite-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{H}(\Lambda)^{m},
such that

\{f(z)w:w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\}\subset\mathcal{M}\quad\text{and}\quad Z_{-}\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{M}.

Choose a basis
f_{1}(z),f_{2}(z),\dots,f_{N}(z)
of
\mathcal{M}
and consider
the

\mathbb{C}^{m\times N}
-valued DA function

F(z):=\begin{pmatrix}f_{1}(z)&f_{2}(z)&\cdots&f_{N}(z)\end{pmatrix},.

Then there exists a matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N},
such that

Z_{-}F(z)=F(z)A

Suppose, to obtain a contradiction, that
A
has an eigenvalue
t\in\mathbf{S}(\Lambda).

Then

(tI-Z_{-})F(z)=F(z)(tI_{N}-A).

If
w
is an eigenvector of
A
associated with the eigenvalue
t,
then
F(z)w=0
because
t
is not an eigenvalue of
Z_{-}
(se Remark
4.2
). This contradicts the linear independence of the columns of
F(z).
Thus
A
satisfies (
5.1
). Furthermore, one has

\displaystyle F(z)-F(0)=Z_{+}Z_{-}F(z)=F(z)\odot(zA),

\displaystyle F(z)\odot(I-zA)=F(0),

\displaystyle F(z)=C(I-zA)^{-\odot},

where
C=F(0).
Since the column space of
f(z)
is contained in
\mathcal{M},
there exists a matrix
B\in\mathbb{C}{N\times n},
such that

f(z)=F(z)B=C(I-zA)^{-\odot}B=CB+C(I-zA)^{-\odot}\odot(zAB).

∎

It remains to provide a non-trivial example of a rational DA function.

Example 5.13
.

Let
w\in\mathbf{V}(\Lambda)
be fixed and let
M>1.
Consider the DA function

K_{w}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{z^{(n)}\overline{w^{(n)}}}{M^{n}}

(in view of (
4.6
), the series converges absolutely for every
z\in\mathbf{V}(\Lambda)
to a function that is DA by Theorem
2.3
).
If
K_{w}(z)
were a rational DA function, then

\tau K_{w}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{t^{n}\overline{w^{(n)}}}{M^{n}}=\overline{e_{\bar{t}/M}(w)},

which, in view of (
4.1
), is a rational function of the complex variable
t
and has no poles in
\mathbf{P}(\Lambda).

Taking into account the fact that
e_{0}(w)=1,
one may conclude that there exist matrices
A,B,C
satisfying (
5.1
) and such that

\overline{e_{\bar{t}/M}(w)}=1+tC(I-tA)^{-1}B.

In particular,

\dfrac{\overline{w^{(n)}}}{M^{n}}=CA^{n-1}B\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

Hence, rational or not, function
K_{w}(z)
can be represented as

K_{w}(z)=K_{w}(z)=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}z^{(n)}CA^{n-1}B.

Observe that

Z_{-}^{m}K_{w}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{(n)}CA^{n+m-1}B\quad\forall m\in\mathbb{N}.

By the Caley-Hamilton theorem, there is
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
a_{0},\dots,a_{N-1}\in\mathbb{C}
such that

A^{N}=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}a_{j}A^{j},

hence

Z_{-}^{N+1}K_{w}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j-1}Z_{-}^{j}K_{w}(z).

Let

\mathcal{M}=\spa_{\mathbb{C}}\{Z_{-}^{j}K_{w}(z):j=0,1,\dots,N\}.

Then
\mathcal{M}
is a
Z_{-}
-invariant subspace of
\mathcal{H}(\Lambda)
of dimension at most
N+1
that contains
K_{w}(z).
By Theorem
5.12
,
K_{w}(z)
is indeed a rational DA function,

K_{w}(z)=\tau^{-1}\left(\overline{e_{\bar{t}/m}(w)}\right).
