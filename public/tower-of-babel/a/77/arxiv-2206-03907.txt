A Unified Convergence Theorem for Stochastic Optimization Methods
Xiao Li, Andre Milzarek

Abstract
In this work, we provide a fundamental unified convergence theorem used for deriving expected and almost sure convergence results for a series of stochastic optimization methods. Our unified theorem only requires to verify several representative conditions and is not tailored to any specific algorithm. As a direct application, we recover expected and almost sure convergence results of the stochastic gradient method ( 𝖲𝖦𝖣 \mathsf{SGD} ) and random reshuffling ( 𝖱𝖱 {\mathsf{RR}} ) under more general settings. Moreover, we establish new expected and almost sure convergence results for the stochastic proximal gradient method ( 𝗉𝗋𝗈𝗑 ​ - ​ 𝖲𝖦𝖣 \mathsf{prox}\text{-}\mathsf{SGD} ) and stochastic model-based methods for nonsmooth nonconvex optimization problems. These applications reveal that our unified theorem provides a plugin-type convergence analysis and strong convergence guarantees for a wide class of stochastic optimization methods.



1
Introduction

Stochastic optimization methods are widely used to solve stochastic optimization problems and empirical risk minimization, serving as one of the foundations of machine learning. Among the many different stochastic methods, the most classic one is the stochastic gradient method (
\mathsf{SGD}
), which dates back to Robbins and Monro
[
35
]
. If the problem at hand has a finite-sum structure, then another popular stochastic method is random reshuffling (
{\mathsf{RR}}
)
[
19
]
. When the objective function has a composite form or is weakly convex (nonsmooth and nonconvex), then the stochastic proximal gradient method (
\mathsf{prox}\text{-}\mathsf{SGD}
) and stochastic model-based algorithms are the most typical approaches
[
17
,
10
]
.
Apart from the mentioned stochastic methods, there are many others like
\mathsf{SGD}
with momentum, Adam, stochastic higher order methods, etc. In this work, our goal is to establish and understand fundamental
convergence
properties of these stochastic optimization methods via a novel unified convergence framework.

Motivations.

Suppose we apply
\mathsf{SGD}
to minimize a smooth nonconvex function
f
.
\mathsf{SGD}
generates a sequence of iterates
\{\bm{x}^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
, which is a stochastic process due to the randomness of the algorithm and the utilized stochastic oracles. The most commonly seen ‘convergence result’ for
\mathsf{SGD}
is the
expected iteration complexity
, which typically takes the form
[
16
]

\min_{k=0,\ldots,T}\ \mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{2}]\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T+1}}\right)\quad\text{or}\quad\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{\bar{k}})\|^{2}]\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T+1}}\right),

(1)

where
T
denotes the total number of iterations and
\bar{k}
is an index sampled uniformly at random from
\{0,\ldots,T\}
. Note that we ignored some higher-order convergence terms and constants to ease the presentation. Complexity results are integral to understand core properties and progress of the algorithm during the first
T
iterations, while the asymptotic convergence behavior plays an equally important role as it characterizes whether an algorithm can eventually approach an exact stationary point or not. We refer to
Appendix

H
for additional motivational background for studying asymptotic convergence properties of stochastic optimization methods. Here, an
expected convergence result
, associated with the nonconvex minimization problem
\min_{\bm{x}}f(\bm{x})
, has the form

\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})\|]=0.

(2)

Intuitively, it should be possible to derive expected convergence from the expected iteration complexity (
1
) by letting
T\to\infty
. However, this is not the case as the ‘
\min
’ operator and the sampled
\bar{k}
are not well defined or become meaningless when
T
goes to
\infty
.

The above results are stated in expectation and describe the behavior of the algorithm by averaging infinitely many runs. Though this is an important convergence measure, in practical situations the algorithm is often only run once and the last iterate is returned as a solution. This observation motivates and necessitates
almost sure convergence results
, which establish convergence with probability
1
for a single run of the stochastic method:

\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})\|=0\quad\text{almost surely}.

(3)

Backgrounds.
Expected and almost sure convergence results have been extensively studied for convex optimization; see, e.g.,
[
9
,
33
,
41
,
45
,
5
,
40
]
. Almost sure convergence of
\mathsf{SGD}
for minimizing a smooth nonconvex function
f
was provided in the seminal work
[
3
]
using very standard assumptions, i.e., Lipschitz continuous
\nabla f
and bounded variance. Under the same conditions, the same almost sure convergence of
\mathsf{SGD}
was established in
[
32
]
based on a much simpler argument than that of
[
3
]
. A weaker ‘
\liminf
’-type almost sure convergence result for
\mathsf{SGD}
with AdaGrad step sizes was shown in
[
25
]
. Recently, the work
[
27
]
derives almost sure convergence of
\mathsf{SGD}
under the assumptions that
f
and
\nabla f
are Lipschitz continuous,
f
is coercive,
f
is not asymptotically flat, and the
\upsilon
-th moment of the stochastic error is bounded with
\upsilon\geq 2
. This result relies on stronger assumptions than the base results in
[
3
]
. Nonetheless, it allows more aggressive diminishing step sizes if
\upsilon>2
.
Apart from standard
\mathsf{SGD}
, almost sure convergence of different respective variants for min-max problems was discussed in
[
21
]
.
In terms of expected convergence, the work
[
6
]
showed
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})\|]=0
under the additional assumptions that
f
is twice continuously differentiable and the multiplication of the Hessian and gradient
\nabla^{2}f(\bm{x})\nabla f(\bm{x})
is Lipschitz continuous.

Though the convergence of
\mathsf{SGD}
is well-understood and a classical topic, asymptotic convergence results of the type (
2
) and (
3
) often require a careful and separate analysis for other stochastic optimization methods — especially when the objective function is simultaneously nonsmooth and nonconvex. In fact and as outlined, a direct transition from the more common complexity results (
1
) to the full convergence results (
2
) and (
3
) is often not possible without further investigation.

Main contributions.

We provide a fundamental
unified convergence theorem
(see
2.1
) for deriving both expected and almost sure convergence of stochastic optimization methods. Our theorem is not tailored to any specific algorithm, instead it incorporates several abstract conditions that suit a vast and general class of problem structures and algorithms. The proof of this theorem is elementary.

We then apply our novel theoretical framework to several classical stochastic optimization methods to recover existing and to establish new convergence results.
Specifically, we recover expected and almost sure convergence results for
\mathsf{SGD}
and
{\mathsf{RR}}
. Though these results are largely known in the literature, we derive unified and slightly stronger results under a general ABC condition
[
23
,
22
]
rather than the standard bounded variance assumption. We also remove the stringent assumption used in
[
6
]
to show (
2
) for
\mathsf{SGD}
.
As a core application of our framework, we derive expected and almost sure convergence results for
\mathsf{prox}\text{-}\mathsf{SGD}
in the nonconvex setting and under the more general ABC condition and for stochastic model-based methods under very standard assumptions. In particular, we show that the iterates
\{\bm{x}^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
generated by
\mathsf{prox}\text{-}\mathsf{SGD}
and other stochastic model-based methods will approach the set of stationary points almost surely and in an expectation sense. These results are
new
to our knowledge (see also
Section

3.5
for further discussion).

The above applications illustrate the general plugin-type purpose of our unified convergence analysis framework. Based on the given recursion and certain properties of the algorithmic update, we can derive broad convergence results by utilizing our theorem, which can significantly simplify the convergence analysis of stochastic optimization methods; see
Section

2.1
for a summary.

2
A unified convergence theorem

Throughout this work, let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
be a filtered probability space and let us assume that the sequence of iterates
\{\bm{x}^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is adapted to the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
, i.e., each of the random vectors
\bm{x}^{k}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
\mathcal{F}_{k}
-measurable.

In this section, we present a unified convergence theorem for the sequence
\{\bm{x}^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
based on an abstract convergence measure
\bm{\Phi}
. To make the abstract convergence theorem more accessible, the readers may momentarily regard
\bm{\Phi}
and
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
as
\nabla f
and the sequence related to the step sizes, respectively. We then present the main steps for showing the convergence of a stochastic optimization method by following a step-by-step verification of the conditions in our unified convergence theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let the mapping
\bm{\Phi}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
and the sequences
\{\bm{x}^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{++}
be given. Consider the following conditions:

(P.1)

The function
\bm{\Phi}
is
{\sf L}_{\Phi}
-Lipschitz continuous for some
{\sf L}_{\Phi}>0
, i.e., we have
\|\bm{\Phi}(\bm{x})-\bm{\Phi}(\bm{y})\|\leq{\sf L}_{\Phi}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|
for all
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(P.2)

There exists a constant
a>0
such that
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\ \mu_{k}\,\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\Phi}(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{a}]<\infty
.

The following statements are valid:

(i)

Let the conditions
(P.1)
–
(P.2)
be satisfied and suppose further that

(P.3)

There exist constants
{\sf A},{\sf B},b\geq 0
and
p_{1},p_{2},q>0
such that

\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{x}^{k+1}-\bm{x}^{k}\|^{q}]\leq{\sf A}\mu_{k}^{p_{1}}\ +\ {\sf B}\mu_{k}^{p_{2}}\,\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\Phi}(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{b}].

(P.4)

The sequence
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
and the parameters
a,b,q,p_{1},p_{2}
satisfy

\{\mu_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}\;\lx@nobreakspace\text{is bounded},\quad{\sum}_{k=0}^{\infty}\ \mu_{k}=\infty,\quad\text{and}\quad a,q\geq 1,\;\;a\geq b,\;\;p_{1},p_{2}\geq q.

Then, it holds that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\Phi}(\bm{x}^{k})\|]=0
.

(ii)

Let the properties
(P.1)
–
(P.2)
hold and assume further that

(P.3
′
)

There exist constants
{\sf A},b\geq 0
,
p_{1},p_{2},q>0
and random vectors
\bm{A}_{k},\bm{B}_{k}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\bm{x}^{k+1}=\bm{x}^{k}+\mu_{k}^{p_{1}}\bm{A}_{k}+\mu_{k}^{p_{2}}\bm{B}_{k}

and for all
k
,
\bm{A}_{k},\bm{B}_{k}
are
\mathcal{F}_{k+1}
-measurable and we have
\mathbb{E}[\bm{A}_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=0
almost surely,
\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{A}_{k}\|^{q}]\leq{\sf A}
, and
\limsup_{k\to\infty}\|\bm{B}_{k}\|^{q}/(1+\|\bm{\Phi}(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{b})<\infty
almost surely.

(P.4
′
)

The sequence
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
and the parameters
a,b,q,p_{1},p_{2}
satisfy
\mu_{k}\to 0
,

{\sum}_{k=0}^{\infty}\ \mu_{k}=\infty,\quad{\sum}_{k=0}^{\infty}\,\mu_{k}^{2p_{1}}<\infty,\quad\text{and}\quad q\geq 2,\;\;qa\geq b,\ \ p_{1}>\frac{1}{2},\;\;p_{2}\geq 1.

Then, it holds that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\bm{\Phi}(\bm{x}^{k})\|=0
almost surely.

The proof of
2.1
is elementary. We provide the core ideas here and defer its proof to
Appendix

A
. Item (i) is proved by contradiction. An easy first result is
\liminf_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\Phi}(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{a}]=0
. We proceed and assume that
\{\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\Phi}(\bm{x}^{k})\|]\}_{k\geq 0}
does not converge to zero. Then, for some
\delta>0
, we can construct two subsequences
\{\ell_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
and
\{u_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
such that
\ell_{t}<u_{t}
and

\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\Phi}(\bm{x}^{\ell_{t}})\|]\geq 2\delta
,
\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\Phi}(\bm{x}^{u_{t}})\|^{a}]\leq\delta^{a}
, and
\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\Phi}(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{a}]>\delta^{a}
for all
\ell_{t}<k<u_{t}
. Based on this construction, the conditions in the theorem, and a set of inequalities, we will eventually reach a contradiction. We notice that the Lipschitz continuity of
\bm{\Phi}
plays a prominent role when establishing this contradiction. Our overall proof strategy is inspired by the analysis of classical trust region-type methods,
see, e.g.,
[
8
, Theorem 6.4.6]
.
For item (ii), we first control the stochastic behavior of the error terms
\bm{A}_{k}
by martingale convergence theory. We can then conduct sample-based arguments to derive the final result, which is essentially deterministic and hence, follows similar arguments to that of item (i).

The major application areas of our unified convergence framework comprise stochastic optimization methods that have non-vanishing stochastic errors or that utilize diminishing step sizes. In the next subsection, we state the main steps for showing convergence of stochastic optimization methods. This also clarifies the abstract conditions listed in the theorem.

2.1
The steps for showing convergence of stochastic optimization methods

In order to apply the unified convergence theorem, we have to verify the conditions stated in the theorem, resulting in three main phases below.

Phase I: Verifying
(P.1)
–
(P.2)
.
Conditions
(P.1)
–
(P.2)
are used for both the expected and the almost sure convergence results. Condition
(P.1)
is a problem property and is very standard. We present the final convergence results in terms of the abstract measure
\bm{\Phi}
. This measure can be regarded as
f-f^{*}
in convex optimization,
\nabla f
in smooth nonconvex optimization, the gradient of the Moreau envelope in weakly convex optimization, etc. In all the situations, assuming Lipschitz continuity of the convergence measure
\bm{\Phi}
is standard and is arguably a minimal assumption in order to obtain iteration complexity and/or convergence results.

Condition
(P.2)
is typically a result of the algorithmic property or complexity analysis. To verify this condition, one first establishes the recursion of the stochastic method, which almost always has the form

\mathbb{E}[\bm{y}_{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq(1+\beta_{k})\bm{y}_{k}-\mu_{k}\|\bm{\Phi}(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{a}+\zeta_{k}.

Here,
\bm{y}_{k}
is a suitable Lyapunov function measuring the (approximate) descent property of the stochastic method,
\zeta_{k}
represents the error term satisfying
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\zeta_{k}<\infty
,
\beta_{k}
is often related to the step sizes and satisfies
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\beta_{k}<\infty
. Then, applying the supermartingale convergence theorem (see
B.1
), we obtain
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\ \mu_{k}\,\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\Phi}(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{a}]<\infty
, i.e., condition
(P.2)
.

Since condition
(P.2)
is typically a consequence of the underlying algorithmic recursion, one can also derive the standard finite-time complexity bound (
1
) in terms of the measure
\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\Phi}(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{a}]
based on it. Hence, non-asymptotic complexity results are also included implicitly in our framework as a special case. To be more specific,
(P.2)
implies
\sum_{k=0}^{T}\mu_{k}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\Phi}(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{a}]\leq M
for some constant
M>0
and some total number of iterations
T
. This then yields
\min_{0\leq k\leq T}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\Phi}(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{a}]\leq M/\sum_{k=0}^{T}\mu_{k}
. Note that the sequence
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is often related to the step sizes. Thus, choosing the step sizes properly results in the standard finite-time complexity result.

Phase II: Verifying
(i)(P.3)
–
(i)(P.4)
for showing expected convergence.
Condition
(i)(P.3)
requires an upper bound on the step length of the update in terms of expectation, including upper bounds for the search direction and the stochastic error of the algorithm. It is often related to certain bounded variance-type assumptions for analyzing stochastic methods. For instance,
(i)(P.3)
is satisfied under the standard bounded variance assumption for
\mathsf{SGD}
, the more general ABC assumption for
\mathsf{SGD}
, the bounded stochastic subgradients assumption, etc. Condition
(i)(P.4)
is a standard diminishing step sizes condition used in stochastic optimization.

Then, one can apply item (i) of
2.1
to obtain
\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\Phi}(\bm{x}^{k})\|]\to 0
.

Phase III: Verifying
(ii)(P.3′)
–
(ii)(P.4′)
for showing almost sure convergence.
Condition
(ii)(P.3′)
is parallel to
(i)(P.3)
. It decomposes the update into a martingale term
\bm{A}_{k}
and a bounded error term
\bm{B}_{k}
. We will see later that this condition holds true for many stochastic methods. Though this condition requires the update to have a certain decomposable form, it indeed can be verified by bounding the step length of the update in conditional expectation, which is similar to
(i)(P.3)
. Hence,
(ii)(P.3′)
can be interpreted as a conditional version of
(i)(P.3)
. To see this, we can construct

\bm{x}^{k+1}=\bm{x}^{k}+\mu_{k}\cdot\underbracket{\begin{minipage}[3ex][t][154.99951pt]\centering$\frac{1}{\mu_{k}}\left(\bm{x}^{k+1}-\bm{x}^{k}-\mathbb{E}[\bm{x}^{k+1}-\bm{x}^{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\right)$\@add@centering\end{minipage}}_{\bm{A}_{k}}+\mu_{k}\cdot\underbracket{\begin{minipage}[3ex][t][90.41638pt]\centering$\frac{1}{\mu_{k}}\mathbb{E}[\bm{x}^{k+1}-\bm{x}^{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]$\@add@centering\end{minipage}}_{\bm{B}_{k}}.

(4)

By Jensen’s inequality, we then have
\mathbb{E}[\bm{A}_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=0
,

\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{A}_{k}\|^{q}]\leq{2^{q}}{\mu_{k}^{-q}}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{x}^{k+1}-\bm{x}^{k}\|^{q}],\quad\text{and}\quad\|\bm{B}_{k}\|^{q}\leq{\mu_{k}^{-q}}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{x}^{k+1}-\bm{x}^{k}\|^{q}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}].

Thus, once it is possible to derive
\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{x}^{k+1}-\bm{x}^{k}\|^{q}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=\mathcal{O}(\mu_{k}^{q})
in an almost sure sense, condition
(ii)(P.3′)
is verified with
p_{1}=p_{2}=1
. Condition
(ii)(P.4′)
is parallel to
(i)(P.4)
and is standard in stochastic optimization. Application of item (ii) of
2.1
then yields
\|\bm{\Phi}(\bm{x}^{k})\|\to 0
almost surely.

In the next section, we will illustrate how to show convergence for a set of classic stochastic methods by following the above three steps.

3
Applications to stochastic optimization methods

3.1
Convergence results of SGD

We consider the standard
\mathsf{SGD}
method for solving the smooth optimization problem
\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,f(\bm{x})
, where the iteration of
\mathsf{SGD}
is given by

\bm{x}^{k+1}=\bm{x}^{k}-\alpha_{k}\bm{g}^{k}.

(5)

Here,
\bm{g}^{k}
denotes a stochastic approximation of the gradient
\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})
. We assume that each stochastic gradient
\bm{g}^{k}
is
\mathcal{F}_{k+1}
-measurable and that the generated stochastic process
\{\bm{x}^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is adapted to the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
. We consider the following standard assumptions:

(A.1)

The mapping
\nabla f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is Lipschitz continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with modulus
{\sf L}>0
.

(A.2)

The objective function
f
is bounded from below on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e., there is
\bar{f}
such that
f(\bm{x})\geq\bar{f}
for all
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(A.3)

Each oracle
\bm{g}^{k}
defines an unbiased estimator of
\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})
, i.e., it holds that
\mathbb{E}[\bm{g}^{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})
almost surely, and there exist
{\sf C},{\sf D}\geq 0
such that

\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{g}^{k}-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq{\sf C}[f(\bm{x}^{k})-\bar{f}]+{\sf D}\quad\text{almost surely}\quad\forall~k\in\mathbb{N}.

(A.4)

The step sizes
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
satisfy
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}=\infty
and
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}^{2}<\infty
.

We now derive the convergence of
\mathsf{SGD}
below by setting
\bm{\Phi}\equiv\nabla f
and
\mu_{k}\equiv\alpha_{k}
.

Phase I: Verifying
(P.1)
–
(P.2)
.

(A.1)
verifies condition
(P.1)
with
{\sf L}_{\Phi}\equiv{\sf L}
. We now check
(P.2)
. Using
(A.2)
,
(A.3)
, and a standard analysis for
\mathsf{SGD}
gives the following recursion (see
Section

C.1
for the full derivation):

\mathbb{E}[f(\bm{x}^{k+1})-\bar{f}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq\left(1+\frac{{\sf LC}\alpha_{k}^{2}}{2}\right)[f(\bm{x}^{k})-\bar{f}]-\alpha_{k}\left(1-\frac{{\sf L}\alpha_{k}}{2}\right)\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{2}+\frac{{\sf LD}\alpha_{k}^{2}}{2}.

(6)

Taking total expectation, using
(A.4)
, and applying the supermartingale convergence theorem (
B.1
) gives
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{2}]<\infty
. Furthermore, the sequence
\{\mathbb{E}[f(\bm{x}^{k})]\}_{k\geq 0}
converges to some finite value. This verifies
(P.2)
with
a=2
.

Phase II: Verifying
(i)(P.3)
–
(i)(P.4)
for showing expected convergence.

For
(i)(P.3)
, we have by (
5
) and
(A.3)
that

\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{x}^{k+1}-\bm{x}^{k}\|^{2}]\leq\alpha_{k}^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{2}]+{\sf C}\alpha_{k}^{2}\mathbb{E}[f(\bm{x}^{k})-\bar{f}]+{\sf D}\alpha_{k}^{2}.

Due to the convergence of
\{\mathbb{E}[f(\bm{x}^{k})]\}_{k\geq 0}
, there exists
{\sf F}
such that
\mathbb{E}[f(\bm{x}^{k})-\bar{f}]\leq{\sf F}
for all
k
.
Thus, condition
(i)(P.3)
holds with
q=2
,
{\sf A}={\sf C}{\sf F}+{\sf D}
,
p_{1}=2
,
{\sf B}=1
,
p_{2}=2
, and
b=2
.
Condition
(i)(P.4)
is verified by
(A.4)
and the previous parameters choices. Therefore, we can apply
2.1
to deduce
\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})\|]\to 0
.

Phase III: Verifying
(ii)(P.3′)
–
(ii)(P.4′)
for showing almost sure convergence.

For
(ii)(P.3′)
, it follows from the update (
5
) that

\bm{x}^{k+1}=\bm{x}^{k}-\alpha_{k}(\bm{g}^{k}-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k}))-\alpha_{k}\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k}).

We have
p_{1}=1
,
\bm{A}_{k}=\bm{g}^{k}-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})
,
p_{2}=1
, and
\bm{B}_{k}=\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})
. Using
(A.2)
,
(A.3)
,
\mathbb{E}[f(\bm{x}^{k})-\bar{f}]\leq{\sf F}
, and choosing any
q=b>0
establishes
(ii)(P.3′)
. As before, condition
(ii)(P.4′)
follows from
(A.4)
and the previous parameters choices. Applying
2.1
yields
\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})\|\to 0
almost surely.

Finally, we summarize the above results in the following corollary.

Corollary 3.1
.

Let us consider
\mathsf{SGD}
(
5
) for smooth nonconvex optimization problems under
(A.1)
–
(A.4)
. Then, we have
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})\|]=0
and
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})\|=0
almost surely.

3.2
Convergence results of random reshuffling

We now consider random reshuffling (
{\mathsf{RR}}
) applied to problems with a finite sum structure

\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(\bm{x}):=\frac{1}{N}{\sum}_{i=1}^{N}f(\bm{x},i),

where each component function
f(\cdot,i):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is supposed to be smooth. At iteration
k
,
{\mathsf{RR}}
first generates a random permutation
\sigma^{k+1}
of the index set
\{1,\dots,N\}
. It then updates
\bm{x}^{k}
to
{\bm{x}^{k+1}}
through
N
consecutive gradient descent-type steps by accessing and using the component gradients
\{\nabla f(\cdot,\sigma_{1}^{k+1}),\dots,\nabla f(\cdot,\sigma_{N}^{k+1})\}
sequentially. Specifically, one update-loop (epoch) of
{\mathsf{RR}}
is given by

\tilde{\bm{x}}_{0}^{k}=\bm{x}^{k},\quad\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i}^{k}=\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i-1}^{k}-\alpha_{k}\nabla f(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i-1}^{k},\sigma_{i}^{k+1}),\quad i=1,\dots,N,\quad\bm{x}^{k+1}=\tilde{\bm{x}}_{N}^{k}.

(7)

After one such loop, the step size
\alpha_{k}
and the permutation
\sigma^{k+1}
is updated accordingly; cf.
[
19
,
29
,
31
]
. We make the following standard assumptions:

(B.1)

For all
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
,
f(\cdot,i)
is bounded from below by some
\bar{f}
and the gradient
\nabla f(\cdot,i)
is Lipschitz continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with modulus
{\sf L}>0
.

(B.2)

The step sizes
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
satisfy
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}=\infty
and
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}^{3}<\infty
.

A detailed derivation of the steps shown in
Section

2.1
for
{\mathsf{RR}}
is deferred to
Section

D.2
. Based on the discussion in
Section

D.2
and on
2.1
, we obtain the following results for
{\mathsf{RR}}
.

Corollary 3.2
.

We consider
{\mathsf{RR}}
(
7
) for smooth nonconvex optimization problems under
(B.1)
–
(B.2)
. Then it holds that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})\|]=0
and
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})\|=0
almost surely.

3.3
Convergence of the proximal stochastic gradient method

We consider the composite-type optimization problem

{\min}_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}~\psi(\bm{x}):=f(\bm{x})+\varphi(\bm{x})

(8)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuously differentiable function and
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,\infty]
is
\tau
-weakly convex (see
Section

E.1
), proper, and lower semicontinuous. In this section, we want to apply our unified framework to study the convergence behavior of the well-known proximal stochastic gradient method (
\mathsf{prox}\text{-}\mathsf{SGD}
):

\bm{x}^{k+1}=\mathrm{prox}_{\alpha_{k}\varphi}(\bm{x}^{k}-\alpha_{k}\bm{g}^{k}),

(9)

where
\bm{g}^{k}\approx\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})
is a stochastic approximation of
\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})
,
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a suitable step size sequence, and
\mathrm{prox}_{\alpha_{k}\varphi}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathrm{prox}_{\alpha_{k}\varphi}(\bm{x}):=\argmin_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(\bm{y})+\frac{1}{2\alpha_{k}}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|^{2}
is the well-known proximity operator of
\varphi
.

3.3.1
Assumptions and preparations

We first recall several useful concepts from nonsmooth and variational analysis. For a function
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,\infty]
, the Fréchet (or regular) subdifferential of
h
at the point
\bm{x}
is given by

\lx@nobreakspace\partial h(\bm{x}):=\{\bm{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:h(\bm{y})\geq h(\bm{x})+\langle\bm{g},\bm{y}-\bm{x}\rangle+o(\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}\|)\;\text{as}\;\bm{y}\to\bm{x}\},

see, e.g.,
[
38
, Chapter 8]
. If
h
is convex, then the Fréchet subdifferential coincides with the standard (convex) subdifferential.
It is well-known that the associated first-order optimality condition for the composite problem (
8
) —
0\in\partial\psi(\bm{x})=\nabla f(\bm{x})+\partial\varphi(\bm{x})
— can be represented as a nonsmooth equation,
[
38
,
20
]
,

F_{\mathrm{nat}}^{\alpha}(\bm{x}):=\bm{x}-\mathrm{prox}_{\alpha\varphi}(\bm{x}-\alpha\nabla f(\bm{x}))=0,\quad\alpha\in(0,\tau^{-1}),

where
F_{\mathrm{nat}}^{\alpha}
denotes the so-called
natural residual
. The natural residual
F_{\mathrm{nat}}^{\alpha}
is a common stationarity measure for the nonsmooth problem (
8
) and widely used in the analysis of proximal methods.

We will make the following assumptions on
f
,
\varphi
, and the stochastic oracles
\{\bm{g}^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
:

(C.1)

The function
f
is bounded from below on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e., there is
\bar{f}
such that
f(\bm{x})\geq\bar{f}
for all
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the gradient mapping
\nabla f
is Lipschitz continuous (on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
) with modulus
{\sf L}>0
.

(C.2)

The function
\varphi
is
\tau
-weakly convex, proper, lower semicontinuous, and bounded from below on
\operatorname{dom}\varphi
, i.e., we have
\varphi(\bm{x})\geq\bar{\varphi}
for all
\bm{x}\in\operatorname{dom}\varphi
.

(C.3)

There exists
{\sf L}_{\varphi}>0
such that
\varphi(\bm{x})-\varphi(\bm{y})\leq{\sf L}_{\varphi}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|
for all
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\operatorname{dom}\varphi
.

(C.4)

Each
\bm{g}^{k}
defines an unbiased estimator of
\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})
, i.e., we have
\mathbb{E}[\bm{g}^{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})
almost surely, and there exist
{\sf C},{\sf D}\geq 0
such that

\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{g}^{k}-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq{\sf C}[f(\bm{x}^{k})-\bar{f}]+{\sf D}\quad\text{almost surely}\quad\forall~k\in\mathbb{N}.

(C.5)

The step sizes
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
satisfy
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}=\infty
and
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}^{2}<\infty
.

Here, we again assume that the generated stochastic processes
\{\bm{x}^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is adapted to the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
. The assumptions
(C.1)
,
(C.2)
,
(C.4)
, and
(C.5)
are fairly standard and broadly applicable. In particular,
(C.1)
,
(C.4)
, and
(C.5)
coincide with the conditions
(A.1)
–
(A.4)
used in the analysis of
\mathsf{SGD}
. We continue with several remarks concerning condition
(C.3)
.

Remark 3.3
.

Assumption
(C.3)
requires the mapping
\varphi
to be Lipschitz continuous on its effective domain
\operatorname{dom}\varphi
. This condition holds in many important applications, e.g., when
\varphi
is chosen as a norm or indicator function. Nonconvex examples satisfying
(C.2)
and
(C.3)
include, e.g., the minimax concave penalty (MCP) function
[
44
]
, the smoothly clipped absolute deviation (SCAD)
[
14
]
, or the student-t loss function. We refer to
[
4
]
and
Section

E.2
for further discussion.

3.3.2
Convergence results of prox-SGD

We now analyze the convergence of the random process
\{\bm{x}^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
generated by the stochastic algorithmic scheme (
9
). As pioneered in
[
10
]
, we will use the Moreau envelope
\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}
,

\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R},\quad\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x}):={\min}_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}~\psi(\bm{y})+\frac{1}{2\theta}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|^{2},

(10)

as a smooth Lyapunov function to study the descent properties and convergence of
\mathsf{prox}\text{-}\mathsf{SGD}
.

We first note that the conditions
(C.1)
and
(C.2)
imply
\theta^{-1}
-weak convexity of
\psi
for every
\theta\in(0,({\sf L}+\tau)^{-1}]
. In this case, the Moreau envelope
\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}
is a well-defined and continuously differentiable function with gradient
\nabla\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{\theta}(\bm{x}-\mathrm{prox}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x}))
; see, e.g.,
[
37
, Theorem 31.5]
.

As shown in
[
12
,
10
]
, the norm of the Moreau envelope —
\|\nabla\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(x)\|
— defines an alternative stationarity measure for problem (
8
) that is equivalent to the natural residual if
\theta
is chosen sufficiently small. A more explicit derivation of this connection is provided in
Lemma

E.1
.

Next, we establish convergence of
\mathsf{prox}\text{-}\mathsf{SGD}
by setting
\bm{\Phi}\equiv\nabla\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}
and
\mu_{k}\equiv\alpha_{k}
. Our analysis is based on the following two estimates which are verified in
Section

E.4
and
Section

E.5
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\{\bm{x}^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be generated by
\mathsf{prox}\text{-}\mathsf{SGD}
and let the assumptions
(C.1)
–
(C.4)
be satisfied. Then, for
\theta\in(0,[3{\sf L}+\tau]^{-1})
and all
k
with
\alpha_{k}\leq\min\{\frac{1}{2\tau},\frac{1}{2(\theta^{-1}-[{\sf L}+\tau])}\}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x}^{k+1})-\bar{\psi}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]

\displaystyle\leq(1+4{\sf C}\theta^{-1}\alpha_{k}^{2})\cdot[\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x}^{k})-\bar{\psi}]

\displaystyle\hskip 17.22217pt-{\sf L}\theta\alpha_{k}\|\nabla\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{2}+2\alpha_{k}^{2}({\sf C}{\sf L}_{\varphi}^{2}+{\sf D}{\theta^{-1}}),

(11)

almost surely, where
\bar{\psi}:=\bar{f}+\bar{\varphi}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\{\bm{x}^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be generated by
\mathsf{prox}\text{-}\mathsf{SGD}
and suppose that the assumptions
(C.1)
–
(C.4)
hold. Then, for
\theta\in(0,[\frac{4}{3}{\sf L}+\tau]^{-1})
and all
k
with
\alpha_{k}\leq\frac{1}{2\tau}
, we have almost surely

\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{x}^{k+1}-\bm{x}^{k}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq 8(2{\sf L}+{\sf C})\alpha_{k}^{2}\cdot[\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x}^{k})-\bar{\psi}]+4(((2{\sf L}+{\sf C})\theta+1){\sf L}_{\varphi}^{2}+{\sf D})\alpha_{k}^{2}.

(12)

Phase I: Verifying
(P.1)
–
(P.2)
.
In
[
20
, Corollary 3.4]
, it is shown that the gradient of the Moreau envelope is Lipschitz continuous with modulus
{\sf L}_{e}:=\max\{{\theta^{-1}},(1-[{\sf L}+\tau]\theta)^{-1}{[{\sf L}+\tau]}\}
for all
\theta\in(0,[{\sf L}+\tau]^{-1})
.
Thus, condition
(P.1)
is satisfied.

Furthermore, due to
\alpha_{k}\to 0
and choosing
\theta\in(0,[3{\sf L}+\tau]^{-1})
, the estimate (
11
) in
Lemma

3.4
holds for all
k
sufficiently large. Consequently, due to
\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x})\geq\psi(\mathrm{prox}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x}))\geq\bar{\psi}
and
(C.5)
,
B.1
is applicable and upon taking total expectation,
\{\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x}^{k})]\}_{k\geq 0}
converges to some
{\sf E}\in\mathbb{R}
. In addition, the sequence
\{\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x}^{k})\}_{k\geq 0}
converges almost surely to some random variable
e^{\star}
and we have
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{2}]<\infty
. This verifies condition
(P.2)
with
a=2
.

Phase II: Verifying
(i)(P.3)
–
(i)(P.4)
for showing convergence in expectation.
Assumptions
(C.1)
–
(C.5)
and
Lemma

3.5
allow us to establish the required bound stated in
(i)(P.3)
. Specifically, taking total expectation in (
12
), we have

\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{x}^{k+1}-\bm{x}^{k}\|^{2}]\leq 8(2{\sf L}+{\sf C})\alpha_{k}^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x}^{k})-\bar{\psi}]+4(((2{\sf L}+{\sf C})\theta+1){\sf L}_{\varphi}^{2}+{\sf D})\alpha_{k}^{2}

for all
k
sufficiently large. Due to
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x}^{k})]\to{\sf E}
, there exists
{\sf F}
such that
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x}^{k})-\bar{\psi}]\leq{\sf F}
for all
k
. Hence,
(i)(P.3)
holds with
q=2
,
{\sf A}=8(2{\sf L}+{\sf C}){\sf F}+4(((2{\sf L}+{\sf C})\theta+1){\sf L}_{\varphi}^{2}+{\sf D})
,
p_{1}=2
, and
{\sf B}=0
. The property
(i)(P.4)
easily follows from
(C.5)
and the parameter choices. Consequently, using
2.1
, we can infer
\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x}^{k})\|]\to 0
.

Phase III: Verifying
(ii)(P.3′)
–
(ii)(P.4′)
for showing almost sure convergence.
We follow the construction in (
4
) and set
\bm{A}_{k}=\alpha_{k}^{-1}(\bm{x}^{k+1}-\bm{x}^{k}-\mathbb{E}[\bm{x}^{k+1}-\bm{x}^{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}])
,
\bm{B}_{k}=\alpha_{k}^{-1}\mathbb{E}[\bm{x}^{k+1}-\bm{x}^{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]
, and
p_{1},p_{2}=1
.
Clearly, we have
\mathbb{E}[\bm{A}_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=0
and based on the previous results in
Phase II
, we can show
\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{x}^{k+1}-\bm{x}^{k}\|^{2}]=\mathcal{O}(\alpha_{k}^{2})
which establishes boundedness of
\{\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{A}_{k}\|^{2}\}_{k\geq 0}
. Similarly, for
\bm{B}_{k}
and by
Lemma

3.5
and Jensen’s inequality, we obtain

\lx@nobreakspace\|\bm{B}_{k}\|^{2}\leq\alpha_{k}^{-2}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{x}^{k+1}-\bm{x}^{k}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq 8(2{\sf L}+{\sf C})\cdot[\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x}^{k})-\bar{\psi}]+\mathcal{O}(1).

Due to
\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x}^{k})\to e^{\star}
almost surely, this shows
\limsup_{k\to\infty}\|\bm{B}_{k}\|^{2}<\infty
almost surely. Hence, all requirements in
(ii)(P.3′)
are satisfied with
q=2
and
b=0
. Moreover, it is easy to see that property
(ii)(P.4′)
also holds in this case. Overall,
2.1
implies
\|\nabla\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x}^{k})\|\to 0
almost surely.

As mentioned, it is possible to express the obtained convergence results in terms of the natural residual
F_{\mathrm{nat}}=F_{\mathrm{nat}}^{1}
, see, e.g.,
Lemma

E.1
. We summarize our observations in the following corollary.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let us consider
\mathsf{prox}\text{-}\mathsf{SGD}
(
9
) for the composite problem (
8
) under
(C.1)
–
(C.5)
. Then, we have
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\|F_{\mathrm{nat}}(\bm{x}^{k})\|]=0
and
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|F_{\mathrm{nat}}(\bm{x}^{k})\|=0
almost surely.

Remark 3.7
.

As a byproduct,
Lemma

3.4
also leads to an expected iteration complexity result of
\mathsf{prox}\text{-}\mathsf{SGD}
by using the ABC condition
(C.4)
rather than the standard bounded variance assumption. This is a nontrivial extension of
[
10
, Corollary 3.6]
. We provide a full derivation in
Section

E.6
.

3.4
Convergence of stochastic model-based methods

In this section, we consider the convergence of stochastic model-based methods for nonsmooth weakly convex optimization problems

{\min}_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}~\psi(\bm{x}):=f(\bm{x})+\varphi(\bm{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim D}[f(\bm{x},\xi)]+\varphi(\bm{x}),

(13)

where both
f
and
\varphi
are assumed to be (nonsmooth) weakly convex functions and
\psi
is lower bounded, i.e.,
\psi(\bm{x})\geq\bar{\psi}
for all
\bm{x}\in\operatorname{dom}\varphi
. Classical stochastic optimization methods — including proximal stochastic subgradient, stochastic proximal point, and stochastic prox-linear methods — for solving (
13
) are unified by the stochastic model-based methods (
\mathsf{SMM}
)
[
13
,
10
]
:

\bm{x}^{k+1}={\argmin}_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\ f_{\bm{x}^{k}}(\bm{x},\xi^{k})+\varphi(\bm{x})+\frac{1}{2\alpha_{k}}\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{k}\|^{2},

(14)

where
f_{\bm{x}^{k}}(\bm{x},\xi^{k})
is a stochastic approximation of
f
around
\bm{x}^{k}
using the sample
\xi^{k}
; see
Section

F.1
for descriptions of three major types of
\mathsf{SMM}
. Setting
\mathcal{F}_{k}:=\sigma(\xi^{0},\ldots,\xi^{k-1})
, it is easy to see that
\{\bm{x}^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is adapted to
\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
.
We analyze convergence of
\mathsf{SMM}
under the following assumptions.

(D.1)

The stochastic approximation function
f_{\bm{x}}
satisfies a one-sided accuracy property, i.e., we have
\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[f_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x},\xi)]=f(\bm{x})
for all
\bm{x}\in U
and

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[f_{\bm{x}}(\bm{y},\xi)-f(\bm{y})]\leq\frac{\tau}{2}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|^{2}\quad\forall~\bm{x},\bm{y}\in U,

where
U
is an open convex set containing
\operatorname{dom}\varphi
.

(D.2)

The function
\bm{y}\mapsto f_{\bm{x}}(\bm{y},\xi)+\varphi(\bm{y})
is
\eta
-weakly convex for all
\bm{x}\in U
and almost every
\xi
.

(D.3)

There exists
{\sf L}>0
such that the stochastic approximation function
f_{\bm{x}}
satisfies

f_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x},\xi)-f_{\bm{x}}(\bm{y},\xi)\leq{\sf L}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|\quad\forall~\bm{x},\bm{y}\in U,\quad\text{and almost every $\xi$}.

(D.4)

The function
\varphi
is
{\sf L}_{\varphi}
-Lipschitz continuous.

(D.5)

The step sizes
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
satisfy
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}=\infty
and
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}^{2}<\infty
.

Assumptions
(D.1)
,
(D.2)
,
(D.3)
are standard for analyzing
\mathsf{SMM}
and identical to that of
[
10
]
.
(D.5)
is convention for stochastic methods. Assumption
(D.4)
mimics
(C.3)
; see
Remark

3.3
for discussions.

We now derive the convergence of
\mathsf{SMM}
below by setting
\bm{\Phi}\equiv\nabla\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}
and
\mu_{k}\equiv\alpha_{k}
. Our derivation is based on the following two estimates, in which the proof of
Lemma

3.9
is given in
Section

F.2
.

Lemma 3.8

(Theorem 4.3 of
[
10
]
)
.

Let
\theta\in(0,(\tau+\eta)^{-1})
and
\alpha_{k}<\theta
be given. Then, we have

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x}^{k+1})\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x}^{k})-\frac{(1-[\tau+\eta]\theta)\alpha_{k}}{2(1-\eta\alpha_{k})}\|\nabla\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{2}+\frac{2{\sf L}^{2}\alpha_{k}^{2}}{(1-\eta\alpha_{k})(\theta-\alpha_{k})}.

Lemma 3.9
.

For all
k
with
\alpha_{k}\leq 1/{(2\eta)}
, it holds that

\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{x}^{k+1}-\bm{x}^{k}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq(16({\sf L}+{\sf L}_{\varphi})^{2}+8{\sf L}^{2})\alpha_{k}^{2}.

Phase I: Verifying
(P.1)
–
(P.2)
.
As before,
[
20
, Corollary 3.4]
implies that the mapping
\nabla\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}
is Lipschitz continuous for all
\theta\in(0,(\tau+\eta)^{-1})

Hence, condition
(P.1)
is satisfied. Using
\alpha_{k}\to 0
, we can apply
B.1
to the recursion obtained in
Lemma

3.8
for all
k
sufficiently large and it follows
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{2}]<\infty
. Thus, condition
(P.2)
holds with
a=2
.

Phase II: Verifying
(i)(P.3)
–
(i)(P.4)
for showing convergence in expectation.
Taking total expectation in
Lemma

3.9
verifies condition
(i)(P.3)
with
q=2
,
{\sf A}=(16({\sf L}+{\sf L}_{\varphi})^{2}+8{\sf L}^{2})
,
p_{1}=2
,
{\sf B}=0
.
Moreover, condition
(i)(P.4)
is true by assumption
(D.5)
and the previous parameters choices. Thus, applying
2.1
gives
\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x}^{k})\|]\to 0
.

Phase III: Verifying
(ii)(P.3′)
–
(ii)(P.4′)
for showing almost sure convergence.

As in (
4
), we can set
\bm{A}_{k}=\alpha_{k}^{-1}(\bm{x}^{k+1}-\bm{x}^{k}-\mathbb{E}[\bm{x}^{k+1}-\bm{x}^{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}])
,
\bm{B}_{k}=\alpha_{k}^{-1}\mathbb{E}[\bm{x}^{k+1}-\bm{x}^{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]
. Applying
Lemma

3.9
and utilizing Jensen’s inequality, we have
\mathbb{E}[\bm{A}_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=0
,
\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{A}_{k}\|^{2}]\leq(4/\alpha_{k}^{2})\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{x}^{k+1}-\bm{x}^{k}\|^{2}]\leq 4(16({\sf L}+{\sf L}_{\varphi})^{2}+8{\sf L}^{2})
and
\|\bm{B}_{k}\|^{2}\leq 16({\sf L}+{\sf L}_{\varphi})^{2}+8{\sf L}^{2}
. Thus, condition
(ii)(P.3′)
is satisfied with
p_{1}=p_{2}=1
,
q=2
. Assumption
(D.5)
, together with the previous parameter choices verifies condition
(ii)(P.4′)
and hence, applying
2.1
yields
\|\nabla\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x}^{k})\|\to 0
almost surely.

Summarizing this discussion, we obtain the following convergence results for
\mathsf{SMM}
.

Corollary 3.10
.

We consider the family of stochastic model-based methods (
14
) for the optimization problem (
13
) under assumptions
(D.1)
–
(D.5)
. Let
\{\bm{x}^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be a generated sequence. Then, we have
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x}^{k})\|]=0
and
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\nabla\mathrm{env}_{\theta\psi}(\bm{x}^{k})\|=0
almost surely.

Remark 3.11
.

The results presented in
Corollary

3.10
also hold under certain extended settings. In fact, we can replace
(D.3)
by a slightly more general Lipschitz continuity assumption on
f
. Moreover, it is possible to establish convergence in the case where
f
is not Lipschitz continuous but has Lipschitz continuous gradient, which is particularly useful when we apply stochastic proximal point method for smooth
f
. A more detailed derivation and discussion of such extensions is deferred to
Section

F.3
.

Conclusion

In this work, we provided a novel convergence framework that allows to derive expected and almost sure convergence results for a vast class of stochastic optimization methods under state-of-the-art assumptions and in a unified way. We specified the steps on how to utilize our theorem in order to establish convergence results for a given stochastic algorithm. As concrete examples, we applied our theorem to derive asymptotic convergence guarantees for
\mathsf{SGD}
,
{\mathsf{RR}}
,
\mathsf{prox}\text{-}\mathsf{SGD}
, and
\mathsf{SMM}
. To our surprise, some of the obtained results appear to be new and provide new insights into the convergence behavior of some well-known and standard stochastic methodologies.
These applications revealed that our unified theorem can serve as a plugin-type tool with the potential to facilitate the convergence analysis of a wide class of stochastic optimization methods.

Finally, it is important to investigate in which situations our convergence results in terms of the stationarity measure
\bm{\Phi}
can be strengthened — say to almost sure convergence guarantees for the iterates
\{\bm{x}^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
. We plan to consider such a possible extension in future work.

E.1
Weakly convex functions

A function
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,\infty]
is said to be
\tau
-weakly convex if

h+\frac{\tau}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}

is convex (on its effective domain
\operatorname{dom}h=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:h(\bm{x})<\infty\}
). The class of weakly convex functions covers many important nonsmooth nonconvex problems. For instance, any function that has the composite form

h(\bm{x})=r(c(\bm{x}))

with
r
being convex Lipschitz continuous and the Jacobian of
c
being Lipschitz continuous is weakly convex. We refer to
[
10
]
for more discussions.

If
h
is
\tau
-weakly convex, proper, and lower semicontinuous, then the proximity operator
\mathrm{prox}_{\alpha h}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, given by

\mathrm{prox}_{\alpha h}(\bm{x}):=\argmin_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h(\bm{y})+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|^{2},

is a well-defined function for all
\alpha\in(0,\tau^{-1})
and Lipschitz continuous with constant
(1-\alpha\tau)^{-1}
, see, e.g.,
[
38
, Proposition 12.19]
or
[
20
, Proposition 3.3]
. The
\tau
-weak convexity is equivalent to

h(\bm{y})\geq h(\bm{x})+\langle\bm{s},\bm{y}-\bm{x}\rangle-\frac{\tau}{2}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|^{2},\quad\forall~\bm{x},\bm{y},\ \ \forall~\bm{s}\in\partial h(\bm{x})

(22)

see, e.g.,
[
43
,
10
]
.

E.2
Examples of weakly convex and Lipschitz continuous regularizers

In this section, we discuss several common nonconvex regularizers that can be shown to be weakly convex and Lipschitz continuous. The functions presented here have been mentioned in
Remark

3.3
.

The minimax concave penalty (MCP), introduced in
[
44
]
, is the parametrized function
\varphi_{\lambda,\theta}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
given by

\varphi_{\lambda,\theta}(x):=\begin{cases}\lambda|x|-\frac{x^{2}}{2\theta}&\text{if }|x|\leq\theta\lambda,\\
\frac{\theta\lambda^{2}}{2}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\lambda,\theta>0
are two positive parameters. This function is
\theta^{-1}
-weakly convex and smooth for
x\neq 0
. Discussing the subdifferential
\partial\varphi_{\lambda,\theta}(x)
and using
[
38
, Theorem 9.13]
, it can be shown that
\varphi_{\lambda,\theta}
is Lipschitz continuous on
\mathbb{R}
with modulus
\lambda
.

The smoothly clipped absolute deviation (SCAD)
[
14
]
is defined by

\varphi_{\lambda,\theta}(x):=\begin{cases}\lambda|x|&\text{if }|x|\leq\lambda,\\
\frac{-x^{2}+2\theta\lambda|x|-\lambda^{2}}{2(\theta-1)}&\text{if }\lambda<|x|\leq\theta\lambda,\\
\frac{(\theta+1)\lambda^{2}}{2}&\text{if }|x|>\theta\lambda,\end{cases}

where
\lambda>0
and
\theta>2
are given parameters. The SCAD function is
(\theta-1)^{-1}
-weakly convex and Lipschitz continuous with modulus
\lambda
.

The student-t loss function is given by
\varphi_{\theta}(x):=\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}\log(1+\theta^{-2}x^{2})
for some
\theta\neq 0
. The first- and second-order derivative of
\varphi_{\theta}
can be calculated as follows:

\varphi_{\theta}^{\prime}(x)=\frac{\theta^{2}x}{\theta^{2}+x^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi_{\theta}^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{\theta^{2}(\theta^{2}-x^{2})}{(\theta^{2}+x^{2})^{2}}.

Some simple computations then show that
\varphi_{\theta}
is
\frac{1}{8}
-weakly convex and Lipschitz continuous with modulus
{|\theta|}/{2}
. Additional examples can be found in
[
4
]
.

Appendix H
Non-asymptotic complexity vs. asymptotic convergence

In this subsection, we provide several additional arguments and illustrations that can help to explain and illuminate the potential differences between typical finite-step complexity rates and asymptotic convergence results — as obtained in
2.1
. To this end, we consider the standard optimization problem

\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(\bm{x}),

(36)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a given, smooth, and nonconvex function. As motivated in the introduction, complexity bounds for the nonconvex problem (
36
) typically take the form

\min_{k=0,\dots,T}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{2}]=\mathcal{O}(({T+1})^{-\frac{1}{2}})\quad\text{or}\quad\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{\bar{k}})\|^{2}]\leq\mathcal{O}(({T+1})^{-\frac{1}{2}}).

(37)

Here,
T
denotes the total number of iterations, the index
\bar{k}
is sampled uniformly at random from
\{0,\dots,T\}
, and the iterates
\{\bm{x}^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
are assumed to be generated by the stochastic gradient descent method, see
[
16
,
6
,
23
,
22
]
. Similar (deterministic) complexity results are also available for the basic gradient descent method,
[
2
]
, and many other related algorithmic schemes,
[
17
,
10
,
29
,
31
]
. In particular, for the gradient descent method, the complexity bounds (
37
) can be strengthened to

\min_{k=0,\dots,T}\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}(({T+1})^{-1}),

(38)

see, e.g.,
[
2
, Theorem 10.15]
.

While the complexity bounds shown in (
37
) (and (
38
)) allow to capture and characterize the overall trend of the minimization procedure during the first
T
iterations, they cannot fully justify a common practice in stochastic optimization: the last iterate
\bm{x}^{T}
is returned as final output of the algorithm. In fact, even as
T
increases, the term
\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{T})\|]
can be arbitrarily large whereas the complexity measure
\min_{k=0,\dots,T}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{2}]
decreases at its respective rate. The asymptotic convergence results

\lx@nobreakspace\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})\|]=0\quad\text{or}\quad\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})\|=0\quad\text{almost surely},

can provide additional information: as
\{\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})\|]\}_{k\geq 0}
converges to zero, the term
\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{T})\|]
will stay small (below any predefined threshold) for all
T
sufficiently large. In tandem with the complexity bounds (
37
), this supports common output strategies that return the last iterate
\bm{x}^{T}
— at least for large
T
. Hence, both non-asymptotic and asymptotic convergence analyses are important and informative — especially in the nonconvex and stochastic setting — and the combination of non-asymptotic and asymptotic convergence guarantees can paint a more complete picture of the convergence behavior of stochastic optimization methods.

We continue with a specific example that can illustrate the mentioned discrepancies and differences between non-asymptotic complexity and asymptotic convergence results. In particular, we construct a nonconvex function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, a corresponding step size sequence
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
, and an initial point
\bm{x}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, for which the standard gradient descent method generates a sequence of iterates
\{\bm{x}^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
that satisfies the non-asymptotic complexity bound (
38
), but we can
not
observe asymptotic convergence
f^{\prime}(\bm{x}^{k})\to 0
.

Figure 1:
Plot of
f
. The function
f
is depicted using a blue color. The dotted orange lines correspond to the functions
x\mapsto x
and
x\mapsto\frac{1}{2}x^{2}

Let

be given parameters. We consider the functions

\lx@nobreakspace c(x):=\begin{cases}e^{-\frac{1}{x}}&\text{if }x>0,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{c}_{\kappa,\nu}(x):=\frac{c(\kappa^{2}-x)}{c(\kappa^{2}-x)+c(x-\nu^{2})}.

The mappings
c
and
\bar{c}_{\kappa,\nu}
are obviously

and we have
\bar{c}_{\kappa,\nu}(x)=0

We now define
\kappa_{j}:=\frac{1}{4j(2j+1)}
,
\nu_{j}:=\frac{1}{8j(2j+1)}
,
g(x):=x-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}
,
\gamma_{j}(x):=g(x)\bar{c}_{\kappa_{j},\nu_{j}}((x-\frac{1}{2j})^{2})
, and

f(x):=\begin{cases}h(x)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{k}(x)&\text{if }x\neq 0,\\
\lx@nobreakspace 0&\text{if }x=0,\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad h(x):=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}x^{2}&\text{if }x\geq 0,\\
8x^{2}(8x^{2}-1)&\text{if }x<0.\end{cases}

An exemplary plot of the function

is shown in
Figure

1
. The function

is continuous on
\mathbb{R}
and smooth for all

f

\bm{x}^{0}=1

\lx@nobreakspace\alpha_{k}:=\frac{1}{k+2},\;\;\text{if $k$ is even}\quad\text{and}\quad\lx@nobreakspace\alpha_{k}:=\frac{1}{(k+1)(k+2)},\;\;\text{if $k$ is odd.}

Then, it follows

\lx@nobreakspace\bm{x}^{k}=\frac{1}{k+1}\quad\text{and}\quad f^{\prime}(\bm{x}^{k})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $k$ is even},\\
\frac{1}{k+1}&\text{if $k$ is odd}.\end{cases}

(39)

We now verify this statement by induction. We first notice that the functions
\{\gamma_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
and derivatives
\{\gamma_{k}^{\prime}\}_{k\geq 0}
have disjoint supports
[\frac{1}{2k}-\frac{1}{4k(2k+1)},\frac{1}{2k}+\frac{1}{4k(2k+1)}]=[\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{2k}+\frac{1}{2k+1}),\frac{1}{2}(\frac{3}{2k}-\frac{1}{2k+1})]
with center point
\frac{1}{2k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. More specifically, we have

\gamma_{k}(x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }|x-\frac{1}{2k}|\geq\frac{1}{4k(2k+1)},\\
g(x)&\text{if }|x-\frac{1}{2k}|\leq\frac{1}{8k(2k+1)},\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma_{k}^{\prime}(x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }|x-\frac{1}{2k}|\geq\frac{1}{4k(2k+1)},\\
g^{\prime}(x)&\text{if }|x-\frac{1}{2k}|\leq\frac{1}{8k(2k+1)}.\end{cases}

Hence, (
39
) clearly holds for the base case
k=0
. Let us now assume that the induction hypothesis (
39
) is true for some
k
and let
k+1=2j
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
, be an even number. Then, we obtain

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\bm{x}^{k}-\alpha_{k}f^{\prime}(\bm{x}^{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k+1}-\frac{1}{(k+1)(k+2)}\cdot\left[\frac{1}{k+1}+\gamma_{j}^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{k+1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k+1}-\frac{1}{(k+1)(k+2)}\cdot 1=\frac{1}{k+2}.

Similarly, if
k+1=2j+1
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
, is odd, we then have
\frac{1}{2j}-\frac{1}{k+1}=\frac{1}{2j(2j+1)}>\frac{1}{4j(2j+1)}
and
\frac{1}{k+1}-\frac{1}{2j+2}=\frac{1}{(2j+1)(2j+2)}>\frac{1}{(2j+2)(2j+3)}
. This yields

\lx@nobreakspace\bm{x}^{k+1}=\bm{x}^{k}-\alpha_{k}f^{\prime}(\bm{x}^{k})=\frac{1}{k+1}-\frac{1}{k+2}\cdot\frac{1}{k+1}=\frac{1}{k+2}.

This finishes the proof of (
39

\lx@nobreakspace\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}\geq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{2k}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2(k+1)}=\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}^{2}<\infty.

Consequently, the step sizes
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k}
satisfy all standard requirements. In addition, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}|f^{\prime}(\bm{x}^{k})|^{2}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(k+1)(k+2)}<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\min_{k=0,\dots,T}|f^{\prime}(\bm{x}^{k})|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{T^{2}}.

Thus, the complexity results in (

do not converge to zero. In particular, for even

\bm{x}^{T}

which obstructs interpretability of
\bm{x}^{T}
and of the complexity bounds
\min_{k=0,\dots,T}|f^{\prime}(\bm{x}^{k})|^{2}\leq\varepsilon
or
|f^{\prime}(\bm{x}^{\bar{k}})|^{2}\leq\varepsilon

is not Lipschitz smooth around

are not applicable here. Of course, these observations have even higher significance in the stochastic setting, when evaluation of the bounds (
37

We conclude and summarize our discussion with a comment by Francesco Orabona on non-asymptotic and asymptotic convergence analyses for
\mathsf{SGD}
(
[
32
]
, blog post: “
Almost sure convergence of SGD on smooth non-convex functions
”, section 5 and 6, Oct. 05, 2020):

“Note that the 20-30 years ago there were many papers studying the asymptotic convergence of SGD and its variants in various settings. Then, the taste of the community changed moving from asymptotic convergence to finite-time rates. As it often happens when a new trend takes over the previous one, new generations tend to be oblivious to the old results and proof techniques. The common motivation to ignore these past results is that the finite-time analysis is superior to the asymptotic one, but this is clearly false (ask a statistician!). It should be instead clear to anyone that both analyses have pros and cons.”
