Frequency analysis and filter design for directed graphs with polar decomposition
Semin Kwak, Laura Shimabukuro, Antonio Ortega

Abstract
In this study, we challenge the traditional approach of frequency analysis on directed graphs, which typically relies on a single measure of signal variation such as total variation. We argue that the inherent directionality in directed graphs necessitates a multifaceted analytical approach that incorporates multiple signal variations definitions. Our methodology leverages the polar decomposition to define two distinct variations, each associated with different matrices derived from this decomposition. This approach provides a novel interpretation in the node domain and reveals aspects of graph signals that may be overlooked with a singular measure of variation. Additionally, we develop graph filters specifically designed to smooth graph signals in accordance with our proposed variations. These filters allow for bypassing costly filtering operations associated with the original graph through effective cascading. We demonstrate the efficacy of our methodology using an M-block cyclic graph example, validating our claims and showcasing the advantages of our multifaceted approach in analyzing signals on directed graphs. † † footnotetext: All figures presented in this manuscript are reproducible using the code available at https://github.com/semink/ICASSP2024 .

Introduction

Graph signal processing (GSP) has gained significant attention by effectively integrating traditional signal processing techniques with graph theory, enabling data processing on irregular domains
[
1
]
.
Many GSP results are well-developed for undirected graphs, leveraging the symmetry of edge connections, which ensures that graph operators (also known as shift operators) of undirected graphs, such as the adjacency matrix and the graph Laplacian, are diagonalizable, leading to the definition of the Graph Fourier Transform (GFT)
[
2
]
.

On the other hand, despite a growing need for applications that involve directed graphs
(digraphs)
[
3
,
4
]
,
the intrinsic asymmetry of their edge connections presents challenges in defining GFTs. First, the basic graph operators become non-diagonalizable for many digraphs
[
5
,
6
]
.
Second, “ordering” the graph Fourier basis to interpret them as low or high frequencies is difficult because the eigenvalues, which can be used for the ordering, can be complex-valued. As a consequence, a variety of digraph GFTs have been proposed to address these two challenges.

For example, studies directly using the adjacency matrix as the graph operator have suggested utilizing the Jordan decomposition
[
7
,
8
]
or the singular value decomposition
[
9
]
of the adjacency matrix for constructing GFT.
Alternatively, a recent study adds a minimal number of edges so that the corresponding graph operator on the augmented graph becomes diagonalizable
[
10
]
.
Symmetrization of the adjacency matrix has also been studied
[
11
,
12
]
.
Finally, other studies define the graph operator as decomposed matrices of adjacency matrix, producing diagonalizable matrices
[
13
,
14
]
.

Although differing in their definition of GFTs, these previous studies coincide in using a single graph operator to measure signal variation, which is the key property in ordering the graph Fourier basis.
Instead, we propose that analyzing the smoothness of signals on digraphs using only a single variation can be unnecessarily restrictive. Specifically, we suggest considering both (i) signal variation between disconnected nodes (which we call
indirect variation
) and (ii) variation between connected nodes (which we call
in-flow variation
) on a graph that has been minimally augmented to permit circular flows.
These two variations offer complementary insights about signals on digraphs.
We utilize the polar decomposition of an adjacency matrix, which results in two different terms: 1) a unitary matrix and 2) a positive semidefinite matrix, and show that the signal variations based on those matrices are related to the in-flow and indirect variations, respectively.

There have been prior attempts to provide a spectral representation of each term of the polar decomposition
[
13
,
14
]
. However, these approaches examined only one term from the polar decomposition and did not develop an interpretation of the resulting frequencies in the node domain.
Here, we connect the polar decomposition with the in-flow and indirect variations, providing a coherent node-domain interpretation.
A significant advancement presented in this study compared to our preliminary pre-print work
[
15
]
is the formulation of graph filters grounded in polar decomposition.
We demonstrate that the proposed graph filters, based on polynomials of graph operators derived from the polar decomposition, provide an alternative to those based on polynomials of the adjacency matrix. Importantly, this design negates the need for spectral domain filtering and the associated computationally expensive eigendecomposition.
With M-block cyclic graphs
[
16
]
, we elucidate the applicability of these filters on graph signals and demonstrate that analyzing graph signals by leveraging polar decomposition, considering both in-flow and indirect variations, provides richer information than analyses based solely on a single variation.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
, we provide key notations and our definition of the GFT.
In Section
3
, we introduce polar decomposition in the context of digraphs. In Section
4
, we analyze graph signal variation via polar decomposition, provide a precise node-domain interpretation, and filter designs based on the polar decomposition. The effectiveness of our proposed approach is subsequently validated in Section
5
.
Finally, Section
6
offers a conclusion and summarizes our findings.

2.2
Graph Fourier transform (GFT)

Definition 1

(Total variation on
graphs
[
7
]
)

Let

\sigma(\mathbf{s})=\lVert{{\mathbf{A}}_{\text{norm}}\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{s}}\rVert_{1},

Definition 2

(Graph Fourier transform on directed graphs
[
7
]
)
.

Consider a graph
{\cal G}({\mathbf{A}})
with diagonalizable adjacency matrix:

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{X}^{-1}.

The set of eigenvectors (columns of
\mathbf{X}
) is termed as the graph Fourier basis of
{\cal G}({\mathbf{A}})

When the basis is ordered such that for
i<j
, the eigenvector
\mathbf{x}_{i}
exhibits lower variation than or equal to that of
\mathbf{x}_{j}
for a given variation measure
\mu
, the ordered matrix
\mathbf{X}
is denoted as
\mathbf{GFT}^{-1}_{\mathbf{A}|\mu}
and its inverse
\mathbf{X}^{-1}
as
\mathbf{GFT}_{\mathbf{A}|\mu}
.

.

When
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, if
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}
then
\mathbf{A}\bar{\mathbf{x}}=\bar{\lambda}\bar{\mathbf{x}}
.

Remark 2.2
.

For simplicity, throughout this paper, we focus on diagonalizable adjacency matrices
\mathbf{A}
to discuss the graph Fourier transform (GFT), but our proposed method applies to non-diagonalizable
\mathbf{A}
.

Remark 2.3
.

Also, for brevity, we omit the sub-index
\mu
(variation measure function) when it is the total variation, i.e., if
\mu=\sigma

\mathbf{GFT}_{\mathbf{A}|\sigma}=\mathbf{GFT}_{\mathbf{A}}
.

Remark 2.4
.

The
i^{\text{th}}

\ell_{1}
-norm normalized eigenvector
\mathbf{x}_{i}
of
\mathbf{A}
is such that
\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{i})=\left|{1-\frac{\lambda_{i}({\mathbf{A}})}{{\text{max}}\left({\left|{\boldsymbol{\lambda}(\mathbf{A})}\right|}\right)}}\right|

Graphs

Remark 3.1
.

For brevity, we omit

{\cal G}({\mathbf{A}_{1}}){\cong}{\cal G}({\mathbf{A}_{2}})

{\cal G}({\mathbf{A}_{1}})\overset{\sigma}{\cong}{\cal G}({\mathbf{A}_{2}})

3
Polar decomposition

We propose to use the polar decomposition to analyze graph signals on directed graphs more effectively.
The polar decomposition of a matrix is analogous to the polar form of a complex value (
z=re^{j\theta}
, where
r\geq 0
and
j=\sqrt{-1}
). It decomposes a matrix through the matrix multiplication of a unitary matrix (analogous to
e^{j\theta}
)
and a positive semi-definite matrix (analogous to
r
):

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{F}\mathbf{Q},

(1)

where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
,
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{U}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
,
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, and
\mathbf{F}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
.

The polar decomposition can be obtained from the singular value decomposition.
For a given matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, the singular value decomposition is represented as
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}
, where
\mathbf{U}\in\mathcal{U}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
,
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{D}_{n}(\mathbb{R})\cap\mathcal{S}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, and
\mathbf{V}\in\mathcal{U}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
.
Consequently, we obtain the terms in (
1
) as
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}
,
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}
, and
\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{U}^{\mathsf{T}}

[
15
]
.

Given that
\mathbf{Q}
is a real-valued unitary matrix, all eigenvalues of
\mathbf{Q}
lie on the unit circle and come in conjugate pairs with associated conjugate eigenvectors.
Consequently, eigenvectors can be ordered by the phase of associated eigenvalues utilizing the variation measure
\mu_{\omega}({\lambda(\mathbf{Q}))}=\omega_{\mathbf{Q}}
for the eigenvalue
\lambda(\mathbf{Q})=e^{j\omega_{\mathbf{Q}}}
(
-\pi\leq\omega_{\mathbf{Q}}\leq\pi
), therefore, the eigenvector can be associated to the corresponding graph (angular) frequency
\omega_{\mathbf{Q}}
.
As a result, the graph Fourier transform of
{\cal G}({\mathbf{Q}})
is defined as
\mathbf{GFT}_{\mathbf{Q}|\mu_{\omega}}
.

In contrast, eigenvalues of
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{F}
are non-negative real values due to the positive semi-definite nature of these matrices. As a result, the total variation of normalized eigenvectors for these matrices is confined within the interval
[0,1]
(refer to Remark
2.4
).
The graph Fourier transforms of
{\cal G}({\mathbf{P}})
and
{\cal G}({\mathbf{F}})
are defined as
\mathbf{GFT}_{\mathbf{P}}
and
\mathbf{GFT}_{\mathbf{F}}
, respectively.

4
Node-domain interpretation

In this section, we clarify how the respective graph signal variations observed in
{\cal G}({\mathbf{P}})
,
{\cal G}({\mathbf{F}})
and
{\cal G}({\mathbf{Q}})
relate to the signal variation of the primary graph
{\cal G}({\mathbf{A}})
from a node-domain perspective.

The graphs
{\cal G}({\mathbf{P}})
and
{\cal G}({\mathbf{F}})
are directly related to the symmetrizations of the directed graph
{\cal G}({\mathbf{A}})
.
With
\mathbf{B}_{\text{in}}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{T}}
and
\mathbf{C}_{\text{out}}=\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{A}
,
{\cal G}({\mathbf{B}_{\text{in}}})
can be viewed as a graph with edges connecting nodes that share common in-links on the original graph
{\cal G}({\mathbf{A}})
, while
{\cal G}({\mathbf{C}_{\text{out}}})
can be viewed as a graph with edges connecting nodes that share common out-links (see Fig.
1
)
[
11
]
.
Since
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}
, their eigendecompositions are:

\mathbf{B}_{\text{in}}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2}\mathbf{U}^{\mathsf{T}}\text{ and }\mathbf{C}_{\text{out}}=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2}\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}.

(2)

Noting that
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{{}^{\mathsf{T}}}
and
\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{U}^{{}^{\mathsf{T}}}
, we can deduce that
{\cal G}({\mathbf{P}})
is equivalent to
{\cal G}({\mathbf{C}_{\text{out}}})
and
{\cal G}({\mathbf{F}})
is equivalent to
{\cal G}({\mathbf{B}_{\text{in}}})
. This is because they share the same eigenvectors, corresponding to
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}
, where the eigenvectors are ordered from low to high frequency in an order that is the same for
\mathbf{\Sigma}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2}
.
Therefore, given
{\cal G}({\mathbf{P}})\cong{\cal G}({\mathbf{C}_{\text{out}}})
, signal variations on
{\cal G}({\mathbf{P}})
reflect the variations between nodes
i
and
j
in
{\cal G}({\mathbf{A}})
that share a descendant node
k
such that both edges
i\rightarrow k
and
j\rightarrow k
exist on the graph
{\cal G}({\mathbf{A}})
.
Similarly, the signal variations on
{\cal G}({\mathbf{F}})
represent variations between nodes
i
and
j
in
{\cal G}({\mathbf{A}})
that share an ancestor node
k
, given that both edges
k\rightarrow i
and
k\rightarrow j
exist on the graph
{\cal G}({\mathbf{A}})
.
In light of these observations, we term the signal variations on
{\cal G}({\mathbf{P}})
and
{\cal G}({\mathbf{F}})
as
indirect variations
on
{\cal G}({\mathbf{A}})
.

On the other hand, we denote the signal variation on
{\cal G}({\mathbf{Q}})
as
in-flow variation
on
{\cal G}({\mathbf{A}})
. A fundamental characteristic of
\mathbf{Q}
is its proximity to
\mathbf{A}
: it is the unitary matrix closest to
\mathbf{A}
with respect to any unitarily invariant norm
[
17
]
.
This relationship suggests that
\mathbf{Q}
is the matrix (graph operator) nearest to
\mathbf{A}
such that the signal norm is preserved after multiplication
1
1

1

The operation
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{s}
can be interpreted as “shift” in the signal processing sense. The signal
\mathbf{s}
can be perfectly reconstructed by the “reverse shift”
\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}
.
, that is
\lVert{\mathbf{s}}\rVert_{2}=\lVert{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{s}}\rVert_{2}
.
Furthermore, in cases where the graph
{\cal G}({\mathbf{Q}})
is strongly connected, it is conjectured to be Hamiltonian
[
18
,
19
]
, implying that graph has a cycle containing each vertex exactly once.
In situations where
{\cal G}({\mathbf{Q}})
becomes disjoint, its individual sub-graphs retain the property of being supportive of unitary matrices, thereby ensuring that a Hamiltonian cycle persists within every sub-graph.
This suggests that every node belongs to a minimum of one cycle (including a potential self-loop) within
{\cal G}({\mathbf{Q}})
.
Given this understanding,
{\cal G}({\mathbf{Q}})
can be viewed as an alternative representation of
{\cal G}({\mathbf{A}})
that introduces additional edges to facilitate cycles.
For instance, as depicted in Fig.

, the graph
{\cal G}({\mathbf{Q}})
incorporates four extra edges (illustrated as dashed red arrows), which allow the introduction of Hamiltonian cycles (e.g., 0-2-4-6-1-3-5-7-0).
Consequently, the signal variation on
{\cal G}({\mathbf{Q}})
captures not only the variation between nodes
i
and
j
connected by the edge
i\rightarrow j
in
{\cal G}({\mathbf{A}})
, but also the variation between the disconnected nodes
j
and
k
in
{\cal G}({\mathbf{A}})
, provided their linkage potentially forms a cycle.
In subsequent sections, we will empirically demonstrate the advantages of analyzing graph signals via
\mathbf{GFT}_{\mathbf{Q}}
rather than
\mathbf{GFT}_{\mathbf{A}}
, given its dual properties of close similarity to
\mathbf{A}
and its Hamiltonian cycle inclusion.

4.1
Normal adjacency matrix case

If
\mathbf{A}
is normal, i.e.,
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{A}
, a direct relationship can be identified between the magnitude and phase components of its eigenvalues given by
\boldsymbol{\lambda}(\mathbf{A})=\left|{\boldsymbol{\lambda}(\mathbf{A})}\right|\odot e^{j\angle{\boldsymbol{\lambda}(\mathbf{A})}}
, and the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{P}
,
\mathbf{F}
, and
\mathbf{Q}
. Specifically, when
\mathbf{A}
is normal,
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{F}
. The magnitude component of each eigenvalue,
\left|{\boldsymbol{\lambda}(\mathbf{A})}\right|
, corresponds to an eigenvalue in
\boldsymbol{\lambda}(\mathbf{P})
, implying
\left|{\boldsymbol{\lambda}(\mathbf{A})}\right|=\pi_{1}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}(\mathbf{P}))
for some permutation
\pi_{1}
. Similarly, the phase of each eigenvalue,
e^{j\angle{\boldsymbol{\lambda}(\mathbf{A})}}
, aligns with an eigenvalue in
\boldsymbol{\lambda}(\mathbf{Q})
, so
e^{j\angle{\boldsymbol{\lambda}(\mathbf{A})}}=\pi_{2}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}(\mathbf{Q}))
for another permutation
\pi_{2}
.

By splitting
\boldsymbol{\lambda}(\mathbf{A})
into its magnitude and phase, we gain insights into the indirect variation and in-flow variation on
{\cal G}({\mathbf{A}})
. However, focusing solely on the eigenvalues in
\boldsymbol{\lambda}(\mathbf{A})
from a total variation perspective may lead to misinterpretations: Eigenvectors associated with eigenvalues that have minimal differences in total variation might be mistakenly perceived as exhibiting similar variations.
For instance, normalized eigenvectors associated with
\lambda_{1}/{\text{max}}\left({|\boldsymbol{\lambda}|}\right)=e^{j\frac{\pi}{2}}
and
\lambda_{2}/{\text{max}}\left({|\boldsymbol{\lambda}|}\right)=(\sqrt{2}-1)e^{j\pi}
yield the same total variation
\sqrt{2}
(as seen in Remark
2.4
), even though they reflect entirely different indirect and in-flow variations.

Filter design

For the normal case, there is a direct correspondence of the cascaded polynomial filtering
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{Q})p_{r}(\mathbf{P})
in terms of polynomial filters on the magnitude and phase of eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
be a real-valued normal matrix with
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{A}
and eigendecomposition
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{X}^{\mathsf{H}}
, where
\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{U}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and
\mathbf{\Lambda}\in\mathcal{D}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. When
\sqrt{{\mathbf{\Lambda}}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\mathsf{H}}}=\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{D}_{n}(\mathbb{R})\cap\mathcal{S}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathbf{\Lambda}=\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{\Theta}
,
for any two polynomial pairs
p_{\theta}
and
p_{r}
,

p_{\theta}(\mathbf{Q})p_{r}(\mathbf{P})=\mathbf{X}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{\Theta})p_{r}(\mathbf{\Sigma})\mathbf{X}^{\mathsf{H}},

(3)

where
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{\Theta})p_{r}(\mathbf{\Sigma})
are “separate” filters on
\mathbf{A}
, with
p_{\theta}
targeting the phase and
p_{r}
addressing the magnitude of the eigenvalues.

Proof.

The eigendecomposition of
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{X}^{\mathsf{H}}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{\Theta}\mathbf{X}^{\mathsf{H}}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{\Theta}^{\mathsf{H}})^{\mathsf{H}}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{H}}
where
\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{\Theta}^{\mathsf{H}}
. Since the multiplication of two unitary matrices produces another unitary matrix,
\mathbf{V}
is a unitary matrix. Therefore,
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{H}}
is a singular value decomposition (SVD) of the adjacency matrix.

By the relationship between SVD and the polar decomposition,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{H}}=\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{\Theta}^{\mathsf{H}})^{\mathsf{H}}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{\Theta}\mathbf{X}^{\mathsf{H}},

(4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{H}}=(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{\Theta}^{\mathsf{H}})\mathbf{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{\Theta}^{\mathsf{H}})^{\mathsf{H}}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{X}^{\mathsf{H}}.

(5)

Therefore,

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(\mathbf{Q})p_{r}(\mathbf{P})

\displaystyle=p_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{\Theta}\mathbf{X}^{\mathsf{H}})p_{r}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{X}^{\mathsf{H}})

(6)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{X}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{\Theta})\mathbf{X}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{X}p_{r}(\mathbf{\Sigma})\mathbf{X}^{\mathsf{H}}

(7)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{X}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{\Theta})p_{r}(\mathbf{\Sigma})\mathbf{X}^{\mathsf{H}}.\qed

(8)

A crucial implication of the presented theorem is the feasibility of implementing separate filters targeting the magnitude and phase of the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
. This is particularly advantageous for large graphs as it obviates the need for the computationally intensive eigendecomposition of
\mathbf{A}
. Instead, we leverage cascaded filtering with
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{P}
. In subsequent sections, our experimental results demonstrate that this cascaded filtering approach can also be applied in cases where the adjacency matrix deviates from normality.

Case study: M-block cyclic graph

This section aims to highlight that even in the context of non-normal matrices, where the direct correspondence of (
3
) does not hold, there still exists a significant advantage in analyzing graph signals using
{\cal G}({\mathbf{P}})
,
{\cal G}({\mathbf{F}})
, and
{\cal G}({\mathbf{Q}})
individually.

We select an
M
-block cyclic graph for our case study
[
16
]
.
An
M
-block cyclic graph comprises
M
distinct blocks of nodes, where edges interlink nodes in adjacent blocks, but there are no connections within the same block. All edge orientations follow a uniform cyclic pattern, either clockwise or counter-clockwise. Fig.

-block cyclic graph divided into

M
-block cyclic graphs, where each block contains an equal number of nodes,
{\cal G}({\mathbf{Q}})
retains the
M
-block cyclic layout of the original
{\cal G}({\mathbf{A}})
but with altered edge weights.

To investigate signal variations across different variations on
{\cal G}({\mathbf{A}})
,
{\cal G}({\mathbf{P}})
, and
{\cal G}({\mathbf{Q}})
(omitting
{\cal G}({\mathbf{F}})
for conciseness), we design graph signals with distinct smoothness levels as follows.
We begin by generating an independent and identically distributed (iid) graph signal
\mathbf{s}
, with its entries drawn from the standard normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Subsequently, we employ cascaded filters on this random signal and study the resultant filtered signal,
\mathbf{y}=p_{\theta}(\mathbf{Q})p_{r}(\mathbf{P})\mathbf{s}
, through diverse variations. For each filter, we selected varying types (lowpass and highpass), and some resultant filtered outputs are illustrated in

This analysis brings forth several insights:
1) The lowpass filter
p_{r}
smoothens signals within a block, reflecting the indirect variation.
2) Filters denoted by
p_{\theta}
influence the smoothness from one block to another in the signal.
3) Applying separate filters on the magnitude and phase of the eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}
produces qualitatively similar signals to those processed by the cascaded filters using identical
p_{r}
and
p_{\theta}
although the equality (
3
) does not hold for non-normal
\mathbf{A}

\mathbf{A}

{\cal G}({\mathbf{P}})
and
{\cal G}({\mathbf{Q}})

{\cal G}({\mathbf{P}})

M
-block cyclic graphs.
Conversely, due to the inherent Hamiltonian cycles in all sub-graphs of
{\cal G}({\mathbf{Q}})

6
Conclusions

In this study, we delve into the analysis of signals on directed graphs leveraging polar decomposition. With a clear node-domain interpretation, we elucidate that the positive-semidefinite matrix offers insights into the indirect variations present within the original graph, whereas the unitary matrix underscores in-flow variations. Furthermore, we propose cascaded graph filters based on the polar decomposition and illustrate their equivalency to separable filtering on the magnitude and phase of the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
. Through a focused case study on an M-block cyclic graph, we demonstrate the efficacy of cascaded filters in smoothing graph signals in terms of indirect and in-flow variations distinctly. Our findings highlight that analyzing these filtered signals in light of the aforementioned variations furnishes a richer perspective than solely probing signal variations from the original graph.
