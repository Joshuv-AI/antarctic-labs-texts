Numerical Approximation of Andrews Plots with Optimal Spatial-Spectral Smoothing
Mitchell Rimerman, Nate Strawn

Abstract
Andrews plots provide aesthetically pleasant visualizations of high-dimensional datasets. This work proves that Andrews plots (when defined in terms of the principal component scores of a dataset) are optimally “smooth” on average, and solve an infinite-dimensional quadratic minimization program over the set of linear isometries from the Euclidean data space to L 2 ​ ( [ 0 , 1 ] ) L^{2}([0,1]) . By building technical machinery that characterizes the solutions to general infinite-dimensional quadratic minimization programs over linear isometries, we further show that the solution set is (in the generic case) a manifold. To avoid the ambiguities presented by this manifold of solutions, we add “spectral smoothing” terms to the infinite-dimensional optimization program to induce Andrews plots with optimal spatial-spectral smoothing. We characterize the (generic) set of solutions to this program and prove that the resulting plots admit efficient numerical approximations. These spatial-spectral smooth Andrews plots tend to avoid some “visual clutter” that arises due to the oscillation of trigonometric polynomials.

Introduction

Data visualization techniques form an essential toolkit for scientists seeking to glean insights from data, and scatterplots constitute an incredibly straightforward tool for building intuition from data. However, scatter plots “compress" data in a lossy manner, which means such intuitions need careful vetting. Andrews plots
[
2
]
offer a way to double-check insights gleaned from conventional 2D or 3D scatterplots because they completely preserve the information within a dataset. Figure
1
displays an example. While 2D and 3D scatterplots yield visualizations where data points are represented by non-overlapping objects (points or dots), embedding high-dimensional data in 2D or 3D necessarily collapses distances between points in general. Andrews plots trade off “visual clutter” for fidelity of distances.

In

\displaystyle f_{x}(t)=

\displaystyle(u_{1}^{T}x)+(u_{2}^{T}x)\sqrt{2}\cos(2\pi t)+(u_{3}^{T}x)\sqrt{2}\sin(2\pi t)

\displaystyle+(u_{4}^{T}x)\sqrt{2}\cos(4\pi t)+(u_{5}^{T}x)\sqrt{2}\sin(4\pi t)+\cdots

The work

The theory developed there proves that an orthonormal basis of principal component vectors arises when we attempt to minimize the “mean quadratic variation" over all the plots. That is, a principal component analysis basis produces “optimally smooth” 2D Andrews plots, and this optimality holds over the entire set of isometries from
\mathbb{R}^{d}

L^{2}([0,1])

The work

In
[

1.1
Our Contributions

In this paper, we provide a characterization of the minimizers of the mean (across a dataset) quadratic variation of isometries from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathcal{H}
, where
\mathcal{H}
is the Sobolev space

\mathcal{H}=\{f\in L^{2}([0,1]):f^{\prime}\in L^{2}([0,1])\}.

This characterization includes the plots involving PCA coefficients originally suggested by Andrews
[
2
]

1

\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})

1

This characterization reveals numerous degrees of freedom due to the translation-invariant subspaces

\text{span}\{\cos(2\pi kt),\sin(2\pi kt)\}.

In particular, any map of the form

\displaystyle x\to(u_{1}^{T}x)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{2}(u_{2}^{T}x)(a_{1}\cos(2\pi t)+b_{1}\sin(2\pi t))

\displaystyle+\sqrt{2}(u_{2}^{T}x)(b_{1}\cos(2\pi t)-b_{1}\sin(2\pi t))

\displaystyle+\sqrt{2}(u_{3}^{T}x)(a_{2}\cos(4\pi t)+b_{2}\sin(4\pi t))

\displaystyle+\sqrt{2}(u_{4}^{T}x)(b_{2}\cos(4\pi t)-a_{2}\sin(4\pi t))

\displaystyle+\cdots

(where
a_{i}^{2}+b_{i}^{2}=1

) constitutes a linear isometry which minimizes the mean quadratic variation of the dataset
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
over the set of linear isometries from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathcal{H}
.

These degrees of freedom motivate the second component of our contributions. Heuristically, the oscillation of trigonometric polynomials contributes to multiple crossings of Andrews plots. Smoothing Fourier coefficients dampens these oscillations. Therefore, for
\alpha>0

\frac{\alpha}{4\pi^{2}}\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}^{2}+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|\hat{f}[k+1]-\hat{f}[k]|^{2}

where the

\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathcal{H}
fits in the framework of Theorem
1

2

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=2}^{d}4\pi^{2}\lfloor k/2\rfloor^{2}\sigma_{k}(X)^{2}

The Plancheral theorem gives us

\displaystyle\|Df\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}^{2}=\|\mathcal{F}^{-1}Df\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|2\pi ik\hat{f}[k]|^{2}=\sum(4\pi^{2}k^{2}\hat{f}[k])\overline{\hat{f}[k]}=\langle A\hat{f},\hat{f}\rangle

3
Optimal Spatial-Spectral Andrews plots

We introduce the spatial-spectral quadratic variation and prove Theorem
2
in this section. For a given parameter
\alpha>0
and a dataset
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define the
minimum mean spatial-spectral quadratic variation program
over
\Phi\in\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathcal{H})
by

\displaystyle\min_{\Phi\in\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathcal{H})}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\alpha}{4\pi^{2}}\|D\Phi(x_{i})\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}^{2}+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\widehat{\Phi(x_{i})}[k+1]-\widehat{\Phi(x_{i})}[k]\right|^{2}\right)

(4)

These second terms under the sum measure “discrete” quadratic variation in the Fourier domain. These terms can be represented in the spatial domain:

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\hat{f}[k+1]-\hat{f}[k]\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(-\hat{f}[k-1]+2\hat{f}[k]-\hat{f}[k+1])\overline{\hat{f}[k]}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{1}(1-\cos(2\pi t))|f(t)|^{2}\>dt.

This form indicates that this term promotes functions that concentrate near
t=0
. We first show that this program fits in the framework provided by Theorem
1
by considering the representation of the spatial-spectral quadratic variation in the Fourier coefficient domain. Lemma
2
establishes this representation, and ensures that the resulting operators are self-adjoint and discrete.

To state and prove Lemma
2
, we need to establish some more notation. Letting
\{e_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
again denote the standard orthonormal basis on
\ell^{2}
, we let

\mathcal{E}=\{e_{0}\}\cup\{(e_{k}+e_{-k})/\sqrt{2}\}_{k\geq 1}\text{ and }\mathcal{O}=\{(e_{k}-e_{-k})/\sqrt{2}\iota\}_{k\geq 1}

and observe that
v\in\ell^{2}
satisfying
v[-k]=\overline{v[k]}
maps isometrically to the real vector space
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})\oplus\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})\cong\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
(where
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0\}\cup\mathbb{N}
) via
v\to(v_{\mathcal{E}},v_{\mathcal{O}})
where

v_{\mathcal{E}}(\langle v,\>e_{0}\rangle,\langle v,\>(e_{1}+e_{-1})/\sqrt{2}\rangle,\ldots

and

v_{\mathcal{O}})=\langle v,\>(e_{1}-e_{-1})/\sqrt{2}\iota\rangle,\>\langle v,\>(e_{2}-e_{-2})/\sqrt{2}\iota\rangle,\ldots.

We call this isometry the
even-odd isometry
.

Lemma 2

For any
\alpha>0
and
f\in\mathcal{H}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{4\pi^{2}}\|Df\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}^{2}+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\widehat{f}[k+1]-\widehat{f}[k]\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle B_{\alpha}\hat{f},\>\hat{f}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}\hat{f}_{\mathcal{E}},\>\hat{f}_{\mathcal{E}}\rangle_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})}+\langle B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}\hat{f}_{\mathcal{O}},\>\hat{f}_{\mathcal{O}}\rangle_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})}

where
B_{\alpha}
is an infinite tridiagonal array with diagonal entries
(B_{\alpha})_{k,k}=\alpha k^{2}+2
and
(B_{\alpha})_{k+1,k}=(B_{\alpha})_{k-1,k}=-1
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, and where

B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}=\begin{pmatrix}2&-\sqrt{2}&0&0&\ldots\\
-\sqrt{2}&\alpha+2&-1&0&\ldots\\
0&-1&4\alpha+2&-1&\ldots\\
0&0&-1&9\alpha+2&\ldots\\

are Jacobi operators. Moreover, the operators

are self-adjoint and positive semi-definite, and

Proof:

The forms of

,

, and

]
about Theorem 4.1 in

]
: for a Jacobi operator

\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}&a_{1}&0&0&\cdots\\
a_{1}&b_{2}&a_{2}&0&\cdots\\

\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}

defined using the real sequences

\square

Lemma 3

The spectrum of
B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}
and the spectrum of
B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}
are both purely discrete with multiplicity
1
for all eigenvalues, and
\sigma(B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}})
and the spectrum of
\sigma(B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}})
are disjoint from each other. Moreover, for any fixed
k\geq 1
,
\lambda_{k}(B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(N))\downarrow\lambda_{k}(B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}})
and
\lambda_{k}(B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}(N))\downarrow\lambda_{k}(B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}})
.

Proof:

We first show that
B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}
has purely discrete spectrum and that every eigenvalue of
B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}
has multiplicity 1. Along the way, we will show that
\lambda_{k}(B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}(N))
(defined for all
N\geq k
) is a monotone decreasing sequence converging to
\lambda_{k}(B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}})
. The same reasoning applies for
B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}
, so we omit this part of the argument. However, at the end of the proof we demonstrate that
\sigma(B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}})
and
\sigma(B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}})
are necessarily disjoint.

To simplify notation, set
Q_{N}=B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}(N)
. Observe that
Q_{N+1}
is a bordered matrix with leading principal
N
by
N
submatrix
Q_{N}
:

Q_{N+1}=\begin{pmatrix}Q_{N}&-e_{N}\\
-e_{N}^{T}&\alpha(N+1)^{2}+2\end{pmatrix}

where
e_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the
N
th standard orthonormal basis member of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
(that is,
0
for all entries except the
N
th entry which is
1
). Because of this relationship, Cauchy interlacing yields

\lambda_{1}(Q_{N+1})\leq\lambda_{1}(Q_{N})\leq\lambda_{2}(Q_{N+1})\leq\lambda_{2}(Q_{N})\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{N}(Q_{N})\leq\lambda_{N+1}(Q_{N+1}).

It follows that, for
N\geq k
,
\lambda_{k}(Q_{N})
is a monotone decreasing sequence. On the other hand, if we define

\tilde{Q}_{N}=Q_{N}-e_{N}e_{N}^{T},

we have that

\tilde{Q}_{N+1}=\begin{pmatrix}Q_{N}&-e_{N}\\
-e_{N}^{T}&\alpha(N+1)^{2}+1\end{pmatrix}

and hence

\lambda_{1}(\tilde{Q}_{N})\leq\lambda_{1}(Q_{N})\leq\lambda_{2}(\tilde{Q}_{N})\leq\lambda_{2}(Q_{N})\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{N}(\tilde{Q}_{N})\leq\lambda_{N}(Q_{N})

follows from Cauchy interlacing for rank-one perturbations. But also observe that if
v
and
\tilde{v}
are unit eigenvectors of
Q_{N}
and
\tilde{Q}_{N}
with eigenvalues
\lambda=\lambda_{k}(Q_{N})
and
\tilde{\lambda}=\lambda_{k}(\tilde{Q}_{N})
, then

\lambda v^{T}\tilde{v}=\tilde{\lambda}v^{T}\tilde{v}-(e_{N}^{T}v)(e_{N}^{T}\tilde{v})

so

(\lambda-\tilde{\lambda})v^{T}v=-(e_{N}^{T}v)(e_{N}^{T}\tilde{v}).

By way of contradiction, suppose
\lambda=\tilde{\lambda}
. Then either
e_{N}^{T}v=0
or
e_{N}^{T}\tilde{v}=0
, but then backsolving one of the equations

(Q_{N}-\lambda I)v=0\text{ or }(\tilde{Q}_{N}-\tilde{\lambda}I)\tilde{v}

will yield
v=0
or
\tilde{v}=0
, contradicting the unit-norm condition. We conclude that
\lambda_{k}(\tilde{Q}_{N})<\lambda_{k}(Q_{N})<\lambda_{k+1}(\tilde{Q}_{N})
for
N\geq k+1
.

On the other hand, we have that

\tilde{Q}_{N+1}=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{Q}_{N}&0\\
0&\alpha(N+1)^{2}\end{pmatrix}+(e_{N}-e_{N+1})(e_{N}-e_{N+1})^{T},

where in this context
e_{N},e_{N+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
are the usual orthonormal basis members. This yields the interlacing conditions

\lambda_{1}(\tilde{Q}_{N})\leq\lambda_{1}(\tilde{Q}_{N+1})\leq\lambda_{2}(\tilde{Q}_{N})\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{N}(\tilde{Q}_{N+1})\leq\alpha(N+1)^{2}\leq\lambda_{N+1}(\tilde{Q}_{N+1})

and hence
\lambda_{k}(\tilde{Q}_{N})
is a monotone increasing sequence for
k\geq N
.

Collecting our observations, for arbitrary
k\geq 1
,

\lambda_{k}(Q_{N})<\lambda_{k+1}(\tilde{Q}_{N})<\lambda_{k+1}(Q_{N})

for all
N\geq k+1
. Monotonicity allows us to set
\lambda_{k}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\lambda_{k}(Q_{N})
,
\tilde{\lambda}_{k+1}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\lambda_{k+1}(\tilde{Q}_{N})
and
\lambda_{k+1}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\lambda_{k+1}(Q_{N})
. Since
\lambda_{k}(Q_{N})
decreases to
\lambda_{k}
, we get

\lambda_{k}<\lambda_{k+1}(\tilde{Q}_{N}).

Since
\lambda_{k+1}(\tilde{Q}_{N})
increases to
\tilde{\lambda}_{k+1}
, we have

\lambda_{k}<\tilde{\lambda}_{k+1}.

Finally,
\lambda_{k+1}(\tilde{Q}_{N})<\lambda_{k+1}(Q_{N})
for all
N\geq k+1
implies
\tilde{\lambda}_{k+1}\leq\lambda_{k+1}
, and the transitive property yields
\lambda_{k}<\lambda_{k+1}
for all
k\geq 1
. On the other hand, the interlacing condition
\alpha k^{2}\leq\lambda_{k}(\tilde{Q}_{k})
(seen above as
\alpha(N+1)^{2}\leq\lambda_{N+1}(\tilde{Q}_{N+1})
) implies
\alpha k^{2}\leq\tilde{\lambda}_{k}\leq\lambda_{k}
for
k\geq 2
.

Now, set

\Lambda=\left\{\lambda:\lambda=\lim_{N\to\infty}\lambda^{(N)}\text{ where }\lambda^{(N)}\in\sigma(Q_{N})\right\}.

That is,
\Lambda
is the set of limits of eigenvalues of
Q_{N}
as
N\to\infty
. We will show that
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots\}
where
\lambda_{k}
are defined above. Let
\lambda^{(N)}=\lambda_{k_{N}}(Q_{N})
and suppose
\lambda=\lim_{N\to\infty}\lambda^{(N)}
. First, it must be the case that
k_{N}
is bounded. By way of contradiction, suppose there is a subsequence
k_{N_{j}}
such that
k_{N_{j}}\to\infty
as
j\to\infty
. Then

\alpha(k_{N_{j}})^{2}\leq\lambda_{k_{N_{j}}}(Q_{N_{j}})

yields that the subsequence
\lambda_{k_{N_{j}}}(Q_{N_{j}})
diverges as
j\to\infty
, a contradiction to the fact that
\lambda_{k_{N}}(Q_{N})
converges to
\lambda
.

Next, we claim that
k_{N}
is eventually constant. By the pigeonhole principle, since
k_{N}
is bounded, there is a
k_{1}
such that
k_{N}=k_{1}
for infinitely many
N
. By way of contradiction, if there is a
k_{2}\not=k_{1}
such that
K_{N}=k_{2}
for infinitely many
N
, then there are subsequences
k_{N_{1,j}}
and
k_{N_{2,j}}
which are eventually
k_{1}\not=k_{2}
(respectively), then

\lambda_{k_{N_{1,j}}}(Q_{N_{1,j}})\to\lambda_{k_{1}}

and

\lambda_{k_{N_{2,j}}}(Q_{N_{2,j}})\to\lambda_{k_{2}}.

But by the above,
k_{1}<k_{2}
then
\lambda_{k_{1}}<\lambda_{k_{2}}
, and if
k_{2}<k_{1}

\lambda_{k_{1}}<\lambda_{k_{2}}
. Either way, we contradict he fact that subsequences of a convergent sequence are convergent to the same limit. We therefore conclude
k_{1}
is the only
k
between
1
and
\sup k_{N}<\infty
for which
k_{N}=k_{1}
infinitely many times. Thus, there is a finite index
M
such that
k_{N}=k_{1}
for all
N\geq M
. Consequently,
\lambda_{k_{1}}=\lambda
.

Theorem 2.4 of
[
7
]
indicates that
\Lambda=\sigma(B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}})
since the diagonal entries of
B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}
diverge and
B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}
also satisfies condition (2.2) of that paper. We conclude that
B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}
has a discrete spectrum. To prove that
\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{k}(B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}})
, suppose
B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}v=\lambda_{k}v
. If
v[1]=0
, then we obtain a contradiction because we inductively conclude
v[k]=0
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. If
v[1]\not=0
, replace
v
with
v[1]^{-1}v
so that
v[1]=1
. Then solving the system inductively necessarily yields unique values. Therefore the dimension of the kernel of
B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}
is
1
.

Finally, we observe that

B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}=\begin{pmatrix}2&-\sqrt{2}e_{2}^{T}\\
-\sqrt{2}e_{2}^{T}&B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}\end{pmatrix}

So given two unit eigenvectors, we have

\lambda v^{T}u=\mu v^{T}u-\sqrt{2}(e_{1}^{T}v)(e_{2}^{T}u).

Then
\lambda=\mu
would imply either
e_{1}^{T}v=0
or
e_{2}^{T}u=0
which would lead to either
v=0
or
u=0
by induction. This contradicts the the fact that
v
and
u
were chosen to be unit vectors.

\square

The next lemma ensures that we can approximate the eigenvectors of
B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}
and
B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}
using the eigenvectors of
B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(N)
and
B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}(N)
, and therefore we obtain an optimal isometry.

Lemma 4

For any fixed
k\geq 1
, given any sequence of unit vectors
\{v_{N}\}_{N=1}^{\infty}
such that
v_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is an eigenvector of
B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}(N)
, there is a choice of signs
\xi_{N}\in\{-1,1\}
such that the padded unit vectors

\tilde{v}_{N}=\xi_{N}\begin{pmatrix}v_{N}\\
0\\
0\\
\vdots\end{pmatrix}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})

converge to a unit vector
v\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
which is an eigenvector for
B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}
with eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}(B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}})
. A similar result holds for
B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}
.

Proof:

Set
Q_{N}=B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}(N)
to simplify notation, and let
\{v_{j}(N)\}_{j=1}^{N}
denote an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of
Q_{N}
associated with the non-decreasing eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{j}(Q_{N})\}_{j=1}^{N}
in a respective fashion. By Lemma
3
, for any
k\geq 1
,
\lambda_{k}(Q_{N})\downarrow\lambda_{k}(B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}})=\lambda_{k}
and
\lambda_{k+1}(Q_{N})\downarrow\lambda_{k+1}(B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}})=\lambda_{k+1}
, and
\lambda_{k}\not=\lambda_{k+1}
. Therefore, there is a
\delta>0
and an
M\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\delta<\lambda_{k+1}-\lambda_{k}(Q_{N})<\lambda_{k+1}(Q_{N})-\lambda_{k}(Q_{N})

for all
N\geq M
. By taking a minimum over
\delta
’s and a maximum over
M
’s, we can obtain a
\delta>0
and an
M\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\delta<\lambda_{k+1}-\lambda_{k}(Q_{N})<\lambda_{k+1}(Q_{N})-\lambda_{k}(Q_{N})

and

\delta<|\lambda_{k}(Q_{N})-\lambda_{j}|<|\lambda_{k}(Q_{N})-\lambda_{j}(Q_{N})|

for all
j<k
.

We note that

(\alpha N^{2}+2-\lambda_{k}(Q_{N}))(e_{N}^{T}v_{k}(N))-(e_{N-1}^{T}v_{k}(N))=0

so

e_{N}^{T}v_{k}(N)=\frac{e_{N-1}^{T}v_{k}(N)}{\alpha N^{2}+2-\lambda_{k}(Q_{N})}

and

-(e_{N-2}^{T}v_{k}(N))+(\alpha(N-1)^{2}+2-\lambda_{k}(Q_{N}))(e_{N-1}^{T}v_{k}(N))-(e_{N}^{T}v_{k}(N))=0.

Then

e_{N-1}^{T}v_{k}(N)=\frac{(e_{N-2}^{T}v_{k}(N))+(e_{N}^{T}v_{k}(N))}{\alpha(N-1)^{2}+2-\lambda_{k}(Q_{N})}

and combining these we have

|e_{N}^{T}v_{k}(N)|\leq\frac{2}{(\alpha N^{2}+2-\lambda_{k}(Q_{N}))(\alpha(N-1)^{2}+2-\lambda_{k}(Q_{N}))}

Let
M
be such than
N\geq M
implies
\lambda_{k}(Q_{N})-\lambda_{k}<2
and
\alpha(N-1)^{2}>\lambda_{k}
. We have the bound

|e_{N}^{T}v_{k}(N)|\leq\frac{2}{(\alpha N^{2}-\lambda_{k})(\alpha(N-1)^{2}-\lambda_{k})}\leq\frac{2}{(\alpha(N-1)^{2}-\lambda_{k})^{2}}

Now,

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(Q_{N+1})v_{j}(N+1)^{T}\tilde{v}_{k}

\displaystyle=v_{j}(N+1)Q_{N+1}\tilde{v}_{k}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{k}(Q_{N})v_{j}(N+1)^{T}\tilde{v}_{k}-(e_{N}^{T}\tilde{v}_{k})(e_{N+1}^{T}v_{j}(N+1)),

so we have

v_{j}(N+1)^{T}\tilde{v}_{k}=-\frac{(e_{N}^{T}\tilde{v}_{k})(e_{N+1}^{T}v_{j}(N+1))}{\lambda_{j}(Q_{N+1})-\lambda_{k}(Q_{N})}

and therefore

|v_{j}(N+1)^{T}\tilde{v}_{k}|^{2}\leq\frac{(e_{N}^{T}\tilde{v}_{k})^{2}}{\delta^{2}}.

Hence,

|v_{k}(N+1)^{T}\tilde{v}_{k}(N)|^{2}=1-\sum_{j\not=k}|v_{j}(N+1)^{T}\tilde{v}_{k}|^{2}\geq 1-|e_{N}^{T}\tilde{v}_{k}|^{2}\sum_{j\not=k}\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}=1-\frac{N}{\delta^{2}}|e_{N}^{T}\tilde{v}_{k}|^{2}.

Therefore, letting
\xi_{N}=\text{sign}(v_{k}(N+1)^{T}v_{k}(N))
,

\displaystyle\|\xi_{N}v_{k}(N+1)-\tilde{v}_{k}(N)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=2-2|v_{k}(N+1)^{T}v_{k}(N)|

\displaystyle<2\left(1-\sqrt{1-\frac{N}{\delta^{2}}|e_{N}^{T}\tilde{v}_{k}|^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2N|e_{N}^{T}\tilde{v}_{k}|^{2}}{\delta^{2}\left(1+\sqrt{1-\frac{N}{\delta^{2}}|e_{N}^{T}\tilde{v}_{k}|^{2}}\right)}

and hence

\|\xi_{N}v_{k}(N+1)-\tilde{v}_{k}(N)\|\leq\frac{\sqrt{2N}|e_{N}^{T}\tilde{v}_{k}(N)|}{\delta}.\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\delta}\frac{\sqrt{N}}{(\alpha(N-1)^{2}-\lambda_{k})^{2}}.

Observe that the terms on the left are
\mathcal{O}(N^{-7/2})
, so by the comparison test and convergence of
p
-series, we have that

\sum_{N=M}^{\infty}\|\xi_{N+1}\tilde{v}_{k}(N+1)-\xi_{N}\tilde{v}_{k}(N)\|<\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\delta}\sum_{N=M}^{\infty}\frac{\sqrt{N}}{(\alpha(N-1)^{2}-\lambda_{k})^{2}}<\infty

Therefore

\xi_{K+1}\tilde{v}_{k}(K+1)-\xi_{M}\tilde{v}_{k}(M)=\sum_{N=M}^{K}\xi_{N+1}\tilde{v}_{k}(N+1)-\xi_{N}\tilde{v}_{k}(N)\to u

as
K\to\infty
, so we have that
\xi_{K+1}\tilde{v}_{k}(K+1)\to v
in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
as
K\to\infty
. By continuity of the norm, we have
\|v\|=1
.

Now we show that
v
is an eigenvector of
B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}
with eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
. We have that

B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}\tilde{v}_{k}(N)=\lambda_{k}(N)\tilde{v}_{k}(N)-(e_{N}^{T}v_{k}(N))e_{N+1}.

so

\displaystyle\|B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}\tilde{v}_{k}(N)-\lambda_{k}v\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\lambda_{k}(N)\tilde{v}_{k}(N)-\lambda_{k}v\|+|e_{N}^{T}v_{k}(N)|

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{k}(N)\|v_{k}(N)-v\|+|\lambda_{k}(N)-\lambda_{k}|\|v\|+|e_{N}^{T}v_{k}(N)|

Since each term on the right hand side converge to
0
as
N\to\infty
, we conclude that
\lim_{N\to\infty}B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}\tilde{v}_{k}(N)=\lambda_{k}v
.
By Lemma
2
,
B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}
is self-adjoint and hence closed. We have verified that
\tilde{v}_{k}(N)\to v
and
B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}\tilde{v}_{k}(N)\to\lambda_{k}v
, so
B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}v=\lambda_{k}v
because
B_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{O}}
is a closed operator.

\square
