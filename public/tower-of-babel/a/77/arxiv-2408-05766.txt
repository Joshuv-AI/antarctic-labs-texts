The motive of a variety with cellular resolution of singularities
Bruno Stonek

Abstract
. A complex variety X X admits a cellular resolution of singularities if there exists a resolution of singularities X ~ → X \widetilde{X}\to X such that its exceptional locus as well as X ~ \widetilde{X} and the singular locus of X X admit a cellular decomposition. We give a concrete description of the motive with compact support of X X in terms of its Borel–Moore homology, under some mild conditions. We give many examples, including rational projective curves and toric varieties of dimension two and three.



1.
Introduction

The theory of motives dates back to Grothendieck in the 1960s. It has seen tremendous advances, notably since the work of Voevodsky, who introduced a category
DM
(standing for “derived category of motives”) satisfying many properties common to all homology theories, such as homotopy invariance, Mayer–Vietoris, Gysin sequences, etc. We work with Voevodsky’s category of motives over the field of complex numbers: this restriction simplifies some technical aspects of the theory.

Any complex variety
X
, smooth or not, determines a motive
M(X)
: it is an object in
DM
. This extends to a functor
M
, and the philosophy is that every cohomology theory should factor through it: this immediately suggests that
M(X)
contains much interesting and subtle information about
X
. It is thus natural to ask for a complete description of it.

We will also consider the compactly-supported variant
M^{c}(X)
: it coincides with
M(X)
when
X
is complete. The object
M^{c}(X)
determines, for example, the Chow groups of
X
, via the formula
\mathrm{CH}_{i}(X)\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{DM}(\mathbb{Z}\{i\},M^{c}(X))
(
[
Voe00
, Section 2.2]
). The Chow groups, in turn, are linked to the easier Borel–Moore homology groups via the cycle map
\mathrm{CH}_{*}(X)\to H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{2*}(X)
. We can turn this around and ask: when is it enough to know the Borel–Moore homology to describe the motive of the variety?

Consider
cellular varieties
, by which we mean varieties which admit a filtration by closed subvarieties such that the relative differences are disjoint unions of affine spaces. Their cycle map is an isomorphism. The odd Borel–Moore homology groups are zero, and the even ones are free, with rank given by the number of cells in each dimension. It was proven by Kahn
[
Kah99
]
that the motive with compact support of a cellular variety is completely determined by its Chow groups, so it is also completely determined by its Borel–Moore homology.

We generalize this by proving that the Borel–Moore homology of
X
completely determines
M^{c}(X)
in the case when
X
admits a cellular resolution of singularities, see
Theorem

2.7
.(
2a
). By this we mean that there exists a resolution of singularities
\widetilde{X}\to X
such that its exceptional locus as well as
\widetilde{X}
and the singular locus of
X
are all cellular. This description of
M^{c}(X)
may not be all that explicit, but adding an extra hypothesis on the Borel–Moore homology of
X
the result becomes very explicit:

Theorem
2.7
.

Let
X
be a variety admitting a cellular resolution of singularities. Suppose that, for all
i=0,\dots,\dim(X)
we have that
H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{2i}(X)
is free or
H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{2i+1}(X)
is zero. Then there is an isomorphism in
DM

M^{c}(X)\cong\bigoplus_{i}H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{2i}(X)\{i\}\oplus H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{2i+1}(X)\{i\}[1].

We give many concrete examples, especially of motives of singular varieties. This was our initial motivation, since the literature contains few explicit computations of such motives.

We first deal with projective rational curves: we prove that their motive is determined by the number of singularities it has as well as their number of branches (see
Proposition

3.1
).

We then turn to toric surfaces determined by fans in the plane. If the fan is complete, so that the surface is projective, the motive is determined by the number of rays as well as the index of the sublattice spanned by the minimal generators of the rays, the latter determining the torsion (
Proposition

3.18
). If the fan is generated by a single cone, so that the variety is affine, then the motive with compact support is determined by the determinant of the two minimal generators of the cone (
Proposition

3.23
). We can also describe the motive with compact support of quasiprojective toric surfaces, under some additional conditions on the fan (see
Example

3.24
).

Finally, we turn to motives of toric varieties of higher dimension. Here, we may have toric varieties which do not admit a cellular resolution of singularities, because the singular locus is not cellular (cf.
Example

3.31
), but we can describe the motive (or motive with compact support) of many varieties of interest. We observe that the motive of the weighted
n
-dimensional projective space
\mathbb{P}(1,\dots,1,k)
,
k\geq 2
, is the same as the motive of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
.

The main result

Definition 2.1

[

A variety

Remark 2.2

The Borel–Moore homology of cellular varieties is zero in odd degrees and free abelian in even degrees

Example 2.3
.

(1)

A zero-dimensional variety is cellular.

(2)

A smooth projective toric variety is cellular thanks to the Białynicki-Birula decomposition

]

[

, since we can choose a

(3)

3.1.
Rational curves

By a

we mean a projective variety of dimension 1.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
C
be a rational curve. Then there is an isomorphism in

M(C)\cong\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{|E|-|Z|}[1]\oplus\mathbb{Z}\{1\},

where
Z
is the singular locus of

and

is the exceptional locus of the resolution of singularities given by normalization.

Proof.

Since
C
is a curve, the normalization map

is simultaneously a resolution of singularities. In particular, it is birational. Since
C
is rational, there is a birational map from
\mathbb{P}^{1}
to
C
.
Therefore,
\widetilde{C}
and
\mathbb{P}^{1}
are birationally equivalent. But smooth projective birationally equivalent curves are isomorphic, so
\widetilde{C}\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}
. In conclusion, the resolution of singularities square looks like this:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{P}^{1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Z}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces j}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}

where

,

are subvarieties of dimension zero, so both they and of course

are cellular. The long exact homology sequence gives

We have

Remark 3.2
.

(1)

The number of inverse images of

under the normalization map is the number of branches of

(2)

The motive

.

(3)

If
C
is a projective toric curve (not necessarily normal), then it is rational and the theorem applies.

Example 3.3
.

The projective toric curve
C=\mathrm{Proj}(\mathbb{C}[x,y,z]/(y^{2}z-x^{3}))
has as unique singularity a cusp at the origin, so it is unibranch. Therefore,
M(C)\cong\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\{1\}
.

Example 3.4
.

The nodal projective curve
C=\mathrm{Proj}(\mathbb{C}[x,y,z]/(y^{2}z-x^{3}-x^{2}z))
is also toric. It has a unique singularity with two branches. Thus,
H_{1}(C)\cong\mathbb{Z}
, and
M(C)\cong\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}[1]\oplus\mathbb{Z}\{1\}
.

Example 3.5
.

For a non-toric example, we can take the ampersand curve, i.e. the quartic with equation
(y^{2}-x^{2})(x-1)(2x-3)=0
.
It has three singularities, each of them with two branches, so
M(C)\cong\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{3}[1]\oplus\mathbb{Z}\{1\}
.

Example 3.6
.

For any
m\geq n\geq 1
we can construct a rational curve
C
with
|Z|=n
and
|E|=m
. Since the structure of the singularities is not relevant for the motive, we can take them to be ordinary: we just define
C
to be the pushout in schemes of
\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{C})^{\sqcup n}\leftarrow\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{C})^{\sqcup m}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
, where the first map is any surjective map.
The pushout exists by
[
Sch05
, 3.9]
.

3.2.
Cellular resolutions of singularities of toric varieties

We now consider the question of the existence of a cellular resolution of singularities for normal toric varieties. Let us start by observing that normal projective toric surfaces always admit one.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\Sigma
be a fan in
N_{\mathbb{R}}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Let
\widetilde{\Sigma}
be a smooth fan refining
\Sigma
by adding rays such that the associated toric morphism
X_{\widetilde{\Sigma}}\to X_{\Sigma}
is a resolution of singularities with exceptional locus
E
. Then
the variety
E
is a disjoint union of trees of projective lines, where by “tree” we mean an intersection of finitely many copies of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, where the first intersects the second transversally in a point, the second intercepts the third transversally in another point, etc., and those are the only intersections between them. In particular,
E
is cellular.

Proof.

First of all, such a refinement exists
[
CLS11
, 10.1.10]
. The exceptional locus
E
corresponds to the rays added to
\Sigma
. All the rays added to one of the cones in
\Sigma
generate one such tree of projective lines: this is
[
Ful93
, Page 47]
;
see also
[
CLS11
, 11.1.10, 11.2.2]
. Considering all the rays added to all the cones gives then such a disjoint union of trees. ∎

Remark 3.8
.

We have
H_{0}(E)=\mathbb{Z}^{s}
, where
s
is the number of singular cones of dimension 2 in
\Sigma
. To compute
H_{2}(E)
, we can proceed as follows. The variety
E
can be presented as an iterated (homotopy) pushout: The (homotopy) pushout of
\mathbb{P}^{1}\leftarrow\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
gives a tree of two projective lines, to which we can add another line via another (homotopy) pushout, etc. A Mayer–Vietoris argument then proves that
H_{2}(E)=\mathbb{Z}^{k}
, where
k
is the number of rays added to
\Sigma
. Therefore,
Theorem

2.7
gives that
M(E)=\mathbb{Z}^{s}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\{1\}^{k}
.

Proposition 3.9
.

Any normal projective toric surface
X
admits a cellular resolution of singularities.

Proof.

The singular locus consists of finitely many points, and as observed in
Proposition

3.7
, we can find a resolution
\widetilde{X}\to X
with exceptional locus cellular;
\widetilde{X}
is smooth projective toric, hence also cellular.
∎

If we take a fan
\Sigma
and refine it to a fan
\widetilde{\Sigma}
so as to obtain the toric resolution of singularities
X_{\widetilde{\Sigma}}\to X_{\Sigma}
, then if
X_{\widetilde{\Sigma}}
is not projective, it may not be cellular. Let us now state a lemma that guarantees that it will be cellular under more general hypotheses than completeness of the fan:

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\widetilde{\Sigma}
be a smooth fan in
N_{\mathbb{R}}
. Suppose
X_{\widetilde{\Sigma}}
is quasiprojective. If there exists a
u\in N
such that:

a)

|\widetilde{\Sigma}|
contains
|\widetilde{\Sigma}|+u
, where
|\widetilde{\Sigma}|
denotes the support of
\widetilde{\Sigma}
,

b)

\langle m_{i},u\rangle\not=0
for all
i
, where
\{m_{i}\}_{i}\subseteq M
is a set consisting of semigroup generators of all the
\sigma^{\vee}\cap M
, for all the maximal cones
\sigma\in\widetilde{\Sigma}
,

then
X_{\widetilde{\Sigma}}
is cellular.

Proof.

Denote by
T_{N}
the torus of
X_{\widetilde{\Sigma}}
. For any
u\in N
, consider the one-parameter subgroup
\lambda^{u}:\mathbb{C}^{*}\to T_{N}
it defines, which gives a
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-action on
X_{\widetilde{\Sigma}}
.

Condition
a
) is equivalent to the limits
\lim_{t\to 0}t\cdot x
existing for all
x\in X_{\widetilde{\Sigma}}
, where
t\cdot x
denotes the
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-action above
[
NP19
, 5.2.1]
.

Condition
b
) is equivalent to the one-parameter subgroup being
regular
, which means that the fixed point set of the action of
T_{N}
on
X_{\widetilde{\Sigma}}
coincides with that of its one-parameter subgroup
[
Car02
, 4.2.5]
. Since
T_{N}
fixes only finitely many points, this guarantees that its one-parameter subgroup does so, too, and thus we are in the conditions of
Example

2.3
.(
3
).
∎

Remark 3.11
.

(1)

The toric variety of a full-dimensional lattice polyhedron is quasiprojective
[
CLS11
, 7.1.10]
. If
\widetilde{\Sigma}
is the refinement of some fan
\Sigma
and
X_{\Sigma}
is quasiprojective (resp. projective), then
X_{\widetilde{\Sigma}}
is also quasiprojective (resp. projective), by
[
Har77
, 7.16]
and
[
GW20
, 13.102]
.

(2)

Condition
a
) is satisfied for any
u\in|\Sigma|\cap N
if
|\Sigma|
is convex: for example, if
\Sigma
is generated by a single one-dimensional cone, so that the toric variety is affine.

(3)

The fan
\Sigma
generated by
\mathrm{Cone}(e_{1},-e_{1}+e_{2})
and
\mathrm{Cone}(e_{1},-e_{1}-e_{2})
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is not convex but it satisfies
a
): the vectors that satisfy it are those in
\mathrm{Cone}(e_{1}+e_{2},e_{1}-e_{2})
which is the image of the “missing cone”
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\mathrm{int}(|\Sigma|)
under the central symmetry at the origin, where
\mathrm{int}
denotes the topological interior.

(4)

An example of a fan that does not satisfy Condition
a
) for any
u\in N
is the fan generated by the cones
\mathrm{Cone}(e_{1},e_{2})
and
\mathrm{Cone}(-e_{1},-e_{2})
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Therefore, this toric variety does not admit any action of a one-parameter subgroup for which the limits
\lim_{t\to 0}t\cdot x
exist for all
x\in X
.

(5)

We now give an example of a fan that satisfies Condition
a
) for some
u
, but does not satisfy Condition
b
) for any vector. Take the fan
\Sigma
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
generated by the two cones
\sigma_{1}=\mathrm{Cone}(-e_{1}+e_{2}+e_{3},-e_{1}-e_{2}+e_{3},e_{3})
and
\sigma_{2}=\mathrm{Cone}(e_{1}+e_{2}+e_{3},e_{1}-e_{2}+e_{3},e_{3})
. Then the only
u\in N
that satisfy Condition
a)
are those in
L\coloneqq\mathrm{Cone}(e_{3})\cap N
. Indeed, a reformulation of Condition
a)
is that
|\Sigma|
is star-shaped around
u
(
[
NP19
, 5.2.1]
), but this only happens for the vectors in
L
. We find that
\sigma_{1}^{\vee}=\mathrm{Cone}(e_{1}^{*}+e_{3}^{*},-e_{1}^{*}+e_{2}^{*},-e_{1}^{*}-e_{2}^{*})
.
The second covector, for example, is perpendicular to
\mathrm{Cone}(e_{3})
, so no vector in
L
can satisfy Condition
b)
.

(6)

If there exists a cone of maximal dimension in
N_{\mathbb{R}}
consisting of vectors satisfying Condition
a)
, then there exists a vector
u\in N
satisfying both
a)
and
b)
. Indeed, the vectors that do not satisfy
b)
are a finite union of hyperplanes: they cannot cover an entire maximal cone. In particular, if the support
|\Sigma|
is convex, then there exists a vector
u\in N
satisfying both conditions. In particular, smooth affine toric varieties are cellular.

(7)

By the same reasoning, if the region
\nu\coloneqq N_{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\mathrm{int}(|\Sigma|)
consists of a cone of maximal dimension, then there exists a vector
u\in N
satisfying both conditions. In this case, it is not hard to see that Condition a) is satisfied for the maximal cone of vectors which are in
c(\nu)
, where
c
is the central symmetry with respect to the origin.

Proposition 3.12
.

Any normal affine toric surface
X
admits a cellular resolution of singularities.

Proof.

Suppose
X
is the toric variety of the cone
\sigma
in
N_{\mathbb{R}}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The singular locus of
X
consists of finitely many points, and as recalled in
Proposition

3.7
, we can refine
\sigma
to a fan
\widetilde{\Sigma}
so that the resolution of singularities
X_{\widetilde{\Sigma}}\to X
has cellular exceptional locus. We now apply the previous lemma. We use
Remark

3.11
: by (
1
),
X_{\widetilde{\Sigma}}
is quasiprojective, so since
|\sigma|=|\widetilde{\Sigma}|
is convex, we have that
X_{\widetilde{\Sigma}}
is cellular by (
6
).
∎

3.3.
Motives of toric surfaces

We can now describe the motives of projective normal toric surfaces, and the motive with compact support of many other normal toric surfaces, notably all the affine ones. We begin by recalling the description of the Borel–Moore homology of normal toric surfaces.

Definition 3.13
.

The
index
of a fan in
N_{\mathbb{R}}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
r
rays is the greatest common divisor of the determinants
\det(u_{i},u_{j})
,
1\leq i<j\leq r
, where the
u_{i}
are the minimal ray generators of the fan.

Remark 3.14
.

Alternatively, the index of a fan is the index of the lattice spanned by the
u_{i}
inside
N\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

Proposition 3.15
.

Let
\Sigma
be a fan in
N_{\mathbb{R}}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The Borel–Moore homology of
X_{\Sigma}
is:

H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{0}(X_{\Sigma})=\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}&\text{if }\Sigma\text{ is complete,}\\
0&\text{else,}\end{cases}\hskip 14.22636ptH^{\mathrm{BM}}_{1}(X_{\Sigma})=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\Sigma\text{ is complete,}\\
\mathbb{Z}^{-d_{2}+d_{1}-d_{0}}&\text{else,}\end{cases}

H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{2}(X_{\Sigma})=\mathbb{Z}^{d_{1}-s}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/m,\hskip 14.22636ptH^{\mathrm{BM}}_{3}(X_{\Sigma})=\mathbb{Z}^{2-s},\hskip 14.22636ptH^{\mathrm{BM}}_{4}(X_{\Sigma})=\mathbb{Z},\hskip 14.22636pt

where
d_{i}
is the number of cones of dimension
i
,
s
is the dimension of the subspace of
N_{\mathbb{R}}
spanned by the rays and
m
is the index of
\Sigma
.

Proof.

This follows from the toric spectral sequence of
[
Jor98
]
, as explained in Theorem 3.4.2 therein.
∎

Remark 3.16
.

If the fan
\Sigma
in
N_{\mathbb{R}}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
is non-degenerate, i.e.
s=2
, then we have that
H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{0}(X_{\Sigma})
is free,
H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{3}(X_{\Sigma})=0
and
H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{4}(X_{\Sigma})
is free, so the conditions of
Theorem

2.7
.(
2a
) on the homology are satisfied.

Corollary 3.17
.

Let
\Sigma
be a complete fan in
N_{\mathbb{R}}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The homology of
X_{\Sigma}
is concentrated in degrees
0,2,4
, where it takes the following values:
H_{0}(X_{\Sigma})=H_{4}(X_{\Sigma})=\mathbb{Z}
, and
H_{2}(X_{\Sigma})=\mathbb{Z}^{r-2}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/m
,
for
r
the number of rays in
\Sigma
and
m
the index of
\Sigma
.

We can now describe the motive of any projective normal toric surface:

Proposition 3.18
.

Let
\Sigma
be a complete fan in
N_{\mathbb{R}}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then

M(X_{\Sigma})\cong\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{r-2}\{1\}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/m\{1\}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\{2\},

where
r=|\Sigma(1)|
and
m
is the index in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
of the lattice spanned by the minimal generators of the rays of
\Sigma
.

Proof.

As observed in
Proposition

3.9
,
X_{\Sigma}
admits a cellular resolution of singularities. The homology groups of
X_{\Sigma}
are given in
Corollary

3.17
: since the odd ones are zero, we are in the conditions of
Theorem

2.7
.(
2b
). The result follows. ∎

Remark 3.19
.

(1)

If
\Sigma
is smooth, then
m=1
, so if
\Sigma
is moreover complete, then:

M(X_{\Sigma})\cong\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\{1\}^{r-2}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\{2\}.

(2)

The motive of
X_{\Sigma}
as in

3.18
is pure Tate if and only if

. In other words: the motive of
X_{\Sigma}
is not pure Tate if and only if all the 2-dimensional cones of
\Sigma
are singular (in particular,
X_{\Sigma}
must have at least three singular points), and the greatest common divisor of their multiplicities is

.

Example 3.27
.

Let
\sigma
be the cone spanned by the vectors
e_{1}+e_{2}
,
-e_{1}+e_{2}
,
e_{3}

N_{\mathbb{R}}\cong\mathbb{R}^{3}

\Sigma

\mathrm{Cone}(e_{1}+e_{2},-e_{1}+e_{2})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}

\mathrm{Cone}(e_{3})\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, so the affine toric variety

can be described as

\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{C}[x,y,z]/(xy-2z))\times\mathbb{C}.

The minimal singular cone of
\Sigma
is generated by
e_{1}+e_{2},-e_{1}+e_{2}
, so the singular locus is
Z\cong\mathbb{C}
.

We can refine
\Sigma
by adding
\mathrm{Cone}(e_{2},e_{3})
, which generates a fan
\widetilde{\Sigma}
. The exceptional locus is then

. We now compute the Borel–Moore homology of
U_{\sigma}
using
torhom

H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{i}(U_{\sigma})=\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}/2&\text{if }i=4,\\
\mathbb{Z}&\text{if }i=6,\\
0&\text{else.}\\

Since
|\widetilde{\Sigma}|
is convex, by
Remark

X_{\widetilde{\Sigma}}
is quasiprojective by
Remark

3.11

Example 3.28
.

Consider the cube with vertices
(\pm 1,\pm 1,\pm 1)
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Let
N
be the lattice generated by the vertices. Let
\Sigma
be the complete fan in

whose six maximal cones are the cones over the faces of the cube. The region bounded inside the cube is the polar polytope of another lattice polytope containing zero
[
CLS11
, 2.3.11]
, so
X_{\Sigma}
is projective. Its singular locus consists of the six points corresponding to the six maximal cones.

We can resolve the singularities of
X_{\Sigma}
by refining the maximal cones, dividing each of them by a diagonal. The exceptional locus is isomorphic to
(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{\sqcup 6}
. Moreover,
X_{\widetilde{\Sigma}}
is a smooth and projective (by
Remark

3.11
.(
1
)) toric variety, so it is cellular. Thus,
X_{\Sigma}
admits a cellular resolution of singularities.

We now find the homology of
X_{\Sigma}
. From
[
Ful93
, Pages 105-106]
, we read the homology groups in all degrees but 1 and 5. In those degrees, we use
[
Jor98
, 3.5.1]
to conclude that

H_{i}(X_{\Sigma})=\mathbb{Z}\text{ for }i=0,2,6,\hskip 14.22636ptH_{3}(X_{\Sigma})=\mathbb{Z}^{2},\hskip 14.22636ptH_{4}(X_{\Sigma})=\mathbb{Z}^{5},\hskip 14.22636ptH_{1}(X_{\Sigma})=H_{5}(X_{\Sigma})=0.

Since all the groups in even grading are free, we can apply
Theorem

2.7
.(
2b

M(X_{\Sigma})\cong\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\{1\}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{2}\{1\}[1]\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{5}\{2\}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\{3\}.

Example 3.29
.

The exceptional locus of a toric resolution of singularities need not be cellular. For example, in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
take the fan generated by the three cones
\sigma_{1}=\mathrm{Cone}(e_{1},e_{2},e_{1}+e_{2}\pm e_{3})
,
\sigma_{2}=\mathrm{Cone}(e_{2},-e_{1}-e_{2},-e_{2}\pm e_{3})
,
\sigma_{3}=\mathrm{Cone}(-e_{1}-e_{2},e_{1},-e_{2}\pm e_{3})
. The singular locus consists of the three points corresponding to the three maximal cones. We can resolve the singularities by subdivision: add the faces generated by the first two cone generators in each of the cones. The exceptional locus consists of three projective lines, where the first/second/third intersects the second/third/first each in a different point: this is not a cellular variety.

Example 3.30
.

Let
\Sigma

e_{1},\dots,e_{n},-e_{1}-\cdots-ke_{n}

\mathbb{P}(1,\dots,1,k)
. The singular locus is a single point, and we can resolve this singularity by adding the ray
-e_{n}

\widetilde{\Sigma}
; the variety
X_{\widetilde{\Sigma}}
is projective by
Remark

3.11
.(
1
). The exceptional divisor is
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}

]
, so applying

2.7
proves that the motive of
\mathbb{P}(1,\dots,1,k)

\mathbb{P}^{n}
