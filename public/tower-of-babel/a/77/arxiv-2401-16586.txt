On counting totally imaginary number fields
A. Raghuram, Qiyao Yu

Abstract
. A number field is said to be a CM-number field if it is a totally imaginary quadratic extension of a totally real number field. We define a totally imaginary number field to be of CM -type if it contains a CM-subfield, and of TR -type if it does not contain a CM-subfield. For quartic totally imaginary number fields when ordered by discriminant, we show that about 69.95% are of TR -type and about 33.05 % are of CM -type. For a sextic totally imaginary number field we classify its type in terms of its Galois group and possibly some additional information about the location of complex conjugation in the Galois group.

Introduction

The past few decades have seen a huge amount of work on counting number fields; see, for example,
[

; these represent only a tiny sampling of a huge and growing body of related literature which the interested reader can search on MathSciNet.
In this article, we introduce a new aspect to the general problem of counting number fields by restricting our attention to totally imaginary number fields and then searching for those which contain a CM subfield.

A number field will mean a finite extension of
\mathbb{Q}
. A number field
is said to be a CM-number field if it is a totally imaginary quadratic extension of a totally real number field. A totally imaginary number field is said to be of
CM
-type if it contains a CM-subfield, and is said to be of
TR
-type if it does not contain a CM-subfield. One can organize this definition in other ways.
Let
F
be a totally imaginary number field; since the compositum of two totally real fields is again totally real,
F
contains a maximal totally real subfield, say,

F_{0}
. There is at most one totally imaginary quadratic extension
F_{1}
of
F_{0}
contained inside
F
; if such a CM field
F_{1}
exists, then
F
is of
CM
-type and
F_{1}
is in fact the maximal CM subfield of
F
; otherwise, we say
F
is of
TR
-type, and we put
F_{1}=F_{0}.
This dichotomy of totally imaginary number fields played an important role in
[
9
]
.

Let
n\geq 2
be an even positive integer. To count totally imaginary number fields
F
of prescribed degree
n=[F:\mathbb{Q}]
and bounded discriminant
\delta_{F}\leq X
, define

N_{n}^{\textbf{TI}}(X)
to be the number of isomorphism classes of totally imaginary number fields
F
of degree
[F:\mathbb{Q}]=n
, with absolute discriminant
\delta_{F}\leq X
.
To count those totally imaginary fields of
CM
-type, define
N_{n}^{\textbf{CM}}(X)
to be the
number of isomorphism classes of totally imaginary number fields
F
of
CM
-type of degree
n
and
\delta_{F}\leq X
. We would like to know the asymptotics of the ratio

R_{n}^{\textbf{CM}}(X):=\frac{N_{n}^{\textbf{CM}}(X)}{N_{n}^{\textbf{TI}}(X)},\quad\mbox{as $X\to\infty$}.

The question is trivial for
n=2
since an imaginary quadratic field is a CM-field;
R_{2}^{\textbf{CM}}(X)=1.
Henceforth, assume that
n\geq 4.

For quartic extensions (
n=4
), in Theorem
3.1
we show that
\lim_{X\to\infty}R_{4}^{\textbf{CM}}(X)\approx 0.33052
. The proof uses the results of
Bhargava
[
3
]
and Cohen–Diaz Y Diaz–Olivier
[
4
]
.

For sextic extensions (
n=6
), in Theorem
4.1
, we classify the
type of a totally imaginary extension in terms of its Galois group and possibly some additional data. If

F
is a sextic totally imaginary extension, and
F_{s}
is its Galois closure,
then the Galois group of
F_{s}/\mathbb{Q}
is a transitive subgroup of
S_{6}
containing
a
(2,2,2)
cycle. There are
11
possible such subgroups of
S_{6}
(up to conjugacy), and for each one of them we can classify the type of
F
. In particular, we show that if

G
is
S_{4},

S_{5},
or
S_{6}
then
F
is of
TR
-type;
if
G
is
C_{6},

S_{3}
,
D_{6},

S_{3}\times C_{3},

A_{4}\times C_{2},

S_{3}\times S_{3},
or
C_{3}^{2}\rtimes D_{4},

then
F
is of
CM
-type, and furthermore in each case one can decide if
F
itself is a CM field or whether it only contains a proper CM-subfield in terms of the Galois group and its internal structure. The case of
D_{6}
offers some piquant features. Finally, the case
G=S_{4}\times C_{2}
is more complicated because there are totally imaginary fields of either type with this Galois group; identifying an order
2
element of
G
that represents complex conjugation serves to distinguish between the two types. Finally, within the framework of currently
available results on counting number fields we are unable to predict a precise value for
\lim_{X\to\infty}R_{6}^{\textbf{CM}}(X).
Even assuming Malle’s conjecture (see
[
8
, Conj. 1.1]
)
on counting number fields it seems difficult to predict a value because of examples like the
S_{4}\times C_{2}
case, and also because we do not apparently know–even conjecturally–the constants of proportionality denoted
c(k,G)
for
k=\mathbb{Q}
in
[
8
]
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notations

The cylic group of order
n
is denoted
C_{n}.
The Klein
4
-group
C_{2}\times C_{2}
is denoted
V_{4}.

The dihedral group of order
2n
is
D_{n}=\{1,r,r^{2},\dots,r^{n-1}\}\rtimes\{1,s\}
, with

r^{n}=s^{2}=1
and
srs=r^{-1}.

The permutation group on
n
letters, usually taken to be
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
,
is denoted
S_{n}
; and
A_{n}
denotes the alternating group–the subgroup of
S_{n}
of all even permutation.

2.2.
Examples

A number field will always mean a finite extension of
\mathbb{Q}
, and by its Galois group one means the Galois group of its normal closure.
There are several families of examples of totally imaginary number fields of the two types as will become clear throughout the paper. We found it especially helpful to search the LMFDB (the
L
-functions and modular forms database
[
7
]
) for number fields with prescribed constraints, such as degree, signature, Galois group, and whether or not it is a CM field.
For example, we can set the degree to be
4
, the signature as

[0,2]
, the Galois group as
S_{4}
or also by its label 4T5 (the fifth group amongst the transitive subgroups of
S_{4}
) and then upon hitting search we get a list ordered by discriminant of quartic totally imaginary number fields with Galois group
S_{4}
–the first item in this list is the number field labelled as 4.0.229.1; the parameters in the label stands for degree, number of real embeddings, absolute discriminant, and the last one is item number for all
the number fields with the given first three parameters. As another example, if we search for number fields with degree
6
,
signature
[0,3]
, and Galois group
A_{6}
(label 6T15), then we get ‘no matches’. Indeed, if

3.
Quartic extensions

In this section, we study the case of quartic number fields;
n=4.

A number field is said to be primitive if it admits no intermediate subfields. Clearly, a totally imaginary primitive number field is of
TR
-type.
The number field labelled 4.0.229.1 in LMFDB mentioned above is an example of a totally imaginary quartic primitive field and hence of
TR
-type.
Given a totally imaginary quartic extension
F/\mathbb{Q}
, it is either primitive or contains a quadratic subfield. If
F
contains a real quadratic extension, then
F
is a CM-field; and if
F
contains an imaginary quadratic extension–which is necessarily a CM-field then
F
is of
CM
-type. Hence, if
F
is not primitive then it is of
CM
-type; whence:

F\text{ is of {TR}-type}\ \ \iff\ F\text{ is primitive}.

Let
F
be a quartic totally imaginary number field and
F_{s}
its Galois closure. Then the Galois group
G=\gal(F_{s}/\mathbb{Q})
is a transitive subgroup of
S_{4}
via its action on
the set of
4
roots of an irreducible quartic polynomial of a primitive element generating
F
. Up to isomorphism,
G
is one of

C_{4},

V_{4},

D_{4},

A_{4},
or
S_{4}
. Let us consider each case.
If
G
is
C_{4}
or
V_{4}
then it is not primitive and hence of
CM
-type. If
G
is
D_{4}
then
H=\gal(F_{s}/F)
is of order
2
, and any order
2
subgroup of
D_{4}
is contained in some subgroup, say
K
, of order
4
of
D_{4}
; then
F_{s}^{K}
is a quadratic subfield of
F
; whence
F
is of
CM
-type.
If
G
is
A_{4}
(resp.,
S_{4}
) then
H=\gal(F_{s}/F)
corresponds to a subgroup of order 3 (resp., 6); such a subgroup is maximal; hence by Galois theory
F
is primitive. To summarize, we have shown

F
is primitive
\iff

\gal(F_{s}/\mathbb{Q})
is
S_{4}
or
A_{4}
.

The argument above does not rely on the fact that
F
is totally imaginary and but only employs Galois theory and elementary group theory.

Thus, to count quartic number fields of
TR
-type (which is equivalent to counting
CM
-type), we need to know the asymptotics for totally imaginary quartic fields with Galois group
S_{4}
and
A_{4}
. Towards this, let us recall some results from the literature on such asymptotics; in the results quoted below, number fields are ordered by discriminant.

(1)

Bhargava
[
3
]
showed that approximately
17.111\%
of quartic fields have Galois group
D_{4}
, and the remaining
82.889\%
of quartic fields have Galois group
S_{4}
; in particular, the contribution from the
C_{4}
,
V_{4},
and
A_{4}
quartic fields are negligible in comparison to the
S_{4}
and
D_{4}
quartic fields.

(2)

Bhargava also proved that asymptotically
30\%
of the
S_{4}
-quartic fields are totally imaginary; see Thm. 1 of
loc. cit.

(3)

Cohen–Diaz Y Diaz–Olivier
[
4
, Prop. 6.2]
proved that asymptotically
71.747\%
of the
D_{4}
-quartic fields are totally imaginary.

From these results one can deduce the first main theorem of this article:

Theorem 3.1
.

When ordered by absolute discriminant, asymptotically, about
66.948\%
of the totally imaginary quartic fields are of
TR
-type, and the remaining
33.052\%

of the totally imaginary quartic fields are of
CM
-type, i.e.,

\lim_{X\to\infty}R_{4}^{\textbf{CM}}(X)\approx 0.33052.

Proof.

Using the results quoted above, the proof is a simple exercise in Bayes’ theorem. Within the sample space of all quartic fields ordered by discriminant (taken to be
bounded by
X
and then letting
X
go to infinity to get the required asymptotics), let
D
stand for the subset of
D_{4}
extensions,
S
stand for the subset of
S_{4}
extensions,
and
T
stand for the subset of totally imaginary extensions. The results quoted can be rephrased as:

(1)

P(D)=0.17111
and
P(S)=0.82889
;

(2)

P(T|S)=0.3
;

(3)

P(T|D)=0.71747
.

We compute then that

P(S|T)=\frac{P(T|S)\cdot P(S)}{P(T|S)\cdot P(S)+P(T|D)\cdot P(D)}=\frac{0.30\times 0.82889}{0.30\times 0.82889+0.71747\times 0.17111}=0.66948,

i.e., of all totally imaginary quartic fields, about 66.948% have Galois group
S_{4}
and hence of
TR
-type and the remaining 33.052% have Galois
group
D_{4}
and hence of
CM
-type.
∎

(5)

(6T8)

4.2.
A digression on possible Galois groups

That
A_{6}
is not the Galois group of a sextic totally imaginary number field is a special case of the following more general lemma:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
F
be a number field of signature
(r_{1},r_{2})
and degree
n=[F:\mathbb{Q}]=r_{1}+2r_{2}
. Let
G=\gal(F_{s}/\mathbb{Q})
be the Galois group of a Galois closure
F_{s}
of
F
.
If
r_{2}
is odd, then
G\not\cong A_{n}
.

Proof.

The number field
F
is defined by an irredcuble polynomial
f(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
. The polynomial discriminant,
\mathrm{disc}(f)
, is well-defined up to non-zero squares. The discriminant root field of the extension
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{\mathrm{disc}(f)})
is well-defined. The Galois group
G
is a subgroup of
S_{n}
, well-defined up to conjugation. The discriminant root field is
the fixed field of
\gal(F_{s}/\mathbb{Q})\cap A_{n}
. Note that
\mathrm{sign}(\delta_{F})=(-1)^{r_{2}}
and
\mathrm{sign}(\delta_{F})=\mathrm{sign}(\mathrm{disc}(f))
. If
r_{2}
is odd, then
\mathrm{sign}(\mathrm{disc}(f))=-1
. Thus,
\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt{\mathrm{disc}(f)}\right)
is an imaginary quadratic extension of
\mathbb{Q}
. Suppose
\gal(F_{s}/\mathbb{Q})=A_{n}
, then by Galois theory

\gal\left(F_{s}/\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt{\mathrm{disc}(f)}\right)\right)\cong\gal(F_{s}/\mathbb{Q})\cap A_{n}=\gal(F_{s}/\mathbb{Q}),
which implies
\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt{\mathrm{disc}(f)}\right)=\mathbb{Q}
, giving us a contradiction.
∎

In particular, if
F
is a totally imaginary number field of degree
n
, and
n/2
is odd, then
\gal(F_{s}/\mathbb{Q})\not\cong A_{n}
. This applies to our case of
n=6
. This ends the digression.

4.3.
The main result on sextic totally imaginary number fields

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
F
be a sextic totally imaginary number field,
F_{s}
its Galois closure, and
G=\gal(F_{s}/\mathbb{Q})
its Galois group.

(1)

If
G
is
S_{4}
,
S_{5}
, or
S_{6}
then
F
is of
TR
-type.

(2)

If
G
is
C_{6}
,
S_{3}
,
D_{6}
,
S_{3}\times C_{3}
,
A_{4}\times C_{2}
,
S_{3}\times S_{3}
, or
C_{3}^{2}\rtimes D_{4}
then
F
is of
CM
-type.

(3)

If
G=S_{4}\times C_{2}
then
F
can be of either type; one can distinguish between the two types by identifying an order
2
element of
G
that represents complex conjugation.

4.4.
Proof of Theorem
4.1

4.4.1.
Preliminary remarks

We still have the basic observation

F\text{ is primitive }\implies F\text{ is of {TR}-type}.

Unlike the degree 4 case, examples of
F
of
TR
-type but not primitive do exist

.
If
F
is not primitive, then it can only contain quadratic or cubic subextensions. Quadratic subextensions of
F
can in principle be either real or imaginary; but if a quadratic subextension is real, then
F
is a cubic totally imaginary extension of a real quadratic field, which by ramification theory is not possible. Thus, the maximal totally real subfield
F_{0}
can either be
\mathbb{Q}
or a totally real cubic subextension. If
F
is not primitive and does not contain a cubic subextension, then the maximal totally real subfield
F_{0}
of

is
\mathbb{Q}
, and
F
is of
CM
-type. If
F
is not primitive and does contain a cubic subextension

, then

can be totally real or of mixed signature; if

is of
CM
-type. Otherwise,
i.e., if

is of

-type if

does not contain a quadratic subextension.
In conclusion,
F
is of

-type if and only if
F
contains neither a quadratic extension nor a totally real cubic extension.

4.4.2.
G=C_{6}

If
\gal(F_{s}/\mathbb{Q})\cong C_{6}

and the subfields of

4.4.3.
G=S_{3}

If
\gal(F_{s}/\mathbb{Q})\cong S_{3}

4.4.6.
G=S_{4}

If
\gal(F_{s}/\mathbb{Q})\cong S_{4}
, then

corresponds to an order 4 non-normal subgroup of

. From the lattice of subgroups of

is the corresponding

has a unique necessarily cubic subfield–denote this as

. We will show that
F_{c}
is not totally real, from which we conclude that
F
is of

TR
-type. To proceed, we first need the following lemma whose proof is an easy exercise.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose
E/F/\mathbb{Q}
is a tower of finite extensions and
E/\mathbb{Q}
is Galois with Galois group
G
. Let
H=\gal(E/F)
. Then
\Hm(F,E)
is in bijection with the set of cosets
G/H
. Furthermore, the bijection is
G
-equivariant.

Let
\Sigma_{F_{c}}=\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}\}
be the set of embeddings of
F_{c}
into
\mathbb{C}
. Suppose, by the way of seeking a contradiction, that
F_{c}
is totally real. If
\mathfrak{c}\in\aut(\mathbb{C})
denotes complex conjugation, we should have
\mathfrak{c}\circ\sigma_{k}=\sigma_{k}
for all
k=1,2,3
. Note that
\mathfrak{c}|_{F_{s}}
can be regarded as an element of order 2 in
S_{4}
. By the lemma above, we have a
S_{4}
-equivariant bijection
\Sigma_{F_{c}}\cong\gal(F_{s}/\mathbb{Q})/\gal(F_{s}/F_{c})=S_{4}/D_{4}^{j}
. Therefore, to say that
\mathfrak{c}\circ\sigma_{k}=\sigma_{k}
for all
k=1,2,3
is equivalent to
saying
\mathfrak{c}(xD_{4}^{j})=xD_{4}^{j}
for all
x\in S_{4}
. This means that

\mathfrak{c}\in\bigcap_{x\in S_{4}}xD_{4}^{j}x^{-1}=\bigcap_{j=1,2,3}D_{4}^{j}=V_{4}^{n}=\left\{1,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\right\}.
Hence,

\mathfrak{c}\in\left\{(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\right\}.

Given that
F
is totally imaginary,
\mathfrak{c}
exchanges pairs of conjugate-embeddings, or equivalently, exchanges pairs of cosets in
S_{4}/H
.
We look at the action of the possible choices of
\mathfrak{c}
on different possibilities of
H
.

First suppose that
H=C_{4}^{j}
. Assume, without loss of generality that

H=\{1,(1234),(13)(24),(1432)\}.

•

If
\mathfrak{c}=(12)(34)
, we have
\mathfrak{c}((14)H)=(14)H
since
(14)(13)(24)(14)=(12)(34).

•

If
\mathfrak{c}=(13)(24)
, we have
\mathfrak{c}H=H
.

•

If

4.4.7.
G=D_{6}

If
\gal(F_{s}/\mathbb{Q})\cong D_{6}
, then
H=\gal(F_{s}/F)
is an order 2 subgroup of
D_{6}
. Since
F/\mathbb{Q}
is not Galois, we rule out the case of
H=\langle r^{3}\rangle
. Any subfield
F^{\prime}\subset F
corresponds to either an order 4 or order 6 subgroup of
D_{6}
containing
H
. By the subgroup lattice of
D_{6}
(App.
A
, Fig.
2
), every order 2 subgroup (that is not
\langle r^{3}\rangle
) of
D_{6}
is contained in a unique subgroup of order 6 and a unique subgroup of order 4. Thus,
F
has a unique imaginary quadratic and a unique cubic subfield. Since the order 4 subgroups are not normal, the cubic subfield can be totally real or mixed signature.

We want to determine when the cubic subfield is totally real (resp., mixed signature). Let
H_{c}
denote the order 4 subgroup corresponding to the cubic subfield
F_{c}=F^{H_{c}}
. Let
\mathfrak{c}
denote complex conjugation whose restriction to
F_{s}
is an element of order 2 in
D_{6}
that we also denote
\mathfrak{c}
. We know that
\mathfrak{c}
acts on the set of embeddings
\Sigma_{F_{c}}
of
F_{c}
into
\mathbb{C}
. Lemma
4.2
implies that
\mathfrak{c}
acts on the set
G/H_{c}
. We can determine whether
F_{c}
is totally real by analyzing the action of
\mathfrak{c}
on
G/H_{c}
. In particular,
F_{c}
is totally real if and only if
\mathfrak{c}
acts trivially on
G/H_{c}
, which is equivalent to
\mathfrak{c}(gH_{c})=gH_{c}
for all
g\in G
. This gives us the condition

\mathfrak{c}\in\bigcap_{g\in G}gH_{c}g^{-1}.

By the same argument, if
H_{q}
denote the order 6 subgroup corresponding to the imaginary quadratic subfield
F_{q}=F^{H_{q}}
, then
\mathfrak{c}
should exchange pairs of cosets in
G/H_{q}
. Similarly, since
F
is totally imaginary,
\mathfrak{c}
should exchange pairs of cosets in
G/H
. From these two cases, we know that
\mathfrak{c}\not\in H_{q}
and
\mathfrak{c}\not\in H
. To summarize,
\mathfrak{c}

should satisfy:

\mathfrak{c}\in\bigcap_{g\in G}gH_{c}g^{-1},\quad\mathfrak{c}\not\in H_{q},\quad\mathfrak{c}\not\in H.\quad\quad\quad\quad(*)

With these properties, we can now enumerate the possible choices for
\mathfrak{c}
given different choices of
H
. From the symmetry in the subgroup lattice of
D_{6}
(App.
A
, Fig.
2
), it suffices to consider the cases when
H\in\{\langle s\rangle,\,\langle sr^{2}\rangle\,\langle sr^{4}\rangle\}
.
When
H=\langle s\rangle
, we know
H_{c}=\langle s,r^{3}\rangle
and
H_{q}=\langle s,r^{2}\rangle
. Since
\mathfrak{c}
is of order 2 and
\mathfrak{c}\not\in H,H_{q}
, one can only have
\mathfrak{c}\in\{r^{3},sr^{5},sr^{3},sr\}
. It is easy to verify that only
\mathfrak{c}=r^{3}
satisfies
(*)
. The other two cases of
H
are identical to the case
H=\langle s\rangle
. We can therefore conclude that
F_{c}
is totally real if and only if
\mathfrak{c}
is central (since
r^{3}
is the only order 2 central element in
D_{6}
). The following two examples show that both cases–
F_{c}
being totally real or mixed signature can happen.

(1)

The number field with label 6.0.309123.1 on LMFDB is a totally imaginary sextic field with Galois group
D_{6}
; it is a CM-field; its unique cubic subfield

F_{c}
with label 3.3.321.1 is totally real (
\mathfrak{c}
is necessarily central); the unique quadratic subfield
F_{q}
is
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3}).

(2)

The number field with label 6.0.14283.1 on LMFDB is a totally imaginary sextic field with Galois group
D_{6}
is not a CM-field; its unique cubic subfield
F_{c}
with label
3.1.23.1 is of mixed signature; the unique quadratic subfield is
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3}).
Hence
F
is of
CM
-type but not a CM-field.

Problem 4.1

When ordered by discriminant, determine what percentage of sextic totally imaginary fields with Galois group
