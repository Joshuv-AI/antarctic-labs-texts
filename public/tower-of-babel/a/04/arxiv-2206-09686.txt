On the equivalence of the BMO-norm of divergence-free vector fields and norm of related paracommutators
M. N. Demchenko

Abstract
We establish an estimate of the BMO-norm of a divergence-free vector field in ℝ 3 {\mathbb{R}}^{3} in terms of the operator norm of an associated paracommutator. The latter is essentially a Ψ \Psi DO, whose symbol depends linearly on the vector field. Together with the result of P. Auscher and M. Taylor concerning the converse estimate, this provides an equivalent norm in the space of divergence-free fields from BMO. Keywords: BMO, paracommutators, divergence-free fields.



1
Introduction

Let
I
be a singular integral operator of convolution type bounded in
L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.
Its compositions
Iu
and
uI
with a pointwise multiplier
u\in L_{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, and therefore, the commutator

I_{u}=[u,I],

(1)

are bounded in
L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
as well, and the corresponding operator norm can trivially be estimated in terms of
\|u\|_{L_{\infty}}
.
As is well known from harmonic analysis,
under some additional assumptions on
I
,
the commutator
I_{u}
can be estimated as follows
[
1
]
:

\|I_{u}\|\leqslant C\|u\|_{\rm BMO},

(2)

where
C
depends only on
d
,
I

(we recall the definition of the space BMO and some of its properties in sec.
3
).
This fact was generalized to a certain class of paradifferential operators
I_{u}
(paracommutators),
which depend on the coefficient
u
in a more general way, than it is prescribed by (
1
).
In particular, this is true for the operators studied in the present paper, which have the following form.
Consider the Weyl’s decomposition for vector-functions in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}

[
2
, Theorems 1.1, 2.1]
:

L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3};{\mathbb{C}}^{3})=G\oplus J,

where the subspaces
G
and
J
are defined as follows

\displaystyle G=\overline{G_{0}}^{L_{2}},\quad G_{0}=\{\partial\varphi\,|\,\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})\},

\displaystyle J=\overline{\{u\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{3};{\mathbb{C}}^{3})\,|\,{\rm div}u=0\}}^{L_{2}}.

To a vector-function
u\in L_{2,\,\rm loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{3};{\mathbb{C}}^{3})
,
we associate a linear operator
I_{u}:G_{0}\to G
acting on
f\in G_{0}
as follows

I_{u}f=P(u\times f),

(3)

where
\times
is the vector product,
P
is the orthogonal projection on
G
acting in
L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3};{\mathbb{C}}^{3})
.
Observe that
u\times f\in L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3};{\mathbb{C}}^{3})
, which means that
I_{u}f
is well-defined.
The operator
I_{u}
assumes a continuation to a bounded operator in
G
,
provided that the norm
\|I_{u}\|
is finite, the latter being defined by

\|I_{u}\|=\sup_{f\in G_{0}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\|I_{u}f\|_{L_{2}}}{\|f\|_{L_{2}}}.

(Note also that if
\|I_{u}\|<\infty
and
u
is a real-valued vector-function, then

I_{u}
is a bounded skew-symmetric operator, i.e.
I_{u}^{*}=-I_{u}
.)
The operator
I_{u}
satisfies estimate (
2
), if
u\in{\rm BMO}

[
3
]
,
[
4
, chap. 3, sec. 8]
.

The present paper concerns the converse of estimate (
2
) for the operators of the form (
3
):

\|u\|_{\rm BMO}\leqslant C\|I_{u}\|.

(4)

Such estimates are known
in the case when a linear mapping
u\mapsto I_{u}

satisfies a certain nondegeneracy condition (see
[
5
]
and the literature cited therein).
In particular, for the commutator (
1
), this result was established in
[
6
]
.
To our knowledge, none of the previous results applies when
I_{u}
is defined by relation (
3
).
In fact, such a relation of the field
u
and the operator
I_{u}
is degenerate, which
is demonstrated by

Theorem 1
.

Let
u\in L_{2,\,\rm loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{3};{\mathbb{C}}^{3})
satisfy the relation
{\rm curl}\,u=0
. Then
I_{u}=0
.

This simple (as it will be seen from the proof) fact indicates that estimate (
4
) can be valid only
under some additional assumptions on
u
.
Sufficient conditions are given by

Theorem 2
.

Let
u\in L_{2,\,\rm loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{3};{\mathbb{C}}^{3})
satisfy the condition

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\frac{|u(x)|}{1+|x|^{4}}\,dx<\infty,

(5)

the relation
{\rm div}u=0
, and
\|I_{u}\|<\infty
.
Then
u
belongs to BMO and satisfies estimate (
4
).

It should be stressed that the condition
u\in L_{2,\,\rm loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{3};{\mathbb{C}}^{3})
concerning local behavior of
u

and condition (
5
) concerning behavior of
u
at the infinity,
which both occur in Theorem
2
,
are not too restrictive in the sense that
they are satisfied by any
u
from BMO.
Thus in view of estimate (
2
) for the operators of the form (
3
) and Theorem
2
, we obtain

Corollary 1
.

There are positive constants
c
,
C
, such that
for any
u
from BMO satisfying
{\rm div}u=0
, we have

c\|I_{u}\|\leqslant\|u\|_{\rm BMO}\leqslant C\|I_{u}\|.

2
Proof of Theorem
1

It suffices to show that under the assumptions of Theorem
1
,
the field
u\times f
is orthogonal to
G
for any
f\in G_{0}
.
For
u\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3};{\mathbb{C}}^{3})
, this follows from the fact that for an arbitrary function
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
, we have

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\langle{}u\times f,\overline{\partial\varphi}\rangle dx=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}{\rm div}(u\times f)\overline{\varphi}\,dx=0

(here and further
\langle{}u,v\rangle
is the bilinear form in
{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
defined on the standard orthonormal frame

\{e_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1,2,3}
by the equality
\langle{}e_{\alpha},e_{\beta}\rangle=\delta_{\alpha\beta}
).
In the last equality we used the identity

{\rm div}(u\times f)=\langle{\rm curl}\,u,f\rangle-\langle{}u,{\rm curl}\,f\rangle

(6)

and the relation
{\rm curl}\,f=0
.

In the case of nonsmooth
u
, one can apply a smoothing mollifier, which provides smooth functions
u^{\varepsilon}
,
\varepsilon>0
,

{\rm curl}\,u^{\varepsilon}=0
,
approximating
u
in the
L_{1}
-norm on the intersection of the supports of
f
and
\varphi
.
We have

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\langle{}u\times f,\overline{\partial\varphi}\rangle dx=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\langle{}u,f\times\overline{\partial\varphi}\rangle dx=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\langle{}u^{\varepsilon},f\times\overline{\partial\varphi}\rangle dx\\
=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\langle{}u^{\varepsilon}\times f,\overline{\partial\varphi}\rangle dx=0.

3
BMO space

A function
u\in L_{1,\,\rm loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{d};{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
is said to belong to BMO if

\|u\|_{\rm BMO}=\sup_{Q}|Q|^{-1}\int_{Q}|u(x)-u_{Q}|\,dx<\infty,

(7)

where the supremum is taken over all cubes
Q\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

u_{Q}=|Q|^{-1}\int_{Q}u(x)\,dx.

For constant functions
u=\rm const
(and only for them), we have
\|u\|_{\rm BMO}=0
.
Thus the functional
\|u\|_{\rm BMO}
is a
seminorm
, rather than a norm.
However, we will call it the BMO-norm.

Functions from BMO belong to
L_{p,\,\rm loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
for any
1\leqslant p<\infty
,
and the norm (
7
) is equivalent to

\sup_{Q}\left(|Q|^{-1}\int_{Q}|u(x)-u_{Q}|^{p}\,dx\right)^{1/p}.

Recall that we have already used the fact that
{\rm BMO}\subset L_{2,\,\rm loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
when we obtained Corollary
1
.

We will need certain characterizations of
L_{2}
and BMO spaces, which differ from their standard definitions.
Let a real-valued radially symmetric function
\Phi
from
C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

satisfy the following conditions

\Phi\not\equiv 0,\quad\widehat{\Phi}(0)=0

(8)

(
\hat{f}
denotes Fourier transform of
f
).
In this case, we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}\widehat{\Phi}(t\xi)^{2}\frac{dt}{t}=c^{2}>0,\quad\xi\neq 0

(9)

(due to (
8
), the integral converges and
c
is positive).
Then
L_{2}
space can be characterized in terms of convolutions of its functions with the functions

\Phi_{t}(x)=t^{-d}\Phi(t^{-1}x),\quad t>0.

(10)

Namely, for any function
u\in L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, we have

\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\Phi_{t}*u\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\frac{dt}{t}\right)^{1/2}=c\|u\|_{L_{2}},

(11)

where
c
is the same constant as in equality (
9
).
This fact is a trivial consequence of Plancherel’s theorem,
since
\widehat{\Phi}_{t}(\xi)=\widehat{\Phi}(t\xi)
.
Thus the convolutions
\Phi_{t}*u
provide an equivalent norm in
L_{2}
space.
As is known, various function spaces are characterized in terms of such convolutions
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
,
which is far less trivial than the same fact for
L_{2}
space.
This concerns Sobolev spaces (or, more generally, Triebel-Lizorkin spaces),
Besov’s spaces, Hardy space, and BMO space.
In most cases, conditions similar to (
8
) (more strong ones, as a rule) are imposed on
\Phi
.
Now we give a characterization of BMO space, which will be used later.
For any
u
from BMO, we have
[
7
]

\sup_{Q}\left(\int_{0}^{l(Q)}\|\Phi_{t}*u\|_{Q}^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}\right)^{1/2}\leqslant C\|u\|_{\rm BMO},

where
l(Q)
is the side length of the cube
Q
,
and the functional
\|\cdot\|_{Q}
is defined as follows

\|u\|_{Q}=\left(|Q|^{-1}\int_{Q}|u|^{2}dx\right)^{1/2}.

The converse estimate holds as well:
if a function
u\in L_{1,\,\rm loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{d};{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
satisfies condition

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{|u(x)|}{1+|x|^{d+1}}\,dx<\infty,

(12)

then
[
7
]

\|u\|_{\rm BMO}\leqslant C\sup_{Q}\left(\int_{0}^{l(Q)}\|\Phi_{t}*u\|_{Q}^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}\right)^{1/2}.

(13)

Condition (
5
) in Theorem
2
is nothing else than condition (
12
) in the case
d=3
.
For any function from BMO, condition (
12
) holds automatically.
Thus the supremum occurring in the last two estimates yields an equivalent norm in BMO space.

Auxiliary assertions

\Phi

\Psi=\Phi*\Phi

\Phi=\Delta\Theta
,

\Theta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\,|\,\,|x|<1/2\})

\Theta\not\equiv 0

\Phi,\Psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
.
Besides, the functions
\Phi

8

For
\alpha=1,2,3

\Phi^{\alpha}=\partial\partial_{\alpha}\Theta.

Let
\Phi_{t}

(

\Phi
,
\Phi^{\alpha}

To a vector-function

u^{\alpha}_{t}=\Phi^{\alpha}_{t}\,\vec{*}\,u,\quad\alpha=1,2,3,\quad t>0,

(u\,\vec{*}\,v)(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}u(x-y)\times v(y)\,dy.

In the proof of Theorem

To this end, we will need the following lemma, which relates them to the functions

Lemma 1
.

For
u\in L_{1,\,\rm loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{3};{\mathbb{C}}^{3})
,
{\rm div}u=0
,
t>0
, we have

\Psi_{t}*u=-\sum_{\alpha=1,2,3}\Phi^{\alpha}_{t}\,\vec{*}\,u^{\alpha}_{t}.

(15)

Proof.

Since
\Psi_{t}
,
\Phi^{\alpha}_{t}
are compactly supported functions,
both sides of equality (
15
) are smooth functions in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.
It is possible to approximate
u
by smooth divergence-free convolutions with an appropriate smooth compactly supported function.
So it will suffice to establish (
15
) for smooth functions
u
.

Now fix an arbitrary point
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and choose a closed ball
B
centered at
x

(its radius will be specified later).
In the ball
B
, we have

u={\rm curl}\,z,\quad z\in C^{\infty}(B;{\mathbb{C}}^{3}),

which is a consequence of the condition
{\rm div}u=0
.
Choose an arbitrary smooth compactly supported extension of
z
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, which will be denoted by the same symbol,
and define the field
v={\rm curl}\,z
entirely in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.
Since
u=v
in
B
, we will have

(\Psi_{t}*u)(x)=(\Psi_{t}*v)(x),\quad\left(\Phi^{\alpha}_{t}\,\vec{*}\,u^{\alpha}_{t}\right)(x)=\left(\Phi^{\alpha}_{t}\,\vec{*}\,v^{\alpha}_{t}\right)(x),

as soon as we choose the radius of
B
sufficiently large
that relations
\Psi_{t}(x-y)=0=\Phi^{\alpha}_{t}(x-y)
hold for any
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\setminus B
.
Therefore we will obtain (
15
) at
x
,
as soon as we establish the same relation for
v
(incidentally, the latter will be established entirely in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}

rather than at the single point
x
).
We have

\widehat{\Phi}^{\alpha}(\xi)=\widehat{\Phi}(\xi)\,\langle\xi,e_{\alpha}\rangle\frac{\xi}{|\xi|^{2}},\quad\widehat{\Phi}^{\alpha}_{t}(\xi)=\widehat{\Phi}^{\alpha}(t\xi)=\widehat{\Phi}_{t}(\xi)\,\langle\xi,e_{\alpha}\rangle\frac{\xi}{|\xi|^{2}},

whence

\hat{v}^{\alpha}_{t}(\xi)=\widehat{\Phi}^{\alpha}_{t}(\xi)\times\hat{v}(\xi)=\widehat{\Phi}_{t}(\xi)\,\langle\xi,e_{\alpha}\rangle\,\frac{\xi\times\hat{v}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{2}}.

Therefore

\left(\sum_{\alpha=1,2,3}\Phi^{\alpha}_{t}\,\vec{*}\,v^{\alpha}_{t}\right)\widehat{}\,(\xi)=\sum_{\alpha=1,2,3}\widehat{\Phi}^{\alpha}_{t}(\xi)\times\hat{v}^{\alpha}_{t}(\xi)=\sum_{\alpha=1,2,3}\widehat{\Phi}_{t}(\xi)^{2}\langle\xi,e_{\alpha}\rangle^{2}\,\frac{\xi\times(\xi\times\hat{v}(\xi))}{|\xi|^{4}}\\
=\widehat{\Phi}_{t}(\xi)^{2}\,\frac{\xi\times(\xi\times\hat{v}(\xi))}{|\xi|^{2}}=-\widehat{\Phi}_{t}(\xi)^{2}\hat{v}(\xi)=-\widehat{\Psi}_{t}(\xi)\hat{v}(\xi)

(we used the equality
\langle\hat{v}(\xi),\xi\rangle=0
, which follows from
{\rm div}v=0
).
Thus we arrive at relation (
15
) for the function
v
.
∎

Lemma 2
.

For
u\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3};{\mathbb{C}}^{3})
, we have

u^{\alpha}_{t}=t(\partial_{\alpha}\Theta)_{t}*{\rm curl}\,u,\quad\alpha=1,2,3,\quad t>0,

(16)

where
(\partial_{\alpha}\Theta)_{t}
is related to
\partial_{\alpha}\Theta
by equality of the form (
10
) with
d=3
.

Proof.

The left hand side of (

) equals

\Phi^{\alpha}_{t}\,\vec{*}\,u=t(\partial(\partial_{\alpha}\Theta)_{t})\,\vec{*}\,u.

So the desired assertion follows immediately from the identity

(\partial f)*g=f*\partial g

for scalar functions, in which
f
should be replaced by
t(\partial_{\alpha}\Theta)_{t}
,
and
g
should be replaced by the components of the vector-function
u
.
∎

In the proof of the following lemma, we will use the representaion of the orthogonal projection
P

in terms of Fourier transform (
v\in L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3};{\mathbb{C}}^{3})
):

\widehat{Pv}(\xi)=\frac{\xi}{|\xi|^{2}}\,\langle\hat{v}(\xi),\xi\rangle.

(17)

Lemma 3
.

For
u\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3};{\mathbb{C}}^{3})
,
f\in G_{0}
, the following equality holds true

\Phi_{t}*I_{u}f=t(\partial\Theta)_{t}*\langle{\rm curl}\,u,f\rangle,\quad t>0.

(18)

Proof.

The scalar function
\langle{\rm curl}\,u,f\rangle
belongs to
L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
,
so equality (
18
) is equivalent to the following equality of functions from
L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3};{\mathbb{C}}^{3})
of the variable
\xi

-t^{2}|\xi|^{2}\widehat{\Theta}(t\xi)(P(u\times f))\,\hat{}\,(\xi)=t^{2}i\xi\,\widehat{\Theta}(t\xi)(\langle{\rm curl}\,u,f\rangle)\,\hat{}\,(\xi).

(19)

In view of (
17
), we have

|\xi|^{2}(P(u\times f))\,\hat{}\,(\xi)=\xi\,\langle(u\times f)\,\hat{}\,(\xi),\xi\rangle.

However, the inner product
\langle(u\times f)\,\hat{}\,(\xi),\xi\rangle

equals Fourier transform of
-i\,{\rm div}(u\times f)
, the latter being an element of
L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
, hence

\langle(u\times f)\,\hat{}\,(\xi),\xi\rangle=-i({\rm div}(u\times f))\,\hat{}\,(\xi)=-i(\langle{\rm curl}\,u,f\rangle)\,\hat{}\,(\xi).

In the second equality we used identity (
6
).
Thus we obtain equality (
19

∎

5
Proof of Theorem
2
in the case of smooth
u

In this section, we prove Theorem
2
under the additional assumption
u\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3};{\mathbb{C}}^{3})
.

Let
Q
be an arbitrary cube in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. Fix
\beta\in\{1,2,3\}
and choose a function
\tilde{f}\in G_{0}
that
is equal to
e_{\beta}
on the cube centered at the origin
with side length

.
Then for the function

f(x)=\tilde{f}\left(\frac{x-c(Q)}{l(Q)}\right)

(
c(Q)
is the center of the cube
Q
), we have

\displaystyle f\big|_{Q^{\prime}}=e_{\beta},

(20)

\displaystyle\|I_{u}f\|_{L_{2}}\leqslant\|I_{u}\|\cdot\|f\|_{L_{2}}\leqslant C\|I_{u}\|\cdot|Q|^{1/2},

(21)

where
Q^{\prime}
is the cube centered at
c(Q)
with side length
(1+\sqrt{3})\,l(Q)
.
Due to (
18
), for
\alpha=1,2,3
,
t>0
, we have

\langle\Phi_{t}*I_{u}f,e_{\alpha}\rangle=t(\partial_{\alpha}\Theta)_{t}*\langle{\rm curl}\,u,f\rangle.

(22)

In the case
t<l(Q)
,
the convolution occurring on the right hand side being restricted on the cube
Q
is determined by
the values of the function
\langle{\rm curl}\,u,f\rangle
on
Q^{\prime}
,
which follows from (
14
).
This can be recorded as follows

\chi_{Q}(t(\partial_{\alpha}\Theta)_{t}*\langle{\rm curl}\,u,f\rangle)=\chi_{Q}(t(\partial_{\alpha}\Theta)_{t}*(\chi_{Q^{\prime}}\langle{\rm curl}\,u,f\rangle))

(here and further
\chi_{E}
is the characteristic function of a set
E
).
According to (
20
), on
Q^{\prime}
we have
\langle{\rm curl}\,u,f\rangle=\langle{\rm curl}\,u,e_{\beta}\rangle
.
Hence the expression we have obtained equals

\chi_{Q}\left(t(\partial_{\alpha}\Theta)_{t}*(\chi_{Q^{\prime}}\langle{\rm curl}\,u,e_{\beta}\rangle)\right)=\chi_{Q}\left(t(\partial_{\alpha}\Theta)_{t}*\langle{\rm curl}\,u,e_{\beta}\rangle\right).

Thus the convolution (
22
) on the cube

equals

t(\partial_{\alpha}\Theta)_{t}*\langle{\rm curl}\,u,e_{\beta}\rangle=\langle{}t(\partial_{\alpha}\Theta)_{t}*{\rm curl}\,u,e_{\beta}\rangle=\langle{}u^{\alpha}_{t},e_{\beta}\rangle

(the last equality follows from (

Therefore

\|\langle{}u^{\alpha}_{t},e_{\beta}\rangle\|_{Q}=\|\langle\Phi_{t}*I_{u}f,e_{\alpha}\rangle\|_{Q}\leqslant|Q|^{-1/2}\|\langle\Phi_{t}*I_{u}f,e_{\alpha}\rangle\|_{L_{2}}\leqslant|Q|^{-1/2}\|\Phi_{t}*I_{u}f\|_{L_{2}}.

Hence

\left(\int_{0}^{l(Q)}\|\langle{}u^{\alpha}_{t},e_{\beta}\rangle\|_{Q}^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}\right)^{1/2}\leqslant C|Q|^{-1/2}\left(\int_{0}^{l(Q)}\|\Phi_{t}*I_{u}f\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}\right)^{1/2}\\
\leqslant C|Q|^{-1/2}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\Phi_{t}*I_{u}f\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}\right)^{1/2}\leqslant C|Q|^{-1/2}\|I_{u}f\|_{L_{2}}\leqslant C\|I_{u}\|.

In the second to last inequality we applied (
11
),
whereas in the last one we used (
21
).
Now varying
\beta=1,2,3
, yields

\left(\int_{0}^{l(Q)}\|u^{\alpha}_{t}\|_{Q}^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}\right)^{1/2}\leqslant C\|I_{u}\|.

(23)

Now we are able to estimate the convolutions
\Psi_{t}*u
.
According to (
15
), we have

\|\Psi_{t}*u\|_{Q}\leqslant\sum_{\alpha=1,2,3}\|\Phi^{\alpha}_{t}\,\vec{*}\,u^{\alpha}_{t}\|_{Q}=|Q|^{-1/2}\sum_{\alpha=1,2,3}\|\chi_{Q}(\Phi^{\alpha}_{t}\,\vec{*}\,u^{\alpha}_{t})\|_{L_{2}}.

For

, we may apply the equality

\chi_{Q}(\Phi^{\alpha}_{t}\,\vec{*}\,u^{\alpha}_{t})=\chi_{Q}(\Phi^{\alpha}_{t}\,\vec{*}\,(\chi_{Q^{\prime}}u^{\alpha}_{t}))

(we have already used a similar argument previously),
and proceed with our calculation

\leqslant C|Q|^{-1/2}\sum_{\alpha=1,2,3}\|\chi_{Q^{\prime}}u^{\alpha}_{t}\|_{L_{2}}\leqslant C\sum_{\alpha=1,2,3}\|u^{\alpha}_{t}\|_{Q^{\prime}}.

Whence

\left(\int_{0}^{l(Q)}\|\Psi_{t}*u\|_{Q}^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}\right)^{1/2}\leqslant C\sum_{\alpha=1,2,3}\left(\int_{0}^{l(Q)}\|u^{\alpha}_{t}\|_{Q^{\prime}}^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}\right)^{1/2}\leqslant C\|I_{u}\|

(the last inequality follows from estimate (
23
) applied to the cube
Q^{\prime}
).

6
Proof of Theorem
2
in the general case

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2
, we may turn from
u
to its smooth approximations
u^{\varepsilon}=\omega^{\varepsilon}*u
,
\varepsilon>0
,
where

\displaystyle\omega\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}),\quad\omega\geqslant 0,\quad\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\omega(x)\,dx=1,\quad\omega^{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{-3}\omega(\varepsilon^{-1}x).

Clearly, the field
u^{\varepsilon}
belongs to
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{3};{\mathbb{C}}^{3})
, satisfies condition (
5
)
and the relation
{\rm div}u^{\varepsilon}=0
.
Besides, we have
\|I_{u^{\varepsilon}}\|\leqslant\|I_{u}\|
.
Indeed, the operator
I_{u^{\varepsilon}}
can be represented as follows

I_{u^{\varepsilon}}f=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\omega^{\varepsilon}(y)I_{u(\cdot-y)}fdy.

Evidently,
\|I_{u(\cdot-y)}\|=\|I_{u}\|
