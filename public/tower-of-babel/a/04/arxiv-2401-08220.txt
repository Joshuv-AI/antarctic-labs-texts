Abstract
In a spoofing attack, a malicious actor impersonates a legitimate user to access or manipulate data without authorization. The vulnerability of cryptographic security mechanisms to compromised user credentials motivates spoofing attack detection in the physical layer, which traditionally relied on channel features, such as the received signal strength (RSS) measured by spatially distributed receivers or access points. However, existing methods cannot effectively cope with the dynamic nature of channels, which change over time as a result of user mobility and other factors. To address this limitation, this work builds upon the intuition that the temporal pattern of changes in RSS features can be used to detect the presence of concurrent transmissions from multiple (possibly changing) locations, which in turn indicates the existence of an attack. Since a localization-based approach would require costly data collection and would suffer from low spatial resolution due to multipath, the proposed algorithm employs a deep neural network to construct a graph embedding of a sequence of RSS features that reflects changes in the propagation conditions. A graph neural network then classifies these embeddings to detect spoofing attacks. The effectiveness and robustness of the proposed scheme are corroborated by experiments with real-data.

Introduction

The prevalence of wireless communications has engendered a
panoply of security threats, including the unauthorized interception of
private data, disruptions to remote services, and user impersonation. Among
these pernicious threats, spoofing attacks pose a particularly troublesome
challenge since, in these attacks, malevolent actors intercept and manipulate
data originally intended for legitimate users
[

,

,

,

]
. The
detection and mitigation of these attacks are pivotal for data security.
Although cryptographic techniques have traditionally been employed across
various communication layers to fortify security, the potential access of
attackers to the credentials of legitimate users introduces a serious
vulnerability. Consequently, the research community has increasingly focused
on detecting spoofing attacks in the physical layer.

For instance,

carrier frequency offset (CFO), in-phase and quadrature (I/Q) offset, and I/Q imbalance, to verify user identity. Regrettably, these methodologies
necessitate knowledge of the communication
protocol and may prove ineffective in the face of environmental
changes, such as fluctuations in temperature

[
6
]
.
These constraints are somehow mitigated
in

[
9
,

,

]
,
which rely on angle of arrival (AoA) and time difference of arrival (TDoA) features,
and in
[
12
]
, where a neural network is
trained using signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) traces. Nonetheless, these
approaches still demand synchronization and/or knowledge of the
communication protocol.
In contrast, techniques reliant on received signal strength (RSS) measurements do not
require knowledge of the communication protocol or signal decoding,
thus significantly augmenting their generality and applicability for
detecting spoofing attempts

[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
.
The predominant approach in this context involves applying
clustering primitives to RSS measurements collected by multiple receivers, such as the access points of a WiFi network

[
13
,
18
,
19
]
. By exploiting
the dependence of RSS signatures on the transmitter
locations, an attack is detected if transmissions with the same user
identifier are found to originate at different locations.
Consequently, this approach results in false alarms when the channel
conditions change, as for example when a legitimate user moves.

To remedy this limitation, the key realization in
this work is that it is possible to tell spoofing from motion and other
effects by analyzing the temporal changes in RSS features. To illustrate this
idea, consider a network that sequentially receives frames from
locations denoted as A, B, C, and D. If all these locations are
distinct, it is natural to ascribe these variations to the movement of the legitimate user.
In contrast, if the received transmissions alternate between points A
and B in a pattern such as A, B, A, B, A, B, etc., it is more likely
that one user is transmitting from location A and another from location
B, which indicates the presence of an attack.
To the best of our knowledge, the work at hand is the first to exploit this kind of information.

To this end, this paper introduces a spoofing
attack detection scheme where a graph embedding is constructed to capture the pattern of changes in RSS features over a sequence of frames. Then, a
graph neural network
(GNN) classifies such graph embeddings as either corresponding to an attack or to legitimate user activity, which may include user movement.
The graph is constructed by utilizing a
position-change detector
(PCD) that determines whether a given pair of frames was transmitted from different locations. Since changes in the RSS measurements corresponding to different frames may be caused either by the movement of the transmitter or by the variability due to the finite number of samples used in the computation of these measurements, the PCD is designed as a deep neural network that detects position changes by implicitly learning the distribution of RSS estimates from signal samples.
The proposed scheme can be readily deployed due to the simplicity of the procedure for collecting the required data set. Specifically, RSS features must be collected at different locations but those locations need not be recorded.

II
Problem Formulation

Let
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
comprise the coordinates of
all points in the spatial region of interest, where both
legitimate users and attackers are located.
A transmitter at
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{X}}
, which can be the
legitimate user or an attacker, sends a signal
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}(t)
, where

t
denotes time. This signal, modeled as an unknown wide-sense
stationary stochastic process, is received by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}N}

receivers, such as the access points or base stations of a wireless network. Let
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},t)
denote the impulse
response of the channel between the transmitter and the
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}
th
receiver, which is assumed to be time-invariant over the duration of a frame. The received signal at the
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}
th receiver
is given by

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},t)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},t)\ast{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}(t)+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}z}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}(t),

(1)

where
\ast
denotes
convolution and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}z}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}(t)
is additive white
Gaussian noise (AWGN) with variance
\sigma^{2}
and
independent of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}(t)
.
Thus, one can define the
received signal strength

(RSS)
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}):={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}}{|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},t)|^{2}},
where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}}
denotes
expectation.

To estimate the RSS of a frame received by the
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}
th receiver, consider a set of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}K}

samples

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{K}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}:=\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},t+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}k}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T})\}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}k}=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}K}}
,
where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T}
is the sampling period and
t
is the time when the frame begins. The RSS can be estimated as

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{f}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}):=\frac{1}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}K}}\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}k}=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}K}}|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},t+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}k}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T})|^{2}.

(2)

If

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}k}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T})
is ergodic for each

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}
, it follows that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{f}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})
converges to

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})
as
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}K}\rightarrow\infty
.

For notational convenience, the
RSS values estimated by all receivers are collected into a
feature vector
, generically represented by

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}):=[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{f}}_{1}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}),\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{f}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}N}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})]^{\top}
.
Note that since
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}K}
is finite,
measuring the RSS
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}I}
times for location
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}
yields
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}I}

different estimates of
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}):=[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}_{1}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}),\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}N}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})]^{\top}
. These estimates will be denoted as

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}^{({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}i}})}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}),{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}i}}=1,\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}I}
.

To introduce the notation for frame sequences,
let
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]}
denote the location of the user that transmits the
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}
th frame at the moment of transmitting that frame and let
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]}:={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]})
.
The feature vectors corresponding to a sequence
of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}J}
frames are collected into matrix
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{F}}}}:=[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[1]},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}J}]}]
.
Out of these

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}J}
frames,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}J}_{L}
belong to the legitimate user and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}J}_{A}
to the
attacker, where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}J}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}J}_{L}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}J}_{A}
.

The
set of indices of the frames belonging to the legitimate user is represented by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{J}_{\text{L}}}\subset\{1,\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}J}_{L}\}

whereas the set of indices of the frames belonging to the attacker is represented by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{J}_{\text{A}}}\subset\{1,\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}J}_{A}\}
.

Given
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{F}}}}
,
the problem is to decide between the following
hypotheses:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{matrix}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}_{0}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{J}_{\text{A}}}=\emptyset\\
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}_{1}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{J}_{\text{A}}}\neq\emptyset.\end{matrix}\right.

(3)

To this end, a dataset comprising the feature vectors

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}:=\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}^{({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}i}})}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}}}),{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}}=1,\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}i}}=1,\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}I}\}
is given, where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M}
is the number of distinct measurement locations, i.e.

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}}}\ \neq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}}^{\prime}}~\forall{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}}\neq{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}}^{\prime}
.

III
Spoofing Detection from RSS Features

Fig. 1
:
RSS measurements along a line vs. their y-coordinate. It is observed that small variations in the y-coordinate often result in larger RSS changes than large variations in the y-coordinate. For example, the difference between
y=24
and
y=29
is around 24 dB, whereas the difference between
y=16
and
y=40
is less than 5 dB.
This is caused mainly by multipath and suggests that accurately estimating the position from RSS measurements is not generally possible.

Evidently, if the time between consecutive frames in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{F}}}}
is too long, then
the vectors
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]}
may
originate at highly distant
locations due to user movement,
even in the absence of attacks. As a result,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]}
may be highly different from
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}-1]}
and

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}+1]}
. Since this would also be the case in the presence of an attack,
solving problem (
3
) becomes challenging as the
distributions of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{F}}}}
under both
hypotheses are highly similar. Therefore, it becomes imperative to introduce the following
assumption:

Assumption 1: The frame rate is high relative
to the speed of the users.

In other words, if
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}+1]}
correspond to the same user, they will be reasonably similar.

In view of Assumption 1, one could consider a strategy
to tackle problem (
3
) where the locations

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]}
are first estimated based on
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]}
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}=1,\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}J}
, and an attack is declared if multiple transmissions are concurrently received from distant (possibly moving) users. However, this approach is not viable first because the data given in the problem formulation of Sec.
II
does not allow a reasonably accurate localization of the transmitters. Indeed,
if the locations
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]}
associated with the vectors in

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}
were given, one could attempt to estimate the locations associated with the frames in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{F}}}}
, for instance via fingerprinting-based localization
[
20
,
21
]
. However, the error of such approaches is typically in the order of 10 m in indoor environments (see e.g.
[
20
]
), which would hinder detecting attacks where the attacker is relatively near the legitimate user. Fig.
1
illustrates why this is the case. Besides, collecting a data set where the positions of the measurement locations need to be recorded is highly costly since it would generally require the deployment of an auxiliary localization system, the use a mobile robot, or to manually measure the spatial coordinates of all measurement locations.

For this reason, the proposed scheme does not attempt to estimate the transmitter locations. Instead, it exploits the pattern of dissimilarities between the feature vectors
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]}
. This is accomplished in two steps: First, each pair of vectors
({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}^{\prime}]})
is compared as described in
Sec.
III-A
. Given these comparisons, a decision is made on the presence of an attack based on a graph embedding, as described in Sec.
III-B
.

III-A

Position-change Detection

This section presents a PCD, which is a detector that determines whether two given frames where transmitted from the same location.
Specifically, given two feature vectors
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]}
and

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}^{\prime}]}
respectively corresponding to (possibly equal) locations

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}^{\prime}]}
,
the goal is to distinguish between the following hypotheses:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{matrix}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}_{0}^{\text{PCD}}}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}^{\prime}]}\\
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\text{PCD}}}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]}\neq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}^{\prime}]}.\end{matrix}\right.

(4)

A PCD is a function that maps a pair of feature vectors to a hypothesis, i.e.,

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d^{\text{PCD}}}:\mathbb{R}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}N}}\times\mathbb{R}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}N}}\rightarrow\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}_{0}^{\text{PCD}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\text{PCD}}}\}
.

To properly address (
4
), it is useful to consider the components behind the dissimilarity between
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]}
and

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}^{\prime}]}
.
First, two
feature vectors
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]}
and

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}^{\prime}]}
are naturally different because
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}K}
is finite,
even when
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}^{\prime}]}
.
Second,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]}
and

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}^{\prime}]}
will be different when
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]}\neq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}^{\prime}]}
because of the different propagation phenomena undergone by the signals propagating from either location to the receivers. This includes effects such as path loss, shadowing, and fading. The latter is caused by multipath and dominates in indoor environments; see for example Fig.
1
.
The complexity of these phenomena calls for a PCD that learns to solve (
4
) in a data-driven fashion. To this end, in this work,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d^{\text{PCD}}}
is implemented using a DNN, as described next.

III-A
1
Architecture

Following standard practice, the detector is designed to decide
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\text{PCD}}}
when a
detection statistic
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T}^{\text{PCD}}:\mathbb{R}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}N}}\times\mathbb{R}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}N}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
exceeds a predefined threshold, and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}_{0}^{\text{PCD}}}
otherwise. In this way, the problem of designing
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d^{\text{PCD}}}
becomes that of designing a function
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T}^{\text{PCD}}
.

In principle, this function could be directly implemented
as a DNN. However, such a simple approach would result in a non-commutative

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T}^{\text{PCD}}
, that is,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T}^{\text{PCD}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}^{\prime})
will generally differ from

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T}^{\text{PCD}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}^{\prime},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}})
, which is clearly undesirable.
To remedy this issue, a symmetrization technique will be adopted.
Specifically,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T}^{\text{PCD}}
will be implemented based on an auxiliary function
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{T}}^{\text{PCD}}
by setting
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T}^{\text{PCD}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}^{\prime}):=({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{T}}^{\text{PCD}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}^{\prime})+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{T}}^{\text{PCD}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}^{\prime},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}))/2
. Observe that this implies that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T}^{\text{PCD}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}^{\prime})={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T}^{\text{PCD}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}^{\prime},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}})
regardless of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{T}}^{\text{PCD}}
. Thereby,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{T}}^{\text{PCD}}
can be safely implemented as a DNN.

The architecture of the subnetwork
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{T}}^{\text{PCD}}
is detailed next. Since
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{T}}^{\text{PCD}}
is concerned with dissimilarities, it is natural to include an
initial non-trainable layer that yields
10\log_{10}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}^{\prime},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}^{\prime}]
when the input to the network is
[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}^{\prime}]
. This facilitates learning from moderate-sized datasets. This layer is followed by three hidden fully-connected layers with
512
neurons and leaky
ReLU activations
[
22
]
. The output
layer contains a single neuron with a linear activation.

III-A
2
Data set

To train
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T}^{\text{PCD}}
, a dataset comprising pairs of vectors from
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}
is constructed. The feature vectors in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}
of these pairs correspond to the same transmitter location. In the remaining
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}
pairs, they correspond to different transmitter locations.

Specifically, the pairs of the first kind are generated for

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}=1,\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}
by first drawing
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}
uniformly at random
from the set
\{1,\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M}\}
. Then,

Recall from Sec.
II
that obtaining
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}
involves collecting the

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}I}
RSS estimates

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}^{({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}i}})}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}}})
,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}i}}=1,\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}I}

for each of the
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M}
locations
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}}},~{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}}=1,\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M}
. A simpler approach may be to collect a single estimate with a large
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}K}
so that it approximately equals

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}}})

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},t)
is approximately Gaussian distributed, which would be the case e.g. if
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}(t)
is an orthogonal frequency division multiplexing (OFDM) signal; see e.g.
[
23
]
.

To this end, express
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},t)

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},t)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},t)
, where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})
is the true RSS and

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},t)
is a circularly symmetric zero-mean Gaussian random variable with
unit variance uncorrelated over
t
. Then, (
2
) becomes

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{f}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})|^{2}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}K}}\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}k}=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}K}}|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},t+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}k}T)|^{2}

(5a)

\displaystyle=\frac{|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})|^{2}}{2{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}K}}\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}k}=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}K}}\left(\left[\sqrt{2}\text{Re}\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},t+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}k}T)\}\right]\right.^{2}

\displaystyle+\left[\sqrt{2}\text{Im}\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},t+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}k}T)\}\right]^{2}\Big).

(5b)

It follows that
2{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}K}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{f}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},t)/|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})|^{2}

is a
\chi^{2}
random variable with
2{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}K}

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}I}
estimates
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{f}}^{({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}i}})}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},t)

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}i}}=1,\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}I}
, can be obtained by generating
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}I}
realizations of such a
\chi^{2}
random variable.

The decisions of the PCD for all pairs of frames will be used next to detect spoofing attacks.
To intuitively understand why this is possible, recall from Sec.
III-A
that the PCD decides
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\text{PCD}}}
when the dissimilarity between the given feature vectors
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]}
and
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}^{\prime}]}
owes to the difference between the propagation phenomena experienced at
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}^{\prime}]}
. In other words, if
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}^{\prime}]}
are so close that both points see similar propagation conditions to all receivers, the PCD decides
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}_{0}^{\text{PCD}}}
.
For didactical purposes, it is useful to split the space into propagation
regions
and assume that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d^{\text{PCD}}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]},\bm{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}^{\prime}]})={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}_{0}^{\text{PCD}}}
if
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}^{\prime}]}
belong to the same region and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d^{\text{PCD}}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]},\bm{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}^{\prime}]})={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\text{PCD}}}
otherwise. Using the decisions of the PCD, one can therefore assign each frame in the given sequence to a region.

This assignment is illustrated in Fig.
2
, which sheds light into why it is possible to solve (
3
) using the decisions of the PCD.
Fig.
2

In turn, Fig.

It is important to note that the above considerations are provided to develop intuition, but in practice do not hold exactly. In particular, the decisions of the PCD will not generally be transitive, that is, it may hold that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d^{\text{PCD}}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]},\bm{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}^{\prime}]})={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}_{0}^{\text{PCD}}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d^{\text{PCD}}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}^{\prime}]},\bm{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}^{\prime\prime}]})={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}_{0}^{\text{PCD}}}
but
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d^{\text{PCD}}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]},\bm{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}^{\prime\prime}]})={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\text{PCD}}}
. However, this may approximately hold.
For this reason, it is useful to construct a graph
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{G}}
where the
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}
-th node corresponds to
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]}
and there is an edge between nodes
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}^{\prime}
if
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d^{\text{PCD}}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}]},\bm{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}^{\prime}]})={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}_{0}^{\text{PCD}}}
. Clearly, since
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d^{\text{PCD}}}
is commutative (cf. Sec.
III-A1
), this graph is undirected.

Clearly, if the space could be split into propagation regions, as discussed earlier, then this graph could be partitioned into one component per region and the presence of an attack would be characterized by an alternating pattern between components as in Fig.

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{G}}
in a data-driven fashion.

III-B
1
Architecture

A GNN
[
24
]
exploits the relation between node features and the graph topology by performing a sequence of message-passing steps or
layers
, where the features associated with a node at layer
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l}
depend on the features of
that node and
the neighboring nodes at layer
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l}-1
.
Specifically, if
\bm{\phi}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nu}}^{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l})}
represents the features of node
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nu}
at layer
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l}
, then

\displaystyle\bm{\phi}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nu}}^{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l})}=G_{1}^{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l})}\left(\bm{\phi}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nu}}^{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l}-1)},\bigoplus_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nu}^{\prime}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{N}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nu}}}G_{2}^{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l})}(\bm{\phi}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nu}}^{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l}-1)},\bm{\phi}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nu}^{\prime}}^{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l}-1)})\right),

(6)

where
G_{1}^{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l})}
and
G_{2}^{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l})}
are conventional DNNs,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{N}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nu}}
contains the set of neighbors of node
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nu}
, and
\bigoplus
is an aggregation operator such as a summation or maximum operator.
The output of the GNN can be computed by another aggregation operator applied to the concatenation of the vectors
\bm{\phi}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nu}}^{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L})}
for all
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nu}
, where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}
is the number of layers.

For the problem at hand, a test statistic is obtained with a GNN and then compared to a threshold to decide between
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}_{0}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}_{1}
. In the adopted architecture,

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}=3
layers and functions
G_{1}^{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l})}
and
G_{2}^{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l})}
are implemented as single-layer fully-connected DNNs with
64
output neurons and ReLU activations. The operator
\bigoplus
is a summation whereas the output of the GNN is obtained by averaging the features of all nodes at the last layer and applying a trainable affine transformation. Since the order of the nodes is relevant (cf. Fig.
2
), the features in the first layer are set so that
\bm{\phi}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nu}}^{(1)}
equals the index of node
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nu}
.

III-B
2
Dataset

To train the GNN, realizations of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{G}}
must be generated under both
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}_{0}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}_{1}

Under
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}_{0}}
, the trajectory
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}(t)
of the (single) user is
generated as follows. First, obtain the time duration of the frame sequence,
given by

, where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}R}_{\text{J}}
is the number of frames per second. The length

Under

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}_{1}[0],\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\bm{f}}}}_{1}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}J}-1]

along
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j}

V
Conclusion

This work considered the problem of detecting spoofing attacks in the physical layer, which is motivated by the vulnerability of cryptographic techniques when the credentials of the legitimate user are compromised. Unfortunately, prior schemes based on RSS measurements raise false alarms in the presence of channel changes or user movement. To remedy this limitation, this work
introduced a deep learning detector robust to these effects. Since localization-based approaches would suffer from a low spatial resolution due to multipath effects, a position change detector based on a deep neural network is used to build a graph embedding of the RSS features. The temporal pattern of changes in the propagation conditions captured by this embedding is then learned by a GNN, which then decides on the presence of a spoofing attack.
