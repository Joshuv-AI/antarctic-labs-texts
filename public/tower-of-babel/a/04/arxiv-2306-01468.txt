Robust Bayesian Inference for Berkson and Classical Measurement Error Models
Charita Dellaporta, Theodoros Damoulas

Abstract
Measurement error occurs when a covariate influencing a response variable is corrupted by noise. This can lead to misleading inference outcomes, particularly in problems where accurately estimating the relationship between covariates and response variables is crucial, such as causal effect estimation. Existing methods for dealing with measurement error often rely on strong assumptions such as knowledge of the error distribution or its variance and availability of replicated measurements of the covariates. We propose a Bayesian Nonparametric Learning framework that is robust to mismeasured covariates, does not require the preceding assumptions, and can incorporate prior beliefs about the error distribution. This approach gives rise to a general framework that is suitable for both Classical and Berkson error models via the appropriate specification of the prior centering measure of a Dirichlet Process (DP). Moreover, it offers flexibility in the choice of loss function depending on the type of regression model. We provide bounds on the generalization error based on the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) loss which allows for generalization to non-Gaussian distributed errors and nonlinear covariate-response relationships. We showcase the effectiveness of the proposed framework versus prior art in real-world problems containing either Berkson or Classical measurement errors.



Keywords:
Errors-in-variables, Input uncertainty, Maximum mean discrepancy, Total least squares, Bayesian nonparametric learning

1
Introduction

An often encountered problem in statistical inference is when one or more covariate variables are subject to measurement error (ME). This is known as the ME, errors-in-variables, or input uncertainty problem and often arises in medical, economic, and natural sciences when it is hard or impossible to measure quantities in the real world exactly.
If ignored, ME in the covariates can result in biased parameter estimates
(
Carroll et al., 1995
, see)
. If the goal is solely to make predictions, ME may not be a significant issue as long as observations continue to be realizations of the same random variable (RV). However, in many cases, we are interested in estimating the true parameter that explains the relationship between two RVs. For example, suppose we want to estimate a parameter that represents the condition of patients based on the prevalence of a specific protein in their blood. This information can help clinicians determine the appropriate dosage of a drug that adjusts the protein levels. However, the available blood test may only provide an approximate measurement of the actual protein percentages. If this ME is ignored, the parameter estimates might give a misleading representation of the effect of the protein levels on the condition, leading to administering incorrect drug doses which could potentially be harmful to patients.

Moreover, in causal inference, a biased estimate can lead to false conclusions about the causal relationship between a covariate and the response variable. This problem has been explored in the causal inference literature by reframing it as a causal effect estimation problem with unobserved confounders
(
Kuroki and Pearl, 2014
;
Adams et al., 2019
;
Finkelstein et al., 2021
, see)
. Such causal effect estimation problems are met in health and epidemiology sciences where we are interested in exposure-response relations
(
Brakenhoff et al., 2018
, see)
. Exposures, often measured with error,
can include both self-reported quantities, such as patient lifestyle changes, and laboratory measurements such as serum cholesterol or the levels of aluminum in the brain
(
Campbell, 2002
)
.

The ME problem has been extensively researched in both frequentist and Bayesian contexts. However, many proposed methods rely on knowing the ME distribution or variance, strong distributional assumptions, or availability of replicate measurements and instrumental variables. Our primary aim is to develop a broadly applicable Bayesian approach, robust to ME on covariates when the model doesn’t explicitly address ME and replicate measurements are unavailable. The focus is to provide informative, well-calibrated posterior densities for parameters of interest while incorporating any available expert prior beliefs on ME, without assuming that these are well-specified. If strong prior beliefs are absent, our method can still be employed by using non-informative priors or reducing the weight on priors. We introduce a Bayesian Nonparametric Learning (NPL) framework suitable for both Classical and Berkson error settings to achieve this.

Numerical optimization Approaches

In the numerical optimization literature, the TLS method
(
Golub and Loan, 1979
;
Golub and Van Loan, 1980
)
has been widely used to approach ME in the covariate. This is a generalization of Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) wherein the residuals of both the response and covariate variables are minimized. The ordinary TLS algorithm has been extended to, among others, the regularised TLS
(
Guo and Renaut, 2002
)
to induce regularisation on the set of admissible solutions and convex TLS
(
Malioutov and Slavov, 2014
)
to deal with varying ME, known error structure and outliers.
Diao et al., 2019
later improved the computational time of solving the TLS problem through fast sketching methods. This group of methods gives very useful computational tools for approaching the ME problem, however, their use in nonlinear regression problems can be challenging while they also make the underlying assumption that the errors are Normally distributed. Moreover, they fail to incorporate any prior beliefs and quantify any uncertainty related to inference outcomes. For this reason, we approach TLS from a probabilistic point of view: we incorporate it as a loss function in the Bayesian NPL framework
(
Lyddon et al., 2018
)
, based on randomized objective functions.

The main idea of TLS is to expand OLS to consider perturbations in both the covariates and response variables. Consider the model described in (
1
). The special case of linear regression corresponds to
g(\theta_{0},x)=\theta_{0}^{T}x
. For observations
w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{x}}
and
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
with associated unobserved errors
\nu=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{x}}
and
\epsilon=(\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
such that
\nu_{1:n}\simiid\F_{N}^{0}
and
\epsilon_{1:n}\simiid\F^{0}_{E}
, we have that:

\displaystyle\forall i=1,\dots,n:\quad y_{i}=\theta^{T}x_{i}+\epsilon_{i},\quad\quad x_{i}=w_{i}+\nu_{i}

(2)

and we wish to correctly estimate
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}\times 1}
. It is straightforward to see that if we naively use OLS to fit
\{(w_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
with the linear model, ignoring ME, the resulting estimator for
\theta
will be biased because each
w_{i}
is correlated with
\nu_{i}
. This bias is not present in the TLS case. Denote by
\|\cdot\|_{F}
the Frobenius norm, then TLS corresponds to the optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\epsilon,\nu,\theta}\left\|[\nu\quad\epsilon]\right\|_{F}^{2}\quad\text{subject to}\quad y=(w+\nu)\theta+\epsilon

or equivalently:

\displaystyle\min_{\nu,\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\nu_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\|y-(w+\nu)\theta\|_{2}^{2}.

(3)

The solution to the problem in (
3
) has been derived for real RVs using the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) by
Golub and Loan, 1979
, and
Golub and Van Loan, 1980
and later generalized by
Van Huffel and Vandewalle, 1988
who relaxed some of the necessary numerical assumptions for the existence of a solution. A detailed solution to the problem is given in Appendix
A.2
. From a statistical point of view, the TLS objective has an underlying assumption of Gaussian distributed errors with (
3
) corresponding to maximizing the joint log-likelihood in the Classical ME case under the assumption that
\F_{N}^{0}
and
\F_{E}^{0}
are both zero mean Gaussian distributions (see Appendix
A.1
). Some finite sample statistical properties of TLS estimators were given by
Xu et al., 2012
. For a more thorough review of TLS methods, we refer the reader to
Van Huffel, 2004
and
Markovsky and Van Huffel, 2007
.

Frequentist Methods

In the statistical literature, most existing methods require knowledge of the distribution of the ME, or its variance
(
Cook and Stefanski, 1994
)
. Specifically, in Deming regression
(
Deming, 1943
)
for Classical ME the ratio of the variances of the errors associated with the response and covariate variables is assumed to be known. For Berkson ME,
Huwang and Huang, 2000
suggested an iterative re-weighted least squares for polynomial regression, assuming Gaussian distributed Berkson error.
Wang, 2003
considered the more general case of nonlinear regression with Gaussian distributed errors and later generalized it to non-Gaussian error models
(
Wang, 2004
)
through a minimum distance estimation approach.

Cook and Stefanski, 1994
proposed the simulation-extrapolation method (SIMEX) which assumes knowledge of the ME variance or the ability to estimate it. Under this information, SIMEX simulates additional ME to estimate its effect on the relationship between covariate and response variables. Although SIMEX has been studied primarily for the Classical error model, some works have extended it to the case of Berkson error models
(
Althubaiti and Donev, 2016
)
and of mixtures of Classical and Berkson errors
(
Carroll et al., 2006
;
Misumi et al., 2018
)
.

Since the ME variance is rarely known in practice, alternative methods assume instead that we have access to repeated measurements of the mismeasured RV. These methods, usually dealing with Classical type error,
(
Liu and Liang, 1992
;
Li and Vuong, 1998
;
Li, 2002
, e.g. see)
treat the true values of the covariates as latent variables and use the replicate measurements to estimate them.
Kato et al., 2021
showed that such techniques achieve robustness against identification failure and confidence bands for the marginal distribution of the true covariate in the Classical ME case. A widely studied method, used in both Classical and Berkson error models, is Regression Calibration
(
Carroll et al., 2006
)
. It is based on a two-step approach, starting with the regression of
X
on
W
and then the substitution of the fitted model into the regression of
Y
on
X
. Although it constitutes a very powerful method, in practice, the first step requires the existence of replicate measurements of the unobserved covariate.

Instrumental variable (IV) methods, for example
Newhouse and McClellan, 1998
;
Bowden and Turkington, 1990
in the Classical setting, relax the above assumption but require access to observations from the so-called IV, which is correlated with the covariate, but not necessarily a replicate of it. The required conditional independence between the IV and the response variable, given the unobserved covariate, overcomes the bias induced by the correlation between the regression error and the covariate in the standard setting. The IV setting has also been generalized to a kernel-based nonparametric estimation of causal effect with Classical ME in the cause, in the presence of unobserved continuous confounding by
Zhu et al., 2022
. In the Berkson error case,
Schennach, 2013
deals with nonparametric regression relying on the availability of an IV. Even in the cases where a candidate RV is available, it is hard to ensure that the necessary IV conditions hold.

In the nonparametric literature, the problem of Classical ME has been tackled through the deconvolution kernel methods
(
Fan and Truong, 1993
;
Wang and Wang, 2011
)
for kernel estimation of nonparametric regression functions. One of the main challenges in this method is the choice of the deconvolving kernel as these do not generally admit a closed form and can be sensitive to the selection of bandwidth.
Lütkenöner, 2015
provides a family of kernels which gives rise to an analytical functional representation of the associated deconvolving kernels when the ME is normally distributed. Later,
Delaigle and Hall, 2016
relaxed certain assumptions and provided a deconvolution approach for unknown, symmetric error distribution. In the same setting,
Kato and Sasaki, 2018
constructed uniform confidence bands under a multiplier-bootstrap method. While the error distribution is assumed to be unknown, the method requires an auxiliary independent sample from the error distribution of the response variable. Although this group of methods has desired theoretical properties, they are usually limited to a particular function class such as sufficiently smooth functions. Nonparametric methods have also been suggested for treating Berkson-type error models.
Delaigle et al., 2006
provided a flexible nonparametric approach based on trigonometric functions. The method assumes some knowledge of the ME distribution or availability of replicate measurements of the error or the covariates.

Bayesian Approaches

In the Bayesian setting, most methods relax the assumptions discussed above but are often reliant on strong parametric assumptions. In the Classical error case, for example,
Berry et al., 2002
restrict the distributions of the covariates, response error and ME to a Gaussian distribution and model the regression function with smoothing splines and penalized mixtures of truncated polynomial splines.
Sarkar et al., 2014
proposed the use of mixtures of B-splines and mixtures induced by Dirichlet Processes (DPs) to target the more general case of heteroscedastic errors. More flexible models based on DPs and Gaussian mixtures have been also proposed by
Müller and Roeder, 1997
;
Carroll et al., 1999
. While these methods overcome the restrictions of simple parametric forms, they are usually computationally expensive to sample from as they rely on MCMC techniques. Recently, neural networks have been used in nonparametric regression with ME
(
Hu et al., 2022
)
to handle more generic regression functions in large-scale scenarios. In this work, the authors employ variational inference and gradient descent to overcome the computational challenges of existing MCMC methods. However, knowledge of the ME distribution type is again required. Finally, in the nonparametric Bayesian literature, Gaussian Processes (GPs) have been employed to tackle ME on the input covariate.
Cervone and Pillai, 2015
adopted GP modeling for location error on the input, based on a Hybrid Monte Carlo algorithm. More recently
Zhou et al., 2023
proposed a fully Bayesian approach based on a GP prior on the regression function coupled with a Dirichlet process Gaussian mixture prior on the distribution of
X
, offering theoretical guarantees on the consistency and convergence of the posterior distribution. Gaussian processes have also been employed in the Berkson error case by
Zhang et al., 2017
who studied tunnel deformation detection. However, to the best of our knowledge, a more general, fully Bayesian treatment of Berkson ME has not yet been offered.

Our framework focuses on Bayesian parametric inference via a nonparametric prior on the unknown conditional distribution of
X\>|\>W
. It can be applied to any parametric model with continuous response, without assuming a well-specified ME distribution a priori. Importantly, it is suitable for both Classical and Berkson error models via a flexible specification of the nonparametric prior. In addition, from a computational point of view, the method gives rise to a parallelizable algorithm and hence overcomes computational challenges related to existing Bayesian methods.

1.2
Contributions and paper outline

We introduce a Bayesian framework for regression problems when the covariate is observed with Berkson or Classical ME. Contrary to existing methods, we do not require replicate measurements of the covariate or instrumental variables and do not assume that the specification of the ME or covariate distributions is correct. Our framework can incorporate prior beliefs about the ME distribution, such as its variance, and the degree of confidence in these. We offer a simply parallelizable algorithm, across bootstrap iterations, for sampling from the desired posterior distribution of the parameters of interest and further provide intuition on the prior specification through theoretical bounds on the generalisation error of our method.

The paper is outlined as follows. Section
2
provides the necessary background on the Bayesian NPL framework. In Section
3
we present the methodology for robust parametric inference in the ME model setting (Robust-MEM) for both Berkson and Classical error models and we suggest two loss functions based on the TLS and the MMD. In Section
4
we theoretically analyze our framework in the generic, nonlinear regression case by providing generalisation error bounds using the MMD-based loss function. Finally, in Section
5
we demonstrate strong empirical results on simulations and mental health and Economics datasets.

2
Bayesian Nonparametric Learning

A flexible way of approaching model misspecification in a Bayesian framework is through the Bayesian NPL setting
(
Lyddon et al., 2018
;
Fong et al., 2019
)
.
By deriving NPL posteriors, rather than standard Bayesian posteriors, we perform Bayesian inference under misspecified error distributions whilst fully characterizing model uncertainty: prior beliefs about
\F_{N}^{0}
are incorporated through a nonparametric prior whereas the model
\P_{g(\theta,\cdot)}
is not assumed to be well-specified as in standard Bayesian inference. The resulting framework provides robustness with respect to the parametric distributional specifications, straightforward incorporation of prior beliefs on the ME distribution, and an efficient parallelizable sampling scheme.

NPL relaxes the standard Bayesian assumption that a parametric model family
\PTheta=\{\Ptheta:\theta\in\Theta\}
can correctly describe the DGP
\P^{0}
, i.e. that
\P^{0}\in\PTheta
.
The approach to incorporating uncertainty in the parameter of interest involves placing uncertainty directly on
\P^{0}
and propagating it to
\theta\in\Theta
. It is accomplished using a conjugate nonparametric prior and associated posterior on
\P^{0}
, which then induces a posterior on the parameter space
\Theta
. This differs from traditional Bayesian inference, which conditions on the parameters rather than the DGP. This framework leads to a parallelizable computational framework to sample from this posterior on
\theta
, called the Posterior Bootstrap.

The DGP is modeled using a DP prior, as proposed by
Lyddon et al., 2018
and
Fong et al., 2019
:
\P\sim\DP(c,\F)
.
Here,
c>0
is a concentration parameter and
\F\in\PX
is a centering measure. Given
(x_{i},y_{i})_{1:n}\simiid\P^{0}
, we can obtain the posterior distribution of
\P
by conjugacy:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\P\>|\>(x_{i},y_{i})_{1:n}\sim\DP\left(c^{\prime},\F^{\prime}\right),\quad c^{\prime}=c+n,\qquad\F^{\prime}=\frac{c}{c+n}\F+\frac{n}{c+n}\mathbb{P}_{n}\end{split}

(4)

where
\mathbb{P}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{(x_{i},y_{i})}
and
\delta_{(x,y)}
is a Dirac measure at
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
. The value of
c
indicates the level of confidence we have in the prior centering measure
\F
.
The NPL framework, in its standard form, is not sufficient to account for ME, since it assumes that we have access to realizations from the DGP. We introduce an approach, suitable for this setting, based on the NPL principles.

3
Methodology

We present a Bayesian framework for ME models (MEMs) by placing a nonparametric prior on the conditional distributions
\{X\>|\>W=w_{i}\}_{1:n}
. This is achieved through a flexible DP distribution, allowing the prior definition for both Berkson and Classical error models. We explore two loss functions within this framework: one based on the TLS objective and the other on the MMD. The latter extends the NPL-MMD framework
(
Dellaporta et al., 2022
)
to the ME context, enabling the handling of non-Gaussian and nonlinear models.

3.1
NPL for measurement error (Robust-MEM)

The standard NPL setting (Section
2
) assumes the availability of a finite number of observations from the DGP and allows for inference on a potentially misspecified model. In the MEM setting, observations from the DGP are not available since we have observations of the pair of random variables
(W,Y)
, instead of
(X,Y)
. In this case, we are interested in setting the uncertainty directly on
X\>|\>W
and propagating this on
\theta
through an NPL posterior.
Recall the observed data
\{(w_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
such that realizations of
X
are observed with some additive, zero mean, and constant variance error, i.e.
w_{i}=x_{i}+\nu_{i}
in the Classical case or
x_{i}=w_{i}+\nu_{i}
in the Berkson case, for all
i=1,\dots,n
. Denote by
\P_{X\>|\>w_{i}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
the distribution of
X\>|\>W=w_{i}
. We set a nonparametric prior on
\P_{X\>|\>w_{i}}
through a DP:

\displaystyle\P_{i}\sim\text{DP}(c,\F_{w_{i}})

(5)

The prior centering measure is indexed by
w_{i}
to indicate the dependence on the observed covariate
w_{i}
.
\F_{w_{i}}
encodes our prior beliefs about the true and noisy covariate relationship through distributional assumptions on the ME and the true covariate while respecting the Berkson or Classical structure of the error. We further discuss the choices of
\F_{w_{i}}
and
c
in Sections
3.2
and
3.3
respectively.
Since we only have one data point
(w_{i},y_{i})
for each conditional
X\>|\>W=w_{i}
, the conjugate posterior DP is:

\displaystyle\P_{i}\>|\>w_{i}\sim\text{DP}\left(c+1,\F_{w_{i}}^{\prime}\right),\quad\F_{w_{i}}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{c+1}\delta_{w_{i}}+\frac{c}{c+1}\F_{w_{i}}.

(6)

Remark 3.1
.

Although we focus on the general case when we only have one data point
(w_{i},y_{i})
for each conditional
X\>|\>W=w_{i}
, in the case of Berkson ME, it is common to assume that there are multiple observations associated with the same observed covariate
w_{i}
but different true covariate value
x_{i}
. For example, this is the case when a fixed dose
w_{i}
is assigned to multiple subjects of a trial
(
Burr, 1988
, see e.g.)
. In this case, the posterior DP conditioned on all the observations from the same set
i
would be defined as
\text{DP}\left(c+n_{j},\F_{w_{i}}^{\prime}\right)
, where
n_{j}
is the number of observations in set
i
and
\F_{w_{i}}^{\prime}=\frac{n_{j}}{c+n_{j}}\delta_{w_{i}}+\frac{c}{c+n_{j}}\F_{w_{i}}.

Let
\P^{0}=\P_{X}\P_{Y\>|\>\cdot}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
be the true joint DGP where
\P_{X}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
denotes the distribution of
X
and
\P_{Y\>|\>x}
denotes the true conditional distribution of
Y\>|\>X=x
for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
. The target for any loss function
l:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\Theta\rightarrow\R
is:

\displaystyle\theta^{0}_{l}=\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\P^{0}}\left[l(x,y,\theta)\right].

(7)

Of course,
\P^{0}
is unknown and we do not have access to observations from
\P_{X}
and consequently
\P^{0}
. We instead have a set of posterior distributions for each of the conditional distributions
X\>|\>W=w_{i}
. The goal is to use the nonparametric posterior of (
6
) to obtain a posterior on
\theta\in\Theta
. This is naturally defined through the push-forward measure
(\theta^{*}_{l})^{\#}\left(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n}\right)
where for probability measures
\P^{1},\dots,\P^{n}
on
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
:

\displaystyle\theta^{*}_{l}(\P^{1},\dots,\P^{n})

\displaystyle:=\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\P^{i}\delta_{y_{i}}}\left[l(x,y,\theta)\right],

(8)

\displaystyle\mu_{i}

\displaystyle:=\DP(c+1,\F_{w_{i}}^{\prime}),\quad\forall i=1,\dots n.

(9)

Sampling can be performed through the principles of the Posterior Bootstrap
(
Fong et al., 2019
)
where at iteration j:

1.

For
i=1,\dots,n:\quad

sample
\P^{(i,j)}\sim\mu_{i}
.

2.

Calculate
\theta^{*}_{l}(\P^{(1,j)},\dots,\P^{(n,j)})=\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\P^{(i,j)}\delta_{y_{i}}}\left[l(x,y,\theta)\right]
.

Exact sampling from a DP, as in step 1, is generally not feasible. The stick-breaking process
(
Sethuraman, 1994
)
offers an exact representation of a DP which can be approximated using the Dirichlet approximation of the stick-breaking process
(
Muliere and Secchi, 1996
;
Ishwaran and Zarepour, 2002
)
which we use in this work. We denote by
\hat{\mu_{i}}
, the probability measure approximating
\mu_{i}
, meaning that
\P^{(i,j)}\sim\hat{\mu_{i}}
is such that:

\displaystyle\P^{(i,j)}=\sum_{k=1}^{T}\xi_{k}^{i}\delta_{\tilde{x}^{i}_{k}}+\xi^{i}_{T+1}\delta_{w_{i}},\quad\tilde{x}^{i}_{1:T}\overset{iid}{\sim}\F_{w_{i}},\quad\xi^{i}_{1:T+1}\sim\Dir\left(\frac{c}{T},\dots,\frac{c}{T},1\right).

(10)

The intuition of this sampling procedure lies in the fact that different marginal distributions of
\P^{(i,j)}
lead to different joint distributions of the form
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\P^{(i,j)}\delta_{y_{i}}
. Uncertainty stems from the marginal distribution of
X
due to ME, hence by bootstrapping over different marginal distributions of
X
we induce robustness to the inference procedure for the joint distribution of
(X,Y)
.

3.3
Prior elicitation

We now focus on the concentration parameter of the DP prior in (
5
). It is clear from the posterior in (
6
) that
c=0
corresponds to the prior belief that there is no ME present on the covariates resulting in all the mass of the posterior centering measure concentrating at
\delta_{w_{i}}
.
This is in contrast to the standard NPL setting (Sec.
2
) where
c=0
corresponds to a non-informative prior. Moreover, it is evident that
c=1
results in splitting the weight of the DP posterior centering measure
\F_{w_{i}}^{\prime}
equally between the prior centering measure
\F_{w_{i}}
and the Dirac measure on the observation
w_{i}
. This choice splits the information between the prior and observation. Lastly, when
c\rightarrow\infty
we see that all the weight is placed in the prior belief.

The interplay between parameter
c
, ME variance and prior specification can be further demonstrated with an example of polynomial regression with Berkson, Normally distributed ME in Figure
3
.
Here, the average MSE over the three parameters of interest is reported for the three limiting choices of
c
, for an increasing ME variance. In the case of a well-specified prior (left), when the ME variance is close to zero, i.e. when there is no ME, all three
c
values perform similarly since the prior correctly indicates no ME, making inference data-driven. Choices of
c>0
perform similarly, yielding lower MSE for non-zero ME variance. Conversely, in the case where the true ME distribution is Gaussian and the prior centering measure
\F_{N}^{0}
is a Student-t distribution with 3 degrees of freedom (right plot),
c=1
corresponds to the lowest average MSE, as expected. A setting of
c=1
appropriately balances prior beliefs and observations, whereas
c=0
wrongly assumes no ME, and
c=100
overly weights the misspecified prior. Overall, overlapping standard deviations in most cases indicate the method’s robustness to
c
values. In the absence of strong prior beliefs on the ME distribution, setting
c=1
splits the weight sensibly between prior beliefs and observations.

3.4
Choice of loss function

In this section, we explore the adaptability of the Robust-MEM framework to various prevalent cases of ME. We propose two distinct loss functions suitable to address these scenarios comprehensively. The initial choice stems from the Total Least Squares objective, offering rapid and robust solutions, particularly tailored for scenarios such as linear regression and Gaussian-distributed ME. To ensure broad applicability across diverse model families and regression relationships, we introduce the MMD-based loss function as a further approach.

3.4.2
Robust-MEM with the Maximum Mean Discrepancy

The use of distance-based loss functions in the NPL framework was suggested by
Dellaporta et al., 2022
to perform robust inference for simulator-based models with the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD). The MMD is defined in a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS)
\mathcal{H}_{k}
with associated kernel
k:\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
. The MMD has already been used in the context of universal robust regression by
Alquier and Gerber, 2024
and is defined through the notion of kernel mean embeddings
For a function
f\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
, the kernel mean embedding
\mu_{\P}\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
with respect to probability measure
\P\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{Z}}
is defined as
\mu_{\P}(\cdot):=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\P}[k(Z,\cdot)]\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
.
The MMD between the probability measures
\P
and
\Q
is defined as
\MMD_{k}(\P,\Q)=\|\mu_{\P}-\mu_{\Q}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
. For a parametric model family
\{\P_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
and a sample
\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\simiid\P^{0}
, the squared MMD can be used as a loss function in (
7
), resulting in a minimum MMD estimator
(
Briol et al., 2019
)
, given by:

\displaystyle\theta^{n}_{\MMD}

\displaystyle:=\arg\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\MMD_{k}^{2}\left(\hat{\P}_{n},\P_{\theta}\right)

\displaystyle=\arg\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\int_{\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}}k(z_{1},z_{2})\P_{\theta}(dz_{1})\P_{\theta}(dz_{2})-\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathcal{Z}}k(z_{i},z_{1})\P_{\theta}(dz_{1})

where
\hat{\P}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{z_{i}}
and
z_{i}:=(x_{i},y_{i})
for each
i=1,\dots,n
.
In practice, this is approximated using a U-statistic and optimized with gradient-based methods. This loss function allows for the specification of nonlinear regression functions
g(\theta,x)
as well as non-Gaussian distributed errors for both
X
and
Y
.

We denote by
\P^{0}
the joint DGP of
X
and
Y
, such that
\P^{0}=\P^{0}_{X}\P^{0}_{Y\>|\>\cdot}

\displaystyle\theta^{0}_{\MMD}

\displaystyle:=\arg\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\MMD_{k}(\P^{0},\P_{X}\P_{g(\theta,\cdot)}).

(14)

In words, the target in (

X
and

The uncertainty here is twofold: firstly

is unknown and secondly, we do not have access to samples from
\P_{X}
, as we only observe realizations

4
Theoretical results for Robust-MEM with the MMD

In this section, we provide generalization error bounds for the Robust-MEM framework with the MMD. We define the target distribution for the Berkson and Classical cases and outline technical assumptions in Section
4.1
. The main results are presented in Section
4.2
. Finally, we analyze the theoretical results under common assumptions in the ME literature, including known error distribution and Gaussian-distributed ME, in Section
4.3
.

4.1
Target distribution & assumptions

The goal is to bound the expected error in terms of the MMD between the true joint distribution of
X,Y
, namely
\P^{0}
, and the estimated model.
The Berkson and Classical error cases lead to different forms of
\P^{0}
, due to the dependence structures of
X
,
N
,
W
.

Recall that in the Berkson error case,
N\cancel{\mathrel{\text{\scalebox{1.07}{$\perp\mkern-10.0mu\perp$}}}}X
but
N\mathrel{\text{\scalebox{1.07}{$\perp\mkern-10.0mu\perp$}}}W
. Since the focus is on the true relationship between
X
and
Y
, we consider the fixed design case by treating the observations
\{w_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
as fixed points. This is a common assumption in the Berkson case as the values are often assigned by an expert
(
Delaigle et al., 2006
, see e.g. the discussion in)
. Recall that by assumption
Y\mathrel{\text{\scalebox{1.07}{$\perp\mkern-10.0mu\perp$}}}W\>|\>X
hence the true joint distribution for any sets
dx,dw\in\sigma_{\mathcal{X}}
and
dy\in\sigma_{\mathcal{Y}}
is:

\displaystyle\P^{0}(W\in dw,X\in dx,Y\in dy)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{w_{i}}(dw)\P^{0}_{X\>|\>w}(dx)\P^{0}_{Y\>|\>x}(dy)

(16)

where
\P^{0}_{X\>|\>w}
and
\P^{0}_{Y\>|\>x}
denote the true conditional distributions of
X\>|\>W=w
and
Y\>|\>X=x
respectively. By marginalizing out
W
we obtain the target joint distribution of
X,Y
which is equal to:

\displaystyle\@add@raggedright\marginpar{\sloppy\raggedright{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\tt Berkson}}\P^{0}(X\in dx,Y\in dy)

\displaystyle:=\P^{0,n}_{X}(dx)\P^{0}_{Y\>|\>x}(dy),

(17)

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad\P_{X}^{0,n}(dx)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\P^{0}_{X\>|\>w_{i}}(dx).

(18)

Further notice that by the assumption of the Berkson error, for each
i=1,\dots n
,
\P^{0}_{X\>|\>w_{i}}
is such that
x_{i}\sim\P^{0}_{X\>|\>w_{i}}
means that
x_{i}=w_{i}+\nu_{i}
and
\nu_{i}\sim\F_{N}^{0}
for the true error distribution
\F_{N}^{0}
or equivalently for any
A\in\sigma_{\mathcal{X}}
we have
\P^{0}_{X\>|\>w_{i}}(A)=\F_{N}^{0}(A_{w_{i}})
where
A_{w_{i}}:=\{\nu:w_{i}+\nu\in A\}
.

In the Classical error case,
X\mathrel{\text{\scalebox{1.07}{$\perp\mkern-10.0mu\perp$}}}N
hence it is not sufficient to define the joint distribution via the conditional distribution of
X\>|\>W
. Instead, we need to go beyond the fixed design case and consider the true marginal distribution of
X
. In this case, the joint distribution of
W,X,Y
is:

\displaystyle\P^{0}(X\in dx,W\in dw,Y\in dy)=\P^{0}_{X}(dx)\P^{0}_{W\>|\>x}(dw)\P^{0}_{Y\>|\>x}(dy).

(19)

whereas the target joint distribution of
X,Y
is simply

\displaystyle\@add@raggedright\marginpar{\sloppy\raggedright{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\tt Classical}}\P^{0}(X\in dx,Y\in dy)=\P^{0}_{X}(dx)\P^{0}_{Y\>|\>x}(dy).

(20)

We can now define the desired generalization error given the target joint and marginal distributions. For random samples
(\P^{i}\sim\mu_{i})_{1:n}
, with
\mu_{i}
as in (
9
) define
\P:=(\P^{1},\dots,\P^{n})
. The estimated model
\P_{\theta^{*}(\P)}
, relative to the noise-free covariates, for each of the Berkson and Classical ME cases is:

\displaystyle\@add@raggedright\marginpar{\sloppy\raggedright{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\tt Berkson}}{}\P^{0}_{\theta^{*}(\P)}(dx\times dy)

\displaystyle:=\P^{0,n}_{X}(dx)\P_{g(\theta^{*}(\P),\cdot)}(dy)\quad\text{or}

(21)

\displaystyle\@add@raggedright\marginpar{\sloppy\raggedright{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\tt Classical}}{}\P^{0}_{\theta^{*}(\P)}(dx\times dy)

\displaystyle:=\P^{0}_{X}(dx)\P_{g(\theta^{*}(\P),\cdot)}(dy)

(22)

respectively. The goal is to examine how well
\P^{0}_{\theta^{*}(\P)}
approximates
\P^{0}
in expectation if we had access to the distribution of the true covariates. That is, we check if the resulting model captures the relationship between the true covariates and the response variable.

In what follows, we provide results for the exact DP case for clarity.
The exact DP is represented through the stick-breaking process
(
Sethuraman, 1994
)
as follows: for each
\P^{i}\sim\mu_{i}:=\DP(c+1,\F_{w_{i}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\P^{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\xi_{k}^{i}\delta_{\tilde{x}_{k}^{i}},\quad\tilde{x}^{i}_{1:\infty}\simiid\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}},\quad\xi^{i}_{1:\infty}\sim\GEM(c+1).

(23)

Using this notation we also define the joint model under samples from the DP posteriors:

\displaystyle\P_{\theta}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\P^{i}\P_{g(\theta,\cdot)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\xi_{k}^{i}\delta_{\tilde{x}_{k}^{i}}\P_{g(\theta,\tilde{x}_{k}^{i})}.

(24)

Recall that
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are two topological spaces, equipped respectively with the Borel

\mathfrak{S}_{\mathcal{X}}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}
, and let
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}

and
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
be the set of probability distributions on
(\mathcal{Z},\mathfrak{S}_{\mathcal{Z}})
.
We let
k:\mathcal{Z}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a reproducing kernel on
\mathcal{Z}
and denote by
\mathcal{H}_{k}
the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) over
\mathcal{Z}
with
k
. We assume throughout this work that the following standard condition on

holds:

Assumption 4.0.1
.

The kernel

is

-measurable and satisfies
|k|\leq 1
. Moreover,
\phi:\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{k}
is such that
k((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))=\left<\phi(x,y),\phi(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\right>_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
.

Next, we let
k_{X}
be a kernel on
\mathcal{X}
,
k_{Y}
be a kernel on
\mathcal{Y}
and we denote by
k_{X}\otimes k_{Y}
the product kernel on

such that

(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Z}
.
With this notation in place, we further assume:

Assumption 4.0.2
.

There is a continuous kernel
k_{X}
on
\mathcal{X}
and a kernel
k_{Y}
on
\mathcal{Y}
such that
|k_{X}|\leq 1
,
|k_{Y}|\leq 1
and
k=k_{X}\otimes k_{Y}
.

As noted in
Alquier and Gerber, 2024
, from
Szabó and Sriperumbudur, 2017
it follows that under Assumption
4.0.2
,
k
is characteristic if
k_{X}
and
k_{Y}
are characteristic, continuous, translation invariant and bounded. When
\mathcal{X}=\R^{d_{x}}
and
\mathcal{Y}=\R^{d_{y}}
examples of such kernels
k_{X}
and
k_{Y}
include the widely-used RBF, exponential kernel and Matérn kernels. Here we restrict our results to the case where
k_{X}
is a translation invariant kernel:

Assumption 4.0.3
.

There exists a bounded, continuous, real-valued, positive definite function
\psi
on
M=\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
such that
k_{X}(x,x^{\prime})=\psi(x-x^{\prime})
.

While this assumption is not strictly necessary for deriving generalization error bounds similar to the ones that follow, it demonstrates the error decomposition relative to the MMD between the true ME distribution and the prior centering measure. This choice also makes intuitive sense for the case of additive ME, as in this work, since the distance between any two points
x+\nu_{1}
and
x+\nu_{2}
is characterized by the distance between
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
.

Lastly, we make an assumption regarding the existence of a bounded conditional mean operator for the model
(\P_{g(\theta,x)})_{x\in\mathcal{X}}
, which was introduced in
Alquier and Gerber, 2024
. Define
\P_{X}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\P^{i}
for
\P^{i}
as in (
23
). The conditional mean embedding operator is used to bound the MMD between
\P_{\theta}:=\P_{X}\P_{g(\theta,\cdot)}
as defined in (
24
) and
{\P^{0}_{\theta}}^{\texttt{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}Classical}}}=\P^{0}_{X}\P_{g(\theta,\cdot)}
or
{\P^{0}_{\theta}}^{\texttt{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}Berkson}}}=\P^{0,n}_{X}\P_{g(\theta,\cdot)}
, in terms of the MMD between
\P_{X}
and
\P_{X}^{0}
or
\P_{X}
and
\P_{X}^{0,n}
respectively. Under Assumptions
4.0.1
and
4.0.2
, the conditional mean embedding operator of a probability measure
\P=\P_{X}\P_{Y\>|\>\cdot}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
is a mapping
\mathcal{C}_{Y\>|\>X}:\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
such that
\mathcal{C}_{Y\>|\>X}k_{X}(x,\cdot)=\mu_{\P_{Y\>|\>X=x}}
where
\mu_{\P_{Y\>|\>X=x}}
is the kernel mean embedding of
\P_{Y\>|\>X=x}
.
Using this definition we make the following assumption:

Assumption 4.0.4
.

For all
\theta\in\Theta
, there exists a bounded linear conditional mean operator
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}:\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
for
(\P_{g(\theta,x)})_{x\in\mathcal{X}}
and
\Lambda:=\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\|\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\|_{o}<\infty
, for
\|\cdot\|_{o}
the operator norm.

A more detailed analysis of kernel mean embeddings can be found in
Klebanov et al., 2020
. We refer the reader to

4.2
Main results

We now bound the generalization error of our method. Proofs for all theoretical results of this section are provided in Appendix
B
. Note that even when the functional relationship
g
is known,
\P_{g(\theta,\cdot)}
might still be misspecified so the expected error is trivially lower bounded by
\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\MMD_{k}(\P^{0},\P_{\theta}^{0})
. Although this type of model misspecification is not directly targeted in this work, we expect to naturally remain robust to such misspecifications due to the robust nature of minimum MMD estimators as studied in
Briol et al., 2019
;
Chérief-Abdellatif and Alquier, 2022
;
Dellaporta et al., 2022
.

Theorem 4.1

(
Berkson
)
.

Let
k^{2}_{X}=k_{X}\otimes k_{X}
and
\P^{0}=\P_{X}^{0,n}\P_{Y\>|\>x}^{0}
. Then under Assumptions 1-4 and
\Lambda
as in Assumption
4.0.4
we have:

\displaystyle\overbrace{\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)_{1:n}\sim\P_{X}^{0}}\left[\E_{\P\sim\mu}\left[\MMD_{k}(\P^{0},\P_{\theta^{\star}(\P)}^{0})\right]\right]-\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\MMD_{k}(\P^{0},\P^{0}_{\theta})}^{\text{Generalisation error}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq\frac{4}{\sqrt{n}}+2(1+\Lambda)\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{c+2}}+\underbrace{\frac{c}{c+1}\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\F_{N},\F_{N}^{0})}_{\text{Prior specification}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{c+1}\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\delta_{0})}_{\text{ME deviation from 0}}\right).

where expectation under
\P:=(\P^{1},\dots,\P^{n})\sim\mu
denotes the expectation under:

\displaystyle\P^{i}\sim\mu_{i},\quad\forall i=1,\dots,n.

Recall that for any set
A\in\sigma_{\mathcal{X}}
,
\P^{0}_{X\>|\>w_{i}}(A)=\F_{N}^{0}(A_{w_{i}})
. As expected, the upper bound of the generalization error depends on the DP prior both through the concentration parameter
c
and the quality of the prior
\F_{N}
with respect to
\F_{N}^{0}
as well as the deviation of the true ME from zero.
Theorem
4.1
highlights an error trade-off based on the selection of
c
and the prior beliefs about
X\>|\>W=w_{i}
, providing a mathematical interpretation of the prior elicitation discussion in Section
3.3
.
The three discussed cases can be seen as follows:

•

c\rightarrow\infty
: this case corresponds to putting all the posterior weight in (
6
) to the prior centering measure
\F_{N}
. As a result, the generalization error is upper bounded by
\frac{4}{\sqrt{n}}+2(1+\Lambda)\MMD_{k_{X}^{2}}(\F_{N},\F_{N}^{0})
, and it depends on the quality of
\F_{N}
.

•

c=0
: the prior belief is that there is no ME since the posterior centering measure is concentrated on the observed data. In this case, the generalization error in Theorem
4.1
is upper bounded by
\frac{4}{\sqrt{n}}+2(1+\Lambda)\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\F_{N},\delta_{0})+\frac{2(1+\Lambda)}{2^{1/2}}
where the second term quantifies the distance of the true ME terms
\nu^{0}_{1:n}
from zero.

•

c=1
: The case of equally splitting the weight between the prior and observations in the nonparametric posterior centering measure. This can be seen in Theorem
4.1
since the two last terms are equally indexed by a constant.

In the Classical error case, we get a similar bound which we present in Theorem
4.2
. Unsurprisingly, the error bound now depends on the quality of the prior centering distribution with respect to
\P_{X}^{0}
.

Theorem 4.2

(
Classical
)
.

Let
k^{2}_{X}=k_{X}\otimes k_{X}
and
\P^{0}=\P_{X}^{0}\P_{Y\>|\>X}^{0}
. Then under Assumptions 1-4 and
\Lambda
as in Assumption
4.0.4
we have:

\displaystyle\overbrace{\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)_{1:n}\sim\P^{0}}\left[\E_{\P\sim\mu}\left[\MMD_{k}(\P^{0},\P_{\theta^{\star}(\P)}^{0})\right]\right]-\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\MMD_{k}(\P^{0},\P^{0}_{\theta})}^{\text{Generalisation error}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq\frac{4}{\sqrt{n}}+2(1+\Lambda)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}(c+1)}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{c+2}}+\underbrace{\frac{c}{c+1}\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0},\F)}_{\text{Prior specification}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{c+1}\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\delta_{0})}_{\text{ME deviation from 0}}\right).

where
\F:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\F_{X\>|\>w_{i}}
and expectation under
\P:=(\P^{1},\dots,\P^{n})\sim\mu
denotes the expectation under:

\displaystyle\P^{i}\sim\mu_{i},\quad\forall i=1,\dots,n.

Similar to the Berkson case, Theorem
4.2
provides clarity on the role of the prior specification. Here,
\F
incorporates prior beliefs about both the marginal distribution of
X
through
\F_{X}
and about the ME distribution through
\F_{W\>|\>X}
. Hence, the quality of the prior will be influenced by both these terms and will depend on how well the resulting measures
\F_{X\>|\>w_{i}}
describe the true distribution
\P_{X}^{0}
.

4.3
Special cases

Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
provide generalization error bounds in the general cases where no assumptions are made regarding the correct specification of the distributions of
X,X\>|\>W
or
N
.

In many real-world cases
(
Berry et al., 2002
;
Fung and Krewski, 1999
, see e.g.)
, the distribution of ME is known or can be estimated. In the Berkson error case, when
\F^{0}_{N}
is known or can be estimated, we can obtain a tighter bound for the generalization error, which vanishes as the data sample size grows large provided that we set
c
large enough.

Corollary 4.2.1

(
Berkson
)
.

Consider the special case when
\F_{N}\equiv\F_{N}^{0}
. Then, if
k^{2}_{X}
is characteristic:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)_{1:n}\sim\P^{0}}\left[\E_{\P\sim\mu}\left[\MMD_{k}(\P^{0},\P_{\theta^{\star}(\P)}^{0})\right]\right]-\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\MMD_{k}(\P^{0},\P^{0}_{\theta})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\leq\frac{4}{\sqrt{n}}+2(1+\Lambda)\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{c+2}}+\frac{1}{c+1}\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\delta_{0})\right).

(25)

In the above Corollary,
k^{2}_{X}
being characteristic is needed to ensure that MMD is a probability metric, i.e.
\MMD_{k^{2}_{\mathcal{X}}}(\P,\Q)=0\iff\P\equiv\Q

(
Sriperumbudur et al., 2010
)
. We note that if
\F_{N}^{0}
is known a priori, then it would make sense to set
c
large so that the nonparametric posterior is centered around the well-specified prior. Taking the limiting case of
c\rightarrow\infty
, we observe that the upper bound of the generalization error approaches
\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}
and hence that the error decreases at a
\sqrt{n}
rate. In the Classical error case, since the target joint distribution involves the marginal distribution of
X
a similar assumption has to be made about the knowledge of
\P_{X}^{0}
.

Corollary 4.2.2

(
Classical
)
.

Consider the special case when
\F\equiv\P_{X}^{0}
. Then, if
k^{2}_{X}
is characteristic:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)_{1:n}\sim\P^{0}}\left[\E_{\P\sim\mu}\left[\MMD_{k}(\P^{0},\P_{\theta^{\star}(\P)}^{0})\right]\right]-\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\MMD_{k}(\P^{0},\P^{0}_{\theta})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\leq\frac{4}{\sqrt{n}}+2(1+\Lambda)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}(c+1)}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{c+2}}+\frac{1}{c+1}\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\delta_{0})\right).

(26)

Another common assumption is that
N
follows a Gaussian distribution
(
Cochran, 1968
;
Spiegelman and Gray, 1991
, see f.ex.)
. While not an assumption in our method, we explore this specific scenario when
k_{X}
is the Gaussian kernel with parameter
l>0
.
This allows for a closed form of the bound in Theorem
4.1
for
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\R^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}
.

Corollary 4.2.3

(
Berkson
)
.

Let
k_{X}
be the RBF kernel,
\F_{N}^{0}=N(0,\sigma_{1}^{2}I)
and
\F_{N}=N(0,\sigma_{2}^{2}I)
. Then:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)_{1:n}\sim\P^{0}}\left[\E_{\P\sim\mu}\left[\MMD_{k}(\P^{0},\P_{\theta^{\star}(\P)}^{0})\right]\right]-\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\MMD_{k}(\P^{0},\P^{0}_{\theta})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq\frac{4}{\sqrt{n}}+2(1+\Lambda)\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{c+2}}+C_{1}\frac{c}{c+1}+\frac{C_{2}}{c+1}\right)

where
C_{1}^{2}:=\left(\frac{l^{2}}{l^{2}+4\sigma_{1}^{2}}\right)^{d_{\mathcal{X}}/2}-2\left(\frac{l^{2}}{l^{2}+2\sigma_{1}^{2}+2\sigma_{2}^{2}}\right)^{d_{\mathcal{X}}/2}+\left(\frac{l^{2}}{l^{2}+4\sigma_{2}^{2}}\right)^{d_{\mathcal{X}}/2}
and
C_{2}^{2}:=\left(\frac{l^{2}}{l^{2}+4\sigma^{2}_{1}}\right)^{\frac{d_{\mathcal{X}}}{2}}-2\left(\frac{l^{2}}{l^{2}+2\sigma^{2}_{1}}\right)^{\frac{d_{\mathcal{X}}}{2}}+1
.

As expected, in the absence of ME, that is when
\sigma_{1}\approx 0
, we have
C_{2}\approx 0
. Similarly, we see that the closer
\sigma_{1}
is to
\sigma_{2}
, the smaller
C_{1}
is. This occurs because
\F_{N}
is closer to the true ME distribution
\F_{N}^{0}
. Under the further assumption that the ME variance is known, i.e.
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}
, as made by various existing methods (see Section
1
), we have
C_{1}=0
.

5.2
Berkson and Classical Measurement Error Studies

ME is especially important in health and social sciences applications in which contamination of the covariate observations can significantly bias the quantification of causal effects and consequently misguide decision-making. In this section, we explore two real-world datasets, one for each case of ME.

California Test Scores

†
†
margin:

Berkson

We first study the
CASchools

AER

Some of the features include school characteristics such as number of students, teachers and income as well as data on students’ grades in topics such as Mathematics and Reading. We employ a polynomial regression model to study the effect of school’s income on students’ test scores, as studied in
Hanck et al., 2021
, by considering a single outcome variable ’Score’ to be the average score between Maths and Reading. To introduce Berkson ME in the dataset we consider the case of group averages which is a common and natural way that Berkson ME arises in real world problems. This is the case when the individual measurements of a covariate are missing but we have data on the group level. People often assign to each individual the group value depending on the group they belong to and then proceed to perform inference ignoring the fact that this produces Berkson ME. To mimic this, we divide the income values range into 15 levels and we assign the within-group average income to all schools of the same income level. This creates Berkson ME in the covariates as each school in level income
i\in\{1,\dots,15\}

which means that

\displaystyle Y=a+bx+cx^{2}+\epsilon,\quad\epsilon\sim N(0,\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}).

6
Conclusion

We introduce a Bayesian NPL framework that is robust to Berkson or Classical ME and misspecified priors.
Empirical findings demonstrate the robustness to ME and improved estimation accuracy of the Robust-MEM (TLS) method compared to prior art. We further instantiate our framework with the MMD-based loss enabling robust inference for nonlinear and non-Gaussian models, offering theoretical guarantees by bounding the generalization error of the Robust-MEM (MMD) method for both Berkson and Classical error models.

The proposed framework is not limited to specific parametric assumptions or availability of replicate measurements but it is flexible to incorporate such information, if it becomes available. For example, replicate measurements could be incorporated into the prior centering measures through an empirical measure. This work opens up the possibility to explore further specialized loss functions suitable for different data spaces, such as discrete, and allows the possibility to handle heteroscedastic and systematic ME scenarios via alternative nonparametric prior structures.
Future work could also focus on the study of distributional properties of posterior NPL estimators, for example through extending the results of loss-likelihood bootstrap methods
(
Lyddon et al., 2019
)
. Such results would further impact other related works based on the NPL framework.

Appendix A
Further details on the TLS problem

A.1
Connection to Gaussian distributed errors

We describe how the TLS objective function is equivalent to maximising the joint log-likelihood in the Gaussian distributed errors case. For simplicity we are going to present the univariate Gaussian case, but the multivariate case follows easily. Consider a linear MEM model with Gaussian noise:

\displaystyle y_{i}

\displaystyle=\alpha x_{i}+\epsilon_{i},\quad\epsilon_{i}\sim N(0,\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2})

(27)

\displaystyle w_{i}

\displaystyle=x_{i}+\nu_{i},\quad\nu_{i}\sim N(0,\sigma_{\nu}^{2}).

(28)

Let
Y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\epsilon=(\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\nu=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then it follows that

\displaystyle Y\>|\>\alpha,x,\sigma_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\sim N(Y|\alpha x,\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}I)

\displaystyle w\>|\>x,\sigma_{\nu}

\displaystyle\sim N(w|x,\sigma^{2}_{\nu}I)

hence under the assumption that
W\mathrel{\text{\scalebox{1.07}{$\perp\mkern-10.0mu\perp$}}}{Y}\>|\>X
the joint negative log-likelihood is

\displaystyle-l(w,Y\>|\>\alpha,\sigma_{\epsilon},\sigma_{\nu},x)=

\displaystyle n\log(\sigma_{\nu})+n\log(\sigma_{\epsilon})+n\log(2\pi)+\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}_{\nu}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(w_{i}-x_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-\alpha x_{i})^{2}.

(29)

Substituting (
27
) and (
28
) above we see that minimising the negative joint log-likelihood above is equivalent to minimising the objective

\displaystyle n\log(\sigma_{\nu})+n\log(\sigma_{\epsilon})+n\log(2\pi)+\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}_{\nu}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-\alpha(x_{i}+\nu_{i}))^{2}

(30)

So for parameters of interest
\theta=(\alpha,\nu)
then the MLE estimator would be

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}=\argmin_{\nu,\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\nu_{i}^{2}+(y_{i}-\alpha(x_{i}+\nu_{i}))^{2}\right]

which is precisely the TLS objective. If we don’t have any extra information on
\nu
, this is generally a non-identifiable problem hence TLS uses numerical optimization techniques to overcome this.

A.2
Solution of the TLS problem

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{x}}
denote the matrix with
w_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
as its i’th row and
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{y}}
be the matrix with
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
in the i’th row. Further denote by
D\in\R^{n\times(d_{x}+d_{y})}
the data matrix
D:=[x\quad Y]
. Let
D=u\Sigma V^{T}
be the SVD of
D
where
\Sigma=\text{diag}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{d_{x}+d_{y}})
with
\sigma_{1}\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{d_{x}+d_{y}}
the singular values of
D
. Moreover denote by
V_{22}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}\times d_{y}}
the bottom right block of
V
. Then the TLS optimization problem in (
3
) has a unique solution if and only if
V_{22}
is non-singular and
\sigma_{d_{x}}\neq\sigma_{d_{x}+1}
. The solution is given by
A=-V_{12}V_{22}^{-1}
where
V_{12}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}\times d_{x}}
is the top right partition of

. A full derivation of the solution can be found at
Golub and Van Loan, 1980
.

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{(c+3)(c+2)}\frac{1}{1-\frac{c+1}{c+3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{c+2}

where in the third equality we used the second moment of the GEM distribution namely
\E_{\xi_{1:\infty}\sim\GEM(c+1)}[\xi_{j}^{2}]=\frac{2(c+1)^{j-1}}{(c+3)^{j}(c+2)}
. By Jensen’s inequality we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\P\sim\mu}[\MMD_{k_{X}}(\P_{X},\F^{\prime})]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\P\sim\mu}[\MMD^{2}_{k_{X}}(\P_{X},\F^{\prime})]}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{c+2}}.

(35)

We now bound the second term in (
31
). By definition of
\F^{\prime}
it follows that

\F^{\prime}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\frac{c}{c+1}\F_{w_{i}}+\frac{1}{c+1}\delta_{w_{i}}\right]=\frac{c}{c+1}\F+\frac{1}{c+1}\P_{W}^{n}

where
\F:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\F_{w_{i}}
and
\P_{W}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}\delta_{w_{i}}
is the empirical measure of the observed, noisy covariates. By linearity of expectations it follows that
\E_{\F^{\prime}}[\cdot]=\frac{c}{c+1}\E_{\F}[\cdot]+\frac{1}{c+1}\E_{\P_{W}^{n}}[\cdot]
and the kernel mean embedding of
\F^{\prime}
is

\mu_{\F^{\prime}}:=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\F^{\prime}}[k(z,\cdot)]=\frac{c}{c+1}\E_{z\sim\F}[k(z,\cdot)]+\frac{1}{c+1}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\P_{W}^{n}}[k(z,\cdot)]=\frac{c}{c+1}\mu_{\F}+\frac{1}{c+1}\mu_{\P_{W}^{n}}.

Using this we can first bound the objective as:

\displaystyle\MMD_{k_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0,n},\F^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\left\|\mu_{\P_{X}^{0,n}}-\frac{c}{c+1}\mu_{\F}-\frac{1}{c+1}\mu_{\P_{W}^{n}}\right\|_{\HkX}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c}{c+1}\|\mu_{\P_{X}^{0,n}}-\mu_{\F}\|_{\HkX}+\frac{1}{c+1}\|\mu_{\P_{X}^{0,n}}-\mu_{\P_{W}^{n}}\|_{\HkX}

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{c+1}\MMD_{k_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0,n},\F)+\frac{1}{c+1}\MMD_{k_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0,n},\P_{W}^{n})

(36)

where we used the triangle inequality for both inequalities.
Let
\phi_{X}
be the feature map associated with the kernel function
k_{X}
. Then,

\displaystyle\MMD_{k_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0,n},\F)

\displaystyle=\|\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\P_{X}^{0,n}}[\phi_{X}(X)]-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\F}[\phi_{X}(X)]\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{X}}}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\P^{0}_{X\>|\>w_{i}}}[\phi_{X}(X)]-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\F_{w_{i}}}[\phi_{X}(X)]\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{X}}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\P^{0}_{X\>|\>w_{i}}}[\phi_{X}(X)]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\F_{w_{i}}}[\phi_{X}(X)]\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{X}}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\nu\sim\F_{N}^{0}}[\phi_{X}(w_{i}+\nu)]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\nu\sim\F_{N}}[\phi_{X}(w_{i}+\nu)]\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{X}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbb{E}_{\nu\sim\F_{N}^{0}}[\phi_{X}(w_{i}+\nu)]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu\sim\F_{N}}[\phi_{X}(w_{i}+\nu)]\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{X}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\MMD_{k_{X}^{i}}(\F_{N}^{0},\F_{N})

where for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
k_{X}^{i}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})=k_{X}(\nu+w_{i},\nu^{\prime}+w_{i})
and
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}
denote the observations. By assumption
4.0.3
,
k_{X}
is translation invariant so for any
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

k_{X}^{i}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})=k_{X}(\nu+w_{i},\nu^{\prime}+w_{i})=\psi(\nu+w_{i}-\nu^{\prime}-w_{i})=\psi(\nu-\nu^{\prime})=k_{X}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})

hence

\displaystyle\MMD^{2}_{k_{X}^{i}}(\F_{N}^{0},\F_{N})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu\sim\F_{N}^{0},\nu^{\prime}\sim\F_{N}^{0}}[k_{X}^{i}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})]-2\mathbb{E}_{\nu\sim\F_{N}^{0},\nu^{\prime}\sim\F_{N}}[k_{X}^{i}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})]+\mathbb{E}_{\nu\sim\F_{N},\nu^{\prime}\sim\F_{N}}[k_{X}^{i}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu\sim\F_{N}^{0},\nu^{\prime}\sim\F_{N}^{0}}[k_{X}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})]-2\mathbb{E}_{\nu\sim\F_{N}^{0},\nu^{\prime}\sim\F_{N}}[k_{X}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})]+\mathbb{E}_{\nu\sim\F_{N},\nu^{\prime}\sim\F_{N}}[k_{X}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=\MMD^{2}_{k_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\F_{N})

and
\MMD_{k_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0},\F)\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\MMD_{k_{X}^{i}}(\F_{N}^{0},\F_{N})=\MMD_{k_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\F_{N})
.
Similarly,

\displaystyle\MMD_{k_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0,n},\P_{W}^{n})

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\P^{0}_{X\>|\>w_{i}}}[\phi_{X}(x)]-\phi_{X}(w_{i})\right]\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{X}}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\nu\sim\F_{N}^{0}}[\phi_{X}(w_{i}+\nu)]-\phi_{X}(w_{i})\right]\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{X}}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\nu\sim\F_{N}^{0}}[k^{i}_{X}(\nu,\cdot)]-k^{i}_{X}(0,\cdot)\right]\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{X}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\nu\sim\F_{N}^{0}}[k_{X}^{i}(\nu,\cdot)]-k_{X}^{i}(0,\cdot)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{X}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\MMD_{k^{i}_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\delta_{0})

\displaystyle=\MMD_{k_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\delta_{0}).

Substituting in (
36
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x_{1:n}\sim\P_{X}^{0,n}}[\MMD_{k_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0,n},\F^{\prime})]\leq\frac{c}{c+1}\MMD_{k_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\F_{N})+\frac{1}{c+1}\MMD_{k_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\delta_{0}).

(37)

Finally, using (
35
) and (
37
) in (
31
) we obtain the required result:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x_{1:n}\sim\P_{X}^{0,n}}[\E_{\P\sim\mu}[\MMD_{k_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0,n},\P_{X})]]\leq

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\frac{1}{\sqrt{c+2}}+\frac{c}{c+1}\MMD_{k_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\F_{N})+\frac{1}{c+1}\MMD_{k_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\delta_{0})

∎

Alquier and Gerber, 2024
:

Lemma B.3

(
Alquier and Gerber, 2024
, Lemma 2)
.

For
\P_{X}^{\prime},\P_{X}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
let
P^{\prime}=P^{\prime}_{X}P_{Y\>|\>\cdot}
and
\P^{\prime\prime}=\P^{\prime\prime}_{X}\P_{Y\>|\>\cdot}
. Assume that
(\P_{Y\>|\>x})_{x\in\mathcal{X}}
admits a bounded linear conditional mean embedding operator
\mathcal{C}_{Y\>|\>X}
. Then

\displaystyle\MMD_{k}(\P^{\prime},\P^{\prime\prime})\leq\|\mathcal{C}_{Y\>|\>X}\|_{o}\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\P^{\prime}_{X},\P^{\prime\prime}_{X}).

where
k^{2}_{X}=k_{X}\otimes k_{X}
.

Lemma B.4
.

(
Berkson
)
Let
\mathbb{P}^{0,n}:=\P_{X}^{n}\P_{Y}^{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{(x_{i},y_{i})}
and
\mathbb{P}_{XY}=\P_{X}\P_{Y}^{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\xi_{j}^{i}\delta_{(z_{j},y_{i})}
where
z^{i}_{1:\infty}\overset{iid}{\sim}\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}},\xi^{i}_{1:\infty}\sim\GEM(c+1)
. Then under assumptions 1-3:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)_{1:n}\sim\P^{0}}[\E_{\P\sim\mu}[\MMD_{k}(\mathbb{P}^{0,n},\mathbb{P}_{XY})]]

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{c+2}}+\frac{c}{c+1}\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\F_{N})+\frac{1}{c+1}\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\delta_{0})

Proof.

Since
\P_{Y}^{n}
does not depend on
X
, it admits the trivial conditional mean embedding operator
\mathcal{C}_{Y}:\HkX\rightarrow\HkY
defined by
\mathcal{C}_{Y}f=\mu_{\P^{n}_{Y}}
for any
f\in\HkX
. Hence, for any
f\in\HkX
, and using Assumption
4.0.2
, we have:

\sup_{y_{1:n}}\|\mu_{\P^{n}_{Y}}\|^{2}_{\HkY}=\sup_{y_{1:n}}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{Y}(y_{i},\cdot)\right\|^{2}_{\HkY}=\sup_{y_{1:n}}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{Y}(y_{i},y_{i})+\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}k_{Y}(y_{i},y_{j})\right)=1

hence
\sup_{y_{1:n}}\|\mu_{\P^{n}_{Y}}\|_{\HkY}\leq 1
and
\|\mathcal{C}_{Y}\|_{o}\leq 1
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)_{1:n}\sim\P^{0}}[\E_{\P\sim\mu}[\MMD_{k}(\mathbb{P}^{0,n},\mathbb{P}_{XY})]]

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{C}_{Y}\|_{o}\mathbb{E}_{x_{1:n}\sim\P_{X}^{0,n}}[\E_{\P\sim\mu}[\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\P_{X}^{n},\P_{X})]]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{x_{1:n}\sim\P_{X}^{0,n}}[\E_{\P\sim\mu}[\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\P_{X}^{n},\P_{X})]]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{x_{1:n}\sim\P_{X}^{0,n}}[\E_{\P\sim\mu}[\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0,n},\P_{X})+\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0,n},\P_{X}^{n})]]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{x_{1:n}\sim\P_{X}^{0,n}}[\E_{\P\sim\mu}[\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0,n},\P_{X})]]+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{c+2}}+\frac{c}{c+1}\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\F_{N})

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{c+1}\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\delta_{0})

where we used Lemma
B.3
for the first inequality, the triangle inequality for the third inequality and Lemma
B.2
with
k_{X}^{2}
for the last inequality. The fact that
k_{X}^{2}
satisfies the required conditions of Lemma
B.2
follows from Assumptions
4.0.2
and
4.0.3
. In particular, if
|k_{X}|\leq 1
then
|k^{2}_{X}|\leq 1
and if
k_{X}
is translation invariant then
k^{2}_{X}(x,x^{\prime})=\psi(x-x^{\prime})\psi(x-x^{\prime})\forall x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
and
k^{2}_{X}
is also translation invariant. Finally, for the fourth inequality, we used Lemma
B.1
with
S_{i}=(x_{i})\sim Q_{i}:=\P^{0}_{X\>|\>w_{i}}
.
∎

Lemma B.5
.

(
Berkson
)
Let
\P^{0}_{\theta}:=\P_{X}^{0,n}\P_{g(\theta,\cdot)}
and
\P_{\theta}:=\P_{X}\P_{g(\theta,\cdot)}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\leq\Lambda\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{c+2}}+\frac{c}{c+1}\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\F_{N})+\frac{1}{c+1}\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\delta_{0})\right)

Using Lemma

and Assumption 4 we have that for any

:

B.1.3
Proof of Corollary
4.2.3
(
Berkson
)

Proof.

First we have that for any
x,x^{\prime}\in\R^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}
,

k^{2}_{X}(x,x^{\prime})=\left(\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2l^{2}}\|x-x^{\prime}\|^{2}\right\}\right)^{2}=\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{l^{2}}\|x-x^{\prime}\|^{2}\right\}

By definition of the MMD,

\displaystyle\MMD^{2}_{k^{2}_{X}}(\F_{N},\F_{N}^{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{\R^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}}\int_{\R^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}}k^{2}_{X}(x,x^{\prime})d\F_{N}^{0}d\F_{N}^{0}-2\int_{\R^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}}\int_{\R^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}}k^{2}_{X}(x,x^{\prime})d\F_{N}^{0}d\F_{N}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\int_{\R^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}}\int_{\R^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}}k^{2}_{X}(x,x^{\prime})d\F_{N}d\F_{N}

(39)

Let
l^{\prime}=\frac{l}{\sqrt{2}}
. Following the argument provided in Section 3.1 of the supplementary material in
Rustamov, 2021

and the definition of the Gaussian kernel and Gaussian density function, the second term above is:

\displaystyle\int_{\R^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}}\int_{\R^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}}k^{2}_{X}(x,x^{\prime})d\F_{N}^{0}d\F_{N}

\displaystyle=\int_{\R^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}}\int_{\R^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{l^{2}}\|x-x^{\prime}\|^{2}\right\}(2\pi\sigma_{1}^{2})^{-d_{\mathcal{X}}/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{1}^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad(2\pi\sigma_{2}^{2})^{-d_{\mathcal{X}}/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{2}^{2}}\|x^{\prime}\|^{2}\right\}dxdx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\R^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}}\int_{\R^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2l^{\prime 2}}\|x-x^{\prime}\|^{2}\right\}(2\pi\sigma_{1}^{2})^{-d_{\mathcal{X}}/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{1}^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad(2\pi\sigma_{2}^{2})^{-d_{\mathcal{X}}/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{2}^{2}}\|x^{\prime}-w\|^{2}\right\}dxdx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(2\pi l^{\prime 2})^{d_{\mathcal{X}}/2}\int_{\R^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}}\left(\int_{\R^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}}(2\pi l^{\prime 2})^{-d_{\mathcal{X}}/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2l^{\prime 2}}\|x-x^{\prime}\|^{2}\right\}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad(2\pi\sigma_{1}^{2})^{-d_{\mathcal{X}}/2}\exp\left\{\left.-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{1}^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\right\}dx\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad(2\pi\sigma_{2}^{2})^{-d_{\mathcal{X}}/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{2}^{2}}\|x^{\prime}-w\|^{2}\right\}dx^{\prime}

where in the second equality we rewrote
\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{2}^{2}}\|x^{\prime}\|^{2}\right\}=\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{2}^{2}}\|x^{\prime}-w\|^{2}\right\}
which holds true in the case
w=0
. The inner integral is the density function of the sum of two Gaussian RVs with mean
0
and variances
l^{2}
and
\sigma_{1}^{2}
respectively. We will denote the sum of these RVs
Z=X+Y
where
X\sim N(0,l^{2}I)
and
Y\sim N(0,\sigma_{1}^{2}I)
such that the inner integral corresponds to the probability density function of
Z
. Hence the above becomes

\displaystyle\int_{\R^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}}\int_{\R^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}}k^{2}_{X}(x,x^{\prime})d\F_{N}^{0}d\F_{N}

\displaystyle=(2\pi l^{\prime 2})^{d_{\mathcal{X}}/2}\int_{\R^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}}f_{Z}(x^{\prime})(2\pi\sigma_{2}^{2})^{-d_{\mathcal{X}}/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{2}^{2}}\|x^{\prime}-w\|^{2}\right\}dx^{\prime}

The remaining integral corresponds to the probability density function of the sum of
W=Z+U
where
Z=X+Y
as above and
U\sim N(0,\sigma_{2}^{2}I)
. Hence, the double integral is equal to the probability density function of
W=X+Y+U\sim N(0,(l^{\prime 2}+\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2})I)
at
w=0
and

\displaystyle\int_{\R^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}}\int_{\R^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}}k^{2}_{X}(x,x^{\prime})d\F_{N}^{0}d\F_{N}

\displaystyle=(2\pi l^{\prime 2})^{d_{\mathcal{X}}/2}\int_{\R^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}}f_{Z}(x^{\prime})(2\pi\sigma_{2}^{2})^{-d_{\mathcal{X}}/2}exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{2}^{2}}\|x^{\prime}-w\|^{2}\right\}dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(2\pi l^{\prime 2})^{d_{\mathcal{X}}/2}(2\pi(l^{\prime 2}+\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}))^{-d_{\mathcal{X}}/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2(l^{\prime 2}+\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2})}\|0\|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{l^{\prime 2}}{l^{\prime 2}+\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}\right)^{d_{\mathcal{X}}/2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{l^{2}}{l^{2}+2\sigma_{1}^{2}+2\sigma_{2}^{2}}\right)^{d_{\mathcal{X}}/2}.

We can use the same argument to derive the other two terms in (
B.1.3
) to obtain

\displaystyle\MMD^{2}_{k^{2}_{X}}(\F_{N},\F_{N}^{0})=\left(\frac{l^{2}}{l^{2}+4\sigma_{1}^{2}}\right)^{d_{\mathcal{X}}/2}-2\left(\frac{l^{2}}{l^{2}+2\sigma_{1}^{2}+2\sigma_{2}^{2}}\right)^{d_{\mathcal{X}}/2}+\left(\frac{l^{2}}{l^{2}+4\sigma_{2}^{2}}\right)^{d_{\mathcal{X}}/2}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\MMD^{2}_{k^{2}_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\delta_{0})

\displaystyle=\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{0}_{1:n}\simiid\F_{N}^{0}}[k_{X}^{2}(\nu,\cdot)]-k_{X}^{2}(0,\cdot)\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{X}^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\E_{\nu\sim\F_{N}^{0},\nu^{\prime}\sim\F_{N}^{0}}[k_{X}^{2}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})]-2\E_{\nu\sim\F_{N}^{0}}[k_{X}^{2}(\nu,0)]+k_{X}^{2}(0,0)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{l^{2}}{l^{2}+4\sigma^{2}_{1}}\right)^{\frac{d_{\mathcal{X}}}{2}}-2\left(\frac{l^{2}}{l^{2}+2\sigma^{2}_{1}}\right)^{\frac{d_{\mathcal{X}}}{2}}+1

By Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{0}_{1:n}\simiid\F_{N}^{0}}\left[\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\delta_{0})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{0}_{1:n}\simiid\F_{N}^{0}}\left[\MMD^{2}_{k^{2}_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\delta_{0})\right]}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(\frac{l^{2}}{l^{2}+4\sigma^{2}_{1}}\right)^{\frac{d_{\mathcal{X}}}{2}}-2\left(\frac{l^{2}}{l^{2}+2\sigma^{2}_{1}}\right)^{\frac{d_{\mathcal{X}}}{2}}+1\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

So by Theorem
4.1
it follows that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)_{1:n}\sim\P^{0}}\left[\E_{\P\sim\mu}\left[\MMD_{k}(\P^{0},\P_{\theta^{\star}(\P)}^{0})\right]\right]-\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\MMD_{k}(\P^{0},\P^{0}_{\theta})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq\frac{4}{\sqrt{n}}+2(1+\Lambda)\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{c+2}}+C_{1}\frac{c}{c+1}+\frac{C_{2}}{c+1}\right)

where
C_{1}^{2}:=\left(\frac{l^{2}}{l^{2}+4\sigma_{1}^{2}}\right)^{d_{\mathcal{X}}/2}-2\left(\frac{l^{2}}{l^{2}+2\sigma_{1}^{2}+2\sigma_{2}^{2}}\right)^{d_{\mathcal{X}}/2}+\left(\frac{l^{2}}{l^{2}+4\sigma_{2}^{2}}\right)^{d_{\mathcal{X}}/2}
and
C_{2}^{2}:=\left(\frac{l^{2}}{l^{2}+4\sigma^{2}_{1}}\right)^{\frac{d_{\mathcal{X}}}{2}}-2\left(\frac{l^{2}}{l^{2}+2\sigma^{2}_{1}}\right)^{\frac{d_{\mathcal{X}}}{2}}+1
.
∎

B.2
Proofs of theoretical results for the
Classical
error model

B.2.1
Proof of Theorem
4.2
(
Classical
)

We first prove a lemma bounding the expected MMD between the true empirical measure of the unobserved
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, namely
\P_{X}^{n}
and the empirical sample from the exact DPs
\P_{X}
in the classical error case.

Lemma B.6
.

(
Classical
)
For
i=1,\dots,n
let
\mathbb{P}^{i}
be a sample from the exact Dirichlet process
\mu_{i}=\DP(c+1,\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}})
and
\P_{X}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\P^{i}
.
Then for any kernel
k_{X}
on
\mathcal{X}
satisfying Assumptions
4.0.2
and
4.0.3
the following statement holds:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x_{1:n}\sim\P_{X}^{0}}[\E_{\P\sim\mu}[\MMD_{k_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0},\P_{X})]]\leq

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}(c+1)}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{c+2}}+\frac{c}{c+1}\MMD_{k_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0},\F)+\frac{1}{c+1}\MMD_{k_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\delta_{0})

Proof.

We use the stick-breaking process representation
(
Sethuraman, 1994
)
of a DP, meaning that for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
\P^{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\xi_{j}^{i}\delta_{z_{j}^{i}}
where
\xi_{1:\infty}\sim\GEM(c+1)
and
z_{1:\infty}\simiid\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}
. For clarity we use the separate expectations
\E_{\xi^{i}_{1:\infty}\sim\GEM(c+1)}
and
\mathbb{E}_{z^{i}_{1:\infty}\simiid\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}
induced by
\mu_{i}
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Further let
\F^{\prime}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}
. Using the triangle inequality we have

\displaystyle\MMD_{k_{X}}(\mathbb{P}_{X}^{0},\mathbb{P}_{X})\leq\MMD_{k_{X}}(\P_{X},\F^{\prime})+\MMD_{k_{X}}(\mathbb{P}_{X}^{0},\F^{\prime})

(40)

We first bound the expected MMD between
\P_{X}
and
\F^{\prime}
. We have that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\P\sim\mu}[\MMD^{2}_{k_{X}}(\P_{X},\F^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\P\sim\mu}[\|\mu_{\P_{X}}-\mu_{\F^{\prime}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{X}}}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\P\sim\mu}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mu_{\P^{i}}-\mu_{\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}})\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{X}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{\xi^{1:n}_{1:\infty}\sim\GEM(c+1)}\left[\mathbb{E}_{z^{1:n}_{1:\infty}\simiid\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\xi_{j}^{i}k_{X}(z_{j}^{i},\cdot)-\mu_{\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}\right)\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{X}}}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E_{\xi^{i}_{1:\infty}\sim\GEM(c+1)}\left[\mathbb{E}_{z^{i}_{1:\infty}\simiid\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}\left[\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\xi_{j}^{i}k_{X}(z_{j}^{i},\cdot)-\mu_{\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{X}}}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E_{\xi^{i}_{1:\infty}\sim\GEM(c+1)}\left[\mathbb{E}_{z^{i}_{1:\infty}\simiid\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}\left[\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\xi_{j}^{i}\left(k_{X}(z_{j}^{i},\cdot)-\mu_{\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}\right)\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{X}}}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E_{\xi^{i}_{1:\infty}\sim\GEM(c+1)}\left[\mathbb{E}_{z^{i}_{1:\infty}\simiid\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(\xi_{j}^{i})^{2}\left\|k_{X}(z_{j}^{i},\cdot)-\mu_{\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{X}}}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\quad\quad\left.\left.+2\sum_{j\neq t}\xi_{j}^{i}\xi_{t}^{i}\left<k_{X}(z_{j},\cdot)-\mu_{\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}},k_{X}(z_{t},\cdot)-\mu_{\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}\right>_{\HkX}\right]\right].

(41)

For any
j\neq t
and each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
we have:

\displaystyle\E_{z_{j},z_{t}\simiid\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}\left[\left<k_{X}(z_{j},\cdot)-\mu_{\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}},k_{X}(z_{t},\cdot)-\mu_{\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}\right>_{\HkX}\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{z_{j},z_{t}\simiid\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}\left[\left<k_{X}(z_{j},\cdot)-\E_{z_{j}\sim\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}[k_{X}(z_{j},\cdot)],k_{X}(z_{t},\cdot)-\E_{z_{t}\sim\F^{\prime}}[(k_{X}(z_{t},\cdot)]\right>_{\HkX}\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{z_{j},z_{t}\simiid\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}[k_{X}(z_{j},z_{t})]-\E_{z_{j}\sim\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}[k_{X}(z_{j},\cdot)]\E_{z_{t}\sim\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}[k_{X}(z_{t},\cdot)]

\displaystyle\qquad-\E_{z_{t}\sim\F_{w_{i}}}[k_{X}(z_{t},\cdot)]\E_{z_{j}\sim\F_{w_{i}}}[k_{X}(z_{j},\cdot)]+\E_{z_{t}\sim\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}[k_{X}(z_{t},\cdot)]\E_{z_{j}\sim\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}[k_{X}(z_{j},\cdot)]

\displaystyle=\E_{z_{j},z_{t}\simiid\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}[k_{X}(z_{j},z_{t})]-\E_{z_{j},z_{t}\simiid\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}[k_{X}(z_{j},z_{t})]-\E_{z_{j},z_{t}\simiid\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}[k_{X}(z_{j},z_{t})]+\E_{z_{j},z_{t}\simiid\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}[k_{X}(z_{j},z_{t})]

\displaystyle=0.

(42)

Moreover, for any
j=1,2,\dots
and each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
we have

\displaystyle\E_{z_{k}\sim\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}\left[\|k_{X}(z_{k},\cdot)-\mu_{\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}\|_{\HkX}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{z_{k}\sim\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}\left[\|k_{X}(z_{k},\cdot)-\E_{z_{k}\sim\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}[k_{X}(z_{k},\cdot)]\|_{\HkX}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{z_{k}\sim\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}\left[\|k_{X}(z_{k},\cdot)\|_{\HkX}^{2}-2\left<k_{X}(z_{k},\cdot),\E_{z_{k}\sim\F_{w_{i}}}[k_{X}(z_{k},\cdot)]\right>\right.

\displaystyle\quad\quad\left.+\|\E_{z_{k}\sim\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}[k_{X}(z_{k},\cdot)]\|_{\HkX}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{z_{k}\sim\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}\left[\|k_{X}(z_{k},\cdot)\|_{\HkX}^{2}\right]-2\left<\E_{z_{k}\sim\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}[k_{X}(z_{k},\cdot)],\right.

\displaystyle\quad\quad\left.\E_{z_{k}\sim\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}[k_{X}(z_{k},\cdot)]\right>+\|\E_{z_{k}\sim\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}[k_{X}(z_{k},\cdot)]\|_{\HkX}^{2}

\displaystyle=\E_{z_{k}\sim\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}\left[\|k(z_{k},\cdot)\|_{\HkX}^{2}\right]-2\|\E_{z_{k}\sim\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}[k_{X}(z_{k},\cdot)]\|_{\HkX}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\|\E_{z_{k}\sim\F^{\prime}}[k_{X}(z_{k},\cdot)]\|_{\HkX}^{2}

\displaystyle=\E_{z_{k}\sim\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}[\|k_{X}(z_{k},\cdot)\|_{\HkX}^{2}]-\|\E_{z_{k}\sim\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}[k_{X}(z_{k},\cdot)]\|_{\HkX}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\E_{z_{k}\sim\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}[\|k_{X}(z_{k},\cdot)\|_{\HkX}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\E_{z_{k}\sim\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}[|k_{X}(z_{k},z_{k})|].

(43)

Substituting (
42
) and (
43
) in (
41
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\P\sim\mu}[\MMD^{2}_{k_{X}}(\P_{X},\F^{\prime})]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\E_{\xi_{1:\infty}^{i}\sim\GEM(c+1)}[(\xi_{j}^{i})^{2}]\E_{z_{k}\sim\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}}[|k_{X}(z_{k},z_{k})|]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\E_{\xi_{1:\infty}^{i}\sim\GEM(c+1)}[(\xi_{j}^{i})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{2(c+1)^{j-1}}{(c+3)^{j}(c+2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{(c+3)(c+2)}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{c+1}{c+3}\right)^{j-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{(c+3)(c+2)}\frac{1}{1-\frac{c+1}{c+3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{c+2}

where in the third equality we used the second moment of the GEM distribution namely
\E_{\xi_{1:\infty}\sim\GEM(c+1)}[\xi_{j}^{2}]=\frac{2(c+1)^{j-1}}{(c+3)^{j}(c+2)}
. By Jensen’s inequality we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\P\sim\mu}[\MMD_{k_{X}}(\P_{X},\F^{\prime})]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\P\sim\mu}[\MMD^{2}_{k_{X}}(\P_{X},\F^{\prime})]}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{c+2}}.

(44)

We now bound the second term in (
40
). By definition of
\F^{\prime}
it follows that

\F^{\prime}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\frac{c}{c+1}\F_{w_{i}}+\frac{1}{c+1}\delta_{w_{i}}\right]=\frac{c}{c+1}\F+\frac{1}{c+1}\P_{W}^{n}

where
\F:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\F_{w_{i}}
and
\P_{W}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}\delta_{w_{i}}
is the empirical measure of the observed, noisy covariates. By linearity of expectations it follows that
\E_{\F^{\prime}}[\cdot]=\frac{c}{c+1}\E_{\F}[\cdot]+\frac{1}{c+1}\E_{\P_{W}^{n}}[\cdot]
and the kernel mean embedding of
\F^{\prime}
is

\mu_{\F^{\prime}}:=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\F^{\prime}}[k(z,\cdot)]=\frac{c}{c+1}\E_{z\sim\F}[k(z,\cdot)]+\frac{1}{c+1}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\P_{W}^{n}}[k(z,\cdot)]=\frac{c}{c+1}\mu_{\F}+\frac{1}{c+1}\mu_{\P_{W}^{n}}.

Using this we can first bound the objective as:

\displaystyle\MMD_{k_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0},\F^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\left\|\mu_{\P_{X}^{0}}-\frac{c}{c+1}\mu_{\F}-\frac{1}{c+1}\mu_{\P_{W}^{n}}\right\|_{\HkX}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c}{c+1}\|\mu_{\P_{X}^{0}}-\mu_{\F}\|_{\HkX}+\frac{1}{c+1}\|\mu_{\P_{X}^{0}}-\mu_{\P_{W}^{n}}\|_{\HkX}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c}{c+1}\|\mu_{\P_{X}^{0}}-\mu_{\F}\|_{\HkX}+\frac{1}{c+1}\|\mu_{\P_{X}^{n}}-\mu_{\P_{W}^{n}}\|_{\HkX}+\frac{1}{c+1}\|\mu_{\P_{X}^{0}}-\mu_{\P_{X}^{n}}\|_{\HkX}

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{c+1}\MMD_{k_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0},\F)+\frac{1}{c+1}\MMD_{k_{X}}(\P_{X}^{n},\P_{W}^{n})+\frac{1}{c+1}\MMD_{k_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0},\P_{X}^{n})

(45)

where we used the triangle inequality for both inequalities. Using Lemma
B.1
with
S_{i}=x_{i}\sim Q_{i}:=\mathbb{P}^{0}_{X}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x_{1:n}\simiid\P_{X}^{0}}[\MMD_{k_{X}}(\mathbb{P}_{X}^{0},\mathbb{P}_{X}^{n})]\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}.

Next, let
\phi_{X}
be the feature map associated with the kernel function
k_{X}
and let
k_{X}^{i}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})=k_{X}(w_{i}-\nu,w_{i}-\nu^{\prime})
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
where
\{w_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
denote the observations. By assumption
4.0.3
,
k_{X}
is translation invariant so for any
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

k_{X}^{i}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})=k_{X}(w_{i}-\nu,w_{i}-\nu^{\prime})=\psi(w_{i}-\nu-w_{i}+\nu^{\prime})=\psi(\nu^{\prime}-\nu)=k_{X}(\nu^{\prime},\nu).

hence

\displaystyle\MMD^{2}_{k_{X}^{i}}(\F_{N}^{0},\delta_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu\sim\F_{N}^{0},\nu^{\prime}\sim\F_{N}^{0}}[k_{X}^{i}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})]-2\mathbb{E}_{\nu\sim\F_{N}^{0}}[k_{X}^{i}(\nu,0)]+k_{X}^{i}(0,0)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu\sim\F_{N}^{0},\nu^{\prime}\sim\F_{N}^{0}}[k_{X}(\nu^{\prime},\nu)]-2\mathbb{E}_{\nu\sim\F_{N}^{0}}[k_{X}(0,\nu)]+k_{X}(0,0)

\displaystyle=\MMD^{2}_{k_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\delta_{0})

where we have used the fact that kernels are symmetric by definition of a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space. Using this result we have,

\displaystyle\E_{x_{1:n}\sim\P^{0}_{X}}[\MMD_{k_{X}}(\P_{X}^{n},\P_{W}^{n})]

\displaystyle=\E_{x_{1:n}\sim\P^{0}}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\phi_{X}(x_{i})-\phi_{X}(w_{i})\right]\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{X}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{\nu^{0}_{1:n}\sim\F_{N}^{0}}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\phi_{X}(w_{i}-\nu_{i}^{0})-\phi_{X}(w_{i})\right]\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{X}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\E_{\nu^{0}_{i}\sim\F_{N}^{0}}[k^{i}_{X}(\nu_{i}^{0},\cdot)]-k^{i}_{X}(0,\cdot)\right]\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{X}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\MMD_{k^{i}_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\delta_{0})

\displaystyle=\MMD_{k_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\delta_{0}).

Substituting in (
45
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x_{1:n}\sim\P_{X}^{0}}[\MMD_{k_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0},\F^{\prime})]\leq\frac{1}{c+1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{c}{c+1}\MMD_{k_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0},\F)+\frac{1}{c+1}\MMD_{k_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\delta_{0}).

(46)

Finally, using (
44
) and (
46
) in (
40
) we obtain the required result:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x_{1:n}\sim\P_{X}^{0}}[\E_{\P\sim\mu}[\MMD_{k_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0},\P_{X})]]\leq

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}(c+1)}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{c+2}}+\frac{c}{c+1}\MMD_{k_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0},\F)+\frac{1}{c+1}\MMD_{k_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\delta_{0})

∎

Lemma B.7
.

(
Classical
)
Let
\mathbb{P}^{0,n}:=\P_{X}^{n}\P_{Y}^{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{(x_{i},y_{i})}
and
\mathbb{P}_{XY}=\P_{X}\P_{Y}^{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\xi_{j}^{i}\delta_{(z_{j},y_{i})}
where
z^{i}_{1:\infty}\overset{iid}{\sim}\F^{\prime}_{w_{i}},\xi^{i}_{1:\infty}\sim\GEM(c+1)
. Then under assumptions 1-3:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)_{1:n}\sim\P^{0}}[\E_{\P\sim\mu}[\MMD_{k}(\mathbb{P}^{0,n},\mathbb{P}_{XY})]]

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\leq\frac{c+2}{\sqrt{n}(c+1)}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{c+2}}+\frac{c}{c+1}\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0},\F)+\frac{1}{c+1}\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\delta_{0})

Proof.

Since
\P_{Y}^{n}
does not depend on
X
, it admits the trivial conditional mean embedding operator
\mathcal{C}_{Y}:\HkX\rightarrow\HkY
defined by
\mathcal{C}_{Y}f=\mu_{\P^{n}_{Y}}
for any
f\in\HkX
. Hence, for any
f\in\HkX
, and using Assumption
4.0.2
, we have:

\sup_{y_{1:n}}\|\mu_{\P^{n}_{Y}}\|^{2}_{\HkY}=\sup_{y_{1:n}}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{Y}(y_{i},\cdot)\right\|^{2}_{\HkY}=\sup_{y_{1:n}}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{Y}(y_{i},y_{i})+\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}k_{Y}(y_{i},y_{j})\right)=1

hence
\sup_{y_{1:n}}\|\mu_{\P^{n}_{Y}}\|_{\HkY}\leq 1
and
\|\mathcal{C}_{Y}\|_{o}\leq 1
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)_{1:n}\sim\P^{0}}[\E_{\P\sim\mu}[\MMD_{k}(\mathbb{P}^{0,n},\mathbb{P}_{XY})]]

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{C}_{Y}\|_{o}\mathbb{E}_{x_{1:n}\sim\P_{X}^{0}}[\E_{\P\sim\mu}[\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\P_{X}^{n},\P_{X})]]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{x_{1:n}\sim\P_{X}^{0}}[\E_{\P\sim\mu}[\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\P_{X}^{n},\P_{X})]]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{x_{1:n}\sim\P_{X}^{0}}[\E_{\P\sim\mu}[\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0},\P_{X})+\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0},\P_{X}^{n})]]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{x_{1:n}\sim\P_{X}^{0}}[\E_{\P\sim\mu}[\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0},\P_{X})]]+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c+2}{\sqrt{n}(c+1)}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{c+2}}+\frac{c}{c+1}\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\F_{N})

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{c+1}\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\delta_{0})

where we used Lemma
B.3
for the first inequality, the triangle inequality for the third and Lemma
B.6
with
k_{X}^{2}
for the last inequality. The fact that
k_{X}^{2}
satisfies the required conditions of Lemma
B.6
follows from Assumptions
4.0.2
and
4.0.3
. In particular, if
|k_{X}|\leq 1
then
|k^{2}_{X}|\leq 1
and if
k_{X}
is translation invariant then
k^{2}_{X}(x,x^{\prime})=\psi(x-x^{\prime})\psi(x-x^{\prime})\forall x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
and
k^{2}_{X}
is also translation invariant. Finally, for the fourth inequality, we used Lemma
B.1
with
S_{i}=(x_{i})\sim Q_{i}:=\P^{0}_{X}
.
∎

Lemma B.8
.

(
Classical
)
Let
\P^{0}_{\theta}:=\P_{X}^{0}\P_{g(\theta,\cdot)}
and
\P_{\theta}:=\P_{X}\P_{g(\theta,\cdot)}
. Under Assumptions 1-4, for any

:

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\leq\Lambda\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}(c+1)}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{c+2}}+\frac{c}{c+1}\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\F_{N})+\frac{1}{c+1}\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\delta_{0})\right)

B.3
and Assumption 4 we have that for any

:

for

.
∎

:

Proof.

:

\displaystyle\quad\quad+2\MMD_{k}(\P^{0},\P^{0,n})+2\MMD_{k}(\P^{0,n},\P_{XY})

where we used the triangle inequality in the first two and last two inequalities and the definition of

with

(47)

Using equation (
47
) and lemmas
B.7
and

, we have that:

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq\frac{4}{\sqrt{n}}+2(1+\Lambda)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}(c+1)}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{c+2}}+\frac{c}{c+1}\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\P_{X}^{0},\F)\right.

\displaystyle\left.\quad\quad\quad\quad+\frac{1}{c+1}\MMD_{k^{2}_{X}}(\F_{N}^{0},\delta_{0})\right).

, to minimize the MMD objective which is approximated via a U-statistic as in
Gretton et al., 2012
using independent samples. In particular, for i.i.d samples
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\simiid\P
and
\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\simiid\Q
the squared MMD between
\P,\Q
can be approximated as:

\displaystyle\widehat{\MMD}^{2}_{k}(\P,\Q)=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\neq i}^{n}k(x_{i},x_{j})+\frac{1}{m(m-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j\neq i}^{m}k(y_{i},y_{j})-\frac{2}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}k(x_{i},y_{j}).

In practice, we take
n=m
to be equal to the number of observations. To initialize the optimization several different approaches can be taken. In the nonlinear regression simulation, we sample uniformly at random from
[-1,4]
at each bootstrap iteration for each regression coefficient. One could also initialize from the nonlinear Least Squares (NLS) estimate however adding randomness to the initialization procedure can aid numerical stability and increase posterior uncertainty leading to better coverage probabilities. In the real-world California Test Scores study we combine the above methods by first finding the NLS estimates and then sampling uniformly from intervals centered around these estimates. Initialization for the Mental Health study which has a more complicated model and a higher number of parameters is discussed in Section
C.3
.

We further used the RBF kernel for both
k_{X}
and
k_{Y}
throughout our experiments. For the nonlinear regression simulation the length scales for
k_{X}
and
k_{Y}
were set to 100 in the Classical error case, whereas the median heuristic
(
Gretton et al., 2012
)
was used in the Berkson case as well as the California Test Scores study. For the Mental Health study the length scales for
k_{X}
and
k_{Y}
were set to 10 and 100 repsectively. These values were chosen to ensure the loss and gradient do not vanish during optimisation.

C.3
Mental Health Study

In this section we give details of the model used in
Harezlak et al., 2018
for the treatment effect estimation in the Mental Health study which we also adopt in Section
5.2
.

In particular, for each observation
i=1,\dots,n_{placebo}
in the placebo group we model

\displaystyle g^{0}(\theta^{0},x)

\displaystyle=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}x+\sum_{k=1}^{K}u_{0k}z_{k}(x),

\displaystyle y_{i}\>|\>x_{i}

\displaystyle\sim\P_{g^{0}(\theta^{0},x_{i})}=N(g^{0}(\theta^{0},x_{i}),\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon})

for parameters
\theta^{0}=(\beta_{0},\beta_{1},u_{01},\dots,u_{0K})
and spline basis
\{z_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
.
Similarly, for each patient
i=1,\dots,n_{treatment}
in the treatment group we model

\displaystyle g^{1}(\theta^{1},x)

\displaystyle=\beta_{0}+\beta_{0}^{\text{drug}}+(\beta_{1}+\beta_{1}^{\text{drug}})x+\sum_{k=1}^{K}u_{1k}z_{k}(x),

\displaystyle y_{i}\>|\>x_{i}

\displaystyle\sim\P_{g^{1}(\theta^{1},x_{i})}=N(g^{1}(\theta^{1},x_{i}),\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}).

We define the parameter of interest
\theta=(\beta,u_{0},u_{1})
where
\beta=(\beta_{0},\beta_{1},\beta_{0}^{\text{drug}},\beta_{1}^{\text{drug}})
,
u_{0}=(u_{01},\dots,u_{0K})
and
u_{1}=(u_{11},\dots,u_{1K})
. Let
n=n_{treatment}+n_{placebo}
and define
Y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
and the design matrices

\displaystyle x=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&x_{1}&1-I_{1}&(1-I_{1})x_{1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
1&x_{n}&1-I_{n}&(1-I_{n})x_{n}\end{pmatrix},\quad Z_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{1}z_{1}(x_{1})&\dots&I_{1}z_{K}(x_{1})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
I_{n}z_{1}(x_{n})&\dots&I_{n}z_{K}(x_{n})\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\quad\quad Z_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}(1-I_{1})z_{1}(x_{1})&\dots&(1-I_{1})z_{K}(x_{1})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
(1-I_{n})z_{1}(x_{n})&\dots&(1-I_{n})z_{K}(x_{n})\end{pmatrix}.

Based on this notation, the full Bayesian model proposed in
Harezlak et al., 2018
is

\displaystyle Y\>|\>x\sim\P_{g(\theta,x)}:=N(x\beta+Z_{0}u_{0}+Z_{1}u_{1},\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}I).

Based on this model, the average treatment effect given a baseline score
x
is:

\displaystyle ATE(x)\approx\beta_{0}^{\text{drug}}+\beta_{1}^{\text{drug}}x+\sum_{k=1}^{K}(u_{1k}-u_{0k})z_{k}(x).

We use the same spline basis as the one used in the
HRW
package by
Harezlak et al., 2018
, namely the penalized spline defined by

z_{k}(x)=(x-\kappa_{k})_{+}

where
x_{+}=\max(x,0)
and the knots
\{\kappa_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
are approximately equally spaced points with respect to the quantiles of the
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

For the implementation of the Robust-MEM (MMD) algorithm in this application we set
c=1
and
T=100
. For the optimization step we used a learning rate of
\eta=0.001
and we employ a random restart method for the initialisation of the optimization: 100 points are sampled uniformly at random and the MMD loss is calculated using the observed data. The point with the smallest loss is used as the initial point and the resulting parameter estimate with the smallest loss during optimisation is picked. The uniform intervals are set to
[0,2]
for the regression parameters
\beta
and
[-0.1,0.1]
for the spline parameters
u_{0},u_{1}
. Posterior samples are obtained from 200 bootstrap iterations. Of course, when the optimisation objective is non-convex, there is usually a trade-off between coverage capability and MSE of the posterior estimates or intervals depending on the initialisation of the optimisation. A wider uniform interval usually leads to higher uncertainty in our posterior beliefs which can exhbit better coverage probability of estimated intervals. On the contrary, if one is interested in the MSE of the posterior estimations then one can perform the above procedure for more than 100 times to get a better initial point of the optimisation or use a narrower uniform distribution based on prior beliefs or historic data.
