Newton’s methods for solving linear inverse problems with neural network coders 0 0 footnotetext: 2000 Mathematics Subject Classification : 65J22, 47J07 0 0 footnotetext: Keywords : Gauss-Newton’s method, inverse problems, neural networks
Otmar Scherzer otmar.scherzer@univie.ac.at, Bernd Hofmann hofmannb@mathematik.tu-chemnitz.de, Zuhair Nashed zuhair.nashed@ucf.edu

Abstract
Neural networks functions are supposed to be able to encode the desired solution of an inverse problem very efficiently. In this paper, we consider the problem of solving linear inverse problems with neural network coders. First we establish some correspondences of this formulation with existing concepts in regularization theory, in particular with state space regularization, operator decomposition and iterative regularization methods. A Gauss-Newton’s method is suitable for solving encoded linear inverse problems, which is supported by a local convergence result. The convergence studies, however, are not complete, and are based on a conjecture on linear independence of activation functions and its derivatives. This paper is dedicated to Heinz Engl’s 70th birthday.

We start the discussion by considering a
general nonlinear operator equation

N({\vec{p}}\,{})={\bf{y}},

(1.1)

where
N:{\vec{P}}\to{\mathbf{Y}}
is a nonlinear operator between Hilbert spaces
{\vec{P}}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}
.
Particular emphasis is placed on the case when the numerical solution of
Equation 1.1
is ill-posed or ill-conditioned.
In this situation regularization methods need to be implemented (see
[
EngHanNeu96
]
)
to guarantee stable solvability. Essentially two classes of methods exist as the basis for solving inverse problems numerically,
which are
variational methods

(see for instance
[

,
SchGraGroHalLen09
,
Hof86
,
SchuKalHofKaz12
,
BakGon94
,
Mor84
]
) and

(see for instance
[
KalNeuSch08
,
BakGon89
]
).

Modern
coding theory
(see for instance
[
BorJalPriDim17
,
DufCamEhr21_report
,
BraRajRumWir22_report
,
RifMesVinMulBen11
,
BorJalPriDim17
]
)
assumes that
natural images
can be represented efficiently by a combination of
neural network
functions.
Therefore the
set of natural images
is given by the range of a nonlinear mapping
\Psi
,
which maps neural network coefficients to images. In coding theory very often the
considered operator equation is assumed to be
linear
and
ill-posed
. Let us consider
therefore the equation

F{\bf{x}}={\bf{y}},

(1.2)

where
F:{\mathbf{X}}\to{\mathbf{Y}}
is a bounded linear operator with non-closed range mapping between the Hilbert spaces
{\mathbf{X}}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}
such that

{\bf{x}}
and
{\bf{y}}
are elements of
{\mathbf{X}}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}
, respectively.
Combining this with the assumption that the solution of
Equation 1.2
is a natural image, or in other words that it can be represented as a
combination of neural network functions, we get the operator equation

N({\vec{p}}\,{})=F\Psi({\vec{p}}\,{})={\bf{y}},

(1.3)

where
\Psi:{\vec{P}}\to{\mathbf{X}}
is the before mentioned nonlinear operator that maps neural network parameters to image functions. We call

•

and

•

, in accordance with a terminology, which we generated in
[
AspKorSch20
,
AspFriKorSch21
]
.

•

{\vec{P}}
is called the
parameter space
. We make a different notation for
{\vec{P}}
, because it represents parametrizations, and is often considered a space of vectors below.

The advantage of this ansatz is that the solution of
Equation 1.2
is sparsely coded.
However, the price to pay is that the reformulated
Equation 1.3
is nonlinear.
Operator equations of the form of
Equation 1.3
are not new:
They have been studied in abstract settings for instance in the context of

•

state space regularization

[
ChaKun93
]
and

•

in the context of the

[

,
Hof99
,
Hof94
,
HofSch94
,
HofTau97
]
as well as of the
degree of nonlinearity

[
HofSch94
]
of nonlinear ill–posed operator equations.

•

Another related approach is
finite dimensional approximation
of regularization in Banach spaces (see for instance
[
PoeResSch10
]
).
Particularly, finite dimensional approximations of regularization methods with neural network functions (in the context of frames and

deep synthesis regularization
) have been studied in
[
ObmSchwHal21
]
.

In this paper we aim to link the general regularization of the degree of ill–posedness and nonlinearity with coding theory.
We investigate generalized Gauss-Newton’s methods for solving
Equation 1.3
; Such methods replace the inverse of standard Newton’s method by approximations of outer inverses (see
[
NasChe93
]
).

The outline of the paper is as follows: In

we review two decomposition cases as stated in
[
Hof94
]
first,
one of them is
Equation 1.3
. The study of decomposition cases follows the work on classifying inverse problems and regularization (see
[
Nas87b
]
). For operators associated to
Equation 1.3
, Newton’s methods seem to be better suited than gradient descent methods, which we support by a convergence analysis (see
Section 3
). Section
is devoted to solving
Equation 1.3
, where
\Psi
is a shallow neural network

2. Decomposition cases

We start with a definition for nonlinear operator equations possessing forward operators that are compositions of a linear and a nonlinear operator. Precisely, we distinguish between a first decomposition case (i), where the linear operator is the inner operator and the nonlinear is the outer one,
and a second decomposition case (ii), where the nonlinear operator is the inner operator and the linear is the outer operator.

Definition 2.1
(Decomposition cases).

Let
{\vec{P}},{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{Y}}
be Hilbert-spaces.

(i)

An operator
N
is said to satisfy the
first decomposition case
in an open, non-empty neighborhood

\mathcal{B}({\vec{p}}\,{}^{\dagger};\rho)\subseteq{\vec{P}}
of some point
{\vec{p}}\,{}^{\dagger}
if there exists a linear operator
F:{\vec{P}}\to{\mathbf{X}}
and a nonlinear operator
\Psi:{\mathbf{X}}\to{\mathbf{Y}}
such that

N({\vec{p}}\,{})=\Psi(F{\vec{p}}\,{})\text{ for }{\vec{p}}\,{}\in\mathcal{B}({\vec{p}}\,{}^{\dagger};\rho).

(ii)

N
is said to satisfy the
second decomposition case
in a neighborhood

\mathcal{B}({\vec{p}}\,{}^{\dagger};\rho)\subseteq{\vec{P}}
of some point
{\vec{p}}\,{}^{\dagger}
if there exists a linear operator
F:{\mathbf{X}}\to{\mathbf{Y}}

and a nonlinear operator
\Psi:{\vec{P}}\to{\mathbf{X}}
such that

N({\vec{p}}\,{})=F\Psi({\vec{p}}\,{})\text{ for }{\vec{p}}\,{}\in\mathcal{B}({\vec{p}}\,{}^{\dagger};\rho).

(2.1)

Typically it is assumed that the nonlinear operator
\Psi
is well–posed.

Remark 2.2
(First decomposition case).

In
[
Hof94
]
, this decomposition case has been studied under structural conditions, relating the second derivative of
N
with the first derivative. Under such assumptions convergence rates conditions (see
[
Hof94
, Lemma 4.1]
) could be proven. The first decomposition case
also arises in inverse option pricing problems in math finance (see
[
HeiHof03
]
and
[
HofKalPoeSch07
, Sect.4]
), where the ill-posed compact linear integration operator occurs
as inner operator and a well-posed Nemytskii operator as outer operator.

Remark 2.3
(Second decomposition case).

Regularization methods for solving operator equations with operators satisfying the second order decomposition case, see
Equation 2.1
, were probably first analyzed in
[
ChaKun93
]
under the name of
state space regularization
. They considered for instance Tikhonov-type regularization methods, consisting in minimization of

J_{\lambda}({\vec{p}}\,{})=\left\|F\Psi({\vec{p}}\,{})-{\bf{y}}\right\|_{\mathbf{Y}}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\Psi({\vec{p}}\,{})-\tilde{{\bf{x}}}\right\|_{\mathbf{X}}^{2},

(2.2)

where
\tilde{{\bf{x}}}
is a prior and
\lambda>0
is a regularization parameter. In
[
ChaKun93
]
they derived estimates for the second derivative of
J_{\lambda}({\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{\lambda})({\bf{h}},{\bf{h}})
, where
{\bf{h}}\in{\vec{P}}
, that is for the curvature of
J_{\lambda}
. If the curvature can be bounded from below by some terms
\left\|{\bf{h}}\right\|_{\vec{p}}\,{}\!\!\!\!{}^{2}
then, for instance, a locally unique minimizer of
J_{\lambda}
can be guaranteed and also domains can be specified where the functional is convex. Conditions, which guarantee convexity are called

curvature to size conditions
.
Subsequently, these decomposition cases have been studied exemplarily in
[
Hof94
]
. The theory developed there directly applies to
Equation 1.3
.

Instead of
J_{\lambda}
researchers often study direct regularization with respect to
{\vec{p}}\,{}
. For instance in
[
DufCamEhr21_report
]

functionals of the form

J_{\lambda}({\vec{p}}\,{})=\left\|F\Psi({\vec{p}}\,{})-{\bf{y}}\right\|_{\mathbf{Y}}^{2}+\lambda\mathcal{L}({\vec{p}}\,{}),

(2.3)

where
\mathcal{L}
is some functional directly regularizing the parameter space. Typically
\mathcal{L}
is chosen to penalize for sparsity of parameters.
The main difference between
Equation 2.2
and
Equation 2.3
is that in the prior regularization is performed with respect to the image space
{\mathbf{X}}
and in the later with respect to the parameter space
{\vec{P}}
. Well-posedness of the functional
J_{\lambda}
in
Equation 2.2
follows if
F\circ\Psi
is lower-semicontinuous, which in turn follows if
\Psi
is invertible.

In the following we study the solution of decomposable operator equations, such as
Equation 1.3
, with Gauss-Newton’s methods.
Decomposition cases have been used in the analysis of iterative regularization methods as well (see
[
KalNeuSch08
]
):

Definition 2.4
(Strong tangential cone condition).

Let
N:\mathcal{D}{(N)}\subset{\vec{P}}\to{\mathbf{Y}}
with
\mathcal{D}{(N)}
its domain be a nonlinear operator.

(i)

Then
N
is said to satisfy the strong tangential cone condition, originally introduced in
[
HanNeuSch95
]
, if

N^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{2})=R_{{\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{2},{\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{1}}N^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{1})\text{ for all }{\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{1},{\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{2}\in\mathcal{D}{(N)}.

(2.4)

where

\left\|R_{{\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{2},{\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{1}}-I\right\|\leq C_{T}\left\|{\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{2}-{\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{1}\right\|_{\vec{P}}.

(2.5)

(ii)

In
[
Bla96
]
the
order reversed
tangential cone condition,

N^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{2})=N^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{1})R_{{\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{2},{\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{1}}\text{ for all }{\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{1},{\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{2}\in\mathcal{D}{(N)},

(2.6)

together with
Equation 2.5
, has been introduced.

Remark 2.5
.

Equation 2.4
has been used for analyzing
gradient descent methods
(see for instance
[
HanNeuSch95
,
KalNeuSch08
]
). For the analysis of Newton’s methods
Equation 2.6
has been used (see
[
Bla96
,
KalNeuSch08
]
).

The relation to the decomposition cases is as follows:

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
N:\mathcal{D}{(N)}\subseteq{\vec{P}}\to{\mathbf{Y}}
with
\mathcal{D}{(N)}=\mathcal{B}({\vec{p}}\,{}^{\dagger};\rho)

satisfy the second decomposition case and assume that
\Psi^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,{})
is invertible for
{\vec{p}}\,{}\in\mathcal{D}{(N)}
. Then
N
satisfies
Equation 2.6
.

Proof
:

If
N
satisfies the second decomposition case,
Equation 2.1
, then
N^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,{})=F\Psi^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,{})
for all
{\vec{p}}\,{}\in\mathcal{D}{(N)}
. Note, that because
F
is defined on the whole space
{\mathbf{X}}
,
\mathcal{D}{(N)}=\mathcal{D}{(\Psi)}
.
By using the invertability assumption on
\Psi
we get

\displaystyle N^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{2})

\displaystyle=F\Psi^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{2})=F\Psi^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{1})\underbrace{\Psi^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{1})^{-1}\Psi^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{2})}_{=:R_{{\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{2},{\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{1}}}=N^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{1})R_{{\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{2},{\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{1}},

which gives the assertion.

\square

As we have shown, decomposition cases have been extensively studied in the regularization literature. One conclusion out of these studies is that the order reversed
tangential cone condition
Equation 2.6
is suitable for analyzing Newton’s methods
[
Bla96
,
KalNeuSch08
]
and thus in turn for the coded linear operator
Equation 1.3
because of

. The standard tool for analyzing Newton’s methods is the Newton-Mysovskii condition as discussed below.

3. The Newton-Mysovskii Conditions

In this section we put abstract convergence conditions for Newton type methods in context with decoding.
We consider first Newton’s methods for solving the
general
operator
Equation 1.1
.
Decomposition cases of the operator
N
will be considered afterwards.

3.1. Newton’s method with invertible linearizations

For Newton’s methods
local convergence
is guaranteed under
Newton-Mysovskii
conditions. For comparison reasons, we first recall a simple Newton’s method analysis in finite dimensional spaces if the nonlinear operator has derivatives which are invertible. The proof of more general results, such as
item
below, applies here as well, and thus here the proof is omitted.
Several variants of Newton-Mysovskii conditions have been proposed in the literature (see for instance
[
DeuHei79
,
DeuPot92
,
NasChe93
]
).
Analysis of Newton’s method was an active research area in the last century, see for instance
[
Ort68
,
Schw79
]
.

Theorem 3.1
(Finite dimensional Newton’s method).

Let
N:\mathcal{D}{(N)}\subseteq\mathds{R}^{n}\to\mathds{R}^{n}
be continuously Fréchet-differentiable on a non-empty, open and convex set
\mathcal{D}{(N)}
. Let
{\vec{p}}\,{}^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{D}{(N)}
be a solution of
Equation 1.1
.
Moreover, we assume that

(i)

N^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,{})
is invertible for all
{\vec{p}}\,{}\in\mathcal{D}{(N)}
and that

(ii)

the
Newton-Mysovskii condition
holds: That is, there exist some
C_{N}>0
such that

\displaystyle\left\|N^{\prime}({\vec{q}}\,{})^{-1}(N^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,{}+s({\vec{q}}\,{}-{\vec{p}}\,{}))-N^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,{}))({\vec{q}}\,{}-{\vec{p}}\,{})\right\|_{{\vec{P}}}\leq sC_{N}\left\|{\vec{p}}\,{}-{\vec{q}}\,{}\right\|_{\vec{P}}^{2}

(3.1)

\displaystyle\text{ for all }{\vec{p}}\,{},{\vec{q}}\,{}\in\mathcal{D}{(N)},s\in[0,1].

Let
{\vec{p}}\,{}^{0}\in\mathcal{D}{(N)}
which satisfies

\overline{\mathcal{B}({\vec{p}}\,{}^{0};\rho)}\subseteq\mathcal{D}{(N)}\text{ with }\rho:=\left\|{\vec{p}}\,{}^{\dagger}-{\vec{p}}\,{}^{0}\right\|_{\vec{P}}\text{ and }h:=\frac{\rho C_{I}C_{L}}{2}<1.

(3.2)

Then the Newton’s iteration with starting point
{\vec{p}}\,{}^{0}
,

\displaystyle{\vec{p}}\,{}^{k+1}={\vec{p}}\,{}^{k}-N^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,{}^{k})^{-1}(N({\vec{p}}\,{}^{k})-{\bf{y}})\quad k\in\mathds{N}_{0},

(3.3)

satisfies that the iterates
\left\{{\vec{p}}\,{}^{k}:k=0,1,2,\ldots\right\}
belong to
\overline{\mathcal{B}({\vec{p}}\,{}^{0};\rho)}
and
converge quadratically to
{\vec{p}}\,{}^{\dagger}\in\overline{\mathcal{B}({\vec{p}}\,{}^{0},\rho)}
.

Now, we turn to the case that
N
is a decomposition operator.

3.2. Newton-Mysovskii conditions with composed operator

Now, we study the case of convergence of Gauss-Newton’s methods where
N:{\vec{P}}\to{\mathbf{Y}}
with
{\vec{P}}=\mathds{R}^{n_{*}}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}
is an infinite dimensional Hilbert space, where
F:{\mathbf{X}}\to{\mathbf{Y}}
is linear and bounded and
\Psi:{\vec{P}}=\mathds{R}^{n_{*}}\to{\mathbf{X}}
. In this case the Moore-Penrose inverse, or even more general the outer inverse, replaces the inverse in a classical Newton’s method (see
Equation 3.3
), because linearizations of
N
will not be invertible anymore as a simple count of dimensions show. We refer now to Gauss-Newton’s methods if the linearizations might not be invertible to distinguish between classical Newton’s methods also by name.

Before we phrase a convergence result for Gauss-Newton’s methods we recall and introduce some definitions:

Notation 3.2
(Inner, outer and Moore-Penrose inverse).

(see
[
Nas76
,
Nas87
]
) Let
L:{\vec{P}}\to{\mathbf{Y}}

{\vec{P}}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}
. Then

(i)

the operator

is called a

to
L
if

BL=I\;.

(ii)

B:{\mathbf{Y}}\to{\vec{P}}
is called a
right inverse
to
L
if

LB=I\;.

Left and right inverses are used in different context:

•

For a left inverse the nullspace of
L
has to be trivial, in contrast to
B
.

•

For a right inverse the nullspace of
B
has to be trivial.

(iii)

B:{\vec{P}}\to{\vec{P}}
is called a
inverse
to
L
if
B
is a right and a left inverse.

(iv)

B:{\vec{P}}\to{\mathbf{Y}}
is an
outer inverse
to
L
if

BLB=B.

(3.4)

(v)

Let
{\vec{P}}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}
be Hilbert-spaces,
L:{\vec{P}}\to{\mathbf{Y}}
be a linear bounded operator. We denote the orthogonal projections

P
and
Q
onto
\mathcal{N}(L)
, the nullspace of
L
(which is closed), and
\overline{}
, the closure of the range of
L
:
That is for all
{\vec{p}}\,{}\in{\vec{P}}
and

P{\vec{p}}\,{}=\text{argmin}\left\{\left\|{\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{1}-{\vec{p}}\,{}\right\|_{\vec{P}}:{\vec{p}}\,\!{}_{1}\in\mathcal{N}(L)\right\}\text{ and }Q{\bf{y}}=\text{argmin}\left\{\left\|{\bf{y}}_{1}-{\bf{y}}\right\|_{\mathbf{Y}}:{\bf{y}}_{1}\in\overline{\mathcal{R}(L)}\right\}.

(3.5)

We therefore have

\begin{aligned} P:{\vec{P}}&\to\mathcal{N}(L)\dot{+}\mathcal{N}(L)^{\bot}\,\\
{\vec{p}}\,{}&\mapsto P{\vec{p}}\,{}+0\end{aligned}\quad\text{ and }\quad\begin{aligned} Q:{\mathbf{Y}}&\to\overline{}\dot{+}\bot.\\
{\bf{y}}&\to Q{\bf{y}}+0\end{aligned}

B:\mathcal{D}{(B)}\subseteq{\mathbf{Y}}\to{\vec{P}}
with
\mathcal{D}{(B)}:={+}\bot
is called the
Moore-Penrose inverse
of

if the following identities hold

\displaystyle LBL

\displaystyle=L,

(3.6)

\displaystyle BLB

\displaystyle=B,

\displaystyle BL

\displaystyle=I-P,

\displaystyle LB

\displaystyle=Q|_{\mathcal{D}{(B)}}.

In coding theory it is often stated that the range of a neural network operator
\Psi
forms a manifold in
{\mathbf{X}}
, a space, which contains the natural images.
This is the basis of the following definition making use of the Moore-Penrose inverse.

Definition 3.3
(Lipschitz-differentiable immersion).

Let
\Psi:\mathcal{D}{(\Psi)}\subseteq{\vec{P}}=\mathds{R}^{n_{*}}\to{\mathbf{X}}

where
\mathcal{D}{(\Psi)}
is open, non-empty, convex and
{\mathbf{X}}
is a seperable (potentially infinite dimensional) Hilbert-space.

(i)

We assume that
\mathcal{M}:=\Psi(\mathcal{D}{(\Psi)})
is a
n_{*}
-dimensional
submanifold
in
{\mathbf{X}}
:

•

Let for all
{\vec{p}}\,{}=(p_{i})_{i=1}^{n_{*}}\in\mathcal{D}{(\Psi)}
denote with
\Psi^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,{})
the Fréchet-derivative of
\Psi
:

\displaystyle\Psi^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,{}):{\vec{P}}

\displaystyle\to{\mathbf{X}},

\displaystyle{\vec{q}}\,{}=(q_{i})_{i=1}^{n_{*}}

\displaystyle\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{p_{i}}\Psi({\vec{p}}\,{})\end{pmatrix}_{i=1,\ldots,n_{*}}{\vec{q}}\,{}.

Here
\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{p_{i}}\Psi({\vec{p}}\,{})\end{pmatrix}_{i=1,\ldots,n_{*}}
denotes the vector of functions consisting of all partial derivatives with respect to
{\vec{p}}\,{}
. In differential geometry notation this coincides with the
tangential mapping

T_{{\vec{p}}\,{}}\Psi
. However, the situation is slightly different here because
{\mathbf{X}}
can be infinite dimensional.

•

The
representation mapping
of the derivative

\displaystyle\Psi^{\prime}:\mathcal{D}{(\Psi)}

\displaystyle\to{\mathbf{X}}^{n_{*}},

\displaystyle{\vec{p}}{}

\displaystyle\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{p_{i}}\Psi({\vec{p}}\,{})\end{pmatrix}_{i=1,\ldots,n_{*}}.

has always the same rank

in
\mathcal{D}{(\Psi)}
, meaning that all elements of
\partial_{{\vec{p}}\,{}}\Psi({\vec{p}}\,{})
are linearly independent.
This assumption means, in particular, that
\Psi
is an
immersion
and
\mathcal{M}
is a submanifold.

(ii)

We define

\displaystyle P_{\vec{p}}\,{}:{\mathbf{X}}

\displaystyle\to{\mathbf{X}}_{\vec{p}}\,{}:=\text{span}\left\{\partial_{p_{i}}\Psi({\vec{p}}\,{}):i=1,\ldots,n_{*}\right\},

(3.7)

\displaystyle{\bf{x}}

\displaystyle\mapsto P_{\vec{p}}\,{}{\bf{x}}:=\text{argmin}\left\{\left\|{\bf{x}}_{1}-{\bf{x}}\right\|_{\mathbf{X}}:{\bf{x}}_{1}\in{\mathbf{X}}_{\vec{p}}\,{}\right\}

as the projection from

{\mathbf{X}}={\mathbf{X}}_{\vec{p}}\,{}\dot{+}{\mathbf{X}}_{\vec{p}}\,{}\!\!\!\!{}^{\bot}

onto
{\mathbf{X}}_{\vec{p}}\,{}
, which is well-defined by the closedness of the finite dimensional subspace
{\mathbf{X}}_{\vec{p}}\,{}
.

Next we define the inverse of
\Psi^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,{})
on
{\mathbf{X}}_{\vec{p}}\,{}

\displaystyle\Psi^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,{})^{-1}:\text{span}\left\{\partial_{p_{i}}\Psi({\vec{p}}\,{}):i=1,\ldots,n_{*}\right\}\to{\vec{P}},

\displaystyle{\bf{x}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{*}}x_{i}\partial_{p_{i}}\Psi({\vec{p}}\,{})\mapsto(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n_{*}}

and consequently on
{\mathbf{X}}

\displaystyle\Psi^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,{})^{\dagger}:{\mathbf{X}}={\mathbf{X}}_{\vec{p}}\,{}\dot{+}{\mathbf{X}}_{\vec{p}}\,{}\!\!\!\!{}^{\bot}\to{\vec{P}},

(3.8)

\displaystyle{\bf{x}}=({\bf{x}}_{1},{\bf{x}}_{2})\mapsto\Psi^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,{})^{-1}{\bf{x}}_{1}

which are both well-defined because we assume that
\Psi
is an immersion.
Note that
x_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n_{*}
are not necessarily the coordinates with respect to an orthonormal system in
\text{span}\left\{\partial_{p_{i}}\Psi({\vec{p}}\,{}):i=1,\ldots,n_{*}\right\}
.

(iii)

Finally, we assume that the operators
\Psi^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,{})
are locally bounded and locally Lipschitz-continuous in

.
That is

\displaystyle\left\|\Psi^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,{})-\Psi^{\prime}({\vec{q}}\,{})\right\|_{{\vec{P}}\to{\mathbf{X}}}\leq C_{L}\left\|{\vec{p}}\,{}-{\vec{q}}\,{}\right\|_{{\vec{P}}}\quad\left\|\Psi^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,{})\right\|_{{\vec{P}}\to{\mathbf{X}}}\leq C_{I}\text{ for }{\vec{p}}\,{},{\vec{q}}\,{}\in\mathcal{D}{(\Psi)}.

(3.9)

If
\Psi
satisfies these three properties we call it a
Lipschitz-differentiable immersion
.

The following lemma is proved by standard means:

Lemma 3.4
.

For a Lipschitz-differentiable immersion

•

the function
\Psi^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,{})^{\dagger}:{\mathbf{X}}\to{\vec{P}}
is in fact the Moore-Penrose inverse of
\Psi^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,{})
and

•

for every point
{\vec{p}}\,{}\in\mathcal{D}{(\Psi)}\subseteq{\vec{P}}
there exists a non-empty closed neighborhood where

\begin{itemize}\itemize@item@Theorem 2 from \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Hor91}{}{}]}: Let $\sigma:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}^{+}$ be a {\bf continuous, bounded and nonconstant} function. Then, for
every function $g\in C(\mathds{R}^{n})$ and every $\nu>0$, there exists a function
\begin{equation}{\vec{x}}\to G({\vec{x}})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}\sigma({\bf{w}}_{j}^{T}{\vec{x}}+\theta_{j})\hskip 16.38895pt\text{ with }N\in\mathds{N},\alpha_{j},\theta_{j}\in\mathds{R},{\bf{w}}_{j}\in\mathds{R}^{n},\end{equation}satisfying
\begin{equation*}|G({\vec{x}})-g({\vec{x}})|<\nu\text{ uniformly for all compact subsets }K\subseteq\mathds{R}^{n}.\end{equation*}\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@Theorem 1 from \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Hor91}{}{}]}: Let $\sigma:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}^{+}$ be {\bf unbounded and nonconstant}. Then for every measure $\mu$ on $\mathds{R}^{n}$ and every constant $\nu>0$ and $p\geq 1$ there exists a function $G$ of the form \autoref{eq:Linear} that satisfies
\begin{equation*}\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}|G({\vec{x}})-g({\vec{x}})|^{p}d{\vec{x}}<\nu.\end{equation*}\end{itemize}\@qedbox{}\end{theorem}The first result applies for instance to the sigmoid and hyperbolic tangent function (see \autoref{eq:sigmoid} and \autoref{eq:tanh}).
The second result applies to the ReLU function (see \autoref{eq:relu}). In particular all approximation properties also hold on the compact set $[0,1]^{n}$, which we are considering.
\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Newton-Mysovskii condition with neural networks}
In the following we verify Newton-Mysovskii conditions for $\Psi$ being the encoder of \autoref{eq:classical_approximation}.
First we calculate the first and second derivatives of $\Psi$ with respect to ${\vec{\alpha}},{\bf{w}}$ and ${\vec{\theta}}$. The computations can be performed for deep neural network encoders as defined in \autoref{eq:deep_approximation} in principle analogously, but are technically and notationally more complicated.
To make the notation consistent we define
\begin{equation*}{\vec{p}}\,{}:=({\vec{\alpha}},{\bf{w}},{\vec{\theta}})\in\mathds{R}^{N}\times\mathds{R}^{n\times N}\times\mathds{R}^{N}=\mathds{R}^{n_{*}}.\end{equation*}\par\begin{lemma}Let $\sigma:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}^{+}$ be a two times differentiable function with uniformly bounded
function values and first, second order derivatives, such as the sigmoid, hyperbolic tangent functions (see \autoref{fig:scheme}). Then, the derivatives of $\Psi$
with respect to the coefficients ${\vec{p}}\,{}$
are given by the following formulas:
\begin{itemize}\itemize@item@Derivative with respect to $\alpha_{s}$, $s=1,\ldots,N$:
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\alpha_{s}}[{\vec{p}}\,{}]({\vec{x}})&=\sigma\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{s}^{i}x_{i}+\theta_{s}\right)\text{ for }s=1,\ldots,N.\end{aligned}\end{equation}\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@Derivative with respect to $w_{s}^{t}$ where $s=1,\ldots,N$, $t=1,\ldots,n$:
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial w_{s}^{t}}[{\vec{p}}\,{}]({\vec{x}})&=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}\sigma^{\prime}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{j}^{i}x_{i}+\theta_{j}\right)\delta_{s=j}x_{t}=\alpha_{s}\sigma^{\prime}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{s}^{i}x_{i}+\theta_{s}\right)x_{t}\\
&\end{aligned}\end{equation}\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@Derivative with respect to $\theta_{s}$ where $s=1,\ldots,N$:
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\theta_{s}}[{\vec{p}}\,{}]({\vec{x}})&=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}\sigma^{\prime}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{j}^{i}x_{i}+\theta_{j}\right)\delta_{s=j}=\alpha_{s}\sigma^{\prime}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{s}^{i}x_{i}+\theta_{s}\right).\end{aligned}\end{equation}\end{itemize}Note, that all the derivatives above are functions in ${\mathbf{X}}=L^{2}([0,1]^{n})$.
In particular, maybe in a more intuitive way, we have
\begin{equation}D\Psi[{\vec{p}}\,{}]({\vec{x}}){\vec{h}}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial{\vec{\alpha}}}[{\vec{p}}\,{}]({\vec{x}})&\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial{\bf{w}}}[{\vec{p}}\,{}]({\vec{x}})&\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial{\vec{\theta}}}[{\vec{p}}\,{}]({\vec{x}})\end{pmatrix}^{T}{\vec{h}}\text{ for all }{\vec{h}}=\begin{pmatrix}{\vec{h}}_{{\vec{\alpha}}}\\
{\bf{h}}_{\bf{w}}\\
{\vec{h}}_{{\vec{\theta}}}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathds{R}^{n_{*}}\text{ and }{\vec{x}}\in\mathds{R}^{n}.\end{equation}Moreover, let $s_{1},s_{2}=1,\ldots,N$, $t_{1},t_{2}=1,\ldots,n$, then we have in a formal way:
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial^{2}\Psi}{\partial\alpha_{s_{1}}\partial\alpha_{s_{2}}}({\vec{x}})&=0,\\
\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi}{\partial\alpha_{s_{1}}\partial w_{s_{2}}^{t_{1}}}({\vec{x}})&=\sigma^{\prime}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{s_{1}}^{i}x_{i}+\theta_{s_{1}}\right)x_{t_{1}}\delta_{s_{1}=s_{2}},\\
\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi}{\partial\alpha_{s_{1}}\partial\theta_{s_{2}}}({\vec{x}})&=\sigma^{\prime}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{s_{1}}^{i}x_{i}+\theta_{s_{1}}\right)\delta_{s_{1}=s_{2}},\\
\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi}{\partial w_{s_{1}}^{t_{1}}\partial w_{s_{2}}^{t_{2}}}({\vec{x}})&=\alpha_{s_{1}}\sigma^{\prime\prime}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{s_{1}}^{i}x_{i}+\theta_{s_{1}}\right)x_{t_{1}}x_{t_{2}}\delta_{s_{1}=s_{2}},\\
\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi}{\partial w_{s_{1}}^{t_{1}}\partial\theta_{s_{2}}}({\vec{x}})&=\alpha_{s_{1}}\sigma^{\prime\prime}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{s_{1}}^{i}x_{i}+\theta_{s_{1}}\right)x_{t_{1}}\delta_{s_{1}=s_{2}},\\
\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi}{\partial\theta_{s_{1}}\partial\theta_{s_{2}}}({\vec{x}})&=\alpha_{s_{1}}\sigma^{\prime\prime}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{s_{1}}^{i}x_{i}+\theta_{s_{1}}\right)\delta_{s_{1}=s_{2}},\end{aligned}\end{equation}where $\delta_{a=b}=1$ if $a=b$ and $0$ else, that is the Kronecker-delta.
\@qedbox{}\end{lemma}The notation of directional derivatives with respect to parameters might be confusing. Note, that for instance
$\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\theta_{s}}[{\vec{p}}\,{}]({\vec{x}})$ denotes a direction derivative of the functional $\Psi$ with respect to the variable $\theta_{s}$ and this derivative is a function, which depends on ${\vec{x}}$. The argument, where the derivative is evaluated is a vector. So in such a formula $\theta_{s}$ has two different meanings. Notationaly differentiating between them would be exact but becomes quite unreadable.
\par\par\begin{remark}\begin{itemize}\itemize@item@In particular \autoref{eq:ca_dd} shows that
\begin{equation}\begin{pmatrix}{\vec{h}}_{{\vec{\alpha}}}&{\bf{h}}_{\bf{w}}&{\vec{h}}_{{\vec{\theta}}}\end{pmatrix}D^{2}\Psi[{\vec{p}}\,{}]({\vec{x}})\begin{pmatrix}{\vec{h}}_{{\vec{\alpha}}}\\
{\bf{h}}_{\bf{w}}\\
{\vec{h}}_{{\vec{\theta}}}\end{pmatrix}\text{ is continuous (for fixed ${\vec{x}}$) with respect to }{\vec{p}}\,{}.\end{equation}\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@We emphasize that under the assumptions of \autoref{le:sigma} the linear space (for fixed ${\vec{p}}\,{}$) \begin{equation*}\left\{D\Psi[{\vec{p}}\,{}]{\vec{h}}:{\vec{h}}=({\vec{h}}_{{\vec{\alpha}}},{\bf{h}}_{\bf{w}},{\vec{h}}_{{\vec{\theta}}})\in\mathds{R}^{N\times(n+2)}\right\}\subseteq L^{2}([0,1]^{n}).\end{equation*}\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@In order to prove convergence of the Gauss-Newton's method, \autoref{eq:newton}, by applying \autoref{th:deupot92}, we have to prove that $\Psi$ is a Lipschitz-continuous immersion. Below
we lack proving one important property so far, namely, that
\begin{equation}\partial_{k}\Psi[{\vec{p}}\,{}],\hskip 8.19447ptk=1,\ldots,n_{*}=N(n+2)\end{equation}are linearly independent functions. In this paper, this will remain open as a conjecture, and the following statements are
valid modulo this conjecture.
\end{itemize}\@qedbox{}\end{remark}In the following we survey some results on linear independence with respect to the coefficients ${\vec{\alpha}},{\bf{w}},{\vec{\theta}}$ of the functions
${\vec{x}}\to\sigma\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{s}^{i}x_{i}+\theta_{s}\right)$, which match the functions
${\vec{x}}\to\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\alpha_{s}}[{\vec{p}}\,{}]({\vec{x}})$, that is with respect to the first $N$ variables.
\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Linear independence of activation functions and its derivatives}
\par The universal approximation results from for instance \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Cyb89,HorStiWhi89,Hor91}{}{}]} do not allow to conclude
that neural networks function as in \autoref{eq:classical_approximation} are linearly independent.
Linear independence is a non-trivial research question:
We recall a result from \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Lam22}{}{}]} from which linear independence of a shallow neural network operator,
as defined in \autoref{eq:classical_approximation}, can be deduced for a variety of activator functions.
Similar results on linear independence of shallow network functions based on sigmoid activation functions have been stated in \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{TamTat97,Gua03}{}{}]}, but the discussion in \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Lam22}{}{}]} raises questions on the completeness of the proofs.
In \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Lam22}{}{}]} it is stated that all activation functions from the \emph{Pytorch library} \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{PasGroMasLerBra19}{}{}]} are linearly independent with respect to almost all parameters ${\bf{w}}$ and $\theta$.
\begin{theorem}For all activation functions \emph{HardShrink, HardSigmoid, HardTanh, HardSwish, LeakyReLU,
PReLU, ReLU, ReLU6, RReLU, SoftShrink, Threshold}, \emph{LogSigmoid, Sigmoid, SoftPlus, Tanh, and TanShrink} and
the \emph{PyTorch} functions \emph{CELU, ELU, SELU} the shallow neural network functions \autoref{eq:classical_approximation} formed by randomly generated vectors $({\bf{w}},{\vec{\theta}})$ are linearly independent.
\@qedbox{}\end{theorem}\begin{remark}\begin{enumerate}\enumerate@item@\autoref{th:lam_main}states that the functions $\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\alpha_{s}}$ (taking into account \autoref{eq:ca_d1}) are linearly independent for \emph{almost all} parameters $({\bf{w}},{\vec{\theta}})\in\mathds{R}^{n\times N}\times\mathds{R}^{N}$. In other words, the first block of the matrix is $D\Psi$ in \autoref{eq:DG} consists of functions, which are linearly independent for almost all parameters $({\bf{w}},{\vec{\theta}})$.
For our results to hold we need on top that the functions $\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial w_{s}^{t}}$ and $\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\theta_{s}}$
from the second and third block (see \autoref{eq:ca_dd}) are linearly independent within the blocks, respectively, and also across the blocks. So far this has not been proven but can be conjectured already from \autoref{fig:scheme}.
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\enumerate@item@For the sigmoid function we have \emph{obvious symmetries} because
\begin{equation}\sigma^{\prime}\left({\bf{w}}_{j}^{T}{\vec{x}}+\theta_{j}\right)=\sigma^{\prime}\left(-{\bf{w}}_{j}^{T}{\vec{x}}-\theta_{j}\right)\text{ for every }{\bf{w}}_{j}\in\mathds{R}^{n},{\vec{\theta}}\in\mathds{R}^{N},\end{equation}or in other words for the function $\Psi$ from \autoref{eq:classical_approximation} we have according to \autoref{eq:ca_d3} that
\begin{equation}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\theta_{s}}[{\vec{\alpha}},{\bf{w}},{\vec{\theta}}]({\vec{x}})=\alpha_{s}\sigma^{\prime}({\bf{w}}_{j}^{T}{\vec{x}}+\theta_{j})=\alpha_{s}\sigma^{\prime}(-{\bf{w}}_{j}^{T}{\vec{x}}-\theta_{j})=\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\theta_{s}}[{\vec{\alpha}},-{\bf{w}},-{\vec{\theta}}]({\vec{x}})\end{equation}or in other words $\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\theta_{s}}[{\vec{\alpha}},{\bf{w}},-{\vec{\theta}}]$ and
$\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\theta_{s}}[{\vec{\alpha}},-{\bf{w}},-{\vec{\theta}}]$ are linearly dependent.
\end{enumerate}\@qedbox{}\end{remark}\begin{conjecture}We define by $\mathcal{D}{(\Psi)}$ a \emph{maximal set of vectors} $({\vec{\alpha}},{\bf{w}},{\vec{\theta}})$ such that
the $n_{*}=N\times(n+2)$ functions in ${\vec{x}}$
\begin{equation*}{\vec{x}}\to\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\alpha_{s}}[{\vec{\alpha}},{\bf{w}},{\vec{\theta}}]({\vec{x}}),\hskip 8.19447pt{\vec{x}}\to\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial w_{s}^{t}}[{\vec{\alpha}},{\bf{w}},{\vec{\theta}}]({\vec{x}}),\hskip 8.19447pt{\vec{x}}\to\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\theta_{s}}[{\vec{\alpha}},{\bf{w}},{\vec{\theta}}]({\vec{x}}),\hskip 8.19447pts=1,\ldots,N,t=1,\ldots,n,\end{equation*}are linearly independent. We assume that $\mathcal{D}{(\Psi)}$ is open and dense in ${\mathbf{X}}\in L^{2}([0,1])^{2}$ The later is guaranteed by \autoref{le:LinearApproximation}. Recall the discussion above: The differentiation variables and the arguments coincide notationally, but are different objects.
\@qedbox{}\end{conjecture}\begin{remark}\begin{itemize}\itemize@item@It can be conjectured that for every element from $\mathcal{D}{(\Psi)}$ only one element in $\mathds{R}^{n_{*}}$ exists,
which satisfies \emph{obvious symmetries} such as formulated in \autoref{eq:antisym}. These ``mirrored'' elements
are a set of measure zero in ${\vec{P}}$. We conjecture that this corresponds to the set of measure zero as stated in \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Lam22}{}{}]}, which is derived with Fourier methods.
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@\autoref{eq:ca_dd}requires that all components of the vector ${\vec{\alpha}}$ are non-zero. This means in particular that for ``sparse solutions'', with less that $n_{*}=N*(n+2)$ coefficients,
convergence is not guaranteed, because of a locally degenerating submanifold.
We consider the manifold given by the function
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned} F:\mathds{R}^{2}&\to\mathds{R}^{2}.\\
\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}&\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}xy\\
x^{2}+y^{2}\end{pmatrix}\end{aligned}\end{equation}Then
\begin{equation*}\nabla F(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}y&x\\
2x&2y\end{pmatrix}.\end{equation*}We have $\det\nabla F(x,y)=2(y^{2}-x^{2})$, which vanishes along the diagonals in $(x,y)$-space. That is of the diagonals the function is locally a submanifold (see \autoref{fig:vis}):
\begin{figure}\begin{center}\includegraphics[width]{vis/vis.pdf}
\@@toccaption{{\lx@tag[ ]{{2}}{ The function $F$ from \autoref{eq:vis}. We have plotted $F(x,y)$ via its polar coordinates, I.e. $r=\left|F(x,y)\right|$ and $\theta=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{xy}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\right)$. The colors correspond to identical angles.
}}}\@@caption{{\lx@tag[: ]{{Figure 2}}{ The function $F$ from \autoref{eq:vis}. We have plotted $F(x,y)$ via its polar coordinates, I.e. $r=\left|F(x,y)\right|$ and $\theta=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{xy}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\right)$. The colors correspond to identical angles.
}}}\end{center}\end{figure}\end{itemize}\@qedbox{}\end{remark}\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Local convergence of Gauss-Newton's method with coding networks}
In the following we prove a local convergence result for a Gauss-Newton's method, for solving operator equations
\autoref{eq:ip_2} where $F$ is complemented by a shallow neural network coder $\Psi$. In order to apply \autoref{th:deupot92} we have to verify that the shallow neural network operator (see \autoref{eq:classical_approximation}) is a Lipschitz-differentiable immersion.
\par\begin{lemma}Let $F:{\mathbf{X}}=L^{2}([0,1]^{n})\to{\mathbf{Y}}$ be linear, bounded, with trivial nullspace and closed range, and let $\sigma$ be strictly monotonic (like sigmoid or hyperbolic tangent) and satisfy the assumptions of \autoref{le:sigma}. Moreover, assume that \autoref{co:independence} holds.
Then
\begin{enumerate}\enumerate@item@For every element ${\vec{p}}\,{}=({\vec{\alpha}},{\bf{w}},{\vec{\theta}})\in\mathds{R}^{n_{*}}$ in the maximal set
$\mathcal{D}{(\Psi)}$ (see \autoref{co:independence}),
$\mathcal{R}(D\Psi[{\vec{p}}\,{}])$ is a linear subspace of the space ${\mathbf{X}}$ of dimension $n_{*}=N\times(n+2)$.
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\enumerate@item@There exists an open neighborhood $\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathds{R}^{N\times(n+2)}$ of vectors $({\vec{\alpha}},{\bf{w}},{\vec{\theta}})$
such that $\Psi$ is a Lipschitz-differentiable immersion in $\mathcal{U}$.
\end{enumerate}\@qedbox{}\end{lemma}\begin{proof}\begin{itemize}\itemize@item@It is clear that for each fixed ${\vec{p}}\,{}$, $D\Psi[{\vec{p}}\,{}]\in L^{2}([0,1]^{n})$ because of the differentiability assumptions of $\sigma$, see \autoref{eq:d2}.
\autoref{co:independence} implies that $\mathcal{R}(D\Psi[{\vec{p}}\,{}])$ is a linear subspaces of ${\mathbf{X}}$ of dimension $N\times(n+2)$ (note the elements are functions).
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@$D^{2}\Psi[{\vec{p}}\,{}]:\mathds{R}^{N\times(n+2)}\to L^{2}([0,1]^{n})$ is continuous (see \autoref{eq:d2}) since we assume that the activation function $\sigma$ is twice differentiable. Now we consider a non-empty open neighborhood $\mathcal{U}$ of a vector ${\vec{p}}\,{}$, with a compact closure.
\begin{itemize}\itemize@item@Then, from the continuity of $D^{2}\Psi$ with respect to ${\vec{p}}\,{}$, it follows that $D\Psi$ is a Fr\`{e}chet-differentiable with Lipschitz-continuous derivative on $\mathcal{U}$. In particular this means that \autoref{it1:immersion} in \autoref{de:immersion} holds. Moreover, \autoref{eq:cl}
holds for $\Psi^{\prime}$. That is, there exists constants $C_{L}$ and $C_{I}$ such that \begin{equation}\left\|\Psi^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,{})-\Psi^{\prime}({\vec{q}}\,{})\right\|_{{\vec{P}}\to{\mathbf{Y}}}\leq C_{L}\left\|{\vec{p}}\,{}-{\vec{q}}\,{}\right\|_{\vec{P}}\text{ and }\left\|\Psi^{\prime}({\vec{p}}\,{})\right\|_{{\vec{P}}\to{\mathbf{Y}}}\leq C_{I}\text{ for }{\vec{p}}\,{},{\vec{q}}\,{}\in\mathcal{D}{(\Psi)}.\end{equation}\end{itemize}\end{itemize}\@qedbox{$\square$}\end{proof}Note that $\Psi^{\prime}(p)^{\dagger}$ as defined in \autoref{eq:MP_Penrose} is also uniformly bounded and Lipschitz-continuous as a consequence of \autoref{le:MPN}.
\par\begin{theorem}Let $F:{\mathbf{X}}=L^{2}([0,1]^{n})\to{\mathbf{Y}}$ be a linear, bounded operator with trivial nullspace and dense range and let $N=F\circ\Psi$, where $\Psi:\mathcal{D}{(\Psi)}\subseteq\mathds{R}^{N\times(n+2)}\to{\mathbf{X}}$ is a shallow neural network operator generated by an activation function $\sigma$ which satisfies the assumptions of \autoref{le:shallow} and \autoref{co:independence}.
Let ${\vec{p}}\,{}^{0}\in\mathcal{D}{(\Psi)}$ be the starting point of the Gauss-Newton's iteration \autoref{eq:newton} and
let ${\vec{p}}\,{}^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{D}{(\Psi)}$ be a solution of \autoref{eq:sol}, which satisfy \autoref{eq:h}.
Then the Gauss-Newton's iterations are locally, that is if ${\vec{p}}\,{}^{0}$ is sufficiently close to ${\vec{p}}\,{}^{\dagger}$, and quadratically converging.
\@qedbox{}\end{theorem}\begin{proof}The proof is an immediate application of \autoref{le:shallow} to \autoref{th:deupot92dg}.
\@qedbox{$\square$}\end{proof}\par\begin{remark}We have shown that a nonlinear operator equation, where the operator is a composition of a linear compact operator and a shallow neural network operator, can be solved with a Gauss-Newton's method with guaranteed local convergence in the parameter space.
\@qedbox{}\end{remark}\par\par\@@unnumbered@section{subsection}{Conclusion} We have shown that Gauss-Newton's methods are efficient algorithms for solving linear
inverse problems, where the solution can be encoded with a neural network.
The convergence studies, however, are not complete, and are based on a conjecture on linear independence of
activation functions and its derivatives.
