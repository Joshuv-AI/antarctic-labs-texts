Time-fractional Moore–Gibson–Thompson equations
Barbara Kaltenbacher 1 and Vanja Nikolić 2

Abstract
. In this paper, we consider several time-fractional generalizations of the Jordan–Moore–Gibson–Thompson (JMGT) equations in nonlinear acoustics as well as their linear Moore–Gibson–Thompson (MGT) versions. Following the procedure described in Jordan (2014), these time-fractional acoustic equations are derived from four fractional versions of the Maxwell–Cattaneo law in Compte and Metzler (1997). Additionally to providing well-posedness results for each of them, we also study the respective limits as the fractional order tends to one, leading to the classical third order in time (J)MGT equation.

1.
Introduction

It is well-known that using the Fourier temperature flux law, given by

(1.1)

\boldsymbol{q}=-\kappa\nabla\theta,

in the derivation of second-order models of nonlinear acoustics may lead to the so-called paradox of infinite speed of propagation; see
[
33
,
26
,
31
,
23
,
25
]
. As a remedy, the Maxwell–Cattaneo law may be used instead

(1.2)

\boldsymbol{q}+\tau\boldsymbol{q}_{t}=-\kappa\nabla\theta,

whereby a time lag
\tau>0
is introduced between the heat flux and the temperature induced by it. This change within the governing equations leads to the third-order in time sound propagation described by a family of Moore–Gibson–Thompson (MGT) equations in linear acoustics:

(1.3)

\displaystyle\tau\psi_{ttt}+\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-(\tau c^{2}+\delta)\Delta\psi_{t}=0

or Jordan–Moore–Gibson–Thompson (JMGT) equations in nonlinear acoustics:

(1.4)

\displaystyle\tau\psi_{ttt}+\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-(\tau c^{2}+\delta)\Delta\psi_{t}=f(\psi_{t},\psi_{tt},\nabla\psi,\nabla\psi_{t});

see the works of Moore and Gibson
[
35
]
, Thompson
[
42
]
, and Jordan
[
24
,
23
]
for a detailed insight into their derivation and physical background and
[
27
,
29
,
6
,
28
,
36
]
for a selection of results on their mathematical analysis.

However, a drawback of using the hyperbolic heat equation is that it may violate the second law
of thermodynamics; see, for example,
[
45
,
17
,
18
]
. Fractional generalizations of the heat flux law have emerged in the literature as a way of interpolating between the properties of the two flux laws; see, e.g.,
[
38
,
11
,
16
,
4
]
and the references contained therein. In
[
11
]
, Compte and Metzler proposed several generalized time-fractional heat-flux laws in the following form:

(1.5)

\displaystyle(1+\tau^{\alpha_{1}}\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha_{1}})\boldsymbol{q}(t)=-\kappa\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha_{2}}\nabla\theta,

where the choice of
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
arises from a particular anomalous diffusion process in complex media. In the present work, we derive and analyze the time-fractional (J)MGT equations that arise from the use of fractional temperature laws (
1.5
) in place of the standard heat-flux law within the governing equations.

One such model coming from the choice of fractional orders
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})=(1,1-\alpha)
in the generalized Cattaneo law (
1.5
) is given by

(1.6)

\tau\psi_{ttt}+\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-(\tau c^{2}+\delta{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha})\Delta\psi_{t}=f(\psi_{t},\psi_{tt},\nabla\psi,\nabla\psi_{t}),\ 0<\alpha\leq 1,

whereas the choice
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})=(\alpha,1-\alpha)
leads to

(1.7)

\tau^{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi_{tt}+\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi-\delta{\textup{D}}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\Delta\psi=f(\psi_{t},\psi_{tt},\nabla\psi,\nabla\psi_{t}),

where we assume that
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
. We refer to Section
2
below for the definition of
\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}
and details on the modeling and to Tables
1
and
2
for a complete list of the fractional models that are considered in this work. In particular, we analyze the time-fractional JMGT equations in terms of local-in-time solvability and the limiting behavior of their solutions as
\alpha\rightarrow 1^{-}
.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work dealing with the mathematical analysis of time-fractional MGT models. We point out that, on the other hand, (J)MGT equations with memories that involve smooth kernel functions represent an active field of research; see, e.g.,
[
34
,
9
,
13
,
3
,
12
,
7
]
and the references contained therein.

Our exposition is organized as follows. In Section
2
we derive four fractional versions of JMGT based on the four instances of (
1.5
) elaborated on in
[
11
]
. After a short Section
3
with mathematical notation and tools, we first in Section
4
focus on the version (
1.6
) of fixed highest order three. We prove its well-posedness in the linear as well as in the nonlinear case without gradient nonlinearity and justify the limit
\alpha\to 1^{-}
. Next, in Section
5
we provide a similar analysis for the other models, that have in common a
2+\alpha
leading derivative. This analysis works out with one exception, where the damping term is too weak to allow for varying coefficients or nonlinearities and whose linear version is analyzed separately in Section
7
based on a reformulation as a second-order wave equation. Before doing so, we return to (
1.6
) in Section
6
and provide well-posedness and the limit
\alpha\to 1^{-}
in its full version, including the gradient nonlinearity, which requires higher-order energy estimates.

2.
Modeling with generalized heat-flux equations

In this section, we consider the four general versions of the constitutive equation (
1.1
) proposed by Compte and Metzler in
[
11
]
and discuss the resulting acoustic equations. These time-fractional general flux equations (GFE) are as follows:

(GFE)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad(1+\tau^{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha})\boldsymbol{q}(t)=

\displaystyle\,-\kappa\nabla\theta;\hphantom{{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}}

(GFE I)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad(1+\tau^{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha})\boldsymbol{q}(t)=

\displaystyle\,-\kappa{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\nabla\theta;

(GFE II)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad(1+\tau^{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha})\boldsymbol{q}(t)=

\displaystyle\,-\kappa\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha-1}\nabla\theta;

(GFE III)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad(1+\tau\partial_{t})\boldsymbol{q}(t)=

\displaystyle\,-\kappa{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\nabla\theta,

where
\boldsymbol{q}
denotes the flux vector,
\theta
the absolute temperature, and
\kappa
is the thermal conductivity. A numerical study and comparison of the four resulting fractional heat equations has been performed in
[
45
]
in a one-dimensional setting. Although they can all predict negative temperatures, the fractional heat equation based on using (GFE I) appears to avoid this nonphysical behavior for
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
close enough to
1/2
.

Note that while Compte and Metzler
[
11
]
state the equations using the Riemann–Liouville fractional derivative, in the present work
\textup{D}_{t}^{\gamma}
always denotes the Caputo–Djrbashian fractional derivative:

(2.1)

\textup{D}_{t}^{\gamma}w(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\gamma)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\gamma}\textup{D}_{t}^{\lceil\gamma\rceil}w(s)\,\textup{d}s,\quad-1<\gamma<1;

see, for example,
[
32
, §1]
and
[
37
, §2.4.1]
for its definition.
Here
n=\lceil\gamma\rceil
,
n\in\{0,1\}
is the integer obtained by rounding up
\gamma
and
\textup{D}_{t}^{n}
is the zeroth or first derivative operator.

(fJMGT)
We begin by discussing the modeling with the first option; that is

(2.2)

\displaystyle(1+\tau^{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha})\boldsymbol{q}(t)=-\kappa\nabla\theta;

cf.
[
45
, Eq. (9)]
. We note that this modification of the heat-flux law is introduced
ad hoc
in
[
11
]
and then disregarded, however numerical studies of the resulting heat equation in
[
45
]
incorporate it as well, and so we include it here.

The derivation of the acoustic equation follows the steps taken in
[
24
, §4]
with now (
2.2
) in place of the Maxwell–Cattaneo law. This derivation employs a weakly-nonlinear approximation, which for our purposes can be restated as

(2.3)

\epsilon<<1,\quad\theta=O(\epsilon),\quad\tilde{K}=O(\epsilon),\quad\tau^{\alpha}=O(\epsilon),\quad|\mathfrak{e}|=O(\epsilon^{2}).

Here
\epsilon
is the Mach number,
\tilde{K}
is the dimensionless thermal diffusivity, and
\mathfrak{e}
the dimensionless entropy. Note that, compared to
[
24
]
, the condition
\tau^{\alpha}=O(\epsilon)
replaces
\tau=O(\epsilon)
here.

It is assumed that the sound wave propagates through a thermally conductive and relaxing liquid or gas with negligible viscosity. Starting from a one-dimensional setting, the governing system is first approximated by

(2.4)

\displaystyle\psi_{tt}+\tfrac{1}{2}\epsilon\partial_{t}(\psi_{x})^{2}-(1+(\gamma-2)s)[\psi_{x}s_{x}+(1+s)\psi_{xx}]=-\epsilon^{-1}\mathfrak{e}_{t},

where
\psi
is the acoustic velocity potential,
\gamma
the adiabatic index, and
s
is known as the condensation; see
[
24
, Eq. (44)–(49) and Eq. (53)]
. Upon employing
s\approx-\epsilon\psi_{t}
, one arrives at

(2.5)

\displaystyle\psi_{tt}+\tfrac{1}{2}\epsilon\partial_{t}(\psi_{x})^{2}-(1-(\gamma-2)\epsilon\psi_{t})[-\epsilon\psi_{x}\psi_{tx}+(1-\epsilon\psi_{t})\psi_{xx}]=-\epsilon^{-1}\mathfrak{e}_{t};

cf.
[
24
, Eq. (49)]
. From the entropy production law

\tilde{\kappa}{\mathfrak{e}_{t}}=-\tilde{K}\boldsymbol{q}_{x},

with
\tilde{\kappa}
being the dimensionless thermal conductivity, and the general heat flux law (
2.2
) in a dimensionless version

(2.6)

(1+\lambda_{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha})\boldsymbol{q}(t)=-\tilde{\kappa}\nabla\theta,

we then have the following entropy equation:

(1+\lambda_{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha})\mathfrak{e}_{t}=\tilde{K}\theta_{xx}.

After utilizing that
\theta\approx-\epsilon(\gamma-1)\psi_{t}
, we can rewrite it as

(2.7)

\displaystyle(1+\lambda_{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha})\mathfrak{e}_{t}=-\epsilon\tilde{K}(\gamma-1)\psi_{txx};

cf.
[
23
, Eq. (57) and (58)]
. Applying the relaxation operator
(1+\lambda_{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha})
to (
2.5
) and using (
2.7
) to eliminate
\mathfrak{e}
then leads to

(2.8)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}(1+\lambda_{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha})\left\{\psi_{tt}+\tfrac{1}{2}\epsilon\partial_{t}(\psi_{x})^{2}-(1-(\gamma-2)\epsilon\psi_{t})[-\epsilon\psi_{x}\psi_{tx}+(1-\epsilon\psi_{t})\psi_{xx}]\right\}\\
=\,\tilde{K}(\gamma-1)\psi_{txx}.\end{multlined}

With
\lambda_{\alpha}=O(\epsilon)
, by neglecting the
O(\epsilon^{2})
terms in the equation above, we arrive at

(2.9)

\displaystyle(1+\lambda_{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha})\psi_{tt}-\lambda_{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi_{xx}-(1-\epsilon(\gamma-1)\psi_{t})\psi_{xx}+\epsilon\partial_{t}(\psi_{x})^{2}=\tilde{K}(\gamma-1)\psi_{txx}.

Dividing this equation by
(1-\epsilon(\gamma-1)\psi_{t})
, using
(1-\epsilon(\gamma-1)\psi_{t})^{-1}\approx 1+\epsilon(\gamma-1)\psi_{t}
for
\epsilon<<1
and neglecting all
O(\epsilon^{2})
terms, yields

(2.10)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\lambda_{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi_{tt}+(1+\epsilon(\gamma-1)\psi_{t})\psi_{tt}-\psi_{xx}-\lambda_{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi_{xx}\\
\hskip 142.26378pt-\tilde{K}(\gamma-1)\psi_{txx}+\epsilon\partial_{t}(\psi_{x})^{2}=0.\end{multlined}

Extrapolating to a dimensionalized 3D model in a mathematically general form gives

(2.11)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\tau^{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi_{tt}+(1+2\tilde{k}\psi_{t})\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi-\delta\Delta\psi_{t}+\tilde{\ell}\partial_{t}|\nabla\psi|^{2}=0.\end{multlined}

Since the quadratic gradient nonlinearity present in this model corresponds to the one in the second-order Kuznetsov equation
[
33
]
, we will henceforth refer to (
2.11
) as the fractional JMGT–Kuznetsov equation, or the fJMGT–K equation for short.

Assuming local nonlinear effects can be neglected so that

(2.12)

|\nabla\psi|^{2}\approx c^{-2}\psi_{t}^{2},

we obtain

(2.13)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\tau^{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi_{tt}+(1+2k\psi_{t})\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi-\delta\Delta\psi_{t}=0.\end{multlined}

The above approximation corresponds to the one commonly used when reducing the Kuznetsov equation to the Westvervelt second-order model of nonlinear acoustics; cf.
[
25
, §2.3]
. For this reason, we will refer to (
2.13
) as the fractional Jordan–Moore–Gibson–Thompson–Westervelt equation, or the fJMGT–W equation for short. This approximation is appropriate when cumulative nonlinear effects dominate the local ones, which is the case, e.g., for sound propagation sufficiently far from the source in terms of wavelengths; see the discussion in
[
22
, Ch. 3, Section 6]
.

(fJMGT I)
As a second option, we employ the general heat-flux model given by

(2.14)

\displaystyle(1+\tau^{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha})\boldsymbol{q}(t)=-\kappa{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\nabla\theta;

see
[
11
, Eq. (14)]
and
[
45
, Eq. (10)]
. The use of this flux law is motivated in
[
11
]
stochastically by fractal time random walks. Retracing the derivation steps from before leads to the following equation:

(2.15)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}(1+\lambda_{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha})\left\{\psi_{tt}+\tfrac{1}{2}\epsilon\partial_{t}(\psi_{x})^{2}-(1-(\gamma-2)\epsilon\psi_{t})[-\epsilon\psi_{x}\psi_{tx}+(1-\epsilon\psi_{t})\psi_{xx}]\right\}\\
=\,\tilde{K}(\gamma-1){\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\psi_{txx}\end{multlined}

in place of (
2.8
). Neglecting the
O(\epsilon^{2})
terms then yields

(2.16)

\displaystyle(1+\lambda_{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha})\psi_{tt}-(1-\epsilon(\gamma-1)\psi_{t})\psi_{xx}-\lambda_{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi_{xx}+\epsilon\partial_{t}(\psi_{x})^{2}=\tilde{K}(\gamma-1){\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\psi_{txx}.

Analogously to before, dividing by
(1-\epsilon(\gamma-1)\psi_{t})
leads to

(2.17)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\lambda_{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi_{tt}+(1+\epsilon(\gamma-1)\psi_{t})\psi_{tt}-\psi_{xx}-\lambda_{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi_{xx}-\tilde{K}(\gamma-1){\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\psi_{txx}\\
+\epsilon\partial_{t}(\psi_{x})^{2}=0\end{multlined}

in place of (
2.10
). Then extrapolating to a general 3D equation gives

(2.18)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\tau^{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi_{tt}+(1+2\tilde{k}\psi_{t})\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi-\delta{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\Delta\psi_{t}+\tilde{\ell}\partial_{t}|\nabla\psi|^{2}=0,\end{multlined}

which we will call the fractional Jordan–Moore–Gibson–Thompson–Kuznetsov equation of type I, or the fJMGT–K I equation for short. By assuming local nonlinear effects can be neglected as before, we arrive at

(2.19)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\tau^{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi_{tt}+(1+2k\psi_{t})\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi-\delta{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\Delta\psi_{t}=0,\end{multlined}

which we will refer to as the fractional Jordan–Moore–Gibson–Thompson–Westervelt equation of type I, or just the fJMGT–W I equation.

(fJMGT II)

Thirdly, we employ the heat-flux model given by

(2.20)

\displaystyle(1+\tau^{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha})\boldsymbol{q}(t)=-\kappa\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha-1}\nabla\theta;

cf.
[
11
, Eq. (14)]
and
[
45
, Eq. (11)]
. This flux law is motivated in
[
11
]
by nonlocal transport theory with memory effects; that is, a nonlocal relation between the flux
\boldsymbol{q}
and temperature
\theta
:

(2.21)

\boldsymbol{q}(x,t)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathfrak{k}(t-s)\nabla\theta(x,s)\,\textup{d}s,

with a suitable choice of the kernel. Analogously to before, we can derive the following general fractional model:

(2.22)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\tau^{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi_{tt}+(1+2\tilde{k}\psi_{t})\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi-\delta{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi+\tilde{\ell}\partial_{t}|\nabla\psi|^{2}=0,\end{multlined}

which we will from now on refer to as the fractional Jordan–Moore–Gibson–Thompson–Kuznetsov equation of type II, or the fJMGT–K II equation for short. If the local nonlinear effects can be neglected, we obtain

(2.23)

\displaystyle\tau^{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi_{tt}+(1+2k\psi_{t})\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-(\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}+\delta){\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi=0,

which we will refer to as the fractional Jordan–Moore–Gibson–Thompson–Westervelt equation of type II, or the fJMGT–W II equation for short.

(fJMGT III)
Finally, we consider the wave-like acoustic models resulting from using the following flux law:

(2.24)

\displaystyle(1+\tau\partial_{t})\boldsymbol{q}(t)=-\kappa{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\nabla\theta;

see
[
11
, Eq. (18)]
and
[
45
, Eq. (12)]
. In
[
11
]
, this law is motivated by a delayed equation that may connect the flux to a generalized force

(2.25)

\boldsymbol{q}(t+\tau)=-\kappa{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\nabla\theta.

Here, weakly-nonlinear acoustic approximation is based on assuming that

(2.26)

\epsilon<<1,\quad\theta=O(\epsilon),\quad\tilde{K}=O(\epsilon),\quad\tau=O(\epsilon),\quad|\mathfrak{e}|=O(\epsilon^{2}).

Retracing our previous derivation steps then quickly leads to

(2.27)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\tau\psi_{ttt}+(1+\epsilon(\gamma-1)\psi_{t})\psi_{tt}-\psi_{xx}-\tau\psi_{txx}-\tilde{K}(\gamma-1){\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\psi_{txx}\\
+\epsilon\partial_{t}(\psi_{x})^{2}=0.\end{multlined}

Extrapolating to a dimensionalized 3D model yields

(2.28)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\tau\psi_{ttt}+(1+2\tilde{k}\psi_{t})\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\tau c^{2}\Delta\psi_{t}-\delta{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\Delta\psi_{t}+\tilde{\ell}\partial_{t}|\nabla\psi|^{2}=0,\end{multlined}

which we will henceforth refer to it as the fractional Jordan–Moore–Gibson–Thompson–Kuznetsov equation of type III, or the fJMGT–K III equation for short. If the local nonlinear effects can be neglected, we obtain

(2.29)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\tau\psi_{ttt}+(1+2k\psi_{t})\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\tau c^{2}\Delta\psi_{t}-\delta{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\Delta\psi_{t}=0.\end{multlined}

We will refer to this model as the fractional Jordan–Moore–Gibson–Thompson–Westervelt equation of type III, or the fJMGT–W III equation for short.

3.
Theoretical preliminaries

In this section, we gather several theoretical results from fractional calculus that will be useful later on. To simplify the notation, we often omit the spatial domain and the time interval when writing norms; for example,
\|\cdot\|_{W_{t}^{p,q}(L^{r})}
denotes the norm on
W^{p,q}(0,t;L^{r}(\Omega))
and
\|\cdot\|_{W^{p,q}(L^{r})}
denotes the norm on
W^{p,q}(0,T;L^{r}(\Omega))
.

Throughout the paper, we assume that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an open, bounded, and sufficiently smooth set, where
n\in\{1,2,3\}
. When writing solution spaces for
\psi
, we use the following notational convention:

(3.1)

\displaystyle{H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)}=

\displaystyle H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\cap H^{2}(\Omega),

\displaystyle{H_{\diamondsuit}^{3}(\Omega)}=

\displaystyle\left\{\psi\in H^{3}(\Omega)\,:\,\mbox{tr}_{\partial\Omega}\psi=0,\ \mbox{tr}_{\partial\Omega}\Delta\psi=0\right\}.

In the analysis, we will rely on the continuous embeddings

H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{4}(\Omega)
and
H^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Omega)
:

(3.2)

\displaystyle\|v\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\leq C_{H^{1},L^{4}}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad

\displaystyle v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C_{H^{2},L^{\infty}}\|\Delta v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad

\displaystyle v\in{H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)}.

We often write
x\lesssim y
instead of
x\leq Cy
. In such cases,
C>0
represents a generic constant that may depend on the medium parameters and the final time
T
, but does not depend on the order of differentiation
\alpha
.

Throughout the paper we make the following assumptions on the (constant) medium parameters:

(3.3)

\tau>0,\quad c>0,\quad\delta>0,\quad k,\,\tilde{k},\,\tilde{\ell}\,\in\mathbb{R}.

Coercivity estimates

When performing energy analysis, we will rely on the following two coercivity estimates.

•

[
2
, Lemma 1]
: For any absolutely continuous function
w
,

(3.4)

{w(t)}\textup{D}_{t}^{\gamma}w(t)\geq\tfrac{1}{2}(\textup{D}_{t}^{\gamma}w^{2})(t).

•

[
14
, Lemma 2.3]
; see also
[
43
, Theorem 1]
: For any
w\in H^{-(1-\alpha)/2}(0,t)
,

(3.5)

\int_{0}^{t}\langle\textup{I}^{1-\alpha}w(s),w(s)\rangle\,\textup{d}s\geq\cos(\tfrac{\pi(1-\alpha)}{2})\|w\|_{H^{-(1-\alpha)/2}(0,t)}^{2},

where
\textup{I}^{\gamma}
denotes the Abel integral operator:

(3.6)

\textup{I}^{\gamma}w(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\gamma-1}w(s)\,\textup{d}s,\quad\gamma\in(0,1)

and the negative norm is defined by

(3.7)

\|w\|_{H^{-\gamma}(0,t)}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+\omega^{2})^{-\gamma}|\hat{w}(\omega)|^{2}\,\textup{d}\omega,\quad\gamma>0,

with
\hat{w}
being the Fourier transform of the extension by zero of
w
to all of
\mathbb{R}
.

The Kato–Ponce inequality

The following product rule estimate holds:

(3.8)

\|fg\|_{W^{\rho,r}(0,T)}\lesssim\|f\|_{W^{\rho,p_{1}}(0,T)}\|g\|_{L^{q_{1}}(0,T)}+\|f\|_{L^{p_{2}}(0,T)}\|g\|_{W^{\rho,q_{2}}(0,T)}

for
0\leq\rho\leq\overline{\rho}<1
,

1<r<\infty
,
p_{1}
,
p_{2}
,
q_{1}
,
q_{2}\in(1,\infty]
, with
\frac{1}{r}=\frac{1}{p_{i}}+\frac{1}{q_{i}}
,
i=1,2
; see, e.g.,
[
20
]
.
We note that the fractional derivative
\partial_{t}^{\rho}=(I-D_{tt})^{\rho/2}

used in
[
20
]
is an isomorphism between
H^{\rho}(0,T)
and
L^{2}(0,T)
for any
\rho\in[0,1]
.

Limits with respect to the order of differentiation

Let
w:[0,T]\mapsto X
, where
X
is a Banach space equipped with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{X}
. It is known that the mapping
\alpha\mapsto{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}w(t)
is left-sided continuous at any integer
\alpha
, while it is discontinuous from the right side unless
w(0)=0
. This property is due to the following two identities that follow by integration by parts:

(3.9)

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 1^{-}}\left\|{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}w(t)-w_{t}(t)\right\|_{X}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 1^{-}}\left\|\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}w_{t}(s)\,\textup{d}s-w_{t}(t)\right\|_{X}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 1^{-}}\left\|\frac{t^{1-\alpha}w_{t}(0)}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}+\frac{1}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{1-\alpha}w_{tt}(s)\,\textup{d}s-w_{t}(t)\right\|_{X}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|w_{t}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}w_{tt}(s)\,\textup{d}s-w_{t}(t)\right\|_{X}=\,0,

as well as

(3.10)

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left\|{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}w(t)-w(t)\right\|_{X}=

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left\|\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}w_{t}(s)\,\textup{d}s-w(t)\right\|_{X}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|w(0)\right\|_{X};

see, for example,
[
37
, §2.4.1]
. These limits extend to
L^{p}(0,T;X)
under relaxed smoothness assumptions on
w
. Here and below we use the notation

(3.11)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}(t)=\frac{t^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)},\quad\mathfrak{g}_{0}(t)=1.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
. For any
w\in W^{1,p}(0,T;X){\,\cap\,W^{2,1}(0,T;X)}
, it holds that

(3.12)

\limsup_{\alpha\rightarrow 1^{-}}\left\|{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}w-w_{t}\right\|_{L^{p}(0,T;X)}=0.

For any
w\in W^{1,1}(0,T;X)
,

(3.13)

{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}w\rightharpoonup w_{t}\mbox{ in }L^{1}(0,T;X)\mbox{ as }\alpha\rightarrow 1^{-},

and

(3.14)

\limsup_{\alpha\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left\|{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}w-w+w(0)\right\|_{L^{p}(0,T;X)}=0\,.

Proof.

To prove (
3.14
), we rely on Young’s convolution inequality and Lebesgue’s Dominated Convergence theorem, which yields

(3.15)

\|\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}-\mathfrak{g}_{0}\|_{L^{p}(0,T)}\rightarrow 0\ \text{as}\ \alpha\to 0^{+},

with the
L^{1}
dominating function
t\mapsto\dfrac{t^{-p\alpha_{0}}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha_{0})^{p}}
for
\alpha\leq\alpha_{0}<1/p
(without loss of generality since
\alpha
tends to zero). Therefore, (
3.14
) holds via

(3.16)

\displaystyle\limsup_{\alpha\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left\|{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}w-w+w(0)\right\|_{L^{p}(0,T;X)}=

\displaystyle\limsup_{\alpha\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left\|(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}-\mathfrak{g}_{0})*w_{t}\right\|_{L^{p}(0,T;X)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\limsup_{\alpha\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left\|\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}-\mathfrak{g}_{0}\right\|_{L^{p}(0,T)}\left\|w_{t}\right\|_{L^{1}(0,T;X)}\,=0.

To show (
3.13
), we use the fact that for any
w\in W^{1,1}(0,T;X)
and
v\in L^{\infty}(0,T;X^{*})
where
X^{*}
is the dual space of
X
,

(3.17)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle\int_{0}^{t}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}(t-s)w_{t}(s)\,\textup{d}s-w_{t}(t),v(t)\right\rangle_{X}\,\textup{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\langle\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}(t-s)w_{t}(s)\,\textup{d}s,v(t)\rangle_{X}\,\textup{d}t-\int_{0}^{T}\langle w_{t}(t),v(t)\rangle_{X}\,\textup{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle w_{t}(s),\int_{s}^{T}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}(t-s)v(t)\,\textup{d}t\right\rangle_{X}\,\textup{d}s-\int_{0}^{T}\langle w_{t}(r),v(r)\rangle_{X}\,\textup{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle w_{t}(r),\Bigl(\int_{r}^{T}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}(t-r)v(t)\,\textup{d}t-v(r)\Bigr)\right\rangle_{X}\textup{d}r\ =:\int_{0}^{T}\psi(r)\textup{d}r,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{X}
denotes the dual pairing. From the identity

(3.18)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle\phi(r),\Bigl(\int_{r}^{T}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}(t-r)v(t)\,\textup{d}t-v(r)\Bigr)\right\rangle_{X}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle\int_{0}^{t}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}(t-s)\phi(s)\,\textup{d}s-\phi(t),v(t)\right\rangle_{X}\,\textup{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\left\langle t^{1-\alpha}\phi(0)+\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{1-\alpha}\phi_{t}(s)\,\textup{d}s-\phi(t),v(t)\right\rangle_{X}\,\textup{d}t\ \to 0\mbox{ as }\alpha\to 1^{-}

for any
\phi\in C^{1}(0,T;X)
, we conclude that
\Bigl(\int_{r}^{T}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}(t-r)v(t)\,\textup{d}t-v(r)\Bigr)
tends to zero pointwise almost everywhere on
(0,T)
and therefore so does the integrand
\psi
in (
3.17
).

On the other hand,
\psi
can be bounded by the
L^{1}(0,T)
function

(3.19)

r\mapsto(C_{T}+1)\|w_{t}(r)\|_{X}\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;X^{*})},

where we have estimated

(3.20)

\displaystyle\left\|\int_{r}^{T}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}(t-r)v(t)\,\textup{d}t\right\|_{X^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;X^{*})}\int_{r}^{T}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}(t-r)\,\textup{d}r=\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;X^{*})}\frac{(T-r)^{1-\alpha}}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}

and set

(3.21)

C_{T}:=\sup_{\alpha\in(\frac{1}{2},1]}\frac{T^{1-\alpha}}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}<\infty.

Thus by Lebesgue’s Dominated Convergence theorem, (
3.17
) tends to zero as
\alpha\to 1^{-}
. Since
v\in L^{\infty}(0,T;X^{*})
is arbitrary, we obtain weak convergence of
{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}w-w_{t}
to zero in
L^{1}(0,T;X)
.
If
X
is a Hilbert space, this computation can be repeated replacing dual pairings with inner products and yields weak convergence in the Hilbert space sense.

Finally, in case
w\in W^{1,p}(0,T;X)\cap W^{2,1}(0,T;X)
, (
3.12
) follows from

(3.22)

\displaystyle\limsup_{\alpha\rightarrow 1^{-}}\left\|{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}w-w_{t}\right\|_{L^{p}(0,T;X)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\limsup_{\alpha\rightarrow 1^{-}}\Bigl(\int_{0}^{T}{\|(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}*w_{t})(t)-w_{t}(t)\|_{X}}^{p}\,\textup{d}t\Bigr)^{1/p}=0\,,

where we have again used Lebesgue’s Dominated Convergence theorem.
Indeed,
(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}*w_{t})-w_{t}
(and therefore the
p
th power of its norm) tends to zero almost everywhere on
(0,T)
as
\alpha\to 1^{-}
, on account of the previous discussion.
Furthermore,

(3.23)

\|(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}*w_{t})-w_{t}\|_{X}^{p}\leq 2^{p-1}\|\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}*w_{t}\|_{X}^{p}+2^{p-1}\|w_{t}\|_{X}^{p}

can be further dominated by the
L^{1}(0,T)
function

(3.24)

2^{p}\max\{1,T\}^{p}\Bigl(\|w_{t}(0)\|_{X}+\|w_{tt}\|_{L^{1}(0,T;X)}\Bigr)^{p}+2^{p-1}\|w_{t}\|_{X}^{p}

due to

(3.25)

\displaystyle\|(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}*w_{t})(t)\|_{X}=\frac{t^{1-\alpha}}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\left\|w_{t}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}(1-\tfrac{s}{t})^{1-\alpha}w_{tt}(s)\,\textup{d}s\right\|_{X}

\displaystyle\leq C_{T}\left(\|w_{t}(0)\|_{X}+\|w_{tt}\|_{L^{1}(0,T;X)}\right);

cf. (
3.9
) with
C_{T}
as above.
∎

4.
Analysis of the equation with the third-order leading term

We begin our analytical considerations by looking at the fJMGT–W III equation

(4.1)

\tau\psi_{ttt}+(1+2k\psi_{t})\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\tau c^{2}\Delta\psi_{t}-\delta{\textup{D}}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\Delta\psi=f,

which has an integer-order leading term; cf. Table
1
. We intend to analyze it by setting up a fixed-point mapping. To this end, we, first of all, study the following linearization:

(4.2)

\tau\psi_{ttt}+(1+\sigma(x,t))\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\tau c^{2}\Delta\psi_{t}-\delta{\textup{D}}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\Delta\psi=f,\quad 0<\alpha\leq 1.

This is the linear fMGT III equation with a variable coefficient, which we assume is uniformly bounded; cf. Table
2
below. More precisely, we assume that there exist
\underline{\sigma}
,
\overline{\sigma}>0
, such that

(4.3)

\underline{\sigma}\leq\sigma(x,t)\leq\overline{\sigma}\ \mbox{ for all }\ x\in\Omega,\,t\in(0,T).

Note that since we study the local-in-time behavior in this work, we do not impose a non-degeneracy condition on
1+\sigma
. In the upcoming analysis, the crucial estimate involving fractional derivatives will be the following:

(4.4)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}({\textup{D}}_{t}^{2-\alpha}w,w_{tt})_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s\geq

\displaystyle\cos(\pi\alpha/2)\|w_{tt}\|_{H^{-\alpha/2}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2},

which follows by (
3.5
). To formulate the first well-posedness result, we introduce the solution space

(4.5)

\displaystyle\quad X^{\textup{low}}_{\textup{fMGT III}}=\,\left\{\vphantom{H^{-\alpha/2}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))}\right.

\displaystyle\psi\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)):\psi_{t}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\psi_{tt}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap H^{-\alpha/2}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\left.\,\textup{I}^{1-\alpha}\|\nabla{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\psi_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\in L^{\infty}(0,T),\ \psi_{ttt}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))\vphantom{H^{-\alpha/2}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))}\right\}

for
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and

(4.6)

\displaystyle X^{\textup{low}}_{\textup{fMGT III}}=W^{1,\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))\cap W^{2,\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap H^{3}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))

in case
\alpha=1
. We denote by
\|\cdot\|_{X^{\textup{low}}_{\textup{fMGT III}}}
the corresponding norm on this space. We claim that the fMGT III equation (
4.2
) has a unique solution in this space under suitable assumptions on the data and the variable coefficient.

Proposition 4.1

(Well-posedness of the fMGT III equation)
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
\sigma\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))
. Given
f\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
and

(4.7)

(\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\psi_{2})\in(H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),L^{2}(\Omega)),

there exists a unique
\psi
in
X^{\textup{low}}_{\textup{fMGT III}}
, such that

(4.8)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\langle\tau\psi_{ttt},v\rangle_{H^{-1},H_{0}^{1}}+((1+\sigma)\psi_{tt},v)_{L^{2}}\\
+(c^{2}\nabla\psi+\tau c^{2}\nabla\psi_{t}+\delta{\textup{D}}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\nabla\psi,\nabla v)_{L^{2}}=(f,v)_{L^{2}}\end{multlined}

for all
v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, a.e. in
(0,T)
, with
(\psi,\psi_{t},\psi_{tt})|_{t=0}=(\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\psi_{2})
. Furthermore, the solution satisfies the following estimate:

(4.9)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\|\psi\|^{2}_{W^{1,\infty}(H^{1})}+\|\psi_{tt}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|\psi_{ttt}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(H^{-1})}\\[5.69054pt]
\hskip 28.45274pt+\cos(\alpha\pi/2)\|\psi_{tt}\|^{2}_{H^{-\alpha/2}(H^{1})}+\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\textup{I}^{1-\alpha}\|\nabla{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\psi_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\end{multlined}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|\nabla\psi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\psi_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

where for
\alpha=1
, the
\cos(\alpha\pi/2)
term should be omitted.

Proof.

We focus in the proof on the case
\alpha\in(0,1)
since the case
\alpha=1
follows in a more straightforward manner. We perform the analysis by employing the standard Galerkin procedure to discretize the problem in space
[
15
, §7]
, with alterations needed to accommodate the third-order derivative and the fractional term. We approximate the solution by

(4.10)

\displaystyle\psi^{n}(x,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi^{n}_{i}(t)\phi_{i}(x),

where
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
are the eigenfunctions of the Dirichlet-Laplacian operator:

(4.11)

-\Delta\phi_{i}=\lambda_{i}\phi_{i}\ \text{ in }\Omega,\qquad\phi_{i}=0\ \text{ on }\partial\Omega.

Denote
V_{n}=\textup{span}\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n}\}
. The semi-discrete problem is given by

(4.12)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}(\tau\psi_{ttt}^{n},\phi_{j})_{L^{2}}+((1+\sigma)\psi_{tt}^{n},\phi_{j})_{L^{2}}\\
+(c^{2}\nabla\psi^{n}+\tau c^{2}\nabla\psi_{t}^{n}+\delta{\textup{D}}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\nabla\psi^{n},\nabla\phi_{j})_{L^{2}}=(f,\phi_{j})_{L^{2}},\end{multlined}

for all
j=1,\ldots,n
, with approximate initial conditions
(\psi^{n},\psi_{t}^{n},\psi_{tt}^{n})|_{t=0}=(\psi_{0}^{n},\psi_{1}^{n},\psi_{2}^{n})
chosen as
L^{2}
projections of
(\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\psi_{2})
onto
V_{n}
. In other words,

(4.13)

\displaystyle\psi_{0}^{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi^{n}_{0,i}\phi_{i}(x),\quad\psi_{1}^{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi^{n}_{1,i}\phi_{i}(x),\quad\psi_{2}^{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi^{n}_{2,i}\phi_{i}(x)

with

(4.14)

\xi^{n}_{0,i}=(\psi_{0},\phi_{i})_{L^{2}},\quad\xi^{n}_{1,i}=(\psi_{1},\phi_{i})_{L^{2}},\quad\xi^{n}_{2,i}=(\psi_{2},\phi_{i})_{L^{2}}.

Then we know that

(4.15)

\displaystyle\|\psi^{n}_{0}\|_{H^{1}}\leq

\displaystyle\|\psi_{0}\|_{H^{1}}\ \text{ and }\

\displaystyle\psi^{n}_{0}\rightarrow\psi_{0}\ \text{ in }\ H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),

\displaystyle\|\psi^{n}_{1}\|_{H^{1}}\leq

\displaystyle\|\psi_{1}\|_{H^{1}}\ \text{ and }\

\displaystyle\psi^{n}_{1}\rightarrow\psi_{1}\ \text{ in }\ H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),

\displaystyle\|\psi^{n}_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\leq

\displaystyle\|\psi_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\ \,\text{ and }\

\displaystyle\psi^{n}_{2}\rightarrow\psi_{2}\ \text{ in }\ L^{2}(\Omega);

cf.
[
39
, §7, Lemma 7.5]
.

(I)
Existence of an approximate solution.
We first show that for a given
n
, a unique approximate solution exists. With
\boldsymbol{\xi}=[\xi^{n}_{1}\ \xi^{n}_{2}\ \ldots\ \xi^{n}_{n}]^{T}
, the approximate problem can be rewritten in matrix form

(4.16)

\displaystyle\tau M\boldsymbol{\xi}_{ttt}+M_{\sigma}(t)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{tt}+c^{2}K\boldsymbol{\xi}+\tau c^{2}K\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}+\delta K{\textup{D}}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{f}

with the entries of matrices
M=[M_{ij}]
,
M_{\sigma}(t)=[M_{\sigma,ij}(t)]
,
K=[K_{ij}]
, and the vector
\boldsymbol{f}(t)=[f_{i}(t)]
given by

(4.17)

\displaystyle M_{ij}=(\phi_{i},\phi_{j})_{L^{2}},\quad

\displaystyle M_{\sigma,ij}(t)=((1+\sigma(t))\phi_{i},\phi_{j})_{L^{2}},

\displaystyle K_{ij}=(\nabla\phi_{i},\nabla\phi_{j})_{L^{2}},\quad

\displaystyle f_{i}(t)=(f(t),\phi_{i})_{L^{2}}.

We also introduce the vectors of coordinates of the approximate initial data in the basis:

(4.18)

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}=[\xi^{n}_{0,1}\ \xi^{n}_{0,2}\ \ldots\ \xi^{n}_{0,n}]^{T},\quad\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}=[\xi^{n}_{1,1}\ \xi^{n}_{1,2}\ \ldots\ \xi^{n}_{1,n}]^{T},\quad\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}=[\xi^{n}_{2,1}\ \xi^{n}_{2,2}\ \ldots\ \xi^{n}_{2,n}]^{T}.

Then by setting
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{ttt}
, we have

(4.19)

\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}+t\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}+\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2}(t-s)^{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}(s)\,\textup{d}s,

and we can restate the semi-discrete problem as a system of Volterra integral equations given by

(4.20)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\tau M\boldsymbol{\mu}(t)+M_{\sigma}(t)\left(\int_{0}^{t}\boldsymbol{\mu}(s)\,\textup{d}s+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\right)+c^{2}K\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}+t\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}+\frac{t^{2}}{2}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2}(t-s)^{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}(s)\,\textup{d}s\right)\\
+\tau c^{2}K\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}+t\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}+\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)\boldsymbol{\mu}(s)\,\textup{d}s\right)+\frac{\delta}{\Gamma(\alpha)}K\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}\left(\int_{0}^{s}\boldsymbol{\mu}(r)\,\textup{d}r+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\right)\textup{d}s\\
=\boldsymbol{f}(t)\hphantom{fill}\end{multlined}

for
t\in(0,T)
. By using Dirichlet’s formula

(4.21)

\displaystyle{\frac{\delta}{\Gamma(\alpha)}}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}\left(\int_{0}^{s}\boldsymbol{\mu}(r)\,\textup{d}r\right)\,\textup{d}s={\frac{\delta}{\Gamma(\alpha)}}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{r}^{t}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}\,\textup{d}s\right)\boldsymbol{\mu}(r)\textup{d}r,

we arrive at an equivalent reformulation

(4.22)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}(t)=\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}K_{\alpha}(t,s)\boldsymbol{\mu}(s)\,\textup{d}s,

where the first term on the right is defined as

(4.23)

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}(t)=

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}-\frac{1}{\tau}M^{-1}\left\{M_{\sigma}(t)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}+c^{2}K(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}+t\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}+\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2})+\tau c^{2}K(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}+t\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2})-\boldsymbol{f}(t)\right.\\
\left.+\frac{\delta}{\Gamma(\alpha)}K\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\,\textup{d}s\right\}\end{multlined}

and the kernel function is given by

(4.24)

\displaystyle K_{\alpha}(t,s)=

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}-\frac{1}{\tau}M^{-1}\left(M_{\sigma}(t)+\frac{1}{2}c^{2}K(t-s)^{2}+\tau c^{2}K(t-s)\right)\\
-\frac{1}{\tau}\frac{\delta}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}M^{-1}K(t-s)^{\alpha}.\end{multlined}

To arrive at the kernel expression, we have employed

(4.25)

\frac{\delta}{\Gamma(\alpha)}K\int_{s}^{t}(t-r)^{\alpha-1}\,\textup{d}r=\frac{\delta}{\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)}K(t-s)^{\alpha}.

Due to the
L^{\infty}(0,T)
regularity of the kernel
K_{\alpha}
on
D=\{(t,s):\ 0\leq s\leq t\leq T\}
and the fact that function
\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}
belongs to
L^{2}(0,T)
, vector equation (
4.22
) has a unique solution
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in L^{2}(0,T)
. This claim directly follows by considering (systems of) integral equations in
L^{2}(0,T)
instead of
C[0,T]
in
[
8
, Theorem 2.1.7]
; see also
[
21
, Theorem 4.2, p. 24 in §9]
. From (
4.19
), taking into account initial data, a unique
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in H^{3}(0,T)
and, in turn,
\psi^{n}\in H^{3}(0,T;V_{n})
exists.

(II)
A priori energy analysis.
We next focus on the derivation of the energy estimate, which goes through by testing the semi-discrete problem by
\psi^{n}_{tt}(t)\in V_{n}
. More precisely, we test (
4.12
) with
\xi^{n}_{tt}(t)
and sum over
j=1,\ldots,n
. After integrating over
(0,t)
, we at first obtain the identity

(4.26)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\frac{1}{2}\tau\|\psi_{tt}^{n}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big|_{0}^{t}+\frac{1}{2}\tau c^{2}\|\nabla\psi_{t}^{n}(s)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big|_{0}^{t}+\delta\int_{0}^{t}(\textup{D}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\nabla\psi^{n},\nabla\psi_{tt}^{n})_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}-\int_{0}^{t}(1+\sigma)\|\psi_{tt}^{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}(f,\psi_{tt}^{n})_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s-c^{2}(\nabla\psi^{n},\nabla\psi_{t}^{n})_{L^{2}}\Big|_{0}^{t}+c^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\psi_{t}^{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\textup{d}s.\end{multlined}

By Young’s
\varepsilon
-inequality, we have

(4.27)

\displaystyle c^{2}\int_{0}^{t}(\nabla\psi^{n},\nabla\psi_{t}^{n})_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s\leq\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}c^{2}(C(T)\|\nabla\psi_{t}^{n}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}+\|\nabla\psi^{n}_{0}\|_{L^{2}})^{2}+\varepsilon c^{2}\|\nabla\psi_{t}^{n}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

For
0<\varepsilon<\tau/2
, employing estimate (
4.4
) and Gronwall’s inequality leads to

(4.28)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\|\psi_{tt}^{n}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\psi_{t}^{n}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\cos(\pi\alpha/2)\|\nabla\psi_{tt}^{n}\|_{H^{-\alpha/2}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}\end{multlined}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\|f\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|\nabla\psi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\psi_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.\end{multlined}

Note that we can also use the estimate

(4.29)

\|\psi^{n}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{\textup{PF}}\|\nabla\psi^{n}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim T\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\|\nabla\psi_{t}^{n}(t)\|_{L^{2}}+{\|\nabla\psi^{n}_{0}\|_{L^{2}}}.

Additionally, standard arguments (cf.
[
15
, §7]
) lead to the bound

(4.30)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\|\psi_{ttt}^{n}\|^{2}_{H^{-1}}\,\textup{d}s\lesssim

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\int_{0}^{t}(1+\overline{\sigma})\|\psi_{tt}^{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\textup{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\psi^{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\textup{d}s\\
+\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\psi_{t}^{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\textup{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\|{\textup{D}}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\nabla\psi^{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\textup{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\textup{d}s.\end{multlined}

We can further estimate the fractional term on the right as follows:

(4.31)

\displaystyle\|{\textup{D}}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\nabla\psi^{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,t;L^{2})}=\|\textup{I}^{\alpha}\nabla\psi_{tt}^{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,t;L^{2})}\lesssim

\displaystyle\|\nabla\psi_{tt}^{n}\|^{2}_{X_{-\alpha}(0,t;L^{2})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|\nabla\psi_{tt}^{n}\|^{2}_{H^{-\alpha/2}(0,t;L^{2})},

where the first inequality follows by
[
19
, Theorem 1]
. Note that
\{X_{\beta}\}_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}}
represents a scale of Hilbert spaces of functions
(0,t)\mapsto L^{2}(\Omega)
; cf.
[
5
, §5]
and
[
19
, Lemma 8]
. The second inequality follows by the fact that

(4.32)

X_{\alpha}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega))\subseteq X_{\alpha/2}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega))=H_{0}^{\alpha/2}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega))

for
\alpha<1
and therefore
\alpha/2<\frac{1}{2}
, together with duality. Note also that, on account of estimate (
3.4
), we know that

(4.33)

\displaystyle(\nabla{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\psi^{n}_{t},\nabla\psi_{tt}^{n})_{L^{2}}=

\displaystyle(\nabla{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\psi^{n}_{t},\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\nabla\textup{D}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\psi_{t}^{n})_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\|\nabla{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\psi_{t}^{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\,\textup{I}^{1-\alpha}\|\nabla{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\psi_{t}^{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

Employing this estimate instead of (
4.4
) in the above derivation yields a uniform bound on
\displaystyle\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\textup{I}^{1-\alpha}\|\nabla{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\psi_{t}^{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}
, which will be needed in the proof of uniqueness.

(III)
Passing to the limit.
Thanks to the uniform bounds and (
4.31
), we have weak convergence of a subsequence, which we do not relabel, in the following sense:

(4.34)

\displaystyle\psi_{ttt}^{n}

\displaystyle\relbar\joinrel\rightharpoonup\psi_{ttt}

\displaystyle\text{ weakly}

\displaystyle\text{ in }

\displaystyle L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\psi_{tt}^{n}

\displaystyle\relbar\joinrel\rightharpoonup\psi_{tt}

\displaystyle\text{ weakly-$\star$}

\displaystyle\text{ in }

\displaystyle L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\psi_{t}^{n}

\displaystyle\relbar\joinrel\rightharpoonup\psi_{t}

\displaystyle\text{ weakly-$\star$}

\displaystyle\text{ in }

\displaystyle L^{\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\psi^{n}

\displaystyle\relbar\joinrel\rightharpoonup\psi

\displaystyle\text{ weakly-$\star$}

\displaystyle\text{ in }

\displaystyle L^{\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)).

Furthermore, we know that

(4.35)

\displaystyle\nabla\psi_{tt}^{n}

\displaystyle\relbar\joinrel\rightharpoonup\nabla\psi_{tt}

\displaystyle\text{ weakly}

\displaystyle\text{ in }

\displaystyle H^{-\alpha/2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle{\textup{D}}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\nabla\psi^{n}

\displaystyle\relbar\joinrel\rightharpoonup{\textup{D}}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\nabla\psi

\displaystyle\text{ weakly}

\displaystyle\text{ in }

\displaystyle L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\textup{I}^{1-\alpha}\|\nabla{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\psi_{t}^{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\relbar\joinrel\rightharpoonup\textup{I}^{1-\alpha}\|\nabla{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\psi_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\text{ weakly-$\star$}

\displaystyle\text{ in }

\displaystyle L^{\infty}(0,T).

We can thus pass to the weak limit in the usual way to conclude that
\psi
solves (
4.8
). Further, weak/weak-
\star
lower semi-continuity of norms implies

(4.36)

\displaystyle\|\nabla\psi_{tt}\|_{H^{-\alpha/2}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|\nabla\psi_{tt}^{n}\|_{H^{-\alpha/2}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2},

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\textup{I}^{1-\alpha}\|\nabla{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\psi_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\textup{I}^{1-\alpha}\|\nabla{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\psi_{t}^{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

and thus by passing to the limit in the energy estimate for
\psi^{n}
, we conclude that
\psi
satisfies (
4.9
).

(IV)
Attainment of the initial conditions.
We next show that
\psi
attains its initial conditions. By (
4.34
) and
[
46
, Lemma 3.1.7]
, we know that

(4.37)

\psi^{n}(0)\relbar\joinrel\rightharpoonup\psi(0)\text{ weakly}\text{ in }H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),

and since
\psi^{n}(0)\rightarrow\psi_{0}
in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, we have
\psi(0)=\psi_{0}
. Further,

(4.38)

\psi_{t}^{n}(0)\relbar\joinrel\rightharpoonup\psi_{t}(0)\text{ weakly}\text{ in }L^{2}(\Omega),

and thus
\psi_{t}(0)=\psi_{1}
as an equality in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Similarly,
\psi_{tt}(0)=\psi_{2}
as an equality in
H^{-1}(\Omega)
; that is,

(4.39)

\langle\psi_{tt}(0),v\rangle_{H^{-1},H^{1}}=\langle\psi_{2},v\rangle_{H^{-1},H^{1}},\quad\forall v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

(V)
Uniqueness.
To prove uniqueness, we should show that the only solution of

(4.40)

\displaystyle\tau\psi_{ttt}+(1+\sigma)\psi_{tt}-\tau c^{2}\Delta\psi_{t}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\delta{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\Delta\psi_{t}=0

with
\psi_{0}=\psi_{1}=\psi_{2}=0
in
X^{\textup{low}}_{\textup{fMGT III}}
is
\psi=0
. The issue, however, is that at this point we are not allowed to directly test (
4.8
) with
\psi_{tt}
due to its low regularity. Instead, in the spirit of
[
41
, §2.4]
, we will prove that such
\psi
satisfies

(4.41)

\displaystyle(\tau\psi_{ttt}-\tau c^{2}\Delta\psi_{t}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\delta{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\Delta\psi_{t},\psi_{tt})_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\gtrsim

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\tau\|\psi_{tt}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\tau c^{2}\|\nabla\psi_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+c^{2}(\nabla\psi,\nabla\psi_{t})_{L^{2}}\right.\\
\left.\hskip 85.35826pt+\frac{\delta}{2}\textup{I}^{1-\alpha}\|\nabla{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\psi_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\right\}-c^{2}\|\nabla\psi_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.\end{multlined}

This estimate combined with (
4.40
) implies that

(4.42)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\tau\|\psi_{tt}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\tau c^{2}\|\nabla\psi_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+c^{2}(\nabla\psi,\nabla\psi_{t})_{L^{2}}\right.\\
\left.\hskip 85.35826pt+\frac{\delta}{2}\textup{I}^{1-\alpha}\|\nabla{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\psi_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\right\}-c^{2}\|\nabla\psi_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim-((1+\sigma\psi_{tt},\psi_{tt})_{L^{2}},\end{multlined}

after which we can proceed as in the previous energy analysis to arrive at
\psi=0
. We note that if
\psi\in X^{\textup{low}}_{\textup{fMGT III}}
solves (
4.40
), then

(4.43)

\displaystyle\psi_{t}\in L^{2}(H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)),\quad\psi_{tt}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap H^{-\alpha/2}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)).

Furthermore, a bootstrap argument yields

(4.44)

\displaystyle\tau\psi_{ttt}-\tau c^{2}\Delta\psi_{t}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\delta{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\Delta\psi_{t}=-(1+\sigma)\psi_{tt}\in L^{2}(L^{2}).

We next construct a regularization of
\psi
which satisfies (
4.41
), following
[
41
, §2.4, Lemma 4.1]
. Let
\tilde{\psi}:\mathbb{R}\mapsto H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
be defined by

(4.45)

\displaystyle\tilde{\psi}=\begin{cases}\theta\psi,\ &\text{ on }(0,T),\\
0,\ &\text{ on }\mathbb{R}\setminus[0,T],\end{cases}

where
\theta:\mathbb{R}\mapsto[0,1]
is a
C^{\infty}
truncation function, equal to
0
on
\mathbb{R}\setminus[0,T]
and to
1
on some sub-interval of
(0,T)
. Then

(4.46)

\displaystyle\tilde{\psi}_{t}\in L^{2}(0,\infty;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)),\quad\tilde{\psi}_{tt}\in L^{2}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap H^{-\alpha/2}(0,\infty;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\tau\tilde{\psi}_{ttt}-\tau c^{2}\Delta\tilde{\psi}_{t}-c^{2}\Delta\tilde{\psi}-\delta{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\Delta\tilde{\psi}_{t}\in L^{2}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\Omega)).

We regularize
\tilde{\psi}
by
\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon}=\varrho_{\varepsilon}*\tilde{\psi}
, with
\varrho_{\varepsilon}
being a
C^{\infty}
mollifier. Then
\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}\mapsto H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
is a
C^{\infty}
function, which satisfies

(4.47)

\displaystyle(\tau\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,ttt}-\tau c^{2}\Delta\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,t}-c^{2}\Delta\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon}-\delta{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\Delta\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,t},\tilde{\psi}_{\,\varepsilon,tt})_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\tau\|\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,tt}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\tau c^{2}\|\nabla\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+c^{2}(\nabla\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon},\nabla\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,t})_{L^{2}}\right\}\\
-c^{2}\|\nabla\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\delta(\nabla{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,t},\nabla\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,tt})_{L^{2}}.\end{multlined}

Similarly to (
4.33
), we have

(4.48)

\displaystyle(\nabla{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,t},\nabla\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,tt})_{L^{2}}=\,(\nabla{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,t},\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\nabla\textup{D}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,t})_{L^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\textup{I}^{1-\alpha}\|\nabla{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

Therefore,

(4.49)

\displaystyle(\tau\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,ttt}-\tau c^{2}\Delta\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,t}-c^{2}\Delta\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon}-\delta{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\Delta\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,t},\tilde{\psi}_{\,\varepsilon,tt})_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\gtrsim

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\tau\|\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,tt}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\tau c^{2}\|\nabla\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+c^{2}(\nabla\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon},\nabla\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,t})_{L^{2}}\right.\\
\left.+\frac{\delta}{2}\textup{I}^{1-\alpha}\|\nabla{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\right\}-c^{2}\|\nabla\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.\end{multlined}

Thanks to (
4.46
), we know that

(4.50)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\ (\tau\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,ttt}-\tau c^{2}\Delta\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,t}-c^{2}\Delta\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon}-\delta{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\Delta\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,t},\tilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon,tt})_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\tau\tilde{\psi}_{ttt}-\tau c^{2}\Delta\tilde{\psi}_{t}-c^{2}\Delta\tilde{\psi}-\delta{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\tilde{\psi}_{t},\tilde{\psi}_{tt})_{L^{2}}.

We can thus pass to the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
in (
4.49
) to arrive at

(4.51)

\displaystyle(\tau\tilde{\psi}_{ttt}-\tau c^{2}\Delta\tilde{\psi}_{t}-c^{2}\Delta\tilde{\psi}-\delta{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\Delta\tilde{\psi}_{t},\tilde{\psi}_{tt})_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\gtrsim

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\tau\|\tilde{\psi}_{tt}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\tau c^{2}\|\nabla\tilde{\psi}_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+c^{2}(\nabla\tilde{\psi},\nabla\tilde{\psi}_{t})_{L^{2}}+\frac{\delta}{2}\textup{I}^{1-\alpha}\|\nabla{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\tilde{\psi}_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\right\}\\
-c^{2}\|\nabla\tilde{\psi}_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.\end{multlined}

By restriction to
(0,T)
, the same holds for
\theta\psi
, from which the claim follows.
∎

To formulate the second well-posedness result, we introduce a higher-order solution space:

(4.52)

\displaystyle X^{\textup{high}}_{\textup{fMGT III}}=W^{1,\infty}(0,T;{H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)})\cap W^{2,\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))\cap H^{3}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

We denote by
\|\cdot\|_{X^{\textup{high}}_{\textup{fMGT III}}}
the corresponding norm on this space. Under stronger regularity assumptions on the data and the coefficient
\sigma
, the fMGT III equation (
4.2
) has a unique solution in this space.

Proposition 4.2

(Higher regularity for the fMGT III equation)
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
\sigma\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap W^{1,4}(\Omega))
. Given
f\in L^{2}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))
and

(4.53)

(\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\psi_{2})\in{H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)}\times{H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)}\times H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),

there exists a unique solution
\psi\in X^{\textup{high}}_{\textup{fMGT III}}
, which solves (
4.8
) in the
L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
sense, and satisfies

(4.54)

\displaystyle\|\psi\|^{2}_{X^{\textup{high}}_{\textup{fMGT III}}}\lesssim\,\begin{multlined}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|\Delta\psi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\Delta\psi_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\psi_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.\end{multlined}

Proof.

The statement when
\alpha=1
follows analogously to
[
29
, Theorem 3.1]
. The proof in the case
\alpha\in(0,1)
can again be conducted by employing a Galerkin analysis in space. We only outline the derivation of the energy estimate, which follows by testing the semi-discrete problem by
-\Delta\psi^{n}_{tt}
. We omit the superscript
n
in the notation below. After integrating over
(0,t)
, we first obtain the identity

(4.55)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\frac{1}{2}\tau\|\nabla\psi_{tt}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big|_{0}^{t}+\frac{1}{2}\tau c^{2}\|\Delta\psi_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big|_{0}^{t}+\delta\int_{0}^{t}(\textup{D}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\Delta\psi,\Delta\psi_{tt})_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\int_{0}^{t}(\nabla f,\nabla\psi_{tt})_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s-\int_{0}^{t}((1+\sigma)\nabla\psi_{tt},\nabla\psi_{tt})_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s-\int_{0}^{t}(\psi_{tt}\nabla\sigma,\nabla\psi_{tt})\,\textup{d}s\\
-c^{2}(\Delta\psi,\Delta\psi_{t})_{L^{2}}\Big|_{0}^{t}+c^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\|\Delta\psi_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\textup{d}s.\end{multlined}

We can rely on the following estimate:

(4.56)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(\nabla f,\nabla\psi_{tt})_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s-\int_{0}^{t}(\psi_{tt}\nabla\sigma,\nabla\psi_{tt})\,\textup{d}s-c^{2}(\Delta\psi,\Delta\psi_{t})_{L^{2}}\Big|_{0}^{t}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}\|\nabla\psi_{tt}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}+\|\psi_{tt}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{4})}\|\nabla\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{4})}\|\nabla\psi_{tt}\|_{L_{t}^{2}(L^{2})}\\
+c^{2}\|\Delta\psi(t)\|_{L^{2}}\|\Delta\psi_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}+c^{2}\|\Delta\psi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}\|\Delta\psi_{1}\|_{L^{2}};\end{multlined}

see also
[
30
, Theorem 3.1]
. We further note that

(4.57)

\displaystyle\|\psi_{tt}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{4})}\|\nabla\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{4})}\|\nabla\psi_{tt}\|_{L_{t}^{2}(L^{2})}\leq C_{H^{1},L^{4}}\|\nabla\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{4})}\|\nabla\psi_{tt}\|_{L_{t}^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}

and that

(4.58)

\displaystyle\|\Delta\psi(t)\|_{L^{2}}\|\Delta\psi_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon}(\sqrt{T}\|\Delta\psi_{t}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}+\|\Delta\psi_{0}\|_{L^{2}})^{2}+\epsilon\|\Delta\psi_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

For fixed, small enough
\epsilon>0
, an application of Gronwall’s inequality thus yields (
4.64
), at first in a discrete setting. Additionally, we obtain

(4.59)

\displaystyle\|\psi_{ttt}\|_{L_{t}^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}\lesssim

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\|\psi_{tt}\|_{L_{t}^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|\Delta\psi\|_{L_{t}^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|\Delta\psi_{t}\|_{L_{t}^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}\\
+\|{\textup{D}}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\Delta\psi\|_{L_{t}^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|f\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}^{2},\end{multlined}

where, similarly to (
4.31
), we can further estimate the fractional term as follows:

(4.60)

\|{\textup{D}}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\Delta\psi\|_{L_{t}^{2}(L^{2})}\lesssim\|\Delta\psi_{tt}\|_{H_{t}^{-\alpha/2}(L^{2})}.

The rest of the arguments follow analogously to the proof of Proposition
4.1
. We point out that in this higher-order setting, we are allowed to test the homogeneous problem (
f=\psi_{0}=\psi_{1}=\psi_{2}=0
) directly with
\psi_{tt}
to prove uniqueness.

Note that for
\psi\in X^{\textup{high}}_{\textup{fMGT III}}
, thanks to the embedding
W^{1,\infty}(0,T;{H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)})\hookrightarrow C([0,T];{H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)})
, we know that
\psi\in C([0,T];{H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)})
. Likewise, we have

(4.61)

\psi_{t}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;{H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)})\cap C([0,T];H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)).

According to
[
41
, §2, Lemma 3.3]
, this implies that
\psi_{t}
is weakly continuous from
[0,T]
into
{H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)}
. Similarly, we can prove that
\psi_{tt}\in C_{w}([0,T];H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))
.
∎

We are now ready to prove a well-posedness result for the nonlinear fJMGT–W III equation.

Theorem 4.1

(Local well-posedness of the fJMGT–W III equation)
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
,
\tilde{T}>0
, and
\varrho>0
. Further, assume that
f\in L^{2}(0,\tilde{T};H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))
and that

(4.62)

\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(H^{1})}+\|\psi_{0}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{1}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{2}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\leq\varrho^{2}.

Then there exists
T=T(\varrho)\leq\tilde{T}
, such that the initial boundary-value problem

(4.63)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tau\psi_{ttt}+&(1+2k\psi_{t})\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\tau c^{2}\Delta\psi_{t}-\delta{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\Delta\psi_{t}=f\hskip-5.69054pt&&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\[2.84526pt]
&\psi=\,0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\[2.84526pt]
&(\psi,\psi_{t},\psi_{tt})=\,(\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\psi_{2})&&\mbox{in }\Omega\times\{0\},\end{aligned}\right.

has a unique solution
\psi\in X^{\textup{high}}_{\textup{fMGT III}}
, which satisfies

(4.64)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\|\psi\|^{2}_{X^{\textup{high}}_{\textup{fMGT III}}}\end{multlined}\lesssim\,\begin{multlined}\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(H^{1})}+\|\psi_{0}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{1}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{2}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}.\end{multlined}

Proof.

The proof follows by applying the Banach Fixed-point theorem to the mapping
\mathcal{T}:w\mapsto\psi
, where
\psi
solves the linearized equation (
4.8
) with
\sigma=2kw_{t}
and

(4.65)

w\in B_{R}:=\{w\in X^{\textup{high}}_{\textup{fMGT III}}\,:\|w\|_{X^{\textup{high}}_{\textup{fMGT III}}}\leq R,\ w(0)=\psi_{0},\,w_{t}(0)=\psi_{1},\,w_{tt}(0)=\psi_{2}\},

with
R>0
specified below. Note that

(4.66)

\displaystyle\|\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{\infty})}+\|\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}(W^{1,4})}\leq

\displaystyle 2C_{H^{2},L^{\infty}}|k|\|w_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}(H^{2})}+2C_{H^{1},L^{4}}|k|\|w_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}(H^{2})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle R.

Thus by employing estimate (
4.54
), where the hidden constant has the form
C_{1}\exp(C_{2}(R+1)\tilde{T})
, it immediately follows that
\mathcal{T}
is a well-defined self-mapping on
B_{R}^{\textup{W}}
, provided
R>0
is chosen so that

(4.67)

\sqrt{C_{1}\exp(C_{2}(R+1)\tilde{T})}\,\varrho\leq R.

Next, we prove that
\mathcal{T}
is strictly contractive. Note that we will prove contractivity with respect to the weaker norm
\|\cdot\|_{X^{\textup{low}}_{\textup{fMGT III}}}
; recall the definition of the space
X^{\textup{low}}_{\textup{fMGT III}}
in (
4.5
) for
\alpha\in(0,1)
and (
4.6
) for
\alpha=1
.

We take any
w^{(1)}
and
w^{(2)}
in
B_{R}^{W}
and set
\psi^{(1)}=\mathcal{T}w^{(1)}
and
\psi^{(2)}=\mathcal{T}w^{(2)}
. We also introduce the short-hand notation for the differences

(4.68)

\overline{\psi}=\psi^{(1)}-\psi^{(2)},\qquad\overline{w}=w^{(1)}-w^{(2)}.

Then we know that
\overline{\psi}
solves the linear equation

(4.69)

\tau\overline{\psi}_{ttt}+(1+2kw_{t}^{(1)})\overline{\psi}_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\overline{\psi}-\tau c^{2}\Delta\overline{\psi}_{t}-\delta{\textup{D}}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\Delta\overline{\psi}_{t}+2k\overline{w}_{t}\psi^{(2)}_{tt}=0

and has zero initial conditions. Employing the lower-order estimate (
4.9
) with
\sigma=2kw_{t}^{(1)}
and
f=-2k\overline{w}_{t}\psi^{(2)}_{tt}
yields the bound

(4.70)

\displaystyle\|\overline{\psi}\|_{X^{\textup{low}}_{\textup{fMGT III}}}\leq

\displaystyle\,\sqrt{C_{1}\exp(C_{2}(R+1)\tilde{T})}\|f\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\begin{multlined}2\sqrt{C_{1}\exp(C_{2}(R+1)\tilde{T})}|k|\|\psi_{tt}^{(2)}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{4})}\sqrt{\tilde{T}}\|\overline{w}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{4})}\end{multlined}

(4.72)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\theta\|\overline{w}\|_{X^{\textup{low}}_{\textup{fMGT III}}}.

Thus we can guarantee that
\theta\in(0,1)
and obtain strict contractivity of
\mathcal{T}
by decreasing
\tilde{T}
.

We note that the space
B_{R}
with the metric induced by the norm
\|\cdot\|_{X^{\textup{low}}_{\textup{fMGT III}}}
is a closed subset of a complete normed space; cf.
[
30
, Theorem 4.1]
. Existence of a unique solution in
B_{R}
then follows by Banach’s Fixed-point theorem.
∎

4.1.
Limiting behavior of the fJMGT–W III equation

We next discuss the limit with respect to the order of differentiation. Given
\alpha\in(0,1)
, under the assumptions of Theorem
4.1
, let
\psi^{\alpha}
be the solution of the fJMGT–W III equation:

(4.73)

\tau\psi^{\alpha}_{ttt}+(1+2k\psi^{\alpha}_{t})\psi^{\alpha}_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi^{\alpha}-\tau c^{2}\Delta\psi^{\alpha}_{t}-\delta{\textup{D}}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\Delta\psi^{\alpha}=f.

Let
\psi
solve the corresponding JMGT–Westervelt equation obtained by setting
\alpha=1
above. Then the difference
\overline{\psi}=\psi^{\alpha}-\psi
solves

(4.74)

\displaystyle\tau\overline{\psi}_{ttt}+(1+2k\psi_{t}^{\alpha})\overline{\psi}_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\overline{\psi}-\tau c^{2}\Delta\overline{\psi}_{t}-\delta\textup{D}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\Delta\overline{\psi}_{t}+2k\overline{\psi}_{t}\psi_{tt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta(\textup{D}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\Delta\psi_{t}-\Delta\psi_{t}).

Similarly to the proof of Proposition
4.1
, testing with
\overline{\psi}_{tt}
(which we are allowed to do in this higher-regularity setting) leads to

(4.75)

\displaystyle\|\overline{\psi}_{tt}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\overline{\psi}_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim\,\|\textup{D}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\nabla\psi_{t}-\nabla\psi_{t}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}.

By recalling Lemma
3.1
, we find that if
\psi_{t}(0)=0
, then

(4.76)

\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 1^{-}}\|\textup{D}_{t}^{1-\alpha}\nabla\psi_{t}-\nabla\psi_{t}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}=0,

and thus arrive at the following result.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
4.1
hold with
\psi_{1}=0
. Let
\{\psi^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in(0,1)}
be the family of solutions to the
fJMGT–W III
equation and let
\psi
solve the corresponding
JMGT
equation with
\alpha=1
. Then
\psi^{\alpha}
converges to
\psi
in
W^{1,\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))\cap W^{2,\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
as
\alpha\rightarrow 1^{-}
.

5.
Analysis of the equations with the fractional leading term

We next discuss to what extent the analysis we have performed for the fJMGT–W III equation carries over to the other versions. To this end, the crucial question is whether the Galerkin approximation procedure based on energy estimates is feasible for a linearized equation:

(5.1)

\tau^{\alpha}\textup{D}_{t}^{2+\alpha}\psi+(1+\sigma)\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi-\delta\textup{D}_{t}^{\beta}\Delta\psi=f\,,

with

(5.2)

\beta=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{ for fMGT},\\
2-\alpha&\mbox{ for fMGT I},\\
\alpha&\mbox{ for fMGT II},\end{cases}

where for the respective nonlinear versions of Westervelt type we have
\sigma=2k\psi_{t}
in mind. These three cases have in common the fact that, unlike with the fMGT III equation, the leading-order time derivative is fractional and varies with
\alpha
.

The analysis of the fMGT and fMGT I equations follows similar lines of reasoning. We thus present the proof for the fMGT I equation with details and only outline the main arguments in the analysis of the fMGT model. To facilitate the analysis, we assume that
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
, which, at least for the equation based on employing (GFE I), appears to be the physically justified range according to the numerical experiments performed in
[
45
]
.

The analysis of the fMGT II equation cannot be carried out in the same manner; we explain why in Remark
1
below and a offer different way of analyzing it when
\sigma=0
in Section
7
.

5.1.
Analysis of the fJMGT–W I equation

In this section we consider the equation

(5.3)

\tau^{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi_{tt}+(1+2k\psi_{t})\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi-\delta{\textup{D}}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\Delta\psi=f.

To carry out the analysis starting from a linearization, we need to assume that the coefficient
\sigma
is small enough in a suitable norm. To this end, for
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
, we introduce the space

(5.4)

X^{\sigma}_{\textup{fMGT I}}=L^{2}(0,T;(W^{1,3}\cap L^{\infty})(\Omega))

equipped with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{X^{\sigma}_{\textup{fMGT I}}}
. Further, the solution space for
\psi
will be

(5.5)

\displaystyle\quad X_{\textup{fMGT I}}=\,\left\{\psi\in H^{2+\alpha}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)):\right.

\displaystyle\left.{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi\in L^{\infty}(0,T;{H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)}),\right.

\displaystyle\left.\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))\right\},

equipped with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{X_{\textup{fMGT I}}}
, which is the space induced by the part of the energy that can be bounded by a uniform constant independent of
\alpha
; see estimate (
5.10
) in Proposition
5.1
below.

Since the coefficient
\sigma
acts as a placeholder for
2k\psi_{t}
, we note that

(5.6)

\|2k\psi_{t}\|_{X^{\sigma}_{\textup{fMGT I}}}\lesssim\|\psi\|_{X_{\textup{fMGT I}}},

due to interpolation

(5.7)

\displaystyle H^{\alpha}(0,T;{H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)})\cap H^{1+\alpha}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))\subseteq

\displaystyle H^{\alpha+\theta}(0,T;H^{1+(1-\theta)}(\Omega))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H^{1}(0,T;H^{1+\alpha}(\Omega))

with
\theta=1-\alpha
and continuity of the embedding
H^{1+\alpha}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow(W^{1,3}\cap L^{\infty})(\Omega)
.

We are now ready to analyze equation (
5.1
) for
\beta=2-\alpha
.

Proposition 5.1

(Well-posedness of the fMGT I equation)
.

Let
\alpha\in[\alpha_{0},1)
for some
\alpha_{0}>1/2
. Assume that
f\in H^{\alpha-1/2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
,
\sigma\in X^{\sigma}_{\textup{fMGT I}}
, and

(5.8)

(\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\psi_{2})\in({H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)},{H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)},H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)).

There exists
\varrho>0
, independent of
\alpha
,
such that if

(5.9)

\|\sigma\|_{X^{\sigma}_{\textup{fMGT I}}}\leq\varrho,

then there is a unique
\psi\in X_{\textup{fMGT I}}
, which satisfies the
fMGT I
equation in the
L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
sense with
(\psi,\psi_{t},\psi_{tt})|_{t=0}=(\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\psi_{2})
. Furthermore, this solution fulfills the following estimate:

(5.10)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\|\textup{D}_{t}^{2+\alpha}\psi\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|\nabla\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\Delta\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\\
+C(\alpha)\Bigl(\|\nabla\psi_{tt}\|_{{H_{t}^{-(1-\alpha)/2}}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|\textup{D}_{t}^{3/2}\Delta\psi\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}^{2}\Bigr)\end{multlined}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|f\|_{H^{\alpha-1/2}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|\Delta\psi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\Delta\psi_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\psi_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

where
C(\alpha)\to 0
as
\alpha\to 1^{-}
.

Proof.

The proof follows by discretizing the problem with respect to the spatial variable, using smooth eigenfunctions of the Dirichlet-Laplacian as the basis.

(I)
Existence of an approximate solution.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
fixed, we first prove that the semi-discrete problem has a unique solution. We employ the same notation as in the proof of Proposition
4.1
; that is,

(5.11)

\displaystyle\psi^{n}(x,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi^{n}_{i}(t)\phi_{i}(x),\quad\psi_{j}^{n}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi^{n}_{j,i}\phi_{i}(x),\quad j\in\{0,1,2\}.

Using the mass matrices
M
and
M_{\sigma}=M_{\sigma}(t)
, the stiffness matrix
K
, and the source vector
\boldsymbol{f}
defined in (
4.17
), the next step is to rewrite the discretized problem as a system of integral Volterra equations. To this end, let

(5.12)

\boldsymbol{\mu}=\textup{D}_{t}^{2+\alpha}\boldsymbol{\xi}\qquad\text{and}\qquad p^{\gamma}(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma+1)}t^{\gamma}.

We can rely on the following identities:

(5.13)

{\textup{I}}^{\gamma}w=p^{\gamma-1}*w,\qquad{\textup{I}}^{\gamma}p^{i}=p^{\gamma+i},\qquad{\textup{I}}^{\gamma}{\textup{I}}^{s}={\textup{I}}^{\gamma+s}

to rewrite the vector solution and its derivatives as

(5.14)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}_{tt}

\displaystyle={\textup{I}}^{1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{ttt}+p^{0}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}={\textup{I}}^{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\mu}+p^{0}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}=p^{\alpha-1}*\boldsymbol{\mu}+p^{0}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}

\displaystyle={\textup{I}}^{1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{tt}+p^{0}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}=\textup{I}^{1+\alpha}\boldsymbol{\mu}+p^{1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}+p^{0}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}=p^{\alpha}*\boldsymbol{\mu}+p^{1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}+p^{0}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}

\displaystyle={\textup{I}}^{1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}+p^{0}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}=\textup{I}^{2+\alpha}\boldsymbol{\mu}+p^{2}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}+p^{1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}+p^{0}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}=p^{\alpha+1}*\boldsymbol{\mu}+p^{2}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}+p^{1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}+p^{0}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}.

Furthermore, we can rewrite the fractional derivatives as

(5.15)

\displaystyle\textup{D}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\boldsymbol{\xi}

\displaystyle={\textup{I}}^{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{tt}={\textup{I}}^{2\alpha}\boldsymbol{\mu}+{\textup{I}}^{\alpha}p^{0}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}=p^{2\alpha-1}*\boldsymbol{\mu}+p^{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2},

\displaystyle\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\xi}

\displaystyle=\textup{I}^{1-\alpha}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}=I^{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\textup{I}^{1-\alpha}p^{1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}+\textup{I}^{1-\alpha}p^{0}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}=p^{1}*\boldsymbol{\mu}+p^{2-\alpha}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}+p^{1-\alpha}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}.

Therefore, the semi-discrete problem can be equivalently rewritten as a system of Volterra integral equations:

(5.16)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\tau^{\alpha}M\boldsymbol{\mu}+M_{\sigma}(t)\Bigl(p^{\alpha-1}*\boldsymbol{\mu}+p^{0}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\Bigr)\\
+c^{2}K\Bigl(p^{\alpha+1}*\boldsymbol{\mu}+p^{2}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}+p^{1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}+p^{0}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}\Bigr)\\
+\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}K\Bigl(p^{1}*\boldsymbol{\mu}+p^{2-\alpha}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}+p^{1-\alpha}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\Bigr)+\delta K(p^{2\alpha-1}*\boldsymbol{\mu}+p^{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2})=f.\end{multlined}

Thus, unique solvability of this system in
L^{2}(0,T)
follows from
[
21
, Theorem 4.2, p. 241 in §9]
. Then from

(5.17)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{tt}=\boldsymbol{\mu}\in L^{2}(0,T),\quad\alpha\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1)\\
&\boldsymbol{\xi}_{tt}(0)=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\end{aligned}\right.

we have a unique
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{tt}\in H^{\alpha}(0,T)
; cf.
[
32
, §3.3]
. Combined with the initial conditions
(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1})
, this yields a unique
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in H^{2+\alpha}(0,T)
and further implies the existence of a unique
\psi^{n}\in H^{2+\alpha}(0,T;V_{n})
.

(II)
A priori energy analysis.
We next focus on deriving a uniform energy estimate for
\psi^{n}
. We will make use of estimate (
3.5
) to treat the fractional terms; that is,

(5.18)

\int_{0}^{t}\langle\textup{I}^{\rho}w(s),w(s)\rangle\,\textup{d}s\geq\cos(\tfrac{\pi\rho}{2})\|w\|_{H^{-\rho/2}(0,t)}^{2}

for
\rho\in(0,1)
, as well as the identity
\int_{0}^{t}\langle w_{t}(s),w(s)\rangle\,\textup{d}s=\frac{1}{2}|w|^{2}\,\big|_{0}^{t}
. Thus, the rule of thumb is that for a coercivity estimate on
\int_{0}^{t}\textup{D}_{t}^{r}(s)w\,\textup{D}_{t}^{\rho}w(s)\,\textup{d}s
to yield a non-negative lower bound (up to initial data), the difference
|r-\rho|
between the fractional orders must not exceed one. We will consider the multiplier

(5.19)

-\Delta\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi^{n}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\xi^{n}_{i}(t)\,\lambda_{i}\phi_{i}(x)\in V_{n},

for which this rule applies and yields non-negative contributions on the left-hand side for the terms containing
\textup{D}_{t}^{2+\alpha}\psi^{n}
,
\psi_{tt}^{n}
,
-\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n}
, and
-\textup{D}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n}
. Multiplying the semi-discrete equation with
-\Delta\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi^{n}
and integrating over space and
(0,t)
at first leads to

(5.20)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}-\tau^{\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}(\textup{D}_{t}^{2+\alpha}\psi^{n},\Delta\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi^{n})_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s-\int_{0}^{t}(\psi_{tt}^{n},\Delta\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi^{n})_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s\\
+\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}\int_{0}^{t}({\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n},\Delta\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi^{n})_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s+\delta\int_{0}^{t}(\textup{D}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n},\Delta\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi^{n})_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}-\int_{0}^{t}(f-\sigma\psi_{tt}^{n}+c^{2}\Delta\psi^{n},\Delta\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi^{n})\,\textup{d}s.\end{multlined}

We next exchange the order of differentiation in the first and third term on the left as follows:

(5.21)

\textup{D}_{t}^{2+\alpha}\psi^{n}=\textup{D}_{t}\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi^{n}-p^{-\alpha}\psi^{n}_{tt}(0)\,,\qquad\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi^{n}=\textup{D}_{t}\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi^{n}-p^{-\alpha}\psi^{n}_{t}(0)\,,

and integrate by parts to obtain

(5.22)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n}(s),p^{-\alpha}(s)\Delta\psi_{t}^{n}(0))_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}-\int_{0}^{t}(\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n}(s)+p^{-\alpha}(s)\Delta\psi_{t}^{n}(0),p^{1-\alpha}\Delta\psi_{t}^{n}(0))_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s\\
+p^{1-\alpha}(t)(\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n}(t),\Delta\psi_{t}^{n}(0))_{L^{2}}\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}(\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n}(s),p^{1-\alpha}(s)\Delta\psi_{t}^{n}(0))_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s+h_{0}(t),

where we have introduced

(5.23)

h_{0}(t)=\frac{t^{2-2\alpha}}{2\Gamma(2-2\alpha)^{2}}\|\Delta\psi_{t}^{n}(0)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+p^{1-\alpha}(t)(\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n}(t),\Delta\psi_{t}^{n}(0))_{L^{2}}.

Thus, we arrive at the following identity:

(5.24)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}-\tau^{\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}(\textup{D}_{t}\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi^{n},\Delta\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi^{n})_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s-\int_{0}^{t}(\psi_{tt}^{n},\Delta\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi^{n})_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s\\
+\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}\int_{0}^{t}({\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n},\Delta\textup{D}_{t}\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi^{n})_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s+\delta\int_{0}^{t}(\textup{D}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n},\Delta\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi^{n})_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}-\int_{0}^{t}(f-\sigma\psi_{tt}^{n}+c^{2}\Delta\psi^{n}+\tau^{\alpha}p^{-\alpha}\psi_{tt}^{n}(0)-\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}p^{1-\alpha}\Delta\psi_{t}^{n}(0),\Delta\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi^{n})_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s\\
\hskip-142.26378pt+\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}h_{0}(t).\end{multlined}

We note that

(5.25)

\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi^{n}=\psi_{tt}^{n}=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega

with our choice of the basis functions. Additionally using
\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi^{n}=\textup{I}^{1-\alpha}\psi^{n}_{tt}
in the second term on the left and integrating by parts in space and time yields

(5.26)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\,\frac{\tau^{\alpha}}{2}\|\nabla\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi^{n}(s)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\big|_{0}^{t}+\int_{0}^{t}(\nabla\psi_{tt}^{n}(s),\textup{I}^{1-\alpha}\nabla\psi_{tt}^{n}(s))_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s\\
+\frac{\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}}{2}\|\Delta\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi^{n}(s)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big|_{0}^{t}+\delta d\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}(\tilde{f}(s),\Delta\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi^{n}(s))_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s+\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}h_{0}(t),

where we have introduced the short-hand notation

(5.27)

\tilde{f}=f+c^{2}\Delta\psi^{n}-\sigma\psi_{tt}^{n}+\tau^{\alpha}\left(p^{-\alpha}\psi_{tt}^{n}(0)-c^{2}p^{1-\alpha}\Delta\psi_{t}^{n}(0)\right)

and

(5.28)

d=\int_{0}^{t}(\textup{D}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n},\Delta\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi^{n})_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s.

By the identity

(5.29)

\displaystyle\textup{D}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\psi^{n}=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\textup{I}}^{\alpha}\psi_{tt}=\textup{I}^{2\alpha-1}\textup{I}^{1-\alpha}\psi_{tt}^{n}=\textup{I}^{2\alpha-1}\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi^{n},

we know that

(5.30)

\displaystyle d=\int_{0}^{t}(\textup{I}^{2\alpha-1}\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n}(s),\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n}(s))_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s.

Since
\alpha>1/2
, this term can be estimated from below using the fact that
\textup{I}^{\gamma}:H^{-\gamma}(0,t)\to L^{2}(0,T)
is an isomorphism for
\gamma\in[0,1/2)
; see
[
19
, Theorem 1]
. Therefore,

(5.31)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{\cos(\pi(\alpha-1/2))}\geq

\displaystyle\|\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n}\|_{H_{t}^{1/2-\alpha}(L^{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\|\textup{I}^{\alpha-1/2}\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}^{2}\sim\|\textup{D}_{t}^{3/2}\Delta\psi^{n}\|_{L_{t}^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}.

We estimate the
\tilde{f}
term on the right-hand side of (
5.26
) with a view on the possibility of bounding it by
d
as follows:

(5.32)

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}(\tilde{f}(s),\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n}(s))_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s\right|\leq\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\|\tilde{f}\|_{H^{\alpha-1/2}_{t}(L^{2})}^{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n}\|_{H^{1/2-\alpha}_{t}(L^{2})}^{2}.

It remains to estimate the terms within
\tilde{f}
in this norm; that is, to bound

(5.33)

\|f+c^{2}\Delta\psi^{n}-\sigma\psi_{tt}^{n}+\tau^{\alpha}\left(p^{-\alpha}\psi_{tt}^{n}(0)-c^{2}p^{1-\alpha}\Delta\psi_{t}^{n}(0)\right)\|_{H^{\alpha-1/2}_{t}(L^{2})}.

For the
c^{2}
term, it is readily checked that the respective contribution of
c^{2}\Delta\psi^{n}
to the above norm of
\tilde{f}
(cf. (
5.27
)) can be bounded by means of
c^{2}\|\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}
as follows:

(5.34)

\displaystyle\|\Delta\psi^{n}\|_{H^{\alpha-1/2}_{t}(L^{2})}\lesssim

\displaystyle\|\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha-1/2}\Delta\psi^{n}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}+\|\Delta\psi^{n}_{0}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\mathfrak{g}_{1/2}*\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}+\|\Delta\psi^{n}_{0}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\mathfrak{g}_{1/2}\|_{L^{1}(0,T)}\|\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}+\|\Delta\psi^{n}_{0}\|_{L^{2}}\,,

and therefore tackled by the second term on the left-hand side of (
5.26
) together with Gronwall’s inequality.

By the Kato–Ponce inequality (
3.8
) with

(5.35)

\rho=\alpha-1/2,\qquad(p_{1},q_{1})=\left(\frac{2}{2\alpha-1},\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\right),\qquad(p_{2},q_{2})=(2,\infty),

we obtain

(5.36)

\displaystyle\|\sigma\psi_{tt}^{n}\|_{H_{t}^{\alpha-1/2}(L^{2})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|\sigma\|_{W_{t}^{\alpha-1/2,\frac{2}{2\alpha-1}}(L^{\infty})}\|\psi_{tt}^{n}\|_{L_{t}^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}(L^{2})}+\|\sigma\|_{L_{t}^{2}(L^{\infty})}\|\psi_{tt}^{n}\|_{W_{t}^{\alpha-1/2,\infty}(L^{2})}.

By the Sobolev embedding
L^{2}(0,T)\hookrightarrow W^{\alpha-1/2,\frac{2}{2\alpha-1}}(0,T)
, we have

(5.37)

\|\sigma\|_{W_{t}^{\alpha-1/2,\frac{2}{2\alpha-1}}(L^{\infty})}\leq C_{H^{2},L^{\infty}}^{\Omega}\|\sigma\|_{X^{\sigma}_{\textup{fMGT I}}}\,,\quad\|\sigma\|_{L_{t}^{2}(L^{\infty})}\leq C_{H^{2},L^{\infty}}^{\Omega}\|\sigma\|_{X^{\sigma}_{\textup{fMGT I}}}\,.

To further estimate the norms of
\psi_{tt}^{n}
in (
5.36
), we will use the leading time derivative term
\textup{D}_{t}^{2+\alpha}\psi^{n}
as well as its representation via the PDE. That is, we rely on the following Sobolev embeddings:

(5.38)

\displaystyle\|\psi_{tt}^{n}\|_{L_{t}^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}(L^{2})}\lesssim

\displaystyle\|\psi_{tt}^{n}\|_{H^{\alpha-1/2}_{t}(L^{2})}\lesssim\|\textup{D}_{t}^{2+\alpha}\psi^{n}\|_{H^{-1/2}_{t}(L^{2})}+\|\psi_{tt}^{n}(0)\|_{L^{2}}

and

(5.39)

\displaystyle\|\psi_{tt}^{n}\|_{W_{t}^{\alpha-1/2,\infty}(L^{2})}\lesssim

\displaystyle\|\psi_{tt}^{n}\|_{H^{\alpha}_{t}(L^{2})}\lesssim\|\textup{D}_{t}^{2+\alpha}\psi^{n}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}+\|\psi_{tt}^{n}(0)\|_{L^{2}},

where

\psi_{tt}^{n}
satisfies the fractional ODE

(5.40)

\tau^{\alpha}\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi_{tt}^{n}+\psi_{tt}^{n}=-r\mbox{ with }r=\sigma\psi_{tt}^{n}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n}-\delta\textup{D}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n}-f

and therefore

(5.41)

\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi_{tt}^{n}=\tau^{-\alpha}\Bigl(-E_{\alpha,1}(-(\tfrac{t}{\tau})^{\alpha})\psi_{tt}^{n}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-(\tfrac{t-s}{\tau})^{\alpha})r(s)\,\textup{d}s-r(t)\Bigr);

see, e.g.,
[
32
, §3]
. Thus, we have

(5.42)

\displaystyle\|\textup{D}_{t}^{2+\alpha}\psi^{n}\|_{H^{-1/2}_{t}(L^{2})}\lesssim\|\textup{D}_{t}^{2+\alpha}\psi^{n}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}\lesssim\|r\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}+\|\psi_{tt}^{n}(0)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\|\sigma\psi_{tt}^{n}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}+c^{2}\|\Delta\psi\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}+\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}\|\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}\\
+\delta\|\textup{D}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}+\|f\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}+\|\psi_{tt}^{n}(0)\|_{L^{2}}.\end{multlined}

In here, the terms with factors
c^{2}
,
\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}
, and
\delta
can be controlled – in a (generalized) Gronwall inequality fashion – by left-hand side terms in (
5.26
); to see this for the latter, consider

(5.43)

\|\textup{D}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}=\|\textup{I}^{\alpha-1/2}\textup{D}_{t}^{3/2}\Delta\psi^{n}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}\lesssim\mathfrak{g}_{3/2-\alpha}*d.

Thus, from (
5.36
) to (
5.42
), we have obtained an estimate of the form

(5.44)

\displaystyle\|\sigma\psi_{tt}^{n}\|_{H_{t}^{\alpha-1/2}(L^{2})}\lesssim

\displaystyle\|\sigma\|_{X^{\sigma}_{\textup{fMGT I}}}\left(\|\textup{D}_{t}^{2+\alpha}\psi^{n}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}+\|\psi_{tt}^{n}(0)\|_{L^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|\sigma\|_{X^{\sigma}_{\textup{fMGT I}}}\left(\|\sigma\psi_{tt}^{n}\|_{H_{t}^{\alpha-1/2}(L^{2})}+\textup{rhs}\right),

where

(5.45)

\textup{rhs}:=c^{2}\|\Delta\psi^{n}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}+\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}\|\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi^{n}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}+\mathfrak{g}_{3/2-\alpha}*d+\|f\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}+\|\psi_{tt}^{n}(0)\|_{L^{2}}.

Thus, provided
\|\sigma\|_{X^{\sigma}_{\textup{fMGT I}}}
is sufficiently small (where the bound can be chosen independent of
\alpha\in[\alpha_{0},1)
for
\alpha_{0}>1/2
),
the term
\|\sigma\psi_{tt}^{n}\|_{H_{t}^{\alpha-1/2}(L^{2})}
is bounded by
a multiple of rhs. By combining this with (
5.26
), (
5.42
), and Gronwall’s inequality in its generalized version, see, e.g.,
[
32
, Lemma 7.2]
, we therefore obtain the following estimate:

(5.46)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\|\textup{D}_{t}^{2+\alpha}\psi^{n}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|\nabla\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi^{n}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\Delta\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi^{n}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\\
+C(\alpha)(\|\nabla\psi_{tt}^{n}\|_{{H_{t}^{-(1-\alpha)/2}}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|\textup{D}_{t}^{3/2}\Delta\psi^{n}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}^{2})\end{multlined}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|f\|_{H^{\alpha-1/2}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|\Delta\psi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\Delta\psi_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\psi_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

where we have also relied on the uniform boundedness of the approximate data.
Here the constant

from the coercivity estimate (

).

\displaystyle\relbar\joinrel\rightharpoonup\textup{D}_{t}^{2+\alpha}\psi

\displaystyle\text{ weakly}

\displaystyle\text{ in }

\displaystyle\text{ in }

(IV)
Uniqueness.
The fact that the obtained solution is unique follows by testing the homogeneous problem

(5.50)

\tau^{\alpha}\textup{D}_{t}^{2+\alpha}\psi+(1+\sigma)\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi-\delta\textup{D}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\Delta\psi=0

(with zero initial data) with
\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi_{t}
. Analogously to above, but replacing

and

, we obtain

(5.51)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\frac{\tau^{\alpha}}{2}\|\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}}{2}\|\nabla\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+C(\alpha)(\|\psi_{tt}\|_{H_{t}^{-(1-\alpha)}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|\textup{D}_{t}^{3/2}\nabla\psi\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}^{2})\end{multlined}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}(c^{2}\Delta\psi-\sigma\psi_{tt},\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi)_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\Bigl(\|c^{2}\nabla\psi\|_{H_{t}^{\alpha-1/2}(L^{2})}+\|\sigma\psi_{tt}\|_{H_{t}^{\alpha-1/2}(H^{-1})}\Bigr)^{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|\textup{D}_{t}^{3/2}\nabla\psi\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}^{2},

where

(5.52)

\displaystyle\|c^{2}\nabla\psi\|_{H^{\alpha-1/2}(L^{2})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\nabla\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}.

Further, on account of the following estimate:

(5.53)

\displaystyle\|ab\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}=

\displaystyle\|a\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}\sup_{v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}}\|v\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}^{-1}\|v\nabla b+b\nabla v\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|a\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}(C_{H^{1},L^{6}}\|\nabla b\|_{L^{3}}+\|b\|_{L^{\infty}}),

we have, similarly to (
5.36
),

(5.54)

\displaystyle\|\sigma\psi_{tt}\|_{H_{t}^{\alpha-1/2}(H^{-1})}\lesssim\,\|\sigma\|_{X^{\sigma}_{\textup{fMGT I}}}\|\psi_{tt}\|_{L_{t}^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}(H^{-1})}+\|\sigma\|_{X^{\sigma}_{\textup{fMGT I}}}\|\psi_{tt}\|_{W_{t}^{\alpha-1/2,\infty}(H^{-1})}.

Again using (
5.41
) with
\psi
in place of
\psi^{n}
yields

(5.55)

\displaystyle\|\psi_{tt}\|_{L_{t}^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}(H^{-1})}+\|\psi_{tt}\|_{W_{t}^{\alpha-1/2,\infty}(H^{-1})}\lesssim\,\|\textup{D}_{t}^{2+\alpha}\psi\|_{L_{t}^{2}(H^{-1})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|\sigma\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi-\delta\textup{D}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\Delta\psi\|_{L_{t}^{2}(H^{-1})},

where
\|\sigma\psi_{tt}\|_{L_{t}^{2}(H^{-1})}\lesssim\|\sigma\psi_{tt}\|_{H_{t}^{\alpha-1/2}(H^{-1})}
. Therefore, these terms can be absorbed for small enough

by the left-hand side or handled by Gronwall’s inequality to conclude that

.

We next prove a well-posedness result for the corresponding nonlinear problem. To guarantee that the coefficient
\sigma
is small enough in the fixed-point iteration, we impose a smallness condition on the data.

Theorem 5.1

(Local well-posedness of the fJMGT–W I equation)
.

Let
\alpha\in[\alpha_{0},1)
for some
\alpha_{0}>1/2
and
T>0
. Further, assume that
f\in H^{\alpha-1/2}(0,{T};L^{2}(\Omega))
. There exists
\varrho=\varrho(\alpha,T)>0
, such that if

(5.56)

\|f\|^{2}_{H^{\alpha-1/2}(L^{2})}+\|\psi_{0}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{1}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{2}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\leq\varrho^{2},

then the initial boundary-value problem

(5.57)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tau^{\alpha}\textup{D}_{t}^{2+\alpha}\psi+&(1+2k\psi_{t})\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi-\delta\textup{D}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\Delta\psi=f\hskip-5.69054pt&&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\[2.84526pt]
&\psi=\,0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\[2.84526pt]
&(\psi,\psi_{t},\psi_{tt})=\,(\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\psi_{2})&&\mbox{in }\Omega\times\{0\},\end{aligned}\right.

has a unique solution
\psi\in X_{\textup{fMGT I}}
, which satisfies

(5.58)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\|\psi\|^{2}_{X_{\textup{fMGT I}}}\end{multlined}\lesssim\,\begin{multlined}\|f\|^{2}_{H^{\alpha-1/2}(L^{2})}+\|\psi_{0}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{1}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{2}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}.\end{multlined}

Proof.

The proof follows by setting up a fixed-point mapping
\mathcal{T}:w\mapsto\psi
, which associates

(5.59)

w\in B_{R}:=\{w\in X_{\textup{fMGT I}}\,:\|w\|_{X_{\textup{fMGT I}}}\leq R,\ w(0)=\psi_{0},\,w_{t}(0)=\psi_{1},\,w_{tt}(0)=\psi_{2}\}

with the solution
\psi
of the linearized problem (
5.1
) with
\sigma=2kw_{t}
. We recall that

(5.60)

X^{\sigma}_{\textup{fMGT I}}=L^{2}(0,T;(W^{1,3}\cap L^{\infty})(\Omega)),

and so

(5.61)

\|\sigma\|_{X^{\sigma}_{\textup{fMGT I}}}=2|k|\|w_{t}\|_{X^{\sigma}_{\textup{fMGT I}}}\leq C\|w\|_{X_{\textup{fMGT I}}}\lesssim R.

Thus,
\sigma
can be made small enough by decreasing
R>0
. The self-mapping is thus an immediate consequence of the energy estimate (
5.10
), provided we choose
\varrho
small enough, so that

(5.62)

C(R,T)(\|f\|^{2}_{H^{\alpha-1/2}(L^{2})}+\|\psi_{0}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{1}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{2}\|_{H^{1}}^{2})\leq C(R,T)\varrho^{2}\leq R^{2}.

We prove strict contractivity of this mapping next. Let
w^{(1)}
,
w^{(2)}\in B_{R}
. Denote
\psi^{(1)}=\mathcal{T}w^{(1)}
and
\psi^{(2)}=\mathcal{T}w^{(2)}
. Contractivity of
\mathcal{T}
follows by considering the difference equation for
\overline{\psi}=\psi^{(1)}-\psi^{(2)}
:

(5.63)

\displaystyle\tau^{\alpha}\textup{D}_{t}^{2+\alpha}\overline{\psi}+(1+2kw_{t}^{(1)})\overline{\psi}_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\overline{\psi}-\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}\Delta\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\overline{\psi}-\delta\textup{D}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\Delta\overline{\psi}_{t}+2k\psi^{(2)}_{tt}\overline{w}_{t}=0,

which is supplemented by zero initial conditions. Similarly to the proof of uniqueness in Proposition
5.1
, testing with
\overline{\psi}_{tt}
yields

(5.64)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\frac{\tau^{\alpha}}{2}\|\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\overline{\psi}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}}{2}\|\nabla\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\overline{\psi}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\\
\hskip 56.9055pt+C(\alpha)(\|\psi_{tt}\|_{H_{t}^{-(1-\alpha)}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|\textup{D}_{t}^{3/2}\nabla\overline{\psi}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}^{2})\end{multlined}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}(-2k\psi^{(2)}_{tt}\overline{w}_{t}+c^{2}\Delta\overline{\psi}-2kw_{t}^{(1)}\overline{\psi}_{tt},\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\overline{\psi})_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\overline{\psi}\|_{L_{t}^{\infty}(L^{2})}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\|-2k\psi^{(2)}_{tt}\overline{w}_{t}\|_{L_{t}^{1}(L^{2})}^{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|\textup{D}_{t}^{3/2}\nabla\overline{\psi}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}^{2}\\
+\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\Bigl(\|c^{2}\nabla\overline{\psi}\|_{H_{t}^{\alpha-1/2}(L^{2})}+\|2kw_{t}^{(1)}\overline{\psi}_{tt}\|_{H_{t}^{\alpha-1/2}(H^{-1})}\Bigr)^{2}.\end{multlined}

We can then rely on the following bound:

(5.65)

\displaystyle\|c^{2}\nabla\overline{\psi}\|_{H^{\alpha-1/2}(L^{2})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\nabla\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\overline{\psi}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}

and, by (
5.36
)–(
5.42
) with
2kw_{t}^{(1)}
in place of
\sigma
, we have

(5.66)

\displaystyle\|2kw_{t}^{(1)}\psi_{tt}\|_{H^{\alpha-1/2}(H^{-1})}\lesssim\|w_{t}^{(1)}\|_{X^{\sigma}_{\textup{fMGT I}}}\|\textup{D}_{t}^{2+\alpha}\psi\|_{L^{2}(H^{-1})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|w_{t}^{(1)}\|_{X^{\sigma}_{\textup{fMGT I}}}\|2kw_{t}^{(1)}\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\overline{\psi}-\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}\Delta\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\overline{\psi}-\delta\textup{D}_{t}^{2-\alpha}\Delta\overline{\psi}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(H^{-1})}.

Thus for
\|w_{t}^{(1)}\|_{X_{\sigma}}\lesssim R
small enough, similarly to the proof of Proposition
5.1
, we obtain

(5.67)

\displaystyle\frac{\tau^{\alpha}}{2}\|\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\overline{\psi}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}}{2}\|\nabla\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\overline{\psi}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\overline{\psi}_{tt}\|_{H_{t}^{-(1-\alpha)}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|\textup{D}_{t}^{3/2}\nabla\overline{\psi}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle R^{2}\|2k\psi_{tt}^{(2)}\overline{w}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(H^{-1})}^{2}+\|2k\psi_{tt}^{(2)}\overline{w}_{t}\|_{L^{1}(L^{2})}.

Furthermore,

(5.68)

\displaystyle R^{2}\|2k\psi_{tt}^{(2)}\overline{w}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(H^{-1})}^{2}+\|2k\psi_{tt}^{(2)}\overline{w}_{t}\|_{L^{1}(L^{2})}\lesssim

\displaystyle R^{2}\|\psi_{tt}^{(2)}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}L^{4}}\|\overline{w}_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(L^{4})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle R^{2}\varrho^{2}\|\overline{w}_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(L^{4})}.

We can further bound the last term as follows:

(5.69)

\displaystyle\|\nabla\overline{w}_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(L^{2})}=\|\textup{I}^{\gamma}\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\gamma}\nabla\overline{w}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(L^{2})}\leq\|\mathfrak{g}_{1-\gamma}\|_{L^{1}(0,T)}\|\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\gamma}\nabla\overline{w}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(L^{2})},

choosing
\gamma=1/2
. Thus, by decreasing
\varrho>0
, we can guarantee that
\mathcal{T}
is strictly contractive in the following norm:

(5.70)

|||\psi|||=\|\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\overline{\psi}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|\nabla\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\overline{\psi}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|\overline{\psi}_{tt}\|_{H^{-(1-\alpha)}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|\nabla\overline{\psi}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}.

The rest of the arguments follow as in Theorem
4.1
and complete the proof. ∎

5.2.
Analysis of the fJMGT–W equation

To formulate the corresponding result for the fMGT-W equation

(5.71)

\tau^{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi_{tt}+(1+2k\psi_{t})\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi-\delta\Delta\psi_{t}=f

we again need smallness of the coefficient
\sigma
in a suitable norm. To this end, let

(5.72)

X^{\sigma}_{\textup{fMGT}}=H^{\alpha/2}(0,T;(W^{1,3}\cap L^{\infty})(\Omega))

for
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
, and denote the corresponding norm by
\|\cdot\|_{X^{\sigma}_{\textup{fMGT}}}
. We also introduce the solution space by

(5.73)

\displaystyle\quad X_{\textup{fMGT}}=\,\left\{\psi\in H^{2+\alpha}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)):\right.

\displaystyle\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha/2}\psi\in L^{\infty}(0,T;{H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)}),

\displaystyle\left.\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))\right\},

equipped with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{X_{\textup{fMGT}}}
. Note that with this choice, again

(5.74)

\|2k\psi_{t}\|_{X^{\sigma}_{\textup{fMGT}}}\lesssim\|\psi\|_{X_{\textup{fMGT}}}

holds but the energy term
\|\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha/2}\Delta\psi\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}
needed for this purpose comes with an
\alpha
-dependent coefficient in (
5.77
).
For this reason, while still being able to show well-posedness also of the nonlinear fJMGT–W equation for each
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we will not obtain a uniform bound quantifying smallness of the initial data. That is, we will not be able to show that for fixed small enough
(\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\psi_{2})
, there exists a family of solutions to the nonlinear problem. Hence, concerning limits as
\alpha\to 1-
, we will restrict ourselves to the linear fMGT equation:

(5.75)

\tau^{\alpha}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi_{tt}+(1+\sigma(x,t))\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}{\textup{D}}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi-\delta\Delta\psi_{t}=f.

We next prove the well-posedness of the linear time-fractional problem. Note that under the same regularity conditions on the initial and boundary data, the fMGT equation allows us to prove slightly better regularity of the solution as compared to fMGT I; cf. Proposition
5.1
.

Proposition 5.2

(Well-posedness of the fMGT equation)
.

Let
\alpha\in[\alpha_{0},1)
for some
\alpha_{0}>1/2
. Assume that
f\in H^{\alpha-1/2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
,
\sigma\in X^{\sigma}_{\textup{fMGT I}}
, and

(5.76)

(\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\psi_{2})\in({H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)},{H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)},H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)).

Then there exists
\varrho=\varrho(\alpha)>0
, such that if
\|\sigma\|_{X^{\sigma}_{\textup{fMGT}}}\leq\varrho
, there is a unique
\psi\in X_{\textup{fMGT}}
, which satisfies the problem in the
L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
sense with
(\psi,\psi_{t},\psi_{tt})|_{t=0}=(\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\psi_{2})
. Furthermore, this solution fulfills the following estimate:

(5.77)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\|\textup{D}_{t}^{2+\alpha}\psi\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|\nabla\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\Delta\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\psi(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\\
+C(\alpha)\Bigl(\|\nabla\psi_{tt}\|_{{H^{-(1-\alpha)/2}}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha/2}\Delta\psi\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}\Bigr)\end{multlined}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|f\|_{H^{\alpha-1/2}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|\Delta\psi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\Delta\psi_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\psi_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

where
C(\alpha)\to 0
as
\alpha\to 1^{-}
.

Proof.

The proof follows similarly to the proof of Proposition
5.1
with the main changes contained in the energy analysis, on which we focus here. Note that now the semi-discrete problem can be equivalently rewritten as a system of Volterra integral equations:

(5.78)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\tau^{\alpha}M\boldsymbol{\mu}+M_{\sigma}(t)\Bigl(p^{\alpha-1}*\boldsymbol{\mu}+p^{0}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\Bigr)+c^{2}K\Bigl(p^{\alpha+1}*\boldsymbol{\mu}+p^{2}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}+p^{1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}+p^{0}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}\Bigr)\\
+\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}\Bigl(p^{1}*\boldsymbol{\mu}+p^{2-\alpha}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}+p^{1-\alpha}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\Bigr)+\delta K(p^{\alpha}*\boldsymbol{\mu}+p^{1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}+p^{0}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1})=f\end{multlined}

in place of (
5.16
); the existence of an approximate solutions follows by the same arguments. We present the energy analysis of the semi-discrete problem here, but omit the superscript
n
below for simplicity. Multiplying the semi-discrete equation with
-\Delta\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi
, yields the energy identity (
5.26
), where now

(5.79)

\displaystyle d=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(\textup{D}_{t}\Delta\psi,\Delta\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi)_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}({\textup{I}}^{\alpha}\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\Delta\psi(s),\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\Delta\psi(s))_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s,

with
d_{0}=(\Delta\psi_{t}(0),\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi(t))_{L^{2}}-p^{1-\alpha}(t)\|\Delta\psi_{t}(0)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
instead of (
5.30
). Here, we have used the identities

(5.80)

\textup{D}_{t}\psi={\textup{I}}^{1}\psi_{tt}+\psi_{t}(0)={\textup{I}}^{\alpha}\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\psi+\psi_{t}(0)

and

(5.81)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(\Delta\psi_{t}(0),\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\Delta\psi(s))_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s=

\displaystyle(\Delta\psi_{t}(0),\int_{0}^{t}((\textup{D}_{t}\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi)(s)-p^{-\alpha}(s)\Delta\psi_{t}(0))\,\textup{d}s)_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\Delta\psi_{t}(0),\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi(t))_{L^{2}}-p^{1-\alpha}(t)\|\Delta\psi_{t}(0)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

The damping term can now be estimated from below as follows

(5.82)

\frac{d+d_{0}}{\cos(\pi\gamma/2)}\geq\|\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\Delta\psi\|_{H_{t}^{-\alpha/2}(L^{2})}^{2}\sim\|\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha/2}\Delta\psi\|_{L_{t}^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}.

Furthermore, we have

(5.83)

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}(\tilde{f}(s),\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha}\Delta\psi(s))_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s\right|\leq\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\|\tilde{f}\|_{H^{\alpha/2}(L^{2})}^{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|\textup{D}_{t}^{1+\alpha/2}\Delta\psi\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}

with

(5.84)

\tilde{f}=f+c^{2}\Delta\psi^{n}-\sigma\psi_{tt}^{n}+\tau^{\alpha}\left(p^{-\alpha}\psi_{tt}^{n}(0)-c^{2}p^{1-\alpha}\Delta\psi_{t}^{n}(0)\right)

as before. By the Kato–Ponce inequality (

) with
\rho=\alpha/2
, we then have

(5.85)

\displaystyle\|\sigma\psi_{tt}\|_{H_{t}^{\alpha/2}(L^{2})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|\sigma\|_{W_{t}^{\alpha/2,p_{1}}(L^{\infty})}\|\psi_{tt}\|_{L_{t}^{q_{1}}(L^{2})}+\|\sigma\|_{L_{t}^{p_{2}}(L^{\infty})}\|\psi_{tt}\|_{W_{t}^{\alpha/2,q_{2}}(L^{2})}

for

. Similarly to before, to further estimate the norms of
\psi_{tt}
in (

and its representation via the fractional ODE

Therefore,

(5.87)

\displaystyle\|\psi_{tt}\|_{L^{q_{1}}(0,t;L^{2})}+\|\psi_{tt}\|_{W^{\alpha/2,q_{2}}(0,t;L^{2})}\lesssim\|\textup{D}_{t}^{2+\alpha}\psi\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|\sigma\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi-\delta\textup{D}_{t}\Delta\psi-f\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}+\|\psi_{tt}(0)\|_{L^{2}},

which leads to

(5.90)

6.

Unlike its Westervelt version, the Kuznetsov versions of these time-fractional equations contain a quadratic gradient nonlinearity, and so their analysis requires the use of higher-order energy estimates. We thus limit our presentation to the analysis of the fJMGT–K III equation, which has the integer-order leading term.

is not controllable by any of the other left-hand side terms resulting from (
7.18
).

7.2.
Limiting behavior of the fMGT II equation

For
\alpha\in(0,1]
, we denote by
\psi^{\alpha}
the solution according to Proposition
7.1
under the assumptions

(7.20)

(\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\psi_{2})\in{H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)}\times\{0\}\times H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),\quad f,\nabla f\in L^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

Let
\psi
be the solution of the corresponding MGT equation. Note that then the corresponding functions
z^{\alpha}
and
z
satisfy the initial conditions

(7.21)

z^{\alpha}(0)=z(0)=\psi_{0},\quad z^{\alpha}_{t}(0)=0,\quad z_{t}(0)=\tau\psi_{2}.

Hence to achieve compatibility, besides
\psi_{1}=0
(see the proof of Proposition
7.1
), we also have to assume
\psi_{2}=0
.
Then the difference
\overline{z}=z^{\alpha}-z
solves

(7.22)

\displaystyle\overline{z}_{tt}-(c^{2}+\tfrac{\delta}{\tau^{\alpha}})\Delta\overline{z}+\delta\,\mathfrak{k}_{\alpha}*\Delta\overline{z}

\displaystyle=\delta\left((\tau^{-\alpha}-\tau^{-1})\Delta z-(\mathfrak{k}_{\alpha}-\mathfrak{k}_{1})*\Delta z\right)

with homogeneous initial data
\overline{z}(0)=\overline{z}_{t}(0)=0
. Testing with
\overline{z}_{t}
leads to

(7.23)

\|\overline{z}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}^{2}+2c^{2}\|\nabla\overline{z}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}^{2}\leq 4\delta\Bigl(|\tau^{-\alpha}-\tau^{-1}|+\|\mathfrak{k}_{\alpha}-\mathfrak{k}_{1}\|_{L^{1}(0,T)}\Bigr)\|\Delta z\|_{L^{1}(L^{2})}^{2},

where we can estimate
\|\Delta z\|_{L^{1}(L^{2})}^{2}
according to (
7.16
) and

(7.24)

\|\mathfrak{k}_{\alpha}-\mathfrak{k}_{1}\|_{L^{1}(0,T)}=\int_{0}^{T}\left|\tau^{-\alpha}t^{\alpha-1}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-(\tfrac{t}{\tau})^{\alpha})-\tau^{-1}\exp(-(\tfrac{t}{\tau}))\right|\,\textup{d}t\to 0\ \mbox{ as }\alpha\to 1^{-},

by Lebesgue’s Dominated Convergence theorem.
Thus, with

(7.25)

\displaystyle\psi^{\alpha}-\psi

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{k}_{\alpha}*z^{\alpha}-\mathfrak{k}_{1}*z=(\mathfrak{k}_{\alpha}-\mathfrak{k}_{1})*z+\mathfrak{k}_{\alpha}*\overline{z}

\displaystyle(\psi^{\alpha}-\psi)_{t}(t)

\displaystyle=(\mathfrak{k}_{\alpha}-\mathfrak{k}_{1})(t)\,z^{\alpha}(0)+((\mathfrak{k}_{\alpha}-\mathfrak{k}_{1})*z_{t})(t)+\mathfrak{k}_{\alpha}(t)\overline{z}(0)+(\mathfrak{k}_{\alpha}*\overline{z}_{t})(t)

we obtain, using
\overline{z}(0)=0
,
z^{\alpha}(0)=\psi_{0}
, and Young’s Convolution inequality,

(7.26)

\displaystyle\|(\psi^{\alpha}-\psi)_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|\nabla(\psi^{\alpha}-\psi)\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|\mathfrak{k}_{\alpha}-\mathfrak{k}_{1}\|_{L^{p}(0,T)}^{2}\|\psi_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\mathfrak{k}_{\alpha}-\mathfrak{k}_{1}\|_{L^{1}(0,T)}^{2}\|z_{t}\|_{L^{p}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|\mathfrak{k}_{\alpha}\|_{L^{1}(0,T)}^{2}\|\overline{z}_{t}\|_{L^{p}(L^{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle+\|\mathfrak{k}_{\alpha}-\mathfrak{k}_{1}\|_{L^{1}(0,T)}^{2}\|\nabla z_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|\mathfrak{k}_{\alpha}\|_{L^{1}(0,T)}^{2}\|\nabla\overline{z}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}^{2}\,,

where we can use Proposition
7.1
and estimate (
7.23
) to further bound the right-hand side terms.
Note that since

(7.27)

\|\mathfrak{k}_{\alpha}-\mathfrak{k}_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T)}=\lim_{t\to 0}|\mathfrak{k}_{\alpha}-\mathfrak{k}_{1}|(t)=+\infty,

we only get an estimate of
\|(\psi^{\alpha}-\psi)_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}^{2}
if we additionally assume
\psi_{0}=0
. Furthermore,

(7.28)

\displaystyle\|\mathfrak{k}_{\alpha}-\mathfrak{k}_{1}\|_{L^{p}(0,T)}\leq

\displaystyle|\tau^{-\alpha}-\tau^{-1}|\|\cdot^{\alpha-1}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-(\tfrac{\cdot}{\tau})^{\alpha})\|_{L^{p}(0,T)}

\displaystyle+\tau^{-1}\|\cdot^{\alpha-1}-1\|_{L^{p}(0,T)}\|E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-(\tfrac{\cdot}{\tau})^{\alpha})\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T)}

\displaystyle+\tau^{-1}\|E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-(\tfrac{\cdot}{\tau})^{\alpha})-\exp(-(\tfrac{\cdot}{\tau}))\|_{L^{p}(0,T)}\to 0\ \ \mbox{ as }\alpha\to 1^{-}\,,

where the only critical term is the one containing the singularity,
\int_{0}|t^{-(1-\alpha)}-1|^{p}\,\textup{d}t
. Its convergence to zero follows from Lebesgue’s Dominated Convergence theorem with the
L^{1}
bound

(7.29)

2^{p-1}(t^{-p(1-\alpha_{0})}+1)

for
1\geq\alpha\geq\alpha_{0}>1-\frac{1}{p}
. Thus we arrive at the following result.

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
\psi_{0}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\cap H^{2}(\Omega)
and
f
,
\nabla f\in L^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
. Further, let
\{\psi^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in(0,1)}
be the family of solutions to the
fMGT II
equation and let
\psi
solve the corresponding equation with
\alpha=1
, where the initial data is in both cases given by

(7.30)

(\psi^{\alpha},\psi^{\alpha}_{t},\psi^{\alpha}_{tt})|_{t=0}=(\psi,\psi_{t},\psi_{tt})|_{t=0}=(\psi_{0},0,0).

Then for any
p\in[1,\infty)
,
\psi^{\alpha}
converges to
\psi
in the
W^{1,p}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))
norm as
\alpha\rightarrow 1^{-}
.
If additionally
\psi_{0}=0
, then we also have convergence in
W^{1,\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))
.

Conclusion and Outlook

In this work, based on physical balance and constitutive laws, we have derived four different fractional-order versions of a well-known third-order in time model of nonlinear acoustics, the JMGT equation. The fractional-order of differentiation
\alpha\in(0,1]
(sometimes restricted to
\alpha\in(1/2,1]
) appears as a parameter in each of these models. We have studied the well-posedness of these equations and their linearizations in appropriate spaces and justified the respective limits as
\alpha\to 1^{-}
, leading to the (J)MGT equation.

Formally taking the limit of these equations as the relaxation time
\tau
vanishes would lead to time-fractional second-order acoustic equations, which are of independent interest as well. An analysis of this limit will be the subject of future research.
