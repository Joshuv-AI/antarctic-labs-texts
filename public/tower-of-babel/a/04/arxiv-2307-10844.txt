Central limit measure for V-monotone independence
Adrian Dacko

Abstract
. We study the central limit distribution μ \mu for V-monotone independence. Using its Cauchy–Stieltjes transform, we prove that μ \mu is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure on ℝ \mathbb{R} and we give its density ρ \rho in an implicit form. We present a computer generated graph of ρ \rho .



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary: 46L53, 60F05. Secondary: 30E99.

1.
Introduction

Central limit theorems are important in all models of noncommutative independence.
In particular, in the central limit theorem for tensor independence (classical independence), free independence and Boolean independence one obtains the standard normal distribution
[
5
,
9
]
, the standard Wigner distribution
[
15
,
17
]
, and the standard Bernoulli distribution
[
16
]
, respectively.
In the case of monotone (and antimonotone) independence, we obtain the standard arcsine distribution
[
14
]

\mu^{\textnormal{M}}(dx)=\rho^{\textnormal{M}}(x)\,dx\text{,}

where

\rho^{\textnormal{M}}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi\cdot\sqrt{2-x^{2}}}

with
x\in(-\sqrt{2},\sqrt{2})
(see also
[
12
,
13
]
). There are also many other noncommutative analogues of the classical central limit theorem related to other notions of independence, including interpolations and Fock space type constructions
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
8
,
10
,
11
,
18
,
19
]
.

The notion of
V-monotone
independence was introduced and studied in
[
6
]
. The V-monotone independence can be considered as a combination of two twin models of independence, monotone independence and antimonotone independence, into one model. This is best seen at the Fock space level since the monotone Fock space is associated with decreasing sequences of indices assigned to direct sums, while the antimonotone Fock space is associated with increasing sequences. The simplest combination of these two families of sequences leads to the
V-monotone
Fock space, which is associated with sequences of indices whose graph has the shape of the letter V (that is, they are either increasing or decreasing, or they decrease up to a certain point and then increase), whence the name
“V-monotone”
.

In the monotone and antimonotone central limit theorems, the moments of the standard Gaussian measure
\mu
are expressed by monotonically labeled noncrossing pair partitions and the antimonotonically labeled ones, respectively. The class of
V-monotonically
labeled noncrossing pair partitions that occurs in the
V-monotone
central limit theorem contains both of the aforementioned classes. Studying the combinatorics of this class of partitions is rather involved.
It is impossible to obtain a simple recursive formula for the moments of the limit measure
\mu
. This results in the fact that the process of obtaining
\mu
is far more complex than in the types of independence known so far, and it is necessary to use a nonstandard approach. Eventually, we have

\mu(dx)=\rho(x)\,dx\text{,}

where
x\in(-\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}},\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}})
with
\gamma_{0}>0
defined in (
2.7
), and the density
\rho
is given in an implicit form. The situation resembles that of the distribution of
TT^{*}
, where
T
is the triangular operator, studied by Dykema and Haagerup
[
7
]
, who have also found the density function in an implicit form (see Theorem 8.9 there).

This article is devoted to determining the standard V-monotone Gaussian measure: first, we obtain its Cauchy–Stieltjes transform for real arguments, and then we extend it, in order to have it for complex arguments.
But extending one of the functions occurring in the formula for the transform is quite involved, i.e. we have to extend a certain inverse
T_{0}^{-1}
of an elementary function. We extend
T_{0}
in Section 3, mostly working on finding its domain.
We invert this extension in Section
4
. In Section
5
we obtain the Cauchy–Stieltjes transform of the V-monotone central limit distribution and then, by means of the transform, we prove that
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
. Finally, we get the density in an implicit form.

In the paper, we present a computer-generated graph of the density, which resembles that of the density of the standard arcsine distribution, but nevertheless differs quite significantly from it, since it has a local maximum at zero and has two symmetric singularities at
x=\pm\sqrt{2}
.

All figures presented in the paper were generated by means of the Wolfram Mathematica system.

2.
Central limit distribution

In this section, using the results from
[
6
]
, we determine, first on the complement of a symmetric closed interval on
\mathbb{R}
, the reciprocal Cauchy–Stieltjes transform
F_{\mu}
of the measure
\mu
obtained as a central limit distribution for V-monotone independence. Then, we analytically extend
F_{\mu}
in order to have it for complex numbers. However, it is quite involved to extend one of the functions occurring in the formula for this transform and we continue to do it only in the next two sections.

Let
(m_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
be the sequence given by

(2.1)

m_{n}=\begin{cases}P_{n/2}(1)&\text{ if $n$ is even, }\\
0&\text{ if $n$ is odd, }\end{cases}

where
(P_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
is the family of polynomials of variable
s\in[0,1]
, defined recursively by

(2.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &P_{0}(s)=1\text{,}\\
&P_{n+1}(s)=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\Big(\int_{0}^{s}P_{m}(t)\,dt+2^{-m}C_{m}(1-s)^{m+1}\Big)P_{n-m}(s)\text{,}\end{aligned}\right.

for any nonnegative integer
n
, where
(C_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
is the sequence of Catalan numbers.

Remark 2.1
.

The sequence
(m_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
has also a combinatorial interpretation (cf.
[
6
, Theorem 5.3]
), which served as its definition there. The equivalence of these definitions is the result of
[
6
, Theorems 6.13 and 7.5]
.

The definitions of
(P_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
, given in (
2.2
) and
[
6
, Definition 7.4]
, are equivalent. Moreover,
\lvert P_{n}(x)\rvert\leq C_{n}
, for each
n
. Both the facts follow from the proof of
[
6
, Proposition 7.6]
. It was also proved in
[
6
, Theorems 6.13 and 7.5]
that
(m_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
forms the moment sequence of a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
.

First, let us recall the V-monotone central limit theorem
[
6
, Theorem 5.3]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
be a
C^{*}
-probability space and let
(a_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
be a family of self-adjoint, V-monotonically independent, and identically distributed random variables with mean
0
and variance
1
. Then

\frac{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{N}}{\sqrt{N}}\overset{d}{\to}\omega\text{,}

where
\omega
is a random variable with the
standard V-monotone Gaussian distribution
given by the moment sequence
(m_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
.

The distribution
\mu
is called standard because it has mean zero and variance one. Since all odd moments vanish,
\mu
is symmetric with respect to
0
. Its support is compact and contained in
[-2,2]
, since its moments are dominated by the respective moments of the standard Wigner distribution.

Definition 2.3
.

The moment generating function of
\mu
is given by the power series

(2.3)

M_{\mu}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}m_{n}z^{n}\text{.}

The Cauchy–Stieltjes transform and the reciprocal Cauchy–Stieltjes transform of this measure will be denoted by
G_{\mu}
and
F_{\mu}
, respectively. By the definition,
F_{\mu}=1/G_{\mu}
.

We temporarily consider
M_{\mu}
,
G_{\mu}
and
F_{\mu}
for real arguments, that is the restrictions of these functions to a suitable subsets of
\mathbb{R}
. To investigate the Cauchy–Stieltjes transform, let us introduce two auxiliary functions. It was shown in
[
6
]
that the moment generating function of the limit distribution is expressed in terms of the function
T_{0}
given below.

Let
T_{0}\colon[0,\infty)\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be given by

(2.4)

T_{0}(s)=-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\bigg(\arctan\bigg(\frac{2s-1}{\sqrt{3}}\bigg)-\frac{\pi}{6}\bigg)-\frac{\textnormal{ln}\,(s^{2}-s+1)}{2}\text{.}

We can write this function in an integral form, namely

(2.5)

T_{0}(s)=\int_{s}^{1}\dfrac{t}{t^{2}-t+1}\,dt\text{,}

for all
s\geq 0
. Indeed, simple calculations yield

\begin{split}\int_{s}^{1}\dfrac{t}{t^{2}-t+1}\,dt&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{1}\dfrac{2t-1}{t^{2}-t+1}\,dt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{1}\dfrac{1}{(t-1/2)^{2}+(\sqrt{3}/2)^{2}}\,dt\\
&=-\frac{1}{2}\textnormal{ln}\,(s^{2}-s+1)-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\bigg(\arctan\bigg(\frac{2s-1}{\sqrt{3}}\bigg)-\frac{\pi}{6}\bigg)=T_{0}(s)\text{.}\end{split}

The derivative of
T_{0}
is negative for
s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\coloneqq(0,\infty)
, therefore
T_{0}
is invertible on
[0,\infty)
and its range is
(-\infty,T_{0}(0)]
, where
T_{0}(0)=\sqrt{3}\pi/9
.

We define
T_{0}^{-1}\colon(-\infty,\sqrt{3}\pi/9]\to[0,\infty)
as the inverse of
T_{0}
. Let
W\colon\mathbb{R}\setminus[-\sqrt{2},\sqrt{2}]\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be given by

(2.6)

W(x)=\frac{1}{2}\textnormal{ln}\,\bigg(1+\dfrac{2}{x^{2}-2}\bigg)\text{}

and let

(2.7)

\gamma_{0}=\frac{2}{e^{2\sqrt{3}\pi/9}-1}\text{.}

We are now ready to write the formula for
F_{\mu}
— at this point only for real numbers.

Theorem 2.4
.

The reciprocal Cauchy–Stieltjes transform of the standard V-monotone Gaussian measure
\mu
is given by

(2.8)

F_{\mu}(x)=xT_{0}^{-1}(W(x))\text{,}

for
x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}},\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}]
.

Proof.

The formula for the moment generating function, namely

(2.9)

\dfrac{1}{M_{\mu}(x)}=T_{0}^{-1}\Bigg(\dfrac{1}{2}\textnormal{ln}\,\bigg(\dfrac{1}{1-2x^{2}}\bigg)\Bigg)\text{,}

was proved in
[
6
, Corollary 7.9]
for
x\in(-1/2,1/2)
. Note that
1/2\cdot\textnormal{ln}\,\big(1+2/\gamma_{0})=\sqrt{3}\pi/9=T_{0}(0)
.
The function
T_{0}^{-1}
is not analytically extendable through the point
\sqrt{3}\pi/9
. Indeed, consider the strip
\Delta_{0}\coloneqq\{u\in\mathbb{C}:-\sqrt{3}/2<\Im u<\sqrt{3}/2\}
and define
\widetilde{T}\colon\Delta_{0}\to\mathbb{C}
as the analytic extension of
T_{0}
, given by

\widetilde{T}(u)=\int_{u}^{1}\dfrac{v}{v^{2}-v+1}\,dv\text{,}

which is well defined, since the poles of the integrand do not belong to
\Delta_{0}
. The point
\widetilde{T}(0)=\sqrt{3}\pi/9
is a branch point of order two for
\widetilde{T}^{-1}
, since
\widetilde{T}^{\prime}(0)=0
and
0\neq\widetilde{T}^{\prime\prime}(0)=-1
. Solving the inequality

\dfrac{1}{2}\textnormal{ln}\,\bigg(\frac{1}{1-2x^{2}}\bigg)<\frac{\sqrt{3}\pi}{9}\text{}

with respect to
x\in(-2^{-1/2},2^{-1/2})
, we see that
M_{\mu}
can be extended analytically to the interval
\big(-(2+\gamma_{0})^{-1/2},(2+\gamma_{0})^{-1/2}\big)
, but not through any of its ends. Indeed, these ends correspond to
T_{0}(0)
(note that
e^{2\sqrt{3}\pi/9}=\big(1-2\cdot(2+\gamma_{0})^{-1}\big)^{-1}
), through which
T_{0}^{-1}
is not analytically extendable. It is well known that the moment generating function of a compactly supported probability measure on the real line has no singularities outside
\mathbbm{R}
, thus the radius of convergence of (
2.3
) is equal to
1/\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}
. Using the formula

F_{\mu}(x)=\frac{x}{M_{\mu}(1/x)}\text{,}

we obtain our assertion.
∎

Corollary 2.5
.

The support of
\mu
is compact and
\textnormal{supp}\,{\mu}\subseteq[-\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}},\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}]
.

Later we will show that the above inclusion is in fact an equality.
Let
\mathbb{C}_{+}
and
\mathbb{C}_{-}
denote the sets
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\Im z>0\}
and
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\Im z<0\}
, respectively. One of our main goals is to determine the Cauchy–Stieltjes transform
G_{\mu}
.

For technical reasons, it is not convenient to write an explicit formula for
G_{\mu}(z)
for every
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
. Since
\mu
is symmetric with respect to
0
, we have

(2.10)

G_{\mu}(z)=\frac{\mu(\{0\})}{z}+2z\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{\mu(dt)}{z^{2}-t^{2}}\text{,}

for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[-\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}},\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}]
, therefore
G_{\mu}(-\overline{z})=-\overline{G_{\mu}(z)}
. This allows us to determine
G_{\mu}
only on

Q\coloneqq\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\Re z>0\text{ and }\Im z>0\}\text{.}

In view of (
2.9
), we expect that

(2.11)

F_{\mu}(z)=zT^{-1}\Bigg(\dfrac{1}{2}\textnormal{Log}\,\bigg(1+\dfrac{2}{z^{2}-2}\bigg)\Bigg)

at least for
z\in Q
, where
T^{-1}
is a suitable analytic extension of
T_{0}^{-1}
. We will prove the above formula by showing that ‘
Log
’ here is the principal value of the logarithm, i.e.

(2.12)

\textnormal{Log}\,z=\textnormal{ln}\,R+i\varphi\text{,}\qquad\text{for $z=Re^{i\varphi}$ with $R>0$ and $-\pi<\varphi\leq\pi$,}

which is analytic on
\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]
,
and by finding
T^{-1}
so that
F_{\mu}
is analytic on
Q
.

We start with extending
x\mapsto W(x)
defined in (
2.6
) so that, for
z\in Q
, the extension is equal to
z\mapsto 1/2\textnormal{Log}\,(1+2/(z^{2}-2))
, which occurs in (
2.11
).

Proposition 2.6
.

The function
W\colon(\mathbb{C}_{+}\cup\mathbb{R})\setminus\{-\sqrt{2},0,\sqrt{2}\}\mapsto\mathbb{C}
, given by

(2.13)

W(z)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{2}\textnormal{Log}\,\bigg(1+\dfrac{2}{z^{2}-2}\bigg)&\text{if $z\notin(0,\sqrt{2})$,}\\
\dfrac{1}{2}\textnormal{ln}\,\bigg\lvert 1-\dfrac{2}{z^{2}-2}\bigg\rvert-\dfrac{i\pi}{2}&\text{if $z\in(0,\sqrt{2})$,}\end{cases}

is a continuous extension of
x\mapsto W(x)
which is analytic on
\mathbb{C}_{+}
. Moreover,
W(Q)=\{w\in\mathbb{C}:-\pi/2<\Im w<0\}
and
W\big((\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}},\infty)\big)=(0,\sqrt{3}\pi/9)
.

Proof.

The proof of the first statement follows from elementary computations: for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{\pm\sqrt{2}\}
, let

(2.14)

U(z)=1+\frac{2}{z^{2}-2}\text{.}

We have

U(x+iy)=1+\frac{2(x^{2}-y^{2}-2)}{(x^{2}-y^{2}-2)^{2}+4x^{2}y^{2}}-\frac{4ixy}{(x^{2}-y^{2}-2)^{2}+4x^{2}y^{2}}\text{.}

Since
U(z)\in(-\infty,0)\Leftrightarrow z\in(-\sqrt{2},\sqrt{2})\setminus\{0\}
, it suffices to compute limits as
z=x+iy
approaches
x_{0}\in(-\sqrt{2},\sqrt{2})\setminus\{0\}
from above (note that
U(z)\in\mathbb{R}\Leftrightarrow\Re z=0\vee\Im z=0
,
\lvert\Re z\rvert\neq\sqrt{2}
).
If
y>0
and
\lvert x-x_{0}\rvert
are small enough, then
\Im U(x+iy)>0
and
\Im U(x+iy)<0
for
x_{0}<0
and
x_{0}>0
, respectively, and moreover
\Re U(x+iy)<0
. Therefore

\lim\limits_{z\to x_{0}+0^{+}\cdot i}\frac{1}{2}\textnormal{Log}\,\big(U(z)\big)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{2}\textnormal{ln}\,\bigg(\dfrac{2}{2-x_{0}^{2}}-1\bigg)+\dfrac{i\pi}{2}&\text{if $x_{0}\in(-\sqrt{2},0)$,}\\
\dfrac{1}{2}\textnormal{ln}\,\bigg(\dfrac{2}{2-x_{0}^{2}}-1\bigg)-\dfrac{i\pi}{2}&\text{if $x_{0}\in(0,\sqrt{2})$,}\end{cases}

and the continuity of the considered restriction of
W
is proved. Finally,

W(Q)=\frac{1}{2}\textnormal{Log}\,(\mathbb{C}_{-})=\bigg\{w\in\mathbb{C}:-\frac{\pi}{2}<\Im w<0\bigg\}\text{}

and

W\big((\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}},\infty)\big)=\frac{1}{2}\textnormal{ln}\,\big((1,e^{2\sqrt{3}\pi/9})\big)=\bigg(0,\frac{\sqrt{3}\pi}{9}\bigg)\text{,}

which finishes the proof.
∎

Now, let us give the explicit form of the complex multivalued function
\boldsymbol{T}
whose branch is an analytic extension of
T_{0}
. Since
\boldsymbol{T}
is expressed by means of two logarithms, we introduce two pairs of polar coordinates. Namely, let

u_{0}=\frac{1+\sqrt{3}i}{2}\text{}

and let
u\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{u_{0},\overline{u_{0}}\}
. We put

(2.15)

r_{1}e^{i\varphi_{1}}=u-u_{0}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad r_{2}e^{i\varphi_{2}}=u-\overline{u_{0}}\text{,}

where
r_{1}
,
r_{2}>0
and
\varphi_{1}
,
\varphi_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proposition 2.7
.

The function
T_{0}
given in (
2.4
) is a branch of a complex multivalued function
\boldsymbol{T}
given by

(2.16)

\boldsymbol{T}(u)=\alpha-\beta\cdot(\textnormal{ln}\,r_{1}+i\varphi_{1})-\overline{\beta}\cdot(\textnormal{ln}\,r_{2}+i\varphi_{2})\text{,}

for
u\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{u_{0},\overline{u_{0}}\}
, where
\alpha=-\sqrt{3}\pi/9
and
\beta=(3-\sqrt{3}i)/6
.

Proof.

The proof is elementary: we first extend the functions occurring in (
2.4
) to the complex multivalued ones, getting

\textnormal{arctan}\,{\bigg(\frac{2u-1}{\sqrt{3}}\bigg)}=\frac{\textnormal{log}\,(u-u_{0})-\textnormal{log}\,(u-\overline{u_{0}})}{2i}+\frac{\pi}{2}\text{}

and

\textnormal{log}\,(u^{2}-u+1)=\textnormal{log}\,(u-u_{0})-\textnormal{log}\,(u-\overline{u_{0}})\text{,}

for
u\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{u_{0},\overline{u_{0}}\}
, where
arctan
and
log
are the multivalued inverse tangent function and logarithm, respectively. Elementary computations lead to

(2.17)

\boldsymbol{T}(u)=-\frac{\sqrt{3}\pi}{9}-\frac{3-\sqrt{3}i}{6}\cdot\textnormal{log}\,(u-u_{0})-\frac{3+\sqrt{3}i}{6}\cdot\textnormal{log}\,(u-\overline{u_{0}})\text{}

Substitutions (
2.15
) finish the proof.
∎

3.
Function
T

As it will be shown later,
F_{\mu}
has the form

F_{\mu}(z)=zT^{-1}(W(z))\text{}

for
z\in Q
, where
T^{-1}
is the inverse of a suitable analytic extension of
T_{0}
, denoted by
T
. We shall define
T
in this section. We start with finding its domain, but in order to do so, we must first change coordinates. For our purposes, it suffices to parameterize only the upper half-plane
\mathbb{C}_{+}
(without one point). From now on,
\textnormal{Int}\,A
,
\textnormal{Cl}\,A
and
\partial A
stand for the interior, closure and boundary of a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, respectively.

Definition 3.1
.

Introduce the following parametrization of
\mathbb{C}_{+}\setminus\{u_{0}\}
:

(3.1)

\begin{cases}R=r_{2}/r_{1}\text{,}\\
\xi=\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2}\text{,}\end{cases}

with
0<r_{1}<r_{2}
and
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
.

It is also possible to parametrize
\mathbb{C}_{-}\setminus\{\overline{u_{0}}\}
in a similar way, but we do not need it.

Proposition 3.2
.

For any
u\in\mathbb{C}_{+}\setminus\{u_{0}\}
, it holds that

u=f(R,\xi)\text{,}

where
f\colon(1,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
is given by

(3.2)

f(R,\xi)=\frac{\sqrt{3}i(Re^{i\xi}-1)}{1-R^{2}}+u_{0}\text{.}

Proof.

First, observe that (
2.15
) implies that

r_{2}e^{i\varphi_{2}}=u-u_{0}+(u_{0}-\overline{u_{0}})=r_{1}e^{i\varphi_{1}}+\sqrt{3}i\text{.}

Combining it with (
3.1
), we get the system

\begin{cases}R\cdot r_{1}\cdot\cos(\xi-\varphi_{1})=r_{1}\cos\varphi_{1}\text{,}\\
R\cdot r_{1}\cdot\sin(\xi-\varphi_{1})=r_{1}\sin\varphi_{1}+\sqrt{3}\text{,}\end{cases}

which, using trigonometric identities, can be rewritten as

\begin{cases}(R\cos\xi-1)\cdot r_{1}\cos\varphi_{1}+R\sin\xi\cdot r_{1}\sin\varphi_{1}=0\text{,}\\
R\sin\xi\cdot r_{1}\cdot\cos\varphi_{1}-(R\cos\xi+1)\cdot r_{1}\sin\varphi_{1}=\sqrt{3}\text{.}\end{cases}

After substituting (
2.15
) and denoting
Re^{i\xi}
and
r_{1}e^{i\varphi_{1}}+u_{0}
by
p+iq
and
s+it
, respectively, we get

\begin{cases}(p-1)(s-1/2)+q(t-\sqrt{3}/2)=0\text{,}\\
q(s-1/2)-(p+1)(t-\sqrt{3}/2)=\sqrt{3}\text{.}\end{cases}

Since
R\neq 1
, we solve the above linear system with respect to
\tilde{s}=s-1/2
and
\tilde{t}=t-\sqrt{3}/2
by using Cramer’s rule. This leads to

s=\dfrac{-\sqrt{3}q}{1-R^{2}}+\dfrac{1}{2}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad t=\dfrac{\sqrt{3}(p-1)}{1-R^{2}}+\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\text{,}

and this is equivalent to (
3.2
).
∎

The function
g
defined below will be used to investigate the properties of
T
— a suitable branch of
\boldsymbol{T}
, which is more natural than investigating these properties by means of
f
. Strictly speaking, the expression
T(g(\eta,\xi))
will be studied.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
g\colon\Xi\to\mathbb{C}_{+}\setminus\{u_{0}\}
, with

\Xi=\{(\eta,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:-3\pi/2<\eta\leq\pi/2\text{ and }\xi<-\eta\}\text{,}

be given by

(3.3)

g(\eta,\xi)=f(e^{-\sqrt{3}(\eta+\xi)},\xi)\text{.}

We now introduce the family of curves by means of which we will define the domain of
T
.

Definition 3.4
.

Introduce a family of curves

(3.4)

\Gamma_{\eta}=\{u=g(\eta,\xi):\xi<-\eta\}\text{}

parameterized by
\xi
, where
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
, and let
\mathcal{G}=\{\Gamma_{\eta}:-3\pi/2<\eta\leq\pi/2\}
.

Remark 3.5
.

We did not consider all
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
while defining
\mathcal{G}
since for any
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
and any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, it holds that

g(\eta+2k\pi,\xi-2k\pi)=g(\eta,\xi)\text{}

and thus
\Gamma_{\eta+2k\pi}=\Gamma_{\eta}
.

Let us now give some properties of
g
(cf. Fig.
2
).

Lemma 3.6
.

The function
g
is a continuous bijection and the inverse
(g\restriction\textnormal{Int}\,\Xi)^{-1}
is continuous. Moreover,

g(\{\pi/2\}\times(-\infty,-\pi/2))=\Gamma_{\pi/2}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad g(\textnormal{Int}\,\Xi)=\mathbb{C}_{+}\setminus(\Gamma_{\pi/2}\cup\{u_{0}\})\text{.}

The latter image is an open, simply connected set.

Proof.

Consider the exterior of the unit disc

E=\{v\in\mathbb{C}:\lvert v\rvert>1\}\text{.}

In order to prove the lemma, let us represent
g
as a composition

g=f_{0}\circ h
, where
h\colon\Xi\to E
and
f_{0}\colon E\to\mathbb{C}_{+}\setminus\{u_{0}\}
are defined by

(3.5)

h(\eta,\xi)=\exp\big(-\sqrt{3}(\eta+\xi)+i\xi\big)\text{}

and by

(3.6)

f_{0}(v)=\frac{\sqrt{3}i(v-1)}{1-\lvert v\rvert^{2}}+u_{0}\text{,}

respectively. Let us prove all necessary properties of
f_{0}
and
h
. The function
f_{0}

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}

\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\Re H=\frac{\partial T_{1}}{\partial s}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\Re g+\frac{\partial T_{1}}{\partial t}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\Im g=\langle\nabla T_{1},\vec{u}_{\xi}\rangle\text{,}

where

\vec{u}_{\xi}\coloneqq\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}(\Re g,\Im g)\text{}

(for convenience, we dropped the arguments of the functions).
By Proposition

we have

0=\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\Im H=\frac{\partial T_{2}}{\partial s}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\Re g+\frac{\partial T_{2}}{\partial t}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\Im g\text{,}

i.e.
\nabla T_{2}\perp\vec{u}_{\xi}
. From the Cauchy–Riemann equations, also
\nabla T_{1}\perp\nabla T_{2}
.

In order to prove that
\xi\mapsto\Re H(\eta,\xi)
is strictly monotonic,
it remains to show that
\nabla T_{1}\parallel\vec{u}_{\xi}
. For this purpose, we need to demonstrate that all
\nabla T_{1}
,
\nabla T_{2}
and
\vec{u}_{\xi}
are nonzero vectors. Indeed, for any suitable
u
, we have

\widehat{T}^{\prime}(u)=\frac{u}{u^{2}-u+1}\text{,}

which is nonzero on
\mathbb{C}_{+}\setminus(\Gamma_{\pi/2}\cup\{u_{0}\})
, therefore both
\nabla T_{1}
and
\nabla T_{2}
are nonzero vectors.
In order to show that also
\vec{u}_{\xi}\neq(0,0)
, let

p+iq=Re^{i\xi}\text{,}\qquad\text{ with }R=e^{-\sqrt{3}(\eta+\xi)}\text{,}

for any
(\eta,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Using (
3.2
) and (
3.3
), we get

\frac{\partial g}{\partial\xi}(\eta,\xi)=\frac{-\sqrt{3}\big(p(1-R^{2})-\sqrt{3}q(1+R^{2})\big)+6i\big(R^{2}-1/2p(1+R^{2})-\sqrt{3}/6q(1-R^{2})\big)}{(1-R^{2})^{2}}

The above derivative is zero if and only if the following system is fulfilled:

\begin{cases}p(1-R^{2})-\sqrt{3}q(1+R^{2})&=0\text{,}\\
R^{2}-1/2p(1+R^{2})-\sqrt{3}/6q(1-R^{2})&=0\text{.}\end{cases}

If we multiply the latter equation by
-2p
and then we replace
p(1-R^{2})
by
\sqrt{3}q(1+R^{2})
in its third summand, we obtain

R^{2}\cdot((p-1)^{2}+q^{2})=0\text{,}

which gives two solutions:
(p,q)=(0,0)
and
(p,q)=(1,0)
, corresponding to
\eta+\xi=\infty
and
\xi=-\eta
, respectively.
None of these solutions belongs to
\textnormal{Int}\,\Xi
, therefore
\vec{u}_{\xi}\neq(0,0)
. Thus we get
\nabla T_{1}\parallel\vec{u}_{\xi}
, i.e.
(\partial/\partial\xi)\Re H
has a constant nonzero sign, which means that
\xi\mapsto\Re H(\eta,\xi)
is strictly monotonic and therefore
H
is indeed an injection.
∎

, we have

\begin{split}W_{1}(Q)&=\textnormal{Int}\,D\text{,}\\
W_{1}((\sqrt{2},\infty)\cup i\cdot\mathbb{R}_{+})&=\frac{1}{2}\textnormal{Log}\,(\mathbb{R}_{+}\setminus\{1\})=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\text{,}\\
W_{1}((0,\sqrt{2}))&=\frac{1}{2}\textnormal{ln}\,\lvert(-\infty,0)\rvert-\frac{i\pi}{2}=\mathbb{R}-\frac{i\pi}{2}\text{.}\end{split}

Let us now show that the function
W_{1}
is invertible and

W_{1}^{-1}(w)=\sqrt{2+\frac{2}{e^{2w}-1}}\text{,}

for
w\in D\setminus\{0\}
, where by the square-root function, we mean a branch

\sqrt{v}=\sqrt{r}\cdot e^{i\varphi/2}\text{,}\qquad\text{for $v=r\cdot e^{i\varphi}$ with $r>0$ and $-\pi/2<\varphi<3\pi/2$, }

whose restriction
\sqrt{\text{ . }}\colon\textnormal{Cl}\,\mathbb{C}_{+}\to\textnormal{Cl}\,Q
is a homeomorphism. The procedure of inverting looks as follows: let
z\in\textnormal{Cl}\,Q\setminus\{0,\sqrt{2}\}
and let
w=W_{1}(z)
. By (
5
), we have
w\in D\setminus\{0\}
and

(5.4)

\displaystyle 1+\frac{2}{z^{2}-2}

\displaystyle=e^{2w}

\displaystyle\in\textnormal{Cl}\,\mathbb{C}_{-}\setminus\{0,1\}\text{,}

\displaystyle z^{2}

\displaystyle=2+\frac{2}{e^{2w}-1}

\displaystyle\in\textnormal{Cl}\,\mathbb{C}_{+}\setminus\{0,2\}\text{,}

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2+\frac{2}{e^{2w}-1}}

\displaystyle\in\textnormal{Cl}\,Q\setminus\{0,\sqrt{2}\}\text{.}

The choice of the branch of the square-root function is based on this procedure. Looking at the just determined images and the inverse of
W_{1}
given by
z
, we conclude that
W_{1}\colon(\textnormal{Cl}\,Q\setminus\{0,\sqrt{2}\})\to D\setminus\{0\}
is a homeomorphism.
Let us now prove that

(5.5)

W_{1}^{-1}(w)=e^{w}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{2}{e^{2w}-1}}\text{.}

Indeed, if
w\in D\setminus\{0\}
, then
2/(e^{2w}-1)\in\textnormal{Cl}\,\mathbb{C}_{+}
, therefore the
RHS
of the above equality (which we denote by
z
) is well defined and continuous; so is the
LHS
. Moreover, since both
W_{1}^{-1}(w)
and
z
are always nonzero, it must hold that either
W_{1}^{-1}(w)=z
for each suitable
w
, or
W_{1}^{-1}(w)=-z
for each suitable
w
. But the former equality is obvious for
w\in(0,\infty)
and thus (
5.5
) holds for all
w\in D\setminus\{0\}
.

Since our measure is symmetric with respect to
0
, it suffices to consider only
x\geq 0
and
\widehat{F}_{\mu}
and
\widehat{G}_{\mu}
for arguments in
\textnormal{Cl}\,Q
.
Let
x\geq 0
and let
(z_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence convergent to
x
, with the elements belonging to
\textnormal{Cl}\,Q\setminus\{0,\sqrt{2},\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}\}
. Let
w_{n}=W(z_{n})
. Now, without loss of generality, we assume that there are two possibilities (see Fig.
4
): either there exists a sequence
(\eta_{n},\xi_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
of elements from
\Theta
such that
w_{n}=H(\eta_{n},\xi_{n})
in which case, for any positive
n
, we put
u_{n}=g(\eta_{n},\xi_{n})
, or (only if
x\leq\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}
) there exists a sequence
(u_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
of nonnegative numbers such that
w_{n}=T_{0}(u_{n})
. In the latter case,
u_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, whereas in the former one,
\Im u_{n}\neq 0
, for any positive
n
.
We will use (A)–(C), the equality (
4.4
) and (A*)–(C*) from the proofs of the mentioned lemmas and of Lemma
4.4
while computing certain limits below.
Let us consider four cases.

Case 1:
x=0
.
In this case,
\Re w_{n}\to-\infty
. Let us demonstrate that

(5.6)

z_{n}\cdot T^{-1}(w_{n})=\sqrt{\frac{2}{e^{2w_{n}}-1}}\cdot e^{w_{n}}\cdot u_{n}\to\sqrt{2}i\cdot e^{-\sqrt{3}\pi/9}\text{.}

If
u_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
for each
n
, then it follows immediately from (
2.4
).
Otherwise, by (B*) from the proof of Lemma
4.4
, we have
\xi_{n}/\eta_{n}\to(-1)^{+}
. We now show that this implies that

(5.7)

u_{n}e^{w_{n}}\to e^{-\sqrt{3}\pi/9}\text{.}

Indeed, let us write

u_{n}e^{w_{n}}=\lvert u_{n}\rvert\cdot e^{w_{n}-i\eta_{n}/2}\cdot e^{i(\textnormal{Arg}\,u_{n}+\eta_{n}/2)}\text{}

and show that

(5.8)

\lvert u_{n}\rvert\cdot e^{w_{n}-i\eta_{n}/2}\to e^{-\sqrt{3}\pi/9}\text{.}

By (
4.2
), we have

\begin{split}\lvert u_{n}\rvert\cdot e^{w_{n}-i\eta_{n}/2}&=\exp\Bigg(-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{6}\bigg(\textnormal{Arg}\,(R_{n}e^{i\xi_{n}}-1)-\textnormal{Arg}\,(u_{n}-\overline{u_{0}})+2\cdot\bigg\lceil\frac{\xi_{n}-\pi}{2\pi}\bigg\rceil\cdot\pi+\frac{\pi}{6}\bigg)\\
&\ \cdot\sqrt{\frac{\lvert u_{n}\rvert^{2}}{\lvert u_{n}-u_{0}\rvert\cdot\lvert u_{n}-\overline{u_{0}}\rvert}}\Bigg)\text{,}\end{split}

where
R_{n}=e^{-\sqrt{3}(\eta_{n}+\xi_{n})}
. For
n
large enough, we have
2\Re u_{n}>1
. Indeed, since
R_{n}>0
and
\eta_{n}/\xi_{n}\to(-1)^{+}
, we must have
\xi_{n}>0
. Since
\eta_{n}+\xi_{n}<0
and
-\pi\leq\eta_{n}\leq 0
, we have
\xi_{n}<\pi
. Therefore
\sin\xi_{n}>0
, i.e.
\Re u_{n}>1/2
. By the definition of
g
(see Definition
3.3
), we have

R_{n}e^{i\xi_{n}}-1=i\cdot(u_{n}-u_{0})\cdot\frac{R_{n}^{2}-1}{\sqrt{3}}\text{,}

therefore, since
\Re(u_{n}-u_{0})>0
, we have

\textnormal{Arg}\,(R_{n}e^{i\xi_{n}}-1)=\textnormal{Arg}\,(i\cdot(u_{n}-u_{0}))=\frac{\pi}{2}+\textnormal{Arg}\,(u_{n}-u_{0})\text{.}

Moreover, since
\Re(u_{n}-\overline{u_{0}})>0
, we use Lagrange Mean Value Theorem, getting

\textnormal{Arg}\,(u_{n}-u_{0})-\textnormal{Arg}\,(u_{n}-\overline{u_{0}})=\frac{\sqrt{3}\Re(u_{n}-u_{0})}{\lvert u^{\prime}_{n}\rvert^{2}}\text{,}

where
\Re u^{\prime}_{n}=\Re(u_{n}-u_{0})
and
\Im(u_{n}-u_{0})<\Im u^{\prime}_{n}<\Im(u_{n}-\overline{u_{0}})
. Since
\lvert u_{n}\rvert\to\infty
(by (A) from the proof of Lemma
3.9
), we get

\textnormal{Arg}\,(R_{n}e^{i\xi_{n}}-1)-\textnormal{Arg}\,(u_{n}-\overline{u_{0}})\to\frac{\pi}{2}\text{.}

Since
\xi_{n}\in(0,\pi)
, we have
\lceil(\xi_{n}-\pi)/2\pi\rceil=0
and thus (
5.8
) holds.

It suffices to show that
\textnormal{Arg}\,u_{n}+\eta_{n}/2\to 0
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
\eta_{n}\to\eta
. From Lemma
3.6
we get
\Re u_{n}>0
and
\Im u_{n}>0
, hence
\textnormal{Arg}\,u_{n}\in(0,\pi/2)
. We consider two cases. First, let
-\pi<\eta\leq 0
. From (
3.13
) we see that
\Re u_{n}\to\infty
and

\frac{\Im u_{n}}{\Re u_{n}}=\frac{1-\cos\xi_{n}}{\sin\xi_{n}}\cdot\big(1+o(1)\big)+o(1)\to\tan\bigg(-\frac{\eta}{2}\bigg)\text{.}

If
\eta=-\pi
, then, also from (
3.13
),
\Im u_{n}\to\infty
and

\frac{\Re u_{n}}{\Im u_{n}}=\frac{\sin\xi_{n}}{1-\cos\xi_{n}}\cdot\big(1+o(1)\big)+o(1)\to 0^{+}\text{.}

This yields

\frac{\Im u_{n}}{\Re u_{n}}\to\tan\bigg(-\frac{\eta}{2}\bigg)\text{}

with the convention
\tan(\pi/2)=\infty
. Therefore
\textnormal{Arg}\,u_{n}+\eta_{n}\to 0
and the proof of (
5.7
) and thus that of (
5.6
) is complete. Note that the limit in (
5.6
) is pure imaginary, which implies that

\lim\limits_{z\to 0+0^{+}\cdot i}G_{\mu}(z)=-\frac{\sqrt{2}i}{2}e^{\sqrt{3}\pi/9}\text{,}

accordingly to the fact that
x\mapsto\Re G_{\mu}(x+iy)
is an odd, and
x\mapsto\Im G_{\mu}(x+iy)
is an even function.

Case 2:
x\in(0,\sqrt{2})
.
From Theorem
4.5
we have

F_{\mu}(z_{n})\to xT^{-1}\Bigg(\frac{1}{2}\textnormal{ln}\,\bigg(\dfrac{2}{2-x^{2}}-1\bigg)-\dfrac{\pi i}{2}\Bigg)=F_{\mu}(x)\text{.}

Case 3:
x=\sqrt{2}
.
We see that
\Re w_{n}\to\infty
and by the definition of
T^{-1}
and, by (A*) from the proof of Lemma
4.4
, we see that
F_{\mu}(w_{n})\to(\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{6}i)/2
.

Case 4:
x>\sqrt{2}
.
Again, Theorem
4.5
implies that

F_{\mu}(z_{n})\to xT^{-1}\Bigg(\frac{1}{2}\textnormal{ln}\,\bigg(1+\dfrac{2}{x^{2}-2}\bigg)\Bigg)=F_{\mu}(x)\text{.}

What we just have proved is that the function
\widehat{G}_{\mu}
is continuous. But its limit as
z
approaches
\mp\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}
is
\infty_{\mathbb{C}}
since
F_{\mu}(x)=0
for
x=\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}
. We now show that the function

z\mapsto\sqrt{\lvert z^{2}-(2+\gamma_{0})\rvert}\cdot\lvert\widehat{G}_{\mu}(z)\rvert

is bounded on any closed upper half-disk of center
0
(with the points
\mp\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}
excluded). Note that
w_{n}\to W(\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}})=\sqrt{3}\pi/9
. By (
2.5
) and Theorem
4.5
, there exists a sequence
(u_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that

w_{n}=\int_{u_{n}}^{1}\frac{\omega}{\omega^{2}-\omega+1}\,d\omega\text{,}

for each positive
n
. Since
u_{n}\to 0
, this integral is well-defined (i.e. there is no problem with the poles of the integrand). Using the definition of
T^{-1}
and the Maclaurin expansion of the above integral, we obtain

\frac{\lvert T^{-1}(w_{n})\rvert}{\sqrt{\lvert z_{n}-\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}\rvert}}=\frac{\lvert T^{-1}(w_{n})\rvert}{\sqrt{\big\lvert w_{n}-\frac{\sqrt{3}\pi}{9}\big\rvert}}\cdot\sqrt{\Bigg\lvert\frac{w_{n}-\frac{\sqrt{3}\pi}{9}}{z_{n}-\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}}\Bigg\rvert}=\frac{\lvert u_{n}\rvert}{\sqrt{\lvert-u_{n}^{2}+o(u_{n}^{2})\rvert}}\cdot\sqrt{\lvert 1+o(1)\rvert}\to 1\text{.}

We are now in the position to show that
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
and its density function is

-\frac{1}{\pi}\Im\widehat{G}_{\mu}(x)\text{,}

for
x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{-\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}},\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}\}
. Indeed, if
\phi
is continuous and compactly supported on
\mathbb{R}
, we have

\lim_{y\to 0^{+}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(x)\Im G_{\mu}(x+yi)dx=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(x)\Im\widehat{G}_{\mu}(x)dx\text{,}

which follows from Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem with the majorant

\frac{\lvert\phi(x)\rvert}{\pi}\cdot\frac{\sup_{z}\big(\sqrt{\lvert z^{2}-(2+\gamma_{0})\rvert}\lvert G_{\mu}(z)\rvert\big)}{\sqrt{\lvert x+\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}\rvert}\sqrt{\lvert x-\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}\rvert}}\text{,}

where the supremum is taken over
\textnormal{supp}\,{\phi}+i\cdot(0,\varepsilon]
, for some
\varepsilon>0
, and where
\lvert x\rvert\neq\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}
. This proves that
\rho
is indeed the density of
\mu
.
∎
