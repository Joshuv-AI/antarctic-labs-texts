Fractional Klein-Gordon Equation on AdS 2+1
Pablo Basteiro, Janine Elfert, Johanna Erdmenger, and

Abstract
We propose a covariant definition of the fractional Klein-Gordon equation with long-range interactions independent of the metric of the underlying manifold. As an example we consider the fractional Klein-Gordon equation on AdS 2+1 , computing the explicit kernel representation of the fractional Laplace-Beltrami operator as well as the two-point propagator of the fractional Klein-Gordon equation. Our results suggest that the propagator only exists if the mass is small compared to the inverse AdS radius, presumably because the AdS space expands faster with distance as a flat space of the same dimension. Our results are expected to be useful in particular for new applications of the AdS/CFT correspondence within statistical mechanics and quantum information. 1 1 1 Submitted to J. Phys. Introduction

In quantum field theory and statistical physics, elementary interactions are usually assumed to be local. For example, Lagrange densities are typically defined in terms of local expressions in the fields and their derivatives, where the derivatives can be understood as operators which couple infinitesimally neighbored field values. Similarly, in statistical physics the most lattice modes are defined in terms of nearest-neighbor interactions.

The premise of local interactions does not necessarily imply that the resulting physics is local as well. For example, the electromagnetic field, defined by a local Lagrangian, exhibits Coulomb forces whose range is infinite. Further examples include critical phenomena in statistical physics and conformal field theories which exhibit long-range correlations with power-law characteristics.

Although in most physical situations the assumption of local interactions appears to be natural, it is interesting to study what changes if we allow the elementary interactions to become non-local. This question has been investigated for long time and has led to a large variety of studies in different contexts (for a review see e.g.
[
1
]
). The impact of non-local interactions has been studied particularly thoroughly in the context of phase transitions, critical phenomena and conformal field theories. Although the results are so diverse that they cannot be summarized in a few lines, a generic picture emerges which can be sketched as follows:

•

Systems with non-local interactions extending over a
finite
range or with
exponentially
decaying coupling strength behave on large scales as if they were local. This includes, for example, next-nearest neighbor interactions in lattice models or the addition of irrelevant higher-order derivatives in quantum field theories. For instance, the phase transition in an Ising model with non-local but exponentially decaying coupling constants still belongs to the Ising universality class.

•

Non-local interactions with long-range couplings decaying asymptotically as a power law
s^{-\kappa}
with the distance
s
are capable to entirely change the macroscopic properties of a physical system. For example, an Ising model with non-local algebraically decaying coupling constants may exhibit a different type of universal behavior on large scales.

In many of these studies algebraically decaying couplings are chosen because this choice allows one to introduce genuine long-range interactions without imposing a particular scale. Therefore, when studying scale-invariant phenomena such as second-order phase transitions or conformal field theories, one expects that scale invariance is not broken by introducing such long-range interactions.

The impact of long-range couplings decaying as
s^{-\kappa}
depends of course on the value of the exponent
\kappa
, which plays here the role of an additional control parameter. Therefore, it is interesting to study how the macroscopic properties of a system change if this exponent is varied. Clearly, for
\kappa=0
the system is globally coupled, resulting in some kind of mean-field behavior. On the other hand, taking
\kappa\to\infty
, the interaction strength decays so rapidly that we expect to recover the short-range behavior with local interactions. Between these two extremes such models are expected to exhibit an interesting crossover behavior.
This crossover regime has been analyzed in a large variety of systems in different contexts, including anomalous diffusion
[
2
]
, Lévy flights
[
3
,
4
]
, equilibrium critical phenomena
[
5
,
6
]
, non-equilibrium phase transitions and driven systems
[
7
,
8
]
, epidemic spreading
[
9
,
10
]
, as well as field-theoretic models
[
11
]
. Most of these studies confirm that the crossover between the short-range and the mean-field regime is observed only in a finite range of the control exponent, where one observes a
\kappa
-dependent universal behavior with continuously varying critical exponents.

Algebraically decaying long-range interactions are closely related to a special topic in mathematics called
fractional calculus

[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
which deals with non-integer derivatives and integrals
[
16
]
. This relation is due to the fact that fractional derivatives can be represented by linear integral kernels with power-law characteristics. Therefore, as a rough guideline, if a short-range model is defined in a continuum description in terms of ordinary derivatives, then the corresponding long-range variant can be obtained by simply replacing ordinary derivatives with fractional ones.

The vast majority of studies on algebraically decaying long-range interactions are concerned with non-relativistic systems in flat geometries (for a review see e.g.
[
17

). Considerable less studies are available for relativistic systems such as the fractional wave equation
[
18

and its massive counterpart, the fractional Klein-Gordon equation

. The same applies to studies of systems with fractional long-range interactions in curved geometries. Therefore, it is interesting to investigate the question whether it is possible to implement long-range interactions by fractional derivatives in relativistic systems on a curved background in a covariant way and to analyze their properties.

In this paper we consider the example of a fractional Klein-Gordon
equation living on curved space. As a curved background, it is natural
to choose a spacetime with maximal symmetry and constant
curvature. However, a compact space with positive curvature such as a
sphere with a finite circumference would truncate the interaction
range. For this reason it is an obvious choice to study a maximally symmetric space with negative curvature, an Anti-de-Sitter (AdS) space.

For reader with a statistical mechanics background, we note that AdS space plays an important role in many areas of physics and
mathematics. In particular, it is central to the AdS/CFT

is a particular matrix large
N

The AdS/CFT correspondence has
allowed for a plethora of progress to be made with regard to

provided new insight into specific areas of physics, such as

, strongly coupled condensed

, quantum information theory

and cosmology

(see

It is expected that in the large

correspondence satisfies the property of

to which the bulk gravity theory is local

,
also in other string theory parameter regions as for instance for

[

. However, non-localities
may potentially arise when venturing beyond the large

Here, we take a new step in this direction by considering fractional
equations of motion for a scalar field in AdS space. We believe our

In view of these potential applications, we refer to the recent work

Instances of fractional calculus within the AdS/CFT correspondence are

In order to implement long-range interactions on curved spaces in a physically meaningful way, we propose a method to “fractionalize” a differential equation of motion covariantly independent of the background metric. We demonstrate this approach for the scalar Klein-Gordon equation and its massless special case, the wave equation, living in AdS space. For simplicity, we decided to restrict the analysis to

One should keep in mind that the AdS space, although being non-compact, singles out a particular scale, namely, the AdS radius

Our main results are the following:

-

We propose a simple method to introduce long-range interactions covariantly independent of the background metric.

-

For the special case of the scalar Klein-Gordon equation in AdS

we derive an explicit kernel representation of the fractional operator which allows us to read off the actual interaction strength as a function of the geodesic distance.

-

We find an explicit expression for the two-point propagator (Green’s function) of the fractional wave equation on AdS
2+1
as well as a series expansion for the massive propagator of the fractional Klein-Gordon equation.

-

Finally, we verify numerically that the differential equation in its kernel representation applied to the propagator exhibits the desired properties.

2
Ordinary Klein-Gordon equation on AdS
2+1

Setting up a fractional equation of motion for a relativistic system, we have to choose a covariant approach, that is, the equation should be invariant under diffeomorphisms which generalize Lorentz transformations in curved spaces. The easiest way to achieve this goal is to reformulate the problem solely in terms of geodesic distances. Since geodesic distances are covariant by themselves, this ensures the covariance of the whole approach. In this section we demonstrate how this can be done in the case of the scalar Klein-Gordon equation (KGE) on AdS
2+1
.

2.1
Ordinary Klein-Gordon equation in flat Minkowski space

Before starting let us fix some notations by briefly recalling the situation in a flat (2+1)-dimensional Minkowski space. Here the Klein-Gordon equation is given by

(\square-m^{2})\phi(\mathbf{x})\;=\;0\,,

(1)

where
\mathbf{x}=(t,x,y)
denotes the position vector,
\phi(\mathbf{x})
is a real-valued scalar field,
m\geq 0
is the mass and
\square=\eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}=-\partial_{t}^{2}+\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2}
is the d’Alembert operator. Throughout this paper we use the
mostly-plus
convention
\eta_{\mu\nu}=\mbox{diag}(-1,1,1)
which leads to the minus sign in front of the squared mass.

In what follows we are concerned with solutions of the KGE which depend solely on the relativistic distance
s=\sqrt{x_{\mu}x^{\mu}}=\sqrt{-t^{2}+x^{2}+y^{2}}
from the origin, analogous to radially symmetric functions in Euclidean spaces. The d’Alembert operator acts on such functions as
\square f(s)=f^{\prime\prime}(s)+\frac{2}{s}f^{\prime}(s)
, turning the KGE into

\phi^{\prime\prime}(s)+\frac{2}{s}\phi^{\prime}(s)-m^{2}\phi(s)\;=\;0

(2)

which can also be written in an equivalent factorized form as

\frac{1}{s^{2}}\partial_{s}\,s^{2}\partial_{s}\,\phi(s)-m^{2}\phi(s)\;=\;0\,.

(3)

For
s\neq 0
the two fundamental solutions of the homogeneous KGE are

\phi_{\pm}(s)=\frac{e^{\pm ms}}{4\pi s}\,.

(4)

In practice, it is often simpler to consider the analytic continuation of the Klein Gordon equation to imaginary time
\tau:=it
, where
s=\sqrt{\tau^{2}+x^{2}+y^{2}}
is the Euclidean distance and
\square=\partial_{\tau}^{2}+\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2}
is just the ordinary 3D Laplacian. Note that in this case the equations (
2
)-(
4
) remain valid, that is, the radial representation is correct in the Lorentzian as well as in the Euclidean setting.

For solving the inhomogeneous KGE, we are especially interested in the Green’s function defined by

(\square-m^{2})G(\mathbf{x})\;=\;\delta^{3}(\mathbf{x})\,.

(5)

which translates into

G^{\prime\prime}(s)+\frac{2}{s}G^{\prime}(s)-m^{2}G(s)\;=\;\frac{\delta(s)}{2\pi s^{2}}\,.

(6)

In the Euclidean case the normalizable solution reads

G(s)\;=\;-\frac{e^{-ms}}{4\pi s}\qquad\mbox{for }s>0\,.

(7)

In Minkowski space one obtains the same Green’s function with an imaginary factor in front.

2.2
Ordinary Klein-Gordon equation on AdS
2+1
in terms of geodesic distances

Let us now turn to the (non-fractional) KGE on an Anti-de-Sitter (AdS) space in 2+1 dimensions. The AdS
2+1
space is defined as a maximally symmetric Riemann manifold with negative curvature and can be represented in various coordinate systems
[
39
]
. In what follows we use Poincaré patch coordinates
(t,x,z)
with the metric

g_{\mu\nu}=\frac{L^{2}}{z^{2}}\,\mbox{diag}(-1,1,1)\,,\quad g^{\mu\nu}=\frac{z^{2}}{L^{2}}\,\mbox{diag}(-1,1,1)\,,\quad\sqrt{|g|}=\frac{L^{3}}{z^{3}}\,,

(8)

where
L>0
is the AdS radius. In these coordinates the Laplace-Beltrami operator, which generalizes the d’Alembert operator in a curved space, reads

\square_{g}\;=\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\partial_{\mu}\sqrt{|g|}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\nu}\;=\;\frac{z^{2}}{L^{2}}\Bigl(-\partial_{t}^{2}+\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2}-\frac{1}{z}\partial_{z}\Bigr)\,.

(9)

The
chordal distance

\xi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
between two points
\mathbf{x}=(t,x,z)
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(t^{\prime},x^{\prime},z^{\prime})
(i.e. the straight-line distance in the embedding space of the AdS hyperboloid) is defined as

\xi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\;=\;\frac{2zz^{\prime}}{z^{2}+{z^{\prime}}^{2}-(t-t^{\prime})^{2}+(x-x^{\prime})^{2}}\,.

(10)

The
geodesic distance

s(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
between two points
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
, defined as the length of the geodesic line within the AdS manifold, can be expressed in terms of the chordal distance via

s(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\;=\;L\,\mbox{arccosh}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\xi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}\Bigr)\,.

(11)

Since the AdS space is homogeneous (i.e. it looks the same from all points), we can declare the point

x^{\prime}=t^{\prime}=0\,,\quad z^{\prime}=1

(12)

as the origin of the coordinate system. Again we are interested in “radially symmetric” functions
f
which depend only on the geodesic distance from the origin:

f(t,x,z)\;\equiv\;f(s)\;=\;f\Bigl(L\,\mbox{arccosh}\Bigl(\frac{1+z^{2}-t^{2}+x^{2})}{2z}\Bigr)\Bigr)\,.

(13)

If the Laplace-Beltrami operator acts on such a function, its action can be rewritten as

\square_{g}f(s)\;=\;\Bigl[\partial_{s}^{2}+\frac{2}{L}\coth\Bigl(\frac{s}{L}\Bigr)\partial_{s}\Bigr]f(s)\,,

(14)

turning the KGE into

\phi^{\prime\prime}(s)+\frac{2}{L}\coth\Bigl(\frac{s}{L}\Bigr)\phi^{\prime}(s)-m^{2}\phi(s)\;=\;0\,

(15)

or in the factorized form

\frac{1}{{\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{s}{L}\right)}}\,\partial_{s}\,\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{s}{L}\right)\,\partial_{s}\,\phi(s)-m^{2}\phi(s)\;=\;0\,.

(16)

As can be seen, taking
L\to\infty
this equation reduces to the KGE (
2
) in a flat space. The same equation is obtained in the Euclidean case if
t
is replaced by
\tau=it
.

2.3
Green’s function of the ordinary Klein-Gordon equation on AdS
2+1

To find the Green’s function of the short-range KGE on AdS
2+1
, we first express the defining inhomogeneous differential equation

(\square_{g}-m^{2})G(\mathbf{x})\;=\;\frac{\delta^{3}(\mathbf{x})}{\sqrt{|g|}}

(17)

in terms of the geodesic distance
s
. Since the r.h.s. contributes only at the origin where
\sqrt{|g|}=1
, we can equivalently study the equation
(\square_{g}-m^{2})G(\mathbf{x})=\delta^{3}(\mathbf{x})
. Moreover, since volume integrals in the Euclidean AdS
2+1
can be expressed as

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mbox{d}^{3}x\,f(\mathbf{x})=4\pi L^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}s\,\sinh^{2}\bigl(\frac{s}{L}\bigr)f(s)

(18)

it is clear that
\delta^{3}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\delta(s)}{2\pi L^{2}\sinh^{2}(s/L)}
. Thus in terms of geodesic distances the Green’s function
G(s)
is defined by the the inhomogeneous equation

G^{\prime\prime}(s)+\frac{2}{L}\coth\Bigl(\frac{s}{L}\Bigr)G^{\prime}(s)-m^{2}G(s)\;=\;\frac{\delta(s)}{2\pi L^{2}\sinh^{2}\bigl(\frac{s}{L}\bigr)}\,.

(19)

This equation has two solutions. Only one of them is normalizable, being given by

G(s)\;=\;-\frac{\exp\Bigl(-\frac{s}{L}\sqrt{1+L^{2}m^{2}}\Bigr)}{4\pi L\sinh\bigl(\frac{s}{L}\bigr)}\,.

(20)

In the limit
L\to\infty
this solution consistently reduces to (
7
). Note that this solution is compatible with existing results in the literature (see
A
) and that it is formally valid even for negative squared mass above the so-called Breitenloher-Freedman bound
m^{2}>-L^{-2}

[
40
]
.

3
Fractional Klein-Gordon equation on AdS
2+1

In this section we turn to the construction of a fractional version of the KGE. As a conceptual problem it turns out that the resulting equation depends crucially on the specific way in which the long-range interactions are implemented. Naively the simplest approach would be to just replace ordinary with fractional derivatives. However, we observed that in a curved space the resulting equation depends on the specific representation in which this replacement is made. Given this non-uniqueness, which of these variants is physically reasonable? In order to avoid this ambiguity, we will adopt a scheme suggested by Bochner
[
41
]
which allows us to define a fractional Laplacian independent of the structure of the underlying manifold.

3.1
Fractional derivatives

Before starting, let us briefly summarize some basic facts about fractional derivatives. In the literature one finds a confusing variety of definitions
[
12
]
which differ in their symmetry properties and function spaces. In practical terms, fractional derivatives are most easily introduced by looking at their action in Fourier space. There are mainly two classes of fractional derivatives. The first one comprises
directed
fractional derivatives which act in Fourier space as

\frac{\mbox{d}^{\alpha}}{\mbox{d}t^{\alpha}}\,e^{i\omega t}=(i\omega)^{\alpha}\,e^{i\omega t}\,,

(21)

where
\alpha>0
is a control exponent. These variants generalize the first derivative (the generator of translations) and are often used to implement algebraically distributed waiting times in statistical mechanics
[
3
,
42
]
. The second class, on which we will focus in the present work, comprises
isotropic
fractional derivatives which act on a plane wave according to

\Delta^{\alpha}\,e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}=-(k^{2})^{\alpha}\,e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}\,.

(22)

These derivatives generalize the second-order Laplacian (the generator of diffusion) and are often used to model isotropic Lévy flights in space
[
43
]
. Similarly, in the KGE, we can define a fractional version of the d’Alembert operator in a relativistic setting via

\square^{\alpha}\,e^{ik_{\mu}x^{\mu}}\;=\;-(k_{\mu}k^{\mu})^{\alpha}\,e^{ik_{\mu}x^{\mu}}\,,

(23)

where
\alpha>0
. By definition this operator is linear and obeys the composition law

\square^{\alpha}\square^{\beta}\;=\;-\square^{\alpha+\beta}\,.

(24)

Moreover, we expect to retrieve the usual short-range d’Alembertian in the limit
\alpha\to 1
.

As any linear operator, the fractional d’Alembert operator can be represented by an integral kernel. As outlined in
[
44
]
, there are several equivalent integral representations. One of them is the so-called singular integral representation in
d
dimensions:

\square^{\alpha}\,f(\mathbf{x})\;=\;-\frac{4^{\alpha}\Gamma\bigl(\frac{d+1}{2}+\alpha\bigr)}{\pi^{\frac{d+1}{2}}\Gamma(-\alpha)}\int\mbox{d}^{d+1}z\,\frac{f(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{z})-f(\mathbf{x})}{\bigl(z_{\mu}z^{\mu}\bigr)^{\frac{d+1}{2}+\alpha}}\,.

(25)

This representation is important because it gives us a direct intuition, namely, it can be interpreted as a non-local transport process (Lévy flight) from the position
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{z}
weighted by the relativistic distance
s=\sqrt{z_{\mu}z^{\mu}}
raised to the power
d+1+2\alpha
. Thus, by replacing an ordinary by a fractional d’Alembert operator we can effectively implement long-range interactions with power-law characteristics.

3.2
Bochner’s approach to fractional derivatives

On curved manifolds, it is not so obvious how to define a fractional version of the Laplace-Beltrami operator defined in (
9
) since Fourier techniques are no longer available.

One possibility would be to simply replace ordinary by fractional derivatives. We first tried this in Minkowski space using the factorized form of the KGE in Eq. (
3
). More specifically, we replaced the partial derivatives by fractional Gerasimov-Caputo derivatives because they return zero on constant functions and reproduce ordinary derivatives in a certain limit. We calculated the corresponding kernel representation and applied it to a exponential functions and powers, getting consistent results. However, we could not reproduce the concatenation law (
24
). On AdS
2+1
, the implementation and the physical meaning of this approach was even less clear.

Therefore, instead of guessing replacement rules, one needs a physically consistent and transparent way for defining a fractional Laplace-Beltrami operator which does not depend on the structure of the underlying manifold. In this paper we suggest to adopt an approach that goes back to S. Bochner
[
41
,
44
]
. Accordingly, any linear operator
\mathbf{L}
acting on some function space can be associated with a fractional version
\mathbf{L}^{\alpha}
defined by

\mathbf{L}^{\alpha}\;:=\;-\frac{1}{\Gamma(-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}\tau\,\tau^{-1-\alpha}\,\bigl(e^{\tau\mathbf{L}}-\mathds{1}\bigl)\,.\qquad\qquad 0<\alpha<1

(26)

In the literature the operator
\mathbf{K}_{\tau}=e^{\tau\mathbf{L}}
is known as the
heat kernel
of
\mathbf{L}
because
\mathbf{K}_{\tau}
is the formal solution of the generalized heat conduction equation
\frac{\mbox{d}}{\mbox{d}\tau}\mathbf{K}_{\tau}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{K}_{\tau}
.

As can be easily proven, Bochner’s definition nicely reproduces the short-range operator in the limit
\alpha\to 1
on a suitable function space. In this limit the prefactor in front of the integral goes to zero as
1-\alpha
, compensated by an increasingly diverging contribution of the integral for small
\tau
, governed by the first-order contribution in the Taylor expansion of
(e^{\tau\mathbf{L}}-\mathds{1})
.

Note that Bochner’s representation is completely independent of the dimension and the metric of the manifold. Moreover, its interpretation is very transparent from the physical point of view. Suppose that
\mathbf{L}
is the generator of some physical process, let it be translation or diffusion. Then this process is carried out by exponentiation over some “distance”
\tau
, and the result is weighted by a fractional power of this distance.

3.3
Fractional Klein-Gordon equation in AdS
2+1

If we apply Bochner’s definition (
26
) to the d’Alembert operator in a flat Minkowski space we get the fractional operator

\square^{\alpha}\,f(\mathbf{x})\;=\;-\frac{1}{\Gamma(-\alpha)}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}\tau\,\tau^{-1-\alpha}\,\bigl(e^{\tau\square}-1)f(\mathbf{x})\,.

(27)

Inserting a plane wave
f(\mathbf{x})=e^{ik_{\mu}x^{\mu}}
with
\square f(\mathbf{x})=-k_{\mu}k^{\mu}f(\mathbf{x})
it is easy to check that

\square^{\alpha}\,e^{ik_{\mu}x^{\mu}}=-\frac{e^{ik_{\mu}x^{\mu}}}{\Gamma(-\alpha)}\,\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}\tau\,\tau^{-1-\alpha}\,\bigl(e^{-\tau k_{\mu}k^{\mu}}-1)}_{=\Gamma(-\alpha)(k_{\mu}k^{\mu})^{\alpha}}=-(k_{\mu}k^{\mu})^{\alpha}\,e^{ik_{\mu}x^{\mu}},

(28)

hence the definition is compatible with (
23
) and (
25
). This encourages us to
define
the fractional Laplace-Beltrami operator
\square_{g}^{\alpha}
on AdS
2+1
in the same way as

\square_{g}^{\alpha}\;:=\;-\frac{1}{\Gamma(-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}\tau\,\tau^{-1-\alpha}\,\bigl(e^{\tau\square_{g}}-\mathds{1}\bigl)\,,

(29)

where
\square_{g}
is the non-fractional Laplace-Beltrami operator defined in (
9
) and (
14
) and where
0<\alpha<1
is the factional control parameter. More specifically, if we apply this operator to a function
f
, the above definition reads

[\square_{g}^{\alpha}f](\mathbf{x})\;=\;-\frac{1}{\Gamma(-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}\tau\,\tau^{-1-\alpha}\,\Bigl(\bigl[e^{\tau\square_{g}}f\bigr](\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x})\Bigr)\,.

(30)

As argued above, we expect to retrieve the short-range operator the limit
\alpha\to 1
, that is
\square_{g}=\lim_{\alpha\to 1}\square_{g}^{\alpha}
. Moreover, one can show that this operator is compatible with the composition law
\square^{\alpha}\square^{\beta}\;=\;-\square^{\alpha+\beta}
, allowing one to extend the range to
\alpha>1
.

4
Kernel representation

4.1
Kernel representation in geodesic distances

As any linear operator, the heat kernel
\mathbf{K}_{\tau}=e^{\tau\square_{g}}
in the expression above can be represented as an integral

[e^{\tau\square_{g}}f](\mathbf{x})\;=\;\int\mbox{d}^{3}y\,\sqrt{|g|}\,K_{\tau}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,f(\mathbf{y})

(31)

with a suitable kernel function
K_{\tau}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
to be determined. Assuming that this kernel function depends only on the geodesic distance
s(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
between the points
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
and likewise that
f
depends only on the geodesic distance from the origin
s(\mathbf{y})
, i.e.,

K_{\tau}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\equiv K_{\tau}\bigl(s(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\bigr)\,,\qquad\quad f\bigl(\mathbf{y}\bigr)\equiv f\bigl(s(\mathbf{y})\bigr)\,,

(32)

we expect the result
e^{\tau\square_{g}}f
to exhibit the same type of symmetry. This allows us to reformulate the integral (
31
) solely in terms of geodesic distances
s=s(\mathbf{x})
,
s^{\prime}=s(\mathbf{y})
, and
s^{\prime\prime}=s(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
(for details see
B
):

[e^{\tau\square_{g}}f](s)\;=\;\frac{2\pi L}{\sinh\bigl(\frac{s}{L}\bigr)}\,\,\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}s^{\prime}\,\sinh\Bigl(\frac{s^{\prime}}{L}\Bigr)\,f(s^{\prime})\,\underbrace{\int_{|s-s^{\prime}|}^{s+s^{\prime}}\mbox{d}s^{\prime\prime}\,\sinh\Bigl(\frac{s^{\prime\prime}}{L}\Bigr)\,\,K_{\tau}(s^{\prime\prime})}_{=:\,J_{\tau}(s,s^{\prime})}\,.

(33)

Thus the determination of the kernel representation essentially amounts to computing the inner integral, denoted as
J_{\tau}(s,s^{\prime})
. Please keep in mind that the expression (
33
) is valid only in 2+1 dimensions.

Inserting the heat kernel (
33
) into Eq. (
30
) and using that
\mathds{1}=e^{\tau\square_{g}}\mathds{1}
we obtain

\eqalign{[\square_{g}^{\alpha}f](s)&=-\frac{1}{\Gamma(-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}\tau\,\tau^{-1-\alpha}\Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{2\pi L}{\sinh\bigl(\frac{s}{L}\bigr)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}s^{\prime}\,\sinh\Bigl(\frac{s^{\prime}}{L}\Bigr)\,J_{\tau}(s,s^{\prime})f(s^{\prime})\Bigr)-f(s)\Bigr]\\
&=-\frac{1}{\Gamma(-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}\tau\,\tau^{-1-\alpha}\,\frac{2\pi L}{\sinh\bigl(\frac{s}{L}\bigr)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}s^{\prime}\,\sinh\Bigl(\frac{s^{\prime}}{L}\Bigr)\,J_{\tau}(s,s^{\prime})\Bigl(f(s^{\prime})-f(s)\Bigr)\\
&=-\frac{2\pi L}{\Gamma(-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}s^{\prime}\frac{\sinh\bigl(\frac{s^{\prime}}{L}\bigr)}{\sinh\bigl(\frac{s}{L}\bigr)}\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}\tau\,\tau^{-1-\alpha}J_{\tau}(s,s^{\prime})}_{=:\,J(s,s^{\prime})}\Bigl(f(s^{\prime})-f(s)\Bigr)\,.}

(34)

4.2
AdS heat kernel

In order to evaluate the inner integral
J_{\tau}(s,s^{\prime})
in Eq. (
33
) we need to know the kernel function
K_{\tau}(s)
in AdS
2+1
as a function of the geodesic distance
s
from the origin. Fortunately, such kernels have already been computed in Ref.
[
45
]
. Converting these existing results into the present notation, the heat kernel in AdS
2+1
is given by

K_{\tau}(s)\;=\;\frac{1}{(4\pi\tau)^{3/2}}\,\frac{\frac{s}{L}}{\sinh\bigl(\frac{s}{L}\bigr)}\,\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\tau}{L^{2}}-\frac{s^{2}}{4\tau}\Bigr)\,.

(35)

In physical terms, this kernel describes how a point-like injection of heat diffuses in AdS
2+1
as a function of the geodesic distance
s
over a fictitious time span
\tau
. As expected, in the flat-space limit
L\to\infty
this expression reduces to a Gaussian.

To convince ourselves that this expression is indeed correct, we check that it solves the heat conduction equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\,K_{\tau}(s)\;=\;\square_{g}K_{\tau}(s)\;=\;\Bigl[\frac{2}{L}\coth\Bigl(\frac{s}{L}\Bigr)\partial_{s}+\partial_{s}^{2}\Bigr]K_{\tau}(s)

(36)

and that its volume integral is correctly normalized for all
\tau>0
:

4\pi L^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}s\,\sinh^{2}\Bigl(\frac{s}{L}\Bigr)\,K_{\tau}(s)\;=\;1\,.

(37)

4.3
Kernel representation of the fractional Laplace-Beltrami operator

Inserting the heat kernel (
35
) into Eq. (
33
), we can now compute the inner integral

J_{\tau}(s,s^{\prime})\;=\;\int_{|s-s^{\prime}|}^{s+s^{\prime}}\mbox{d}s^{\prime\prime}\,\sinh\Bigl(\frac{s^{\prime\prime}}{L}\Bigr)\,\,K_{\tau}(s^{\prime\prime})\;=\;\frac{e^{-\frac{(s-s^{\prime})^{2}}{4\tau}}-e^{-\frac{(s+s^{\prime})^{2}}{4\tau}}}{4\,L\,\pi^{3/2}\,\sqrt{\tau}}\,e^{-\frac{\tau}{L^{2}}}\,.

(38)

Carrying out the outer integration

J(s,s^{\prime})\;=\;\int_{0}^{\infty}\,\mbox{d}\tau\,\tau^{-1-\alpha}\,J_{\tau}(s,s^{\prime})

(39)

in Eq. (
35
) we find that

J(s,s^{\prime})\;=\;\frac{2^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}}{L^{\alpha+\frac{3}{2}}\pi^{\frac{3}{2}}}\,\Bigl(\bigl|s-s^{\prime}\bigr|^{-\frac{1}{2}-\alpha}K_{\frac{1}{2}+\alpha}\bigl(\frac{|s-s^{\prime}|}{L}\bigr)-\bigl(s+s^{\prime}\bigr)^{-\frac{1}{2}-\alpha}K_{\frac{1}{2}+\alpha}\bigl(\frac{s+s^{\prime}}{L}\bigr)\Bigr)\,,

(40)

where
K_{\nu}(z)
denotes the modified Bessel function. Hence we arrive at the kernel representation of the fractional Laplace-Beltrami operator

\eqalign{\bigl[\square_{g}^{\alpha}f\bigr](s)\;=\;&-\frac{2^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}}{L^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}s^{\prime}\,\frac{\sinh{\bigl(\frac{s^{\prime}}{L}}\bigr)}{\sinh{\bigl(\frac{s}{L}}\bigr)}\,\Bigl(\bigl|s-s^{\prime}\bigr|^{-\frac{1}{2}-\alpha}K_{\frac{1}{2}+\alpha}\bigl(\frac{|s-s^{\prime}|}{L}\bigr)\\
&\hskip 113.81102pt-\bigl(s+s^{\prime}\bigr)^{-\frac{1}{2}-\alpha}K_{\frac{1}{2}+\alpha}\bigl(\frac{s+s^{\prime}}{L}\bigr)\Bigr)\Bigl(f(s^{\prime})\,-f(s)\Bigr)}

(41)

which is the first main result of this paper.

4.4
Special limits of the kernel representation

To cross-check our result in (
41
) we carried out the following limits:

•

In the flat-space limit
L\to\infty
the arguments of the Bessel functions become small and can be approximated by
K_{\nu}(z)\;\approx\;z^{-\nu}\,2^{\nu-1}\,\Gamma(\nu)
so that Eq. (
41
) reduces to

\bigl[\square^{\alpha}f\bigr](s)=-\frac{2^{2\alpha}\,\Gamma\bigl(\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\bigr)}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma\bigl(-\alpha\bigr)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}s^{\prime}\,\frac{s^{\prime}}{s}\,\Bigl(\bigl|s-s^{\prime}\bigr|^{-1-2\alpha}-\bigl(s+s^{\prime}\bigr)^{-1-2\alpha}\Bigr)\Bigl(f(s^{\prime})\,-f(s)\Bigr).

(42)

•

In the short-range limit
\alpha\to 1
the fractional Laplace-Beltrami operator reduces to the non-fractional one (see
C
):

\lim_{\alpha\to 1}\bigl[\square_{g}^{\alpha}f\bigr](s)\;=\;\bigl[\square_{g}f\bigr](s)\;=\;f^{\prime\prime}(s)+\frac{2}{L}\coth\bigl(\frac{s}{L}\bigr)f^{\prime}(s)

(43)

•

In the combined limit
L\to\infty
and
\alpha\to 1
one consistently recovers the ordinary d’Alembert operator

\lim_{L\to\infty,\alpha\to 1}\bigl[\square_{g}^{\alpha}f\bigr](s)\;=\;\bigl[\square\bigr](s)\;=\;f^{\prime\prime}(s)+\frac{2}{s}f^{\prime}(s)

(44)

5
Fractional Green’s function

In this section our aim is to compute the two-point propagator
G_{m}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
of the fractional KGE on AdS
2+1
. Being a maximally symmetric space, we expect this function to depend only on the geodesic distance
s(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
, obeying the differential equation

(\square_{g}^{\alpha}-m^{2})G_{m}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},0)=\delta^{3}(\mathbf{x})\,.

(45)

In terms of geodesic distances
s=s(\mathbf{x})
, we can write equivalently

(\square_{g}^{\alpha}-m^{2})G_{m}^{\alpha}(s)=\frac{\delta(s)}{2\pi L^{2}\sinh^{2}(\frac{s}{L})}\,.

(46)

5.1
Inverse eigenmode decomposition

In the following we determine the function
G_{m}^{\alpha}(s)
without using the explicit kernel representation derived in the previous section. To this end we first solve the eigenvalue problem

\square_{g}^{\alpha}\,\psi_{\mu}(s)\;=\;-\mu^{\alpha}\,\psi_{\mu}(s)\,,

(47)

finding that the real-valued eigenfunctions of the fractional Laplace-Beltrami operator are given by

\psi_{\mu}(s)\;=\;\frac{\sin(s\sqrt{\mu-L^{-2}})}{\sinh(\frac{s}{L})}\,.

(48)

These eigenmodes are oscillatory above the Breitenloher-Freedman bound
\mu>\frac{1}{L^{2}}
. Note that the result is consistent with the composition law
\square^{\alpha}\square^{\beta}\;=\;-\square^{\alpha+\beta}
in Eq. (
24
).

The next important step is to realize that the right hand side of of Eq. (
46
) can be expanded in terms of these eigenfunctions by

\frac{\delta(s)}{2\pi L^{2}\,\sinh^{2}\bigl(\frac{s}{L}\bigr)}\;=\;\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}L}\int_{L^{-2}}^{\infty}\mbox{d}\mu\,\psi_{\mu}(s)\,.

(49)

Therefore, Eq. (
46
) can be recast as

(\square_{g}^{\alpha}-m^{2})G_{m}^{\alpha}(s)\;=\;\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}L}\int_{L^{-2}}^{\infty}\mbox{d}\mu\,\psi_{\mu}(s)\,.

(50)

For this reason we arrive at the formal solution

\eqalign{G_{m}^{\alpha}(s)\;&=\;(\square_{g}^{\alpha}-m^{2})^{-1}\,\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}L}\int_{L^{-2}}^{\infty}\mbox{d}\mu\,\psi_{\mu}(s)\\
&=\;\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}L}\int_{L^{-2}}^{\infty}\mbox{d}\mu\,(\square_{g}^{\alpha}-m^{2})^{-1}\,\psi_{\mu}(s)\\
&=\;-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}L}\int_{L^{-2}}^{\infty}\mbox{d}\mu\,\frac{1}{\mu^{\alpha}+m^{2}}\psi_{\mu}(s)\,.}

(51)

5.2
Massless case
m=0

Massless Green’s function
G_{0}^{\alpha}(s)
for various values of
\alpha

Setting
m=0
the integral (
51
) can be evaluated explicitly, leading to our second main result, namely, the massless propagator of the fractional wave equation in AdS
2+1
:

G_{0}^{\alpha}(s)\;=\;-\frac{\bigl(Ls\bigr)^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}}{L^{2}\,\pi^{\frac{3}{2}\,}2^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}\,\Gamma(\alpha)}\,\cdot\,\frac{K_{\alpha-\frac{3}{2}}\bigl(\frac{s}{L}\bigr)}{\sinh\bigl(\frac{s}{L}\bigr)}

(52)

which is plotted in Fig.

. This function has the following properties:

•

The short-range limit reads

\lim_{\alpha\to 1}G_{0}^{\alpha}(s)\;=\;-\frac{e^{-\frac{s}{L}}}{4\pi L\,\sinh(\frac{s}{L})}\,.

(53)

•

The flat-space limit

\lim_{L\to\infty}G_{0}^{\alpha}(s)\;=\;-\frac{s^{2\alpha-3}\,\Gamma(\frac{3}{2}-\alpha)}{2^{2\alpha}\,\pi^{\frac{3}{2}}\,\Gamma(\alpha)}\;=\;-\frac{\Gamma(2-2\alpha)\,\sin(\pi\alpha)}{2\,\pi^{2}\,s^{3-2\alpha}}

(54)

is valid only for
\alpha<\frac{3}{2}
.

•

In the large-distance limit
s\to\infty
we find the approximation

G_{0}^{\alpha}(s)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle s\to\infty}}{{\approx}}\;-\frac{e^{-s/L}\,L^{\alpha-2}\,s^{\alpha-1}}{2^{1+\alpha}\,\pi\,\Gamma(\alpha)\,\sinh\bigl(\frac{s}{L}\bigr)}

(55)

•

In the small-distance limit
s\to 0
the Green’s function behaves as

G_{0}^{\alpha}(s)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle s\to 0}}{{\approx}}\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\frac{s^{2\alpha-3}\,\Gamma(\frac{3}{2}-\alpha)}{2^{2\alpha}\,\pi^{3/2}\,\Gamma(\alpha)}&\quad\mbox{if }\alpha<\frac{3}{2}\\[5.69054pt]
-\frac{L^{2\alpha-3}\,\Gamma(\alpha-\frac{3}{2})}{8\,\pi^{3/2}\,\Gamma(\alpha)}&\quad\mbox{if }\alpha>\frac{3}{2}\end{array}\right.

(56)

•

We accidentally discovered an exact identity between
G_{0}^{\alpha}
and
G_{0}^{3-\alpha}
which is valid in the range
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<\frac{3}{2}
:

G_{0}^{3-\alpha}(s)\;=\;\frac{2^{2\alpha-3}\,L^{3-2\alpha}\,s^{3-2\alpha}\,\Gamma(\alpha)}{\Gamma(3-\alpha)}\,G_{0}^{\alpha}(s)

(57)

5.3
Massive case

For
m>0
the propagator (
52
) cannot be evaluated in a closed form. The only exception is the short-range limit
\alpha\to 1
in which we find the expression

G_{m}(s)\;=\;-\frac{\exp\bigl(-s\sqrt{\frac{1}{L^{2}}+m^{2}}\bigr)}{4\pi L\,\sinh(\frac{s}{L})}\,.

(58)

This results coincides with the known propagator of the KGE on AdS
2+1
, see e.g.
[
30
]
.

For
\alpha<1
we can expand the integrand as a power series in the mass parameter

\frac{1}{\mu^{\alpha}+m^{2}}\;=\;\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-m^{2})^{n-1}\,\mu^{-\alpha n}

(59)

allowing us to formally express the massive Green’s function as a series

G_{m}^{\alpha}(s)\;=\;\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-m^{2})^{n-1}G_{0}^{\alpha n}(s)

(60)

leading to our third main result:

G_{m}^{\alpha}(s)\;=\;-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-m^{2})^{n-1}\bigl(Ls\bigr)^{\alpha n-\frac{1}{2}}K_{\alpha n-\frac{3}{2}}\bigl(\frac{s}{L}\bigr)}{L^{2}\,\pi^{\frac{3}{2}\,}2^{\alpha n+\frac{1}{2}}\,\Gamma(\alpha n)\sinh\bigl(\frac{s}{L}\bigr)}\,.

(61)

As shown in

, this series converges for

|m^{2}|<L^{-2\alpha}\,.

(62)

On the one hand, this result suggests that for
m>0
a convergent flat-space limit
L\to\infty
does not exist. On the other hand, the result also suggests that a negative squared mass is formally still allowed, provided that the Breitenloher-Freedman bound (
m^{2}>-L^{-2}
in the short-range case) is replaced by
m^{2}>-L^{-2\alpha}
in the fractional case.

In order to verify the main results of this paper, we performed numerical tests by applying the kernel representation (

•

7
Conclusions

In this work we have investigated the fractional Klein-Gordon equation and its massless special case, the wave equation, on a 2+1-dimensional AdS space. To this end we proposed a systematic method based on Bochner’s approach which allows one to “fractionalize” a given equation of motion independent of the background metric.

Our main results are the kernel representation of the Laplace-Beltrami operator (
41
) on AdS
2+1
, the massless fractional Green’s function (
52
) of the wave equation, and the massive Green’s function of the fractional Klein-Gordon equation in a series representation (
61
). All results are expressed covariantly in terms of geodesic distances. We have restricted the analysis to the simplest non-trivial case of 2+1 dimensions, but in principle it should be possible to obtain similar results in arbitrary dimensions.

Surprisingly our findings suggest that for the fractional Klein-Gordon equation with non-vanishing mass a Green’s function does not exist on a flat 2+1-dimensional Minkowski space, but apparently it does exist on AdS
2+1
, provided that the mass
|m^{2}|<L^{-2\alpha}
is sufficiently small, cf. Eq. (
61
). This leads to the conjecture that massive scalar fields with a fractional dynamics cannot exist in flat spaces but only in spaces with negative curvature. We have therefore identified a phenomenon which relies on the negative curvature of AdS spaces and does not have a zero-curvature limit. Apparently the AdS space offers enough room for a point-like source to spread according to a fractional dynamics while a flat space is too tight.

With regard to the AdS/CFT correspondence, important questions arise: Is it still possible to define bulk-to-boundary and boundary-to-boundary correlators in the usual way? Is the induced dynamics at the boundary still conformal? As the long-range interactions decay according to a power law, they do not introduce an additional scale that would rule out a conformal behavior at the boundary. A promising starting point will be to identify the action functional giving rise to the fractional KGE in AdS
2+1
, as well as to analyze the asymptotic behavior of fractional scalar fields near the AdS boundary and to compare it with standard results in AdS/CFT, cf.
[
30
]
. We expect the present paper to be a basis for further studies in this direction.

Appendix B
Radial kernel representation

In this section we show how a kernel integral on AdS
2+1
can be represented in geodesic distances, generalizing known methods in flat spaces. Starting point is a Cartesian kernel representation

g(\mathbf{x})\;=\;\int\mbox{d}^{3}y\,\sqrt{|g|}\,K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,f(\mathbf{y})

(68)

in a certain coordinate systems
\vec{x}=(x^{0},x^{1},x^{2})
and
\vec{y}=(y^{0},y^{1},y^{2})
. We assume that both
K
and
f
depend only on geodesic distances, i.e.,

g\bigl(s(\mathbf{x})\bigr)\;=\;\int\mbox{d}^{3}y\,\sqrt{|g|}\,K\bigl(s(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\bigr)\,f\bigl(s(\mathbf{y})\bigr)\,.

(69)

Inserting a unit factor, namely,
1=\int\mbox{d}s^{\prime}\int\mbox{d}s^{\prime\prime}\,\delta\bigl(s^{\prime\prime}-s(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\bigr)\,\delta\bigl(s^{\prime}-s(\mathbf{y})\bigr)
, we obtain

\eqalign{g(s)&=\int\mbox{d}^{d}y\,\sqrt{|g|}\,\int\mbox{d}s^{\prime}\int\mbox{d}s^{\prime\prime}\,\delta\Bigl(s^{\prime\prime}-s(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\Bigr)\,\delta\Bigl(s^{\prime}-s(\mathbf{y})\Bigr)\,K\bigl(\underbrace{s(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}_{=s^{\prime\prime}}\bigr)\,f\bigl(\underbrace{s(\mathbf{y})}_{=s^{\prime}}\bigr)\\
&=\int\mbox{d}s^{\prime}\int\mbox{d}s^{\prime\prime}\,\underbrace{\int\mbox{d}^{d}y\,\sqrt{|g|}\,\delta\Bigl(s^{\prime\prime}-s(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\Bigr)\,\delta\Bigl(s^{\prime}-s(\mathbf{y})\Bigr)}_{=\chi(s,s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime})}\,K\bigl(s^{\prime\prime}\bigr)\,f\bigl(s^{\prime}\bigr)\,,}

(70)

where
\mathbf{x}
is a freely point in distance
s
from the origin. What remains is to determine
\chi(s,s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime})
which depends only on the structure of the manifold. To this end one has to rewrite (
70
) in a specific coordinate system of AdS
2+1
, for example, in global coordinates
t,r,\phi
. Due to radial symmetry, the integration over
\phi
drops out, giving a factor
2\pi
. The remaining two integrations can be evaluated by using the rule
\delta(y-g(x))=\frac{1}{|g^{\prime}(x_{0})|}\delta(x-x_{0})
, where
x_{0}
is the root of
y-g(x)=0
. Elementary calculations (not shown here) lead to the result

\chi(s,s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime})\;=\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2\pi L\frac{\sinh(\frac{s^{\prime}}{L})\sinh(\frac{s^{\prime\prime}}{L})}{\sinh(\frac{s}{L})}&\mbox{if }|s-s^{\prime}|<s^{\prime\prime}<|s+s^{\prime}|\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(71)

Alternatively, this expression can be derived by a coordinate transformation
(s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime})\leftrightarrow(t,r)
. In this context it is interesting to note that the Jacobian can be expressed in terms of the well-known Källén function
[
46
]
generalized to AdS:

\eqalign{\Lambda(s,s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime})\;=\;16&\,L^{4}\,\sinh\Bigl(\frac{s+s^{\prime}+s^{\prime\prime}}{2L}\Bigr)\sinh\Bigl(\frac{s+s^{\prime}-s^{\prime\prime}}{2L}\Bigr)\\
&\times\;\sinh\Bigl(\frac{s-s^{\prime}+s^{\prime\prime}}{2L}\Bigr)\sinh\Bigl(\frac{s-s^{\prime}-s^{\prime\prime}}{2L}\Bigr)}

(72)

To our knowledge this generalization has not been known so far. This function has to be positive, explaining immediately the bounds in (
71
).

Appendix C
Short-range limit in the kernel representation

In this Appendix we show that the kernel representation (
41
) of the fractional Laplace-Beltrami operator, given by

\eqalign{\bigl[\square_{g}^{\alpha}f\bigr](s)\;=\;&-\frac{2^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}}{L^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}s^{\prime}\,\frac{\sinh{\bigl(\frac{s^{\prime}}{L}}\bigr)}{\sinh{\bigl(\frac{s}{L}}\bigr)}\,\Bigl(\bigl|s-s^{\prime}\bigr|^{-\frac{1}{2}-\alpha}K_{\frac{1}{2}+\alpha}\bigl(\frac{|s-s^{\prime}|}{L}\bigr)\\
&\hskip 113.81102pt-\bigl(s+s^{\prime}\bigr)^{-\frac{1}{2}-\alpha}K_{\frac{1}{2}+\alpha}\bigl(\frac{s+s^{\prime}}{L}\bigr)\Bigr)\Bigl(f(s^{\prime})\,-f(s)\Bigr)}

(73)

reduces neatly to the short-range differential equation in the limit
\alpha\to 1
. Starting point is the observation that
\Gamma(-\alpha)\approx\frac{1}{\alpha-1}
diverges in this limit, eliminating all non-divergent contributions of the integral. Since the divergent contributions are peaked around
s^{\prime}\approx s
, we can limit the integration range to
s^{\prime}\in[s-\epsilon,s+\epsilon]
and finally show that in the limit
\alpha\to 1
the result becomes independent of
\epsilon
.

Since the singularity at
s^{\prime}\approx s
is produced only by the first Bessel function in (
73
), we can discard the second Bessel function which depends on
s+s^{\prime}
. The remaining factors can be Taylor-expanded in
s^{\prime}
around
s
as

\eqalign{&\frac{\sinh{\bigl(\frac{s^{\prime}}{L}}\bigr)}{\sinh{\bigl(\frac{s}{L}}\bigr)}\;=\;1+\frac{1}{L}\coth\bigl(\frac{s}{L}\bigr)(s^{\prime}-s)+\ldots\\
&\bigl|s-s^{\prime}\bigr|^{-\frac{1}{2}-\alpha}K_{\frac{1}{2}+\alpha}\bigl(\frac{|s-s^{\prime}|}{L}\bigr)\;=\;\frac{2^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\,L^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}\,\Gamma(\alpha+\frac{1}{2})}{|s-s^{\prime}|^{2\alpha+1}}+\ldots\\
&f(s^{\prime})-f(s)\;=\;(s^{\prime}-s)f^{\prime}(s)+\frac{1}{2}(s^{\prime}-s)^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(s)+\ldots}

In the integrand only symmetric contributions in
s-s^{\prime}
will survive:

\eqalign{\lim_{\alpha\to 1}\bigl[\square_{g}^{\alpha}f\bigr](s)&\;=\;-\lim_{\alpha\to 1}\frac{2^{2\alpha}\Gamma(\alpha+\frac{1}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(-\alpha)}\int_{s-\epsilon}^{s+\epsilon}\mbox{d}s^{\prime}\,\frac{1}{|s-s^{\prime}|^{2\alpha+1}}\Bigl(1+\frac{\coth\bigl(\frac{s}{L}\bigr)(s^{\prime}-s)}{L}\Bigr)\\
&\hskip 156.49014pt\times\;\Bigl((s^{\prime}-s)f^{\prime}(s)+\frac{(s^{\prime}-s)^{2}}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(s)\Bigr)\\
&\;=\;-2\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(s)+\frac{1}{L}\coth\bigl(\frac{s}{L}\bigr)f^{\prime}(s)\Bigr)\lim_{\alpha\to 1}\,\underbrace{(\alpha-1)\int_{s-\epsilon}^{s+\epsilon}\mbox{d}s^{\prime}\,\frac{1}{|s-s^{\prime}|^{2\alpha-1}}}_{=\epsilon^{2-2\alpha}}\\
&\;=\;f^{\prime\prime}(s)+\frac{2}{L}\coth\bigl(\frac{s}{L}\bigr)f^{\prime}(s)\;=\;\bigl[\square_{g}f\bigr](s)\,.}

Appendix D
Convergence radius in the massive case

In the following we sketch a possible way to determine the convergence radius of the massive Green’s function (
61
)

G_{m}^{\alpha}(s)\;=\;-\frac{1}{L^{2}\pi^{\frac{3}{2}}\sinh\bigl(\frac{s}{L}\bigr)}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-m^{2})^{n-1}\frac{\bigl(Ls\bigr)^{\alpha n-\frac{1}{2}}K_{\alpha n-\frac{3}{2}}\bigl(\frac{s}{L}\bigr)}{2^{\alpha n+\frac{1}{2}}\,\Gamma(\alpha n)}\,.

(74)

Let us first consider the case that
s>0
is real. Since the modified Bessel function is positive in this case, we can estimate

|G_{m}^{\alpha}(s)|\leq\frac{1}{L^{2}\sinh\bigl(\frac{s}{L}\bigr)}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|m^{2}|^{n-1}\frac{\bigl(Ls\bigr)^{\alpha n-\frac{1}{2}}K_{\alpha n-\frac{3}{2}}\bigl(\frac{s}{L}\bigr)}{\pi^{\frac{3}{2}\,}2^{\alpha n+\frac{1}{2}}\,\Gamma(\alpha n)\sinh\bigl(\frac{s}{L}\bigr)}

(75)

For
\nu>0
and
z\geq 0
the modified Bessel function obeys the inequality

K_{\nu}(z)\,\leq\,\frac{1}{2}\frac{\Gamma(\nu)}{(z/2)^{\nu}}\,.

(76)

Thus, for
s>0
the convergence of

\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\frac{m^{2n}(Ls)^{\alpha n}\Gamma(\alpha n-\frac{3}{2})}{2^{\alpha n}\Gamma(\alpha n)(\frac{s}{2L})^{\alpha n}}\;=\;\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}|m^{2}|^{n}L^{2\alpha n}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha n-\frac{3}{2})}{\Gamma(\alpha n)}

(77)

implies the convergence of
G_{m}^{\alpha}(s)
. Here we omitted the first
N-1
terms, where
N
is the smallest integer such that the order of the Bessel function
\alpha N-\frac{3}{2}
is positive. Since
\frac{\Gamma(\alpha n-\frac{3}{2})}{\Gamma(\alpha n)}
converges quickly to
(\alpha n)^{-3/2}
, one obtains essentially an expression involving a polylogarithm
\mbox{Li}_{3/2}\bigl(|m^{2}|L^{2\alpha}\bigr)
, the power series of which is convergent only for arguments smaller than 1. Thus the convergence radius is equal or larger than
|m^{2}|<L^{-2\alpha}
. A numerical survey suggests that this is in fact the actual convergence radius of the series expansion (
61
) and that this holds even for imaginary values of
s
.
