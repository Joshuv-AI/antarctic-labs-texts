Bulcsú Sándor, András Rusu, Károly Dénes, Mária Ercsey-Ravasz, Zsolt I. Lázár

Abstract
There is a growing interest in methods for detecting and interpreting changes in experimental time evolution data. Based on measured time series, the quantitative characterization of dynamical phase transitions at bifurcation points of the underlying chaotic systems is a notoriously difficult task. Building on prior theoretical studies that focus on the discontinuities at q = 1 q=1 in the order- q q Rényi-entropy of the trajectory space, we measure the derivative of the spectrum. We derive within the general context of Markov processes a computationally efficient closed-form expression for this measure. We investigate its properties through well-known dynamical systems exploring its scope and limitations. The proposed mathematical instrument can serve as a predictor of dynamical phase transitions in time series.



.1
Entropies

Here we summarize the entropy-related definitions from the main paper.
The order-
q
Rényi entropy for a discrete distribution
\bm{p}=(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{N})
is defined as

H_{q}(\bm{p})=\frac{1}{1-q}\ln\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}^{q}\,,

(1)

where
H_{q}\geq H_{q^{\prime}}
whenever
q^{\prime}>q

.
The Shannon entropy of the same distribution can be obtained in the
q=1
limit, as the mean

H_{1}(\bm{p})=-\langle\ln\bm{p}\rangle_{\bm{p}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\ln p_{i}\,.

(2)

The same limit of the derivative of the Rényi-entropy, with respect to
q
, gives the variance

H_{1}^{\prime}(\bm{p})=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{2}_{\bm{p}}\ln(\bm{p})=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\langle\ln^{2}\bm{p}\rangle_{\bm{p}}-\langle\ln\bm{p}\rangle^{2}_{\bm{p}}\right)\,.

(3)

For a dynamical system with coarse-grained natural distribution
{\bm{\rho}}^{*}
on the attractor we denote with

C_{1}=H_{1}({\bm{\rho}}^{*})\,,\qquad C_{2}=-2H^{\prime}_{1}({\bm{\rho}}^{*})\,.

(4)

The dynamical Rényi-entropy is defined as the time-normalized entropy of the trajectory probabilities
{\mathbfcal{P}}
:

K_{q}({\mathbfcal{P}})=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}H_{q}({\mathbfcal{P}}(t))\,.

(5)

The
q=1
value is of special interest, and it is called the Kolmogorov-Sinai entropy or metric entropy
Boffetta et al. 2002
:

S=K_{1}({\mathbfcal{P}})\,,\qquad\Lambda=-{2}K_{1}^{\prime}({\mathbfcal{P}})\,.

(6)

.2
Markov chain formalism

Left and right eigenvectors,
{\bm{\rho}}_{\nu}^{\top}
and
\mathbf{v}_{\nu}
, respectively, of the stochastic matrix
\mathbf{W}=(w_{ij})
, with
i,j=1,\dots,N
, are defined as:

\mathbf{W}^{\top}{\bm{\rho}}_{\nu}=\alpha_{\nu}\cdot{\bm{\rho}}_{\nu}\,,\qquad\qquad\mathbf{W}\mathbf{v}_{\nu}=\alpha_{\nu}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{\nu}\,,

(7)

with
\alpha_{\nu}
eigenvalues, where
\alpha_{1}=1
, and
{\bm{\rho}}_{1}
is the stationary distribution of the corresponding Markov chain, for which the normalization is given by
{\bm{\rho}}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{1}=1
with
\mathbf{v}_{1}=(1,1,\dots,1)
.
Note that as symbols with zero probability play no role in this representation, the stationary distribution of the Markov chain
{\bm{\rho}}_{1}
, for states of order
m=1
, provides an approximation of the natural distribution on the attractor
{\bm{\rho}}^{*}
when omitting cells with zero probability,
{\bm{\rho}}_{1}={\bm{\rho}}^{*}
.

A useful identity concerning the powers of the transition matrix is

\left(\mathbf{W}-\alpha_{\nu}\mathbf{v}_{\nu}{\bm{\rho}}_{\nu}^{\top}\right)^{k}=\mathbf{W}^{k}-\alpha_{\nu}^{k}\mathbf{v}_{\nu}{\bm{\rho}}_{\nu}^{\top}\,,\qquad\quad k\geq 1\,.

(8)

In the following we denote
{\bm{\rho}}={\bm{\rho}}_{1}
and
\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{v}_{1}
for simplicity.

.3
Rényi entropy spectrum

The full spectrum of the truncated Rényi-entropies can be given as

\displaystyle\tilde{K}_{q}=\frac{1}{1-q}\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{k}\ln\left(\sum_{i,j}\rho_{i}^{q}(\mathbf{W}_{q}^{k})_{ij}1_{j}\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-q}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\ln\left(\frac{\sum_{i,j}\rho_{i}^{q}(\mathbf{W}_{q}^{k+1})_{ij}1_{j}}{\sum_{i,j}\rho_{i}^{q}(\mathbf{W}_{q}^{k})_{ij}1_{j}}\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{\ln\alpha_{\text{max}}(q)}{1-q}\,,

(9)

where
\alpha_{\text{max}}(q)
is the largest eigenvalue of the
(\mathbf{W}_{q})_{ij}=w_{ij}^{q}
matrix (element-wise
q
power).
According to the Perron-Frobenius theorem for irreducible non-negative matrices the spectral radius
\alpha_{\text{max}}
is a positive real number.

For random systems (without memory), viz. for
w_{ij}=1_{i}\rho_{j}
, where
1_{i}
denotes the
i
-th component of a vector of all-ones, the above expression reduces to the Rényi entropy of the stationary distribution,

\tilde{K}_{q}=\frac{1}{1-q}\ln\sum_{i=1}^{N}\rho_{i}^{q}=H_{q}({\bm{\rho}})\,.

(10)

Hence, for memory-less dynamics and first-order states, the truncated estimation of the entropy rate,
S
, and diffusion coefficient,
\Lambda
, from Eq. (
6
) turn into the bit-number statistics,
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, from Eq. (
4
).

.4
Path length statistics and entropies

Using the notations
{\mathbfcal{P}}(t)\equiv\rho_{s_{1}}{\mathbfcal{P}}(\mathbfcal{X}(t)|s_{1})
for the set of probabilities of all trajectories of
t
steps,
and
\mathbfcal{L}(t)\equiv-\ln{\mathbfcal{P}}(\mathbfcal{X}(t)|s_{1})
for the length of trajectories with given initial state, Eqs. (
1
) and (
5
) can be combined into

\displaystyle{K}_{1}({\mathbfcal{P}})

\displaystyle=-\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\langle\ln{\mathbfcal{P}}(t)\rangle_{\mathbfcal{P}}=

\displaystyle=-\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\left({\langle\ln{\bm{\rho}}\rangle_{{\bm{\rho}}}}-\langle\mathbfcal{L}(t)\rangle_{\mathbfcal{P}}\right)=

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\langle\mathbfcal{L}(t)\rangle_{\mathbfcal{P}}\,,

(11)

where averaging is done over all states
s_{i}
, realizations of trajectories
\mathcal{X}(t)
of length
t
and initial states
s_{1}
, respectively.

Similarly, Eqs. (
3
) and (
5
) yield

{K}_{1}^{\prime}({\mathbfcal{P}})=-\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2t}\Delta_{\mathbfcal{P}}^{2}\ln({\mathbfcal{P}}(t))\,.

(12)

Since generally
\Delta^{2}(x+y)=\Delta^{2}x+\Delta^{2}y+2\text{Cov}(x,y)
for random variables
x
and
y
, and the covariance between
\rho_{i}
and
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}(t)|s_{1})
goes to zero as the number of steps,
t
, goes to infinity,
\mathrm{Cov}(\rho_{i},\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}(t)|s_{1}))\rightarrow 0
, we get

\displaystyle{K}_{1}^{\prime}({\mathbfcal{P}})

\displaystyle=-\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2t}\left(\Delta_{\bm{\rho}}^{2}\ln{\bm{\rho}}+\Delta_{\mathbfcal{P}}^{2}\mathbfcal{L}(t)\right)=

\displaystyle=-\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2t}\Delta_{\mathbfcal{P}}^{2}\mathbfcal{L}(t)\,.

(13)

.5
Interpretation of the KS entropy

The entropy rate determines the mean of the normal distribution, see Eq. (
11
), and can be interpreted as the amount of uncertainty removed by evolving the Markov chain with one step:

S=\sum_{i}^{N}\rho_{i}S_{i}\,,\qquad S_{i}=-\sum_{j}^{N}w_{ij}\ln w_{ij}\,,

(14)

averaged over all state entropies
S_{i}
.
The KS entropy can be decomposed into the difference between the transition’s entropy and the entropy of the stationary distribution of states:

S=S_{\mathrm{tr}}-C_{1}

(15)

where

S_{\mathrm{tr}}=-\sum_{i,j}^{N}\rho_{i}w_{ij}\ln\left(\rho_{i}w_{ij}\right)\,,\quad C_{1}=-\sum_{i}^{N}\rho_{i}\ln\rho_{i}

(16)

are respectively the transition entropy and state entropy, compare Eq. (
4
).
Note that using transition probabilities and
q=1
we get a proper extensive (in time) statistical physics.
The mean bit number
C_{1}
has, however, no straightforward interpretation as an extensive quantity.

.6
Lyapunov measure as ensemble averages

In order to compute the Lyapunov measure
\Lambda
, first we need to estimate the auto-covariances,

\mathrm{Cov}(l_{1},l_{1+\tau})=\langle l_{1}l_{1+\tau}\rangle-\langle l_{1}\rangle\langle l_{1+\tau}\rangle\,,

(17)

for all possible time-lags
\tau
, where
l_{1}
is the edge length in the first step.
Due to the stationarity of the process, considering an ensemble of random walkers with initial distribution
{\bm{\rho}}
, the ensemble-averaged step length is constant along the
\tau=1,\dots,t
steps of the random walks,
\langle l\rangle=\langle l_{\tau}\rangle
, and it can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\langle l\rangle=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\rho_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{ij}\ln w_{ij}={\bm{\rho}}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{1}\mathbf{1}\,,

(18)

where
(\mathbf{L}_{1})_{ij}=-w_{ij}\ln w_{ij}
is the probability-weighted edge-length matrix. Note also that for
t\gg 1
the ensemble-averaged edge lengths during the random walks do not depend on the starting positions of the walks.

Now, considering time-lags
\tau\in\{1,2,\dots\}
we calculate the
\tau
-th order covariance of step-lengths
l
,

\displaystyle\langle l_{1}l_{{1+\tau}}\rangle=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{s_{1}=1}^{N}\rho_{s_{1}}\sum\limits_{s_{2}=1}^{N}w_{s_{1}s_{2}}(-\ln w_{s_{1}s_{2}})\sum\limits_{s_{3}=1}^{N}w_{s_{2}s_{3}}\dots

\displaystyle...\sum\limits_{s_{\tau+1}=1}^{N}w_{s_{\tau}s_{\tau+1}}(-\ln w_{s_{\tau}s_{\tau+1}})=

\displaystyle={\bm{\rho}}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{1}\mathbf{W}^{\tau-1}\mathbf{L}_{1}\mathbf{1}\,.

Using identity (
8
) with
\alpha_{1}=1
, for the infinite sum of covariances we need to calculate

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}=\sum_{\tau=1}^{\infty}(\mathbf{W}^{\tau-1}-\mathbf{W}^{\infty})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(\mathbf{W}^{k}-\mathbf{1}{\bm{\rho}}^{\top})=

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{1}{\bm{\rho}}^{\top})+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{1}{\bm{\rho}}^{\top})^{k}\,,

where
\mathbf{W}^{\infty}\equiv\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathbf{W}^{k}
. Denoting with
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{1}{\bm{\rho}}^{\top}
and knowing that
\mathbf{W}^{\infty}=\mathbf{1}{\bm{\rho}}^{\top}
the sum

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{A}^{i}=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A}^{k+1})=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A})^{-1}\,,

since from Eq. (
8
)
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathbf{A}^{k}=\mathbf{0}
.

Finally, we have

\mathbf{B}=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{1}{\bm{\rho}}^{\top})+(\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{1}{\bm{\rho}}^{\top})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{1}{\bm{\rho}}^{\top})^{-1}\,,

(19)

and hence the infinite sum of auto-covariances from Eq. (
17
) is given by

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{Cov}(l_{1},l_{1+\tau})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau=1}^{\infty}{\bm{\rho}}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{1}\,(\mathbf{W}^{\tau-1}-\mathbf{W}^{\infty})\mathbf{L}_{1}\,\mathbf{1}=

\displaystyle={\bm{\rho}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{L}_{1}\,\mathbf{B}\,\mathbf{L}_{1}\,\mathbf{1}\,.

(20)

Bringing everything together, the variance of individual edge lengths is

\sigma_{l}^{2}=\langle l^{2}\rangle-\langle l\rangle^{2}\ ={\bm{\rho}}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{2}\,\mathbf{1}-({\bm{\rho}}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{1}\,\mathbf{1})^{2}\,,

(21)

where
(\mathbf{L}_{2})_{ij}=w_{ij}\ln^{2}w_{ij}
,
the entropy rate can be expressed by using Eq. (
18
) as

S={\bm{\rho}}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{1}\mathbf{1}\,,

(22)

while the Lyapunov measure is given by the

\Lambda={\bm{\rho}}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{2}\,\mathbf{1}-({\bm{\rho}}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{1}\,\mathbf{1})^{2}+2{\bm{\rho}}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{1}\,\mathbf{B}\,\mathbf{L}_{1}\,\mathbf{1}

(23)

closed-form expression, where
{\bm{\rho}}^{\top}
and
\mathbf{1}=(1,1,\dots,1)
are the left and right eigenvectors of the stochastic transition matrix
\mathbf{W}
with eigenvalue
\alpha_{1}=1
, and

\mathbf{B}\equiv(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{1}{\bm{\rho}}^{\top})^{-1}-\mathbf{1}{\bm{\rho}}^{\top}\,.

(24)

For systems defined by a random transition matrix
\mathbf{W}
with uniformly distributed elements, the Lyapunov-measure
\Lambda
approaches 1/4 as the number of states goes to infinity
Sándor et al. 2021
.

.8
Numerical estimation of
S
and
\Lambda

The computational complexity of evaluating
S
in Eq. (
22
) and the first two terms of
\Lambda
in Eq. (
23
) are of
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
where
N
is the number of symbols.
The third term contains a matrix inverse but in conjunction with the inner product allowing for the application of a linear solver instead of inversion.
More precisely we are looking for the vector

\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{1}{\bm{\rho}}^{\top})^{-1}\mathbf{L}_{1}\mathbf{1}\;.

Since the number of symbols increases exponentially with the order of the representation the computational cost of the inverse calculation becomes prohibitive for high orders.
However, in this case,
\mathbf{W}
becomes a sparse matrix facilitating the use of high-efficiency iterative solvers.
The solution in
\mathbf{z}
is obtained as the fix-point of the iteration

\mathbf{z}^{\prime}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{1}\langle z\rangle+\mathbf{L}_{1}\mathbf{1}\;.

where
\langle z\rangle\equiv{\bm{\rho}}^{\top}\mathbf{z}
.
Using a random vector as an initial value of
\mathbf{z}
the iteration is convergent for all systems studied here.
