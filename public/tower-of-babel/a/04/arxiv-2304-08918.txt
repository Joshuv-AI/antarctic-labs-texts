Symmetric ( σ , τ ) (\sigma,\tau) -algebras and ( σ , τ ) (\sigma,\tau) -Hochschild cohomology
Kwalombota Ilwale

Abstract
. On an associative algebra, we introduce the concept of symmetric ( σ , τ ) (\sigma,\tau) -derivations together with a regularity condition and prove that strongly regular symmetric ( σ , τ ) (\sigma,\tau) -derivations are inner. Symmetric ( σ , τ ) (\sigma,\tau) -derivations are ( σ , τ ) (\sigma,\tau) -derivations that are simultaneously ( σ , τ ) (\sigma,\tau) -derivations as well as ( τ , σ ) (\tau,\sigma) -derivations, generalizing a property of commutative algebras. Motivated by this notion, we explore the geometry of symmetric ( σ , τ ) (\sigma,\tau) -algebras and prove that there exist a unique strongly regular symmetric ( σ , τ ) (\sigma,\tau) -connection. Furthermore, we introduce ( σ , τ ) (\sigma,\tau) -Hochschild cohomology and show that, in first degree, it describes the outer ( σ , τ ) (\sigma,\tau) -derivations on an associative algebra. Along the way, examples are provided to illustrate the novel concepts.



1.
Introduction

In a derivation-based approach to noncommutative geometry, derivations
play a significant role, especially in describing connections,
curvature, and the torsion of a connection. Let
\mathcal{A}
be a unital
associative algebra over
\mathbb{C}
. For a
derivation
X
, i.e a
\mathbb{C}
-linear map
X:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
satisfying
Leibniz rule
X(fg)=fX(g)+X(f)g
, a connection

\nabla_{X}:M\to M,
on a left
\mathcal{A}
-module
M,
satisfies Leibniz
rule

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}(fm)=f\nabla_{X}(m)+X(f)m

(see e.g
[
DV88
]
,

[
DVM96
]
. On the other hand, there are
certain twisted derivations, called
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations which are
usually not considered in the derivation-based approach but do play an
important role in certain deformations of Lie algebras as well as in
quantum groups. For example, in
[
HLS06
]
and

[
LS07
]
, a
q
-deformed Witt algebra was realized
through the use of
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations and a family of

q
-deformed Lie algebra of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F})
was
constructed using
\sigma
-derivations respectively.

A
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation is a
\mathbb{C}
-linear map
X:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
satisfying a
twisted Leibniz rule

\displaystyle X(fg)=\sigma(f)X(g)+X(f)\tau(g),

for algebra endomorphisms
\sigma,\tau:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
. In

[
AI22
]
, a framework for noncommutative
Riemmanian geometry is considered by constructing connections,
curvature, and torsion over certain modules based on

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations. In this framework, given a left

\mathcal{A}
-module
M
, a
(\sigma,\tau)
-connection
\nabla_{X}:M\to M,

satisfies a twisted Leibniz rule

(1.1)

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}(fm)=\sigma(f)\nabla_{X}(m)+X(f)\hat{\tau}(m)

for a
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation
X:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
and a
\mathbb{C}
-linear map

(satisfying
\hat{\tau}(fm)=\tau(f)\hat{\tau}(m)
). For
instance, in the quest to explore connections on the quantum

3
-sphere,
[

]
and
[

]
constructed

) with
respect to a set of
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations
X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}
appearing
naturally as deformations of the Lie algebra of vector fields on the
3-sphere. These results motivated the construction of a general
framework for noncommutative geometry of twisted derivations developed
in
[
AI22
]
, introducing the concept of a

(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra together with a corresponding notion of

\Sigma
-modules.

In the current work, we introduce the notion of symmetric

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations. A symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation is a

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation that is also a
(\tau,\sigma)
-derivation. For

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations on a commutative algebra are

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations. Based on such

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations, we introduce symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebras

\Sigma
-modules on which symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-connections are

-derivations, we introduce the concept of regular and

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations on an associative algebra, we

(\sigma,\tau)
-version of Hochschild cohomology for

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations on an
associative algebra.

In Section

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations on an

(\sigma,\tau)

-derivations. In Section

2.
Symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations

In this Section, we introduce a notion of symmetric

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations on associative algebras. These are

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations that are also
(\tau,\sigma)
-derivations. One
may think of symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations as a generalization of

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations on commutative algebras (where every

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation is symmetric) to noncommutative algebras. In
the following, we consider a unital associative algebra
\mathcal{A}
over a
field
\mathbb{K}
of characteristic zero. Let us recall some definitions.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\sigma,\tau\in\operatorname{End}(\mathcal{A})
and let
M
be an
\mathcal{A}
-bimodule. A

\mathbb{K}
-linear map
X:\mathcal{A}\to M
is called a
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation with
values in
M
if

(2.1)

X(fg)=\sigma(f)X(g)+X(f)\tau(g)

for all
f,g\in\mathcal{A}.

Let
\mbox{Der}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A})
denote the set of

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations of
\mathcal{A}
. In general,

\mbox{Der}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A})
is not an
\mathcal{A}
-module, but it turns out
to be a module over
Z(\mathcal{A})
, the center of
\mathcal{A}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

\mbox{Der}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A})
is a
Z(\mathcal{A})
-bimodule.

Proof.

Assume
X
is a
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation and let
f\in Z(\mathcal{A}).
Set

\displaystyle(f\cdot X)(a)=fX(a)\quad\text{and}\quad(X\cdot f)(a)=X(a)f

for
a\in\mathcal{A}
. It is clear that the above action defines a bimodule
structure on
\mbox{Der}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A})
as long as
f\cdot X
and

X\cdot f
are
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations. For
a,b\in\mathcal{A},
one has

\displaystyle f\cdot X(ab)=fX(ab)=f\sigma(a)X(b)+fX(a)\tau(b)=\sigma(a)fX(b)+fX(a)\tau(b)

\displaystyle\sigma(a)f\cdot X(b)+f\cdot X(a)\tau(b)

showing that
f\cdot X
is a
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation. Similarly, one
shows that
X\cdot f
is a
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation.
∎

In the definition of
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation, one obtains an
ordinary derivation (with values in
M
) when both
\sigma
and
\tau

are the identity maps on
\mathcal{A}
. A derivation
X
is inner if it can be
written as
X(f)=[f,g_{0}]=m_{0}f-fm_{0}
for some fixed
m_{0}\in M
. These
usually give an idea of how noncommutative an algebra is. In the case
of
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations, one defines inner derivations as follows.

Definition 2.3
.

A
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation
X
with values in
M
is called
inner
if there exists

m_{0}\in M
such that

X(f)=m_{0}\tau(f)-\sigma(f)m_{0}

for all
f\in\mathcal{A}.

Note that although there are no inner derivations on a commutative
algebra, in general there exist inner (nontrivial)

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations on a commutative algebra if
\sigma\neq\tau
.

Definition 2.4
.

A
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation with values in
M
is called

symmetric
if it is also a
(\tau,\sigma)
-derivation with values in
M.

Let us look at a few examples of symmetric

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations. For arbitrary
\sigma,\tau\in\operatorname{End}(\mathcal{A})
the
linear map
X:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
defined by

(2.2)

\displaystyle X(f)=\tau(f)-\sigma(f)

for
f\in\mathcal{A}
is a symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation. Indeed,
for
f,g\in\mathcal{A},
one has

\displaystyle X(fg)=\tau(f)\tau(g)-\sigma(f)\sigma(g)=\tau(f)\tau(g)-\sigma(f)\tau(g)+\sigma(f)\tau(g)-\sigma(f)\sigma(g)

\displaystyle=(\tau(f)-\sigma(f))\tau(g)+\sigma(f)(\tau(g)-\sigma(g))=X(f)\tau(g)+\sigma(f)X(g)

showing that
X
is a
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation. On the other hand, for

f,g\in\mathcal{A}
one has

\displaystyle X(fg)=\tau(f)\tau(g)-\sigma(f)\sigma(g)=\tau(f)\tau(g)-\tau(f)\sigma(g)+\tau(f)\sigma(g)-\sigma(f)\sigma(g)

\displaystyle=\tau(f)(\tau(g)-\sigma(g))+(\tau(f)-\sigma(f))\sigma(g)=\tau(f)X(g)+X(f)\sigma(g)

showing that
X
is also a
(\tau,\sigma)
-derivation. Hence,
X
is a
symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation. The derivation given by (
2.2
) is an inner
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation which is
symmetric. Another example of a
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation is the Jackson
derivative
D_{q}
on the polynomial algebra
\mathbb{C}[x].
The
Jackson derivative is defined as

(2.3)

D_{q}(f(x))=\dfrac{f(qx)-f(x)}{(q-1)x}

for
f(x)\in\mathbb{C}[x]
and
q\in\mathbb{C}
with
q\neq 1.
For

f(x),g(x)\in\mathbb{C}[x],
one has

\displaystyle D_{q}(f(x)g(x))=f(qx)D_{q}(g(x))+D_{q}(f(x))g(x)=D_{q}(f(x))g(qx)+f(x)D_{q}(g(x)).

With endomorphisms
\sigma(f)(x)=f(qx)
and
\tau(f)(x)=f(x),
the
Jackson derivative
D_{q}
is a symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation. This
an example of
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation on the polynomial algebra

\mathbb{C}[x]
which is not inner (see also Section
3.3
).

On an associative algebra
\mathcal{A},
one can generalize (
2.2
) to construct symmetric inner
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations with values in a bimodule.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\sigma,\tau\in\operatorname{End}(\mathcal{A})
and let
M
be an
\mathcal{A}
-bimodule. If

m_{0}\in M
such that
[m_{0},\tau(f)]=[m_{0},\sigma(f)]=0

for all
f\in\mathcal{A}
then
X:\mathcal{A}\to M
given by

\displaystyle X(f)=m_{0}\tau(f)-\sigma(f)m_{0}

for
f\in\mathcal{A}
, is a symmetric inner
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation with values in
M
.

Proof.

By definition,
X
is an inner
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation. We want to
show that
X
is also
(\tau,\sigma)
-derivation.
Since
[m_{0},f]=0=[m_{0},\sigma(f)]

for all
f\in\mathcal{A},
one has

\displaystyle X(f)=m_{0}\tau(f)-\sigma(f)m_{0}=\tau(f)m_{0}-m_{0}\sigma(f).

For
f,g\in\mathcal{A},
one has

\displaystyle X(fg)=\tau(f)\tau(g)m_{0}-\tau(f)m_{0}\sigma(g)+\tau(f)m_{0}\sigma(g)-m_{0}\sigma(f)\sigma(g)

\displaystyle=\tau(f)(\tau(g)m_{0}-m_{0}\sigma(g))+(\tau(f)m_{0}-m_{0}\sigma(f))\sigma(g)

\displaystyle=\tau(f)X(g)+X(f)\sigma(g),

showing that
X
is a
(\tau,\sigma)
-derivation. Since we have shown
that
X
is both
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation and
(\tau,\sigma)
-derivation,
it is symmetric.
∎

Let us now introduce a notion of regularity for pairs of algebra
endomorphisms. Given
\sigma,\tau\in\operatorname{End}(\mathcal{A})
we write

(2.4)

\displaystyle\delta(f)=\tau(f)-\sigma(f)

for
f\in\mathcal{A}
. In what follows, the properties of
\delta
will be
important, and to this end we introduce the concept of regularity.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
\sigma,\tau\in\operatorname{End}(\mathcal{A})
. The pair
(\sigma,\tau)
is
called
regular
if there exists
f\in\mathcal{A}
such that

\delta(f)=\tau(f)-\sigma(f)
is not a zero divisor. Moreover, the pair

(\sigma,\tau)
is called
strongly regular
if there exists

f\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\delta(f)
is invertible.

Let us now look at some examples of regular and strongly regular pairs
of endomorphisms. Let us take, for example,
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{C}[x],
the
polynomial algebra in a variable
x.
Since there are no zero divisors
in
\mathbb{C}[x],
any pair
(\sigma,\tau)
such that
\sigma\neq\tau
is
a regular pair of endomorphisms. Now, let
\sigma(x)=qx
and

\tau(x)=x
for
q\in\mathbb{C},q\neq 1
. Since
\sigma\neq\tau

the pair
(\sigma,\tau)
is regular. Moreover, for a polynomial

p(x)=\sum_{i\geq 0}a_{i}x^{i}\in\mathbb{C}[x],

\displaystyle\delta(p)(x)=\sum_{i\geq 1}(1-q^{i})a_{i}x^{i}

is not invertible in
\mathbb{C}[x].
Thus the pair
(\sigma,\tau)
such
that
\sigma(x)=qx
and
\tau(x)=x,
is not a strongly regular
pair of endomorphisms of
\mathbb{C}[x].
On the other hand, given

\sigma(x)=x-h
and
\tau(x)=x,
for
h\in\mathbb{C}
(
h\neq 0
), then

\displaystyle\delta(x)=h

is invertible in
\mathbb{C}[x],
implying that the pair
(\sigma,\tau)

such that
\sigma(x)=x-h
and
\tau(x)=x
is a strongly regular
pair of endomorphisms over
\mathbb{C}[x].

In the following Lemma, we give a property of symmetric

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations that motivates a relationship between regular
pairs of endomorphisms and symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations.

Lemma 2.7
.

If
X
is a symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation with values in the
\mathcal{A}
-bimodule
M
, then

(2.5)

X(f)\delta(g)=\delta(f)X(g)

for all
f,g\in\mathcal{A}
, where
\delta=\tau-\sigma
.

Proof.

Assume that
X
is a symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation. For
f,g\in\mathcal{A},
one has

\displaystyle 0=X(fg)-\sigma(f)X(g)-X(f)\tau(g)

\displaystyle=\tau(f)X(g)+X(f)\sigma(g)-\sigma(f)X(g)-X(f)\tau(g)

\displaystyle=(\tau(f)-\sigma(f))X(g)+X(f)(\sigma(g)-\tau(g))

\displaystyle=\delta(f)X(g)-X(f)\delta(g),

implying that

X(f)\delta(g)=\delta(f)X(g).\qed

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
(\sigma,\tau)
be a
strongly regular pair of endomorphisms of
\mathcal{A}
and let
X
be a
symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation with values in the
\mathcal{A}
-bimodule

M
. Then there exists a unique
m_{0}\in M
such that

\displaystyle X(f)=m_{0}\tau(f)-\sigma(f)m_{0}

and

\displaystyle[m_{0},\tau(f)]=[m_{0},\sigma(f)]=0

for all
f\in\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

Since
X
is a symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation, one concludes from
Lemma
2.7
that

(2.6)

\displaystyle X(f)\delta(g)=\delta(f)X(g)

for all
f,g\in\mathcal{A}
. Since
(\sigma,\tau)
is assumed to be a strongly
regular pair of endomorphisms, there exists
g_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
such that

\delta(g_{0})
is invertible. It then follows from (
2.6
)
that

(2.7)

X(f)=\delta(f)X(g_{0})\delta(g_{0})^{-1}

for all
f\in\mathcal{A}
. Set
m_{0}=X(g_{0})\delta(g_{0})^{-1}
, use
(
2.7
) to compute

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=X(fg)-\sigma(f)X(g)-X(f)\tau(g)=\delta(fg)m_{0}-\sigma(f)\delta(g)m_{0}-\delta(f)m_{0}\tau(g)

\displaystyle=\big(\tau(f)\tau(g)-\sigma(f)\sigma(g)\big)m_{0}-\sigma(f)\big(\tau(g)-\sigma(g)\big)m_{0}-\big(\tau(f)-\sigma(f)\big)m_{0}\tau(g)

\displaystyle=\tau(f)\tau(g)m_{0}-\sigma(f)\tau(g)m_{0}-\tau(f)m_{0}\tau(g)+\sigma(f)m_{0}\tau(g)

\displaystyle=\tau(f)[\tau(g),m_{0}]-\sigma(f)[\tau(g),m_{0}]=\delta(f)[\tau(g),m_{0}],

and setting
f=g_{0}
, implying that
\delta(g_{0})
is invertible,
gives
[\tau(g),m_{0}]=0
for all
g\in\mathcal{A}
. Similarly, the identity

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=X(fg)-\tau(f)X(g)-X(f)\sigma(g)

implies that
[\sigma(g),m_{0}]=0
for all
g\in\mathcal{A}
. It follows that

\displaystyle X(f)=\delta(f)m_{0}=m_{0}\delta(f)=m_{0}\tau(f)-\sigma(f)m_{0}.

Finally, let us prove that
m_{0}
is unique. To this end, assume that

\displaystyle X(f)=\delta(f)m_{0}=\delta(f)n_{0}

for all
f\in\mathcal{A}
, implying that
\delta(f)(m_{0}-n_{0})=0
for all

f\in\mathcal{A}
. Setting
f=g_{0}
gives
m_{0}=n_{0}
since
\delta(g_{0})
is
invertible.
∎

Corollary 2.9
.

If
(\sigma,\tau)
is a strongly regular pair of endomorphisms of
\mathcal{A}
then
every symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation with values in an
\mathcal{A}
-bimodule
M
is inner.

3.
(\sigma,\tau)
-Hochschild Cohomology

The notion of Hochschild cohomology was first introduced in

[
Hoc45
]
. Hochschild cohomology theory in low dimension is
concerned with derivations of associative algebras. For instance, the
first cohomology group determines the outer derivations of the
algebra.
In this Section, we introduce a
(\sigma,\tau)
-version of Hochschild
cohomology, which we refer to as
(\sigma,\tau)
-Hochschild
cohomology. The
(\sigma,\tau)
-Hochschild cohomology will be defined as
the Hochschild cohomology with coefficients in a particular twisted
bimodule, which we denote as
M_{(\sigma,\tau)}.
In this case of the
Hochschild cohomology,
1
-cocycles are
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations and

1
-coboundaries are inner
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations. Therefore, first

(\sigma,\tau)
-Hochschild cohomology group, which we denote by

H^{1}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A},M)
, determines the outer

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations. Let us first look at the preliminaries before
we go into details.

3.1.
Hochschild cohomology preliminaries

Let

\mathcal{A}^{\otimes n}=\mathcal{A}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\cdots\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{A}

be the
n
-fold tensor product of
\mathcal{A}
and let
M
be an

\mathcal{A}
-bimodule. A
\mathbb{C}
-linear map
\omega:\mathcal{A}^{\otimes n}\to M

is called an
n
-cochain of an algebra
\mathcal{A}
with values in
M
and the
linear space of all
n
-cochains is denoted
C^{n}(\mathcal{A},M)
for

n\geq 1.
For
n=0,
let
C^{0}(\mathcal{A},M)=M.

The Hochschild cochain complex of
\mathcal{A}
with coefficients in
M
,
denoted
C^{\ast}(\mathcal{A},M,\delta)
is the sequence of linear
spaces of cochains

\displaystyle 0\to M\to C^{1}(\mathcal{A},M)\to C^{2}(\mathcal{A},M)\to\cdots\to C^{n}(\mathcal{A},M)\to C^{n+1}(\mathcal{A},M)\to\cdots

together with the differentials
\delta_{n}:C^{n}(\mathcal{A},M)\to C^{n+1}(\mathcal{A},M)
, defined by

\displaystyle(\delta_{0}m)(a)=ma-am

for
m\in M
and
a\in A
, and

\displaystyle(\delta_{n}\omega)(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n+1})=a_{1}

\displaystyle\omega(a_{2},\ldots,a_{n+1})+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{j}\omega(a_{1},\ldots,a_{j}a_{j+1},\ldots,a_{n+1})

\displaystyle+(-1)^{n+1}\omega(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})a_{n+1}

for
\omega\in C^{n}(\mathcal{A},M)
and
n\geq 1
. One shows that
\delta_{n}\circ\delta_{n-1}=0
.

An
n
-cochain
\omega
is called
n
-cocycle if
\delta\omega=0,

and it is called an
n
-coboundary if there exist an
(n-1)
-cochain

\rho
such that
\omega=\delta\rho.
The set of
n
-cocycles form
a subgroup of the additive group of
n
-cochains. The
n
-dimensional
cohomology group of
\mathcal{A}
with coefficients in
M
, denoted by

H^{n}(\mathcal{A},M),
is the group of
n
-dimensional cocycles
modulo the subgroup of
n
-dimensional coboundaries. Namely, denoting
by
Z^{n}(\mathcal{A},M)
the set of
n
-cocycles, i.e.

\displaystyle Z^{n}(\mathcal{A},M)=\{\omega\in C^{n}(\mathcal{A},M):\delta\omega=0\}

and denoting by
B^{n}(\mathcal{A},M)
the set of
n
-coboundaries, i.e.

\displaystyle B^{n}(\mathcal{A},M)=\{\omega\in C^{n}(\mathcal{A},M):\omega=\delta\rho,\hskip 2.84544pt\mbox{ for}\hskip 2.84544pt\rho\in C^{n-1}(\mathcal{A},M)\},

one defines

\displaystyle H^{n}(\mathcal{A},M)=Z^{n}(\mathcal{A},M)/B^{n}(\mathcal{A},M).

Now, let us examine the cohomology groups for the cases when

n=0,1.
For
n=0,
the Hochschild cohomology group is given by

\displaystyle H^{0}(\mathcal{A},M)=\mbox{ker}(\delta_{0})=\{m\in M:ma=am,\hskip 2.84544pt\mbox{for}\hskip 2.84544pta\in\mathcal{A}\},

which is simply the center of
M.
For
n=1,
one considers

\displaystyle(\delta_{1}\omega)(a_{1},a_{2})=a_{1}\omega(a_{2})-\omega(a_{1}a_{2})+\omega(a_{1})a_{2}

for
\omega\in C^{1}(\mathcal{A},M)
and the boundary map

\displaystyle(\delta_{0}m)(a_{1})=[m,a_{1}],

for
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathcal{A},m\in M.
Given that
\omega\in\mbox{ker}(\delta_{1}),
then one has

\displaystyle\omega(a_{1}a_{2})=a_{1}\omega(a_{2})+\omega(a_{1})a_{2}

implying that the linear map
\omega:\mathcal{A}\to M
is a derivation of
\mathcal{A}

with values in
M.
One writes

\displaystyle Z^{1}(\mathcal{A},M)=\{\omega\in C^{1}(\mathcal{A},M):\omega(ab)=a\omega(b)+\omega(a)b\quad\forall\,a,b\in\mathcal{A}\}=\mbox{Der}(\mathcal{A},M),

where
\mbox{Der}(\mathcal{A},M)
denotes the set of all derivations of
\mathcal{A}

with values in
M.
For
\omega
in the image of
\delta_{0},
one has

\displaystyle B^{1}(\mathcal{A},M)=\{\omega\in C^{1}(A,M):\omega=(\delta_{0}m)(a)=[m,a],\forall\,a\in\mathcal{A}\}=\mbox{Inn}(\mathcal{A},M),

where
\mbox{Inn}(\mathcal{A},M)
denotes the set of inner derivations of

\mathcal{A}
with values in
M.
Thus, it follows that

\displaystyle H^{1}(\mathcal{A},M)=Z^{1}(\mathcal{A},M)/B^{1}(\mathcal{A},M)=\mbox{Der}(\mathcal{A},M)/\mbox{Inn}(\mathcal{A},M),

represents the set of outer derivations of
\mathcal{A}
with values in
M.

3.2.
(\sigma,\tau)
-Hochschild cohomology

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an associative algebra and
M
be an
\mathcal{A}
-bimodule. Given

\sigma,\tau\in\operatorname{End}(\mathcal{A})
, we will construct a new
\mathcal{A}
-bimodule
structure on
M
, denoted by
M_{(\sigma,\tau)}
, by twisting the
action on
M
by the endomorphisms
\sigma
and
\tau.
Namely, let

\displaystyle f\cdot m=\sigma(f)m,\quad m\cdot f=m\tau(f)

for
f\in\mathcal{A}
and
m\in M.
It is easy to show that
M_{(\sigma,\tau)}
is again
an
\mathcal{A}
-bimodule. We want to compute the Hochschild cohomology group
of
\mathcal{A}
with values in
M_{(\sigma,\tau)}.
The boundary map

\delta_{n}:C^{n}(\mathcal{A},M_{(\sigma,\tau)})\to C^{n+1}(\mathcal{A},M_{(\sigma,\tau)})
is now
obtained as

\displaystyle(\delta_{0}m)(a)=m\cdot a-a\cdot m=m\tau(a)-\sigma(a)m

for
m\in M_{(\sigma,\tau)}
and
a\in\mathcal{A},
and

\displaystyle(\delta_{n}\omega)(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n+1})

\displaystyle=a_{1}\cdot\omega(a_{2},\ldots,a_{n+1})+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{j}\omega(a_{1},\ldots,a_{j}a_{j+1},\ldots,a_{n+1})

\displaystyle\qquad+(-1)^{n+1}\omega(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\cdot a_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\sigma(a_{1})\omega(a_{2},\ldots,a_{n+1})+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{j}\omega(a_{2},\ldots,a_{j}a_{j+1},\ldots,a_{n+1})

\displaystyle\qquad+(-1)^{n+1}\omega(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\tau(a_{n+1})

for
n\geq 1
. In particular, for
n=1
one obtains

\displaystyle(\delta_{1}\omega)(a_{1},a_{2})=\sigma(a_{1})\omega(a_{2})+\omega(a_{1})\tau(a_{2})-\omega(a_{1}a_{2})

implying that a
1
-cocycle
\omega\in C^{1}(\mathcal{A},M_{(\sigma,\tau)})
is a

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation with values in
M
. Let us denote
by
\mbox{Der}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A},M)
and
\mbox{Inn}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A},M)

the set of all
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations and the set of all inner

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations of
\mathcal{A}
with values in
M
respectively. Then
one sets

\displaystyle Z^{n}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A},M)=Z^{n}(\mathcal{A},M_{(\sigma,\tau)})

\displaystyle B^{n}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A},M)=B^{n}(\mathcal{A},M_{(\sigma,\tau)})

noting that

\displaystyle Z^{1}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A},M)

\displaystyle=\mbox{Der}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A},M),

\displaystyle B^{1}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A},M)

\displaystyle=\mbox{Inn}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A},M).

Hence, the Hochschild
cohomology groups of
\mathcal{A}
with values in
M_{(\sigma,\tau)}
are defined as

\displaystyle H^{n}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A},M)=Z^{n}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A},M)/B^{n}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A},M).

Let us now consider the
(\sigma,\tau)
-Hochschild cohomology
groups, for
n=0,1.
For
n=0
one has

\displaystyle H^{0}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A},M)=\{m\in M_{(\sigma,\tau)}:m\tau(a)=\sigma(a)m,\hskip 2.84544pt\mbox{for}\hskip 2.84544pta\in\mathcal{A}\}.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a commutative algebra and
(\sigma,\tau)
be a regular
pair. Then

\displaystyle H^{0}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A})=0.

Proof.

Let
b\in H_{(\sigma,\tau)}^{0}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A}),
then
b\delta(a)=0
for

a\in\mathcal{A}
(using that
\mathcal{A}
is commutative). Moreover, since
(\sigma,\tau)
is
a regular pair, there exists
a_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\delta(a_{0})
is not
a zero divisor. Then

\displaystyle b\delta(a_{0})=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad b=0.

Hence,
H_{(\sigma,\tau)}^{0}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A})=0.

∎

For
n=1,

(\delta_{1}\omega)(a_{1},a_{2})=0
implies that

\displaystyle\omega(a_{1}a_{2})=\sigma(a_{1})\omega(a_{2})+\omega(a_{1})\tau(a_{2})

showing that
\omega\in Z^{1}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A},M)
is a

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation. For
\omega\in C^{1}(\mathcal{A},M_{(\sigma,\tau)})
such
that

\displaystyle\omega=(\delta_{0}m)(a)=m\tau(a)-\sigma(a)m

implies that
\omega\in B_{(\sigma,\tau)}^{1}(\mathcal{A},M)
is an inner

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation. Hence

\displaystyle H_{(\sigma,\tau)}^{1}(\mathcal{A},M)=\mbox{Der}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A},M)/\mbox{Inn}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A},M)

constitute all outer
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations of
\mathcal{A}
with values in

M.
When
M=\mathcal{A}
we shall simply write

H^{1}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A})=H^{1}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A})
. Note that if

H^{1}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A})=0
then every
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation is
inner.

Let us now consider a few cases where one can determine
the
(\sigma,\tau)
-cohomology in lower degrees. First of all, it
follows immediately from Corollary
2.9

that
H^{1}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A},M)
vanishes for strongly regular symmetric

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations, which we state as follows.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\sigma,\tau\in\operatorname{End}(\mathcal{A})
be such that
(\sigma,\tau)
is a strongly
regular pair of endomorphisms. If every
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation on

\mathcal{A}
with values in
M
is symmetric, then

H^{1}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A},M)=H^{1}_{(\tau,\sigma)}(\mathcal{A},M)=0
.

In particular, for a commutative algebra
\mathcal{A}
, every

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation is symmetric and one can conclude that

H^{1}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A})=0
if
(\sigma,\tau)
is a strongly regular pair of
endomorphisms.

3.3.
Examples of
(\sigma,\tau)
-Hochchild cohomology

Let
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{K}[x],
be the polynomial algebra in the
indeterminate
x
over the field
\mathbb{K}.
Both the zero and one
dimensional Hochschild cohomology of
\mathbb{K}[x]
is isomorphic to

\mathbb{K}[x],
i.e.,
H^{0}(\mathbb{K}[x])\simeq\mathbb{K}[x]
and

H^{1}(\mathbb{K}[x])\simeq\mathbb{K}[x]\frac{d}{dx}
. (see for example

[
Wit20
]
). In this section we describe the
(\sigma,\tau)
-Hochschild cohomology groups in degree 0 and 1.

In degree 0, one can apply Proposition
3.1
, assuming
that
\sigma\neq\tau
. In that case, there exists a polynomial

p_{0}\in\mathbb{K}[x]
such that
\delta(p_{0})\neq 0
implying that

\delta(p_{0})
is not a zero divisor since
\mathbb{K}[x]
is an integral
domain. Hence

\displaystyle H^{0}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathbb{K}[x])=0.

Next, let us consider
H^{1}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathbb{K}[x])
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\sigma,\tau\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{K}[x])
and let
\delta=\tau-\sigma
. Then

\displaystyle H^{1}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathbb{K}[x])=\mathbb{K}[x]/I_{\delta},

where

\displaystyle I_{\delta}=\{p(x)\delta(x):p(x)\in\mathbb{K}[x]\}

is the ideal generated by
\delta(x)
.

Proof.

In
\mathbb{K}[x],
a
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation
X
is completely determined by

\displaystyle X(x)=f_{0}(x)\in\mathbb{K}[x]

and, conversely, any such choice of
f_{0}\in\mathbb{K}[x]
gives
a
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation by using the twisted Leibniz rule, implying
that

Z^{1}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathbb{K}[x])\simeq\mathbb{K}[x]
, where the isomorphism is provided by

X\mapsto X(x)=f_{0}(x)
. Now if

X\in B^{1}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathbb{K}[x])
then

\displaystyle X(x)=g_{0}(x)(\tau(x)-\sigma(x))=g_{0}(x)\delta(x),

for some
g_{0}\in\mathbb{K}[x]
, implying that

\displaystyle B^{1}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathbb{K}[x])\simeq I_{\delta}.

Hence

H^{1}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathbb{K}[x])=Z^{1}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathbb{K}[x])/B^{1}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathbb{K}[x])=\mathbb{K}[x]/I_{\delta}.\qed

To illustrate the above result, let us apply it to a particular case.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\sigma,\tau
be endomorphisms of the polynomial algebra

\mathbb{K}[x]
given by
\sigma(x)=qx
and
\tau(x)=x
for

q\neq 0,1.
Then

\displaystyle H^{1}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathbb{K}[x],\mathbb{K}[x])=\{\lambda 1:\lambda\in\mathbb{K}\}.

Proof.

Using the definition of
\sigma
and
\tau,
one obtains

\displaystyle\delta(x)=\tau(x)-\sigma(x)=(1-q)x

and note that

\displaystyle\left(\dfrac{1}{1-q}\right)\delta(x)=x,\left(\dfrac{x}{1-q}\right)\delta(x)=x^{2},\ldots,\left(\dfrac{x^{n-1}}{1-q}\right)\delta(x)=x^{n},

are all in the ideal

I_{\delta}=\langle p(x)\delta(x)|p(x)\in\mathbb{K}[x]\rangle

except
1.
Therefore,
I_{\delta}
is the polynomial algebra

\mathbb{K}[x]
without
\lambda 1
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{K}.

Using Proposition
3.3
, the outer

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations of
\mathbb{K}[x]
for the endomorphisms

\sigma(x)=qx
and
\tau(x)=x
are

\mathbb{K}[x]/I_{\delta}=\{\lambda 1|\lambda\in\mathbb{K}\}.\qed

Corollary 3.5
.

The Jackson derivative is an outer
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation of
\mathbb{K}[x].

Proof.

In
\mathbb{K}[x]
, every
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation
X
is determined
by
X(x)=f_{0}(x)\in\mathbb{K}[x].
Let
X
be the Jackson
derivative. It is easy to show that
X
is a
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation
with
\sigma(x)=qx
and
\tau(x)=x
and
X(x)=1.
Since the
Jackson derivative is determined by
f_{0}(x)=1
then by
Proposition
3.4
, it is an outer derivation of

\mathbb{K}[x].

∎

Next, let
\mathcal{A}=\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
be the algebra of
(N\times N)
complex
matrices. If
\sigma,\tau\in\operatorname{End}(\mathcal{A})
are automorphisms, one can
easily describe
H^{0}_{\sigma\tau}(\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C}))
.

Proposition 3.6
.

If
\sigma,\tau\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C}))
then
H^{0}_{(\sigma,\tau)}\big(\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})\big)\simeq\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

Since
\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
is a central simple algebra, the Skolem-Noether theorem
implies that all automorphisms are inner; that is, there exist

U,V\in\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
(unique up to multiplication by a complex number) such
that
\tau(A)=U^{-1}AU
and
\sigma(A)=V^{-1}AV
for all

A\in\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
. By definition

\displaystyle H^{0}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C}))=\{A\in\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C}):A\tau(B)=\sigma(B)A\quad\forall\,B\in\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})\},

and one computes

\displaystyle A\tau(B)=\sigma(B)A\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad AU^{-1}BU=V^{-1}BVA\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad[VAU^{-1},B]=0.

For the above relation to be true for all
B\in\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
one necessarily
has that
VAU^{-1}=z\mathds{1}_{N}
for some
z\in\mathbb{C}
, where
\mathds{1}_{N}

denotes the identity matrix. Thus, it follows that

H^{0}_{(\sigma,\tau)}\big(\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})\big)=\{zV^{-1}U:z\in\mathbb{C}\}\simeq\mathbb{C}.\qed

It is well known that all derivations on
\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
are inner, implying
that

H^{1}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C}))=0.

Moreover, it is a fact that
H^{n}(\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),M)=0
for
n\geq 1
and
arbitrary bimodule
M
(see e.g.
[
Red01
]
), implying the
following result.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
denote the algebra of
N\times N
complex matrices, and let
M
be a
\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
-bimodule. Then

\displaystyle H^{n}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),M)=0

for
n\geq 1
.

Proof.

Since for any bimodule
M
and
n\geq 1
one has
H^{n}(\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),M)=0
, it follows that

H^{1}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),M)=H^{1}(\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),M_{(\sigma,\tau)})=0.\qed

In particular, the above result implies that every
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation on
\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
is inner.

4.
Symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebras

The framework to study
(\sigma,\tau)
-connections on
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebras
has been studied in
[
AI22
]
,
[
AIL20
]

and
[
AIL22
]
. In this Section, we extend the framework to
accommodate symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations already introduced in
Section
2.
To this effect, we introduce symmetric

(\sigma,\tau)
-algebras and symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-connections and study
how the strongly regular condition of endomorphisms in a

(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra influence such connections. Let us recall a few basic
definitions from
[
AI22
]
.

Definition 4.1
.

A
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra
\Sigma=(\mathcal{A},\{X_{a}\}_{a\in I})
is a
pair where
\mathcal{A}
is an associative algebra and
X_{a}
is a

(\sigma_{a},\tau_{a})
-derivation of
\mathcal{A}
for
a\in I.

Definition 4.2
.

For a
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra
\Sigma=(\mathcal{A},\{X_{a}\}_{a\in I}),
we let

T\Sigma\subseteq\mbox{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A})

be the vector space generated by
\{X_{a}\}_{a\in I}
. We call

T\Sigma
the
tangent space of
\Sigma
.

Recall that a pair
(\sigma,\tau)
of endomorphisms is strongly regular if
there exists
f_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
such that

\delta(f_{0})=\tau(f_{0})-\sigma(f_{0})
is invertible. In a

(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra, there are several pairs of endomorphisms, and for
each
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation
X_{a}
in the
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra, there
are pairs
(\sigma_{a},\tau_{a})
for
a\in I.
We would like to
consider the case that all the pairs of endomorphisms in the

(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra are simultaneously strongly regular, i.e., there
exist
f_{a}\in\mathcal{A}
for
a\in I
such that

\delta_{a}(f_{a})=\tau_{a}(f_{a})-\sigma_{a}(f_{a})
is
invertible. We shall call a
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra with this property
strongly regular. Similarly, if each
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation in the

(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra is symmetric, we shall call the

(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra symmetric. In the following, we give precise
definitions.

Definition 4.3
.

A
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra
\Sigma=(\mathcal{A},\{X_{a}\}_{a\in I})
is
called
(strongly) regular
if
(\sigma_{a},\tau_{a})
is a
(strongly) regular pair of endomorphisms of
\mathcal{A}
for all
a\in I
.

Definition 4.4
.

A symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra

\Sigma=(\mathcal{A},\{X_{a}\}_{a\in I})
is a
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra such
that
X_{a}
is a symmetric
(\sigma_{a},\tau_{a})
-derivation for

a\in I
.

Note that if
\mathcal{A}
is a commutative algebra, then any

(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra
\Sigma=(\mathcal{A},\{X_{a}\}_{a\in I})
is symmetric
since
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations on commutative algebras are
symmetric. For such an algebra to be a regular
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra,
one may consider the case when the algebra has no zero divisors and

\sigma_{a}\neq\tau_{a}
for all
a\in I.
Again, this is possible
because then

\delta_{a}(f_{a})=\tau_{a}(f_{a})-\sigma_{a}(f_{A})\neq 0
for some

f_{a}\in\mathcal{A}
.
To obtain a strongly regular algebra, one takes for example, the algebra
\mathbb{K}[x]
of
polynomials in one variable
x
and assumes that
\sigma_{a}=\sigma

with
\sigma(p)(x)=p(x-h)
for
h\in\mathbb{K}
and
p(x)\in\mathbb{K}
and
assume that
\tau_{a}=\tau
with
\tau(p)(x)=p(x).
In this case
each pair
(\sigma_{a},\tau_{a})
is strongly regular in
\mathbb{K}[x].

Let us now recall the definition of modules over
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebras.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
\Sigma=(\mathcal{A},\{X_{a}\}_{a\in I})
be a
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra. A

left
\Sigma
-module
(M,\{(\hat{\sigma}_{a},\hat{\tau}_{a})\}_{a\in I})

is a left
\mathcal{A}
-module
M
together with
\mathbb{C}
-linear maps

\hat{\sigma}_{a},\hat{\tau}_{a}:M\to M
such that

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{a}(fm)=\sigma_{a}(f)\hat{\sigma}(m)

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{a}(fm)=\tau_{a}(f)\hat{\tau}_{a}(m)

for
f\in\mathcal{A}
,
m\in M
and
a\in I
.

Similarly, one defines right
\Sigma
-modules as well as

\Sigma
-bimodules (cf.
[
AI22
]
).
Let now us recall a few examples of
\Sigma
-modules.

Example 4.6
.

Let
\Sigma=(\mathcal{A},\{X_{a}\}_{a\in I})
be a
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra and
let
\mathcal{A}^{n}
be a free left
\mathcal{A}
-module with basis
\{e_{i}\},

i=1,\ldots,n.
One introduces a canonical
\Sigma
-module
structure on
\mathcal{A}^{n}
by setting

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{a}^{0}(m)=\sigma_{a}(m^{i})e_{i},\quad\hat{\tau}_{a}^{0}(m)=\tau_{a}(m^{i})e_{i}

where
m=m^{i}e_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{n}
(summation over
i
from
1
to

n
is implied). Now, let
f\in\mathcal{A}
and
m\in\mathcal{A}^{n},
one has

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{a}^{0}(fm)=\sigma_{a}(fm^{i})e_{i}=\sigma_{a}(f)\sigma_{a}(m^{i})e_{i}=\sigma_{a}(f)\hat{\sigma}_{a}^{0}(m)

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{a}^{0}(fm)=\tau_{a}(fm^{i})e_{i}=\tau_{a}(f)\tau_{a}(m^{i})e_{i}=\tau_{a}(f)\hat{\tau}_{a}^{0}(m),

showing that
(\mathcal{A}^{n},\{(\hat{\sigma}_{a}^{0},\hat{\tau}_{a}^{0})\}_{a\in i})
is a left
\Sigma
-module.

The example above is an example of a free
\Sigma
-module. Let us now
look an example of a
\Sigma
-module which is not free.

Example 4.7
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),
the algebra of complex
(N\times N)
-matrices and
let
\Sigma=(\mathcal{A},\{X_{a}\}_{a=1}^{n})
where
X_{a}:\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})\to\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})

is given by

\displaystyle X_{a}(A)=A-\sigma_{a}(A)

with endomorphism
\sigma_{a}(A)=U_{a}AU_{a}^{-1}
for

A\in\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
and some complex
(N\times N)
-matrices
U_{a}
such
that
[U_{a},U_{b}]=0
for
a,b=1,\ldots,n.
Then
X_{a}

is a
(\sigma_{a},\operatorname{Id})
-derivation and

(\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\{(\sigma_{a},\operatorname{Id})\}_{a=1}^{n})
is a free

\Sigma
-module.

According to
[
AI22
]
, applying an appropriate
projector to any
\Sigma
-module, one obtains a projective

\Sigma
-module. Let
p=v_{0}v_{0}^{\dagger}
for

v_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
with
|v_{0}|=1
be the projector.
Then
(\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})p,\{(\hat{\sigma}_{a},\hat{\tau}_{a})\}_{a=1}^{n})
, with

\hat{\sigma}_{a}(A)=\sigma_{a}(A)p
and
\hat{\tau}_{a}(A)=Ap
, is a
projective
\Sigma
-module and one notes that
\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})p
is
isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}^{N}
, which is not a free left

\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
-module.

Now, let us recall the definition of
(\sigma,\tau)
-connections on
\Sigma
-modules.

Definition 4.8
.

Let
\Sigma=(\mathcal{A},\{X_{a}\}_{a\in I})
be a
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra and let

(M,\{(\hat{\sigma}_{a},\hat{\tau}_{a})\}_{a\in I})
be a left
\Sigma
-module. A
left
(\sigma,\tau)
-connection on
M
is a map

\nabla:T\Sigma\times M\to M
satisfying

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}(m_{1}+m_{2})=\nabla_{X}m_{1}+\nabla_{X}m_{2}

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}(\lambda m)=\lambda\nabla_{X}m

\displaystyle\nabla_{X+Y}m=\nabla_{X}m+\nabla_{Y}m

\displaystyle\nabla_{\lambda X}m=\lambda\nabla_{X}m

\displaystyle\nabla_{X_{a}}(fm)=\sigma_{a}(f)\nabla_{X_{a}}m+X_{a}(f)\hat{\tau}_{a}(m)

for all
X,Y\in T\Sigma
,
m,m_{1},m_{2}\in M
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{C},

f\in\mathcal{A}
and
a\in I
.

Analogously, a right
(\sigma,\tau)
-connection on a right
\Sigma
-module

(M,\{(\hat{\sigma}_{a},\hat{\tau}_{a})\}_{a\in I})
satisfies

\displaystyle\nabla_{X_{a}}(mf)=\hat{\sigma}_{a}(m)X_{a}(f)+\big(\nabla_{X_{a}}m\big)\tau_{a}(f),

in addition to the linearity properties. Moreover, if
M
is a

(\sigma,\tau)
-bimodule and
\nabla
is a left
(\sigma,\tau)
-connection,
as well as a right
(\sigma,\tau)
-connection, we say that
\nabla
is a

(\sigma,\tau)
-bimodule connection (cf.
[
AI22
]
).

We would like to introduce a
(\sigma,\tau)
-connection involving
symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations. Since symmetric

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations satisfy two Leibniz rules and one thinks of

(\sigma,\tau)
-connections as covariant derivatives in the direction of
the
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation, it is natural to have a notion of
symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-connection satisfying two Leibniz rules in
correspondence to those of the symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations. In
the following, we define symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-connections and discuss
such connections on (strongly) regular and symmetric

(\sigma,\tau)
-algebras.

Definition 4.9
.

Let
\Sigma=(\mathcal{A},\{X_{a}\}_{a\in I})
be a
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra and let

(M,\{(\hat{\sigma}_{a},\hat{\tau}_{a})\}_{a\in I})
be a left
\Sigma
-module. A
left
(\sigma,\tau)
-connection on
M
is called
symmetric
if

(4.1)

\displaystyle\nabla_{X_{a}}(fm)=\tau_{a}(f)\nabla_{X_{a}}m+X_{a}(f)\hat{\sigma}_{a}(m)

for all
a\in I
and
m\in M
.

On
\Sigma
-bimodules, it is easy to construct connections
corresponding to inner
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations.

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
\Sigma=(\mathcal{A},\{X_{a}\}_{a\in I})
be a
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra such
that
X_{a}
is an inner
(\sigma_{a},\tau_{a})
-derivation for all
a\in I
; i.e
there exist
\{f_{a}\}_{a\in I}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
such that

\displaystyle X_{a}(f)=f_{a}\tau_{a}(f)-\sigma_{a}(f)f_{a}

for
a\in I
. If
(M,\{(\hat{\sigma}_{a},\hat{\tau}_{a})\}_{a\in I})
is a

\Sigma
-bimodule then

\displaystyle\nabla_{X_{a}}(m)=f_{a}\hat{\tau}_{a}(m)-\hat{\sigma}_{a}(m)f_{a}

defines a
(\sigma,\tau)
-bimodule connection on
(M,\{(\hat{\sigma}_{a},\hat{\tau}_{a})\}_{a\in I})
.

Proof.

It is easy to show linearity in
T\Sigma
and
M
is satisfied.
Let us show that
\nabla
satisfies both the left and the right twisted Leibniz rule. For
f\in\mathcal{A}
and
m\in M,
one has

\displaystyle\nabla_{X_{a}}(fm)=f_{a}\hat{\tau}_{a}(fm)-\hat{\sigma}_{a}(fm)f_{a}=f_{a}\tau_{a}(f)\hat{\tau}_{a}(m)-\sigma_{a}(f)\hat{\sigma}_{a}(m)f_{a}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{a}(f)f_{a}\hat{\tau}_{a}(m)-\sigma_{a}(f)\hat{\sigma}_{a}(m)f_{a}+f_{a}\tau_{a}(f)\hat{\tau}_{a}(m)-\sigma_{a}(f)f_{a}\hat{\tau}_{a}(m)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{a}(f)(f_{a}\hat{\tau}_{a}(m)-\hat{\sigma}_{a}(m)f_{a})+(f_{a}\tau_{a}(f)-\sigma_{a}(f)f_{a})\hat{\tau}_{a}(m)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{a}(f)\nabla_{X_{a}}(m)+X_{a}(f)\hat{\tau}_{a}(m),

showing that
\nabla
satisfies the left twisted Leibniz rule. In a similar manner, Let us check that
\nabla
satisfies the right twisted Leibniz rule. One has

\displaystyle\nabla_{X_{a}}(mf)=f_{a}\hat{\tau}_{a}(mf)-\hat{\sigma}_{a}(mf)f_{a}=f_{a}\hat{\tau}_{a}(m)\tau_{a}(f)-\hat{\sigma}_{a}(m)\sigma_{a}(f)f_{a}

\displaystyle=\hat{\sigma}_{a}(m)f_{a}\tau_{a}(f)-\hat{\sigma}_{a}(m)\sigma_{a}(f)f_{a}+f_{a}\hat{\tau}_{a}(m)\tau_{a}(f)-\hat{\sigma}_{a}(m)f_{a}\tau_{a}(f)

\displaystyle=\hat{\sigma}_{a}(m)(f_{a}\tau_{a}(f)-\sigma_{a}(f)f_{a})+(f_{a}\hat{\tau}_{a}(m)-\hat{\sigma}_{a}(m)f_{a})\tau_{a}(f)

\displaystyle=\hat{\sigma}_{a}(m)X_{a}(f)+\nabla_{X_{a}}(m)\tau_{a}(f).\qed

Now, let
(\mathcal{A},\{X_{a}\}_{a\in I})
be a symmetric strongly regular

(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra; it follows from Proposition
2.8
that there exists
\{f_{a}\}_{a\in I}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
such that

[f_{a},\sigma_{a}(f)]=[f_{a},\tau_{a}(f)]=0
and

\displaystyle X_{a}(f)=f_{a}\delta_{a}(f)=f_{a}\big(\tau_{a}(f)-\sigma_{a}(f)\big)

for
f\in\mathcal{A}
. Given a (left)
\Sigma
-module

(M,\{\hat{\sigma}_{a},\hat{\tau}_{a}\}_{a\in I})
one can easily check that

\displaystyle\nabla_{X_{a}}m=f_{a}\hat{\delta}_{a}(m)=f_{a}\big(\hat{\tau}_{a}(m)-\hat{\sigma}_{a}(m)\big)

defines a symmetric left
(\sigma,\tau)
-connection on

(M,\{\hat{\sigma}_{a},\hat{\tau}_{a}\}_{a\in I})
. The following result states
that this every symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-connection on a strongly
regular symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra is of this form.

Proposition 4.11
.

Let
\Sigma=(\mathcal{A},\{X_{a}\}_{a\in I})
be a strongly regular
symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra and write

\displaystyle X_{a}(f)=f_{a}\delta_{a}(f)=f_{a}\big(\tau_{a}(f)-\sigma_{a}(f)\big)

for
f_{a}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
[f_{a},\tau_{a}(f)]=[f_{a},\sigma_{a}(f)]=0.
If
\nabla
is a (left) symmetric

(\sigma,\tau)
-connection on a
\Sigma
-module

(M,\{\hat{\sigma}_{a},\hat{\tau}_{a}\}_{a\in I})
then

\displaystyle\nabla_{X_{a}}(m)=f_{a}\hat{\delta}_{a}(m)=f_{a}\big(\hat{\tau}(m)-\hat{\sigma}(m)\big)

for
m\in M
and
a\in I
.

Proof.

Since
\nabla
is assumed to be symmetric, one has

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\nabla_{X_{a}}(fm)-\nabla_{X_{a}}(fm)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{a}(f)\nabla_{X_{a}}m+X_{a}(f)\hat{\tau}_{a}(m)-\tau_{a}(f)\nabla_{X_{a}}m-X_{a}(f)\hat{\sigma}_{a}(m)

\displaystyle=(\sigma_{a}(f)-\tau_{a}(f))\nabla_{X_{a}}m+X_{a}(f)(\hat{\tau}_{a}(m)-\hat{\sigma}_{a}(m))

\displaystyle=-\delta_{a}(f)\nabla_{X_{a}}m+X_{a}(f)\hat{\delta}_{a}(m).

Using that
X_{a}(f)=f_{a}\delta_{a}(f)
together with
[f_{a},\delta_{a}(f)]=0
, it follows that

(4.2)

\displaystyle\delta_{a}(f)\big(\nabla_{X_{a}}m-f_{a}\hat{\delta}_{a}(m)\big)=0

for
f\in\mathcal{A}
and
m\in M
. Since
(\sigma,\tau)
is a strongly regular
pair, there exists
g_{a}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\delta(g_{a})
is
invertible. Thus, setting
f=g_{a}
in (
4.2
) gives

\displaystyle\nabla_{X_{a}}m=f_{a}\hat{\delta}_{a}(m)

for
m\in M
.
∎

Let us summarize these results in the following corollary.

Corollary 4.12
.

Let
\Sigma=(\mathcal{A},\{X_{a}\}_{a\in I})
be a strongly regular
symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra and let

(M,\{\hat{\sigma}_{a},\hat{\tau}_{a}\}_{a\in I})
be a left
\Sigma
-module. Then
there exists a unique symmetric left
(\sigma,\tau)
-connection on

(M,\{\hat{\sigma}_{a},\hat{\tau}_{a}\}_{a\in I})
.

5.
Curvature

Curvature of
(\sigma,\tau)
-connections was defined in

[
AI22
]
. In this Section, we explore the
possibility of curvatures of symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-connections to
satisfy a twisted linearity condition. In classical differential
geometry, curvature of connections are discussed with reference to the
Lie-algebra structure induced by vector fields on a manifold. In the
case of
(\sigma,\tau)
-connections, the curvature is discussed with
reference to the
(\sigma,\tau)
-Lie algebra structure. Let us recall a
few definitions.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\Sigma=(\mathcal{A},\{X_{a}\}_{a\in I})
be a
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra
and let
T\Sigma
denote the complex vector space generated by

\{X_{a}\}_{a\in I}.
Given a
\mathbb{C}
-bilinear map

R:T\Sigma\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}T\Sigma\to T\Sigma\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}T\Sigma,
we say that
(T\Sigma,R)
is a

(\sigma,\tau)
-
Lie algebra if

(1)

R^{2}=\mbox{id}_{T\Sigma\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}T\Sigma},

(2)

m(X_{a}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}X_{b}-R(X_{a}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}X_{b}))\in T\Sigma,

for all
a,b\in I,
where

m:T\Sigma\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}T\Sigma\to\mbox{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(A)

denotes the composition map given by

m(X_{a}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}X_{b})=X_{a}\circ X_{b}.

Given a
(\sigma,\tau)
-Lie algebra
(T\Sigma,R)
one introduces the
bracket
[\cdot,\cdot]_{R}:T\Sigma\times T\Sigma\to T\Sigma
as

\displaystyle[X,Y]_{R}=m(X_{a}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}X_{b}-R(X_{a}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}X_{b}))

for
X,Y\in T\Sigma
. Moreover, if
I
is a finite set, one introduces
the components of
R
, as well as structure constants

C_{ab}^{p}\in\mathbb{C}
, as

\displaystyle[X_{a},X_{b}]_{R}=R_{ab}^{pq}X_{p}\otimes X_{q}=C_{ab}^{p}X_{p}

(where summation over repeated indices is implied). Now, let us
recall the definition of curvature with respect to a
(\sigma,\tau)
-Lie
algebra structure on
T\Sigma
.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
\Sigma=(\mathcal{A},\{X_{a}\}_{a\in I})
be a
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra,
such that
(T\Sigma,R)
is a
(\sigma,\tau)
-Lie algebra, and let

(M,\{(\hat{\sigma}_{a},\hat{\tau}_{a})\}_{a\in I})
be a

\Sigma
-module. Given a
(\sigma,\tau)
-connection
\nabla,
the
curvature of
\nabla
is defined as

\displaystyle\mbox{Curv}(X,Y)n=m_{\nabla}(X\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}Y-R(X\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}Y))n-\nabla_{[X,Y]_{R}}n

for
n\in M
and
X,Y\in T\Sigma,
where

m_{\nabla}(X\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}Y)=\nabla_{X}\circ\nabla_{Y}.

In components, the curvature can be written as

\displaystyle\mbox{Curv}(X_{a},X_{b})=\nabla_{X_{a}}\circ\nabla_{X_{b}}-R_{ab}^{pq}\nabla_{X_{p}}\circ\nabla_{X_{q}}-C_{ab}^{p}\nabla_{X_{p}}.

Let us now discuss the linearity properties of curvature of

(\sigma,\tau)
-connections. In general, the curvature of

(\sigma,\tau)
-connections does not satisfy any linearity property. The
linearity property of curvature can be satisfied depending on how the
map
R
on the
(\sigma,\tau)
-Lie algebra is defined, how the
endomorphisms
\sigma_{a}
’s and
\tau_{a}
’s on the algebra interact
and how the maps
\hat{\tau}_{a}
’s on the
\Sigma
-module interact with
the connection. For example, for
f\in\mathcal{A}
and
m\in M,
one has

\displaystyle\mbox{Curv}(X_{a},X_{b})(fm)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{X_{a}}(\nabla_{X_{b}}(fm))-R_{ab}^{pq}\nabla_{X_{p}}(\nabla_{X_{q}}(fm))-C_{ab}^{p}\nabla_{X_{p}}(fm)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{X_{a}}(\sigma_{b}(f)\nabla_{X_{b}}(m)+X_{b}(f)\hat{\tau}_{b}(m))-R_{ab}^{pq}\nabla_{X_{p}}(\sigma_{q}(f)\nabla_{X_{q}}(m)

\displaystyle\qquad+X_{q}(f)\hat{\tau}_{q}(m))-C_{ab}^{p}(\sigma_{p}(f)\nabla_{X_{p}}(m)+X_{p}(f)\hat{\tau}_{p}(m))

\displaystyle=\sigma_{a}(\sigma_{b}(f))\nabla_{X_{a}}(\nabla_{X_{b}}(m))+X_{a}(\sigma_{b}(f))\hat{\tau}_{a}(\nabla_{X_{b}}(m))

\displaystyle\qquad+\sigma_{a}(X_{b}(f))\nabla_{X_{a}}(\hat{\tau}_{a}(m))+X_{a}(X_{b}(f))\hat{\tau}_{a}(\hat{\tau}_{b}(m))

\displaystyle\qquad-R_{ab}^{pq}(\sigma_{p}(\sigma_{q}(f))\nabla_{X_{p}}(\nabla_{X_{q}}(m))+X_{p}(\sigma_{q}(f))\hat{\tau}_{p}(\nabla_{X_{q}}(m)))

\displaystyle\qquad-R_{ab}^{pq}(\sigma_{p}(X_{q}(f))+X_{p}(X_{q}(f))\hat{\tau}_{p}(\hat{\tau}_{q}(m)))

\displaystyle\qquad-C_{ab}^{p}(\sigma_{p}(f)\nabla_{X_{p}}(m)+X_{p}(f)\hat{\tau}_{p}(m)).

Despite that the curvature of a general
(\sigma,\tau)
-connection potrays no linearity property in general, for specific
(\sigma,\tau)
-Lie algebra or specific
(\sigma,\tau)
-connections, the curvature can satisfy a linearity property. For example in
[
AI22
]
, the curvature of a
(\sigma,\tau)
-connection on a matrix algebra satisfies a certain twisted linearity property. To this effect, let us consider curvature of a general
(\sigma,\tau)
-connection with reference to a specific
(\sigma,\tau)
-Lie algebra. Assume that

\displaystyle R_{ab}^{pq}=\delta_{a}^{q}\delta_{b}^{p}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{if}\quad a=q,b=p,\\
0&\mbox{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

and
C_{ab}^{p}=0
for all
a,b\in I.
Then the bracket on the generators of
(T\Sigma,R)
is

(5.2)

\displaystyle[X_{a},X_{b}]_{R}=X_{a}\circ X_{b}-X_{b}\circ X_{a}=0,

implying that the curvature of a
(\sigma,\tau)
-connection
\nabla
can now be written as

\displaystyle\mbox{Curv}(X_{a},X_{b})=\nabla_{X_{a}}\circ\nabla_{X_{b}}-\nabla_{X_{b}}\circ\nabla_{X_{a}}.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\Sigma=(\mathcal{A},\{X_{a}\}_{a\in I})
be a
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra such that
(T\Sigma,R)
is the
(\sigma,\tau)
-Lie algebra with bracket

\displaystyle[X_{a},X_{b}]_{R}=X_{a}\circ X_{b}-X_{b}\circ X_{a}=0

and let
(M,\{(\hat{\sigma}_{a},\hat{\tau}_{a})\}_{a\in I})
be a left
\Sigma
-module. If
\nabla:T\Sigma\times M\to M
is a left
(\sigma,\tau)
-connection and

(5.3)

\displaystyle[\sigma_{a},\sigma_{b}]=0=[\sigma_{a},X_{b}],

\displaystyle[\hat{\tau}_{a},\hat{\tau}_{b}]=0=[\hat{\tau}_{a},\nabla_{X_{b}}]

for all
a,b\in I,
then

(5.4)

\displaystyle\mbox{Curv}(X_{a},X_{b}(fm))=\sigma_{a}(\sigma_{b}(f))\mbox{Curv}(X_{a},X_{b})m

for
f\in\mathcal{A}
and
m\in M.

Proof.

Since the
(\sigma,\tau)
-Lie algebra bracket is given by (
5.2
), the curvature is written as

\displaystyle\mbox{Curv}(X_{a},X_{b})=\nabla_{X_{a}}\circ\nabla_{X_{b}}-\nabla_{X_{b}}\circ\nabla_{X_{a}}.

Now, for
f\in\mathcal{A}
and
m\in M,
and applying the commutation relations (
5.3
), one has

\displaystyle\mbox{Curv}(X_{a},X_{b})(fm)=\nabla_{X_{a}}(\nabla_{X_{b}}(fm))-\nabla_{X_{b}}(\nabla_{X_{a}}(fm))

\displaystyle=\nabla_{X_{a}}(\sigma_{b}(f)\nabla_{X_{b}}(m)+X_{b}(f)\hat{\tau}_{b}(m))-\nabla_{X_{b}}(\sigma_{a}(f)\nabla_{X_{a}}(m)+X_{a}(f)\hat{\tau}_{a}(m))

\displaystyle=\sigma_{a}(\sigma_{b}(f))\nabla_{X_{a}}(\nabla_{X_{b}}(m))+X_{a}(\sigma_{b}(f))\hat{\tau}_{a}(\nabla_{X_{b}}(m))+\sigma_{a}(X_{b}(f))\nabla_{X_{a}}(\hat{\tau}_{b}(m))

\displaystyle+X_{a}(X_{b}(f))\hat{\tau}_{a}(\hat{\tau}_{b}(m))

\displaystyle-\sigma_{b}(\sigma_{a}(f))\nabla_{X_{b}}(\nabla_{X_{a}}(m))-X_{b}(\sigma_{a}(f))\hat{\tau}_{b}(\nabla_{X_{a}}(m))-\sigma_{b}(X_{a}(f))\nabla_{X_{b}}(\hat{\tau}_{a}(m))

\displaystyle-X_{b}(X_{a}(f))\hat{\tau}_{b}(\hat{\tau}_{a}(m))

\displaystyle=\sigma_{a}(\sigma_{b}(f))(\nabla_{X_{a}}(\nabla_{X_{b}}(m))-\nabla_{X_{b}}(\nabla_{X_{a}}(m)))

\displaystyle=(\sigma_{a}\circ\sigma_{b})(f))\mbox{Curv}(X_{a},X_{b})m,

showing the twisted linearity property.
∎

From the Proposition above, not all left
(\sigma,\tau)
-connections have
a curvature that satisfies the twisted linearity property (
5.4
). The commutation relations (
5.3
) of Proposition
5.3
restricts the
left
(\sigma,\tau)
-connections to be considered due to that the
connection is required to commute with the maps
\hat{\tau}_{a}
for all

a,
i.e.,
[\hat{\tau}_{a},\nabla_{X_{b}}]=0
for all
a,b.
In this
Proposition, we would like curvature to satisfy the twisted linearity
property (
5.4
) without much restrictions on
the left
(\sigma,\tau)
-connection. However, this may impose additional
conditions on the endomorphisms and the
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations. Let
us take for example, the left
(\sigma,\tau)
-connection given by

\nabla_{X_{a}}(e_{i})=\gamma_{ai}^{k}e_{k}
with

\gamma_{ai}^{k}\in\mathcal{A}
, defined on the free
\Sigma
-module of
Example
4.6
. One has

\displaystyle[\hat{\tau}_{a},\nabla_{X_{b}}](m^{i}e_{i})=\hat{\tau}_{a}(\nabla_{X_{b}}(m^{i}e_{i}))-\nabla_{X_{b}}(\tau_{a}(m^{i})e_{i})

\displaystyle=\tau_{a}(\sigma_{b}(m^{i}))\tau_{a}(\gamma_{bi}^{k})e_{k}+\tau_{a}(X_{b}(m^{i}))e_{i}-\sigma_{b}(\tau_{a}(m^{i}))\gamma_{bi}^{k}e_{k}-X_{b}(\tau_{a}(m^{i}))e_{i}

\displaystyle=(\tau_{a}(\sigma_{b}(m^{i}))\tau_{a}(\gamma_{bi}^{k})-\sigma_{b}(\tau_{a}(m^{i}))\gamma_{bi}^{k}+\tau_{a}(X_{b}(m^{k}))-X_{b}(\tau_{a}(m^{k})))e_{k},

which does not equal zero naturally without imposing further
conditions. In this example, one may choose the additional condition

[\tau_{a},\sigma_{b}]=0=[\tau_{a},X_{b}]
and set

\tau_{a}(\gamma_{bi}^{k})=\gamma_{bi}^{k},
for all
a,b.
To
stick to the conditions in Proposition
5.3
and
to the additional conditions imposed by the example above, it is
natural to choose
\tau_{a}=\mbox{Id}
to get a curvature that
satisfies the twisted linearity property. In the following Corollary,
the curvature of all left
(\sigma,\mbox{Id})
-connections for the
said
(\sigma,\tau)
-Lie algebra satisfies the twisted linearity property.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
\Sigma=(\mathcal{A},\{X_{a}\}_{a\in I}
be a
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra such
that
(T\Sigma,R)
is a
(\sigma,\tau)
-Lie algebra with bracket

\displaystyle[X_{a},X_{b}]_{R}=X_{a}\circ X_{b}-X_{b}\circ X_{a}=0

such that
X_{a}
is a
(\sigma_{a},\mbox{Id})
-derivation satisfying

(5.5)

\displaystyle[\sigma_{a},\sigma_{b}]=0=[\sigma_{a},X_{b}]

for
a,b\in I.
Then it follows that the curvature of all left

(\sigma,\mbox{Id})
-connections on the
\Sigma
-module

(M,\{(\hat{\sigma}_{a},\mbox{Id})\}_{a\in I})
satisfy the twisted
linearity property

\displaystyle\mbox{Curv}(X_{a},X_{b})(fm)=\sigma_{a}\big(\sigma_{b}(f)\big)\mbox{Curv}(X_{a},X_{b})m.

for
f\in\mathcal{A}
and
m\in M

Let us now discuss the curvature of the unique symmetric

(\sigma,\tau)
-connection of Proposition
4.11
. Recall that the
unique connection
\nabla_{X_{a}}(m)=f_{a}\hat{\delta}_{a}(m)
is
defined on a strongly regular symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra

(\mathcal{A},\{X_{a}\}_{a\in I})
where
X_{a}=f_{a}\delta_{a}
are inner

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations for
a\in I.
Choosing

\hat{\sigma}_{a}=\hat{\tau}_{a}=\mbox{Id}
leaves the connection
trivial. We would like to focus on the special case when

\hat{\sigma}_{a}\neq\hat{\tau}_{a}=\mbox{Id}
and
f_{a}\in\mathbb{C},

for
a\in I.
To this end, let
\Sigma=(\mathcal{A},\{X_{a}\}_{a\in I})
be the
strongly regular symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra where

X_{a}=f_{a}\delta_{a}=f_{a}(\mbox{Id}-\sigma_{a})
for

f_{a}\in\mathbb{C}
and
a\in I.

In this case the vector space
T\Sigma
is spanned by
\{\delta_{a}\}_{a\in I}
.
Define

R:T\Sigma\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}T\Sigma\to T\Sigma\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}T\Sigma
by

\displaystyle R(X_{a}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}X_{b})=R(f_{a}\delta_{a}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}f_{b}\delta_{b})=f_{b}f_{a}(\delta_{b}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\delta_{a})

implying that
R^{2}=\mbox{Id}.
Assuming that

[\sigma_{a},\sigma_{b}]=0,
one has

\displaystyle[X_{a},X_{b}]_{R}=m(X_{a}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}X_{b}-R(X_{a}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}X_{b}))=f_{a}f_{b}(\delta_{a}\circ\delta_{b}-\delta_{b}\circ\delta_{a})=0,

showing that the bracket,
[X_{a},X_{b}]_{R}=0,
on the

(\sigma,\tau)
-Lie algebra
(T\Sigma,R).
Let us compute the curvature
of the unique connection for the
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra described
above. Since
[X_{a},X_{b}]_{R}=0,
then

\nabla_{[X_{a},X_{b}]_{R}]}(m)=0
for
m\in M
and

X_{a},X_{b}\in T\Sigma,
and the curvature of the unique
connection is

\displaystyle\mbox{Curv}(X_{a},X_{b})m=f_{a}f_{b}(\hat{\delta}_{a}\circ\hat{\delta}_{b}-\hat{\delta}_{b}\circ\hat{\delta}_{a})(m).

We remark that the curvature above is not zero in general because

[\sigma_{a},\sigma_{b}]=0
does not imply

[\hat{\sigma}_{a},\hat{\sigma}_{b}]=0.
Given Corollary
5.4
, one checks that the commutation relations (
5.5
) are satisfied on the
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra and since
the bracket
[X_{a},X_{b}]_{R}=0,
one concludes that the unique

(\sigma,\mbox{Id})
-connection satisfies the twisted linearity
condition (
5.4
).

Let us now review the example of a
(\sigma,\tau)
-connection over the
matrix algebra
\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
, constructed in
[

]
, and
relate it to symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-connections.

Consider the matrix algebra
\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
and choose invertible

(N\times N)
-matrices
U_{a},
for

[U_{a},U_{b}]=0
for all
a,b.
Define endomorphisms

\sigma_{a}:\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})\to\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
by

\displaystyle\sigma_{a}(A)=U_{a}AU_{a}^{-1}

and symmetric (inner)
(\sigma_{a},\mbox{Id})
-derivations

X_{a}:\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})\to\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
by

\displaystyle X_{a}(A)=A-U_{a}AU_{a}^{-1}

for
A\in\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
and
a=1,\ldots,n.
As described in

[
AI22
]
,
\Sigma=(\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\{X_{a}\}_{a=1}^{n})

is a
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra and
(T\Sigma,R)
, with

R(X\otimes Y)=Y\otimes X

(\sigma,\tau)
-Lie algebra such that

\displaystyle(X_{a}\circ X_{b})(A)

\displaystyle=X_{a}(X_{b}(A))=X_{a}(A-U_{b}AU_{b}^{-1})

\displaystyle=(A-U_{b}AU_{b}^{-1})-U_{a}(A-U_{b}AU_{b}^{-1})U_{a}^{-1}

\displaystyle=(A-U_{a}AU_{a}^{-1})-U_{b}(A-U_{a}AU_{a}^{-1})U_{b}^{-1}

\displaystyle=X_{b}(A-U_{a}AU_{a}^{-1})=(X_{b}\circ X_{a})(A)

showing that
[X_{a},X_{b}]_{R}=0.

Since
\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
is a free module over itself and generated by the
identity matrix
\mathds{1}
, then

(\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\{(\sigma_{a},\mbox{Id})\}_{a=1}^{n})
is a free

\Sigma
-module. On this
\Sigma
-module, one can construct a

(\sigma,\tau)
-connection by setting

\nabla_{X_{a}}(\mathds{1})=\tilde{\Gamma}_{a}
and

\Gamma=\mathds{1}-\tilde{\Gamma}_{a}
where
\tilde{\Gamma}_{a}\in\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})

for
a=1,\ldots,n.
This connection is explicitly given by

(5.6)

\displaystyle\nabla_{X_{a}}(A)=A-U_{a}AU_{a}^{-1}\Gamma_{a}.

Let us show that the left
(\sigma,\mbox{Id})
-connection
(
5.6
) is a symmetric left

(\sigma,\tau)
-connection. Let us take the same
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra

\Sigma=(\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\{X_{a}\}_{a\in I})
and the same free module
\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C}).

Now define the maps
\hat{\sigma}_{a}:\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})\to\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
by

\hat{\sigma}_{a}(A)=U_{a}AU_{a}^{-1}\Gamma_{a}
for
a=1,\ldots,n.
Then

(\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\{(\hat{\sigma}_{a},\mbox{Id})\}_{a=1}^{n})
is a left

\Sigma
-module. Define the
(\sigma,\mbox{Id})
-connection
(
5.6
), on this new left
\Sigma
-module

(\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\{(\hat{\sigma}_{a},\mbox{Id})\}_{a=1}^{n}).
Since
(
5.6
) is a left

(\sigma,\mbox{Id})
-connection, it suffices to show that it
satisfies (
4.1
). Let

A,B\in\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),
one has

\displaystyle\nabla_{X_{a}}(AB)=AB-U_{a}ABU_{a}^{-1}\Gamma_{a}

\displaystyle=AB-AU_{a}BU_{a}^{-1}\Gamma_{a}+AU_{a}BU_{a}^{-1}\Gamma_{a}-U_{a}AU_{a}^{-1}U_{a}BU_{a}^{-1}\Gamma_{a}

\displaystyle=A(B-U_{a}BU_{a}^{-1}\Gamma_{a})+(A-U_{a}AU_{a}^{-1})U_{a}BU_{a}^{-1}\Gamma_{a}

\displaystyle=A\nabla_{X_{a}}(B)+X_{a}(A)\hat{\sigma}_{a}(B),

showing that (
5.6
) is a symmetric left

(\sigma,\tau)
-connection on
(\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\{\hat{\sigma}_{a},\operatorname{Id}\}_{a=1}^{n})
. It is shown in
[
AI22
]

that the curvature of this connection satisfies the twisted linearity
condition (
5.4
). Since the bracket on

(T\Sigma,R)
is zero, i.e.,
[X_{a},X_{b}]_{R}=0
for

X_{a},X_{b}\in T\Sigma,
the curvature of any

(\sigma,\tau)
-connection on

(\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\{(\hat{\sigma}_{a},\mbox{Id})\}_{a=1}^{n})
is given as

\displaystyle\mbox{Curv}(X_{a},X_{b})=\nabla_{X_{a}}\circ\nabla_{X_{b}}-\nabla_{X_{b}}\circ\nabla_{X_{a}}.

Explicitly, the curvature of the
(\sigma,\mbox{Id})
-connection (
5.6
) is

\displaystyle\mbox{Curv}(X_{a},X_{b})A=U_{a}U_{b}A[U_{b}^{-1}\Gamma_{b},U_{a}^{-1}\Gamma_{a}]

for
A\in\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C}).
We would like to verify that the commutation relations
(
5.3
) is satisfied in this case. Since

\hat{\tau}_{a}=\tau_{a}=\mbox{Id},
the two commutation relations

[\hat{\tau}_{a},\hat{\tau}_{b}]=0
and
[\hat{\tau}_{a},\nabla_{X_{b}}]=0
are
already satisfied. One needs to verify
[\sigma_{a},\sigma_{b}]=0

and
[\sigma_{a},X_{b}]=0.
Let
A\in\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),
one has

\displaystyle[\sigma_{a},\sigma_{b}](A)=\sigma_{a}(\sigma_{b}(A))-\sigma_{b}(\sigma_{a}(A))=\sigma_{a}(U_{b}AU_{b}^{-1})-\sigma_{b}(U_{a}AU_{a}^{-1})

\displaystyle=U_{a}U_{b}AU_{b}^{-1}U_{a}^{-1}-U_{b}U_{a}AU_{a}^{-1}U_{b}^{-1}=0,

and

\displaystyle[\sigma_{a},X_{b}](A)=\sigma_{a}(X_{b}(A))-X_{b}(\sigma_{a}(A))=\sigma_{a}(A-U_{b}AU_{b}^{-1})-X_{b}(U_{a}AU_{a}^{-1})

\displaystyle=U_{a}(A-U_{b}AU_{b}^{-1})U_{a}^{-1}-U_{a}AU_{a}^{-1}+U_{b}(U_{a}AU_{a}^{-1})U_{b}^{-1}

\displaystyle=U_{a}AU_{a}^{-1}-U_{a}U_{b}AU_{b}^{-1}U_{a}^{-1}-U_{a}AU_{a}^{-1}+U_{b}U_{a}AU_{a}^{-1}U_{b}^{-1}=0,

(using that
[U_{a},U_{b}]=0
) showing that the commutation relations
(
5.3
) are satisfied. Therefore, by Corollary

5.4
, we have verified that the left symmetric

(\sigma,\mbox{Id})
-connection (
5.6
) defined
on the
\Sigma
-module

(\mbox{Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\{(\hat{\sigma}_{a},\mbox{Id})\}_{a=1}^{n})
such that the
relations (
5.3
) are satisfied and the
bracket of the
(\sigma,\tau)
-Lie algebra
(T\Sigma,R)
satisfies
(
5.2
) has curvature satisfying the twisted
linearity property (
5.4
).

6.
Commutative
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebras

In this Section, we demonstrate that the notion of regular and
strongly regular pairs on a commutative algebra can be used to
construct modules of
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations on which curvature and
torsion of some connections can be computed. Note that when
\mathcal{A}
is
commutative,
\mbox{Der}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A})
is an
\mathcal{A}
-module and it
consist of symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations. For example, on a unique
factorisation domain
\mathcal{A},
the
\mathcal{A}
-module

\mbox{Der}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A})
is free and of rank one with generator

\frac{\delta}{g}
where
g
is the greatest common divisor of
the image of
\delta

[
HLS06
]
.
It is further shown
in
[
HLS06
]
that every
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation is of
the form
X(f)=f_{0}\frac{\delta(f)}{g}.
Let us recall these results.

Theorem 6.1

(
[
HLS06
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a unique factorization domain and let

\sigma,\tau\in\operatorname{End}(\mathcal{A})
such that
\sigma\neq\tau
. Then

\operatorname{Der}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A})
is a free
\mathcal{A}
-module of rank one generated by

(6.1)

\Delta=\dfrac{(\tau-\sigma)}{g}:x\longmapsto\dfrac{(\tau-\sigma)(x)}{g}

where
g=\mbox{gcd}((\tau-\sigma)(A)).

Note that the
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation
\Delta
in
(
6.1
) is not necessarily an inner

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation when
g
is not invertible in
\mathcal{A}
. Now, let us
try to understand what happens on a commutative algebra that is not a
unique factorization domain. Let us start by showing that in case
there is a common divisor, then a
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation has to have
a similar form as in (
6.1
).

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
X
be a
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation on a commutative algebra
\mathcal{A}.

If there exists
g_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\delta(g_{0})
divides

\delta(f)X(g_{0})
for all
f\in\mathcal{A},
then

\displaystyle X(f)=\dfrac{X(g_{0})\delta(f)}{\delta(g_{0})}.

Proof.

Since
X
is a symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation, Lemma
2.7
implies that

\displaystyle X(f)\delta(g)=\delta(f)X(g)

for
f,g\in\mathcal{A}.
Now, let
g_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
be such that
\delta(g_{0})
divides
X(g_{0})\delta(f)
for all
f\in\mathcal{A},
one has

\displaystyle\dfrac{X(f)\delta(g_{0})}{\delta(g_{0})}=\dfrac{X(g_{0})\delta(f)}{\delta(g_{0})}\quad\Rightarrow\quad X(f)=\dfrac{X(g_{0})\delta(f)}{\delta(g_{0})},

as required.
∎

The existence of a common divisor is in fact a
characterization of inner
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations, as the next
result shows.

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
X
be a
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation on a commutative algebra
\mathcal{A}.

Then
X
is inner if and only if there exist
g_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
such that

\delta(g_{0})
divides
X(g_{0}).

Proof.

Assume that there exist
g_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\delta(g_{0})

divides
X(g_{0}),
then
\delta(g_{0})
divides
X(g_{0})\delta(f)

for all
f\in\mathcal{A}
and by Proposition
6.2
,

\displaystyle X(f)=\dfrac{X(g_{0})\delta(f)}{\delta(g_{0})}.

Since
\delta(g_{0})
divides
X(g_{0})
let
P_{0}
be such that

X(g_{0})=P_{0}\delta(g_{0}),
one has

\displaystyle X(f)=\dfrac{X(g_{0})\delta(f)}{\delta(g_{0})}=\dfrac{P_{0}\delta(g_{0})\delta(f)}{\delta(g_{0})}=P_{0}\delta(f),

showing that
X
is inner. Conversely, assume that
X
is inner. Then, by definition,

\delta(f)
divides
X(f)
for all
f\in\mathcal{A}.
Thus there exist

g_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\delta(g_{0})
divides
X(g_{0}).

∎

Let us look at how Proposition
6.2
applies to

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations on the polynomial algebra
\mathbb{K}[x]

(which is a unique factorization domain, implying that
Theorem
6.1
may also be applied). Assume that

\sigma(p)(x)=p(qx)
and
\tau(p)(x)=p(x)
for

p(x)\in\mathbb{K}[x]
and
q\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}.
One has

\displaystyle\delta(p)=\tau\left(\sum_{i=0}^{N}a_{i}x^{i}\right)-\sigma\left(\sum_{i=0}^{N}a_{i}x^{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-q^{i})a_{i}x^{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\big((1+q+\cdots+q^{k})a_{k+1}x^{k}\big)(1-q)x=r(x)(1-q)x,

implying that
(1-q)x
is the greatest common divisor of

\delta(\mathbb{K}[x]).
From Proposition
6.2
,
setting
g_{0}=x,
one has
\delta(x)=(1-q)x
and it follows
that any
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation
X
on
\mathbb{K}[x]
is of the form

(6.2)

X=p(x)\dfrac{\delta}{(1-q)x}

for
p(x)=\mathbb{K}[x]
determined by
X(x).
From
Corollary
6.3
it follows that the derivation
X

above is inner if an only if
p(x)
is divisible by
x
, that is if

p(x)
has no constant term. In other words, if
p(x)
has a
constant term, then the derivation
X
in (
6.2
) is an
outer derivation. Furthermore, this implies that the Jackson
derivative, defined in (
2.3
), is an outer

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation on
\mathbb{K}[x]
since
p(x)=1
in that
case.

Let us now consider a commutative algebra
\mathcal{A}
(not necessarily a
unique factorization domain) and let
\hat{g}\in\mathcal{A}
be a common
divisor of
\delta(\mathcal{A})
; i.e.
\hat{g}
divides
\delta(f)
for all

f\in\mathcal{A}
. For each
f\in\mathcal{A},
define a linear map

X_{f}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
by

\displaystyle X_{f}(a)=f\frac{\delta(a)}{\hat{g}}

for all
a\in\mathcal{A}.
One has

\displaystyle X_{f}(ab)=f\frac{\delta(ab)}{\hat{g}}=f\frac{\sigma(a)\delta(b)+\delta(a)\tau(b)}{\hat{g}}=\sigma(a)f\frac{\delta(b)}{\hat{g}}+f\frac{\delta(a)}{\hat{g}}\tau(b)

\displaystyle=\sigma(a)X_{f}(b)+X_{f}(a)\tau(b),

showing that
X_{f}
is a
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation. Since
\mathcal{A}
is
commutative,
X_{f}
is symmetric. In analogy with Theorem
6.1
, let us show that if the common divisor actually lies in the
image of
\delta
and
(\sigma,\tau)
is a regular pair, then every

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivation is of this form.

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
(\sigma,\tau)
be a regular pair of endomorphisms on a commutative
algebra
\mathcal{A}
. If
\hat{g}
is a common divisor of
\operatorname{Im}(\delta)
such
that
\hat{g}\in\operatorname{Im}(\delta)
then
\mbox{Der}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A})
is a
free module of rank one with basis
\delta/\hat{g}
.

Proof.

Let
g_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\delta(g_{0})=\hat{g}
. Lemma
2.7
implies that

\displaystyle X(f)\hat{g}=\delta(f)X(g_{0})\quad\Rightarrow\quad X(f)=X(g_{0})\frac{\delta(f)}{\hat{g}}

since
\hat{g}
is a common divisor of the image of
\delta
, which
shows that
\delta/\hat{g}
generates

\mbox{Der}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A})
. Now, assuming
f_{0}\delta(f)/\hat{g}=0

for all
f\in\mathcal{A}
implies that
f_{0}\delta(f)=0
for all
f\in\mathcal{A}

which gives
f_{0}=0
since
(\sigma,\tau)
is assumed to be a regular
pair. Hence,
\delta/\hat{g}
is a basis for
\mbox{Der}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A})
.
∎

The set

M_{\hat{g}}=\{f\frac{\delta}{\hat{g}}:f\in\mathcal{A}\}
is a collection
of symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations of
\mathcal{A}
and the pair

(\mathcal{A},M_{\hat{g}})
is a symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra. Let

\Sigma_{\hat{g}}=(\mathcal{A},M_{\hat{g}})
and let
T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}
be the
tangent space generated by
M_{\hat{g}}
.
It is easy to see that
T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}=M_{\hat{g}}
since

\displaystyle X_{f}+X_{g}=(f+g)\frac{\delta}{\hat{g}}=X_{f+g}.

Moreover, the vector space
T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}
can be endowed with an
A
-module structure by defining

f\cdot X_{g}=X_{fg}

for
f,g\in\mathcal{A}.
Indeed, if
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathcal{A}
and
X_{g}\in T\Sigma_{\hat{g}},
then one has

\displaystyle(f_{1}+f_{2})\cdot X_{g}=X_{(f_{1}+f_{2})g}=X_{f_{1}g+f_{2}g}=X_{f_{1}g}+X_{f_{2}g}=f_{1}\cdot X_{g}+f_{2}\cdot X_{g}

and

\displaystyle f_{1}\cdot(f_{2}\cdot X_{g})=f_{1}\cdot X_{f_{2}g}=X_{f_{1}(f_{2}g)}=X_{(f_{1}f_{2})g}=(f_{1}f_{2})\cdot X_{g},

as well as

\displaystyle f\cdot(X_{g_{1}}+X_{g_{2}})=f\cdot X_{g_{1}+g_{2}}=X_{fg_{1}+fg_{2}}=f\cdot X_{g_{1}}+f\cdot X_{g_{2}}.

for
g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathcal{A}
.

Thus,
T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}
is an
\mathcal{A}
-module and it consists of

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations of
\mathcal{A}
and it is
a submodule of the module of

(\sigma,\tau)
-derivations
\mbox{Der}_{(\sigma,\tau)}(\mathcal{A})
.
The next result shows that if
(\sigma,\tau)
is a regular pair, then

\delta/\hat{g}
is a basis for
T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}
.

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
(\sigma,\tau)
be a regular pair of endomorphisms on a commutative
algebra
\mathcal{A}
and let
\Sigma_{\hat{g}}=(\mathcal{A},M_{\hat{g}})
where

\hat{g}
is a common divisor of
\delta(\mathcal{A}).
Then

\frac{\delta}{\hat{g}}
is a basis of
T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}.

Proof.

We would like to show that if
f\frac{\delta}{\hat{g}}=0
then

f=0
. Since
(\sigma,\tau)
is a regular pair, there exist

a_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\delta(a_{0})
is not a zero divisor. Let
us first show that if
\delta(a_{0})
is not a zero divisor then

\frac{\delta(a_{0})}{\hat{g}}
is not a zero divisor. For the sake
of argument, assume that
\frac{\delta(a_{0})}{\hat{g}}
is a zero
divisor. Then there exist
f_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
such that

f_{0}\frac{\delta(a_{0})}{\hat{g}}=0
, which implies

(6.3)

\displaystyle\hat{g}f_{0}\frac{\delta(a_{0})}{\hat{g}}=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad f_{0}\delta(a_{0})=0

contradicting the fact that
\delta(a_{0})
is not a zero
divisor. Hence
\frac{\delta(a_{0})}{\hat{g}}
is not a zero
divisor. It follows that

\displaystyle f\frac{\delta(a)}{\hat{g}}=0,\quad\forall\,a\in\mathcal{A}\quad\Rightarrow\quad f\frac{\delta(a_{0})}{\hat{g}}=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad f=0,

showing that
\frac{\delta}{\hat{g}}
is indeed a basis for
T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}.

∎

Next, let us explore the possibility of defining a
(\sigma,\tau)
-Lie
algebra structure on
T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}
.

Proposition 6.6
.

Let
\sigma\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{A})
and assume that
(\sigma,\operatorname{Id})
is a regular
pair of endomorphisms. If

R:T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}\to T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}
is defined
by

\displaystyle R(X_{f}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}X_{g})=X_{\sigma(g)}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}X_{\sigma^{-1}(f)}

for
X_{f},X_{g}\in T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}
then

(T\Sigma_{\hat{g}},R)
is a
(\sigma,\tau)
-Lie algebra.

Proof.

Let

m:T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}\to T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}
denote the composition

m(X_{f}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}X_{g})=X_{f}\circ X_{g}.
To prove that

(T\Sigma_{\hat{g}},R)
is a
(\sigma,\tau)
-Lie algebra, one needs to
show that

R^{2}=\mbox{Id}_{T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}}

and

(6.4)

\displaystyle m(X_{f}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}X_{g}-R(X_{f}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}X_{g}))\in T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}.

Let us first prove that

R^{2}=\mbox{Id}_{T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}}.

For
X_{f},X_{g}\in T\Sigma_{\hat{g}},
one has

\displaystyle R^{2}(X_{f}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}X_{g})=R(X_{\sigma(g)}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}X_{\sigma^{-1}(f)})=X_{\sigma(\sigma^{-1}(f))}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}X_{\sigma^{-1}(\sigma(g))}=X_{f}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}X_{g}.

To prove (
6.4
), note that

\displaystyle m(X_{f}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}X_{g})(a)=X_{f}\circ X_{g}(a)=fX_{\mathds{1}}(gX_{\mathds{1}}(a))

for
a\in\mathcal{A}
. One has

\displaystyle m(X_{f}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}X_{g}-R(X_{f}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}X_{g}))(a)=X_{f}\circ X_{g}(a)-X_{\sigma(g)}\circ X_{\sigma^{-1}(f)}(a)

\displaystyle=fX_{\mathds{1}}(gX_{\mathds{1}}(a))-\sigma(g)X_{\mathds{1}}(\sigma^{-1}(f)X_{\mathds{1}}(a))=f\sigma(g)X_{\mathds{1}}(X_{\mathds{1}}(a))+fX_{\mathds{1}}(g)X_{\mathds{1}}(a)

\displaystyle-\sigma(g)\sigma(\sigma^{-1}(f))X_{\mathds{1}}(X_{\mathds{1}}(a))-\sigma(g)X_{\mathds{1}}(\sigma^{-1}(f))X_{\mathds{1}}(a)

\displaystyle=fX_{\mathds{1}}(g)X_{\mathds{1}}(a)-\sigma(g)X_{\mathds{1}}(\sigma^{-1}(f))X_{\mathds{1}}(a)

\displaystyle=(X_{f}(g)-X_{\sigma(g)}(\sigma^{-1}(f)))X_{\mathds{1}}(a)=X_{X_{f}(g)-X_{\sigma(g)}(\sigma^{-1}(f))}(a)

implying that

m(X_{f}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}X_{g}-R(X_{f}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}X_{g}))\in T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}.
Thus,
(T\Sigma_{\hat{g}},R)
is a

(\sigma,\tau)
-Lie algebra.
∎

The bracket on the
(\sigma,\tau)
-Lie algebra
(T\Sigma_{\hat{g}},R)
in
the proposition above is given by

\displaystyle[X_{f},X_{g}]_{R}=\big(X_{f}(g)-X_{\sigma(g)}(\sigma^{-1}(f))\big)X_{\mathds{1}}

for
X_{f},X_{g}\in T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}
.

For arbitrary
\sigma,\tau\in\operatorname{End}(\mathcal{A})
,
T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}
can be
given the structure of a
\Sigma_{\hat{g}}
-module. Namely, defining

\hat{\sigma},\hat{\tau}:T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}\to T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}
by

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}(X_{f})=X_{\sigma(f)}\quad\text{ and }\quad\hat{\tau}(X_{f})=X_{\tau(f)}

one finds that

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}(fX_{g})=\hat{\sigma}(X_{fg})=X_{\sigma(f)\sigma(g)}=\sigma(f)X_{\sigma(g)}=\sigma(f)\hat{\sigma}(X_{g})

showing (together with obvious linearity properties and a similar
computation for
\hat{\tau}
) that
(T\Sigma,\{\hat{\sigma},\hat{\tau}\})
is a

\Sigma_{\hat{g}}
-module. Let us now study the curvature of a
connection on the
\Sigma_{\hat{g}}
-module

(T\Sigma,\{\hat{\sigma},\hat{\tau}\})
where
(\sigma,\tau)
is assumed to be a
regular pair of endomorphisms, implying that
T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}
is a
free module. Define

\nabla:T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}\times T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}\to T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}
by

(6.5)

\nabla_{X_{f}}(X_{g})=fX_{\delta(g)}=f\big(\hat{\tau}(X_{g})-\hat{\sigma}(X_{g})\big)

for
X_{f},X_{g}\in T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}
, and note that for
f,g,h\in\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\nabla_{X_{f}}(gX_{h})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{X_{f}}(X_{gh})=fX_{\delta(gh)}=fX_{\sigma(g)\delta(h)+\delta(g)\tau(h)}

\displaystyle=f\sigma(g)X_{\delta(h)}+f\delta(g)X_{\tau(h)}=\sigma(g)\nabla_{X_{f}}(X_{h})+X_{f}(g)\hat{\tau}(X_{h})

\displaystyle\nabla_{X_{f}}(gX_{h})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{X_{f}}(X_{gh})=fX_{\delta(gh)}=fX_{\tau(g)\delta(h)+\delta(g)\sigma(h)}

\displaystyle=f\tau(g)X_{\delta(h)}+f\delta(g)X_{\sigma(h)}=\tau(g)\nabla_{X_{f}}(X_{h})+X_{f}(g)\hat{\sigma}(X_{h})

showing that
\nabla
is a symmetric
(\sigma,\tau)
-connection. Moreover,
it holds that

\displaystyle\nabla_{fX_{g}}(X_{h})=\nabla_{X_{fg}}(X_{h})=fgX_{\delta(h)}=f\nabla_{X_{g}}(X_{h})

for
f,g,h\in\mathcal{A}
. Note that if
(\sigma,\tau)
is a strongly regular pair,

Proposition 6.7
.

Assume that
\Sigma_{\hat{g}}=(\mathcal{A},M_{\hat{g}})
is a regular
(\sigma,\tau)
-algebra such that

(T\Sigma_{\hat{g}},R)
is a
(\sigma,\mbox{Id})
-Lie algebra with

R
given by

\displaystyle R(X_{f}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}X_{g})=X_{\sigma(g)}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}X_{\sigma^{-1}(f)}

for
X_{f},X_{g}\in T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}
and let

(M,\{(\hat{\sigma},\hat{\tau})\})
be a (left)

\Sigma_{\hat{g}}
-module. If

\nabla:T\Sigma_{\hat{g}}\times M\to M
is a left

(\sigma,\mbox{Id})
-connection such that

\nabla_{fX_{g}}(m)=f\nabla_{X_{g}}(m)
for all
f,g\in\mathcal{A},

then the curvature of
\nabla
is zero.

Proof.

For
f,g\in\mathcal{A}
one computes

\displaystyle\operatorname{Curv}(X_{f},X_{g})(m)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{X_{f}}(\nabla_{X_{g}}(m))-\nabla_{X_{\sigma(g)}}(\nabla_{X_{\sigma^{-1}(f)}}(m))-\nabla_{[X_{f},X_{g}]_{R}}(m)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{X_{f}}(g\nabla_{X_{\mathds{1}}}(m))-\nabla_{X_{\sigma(g)}}(\sigma^{-1}(f)\nabla_{X_{\mathds{1}}}(m))-\nabla_{[X_{f},X_{g}]_{R}}(m)

\displaystyle=\sigma(g)\nabla_{X_{f}}(\nabla_{X_{\mathds{1}}}(m))+X_{f}(g)\nabla_{X_{1}}(m)-\sigma(\sigma^{-1}(f))\nabla_{X_{\sigma(g)}}(\nabla_{X_{\mathds{1}}}(m))

\displaystyle\qquad-X_{\sigma(g)}(\sigma^{-1}(f))\nabla_{X_{\mathds{1}}}(m)-\nabla_{[X_{f},X_{g}]_{R}}(m)

\displaystyle=\sigma(g)f\nabla_{X_{\mathds{1}}}(\nabla_{X_{\mathds{1}}}(m))+fX_{\mathds{1}}(g)\nabla_{X_{1}}(m)-f\sigma(g)\nabla_{X_{\mathds{1}}}(\nabla_{X_{\mathds{1}}}(m))

\displaystyle\qquad-\sigma(g)X_{\mathds{1}}(\sigma^{-1}(f))\nabla_{X_{\mathds{1}}}(m)-\nabla_{[X_{f},X_{g}]_{R}}(m)

\displaystyle=(fX_{\mathds{1}}(g)-\sigma(g)X_{\mathds{1}}(\sigma^{-1}(f)))\nabla_{X_{\mathds{1}}}(m)-\nabla_{[X_{f},X_{g}]_{R}}(m)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{[X_{f},X_{g}]_{R}}(m)-\nabla_{[X_{f},X_{g}]_{R}}(m)=0,

showing that the curvature is indeed zero.
