Spectral asymptotics for the fourth-order operator with periodic coefficients
Dmitry M. Polyakov

Abstract
. We consider the self-adjoint fourth-order operator with real 1 1 -periodic coefficients on the unit interval. The spectrum of this operator is discrete. We determine the high energy asymptotics for its eigenvalues.



.

1.
Introduction and main result

We consider the self-adjoint fourth-order operator
H
acting in
the Hilbert space
L^{2}(0,1)
and given by

Hy=y^{(4)}+(py^{\prime})^{\prime}+qy,\quad y^{\prime}(0)=y^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)+p(0)y^{\prime}(0)=y(1)=y^{\prime\prime}(1)=0,

where
p
and
q
are real
1
-periodic coefficients,
p
,
q\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
,

\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Z}
. This operator is determined on the domain

\displaystyle\mathrm{Dom}(H)=\{y\in L^{2}(0,1):y^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime},\,

\displaystyle y^{\prime\prime\prime}+py^{\prime}\in L^{1}(0,1),y^{(4)}+(py^{\prime})^{\prime}+qy\in L^{2}(0,1),

\displaystyle y^{\prime}(0)=y^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)+p(0)y^{\prime}(0)=y(1)=y^{\prime\prime}(1)=0\}.

The results for the operator
H
are used in analysis of thin liquid polymer films
of nanometer thickness. These thin films are often found to destabilize and dry from an
underlying solid substrate due to intermolecular forces between the liquid film and
the substrate. The late phases of the ensuing complex dewetting process
are found to be configurations of droplets. The droplets tend to assume their own slow
dynamics via mass exchange through an even thinner film connecting the droplets.
Using the scale separation between the height of the thin film and the extent
of the evolving patterns, the free boundary problem for the Navier-Stokes equations can
be reduced to the equation for the profile of the film (see
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
).
The evolution of the profile of the film is described by the corresponding
one-dimensional thin film equation

\partial_{t}y=-\partial_{x}\Big(y^{3}\partial_{x}\big(\partial_{xx}y-\Pi(y)\big)\Big),

considered on the interval
[0,1]
with the boundary conditions

\partial_{xxx}y=0,\qquad\partial_{x}y=0,\qquad\text{at}\qquad x=0,\qquad x=1.

Here
\Pi
is potential function. The linearization of this equation
(see
[
3
]
and
[
4
]
) leads to the eigenvalue problem for the operator
H
.

The main goal of the present paper is to determine high energy eigenvalue asymptotics
for fourth-order operator
H
on the unit interval.

The spectrum
\sigma(H)
of the operator
H
is pure discrete
(see
[
22
, Ch.I.2, I.4]
). We consider the differential equation

(1.1)

y^{(4)}+(py^{\prime})^{\prime}+qy=\lambda y,\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}.

Introduce the fundamental solutions
\varphi_{j}
,
j=1,2,3,4
, of this equation
satisfying the conditions
\varphi^{(k-1)}_{j}(0,\lambda)=\delta_{jk}
,
k=1,2,3
,

(\varphi_{j}^{\prime\prime\prime}+p\varphi_{j}^{\prime})(0,\lambda)=\delta_{j4}
, where
\delta_{jk}
is the Kronecker symbol.
Each
\varphi_{j}(x,\cdot)
,
x\in[0,1]
, is an entire function.

The spectrum
\sigma(H)
consists of real eigenvalues and satisfies

\sigma(H)=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:D(\lambda)=0\},

where
D
is an entire function given by

(1.2)

D(\lambda)=-\det\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{1}(1,\lambda)&\varphi_{3}(1,\lambda)\\
\varphi_{1}^{\prime\prime}(1,\lambda)&\varphi_{3}^{\prime\prime}(1,\lambda)\\
\end{pmatrix},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}.

Consider the unperturbed operator
H_{0}y=y^{(4)}
.
Therefore, the function
D
has the form
D_{0}(\lambda)=-\cos z\cos(iz)

(see also (
2.11
)), where

z=\lambda^{1/4},\quad\arg z\in\Big(-\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{\pi}{4}\Big],\quad\text{as}\quad\arg\lambda\in(-\pi,\pi].

The eigenvalues of
H_{0}
are the zeros of
D_{0}
and have the form

\mu_{n}^{0}=\big(\frac{\pi}{2}+\pi n\big)^{4},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}.

Note that the eigenvalues
\mu_{n}^{0}
are simple.

We denote the eigenvalues of the perturbed problem by
\mu_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

(see details in Section
3.1
). Our main result is devoted to the high energy asymptotics
of these eigenvalues. We use the Birkhoff method (see
[
31
]
,

[
32
]
and
[
22
]
) in the matrix form
[
13
]
,
[
33
]

for the proof of our result. We give the asymptotics
for the coefficients
p
,
q\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
in terms of Fourier coefficients
of
p
. In order to prove convergence of the series in the trace formula, we obtain these
asymptotics for smooth coefficients
p
and
q
.

Introduce the Fourier coefficients

\displaystyle f_{0}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\,dx,\quad\widehat{f}_{n}=\int_{0}^{1}f(x)e^{-i\pi(2n+1)x}\,dx,

\displaystyle\widehat{f}_{cn}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\cos\pi(2n+1)x\,dx,\quad\widehat{f}_{sn}=\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\sin\pi(2n+1)x\,dx,\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

We formulate our main result.

Theorem 1
.

Let
p
,
q\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
and let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be large enough. Then the
eigenvalues
\mu_{n}
are real and has algebraic multiplicity one. Moreover,

(1.3)

\mu_{n}=\big(\frac{\pi}{2}+\pi n\big)^{4}+\big(\frac{\pi}{2}+\pi n\big)^{2}(\widehat{p}_{cn}-p_{0})+\mathcal{O}(n),

as
n\to+\infty
. If, in addition,
p^{\prime\prime\prime}
,
q^{\prime}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
, then

(1.4)

\displaystyle\mu_{n}

\displaystyle=\big(\frac{\pi}{2}+\pi n\big)^{4}+\big(\frac{\pi}{2}+\pi n\big)^{2}\big(\widehat{p}_{cn}-p_{0}\big)+\frac{p_{0}^{2}-\|p\|^{2}}{8}+q_{0}+\widehat{q}_{cn}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-2}),

as
n\to+\infty
.

In summary, the results of our present paper are an essential step to obtain
the trace formula for operator
H
and the first step for the solution of inverse spectral
problem for this operator.

2
contains some preliminary

The eigenvalue asymptotics for the case

,

, for the case

,

2.
Fundamental solutions

2.1.
Fundamental matrix

Instead of the fundamental solutions
\varphi_{j}
,
j=1,2,3,4
, considered in previous section,
we introduce the fundamental solutions
\phi_{j}
,
j=1,2,3,4
, whose asymptotics are well
controlled. In order to describe these fundamental solutions, we need additional notation.

Recall that
z=\lambda^{1/4}
,

,
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, where

\overline{\mathcal{Z}}=\bigg\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathrm{arg}\,z\in\Big(-\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{\pi}{4}\Big]\bigg\},\quad\mathcal{Z}=\bigg\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathrm{arg}\,z\in\Big(-\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{\pi}{4}\Big)\bigg\}.

If
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
, then
z\in\mathcal{Z}_{+}
, where

\mathcal{Z}_{+}=\bigg\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathrm{arg}\,z\in\Big(0,\frac{\pi}{4}\Big)\bigg\}.

Introduce the numbers
\omega_{1}=-\omega_{4}=i

Note that unperturbed equation (
1.1
) with

\phi_{j}^{0}(x,z)=e^{izx\omega_{j}},\quad j=1,2,3,4.

Consider the perturbed equation (
1.1
). Let

Then equation (

,

,

\displaystyle\phi_{j}(x,z)

\displaystyle=\phi_{j}^{0}(x,z)(1+\mathcal{O}(z^{-1})),\quad\phi_{j}^{\prime}(x,z)=(\phi_{j}^{0})^{\prime}(x,z)(1+\mathcal{O}(z^{-1})),

\displaystyle\phi_{j}^{\prime\prime}(x,z)

\displaystyle=(\phi_{j}^{0})^{\prime\prime}(x,z)(1+\mathcal{O}(z^{-1})),

\displaystyle(\phi_{j}^{\prime\prime\prime}+p\phi_{j}^{\prime})(x,z)

Here and below the asymptotics for

A^{\prime}=\mathcal{P}A,\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{P}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&0\\
0&0&1&0\\

Now we rewrite the determinant
D
, defined by (
1.2
), in terms of the fundamental

\phi

(2.4)

\phi(z)=\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{1}^{\prime}(0,z)&\phi_{2}^{\prime}(0,z)&\phi_{3}^{\prime}(0,z)&\phi_{4}^{\prime}(0,z)\\

\phi_{1}^{\prime\prime}(1,z)&\phi_{2}^{\prime\prime}(1,z)&\phi_{3}^{\prime\prime}(1,z)&\phi_{4}^{\prime\prime}(1,z)\end{pmatrix}.

The function

(2.5)

D(\lambda)=\frac{\det\phi(z)}{\det A(0,z)},\quad\lambda=z^{4}.

The proof of this formula repeats the arguments from

. Therefore, the function

is entire and the

onto

is characteristic function of the

. But this function is not an entire function of the variable

\det\phi

The matrix-valued function
\phi=\phi_{0}

(2.9)

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(z)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}(\phi_{1}^{0})^{\prime}(0,z)&(\phi_{2}^{0})^{\prime}(0,z)&(\phi_{3}^{0})^{\prime}(0,z)&(\phi_{4}^{0})^{\prime}(0,z)\\

Then the identities (

) and (

Now we transform equation (

Let

2.2.
The Birkhoff method

The fundamental matrix contains both exponentially increasing, bounded, and decreasing
entries at high energy. Therefore, asymptotic analysis of this matrix is rather difficult.
In various combinations of entries of the fundamental matrix, the contribution of bounded
and decreasing entries completely disappears against the background of
the contribution of increasing ones. Birkhoff’s method yields a factorization
of the fundamental matrix, where the exponential entries are separated into a
separate diagonal matrix. This allows good control over their
contribution when calculating the asymptotics.

Now we write the basic concept for the matrix form of the Birkhoff method
(see
[
13
]
,
[
33
]
). We consider the differential equation

(2.46)

\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}-iz\Theta\mathcal{Y}=\frac{1}{z^{m}}\Phi\mathcal{Y}

on the interval
[0,1]
with the unknown
4\times 4
-matrix-valued function

\mathcal{Y}
, where
z\in\mathcal{Z}_{+}(r)
,
r>0
is large enough,
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
and the
4\times 4
-matrix-valued functions

\Theta(x,z)
and
\Phi(x,z)
satisfy the following conditions:

1)
\Theta=\mathrm{diag}\,(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2},\Theta_{3},\Theta_{4})
is diagonal;

2)
\Phi(\cdot,z)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
and
\Theta(\cdot,z)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})

for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}_{+}(r)
;

3) for a. e.
x\in[0,1]
the functions
\Phi(x,z)
and
\Theta(x,z)
are analytic in

\mathcal{Z}_{+}(r)
and

(2.47)

\Phi(x,z)=F(x)+\mathcal{O}(z^{-1}),\qquad\qquad\Theta(x,z)=\mathcal{T}+\mathcal{O}(z^{-1}),

as
|z|\to+\infty
,
z\in\mathcal{Z}_{+}
, uniformly in
x\in[0,1]
, where
the matrix
\mathcal{T}
is given by (
2.13
) and
the
4\times 4
-matrix-valued function

;

4)

is off-diagonal, that is,

,

.

Note that equations (
2.14
), (
2.24
), (
2.31
), and (
2.40
)
have the form (
2.46
).

In fact, using the Birkhoff method, we rewrite the differential equation (
2.46
) in the form
of a specific Fredholm integral equation with a small kernel at high energy.

Let
K
be an integral operator in the space
C[0,1]
of
4\times 4
matrix-valued
functions given by

(K\mathcal{X})_{lj}(x,z)=\int_{0}^{1}K_{lj}(x,s,z)(\Phi\mathcal{X})_{lj}(s,z)\,ds,\quad l,j=1,2,3,4,\quad(x,z)\in[0,1]\times\mathcal{Z}_{+}(r),

for all

(2.48)

K_{lj}(x,s,z)=\begin{cases}e^{iz\int_{s}^{x}(\Theta_{l}(u,z)-\Theta_{j}(u,z))\,du}\,\chi(x-s),\quad l<j,\\
-e^{iz\int_{s}^{x}(\Theta_{l}(u,z)-\Theta_{j}(u,z))\,du}\,\chi(s-x),\quad l\geqslant j,\end{cases}\quad\chi(s)=\begin{cases}1,\quad s\geqslant 0,\\
0,\quad s<0.\end{cases}

Then for

the integral operator

is a contraction.
Therefore, the matrix-valued integral equation

(2.49)

\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{I}_{4}+\frac{1}{z^{m}}K\mathcal{X},\qquad m\in\mathbb{N},

has a unique solution
\mathcal{X}(\cdot,z)\in C[0,1]
. Moreover,

and each matrix-valued function

\mathcal{X}(x,\cdot)
,

, is analytic on
\mathcal{Z}_{+}(r)
and
satisfies the asymptotics

(2.50)

\mathcal{X}(x,z)=\mathbb{I}_{4}+\frac{1}{z^{m}}(K\mathbb{I}_{4})(x,z)+\mathcal{O}(z^{-2m}),

as
|z|\to+\infty
,

, uniformly in

, where

(K\mathbb{I}_{4})_{lj}(x,z)=\int_{0}^{1}K_{lj}(x,s,z)\Phi_{lj}(s,z)\,ds,\qquad l,j=1,2,3,4.

The integral equation (

are equivalent in the following sense.
