Taking the temperature of a pure quantum state
Mark T. Mitchison, Archak Purkayastha, Marlon Brenes, Alessandro Silva, John Goold

Abstract
Temperature is a deceptively simple concept that still raises deep questions at the forefront of quantum physics research. The observation of thermalisation in completely isolated quantum systems, such as cold-atom quantum simulators, implies that a temperature can be assigned even to individual, pure quantum states. Here, we propose a scheme to measure the temperature of such pure states through quantum interference. Our proposal involves interferometry of an auxiliary qubit probe, which is prepared in a superposition state and subsequently decoheres due to weak coupling with a closed, thermalised many-body system. Using only a few basic assumptions about chaotic quantum systems — namely, the eigenstate thermalisation hypothesis and the emergence of hydrodynamics at long times — we show that the qubit undergoes pure exponential decoherence at a rate that depends on the temperature of its surroundings. We verify our predictions by numerical experiments on a quantum spin chain that thermalises after absorbing energy from a periodic drive. Our work provides a general method to measure the temperature of isolated, strongly interacting systems under minimal assumptions.



Advances in our understanding of thermodynamic concepts have always been inspired by the technologies of the time, from steam engines in the nineteenth century to ultra-cold atom simulators in the twenty-first. Irrespective of the historical era, the importance of measuring temperature cannot be overstated. In 1798, the American military man and scientist, Count Rumford, noticed that he could generate heat from friction while boring cannons in the arsenal of the Bavarian army he was tasked with reorganising. Rumford reported the systematic temperature increase of the water in which the cannon barrels were immersed
Thompson 1798
, challenging the prevailing caloric theory of heat and inspiring James Joule to perform the decisive experiments that established energy conservation as the first law of a new thermodynamic theory. In his famous paddle-bucket experiment, Joule measured the mechanical equivalent of heat by observing the temperature change induced by stirring fluid in a thermally isolated container
Joule 1850
. Here, we show that recasting Joule’s experiment as a fully quantum-mechanical process leads to a fundamentally new scheme to measure the temperature of an isolated quantum many-body system. Our proposal relies on entangling the system with an auxiliary qubit that undergoes decoherence with a temperature-dependent rate. This thermometer scale is defined entirely through quantum interference and allows the measurement of temperature for generic systems in pure quantum states.

In the last two decades, experimental progress in cold-atom physics has enabled coherent quantum dynamics to persist over extraordinary timescales: long enough to observe isolated many-body systems thermalise without coupling to any external bath
Trotzky et al. 2012
;
Clos et al. 2016
;
Kaufman et al. 2016
;
Bordia et al. 2017
;
Tang et al. 2018
. The emergence of thermodynamics in this context is elegantly explained by the eigenstate thermalisation hypothesis (ETH)
Deutsch 1991
;
Srednicki 1994
;
Rigol et al. 2008
. The ETH posits that, in a sufficiently complex and chaotic system, each energy eigenstate encodes the properties of the equilibrium ensemble. As a result, local observables in a far-from-equilibrium scenario eventually thermalise under unitary evolution
D’Alessio et al. 2016
. The final temperature is set by the energy density of the initial condition, which may be effectively a pure quantum state. Thermal fluctuations thus arise locally because of quantum entanglement between different parts of the system
Goldstein et al. 2006
;
Popescu et al. 2006
rather than by any classical statistical mixing. This begs the question: can the temperature of a pure state also be
measured
in a completely quantum-mechanical way?

Our pure-state thermometry scheme, depicted in Fig.
1
, draws inspiration from Joule’s pioneering experiment, for which thermal isolation was vital. We consider the extreme case of an isolated quantum system such as an ultra-cold atomic gas. Work is performed by changing some external constraint, thus driving the system out of equilibrium in analogy to Joule’s paddles. The driving force is then removed and the system relaxes under unitary evolution. Local observables thermalise to a temperature governed by the work performed, i.e. the mechanical equivalent of heat. Joule’s apparatus included an
in situ
thermometer to measure the temperature change of the insulated fluid. In our setup, this role is played by an auxiliary qubit that becomes entangled with the many-body system. Assuming only the ETH and the equations of diffusive hydrodynamics, we show that the qubit undergoes pure exponential decoherence at a temperature-dependent rate that can be interferometrically measured
Cetina et al. 2015
;
Cetina et al. 2016
;
Skou et al. 2021
, providing a uniquely quantum thermometer for pure states.

Our work contributes to a growing body of literature seeking to establish the fundamental quantum limits of thermometry
Mehboudi et al. 2019
. The traditional approach — used in Joule’s measurements, for example — is to let the thermometer exchange energy with its surroundings and wait for equilibration. Unfortunately, this becomes challenging to implement at low temperature, where a precise thermometer needs small energy scales and correspondingly long thermalisation times
Correa et al. 2015
. These drawbacks can be avoided by inferring temperature from the non-equilibrium dynamics of a probe, assuming a reliable model of the process is available
Bruderer and Jaksch 2006
;
Stace 2010
;
Sabín et al. 2014
;
Hangleiter et al. 2015
;
Jevtic et al. 2015
;
Johnson et al. 2016
;
Razavian et al. 2019
;
Mitchison et al. 2020
;
Bouton et al. 2020
;
Adam et al. 2021
. In particular, Refs.
Johnson et al. 2016
;
Razavian et al. 2019
;
Mitchison et al. 2020
have shown that pure decoherence dynamics can encode temperature with a precision that is completely independent of the probe’s energy. However, these proposals require the thermal system to be described by the canonical ensemble, as appropriate for an open system coupled to a heat reservoir. In contrast, our protocol offers a general solution to the problem of thermometry for isolated quantum systems, without the inherent limitations of small thermal probes that equilibrate with the system.

After the drive is switched off, the system evolves autonomously and local observables relax to equilibrium [Fig.
2
(c)], exhibiting small fluctuations around a value that is close to the prediction of the microcanonical ensemble [Fig.
2
(d)]. This ensemble is characterised by a single parameter: the average energy,
\bar{E}
, with the corresponding inverse temperature
T^{-1}\equiv\beta=\beta(\bar{E})
given by the fundamental definition
\beta(E)=\mathrm{d}\mathcal{S}/\mathrm{d}E
, where
\mathcal{S}(E)
is the microcanonical entropy. Similar thermal behaviour is observed in correlation functions like
C(t^{\prime},t)=\braket{\hat{A}(t^{\prime})\hat{A}(t)}-\braket{\hat{A}(t^{\prime})}\braket{\hat{A}(t)}
, with
\hat{A}
a local observable, which become approximately stationary at long times, i.e.
C(t+\tau,t)\approx C(\tau)
[Fig.
2
(e)]. Conventionally, one writes
C(\tau)
in terms of the symmetrised noise function
S(\tau)=\,\mathrm{Re}\,[C(\tau)]
and the dissipative response function
\chi^{\prime\prime}(\tau)=\mathrm{i}\,\mathrm{Im}\,[C(\tau)]
. After relaxation, their Fourier transforms are related by the fluctation-dissipation theorem (FDT),
\tilde{S}(\omega)=\coth(\beta\omega/2)\tilde{\chi}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)
, as expected in thermal equilibrium [Fig.
2
(f)].

The thermalisation of these “paddle-bucket” preparations is striking in light of the highly non-equilibrium energy distribution displayed in Fig.
2
(b). Nevertheless, this behaviour is completely generic and fully explained by the ETH, which can be formulated as an ansatz for the matrix elements of an arbitrary local observable,
\hat{A}
, in the energy eigenbasis
Srednicki 1999
, i.e.
A_{mn}=\langle E_{m}|\hat{A}|E_{n}\rangle
, where
\hat{H}\ket{E_{n}}=E_{n}\ket{E_{n}}
. The ansatz reads as

A_{mn}=\begin{cases}\hskip 5.69054ptA(E_{n})+\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{D}^{-1/2}),&m=n,\\
\hskip 5.69054pt\mathrm{e}^{-\mathcal{S}(E_{mn})/2}f(E_{mn},\omega_{mn})R_{mn}+\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{D}^{-1}),&m\neq n,\end{cases}

(2)

where
A(E_{n})
and
f(E_{mn},\omega_{mn})
are smooth functions of their arguments,
E_{mn}=\tfrac{1}{2}(E_{m}+E_{n})
and
\omega_{mn}=E_{m}-E_{n}
, while
R_{mn}
is a Hermitian matrix of random numbers with zero mean and unit variance, and
\mathcal{D}
is the Hilbert-space dimension. See Fig.
3
for an example and Ref.
SM
for further details. As is well known
D’Alessio et al. 2016
, the ETH (
2
) implies that any highly excited state with sub-extensive energy fluctuations will thermalise under unitary dynamics. More precisely, the expectation value of a local observable converges to its time average
\overline{\braket{\hat{A}}}=\sum_{n}|\braket{E_{n}|\psi}|^{2}A_{nn}=A(\bar{E})+\mathcal{O}(\Delta E^{2}/\bar{E}^{2}_{*})
, with
A(\bar{E})
equal to the microcanonical average at inverse temperature
\beta(\bar{E})
, while the spectral function
f(\bar{E},\omega)
determines the noise and response functions (up to sub-extensive corrections) as
D’Alessio et al. 2016
;
Brenes et al. 2020a

\displaystyle\tilde{S}(\omega)=2\pi\cosh(\beta\omega/2)|f(\bar{E},\omega)|^{2},

(3)

\displaystyle\tilde{\chi}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)=2\pi\sinh(\beta\omega/2)|f(\bar{E},\omega)|^{2},

(4)

immediately implying the FDT. Although these features of the ETH have long been understood, the low-frequency behaviour of the spectral function has only recently been identified as a sensitive indicator of quantum many-body chaos
Brenes et al. 2020b
;
Pandey et al. 2020
. For a generic observable in a non-integrable system,
f(E,0)
is non-zero and may vary significantly with temperature [Fig.
3
(b)]. This observation forms the basis of our thermometry scheme.

Thermometry protocol.—
Our thermometer comprises a qubit with energy eigenstates
\ket{\uparrow}
and
\ket{\downarrow}
, coupled to the system by an interaction of the form
\hat{H}_{\rm int}=\ket{\uparrow}\bra{\uparrow}\otimes g\hat{A}
for some local observable
\hat{A}
and coupling constant
g
. This kind of interaction — which can be engineered, for example, using Feshbach resonances in ultracold gases
Cetina et al. 2016
— conserves the qubit’s energy and ensures that it does not participate in the dynamics while in its ground state
\ket{\downarrow}
. Suppose that at time
t_{0}
, the thermal system of interest is in the pure state
\ket{\psi(t_{0})}=\ket{\psi_{0}}
. The protocol begins by exciting the qubit into a superposition
\ket{+}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{\uparrow}+\ket{\downarrow})
with a
\pi/2-
pulse, preparing the joint product state
\ket{\Psi(t_{0})}=\ket{+}\ket{\psi_{0}}
. In a frame rotating at the qubit precession frequency, the Schrödinger evolution is then
\ket{\Psi(t)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{H}(t-t_{0})}\ket{\downarrow}\ket{\psi_{0}}+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\hat{H}+g\hat{A})(t-t_{0})}\ket{\uparrow}\ket{\psi_{0}}\right)
. Entanglement develops between the probe and the system, leading to a loss of distinguishability quantified by the fidelity between many-body system states

|\varv(t)|^{2}=|\braket{\psi_{0}|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\hat{H}(t-t_{0})}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\hat{H}+g\hat{A})(t-t_{0})}|\psi_{0}}|^{2}.

(5)

The resulting decrease in interference contrast is reflected in the off-diagonal elements of the qubit density matrix,
\hat{\rho}_{\rm q}(t)=\mathrm{Tr}_{\rm sys}\,\ket{\Psi(t)}\bra{\Psi(t)}
, which decay in time according to
\braket{\downarrow\rvert\hat{\rho}_{\rm q}(t)\lvert\uparrow}=\tfrac{1}{2}\varv(t)
. This decoherence is finally probed by applying a second
\pi/2
-pulse with a phase
\theta
relative to the first one, then measuring the excited-state probability of the qubit,
P_{\uparrow}=\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\,\mathrm{Re}\,[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\varv(t)]).
The time-dependent overlap
\varv(t)
is thus reconstructed by varying
\theta
.

Precision at weak coupling.—
To assess the temperature dependence of the interference contrast, we focus on the weak-coupling regime and approximate the fidelity (
5
) by a cumulant expansion to second order in
g

SM
. We obtain
|\varv(t)|^{2}=\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma(t)}
, where

\displaystyle\Gamma(t)=4g^{2}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\,\tilde{S}(\omega)\frac{\sin^{2}[\omega(t-t_{0})/2]}{\omega^{2}}.

(6)

At weak coupling, the largest effects are seen for
t-t_{0}\gg\tau_{c}
, where
\tau_{c}
is the characteristic timescale for the correlation function
C(\tau)
to decay to zero. The integral in Eq. (
6
) is then dominated by the contribution near
\omega=0
, which implies pure exponential decoherence,
|\varv(t)|^{2}\sim\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma(t-t_{0})}
, with an asymptotic decay rate
\gamma=g^{2}\tilde{S}(0)\propto|f(\bar{E},0)|^{2}
. We numerically confirm this behaviour in Fig.
4
(a), which shows the fidelity for a probe coupled to a spin chain heated by the procedure of Fig.
2
. Even for moderate coupling strengths, we observe near-perfect exponential decay with a temperature-dependent rate in close agreement with the weak-coupling prediction. The decoherence is associated with a growth in the entanglement entropy
\mathsf{S}[\hat{\rho}_{\rm q}]=-\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\rho}_{\rm q}\ln\hat{\rho}_{\rm q}]
, which saturates to the temperature-independent value
\mathsf{S}[\hat{\rho}_{\rm q}]\to\ln 2
characterising a maximally entangled state [Fig.
4
(b)]. This distinguishes our non-equilibrium protocol from a thermalisation process. In Fig.
4
(c), the temperature dependence of the decoherence rate is analysed in more detail. We find that
\gamma
depends almost linearly on energy density [Fig.
4
(c) inset], which translates into a non-linear variation with temperature [Fig.
4
(c) main panel] that is greatest at low temperatures.

We quantify the temperature information that can be extracted from our protocol using the quantum Fisher information (QFI). Consider a temperature estimate constructed from
M
independent measurements in a given basis,
\mu
, on identical qubit preparations. For large
M
, the statistical error of any unbiased estimate is asymptotically bounded by
\Delta T^{2}\geq 1/M\mathcal{F}^{\mu}_{T}\geq 1/M\mathcal{F}_{T}^{Q}
. Here,
\mathcal{F}^{\mu}_{T}
is the Fisher information for the chosen basis while the QFI,
\mathcal{F}_{T}^{Q}=\max_{\mu}\mathcal{F}_{T}^{\mu}
, is the maximum over all measurements and thus describes the ultimate uncertainty limit imposed by quantum mechanics
Braunstein and Caves 1994
. The temperature can be inferred from the exponential decay of
|\varv(t)|
by measuring in the eigenbasis of
\hat{\rho}_{\rm q}(t)
, i.e. by applying a final
\pi/2
-pulse with phase
\theta=-\arg\varv(t)

SM
. Fig.
4
(d) shows the corresponding Fisher information,
\mathcal{F}^{\parallel}_{T}
, in the weak-coupling limit. Since
\mathcal{F}_{T}^{\parallel}\approx\mathcal{F}^{Q}_{T}
, we conclude that the decoherence rate captures almost all temperature information available from the probe in this example. For instance, we obtain the value
T^{2}\mathcal{F}^{\parallel}_{T}\approx 0.2
at temperature
T=5J
, implying that
M=500
measurements could suffice to achieve a precision of
\Delta T/T\gtrsim 10\%
. Note that a single ultra-cold gas sample may host thousands of independent impurities
Cetina et al. 2016
. We emphasise that the achievable precision is independent of the qubit’s energy gap, unlike a thermalised probe whose QFI depends exponentially on this gap at low temperature
Correa et al. 2015
.

Hydrodynamic decoherence.—
Our results show that the temperature of an isolated system can be measured using the most primitive features of quantum dynamics: namely, unitarily evolving wavefunctions and entanglement between subsystems. The scale of our thermometer is defined not through the energetic fluctuations of some statistical mixture, but by the rate of entanglement growth in a quantum decoherence process
1
1

1

;

and can be interferometrically measured

;
Pichler et al. 2016
.
. While this rate should generally increase with temperature, the precise dependence is system- and observable-specific. Nevertheless, since a generic system should display hydrodynamic behaviour at long times

, we can obtain a general form for
\gamma(T)
assuming that the probe couples to diffusive modes of a conserved density. In
d=3
spatial dimensions, we obtain

\gamma=\frac{2\bar{g}^{2}\chi_{0}T}{D},

(7)

where

is the diffusion coefficient,
\chi_{0}
is the thermodynamic susceptibility to long-wavelength density perturbations and

is a renormalised coupling that depends only on the probe’s spatial profile. According to Eq. (

), the qubit’s decoherence rate provides an ideal, linear thermometer scale within any temperature range where

\chi_{0}
are approximately constant, and allows for accurate thermometry in general whenever

and
\chi_{0}
are known as a function of temperature.

In low-dimensional systems — such as our spin-chain example — similar hydrodynamic arguments predict non-exponential decoherence at intermediate times,

\Gamma(t)\sim\gamma t
, when

. The asymptotic decoherence rate
\gamma
depends on temperature as in Eq. (

), but both
\gamma
and
\tau_{c}
grow with the system size for
d<3

SM
. However,
\tau_{c}
is too small to clearly distinguish the crossover at system sizes accessible in our simulations, where only the long-time exponential decay is observed. This interesting competition of timescales calls for further research to characterise how Markovian dynamics

Conclusion.—
Accurate,
in situ
thermometry of isolated quantum systems is an outstanding problem in cold-atom physics, where strong, short-ranged correlations confound destructive global measurement techniques such as time-of-flight imaging. Conversely, a small quantum probe facilitates local, minimally destructive temperature measurements, in principle
Hohmann et al. 2016
;
Bouton et al. 2020
. Our proposal to infer temperature from decoherence dynamics does not require thermalisation of the qubit nor fine-tuning of its energy levels, and is applicable to generic many-body systems in arbitrary states with sub-extensive energy fluctuations. This opens a pathway for the toolbox of quantum-enhanced thermometry
Mehboudi et al. 2019
to probe the ultimate limit of an isolated system in a pure quantum state.

is a Gaussian profile centred on site
j_{0}
, where
j_{0}=L/2
if
L/2
is odd and
j_{0}=L/2+1
if
L/2
is even. To improve numerical efficiency, we set
u_{j}=0
on all sites where
\mathrm{e}^{-(j-j_{0})^{2}}<10^{-3}
, and then normalise as
\sum_{j}u_{j}=1
. This generates an observable with support restricted to five sites of the lattice.

It is important to remark that, in order to obtain the function
f(E,\omega)
from the coarse-grained average of the off-diagonal matrix elements of a local operator
Mondaini and Rigol 2017
;
Khatami et al. 2013
;
Brenes et al. 2020a
;
Brenes et al. 2020c
;
Brenes et al. 2020b
, all symmetries of the model should be resolved. Resolving these symmetries amounts to restricting the block-diagonal Hamiltonian to a single symmetry sub-sector of states corresponding to a given eigenvalue of the symmetry generator (performing a separate calculation for each block, if more than one is required). If the operators admix the symmetry sub-sectors
LeBlond and Rigol 2020
, resolving the corresponding symmetry is not required to obtain
f(E,\omega)
from the coarse-grained average of the off-diagonal matrix elements.

The function
f(E,\omega)
obtained from this procedure is shown in Fig.
3
(b). In contrast with the open-boundary chain, the model with periodic boundary conditions is translation-invariant. Instead of resolving this symmetry, we break it by augmenting the magnetic field acting on site
j=1
by a small amount
\delta h=0.1h
. Even with the addition of this small perturbation, in the zero-magnetisation sector, an underlying spatial reflection symmetry remains. This symmetry is broken by the operator
\hat{A}
when
L/2
is odd, as in Fig.
3
(b).

S1.2
Eigenstate thermalisation

The ETH posits that the matrix elements of an observable
\hat{A}
in the energy eigenbasis are of the form

A_{mn}=\begin{cases}\hskip 5.69054ptA(E_{n})+\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{D}^{-1/2}),&m=n,\\
\hskip 5.69054pt\mathrm{e}^{-\mathcal{S}(E_{mn})/2}f(E_{mn},\omega_{mn})R_{mn}+\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{D}^{-1}),&m\neq n.\end{cases}

(S2)

The meaning of each term on the right-hand side is illustrated in Fig.
3
of the main text and explained as follows. The diagonal matrix elements (
m=n
) are given by a smooth function of energy,
A(E)
, up to fluctuations that scale inversely with the square root of the Hilbert-space dimension,
\mathcal{D}
[Fig.
3
(a)]. Therefore, all energy eigenstates near a given energy
E
yield the same expectation value
\langle\hat{A}\rangle=A(E)
in the thermodynamic limit. This identifies
A(E)
as the microcanonical average of
\hat{A}
at inverse temperature
\beta(E)
. In Fig.
3
(a), this is indicated by the black line, which shows a running average of the diagonal matrix elements
A_{nn}
within microcanonical windows of width
\delta\epsilon=0.02
for a system size of
L=20
.

Meanwhile, the off-diagonal matrix elements (
m\neq n
) are exponentially small and erratically distributed [Fig.
3
(b) inset], as described in Eq. (
S2
) by a Hermitian matrix
R_{mn}
of random numbers with zero mean and unit variance. Underlying this distribution is a smooth spectral function
f(E,\omega)
of the mean energy,
E_{mn}=\tfrac{1}{2}(E_{m}+E_{n})
and transition frequency,
\omega_{mn}=E_{m}-E_{n}
, which is revealed in the variance of the matrix elements within small energy and frequency windows [Fig.
3
(b) main panel]. Specifically, the data in Fig.
3
(b) are generated by finding all off-diagonal matrix elements at energy
E_{mn}
consistent with a given temperature,
T=[\beta(E_{mn})]^{-1}
, then computing the variance,
{\rm var}[A_{mn}]=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathcal{S}(E_{mn})}|f(E_{mn},\omega_{mn})|^{2}
, within small frequency windows
\delta\omega\sim 0.2J
.

Aside from energy eigenstates, the ETH also describes the ergodic dynamics of non-equilibrium pure states seen in Fig.
2
. Using Eq. (
S2
), the time average of an observable is found to be
\overline{\braket{\hat{A}}}=\sum_{n}|\braket{E_{n}|\psi}|^{2}A_{nn}=A(\bar{E})+\mathcal{O}(\Delta E^{2}/\bar{E}^{2}_{*})
, while temporal fluctuations away from this value are proportional to the off-diagonal elements
A_{mn}
and thus are exponentially suppressed
D’Alessio et al. 2016
. The same applies to two-point correlation functions
C(t+\tau,t)
, which for large
t
tend to their equilibrium, time-homogeneous value
C(t+\tau,t)\approx C(\tau)
. The latter is determined by the noise and response functions given in Eqs. (
3
) and (
4
) of the main text.

S1.3
Dynamical evolution

To compute dynamical quantities we solve the Schrödinger equation

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}\ket{\psi(t)}=\hat{H}\ket{\psi(t)},

(S3)

to obtain the state vector
\ket{\psi(t)}
, from which any observable
\braket{\hat{A}(t)}=\braket{\psi(t)|\hat{A}|\psi(t)}
can be calculated. The decoherence function itself is obtained from the overlap
\varv(t)=\braket{\psi(t)|\psi^{\prime}(t)}
, where the states
\ket{\psi(t)}
and
\ket{\psi^{\prime}(t)}
are propagated under Eq. (
S3
) with Hamiltonians
\hat{H}
and
\hat{H}^{\prime}=\hat{H}+g\hat{A}
, respectively. We integrate Eq. (
S3
) using a standard fourth-order Runge-Kutta
Steinigeweg et al. 2015
algorithm with time step
J\delta t=0.01
. This choice yields an excellent approximation to unitary evolution, e.g. after an evolution time
Jt=50
the normalisation
\braket{\psi(t)|\psi(t)}
drops by less than 0.1% at temperature
T=20J
and by less than 1% at
T=5J
. For very long evolutions, such as those required to compute
\varv(t)
at weak coupling, we normalise the state after each time step to enforce unitarity.

To compute two-point correlation functions, we follow the procedure described, for example, in Ref.
Steinigeweg et al. 2015
. For
t^{\prime}>t
we can write

\displaystyle\braket{\hat{A}(t^{\prime})\hat{A}(t)}

\displaystyle=\braket{\psi(t_{0})|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\hat{H}(t^{\prime}-t_{0})}\hat{A}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{H}(t^{\prime}-t)}\hat{A}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{H}(t-t_{0})}|\psi(t_{0})}

\displaystyle=\braket{\psi(t^{\prime})|\hat{A}|\phi(t^{\prime})},

(S4)

where
\ket{\psi(t_{0})}
and
t_{0}
are the (arbitrary) initial state and time, and
\ket{\phi(t^{\prime})}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{H}(t^{\prime}-t)}\hat{A}\ket{\psi(t)}
is obtained by propagating the Schrödinger equation (
S3
) for a time interval
t^{\prime}-t
starting from the initial condition
\hat{A}\ket{\psi(t)}
. The case
t^{\prime}<t
is obtained by complex conjugation.

\tau_{*}

\tilde{\chi}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)/\tilde{S}(\omega)
over the frequency range
|\omega|\leq 2J

to an excellent approximation for the temperatures of interest.

S1.4
Microcanonical predictions

Predictions of the microcanonical ensemble are evaluated using the kernel polynomial method (KPM)
Weiße et al. 2006
;
Yang et al. 2020
. The three quantities that we compute in this way are the density of states,
\Omega(E)
, the microcanonical expectation value,
A(E)
, and the local density of states,
|\psi(E)|^{2}
, given respectively by

\displaystyle\Omega(E)=\sum_{n}\delta(E-E_{n}),

(S5)

\displaystyle A(E)=\frac{1}{\Omega(E)}\sum_{n}A_{nn}\delta(E-E_{n}),

(S6)

\displaystyle|\psi(E)|^{2}=\sum_{n}|\braket{E_{n}|\psi}|^{2}\delta(E-E_{n}).

(S7)

The microcanonical entropy and temperature are extracted from the density of states via Boltzmann’s relation
\mathcal{S}(E)=\ln W(E)
, where
W(E)=\Omega(E)\mathrm{d}E
corresponds to the number of microstates in a small energy interval
\mathrm{d}E
.

The kernel polynomial method works by expanding the above functions in the basis of orthogonal Chebyshev polynomials,
T_{n}(E)
. Since these polynomials are defined only on the interval
E\in[-1,1]
, we first rescale the Hamiltonian spectrum to lie within this interval. Then, following the standard procedure detailed in Ref.
Weiße et al. 2006
, we approximate a continuous function of energy
\Xi(E)
by evaluating a finite number of Chebyshev moments,
\mu_{m}=\int\mathrm{d}E\,\Xi(E)T_{m}(E)
. The function is then reconstructed as the truncated basis expansion

\Xi(E)\approx\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{1-E^{2}}}\left[g_{0}\mu_{0}+2\sum_{m=1}^{M_{\rm Cheb}}g_{m}\mu_{m}T_{m}(E)\right],

(S8)

where
g_{m}
are coefficients that decay with increasing
m
, which smooth the high-frequency oscillations (Gibbs phenomenon) that would otherwise result from truncating the expansion at finite order,
M_{\rm Cheb}
. We use the values of
g_{m}
corresponding to the Jackson kernel, which is known to be optimal and effectively broadens the delta functions entering Eqs. (
S5
)–(
S7
) to Gaussians of width
\lesssim\pi/M_{\rm Cheb}
; see Ref.
Weiße et al. 2006
for details.

For example, the density of states is evaluated from the moments

\mu_{m}=\mathrm{Tr}[T_{m}(\hat{H})]\approx\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\braket{r|T_{m}(\hat{H})|r}.

(S9)

The last step approximates the trace using a set of
R
random vectors, where each component of the vector
\ket{r}
is chosen independently from a Gaussian distribution. In a large Hilbert space only a few random vectors, say
R\lesssim 10
for spin chain of length
L=26
, are typically needed to achieve excellent convergence within the relevant energy range for thermalisation. In the form (
S9
), the moments can be computed recursively using the defining three-term relation of the Chebyshev polynomials,

\displaystyle T_{m+1}(\hat{H})=2\hat{H}T_{m}(\hat{H})-T_{m-1}(\hat{H}),

(S10)

for
m\geq 1
, with
T_{0}(\hat{H})=1
and
T_{1}(\hat{H})=\hat{H}
. Acting this expression on a vector as
\ket{v_{m}}=T_{m}(\hat{H})\ket{r}
generates the recursion relation
\ket{v_{m+1}}=2\hat{H}\ket{v_{m}}-\ket{v_{m-1}}
. Each iteration thus requires a single matrix-vector multiplication, making the KPM a very efficient method for large, sparse matrices such as Eq. (
S1
).

Figure S2:
Density of states for a spin chain of
L=20
sites. The bars show a normalised histogram of the energy levels obtained from exact diagonalisation. The blue solid line is the density of states obtained from KPM with
M_{\rm Cheb}=100
moments averaged over
R=150
random vectors. Dividing
\Omega(E)
by the Hilbert-space dimension ensures normalisation:
\int\mathrm{d}E\,\Omega(E)/\mathcal{D}=1
.

In all our calculations of the inverse temperature and microcanonical averages, we take
M_{\rm Cheb}=100
moments in total. This is sufficient to obtain an accurate, smooth approximation, as shown in Fig.
S2
for the density of states, for example. Larger values of
M_{\rm Cheb}
can lead to spurious oscillations due to the underlying discreteness of the finite-dimensional Hilbert space, especially at low temperatures. For the local density of states shown in Fig.
2
(b), we take
M_{\rm Cheb}=250
in order to better resolve the sharp features.

S2
Weak-coupling and long-time limit of the decoherence function

In this section we derive the weak-coupling expansion of the decoherence function,
\varv(t)
, and discuss its convergence to the long-time limit. To simplify the notation we set the initial time of the protocol to be
t_{0}=0
in this section. Our starting point is the general expression for the decoherence function [c.f. Eq. (
5
)]

\varv(t)=\Braket{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\hat{H}t}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\hat{H}+g\hat{A})t}}=\Braket{{\rm T}\exp\left[-\mathrm{i}g\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\hat{A}(t^{\prime})\right]},

(S11)

where we recognise the definition of the time-ordered exponential, with
\hat{A}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\hat{H}t}\hat{A}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{H}t}
the Heisenberg-picture operator with respect to the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
. Here, and in the following, expectation values are taken with respect to the initial state of the many-body system,
\braket{\bullet}=\mathrm{Tr}[\bullet\hat{\rho}]
, e.g.
\hat{\rho}=\ket{\psi_{0}}\bra{\psi_{0}}
in the case of a pure initial state as considered in the main text. Note, however, that our framework can be equally applied to any mixed state for which the one- and two-point functions of the operator
\hat{A}
are approximately thermal.

We proceed by expanding Eq. (
S11
) into time-ordered cumulants
Kubo 1962
and neglecting terms of order
\mathcal{O}(g^{3})
, which yields

\displaystyle-\ln\varv(t)

\displaystyle\approx\mathrm{i}g\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,\braket{\hat{A}(t^{\prime})}+g^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime\prime}\,C(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})

(S12)

\displaystyle\approx\mathrm{i}gt\overline{\braket{\hat{A}}}

\displaystyle\quad+g^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime\prime}\,\left[S(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})+\chi^{\prime\prime}(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})\right],

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\Phi(t)+\frac{1}{2}\Gamma(t)

(S13)

where the first line is the second-order cumulant expansion, while the second line follows by assuming the noise statistics are approximately stationary. On the final line, we moved to the Fourier domain, e.g.
S(\tau)=\int\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega\tau}\tilde{S}(\omega)/2\pi
, and defined

\displaystyle\Gamma(t)=4g^{2}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\,\tilde{S}(\omega)\frac{\sin^{2}(\omega t/2)}{\omega^{2}},

(S14)

\displaystyle\Phi(t)=gt\overline{\braket{\hat{A}}}+g^{2}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\,\tilde{\chi}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)\frac{\sin(\omega t)-\omega t}{\omega^{2}}.

(S15)

In order to analyse convergence to the long-time limit, it is convenient to remain in the time domain. Consider the second-order contribution to Eq. (
S12
) in the stationary approximation

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime\prime}\,C(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,(t-\tau)C(\tau),

(S16)

where we introduced the variables
\tau=t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime}
and
\bar{t}=(t^{\prime}+t^{\prime\prime})/2
and performed the trivial integral over
\bar{t}\in[\tau/2,t-\tau/2]
. Let
\tau_{c}
denote the correlation time after which
C(\tau)
has decayed to zero. Assuming that the improper integral
\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\tau C(\tau)
exists, it gives a sub-leading (i.e. constant) correction for large
t
. For
t\gg\tau_{c}
we can therefore neglect this term and take the upper integration limit to infinity, obtaining

\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,(t-\tau)C(\tau)\approx t\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,C(\tau)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\tilde{S}(0)-\mathrm{i}\chi_{\hat{A}}\right],

(S17)

where
\chi_{\hat{A}}=\int\mathrm{d}\omega\,\tilde{\chi}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)/\pi\omega
is the thermodynamic susceptibility corresponding to the observable
\hat{A}
. This result also follows from taking
t\to\infty
directly in Eqs. (
S14
) and (
S15
). Eq. (
S17
) implies the emergence of pure exponential decay for times
t\gg\tau_{c}
, with the rate
\gamma=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathrm{d}\Gamma/\mathrm{d}t=g^{2}\tilde{S}(0)
. Therefore, self-consistency of the exponential approximation requires that the decoherence function evolves slowly so that
\gamma\tau_{c}\ll 1
. This condition is well satisfied by our examples, where the correlation function fully decays after a time
J\tau_{c}\lesssim 10
[Fig.
S1
(a)], while the characteristic timescale for
\varv(t)
is an order of magnitude longer [Fig.
4
(a)].

Note that the above arguments break down whenever
C(\tau)\sim\tau^{-p}
with
p\leq 1
for large
\tau
. In particular, diffusion in the thermodynamic limit implies that
S(\tau)=\,\mathrm{Re}\,[C(\tau)]\sim(D\tau)^{-d/2}
in
d
spatial dimensions with diffusion coefficient
D
, as discussed in Sec.
S4
. For
d=1
, therefore, Eq. (
S16
) is dominated by the second term in parentheses on the right-hand side, which implies the long-time behaviour
\Gamma(t)\sim t^{3/2}
. For
d=2
, we obtain the asymptotic scaling
\Gamma(t)\sim t\ln(Dt/\ell^{2})
, where
\ell
is the length scale characterising the probe [see Eq. (
S4
)]. In a finite system, however, diffusive dynamics persists up to the Thouless time,
t_{\rm T}\sim L^{2}/D
(
L
is the linear dimension of the system, defined in Sec.
S4
), after which the correlation function
C(\tau)
drops to zero (up to small fluctuations, see Fig.
S1
). One therefore expects Eq. (
S17
) to hold for low-dimensional diffusive systems after a correlation time scaling as
\tau_{c}\sim t_{\rm T}
.

S3
Quantum Fisher information

In this section we discuss the quantum Fisher information and its contributions from the norm and phase of the decoherence function. Let us first briefly recap the meaning of the Fisher information in the context of parameter estimation. Suppose that the qubit probe is in the state
\hat{\rho}_{\rm q}(T)
, which depends on the temperature
T
. We consider a measurement described by a set of positive operators,
\{\hat{\Pi}(\xi)\}
, such that
\int\mathrm{d}\xi\,\hat{\Pi}(\xi)=1
, where the possible measurement outcomes are labelled by the continuous index
\xi
without loss of generality. A temperature estimate
T_{\rm est}(\bm{\xi})
is constructed from the outcomes
\bm{\xi}=\{\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{M}\}
of a large number,
M
, of identical measurements on independent preparations of the state
\hat{\rho}_{\rm q}(T)
. We consider (asymptotically) unbiased estimators such as maximum likelihood estimation, which satisfy
\mathbb{E}[T_{\rm est}]=T
, where

\mathbb{E}[T_{\rm est}]=\int\mathrm{d}\xi_{1}\cdots\int\mathrm{d}\xi_{M}p(\xi_{1}|T)\cdots p(\xi_{M}|T)T_{\rm est}(\bm{\xi}),

(S18)

while
p(\xi|T)=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\Pi}(\xi)\hat{\rho}_{\rm q}(T)]
denotes the probability of obtaining outcome
\xi
in a single measurement. The expected error in the temperature estimate is thus
\Delta T^{2}=\mathbb{E}[(T_{\rm est}-T)^{2}]
. This obeys the Cramér-Rao bound
Jaynes 2003
,
\Delta T^{2}\geq 1/M\mathcal{F}_{T}
, where the Fisher information is given by

\mathcal{F}_{T}=\int\mathrm{d}\xi\,p(\xi|T)\left(\frac{\partial\ln p(\xi|T)}{\partial T}\right)^{2},

(S19)

which measures the sensitivity of the distribution to changes in the parameter
T
. The Fisher information depends on the choice of measurement basis, and is upper-bounded by the quantum Fisher information
Braunstein and Caves 1994
(QFI),
\mathcal{F}_{T}\leq\mathcal{F}_{T}^{Q}
. The bound is saturated by the measurement of a specific observable: the symmetric logarithmic derivative (SLD),
\hat{\Lambda}_{T}
.

For a pure dephasing evolution, the qubit state is of the form
\hat{\rho}_{\rm q}=\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\bm{v}\cdot\bm{\hat{\sigma}}),
where
\bm{\hat{\sigma}}=(\hat{\sigma}_{x},\hat{\sigma}_{y},\hat{\sigma}_{z})^{\sf T}
is a vector of Pauli operators and
\bm{v}=(\,\mathrm{Re}\,[\varv],-\,\mathrm{Im}\,[\varv],0)^{\sf T}
is the Bloch vector. Parameterising the decoherence function as
\varv=|\varv|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\phi}
, the QFI takes the form
Mitchison et al. 2020

\mathcal{F}_{T}^{Q}=\frac{1}{1-|\varv|^{2}}\left(\frac{\partial|\varv|}{\partial T}\right)^{2}+|\varv|^{2}\left(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial T}\right)^{2}=\mathcal{F}^{\parallel}_{T}+\mathcal{F}^{\perp}_{T}.

(S20)

These two terms respectively correspond to the Fisher information for measurements of
\hat{\sigma}_{\parallel}=\cos(\phi)\hat{\sigma}_{x}-\sin(\phi)\hat{\sigma}_{y}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{\perp}=\cos(\phi)\hat{\sigma}_{y}+\sin(\phi)\hat{\sigma}_{x}
, i.e. the bases parallel and perpendicular to
\bm{v}
in the equatorial plane of the Bloch sphere [see Fig. 1 of the main text]. Up to irrelevant additive and multiplicative factors, the SLD is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\Lambda}_{T}\propto\cos(\varphi)\hat{\sigma}_{\parallel}+\sin(\varphi)\hat{\sigma}_{\perp},

(S21)

\displaystyle\tan(\varphi)=\frac{|\varv|(1-|\varv|)^{2}\partial_{T}\phi}{\partial_{T}|\varv|}.

Figure S3:

g=0.2J

, in the weak-coupling limit, as extracted from a dynamical calculation of

.

The main text considers thermometry using measurements of the norm
|\varv(t)|
, neglecting the phase
\phi
. In Fig.

(a) we plot the phase at the same coupling
g=0.2J
as considered in the main text. The results are compared to the asymptotic weak-coupling approximation,

in Fig.

\mathcal{O}(g^{2})
. The difficulty of predicting the phase accurately with perturbative approximations is well known, e.g. from studies of the Fermi edge singularity where the cumulant expansion for
\phi
diverges already at second order
Mitchison et al. 2020
. In Fig.

S20
) is dominated by the first contribution,
\mathcal{F}^{\parallel}_{T}
, as shown in Fig.

\hat{\sigma}_{\parallel}
could be enacted by tuning the phase of the final
\pi/2

\theta

To obtain a smooth prediction for the Fisher information in the weak-coupling limit, as shown in Fig.

.

S4
Hydrodynamic response function

In this section, we discuss the form of the response function that follows from diffusive hydrodynamics, and the resulting temperature dependence of the decoherence rate for large systems. We closely follow the classic analysis of Kadanoff & Martin
Kadanoff and Martin 1963
;
Forster 1990
, generalising it to a large but finite system in
d
spatial dimensions. We consider a translation-invariant system of volume
L^{d}
under periodic boundary conditions, described by the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
. Let
\hat{A}=\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}\,u(\mathbf{r})\hat{n}(\mathbf{r})
be a local observable written in terms of the density,
\hat{n}(\mathbf{r})
, of a globally conserved charge,
\hat{N}=\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}\,\hat{n}(\mathbf{r})
, such that
[\hat{H},\hat{N}]=0
. The slowly varying function
u(\mathbf{r})
represents the spatial profile of the probe, which we normalise as
\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}\,u(\mathbf{r})=1
without loss of generality.

Translation invariance allows us to separately analyse each Fourier component of the density,
\hat{n}_{\mathbf{k}}=\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\hat{n}(\mathbf{r})
, where the discrete wavevector
\mathbf{k}
has components
k_{j}=2\pi n_{j}/L
with
n_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
j=1,\ldots d
. For any state that is invariant under space and time translations we can then define the density response function via

\frac{1}{2L^{d}}\braket{[\hat{n}_{\mathbf{k}}(t+\tau),\hat{n}_{-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(t)]}=\delta_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\chi^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau),

(S22)

with
\tilde{\chi}^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbf{k}}(\omega)
the Fourier transform of
\chi^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)
. Note that
\chi^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbf{k}=0}(\tau)=0
identically as a consequence of the conservation of total charge,
\hat{N}=\hat{n}_{\mathbf{k}=0}
. The probe observable reads
\hat{A}=L^{-d}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}c_{-\mathbf{k}}\hat{n}_{\mathbf{k}}
, with the corresponding response function
\tilde{\chi}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)=L^{-d}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\neq 0}|u_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}\tilde{\chi}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)
, where
u_{\mathbf{k}}
is the Fourier transform of
u(\mathbf{r})
. For example, if
u(\mathbf{r})\propto e^{-r^{2}/2\ell^{2}}
is a Gaussian of width
\ell
,
u_{\mathbf{k}}=\mathrm{e}^{-\ell^{2}k^{2}/2}
cuts off wavevectors
k\gg\ell^{-1}
; other smooth profiles show similar behaviour. Therefore, so long as
u(\mathbf{r})
is slowly varying (i.e.
\ell
is large), only long-wavelength diffusive modes contribute significantly to
\tilde{\chi}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)
.

To find the response function associated with diffusion, a small density modulation is introduced by adiabatically switching on a weak force,
F(\mathbf{r})
, and then suddenly removing it at
t=0
. This is modelled by the potential

\hat{V}(t)=-\Theta(-t)\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon t}\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}\,F(\mathbf{r})\hat{n}(\mathbf{r}),

(S23)

where
\Theta(t)
is the unit step function and
\varepsilon\to 0
is a small, non-negative convergence parameter. The resulting density deviation is then computed from linear-response theory, starting from an equilibrium state at the initial time
t_{0}\to-\infty
. For
t<0
, the system adiabatically follows the slow perturbation, thus remaining in thermal equilibrium,
\langle\hat{n}_{\mathbf{k}}(t\leq 0)\rangle=\langle\hat{n}_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle_{\rm eq}
. Meanwhile, linear-response theory yields
\langle\hat{n}_{\mathbf{k}}(t\leq 0)\rangle=\chi_{\mathbf{k}}F_{\mathbf{k}}
, where

\chi_{\mathbf{k}}=\int\mathrm{d}\omega\,\frac{\tilde{\chi}^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbf{k}}(\omega)}{\pi\omega}=\left.\frac{\partial\langle\hat{n}_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle_{\rm eq}}{\partial F_{\mathbf{k}}}\right\rvert_{F=0},

(S24)

which is identified as the thermodynamic susceptibility. For
t>0
, we obtain

\braket{\hat{n}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)}=\int\mathrm{d}\omega\,\frac{\tilde{\chi}^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbf{k}}(\omega)F_{\mathbf{k}}}{\pi\omega}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t},

(S25)

which, assuming a slowly varying
F(\mathbf{r})
, should evolve according to the diffusion equation
(\partial_{t}+Dk^{2})\braket{\hat{n}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)}=0
. This is readily solved by
\braket{\hat{n}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)}=\chi_{\mathbf{k}}F_{\mathbf{k}}\mathrm{e}^{-Dk^{2}t}
, given the initial condition at
t=0
. Comparing these two solutions for
\braket{\hat{n}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)}
in the Laplace domain, we get

\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega^{\prime}}{\mathrm{i}\pi}\frac{\tilde{\chi}^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbf{k}}(\omega^{\prime})}{\omega^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime}-z)}=\frac{\chi_{\mathbf{k}}}{Dk^{2}-\mathrm{i}z},

(S26)

where the Laplace variable obeys
\,\mathrm{Im}\,z>0
. Finally, by continuing
z\to\omega+\mathrm{i}0
to the real axis one deduces the density response function
\tilde{\chi}^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbf{k}}(\omega)
. The response function for
\hat{A}
then follows as

\tilde{\chi}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)=\frac{1}{L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\neq 0}\frac{\chi_{\mathbf{k}}|u_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}Dk^{2}\omega}{\omega^{2}+(Dk^{2})^{2}}.

(S27)

In a sufficiently large system, the summation is well approximated by an integral using the standard prescription
L^{-d}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\to(2\pi)^{-d}\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{k}
. If we also assume that
u_{\mathbf{k}}
samples only small wavevectors such that
\chi_{\mathbf{k}}\approx\lim_{\mathbf{k}\to 0}\chi_{\mathbf{k}}\equiv\chi_{0}
, then the integral can be carried out explicitly. At small positive frequencies, we obtain the limiting behaviour

\tilde{\chi}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)\sim\begin{cases}\sqrt{\omega}&(d=1)\\
-\omega\ln(\ell^{2}\omega/D)&(d=2)\\
\omega&(d\geq 3).\end{cases}

(S28)

For
d=2
only, the low-frequency response function depends sensitively on the ultraviolet (UV) cutoff scale,
D/\ell^{2}
, where
\ell
is the width of the probe function
u(\mathbf{r})
discussed above. For
d\geq 3
, the response is Ohmic and the zero-frequency limit of the symmetrised noise
\tilde{S}(\omega)=\coth(\beta\omega/2)\tilde{\chi}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)
is well behaved. The long-time decoherence rate thus follows from the limit
\gamma=\lim_{\omega\to 0}2\tilde{\chi}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)/\beta\omega
, which can be taken directly in Eq. (
S27
) to obtain

\gamma=\frac{2g^{2}T}{D}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\chi_{\mathbf{k}}|u_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}}{k^{2}}\approx\frac{2\bar{g}^{2}T\chi_{0}}{D},

(S29)

where the final approximation follows from the assumption that
u_{\mathbf{k}}
samples only long wavelengths, and we defined the renormalised coupling
\bar{g}^{2}=g^{2}\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{k}|u_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}/(8\pi^{3}k^{2}),
which depends only on properties of the probe.

For
d<3
, the low-frequency limit of Eq. (
S27
) is ill-behaved in an infinite system. For any finite system, however, the lower
k
-integration limit is cut off by the exclusion of
\mathbf{k}=0
. In 1D this yields

\gamma=\frac{2g^{2}T}{\pi D}\int_{2\pi/L}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}k\,\frac{\chi_{k}|u_{k}|^{2}}{k^{2}}\sim L,

(S30)

since for large
L
the integral is dominated by its lower limit. A similar argument in 2D yields
\gamma\sim\ln(L/\ell)
, where again the UV cutoff appears explicitly for dimensional reasons. This diverging zero-frequency noise in low dimensions originates from the long diffusive tail of the correlation function in time. To see this explicitly, we take the Fourier transform of Eq. (
S27
) to obtain, for
\tau>0
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\chi^{\prime\prime}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\neq 0}\chi_{\mathbf{k}}|u_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}Dk^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-Dk^{2}\tau}

(S31)

Assuming as above that
\chi_{\mathbf{k}}|u_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}\approx\chi_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-\ell^{2}k^{2}/2}
, we obtain
\chi(\tau)\propto(\ell^{2}+2D\tau)^{-(d/2+1)}
in the thermodynamic limit. The leading-order contribution to
C(\tau)
arises from the corresponding symmetrised noise, which for
\tau\to\infty
can be approximated from the low-frequency response as

\displaystyle S(\tau)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\coth(\beta\omega/2)\tilde{\chi}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega\tau}

\displaystyle\approx\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\frac{2\tilde{\chi}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)}{\beta\omega}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega\tau}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\neq 0}\chi_{\mathbf{k}}|u_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-Dk^{2}\tau},

(S32)

from which we deduce
S(\tau)\sim\left(\ell^{2}+2D\tau\right)^{-d/2}.
