Impurity screening by defects in (1+1) d d quantum critical systems
Ying-Hai Wu, Hong-Hao Tu, Meng Cheng

Abstract
We propose a novel mechanism of impurity screening in (1+1) d d quantum critical states described by conformal field theories (CFTs). An impurity can be screened if it has the same quantum numbers as some gapless degrees of freedom of the CFT. The common source of these degrees of freedom is the chiral primary fields of the CFT, but we uncover that topological defect lines of the CFT may also take this role. Theoretical analysis relies on the insight that the impurities can be interpreted as edge modes of certain symmetry-protected topological (SPT) states. By stacking a SPT state with a CFT, one or two interfaces on which the SPT edge modes reside are created. If screening occurs due to topological defect lines, a symmetry-enriched CFT with exotic boundary states are obtained. The boundary conditions that appear in these cases are difficult to achieve using previously known methods. Numerical simulations are performed in two quantum spin chains whose bulks are described by the SU(3) 1 and Spin(5) 1 CFTs and edges are coupled to spin-1/2 impurities. In both cases, low-energy eigenvalues are consistent with analytical predictions.



Introduction
— Quantum many-body systems with strong interactions are intrinsically difficult to study. The physics in one spatial dimension is most well-understood thanks to the experiences accumulated during the past century. It is useful to divide phases of matter into gapless and gapped ones. A prominent example of 1D gapless phase is the spin-1/2 Heisenberg model, which was solved exactly by Bethe shortly after its inception
Bethe 1931

If a SPT state is realized with open boundary condition (OBC), there would be edge modes that transform as a projective representation of the symmetry group.

In the case of OBC, an edge mode of the SPT state can be interpreted as an “impurity” that is added to the edge of the gapless state. In spite of the coupling with the gapless bulk, the edge modes could survive in some cases and lead to ground state degeneracy in finite-size energy spectra.

It is also possible for the impurity to be “screened” by the gapless bulk, which is familiar in the context of Kondo effect

If these fields carry the same quantum numbers as the impurity, it is natural for the impurity to be screened. However, this picture is inadequate because other screening mechanisms can be found. In this Letter, we propose that an edge impurity may be screened by topological defect lines (TDLs) of CFTs
Chang et al. 2019

Conclusions
— In summary, we have demonstrated that an impurity coupled to a CFT can be screened by TDLs in this CFT even if its chiral primary fields fail to do so. This mechanism enriches our knowledge of the BCFT as it provides us unconventional boundary conditions beyond the well-known Cardy states. A crucial step is to view different types of impurity as the edge modes of certain SPT states. For a given SPT edge mode described by a specific projective class of the global symmetry, screening is expected when there is a TDL labeled by the same projective class. For two specific CFTs in spin chains, non-Cardy boundary states are constructed using conformal embedding and the associated partition functions are verified by numerical simulations. It will be interesting to study the stability of these boundary conditions and the renormalization group flows between them. Another important future direction is to generalize the idea to higher dimensions.

Appendix A: Brief review of TDLs

For a given (1+1)
d
CFT, its TDLs are line operators that commute with the stress tensor
T(z)
on the spacetime worldsheet. In other words, such lines can be freely deformed. In the Hamiltonian formalism, a TDL corresponds to a generalized symmetry operator acting on the CFT Hilbert space. The ordinary global symmetry transformations represented by unitary operators are examples of invertible TDLs. Two TDLs
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
can be fused to generate another TDL
\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. In the operator language, fusion simply means the multiplication of two operators. Another useful operation is the direct sums of TDLs. These two operations make the TDLs into a fusion ring that generalizes the group structure of ordinary symmetry. In particular, there is a set of
simple
TDLs whose members cannot be further decomposed into TDLs. We denote these simple TDLs as
\mathcal{D}_{a},\mathcal{D}_{b},\cdots
. The fusion ring structure is dictated by the fusion rules

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{a}\times\mathcal{D}_{b}=\sum_{c}N^{c}_{ab}\mathcal{D}_{c}

(S1)

with integer coefficients
N^{c}_{ab}\geq 0
. More details about the algebraic structure of TDLs can be found in Refs.
Chang et al. 2019
;
Bhardwaj and Tachikawa 2018
. A TDL can terminate at a defect operator. All defect operators for a given TDL
\mathcal{D}
form a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}}
. In view of the state-operator correspondence,
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}}
is also the CFT Hilbert space on a circle which has the
\mathcal{D}
inserted along the time direction. While TDLs are usually discussed in the continuum theory, they can also be studied directly in lattice models. A large class of TDLs have been constructed in the “anyon chain” models.

Let us illustrate the general theory using an example. It is well-known that the Ising CFT has a self-duality symmetry that maps the order parameter
\sigma
to the disorder parameter
\mu
. For a lattice realization (e.g. the transverse-field Ising model), this is implemented by the Kramers-Wannier duality transformation. We denote the TDL that corresponds to this self-duality as
\mathcal{N}
. The ordinary
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry of Ising CFT generates an invertible TDL called
\eta
. One can show that the fusion rules are

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{N}=1+\eta,\quad\eta\times\eta=1,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\times\eta=\eta\times\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{N}.

(S2)

An explaination of the rule
\eta\times\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{N}
is helpful. Consider the action of
\mathcal{N}
on the spin operator
\sigma(z)
with
z
being the complex coordinate. Pictorially, a TDL is dragged through the insertion of
\sigma(z)
. Under the duality,
\sigma(z)
becomes the disorder operator
\mu(z)
, which must be attached to the end of a
\eta
line. Since the action is local, the
\eta
line has to end on the
\mathcal{N}
line, which gives the fusion rule
\eta\times\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{N}
. This process is shown as the equation

Appendix C
Appendix C: Boundary states from conformal embedding

For a rational CFT, all boundary states that preserve (half of) the chiral algebra are of the Cardy type. However, non-Cardy boundary states with reduced symmetry may also exist. Here we explain how to construct them using conformal embedding. Let us consider a pair of chiral rational CFTs
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
, where the latter is obtained from the former by an extension of the chiral algebra. In other words, there is a conformal embedding
\mathcal{R}\subset\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
.

For this purpose, we can focus on the algebraic data of the CFT. A chiral CFT is associated with a modular tensor category (MTC), which is mathematically the representation category of the chiral algebra. Denote the MTCs associated with
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
by
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, respectively. In this language, chiral algebra extension means
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
is obtained from
\mathcal{C}
by anyon condensation. Mathematically, the condensate is described by a separable, commutative Frobenius object
A
in
\mathcal{C}
. The interface between
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
can be endowed with a fusion category
\mathcal{T}
, which contains both the deconfined anyons (i.e.
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
) and the confined defects. All simple objects in
\mathcal{T}
can be lifted to
\mathcal{C}
. More precisely, the lifting of
\alpha\in\mathcal{T}
is

\displaystyle l(\alpha)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{C}}n_{{\alpha}a}\;a,\quad n_{{\alpha}a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{{\geq}0}.

(S7)

In particular, the lifting of the vacuum in
\mathcal{T}
is the algebra object
A
. A subcategory of
\mathcal{T}
which contains all deconfined anyons becomes
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, and the remaining ones become topological defects in
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
. If the characters of the CFT
\mathcal{R}
are
\chi_{a}
, then the characters of the CFT
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\chi_{\alpha}=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{C}}n_{{\alpha}a}\;\chi_{a},\quad\alpha\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}.

(S8)

Each object
\alpha
in
\mathcal{T}
defines a boundary state for
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
and those belong to
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
correspond to the Cardy states. For a cylinder with boundary states
\alpha,\beta
at the two ends, its partition function is
Huang and Cheng 2023

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha,\beta}=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{T}}N^{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}\chi_{\gamma}.

(S9)

Let us turn to specific examples. For the SU(3)
1
CFT, non-Cardy boundary states are obtained using the conformal embedding SU(3)

SU(2)
4

4

and SU(2)
4

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}\simeq 0+2,\quad\mathbf{3}\simeq 1_{+},\quad\bar{\mathbf{3}}\simeq 1_{-},

(S10)

where
1_{\pm}
means that the spin-1 field splits into
\mathbf{3}
and
\bar{\mathbf{3}}

\mathcal{D}_{\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3}{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\frac{1}{2}}\times\mathcal{D}_{\frac{1}{2}}=\mathbf{0}+\mathbf{3}+\bar{\mathbf{3}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\frac{1}{2}}\times\mathbf{3}=\mathcal{D}_{\frac{1}{2}}\times\bar{\mathbf{3}}=\mathcal{D}_{\frac{1}{2}}.

(S11)

Appendix D
Appendix D: More details about numerical simulations

As displayed in Fig.
1
, there are two equivalent ways to represent our system. In our numerical simulations of the
S=1
case, we adope the one in Fig.
1
(b) because it is directly related to theoretical analysis that relies on SPT states. The system has a CFT part with
L_{c}
spins and a SPT part with
L_{s}
spins. It is favorable to set the SPT part at the AKLT point for its short correlation length. The full Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle H=H_{\rm CFT}+H_{\rm SPT}+H_{\rm BC},

(S16)

where
H_{\rm CFT}=H(\beta=1)
contains the indices
j\in\left[1,L_{c}-1\right]
,
H_{\rm SPT}=10\,H(\beta=\frac{1}{3})
contains the indices
j\in\left[L_{c},L_{c}+L_{s}-1\right]
, and
H_{\rm BC}
specifies the boundary condition.
L_{c}
is chosen to be a multiple of 3 such that the
\ket{\mathbf{0}}
boundary state is realized in the absence of the SPT part. The prefactor
10
in
H_{\rm SPT}
creates a large gap such that the CFT part only has non-negligible coupling with the AKLT edge modes. If the system has periodic boundary condition (PBC), the last and first sites are connected by

\displaystyle H_{\rm BC}=10\left[\mathbf{S}_{L_{c}+L_{s}}\cdot\mathbf{S}_{1}+\frac{1}{3}\left(\mathbf{S}_{L_{c}+L_{s}}\cdot\mathbf{S}_{1})^{2}\right)\right].

(S17)

If the system has OBC, the open end of the AKLT part is connected to a spin-1/2 by

\displaystyle H_{\rm BC}=10\;\mathbf{S}_{L_{c}+L_{s}}\cdot\mathbf{S}_{\frac{1}{2}},

(S18)

which removes an undesirable degeneracy caused by the SPT edge mode. In addition to the ULS-AKLT model, we have also studied ULS chains with spin-1/2 impurities attached to the edges. This allows for numerical calculations in larger systems. The coupling at the left edge is

\displaystyle J_{1}\mathbf{S}_{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\mathbf{S}_{1}+J_{2}\mathbf{S}_{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\mathbf{S}_{2}

(S19)

where
\mathbf{S}_{1/2}
is the spin-1/2 impurity and
\mathbf{S}_{1}
(
\mathbf{S}_{2}
) is the first (second) site of the ULS chain. The coupling at the right edge (if there are two impurites) is similar. If
J_{1}\gtrsim 5
and
J_{2}=0
, the results have negligible dependence on
J_{1}
. The cases with one impurity still exhibit strong finite-size effects. If we tune
J_{2}
to about
-1
, finite-size effects can be suppressed. For example, the gap ratio at
L_{c}=60
is
2.236
for
J_{1}=10,J_{2}=0
and
2.168
for
J_{1}=10,J_{2}=-3
.

For the spin-2 model, it is also possible to design two ways to explore the physics of our interest. However, if we use a SPT state to represent the impurities, the computational cost for PBC would be too high. Firstly, it is well-known that DMRG is more difficult for periodic systems. Secondly, the bond dimension of the matrix product operator representing the Hamiltonian also increases considerably when using PBC. Due to these complications, we have only studied the systems as shown in Fig.
1
(a). The edge coupling is also described by Eq. (
S19
) but now
\mathbf{S}_{1,2}
are spin-2 operators. The results in Fig.
3
correspond to
J_{1}=2
and
J_{2}=-0.2
. The effects of tuning
J_{1,2}
have been investigated. For various
J_{1}\in[1,10]
and
J_{2}=0
, the lowest three levels in the presence of two impurities have
S=0,1,2
. The gap ratio at the same
L_{c}
increases as
J_{1}
gets smaller but is never very close to
3
. If there is one impurity, the same parameters may not give the expected quantum numbers for small
L_{c}
. For
J_{1}=2
, the fourth eigenstate has
S=7/2
at
L_{c}=12
but changes to
S=1/2
at
L_{c}=16
. If
J_{1}
increases, a larger
L_{c}
is needed to ensure that the four eigenstate has
S=1/2
. If we turn on a small negative
J_{2}
, finite-size effects are suppressed, but the gap ratios are still quite different from the theoretical values.

Appendix E
Appendix E: SO(
n
) spin chains

Both examples studied in the main text belong to a class of SO
(n)
quadratic-biquadratic models. In general, we have the SO
(n)
Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H=\sum_{j}\left[\cos\theta\sum_{a<b}T^{ab}_{j}T^{ab}_{j+1}+\sin\theta\Big(\sum_{a<b}T^{ab}_{j}T^{ab}_{j+1}\Big)^{2}\right].

Here
T^{ab}
are SO(
n
) generators in the
n
-dimensional vector representation with normalization chosen to be
\sum_{a<b}(T^{ab})^{2}=n-1
. The spin-1 (spin-2) model in the main text corresponds to the
n=3
(
n=5
) case. The phase diagrams of these models have been studied before
Tu et al. 2008
;
Tu and Orús 2011
.

We are interested in the parameter range
\theta\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
. There are two critical angles

\displaystyle\theta_{\rm ULS}=\arctan\frac{1}{n-2},\quad\theta_{\rm R}=\arctan\frac{n-4}{(n-2)^{2}},

(S21)

which divide the region into three phases:

1.

For
\theta\in(\theta_{\rm ULS},\pi/2)
, the model is in a critical phase described by
(n-1)
component Luttinger liquid.

2.

For
\theta\in(\theta_{\rm R},\theta_{\rm ULS})
, the model is gapped and realizes the SO
(n)
SPT state. Notably, at
\theta=\arctan\frac{1}{n}
, the ground state has an exact MPS representation that can be viewed as generalization of the AKLT state. For even
n
, the lattice translation symmetry is spontaneously broken (this is unavoidable because the SO
(n)
vector representation is projective for even
n
). The two degenerate states are both SPT phases, which correspond to the two irreducible spinor representations of SO
(n)
.

3.

For
\theta\in(-\pi/2,\theta_{\rm R})
, the model is in a dimerized phase that spontaneously breaks lattice translation symmetry.

The critical point at
\theta=\theta_{\rm ULS}
has an enlarged SU
(n)
