On networks over finite rings footnoteinfo
Daizhan Cheng

Abstract
A (control) network over a finite ring is proposed. Using semi-tensor product (STP) of matrices, a set of algebraic equations are provided to verify whether a finite set with two binary operators is a ring. It is then shown that the STP-based technique developed for logical (control) networks are applicable to (control) networks over finite rings. The sub- (control) network over an ideal of the bearing ring is revealed. Then the product ring is proposed and the (control) network over product ring is investigated. As a key result, the decomposition theorem, called the Decomposition Principle (DP), is proposed, which shows a (control) network over a product ring is decomposable into sub- (control)networks over each factor rings, which makes the (control) properties of a network can be revealed by its factor sub-networks over factor rings. Using DP, the control problems of a control network are investigated via its sub control networks. Particularly, using DP, the control of linear networks over product rings is discussed in detail. Finally, the representation theorem is presented, which shows that each (control) network over a finite set can be expressed as a (control) network over a product ring.



keywords

Network over finite ring, sub-network over ideal, product ring, decomposition theorem, presentation theorem.

Introduction

In recent years, the networks over finite sets attract a considerable attention from control community
[
9
,
17
]
. As pointed by
[
14
]
, “finite sets are more realistic in dealing with limited storage capacities and communication bandwidths”. Among these finite set approaches, networks over finite fields are of particular interest
[
16
,
15
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
.

Finite field, also called Galois field, is of the form
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
where
p
is a primer number
[
10
]
.
From existing results one sees easily that finite field approach has several advantages, such as reducing the computational complexity
[
14
]
, using semi-tensor product (STP) technique
[
13
]
, easily applicable to other problems
[
16
,
15
,
11
]
. In addition, the field structure provides convenience in theoretical analysis.

An obvious restriction on finite field approach is that the cardinal number (number of elements) of a finite set considered should be a prime number. This restriction is practically unreasonable. By the author’s understanding, it comes from technical point of view. That is, the treatment tool for finite fields is ready and convenient. A natural generalization is: instead of finite fields can we consider certain more general structures, say,
{\mathbb{Z}}_{r}
, for any integer
r\geq 2
to replace finite fields? Hence, this paper considers the networks over finite rings which contain finite fields or even
{\mathbb{Z}}_{r}
with any
r>3
as special cases. This is the original motivation for this paper.

Recently, STP of matrices has been proposed and used to analysis and control design of Boolean networks as well as
k
-valued logical networks
[
1
,
2
]
. STP was first used to investigate networks over finite field by
[
13
]
. STP is also a fundamental tool for the investigation in this paper.

After a brief survey on rings and the STP approach to logical networkers, this paper provides a set of algebraic equations for finite rings, which can be used to construct finite rings and investigate their properties. After a finite and exhausting searching, we found that there are exactly
6
rings of cardinal number
4
. Then we show that the STP-based techniques developed for logical (control) networks are applicable to (control) networks over finite rings. When
S
is an ideal of the bearing ring
R
of a network, it is shown that a sub-network is obtained over
S
, which can be considered as a sub-network over an invariant subspace. Motivated by this, the product ring of some fundamental rings is proposed. All the factor rings are shown to be ideals of the product ring. A general product ring
{\mathbb{Z}}^{\kappa}
is proposed. When
\kappa=\prod_{i=1}^{s}k_{i}
is a prime number decomposition,
{\mathbb{Z}}^{\kappa}
is the product ring of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{k_{i}}
,
i\in[1,s]
. Using product ring, a key result, called the decomposition principle (DP), is presented, which says each (control) network over
{\mathbb{Z}}^{\kappa}
can be decomposed into
s
sub- (control) networks over its component sub-rings
{\mathbb{Z}}_{k_{i}}
, which are finite fields. Decomposition principle allows us to understand and design control for original networks by analyzing and controlling all component sub-networks. In addition, it also makes all results for finite fields applicable to finite rings. As an application, the control of networks over finite rings is considered by designing controls for all factor sub networks. Particularly, using DP, the linear control networks over
{\mathbb{Z}}^{\kappa}
are discussed in detail. Finally, another key result, called the representation theorem, is presented, which shows that any (control) network over finite sets, say
\Xi
, can be expressed as a (control) network over
{\mathbb{Z}}^{\kappa}
, where
\kappa=|\Xi|
. That is, the technique developed for networks over finite rings is universally applicable to any networks over finite sets, including Boolean (control) networks,
k
-valued or mix-valued logical (control) networks, (control) networks over finite fields, shift registers
[
19
]
, and strategy profile dynamics of finite games, etc.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 is a brief survey for finite rings and STP technique to logical (control) networks. Using STP, Section 3 provides a set of algebraic equations for finite rings. As an application, all the finite rings with
4
elements are obtained. Section 4 shows that the STP-based technique developed for logical (control) networks are applicable to networks over finite rings. For a given (control) network over a ring the sub-network over an ideal is investigated in Section 5. Section 6 proposes the product ring of finite rings. The networks over product rings are also proposed and studied. A key theorem, called decomposition principle, is presented in Section 7, which shows that a network over a product ring can be decomposed into sub-networks on each factor rings.
Section 8 considers the control of networks over product rings. As a special case of Section 8, the decomposition of linear (control) networks over product rings are investigated in Section 9. Section 10 presents another key theorem, called representation theorem, which shows any (control) networks over finite sets can be expressed as networks over product rings. Section 11 is a brief conclusion.

2.1
Rings and Sub-Rings

Definition
\thethm

[
10
]
Let
R\neq\emptyset
.
R
is called a ring if there exist two binary operators
(\oplus,\odot)
such that

(i)

(R,\oplus)
is an Abelian group with
{\bf 0}
as its identity element.

(ii)

(R,\odot)
is a monoid, i.e., a semi-group with identity
{\bf 1}\neq{\bf 0}
.

(iii)

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}(a\oplus b)\odot c=a\odot c\oplus b\odot c,\\
a\odot(b\oplus c)=a\odot b\oplus a\odot c,\quad a,b,c\in R.\end{array}

In addition, if

\displaystyle a\odot b=b\odot a,\quad a,b\in R,

(3)

then
R
is called a commutative ring.

Remark 1
.

(i)

In some literature a ring may have no (product) identity. Here we follow the definition in
[
10
]
, and consider a ring with identity as a ring. To emphasize the operators on
R
we sometimes also express
R
as a triple
(R,\oplus,\odot)
.

(ii)

If
R
is a ring and
(R\backslash\{0\},\times)
is also an Abelian group, then
R
is a field.
Hence a finite field is a special finite ring.

(iii)

Since
(R,\oplus)
is a group, for each
X\in R
there exists its unique inverse, denoted by
\neg X
(or
-X
) such that
X\oplus(\neg X)={\bf 0}
. We also use
\ominus
as
X\ominus Y:=X\oplus(\neg Y)
.

Some fundamental properties of a ring are as follows:

Proposition 2

[
8
]
Let
R
be a ring with
{\bf 1}\neq{\bf 0}
. Then

(i)

\displaystyle{\bf 0}\odot a=a\odot{\bf 0}={\bf 0},\quad a\in R.

(4)

(ii)

\displaystyle(\neg a)\odot b=a\odot(\neg b)=\neg(a\odot b),\quad a,b\in R.

(5)

(iii)

\displaystyle(na)\odot b=a\odot(nb)=n(a\odot b),\quad n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+},\;a,b\in R.

(6)

Note that
na:=\underbrace{a\oplus a\oplus\cdots\oplus a}_{n}
.

(iv)

\displaystyle\left(\oplus_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\right)\left(\oplus_{j=1}^{n}b_{j}\right)=\oplus_{i=1}^{m}\oplus_{j=1}^{n}a_{i}\odot b_{j},\quad a_{i},b_{j}\in R.

(7)

Definition 3

Let
(S,+,\times)
and
(R,\oplus,\otimes)
be two rings.

(i)

A mapping
\pi:S\rightarrow R
is called a ring homomorphism, if

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\pi(s_{1}+s_{2})=\pi(s_{1})\oplus\pi(s_{2})\\
\pi(s_{1}\times s_{2})=\pi(s_{1})\otimes\pi(s_{2}),\quad s_{1},s_{2}\in S.\\
\end{cases}

(8)

If there exists a homomorphism
\pi:S\rightarrow R
, then
S
is said to be homomorphic to
R
, denoted by
S\simeq R
.

(ii)

Let
\pi:S\rightarrow R
be a ring homomorphism.
\pi
is called a ring isomorphism if
\pi
is a bijective mapping. Then
S
and
R
are said to be isomorphic, denoted by
S\cong R
.

(iii)

If
\pi:R\rightarrow R
is a ring isomorphism, it is called an automorphism.

Remark 4
.

Let
S
and
R
be two rings.

(i)

If
\pi:S\rightarrow R
is a ring homomorphism, then it is easy to see that
\pi(1)=1
,
\pi(0)=0
, and

\pi[(s_{1}+s_{2})\times s_{3}]=\pi(s_{1})\odot\pi(s_{3})\oplus\pi(s_{2})\odot\pi(s_{3}),

etc.

(ii)

For inverse element
\neg x
, we have

\pi(\neg x)=\neg\pi(x).

Definition 5

(i)

Let
(R,\oplus,\odot)
be a ring.
S\subset R
. If
(S,\oplus,\odot)
is also a ring,
S
is called a sub-ring of
R
.

(ii)

A sub-ring
S\subset R
is said to be an ideal of
R
, if

r\odot S\subset S,\quad S\odot r\subset S,\quad r\in R.

r\odot S
(
S\odot r
) is called the left (right) coset of
S
. If
R
is commutative, then
r\odot S=S\odot r
.

Example 6

Consider
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
. The operators

a+_{p}b:=a+b(\mbox{mod}~p),\quad a\times_{p}b:=a\times b(\mbox{mod}~p).

(i)

Consider
{\mathbb{Z}}_{6}
. Let
S=\{3,0\}
.
Then it is easy to verify that
(S,+_{6},\times_{6})
is a ring. Hence,
S
is a sub-ring of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{6}
.

(ii)

Let
\pi:S\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
, defined by

\pi(x)=\begin{cases}1,\quad x=3\\
0,\quad x=0.\end{cases}

Then it is easy to see that
\pi:S\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
is an isomorphism.

(iii)

It is clear that
S
is an ideal of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{6}
.

Let
|S|=r
.
S
is isomorphic to a ring
(E,\oplus_{E},\odot_{E})
, where
E=\{1,2,\cdots,r-1.0\}
, then
E
is called the essential ring of
S
.

Consider
S=\{3,0\}\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{6}
. The essential ring of

is
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
.

2.2
STP Approach to Logical Networks

The following is a brief survey for STP. We refer to
[
1
,
2
]
for more details.

Definition 7

Let
M\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
and
N\in{\mathcal{M}}_{p\times q}
,
t=\lcm(n,p)
be the least common multiple of
n
and
p
. Then the STP of
M
and
N
is defined by

\displaystyle A\ltimes B:=\left(A\otimes I_{t/n}\right)\left(B\otimes I_{t/p}\right),

(9)

where
\otimes
is the Kronecker product.

Remark 8
.

(i)

If
n=p
, then
A\ltimes B=AB
. Hence the STP is a generalization of the conventional matrix product.

(ii)

STP keeps all the main properties of the conventional matrix product available. Hence in most cases the symbol
\ltimes
is omitted if there is no possible confusion.

(iii)

Hereafter, all the matrix products in this paper are assumed to be SPT unless elsewhere stated.

The following property is useful in the sequel.

Proposition 9

Let
M
be a matrix,
X\in{\mathbb{R}}^{t}
be a column vector. Then

\displaystyle XM=(I_{t}\otimes M)X.

(10)

The swap matrix
W_{[m,n]}
is defined as

\displaystyle W_{[m,n]}=[I_{n}\delta_{m}^{1},I_{n}\delta_{m}^{2},\cdots,I_{n}\delta_{m}^{m}],

(11)

where
\delta_{m}^{i}
is the
i
th column of identity matrix
I_{m}
.

The main function of a swap matrix is shown in the following proposition.

Proposition 10

Let
X\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
Y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be two column vectors. Then

\displaystyle W_{[m,n]}XY=YX.

(12)

Next, we briefly survey the STP based technique for logical (control) networks.

Let
{\mathcal{D}}_{k}=\{1,2,\cdots,k-1,0\}
be a finite set. It can be used to represent any finite set
S
with
|S|=k
. Denote by
\Delta_{k}:=\Col(I_{k})
the set of columns of the identity matrix
I_{k}

{\mathcal{D}}_{k}

\displaystyle\vec{i}=\begin{cases}\delta_{k}^{i},\quad 1\leq i\leq k-1\\
\delta_{k}^{k},\quad i=0.\end{cases}

(13)

A matrix
L\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
is called a logical matrix, if its columns

\Col(L)\subset\Delta_{m}
. Then it can be expressed as

L=\delta_{m}[i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{n}]:=[\delta_{m}^{i_{1}},\delta_{m}^{i_{2}},\cdots,\delta_{m}^{i_{n}}].

The set of
m\times n

{\mathcal{L}}_{m\times n}

Proposition 11

Consider a
k
-valued logical function

\displaystyle Y=f(X_{1},X_{2},\cdots,X_{n}),

(14)

where
X_{i}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{k}
,
i\in[1,n]
. Then there exists a unique
M_{f}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{k\times\kappa}
, (
\kappa=k^{n}
), called
the structure matrix of
f
, such that

\displaystyle y=M_{f}\ltimes_{i=1}^{n}x_{i},

(15)

where
y=\vec{Y}
and
x_{i}=\vec{X}_{i}
,
i\in[1,n]
, are vector forms of
Y
and
X_{i}
respectively.

Using Proposition
11
, a logical dynamic (control) network can be expressed into its vector form as follows:

Proposition 12

Consider a
k
-valued logical network

\displaystyle\begin{cases}X_{1}(t+1)=f_{1}(X_{1}(t),X_{2}(t),\cdots,X_{n}(t))\\
X_{2}(t+1)=f_{2}(X_{1}(t),X_{2}(t),\cdots,X_{n}(t))\\
\vdots\\
X_{n}(t+1)=f_{n}(X_{1}(t),X_{2}(t),\cdots,X_{n}(t)).\\
\end{cases}

(16)

Then in vector form it can be expressed as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}x_{1}(t+1)=M_{1}x(t)\\
x_{2}(t+1)=M_{2}x(t)\\
\vdots\\
x_{n}(t+1)=M_{n}x(t),\\
\end{cases}

(17)

where
x(t)=\ltimes_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}(t)
.
M_{i}
is the structure matrix of
f_{i}
. (
17
) is called the component-wise algebraic state space representation (ASSR) of (
16
).

Moreover, (
17
) can further be expressed as

\displaystyle x(t+1)=Mx(t),

(18)

where

M=M_{1}*M_{2}*\cdots*M_{n},

where
*
is the Khatri-Rao product
[
2
]
.

(

) is called the ASSR of (
16
).

Proposition 13

Consider a

-valued logical control network

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\begin{cases}X_{1}(t+1)=f_{1}(X_{1}(t),\cdots,X_{n}(t),U_{1}(t),\cdots,U_{m}(t))\\
X_{2}(t+1)=f_{2}(X_{1}(t),\cdots,X_{n}(t),U_{1}(t),\cdots,U_{m}(t))\\
\vdots\\
X_{1}(t+1)=f_{n}(X_{1}(t),\cdots,X_{n}(t),U_{1}(t),\cdots,U_{m}(t)),\\
\end{cases}\\
~~~Y_{\ell}=g_{\ell}(X_{1}(t),X_{2}(t),\cdots,X_{n}(t)),\quad\ell\in[1,p].\end{array}

Similarly to logical case, its component-wise ASSR is

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\begin{cases}x_{1}(t+1)=L_{1}u(t)x(t)\\
x_{2}(t+1)=L_{2}u(t)x(t)\\
\vdots\\
x_{n}(t+1)=L_{n}u(t)x(t),\\
\end{cases}\\
~~~y_{\ell}(t)=E_{\ell}x(t),\quad\ell\in[1,p],\end{array}

where
u(t)=\ltimes_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}(t)
,
L_{i}
and
E_{\ell}
are structure matrices of
L_{i}
and
g_{\ell}
respectively.

Moreover, (
13
) can further be expressed into its ASSR form as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}x(t+1)=Lx(t),\\
y(t)=Ex(t),\end{cases}

(23)

where

\begin{array}[]{l}L=L_{1}*L_{2}*\cdots*L_{n},\\
E=E_{1}*E_{2}*\cdots*E_{p}.\end{array}

A useful tool for deducting ASSR of logical (control) networks is the following matrix, called a power reducing matrix:

\displaystyle PR_{n}:=diag(\delta_{n}^{1},\delta_{n}^{2},\cdots,\delta_{n}^{n})\in{\mathcal{L}}_{n^{2}\times n}.

(24)

Proposition 14

Let

\displaystyle PR_{n}x^{2}=x,\quad x\in\Delta_{n}.

3
Matrix Expression of Operators on Finite Rings

Consider a finite set
R=\{1,2,\cdots,k-1,0\}
. Assume it has two binary operators
\oplus
and
\odot
. Using vector form expression (
13
),
R\equiv\Delta_{k}
.
Assume the structure matrices for
\oplus
and
\otimes
are
M_{\oplus},~M_{\odot}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{k\times k^{2}}
respectively. That is,

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}x\oplus y=M_{\oplus}xy,\\
x\otimes y=M_{\odot}xy,\quad x,y\in\Delta_{k}.\\
\end{array}

As a convention, in a ring
R=\Delta_{k}
, we always assume
\delta_{k}^{1}=\vec{\bf 1}
and
\delta_{k}^{k}=\vec{\bf 0}
.

In vector form, denote by
M_{\neg}
the structure matrix of
\neg
, then
X\oplus(\neg X)=0
can be expressed in vector form as

\displaystyle M_{\oplus}x(M_{\neg}x)=\delta_{k}^{k},\quad x\in\Delta_{k}.

(28)

Similarly,
X\ominus Y
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ccl}M_{\ominus}xy&=&M_{\oplus}xM_{\neg}y=M_{oplus}\left(I_{k}\otimes M_{\neg}\right)xy,\\
&&\quad x,y\in\Delta_{k}.\end{array}

Note that in a ring,
\neg
and
\ominus
are not independent operators. They are uniquely determined by
\oplus
. But for computational ease, usually we also define
M_{\neg}
and use it in the ASSR representation of networks over finite rings.

Theorem 15
.

Given a finite set
R
(where
|R|=k
) with two binary operators
\oplus
and
\odot
, then the properties of
R
can be verified by the corresponding properties of its operators. That is,

(i)

(Commutativity of
\oplus
):
(R,\oplus)
is commutative, if and only if,

\displaystyle M_{\oplus}=M_{\oplus}W_{[k,k]}.

(31)

(ii)

(Associativity of
\oplus
):
(R,\oplus)
is associative, if and only if,

\displaystyle M_{\oplus}^{2}=M_{\oplus}\left(I_{k}\otimes M_{\oplus}\right).

(32)

(iii)

(Identity of
\oplus
):
\delta_{k}^{k}
is the identity for
(R,\oplus)
, if and only if,

\displaystyle M_{\oplus}\delta_{k}^{k}=M_{\oplus}W_{[k,k]}\delta_{k}^{k}=I_{k}.

(33)

(iv)

(Invertibility of
\oplus
):
(R,\oplus)
is invertible, if and only if, there exists a unary operator
\neg
, such that

\displaystyle M_{\oplus}\left(I_{k}\otimes M_{\neg}\right)PR_{k}=\delta_{k}\otimes{\bf 1}_{k}^{T}.

(34)

(v)

(Associativity of
\odot
):
(R,\odot)
is associative, if and only if,

\displaystyle M_{\odot}^{2}=M_{\odot}\left(I_{k}\otimes M_{\odot}\right).

(35)

(vi)

(Identity of
\odot
):
\delta_{k}^{1}
is the identity for
(R,\odot)
, if and only if,

\displaystyle M_{\odot}\delta_{k}^{1}=M_{\odot}W_{[k,k]}\delta_{k}^{1}=I_{k}.

(36)

(vii)

(Distributivity of
\oplus
with respect to
\odot
):
(R,\oplus,\odot)
is distributive, if and only if,

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{lr}M_{\odot}M_{\oplus}=M_{\oplus}M_{\odot}\left(I_{k^{2}}\otimes M_{\odot}\right)&\\
~~~\left(I_{k}\otimes W_{[k,k]}\right)\left(I_{k^{2}}\otimes PR_{k}\right),&(a)\\
M_{\odot}\left(I_{k}\otimes M_{\oplus}\right)=M_{\oplus}M_{\odot}\left(I_{k^{2}}\otimes M_{\odot}\right)&\\
~~~\left(I_{k}\otimes W_{[k,k]}\right)PR_{k}.&(b)\\
\end{array}

Proof
. We prove (
(vii)
) (b) only. The proofs for other equations are similar.
To this end, we need only to show that the (
(vii)
) (b) is equivalent to

\displaystyle X\odot(Y\oplus Z)=(X\odot Y)\oplus(X\odot Z),\quad X,Y,Z\in R.

(41)

Expressing both sides of (
41
) into vector form, we have

LHS=M_{\odot}xM_{\oplus}yz=M_{\odot}\left(I_{k}\otimes M_{\oplus}\right)xyz.

\begin{array}[]{ccl}RHS&=&M_{\oplus}M_{\odot}xyM_{\odot}xz\\
&=&M_{\oplus}M_{\odot}\left(I_{k^{2}}\otimes M_{\odot}\right)xyxz\\
&=&M_{\oplus}M_{\odot}\left(I_{k^{2}}\otimes M_{\odot}\right)xW_{[k,k]}xyz\\
&=&M_{\oplus}M_{\odot}\left(I_{k^{2}}\otimes M_{\odot}\right)\left(I_{k}\otimes W_{[k,k]}\right)x^{2}yz\\
&=&M_{\oplus}M_{\odot}\left(I_{k^{2}}\otimes M_{\odot}\right)\left(I_{k}\otimes W_{[k,k]}\right)PR_{k}xyz\\
\end{array}

Since
x,y,z\in\Delta_{k}
are arbitrary, setting LHS=RHS yields (
(vii)
) (b). Hence, (
(vii)
) (b) is equivalent to (
41
).

\Box

Since a finite ring
R
is uniquely determined by its

\odot

)-(
(vii)
) are satisfied,

is a ring. In addition, if (

is a commutative ring. Using it, a finite ring can be constructed by solving a set of matrix equations. We give an example for this.

Example 16

1.

Consider
R=\{1,2,\cdots,p-1,0\}
, where

R\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
.

{\bf 1}

, which is a contradiction. Next, consider the product. Similar argument shows

R\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
.

Consider

. Assume the structures of its operators

and

M_{\odot}=\delta_{4}[1,2,3,4,2,1,3,4,3,3,4,4,4,4,4,4].\end{array}

(ii)

, and

5
Sub-networks over Ideals

Hereafter, we consider only finite commutative rings. The “commutativity” is always assumed and will not be mentioned repeatedly.

Consider a finite ring
R
. Let
S\subset R
be an ideal. Since
S
itself is a ring, there exist a ring
R_{S}
, called the essential ring of
S
, and an isomorphism
\pi:S\rightarrow R_{S}
.

Now for each
a\in R
, since
S
is an idea, then for each
s_{i}\in S
there exists a unique
s_{j}\in S
such that

\displaystyle as_{i}=s_{j},\quad s_{i},s_{j}\in S.

(67)

That is, each
a\in R
can induce a mapping
S\rightarrow S
, denoted by
\theta_{a}
. Now let
R_{S}:=\{1,2,\cdots,k-1,0\}
, where
k=|S|
. Then in vector form
\theta_{a}
has its structure matrix as
\Theta_{a}
, such that

\displaystyle\pi(as_{i})=\Theta_{a}\pi(s_{i}).

(68)

We use an example to explain this.

Example 21

Consider
{\mathbb{Z}}_{6}
, and
S=\{3,0\}\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{6}
. It is easy to see that

(i)

S
is an ideal of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{6}
.

(ii)

R_{S}={\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
, and the isomorphism is

\pi(3)=1,\quad\pi(0)=0.

(iii)

Since
1\times_{6}3=3
,
1\times_{6}6=6
,

\Theta_{1}=\delta_{2}[1,2].

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\Theta_{1}=\Theta_{3}=\Theta_{5}=I_{2},\\
\Theta_{2}=\Theta_{4}=\Theta_{6}=\delta^{2}[2,2].\\
\end{array}

Definition 22

Let
R
be a finite ring and
S\subset R
be an ideal.
S
is called a proper idea if there exists a mapping
\phi:R\rightarrow S
such that

\displaystyle\Theta_{a}(s)=\phi(a)s,\quad a\in R,s\in S.

(71)

7
Decomposition Principle

Assume
R=R_{1}\times R_{2}
, where
|R_{i}|=k_{i}
,
i=1,2
, then
|R|=k_{1}k_{2}:=\kappa
.
The natural projections
\phi_{i}:R\rightarrow R_{i}
,
i=1,2
have been defined in Definition
. In fact, each
x\in R
has unique decomposed expression as

x=\delta_{\kappa}^{r}=\delta_{k_{1}}^{\alpha}\delta_{k_{2}}^{\beta},

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are as shown in formula (
(ii)
). Moreover,

\phi_{1}(\delta_{\kappa}^{r})=\delta_{k_{1}}^{\alpha},\quad\phi_{2}(\delta_{\kappa}^{r})=\delta_{k_{2}}^{\beta}.

Lemma 42
.

Let
R=R_{1}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{2}
. Then
\phi_{i}:R\rightarrow R_{i}
,
i=1,2
are two ring homomorphisms.

Proof
. Let
z_{1},z_{2}\in R=\Delta_{\kappa}
. In vector form we denote

z_{1}=\delta_{\kappa}^{p}=\delta_{k_{1}}^{p_{1}}\delta_{k_{2}}^{p_{2}},\quad z_{2}=\delta_{\kappa}^{q}=\delta_{k_{1}}^{q_{1}}\delta_{k_{2}}^{q_{2}}.

Then

z_{1}\oplus z_{2}=M_{\oplus}\delta_{\kappa}^{p}\delta_{\kappa}^{q}=\left[M_{\oplus_{1}}\delta_{k_{1}}^{p_{1}}\delta_{k_{1}}^{q_{1}}\right]\left[M_{\oplus_{2}}\delta_{k_{2}}^{p_{2}}\delta_{k_{2}}^{q_{2}}\right].

Hence

\phi_{1}(z_{1}\oplus z_{2})=\left[M_{\oplus_{1}}\delta_{k_{1}}^{p_{1}}\delta_{k_{1}}^{q_{1}}\right]=\phi_{1}(z_{1})\oplus_{1}\pi_{1}(z_{2});

and

\phi_{2}(z_{1}\oplus z_{2})=\left[M_{\oplus_{2}}\delta_{k_{2}}^{p_{2}}\delta_{k_{2}}^{q_{2}}\right]=\phi_{2}(z_{1})\oplus_{1}\pi_{2}(z_{2}).

Similarly, we can prove the homomorphic property for product and minus.

\Box

Definition 43

Let
R=R_{1}\times R_{2}
be a product finite ring. Let
\Sigma
be a network over
R
, and
\Sigma_{i}
,
i=1,2
be two networks over
R_{i}
respectively.
\Sigma
is said to be the product of
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
, denoted by
\Sigma=\Sigma_{1}\times\Sigma_{2}
, if for any
z_{0}\in R
,
z_{0}=x_{0}y_{0}
, where
x_{0}\in R_{1}
and
y_{0}
in
R_{2}
, the trajectories satisfy

\displaystyle z(t,z_{0})=x(t,x_{0})y(t,y_{0}),\quad t>0.

(171)

The following theorem, called the decomposition principle, shows that a network over a product ring is decomposable.

Theorem 44
.

(Decomposition Principle) Assume
\Sigma
is a (control) network over a product ring
R=R_{1}\times R_{2}
.
Define
\Sigma_{i}:=\phi_{i}(\Sigma),\quad i=1,2
.
Then

\displaystyle\Sigma=\Sigma_{1}\times\Sigma_{2}.

(172)

Proof
. Note that in a network over a finite ring, there are only three operators: addition, product, and minus. Consider an
n
-nod network and let
z_{s}(t)=x_{s}(t)y_{s}(t)
,
s\in[1,n]
. Using Lemma
42
, it is clear that at each step we have

\begin{array}[]{l}z_{i}(t)\oplus z_{j}(t)=[x_{i}(t)\oplus_{1}x_{j}(t)][y_{i}(t)\oplus_{2}y_{j}(t)],\\
z_{i}(t)\odot z_{j}(t)=[x_{i}(t)\odot_{1}x_{j}(t)][y_{i}(t)\odot_{2}y_{j}(t)],\\
\neg z_{s}(t)=[\neg x_{s}(t)][\neg y_{s}(t)],\quad i,j,s\in[1,n].\\
\end{array}

Hence, such a decomposition is also available for any polynomial, which contains only these three operators. It follows that

z_{s}(t+1)=x_{s}(t+1)y_{s}(t+1),\quad s\in[1,n].

The conclusion follows.

\Box

Representation Theorem

The purpose of this section is to show that all the
\kappa
-valued logical networks can be expressed as networks over finite ring

\kappa

Remark 55
.

From the proof of Theorem
52
(or Example
54
) one sees easily that in the representation of arbitrary finite network to networks over
{\mathbb{Z}}^{\kappa}
the operators over factor rings
{\mathbb{Z}}_{k_{i}}

are also used. Therefore, precisely speaking, an arbitrary finite network (with
\kappa
elements) can be expressed into a network over
{\mathbb{Z}}^{\kappa}
and its factor rings. This is because the projections
\phi_{i}:{\mathbb{Z}}^{\kappa}\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}_{k_{i}}
are used in the presentation. But it does not matter in most applications. For instance, decomposition principle is still available. Because in the decomposing process, the projection becomes either identity, if the projected sub-ring coincides with the object ring of the decomposition, or zero, if the projected sub-ring is not the object ring of the decomposition.

11
Conclusion

In this paper the networks over finite rings are proposed and investigated. Using STP, the algebraic conditions are obtained for verifying whether a finite set with two operators is a (commutative) ring, which provides a method to construct more finite (commutative) rings. Then the networks over finite rings are investigated. The networks over finite rings are obvious generalization of the networks over finite fields discussed in some current literature, and it significantly enlarged the application area of finite networks. It is also shown that all existing STP based technique developed for multi and mix valued logical (control) networks are applicable to networks over finite rings.

Then we show that if the bearing finite ring of a network has a proper idea, then a sub-network can be obtained. This sub-network is exactly an invariant subspace for the dynamics of the original (control) network.

Moreover, the product rings are introduced. Its properties are explored. A set of important product rings, denoted by
{\mathbb{Z}}^{\kappa}
are introduced, which is the product of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{k_{i}}
, where
k_{i}
are prime facts of
\kappa
. Using product rings, the decomposition of networks over product rings into its factor rings is revealed. This decomposition theorem is called the decomposition principle. Decomposition principle can reduce the computational complexity of networks over finite rings by investigating sub-networks over factor rings to analyse and control the overall network. Then the control problems for networks over finite rings are investigated via its factor sub-networks using decomposition principle. Particularly, the linear control networks over finite rings are analyzed in detail.

Finally, the representation theorem is presented, which shows that any finite network over finite set
S
with
|S|=\kappa
can be expressed as a network over product ring
{\mathbb{Z}}^{\kappa}
. This result is powerful, because it claims that the technique developed in this paper for networks over finite rings is applicable to arbitrary finite networks.

For the sake of applications, the most challenging and urgent issue to be solved is to develop an easily computable technique to convert an (arbitrary) finite network into a network over
{\mathbb{Z}}^{\kappa}
.
