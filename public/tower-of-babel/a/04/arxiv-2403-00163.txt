Uniformly Decaying Subspaces for Error Mitigated Quantum Computation
Nishchay Suri, Jason Saied, Davide Venturelli

Abstract
We present a general condition to obtain subspaces that decay uniformly in a system governed by the Lindblad master equation and use them to perform error mitigated quantum computation. The expectation values of dynamics encoded in such subspaces are unbiased estimators of noise-free expectation values. In analogy to the decoherence free subspaces which are left invariant by the action of Lindblad operators, we show that the uniformly decaying subspaces are left invariant (up to orthogonal terms) by the action of the dissipative part of the Lindblad equation. We apply our theory to a system of qubits and qudits undergoing relaxation with varying decay rates and show that such subspaces can be used to eliminate bias up to first order variations in the decay rates without requiring full knowledge of noise. Since such a bias cannot be corrected through standard symmetry verification, our method can improve error mitigation in dual-rail qubits and given partial knowledge of noise, can perform better than probabilistic error cancellation.

While significant efforts are being made to drive down the error rates at the hardware level, continued progress is taking place in the fields of quantum error correction (QEC) and quantum error mitigation (QEM) to combat realistic noise with the aim of using the current noisy intermediate scale quantum (NISQ) hardware to its full potential.

Powerful methods to protect quantum information were developed such as by encoding it in decoherence free subspaces (DFS) that are completely immune to noise

;

;
McClean et al. 2017
;
McArdle et al. 2019
are effective at identifying and removing specific errors making the system immune to noise that breaks the symmetry of the quantum state, but have no way of removing symmetry-preserving errors.
In Ref.

, the author develops a sampling based method allowing for partial cancellation of errors undetectable by symmetry verification.
Probabilistic Error Cancellation (PEC) on the other hand, can completely eliminate bias, however demands full knowledge of noise that may be hard to attain

;
Cai et al. 2022
.

In this article, we show that for certain realistic local noise for which a DFS offering complete protection may not exist, we can obtain subspaces that are uniformly affected by noise. The expectation values of dynamics encoded in such
Uniformly Decaying Subspaces
(UDS) are unbiased estimators of noise-free expectation values and therefore can be used for error mitigation.
Specifically, where DFS are left invariant by the action of Lindblad operators, we show that the uniformly decaying subspaces are left invariant (up to orthogonal terms) by the action of the dissipative part of the Lindblad equation.
We present the general conditions for construction of such subspaces and apply our theory to a system of qubits and qudits undergoing strong individual relaxation with varying decay rates.
The varying decay rates introduce a bias in estimation which cannot be detected through standard symmetry verification. We show that such a bias can be eliminated up to first order variations in decay rates using an ensemble of runs without requiring the knowledge of individual decay rates.
Lastly we apply our theory to implement the transverse field Ising model (TFIM) in analog and circuit-model settings using dual-rail qubits

;
Kubica et al. 2023
;
Teoh et al. 2023
;
Chou et al. 2023
;
Campbell et al. 2020
;
Levine et al. 2023
.
Our method improves on the standard symmetry verification for QEM used in dual-rail qubits and given partial knowledge of noise, can perform better than probabilistic error cancellation.
We note that some earlier works have pointed out that coherences or off-diagonal parts of density matrix can decay with the same rate in

Preliminaries-

Consider a quantum system described by a density operator
\rho
interacting with the environment. Under the standard assumptions that a Markovian environment started to couple to the system at some point in the past, the dynamics is completely positive and trace preserving, described by the Lindblad equation of the form

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\rho=\mathcal{H}\rho+\mathcal{D}\rho\;,

(1a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\rho

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\gamma_{i}\mathcal{D}[A_{i}]\rho\;,

(1b)

where
\mathcal{H}\rho=-i[H,\rho]
describes the unitarily evolving part under the Hamiltonian
H
and
\mathcal{D}[A]\rho=A\rho A^{\dagger}-\{A^{\dagger}A,\rho\}/2
is the dissipative part with a general Lindblad operator
A
.
We re-write the above equation in the Liouville space or third-quantization formalism
Prosen 2008
;
Krishna et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2014
as
|{\dot{\rho}}\rrangle=\hat{\mathcal{L}}|{\rho}\rrangle\;,
where
\hat{\mathcal{L}}=\hat{\mathcal{H}}+\hat{\mathcal{D}}
.
We review the Liouville formalism in Appendix
A
.
The expectation value of observable
O
is then given as

\displaystyle\langle O(t)\rangle=\llangle{O}|e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}t}|\rho(0)\rrangle=\sum_{\alpha}e^{\lambda_{\alpha}t}\llangle{\ell_{\alpha}}|\rho(0)\rrangle\llangle O|{r_{\alpha}}\rrangle\;,

(2)

where
|{\ell_{\alpha}}\rrangle,|{r_{\alpha}}\rrangle
are bi-orthonormalized left and right eigenvectors of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
and
\lambda_{\alpha}
is the corresponding eigenvalue.

Figure 1:
(a) The distribution of eigenvalues of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
in the complex plane, with the black and blue circles indicating coherent and noisy dynamics and the corresponding (b) expectation values plotted with time. (c) The distribution of eigenvalues of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
for the uniformly decaying subspace marked by green circles and the corresponding (d) expectation value plotted with time.

Given the Hamiltonian
H
such that
H\ket{E_\alpha}=E_{\alpha}\ket{E_\alpha},
the eigenvalues of the Liouville operator for coherent dynamics are purely imaginary, given by
\lambda^{C}_{\alpha\beta}=-i(E_{\alpha}-E_{\beta})
and marked by black circles in Fig.
1
(a). The presence of noise causes the eigenvalues to be scattered in the complex plane as marked by the blue circles forcing different parts of the Hilbert space to decay at different rates and ruining the system’s coherence. As an example, one qubit experiencing relaxation with decay rate
\gamma
already has two different decaying timescales
T_{1}=1/\gamma
for diagonal and
T_{2}=1/(2\gamma)
for off-diagonal parts of the density matrix. Coherence of systems is therefore preserved only for time scales
t\ll 1/\gamma
. The expectation value of an observable corresponding to the noisy and noise free cases are shown in Fig.
1
(b).

In cases with high symmetry, usually resulting from collective decoherence mechanisms, there can exist decoherence-free subspaces.
If the Lindblad operators
A_{i}
are normal and share a common set of eigenvectors
\ket{\mc P_j}
, where
A_{i}\ket{\mc P_j}=c_{i}\ket{\mc P_j}\forall i,j
, then the subspace
\mathcal{S}
spanned by
\{\ket{\mc P_j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
is protected from decoherence as
\mathcal{D}\ket{\mc P_i}\bra{\mc P_j}=0

Lidar et al. 1998
.
The later work
Karasik et al. 2008
obtained a necessary and sufficient condition for the above when the
A_{i}
are not necessarily normal, by noting that in addition to the previous constraint,
\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}A_{i}^{\dagger}A_{i}
should also act by a scalar on
\mathcal{S}
:
\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}A_{i}^{\dagger}A_{i}\ket{\mc P_j}=\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}|c_{i}|^{2}\ket{\mc P_j}
. Further, if
H
leaves
\mathcal{S}
invariant, then the subspace
\mathcal{S}
is a DFS. Then, the Lindbladian is composed of only the Hamiltonian term, and the dynamics is identical to the noise-free case in Fig.
1
(a),(b).

Uniformly Decaying Subspaces-

Analogous to the DFS, for certain special cases if instead the entire Lindblad dissipator operator
\mathcal{D}
leaves a set of vectors
\ket{\mc P_i}\bra{\mc P_j}
unaffected up to an orthogonal component, then there exists a uniformly decaying subspace
\mathcal{S}
that is spanned by
\{\ket{\mc P_j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
.
The condition is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\ket{\mc P_i}\bra{\mc P_j}\oplus\mathcal{P}^{\perp})=-\gamma\ket{\mc P_i}\bra{\mc P_j}\oplus\mathcal{P}^{\prime\perp}\;,

(3)

\forall i,j
where
\mathcal{P}^{\perp},\mathcal{P}^{\prime\perp}
are in a subspace orthogonal to operators on
\mathcal{S}
.
A sufficient condition can be obtained in terms of the Lindblad operators in a similar spirit to DFS.
Instead of the eigenvalue condition, we require that
A_{i}\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{S}^{\perp}
for any
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{S}\oplus\mathcal{S}^{\perp}
, where the subspace
\mathcal{S}^{\perp}
is orthogonal to
\mathcal{S}
.
Similarly to DFS, we require
\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}A_{i}^{\dagger}A_{i}
to act by a scalar
-\gamma
on
\mathcal{S}
:
\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}A_{i}^{\dagger}A_{i}\ket{\mc P_j}=-\gamma\ket{\mc P_j}
.
We will later loosen this second restrictive condition by showing that the UDS can be made resistant to first order deviations from the scalar
-\gamma
, using the example of inhomogeneous qubit and qudit relaxation.
More general discussion is provided in Appendix
B
.

The condition Eq.
3
for uniformly decaying subspaces can be rewritten in the Liouville formalism as
\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{D}}=-\gamma\hat{\mathcal{P}}
, where
\hat{\mathcal{P}}=P\otimes{P^{T}}
and
P=\sum_{i}\ket{\mc P_i}\bra{\mc P_i}
is the projector onto
\mathcal{S}
. We encode the initial density matrix and observable in the subspace:
|{\rho(0)}\rrangle=\hat{\mathcal{P}}|{\rho(0)}\rrangle
and
|{O}\rrangle=\hat{\mathcal{P}}|{O}\rrangle
. We require that our chosen Hamiltonian must commute with
P
, implying
\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}=\hat{\mathcal{H}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}=\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}
. Using the conditions above, we evaluate the expectation value of the observable in the subspace after evolving for a time
t

\displaystyle\langle O(t)\rangle_{\text{UDS}}

\displaystyle=\llangle{O}|\hat{\mathcal{P}}e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}t}\hat{\mathcal{P}}|{\rho(0)}\rrangle\;,

(4a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\llangle{O}|\hat{\mathcal{P}}(\hat{\mathcal{H}}-\gamma)^{n}\hat{\mathcal{P}}|{\rho(0)}\rrangle\;,

(4b)

\displaystyle=e^{-\gamma t}\langle O\rangle_{C}\;,

(4c)

where
\langle O\rangle_{C}\equiv\llangle{O}|e^{\hat{\mathcal{H}}t}|{\rho(0)}\rrangle
is the noise-free or coherent expectation value. We therefore see that the expectation value of an observable encoded in the subspace is an unbiased estimator of noise-free expectation value up to an overall decaying factor. The eigenvalues of the encoded dynamics are
\lambda_{\alpha\beta}=-\gamma+i(E_{\alpha}-E_{\beta})
shown with green circles in Fig.
1
(c) and are identical those for coherent dynamics translated by a common constant. The measured expectation value in the subspace is shown in Fig.
1
(d) with the green line which when rescaled gives back the coherent dynamics shown with the dashed black line.

We estimate the expectation value of the observable using
N_{\text{UDS}}
number of shots with the estimator
\langle\hat{O}\rangle_{\text{UDS}}=\sum_{i}\langle O\rangle_{\text{UDS},i}/{N_{\text{UDS}}}
, where
\langle O\rangle_{\text{UDS},i}
is the
i^{\text{th}}
measurement outcome. To obtain the estimate with the same confidence as that of the noise-free case we require that
\text{Var}\langle\hat{O}\rangle_{\text{UDS}}=\text{Var}\langle\hat{O}\rangle_{\text{C}}
, where
\langle\hat{O}\rangle_{\text{C}}=\sum_{i}\langle O\rangle_{C,i}/{N_{C}}
is the estimator for noise-free case. Then, we use Eq.
4c
to obtain the sampling overhead
N_{\text{UDS}}/N_{C}=e^{2\gamma t}
. The elimination of bias therefore comes at the cost of requiring exponentially more measurements compared to the noise free case, which is often true for error mitigation methods
Cai et al. 2022
.

The observable
\hat{\mathcal{P}}|{O}\rrangle
or equivalently
POP
in the UDS subspace can be written in Pauli-decomposition as
POP=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\alpha_{i}\bm{\sigma}_{i}
, where each Pauli string
\bm{\sigma}_{i}\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
can be measured separately. Monte Carlo sampling can be used to achieve the sampling overhead
(\sum_{i=1}^{K}|\alpha_{i}|)^{2}

Cai et al. 2022
.
Alternatively, we can measure
O
after measuring
P
, post-selecting for the case
P=1
, using an ancilla qubit, which follows the scheme behind symmetry verification
Bonet-Monroig et al. 2018
;
Bonet-Monroig et al. 2018
;
McClean et al. 2017
;
McArdle et al. 2019
.
For example, for each dual rail qubit
Kubica et al. 2023
;
Levine et al. 2023
where
{\ket{0}_{L}\equiv\ket{01},\ket{1}_{L}\equiv\ket{10}}
, an additional ancilla qubit is required to check for decay to
\ket{00}
state by measuring
P=I-\ket{00}\bra{00}
. The state is measured in the computational subspace
\mathcal{S}
with probability
\llangle P|e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}t}|\rho(0)\rrangle=e^{-\gamma t}
, and the post-selected expectation value is
\langle O(t)\rangle_{\text{UDS}}^{\text{Post}}=\llangle O|\hat{\mathcal{P}}e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}t}\hat{\mathcal{P}}|\rho(0)\rrangle/\llangle P|e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}t}|\rho(0)\rrangle=\langle O\rangle_{C}
.
The uniformly decaying part now represents the exponentially decreasing probability of finding the system in the correct subspace.
Any errors taking the system out of the computational subspace are removed by post-selection. Under post-selection, the UDS conditions can be viewed as equivalent to that of symmetry verification with perfect error detection. We now extend its applicability by allowing for perturbations undetectable by symmetry verification.

Qubits/qudits under inhomogeneous relaxation-
We apply our theory to
n
qubits subject to strong individual relaxation, described by Eq.
1
with the dissipator operators
A_{i}=\sigma_{i}^{-}=\ket{0}\bra{1}
and decay rates given by
\gamma_{i}
.
Such noise is a generic realistic scenario in many NISQ devices for example, in cQED superconducting qubits
Yan et al. 2018
;
Burnett et al. 2019
and photonic qubits where the dominant error is photon loss
Teoh et al. 2023
;
Chou et al. 2023
.
In transmon qubits, such a noise bias can be engineered by operating them in resonance forming the dual-rail qubits with relaxation as the dominant error
Kubica et al. 2023
;
Campbell et al. 2020
;
Levine et al. 2023
. Moreover, dynamical decoupling can be used to further suppress dephasing leading to an even greater increase in the bias
Viola et al. 1999
;
Lidar 2014
;
De Lange et al. 2010
;
Biercuk et al. 2009
. The decay rates for different qubits are different and even fluctuate over time
Carroll et al. 2022
.

We assume that we do not know the individual decay rates
\gamma_{i}
and only have the knowledge of the average decay rate
\bar{\gamma}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\gamma_{i}
. We parameterize
\gamma_{i}=\bar{\gamma}+\Delta\gamma_{i}
and write Eq.
1
as
\mathcal{L}\rho=\mathcal{L}_{0}\rho+\mathcal{L}_{1}\rho\;,
where

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{0}\rho

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}\rho+\bar{\gamma}\sum_{i}\mathcal{D}[\sigma_{i}^{-}]\rho\;,

(5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{1}\rho

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\Delta\gamma_{i}\mathcal{D}[\sigma_{i}^{-}]\rho\;.

(6)

We provide the solution to the above equations by solving for the
\mathcal{L}_{0}\rho
term exactly and treat the second term
\mathcal{L}_{1}\rho
perturbatively. We require that the decay timescales of qubits are close to one another or
|\Delta\gamma|_{\max}\ll\bar{\gamma},\|H\|
.
We have
\|\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\|=|\Delta\gamma|_{\max}=\max_{i}|\Delta\gamma_{i}|
. The condition above poses no restrictions on the average decay time scales, which in principle can be large.

We consider the basis
\ket{\mc P_{\bm\alpha}}=\ket{\alpha_1 \dots\alpha_n}
, where
\alpha_{i}\in\{0,1\}
denotes the state of the
i\text{th}
qubit.
We define our computational subspace
\mathcal{S}_{k}
as the space spanned by all
\ket{\mc P_{\bm\alpha}}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}=k
.
In other words, each vector in this constant Hamming weight subspace has
k
qubits in state
\ket{1}
and
n-k
qubits in state
\ket{0}
.
Therefore the dimension of the computational subspace is given by
N=\text{dim}(\mathcal{S}_{k})={n\choose k}
.
We see that the dissipation operator of the unperturbed term
\mathcal{D}_{0}\equiv\bar{\gamma}\sum_{i}\mathcal{D}[\sigma_{i}^{-}]
satisfies Eq.
3
with
\gamma=k\bar{\gamma}
where
\mathcal{S}^{\perp}=\oplus_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathcal{S}_{i}
.
Following the rest of the derivation, we see from Eq.
4
that
\langle O(t)\rangle_{0}=\llangle{O}|\hat{\mathcal{P}}e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}_{0}}t}\hat{\mathcal{P}}|{\rho(0)}\rrangle=e^{-k\bar{\gamma}t}\langle O\rangle_{C}
. Therefore, given
n
qubits decaying with the same rate, we can obtain an unbiased estimation of expectation values by encoding the dynamics in
\mathcal{S}_{k}
. It is worthwhile to note that even for this apparently symmetric unperturbed term
\mathcal{D}_{0}
, there exists only a trivial DFS with one ket
\ket{0\dots 0}
, which cannot be used for computation.

We now consider the second term
\mathcal{L}_{1}
perturbatively, calculating the bias in the estimation of expectation value of the observable
\langle O\rangle
as
\Delta\langle O\rangle=\langle O\rangle_{\text{UDS}}-e^{-k\bar{\gamma}t}\langle O\rangle_{C}
. We have

\displaystyle\Delta\langle O\rangle

\displaystyle=\llangle O|e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t^{\prime})|{\rho(0)}\rrangle+\mathcal{O}(|\Delta\gamma|_{\max}^{2})\;,

(7)

where
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t)\equiv e^{-\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}
.
We see that the variation of decay rates introduces additional bias that cannot be detected just by symmetry-verification.
We define a unitary translation operator
T
that shifts the qubits by one position, acting on the basis as
T\ket{\alpha_1 \alpha_2 \dots\alpha_{n-1}\alpha_n}=\ket{\alpha_n\alpha_1 \dots\alpha_{n-2}\alpha_{n-1}}
, which is also equivalent to qubit relabeling. We use this shifted basis for computation, i.e. rotate the initial state, Hamiltonian and observable by
T
, and see that it leaves the unperturbed term invariant and only affects the perturbed term as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{1}(T\rho T^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=T\sum_{i}\Delta\gamma_{i-1}\mathcal{D}[\sigma_{i}^{-}](\rho)T^{\dagger}\;.

(8a)

If we consider an ensemble of
n
evolutions with encodings shifted by one each corresponding to
T^{j}
where
j=0,\dots,n-1
and take the average of expectation values, the first order term is proportional to
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Delta\gamma_{i-j}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Delta\gamma_{k}=0
as
\gamma_{i}=\bar{\gamma}+\Delta\gamma_{i}
. The bias
\Delta\langle O\rangle
after taking the average over
n
evolutions is given by

\displaystyle{e^{k\bar{\gamma}t}\llangle O\rrangle_{\text{UDS}}-\langle O\rangle_{C}=\mathcal{O}\bigg({k^{2}|\Delta\gamma|_{\max}^{2}t^{2}\|O\|}\bigg)\;,}

(9)

where
\llangle O\rrangle_{\text{UDS}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\langle O\rangle_{j,\text{UDS}}
. We therefore see that the bias is only second order in deviation of decay rates. The cancellation works similarly for the post selected case. Importantly, since the bias only depends on the deviation of the decay rates, and not on the magnitude of their average, it is possible to lower the bias arbitrarily by worsening the decay rates of all qubits until they align with the fastest decaying qubit, for example by tuning operating frequencies of the qubits. This comes at the expense of requiring more shots as the average decay rate increases. It is also noteworthy to point out that the knowledge of individual decay rates is not required to achieve this cancellation.

Furthermore, we also consider the more general case of amplitude damping channels where
A_{i}=a_{i}
is the the bosonic annihilation operator. This noise model is especially important for photonic
d
-level qudits as photon loss is the dominant source of noise
Chuang et al. 1997
;
Gardiner and Zoller 2004
.
Our calculations generalize in a straightforward manner, with the details given in Appendix
B
.

Examples-
We now illustrate the QEM method by simulating the random transverse field Ising model (TFIM) in analog and circuit-model settings. Given
n
physical qubits, we choose
k=n/2
to maximize the encoding efficiency, however we restrict our computational subspace to dual-rail qubits

\displaystyle H=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij=1}^{n_{L}}J_{ij}Z_{i}^{L}Z_{j}^{L}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L}}h^{x}_{i}X^{L}_{i}\;,

i^{\text{th}}
qubit where

We can see that it preserves the dual-rail subspace and therefore commutes with the projector
P
to that subspace. We take
100

from a Gaussian distribution with mean

B.5
.

For completeness, we remark that in the example above, the observable commutes with individual
Z_{i}
, allowing for the simultaneous measurement of
O
and
Z_{i}
. This need not be the case in general. For example, if we choose
O=PX_{0}^{L}P
, since
X_{0}^{L}
does not commute with individual
Z_{i}
, we therefore cannot measure
O
and
Z_{i}
simultaneously. One option is to use post-selection, i.e. use an ancilla qubit to first measure each
P_{i}
and only proceed to measure
X_{0}^{L}
when
P_{i}=1
. Note that this requires additional number
n_{L}
of ancilla qubits. This can be done at the circuit level by requiring
2n_{L}
CNOT gates to measure
P

Bonet-Monroig et al. 2018
or done at pulse level in the hardware directly
Kubica et al. 2023
;
Levine et al. 2023
. Alternatively, since
O
is a sum of Paulis, explicitly
O=\frac{1}{4}(X_{0}X_{1}+Y_{0}Y_{1}-X_{0}X_{1}Z_{2}Z_{3}-Y_{0}Y_{1}Z_{2}Z_{3})
, it can be measured efficiently by measuring individual terms or using Monte Carlo sampling
Cai et al. 2022
, without any additional qubit overhead.

Although the example above discusses the implementation of the TFIM Hamiltonian specifically, we note that the UDS method and the first order cancellation is applicable for any arbitrary quantum circuit as the dual rail qubits can implement universal quantum computation
Lidar 2014
. The dual rail encoding requires
2n_{L}
physical qubits to encode
n_{L}
logical qubits. We can realize an arbitrary SU(2) rotation on the logical qubit
i
by using the Euler rotations
e^{i\gamma X_{i}^{L}}e^{i\beta Z_{i}^{L}}e^{i\alpha X_{i}^{L}}
, which together with logical control Z gate
CZ^{L}_{i,j}=CZ_{2i+1,2j+1}
form a universal gate set. This requires native XY gate and control Z operations, readily available in many platforms. Additionally, to perform the first order cancellation, we require the physical qubits to be connected in a ring topology to implement evolution with respect to the shifted basis. We can see this by considering the shifted Hamiltonian
THT^{\dagger}
, which requires XY connectivity between the first and last physical qubit. For measurements as discussed above, no ancilla qubit is required if we use Monte Carlo sampling, and
n_{L}
ancilla qubits are needed if we use post-selection to measure each
P_{i}
first.

Discussion-

We presented conditions to obtain subspaces that decay uniformly and used them to perform error mitigated quantum computation. Where DFS are left invariant by the action of Lindblad operators, we show that the uniformly decaying subspaces are left invariant (up to orthogonal terms) by the action of the dissipative part of the Lindblad equation. For localized noise where a DFS offering complete protection may not exist, we show that we can still encode quantum information into UDS which can be used for error mitigation. We applied our theory to a system of qubits undergoing strong individual relaxation with varying decay rates and showed that the first order bias can be eliminated using an ensemble of runs without requiring the knowledge of individual decay rates. The subspaces therefore can be used to obtain unbiased estimators of the true expectation value up to second order variations in decay rates. We demonstrated our method by implementing the transverse field Ising model in analog and digital settings obtaining lower bias estimates as compared to symmetry verification and probabilistic error cancellation methods.
Lastly, our framework can be extended to other noise models, such as correlated decays with
A_{ij}=\sigma_{i}^{-}\otimes\sigma_{j}^{-}
, which similarly give a UDS with cancellation of first-order errors, discussed in Appendix
B
. However, the cancellation becomes less straightforward with inefficient scaling. Finally, the UDS method may also be naturally suited for Fermionic Hamiltonian simulation problems that naturally conserve particle number
Setia and Whitfield 2018
;
Bravyi and Kitaev 2002
. The techniques introduced open up new possibilities in achieving lower biases than previously possible with symmetry verification for error mitigation, without requiring complete knowledge of noise. Since our method is a passive encoding scheme, it can be used as a base encoding with other QEM or QEC methods.

Appendix A
Liouville Space Representation

Let
\mathcal{W}
be a Hilbert space of pure states. For any subspace
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
, let
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{V})=\textnormal{End}(V)
denote the space of linear operators
\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}
.

For
\rho\in\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{W})
, the Lindblad master equation is given as
\dot{\rho}=\mathcal{L}\rho=\mathcal{H}\rho+\mathcal{D}\rho\;,
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\rho

\displaystyle=-i[H,\rho]\;,

(11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\rho

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}\mathcal{D}[A_{i}]\rho\;,

(12)

where
\mathcal{D}[A]\rho=A\rho A^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{A^{\dagger}A,\rho\}
.
In many scenarios, analyzing the equation and its evolution becomes easier in the vectorized or Liouville space representation also known as the third-quantization. The rows of
N\times N
density matrix
\rho
can be stacked on top of one another to obtain its vectorized form
|{\rho}\rrangle
with dimension
N^{2}\times 1
, identifying
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{V})
with
V\otimes V^{*}
. As an example the
2\times 2
density matrix transforms as
\rho=\begin{bmatrix}\rho_{00}&\rho_{01}\\
\rho_{10}&\rho_{11}\end{bmatrix}\iff|{\rho}\rrangle=\begin{bmatrix}\rho_{00}&\rho_{01}&\rho_{10}&\rho_{11}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
.
Given a superoperator
\mathcal{F}
on
{\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{V})}
, we denote the corresponding operator in the Liouville space representation as
\hat{\mathcal{F}}
.
Using the operator transformation rule
A\rho B\iff A\otimes B^{T}|{\rho}\rrangle
, we can write the above Lindblad master equation in the Liouville space
|{\dot{\rho}}\rrangle=\hat{\mathcal{L}}|{\rho}\rrangle\;,
where

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{H}}+\hat{\mathcal{D}}=-i(H\otimes I-I\otimes H^{T})+\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}\bigg(A_{i}\otimes A_{i}^{*}-\frac{1}{2}(A_{i}^{\dagger}A_{i}\otimes I+I\otimes A_{i}^{T}A_{i}^{*})\bigg)\;.

(13)

We can now diagonalize the superoperator

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}|{r_{\alpha}}\rrangle

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\alpha}|{r_{\alpha}}\rrangle\;,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\dagger}|{l_{\alpha}}\rrangle

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\alpha}^{*}|{l_{\alpha}}\rrangle\;,

(14)

where
|{r_{\alpha}}\rrangle,|{l_{\alpha}}\rrangle
are the corresponding right and left eigenvectors and
\lambda_{\alpha}
is the eigenvalue. Bi-orthonormality can be imposed such that
\llangle{l_{\alpha}}|r_{\beta}\rrangle=\delta_{\alpha\beta}
, where the inner product in the Liouville space is equivalent to the one defined for operators
\llangle A|B\rrangle=\tr(A^\dagger B)
. The evolution of the density matrix is then given as

\displaystyle|{\rho(t)}\rrangle=\sum_{\alpha}e^{\lambda_{\alpha}t}\llangle{l_{\alpha}}|\rho(0)\rrangle|{r_{\alpha}}\rrangle\;{.}

(15)

The expectation value of an observable can be calculated as

\displaystyle\langle O\rangle=\llangle{O}|\rho(t)\rrangle=\sum_{\alpha}e^{\lambda_{\alpha}t}\llangle{l_{\alpha}}|\rho(0)\rrangle\llangle O|{r_{\alpha}}\rrangle\;.

(16)

We can also write the Lindblad-like form for the corresponding Liouville representation of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\dagger}
given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\rho=+i[H,\rho]+\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}[A_{i}]\rho\;,

(17)

where
\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}[A]\rho=A^{\dagger}\rho A-\frac{1}{2}\{A^{\dagger}A,\rho\}\;.

Appendix B
Uniformly Decaying Subspaces

B.1
General Derivation

We present the general conditions to achieve Uniformly Decaying Subspaces (UDS).
As above, we let
\mathcal{W}
be the entire (pure) state space and let
\mathcal{V}
be a chosen subspace.
The expectation value of an observable
O
is given as

\displaystyle\langle O\rangle=\llangle{O}|e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}t}|{\rho(0)}\rrangle\;,

(18)

where
{\hat{\mathcal{L}}}=\hat{\mathcal{H}}+\hat{\mathcal{D}}
is a general vectorized Lindblad operator.
Let
\mathcal{S}
be a subspace of the state space
\mathcal{V}
, with space of operators
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{S})=\textnormal{End}(\mathcal{S})
and projector
P
onto
\mathcal{S}
.
We write
{\mathcal{P}}
to denote the corresponding projector onto
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{S})
, with

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\rho)

\displaystyle=P\rho P

(19)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{P}}|{\rho}\rrangle

\displaystyle=P\otimes{P^{T}}|{\rho}\rrangle

(20)

in the operator and Liouville space representations respectively.
We will use the notation
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{S})^{\perp}
to denote the orthogonal complement of
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{S})
in
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{V})
(not the full orthogonal complement in
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{W})
).
We obtain a uniformly decaying subspace if

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{D}}=-\gamma\hat{\mathcal{P}}

(21)

as operators on
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{V})
.
In other words,
\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{D}}
is a diagonalizable operator on
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{V})
, with eigenvalue
-\gamma
on
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{S})
and eigenvalue
0
on
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{S})^{\perp}
.
We will often find it more intuitive to decompose this into two equalities:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{D}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}

\displaystyle=-\gamma\hat{\mathcal{P}}\;,

(22)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{D}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\;,

(23)

as operators on
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{V})
.
The first tells us that
\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{D}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}
has eigenvalue
-\gamma
on
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{S})
.
The second tells us that
\hat{\mathcal{D}}
maps
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{S})^{\perp}
to
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{S})^{\perp}
.
Viewing
\hat{\mathcal{D}}
as a matrix, we have

\hat{\mathcal{D}}=\begin{bmatrix}-\gamma I&0\\
*&*\end{bmatrix}.

(24)

In more mathematical language, we would say that
\hat{\mathcal{D}}
leaves the spaces
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{V})
and
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{S})^{\perp}
invariant, and therefore that
\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{D}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}
is an operator on the quotient space
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{V})/\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{S})^{\perp}
(which is isomorphic to
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{S})
as a vector space).
Eq. (
22
) is the assumption that this operator on the quotient space is a scalar.

We construct a Hamiltonian that commutes with
\mathcal{P}
, implying

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}=\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}=\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\;.

(25)

We encode the initial state and observable in the subspace
\mathcal{S}
, taking

\displaystyle|{\rho(0)}\rrangle

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{P}}|{\rho(0)}\rrangle\;,

\displaystyle|{O}\rrangle

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{P}}|{O}\rrangle\;.

(26)

Using Eq.
26
, we rewrite the expectation value

\displaystyle\langle O\rangle_{\text{UDS}}=\llangle{O}|\hat{\mathcal{P}}e^{{\hat{\mathcal{L}}}t}\hat{\mathcal{P}}|{\rho(0)}\rrangle=\sum_{n}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\llangle{O}|\hat{\mathcal{P}}(\hat{\mathcal{H}}+\hat{\mathcal{D}})^{n}\hat{\mathcal{P}}|{\rho(0)}\rrangle=\llangle{O}|e^{\hat{\mathcal{P}}{\hat{\mathcal{L}}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}t}|{\rho(0)}\rrangle=e^{-\gamma t}\langle O\rangle_{C}\;,

(27)

where
\langle O\rangle_{C}\equiv\llangle{O}|e^{\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}t}|{\rho(0)}\rrangle
is the expectation value of the observable in the coherent case without any dissipation. We used Eq.
23
and Eq.
25
to obtain the second last equality. We can see that the decay part factors out and the effective evolution inside the uniformly decaying subspace is coherent up to the overall scaling factor.

B.2
Sufficient conditions using Lindblad equation

We now consider the
Lindblad equation

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}=\mathcal{L}\rho=\mathcal{H}\rho+\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}\gamma_{\beta}\mathcal{D}[A_{\beta}]\rho\;,

(28)

where
\mathcal{B}
is some index set and each
A_{\beta}
is an operator on the state space
\mathcal{W}
.
We will give sufficient conditions on the operators
A_{\beta}
to obtain a UDS as above.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be the associative algebra generated by the
A_{\beta}
and
A_{\beta}^{\dagger}A_{\beta}
(note we do not generally have
A_{\beta}^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{A}
).
Locate a pair of nested subspaces
\mathcal{S}^{\perp}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
of
\mathcal{W}
that are invariant subspaces for the algebra
\mathcal{A}
,
and let
\mathcal{S}
(the computational subspace) be the orthogonal complement to
\mathcal{S}^{\perp}
in
\mathcal{V}
, with projector
\mathcal{P}
.
We in fact add the stronger assumption that
A_{\beta}
maps
\mathcal{V}
into
\mathcal{S}^{\perp}
.
Graphically, we summarize these conditions as

\mathcal{V}\xrightarrow{A_{\beta}^{\dagger}A_{\beta}}\mathcal{V},\,\hskip 72.26999pt\mathcal{S}^{\perp}\xrightarrow{A_{\beta}^{\dagger}A_{\beta}}\mathcal{S}^{\perp},\,\hskip 72.26999pt\mathcal{V}\xrightarrow{A_{\beta}}\mathcal{S}^{\perp}\xrightarrow{A_{\beta}}\mathcal{S}^{\perp}.

(29)

Equivalently, for all
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{V}
and all indices
\beta
, we have:

\displaystyle PA_{\beta}\ket{\psi}=0,

(30)

\displaystyle PA_{\beta}^{\dagger}A_{\beta}\ket{\psi}=PA_{\beta}^{\dagger}A_{\beta}P\ket{\psi}.

(31)

We also assume that for some scalar
c>0
,

P\left(\sum_{\beta}A_{\beta}^{\dagger}A_{\beta}\right)P=cP.

(32)

Given the above assumptions, we let
\overline{\gamma}
be the average of the coefficients
\gamma_{\beta}
, and define

{\mathcal{D}_{0}}=\overline{\gamma}\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}{\mathcal{D}}[A_{\beta}]

(33)

(with corresponding Liouville space representation
{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}=\overline{\gamma}\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}{\hat{\mathcal{D}}}[A_{\beta}]
).
We will consider
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}=\hat{\mathcal{H}}+{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}
, where

\mathcal{H}\rho=-i[H,\rho],

(34)

corresponding to a Hamiltonian
H
on
\mathcal{V}
that commutes with
\mathcal{P}
.
Writing

\Delta\gamma_{\beta}=\gamma_{\beta}-\overline{\gamma},

(35)

we have
\hat{\mathcal{L}}=\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}+\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}
, where
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}=\sum_{\beta}\Delta\gamma_{\beta}{\hat{\mathcal{D}}}[A_{\beta}]
.

It is straightforward to see that the operator
{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}
satisfies the condition (
21
) discussed above; we give the calculation explicitly here.
Note that by (
30
) and (
31
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}{\mathcal{D}}[A_{\beta}](\rho)

\displaystyle=PA_{\beta}\rho A_{\beta}^{\dagger}P-\frac{1}{2}P\left(A_{\beta}^{\dagger}A_{\beta}\rho+\rho A_{\beta}^{\dagger}A_{\beta}\right)P

(36)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}P\left(A_{\beta}^{\dagger}A_{\beta}P\rho P+P\rho PA_{\beta}^{\dagger}A_{\beta}\right)P.

(37)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}{\mathcal{D}}[A_{\beta}]\mathcal{P}(\rho).

(38)

Summing the penultimate line over all
\beta
and applying (
32
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}{\mathcal{D}_{0}}(\rho)

\displaystyle={-\frac{1}{2}\overline{\gamma}\left(P\left(\sum_{\beta}A_{\beta}^{\dagger}A_{\beta}\right)P\rho P+P\rho P\left(\sum_{\beta}A_{\beta}^{\dagger}A_{\beta}\right)P\right)}

(39)

\displaystyle={-\frac{1}{2}c\overline{\gamma}(P\rho P+P\rho P)}

(40)

\displaystyle=-c\overline{\gamma}\mathcal{P}(\rho).

(41)

This is (
21
) with
\gamma=c\overline{\gamma}
.
Then by the calculations of Appendix
B.1
, we conclude that (
27
) holds for
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}=\hat{\mathcal{H}}+{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}
as above.

After considering some examples, we will discuss the effect of the perturbation term
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}
.
We note that the fact that
\hat{\mathcal{P}}{\hat{\mathcal{D}}}[A_{\beta}]=\hat{\mathcal{P}}{\hat{\mathcal{D}}}[A_{\beta}]\hat{\mathcal{P}}
implies that

\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}=\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\hat{\mathcal{P}}.

(42)

B.3
Main example: amplitude damping channel

We now consider the example of the bosonic amplitude damping channel
Chuang et al. 1997
, generalizing the qubit case considered in the main text.
Let
\mathcal{W}
be the state space of
n
qudits, each of dimension
d
.
Let
a=\sum_{m\geq 0}\sqrt{m}\outerproduct{m-1}{m}
be the bosonic annihilation operator, with
a_{i}
denoting the annihilation operator on qudit
i
.
We consider the Lindblad equation (
28
) with
\mathcal{B}=\{1,\dots,n\}
and
A_{i}=a_{i}
:

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{0}+\mathcal{L}_{1},\,\,\mathcal{L}_{1}=\sum_{i}\Delta\gamma_{i}{\mathcal{D}}[A_{i}],

(43)

where all notation is as above.
(Note that
d=2
gives the qubit case of the main text, with
a_{i}=\sigma_{i}^{-}
.)

Let the
degree
of a Fock basis vector
\ket{\alpha}=\ket{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_n}
be
\deg\ket{\alpha}=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}
,
and define
\mathcal{S}_{k}
to be the space spanned by all Fock basis vectors of degree
k
.
We fix a degree
k
and consider

\mathcal{V}=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{k}\mathcal{S}_{i},\,\,\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{k},\,\,\mathcal{S}^{\perp}=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathcal{S}_{i}.

(44)

It is easy to see that these operators satisfy the assumptions of the previous section.
In particular, (
29
) is immediate from the fact that
a_{i}
is degree-lowering and
a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}
is degree-preserving.
We obtain (
32
) by noting that

a^{\dagger}a=\sum_{m\geq 0}m\outerproduct{m}{m}

(45)

and therefore, on the degree-
k
subspace
\mathcal{S}_{k}
, we have

\sum_{i}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}\ket{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_n}=\left(\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\right)\ket{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_n}=k\ket{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_n}.

(46)

Thus we obtain (
27
) for
\mathcal{L}_{0}
, with
\gamma=\overline{\gamma}\deg\ket{\alpha}
.

We further note that, since the dual-rail subspace is a subspace of
\mathcal{S}_{k}
in the case
d=2
, the above also proves the UDS conditions are satisfied for that subspace.

B.3.1
Variant: unitary transformations

We also consider the following more general variant.

We express a general Fock basis vector in terms of creation operators,
\ket{\alpha_1 \cdots\alpha_n}=(a_{1}^{\dagger})^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots(a_{n}^{\dagger})^{\alpha_{n}}\ket{\vec{0}}
,
where
\ket{\vec{0}}=\ket{0\cdots 0}
is the vacuum state.
For any
n\times n
unitary matrix
U=(u_{ij})
,
we consider a degree or particle number preserving unitary transformation
\widetilde{U}
with the property
\widetilde{U}a_{j}^{\dagger}\widetilde{U}^{\dagger}=\sum_{i}u_{ij}a_{i}^{\dagger}
. In photonics, these are precisely the linear optical unitaries, a proper subgroup of the group of all photon number-preserving unitaries. Additionally, notice that
\widetilde{U}\ket{\vec{0}}=\ket{\vec{0}}
(since
\tilde{U}
preserves particle number, it does not change the vacuum state).
Explicitly,
\widetilde{U}
satisfies

\displaystyle{\widetilde{U}\ket{\alpha_1 \cdots\alpha_n}}

\displaystyle{=\widetilde{U}(a_{1}^{\dagger})^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots(a_{n}^{\dagger})^{\alpha_{n}}\ket{\vec{0}}}

\displaystyle{=(\widetilde{U}a_{1}^{\dagger}\widetilde{U}^{\dagger})^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots(\widetilde{U}a_{n}^{\dagger}\widetilde{U}^{\dagger})^{\alpha_{n}}\widetilde{U}\ket{\vec{0}}}

\displaystyle{=(u_{11}a_{1}^{\dagger}+u_{12}a_{2}^{\dagger}+\dots+u_{1n}a_{n}^{\dagger})^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots(u_{n1}a_{1}^{\dagger}+u_{n2}a_{2}^{\dagger}+\dots+u_{nn}a_{n}^{\dagger})^{\alpha_{n}}\ket{\vec{0}},}

where
a_{i}^{\dagger}
refers to the creation operator on the
i
th mode throughout.
Note that we have

{\widetilde{U}}a_{j}{\widetilde{U}}^{\dagger}=\sum_{i}u_{ij}^{*}{a_{i}},

a sum of annihilation operators on different qubits.

We now consider the case of (
28
) with
\mathcal{B}=\{1,\dots,n\}
and
A_{i}={\widetilde{U}}a_{i}{\widetilde{U}}^{\dagger}
.
We use the same subspaces
\mathcal{V}
,
\mathcal{S}_{k}
, etc., as in the previous example.
Since
A_{i}
is a sum of annihilation operators, it lowers the degree of Fock basis vectors by
1
, and (
32
) follows as before.
Note also that
{\widetilde{U}}
preserves the degree and therefore leaves the computational subspace
\mathcal{S}_{k}
of degree-
k
vectors invariant.
Then for (
32
), we have

\sum_{i}A_{i}^{\dagger}A_{i}\ket{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_n}=\sum_{i}{\widetilde{U}}a_{i}^{\dagger}{\widetilde{U}}^{\dagger}{\widetilde{U}}a_{i}{\widetilde{U}}^{\dagger}\ket{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_n}=\sum_{i}{\widetilde{U}}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}{\widetilde{U}}^{\dagger}\ket{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_n}=\deg\alpha\ket{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_n}=k\ket{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_n}.

(47)

We note that this example behaves very similarly to the above, but now the operators
A_{i}
are no longer required to correspond to single-qudit noise.

B.3.2
Correlated Decays

Another potential example corresponds to correlated decays, as in the Lindblad operator

\mathcal{L}\rho=\mathcal{H}\rho+\sum_{i\neq j}\gamma_{ij}{\mathcal{D}}[a_{i}a_{j}]\rho.

Note that

\sum_{i\neq j}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{i}a_{j}\ket{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_n}=\sum_{i\neq j}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}\ket{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_n}=\left(\left(\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\right)^{2}-\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}\right)\ket{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_n},

(48)

so in order to satisfy (
32
), we must change our computational subspace to the span of Fock basis vectors with a fixed nonzero “variance.”
(This distinction is irrelevant in the qubit case, since all
\alpha_{i}
are
0
or
1
.)
This change gives (
32
), and (
29
) is easily checked.

Alternatively, one may consider

\mathcal{L}\rho=\mathcal{H}\rho+\sum_{i,j}\gamma_{ij}{\mathcal{D}}[a_{i}a_{j}]\rho,

where the restriction
i\neq j
is removed. For
\ket{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_n}
in the degree-
k
subspace, so that
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=k
, this satisfies

\sum_{i,j}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{i}a_{j}\ket{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_n}=\left(\left(\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\right)^{2}-\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\right)\ket{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_n}=(k^{2}-k)\ket{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_n}.

(49)

Then this setting may use the usual degree-
k
computational subspace, and the UDS assumptions (
29
), (
32
) easily follow.
Since this example uses the same computational subspace as the standard amplitude damping case, one can actually show that they are compatible: with the Lindblad equation

\mathcal{L}\rho=\mathcal{H}\rho+\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}{\mathcal{D}}[a_{i}]\rho+\sum_{i,j}\gamma_{ij}{\mathcal{D}}[a_{i}a_{j}]\rho,

one may define

{\mathcal{D}_{0}^{\prime}}=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{b}\gamma_{b}\right)\sum_{i}{\mathcal{D}}[a_{i}]\rho+\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{b,d}\gamma_{b,d}\right)\sum_{i,j}{\mathcal{D}}[a_{i}a_{j}]\rho

and find that (
27
) holds for
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}=\hat{\mathcal{H}}+{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{0}^{\prime}}
.

Similar considerations allow for transformations by a unitary
U
as above and correlated decays between more than
2
qudits.
We will observe in Appendix
B.4.3
, however, that the examples based on correlated decays seem not to conveniently allow for cancellation of the first-order error.

B.4
Perturbative Cancellation

B.4.1
Additional assumption and its consequences

We now return to the notation and generality of Appendix
B.2
, in particular (
28
), (
29
), (
32
).
In addition to these assumptions, we let
T
be a unitary operator that commutes with
\mathcal{P}
, with corresponding superoperator
\mathcal{T}(\rho)=T\rho T^{\dagger}
.
We assume there exists a permutation
\pi
of the index set
\mathcal{B}
such that

TA_{\beta}T^{\dagger}=A_{\pi(\beta)}\textnormal{ for all }\beta,

(50)

and that
\pi
acts transitively on
\mathcal{B}
.
As a consequence, note that
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
acts on the operators
{\mathcal{D}}[A]
similarly, with

\hat{\mathcal{T}}{\hat{\mathcal{D}}}[A_{\beta}]\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}={\hat{\mathcal{D}}}[A_{\pi(\beta)}].

(51)

For
{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}
defined in (
33
), this implies

\hat{\mathcal{T}}{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}={\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}.

(52)

For the amplitude damping case considered in Appendix
B.3
,
we will take
T
to be the cyclic shift operator given by

T\ket{\alpha_1 \cdots\alpha_n}=\ket{\alpha_n \alpha_1 \alpha_2 \cdots\alpha_{n-1}},

(53)

so that
\pi
is the permutation
(1\,2\,\cdots\,n)
and we have
Ta_{i}T^{\dagger}=a_{i+1}
(indices taken modulo
n
).

We now consider the perturbation by
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}
and show that the uniformly decaying subspace is stable to first order under varying decay rates.
We note that the perturbation
\mathcal{L}_{1}
induces a bias in the expectation value.
We will account for this by averaging over multiple different experiments, shifting by a power of
T
each time.
Let
j
satisfy

0\leq j<m
,
where
m=|\mathcal{B}|
is the order of
T
.
We implement a
T^{j}
-shifted version of the Hamiltonian,
\mathcal{T}^{j}(H)=T^{j}HT^{-j}
, which corresponds to replacing
\hat{\mathcal{H}}
by
\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{j}\hat{\mathcal{H}}\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-j}
and
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
by
\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{j}\hat{\mathcal{H}}\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-j}+{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}
.
We similarly implement shifted versions of the state and observable,
\mathcal{T}^{j}(\rho)
and
\mathcal{T}^{j}(O)
respectively.
We observe that, by (
52
),

\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-j}(\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{j}\hat{\mathcal{H}}\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-j}+{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}+\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1})\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{j}=\hat{\mathcal{H}}+{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}+\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-j}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{j}=\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}+\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-j}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{j}.

(54)

The expected value of the
T^{j}
-shifted experiment is then

\displaystyle\llangle{O}|\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-j}e^{t(\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{j}\hat{\mathcal{H}}\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-j}+{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}+\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1})}\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{j}|{\rho(0)}\rrangle

\displaystyle=\llangle{O}|e^{t(\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}+\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-j}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{j})}|{\rho(0)}\rrangle,

(55)

the same as the unshifted case with
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}
replaced by
\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-j}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{j}
.
We note that by (
51
) and the definition of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}
,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-j}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{j}=\sum_{\beta}\Delta\gamma_{\beta}\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-j}{\hat{\mathcal{D}}}[A_{\beta}]\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{j}=\sum_{\beta}\Delta\gamma_{\beta}{\hat{\mathcal{D}}}[A_{\pi^{-j}(\beta)}]=\sum_{\beta}\Delta\gamma_{\pi^{j}(\beta)}{\hat{\mathcal{D}}}[A_{\beta}].

(56)

In particular, recalling that

\pi
transitively permutes the indices
\beta\in\mathcal{B}
and that

\sum_{\beta}\Delta\gamma_{\beta}=\sum_{\beta}\left(\gamma_{\beta}-\overline{\gamma}\right)=\sum_{\beta}\gamma_{\beta}-m\overline{\gamma}=0,

we observe that

\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-j}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{j}=\sum_{\beta}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\Delta\gamma_{\pi^{j}(\beta)}\right){\hat{\mathcal{D}}}[A_{\beta}]=\sum_{\beta}\left(\sum_{\beta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}}\Delta\gamma_{\beta^{\prime}}\right){\hat{\mathcal{D}}}[A_{\beta}]=0.

(57)

B.4.2
First order error cancellation

Let us briefly return to the unshifted case. We work in the Liouville space

\displaystyle|{\dot{\rho}(t)}\rrangle=(\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}+\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1})|{\rho(t)}\rrangle.

(58)

We consider the interaction picture where we define
|{\rho_{I}(t)}\rrangle\equiv e^{-\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}|{\rho(t)}\rrangle
and
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t)\equiv e^{-\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}
and subsequently obtain the equation of motion

\displaystyle|{\dot{\rho}_{I}(t)}\rrangle=\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t)|{\rho_{I}(t)}\rrangle\;.

(59)

We can now perturbatively solve the above equation as

\displaystyle|{\rho_{I}(t)}\rrangle=|{\rho(0)}\rrangle+\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t^{\prime})|{\rho(0)}\rrangle+\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime\prime}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t^{\prime})\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t^{\prime\prime})|{\rho_{I}(t^{\prime\prime})}\rrangle\;.

(60)

where we used
|{\rho_{I}(0)}\rrangle=|{\rho(0)}\rrangle
.
Applying
e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}
, we obtain

\displaystyle|{\rho(t)}\rrangle=e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}|{\rho(0)}\rrangle+\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t^{\prime})|{\rho(0)}\rrangle+\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime\prime}e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t^{\prime})\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t^{\prime\prime})|{\rho_{I}(t^{\prime\prime})}\rrangle\;.

(61)

Therefore the expectation value of the observable
O
now is

\displaystyle\langle O\rangle_{\text{UDS}}=\llangle{O}|{\rho(t)}\rrangle

\displaystyle=\llangle O|e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}\bigg(|{\rho(0)}\rrangle+\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t^{\prime})|{\rho(0)}\rrangle+\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime\prime}e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t^{\prime})\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t^{\prime\prime})|{\rho_{I}(t^{\prime\prime})}\rrangle\bigg)\;

(62)

\displaystyle=e^{-c\bar{\gamma}t}\langle O\rangle_{C}+\llangle O|e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t^{\prime})|{\rho(0)}\rrangle+\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime\prime}e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t^{\prime})\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t^{\prime\prime})|{\rho_{I}(t^{\prime\prime})}\rrangle\bigg)\;,

(63)

where we apply (
27
) in the last line.
We will consider the
0
th and
1
st order terms, observing that averaging over all
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
-shifts leaves the
0
th order invariant while cancelling the
1
st order terms.
We will consider the problem of bounding the
2
nd order terms in Appendix
B.4.4
.

Since
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
is unaffected by
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
-shifting (up to the change in encoding of the Hamiltonian), the constant term
e^{-c\bar{\gamma}t}\langle O\rangle_{C}
is unaffected.
Also recall
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t)\equiv e^{-\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}
, so the
\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{j}
-shifted version is

e^{-\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-j}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{j}e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}
.
Then the expected value averaged over all
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
-shifts, denoted by
\llangle O\rrangle_{\text{UDS}}
, satisfies

\displaystyle\llangle O\rrangle_{\text{UDS}}-e^{-c\bar{\gamma}t}\langle O\rangle_{C}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\llangle O|e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}e^{-\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-j}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{j}e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}|{\rho(0)}\rrangle+\textnormal{2nd order}\;

(64)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\llangle O|e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}e^{-\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-j}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{j}\right)e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}|{\rho(0)}\rrangle+\textnormal{2nd order}\;

(65)

\displaystyle=0+\textnormal{2nd order},

(66)

where we apply (
57
) in the last line.
In particular, we were able to negate the effect of first order deviations in decay rates even without the knowledge of perturbations. Therefore, we see that uniformly decaying subspaces can be made stable up to first order.

B.4.3
Amplitude damping channel examples

We now return to the examples related to the amplitude damping channel, discussed in Appendix
B.3
.
As stated above, in the standard case
A_{i}=a_{i}
we take
T
to be the cyclic shift operator given by

T\ket{\alpha_1 \cdots\alpha_n}=\ket{\alpha_n \alpha_1 \alpha_2 \cdots\alpha_{n-1}},

(67)

so that
Ta_{i}T^{\dagger}=a_{i+1}
(indices taken modulo
n
).

More generally, we recall the variant with
A_{i}={\widetilde{U}}a_{i}{\widetilde{U}}^{\dagger}
where
U
is an
n\times n
unitary matrix.
We note that

({\widetilde{U}}T{\widetilde{U}}^{\dagger})A_{i}({\widetilde{U}}T{\widetilde{U}}^{\dagger})^{\dagger}={\widetilde{U}}Ta_{i}T^{\dagger}{\widetilde{U}}^{\dagger}={\widetilde{U}}a_{i+1}{\widetilde{U}}^{\dagger}=A_{i+1},

where we continue to take the indices of the

n
.
Then in the

{\widetilde{U}}T{\widetilde{U}}^{\dagger}

For the case of correlated decays,
A_{ij}=a_{i}a_{j}

In principle, one can simply replace the role of

S_{n}
,

But of course this would require one to perform

B.4.4
Second order error bound

We now consider the last term in (
63
).
We have shown that under the assumptions of the previous section, the middle (first order) term can be cancelled.
Thus if one wants to estimate
\langle O\rangle_{C}\approx e^{c\bar{\gamma}t}\llangle O\rrangle_{\text{UDS}}
,
the error is given by the last term of (
63
).
To obtain this bound, we follow the techniques of the supplemental material of
Temme et al. 2017
.

Since
\llangle{O}|=\llangle{O}|\hat{\mathcal{P}}
,
\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{i}=\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{i}\hat{\mathcal{P}}
, and
\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}\hat{\mathcal{P}}=\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}-c\overline{\gamma}\hat{\mathcal{P}}
, we have

\displaystyle\llangle{O}|e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t^{\prime})\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t^{\prime\prime})|{\rho_{I}(t^{\prime\prime})}\rrangle

\displaystyle=\llangle{O}|\hat{\mathcal{P}}e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t^{\prime})\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t^{\prime\prime})|{\rho_{I}(t^{\prime\prime})}\rrangle

(68)

\displaystyle=\llangle{O}|e^{\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}\hat{\mathcal{P}}t}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t^{\prime})\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t^{\prime\prime})\hat{\mathcal{P}}|{\rho_{I}(t^{\prime\prime})}\rrangle

(69)

\displaystyle=e^{-c\overline{\gamma}t}\llangle{O}|e^{\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}t}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t^{\prime})\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t^{\prime\prime})\hat{\mathcal{P}}|{\rho_{I}(t^{\prime\prime})}\rrangle.

(70)

We now apply the mean value theorem: there exist
\zeta^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime\prime}\in[0,t]
such that

\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime\prime}e^{-c\overline{\gamma}t}\llangle{O}|e^{\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}t}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t^{\prime})\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t^{\prime\prime})\hat{\mathcal{P}}|{\rho_{I}(t^{\prime\prime})}\rrangle=\dfrac{t^{2}}{2}e^{-c\overline{\gamma}t}\llangle{O}|e^{\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}t}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(\zeta^{\prime})\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(\zeta^{\prime\prime})\hat{\mathcal{P}}|{\rho_{I}(\zeta^{\prime\prime})}\rrangle.

(71)

We briefly recall the Schatten
p
-norms, for
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
||T||_{p}=\left(\Tr( \sqrt{T^\dagger T}^p )\right)^{1/p}
.
For
p=\infty
, this is the standard operator norm. Rewriting the above as a
1
-norm and applying Hölder’s inequality for Schatten
p
-norms, we have

\displaystyle\dfrac{t^{2}}{2}e^{-c\overline{\gamma}t}\left|\llangle{O}|e^{\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}t}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(\zeta^{\prime})\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(\zeta^{\prime\prime})\hat{\mathcal{P}}|{\rho_{I}(\zeta^{\prime\prime})}\rrangle\right|

\displaystyle=\dfrac{t^{2}}{2}e^{-c\overline{\gamma}t}||\llangle{O}|e^{\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}t}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(\zeta^{\prime})\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(\zeta^{\prime\prime})\hat{\mathcal{P}}|{\rho_{I}(\zeta^{\prime\prime})}\rrangle||_{1}

(72)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{t^{2}}{2}e^{-c\overline{\gamma}t}||\llangle{O}|e^{\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}t}||_{\infty}\cdot||\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(\zeta^{\prime})\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(\zeta^{\prime\prime})\hat{\mathcal{P}}|{\rho_{I}(\zeta^{\prime\prime})}\rrangle||_{1}.

(73)

Since
e^{\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}t}
is unitary, we have
||\llangle{O}|e^{\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}t}||_{\infty}=||O||_{\infty}=||O||_{op}
, the standard operator norm of
O
.
Then we must only bound

\displaystyle||\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(\zeta^{\prime})\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(\zeta^{\prime\prime})\hat{\mathcal{P}}|{\rho_{I}(\zeta^{\prime\prime})}\rrangle||_{1}

\displaystyle=||\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(\zeta^{\prime})\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(\zeta^{\prime\prime})\hat{\mathcal{P}}|{\rho_{I}(\zeta^{\prime\prime})}\rrangle||_{1}

(74)

\displaystyle=||e^{-\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\zeta^{\prime}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\hat{\mathcal{P}}e^{\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}\hat{\mathcal{P}}(\zeta^{\prime}-\zeta^{\prime\prime})}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\hat{\mathcal{P}}|{\rho(t^{\prime\prime})}\rrangle||_{1}

(75)

\displaystyle=e^{-c\overline{\gamma}(-\zeta^{\prime}+(\zeta^{\prime}-\zeta^{\prime\prime})}||e^{-\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\zeta^{\prime}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\hat{\mathcal{P}}e^{\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}(\zeta^{\prime}-\zeta^{\prime\prime})}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\hat{\mathcal{P}}|{\rho(\zeta^{\prime\prime})}\rrangle||_{1}

(76)

\displaystyle\leq e^{c\overline{\gamma}\zeta^{\prime\prime}}||e^{-\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\zeta^{\prime}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\hat{\mathcal{P}}e^{\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}(\zeta^{\prime}-\zeta^{\prime\prime})}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\hat{\mathcal{P}}||_{op,1}

(77)

\displaystyle=e^{c\overline{\gamma}\zeta^{\prime\prime}}||\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\hat{\mathcal{P}}||_{op,1}^{2},

(78)

where
||\cdot||_{op,1}
is the operator norm with respect to the Schatten
1
-norm, and we use the fact that operator norms are unitarily invariant.
In total, then, the error term of interest satisfies

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime\prime}e^{-c\overline{\gamma}t}\llangle{O}|e^{\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}t}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t^{\prime})\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{I}(t^{\prime\prime})\hat{\mathcal{P}}|{\rho_{I}(t^{\prime\prime})}\rrangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{t^{2}}{2}e^{-c\overline{\gamma}(t-\zeta^{\prime\prime})}||O||_{op}\cdot||\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\hat{\mathcal{P}}||_{op,1}^{2}

(79)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{t^{2}}{2}||O||_{op}\cdot||\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\hat{\mathcal{P}}||_{op,1}^{2}.

(80)

The analogous result holds in the
T
-shifted case. Combining this with the results of Appendix
B.4
, we obtain

\Big|\llangle O\rrangle_{\text{UDS}}-e^{-c\bar{\gamma}t}\langle O\rangle_{C}\Big|\leq\dfrac{t^{2}}{2}||O||_{op}\cdot||\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\hat{\mathcal{P}}||_{op,1}^{2}.

(81)

B.4.5
Return to amplitude damping example

We return to the example of Appendix
B.3
, with qudits of dimension
d
,
A_{i}=a_{i}
the annihilation operator on the
i
th qudit, and computational subspace the degree-
k
subspace
\mathcal{S}_{k}
.
The bound above is determined by the operator norm of
\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\hat{\mathcal{P}}
.
We note

\mathcal{P}{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\mathcal{P}(\rho)=P\mathcal{L}_{1}(P\rho P)P=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\Delta\gamma_{i}\{a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i},\rho\}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\Delta\gamma_{i}\left\{\sum_{s}s\outerproduct{s}{s},\rho\right\}.

In particular,
\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\hat{\mathcal{P}}
is diagonalizable in the Fock basis
\outerproduct{\alpha_1 \cdots\alpha_n}{\alpha'_1\cdots\alpha'_n}
,
so its operator norm is the modulus of the largest eigenvalue.
One can easily verify that this largest eigenvalue is bounded above by

, where
\lambda=\max_{i}|\Delta\gamma_{i}|
.
This gives the bound

\Big|\llangle O\rrangle_{\text{UDS}}-e^{-k\bar{\gamma}t}\langle O\rangle_{C}\Big|\leq\dfrac{(tk\lambda)^{2}}{2}||O||_{op}.

(82)

The same result holds in the case

B.5
Circuit case

We now discuss why one can apply the techniques discussed above, specifically the cancellation of errors up to first order, to the discretized example at the end of the main text.

We let
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}(\eta)
be a single-qudit discretized amplitude damping channel, given by the following Kraus operators
K_{s}(\eta)
for
s\geq 0
and a parameter
\eta

Chuang et al. 1997
(where
\eta=1-\gamma
in ibid.):

K_{s}(\eta)=\sum_{r}\sqrt{\binom{r}{s}\eta^{r-s}(1-\eta)^{s}}\outerproduct{r-s}{r}.

(83)

We write
\mathcal{E}_{i}(\eta_{i})
for
\mathcal{E}
acting on the
i
th qudit with parameter
\eta=\eta_{i}
.

Now suppose we prepare a state
\rho\in\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{V})
, apply unitaries
U_{1},\dots,U_{N_{T}}
on
\mathcal{V}
that commute with
P
(with corresponding operators
\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{1},\dots,\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{N_{T}}
on the Liouville space), and measure an observable
O
with
POP=O
.
We model dissipation in a discretized manner by replacing each
\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{j}
by
(\bigotimes_{i}\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{i}(\eta_{i}))\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{j}
.
The expectation value is the following:

\langle O\rangle=\llangle{O}|\hat{\mathcal{P}}(\bigotimes_{i}\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{i}(\eta_{i}))\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{1}(\bigotimes_{i}\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{i}(\eta_{i}))\cdots(\bigotimes_{i}\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{i}(\eta_{i}))\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{N_{T}}|{\rho}\rrangle.

(84)

Since all
K_{s}
preserve or lower the degree, keeping
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{S})^{\perp}
and
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{V})
invariant, we have
\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{i}=\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{i}\hat{\mathcal{P}}
. Further, the
\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{i}
commute with
\hat{\mathcal{P}}
by assumption. Therefore,

\langle O\rangle=\llangle{O}|(\bigotimes_{i}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{i}(\eta_{i})\hat{\mathcal{P}})\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{1}(\bigotimes_{i}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{i}(\eta_{i})\hat{\mathcal{P}})\cdots(\bigotimes_{i}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{i}(\eta_{i})\hat{\mathcal{P}})\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{N_{T}}|{\rho}\rrangle.

(85)

Since
POP=O
(meaning that we post-select for measurements in the degree-
k
subspace), and
|{\rho}\rrangle
involves only terms of degree
k
or lower, all terms involving the degree-raising operators
K_{s}
for
s>0
must vanish. Then only the
K_{0}
term of each
\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{i}\hat{\mathcal{P}}
contributes to the expectation.
Letting
\hat{K}_{0}^{(i)}(\eta)
be the corresponding operator on
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{V})
, we note that we may replace
\bigotimes_{i}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{i}(\eta_{i})\hat{\mathcal{P}}
with

\hat{\mathscr{E}}(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n})\equiv\bigotimes_{i}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{K}_{0}^{(i)}(\eta_{i})\hat{\mathcal{P}},

(86)

and write

\langle O\rangle=\llangle{O}|\hat{\mathscr{E}}(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n})\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{1}\hat{\mathscr{E}}(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n})\cdots\hat{\mathscr{E}}(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n})\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{N_{T}}|{\rho}\rrangle.

(87)

We will show that the first-order cancellation observed above extends to this discretized setting.
Toward that end, for comparison with the continuous case,
we take
\eta_{i}=\exp(-\Delta t \gamma_i)
, where
\gamma_{i}
is the parameter in the Lindblad master equation (
43
) and
\Delta t=t/{N_{T}}
is the time elapsed per unitary (here
t
is the total time elapsed, as above).
We define

\overline{\eta}=(\eta_{1}\cdots\eta_{n})^{1/n}=\exp(-\frac{1}{n}\Delta t \sum_i \gamma_i)=\exp(-\Delta t \olg)\textnormal{ and }\Delta\eta_{i}=\eta_{i}/\overline{\eta}=\exp(-\Delta t \Delta\gamma_i),

(88)

and note that
\hat{K}_{0}^{(i)}(\eta_{i})=\hat{K}_{0}^{(i)}(\overline{\eta})\hat{K}_{0}^{(i)}(\Delta\eta_{i})
, so that

\hat{\mathscr{E}}(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n})=\hat{\mathscr{E}}(\overline{\eta},\dots,\overline{\eta})\hat{\mathscr{E}}(\Delta\eta_{1},\dots,\Delta\eta_{n}).

(89)

For
|{\rho^{\prime}}\rrangle
in the degree-
k
subspace, we note that

\hat{\mathscr{E}}(\overline{\eta},\dots,\overline{\eta})|{\rho^{\prime}}\rrangle=\overline{\eta}^{k}=\exp(-k\Delta t\olg),

(90)

so we obtain

\langle O\rangle=e^{-k{N_{T}}\Delta t\overline{\gamma}}\llangle{O}|\hat{\mathscr{E}}(\Delta\eta_{1},\dots,\Delta\eta_{n})\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{1}\hat{\mathscr{E}}(\Delta\eta_{1},\dots,\Delta\eta_{n})\cdots\hat{\mathscr{E}}(\Delta\eta_{1},\dots,\Delta\eta_{n})\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{N_{T}}|{\rho}\rrangle,

(91)

where we note that
{N_{T}}\Delta t=t
is the total time.

Now, we consider a single expression
