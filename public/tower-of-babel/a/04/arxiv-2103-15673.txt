The Initial boundary value problem and quasi-local Hamiltonians in general relativity
Zhongshan An and Michael T. Anderson

Abstract
. We discuss relations between the initial boundary value problem (IBVP) and quasi-local Hamiltonians in GR. The latter have traditionally been based on Dirichlet boundary conditions, which however are shown here to be ill-posed for the IBVP. We present and analyse several other choices of boundary conditions which are better behaved with respect to the IBVP and carry out a corresponding Hamiltonian analysis, using the framework of the covariant phase space method.



1.
Introduction

This article is concerned with the initial boundary value problem (IBVP) for the vacuum Einstein equations and its relation to
the existence of quasi-local Hamiltonians in general relativity. We consider then space-times
M
topologically
of the form
M\cong I\times S
, where
I=(-1,1)
and
S
is a compact 3-manifold with non-empty boundary
\partial S=\Sigma
. Let

{\mathcal{C}}=I\times\Sigma
denote the boundary
\partial M
of
M
with
\Sigma=\partial S
. The initial boundary value problem is the problem
of finding Lorentz metrics
g
on
M
satisfying the vacuum Einstein equations

(1.1)

\mathrm{Ric}_{g}=0,

together with prescribed boundary conditions along
{\mathcal{C}}
and initial conditions along a Cauchy surface (e.g.
S=\{0\}\times S\subset M
). More precisely, one would like to establish existence, uniqueness and stability of solutions with prescribed initial and boundary
data.

The analogous situation for the Cauchy or initial value problem has long been well-understood and it is worthwhile to recall this
briefly to gain the general perspective. The initial data
(g_{S},K)
on
S
consist of a Riemannian metric
g_{S}
and symmetric
bilinear form
K
satisfying the vacuum Einstein constraint equations; these give the induced metric and second fundamental
form of a solution
g
on
S
.

Let
(V,g)
denote a vacuum development of an initial data set
(S,g_{S},K)
, i.e. a globally hyperbolic vacuum
spacetime
V
containing
(S,g_{S},K)
. It was proved by Choquet-Bruhat
[
12
]
that vacuum developments always exist.
Two vacuum developments
(V_{1},g_{2})
,
(V_{2},g_{2})
are called equivalent if they contain a common sub-development.
Choquet-Bruhat and Geroch
[
13

further proved that there is a maximal development
V_{\max}
, unique up to isometry,
i.e. unique up to the action of the group
\mathrm{Diff}_{0}(V_{\max})
of diffeomorphisms of
V_{\max}
fixing
S
pointwise.
Let
{\mathcal{V}}
be the space of such isometry classes of maximal solutions.

The main results on the solution of the Cauchy problem can then be summarized in the following statement: for each Cauchy surface

S
, there is a bijective correspondence

(1.2)

E_{S}:{\mathcal{V}}\rightarrow\mathcal{I},\ \ g\rightarrow(g_{S},K),

where
\mathcal{I}
is the space of all initial data
(g_{S},K)
satisfying the vacuum constraint equations.
The bijection (
1.2
) gives an effective parametrization of the space of maximal solutions of (
1.1
) by their initial data.
It would appear to be likely that
\mathcal{I}
and hence
{\mathcal{V}}
may be given the structure of a smooth infinite dimensional manifold, possibly
away from a small singular set of special data. Further by work of Fischer, Marsden and Moncrief, cf.
[
15
]
,
{\mathcal{V}}
or
\mathcal{I}
should
carry a naturally defined (non-degenerate) symplectic form
\Omega
.

D_{S}:{\mathcal{V}}\rightarrow\mathcal{I}\times_{c}{\mathcal{B}},\ \ g\rightarrow((g_{S},K),b)

Next, we relate the issues above with a Hamiltonian analysis of GR on finite manifolds-with-boundary.
Hamiltonian analyses of GR on manifolds with boundary at infinity are well-known and well-developed. The ADM and
Regge-Teitelboim Hamiltonian analysis for asymptotically flat space-times with boundary at spatial or null infinity are foundational
works for the notions of global energy-momentum and angular momentum (the “conserved charges”) of such space-times.
The case of time-like anti-de-Sitter boundaries at conformal infinity has also been extensively studied in connection with the
AdS-CFT correspondence. In contrast, there has been much less detailed analysis in the case of finite, time-like boundaries,
although see
[
6

The main concern of this paper is then a more rigorous understanding of the definition of quasi-local energies (Hamiltonians)
through an analysis of the behavior of the space of solutions of a well-posed IBVP. There is a large literature on Hamiltonian approaches to a
suitable definition of quasi-local energy, starting with the foundational work of Brown-York
[
10

[
37

detailed survey. These are all based on Hamiltonians with Dirichlet boundary conditions. The focus here is instead on both general
boundary data, and specific boundary data, namely the CH data (
1.4

[
2

The traditional canonical phase space approach toward a Hamiltonian analysis breaks the general covariance of the theory by a
choice time evolution and corresponding time-like vector field

space-time, and an associated lapse-shift pair

\partial_{t}=NT+X

{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{H}}_{(N,X)}

[
37

,
[
38

A very useful alternative to this approach which does not break the full covariance of the theory is the covariant phase space method,
which directly gives a (pre)-symplectic structure to the space of all vacuum solutions (with given boundary conditions).
We refer to the survey article
[
24

for a detailed description of the background and history of this method; important contributions
to this topic are those of Crnkovic-Witten
[

[

Here we are interested in the context of space-times

which has been discussed much less in the
literature. Fortunately, an excellent description of the covariant phase space method with boundary has recently been given by Harlow-Wu

[

this method, as applied to general (not only Dirichlet) boundary data. We also develop a modification of the method needed to deal with
the diffeomorphism-invariant AA boundary data from
[

In Propositions 4.1 and 4.3 of §4, we show that the CH boundary data and a slight modification of the AA boundary data

In §5, we then discuss the basic issue of the choice of zero-point energies for Hamiltonians. For Dirichlet boundary data,

2.
Well-posed boundary data in GR

The primary method to solve an IBVP for the Einstein equations is to find a gauge in which the reduced Einstein equations form
a quasilinear hyperbolic system and then determine boundary conditions in which reduced system is a well-posed IBVP. Of course
one also needs to ensure that all solutions of the reduced Einstein equations are true vacuum solutions; this is the issue of constraint
preservation. For such a method of solution, a necessary condition for well-posedness is that the IBVP localized at a standard flat Minkowski
corner region
{\bf R}=\{t\geq 0,x^{1}\leq 0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{1,3}
is well-posed, i.e. the frozen coefficient IBVP is well-posed. All
known existence and uniqueness results for the vacuum IBVP are based on this method.

In this respect, we note the following:

Proposition 2.1
.

With respect to either Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions, the linearization of the vacuum equations (
1.1
) at a standard Minkowski
background is not a well-posed IBVP, for any choice of gauge reduction.

Proof.

The proof uses only the structure of the vacuum Einstein constraint equations (not the full Einstein equations) on the
boundary
{\mathcal{C}}
;

(2.1)

|A|^{2}-H^{2}-R_{\gamma}=E(\nu,\nu)=0,

(2.2)

\mathrm{div}(A-H\gamma)=2E(\nu,\cdot)=0.

Here
E
is the Einstein tensor of
g
,
R_{\gamma}
is the scalar curvature of the boundary metric
({\mathcal{C}},\gamma)
and
\mathrm{div}
is the divergence
operator on symmetric
(2,0)
tensors on
({\mathcal{C}},\gamma)
;
\mathrm{div}h=\mathrm{tr}\nabla h
. As above,
A
is the second fundamental form of

({\mathcal{C}},\gamma)\subset(M,g)
and the mean curvature
H
is its trace.

Consider first Dirichlet boundary data, where the boundary metric
\gamma
is prescribed. The linearization of the Hamiltonian or
scalar constraint (
2.1
) in the direction of the variation
h
of
g
is given by

2\langle A^{\prime}_{h},A\rangle-2\langle A^{2},h^{T}\rangle-2HH^{\prime}_{h}-R^{\prime}_{h^{T}}=0,

where
h^{T}
is the variation of the induced metric on the boundary. The linearization of these equations at the standard solid corner region

({\bf R},g_{0})
of Minkowski space gives
A=H=0
,
so that

R^{\prime}_{h^{T}}=0.

Thus, at the linearized level, only scalar flat deformations
h^{T}
of the boundary metric
\gamma_{0}=g_{0}|_{{\mathcal{C}}}
,
{\mathcal{C}}=\{x^{1}=0\}
, are
possible. This is an infinite dimensional restriction; for example, most conformal deformations are excluded. It follows that the linearized
constraint (
2.1
) is not solvable for generic variations of boundary data
h^{T}
; the same applies then to the (more restrictive)
full Einstein equations.

Next consider Neumann boundary data, where the second fundamental form
A
is prescribed. The linearization of the
momentum constraint (
2.2
) is given by

(2.3)

\mathrm{div}_{\gamma}(A_{h}^{\prime}-H_{h}^{\prime}\gamma-Hh^{T})=-(\mathrm{div})_{h}^{\prime}(A-H\gamma).

At the standard solid region of Minkowski space, this gives

(2.4)

\mathrm{div}_{\gamma_{0}}(A^{\prime}_{h}-H^{\prime}_{h}\gamma_{0})=0.

where
\mathrm{div}_{\gamma_{0}}
is the divergence operator with respect to the standard background Lorentz metric
\gamma_{0}
on
\mathbb{R}^{1,2}
. Thus the
linearization of (
2.2
) is not solvable for generic
A^{\prime}_{h}
; the form
A^{\prime}_{h}
has 6 degrees of freedom while (
2.4
) gives
3 scalar conditions on
A^{\prime}_{h}
. This shows again that the linearized Einstein equations are not generically solvable at
g_{0}

for prescribed variations
A^{\prime}_{h}
.

∎

From this, one expects that the space of vacuum solutions having given boundary metric (Dirichlet boundary data) or given
second fundamental form (Neumann boundary data) is generically empty; the space of boundary data admitting solutions
g
is of
infinite codimension in the space
{\mathcal{B}}
of all boundary data. While Proposition 2.1 does not quite prove this, it does show that
any proof of well-posedness cannot be based on solving a gauged IBVP for a quasi-linear hyperbolic system which localizes.
More generally, one expects the behavior of the space of solutions with respect to either Dirichlet or Neumann boundary data
to be highly unstable; given one solution with (say) Dirichlet boundary data
\gamma
on
{\mathcal{C}}
, an open set of nearby solutions have boundary
data only in a subset of infinite codimension in the space
{\mathcal{B}}=\Met({\mathcal{C}})
of boundary data. In particular, there is no correspondence as in
(
1.3
).

We also point out that there is a strong failure of uniqueness naturally associated with the failure of existence above.

Proposition 2.2
.

In harmonic gauge, solutions to the linearized IBVP for the Einstein equations with Dirichlet boundary data have an infinite-dimensional
kernel.

Proof.

Consider the initial boundary value problem for vacuum Einstein equations with Dirichlet boundary data:

\begin{split}&\mathrm{Ric}_{g}=0\ \ {\rm in}~M,\ \ g|_{S}=g_{S},~\tfrac{1}{2}L_{T}g|_{S}=K\ \ {\rm on}~S,\ \ g_{{\mathcal{C}}}=\gamma\ \ {\rm on}~{\mathcal{C}}.\end{split}

Let
x^{\alpha}=(t,x^{1},x^{2},x^{3})
be a local chart near the corner of
M
, mapping to the corner region
{\bf R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{1,3}
.
Assume
x^{\alpha}
are harmonic, so that
V^{\alpha}=\Box_{g}x^{\alpha}=0
. The kernel of the linearization of the
system above at the Minkowski corner
{\bf R}
is then given by:

(2.5)

\begin{split}&\mathrm{Ric}^{\prime}_{h}+\delta^{*}(V^{\prime}_{h})=0\ \ {\rm in}~\mathbf{R}\\
&h_{\alpha\beta}=0,~\partial_{t}h_{\alpha\beta}=0\ \ {\rm on}~\{t=0,x^{1}\leq 0\}\\
&V^{\prime}_{h}=0,~h^{T}=0\ \ {\rm on}~\{x^{1}=0\},\end{split}

where
V^{\prime}_{h}=-\mathrm{div}h+\frac{1}{2}d\mathrm{tr}h
. We show that the system (
2.5
) has an infinite-dimensional space of solutions.

Set
h_{\alpha\beta}=0
in
\mathbf{R}
for all
\alpha,\beta
except
(\alpha,\beta)=(1,1),(0,1)
and let

(2.6)

h_{01}=\tfrac{1}{2}h_{11}=f(x^{1}+t)

for some
f
determined below. Then clearly
(\partial_{t}-\partial_{x^{1}})h_{11}=0,\partial_{x^{A}}h_{11}=0
for
A=2,3
. It follows that the bulk
equation in (
2.5
) holds:
(\mathrm{Ric}^{\prime}_{h}+\delta^{*}(V^{\prime}_{h}))_{\alpha\beta}=\Box h_{\alpha\beta}=0
in
{\bf R}
, for all
\alpha,\beta
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be any smooth function such that
f(x)=0,f^{\prime}(x)=0
for all
x\leq 0
but
f(x)\neq 0
for
x>0
. Then on the
initial surface
\{t=0,x^{1}\leq 0\}
,
h_{01}=h_{11}=\partial_{t}h_{01}=\partial_{t}h_{11}=0
, so that
h_{\alpha\beta}
satisfies the initial conditions.
It remains to verify the boundary conditions. The Dirichlet boundary condition
h^{T}=0
, which reads
h_{00}=h_{0A}=h_{AB}=0

for
A,B=2,3
, is obviously true. Further simple calculation also gives
V^{\prime}_{h}=0
, cf.
[
2
, eqn. (3.6)]
for example. (This is
the reason for the particular relation between
h_{01}
and
h_{11}
in (
2.6
)).

Thus the deformation
h
constructed above solves the homogeneous IBVP; however,
h_{11}\neq 0
in the region
\{t+x^{1}>0\}\subset{\bf R}
.
In other words, incoming wave solutions generated by such
f
show that solutions to the linearized IBVP (
2.5
) are not unique outside the
domain of dependence of the initial surface.
∎

Note that the same result and proof also work well for Neumann boundary data.

This raises the question of whether there are any geometric boundary data on
{\mathcal{C}}
for which the IBVP could be well-posed. We note that
in the simpler Riemannian or Euclidean setting, it was proved in
[
3
]
that neither Dirichlet nor Neumann boundary data give a
well-posed elliptic boundary value problem for the vacuum Einstein equations, in any gauge. On the other hand, it was shown there
that the data

(2.7)

([\gamma],H),

consisting of the conformal class
[\gamma]
of the boundary metric
\gamma
and the mean curvature
H=trA
, are well-posed
elliptic boundary data, in a suitable gauge. (There exist other choices of well-posed elliptic boundary data, but the choice (
2.7
)
is perhaps the most natural). This was extended to the parabolic Ricci-flow setting in
[
19
]
.

By analogy, it is natural then to consider the same data for the IBVP in the Lorentzian setting (
1.1
). We make
the following

Conjecture 2.3
.

The IBVP for the vacuum Einstein equations (
1.1
) is well-posed with respect to the mixed boundary data
([\gamma],H)
on
{\mathcal{C}}
.

There is strong evidence in favor of this conjecture, but a detailed discussion would be lengthy and is out of place here; we hope to
address Conjecture 2.3 in detail elsewhere.

As a first justification for the conjecture, we note the following result, which contrasts strongly with Proposition 2.1.

Proposition 2.4
.

The boundary data
([\gamma],H)
are well-posed in terms of the constraint equations (
2.1
)-(
2.2
). In particular, the
constraint equations on the boundary
{\mathcal{C}}
can be solved with any prescribed
([\gamma],H)
.

Proof.

This is proved by transferring the usual conformal method of Lichnerowicz-Choquet-Bruhat-York from the space-like
elliptic setting to the time-like hyperbolic setting.

Let
\gamma_{0}
be a representative metric in
[\gamma]
(not to be confused with the Minkowski metric on
\mathbb{R}^{1,2}
used above), with volume
form
dv_{\gamma_{0}}
and let
\alpha
be a background volume form on
{\mathcal{C}}
. Form then the so-called densitized lapse (cf.
[
31
]
),

\mu={\tfrac{1}{2}}\frac{dv_{\gamma_{0}}}{\alpha}.

Let
\sigma
be a transverse-traceless (2,0) tensor with respect to
\gamma_{0}
;
\mathrm{div}\sigma=\mathrm{tr}\sigma=0
.
In the conformal method, cf.
[
31
]
again, one then constructs the Cauchy data
(\gamma,A)
at
{\mathcal{C}}
by setting

\gamma=\lambda^{4}\gamma_{0},

A=\lambda^{-2}(\sigma+{\tfrac{1}{2\mu}}\widehat{L}_{X}\gamma_{0})+{\tfrac{1}{3}}H\lambda^{4}\gamma_{0},

where
\widehat{L}_{X}\gamma_{0}
is the conformal Killing operator with respect to
\gamma_{0}
:
\widehat{L}_{X}\gamma_{0}=L_{X}\gamma_{0}-(\frac{2}{3}\mathrm{div}_{\gamma_{0}}X)\gamma_{0}
. The constraint equations (
2.1
)-(
2.2
) in this time-like setting then become
a coupled system of equations for
(\lambda,X)
which take the form

(2.8)

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathrm{div}_{\gamma_{0}}({\tfrac{1}{2\mu}}\widehat{L}_{X}\gamma_{0})={\tfrac{2}{3}}\lambda^{6}dH,\\
8\Box_{\gamma_{0}}\lambda=R_{\gamma_{0}}\lambda-|\sigma+{\tfrac{1}{2\mu}}\widehat{L}_{X}\gamma_{0}|^{2}\lambda^{-7}+{\tfrac{2}{3}}H^{2}\lambda^{5}.\end{array}

Here
\mathrm{div}_{\gamma_{0}}
and
\Box_{\gamma_{0}}
are the divergence and wave operator with respect to
\gamma_{0}\in[\gamma]
and
R_{0}
is the scalar
curvature of
\gamma_{0}
. One has
\mathrm{div}\widehat{L}_{X}(\gamma_{0})=\mathrm{div}\delta^{*}X-\frac{2}{3}d\mathrm{div}X
and standard Weitzenbock formulas show that

\mathrm{div}\widehat{L}_{X}(\gamma_{0})=-D^{*}DX+\frac{1}{2}d\mathrm{div}X+E(X)
, where
E(X)
involves only the curvature of
\gamma_{0}
and no derivatives of
X
.

The system (
2.8
) is thus a coupled system of semi-linear wave equations for
(\lambda,X)
and so by standard theory is
well-posed; one has existence and uniqueness of solutions for at least some positive time
t\in[0,T)
, given suitable initial conditions.

It is also easy to see that the York decomposition
[
42
]
holds on
({\mathcal{C}},[\gamma])
; any symmetric bilinear form
h
, (e.g.
h=A
above)
admits a decomposition
h=\sigma+\frac{\mathrm{tr}h}{3}\gamma_{0}+\widehat{L}_{X}\gamma_{0}
, where
\sigma
is transverse-traceless with respect to
\gamma_{0}
.
The vector field
X
is unique up to choice of initial conditions on a slice
\Sigma
to
{\mathcal{C}}
. The proof of this is essentially the same as
in the Riemannian or space-like case, using hyperbolic equations in place of elliptic equations.

∎

Thus the space of solutions of the Einstein constraint equations on a time-like boundary
{\mathcal{C}}
is parametrized by the free
data
([\gamma],H,\sigma)
and initial conditions at
\Sigma
. This time-like boundary setting is actually significantly better behaved than the analogous
situation for initial data, where the corresponding result is false. In general, given
([g_{S}],k,\sigma)
,
k=\mathrm{tr}_{S}K
, on an initial slice

S
, it is not always possible obtain existence of (unique) solutions to the constraint equations. The conformal method does not
always work well for example when
H
is far away from the set of constant functions (the far from CMC regime).

Remark 2.5
.

We make some further remarks comparing Propositions 2.1 and 2.4, focusing on the constraint equations with Dirichlet data.

Consider first initial data, i.e. the issue of solving the constraint equations on a Cauchy surface with given Dirichlet data, i.e. initial
metric
\gamma
. In bulk dimension 3, this is the problem of isometric immersion of a given Riemannian surface
(S,g_{S})
into

\mathbb{R}^{1,2}
, by the fundamental theorem of hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{1,2}
, (at least when
S
is simply connected). We are not aware
of significant results on this topic. The analogous isometric immersion problem of a Riemannian surface in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}

is notoriously hard and largely unresolved except in the case of positive Gauss curvature
K_{\gamma}>0
; this is the solution of the
Weyl embedding problem by Nirenberg and Pogorelov.
3
3

3

Note that in this Riemannian situation, the condition
K_{\gamma}>0

is an open condition, so that there are open sets of
C^{\infty}
solutions of the constraint equations with given Dirichlet data, in
strong contrast with Proposition 2.1. Nevertheless, the constraint equations (
2.1
)-(
2.2
) are not a well-posed or elliptic
system, even when
K_{\gamma}>0
. Instead, the main idea is to quotient the space
\Met(S)
by the positive definite second fundamental

, where the problem does become (underdetermined) elliptic, cf.
[
20
]
,
[
3
]
,
[

]
. This method is thus restricted
to the convex case.

Passing to Dirichlet data on a Cauchy surface in 4 dimensions is the problem of isometrically immersing a given Riemannian 3-manifold
into some vacuum space-time. Again, we are not aware of significant results on this problem.

It seems even less is known in the setting of time-like boundaries, i.e. results or a theory of isometrically immersing Lorentzian
surfaces into flat
\mathbb{R}^{1,2}
or similarly, a theory of isometrically immersing Lorentzian 3-manifolds into a vacuum spacetime.
On the other hand, Proposition 2.4 shows the situation is very well understood for
([\gamma],H)
data (for all dimensions).

Remark 2.6
.

Once one solves the time-like constraint equations, if the data are analytic, then the Cauchy-Kovalevsky theorem can be
used to obtain actual vacuum solutions defined in a neighbhorhood of
{\mathcal{C}}
; this gives local two-sided solutions, i.e. solutions
extending to each side of the boundary
{\mathcal{C}}
. In more detail, given a pair
(\gamma,A)
of analytic data satisfying the constraint equations
(
2.1
)-(
2.2
), one may work in the gauge of geodesic normal coordinates normal to
{\mathcal{C}}
where
g(\nu,\cdot)=\delta_{\nu\,\cdot}
.
In this gauge, the Einstein equations are “evolution equations” off the boundary in the normal
\nu
-direction, and for analytic data the
Cauchy-Kovalevsky theorem gives the existence of analytic solutions in a neighborhood of
{\mathcal{C}}
. Of course the simple scalar
model here is the Laplace equation
\Delta u=0
or wave equation
\Box u=0
with prescribed Cauchy data on
{\mathcal{C}}
.

While this is not a well-posed boundary value problem, it is a delicate issue to understand whether a solution
g
defined in a
neighborhood of
{\mathcal{C}}
extends to a smooth solution on
M
.

In the discussion of geometric boundary conditions above, note that the number of degrees of freedom for the boundary data
(
\gamma
, or
A
, or
([\gamma],H)
) is
6
. This is the correct and expected number for the gauge group
\mathrm{Diff}_{0}(M)
of diffeomorphisms
fixing
{\mathcal{C}}
pointwise, i.e. (maximal) solutions with the same initial and boundary data are expected to be unique up to diffeomorphisms
fixing an initial slice
S
and the boundary
{\mathcal{C}}
. However, the space of all solutions is invariant under the larger gauge group

\mathrm{Diff}(M)
, which essentially differs from
\mathrm{Diff}_{0}(M)
by the group
\mathrm{Diff}({\mathcal{C}})
of boundary diffeomorphisms.
The group
\mathrm{Diff}({\mathcal{C}})
has 3 degrees of freedom and so the number of true boundary conditions describing non-isometric
solutions should be
6-3=3
. Of these
3
degrees of freedom,
2
account for the degrees of freedom of the gravitational field,
and
1
for the location or evolution of the boundary
{\mathcal{C}}
off the initial corner surface
\Sigma
. Note that it may not be easy to find a
global slice to (for instance) the space of Dirichlet data
\Met({\mathcal{C}})/\mathrm{Diff}({\mathcal{C}})
.

Next we discuss the boundary data introduced in
[
2
]
. This is based on the construction of a global preferred
harmonic-type gauge for a metric
(M,g)
, at least near the boundary
{\mathcal{C}}
. The preferred gauge depends on the
space-time
(M,g)
as well as a vector field
\Theta_{{\mathcal{C}}}
defined on
{\mathcal{C}}
; different choices of
\Theta_{{\mathcal{C}}}
give
different choices of time evolution or space-like foliation of the space-time
(M,g)
. The gauge is given by a wave map

(2.9)

\begin{array}[]{c}\varphi_{g}:(M,g)\rightarrow(M_{0},g_{R}),\\
\Box_{g}\varphi_{g}=0,\end{array}

where for simplicity the target space
M_{0}
is taken to be a standard solid cylinder
I\times B^{3}
in Minkowski space
\mathbb{R}^{1,3}
with
standard time function
t
. The Riemannian metric
g_{R}
is the associated flat Euclidean metric. Initial data for
\varphi_{g}
on a
Cauchy slice
S
are given by

(2.10)

\varphi_{g}=E_{g_{S}},\ \ (\varphi_{g})_{*}(T_{g})=T_{g_{R}}\ \ {\rm on}\ \ S,

where
T_{g}
is the
g
-future unit normal to
S
in
(M,g)
,
T_{g_{R}}
is the future unit normal to
\{t=0\}
in
M_{0}
and

E_{g_{S}}
is determined by
g_{S}=g|_{S}
equivariantly with respect to
\mathrm{Diff}(S)
near the boundary
\Sigma
, i.e.

E_{\psi^{*}(g_{S})}=\psi^{*}E_{g_{S}}=E_{g_{S}}\circ\psi.

We refer to
[
2
]
for details regarding the assignment
g_{S}\rightarrow E_{g_{S}}
and for concrete examples of such.

The boundary data for
\varphi_{g}
are of Sommerfeld type given by

(2.11)

[(\varphi_{g})_{*}(T_{g}+\nu_{g})]^{T}=\Theta_{{\mathcal{C}}},

together with the (scalar) Dirichlet condition that
\varphi_{g}:{\mathcal{C}}\rightarrow\partial M_{0}=I\times S^{2}
. Here
\nu
is the
g
-unit normal of

{\mathcal{C}}\subset M
and the superscript
T
denotes orthogonal projection with respect to
g_{R}
onto
\partial M_{0}
. Thus, we view
\Theta_{{\mathcal{C}}}

as a vector field tangent to
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}=\partial M_{0}
.

The equations (
2.9
) are hyperbolic and initial boundary value problems of this type are well-understood; it follows from such
known results that the IBVP (
2.9
)-(
2.11
) is well-posed. In particular, given a background metric
g
, there exists a unique
solution
\varphi_{g}
which is a diffeomorphism in a neighborhood of the initial and time-like boundary
T=S\cup{\mathcal{C}}
. Moreover (at least
near
T
), the solution is equivariant with respect to diffeomorphisms, so that

(2.12)

\varphi_{\psi^{*}g}=\psi^{*}\varphi_{g},

for any
\psi\in\mathrm{Diff}(M)
.

Next consider the coupled system
(g,\varphi_{g})

(2.13)

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathrm{Ric}_{g}=0,\\
\Box_{g}\varphi_{g}=0,\end{array}

with initial conditions for
g
given by

(2.14)

g|_{S}=g_{S},\ \ K_{g}|_{S}=K,

where
(g_{S},K)
satisfy the constraint equations (
2.1
)-(
2.2
). The boundary conditions for
g
are expressed on the target

{\mathcal{C}}_{0}=\partial M_{0}
as:

(2.15)

([\gamma^{t}],H^{*},\Theta_{{\mathcal{C}}}).

Here
\gamma^{t}=(\varphi_{g}^{-1})^{*}g|_{\Sigma_{t}}
, is the metric induced by the pullback
(\varphi_{g}^{-1})^{*}
of
g
to
M_{0}
restricted to the
slices
\Sigma_{t}=\{t=constant\}
in
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
and
[\cdot]
denotes the conformal class of the 2-metric. The term
H^{*}
is a combination
of mean curvatures of the form

(2.16)

H^{*}=\alpha\mathrm{tr}_{{\mathcal{C}}}A|_{\Sigma_{t}}+\beta\mathrm{tr}_{\Sigma_{t}}A+\gamma\mathrm{tr}_{\Sigma_{t}}K,

for certain values of
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
, (cf.
[
2
]
for the exact allowable coefficients). One admissible choice of
H^{*}
, which also appears
in
[
27
]
, is
H^{*}=2\mathrm{tr}_{{\mathcal{C}}}A-\mathrm{tr}_{\Sigma_{t}}A
; this choice will be discussed further in §4. The initial conditions for
\varphi_{g}
are
as in (
2.10
).

It is proved in
[
2
]
that this is a well-posed IBVP. For any choice of compatible initial data (
2.14
) and boundary data
(
2.15
), there is a vacuum solution
g
realizing the initial and boundary conditions. Such solutions have the covariance property

(2.17)

\bar{g}=\psi_{g}^{*}(g)=\psi_{\chi^{*}g}^{*}(\chi^{*}g),

for any
\chi\in\mathrm{Diff}(M)
, where
\psi_{g}=\varphi_{g}^{-1}
. This gives a slice (at least near the boundary
{\mathcal{C}}
) to the action of

\mathrm{Diff}(M)
on the space of vacuum solutions. Thus, diffeomorphisms no longer act on the metrics
\bar{g}
; these metrics are
evaluated in the fixed standard coordinates
(t,x^{i})
on
\mathbb{R}^{1,3}
. The solutions
\bar{g}
are unique with given initial and boundary
data and one has stability of solutions and the existence of maximal solutions. Moreover, any vacuum solution is isometric
to such a solution in a preferred gauge. Note that since the boundary data
([\gamma^{t}],H^{*},\Theta_{{\mathcal{C}}})
always
admit solutions, it follows that the constraint equations (
2.1
)-(
2.2
) on
{\mathcal{C}}
are automatically satisfied; in fact the
form of the
H^{*}
term in (
2.16
) derives partly from the form of the constraint equations.

An important point is that, by construction, the boundary conditions (
2.15
) are invariant under the action of

\mathrm{Diff}(M)|_{{\mathcal{C}}}=\mathrm{Diff}({\mathcal{C}})
on the space of vacuum solutions. However, the choice
of
\Theta_{{\mathcal{C}}}
determines a particular or preferred gauge
\varphi_{g}
, giving a particular foliation or 3+1 decomposition of
the space-time
(M,g)
in which the boundary data are measured. The relevant space of (geometric) boundary data is then

(2.18)

{\mathcal{B}}_{geom}=I\times\mathrm{Conf}(\Sigma)\times C^{\infty}(\Sigma)=\{(t,[\gamma^{t}]_{\Sigma_{t}},H^{*}_{\Sigma_{t}})\},

where
\mathrm{Conf}(\Sigma)
is the space of pointwise conformal classes
[\gamma^{t}]
of smooth metrics on
\Sigma
and
H^{*}\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma)
.
The space
{\mathcal{B}}_{geom}
has
3
degrees of freedom, corresponding to
\mathrm{Diff}({\mathcal{C}})
invariant boundary data.
This is in marked contrast with the discussion of geometric boundary data (Dirichlet, Neumann or
([\gamma],H)
) above, which are
not
\mathrm{Diff}({\mathcal{C}})
invariant.

The well-posedness of this IBVP then gives a family of effective parametrizations as in (
1.3
), i.e.

(2.19)

D_{S,\Theta_{{\mathcal{C}}}}:{\mathcal{V}}_{geom}\rightarrow\mathcal{I}_{geom}\times_{c}{\mathcal{B}}_{geom},

depending on the choice of Cauchy surface and boundary gauge
\Theta_{{\mathcal{C}}}
. The space
{\mathcal{V}}_{geom}
is now the space of full isometry
classes of solutions (vacuum Einstein metrics), i.e. the space of all solutions modulo the action of the full diffeomorphism group

\mathrm{Diff}(M)
. Accordingly
\mathcal{I}_{geom}
is the space of equivalence classes of Cauchy data
(g_{S},K)
on a Cauchy slice,
where
(g_{S},K)\sim(g_{S}^{\prime},K^{\prime})
if there is a space-time diffeomorphism
\chi\in\mathrm{Diff}(M)
,
\chi(S)=S
, such that

(\chi^{*}g_{S}^{\prime},\chi^{*}K^{\prime})=(g_{S},K)
.

A variational description and Hamiltonian analysis of this boundary data will be discussed in the following sections.

3.
The IBVP and Quasi-local Hamiltonians

In this section, we discuss the covariant phase space method applied to vacuum GR with a time-like boundary, following

[
21
]
,
[
28
]
,
[
22
]
.

Consider Lagrangians with boundary terms describing vacuum gravity,

(3.1)

I(g)=I_{EH}(g)+\int_{{\mathcal{C}}}\ell(g)=\int_{M}R_{g}dv_{g}+\int_{{\mathcal{C}}}\ell(g).

(We have chosen units where
16\pi G=1
).
The bulk Einstein-Hilbert action
I_{EH}
is invariant under the diffeomorphism group
\mathrm{Diff}(M)
; only boundary
actions
\int_{{\mathcal{C}}}\ell
which are also
\mathrm{Diff}({\mathcal{C}})
invariant will be considered. Different choices of boundary action
correspond to different choices of boundary data space
{\mathcal{B}}
. As a simple example, consider the case of a scalar field

u:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with action given by

(3.2)

I_{D}(u)=\int_{M}({\tfrac{1}{2}}|du|^{2}+V(u))dv_{g},

where
g
is a fixed background globally hyperbolic Lorentz metric on
M
and
V(u)
is a function involving only 0-jet of
u
.
Then the variation of
I_{D}
in the direction
v=u^{\prime}
is given by

\frac{d}{dt}I_{D}(u+tv)|_{t=0}=-\int_{M}v(\Box u-V^{\prime}(u))-\int_{{\mathcal{C}}}v\partial_{\nu}u-\int_{S^{+}\cup S^{-}}\Psi_{u}(v),

where
\Psi_{u}(v)=v\partial_{T}u
and
T
is the unit outward time-like normal to the future and past the slices
S^{\pm}
. This term is
ignored in the variational problem, cf.
[
21
]
, so that the variational derivative is given by

\delta I_{D}(v)=-\int_{M}v(\Box u-V^{\prime}(u))-\int_{{\mathcal{C}}}v\partial_{\nu}u.

The boundary condition that
I_{D}
is stationary
\delta I_{D}=0
then requires that
v=0
at
{\mathcal{C}}
, i.e. the Dirichlet data
u|_{{\mathcal{C}}}
of
u

is fixed. Hence the space
{\mathcal{B}}
is the space of Dirichlet boundary data of
u
,
{\mathcal{B}}=C^{\infty}({\mathcal{C}})
. The corresponding equations of
motion are

(3.3)

\Box u+V^{\prime}(u)=0.

If one adds a boundary action of the form

(3.4)

I_{N}(u)=\int_{M}({\tfrac{1}{2}}|du|^{2}+V(u))dv_{g}+\int_{{\mathcal{C}}}u\partial_{\nu}u,

then a similar analysis shows that the boundary condition becomes
\partial_{\nu}v=0
, i.e. the Neumann boundary data
\partial_{\nu}u

of
u
is fixed, with corresponding boundary data space again
{\mathcal{B}}=C^{\infty}({\mathcal{C}})
.

In either case above, one has a well-defined variational problem on the field or configuration space
{\mathcal{F}}=C_{b}^{\infty}(M)
of functions

u:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
u
satisfying a fixed boundary condition
b\in{\mathcal{B}}
(Dirichlet or Neumann) at the boundary
{\mathcal{C}}
; namely,

I_{D}
, resp.
I_{N}
, is a smooth function on
C^{\infty}(M)
whose critical points are solutions of the Euler-Lagrange equations
(
3.3
). Moreover, standard theory of the IBVP for the (non-linear) wave equation (
3.3
) implies that the space of solutions
\mathbb{P}
of
(
3.3
) satisfies the analog of (
1.3
),

\mathbb{P}\simeq\mathcal{I}\times_{c}{\mathcal{B}}.

The covariant phase space method discussed below defines a natural symplectic structure on
\mathbb{P}
, with corresponding Hamiltonians,
cf.
[
11
]
,
[
21
]
for further details.

We emphasize here that the issues of having a well-defined variational problem for a field theory and a well-posed IBVP for
the equations of motion are fundamentally distinct and independent. In particular, the property of having a well-defined variational
problem does not at all imply the property of having a well-posed IBVP.

Returning to GR and the action (
3.1
), let
{\mathcal{F}}=\Met(M)
be the configuration space of all maximal globally hyperbolic Lorentz metrics
on
M
with time-like boundary
{\mathcal{C}}
. The pre-phase space

\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}

is defined to be the space of globally hyperbolic solutions of the vacuum Einstein equations on
M
.
(The terminology on-shell for data in
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
and off-shell for data in
{\mathcal{F}}
is often used). The bulk diffeomorphism
group
\mathrm{Diff}(M)
, mapping
{\mathcal{C}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{C}}
, acts naturally on both spaces by pullback. Although it is geometrically
natural to consider the quotient space of isometric metrics
\Met(M)/\mathrm{Diff}(M)
, it will be seen below that the quotient

\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}/\mathrm{Diff}(M)
does not generally serve as a suitable candidate for the phase space.

We first discuss the “bare” situation without any boundary terms. For the bare EH action,

I_{EH}(g)=\int_{M}R_{g}dv_{g},

one has
\delta_{g}(R_{g}dv_{g})(h)=-\langle E_{g},h\rangle dv_{g}+d\theta(h)
where
d\theta(h)=(-\Box trh+\mathrm{div}\mathrm{div}h)dv_{g}
. The 3-form

(3.5)

\theta(h)=-\star(d\mathrm{tr}h-\mathrm{div}h),

is called the pre-symplectic potential and plays a central role in this discussion. Note that for any 2-form
C
on
M
,

d(\theta-dC)=d\theta
. On-shell, i.e. tangent to
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
,
(R_{g}dv_{g})^{\prime}=0
and
E_{g}=0
, so that, on
T\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
,

(3.6)

d\theta(h)=0

pointwise on
M
. In other words,
\theta
is a closed 3-form on
M
on-shell. It follows that

(3.7)

\frac{d}{dt}I_{EH}(g+th)|_{t=0}=0,

on-shell. Thus (in contrast to the situation in (
3.2
) or (
3.4
)) it might appear that there are
no
boundary conditions
imposed by the requirement of stationarity. However, (
3.7
) only gives
\int_{{\partial M}}(\theta-dC)(h)=0
, i.e.

\int_{{\mathcal{C}}}(\theta-dC)(h)=\int_{S^{+}}(\theta-dC)(h)-\int_{S^{-}}(\theta-dC)(h).

This shows there is a (global) coupling of the variation at the future and past Cauchy surfaces with the variation at the boundary
{\mathcal{C}}
.
For a well-defined variational problem, one requires instead that

(3.8)

\delta I_{EH}(h)=\int_{{\mathcal{C}}}(\theta-dC)(h)=0,

for all on-shell variations
h
.

On
{\mathcal{C}}
,
\theta(h)=-(\nu(\mathrm{tr}h)-\mathrm{div}h(\nu))dv_{\gamma}
is a gauge, i.e.
\mathrm{Diff}_{0}(M)
dependent term. To obtain a
geometric or gauge-invariant boundary condition, recall the basic identity (valid on any hypersurface)

(3.9)

-\theta(h)=\big(\nu(\mathrm{tr}h)-(\mathrm{div}h)(\nu)\big)dv_{\gamma}=\big(2H^{\prime}_{h}+\langle A,h\rangle+\mathrm{div}(h(\nu)^{T})\big)dv_{\gamma}.

It is natural to set

(3.10)

C(h)=\star h(\nu)^{T}\ \ {\rm on}\ \ {\mathcal{C}},

and we do so for the remainder of the paper; the discussion to follow is independent of the extension of
C
off
{\mathcal{C}}
. Also set

(3.11)

\widehat{\theta}=\theta-dC.

Then by (
3.9
), (
3.8
) is equivalent to

(3.12)

\int_{{\mathcal{C}}}(\langle A,h\rangle+2H^{\prime}_{h})dv_{\gamma}=0,

This corresponds to geometric boundary conditions
h^{T}=H^{\prime}_{h}=0
. However, these are now
7
boundary conditions and so these
are over-determined boundary conditions, (cf. the discussion in §2).

This shows that it is necessary to include boundary Lagrangians
\ell
to obtain a well-defined variational problem. To begin, the
action (
3.1
) is stationary on the subspace of
h\in T\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
such that

(3.13)

[(\theta-dC+\delta\ell)|_{{\mathcal{C}}}](h)=0.

Thus associate to the action (
3.1
) a space of (geometric) boundary data
{\mathcal{B}}
and consider the natural evaluation or restriction map

R:\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{B}},

equivariant with respect to the action of diffeomorphisms. Given a choice of boundary data
b\in{\mathcal{B}}
, the pre-phase space

\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}
is defined as the inverse image

(3.14)

\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}=R^{-1}(b):

\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}
is the space of vacuum metrics with boundary data in
b
. Then (
3.13
) is equivalent to

T\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}={\rm Ker}\,([\theta-dC+\delta\ell]_{{\mathcal{C}}}).

Via (
3.9
), the boundary condition (
3.13
) is thus equivalent to

(3.15)

-\langle A,h\rangle-2H^{\prime}_{h}+\ell^{\prime}_{h}=0\ \ {\rm on}\ \ {\mathcal{C}}.

Of course in general
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}
will no longer have an effective induced action of
\mathrm{Diff}(M)
; the choice of
b

breaks the symmetry, i.e. breaks the isometric action of
\mathrm{Diff}(M)
on
\Met(M)
to that of the subgroup
\mathrm{Diff}_{b}(M)

preserving the boundary condition
b\in{\mathcal{B}}
.

The pre-symplectic current
\omega
on
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}
, or more generally on
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
or
{\mathcal{F}}
, is given by

(3.16)

\omega=\delta\widehat{\theta},

where
\delta
is the exterior derivative on
{\mathcal{F}}
. Thus,

\omega(h_{1},h_{2})=(\widehat{\theta}(h_{2}))^{\prime}_{h_{1}}-(\widehat{\theta}(h_{1}))^{\prime}_{h_{2}},

where
h_{1}
,
h_{2}
are tangents to a 2-parameter deformation in
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}
, (so that the formal correction term

\theta([h_{1},h_{2}])
vanishes).

Given a Cauchy surface
S
, the pre-symplectic form is a 2-form on
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}
defined by

\widetilde{\Omega}(h_{1},h_{2})=\int_{S}\omega(h_{1},h_{2})=\int_{S}(\widehat{\theta}(h_{2}))^{\prime}_{h_{1}}-(\widehat{\theta}(h_{1}))^{\prime}_{h_{2}}.

Using (
3.9
), this gives

\widetilde{\Omega}(h_{1},h_{2})=-\int_{S}\langle K^{\prime}_{h_{1}},h_{2}\rangle-\langle K^{\prime}_{h_{2}},h_{1}\rangle+{\tfrac{1}{2}}[tr_{S}h_{1}(\langle K,h_{2}\rangle+2k^{\prime}_{h_{2}})-tr_{S}h_{2}(\langle K,h_{1}\rangle+2k^{\prime}_{h_{1}})],

where
K
is the extrinsic curvature of
S
in
(M,g)
and
k=tr_{S}K
. Writing
\pi=(K-kg_{S})dv_{g_{S}}
, where
K-kg_{S}
is
the momentum conjugate to
g_{S}
, this may be written (in standard form) as

(3.17)

\widetilde{\Omega}(h_{1},h_{2})=-\int_{S}\langle\pi^{\prime}_{h_{1}},h_{2}\rangle-\langle\pi^{\prime}_{h_{2}},h_{1}\rangle.

This gives a non-degenerate symplectic form off-shell, i.e. on the configuration space
{\mathcal{F}}
, cf.
[
28
]
. However, the vacuum constraints
and boundary conditions imply that
\widetilde{\Omega}
is degenerate on-shell, i.e. on
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
or
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}
.

The boundary condition (
3.13
) implies
\omega=\delta\delta\ell=0
on
{\mathcal{C}}
. Since
\omega
is a closed
3
-form on
M
, it follows
that
\widetilde{\Omega}
is well-defined, independent of the Cauchy surface
S
, on
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}
. This is not the case without the boundary
condition (
3.13
); thus
\widetilde{\Omega}=\widetilde{\Omega}_{S}
gives a pre-symplectic form on the full space
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
which depends on the
Cauchy surface
S
.

Hamiltonians are functions
{\mathcal{H}}:\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for which there is a vector field
X_{{\mathcal{H}}}
such that

(3.18)

\widetilde{\Omega}(X_{{\mathcal{H}}},\cdot)=\delta{\mathcal{H}}.

If
\widetilde{\Omega}
were non-degenerate, then any smooth function
{\mathcal{H}}:\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a Hamiltonian. Namely, setting
\widetilde{\Omega}=\Omega
in
the non-degenerate situation, the symplectic gradient
X_{{\mathcal{H}}}
is given by

X_{{\mathcal{H}}}=\Omega^{-1}(\delta{\mathcal{H}},\cdot),

where
\Omega
defines the pairing:
T\mathbb{P}\rightarrow T^{*}\mathbb{P}
. If, as it is here,
\widetilde{\Omega}
is degenerate, then Hamiltonians may not exist.
A Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}}
must be invariant under the flow generated by the degenerate directions
{\rm Ker}\,\widetilde{\Omega}
. Moreover,
{\mathcal{H}}

is always conserved, i.e.
{\mathcal{H}}
is invariant under the flow of the Hamiltonian vector field
X_{{\mathcal{H}}}
on
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}
.

The actual phase
\mathbb{P}_{b}
with boundary data
b\in{\mathcal{B}}
is given by a standard symplectic reduction process, cf.
[
30
]
, as the
formal quotient

(3.19)

\mathbb{P}_{b}=\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}/{\mathcal{G}},

where
{\mathcal{G}}
is the Lie group generated by the Lie algebra
{\rm Ker}\,\widetilde{\Omega}
. Then
\widetilde{\Omega}
descends to a (non-degenerate) symplectic
form on
\mathbb{P}_{b}
, giving it formally a symplectic structure.

As the primary example of interest here, consider a vector field
\xi
generating a flow in
\mathrm{Diff}(M)
by spacetime
diffeomorphisms of
M
. This generates a flow on
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}
in the usual way by pullback, with tangent vector
L_{\xi}g\in T_{g}\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}
.

As shown in
[
28
]
,
[
22
]
,
[
21
]
, the Hamiltonian

{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}
is given by

(3.20)

{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}=\int_{\Sigma}q_{\xi}+\xi\cdot\ell-C(L_{\xi}g),

where
q_{\xi}
is the Noether potential given by

q_{\xi}=-\star d\xi,

viewing
\xi
as a 1-form on
{\mathcal{C}}
. We consider
\ell
as a scalar density,
\ell=\ell dv_{\gamma}
on
{\mathcal{C}}
, so that
\xi\cdot\ell=\xi\lfloor dv_{{\mathcal{C}}}
. On
\Sigma
,
\star d\xi=d\xi(T,\nu)=\langle\nabla_{T}\xi,\nu\rangle-\langle\nabla_{\nu}\xi,T\rangle
,
where
\nu
is as above and
T
is the unit normal to
\Sigma\subset{\mathcal{C}}
. Since
L_{\xi}g(T,\nu)=\langle\nabla_{T}\xi,\nu\rangle+\langle\nabla_{\nu}\xi,T\rangle
and
\langle\nabla_{T}\xi,\nu\rangle=-A(\xi,T)
, this gives

(3.21)

{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}=\int_{\Sigma}(-2A(\xi,T)+\ell\langle\xi,T\rangle)dv_{\gamma_{\Sigma}}.

Note that
{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}
is independent of the slice
\Sigma
since the variation
h=L_{\xi}g
satisfies the boundary
condition (
3.13
), i.e.
L_{\xi}g\in T\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}
. Otherwise, as with
\widetilde{\Omega}
, this will not be the case for general

\xi\in T\mathrm{Diff}(M)
.

Clearly if
\xi=0
on
{\mathcal{C}}
(or on suitable domains in
{\mathcal{C}}
), then
{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}=\delta{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}=0
. so the symplectic form
\widetilde{\Omega}
is
degenerate on vectors tangent to
\mathrm{Diff}_{0}(M)
. Thus one always has

(3.22)

\mathrm{Diff}_{0}(M)\subset{\mathcal{G}}.

On the other hand, the existence of non-trivial Hamiltonians generated by
\xi
as above shows that generally
\mathrm{Diff}_{b}(M)
is not
contained in
{\mathcal{G}}
.

Finally we note that a Hamiltonian
H_{\xi}:\mathbb{P}_{b}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as in (
3.20
) is determined by (
3.18
) only up to an
additive constant; the constant may depend on the boundary condition
b\in{\mathcal{B}}
. The choice of the normalizing
constant plays an important role in Hamitonian approaches to the issue of quasi-local energy and is discussed in more detail in §5.

The covariant phase space method is related to the perhaps more well-known canonical phase space method by the choice of a
Cauchy surface
S
. Namely, given such an
S
, one has a
formal
identification

(3.23)

E_{S}:\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{b}\simeq\mathcal{I},\ \ E_{S}(g)=(g_{S},K),

where
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{b}=\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}/\mathrm{Diff}_{0}(M)
and
\mathcal{I}
is the space of initial data
(g_{S},K)
satisfying the constraint
equations. Here
\mathrm{Diff}_{0}(M)
is the group of diffeomorphisms of
M
fixing
T=S\cup{\mathcal{C}}
. Of course different choices
of Cauchy surface give different parametrizations
E_{S}
. The data
(g_{S},K)
, or more precisely
(g_{S},\pi)
, serve as the “
q
,
p

variables” for equivalence classes of solutions in
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{b}
and so such data effectively serve as a choice of coordinates for

\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{b}
. Solutions
g\in\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}
are then given by a specific choice of time evolution along a curve of Cauchy surfaces

S_{t}
with associated lapse-shift data
(N,X)
.

This brief summary of the covariant phase space method with boundary neglects however several significant issues.
As discussed in §2, there are seemingly natural situations, e.g. Dirichlet boundary data where
{\mathcal{B}}=\Met({\mathcal{C}})
, for
which the identification (
3.23
) fails badly. For generic boundary data
b\in{\mathcal{B}}
, one may have
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}=\emptyset
.
Moreover, the structure of
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}
may be highly unstable with respect to variations of
b\in{\mathcal{B}}
.
For the purely formal considerations outlined above to accurately reflect the behavior of the space of solutions
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
or
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}

(or their symplectic quotients), these spaces should be smooth infinite dimensional manifolds (or with small, understood singular
sets) with the submanifolds
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}
varying smoothly with
b
(at least generically). For this, one needs at least the
a priori

existence of charts for these spaces effectively parametrizing them locally.

It appears that the only method to do this is to prove that the IBVP associated to
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}
,
b\in{\mathcal{B}}
, is well-posed. In this case,
one has natural parametrizations

(3.24)

\widehat{\mathbb{P}}=\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}/\mathrm{Diff}_{0}(M)\simeq\mathcal{I}\times_{c}{\mathcal{B}},

as in (
1.3
). As discussed in §2, this does not hold for Dirichlet or Neumann boundary data. Further, based on the analogous
result for closed or asymptotically flat Cauchy surfaces
S
, cf.
[
15
]
, one would expect that

\widehat{\mathbb{P}}\simeq\mathbb{P},

at least generically.

Remark 3.1
.

An alternate path to the definition of Hamiltonians is the Hamilton-Jacobi method, used for instance by Brown-York
[
10
]
,
cf. also
[
37
]
for a survey. Here one also considers the space
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
of (maximal) globally hyperbolic vacuum solutions on
M

and a corresponding space
{\mathcal{B}}
of boundary data (Dirichlet data in the case of
[
10
]
). Analogous to the Hamilton-Jacobi method in
classical mechanics, a Hamiltonian is defined as the variation of the on-shell action (
3.1
) with respect to a choice of time translation
\partial_{t}

of the boundary data
b\in{\mathcal{B}}
, expressed in terms of a lapse-shift
(N,X)
decomposition of
{\mathcal{C}}
. For this approach to be effective,
one needs to know that the action
\mathcal{I}:\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function of
b
and so in particular the space

\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}
varies smoothly with
b
. Again, as discussed in §2, this cannot be expected to be true for Dirichlet
(or Neumann) boundary data.

Remark 3.2
.

A shortcoming of the Hamiltonian approach above is that Hamiltonians associated to infinitesimal generators
\xi\in T\mathrm{Diff}({\mathcal{C}})

only exist for
\xi
preserving the boundary condition, i.e.
\xi\in T\mathrm{Diff}_{b}({\mathcal{C}})
. For generic boundary data, no such
\xi

exist and so the theory applies only in very special circumstances.

One would thus like to develop a well-defined Hamiltonian for arbitrary
\xi\in T\mathrm{Diff}({\mathcal{C}})
. Note first that it
follows easily from the constraint equations that
\delta I(L_{\xi}g)=0
for any vector field
\xi
of
compact support on
{\mathcal{C}}
(for any diffeomorphism invariant boundary action). More generally, for
h=L_{\xi}g
, the constraint
equations show that the boundary condition (
3.15
) may be rewritten in the form

(3.25)

\mathrm{div}(\tau(\xi))+\mathrm{div}(\ell\xi)+\mathrm{div}h(\nu)^{T}-dC(h)=0\ \ {\rm on}\ \ {\mathcal{C}}.

Hence for infinitesimal diffeomorphisms, the boundary condition (
3.13
) or (
3.15
) is only a condition at the
future and past boundaries
\Sigma^{\pm}
. This suggests that ideally one should seek boundary conditions which are
preserved under the action of
\mathrm{Diff}({\mathcal{C}})
.

Nevertheless, (
3.25
) does not imply one can construct Hamiltonians with, for example, diffeomorphism-invariant Dirichlet boundary
data, i.e. boundary data in the quotient space
\Met({\mathcal{C}})/\mathrm{Diff}({\mathcal{C}})
. For such a larger space of admissible boundary data,
Hamiltonians will no longer be conserved and it is necessary to work in the context of time-dependent Hamiltonians. This has been done
using extended phase space techniques in work of Booth
[
8
]
and Booth-Fairhurst
[
9
]
.

The covariant phase space method applied to the
\mathrm{Diff}({\mathcal{C}})
-invariant boundary data in
[
2
]
discussed in §2 is rather
different from the discussion above. To explain the situation, as before let
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
be the space of all (maximal, globally hyperbolic)
vacuum Einstein metrics on
M
. Given a Cauchy surface
S\subset M
, and a choice of boundary vector field
\Theta_{{\mathcal{C}}}
on

{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
as in (
2.11
), as discussed in §2 there is a unique wave map
\varphi_{g}:(M,g)\rightarrow(M_{0},g_{R})
associated to
g
. As in
(
2.12
), the assignment
g\rightarrow\varphi_{g}
is equivariant with respect to the action of
\mathrm{Diff}(M)
on
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
. Let

\psi_{g}=\varphi_{g}^{-1}
and let
\chi({\mathcal{C}}_{0})
be the space of vector fields on the boundary
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
. Define then a map

(3.26)

\begin{array}[]{c}\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}\times\chi({\mathcal{C}}_{0})\rightarrow\bar{\mathbb{P}},\\
(g,\Theta_{{\mathcal{C}}})\rightarrow\bar{g}=\psi_{g}^{*}g,\end{array}

where
\bar{\mathbb{P}}
is the space of vacuum metrics on
M_{0}
in the standard
(t,x^{i})
Minkowski coordinates.
The group
\mathrm{Diff}(M)
acts on the first factor on the left and acts trivially on the second and the map (
3.26
)
descends to the quotient to give a bijection

(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}\times\chi({\mathcal{C}}_{0}))/\mathrm{Diff}(M)\rightarrow\bar{\mathbb{P}}.

Variations
\bar{h}
of
\bar{g}
are of the form

(3.27)

\bar{h}=\psi_{g}^{*}h-L_{X_{h}}\bar{g}-L_{X_{\Theta^{\prime}}}\bar{g}\in T\bar{\mathbb{P}},

where
h
is an on-shell variation of
g
and
X_{h}
,
X_{\Theta^{\prime}}
are the variations of the wave map
\varphi_{g}
induced by the
variation
h
of
g
(with
\Theta_{{\mathcal{C}}}
fixed) and variations
\Theta^{\prime}
of
\Theta_{{\mathcal{C}}}
with
g
fixed. Regarding the latter,
fix a vacuum solution
g
and consider variations
\Theta_{{\mathcal{C}}}(t)
of the Sommerfeld boundary data
\Theta_{{\mathcal{C}}}
for
\varphi_{g}
,
satisfying the compatibility conditions at the corner
\Sigma
, holding the initial conditions fixed. One has unique wave map
solutions
\varphi_{g}(t)
of (
2.9
)-(
2.11
) realizing the boundary conditions
\Theta_{{\mathcal{C}}}(t)
. By uniqueness, the pair

(g,\varphi_{g}(t))
are solutions of the coupled system (
2.13
)-(
2.15
) satisfying the initial data and boundary data

([\gamma^{t}],H^{*},\Theta_{{\mathcal{C}}}(t))
. This gives a curve of gauges, i.e. 3+1 decomposition or space-time foliation of the fixed metric

g
. Then

(3.28)

X_{\Theta^{\prime}}=\frac{d}{dt}\varphi_{g}(t)|_{t=0},

where
\Theta^{\prime}=\frac{d}{dt}\Theta_{{\mathcal{C}}}(t)
.

One may now carry out the covariant phase space method on the space
\bar{\mathbb{P}}
. The definition of the corresponding pre-symplectic
current
\bar{\theta}
is just as in (
3.5
), acting on
\bar{h}\in T\bar{\mathbb{P}}
. Similarly
\bar{\omega}
is defined as in (
3.16
), leading to
the (pre)-symplectic form

(3.29)

\bar{\Omega}(\bar{h}_{1},\bar{h}_{2})=\int_{S_{0}}\langle\pi^{\prime}_{\bar{h}_{1}},\bar{h}_{2}\rangle-\langle\pi^{\prime}_{\bar{h}_{2}},\bar{h}_{1}\rangle,

where
S_{0}=\{t=0\}
. Although

,
\bar{\omega}
is non-zero on
{\mathcal{C}}
in general, without any boundary condition, so that

\bar{\Omega}
is not conserved, i.e. independent of the Cauchy slice
S_{0}
.

A boundary action and associated boundary condition is discussed below in §4, cf. Proposition 4.3. We also introduce there an
expanded phase space allowing for the existence of non-trivial Hamiltonians; see Propositions 4.4 and 4.5 below.

4.
Boundary actions in GR

In this section, we consider several boundary actions
\int_{{\mathcal{C}}}\ell
for the Einstein-Hilbert action
I_{EH}
and discuss
the resulting Hamiltonians generated by space-time diffeomorphisms.

The physics literature has long (only) considered the Gibbons-Hawking-York boundary term
\frac{1}{8\pi}\int_{{\mathcal{C}}}Hdv_{\gamma}

and the corresponding action

I_{GHY}=I_{EH}+2\int_{{\mathcal{C}}}Hdv_{\gamma},

(where again we set
16\pi G=1
). The variation of the boundary Lagrangian is
(H^{\prime}_{h}+\frac{1}{2}\langle H\gamma,h^{T}\rangle)dv_{\gamma}
,
where
h^{T}
is the variation of the boundary metric
\gamma
. Inserting this in (
3.15
) gives the well-known
formula

(4.1)

\delta I_{GHY}=-2\int_{{\mathcal{C}}}\langle\tau,h^{T}\rangle dv_{\gamma},

on
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
where
\tau=A-H\gamma
is the momentum conjugate to the boundary metric
\gamma
. The form
\tau
is also the
Brown-York stress-energy tensor of the boundary
{\mathcal{C}}
, i.e. the variation of the action with respect to variation of the Dirichlet boundary
data, cf.
[
10
]
,
[
21
]
.

This gives a well-defined variational problem, i.e. (
3.13
) holds, for Dirichlet boundary conditions
h^{T}=0
, so that

{\mathcal{B}}=Met({\mathcal{C}})
. However, as discussed in §2, Dirichlet boundary conditions are not well-posed for the IBVP; for
generic boundary data
\gamma
, there is not any vacuum solution realizing
\gamma
, i.e.
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{\gamma}=\emptyset
.

Nevertheless, one may formally compute the Hamiltonian of a vector field
\xi
tangent to
{\mathcal{C}}
preserving the boundary
condition, i.e.
\xi
a Killing field on
({\mathcal{C}},\gamma)
. Simple computation from (
3.21
) gives

(4.2)

{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}^{Dir}=-2\int_{\Sigma}\tau(\xi,T)dv_{\gamma_{\Sigma}}.

This is the Dirichlet or Brown-York “bare” Hamiltonian; normalizations of
{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}^{Dir}
corresponding to a choice of zero-point
energy are discussed further in §5. One may of course always define (
4.2
) formally or by fiat, but the Hamiltonian interpretation
is only well-defined in the special circumstances where the boundary
({\mathcal{C}},\gamma)
admits a Killing field, cf. Remark 3.2.
Recall also from §3 that in this case the Hamiltonian is independent of the choice of slice
\Sigma
.

Consider next the boundary conditions
([\gamma],H)
in (
2.7
) which are conjectured to be well-posed for the IBVP,
cf. also
[
4
]
.

Proposition 4.1
.

The boundary conditions
([\gamma],H)
have a well-defined variational formulation. In fact, the action

(4.3)

I_{CH}=\int_{M}Rdv_{g}+\frac{2}{3}\int_{{\mathcal{C}}}Hdv_{\gamma},

has variational derivative given by

(4.4)

\delta I_{CH}(h)=-\int_{M}\langle E,h\rangle dv_{g}-\int_{{\mathcal{C}}}(\langle\tau_{0},h^{T}_{0}\rangle+\frac{4}{3}H^{\prime}_{h})dv_{\gamma},

where the subscript
0
denotes the trace-free part with respect to
({\mathcal{C}},\gamma)
.

If
\xi
is a vector field on
{\mathcal{C}}
which preserves the boundary conditions, i.e.
\xi
is a conformal Killing field preserving
H
, then

(4.5)

{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}^{CH}=-2\int_{\Sigma}\tau_{0}(\xi,T)dv_{\gamma_{\Sigma}}.

Proof.

The first statement follows from (
4.1
) and the simple computation
-\frac{4}{3}(\int Hdv_{\gamma})_{h}^{\prime}=-\frac{4}{3}\int(H^{\prime}_{h}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}h)dv_{\gamma}
. The second statement also follows easily from (
3.21
).

∎

Remark 4.2
.

The on-shell actions
I_{GHY}
and
I_{CH}
are essentially the same; one has
I_{CH}={\tfrac{1}{3}}I_{GHY}
on
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
,
so that on-shell

(4.6)

\delta I_{CH}={\tfrac{1}{3}}\delta I_{GHY}.

However, the expressions (
4.2
) and (
4.5
) for the Hamiltonians associated to
\xi
are not related in this simple way.
The reason for this is the different boundary conditions; Dirichlet for
I_{GHY}
and conformal class-mean curvature for
I_{CH}
.
Distinct boundary conditions give different spaces
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{b}
and so different symplectic forms and hence different Hamiltonians.

Suppose
\xi
is a Killing field on
({\mathcal{C}},\gamma)
which also preserves the mean curvature
\xi(H)=0
, so that
h=L_{\xi}g
preserves
both both boundary conditions, i.e.
h\in T(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{\gamma})\cap T(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{([\gamma],H)})
. By (
3.21
), the Hamiltonians
{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}^{Dir}
and

{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}^{CH}
have the same relation (
4.6
) as the actions,

(4.7)

{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}^{CH}={\tfrac{1}{3}}{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}^{Dir},

if and only if the Noether charge vanishes,

(4.8)

Q_{\xi}=\int_{\Sigma}A(\xi,T)dv_{\gamma_{\Sigma}}=0.

We note that it can be shown that if
\xi
extends to a (stationary) Killing field of the bulk solution
(M,g)
, then (
4.8
) does hold.
However, it appears unlikely that (
4.8
) holds in general.

Next we point out that the
\mathrm{Diff}({\mathcal{C}})
-invariant boundary data
([\gamma^{t}],H^{*})
from
[
2
]
may be given a
variational formulation for a natural choice of
H^{*}
, at least when the Sommerfeld boundary condition (
2.11
) for the
gauge
\Theta_{{\mathcal{C}}}
is modified to the Dirichlet boundary condition

(4.9)

(\varphi_{g})_{*}(T_{g}^{c})=\widehat{\Theta}_{{\mathcal{C}}}.

Here
T_{g}^{c}
is the future-pointing time-like unit normal to
\varphi_{g}^{-1}(\Sigma_{t})\subset({\mathcal{C}},g)
and
\widehat{\Theta}_{{\mathcal{C}}}
is a
given vector field tangent to
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
.

Recall from §2 that we work with vacuum solutions in preferred gauge
\varphi_{g}
and let
\bar{g}=\psi_{g}^{*}g
,
\psi_{g}=\varphi_{g}^{-1}
,
defined on the standard cylinder
M_{0}
. Let
\mathrm{tr}_{\Sigma}A
be the trace of
A
along the level set
\Sigma_{t}=\{t=constant\}\subset{\mathcal{C}}_{0}

of the boundary
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}\subset(M_{0},\bar{g})
.

Proposition 4.3
.

The action

(4.10)

I_{AA}(\bar{g})=\int_{M_{0}}R_{\bar{g}}dv_{\bar{g}}+\int_{{\mathcal{C}}}\mathrm{tr}_{\Sigma}Adv_{\bar{\gamma}},

gives a well-defined variational problem for the boundary data
([\gamma^{t}],H^{*},\widehat{\Theta}_{{\mathcal{C}}})
, where

(4.11)

H^{*}=2\mathrm{tr}_{{\mathcal{C}}}A-\mathrm{tr}_{\Sigma}A.

Thus, critical points of the action on the field space
{\mathcal{F}}_{b}=\Met_{b}(M_{0})
with fixed boundary data
b=([\gamma^{t}],H^{*},\widehat{\Theta}_{{\mathcal{C}}})

are the vacuum Einstein metrics with given boundary data
b
.

Proof.

Recall from (
3.27
) that general variations
\bar{h}
of
\bar{g}
are of the form

\bar{h}=\psi^{*}_{g}h-L_{X_{h}}\bar{g}
. The variation of the bulk Lagrangian is

\begin{split}&\delta(R_{\bar{g}}dv_{\bar{g}})=\langle-E_{\bar{g}},\psi^{*}h\rangle dv_{\bar{g}}+\langle E_{\bar{g}},L_{X}\bar{g}\rangle dv_{\bar{g}}+{\rm div}[{\rm div}\bar{h}-(d\mathrm{tr}\bar{h})]dv_{\bar{g}}\\
&=\langle-E_{\bar{g}},\psi^{*}h\rangle dv_{\bar{g}}+{\rm div}[2E_{\bar{g}}(X)+{\rm div}\bar{h}-(d\mathrm{tr}\bar{h})]dv_{\bar{g}},\end{split}

where we have applied the Bianchi identity in the second equality. Thus the equations of motion generated by
I_{AA}
are the
vacuum Einstein equations. Using (
3.9
) or (
3.15
), the on-shell variation of the action

with respect to deformation

(4.12)

\delta I_{AA}(\bar{h})=\int_{{\mathcal{C}}}(-\langle A,\bar{h}\rangle-2(\mathrm{tr}_{{\mathcal{C}}}A)^{\prime}_{\bar{h}}+(\mathrm{tr}_{\Sigma}A)^{\prime}_{\bar{h}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}_{\Sigma}A\mathrm{tr}_{{\mathcal{C}}}\bar{h})dv_{\bar{\gamma}}.

Let
\bar{h}_{\Sigma}
be the restriction of
\bar{h}
to
\Sigma_{t}
and let
(\bar{h}_{\Sigma})_{0}
denote its trace-free part. The first and fourth terms in
(

) may then be combined and rewritten as

-\langle A,\bar{h}\rangle+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}_{\Sigma}A\langle\bar{\gamma},\bar{h}\rangle=-\langle A,(\bar{h}_{\Sigma})_{0}\rangle-\langle q(T),\bar{h}(T)\rangle
, where
q(T)=2A(T)_{\Sigma}+(-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}_{\Sigma}A+A(T,T))T
. Also, setting
H^{*}=2\mathrm{tr}_{{\mathcal{C}}}A-\mathrm{tr}_{\Sigma}A
, i.e. setting
\alpha=2
,
\beta=-1
,
\gamma=0

in (
2.16
), (

) may be rewritten in the form

(4.13)

\delta I_{AA}(\bar{h})=-\int_{{\mathcal{C}}}\langle A,(\bar{h}_{\Sigma})_{0}\rangle+(H^{*})^{\prime}_{\bar{h}}+\langle q(T),\bar{h}(T)\rangle)dv_{\bar{\gamma}}

The vanishing of the terms
(\bar{h}_{\Sigma})_{0}
,
(H^{*})^{\prime}_{\bar{h}}
and

is equivalent to fixing the boundary data

([\gamma^{t}],H^{*},\widehat{\Theta}_{{\mathcal{C}}})
.

∎

We note that for fixed

(g,\varphi_{g})
as in (

In this setting, the phase space
\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{b}
may be defined as in (

in
(

.
This is partly due to the fact that the boundary conditions are

invariant but mainly due to the dependence of the

\varphi_{g}
on the choice of the Cauchy surface

, as in (

In order to obtain non-trivial Hamiltonians, we consider the same action on an enlarged space consisting of the pairs
(g,F)
where

denotes a general diffeomorphism

which maps
{\mathcal{C}}
to
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}

\bar{g}=(F^{-1})^{*}g

defined on

. Choose fixed boundary data

\big([\gamma^{t}],H^{*},\widehat{\Theta}_{\mathcal{C}}\big)

be the configuration space

(4.14)

{\bf C}_{b}=\{(g,F):~\bar{g}=(F^{-1})^{*}g\mbox{ has boundary data }b=\big([\gamma^{t}],H^{*},\widehat{\Theta}_{\mathcal{C}}\big)\ {\rm with}\ F|_{{\mathcal{C}}}=G\}.

Proposition 4.4
.

The action

(4.15)

\bar{I}_{AA}(\bar{g})=\int_{M_{0}}R_{\bar{g}}dv_{\bar{g}}+\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{0}}\mathrm{tr}_{\Sigma}A_{\bar{g}}dv_{\bar{\gamma}},

gives a well-defined variational problem on

Thus, critical points of the action on

.

A vector field
\xi
on
{\mathcal{C}}
which preserves the boundary conditions
([\gamma^{t}],H^{*},\widehat{\Theta}_{{\mathcal{C}}})
has an associated Hamiltonian

(4.17)

{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}^{AA}=\int_{\Sigma}-2A_{\bar{g}}(\bar{\xi},T_{\bar{g}})+\langle\bar{\xi},T_{\bar{g}}\rangle\mathrm{tr}_{\Sigma}A_{\bar{g}}dv_{\bar{\gamma}_{\Sigma}},

where
\bar{\xi}=G_{*}(\xi)
.

Proof.

The proof of the first statement is the same as the proof of Proposition 4.3, with deformation

of
g

replaced by the deformation
(h,X)
of
(g,F)\in{\bf C}_{b}
and with corresponding deformation

\bar{g}

\bar{h}=(F^{-1})^{*}h-L_{X}\bar{g}
of
\bar{g}

gives a well-defined

{\bf C}_{b}
for any

The resulting pre-phase space
\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{b}
is given by

(4.18)

\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{b}=\{(g,F)\in{\bf C}_{b}:\mathrm{Ric}_{g}=0\}.

The pre-symplectic potential

\bar{\theta}(\bar{h})=\star_{\bar{g}}[\mathrm{div}\bar{h}-(d\mathrm{tr}\bar{h})]
. This leads as before to the pre-symplectic form as in (

5.
Normalized Hamiltonians

As noted in the Introduction, several approaches to the definition of quasi-local energy-momentum or angular momentum of a space-like
2-surface
\Sigma
are based on a Hamiltonian or the closely related Hamilton-Jacobi approach. This was initiated by Brown-York
[
10
]
,
with modifications developed by numerous authors including Epp, Kijowski, Liu-Yau, Wang-Yau and others; we refer to
[
37
]
for a detailed
survey and further references. For all of these definitions, the space
{\mathcal{B}}
of boundary data is the Dirichlet data space
{\mathcal{B}}=\Met({\mathcal{C}})
; the
various definitions of quasi-local energies (or momenta) differ by a choice of normalization term, i.e. a choice of zero-point energy (or momenta).

In general, the starting point of such a Hamiltonian approach is a space-like surface
\Sigma
with data on
\Sigma
obtained by fixing a choice of
boundary data
b\in{\mathcal{B}}
restricted to
\Sigma
. One assumes then that
\Sigma
with such data can be extended as a Cauchy slice in a time-like
hypersurface
{\mathcal{C}}=\partial M
, where
(M,g)
is a vacuum space-time. Moreover, one assumes the extension to
{\mathcal{C}}
has a vector
field
\xi\in T{\mathcal{C}}
with flow preserving the boundary condition
b\in{\mathcal{B}}
. For Dirichlet boundary data, this means
\xi
is a
Killing field of
({\mathcal{C}},\gamma)
,
L_{\xi}\gamma=0
. If
\xi
is time-like, then the Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}
serves as a notion of bare
quasi-local energy.

Recall from (
4.2
) that the bare Brown-York quasi-local energy is given by

(5.1)

{\mathcal{H}}^{Dir}=-2\int_{\Sigma}\tau(\xi,T)dv_{\gamma_{\Sigma}},

where
T
is the unit time-like normal to
\Sigma\subset{\mathcal{C}}
. Here the data on
\Sigma
consist of the specification of a time-like
3-metric
\gamma
on
{\mathcal{C}}
(Dirichlet data) restricted to
\Sigma
for which
\xi
is a Killing field at
\Sigma
. The initial choice of Brown-York
is simply to take

(5.2)

\gamma=-dt^{2}+\gamma_{\Sigma},

with
\xi=T=\partial_{t}
representing time translation. A simple computation shows that

(5.3)

{\mathcal{H}}^{Dir}=-2\int_{\Sigma}H_{\Sigma}dv_{\gamma_{\Sigma}},

where
H_{\Sigma}=\mathrm{tr}_{\Sigma}A
.

This expression is of course non-vanishing for general surfaces
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the prescription of Brown-York is to
normalize the bare Hamiltonian by subtracting its Euclidean value:

(5.4)

{\mathcal{H}}^{BY}=2\int_{\Sigma}(H_{\Sigma}^{0}-H_{\Sigma})dv_{\gamma_{\Sigma}},

where
H_{\Sigma}^{0}
is the mean curvature of an isometric immersion
\iota(\Sigma,\gamma_{\Sigma})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Thus, the subtraction term is
the Brown-York Hamiltonian of
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}=\mathbb{R}\times\iota(\Sigma)\subset\mathbb{R}^{1,3}
with respect to the standard time translation
t

in Minkowski space. At least in the case of
\gamma_{\Sigma}
with positive Gauss curvature, such an embedding exists by fundamental work
of Nirenberg and Pogorelov, and is unique (up to congruence) by fundamental work of Cohn-Vossen. Unfortunately, little is known
about such existence and uniqueness results outside the case of positive Gauss curvature.

More generally, the Wang-Yau energy
[
40
]
considers families of isometric embeddings
(\Sigma,\gamma_{\Sigma})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{1,3}
and
chooses the reference cylinder
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
to be spanned by arbitrary Minkowski time-translation fields transverse to
\iota(\Sigma)
. This may be
viewed as a space-time generalization of the Brown-York prescription and requires developing an understanding of the possible
isometric immersions
\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{1,3}
, carried out in detail in
[
40
]
.

Returning to the general expression (
5.1
), since Dirichlet data are not well-posed, it is of course not well-understood when

(\Sigma,\gamma_{\Sigma})
can be embedded in
({\mathcal{C}},\gamma)\subset(M,g)
with time-like Killing field
\xi
on
{\mathcal{C}}
, as in (
5.2
) for instance.
Thus, (
5.1
) can only be considered as being formally defined, not well-defined.

Note also that even if (
5.1
) is defined, i.e. one has a vacuum space-time
(M,g)
with boundary
({\mathcal{C}},\gamma)
extending
\Sigma
and
possessing a time-like Killing field
\xi
, the Euclidean (Brown-York) or Minkowski (Wang-Yau) subtraction term above may not be defined.
To make this explicit, consider the space
\Met_{0}({\mathcal{C}})
of Lorentz metrics on
{\mathcal{C}}
with a time-like Killing field
\xi
. Such metrics are
parametrized by metrics
\Met(\Sigma)
on
\Sigma
, together with a choice of lapse and shift
(N,X)
on
\Sigma
. This data is time-independent,
i.e. extended to be invariant under the flow of
\xi
, but the data
(N,X)
may be arbitrarily chosen over
\Sigma
. However, in
\mathbb{R}^{1,3}

there is only a 4-dimensional space of time-translation Killing fields; thus most Killing fields on
({\mathcal{C}},\gamma)
cannot be renormalized to
zero-point energy in this way.

Remark 5.1
.

When the IBVP is well-posed so that (
3.23
) holds, a Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}:\mathbb{P}_{b}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
becomes a function on the space of
initial data
{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}:\mathcal{I}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. The normalization terms above then correspond to solutions evaluated on flat initial data

(g_{S},K)
. Recall that
(g_{S},K)
represent flat initial data if and only if the flat constraint equations hold; in schematic form

(5.5)

\begin{array}[]{c}dK=0,\\
R_{g_{S}}+K^{2}=0.\end{array}

The traces of these equations give the vacuum Einstein constraint equations (
2.1
)-(
2.2
). If (
5.5
) holds, then by the
fundamental theorem of hypersurfaces in space-forms,
S
has an isometric immersion into
\mathbb{R}^{1,3}
unique up to isometry of

\mathbb{R}^{1,3}
, (at least when
S
is simply connected). In the time-symmetric (Brown-York) case,
K=0
and so
g_{S}
is a flat metric
on
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, or more precisely an immersion of
S
into
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. However, in general it is not well understood when a
given geometry at the boundary
\Sigma
, i.e. boundary data
b\in{\mathcal{B}}
, has a fill-in by a flat metric on
S
, or when such fillings are unique.

We consider instead a natural definition of the normalization term that is formally valid in general:

Definition 5.2
.

Given a Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}:\mathbb{P}_{b}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
associated with a boundary condition
b\in{\mathcal{B}}
, define the normalized Hamiltonian by

\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}={\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}-\inf_{\mathbb{P}_{b}}{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}.

For this to be well-defined, one needs of course

(5.6)

\inf_{\mathcal{I}}{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}>-\infty.

When the identification (
3.23
) holds, so that
\mathbb{P}_{b}\simeq\mathcal{I}
, this gives the more concrete expression

(5.7)

\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}={\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}-\inf_{\mathcal{I}}{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}.

An advantage of this definition is that it immediately implies the positivity property:

\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}\geq 0\ \ {\rm on}\ \ \mathcal{I}.

Consider first the time-symmetric case where
K=0
. If there exists a flat solution with time-symmetric initial data, i.e. flat initial data

(g_{S}^{0},0)\in\mathcal{I}
filling in the boundary data
b\in{\mathcal{B}}
, then it is natural to ask if the infimum
\inf_{\mathcal{I}}{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}
is realized by flat
data:

\inf_{\mathcal{I}}{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}={\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}(g_{S}^{0},0),

i.e.

\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}(g_{S}^{0},K^{0})=0.

It is also natural to consider the corresponding uniqueness or rigidity issue. For Dirichlet boundary data
\gamma_{\Sigma}
with positive Gauss
curvature and with
N=1
on
\Sigma
(the Brown-York case as in (
5.2
)-(
5.3
)), this is proved to be true by a basic result
of Shi-Tam
[
35
]
. A generalization of this result to the setting with non-zero
K
(the space-time setting) has been proved in
basic work of Wang-Yau
[
40
]
.

The term
\inf_{\mathcal{I}}{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}
bears a formal similarity with the definition of the Bartnik quasi-local energy
[
7
]
, which is based on
minimizing the ADM Hamiltonian for an asymptotically flat space-time in a region exterior to the Cauchy surface
S
; thus the interior
minimization problem considered here is replaced in the Bartnik program by minimization over a complementary exterior region.
Bartnik conjectures that such infima are realized by stationary vacuum solutions, i.e. vacuum solutions for which the asymptotic
time-like Killing field extends to a time-like Killing field over the full exterior region. It is of course natural to make the analgous
conjecture in this (interior) setting. This will be discussed in more detail elsewhere.

In the case that the Killing field
\xi
on
{\mathcal{C}}
is hypersurface orthogonal, i.e. the shift

of
\xi

, it is proved in

[

that critical points of

on

\Sigma

[

that the bound (

this case.
