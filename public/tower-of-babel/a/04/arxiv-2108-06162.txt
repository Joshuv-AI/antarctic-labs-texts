Thermal Hall response: violation of gravitational analogues and Einstein relations
Jinhong Park, Omri Golan, Yuval Vinkler-Aviv, Achim Rosch

Abstract
The response of solids to temperature gradients is often described in terms of a gravitational analogue: the effect of a space-dependent temperature is modeled using a space dependent metric. We investigate the validity of this approach in describing the bulk response of quantum Hall states and other gapped chiral topological states. To this end, we consider the prototypical Haldane model in two different cases of (i) a space-dependent electrostatic potential and gravitational potential and (ii) a space-dependent temperature and chemical potential imprinted by a weak coupling to non-interacting electron baths or phonons. We find that the thermal analogue is invalid ; while a space dependent gravitational potential induces transverse energy currents proportional to the third derivative of the gravitational potential, the response to an analogous temperature profile vanishes in limit of weak coupling to the thermal bath. Similarly, the Einstein relation, the analogy between the electrostatic potential and the internal chemical potential, is not valid in such a setup.



Introduction.—

Thermal transport in topological matter has been of high interest as its quantization can reveal the topological nature of the underlying state of matter
Kane and Fisher 1997
;
Cappelli et al. 2002
. Recently, a half-integer quantized thermal conductance has been measured in
\nu=5/2
fractional quantum Hall state
Banerjee et al. 2018
and
\alpha
-
\rm{RuCl_{3}}

Kasahara et al. 2018
;
Bruin et al. 2021
;
Yokoi et al. 2021
; the latter is a candidate for realistic materials of chiral Kitaev spin liquid
Kitaev 2006
. Here a half-integer thermal conductance can be viewed as a smoking-gun signature of the existence of gapless chiral Majorana fermion on the edges. While such thermal edge transport in topological materials is fairly well understood (e.g., for a chiral spin liquid
Kitaev 2006
;
Vinkler-Aviv and Rosch 2018
;
Ye et al. 2018
), topological response in the bulk and the corresponding bulk-boundary correspondence is actively discussed
Bradlyn and Read 2015
;
Nakai et al. 2017
;
Kapustin and Spodyneiko 2020
;
Huang et al. 2021
.

In contrast with charge transport, where
the electrostatic potential is coupled to the electron density and thus the transport coefficients can be derived from the linear response theory, there is no apparent term added in the Hamiltonian for the thermal transport. Luttinger
Luttinger 1964
suggested that thermal transport can be investigated via the coupling to fictitious and spatially varying metric tensor (i.e., gravity). In this case the Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle H[\psi]=\int\!d^{d}{\boldsymbol{r}}\,\sqrt{g({\boldsymbol{r}})}\,h({\boldsymbol{r}})=\int\!d^{d}{\boldsymbol{r}}\,(1+\psi({\boldsymbol{r}}))\,h({\boldsymbol{r}})

(1)

where
h({\boldsymbol{r}})
is the (flat-space) energy density and the gravitational potential
\psi({\boldsymbol{r}})
describes how the metric varies spatially.

Roughly, the analogy of transport in curved space with the problem of a space-dependent temperature
T({\boldsymbol{r}})
is obtained when one considers
the density matrix

\displaystyle\rho\sim e^{-\int\!d^{d}{\boldsymbol{r}}\,\beta({\boldsymbol{r}})h({\boldsymbol{r}})}

(2)

with a space-dependent inverse temperature
\beta({\boldsymbol{r}})=1/T({\boldsymbol{r}})
. Comparing this to
e^{-\overline{\beta}H[\Psi]}
suggests to
identify

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T({\boldsymbol{r}})}=\beta({\boldsymbol{r}})=\overline{\beta}(1+\psi({\boldsymbol{r}})).

(3)

where
\overline{T}=1/\overline{\beta}
is a reference temperature. While this analogy is appealing, it is also rather obvious that it can only be of limited validity. Importantly, any problem with a space-dependent temperature is by definition a non-equilibrium problem. Thus the density matrix of Eq. (
2
) does
not
describe the steady state of the system.

The gravitational analogy is well established for the calculation of thermal transport in the thermodynamics limit and has, for example, been used to classify topological matter
Ryu et al. 2012
.
According to the so-called
Luttinger relation
,
the thermal conductivity tensor
\kappa
describes both the response to gradients of
\psi
and
T

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{J}}^{E}=-\kappa(T{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\psi+{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}T)

(4)

where
{\boldsymbol{J}}^{E}
is the energy current density. Within linear response theory,
\kappa
is therefore routinely calculated by considering the response to a space- and time-dependent
\psi\sim e^{i({\boldsymbol{q}}{\boldsymbol{r}}-\Omega t)}
. Importantly, one has to use the ‘transport limit’ for such a calculation by taking first the limit
{\boldsymbol{q}}\to 0
and only then
\Omega\to 0
. In case that both
\psi
and
T
are present, one has to identify
T({\boldsymbol{r}})
with the internal temperature calculated from the local energy density rather than the thermodynamic temperature
Cooper et al. 1997
.
The Luttinger relation is in close analogy to the Einstein relation for electric transport,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{J}}^{C}=\sigma{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}(\phi+\mu),

(5)

where
\phi
is an external potential and
\mu
the internal chemical potential calculated from the local density (and not the electrochemical potential).
In the case of broken time-reversal symmetry, one has to be careful when defining the correct ‘transport currents’
which have to be distinguished from equilibrium currents related to the magnetization of the sample, see Refs.
Cooper et al. 1997
;
Qin et al. 2011
;
Bradlyn and Read 2015
;
Gromov and Abanov 2015
;
Kapustin and Spodyneiko 2020
;
Huang et al. 2021
.

In a quantum Hall system both
\sigma_{xy}
and
\kappa_{xy}/T
are quantized while the longitudinal conductivites vanish,
\kappa_{xx}=\sigma_{xx}=0
.
There is, however, a remarkable difference in the response to a static, space-dependent
electrostatic potential
\phi({\boldsymbol{r}})
and a gravitational potential
\psi({\boldsymbol{r}})
. For example,
\phi({\boldsymbol{r}})
may arise from an electric charge close to the surface of a topological insulator
Qi et al. 2009
. In this case, the potential induces circulating currents perpendicular to the potential gradients which can be computed directly from Eq. (
5
). Remarkably, this is
not
the case when the response to
\psi({\boldsymbol{r}})
is calculated which may arise due to a bump in the 2d material, see Fig.
1
(b). In the context of relativistic field theories, the bulk response is given by
Stone 2012

\displaystyle J^{E}_{i}({\boldsymbol{r}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar c^{2}(c_{R}-c_{L})}{96\pi}\epsilon^{ij}\partial_{j}R

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{\hbar c^{2}(c_{R}-c_{L})}{48\pi}\epsilon^{ij}\partial_{j}\nabla^{2}\psi

(6)

where
c
is the speed of light,
R=-2\nabla^{2}\psi+O(\psi^{2})
the curvature, and
c_{R}-c_{L}
the difference of left-moving and right-moving central charge characterizing the edge modes of the system.
c_{R}-c_{L}
is directly related to the quantized thermal Hall conductivity

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa_{xy}}{T}=(c_{R}-c_{L})\frac{\pi^{2}k_{B}^{2}}{3h}.

(7)

Remarkably, Eq. (
Thermal Hall response: violation of gravitational analogues and Einstein relations
) predicts that the thermal topological response to a gravitational potential is proportional to
the third derivative of
\psi({\boldsymbol{r}})
while the Luttinger relation suggests a response Eq. (
4
) proportional to the first derivative.
This is not a direct contradiction because Eq. (
Thermal Hall response: violation of gravitational analogues and Einstein relations
) has been calculated for a smooth
static
potential, i.e., by taking first the limit
\Omega\to 0
, while Eq. (
4
) is valid in the opposite limit where one first considers the limit
{\boldsymbol{q}}\to 0
. The topological bulk response Eq. (
Thermal Hall response: violation of gravitational analogues and Einstein relations
) is directly linked to the gravitational anomaly
Alvarez-Gaumé and Witten 1984
of the edge
theory: an apparent violation of energy conservation at the edge in the presence of gravitational potentials can be explained by the inflow of energy from the bulk
Stone 2012
;
Golan and Stern 2018
.

An interesting observation from Eq. (
Thermal Hall response: violation of gravitational analogues and Einstein relations
) is that the topological bulk response of relativistic theories is proportional to
c^{2}
which immediately suggests that the effect cannot be fully universal in non-relativistic topological phases where it is unclear what should replace the speed of light.
In this context, it would be desirable to understand the bulk response for non-relativistic theories realized in all condensed matter settings.

In this paper, we investigate whether the gravitational analogy and Luttinger relations can be used to calculate the response to space-dependent temperature profiles which arise when
a system is heated locally (see Fig.
1
(a)). More specifically, we will show that the gravitational analogy does
not
hold for temperature profiles
T({\boldsymbol{r}})

even in a regime where
T({\boldsymbol{r}})
is much smaller than the gap of the system. Similar statements hold for the electric case. Furthermore, we argue that the bulk response to local gravitational potentials is not quantized in non-relativistic theories.

Gravitational response.—
As a concrete example, we consider the Haldane model
Haldane 1988
, which describes a Chern insulator, defined on a honeycomb lattice.

\displaystyle\hat{H}[\psi]=-\sum_{i,j}t_{ij}(1+\psi({\boldsymbol{r}}_{ij}))c^{\dagger}_{i}c_{j}+\sum_{i}v_{i}(1+\psi({\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}))c^{\dagger}_{i}c_{i}

(8)

where
t_{ij}
encodes a real-valued nearest-neighbor hopping
t_{1}
and a purely imaginary next-nearest neighbor hopping

\pm it_{2}
(see Fig.
2
).
v_{i}=\pm v_{s}
is a staggered potential.
To model the effect of a gravitational potential, all terms in the Hamiltonian depend on a smoothly varying gravitational potential

\psi({\boldsymbol{r}}_{ij})
with
{\boldsymbol{r}}_{ij}=({\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}+{\boldsymbol{r}}_{j})/2
.

At
v_{s}<3\sqrt{3}t_{2}
, the system is in a topological phase with Chern number
c_{R}-c_{L}=-1
. At the quantum phase transition to the trivial phase at
v_{s}=3\sqrt{3}t_{2}
, the gap closes at the
K
while it remains finite at the
K^{\prime}
point
Haldane 1988
.
Close to this transition, the system is accurately described by its continuum limit

\displaystyle\hat{H}[\psi]

\displaystyle\approx\hat{H}_{c}[\psi]=\int(1+\psi({\boldsymbol{r}}))\hat{h}_{c}({\boldsymbol{r}})d^{2}{\boldsymbol{r}}

(9)

\displaystyle\hat{h}_{c}({\boldsymbol{r}})

\displaystyle=\Psi^{\dagger}({\boldsymbol{r}})\left(-iv{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{r}}}+(M-\lambda^{2}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{r}}}^{2})\sigma_{z}\right)\Psi({\boldsymbol{r}}).

with
M=v_{s}-3\sqrt{3}t_{2}
,
v=-\sqrt{3}t_{1}a/2
,
\lambda^{2}=3\sqrt{3}t_{2}a^{2}/4
. Here
a
is the lattice constant. The two-component spinor
\Psi^{\dagger}=(\Psi^{\dagger}_{1},\Psi^{\dagger}_{2})
creates electrons close to the
K
point.

Employing this continuum model, we first consider the response to a gravitational potential
\psi({\boldsymbol{r}})
and an electrostatic potential
\phi({\boldsymbol{r}})
at zero temperature and for vanishing chemical potential.
For simplicity, it is assumed that
\psi({\boldsymbol{r}})
and
\phi({\boldsymbol{r}})
vary only in the
x
direction, but are constant in the
y
direction.

For the calculation of the gravitational response, we first define the energy current density operator
\hat{{\boldsymbol{J}}}{}^{E}_{\psi}({\boldsymbol{r}})
in the presence of
\psi({\boldsymbol{r}})
.
\hat{{\boldsymbol{J}}}{}^{E}_{\psi}({\boldsymbol{r}})
can be uniquely determined by (i) requiring the continuity equation
\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{r}}}\cdot\hat{{\boldsymbol{J}}}{}_{\psi}^{E}({\boldsymbol{r}})=i[(1+\psi({\boldsymbol{r}}))\hat{h}_{c}({\boldsymbol{r}}),\hat{H}_{c}[\psi]]/\hbar

and (ii) imposing that
\hat{{\boldsymbol{J}}}{}_{\psi}^{E}
is related to the zero-potential energy current operator
\hat{{\boldsymbol{J}}}{}^{E}
as
\hat{{\boldsymbol{J}}}{}_{\psi}^{E}=(1+\psi)^{2}\hat{{\boldsymbol{J}}}{}^{E}

Cooper et al. 1997
;
Qin et al. 2011
;
Vinkler-Aviv 2019
. The calculation is done most conveniently in momentum space, where
\hat{{\boldsymbol{J}}}{}^{E}({\boldsymbol{q}})
has a form
\hat{{\boldsymbol{J}}}{}^{E}({\boldsymbol{q}})=\int d{\boldsymbol{K}}/(2\pi)^{2}\Psi^{\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{K}}-{\boldsymbol{q}}/2}{\boldsymbol{J}}{}^{E}_{{\boldsymbol{K}}-{\boldsymbol{q}}/2;{\boldsymbol{K}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}/2}\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{K}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}/2}
.
Using standard linear-response theory, the expectation value of the energy current operator
{\boldsymbol{J}}_{\psi}^{E}({\boldsymbol{r}})\equiv\langle\hat{{\boldsymbol{J}}}{}_{\psi}^{E}({\boldsymbol{r}})\rangle
can be obtained in the linear order of the
static
gravitational potential
\psi
(see the supplemental material
Sup
for details).
Expanding in
{\boldsymbol{q}}
for smoothly varying
\psi(x)
we obtain

\displaystyle J_{y,\psi}^{E}({\boldsymbol{r}})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\partial_{x}^{3}\psi({\boldsymbol{r}})}{96\pi}\bigg[4\lambda^{2}M\theta(-M)

\displaystyle+v^{2}\left\{\textrm{sgn}(M)-3\log\left(\frac{4e^{-5/3}K_{\rm{cut}}^{2}\lambda^{4}}{v^{2}+4\lambda^{2}M\theta(M)}\right)\right\}\bigg].

(10)

where we used that
\psi({\boldsymbol{r}})=\psi(x)
depends only on the
x
-coordinate in our setup.
Here

is a ultra-violet momentum cutoff which is needed to obtain a finite result.

Two main conclusions can be drawn from Eq. (
Thermal Hall response: violation of gravitational analogues and Einstein relations
).
(i) We have confirmed that the response to a
static
gravitational potential is
not
proportional
\partial_{x}\psi
as suggested by the Luttinger relation, Eq. (
4
),
(which has been derived for a time-dependent potential in the ‘transport limit’).
It is instead proportional to
\partial_{x}^{3}\psi
as suggested by the relativistic anomaly formula, Eq. (

).
(ii) The prefactor of the anomaly response is, however, not simply given by

(cf. Eq. (
Thermal Hall response: violation of gravitational analogues and Einstein relations

Thermal Hall response: violation of gravitational analogues and Einstein relations

We have checked that the same calculation which results in Eq. (
Thermal Hall response: violation of gravitational analogues and Einstein relations
), leads to

In contrast, a calculation for the charge response to

Response to temperature bump.—

We next consider the temperature bump
T({\boldsymbol{r}})
and the chemical potential bump
\mu({\boldsymbol{r}})
in the absence of
\psi({\boldsymbol{r}})
and
\phi({\boldsymbol{r}})
.
To be able to change locally the temperature and the chemical potential, we couple weakly to each lattice site
i
of a Chern insulator
Sup
, both a bath of phonons with temperature
T_{i}
and a wire with a chemical potential
\mu_{i}
and the same temperature
T_{i}
, see Fig.

V

The coupling is described by
\hat{H}_{t}+\hat{H}_{\rm ph}
with

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,q}\epsilon_{q}d^{\dagger}_{i,q}d_{i,q}+Vd^{\dagger}_{i,q}c_{i}+h.c.

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\rm{ph}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,q}\omega_{q}a^{\dagger}_{i,q}a_{i,q}+g\,c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{i}\left(a^{\dagger}_{i,q}+a_{i,q}\right),

(12)

where we parametrize the (ohmic) phonon coupling by the parameter
\alpha
with
\pi g^{2}\sum_{q}\delta(\omega-\omega_{q})=\alpha\omega

and the tunnel coupling by

Conclusion.—

In this paper we have analyzed and clarified to what extent the Luttinger relation, Eq. (
4
), the Einstein relation, Eq. (
5
),
and the gravitational analogy, Eqs. (
3
) and (
Thermal Hall response: violation of gravitational analogues and Einstein relations
) can be used to describe the response to
local and static variations of
temperature and chemical potential in Chern insulators. It turns out that none of these relations apply. A local temperature profile,

T({\boldsymbol{r}})
, imprinted by heating a Chern insulator locally does, for example, not produce any intrinsic heat currents at least as long as
T({\boldsymbol{r}})

remains small compared to the gap. Thus the physics of a space-dependent temperature is completely different from the physics of a gravitational potential which produces heat currents. Similarly, local variations of the chemical potential,
\mu({\boldsymbol{r}})
, do not induce intrinsic transverse electric currents, while an external potential does. Ultimately, this difference can be traced back to the fact that space-dependent
T({\boldsymbol{r}})
and
\mu({\boldsymbol{r}})
simply do not show up as terms in the Hamiltonian but are effective quantities encoded in distribution functions which arise either from the coupling to a local bath or by local equilibration.
The Luttinger and Einstein relations remain fully valid only in the so-called transport limit (
\Omega\to 0
after
{\boldsymbol{q}}\to 0
).

We have also shown that the gravitational bulk response of Chern insulators is not
universal but depends on high-energy properties of the model. Transverse energy currents proportional to the third derivative of the gravitational potential may even be induced in topologically trivial phases. Unfortunately, such dissipationless energy currents are much more difficult to measure than the magnetic field created by their electric counterpart. It would be interesting to explore whether it is possible to induce gravitational potentials (e.g., by modulating the laser intensity) and measure such
currents in ultracold-atom experiments in optical lattices, using, e.g., time-of-flight measurements.

IV
Response to temperature or chemical potential bump

In this section, we analytically calculate the Hall response to a smoothly varying chemical potential,
\mu({\boldsymbol{r}})
, and temperature,
T({\boldsymbol{r}})
, in the absence of
\psi({\boldsymbol{r}})
and
\phi({\boldsymbol{r}})
employing the gradient expansion approach.
To be able to change locally the temperature and the chemical potential of the system, we couple weakly to each lattice site
i
of the honeycomb model, Eq. (8) in the main text, a wire with a chemical potential
\mu_{i}
and temperature
T_{i}
, see Fig. 2 in the main text, using a tunneling contact of strength
V
. For simplicity, the chemical potential and the temperature are assumed to vary in the
x
direction while being constant in the
y
direction. In this section, we neglect phonon baths attached to the system, but the effect of phonons will be discussed in Sec.
VI
.

In the continuum limit one can describe the coupling to these wires by the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{t}=\int d^{2}{\boldsymbol{r}}\,\sum_{q,\sigma}\left(\epsilon_{q}d^{\dagger}_{\sigma,{\boldsymbol{r}},q}d_{\sigma,{\boldsymbol{r}},q}+Vd^{\dagger}_{\sigma,{\boldsymbol{r}},q}\Psi_{\sigma}({\boldsymbol{r}})+h.c.\right),

(63)

where
d^{\dagger}_{\sigma,{\boldsymbol{r}},q}
creates an electron with energy
\epsilon_{q}
in a wire attached to the point
{\boldsymbol{r}}
and sublattice
\sigma
of the Haldane model.
Chemical potentials
\mu_{i}
and temperatures
T_{i}
of the wires vary in the real space:
\mu_{i}=\mu({\boldsymbol{r}}_{i})
and
T_{i}=T({\boldsymbol{r}}_{i})
. This information is, however, not encoded in the Hamiltonian but in the Fermi function
f(\omega,{\boldsymbol{r}})
describing incoming electrons of the wire attached at position
{\boldsymbol{r}}
. We use the continuum limit of the Haldane model, Eq. (9).

The process of tunneling back and forth between the system and the wires is encoded in the retarded and advanced part of the self energy

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\sigma{\boldsymbol{k}},\sigma^{\prime}{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}}^{R/A}

\displaystyle=\delta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\sum_{q}\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}}e^{-i{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{r}}}\frac{|V|^{2}}{\omega-\epsilon_{q}\pm i\eta}e^{i{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{r}}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\delta_{{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}}\sum_{q}\frac{|V|^{2}}{\omega-\epsilon_{q}\pm i\eta}

\displaystyle\approx\mp i\delta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\delta_{{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}}\Gamma

(64)

where we assumed a constant density of states,
N_{F}
, of the wires with a large bandwidth.
The tunneling rate
\Gamma\equiv\pi N_{F}|V|^{2}
is thus frequency independent.
Note that the retarded and advanced part of the self energy are translationally invariant and momentum independent. The lesser part of the self energy, on the other hand, does depend on position and therefore also on momentum

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\sigma{\boldsymbol{k}},\sigma{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}}^{<}

\displaystyle=\sum_{q}\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}}e^{-i{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{r}}}|V|^{2}\left(2\pi i\delta(\omega-\epsilon_{q})\right)f(\epsilon_{q},{\boldsymbol{r}})e^{i{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{r}}}

\displaystyle=2i\Gamma\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}}f(\omega,{\boldsymbol{r}})e^{-i({\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{r}}},

(65)

reflecting the momentum transfer to the wires. Employing the Keldysh equations,

\displaystyle G^{R/A}(\omega)

\displaystyle=g^{R/A}(\omega)+g^{R/A}(\omega)\Sigma^{R/A}(\omega)G^{R/A}(\omega),

(66)

\displaystyle G^{<}(\omega)

\displaystyle=G^{R}(\omega)\Sigma^{<}(\omega)G^{A}(\omega),

(67)

the full dressed retarded and advanced Green’s function, and the lesser Green’s function are given by

\displaystyle G^{R/A}_{\sigma^{\prime}{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}},\sigma{\boldsymbol{k}}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{\omega-h\left[{\boldsymbol{k}}\right]\pm i\Gamma}\right)_{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}\delta_{{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}},

(68)

\displaystyle G^{<}_{\sigma^{\prime}{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}},\sigma{\boldsymbol{k}}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=2i\Gamma\sum_{{\boldsymbol{r}}}f(\omega,{\boldsymbol{r}})e^{i({\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{r}}}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\omega-h\left[{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\right]+i\Gamma}\frac{1}{\omega-h\left[{\boldsymbol{k}}\right]-i\Gamma}\right)_{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}.

(69)

IV.1
Response to chemical potential bump

We next consider the charge Hall current in response to a chemical potential bump with

. The information of
\mu(x)
is encoded in the Fermi function

Plugging Eqs. (

\displaystyle-2\Gamma e\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\int\frac{d{\boldsymbol{K}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{d{\boldsymbol{q}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\int d{\boldsymbol{r}_{1}}f(\omega,{\boldsymbol{r}}_{1})e^{i{\boldsymbol{q}}\cdot({\boldsymbol{r}}-{\boldsymbol{r}}_{1})}\textrm{Tr}\Big[\left(v\sigma_{y}+2\lambda^{2}\sigma_{z}K_{y}\right)

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{\omega-h({\boldsymbol{K}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}/2)+i\Gamma}\frac{1}{\omega-h({\boldsymbol{K}}-{\boldsymbol{q}}/2)-i\Gamma}\Big].

Being interested in smooth potentials, we expand the term inside the trace in

.
The leading contribution comes from the linear order in

\displaystyle J_{y,\mu}^{C}({\boldsymbol{r}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar\Gamma ev^{2}\partial_{x}\mu}{\pi^{2}}\int\frac{dKK(M-\lambda^{2}K^{2})}{\left(\left(\lambda^{2}K^{2}+M\right)^{2}+v^{2}K^{2}+\Gamma^{2}\right)^{2}}.

We have also expanded

As

in Eq. (

and

and thus

for
\Gamma\to 0

The physical interpretation of this result is that some charge tunnels between the attached wires through the gapped topological insulator. This type of transport does, however, vanish in the limit
\Gamma\to 0
. We conclude that spatially varying chemical potentials do not induce any topological currents in Chern insulators, implying that the Einstein relation is not valid.

\displaystyle=2\Gamma\int\frac{dK}{2\pi}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\int\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{q}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int d{\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}\frac{iK(\lambda^{2}K^{2}-M)v^{2}q_{x}e^{i{\boldsymbol{q}}\cdot\left({\boldsymbol{r}}-{\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}\right)}f(\omega,{\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}})\omega}{\left[\left(\lambda^{2}K^{2}+M\right)^{2}+K^{2}v^{2}+\Gamma^{2}\right]^{2}-2\omega^{2}\left[\left(\lambda^{2}K^{2}+M\right)^{2}+K^{2}v^{2}-\Gamma^{2}\right]+\omega^{4}}

\displaystyle=2\Gamma\int\frac{dK}{2\pi}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\partial_{x}f(\omega,x)\frac{K(\lambda^{2}K^{2}-M)v^{2}\omega}{\left[\left(\lambda^{2}K^{2}+M\right)^{2}+K^{2}v^{2}+\Gamma^{2}\right]^{2}-2\omega^{2}\left[\left(\lambda^{2}K^{2}+M\right)^{2}+K^{2}v^{2}-\Gamma^{2}\right]+\omega^{4}}.

(73)

The chain rule
\partial_{x}f(\omega,x)=\partial_{x}T\partial_{T}f(\omega)
and the Sommerfeld expansion yield

\displaystyle J_{y,T}^{E}({\boldsymbol{r}})

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}T(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial_{T}}\left(\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\int\frac{dK}{2\pi}\frac{2\Gamma K(\lambda^{2}K^{2}-M)v^{2}\omega f(\omega)}{\left[\left(\lambda^{2}K^{2}+M\right)^{2}+K^{2}v^{2}+\Gamma^{2}\right]^{2}-2\omega^{2}\left[\left(\lambda^{2}K^{2}+M\right)^{2}+K^{2}v^{2}-\Gamma^{2}\right]+\omega^{4}}\right)

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}T(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial_{T}}\left(\frac{\pi^{2}(k_{B}T)^{2}}{6h}\left(\int\frac{dK}{2\pi}\frac{2\Gamma K(\lambda^{2}K^{2}-M)v^{2}}{\left[\left(\lambda^{2}K^{2}+M\right)^{2}+K^{2}v^{2}+\Gamma^{2}\right]^{2}}+O\left(\frac{(k_{B}T)^{2}}{|M|^{2}}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\pi^{2}k_{B}^{2}T}{3h}\partial_{x}T\left(\int\frac{dK}{\pi}\frac{\Gamma K(\lambda^{2}K^{2}-M)v^{2}}{\left[\left(\lambda^{2}K^{2}+M\right)^{2}+K^{2}v^{2}+\Gamma^{2}\right]^{2}}\right).

(74)

In the last expression, we neglect the higher order terms
O((k_{B}T/|M|)^{2})
, which in turn yields the exponential suppression by the energy gap.
The direct integration over momentum
K
in the limit
\Gamma\to 0
, and
|M|\ll v^{2}/\lambda^{2}
leads to

\displaystyle J_{y,T}^{E}({\boldsymbol{r}})\approx-\frac{\pi^{2}k_{B}^{2}T}{6h}\frac{\Gamma}{\pi M}\left(\partial_{x}T+\frac{v^{2}}{6M^{2}}\partial_{x}^{3}T+O(\partial^{5}_{x}T)\right).

(75)

Here, the gradient term
\propto\partial_{x}T
is directly from Eq. (
IV.2
) while the third derivative term
\propto\partial^{3}_{x}T
is the next leading contribution obtained from the cubic term
q_{x}^{3}
in the gradient expansion using the similar procedure as in Eqs. (
IV.2
) and (
IV.2
). Our calculation confirms the absence of a thermal Hall response in the weak coupling limit,
\Gamma\to 0
. Note that also all contributions proportional to higher derivatives of

vanish in this limit.

In this section we show numerically that the local temperature of the Chern insulator is determined
with high precision by the temperature of the locally attached wires under the conditions specified below.
To show this, we consider the local distribution function of electrons in the Chern insulator defined by

\displaystyle f_{{\boldsymbol{r}}}^{\text{eff}}(\omega)=-\frac{G^{<}_{{\boldsymbol{r}},{\boldsymbol{r}}}(\omega)}{(G^{R}-G^{A})(\omega)}.

(76)

The retarded and advanced Green function of the system are simply given by

G^{R/A}_{\sigma^{\prime}{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}},\sigma{\boldsymbol{k}}}(\omega)=\left(\frac{1}{\omega-h\left[{\boldsymbol{k}}\right]\pm i\Gamma}\right)_{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}\delta_{{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}}

i\Gamma

G^{<}
, in contrast, is not translationally invariant but can be computed exactly from
f(\omega,{\boldsymbol{r}})
and
G^{R,A}
in position space

\displaystyle G^{<}_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{j},{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}}(\omega)=

\displaystyle 2i\Gamma\sum_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{n}}G^{R}_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{j},{\boldsymbol{r}}_{n}}(\omega)f(\omega,{\boldsymbol{r}}_{n})G^{A}_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{n},{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}}(\omega).

(77)

Furthermore, we study the value of the local temperature defined by the following procedure:
we attach to position
{\boldsymbol{r}}
of the system a quantum wire (the ‘thermometer’) by a tunneling contact with temperature
T_{\rm{th}}
. The thermometer is in local thermal equilibrium with the system if the energy current into or from the thermometer vanishes,
J^{E}_{\rm{th}}({\boldsymbol{r}})
. Here the energy current is obtained from
the Meir-Wingreen formula
Meir and Wingreen 1992
as

\displaystyle J^{E}_{\rm{th}}({\boldsymbol{r}})

\displaystyle=\frac{ie}{h}\int d\omega\,\omega\,\rho_{\rm{th}}|t_{\rm{th}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\times\textrm{Tr}\left[f_{\rm{th}}(\omega)(G^{R}-G^{A})(\omega)+G^{<}_{{\boldsymbol{r}},{\boldsymbol{r}}}(\omega)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{ie}{h}\int d\omega\omega\rho_{\rm{th}}|t_{\rm{th}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\times\textrm{Tr}\left[(f_{\rm{th}}(\omega)-f_{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\text{eff}}(\omega))(G^{R}-G^{A})(\omega)\right],

(78)

where
f_{\rm{th}}(\omega)=1/[\exp\left(\omega/T_{\rm{th}}\right)+1]
is the distribution function of the thermometer with constant density of states
\rho_{\rm{th}}
and tunneling rate
t_{\rm{th}}
.
Therefore, the effective local temperature
T^{\rm eff}({\boldsymbol{r}})=T_{\rm th}
is determined from the condition

J^{E}_{\rm{th}}({\boldsymbol{r}})=0
.
This thermometer should not be confused with the attached wires considered in the paper so far.

G^{<}_{{\boldsymbol{r}},{\boldsymbol{r}}}
,
G^{R}
, and
G^{A}

{\boldsymbol{r}}

f(\omega,{\boldsymbol{r}})=1/(\exp(\omega/T({\boldsymbol{r}}))+1)
. The perfect agreement shows that the temperature of the Chern insulator matches indeed the local temperature of the wires in this example for low
T
and
\sigma
much larger than the correlation length
\xi=v/|M|
of the Chern insulator. Fig.

(b) shows the comparison of the temperature profile
T({\boldsymbol{r}})
of the attached wires (solid line) to the effective temperature

obtained from the attached thermometer described above.

The fact that the temperature of the wire is directly imprinted onto our Chern insulator can be understood by inspecting the formula for
G^{<}
, Eq. (
77
).
Provided that (i) all temperatures are much smaller than the gap
T({\boldsymbol{r}})\ll|M|
, the main contribution of the integral in Eq. (
V
) arises within the frequency range of
\omega\in(-T_{0},T_{0})
inside the gap in which the spectral function
A(\omega)=-\textrm{Im}\left[(G^{R}-G^{A})(\omega)\right]/\pi
is proportional to
\Gamma
.
In this frequency range the retarded and advanced Green functions decay on the length scale
\xi=v/|M|
. Thus
G^{<}_{{\boldsymbol{r}},{\boldsymbol{r}}}
is only sensitive to distribution functions in the proximity of
{\boldsymbol{r}}
. As we considered a case where

\xi
is much smaller than a length scale
\sigma
over which the temperature profile varies, the local
G^{<}
is
thus only affected by temperatures very close to
T({\boldsymbol{r}})
. This explains the perfect agreement of the measured temperature to the
temperature of the attached wires.

These arguments remains valid as long as thermal excitations across the gap – which are not localized on the length scale
\xi
– can be neglected. They are exponentially suppressed with
e^{-|M|/T_{0}}
.
But, when
\Gamma
is extremely small such that

\Gamma/|M|\lesssim e^{-|M|/T_{0}}

V
)
does not arise from inside the gap, but rather from thermal excitations above the gap. These thermal excitations in turn renders the local temperatures of the Chern insulators to deviate from the temperature profiles of the wires.

In conclusion, we have shown that under the conditions specified above, the temperature profile of the wires is successfully imprinted onto the Chern insulator.
Therefore, a genuine response to a temperature or chemical potential profile can be obtained from the model with attached wires.

VI
Violation of the Luttinger relation in a phonon-coupled Chern insulator

In this section we consider a model where additionally we attach phonon baths to a Chern insulator
and demonstrate that the Luttinger relation is invalid.
Attaching the phonon baths has two advantages. First, the model leads to equilibration of the system even in the absence of any attached wires. Second, the phonon bath allows to imprint a temperature profile onto the system in a way which is much closer to an experimental setting, where one would use, e.g., a laser to heat the system locally. If such an experiment is done on an insulator, phonons provide the dominant equilibration channel.

To describe a Chern insulator with a simpler model, we consider a discretized version of the continuum model
defined in Eq. (
I.1
) realized on the square lattice (avoiding the longer-ranged hoppings of the Haldane model).
The model contains two orbitals
\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow
per lattice site
{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}
. It includes two nearest hopping terms and an on-site term

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=H_{1}+H_{2}+H_{3},

\displaystyle H_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{i\hbar v}{2a}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}}\left(\Psi^{\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}+a\hat{x}}\sigma_{x}\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}}+\Psi^{\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}+a\hat{y}}\sigma_{y}\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}}-\textrm{H.c.}\right),

\displaystyle H_{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{a^{2}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}}\left(\Psi^{\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}+a\hat{x}}\sigma_{z}\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}}+\Psi^{\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}+a\hat{y}}\sigma_{z}\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}}+\textrm{H.c.}\right),

\displaystyle H_{3}

\displaystyle=\left(M+4\frac{\lambda^{2}}{a^{2}}\right)\sum_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}}\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}}^{\dagger}\sigma_{z}\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}},

(79)

Here
a
is the lattice constant.
The two-component spinor
\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}}^{\dagger}=(\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}\uparrow}^{\dagger},\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}\downarrow}^{\dagger})
creates electrons in the two orbitals,
\uparrow
and
\downarrow
, at site
{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}
.
Note that the Fourier transform of
Eq. (
VI
) is identical with Eq. (
I.1
)
in the continuum limit,
{\boldsymbol{k}}\rightarrow 0
.
At
-4\lambda^{2}/a^{2}<M<0
(
-8\lambda^{2}/a^{2}<M<-4\lambda^{2}/a^{2}
), the system is in a topological phase with Chern number
c_{R}-c_{L}=-1
(
+1
) while at
M>0
or
M<-8\lambda^{2}/a^{2}
, the system is in a trivial phase. At the quantum phase transition to the trivial phase at
-4\lambda^{2}/a^{2}=M
, the gap closes at
{\boldsymbol{k}}=(\pm\pi/a,0)
and
{\boldsymbol{k}}=(0,\pm\pi/a)
while it remains finite at
{\boldsymbol{k}}=(0,0)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\rm{ph}}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}\sigma q}\left(g\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}\sigma}^{\dagger}\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}\sigma}\left(a^{\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}\sigma q}+a_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}\sigma q}\right)+\omega_{q}a^{\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}\sigma q}a_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}\sigma q}\right),

(80)

g

\omega_{q}
and momentum
q
attached to the point
{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}

The effect of the electron-phonon coupling is captured within Keldysh theory using a self-consistent one-loop approximation (i.e., the second order perturbation in
g

\hat{y}
direction, we
introduce momenta
k_{y}

x

\Sigma(x)

The local lesser self-energy
\Sigma^{<}_{\rm{ph}}(x)
and the imaginary part of the local retarded self-energy
\textrm{Im}\Sigma^{R}_{\rm{ph}}(x)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{<}_{\textrm{ph}}(x,\omega)

\displaystyle=-2g^{2}\int\frac{d\omega_{1}}{(2\pi)}b(\omega-\omega_{1},x)\sum_{q}\textrm{Im}D^{R}(x,q,\omega-\omega_{1})\sum_{k_{y}}G^{<}(x,k_{y},\omega_{1}),

\displaystyle\textrm{Im}\Sigma^{R}_{\textrm{ph}}(x,\omega)

\displaystyle=-g^{2}\int\frac{d\omega_{1}}{(2\pi)}\sum_{q}\textrm{Im}D^{R}(x,q,\omega-\omega_{1})\sum_{k_{y}}\left(\textrm{Im}G^{<}(x,k_{y},\omega_{1})-2b(\omega_{1}-\omega,x)\textrm{Im}G^{R}(x,k_{y},\omega_{1})\right).

(81)

Here
b(\omega,x)
is the Bose function at position
x

b(\omega,x)=1/(\exp(\omega/(k_{B}T(x)))-1)
, and

is the phonon retarded Green function.
The real part of the retarded self energy is numerically obtained from the Kramers-Kronig relation, i.e.,
\textrm{Re}\Sigma^{R}_{\textrm{ph}}(x,\omega)=P\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega^{\prime}\textrm{Im}\Sigma^{R}_{\textrm{ph}}(x,\omega^{\prime})/(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)/\pi

g^{2}\sum_{q}\textrm{Im}D^{R}(x,q,\omega)\equiv\alpha\omega
with dimensionless and spatially independent coupling constant
\alpha
. The Green functions can be written in terms of the self energies

\displaystyle G^{R}(k_{y},\omega)

\displaystyle=\left(\left(g^{R}(k_{y},\omega)\right)^{-1}-\Sigma^{R}(\omega)\right)^{-1},

\displaystyle G^{<}(k_{y},\omega)

\displaystyle=G^{R}(k_{y},\omega)\Sigma^{<}(\omega)G^{A}(k_{y},\omega),

(82)

with
\Sigma^{R}=\Sigma^{R}_{\textrm{ph}}+\Sigma^{R}_{\textrm{wire}}
,

\Sigma^{<}=\Sigma^{<}_{\textrm{ph}}+\Sigma^{<}_{\textrm{wire}}

g^{R}(k_{y},\omega)
.
The
\Sigma_{\textrm{wire}}
’s are the self-energies from the attached wires and can be written as
\Sigma_{\textrm{wire}}^{<}(\omega,x)=2i\Gamma f(\omega,x)

VI
) and (
VI

Our iteration scheme is as follows: For the initialization, the Green functions are obtained in the absence of the phonon baths. The Green functions are plugged into Eq. (
VI
) to obtain the self-energies, which are used to calculate the Green functions by matrix inversion employing Eq. (
VI
). This procedure is repeated until the Green functions converge.
Convergence is typically reached by less than 10 iterations.

As specified in Sec.
V
, provided that (i) all temperatures are much smaller than the gap
T({\boldsymbol{r}})\ll|M|
, the main contribution of the integral in Eq. (
V
) arises within the frequency range of
\omega\in(-T_{0},T_{0})
inside the gap. In this frequency range, the retarded Green function decays on the length scale
\xi=v/|M|
and hence the temperature at
{\boldsymbol{r}}
is only sensitive to the temperatures in the vicinity of
{\boldsymbol{r}}
. Therefore, if (ii)
\xi\gg\sigma
, the temperature of the system is imprinted by the temperature profile of the phonons. At higher temperatures, when one has to consider the presence of thermally excited quasiparticles, one has also to make sure that
\sigma
is large compared to the scattering length
\xi_{\rm ph}
due to phonon scattering. For the plots shown in the paper, we focus, however, on the low-
T

Those arguments above can be checked from the effective distribution function
f_{{\boldsymbol{r}}}^{\text{eff}}(\omega)
of the Chern insulator, Eq. (
76

G^{<}(x,x)
,
G^{R}(x,x)
, and
G^{A}(x,x)
, explicitly.
N_{x}=32
and
\sigma=8

\omega

T({\boldsymbol{r}})
of the phonons (solid line) to the effective temperature

obtained from the attached thermometer.

J^{C}_{y}(x)

We have thus obtained very similar results for a model where a temperature profile is imprinted by attached wire and a model where
it arises from the coupling to a phonon bath.
This shows that the violation of the Luttinger relation is a generic feature of systems with spatially varying temperature profiles.
It is independent on how the temperature is induced and independent of whether the system is interacting or not.
