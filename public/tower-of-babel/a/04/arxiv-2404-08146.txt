Mean dimension explosion of induced homeomorphisms
Gabriel Lacerda, Sergio Romaña

Abstract
Let X X be a compact metric space and T : X → X T:X\to X a continuous function. The induced hyperspace map T 𝒦 T_{\mathcal{K}} acts on the hyperspace 𝒦 ⁡ ( X ) \mathcal{K}(X) of closed and nonempty subsets of X X , and on the continuum hyperspace 𝒞 ⁡ ( X ) ⊂ 𝒦 ⁡ ( X ) \mathcal{C}(X)\subset\mathcal{K}(X) of connected sets. This work studies the mean dimension explosion phenomenon: when the base system T T has zero topological entropy, but the mean dimension of the induced map T 𝒦 T_{\mathcal{K}} is infinite. In particular, this phenomenon occurs for Morse-Smale diffeomorphisms. Furthermore, for a circle homeomorphism H H , the mean dimension explosion does not occur if and only if H H is conjugate to a rotation. For the metric mean dimension, a different result is obtained: we establish sufficient conditions for the induced hyperspace map to have zero or infinite metric mean dimension.

Introduction

The study of dynamical systems is primarily concerned with the complexity of the orbits of a map
T:X\to X
, where
X
is a compact metric space and
T
is a continuous function. There exist numerous tools to evaluate this complexity, and the mean dimension is one of them. Proposed by M. Gromov in
Gromov (1999)
, the
mean dimension
, denoted as
\text{mdim}(X,T)
, is a topological invariant of dynamical systems, and it is useful to classify maps acting on infinite-dimensional topological spaces, as the mean dimension of
(X,T)
is zero when the dimension of the phase space
X
is finite.

E. Lindenstrauss and B. Weiss later developed the mean dimension in
Lindenstrauss and Weiss (2000)
, where they defined the metric mean dimension. It is a simpler tool to use compared to the original mean dimension, as evidenced by several recent results in the literature:
Hayes (2017)
;
Lindenstrauss and Tsukamoto (2018)
;
Carvalho et al. (2020)
;
Acevedo et al. (2024)
.

This work applies the theory of mean dimension and metric mean dimension to the induced hyperspace map
T_{\mathcal{K}}
acting on the hyperspace of compact sets,
\mathcal{K}(X)
, and on the hyperspace of subcontinua,
\mathcal{C}(X)
. Since these hyperspaces are often infinite-dimensional, studying the mean dimension of the induced hyperspace map offers an alternative to classifying the complexity of well-known finite-dimensional dynamical systems. Moreover, the mean dimension appears as an alternative for evaluating the complexity of these toy models of infinite-dimensional dynamical systems.

In particular, we prove that a broad class of zero topological entropy dynamical systems acting on a finite-dimensional phase space have infinite mean dimension in the hyperspace, a phenomenon we term
explosion
.

The investigation of the relationship between the base system
T
and the induced map
T_{\mathcal{K}}
began with W. Bauer and K. Sigmund in
Bauer and Sigmund (1975)
, where, in particular, they proved that
if
T_{\mathcal{K}}:\mathcal{K}(X)\to\mathcal{K}(X)
is topologically transitive, then so is
T:X\to X
. Recently, M. Lampart and P. Raith established in
Lampart and Raith (2010)
a sufficient condition on
(X,T)
for which the topological entropy
h(T_{\mathcal{K}})
is infinite. Therefore, it is a valid question whether the metric mean dimension of an induced map is finite or infinite. Surprisingly, we present a sufficient condition on
T
ensuring that the mean dimension of the induced map,
\text{mdim}(\mathcal{K}(X),T_{\mathcal{K}})
, is infinite. For the following, a
continuum
is a compact and connected metric space.

Theorem A
.

Let
X
be a locally connected continuum and
T:X\to X
a homeomorphism. If the nonwandering set
\Omega(T)
is a strict subset of
X
, then
{\text{mdim}}(\mathcal{K}(X),T_{\mathcal{K}})=\infty
.

The identity map Id on
X
satisfies
\Omega(\text{Id})=X
and
\text{mdim}(\mathcal{K}(X),\text{Id}_{\mathcal{K}})=0
. Furthermore, in the special case of
X=S^{1}
, we exhibit a family of dynamics
H
such that
\Omega(H)=S^{1}
and
\text{mdim}(\mathcal{K}(S^{1}),H_{\mathcal{K}})=0
, demonstrating that the condition in Theorem
A
is sharp (see Corollary
2
).

Theorem
A
applies to a certain class of dynamical systems with zero topological entropy, which includes the Morse-Smale diffeomorphisms. In essence, the phenomenon of mean dimension explosion arises for the induced hyperspace map of these systems.

Another application of the above theorem is that every minimal dynamical system with finite mean dimension can be embedded in a subsystem of
T_{\mathcal{K}}
, when the nonwandering set of
T
is strictly contained in the phase space. For context, Y. Gutman and M. Tsukamoto showed in
Gutman and Tsukamoto (2020)
that any minimal map with
\text{mdim}(X,T)<\frac{N}{2}
can be embedded into the shift on
([0,1]^{N})^{\mathbb{Z}}
. Moreover, Theorem
A
provides examples of maps that cannot be embedded into the shift on the Hilbert cube.

One could ask whether, for induced dynamics that attain infinite mean dimension, there exist subdynamics that achieve a mean dimension equal to any prescribed non-negative real number. The answer is positive, as indicated by the following result.

Corollary 1
.

Let
X
be a locally connected continuum and
T:X\to X
a homeomorphism such that
\Omega(T)\subsetneq X
. Then, for every non-negative real number
\alpha
, there exists a
T_{\mathcal{K}}
-invariant set
Y_{\alpha}\subset\mathcal{K}(X)
such that
\emph{mdim}(Y_{\alpha},T_{\mathcal{K}}|_{Y_{\alpha}})=\alpha
.

In other words, Corollary
1
states that
(X,T)
has quasi-factors of any prescribed mean dimension. This interpretation uses the concept of quasi-factors, which was introduced by E. Glasner and B. Weiss in
Glasner and Weiss (1995)
.

In
(
Bauer and Sigmund, 1975
, Proposition 6)
, the authors proved that
if the topological entropy
h(T)
is positive, then
h(T_{\mathcal{K}})
is infinite
. This fact leads to the following question:

Question
.

Let
X
be a locally connected continuum and
T:X\to X
a homeomorphism. If
h(T)>0
, then we also have
{\text{mdim}}(\mathcal{K}(X),T_{\mathcal{K}})=\infty
?

The condition of being a locally connected continuum is necessary because D. Burguet and R. Shi gave in
Burguet and Shi (2022)
an example of a dynamical system
(X,T)
, where
X
is zero-dimensional, such that the topological entropy
h(T)
is positive, but
\text{mdim}(\mathcal{K}(X),T_{\mathcal{K}})
is zero. Furthermore, we show in Theorem
E
the existence of a dynamical system such that the base map has positive topological entropy and the induced hyperspace map has zero metric mean dimension.

The continuum hyperspace
\mathcal{C}(X)
is an infinite-dimensional topological space when
X
is a continuum with no free arcs. Given this, it is valid to ask if there is an analog of Theorem
A
for such topological spaces
X
. In particular, A. Arbieto and J. Bohorquez proved in
(
Arbieto and Bohorquez, 2023
, Theorem B)
that
the topological entropy of the induced continuum map
F_{\mathcal{K}}:\mathcal{C}(N^{m})\to\mathcal{C}(N^{m})
is either zero or infinite
where
F:N^{m}\to N^{m}
is a Morse-Smale diffeomorphism acting on a connected and compact boundaryless
m
-dimensional manifold
N^{m}
. Our second result deals with the mean dimension of the induced continuum map of Morse-Smale dynamics. In fact, we establish the following result:

Theorem B
.

Let
F:N^{m}\to N^{m}
be a Morse-Smale diffeomorphism. Hence, the following dichotomy holds:

•

if
m=1
, then
{\text{mdim}}(\mathcal{C}(N^{m}),F_{\mathcal{K}})=0
;

•

if
m>1
, then
{\text{mdim}}(\mathcal{C}(N^{m}),F_{\mathcal{K}})=\infty
.

The hypothesis of Theorem
B
is weaker than the hypothesis of Theorem
A
because the nonwandering set of a Morse-Smale diffeomorphism is finite. As a consequence, Theorem
B
states that the mean dimension explosion also occurs for the induced continuum map, when the dimension of the phase space is greater than one.

Another kind of dichotomy holds for the mean dimension as well. It is a stronger version of the following fact:
if
H
is a homeomorphism on
S^{1}
, then the topological entropy of
H_{\mathcal{K}}:\mathcal{K}(S^{1})\to\mathcal{K}(S^{1})
is either zero or infinite
. This fact is proved in
(
Lampart and Raith, 2010
, Theorem 5)
. Based on this result, we have the following statement:

Theorem C
.

Given
H:S^{1}\to S^{1}
a homeomorphism, then
{\text{mdim}}(\mathcal{K}(S^{1}),H_{\mathcal{K}})
is either
0
or
\infty
.

The ideas used in the proof of Theorem
C
are similar to those presented in
Lampart and Raith (2010)
, where the presence of periodic orbits plays a crucial role. Furthermore, since orientation-preserving homeomorphisms on the circle are well understood, we can utilize Theorem
C
to classify them based on the mean dimension of their induced hyperspace map. In other words, the phenomenon of mean dimension explosion is fully understood for circle homeomorphisms, since all circle homeomorphisms have zero topological entropy.

Corollary 2
.

Let
H:S^{1}\to S^{1}
be an orientation-preserving homeomorphism, then
{\text{mdim}}(\mathcal{K}(S^{1}),H_{\mathcal{K}})=0
if, and only if,
H
is conjugated to a rotation.

Recently, B. Zhu, X. Huang, and X. Wang proved in
Zhu et al. (2025)
that if the mean dimension of the base system
T
is positive, then the mean dimension of the induced hyperspace system
T_{\mathcal{K}}
is infinite. We would like to point out that this does not explain the phenomenon of mean dimension explosion, as this phenomenon specifically occurs when the base system is defined on a finite-dimensional phase space and has zero topological entropy.

It is important to state that the mean dimension explosion phenomenon does not occur for the induced system of Borel probability measures
\mathcal{M}(X)
equipped with the push-forward map
T_{*}:\mu\mapsto\mu\circ T^{-1}
, where
T:X\to X
is a continuous map, because D. Burguet and R. Shi proved in
Burguet and Shi (2022)
that
h(T)>0
if, and only if,
\text{mdim}(\mathcal{M}(X),T_{*})=\infty
.

In the latter part of this work, we study the
metric mean dimension
of induced maps. If
d
is a metric on
X
, the metric mean dimension is denoted by
\text{mdim}_{M}(X,d,T)
. The standard metric for both hyperspaces, which coincides with the natural topology on
\mathcal{K}(X)
, is the Hausdorff metric
d_{H}
. In our first result of this part, we investigate under which conditions on
T
the upper metric mean dimension of the induced hyperspace map
T_{\mathcal{K}}
is zero. This condition is given in terms of the
polynomial entropy
of
T
,
h_{\text{pol}}(T)
, a refinement of the topological entropy for maps with low complexity. It was defined by C. Labrousse and J.P. Marco in
Labrousse and Marco (2014)
. Our result relies on the fact that any locally connected continuum admits a convex metric, which is a type of geodesic metric.

Theorem D
.

Consider
X
a locally connected continuum endowed with a convex metric
d
and
T:X\to X
a map. If
h_{\emph{pol}}(T)<1
, then
\emph{mdim}_{M}(\mathcal{K}(X),d_{H},T_{\mathcal{K}})=0
.

In fact, the above result follows from a stronger statement: if the polynomial entropy of
T
is strictly less than
1
, then the topological entropy of the induced hyperspace map
T_{\mathcal{K}}
is zero. This statement is proved in a lemma in this work.

A simpler condition for obtaining zero metric mean dimension for the induced hyperspace map
T_{\mathcal{K}}
is that the base map
T
is an isometry. This implies that
T_{\mathcal{K}}
is an isometry too, and hence the complexity of its orbits is low; therefore, it does not have a positive upper metric mean dimension.

A valid question is whether the converse of the above theorem holds. The converse does not hold for a general compact metric space, as the following result shows.

Theorem E
.

There exist a compact metric space
(\Lambda,\rho)
and a map
T:\Lambda\to\Lambda
such that
h(T)>0
and
\emph{mdim}_{M}(\mathcal{K}(\Lambda),\rho_{H},T_{\mathcal{K}})=0
.

Since the polynomial entropy is a finite number only if the topological entropy is zero, the above result shows that the converse of Theorem
D
does not hold for a compact metric space. In fact, the behavior of the metric mean dimension for induced hyperspace maps is more complex when the base space is a general compact metric space. Indeed, X. Huang and X. Wang proved in
Huang and Wang (2022)
that for the shift map
\sigma
on the symbolic space of
N
symbols,
\Sigma_{N}
, endowed with the usual metric
d(\xi,\eta)=2^{-\min\{n\geq 1:\,\xi_{n}\neq\eta_{n}\}}
, one has
\text{mdim}(\mathcal{K}(\Sigma_{N}),d_{H},\sigma_{\mathcal{K}})=\infty
.

Question
.

Let
X
be a locally connected continuum endowed with a convex metric
d
, and let
T:X\to X
be a map. If
h(T)>0
, does it follow that
\emph{mdim}_{M}(\mathcal{K}(X),d_{H},T_{\mathcal{K}})=\infty
?

Alternatively, if
X
is a compact metrizable space, is there always some metric
\rho
on
X
such that
h(T)>0
implies
\emph{mdim}_{M}(\mathcal{K}(X),\rho_{H},T_{\mathcal{K}})=\infty
?

The hypothesis on
X
being a locally connected continuum is crucial because the topological dimension of
\mathcal{K}(X)
is infinite only if
X
is this type of topological space. Hence, it is possible for the upper metric mean dimension of
T_{\mathcal{K}}
to be infinite, since it is bounded above by the box-counting dimension of the phase space. Moreover, it is important to note that the metric mean dimension depends on the choice of metric for the phase space.

As a final result, we present a slight generalization of Theorem
A
. More precisely, if we do not require the map
T
to be injective, then metric mean dimension of
T_{\mathcal{K}}
is infinite.

Theorem F
.

Let
X
be a locally connected continuum endowed with a convex metric
d
. If
T:X\to X
is a surjective map and
\Omega(T)\subsetneq X
, then
\emph{mdim}_{M}(\mathcal{K}(X),d_{H},T_{\mathcal{K}})=\infty
.

We point out that the condition of the nonwandering set being strictly contained in the phase space does not preclude a dynamical system from having positive topological entropy, since there are numerous examples of maps with both positive entropy and wandering sets.

Basic Definitions

From now on, let
(X,d)
be a compact metric space, and let
T:X\to X
be a continuous function, or simply, a map. A dynamical system is the pair
(X,T)
. In specific cases,
X
is a nondegenerate locally connected continuum. In other words,
(X,d)
is a Peano continuum, which is arcwise connected; that is, any two points of
X
can be joined by an arc in
X
. Moreover,
d
can be chosen to be a convex metric. Precisely, a convex metric
d
on
X
is a metric that induces the topology on
X
and for which midpoints always exist. That is, for any
x,y\in X
, there exists
z\in X
such that

d(x,z)=\frac{1}{2}d(x,y)=d(z,y).

For further information on locally connected continua and convex metrics, see
(
Illanes and Jr., 1999
, Section 10)
. To set some notation, for a given
\varepsilon>0
, define
B_{\varepsilon}(x)=\{y\in X:d(x,y)<\varepsilon\}
.

To avoid excessive use of parentheses, we write
Tx
for the image of
x\in X
under the map
T
. We say that a closed set
Y\subset X
is
T
-invariant
if, for every
y\in Y
,
Ty\in Y
, that is,
T(Y)\subset Y
. In this case,
(Y,T|_{Y})
is also a dynamical system. Moreover,
Y
is
totally invariant
if
T(Y)=Y
. An important set in the study of dynamical systems is the
nonwandering set
of
T
, denoted by
\Omega(T)
. A point
x\in\Omega(T)
if, for every neighborhood
U
of
x
in
X
, there exists an integer
n\geq 1
such that
T^{n}(U)\cap U\neq\varnothing
. Equivalently, there exists
m\geq 1
such that
T^{-m}(U)\cap U\neq\varnothing
. The nonwandering set is nonempty, compact, and invariant.

In general, if
E
is a finite set, we denote its cardinality by
\#E
.

2.1
Hyperspaces and induced maps

A
hyperspace
is a designated collection of subsets of
X
. In this work, we study the following hyperspaces:

•

\mathcal{K}(X)=\{A\subset X;A\text{ is closed and nonempty}\}
;

•

\mathcal{C}(X)=\{A\in\mathcal{K}(X);A\text{ is connected}\}
, when
X
is a continuum.

Observe that
\mathcal{C}(X)\subset\mathcal{K}(X)
. In this text, we designate these as the
compacta hyperspace
and the
continuum hyperspace
, respectively. We always assume that
X
is not a singleton. In both hyperspaces, we consider the
Hausdorff metric

d_{H}
defined as

d_{H}(A,B)=\inf\{\varepsilon\geq 0;A\subset B_{\varepsilon}\text{ and }B\subset A_{\varepsilon}\},

where
A_{\varepsilon}=\bigcup_{x\in A}\{y\in X;d(x,y)\leq\varepsilon\}
is the
\varepsilon
-fattening of A
. In this context, given
\varepsilon>0
, define
\textbf{B}^{H}(A,\varepsilon)=\{B\in\mathcal{K}(X);d_{H}(A,B)<\varepsilon\}
. Moreover, whenever
X
is a compact topological space, the topology generated by
d_{H}
coincides with the
Vietoris topology
, whose basis consists of the following collection of sets indexed by
k\in\mathbb{N}
:

\langle U_{1},\dots,U_{k}\rangle=\{A\in\mathcal{K}(X);A\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}U_{i}\text{ and }A\cap U_{i}\neq\varnothing\text{ for each }1\leq i\leq k\},

where
U_{1},\dots,U_{k}
are nonempty open subsets of
X
.

It is known that
(\mathcal{K}(X),d_{H})
is a compact metric space
(
Illanes and Jr., 1999
, Theorem 3.5)
. Moreover, if
X
is a nondegenerate locally connected continuum, then
\mathcal{K}(X)
is homeomorphic to the Hilbert cube
[0,1]^{\mathbb{Z}}
. If, in addition,
X
has no free arcs
1
1

1

A continuum
X
has no free arcs if, for every
\gamma\subset X
homeomorphic to the unit interval
[0,1]
, the interior of
\gamma
is not open in
X
.
, then
\mathcal{C}(X)
is also homeomorphic to the Hilbert cube (cf.
Curtis and Schori (1974)
). In both cases, the topological dimension of
\mathcal{K}(X)
and
\mathcal{C}(X)
is infinite.

Let
F:X\to X
be a map, the
induced hyperspace map

F_{\mathcal{K}}:\mathcal{K}(X)\to\mathcal{K}(X)
is given by
F_{\mathcal{K}}(A):=F(A)
. Note that
F(A)
is closed in
X
, because
F
is continuous, and therefore
F_{\mathcal{K}}
is well defined. It is also well known that
F_{\mathcal{K}}
is continuous, and a homeomorphism if and only if
F
is a homeomorphism. The
induced continuum map
is intuitively defined as the map
F_{\mathcal{K}}
restricted to
\mathcal{C}(X)
, that is,
F_{\mathcal{K}}:\mathcal{C}(X)\to\mathcal{C}(X)
. An alternate notation is
F_{\mathcal{K}}|_{\mathcal{C}(X)}:\mathcal{C}(X)\to\mathcal{C}(X)
.

2.2
Mean dimension

Consider
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{i}\}_{i\in I}
a finite open cover of
X
. The
order

\text{ord}(\mathcal{U})
is the maximum integer
k\geq 0
such that there are pairwise distinct
i_{0},...,i_{k}\in I
satisfying
U_{i_{0}}\cap...\cap U_{i_{k}}\neq\varnothing
. A
refinement
of
\mathcal{U}
is an open cover
\mathcal{V}=\{V_{j}\}_{j\in J}
of
X
such that for every
V_{j}\in\mathcal{V}
there is
U_{i}\in\mathcal{U}
with
V_{j}\subset U_{i}
. The
topological dimension
of
X
is given by the supremum of the
degree

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{U})
, which is the minimum order
\text{ord}(\mathcal{V})
over all refinements
\mathcal{V}
of
\mathcal{U}
(see Definition 1.6.7 in
Engelking (1978)
for details). The dimension of a topological space is denoted by
\dim(X)
. In general, the topological dimension coincides with the usual notion of dimension for more familiar spaces, such as manifolds. Moreover,
\dim([0,1]^{\mathbb{Z}})=\infty
.

For two open covers
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
of
X
, the
joint
is given by
\mathcal{U}\vee\mathcal{V}=\{U_{i}\cap V_{j};i\in I,j\in J\}
. Since
T:X\to X
is continuous, observe that
T^{-1}\mathcal{U}=\{T^{-1}U_{i};i\in I\}
is also an open cover of
X
. Therefore, the
(topological) mean dimension
of
(X,T)
is given by

\text{mdim}(X,T)=\sup\limits_{\mathcal{U}}\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{U}\vee T^{-1}\mathcal{U}\vee\cdots\vee T^{-n+1}\mathcal{U})}{n},

where
\mathcal{U}
runs over all finite open covers of
X
. Observe that the mean dimension is the dynamical analog of the topological dimension.

Proposition 1

(
Lindenstrauss and Weiss (2000)
;
Coornaert (2015)
)
.

Some useful basic properties of the mean dimension are:

1.

The mean dimension is a topological invariant and takes values in
[0,\infty]
;

2.

If the topological entropy of the dynamical system is finite, then its mean dimension is zero;

3.

If
Y
is a closed
T
-invariant subset of
X
, then
\emph{mdim}(Y,T)\leq\emph{mdim}(X,T)
;

4.

If
X
is finite-dimensional, then
\emph{mdim}(X,T)=0
;

5.

For any dynamical system
(X,T)
,
\emph{mdim}(X,T^{n})=n\cdot\emph{mdim}(X,T)
;

6.

If the phase space is the Hilbert cube
[0,1]^{\mathbb{Z}}
, and
\sigma:[0,1]^{\mathbb{Z}}\to[0,1]^{\mathbb{Z}}
is the shift transformation, then
\emph{mdim}([0,1]^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma)=1
. Generally,
\emph{mdim}(([0,1]^{d})^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma)=d
.

2.3
Topological entropy and Metric mean dimension

Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define the
dynamical distance

d_{n}
as

d_{n}(x,y)=\max\{d\big(T^{i}x,T^{i}y\big),0\leq i\leq n-1\},

where
T^{0}
is the identity. It is well known that
d_{n}
is still a distance function on
X
and generates the same topology as
d
. For
x\in X
and
\varepsilon>0
, we call
B_{(n,\varepsilon)}(x)=\{y\in X;d_{n}(x,y)<\varepsilon\}
the
(n,\varepsilon)
-dynamical ball around x
.

Call
A\subset X
a
(n,\varepsilon)
-separated set
if for any distinct points
x,y\in A
,
d_{n}(x,y)\geq\varepsilon
. Denote by
\text{Sep}(T,n,\varepsilon)
the maximal cardinality of a
(n,\varepsilon)
-separated set, which is finite by the compactness of
X
. We say that
E\subset X
is a
(n,\varepsilon)
-spanning set
for
X
if for any
x\in X
, there exists
y\in E
such that
d_{n}(x,y)<\varepsilon
. In other words,
E
is a
(n,\varepsilon)
-spanning set if
X
can be covered by the interior of the union of
(n,\varepsilon)
-dynamical balls centered at points of
E
. That is,

X\subset\bigcup\limits_{y\in E}B_{(n,\varepsilon)}(y).

Let
\text{Span}(T,n,\varepsilon)
be the minimum cardinality of any
(n,\varepsilon)
-spanning set. Define

h(T,\varepsilon)=\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\text{Sep}(T,n,\varepsilon)}{n}\text{ and }\widetilde{h}(T,\varepsilon)=\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\text{Span}(T,n,\varepsilon)}{n}.

It is well known that
h(T,\varepsilon)\geq\widetilde{h}(T,\varepsilon)
(cf.
(
Katok and Hasselblatt, 1995
, Chapter 3)
). Note that if
\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{2}
, then
\widetilde{h}(T,\varepsilon_{1})\geq\widetilde{h}(T,\varepsilon_{2})
. The
topological entropy

h(T)
is the limit of both
h(T,\varepsilon)
and
\widetilde{h}(T,\varepsilon)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, which coincide. That is,

h(T)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}h(T,\varepsilon)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\widetilde{h}(T,\varepsilon).

When the topological entropy is infinite, we are interested in the growth of
\widetilde{h}(T,\varepsilon)
with respect to
\varepsilon
. That is, the growth of the rate of exponential balls which cover
X
in mean. This motivates the definition of upper and lower metric mean dimension, given in
Lindenstrauss and Weiss (2000)
.

The
lower metric mean dimension
and the
upper metric mean dimension
of
(X,T)
are defined, respectively, by

\underline{\text{mdim}_{M}}(X,d,T)=\liminf\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{h(T,\varepsilon)}{-\log\varepsilon}\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\overline{\text{mdim}_{M}}(X,d,T)=\limsup\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{h(T,\varepsilon)}{-\log\varepsilon}.

(1)

In these definitions,
h(T,\varepsilon)
can be replaced with
\widetilde{h}(T,\varepsilon)
. When both limits coincide, we denote this common value by
\text{mdim}_{M}(X,d,T)
. Unlike topological entropy, the metric mean dimension may change if the metric
d
is changed.

The following important inequalities relate the mean dimension, the metric mean dimension, and the topological entropy whenever
d
is a metric compatible with the topology on
X

(
Lindenstrauss and Weiss, 2000
, Theorem 4.2)
:

\text{mdim}(X,T)\leq\underline{\text{mdim}_{M}}(X,d,T)\leq\overline{\text{mdim}_{M}}(X,d,T)\leq h(T).

(2)

Note that if
h(T)
is finite, then the metric mean dimension is zero. Therefore, the metric mean dimension is useful for distinguishing between dynamical systems with infinite topological entropy.

In contrast, the concept of
polynomial entropy
is useful for distinguishing between dynamical systems with topological entropy equal to zero. It was introduced by C. Labrousse and J.P. Marco in
Labrousse and Marco (2014)
and is given by:

h_{\text{pol}}(T)=\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\text{Sep}(T,n,\varepsilon)}{\log n}=\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\text{Span}(T,n,\varepsilon)}{\log n}.

(3)

2.4
Morse-Smale diffeomorphisms.

For
N^{m}
an
m
-dimensional compact and connected smooth manifold without boundary, set
\text{Diff}^{r}(N^{m})
as the set of
C^{r}
diffeomorphisms endowed with the
C^{r}
topology, for
r\geq 1
. A periodic point
x\in N^{m}
of period
k\geq 1
for
F\in\text{Diff}^{r}(N^{m})
is
hyperbolic
if the derivative
(DF^{k})_{x}
has its spectrum disjoint from the unit circle in
\mathbb{C}
. It is well known that in this case, we have the existence of stable and unstable manifolds of
x
, denoted by
W^{s}(x)
and
W^{u}(x)
.

Definition 1
.

F\in\emph{Diff}^{r}(N^{m})
is Morse-Smale if it satisfies the following conditions:

1.

The set of nonwandering points,
\Omega(F)
, contains only a finite number of hyperbolic periodic points;

2.

The stable and unstable manifolds of the periodic points are all transversal to each other.

The Morse-Smale diffeomorphisms have the following properties:

•

Every Morse-Smale diffeomorphism has an
attractor
periodic point and a
repeller
periodic point, that is, the stable (unstable) manifold of the attractor (repeller) periodic point is an immersed submanifold of dimension
m
;

•

If
p
is an attractor periodic point, then there is
q
a repeller periodic point such that the stable manifold of
p
intersects transversely the unstable manifold of
q
and
W^{s}(p)\cap W^{u}(q)\neq\varnothing
;

•

The topological entropy of a Morse-Smale diffeomorphism is always zero.

Further information on Morse-Smale diffeomorphisms can be found at
Arbieto and Bohorquez (2023)
.

3
Mean dimension of induced homeomorphisms

This section is devoted to the study of the topological mean dimension of induced hyperspace maps for homeomorphisms defined in a locally connected continuum. A topological space is said to be
arcwise connected
provided that any two of its points can be joined by an arc contained in this space.

Proposition 2

(
Illanes and Jr. (1999)
, Theorem 10.2)
.

Every locally connected continuum is arcwise connected.

3.1
Proof of Theorem
A

Recall that from our hypothesis we suppose that
X
is a locally connected continuum and
T:X\to X
is a homeomorphism such that
\Omega(T)\subsetneq X
. The following proof relies on the fact that there is a wandering arc
\gamma_{x}
contained in
X\setminus\Omega(T)
, which is guaranteed by Proposition
2
, and we show how to use it to increase the mean dimension of the hyperspace by identifying the hyperspace of the arc with the Hilbert cube.

Proof of Theorem
A
.

Remember that the nonwandering set is compact, hence
X\setminus\Omega(T)
is an open set. Given
x\in X\setminus\Omega(T)
, there is
U_{x}
a neighborhood of
x
such that
T^{n}(U_{x})\cap U_{x}=\varnothing
, where
n
is a non-zero integer, because
T
is a homeomorphism. As a consequence,
T^{n_{1}}(U_{x})\cap T^{n_{2}}(U_{x})=\varnothing
, for
n_{1}\neq n_{2}
in
\mathbb{Z}
. Let
\gamma_{x}
be an arc contained in
U_{x}
, which exists because
X
is a locally connected continuum
(
Illanes and Jr., 1999
, Theorem 10.2)
. Thus, consider
\varphi:[0,1]\to\gamma_{x}
a homeomorphism and
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Choose
k
disjoint closed intervals
J_{i}
in
[0,1]
. For each
J_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq k
, consider a homeomorphism
\psi_{i}:[0,1]\to J_{i}
. If
x=(x_{1},...,x_{k})
is a point in
[0,1]^{k}
, then
\varphi(\{\psi_{i}(x_{i}),1\leq i\leq k\})\in\mathcal{K}(X)
. Set
\psi(x)=\{\psi_{i}(x_{i}),1\leq i\leq k\}
, hence
\varphi\circ\psi(x)
is an element of
\mathcal{K}(X)
. Denote
\gamma_{i}=\varphi(J_{i})
a subset of
\gamma_{x}
.

Define a map
\Phi:([0,1]^{k})^{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathcal{K}(X)
, for
\xi=(...,\xi_{-1},\xi_{0},\xi_{1},...)\in([0,1]^{k})^{\mathbb{Z}}
, as

\Phi(\xi)=\Phi(...,\xi_{-1},\xi_{0},\xi_{1},...)=\left(\bigcup\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}T^{n}\circ\varphi\circ\psi(\xi_{n})\right)\cup\Omega(T).

Observe that the limit points of
\bigcup\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}T^{n}\circ\varphi\circ\psi(\xi_{n})
are contained in the nonwandering set
\Omega(T)
, because
T^{n}\circ\varphi\circ\psi(\xi_{n})\subset T^{n}(\gamma_{x})
, for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, and the limit points of the wandering arc
\gamma_{x}
are contained in the nonwandering set. Thus,
\Phi(\xi)
is an element of the hyperspace.

Given that
T_{\mathcal{K}}(\Omega(T))=\Omega(T)
, we also have

T_{\mathcal{K}}\circ\Phi(\xi)

=

\left(\bigcup\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}T^{n+1}\circ\varphi\circ\psi(\xi_{n})\right)\cup\Omega(T)

=

\left(\bigcup\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}T^{n}\circ\varphi\circ\psi(\xi_{n-1})\right)\cup\Omega(T)

=

\Phi\circ\sigma^{-1}(\xi),

where
\sigma:([0,1]^{k})^{\mathbb{Z}}\to([0,1]^{k})^{\mathbb{Z}}
is the shift transformation. Lindenstrauss & Weiss proved in
Lindenstrauss and Weiss (2000)
that
\text{mdim}(([0,1]^{k})^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma)=k
. Since the mean dimension is a topological invariant, the rest of this proof is dedicated to proving that
\Phi
is injective and continuous. Note that the above equation also shows that
\Phi\big(([0,1]^{k})^{\mathbb{Z}}\big)
is invariant by
T_{\mathcal{K}}
.

It is not hard to prove that
\Phi
is injective because if
\xi,\eta\in([0,1]^{k})^{\mathbb{Z}}
are such that
\xi\neq\eta
, then there is
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\xi_{\ell}\neq\eta_{\ell}
. That is, there is
i\in\{1,...,k\}
such that
\xi_{\ell,i}\neq\eta_{\ell,i}
. Then,
\psi_{i}(\xi_{\ell,i})\neq\psi_{i}(\eta_{\ell,i})
. Therefore, by construction,
\Phi(\xi)\neq\Phi(\eta)
.

To prove continuity, consider
\xi\in([0,1]^{k})^{\mathbb{Z}}
and
\varepsilon>0
. There exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
where
\lvert n\rvert\geq N
, the distance between
T^{n}\circ\varphi\circ\psi(\xi_{n})
and
\Omega(T)
is less than
\varepsilon/4
, that is, there is
z\in\Omega(T)
such that
d(z,T^{n}\circ\varphi\circ\psi(\xi_{n}))<\varepsilon/4
. Such
N
exists because the limit points of the sequence
(T^{n}\circ\varphi\circ\psi(\xi_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
are all contained in
\Omega(T)
. Let
d_{*}
be a metric in
([0,1]^{k})^{\mathbb{Z}}
given by

d_{*}(\xi,\eta)=\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-\lvert n\rvert}\lVert\xi_{n}-\eta_{n}\rVert,

where
\lVert.\rVert
is the Euclidean metric in
[0,1]^{k}
. It is well known that such a metric is compatible with the topology of
([0,1]^{k})^{\mathbb{Z}}

van Mill (1989)
. Let
\delta>0
and
\eta\in([0,1]^{k})^{\mathbb{Z}}
be such that
d_{*}(\xi,\eta)<\delta
and
\xi_{i}=\eta_{i}
for
\lvert i\rvert\leq N
. Note that
\bigcup\limits_{\lvert i\rvert\leq N}T^{i}\circ\varphi\circ\psi(\xi_{i})=\bigcup\limits_{\lvert i\rvert\leq N}T^{i}\circ\varphi\circ\psi(\eta_{i})
. Therefore

d_{H}(\Phi(\xi),\Phi(\eta))

=

d_{H}(\Phi(\xi)\setminus\bigcup\limits_{\lvert i\rvert\leq N}T^{i}\circ\varphi\circ\psi(\xi_{i}),\Phi(\eta)\setminus\bigcup\limits_{\lvert i\rvert\leq N}T^{i}\circ\varphi\circ\psi(\eta_{i}))

\leq

d_{H}(\Phi(\xi)\setminus\bigcup\limits_{\lvert i\rvert\leq N}T^{i}\circ\varphi\circ\psi(\xi_{i}),\Omega(T))+d_{H}(\Omega(T),\Phi(\eta)\setminus\bigcup\limits_{\lvert i\rvert\leq N}T^{i}\circ\varphi\circ\psi(\eta_{i}))

\leq

\varepsilon/4+\varepsilon/4<\varepsilon
,

because
\Omega(T)\subset\Phi(\xi)
and
\Omega(T)\subset\Phi(\eta)
.

Finally,
\Phi
is a homeomorphism of
([0,1]^{k})^{\mathbb{Z}}
onto its image. Hence

\text{mdim}(\mathcal{K}(X),T_{\mathcal{K}})\geq\text{mdim}(\Phi(([0,1]^{k})^{\mathbb{Z}}),T_{\mathcal{K}})=k.

Since
k\in\mathbb{N}
is arbitrary, then
\text{mdim}(\mathcal{K}(X),T_{\mathcal{K}})=\infty
.
∎

Whenever the nonwandering set is finite, the topological entropy of
T
is zero
(
Lampart and Raith, 2010
, Proposition 1 & 2)
. Therefore, Theorem
A
proves that for homeomorphisms with finite nonwandering set, the explosion phenomenon occurs. The most common examples of these systems are the Morse-Smale diffeomorphisms, defined in §
2.4
. The following consequence is trivial if one recalls Equation (
2
).

Remark 1
.

Let
X
be a locally connected continuum and
T:X\to X
a homeomorphism. If the nonwandering set
\Omega(T)
is a strict subset of
X
, then
{\text{mdim}}(\mathcal{K}(X),\rho,T_{\mathcal{K}})=\infty
, where

. In particular,
\emph{mdim}(\mathcal{K}(X),d_{H},T_{\mathcal{K}})=\infty
.

To show Corollary
1

Proposition 3

(
Coornaert (2015)
, Corollary 7.6.3)
.

Let

be a positive integer and
\alpha
be a real number such that

. Then there exists a subshift

Note that for every real number

Proof of Theorem
B

The core of its proof relies on the same idea as in Theorem

B
.

Given

3.3
Explosion on the circle

The explosion of the mean dimension for the induced hyperspace map is better understood for homeomorphisms defined on one-dimensional topological manifolds because they all have zero topological entropy. Precisely, there is a dichotomy for such homeomorphisms: the mean dimension of its induced hyperspace map is zero or infinite. The following lemma states this dichotomy for interval homeomorphisms and is crucial to proving the analogous statement for circle homeomorphisms.

Lemma 1
.

Let
T:[0,1]\to[0,1]
be a homeomorphism such that
T^{2}
is not the identity, then
{\text{mdim}}(\mathcal{K}([0,1]),T_{\mathcal{K}})=\infty
.

Proof.

The first step is to prove that
T^{2}
is increasing. In fact, since
T
is a homeomorphism,
T
is a strictly increasing or decreasing function. If
T
is increasing, then it is obvious. If
T
is decreasing, then
Ta<Tb
, when
a>b
in
[0,1]
. Therefore,
T^{2}a>T^{2}b
.

Since
T^{2}
is not the identity, then
T^{2}x<x
or
T^{2}x>x
, for some
x\in[0,1]
. Suppose, without loss of generality, that
T^{2}x<x
. Hence
T^{2n_{2}}x<T^{2n_{1}}x
for
n_{2}>n_{1}
in
\mathbb{Z}
.

Notice that
(T^{2n}x)_{n\geq 0}
is a decreasing sequence, then
T^{2n}x
converges to a fixed point of
T^{2}
. The same happens to
(T^{2k}x)_{k\leq 0}
, because
T^{2k}x
is an increasing sequence. Recall that
T^{2}
has at least two fixed points because
T^{2}
is increasing.

The second step is to choose a closed interval
J
contained in the interval
(T^{2}x,x)
, therefore
T^{2n_{2}}(J)\cap T^{2n_{1}}(J)=\varnothing
for
n_{2}>n_{1}
in
\mathbb{Z}
, and
\lim_{n\to\pm\infty}T^{2n}(J)
is a fixed point of
T^{2}
. Since
J
is also a wandering interval, just as in the proof of Theorem
A
, we have
\text{mdim}(\mathcal{K}([0,1]),T_{\mathcal{K}}^{2})=\text{mdim}(\mathcal{K}([0,1]),T_{\mathcal{K}})=\infty
.
∎

Remark 2
.

If
T^{2}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
is the identity, then
{\text{mdim}}(\mathcal{K}([0,1]),T_{\mathcal{K}}^{2})={\text{mdim}}(\mathcal{K}([0,1]),T_{\mathcal{K}})=0
. This proves the dichotomy for homeomorphisms defined in the unit interval
[0,1]
.

Before proceeding, we would like to point out the following remark about the hyperspace map of isometries.

Remark 3
.

If
R:X\to X
is an isometry, then the induced map
R_{\mathcal{K}}:\mathcal{K}(X)\to\mathcal{K}(X)
is also an isometry. In fact, since
R
is an isometry, for every
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
we have
d(Rx_{1},Rx_{2})=d(x_{1},x_{2})
. Now let
A,B\subset X
be closed sets such that
d_{H}(A,B)=\varepsilon
. Because
A\subset B_{\varepsilon}
, it follows that
R(A)\subset R(B)_{\varepsilon}
. If
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,\varepsilon)
, then
A\not\subset B_{\varepsilon_{0}}
, so there exist
a\in A
and
b\in B
with
d(a,b)>\varepsilon_{0}
. Thus
d(Ra,Rb)=d(a,b)>\varepsilon_{0}
, which shows that
R(A)\not\subset R(B)_{\varepsilon_{0}}
. Therefore no change occurs in the Hausdorff distance, and we conclude that
d_{H}(R_{\mathcal{K}}(A),R_{\mathcal{K}}(B))=d_{H}(A,B)
.

To prove the explosion phenomenon on the circle
S^{1}
, we will refer to the consequences of the rotation number theory of circle homeomorphisms. A great exposition of this subject is given in
de Faria and Guarino (Versão 1.3, Junho de 2022)
. Recall that two dynamical systems
T:X\to X

Proof of Theorem

Suppose that

and

. The function

conjugates

to a homeomorphism

given by

. If

is the identity, then

is the identity. Thus,

. Otherwise, by Lemma

, there is a

, then
\psi^{-1}(J)

. Therefore, by Theorem

\text{mdim}(\mathcal{K}(S^{1}),H_{\mathcal{K}})=\infty
.

The last step is to consider that

has no periodic points. If

is conjugated to an irrational rotation

by the homeomorphism

is conjugated to

. Since

is zero. Therefore,
\text{mdim}(\mathcal{K}(S^{1}),H_{\mathcal{K}})=0
. Otherwise, the non-wandering set of

. In this case, by the proof of Theorem

∎

Given

an orientation-preserving homeomorphism, if

is conjugated to a rotation, then

is an isometry with respect to some metric compatible with the usual topology on

•

;

•

. Suppose that

is not the identity; then

Constantin and Kolev (1994)

As a consequence, this discussion proves Corollary

Remark 4

The orientation-preserving condition on Corollary

{\text{mdim}}(\mathcal{K}(S^{1}),H_{\mathcal{K}})=0

3.4
Differences between mean dimension and topological dimension

As discussed above, the mean dimension is the dynamical analog of the topological dimension. The reader may wonder if results from classical dimension theory also hold for the mean dimension. In the context of hyperspaces, for instance, a classical result states that for any
T_{1}
-space
2
2

2

A
T_{1}
space
is a topological space in which, for every pair of distinct points, each has a neighborhood not containing the other point.

X
, we have
\dim(X)=0
if and only if
\dim(\mathcal{K}(X))=0

(
Illanes and Jr., 1999

.

Surprisingly, this result’s analog does not hold for the mean dimension. Indeed, as a consequence of our previous results, there exists a dynamical system
(X,T)
on a finite-dimensional phase space (hence
\text{mdim}(X,T)=0
) such that
\text{mdim}(\mathcal{K}(X),T_{\mathcal{K}})=\infty
. However, the converse is true, and this is an easy consequence of a property of mean dimension.

Proposition 4
.

If
(X,d)
is a compact metric space,
T:X\to X
is a continuous function, and
\emph{mdim}(\mathcal{K}(X),T_{\mathcal{K}})=0
, then
\emph{mdim}(X,T)=0
.

Proof.

Consider
\Psi:X\to\mathcal{K}(X)
given by
\Psi(x)=\{x\}
. Then
\Psi
is injective and continuous. Furthermore,
\Psi
is a topological conjugation, that is,

\Psi\circ T(x)=\{Tx\}=T_{\mathcal{K}}(\{x\})=T_{\mathcal{K}}\circ\Psi(x).

Therefore,
0=\text{mdim}(\mathcal{K}(X),T_{\mathcal{K}})\geq\text{mdim}(X,T)
.
∎

In other words, since
(X,T)
is always a factor of
(\mathcal{K}(X),T_{\mathcal{K}})
, the above Proposition is a direct consequence of the following fact:

Lemma 2
.

Given
(X,d)
a compact metric space and
T:X\to X
a continuous map,
\emph{mdim}(\mathcal{K}(X),T_{\mathcal{K}})\geq\emph{mdim}(X,T)
.

4
Metric mean dimension of induced maps

For an arbitrary map,
T:X\to X
, the metric mean dimension offers a viable alternative when it is not yet possible to calculate the mean dimension of its induced hyperspace map. The calculation of the metric mean dimension is simpler because it relies on how the dynamics separates points in the phase space.

To compute this dimension in the hyperspace
\mathcal{K}(X)
, we must first define a dynamical distance based on the Hausdorff metric

. Precisely, for

and

\mathcal{H}_{n}(A,B)=\max\{d_{H}(T_{\mathcal{K}}^{i}A,T_{\mathcal{K}}^{i}B);0\leq i\leq n-1\},

and for

Hausdorff
(n,\varepsilon)
-dynamical ball
around

is defined as

\mathbf{B}^{H}_{(n,\varepsilon)}(A)=\{B\in\mathcal{K}(X);\mathcal{H}_{n}(A,B)<\varepsilon\}.

Before showing some properties of the dynamical Hausdorff metric, we need to establish certain topological properties of locally connected continuum spaces. We begin with the following well-known result.

Proposition 5

(

, Proposition 10.4)
.

Let

be a locally connected continuum with a convex metric

. Then any two points,

in

such that

is isometric to the closed interval

As a consequence of the above proposition, we obtain the expected result regarding the strict inclusion of open sets when there is a strict inequality between their diameters.

Lemma 3
.

Let
(X,d)
be a locally connected continuum where

Proof.

Let

be such that
2\varepsilon<\text{diam}(X)
. Hence, for any

. Since

is a locally connected continuum, there is an arc
\gamma
from

such that

, there is

such that

. Therefore,

and

.
∎

Remark 5

Given

(n,\varepsilon)

\dim_{B}(X,d)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\log N_{\varepsilon}(X)}{-\log\varepsilon},

whenever this limit exists.

Lemma 8
.

Given the usual Euclidean metric
\rho
on
[0,1]
, there exists a Cantor set
\Lambda\subset[0,1]
such that
\dim_{B}(\mathcal{K}(\Lambda),\rho_{H})=0
.

Proof.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\geq 2
, consider the following construction of a Cantor set: in the first step, remove an open interval
J_{1,1}\subset[0,1]
such that
J_{1,1}^{\complement}\cap[0,1]=I_{1,0}\cup I_{1,1}
, where
\text{diam}(I_{1,0})=\text{diam}(I_{1,1})=2^{-4}
, and call
I_{1,0}\cup I_{1,1}=\Lambda_{1}
. In step
k
, remove the open intervals
J_{k,1},...,J_{k,2^{k-1}}
from
\Lambda_{k-1}
such that

J_{k,1}^{\complement}\cap...\cap J_{k,2^{k-1}}^{\complement}\cap\Lambda_{k-1}=I_{k,1}\cup...\cup I_{k,2^{k}}=\Lambda_{k},

where
\text{diam}(I_{k,j})=2^{-(4^{k})}
, for
1\leq j\leq 2^{k}
. The Cantor set
\Lambda
is given by
\Lambda=\lim_{k\to\infty}\Lambda_{k}
. To show that the box-counting dimension of
\mathcal{K}(\Lambda)
is equal to
0
, observe that the minimum number of open sets of diameter at most
2^{-(4^{k})}
required to cover
\Lambda
is
2^{k+1}
. By Equation (
4
) in Lemma
7
,

N_{k}(\mathcal{K}(\Lambda))\leq 2^{N_{k}(\Lambda)}-1\leq 2^{2^{k+1}},

where
N_{k}
is the smallest number of open sets of diameter at most
2^{-(4^{k})}
. Therefore,

\dim_{B}(\mathcal{K}(\Lambda),\rho_{H})\leq\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\log 2^{2^{k+1}}}{-\log 2^{-(4^{k})}}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{2^{k+1}}{4^{k}}=0.

∎

The box-counting dimension is strongly connected to the metric mean dimension for any map
T
: it provides an upper bound. More precisely, we have the inequality

\text{mdim}_{M}(X,d,T)\leq\dim_{B}(X,d)

(5)

whenever both dimensions exist, which is a specific case of a more general result. Proved in Remark 4 of
Velozo and Velozo (2017)
, this inequality, along with the above lemma, allows us to construct an example of a map whose topological entropy is positive, but whose induced hyperspace map has a metric mean dimension of zero.

Proof of Theorem
E
.

Consider the Cantor set
\Lambda\subset[0,1]
from Lemma
8
. It is a compact metric space endowed with the Euclidean metric
\rho
. Given the one-sided symbolic space
\Sigma_{2}
, there exists a homeomorphism between
\Lambda
and
\Sigma_{2}
. Hence, there exists a continuous map
T:\Lambda\to\Lambda
that is conjugate to the one-sided shift
\sigma:\Sigma_{2}\to\Sigma_{2}
. Indeed, if
\Psi:\Lambda\to\Sigma_{2}
is a homeomorphism, then define
T
as
T=\Psi^{-1}\circ\sigma\circ\Psi
. This implies
h(T)>0
. Finally, by Equation (
5
), the metric mean dimension of
T_{\mathcal{K}}
is bounded above by the box-counting dimension of
\mathcal{K}(\Lambda)
, and therefore
\text{mdim}_{M}(\mathcal{K}(X),\rho_{H},T_{\mathcal{K}})=0
.
∎

Given a surjective map
T:X\to X
and a finite set of points
E\subset X
. A
first trail of
E
is a set
E_{1}\subset T^{-1}(E)
such that
\#E_{1}=\#E
and
T(E_{1})=E
. Moreover, for
n\geq 2
, an
n
-th trail of
E
is a set
E_{n}
such that
\#E_{n}=\#E_{n-1}
and
T(E_{n})=E_{n-1}
. In particular,
T^{n}(E_{n})=E
.

In what follows, we prove a result similar to Theorem
A
. Precisely, we show that if
X
is a locally connected continuum endowed with a convex metric
d
and
T:X\to X
is a surjective map such that
\Omega(T)\subsetneq X
, then
\text{mdim}_{M}(\mathcal{K}(X),d_{H},T_{\mathcal{K}})=\infty
.

Proof of Theorem
F
.

Choosing
x\in X\cap\Omega(T)^{\complement}
, then
x
is a wandering point of
X
. Hence, there exists a neighborhood
U
of
x
such that for all
n\geq 1
,
T^{n}(U)\cap U=\varnothing
and
T^{-n}(U)\cap U=\varnothing
. Since
U^{\complement}
is compact,
\eta:=\inf_{x_{0}U^{\complement}}d(x,x_{0})
is a positive real number. This implies
B_{\eta}(x)\subset U
. Given
y\in B_{\eta}(x)
, there exists an arc
\gamma
connecting
x
to
y
that is isometric to
[0,d(x,y)]
, by Proposition
5
. Furthermore, by Remark
5
,
\gamma\subset B_{\eta}(x)
. Clearly,
\gamma\subset U
.

Since
U
is an open set and
\gamma
is compact, there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
and for all
z\in\gamma
,
B_{\varepsilon}(z)\subset U
. Set
\alpha_{0}:=d(x,y)
and choose
\lfloor\frac{\alpha_{0}}{\varepsilon}\rfloor
distinct points in
\gamma
such that the distance between any pair of points is greater than or equal to a sufficiently small
\varepsilon
. That is, since
\gamma
is isometric to
[0,\alpha_{0}]
, there exists a set
E=\{x_{1},...,x_{M}\}\subset\gamma
, where
M=\lfloor\frac{\alpha_{0}}{\varepsilon}\rfloor
, such that
d(x_{i},x_{j})\geq\varepsilon
, for
1\leq i<j\leq M
. Observe that, by Remark
6
, for distinct nonempty sets
A,B\subset E
,
d_{H}(A,B)\geq\varepsilon
.

Denote all the nonempty subsets of
E
by
A^{i}_{0}
, for
1\leq i\leq 2^{M}-1
, and the
n
-th trail of
A^{i}_{0}
as
A^{i}_{n}
. Given
(i_{0},i_{1},...,i_{n-1})\in\{1,...,2^{M}-1\}^{n}
, define

A_{i_{0},...,i_{n-1}}=\bigcup\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}A^{i_{k}}_{k}.

(6)

Observe that
A_{i_{0},...,i_{n-1}}
is a point of
\mathcal{K}(X)
because it is a finite union of compact sets. Moreover,
A^{i_{k}}_{k}\subset T^{-k}(U)
, hence, for
k\geq 1
, the Hausdorff distance from
A^{i_{k}}_{k}
to any subset of
E
is always greater than or equal to
\varepsilon
. For distinct
(j_{0},j_{1},...,j_{n-1})\in\{1,...,2^{M}-1\}^{n}
, we claim that

\mathcal{H}_{n}(A_{i_{0},...,i_{n-1}},A_{j_{0},...,j_{n-1}})\geq\varepsilon,

(7)

which we will assume is true for now. This implies
\text{Sep}(T_{\mathcal{K}},n,\varepsilon)\geq(2^{M}-1)^{n}
. Thus,

h(T_{\mathcal{K}},\varepsilon)=\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\text{Sep}(T_{\mathcal{K}},n,\varepsilon)}{n}\geq M\log 2.

Therefore,

\underline{\text{mdim}_{M}}(\mathcal{K}(X),d_{H},T_{\mathcal{K}})=\liminf\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{h(T_{\mathcal{K}},\varepsilon)}{-\log\varepsilon}\geq\liminf\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{M\log 2}{-\log\varepsilon}=\liminf\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left\lfloor\frac{\alpha_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right\rfloor\frac{\log 2}{-\log\varepsilon}=\infty.

To conclude this proof, we only need to show that Equation (
7
) is valid. Consider two distinct elements
(i_{0},i_{1},...,i_{n-1})
and
(j_{0},j_{1},...,j_{n-1})
of
\{1,...,2^{M}-1\}^{n}
. Hence, there is
k\in\{0,...,n-1\}
such that
i_{k}\neq j_{k}
. That is, by Equation (
6
), the
k
-th trail of
A^{i_{k}}
belongs to
A_{i_{0},...,i_{n-1}}
, and the
k
-th trail of
A^{j_{k}}
belongs to
A_{j_{0},...,j_{n-1}}
. In other words,

A^{i_{k}}\subset T^{k}(A_{i_{0},...,i_{n-1}})\text{ and }A^{j_{k}}\subset T^{k}(A_{j_{0},...,j_{n-1}}).

Since
A^{i_{k}}
and
A^{j_{k}}
are distinct subsets of
E
, then
d_{H}(A^{i_{k}},A^{j_{k}})\geq\varepsilon
, by Remark
6
. Therefore,

d_{H}(T^{k}(A_{i_{0},...,i_{n-1}}),T^{k}(A_{j_{0},...,j_{n-1}}))\geq\varepsilon.

In fact, consider
k_{0}
distinct of
k
in
\{0,...,n-1\}
, then both
T^{k}(A_{k_{0}}^{i_{k_{0}}})
and
T^{k}(A_{k_{0}}^{j_{k_{0}}})
belongs to
T^{k-k_{0}}(U)
, where
T^{k-k_{0}}(U)\cap U=\varnothing
, because
k-k_{0}\neq 0
. This implies

\min\{d_{H}(T^{k}(A_{k_{0}}^{i_{k_{0}}}),A^{i_{k}}),d_{H}(T^{k}(A_{k_{0}}^{i_{k_{0}}}),A^{j_{k}}),d_{H}(T^{k}(A_{k_{0}}^{j_{k_{0}}}),A^{i_{k}}),d_{H}(T^{k}(A_{k_{0}}^{j_{k_{0}}}),A^{j_{k}})\}\geq\varepsilon.

Thus,

d_{H}(T^{k}(A_{i_{0},...,i_{n-1}}),T^{k}(A_{j_{0},...,j_{n-1}}))\geq d_{H}(A^{i_{k}},A^{j_{k}})\geq\varepsilon.

∎

The hypothesis that
X
is a locally connected continuum is essential, as it is a necessary condition for the metric mean dimension of
T_{\mathcal{K}}
to attain infinity with respect to the Hausdorff metric induced by some metric
d
.

5
Final Remarks

The mean dimension explosion phenomenon indicates that the induced hyperspace map has too many different orbits; hence, its complexity is overwhelmed by even slight separation of points in the base system. Future research may be directed toward classifying zero topological entropy systems with finite (metric) mean dimension of their induced maps.
