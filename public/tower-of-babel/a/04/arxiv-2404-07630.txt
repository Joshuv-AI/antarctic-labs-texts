Abstract
We investigate the voluntary disclosure decision of investors under uncertainty about information endowment ( Dye 1985 ). In our model, an investor first uncovers initial evidence about the target firm and then seeks additional information to help interpret the initial evidence. The investor takes a position in the firm’s stock, then voluntarily discloses some or all of their findings, and finally closes their position after the disclosure. We present two main findings. First, the investor will always disclose the initial evidence, even though the market is uncertain about whether the investor possesses such evidence. Second, the investor’s disclosure strategy of the additional information increases stock price volatility: they disclose extreme news and withhold moderate news.



Keywords:
verifiable disclosure, flexible trading, complex information

Introduction

In recent years, there has been a rise in investors voluntarily disclosing their information to the market. Many investors share their research and analysis on crowdsourced financial information platforms such as Yahoo! Finance and Seeking Alpha (
Dyer and
Kim 2021
and
Farrell
et al. 2022
). Furthermore, an increasing number of activist short sellers publicly disseminate their research reports on target firms (
Ljungqvist and
Qian 2016
and
Brendel and
Ryans 2021
). Despite the growing empirical interest in investors’ disclosure, there is little theoretical guidance on their disclosure strategies. Prior literature on voluntary disclosure has focused almost exclusively on the disclosure strategies of corporate managers (e.g.,
Dye 1985
and
Verrecchia 1983
). In this paper, we study investors’ communication to the capital market by highlighting one major difference of the disclosure by managers and investors. All managerial incentive systems are designed to better align the interests of managers with those of the shareholders. As a result, managers often have long exposure to the firm’s stock prices and thus aim to maximize stock prices (
Beyer
et al. 2010
). In contrast, investors can flexibly choose their positions on target firms before making the disclosure. Therefore, we examine investors’ disclosure strategies by augmenting
Dye 1985
with a key departure that the sender (investors) can choose a position before disclosure.

In our model, an investor receives two pieces of information stochastically, the initial evidence and the additional evidence about the value relevance of the initial evidence. The investor first takes a position in the firm’s stock, then voluntarily discloses some or all of his information, and finally closes his position after the disclosure. To illustrate the idea, suppose an investor has identified a red flag at a coffee chain: a sample of customer receipts shows that items per order has recently dropped.

1

This could be negative news for the company if the demand of existing customers is declining. However, the news could be positive if the company is attracting new customers who may place smaller orders. Conversely, an increase in items per order, a “green flag”, could be positive news if existing customers are ordering more, but could be negative news if the company is losing less loyal customers. The investor may uncover additional evidence that helps better interpret the value implications of the initial information. Thus, the investors’ two signals are complements, as in
Cheynel and
Levine 2020
.

Our first finding establishes that the investor informed about the initial evidence always discloses it despite the market’s uncertainty about whether he possesses such evidence. This result contrasts with prior work such as
Dye 1985
. In
Dye 1985
, the manager with long exposure to the stock price withholds his news if its revelation lowers the stock price. In our model, the investor can trade in both directions before disclosing and thus profit from price movements in either direction. In other words, the investor’s objective is to maximize
price volatility
through his disclosure. In contrast, if the investor withholds the initial evidence, the price will remain unchanged, and his profit will be zero. Therefore, the investor will never remain silent.

Our second result shows that the investor discloses the additional evidence if and only if it induces sufficiently large price volatility: he discloses extreme news and withholds moderate news. In the previous example, first consider the case where the initial signal is a red flag and is bad news on average. Thus, the disclosure of the initial signal alone generates a negative price impact. If the additional evidence strongly indicates that the coffee company is facing a reduction in customer demand, its disclosure reinforces the initial negative price impact. But if the additional evidence shows convincingly that the company is attracting new customers, then its disclosure reverses the initial negative price impact and leads to a sufficiently positive price impact. In both cases, the disclosure of the additional evidence increases price volatility compared with the disclosure of the initial signal alone. However, when the additional evidence is moderate, its disclosure only offsets the initial negative price impact, resulting in a marginal change in price volatility. Therefore, the investor will withhold the additional evidence.

Similar intuitions apply when the investor’s initial signal is a green flag. If the additional evidence shows convincingly that the news is very good (e.g., existing customers are placing bigger orders) or very bad (e.g., the company is losing less loyal customers), the investor will disclose the additional evidence along with the green flag. When the additional evidence is moderate, the investor will withhold it.

We show that the withholding of the additional evidence results in misleading disclosure. Specifically, when the disclosure of additional evidence reverses the impact of the initial evidence by only a small magnitude, the investor withholds it and issues a report with only the initial evidence. The market is misled by such a report, as the price would move in the opposite direction if the investor disclosed the additional evidence. This finding implies that both activist short-sellers and long investors can issue misleading disclosures. It is consistent with partial and misleading disclosure being one of the common complaints from target firms about activist short-sellers. In fact,
Brendel and
Ryans 2021
documents that 12% of target firms in their sample respond to activist short sellers by providing additional disclosure.
2
2

2

For example, in 2011, short-seller Citron Research published
a short selling report
on the Chinese internet company Qihoo 360. Citron’s report pointed out several red flags for Qihoo 360’s user number. However, Qihoo’s
response
clarifies that Citron’s analysis on their user number is incomplete and misleading.

We then conduct comparative statics to explore the model’s empirical predictions and show how the endogenous nature of the investor’s disclosure interacts with those predictions. We focus on two parameters, the investor’s competence modeled as the probability that the investor receives the additional information, and the firm’s information environment modeled as the probability that the firm’s fundamental is revealed by sources other than the investor. We show that a more competent investor is less likely to withhold information and engage in misleading disclosure, but the price reaction to his publication of a simple report (the initial signal alone without the additional evidence) is weaker after controlling for the report’s content. The last prediction, which may disagree with one’s intuition that market response to a report increases with the investor’s competence, is driven by the investor’s endogenous disclosure. Since a more competent investor is more likely to discover additional evidence, the market, upon observing a simple report from such an investor, ascribes a larger probability that he has withheld the additional evidence and responds with a lower price reaction.

When the firm’s information environment improves, the price reaction to the investor’s simple report is larger. Moreover, in the case where the initial signal is a red flag, the investor discloses more good news but withholds more bad news, and is less likely to engage in misleading disclosure. Information from other sources defeats the investor’s strategic withholding and results in a loss to his short position. Therefore, the firm’s information environment plays a disciplinary role on misleading disclosure. As the investor discloses more good news, the average withheld news becomes worse. As a result, the non-disclosure induces a larger negative price reaction, encouraging the investor to withhold more bad news. Similarly, in the case where the initial signal is a “green” flag, the investor discloses more bad news but withholds more good news, and is less likely to engage in misleading disclosure.

Finally, we consider two extensions of the baseline model to check the robustness of our results and derive more insights. In the first extension, we allow the investor’s signal about the additional information to be noisy. In the second extension, we consider the target firm’s endogenous response to activist short-sellers, an important empirical phenomenon documented by
Brendel and
Ryans 2021
. In both extensions, our main result—that the investor discloses extreme news and withholds moderate news—continues to hold. Perhaps surprisingly, in the second extension, we find that misleading disclosure may not decrease with the firm’s tendency to respond, modeled as the probability that the firm is informed about the additional evidence. In fact, there are two forces at play when the firm is more likely to be informed about the additional evidence. On the one hand, similar to the baseline where information revelation by other sources is exogenous, the firm’s response has a disciplinary effect in misleading disclosure. On the other hand, the market becomes more skeptical about the firm’s lack of response (i.e., non-disclosure of the additional evidence) and forms a lower price upon disclosure. This implies that the price impact of a simple report with only the red flag increases, giving the investor a stronger incentive to engage in misleading disclosure.

We have adopted the verifiable disclosure approach to study investors’ disclosure. Corporate communication in practice ranges from easily verifiable content to potentially fraudulent reports to sometimes even completely unverifiable information. Accordingly, the corporate disclosure literature has used different approaches ranging from verifiable disclosure (e.g.,
Dye 1985

Gao 2013

Stocken 2000

Qian 2016

Ryans 2021

et al. 2022

Laroque 1992

Van Bommel 2003

3.1
Preliminary Analysis

The (informed) investor has two private signals, which complicates the
analysis and the exposition. Thus, we simplify the analysis by first pinning
down the disclosure of the first signal, the initial evidence
r
.

Proposition 1

The investor always discloses the initial evidence
r
,
regardless of its realization.

Proposition
1
shows that the investor always discloses the initial evidence even though the market is uncertain about whether he has received it or not. This unraveling result is different from the partial disclosure result found in
Dye 1985
and
Jung and
Kwon 1988
because the investor in our setting can trade in both directions before the disclosure. The sender (manager) in
Dye 1985
has long exposure to the stock price and benefits only from positive price movement. When the news is negative and its disclosure moves the stock price downward, the manager has incentives to hide such information. In contrast, the trading flexibility of the sender (investor) in our model enables him to profit from any price movement, regardless of its direction. He can make a profit by taking a short (long) position if his report decreases (increases) the price. Thus, the investor tries to move the price as much as possible, regardless of the direction. Anticipating this incentive, if the investor does not disclose any report, the market infers that the investor must be uninformed about
r
, and as a result, the price does not move. Thus, the investor always disclosing
r
is an equilibrium. The proof of the equilibrium’s uniqueness requires that we determine the market’s belief upon the off-equilibrium disclosure of
r
. In the appendix, we apply neologism-proofness as in
Bertomeu and
Cianciaruso 2018
to accomplish the task. Readers can also refer to Lemma
2
in the appendix for the unraveling result in the case of a single signal.

Moreover, when the investor discloses
r
and withholds
x
, the potential revelation of the firm’s value by other information sources will dilute his profit. However, this does not prevent the investor from disclosing
r
, as withholding it would result in zero profit. If the investor expects to incur negative profit from withholding
x
, he can always switch to full disclosure of both
r
and
x
and take positions accordingly, thereby guaranteeing positive profit.

In addition, firms with better information environments tend to have more aggressive negative reports and less aggressive positive reports.

Part 1(b) is perhaps surprising since one might expect that market reaction would increase with competence. However, this intuition may not be true after considering the investor’s endogenous disclosure strategy. More successful hedge funds are more able to produce elaborate reports, which makes their simple reports more suspicious, and thus, the market reaction decreases with competence. Part 1(b) also links the market’s reaction with the firm’s information environment. This relationship would be ambiguous without solving for the investor’s endogenous disclosure strategy.

As a result, the probability of misleading disclosure decreases with the likelihood of information revelation by other sources.

5.2
Target Firm’s Response to Activist Short Selling

Brendel and
Ryans 2021
documents that 31% of the target firms in their sample issue responses to activist short sellers’ reports. In our baseline model, we assume the information revelation by other information sources is exogenous. While this assumption may hold for less biased sources such as media and analysts, the target firm may not voluntarily issue information that worsens the market’s belief. To better study the phenomenon of firms responding to short selling reports, we extend our baseline model to consider the target firm’s strategic response and reexamine the investor’s strategies.

We assume after the investor issues a simple report that contains only a red flag (i.e.,
r<r_{0}
), the target firm decides whether to respond. Specifically, the target firm is informed about the additional evidence
x
with probability
p\in(0,1)
. The informed type can truthfully disclose
x
or stay silent. The uninformed type can only stay silent. The firm’s objective is to maximize the date 3 stock price.
15
15

15

An alternative approach to model the firm’s response is to assume the firm can acquire
x
with probability
1
at a cost
c>0
. In this scenario, the firm will never acquire
x
. This is because, conditional on the investor’s non-disclosure of
x
, the firm’s expectation of
x
is the same as the market’s (i.e.,
\mu_{ND}
). Therefore, from the firm’s perspective, the expected price will remain the same if it acquires and discloses
x
(i.e.,
E[P_{2}|m=(r,\varnothing)]=E[rx|m=(r,\varnothing)]=r\mu_{ND}
). As a result, none of our baseline results changes. For this reason, we choose to model the firm’s response under uncertainty about information endowment (
Dye 1985
).

Note that we assume the target firm can only respond to the short-selling report and cannot make a standalone disclosure without the short-selling report. While this assumption is restrictive, it provides tractability and allows us to focus on the firm’s response to activist short sellers.

The assumption
p\in(0,1)
reflects that target firms do not always respond to activist short sellers. Activist short sellers often conduct extensive research before publishing their reports, and it is not always feasible for target firms to respond immediately. Returning to our example, after the investor issues a simple report pointing out the decline in items per order, the coffee chain may need time to gather data on existing and new customers. Although the company may eventually find out the cause of the decline, it may not be able to do so before the investor closes his position.

The timeline is as follows:

•

At date 1, the investor receives two private signals and establishes
an initial position
\rho
at price
P_{1}=E[\theta]=r_{0}\mu_{0}
.

•

At date 2, the investor makes the disclosure decisions. After the investor issues a simple report with
r<r_{0}
, the informed firm decides whether to disclose
x
.

•

At date 3, the investor closes her initial position at the updated stock
price
P_{2}
.

Conclusions

Our model suggests that misleading disclosure can occur when investors selectively withhold multi-dimensional information. This practice is more defensible legally than spreading unfounded rumors or disseminating fabricated reports, as it is difficult for regulators to prove that investors have deliberately withheld evidence. Therefore, we believe that this practice is more widespread than those involving misinformation and rumors. While the model’s insights primarily apply to investors with direct trading incentives, such as hedge funds, they also apply to those with incentives to indirectly generate trading volume. For instance, considering that sell-side analysts are compensated based on the trading volume generated by their reports (e.g.,
Jackson 2005
and
Karmaziene 2023
), our results suggest that they may also engage in misleading disclosure.

Our study has the following limitations. First, we assume the investor has a short horizon and closes his position after disclosure. If the investor can trade multiple times, he may engage in dynamic disclosure management. For instance, the investor can first disclose the decline in items per order, buy at depressed prices, and then resell after disclosing that the decline in items per order is actually good news. We examine this possibility in the online appendix.

Second, our analysis focuses solely on the price impact of the investor’s disclosure and does not model the price impact of the investor’s trading. Incorporating the investor’s multi-dimensional information into a trading game, such as
Kyle 1985
and
Glosten and
Milgrom 1985
, would render our model intractable. Therefore, we present our analysis, specifically the unraveling result in Proposition
1
, as a benchmark. An interesting avenue for future research is to examine whether and how the investor’s disclosure incentives will be affected by the price impact of trading.

Third, we only model investors’ verifiable disclosure. Future research can examine alternative situations where investors’ disclosure is partially unverifiable. For example, one can consider a scenario similar to
Dutta and
Trueman 2002
, where the initial evidence
r
is hard information that can be credibly disclosed, whereas the additional signal
x
is soft information that cannot be credibly disclosed.
18
18

18

Dutta and
Trueman 2002
considers only disclosures by corporate managers. In our model, if we assume
x
cannot be credibly disclosed, then the equilibrium is straightforward: First, the investor will always disclose
r
for the same reason as Proposition
1
in the baseline. Second, there is no communication of
x
. Future research may introduce other frictions so that the equilibrium may feature credible communication of
x
despite the information itself is unverifiable.

Finally, we do not investigate how investors’ disclosure affects firms’ behavior. For example, whereas activist short-selling may deter firms from engaging in earnings management, it may also disrupt firms’ normal communication using discretionary accruals, because discretionary accruals can be seen as red flags for earnings management. How does investors’ disclosure affect the overall amount of information reflected in stock prices? Can the benefits of activist short-selling be outweighed by the costs? We leave these questions to future research.
