Achieving Tight O ⁡ ( 4 k ) O(4^{k}) Runtime Bounds on Jump k by Proving that Genetic Algorithms Evolve Near-Maximal Population Diversity
Andre Opris, Johannes Lengler, Dirk Sudholt

Abstract
The Jump k \textsc{Jump}_{k} benchmark was the first problem for which crossover was proven to give a speed-up over mutation-only evolutionary algorithms. Jansen and Wegener (2002) proved an upper bound of O ⁡ ( poly ⁡ ( n ) + 4 k / p c ) O(\mathrm{poly}(n)+4^{k}/p_{c}) for the ( μ \mu +1) Genetic Algorithm (( μ \mu +1) GA), but only for unrealistically small crossover probabilities p c p_{c} . To this date, it remains an open problem to prove similar upper bounds for realistic p c p_{c} ; the best known runtime bound , in terms of function evaluations, for p c = Ω ⁡ ( 1 ) p_{c}=\Omega(1) is O ⁡ ( ( n / χ ) k − 1 ) O((n/\chi)^{k-1}) , χ \chi a positive constant. We provide a novel approach and analyse the evolution of the population diversity, measured as sum of pairwise Hamming distances, for a variant of the ( μ \mu +1) GA on Jump k \textsc{Jump}_{k} . The ( μ \mu +1)- λ c {\lambda_{c}} -GA creates one offspring in each generation either by applying mutation to one parent or by applying crossover λ c {\lambda_{c}} times to the same two parents (followed by mutation), to amplify the probability of creating an accepted offspring in generations with crossover. We show that population diversity in the ( μ \mu +1)- λ c {\lambda_{c}} -GA converges to an equilibrium of near-perfect diversity. This yields an improved time bound of O ⁡ ( μ ​ n ​ log ⁡ ( μ ) + 4 k ) O(\mu n\log(\mu)+4^{k}) function evaluations for a range of k k under the mild assumptions p c = O ⁡ ( 1 / k ) p_{c}=O(1/k) and μ ∈ Ω ⁡ ( k ​ n ) \mu\in\Omega(kn) . For all constant k k , the restriction is satisfied for some p c = Ω ⁡ ( 1 ) p_{c}=\Omega(1) and it implies that the expected runtime for all constant k k and an appropriate μ = Θ ⁡ ( k ​ n ) \mu=\Theta(kn) is bounded by O ⁡ ( n 2 ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n^{2}\log n) , irrespective of k k . For larger k k , the expected time of the ( μ \mu +1)- λ c {\lambda_{c}} -GA is Θ ⁡ ( 4 k ) \Theta(4^{k}) , which is tight for a large class of unbiased black-box algorithms and faster than the original ( μ \mu +1) GA by a factor of Ω ⁡ ( 1 / p c ) \Omega(1/p_{c}) . We also show that our analysis can be extended to other unitation functions such as Jump k , δ \textsc{Jump}_{k,\delta} and Hurdle .

Runtime analysis, diversity, population dynamics

Introduction and Motivation

Evolutionary algorithms (EAs) are randomised search heuristics inspired by principles of natural evolution. They evolve a population (a multiset) of search points to explore different parts of the search space in parallel. In one generation, they apply evolutionary operators such as mutation, crossover (also called recombination) and selection to create new search points from the current population, and to create a new population from the most promising search points.

Evolutionary algorithms are known for their ability to generate novel and unexpected solutions—a hallmark of creativity
[
17
]
—and have been described as the “next deep learning”
[
22
]
and the future of creative AI
[
21
]
.
EAs are applicable in a black-box setting where knowledge about the problem in hand is not available or difficult to model. Their population-based approach implies that EAs search the solution space more broadly than many traditional optimisation methods, which increases the likelihood of discovering a global optimum.

A major drawback is that their dynamic behaviour is as little understood as it was almost two decades ago
[
13
]
. It is often not clear when and why EAs perform well, and which components contribute most to their success
[
23
,
14
,
7
]
. Even some of the most fundamental questions are unresolved.
Since the invention of evolutionary algorithms (EAs), researchers have wondered about the usefulness of the crossover operator. For decades, only empirical evidence was available
,
suggesting that using crossover in addition to mutation improves performance.

In a breakthrough result,
Jansen and Wegener [15]
presented the first proof that the use of crossover can speed up the expected optimisation time of a (
\mu
+1) GA, for the

benchmark

1.1
Our contribution

We give massively improved bounds
in the regime of large
p_{c}
. We study a variant of the (
\mu
+1) GA that we call
(
\mu
+1) GA with competing crossover offspring
, denoted as (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA for short, under a mild condition on
p_{c}
. In a generation executing crossover, the (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA creates
{\lambda_{c}}
offspring by crossing the same parents, applying mutation and then continuing the replacement selection with the best such offspring. In generations without crossover, it creates one offspring as usual.
Hence, the (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA remains a steady-state algorithm, creating one new offspring in each generation.
The reasons for creating multiple offspring in generations with crossover are as follows. When the whole population consists of local optima of
\textsc{Jump}_{k}
, which are search points with
n-k
bits set to 1, crossing two local optima with a large Hamming distance is likely to create an individual of worse fitness that would be removed immediately. If the two parents have small Hamming distance then the offspring is more likely to be accepted. Hence, among local optima of
\textsc{Jump}_{k}
, pairs of small Hamming distance are more likely to generate accepted offspring than pairs of large Hamming distance. The (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA evens out this imbalance: by creating several offspring from the same pair of parents, it amplifies the probability of creating at least one acceptable offspring per generation. Our choice of
{\lambda_{c}}
will ensure that all pairs of parents reach roughly the same probability of almost
1
. Hence, the (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA prevents idle generations with crossover.

A second effect of the (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA is that it amplifies the probability of creating the global optimum in a generation executing crossover. This makes sense since, as we will show, the algorithm evolves sufficient diversity such that generations with crossover are much more likely to create the optimum than those using only mutation. Steps without crossover are still needed to evolve and maintain a sufficient diversity. But to achieve this, a single offspring is sufficient.

Related work on crossover for
\textsc{Jump}_{k}

Jansen and Wegener [15]
presented the first rigorous theoretical proof that crossover can speed up optimisation. On the function
\textsc{Jump}_{k}
(formally defined in Section
2
)
,
evolutionary algorithms typically get stuck on
plateau
and then have to cross a fitness valley to “jump” to the optimum
1^{n}
by flipping
the remaining

k
zeros to one. For

k=O(\log n)
,
they proved that a (
\mu
+1) Genetic Algorithm ((
\mu
+1) GA) using crossover optimises the function in expected polynomial time, whereas the (1+1) EA using only standard bit mutation requires expected time
\Theta(n^{k})
.
This holds for the standard (
\mu
+1) GA and a variant that prevents replicates from entering the population in steps where no crossover is executed.
The reason for crossover’s superior performance is that, once the population only contains individuals with
n-k
ones, crossover can combine parents having zeros at different positions to create the global optimum
1^{n}
. In the best case, two parents have zeros at disjoint positions. This is called a
complementary pair
and then the probability of uniform crossover turning all these
2k
positions to 1 is
(1/2)^{2k}=4^{-k}
.

Jansen and Wegener [15]
showed that the (
\mu
+1) GA typically evolves a population in which at any fixed bit position at most
\mu/(2k)
individuals carry a zero. Then
for any fixed parent
x

\mu/(2k)
other search points sharing a zero at this position, and there are at most
k\cdot\mu/(2k)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mu/2}
search points sharing any zero-bit with
x
. Thus,
the probability of selecting a complementary pair as parents is at least
1/2
.
This analysis, however, comes with two limitations. It only works for small
k
,
k=O(\log n)
. More importantly, it requires the crossover probability
p_{c}
to be unrealistically small:
p_{c}=O(1/(kn))
. The reason is that it was unclear how crossover affects the diversity of the population and this was handled using pessimistic
arguments
.
It was left as an open problem to analyse the (
\mu
+1) GA with more realistic crossover probabilities.

\mu

1.3
Outline and Extension

We first
give
an exact formula for the equilibrium of population diversity for the standard (
\mu
+1) EA without crossover in Section
3
. These results are formulated and proven for arbitrary
unbiased
mutation operators
, that is, mutation operators that treat all bit values and all bit positions symmetrically
[
18
]
. In Section
4
we then bound the equilibrium for the (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA.
Runtime bounds are derived from this in Section
5
, including a lower bound showing that the dominant term
4^{k}
is necessary for a large class of unbiased black-box algorithms, and that a stronger lower bound of
\Omega(4^{k}/p_{c})
applies to algorithms creating the same number of offspring in generations with or without crossover. This lends credence to the design of the (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA and justifies why it creates more offspring in generations with crossover.
Related to this, in Section
4
we also give an example of a population for which the drift in terms of population diversity is positive for the (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA, but negative for the original (
\mu
+1) GA (cf. Lemmas
4.10
and
4.13
). This counterexample refutes the conjecture that our drift bounds can be transferred to the original (
\mu
+1) GA. Proofs for these lemmas are placed in an appendix to keep the main part streamlined.
Finally, in Section
6
we show that our analyses can be extended to other functions of unitation, including a generalisation of the
\textsc{Jump}_{k}
function and the
Hurdle
function that requires multiple jumps to reach the global optimum.

This manuscript extends a preliminary conference paper
[
27
]
in several ways. This paper contains full proofs that had to be omitted in
[
27
]
owing to space restrictions. We have added Section
6
on applications to other fitness functions and the mentioned counterexample for the drift in the original (
\mu
+1) GA (Lemmas
4.10
and
4.13
). The analyses in Section
5
have been rewritten to increase rigour and to fix some technical issues in
[
27
]
. In particular, our revised analysis treats rounding issues more rigorously. This avoids problems with pathological parameter settings like excessively large values for
{\lambda_{c}}
or
1/p_{c}
, under which our previous bounds could become incorrect. In the last bound of Theorem 5.2 in
[
27
]
we previously chose
{\lambda_{c}}=O(1/p_{c})
to absorb a factor of
\lceil{\lambda_{c}}p_{c}\rceil
. However, this introduced the limitation
k=\Omega(\log^{2}\mu)
. Here we
use
a different argument to bound
\lceil{\lambda_{c}}p_{c}\rceil
that also works for constant
k
.

Most importantly, we noticed that our bounds could be improved for appropriate parameter settings by replacing the term
O(4^{k}/p_{c})
with
O(4^{k})
. The intuition behind the improved bound is as follows. If
{\lambda_{c}}\geq 1/p_{c}
,
then the (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA, on average, spends at least half of its fitness evaluations in generations with crossover. Since we show that the probability of generating the optimum in a constant fraction of those offspring creations is
\Omega(4^{-k})
, the upper bound only contains an additive term of
O(4^{k})
. This contradicts the lower bound of
\Omega(4^{k}/p_{c})
claimed in Theorem 5.3 of
[
27
]
. Its proof was faulty since it computed the fraction of offspring creations by crossover incorrectly, not taking into account that generations with crossover create several offspring. This is fixed in the present manuscript: we give a general lower bound of
\min\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Omega}(4^{k}),\binom{n}{k}\}
for a large class of black-box algorithms and tighten this to
\Omega(\min\{4^{k}/p_{c},\binom{n}{k}\})
for algorithms that always produce the same number
\lambda
of offspring, regardless of whether crossover is used or not. Together, these lower bounds show that the (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA has an asymptotically optimal expected optimisation time on
\textsc{Jump}_{k}
and that it is provably faster than the original (
\mu
+1) GA by a factor of at least
\Omega(1/p_{c})
, for all
k=O(\sqrt{n})
.

H_{r}(x,y)=0
otherwise.

The fitness function
\textsc{Jump}_{k}
is defined as

\textsc{Jump}_{k}(x):=\begin{cases}\left|x\right|_{1}+k&\text{if }\left|x\right|_{1}=n\text{ or }\left|x\right|_{1}\leq n-k,\\
n-\left|x\right|_{1}&\text{otherwise}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}.}\end{cases}

By

.
We define
\textsc{Jump}_{k}^{\prime}

\textsc{Jump}_{k}^{\prime}(x)=\textsc{Jump}(x)

{x\neq 1^{n}}
and
\textsc{Jump}_{k}^{\prime}(x)=0

Note that every

2.3
Diversity Measure

In this paper we use the sum of Hamming distances as a diversity measure which is defined in the following
.

Definition 2.2
.

For a population
P_{t}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{\mu}\}
and a search point
y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
we define

S_{P_{t}}(y)\coloneqq\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{\mu}H(x_{i},y)\quad\text{and}\quad S(P_{t})\coloneqq\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{\mu}\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{\mu}H(x_{i},x_{j}),

and for
r\in[n]
we write
S_{r}(P_{t}):=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{\mu}\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{\mu}H_{r}(x_{i},x_{j})
, so that
S(P_{t})=\sum\nolimits_{r=1}^{n}S_{r}(P_{t})
.

The population diversity can be computed by considering each bit position individually and counting how many population members have a zero or a one, respectively.

Lemma 2.3

(Theorem 1a in
[
34
]
)
.

Let
P_{t}
be a population of size
\mu
and
m_{i}
denote the number of members of
P_{t}
having a zero at position
i
, then
S(P_{t})=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}2m_{i}(\mu-m_{i})
.

Note the symmetry of zeros and ones in the expression
m_{i}(\mu-m_{i})
. The factor 2 accounts for the fact that every pair
(x_{i},x_{j})
with
i\neq j
is counted twice in
\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{\mu}\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{\mu}H(x_{i},x_{j})
. According to
[
19
]
,
S(P_{t})
is twice the sum of all Hamming distances of each pair
x_{i},x_{j}
with
i\neq j
, the average value of
S_{P_{t}}(y)

over

y\in P_{t}
is
S(P_{t})/\mu
and the average Hamming distance of two random points in
P_{t}
is
S(P_{t})/\mu^{2}
. The latter implies that if
S(P_{t})>a\mu^{2}
for
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
then
there is at least
one
pair
(x_{1},x_{2})
of individuals with
H(x_{1},x_{2})>a
. Note that the average Hamming distance of two
different
random
search points is
S(P_{t})/(\mu^{2}-\mu)
.
For populations
P_{t}\subset\textsc{plateau}
, the maximum Hamming distance between two different search points is
2k
and thus
by summing
H(x_{i},x_{j})
over all search points we obtain

{S(P_{t})\leq 2k\mu(\mu-1)}

since there are
\mu^{2}
summands in total and the
\mu
summands of the form
H(x_{i},x_{i})
for
i\in[\mu]
are zero
. More precisely, the maximum diversity is obtained if the zeros in the population are spread across bit positions as evenly as possible,
i.e.
if every bit position has a zero in
\lfloor k\mu/n\rfloor
or
\lceil k\mu/n\rceil
individuals.

3
Equilibrium State for
S(P_{t})
on
Plateau
for the (
\mu
+1) EA

Now we will compute the expected change of
S(P_{t})
, i.e.,
\mathrm{E}(S(P_{t+1})\mid S(P_{t}))
if all individuals from
S(P_{t})
are in
plateau
. To this end
,
we investigate the population dynamics of evolutionary algorithms on the modification
\textsc{Jump}_{k}^{\prime}
of the
\textsc{Jump}_{k}

benchmark
where the global optimum is removed.

We break the process down into several steps.
Even though our main goal is to analyse the (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA with standard bit mutation as described in Algorithm
2
, we make an effort to keep structural results as general as possible as they might be of independent interest. In particular, we start by considering arbitrary mutation operators, then focus on unbiased mutation and finally arrive at standard bit mutation. For the same reason, we formulate many statements for
q=\min\{k,n-k\}
, which considers jumps of
size

k>n/2
, even though our runtime bounds are limited to
k=o(n)
, that is,
q=k
.

We start with a lemma which describes, for given
x,y\in

plateau
, the effect of flipping
\ell
random
zero
-bits and
\ell
random
one
-bits of
y
on the distance from
x
. Note that this yields an offspring in
plateau
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that
x,y\in\textsc{plateau}
(i.e.
\left|x\right|_{1}=\left|y\right|_{1}=n-k
) and let
z
be generated from
y
by flipping
\ell
ones and
\ell
zeros chosen uniformly at random for
\ell\in\{0,\dots,q\}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(H(x,z))

\displaystyle=2\ell+\big(1-\tfrac{\ell n}{k(n-k)}\big)H(x,y).

Proof.

We write
R:=\{i\in[n]\mid y_{i}=0\}
and
R^{c}:=[n]\setminus R
. Note that
|R|=k
and
R^{c}=n-k
since
y
is in
plateau
. Moreover, for any
x_{1},x_{2}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
we write
H_{R}(x_{1},x_{2})
for the number of bits
in
R
in which
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
differ, and similarly for
R^{c}
. In particular,
H(x_{1},x_{2})=H_{R}(x_{1},x_{2})+H_{R^{c}}(x_{1},x_{2})
. Moreover, since
x
and
y
are both in
plateau
, we have
H_{R}(x,y)=H_{R^{c}}(x,y)=H(x,y)/2
.

Consider
z_{0},z_{1}
where

•

z_{0}

is
generated from
y
by flipping
\ell
zeros chosen uniformly at random,
and

•

z_{1}

is
generated from
y
by flipping
\ell
ones chosen uniformly at random.

Note that
\mathrm{E}(H(x,z_{0}))=\mathrm{E}(H_{R}(x,z))+H_{R^{c}}(x,y)
since
z_{0}
is obtained from
y
by only flipping zeros and the set
R^{c}
is untouched by the mutation. Similarly we see
\mathrm{E}(H(x,z_{1}))=\mathrm{E}(H_{R^{c}}(x,z))+H_{R}(x,y)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(H(x,z))=\mathrm{E}(H_{R}(x,z))+\mathrm{E}(H_{R^{c}}(x,z))=\mathrm{E}(H(x,z_{0}))+\mathrm{E}(H(x,z_{1}))-H(x,y).

Hence, it is enough to show

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(H(x,z_{0}))

\displaystyle=\ell+\left(1-\frac{\ell}{k}\right)H(x,y),

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(H(x,z_{1}))

\displaystyle=\ell+\left(1-\frac{\ell}{n-k}\right)H(x,y).

We start with
z_{0}
. For the
k
positions in
R
, each of them is flipped with probability
\ell/k
. There are
H_{R}(x,y)
bits in
R
in which
x
and
y
differ, and for any such bit the Hamming distance decreases by one if the bit is flipped. Likewise, there are
|R|-H_{R}(x,y)
bits in
R
in which
x
and
y
coincide, and for any such bit the Hamming distance increases by one if the bit is flipped. Combining these two cases yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(H_{R}(x,z_{0}))

\displaystyle=H_{R}(x,y)+\frac{\ell}{k}\cdot\left(-H_{R}(x,y)+(|R|-H_{R}(x,y))\right)

\displaystyle=H_{R}(x,y)+\frac{\ell}{k}\cdot\left(|R|-2H_{R}(x,y)\right).

Plugging in
H_{R}(x,y)=H(x,y)/2
and
|R|=k
yields after simplifying

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(H_{R}(x,z_{0}))

\displaystyle=\ell+\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\ell}{k}\right)H(x,y),

(3)

and hence

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(H(x,z_{0}))

\displaystyle=H_{R^{c}}(x,z_{0})+\mathrm{E}(H_{R}(x,z_{0}))

\displaystyle=\frac{H(x,y)}{2}+\ell+\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\ell}{k}\right)H(x,y)

\displaystyle=\ell+\left(1-\frac{\ell}{k}\right)H(x,y).

For
z_{1}
, the roles of
R
and
R^{c}
are reversed, with the notable difference that the probability of flipping each bit in
R^{c}
is now
\ell/|R^{c}|=\ell/(n-k)
. The formula

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(H_{R^{c}}(x,z_{1}))

\displaystyle=\ell+\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\ell}{n-k}\right)H(x,y)

(4)

and the final result for
\mathrm{E}(H(x,z_{1}))
follows analogously.
∎

For further steps we need the following lemma from
[
19
]
to describe the expected change of
S(P_{t})
for a fixed offspring
y^{\prime}
. This result works on all fitness functions.

Lemma 3.2

(
[
19
]
, Lemma 6)
.

Consider a population
P_{t}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{\mu}\}
and a search point
y^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. Let
P_{t+1}\coloneq(P_{t}\cup\{y^{\prime}\})\setminus\{x_{d}\}
for a uniform random
d\in[\mu]
. Then

\mathrm{E}(S(P_{t+1})\mid S(P_{t}),y^{\prime})=\left(1-\tfrac{2}{\mu}\right)S(P_{t})+\tfrac{2(\mu-1)}{\mu}S_{P_{t}}(y^{\prime}).

Now we combine Lemma
3.1
with Lemma
3.2
to describe the expected change of
S(P_{t})
if
y^{\prime}
arises from another
y\in P_{t}
by a mutation operator which flips
\ell
ones and zeros chosen uniformly at random and every
x\in P_{t}
is in
plateau
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
P_{t}\subset\textsc{plateau}
be a population of size
\mu
. Let
y\in P_{t}

be fixed
and suppose that
y^{\prime}
results from
y
by a mutation operator which flips
\ell
ones and
\ell
zeros chosen uniformly at random. Let
P_{t+1}=(P_{t}\cup\{y^{\prime}\})\setminus\{x_{d}\}
for a uniform random
d\in[\mu]
. Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(S(P_{t+1})\mid S(P_{t}))=\left(1-\tfrac{2}{\mu}\right)S(P_{t})+4\ell(\mu-1)+\tfrac{2(\mu-1)}{\mu}\Big(1-\tfrac{\ell n}{k(n-k)}\Big)S_{P_{t}}(y).

Proof.

For
z\in P_{t}
we have
S(z):=S_{P_{t}}(z)=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{\mu}H(x_{i},z)
. By Lemma
3.2
and the law of total probability we obtain

\mathrm{E}(S(P_{t+1})\mid S(P_{t}))=\left(1-\frac{2}{\mu}\right)S(P_{t})+\frac{2(\mu-1)}{\mu}\mathrm{E}(S(y^{\prime})).

(5)

By Lemma
3.1
we obtain for all
i\in[\mu]

\mathrm{E}(H(x_{i},y^{\prime}))=2\ell+\left(1-\frac{\ell n}{k(n-k)}\right)H(x_{i},y).

Using linearity of expectation,

\mathrm{E}(S(y^{\prime}))=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{\mu}\mathrm{E}(H(x_{i},y^{\prime}))=2\ell\mu+\left(1-\frac{\ell n}{k(n-k)}\right)S(y)

and plugging this into (
5
) yields the claim.
∎

Remarkably, the right-hand side
only depends on
S_{P_{t}}(y)
, not on
y
itself. This means that all
y
with the same value of
S_{P_{t}}(y)
yield the same dynamics. The following corollary describes how the diversity evolves in the case of a
(\mu+1)
EA with a mutation operator which flips
\ell
ones and zeros chosen uniformly at random. This result transfers straightforward
ly
to unbiased mutation operators if one knows the probability
p_{\ell}
to flip
\ell
ones and
\ell
zeros.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
\ell\in\{0,\ldots,q\}
(where
q=\min(k,n-k)
).
Consider
the

(\mu+1)
EA (see Algorithm
1
) on
\textsc{Jump}_{k}
with a mutation operator which flips
\ell
ones and
\ell
zeros chosen uniformly at random. Suppose its current population is
P_{t}\subset\textsc{plateau}
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(S(P_{t+1})\mid S(P_{t}))=\left(1-\frac{2}{\mu^{2}}-\frac{2(\mu-1)\ell n}{\mu^{2}k(n-k)}\right)S(P_{t})+4\ell(\mu-1).

Proof.

By
Lemma
3.3
and
the law of total probability we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(S(P_{t+1})\mid S(P_{t}))

\displaystyle=\left(1-\tfrac{2}{\mu}\right)S(P_{t})+4\ell(\mu-1)+\tfrac{2(\mu-1)}{\mu}\Big(1-\tfrac{\ell n}{k(n-k)}\Big)\mathrm{E}(S(y))

where
y
denotes the parent chosen uniformly at random. With

\mathrm{E}(S(y))=\frac{1}{\mu}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{\mu}\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{\mu}H(x_{i},x_{j})=S(P_{t})/\mu

we obtain the result.
∎

Now we extend our analysis to arbitrary unbiased mutation operators.

Corollary 3.5
.

Consider a
(\mu+1)
EA (see Algorithm
1
) on
\textsc{Jump}_{k}^{\prime}
with a current population
P_{t}\subset\textsc{plateau}
and an unbiased mutation operator
\mathrm{mut}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(S(P_{t+1})\mid S(P_{t}))

\displaystyle=S(P_{t})-\sum\nolimits_{\ell=0}^{q}p_{\ell}\left(\frac{2}{\mu^{2}}+\frac{2(\mu-1)\ell n}{\mu^{2}k(n-k)}\right)S(P_{t})+4(\mu-1)\sum\nolimits_{\ell=0}^{q}\ell p_{\ell}

where
p_{\ell}
denotes the probability that
\mathrm{mut}
flips
\ell
ones and
\ell
zeros where
\ell\in\{0,\ldots,q\}
.

Proof.

For
\ell\in\{0,\ldots,q\}
denote by
A_{\ell}
the event that
\ell
zeros and
\ell
ones are flipped. Since
\mathrm{mut}
is unbiased, it flips
\ell
ones and
\ell
zeros chosen uniformly at random if
A_{\ell}
occurs. Since every individual
x\in P_{t}
is in
plateau
(i.e. has
k
zeros and
n-k
ones), we see that the outcome
y
is in
plateau
if and only if one of the events
A_{0},A_{1},\ldots,A_{q}
occurs. Let
B
be the event that
y
has a worse fitness than every
x\in P_{t}
, i.e.
y
is not in
plateau
. Then
\mathrm{Pr}(B)+\sum\nolimits_{\ell=0}^{q}p_{\ell}=1
and by the law of total probability

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{E}(S(P_{t+1})\mid S(P_{t}))=}\;

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{E}(S(P_{t+1})\mid S(P_{t}),B)\cdot\mathrm{Pr}(B\mid S(P_{t}))}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}+\sum\nolimits_{\ell=0}^{q}\mathrm{E}(S(P_{t+1})\mid S(P_{t}),A_{\ell})\cdot p_{\ell}.}

If
B
occurs,
S(P_{t})
does not change, i.e.
\mathrm{E}(S(P_{t+1})\mid S(P_{t}),B)=S(P_{t})
. Since

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(S(P_{t+1})\mid S(P_{t}),A_{\ell})=\left(1-\frac{2}{\mu^{2}}-\frac{2(\mu-1)\ell n}{\mu^{2}k(n-k)}\right)S(P_{t})+4\ell(\mu-1)

by Corollary
3.4
, we obtain the result.
∎

As for flat fitness landscapes in
[
19
]
(i.e. the fitness function is constant over the whole search space), Corollary
3.5
shows that there is an equilibrium state in
plateau
. We define

\displaystyle\beta:=4(\mu-1)\sum\nolimits_{\ell=0}^{q}\ell p_{\ell}\text{ \ and \ }\gamma:=\sum\nolimits_{\ell=0}^{q}p_{\ell}\left(\frac{2}{\mu^{2}}+\frac{2(\mu-1)\ell n}{\mu^{2}k(n-k)}\right).

(6)

Then
, for all populations
P_{t}\subset\textsc{plateau}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(S(P_{t+1})\mid S(P_{t}))=(1-\gamma)S(P_{t})+\beta.

(7)

As in
[
19
]
this condition is a negative multiplicative drift with an additive disturbance (this terminology stems from
[
6
]
). An equilibrium state for
S(P_{t})
with zero drift is attained for

\displaystyle S_{0}:=\frac{\beta}{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\frac{4(\mu-1)\sum\nolimits_{\ell=0}^{q}\ell p_{\ell}}{\frac{2}{\mu^{2}}\left(\sum\nolimits_{\ell=0}^{q}p_{\ell}\right)+\frac{2(\mu-1)n}{\mu^{2}k(n-k)}\sum\nolimits_{\ell=0}^{q}\ell p_{\ell}}=\frac{2(\mu-1)\mu^{2}}{\frac{\sum\nolimits_{\ell=0}^{q}p_{\ell}}{\sum\nolimits_{\ell=0}^{q}\ell p_{\ell}}+\frac{(\mu-1)n}{k(n-k)}}

(8)

if
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{q})\neq 0
and
S_{0}=S(P_{0})
otherwise.
The major difference to
[
19
]
is that this equilibrium depends not only on the number of bits flipped in expectation, but also on the probabilities
p_{\ell}
to flip exactly
\ell
ones and
\ell
zeros. For
bitwise mutation with mutation
probability
\chi/n
where
0\leq\chi\leq n
.

\displaystyle p_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\binom{k}{\ell}\cdot\binom{n-k}{\ell}\cdot\left(\frac{\chi}{n}\right)^{2\ell}\cdot\left(1-\frac{\chi}{n}\right)^{n-2\ell}.

(9)

The following lemma gives the equilibrium state for standard bit mutation with mutation probability
\chi/n
if
\chi\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}O}(1)

and
\chi>0
. Recall that
q=\min(k,n-k)
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Consider standard bit mutation with a mutation probability
\chi/n
with
\chi\in O(1)
. Then
\sum\nolimits_{\ell=0}^{q}p_{\ell}=e^{-\chi}(1+\nu_{1})
and
\sum\nolimits_{\ell=0}^{q}\ell p_{\ell}=k(n-k)(\chi/n)^{2}\cdot e^{-\chi}(1+\nu_{2})
for
|\nu_{1}|,|\nu_{2}|\in O(q/n)
.
Therefore, if
\chi>0
,

\frac{\sum\nolimits_{\ell=0}^{q}p_{\ell}}{\sum\nolimits_{\ell=0}^{q}\ell p_{\ell}}=\frac{n^{2}}{k\chi^{2}(n-k)}(1+\xi)

for
|\xi|\in O(q/n)
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{\ell=0}^{q}\ell p_{\ell}=\frac{\chi^{2}}{n^{2}}\Big(1-\frac{\chi}{n}\Big)^{n-2}\sum\nolimits_{\ell=1}^{q}\Big(\ell\binom{k}{\ell}\binom{n-k}{\ell}\cdot\Big(\frac{\chi}{n-\chi}\Big)^{2\ell-2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{k(n-k)\chi^{2}}{n^{2}}\Big(1-\frac{\chi}{n}\Big)^{n-2}\sum\nolimits_{\ell=1}^{q}\Big(\frac{\chi^{2\ell-2}\prod_{j=1}^{\ell-1}(k-j)(n-k-j)}{(n-\chi)^{2\ell-2}(\ell-1)!\ell!}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{k(n-k)\chi^{2}}{n^{2}}\Big(1-\frac{\chi}{n}\Big)^{n-2}\Big(1+\frac{q}{n}\sum\nolimits_{\ell=2}^{q}\frac{n\chi^{2\ell-2}\prod_{j=1}^{\ell-1}(k-j)(n-k-j)}{q(n-\chi)^{2\ell-2}(\ell-1)!\ell!}\Big).

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n^{2}q(k-1)^{\ell-2}(n-k-1)^{\ell-2}}{q(n-\chi)^{2\ell-2}}\leq\Big(\frac{n}{n-\chi}\Big)^{2\ell-2}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\;\leq\;}\Big(1+\frac{\chi}{n-\chi}\Big)^{2\ell}\leq e^{\frac{\chi}{n-\chi}\cdot 2\ell}\leq e^{\frac{\chi}{n-\chi}\cdot 2k}\in O(1).

We have

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{\ell=0}^{q}p_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\chi}{n}\right)^{n}\sum\nolimits_{\ell=0}^{q}\binom{k}{\ell}\cdot\binom{n-k}{\ell}\cdot\left(\frac{\chi}{n-\chi}\right)^{2\ell}

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{\chi}{n}\Big)^{n}\sum\nolimits_{\ell=0}^{q}\Big(\frac{\chi^{2\ell}\prod_{j=0}^{\ell-1}(k-j)(n-k-j)}{(n-\chi)^{2\ell}\ell!\ell!}\Big)

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{\chi}{n}\Big)^{n}\left(1+\frac{q}{n}\sum\nolimits_{\ell=1}^{q}\Big(\frac{n\chi^{2\ell}\prod_{j=0}^{\ell-1}(k-j)(n-k-j)}{q(n-\chi)^{2\ell}\ell!\ell!}\Big)\right).

\displaystyle\frac{n\prod_{j=0}^{\ell-1}(k-j)(n-k-j)}{q(n-\chi)^{2\ell}}

\displaystyle=\frac{nq(n-q)\prod_{j=1}^{\ell-1}(k-j)(n-k-j)}{q(n-\chi)^{2\ell}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n^{2\ell}}{(n-\chi)^{2\ell}}\leq e^{\frac{\chi}{n-\chi}\cdot 2k}\in O(1).\qed

and flipping

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(S(P_{t+1})\mid y,B_{i+\ell,i})

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{E}(S(Q_{t})\mid y)-2(i+\ell)(\mu-1)+2i\left(\mu-1-\frac{2k\mu}{n-2k}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}(S(Q_{t})\mid y)-2\ell(\mu-1)-\frac{4ki\mu}{n-2k},

and similar as above

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(S(P_{t+1})\mid A_{-\ell},B_{i-\ell,i})

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{E}(S(Q_{t})\mid A_{-\ell})-2\ell(\mu-1)-\frac{4ki\mu}{n-2k}

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{E}(S(Q^{b}_{t}))-4\ell(\mu-1)-\frac{4ki\mu}{n-2k}.

The statement

\blacksquare

The proof of Theorem

by

. Denote

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(S(P_{t+1})\mid V)\mathrm{Pr}(V)

\displaystyle+\sum\nolimits_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum\nolimits_{i=0}^{k}\mathrm{E}(S(P_{t+1})\mid A_{-\ell},B_{i+\ell,i})\rho_{-\ell}q_{i+\ell,i,-\ell}

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{E}(S(Q^{b}_{t}))\mathrm{Pr}(V)-T_{1}-T_{2},

(17)

where

\displaystyle T_{2}:=\sum\nolimits_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum\nolimits_{i=0}^{k}\left(4\ell\mu+\frac{4ki\mu}{n-2k}\right)\rho_{-\ell}q_{i+\ell,i,-\ell}.

Claim 4.7

We have that

Proof of Claim.

To bound

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4k\mu}{n-2k}\left(\sum\nolimits_{\ell=0}^{k}\ell q_{0,\ell,\ell}+\sum\nolimits_{\ell=0}^{k}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{k-\ell}(i+\ell)q_{i,\ell+i,\ell}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4k\mu}{n-2k}\left(\sum\nolimits_{\ell=0}^{k}\ell q_{0,\ell,\ell}+\sum\nolimits_{\ell=0}^{k}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{k}(i+\ell)q_{0,\ell,\ell}O(k^{i}/n^{i})\right)

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\leq}\frac{4k\mu}{n-2k}\left(\chi+\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{k}(i+\chi)O(k^{i}/n^{i})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{4k\mu\chi}{n-2k}\left(1+\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{k}(1+i/\chi)O(k^{i}/n^{i})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{4k\mu\chi}{n}(1+O(k/n)).

Note also that

which implies

and
\sum\nolimits_{\ell\geq 0}\ell q_{\ell,0,-\ell}\leq k\chi/n

and thus again with Inequality (

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum\nolimits_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum\nolimits_{i=0}^{k}\left(4\ell\mu+\frac{4ki\mu}{n-2k}\right)q_{i+\ell,i,-\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\sum\nolimits_{\ell=1}^{k}4\ell\mu q_{\ell,0,-\ell}+\sum\nolimits_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{k}\left(4\ell\mu+\frac{4ki\mu}{n-2k}\right)q_{i+\ell,i,-\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4k\chi\mu}{n}+\sum\nolimits_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{k}\left(4\ell\mu+\frac{4ki\mu}{n-2k}\right)q_{\ell,0,-\ell}O(k^{i}/n^{i})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4k\chi\mu}{n}+\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{4k\chi\mu}{n}+\frac{4ki\mu}{n-2k}\right)O(k^{i}/n^{i})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4k\chi\mu}{n}\left(1+\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{k}\left(1+\frac{i}{\chi(1-2k/n)}\right)O(k^{i}/n^{i})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4k\mu\chi}{n}(1+O(k/n))

since the latter sum converges owing to

.

\blacksquare

4.3
Maintaining Diversity Close to the Maximum

We now provide a lemma which shows that if
\alpha/\delta
is close to
2k\mu^{2}
then after some short initial time
\tau_{0}
it is likely
that
during the following
T
generations the population diversity is also close to
2k\mu^{2}
for at least
T/4
generations. Here
T
is an integer that can be chosen arbitrarily. This lemma presents a novel approach, compared to previous work.
Dang et al. [4]
showed that population diversity (measured with respect to the size of the largest species, that is, the largest number of copies of the same genotype in the population) is built up within a fixed period of time (
O(\mu n+\mu^{2}\log\mu)
generations) and then remains high for another period of time (
\Theta(\mu^{2})
generations) with good probability.
Doerr et al. [10]
showed that diversity is maintained for a period of time that is exponential in
\mu
. Our lemma offers a different approach: It shows that with good probability a constant fraction of generations has good diversity, during
any
desired period of time
T
.
Remarkably, this powerful statement is shown even though we only have statements on the
expected
population diversity.

Lemma 4.14
.

In Situation
4.8
,
let
\alpha
and
\delta
as in (
21
) and (
22
), respectively. Suppose that
\alpha/\delta<2k\mu^{2}
and let
\varepsilon\geq 1-\frac{\alpha}{2k\mu^{2}\delta}>0
,
or equivalently

\displaystyle\alpha/\delta\geq(1-\varepsilon)2k\mu^{2}.

(31)

Let
\tau_{0}:=\lceil\ln(1/\varepsilon)/\delta\rceil
, let
T\in\mathds{N}
, and let
\mathcal{E}_{T}
be the event that
|\{\tau\in[T]:\ S(P_{t+\tau_{0}+\tau})\geq(1-4\varepsilon)2k\mu^{2}\}|\geq T/4
. Then
\mathrm{Pr}(\mathcal{E}_{T})\geq 1/3
.

Proof.

We start with the following lemma where we show that
\mathrm{E}(S(P_{t}))
converges quickly to
\alpha/\delta
. Note that this lemma only examines the expectation
\mathrm{E}(S(P_{t}))
. In the subsequent proof, we will then deduce that similar statements also hold for the random variable
S(P_{t})
.

Lemma 4.15
.

In Situation
4.8
, let
\alpha
and
\delta
be as in (
21
) and (
22
) respectively, and suppose that its current population is
P_{t}\subset\textsc{plateau}
. Then for every
\tau\in\mathds{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(S(P_{t+\tau})\mid S(P_{t}))\geq(1-e^{-\delta\tau})\alpha/\delta.

(32)

Proof.

Using (
23
) from Corollary
4.11
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{\delta}-\mathrm{E}(S(P_{t+1})\mid S(P_{t}))\leq\frac{\alpha}{\delta}-(1-\delta)S(P_{t})-\alpha=(1-\delta)\left(\frac{\alpha}{\delta}-S(P_{t})\right).

Hence, the random variable
Y_{t}:=\alpha/\delta-S(P_{t})
satisfies the recursion
\mathrm{E}(Y_{t+1}\mid Y_{t})\leq(1-\delta)Y_{t}
. Iterating this recursion
\tau
times and using the law of total expectation, we obtain
\mathrm{E}(Y_{t+\tau}\mid Y_{t})\leq(1-\delta)^{\tau}Y_{t}
. Written out and using
S(P_{t})\geq 0
, this is

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{\delta}-\mathrm{E}(S(P_{t+\tau})\mid S(P_{t}))\leq(1-\delta)^{\tau}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\delta}-S(P_{t})\right)\leq(1-\delta)^{\tau}\frac{\alpha}{\delta}\leq e^{-\delta\tau}\frac{\alpha}{\delta}.

This is equivalent to (
32
) and proves the lemma.
∎

Continuing the proof of Lemma
4.14
, fix
\tau\in\mathds{N}
. We first use Lemma
4.15
for
\tau_{0}+\tau\geq\tau_{0}
and find that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{E}(S(P_{t+\tau_{0}+\tau})\mid S(P_{t}))&\geq(1-e^{-\delta(\tau_{0}+\tau)})\frac{\alpha}{\delta}\geq(1-e^{-\delta\tau_{0}})\frac{\alpha}{\delta}\\
&\geq(1-\varepsilon)\frac{\alpha}{\delta}\geq(1-\varepsilon)^{2}2k\mu^{2}\geq(1-2\varepsilon)2k\mu^{2}.\end{split}

(33)

Let us write
\rho:=\Pr(S(P_{t+\tau_{0}+\tau})<(1-4\varepsilon)2k\mu^{2})
.
Any two individuals in
P_{t+\tau_{0}+\tau}
have Hamming distance
k
from the optimum, so they have Hamming distance at most
2k
from each other.
Since
S(P_{t+\tau_{0}+\tau})
is defined as the sum over
\mu^{2}
such Hamming distances, we have
S(P_{t+\tau_{0}+\tau})\leq 2k\mu^{2}
.
Therefore, we may bound

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(S(P_{t+\tau_{0}+\tau})\mid S(P_{t}))\leq\rho\cdot(1-4\varepsilon)2k\mu^{2}+(1-\rho)\cdot 2k\mu^{2}=2k\mu^{2}-4\varepsilon\cdot 2k\mu^{2}\cdot\rho.

This implies

\displaystyle\rho\leq\frac{2k\mu^{2}-\mathrm{E}(S(P_{t+\tau_{0}+\tau})\mid S(P_{t}))}{4\varepsilon\cdot 2k\mu^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:SPt-is-large-1}}}{{\leq}}\frac{2\varepsilon\cdot 2k\mu^{2}}{4\varepsilon\cdot 2k\mu^{2}}=1/2.

(34)

Now consider the random variable

N_{T}:=|\{\tau\in[T]:\ S(P_{t+\tau_{0}+\tau})<(1-4\varepsilon)2k\mu^{2}\}|.

By definition of
\rho
and by (
34
), it has expectation
\mathrm{E}(N_{T})\leq T/2
. By Markov’s inequality, we obtain
\mathrm{Pr}(N_{T}>3T/4)\leq(T/2)/(3T/4)=2/3
. On the other hand, the event
\mathcal{E}_{T}
from the lemma is the complement of the event “
N_{T}>3T/4
”, and hence
\mathrm{Pr}(\mathcal{E}_{T})=1-\mathrm{Pr}(N_{T}>3T/4)\geq 1/3
. This concludes the proof.
∎

5
Runtime Bounds for the (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA on
Jump

Now we exploit the lower bounds on population diversity to derive the desired runtime bounds of the (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA on
\textsc{{Jump} }_{k}
. We use the fact that the population diversity approaches an equilibrium state to conclude that, in most generations, the population contains a good number of potential parents with a Hamming distance close to the equilibrium. In a generation with crossover, such good parents have a much improved chance to create the optimum via crossover and mutation, compared to using only mutation.

This section is structured as follows. In Section
5.1
, we first prove an upper bound on the expected time for the entire population to reach the plateau (Theorem
5.1
). In Section
5.2
, we show that our diversity estimates imply that, while the population evolves on the plateau, it typically contains many pairs of diverse parents (Lemma
5.2
). We use this to derive general upper runtime bounds under loose conditions on
p_{c}
and
k
(Theorem
5.3
), followed by stronger and simpler runtime bounds under tighter conditions on
p_{c}
and
k
(Theorem
5.4
). Finally, Section
5.3
provides matching lower bounds for classes of black-box algorithms (Theorem
5.5
).

5.1
Bounding the Expected Time to Reach the Plateau

We first give an upper bound on the expected time for the whole population of the (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA to reach
plateau
. It is similar to
Lemma 2.1 in
[
4
]
, but it includes evaluations for
{\lambda_{c}}
competing crossover offspring and allows arbitrary unbiased mutation operators.

Theorem 5.1
.

Consider the (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA applying uniform crossover with crossover probability
p_{c}
and an unbiased mutation operator flipping no bits with probability
f_{0}
and flipping a single bit with probability
f_{1}
on
\textsc{Jump}_{k}
. For all parameters
0\leq k\leq n
,
p_{c}<1
,
f_{0}>0
,
f_{1}>0
,
\mu,{\lambda_{c}}\in\mathds{N}
and every initial population, the expected time for reaching the global optimum or creating a population on
plateau
is

O\left(\left(1+\frac{{\lambda_{c}}p_{c}}{1-p_{c}}\right){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{1}{1-p_{c}}}\left(\mu n\left(\frac{1}{f_{0}}+\frac{1}{f_{1}}\right)+\frac{n\log n}{f_{1}}+\frac{\mu\log\mu}{f_{0}}\right)\right){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}.}

For standard bit mutation with mutation probability
\Theta(1/n)
and
0<p_{c}\leq 1-\Omega(1)
this simplifies to
O(\lceil{\lambda_{c}}p_{c}\rceil(\mu n+n\log(n)+\mu\log\mu))
.
Both bounds also apply to the (
\mu
+1) GA when setting
{\lambda_{c}}\coloneqq 1
.

Proof.

The proof is a generalisation of Witt’s analysis of the (
\mu
+1) EA on
OneMax

[
35
]
.

We first focus only on mutation-only steps, that is, generations that do not apply crossover, and later analyse the number of function evaluations performed in generations with crossover. More specifically, we will estimate the expected time to improve the best-so-far fitness and the expected time to generate a sufficient number of individuals with the current best fitness. Owing to elitism, generations applying crossover cannot decrease the best-so-far fitness, nor can they reduce the number of search points that have a fitness of at least
i
, for any fixed value
i
. We denote by
M
the random number of mutation-only steps performed until the entire population reaches the plateau, and bound
\mathrm{E}(M)
from above without taking advantage of generations with crossover.

Let
T_{i}
denote the number of generations in which the best individual has a fitness of
i
and
only mutation is performed. Let

s_{i}
denote the probability of mutation creating an offspring of fitness larger than
i
from a parent with fitness
i
.
Let
j\in\{1,\dots,\mu\}
. We first bound the expected
number of mutation steps
until the population contains at least
j
individuals of fitness
i
, or until it contains an individual of fitness larger than
i
. Note that, if an individual of fitness
i
is cloned, an individual with smaller fitness is removed from the population. If there are
\ell
individuals of fitness
i
, a sufficient condition for the former event is not to execute crossover, to select one of
\ell
fitness-
i
individuals as parent and to not flip any bits. As these events are independent, the probability of these events is
(1-p_{c})\cdot{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(\ell/\mu)}\cdot f_{0}
. The expected waiting time for increasing the number of individuals with fitness at least
i
is thus at most
\mu/((1-p_{c})\ell f_{0})
. Summing up all times for
\ell\in\{1,\dots,j-1\}
yields an upper time bound of

\frac{\mu}{(1-p_{c})f_{0}}\cdot H(j-1)\leq\frac{\mu}{(1-p_{c})f_{0}}\cdot(\ln(j)+1)

(35)

where
H(j):=\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}1/\ell
. Once the population contains at least
j
individuals of fitness
i
, the probability of creating an offspring with fitness larger than
i
is at least
(1-p_{c})\cdot{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(j/\mu)}\cdot s_{i}
. The expected waiting time for this event is at most
\mu/(js_{i}(1-p_{c}))
. Together, we obtain the following family of upper bounds for all values of
j\in\{1,\dots,\mu\}
:

\mathrm{E}(T_{i})\leq\frac{\mu}{1-p_{c}}\left(\frac{\ln(j)+1}{f_{0}}+\frac{1}{js_{i}}\right).

Now we choose
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j:=j_{i}}\coloneqq\min\{\lceil f_{1}/s_{i}\rceil,\mu\}
, then we can bound the expected time to first reach the plateau or the global optimum as follows:

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathrm{E}(T_{i})\leq\frac{\mu}{1-p_{c}}\sum\nolimits_{i=0}^{n-1}\left(\frac{\ln(j)+1}{f_{0}}+\frac{1}{js_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{1-p_{c}}\left(\frac{n}{f_{0}}+\frac{1}{f_{0}}\sum\nolimits_{i=0}^{n-1}\ln(\lceil f_{1}/s_{i}\rceil)+\sum\nolimits_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{\min\{\lceil f_{1}/s_{i}\rceil,\mu\}s_{i}}\right).

(36)

Note that for
0\leq i\leq k-1
we have
s_{i}\geq f_{1}(n-i)/n

since a parent with fitness
i
has

n-i
ones and it suffices to flip a single one to obtain a fitness improvement.
In this case
\max\{f_{1}/s_{i},1\}\leq n/(n-i)
.
Similarly, for
k\leq i\leq n-1
we have
s_{i}\geq f_{1}(n+k-i)/n
as all search points
with fitness
i
have
i-k
ones and
n-(i-k)=n+k-i
zeros and flipping a single zero is sufficient for a fitness improvement.
In this case
\max\{f_{1}/s_{i},1\}\leq n/(n+k-i)
.

We obtain

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{i=0}^{n-1}\ln(\max\{1,f_{1}/s_{i}\})

\displaystyle\leq\sum\nolimits_{i=0}^{k-1}\ln\left(\frac{n}{n-i}\right)+\sum\nolimits_{i=k}^{n-1}\ln\left(\frac{n}{n+k-i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum\nolimits_{i=0}^{n-1}\ln\left(\frac{n}{n-i}\right).

If
f_{1}/s_{i}\leq 1
then
\lceil f_{1}/s_{i}\rceil=1
.

If
f_{1}/s_{i}>1
then
\lceil f_{1}/s_{i}\rceil\leq 2f_{1}/s_{i}
. Hence, we obtain
\lceil f_{1}/s_{i}\rceil\leq\max\{1,2f_{1}/s_{i}\}\leq 2\max\{1,f_{1}/s_{i}\}
, which implies

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{i=0}^{n-1}\ln(\lceil f_{1}/s_{i}\rceil)\leq\sum\nolimits_{i=0}^{n-1}\ln({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2\max\{1,f_{1}/s_{i}\}})\leq\ln(2)n+\sum\nolimits_{i=0}^{n-1}\ln({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\max\{1,f_{1}/s_{i}\}})

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\ln(2)n+2\sum\nolimits_{i=0}^{n-1}\ln\left(\frac{n}{n-i}\right)=\ln(2)n+2\ln\left(\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{n}{n-i}\right)

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\ln(2)n+2\ln\left(\frac{n^{n}}{n!}\right)\leq\ln(2)n+2\ln\left(\frac{n^{n}}{(n/e)^{n}}\right)=\ln(2)n+2\ln(e^{n})=(\ln(2)+2)n.

The second sum is bounded as follows.

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{\min\{\lceil f_{1}/s_{i}\rceil,\mu\}s_{i}}\leq\sum\nolimits_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{\min\{f_{1},\mu s_{i}\}}\leq\sum\nolimits_{i=0}^{n-1}\left(\frac{1}{f_{1}}+\frac{1}{\mu s_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\frac{n}{f_{1}}+\frac{1}{\mu}\sum\nolimits_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{s_{i}}\leq\frac{n}{f_{1}}+\frac{2}{f_{1}\mu}\sum\nolimits_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{n}{n-i}=\frac{n}{f_{1}}+\frac{2nH(n)}{f_{1}\mu}.

Plugging this back into (
36
), we obtain an upper bound of

\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{1-p_{c}}\left(\frac{n}{f_{0}}+\frac{(\ln(2)+2)n}{f_{0}}+\frac{n}{f_{1}}+\frac{2nH(n)}{f_{1}\mu}\right)

\displaystyle=\;O\left(\frac{\mu n}{1-p_{c}}\left(\frac{1}{f_{0}}+\frac{1}{f_{1}}\right)+\frac{n\log n}{(1-p_{c})f_{1}}\right)

for the expected time until the first point on the plateau (or a global optimum) is created, that is, a fitness of at least
n
. Unless a global optimum is reached, we still need to account for the expected time until
all
individuals have reached the plateau (or a global optimum is reached).
By (
35
), the expected time until
j\coloneqq\mu
individuals have fitness
n
(or a global optimum is reached) is at most

\frac{\mu}{(1-p_{c})f_{0}}\cdot(\ln(\mu)+1)

and together this yields

the following upper bound on the expected number of mutation-only steps:

\mathrm{E}(M)=\frac{1}{1-p_{c}}\cdot O\left(\mu n\left(\frac{1}{f_{0}}+\frac{1}{f_{1}}\right)+\frac{n\log n}{f_{1}}+\frac{\mu\log\mu}{f_{0}}\right).

(37)

Now we take into account the number of fitness evaluations performed in generations applying crossover. Recall that such generations perform
{\lambda_{c}}
fitness evaluations. Let

L
be the random number of evaluations made up to and including the first mutation-only step. Note that
L
does not depend on the current population, and thus we have a sequence of iid random variables with the same distribution as
L
. Thus, we can upper bound the expected number of evaluations for the (
\mu
+1) GA by
\mathrm{E}(M)\cdot\mathrm{E}(L)
using Wald’s equation. It remains to work out
\mathrm{E}(L)
. If the first mutation-only step happens in step
t
, the (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA makes
(t-1){\lambda_{c}}+1
evaluations. The probability of this happening in step
t
is
p_{c}^{t-1}(1-p_{c})
. Together,

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(L)

\displaystyle=1+\sum\nolimits_{t=1}^{\infty}p_{c}^{t-1}(1-p_{c})\cdot(t-1){\lambda_{c}}=1+(1-p_{c}){\lambda_{c}}\sum\nolimits_{t=0}^{\infty}p_{c}^{t}\cdot t

\displaystyle=1+(1-p_{c}){\lambda_{c}}\cdot\frac{p_{c}}{(1-p_{c})^{2}}=1+\frac{p_{c}{\lambda_{c}}}{1-p_{c}}.

Multiplying this with the upper bound from (
37
) yields the claimed bound.

The final statement follows easily using

1+x\leq 2=2\lceil x\rceil
if
0<x\leq 1
and
1+x\leq 2x\leq 2\lceil x\rceil
if
x>1
, thus
1+x\leq 2\lceil x\rceil
and
1+p_{c}{\lambda_{c}}/(1-p_{c})=O(\lceil{\lambda_{c}}p_{c}/(1-p_{c})\rceil)=O(\lceil{\lambda_{c}}p_{c}\rceil)
since
p_{c}=1-\Omega(1)
.
In addition, the factor
1/(1-p_{c})
from (
37
) is in
O(1)
.

∎

We remark that one can easily get a similar, but slightly weaker upper bound for
p_{c}=1
because when selecting two identical parents
for crossover, a standard bit mutation is performed. Since we always consider
p_{c}\leq 1-\Omega(1)
, we leave this for future work. The interested reader is referred to Lemma 1 in
[
4
]
for a similar bound holding for the classical (
\mu
+1) GA with
p_{c}=\Omega(1)
, including
p_{c}=1
.

5.2
Bounding the Expected Time on the Plateau

Now we use our analysis of population diversity to bound the expected time to reach a global optimum, when starting from a population on the plateau.

The following lemma gives a lower bound on the Hamming distance of individuals if the population diversity is large. In this case, many pairs of large Hamming distances exist.
More specifically, we assume that
S(P_{t})\geq 2k\mu^{2}(1-\varepsilon)
for a quantity
\varepsilon\coloneqq\varepsilon(k)
that may depend on
k
. As the maximum possible diversity is
2k\mu(\mu-1)
, the diversity of
P_{t}
is by a factor of roughly
1-\varepsilon
away from the maximum (ignoring the small difference between
\mu^{2}
and
\mu(\mu-1)
).

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\varepsilon\coloneqq\varepsilon(k)>0
and let
P_{t}\subset\textsc{plateau}
be a population of size
\mu
. Suppose that
S(P_{t})\geq 2k\mu^{2}(1-\varepsilon)
. Then there are at least
\mu^{2}/2
pairs
(x,y)\in P_{t}^{2}
with
H(x,y)>(1-2\varepsilon)2k
.

Proof.

Let
D
be the set of pairs
(x,y)\in P_{t}^{2}
with
H(x,y)>(1-2\varepsilon)2k
. Note that
H(x,y)\leq 2k
since
x,y
are in
plateau
. Hence, each pair in
D
has Hamming distance at most
2k
, and each pair in
P_{t}^{2}\setminus D
has distance at most
(1-2\varepsilon)2k
. Hence

S(P_{t})\leq|D|\cdot 2k+(\mu^{2}-|D|)(1-2\varepsilon)2k=2k\mu^{2}(1-2\varepsilon)+4\varepsilon k|D|.

Together with
S(P_{t})\geq 2k\mu^{2}(1-\varepsilon)
, this implies
2k\mu^{2}\varepsilon\leq 4\varepsilon k|D|
, and thus
|D|\geq\mu^{2}/2
as required.
∎

The next
theorem
gives an upper bound for the expected time until the (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA (Algorithm
2
) finds the global optimum if we use our results from Lemma
5.2
and Lemma
4.14
in a general setting.

Theorem 5.3
.

Consider the (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA as in Algorithm
2
, using standard bit mutation with mutation probability
\chi/n
and uniform crossover with crossover probability
p_{c}
on
\textsc{Jump}_{k}
.
Let
k\in o(n)
,
{\lambda_{c}}\geq 6\sqrt{k}e^{\chi}\ln(\mu)
,
\chi=\Theta(1)
, and
0<\varepsilon\coloneqq\varepsilon(k)<1/4
.
Suppose
p_{c}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\frac{2C_{2}}{2C_{2}+9\chi/4}
and
\mu\geq 1+\frac{3n}{\varepsilon}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{2C_{1}+3p_{c}}{2C_{2}}}
and
k\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(n-k)\varepsilon}/3
, where
C_{1},C_{2}
are from (
27
). Then the global optimum is found in

O\left(\lceil{\lambda_{c}}p_{c}\rceil\mu(n\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log(\mu))+{\lambda_{c}}+\frac{1}{p_{c}}+4^{k-\lfloor 8\varepsilon k\rfloor}\left(n/\chi\right)^{\lfloor 8\varepsilon k\rfloor}\left(1+\frac{1}{p_{c}{\lambda_{c}}}\right)\right)

expected fitness evaluations.
If, additionally,
{\lambda_{c}}=\Omega(1/p_{c})
,
this simplifies to

O\left(\lceil{\lambda_{c}}p_{c}\rceil\mu(n\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log(\mu))+{\lambda_{c}}+4^{k-\lfloor 8\varepsilon k\rfloor}\left(n/\chi\right)^{\lfloor 8\varepsilon k\rfloor}\right).

Loosely speaking, the first term
\lceil{\lambda_{c}}p_{c}\rceil\mu(n\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log(\mu))
bounds the expected time to reach a population on the plateau, and to build up sufficient diversity to facilitate a jump to the optimum. The remaining summands
{\lambda_{c}}+1/p_{c}+4^{k-\lfloor 8\varepsilon k\rfloor}\left(n/\chi\right)^{\lfloor 8\varepsilon k\rfloor}\left(1+1/(p_{c}{\lambda_{c}})\right)
bound the expected time to create the global optimum from a pair of diverse parents.

Note that the parameter
{\lambda_{c}}
in the (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA is reflected in the first term, as a factor of
\lceil{\lambda_{c}}p_{c}\rceil
, since a large value of
{\lambda_{c}}
may slow down the approach to the plateau. The bound contains a summand
+{\lambda_{c}}
, which only dominates the runtime if
{\lambda_{c}}
is excessively large. For moderate or large values of
k
(and
\varepsilon
not too small), the term
4^{k-\lfloor 8\varepsilon k\rfloor}\left(n/\chi\right)^{\lfloor 8\varepsilon k\rfloor}\left(1+1/(p_{c}{\lambda_{c}})\right)
dominates the runtime bound. Here
{\lambda_{c}}
has no effect if the term
\left(1+1/(p_{c}{\lambda_{c}})\right)
is dominated by
1
, that is, if
{\lambda_{c}}=\Omega(1/p_{c})
, and otherwise even has a positive effect on the runtime bound.
The reason large values of
{\lambda_{c}}
are not harmful is that, once the population is on the plateau and sufficiently diverse, generations executing crossover provide
{\lambda_{c}}
independent trials for a successful crossover. Although such a generation comes at a cost of
{\lambda_{c}}
fitness evaluations, the probability of achieving a successful crossover is amplified by a factor of roughly
{\lambda_{c}}
. These two effects balance each other out. If crossover is executed and sufficiently diverse parents are chosen, it is generally beneficial to use a large
{\lambda_{c}}
to amplify the probability of creating a global optimum.

Proof of Theorem
5.3
.

Consider the algorithm on
\textsc{Jump}_{k}^{\prime}
, so that the process continues after sampling the all-one-string.
According to Theorem
5.1
, we have that in
O(\lceil{\lambda_{c}}p_{c}\rceil(\mu n+n\log(n)+\mu\log\mu))

expected
fitness evaluations
P_{t}\subset\textsc{plateau}
. Note that then all conditions of Situation
4.8
are met.
Since by assumption
\mu=\Omega(n)
, we have
n\log n\in O(\mu n)
and the bound simplifies to a term absorbed by the stated bound:

O(\lceil{\lambda_{c}}p_{c}\rceil(\mu n+\mu\log\mu))=O(\lceil{\lambda_{c}}p_{c}\rceil\mu(n\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log(\mu)))

(38)

since
\varepsilon<1/4
and thus
\log(1/\varepsilon)\geq 2
.
The idea behind analysing the expected remaining time to find the global optimum is as follows. The remaining optimisation time is divided into so-called
trials
of length
\tau_{0}+T
, where
\tau_{0}:=\lceil\ln(1/\varepsilon)/\delta\rceil
is chosen according to Lemma
4.14
. Intuitively,
\tau_{0}
reflects an initial time to build up diversity in the population. The remaining time
T
is chosen in such a way that the algorithm has sufficient chances during the
\tau_{0}+T
generations to generate the optimum, assuming the population is sufficiently diverse. We will choose
T
in such a way that the probability of creating the optimum during a trial is
\Omega(1)
. If a trial is unsuccessful, we consider another trial.
Thus, in expectation,
O(1)
trials are sufficient and the expected number of

, starting with a population on the plateau, is
O(\tau_{0}+T)
.
To bound the number of

, we argue as follows. In every generation, the (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA performs
1+p_{c}({\lambda_{c}}-1)\leq 1+p_{c}{\lambda_{c}}
fitness evaluations in expectation as it performs one evaluation for sure and a generation with crossover performs
{\lambda_{c}}-1
additional evaluations. Thus, the expected number of function evaluations, starting with a population on the plateau, is

O((\tau_{0}+T)(1+p_{c}{\lambda_{c}}))=O(\lceil{\lambda_{c}}p_{c}\rceil\tau_{0}+(1+p_{c}{\lambda_{c}})T)

(39)

where in the first term we used
1+p_{c}{\lambda_{c}}\leq 2\lceil{\lambda_{c}}p_{c}\rceil
.

We now simplify this first summand in (

\tau_{0}:=\lceil\ln(1/\varepsilon)/\delta\rceil
and note that by Corollary
4.12

1/\delta=O(\mu n)
, which implies
\tau_{0}=O(\mu n\log(1/\varepsilon))
. Together, we have

\lceil{\lambda_{c}}p_{c}\rceil\tau_{0}=O(\lceil{\lambda_{c}}p_{c}\rceil\mu n\log(1/\varepsilon)),

which is absorbed by the first term of the claimed upper bound (
38
).

It remains to define
T
in such a way that the probability of a successful trial is
\Omega(1)
, and then to bound the remaining term in (
39
),
(1+p_{c}{\lambda_{c}})T
, from above. To this end, we first consider a bound
p^{*}
on the probability of crossover creating the optimum if crossover is executed and sufficiently diverse parents are chosen. Building up to invoke Lemma
4.14
, by Corollary
4.12
we have
\alpha/\delta\geq(1-\varepsilon)2k\mu^{2}

as in (
31
)
. Let
\mathcal{E}
be the event that during the trial we have
S(P_{t})\geq(1-4\varepsilon)2k\mu^{2}
for at least
T/4
generations in the trial. We call such generations
good

by Lemma
4.14
.

By Lemma
5.2
, each good generation has at least
\mu^{2}/2
pairs
(x_{1},x_{2})\in P_{t}^{2}
with distance

from each other, which we call

. This implies that the number

of positions in which both
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
have a zero-bit is less than
8\varepsilon k
. Since

r\leq\lfloor 8\varepsilon k\rfloor
. In a good generation, a good pair is picked for crossover with probability at least

.
We call this a

as the algorithm produces a batch of
{\lambda_{c}}
offspring via crossing the same good parents.
With each offspring in such a batch it may create the optimum by (i) setting all

differing bit positions to

via crossover, (ii) correcting the remaining

bits via mutation, and (iii) not flipping any of the correct bits with mutation. The probability of each of these steps is

,

, and

, respectively. As all these events are independent and
r\leq\lfloor 8\varepsilon k\rfloor
, the probability of a fixed offspring in a good-crossover batch being the optimum is at least

p^{*}\coloneqq 4^{\lfloor 8\varepsilon k\rfloor-k}\cdot(\chi/n)^{\lfloor 8\varepsilon k\rfloor}\cdot(1-\chi/n)^{n-\lfloor 8\varepsilon k\rfloor}.

Now we use
p^{*}
to define the time period

T\coloneqq\left\lceil\frac{{16}}{p_{c}}\cdot\max\left\{\frac{1}{{\lambda_{c}}p^{*}},{8}\right\}\right\rceil.

Denote by
G
the number of good-crossover batches during a trial, and let
\mathcal{A}
be the event “
G\geq p_{c}T/16
.” We claim that
\Pr(\mathcal{E}\cap(\neg\mathcal{A}))\leq 1/6
. To see this, consider an auxiliary process in which the duration of the trial is made infinite, i.e., we just continue the trial beyond the first
\tau+T
generations. Let
G^{\prime}
be the number of good-crossover batches among the first
T/4
good generations of the auxiliary process. Note that these exist because we made the process infinitely long and Lemma
4.14
can be applied repeatedly.

Recall that a good generation begins with a population of high diversity that contains at least
\mu^{2}/2
good pairs. The decision to perform crossover, as well as the selection of parents, is independent of decisions made in other generations. Therefore, in every good generation, a good crossover batch occurs independently with probability at least
p_{c}/2
. This implies that

stochastically dominates a binomial random variable

Moreover, we have
(\mathcal{E}\cap(G<p_{c}T/16))\subseteq(\mathcal{E}\cap(G^{\prime}<p_{c}T/16))

implies that

.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\Pr(\mathcal{E}\cap(\neg\mathcal{A}))

\displaystyle=\Pr(\mathcal{E}\cap(G<p_{c}T/16))

\displaystyle\leq\Pr(\mathcal{E}\cap(G^{\prime}<p_{c}T/16))

\displaystyle\leq\Pr(G^{\prime}<p_{c}T/16)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr(X<p_{c}T/16)\leq e^{-\mathrm{E}[X]/8}\leq e^{-2}\leq\frac{1}{6},

where the first step in the last line follows from

and the Chernoff bound
[

. In particular,

\displaystyle\Pr(\neg(\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{A}))\leq\Pr(\neg\mathcal{E})+\Pr(\mathcal{E}\cap(\neg\mathcal{A}))\leq\frac{2}{3}+\frac{1}{6}=\frac{5}{6},

and thus

.

If
\mathcal{A}
occurs then

, hence at least
{\lambda_{c}}G\geq 1/p^{*}
offspring are created in good-crossover batches. Then the optimum is created during the trial with probability at least

1-(1-p^{*})^{1/p^{*}}\geq 1-\frac{1}{e}.

To conclude, let

be the event that the optimum is created during the trial. Then

\displaystyle\Pr(\mathcal{B})\geq\Pr(\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{B})

\displaystyle=\Pr(\mathcal{B}\mid\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{A})\cdot\Pr(\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{A})\geq(1-1/e)/6.

Hence, each trial is successful with probability

and the term

\displaystyle(1+p_{c}{\lambda_{c}})\left(1+\frac{{16}}{p_{c}{\lambda_{c}}p^{*}}+\frac{{128}}{p_{c}}\right)=\;

\displaystyle O\left({\lambda_{c}}+\frac{1}{p_{c}}+\frac{1}{p^{*}}\left(1+\frac{1}{p_{c}{\lambda_{c}}}\right)\right).

Plugging in

The second bound follows easily from the first one. Since
{\lambda_{c}}=\Omega(1/p_{c})
, the term
+1/p_{c}
is absorbed by the

term and the term

O(1)
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.5
.

Consider a generation executing crossover on two individuals
x_{1},x_{2}\in P_{t}
with Hamming distance
2d
on the plateau. (The number must be even since the number of
0/1
-bits and
1/0
bits in
x_{1},x_{2}
must be equal when both are in
plateau
.)
Let

y
be an offspring created by crossover and standard bit mutation with mutation probability
p_{m}
. Note that
2d\leq 2k
by the triangle inequality, because both parents have distance
k
from the optimum.

Now, if crossover sets
i
of the
2d
bits to one in which the parents differ, the offspring has
k+d-i
zeros. To create the optimum, all zeros and no ones must be flipped. It is well known (see, e. g
.,
Lemma 1 in
[
9
]
) that every unbiased mutation operator can be described as follows: pick a radius
r
(deterministically or according to some probability distribution) and then flip
r
different bits chosen uniformly at random. Assuming that the mutation operator picks radius
r=k+d-i
(otherwise it is impossible to create the optimum), the probability of choosing precisely all zeros to be flipped is
1/\binom{n}{r}=1/\binom{n}{k+d-i}
.
Thus, the probability of
y
being the optimum is

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}[y=\vec{1}]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\;\leq\;}\sum\nolimits_{i=0}^{2d}\binom{2d}{i}2^{-2d}\cdot\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k+d-i}}=4^{-d}\sum\nolimits_{i=0}^{2d}\binom{2d}{i}/\binom{n}{k+d-i}.

(43)

(This generalises
[
4
, Lemma 2]
to arbitrary unbiased mutation operators.)
Denoting the summands in (
43
) as
S_{0},\dots,S_{2d}
, we have, for all
i\in\{1,\dots,2d\}
,

\displaystyle\frac{S_{i}}{S_{i-1}}=\frac{\binom{2d}{i}}{\binom{2d}{i-1}}\cdot\frac{\binom{n}{k+d-i+1}}{\binom{n}{k+d-i}}=\frac{2d-i+1}{i}\cdot\frac{n-k-d+i}{k+d-i+1}=\frac{2d-i+1}{k+d-i+1}\cdot\frac{n-k-d+i}{i}

and we observe that both factors are non-increasing with
i
. Thus, the minimum of
S_{i}/S_{i-1}
is attained for
i=2d
and we get

\displaystyle\frac{S_{i}}{S_{i-1}}\geq\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k-d+1}\cdot\frac{n-k+d}{2d}\geq\frac{n-k}{2d(k-d+1)}.

The denominator is maximised for
d=(k+1)/2
, which implies

\displaystyle\frac{S_{i}}{S_{i-1}}\geq\;

\displaystyle\frac{n-k}{(k+1)((k-1)/2+1)}=\frac{2(n-k)}{(k+1)^{2}}\geq 2

by assumption on
k
.
Hence, the whole sum is dominated by the term for
i=2d
and

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}[y=\vec{1}]=O\left(4^{-d}/\binom{n}{k-d}\right),

(44)

which is maximised for
2d=2k
where
\binom{n}{k-d}=1
. Therefore, in a crossover step followed by mutation, for any two parents on the plateau, the probability that the offspring is the optimum is
O(4^{-k})
.

In generations without crossover, the parent has
k
zeros and the probability of creating the optimum by mutation is at most

. Since in every offspring creation the probability of creating the optimum is bounded by
\max\{4^{-k}/C,1/\binom{n}{k}\}
, where

The second statement follows from noticing that, taking a union bound over

and so the expected number of generations is at least

Multiplying by

6
Extensions to Other Functions

At first glance, it might seem that our diversity estimates and the resulting runtimes are highly specific to
\textsc{Jump}_{k}
. However, these insights can also be applied in other contexts. Consider a fitness function in which there is some search point
x^{*}
such that all search points with the same Hamming distance
k
from
x^{*}
have the same fitness. Let us denote this set by
S
. Once the (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA has reached a population
P_{t}\subset S
, it will behave as on
\textsc{Jump}_{k}^{\prime}
until a search point outside of
S
is accepted. The reason is that the (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA is agnostic to the bit values 0 and 1, and thus it shows the same dynamic behaviour on
S
(all search points with Hamming distance
k
to
x^{*}
) as on
plateau
(all search points with Hamming distance
k
to
1^{n}
). Thus, the diversity shows the same dynamics in both scenarios up to the point where any search point
x\notin S
is accepted. If no other search point has the same fitness as search points in
S
, this implies that
f(x)>f(s)
for all
s\in S
and a strict fitness improvement was found. We can then easily derive upper bounds on the expected time to find fitness improvements. This argument can possibly be iterated or be integrated in a custom analysis.

6.1

As an example, we consider the function

]

In case of small gaps

It comes with a parameter
w\coloneqq w(n)\in\mathds{N}
called the
hurdle width
that determines the gap between neighbouring local optima with respect to the number of zeros in the bit string, denoted as
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left|x\right|_{0}}
. Now
Hurdle
is defined as

\textsc{Hurdle}(x)=-\left\lceil{\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left|x\right|_{0}}}{w}}\right\rceil-\frac{\mathrm{rem}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left|x\right|_{0}},w)}{w}.

where
\mathrm{rem}(z(x),w)
is the remainder of
z(x)
divided by
w
. Figure
1
shows a sketch of the
Hurdle
function for hurdle width
w=5
, where the
x
axis reflects the number of ones (instead of zeros) to show the similarity with the
\textsc{Jump}_{k}
function.

It is evident from Figure
1
and it was formally shown in
[
24
]
that
1^{n}
is the only global optimum, all search points with
iw
zeros,
i\in\mathds{N}
, are local optima, and all search points with at most
iw-w
zeros have a strictly larger fitness than all search points with
iw
zeros.

Nguyen and Sudholt [24]
gave an asymptotically tight upper bound on the expected optimisation time of the (1+1) EA on the
Hurdle
function of

O(n\log n)+\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/w\rfloor}\frac{en^{w}}{i^{w}}=O(n^{w}).

Here the term
\frac{en^{w}}{i^{w}}
is an upper bound on the expected time to improve the fitness from a search point of Hamming distance
iw
to the optimum. Note that these upper bounds decrease drastically with increasing Hamming distance, and that the expected time to overcome the last hurdle at Hamming distance
1\cdot w
dominates the expected optimisation time.

Note that, once the population only contains search points with exactly
iw
zeros, for any value
i\in\mathds{N}
, the algorithm temporarily behaves as on
\textsc{Jump}_{iw,w}
since only search points with
iw
or at most
iw-w
zeros are accepted. Hence, we can easily re-use our previous bound for
\textsc{Jump}_{iw,w}
. The only catch is that Theorem
5.4
requires
k^{2}\leq(n-k)/48
. This is implied by
k\leq\sqrt{n}/7
if
n
is large enough. Our general bound from Theorem
5.3
makes looser assumptions and only requires
k=o(n)
, but it provides weaker guarantees on the population diversity. For local optima far from the global optimum, this is not a huge issue since even diversity far below the maximum value suffices to escape the local optimum. After all, at a Hamming distance
iw
from the optimum, it suffices to create a search point at Hamming distance
(i-1)w
by creating a surplus of
w
ones during crossover. However, for the sake of simplicity, we restrict our attention to tackling hurdles at Hamming distance at most
\sqrt{n}/7
to the optimum. As discussed for the (1+1) EA, these hurdles are by far the hardest obstacles and we conjecture that the expected time to overcome these last hurdles dominates the overall expected optimisation time.

Now, assuming the population consists of search points with
iw
zeros, the expected time to improve the fitness on
Hurdle
equals the expected time to improve the fitness on
\textsc{Jump}_{k,\delta}
with
k\coloneqq iw
and
\delta\coloneqq w
. Theorem
6.1
yields an upper bound of

O\left(\mu n\log(\mu)+4^{iw}\cdot\binom{2iw}{iw+w}^{-1}\right)

for this expected time. Note that the term
O(\mu n\log\mu)
also covers the expected time for reaching a population of local optima.
For
i=1
we have
4^{iw}\cdot\binom{2iw}{iw+w}^{-1}=4^{w}
. For
i\geq 2
,
using Corollary 1.4.11 in
[
5
]
, we can simplify the binomial coefficient as follows.

\displaystyle 4^{iw}\binom{2iw}{iw+w}^{-1}=\;

\displaystyle 4^{iw}\cdot\Theta\left(\sqrt{\frac{(iw+w)(iw-w)}{2iw}}\right)\left(\frac{iw+w}{2iw}\right)^{iw+w}\left(\frac{iw-w}{2iw}\right)^{iw-w}

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\Theta\left(\sqrt{\frac{(iw+w)(iw-w)}{2iw}}\right)\left(\frac{i+1}{i}\right)^{iw+w}\left(\frac{i-1}{i}\right)^{iw-w}

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\Theta\left(\sqrt{iw}\right)\left(\left(\frac{i+1}{i}\right)^{i+1}\left(\frac{i-1}{i}\right)^{i-1}\right)^{w}.

The term in brackets is simplified as

\displaystyle\left(\frac{i+1}{i}\right)^{i+1}\left(\frac{i-1}{i}\right)^{i-1}=\;

\displaystyle\frac{i+1}{i-1}\cdot\left(\frac{(i+1)(i-1)}{i^{2}}\right)^{i}

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\frac{i+1}{i-1}\cdot\left(\frac{i^{2}-1}{i^{2}}\right)^{i}

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\frac{i+1}{i-1}=1+\frac{2}{i-1}.

Adding up
O(\mu n\log(\mu)+4^{w})
for
i=1
and
O(\mu n\log(\mu)+\Theta\left(\sqrt{iw}\right)(1+2/(i-1))^{w})
for all
i\in\{2,\dots,\lfloor\sqrt{n}/(7w)\rfloor\}
, we get a total upper bound of

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{\mu n^{3/2}\log(\mu)}{w}+4^{w}+\sum_{i=2}^{\lfloor\sqrt{n}/(7w)\rfloor}\sqrt{iw}\left(1+\frac{2}{i-1}\right)^{w}\right)

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{\mu n^{3/2}\log(\mu)}{w}+4^{w}+\sum_{i=2}^{w}\sqrt{iw}\left(1+\frac{2}{i-1}\right)^{w}+\sum_{i=w+1}^{\lfloor\sqrt{n}/(7w)\rfloor}\sqrt{iw}\left(1+\frac{2}{i-1}\right)^{w}\right)

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{\mu n^{3/2}\log(\mu)}{w}+4^{w}+\sum_{i=2}^{w}w\cdot 3^{w}+\sum_{i=w+1}^{\lfloor\sqrt{n}/(7w)\rfloor}n^{1/4}\left(1+\frac{2}{w}\right)^{w}\right)

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{\mu n^{3/2}\log(\mu)}{w}+4^{w}+w^{2}\cdot 3^{w}+\frac{\sqrt{n}}{w}\cdot n^{1/4}\cdot e^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{\mu n^{3/2}\log(\mu)}{w}+4^{w}+w^{2}\cdot 3^{w}+\frac{n^{3/4}}{w}\right).

Note that the term
+n^{3/4}/w
can be absorbed by
+\mu n^{3/2}\log(\mu)/w
and further, since
w^{2}\cdot 3^{w}=O(4^{w})
, the term
w^{2}\cdot 3^{w}
is absorbed by
+4^{w}
.
Hence, we have shown the following.

Theorem 6.2
.

The expected optimisation time of the (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA on
Hurdle
with hurdle width
w
, using parameters
\mu,{\lambda_{c}}
and
p_{c}
as in the second statement of Theorem
5.4
, when starting with a population of search points that all have at most
\sqrt{n}/7
zeros, is at most

O\left(\frac{\mu n^{3/2}\log(\mu)}{w}+4^{w}\right).

Note that for large enough
w
the term
4^{w}
dominates the above bound. This makes sense as, like for
\textsc{Jump}_{k}
, the final jump is the hardest one. Then the resulting time bound is much smaller than the expected runtime of
\Theta(n^{w})
proven for the (1+1) EA
[
24
]
.

7
Conclusions

We have presented the first runtime bounds for a (
\mu
+1) GA variant that show strong benefits of crossover in the regime of large crossover probabilities
p_{c}
without explicit diversity-enhancing mechanisms. Our analysis has shown that the population evolves near-perfect diversity, where most pairs of parents have the maximum Hamming distance of
2k
.
We believe that our analysis can be easily transferred to a (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA when omitting mutation after crossover as, unlike the work in
[
4
]
, the optimum can be reached without mutation.

A limitation of our approach is that we had to modify the original (
\mu
+1) GA to include
{\lambda_{c}}
competing offspring. An important task for future work is therefore to show similar results for the original (
\mu
+1) GA. Another research question is whether the dependency on
p_{c}
can be further relaxed, possibly up to
p_{c}=1
as in
[
4
,
10
]
. For this one might need to carefully consider the possible advantages that crossover can provide for population diversity.

We have also shown that our approach can be applied to other functions where we have plateaus of all search points with the same Hamming distance from some fixed search point
x^{*}
. We have demonstrated this for the
\textsc{Jump}_{k,\delta}
benchmark
[
2
]
that contains a fitness valley of a specified width
\delta
, and for the function
Hurdle

[
28
]
that contains many peaks.

We leave the following open problems and research questions for future work.

1.

Can our upper bound for the (
\mu
+1)-
{\lambda_{c}}
-GA be transferred to the original (
\mu
+1) GA, when allowing for a factor of
1/p_{c}
in the term
4^{k}
? We suspect that this might be true, but the examples in Lemmas
4.10
and
4.13
suggest that this would require more fine-grained information about the population
P_{t}
than provided by the diversity
S(P_{t})
.

2.

Can the conditions on
p_{c}
be relaxed? This could possibly be achieved with stronger guarantees on how generations with crossover affect the population diversity (see Lemma
4.9
).

3.

Our analysis requires a large population size of
\mu=\Omega(kn)
, although empirical results
[
20
,
4
]
suggest that small populations are highly effective as well. Can our results be extended towards smaller population sizes?

4.

Related to this, can the upper bound
O(\mu n\log(\mu))=O(n^{2}\log^{2}n)
for
k=O(\log n)
and
\mu=\Theta(kn)
be improved further?

5.

Do our diversity estimates and runtime bounds translate to (
\mu
+
\lambda
) GAs in which more than one new search point can enter the population in the same generation? The algorithm might still create a larger number
{\lambda_{c}}\gg\lambda
of offspring in generations with crossover.

Appendix A
Details on Adapted Runtime Bounds in Table
1

In this short section we give further details on adaptations made to published bounds from
[
16
]
and
[
10
]
included in Table
1
.

Kötzing et al. [16]
showed a bound of

O(\mu n\log(n)+e^{6k}\mu^{k+2}n)

for
p_{c}\leq k/n
and for a mutation operator which never creates duplicates of the parent. Inspecting the proof of
[
16
, Theorem 7]
, we found a minor bug. The probability of event E6 of crossover happening at least once during
n/k
generations and no mutation leading to an accepted offspring is bounded from below by

1-(1-p_{c})^{n/k}\cdot\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)^{k/n}=\Omega(1).

There are two typos in the expression on the left-hand side;
the statement
should read

(1-(1-p_{c})^{n/k})\cdot\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)^{n/k}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\;=\Omega(1)}.

This corrected statement holds
for
p_{c}=\Theta(k/n)
, but for smaller
p_{c}
we only have
1-(1-p_{c})^{n/k}=\Theta(p_{c}\cdot n/k)
(cf.
[
1
, Lemma 10]
). This leads to an additional factor of
1/(p_{c}\cdot n/k)=k/(np_{c})
in the asymptotic bound, which then becomes

O(\mu n\log(n)+e^{6k}\mu^{k+2}k/p_{c}).

Doerr et al. [10]
very recently proved an upper bound of

\displaystyle O\Big(

\displaystyle\mu n\log(\mu)+n\log(n)+\frac{(n/k+\mu)\log(\mu)}{(n/\chi)^{-k+1}\min(\exp(p_{c}\mu/(2048e)),(n/\chi)^{+k-1})}+\left(\frac{n}{\chi}\right)^{k-1}\Big)

for
2\leq\mu=o(n)
.
The third summand can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{(n/k+\mu)\log(\mu)}{\min((n/\chi)^{-k+1}\exp(p_{c}\mu/(2048e)),1)}\leq\frac{(n/k+\mu)\log(\mu)}{(n/\chi)^{-k+1}\exp(p_{c}\mu/(2048e))}+\frac{(n/k+\mu)\log(\mu)}{1}.

Since
(n/k+\mu)\log(\mu)=O(\mu n\log(\mu))
, the last term can be omitted and we obtain a simplified bound of

\displaystyle O\left(\mu n\log(\mu)+n\log(n)+\left(\frac{n}{\chi}\right)^{k-1}\left(1+\frac{(n/k+\mu)\log(\mu)}{\exp(p_{c}\mu/(2048e))}\right)\right).

Proof.

The formula for

Hence, by Lemma
4.2
