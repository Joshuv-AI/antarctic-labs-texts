Abstract
. We study the multiplier ideals and the corresponding jumping numbers and multiplicities { m ⁡ ( c ) } c ∈ ℝ \{m(c)\}_{c\in\mathbb{R}} in the following context: ( X , o ) (X,o) is a complex analytic normal surface singularity, 𝔞 ⊂ 𝒪 X , o {\mathfrak{a}}\subset{\mathscr{O}}_{X,o} is an 𝔪 X , o {\mathfrak{m}}_{X,o} –primary ideal, ϕ : X ~ → X \phi:\widetilde{X}\to X is a log resolution of 𝔞 \mathfrak{a} such that 𝔞 ​ 𝒪 X ~ = 𝒪 X ~ ​ ( − F ) \mathfrak{a}{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}={\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(-F) , for some nonzero effective divisor F F supported on ϕ − 1 ​ ( 0 ) \phi^{-1}(0) . A priori, { m ⁡ ( c ) } c ∈ ℝ \{m(c)\}_{c\in\mathbb{R}} depends on the Hilbert function associated with the resolution and F F . We prove that { m ⁡ ( c ) } c > 0 \{m(c)\}_{c>0} is combinatorially computable from F F and the resolution graph Γ \Gamma of ϕ \phi , and we provide several formulae. We also extend Budur’s result (valid for ( X , o ) = ( ℂ 2 , 0 ) (X,o)=(\mathbb{C}^{2},0) ), which makes an identification of ∑ c ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] m ⁡ ( c ) ​ t c \sum_{c\in[0,1]}m(c)t^{c} with a certain Hodge spectrum. In our general case we use Hodge spectrum with coefficients in a mixed Hodge module. We show that { m ⁡ ( c ) } c ≤ 0 \{m(c)\}_{c\leq 0} usually depends on the analytic type of ( X , o ) (X,o) . However, for some distinguished analytic types we determine it concretely. E.g., when ( X , o ) (X,o) is weighted homogeneous (and F F is associated with the central vertex), we recover ∑ c m ⁡ ( c ) ​ t c \sum_{c}m(c)t^{c} from the Poincaré series of ( X , o ) (X,o) . Also, when ( X , o ) (X,o) is a splice quotient then we recover ∑ c m ⁡ ( c ) ​ t c \sum_{c}m(c)t^{c} from the multivariable topological Poincaré (zeta) function of Γ \Gamma .

1.
Introduction

Assume that
(X,o)
is a complex analytic normal surface singularity, let
{\mathscr{O}}_{X,o}
be its
local algebra with maximal ideal
{\mathfrak{m}}_{X,o}
. Let us also fix an

{\mathfrak{m}}_{X,o}
–primary ideal
{\mathfrak{a}}\subset{\mathscr{O}}_{X,o}
. Then there exists a log resolution

\phi:\widetilde{X}\to X
and a nonzero
integral cycle
F
supported on the exceptional curve
\phi^{-1}(0)=E
such that
{\mathfrak{a}}{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}={\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(-F)
. In fact, in our study we will start with some log resolution

\phi
and such a nonzero antinef cycle
F
.

We denote by
Z_{K}
the anticanonical cycle associated with
\phi
, namely
-c_{1}(\Omega^{2}_{\widetilde{X}})=-c_{1}({\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(K_{\widetilde{X}}))\in H^{2}(\widetilde{X},\mathbb{Z})
,
identified with a rational cycle (via the adjunction relations).
We consider for any
c\in\mathbb{R}
the cycle

L(c):=\lfloor Z_{K}+cF\rfloor
. Then the tower of
multiplier ideals
is defined as

\mathfrak{I}(c,F):=\phi_{*}{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(-L(c))\subset{\mathscr{O}}_{X,o}
. They measure the complexity of the singularity
(X,o)
compatibly with
F
(or with
\mathfrak{a}
). They were introduced (at least implicitly)
in
[
23
,
24
,
45
,
46
]
and in the present form in

[
12
,
22
]
.
Since then they were intensively studied, see
[
5
,
3
,
2
,
15
,
14
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
25
,
26
,
44
]
with all their connections with different aspects of singularities and singular map-germs. In fact, the definition and study
extends to any dimension, see e.g.
[
12
,
22
,
34
]
and the references therein.

It turns out that for any
c\in\mathbb{R}
, the ideal
\mathfrak{I}(c,F)\subset{\mathscr{O}}_{X,o}

has finite codimension,
\mathfrak{I}(c,F)\subset\mathfrak{I}(c^{\prime},F)
for
c^{\prime}<c
, and
\mathfrak{I}(c,F)={\mathscr{O}}_{X,o}
for

c\ll 0
. The number
c
is called
jumping number
with
jumping multiplicity

m(c)

if
m(c):=\dim\,\mathfrak{I}(c-\epsilon,F)/\mathfrak{I}(c,F)\not=0
for
0<\epsilon\ll 1
.
One verifies that in any compact interval there are only finitely many jumping numbers and they are all rational.

Compared with the earlier literature in the subject we make two major extensions:

(A)
We do not assume any restriction regarding the surface singularity
(X,o)
, e.g. we do not assume that the link is a rational homology sphere (i.e. the dual graph might have loops and the irreducible exceptional divisors might have higher genera).
Also, we do not restrict the analytic type of
(X,o)
: on the supporting topological type the analytic type might vary (which in the non-rational or non-minimally elliptic cases
might have considerable effects). We emphasize that in general the classification
of such analytic types (or the description of the corresponding moduli spaces)
is still an open problem. Under this generality the general analytic tools
(e.g. vanishing theorems) are more complex (and the zones what they do not cover
are wider).

(B)
In the earlier studies only the cases
c>0
were treated.
Our study of the cases
c\leq 0
opens new perspectives. If
(X,o)
is smooth or log terminal then
m(c)=0
for
c\leq 0
, however, in the general case (even for rational
(X,o)
) the negative zone appears as a very challenging part.

In fact (and this is one of the main messages of the note),
we show that the study of
\{m(c)\}_{c\in\mathbb{R}}
(equivalently,
the Poincaré series of multiplier ideals
\sum_{c\in\mathbb{R}}m(c)t^{c}
introduced first in
[
15
]
) breaks into two major parts with rather different aspects.

(i)

(the
c>0
zone)
We also call this the
Riemann–Roch zone
,
since in this case we determine
\sum_{c>0}m(c)t^{c}
completely topologically from the resolution graph
\Gamma
of
\phi
and the cycle
F
(without any restriction regarding the link or the analytic type of
(X,o)
).

We provide several combinatorial formulae. The first one, Proposition
3.3.4
,
is a generalization of
expressions obtained in
[
2
,
3
,
44
]
proved in those articles for rational singularities.

We also compute
m(c+1)-m(c)
(see Corollary
3.3.5
), thus we recover
\sum_{c>0}m(c)t^{c}
from

\sum_{c\in(0,1]}m(c)t^{c}
and
(D_{c},F)
, where
D_{c}=L(c)-L(c-\epsilon)

(
0<\epsilon\ll 1
) is the
jumping
divisor
(introduced in
[
2
]
), computable combinatorially.
(This fact was also shown for rational
(X,o)
in
[
3
]
.)

Hence, the first interval and

\sum_{c\in(0,1]}m(c)t^{c}
has a distinguished role.

Regarding this we stress out two facts. Firstly, Budur in
[
5
]

identified
\sum_{c\in(0,1]}m(c)t^{c}
with the Hodge spectrum
{\rm Sp}_{(0,1]}(f,t)

of a function germ
f
, where in his case,
(X,o)=(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
is smooth,
f:(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\to(\mathbb{C},0)
is a function germ with an isolated singularity,
\phi
is a log embedded resolution and
F
is the divisor
{\rm div}_{\phi,E}(f)
of
\phi^{*}(f)
along
\phi^{-1}(0)=E
. This was reproved in
[
3
]
as well.
(For the theory of mixed Hodge structures and of the spectrum associated with isolated hypersurface singularities see the work of
Steenbrink, e.g.
[
40
,
41
]
or
[
39
]
.)

In this note we succeed to generalize this statement. The first step is that we rewrite

J_{[0,1]}(t)=\sum_{c\in[0,1]}m(c)t^{c}
in a different way, in terms of
\Gamma
and the multiplicities of
F
, for any
(X,o)
and any
F
(cf. Theorem
4.2.3
).
This is the second combinatorial formula of the jumping multiplicities.

This formula will resonate perfectly with the Hodge spectrum computations of a generic element
f\in\mathfrak{a}
. Indeed, if
(X,o)
is numerically Gorenstein
(that is,
Z_{K}
is an integral cycle), and

f
is an analytic function gems
(X,o)\to(\mathbb{C},0)
with isolated singularities and
{\rm div}_{\phi,E}(f)=F
,
we prove the generalization of the statement of Budur:

J_{[0,1]}(t)={\rm Sp}_{[0,1]}(f,t)
, cf. Corollary
5.1.3
. However, when
\Gamma
is not numerically Gorenstein,
\{Z_{K}\}\not=0
, then we need to use a more general spectrum formula
established in
[
27
]
. In
[
27
]
the ‘usual’ spectrum is replaced by the spectrum of
f
with coefficients in a mixed Hodge module
{\mathscr{M}}

of
(X,o)
,
such that
{\mathscr{M}}
restricted to
X\setminus\{o\}
is a variation of Hodge structure whose underlying representation is abelian.
Indeed, under the assumption that the link is a rational homology sphere,
from
Z_{K}
we construct a character of the first homology of the link, hence a
rank one variation of Hodge structure
{\mathbb{V}}
on
X\setminus\{o\}
, and we prove the identity

J_{[0,1]}(t)={\rm Sp}_{[0,1]}(f,j_{*}{\mathbb{V}},t)
(where

j:X\setminus\{o\}\subset X
is the inclusion), see Theorem
5.3.2
.
The construction of
{\mathbb{V}}

can be done using the canonical cyclic covering of
(X,o)
(associated with
Z_{K}
,
or in
\mathbb{Q}
–Gorenstein case associated with the canonical divisor
K_{\widetilde{X}}
, as well). We emphasize again, though a priori both
objects
J_{[0,1]}(t)
and
{\rm Sp}_{[0,1]}(f,j_{*}{\mathbb{V}},t)
are analytical, we provide combinatorial formulae for them. It would be interesting to compare these results with the general (Hodge) theoretical construction
of
[
38
]
valid for complex manifolds, and with the Bernstein-Sato polynomial connection from
[
13
]
.

The second new aspect regarding
\sum_{c\in(0,1]}m(c)t^{c}
is the following connection.
Consider again a function
f:(X,o)\to(\mathbb{C},0)
with an isolated singularity such that
F={\rm div}_{\phi,E}(f)
. Set
(C,o):=(f^{-1}(0),o)
and let

\delta(C,o)
be its delta invariant.
Then
\sum_{c\in(0,1]}m(c)=\delta(C,o)+(\{Z_{K}\},F)
.

(ii)

(the
c\leq 0
zone)
We show by examples that the multiplicity
m(0)
cannot be determined
topologically. However, we characterize it completely in terms of
the analytic type of
(X,o)
. The analytic contribution is a term
\mathfrak{h}^{0}\in\{0,1\}

characterized by an
h^{0}
–sheaf cohomology (see
3.3
).

The
c\leq 0
zone can be called the
‘
h^{0}
–zone’, where the
h^{1}
–vanishing theorems (hence the Riemann–Roch expressions)
cannot be applied.
In this zone
m(c)
usually cannot be computed form
\Gamma
and
F
. We exemplify this fact
by concrete examples when we fix
\Gamma
and
F
and we consider several different analytic realizations, and we compute the
m(c)
’s. Interestingly enough, already the numerical invariant
\sum_{c\leq 0}m(c)

measures the analytic type and it is connected to the most important analytic invariant of
(X,o)
, the geometric genus
p_{g}(X,o)
. More precisely, we prove that

\sum_{c\leq 0}m(c)=p_{g}(X,o)+\chi(\lfloor Z_{K}\rfloor)
.
Already when
(X,o)
is rational (i.e.
p_{g}(X,o)=0
),
\sum_{c\leq 0}m(c)
can be arbitrarily large
(see e.g. Example
3.2.2
).

On the other hand, even for this zone, we provide concrete formulae valid for certain

distinguished analytic types
.

\bullet
In the case of
a weighted homogeneous germs

(X,o)
with local graded algebra

\oplus_{\ell\geq 0}R_{\ell}

(and
F
is associated with the central vertex),
we show the correspondence
\dim(R_{\ell})=m(c)
with
c=(\ell-\mathfrak{r})/\alpha
(for notations and precise statement see subsection
3.4
). For
\ell>{\mathfrak{r}}

(corresponding to
c>0
)
\dim(R_{\ell})
is topological computable from the Seifert invariants of the link, however in general it is not.
But, if the link is a rational homology sphere, then by Dolgachev-Pinkham-Demazure formulae
all the terms for any
\ell
are topological. (For the theory of weighted homogeneous germs see e.g.
[
35
]
.)

\bullet
In the next case we assume that
the link is a rational homology sphere, and we fix a resolution graph
\Gamma
, and we assume that the analytic type of
(X,o)
is
splice quotient
with respect to

\Gamma
(a family introduced by Neumann and Wahl, cf.
[
32
]
).
Then in Proposition
3.1.4
we provide a combinatorial formula
for
\sum_{c\in\mathbb{R}}m(c)t^{c}
in terms of
\{L(c)\}_{c}
and
the multivariable topological Poincaré (zeta)
function
Z({\bf t})
of
\Gamma
. This includes e.g. all the cases when
(X,o)

is rational or minimally elliptic (with
\Gamma
minimal). (For more on
Z({\bf t})
see
[
6
,
7
,
30
]
.)

But we emphasize again that in general, the computation of
\{m(c)\}_{c\leq 0}

is of analytic nature, hence it runs in parallel with the classification of the analytic types
of
(X,o)
supported of a fixed topological type identified by
\Gamma
. We will return back to the study
of these analytic aspects in the forthcoming manuscript.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notations regarding resolutions and resolution graphs

2.1.1
.

Let us fix a normal surface singularity
(X,o)
and one of its log (good) resolutions
\phi:\widetilde{X}\to X
.
Let
\Gamma
be the dual resolution graph of
\phi
, we denote its vertices by
\mathscr{V}
and its edges by
\mathscr{E}
.
We write
E
for the irreducible exceptional curve
\phi^{-1}(o)
, and let
\{E_{i}\}_{i\in\mathscr{V}}
be its irreducible components.

The lattice
L:=H_{2}(\widetilde{X},\mathbb{Z})
is a
free
\mathbb{Z}
–module generated by the classes of
\{E_{i}\}_{i\in\mathscr{V}}

and it is endowed
with the natural negative definite intersection form
(\,,\,)
.
The dual lattice is
L^{\prime}={\rm Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(L,\mathbb{Z})\simeq\{l^{\prime}\in L\otimes\mathbb{Q}\,:\,(l^{\prime},L)\in\mathbb{Z}\}\subset L\otimes\mathbb{Q}
.
We use the same symbol
(\,,\,)
for the extended intersection
form to
L\otimes\mathbb{Q}
. The overlattice

L^{\prime}
is generated over
\mathbb{Z}

by the (anti)dual classes
\{E^{*}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathscr{V}}
defined
by
(E^{*}_{i},E_{j})=-\delta_{ij}
(where
\delta_{ij}
stays for the Kronecker symbol).

L^{\prime}
can also identified with
H^{2}(\widetilde{X},\mathbb{Z})
(the target of
the first Chern class
c_{1}:{\rm Pic}(\widetilde{X})\to H^{2}(\widetilde{X},\mathbb{Z})
).

We denote by
M
the link of
(X,o)
. It is known that
M
is a rational homology sphere if and only if each
E_{i}
is rational and
\Gamma
is a tree.
It is an integral homology sphere if additionally
\det\,(\,,\,)=\pm 1
.
More generally, if
g_{i}
denotes the genus of
E_{i}
,
g:=\sum_{i}g_{i}
,
and
h
is the first Betti number of the topological realization of
\Gamma
(the number of independent
cycle in the graph), then the first Betti number of
M
is
2g+h
, and

{\rm Tors}\,H_{1}(M,\mathbb{Z})\simeq L^{\prime}/L
.

There is a natural partial ordering of
L\otimes\mathbb{Q}
: we write
l_{1}^{\prime}\geq l_{2}^{\prime}
if

l_{1}^{\prime}-l_{2}^{\prime}=\sum_{i}a_{i}E_{i}
with every
a_{i}\geq 0
.
We set
L_{\geq 0}=\{l\in L\,:\,l\geq 0\}
and

L_{>0}=L_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}
.

We define the rational
Lipman cone
as
{\mathscr{S}}_{\mathbb{Q}}:=\{l^{\prime}\in L\otimes\mathbb{Q}\,:\,(l^{\prime},E_{i})\leq 0\ \mbox{for all $i$}\}
, and we also
set
{\mathscr{S}}^{\prime}:={\mathscr{S}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\cap L^{\prime}
and
{\mathscr{S}}:={\mathscr{S}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\cap L
.
As a monoid
{\mathscr{S}}^{\prime}
is generated over
{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
by
\{E^{*}_{i}\}_{i}
.
If
s^{\prime}\in{\mathscr{S}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\setminus\{0\}
, then
all its
E_{i}
–coordinates are strictly positive (see
[
30
, Corollary 2.1.19]
).

Let
K_{\widetilde{X}}
be the
canonical divisor
of
\widetilde{X}
(well–defined up to a linear equivalence), that is,
{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(K_{\widetilde{X}})\simeq\Omega^{2}_{\widetilde{X}}
, the sheaf of holomorphic 2–forms. We denote
its first Chern class

c_{1}({\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(K_{\widetilde{X}}))\in H^{2}(\widetilde{X},\mathbb{Z})\simeq L^{\prime}
by
-Z_{K}
;
Z_{K}
is called
the
(anti)canonical cycle
of
\phi
.
The (rational) cycle
Z_{K}\in L^{\prime}
can also be determined combinatorially from

(\,,\,)
by the

adjunction formulae

(Z_{K},E_{i})=(E_{i},E_{i})+2-2g_{i}
for all
i\in\mathscr{V}
.

We call
(X,o)

numerically Gorenstein
if
Z_{K}\in L
, i.e. if all its
E_{i}
–coefficients are integral (this condition is independent of the choice of
\phi
).
We call
(X,o)

Gorenstein
if
Z_{K}\in L
and one can choose
K_{\widetilde{X}}
as

-Z_{K}
, i.e.
{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(K_{\widetilde{X}}+Z_{K})\simeq{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}
. In a different language,

(X,o)
is numerically Gorenstein (resp. Gorenstein)
exactly when the line bundle
\Omega^{2}_{X\setminus\{o\}}
is topologically (resp. analytically) trivial.
(For a different characterization see also
5.4.1
.)

Finally, we set for any
l^{\prime}\in L^{\prime}
the Riemann-Roch expression
\chi(l^{\prime})=(Z_{K}-l^{\prime},l^{\prime})/2
. If
l\in L_{>0}
, then
\chi(l)=h^{0}({\mathscr{O}}_{l})-h^{1}({\mathscr{O}}_{l})
. E.g.,
\chi(E)=1-g-h
.
As usual, we also write
\chi({\mathscr{O}}_{l}(D))
for
h^{0}({\mathscr{O}}_{l}(D))-h^{1}({\mathscr{O}}_{l}(D))
,
which, by Riemann-Roch, equals
\chi(l)+(D,l)
(
D\in L
).

For any
{\mathbb{R}}
-divisor
D=\sum_{i}d_{i}E_{i}
of
\widetilde{X}
(
d_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
) we define its integral part

\lfloor D\rfloor
and fractional part
\{D\}
by applying the corresponding operation to each coefficient
d_{i}
.

For more details see e.g.
[
28
,
30
]
.

2.1.2
.

Assume that
(C,o)
is a reduced Weil divisor in
(X,o)

(i.e. it is an isolated curve singularity in
(X,o)
).
Then we can consider a log embedded resolutions of the pair
(C,o)\subset(X,o)
, that is, we require that in the log resolution
the union of
E
with the strict transform
\tilde{C}
of
(C,o)
form a simple normal crossing divisor.
Moreover, we can consider the corresponding embedded resolution graph too. Usually, the strict transform will be denoted by arrows on the graph.
Their index set will be denoted by
{\mathscr{A}}
corresponding to the
irreducible decomposition
\cup_{a\in{\mathscr{A}}}C_{a}
of
(C,o)
.

Once an embedded resolution and the strict transform
\tilde{C}\subset\widetilde{X}
is fixed, the embedded topological type of the pair
(C,o)\subset(X,o)

is basically coded in the information that how many components of
\tilde{C}
intersect each
E_{i}
, that is, in the intersection numbers
(\tilde{C},E_{i})_{\widetilde{X}}
. Then we define the rational cycle
l^{\prime}_{C}\in{\mathscr{S}}^{\prime}\setminus\{0\}\subset L^{\prime}_{>0}
, associated with
C
and
\phi
,
via the linear system of equations

(l^{\prime}_{C},E_{i})+(\tilde{C},E_{i})_{\widetilde{X}}=0
for every

i\in\mathscr{V}
. (Hence,
l^{\prime}_{C}+\tilde{C}
, as a rational divisor of
\widetilde{X}
, is numerically trivial.
It is the total transform of
C
, denoted by
\phi^{*}C
.)
If the arrow
a
is supported by the vertex
i(a)
, then
l^{\prime}_{C}=\sum_{a\in{\mathscr{A}}}E_{i(a)}^{*}
.

If
(C,o)
is cut out by a germ
f:(X,o)\to(\mathbb{C},0)
such that
(C,o)=(f^{-1}(0),o)
is reduced (that is,
f
defines an isolated singularity), then we say that
(C,o)
is
a Cartier divisor of
(X,o)
. In such a case
\phi^{*}C={\rm div}_{\widetilde{X}}(\phi^{*}f)
. Hence, in this case,
l^{\prime}_{C}
is integral.
It will also be denoted by
{\rm div}_{\phi,E}(f)
.

2.1.3
.

Next, we fix a cycle
F\in{\mathscr{S}}\setminus\{0\}
. Some geometrical situations
(motivated by the present note) how such a cycle might appear is listed below.

(A)
Let
\mathfrak{m}_{X,o}
denote the maximal ideal of the local ring
{\mathscr{O}}_{X,o}
of
(X,o)
.
Let
\mathfrak{a}
be an
\mathfrak{m}_{X,o}
–primary ideal of
{\mathscr{O}}_{X,o}
. Then there exists
a log resolution
\phi
such that
\mathfrak{a}\cdot{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}={\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(-F)
for some
F\in{\mathscr{S}}\setminus\{0\}
.

(B)
Let
(C,o)\subset(X,o)
be a reduced embedded curve germ, and
\phi
an embedded good resolution. Then
l^{\prime}_{C}\in{\mathscr{S}}^{\prime}\setminus\{0\}
, hence a convenient multiple of it is in
{\mathscr{S}}\setminus\{0\}
. Cf. also with Remark
2.2.2
.

(C)
Let
f:(X,o)\to(\mathbb{C},0)
be a germ, then in any good embedded resolution
F:={\rm div}_{\phi,E}(f)\in{\mathscr{S}}\setminus\{0\}
.

If
\mathfrak{a}
is as in
(A)
, and we fix a resolution
\phi
with
\mathfrak{a}\cdot{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}={\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(-F)
, then for any generic element

f
of
\mathfrak{a}
one has
{\rm div}_{\phi,E}(f)=F
.

(D)
Conversely, let us fix a semigroup element
F\in{\mathscr{S}}\setminus\{0\}
, a divisor of a resolution of some analytic germ
(X,o)
.
If
(X,o)
is rational then there exists a germ
f
such that

{\rm div}_{\phi,E}(f)=F
, but for an arbitrary singularity
(X,o)
this is not the case. However, if we fix the topological type of
(X,o)
, identified by
M
or by some resolution graph

\Gamma
, then there exists an analytic type, say
(X_{F},o)
supported on
M
and a germ
f_{F}:(X_{F},0)\to(\mathbb{C},0)
such that
{\rm div}_{\phi_{F},E_{F}}(f_{F})=F
.

(E)
The correspondence
\mathfrak{a}\mapsto F
given by

\mathfrak{a}\cdot{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}={\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(-F)
(via some good resolution)
is not injective. E.g., if we start with such an
\mathfrak{a}
and
F
, then
\phi_{*}{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(-F)
is the integral closure
\bar{\mathfrak{a}}
of

\mathfrak{a}
in
{\mathscr{O}}_{X,o}
, and
\bar{\mathfrak{a}}\cdot{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}=\mathfrak{a}\cdot{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}={\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(-F)

too. Moreover, for any other ideal
\mathfrak{b}
the identity

\bar{\mathfrak{a}}=\bar{\mathfrak{b}}
is equivalent with

\mathfrak{b}\cdot{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}=\mathfrak{a}\cdot{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}
.

2.2.
The multiplier ideals

2.2.1
.

The definition of multiplier ideals,
jumping numbers and jumping multiplicities.

We fix a normal surface singularity
(X,o)
, a good resolution
\phi
, and a cycle
F\in{\mathscr{S}}\setminus\{0\}
.

Following
[
12
,
22
]

we define the
multiplier ideal
associated with
F
and a real number

c\in{\mathbb{R}}
as

\mathfrak{I}(c,F)=\phi_{*}{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(-\lfloor Z_{K}+cF\rfloor)\subset{\mathscr{O}}_{X,o}.

For simplicity, we denote the divisor
\lfloor Z_{K}+cF\rfloor
by
L(c)
.
Though
Z_{K}
and
F
are combinatorial cycles associated with the graph
\Gamma
, the ideals
\mathfrak{I}(c,F)
might depend essentially on the analytic structure of the germ
(X,o)
.
Since
\phi:\widetilde{X}\setminus E\rightarrow X\setminus o
is an isomorphism and
L(c)
is supported on
E
,
we obtain that
\mathfrak{I}(c,F)={\mathscr{O}}_{X,o}
whenever
L(c)\leq 0
, and
\mathfrak{I}(c,F)\subset{\mathscr{O}}_{X,o}
is an
\mathfrak{m}_{X,o}
–primary ideal otherwise.
Also,
\phi_{*}{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(-L(c))=\phi_{*}{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(-\max\{0,L(c)\})
(see e.g. Lemma
3.1.1
).
As a corollary we get that

\dim({\mathscr{O}}_{X,o}/\mathfrak{I}(c,F))<\infty

If
c\leq c^{\prime}
, then

\mathfrak{I}(c,F)\supseteq\mathfrak{I}(c^{\prime},F)
.
Whenever we have strict inclusion

\mathfrak{I}(c-\varepsilon,F)\supsetneq\mathfrak{I}(c,F)

for arbitrarily small
\varepsilon>0
, we say that
c
is a
jumping number
, and its

jumping multiplicity
is:

m(c):=\dim(\mathfrak{I}(c-\varepsilon,F)/\mathfrak{I}(c,F)).

Define the jumping divisor corresponding to
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
(cf.
[
2
,
3
,
44
]
) as

D_{c}:=L(c)-L(c-\varepsilon)\ \ \ (1\gg\varepsilon>0).

Obviously,
D_{c}
is a reduced divisor and it is ‘periodical’:
D_{c+1}=D_{c}
.
In fact, if
Z_{K}=\sum k_{i}E_{i}
and
F=\sum m_{i}E_{i}
, then
E_{i}
is in the support of
D_{c}
precisely when
k_{i}+cm_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
This shows that in any bounded interval there are only finitely many values
c
with
D_{c}\not=0
, hence with
m(c)>0
. Note that
D_{c}\not=0
usually does not imply
m(c)>0
(see e.g. Example
3.2.1
).
We also define

\Delta_{c}:=\{Z_{K}+cF\}.

Then
any
E_{i}
is either in the support of
D_{c}
or of
\Delta_{c}
, but not in both.

Since
F\in{\mathscr{S}}\setminus\{0\}
, hence all the coefficient of
F
are strict positive,
if
c\ll 0
then
Z_{K}+cF\leq 0
, and

\mathfrak{I}(c,F)={\mathscr{O}}_{X,o}
. In particular,
m(c)=0
for
c\ll 0
. Thus, in any interval
of type
(-\infty,c_{0}]
there are only finitely many jumping numbers
c
.

Remark 2.2.2
.

The assumption that
F
is integral is just a convenient choice.
In all our arguments regarding the cycle
F
,
we can equally start with any
F\in{\mathscr{S}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\setminus\{0\}
, or, we can replace the integral
F
by any
rF
, where
r\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
. Indeed,
the effect of the replacement
F
by
rF
is

m_{rF}(c)=m_{F}(rc)
.

3.
Jumping multiplicities and the Hilbert function

3.1.
Multiplier ideals and the Hilbert function

Let us start with the following (known) lemma.

Lemma 3.1.1
.

Fix some
l\in L
and write it as
l=P-N
, with

P,N\in L_{\geq 0}
in such a way that in their support they have no common
E_{i}
–component. Then

(a)
H^{0}(\widetilde{X},{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(-P))\to H^{0}(\widetilde{X},{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(-l))
is an isomorphism,

(b)
h^{1}({\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(-P))-h^{1}({\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(-l))=\chi(N)-(N,l)
.

Proof.

Consider the short exact sequence of sheaves

0\to{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(-P)\to{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(-l)\to{\mathscr{O}}_{N}(-l)\to 0
.

Then

(c_{1}({\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(-P)),E_{i})=(-P,E_{i})\leq 0
for all
E_{i}
in the support of
N
.
Therefore by the
h^{0}
-vanishing theorem (see
[
30
, Theorem 6.4.2.]
),
the dual of the Grauert-Riemenschneider vanishing,
we obtain
H^{0}(N,{\mathscr{O}}_{N}(-P+N))=0
. Then use the
long cohomological exact sequence.
∎

This shows that for any
l\in L
we have

H^{0}(\widetilde{X},{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(-l))=H^{0}(\widetilde{X},{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(-\max\{0,l\}))
, which is canonically a subspace of

H^{0}(\widetilde{X},{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}})
.
The Hilbert function
\mathfrak{h}(l)
(associated with the divisorial filtration of the resolution) is defined as

L\ni l\mapsto\mathfrak{h}(l):=\dim\,H^{0}(\widetilde{X},{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}})/H^{0}(\widetilde{X},{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(-\max\{0,l\}))=\dim\,{\mathscr{O}}_{X,o}/\phi_{*}{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(-l).

Note that

\mathfrak{h}(L(c))=\dim{\mathscr{O}}_{X,o}/\mathfrak{I}(c,F)
, hence

(3.1.2)

m(c)=\mathfrak{h}(L(c))-\mathfrak{h}(L(c-\varepsilon)).

3.1.3
.

Some general comments regarding the formula (
3.1.2
).

Usually, under the assumption that we fix the combinatorial data
\Gamma
,
the Hilbert function
\mathfrak{h}
might depend
essentially on the choice of the analytic type of
(X,o)
.
However, for any rational singularity, for any fixed resolution with graph
\Gamma
,
\mathfrak{h}(l)
can be determined from
\Gamma
for any
l\in L
. In fact, this is true for any
singularity whose link is a rational homology sphere and its analytic type is
splice quotient
.
This family of normal surface singularities was introduced by Neumann and Wahl
[
32
]

and it includes (among others) all rational, minimally elliptic and weighted homogeneous singularities
(with
\mathbb{Q}HS^{3}
link).
For such germs,
\mathfrak{h}(l)
follows combinatorially via the following two steps:
by
[
30
, (8.2.10)]

\mathfrak{h}(l)
can be computed from the multivariable (analytic)
Poincaré series
P_{0}({\mathbf{t}})=\sum_{l}\mathfrak{p}(l){\bf t}^{l}
of
(X,o)
as

\mathfrak{h}(l)=\sum_{\tilde{l}\not\geq l}\mathfrak{p}(\tilde{l})

(this is true for any normal surface singularities), while
by
[
30
, Corollary 8.5.35]

P_{0}({\mathbf{t}})=Z_{0}({\mathbf{t}})
, where
Z_{0}({\mathbf{t}})

is the topological multivariable Poincaré series (or zeta function) associated with
\Gamma
, for its expression see Proposition below, (at this step the splice quotient assumption is crucial).
(For the definition and certain properties of analytic and topological multivariable
Poincaré series see also

[
6
,
7
,
8
]
.)

In particular, in principle, for all splice quotient singularities (and for any

F\in\mathscr{S}\setminus\{0\}
) all the jumping multiplicities
\{m(c)\}_{c\in\mathbb{R}}
are computable from
\Gamma
and
F
.
Here is the precise statement.

Proposition 3.1.4
.

Assume that the link of
(X,o)
is a rational homology sphere and
(X,o)
is splice quotient
(associated with its resolution graph
\Gamma
). Consider free variables
{\bf t}=\{t_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathscr{V}}}

indexed by the set of vertices, and for any
l^{\prime}=\sum l^{\prime}_{i}E_{i}\in L^{\prime}
write
{\bf t}^{l^{\prime}}=\prod_{i}t_{i}^{l^{\prime}_{i}}
.
Let
Z({\bf t})=\sum_{l^{\prime}\in{\mathscr{S}}^{\prime}}\mathfrak{p}(l^{\prime}){\bf t}^{l^{\prime}}

be the Taylor expansion at the origin of the rational function

\prod_{i\in{\mathscr{V}}}(1-{\bf t}^{E^{*}_{i}})^{\kappa_{i}-2},

where
\kappa_{i}
is the valency of the vertex
i
. Let
Z_{0}({\bf t})
be its integral part, namely

Z_{0}({\bf t})=\sum_{l\in{\mathscr{S}}}\mathfrak{p}(l){\bf t}^{l}\in\mathbb{Z}[{\mathscr{S}}]
.
Then for any
c\in\mathbb{R}
one has

m(c)=\sum_{l\geq{L(c)-D_{c}},\ l\not\geq L(c)}\ \ \mathfrak{p}(l).

However, we wish to emphasise, that in the non-rational splice quotient cases, if we keep the topological type (say
\Gamma
) and we modify the analytic structure of
(X,o)
(into a non-splice quotient one) then
the Hilbert function and the jumping multiplicities might change.

But, in the rational case (since any analytic type is splice quotient)
independently of the analytic realizations of the topological type, the Hilbert function and
m(c)
are combinatorial expressible from
\Gamma
and
F
(via the above recipe).

We also observe that in the general splice quotient case the explicit computation via the above recipe can be rather technical and computational.

In this note, for
c\geq 0
and for arbitrary
(X,o)

we run a completely different approach (inspired from mixed Hodge theory). In fact, for
c>0
we will be able to determine

m(c)

completely combinatorially
for any
(X,o)
and
F
, independently of the analytic structure of
(X,o)
,
and we provide explicit formulae. The number
m(0)
might depend on the analytic structure, but we determine it completely.

Now, in fact, we arrived to a main point of the discussion:
the behaviour of
m(c)
is very different for
c>0
and for
c\leq 0
.
As we already said,
m(c)
for
c>0
is topological, independent of the analytic structure of
(X,o)
. For
c\leq 0
, the knowledge of
\Gamma
and
F
is not enough, in general the analytic structure of
(X,o)

plays a crucial role. Hence, in this
c\leq 0
case, the study of
the jumping numbers and multiplicities goes in parallel with the study of (yet open problem of classification of) analytic structures supported on a fixed topological type.

In subsection
3.2
we provide some concrete examples in the case
c<0

to show how the variation on the analytic structure enters in the picture.
Moreover, for weighted homogeneous germs we determine
m(c)
for any
c\in\mathbb{R}
in terms of the (analytic) Poincaré series of
(X,o)
.
But a more complete study regarding
\{m(c)\}_{c<0}
will be presented in forthcoming manuscript including several other analytic aspects and connections.

The difference between the negative and positive half-lines is already visible via
the following formula (cf. the first part of the proof
of Proposition
3.3.4
).

(3.1.5)

\sum_{c^{\prime}\leq c}m(c^{\prime})=\dim\,{\mathscr{O}}_{X,o}/\mathfrak{I}(c,F)=\mathfrak{h}(L(c))=\chi(L(c))+p_{g}-h^{1}({\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(-L(c)).

Here
p_{g}=p_{g}(X,o)
is the geometric genus of
(X,o)
,
defined as
\dim\,H^{1}(\widetilde{X},{\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}})
. Usually it is not a topological invariant of
(X,o)
, however, it is a
c
–independent ‘universal’
analytic invariant. Thus, let us focus on the
difference
\mathfrak{h}(L(c))-p_{g}
. It has two terms,
\chi(L(c))
is combinatorial,
however,
h^{1}({\mathscr{O}}_{\widetilde{X}}(-L(c))
is not. The point is that for

c\geq 0
this last term is zero (see the second part of the proof of Proposition
3.3.4
) by the Generalized Grauert-Riemenshneider vanishing theorem, independently of the analytic structure of
(X,o)
. On the other hand, for
c<0
this term can be nonzero, and usually depends essentially on the choice of analytic structure
(see also examples from subsection
3.2
, and Remark
3.4.6
).
We can say that
c>0
is the ‘Riemann–Roch zone’, while
c\leq 0
is the
‘
h^{0}
–zone’.

.

On the other hand,

3.4.
The weighted homogenous case

Our goal is to determine
\sum_{c\in\mathbb{R}}m(c)t^{c}
completely and to discuss some examples.

3.4.1
.

The affine algebraic (complex) variety
Y\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is called weighted homogeneous with integral weights
\{w_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}

if it is stable with respect to the action
of
\mathbb{C}^{*}
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
t*(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=(t^{w_{1}}x_{1},\ldots,t^{w_{n}}x_{n})
.
We assume that (i) each
w_{i}
is positive (that is, the action is
good
:
the origin is contained in the closure of any orbit), and
(ii)
{\rm gcd}\{w_{i}\}_{i}=1
(that is, the action is
effective
: if
t*x=x
for all
x\in Y
then
t=1
).
For such a variety
Y
the affine coordinate ring is
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
–graded,
R=\oplus_{\ell\geq 0}R_{\ell}
. (Usually the normality of
R
is not guaranteed.)

A local normal surface singularity
(X,o)
is called weighted homogeneous if there exists a normal affine surface
Y
with a good and effective
\mathbb{C}^{*}
action, and a singular point
y\in Y
such that
(X,o)
and
(Y,y)
are analytically isomorphic as complex analytic germs. In this case, the local algebra of
(X,o)
is graded:
we use the same notation
{\mathscr{O}}_{X,o}=\oplus_{\ell\geq 0}R_{\ell}
.

We fix such a singularity and let
\phi
be its minimal good resolution. The graph is
star-shaped with
\nu
legs (strings).
Let
-b_{0}
and
g
denote the Euler decoration and the genus of
the central vertex
v_{0}
. Each leg for
1\leq j\leq\nu
has Euler decorations

-b_{j1},\ldots,-b_{js_{j}}
,
b_{ji}\geq 2
, determined by the Hirzebruch continued fraction
\alpha_{i}/\omega_{i}=[[b_{j1},\ldots,b_{js_{j}}]]
, where

{\rm gcd}(\alpha_{j},\omega_{j})=1
,
0<\omega_{j}<\alpha_{j}
. In the graph,
for each
j
the central vertex
v_{0}
is connected to
v_{j1}

by an edge. We assume that either
g>0
or
\nu\geq 3
.
(Otherwise, the graph is a string with all
g_{i}=0
, hence
(X,o)
is a quotient singularity, or a numerically log terminal singularity with all

; this case
is treated in Example
3.1.9
and in subsection

The link is a Seifert 3-manifold with Seifert invariants
(b_{0},g;\{(\alpha_{j},\omega_{j})\}_{j})
. It has several important numerical invariants (for details see e.g.
[
35
]
and
[
30
]

\bullet
the orbifold Euler number

(

(\,,\,)

\bullet
the orbifold Euler characteristic
\chi:=2-2g-\sum_{j}(\alpha_{j}-1)/\alpha_{j}

\bullet

\mathfrak{r}=\chi/e

is called the log discrepancy of
(X,o)

\bullet
the order of

\bullet
the order of

\bullet

\bullet
the

Associated with

5.
Jumping multiplicities and Hodge spectrum

5.1.
The Milnor package and Hodge spectrum of an analytic germ

5.1.1
.

Let
(X,o)
be a normal surface singularity and
f:(X,o)\to(\mathbb{C},0)

an analytic germ which defines an isolated singularity
(C,o):=(f^{-1}(0),o)\subset(X,o)
.
Let
F_{o}
be the Milnor fiber of
f
. It is a 1-dimensional Stein space, hence its
cohomology
H^{n}:=H^{n}(F_{o},\mathbb{C})
can be nonzero only for
n=0,1
. Moreover,
F_{o}
is connected, hence

H^{0}=\mathbb{C}
. Let
T^{*}
be the monodromy operator
T^{*}:H^{*}\to H^{*}
.
In fact,
T^{0}={\rm id}_{\mathbb{C}}
. The operator
T^{n}
defines a generalized eigenspace decomposition

H^{n}=\oplus_{\lambda}H^{n}_{\lambda}
. The corresponding dimensions are codified in the monodromy zeta function

\zeta(t)=\prod_{\lambda}(t-\lambda)^{\dim\,H^{1}_{\lambda}-\dim\,H^{0}_{\lambda}}=\det(t\cdot Id-T^{1})/\det(t\cdot Id-T^{0}).

If
\phi
is a log embedded resolution of
(C,o)\subset(X,o)
(and we use the notations of

4.1.1
)
then by A’Campo’s Theorem
[
1
]

\zeta(t)=\prod_{v\in{\mathscr{V}}}(1-t^{m_{v}})^{2g_{v}-2+\#({\mathscr{W}}_{v})}.

In particular,
\dim H^{0}_{1}=1
and
\dim H^{1}_{1}=\#{\mathscr{A}}+2g+2h-1
.

By
[
40
,
36
,
37
]

H^{1}
carries a mixed Hodge structure, set
H^{pq}:=Gr^{W}_{p+q}Gr^{p}_{F}H^{1}
.
The semisimple part of
T^{1}
acts on each
H^{pq}
, let
H^{pq}_{\lambda}
be the eigenspace with dimension

h^{pq}_{\lambda}
.
Then the spectrum of
H^{1}
is defined as
Sp(H^{1}):=\sum_{p,q,\lfloor\alpha\rfloor=p}h^{pq}_{e^{2\pi i\alpha}}t^{\alpha}
.
One defines similarly the spectrum
Sp(H^{0})
of
H^{0}
too, but in fact it is
t^{0}
.
Hence, the ‘total’ (Euler characterisitc type) spectrum of
f
, as lifting of
\zeta
, is

Sp(f,t):=Sp(H^{1})-Sp(H^{0})=-t^{0}+\sum_{p,q,\lfloor\alpha\rfloor=p}\ h^{pq}_{e^{2\pi i\alpha}}\ t^{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}[\mathbb{Q}].

We write
Sp(f,t)=\sum_{\alpha}\sigma(\alpha)t^{\alpha}.

It turns out that
\sigma(\alpha)=0
whenever
\alpha\not\in[0,2)
,

\sigma(0)=g+h-1
and
\sigma(1)=\#{\mathscr{A}}+g+h-1
. Moreover, for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, one has the symmetry

\sigma(\alpha)=\sigma(2-\alpha)
. Hence
Sp(f,t)
is completely determined by

Sp_{[0,1]}(f,t):=\sum_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\sigma(\alpha)t^{\alpha}
.

In the next theorem we fix an embedded resolution and its decorated graph (as in
4.1.1
).

Theorem 5.1.2
.

[
39
,
27
]

Sp_{[0,1]}(f,t)=(g+h-1)t^{0}+(\#{\mathscr{A}}+g+h-1)t^{1}+\sum_{e\in{\mathscr{E}}}\ \sum_{0<s<m_{e}}t^{\frac{s}{m_{e}}}+\sum_{v\in{\mathscr{V}}}\ \sum_{0<s<m_{v}}(R^{s}_{v}+g_{v}-1)t^{\frac{s}{m_{v}}},

Note that the right hand side is exactly

Hence, Theorems

and

.

Assume that
(X,o)
is a numerically Gorenstein singularity, and

Though both invariants

Consider the rational graph
\Gamma
as in Example
