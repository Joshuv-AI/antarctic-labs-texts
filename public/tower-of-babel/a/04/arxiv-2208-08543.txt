On the Relationship between CH 3 ​ OD \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD} Abundance and Temperature in the Orion KL Nebula
Olivia H. Wilkins, Geoffrey A. Blake

Abstract
The relative abundances of singly-deuterated methanol isotopologues, [ CH 2 ​ DOH \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{DOH} ]/[ CH 3 ​ OD \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD} ] , in star-forming regions deviate from the statistically expected ratio of 3. In Orion KL, the nearest high-mass star-forming region to Earth, the singly-deuterated methanol ratio is about 1, and the cause for this observation has been explored through theory for nearly three decades. We present high-angular resolution observations of Orion KL using the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array to map small-scale changes in CH 3 ​ OD \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD} column density across the nebula, which provide a new avenue to examine the deuterium chemistry during star and planet formation. By considering how CH 3 ​ OD \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD} column densities vary with temperature, we find evidence of chemical processes that can significantly alter the observed column densities. The astronomical data are compared with existing theoretical work and support D-H exchange between CH 3 ​ OH \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OH} and heavy water (i.e., HDO and D 2 ​ O \text{D}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O} ) at methanol’s hydroxyl site in the icy mantles of dust grains. The enhanced CH 3 ​ OD \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD} column densities are localized to the Hot Core-SW region, a pattern that may be linked to the coupled evolution of ice mantel chemistry and star formation in giant molecular clouds. This work provides new perspectives on deuterated methanol chemistry in Orion KL and informs considerations that may guide future theoretical, experimental, and observational work.

Introduction

The relative abundances of site-specific stable isotopologues
, particularly those involving deuterated compounds, are powerful tools that can be used to trace chemical evolution in the interstellar medium, and
during
star and planet formation. For example, the relative abundances of heavy water
(i.e., HDO and
\text{D}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
)
in comets and meteorites can provide insights into the processing of water between the primordial molecular cloud and present-day Earth.
(
Altwegg et al. 2015
;
Altwegg et al. 2017
)

Interstellar compounds such as
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OH}

{\sim}10^{-5}
,
(

)
and that ratio is a function of temperature, with higher D/H ratios signalling formation in colder, typically denser, environments.
(
Fontani et al. 2015

In many high-mass and low-mass protostars alike, the deuterium chemistry of methanol—namely, the relative abundances of the singly-deuterated isotopomers
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{DOH}
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
—has presented itself as a mystery. Methanol is one of the simplest complex (having
\geq
6 atoms) organic molecules, and it is found at every stage of star formation, from cold cloud cores and hot cores/corinos to outflows and circumstellar disks.
(
Herbst and van Dishoeck 2009
;
Walsh et al. 2016
)
As such, it is commonly used as a tracer of other complex organics.

There has been extensive debate about whether the apparent

\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}

(
Charnley et al. 1997

Rodgers and Charnley 2002

\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{DOH}

\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}

\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}

\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}

provided methanol is efficiently regenerated in the dissociative recombination of protonated methanol with electrons

This work also finds
that
D-H exchange
on the methyl site is inefficient.

Methods

2.1
Observations

Observations of Orion KL were taken in ALMA Band 4 during Cycle 5 (project code: ADS/JAO.ALMA#2017.1.01149, PI: Wilkins) on 2017 December 14, completely on the main 12-m array. The pointing center was set to
\alpha_{\mbox{\scriptsize J2000}}=05^{\mbox{\scriptsize h}}35^{\mbox{\scriptsize m}}14.\!\!^{\mbox{\scriptsize s}}50
,
\delta_{\mbox{\scriptsize J2000}}=-05^{\circ}22^{\prime}30.\!\!^{\prime\prime}9
. These observations employed 49 antennas during one execution block. All spectra were obtained in a single local oscillator set-up consisting of 10 spectral windows; as such, the uncertainties for quantities derived from these spectra are dominated by thermal noise and mostly unaffected by calibration uncertainty. Of these spectral windows, the targeted
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
lines were contained in three spectral windows with a spectral resolution of 244 kHz (
\sim
0.5 km s
-1

\lambda

\lambda\sim 2
mm is the wavelength
), and the primary beam was 39.1
′′
. The on-source integration time was 2062 s. Precipitable water vapor was 3.7 mm, and typical system temperatures were around 75-125 K.

Calibration was completed using standard
CASA
(version 5.1.1-5) calibration pipeline scripts. The source J0423
-
0120 was used as a calibrator for amplitude, atmosphere, bandpass, pointing, and WVR (Water Vapor Radiometer) variations, and J0541
-
0211 was used as the phase and WVR calibrator.

The

3.2
Grain-Surface Processes

The rapid rise in gas-phase
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
column density between
\sim
110 K and
\sim
120 K is consistent with
D-H exchange
between methanol and heavy water (HDO,
\text{D}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
) on the ices at
\sim
100 K.
Souda et al. 2003
experimentally investigated hydrogen bonding between water and methanol in low-temperature ices warmed from 15 K to 200 K under ultrahigh vacuum conditions. They observed that when
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OH}
was adsorbed onto
\text{D}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
ice, secondary
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
ions—evidence of
D-H exchange
at the hydroxyl site—sputtered off the ice analogue surfaces predominantly between 140 and 175 K. Follow-up analyses by
Kawanowa et al. 2004
describes this as a “rapid and almost complete H/D exchange” to yield the sputtered
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
species. The fact that we see a similar sudden increase in
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
column densities at similar temperatures (Figure
3
b, dotted line), with discrepancies owing to the differences in pressure between ultrahigh vacuum and even the densest regions of interstellar medium, supports a similar rapid exchange in Orion KL.

Models of
D-H exchange
between water and methanol in ices by
Faure et al. 2015
successfully
reproduced
gas-phase
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OH}
deuterium fractionation in Orion KL using initial ice abundances of
n_{S}({}{}\mathrm{CH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}\mathrm{OH})=2.0\times 10^{-6}n_{{}\mathrm{H}}
,
n_{S}({}\mathrm{HDO})=3.0\times 10^{-7}n_{{}\mathrm{H}}
, and
n_{S}({}{}\mathrm{CH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}\mathrm{OD})=6.0\times 10^{-9}n_{{}\mathrm{H}}
.
Taking these initial ice abundances, we modeled the change in gas-phase
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
column density following rapid D-H exchange on the ices and subsequent desorption. Specifically, we assumed an initial ice column density of
N_{S}({}{}\mathrm{CH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}\mathrm{OD})=6.0\times 10^{-9}N_{{}\mathrm{H}}=6.0\times 10^{14}\mbox{ cm}^{-2}
, since
N_{{}\mathrm{H}}\sim 10^{23}
cm
-2
across Orion KL (including the Hot Core, Compact Ridge, and Extended Ridge),
(
Neill et al. 2013
;
Crockett et al. 2014
)
and an initial gas-phase column density of
N({}{}\mathrm{CH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}\mathrm{OD})=1\times 10^{16}\mbox{ cm}^{-2}
, based on the column densities measured at 100 K in this work after subtracting the underlying power-law in eq
4
.

The enhancement of
gas-phase

\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
column density from
D-H exchange
with water was then modeled.
In the absence of directly analogous temperature-programmed desorption measurements of
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OH}
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
themselves, we fit the
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
curve of
Souda et al. 2003
(Figure
3
b, dotted line) between 110 K and 145 K. The relative intensity amplitude was normalized to the column densities observed for
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
in our Orion KL observations, resulting in a relationship of the form

N_{S}^{\prime}({}{}\mathrm{CH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}\mathrm{OD})=6.2\times 10^{14}T-6.0\times 10^{16}

(5)

where
N_{S}^{\prime}
is the additional (solid)
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
available [cm
-2
] for desorption at a given temperature
T
.
The desorption rate coefficient is expressed as

k_{des}=\nu_{des}e^{(-E_{d}/T)}

(6)

where the pre-exponential factor
\nu_{des}
and the binding energy
E_{d}
are taken to be approximately the values for annealed amorphous solid water—
2.0\times 10^{12}
s
-1
and 5200 K, respectively—under the assumption that the methanol desorbs with water, which is in excess.
(
Sandford and Allamandola 1988
;
Fraser et al. 2001
;
Brown and Bolina 2007
;
Dulieu et al. 2013
;
Faure et al. 2015
;
Penteado et al. 2017
)

It follows that the change in gas-phase
N({}{}\mathrm{CH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}\mathrm{OD})
is approximated by multiplying the rate coefficient (eq
6
) by the total solid
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
column density, which includes the additional solid
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
available following D-H exchange (eq
5
) at temperature
T
. Thus, the rate at which
N({}{}\mathrm{CH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}\mathrm{OD})
changes in the gas phase between 100 and 150 K is approximated by

\frac{d}{dt}N({}{}\mathrm{CH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}\mathrm{OD})=k_{des}\left[N_{S}({}{}\mathrm{CH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}\mathrm{OD})+N_{S}^{\prime}({}{}\mathrm{CH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}\mathrm{OD})\right]

(7)

using temperature steps of 1 K, the corresponding
time steps for which were determined by

\Delta t=\left(\frac{T-T_{0}}{T_{max}-T_{0}}\right)^{1/n}t_{h}

(8)

where
\Delta t
is the time elapsed since
t=0
;
T_{0}
and
T_{max}
are the initial (10 K) and maximum (
300
K) temperatures, respectively;
t_{h}
is the heating timescale; and
n
is the order of heating, which is assumed to be 2 following the previous work.
(
Taniguchi et al. 2019
)

The initial gas and solid
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
column densities were assumed, respectively, to be
N({}{}\mathrm{CH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}\mathrm{OD})=9.0\times 10^{15}
cm
-2
(approximated from the
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
density profile with the underlying power-law subtracted) and
N_{S}({}{}\mathrm{CH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}\mathrm{OD})=6.0\times 10^{-9}N_{{}\mathrm{H}}
cm
-2
(based on assumptions used by
Faure et al. 2015
in their D-H exchange models).

The resulting desorption model from eq
7
with
t_{h}=10^{3}
yr is shown by the black dashed line in Figure
3
a. Longer timescales (i.e.,
t_{h}\geq 10^{4}
yr) characteristic of massive YSOs do not follow the increasing
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
profile as closely. Although potential internal heating sources have been suggested within the Hot Core-SW,
(
Wilkins et al. 2022
)

Li et al. 2020
conclude that this region, part of the “elongated ridge” comprising the Hot Core and Source I, is predominantly heated externally by shocks induced by the Orion KL explosion, which took place about 500 years ago. Furthermore, the models by
Faure et al. 2015
suggest the
D-H exchange
in Orion KL reaches steady-state in
{<}10^{3}
yr. After
D-H exchange
, the power-law relationship from eq
4
applies, but with a coefficient of
3.2\times 10^{14}

(dotted blue curve in Fig.
3
a)
.

The hypothesis that the
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
column density profile in Figure
3
is the result of rapid
D-H exchange
on the grains relies on two assumptions regarding temperature. First, we assume that the rotational temperature
T_{\rm rot}
is an appropriate estimate for the kinetic temperature
T_{kin}
. This assumption is based on the fact that the Compact Ridge
, a spatial component toward the southwestern region of Orion KL that is characterized as being rich in oxygen-bearing molecules,
has a fairly high density of
{\sim}10^{6}
cm
-3
,
(
Blake et al. 1987
;
Genzel and Stutzki 1989
)
implying that LTE is a reasonable assumption. Second, we assume that the dust and gas are thermally coupled.
Li et al. 2003
found that other quiescent regions (no infrared sources, no evident outflows) in the Orion Molecular Cloud are thermally coupled. Models by
Bruderer et al. 2009
also support coupling between dust and gas temperature at
the relevant
densities.
This gas-grain thermal coupling was also demonstrated in models of several massive star-forming regions, including the Orion KL Compact Ridge, by
Garrod and Herbst 2006
.
(
Garrod and Herbst 2006
)
As such, it is reasonable to assume that the gas temperatures shown in Figure
3
are also representative of the temperatures of the dust, on which methanol forms, and that any decoupling between the dust and gas is negligible for the purposes of this discussion.

Another mechanism considered was the neutral-radical reaction with the hydroxyl radical (OH); however, this reaction is too slow at
\sim
200 K to account for the observed patterns (see Section S4 of the Supporting Information).

Instead, we propose that the drop in enhanced
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
column density is the result of environment rather than chemistry. That is, the enhanced
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
column density profile is limited to warm gas in the Hot Core-SW with temperatures that max out around 200 K. The enhanced deuteration in this region, brought about by temperature-dependent surface D-H exchange, may be the result of evolutionary state. However, this region is not directly associated with any known YSOs, including SMA1 (a young, high-mass protostar) and C22 (a possible hot core).
(
Beuther et al. 2006
;
Friedel and Widicus Weaver 2011
)
Although there is no known self-illuminated source (e.g., embedded protostar) in the Hot Core-SW to drive grain warming and associated D-H exchange in ices, it has been suggested that there is a potential (hidden) source of internal heating there.
(
Wilkins et al. 2022
)
And, the complex history and star formation patterns in Orion KL will lead to quite varied thermal histories of various sub-regions of the giant molecular cloud complex. Thus, dedicated work to elucidate the nature of the Hot Core-SW region is needed to better understand how this environment could affect the observed chemistry.

3.4
Methyl Group Chemistry

If the hump observed in the
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
column density versus temperature profile is indeed evidence of surface
D-H exchange
at the hydroxyl site, then we would expect a smooth profile (i.e., without a similar hump) in the profile of
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{DOH}
. Unfortunately, we do not have sufficient
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{DOH}
transitions in our data to test this hypothesis directly. However,
Carroll 2018
mapped the physical parameters of
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{DCN}
toward Orion KL using data from ALMA (project: ADS/JAO.ALMA#2013.1.01034
, PI: Crockett
). Figure
5
shows a histogram of the
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{DCN}
column density and rotational temperature using these data where they overlap with
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
emission in the current observations. In this plot, we see a consistent power-law relationship between temperature and column density, which supports the conclusion by
Osamura et al. 2004
that the methyl groups of complex organics would not undergo grain
-surface
or gas-phase
D-H exchange
. In other words, the hump visible in Figure
3
a is indeed likely the result of chemistry specific to the hydroxyl site of methanol.
Future dedicated observations of
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{DOH}
lines with ALMA in this region are necessary to confirm this hypothesis.

3.5
Comparison to Past Studies

The evidence presented here adds to the growing list of observational and theoretical evidence in favor of a grain-surface mechanism for
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
enrichment in massive star-forming regions. A key difference between this work and that of past observations is that, here, we map
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
column density across much of Orion KL, including the Compact Ridge, whereas past work derives one value for the Compact Ridge as a whole. Furthermore, resolving the small-scale structure of
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
column densities (and temperature) allows us to look at how column density is related to the line-of-site temperature, something that has not yet been extensively investigated through observations but is now possible with the sensitivity and
spatial
grasp of ALMA.

Computational models to assess
D-H exchange
generally have investigated
temporal
variations in relative
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
abundances at a single temperature or have looked at singly-deuterated methanol chemistry across a range of temperatures but less than 140 K.
(
Osamura et al. 2004
;
Faure et al. 2015
;
Bøgelund et al. 2018
)

Previous models of grain-surface D-H exchange in Orion KL have assumed methanol is completely sublimated at temperatures
>
110 K, whereas our observations suggest D-H exchange on the ice may be important up to 125 K.
Thus, the observations presented here probe a temperature regime beyond that of existing astrochemical models and call for revised models to investigate D/H chemistry at higher temperatures.
Specifically, temperature-dependent models of grain-surface D-H exchange in which methanol sublimates completely at higher temperatures (e.g., 125 K) are needed to more robustly explain the patterns observed in this work.

Perhaps the leading criticism of proposed grain-surface chemistry prompting the enhancement of
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
abundances relative to
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{DOH}
is that such processes would require a large initial [HDO]/[
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
] ratio
. For example, models by
Charnley et al. 1997
suggest that the initial [HDO]/[
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
] ratio in the ice mantles would need to be

\sim
0.1,
(
Charnley et al. 1997
)
which is significantly larger than the ratio of
\sim
0.003 reported by
Neill et al. 2013
for compact regions of Orion KL. However,
Thi et al. 2010
suggest that the [HDO]/[
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
] ratio can exceed 0.01 in dense (
{\geq}10^{6}
cm
-3
), warm (
T>100
K) regions (such as those observed here) via neutral-neutral reactions
—such as the formation of
HDO
from
OH
+
HD
,
OD
+
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, and
OD
+
OH
—
which may be promising for the hypothesis of a grain-surface
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
enhancement. Even more promising is a model presented by
Faure et al. 2015
, who reproduced observed [
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{DOH}
]/[
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
] ratios in the Compact Ridge assuming a primitive [HDO]/[
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
] fractionation of 0.006, only a factor of 2 larger than the observed ratio reported by
Neill et al. 2013
. That is, the key initial condition is that of the deuteration state of methanol and water in the icy grain mantles, which are exceedingly difficult to measure directly with previous observational capabilities. JWST will offer greatly improved capabilities to attempt such measurements, going forward.

A remaining question, then, is what makes methanol deuteration in low-mass star-forming regions so different from that in high-mass star-forming regions?
Ratajczak et al. 2011
suggest observational biases, namely that, since high-mass objects tend to be further away than those low-mass objects where deuterium chemistry has been studied, measurements of the [
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{DOH}
]/[
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
] ratio may be affected, particularly if the spatial distributions of the two isotopomers are different. High angular resolution mapping
of high-mass star-forming regions
, like that presented in Figure
1
, would address this by comparing the
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{DOH}
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
column densities only where their emission overlapped
, as was done for different spectral components of the high-mass star-forming region NGC 6334I by
Bøgelund et al. 2018
As stated previously, the observations presented here do not have sufficient
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{DOH}
lines available to test
the spatial correlation of site-specific deuterated isotopologues, and
dedicated high angular resolution observations targeting low-energy
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{DOH}
lines are necessary to further address
this issue
.

Another conjecture for the different deuterium fractionation patterns in massive YSOs compared to low-mass star-forming regions is that there is simply less deuteration in massive protostars because of the warmer environments.
(
Bøgelund et al. 2018
)

Faure et al. 2015
reproduced relative singly-deuterated methanol abundances for Orion KL (a high-mass source) and IRAS 16293-2422 (a low-mass object) using kinetic models that were identical except for the initial deuterium fractionation ratios. They reported that the Orion KL Compact Ridge’s gas-phase deuterium chemistry could be modeled assuming similar primitive deuteration of water and methanol ices (
\sim
0.2-0.3%), whereas IRAS 16293’s gas-phase deuterated methanol chemistry required a significantly higher deuterium fractionation in methanol (12%) than water (1%). Their model shows complete methanol desorption by
\sim
110 K. Such conditions of extreme deuteration only occur in very cold, dense environments where extensive molecular depletion occurs, including that of CO and N
2
. Under such conditions, D
{}_{3}^{+}
becomes the dominant molecular ion, whose dissociative recombination results in the arrival of
hydrogen
atoms onto grain mantles
with a D/H
ratio of
>
1.

As seen in Figure

Faure et al. 2015
(dashed line) matches nicely the
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}

\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}

\sim

\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}

\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
detected by
Souda et al. 2003

Furthermore, thermal desorption strongly depends on the composition of the underlying surface.
Such questions require more robust chemical networks for deuterium chemistry as well as a better understanding of the initial chemical conditions of both high-mass and low-mass star-forming regions.

4
Conclusion

We provide observational evidence in support of rapid
D-H exchange
in methanol
-containing ices
, specifically at the hydroxyl site, between
\sim
100 and 125 K in Orion KL, using high angular resolution ALMA Band 4 observations of
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
to map the small-scale variations in
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
column density for the first time, and to compare the observed column densities to the line-of-site rotational temperatures mapped at the same angular resolution (and derived previously from
\hphantom{{}^{\text{13}}_{\text{}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{13}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{13}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{13}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{13}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OH}
).
(
Wilkins et al. 2022
)

We fit power-law relationships and toy models of
D-H exchange
at methanol’s hydroxyl (-OH) site followed by
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}

thermal
desorption to the observed
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
column density profile in Orion KL. These analyses suggest that
D-H exchange
and rapid
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
desorption increase the gas-phase
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
column density between 100 and 125 K.
Enhanced
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
column densities are limited to the Hot Core-SW, which has been previously suggested to harbor a potential source of internal heating, which could explain the enhanced
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
column densities between 125 and 185 K. In this interpretation, there is simply little gas in this region at higher temperatures that would display
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
enhancements.

Future investigations—through observations, experiments, and computational models—are needed to further constrain the peculiar D-methanol chemistry in Orion KL and other star-forming regions. The work presented here would be aided by dedicated high-resolution observations of
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{DOH}
, spectroscopic experiments measuring the kinetics of
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
formation via
D-H exchange
in heavy water ice
(HDO and
\text{D}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
)
and subsequent desorption, and temperature-dependent astrochemical models of possible
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OD}
loss at higher temperatures (185-225 K).
