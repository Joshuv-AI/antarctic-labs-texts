A FETI approach to domain decomposition for meshfree discretizations of nonlocal problems
Xiao Xu, Christian Glusa, Marta D’Elia, John T. Foster

Abstract
We propose a domain decomposition method for the efficient simulation of nonlocal problems. Our approach is based on a multi-domain formulation of a nonlocal diffusion problem where the subdomains share “nonlocal” interfaces of the size of the nonlocal horizon. This system of nonlocal equations is first rewritten in terms of minimization of a nonlocal energy, then discretized with a meshfree approximation and finally solved via a Lagrange multiplier approach in a way that resembles the finite element tearing and interconnect method. Specifically, we propose a distributed projected gradient algorithm for the solution of the Lagrange multiplier system, whose unknowns determine the nonlocal interface conditions between subdomains. Several two-dimensional numerical tests illustrate the strong and weak scalability of our algorithm, which outperforms the standard approach to the distributed numerical solution of the problem. This work is the first rigorous numerical study in a two-dimensional multi-domain setting for nonlocal operators with finite horizon and, as such, it is a fundamental step towards increasing the use of nonlocal models in large scale simulations.

Introduction

Nonlocal models have become alternatives to classical, differential equation models to describe phenomena where long-range interactions at small scales affect the global behavior of the solution. In particular, they are the models of choice in several scientific and engineering applications where multiscale effects, anomalous behavior, and discontinuities in the solutions must be taken into account for reliable predictions. Applications of interest include, fracture mechanics
[
27
,
21
]
, subsurface flow
[
6
,
26
]
, image processing
[
11
,
20
]
, stochastic processes
[
13
,
23
]
, and many others.

The multiscale nature of nonlocal models manifests itself in the form of integral operators that embed length-scales in their definition. In their simplest form, they can be defined as

\mathcal{L}u({\bf x})=2\int_{B_{\delta}({\bf x})}(u({\bf y})-u({\bf x})){\gamma}({\bf x},{\bf y})\,d{\bf y},

where
B_{\delta}({\bf x})
is a norm-induced ball (e.g. Euclidean) centered at
{\bf x}
of radius
\delta
, also known as
horizon
, that determines the extent of the nonlocal, long-range interactions, described by the
kernel

\gamma
. The integral form also allows for discontinuities in the solutions, which means that regularity requirements are much less strict than for partial differential equations (PDEs)
[
16
]
.

However, despite their modelling generality, the use of nonlocal models is still hindered by several mathematical and computational challenges. In this paper we focus on the prohibitively expensive computational cost, particularly apparent when the ratio between horizon and discretization length becomes large. Moreover, we explore the treatment of virtual nonlocal interfaces, which, for nonlocal problems, is still in its infancy
[
5
,
8
]
. Specifically, we propose a new substructuring-based domain decomposition (DD) approach for meshfree discretizations of nonlocal equations that resembles non-overlapping substructuring DD approaches for PDEs.

The first approach to nonlocal DD can be found in
[
3
]
; here, the authors consider a substructuring scheme based on the solution of a Schur complement problem in a two-domain configuration. As such, this work, while rigorous, does not address issues such as scalability, performance, and numerical treatment of floating sub-domains. In
[
9
]
the authors introduce a rigorous multi-domain DD formulation, but they do not pursue a specific algorithm for its numerical solution nor do they present a numerical study.

In this paper, we consider a meshfree discretization of the multi-domain formulation introduced in
[
9
]
and propose to solve the multi-domain system via a Lagrange multiplier approach, in a way that resembles the finite element tearing and interconnect (FETI) method
[
18
]
. Our main contributions are summarized below.

•

We introduce a meshfree discretization of the multi-domain DD formulation described in
[
9
]
and propose a solution method based on a Lagrange-multiplier approach.

•

Following FETI’s literature, we design an iterative solver for the solution of Lagrange-multiplier system that asymptotically converges in a constant number of iterations.

•

We show the improved performance of nonlocal FETI compared to standard parallel solvers for the single-domain problem.

•

We perform several weak and strong scalability studies that illustrate the efficiency of the proposed approach.

We highlight the fact that this is the first rigorous DD numerical study in a two-dimensional multi-domain setting for nonlocal operators with finite nonlocal interactions. As such, this work is the first step towards increasing the use of finite-horizon nonlocal models in large-scale simulations.

Furthermore, the impact of this work extends beyond nonlocal models and finds applicability in meshfree discretizations of PDEs. In fact, when the latter are characterized by long stencils
[
7
]
, their discretization matrices feature a wide bandwidth, proportional to the number of degrees of freedom involved in the discretization of a certain point in the domain. This situation resembles nonlocal interactions, that result in discretization matrices whose bandwidth is proportional to the radius of interactions. The approach proposed in this work extends to long-stencil meshfree PDE discretizations in a straightforward manner and its implementation is part of our current work.

3.1
Reduced system for the Lagrange multiplier and its solution

In this section we show how to derive a reduced system for the Lagrange multiplier
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and describe an iterative method for its numerical solution.

By projecting the first equation of (
3
) onto the nullspace, the following compatibility condition is derived:

\displaystyle Z^{T}(\mathbf{f}_{\epsilon}-M^{T}\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\mathbf{0}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad G^{T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\mathbf{g},

(33)

where
Z
is the matrix that spans the nullspace of
A_{\epsilon\epsilon}
,
G:=MZ
and
\mathbf{g}:=Z^{T}\mathbf{f}_{\epsilon}
. By using the condition
G^{T}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\mathbf{g}
, the generalized solution to the first block row in (
3
) can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}=A^{\dagger}_{\epsilon\epsilon}(\mathbf{f}_{\epsilon}-M^{T}\boldsymbol{\lambda})+Z\boldsymbol{\alpha},

(34)

where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is yet to be determined vector and the symbol
\dagger
denotes the
Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse
. By substituting
(
34
)
into the continuity constraints equation in
(
3
)
we obtain

\displaystyle K\boldsymbol{\lambda}-G\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\mathbf{e},

(35)

where
K=MA_{\epsilon\epsilon}^{\dagger}M^{T}
and
\mathbf{e}=MA_{\epsilon\epsilon}^{\dagger}\mathbf{f}_{\epsilon}
. The term
G\boldsymbol{\alpha}
can be further eliminated by applying the projection
P_{0}:=I-G(G^{T}G)^{\dagger}G^{T}
. Therefore, together with the compatibility equation, the reduced system for the Lagrange multiplier
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
can be written as

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}P_{0}K\boldsymbol{\lambda}&=&P_{0}\mathbf{e}\\
G^{T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}&=&\mathbf{g}.\end{array}\right.

To solve the reduced system
(\ref{eqn:reduced_system})
, we utilize the preconditioned iterative parallel algorithm reported in Algorithm
1
. We describe the choice of the preconditioner in Section
3.1.1
and the extension of the standard iterative solver to the parallel setting in Section
3.1.2
.

3.1.1
A preconditioner based on the Schur complement

We exploit the diagonal block structure of
A_{\epsilon\epsilon}
and equation
(
32
)
to write the matrix
K
as

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=MA_{\epsilon\epsilon}^{\dagger}M^{T}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}(R^{D}_{n})^{T}M_{n}R^{P}_{n}\right)\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}(R^{P}_{n})^{T}A_{n}^{\dagger}R^{P}_{n}\right)\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}(R^{P}_{n})^{T}M_{n}^{T}R^{D}_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}(R^{D}_{n})^{T}M_{n}A_{n}^{\dagger}M_{n}^{T}R^{D}_{n},

(38)

where we have used (

), (
32
) and the fact that
R^{P}_{n}(R_{n^{\prime}}^{P})^{T}=\delta_{nn^{\prime}}I_{s_{n}}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that the columns of each matrix
M_{n}
are ordered in such a way that the interior nodes precede the interface nodes on
\widehat{\Gamma}_{n}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle M_{n}=[M_{n}^{(\Omega_{n})}\quad M_{n}^{(\widehat{\Gamma}_{n})}]=[0\quad M_{n}^{(\widehat{\Gamma}_{n})}],

where
M_{n}^{(\Omega_{n})}=0
because the continuity constraints only involve boundary nodes. Similarly, matrix
A_{n}
can also be written as

\displaystyle A_{n}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A_{n}^{(\Omega_{n}\Omega_{n})}A_{n}^{(\Omega_{n}\widehat{\Gamma}_{n})}\\
A_{n}^{(\widehat{\Gamma}_{n}\Omega_{n})}A_{n}^{(\widehat{\Gamma}_{n}\widehat{\Gamma}_{n})}\end{array}\right).

Using (

can then be written as

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}(R^{D}_{n})^{T}[0\quad M_{n}^{(\widehat{\Gamma}_{n})}]A_{n}^{\dagger}[0\quad M_{n}^{(\widehat{\Gamma}_{n})}]^{T}R^{D}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}(R^{D}_{n})^{T}M^{(\widehat{\Gamma}_{n})}_{n}S_{n}^{\dagger}M^{(\widehat{\Gamma}_{n})^{T}}_{n}R^{D}_{n},

where

with respect to boundary nodes of

\displaystyle S_{n}=A_{n}^{(\widehat{\Gamma}_{n}\widehat{\Gamma}_{n})}-A_{n}^{(\widehat{\Gamma}_{n}\Omega_{n})}A_{n}^{(\Omega_{n}\Omega_{n})^{-1}}A_{n}^{(\Omega_{n}\widehat{\Gamma}_{n})}.

Since

\displaystyle Q=\sum_{n=1}^{N}(R^{D}_{n})^{T}M^{(\widehat{\Gamma}_{n})}_{n}S_{n}M^{(\widehat{\Gamma}_{n})^{T}}_{n}R^{D}_{n}.

(39)

3.1.2
The

For an arbitrary global vector

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}=

Numerical experiments

In this section we illustrate the efficiency and scalability of our approach with several two-dimensional numerical tests. We first provide details regarding the problem setting and discretization, the hardware used in our simulations, and the expected scalability properties of our algorithm.

Problem setting

Let
\Omega=[0,1]^{2}
and let
B_{\delta}({\bf x})=\{|{\bf x}-{\bf y}|_{\ell^{\infty}}\leq\delta\}
, i.e. we consider square nonlocal neighborhoods
3
3

3

Note that even though Euclidean neighborhoods are the standard choice in nonlocal modeling, some recent works have considered the use of square neighborhoods, for which the associated nonlocal problem is well posed
[
8
,
28
]
.
. In all our tests we consider the kernel
\gamma({\bf x},{\bf y})=C|{\bf x}-{\bf y}|^{-1}
with scaling constant
C\sim\delta^{-3}
, and the manufactured solution
u({\bf x})={\bf x}_{1}^{2}+{\bf x}_{2}^{2}
for which the corresponding forcing term is given by
f({\bf x})=-4
.

For the meshfree discretization described in Section
3
, we consider
L\times L
,
L\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, uniformly spaced particles in
\Omega
, resulting in a particle spacing of
h=L^{-1}
.
Each particle interacts with the square neighborhood
B_{\delta}({\bf x})
, of
(2m+1)\times(2m+1)
particles, where
m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, i.e.
\delta=mh=m/L
.
In what follows, we always choose a zero initial guess and solve the problem in primal variables (
20
) to a relative tolerance of 1e
-5
.
The solve times discussed below exclude the setup costs.

Computational resources

All experiments were performed on the Solo cluster at Sandia National Laboratories
[
1
]
.
Solo consists of 374 nodes with dual socket 2.1 GHz Intel Broadwell CPUs.
Each node has 36 cores and 128 GB of RAM.
Solo uses an Intel Omni-Path interconnect.

Expected scalability properties

We distribute the problem over
p\times p
subdomains, so that each subdomain consists of
s_{i}=(L/p)^{2}
particles.
Since every particle interacts with
(2m+1)^{2}
particles, the subdomain matrices
A_{i}
have

\displaystyle\operatorname{nnz}(A_{i})\sim\left(\frac{Lm}{p}\right)^{2}\sim\left(\frac{L^{2}\delta}{p}\right)^{2}

entries.
The condition number
\kappa
of the global linear system
A
defined in (
19
) scales like

\displaystyle\kappa\sim\delta^{-2},

as can be seen by comparing this particular case of a meshfree discretization with finite elements on a uniform mesh
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
.

Therefore, we expect

\displaystyle I_{\text{Krylov}}\sim\sqrt{\kappa}\sim 1/\delta\sim\frac{L}{m}

(42)

iterations when using a Krylov method to solve the equations involving
A
.
The cost per iteration, however, will be proportional to the cost of a local matrix-vector product, i.e.
\operatorname{nnz}(A_{i})
, so that overall solve time
T_{\text{Krylov}}
of an unpreconditioned Krylov method such as the conjugate gradient method will scale like

\displaystyle T_{\text{Krylov}}\sim I_{\text{Krylov}}\operatorname{nnz}(A_{i})

\displaystyle\sim\left(\frac{L^{2}}{p}\right)^{2}\delta\sim\left(\frac{L}{p}\right)^{2}Lm.

(43)

We are contrasting this scaling with the proposed domain decomposition solvers.
Solve times are dominated by the projected CG iteration.
Each matrix-vector product involving the operator
P_{o}K
involves the parallel solution of one linear system per subdomain.
We are employing a direct solver in this step, meaning that the cost per operator apply will at most be

\displaystyle s_{i}^{2}\sim\left(\frac{L}{p}\right)^{4}.

It will transpire from numerical experiments that the DD methods display a number of iterations that admits a bound
I_{\text{DD}}(L/p)

I_{\text{DD}}(\cdot)
is monotonically increasing in its argument.
This leads us to expect overall solve times proportional to

\displaystyle T_{\text{DD}}\sim\penalty\ I_{\text{DD}}(L/p)\left(\left(\frac{L}{p}\right)^{4}+\log p\right).

(44)

Here, the additional
\log p
contribution is due to the projection step, which involves global communication.

Further implementation details of the methods such as the used solvers will be discussed below.

5
Conclusion

We have presented a domain decomposition technique for the efficient numerical solution of nonlocal diffusion problems.
Our method, based on a meshfree discretization of the variational multi-domain formulation that was previously introduced in
[
9
]
, solves the discretized problem in a FETI fashion and exploits effective preconditioning techniques introduced for local domain-decomposition problems.

Our rigorous numerical studies highlight the fact that the proposed approach outperforms commonly used parallel solvers.
In fact, based on the scaling estimates derived in Section
4
and the corresponding numerical studies, we expect speedups (with respect to standard single-domain solvers) larger than 6.5x on problems of increasing size. Furthermore, our tests in Sections
4.1

This work is the first rigorous numerical study in a two-dimensional multi-domain setting for nonlocal operators with finite horizon and, as such, it represents a fundamental step towards increasing the usability of nonlocal models in large scale simulations. It is possible to generalize the proposed technique to any linear nonlocal problem, including nonlocal elasticity, in a straightforward way just by adapting the discretization matrices associated with the sub-problems. Furthermore, due to the similarities between discretized nonlocal problems and systems resulting from long-stencil meshfree discretizations of PDEs, the impact of this work extends beyond nonlocal problems, as our algorithm can be applied without modifications to meshfree discretizations of PDEs.
