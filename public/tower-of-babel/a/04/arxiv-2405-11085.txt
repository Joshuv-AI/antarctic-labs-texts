Decidability and Complexity of Decision Problems for Affine Continuous VASS
A. R. Balasubramanian

Abstract
. Vector addition system with states (VASS) is a popular model for the verification of concurrent systems. VASS consists of finitely many control states and a set of counters which can be incremented and decremented, but not tested for zero. VASS is a relatively well-studied model of computation and many results regarding the decidability of decision problems for VASS are well-known. Given that the complexity of solving almost all problems for VASS is very high, various tractable over-approximations of the reachability relation of VASS have been proposed in the literature. One such tractable over-approximation is the so-called continuous VASS , in which counters are allowed to have non-negative rational values and whenever an update is performed, the update is first scaled by an arbitrary non-zero fraction. In this paper, we consider affine continuous VASS, which extend continuous VASS by allowing integer affine operations. Affine continuous VASS serve as an over-approximation to the model of affine VASS, in the same way that continuous VASS over-approximates the reachability relation of VASS. We investigate the tractability of affine continuous VASS with respect to the reachability, coverability and state-reachability problems for different classes of affine operations and we prove an almost-complete classification of the decidability of these problems. Namely, except for the coverability problem for a single family of classes of affine operations, we completely determine the decidability status of these problems for all classes. Furthermore, except for this single family, we also complement all of our decidability results with tight complexity-theoretic upper and lower bounds.

1.
Introduction

Vector addition system with states (VASS) are one of the most popular and most studied infinite-state systems and have many applications in
verifying and modeling concurrent and business processes
(
16
,
14
,
13
,
22
)
. VASS consist of a finite set of states along
with a set of counters with values ranging over
\mathbb{N}
. Hence, a configuration of a VASS is a pair consisting of the current state of the VASS along with the current values of its counters.
Transitions of the VASS allow it to move from one state to another while incrementing or decrementing the values of the counters, provided that the new values of the counters do not drop below zero.
Alternatively, this can be summarized as having update instructions of the form
\mathbf{x}\leftarrow\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}

which are fireable as long as
\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}\geq\mathbf{0}
.
One of the most central questions regarding VASS
is the
reachability
problem: Given two configurations
C
and
C^{\prime}
of a VASS, can
C
reach
C^{\prime}
?
After decades of being open, the complexity of the problem was recently shown
to be
ACKERMANN
-complete
(
19
,
12
)
.

As mentioned before, the model of VASS has many applications. However, since basic verification questions
such as reachability have extremely high complexity, much of the research has been concentrated on finding

over-approximations
of the reachability relation which trade expressiveness for tractability.
Note that since reachability is usually used to check the existence of a path to an unsafe configuration,
if we prove that the unsafe state is not reachable in an over-approximate model,
then the same holds for the original model as well.
Many heuristics and over-approximations for the reachability relation of a VASS are known
(
5
)
and they have been applied in practice quite successfully
(
5
,
6
)
.
Here we concentrate on one such over-approximation, namely the
continuous semantics
.

In the continuous semantics, transitions are allowed to be fired
fractionally
.
More precisely, in order to fire a transition
t
, we are allowed to first pick a non-zero fraction
\alpha\in(0,1]
and then execute the updates of
t
by the fraction
\alpha
, i.e., if
t
originally decremented (resp. incremented) a counter by
c
, we now decrement (resp. increment) that counter by
\alpha\cdot c
, as long as the new value does not drop below zero.
As a result of this “fractional update”, counters are now relaxed to have values over the non-negative rationals
\mathbb{Q}_{+}
. Note that the continuous semantics is an over-approximation of the usual semantics.
It turns out that the continuous semantics is quite well-behaved in terms of complexity: Reachability in continuous VASS is
NP
-complete and is hence much lower than the complexity for the usual semantics.
Besides being a tractable over-approximation, it is also useful in practice
(
6
,
9
,
10
)

and is also useful to derive sound and complete algorithms for various problems in parameterized
verification
(
3
,
2
)
.

Coming back to VASS, as mentioned in
(
11
)
, although they are useful for modeling many systems, the features of VASS might
be too restricted in certain cases. For instance, VASS cannot model counter systems which
are allowed to reset their counters to 0, or transfer or copy the contents of one counter to another. It turns out that such extensions are quite desirable to
model certain applications
(
18
,
13
,
20
)
. Many of these extensions can be modeled
as specific
classes of affine VASS
. Affine VASS are VASS extended with affine operations, i.e.,
transitions can have updates of the form
\mathbf{x}\leftarrow\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}
for some matrix
\mathbf{A}

and some vector
\mathbf{b}
over the integers and such transitions are fireable as long as
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}\geq 0
.
However, this huge expressiveness comes at a cost; almost all interesting verification problems
are either undecidable or have very high complexity for affine VASS
(
11
)
.

As a result of this undecidability/high complexity barrier for affine VASS, it would then be
natural to wonder if the tractability of over-approximations developed for VASS can also
be transferred to affine VASS. In particular, a natural question might be to ask

Does the tractability of the continuous semantics hold in the affine case as well?

Our contribution.

In this paper, we consider the above question and introduce the model
of
affine continuous VASS
. An affine continuous VASS is just like a VASS, except that
transitions are now allowed to be fired fractionally, i.e., before firing a transition
t
(whose instruction is given by
\mathbf{x}\leftarrow\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}
where
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{b}
are a matrix and a vector over the integers respectively),
we pick a non-zero fraction
\alpha
and update
the counters by the rule
\mathbf{x}\leftarrow\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\alpha\mathbf{b}
provided that
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\alpha\mathbf{b}\geq\mathbf{0}
. To put it in words, to get the new counter values,
we multiply the old counter values with the matrix
\mathbf{A}
and then add the vector
\alpha\mathbf{b}
,
provided that the final result we get does not make the value of any counter drop below zero.

At this point, the reader might wonder why the semantics for the continuous case was not defined so that the update looks like
\mathbf{x}\leftarrow\alpha\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\alpha\mathbf{b}
. First, defining the semantics
this way does not allow us to model various important features such as resets or transfers.
Second, we would ideally like the semantics that we define for affine continuous VASS to
extend the semantics for continuous VASS, i.e., any meaningful extension of continuous VASS
to the affine setting should allow for a continuous VASS to be interpreted as is in the extended semantics by thinking of each transition as being equipped with an identity matrix. If we define the semantics
for the affine case by the rule
\mathbf{x}\leftarrow\alpha\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\alpha\mathbf{b}
, then
this feature is lost.
For these two reasons, we choose the semantics of an affine continuous VASS
so that the
matrix is not multiplied with the chosen fraction

\alpha
.

Having defined this model, we study three of the most fundamental verification problems: Reachability (can a given configuration reach another given configuration?), Coverability
(given two configurations
(p,\mathbf{u})
and
(q,\mathbf{v})
can
(p,\mathbf{u})
reach
some
(q,\mathbf{v}^{\prime})
with
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\geq\mathbf{v}
?) and State-reachability (given a configuration
(p,\mathbf{u})
and a state
q
, can
(p,\mathbf{u})
reach some configuration whose state is
q
?).
We present an almost-complete classification of the decidability status of these problems
with respect to the various
classes of affine operations
that are allowed, i.e., with
respect to the various classes of matrices that are allowed. We now briefly summarize our main
contributions.

Reachability.

As our first contribution, we completely resolve the decidability status of reachability. We show that reachability for a class of affine continuous VASS is decidable if
the class contains only permutation matrices and is undecidable for
any other class
.

State-Reachability.

As our second contribution, we completely resolve
the decidability status of state-reachability. We show that state-reachability
for a class of affine continuous VASS is decidable (and in
PSPACE
) if it contains only non-negative matrices and is undecidable for any other class. This proves, for instance, that state-reachability is in

PSPACE
for continuous VASS with resets, transfers, copies or doubling operations. To the best of our knowledge, the
PSPACE
upper bounds for these models were not known before.

Coverability.

As our third contribution, except for a single family of classes of
of affine matrices, we resolve the decidability status of coverability.
We show that coverability is decidable for classes containing only permutation matrices
and is undecidable for any class containing a matrix with a negative entry or a matrix with a row or a column that is entirely comprised of zeroes. Since continuous VASS with resets or transfers
belong to the last category of matrices, it follows that
the coverability problem
for continuous VASS with resets or transfers is undecidable
.
This result is surprising, because the coverability
problem for (usual) VASS with resets or transfers is decidable
(
15
)
. Hence, while
the continuous semantics drastically reduces the complexity for VASS, for resets and
transfers it actually strictly increases in complexity and power.

As we show in the next section, the only remaining classes of matrices for which we were not able to prove a decidability or undecidability
result for coverability are non-negative classes without any zero-rows or columns which contain at least one matrix possessing a weighted edge or two overlapping edges.
We will explain these notions formally in Section
2
, but we describe them informally here.
We can think of a matrix as a weighted adjacency matrix of some graph. A matrix
is then said to have a weighted edge if there is a edge in its graph whose value is strictly
larger than 1.
Also, a matrix is said to have
two overlapping edges if there are two edges in the graph whose target node is the same.
Even though we were not able to resolve the decidability status for this family,
we identify a non-trivial sub-family of matrices in this family for which coverability is decidable.
This sub-family contains any class that contains only non-negative self-loop matrices,
i.e., non-negative matrices such that each diagonal entry is non-zero. For any such class,
we show that coverability is decidable and
NP
-complete, and hence
no harder than coverability for continuous VASS.

Computational Complexity.

We also complement all of our upper bounds with matching lower bounds, except for a single case: State-reachability for non-negative classes without any zero-rows or columns containing weighted/overlapping edges.

More precisely, we show that reachability is
NEXP
-complete for classes containing only permutation matrices with at least one non-trivial permutation matrix i.e., a permutation matrix that is not the identity matrix.
We also show that coverability (resp. state-reachability) is
NEXP
-complete (resp.
PSPACE
-complete) for classes containing only permutation matrices
with at least one non-trivial permutation matrix and is
NP
-complete for non-negative classes with only self-loop matrices.
Finally, we show that state-reachability is
PSPACE
-complete for non-negative classes containing a matrix which has either a row or a column that is completely zero.

Related work.

The closest work to ours is the paper
(
11
)
.
In this paper, the authors consider reachability in affine VASS and affine integer VASS according to classes of affine operations and they completely classify
the decidability of reachability for both these models across all classes. The latter model
is an over-approximation of affine VASS in which
the counters are allowed to go below zero. The authors also prove a complexity trichotomy
for affine integer VASS. In particular, the authors prove that reachability for integer VASS with resets
or transfers is decidable and mention that it is undecidable for the continuous case.
In this paper, we also use the formalization of classes used in
(
11
)
.

The paper
(
8
)
also discusses affine integer VASS. The authors of that paper
prove that when the set of matrices that are considered form a finite monoid, then reachability becomes
decidable for affine integer VASS. They also prove some other results for infinite monoids.

Our techniques.

Our results use a number of novel techniques that are different
from the ones used for affine VASS and affine integer VASS. Below, we explain some of our techniques which illustrate why many of the results developed for affine VASS and affine integer VASS
are not applicable to affine continuous VASS. This also exhibits many surprising phenomena in the continuous case.

Continuous VASS with resets.

As a first point of departure from the usual and integer semantics, we show that coverability for continuous VASS with resets is undecidable. This is surprising, because
in both the usual and the integer semantics, this problem is decidable. This illustrates
the complex nature of the continuous semantics - while it reduces the complexity for VASS,
for VASS with resets it actually makes it harder. To prove this undecidability result,
we first show that the 1-bounded coverability problem for continuous VASS with zero-tests is undecidable. In this problem, we are given a continuous VASS which is allowed to perform zero-tests and two of its configurations.
We are then asked to check if the first configuration can cover the second one by a run
in which all the counter values always stay below 1. We show that this problem is undecidable
by observing that runs in the continuous semantics can be shrunk by arbitrary fractions.

We then reduce 1-bounded coverability for continuous VASS with zero-tests to coverability in reset VASS.
To do this, for every counter
x
of the given continuous VASS with zero-tests, we introduce a new

complementary counter

\overline{x}
and maintain the invariant that the sum of the values
of
x
and
\overline{x}
is 1 throughout. Then we replace every zero-test with a reset. The idea
is that if the new machine resets some counter
x
when it has a non-zero value, then the invariant
that
x+\overline{x}=1
is permanently destroyed and it instead becomes
x+\overline{x}<1
.
Since the sum of
x
and
\overline{x}
in the final configuration will be 1, it would
then follow that the new machine will reset a counter only when it achieves the value 0, thereby
mimicking zero-tests.

Reachability for weighted/overlapping edges.

While we do not resolve the
status of coverability for classes with non-negative weighted/overlapping edges, we prove that reachability
for such classes is undecidable.
A similar result is true for affine VASS, which can be proved by showing that such VASS can implement the doubling operation
(
11
)
. The reduction in this case crucially hinges on being able to double the current value of a counter and also being able to add 1 to a counter. While
the former can also be mimicked in the continuous case, the latter cannot be, as the additive updates
are scaled with a non-deterministically chosen fraction
\alpha\in(0,1]
. It might
be tempting to overcome this by adding a fresh counter which always has the value 1 and
then by using matrix operations, add this fresh counter’s value whenever we want to add 1; however,
this operation might not be allowed within the class.
We overcome this problem
by giving a reduction from the 1-bounded reachability problem for continuous VASS with zero-tests.
Given a continuous VASS with zero-tests, to every counter
x
, we attach a complementary counter
\overline{x}
and a collection of other dummy counters. Further, we maintain the invariant that the sum of the values of
x
,
\overline{x}
and the dummy counters is 1.
We then identify a matrix from this class such that when it is multiplied with a counter valuation in which
x
and the dummy counters have value 0, then the invariant remains; otherwise
the invariant is permanently broken. This then allows us to simulate zero-tests.

State-reachability for non-negative matrices.

In affine VASS, state-reachability is inter-reducible with coverability, which is decidable for classes which contain only non-negative matrices, i.e., matrices in which all entries are non-negative.
This is because affine VASS with non-negative matrices are well-structured
(
15
,
1
)
.
However, since non-negative rationals are not well-quasi ordered (under the usual ordering),
we cannot apply the theory
of well-structured transition systems in our setting of affine continuous VASS.
Nevertheless, we show that
the state-reachability problem is decidable and in
PSPACE
for affine continuous VASS
for classes that contain only non-negative matrices. We do this by constructing a
support
abstraction
graph from a given affine continuous VASS. The nodes of this graph only maintain the current
control state and the current set of counters which have a non-zero value. Edges
between the nodes of this graph are defined in a manner which satisfy some basic conditions: For instance,
if a transition
t
increments some counter
x
and if
(p,S)\xrightarrow{t}(p^{\prime},S^{\prime})
is an edge
in this graph labelled by
t
then
S^{\prime}
must contain
x
. This is because whenever
t
is fired
in the affine continuous VASS it will necessarily lead to a configuration with a non-zero value in
x
.
We then show that maintaining some such basic conditions is necessary and sufficient to decide
state-reachability and that this can be done in
PSPACE
. As a particular example, this
proves that state-reachability for continuous VASS with resets is decidable and proves that,
unlike the case of affine VASS,
coverability and state-reachability are two different problems for affine continuous VASS.

Coverability for self-loop matrices.

Finally, to prove that coverability for non-negative self-loop matrices (i.e., non-negative matrices in which no diagonal entry is zero) is in
NP
, we use results developed for continuous VASS. By adapting some techniques developed for continuous VASS, we show
that the coverability relation of an affine continuous VASS containing only non-negative self-loop matrices
can be defined as a formula in the existential theory of rationals equipped with the addition and the order operation (ELRA). The techniques developed for continuous VASS cannot be applied in a black-box manner
and require some careful restructuring of the arguments so that it can be used for the affine case, tailored specifically to the case of coverability. This is because for any class of matrices
containing a self-loop matrix that is not the identity matrix, the reachability relation
cannot be reduced to formulas in the ELRA theory, since our results for reachability imply undecidability in this case.
This is in contrast to the case of continuous VASS
for which the reachability relation can be embedded in ELRA.

To prove the decidability of coverability in self-loop matrices, we first identify counters
which could be potentially
pumped
, i.e., these are counters which while taking some transition,
either double their old value or retain their old value and also get the value of another counter by means of the matrix of that transition.
Since pumpable counters only depend on the matrix of a transition and not on its additive update,
we can choose arbitrarily small fractions and still get big values for pumpable counters.
Subject to some mild technical constraints, we show how to modify a given run that starts and ends at the same state and pumps some counters,
into a run so that all pumped counters attain arbitrarily high values whilst not modifying the
values of the non-pumped counters. This is done by roughly splitting a run into many copies of the same run
with smaller fractions.
In the end, this would mean that with respect to coverability, we can get rid of the pumpable counters
and only focus on the non-pumpable ones. We then notice that the affine VASS with only the non-pumpable
counters is a continuous VASS, for which we know how to decide coverability.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we define the notion of affine continuous VASS and the associated problems that we shall consider. Then, we define the notion of a
class of matrices
, akin to
(
11
)

and state our main results.

Throughout this paper we let
\mathbb{Q}_{+}
denote the set of non-negative rational numbers
and
\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
denote the set of positive rational numbers. We usually write
vectors
(u_{1},\dots,u_{d})\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}
in bold as
\mathbf{u}
and we use
\mathbf{u}(i)
to
denote
u_{i}
. Given some
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}
and a vector
\mathbf{v}
, we let
\alpha\mathbf{v}

be the vector given by
(\alpha\mathbf{v})(i)=\alpha\cdot\mathbf{v}(i)
.
The support of a vector
\mathbf{v}
, denoted by
{\llbracket\mathbf{v}\rrbracket}
is the set
\{i:\mathbf{v}(i)\neq 0\}
.
We use
\mathbf{0}
to denote the vector that is 0 everywhere and we let
\mathbf{e}_{i}
denote the

i^{th}
unit vector, i.e.,

\mathbf{e}_{i}
is 0 everywhere, except in the
i^{th}
co-ordinate, where its value is 1.
Given two vectors
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}
we say that
\mathbf{u}\leq\mathbf{v}
if
\mathbf{u}(i)\leq\mathbf{v}(i)
for every
i
.

Given a matrix
\mathbf{A}
we use
\mathbf{A}(i,j)
to denote its entry in the
i^{th}
row
and the
j^{th}
column. Further, given a matrix
\mathbf{A}
and a vector
\mathbf{u}
, we let

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}
denote the vector obtained by multiplying
\mathbf{A}
with
\mathbf{u}
.
Throughout this paper, we will only work with
square matrices, i.e., matrices whose number of rows and columns are the same.
A matrix
\mathbf{A}
is called a
diagonal matrix
if
all of its non-diagonal entries are 0, i.e.,

\mathbf{A}_{i,j}=0
whenever
i\neq j
.

Affine continuous VASS.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
. A
d
-affine continuous VASS (or simply affine continuous VASS) is a tuple
\mathcal{M}=(Q,T)
where
Q
is a finite set of
control states
and

T\subseteq Q\times\mathbb{Z}^{d\times d}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times Q
is a finite set of
transitions
.
Note that the matrices and the vectors appearing in the transitions range over the integers.
Intuitively,
\mathcal{M}
has access to
d
counters, each of which can store a non-negative rational value.
The values of these counters can be manipulated by the transitions in
T
as follows.

Given a transition
t=(p,\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b},q)
, we will call
\mathbf{A}
as the matrix of
t
and
\mathbf{b}

as the additive update of
t
.
A
configuration
of
\mathcal{M}
is a pair
(p,\mathbf{u})
(mostly written as
p(\mathbf{u})
), where
p\in Q
is a state and
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{d}

is a non-negative vector representing the current values of the
d
counters. If
C=p(\mathbf{u})

is a configuration, then we let
C(i)=\mathbf{u}(i)
for any counter
i
.
Given two configurations

p(\mathbf{u}),q(\mathbf{v})
, a non-zero fraction
\alpha\in(0,1]
, and a transition
t
of the form
t=(p,\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b},q)
, we say that there is a step from
p(\mathbf{u})
to
q(\mathbf{v})
by means
of the pair
\alpha t
, denoted by

p(\mathbf{u})\xrightarrow{\alpha t}q(\mathbf{v})
if
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}+\alpha\mathbf{b}
.
Intuitively, from the configuration
p(\mathbf{u})
, the machine executes the transition
t
and goes to the state
q
and updates the counters by multiplying the current vector
\mathbf{u}
with the matrix
\mathbf{A}
and then
adding the vector
\alpha\mathbf{b}
. Note that by the definition of a configuration,
we implicitly require that
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}+\alpha\mathbf{b}\geq\mathbf{0}
.
We say that
p(\mathbf{u})\xrightarrow{}q(\mathbf{v})
, if there exists some non-zero fraction
\alpha
and some transition
t
such that
p(\mathbf{u})\xrightarrow{\alpha t}q(\mathbf{v})
.

A
firing sequence

\sigma
is a sequence in
((0,1]\times T)^{*}
.
Given a firing sequence
\sigma=\alpha_{1}t_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}t_{k}
and a non-zero fraction
\beta
, we let
\beta\sigma
denote the firing sequence
\beta\sigma=\beta\alpha_{1}t_{1},\dots,\beta\alpha_{k}t_{k}
.

Given two configurations

C
and
C^{\prime}
and a firing sequence
\sigma=\alpha_{1}t_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}t_{k}
, we say that

C\xrightarrow{\sigma}C^{\prime}
if there exists configurations
C_{1},\dots,C_{k-1}
such that

C\xrightarrow{\alpha_{1}t_{1}}C_{1}\xrightarrow{\alpha_{2}t_{2}}C_{2}\dots C_{k-1}\xrightarrow{\alpha_{k}t_{k}}C^{\prime}
.
Finally, we write
C\xrightarrow{*}C^{\prime}
to mean that there is some firing sequence
\sigma
such that

C\xrightarrow{\sigma}C^{\prime}
.

Note that if all the matrices appearing in an affine continuous VASS
\mathcal{M}
are the identity matrices,
then we get the usual notion of continuous VASS
(
7
)
.

Classes of matrices.

Our aim in this paper is to provide a classification of
“classes of affine continuous VASS” with respect to decidability of the reachability, coverability and state-reachability problems. To this end, we need to formalize the notion of a “class of affine continuous VASS”.
For this purpose, we use the framework used in
(
11
)
, which was used
for classifying
affine VASS
and
affine integer VASS
with respect to the reachability problem.

A class of affine VASS extends the usual notion of a VASS by providing some extra operations that can be applied to the counters by means of the affine transformations that it provides,
i.e., the set of allowed matrices in its transitions. Since affine VASS extend VASS, the identity matrix
must always be allowed. Also, the authors of
(
11
)
noticed that the set of allowed matrices must be closed under multiplication. Finally, a class of affine VASS typically does not pose any restrictions on the number of counters that can be used, or on the subset of counters to which the operations can be applied. This means that a matrix can be extended to arbitrary dimensions and can be applied to
any subset of counters. These observations are utilized in the definition of a class used in
(
11
)
. We now describe this formal defintion of a class and use it to classify affine continuous VASS as well.

For every
k\geq 1
, let
\mathbf{I}_{k}
be the
k\times k
identity matrix. We use
\mathcal{S}_{k}
to denote
the set of all permutations over
\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
. For any permutation
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
, let
\mathbf{P}_{\sigma}\in\{0,1\}^{k\times k}
be its corresponding matrix, i.e., for any
1\leq i,j\leq k
,
\mathbf{P}_{\sigma}(i,j)
is 1 if
i
is mapped to
j
and 0 otherwise.
For any matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k\times k}
and any permutation
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
we let

\sigma(\mathbf{A}):=\mathbf{P}_{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{P}_{\sigma^{-1}}

and for any
n\geq 1
, we let

{\mathbf{A}}\!\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{n}
be the
(k+n)\times(k+n)
matrix defined as

{\mathbf{A}}\!\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{n}:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{0}_{\phantom{n}}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}

Intuitively, if
\mathbf{A}
is a
k\times k
matrix that dictates the updates of some
k
counters
of an affine continuous VASS, then
\sigma(\mathbf{A})
essentially renames the
k
counters according to the
permutation
\sigma
, applies
\mathbf{A}
according to this renaming and then renames the counters back
to the original names. The operation
{\mathbf{A}}\!\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{n}
can be thought of as adding
n
fresh counters
and then applying the matrix
\mathbf{A}
only on the old
k
counters, while leaving the new
n
counters
unchanged.

Now, a
class of matrices

\mathcal{C}
is any subset of matrices in
\cup_{k\geq 1}\mathbb{Z}^{k\times k}
that
satisfies the following four closure properties:

•

Identity: For every
n
,
\mathbf{I}_{n}\in\mathcal{C}
.

•

Multiplication: If
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathcal{C}
are
k\times k
matrices for some
k\geq 1
, then the matrix
\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}
is also in
\mathcal{C}
.

•

Extension: If
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{C}
, then for every
n
, the matrix
{\mathbf{A}}\!\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{n}
is also in
\mathcal{C}
.

•

Counter renaming: If
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{C}
is a
k\times k
matrix, then
\sigma(\mathbf{A})
is also in
\mathcal{C}
for any permutation
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
.

We also define a new operation called Application: If
\mathbf{A}
is a
k\times k
matrix and
i,n
are numbers
such that
k\leq n
and
i+k-1\leq n
, then
\text{Apply}_{n}(\mathbf{A},i)
is the
n\times n
matrix defined as

\text{Apply}_{n}(\mathbf{A},i):=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{i-1}&\mathbf{0}_{\phantom{n}}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{I}_{n-i-k+1}\end{pmatrix}

Intuitively,
\text{Apply}_{n}(\mathbf{A},i)
applies the matrix
A
to dimensions
i,i+1,\dots,i+k-1
and leaves the other dimensions unchanged.
Note that
\text{Apply}_{n}(\mathbf{A},i)
can be obtained by extending
\mathbf{A}
and renaming some counters of its extension: Indeed,
\text{Apply}_{n}(\mathbf{A},i)=\sigma({\mathbf{A}}\!\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{n})
where
\sigma
is the permutation that maps
every
j
to
i-1+j
if
i-1+j\leq n
and otherwise to
(i-1+j)\bmod n
. Hence, Application is
simply a syntactic sugar and all classes are closed under the Application operation. As we shall
see later on, the introduction of the Application operation will help us state us our constructions in an easier way.

We now show that the notion of a class of matrices can capture various sub-models of affine continuous VASS, each of which arise from continuous VASS by adding some new feature.

Example 2.1 (Classes of matrices).

As mentioned before, a continuous VASS is an affine continuous VASS in which all the matrices
are identity matrices. This is captured by the
identity class
of matrices, i.e.,
the class that contains only the set of all identity matrices.
For a
d
-continuous VASS
\mathcal{M}=(Q,T)
we will simply ignore the (identity) matrices in the transitions
and denote
T
by
T\subseteq Q\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times Q
.

A continuous VASS with reset operations is a continuous VASS in which transitions are further
allowed to reset the value of a counter to 0. This model is captured by affine continuous VASS with
matrices from the class
\mathcal{C}_{R}
which contains the set of all diagonal matrices with entries
from
\{0,1\}
. Intuitively, if
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{C}_{R}
, and its
i^{th}
diagonal entry is a 0, then this means that the
i^{th}
counter
is reset to 0 and if it is a 1, then the
i^{th}
counter is left unchanged.

Another example is continuous VASS with transfer operations which extend continuous VASS
with operations which allow the value of a counter
i
to be transferred to a counter
j
.
This model is captured by affine
continuous VASS with matrices from the class of all matrices
with entries from
\{0,1\}
in which every column has at most one non-zero entry.

2.1.
Contribution Part I - Decidability results

We now state the main contributions of the paper.
Given a class
\mathcal{C}
of matrices, we say that an affine continuous VASS
\mathcal{M}
is a
\mathcal{C}
-continuous VASS if all the matrices appearing in
\mathcal{M}
are in
\mathcal{C}
. We are now
ready to define the problems of interest to us and state our main results.

The
reachability problem
for a class
\mathcal{C}
of matrices is defined as the following:

Given:
A
\mathcal{C}
-continuous VASS
\mathcal{M}
and two of its configurations
p(\mathbf{u}),q(\mathbf{v})
.

Decide:
Whether
p(\mathbf{u})
can reach
q(\mathbf{v})
.

Our first main result is a classification of the reachability problem, which completely
characterizes the decidability/undecidability border. To state this result, we set up a notation:
We say that a matrix
\mathbf{A}
is a
permutation matrix
if
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{P}_{\sigma}
for some permutation
\sigma
. Our result for the reachability problem can then be phrased as the following.

Theorem 2.2.

The reachability problem for a class
\mathcal{C}
is decidable (and in
NEXP
) if
\mathcal{C}
contains
only permutation matrices. Otherwise, the reachability problem is undecidable.

For our second result, we consider the
state-reachability problem
for a class
\mathcal{C}
which is defined
as follows:

Given:
A
\mathcal{C}
-continuous VASS
\mathcal{M}
, a configuration
p(\mathbf{u})
and a state
q
.

Decide:
Whether
p(\mathbf{u})
can reach
some configuration of the form
q(\mathbf{v}^{\prime})
for any non-negative
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}
.

Our second main result completely classifies the decidability/undecidability border for the
state-reachability problem. To state this result, we set up a notation:
We say that a matrix
\mathbf{A}
is a
non-negative matrix
if all of its entries are non-negative.
Our result for the state-reachability problem can then be phrased as the following.

Theorem 2.3.

The state-reachability problem for a class
\mathcal{C}
is decidable (and in
PSPACE
) if
\mathcal{C}
contains
only non-negative matrices. Otherwise, the state-reachability problem is undecidable.

Finally, we consider the
coverability problem
for a class
\mathcal{C}
which is defined
as follows:

Given:
A
\mathcal{C}
-continuous VASS
\mathcal{M}
, and two of its configurations
p(\mathbf{u}),q(\mathbf{v})
.

Decide:
Whether
p(\mathbf{u})
can
cover

q(\mathbf{v})
, i.e., whether
p(\mathbf{u})
can reach
some configuration of the form
q(\mathbf{v}^{\prime})
where
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\geq\mathbf{v}
.

Our third main result classifies the decidability/undecidability border for the
coverability problem, except for a single family of classes.
To state our result for this problem we need a notation.
A matrix is said to have a
zero-row
(resp.
zero-column
) if it contains
a row that is completely zero (resp. a column that is completely zero).
Our result for the coverability problem is then as follows.

Theorem 2.4.

The coverability problem for a class
\mathcal{C}
is undecidable if
\mathcal{C}
contains
a matrix with either a negative entry or a zero row or a zero column.
It is decidable (and in
NEXP
) if
\mathcal{C}
contains only permutation matrices.

To see how this result classifies the coverability problem except for a single family,
we need some definitions.
A matrix
\mathbf{A}
is said to have a weighted edge if there exists
indices
i,j
such that
\mathbf{A}(i,j)>1
. Further,
\mathbf{A}
is said to
have two overlapping edges if there exists indices
i\neq j
and
k
such that

\mathbf{A}(i,k)>0
and
\mathbf{A}(j,k)>0
.
The intuitive idea is to think of
\mathbf{A}
as the weighted adjacency matrix of a graph.
Having a weighted edge in
\mathbf{A}
corresponds to having an edge in the graph whose value
is strictly bigger than 1. Having two overlapping edges in
\mathbf{A}
corresponds
to having two edges in the graph whose target nodes are the same.

Now, suppose
\mathbf{A}
is a non-negative matrix (over
\mathbb{Z}
)
which does not contain a zero-row, a zero-column, a weighted edge or two overlapping edges.
Hence, for every index
\ell
, there is a unique index
\sigma(\ell)
such that

\mathbf{A}(\sigma(\ell),\ell)=1
and
\mathbf{A}(\ell^{\prime},\ell)=0
for every
\ell^{\prime}\neq\sigma(\ell)
.
We claim that
\sigma(i)\neq\sigma(j)
whenever
i\neq j
.
Suppose there exist
i\neq j
such that
\sigma(i)=\sigma(j)=k
.
Then, since
\mathbf{A}
does not contain a zero-row, for every index
\ell
, there is some index
\tau(\ell)
such that
\mathbf{A}(\ell,\tau(\ell))>0
. Note that if
\ell^{\prime}=\tau(\ell)
, then

\sigma(\ell^{\prime})=\ell
.

Now, starting from
i
, using
\tau
, we get
a sequence of the form
\tau^{0}(i):=i,\ \tau^{1}(i):=\tau(i),\ \tau^{2}(i):=\tau(\tau(i))\ldots
Hence, there must be some
r
such that
\tau^{0}(i),\cdots,\tau^{r}(i)
are all distinct
and
\tau^{r+1}(i)=\tau^{e}(i)
for some
0\leq e\leq r
.
If
e\neq 0
, then
\mathbf{A}(\tau^{e-1}(i),\tau^{e}(i))>0
and
\mathbf{A}(\tau^{r}(i),\tau^{e}(i))>0
, which is a contradiction because this leads to two overlapping edges.
Hence,
e=0
and so
\tau^{r}(i)=\sigma(i)=k
. Similarly, when this argument is applied to
j
, we get a similar sequence of indices
j,\tau(j),\cdots,\tau^{w}(j)=k
for some
w
.

Now, by definition of
\sigma
and
\tau
we have that,
\sigma^{r}(k)=i
and
\sigma^{w}(k)=j
.
Since
i\neq j
, we have
w\neq r
. Without loss of generality, let
w<r
. This means that
\sigma^{r-w}(j)=i
and so
\tau^{r-w}(i)=j
. Since
\tau^{r}(i)=k
and since
\mathbf{A}(k,j)>0
, we have
\mathbf{A}(\tau^{r-w-1}(i),j)>0
and
\mathbf{A}(\tau^{r}(i),j)>0
,
which is a contradiction because this leads to two overlapping edges.
This means that
\sigma(i)\neq\sigma(j)
whenever
i\neq j
. It can then be easily seen
that
\mathbf{A}
is a permutation matrix.

The discussion above then shows that our result for coverability covers all classes
except those that contain matrices with weighted/overlapping edges,
which we will call the weighted/overlapping family.
However, even within this family, we identify a subfamily for which the coverability problem is decidable.
We now describe this class of matrices.

A matrix
\mathbf{A}
is said to be a
self-loop matrix
if no entry on the diagonal is zero. The intuition once again comes from graphs: If
\mathbf{A}
is a self-loop matrix, then
in the graph of
\mathbf{A}
, all nodes will have a self-loop.
We show that

Theorem 2.5.

The coverability problem for
\mathcal{C}
is decidable (and in
NP
) if
\mathcal{C}
contains only
non-negative self-loop matrices.

Note that
NP
is the best possible upper bound possible for a class,
since the coverability problem for continuous VASS is
NP
-complete. Hence,
we have strictly extended the model of continuous VASS whilst retaining the same complexity
for the coverability problem.

It is easy to see that state-reachability is a special case of coverability where

3.
Undecidability results

In this section, we prove all of our undecidability results.
We begin with our undecidability result on classes containing a matrix with a negative entry.

3.1.
State-reachability for classes with negative entries

Our main result of this section is that

Theorem 3.1.

The reachability, coverability and state-reachability problems for
\mathcal{C}
-continuous VASS is undecidable
if
\mathcal{C}
contains a matrix with some negative entry.

Note that by Theorem
2.8
, it suffices to prove the above theorem
for only the state-reachability problem.
To prove this result, we introduce the notion of a continuous VASS with zero tests.
These are continuous VASS which can additionally test if the value of a counter is zero.
Formally, a
d
-continuous VASS with zero tests is a tuple
\mathcal{M}=(Q,T,T_{=0})
where

Q
is a finite set of states,
T\subseteq Q\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times Q
is a finite
set of transitions and
T_{=0}\subseteq Q\times[d]\times Q
is a finite set of zero-tests.
Configurations of
\mathcal{M}
and steps of
\mathcal{M}
by transitions from
T
are defined in the same way as for a continuous VASS. In addition, we also have steps defined by zero-tests in the following manner.
Given two configurations
p(\mathbf{u}),q(\mathbf{v})
and a zero-test
t
of the form
t=(p,i,q)
, we say that there is a step from
p(\mathbf{u})
to
q(\mathbf{v})
by means of the zero-test
t
,
denoted by
p(\mathbf{u})\xrightarrow{t}q(\mathbf{v})
if
\mathbf{u}(i)=0
and
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}
. (Note that steps corresponding to a zero-test do not have any chosen fractions).
A run is then defined as a sequence of steps (defined by transitions and zero-tests) from one
configuration to another. Reachability, coverability and state reachability are then defined in the same way as for a continuous VASS.
From
(
7
, Theorem 4.17)
, we have the following result.

Theorem 3.2.

The reachability, coverability and state-reachability problems for continuous VASS with zero-tests
are undecidable.

Using this result, we shall prove Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a class of matrices which has a matrix
\mathbf{A}
(of size
k\times k
for some
k
)
such that
\mathbf{A}(i,j)<0
for some indices
i
and
j
. First, we note the following property: For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}
,
we have

(1)

\mathbf{A}\cdot\lambda\mathbf{e}_{j}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{0}&\text{if }\lambda=0\\
\lambda\cdot\mathbf{A}_{j}&\text{otherwise, where }\mathbf{A}_{j}\text{ is the }j^{th}\text{ column of }\mathbf{A}\end{cases}

This means that if we multiply
\mathbf{A}
by a non-negative vector
\mathbf{v}
which is zero everywhere, except possibly in the
j^{th}
counter,
then the only way we can have a non-negative vector again is if

\mathbf{v}
is
\mathbf{0}
. Intuitively, this means that if we have a configuration which is zero everywhere, except possibly
in the
j^{th}
counter, then to test if the value of the
j^{th}
counter is 0,
we simply have to multiply the configuration with the matrix
\mathbf{A}
. In this way, we can simulate
a zero-test on counter
j
by the matrix
\mathbf{A}
.

We now show that
\mathcal{C}
-continuous VASS can simulate continuous VASS with zero tests, which by Theorem
3.2
will prove
Theorem
3.1
. The reduction is similar to the one given in
(
11
, Proposition 4.3)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=(Q,T,T_{=0})
be a
d
-continuous VASS with zero tests for some
d
.
From
\mathcal{M}
, we will create a
n=dk
dimensional
\mathcal{C}
-continuous VASS
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.
For every
1\leq i\leq d
, the
(j+(i-1)k)^{th}
counter of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
(which we will denote
by
x_{i}
) will be responsible for simulating the
i^{th}
counter of
\mathcal{M}
.
These counters,
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d}
, will be called the
primary counters

of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.
The remaining counters of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}

will be called
dummy counters
which will always have the value 0.

Given a vector
\Delta\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}
, let ext
(\Delta)\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
be the vector which is 0 everywhere,
except in counters
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d}
where the values are respectively

\Delta(1),\Delta(2),\dots,\Delta(d)
. With this notation set up, we are ready to state
the desired reduction.

The set of states of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
will be the same as
Q
.
For each
t\in T\cup T_{=0}
of
\mathcal{M}
,

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
will have a corresponding transition
t^{\prime}
.
Before we state this transition
t^{\prime}
, we will state two properties that
we shall prove will be satisfied by
t^{\prime}
.

Property 1:
If

(p,\text{ext}(\mathbf{u}))\xrightarrow{\alpha t^{\prime}}q(\mathbf{v}^{\prime})
is a step in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
, then
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}=\text{ext}(\mathbf{v})

for some vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{d}
.

Property 2a):
If
t\in T
is a transition of
\mathcal{M}
,
then
p(\mathbf{u})\xrightarrow{\alpha t}q(\mathbf{v})
is a step in
\mathcal{M}
if and only if

p(\text{ext}(\mathbf{u}))\xrightarrow{\alpha t^{\prime}}q(\text{ext}(\mathbf{v}))
is a step in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.

Property 2b):
If
t\in T_{=0}
is a zero-test of
\mathcal{M}
,
then
p(\mathbf{u})\xrightarrow{t}q(\mathbf{v})
is a step in
\mathcal{M}
if and only if

p(\text{ext}(\mathbf{u}))\xrightarrow{\alpha t^{\prime}}q(\text{ext}(\mathbf{v}))
is a step in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
for all
\alpha\in(0,1]
.

We now proceed to state the transitions of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.
If
t=(p,\Delta,q)
is a transition in
T
, then corresponding to
t
, we will have
the transition
t^{\prime}=(p,\mathbf{I}_{n},\text{ext}(\Delta),q)
in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.
Note that
t^{\prime}
updates the counters
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d}
of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
in the same way
as
t
updates the counters
1,2,\dots,d
of
\mathcal{M}
. Further,
t^{\prime}
does not update the dummy counters
of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
at all. It can then be easily seen that
t^{\prime}
satisfies both the properties.

If
t=(p,\ell,q)
is a zero-test in
T_{=0}
, then corresponding to
t
,
we will have the transition
t^{\prime}=(p,\text{Apply}_{n}(\mathbf{A},(\ell-1)k+1),\mathbf{0},q)
in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.
By definition of the Application operation,
t^{\prime}
does not update
any counters in the set
\{1,2,\dots,(\ell-1)k\}\cup\{\ell k+1,\dots n\}
. Furthermore, by
equation
1
, if at some configuration, the values of the counters in the set
\{(\ell-1)k+1,\dots,(\ell-1)k+j-1\}\cup\{(\ell-1)k+j+1,\dots,\ell k\}
are zero, then the only way

t^{\prime}
can be fired from that configuration is if the value of the counter
(\ell-1)k+j=x_{\ell}

is also zero.
Further, firing
t^{\prime}
only results in all of the counters in the set
\{(\ell-1)k+1,(\ell-1)k+2,\dots,\ell k\}
taking
the value 0.
Hence,
t^{\prime}
also satisfies both of these properties.

By using both the properties, it then follows that in
\mathcal{M}
, a configuration
p(\mathbf{u})

can reach a configuration with state
q
if and only if in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
, the configuration
p(\text{ext}(\mathbf{u}))

can reach a configuration with state
q
. Hence, the desired reduction is complete.

3.2.
Coverability for non-negative classes with zero-rows/columns

Because of the negative result from the previous subsection, it suffices to only consider classes
which only contain non-negative matrices for the rest of the paper. Such a class will be called a non-negative class. We are now ready to state the main result of this subsection.

Theorem 3.3.

The reachability and coverability problems for
\mathcal{C}
-continuous VASS is undecidable if
\mathcal{C}
is a non-negative class which contains
a zero-row or a zero-column matrix.

By Theorem
2.8
, it suffices to prove the above theorem only for the coverability problem. We do this in two stages.
In the first stage, we show that coverability for continuous VASS with resets, i.e.,
\mathcal{C}_{R}
-continuous VASS
where
\mathcal{C}_{R}
is the class of all diagonal matrices with entries from
\{0,1\}
is undecidable.
In the second stage, we reduce from coverability for continuous VASS with resets to
coverability for
\mathcal{C}
-continuous VASS where
\mathcal{C}
contains a zero-row or a zero-column matrix.

Stage 1: Continuous VASS with resets.

A continuous VASS with resets is simply an affine continuous VASS in which all
the matrices that appear are diagonal matrices with entries from
\{0,1\}
.
For every
d,i
, let
\mathbf{R}^{(d,i)}
be the
d\times d
diagonal matrix
which has a 1 in each entry of the diagonal, except at the position
(i,i)
, where the value is 0.
Intuitively, if
\mathbf{u}
is some vector and
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{R}^{(d,i)}(\mathbf{u})
,
then
\mathbf{v}
is the same as
\mathbf{u}
, except that
\mathbf{v}(i)=0
. The idea is that if
\mathbf{u}
represents
the current contents of all the counters, then
\mathbf{R}^{(d,i)}
resets the value of the counter
i

to 0 and does not alter the contents of the other counters. In the sequel,
whenever we say that a transition resets counter
i
, we mean that the current counter values
are multiplied by
\mathbf{R}^{(d,i)}
.
We will now show that

Theorem 3.4.

The coverability problem for continuous VASS with resets
is undecidable.

This theorem is surprising, because coverability in the usual VASS model enriched with resets
is decidable
(
15
)
. Hence, while the continuous semantics drastically reduces the expressive power
in VASS,
upon addition of resets,
the continuous semantics becomes even more powerful than the usual semantics.

To prove Theorem
3.4
, we first introduce the following notion.
Suppose
\mathcal{M}
is a continuous VASS
with zero-tests
and
C
and
C^{\prime}
are configurations of
\mathcal{M}
.
A 1-bounded run between
C
and
C^{\prime}
is a run in which all the counter values along the run are at most 1;
in particular this means that the counter values of
C
and
C^{\prime}
are at most 1. The 1-bounded coverability problem for continuous VASS with zero-tests is the following: Given a tuple
(\mathcal{M},C,C^{\prime})
, decide if there is a 1-bounded run from
C
which can cover
C^{\prime}
.
In order to prove Theorem
3.4
, we first prove the following lemma.

Lemma 3.5.

The 1-bounded coverability problem is undecidable for continuous VASS with zero-tests.

The proof of this lemma can be found in Subsection
A.1
.
The intuitive idea behind this lemma is that continuous VASS with zero-tests have a
scaling property
,
which allows runs to be
shrunk
by arbitrary fractions. More precisely, it is easy
to see that whenever
D\xrightarrow{\alpha t}D^{\prime}
is a step in a continuous VASS with zero-tests
where
t
is a transition, then so is
\beta D\xrightarrow{\beta\alpha t}\beta D^{\prime}
for any
\beta>0

such that
\beta\cdot\alpha\leq 1
. (The converse holds as well). This means
that runs and the configurations appearing in them can be shrunk down to very small values,
hence enabling us to reduce the coverability problem for continuous VASS with zero-tests
to its 1-bounded version.

Assuming that Lemma
3.5
is true, we shall now prove Theorem
3.4
.
Let
(\mathcal{M},C,D)
be an instance of the 1-bounded coverability problem for
continuous VASS with zero-tests.
To every counter
x
of
\mathcal{M}
, we add a
complementary counter

\overline{x}
and modify the transitions of
\mathcal{M}
so that the sum of the values of
x
and
\overline{x}
is 1 at any point.
This can be done by modifying each transition
t
of
\mathcal{M}

so that if
t
increments (resp. decrements)
x
by some value
a
, then
t
decrements (resp. increments)
\overline{x}
by
a
and vice versa.
Then, we replace each zero-test of
\mathcal{M}
on the counter
x
, by a reset on the counter
x
.
Call this new machine
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
. Note that
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is a continuous VASS with resets.
Let
C^{\prime}
(resp.
D^{\prime}
) be the configuration of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
such that

C^{\prime}(x)=C(x)
and
C^{\prime}(\overline{x})=1-C(x)
(resp.
D^{\prime}(x)=D(x)
and
D^{\prime}(\overline{x})=1-D(x)
)
for every counter
x
of
\mathcal{M}
. Note that since
(\mathcal{M},C,D)
is an
instance of the 1-bounded coverability problem,
C(x),D(x)\leq 1
for every
x
and so
C^{\prime}(\overline{x}),D^{\prime}(\overline{x})
are non-negative rationals.

If in any run of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
, a counter
x
is reset when it already has the value 0, then this reset
does not disturb the invariant that the sum of the values of
x
and
\overline{x}
is 1.
On the other hand, if a run of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
resets
x
when it has a positive value
\alpha>0
,
then this reset will make the sum of the values of
x
and
\overline{x}
to be
1-\alpha
.
Since all the other transitions either reset a counter or maintain the sum of
x
and
\overline{x}
to be a constant, it would follow that such a run can never cover the configuration
D^{\prime}
.
Hence, in a run which covers
D^{\prime}
from
C^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
, a counter
x
is reset only when it has the value 0. Since we have replaced zero-tests in
\mathcal{M}
with resets in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
, it then
follows that any run that covers
D
from
C
in
\mathcal{M}
can be uniquely mapped to a run
that covers
D^{\prime}
from
C^{\prime}
and vice-versa. This then completes the proof of Theorem
3.4
.

Stage 2: Reduction from resets to zero-row/zero-column matrices.

We now show that affine classes that contain a matrix with a zero-row/column can simulate resets.
To this end, let
\mathcal{C}
be a non-negative class such that
\mathcal{C}
contains a matrix
\mathbf{A}
(of size
k\times k
for some
k
) which either has a
zero-row or a zero-column. Let
j
be the row or column of
\mathbf{A}
which is completely zero.
Note that

(2)

\text{If $j$ is a row then for any $\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{k}$, }\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v}\\
\text{ where }\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{k}\text{ satisfies }\mathbf{v}(j)=0

and

(3)

\text{If $j$ is a column then for any $\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}$, }\mathbf{A}\cdot\lambda\mathbf{e}_{j}=\mathbf{0}

Equation
2
tells us that we can use the matrix to always reset the counter
j
.
Equation
3
tells us that as long as all the counters apart from
j

on which we are applying
\mathbf{A}
have the value 0, then we can reset the counter
j
.
We can then exploit these equations to
give a reduction from continuous VASS with resets to
\mathcal{C}
-continuous VASS,
similar in style to the proof of Theorem
3.1
.
For full details, we refer the reader to Section
A.2
of the appendix.

3.3.
Reachability for non-negative classes with weighted/overlapping edges

We now move on to our final undecidability result. Our main result of this subsection is that

Theorem 3.6.

The reachability problem for
\mathcal{C}
-continuous VASS is undecidable if
\mathcal{C}
is a non-negative
class which contains a matrix with a weighted edge or two overlapping edges.

The proof of this theorem will combine ideas from the previous two reductions. More specifically, we prove this theorem by giving a reduction from the 1-bounded reachability problem for continuous VASS with zero-tests.
In this problem, we are given a continuous VASS with zero-tests
\mathcal{M}
and two configurations

C,D
, and we have to check if
C
can reach
D
by a run in which all the counter values are at most 1 at any point.
In Lemma
3.5
, we showed that the 1-bounded
coverability
problem for continuous VASS with zero-tests is undecidable. By mimicking the transformation given in Theorem
2.8
,
it then follows that the 1-bounded reachability problem for continuous VASS with zero-tests is also undecidable.

Proof of Theorem
3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a non-negative class. We can assume that
\mathcal{C}
does not have any matrices with zero-rows/columns,
as otherwise Theorem
3.3
applies.
Let
\mathbf{A}
be a matrix (of size
k\times k
for some
k
) which either has a weighted edge
or two overlapping edges. This implies that there is an index
z
such that

\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}\mathbf{A}(i,z)>1
. This then allows us to deduce
the following equation.

(4)

\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{u}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{0}&\text{if }\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{v}&\text{with }\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}\mathbf{v}(i)>\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}\mathbf{u}(i)\text{ if }\mathbf{u}(z)>0\end{cases}

Intuitively, this equation gives us an ability to perform a zero-test on the
z^{th}
counter.
As in the reductions before, we can think of the
z^{th}
counter as a primary counter
and all the other
k-1
counters as dummy counters holding the value 0.
In our reduction below, we will pair the
z^{th}
counter with a complementary counter
\overline{z}

such that any additive increase in the
z^{th}
counter will be accompanied by an additive
decrease in its complementary counter and vice-versa. This will ensure that the
sum of the values of
z,\overline{z}
and the
k-1
dummy counters
will be 1 with respect to the additive updates.
If at some point, the
z^{th}
counter along with all the dummy counters have value 0,
then multiplying by

\mathbf{A}
has no effect. On the other hand, if the
z^{th}
counter has a non-zero value,
then multiplying by
\mathbf{A}
has the effect of strictly increasing the sum of the values
of these counters.
Since, the overall sum of the values
of these counters in the final configuration will also be 1,
it follows that in any run between
the initial and the final configuration, we can multiply our current valuation with the matrix
\mathbf{A}
at some point
only if the value of
z
at that point is 0, which enables us to do a zero-test.
Hence, we can then give a reduction from the 1-bounded reachability problem
for continuous VASS with zero-tests in a style similar to Theorem
3.1
, which will prove the required
undecidability result.
For full details, we refer the reader to Section
B
of the appendix.

Note that this same reduction will
not
work for coverability, because in coverability it is permissible to have strictly higher values than the target configuration.
∎

Let us now look at the big picture for all of our undecidability results.
We have shown all the undecidability results mentioned in Theorems
2.3

and
2.4
. As for the reachability problem,
the discussion given in Subsection
2.1
tells
us that if
\mathcal{C}
contains a matrix
\mathbf{A}
that is not the
permutation matrix, then
\mathbf{A}
either has a negative entry, or has a zero-row/column,
or has weighted/overlapping edges. It then
follows that we have also proved all the undecidability results for the reachability problem mentioned in Theorem
2.2
.

4.
Decidability results and complexity upper bounds

We now prove all of our decidability results.
We begin by showing decidability of state reachability for non-negative classes.

4.1.
State-reachability for non-negative classes

Our main result of this subsection is that

Theorem 4.1.

The state-reachability problem for
\mathcal{C}
-continuous VASS is in
PSPACE
if
\mathcal{C}
contains only non-negative matrices.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a class containing only non-negative matrices and
let
\mathcal{M}=(Q,T)
be a
d
-dimensional
\mathcal{C}
-continuous VASS.
For every counter
x
and every transition
t=(p,\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b},q)
of
\mathcal{M}
,
we say that

•

x
is additively incremented by
t
if
\mathbf{b}(x)>0
.

•

x
is additively decremented by
t
if
\mathbf{b}(x)<0
. In this case,
we let
{\llbracket t\rrbracket}^{-}_{x}
be the set
\{y:\mathbf{A}(x,y)>0\}
.

The intuition behind additive increments/decrements is that if
x
is additively incremented/decremented
by
t
, then the additive update of
t
increases/decreases the value of
x
.
The intuition behind
{\llbracket t\rrbracket}^{-}_{x}
is that,
if
x
is additively decremented by
t
, then to fire
t
at some point,
there must be some counter
y\in{\llbracket t\rrbracket}^{-}_{x}

which has a non-zero value at that point.
We will expand upon these intuitions in the upcoming paragraphs, but first, we state
the main object of our algorithm, namely the
support abstraction
.

Corresponding to
\mathcal{M}
, we associate an edge-labelled graph
SU_{\mathcal{M}}
, called its support abstraction, as follows.
The set of vertices of
SU_{\mathcal{M}}
will be
Q\times 2^{\{1,\dots,d\}}
. Intuitively, each configuration
p(\mathbf{u})
of
\mathcal{M}
corresponds to the vertex
(p,{\llbracket\mathbf{u}\rrbracket})
of
SU_{\mathcal{M}}
.
The set of edges of
SU_{\mathcal{M}}
(and its labels) are as follows.
Suppose
t=(p,\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b},q)
is a transition of
\mathcal{M}
. Then, there exists an edge between vertices
(p,S)
and
(q,S^{\prime})
labelled by
t
if and only if
for all counters
x
,

•

If
x
is additively decremented by
t
, then
{\llbracket t\rrbracket}^{-}_{x}\cap S\neq\emptyset

•

If
x
is additively incremented by
t
, then
x\in S^{\prime}
and

•

If
x\in S^{\prime}
then either
x
is additively incremented by
t
or
{\llbracket t\rrbracket}^{-}_{x}\cap S\neq\emptyset
.

The intuitive idea behind this edge relation is as follows: Suppose
p(\mathbf{u})\xrightarrow{\alpha t}q(\mathbf{v})

is a step in
\mathcal{M}
with
t=(p,\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b},q)
. Then
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}+\alpha\mathbf{b}
.
If
x
is additively decremented by
t
this means that
\alpha\mathbf{b}(x)<0
, and so
it must be the case that,
(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u})(x)>0
. Since
\mathbf{A}
is a non-negative matrix, the latter condition is equivalent to

{\llbracket t\rrbracket}^{-}_{x}\cap{\llbracket\mathbf{u}\rrbracket}\neq\emptyset
.
Furthermore, notice that if
\mathbf{b}(x)>0
, then so is
\mathbf{v}(x)
and if
\mathbf{v}(x)>0
then
either
(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u})(x)>0
or
\mathbf{b}(x)>0
. This argument, along with
the definition of edges in the support graph then allows us to conclude that

Soundness:
Suppose
p(\mathbf{u})\xrightarrow{\alpha t}q(\mathbf{v})
is a step in
\mathcal{M}
.
Then
(p,{\llbracket\mathbf{u}\rrbracket})\xrightarrow{t}(q,{\llbracket\mathbf{v}\rrbracket})
is an edge in the support abstraction.

Furthermore by analysing the construction of the support graph, we can show that

Completeness:
Suppose
(p,S)\xrightarrow{t}(q,S^{\prime})
is an edge in the support abstraction

SU_{\mathcal{M}}

and suppose
p(\mathbf{u})
is a configuration of
\mathcal{M}
such that
S\subseteq{\llbracket\mathbf{u}\rrbracket}
. Then,
there exists a configuration
q(\mathbf{v})
and a fraction
\alpha
such that
S^{\prime}\subseteq{\llbracket\mathbf{v}\rrbracket}
and

p(\mathbf{v})\xrightarrow{\alpha t}q(\mathbf{v})
is a step in
\mathcal{M}
.

Combining these two properties along with a simple induction on the length of a run
allows us to prove the following:

p(\mathbf{u})
can reach a configuration whose state is
q
in
\mathcal{M}
if and only if
(p,{\llbracket\mathbf{u}\rrbracket})

can reach some vertex of the form
(q,S)
in the graph
SU_{\mathcal{M}}
.

For more details behind the proof of correctness of these claims, we refer the reader to Section
C
.

Hence, to decide state reachability in
\mathcal{M}
, it suffices to explore the support abstraction graph. By standard techniques of constructing this graph on-the-fly in a non-deterministic manner,
we get a non-deterministic polynomial space algorithm for deciding state reachability of
\mathcal{M}
.
Applying Savitch’s theorem then gives us a
PSPACE
algorithm for deciding state reachability.

4.2.
Coverability for non-negative classes with only self-loop matrices

The main result of this subsection is that

Theorem 4.2.

The coverability and state-reachability problems for
\mathcal{C}
-continuous VASS is in NP if
\mathcal{C}
is a non-negative class
that only contains self-loop matrices.

Note that by Theorem
2.8
, we need only prove the above theorem for coverability.
We will prove this by capturing the coverability relation of such a
\mathcal{C}
-continuous VASS as a formula in existential linear rational arithmetic (ELRA). Here ELRA is the existential theory of
the rationals equipped with the addition and order operations.
Since satisfiability of this existential theory can be done in
NP

(
21
)
,
Theorem
4.2
would then follow.

We capture the coverability relation in ELRA by adapting some techniques developed for continuous VASS.
Let us fix a class
\mathcal{C}
that contains only non-negative self-loop matrices and let us fix a
d
-dimensional
\mathcal{C}
-continuous VASS
\mathcal{M}
for the rest of this section. We first state a couple of basic facts
regarding
\mathcal{M}
.

Lemma 4.3.

The following statements are true for any firing sequence
\pi
and any fraction
\alpha\in(0,1]
:

(1)

Suppose
p(\mathbf{u})\xrightarrow{\pi}q(\mathbf{v})
. Then,
p(\alpha\mathbf{u})\xrightarrow{\alpha\pi}q(\alpha\mathbf{v})
.

(2)

Suppose
p(\mathbf{u})\xrightarrow{\pi}q(\mathbf{v})

\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{d}

p(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w})\xrightarrow{\pi}q(\mathbf{v}^{\prime})

.

(3)

Suppose

. Then,
p(\mathbf{u})\xrightarrow{\alpha\pi}q(\mathbf{v}^{\prime})

where
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\geq\alpha\mathbf{v}+(1-\alpha)\mathbf{u}
.

Indeed in all these cases, the proofs are immediate when

The general case then follows by induction on the length of
\pi
.
A formal proof can be found in Section
D.1
of the appendix.

Now we move on to proving our main result, i.e., Theorem

.
To prove the result, first we introduce a notation. We say that an ELRA formula

then we can test for satisfiability of

of

NP

(
21
)
.

Equipped with this notion, we now prove Theorem
4.2
.
For this, notice that it suffices to show the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4.

Given a state

of
\mathcal{M}
, in

Indeed, by standard arguments on decomposing paths into steps and (non-simple) cycles

More details can be found in Subsection

Proving Lemma

Hence, all that remains is to prove Lemma

To prove this, we

Recall that we have fixed a class

\mathcal{M}

With these notations set up, we now give a high-level overview behind the proof of Lemma

we know how to decide coverability. Hence, intuitively the overall idea is to then identify pumpable counters, remove
them from
\mathcal{M}
and only concentrate on the machine with the remaining counters which behaves like a continuous VASS and which we know how to solve. This reasoning is proved in another lemma which

Complexity lower bounds

We now prove complexity lower bounds for almost all of the upper bounds that

5.1.

In this subsection, we show that

To prove that state-reachability for continuous VASS with resets is PSPACE-hard,

For a formal proof, we refer the reader to Section

of the appendix.

6.
Conclusion

We have introduced the model of affine continuous VASS and studied the reachability, state-reachability
and coverability problems for different classes of integer affine operations. We have shown a classification
for decidability of reachability and state-reachability and an almost-complete classification for coverability.
We have also complemented most of our decidability results with tight complexity bounds.

As part of future work, we would like to completely classify the decidability status of the coverability problem by solving it for the weighted/overlapping family. Extending the current approach for self-loop classes to this general case is challenging.
This is because for the self-loop classes, we crucially exploited the fact that at each step, the matrix in the underlying transition does not allow the counter values to drop.
This property is however lost in the general case, which indicates that different methods might be necessary to solve the general case.

Finally, it might be worth studying affine operations in which the matrices
are allowed to have entries from the rationals. Many of our undecidability and decidability
results already apply in this case as well; however, a complete classification of problems in this case
is a possible direction for future work.

\mathbf{u}(j)=0

\mathbf{P}_{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v}\text{ where }\mathbf{v}(j)=0\text{ for every }j\neq\sigma(z)

Similarly, suppose
\mathbf{u}

\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{k}

\mathbf{u}(j)=0

\mathbf{P}_{\sigma}^{n-1}\cdot\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v}\text{ where }\mathbf{v}(j)=0\text{ for every }j\neq z

Intuitively,

transfers the value of

\sigma(z)

Similarly,

\sigma(z)
to

G.1.
PSPACE
lower bound for state-reachability

First, we will show that the state-reachability problem for
\mathcal{C}
-continuous VASS is PSPACE-hard
by giving a reduction from the state-reachability problem for Boolean programs.
To this end, let
\mathcal{M}=(Q,T)
be a
d
-Boolean program. We will construct
a
dk
-dimensional
\mathcal{C}
-continuous VASS which will simulate
\mathcal{M}
.
The counters
x_{1}=z,x_{2}=z+k,\dots,x_{d}=z+(d-1)k
will be called the positive primary counters of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
,
the counters
\overline{x_{1}}=\sigma(z),\overline{x_{2}}=\sigma(z)+k,\dots,\overline{x_{d}}=\sigma(z)+(d-1)k
will be called
the negative secondary counters of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}

and the remaining counters will be called dummy counters, which will always have the value zero.
The intuition here is that
the Boolean variable
i
of
\mathcal{M}
will be simulated by the positive primary counter
x_{i}
and the negative primary counter
\overline{x_{i}}
in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.
At any point, we will have the following invariant in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
:
Exactly one of the counters
x_{i}
and
\overline{x_{i}}
will have a non-zero value.
If
x_{i}
(resp.
\overline{x_{i}}
) has a non-zero value, then this corresponds to the Boolean variable
i
being true (resp. false).

Note that with this invariant, tests on the Boolean variable
i
in
\mathcal{M}
can be simulated by appropriate increments/decrements on the primary counters
x_{i}
and
\overline{x_{i}}
.
On the other hand,
to simulate set transitions on the Boolean variable
i
in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
, we will rely on equations
13
and
14
.
The idea is that if we want to set the Boolean variable
i
to 1 in
\mathcal{M}
,
then we have to make the value of
x_{i}
to be non-zero and the value of
\overline{x_{i}}
to be zero. We first check which of these current values is non-zero, by non-deterministically decrementing either
x_{i}
or
\overline{x_{i}}
. Note that exactly
one of these choices can succeed, thanks to our invariant.
If we successfully decrement
x_{i}
, then it turns out that the original value of
x_{i}
was already non-zero and so we simply have to
increment
x_{i}
, in order to fully simulate the set transition on the Boolean variable
i
.
On the other hand, suppose we decrement
\overline{x_{i}}
. Then this means that originally the
value of
\overline{x_{i}}
was non-zero and the value of
x_{i}
was zero. We then increment

\overline{x_{i}}
and then transfer the value of
\overline{x_{i}}
to
x_{i}
by multiplying with

\text{Apply}_{dk}(\mathbf{P}_{\sigma}^{n-1},(i-1)k+1)
. By the definition of the Apply operation and
equation
14
, it follows that the value of
x_{i}
is now non-zero, the value
of
\overline{x_{i}}
is zero and the value of every dummy counter is 0 and so we have successfully simulated a set transition to the value 1.
Similarly by using equation
13
, we can simulate a set transition to the value 0.

G.2.
NEXPTIME lower bound for coverability and reachability

Now, we move on to proving
NEXP
lower bounds for the coverability and reachability problems for
\mathcal{C}
-continuous VASS.
In the previous paragraphs, we have shown that
\mathcal{C}
-continuous VASS can simulate Boolean programs.
We can enrich the model of Boolean programs by allowing continuous counters to get the model
of continuous Boolean programs where, in addition to a set of Boolean variables we are also
given access to a set of continuous counters. From
(
4
)
, it is known that the
coverability and reachability problems for continuous Boolean programs are
NEXP
-hard.
The reduction given in the previous subsection then allows us to derive a
NEXP
lower bound for the
coverability and reachability problems for
\mathcal{C}
-continuous VASS in a straightforward manner.
