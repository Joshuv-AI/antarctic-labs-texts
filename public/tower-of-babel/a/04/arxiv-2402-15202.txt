Fine-Grained Detoxification via Instance-Level Prefixes for Large Language Models
Xin Yi, Linlin Wang, Xiaoling Wang, Liang He

Abstract
Impressive results have been achieved in natural language processing (NLP) tasks through the training of large language models (LLMs). However, these models occasionally produce toxic content such as insults, threats, and profanity in response to certain prompts, thereby constraining their practical utility. To tackle this issue, various finetuning-based and decoding-based approaches have been utilized to mitigate toxicity. However, these methods typically necessitate additional costs such as high-quality training data or auxiliary models. In this paper, we propose fine-grained detoxification via instance-level prefixes (FGDILP) to mitigate toxic text without additional cost. Specifically, FGDILP contrasts the contextualized representation in attention space using a positive prefix-prepended prompt against multiple negative prefix-prepended prompts at the instance level. This allows for constructing fine-grained subtoxicity vectors, which enables collaborative detoxification by fusing them to correct the normal generation process when provided with a raw prompt. We validate that FGDILP enables controlled text generation with regard to toxicity at both the utterance and context levels. Our method surpasses prompt-based baselines in detoxification, although at a slight cost to generation fluency and diversity.



1
Introduction

The large language models (LLMs) such as GPT
(
Radford et al. 2019
;
Brown et al. 2020
)
and Llama
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
series succeed across a diverse spectrum of natural language processing (NLP) tasks. However, LLMs are capable of generating threat, hate, insults, profanities, or otherwise toxic language
Gehman et al. 2020
;
Rae et al. 2021
;
Deshpande et al. 2023
which poses potential security risks to NLP system applications. A realistic consideration is to mitigate the generation of toxic text by some flexible techniques, referred to as detoxification in this paper.

Researchers are increasingly focused on ensuring the generation of harmless content by LLMs. For instance, finetuning-based techniques
(
Keskar et al. 2019
;
Gururangan et al. 2020
)
involve additional training of the base model on nontoxic datasets to mitigate toxic outputs. However, this approach requires substantial computational resources, especially for LLMs. Moreover, fine-tuning a model on specific data compromises its ability to generalize to other tasks
(
Kumar et al. 2022
)
. Another methods involve adjusting the probabilities of potential toxic tokens during the decoding stage
(
Krause et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2021
;
Hallinan et al. 2023
)
. Although further training of the base model is not necessary, this process requires an additional guiding model for modifying the probability distribution. Additionally, the memory occupied by this additional model must be maintained at each decoding step. The third category is a prompt-based approach
(
Schick et al. 2021
;
Leong et al. 2023
)
, which utilizes prompts to trigger the model’s internal knowledge related to toxicity for detoxification. However, there is a lack of understanding of the working mechanisms of safety prompts, which limits the potential for optimizing them to improve LLM safety
(
Zheng et al. 2024
)
.

In this paper, we explore the detoxification by self-generated prefixes without requiring additional training parameters or data. Our approach is built on three observations:
(1)
Texts generated by LLMs reflects the types of inherent toxicity present within these models
(
Klein and Nabi 2024
)
. At the same time,
Schick et al. 2021
demonstrates that LLMs recognize, to a considerable degree, their undesirable toxicity of the content they produce. Drawing on the above findings, models can generate various texts by sampling from a raw prompt
(
Cheng et al. 2023
)
(referred as self-generation). These texts can be classified as either toxic or nontoxic (referred as self-diagnosis), representing potential types of toxicity associated with the input.
(2)

Leong et al. 2023
indicates that prepending positive prefixes (i.e. harmless instructions) or negative prefixes (harmful instructions) to raw prompts can steer the model to generate content with either nontoxic or toxic attributes. Concurrently, the contextualized representations of prompt that are developing in the internal layers of LLMs can be conceived as information flows, primarily facilitated by attention heads for the movement of information between the layers
(
Elhage et al. 2021
)
. Inspired by them, We can dynamically add self-generated text as positive and negative prefixes, thereby guiding the context representations of the prompts. Thus, they form differentiated information flows at the attention layer. We construct subtoxicity vectors based on two types of information flows.
(3)
At each prompt may correspond to multiple negative prefixes, the resulting multiple toxicity vectors represent different subtoxic behaviors. We aim to fuse these toxicity vectors to maximize the coverage of potential toxic behaviors.
Ilharco et al. 2023
proposes arithmetic operations in the weight space for multi-task learning in models. Motivated by this, we aim to perform arithmetic operations on subtoxicity vectors to fuse various subtoxicities. Ultimately, detoxification generation, equipped with the raw prompt, is accomplished by utilizing a fused toxicity vector within the attention space.

Therefore, we propose FGDILP, a detoxification framework considering subtoxicity information through instance-level prefixes. Specifically, we utilize the model to self-generate multiple sampled texts of a raw prompt and self-diagnose them for classifying into subtoxic and nontoxic categories as prefixes. At each decoding step, a batch synthetic prompts, consisting of a positive prefix-prepended prompt and multiple negative prefix-prepended prompts, is passed through the attention layer to construct vectors representing different subtoxic behaviors. Finally, we fuse them using arithmetic operations to collaboratively rectify the information flow of the raw prompt within the the model, thereby aiding the detoxification process.

In summary, our contributions are as follows:

\bullet

FGDILP dynamically constructs multiple prefixes to collaboratively correct the contextualized representation for removal of fine-grained subtoxicities.

\bullet

Our approach surpasses current prompt-based methods while maintaining a lightweight structure compared to other methods.

\bullet

We deeply analyze the construction of a synergistic mechanism involving the generation of prefixes and the fusion of toxicity vectors for instance-level detoxification, paving the way for exploring the detoxification of various fine-grained subtoxicities.

2
Model

2.1
Task Formulation

Given a prompt
t=\{t_{1},t_{2},...,t_{N}\}
with
N
tokens, a causal language modeling (CLM) can generate fluent text based on
t
. The task of language detoxification is to mitigate toxic content within the text. Specifically, toxic content represents the insults, threats, profanity, etc
(
Rae et al. 2021
)
.

2.2
Model Overview

In this paper, we propose fine-grained detoxification by instance-level prefixes (FGDILP) for large language models. As illustrated in Figure
1
, FGDILP first prepares instance-level prefixes encompassing negative and a positive prefixes. It then prepends all prefixes to the prompt of CLM to construct subtoxicity vectors and fuse them for exhibiting instance-level toxic behaviors. The resulting vector steers the detoxification process.

Figure 1:
Overview of FGDILP. In Step 1-1, multiple outputs are sampled from the model through self-generation. In Step 1-2, all outputs are categorized into toxic (negative) and nontoxic (positive) prefixes by self-diagnosis. In Step 2, a positive prefix and multiple negative prefixes (one for each subtoxicity) are prepended with the raw prompt to form a batch. During the forward pass, their contextualized representations are compared to construct all subtoxicity vectors, which are then fused into one in attention space. In step 3, information flow is corrected when the raw prompt passes through the model for detoxification.

2.3
Generation of Instance-Level Prefixes

With a CLM and a prompt
t
, each text
x
can be sampled by self-generation. Subsequently, following the subtoxicity descriptions
y
and
x
, we adpot the approach proposed by
Schick et al. 2021
and
Ma et al. 2023
to construct a sef-diagnosis (sdg) input (i.e. sdg
(x,y)=[x;y]
) using the template provided in Table
9
. Specifically, this entails concatenating the generated text
x
with a query regarding the presence of a subtoxicity and instructing the model to produce either “Yes” or “No”. We calculate the probability of
x
exhibiting attribute
y
as:

P(y|x)=\frac{P_{CLM}(\text{Yes}|sdg(x,y))}{\sum_{t\in\{\text{Yes, No}\}}P_{C}LM(t|(sdg(x,y)))}

(1)

based on the probabilities assigned by the CLM to the terms “Yes” and “No” in response to the self-diagnostic input. We select texts with the highest probability for each subtoxicity as negative prefixes (i.e.
NP_{1}
,
NP_{2}
,…). The positive prefix (i.e.
PP
) is derived from texts with the lowest “toxicity”. Section
4.1
demonstrates the efficacy of instance-level prefixes for reducing fine-grained subtoxicity.

2.4
Utilization of Subtoxicity Vectors

Subtoxicity Vector Construction
Each negative prefix may evoke different subtoxic behaviors. We construct an array of subtoxicity vectors, which are established through the comparative analysis of a positive prefix against all negative prefixes ground in contextualized representations. Subsequently, these vectors are fused as a toxicity probe that guides the detoxification process.

For a CLM, the
l
-th transformer layer primarily consists of two modules: multi-head self-attention (MHSA) and a multi-layer perceptron (MLP). We manipulate the attention sublayers of the MHSA to mitigate toxicity. The
l
-th attention sublayer contains three project matrices,
W_{Q}^{l}
,
W_{K}^{l}
and
W_{V}^{l}

\in

\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Each project matric is divided into
N
heads denoted as
W_{Q}^{l,n}
,
W_{K}^{l,n}
,
W_{V}^{l,n}

\in

\mathbb{R}^{d\times\frac{d}{N}}
for
n\in[1,N]
. Ultimately, the contextualized representation
v_{i}^{l,n}

\in

\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the
i
-th token at the
n
-th head can be formulated as:

\displaystyle v^{l,n}

\displaystyle=A^{l,n}(t_{i}^{l-1}W_{V}^{l,n})

\displaystyle A^{l,n}

\displaystyle=Softmax(\frac{t_{i}^{l-1}W_{Q}^{l,n}(t_{i}^{l-1}W_{K}^{l,n})^{T}}{\sqrt{d/N}})

(2)

We introduce a positive prefix-prepended prompt and J negative prefix-prepended prompts as
P^{+}=[PP;t]
and
P_{1}^{-}=[NP_{1};t]
,…,
P_{J}^{-}=[NP_{J};t]
, to form batches for input into the CLM. We construct multiple subtoxicity vectors by contrasting the contextualized representation of the last token obtained from all negative inputs (i.e.
P_{1}^{-}
,
P_{2}^{-}
…,
P_{J}^{-}
) with that a positive input (i.e.
P_{+}
). Specifically, the
j
-th subtoxicity vector
\Delta_{j}^{l}

\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
representing the
l
-th layer in the CLM is denoted as follows:

\displaystyle\Delta_{j}^{l}

\displaystyle=v_{P_{j}^{-}}^{l}-v_{p^{+}}^{l},j\in[1,J]

\displaystyle v_{P_{j}^{-}}^{l}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{C}\text{oncatenate}(v_{P_{j}^{-}}^{l,1},...,v_{P_{j}^{-}}^{l,N})

(3)

where
v_{P_{j}^{-}}^{l,1}

\in\mathbb{R}^{\frac{d}{N}}
denotes the contextualized representation obtained from the first attention head of the
l
-th layer when the input includes the
j
-th negative prefix as defined
in Equation
2
.
\mathbb{C}
oncatenate merges all vectors from N attention heads, resulting in
v_{P_{j}^{-}}^{l}

\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Similarly,
v_{p^{+}}^{l}

\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
represents the contextualized representations of the
j
-th positive prefix-prepended prompt at the
l
-th layer.

Multiple Subtoxicity Vectors Fusion
Subtoxicity vectors influence the information flow in the model’s output across different types of toxic text. Redundancy and interference are present among these vectors. To emphasize both shared and distinct characteristics within toxicity vectors and balance the influence of the most significant toxic subclasses. We propose a three-step operation for fusing subtoxicity vectors: Masking, Symbolization, Alignment.
(1) Masking: Each vector
\Delta_{j}^{l}
, denoted as
v_{j}
, undergoes a masking process in which only the top-
k
% of values are retained, while the bottom (1-
k
)% are set to 0 based on their magnitudes. (2) Symbolization: We create a sign vector
s
for the fused vector. At each position
p

\in\{1,..,d\}
, we select a sign based on the greatest total magnitude across all vectors. Specifically, we separate the element
\{v_{j}^{p}\}_{p=1}^{d}
based on their sign (+1 or -1) and compute the total magnitude by summing those with opposite signs.
s^{p}
is then assigned the sign associated with the greater total magnitude, expressed as
s^{p}
=
sgn(\sum_{j=1}^{J}v_{j}^{p})
. (3) For each element of
v_{j}
at
p
, elements whose signs align with the
s^{p}
are retained, followed by the determination of their maximum value. Formally, let
O^{p}=\{j\in[J]|\hat{s}_{j}^{p}=s^{p}\}
. Then the fused value at position
p
, denoted as
V^{p}
, is defined as
V^{p}=max\{{v}_{j}^{p}\}
, where
j\in O^{p}
. Therefore, the final merged toxicity vector
V
at layer
l
(referred as
\Delta^{l}
) is given by:

\Delta^{l}=\mathbb{F}\text{usion}(\Delta_{1}^{l},\Delta_{2}^{l},..,\Delta_{N}^{l})

(4)

where
\mathbb{F}
usion denotes the three steps that fuse multiple subtoxicity vectors, with
N
representing the total number of vectors. Section
4.3
verifies the necessity of the three components within the subtoxicity vectors fusion phase.

2.5
Detoxification

The raw vector at the
l
-th attention layer is denoted as
v_{P_{0}}^{l}
when the raw prompt passes through the CLM during the inference stage. Our approach aims to reduce the presence of toxic elements in this vector by steering it away from toxic directions. The detoxified vector,
v_{P}^{l}
, is defined as follows:

\displaystyle v_{P}^{l}

\displaystyle=v_{P_{0}}^{l}-\lambda_{\text{norm}}^{\alpha}\lambda_{\text{sim}}^{\beta}\Delta^{l}

\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{norm}}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=1+\|v_{P_{0}}^{l}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{sim}}^{\beta}

\displaystyle=1+max\{0,cos(v_{P}^{l},v_{P_{\text{all}}^{-}}^{l})\}

\displaystyle v_{P_{\text{all}}^{-}}^{l}

\displaystyle=mean(v_{P_{1}^{-}}^{l},...,v_{P_{N}^{-}}^{l})

(5)

where
\lambda_{\text{sim}}
calculates the similarity between the vector when generated by the raw prompt at the current token and the vector at the last token when provided with all negative prepended-prefix prompts.
\lambda_{\text{norm}}^{\alpha}
scales the strength of modification by the
L^{2}
-norm between different vectors.
v_{P_{\text{all}}^{-}}^{l}
denotes the mean of all subtoxicity vectors. Two hyperparameters,
\alpha
and
\beta
, regulate the extent of detoxification.

3.4
Human Evaluation

Automatic evaluation is inherently susceptible to data bias and errors. Therefore, human evaluation is essential for a more dependable assessment of our FGDILP. We randomly sample 150 prompts (comprising 50 for “Ours” vs. DAPT, 50 for “Ours” vs. GeDi, and 50 for “Ours” vs. “SDVTR”) from RealToxicityPrompts dataset. For each prompt, we gather 3 texts from 25 texts generated using each detoxification method. Evaluators are required to assess along three dimensions: (1)
Less Toxic
, determining which one exhibits lower levels of rudeness, offensiveness, and harm. (2)
More Fluent
, identifying the narrative that is more cohesive and easy to follow. (3)
More Coherent
, assessing the adherence of language style and topic consistency to the provided prompt.

Figure
2
shows that our method has significant advantages over DAPT across various evaluation dimensions. While GeDi excels in mitigating toxicity, it concurrently compromises fluency and coherent. In comparison to SDVTR, our method exhibits a decline in fluency according to automatic evaluation. However, human evaluation indicates higher fluency in the content generated by our approach. It underscores the limitation of automatic evaluation indicators in fully capturing fluency in continuously generated content. Human evaluation emerges as the more reliable and comprehensive measure. The human evaluation for FFT is presented in Appendix
D.5
.

Analysis of Toxicity Vectors

Figure

Related Work

Detoxifying Language Model
LLMs exhibit exceptional performance across various text generation tasks
(
Qian et al. 2022
;
Wei et al. 2022
;
Zhou et al. 2023
)
due to their exposure to extensive textual corpora during pre-training. However, corpora inevitably contain toxic content, which may lead the model to occasionally generate toxic language. Recent studies primarily categorize detoxification into three approaches: finetuning-based, decoding-based, and prompt-based. Firstly,
finetuning-based
approaches further train the model on carefully selected nontoxic corpora
(
Gururangan et al. 2020
;
Gehman et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2022
)
. Training the model based on instruction prefixes is another option
(
Keskar et al. 2019
)
. Secondly,
decoding-based
approaches mitigate toxicity by altering the output distribution of language models at the token level. Generative Discriminator (GeDi)
Krause et al. 2020
and Discriminative Language Model (ADLM)
Kwak et al. 2023
use an attribute discriminator to guide decoding process. Furthermore, DEXPERTS
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
and MARCO
(
Hallinan et al. 2023
)
train expert models on toxic datasets and anti-expert models on nontoxic datasets based on a product of experts
(
Hinton 2002
)
. Tokens receive high probability only if they are deemed likely by the experts and unlikely by the anti-experts. Similarly, DETOXIGEN
(
Niu et al. 2024
)
trains a detoxifier to produce undesirable tokens. Thirdly,
prompt-based
methods direct the model to refrain from producing specific toxic words based on internal knowledge
(
Tang et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2023a
)
. SD
(
Schick et al. 2021
)
give a textual description of the undesired behavior to reduce the probability of a
language model producing problematic text. Differently, SDTVR
(
Leong et al. 2023
)
executes toxification reversal at the attention layer to prevent the generations associated with toxicity. However, fine-tuning-based methods require additional high-quality training data, whereas decoding-based methods require auxiliary models.

Task Arithmetic
It is a method that merges models by generating task vectors and performing arithmetic operations
(
Ilharco et al. 2023
)
. Particularly,
(
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Hu et al. 2023
)
handle the parameter-efficient modules via linear arithmetic operations, thus integrating various module capabilities for unlearning tasks. Another application is Multi-task learning (MTL), which empowers a model to tackle multiple tasks simultaneously. Each fine-tuned module for distinct tasks can be directly integrated into a single model to execute MTL without necessitating a retraining process
(
Yang et al. 2023
;
Daheim et al. 2023
;
Yu et al. 2023
;
Yadav et al. 2023
)
.

Various prompts trigger different subtoxicities responses. Existing methods lack instance-level detoxification strategies. Instead, we consider the subtoxicities evoked by each instance. In detail, self-generated prefixes appended to the prompt not only allows for personalized detoxification but does not require extra data resources.

6
Conclusion

In this study, we propose fine-grained detoxification via instance-level prefixes, FGDILP, aimed at detoxifying language models without requiring additional data or models. Our FGDILP contrasts contextualized representations obtained from a singular positive prefix-prepend prompt with those derived from multiple fine-grained negative prefix-prepend prompts at the instance level. The resulting multiple subtoxicity vectors collectively steer the flow of information within the model. Our method has demonstrated effectiveness in detoxifying texts at both the utterance-level and context-level, outperforming all prompt-based methods in automatic and human evaluations.

Limitations

Our approach necessitates segregating the self-generated content via self-diagnosis into negative and positive prefixes for constructing toxicity vectors. However, the assessment of prefix attributes relies on the pre-trained model’s own cognizance of various subtoxicity types. When specific toxicity types are encountered that are absent in the pre-training data, the identification of prefix attributes becomes uncertain. Moreover, even if the model is acquainted with relevant toxicity concepts, the automatic utilization of predetermined template structures during self-diagnosis unavoidably impacts the classification of specific subtoxicities. Overall, the performance of our method is influenced by the pre-trained model’s coverage of toxicity knowledge during the pre-training phase and its understanding of instruction templates.

Additionally, our approach relies on Perspective API for automatically assessing the toxicity at the utterance-level and Llama-Guard for evaluating toxicity at the context-level. Failure to identify subtoxicities by either tool can result in inaccurate scoring outcomes. Firstly, various implicitly toxic elements generated by language models, implying toxicity without using explicit toxic words, pose a challenge for detection through the Perspective API
(
Wen et al. 2023
)
. Conversely, despite being a large model fine-tuned on high-quality data, Llama-Guard falls short of ensuring flawless coverage of security policies across specific test datasets, often exhibiting subpar performance. This concern is addressed by incorporating supplementary human evaluation, despite its inherent limitations. Constraints in funding and resources result in a notably smaller sample size for human evaluation compared to automatic assessment, potentially influenced by assessors’ personal biases and cognitive tendencies on classification outcomes.

Ethics Statement

Not only constrained by the limitations stated in section
Limitations
, but also by various unexplored factors, our method remains unable to entirely mitigate the toxic content. Our approach aims at achieving the short-term objective of reducing the probability and severity of toxic behaviors resulting from the outputs of LLMs. Therefore, more testing and consideration are necessary to refine this approach when deploying it for real-world applications.

We acknowledge the potentially harmful content generated by the model, as demonstrated in the examples provided in Appendix
H
. Such content may cause discomfort to readers or raise ethical concerns. It is important to stress that the selection of these examples is not based on subjective viewpoints. Our intention is to conduct a case study and compare the specific disparities between scenarios with and without detoxification measures. We advise against utilizing these examples to propagate negative opinions or perceptions.

Appendix A
Baselines

Fintuning-based
The idea allows the target model to continue training on nontoxic data for mitigating toxic behaviors. Domain-Adaptive Pretraining (DAPT)
(
Gururangan et al. 2020
)
and Attribute Conditioning (ATCON)
(
Keskar et al. 2019
)
serve as the two baselines in our experiments. DAPT is a positive language model obtained by continuing to train on positive corpora from OpenWebText, aimed at generating nontoxic content. ATCON is fine-tuned using prefixes such as “nontoxic”. Therefore, during the inference process, prefixes are incorporated into the prompts to produce nontoxic texts without additional training.

Decoding-based
The methods dynamically reduce the likelihood of tokens expressing toxic viewpoints. To this end, the principles for adjusting the token probability distribution are mainly divided into training the guidance model or comparative retrieval. The representative works in the former category are GeDi
(
Krause et al. 2020
)
and DEXPERTS
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
, whereas for the latter, we examine GOODTRIEVER
(
Pozzobon et al. 2023
)
. GeDi guides generation at each step by computing classification probabilities for all possible next tokens. DEXPERTS reweights the predictions of language models based on expert (i.e. tends to generate nontoxic tokens) and anti-expert (i.e. tending to generating toxic content). GOODTRIEVER eliminates the necessity for training additional models. It retrieves relevant toxic text segments and nontoxic text segments based on the current prefix to ensemble the next token probabilities. Specifically, it readjusts the probabilities of originally high-probability words when generating texts without prefixes.

Prompt-based
The prompt-based method mitigates the generation of toxic content based on the model’s inherent of toxicity. SD
(
Schick et al. 2021
)
employs a negative prefix to adjust the probability of toxic tokens. SDVTR
(
Leong et al. 2023
)
is a method for identifying toxic directions using a negative prefix as the direction bias. It is subtracted during generation when no prefix is used. Our methodology belongs to this specific subclass. However, a significant difference lies in our approach, wherein we construct subtoxicity vectors based on multiple sample-level prefixes and eliminate biases associated with subtoxicities. It significantly enhances the coverage of fine-grained toxicities during the detoxification process.

F.1
The Importance of Symbolization

As the number of subtoxicities increases from 2 to 6, the count of values exhibiting contradictory signs at corresponding positions between vectors also rises in Figure
4
. Furthermore, the proportion of sign contradictions between vectors in higher layers increases. In comparison to GPT-2-Large, Llama-2-7B exhibits a higher frequency of symbol contradictions. The essence of symbolization lies in the minority adhering to the majority to steer the direction of toxicity and mitigate interference. Symbolization is dependent on the signs of values at corresponding positions within the toxicity vectors. Specifically, the outcome of symbolization is dictated by the summation of the elements within the set, resulting in either a positive or negative outcome.

F.2
The Importance of Alignment

After applying the three alignment methods (“Max”, “Mean”, “Sum”), we obtain the distribution of fused vector values. The default model is Llama-2-7B. We utilize the “Max" alignment method, which selects the highest element value based on the predetermined symbol direction. Figure

illustrates that the “Mean” method more effectively compresses the values of high-magnitude elements (> 0.2 and < -0.2) in the vector compared to the “Max” method. The “Sum” method significantly increases the count of high-magnitude element values. However, it also simultaneously increases the potential risk of disrupting the original flow of information.
