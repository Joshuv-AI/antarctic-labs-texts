On the dynamics of the roots of polynomials under differentiation
Thomas Alazard, Omar Lazar, Quoc-Hung Nguyen

Abstract
This article is devoted to the study of a nonlinear and nonlocal parabolic equation introduced by Stefan Steinerberger to study the roots of polynomials under differentiation; it also appeared in a work by Dimitri Shlyakhtenko and Terence Tao on free convolution. Rafael Granero-Belinchón obtained a global well-posedness result for initial data small enough in a Wiener space, and recently Alexander Kiselev and Changhui Tan proved a global well-posedness result for any initial data in the Sobolev space H s ​ ( 𝕊 ) H^{s}(\mathbb{S}) with s > 3 / 2 s>3/2 . In this paper, we consider the Cauchy problem in the critical space H 1 / 2 ​ ( 𝕊 ) H^{1/2}(\mathbb{S}) . Two interesting new features, at this level of regularity, are that the equation can be written in the form ∂ t u + V ​ ∂ x u + γ ​ Λ ​ u = 0 , \partial_{t}u+V\partial_{x}u+\gamma\Lambda u=0, where V V is not bounded and γ \gamma is not bounded from below. Therefore, the equation is only weakly parabolic. We prove that nevertheless the Cauchy problem is well posed locally in time and that the solutions are smooth for positive times. Combining this with the results of Kiselev and Tan, this gives a global well-posedness result for any initial data in H 1 / 2 ​ ( 𝕊 ) H^{1/2}(\mathbb{S}) . Our proof relies on sharp commutators estimates and introduces a strategy to prove a local well-posedness result in a situation where the lifespan depends on the profile of the initial data and not only on its norm.

Introduction

Stefan Steinerberger studied in
[
37
]

the following question:
Considering a polynomial
p_{n}
of degree
n
having all its roots
roots on the real line distributed according to a smooth function
u_{0}(x)
, and a real-number
t\in(0,1)
,
how is the distribution of the roots of the derivatives
\partial_{x}^{k}p_{n}
distributed with
k=\lfloor t\cdot n\rfloor
?
This question led him to discover a nice nonlocal nonlinear equation of the form

\partial_{t}u+\frac{1}{\pi}\partial_{x}\left(\arctan\left(\frac{Hu}{u}\right)\right)=0,

(1)

where the unknown
u=u(t,x)
is a positive real-valued function.

Besides its aesthetic aspect, this equation has many interesting features.
Shlyakhtenko and Tao
[
35
]
derived the same equation in the context of free probability and random matrix theory
(see also
[
38
]
). However, our motivation comes from the links between this equation and many models studied in fluid dynamics.

In this paper, we assume that
the space variable
x
belongs to the circle
\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{R}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})
, and
H
is the
circular Hilbert transform (which acts on periodic functions),
defined by

Hg(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\pv\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{g(x)-g(x-\alpha)}{\tan(\alpha/2)}\diff\!\alpha,

(2)

where the integral is understood as a principal value. Granero-Belinchón (
[
25
]
)
proved the local existence of smooth solutions
for initial data
u_{0}
in the Sobolev space
H^{2}(\mathbb{S})=\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})\,;\,\partial_{x}^{2}u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})\}
,
as well as the global existence under a
condition in appropriate Wiener spaces.
Then, Kiselev and Tan proved
[
28
]
that the Cauchy problem for (
1
) is globally well-posed in the homogeneous Sobolev space
\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{S})
for all
s>3/2
,
where
\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{S})=\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})\,;\,\Lambda^{s}u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})\}
where
\Lambda
denotes the fractional Laplacian:

\Lambda=\partial_{x}H=(-\partial_{xx})^{\frac{1}{2}}.

In fact, the equation (
1
) enters the family of fractional parabolic equations, which has attracted a lot of attention in recent years.
To see this, introduce the coefficients

V=-\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{Hu}{u^{2}+(Hu)^{2}}\quad,\quad\gamma=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{u}{u^{2}+(Hu)^{2}}\cdot

Then the equation (
1
) has the following form

\partial_{t}u+V\partial_{x}u+\gamma\Lambda u=0.

(3)

This last equation shares many characteristics
with the Hele-Shaw equation, the Muskat equation, the
dissipative quasi-geostrophic equation, and 1D models for the 3D incompressible Euler equation (
[
19
]
), to name a few.
Many different techniques have been introduced to study these problems.

For the dissipative SQG equation, the global regularity
has been proved by Kiselev, Nazarov and Volberg
[
27
]
, Caffarelli-Vasseur
[
8
]

and
Constantin-Vicol
[
20
]
(see also
[
26
,
36
,
40
,
32
]
). The nonlinearity in the Muskat equation is more
complicated. However,
Cameron has succeeded in
[
9
]
to apply
the method introduced by Kiselev-Nazarov-Volberg to prove
the existence of global solutions in time
when the product of the maximum and minimum slopes is less than
1
.
Recently, many works have extended this last result.
The main results in
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
imply that
the Cauchy problem can be solved for non-Lipschitz initial
data, following earlier work
by Deng, Lei and Lin
[
23
]
, Cameron
[
9
]
,
Córdoba and Lazar
[
22
]
, Gancedo
and Lazar
[
24
]
which allowed arbitrary slopes
of large size. Recently, in
[
13
]
the third author proved
local existence with
C^{1}
initial data.
The main issue is that it is difficult to dispense with the
assumption of finite slopes. Indeed, the classical nonlinear estimates
require to control the
L^{\infty}
norm of some factors,
which is the same for the Muskat problem
to control the
L^{\infty}
norm of the slope, which in turn
amounts to controlling
the Lipschitz norm of
f
.
Second, the Muskat equation is a degenerate parabolic equation
for solutions which
are not controlled in the Lipschitz norm. We also refer
interested readers to
[
14
]
for another non-local parabolic equation.

Inspired by these results, our goal here is to solve the Cauchy problem
for (
1
)
in the critical Sobolev space
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{S})
.
Several interesting difficulties appear at that level of regularity.

The main result of this paper is the following

Theorem 1.1
.

For all initial data
u_{0}
in
\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{T})

such that
\inf u_{0}>0
,
the Cauchy problem has a global in time solution
u

satisfying the following properties:

(i)

u\in C^{0}([0,+\infty);\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{S}))\cap L^{2}((0,+\infty);\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{S}))

together with the estimate

\sup_{t>0}\left\|u(t)\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{u|\Lambda u|^{2}}{u^{2}+(Hu)^{2}}\diff\!x\diff\!t\leq 10\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}.

(4)

(ii)

u\in C^{\infty}((0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{S})
and moreover
for any
s>0
and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
there exists a constant

C(s,\varepsilon_{0})
such that

\sup_{t>0}t^{s+\epsilon_{0}}\left\|u(t)\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}+s}}\leq C(s,\varepsilon_{0})\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

(iii)

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in\mathbb{S}}u(t,x)\geq\displaystyle\inf_{x\in\mathbb{S}}u_{0}(x)
for
t>0
.

Remark 1.2
.

The main difficulty is that
the coercive quantity that appears in the left-hand member of (
4

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{u|\Lambda u|^{2}}{u^{2}+(Hu)^{2}}\diff\!x\diff\!t,

(5)

is insufficient to control
the
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{L^{2}_{t}\dot{H}_{x}^{1}}
-norm of

.
Indeed, even if we assume that the initial value of

is bounded
(which propagates in time), since the Hilbert transform is
not bounded from
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S})
to itself, we would

.

Regarding uniqueness, we have the following theorem,

Theorem 1.3
.

For any
a>0
and initial data
u_{0}\in\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}+a}(\mathbb{S})
,
such that
\inf u_{0}>0
, the Cauchy problem has a unique global in time solution.

Remark 1.4
.

Our uniqueness theorem improves the one obtained by Kiselev and Tan
[
28
]
. Indeed, they proved uniqueness in the space
\dot{H}^{\frac{3}{2}+a}(\mathbb{S})
while our result deals with data in the space
\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}+a}(\mathbb{S})
. The question of uniqueness in the case
\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{S})
remains open.

Plan of the paper

Since Kiselev and Tan (

) proved that

2
Estimate in
\dot{H}^{1/2}
for a toy model

To prove Theorem
1.1
,
the main difficulty is that, even if we know that
u
satisfies
a maximum principle, the coefficient
\gamma
is not bounded from
below by a positive constant. Indeed, it is well-known that the Hilbert
transform is not bounded on
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S})
.
This means that (
3
) is a degenerate parabolic equation.

Although it is not essential for the rest of the paper, it helps if we
begin by examining a model equation with some non-degenerate dissipative term.
Our goal here is to introduce a basic
commutator estimate which allows to deal with equations of the form (
3
).

Consider the equation

\partial_{t}u+a(u,Hu)\Lambda u=b(u,Hu)H\Lambda u,

(6)

where
a
and
b
are two
C^{\infty}
real-valued functions
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, satisfying

a\geq m>0
for some given positive constant
m
,
together with

\sup_{(x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{4}}\frac{\left|b(x,y)-b(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\right|}{\left|x-x^{\prime}\right|+\left|y-y^{\prime}\right|}<+\infty.

Proposition 2.1
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that, for all
T>0
and for
all
u\in C^{1}([0,T];\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{S}))
solution to (
6
), there holds

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\diff}{\diff\!t}\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}+m\int_{\mathbb{S}}\left|\Lambda u\right|^{2}\diff\!x\leq C\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left\|\Lambda u\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(7)

Remark 2.2
.

Using classical arguments, it is then possible to infer
from the a priori estimate (
7
) a global well-posedness result for
initial data which are small
enough in
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{S})
.
However, the study of the local well-posedness of the Cauchy problem
for large data is more difficult and requires and extra argument
which is explained in the next section.

Proof.

Let us use the short notations

a=a(u,Hu)
and
b:=b(u,Hu)
.
Multiplying the equation (
6
) by
\Lambda u
and integrating over
\mathbb{S}
, we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\diff}{\diff\!t}\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{S}}a\left|\Lambda u\right|^{2}\diff\!x=I\mathrel{:=}\int_{\mathbb{S}}b(H\Lambda u)(\Lambda u)\diff\!x.

(8)

To estimate
I
, we exploit the fact that

H^{*}=-H
to write

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}}b(H\Lambda u)(\Lambda u)\diff\!x-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}}(\Lambda u)H\big(b\Lambda u\big)\diff\!x

(9)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\big(\big[b,H\big]\Lambda u\big)\Lambda u\diff\!x.

Now we claim that

\left\|\big[b,H\big]\Lambda u\right\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\left\|b\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left\|\Lambda u\right\|_{L^{2}}.

(10)

Indeed, this follows from
the
Sobolev embedding
\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}\subset\text{BMO}
and the classical commutator estimate

\left\|[H,f]v\right\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{\text{BMO}}\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}}.

(11)

(Alternatively one can prove (
10
)
directly using the definition of the Hilbert transform
as a singular integral and the Gagliardo semi-norm; see below.) It follows that

I\lesssim\left\|b\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left\|\Lambda u\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(12)

Now we estimate the
\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}
-norm of
b
by means of the following elementary estimate.

Lemma 2.3
.

Consider a
C^{\infty}
function
\sigma\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying

\forall(x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{4},\qquad\left|\sigma(x,y)-\sigma(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\right|\leq K\left|x-x^{\prime}\right|+K\left|y-y^{\prime}\right|.

Then, for all
s\in(0,1)
and all
u\in\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R})
,
one has
\sigma(u,Hu)\in\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R})
together with the estimate

\left\|\sigma(u,Hu)\right\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}\leq K\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}.

(13)

Proof.

By assumption, for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\left\|\delta_{\alpha}\sigma(u,Hu)\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq K\left\|\delta_{\alpha}u\right\|_{L^{2}}+\left\|\delta_{\alpha}Hu\right\|_{L^{2}}.

Then by using the Gagliardo semi-norms, we get

\left\|\sigma(u,Hu)\right\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}\leq K\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}+K\left\|Hu\right\|_{\dot{H}^{s}},

and the desired result follows since

\left\|Hu\right\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}=\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}
.
∎

The previous lemma implies that

\left\|b\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\lesssim\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}+\left\|Hu\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\lesssim\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}},

and we deduce from (
12
) that

I\lesssim\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left\|\Lambda u\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Therefore the wanted result (
7
) follows from (
8
).
∎

3
Local well-posedness

We construct solutions to (
1
)
as limits of solutions to a sequence of
approximate nonlinear systems.
We divide the analysis into three parts.

1.

We start by proving that the Cauchy problem for these systems
systems are well posed globally
in time and satisfy the maximum principles. In particular,
the approximate solutions are bounded by a positive constant.

2.

Then, we show that
the solutions of the approximate systems are
bounded in
C^{0}([0,T];\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{S}))
.
on a uniform time interval that depends on the profile of the initial
data (and not only on their norm).

3.

The third task is to show that these approximate solutions converge to a limit which is
a solution of the original equation.
To do this, we use interpolation and compactness arguments.

3.1
Approximate systems

Fix
\delta\in(0,1]
and
consider the following approximate Cauchy problem:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}u+\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{u\Lambda u-(Hu)\partial_{x}u}{\delta+u^{2}+(Hu)^{2}}-\delta\partial_{x}^{2}u=0,\\
&u\arrowvert_{t=0}=e^{\delta\partial_{x}^{2}}u_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

(14)

The following lemma states that this Cauchy problem
has smooth solutions.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any positive initial data
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})

and for any
\delta>0
, the initial value problem (
14
)
has a unique solution

u
in
C^{1}([0,+\infty);H^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}))
.
This solution is such that, for all
t\in[0,+\infty)
,

\inf_{x\in\mathbb{S}}u(t,x)\geq\inf_{x\in\mathbb{S}}u_{0}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad\max_{x\in\mathbb{S}}u(t,x)\leq\max_{x\in\mathbb{S}}u_{0}(x).

(15)

Proof.

The proof is classical and follows from arguments already introduced by Granero in
[
25
]
, but we repeat it for completeness.

Fix
\delta>0
.
The Cauchy problem (
14
) has the following form

\partial_{t}u-\delta\partial_{x}^{2}u=F_{\delta},\quad u\arrowvert_{t=0}=e^{\delta\partial_{x}^{2}}u_{0},

(16)

where

F_{\delta}=-\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{u\Lambda u-(Hu)\partial_{x}u}{\delta+u^{2}+(Hu)^{2}}\cdot

Step 1: existence of mild solution locally in time
.
Since
H^{\frac{1}{2}+\nu}(\mathbb{S})\subset L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S})

for all
\nu>0
and since the circular Hilbert transform

H
is bounded on

H^{\frac{1}{2}+\nu}(\mathbb{S})
,
it is easy to verify that

\displaystyle\left\|F_{\delta}(u)-F_{\delta}(v)\right\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\delta}\big(\left\|u\right\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2}+\nu}}+\left\|v\right\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2}+\nu}}\big)\left\|u-v\right\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\quad+\big(\left\|u\right\|_{H^{1}}+\left\|v\right\|_{H^{1}}\big)\left\|u-v\right\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2}+\nu}},

where the notation
\lesssim_{\delta}
is intended to indicate that the
implicit constant depends on
\delta
.
Therefore, it follows from the interpolation inequality in Sobolev space, from the fixed point theorem and from
the usual energy estimate for the heat equation that
the Cauchy problem has a unique mild
solution in
C^{0}([0,T_{\delta});L^{2}(\mathbb{S}))\cap L^{2}(0,T_{\delta};H^{1}(\mathbb{S}))
, where

T_{\delta}
is estimable from below in terms of
\left\|u\arrowvert_{t=0}\right\|_{L^{2}}
(see
[
39
, Section 15.1]
).
In particular, we have the following alternative: either

T_{\delta}=+\infty\qquad\text{or}\qquad\limsup_{t\rightarrow T_{\delta}}\left\|u(t)\right\|_{L^{2}}=+\infty.

(17)

Step 2 : Global well-posedness

On the other hand, directly from the obvious estimate

\displaystyle\left\|F_{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim_{\delta}\left\|u\right\|_{H^{1}},

the energy estimate for the heat equation implies that

\limsup_{{t\rightarrow T_{n}}}\left\|u(t)\right\|_{L^{2}}=+\infty

is impossible with
T_{n}<+\infty
. This proves that the solution exists globally in time.

Step 3: Regularity.
We verify that the solution defined above
is regular by noting that one can solve the Cauchy problem in
H^{s}(\mathbb{S})

for all
s>3/2
using the usual nonlinear estimates in Sobolev
spaces and the argument above. By uniqueness, this implies that
the mild solution defined above is continuous in time with values in
H^{s}(\mathbb{S})
for all
s
.

Step 4: maximum principle.

The claim (
15
)
follows from the classical arguments. Firstly, notice that

\inf_{x\in\mathbb{S}}e^{\delta\partial_{x}^{2}}u_{0}(x)\geq\inf_{x\in\mathbb{S}}u_{0}(x).

On the other hand, at a point
x_{t}
where the function
u(t,\cdot)
reaches its minimum, we have

\partial_{x}u(t,x_{t})=0,\quad\partial_{x}^{2}u(t,x_{t})\geq 0,\quad\Lambda u(t,x_{t})\leq 0,

where the last inequality follows from the fact that

\Lambda u(x)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\pv\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{u(x)-u(x-\alpha)}{\sin(\alpha/2)^{2}}\diff\!\alpha.

It follows that
\inf_{x\in\mathbb{S}}u(t,x)\geq\inf_{x\in\mathbb{S}}u_{0}(x)
.

By similar arguments, we obtain the second inequality

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{S}}u(t,x)\leq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{S}}u_{0}(x)
.
This completes the proof.
∎

3.2
Uniform estimates

Fix
\delta>0
and
c_{0}
and consider an initial data

u_{0}
in
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{S})
with
\inf_{x\in\mathbb{S}}u_{0}(x)\geq c_{0}
.
As we have seen in the previous paragraph, there exists a unique
function

u\in C^{1}([0,+\infty);H^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}))
satisfying

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}u+\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{u\Lambda u-(Hu)\partial_{x}u}{\delta+u^{2}+(Hu)^{2}}-\delta\partial_{x}^{2}u=0,\\
&u\arrowvert_{t=0}=e^{\delta\partial_{x}^{2}}u_{0},\\
&\inf_{x\in\mathbb{S}}u(t,x)\geq c_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

(18)

We shall prove estimates which are uniform
with respect to
\delta\in(0,1]
(this is why
we are writing
simply
u
instead of
u_{\delta}
, to simplify notations).

Let
\varepsilon>0
. We want to estimate

v\mathrel{:=}u-e^{(\varepsilon+\delta)\partial_{x}^{2}}u_{0}.

Set

u_{0,\varepsilon}=e^{(\varepsilon+\delta)\partial_{x}^{2}}u_{0},

and introduce the coefficients

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{u}{\delta+u^{2}+(Hu)^{2}},

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{Hu}{\delta+u^{2}+(Hu)^{2}},

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\delta+u^{2}+(Hu)^{2}}.

With the previous notations, we have

\partial_{t}v+V\partial_{x}v+\gamma\Lambda v-\delta\partial_{x}^{2}v=R_{\varepsilon}(u,u_{0})

(19)

where

R_{\varepsilon}(u,u_{0})=-\gamma\Lambda u_{0,\varepsilon}-V\partial_{x}u_{0,\varepsilon}+\delta\partial_{x}^{2}u_{0,\varepsilon}.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any
u_{0}\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{S})
with
\inf_{x\in\mathbb{S}}u_{0}(x)>0
,
there exist a constant
\varepsilon_{0}
and a
function
T\colon(0,1]\to(0,1)
with

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}T(\varepsilon)=0,

such that the following result holds:
for all
\delta\in(0,1]
, all
u\in C^{1}([0,+\infty);H^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}))

satisfying (
18
) with initial data
u\arrowvert_{t=0}=e^{\delta\partial_{x}^{2}}u_{0}
,
and for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}]
, the function

v=u-e^{(\varepsilon+\delta)\partial_{x}^{2}}u_{0}
satisfies

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T(\varepsilon)]}\left\|v(t)\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T(\varepsilon)}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{u\left|\Lambda v\right|^{2}}{\delta+u^{2}+(Hu)^{2}}\diff\!x\diff\!t+\delta\int_{0}^{T(\varepsilon)}\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{3}{2}}}^{2}\diff\!t\leq\mathcal{F}(T(\varepsilon)),

(20)

for some function
\mathcal{F}\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
\lim_{\tau\to 0}\mathcal{F}(\tau)=0
.

Proof.

Hereafter,
C
denotes various constants which depend only on the constant
c_{0}
(remembering that
c_{0}
is some given constant such that
\inf u(t,x)\geq\inf u_{0}\geq c_{0}
) and we use the notation
A\lesssim_{c_{0}}B
to indicate that
A\leq CB
for such a constant
C
.

Consider a parameter
\kappa\in(0,1]
whose value is to be determined. Then decompose the Hilbert transform as

H=H_{\kappa,1}+H_{\kappa,2}
where

\displaystyle H_{\kappa,1}g(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}}g(x-\alpha)\chi\left(\frac{\alpha}{\kappa}\right)\frac{\diff\!\alpha}{\tan(\alpha/2)},

\displaystyle H_{\kappa,2}g(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}}g(x-\alpha)\left(1-\chi\left(\frac{\alpha}{\kappa}\right)\right)\frac{\diff\!\alpha}{\tan(\alpha/2)},

for some cut-off function
\chi\in C^{\infty}
satisfying

\chi=1
in
[-1,1]
and
\chi=0
in
\mathbb{R}\setminus[-2,2]
.

Multiply equation (
19
) by
\Lambda v
and then integrate over
\mathbb{S}
,
to obtain

\frac{1}{2}{\frac{\diff}{\diff\!t}}\left\|v\right\|_{{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}}^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{S}}\gamma(\Lambda v)^{2}\diff\!x=A+B+R

(21)

where

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}}V(H_{\kappa,1}\Lambda v)(\Lambda v)\diff\!x,

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}}V(H_{\kappa,2}\Lambda v)(\Lambda v)\diff\!x,

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}}R(u,u_{0,\varepsilon})(\Lambda v)\diff\!x.

Set

W\mathrel{:=}\sqrt{\gamma}\Lambda v,

so that the dissipative term in (
21
) is of the form

\int_{\mathbb{S}}\gamma(\Lambda v)^{2}\diff\!x=\int_{\mathbb{S}}W^{2}\diff\!x.

Step 1: estimate of
B
and
R
.

Directly from the definition of

\gamma
and
V
, we have

\gamma\leq\frac{1}{\pi c_{0}}\quad,\quad\left|V\right|\leq\frac{1}{2\pi c_{0}}.

(22)

One important feature of the critical problem is that the dissipative term is degenerate. This means that the coefficient
\gamma
is not bounded from below by a fixed positive constant.
As a result, we do not control the
L^{2}
-norm of

\Lambda v
.
Instead, we merely control the
L^{2}
-norm of
W=\sqrt{\gamma}\Lambda v
. Therefore, we will systematically write
\Lambda v
under the form

\Lambda v=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\sqrt{\gamma}\Lambda v=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}W.

To absorb the contribution of the factor
1/\sqrt{\gamma}
in the estimates for
B
and
R
, it will be sufficient to notice that we have the pointwise bound

\left|\frac{V}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\right|=\frac{1}{u\sqrt{\delta+u^{2}+(Hu)^{2}}}\left|Hu\right|\lesssim_{c_{0}}\left|Hu\right|.

In particular, remembering that the Hilbert transform is bounded from
L^{p}(\mathbb{S})
to
L^{p}(\mathbb{S})
for any
p\in(1,+\infty)
and using the Sobolev embedding
H^{s}(\mathbb{S})\subset L^{2/(1-2s)}(\mathbb{S})
, we deduce that

\left\|V/\sqrt{\gamma}\right\|_{L^{4}}\lesssim_{c_{0}}\left\|Hu\right\|_{L^{4}}\lesssim_{c_{0}}\left\|u\right\|_{L^{4}}\lesssim_{c_{0}}\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

(23)

Then it follows
from Hölder’s inequality that

\displaystyle\left|B\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{V}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\left|H_{\kappa,2}\Lambda v\right|\left|\sqrt{\gamma}\Lambda v\right|\diff\!x\lesssim_{c_{0}}\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left\|H_{\kappa,2}\Lambda v\right\|_{L^{4}}\left\|W\right\|_{L^{2}}.

On the other hand,

\left\|H_{\kappa,2}\Lambda v\right\|_{L^{4}}=\left\|H_{\kappa,2}\partial_{x}Hv\right\|_{L^{4}}\lesssim\kappa^{-1}\left\|Hv\right\|_{L^{4}}\lesssim\kappa^{-1}\left\|v\right\|_{L^{4}},

where we used the definition of
H_{\kappa,2}
, noting that
g_{x}(x-\alpha)=\partial_{\alpha}(g(x)-g(x-a))
and integrating by parts in
\alpha
.

By combining the previous estimates, we conclude that

\left|B\right|\lesssim_{c_{0}}\kappa^{-1}\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left\|W\right\|_{L^{2}}.

The estimate of
R
is similar. Recall that

R_{\varepsilon}(u,u_{0})=-\gamma\Lambda u_{0,\varepsilon}-V\partial_{x}u_{0,\varepsilon}+\delta\partial_{x}^{2}u_{0,\varepsilon}.

To estimate the contribution of the first term, we write

\left|\int_{\mathbb{S}}\gamma(\Lambda u_{0,\varepsilon})(\Lambda v)\diff\!x\right|\leq\left\|\sqrt{\gamma}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\left\|\Lambda u_{0,\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}}\left\|\sqrt{\gamma}\Lambda v\right\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim_{c_{0}}\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left\|W\right\|_{L^{2}},

where we have used the elementary inequality

\left\|u_{0,\varepsilon}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}\lesssim(\varepsilon+\delta)^{-\frac{1}{4}}\left\|u_{0,\varepsilon}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{4}}\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}},

since the Fourier transform of
u_{0,\varepsilon}=e^{(\varepsilon+\delta)\partial_{x}^{2}}u_{0}
is essentially localized in the interval
\left|\xi\right|\lesssim\sqrt{\varepsilon+\delta}
.
With regards to the second term, we use again the
estimate (
23
) to get

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{S}}V(\partial_{x}u_{0,\varepsilon})(\Lambda v)\diff\!x\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|V/\sqrt{\gamma}\right\|_{L^{4}}\left\|\partial_{x}u_{0,\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{4}}\left\|\sqrt{\gamma}\Lambda v\right\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{c_{0}}\varepsilon^{-1}\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left\|W\right\|_{L^{2}}.

Eventually,
we have

\left\|\delta\partial_{x}^{2}u_{0,\varepsilon}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}=\left\|\delta\partial_{x}^{2}e^{(\varepsilon+\delta)\partial_{x}^{2}}u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\lesssim\left\|\delta\partial_{x}^{2}e^{\delta\partial_{x}^{2}}u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\lesssim\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

So, by combining the previous inequalities, we conclude that

B+R\lesssim_{c_{0}}\kappa^{-1}\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left\|W\right\|_{L^{2}}+\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1+\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}})\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left\|W\right\|_{L^{2}}+\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}},

hence, replacing
u
by
v+u_{0,\varepsilon}
in the right-hand side, we conclude that

\displaystyle B+R

\displaystyle\lesssim_{c_{0}}\left(\kappa^{-1}\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}+\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left\|W\right\|_{L^{2}}

(24)

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big(\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}+\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}\big)\left\|W\right\|_{L^{2}}+\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

Step 2: Estimate of
A
.
By an argument
parallel to (
9
), the fact that

H_{\kappa,1}^{*}=-H_{\kappa,1}
implies that

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}}V(H_{\kappa,1}\Lambda v)(\Lambda v)\diff\!x-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}}(\Lambda v)H_{\kappa,1}(V\Lambda v)\diff\!x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\big(\big[V,H_{\kappa,1}\big]\Lambda v\big)\Lambda v\diff\!x.

Then, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality implies that

A\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}}\gamma^{-1}\big|[V,H_{\kappa,1}](\Lambda v)\big|^{2}\diff\!x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\|\sqrt{\gamma}\Lambda v\right\|_{L^{2}}.

(25)

We now have to estimate the commutator
[V,H_{\kappa,1}]
.
Previously in §
2
, we deduced the
commutator estimate (
7
) from (
11
). This time, we will proceed directly from the definition of
H_{\kappa,1}
, without a détour by
BMO
. The main new point is that this will allow us to obtain an estimate in terms of
\left\|W\right\|_{L^{2}}
instead of
\left\|\Lambda v\right\|_{L^{2}}
. Namely, directly from the definition of
H_{\kappa,1}
, we have

\displaystyle[V,H_{\kappa,1}](\Lambda v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\pv\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{V(x)(\delta_{\alpha}\Lambda v)(x)-\delta_{\alpha}(V\Lambda v)(x)}{\tan(\alpha/2)}\chi\left(\frac{\alpha}{\kappa}\right)\diff\!\alpha

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\pv\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{(\delta_{\alpha}V)(x)(\Lambda v)(x-\alpha)}{\tan(\alpha/2)}\chi\left(\frac{\alpha}{\kappa}\right)\diff\!\alpha

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\pv\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{(\delta_{\alpha}V)(x)}{\sqrt{\gamma}(x-\alpha)}W(x-\alpha)\chi\left(\frac{\alpha}{\kappa}\right)\frac{\diff\!\alpha}{\tan(\alpha/2)}

where we replaced
\Lambda v
by
W/\sqrt{\gamma}
to obtain the last identity.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}}\gamma^{-1}\big|[V,H_{\kappa,1}](\Lambda v)\big|^{2}\diff\!x

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{S}}(\gamma(x))^{-1}\left(\int_{|\alpha|\leq 2\kappa}\left|\delta_{\alpha}V(x)\right|\gamma(x-\alpha)^{-1/2}\left|W(x-\alpha)\right|\frac{\diff\!\alpha}{|\tan(\alpha/2)|}\right)^{2}\diff\!x

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim_{c_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}(\rho^{2}(x)\left(\int_{|\alpha|\leq 2\kappa}\left|\delta_{\alpha}V(x)\right|\rho(x-\alpha)\left|W(x-\alpha)\right|\frac{\diff\!\alpha}{|\tan(\alpha/2)|}\right)^{2}\diff\!x.

Lemma 3.3
.

Introduce the notation

Q_{\alpha}(g)\mathrel{:=}|\delta_{\alpha}g|+|\delta_{\alpha}Hg|.

Then there holds

\displaystyle\left|\delta_{\alpha}V\right|\lesssim_{c_{0}}\frac{Q_{\alpha}(v)(1+Q_{\alpha}(v))^{2}}{\rho^{2}}+\varepsilon^{-3}(1+\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2}}})^{3}\left|\alpha\right|.

(26)

Proof.

One has

\displaystyle|\delta_{\alpha}V(x)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{|\delta_{\alpha}Hu(x)|}{\rho^{2}(x)}+|Hu(x-\alpha)|\frac{|\delta_{\alpha}u(x)|(|u(x)|+|u(x-\alpha)|)}{\rho^{2}(x-\alpha)\rho^{2}(x)}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{c_{0}}\frac{|\delta_{\alpha}Hu(x)|}{\rho^{2}(x)}+\frac{|\delta_{\alpha}u(x)|^{2}+|\delta_{\alpha}Hu(x)|^{2}}{\rho(x-\alpha)\rho(x)}+\frac{|\delta_{\alpha}u(x)|+|\delta_{\alpha}Hu(x)|}{\rho(x-\alpha)\rho(x)}.

Since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\rho(x-\alpha)}\lesssim\frac{1}{\rho(x)}(1+|\delta_{\alpha}u(x)|+|\delta_{\alpha}Hu(x)|),

we obtain

\left|\delta_{\alpha}V\right|\lesssim_{c_{0}}\frac{Q_{\alpha}(v)(1+Q_{\alpha}(v))^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\cdot

To get the wanted result (
26
) from this, we
replace
u
by
v+u_{0,\varepsilon}
and use the two
following elementary ingredients:

\displaystyle\frac{Q_{\alpha}(u_{0,\varepsilon})(1+Q_{\alpha}(u_{0,\varepsilon}))^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\lesssim_{c_{0}}Q_{\alpha}(u_{0,\varepsilon})(1+Q_{\alpha}(u_{0,\varepsilon}))^{2}

\displaystyle Q_{\alpha}(u_{0,\varepsilon})\leq\big(\left\|\partial_{x}u_{0,\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+\left\|\partial_{x}(Hu_{0,\varepsilon})\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\big)\left|\alpha\right|\lesssim\left\|u_{0,\varepsilon}\right\|_{H^{2}}\left|\alpha\right|\lesssim\varepsilon^{-\frac{3}{4}}\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left|\alpha\right|.

Since
\left|\alpha\right|^{3}\lesssim\left|\alpha\right|
,
this completes the proof.
∎

Set
K(\varepsilon)\mathrel{:=}\varepsilon^{-3}(1+\left\|u_{0,\varepsilon}\right\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2}}})^{3}
.
It follows from the previous lemma and the preceding inequality that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}}\gamma^{-1}\big|[V,H_{\kappa,1}](\Lambda v)\big|^{2}\diff\!x\lesssim_{c_{0}}(I)+(II),

where

\displaystyle(I)\mathrel{:=}\underset{\mathbb{S}}{\int}\rho(x)^{2}\bigg(\underset{|\alpha|\leq 2\kappa}{\int}\frac{Q_{\alpha}(v)(x)(1+Q_{\alpha}(v)(x))^{2}}{\rho(x)^{2}}\rho(x-\alpha)\left|W(x-\alpha)\right|\frac{\diff\!\alpha}{|\tan(\frac{\alpha}{2})|}\bigg)^{2}\diff\!x,

\displaystyle(II)\mathrel{:=}K(\varepsilon)^{2}\underset{\mathbb{S}}{\int}\rho(x)^{2}\bigg(\underset{|\alpha|\leq 2\kappa}{\int}\rho(x-\alpha)\left|W(x-\alpha)\right|\diff\!\alpha\bigg)^{2}\diff\!x.

Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we see that

(I)\lesssim\bigg(\iint_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}Q_{\alpha}(v)(x)^{2}(1+Q_{\alpha}(v)(x))^{4}\frac{\rho^{2}(x-\alpha)}{\rho^{2}(x)}\frac{\diff\!\alpha\diff\!x}{|\alpha|^{2}}\bigg)\left\|W\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Since

\displaystyle\frac{\rho^{2}(x-\alpha)}{\rho^{2}(x)}\lesssim 1+|Q_{\alpha}(u)(x)|^{2},

we end up with

(I)\lesssim\bigg(\iint_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}Q_{\alpha}(v)(x)^{2}(1+Q_{\alpha}(v)(x))^{4}(1+Q_{\alpha}(u)(x))^{2}\frac{\diff\!\alpha\diff\!x}{|\alpha|^{2}}\bigg)\left\|W\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}Q_{\alpha}(v)(x)^{2}(1+Q_{\alpha}(v)(x))^{4}(1+Q_{\alpha}(u)(x))^{2}\frac{\diff\!\alpha\diff\!x}{|\alpha|^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\iint_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}Q_{\alpha}(v)(x)^{2}(1+Q_{\alpha}(v)(x))^{4}\frac{\diff\!\alpha\diff\!x}{|\alpha|^{2}}

\displaystyle+\left(\iint_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}Q_{\alpha}(v)(x)^{4}(1+Q_{\alpha}(v)(x))^{8}\frac{\diff\!\alpha\diff\!x}{|\alpha|^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\iint_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}Q_{\alpha}(u)(x)^{4}\frac{\diff\!\alpha\diff\!x}{|\alpha|^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}\big(1+\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\big)^{4}\big(1+\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}})^{2},

where we used the fact that
Q_{\alpha}(f)(x)=|\delta_{\alpha}f(x)|+|\delta_{\alpha}Hf(x)|
and

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\Big(|\delta_{\alpha}f(x)|^{2\gamma}+|\delta_{\alpha}Hf(x)|^{2\gamma}\Big)\frac{\diff\!\alpha\diff\!x}{|\alpha|^{2}}\lesssim||f||_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2\gamma}

for any
\gamma\geq 1
.

This gives

(I)\lesssim\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}\big(1+\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\big)^{4}\big(1+\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}})^{2}\left\|W\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle(II)\lesssim K(\varepsilon)^{2}\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{4}\left\|W\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

Therefore, it follows from (
25
) that

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}}\gamma^{-1}\big|[V,H_{\kappa,1}](\Lambda v)\big|^{2}\diff\!x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\|W\right\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\big((I)+(II)\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\|W\right\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\big(1+\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\big)^{2}\big(1+\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}})\left\|W\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+K(\varepsilon)\kappa^{\frac{1}{4}}\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}\left\|W\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

By combining this with (
24
), we get from (
21
) that there exists a constant
C
depending only on
c_{0}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\frac{\diff}{\diff\!t}}\left\|v\right\|_{{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}}^{2}+\left\|W\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\big(1+\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\big)^{2}\big(1+\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}})\left\|W\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

(27)

\displaystyle+CK(\varepsilon)\kappa^{\frac{1}{4}}\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}\left\|W\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle+C\left(\kappa^{-1}\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}+C\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left\|W\right\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle+C\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}+\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}\Big)\left\|W\right\|_{L^{2}}.

Using the Young’s inequality,
this immediately implies that an inequality of the form

\frac{1}{2}{\frac{\diff}{\diff\!t}}\left\|v\right\|_{{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}}^{2}+\Upsilon\left\|W\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq M\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}+F,

(28)

where

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}4C^{2}\varepsilon^{-1}\Big(\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}+\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}\Big)^{2},

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}4C^{2}\varepsilon^{-1}\Big(\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}+\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}\Big)^{2},

\displaystyle\Upsilon

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}\frac{1}{4}-C\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\big(1+\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\big)^{2}\big(1+\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}})

\displaystyle\quad-CK(\varepsilon)\kappa^{\frac{1}{4}}\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-C^{2}\left(\kappa^{-1}\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}+\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)^{2}\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}.

In particular, as long as
\Upsilon\geq 0
, we have

\left\|v(t)\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}\leq e^{2Mt}\left\|v(0)\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}+\frac{e^{tM}-1}{M}F.

If one further assumes that
tM\leq 1
, it follows that

\left\|v(t)\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}\leq e^{2Mt}\left\|v(0)\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}+tF.

Introduce the parameter

\nu(\varepsilon)\mathrel{:=}2\left\|v\arrowvert_{t=0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{S})}=2\left\|u_{0}-e^{\varepsilon\partial_{x}^{2}}u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{S})}.

Then choose
\varepsilon
small enough, so that

C\nu(\varepsilon)\big(1+\nu(\varepsilon)\big)^{2}\big(1+2\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}})\leq\frac{1}{16}.

We
then fix
\kappa
small enough to that

CK(\varepsilon)\kappa^{\frac{1}{4}}(2\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}})^{2}\leq\frac{1}{16},

where recall that
K(\varepsilon)\mathrel{:=}\varepsilon^{-3}(1+\left\|u_{0,\varepsilon}\right\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2}}})^{3}
.

We then deduce the wanted uniform estimate by an elementary continuation argument.
∎

3.3
Compactness

Previously, we have proved
a priori
estimates for the spatial derivatives. In this paragraph, we collect results from which we will derive estimates for the time derivative as well as for the nonlinearity. These estimates are used to pass to the limit in
the equation.

Recall the notations introduced in the previous section, as well as the estimates proved there. Fix
c_{0}>0
. Given
\delta\in(0,1]
and an initial data
u_{0}\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{S})
satisfying
u_{0}\geq c_{0}
, we have seen that there exists a (global in time) solution
u_{\delta}
to the Cauchy problem:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}u_{\delta}+\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{u_{\delta}\Lambda u_{\delta}-(Hu_{\delta})\partial_{x}u_{\delta}}{\delta+u_{\delta}^{2}+(Hu_{\delta})^{2}}-\delta\partial_{x}^{2}u_{\delta}=0,\\
&u_{\delta}\arrowvert_{t=0}=e^{\delta\partial_{x}^{2}}u_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

(29)

Moreover, we have proved that one can fix
\varepsilon
small enough
such that one can write
u_{\delta}
under the form

u_{\delta}(t,x)=(e^{(\varepsilon+\delta)\partial_{x}^{2}}u_{0})(x)+v_{\delta}(x),

and there exist
T>0
and
M>0
depending on
u_{0}
such that, for all
\delta\in(0,1]
,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\|v_{\delta}(t)\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{u_{\delta}\left|\Lambda v_{\delta}\right|^{2}}{\delta+u_{\delta}^{2}+(Hu_{\delta})^{2}}\diff\!x\diff\!t+\delta\int_{0}^{T}\left\|v_{\delta}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{3}{2}}}^{2}\diff\!t\leq M.

(30)

Now, to pass to the limit in the equation (
29
), we need to extract some uniform estimates for the time derivative. Since
\partial_{t}u_{\delta}=\partial_{t}v_{\delta}
, it is sufficient to estimate the latter quantity.
It is given by

\partial_{t}v_{\delta}=-V_{\delta}\partial_{x}v_{\delta}-\gamma_{\delta}\Lambda v_{\delta}+\delta\partial_{x}^{2}v_{\delta}+R_{\delta},

(31)

where

\displaystyle\gamma_{\delta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{u_{\delta}}{\delta+u_{\delta}^{2}+(Hu_{\delta})^{2}}\quad,\quad V_{\delta}=-\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{Hu_{\delta}}{\delta+u_{\delta}^{2}+(Hu_{\delta})^{2}},

\displaystyle R_{\delta}

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{\delta}(\Lambda e^{(\varepsilon+\delta)\partial_{x}^{2}}u_{0})-V_{\delta}(\partial_{x}e^{(\varepsilon+\delta)\partial_{x}^{2}}u_{0})+\delta\partial_{x}^{2}e^{(\varepsilon+\delta)\partial_{x}^{2}}u_{0}.

As already seen in (
22
), we have

\gamma_{\delta}\lesssim_{c_{0}}1
and
\left|V_{\delta}\right|\lesssim_{c_{0}}1
.
By combining this with the fact that

e^{\varepsilon\partial_{x}^{2}}
is a smoothing operator,
we immediately see that

\left\|R_{\delta}\right\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T];L^{2})}\lesssim_{c_{0},\varepsilon}\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

Here the implicit constant depends on
\varepsilon
, but this is harmless since
\varepsilon
is fixed now. On the other, directly from (
30
), we get that

\left\|\gamma_{\delta}\Lambda v_{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}([0,T];L^{2})}\lesssim_{c_{0}}\left\|\sqrt{\gamma_{\delta}}\Lambda v_{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}([0,T];L^{2})}\lesssim_{c_{0}}M,

and

\delta\left\|\partial_{x}^{2}v_{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}([0,T];H^{-\frac{1}{2}})}\leq\sqrt{\delta}\left\|\partial_{x}^{2}v_{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}([0,T];H^{-\frac{1}{2}})}\leq M.

It remains only to estimate the contribution of
V_{\delta}\partial_{x}v_{\delta}
.
For this, we begin by proving that
(v_{\delta})_{\delta\in(0,1]}
is bounded in
L^{p}([0,T];\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{S}))
for any
1\leq p<2
. Indeed, we can write

\displaystyle\left\|v_{\delta}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{u_{\delta}^{2}+(Hu_{\delta})^{2}}{u_{\delta}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{u_{\delta}\left|\Lambda v_{\delta}\right|^{2}}{u_{\delta}^{2}+(Hu_{\delta})^{2}}\diff\!x

\displaystyle\lesssim_{c_{0}}\left\|(u_{\delta},Hu_{\delta})\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{u_{\delta}\left|\Lambda v\right|^{2}}{u_{\delta}^{2}+(Hu_{\delta})^{2}}\diff\!x

\displaystyle\lesssim_{c_{0}}\Big(\left\|(v_{\delta},Hv_{\delta})\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}\Big)\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{u_{\delta}\left|\Lambda v\right|^{2}}{u_{\delta}^{2}+(Hu_{\delta})^{2}}\diff\!x

\displaystyle\lesssim_{c_{0}}\Big(\left\|v_{\delta}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}\log\big(2+\left\|v_{\delta}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}\big)+\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}\Big)\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{u_{\delta}\left|\Lambda v_{\delta}\right|^{2}}{u_{\delta}^{2}+(Hu_{\delta})^{2}}\diff\!x,

to conclude that

\displaystyle\frac{\left\|v_{\delta}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}}{\log(2+\left\|v_{\delta}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{1}})}\lesssim_{c_{0},\varepsilon,\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2}}}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{u_{\delta}|\Lambda v_{\delta}|^{2}}{u_{\delta}^{2}+(Hu_{\delta})^{2}}\diff\!x.

Remembering that
\left|V_{\delta}\right|\lesssim_{c_{0}}1
, it immediately follows that, for any
p\in[1,2)
,

\left\|V_{\delta}\partial_{x}v_{\delta}\right\|_{L^{p}([0,T];L^{2})}\lesssim M.

Now, by combining all the previous estimates, we see that

(v_{\delta})_{\delta\in(0,1]}
is bounded in the space

X_{p}=\left\{u\in C^{0}([0,T];H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{S})\cap L^{p}([0,T];H^{1}(\mathbb{S}))\,;\,\partial_{t}u\in L^{p}([0,T];H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{S}))\right\}.

Since
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{S})
(resp.
H^{1}(\mathbb{S})
)
is compactly embedded into
H^{s}(\mathbb{S})
(resp.
H^{\frac{1}{2}+s}(\mathbb{S})

for any
s<1/2
,
By the classical Aubin-Lions lemma, this in turn implies that one extract a sequence
(u_{\delta_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
which converges strongly in

C^{0}([0,T];H^{s}(\mathbb{S})\cap L^{p}([0,T];H^{\frac{1}{2}+s}(\mathbb{S})).

Then it is elementary to pass to the limit in the equation.

4
Smoothing effect

The goal of this section is to prove the second statement in Theorem
1.1
which asserts that the solution are smooth.
By classical methods for parabolic equations (see
[
39
, Chapter 15]
),
it is easy to prove that solutions which are smooth enough (say with initial data in
H^{2}(\mathbb{S})
) are
C^{\infty}
for positive time. So it is sufficient to prove that the solution are at least
H^{2}
for positive times. This is the purpose of the following

Proposition 4.1
.

The solution
u
constructs in the previous section is such that, for any
\xi>4
, there exists a constant
C=C(\xi)
such that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}t^{\xi}\left\|u(t)\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{5}{2}}}^{2}<+\infty.

(32)

Proof.

Since
u
was constructed as the limit of smooth solutions
(see §
3.1
and §
3.3
), we will
prove only
a priori
estimates.

As in the previous part, we work with the function

v=u-e^{\varepsilon\partial_{x}^{2}}u_{0},

with
\varepsilon
small enough.
Recall from (
19
) that
v
solves

\partial_{t}v+V\partial_{x}v+\gamma\Lambda v=R_{\varepsilon}(u,u_{0})

(33)

where

R_{\varepsilon}(u,u_{0})=-\gamma\Lambda u_{0,\varepsilon}-V\partial_{x}u_{0,\varepsilon}.

We will estimate the
\dot{H}^{2}
-norm of
v
.
For this introduce

\tilde{v}=\partial_{x}^{2}v
, solution to

\partial_{t}\tilde{v}+\gamma\Lambda\tilde{v}=V\big(H_{\kappa,1}\Lambda\tilde{v}\big)+R_{0}+R_{1}

(34)

where

\displaystyle R_{0}=\partial_{x}^{2}\left(V\left(H_{\kappa,2}\Lambda v\right)\right)+\partial_{x}^{2}R_{\varepsilon}(u,u_{0})

\displaystyle R_{1}=-(\partial_{x}^{2}\gamma)(\Lambda v)-2(\partial_{x}\gamma)(\Lambda\partial_{x}v)+(\partial_{x}^{2}V)(H_{\kappa,1}\Lambda v)+2(\partial_{x}(VH_{\kappa,1}\Lambda\partial_{x}v)).

Now, we multiply (
34
) by
\Lambda\tilde{v}

and then integrate in
x
over
\mathbb{S}
, to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\diff}{\diff\!t}\left\|\tilde{v}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{S}}\gamma\big|\Lambda\tilde{v}\big|^{2}\diff\!x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}}[V,H_{\kappa,1}](\Lambda\tilde{v})\Lambda\tilde{v}\diff\!x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\mathbb{S}}R_{1}\Lambda\tilde{v}\diff\!x+\int_{\mathbb{S}}R_{0}\Lambda\tilde{v}\diff\!x.

Set

\tilde{W}\mathrel{:=}\sqrt{\gamma}\Lambda\tilde{v}.

By using arguments parallel to those used in the first step of the proof of Lemma
3.2
, one finds that

\displaystyle\left|\int[V,H_{\kappa,1}](\Lambda\tilde{v})\Lambda\tilde{v}\ dx\right|\lesssim_{c_{0}}\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}}(1+\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}})^{3}||\tilde{w}||_{L^{2}}^{2}+C(\varepsilon)\kappa^{\frac{1}{4}}(\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}}+1)^{2}||\tilde{w}||_{L^{2}}^{2}

and,

\displaystyle|\int R_{0}\Lambda\tilde{v}\ dx|\lesssim_{c_{0}}C(\varepsilon,\kappa)||\tilde{w}||_{L^{2}}^{\frac{9}{5}}+C(\varepsilon,\kappa).

Since

\displaystyle|\partial_{x}\gamma|+|\partial_{x}V|\lesssim\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\left(|\partial_{x}u|+|\Lambda u|\right),

\displaystyle|\partial_{x}^{2}\gamma|+|\partial_{x}^{2}V|\lesssim\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\left(|\partial_{x}^{2}u|+|H\partial_{x}^{2}u|\right)+\frac{1}{V^{3/2}}\left(|\partial_{x}u|^{2}+|\Lambda u|^{2}\right),

then one finds

\displaystyle|R_{1}|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{c_{0}}\left(\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\left(|\partial_{x}^{2}u|+|H\partial_{x}^{2}u|\right)+\frac{1}{\rho^{3}}\left(|\partial_{x}u|^{2}+|\Lambda u|^{2}\right)\right)\left(\left|\Lambda v\right|+|H_{\kappa,1}\Lambda v|\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\left(|\partial_{x}u|+|\Lambda u|\right)\left(|\Lambda\partial_{x}v|+|H_{\kappa,1}\Lambda\partial_{x}v|\right).

We obtain,

\displaystyle|R_{1}|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{c_{0}}\left(\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\left(|\partial_{x}^{2}v|+|H\partial_{x}^{2}v|\right)+\frac{1}{\rho^{3}}\left(|\partial_{x}v|^{2}+\left|\Lambda v\right|^{2}\right)\right)\left(\left|\Lambda v\right|+|H_{\kappa,1}\Lambda v|\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\left(|\partial_{x}v|+\left|\Lambda v\right|\right)\left(|\Lambda\partial_{x}v|+|H_{\kappa,1}\Lambda\partial_{x}v|\right)+C(\varepsilon)\left(\left|\Lambda v\right|+|H_{\kappa,1}\Lambda v|+|\Lambda\partial_{x}v|+|H_{\kappa,1}\Lambda\partial_{x}v|\right)

Thus,

\displaystyle\left|\int R_{1}\Lambda\tilde{v}\ dx\right|\lesssim_{c_{0}}\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}}(1+\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}})^{3}||\tilde{w}||_{L^{2}}^{2}+C(\varepsilon)\kappa^{\frac{1}{4}}(\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}}+1)^{4}||\tilde{w}||_{L^{2}}^{2}+C(\kappa,\varepsilon)

Therefore, we find

\displaystyle\partial_{t}||\tilde{v}||_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}+\int|\tilde{w}|^{2}\ dx

\displaystyle\lesssim_{c_{0}}\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}}(1+\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}})^{3}||\tilde{w}||_{L^{2}}^{2}+C(\varepsilon)\kappa^{\frac{1}{4}}(\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}}+1)^{4}||\tilde{w}||_{L^{2}}^{2}+C(\kappa,\varepsilon)

Choosing
\varepsilon
and then
\kappa
small enough, we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}||\tilde{v}||_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{S}}|\tilde{w}|^{2}\ dx

\displaystyle\lesssim C(c_{0},\kappa,\varepsilon),

for any
t\in(0,T)
. So, integrating in time
\tau\in(s,t)
we have obtained that,

\displaystyle||v(t)||_{\dot{H}^{\frac{5}{2}}}^{2}+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{u|\Lambda^{3}v|^{2}}{u^{2}+(Hu)^{2}}\ dx\ d\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim C(c_{0},\kappa,\varepsilon)+||v(s)||_{\dot{H}^{\frac{5}{2}}}^{2}.

Then, in order to measure the decay rate in time, we multiply the last inequality by
s^{\xi-1}
and then integrate in
s\in[0,T]
, one finds

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}s^{\xi-1}||v(t)||_{\dot{H}^{\frac{5}{2}}}^{2}\ ds+\int_{0}^{T}s^{\xi-1}\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{u|\Lambda^{3}v|^{2}}{u^{2}+(Hu)^{2}}\ dx\ d\tau\ ds

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{T}s^{\xi-1}C(c_{0},\kappa,\varepsilon)+s^{\xi-1}||v(s)||_{\dot{H}^{\frac{5}{2}}}^{2}\ ds.

Then,

\displaystyle\sup_{0<t<T}t^{\xi}||v(t)||_{\dot{H}^{\frac{5}{2}}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}s^{\xi}\int\frac{u|\Lambda^{3}v|^{2}}{u^{2}+(Hu)^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim T^{\xi}C(c_{0},\kappa,\varepsilon)+\int_{0}^{t}s^{\xi-1}||v(s)||_{\dot{H}^{\frac{5}{2}}}^{2}\ ds.

Since

\displaystyle\left\|v\right\|_{H^{3}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{c_{0}}\left\|(u,Hu)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\int\frac{u|\Lambda^{3}v|^{2}}{u^{2}+(Hu)^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{c_{0}}(\left\|(v,Hv)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\varepsilon^{-1/2}\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{H^{1/2}}^{2})\int\frac{u|\Lambda^{3}v|^{2}}{u^{2}+(Hu)^{2}}\ dx

\displaystyle\lesssim_{c_{0}}(\left\|v\right\|_{H^{1/2}}^{2}\log(2+\left\|v\right\|_{H^{3}})+\varepsilon^{-1/2}\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{H^{1/2}}^{2})\int\frac{u|\Lambda^{3}v|^{2}}{u^{2}+(Hu)^{2}}\ dx.

Then, using the inequality

\displaystyle\frac{\left\|v\right\|_{H^{3}}^{2}}{\log(2+\left\|v\right\|_{H^{3}})}\lesssim_{c_{0},\varepsilon,\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{H^{1/2}}}\int\frac{u|\Lambda^{3}v|^{2}}{u^{2}+(Hu)^{2}},

we find that,

\displaystyle\sup_{0<t<T}t^{\xi}||v(t)||_{\dot{H}^{\frac{5}{2}}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}s^{\xi}\frac{\left\|v\right\|_{H^{3}}^{2}}{\log(2+\left\|v\right\|_{H^{3}})}

\displaystyle\lesssim t^{\xi}C(c_{0},\kappa,\varepsilon)+\int_{0}^{t}s^{\xi-1}||v(s)||_{\dot{H}^{\frac{5}{2}}}^{2}.

Then, we use the fact that some
\delta>0
small enough,
a^{2-\delta}\lesssim\frac{a^{2}}{log(2+a)},

we find

\displaystyle\sup_{0<t<T}t^{\xi}||v(t)||_{\dot{H}^{\frac{5}{2}}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}s^{\xi}\|u(s)\|^{2-\delta}_{\dot{H}^{3}}

\displaystyle\lesssim T^{\xi+1}+\int_{0}^{T}s^{\xi-1}\|u(s)\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{5/2}}\ ds.

(35)

Let
\epsilon>0
and
\delta_{0}>0
, using the following interpolation inequality

\displaystyle s^{\xi-1}\|u(s)\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{5/2}}\leq\epsilon s^{\xi-1+\delta_{0}}\|u(s)\|^{8/5}_{\dot{H}^{3}}\ \epsilon^{-1}s^{-\delta_{0}}\|u(s)\|^{2/5}_{\dot{H}^{1/2}},

together with Young’s inequality (with the conjugate exponents
p=\frac{10-5\delta}{8}
and
q=\frac{10-5\delta}{2-5\delta}
) we find

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}s^{\xi-1}\|u(s)\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{5/2}}\ ds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon^{p}}{p}\int_{0}^{T}s^{p\xi-p+p\delta_{0}}\|u(s)\|^{2-\delta}_{\dot{H}^{3}}\ ds

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{q\epsilon^{q}}\sup_{s\in(0,T)}\|u(s)\|^{2q/5}_{\dot{H}^{1/2}}\int_{0}^{T}s^{-q\delta_{0}}\ ds.

Therefore, inequality (
35
) becomes

\displaystyle\sup_{0<t<T}t^{\xi}||v(t)||_{\dot{H}^{\frac{5}{2}}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}s^{\xi}\|u(s)\|^{2-\delta}_{\dot{H}^{3}}\ ds

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle T^{\xi+1}+\frac{\epsilon^{p}}{p}\int_{0}^{T}s^{p\xi-p+p\delta_{0}}\|u(s)\|^{2-\delta}_{\dot{H}^{3}}\ ds

\displaystyle\ +\ \frac{1}{q\epsilon^{q}}\sup_{s\in(0,T)}\|u(s)\|^{2q/5}_{\dot{H}^{1/2}}\int_{0}^{T}s^{-q\delta_{0}}\ ds.

(36)

Choosing
m,\delta_{0}
such that
q\delta_{0}<1
(so that the last integral in the right hand side is finite) and, for a scaling purpose, we also need that

\displaystyle\xi=p\xi-p+p\delta_{0}.

So,

\xi=q-\delta_{0}q>q-1.

Then, for any
\xi>4
(note that
q>4
for any
\delta>0
) and any
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small, we may absorb the
\dot{H}^{3}
in the left hand side of inequality (
4
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{0<t<T}t^{\xi}||v(t)||_{\dot{H}^{\frac{5}{2}}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}s^{\xi}\|u(s)\|^{2-\delta}_{\dot{H}^{3}}ds\lesssim 1.

In particular, for any
\xi>4

\displaystyle\sup_{0<t<T}t^{\xi}||v(t)||_{\dot{H}^{\frac{5}{2}}}^{2}\leq C.

∎

5
Uniqueness

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.3
. For any data

u_{0}\in H^{1/2+a}(\mathbb{S})
we know from Theorem
1.1
that
u
in
C^{0}([0,+\infty);\dot{H}^{\frac{1+a}{2}}(\mathbb{S}))\cap L^{2}((0,+\infty);\dot{H}^{1+\frac{a}{2}}(\mathbb{S}))
and satisfy the following condition

\displaystyle\sup_{t>0}t^{s+\varepsilon_{0}}||u(t)||_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1+a}{2}+s}}<\infty

for any
s>0.

We will follow an idea in
[
13
]
.
Let
u_{1},u_{2}
be two solutions of (
3
) with same initial data. Assume that
u_{1},u_{2}\geq c_{0}>0
. Set
\overline{u}=u_{1}-u_{2}
. Let
x_{t},x^{t}
satisfy
\overline{u}(x_{t},t)=\displaystyle\sup_{x}\overline{u}(x,t)
and
-\overline{u}(x^{t},t)=\displaystyle\sup_{x}(-\overline{u})(x,t)
. By evaluating the evolution equation at
x=x_{t}
, one finds

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\overline{u}(x_{t},t))+\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{u_{1}}{u_{1}^{2}+(Hu_{1})^{2}}\ \Lambda\overline{u}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{u_{1}}{u_{1}^{2}+(Hu_{1})^{2}}-\frac{u_{2}}{u_{2}^{2}+(Hu_{2})^{2}}\right)\Lambda u_{2}+\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{Hu_{1}}{u_{1}^{2}+(Hu_{1})^{2}}-\frac{Hu_{2}}{u_{2}^{2}+(Hu_{2})^{2}}\right)\partial_{x}u_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{c_{0}}\left(||\overline{u}||_{L^{\infty}_{x}}+|H\overline{u}(x_{t},t)|\right)||(\Lambda u_{2},\partial_{x}u_{2})||_{L^{\infty}_{x}}.

We need to control
|H\overline{u}(x_{t},t)|
by
\Lambda\overline{u}(x_{t},t)
and
||\overline{u}||_{L^{\infty}_{x}}
. For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
, since
\overline{u}(x_{t},t)\geq 0
, we may write

\displaystyle|H\overline{u}(x_{t},t)|\lesssim|\log(\varepsilon)|||\overline{u}||_{L^{\infty}_{x}}+\int_{|\alpha|\leq\varepsilon}\delta_{\alpha}\overline{u}(x_{t},t)\frac{d\alpha}{|\alpha|}\lesssim|\log(\varepsilon)|||\overline{u}||_{L^{\infty}_{x}}+\varepsilon|\Lambda\overline{u}(x_{t},t)|

Thus, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\overline{u}(x_{t},t))+\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{u_{1}}{u_{1}^{2}+(Hu_{1})^{2}}\ \Lambda\overline{u}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{c_{0}}|\log(\varepsilon)|||(\Lambda u_{2},\Lambda u_{2})||_{L^{\infty}_{x}}||\overline{u}||_{L^{\infty}_{x}}+\varepsilon||(\Lambda u_{2},\partial_{x}u_{2})||_{L^{\infty}_{x}}\Lambda\overline{u}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{c_{0}}|\log(\varepsilon)|||(\Lambda u_{2},\Lambda u_{2})||_{L^{\infty}_{x}}||\overline{u}||_{L^{\infty}_{x}}+\varepsilon(1+||u_{1}||_{L^{\infty}\dot{H}^{\frac{1+a}{2}}})||(\Lambda u_{2},\partial_{x}u_{2})||_{L^{\infty}_{x}}\frac{u_{1}}{u_{1}^{2}+(Hu_{1})^{2}}\ \Lambda\overline{u}.

Letting
\varepsilon>0

\varepsilon(1+||u_{1}||_{L^{\infty}\dot{H}^{\frac{1+a}{2}}})||(\Lambda u_{2},\partial_{x}u_{2})||_{L^{\infty}_{x}}\ll 1,

thus one gets,

\partial_{t}(\overline{u}(x_{t},t))\lesssim_{c_{0}}\log\left((2+||u_{1}||_{\dot{L}^{\infty}H^{\frac{1+a}{2}}})(1+||(\Lambda u_{2},\partial_{x}u_{2})||_{L^{\infty}_{x}})\right)||(\Lambda u_{2},\partial_{x}u_{2})||_{L^{\infty}_{x}}||\overline{u}||_{L^{\infty}_{x}}.

Using similar arguments, it is easy to get that

\partial_{t}(\overline{u}(x^{t},t))\lesssim_{c_{0}}\log\left((2+||u_{1}||_{\dot{L}^{\infty}H^{\frac{1+a}{2}}})(1+||(\Lambda u_{2},\partial_{x}u_{2})||_{L^{\infty}_{x}})\right)||(\Lambda u_{2},\partial_{x}u_{2})||_{L^{\infty}_{x}}||\overline{u}||_{L^{\infty}}.

Thus, we obtain,

\displaystyle||\overline{u}(t)||_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim||\overline{u}(0)||_{L^{\infty}}+\int_{0}^{t}\omega(\tau)||\overline{u}(\tau)||_{L^{\infty}}d\tau,

where,

\displaystyle\omega(t)=\log\left((2+||u_{1}||_{\dot{L}^{\infty}H^{\frac{1+a}{2}}})(1+||(\Lambda u_{2},\partial_{x}u_{2})(t)||_{L^{\infty}_{x}})\right)||(\Lambda u_{2},\partial_{x}u_{2})(t)||_{L^{\infty}_{x}}

Note that, for any
a_{0}\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\omega(\tau)d\tau\lesssim_{a_{0}}(2+||u_{1}||_{\dot{L}^{\infty}H^{\frac{1+a}{2}}})\left[1+\sup_{\tau\in(0,t)}\left(\tau^{1-a_{0}}||(\Lambda u_{2},\partial_{x}u_{2})(\tau)||_{L^{\infty}_{x}}\right)\right]^{2}.

By Sobolev’s inequality for any
a_{0}\in(0,a/10)

\displaystyle\sup_{\tau\in(0,t)}\left(\tau^{a_{0}}||(\Lambda u_{2},\partial_{x}u_{2})(\tau)||_{L^{\infty}_{x}}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim t||u_{1}||_{\dot{L}^{\infty}H^{\frac{1}{2}}}+\sup_{\tau\in(0,t)}\left(\tau^{1-a_{0}}||u_{2}(\tau)||_{H^{\frac{3}{2}+\frac{a}{4}}}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim C_{t}<\infty.

Therefore, by using Gronwall’s inequality, one obtains

\displaystyle||\overline{u}(t)||_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim||\overline{u}(0)||_{L^{\infty}}\exp(C(c_{0})\int_{0}^{t}\omega(\tau)d\tau)\lesssim C_{t}||\overline{u}(0)||_{L^{\infty}}.

Hence, we proved the wellposedness of (
3
) in
H^{\frac{1}{2}+a}
initial data for any
a>0
.
