The background method: Theory and computations
Giovanni Fantuzzi, Ali Arslan, Andrew Wynn

Abstract
The background method is a widely used technique to bound mean properties of turbulent flows rigorously. This work reviews recent advances in the theoretical formulation and numerical implementation of the method. First, we describe how the background method can be formulated systematically within a broader “auxiliary function” framework for bounding mean quantities, and explain how symmetries of the flow and constraints such as maximum principles can be exploited. All ideas are presented in a general setting and are illustrated on Rayleigh–Bénard convection between stress-free isothermal plates. Second, we review a semidefinite programming approach and a timestepping approach to optimizing bounds computationally, revealing that they are related to each other through convex duality and low-rank matrix factorization. Open questions and promising directions for further numerical analysis of the background method are also outlined.

1  Introduction

Making quantitative predictions for key properties of incompressible turbulent flows, such as the mean energy dissipation in a pipe or the average amount of heat transported by natural convection, is a fundamental problem in fluid mechanics. A particular challenge is to determine the functional dependence of these mean quantities on relevant nondimensional parameters, such as the Reynolds number, directly from the equations governing the flow and without introducing physically reasonable, but unproven, assumptions.

One way to study mean quantities rigorously, pioneered by Malkus, Howard and Busse
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
, is to bound their values using variational techniques. The idea is simple: rather than optimizing the mean quantity of interest over solutions to the flow’s governing equations, one optimizes it over a larger set of incompressible flow fields that satisfy only integral constraints in the form of energy and flux balances, which are weaker but more tractable. The latter optimum is often still hard to evaluate, but conservative one-sided estimates (lower bounds for minima and upper bounds for maxima) can be obtained with the background method of Doering&Constantin
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
.

To illustrate the method, suppose we seek bounds on the mean energy dissipation for the flow of an incompressible fluid in a domain
\Omega

\mathbf{u}

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}=\Rey^{-1}\Delta\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}-\nabla p+\mathbf{f},

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0,

where

\overline{\Phi}=\overline{\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}\right|^{2}{\rm d}\mathbf{x}},

where we use overbars to indicate averaging over infinite time.
The background method begins by considering the energy

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\overline{\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{U}\right|^{2}{\rm d}\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=\overline{\int_{\Omega}\left(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{U}\right)\cdot\left(\Rey^{-1}\Delta\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}-\nabla p+\mathbf{f}\right){\rm d}\mathbf{x}}

(1.3)

\displaystyle=\overline{\int_{\Omega}\left(-\Rey^{-1}\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}\right|^{2}+\Rey^{-1}\nabla\mathbf{U}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{U}\cdot\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{f}\cdot\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{f}\cdot\mathbf{U}\right){\rm d}\mathbf{x}}.

\int_{\Omega}\left((1-\tfrac{\lambda}{\Rey})\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}\right|^{2}+\tfrac{\lambda}{\Rey}\nabla\mathbf{U}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}-\lambda\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{U}\cdot\mathbf{u}+\lambda\mathbf{f}\cdot\mathbf{u}-\lambda\mathbf{f}\cdot\mathbf{U}\right)\leq\gamma

for some constant

and all incompressible vector fields
\mathbf{u}
satisfying the no-slip boundary conditions. Then, the inequality holds also for all possible solutions of the Navier–Stokes equations (
1.1
a,b) and identity

This work reviews recent advances in the theoretical formulation and computational implementation of the background method. The latter are of particular interest because, even though no algorithm can produce rigorous bounds for all possible values of a flow’s governing parameters, numerical approximations of the best bound available to the background method can reveal whether suboptimal bounds proven analytically exhibit optimal parameter dependence. If they do not, moreover, computations can guide improved analysis. This was recently demonstrated for Bénard–Marangoni convection at infinite Prandtl number
[
42
,
41
]
and for internally heated convection
[
51
,
53
]
.

On the theoretical side, we show how to formulate the background method systematically within a more general framework for bounding infinite-time averages, which is based on auxiliary functions
[
74
,
75
,
76
]
and is known to be sharp for well-posed ordinary and partial differential equations
[
77
,
78
]
. This interpretation of the background method, discussed in
[
74
,
79
,
30
,
76
,
51
,
52
]
and already used in the example given above, will be described in a general setting in
section

3
. Its main advantages compared to the original formulation of the method by Doering & Constantin
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
are that (i) it allows for the consideration of generalized perturbation energies and generalized background fields, (ii) it can be used easily to bound mean quantities not equivalent to the dissipation rate, and (iii) it always reduces the search for a bound to a
convex
variational principle. Indeed, observe that inequality
eq.

1.5
is jointly convex in the balance parameter
\lambda
, the scaled background field
\lambda\mathbf{U}
, and the bound
\gamma
. We also explain how this convex variational principle can be simplified using symmetries and, sometimes, improved by incorporating constraints such as maximum principles.

On the computational side (
section

4
), we first review two recent approaches to optimizing background fields—one based on semidefinite programming

,

,

,
36
]
and one based on timestepping

,

) for their generality, robustness, and simplicity.
We then reveal a previously unrecognised connection between these two approaches, which opens new avenues for the numerical analysis of the background method and may enable the efficient optimization of bounds for complex flows.

We consider an incompressible fluid in a domain

\overline{\Phi(\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{u}(t))}:=\limsup_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\Phi(\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{u}(t))\,{\rm d}t.

\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{w}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{w}\,{\rm d}\mathbf{x}=0
,
\int\nabla\vartheta\cdot\mathbf{w}\vartheta\,{\rm d}\mathbf{x}=0
, and
\int_{\Omega}\nabla w_{3}\cdot\mathbf{w}\vartheta\,{\rm d}\mathbf{x}=-\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{w}\cdot\nabla(\vartheta\mathbf{e}_{3})\cdot\mathbf{w}\,{\rm d}\mathbf{x}
. Thus, for any
\Lambda
in the space
\mathbb{M}
specified above and any
(\mathbf{w},\vartheta)\in\mathbb{U}
, the quantity

(2.12)

b(\Lambda\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})=\int_{\Omega}\left(\mathbf{w}\cdot\nabla\left(\tfrac{\lambda_{1}\mathbf{w}}{\Pran\Ra}+\lambda_{3}\vartheta\mathbf{e}_{3}\right)\cdot\mathbf{w}+\nabla(\lambda_{3}w_{3}+\lambda_{2}\vartheta)\cdot\mathbf{w}\vartheta\right){\rm d}\mathbf{x}

vanishes identically as required. Similarly, since
\mathbf{w}
is divergence-free, the quantity

(2.13)

c(\Lambda\mathbf{u},p)=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\left(\tfrac{\lambda_{1}\mathbf{w}}{\Pran\Ra}+\lambda_{3}\vartheta\mathbf{e}_{3}\right)\,p\,{\rm d}\mathbf{x}=\lambda_{3}\int_{\Omega}p\,\partial_{z}\vartheta\,{\rm d}\mathbf{x}

vanishes identically for all
(\mathbf{w},\vartheta)\in\mathbb{U}
and all
p\in\mathbb{P}
only if
\lambda_{3}=0
, so
\Lambda\in\mathbb{M}_{0}
.

3  The background method via auxiliary functions

Upper bounds on the maximal infinite-time average
\overline{\Phi}^{*}
can be derived using a general strategy based on the following simple observation: the time derivative of any bounded and differentiable function
V:\mathbb{U}\to\mathbb{R}
, which we refer to as an
auxiliary function
, has zero infinite-time average.
Specifically, let
\mathcal{L}V:\mathbb{U}\times\mathbb{P}\to\mathbb{R}
be the Lie derivative of
V
along solutions of
eq.

2.1
, meaning that
\mathcal{L}V(\mathbf{u}(t),p(t))=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}V(\mathbf{u}(t))
. By the fundamental theorem of calculus, the infinite-time average of
\mathcal{L}V(\mathbf{u}(t),p(t))
vanishes if
V(\mathbf{u}(t))
is bounded uniformly in time, so

(3.1)

\overline{\Phi(\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{u}(t))}=\overline{\Phi(\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{u}(t))+\mathcal{L}V(\mathbf{u}(t),p(t))}

irrespective of the initial state
\mathbf{u}(0)
. If
V
can be chosen such that

(3.2)

\Phi(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})+\mathcal{L}V(\mathbf{u},p)\leq\gamma\qquad\forall(\mathbf{u},p)\in\mathbb{U}\times\mathbb{P}

for some constant
\gamma
, then the right-hand side of
eq.

3.1
is also bounded above by
\gamma
irrespective of the initial state, and we conclude that
\overline{\Phi}^{*}\leq\gamma
.

The background method amounts to searching for the smallest possible bound
\gamma
over
quadratic
auxiliary functions
V
constructed to ensure that inequality
eq.

3.2
is quadratic in
\mathbf{u}
and
p

[
74
,
79
]
. The body-forced flow example given in the Introduction, for instance, uses the auxiliary function
V(\mathbf{u})=\frac{\lambda}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{U}\right|{\rm d}\mathbf{x}
, which represents the energy of perturbations from an incompressible background field
\mathbf{U}
, scaled by the balance parameter
\lambda
. In this case, the general inequality
eq.

3.2
reduces exactly to
eq.

1.5
and the pressure drops out by virtue of the incompressibility of
\mathbf{U}
.
Section

3.1
extends this example, showing how generalized background fields and generalized perturbation energies can be used to derive bounds for any flow whose governing equations fit within the abstract setting introduced in
section

2
.
Section

3.2
, instead, discusses how symmetries in the governing equations and in
\Phi
can be exploited when optimizing
\gamma
. Finally,
section

3.3
outlines how additional constraints on the asymptotic behaviour of the flow can be taken into account to try and improve the optimal bound.

3.1  General formulation

The background method considers quadratic auxiliary functions of the form

(3.3)

V(\mathbf{u})=\tfrac{1}{2}\langle\Lambda\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}\rangle-\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}\rangle,

where
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{V}
is a time-independent vector field and
\Lambda\in\mathbb{M}
is a symmetric matrix satisfying assumption
item

3
.
Traditional formulations of the method
[
8
,
81
,
10
]
, including that given in the Introduction for body-forced flows, additionally take
\mathbf{v}=\Lambda\mathbf{U}
for some
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{U}
. In this case, one may rewrite the

in
eq.

3.3
as

(3.4)

V(\mathbf{u})=\tfrac{1}{2}\langle\Lambda(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{U}),\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{U}\rangle+\tfrac{1}{2}\langle\Lambda\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U}\rangle

and interpret this as the “energy” of perturbations from the
background field

\mathbf{U}
, weighted by the
balance parameter

\Lambda
, plus a constant term that could be dropped if desired because it gives no contribution to the Lie derivative
\mathcal{L}V
appearing in
eq.

3.2
.
Here, however, we remove the restriction that
\mathbf{v}=\Lambda\mathbf{U}
and consider arbitrary
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{V}
, which will be called the
generalized background field
. This generalization is convenient for two reasons. First, it may improve the best upper bound on
\overline{\Phi}^{*}
that can be proven, because the vectors
\mathbf{v}=\Lambda\mathbf{U}
generated by classical background fields usually span only a strict subspace of
\mathbb{V}
. Second, as shown below, minimizing the upper bound
\gamma
on
\overline{\Phi}^{*}
over
\Lambda\in\mathbb{M}
and
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{V}
is a convex problem, while doing so over
\Lambda\in\mathbb{M}
and
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{U}
is not. This convexity allows for the efficient computational optimization of
\gamma
using the approaches described in
section

4
.

Differentiating
eq.

3.3
along solutions of
eq.

2.1
, using the identities (
2.2
a–c), and invoking assumption
item

3
yields

\displaystyle\Phi(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})+\mathcal{L}V(\mathbf{u},p)

\displaystyle=\Phi(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})+\langle\Lambda\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v},\,\mathscr{A}\mathbf{u}+\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})+\mathscr{C}p+\mathbf{f}\rangle

(3.5)

\displaystyle=c(\Lambda\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v},p)-\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{f}\rangle-2\ell(\mathbf{u})-q(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}),

where
q
and

are quadratic and linear forms, respectively, defined as

(3.6a)

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle:=b(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})-a(\Lambda\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})-\Phi(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}),

(3.6b)

\displaystyle\ell(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle:=\tfrac{1}{2}a(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u})-\tfrac{1}{2}\langle\mathbf{f},\Lambda\mathbf{u}\rangle.

Inequality

3.2

and the smallest value of

for which this condition holds is the best upper bound on

that can be proven with the background method as formulated in this section. As anticipated above, minimizing

\mathbf{v}
and balance parameter

3.7
is linear in the optimization variables.

For fixed
\Lambda

3.7

3.2  Symmetry reduction

Analysis and numerical treatment of the optimization problem
eq.

3.8a
for the best bound available to the background method can be considerably simplified in the presence of symmetries. Such symmetries may include, but are not limited to, reflection of the flow variables about a symmetry plane and translation invariance for flows on periodic domains. Abstractly, a symmetry can be described using a group
\mathbb{G}
of orthogonal linear transformations
(\mathbf{u},p)\mapsto\mathscr{G}(\mathbf{u},p)=(\mathscr{G}_{1}\mathbf{u},\mathscr{G}_{2}p)
acting on
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\times\mathbb{P}
. We assume that the subspaces
\mathbb{U}
and
\mathbb{V}
are closed under
\mathbb{G}
, meaning that
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{U}
(resp.
\mathbb{V}
) implies
\mathscr{G}_{1}\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{U}
(resp.
\mathbb{V}
). If the governing equation
eq.

2.1
and the quantity of interest
\Phi(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})
are invariant under
\mathbb{G}
in a sense made precise by
proposition

2
below, then a symmetrization argument similar to that in
[
76
, Appendix A]
shows that auxiliary functions used to bound
\overline{\Phi}^{*}
may be taken to be invariant without loss of generality.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a group of orthogonal linear transformations
(\mathbf{u},p)\mapsto\mathscr{G}(\mathbf{u},p)=(\mathscr{G}_{1}\mathbf{u},\mathscr{G}_{2}p)
. Suppose that:

1.

\Phi
is invariant under
\mathbb{G}
, meaning that
\Phi(\mathscr{G}_{1}\mathbf{u},\mathscr{G}_{1}\mathbf{u})=\Phi(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})
for all
\mathscr{G}\in\mathbb{G}
.

2.

The operators
\mathscr{A}
,
\mathscr{B}
and
\mathscr{C}
in
eq.

2.1
are equivariant under
\mathbb{G}
, meaning that

\mathscr{A}\mathscr{G}_{1}\mathbf{u}=\mathscr{G}_{1}\mathscr{A}\mathbf{u}
,

\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{G}_{1}\mathbf{u},\mathscr{G}_{1}\mathbf{u})=\mathscr{G}_{1}\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})
,

\mathscr{C}\mathscr{G}_{2}p=\mathscr{G}_{1}\mathscr{C}p
for all
\mathscr{G}\in\mathbb{G}
.

3.

The forcing
\mathbf{f}
is invariant under
\mathbb{G}
, meaning that
\mathscr{G}_{1}\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{f}
for all
\mathscr{G}\in\mathbb{G}
.

Then, any upper bound
\overline{\Phi}^{*}\leq\gamma
provable using an auxiliary function satisfying
eq.

3.2
can also be proven using an auxiliary function that is invariant under
\mathbb{G}
.

This result is true for general auxiliary functions, not just the quadratic one in
eq.

3.3
used in the background method. When applied to the latter, it imposes structure to the generalized background field
\mathbf{v}
that reduces the number of optimization variables in
eq.

3.8a
and, more importantly, reveals which dynamical information the background method extracts from the governing equation
eq.

2.1
. The next example, for instance, proves that the generalized background velocity field
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
for the RB convection problem in
example

2
may be set to zero without worsening the bound on the heat transport.
3
3

3

This fact was known to Charlie Doering, who described it to one of the authors as due to a “convexity argument”. Unfortunately, we could not find a published reference to this argument.
This confirms rigorously the recent observation
[

]
that the background method as described above cannot exploit the flow’s momentum equation beyond the “energy” balances encoded by the quadratic term in the auxiliary function
eq.

3.3
.

3.3  Imposing additional constraints

The bound on
\overline{\Phi}^{*}
obtained in
theorem

1
can sometimes be improved by considering further information about the asymptotic behaviour of the flow state
\mathbf{u}
and pressure
p
. Indeed, suppose there exists an absorbing subset
\mathbb{A}\subsetneq\mathbb{U}\times\mathbb{P}
in which all solutions
\mathbf{u}(t),p(t)
of
eq.

2.1
remain after an initial transient, or which is at least approached exponentially quickly. Then,
\overline{\Phi}^{*}
is determined by trajectories that start and remain in
\mathbb{A}
for all times, and to derive an upper bound
\gamma
it suffices to replace constraint
eq.

3.7
with

(3.18)

c(\Lambda\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v},p)-\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{f}\rangle-2\ell(\mathbf{u})-q(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})\leq\gamma\qquad\forall(\mathbf{u},p)\in\mathbb{A}.

Clearly, imposing the inequality on
\mathbb{A}
rather than on the full space
\mathbb{U}\times\mathbb{P}
cannot worsen the optimal bound
\overline{\Phi}^{*}\leq\gamma
available to the background method.

To ensure that the bound remains rigorous, the set
\mathbb{A}
must be defined using constraints that are derived from the governing equations
eq.

2.1
. When studying convective flows, for example, one can often invoke extremum principles to enforce uniform pointwise bounds for the fluid’s temperature, and one can sometimes consider integral estimates involving the pressure; see
[
51
,
37
]
and
example

4
below for more details. If no rigorous constraints are available, one may also consider sets
\mathbb{A}
defined by reasonable but unproven conditions, such as bounds on the energy spectrum of the flow variables
[
14
]
. In this case, one obtains
conditional
bounds that apply only to solutions of
eq.

2.1
satisfying the imposed conditions, which may or may not exist.

Irrespective of whether the absorbing set
\mathbb{A}
is defined rigorously or not, we now assume for concreteness that it can be described using one pointwise linear inequality (modelling, say, an extremum principle) and one inequality involving quadratic forms (modelling, say, an integral estimate). Specifically, we let

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}:=\big\{(\mathbf{u},p)\in\mathbb{U}\times\mathbb{P}:\quad

\displaystyle h+\mathscr{K}\mathbf{u}+\mathscr{L}p\geq 0\text{ a.e. on }\Omega,

(3.19)

\displaystyle d_{0}+d_{1}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})+d_{2}(\mathbf{u},p)+d_{3}(p,p)\geq 0\big\},

where the function
h\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, the linear operators
\mathscr{K}:\mathbb{U}\to L^{2}(\Omega)
and
\mathscr{L}:\mathbb{P}\to L^{2}(\Omega)
, the constant
d_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, and the bilinear forms
d_{1}:\mathbb{U}\times\mathbb{U}\to\mathbb{R}
,
d_{2}:\mathbb{U}\times\mathbb{P}\to\mathbb{R}
and

are given. Our discussion can be easily generalized to include pointwise quadratic inequalities, equality constraints, and multiple conditions of each type.

The constraints that define the absorbing set
\mathbb{A}
can be enforced in

using Lagrange multipliers. Indeed, suppose that if

and

satisfy

\displaystyle\gamma\geq\,c(\Lambda\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v},p)-\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{f}\rangle-2\ell(\mathbf{u})-q(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})\qquad

\displaystyle+\alpha[d_{0}+d_{1}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})+d_{1}(\mathbf{u},p)+d_{3}(p,p)]\quad

(3.20)

\displaystyle+\langle s,h+\mathscr{K}\mathbf{u}+\mathscr{L}p\rangle

\displaystyle\quad\forall(\mathbf{u},p)\in\mathbb{U}\times\mathbb{P}.

We therefore arrive at the following result.

(Background method with extra constraints)
.

The best upper bound

on

\gamma^{*}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathbb{R},\;\Lambda\in\mathbb{M}\\
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{V},\;s\in L^{2}(\Omega)\end{subarray}}\left\{\gamma:\;\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{e:s-procedure}},\;\alpha\geq 0,\;s(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\text{ a.e. on }\Omega\right\}.

4  Computational implementation

Calculating the best upper bound on
\overline{\Phi}^{*}
that the background method has to offer requires solving the minimization problem
eq.

3.8a
or its improved version
eq.

3.21
. Usually, these problems are analytically intractable and must be tackled numerically.

The computational implementation of the background method is complicated by the fact that the Euler–Lagrange equations for the optimal
\Lambda
and
\mathbf{v}
in
eq.

3.8a
and for the optimal
\mathbf{u}
in
eq.

3.8b
are nonlinear and, often, admit multiple solutions. Only solutions satisfying the spectral constraint
eq.

3.9
correspond to the saddle point of problem
eq.

3.8b
and give the optimal bound on
\overline{\Phi}^{*}
; the others are “spurious” stationary points for which the eigenvalue problem
eq.

3.10
has negative eigenvalues (see
[
35
, Figure 2]
for a schematic illustration), and which therefore do not produce valid bounds.

This issue has historically been addressed using delicate numerical schemes based on continuation and bifurcation analysis
[
33
,
62
,
63
,
39
,
44
,
64
,
20
,
34
]
. Recently, two simpler alternatives have emerged that do not require continuation and have been applied successfully to a variety of flows.
One approach
[
80
,
84
,
65
,
85
,
36
,
42
,
37
,
51
]
discretizes the minimization problem
eq.

3.8a
into a semidefinite program (SDP)—a convex optimization problem where matrices with affine dependence on the optimization variables are constrained to be positive semidefinite. This SDP can then be solved using algorithms with polynomial-time complexity
[
86
,
87
,
88
,
89
,
90
]
.
The other approach
[
91
,
46
,
35
,
47
,
69
,
66
]
applies the steepest gradient method to the saddle-point formulation
eq.

3.8b
to derive time-dependent versions of the Euler–Lagrange equations, which can be timestepped until convergence to a stationary solution.
The next subsections review these two approaches and reveal a new connection between them.

4.1  Optimal bounds with semidefinite programming

To discretize problem
eq.

3.8a
into an SDP, suppose that all balance parameters in the linear space
\mathbb{M}_{0}
can be represented as

(4.1)

\Lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\lambda_{i}\Lambda_{i}

for fixed symmetric matrices
\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{s}
and some vector of coefficients
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{s})
. Further, replace the spaces
\mathbb{V}
,
\mathbb{U}
and
\mathbb{P}
with finite-dimensional subspaces

(4.2a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{h}

\displaystyle:=\linspan(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m_{v}}),

(4.2b)

\displaystyle\mathbb{U}_{h}

\displaystyle:=\linspan(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\eta}_{m_{u}}),

(4.2c)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{h}

\displaystyle:=\linspan(\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{m_{p}}),

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}
,
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}
and
\zeta_{i}
are prescribed basis functions and
h
is a discretization parameter inversely proportional to the dimension of the discrete spaces (e.g.,
h
represents the size of a mesh for the fluid’s domain). We assume for simplicity that
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}
satisfy all constraints used to define
\mathbb{V}
and
\mathbb{U}
, such as boundary conditions and incompressibility; other choices may be more convenient in practice, but require enforcing these conditions using projection operators or Lagrange multipliers that complicate the exposition.

Expanding

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{v}}\hat{v}_{i}\,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}(\mathbf{x}),

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{u}}\hat{u}_{i}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}(\mathbf{x}),

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{p}}\hat{p}_{i}\,\zeta_{i}(\mathbf{x}),

and writing

\hat{\mathbf{v}}=(\hat{v}_{1},\ldots,\hat{v}_{m_{v}})
and
\hat{\mathbf{u}}=(\hat{u}_{1},\ldots,\hat{u}_{m_{u}})
for the vectors of expansion coefficients, the inequality constraint in
eq.

3.8a
discretizes into

(4.4)

\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\hat{\mathbf{u}}\end{pmatrix}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\gamma+\mathbf{f}^{h}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{v}}&\mathbf{l}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\hat{\mathbf{v}})^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\\
\mathbf{l}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\hat{\mathbf{v}})&\tfrac{1}{2}Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\hat{\mathbf{v}})+\tfrac{1}{2}Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\hat{\mathbf{v}})^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\end{pmatrix}}_{=:S(\gamma,\boldsymbol{\lambda},\hat{\mathbf{v}})}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\hat{\mathbf{u}}\end{pmatrix}\geq 0\quad\forall\hat{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{u}},

where the (column) vectors
\mathbf{f}^{h}
and
\mathbf{l}
and the matrix
Q
have entries

(4.5a)

\displaystyle f^{h}_{i}:=\langle\mathbf{f},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\rangle,\quad i=1,\ldots,m_{v},

(4.5b)

\displaystyle{l}_{i}:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{v}}a(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i})\,\hat{v}_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\langle\mathbf{f},\Lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}\rangle\lambda_{j},\quad i=1,\ldots,m_{u},

(4.5c)

\displaystyle Q_{ij}:=\sum_{k=1}^{m_{v}}b(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{j})\hat{v}_{k}-\sum_{k=1}^{s}a(\Lambda_{k}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{j})\lambda_{k}-\Phi(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{j}),\quad i,j=1,\ldots,m_{u}.

It is well known that
eq.

4.4
holds if and only if the matrix
S(\gamma,\boldsymbol{\lambda},\hat{\mathbf{v}})
is positive semidefinite, a
linear matrix inequality
(LMI) constraint which we denoted by
S(\gamma,\boldsymbol{\lambda},\hat{\mathbf{v}})\succeq 0
. The constraint
\mathscr{C}^{\star}\mathbf{v}=0
, instead, can be imposed in a weak sense by requiring
c(\mathbf{v},p)=0
for all
p\in\mathbb{P}_{h}
, leading to the linear constraints
C^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\hat{\mathbf{v}}=0
with matrix coefficients
C_{ij}:=c(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i},\zeta_{j})
for
i=1,\ldots,m_{v}
and
j=1,\ldots,m_{p}
. Thus, one obtains the SDP

(4.6)

\gamma_{h}^{*}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{s}\\
\hat{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{v}}\end{subarray}}\;\left\{\gamma:\;S(\gamma,\boldsymbol{\lambda},\hat{\mathbf{v}})\succeq 0\text{ and }C^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\hat{\mathbf{v}}=0\right\}.

If the discrete spaces
\mathbb{V}_{h}
,
\mathbb{U}_{h}
and
\mathbb{P}_{h}
enjoy suitable approximation properties in
\mathbb{V}
,
\mathbb{U}
and
\mathbb{P}
, it is reasonable to expect that the optimal solution
\gamma_{h}^{*}
of this SDP converges to the optimal bound
\gamma^{*}
in
theorem

1
as the discretization is refined (
h\to 0
). To the best of our knowledge, however, rigorous convergence results are yet to be proven.

The SDP
eq.

4.6
can be solved using a variety of general-purpose algorithms
[
86
,
87
,
88
,
89
,
90
]
, which can be divided into the two categories of
interior-point methods
and
first-order methods
. The former are versions of Newton’s method in which positive semidefinite constraints are imposed via so-called barrier functions, and converge to
\varepsilon
-suboptimal solutions in
O(\left|\log\varepsilon\right|)
iterations
[
92
, §4.3.3]
(often, no more than 10–50). However, for an SDP with an
n\times n
LMI and
m
optimization variables, each iteration of a standard interior-point method requires
O(n^{2}+m^{2})
memory and
O(m^{3}+n^{2}m^{2}+n^{3}m)
floating-point operations
[
92
, §4.3.3]
, both of which increase quickly as
n
or
m
are raised. First-order methods, instead, have a lower complexity because they use only gradient information, but also converge more slowly. For example, the algorithm in
[
90
]
finds
\varepsilon
-suboptimal solutions in
O(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
iterations that require
O(m^{2}+n^{3})
floating-point operations each.

It must be observed that the solution
\gamma_{h}^{*}
of
eq.

4.6
is not a rigorous upper bound on
\overline{\Phi}^{*}
, because replacing the spaces
\mathbb{U}
and
\mathbb{P}
with their subspaces
\mathbb{U}_{h}
and
\mathbb{P}_{h}
relaxes the constraint in the original problem
eq.

3.8a
. On the other hand, one can always optimize
\mathbf{v}
over the finite-dimensional subspace
\mathbb{V}_{h}
of
\mathbb{V}
at the expense of worsening the bound on
\overline{\Phi}^{*}
. Consequently,
\gamma_{h}^{*}
estimates from
below
the best (suboptimal) upper bound on
\overline{\Phi}^{*}
that can be proven with
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
. To obtain an estimate from above, one must modify the constraints of
eq.

4.6
to account for the difference between
\mathbb{U}
and
\mathbb{P}
and their discrete counterparts. This is achieved in
[
65
,
84
,
85
]
for discretizations based on Legendre expansions in one coordinate and Fourier expansions in the remaining ones: using estimates that rely on the special properties of the Fourier and Legendre bases, such as orthogonality and differentiation rules, the infinite-dimensional constraint in
eq.

3.8a
can be
strengthened
into sufficient finite-dimensional LMIs, which depend on a finite set of expansion coefficients and on the
L^{2}
norm of the expansion ‘tail’. Generalizing these results to other expansion bases remains an open problem.

Another crucial observation is that the choice of basis functions for
\mathbb{V}_{h}
,
\mathbb{U}_{h}
and
\mathbb{P}_{h}
strongly influences the structure of the LMI constraint in
eq.

4.6
and, therefore, its computational complexity. For instance,
fig.

2
illustrates different LMI structures obtained when a two-dimensional version of the RB problem considered in
example

2
, simplified as described in
example

3
, is discretized using sinusoidal functions with wavenumber
{k}
in the horizontal direction and either Legendre polynomials or compactly-supported piecewise-cubic functions in the vertical direction. The block-diagonal structure corresponds to the decoupling of different wavenumbers, and the positive semidefiniteness of each block can be imposed separately to obtain an SDP with multiple smaller LMIs. This is convenient because SDPs of this type can currently be solved more efficiently than SDPs with a single large LMI; for instance, standard interior point methods require
O(m^{3}+ks^{2}m^{2}+ks^{3}m)
floating-point operations per iteration to handle a
ks\times ks
block-diagonal LMI with
m
optimization variable and
k
diagonal blocks of size
s\times s
, which is usually much smaller than the
O(m^{3}+k^{2}s^{2}m^{2}+k^{3}s^{3}m)
operations required when the block-diagonal structure is not exploited.

4.2  Optimal bounds with timestepping

An alternative approach, used to optimize bounds for the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation in
[
91
]
and extended to fluid flows in
[
46
,
35
]
, is to view the optimization variables in the saddle-point problem
eq.

3.8b
as time-dependent and derive evolution equations ensuring the steepest ascent in
\mathbf{u}
and the steepest descent in
\mathbf{v}
and
\Lambda
. Well-known and efficient numerical schemes for differential equations can then be employed to solve such equations until convergence to a steady solution, which is a critical point for
eq.

3.8b
and, very often, is the correct saddle point. This approach has been shown to work robustly for Rayleigh–Bénard convection
[
35
]
, porous media convection
[
47
]
, plane Couette flow with injection and suction
[
66
]
, and Taylor–Couette flow
[
69
]
.

A subtle but essential observation (see, e.g.,
[
98
, Ch. 3]
and
[
38
]
) is that tracking the time evolution of a single field
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{U}
may not be sufficient to solve
eq.

3.8b
correctly. This is because, when the balance parameter
\Lambda
and the generalized background field
\mathbf{v}
are optimal, the quadratic form
q
may vanish for a number
\rho
of fields
\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{\rho}\in\mathbb{U}
, which are often called
critical modes

[
39
,
46
,
35
]
and are eigenfunctions with zero eigenvalue for the eigenvalue problem
eq.

3.10
associated to the spectral constraint. For reasons that will become clear in
section

4.3
these critical fields must be tracked explicitly to impose the spectral constraint, even though they give no contribution to the optimal value of
eq.

3.8b
by
proposition

1
item

1
. One must also track a generic field, denoted by
\mathbf{u}_{0}
, for which
q
need not vanish and which plays the role of
\mathbf{u}
in
eq.

3.8b
. In other words, rather than considering the objective functional in
eq.

3.8b
, one must look for a saddle point of the modified Lagrangian

(4.7)

L(\mathbf{u}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{\rho},p,\mathbf{v},\Lambda):=-c(\mathbf{v},p)-\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{f}\rangle-2\ell(\mathbf{u}_{0})-\sum_{r=0}^{\rho}q(\mathbf{u}_{r},\mathbf{u}_{r}),

where
p
is a Lagrange multiplier enforcing the constraint
\mathscr{C}^{\star}\mathbf{v}=0
.

Writing
\Lambda=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\lambda_{j}\Lambda_{j}
as in
section

4.1
, the time derivative of this Lagrangian is

(4.8)

\frac{{\rm d}L}{{\rm d}t}=\left\langle\frac{\delta L}{\delta\mathbf{v}},\frac{{\rm d}\mathbf{v}}{{\rm d}t}\right\rangle+\sum_{j=1}^{s}\left\langle\frac{\partial L}{\partial\lambda_{j}},\frac{{\rm d}\lambda_{j}}{{\rm d}t}\right\rangle+\sum_{r=0}^{\rho}\left\langle\frac{\delta L}{\delta\mathbf{u}_{r}},\frac{{\rm d}\mathbf{u}_{r}}{{\rm d}t}\right\rangle-\left\langle\mathscr{C}^{\star}\mathbf{v},\frac{{\rm d}p}{{\rm d}t}\right\rangle,

where
\frac{\delta L}{\delta\psi}
denotes the variation (Fréchet derivative) of
L
with respect to a field
\psi
. Insisting that
\mathscr{C}^{\star}\mathbf{v}=0
pointwise in time, so the last term vanishes, the evolution equations ensuring the fastest growth in
L
with respect to each
\mathbf{u}_{r}
and the fastest reduction with respect to
\mathbf{v}
and each
\lambda_{j}
are

(4.9a)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\mathbf{v}}{{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\delta L}{\delta\mathbf{v}}\equiv\mathscr{A}\mathbf{u}_{0}+\sum_{r=0}^{\rho}\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{u}_{r},\mathbf{u}_{r})+\mathscr{C}p+\mathbf{f},

(4.9b)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\lambda_{j}}{{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial L}{\partial\lambda_{j}}\equiv-\langle\mathbf{f},\Lambda_{j}\mathbf{u}_{0}\rangle-\sum_{r=0}^{\rho}a(\Lambda_{j}\mathbf{u}_{r},\mathbf{u}_{r})

\displaystyle\text{for }j=1,\ldots,s,

(4.9c)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\mathbf{u}_{r}}{{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle=\;\frac{\delta L}{\delta\mathbf{u}_{r}}\;\equiv\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{s}\lambda_{j}\Lambda_{j}\mathbf{f}-\mathscr{A}^{\star}\mathbf{v}\bigg)\delta_{0r}-\mathscr{F}(\mathbf{v})\mathbf{u}_{r}

\displaystyle\text{for }r=0,\ldots,\rho,

where
\delta_{0r}
is the usual Kronecker delta and we have introduced the linear operator
\mathscr{F}(\mathbf{v}):=\mathscr{H}(\mathbf{v})+\mathscr{H}(\mathbf{v})^{\star}-\Lambda\mathscr{A}-\mathscr{A}^{\star}\Lambda-\mathscr{P}-\mathscr{P}^{\star}
for notational convenience. Stationary solutions to these equations are stationary points of the Lagrangian
L
, and those satisfying the spectral constraint
eq.

3.9
provide optimal solutions to
eq.

3.8b
.

Projecting
eqs.

4.9a
,
4.9b
and
4.9c
onto the finite-dimensional spaces
\mathbb{U}_{h}
,
\mathbb{V}_{h}
and
\mathbb{P}_{h}
defined in

4.2
yields the set of ODEs

(4.10a)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}(V\hat{\mathbf{v}})}{{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle=A\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{0}+\sum_{r=0}^{\rho}\mathbf{b}(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{r},\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{r})+C\hat{\mathbf{p}}+\mathbf{f}^{h},

(4.10b)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\lambda_{j}}{{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{m_{u}}\langle\mathbf{f},\Lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}\rangle\hat{u}_{0i}-\sum_{r=0}^{\rho}\,\sum_{i,k=1}^{m_{u}}\!a(\Lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{k})\hat{u}_{ri}\hat{u}_{rk}

\displaystyle\text{for }j=1,\ldots,s,

(4.10c)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}(U\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{r})}{{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle=-2\mathbf{l}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\hat{\mathbf{v}})\delta_{0r}-\left[Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\hat{\mathbf{v}})+Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\hat{\mathbf{v}})^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\right]\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{r},\phantom{\sum_{r=0}^{\rho}}

\displaystyle\text{for }r=0,\ldots,\rho.

Here,
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{r}=(\hat{u}_{r1},\ldots,\hat{u}_{rm_{u}})
is the vector of expansion coefficients of
\mathbf{u}_{r}
in the chosen basis for
\mathbb{U}_{h}
, the vector
\mathbf{b}(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{r},\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{r})
has entries

(4.11)

b_{i}(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{r},\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{r}):=\sum_{j,k=1}^{m_{u}}b(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{j},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{k})\hat{u}_{rj}\,\hat{u}_{rk},\qquad i=1,\ldots,m_{v},

the matrices
V
,
A
and
U
have entries

(4.12a)

\displaystyle V_{ij}

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j}\rangle,

\displaystyle i,j

\displaystyle=1,\ldots,m_{v},

(4.12b)

\displaystyle A_{ij}

\displaystyle=a(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{j}),

\displaystyle i

\displaystyle=1,\ldots,m_{v},\;j=1,\ldots,m_{u},

(4.12c)

\displaystyle U_{ij}

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{j}\rangle,

\displaystyle i,j

\displaystyle=1,\ldots,m_{u},

and all other quantities are defined in
section

4.1
.
The ODEs in
eq.

4.10
, complemented by the algebraic condition
C^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\hat{\mathbf{v}}=0
obtained upon discretizing the constraint
\mathscr{C}^{\star}\mathbf{v}=0
as explained in
section

, can be integrated in time until convergence to a stationary solution using standard numerical schemes, starting from any randomly generated nonzero initial condition.

Remark 5
.

The value of
\rho
in
eq.

4.7
must be chosen
a priori
and must be large enough to ensure that the saddle point of
L
is the only stationary attractor for
eq.

4.9
, so the timestepping process cannot converge to a “spurious” solution. The discussion in
section

4.3
below and the numerical results in
[
46
,
35
,
47
,
69
,
66
]
and
[

\rho
should be equal to the largest possible multiplicity of the principal eigenvalue of the eigenvalue problem
eq.

3.10
associated to the spectral constraint
eq.

3.9
. Analysis in
[

establishes this conjecture when the background method is applied with
fixed

\Lambda
(i.e., fixed
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{s}
) to RB convection with stress-free isothermal boundaries, plane Couette flow, and porous media convection. It remains an open theoretical problem to extend these specialized arguments to the general setting considered in the present work.

Remark 6
.

In highly turbulent regimes (e.g., convection at very high Rayleigh number), the numerical solution of the ODE system
eq.

4.10
typically requires small timesteps to ensure numerical stability. In such regimes, therefore, convergence to a stationary solution can be slow. One way to mitigate this issue is to employ a two-step approach
[
35
]
in which the timestepping is stopped as soon as the magnitude of the time derivatives drops below a moderate tolerance, and the resulting approximately stationary solution is used to initialize Newton–Kantorovich iterations for the Euler–Lagrange equations of the saddle-point problem
eq.

4.7
. As discussed in
[
46
,
35
]
, efficiency can be further improved by adaptively dropping from the computation any vectors
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{r}
with
r\geq 1
that appear to converge to zero, because they have no effect on the long-time behaviour of solutions to
eq.

4.10
.

4.3  Connecting the two approaches

The timestepping strategy of
section

4.2
is closely related to the semidefinite programming approach of
section

4.1
, in the sense that it provides a particular numerical scheme for solving the SDP
eq.

4.6
. Precisely, the ODEs in
eq.

4.10
can be viewed as a steepest-gradient algorithm applied to the SDP
eq.

4.6
when the matrix Lagrange multiplier for its LMI constraint is represented using a low-rank factorization.

To make this interpretation evident, let us assume that the SDP
eq.

4.6
is strictly feasible, meaning that there exist
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and
\hat{\mathbf{v}}
such that
C^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\hat{\mathbf{v}}=0
and that the matrix
S(\gamma,\boldsymbol{\lambda},\hat{\mathbf{v}})
is strictly positive definite. With this additional (mild) assumption, standard duality theory for SDPs
[
99
, §5.9.1]
guarantees that

(4.13)

\gamma_{h}^{*}=\mathop{\vphantom{p}\inf}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\mathbb{R},\;\hat{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{v}}\\
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}\end{subarray}}\;\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{p}}\\
Z\succeq 0\end{subarray}}\;\left\{\gamma-\hat{\mathbf{v}}\cdot C\hat{\mathbf{p}}-Z\cdot S(\gamma,\boldsymbol{\lambda},\hat{\mathbf{v}})\right\},

where
\hat{\mathbf{p}}
and
Z
are Lagrange multipliers for the constraints of
eq.

4.6
. Moreover, by a condition known as
complementary slackness
, the ranks of the optimal
Z
and of the optimal
S(\gamma,\boldsymbol{\lambda},\hat{\mathbf{v}})
must sum to the matrix size
m_{u}+1
. Therefore,
if one can anticipate that the optimal solution of the SDP
eq.

4.6
satisfies
\rank(S(\gamma,\boldsymbol{\lambda},\hat{\mathbf{v}}))\geq m_{u}-\rho
for some integer
\rho\leq m_{u}
, then the corresponding optimal multiplier must have
\rank(Z)\leq\rho+1
.

Now, any positive semidefinite matrix
Z
of dimension
m_{u}+1
and rank no larger than
\rho+1
can be expressed as the sum of at most
\rho+1
rank-1 matrices. In particular, we can write

(4.14)

Z=\begin{pmatrix}t^{2}&t\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\\
t\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{0}&\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\end{pmatrix}+\sum_{r=1}^{\rho}\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbf{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\\
\mathbf{0}&\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{r}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\end{pmatrix}

for some vectors
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{0},\ldots,\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{u}}
and some nonnegative scalar
t
. Substituting this decomposition and the definition of
S(\gamma,\boldsymbol{\lambda},\hat{\mathbf{v}})
from
eq.

4.4
into
eq.

4.13
, and unfolding matrix inner products involving rank-1 matrices into standard matrix-vector products, leads to

(4.15)

\gamma_{h}^{*}=\mathop{\vphantom{p}\inf}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\mathbb{R}\\
\hat{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{v}}\\
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}\end{subarray}}\;\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{0},\ldots,\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{u}}\\
\hat{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{p}}\\
t\geq 0\end{subarray}}\bigg\{(1-t^{2})\gamma-\hat{\mathbf{v}}\cdot C\hat{\mathbf{p}}-t^{2}\,\mathbf{f}^{h}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{v}}\\[-25.83325pt]
-2t\,\mathbf{l}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\hat{\mathbf{v}})\cdot\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{0}-\sum_{r=0}^{\rho}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{r}\cdot Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\hat{\mathbf{v}})\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{r}\bigg\}.

Optimizing over
\gamma
yields
t=1
, in which case the argument of this inf-sup problem reduces
exactly
to the discrete version of the Lagrangian
L
in
eq.

4.7
. Further optimizing over
\hat{\mathbf{p}}
returns the constraint
C^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\hat{\mathbf{v}}=0
, while the ODEs in
eq.

4.10
are recovered upon fixing
t=1
and applying steepest ascent in each
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{r}
and steepest descent in
\hat{\mathbf{v}}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
with respect to the discrete norms

\|\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{r}\|_{\mathbb{U}_{h}}=(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{r}\cdot U\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{r})^{1/2}
,

\|\hat{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\mathbb{V}_{h}}=(\hat{\mathbf{v}}\cdot V\hat{\mathbf{v}})^{1/2}
and

\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
.
At the discrete level, therefore, the timestepping approach of
section

4.2
is a particular example of general low-rank factorization strategies for SDPs that have been studied extensively
[
100
,
101
,
102
,
103
,
104
,
105
]
.

This realization enables us to make interesting observations regarding the theoretical convergence properties of the timestepping approach—specifically, about whether one can guarantee the avoidance of spurious solutions in general, as suggested by the numerical evidence in
[
46
,
35
,
47
,
69
,
66
]
. On the one hand, the timestepping approach should not be expected to work correctly unless
\rho+1
is at least as large as the rank of the optimal matrix
Z
in
eq.

4.13
. This is why it is usually necessary to include the fields
\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{\rho}
in
eq.

4.7
. By complementary slackness, the rank of the optimal
Z
coincides with the number of zero eigenvalues of the optimal
S(\gamma,\boldsymbol{\lambda},\hat{\mathbf{v}})
. Since the bottom-right block of this matrix is the discrete counterpart of the self-adjoint operator on the left-hand side of the eigenvalue problem
eq.

3.10
, we conclude that
\rho
should be no smaller than the maximum possible multiplicity of the principal eigenvalue of that problem as
\mathbf{v}
and
\Lambda=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\lambda_{j}\Lambda_{j}
are varied.

On the other hand, if we assume that the SDP
eq.

4.6
and its dual have smooth constraint sets in the sense of
[
104
]
, then general results for low-rank factorization methods for SDPs
[
104
]
guarantee that the timestepping approach avoids spurious solutions when
\rho+1
is larger than an upper bound on the rank of the optimal
Z
derived in
[
106
]
, which depends only on the size of the optimization variables in
eq.

4.13
. This bound, however, is usually much larger than the maximum eigenvalue multiplicity mentioned above, so its use results in significantly more expensive computations. Unfortunately, it is also sharp
[
105
]
: there exist SDPs (not necessarily arising from applications of the background method) for which setting
\rho+1
to be larger than the rank of the optimal
Z
but smaller than the upper bound from
[
106
]
can result in convergence to locally stable spurious solutions. This difficulty, moreover, cannot be resolved by replacing gradient-based timestepping with another algorithm for saddle-point problems because the results in
[
105
]
are independent of the numerical scheme used to solve
eq.

4.15
. It would be interesting to investigate whether locally stable spurious solutions can arise for SDPs coming from the background method, and one possible way forward is to try and combine the general analysis in
[
104
,
105
]
with the convergence proofs for specific flows in
[
35
]
. Based on the available numerical evidence in
[
46
,
35
,
47
,
69
,
66
]
and in
[
98
, Ch. 3]
, we make the following conjecture.

Conjecture
.

Taking
\rho
equal to the maximum possible multiplicity of the eigenvalue problem
eq.

3.10
suffices to optimize background fields correctly.

While proving or disproving this statement remains an open theoretical problem, we stress that the timestepping approach works robustly in practice and we are not aware of any examples where locally stable spurious solutions arise once
\rho
is chosen according to our conjecture. (An example that smaller values can result in convergence to spurious solutions, instead, is given in
[
98
, Ch. 3]
). Furthermore, in particular implementations one can simply choose
\rho
as conjectured, check if timestepping returns a spurious solution by solving the eigenvalue problem
eq.

3.10
and, in that case, repeat the calculation with a larger
\rho
.

5  Conclusions

Despite being established over 30 years ago, the background method remains one of the main tools to place rigorous bounds on time-averaged properties of turbulent flows. Here, we have reviewed a recent interpretation of the method as an application of the auxiliary function framework
[
74
,
75
,
79
]
with quadratic auxiliary functions in the form
eq.

3.3
, which are parametrized by generalized background fields and balance parameters. This interpretation allows one to systematically formulate convex variational principles to search for bounds on mean quantities that, contrary to the traditional scope of the background method
[
81
]
, need not be equivalent to the mean dissipation in the flow. We have also shown that symmetries can be used to simplify the choice of (generalized) background fields—proving, in particular, that nonzero background velocity fields cannot improve existing bounds on the heat transport in Rayleigh–Bénard convection—and that additional constraints, such as maximum principles, can be enforced using Lagrange multipliers. We hope that our general presentation using the abstract flow description of
section

2
can provide a “recipe” for future applications of the background method to a wider variety of flows, and offer a basis for future refinements and extensions.

We have also provided a general description of two approaches for the computational implementation of the background method, which have been applied successfully to a variety of shear and convective flows in horizontally periodic domains (see
table

1
for references). The first approach, based on semidefinite programming, is extremely flexible, has general convergence guarantees, and can be implemented using general-purpose interior-point SDP solvers that are available open-source. To use such solvers in turbulent regimes that require accurate discretization of thin boundary layers, however, one must employ advanced decomposition techniques that have only recently been developed by the optimization community (see
[
97
]
for a review). The second approach to optimizing background fields we have reviewed, instead, relies on timestepping. This is generally more familiar to the fluid mechanics community and can more easily reach highly turbulent regimes, but can converge slowly and presently lacks general theoretical convergence guarantees beyond the particular ones in
[
35
]
.

Given the poor scalability of current interior-point SDP solvers and the possibly slow convergence of the timestepping approach in dynamically complicated flow regimes (see
remark

6
),
further progress is needed before complex flows of industrial relevance can be tackled robustly and at a reasonable computational cost. We expect that significant efficiency gains may be achieved by exploiting sparsity in SDPs via matrix decomposition, as outlined in
section

4.1
, as well as the newly established connection between the timestepping approach of
section

4.2
and low-rank factorization strategies for SDPs. Particular open questions that warrant deeper investigation include whether the (restrictive) technical assumptions required by chordal matrix decomposition can be weakened, and whether sophisticated analysis techniques and optimization algorithms for SDPs with low-rank solutions can guarantee fast convergence to the optimal bounds whilst provably avoiding spurious solutions.

Despite the many recent advances, therefore,
the numerical analysis of the background method remains in our opinion far from complete and offers numerous opportunities for interesting research. Pursuing these opportunities promises to extend the range of flows for which bounds on mean quantities can be computed efficiently. Moreover, and perhaps more excitingly, any lessons learnt in the process may reveal practical ways to optimize auxiliary functions more general than the quadratic ones underpinning the background method. These can in principle produce arbitrarily sharp bounds on the properties of turbulent flows
[
78
]
, but currently remain beyond reach.
