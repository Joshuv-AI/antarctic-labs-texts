Non-uniqueness of Hölder continuous solutions for stochastic Euler and Hypodissipative Navier-Stokes equations
Kush Kinra, Ujjwal Koley

Abstract
. We construct infinitely many Hölder continuous, global-in-time, and stationary solutions to the stochastic Euler equations and the hypodissipative Navier-Stokes equations, taking values in the space C ⁡ ( ℝ , C ϑ ) C(\mathbb{R};C^{\vartheta}) . For the Euler case, the Hölder exponent ϑ \vartheta satisfies 0 < ϑ < 5 7 ​ β 0<\vartheta<\frac{5}{7}\beta with 0 < β < 1 200 0<\beta<\frac{1}{200} , while for the hypodissipative Navier-Stokes equations, β \beta must additionally satisfy 0 < β < min { 2 ​ ( 1 − 2 ​ α ) 21 , 1 200 } 0<\beta<\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{2(1-2\alpha)}{21},\frac{1}{200}}}\right\} . The construction relies on a modified stochastic convex integration scheme, which is central to the analysis. This scheme incorporates Beltrami flows as building blocks and carefully tracks inductive estimates, both pathwise and in expectation. These refinements allow us to achieve improved Hölder regularity for solutions to the underlying stochastic equations, advancing the scope of convex integration techniques in the stochastic setting.

Introduction

1.1.
The equations

In this article, we are interested in Euler and hypodissipative Navier-Stokes equations perturbed by additive noise in a three-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{3}={\mathbb{R}^{3}}/{(\mathrm{2\pi}\mathbb{Z})^{3}}
. The stochastic hypodissipative Navier-Stokes equations are given by

(1.1)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \/\mathrm{d}\/\boldsymbol{u}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\nu(-\Delta)^{\alpha}\boldsymbol{u}+\mathrm{div}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{u}}}\right)+\nabla p}}\right]\/\mathrm{d}\/t&=\/\mathrm{d}\/\mathrm{W},&&\text{ in }\ \mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}^{3},\\
\mathrm{div}\;\boldsymbol{u}&=0,&&\text{ in }\ \mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}^{3},\end{aligned}}}\right.

where
\boldsymbol{u}(t,x):\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

is a

Moreover, the stochastic Euler equations are given by

(1.2)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \/\mathrm{d}\/\boldsymbol{u}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathrm{div}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{u}}}\right)+\nabla p}}\right]\/\mathrm{d}\/t&=\/\mathrm{d}\/\mathrm{W},&&\text{ in }\ \mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}^{3},\\
\mathrm{div}\;\boldsymbol{u}&=0,&&\text{ in }\ \mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}^{3}.\end{aligned}}}\right.

1.2.
Convex integration related results

Convex integration has its beginnings, at least in the deterministic setting, in the work of Nash
[
32
]
and Kuiper
[
28
]
on
C^{1}
-isometric embeddings of Riemannian manifolds. In the context of fluid flow models, convex integration techniques have been used successfully by De Lellis and Székelyhidi
[
15
]
to construct non-unique weak solutions for the incompressible Euler equations in the class of
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
functions. Since their method was based on Tartar’s plane wave analysis (
[
36
]
), it was not supporting to construct continuous solutions. A significant advancement occurred when De Lellis and Székelyhidi (
[
16
]
) made use of Beltrami waves (introduced in
[
9
]
long back) as building blocks. Later, in
[
17
]
, they were able to construct infinitely many weak solutions in the Hölder space
C^{\frac{1}{10}-}
with the help of Beltrami waves, which proved to be a significant step towards well-known Onsager’s conjecture (
[
33
]
). Finally, the author in
[
26
]
was able to provide a rigorous proof of Onsager’s conjecture, see
[
4
]
also. A new class of construction blocks, known as Mikado flows, was taken into consideration by Daneri and Székelyhidi in
[
13
]
, which led to significant advancements over the original techniques. A short while later, the authors in
[
5
]
presented an additional kind of building blocks, known as intermittent Beltrami flows, to demonstrate the non-uniqueness of weak solutions to the 3D Navier-Stokes equations. We also refer readers to
[
6
]
for a detailed study of convex integration techniques.

Given this long list of deterministic results on non-uniqueness, one would naturally wonder if the situation is different when stochastic forces are present. In fact, it is a well-known that certain PDEs can be regularized by applying random forces to the equation under the right circumstances. This is because some ill-posed deterministic problems have well-posed stochastic counterparts. To put it briefly, the phenomenon of regularization by noise occurs when there is enough active noise, acting non-trivially in multiple directions, to push solutions away from singularities in the underlying vector field, see
[
1
,
7
,
19
,
20
]
, etc.

Given these outcomes, the ill-posedness results from convex integration techniques previously described did not exclude the possibility that their stochastic counterparts could be well-posed. But, the authors in
[
25
]
dashed widespread expectations that (pathwise) uniqueness may exist for well-known fluid dynamical stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs). More precisely, the authors in
[
25
]
introduced a stochastic version of convex integration technique and establish the non-uniqueness in law of stochastic 3D Navier-Stokes equations. Note that the authors in
[
25
]
used stopping time arguments to produce the required result. Later, many mathematicians followed the work
[
25
]
and obtained non-uniqueness of many other stochastic models, see
[
11
,
22
,
24
,
27
,
29
,
34
,
38
]
, and references therein. Thereafter, the authors in
[
11
]
develop a new stochastic convex integration scheme which allow us to avoid stopping time arguments, see also
[
23
,
30
]
, etc. More specifically, the authors in
[
11
]
were able to iterate inductive estimates in expectation in the stochastic convex integration scheme and utilized stationary Mikado flows as building blocks. Recently, the authors in
[
30
]
used Beltrami waves as building blocks and iterated inductive estimates both pathwise as well as in expectation in their inductive scheme. This makes the calculations easier and improves the solutions’ regularity.

The goal of this work is to implement the concepts of
[
11
,
30
]
to stochastic hypodissipative Navier-Stokes equations as well as stochastic Euler equations. In particular, inspired by the work of
[
11
,
30
]
, we demonstrate existence of infinitely many
global (in-time)
solutions by implementing the idea of iterating both pathwise estimates and expectation estimates in the inductive scheme. Note that convex integration techniques have also been applied to the fractional (hypodissipative as well as hyperdissipative) Navier-Stokes equations, in two and three spatial dimensions, and in both the deterministic and stochastic cases, we refer the readers to the works
[
8
,
18
,
31
,
37
]
, and references therein. Moreover, the Euler equations (deterministic and stochastic) have a rich literature in the context of convex integration, we refer readers to the works
[
3
,
4
,
6
,
13
,

,

,
17
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
26
,
29
,
30
]
, and references therein.

than the moment bounds necessary at step

. It could be inferred from this that one has to bound all finite moments at every step, which could potentially blow up during iterations. Recently, the authors in
[
30
]
used Beltrami waves as building blocks analogous to the deterministic scenario shown in
[
6
]
, therefore they introduced pathwise as well as expectation estimates in their inductive scheme. In this work, we also use Beltrami waves as building blocks (see Appendix
B
) to construct the velocity perturbation. To be more precise, this concept uses the cutoff method to regulate the noise growth, which allows pathwise estimates to be introduced throughout the inductive scheme. The desired uniform estimates are derived by combining this with moment bounds. The benefit of this technique is that it only requires lower moments of the solutions and does not require higher moments, in contrast to the inductive scheme in
[
11
,
23
]
that relies solely on moment bounds. This change also aids in improving the solutions’ regularity.

a_{\zeta}
is an modification of the amplitude functions considered in
[
23
]
(see (
4.11
) and (
4.12
) below), meanwhile, in accordance with
[
6
]
, we acquire an acceptable transport error by including the solutions for transport equations into Beltrami waves. We propose a cutoff function (
3.5
) to control the growing amount of noise, since we need pathwise positive lower bounds of the solutions to the transport equations. Additionally, in Proposition
3.5
, we include pathwise estimations of the velocity field
\boldsymbol{v}_{q}
in the inductive iteration. Finally, we construct new stress
\mathring{R}_{q+1}
with the help of
\boldsymbol{v}_{q}
and
\omega_{q+1}
.

4.1.
Choice of parameters

We fix
b=5.75=\frac{23}{4}
and choose any
0<\beta<\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{2(1-2\alpha)}{21},\frac{1}{200}}}\right\}
and
0<\varepsilon<\frac{1}{198.375}-\beta
. The parameter
a\in 2^{3\mathbb{N}}
has been chosen sufficiently large such that
a^{\frac{1}{3}}\in\mathbb{N}
and
224<a^{\varepsilon}
. The choice of
a
guarantee
f(q)\in\mathbb{N}
and both the inequalities in (
3.7
) hold. The subsequent sections will make advantage of the aforementioned parameter requirements. We increase
a
in the sequel to absorb various implicit and universal constants.

4.2.
Mollification

To ensure smoothness during the construction, we substitute a mollified velocity field
\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}
for
\boldsymbol{v}_{q}
. To do this, we select a small parameter

(4.1)

\displaystyle\ell=\lambda_{q}^{-\frac{8}{5}}.

Let
\{\uppsi_{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon>0}
be a family of mollifiers on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and
\{\psi_{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon>0}
be a family of mollifiers with support in
(0,1)
. Note that we have considered one-sided mollifiers to reserve the adaptedness. Let us define a mollification of
\boldsymbol{v}_{q},\mathring{R}_{q}
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{q}
in space and time by convolution as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}:=(\boldsymbol{v}_{q}\ast_{x}\uppsi_{\ell})\ast_{t}\psi_{\ell},\;\;\;\mathring{R}_{\ell}

\displaystyle:=(\mathring{R}_{q}\ast_{x}\uppsi_{\ell})\ast_{t}\psi_{\ell},\;\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\;\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell}:=(\boldsymbol{z}_{q}\ast_{x}\uppsi_{\ell})\ast_{t}\psi_{\ell},

where

(4.2)

\displaystyle\uppsi_{\ell}(\cdot):=\ell^{-3}\uppsi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\cdot}{\ell}}}\right)\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\psi_{\ell}(\cdot):=\ell^{-1}\psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\cdot}{\ell}}}\right).

By the definition, it is immediate to see that
\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell},\mathring{R}_{\ell}
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell}
are
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
-adapted. From system (
3.4
), we find that
(\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell},\mathring{R}_{\ell})
satisfy the following system:

(4.3)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}+\nu(-\Delta)^{\alpha}\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}+\mathrm{div}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}+\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell})\otimes(\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}+\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell})}}\right)+\nabla p_{\ell}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell}&=\mathrm{div}(\mathring{R}_{\ell}+R_{com1}),\\
\mathrm{div}\;\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}&=0,\end{aligned}}}\right.

where the commutator stress

(4.4)

\displaystyle R_{com1}:=(\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}+\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell})\mathring{\otimes}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}+\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell})-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\boldsymbol{v}_{q}+\boldsymbol{z}_{q})\mathring{\otimes}(\boldsymbol{v}_{q}+\boldsymbol{z}_{q})}}\right)\ast_{x}\uppsi_{\ell}}}\right)\ast_{t}\psi_{\ell},

and

(4.5)

\displaystyle p_{\ell}:=(p_{q}\ast_{x}\uppsi_{\ell})\ast_{t}\psi_{\ell}-\frac{1}{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}+\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell}|^{2}-(|\boldsymbol{v}_{q}+\boldsymbol{z}_{q}|^{2}\ast_{x}\uppsi_{\ell})\ast_{t}\psi_{\ell}}}\right).

4.3.
Velocity perturbation

It is well known from the literature of convex integration methods that one needs to guarantee of smoothness of new velocity during the construction. A perturbation of
\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}
will thus be used to construct the new velocity field
\boldsymbol{v}_{q+1}
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{q+1}=\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}+\omega_{q+1},

where the perturbation
\omega_{q+1}
will be constructed by following the work
[
6
]
. Next, we discuss transport equations and time cutoffs which will be helpful in the sequel to construct
\omega_{q+1}
.

4.3.1.
Flow maps and cutoffs

One must estimate the transport error in order to estimate the new stress
\mathring{R}_{q+1}
, that is, the perturbation
\omega_{q+1}
is transported by the flow of the vector field
\partial_{t}+(\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}+\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell})\cdot\nabla
. The process of estimating the transport error has been discussed in
[
6
]
and
[
30
]
for deterministic and stochastic cases, respectively. As discussed in
[
6
,
30
]
, to obtain the appropriate estimate, we must substitute the nonlinear phase
\zeta\cdot\Phi(t,x)
, where
\Phi
is transported by the aforementioned described vector field, for the linear phase
\zeta\cdot x
in the definition of the Beltrami wave
\mathbb{W}_{\zeta,\lambda}
(see Appendix
B
). In comparison to the work
[
6
]
, the authors in
[
30
]
expanded the definition of
\Phi
for
\mathbb{R}
with respect to the time variable and we are also following the same.

Let us fix

(4.6)

\displaystyle m_{q}=\lambda_{q+1}^{-\frac{3}{4}}\lambda_{q}^{-\frac{3}{4}}\delta_{q+1}^{-\frac{1}{4}}\delta_{q}^{-\frac{1}{4}}.

We divide
[k,k+1]
into temporal intervals of size
m_{q}
for any integer
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, and then solve transport equations on these intervals. For
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,\lceil m_{q}^{-1}\rceil\}
, we define the adapted map
\Phi_{k,j}:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[k+(j-1)m_{q},k+(j+1)m_{q}]\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
as the
\mathbb{T}^{3}
-periodic solution of

(4.7)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial_{t}+(\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}+\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell})\cdot\nabla}}\right)\Phi_{k,j}&=0,\\
\Phi_{k,j}(k+(j-1)m_{q},x)&=x.\end{aligned}}}\right.

In Appendix
C
, we give the detailed proof of the estimates of
\Phi_{k,j}
. There are two main estimates for
\Phi_{k,j}
which are as follows:

(4.8)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[k+(j-1)m_{q},k+(j+1)m_{q}]}\|\nabla\Phi_{k,j}(t)-\mathrm{Id}\|_{C^{0}_{x}}\leq\lambda_{q}^{-\frac{8}{5}}<\!<1,

(4.9)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[k+(j-1)m_{q},k+(j+1)m_{q}]}\|\nabla\Phi_{k,j}(t)\|_{C^{0}_{x}}\leq 2.

Remark 4.1
.

To obtain the estimate (
4.8
), pointwise bounds for both
\boldsymbol{v}_{q}
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{q}
are required. For this purpose, we introduced the cutoff function
\boldsymbol{z}_{q}
(see (
3.5
)) and the inductive estimate (
3.15
). This approach differs from that in
[
23
]
, where the authors considered only moment bounds in their inductive estimates. Moreover, the approach in this article differs from that of
[
30
]
, primarily due to the following two aspects:

•

First, as noted in Remark
3.2
, we construct two smooth truncation functions,
\chi_{q}
and
\widetilde{\chi}_{q}
, which are used to define the cutoff function
\boldsymbol{z}_{q}
. This modification has a cascading effect on the estimation of various error terms, thereby leading to an improvement in the Hölder regularity of the solutions.

•

Second, unlike
[
30
]
, where the authors employed different energy functions to construct infinitely many weak solutions, our approach relies on a bifurcating induction proposition (see Proposition
3.6
) to generate infinitely many solutions.

We also let
\eta
be a non-negative bump function supported in
(-1,1)
, which is
1
on
(-\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4})
and such
that the square of the shifted bump functions

\displaystyle\eta_{j}(t)=\eta(m_{q}^{-1}t-j)

form a partition of unity

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\eta_{j}^{2}(t)=1,

for all
t\in[0,1]
. We then extend the definition of
\eta
to the whole real line
\mathbb{R}
. In particular, let
\eta^{(k)}(t)=\eta(t-k)
, then we know
\mathrm{supp}\eta^{(k)}\subset(k-1,k+1)
. Similarly, the shifted bump functions

\displaystyle\eta_{j,k}(t)=\eta(m_{q}^{-1}(t-k)-j)

form a partition of unity

(4.10)

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\eta_{j,k}^{2}(t)=1,

for all
t\in[k,k+1]
.

4.3.2.
Amplitudes

We will now start the process to construct the velocity perturbation
\omega_{q+1}
. Since Proposition
3.5
uses moment bounds of
\mathring{R}_{q}
to the iterative estimates, we must modify the amplitude functions
a_{\zeta}
such that Lemma
B.3
can be used in our context. Specifically, we first define
\varrho
as follows

(4.11)

\displaystyle\varrho(t,x)

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{\ell^{2}+|\mathring{R}_{\ell}(t,x)|^{2}}.

Then, we define the amplitude function

(4.12)

\displaystyle a_{\zeta}(t,x):=a_{q+1,j,\zeta}(t,x):=c_{\ast}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\varrho^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\eta_{j,k}(t)\cdot\Gamma_{\zeta}^{(j)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{Id}-\frac{c_{\ast}\mathring{R}_{\ell}}{\varrho}}}\right),\;\;\zeta\in\Lambda_{j}\;\;\text{and}\;\;t\in[k,k+1],

where
\Gamma_{\zeta}^{(j)}
is introduced in Appendix
B
and
\Lambda_{j}\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{3}
is finite subset. By the definition of
\varrho
, we have

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathrm{Id}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{Id}-\frac{c_{\ast}\mathring{R}_{\ell}(t,x)}{\varrho}}}\right)}}\right\|_{C^{0}_{t,x}}\leq c_{\ast}.

Consequently,
\mathrm{Id}-\frac{c_{\ast}\mathring{R}_{\ell}}{\varrho}
belongs to the domain of the function
\Gamma_{\zeta}^{(j)}
and we obtain from (
4.10
) and Lemma
B.3
that

(4.13)

\displaystyle c_{\ast}^{-1}\varrho\mathrm{Id}-\mathring{R}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\sum_{\zeta\in\Lambda_{j}}a_{\zeta}^{2}(\mathrm{Id}-\zeta\otimes\zeta),

holds pointwise. Also, it is adequate to think of index sets
\Lambda_{0}
and
\Lambda_{1}
as having 12 elements, and we write
\Lambda_{j}=\Lambda_{j\mod 2}
for
j\in\mathbb{Z}
.

4.3.3.
Construction of velocity perturbation
\omega_{q+1}

Firstly, we define the principal part
\omega_{q+1}^{(p)}
of the velocity perturbation
\omega_{q+1}
using (
4.12
). For this purpose, we make use of Beltrami wave as presented in
[
6
]
(see
[
30
]
also), which we have recalled in Appendix
B
. More precisely, for
t\in[k,k+1]
, we define

\displaystyle\omega_{\zeta}(t,x):=a_{q+1,j,\zeta}(t,x)B_{\zeta}e^{i\lambda_{q+1}\zeta\cdot\Phi_{k,j}(t,x)},

and

\displaystyle\omega_{q+1}^{(p)}(t,x)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j}\sum_{\zeta\in\Lambda_{j}}a_{q+1,j,\zeta}(t,x)B_{\zeta}e^{i\lambda_{q+1}\zeta\cdot\Phi_{k,j}(t,x)}

(4.14)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\sum_{\zeta\in\Lambda_{j}}c_{\ast}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\varrho^{\frac{1}{2}}(t)\cdot\eta_{j,k}(t)\cdot\Gamma_{\zeta}^{(j)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{Id}-c_{\ast}\frac{\mathring{R}_{\ell}(t,x)}{\varrho(t)}}}\right)\cdot B_{\zeta}e^{i\lambda_{q+1}\zeta\cdot\Phi_{k,j}(t,x)},

where
B_{\zeta}
is an appropriate vector defined in Appendix
B
. Since
a_{\zeta}
and
\Phi_{k,j}(t,x)
are
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
-adapted, we deduce that
\omega_{q+1}^{(p)}
is
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
-adapted.

Next, we define the incompressible corrector
\omega_{q+1}^{(c)}
. In order to make
\omega_{q+1}^{(p)}
perfect curl and so divergence-free, we want to add a corrector to it. The following scalar phase function is helpful in achieving this

\displaystyle\phi_{\zeta}(t,x):=e^{i\lambda_{q+1}\zeta\cdot(\Phi_{k,j}(t,x)-x)},\;\;\;t\in[k,k+1].

Next we define

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{\zeta}(x):=B_{\zeta}e^{i\lambda_{q+1}\zeta\cdot x}.

Since
\mathrm{curl}\mathbb{W}_{\zeta}=\lambda_{q+1}\mathbb{W}_{\zeta}
(see Appendix
B
), and
\phi_{\zeta}
and
a_{\zeta}
are scalar functions, we have

\displaystyle a_{\zeta}\phi_{\zeta}\mathbb{W}_{\zeta}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{q+1}}\mathrm{curl}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{\zeta}\phi_{\zeta}\mathbb{W}_{\zeta}}}\right)-\frac{1}{\lambda_{q+1}}\nabla\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{\zeta}\phi_{\zeta}}}\right)\times\mathbb{W}_{\zeta}.

We therefore define

\displaystyle\omega_{\zeta}^{(c)}(t,x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\lambda_{q+1}}\nabla\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{\zeta}\phi_{\zeta}}}\right)\times B_{\zeta}e^{i\lambda_{q+1}\zeta\cdot x}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\nabla a_{\zeta}}{\lambda_{q+1}}+ia_{\zeta}(\nabla\Phi_{k,j}(t,x)-\mathrm{Id})\zeta}}\right)\mathbb{W}_{\zeta}(\Phi_{k,j}(t,x)),\;\;\;t\in[k,k+1].

The incompressibility corrector
\omega_{q+1}^{(c)}
of perturbation is defined by

(4.15)

\displaystyle\omega_{q+1}^{(c)}(t,x)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j}\sum_{\zeta\in\Lambda_{j}}\omega_{\zeta}^{(c)}(t,x)

for all
t\in[k,k+1]
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Since the coefficients
a_{\zeta}
and
\Phi_{k,j}(t,x)
are
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
-adapted, we deduce that
\omega_{q+1}^{(c)}
is
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
-adapted.

Finally, in view of (
4.14
) and (
4.15
), the new velocity perturbation is defined as

(4.16)

\displaystyle\omega_{q+1}:=\omega_{q+1}^{(p)}+\omega_{q+1}^{(c)}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{q+1}}\sum_{j}\sum_{\zeta\in\Lambda_{j}}\mathrm{curl}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{\zeta}\phi_{\zeta}\mathbb{W}_{\zeta}}}\right),

and so clearly
\omega_{q+1}
is mean zero, divergence-free and
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
-adapted. At last, we define the new velocity field
\boldsymbol{v}_{q+1}
as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{q+1}:=\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}+\omega_{q+1}.

Thus, by the previous discussion, it is also
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
-adapted.

4.4.
New Reynolds stress
\mathring{R}_{q+1}

We have the system (
3.4
) at the level
q+1
as follows:

(4.17)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}_{q+1}+\nu(-\Delta)^{\alpha}\boldsymbol{v}_{q+1}+\mathrm{div}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\boldsymbol{v}_{q+1}+\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1})\otimes(\boldsymbol{v}_{q+1}+\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1})}}\right)-\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1}+\nabla p_{q+1}&=\mathrm{div}\mathring{R}_{q+1},\\
\mathrm{div}\;\boldsymbol{v}_{q+1}&=0.\end{aligned}}}\right.

Subtracting the system (
4.3
) from the system (
4.17
), we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}\mathring{R}_{q+1}-\nabla p_{q+1}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}_{q+1}+\nu(-\Delta)^{\alpha}\boldsymbol{v}_{q+1}+\mathrm{div}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\boldsymbol{v}_{q+1}+\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1})\otimes(\boldsymbol{v}_{q+1}+\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1})}}\right)-\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}+\omega_{q+1})+\nu(-\Delta)^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}+\omega_{q+1})+\mathrm{div}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}+\omega_{q+1}+\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1})\otimes(\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}+\omega_{q+1}+\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1})}}\right)-\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1}

\displaystyle=-\nabla p_{\ell}+\mathrm{div}(\mathring{R}_{\ell}+R_{com1})+(\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell}-\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1})+\partial_{t}\omega^{(p)}_{q+1}+\partial_{t}\omega^{(c)}_{q+1}+\nu(-\Delta)^{\alpha}\omega_{q+1}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{div}\big(\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}\otimes\omega^{(p)}_{q+1}+\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}\otimes\omega^{(c)}_{q+1}+\omega_{q+1}\otimes\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}+\omega^{(p)}_{q+1}\otimes\omega^{(p)}_{q+1}+\omega^{(p)}_{q+1}\otimes\omega^{(c)}_{q+1}+\omega^{(c)}_{q+1}\otimes\omega_{q+1}\big)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{div}\big(\boldsymbol{v}_{q+1}\otimes(\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell})+\omega_{q+1}\otimes\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell}+(\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell})\otimes\boldsymbol{v}_{q+1}+\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell}\otimes\omega_{q+1}\big)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{div}\big((\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell})\otimes\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell}+\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1}\otimes(\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell})\big)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\nu(-\Delta)^{\alpha}\omega_{q+1}}_{\mathrm{div}R_{lin}}+\underbrace{(\partial_{t}+(\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}+\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell})\cdot\nabla)\omega^{(p)}_{q+1}}_{\mathrm{div}R_{trans}}+\underbrace{(\omega_{q+1}\cdot\nabla)(\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}+\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell})}_{\mathrm{div}R_{Nash}}+\underbrace{\mathrm{div}\big(\omega_{q+1}^{(p)}\otimes\omega_{q+1}^{(p)}+\mathring{R}_{\ell}\big)}_{\mathrm{div}R_{osc}+\nabla p_{osc}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{(\partial_{t}+(\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}+\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell})\cdot\nabla)\omega^{(c)}_{q+1}+\mathrm{div}(\omega_{q+1}^{(c)}\otimes\omega_{q+1}+\omega_{q+1}^{(p)}\otimes\omega_{q+1}^{(c)})}_{\mathrm{div}R_{corr}+\nabla p_{corr}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\mathrm{div}\big(\boldsymbol{v}_{q+1}\otimes(\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell})+(\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell})\otimes\boldsymbol{v}_{q+1}+\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1}\otimes\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell}\otimes\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell}\big)-(\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell})}_{\mathrm{div}R_{com2}+\nabla p_{com2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{div}R_{com1}-\nabla p_{\ell}.

Here
R_{com1}
and
p_{\ell}
are defined in (
4.4
) and (
4.5
), respectively, and by making use of the operator
\mathcal{R}
discussed in Subsection
2.2
, we define

\displaystyle R_{lin}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{R}\bigg[\nu(-\Delta)^{\alpha}\omega_{q+1}\bigg],

\displaystyle R_{trans}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{R}\bigg[(\partial_{t}+(\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}+\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell})\cdot\nabla)\omega^{(p)}_{q+1}\bigg],

\displaystyle R_{Nash}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(\omega_{q+1}\cdot\nabla)(\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}+\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell})}}\right],

\displaystyle R_{corr}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{R}\bigg[(\partial_{t}+(\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}+\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell})\cdot\nabla)\omega^{(c)}_{q+1}\bigg]+\omega_{q+1}^{(c)}\mathring{\otimes}\omega_{q+1}+\omega_{q+1}^{(p)}\mathring{\otimes}\omega_{q+1}^{(c)},

\displaystyle R_{com2}

\displaystyle:=\boldsymbol{v}_{q+1}\mathring{\otimes}(\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell})+(\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell})\mathring{\otimes}\boldsymbol{v}_{q+1}+\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1}\mathring{\otimes}\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell}\mathring{\otimes}\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell}-\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell}),

\displaystyle p_{corr}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\omega_{q+1}^{(p)}\cdot\omega_{q+1}^{(c)}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\omega_{q+1}^{(c)}}}\right|^{2}}}\right),

\displaystyle p_{com2}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{3}\big(\boldsymbol{v}_{q+1}\cdot(\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell})+(\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{q+1}+|\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1}|^{2}-|\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell}|^{2})\big).

In order to define
R_{osc}
and
p_{osc}
, we first pay attention that for
j,j^{\prime}
such that
|j-j^{\prime}|\geq 2
, we have
\eta_{j,k}(t)\eta_{j^{\prime},k}(t)=0
. Second, we have
\Lambda_{j}\cap\Lambda_{j^{\prime}}=\varnothing
for
|j-j^{\prime}|=1
, and by Lemma
B.1
we have

\mathrm{div}(\mathbb{W}_{\zeta}\otimes\mathbb{W}_{\zeta^{\prime}}+\mathbb{W}_{\zeta^{\prime}}\otimes\mathbb{W}_{\zeta})=\nabla(\mathbb{W}_{\zeta}\cdot\mathbb{W}_{\zeta^{\prime}})

(using
(A\cdot\nabla)B+(B\cdot\nabla)A=\nabla(A\cdot B)-A\times\nabla\times B-B\times\nabla\times A
). Therefore, in view of Lemma
B.3
and (
4.13
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}\big(\omega_{q+1}^{(p)}\otimes\omega_{q+1}^{(p)}+\mathring{R}_{\ell}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{div}\bigg(\sum_{j,j^{\prime},\zeta,\zeta^{\prime}}\omega_{\zeta}\otimes\omega_{\zeta^{\prime}}+\mathring{R}_{\ell}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{div}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{j,\zeta}c_{\ast}^{-1}\varrho\cdot\eta_{j,k}^{2}\{\Gamma_{\zeta}^{(j)}(\mathrm{Id}-c_{\ast}\varrho^{-1}\mathring{R}_{\ell})\}^{2}B_{\zeta}\otimes B_{-\zeta}+\mathring{R}_{\ell}}}\right)+\mathrm{div}\bigg(\sum_{j,j^{\prime},\zeta+\zeta^{\prime}\neq 0}a_{\zeta}a_{\zeta^{\prime}}\mathbb{W}_{\zeta}(\Phi_{k,j})\otimes\mathbb{W}_{\zeta^{\prime}}(\Phi_{k,j^{\prime}})\bigg)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{div}\bigg(c_{\ast}^{-1}\varrho(\mathrm{Id}-c_{\ast}\varrho^{-1}\mathring{R}_{\ell})+\mathring{R}_{\ell}\bigg)+\mathrm{div}\bigg(\sum_{j,j^{\prime},\zeta+\zeta^{\prime}\neq 0}a_{\zeta}a_{\zeta^{\prime}}\phi_{\zeta}\phi_{\zeta^{\prime}}\mathbb{W}_{\zeta}\otimes\mathbb{W}_{\zeta^{\prime}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\nabla(c_{\ast}^{-1}\varrho)+\sum_{j,j^{\prime},\zeta+\zeta^{\prime}\neq 0}\mathbb{W}_{\zeta}\otimes\mathbb{W}_{\zeta^{\prime}}\nabla\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{\zeta}a_{\zeta^{\prime}}\phi_{\zeta}\phi_{\zeta^{\prime}}}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,j^{\prime},\zeta+\zeta^{\prime}\neq 0}a_{\zeta}a_{\zeta^{\prime}}\phi_{\zeta}\phi_{\zeta^{\prime}}\nabla(\mathbb{W}_{\zeta}\cdot\mathbb{W}_{\zeta^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\nabla(c_{\ast}^{-1}\varrho)+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{j,j^{\prime},\zeta+\zeta^{\prime}\neq 0}a_{\zeta}a_{\zeta^{\prime}}\phi_{\zeta}\phi_{\zeta^{\prime}}(\mathbb{W}_{\zeta}\cdot\mathbb{W}_{\zeta^{\prime}})}}\right)+\sum_{j,j^{\prime},\zeta+\zeta^{\prime}\neq 0}\biggl\{\mathbb{W}_{\zeta}\otimes\mathbb{W}_{\zeta^{\prime}}-\frac{\mathbb{W}_{\zeta}\cdot\mathbb{W}_{\zeta^{\prime}}}{2}\mathrm{Id}\biggr\}\nabla\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{\zeta}a_{\zeta^{\prime}}\phi_{\zeta}\phi_{\zeta^{\prime}}}}\right).

Therefore

\displaystyle R_{osc}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j,j^{\prime},\zeta+\zeta^{\prime}\neq 0}\mathcal{R}\bigg[\biggl\{\mathbb{W}_{\zeta}\otimes\mathbb{W}_{\zeta^{\prime}}-\frac{\mathbb{W}_{\zeta}\cdot\mathbb{W}_{\zeta^{\prime}}}{2}\mathrm{Id}\biggr\}\nabla\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{\zeta}a_{\zeta^{\prime}}\phi_{\zeta}\phi_{\zeta^{\prime}}}}\right)\bigg],

\displaystyle p_{osc}

\displaystyle:=c_{\ast}^{-1}\varrho+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,j^{\prime},\zeta+\zeta^{\prime}\neq 0}a_{\zeta}a_{\zeta^{\prime}}\phi_{\zeta}\phi_{\zeta^{\prime}}(\mathbb{W}_{\zeta}\cdot\mathbb{W}_{\zeta^{\prime}}).

Finally, we have

(4.18)

\displaystyle\mathring{R}_{q+1}=R_{lin}+R_{trans}+R_{Nash}+R_{osc}+R_{Corr}+R_{com1}+R_{com2},

and

\displaystyle p_{q+1}=p_{\ell}-p_{osc}-p_{corr}-p_{com2}.

Note that
\omega_{q+1}^{(p)}
,
\omega_{q+1}^{(c)}
,
\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}
,
\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell}
,
\boldsymbol{v}_{q+1}
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{q+1}
are
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
-adapted, therefore
R_{q+1}
and

Appendix B
Beltrami waves

In this section, we recall the Beltrami waves from
[
6
, Section 5.4]
which is adapted to the convex integration technique in Proposition
3.5
. Note that the construction discussed below is independent of sample points
\omega
, that is, it is purely deterministic. First of all we recall the definition of Beltrami waves.

Given
\zeta\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{3}
, let
A_{\zeta}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{3}
satisfy
A_{\zeta}\cdot\zeta=0
and
A_{-\zeta}=A_{\zeta}
. Furthermore, we also define a complex vector

(B.1)

\displaystyle B_{\zeta}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(A_{\zeta}+i\zeta\times A_{\zeta})\in\mathbb{C}^{3}.

It is immediate from the definition of
B_{\zeta}
that it satisfies

\displaystyle|B_{\zeta}|=1,\;\;B_{\zeta}\cdot\zeta=0,\;\;i\zeta\times B_{\zeta}=B_{\zeta},\;\;B_{-\zeta}=\overline{B_{\zeta}}.

With the above preparation, we infer that for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}
, such that
\lambda\zeta\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
, the function

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{\zeta}:=\mathbb{W}_{\zeta,\lambda}(x):=B_{\zeta}e^{i\lambda\zeta\cdot x},

is
\mathbb{T}^{3}
-periodic, divergence-free, and is an eigenfunction of the curl operator with eigenvalue
\lambda
. That is,
\mathbb{W}_{\zeta}
is a complex Beltrami plane wave. Next, we discuss a useful property of linear combinations of complex Beltrami plane waves.

Lemma B.1

(
[
6
, Proposition 5.5]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a given finite subset of
\mathbb{S}^{2}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{3}
such that
-\Lambda=\Lambda
, and let
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}
be such that
\lambda\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}
. Then for any choice of coefficients
a_{\zeta}\in\mathbb{C}
with
\overline{a_{\zeta}}=a_{-\zeta}
the vector field

(B.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}(x)=\sum_{\zeta\in\Lambda}a_{\zeta}B_{\zeta}e^{i\lambda\zeta\cdot x},

is a real-valued, divergence-free Beltrami vector field
\mathrm{curl}\mathbb{W}=\lambda\mathbb{W}
, and satisfies stationary Euler equations

(B.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}(\mathbb{W}\otimes\mathbb{W})=\nabla\frac{|\mathbb{W}|^{2}}{2}.

Furthermore, we also have

(B.4)

\displaystyle\langle\mathbb{W}\otimes\mathbb{W}\rangle:=\fint_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\mathbb{W}(x)\otimes\mathbb{W}(x)\;\/\mathrm{d}\/x=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\zeta\in\Lambda}|a_{\zeta}|^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{Id}-\zeta\otimes\zeta}}\right).

Remark B.2
.

The key point of the construction is that the abundance of Beltrami flows allows to find several flows
v
such that

\displaystyle\langle v\otimes v\rangle(t):=\fint_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}(v\otimes v)(t,x)dx=R,

for some prescribed symmetric matrix
R
. It is true that we must choose these flows carefully such that they rely smoothly on the matrix
R
, at least when
R
belongs to a neighborhood of the identity matrix. In view of (
B.4
), such selection is possible (see Lemma
B.3
below).

The following lemma can be found in
[
6
]
(see
[
6
, Proposition 5.6]
).

Lemma B.3

(Geometric Lemma)
.

There exists a constant (sufficient small)

with the following property. Let
B_{c_{\ast}}(\mathrm{Id})
denote the closed ball of symmetric

matrices, centered at
\mathrm{Id}
, of radius

. Then, there exists pairwise disjoint subsets

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\alpha}\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{3},\;\;\;\alpha\in\{0,1\},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\zeta}^{(\alpha)}\in C^{\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{c_{\ast}}(\mathrm{Id})}}\right),\;\;\;\;\alpha\in\{0,1\},\zeta\in\Lambda_{\alpha},

such that the following hold:

For every
\zeta\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
, we have
-\zeta\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
and
\Gamma_{\zeta}^{(\alpha)}=\Gamma_{-\zeta}^{(\alpha)}
. For each
R\in B_{c_{\ast}}(\mathrm{Id})

(B.5)

\displaystyle R=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\zeta\in\Lambda_{\alpha}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Gamma_{\zeta}^{(\alpha)}(R)}}\right]^{2}(\mathrm{Id}-\zeta\otimes\zeta).

It has been noticed (see

Let us first introduce the following lemma which makes rigorous the fact that

Lemma D.1

(Stationary phase Lemma)
.

Given
\zeta\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{3}
, let
\lambda\zeta\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Assume that
a\in C^{m,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
and
\Phi\in C^{m,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{3};\mathbb{R}^{3})
are smooth functions such that the phase function
\Phi
obeys

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C}\leq|\nabla\Phi|\leq C,

on
\mathbb{T}^{3}
, for some constant
C\geq 1
. Then, with the inverse divergence operator
\mathcal{R}
defined in Subsection
2.2
, we have for any
m\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathcal{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a(x)e^{i\lambda\zeta\cdot\Phi(x)}}}\right)}}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\|a\|_{C^{0}}}{\lambda^{1-\alpha}}+\frac{\|a\|_{C^{m,\alpha}}+\|a\|_{C^{0}}\|\nabla\Phi\|_{C^{m,\alpha}}}{\lambda^{m-\alpha}},

where the implicit constant depends on
C
,
\alpha
and
m
(in particular, not on the frequency
\lambda
).
