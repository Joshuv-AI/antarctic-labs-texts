Efficient Calculations of the Mode-Resolved ab-initio Thermal Conductivity in Nanostructures
Giuseppe Romano

Abstract
First-principles calculations of thermal transport in homogeneous materials have reached remarkable predicting power. Modeling deterministically phonon transport in nanostructures, however, poses novel challenges; notably, it entails solving as many algebraic equations as the number of combinations of wave vectors in the discretized Brillouen and polarizations. We show that, within the relaxation time approximation of the Boltzmann transport equation (BTE), this issue is resolved by interpolating the phonon distributions in the vectorial phonon mean free paths (MFP) space. The coupling between structure and mode-resolved heat transport is investigated in terms of angular-resolved bulk thermal conductivity and phonon suppression function, the latter being associated primarily to the material’s geometry. Our method, termed the anisotropic MFP-BTE (aMFP-BTE), allows for fast and accurate thermal conductivity calculations in nanomaterials regardless of the number of phonon branches and wave vectors. Furthermore, it naturally blends with first-principles thermal transport calculations, therefore allowing for multiscale, parameter-free simulations. We apply the aMFP-BTE to compute the mode-resolved effective thermal conductivity of porous Si membranes, achieving up to 50x speed with respect to the case with no interpolation. The proposed approach unlocks the engineering of novel nanostructures, with applications to thermoelectrics and heat management.



I
Introduction

Tuning thermal transport via nanostructuring is beneficial to several applications, including thermal energy harvesting
Vineis et al. 2010
;
Lorenzi et al. 2018
;
Lee et al. 2010
, heat management
Kim et al. 2007
;
Moore and Shi 2014
and routing
Anufriev et al. 2017
;
Zhang et al. 2013
. The key advantage of nanomaterials is the possibility of exploiting ballistic phonon effects, in contrast to macroscopic systems, where transport is mostly diffusive
Chen 2005
;
Murthy et al. 2005
. Modeling this transport regime, however, is challenging because it entails solving, at the very least, the Boltzmann Transport Equation (BTE), a much more complicated model than the standard Fourier equation
Ziman 2001
. The steady-state, mode-resolved BTE, in fact, requires tracking phonons in both real and momentum space, and, when solved deterministically, amounts to inverting as many matrices as the number of phonon modes in our system
Murthy and Mathur 1998
, which can easily reach hundreds of thousands in realistic materials. To overcome this limitation, several approaches have been proposed; for example in the frequency-dependent BTE (FD-BTE), the BZ is made isotropic from a slice taken along a high-symmetry path, and then sampled in frequency space
Minnich et al. 2011
;
Singh et al. 2011
;
Loy et al. 2013
. In a previous work, we developed a formalism, called the MFP-BTE, where the sampling is carried out in the MFP-space
Romano and Grossman 2015
;
Romano and Kolpak 2017
. Recently, a deterministic approach based on the self-adjoint version of the BTE has been proposed
Harter et al. 2019
. While these methods initially considered isotropic dispersions, they can be in principle extended to arbitrary anisotropic dispersions, and unnravel the rich physics of the coupling between the crystal structure and nanoscale geometries, as captured by several MonteCarlo simulations
Vermeersch et al. 2016
;
Wu et al. 2016
;
Mei et al. 2014
;
Landon and Hadjiconstantinou 2014
. Furthermore, these approaches, unlike single-MFP methods, have allowed for incorporating
first-principle
data
Romano and Grossman 2014
;
Hao et al. 2009
;
Romano et al. 2012
. Building upon the MFP-BTE formalism, we propose a technique to solve efficiently the BTE over the whole BZ, basing on the fact that, in the relaxation-time-approximation (RTA), the nonequilibrium phonon populations are a smooth function of the their vectorial MFPs; we exploit this property by interpolating them onto a regular spherical grid, reducing dramatically computing times while not compromising on the accuracy. Crucially, our method computes heat transport in constant time with respect to the number of phonon branches and wave vectors, opening up the possibility of simulating complex unit-cell materials, such as Bi
2
Te
3
and SnSe- based nanostructures. In deriving our approach, we also introduce the spherical and polar representations of the thermal conductivity and
phonon suppression function
, both pivotal to unraveling the coupling between the geometry and the underlying material. As an example, we first apply our method to Si membrane with infinite thickness, obtaining a speed up of about 50x with respect to the case with no interpolation. A potential speed up of about 3 is found for a three-dimensioanal membrane, which is bound to increase for more complex materials. The proposed method in practice extends the MFP-BTE to anistropic materials, thus we call it the
anisotropic
MFP-BTE, or aMFP-BTE. Taken together, we expect our approach to significantly expands the engineering space of materials for thermoelectrics and heat management applications, while keeping the computational effort amenable to most laptops.

II
The Mode-Resolved Boltzmann transport equation

In absence of perturbation, e.g. an applied temperature gradient, the phonon populations are in equilibrium with temperature
T_{0}
and are given by the Bose-Einstein distribution

\bar{n}_{p}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{1}{e^{\frac{\hbar\omega_{p}(\mathbf{q})}{k_{B}T_{0}}}-1},

(1)

where

\mathbf{q}
the wave vector. When a temperature gradient is applied, the phonon distributions depart from the equilibrium ones,

where
A_{pp^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
is the scattering operator and
\mathbf{v}_{p}(\mathbf{q})
is the group velocity. Equation

N_{p}

N_{q}
control volumes
\Delta\mathbf{q}_{k}

\Delta\mathbf{q}_{k}

-\mathbf{v}_{\mu}\cdot\nabla\Delta n_{\mu}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{\mu^{\prime}}A_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}(\mathbf{r})\Delta n_{\mu^{\prime}}(\mathbf{r}),

(3)

where

W_{\mu\nu}\approx C_{\nu}\tau_{\nu}^{-1}\left(\delta_{\mu\nu}\Delta T_{\nu}-\Delta T^{L}\right)

\Delta T^{L}
is a local pseudotemperature. This quantity is computed by setting
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}=0

W_{\mu\nu}=C_{\nu}/\tau_{\nu}\left(\delta_{\mu\nu}-a_{\nu}\right)
, where

\mathbf{F}_{\mu}\cdot\nabla\Delta T_{\mu}^{(n)}+\Delta T_{\mu}^{(n)}=\sum_{\nu}a_{\nu}\Delta T_{\nu}^{(n-1)},

(5)

where
\mathbf{F}_{\mu}=\tau_{\mu}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}
is the vectorial MFP. Equation

given by the diffusive equation
Romano and Grossman 2015
.

We consider periodic materials along both
x
- and
y
- axis, whereas heat flux is enforced by applying difference of temperature
\Delta T_{\mathrm{ext}}=\Delta T_{\mu}^{\mathrm{R}}-\Delta T_{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}}
along the
x
-axis. With

being the distance between the right (R) and left (L) contact, the effective thermal conductivity is computed by

\kappa^{\mathrm{eff}}=-\frac{L}{\Delta T_{\mathrm{ext}}A_{\mathrm{R}}}\int_{\mathrm{R}}\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}dS,

(6)

where
A_{\mathrm{R}}
is the area of the right contact. Along the wall we apply the total diffuse scattering boundary condition, e.g. phonons outgoing from a pore are fixed to the boundary temperature
Landon and Hadjiconstantinou 2014

T_{B}=\sum_{\mu}\Delta T_{\mu}\mathrm{ReLu}(\mathbf{S}_{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}})\left[\sum_{\nu}\mathrm{ReLu}(\mathbf{S}_{\nu}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}})\right]^{-1},

(7)

where
\mathrm{ReLu}(x)
is

\mathrm{ReLu}(x)=\begin{cases}x,&\text{if }x>0\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(8)

It’s straightforward to show that Eq.

\sum_{\mu}\mathbf{J}_{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}=0
. Equation

obtained from first-principles calculations. In practice, however, solving directly Eq.

N_{p}N_{q}
), which can easily lead to prohibitive computational load. To overcome this issue, we present the
anisotropic
MFP-BTE (aMFP-BTE), described in the next section.

III
The anisotropic MFP-BTE

At each iteration, the mode-resolved RTA-BTE, encoded in Eq.
5

\mathbf{F}_{\mu}

VI
Conclusion

We introduce the aMFP-BTE, a method that reduces dramatically the computational demand of thermal conductivity calculations in nanostructures, leveraging interpolation of phonon distributions in the vectorial MFP space. The application of the aMFP-BTE to Si porous membranes with infinite thickness reveals a speed-up of about 50x. Taking into account the whole BZ, our method is able to elucidate on the interplay between the anisotropy of the bulk thermal conductivity and the material’s geometry, highlighting novel mechanisms for thermal transport tuning. The aMFP-BTE solves for thermal transport in constant time with respect to the number of phonon branches and wave vectors, opening up the possibility of simulating phonon size effects in promising thermoelectric materials, such as SnSe and Bi
2
Te
3
. The code used for this work will be released with the package OpenBTE
Romano 2019
.

Finite-Volume Formulation of the BTE

We discretize Eqs.
2
-
13
using the finite-volume techniques. For simplicity, we label the phonon temperatures with
\mu
, but the same procedure holds for both the MR-BTE and the aMFP-BTE. The mesh, generated by GMSH
Geuzaine and Remacle 2009
, is unstructured, with elements at the border of domain matching their periodic counterparts. The BTE for a generic
\mathbf{F}_{\mu}
reads

\mathbf{F}_{\mu}\cdot\nabla\Delta T_{\mu}(\mathbf{r})+\Delta T_{\mu}(\mathbf{r})=\Delta T^{L}(\mathbf{r}),

(20)

which, after integrating both side over the control volume
V_{c}
, becomes

\frac{1}{V_{c}}\int_{\partial V_{c}}\Delta T_{\mu}\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}dS+\Delta T_{c\mu}=\Delta T^{L}_{c},

(21)

where we uses Gauss’ theorem. The RHS of Eq.
21
is simply given by
\Delta T^{L}_{c}=\sum_{\nu}a_{\nu}\Delta T_{c\nu}
. The term associated to the surface integral is discretized according to the upwind method
Murthy and Mathur 1998
;
Romano and Di Carlo 2011
, yielding

\frac{1}{V_{c}}\int_{\partial V_{c}}\Delta T\mathbf{F}_{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}dS=I_{c\mu}^{\mathrm{in}}+I_{c\mu}^{\mathrm{out}}+I_{c\mu}^{\mathrm{B}}+I_{c\mu}^{\mathrm{P}}.

(22)

The contribution
I_{c\mu}^{\mathrm{in}}
accounts for all the flux incoming to a given element, and is given by

I_{c\mu}^{\mathrm{in}}=-\sum_{c^{\prime}}\mathrm{ReLu}(-\mathbf{F}_{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{cc^{\prime}})\Delta T_{c^{\prime}\mu},

(23)

where

\mathbf{R}_{cc^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{V_{c}}A_{cc^{\prime}}\mathbf{\hat{n}}_{cc^{\prime}}^{\prime},&\text{if }c\text{ and }c^{\prime}\text{ are neighbors}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(24)

The terms
\mathbf{\hat{n}}_{cc^{\prime}}
and
A_{cc^{\prime}}
are the normal (pointing toward the volume
c^{\prime}
) and the area of the side between the volume
c
and
c^{\prime}
. The ReLu function filters only the incoming contributions to the element
c
, while
\mathbf{R}_{cc^{\prime}}
represents the connections between the volumes.
The contribution
I_{c\mu}^{\mathrm{out}}
is the flux leaving the volume, and reads

I_{c\mu}^{\mathrm{out}}=\sum_{c^{\prime\prime}}\mathrm{ReLu}(\mathbf{F}_{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{cc^{\prime\prime}})\Delta T_{c^{\prime\prime}}.

(25)

In the upwind scheme, this term amounts to the diagonal of the stiffness matrix. The third term of Eq.
22
arises from the flux bouncing back from an adiabatic boundary, and is given by

I_{c\mu}^{\mathrm{B}}=-\sum_{s}\mathrm{ReLu}(-\mathbf{F}_{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}_{s})g_{sc}\Delta T_{s}.

(26)

where
\mathbf{\hat{n}_{s}}
is the normal of the surface pointing outward with respect to the computational domain, and
T_{s}
is the boundary temperature, computed with Eq.
7
. The term
g_{sc}
is

g_{sc}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{V_{c}}A_{s},&\text{if }s\text{ is a side of }c\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(27)

where
A_{s}
is the area of the side
s
. Using Eq.
7
, we can rewrite the boundary contribution as
I_{c\mu}^{\mathrm{B}}=\sum_{\nu}H_{\mu\nu c}\Delta T_{c\nu}
, where

H_{c\mu\nu}=-\sum_{s}\mathrm{ReLu}(-\mathbf{F}_{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}_{s})g_{sc}\frac{\mathrm{ReLu}(\mathbf{S}_{\nu}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}_{s})}{\sum_{k}\mathrm{ReLu}(\mathbf{S}_{k}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}_{s})}.

(28)

The fourth and last contribution is due to the periodic boundary conditions,

I_{c\nu}^{\mathrm{P}}=-\sum_{c^{\prime}}\mathrm{ReLu}(-\mathbf{F}_{\nu}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{cc^{\prime}}^{P})\Delta T_{\mathrm{ext}}

(29)

where

\mathbf{R}_{cc^{\prime}}^{P}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{V_{c}}A_{cc^{\prime}}\mathbf{\hat{n}}_{cc^{\prime}},&\text{if }c\text{ and }c^{\prime}\text{ are periodic}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(30)

Putting these contributions together, along with Eq.
20
, we obtain the iterative linear system

\sum_{c^{\prime}}A_{cc^{\prime}\mu}\Delta T_{c^{\prime}\mu}^{(n)}=\sum_{\nu}(a_{\nu}+H_{c\mu\nu})\Delta T_{c\nu}^{(n-1)}+I_{c\mu}^{P}

(31)

where

\displaystyle A_{cc^{\prime}\mu}

\displaystyle=\delta_{cc^{\prime}}+\sum_{c^{\prime\prime}}\mathrm{ReLu}(\mathbf{F}_{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{cc^{\prime\prime}})\delta_{cc^{\prime}}-

(32)

\displaystyle-\mathrm{ReLu}(-\mathbf{F}_{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{cc^{\prime\prime}})\delta_{c^{\prime}c^{\prime\prime}}.

Finally, we can write Eq.
31
in vector notation

\mathbf{A}_{\mu}\mathbf{\Delta T_{\mu}}^{(n)}=\sum_{\nu}\mathbf{S}_{\mu\nu}\mathbf{\Delta T}_{\nu}^{(n-1)}+\mathbf{I}_{\mu},

(33)

where
S_{c\mu\nu}=\alpha_{\nu}+H_{c\mu\nu}
