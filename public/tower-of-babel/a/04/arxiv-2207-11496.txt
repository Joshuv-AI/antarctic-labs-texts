A p p -centered coloring for the grid using O ⁡ ( p ) O(p) colors
Mathew Francis, Drimit Pattanayak

Abstract
A p p -centered coloring of a graph G G , where p p is a positive integer, is a coloring of the vertices of G G in such a way that every connected subgraph of G G either contains a vertex with a unique color or contains more than p p different colors. We give an explicit construction of a p p -centered coloring using O ⁡ ( p ) O(p) colors for the planar grid.

Introduction

We denote the vertex set and edge set of a graph
G
by
V(G)
and
E(G)
respectively.
For a graph
G
and a set
S\subseteq V(G)
we denote by
G[S]
the subgraph induced in
G
by
S
, i.e.
V(G[S])=S
and
E(G[S])=\{uv:u,v\in S,\,\,uv\in E(G)\}
.
Please refer to
[
3
]
for any term that is not defined here.

For a graph
G
, a
coloring
of the vertices of
G
is a mapping
\phi:V(G)\rightarrow Q
, where
Q
is any set. Given such a coloring
\phi
, we say that the set
Q
is the set of
colors
used by
\phi
and that a vertex
u\in V(G)
has the color
\phi(u)
. Given a graph
G
and a positive integer
p
, a
p
-centered coloring
of
G
is a coloring of the vertices of
G
using a set of colors
Q
such that for every connected subgraph
H
of
G
, either there exists
i\in Q
such that
|\phi^{-1}(i)\cap V(H)|=1
or
|\phi(V(H))|>p
, or in other words, every connected subgraph of
G
either contains a vertex with a unique color or contains more than
p
colors.

The notion of
p
-centered colorings of graphs was introduced by Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez
[
5
]
in the course of their development of the theory of “structurally sparse” graph classes. In 2004, DeVos et al.
[
2
]
showed that every proper minor closed class of graphs has a
low treewidth coloring
: there exists a function
f:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
such that the vertex set of any graph in the class can be partitioned into
f(p)
parts in such a way that for every
i\leq p
, the union of any
i
parts induces a subgraph of treewidth at most
i-1
. Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez
[
5
]
introduced the
treedepth
of a graph and showed that it is exactly the minimum number of colors needed in a
centered coloring
of the graph: a coloring of the vertices such that every connected subgraph contains a vertex having a unique color (this parameter also turns out to be equal to some other graph parameters that were known in the literature; see
[
5
]
for details). It is not difficult to see that a
p
-centered coloring of a graph
G
is a coloring of
V(G)
such that for every
i\leq p
, the union of
i
color classes induces a subgraph of
G
whose each connected component has a centered coloring using at most
i
colors. Thus, a
p
-centered coloring can be said to be a “low treedepth coloring”. Since the treedepth of a graph is always at least one more than its treewidth
[
1
]
(in fact, one more than its pathwidth) and the treewidth of any graph is equal to the maximum of the treewidth of its connected components, low treedepth colorings are a generalization of low treewidth colorings. The following generalization of the result of DeVos et al. was shown in
[
5
]
: for every proper minor closed class of graphs, there exists a function
f:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
such that for every integer
p\geq 1
, every graph in the class has a
p
-centered coloring using at most
f(p)
colors. In a landmark paper, Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez
[
6
]
improved this further and showed that the classes of graphs having low treedepth colorings, the classes of graphs having low treewidth colorings, the classes of graphs for which there exists a function
f:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
such that every graph in the class has a
p
-centered coloring using at most
f(p)
colors for every
p\geq 1
, are all the same and characterized these classes as the classes of graphs with
bounded expansion
(see
[
6
]
for details).

It was first shown by Pilipczuk and Siebertz
[
7
]
that for every proper minor closed family of graphs, there exists a
polynomial
function
f:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
such that for every positive integer
p
, every graph in the class has a
p
-centered coloring using at most
f(p)
colors — or in other words, proper minor closed families of graphs “admit polynomial centered colorings”.
Dębski, Felsner, Micek and Schröder
[
4
]
combined different techniques to obtain several path-breaking results on
p
-centered colorings, including substantial improvements to and tightening of some bounds in
[
7
]
. Perhaps the most surprising result in their work is their proof using entropy compression that graphs of bounded degree have
p
-centered colorings using just
O(p)
colors: they showed that for any positive integer
p
, every graph
G
having maximum degree
\Delta
has a
p
-centered coloring using
O(\Delta^{2-\frac{1}{p}}p)
colors. However, their technique does not provide an explicit construction of a
p
-centered coloring using
O(p)
colors for graphs of bounded maximum degree, and they remark that an explicit construction of such a coloring is not known even for the planar grid. In this paper, we give a simple and direct construction of a
p
-centered coloring of the planar grid using
O(p)
colors.

1.1
Definitions and Notation

Given a graph
G
and a coloring
\phi
of it, a “violator” in
G
with respect to
\phi
is a connected subgraph that contains at most
p
colors and also does not contain any vertex with a unique color. Thus, a coloring of a graph is a
p
-centered coloring for it if and only if there is no violator in the graph with respect to that coloring.

For two positive integers
a
and
b
, we define
\mathrm{mod}(a,b)=a-b\left\lfloor\frac{a}{b}\right\rfloor
. Also, we write
b\mid a
to mean “
b
divides
a
”, or in other words
\mathrm{mod}(a,b)=0
, and we write
b\nmid a
to mean “
b
does not divide
a
”. For two integers
a
and
b
such that
a\leq b
, we denote by
[a,b]
the set
\{n\in\mathbb{N}\colon a\leq n\leq b\}
.
We abbreviate
\log_{2}
as
\lg
.

For the purposes of this paper, we shall use the term
grid
to denote the (infinite) graph
G
having
V(G)=\mathbb{N}^{2}
and
E(G)=\{(i,j)(i^{\prime},j^{\prime}):i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
and
|i-i^{\prime}|+|j-j^{\prime}|=1\}
. We shall use
G
to denote the grid graph. As planar grids of all sizes occur as subgraphs of
G
, it suffices to demonstrate a
p
-centered coloring for
G
using
O(p)
colors.

For a vertex
(x,y)\in V(G)
, we define
\pi_{x}(x,y)=x
and
\pi_{y}(x,y)=y
.
A set
S\subseteq V(G)
is said to be
row contiguous
if
\pi_{x}(S)=[\min\pi_{x}(S),\max\pi_{x}(S)]
and
column contiguous
if
\pi_{y}(S)=[\min\pi_{y}(S),\max\pi_{y}(S)]
.
In other words, a set
S
is row contiguous if and only if for
x,a,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
x<a<x^{\prime}
, we have
x,x^{\prime}\in\pi_{x}(S)\Rightarrow a\in\pi_{x}(S)
, and a set
S
is column contiguous if and only if for
y,b,y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
y<b<y^{\prime}
, we have
y,y^{\prime}\in\pi_{y}(S)\Rightarrow b\in\pi_{y}(S)
.
We say that a row contiguous set
S

spans
|\pi_{x}(S)|
rows
and that a column contiguous set
S

spans
|\pi_{y}(S)|
columns
. Note that if
H
is any connected subgraph of
G
, then
V(H)
is both row and column contiguous.

2
The coloring

Our proof strategy is more or less as follows:
we first show a coloring of the grid in which every violator is
large
—i.e. every violator spans either “many” rows or “many” columns, and
then show another coloring of the grid, in which every violator is
small
, or in other words, no violator spans “many” rows or columns.
Our final
p
-centered coloring of the grid is constructed by “mixing” or “interleaving” these two colorings in a special way. The accurate meanings of the words in quotes shall become clear when we give the formal definitions of the colorings.

We show the construction for the case when
p
is a power of 2 that is greater than 1, or in other words,
p=2^{i}
, where
i>0
.

2.1
A coloring in which every violator is large

We describe a coloring
\mu
of the grid
G
that contains no “small” violators; more accurately, the coloring will have the property that any violator in
G
with respect to this coloring spans either more than
4p
rows or more than
4p
columns. We actually show a stronger property: any row and column contiguous subset of
V(G)
contains a uniquely colored vertex or spans either more than
4p
rows or more than
4p
columns.

Define the function
f:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
as
n\mapsto\max\{i\in[0,\lg(4p)]:2^{i}\mid n\}
.

We further define
\alpha:\mathbb{N}^{2}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
,
l:\mathbb{N}^{2}\rightarrow[0,\lg(4p)]
, and
\rho:\mathbb{N}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
as follows.
Let
(x,y)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
.

If
f(x)\geq f(y)
, then we define
\alpha(x,y)=0
,
l(x,y)=f(x)
, and
\rho(x,y)=\mathrm{mod}(y,2^{f(x)+1})
.

If
f(x)<f(y)
, then we define
\alpha(x,y)=1
,
l(x,y)=f(y)
, and
\rho(x,y)=\mathrm{mod}(x,2^{f(y)+1})
.

Notice that for any
(x,y)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
, we have
l(x,y)=\max\{f(x),f(y)\}
.

Finally, we define
\mu:\mathbb{N}^{2}\rightarrow\{0,1\}\times[0,\lg(4p)]\times\mathbb{N}
as
(x,y)\mapsto(\alpha(x,y),l(x,y),\rho(x,y))
.
We consider
\mu
to be a coloring of
V(G)
.

Observe that for any
(x,y)\in V(G)
, we have
\rho(x,y)\in[0,2^{l(x,y)+1}-1]
. This immediately implies that
|\mu(V(G))|\leq 2\sum_{i=0}^{\lg(4p)}2^{i+1}\leq 32p
.

Lemma 1

Let
S\subseteq V(G)
. If
S
is both row and column contiguous, and does not contain any uniquely colored vertex with respect to the coloring
\mu
of
G
, then either
S
spans more than
4p
rows or
S
spans more than
4p
columns.

Proof

Suppose that
S
is both row and column contiguous and does not contain any uniquely colored vertex with respect to the coloring
\mu
. Consider a vertex
(x,y)\in S
such that
l(x,y)=\max\{l(a,b)\colon(a,b)\in S\}
. Since there is no uniquely colored vertex in
S
, there exists
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in S
such that
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\neq(x,y)
and
\mu(x,y)=\mu(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
. By definition of
\mu
, we have
\alpha(x,y)=\alpha(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
,
l(x,y)=l(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
, and
\rho(x,y)=\rho(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
. We assume that
\alpha(x,y)=\alpha(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=0
, since the other case, i.e.
\alpha(x,y)=\alpha(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=1
, is symmetric. Then
f(x)\geq f(y)
and
f(x^{\prime})\geq f(y^{\prime})
, from which it follows that
l(x,y)=f(x)
and
l(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=f(x^{\prime})
. As
l(x,y)=l(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
, we now have
f(x)=f(x^{\prime})=t
(say).

We first consider the case when
t<\lg(4p)
.
Suppose that
x\neq x^{\prime}
. We shall assume without loss of generality that
x<x^{\prime}
. Since
f(x)=t<\lg(4p)
, we have that
2^{t}\mid x
and that
2^{t+1}\nmid x
. This implies that
2^{t+1}\mid x+2^{t}
. As
2^{t}\mid x
and
2^{t}\mid x^{\prime}
, we have
x^{\prime}\geq x+2^{t}
. Since
f(x^{\prime})=t<\lg(4p)
, we have
2^{t+1}\nmid x^{\prime}
, from which it follows that
x^{\prime}>x+2^{t}
. As
S
is row contiguous, there exists
b\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(x+2^{t},b)\in S
. As
t<\lg(4p)
and
2^{t+1}\mid x+2^{t}
, we have that
f(x+2^{t})\geq t+1
. But now we have
l(x+2^{t},b)=\max\{f(x+2^{t}),f(b)\}\geq t+1>l(x,y)
, which is a contradiction to our choice of
(x,y)
. So we can assume that
x=x^{\prime}
. As
(x,y)\neq(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
, we have
y\neq y^{\prime}
. We assume without loss of generality that
y<y^{\prime}
. As
\rho(x,y)=\rho(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
, we have
\mathrm{mod}(y,2^{t+1})=\mathrm{mod}(y^{\prime},2^{t+1})=r
(say). This implies that
y^{\prime}\geq y+2^{t+1}
. Since
r<2^{t+1}
, it follows that
y^{\prime}-r>y
. As
t<\lg(4p)
and
l(x,y)=\max\{f(x),f(y)\}=t
, we know that
2^{t+1}\nmid y
, or in other words,
r\neq 0
. Thus
y^{\prime}-r<y^{\prime}
. We now have
y<y^{\prime}-r<y^{\prime}
. As
S
is column contiguous there exists
a\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(a,y^{\prime}-r)\in S
. Notice that
2^{t+1}\mid y^{\prime}-r
, which means that
f(y^{\prime}-r)\geq t+1
. Then
l(a,y^{\prime}-r)=\max\{f(a),f(y^{\prime}-r)\}\geq t+1>l(x,y)
. But this is again a contradiction to our choice of
(x,y)
.

From the above argument, we can conclude that
t=\lg(4p)
. Hence both
x
and
x^{\prime}
are divisible by
4p
. If
x\neq x^{\prime}
, then this means that
|x-x^{\prime}|\geq 4p
, which implies that
S
spans more than
4p
rows, and we are done. So we shall assume that
x=x^{\prime}
, which means that
y\neq y^{\prime}
. Since
\rho(x,y)=\rho(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
, we have that
\mathrm{mod}(y,2^{t+1})=\mathrm{mod}(y^{\prime},2^{t+1})
, or in other words,
\mathrm{mod}(y,8p)=\mathrm{mod}(y^{\prime},8p)
. Hence
|y-y^{\prime}|\geq 8p
(as
y\neq y^{\prime}
), implying that
S
spans more than
8p
columns, and we are again done. This completes the proof.
∎

Notice that the coloring
\mu
of
G
is not a
p
-centered coloring of
G
because of the presence of “large” violators. For example, consider the subgraph
H=G[\{(0,1),(1,1),(2,1),\ldots,(8p,1)\}]
. Clearly, for each vertex
u\in V(H)
, we have
\alpha(u)=0
,
\rho(u)=1
, and
l(u)\in[0,\lg(4p)]
, which implies that
|\mu(V(H))|\leq\lg(4p)+1
. So when
p\geq 8
,
|\mu(V(H))|<p+1
. At the same time, we have that for each
x\in[0,4p]
, the vertices
(x,1),(4p+x,1)\in V(H)
and
\mu(x,1)=\mu(4p+x,1)
. Thus, no vertex in
H
has a unique color. As
H
is clearly a connected subgraph of
G
, we can conclude that
\mu
is not a
p
-centered coloring of
G
for any value of
p
greater than or equal to 8.

2.2
A coloring in which every violator is small

We now show another coloring of the grid
G
which is again not
p
-centered, but this time has no “large violators”; in particular, it has the property that any violator in
G
spans at most
2(p+1)
rows and at most
2(p+1)
columns. Note that we mention this coloring of the grid just to develop intuition; it is not directly used in the proof of the main result.

R=\{(x,y)\in V(G)\colon x+y

. Consider the coloring
\theta:\mathbb{N}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
of the vertices of

2.3
Obtaining a
p
-centered coloring

We now describe a new coloring
\lambda
of the grid
G
. First, we partition
V(G)
into two sets in two different ways. The vertex set
V(G)
is partitioned into two sets
R,C
as follows: define
R=\{(x,y):\left\lfloor\frac{x}{3}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{y}{3}\right\rfloor
is even
\}
and
C=V(G)\setminus R
. Again,
V(G)
is partitioned into two sets
A,B
in the following way: define
A=\{(x,y)\in V(G):\mathrm{mod}(x,3)+\mathrm{mod}(y,3)
is odd
\}
and
B=V(G)\setminus A
. See Figure
3
for an illustration of the two partitions of
V(G)
.
The mapping
I:V(G)\rightarrow V(G)
is defined as
(x,y)\mapsto(\left\lfloor\frac{x}{3}\right\rfloor,\left\lfloor\frac{y}{3}\right\rfloor)
.
Finally, we define the coloring
\lambda
of
V(G)
as the function given below:

\begin{array}[]{llll}(x,y)&\mapsto&(\mu(I(x,y)),\mathrm{mod}(x,3),\mathrm{mod}(y,3))&\mbox{when }(x,y)\in A\\
(x,y)&\mapsto&(0,\mathrm{mod}(x,6p+6))&\mbox{when }(x,y)\in R\cap B,\mbox{ and }\\
(x,y)&\mapsto&(1,\mathrm{mod}(y,6p+6))&\mbox{when }(x,y)\in C\cap B\end{array}

Notice that
I(V(G))=V(G)
and therefore
|\mu(I(V(G)))|\leq 32p
as observed in Section
2.1
. This implies that
|\lambda(V(G))|\leq 4(32p)+2(6p+6)=140p+12
.

Observation 1

If
(x,y)(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in E(G)
such that
I(x,y)\neq I(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
, then one of
(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
is in
R
and the other in
C
.

Proof

Since
(x,y)(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in E(G)
, we have
|x-x^{\prime}|+|y-y^{\prime}|=1
. This means that either
x=x^{\prime}
or
y=y^{\prime}
. We shall only consider the case when
x=x^{\prime}
as the other case is symmetric. Note that we now have that
|y-y^{\prime}|=1
. As
\Big\lfloor\frac{x}{3}\Big\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{x^{\prime}}{3}\right\rfloor
, the fact that
I(x,y)\neq I(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
implies that
\Big\lfloor\frac{y}{3}\Big\rfloor\neq\left\lfloor\frac{y^{\prime}}{3}\right\rfloor
. Since
|y-y^{\prime}|=1
, we can conclude that
\Big|\Big\lfloor\frac{y}{3}\Big\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{y^{\prime}}{3}\right\rfloor\Big|=1
. Then the parity of
\left\lfloor\frac{x^{\prime}}{3}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{y^{\prime}}{3}\right\rfloor
is different from that of
\left\lfloor\frac{x}{3}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{y}{3}\right\rfloor
. Thus, we have that one of
(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
is in
R
and the other in
C
. ∎

Observation 2

Let
(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\in V(G)
and let
P
be a path in
G
having endvertices
(x_{1},y_{1})
and
(x_{2},y_{2})
. If
y_{1}<y_{2}
, then for every
b
such that
y_{1}<b\leq y_{2}
, there exists an edge
(a,b-1)(a,b)\in E(P)
, for some
a\in\mathbb{N}
. Similarly, if
x_{1}<x_{2}
, then for every
a
such that
x_{1}<a\leq x_{2}
, there exists an edge
(a-1,b)(a,b)\in E(P)
, for some
b\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof

We prove only the first statement as the second one is symmetric. Suppose that
y_{1}<y_{2}
. Let
u
be the first vertex on
P
for which
\pi_{y}(u)\geq b
when traversing it starting from the endpoint
(x_{1},y_{1})
, and let
v
be the vertex encountered just before
u
in the traversal. By our choice of
u
, we have that
\pi_{y}(v)<b
. Since
uv\in E(P)\subseteq E(G)
, we also have that
|\pi_{y}(u)-\pi_{y}(v)|\leq 1
, which implies that
\pi_{y}(v)=b-1
and
\pi_{y}(u)=b
. As
\pi_{y}(v)\neq\pi_{y}(u)
, we have that
\pi_{x}(v)=\pi_{x}(u)=a
(say). Then
vu=(a,b-1)(a,b)\in E(P)
, as required. ∎

Lemma 2

Let
H
be a connected subgraph of
G
such that
V(H)
spans at least
6p+9
rows or at least
6p+9
columns, then
|\lambda(V(H))|\geq p+1

Proof

We shall only give a proof for the case when

spans at least
6p+9
columns, as the other case is symmetric.
Let
y_{1}=\min\pi_{y}(V(H))
and
y_{2}=\max\pi_{y}(V(H))
. Then we have
y_{2}-y_{1}\geq 6p+8
. Clearly, there exist
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\in V(H)
. As
H
is a connected subgraph of

, there exists a path

in

having endvertices
(x_{1},y_{1})
and
(x_{2},y_{2})
.

Claim

Let
b\in\mathbb{N}
such that

and

. Then there exists
(x,y)\in V(P)\cap C\cap B
such that
b-2\leq y\leq b+1
.

The proof of this claim is obvious once it is noted that the vertices in
C\cap B\cap(\mathbb{N}\times[b-2,b+1])
form a zig-zag separator that separates every vertex on its left from the ones on its right, as shown in Figure

. However, we give a formal proof for the sake of completeness.

Theorem 2.1

\lambda
is a
p
-centered coloring of
G
.

Proof

We shall show that with respect to the coloring
\lambda
, every connected subgraph of
G
either contains more than
p
colors or a vertex with a unique color.
Let
H
be any connected subgraph of
G
. Clearly,
V(H)
is both row and column contiguous. If
V(H)
spans either at least
6p+9
rows or at least
6p+9
columns, then by Lemma
2
, we have
|\lambda(V(H))|\geq p+1
and we are done. So let us assume that
V(H)
spans at most
6p+8
rows and at most
6p+8
columns. We will show that
H
contains a uniquely colored vertex. Since this is trivially true when
|V(H)|=1
, we shall assume that
|V(H)|>1
.

First suppose that
V(H)\cap A=\emptyset
. It should be obvious from Figure
3
and the definition of
\lambda
that in this case,
H
is isomorphic to a cycle on four vertices or a path on at most three vertices, and every vertex of
H
has a different color. Nevertheless, we give a formal proof for the sake of completeness. Consider any
(x,y)\in V(H)
. Clearly,
(x,y)\in B
. Notice that if either
\mathrm{mod}(x,3)=1
or
\mathrm{mod}(y,3)=1
, then
\mathrm{mod}(x,3)=\mathrm{mod}(y,3)=1
and all neighbours of
(x,y)
in
G
belong to
A
. Since
H
is connected, this implies that if there exists
(x,y)\in V(H)
such that
\mathrm{mod}(x,3)=1
or
\mathrm{mod}(y,3)=1
, then
|V(H)|=1
, contradicting our assumption that
|V(H)|>1
. So we assume that
V(H)\subseteq\{(x,y)\in V(G)\colon\mathrm{mod}(x,3),\mathrm{mod}(y,3)\in\{0,2\}\}
. Since
V(H)
is both row and column contiguous, this implies that
|\pi_{x}(V(H))|,|\pi_{y}(V(H))|\leq 2
. Suppose that there are distinct vertices
u,v\in V(H)
such that
\lambda(u)=\lambda(v)
. Then as
V(H)\subseteq B
, we have that either
u,v\in R
or
u,v\in C
. Since the cases are symmetric, we assume that
u,v\in R
. Then
\lambda(u)=\lambda(v)
implies that
\mathrm{mod}(\pi_{x}(u),6p+6)=\mathrm{mod}(\pi_{x}(v),6p+6)
. As
|\pi_{x}(V(H))|\leq 2
, this further implies that
\pi_{x}(u)=\pi_{x}(v)
. Then since
|\pi_{y}(V(H))|\leq 2
and
u\neq v
, we have
|\pi_{y}(u)-\pi_{y}(v)|=1
. Assuming without loss of generality that
\pi_{y}(v)>\pi_{y}(u)
, we have
\pi_{y}(v)=\pi_{y}(u)+1
. Recalling that
\mathrm{mod}(\pi_{y}(u),3),\mathrm{mod}(\pi_{y}(v),3)\in\{0,2\}
, we then have
\pi_{y}(u)=3k-1
and
\pi_{y}(v)=3k
, for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus
\left\lfloor\frac{\pi_{y}(v)}{3}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{\pi_{y}(u)}{3}\right\rfloor+1
. Since
\left\lfloor\frac{\pi_{x}(u)}{3}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{\pi_{x}(v)}{3}\right\rfloor
, we now have that
\left\lfloor\frac{\pi_{x}(u)}{3}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{\pi_{y}(u)}{3}\right\rfloor
and
\left\lfloor\frac{\pi_{x}(v)}{3}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{\pi_{y}(v)}{3}\right\rfloor
are of different parity. This contradicts the fact that

. Thus, there exist no two vertices in
V(H)
that have the same color with respect to the coloring
\lambda
, or in other words, every vertex in

Next, suppose that

2.4
Generalizing to all values of
p

It is quite easy to see that the above construction can be used to get a
p
-centered coloring of
G
for arbitrary values of
p
as follows.
Let
p
be any positive integer. If
p=1
, then any proper coloring of the grid is a
p
-centered coloring, and hence we have that there is a
p
-centered coloring of the grid using just 2 colors (for
p=1
, the coloring
\lambda
, if constructed overlooking the fact that the condition that
p
has to be at least 2 is not met, is also a proper coloring of
G
and hence is a
p
-centered coloring of
G
). So we assume that
p>1
. Then let
p^{\prime}
be the smallest power of 2 that is greater than or equal to
p
. Clearly,
p^{\prime}\geq 2
and
p^{\prime}\leq 2p
. Using the construction explained above, we can get a
p^{\prime}
-centered coloring
\lambda
of
G
using at most
140p^{\prime}+12
colors. Notice that any
p^{\prime}
-centered coloring of
G
is also a
p
-centered coloring of
G
as
p\leq p^{\prime}
. Recalling that
p^{\prime}\leq 2p
, we now have that
\lambda
is a
p
-centered coloring of
G
using at most
140p^{\prime}+12\leq 280p+12
colors. Thus
G
has a
p
-centered coloring using
O(p)
colors for every positive integer
p
.

3
Conclusion

We hope that our strategy for constructing a
p
-centered coloring for the planar grid can serve as a starting point for research on explicit constructions of
p
-centered colorings using
O(p)
colors for more general classes of graphs with bounded maximum degree. A natural next step could be to try if similar strategies work for bounded degree graphs that are similar to the planar grid — for example, the planar grid with both diagonals added in each square, or the three dimensional grid.
