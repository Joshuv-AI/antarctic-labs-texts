Relativistic hyperpolarizabilities for atomic H, Li, and Be + systems
Shan-Shan Lu, 1,2 Hong-Yuan Zheng, 1,2 Zong-Chao Yan, 3,1 James F. Babb, 4 and Li-Yan Tang 1,∗

Abstract
For atoms in external electric fields, the hyperpolarizabilities are the coefficients describing the nonlinear interactions contributing to the induced energies at the fourth power of the applied electric fields. Accurate evaluations of these coefficients for various systems are crucial for improving precision in advanced atom-based optical lattice clocks and for estimating field-induced effects in atoms for quantum information applications. However, there is a notable scarcity of research on atomic hyperpolarizabilities, especially for the relativistic realm. Our work addresses this gap by establishing a novel set of alternative formulas for the hyperpolarizability based on fourth-order perturbation theory. These formulas offer a more reasonable regrouping of scalar and tensor components compared to previous formulas, thereby enhancing their correctness and applicability. To validate our formulas, we perform the calculations for the ground and low-lying excited pure states of few-electron atoms H, Li, and Be + . The highly accurate results obtained for the H atom could serve as benchmarks for further development of other theoretical methods.

I
Introduction

The response of an atom to an applied electric field is described at leading order by the polarizability (linear response to the field) and at higher order by the hyperpolarizability (nonlinear response to the field). The literature on precision calculations of polarizabilities for atoms

;
tang12b

;
tang10a

Here, our intent is to present a theoretical formalism and some highly-accurate benchmark values for hyperpolarizabilities of few-electron atoms.

Hyperpolarizabilities
bishop95a
;
lupinetti06b
;
shelton94
are the coefficients describing the response induced in atoms at the order of the fourth power of applied electric fields (and describe induced dipole moments at the third power of applied electric fields). As perturbative corrections to the energies of atoms in electric fields, the relative values of the hyperpolarizabilities compared to the values of the polarizabilities (which appear at the second power of applied electric fields) are fundamental to estimating the impact of field intensities on nonlinear phenomena
manakov86b
;
manakov86c
. Knowledge of the values of hyperpolarizabilities plays a crucial role in arrangements to suppress nonlinear effects for precision laser spectroscopy measurements
henson22
;
yang16
;
sheng18
;
guo20

nicholson15
;
brown17
;
ushijima18
;
kim23
. Moreover, applications of highly excited Rydberg states in neutral atom quantum computing are rapidly developing and it is reasonable to expect that improvements in quantification of hyperpolarizabilities will be needed as control of these atoms in electric fields is sought [see, for example, Eq. (11) of Ref.
bohorquez2023
].

In this study, we present a set of general formulas for the atomic hyperpolarizabilities, which improves our previously published theory

+

+
systems, we leverage the nonrelativistic numerical values obtained using our previous theory

An alternative formula

When an atom is placed in an uniform external electric field, its energy levels change due to the influence of the field. Specifically, the energy at second-order in the electric field depends on the atomic dipole polarizability. The fourth-order shift depends on the atomic hyperpolarizability
\gamma
and can be expressed in the form:

\displaystyle\Delta E_{4}=-\frac{\varepsilon^{4}}{24}\gamma=-\frac{\varepsilon^{4}}{24}\bigg\{\gamma_{0}+g_{2}(J,M_{J})\gamma_{2}+\big[g_{4}(J,M_{J})\gamma_{4}^{(1)}+g_{2}^{2}(J,M_{J})\gamma_{4}^{(2)}\big]\bigg\}\,,

\frac{3M_{J}^{2}-J(J+1)}{J(2J-1)},&{\rm otherwise}\,,\end{cases}

(2)

\displaystyle g_{4}(J,M_{J})=\begin{cases}0,&J\leq\frac{3}{2}\,,\\
\frac{3(5M_{J}^{2}-J^{2}-2J)(5M_{J}^{2}+1-J^{2})-10M_{J}^{2}(4M_{J}^{2}-1)}{J(2J-1)(2J-2)(2J-3)},&{\rm otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

(3)

III
Computational Methods

III.1
Single-electron Schrödinger and Dirac equations

When solving the hydrogen, the nuclear mass is set to be infinite, and the point nucleus model is adopted.
For the nonrelativistic case, the Schrödinger equation for hydrogen is given by

\displaystyle\left[-\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla^{2}+V(r)\right]\psi_{S}(\bm{r})=E_{S}\psi_{S}(\bm{r)}\,,

(8)

where
V(r)=-Z/r
and
Z
is the nuclear charge. Due to its rotational symmetry, there exist solutions to Eq. (
8
) of the separable form

\displaystyle\psi_{S}(\bm{r})=U_{nl}(r)Y_{lm}(\theta,\phi)\,,

(9)

where
Y_{lm}(\theta,\phi)
is a spherical harmonic function and the radial function
U_{nl}(r)
satisfies the differential equation

\displaystyle\left[-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}-\frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}+\frac{l(l+1)}{2r^{2}}+V(r)\right]U_{nl}(r)=E_{S}U_{nl}(r)\,.

(10)

For the relativistic case, the Dirac equation for hydrogen is given by

\displaystyle\big[c\bm{\alpha}\cdot\bm{p}+\beta c^{2}+V(r)\big]\psi_{D}(\bm{r})=E_{D}\psi_{D}(\bm{r)}\,,

(11)

where
c=137.035\,999\,084
is the speed of light
tiesinga21
,
\bm{p}
is the momentum operator, and
\bm{\alpha}
and
\beta
are the usual
4\times 4
Dirac matrices
kaneko77a
.
The solutions to Eq. (
11
) can be expressed in the following separable form:

\displaystyle\psi_{D}(\bm{r})=\frac{1}{r}\begin{pmatrix}iP_{n\kappa}(r)\Omega_{\kappa m}(\hat{\bf r})\\
Q_{nk}(r)\Omega_{-\kappa m}(\hat{\bf r})\end{pmatrix}\,,

(12)

where
P_{n\kappa}(r)
and
Q_{n\kappa}(r)
are the large and small components of the radial function, and
\Omega_{\kappa m}(\hat{\bf r})
and
\Omega_{-\kappa m}(\hat{\bf r})
are the angular components.
By substituting Eq. (
12
) into Eq. (
11
), we obtain the following coupled first-order differential equations that describe the behavior of
P_{n\kappa}(r)
and
Q_{n\kappa}(r)
:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}V(r)&c\left(\frac{d}{dr}-\frac{\kappa}{r}\right)\\
-c\left(\frac{d}{dr}+\frac{\kappa}{r}\right)&-2c^{2}+V(r)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}P_{n\kappa}(r)\\
Q_{n\kappa}(r)\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\begin{pmatrix}P_{n\kappa}(r)\\
Q_{n\kappa}(r)\end{pmatrix}\,,

(13)

where
E=E_{D}-c^{2}
.

While it is possible to solve the Schrödinger equation Eq. (
10
) and the Dirac equation Eq. (
13
) analytically, in this study, we choose to solve them numerically using
B
-spline functions within a specific cavity
johnson88a
;
fischer93
;
bachau01a
;
tang12b
. This approach enables us to validate our numerical method, which will be subsequently used to diagonalize the Hamiltonian.
For example, by using the complete
B
-spline basis, we can express
U_{nl}(r)
as a discrete sum of
N
-dimensional,
k
-order
B
-spline basis functions:

\displaystyle U_{nl}(r)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}B_{i}^{k}(r)\,.

(14)

The function
B_{i}^{k}(r)
is non-zero only within the knot intervals
t_{i}\leq r\leq t_{i+k}
, where
{t_{i}}
represents the knot sequence. In the nonrelativistic case, the radial wave functions must satisfy the boundary conditions
U_{nl}(0)=U_{nl}(R)=0
, where
R
is the radius of the cavity. In the relativistic case, the Notre Dame (ND) boundary conditions
johnson88a
;
chodos74a
of
P_{n\kappa}(0)=0
and
P_{n\kappa}(R)=Q_{n\kappa}(R)
are imposed to address the issues related to the “Klein Paradox”.
In order to obtain accurate bound-state wave functions, it is more appropriate to use an exponential knot distribution for
B_{i}^{k}(r)
given by

\displaystyle t_{i+k-1}=R\times\frac{\mathrm{exp}\left[\eta\left(\frac{i-1}{n-1}\right)\right]-1}{\mathrm{exp}(\eta)-1}\,,\ \ i=1,2,\ldots,N-k+2\,.

(15)

Here,
\eta=a\times R
is an adjustable exponential knot parameter. In the present work, the radii of the confining cavity are fixed at
R=400
a.u. and
R=600
a.u. for nonrelativistic and relativistic calculations, respectively. The value of
a
is optimized by reproducing the exact energies
bethe77a
of low-lying states up to 20 significant digits.

III.2
DFCP method

Since the Li atom and Be
+
ion can be regarded as a frozen core combined with one valence electron, these systems can be solved using the Dirac-Fock plus core polarization (DFCP) method
johnson88a
;
bachau01a
. As described in previous studies
tang13b
;
jiang16
;
wu19
, the initial step in the DFCP method is to perform a Dirac-Fock calculation of the frozen core. For monovalent electron systems, the effective Hamiltonian can be written as:

h_{\rm DFCP}(\bm{r})=c{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot{\bm{p}}+(\beta-1)c^{2}+V(r)+V_{\rm dir}(r)+V_{\rm exc}(r)+V_{\rm 1}(r)\,.

(16)

Here,
V_{\rm dir}(r)
and
V_{\rm exc}(r)
are the direct and exchange potentials between the core electrons and the valence electron, respectively, and
V_{\rm 1}(r)
is the semiempirical polarization potential,

\displaystyle V_{1}(r)=-\frac{\alpha_{\rm core}}{2r^{4}}\bigg[1-{\rm exp}\bigg(-\frac{r^{6}}{\rho_{\kappa}^{6}}\bigg)\bigg]\,,

(17)

where
\alpha_{\rm core}
is the core dipole polarizability,
\rho_{\kappa}
is the cutoff parameter, and
\kappa
is the angular quantum number. In the present work, we used
\alpha_{\rm core}=0.1894
a.u. for Li
+
and
\alpha_{\rm core}=0.05182
a.u. for Be
2+

johnson83
. The cutoff parameter
\rho_{\kappa}
is fine-tuned to accurately replicate the experimental energy of the lowest state associated with each value of
\kappa
.

Appendix A
Derivation of the hyperpolarizability

In our previous work, which is referenced as Ref.
tang09a
, we derived the hyperpolarizability. However, we unintentionally overlooked a crucial constraint involving the Kronecker
\delta(n,0)
. This constraint is necessary when dealing with the second intermediate states
|n\rangle
with the initial state
|0\rangle
in the second term of the fourth-order energy correction
\Delta E_{4}
, as shown in Eqs. (A34) and (A35) of Ref.
tang09a
.
In this Appendix, we will revisit the derivation process from the beginning, taking into account the presence of the
\delta(n,0)
constraint. Remarkably, we have discovered that the new formula we derived is in complete agreement with the previous one, despite having different partitioning in terms of “
\gamma
” components.

The Hamiltonian of an atom under the influence of a weak external electric field is given by

H=H_{0}+H^{\prime}=H_{0}-\bm{\varepsilon}\cdot\bm{d}\,.

(18)

Here,
H_{0}
is the atom’s unperturbed Hamiltonian,
\bm{\varepsilon}
is the electric field strength, and
\bm{d}
is the atom’s electric dipole moment, given by
\bm{d}=-e\bm{r}
, where
\bm{r}
is the electron’s position vector relative to a laboratory frame. The perturbation,
H^{\prime}
, can be expressed as
\bm{r}\cdot\bm{\varepsilon}
in atomic units. It is noted that the electric quadrupole and magnetic dipole interactions have been omitted from Eq. (
18
), and the treatment of these higher-order terms can be found in Refs.
kolb82
;
singh18
.

According to perturbation theory, the fourth-order energy shift
manakov86c
contains two terms:
\Delta E_{4}^{(1)}
and
\Delta E_{4}^{(2)}
,

\displaystyle\Delta E_{4}=\Delta E_{4}^{(1)}+\Delta E_{4}^{(2)}=\sum_{mnk}\frac{\langle 0|H^{\prime}|m\rangle\langle m|H^{\prime}|n\rangle\langle n|H^{\prime}|k\rangle\langle k|H^{\prime}|0\rangle}{(E_{0}-E_{m})(E_{0}-E_{n})(E_{0}-E_{k})}-\sum_{mk}\frac{\langle 0|H^{\prime}|m\rangle\langle m|H^{\prime}|0\rangle\langle 0|H^{\prime}|k\rangle\langle k|H^{\prime}|0\rangle}{(E_{0}-E_{m})(E_{0}-E_{k})^{2}}\,.

(19)

Using the spherical tensor operator technique, we can expand
H^{\prime}|n\rangle\langle n|H^{\prime}
of Eq. (
19
) as

\displaystyle H^{\prime}|n\rangle\langle n|H^{\prime}=\sum_{Kq}(-1)^{K+q}[r^{(1)}\otimes\lambda_{n}r^{(1)}]_{q}^{K}[\varepsilon^{(1)}\otimes\varepsilon^{(1)}]_{-q}^{K}\,,

(20)

where
r_{\mu}^{(1)}=\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{3}}rY_{1\mu}
and
\lambda_{n}=|n\rangle\langle n|
. Assuming that the external electric field
\bm{\varepsilon}
is linearly polarized parallel to the
z
axis, only the
q=0
component exists in Eq. (
20
). Thus we have

\displaystyle H^{\prime}|n\rangle\langle n|H^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{K}(-1)^{K}\sqrt{2K+1}\sum\limits_{q_{1}q_{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1&K\\
q_{1}&q_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}r_{q_{1}}^{(1)}\lambda_{n}r_{q_{2}}^{(1)}[\varepsilon^{(1)}\otimes\varepsilon^{(1)}]_{0}^{K}\,.

(21)

Substituting Eq. (
21
) into Eq. (
19
),
\Delta E_{4}^{(1)}
and
\Delta E_{4}^{(2)}
can be written as

\displaystyle\Delta E_{4}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{mnk}\sum\limits_{k_{1}k_{2}}\sum\limits_{q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4}}(-1)^{k_{1}+k_{2}}\sqrt{(2k_{1}+1)(2k_{2}+1)}\begin{pmatrix}1&1&k_{1}\\
q_{1}&q_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1&k_{2}\\
q_{3}&q_{4}&0\end{pmatrix}[\varepsilon^{(1)}\otimes\varepsilon^{(1)}]_{0}^{k_{1}}[\varepsilon^{(1)}\otimes\varepsilon^{(1)}]_{0}^{k_{2}}

(22)

\displaystyle\times\frac{\langle 0|r_{q_{1}}^{(1)}|m\rangle\langle m|r_{q_{2}}^{(1)}|n\rangle\langle n|r_{q_{3}}^{(1)}|k\rangle\langle k|r_{q_{4}}^{(1)}|0\rangle}{(E_{0}-E_{m})(E_{0}-E_{n})(E_{0}-E_{k})}\,,

\displaystyle\Delta E_{4}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum\limits_{m}\sum\limits_{k_{1}}\sum\limits_{q_{1}q_{2}}(-1)^{k_{1}}\sqrt{2k_{1}+1}\begin{pmatrix}1&1&k_{1}\\
q_{1}&q_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}\frac{\langle 0|r_{q_{1}}^{(1)}|m\rangle\langle m|r_{q_{2}}^{(1)}|0\rangle}{(E_{0}-E_{m})}[\varepsilon^{(1)}\otimes\varepsilon^{(1)}]_{0}^{k_{1}}

(23)

\displaystyle\times\sum\limits_{k}\sum\limits_{k_{2}}\sum\limits_{q_{3}q_{4}}(-1)^{k_{2}}\sqrt{2k_{2}+1}\begin{pmatrix}1&1&k_{2}\\
q_{3}&q_{4}&0\end{pmatrix}\frac{\langle 0|r_{q_{3}}^{(1)}|k\rangle\langle k|r_{q_{4}}^{(1)}|0\rangle}{(E_{0}-E_{k})^{2}}[\varepsilon^{(1)}\otimes\varepsilon^{(1)}]_{0}^{k_{2}}\,,

where all the initial and intermediate states are simply denoted as
|0\rangle=|n_{0}JM_{J}\rangle
,
|m\rangle=|mJ_{a}M_{a}\rangle
,
|n\rangle=|nJ_{b}M_{b}\rangle
,
|k\rangle=|kJ_{c}M_{c}\rangle
, with
n_{0}
,
m
,
n
, and
k
being the corresponding principal quantum numbers of states. The summation in Eqs. (
22
) and (
23
) over
m
,
n
, and
k
actually represents the summation over all three sets of quantum numbers
\{m,J_{a},M_{a}\}
,
\{n,J_{b},M_{b}\}
, and
\{k,J_{c},M_{c}\}
.

By utilizing the Wigner-Eckart theorem to simplify Eqs. (
22
) and (
23
), we can arrive at the following expressions:

\displaystyle\Delta E_{4}^{(1)}=-\sum\limits_{J_{a}J_{b}J_{c}}\sum\limits_{k_{1}k_{2}}(-1)^{k_{1}+k_{2}}\sqrt{(2k_{1}+1)(2k_{2}+1)}[\varepsilon^{(1)}\otimes\varepsilon^{(1)}]_{0}^{k_{1}}[\varepsilon^{(1)}\otimes\varepsilon^{(1)}]_{0}^{k_{2}}\mathcal{C}_{1}\mathcal{T}_{1}(J_{a},J_{b},J_{c})\,,

(24)

\displaystyle\Delta E_{4}^{(2)}=\sum\limits_{J_{a}J_{c}}\sum\limits_{k_{1}k_{2}}(-1)^{k_{1}+k_{2}}\sqrt{(2k_{1}+1)(2k_{2}+1)}[\varepsilon^{(1)}\otimes\varepsilon^{(1)}]_{0}^{k_{1}}[\varepsilon^{(1)}\otimes\varepsilon^{(1)}]_{0}^{k_{2}}\mathcal{C}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{2}(J_{a},J_{c})\,,

(25)

where
\mathcal{T}_{1}(J_{a},J_{b},J_{c})
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}(J_{a},J_{c})
are radial-dependent terms,
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
are the angular coefficients. The explicit expressions are as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{1}(J_{a},J_{b},J_{c})=\sum\limits_{mnk}^{\prime}\frac{\langle n_{0}J\|r^{(1)}\|mJ_{a}\rangle\langle mJ_{a}\|r^{(1)}\|nJ_{b}\rangle\langle nJ_{b}\|r^{(1)}\|kJ_{c}\rangle\langle kJ_{c}\|r^{(1)}\|n_{0}J\rangle}{\left[E_{k}(J_{c})-E_{n_{0}}(J)\right]\left[E_{m}(J_{a})-E_{n_{0}}(J)\right]\left[E_{n}(J_{b})-E_{n_{0}}(J)\right]}\,,

(26)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{2}(J_{a},J_{c})=\sum\limits_{m}^{\prime}\frac{\langle n_{0}J\|r^{(1)}\|mJ_{a}\rangle\langle mJ_{a}\|r^{(1)}\|n_{0}J\rangle}{\left[E_{m}(J_{a})-E_{n_{0}}(J)\right]}\sum\limits_{k}^{\prime}\frac{\langle n_{0}J\|r^{(1)}\|kJ_{c}\rangle\langle kJ_{c}\|r^{(1)}\|n_{0}J\rangle}{\left[E_{k}(J_{c})-E_{n_{0}}(J)\right]^{2}}\,,

(27)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{M_{a}M_{b}M_{c}}\sum\limits_{q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4}}(-1)^{J-M_{J}+J_{a}-M_{a}+J_{b}-M_{b}+J_{c}-M_{c}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1&k_{1}\\
q_{1}&q_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1&k_{2}\\
q_{3}&q_{4}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}J&1&J_{a}\\
-M_{J}&q_{1}&M_{a}\end{pmatrix}

(28)

\displaystyle\times\begin{pmatrix}J_{a}&1&J_{b}\\
-M_{a}&q_{2}&M_{b}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}J_{b}&1&J_{c}\\
-M_{b}&q_{3}&M_{c}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}J_{c}&1&J\\
-M_{c}&q_{4}&M_{J}\end{pmatrix}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{M_{a}M_{c}}\sum\limits_{q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4}}(-1)^{J_{a}-M_{a}+J_{c}-M_{c}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1&k_{1}\\
q_{1}&q_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}J&1&J_{a}\\
-M_{J}&q_{1}&M_{a}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}J_{a}&1&J\\
-M_{a}&q_{2}&M_{J}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1&k_{2}\\
q_{3}&q_{4}&0\end{pmatrix}

(29)

\displaystyle\times\begin{pmatrix}J&1&J_{c}\\
-M_{J}&q_{3}&M_{c}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}J_{c}&1&J\\
-M_{c}&q_{4}&M_{J}\end{pmatrix}\,,

where the primes over the summations in Eqs. (
26
) and (
27
) mean the omission of any intermediate states that are degenerate with the initial state, and the summations over intermediate states include the negative-energy states in the relativistic case.
By using the graphical method of angular momentum, we can simplify
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{J-M_{J}}\begin{Bmatrix}1&1&k_{1}\\
J&J_{b}&J_{a}\end{Bmatrix}\begin{Bmatrix}1&1&k_{2}\\
J&J_{b}&J_{c}\end{Bmatrix}\sum\limits_{\Lambda}(2\Lambda+1)\begin{pmatrix}J&J&\Lambda\\
-M_{J}&M_{J}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}k_{1}&k_{2}&\Lambda\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{Bmatrix}k_{1}&k_{2}&\Lambda\\
J&J&J_{b}\end{Bmatrix}\,,

(30)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}J&J&k_{1}\\
-M_{J}&M_{J}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}J&J&k_{2}\\
-M_{J}&M_{J}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{Bmatrix}1&1&k_{1}\\
J&J&J_{a}\end{Bmatrix}\begin{Bmatrix}1&1&k_{2}\\
J&J&J_{c}\end{Bmatrix}\,.

(31)

Since
\bm{\varepsilon}
is oriented along the
z
axis, we can express
[\varepsilon^{(1)}\otimes\varepsilon^{(1)}]_{0}^{k}
in the form:

\displaystyle[\varepsilon^{(1)}\otimes\varepsilon^{(1)}]_{0}^{k}=\sqrt{2k+1}\begin{pmatrix}1&1&k\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\varepsilon^{2}\,.

(32)

By substituting Eqs. (
30
), (
31
), and (
32
) into Eqs. (
24
) and (
25
), we obtain the final expressions for
\Delta E_{4}^{(1)}
and
\Delta E_{4}^{(2)}
:

\displaystyle\Delta E_{4}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\varepsilon^{4}\sum\limits_{J_{a}J_{b}J_{c}}\mathcal{T}_{1}(J_{a},J_{b},J_{c})\sum\limits_{\lambda}(-1)^{J-M_{J}}\begin{pmatrix}J&J&\lambda\\
-M_{J}&M_{J}&0\end{pmatrix}\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}^{(1)}(J,J_{a},J_{b},J_{c})\,,

(33)

\displaystyle\Delta E_{4}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{4}\sum\limits_{J_{a}J_{c}}\mathcal{T}_{2}(J_{a},J_{c})\sum\limits_{k_{1}k_{2}}\begin{pmatrix}J&J&k_{1}\\
-M_{J}&M_{J}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}J&J&k_{2}\\
-M_{J}&M_{J}&0\end{pmatrix}\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}k_{2}}^{(2)}(J,J_{a},J_{c})\,,

(34)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}^{(1)}(J,J_{a},J_{b},J_{c})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k_{1}k_{2}}(\lambda,k_{1},k_{2})\begin{pmatrix}1&1&k_{1}\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1&k_{2}\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}k_{1}&k_{2}&\lambda\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{Bmatrix}1&1&k_{1}\\
J&J_{b}&J_{a}\end{Bmatrix}\begin{Bmatrix}1&1&k_{2}\\
J&J_{b}&J_{c}\end{Bmatrix}\begin{Bmatrix}k_{1}&k_{2}&\lambda\\
J&J&J_{b}\end{Bmatrix}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}k_{2}}^{(2)}(J,J_{a},J_{c})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(k_{1},k_{2})\begin{pmatrix}1&1&k_{1}\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1&k_{2}\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{Bmatrix}1&1&k_{1}\\
J&J&J_{a}\end{Bmatrix}\begin{Bmatrix}1&1&k_{2}\\
J&J&J_{c}\end{Bmatrix}\,,

(36)

with the notation
(\lambda,k_{1},k_{2})=(2\lambda+1)(2k_{1}+1)(2k_{2}+1)

(k_{1},k_{2})

According to the properties of the

symbol, the permissible values for

and

) are 0 and 2, while the permissible values for

Also using the following formulas:

\displaystyle(-1)^{J-M_{J}}\begin{pmatrix}J&J&0\\
-M_{J}&M_{J}&0\end{pmatrix}=(-1)^{2J}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2J+1}}\,,

(37)

\displaystyle(-1)^{J-M_{J}}\begin{pmatrix}J&J&2\\

(38)

(39)

the fourth-order energy shift

\displaystyle\Delta E_{4}=\Delta E_{4}^{(1)}+\Delta E_{4}^{(2)}=-\frac{\varepsilon^{4}}{24}[\gamma^{(1)}+\gamma^{(2)}]\,,

(40)

(41)

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma^{(1)}+\gamma^{(2)}=\gamma_{0}+g_{2}(J,M_{J})\gamma_{2}+[g_{4}(J,M_{J})\gamma_{4}^{(1)}+g_{2}^{2}(J,M_{J})\gamma_{4}^{(2)}]\,,

\displaystyle\gamma_{4}^{(1)}

\displaystyle(-1)^{2J}24\sqrt{\frac{J(2J-1)(J-1)(2J-3)}{(2J+5)(J+2)(2J+3)(J+1)(2J+1)}}\sum\limits_{J_{a}J_{b}J_{c}}\mathcal{G}_{4}^{(1)}(J,J_{a},J_{b},J_{c})\mathcal{T}_{1}(J_{a},J_{b},J_{c})\,.

(48)

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}^{(2)}=-\frac{24}{2J+1}\sum\limits_{J_{a}J_{c}}\mathcal{G}_{00}^{(2)}(J,J_{a},J_{c})\mathcal{T}_{2}(J_{a},J_{c})\,,
