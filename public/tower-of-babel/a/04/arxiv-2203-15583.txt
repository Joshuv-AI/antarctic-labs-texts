The Master Equation in a Bounded Domain with Absorption Journal: Journal of Differential Equations
Luca Di Persio, Matteo Garbelli, Michele Ricciardi

Abstract
We analyze the Master Equation and the convergence problem within Mean Field Games (MFG) theory considering a bounded domain with homogeneous Dirichlet conditions. Concerning the N-players differential game, the player’s dynamic ends when touching the boundary. We analyze the well-posedness of the Master Equation and the regularity of its solutions for a suitable class of parabolic equations. Such results are then exploited to consider the convergence problem of the Nash equilibria towards the Master Equation solution, also proving the convergence of optimal trajectories. Eventually, we apply our findings to solve a toy model related to an optimal liquidation scenario.

Introduction

Mean Field Games (MFG) theory was introduced in 2006 by J.-M. Lasry and P.-L. Lions (
[
55
,
56
,
57
,
58
]
) to describe the asymptotic behaviour of differential games with a large number of players, also called agents. A similar definition was given in the same years by Caines, Huang and Malhamé
[
47
]
. The term
mean field
refers to the highly symmetric form of interaction and the indistinguishability of the players whose number is usually denoted by
N
.

A formal description of this limit problem is obtained by analyzing the so-called
MFG system
, namely a forward-backward PDEs system. The latter has been widely studied, and most of the usual topics as the existence, uniqueness and stability of solutions have been proved in many different frameworks. Conversely, results about the convergence problem, namely when the
N
-players differential game approaches the MFG system, are considerably fewer. In particular, most of the literature mainly considers the case where the players’ state space is the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, hence a periodic framework, or the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{d}\,
.

Nevertheless when dealing with
real world problems
as those related to biological or financial models, see, e.g.,
[
1
]
,
[
15
]
,

[
14
]
,
[
13
]

[
45
]
, boundary conditions become unavoidable characteristics we have to deal with, being obliged to consider non periodic settings in bounded domains.

In this article we study the convergence problem in a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, via
Master Equation
, whose precise definition will be given later, requiring an
absorbing
condition at the boundary
\partial\Omega
, namely we ask for each player to remain in the game as long as his dynamic lies in the interior
\textit{int}(\Omega)
of the domain
\Omega
. Within the latter framework, the dynamic of the player
i
,
1\leq i\leq N
, is described by the following Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE), with initial time
t_{0}>0
and final time
T>t_{0}
:

\begin{cases}dX_{t}^{i,t_{0}}=b(t,X_{t}^{i,t_{0}},\alpha_{t}^{i})dt+\sqrt{2}\sigma(X_{t}^{i,t_{0}})dB_{t}^{i}\\
X_{t_{0}}^{i,t_{0}}=x_{0}^{i}.\end{cases}

(1)

\alpha^{i}_{t}
being the control of the player
i
, i.e. a progressively measurable process taking values in a certain set
A
, while
b:[0,T]\times\Omega\times A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the
drift
function and
\sigma:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
represents the
diffusion matrix
. Henceforth, for simplicity, we will shortly write
X_{t}^{i}\,.

The process
X_{\cdot}^{i}
is Markovian providing standard hypotheses on the drift and the coefficient terms. Therefore, we are allowed to define the hitting time
\tau
at
\partial\Omega
in
[0,T]
as

\tau(X_{\cdot}^{i})=\inf\Big\{t\in[t_{0},T]\,|\,X_{t}^{i}\in\partial\Omega\Big\}\wedge T\,,

(2)

where
a\wedge b:=\min\{a,b\}
and we abbreviate
\tau(X_{t}^{i})
by
\tau^{i}
, whenever no confusion is possible.

From now on, we indicate a vector of
\mathbb{R}^{Nd}
with
\bm{v}:=(v_{1},\dots,v_{N})
, with
v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
i\in\{1,...,N\}
. A
cost functional
is associated with each player
i
, which chooses his own strategy in order to minimize it. To obtain a convergence result, we have to require the cost functional to have a symmetric structure. Namely, we assume that the cost for the player
i
depends on both its position
x^{i}
and on the other players’ empirical distribution, defined as follows:

{m}_{\textbf{x}}^{N,i}=\dfrac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\delta_{x_{j}}\mathds{1}_{\{x_{j}\in\mathop{\textit{}}{int}(\Omega)\}}\,,

(3)

where
\delta_{x}
is the Dirac mass at
x
. The indicator function specifies
that we are interested only in those players in the interior of
\Omega
. Let us underline that the presence of such an indicator function slightly modifies the classic theoretical setting of the problem: for the rest of the paper,
{m}_{\textbf{x}}^{N,i}
belongs to
\mathcal{P}^{sub}
, the space of subprobability measure, as in-depth analyzed in Section
2.2
.

Consequently, the cost functional is defined in the following way

J_{i}^{N}(t_{0},\textbf{x}_{0},\bm{\alpha})=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t_{0}}^{\tau^{i}}\left(L\left(s,X_{s}^{i},{\alpha}_{s}^{i}\right)+F\left(s,X_{s}^{i},{m}_{\textbf{X}_{s}}^{N,i}\right)\right)ds+G\left(X_{\tau^{i}}^{i},{m}_{\textbf{x}}^{N,i}\right)\right],

(4)

where
L
,
F
and
G
are, respectively, the Lagrangian term, the running cost and the final cost to pay at the hitting time, or at the final time when no boundary hitting happens.

Each player wishes to minimize its cost functional, which also depends on the strategies of the other players. The set of strategies
\bm{\alpha^{*}}
which allows the agents to play their optimal strategy, in relation to the other agents, is called
Nash equilibrium
. Namely,

J_{i}^{N}\big(t_{0},x_{0},\bm{\alpha^{*}}\big)\leq J_{i}^{N}\big(t_{0},x_{0},\alpha_{i},(\alpha^{*}_{j})_{j\neq i}\big)\,.

We define
v^{N}_{i}(t,\bm{x}):=J^{N}_{i}(t,\bm{x},\bm{\alpha}^{*})
. Exploiting both the Itô-Doeblin formula and the dynamic programming principle, we have that
\bm{\alpha^{*}}
is a Nash equilibrium if
v^{N}_{i}
solves a coupled system of parabolic equations, traditionally called
Nash system
. Namely, for
(t,\bm{x})\in(0,T)\times\Omega^{N}\,
and for
i=1,...,N
, we have

\begin{cases}\displaystyle-\partial_{t}v_{i}^{N}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{tr}\big(a(x_{j})D^{2}_{x_{j}x_{j}}v_{i}^{N}\big)+H(t,x_{i},D_{x_{i}}v_{i}^{N})\\
\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}H_{p}(t,x_{j},D_{x_{j}}v_{j}^{N})\cdot D_{x_{j}}v_{i}^{N}=F(t,x_{i},m_{\textbf{x}}^{N,i})\,,\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\\
\displaystyle v_{i}^{N}(T,\textbf{x})=G(x_{i},{m}_{\textbf{x}}^{N,i})\,,\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\\
\displaystyle v^{N}_{i}(t,\textbf{x})_{|x_{i}\in\partial\Omega}=0\,,\forall t\in[0,T]\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\\
\end{cases}

(5)

H(t,x,p)=\sup\limits_{\alpha\in A}(-b(t,x,\alpha)\cdot p-L(t,x,\alpha))
being the Hamiltonian of the system, and with
H_{p}
its derivative in
p
and
a=\sigma\sigma^{*}
.

Knowing the initial distribution, independently from the individual starting position on
\partial\Omega
, implies that these
players suddenly stop their dynamic at the initial time, hence making no contribution to the evolution of other players. Therefore, for all
i,j
, the following equation holds:

v^{N}_{i}(t,x_{1},\,...\,,x_{j-1},x,x_{j+1},\,...\,,x_{N})=v^{N}_{i}(t,x_{1},\,...\,,x_{j-1},y,x_{j+1},\,...\,,x_{N})\,,

(6)

for all
x,y\in\partial\Omega
. Hence, the value function
v^{N}_{i}
does not depend on
x_{j}
when
x_{j}\in\partial\Omega
.
Roughly speaking, the degrees of freedom of the solution
v^{N}_{i}
decreases from
N
to
N-(\textit{number of players on }\partial\Omega)
. This fact has a direct correspondence also in the dynamic of the game in terms of a leak of mass in the Fokker-Planck equation, as we will see in (
10
) and in the
\mathcal{P}^{sub}
formulation of the Master Equation w.r.t. to a
new
boundary condition as proved in Corollary
12
.

It is possible to provide a suitable approximation for the asymptotic configuration when
N\to+\infty
by introducing the MFG system. Roughly speaking, a generic player behaves according to the following dynamic:

\begin{cases}dX_{t}=b(X_{t},\alpha_{t})\,dt+\sqrt{2}\sigma(X_{t})\,dB_{t}\,,\\
X_{t_{0}}=x_{0}\,,\end{cases}

and the cost functional is given by
(
here
\tau=\tau(X_{\cdot})
)

J(t_{0},x_{0},\alpha_{\cdot})=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t_{0}}^{\tau}\big(L(s,X_{s},\alpha_{s})+F(X_{s},m(s))\big)\,ds+G(X_{\tau},m(\tau))\right]\,.

The solution of a MFG represents the analogous to a Nash equilibrium for a non-cooperative game, see
[
16
,
18
,
23
,
55
,
56
]
for more details. Moreover, the optimal strategies in the MFG system provide approximated
\varepsilon
-Nash equilibria in the Nash system, as proved, e.g., in
[
22
,
48
,
50
]
.
However, the lack of compactness properties of the problem creates many difficulties in the “very" convergence proof, i.e. in proving the convergence of the Nash equilibria in the
N
-players game towards the optimal strategies of the MFG system.

In order to handle this problem, Lasry and Lions in
[
57
]
proved that the solutions of the Mean Field Games are just the trajectories of a non-local transport equation, called the
Master Equation
, in the space of measures. In particular, if
(t,x,m)\in(0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathcal{P}^{sub}(\Omega)
, then the Master Equation is defined as follows:

\begin{split}\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&-\,\partial_{t}U(t,x,m)-\mathrm{tr}\left(a(x)D_{x}^{2}U(t,x,m)\right)+H\left(t,x,D_{x}U(t,x,m)\right)\\
&\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{tr}\left(a(y)D_{y}D_{m}U(t,x,m,y)\right)dm(y)\\
&\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega}D_{m}U(t,x,m,y)\cdot H_{p}(t,y,D_{x}U(t,y,m))dm(y)=F(x,m)\\
&\hskip 136.5733pt\mbox{in }[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathcal{P}^{sub}(\Omega)\hskip 2.27626pt,\\
&U(T,x,m)=G(x,m)\hskip 34.14322pt\mbox{in }\Omega\times\mathcal{P}^{sub}(\Omega)\hskip 2.27626pt,\\
&\displaystyle U(t,x,m)=0\hskip 51.21504pt\quad\,\,\mbox{for }(t,x,m)\in[0,T]\times\partial\Omega\times\mathcal{P}^{sub}(\Omega)\,,\\
&\displaystyle\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(t,x,m,y)=0\hskip 51.21504pt\mbox{for }(t,x,m,y)\in[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathcal{P}^{sub}(\Omega)\times\partial\Omega\,,\end{array}\right.\end{split}

(7)

where
D_{m}U
and
\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}
are two derivatives, considered with respect to the measure variable
m
, whose precise definition will be given later.

Furthermore, when we consider the limiting procedure by taking the number of agents
N
to
+\infty
, the solution of the Nash system will converge to the solution of the Master equation, as proved by Cardaliguet, Delarue, Lasry and Lions in
[
18
]
for the periodic setting (
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{d}
).

For the sake of completeness, let us underline that
our formulation of (
7
),
is typically referred to as
First order Master Equation
, or
Master Equation without common noise
, appearing when the dynamic of the generic player has the form (
1
). Besides, in the
Second order Master Equation
, or
Master Equation with common noise
, the dynamic of the generic player presents an additional Brownian term
dW_{t}
, common to all the players. In the latter case, the Master Equation presents other terms depending on
D_{mm}U
, a second order derivative of
U
with respect to the measure, and the overall system difficulty significantly grows.

The main contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows:

1.

Existence of the Master Equation solution for a bounded domain with Dirichlet setting
: the solution corresponds to an equilibrium of a (random) flow of sub-probability measures. Here, the main novelty relies upon the study of the Master Equation (
7
) in
\mathcal{P}^{sub}(\Omega)
instead of
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, the space of Borel probability measures in
\Omega
. This particular measure space is essential to handle the dissipation of mass related to the framework of Dirichlet’s boundary conditions. Furthermore, we use specific tools such as the generalized Wasserstein distance introduced in
[
63
]
to deal with subprobability measures.

2.

A new boundary condition in the Master Equation
: we stress the fact that the last boundary condition
\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}=0
in (
7
) is completely new in the literature. It relies on the fact that the Dirichlet boundary condition in the Nash system (
5
) provides a smoothing effect near the boundary while approaching the asymptotic configuration.

The article is basically divided into three parts: first, we study the Master Equation (
7
), proving the existence, uniqueness and regularity of solutions; then we use previously obtained results on the Master Equation to prove the convergence problem, in a suitable sense; while in the third part, we provide an economical application, inspired by the models proposed by
[
20
,
46
,
54
]
. More precisely, in Section
2
, we introduce the basic notations and hypotheses we will need throughout the article, and we state the two main results we want to prove. In Section
3
we give a formal proof for the well-posedness of the Master Equation, which becomes rigorous provided some strong regularity results of the function
U
. In Sections
4
and
5
we state the latter regularity results for
U
: in particular, Section
4
is devoted to the study of the Fokker-Planck equation and the Mean Field Games, whereas in Section
5
we analyze a MFG linearized system which provides a suitable regularity for the function
U
. In Section
6
we derive results about the convergence of the solution and we conclude with
7
, presenting an optimal liquidation toy model.

The function
U
is defined as in (
9
), and various estimates, such as global bounds and global Lipschitz regularity, are established. Notably, one of the key challenges in demonstrating that
U
satisfies (
7
) is establishing its
\mathcal{C}^{1}
continuity with respect to
m
. This step necessitates a meticulous analysis of the linearized mean field game system (refer to
[
18
,
66
]
) to establish strong regularity of
U
in both the spatial and measure variables.

It is worth highlighting that these regularity estimates demand strong regularity not only in the spatial domain but also in the measure variable.

In the spatial domain, the regularity is derived in
[
18
]
through differentiation of the equation with respect to
x
. However, in the case of Dirichlet boundary conditions, as well as in general for any boundary conditions, such methods are not directly applicable. Instead, we obtain these bounds by employing a distinct set of space-time estimates that require careful handling.

Moreover, it’s essential to note that regularity estimates for the Dirichlet parabolic equation necessitate compatibility conditions between initial and boundary data. Unfortunately, these compatibility conditions cannot always be guaranteed within this context. Consequently, we extend the estimates obtained in
[
18
]
through an in-depth investigation of the regularity of solutions to the Fokker-Planck equation.

A noteworthy novelty in this work is the meticulous study of the Fokker-Planck equation and the linearized Mean Field Games system. This study is conducted within negative-order Hölder spaces, and the well-posedness is established via approximation using smooth functions. However, it’s important to emphasize that smooth functions are not dense in the dual of Hölder spaces, as pointed out in
[
49
]
. As a result, we must confine our analysis to appropriate subsets of these dual spaces. Notably, these ideas can be applied also to adjust the Neumann case
[
66
]
.

2
Problem Formulation and Main Results

In the first part of the article, we analyze the well-posedness of the Master Equation defined on
[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathcal{P}^{sub}(\Omega)
with a homogeneous Dirichlet condition on
\partial\Omega
.

Our aim is to find a solution
U:[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathcal{P}^{sub}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
which solves strongly the equation (
7
). Following the classical framework, described in
[
18
]
, for each initial data
(t_{0},m_{0})\in[0,T]\times\mathcal{P}^{sub}(\Omega)
, we consider the MFG system in
[t_{0},T]\times\Omega
with a homogeneous Dirichlet conditions:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle-u_{t}-\mathrm{tr}(a(x)D^{2}u)+H(t,x,Du)=F(t,x,m(t))\,,\\
\displaystyle m_{t}-\sum\limits_{ij}\partial^{2}_{ij}(a_{ij}(x)m)-\mathrm{div}(mH_{p}(t,x,Du))=0\,,\\
m(t_{0})=m_{0},\hskip 51.21504ptu(x,T)=G(x,m(T))\,,\\
\displaystyle{u}_{|\partial\Omega}=0\,,\qquad{m}_{|\partial\Omega}=0\,,\end{cases}

(8)

where, as always, a backward Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation for the value function
u
of the generic player is coupled with a forward Fokker-Planck equation for the density
m
of the population. Then we define

U(t_{0},x,m_{0})=u(t_{0},x)\,,

(9)

and we want to prove that, under certain assumptions,
U
is the solution of the Master Equation (
7
).

First and foremost, we point out that assuming a Dirichlet boundary condition implies the following preliminary estimate on
m
:

\left\Arrowvert m(t)\right\Arrowvert_{L^{1}}\leq 1\qquad\forall\,m_{0}\in\mathcal{P}^{sub}(\Omega)\,.

(10)

This is a classical result, under certain assumptions on
H
(which will be included in our hypotheses), and it was proved, for example, in
Proposition 3.10
of
[
65
]
for
m_{0}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
; by a density argument, it can be easily extended for
m_{0}\in\mathcal{P}^{sub}(\Omega)
.

Hence,
m
can not be represented as a probability measure as it occurs in the periodic setting
[
18
]
or in the Neumann formulation
[
66
]
. This is why we have to work with
m\in\mathcal{P}^{sub}(\Omega)
.

Actually, this is not a strange result: following the stochastic interpretation and the
N
-players game, the agents exit the bounded boundary when they hit
\Omega
and, thus, the corresponding distribution
m_{\bm{x}}^{N,i}
should decrease in terms of mass.

2.1
Hölder spaces

In what follows we briefly recall basic notions about the Banach functions spaces then used throughout the paper, see, e.g.,
[
51
,
66
]
, for more details.

Let
T>0
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the closure of an open, bounded and connected set, with the boundary of class
\mathcal{C}^{2+\alpha}
, for some
\alpha>0
; we denote with
Q_{T}
the set
Q_{T}:=[0,T]\times\Omega
.
We call
d(\cdot)
the oriented distance function from the boundary of
\Omega
, defined as

d(x)=\begin{cases}\,\,\,\ \mathrm{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)&x\in\Omega\,,\\
-\mathrm{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)&x\notin\Omega\,.\end{cases}

Thanks to
[
31
]
, we have
d(\cdot)\in\mathcal{C}^{2+\alpha}
in a neighbourhood of the boundary. Since we are only interested in the local character of
d
near
\partial\Omega
, when we write
d(\cdot)
in this paper, we mean a
\mathcal{C}^{2+\alpha}
function coinciding with
d
in a neighbourhood of the boundary.

For
n\geq 0
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
\mathcal{C}^{n+\alpha}(\Omega)
is defined as the space of functions
n
-times differentiable, with derivatives
\alpha
-Hölder continuous.
Being
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{n+\alpha}(\Omega)
, its norm is defined in the following way:

\displaystyle\left\Arrowvert\phi\right\Arrowvert_{n+\alpha}:=\sum\limits_{|\ell|\leq n}\left\Arrowvert D^{l}\phi\right\Arrowvert_{\infty}+\sum\limits_{|\ell|=n}\sup\limits_{x\neq y}\frac{|D^{\ell}\phi(x)-D^{\ell}\phi(y)|}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\,.

Similarly, the parabolic space
\mathcal{C}^{\frac{n+\alpha}{2},n+\alpha}(Q_{T})
consists of functions
\phi
admitting derivatives
D_{t}^{r}D_{x}^{s}\phi
, with
2r+s\leq n
, and with norm

\displaystyle\left\Arrowvert\phi\right\Arrowvert_{\frac{n+\alpha}{2},n+\alpha}:=\sum\limits_{2r+s\leq n}

\displaystyle\left\Arrowvert D_{t}^{r}D^{s}_{x}\phi\right\Arrowvert_{\infty}+\sum\limits_{2r+s=n}\sup\limits_{t}\left\Arrowvert D_{t}^{r}D_{x}^{s}\phi(t,\cdot)\right\Arrowvert_{\alpha}

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{0<n+\alpha-2r-s<2}\sup\limits_{x}\left\Arrowvert D_{t}^{r}D_{x}^{s}\phi(\cdot,x)\right\Arrowvert_{\frac{n+\alpha-2r-s}{2}}\,.

In order to work with Dirichlet boundary conditions, we define the spaces
\mathcal{C}^{n+\alpha,D}(\Omega)
and
\mathcal{C}^{\frac{n+\alpha}{2},n+\alpha,D}(Q_{T})
as the subspaces of functions belonging to
\mathcal{C}^{n+\alpha}(\Omega)
, resp. to
\mathcal{C}^{\frac{n+\alpha}{2},n+\alpha}(Q_{T})
, vanishing at
\partial\Omega
. When there is no risk of confusion, we will omit the set
\Omega
or
Q_{T}
.

Analogously, we define the spaces
\mathcal{C}^{0,\alpha}
,
\mathcal{C}^{\alpha,0}
,
\mathcal{C}^{1,2+\alpha}
.
For the sake of completeness, also because of its relevance throughout the paper, let us specify the norm equipping the latter functional space:

\left\Arrowvert\phi\right\Arrowvert_{1,2+\alpha}:=\left\Arrowvert\phi\right\Arrowvert_{\infty}+\left\Arrowvert\phi_{t}\right\Arrowvert_{0,\alpha}+\left\Arrowvert D_{x}\phi\right\Arrowvert_{\infty}+\left\Arrowvert D^{2}_{x}\phi\right\Arrowvert_{0,\alpha}\,.

Eventually, we have to work with suitable subsets of the dual spaces of
\mathcal{C}^{n+\alpha}
and
\mathcal{C}^{n+\alpha,D}
. In particular, we denote as
\mathcal{C}^{-(n+\alpha)}
the set

\mathcal{C}^{-(n+\alpha)}:=\left\{\rho\in\left(\mathcal{C}^{(n+\alpha)}\right)^{\prime}\,\Big|\,\langle\rho,\phi\rangle=\sum\limits_{|\gamma|\leq n}\int_{\Omega}\partial^{\gamma}\phi(x)\,\rho_{\gamma}(dx)\quad\forall\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{n+\alpha}\,,\quad{(\rho_{\gamma})}_{\gamma}\mbox{ measures on }\Omega\right\}\,.\\

In the same way we define the set
\mathcal{C}^{-(n+\alpha),D}
and
\mathcal{C}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2},-\alpha}
. The norms on these spaces are inherited by the classical dual spaces norm:

\displaystyle\left\Arrowvert\rho\right\Arrowvert_{-(n+\alpha)}=\sup\limits_{\left\Arrowvert\phi\right\Arrowvert_{n+\alpha}\leq 1}\langle\rho,\phi\rangle\,,\qquad\left\Arrowvert\rho\right\Arrowvert_{-(n+\alpha),D}=\sup\limits_{\left\Arrowvert\phi\right\Arrowvert_{n+\alpha,D}\leq 1}\langle\rho,\phi\rangle\,,

\displaystyle\left\Arrowvert\rho\right\Arrowvert_{-\left(\frac{\alpha}{2},\alpha\right)}=\sup\limits_{\left\Arrowvert\phi\right\Arrowvert_{\frac{\alpha}{2},\alpha}\leq 1}\langle\rho,\phi\rangle\,.

The importance of these subspaces is given by the following result.

Lemma 1
.

The space
\mathcal{C}^{-(n+\alpha)}
is a norm-closed subset of
\left(\mathcal{C}^{n+\alpha}\right)^{\prime}
. Moreover, if
\rho\in\mathcal{C}^{-n}
, then there exist a sequence
\{\rho_{k}\}_{k}\subset\mathcal{C}^{2+\alpha}
such that
\rho_{k}\to\rho
in
\mathcal{C}^{-(n+\alpha)}
and
{\rho_{k}}_{|\partial\Omega}=0
.

Proof.

Thanks to
[
49
, Lemma 2.6]
, we know that
\mathcal{C}^{-(n+\alpha)}
is a norm-closed subset of
\left(\mathcal{C}^{n+\alpha}\right)^{\prime}
. Let
\rho\in\mathcal{C}^{-n}
. Then there exist measures
\{\rho_{\gamma}\}_{|\gamma|\leq n}
such that

\rho=\sum\limits_{|\gamma|\leq n}\partial^{\gamma}\rho_{\gamma}(dx)\,.

Defining
\rho_{\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega)=0
, we can consider
\{\rho_{\gamma}\}_{\gamma}
as measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Let
\{n_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon}
be smooth mollifiers. We define
\rho_{\gamma}^{k}:=\rho_{\gamma}*\eta_{\frac{1}{k}}
and
\tilde{\rho}_{k}:=\sum\limits_{|\gamma|\leq n}\partial^{\gamma}\rho^{k}_{\gamma}(dx)\,.
Thanks to
[
49
, Lemma 2.3, Lemma 2.7]
, we have
\tilde{\rho}_{k}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
and
\tilde{\rho}_{k}\to\rho
in
\mathcal{C}^{-(n+\beta)}
for all
\beta>0
.

Then, we consider a nonnegative smooth function
\xi_{k}(s)\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\xi_{k}(s)=1
for
|s|\geq\frac{1}{k}
and
\xi_{k}(0)=0
. We define
\rho_{k}(x)=\tilde{\rho}_{k}(x)\,\xi_{k}(d(x))
. Then we have
\rho_{k}\in\mathcal{C}^{2+\alpha}
and the convergence to
\rho
in
\mathcal{C}^{-(n+\alpha)}
is preserved.
∎

2.2
Subprobability Measures and Generalized Wasserstein Measure

Let us start the present subsection with a
proper

notion of distance for elements belonging to
\mathcal{P}^{sub}(\Omega)
.
Since the usual Wasserstein distance
W_{p}(\mu,\nu)
is defined only when the two measures
\mu,\nu
have the same mass, in what follows we rely on the so-called
generalized Wasserstein distance
which allows computing distance between measures with different masses, see e.g.
[
63
,
64
]
for a complete description and
[
37
,
68
]
for the corresponding probabilistic interpretation based on unbalanced optimal transport.

According to
[
64
]
, we introduce also the following definition which is the one we will use throughout the article.

Definition 1
.

Let
m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathcal{P}^{sub}(\Omega)
be two Borel sub-probability measures on
\Omega
. We call the generalized Wasserstein distance between
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
, and we write
\mathbf{d}_{1}(m_{1},m_{2})
the quantity

\mathbf{d}_{1}(m_{1},m_{2}):=\sup\limits_{Lip(\phi)\leq 1\,,\,\left\Arrowvert\phi\right\Arrowvert_{C^{0}}\leq 1}\,\int_{\Omega}\phi(x)d(m_{1}-m_{2})(x)\,,

(11)

We remark that
\mathbf{d}_{1}(m_{1},m_{2})
is equivalent to the well-known
flat metric
over the space of Radon measures with finite mass on
\Omega
.

Furthermore, we define a suitable derivation of
U
with respect to the (sub)measure
m
as it appears in the Master Equation (
7
). Namely, we have:

Definition 2
.

Let
U:\mathcal{P}^{sub}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
. We say that
U
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{1}
if there exists a continuous map
K:\mathcal{P}^{sub}(\Omega)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
such that, for all
m_{1}
,
m_{2}\in\mathcal{P}^{sub}(\Omega)
we have

\lim\limits_{s\to 0}\frac{U(m_{1}+s(m_{2}-m_{1}))-U(m_{1})}{s}=\int_{\Omega}K(m_{1},x)(m_{2}(dx)-m_{1}(dx))\,.

(12)

We define
\displaystyle\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(m,x):=K(m,x)
.

Remark 1
.

Condition (
12
) defines uniquely the derivative
K
. Conversely, in the classical setting, where
U:\mathcal{P}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
, see e.g.
[
18
,
66
]
, the function
K
is defined up to an additive constant, since, for
c\in\mathbb{R}
and
m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
,
\int_{\Omega}c\,d(m_{2}-m_{1})=0
. Hence, in those cases a normalization condition is needed to uniquely identify
K
.

Let us note that, exploiting Eq. (
12
), we have the following equality for
m_{1},\,m_{2}\in\mathcal{P}^{sub}(\Omega)
,

U(m_{2})-U(m_{1})=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(m_{1}+s(m_{2}-m_{1}),x)(m_{2}(dx)-m_{1}(dx))ds\,.

which turns out to be very useful in our computations.
Moreover, we note that, if
\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
in the space variable, we have

|U(m_{2})-U(m_{1})|\leq\sup\limits_{m}\left\Arrowvert D_{x}\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(m,\cdot)\right\Arrowvert_{\infty}\mathbf{d}_{1}(m_{1},m_{2})\,,

suggesting us define the
intrinsic derivative
of
U
with respect to
m
, which directly appears in the Master Equation (
7
).

Definition 3
.

Let
U:\mathcal{P}^{sub}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
. If
U
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{1}
and
\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{1}
with respect to the last variable, we define the intrinsic derivative
D_{m}U:\mathcal{P}^{sub}(\Omega)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
as

D_{m}U(m,x):=D_{x}\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(m,x)\,.

2.3
Assumptions

We require the following hypotheses throughout the paper:

Hypotheses 1

Let

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\mathrm{Lip}\left(\frac{\delta G}{\delta m}\right):=\\

3
Well-posedness of the Master Equation

A formal proof of existence and uniqueness of solutions for (
7
) can be easily given directly using the very definition of
U
. The difficulty here relies on the proof of the
\mathcal{C}^{1}
character of
U
with respect to
m
. First, we establish the required regularity for
U
and a related compatibility condition based on the boundary conditions. The existence and uniqueness theorem are rigorously proved, as we see in detail in the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose hypotheses
1
are satisfied. Take
U
defined as in (
9
), suppose that
\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}
exists, being also bounded in
\mathcal{C}^{2+\alpha}_{x}\times C^{2+\alpha}_{y}
, uniformly in
t
and continuously in
m
, and that boundary conditions of (
7
) hold for
U
. Then
U
is the unique classical solution
U
of the Master Equation (
7
).

Proof.

Existence
. By a density argument, we only need to prove that
U
solves (
7
) at
(t_{0},x,m_{0}
), where
m_{0}
is a smooth and positive function satisfying
{m_{0}}_{|\partial\Omega}=0
and
\int_{\Omega}m_{0}(x)dx\leq 1
.
Taking
(u,m)
as the solution of the
MFG
system starting from
m_{0}
at time
t_{0}
, by the definition of
U
, we have that for all
s>t_{0}

U(s,x,m(s))=u(s,x)\,,

therefore, we can write

\partial_{t}U(t_{0},x,m_{0})=\lim\limits_{h\to 0}\frac{U(t_{0}+h,x,m_{0})-U(t_{0}+h,x,m(t_{0}+h))}{h}+u_{t}(t_{0},x)\,.

Again by the very definition of
U
:

\displaystyle u_{t}(t_{0},x)=-\mathrm{tr}(a(x)D^{2}_{x}U(t_{0},x,m_{0}))+H(t_{0},x,D_{x}U(t_{0},x,m_{0}))-F(t_{0},x,m_{0})\,.

As regards the limit part, we define
m_{s}:=(1-s)m(t_{0})+sm(t_{0}+h)
. Since
U
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
with respect to
m
, then:

\displaystyle-\lim\limits_{h\to 0}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(t_{0}+h,x,m_{s},y)\frac{(m(t_{0}+h,y)-m(t_{0},y))}{h}\,dyds=

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(t_{0},x,m_{0},y)\Bigg(\sum\limits_{ij}\partial^{2}_{ij}(a_{ij}(x)m(t_{0},y))

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt+\mathrm{div}\big(m(t_{0},y)H_{p}(t_{0},y,Du(t_{0},y))\big)\Bigg)dy\,.

Integrating by parts, and using the boundary conditions of
\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}
and
m
, we can rewrite the right-hand side as

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Bigg[H_{p}(t_{0},y,D_{x}U(t_{0},y,m_{0}))D_{m}U(t_{0},x,m_{0},y)

\displaystyle\hskip 170.71652pt-\mathrm{tr}\Big(a(y)D_{y}D_{m}U(t_{0},x,m_{0},y)\Big)\Bigg]dm_{0}(y)\,,

therefore

\displaystyle\partial_{t}U(t,x,m)=-\mathrm{tr}\left(a(x)D_{x}^{2}U(t,x,m)\right)+H\left(t,x,D_{x}U(t,x,m)\right)

\displaystyle\quad-{\int_{\Omega}}\mathrm{tr}\left(a(y)D_{y}D_{m}U(t,x,m,y)\right)dm(y)

\displaystyle\quad+{\int_{\Omega}}D_{m}U(t,x,m,y)\cdot H_{p}(t,y,D_{x}U(t,y,m))dm(y)-F(t,x,m)\,,

hence concluding the existence part.

Uniqueness
. Suppose that there exists a second solution
V
of (
7
). We fix
(t_{0},m_{0})
, with
m_{0}
smooth s.t.
{m_{0}}_{|\partial\Omega}=0
, and we take
\tilde{m}
as the solution of

\begin{cases}\tilde{m}_{t}-\sum\limits_{ij}\partial^{2}_{ij}(a_{ij}(x)\tilde{m})-\mathrm{div}\big(\tilde{m}H_{p}(t,x,D_{x}V(t,x,\tilde{m}))\big)=0\,,\\
\tilde{m}(t_{0})=m_{0}\,,\\
{\tilde{m}}_{|\partial\Omega}=0\,\end{cases}

which is well-posed since
D_{x}V
is Lipschitz continuous with respect to the measure. Then we define
\tilde{u}(t,x)=V(t,x,\tilde{m}(t))
and we want to prove that
\tilde{u}
solves a Hamilton-Jacobi equation. Using the equations of
V
and
\tilde{m}
, we get

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{t}(t,x)=

\displaystyle\,V_{t}(t,x,\tilde{m}(t))+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta V}{\delta m}(t,x,\tilde{m}(t),y)\,\tilde{m}_{t}(t,y)\,dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,V_{t}(t,x,\tilde{m}(t))+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta V}{\delta m}(t,x,\tilde{m}(t),y)\,\sum\limits_{ij}\partial^{2}_{ij}(a_{ij}(x)\tilde{m}(t,y))\,dy

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\,\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta V}{\delta m}(t,x,\tilde{m}(t),y)\,\mathrm{div}\big(\tilde{m}H_{p}(t,x,D_{x}V(t,x,\tilde{m}))\big)\,dy\,.

Previous integrals can be easily estimated with an integration by parts, and taking into account the boundary condition for
V
. As regards the first, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta V}{\delta m}(t,x,\tilde{m}(t),y)\,\sum\limits_{ij}\partial^{2}_{ij}(a_{ij}(x)\tilde{m}(t,y))\,dy=\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt

\displaystyle\qquad\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{tr}(a(y)D_{y}D_{m}V(t,x,\tilde{m}(t),y))\,\tilde{m}(t,y)\,dy\,,

while for the second

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta V}{\delta m}(t,x,\tilde{m}(t),y)\,\mathrm{div}\big(\tilde{m}H_{p}(t,x,D_{x}V(t,x,\tilde{m}))\big)\,dy\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt

\displaystyle\qquad=-\int_{\Omega}H_{p}(t,x,D_{x}V(t,x,\tilde{m}))D_{m}V(t,x,\tilde{m},y)\tilde{m}(t,y)dy\,.

Previous estimates allow us to write

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{t}(t,x)=

\displaystyle\,V_{t}(t,x,\tilde{m}(t))+\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{tr}(a(y)D_{y}D_{m}V(t,x,\tilde{m},y))\,d\tilde{m}(y)\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt

\displaystyle\qquad-\,\int_{\Omega}H_{p}(t,y,D_{x}V(t,y,\tilde{m}))D_{m}V(t,x,\tilde{m},y)\,d\tilde{m}(y)\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,-\mathrm{tr}(a(x)D^{2}\tilde{u}(t,x))+H(t,x,D\tilde{u}(t,x))-F(t,x,\tilde{m}(t))\,,\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt

hence
(\tilde{u},\tilde{m})
is a solution of the MFG system (
8
). Then, by uniqueness of solutions for the MFG system, we get
(\tilde{u},\tilde{m})=(u,m)
, implying that
V(t_{0},x,m_{0})=U(t_{0},x,m_{0})
, whenever
m_{0}
is smooth. Therefore, by density, the uniqueness is proved.
∎

In the next two sections, we will prove the
\mathcal{C}^{1}
character of
U

m

\mathcal{C}^{2}

and
y
of

and the boundary conditions for

, hence justify the hypotheses previously given, also allowing us to then apply Theorem
3
and finally prove the well-posedness of (
7
).

The present section is focused on the study of the following Fokker-Planck equation

\begin{cases}\mu_{t}-\mathrm{div}(a(x)D\mu)-\mathrm{div}(\mu b)=\mathrm{div}(c)\,,\\
\mu(t_{0})=\mu_{0}\,,\\
\mu_{|\partial\Omega}=0\,,\end{cases}

where

,
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{C}^{-(1+\alpha)}
, and

Let us underline that the latter equation plays a fundamental role
in studying both the Mean Field Games system and the linearized one.

The main difficulty here relies on low regularity of both data and coefficients. Hence, the main idea is to start with the regular case, where we have the existence and uniqueness of solutions,
to then obtain estimates that we will then exploit to pass to the limit in the general case.

Let us start by giving a suitable definition of a solution for the system (
16
):

Definition 4
.

Let
c\in L^{1}
,
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{C}^{-1}
,

\begin{cases}-\phi_{t}-\mathrm{div}(aD\phi)+bD\phi=\psi\,,\\
\phi(t)=\xi\,,\\
\phi_{|\partial\Omega}=0\,,\end{cases}

(17)

with
\psi\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
\xi\in\mathcal{C}^{1+\alpha,D}
, it holds true:

\langle\mu(t),\xi\rangle+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\mu(s,x)\psi(s,x)\,dxds=\langle\mu_{0},\phi(t_{0},\cdot)\rangle-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\int_{\Omega}c(s,x)\cdot D\phi(s,x)\,dxds\,,

(18)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes, respectively, the duality between
\mathcal{C}^{-(1+\alpha),D}
and
\mathcal{C}^{1+\alpha,D}
,
\mathcal{C}^{-(1+\alpha)}
and
\mathcal{C}^{1+\alpha}
.

Note that Lemma

implies

Let

,

\displaystyle\hskip 34.14322pt\displaystyle\leq C\left\Arrowvert\xi\right\Arrowvert_{1+\alpha}\left(\left\Arrowvert\mu_{0}\right\Arrowvert_{-(1+\alpha)}+\left\Arrowvert c\right\Arrowvert_{L^{1}}\right)\,.

4.2
Mean Field Games system and Lipschitz regularity of U

Now, we turn out to the study of the Mean Field Games system and its properties to derive essential results we will then exploit to obtain some first regularity estimates of the solution
U
of the Master Equation.

In particular, the MFG system has the following form:

\begin{cases}-\partial_{t}u-\mathrm{tr}(a(x)D^{2}u)+H(t,x,Du)=F(t,x,m(t))\,,\\
\partial_{t}m-\sum\limits_{i,j}\partial_{ij}^{2}(a_{ij}(x)m)-\mathrm{div}(mH_{p}(t,x,Du))=0\,,\\
u(T)=G(x,m(T))\,,\qquad m(0)=m_{0}\,,\\
u_{|\partial\Omega=0}\,,\qquad m_{|\partial\Omega=0}\,.\end{cases}

(25)

The first result is obtained by studying some regularity properties of (
25
) uniformly in
m_{0}
.

Proposition 7
.

The system (
25
) has a unique classical solution
(u,m)
, with
u\in\mathcal{C}^{1+\frac{\alpha}{2},2+\alpha}
and
m\in\mathcal{C}([t_{0},T];\mathcal{P}^{sub}(\Omega))

has a positive density for

, and the following estimates hold:

\left\Arrowvert m\right\Arrowvert_{\frac{\alpha}{2},-(1+\alpha),D}+\left\Arrowvert m\right\Arrowvert_{L^{p}}+\left\Arrowvert u\right\Arrowvert_{1+\frac{\alpha}{2},2+\alpha}\leq C\,.

(26)

Furthermore, if

(27)

Proof.

Step 1: Existence and uniqueness of solutions.
We want to apply Schauder’s fixed point Theorem. Let

For

\begin{cases}-u_{t}-\mathrm{tr}(a(x)D^{2}u)+H(t,x,Du)=F(t,x,\gamma(t))\,,\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\

Using hypotheses on

and

of
[

. Moreover, by Taylor formula, we can write

for a certain

, thanks to the global boundedness of

, obtaining a linear equation satisfied by

.
Then, exploiting both the Corollary of

\displaystyle\left\Arrowvert u\right\Arrowvert_{1+\frac{\alpha}{2},2+\alpha}\leq C\left(\left\Arrowvert F(\cdot,\cdot,\gamma(\cdot))\right\Arrowvert_{\frac{\alpha}{2},\alpha}+\left\Arrowvert G(\cdot,\gamma(T))\right\Arrowvert_{2+\alpha}\right)\,,

\Phi(\beta)=m
, where

\begin{cases}m_{t}-\sum\limits_{i,j}\partial_{ij}^{2}(a_{ij}(x)m)-\mathrm{div}(mH_{p}(t,x,Du))=0\,,\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\

\displaystyle c_{2}(t)=m_{1}\int_{0}^{1}\left(H_{pp}(t,x,Du_{1+s})-H_{pp}(t,x,Du_{2})\right)(Du_{1}-Du_{2})\,ds\,,\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt

\displaystyle z_{T}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\delta G}{\delta m}(x,m_{1+s}(T),y)-\frac{\delta G}{\delta m}(x,m_{2}(T),y)\right)(m_{1}(T)-m_{2}(T))(dy)ds\,.\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt

Applying (
42
) one has

\left\Arrowvert z\right\Arrowvert_{1,2+\alpha}+\sup\limits_{t\in[0,T]}\left\Arrowvert\rho(t)\right\Arrowvert_{-(1+\alpha),D}\leq C\left(\left\Arrowvert h\right\Arrowvert_{0,\alpha}+\left\Arrowvert c\right\Arrowvert_{L^{1}}+\left\Arrowvert z_{T}\right\Arrowvert_{2+\alpha}\right)\,.

(49)

We want to estimate the right-hand side term to obtain the desired Taylor expansion. As regards

, exploiting
eq. (

) and Hölder norm properties, we have:

\displaystyle\left\Arrowvert h_{1}\right\Arrowvert_{0,\alpha}

\displaystyle=\left\Arrowvert\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}s\,\langle H_{pp}(t,x,Du_{1+rs})\,(Du_{1}-Du_{2})\,,\,(Du_{1}-Du_{2})\rangle\,drds\right\Arrowvert_{0,\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbf{d}_{1}(m_{01},m_{02})^{2}\,,

Analogously, again by eq. (

) and exploiting regularity of both

and

, the same estimate also holds for

and
z_{T}
.
For the function

, we can write

\displaystyle\left\Arrowvert c_{1}\right\Arrowvert_{L^{1}}=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}H_{pp}(t,x,Du_{2})(Du_{1}-Du_{2})(m_{1}(t,dx)-m_{2}(t,dx))\,dt

\displaystyle\leq C\left\Arrowvert u_{1}-u_{2}\right\Arrowvert_{1,2+\alpha}\left\Arrowvert m_{1}(t)-m_{2}(t)\right\Arrowvert\leq C\mathbf{d}_{1}(m_{01},m_{02})^{2}\,,

then, proceeding as above, we have

\displaystyle\left\Arrowvert c_{2}\right\Arrowvert_{L^{1}}=

\displaystyle\qquad\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(H_{pp}(t,x,Du_{1+s})-H_{pp}(t,x,Du_{2})\right)(Du_{1}-Du_{2})m_{1}(t,dx)\,dtds

\displaystyle\quad\leq\,C\left\Arrowvert Du_{1}-Du_{2}\right\Arrowvert_{\infty}^{2}\leq C\mathbf{d}_{1}(m_{0}^{1},m_{0}^{2})^{2}\,.

Substituting these estimates in (
49
), we obtain (
48
). Using the representation (
45
) for

, we get

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\left\Arrowvert U(t_{0},\cdot,m_{01})-U(t_{0},\cdot,m_{02})-\int_{\Omega}K(t_{0},\cdot,m_{02},y)(m_{01}-m_{02})(dy)\right\Arrowvert_{\infty}\\
\displaystyle\hskip 199.16928pt\leq C\mathbf{d}_{1}(m_{01},m_{02})^{2}\,.\end{array}

As a straightforward consequence, we have that

is differentiable with respect to

and

\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(t,x,m,y)=K(t,x,m,y)\,,

hence concluding the proof.
∎

Using (
46

\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}
:

\sup\limits_{t}\left\Arrowvert\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(t,\cdot,m,\cdot)\right\Arrowvert_{2+\alpha,2+\alpha}\leq C\,.

(50)

This gives sense to the Master Equation (

), since the quantity
D_{y}D_{m}U
is now well-defined. However, to apply Theorem

we still need to prove the continuity of

in the measure variable.

In the next result we prove a Lipschitz bound for
\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}
, with respect to the measure

. This bound plays a key role, since not only it implies the continuity of

with respect to

Theorem 11
.

Let Assumptions
1
hold. Then the derivative of the solution of the Master Equation
\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}
is Lipschitz continuous with respect to the measure
m
:

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{{m}_{1}\neq{m}_{2}}(\mathbf{d}_{1}({m}_{1},{m}_{2}))^{-1}\Bigg|\Bigg|\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(t,\cdot,{m}_{1},\cdot)-\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(t,\cdot,{m}_{2},\cdot)\Bigg|\Bigg|_{2+\alpha,2+\alpha}\,\leq\,C

(51)

where
C
depends on
n
,
F
,
G
,
H
and
T
.

Proof.

We consider, for
i=1,2
, the solution
(v_{i},\mu_{i})
of the linearized system (
40
) related to
(u_{i},m_{i})\,.

To avoid too heavy notations, we take
t_{0}=0
and we define

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}H^{\prime}_{i}(t,x):=H_{p}(t,x,Du_{i}(t,x))\,,&H^{\prime\prime}_{i}(t,x)=H_{pp}(t,x,Du_{i}(t,x))\,,\\
F^{\prime}(t,x,m,\mu)=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta F}{\delta m}(t,x,m,y)\,\mu(dy)\,,&G^{\prime}(x,m,\mu)=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta G}{\delta m}(x,m,y)\,\mu(dy)\,.\end{array}

Then the couple
(z,\rho):=(v_{1}-v_{2},\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})
satisfies the following linear system:

\begin{cases}-z_{t}-\mathrm{tr}(a(x)D^{2}z)+H^{\prime}_{1}\cdot Dz=F^{\prime}(t,x,m_{1}(t),\rho(t))+h\,,\\
\rho_{t}-\sum\limits_{i,j}\partial^{2}_{ij}(a_{ij}(x)\rho)-\mathrm{div}(\rho H^{\prime}_{1})-\mathrm{div}(m_{1}H^{\prime\prime}_{1}Dz+c)=0\,,\\
z(T,x)=G^{\prime}(x,m_{1}(T),\rho(T))+z_{T}\,,\qquad\rho(t_{0})=0\,,\\
z_{|\partial\Omega}=0\,,\qquad\rho_{|\partial\Omega}=0\,,\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle h(t,x)=h_{1}(t,x)+h_{2}(t,x)\,,

\displaystyle h_{1}(t,x)=F^{\prime}(t,x,m_{1}(t),\mu_{2}(t))-F^{\prime}(t,x,m_{2}(t),\mu_{2}(t))\,,

\displaystyle h_{2}(t,x)=\big(H^{\prime}_{1}(t,x)-H^{\prime}_{2}(t,x)\big)\cdot Dv_{2}(t,x)\,,

\displaystyle c(t,x)=\mu_{2}(t)\big(H^{\prime}_{1}-H^{\prime}_{2})(t,x)+\big[(m_{1}H^{\prime\prime}_{1}-m_{2}H^{\prime\prime}_{2})\big](t,x)\,,

\displaystyle z_{T}(x)=G^{\prime}(x,m_{1}(T),\mu_{2}(T))-G^{\prime}(x,m_{2}(T),\mu_{2}(T))\,.

Applying (
42
) we obtain this estimate on
z
:

\left\Arrowvert z\right\Arrowvert_{1,2+\alpha}\leq C\left(\left\Arrowvert z_{T}\right\Arrowvert_{2+\alpha}+\left\Arrowvert h\right\Arrowvert_{0,\alpha}+\left\Arrowvert c\right\Arrowvert_{L^{1}}\right)\,.

Now we estimate the terms in the right-hand side.

The term with
z_{T}
, thanks to (
44
) and the hypothesis
(v)
of
1
, is immediately estimated:

\left\Arrowvert z_{T}\right\Arrowvert_{2+\alpha}\leq\left\Arrowvert\frac{\delta G}{\delta m}(\cdot,m_{1}(T),\cdot)-\frac{\delta G}{\delta m}(\cdot,m_{2}(T),\cdot)\right\Arrowvert_{2+\alpha,2+\alpha}\left\Arrowvert\mu_{2}(T)\right\Arrowvert_{-(2+\alpha),D}\leq C\mathbf{d}_{1}(m_{01},m_{02})\left\Arrowvert\mu_{0}\right\Arrowvert_{-(2+\alpha)}\,.

As regards the space estimate for
h
, we have

\left\Arrowvert h(t,\cdot)\right\Arrowvert_{\alpha}\leq\left\Arrowvert F^{\prime}(\cdot,m_{1}(t),\mu_{2}(t))-F^{\prime}(\cdot,m_{2}(t),\mu_{2}(t))\right\Arrowvert_{\alpha}+\left\Arrowvert(H^{\prime}_{1}-H^{\prime}_{2})(t,\cdot)Dv_{2}(t,\cdot)\right\Arrowvert_{\alpha}\,.

The first term is bounded as
z_{T}:

\left\Arrowvert F^{\prime}(\cdot,m_{1}(t),\mu_{2}(t))-F^{\prime}(\cdot,m_{2}(t),\mu_{2}(t))\right\Arrowvert_{\alpha}\leq C\mathbf{d}_{1}(m_{01},m_{02})\left\Arrowvert\mu_{0}\right\Arrowvert_{-(2+\alpha)}\,.

The second term, using (
27
) and (
44
), can be estimated in this way:

\left\Arrowvert(H^{\prime}_{1}-H^{\prime}_{2})(t,\cdot)Dv_{2}(t,\cdot)\right\Arrowvert_{\alpha}\leq C\left\Arrowvert(u_{1}-u_{2})(t)\right\Arrowvert_{1+\alpha}\left\Arrowvert v_{2}(t)\right\Arrowvert_{1+\alpha}\leq C\mathbf{d}_{1}(m_{01},m_{02})\left\Arrowvert\mu_{0}\right\Arrowvert_{-(2+\alpha)}\,.

In summary,

\left\Arrowvert h\right\Arrowvert_{0,\alpha}=\sup\limits_{t\in[0,T]}\left\Arrowvert h(t,\cdot)\right\Arrowvert_{\alpha}\leq C\mathbf{d}_{1}(m_{01},m_{02})\left\Arrowvert\mu_{0}\right\Arrowvert_{-(2+\alpha)}\,.

Finally, we estimate
\left\Arrowvert c\right\Arrowvert_{L^{1}}
. We have

\displaystyle\left\Arrowvert c\right\Arrowvert_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(H^{\prime}_{1}-H^{\prime}_{2})(t,x)\,\mu_{2}(t,dx)\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}H^{\prime\prime}_{1}(t,x)Dv_{2}(t,x)(m_{1}(t)-m_{2}(t))(dx)\,dt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(H^{\prime}_{1}-H^{\prime}_{2})(t,x)\,Dv_{2}(t,x)\,m_{2}(t,dx)\,dt\leq C\left\Arrowvert u_{1}-u_{2}\right\Arrowvert_{\frac{1+\alpha}{2},1+\alpha}\left\Arrowvert\mu_{2}\right\Arrowvert_{-\left(\frac{\alpha}{2},\alpha\right)}

\displaystyle+C\left\Arrowvert u_{1}\right\Arrowvert_{\frac{1+\alpha}{2},1+\alpha}\left\Arrowvert v_{2}\right\Arrowvert_{\frac{1+\alpha}{2},1+\alpha}\left\Arrowvert m_{1}-m_{2}\right\Arrowvert_{L^{1}}+C\left\Arrowvert u_{1}-u_{2}\right\Arrowvert_{\frac{1+\alpha}{2},1+\alpha}\left\Arrowvert v_{2}\right\Arrowvert_{\frac{1+\alpha}{2},1+\alpha}\,.

The first term in the right-hand side, thanks to (
27
) and (

), is bounded by

C\left\Arrowvert u_{1}-u_{2}\right\Arrowvert_{\frac{1+\alpha}{2},1+\alpha}\left\Arrowvert\mu_{2}\right\Arrowvert_{-\left(\frac{\alpha}{2},\alpha\right)}\leq C\mathbf{d}_{1}(m_{01},m_{02})\left\Arrowvert\mu_{0}\right\Arrowvert_{-(2+\alpha)}\,.

The second and the third term are estimated in the same way, using (

), (
27
) and (

). Then

\left\Arrowvert c\right\Arrowvert_{L^{1}}\leq C\mathbf{d}_{1}(m_{01},m_{02})\left\Arrowvert\mu_{0}\right\Arrowvert_{-(2+\alpha)}\,.

Putting together all these estimates, we finally obtain:

\left\Arrowvert z\right\Arrowvert_{1,2+\alpha}\leq C\mathbf{d}_{1}(m_{01},m_{02})\left\Arrowvert\mu_{0}\right\Arrowvert_{-(2+\alpha)}\,.

Since

z(t_{0},x)=\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(t_{0},x,m_{1},y)-\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(t_{0},x,m_{2},y)\right)\,\mu_{0}(dy)\,,

we have proved (

) .
∎

We stress the fact that in the bound (

.

To conclude, we are still left proving the boundary condition for

in (

) to make true all the hypotheses needed to apply Theorem

. The latter will be the last result of this section.

Corollary 12
.

If hypotheses
1
hold true, then we have the following boundary conditions for
U
:

\begin{split}&\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(t,x,m,y)=0\,,\qquad\forall x\in\partial\Omega,y\in\Omega,t\in[0,T],m\in\mathcal{P}^{sub}(\Omega)\,,\\
&\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(t,x,m,y)=0\,,\qquad\forall x\in\Omega,y\in\partial\Omega,t\in[0,T],m\in\mathcal{P}^{sub}(\Omega)\,.\end{split}

Proof.

The proof of the first boundary condition is trivial, since
\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(t_{0},x,m_{0},y)=v(t_{0},x;\delta_{y})
and
v
satisfies a Dirichlet boundary condition.

For the second condition, let us consider
y\in\partial\Omega
. To prove that
v(t_{0},x;\delta_{y})=0
, it is enough to check that
v=0
solves

\begin{cases}\displaystyle-v_{t}-\mathrm{tr}(a(x)D^{2}v)+H_{p}(t,x,Du)\cdot Dv={\frac{\delta F}{\delta m}}(t,x,m(t))(\mu(t))\,,\\
\displaystyle v(T,x)={\frac{\delta G}{\delta m}}(x,m(T))(\mu(T))\,,\\
\displaystyle{v}_{|\partial\Omega}=0\,,\end{cases}

(52)

where
\mu
is the unique solution, in the sense of Definition
4
, of

\begin{cases}\mu_{t}-\sum\limits_{ij}\partial^{2}_{ij}(a_{ij}(x)\mu)-\mathrm{div}(\mu H_{p}(t,x,Du))=0\,,\\
\mu(t_{0})=\mu_{0}\,,\\
{\mu}_{|\partial\Omega}=0\,.\end{cases}

In this way we have
mH_{pp}(t,x,Du)Dv=0
, and so the couple
(v,\mu)
turns out to be a solution of

\begin{cases}\displaystyle-v_{t}-\mathrm{tr}(a(x)D^{2}v)+H_{p}(t,x,Du)Dv={\frac{\delta F}{\delta m}}(t,x,m(t))(\rho(t))\,,\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\\
\displaystyle\mu_{t}-\sum\limits_{ij}\partial^{2}_{ij}(a_{ij}(x)\mu)-\mathrm{div}\big(\mu H_{p}(t,x,Du)+mH_{pp}(t,x,Du)Dv\big)=0,\\
\displaystyle v(T,x)={\frac{\delta G}{\delta m}}(x,m(T))(\mu(T))\,,\qquad\mu(t_{0})=\mu_{0}\,,\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\\
\displaystyle{v}_{|\partial\Omega}=0\,,\qquad{\mu}_{|\partial\Omega}=0\,\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt,\end{cases}

which is exactly the pure linearized system.

Due to the linearity character of (
52
), we only need to prove that

\frac{\delta F}{\delta m}(t,x,m(t))(\mu(t))=\frac{\delta G}{\delta m}(x,m(T))(\mu(T))=0\,.

Thanks to the hypotheses
1
, both
\frac{\delta F}{\delta m}(x,m(t),\cdot)
and
\frac{\delta G}{\delta m}(x,m(T),\cdot)
satisfy a Dirichlet boundary condition, being also elements of
\mathcal{C}^{2+\alpha}
. Then, choosing
\phi(t,y)
satisfying (
17
) with
\psi(t,y)=0
and
\xi(y)=\frac{\delta F}{\delta m}(t,x,m(t),y)
, we have

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\frac{\delta F}{\delta m}(t,x,m(t))(\mu(t))=\left\langle\mu(t),\frac{\delta F}{\delta m}(t,x,m(t),\cdot)\right\rangle\\
\displaystyle\qquad=\langle\mu_{0},\phi(0,\cdot)\rangle=\langle\delta_{y},\phi(0,\cdot)\rangle=\phi(0,y)=0\,,\end{array}

since
\psi
satisfies a Dirichlet boundary condition. The same computations hold for
\frac{\delta G}{\delta m}
, therefore we have:

\displaystyle\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(t_{0},x,m_{0},y)=\left\langle\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(t_{0},x,m_{0},\cdot),\delta_{y}\right\rangle=v(t_{0},x)=0\,.

∎

6
Convergence of the Nash System

The aim of this section is twofold: proving that, for an integer
N\geq 2
, a classical solution
(v^{N,i})_{i\in\{1,...,N\}}
of the Nash system (
5
) converges in a suitable sense to the solution of the Master Equation (
7
), and to prove that also optimal trajectories converge.

6.1
Finite Dimensional Projections of U

The symmetrical structure of the problem suggests considering
suitable
finite dimensional projections of
U
, along the empirical distributions
m^{N,i}_{\mathbf{x}}
. Therefore, for
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, we define:

u^{N,i}(t,\textbf{x}):=U(t,x_{i},m_{\textbf{x}}^{N,i})\,,

(53)

where
\textbf{x}=(x_{1},...,x_{N})\in\Omega^{N}
and
m_{\bm{x}}^{N,i}
is defined as in (
3
).
Exploiting the regularity of
U
, we already know that

u^{N}_{i}\in\mathcal{C}^{1,2+\alpha}

with respect to the couple
(t,x_{i})
.

In order to prove a regularity result for
(x_{j})_{j\neq i}
, we need two technical results about the derivative of the solution of (
7
) with respect to
m
.

Theorem (
11
) implies the following lemma, which turns out to be crucial to prove the representation formula for the derivatives of
u^{N}_{i}
. Since the related proof is analogous to
Proposition 7.1, 7.4
of
[
18
]
, we omit it.

Lemma 13
.

Suppose hypotheses
1
are satisfied, then, if
m\in\mathcal{P}^{sub}(\Omega)
and
\phi\in L^{2}(m,\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a bounded vector field, we have

\displaystyle\Big|\Big|U(t,\cdot,(id+\phi)\sharp m)-U(t,\cdot,m)-\int_{\Omega}D_{m}U(t,\cdot,m,y)\cdot\phi(y)dm(y)\Big|\Big|_{1+\alpha}\leq C||\phi||^{2}_{L^{2}(m)}

(54)

Now we can prove the following representation formula for the derivatives of
u^{N}_{i}
:

Proposition 14
.

The following equations for the derivative of
u_{i}^{N}
hold for all
j\neq i:

\displaystyle D_{x_{j}}u_{i}^{N}(t,\textbf{x})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{N-1}D_{m}U(t,x_{i},{m}_{\textbf{x}}^{N,i},x_{j})\,,

(55)

\displaystyle D^{2}_{x_{i},x_{j}}u_{i}^{N}(t,\textbf{x})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{N-1}D_{x}D_{{m}}U(t,x_{i},{{m}}_{\textbf{x}}^{N,i},x_{j})\,,

(56)

\displaystyle\Big|D^{2}_{x_{j},x_{j}}u_{i}^{N}(t,\textbf{x})

\displaystyle-\dfrac{1}{N-1}D_{y}D_{m}U(t,x_{i},{{m}}_{\textbf{x}}^{N,i},x_{j})\Big|\leq\dfrac{C}{N^{2}}\,,

where the last inequality holds a.e.
\textbf{x}\in\Omega^{N}

Proof.

We can limit ourselves to prove (
55
) and (

), since the second formula is a direct consequence of the first one.
We consider
\textbf{x}=(x_{j})_{j\in\{1,...,N\}}
,
\textbf{v}=(v_{j})
s.t.
\bm{x},\bm{v}\in\Omega^{N}
, with
x_{j}\neq x_{k}
for any

For

\varphi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\varphi(x)=v_{j}
for

u^{N}_{i}(t,\textbf{x}+\textbf{v})=U(t,x_{i},(id+\varphi)\sharp m_{\textbf{x}}^{N,i})

u^{N}_{i}(t,\textbf{x}+\textbf{v})=u^{N}_{i}(t,\textbf{x})+\int_{\Omega}D_{m}U(t,x_{i},m_{\textbf{x}}^{N,i},y)\cdot\varphi(t)dm_{\textbf{x}}^{N,i}(y)+o\left(||v||_{L^{2}(m_{\textbf{x}}^{N,i}))}\right)\,,

exploiting the definition of

, we compute the integral obtaining

u^{N}_{i}(t,\textbf{x}+\textbf{v})=u^{N}_{i}(t,\textbf{x})+\frac{1}{N-1}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}D_{m}U(t,x_{i},m_{\bm{x}}^{N,i},x_{j})\cdot v_{j}+o(|v|)\,,

concluding the proof of (
55

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle|D_{x_{j}}u_{i}^{N}(t,\textbf{x})-D_{x_{j}}u_{i}^{N}(t,\textbf{y}))|\leq\dfrac{C}{N}|D_{m}U(t,x_{i},m_{\textbf{x}}^{N,i},x_{j})-D_{m}U(t,x_{i},m_{\textbf{x}}^{N,i},y_{j})|\\
\displaystyle\quad+\dfrac{C}{N}\left(|D_{m}U(t,x_{i},m_{\textbf{x}}^{N,i},y_{j})-D_{m}U(t,x_{i},m_{\textbf{y}}^{N,i},y_{j})|\right)\leq\frac{C}{N}|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|\,,\end{array}

where the last inequality comes from (

) and (
51

.
Considering
\textbf{e}^{\textbf{k}}_{\textbf{j}}=(e_{jk}^{1},...,e_{jk}^{N})

as a basis over
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
, we can write

\displaystyle\qquad\hskip 4.55254pt\Bigg|\dfrac{D_{x_{j}}u^{N,i}(t,\textbf{x}+h\textbf{e}^{\textbf{k}}_{\textbf{j}})-D_{x_{j}}u^{N,i}(t,\textbf{x})}{h}-\dfrac{1}{N-1}\partial_{y_{k}}D_{m}U(t,x_{i},m_{\textbf{x}}^{N,i},x_{j})\Bigg|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{C}{N}\Bigg|\dfrac{D_{m}U(t,x_{i},m_{\textbf{x}+h\textbf{e}^{\textbf{k}}_{\textbf{j}}}^{N,i},x_{j}+he_{k})-D_{m}U(t,x_{i},m_{\textbf{x}}^{N,i},x_{j}+he_{k})}{h}\Bigg|+\dfrac{C}{N}

\displaystyle\hskip 8.5359pt\displaystyle\Bigg|\dfrac{D_{m}U(t,x_{i},m_{\textbf{x}}^{N,i},x_{j}+he_{k})-D_{m}U(t,x_{i},m_{\textbf{x}}^{N,i},x_{j})}{h}-\partial_{y_{k}}D_{m}U(t,x_{i},m_{\textbf{x}}^{N,i},x_{j})\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{C}{N}\Big|\partial_{y_{k}}D_{m}U(t,x_{i},m_{\textbf{x}}^{N,i},x_{h})-\partial_{y_{k}}D_{m}U(t,x_{i},m_{\textbf{x}}^{N,i},x_{j})\Big|

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\dfrac{C}{Nh}\textbf{d}_{1}\left(m_{\textbf{x}+h\textbf{e}^{\textbf{k}}_{\textbf{j}}}^{N,i},m_{\textbf{x}}^{N,i}\right)\leq\dfrac{C}{N^{2}}

∎

We can prove now that each finite projection

Theorem 15
.

Assume Assumptions

We consider a variable interest rate - constant for certain periods of time and then variable at the discretion of the issuer of the loan. The investment ends when the price reaches

We focus on an

The
N

[0,T]

Each player

uses a self-financing strategy
(\pi^{i}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
and can control its own drift. The corresponding wealth, denoted by
(X_{t}^{i})_{t\in[0,T]}

dX_{t}^{i}=\pi^{i}_{t}dt+\sqrt{2}\sigma dB_{t}^{i}\,,

(69)

assuming a control
\pi^{i}_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
and a constant volatility
\sigma
. The self-financing strategies
\pi^{i}
belong to the set of admissible policies
\mathcal{A}
defined as

\mathcal{A}=\left\{\pi_{\cdot}^{i}:[0,T]\to L^{2}(\tilde{\Omega})\,\,\mathcal{F}\textsl{-progr.\! measurable with }\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau^{i}}\sigma^{2}\pi_{s}^{2}ds\right]<\infty\right\}

with
\tau^{i}
corresponding to the hitting time of the
i^{th}
wealth, which corresponds to the time when
X_{t}^{i}
reaches one of the critical values defined by the bounds,
l
or
u
:

\tau^{i}=\inf\left\{t\in[0,T]\,:\,X_{t}^{i}=l\vee X_{t}^{i}=u\right\}

Following the setting stated in
[
54
]
, players optimize their expected terminal utility being also concerned with the performance of their competitors. Accordingly, we fix an arbitrary policy
\left(\pi_{1},...,\pi_{i-1},\pi_{i+1},...,\pi_{N}\right)
for the other players, whereas the
i^{th}
player aims to maximize

\displaystyle J^{i}(t,\bm{x},\pi_{s}^{i})=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{\tau^{i}}\dfrac{1}{2}\left({\pi_{s}^{i}}\right)^{2}\,ds+G\left(X_{\tau^{i}}^{i},m_{\bm{X}_{\tau^{i}}^{i}}^{N,i}\right)\,\big|\bm{X_{t}}=\bm{x}\right]\,,

with the terminal payoff
G
defined as follows:

G\left(x,m\right)=x(1-x)\int_{0}^{1}z(1-z)dm(z)\,.

We underline that functions
H(p)=\frac{1}{2}|p|^{2}
and
G
both satisfy hypotheses
1
.

For our
1
-dimensional trading example, we enforce a simplified choice of the domain by prescribing both lower and upper limits, i.e.
l=0
and
u=1
. Moreover, we fix the volatility
\sigma=1
.

Accordingly, the Nash system takes the following form.

\begin{cases}-\displaystyle\partial_{t}v_{i}^{N}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\partial^{2}_{x_{j}x_{j}}v_{i}^{N}(t,\bm{x})+\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{x_{i}}v_{i}^{N}(t,\bm{x})\right)^{2}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\partial_{x_{j}}v_{j}^{N}(t,\bm{x})\partial_{x_{j}}v_{i}^{N}(t,\bm{x})=0\,,\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\\
\displaystyle v_{i}^{N}(T,\textbf{x})=\frac{1}{N-1}x_{i}(1-x_{i})\sum\limits_{j\neq i}^{N}x_{j}(1-x_{j})\,,\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\\
\displaystyle v^{N}_{i}(t,\textbf{x})_{|x_{i}\in\{0,1\}}=0\,,\quad i=1,...,N\,.\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\end{cases}

We will study models of mean field type which can be seen as the limiting problem of the above-described one.
When
N\rightarrow\infty
, the dynamic for a representative player is described by

dX_{t}=\pi_{t}dt+\sqrt{2}dB_{t}\,.

The corresponding functional reads

J(x,\pi_{t})=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}\dfrac{1}{2}{\pi_{s}}^{2}ds+X_{\tau}(1-X_{\tau})\int_{0}^{1}z(1-z)dm_{\tau}(z)\,\big|X_{t}=x\right]\,,

where
m_{\tau}
is the density of the process
(X_{\tau})
.

The Master Equation for this simplified setting reads

\begin{cases}\displaystyle-\partial_{t}U(t,x,m)-\partial_{xx}U(t,x,m)+\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{x}U(t,x,m)\right)^{2}+\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt-\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{y}\left(D_{m}U(t,x,m,y)\right)dm(y)+\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt+\int_{0}^{1}D_{m}U(t,x,m,y)\cdot\partial_{x}\displaystyle U(t,y,m)dm(y)=0\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\\
\displaystyle U(T,x,m)=x(1-x)\int_{0}^{1}z(1-z)dm(z)\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\\
\displaystyle U(t,0,m)=U(t,1,m)=0\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\\
\displaystyle\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(t,x,m,0)=\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(t,x,m,1)=0\,.\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\end{cases}

Although the solution of the Nash system is not easy to compute, we can directly approach the Master Equation, using the definition of
U
accordingly to (
9
). In particular, the Hamilton-Jacobi equation for our formulation reads as follows:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle-u_{t}-u_{xx}+\dfrac{1}{2}\left(u_{x}\right)^{2}=0\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
\displaystyle u(T,x)=x(1-x)\int_{0}^{1}z(1-z)dm_{T}(z)\,,\qquad\forall x\in[0,1],,\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
\displaystyle u(t,0)=u(t,1)=0\,,\qquad\forall t\in[t_{0},T]\,.\end{cases}

By the change of variable
w=e^{-\frac{1}{2}u}
, see also
[
19
]
, the equation for the function
w
becomes

\begin{cases}\displaystyle-w_{t}-w_{xx}=0\,,\\
\displaystyle w(T,x)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}x(1-x)\int_{0}^{1}z(1-z)dm_{T}(z)\right)\,,\\
\displaystyle w(t,0)=w(t,1)=1\,.\end{cases}

hence a classical heat equation whose solution can be computed with standard methods.
Coming back to the change of variable
u=-2\log(w)
and (
9
), the function
U(t_{0},\cdot,m_{0})=u(t_{0},\cdot)
admits the following representation:

\displaystyle U(t_{0},x,m_{0})=-2\ln\left(1+\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}b_{k}e^{-k^{2}\pi^{2}(T-t_{0})}\sin(k\pi x)\right)\,,

being
b_{k}=2\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\frac{1}{2}y(1-y)\int_{0}^{1}z(1-z)dm_{T}(z)}\sin(k\pi y)dy
and
m_{T}=m(T,\cdot)
, where
m
is the solution of the Fokker-Planck equation

\begin{cases}\displaystyle m_{t}-m_{xx}-\left(mu_{x}\right)_{x}=0\,,\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
\displaystyle m(t_{0},x)=m_{0}\,,\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\quad\,\quad\qquad\forall x\in[0,1]\,,\\
\displaystyle m(t,0)=m(t,1)=0\,,\quad\,\forall t\in[t_{0},T]\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\,.\\
\end{cases}
