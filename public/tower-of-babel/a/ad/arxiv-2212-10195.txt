An equivalent definition for multiplicities of a quaternionic eigenvalue
Dr Stefano Spessato ORCID: 0000-0003-0069-5853

Abstract
In this paper we introduce two definitions for algebraic and geometric multiplicities of a quaternion right eigenvalue. This definitions are equivalent to the classical ones. However, differently from the usual definitions, the notions of root subspace and Jordan form of a quaternion matrix are not required and all the properties can be proved easily.



2022

keywords
quaternion, right eigenvalue, geometric multiplicity, algebraic multiplicity

Introduction

Spectral properties of matrices with quaternion entries are an important research topic in last years. This happens for the large number of applications of quaternions both in Pure and Applied Mathematics.

However, since the space of quaternions
\mathbb{H}
is not a commutative algebra, is quite difficult to obtain the quaternion version of the classical theorems of Complex Linear Algebra. One of this result is the Quaternion Spectral Theorem of Farenick and Pidkowich
Spec
. Sometimes it is also difficult to define the equivalent notions: for example a quaternion matrix has at least two kinds spectra, which are called the
right
spectrum and the
left
spectrum. In particular, in this case, the most common choice is to study the right spectrum of a quaternion matrix.

In this paper, after some basiscs of Quaternion Linear Algebra (Section
1
), we give two new definitions for the algebraic and geometric multiplicity (resp.
a.m.
and
g.m.
) of a right eigenvalue of a quaternion matrix (Section
2
). Moreover, always in the same Section, we also give the proofs of the main properties of this multiplicities. In particular we prove

•

the invariance of the multiplicities under the similarity relation

•

the classical inequality
a.m.(q,A)\geq g.m.(q,A)
, where
q
is a right-eigenvalue of a quaternion matrix
A
,

•

the equality
a.m.(q,A)=g.m.(q,A)
, if
A
is a normal matrix.

Finally, in Section
3
, we prove that our definitions are equivalent to the classical ones introduced in the book of Rodman
Quat
.

The advantage of our definitions is that they can be given without the notions of
root subspace
or
Jordan form
of a quaternion matrix and so they can be used also by people which are not in Quaternion Linear Algebra. Moreover also the main properties can be proved without this tools.

1.1
Quaternions

Let

be the unital associative
\mathbb{R}
-algebra whose generators are

,

and

i^{2}=j^{2}=k^{2}=ijk=-1.

(1)

\mathbb{H}
is not a commutative algebra, indeed

(2)

Two quaternions

If

in
\mathbb{H}

there is a unique

and

(4)

1.2
Right spectrum of a quaternion matrix

Let us denote by
\mathbb{H}^{n}
the space of column vectors of length
n
with quaternionic entries. Let
\mathbb{H}^{m\times n}
be the space of

\mathbb{H}^{m\times n}

of

as the
n\times m
matrix
A^{*}:=(\overline{a_{ji}})
. A square matrix

is

if

and it is normal if
AA^{*}=A^{*}A
.

For a square matrix

in
\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}

We say

of

Notice that if

is an element of

then also

is a right eigenvalue of

. Indeed if
q^{\prime}=sqs^{-1}
and

then
A(ws^{-1})=(ws^{-1})q^{\prime}
.

Observe that each quaternion
q=a+bi+cj+dk
can be seen as
q=(a+ib)+(c+id)j
where
a+ib
and
c+id
are complex numbers. In the same way every quaternion matrix
A
in

can be decomposed as

where

and
A_{2}
are
n\times n
-complex matrices.

Then the

of

in

f(A):=\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&&A_{2}\\
-\overline{A}_{2}&&\overline{A}_{1}\end{bmatrix}

(5)

Proposition 1.1

(pag. 78 of
Spec
)
.

For each
A,B
in
\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
the following properties hold:

•

f(Id_{n})=Id_{2n}
;

•

f(A+B)=f(A)+f(B)
;

•

f(A\cdot B)=f(A)\cdot f(B);

•

f(A^{*})=f(A)^{*}
;

•

f(A^{-1})=f(A)^{-1}
.

These properties are very useful in order to investigate the right spectrum of a quaternion matrix. In particular we obtain the following result

Proposition 1.2
.

Let
A
be a matrix in
\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
. Then

Spec(f(A))=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}|[\lambda]=[q]\mbox{ for each }q\in\sigma_{r}(A)\}.

(6)

In particular, if
A=A^{*}
, then
\sigma_{r}(A)\subset\mathbb{R}
and

Spec(f(A))=\sigma_{r}(A).

(7)

Proof.

This is essentially the proof of points 1. and 2. of Lemma 2.5 of
Spec
.
∎

Observe that if
A
is a
1\times 1
-matrix, then
f:\mathbb{H}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
. So, for each matrix
A=(a_{rs})
in
\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
, we obtain a block matrix
\pi(A):=(\pi(a_{rs}))=(f(a_{rs}))
in
\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 2n}
.

.

For each matrix

in
\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
, the matrices

and
\pi(A)
are similar. This implies that

Spec(f(A))=Spec(\pi(A))

(8)

and, if we denote by

a.m.(\lambda,\pi(A))=a.m.(\lambda,f(A))\mbox{ and }g.m.(\lambda,\pi(A))=g.m.(\lambda,f(A)).

(9)

Proof.

The proof of this fact is similar to the the proof Theorem 5.9 of the work of Donno, Cavaleri and S.
Godsil

and

are similar as complex matrices. So this fact implies also our statement. However, for sake of completeness, we report the proof of the similarity of

and

.

Notice that, for each entry
a_{rs}
, of

there are two complex numbers
a_{rs}^{[2]}
and

such that

a_{rs}=a_{rs}^{[1]}+a_{rs}^{[2]}j.

(10)

Then, the matrix
\pi(A)
, is given by

\pi(A)=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1,1}^{[1]}&a_{1,1}^{[2]}&...&a_{1,n}^{[1]}&a_{1,n}^{[2]}\\
-\overline{a}_{1,1}^{[2]}&\overline{a}_{1,1}^{[1]}&...&-\overline{a}_{n,n}^{[2]}&\overline{a}_{n,n}^{[1]}\\
\vdots&\vdots&...&\vdots&\vdots\\
a_{n,1}^{[1]}&a_{n,1}^{[2]}&...&a_{n,n}^{[1]}&a_{n,n}^{[2]}\\
-\overline{a}_{n,1}^{[2]}&\overline{a}_{n,1}^{[1]}&...&-\overline{a}_{n,n}^{[2]}&\overline{a}_{n,n}^{[1]}\\
\end{bmatrix}

(11)

Let us denote by
\tau:\{1,...,2n\}\longrightarrow\{1,...,2n\}
the permutation defined as

\tau(r)=\begin{cases}n+k\mbox{ if }r=2k\\
k+1\mbox{ if }r=2k+1\end{cases}

(12)

Let
e_{b}
be the
2n
-vector which has
1
in the
b
-th entry and
0
otherwise. Let us denote by

the permutation

-matrix such that the

-th column is

and let

be its transpose. Then

f(A)=R_{\tau}\pi(A)C_{\tau}.

(13)

Indeed

\pi(A)C_{\tau}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1,1}^{[1]}&a_{1,2}^{[1]}&...&a_{1,n}^{[1]}&a_{1,1}^{[2]}&...&a_{1,n-1}^{[2]}&a_{1,n}^{[2]}\\

\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
a_{n,1}^{[1]}&a_{n,2}^{[1]}&...&a_{n,n}^{[1]}&a_{n,1}^{[2]}&...&a_{n,n-1}^{[2]}&a_{n,n}^{[2]}\\
-\overline{a}_{n,1}^{[2]}&-\overline{a}_{n,2}^{[2]}&...&-\overline{a}_{n,n}^{[2]}&\overline{a}_{n,1}^{[1]}&...&\overline{a}_{n,n-1}^{[1]}&\overline{a}_{n,n}^{[1]}\end{bmatrix}

(14)

and

R_{\tau}\pi(A)C_{\tau}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1,1}^{[1]}&a_{1,2}^{[1]}&...&a_{1,n}^{[1]}&a_{1,1}^{[2]}&...&a_{1,n-1}^{[2]}&a_{1,n}^{[2]}\\
a_{2,1}^{[1]}&a_{2,2}^{[1]}&...&a_{2,n}^{[1]}&a_{2,1}^{[2]}&...&a_{2,n-1}^{[2]}&a_{2,n}^{[2]}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
a_{n,1}^{[1]}&a_{n,2}^{[1]}&...&a_{n,n}^{[1]}&a_{n,1}^{[2]}&...&a_{n,n-1}^{[2]}&a_{n,n}^{[2]}\\
-\overline{a}_{1,1}^{[2]}&-\overline{a}_{1,2}^{[2]}&...&-\overline{a}_{1,n}^{[2]}&\overline{a}_{1,1}^{[1]}&...&\overline{a}_{1,n-1}^{[1]}&\overline{a}_{1,n}^{[1]}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
-\overline{a}_{n-1,1}^{[2]}&-\overline{a}_{n-1,2}^{[2]}&...&-\overline{a}_{n-1,n}^{[2]}&\overline{a}_{n-1,1}^{[1]}&...&\overline{a}_{n-1,n-1}^{[1]}&\overline{a}_{n-1,n}^{[1]}\\
-\overline{a}_{n,1}^{[2]}&-\overline{a}_{n,2}^{[2]}&...&-\overline{a}_{n,n}^{[2]}&\overline{a}_{n,1}^{[1]}&...&\overline{a}_{n,n-1}^{[1]}&\overline{a}_{n,n}^{[1]}\end{bmatrix}

(15)

Then if
A=(a_{rs})
, then

R_{\tau}\pi(A)C_{\tau}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{rs}^{[1]}&a_{rs}^{[2]}\\
-\overline{a}_{rs}^{[2]}&\overline{a}_{rs}^{[1]}\end{bmatrix}=f(A).

(16)

Notice that, since

is a permutation matrix, then

and so

are similar. This means that they have the same spectrum and the same multiplicities.

1.3
Schur’s triangularization theorem

We conclude this Section with a classical result on Quaternion Linear Algebra. This is an immediate consequences of Fundamental Theorem of Algebra. A short proof of this result can be found in the book of Rotman
Quat

Lemma 1.4

(Theorem 5.3.5. of
Quat
)
.

For every
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
there exists an eigenvalue which is a complex number with nonnegative imaginary part.

As a consequence of this result, by applying the usual technique from Complex Linear Algebra, it is possible to prove a quaternionic version of Schur’s triangularization theorem.

Lemma 1.5

(Lemma 3.3 of
Spec
, Theorem 5.3.6 of
Quat
)
.

Let
A
be a matrix in
\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
. Then there are
U,T
in
\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
such that
U
is unitary,
T
is an upper triangular matrix and

T=U^{*}AU.

(17)

Moreover the entries on the diagonal of
T
are complex numbers
\mu_{i}
such that
Im(\mu_{i})\geq 0.

Quaternionic dimension of a right

Definition 1.1
.

An additive abelian group

-vector space if there is a map

under which the image of each pair

, such that

•

,

•

v(q_{1}q_{2})=(vq_{1})q_{2}
, and

•

v1=v

.

If

is a nonzero right

-vector space and if

1.

is a

in

(18)

where

-independent set

and for any distinct

is satisfied only for

.

3.

X
is a

for

if

is generating and
\mathbb{H}
-independent set.

.

Every generating space

-vector space

(Theorem 4.2 of

)

If a right

.

Let

-vector space and let

. Then the

Remark 2
.

Let

Multiplicities of a right eigenvalue

2.1
Algebraic multiplicity

Our next step is to define the algebraic multiplicity for a right eigenvalue of a quaternion matrix.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
A
be a matrix in
\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
and let
q
be a right eigenvalue of
A
. Let
\lambda_{q}
be the complex number such that
Im(\lambda_{q})\geq 0
and
[q]=[\lambda_{q}]
. Let
U
and
T=U^{*}AU
be two matrices satisfying Lemma
1.5
. Then the
algebraic multiplicity
of
q
(or
a.m.(q,A)
) is the number of occurrences of
\lambda_{q}
in the diagonal of
T
.

Proposition 2.1
.

The Definition
2.1
is well-given and it does not depend on the choice of
T
. Moreover, if
q
is a right eigenvalue of a quaternionic matrix
A
such that
[q]=[\lambda_{q}]
where
\lambda_{q}\in\mathbb{C}
and

, then

a.m.(q,A)=a.m.(\lambda_{q},f(A))=a.m.(\lambda_{q},\pi(A))

(20)

if
Im(\lambda_{q})\neq 0
, and

a.m.(q,A)=\frac{1}{2}a.m.(\lambda_{q},f(A))=\frac{1}{2}a.m.(\lambda_{q},\pi(A))

(21)

if
\lambda_{q}
is a real number.

Proof.

Observe that
T=U^{*}AU
implies that
\pi(T)
and
\pi(A)
are similar, indeed
\pi(T)\sim f(T)=f(U)^{*}f(A)f(U)\sim f(A)\sim\pi(A)
. So, if
[q]
is a right eigenvalue of
A
, then
\lambda_{q}
is an eigenvalue for
\pi(A)
and also for
\pi(T)
. This means that
\lambda_{q}
occurs in the diagonal of
\pi(T)
. Indeed
\pi(T)
is a triangular matrix since the
i
-th diagonal block of
\pi(T)
is
\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{i}&0\\
0&\overline{\mu_{i}}\end{bmatrix}
where
\mu_{i}
are the diagonal entries of
T
. So
\lambda_{q}
occurs also in the diagonal of
T
and, in particular,

a.m.(q,A)=a.m.(\lambda_{q},\pi(T))=a.m.(\lambda_{q},\pi(A))=a.m.(\lambda_{q},f(A))

(22)

if
Im(\lambda)\neq 0
, and

a.m.(q,A)=\frac{1}{2}a.m.(\lambda_{q},\pi(T))=\frac{1}{2}a.m.(\lambda,\pi(A))=\frac{1}{2}a.m.(\lambda,f(A))

(23)

otherwise.

Finally, if there are some matrices

and

satisfying the Lemma

, then

. So

are similar because

are similar.

This proves that the definition of algebraic multiplicity is well-given.

Our next step is to define the geometric multiplicity of a right eigenvalue

.

Let

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
A

g.m.(q,A)=g.m.(\lambda_{q},f(A))=g.m.(\lambda_{q},\pi(A))

g.m.(q,A)=\frac{1}{2}g.m.(\lambda_{q},f(A))=\frac{1}{2}g.m.(\lambda_{q},\pi(A)).

Proof.

Fix a right eigenvalue
q
of
A
. Then choose a basis

and complete it in order to obtain a basis

. Let

of

. Let

be the vector in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
which has the

-th entry equal to
1
and
0
otherwise. Observe that
\mathcal{C}_{X}
is invertible and its inverse is given by the matrix which has in the

-th column the components of

with respect to

. Then, we define

D_{\lambda}:=\mathcal{C}_{X}^{-1}\cdot A\cdot\mathcal{C}_{X}.

(29)

The matrix

D_{\lambda}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{q}Id_{d}&B_{1}\\
0&B_{2}\end{bmatrix}

(30)

The matrix

Equivalence of the definitions

As stated in the Introduction, the definitions given Section
2
are equivalent to the definitions of
Quat

In order to define the classical algebraic and the geometric multiplicity of a right eigenvalue we need the notions of minimal polynomial
p^{A}
related to
A
and of the root subspace
\mathcal{M}
of
A
corresponding to
p^{A}
.

Notice that
\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
is a finite dimensional, real vector space. Then there are some polynomials
p(t)\in\mathbb{R}[t]
such that
p(A)=0
.

Definition 3.1
.

The
minimal polynomial of

A
is the polynomial
p^{A}(t)\neq 0
which is monic and it has minimal degree.

Remark 5
.

The minimal polynomial
p^{A}
of a matrix
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
is unique.

Assume that

p^{A}(t)=p_{1}(t)^{m_{1}}\cdot...\cdot p_{k}(t)^{m_{k}}

(38)

where
p_{j}(t)
are distinct irreducible real polynomial which are monic. Notice that for each eigenvalue
\lambda
of
A
there is exactly one polynomial
p_{j}
such that
\lambda
is a root of
p_{j}
. Then the following definitions make sense.

Definition 3.2
.

A
root subspace

\mathcal{M}_{j}
of
A
is the set

\mathcal{M}_{j}:=\{u\in\mathbb{H}^{n}|p_{j}(A)^{m_{j}}u=0\}

(39)

where
j=1,...,k
. The
root subspace of the eingenvalue

\lambda
is the root subspace related to the polynomial
p_{j}
which has
\lambda
as a root.

Remark 6
.

Given a right eigenvalue
q
of a quaternion matrix
A
, then
V_{[q]}\neq\mathcal{M}_{j}
, where
\mathcal{M}_{q}
is the root subspace related to
q
. An example is the following: notice that

is an eigenvalue of the matrix

1&0&0&0\\

\end{bmatrix}.

(40)

Then the minimal polynomial of

is

and so

is equal to

.

.

\lambda
