Geometric Approaches to Lagrangian Averaging
Andrew D. Gilbert, Jacques Vanneste

Abstract




Lagrangian averaging theories, most notably the Generalised Lagrangian Mean (GLM) theory of
Andrews and McIntyre (1978a)
, have been primarily developed in Euclidean space and Cartesian coordinates. We re-interpret these theories using a geometric, coordinate-free formulation. This gives central roles to the flow map, its decomposition into mean and perturbation maps, and the momentum 1-form dual to the velocity vector. In this interpretation, the Lagrangian mean of any tensorial quantity is obtained by averaging its pull back to the mean configuration. Crucially, the mean velocity is not a Lagrangian mean in this sense. It can be defined in a variety of ways, leading to alternative Lagrangian mean formulations that include GLM and
Soward and Roberts
’s (
2010
) glm. These formulations share key features which the geometric approach uncovers. We derive governing equations both for the mean flow and for wave activities constraining the dynamics of the pertubations. The presentation focusses on the Boussinesq model for inviscid rotating stratified flows and reviews the necessary tools of differential geometry.

Keywords:

wave–mean flow interactions, generalised Lagrangian mean, flow map, pseudomomentum, wave activity

1
Introduction

Numerous fluid dynamical phenomena are the result of interactions between small-scale or high-frequency fluctuations – associated with waves or turbulence – and mean flows. Examples include acoustic streaming, the formation of jets in geophysical and astrophysical fluids, and the dynamo effect in conducting fluids.

Averaging, over time, space or ensembles of flow realisations, is central to the analysis of these phenomena since it enables the decomposition between mean flow and fluctuations that is required both conceptually and practically. It has long been recognised that the most straightforward form of averaging, Eulerian averaging, that is averaging at fixed spatial location, often leads to unsatisfactory decompositions. This especially true for advection-dominated, high-Reynolds-number flows whose dynamics is controlled by material transport (of scalars such as temperature and potential vorticity, or of vectors such as vorticity and magnetic field). Eulerian averaging does not preserve the structure of the advective terms in the equations governing fluid motion: the average of the material derivative along the flow differs from the material derivative along an averaged flow by terms – Reynolds stresses or similar – whose impact is often difficult to ascertain. As a result, the strong constraints that material conservation laws impose on the dynamics are obscured by the averaging process.

Lagrangian averaging offers a solution. It replaces averaging at fixed position by averaging at fixed particle label or, in other words, averaging along fluid particle trajectories. Lagrangian averaging has a long history, dating back to pioneering work by
Eckart (1963)
,
Dewar (1970)
,
Bretherton (1971)
,
Soward (1972)
,
Grimshaw (1975)
and others. The landmark paper by
Andrews and McIntyre (1978a)
provides solid foundations for Lagrangian averaging in the form of the theory of the Generalised Lagrangian Mean (GLM).

A key idea of GLM and its predecessors is to use a suitably defined mean position of particles as a proxy for their label. This avoids the difficulties caused by the intricate nature of the mapping between particle labels and positions and gives the averaged equations of motion a familiar, Eulerian-looking appearance.
Andrews and McIntyre (1978a)
derive these averaged equations for a general compressible rotating fluid.

There is now a large body of literature that applies these or similar GLM equations obtained for other fluid models to study the effect of waves on mean flows in the atmosphere and ocean
(
Bühler and McIntyre, 1998
,
Bühler and McIntyre, 2005
,
Holmes-Cerfon et al., 2011
,
Xie and Vanneste, 2015
, e.g.)
. A widespread application is to ocean surface waves. Their impact on currents is modelled using the Craik–Leibovich equations
(
Craik and Leibovich, 1976
)
, a version of the GLM equations specialised for high-frequency potential waves
(
Leibovich, 1976
,
Holm, 1996
,
Suzuki and Fox-Kemper, 2016
)
. The book by
Bühler (2014)
gives a detailed account of GLM theory and its applications.

The derivation of GLM equations from the parent fluid model requires elaborate manipulations. The resulting equations have attractive properties but also disconcerting features. For instance, the key Lagrangian mean momentum equation (Theorem I of
Andrews and McIntyre (1978a)
, here ignoring the Coriolis terms) appears in the form

(\partial_{t}+\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathrm{L}}\cdot\nabla)(\overline{u}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i}-\mathsf{p}_{i})+\partial_{i}\overline{u}^{\mathrm{L}}_{k}\,(\overline{u}^{\mathrm{L}}_{k}-\mathsf{p}_{k})=-\partial_{i}(\cdots),

(1)

where
\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathrm{L}}
, in components
\overline{u}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i}
, is the GLM mean velocity,
\mathsf{p}_{i}
the components of the pseudomentum, and we do not detail the complicated pressure-like term on the right-hand side. The appearance of both
\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathrm{L}}
and
\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathrm{L}}-\bm{\mathsf{p}}
as velocity-like vectors is notable, as is the unusual contraction in the second term on the left-hand side.

In this review, we make the case that a geometric approach to Lagrangian averaging, using basic tools of exterior calculus, offers a straightforward route for the derivation and interpretation of GLM equations.
The geometric viewpoint arises naturally in variational approaches to GLM
(
Grimshaw, 1984
,
Gjaja and Holm, 1999
,
Holm, 2002b
,
Holm, 2002a
,
Salmon, 1998
,
Salmon, 2013
,
Salmon, 2016
)
but it is also useful when dealing directly with the equations of motion, as we do here. At its most basic, a geometric approach makes it clear that Eq. (
1
) should be interpreted as

(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathrm{L}}})\overline{\bm{\nu}}^{\mathrm{L}}=-d(\cdots),

(2)

where
\overline{\bm{\nu}}^{\mathrm{L}}=(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathrm{L}}-\bm{\mathsf{p}})\cdot d\mathbf{x}
is a 1-form,
\mathcal{L}_{\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathrm{L}}}
denotes the Lie derivative along
\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathrm{L}}
and
d
is the differential.
The GLM version of Kelvin’s circulation theorem is then a short step away.
Beyond this, the geometric approach provides a unified way to understand a broad class of Lagrangian mean theories that generalize
Andrews and McIntyre
’s GLM. These theories differ in their definition of the Lagrangian mean flow but share many of GLM’s properties, including the form of Eq. (
2
). Lagrangian mean theories alternative to GLM have been proposed by
Roberts and Soward (2006b)
,
Roberts and Soward (2006a)
and
Soward and Roberts (2010)
,
Soward and Roberts (2014)
building on the pioneering work of
Soward (1972)
. The key feature of their so-called ‘glm’ theory is that, for an incompressible fluid, the Lagrangian mean velocity is divergence free, unlike that of standard GLM
(
Vanneste and Young, 2022
, see also)
.
Gilbert and Vanneste (2018)
point out the underlying geometric foundations of Lagrangian mean theories and put forward alternatives to both GLM and glm.

A virtue of the geometric approach is that it leads to general results valid on arbitrary Riemannian manifolds and with arbitrary coordinate systems. This is useful for applications that include two-dimensional flows on the sphere or three-dimensional flows described in spherical coordinates. The geometric approach also sheds light on features of standard GLM that depend on the Euclidean structure assumed by
Andrews and McIntyre (1978a)
and
Bühler (2014)
. The most prominent of such features is the definition of the Lagrangian mean velocity itself, which in GLM relies on the averaging of velocity vectors based at different points in space. Here, we follow
Gilbert and Vanneste (2018)
in giving the lead role to the mean flow map
(
Holm, 2002b
,
Holm, 2002a
, see also)
.
The mean flow map can be defined in a variety of ways, none of which should be thought of as resulting from the application of an averaging operator to flow maps (averaging operators involve summations and hence only apply to elements of vector spaces, which flow maps are not). The mean velocity is then simply the time derivative of the mean flow map. This viewpoint
accommodates alternative Lagrangian mean theories (GLM, glm, etc.) in a unified way.

This review focusses on two aspects of Lagrangian averaging: (i) the geometric derivation of the mean momentum equation, generalising Eq. (
2

Andrews and McIntyre (1978b)
and
Bühler (2014, §10.3)

(
Grimshaw, 1984

Salmon, 2013

2

We do not discuss the detailed models of perturbations that are required to close Eq. (
2

(

Bühler and McIntyre, 1998
,
Gjaja and Holm, 1999

Holmes-Cerfon et al., 2011
,
Bühler, 2014
,
Wagner and Young, 2015
,
Xie and Vanneste, 2015

,
Holm et al., 2023

and heuristic closures, either deterministic
(

,
Marsden and Shkoller, 2001
,
Marsden and Shkoller, 2003
,
Roberts and Soward, 2009

Soward and Roberts, 2008

or stochastic
(
Holm, 2019
,
Holm, 2021
, e.g.)

We concentrate the presentation on the Boussinesq model of rotating stratified fluid
(
Vallis, 2006
, e.g.)
. This model is widely used in geophysical fluid dynamics. From the perspective of this review, it has the advantage of illustrating the main features of a geometric approach to Lagrangian averaging. Extensions to fully compressible fluids or to magnetohydrodynamics are straightforward, if unwieldy
(
Gilbert and Vanneste, 2018
,
Gilbert and Vanneste, 2021
)
. We also restrict our attention to non-dissipative, unforced dynamics. Finally, we consider averaging as a general, abstract procedure, in the manner of
Andrews and McIntyre (1978a)
and most authors since. This is best thought of as ensemble averaging, that is, averaging over a collection of independent flow realisations. This can be approximated in practice by temporal or spatial averaging in a single flow, usually building on a time scale or spatial scale separation.

The differential geometry machinery we rely on is standard and exposed, for instance, in the books by
Schutz (1980)
or
Frankel (2004)

We primarily use a coordinate-free notation which greatly simplifies most derivations. The geometric approach is also effective when it comes to obtain explicit coordinate expressions. We illustrate this with a few detailed computations.

Vectors and forms

The familiar (contravariant) vectors have duals, namely 1-forms (or covariant vectors) defined as linear maps on vectors, such that the pairing
\bm{\alpha}(\mathbf{v})

of the 1-form
\bm{\alpha}
with the vector
\mathbf{v}
is a real number. In coordinates
x^{i}
, vectors and 1-forms are written as

\mathbf{v}=v^{i}\bm{\partial}_{i}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\bm{\alpha}=\alpha_{i}\,dx^{i},

(5)

where
\bm{\partial}_{i}=\mathbf{e}_{i}
are basis vectors (the notation calls to mind the interpretation of vectors as directional derivatives) and
dx^{i}
are basis 1-forms, respectively, and summation is implied. The two bases are dual in the sense that
dx^{i}(\bm{\partial}_{j})=\delta^{i}_{j}
. Together with linearity, this implies the coordinate expression

\bm{\alpha}(\mathbf{v})=\alpha_{i}v^{i}

(6)

for the pairing between
\bm{\alpha}
and
\mathbf{v}
.

In three dimensions, there are three other differential forms: 0-forms, which are just scalars, and 2- and 3-forms. The 2-forms
\bm{\beta}
and 3-forms
\bm{\gamma}
are defined as bi- and tri-linear antisymmetric maps on vectors, with

\bm{\beta}=\tfrac{1}{2}\beta_{ij}\,dx^{i}\wedge dx^{j}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\bm{\gamma}=\tfrac{1}{6}\gamma_{ijk}\,dx^{i}\wedge dx^{j}\wedge dx^{k}.

(7)

Here
\beta_{ij}
and
\gamma_{ijk}
are anti-symmetric in their indices and
(dx^{i}\wedge dx^{j})(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=u^{[i}v^{j]}=u^{i}v^{j}-u^{j}v^{i}
and
(dx^{i}\wedge dx^{j}\wedge dx^{k})(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})=u^{[i}v^{j}w^{k]}
, where
[\cdots]
denotes full (unnormalised) antisymmetrisation. 1-, 2- and 3-forms integrate over lines, surfaces and volumes, respectively.

The interior product
\mathop{\lrcorner}
pairs a
k
-form with a vector to yield a
(k-1)
-form, with

\mathbf{u}\mathop{\lrcorner}\bm{\alpha}=\bm{\alpha}(\mathbf{u})=\alpha_{i}u^{i},\quad\mathbf{u}\mathop{\lrcorner}\bm{\beta}=u^{i}\beta_{ij}\,dx^{j}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\mathbf{u}\mathop{\lrcorner}\bm{\gamma}=\tfrac{1}{2}u^{i}\gamma_{ijk}\,dx^{j}\wedge dx^{k}.

(8)

The exterior derivative
d
is a differential operator from fields of
k
-forms to fields of
(k+1)
-forms, with

df=\partial_{i}f\,dx^{i},\quad d\bm{\alpha}=\partial_{j}\alpha_{i}\,dx^{j}\wedge dx^{i},\quad d\bm{\beta}=\partial_{k}\beta_{ij}\,dx^{k}\wedge dx^{i}\wedge dx^{j}

(9)

and
d\bm{\gamma}=0
. It satisfies
d^{2}\equiv d\circ d=0
and encodes gradient, divergence and curl.

Action of maps: push-forward and pull-back

A map
\varphi
such as the flow map (using the position at
t=0
as label) takes a point
x
in the fluid domain and sends it to another,
\varphi(x)
. Maps have a natural action on vectors and differential forms. A vector
\mathbf{v}
at
x
is
pushed forward
to the vector
\varphi_{*}\mathbf{v}
at
\varphi(x)
obtained by joining the images under
\varphi
of the two infinitesimally close endpoints
x
and
x+\varepsilon\mathbf{v}
of
\mathbf{v}
with
\varepsilon\to 0
. The
push-forward
has coordinates

(\varphi_{*}\mathbf{v})^{i}(\varphi(x))=v^{j}(x)\,\partial_{j}\varphi^{i}(x).

(10)

Conversely, a 1-form
\bm{\alpha}
at
\varphi(x)
is
pulled back
to the 1-form
\varphi^{*}\bm{\alpha}
at
x
in such a way that
\bm{\alpha}(\varphi_{*}\mathbf{v})=(\varphi^{*}\bm{\alpha})(\mathbf{v})
for all
\mathbf{v}
. In coordinates,

(\varphi^{*}\bm{\alpha})_{i}(x)=\alpha_{j}(\varphi(x))\,\partial_{i}\varphi^{j}(x).

(11)

For an invertible map, the pull-back of vectors and push-forward of 1-forms are defined as the push-forward and pull-back by the inverse map
\varphi^{-1}
. Push-forward and pull-back can be applied to any tensors, starting with scalar functions for which

(\varphi_{*}f)(x)=f(\varphi^{-1}(x))

and

(\varphi^{*}f)(x)=f(\varphi(x))

The definition for higher-order tensors uses their interpretation as multilinear maps on vectors or 1-forms. A useful property is that push-forward and pull-back commute with the exterior derivative
d
.

Lie derivative

The notion of material derivative, or derivative along a flow, is expressed by the Lie derivative, an infinitesimal version of the pull-back which applies to scalars, vectors, 1-forms and higher-order tensors. Given a vector field
\mathbf{v}
, we can define the associated flow map
\psi_{s}
, such that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\psi_{s}(x)=\mathbf{v}(\psi_{s}(x))\quad\textrm{and}\quad\psi_{0}(x)=x,

(12)

with
s
a time-like variable (fictitious time), independent of the actual time
t
. The Lie derivative of a tensor field
\tau
(independent of
s
) is then

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}\tau=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right|_{s=0}\psi_{s}^{*}\tau.

(13)

This is the rate of change of
\tau
as we pull it back under the flow
\psi_{s}
associated with
\mathbf{v}
.
Applying this to scalar, vector and 1-form fields gives the coordinate expressions

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}\,f=v^{j}\partial_{j}f,\quad(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}\,\mathbf{u})^{i}=v^{j}\partial_{j}u^{i}-u^{j}\partial_{j}v^{i}\quad\textrm{and}\quad(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}\,\bm{\alpha})_{i}=v^{j}\partial_{j}\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j}\partial_{i}v^{j}.

(14)

The useful formula

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,\varphi^{*}\tau=\varphi^{*}\left(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\right)\tau

(15)

relates the time derivative of the pull-back of any time dependent tensor
\tau
by the flow map
\varphi
to its Lie derivative with respect to the velocity field
\mathbf{u}
. Kelvin’s circulation theorem and the frozen-in nature of vorticity for the Euler equations are immediate applications of this formula.
The Lie derivative commutes with the exterior derivative operator
d
and transforms naturally under pull-back:

\displaystyle d\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}\tau=\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}\,d\tau\quad\textrm{and}\quad\varphi^{*}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}\,\tau=\mathcal{L}_{\varphi^{*}\mathbf{v}}\,\varphi^{*}\tau.

(16)

Geometric formulation of the Boussinesq equations

We use the Boussinesq equations governing the dynamics of incompressible rotating stratified fluids as representative of the class of models typically considered in studies of wave–mean-flow interactions. In standard form, they read

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{f}\times\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=-\nabla p+b\,\mathbf{e}_{3},

(17a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}b+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla b

\displaystyle=0,

(17b)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=0,

(17c)

where
\mathbf{u}=(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3})
is the velocity vector,
\mathbf{e}_{3}
is the vertical unit vector,
\mathbf{f}=f\,\mathbf{e}_{3}
, with
f
the Coriolis parameter,
p
is the pressure scaled by the uniform background density
\rho_{0}
, and
b
is the buoyancy acceleration, defined as
b=-g\rho/\rho_{0}
, with
\rho
the density perturbation
(
Vallis, 2006
, e.g.)
.

Eqs. (
17
) apply to Euclidean space and Cartesian coordinates. They generalise to arbitrary manifolds and coordinates in a way that is straightforward in appearance only: the derivative
\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}
needs to be interpreted as the covariant derivative
\nabla_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{u}
, a sophisticated object that involves Christoffel symbols and is difficult to average. It is therefore preferable to write the Boussinesq equations in an alternative form that involves the simpler Lie derivative – the natural derivative along the flow. This necessitates making use of the momentum 1-form
\bm{\nu}=\nu_{i}\,dx^{i}
as well as of the velocity vector
\mathbf{u}=u^{i}\,\bm{\partial}_{i}
.
Here and in what follows, summation over repeated indices is understood.

For Eqs. (
17
),
\bm{\nu}
is just a version of
\mathbf{u}
, obtained by lowering the superscript using the metric
g
,

\bm{\nu}=\mathbf{u}_{\flat}=g(\mathbf{u},\cdot),\quad\textrm{or, in coordinates,}\ \ \nu_{i}=g_{ij}u^{j},

(18)

In Cartesian coordinates, since
g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}
, this is simply

\bm{\nu}=u_{1}\,dx_{1}+u_{2}\,dx_{2}+u_{3}\,dx_{3}=\mathbf{u}\cdot d\mathbf{x},

(19)

where we use
\mathbf{x}=(x^{1},x^{2},x^{3})
and the dot product formally, as shorthand for handling multiple components, with no implication that
\mathbf{x}
or
d\mathbf{x}
should be regarded as vectors.

In terms of
\bm{\nu}
, Eqs. (
17
) take the form

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}})(\bm{\nu}+\bm{\lambda})

\displaystyle=-d\pi+b\,dx^{3},

(20a)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}})b

\displaystyle=0,

(20b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\,\mu

\displaystyle=0,

(20c)

where

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}
denotes the Lie derivative along
\mathbf{u}
(see sidebar),

\pi=p-\tfrac{1}{2}|\mathbf{u}|^{2}-\bm{\lambda}(\mathbf{u})
with
|\mathbf{u}|^{2}=g(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})=\bm{\nu}(\mathbf{u})
, and

\bm{\lambda}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{f}\times\mathbf{x})\cdot d\mathbf{x}=\tfrac{1}{2}f(-x^{2}\,dx^{1}+x^{1}\,dx^{2}).

(21)

The Coriolis terms can be verified to reduce to that in Eq. (
17a
) by making use of Cartan’s formula to write

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\bm{\lambda}-d\bm{\lambda}(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{u}\mathop{\lrcorner}d\bm{\lambda}=(\mathbf{f}\times\mathbf{u})\cdot d\mathbf{x}.

(22)

The incompressibility condition (
20c
) can equivalently be written as
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\mathbf{u}=0
since
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\,\mu=(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\mathbf{u})\mu
defines the divergence.

The form of Eq. (
20

Arnold, 1966

Arnold and Khesin, 1998

Holm et al., 1998

and the replacement of

The Lie derivative
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}
which appears in the momentum Eq. (
20a

\bm{\nu}
along the flow of
\mathbf{u}
. Unlike the covariant derivative of
\mathbf{u}
that appears in Eq. (

For our purposes, it is essential to keep a clear distinction between the momentum 1-form field
\bm{\nu}
and the vector field
\mathbf{u}
, that is, between the advected and advecting fields in Eq. (

\bm{\nu}

\bm{\nu}=u^{1}\,dx^{1}+u^{2}\,dx^{2},

(23)

while retaining the three components of
\mathbf{u}
elsewhere. Holm’s

(
Holm, 1999
,
Marsden and Shkoller, 2001
,
Marsden and Shkoller, 2003
,
Oliver and Vasylkevych, 2019
)
or Tao’s modification of the Euler equations
(
Tao, 2016
)
are other, more involved examples of non-trivial relations between
\bm{\nu}
and
\mathbf{u}
. In the presence of rotation, it is convenient
to rewrite Eq. (
20a

(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}})\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}=-d\pi+b\,dx^{3}.

(24)

This introduces the absolute momentum

\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}=\bm{\nu}+\bm{\lambda},

(25)

which is also not wholly related to
\mathbf{u}
via the metric.

Momentum equation: from 1-form to components

We illustrate the direct manipulation of forms by deriving the Boussinesq momentum equation in coordinates from the 1-form formulation Eq. (
20a
) The most useful rules for such manipulations are the Leibniz product rule for the Lie derivative and the commutation of Lie derivative and exterior derivative. With
\bm{\lambda}=\lambda_{i}\,dx^{i}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{f}\times\mathbf{x})_{i}\,dx^{i}
, we write the left-hand side of Eq. (
20a
) as

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}})(\nu_{i}\,dx^{i}+\lambda_{i}\,dx^{i})

\displaystyle=\bigl((\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}})\nu_{i}\bigr)\,dx^{i}+\nu_{i}d(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\,x^{i})+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}(\lambda_{i})\,dx^{i}+\lambda_{i}\,d(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\,x^{i})

(26a)

\displaystyle=\bigl((\partial_{t}+u^{j}\partial_{j})\nu_{i}\bigr)\,dx^{i}+\nu_{i}\,du^{i}+u^{j}\partial_{j}\lambda_{i}\,dx^{i}+\lambda_{i}\,du^{i}.

(26b)

The right-hand side reads

-d(p-\tfrac{1}{2}\nu_{i}u^{i}-\lambda_{i}u^{i})+b\,dx^{3}=-\partial_{i}p\,dx^{i}+\tfrac{1}{2}\nu_{i}\,du^{i}+\tfrac{1}{2}u_{i}\,d\nu^{i}+\lambda_{i}\,du^{i}+u^{i}\partial_{j}\lambda_{i}\,dx^{j}+b\,dx^{3}.

(27)

Equating Eqs. (
26b
) and (
27
) and noting that
u^{j}\partial_{j}\lambda_{i}\,dx^{i}-u^{i}\partial_{j}\lambda_{i}\,dx^{j}=(\mathbf{f}\times\mathbf{u})\cdot d\mathbf{x}

and that, in Euclidean space,
\nu_{i}=u^{i}
gives

\bigl((\partial_{t}+u^{j}\partial_{j})u_{i}+(\mathbf{f}\times\mathbf{u})_{i}\bigr)\,dx^{i}=-\partial_{i}p\,dx^{i}+b\,dx^{3},

(28)

matching the standard form Eq. (
17a
)
of the Boussinesq momentum equation.

3
Lagrangian averaging

Fluid models involve material transport, of momentum, vorticity and buoyancy in the case of the Boussinesq equations, as is made plain by the presence of the operator
\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}
in Eqs. (
20
)
Lagrangian averaging preserves this structure by averaging various fields not at fixed Eulerian position
x
, but at fixed Lagrangian label
a
. We now review how this averaging is carried out.

3.1
Scalar example: buoyancy equation

Let us consider the (scalar) buoyancy
b
as a straightforward example. Averaging Eq. (
20b
) at fixed
x
, that is, performing an Eulerian average, gives

\partial_{t}\bar{b}+\overline{\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\,b}=\partial_{t}\bar{b}+\overline{u_{j}\partial_{j}b}=\partial_{t}\bar{b}+\mathcal{L}_{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}\,\bar{b}+\overline{u^{\prime}_{j}\partial_{j}b^{\prime}}=0,

(29)

where we have introduced perturbations to the mean fields, denoted by primes, by writing
\mathbf{u}=\bar{\mathbf{u}}+\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
and
b=\bar{b}+b^{\prime}
. The last term in Eq. (
29
), involving a product of perturbations, spoils the transport structure of the buoyancy equation. In contrast, if we let
B(a)=b(\varphi(a))
, Eq. (
20b
) becomes
\partial_{t}B=0
, leading upon averaging to

\partial_{t}\bar{B}=0,

(30)

with
\bar{B}(a)=\overline{b(\varphi(a))}
the Lagrangian average of the buoyancy.

While this equation is formally simple it is not practical: the intricate nature of the flow map in all but the simplest flows makes it impossible to recover useful information about
b
from
B
or
\bar{B}
. Generalised Lagrangian Mean (GLM) theories offer a solution by regarding
\bar{B}
not as a function of the label
a
but as a function of a mean position ascribed to the particle identified by
a
.

GLM relies on the decomposition

\varphi=\Xi\circ\bar{\bar{\varphi}}

(31)

of the flow map, factorised as the composition of the mean flow map
\bar{\bar{\varphi}}
and a (realisation-dependent) perturbation map
\Xi
. The precise definition of
\bar{\bar{\varphi}}
is unimportant for now. What matters is that it is chosen such that, in all flow realisations, the perturbations
\Xi
remain close to the identity for long times. This is required so that
\bar{\bar{\varphi}}
can serve as a representative of the ensemble of flow maps, in accordance with the intuitive interpretation of the mean. It is also necessary in practice to ensure the convergence of asymptotic or numerical procedures for the computation of Lagrangian mean quantities.

3.2
General Lagrangian averaging and the momentum equation

An alternative derivation of Eq. (
35
) uses the geometric machinery of pull-back and Lie derivative: first note that
\overline{b}^{\mathrm{L}}(x)=\overline{b(\Xi(x))}
, that is,

\overline{b}^{\mathrm{L}}=\overline{\Xi^{*}b}.

(36)

The key identity

\Xi^{*}(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}})\tau=(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}})\Xi^{*}\tau,

(37)

valid for any tensor field
\tau
,
can then be exploited
(
Gilbert and Vanneste, 2018
)
. Applying
\Xi^{*}
to Eq. (
20b
) gives, after averaging,

(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}})\overline{b}^{\mathrm{L}}=0,

(38)

i.e. Eq. (
35
)

The advantage of this derivation is that it readily generalises: equations involving the material transport of an arbitrary tensor
\tau
expressed as a Lie derivative are handled by first pulling them back to the mean configuration using
\Xi
, then averaging. This gives

\overline{\Xi^{*}(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}})\tau}=(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}})\overline{\tau}^{\mathrm{L}}.

(39)

Here,
\overline{\tau}^{\mathrm{L}}
denotes the Lagrangian mean of
\tau
, defined as

\overline{\tau}^{\mathrm{L}}=\overline{\Xi^{*}\tau},

(40)

generalising the scalar definition of Eq. (
36
) We emphasise that Eq. (
40
) provides a natural, coordinate-independent construction of the Lagrangian mean, one that applies to any tensorial object. It is geometrically sound, pulling back the ensemble of values of the tensor
\tau
to the same point before carrying out the average. We reserve the notation

\overline{\phantom{u}}^{\mathrm{L}}
for this construction and caution that this convention is not adopted in standard GLM literature where
\overline{\phantom{u}}^{\mathrm{L}}
is sometimes used for mean fields constructed differently.

With the definition in Eq. (
40
), applying
\Xi^{*}
to Eq. (
24
) yields

(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}})\Xi^{*}{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}=-d\,\Xi^{*}{\pi}+\Xi^{*}({b\,dx^{3}}),

(41)

using that pull-back and exterior derivative
d
commute. Averaging then gives

(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}})\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}^{\mathrm{L}}=-d\overline{\pi}^{\mathrm{L}}+\overline{b\,dx^{3}}^{\mathrm{L}}.

(42)

The Lagrangian mean momentum equation (
42
) closely resembles the original momentum equation (
20a
). The key difference is that the link between advected momentum and advecting velocity field – the trivial
\bm{\nu}=\mathbf{u}_{\flat}
of the original equation – is broken:
\overline{\bm{\nu}}^{\mathrm{L}}
and
\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}
are not directly related,
\overline{\bm{\nu}}^{\mathrm{L}}\not=\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}_{\flat}
. Physically, this is because perturbations to the mean have a dynamical effect which is manifested in the mismatch. It is natural to introduce a field that quantifies the mismatch and hence encodes the impact of the perturbations on the mean. This field is the pseudomomentum (or at least a version thereof).

To include rotation, we define the pseudomomentum 1-form
\bm{\mathsf{p}}
as (minus) the difference between the absolute Lagrangian-averaged momentum
\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}^{\mathrm{L}}=\overline{\bm{\nu}}^{\mathrm{L}}+\overline{\bm{\lambda}}^{\mathrm{L}}
and the absolute momentum associated with the mean velocity, that is,

-\bm{\mathsf{p}}=\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}^{\mathrm{L}}-(\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}_{\flat}+\bm{\lambda}).

(43)

With this definition, we can rewrite the Lagrangian-mean momentum Eq. (
42
) as

(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}})(\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}_{\flat}-\bm{\mathsf{p}})+\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}\mathop{\lrcorner}d\bm{\lambda}=-d\bigl(\overline{\pi}^{\mathrm{L}}+\bm{\lambda}(\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}})\bigr)+\overline{b\,dx^{3}}^{\mathrm{L}}.

(44)

This is a geometric form of Theorem I of
Andrews and McIntyre (1978a)
.

Several points are noteworthy. First, because the Lie derivative in Eq. (
44
) is applied to a 1-form, namely
\bm{\alpha}=\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}_{\flat}-\bm{\mathsf{p}}
, its coordinate expression reads
\bar{\bar{u}}^{j}\partial_{j}\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j}\partial_{i}\bar{\bar{u}}^{j}
, which explains what might otherwise be thought as a peculiar combination of terms in Theorem I, as noted in Eq. (
1
). Second, the Coriolis term

\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}\mathop{\lrcorner}d\bm{\lambda}

on the left-hand side involves the Lagrangian mean velocity rather than, say, the Lagrangian mean momentum or the Eulerian mean velocity. This is central to the conclusion that, for fast rotation, geostrophic balance is between the Lagrangian mean velocity and the gradient of a pressure-like mean field
(
Moore, 1970
,
Andrews and McIntyre, 1978a
,
Xie and Vanneste, 2015
,
Wagner and Young, 2015
,
Kafiabad et al., 2021
)
. Third, while the pressure-like term is complicated,

\overline{\pi}^{\mathrm{L}}+\bm{\lambda}(\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}})=\overline{p\circ\Xi-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{u}\circ\Xi)\cdot\left(\mathbf{u}\circ\Xi+\mathbf{f}\times\bm{\Xi}\right)}+\tfrac{1}{2}\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot(\mathbf{f}\times\mathbf{x}),

(45)

with
\bm{\Xi}=(\Xi^{1},\Xi^{2},\Xi^{3})
, its origin is clear. The same can be said of the coordinate expression for
\bm{\mathsf{p}}
, deduced from Eq. (
43
) to be

-\mathsf{p}_{i}=\overline{(\nu_{j}\circ\Xi)\,\partial_{i}\Xi^{j}}-g_{ij}\bar{\bar{u}}^{j}+\tfrac{1}{2}\overline{\left(\mathbf{f}\times\bm{\Xi}\right)_{j}\partial_{i}\Xi^{j}}-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{f}\times\mathbf{x})_{i}.

(46)

Fourth, the buoyancy term in Eq. (
44
),
\overline{b\,dx^{3}}^{\mathrm{L}}=\overline{(\Xi^{*}b)\,d\,\Xi^{3}}=\overline{(b\circ\Xi)\,d\,\Xi^{3}}
,
can be expressed in terms of the Lagrangian mean buoyancy under the assumption, which we make henceforth, that the ensemble of buoyancy fields to be averaged results from the same initial field,
b_{0}
say. In this case, Eq. (
20b
) implies that
b=\varphi_{*}b_{0}=\Xi_{*}\bar{\bar{\varphi}}_{*}b_{0}
hence

\Xi^{*}b=\bar{\bar{\varphi}}_{*}b_{0}

(47)

is independent of the flow realisation and equal to its average,

\Xi^{*}b=\overline{\Xi^{*}b}=\overline{b}^{\mathrm{L}}.

(48)

This reduces the buoyancy term in Eq. (
44
) to

\overline{b\,dx^{3}}^{\mathrm{L}}=\overline{b}^{\mathrm{L}}d\overline{\Xi^{3}}.

(49)

3.3
Circulation, vorticity and potential vorticity

The circulation around a closed curve
\mathscr{C}
is expressed in terms of the momentum as

\oint_{\mathscr{C}}\bm{\nu},

(50)

since 1-forms are naturally integrated along curves. The form of the momentum Eq. (
20a
) is ideal to derive Kelvin’s circulation theorem. Let
\mathscr{C}_{t}=\varphi_{*}\mathscr{C}_{0}
denote a material closed curve, thought of as the image at time
t
of a curve
\mathscr{C}_{0}
in label space. Then,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\oint_{\mathscr{C}_{t}}\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\oint_{\mathscr{C}_{0}}\varphi^{*}\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}=\oint_{\mathscr{C}_{0}}\varphi^{*}(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}})\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}=\oint_{\mathscr{C}_{t}}(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}})\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}

(51a)

\displaystyle=\oint_{\mathscr{C}_{t}}b\,dx^{3}=-\oint_{\mathscr{C}_{t}}x^{3}\,db,

(51b)

where we use Eq. (
24
), that
b\,dx^{3}=d(bx^{3})-x^{3}\,db
and the fact that integrals of exact differentials over closed curves vanish. The absolute circulation
\oint_{\mathscr{C}_{t}}\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}
is conserved provided that
\mathscr{C}_{t}
lies on a constant-buoyancy surface
b=\mathrm{const}.
(equivalently,
\mathscr{C}_{0}
lies on a surface
b_{0}=\mathrm{const}
).

The steps leading to Eq. (
51b
) can be applied to the Lagrangian mean momentum Eq. (
42
) For a contour
\overline{\mathscr{C}}_{t}=\bar{\bar{\varphi}}_{*}\mathscr{C}_{0}=\Xi^{*}\mathscr{C}_{t}
that moves with the mean velocity
\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}
, this gives

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\oint_{\overline{\mathscr{C}}_{t}}\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}^{\mathrm{L}}=\oint_{\overline{\mathscr{C}}_{t}}\overline{b\,dx_{3}}^{\mathrm{L}}=-\oint_{\overline{\mathscr{C}}_{t}}\overline{\Xi^{3}}\,d\overline{b}^{\mathrm{L}},

(52)

using Eq. (
49
) The same result is obtained by averaging Eq. (
51b
) directly, noting that

\overline{\oint_{\mathscr{C}_{t}}\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}=\oint_{\overline{\mathscr{C}}_{t}}\overline{\Xi^{*}\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}=\oint_{\overline{\mathscr{C}}_{t}}\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}^{\mathrm{L}}.

(53)

Vorticity is best considered as the 2-form
d\bm{\nu}
obtained by taking the exterior derivative of the 1-form
\bm{\nu}
:

\displaystyle d\bm{\nu}

\displaystyle=d(\nu_{1}\,dx^{1}+\nu_{2}\,dx^{2}+\nu_{3}\,dx^{3})

(54a)

\displaystyle=(\partial_{2}\nu_{3}-\partial_{3}\nu_{2})\,dx^{2}\wedge dx^{3}+(\partial_{3}\nu_{1}-\partial_{1}\nu_{3})\,dx^{3}\wedge dx^{1}+(\partial_{1}\nu_{2}-\partial_{2}\nu_{1})\,dx^{1}\wedge dx^{2}.

(54b)

In three dimensions,
d\bm{\nu}
can be identified with a vorticity vector
\bm{\zeta}
via
\bm{\zeta}\mathop{\lrcorner}\mu=d\bm{\nu}
. (In
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
dx^{2}\wedge dx^{3}
is identified with
\mathbf{e}_{1}=\bm{\partial}_{1}
, etc., and the components in Eq. (
54b
) are also those of
\bm{\zeta}
.)
The Boussinesq vorticity equation is a short step away from the momentum Eq. (
24
): applying
d
, using its commutation with the Lie derivative and that
d^{2}=0
gives

(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}})d\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}=db\wedge dx^{3}.

(55)

In
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and accounting for incompressibility, the corresponding vector form
(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}})\bm{\zeta}_{\mathrm{a}}=\partial_{2}b\,\mathbf{e}_{1}-\partial_{1}b\,\mathbf{e}_{2}
, involves the familiar transport term
\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\bm{\zeta}-\bm{\zeta}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}
.

The material conservation of potential vorticity is deduced by taking the wedge product of Eq. (
55
) with
db
, which satisfies
(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}})db=0
, and using that
db\wedge db=0
to obtain the material conservation law

(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}})Q=0,

(56)

where
Q
is the potential vorticity 3-form

Q=d\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}\wedge db.

(57)

As a 3-form,
Q
is naturally integrated over volumes. It can be interpreted as
Haynes and McIntyre
’s (
1987
,
1990
) ‘potential vorticity substance’, with its nature as a divergence made clear by rewriting it as
Q=-d(b\,d\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}})

(
Bretherton and Schär, 1993
, see also)
. The usual scalar potential vorticity
q
is defined by

q\mu=Q.

(58)

It is also conserved,
(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}})q=0
, thanks to the incompressibility condition
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\,\mu=0
.

The Lagrangian mean counterparts of Eqs. (
55
) and (
56
) are readily obtained by pulling back these equations and averaging. With
\Xi^{*}b=\overline{\Xi^{*}b}=\overline{b}^{\mathrm{L}}
, we obtain from Eq. (
55
) that

(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}})\overline{d\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}^{\mathrm{L}}=d\overline{b}^{\mathrm{L}}\wedge d\overline{\Xi^{3}},\quad\textrm{where}\quad\overline{d\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}^{\mathrm{L}}=\overline{\Xi^{*}d\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}=d\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}^{\mathrm{L}}

(59)

is both the Lagrangian mean of the vorticity, and the vorticity associated (via the exterior derivative, equivalent to a curl) with the Lagrangian mean momentum. Similarly, Eq. (
56
) gives

(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}})\overline{Q}^{\mathrm{L}}=0,\quad\textrm{where}\quad\overline{Q}^{\mathrm{L}}=\overline{\Xi^{*}Q}=d\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}^{\mathrm{L}}\wedge d\overline{b}^{\mathrm{L}}

(60)

is both the Lagrangian mean of the potential vorticity, and the potential vorticity constructed from
\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}^{\mathrm{L}}
and
\overline{b}^{\mathrm{L}}
.

In summary, provided that the Lagrangian mean momentum 1-form
\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}^{\mathrm{L}}
is taken as starting point instead of the mean velocity vector
\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}
(or the vector
\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{f}\times\mathbf{x}
), the relations between Lagrangian mean momentum, vorticity and potential vorticity are straightforward and parallel those of the original Boussinesq equations. The differential geometric machinery makes the derivation of these relations particularly simple, relying on the consistent use of Eq. (
40
) to define all Lagrangian mean quantities and on the commutation of the exterior derivative with the pull-back
\Xi^{*}
and Lie derivative.

A subtlety arises with the scalar potential vorticity. This is because the Lagrangian mean velocity
\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}
need not be
divergence free or, equivalently, the mean map
\bar{\bar{\varphi}}
need not be
volume preserving, unlike the divergence free
\mathbf{u}
and volume preserving
\varphi
in each flow realisation (we discuss this further in §
4
).
To handle this, we define the Lagrangian mean mass 3-form

\overline{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}}=\Xi^{*}\mu=\bar{\bar{\varphi}}_{*}\mu.

(61)

The second equality follows from the incompressibility condition
\varphi_{*}\mu=\mu
. It shows that
\Xi^{*}\mu
is realisation independent and hence equal to its average
\overline{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}}
. In Euclidean space, with
\mu=dx^{1}\wedge dx^{2}\wedge dx^{3}
,

\overline{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}}=d\Xi^{1}\wedge d\Xi^{2}\wedge d\Xi^{3}=\det\bigl(\partial_{j}\Xi^{i}\bigr)\,\mu.

(62)

Pulling back the continuity equation with
\Xi^{*}
shows that
\overline{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}}
satisfies

(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}})\overline{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}}=0.

(63)

Combining this with Eq. (
60
) gives the material conservation along the mean flow
\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}
of the scalar
\bar{\bar{q}}
defined by
\overline{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}}\bar{\bar{q}}=Q
. This differs from the Lagrangian mean
\overline{q}^{\mathrm{L}}
of
q
unless
\bar{\bar{\varphi}}
preserves volume. The effective mean density
\tilde{\rho}
used by
Andrews and McIntyre (1978a)
,
Bühler (2014)
and others to handle the divergence of the mean flow is such that
\tilde{\rho}\mu=\overline{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}}
.

4
Mean flow definitions

So far, we have not specified how the mean velocity
\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}
is chosen beyond the loose requirement that the associated flow map
\bar{\bar{\varphi}}
be a good representative of the ensemble of flow maps
\varphi
. This makes it clear that the results of the previous sections are independent of this choice. The conventional definition of
\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}
and
\bar{\bar{\varphi}}
is that of the GLM theory of
Andrews and McIntyre (1978a)
. The simplest way of expressing this definition is as follows. For a choice of coordinates
x^{i}
, the flow map has coordinates
\varphi^{i}(a)
. The mean flow map is defined coordinate-wise by

\bar{\bar{\varphi}}^{i}(a)=\overline{\varphi^{i}(a)}.

(64)

Thus the mean position of particle
a
at time
t
is obtained by averaging the three coordinates of that particle at time
t
over flow realisations. Equivalently, we can write this as
\overline{\Xi^{i}(x)}=x^{i}
or, introducing the displacement components
\xi^{i}
such that

\Xi^{i}(x)=x^{i}+\xi^{i}(x),

(65)

as

\overline{\xi^{i}(x)}=0.

(66)

This implicit form is the one originally proposed by
Andrews and McIntyre (1978a)
and used since. We emphasise that the construction is based on a choice of coordinates. Only in Euclidean space, where positions can be interpreted as vectors, have Eqs. (
64
)–(
66
) an intrinsic vectorial meaning.

The GLM mean velocity is obtained by differentiating Eq. (
64
) with respect to
t
to find
\bar{\bar{u}}^{i}(\bar{\bar{\varphi}}(a))=\overline{u^{i}(\varphi(a))}
, hence

\bar{\bar{u}}^{i}(x)=\overline{u^{i}(\Xi(x))},\quad\textrm{i.e.}\quad\bar{\bar{u}}^{i}=\overline{u^{i}\circ\Xi}.

(67)

In general, this expression is not geometrically intrinsic: it depends on the choice of coordinates in which the components
u^{i}
are expressed. The tempting coordinate-free version
\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}=\overline{\mathbf{u}\circ\Xi}
is not well defined: the vectors
\mathbf{u}\circ\Xi
corresponding to different flow realisations live in different tangent planes and hence cannot be averaged, unless in Euclidean space where vectors can be translated freely.

The lack of intrinsic geometric meaning results in inconvenient properties.
First, the Lagrangian mean velocity is generally divergent, that is, the Lagrangian mean flow does not preserve volume, unlike all the original flows in the ensemble
(
McIntyre, 1988
)
. Second, the range of
\bar{\bar{\varphi}}
may differ from the fluid domain, most strikingly if the domain is a spherical shell or the surface of a 2-sphere. On the other hand, Eqs. (
64
) and (
66
) lead to simplifications. For instance, the pseudomomentum in Eq. (
46
) reduces to

-\mathsf{p}_{i}=\overline{(u^{j}\circ\Xi)\,\partial_{i}\xi^{j}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\overline{(\mathbf{f}\times\bm{\xi})_{j}\,\partial_{i}\xi^{j}},

(68)

when we further assume the space to be Euclidean so that
g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}
. The explicit definition of the GLM mean flow is also convenient for the numerical computation of Lagrangian averages
(
Kafiabad, 2022
,
Kafiabad and Vanneste, 2023
)
.

An alternative definition of the mean flow is the glm definition proposed by
Soward and Roberts (2010)
. It replaces Eq. (
66
) by a geometrically meaningful condition. The central idea is to use a (realisation-dependent) vector field,
\mathbf{q}
say, as a proxy for the perturbation map
\Xi
(
\mathbf{q}
should not be confused with the scalar potential vorticity
q
introduced earlier). The vector field
\mathbf{q}
generates
\Xi
in the sense that the flow of
\mathbf{q}
from some fictitious time
\varepsilon=0
to
\varepsilon=1
is
\Xi
. Explicitly,
\mathbf{q}
defines a one-parameter family of flow maps
\Xi_{\varepsilon}
(at fixed
t
) with

\partial_{\varepsilon}\Xi_{\varepsilon}(x)=\mathbf{q}(\Xi_{\varepsilon}(x)),\quad\quad\Xi_{0}(x)=x\ \ \textrm{and}\ \ \Xi_{1}(x)=\Xi(x).

(69)

The glm mean flow is then prescribed by requiring that

\overline{\mathbf{q}}=0.

(70)

This is a geometric statement that applies on any manifold and is independent of the choice of coordinates. Importantly, for incompressible fluids, the volume preservation of
\Xi
and hence of
\bar{\bar{\varphi}}
is easily enforced through the linear constraint

\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\mathbf{q}=0,

(71)

which survives averaging. A disadvantage of glm is that the property
\overline{\xi^{i}}=0
does not hold, which precludes simplifications such as those leading to the compact form Eq. (
66
) Another is that the correspondence between
\mathbf{q}
and
\Xi
is only formal: not all diffeomorphisms can be obtained as time-
1
flows of a steady vector field. It can however be implemented using asymptotic expansions, disregarding questions of convergence.

Definitions of the mean flow other than those of GLM or glm are possible.
Gilbert and Vanneste (2018)
propose to take
\bar{\bar{\varphi}}
as the map closest to the realisations
\varphi
in a least-square sense, with the distance between flow maps taken as the ‘Arnold distance’ that is, the distance whose geodesics are solutions of the Euler equations
(
Arnold, 1966
,
Ebin and Marsden, 1970
,
Arnold and Khesin, 1998
,
Holm et al., 1985
)
. Different definitions lead to a different decomposition of the flow maps
\varphi
between mean part
\bar{\bar{\varphi}}
and perturbations
\Xi
. All acceptable definitions should lead to mean flow maps
that remain close to one another for long times.

5
Small-amplitude perturbations

Practical results are obtained using perturbation expansions based on the closeness of the perturbation maps
\Xi
to the identity. A typical set up expands the velocity as

\mathbf{u}=\varepsilon\mathbf{u}^{(1)}+\varepsilon^{2}\mathbf{u}^{(2)}+\cdots,

(72)

where
\mathbf{u}^{(1)}
is a wave field with
\overline{\mathbf{u}^{(1)}}=0
and
\varepsilon\ll 1
an amplitude parameter. Here and in what follows
\cdots
denotes neglected terms that are
O(\varepsilon^{3})
.
In Euclidean space and with the GLM definition, the mean velocity is

\displaystyle\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathbf{u}(x+\varepsilon\xi^{(1)}+\varepsilon^{2}\xi^{(2)}+\cdots)}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\varepsilon^{2}\overline{{\mathbf{u}^{(2)}}}}_{\textrm{Eulerian mean}}+\underbrace{\varepsilon^{2}\overline{\bm{\xi}^{(1)}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}^{(1)}}}_{\textrm{Stokes drift }\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{{\mathrm{S}}}}+\cdots.

(73)

The Stokes drift and hence Lagrangian mean velocity are divergent, with

\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}=\partial_{i}\bar{\bar{u}}^{i}=\varepsilon^{2}\,\partial_{t}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{ij}\bigl(\overline{\xi^{(1)i}\xi^{(1)j}}\bigr)\bigr)+\cdots,

(74)

where we use that
\partial_{t}\bm{\xi}^{(1)}=\mathbf{u}^{(1)}
. Because this is an exact time derivative, the volume change associated with this divergence stays bounded and small over long times.

The Lagrangian mean momentum is

\displaystyle\overline{{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\overline{\left(\nu_{j}\circ\Xi+\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{f}\times\bm{\Xi})_{j}\right)\partial_{i}\Xi^{j}}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{f}\times\mathbf{x})_{i}+\varepsilon^{2}\overline{{\nu}_{i}^{(2)}}+\varepsilon^{2}\,\overline{\xi^{(1)j}\partial_{j}\nu^{(1)}_{i}+\nu^{(1)}_{j}\partial_{i}\xi^{(1)j}+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{f}\times\bm{\xi}^{(1)}\right)_{j}\partial_{i}\xi^{(1)j}}+\cdots.

(75)

In Euclidean space,
u^{i}=\nu_{i}
and we can freely raise or lower indices. The pseudomomentum is then

-\mathsf{p}_{i}=\varepsilon^{2}\,\overline{\bigl(u^{(1)j}\partial_{i}\xi^{(1)j}+\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{f}\times\bm{\xi}^{(1)})_{j}\partial_{i}\xi^{(1)j}\bigr)}+\cdots,

(76)

consistent with Eq. (
68
)
Eqs. (
73
)–(
76
) are standard results of GLM theory. Together with the Lagrangian mean momentum equation, they make it possible to predict the evolution of the Lagrangian and Eulerian mean flows when the wave field
\mathbf{u}^{(1)}
is prescribed.

To obtain their glm counterparts, we implement a perturbative approach that uses
\Xi_{\varepsilon}
in Eq. (
69
) rather than
\Xi_{1}
as the perturbation map. We use the formal representation

\Xi_{\varepsilon}^{*}=\exp\left({\int^{\varepsilon}_{0}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{q}}\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\right)=\mathrm{id}+\varepsilon\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{q}^{(1)}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{q}^{(1)}}^{2}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{q}^{(2)}}\right)+\cdots

(77)

of the pull-back by
\Xi
in terms of the generating vector field
\mathbf{q}

(
Lichtenberg and Lieberman, 1992
,
Vanneste and Young, 2022
, e.g.)
. In particular,

\Xi^{i}(x)=\Xi^{*}x^{i}=x^{i}+\varepsilon q^{(1)i}+\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\bigl(q^{(1)j}\partial_{j}q^{(1)i}+q^{(2)i}\bigr)+\cdots,

(78)

so that

\bm{\xi}^{(1)}=\mathbf{q}^{(1)}\quad\textrm{and}\quad{\bm{\xi}}^{(2)}=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathbf{q}^{(1)}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{q}^{(1)}+\mathbf{q}^{(2)}\bigr).

(79)

Thus, at a linear,
O(\varepsilon)
level, the displacement
\bm{\xi}^{(1)}
and the generating vector
\mathbf{q}^{(1)}
can be identified. The constraint
\overline{\mathbf{q}}=0
implies the non-zero quadratic mean displacement

\overline{\bm{\xi}^{(2)}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\overline{\bm{\xi}^{(1)}\cdot\nabla\bm{\xi}^{(1)}}.

(80)

This stands in contrast with the GLM defining assumption of zero mean displacements, Eq. (
66
).

Time differentiating Eq. (
79
) further gives the perturbation velocity
\mathbf{w}=(\partial_{t}\Xi)\circ\Xi^{-1}
as

\mathbf{w}=\varepsilon\,\partial_{t}\mathbf{q}^{(1)}+\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\bigl(\partial_{t}\mathbf{q}^{(2)}-\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{q}^{(1)}}\partial_{t}\mathbf{q}^{(1)}\bigr)+\cdots.

(81)

Using Eq. (
77
) in Eq. (
33
) leads to

\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}=\varepsilon\bigl(\mathbf{u}^{(1)}-\partial_{t}\mathbf{q}^{(1)}\bigr)+\varepsilon^{2}\bigl(\mathbf{u}^{(2)}-\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{t}\mathbf{q}^{(2)}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{q}^{(1)}}\mathbf{u}^{(1)}-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{q}^{(1)}}\partial_{t}\mathbf{q}^{(1)}\bigr)+\cdots.

(82)

The
O(\varepsilon)
term needs to vanish for the left-hand side to be a mean quantity, hence
\partial_{t}\mathbf{q}^{(1)}=\mathbf{u}^{(1)}
. Taking this into account in the
O(\varepsilon^{2})
term and averaging leads to

\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}=\underbrace{\varepsilon^{2}\,\overline{\mathbf{u}^{(2)}}}_{\textrm{Eulerian mean}}+\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\,\overline{\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{q}^{(1)}}\mathbf{u}^{(1)}}}_{\textrm{solenoidal Stokes drift }\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{{\mathrm{S}}}_{\textrm{sol}}}+\,\cdots.

(83)

This is divergence free, in contrast to the GLM equivalent in Eq. (
73
)

Vanneste and Young (2022)
argue in the context of surface waves that the glm, solenoidal Stokes drift, which in Eucidean space can be rewritten as

\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{{\mathrm{S}}}_{\mathrm{sol}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\,\overline{\mathbf{q}^{(1)}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}^{(1)}-\mathbf{u}^{(1)}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{q}^{(1)}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\,\nabla\times\bigl(\overline{\mathbf{u}^{(1)}\times\mathbf{q}^{(1)}}\bigr),

(84)

is an advantageous alternative to the traditional Stokes drift
\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{{\mathrm{S}}}=\varepsilon^{2}\overline{\bm{\xi}^{(1)}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}^{(1)}}
.
The difference,

\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{{\mathrm{S}}}-\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{{\mathrm{S}}}_{\mathrm{sol}}=\varepsilon^{2}\,\bigl(\overline{\bm{\xi}^{(1)}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}^{(1)}-\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\xi}^{(1)}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}^{(1)}+\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathbf{u}}^{(1)}\cdot\nabla\bm{\xi}^{(1)}}\bigr)=\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\,\partial_{t}\bigl(\overline{\bm{\xi}^{(1)}\cdot\nabla\bm{\xi}^{(1)}}\bigr)

(85)

(using that
\mathbf{q}^{(1)}=\bm{\xi}^{(1)})
, is a time derivative and hence stays bounded over long time scales. This confirms that the mean trajectories according to GLM and glm definitions stay close to one another. The glm Lagrangian mean momentum is obtained directly using Eq. (
77
) as

\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}^{\mathrm{L}}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{f}\times\mathbf{x})\cdot d\mathbf{x}+\varepsilon^{2}\,\overline{\bm{\nu}^{(2)}}+\varepsilon^{2}\bigl(\overline{\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{q}^{(1)}}\bm{\nu}^{(1)}+\tfrac{1}{4}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{q}^{(1)}}^{2}(\mathbf{f}\times\mathbf{x})\cdot d\mathbf{x}}\bigr)+\cdots.

(86)

We note that, regardless of the specific definition of the mean, the Lagrangian mean of the velocity in the sense of Eq. (
40
) is approximated as

\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathrm{L}}=\overline{\Xi^{*}\mathbf{u}}=\varepsilon^{2}\,\overline{\mathbf{u}^{(2)}}+\varepsilon^{2}\,\overline{\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{q}^{(1)}}\mathbf{u}^{(1)}}+\cdots.

(87)

This differs from the mean velocity fields
\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}
obtained for GLM and glm, by an
O(\varepsilon^{2})
term that is not an exact time derivative. Thus the integral curves of
\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathrm{L}}
drift away from those of
\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}
(the mean trajectories
\bar{\bar{\varphi}}
), by
O(1)
distances after long,
O(\varepsilon^{-2})
times leading to perturbation maps
\Xi
well away from the identity. This makes
\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathrm{L}}
so defined unsuitable as a mean velocity.

For simplicity, we have assumed in the above that the velocity
\mathbf{u}
has no
O(1)
term.
Gilbert and Vanneste (2018)
give more general results that include the contributions from an
O(1)
, realisation-independent velocity
\mathbf{u}^{(0)}=\overline{\mathbf{u}^{(0)}}
.

6
Wave action

We now turn to the geometric formulation of wave activity conservation laws. Wave activities are quantities that, in the small-amplitude limit, are quadratic in the perturbation fields and so can be approximated to leading order, that is, to
O(\varepsilon^{2})
, from knowledge of an
O(\varepsilon)
approximation to the perturbation fields. Their conservation constrains the dynamics of the perturbations and can serve as a basis to parameterise wave–mean flow interactions. We use geometric language to extend results of
Andrews and McIntyre (1978b)
and
Bühler (2014, §10.3)
beyond the Euclidean setting, highlighting the role of symmetries.

We begin with the conservation of wave action, the wave activity associated with phase averaging. Suppose that the fluctuation maps
\Xi
in the decomposition Eq. (
31
) depend periodically on a phase parameter
\alpha
. An ensemble of flows is then obtained by varying
\alpha
. The associated average corresponds to integration with respect to
\alpha
, so that any average of an
\alpha
derivative vanishes,
\overline{\partial_{\alpha}\,\cdot\,}=0
.
Naturally the metric
g
and volume form
\mu
do not depend on
\alpha
and so for example we can write

\displaystyle\partial_{\alpha}g(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})=\partial_{\alpha}(\bm{\nu}(\mathbf{u}))=2g(\mathbf{u},\partial_{\alpha}\mathbf{u})=2\bm{\nu}(\partial_{\alpha}\mathbf{u}).

(88)

The mean map
\bar{\bar{\varphi}}
is also independent of
\alpha
. With this we can differentiate the composition of maps
\varphi=\Xi\circ\bar{\bar{\varphi}}
with respect to time
t
or
\alpha
and obtain two vector fields:
\mathbf{u}=(\partial_{t}\varphi)\circ\varphi^{-1}
, as earlier, and
\mathbf{z}=(\partial_{\alpha}\varphi)\circ\varphi^{-1}=(\partial_{\alpha}\Xi)\circ\Xi^{-1}
. These vector fields may be related through equating mixed second derivatives to obtain the key link

\displaystyle\partial_{\alpha}\mathbf{u}=\partial_{t}\mathbf{z}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\,{\mathbf{z}}.

(89)

Our starting point in deriving the wave action equation is the momentum equation (
20a
), which we first contract with
\mathbf{z}
. Let us focus first on the key term
(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}})\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}
to obtain

\displaystyle[(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}})\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}](\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}})(\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{z}))-\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}((\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}})\mathbf{z})

(90a)

\displaystyle=(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}})(\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{z}))-\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\partial_{\alpha}\mathbf{u})

(90b)

\displaystyle=(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}})(\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{z}))-\partial_{\alpha}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\nu}(\mathbf{u})+\bm{\lambda}(\mathbf{u})\right),

(90c)

making use of Eqs. (
88
) and (
89
)
This is written as transport of
\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{z})
and an
\alpha
-derivative that vanishes under averaging
(
Gilbert and Vanneste, 2018
)
.
However the scalar
\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{z})
does not provide a satisfactory definition of wave activity for the full equation of motion since its approximation to
O(\varepsilon^{2})
requires evaluation of the vector field
\mathbf{z}
to
O(\varepsilon^{2})
. A more suitable definition
(
Andrews and McIntyre, 1978b
, following)
is obtained by applying the pull-back
\Xi^{*}
and using Eq. (
37
) to find

\displaystyle\Xi^{*}\bigl([(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}})\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}](\mathbf{z})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\Xi^{*}\left[(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}})(\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{z}))\right]-\Xi^{*}\partial_{\alpha}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\nu}(\mathbf{u})+\bm{\lambda}(\mathbf{u})\right)

(91a)

\displaystyle=(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}})\Xi^{*}(\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{z}))-\partial_{\alpha}h+(\partial_{\alpha}\Xi^{*})\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\nu}(\mathbf{u})+\bm{\lambda}(\mathbf{u})\right),

(91b)

where
h=\Xi^{*}(\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\nu}(\mathbf{u})+\bm{\lambda}(\mathbf{u}))
and is unimportant as
\partial_{\alpha}h
vanishes under averaging.
Now the derivative of the pull-back
\partial_{\alpha}\Xi^{*}
is linked to the Lie derivative by
\mathbf{z}
according to

\displaystyle(\partial_{\alpha}\Xi^{*})\tau=\Xi^{*}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{z}}\,\tau

(92)

for any tensor
\tau
, generalising Eq. (
13
) Thus we obtain

\displaystyle\Xi^{*}\bigl([(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}})\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}](\mathbf{z})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\ (\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}})\Xi^{*}(\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{z}))-\partial_{\alpha}h+\Xi^{*}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{z}}\,(\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\nu}(\mathbf{u})+\bm{\lambda}(\mathbf{u})).

(93)

Returning to Eq. (
20a
), for the pressure-like term we have

\Xi^{*}(-d\pi(\mathbf{z}))=-\Xi^{*}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{z}}\pi
, which combines with the last terms of Eq. (
93
) to give
-\Xi^{*}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{z}}p
, with
p
the original pressure.
For the buoyancy term
b\,dx^{3}
, contracting with
\mathbf{z}
and pulling back by
\Xi^{*}
gives

\displaystyle\Xi^{*}[(b\,dx^{3})(\mathbf{z})]

\displaystyle=(\Xi^{*}b)\,\Xi^{*}[dx^{3}(\mathbf{z})]=\overline{b}^{\mathrm{L}}\,\Xi^{*}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{z}}\,x^{3}=\overline{b}^{\mathrm{L}}\,\partial_{\alpha}(\Xi^{*}x^{3})=\partial_{\alpha}(\overline{b}^{\mathrm{L}}\Xi^{3}),

(94)

making use of Eq. (
48
) and again of Eq. (
92
)
The definition of the wave activity as
\Xi^{*}(\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{z}))
, instead of
\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{z})
, is key to Eq. (
94
) which reduces the buoyancy term to an
\alpha
-derivative, to vanish on averaging.

Putting all this together, from applying
\Xi^{*}
to the contraction of Eq. (
20a
) with
\mathbf{z}
, we obtain

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}})\Xi^{*}(\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{z}))-\partial_{\alpha}\bigl(h+\bar{\bar{b}}\,\Xi^{3}\bigr)=-\Xi^{*}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{z}}\,p.

(95)

We now average to eliminate the second term on the left-hand side, giving

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}})\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{z})}^{\mathrm{L}}=-\overline{\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{z}}\,p}^{\mathrm{L}}.

(96)

We then multiply by
\overline{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}}=\Xi^{*}\mu
, and simplify using Eq. (
63
), Cartan’s formula and

\overline{\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{z}}\,p}^{\mathrm{L}}\,\overline{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}}=d(\overline{p\mathbf{z}}^{\mathrm{L}}\mathop{\lrcorner}\overline{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}})
,
which follows from writing

\Xi^{*}(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{z}}\,p)\overline{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}}=\Xi^{*}\bigl((\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{z}}\,p)\mu\bigr)=\Xi^{*}\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{z}}(p\mu)\bigr)=\mathcal{L}_{\Xi^{*}\mathbf{z}}\bigl((\Xi^{*}p)\overline{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}}\bigr)=d\bigl(\Xi^{*}(p\mathbf{z})\mathop{\lrcorner}\overline{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}}\bigr)

(97)

and averaging.
This leads to a conservation law with local form

\partial_{t}(\mathsf{A}\,\overline{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}})+d\bigl((\mathsf{A}\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}+\bm{\mathsf{B}})\mathop{\lrcorner}\overline{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}}\bigr)=0,

(98)

where we have defined the wave action density
\mathsf{A}
and non-advective flux
\bm{\mathsf{B}}
as

\mathsf{A}=\overline{\Xi^{*}(\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{z}))}=\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{z})}^{\mathrm{L}}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\bm{\mathsf{B}}=\overline{\Xi^{*}(p\mathbf{z})}=\overline{p\mathbf{z}}^{\mathrm{L}}.

(99)

The corresponding global form is deduced by integrating over a domain
\mathscr{D}
with boundary
\partial\mathscr{D}
to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathscr{D}}\mathsf{A}\,\overline{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}}+\int_{\partial\mathscr{D}}(\mathsf{A}\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}+\bm{\mathsf{B}})\mathop{\lrcorner}\overline{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}}=0,

(100)

using Stokes theorem.

Eqs. (
98
)–(
99
) give a coordinate-free version of the wave action density and flux derived by
Andrews and McIntyre (1978b)
and
Bühler (2014)
. We obtain coordinate expressions matching theirs by noting that

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}

\displaystyle=\overline{\Xi^{*}({{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}_{i}\,z^{i})}=\overline{({{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}_{i}\circ\Xi)\,(z^{i}\circ\Xi)}=\overline{({{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}_{i}\circ\Xi)\,\partial_{\alpha}\Xi^{i}}

(101a)

\displaystyle\textrm{and}\quad\mathsf{B}^{i}

\displaystyle=\overline{(p\circ\Xi)\,(\Xi^{*}\mathbf{z})^{i}}=\overline{(p\circ\Xi)\,(\partial_{j}\Xi^{i})^{-1}\,(z^{j}\circ\Xi)}=\overline{(p\circ\Xi)\,(\partial_{j}\Xi^{i})^{-1}\,\partial_{\alpha}\Xi^{j}},

(101b)

where
(\partial_{j}\Xi^{i})^{-1}
denotes the
(i,j)
entry of the inverse of the matrix
\partial_{j}\Xi^{i}
and can be expressed in terms of the co-factors of this matrix.

We check that the wave action is a wave activity by showing that
\mathsf{A}
and
\bm{\mathsf{B}}
can be calculated to leading order
solely from the leading order perturbation fields.
With the expansions

\Xi^{i}(x)=x^{i}+\varepsilon\xi^{(1)i}+\varepsilon^{2}\xi^{(2)i}+\cdots\quad\textrm{and}\quad\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}=\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}^{(0)}+\varepsilon\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}^{(1)}+\varepsilon^{2}\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}^{(2)}+\cdots,

(102)

where
\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}^{(0)}=\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}^{(0)}}
is independent of
\alpha
, we compute

\partial_{\alpha}\Xi^{i}=\varepsilon\partial_{\alpha}\xi^{(1)i}+\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{\alpha}\xi^{(2)i}+\cdots\quad\textrm{and}\quad\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}\circ\Xi=\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}^{(0)}+\varepsilon\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}^{(1)}+\varepsilon\bm{\xi}^{(1)}\cdot\nabla\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}^{(0)}+O(\varepsilon^{2}),

(103)

Introducing this into Eq. (
101a
) and noting that the average removes

\varepsilon\nu_{\mathrm{a}i}^{(0)}\partial_{\alpha}\xi^{(1)i}

and, for GLM,

\varepsilon^{2}\nu_{\mathrm{a}i}^{(0)}\partial_{\alpha}\xi^{(2)i}
,
leaves

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}

\displaystyle=\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{z})}^{\mathrm{L}}=\varepsilon^{2}\,\overline{\nu_{\mathrm{a}i}^{(1)}\,\partial_{\alpha}\xi^{(1)i}}+\varepsilon^{2}\,\partial_{j}\nu_{\mathrm{a}i}^{(0)}\,\overline{\xi^{(1)j}\,\partial_{\alpha}\xi^{(1)i}}+\cdots.

(104)

For glm, the term
\varepsilon^{2}\overline{\nu_{\mathrm{a}i}^{(0)}\partial_{\alpha}\xi^{(2)i}}
persists, but Eq. (
80
) can be used to express it in terms of the first order field
\bm{\xi}^{(1)}
. The
O(\varepsilon^{2})
leading order approximation to
\bm{\mathsf{B}}
can be derived similarly.

7
Symmetry and Killing vectors

We have developed theory so far in a geometric setting, making use of a general metric
g
and associated volume form
\mu
, although for simplicity we have used an Euclidean formulation of the Coriolis term and buoyancy force. It is often natural to single out a preferred direction, in particular in order to consider a mean flow
\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}
and ensemble of perturbations
\Xi
that are invariant under translations in this direction. The Euclidean setting of the original GLM theory gives a privileged role to the directions along the three Cartesian coordinates. This is reflected in many of its results, especially those framed in terms of energy–momentum tensors
(
Andrews and McIntyre, 1978b
,
Grimshaw, 1984
,
Salmon, 2013
)
.
In spherical geometry, the longitude
\phi
is typically singled out, leading to the consideration of axisymmetric mean flows and zonal averaging.

To develop theory along these lines, and capture the notion of some ignorable coordinate geometrically, we introduce the notion of a Killing vector field
\mathbf{k}

(
Schutz, 1980
,
Frankel, 2004
)
. This has the properties

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{k}}\,g=0\quad\textrm{and}\quad\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{k}}\,\mu=0

(105)

(the second follows from the first).
The first property can also be expressed as

\displaystyle\nabla\mathbf{k}_{\flat}+(\nabla\mathbf{k}_{\flat})^{\intercal}=0\quad\text{or}\quad\nabla_{i}k_{j}+\nabla_{j}k_{i}=0,

(106)

where
\nabla
denotes the covariant derivative and
k_{i}=(\mathbf{k}_{\flat})_{i}
,
and so
\mathbf{k}
is familiar to fluid dynamicists as a vector field with zero rate-of-strain tensor: the flow of
\mathbf{k}
does not distort fluid elements. We denote by
\psi_{\alpha}
the flow map obtained by integrating along
\mathbf{k}
for a fictitious time
\alpha
. In Cartesian geometry we could take, for example,
\mathbf{k}=\bm{\partial}_{1}
and
\psi_{\alpha}
is then the translation
x^{1}\mapsto x^{1}+\alpha
through
\alpha
along the
x^{1}
-axis. In spherical geometry, typically
\mathbf{k}=\bm{\partial}_{\phi}
and the transformation
\psi_{\alpha}
is the rotation
\phi\mapsto\phi+\alpha
.
With a Killing vector field
\mathbf{k}
, we can express the invariance of the mean flow
\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}
in this direction by

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{k}}\,\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}=0
,
and likewise for any other tensor.

Let us first make use of a Killing field to obtain a conserved momentum. Contracting Eq. (
24
) with
\mathbf{k}
yields, using Eq. (
105
) and standard manipulations,

(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}})(\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{k}))-(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{k}}\,\bm{\lambda})(\mathbf{u})=-\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{k}}\,p+b\,dx^{3}(\mathbf{k}),

(107)

where
dx^{3}(\mathbf{k})=k^{3}
, the vertical component of
\mathbf{k}
.
To gain a conservation law from this we need to remove the Coriolis and buoyancy terms
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{k}}\,\bm{\lambda}
and
bk^{3}
. The latter vanishes provided that
\mathbf{k}
lies wholly in the
(x^{1},x^{2})
directions.
If
\mathbf{k}
represents a rotation about the
x^{3}
axis then
\bm{\lambda}\propto\mathbf{k}_{\flat}
and the Coriolis term also vanishes. However if
\mathbf{k}
is a translation, that is constant
k^{1}
and
k^{2}
, then it does not, with
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{k}}\,\bm{\lambda}=\tfrac{1}{2}f(-k^{2}\,dx^{1}+k^{1}\,dx^{2})
. In this case we can use the gauge freedom discussed in
Bühler (2014, §10.4.4)
: since the Coriolis term in Eq. (
20a
) depends on
d\bm{\lambda}
rather than
\bm{\lambda}
(as shown using Cartan’s formula),
\bm{\lambda}
is defined up to the transformation
\bm{\lambda}\mapsto\bm{\lambda}+dh
for any scalar function
h
.
For a translation, say
\mathbf{k}=\bm{\partial}_{1}
, we can pick
h=-\tfrac{1}{2}fd(x^{1}x^{2})
leading to the new, equivalent Coriolis term
\bm{\lambda}=-fx^{2}\,dx^{1}

which satisfies
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{k}}\,\bm{\lambda}=0
as required.

With the Coriolis and buoyancy terms removed by whatever means,
integrating Eq. (
107
) times
\mu
over a domain
\mathscr{D}

gives

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathscr{D}}\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{k})\,\mu+\int_{\partial\mathscr{D}}\bigl(\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{k})\mathbf{u}+p\mathbf{k}\bigr)\mathop{\lrcorner}\mu=0,

(108)

which is conservation, in each realisation in the ensemble, of linear momentum if
\mathbf{k}
generates translations and angular momentum if
\mathbf{k}
generates rotations.

The evolution of momentum on Lagrangian parcels in Eq. (
107
) can also be pulled back to the mean flow by applying
\Xi^{*}
, to obtain

(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}})\Xi^{*}(\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{k}))-\Xi^{*}\!\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{k}}(\bm{\lambda})(\mathbf{u})\right)=-\Xi^{*}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{k}}\,p+\overline{b}^{\mathrm{L}}\,\Xi^{*}(dx^{3}(\mathbf{k})),

(109)

using that
\Xi^{*}b=\overline{b}^{\mathrm{L}}
.
Averaging then gives

(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}})\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{k})}^{\mathrm{L}}-\overline{\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{k}}(\bm{\lambda})(\mathbf{u})}^{\mathrm{L}}=-\overline{\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{k}}\,p}^{\mathrm{L}}+\overline{b}^{\mathrm{L}}\,\overline{dx^{3}(\mathbf{k})}^{\mathrm{L}}.

(110)

When, as above, the Coriolis and buoyancy terms are removed, this corresponds to a conservation law, namely

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathscr{D}}\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{k})}^{\mathrm{L}}\,\overline{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}}+\int_{\partial\mathscr{D}}\bigl(\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{k})}^{\mathrm{L}}\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}+\overline{p\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{L}}\bigr)\mathop{\lrcorner}\overline{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}}=0.

(111)

obtained by multiplying by
\overline{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}}
and integrating over
\mathscr{D}
.

On the other hand, we can contract the Lagrangian mean momentum equation (
42
) with
\mathbf{k}
to obtain

(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}})\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}^{\mathrm{L}}(\mathbf{k})+\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}^{\mathrm{L}}\!\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{k}}\,\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}\right)=-\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{k}}\,\overline{\pi}^{\mathrm{L}}+\overline{b}^{\mathrm{L}}d\overline{x^{3}}^{\mathrm{L}}(\mathbf{k}).

(112)

This leads to the conservation law

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathscr{D}}\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}^{\mathrm{L}}(\mathbf{k})\,\overline{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}}+\int_{\partial\mathscr{D}}\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{k})}^{\mathrm{L}}\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}\mathop{\lrcorner}\overline{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}}=0,

(113)

provided that the mean fields are invariant to translation along the Killing vector field
\mathbf{k}
(since
d\overline{x^{3}}^{\mathrm{L}}(\mathbf{k})=\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{k}}\overline{x^{3}}^{\mathrm{L}}
), as is the case for spatial averaging in the direction of
\mathbf{k}
.

Subtracting Eq. (
112
) from Eq. (
110
) leads to the equation

(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}})\mathsf{p}_{\mathbf{k}}-\overline{\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{k}}(\bm{\lambda})(\mathbf{u})}^{\mathrm{L}}-\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}^{\mathrm{L}}\!\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{k}}\,\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}\right)=-\overline{\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{k}}\,p}^{\mathrm{L}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{k}}\,\overline{\pi}^{\mathrm{L}}+\overline{b}^{\mathrm{L}}\,\bigl(\overline{dx^{3}(\mathbf{k})}^{\mathrm{L}}-d\overline{x^{3}}^{\mathrm{L}}(\mathbf{k})\bigr)

(114)

for the wave activity

\displaystyle-\mathsf{p}_{\mathbf{k}}=\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}^{\mathrm{L}}(\mathbf{k})-\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{k})}^{\mathrm{L}}.

(115)

This wave activity can be interpreted as a form of pseudomomentum, related to but distinct from the general pseudomomentum defined in Eq. (
46
) We emphasise the differences:
\mathsf{p}_{\mathbf{k}}
is a scalar defined for a specific Killing field
\mathbf{k}
, whereas
\bm{\mathsf{p}}
is a 1-form defined independently of such a field. Assuming that
\bm{\lambda}(\mathbf{k})=0
, the natural relation

\bm{\mathsf{p}}(\mathbf{k})=\mathsf{p}_{\mathbf{k}}

(116)

only holds provided that
\overline{\bm{\nu}(\mathbf{k})}^{\mathrm{L}}=\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}_{\flat}(\mathbf{k})
, which clearly depends on the definition chosen for the mean flow.
In Euclidean space, we can take
\mathbf{k}=\bm{\partial}_{i}
for
i=1,2,3
.
With the GLM definition of the mean, we then have

\overline{\bm{\nu}(\bm{\partial}_{i})}^{\mathrm{L}}=\overline{\nu_{i}}^{\mathrm{L}}=\overline{\nu_{i}\circ\Xi}=\overline{u^{i}\circ\Xi}=\bar{\bar{u}}^{i}.

(117)

As a result, the two versions of the pseudomomentum coincide:

\mathsf{p}_{i}=\mathsf{p}_{\mathbf{k}}\quad\text{for}\quad\mathbf{k}=\bm{\partial}_{i}.

(118)

This is an advantage of the GLM definition of the mean flow which is tied to the coordinate representation of the flow map and velocity field or, in the language of this section, to a choice of three independent Killing fields.

Eq. (
114
) reduces to the (Boussinesq form of the) GLM pseudomomentum equation
(
Bühler, 2014
, Eq. (10.125))
. When
\bm{\lambda}(\mathbf{k})=0
,
k^{3}=0
and the mean flow is invariant under translation along
\mathbf{k}
it yields the conservation law (the difference of Eqs. (
111
) and (
113
)),

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathscr{D}}\mathsf{p}_{\mathbf{k}}\,\overline{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}}+\int_{\partial\mathscr{D}}\bigl(\mathsf{p}_{\mathbf{k}}\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}+\overline{p\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{L}}\bigr)\mathop{\lrcorner}\overline{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}}=0.

(119)

We conclude by observing the relation between the construction leading to Eq. (
115
) and the derivation of the wave action in §
6
. Given a Killing field
\mathbf{k}
and an ensemble of perturbation maps
\Xi
, we can generate a broader ensemble of maps
\Xi_{\alpha}

by conjugation under the translation
\psi_{\alpha}
,

\Xi_{\alpha}=\psi_{-\alpha}\circ\Xi\circ\psi_{\alpha}.

(120)

We can associate with this the
\alpha
-independent vector field

\mathbf{z}=\left.(\partial_{\alpha}\Xi_{\alpha})\circ\Xi_{\alpha}^{-1}\right|_{\alpha=0}=\Xi_{*}\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}.

(121)

The action in Eq. (
99
) corresponding to this is

\mathsf{A}=\overline{\Xi^{*}(\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{z}))}=\overline{(\Xi^{*}\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}})(\mathbf{k})}-\overline{\Xi^{*}(\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{k}))}=\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}}^{\mathrm{L}}(\mathbf{k})-\overline{\bm{\nu}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{k})}^{\mathrm{L}}=-\mathsf{p}_{\mathbf{k}}.

(122)

Eq. (
114
) for
\mathsf{p}_{\mathbf{k}}
is closely related to wave action conservation, with some differences that disappear when the averaging is along the direction of
\mathbf{k}
. In variational formulations of Lagrangian averaging
(
Grimshaw, 1984
,
Holm, 2002b
,
Holm, 2002a
,
Salmon, 2013
)
, the conservation of
\mathsf{p}_{\mathbf{k}}
in this case is interpreted as arising from Noether’s theorem.

8
Summary points

1.

Lagrangian averaging is best formulated starting with a geometric formulation of fluid models in terms of the momentum 1-form
\bm{\nu}
.

2.

When decomposing flow maps as
\varphi=\Xi\circ\bar{\bar{\varphi}}
, various choices can be made for the mean map
\bar{\bar{\varphi}}
and corresponding mean velocity
\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}
.
Andrews and McIntyre
’s GLM and
Soward and Roberts
’s glm theories correspond to two distinct choices.

3.

The Lagrangian mean of any tensor
\tau
is defined as
\overline{\tau}^{\mathrm{L}}=\overline{\Xi^{*}\tau}
. The Lagrangian mean momentum
\overline{\bm{\nu}}^{\mathrm{L}}
is the natural dynamical variable for the mean equations. Its difference with the 1-form
\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}_{\flat}
associated with
\bar{\bar{\mathbf{u}}}
, defined as pseudomomentum, encodes the impact of perturbations on the mean.

4.

Geometrically intrinsic forms of wave activities are readily derived. The conservation law of wave action arises for an ensemble of perturbations parameterised by a phase; further conservation laws are generated by Killing vector fields leaving the mean fields invariant.

5.

The approach is general and easily handles different coordinate systems, for example spherical polar coordinates and axisymmetric fields, and different manifolds, for example flow on the surface of a 2-sphere.
