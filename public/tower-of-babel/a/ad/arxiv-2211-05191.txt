Boundary triples and Weyl functions for Dirac operators with singular interactions
Jussi Behrndt, Markus Holzmann, Christian Stelzer, Georg Stenzel

Abstract
. In this article we develop a systematic approach to treat Dirac operators A η , τ , λ A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda} with singular electrostatic, Lorentz scalar, and anomalous magnetic interactions of strengths η , τ , λ ∈ ℝ \eta,\tau,\lambda\in\mathbb{R} , respectively, supported on points in ℝ \mathbb{R} , curves in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} , and surfaces in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} that is based on boundary triples and their associated Weyl functions. First, we discuss the one-dimensional case which also serves as a motivation for the multidimensional setting. Afterwards, in the two- and three-dimensional situation we construct quasi, generalized, and ordinary boundary triples and their Weyl functions, and provide a detailed characterization of the associated Sobolev spaces, trace theorems, and the mapping properties of integral operators which play an important role in the analysis of A η , τ , λ A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda} . We make a substantial step towards more rough interaction supports Σ \Sigma and consider general compact Lipschitz hypersurfaces. We derive conditions for the interaction strengths such that the operators A η , τ , λ A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda} are self-adjoint, obtain a Krein-type resolvent formula, and characterize the essential and discrete spectrum. These conditions include purely Lorentz scalar and purely non-critical anomalous magnetic interactions as well as the confinement case, the latter having an important application in the mathematical description of graphene. Using a certain ordinary boundary triple, we also show the self-adjointness of A η , τ , λ A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda} for arbitrary critical combinations of the interaction strengths under the condition that Σ \Sigma is C ∞ C^{\infty} -smooth and derive its spectral properties. In particular, in the critical case, a loss of Sobolev regularity in the operator domain and a possible additional point of the essential spectrum are observed.



1.
Introduction

Dirac operators with singular perturbations supported on points in
\mathbb{R}
, curves in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and
surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
have received a lot of attention recently. This class of operators appears as an idealized model in the description of relativistic spin
1/2
particles propagating in strongly localized external potentials. The MIT bag model describing quark confinement is an example of this sort of physical system
[
3
,
30
,
38
,
39
,
69
]
. Dirac operators with singular perturbations are also important in the description of graphene and other innovative materials
[
1
,
24
,
25
,
78
]
. For a rigorous understanding of these physical problems a detailed analysis of the involved operators and their spectral properties is necessary.

1.1.
The free Dirac equation

In 1928 Paul Dirac derived in
[
47
]
the free Dirac equation to find a quantum mechanical description of spin 1/2 particles with mass
m\geq 0
propagating in
{\mathbb{R}}^{q}
with
q\in\{1,2,3\}
taking relativistic effects into account.
Choosing units such that the speed of light and Planck’s constant are both equal to one yields the relativistic energy-momentum relationship

(1.1)

E^{2}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{q}p_{j}^{2}+m^{2}.

To associate it with a linear differential equation, which is of first order in time, Dirac made the ansatz

(1.2)

\bigg(E-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{q}\alpha_{j}p_{j}-m\alpha_{0}\bigg)\bigg(E+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{q}\alpha_{j}p_{j}+m\alpha_{0}\bigg)=0.

The equations (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) are only equivalent, if the coefficients
\alpha_{j}
satisfy the anti-commutation relations

(1.3)

\alpha_{k}\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{j}\alpha_{k}=2\delta_{kj}I_{N}\quad\text{for all}\quad k,j\in\{0,1,\dots,q\},

and hence one has to choose the coefficients matrix-valued,
\alpha_{j}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N\times N}
, where
N=2
for
q\in\{1,2\}
and
N=4
for
q=3
, and

I_{N}
denotes the
N\times N
-identity matrix.
For
q\in\{1,2\}
the matrices
\alpha_{j}
can be chosen as the Pauli spin matrices
and for
q=3
as the Dirac matrices, see (
1.17
) and (
1.18
).
Applying the usual substitution rules
i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
and
-i\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}
for
E
and
p_{j}
, respectively, in one of the factors in (
1.2
), say in the first one, one obtains the free Dirac equation

i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Psi=\bigg(-i\sum\limits_{j=1}^{q}\alpha_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}+m\alpha_{0}\bigg)\Psi,

which describes the propagation of a relativistic particle with spin
1/2
such as an electron in
{\mathbb{R}}^{q}
.

Besides this original motivation another important application of the Dirac equation was found recently
in
[
1
,
78
]
where it was demonstrated that the two-dimensional Dirac equation plays an important role in the mathematical description of graphene. This fact and the importance of graphene in many modern applications led to an increased interest in the Dirac equation in the last 15 years. In particular, depending on the way how the graphene is cut out of the planar sheet Dirac operators with different boundary conditions known as quantum dot boundary conditions are of interest and were studied in
[
14
,
24
,
25
,
82
]
. Since graphene is a hexagonal lattice, it is of importance that the corresponding operators are investigated on rough domains with Lipschitz boundaries.

1.2.
The free Dirac operator

As in the case of the Schrödinger equation, one defines the free Dirac operator as the right-hand side of the free Dirac equation by

(1.4)

\begin{split}A_{0}f&=\big(-i(\alpha\cdot\nabla)+m\alpha_{0}\big)f,\qquad\text{dom}\,A_{0}=H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{q};{\mathbb{C}}^{N}),\end{split}

where
H^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})
denotes the (
L^{2}
-based) Sobolev space of
k
-times weakly differentiable functions and where we use for
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{q})\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
the notations

\alpha\cdot x:=\sum_{j=1}^{q}\alpha_{j}x_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha\cdot\nabla:=\sum_{j=1}^{q}\alpha_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}.

With the help of the Fourier transform and (
1.3
) it is not difficult to verify that
A_{0}
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{q};{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
, that
A_{0}
has purely absolutely continuous spectrum

(1.5)

\sigma(A_{0})=\sigma_{\textup{ac}}(A_{0})=(-\infty,-m]\cup[m,\infty),

and that

(1.6)

(A_{0}-z)(A_{0}+z)=\left(-\Delta+m^{2}-z^{2}\right)I_{N},

where
-\Delta
is the free Laplace operator defined on
{\rm dom\,}(-\Delta)=H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C})
; see
[
90
]
or
[
93
]
for details in dimension
q=3
. This implies

(1.7)

(A_{0}-z)^{-1}=\big(-i(\alpha\cdot\nabla)+m\alpha_{0}+zI_{N}\big)(-\Delta+m^{2}-z^{2})^{-1}I_{N}

for
z\in\mathbb{(}\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})\cup(-m,m)
.
Using the well known form of the resolvent of
-\Delta
, one finds that
(A_{0}-z)^{-1}
is an integral operator in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})
with integral kernel
G_{z,q}(x-y)
given by (
1.19
).

1.3.
Coupling of singular potentials

To model the influence of external fields the free Dirac operator
A_{0}
is coupled with potentials. In the following, we consider the physically relevant dimensions
q=2,3
; motivated by this, similar mathematical expressions are studied also for
q=1
. For a scalar potential
\Phi_{s}
and a static electromagnetic potential
(\Phi_{e},A_{m})
taking also anomalous magnetic interactions with coupling constant
\mu
into account, the corresponding Dirac operator is given by

(1.8)

A_{0}+\Phi_{e}I_{N}+\Phi_{s}\alpha_{0}-(\alpha\cdot A_{m})+i\mu(\alpha\cdot\nabla\Phi_{e})\alpha_{0}+\mu V_{q},

where the term
V_{q}
that contributes to the description of the anomalous magnetic moment depends on the space dimension
q
and is given by
V_{2}=(\nabla\times A_{m})I_{2}
for
q=2
and
V_{3}=\alpha\cdot(\nabla\times A_{m})\alpha_{0}\alpha_{1}
for
q=3
;
cf.
[
62
,
90
]
.

In the case of strongly localized potentials that have large values on a small neighbourhood of a set
\Sigma\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{q}
with measure zero and small values otherwise, it is often a useful simplification to replace the regular fields by
\delta
-potentials supported on
\Sigma
. To be more precise, assume in the following that
\Sigma
is the boundary of a bounded sufficiently smooth domain
\Omega_{+}\subset\mathbb{R}^{q}
,
q\in\{2,3\}
, with normal vector
\nu
pointing outwards of
\Omega_{+}
and set
\Omega_{-}:=\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{+}}
. Then, each point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
in a tubular neighborhood of
\Sigma
(which are all points
x
with
\textup{dist}(x,\Sigma)<b
) with a sufficiently small half-width
b>0
has the form
x=x_{\Sigma}+t\nu(x_{\Sigma})
with unique
x_{\Sigma}\in\Sigma
and
t\in(-b,b)
. Consider now static electromagnetic and Lorentz scalar potentials whose strengths depend only on the distance to
\Sigma
, that are supported in the tubular neighborhood of
\Sigma
, and where the magnetic field is pointing in the normal direction, i.e.

\Phi_{s}(x)=f_{s}(t),\quad\Phi_{e}(x)=f_{e}(t),\quad\text{and }A_{m}(x)=\nu(x_{\Sigma})f_{m}(t),\quad x=x_{\Sigma}+t\nu(x_{\Sigma}),

with smooth scalar-valued functions
f_{s},f_{e}
, and
f_{m}
supported in
(-b,b)
. For such potentials
one verifies
\alpha\cdot\nabla\Phi_{e}=(\alpha\cdot\nu)f^{\prime}_{e}
and
V_{q}=0
, and hence
the expression in (
1.8
) simplifies to

A_{0}+f_{e}I_{N}+f_{s}\alpha_{0}-(\alpha\cdot\nu)f_{m}+i\mu(\alpha\cdot\nu)\alpha_{0}f^{\prime}_{e}.

For suitable choices of
f_{e},f_{s},f_{m}
, or
\mu f_{e}^{\prime}
one can replace the term
f_{e}I_{N}
by
\eta I_{N}\delta_{\Sigma}
,
f_{s}\alpha_{0}
by
\tau\alpha_{0}\delta_{\Sigma}
,
(\alpha\cdot\nu)f_{m}
by
\omega(\alpha\cdot\nu)\delta_{\Sigma}
, or
i\mu(\alpha\cdot\nu)\alpha_{0}f^{\prime}_{e}
by
i\lambda(\alpha\cdot\nu)\alpha_{0}\big)\delta_{\Sigma}
with interaction strengths
\eta,\tau,\omega,\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
. From a mathematical point of view it makes sense to study Dirac operators with all of the above singular potentials simultaneously, so we arrive at the expression

A_{0}+\big(\eta I_{N}+\tau\alpha_{0}-\omega(\alpha\cdot\nu)+i\lambda(\alpha\cdot\nu)\alpha_{0}\big)\delta_{\Sigma};

cf.
[
4
,
5
,
8
,
26
,
37
]
. The latter replacement was justified rigorously by approximation procedures in
[
37
,
74
,
75
,
88
,
92
]
.
We note that, without loss of generality, one can assume
\omega=0
, since this term can be gauged away by an appropriate unitary transformation, see
[
37
, Theorem 2.1]
or
[
73
, Theorems 1.2 and 1.3]
. This finally leads to the
main object of interest in the present paper: For interaction strengths
\eta,\tau,\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
we will consider Dirac operators
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}

with singular perturbations formally given by

(1.9)

A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}=A_{0}+\big(\eta I_{N}+\tau\alpha_{0}+i\lambda(\alpha\cdot\nu)\alpha_{0}\big)\delta_{\Sigma};

here we call
\eta I_{N}\delta_{\Sigma}
the electrostatic,
\tau\alpha_{0}\delta_{\Sigma}
the Lorentz scalar, and
i\lambda(\alpha\cdot\nu)\alpha_{0}\delta_{\Sigma}
the anomalous magnetic
\delta
-shell interaction, respectively.

The “action” of the
\delta
-potential on functions that are sufficiently smooth in
\Omega_{\pm}
such that they admit Dirichlet traces
\gamma_{D}^{\pm}f_{\pm}
,
f_{\pm}:=f\upharpoonright\Omega_{\pm}
, on
\Sigma
in a suitable sense is defined as the anti-linear distribution

\langle\delta_{\Sigma}f,\varphi\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}\big(\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{+}+\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{-}\big)\overline{\varphi}d\sigma,\quad\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{q}).

A formal integration by parts shows that the distribution
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}f
is regular if and only if
(\alpha\cdot\nabla)f_{\pm}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{\pm};{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
and
f
satisfies the jump condition

(1.10)

i(\alpha\cdot\nu)(\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{+}-\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{-})+\frac{1}{2}\big(\eta I_{N}+\tau\alpha_{0}+i\lambda(\alpha\cdot\nu)\alpha_{0}\big)(\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{+}+\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{-})=0;

cf.
[
4
,
8
]
. In a similar way, one finds in space dimension
q=1
with
\Sigma=\{0\}
,
\Omega_{+}=(0,\infty)
,
\Omega_{-}=(-\infty,0)
, and
\nu=-1
that
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}f
is regular if and only if
f_{\pm}\in H^{1}(\Omega_{\pm};{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
and
f
fulfills

(1.11)

-i\alpha_{1}(f(0+)-f(0-))+\frac{1}{2}\big(\eta I_{2}+\tau\alpha_{0}-i\lambda\alpha_{1}\alpha_{0}\big)(f(0+)+f(0-))=0.

Hence, we will define
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
on the set of functions that satisfy (
1.10
) or (
1.11
). To do this in a mathematically rigorous way, it is necessary to study Sobolev spaces that are suitable for the investigation of Dirac operators and to provide trace theorems on them. It is one goal in this paper to do this also in the case that
\Sigma
is only a Lipschitz smooth curve in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
or surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

The non-relativistic counterpart of (
1.9
), Schrödinger operators with singular
\delta
-potentials, were studied intensively and led to the discovery of several interesting spectral effects
[
10
,
49
,
51
,
52
,
54
]
. While the replacement of regular potentials by
\delta
-potentials may simplify the spectral analysis of the Hamiltonian considerably, it leads to technical difficulties in its mathematically rigorous definition due to the appearance of jump conditions in the operator domain in a similar vein as in (
1.10
). One natural way to introduce and study Schrödinger operators with singular interactions is via the corresponding quadratic form
[
31
,
49
,
53
,
63
,
89
]
, but also extension theoretic approaches and in particular boundary triple techniques were successfully applied to investigate these models
[
9
,
21
,
22
,
72
]
. Form methods are not suitable to study Dirac operators with singular interactions due to a lack of semi-boundedness. However, it seems natural to use boundary triple methods for this purpose.

1.4.
Boundary triples and Weyl functions

Assume that
S
is a densely defined closed symmetric operator in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Then an (ordinary) boundary triple
\{\mathcal{G},\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}
consists of a Hilbert space
\mathcal{G}
and two linear mappings
\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}:{\rm dom\,}S^{\ast}\to\mathcal{G}
such that

(\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1})^{\top}:{\rm dom\,}S^{\ast}\to\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}
is surjective and the so-called “abstract Green’s identity”

(1.12)

(S^{\ast}f,g)_{\mathcal{H}}-(f,S^{\ast}g)_{\mathcal{H}}=(\Gamma_{1}f,\Gamma_{0}g)_{\mathcal{G}}-(\Gamma_{0}f,\Gamma_{1}g)_{\mathcal{G}}

is valid for all
f,g\in{\rm dom\,}S^{\ast}
. To a boundary triple one associates a self-adjoint reference operator
A_{0}:=S^{*}\upharpoonright{{\rm ker\,}\,}\Gamma_{0}
in
\mathcal{H}
and the Weyl function
z\mapsto M(z)
via

M(z)\Gamma_{0}f_{z}=\Gamma_{1}f_{z}
for
f_{z}\in{{\rm ker\,}\,}(S^{\ast}-z)
,
z\in\rho(A_{0})
.
We note that the Weyl function is a Nevanlinna function whose values are bounded operators in
\mathcal{G}
.
Extensions of
S
are now defined as restrictions of
S^{\ast}
by

A_{\vartheta}=S^{\ast}\upharpoonright{{\rm ker\,}\,}(\Gamma_{1}-\vartheta\Gamma_{0}),

where the parameter
\vartheta
is a linear operator (or relation) in
\mathcal{G}
. Thus, the functions
f\in{\rm dom\,}A_{\vartheta}
satisfy the abstract boundary condition
\Gamma_{1}f=\vartheta\Gamma_{0}f
.
In the context of ordinary boundary triples it turns out that
A_{\vartheta}
is self-adjoint in
\mathcal{H}
if and only if
\vartheta
is self-adjoint in
\mathcal{G}
and, moreover,
one has an explicit Krein-type formula for the resolvent of
A_{\vartheta}

and
the spectral properties of
A_{\vartheta}
are encoded in the parameter
\vartheta
and the Weyl function
M
. Setting formally
B=-\vartheta^{-1}
the parametrization of the extensions of
S
can also be given in the form

A_{[B]}=S^{\ast}\upharpoonright{{\rm ker\,}\,}(\Gamma_{0}+B\Gamma_{1}),

the other aspects
of the theory translate in a straightforward manner; we refer to the monograph
[
11
]
and also Section
2
for more details.

In applications to partial differential operators it is natural and
desirable to identify
\mathcal{G}
as an
L^{2}
-space on the boundary or interface
and to choose the boundary maps
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{1}
as appropriate trace maps,
e.g. Dirichlet and Neumann traces in the case of second order elliptic operators,
which also implies that the associated Weyl function is the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map.
However, it turns out that the values of the trace maps on the maximal domain
of a partial differential operator (which is typically
{\rm dom\,}S^{*}
) are often
too singular and the classical Green’s identity can not be extended onto the full maximal domain.
One way to overcome these problems is to generalize the notion of ordinary boundary
triples by defining
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{1}
and requiring (
1.12
) only on a core of
S^{*}
.
This leads to the notion of quasi boundary triples proposed in
[
19
]
, which
is tailormade for extension problems involving partial differential operators. We refer also to

[
20
,
22
,
23
]
and
[
40
,
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
,
46
]

for generalized boundary triples and other extensions of the notion
of boundary triples and their Weyl functions. While there is no one to one correspondence of self-adjoint parameters
\vartheta
and extensions
A_{\vartheta}
for quasi and generalized boundary triples, there are other criteria that are useful to show the self-adjointness and spectral properties of
A_{\vartheta}
, see Section
2
for details.

In the present situation of Dirac operators the adjoint operator
S^{*}
is given as the unperturbed Dirac operator

-i(\alpha\cdot\nabla)+m\alpha_{0}

on
\Omega_{\pm}
, defined for all
L^{2}
-functions
f=f_{+}\oplus f_{-}
that satisfy
(\alpha\cdot\nabla)f_{\pm}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{\pm};{\mathbb{R}}^{N})
. The crucial jump condition (
1.10
) for the definition of the Dirac operator
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
can be fit in the
abstract approach of quasi boundary triples or generalized boundary triples
by using the boundary mappings

(1.13)

\Gamma_{0}f=i(\alpha\cdot\nu)(\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{+}-\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{-})\quad\text{and}\quad\Gamma_{1}f=\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{+}+\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{-})

defined on the core
{\rm dom\,}S^{*}\cap H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
for
s\in[\tfrac{1}{2},1]
. Furthermore, the matrix
B=\eta I_{N}+\tau\alpha_{0}+\lambda i(\alpha\cdot\nu)\alpha_{0}
acts as the parameter
in the boundary space
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
. Hence,
the condition (
1.10
) is satisfied by a function
f\in{\rm dom\,}S^{*}\cap H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
if and only if

f\in{{\rm ker\,}\,}(\Gamma_{0}+B\Gamma_{1})
. This leads to a rigorous definition of the operator
in (
1.9
) as

(1.14)

\begin{split}A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}f&=\big(-i(\alpha\cdot\nabla)f_{+}+m\alpha_{0}f_{+}\big)\oplus\big(-i(\alpha\cdot\nabla)f_{-}+m\alpha_{0}f_{-}\big),\\
{\rm dom\,}A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}&={{\rm ker\,}\,}(\Gamma_{0}+B\Gamma_{1}).\end{split}

Depending on the Sobolev regularity of
the core
{\rm dom\,}S^{*}\cap H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
the triple
\{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}),\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}
is a quasi boundary triple for
s\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1]

and a generalized
boundary triple in the case
s=\tfrac{1}{2}
. Note that the convenient choice of the parameter
s
is related to the smoothness of
\Sigma
: while for smooth
\Sigma
the value
s=1
is most suitable, the smaller value
s=\frac{1}{2}
is particularly useful for the case of Lipschitz smooth
\Sigma
, which is considered in this paper.
For completeness we mention that
in the one-dimensional case matters simplify and the one-dimensional analogue of the boundary maps in (
1.13
), that is,

(1.15)

\Gamma_{0}f=-i\alpha_{1}(f(0+)-f(0-))\quad\text{and}\quad\Gamma_{1}f=\frac{1}{2}(f(0+)+f(0-)),

leads to an ordinary boundary triple

\{\mathbb{C}^{2},\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}
.

1.5.
Dirac operators and extension theory

Using boundary triples and closely related techniques, several advances were made in the recent years in the study of the operator
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
in (
1.14
). It turned out for sufficiently smooth and compact interaction supports
\Sigma
that the operator with interaction strengths
\eta,\tau,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(1.16)

\left(\frac{\eta^{2}-\tau^{2}-\lambda^{2}}{4}-1\right)^{2}-\lambda^{2}\neq 0,

which is referred to as
non-critical case
, has properties as one may expect them from the model. The essential spectrum coincides with
\sigma(A_{0})
in (
1.5
), there are at most finitely many discrete eigenvalues in the gap
(-m,m)
of
\sigma(A_{0})
, and functions in
{\rm dom\,}A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
have
H^{1}
-smoothness in
\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma
. However, in the
critical case
there is a lack of Sobolev regularity in
{\rm dom\,}A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
and there may be additional points or even intervals in the essential spectrum. Furthermore, it was observed that
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
decouples into the orthogonal sum of Dirac operators in
L^{2}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
with certain
boundary conditions on
\Sigma
if
\eta^{2}-\tau^{2}-\lambda^{2}=-4
; cf.
[
5
,
15
,
37
,
48
]
and Section
5.2
. This phenomenon is known as confinement and means that the
\delta
-potential is, in this case, impenetrable for the particle. Now, the above mentioned boundary conditions are exactly the quantum dot boundary conditions that appear in the analysis of graphene
and hence, properties of
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
are of interest in the study of graphene and similar materials.

Historically,
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
was first studied in the one-dimensional case in
[
60
]
and then further investigated in
[
2
,
29
,
33
,
34
,
35
,
64
,
68
,
80
]
. Using these results and a decomposition to spherical harmonics,
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
was studied in the three-dimensional setting for general interaction strengths and
\Sigma
being the sphere in
[
48
]
. Then, after 25 years with only little progress in the investigation of
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
in higher dimensions, an extension theoretic approach that is closely related to boundary triple techniques was used in
[
4
]
to study the case of non-critical purely electrostatic interactions
(
\tau=\lambda=0
)
supported on the boundary of a bounded
C^{2}
-domain in dimension three; with the same approach the authors continued their work in
[
5
,
6
]
and studied several spectral properties, Lorentz scalar interactions, and the confinement case. In
[
7
]
quasi boundary triple techniques were used for a qualitative spectral analysis of
A_{\eta,0,0}
for interactions supported on smooth compact surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
in the non-critical case; see also
[
8
,
13
,
67
,
84
,
85
]
for further spectral and scattering properties of
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
in dimension three for compact surfaces
\Sigma
and non-critical interactions with
\lambda=0
.
The case of critical electrostatic interactions was first handled in dimension three in
[
12
,
79
]
, where an ordinary boundary triple was used in
[
12
]
and a closely related idea in
[
79
]
. In particular, it was shown in
[
12
]
that
0
may appear as an additional point in the essential spectrum and that there is a loss of Sobolev regularity in the operator domain in the critical case under additional geometrical assumptions.
In dimension two, the self-adjointness and spectral properties of
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
for
\lambda=0
and general
C^{\infty}
-smooth closed curves
\Sigma
were studied first in
[
15
]
– here both the critical and the non-critical case were considered and it was shown that for critical interactions
-m\frac{\tau}{\eta}
belongs to the essential spectrum. In
[
26
,
37
]
the magnetic interactions were introduced and the self-adjointness and spectral properties were discussed also in the critical purely anomalous magnetic case (
\eta=\tau=0
). In
[
26
]
also the self-adjointness of
A_{\eta,\tau,0}
with critical combinations of electrostatic and Lorentz scalar interactions supported on

1.6.
Content of this paper

It is our main goal to develop a systematic extension theoretic approach to study the self-adjointness and the spectral properties of Dirac operators with singular interactions supported on a point in
\mathbb{R}
, compact and closed curves in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. In particular, we describe self-adjoint realizations of
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
also for Lipschitz smooth interaction supports and study
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
with critical interactions involving all three parameters
\eta,\tau,\lambda
supported on smooth curves and surfaces
\Sigma
.

As a starting point, we follow the approach in
[
80
]
and consider the one-dimensional case. It will be shown that the boundary maps in (
1.15
)
give rise to an ordinary boundary triple
\{\mathbb{C}^{2},\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}

and the corresponding
\gamma
-field and Weyl function are computed; as
a consequence we obtain the self-adjointness and spectral properties
of
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
.

The construction of boundary mappings and the investigation of the
Dirac operators
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
in the two- and
three-dimensional setting is more involved and requires a careful analysis of the
underlying function spaces, trace maps, various integral operators, and their mapping properties.
Indeed, it is one goal in this article to investigate these objects
also in the case that the interaction support is only a compact and closed Lipschitz smooth curve in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
or surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
; cf. Sections
4.1
–
4.2
.
More precisely, Sobolev spaces associated with the Dirac operator and
the mapping properties of the Dirichlet trace in this context are necessary in our analysis
[
12
,
15
,
24
,
79
]
.
In particular, it will turn out that for
s\in[0,1]
functions
f\in H^{s}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
with
(\alpha\cdot\nabla)f\in L^{2}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
in the distributional sense admit Dirichlet traces in suitable Sobolev spaces on
\Sigma
– this is essential for the rigorous interpretation of the jump condition (
1.10
) and the definition of the boundary maps in (
1.13
)
and, thus, leads to a quasi or generalized boundary triple

\{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}),\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}

as already indicated
in Section
1.4
, see Sections
4.1
and
4.2
for more details. In the further investigations,
a potential operator
\Phi_{z}
and a strongly singular boundary integral operator
\mathcal{C}_{z}
associated with the fundamental solution of the unperturbed Dirac equation play an important role, see also
[
4
,
5
,
12
,
14
,
15
,
27
,
79
]
, where various properties of these objects were studied before in different situations. The map
\mathcal{C}_{z}
is closely related to the Cauchy transform on
\Sigma
in dimension two and the Riesz transform on
\Sigma
in dimension three; it will
be identified as the Weyl function corresponding to
\{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}),\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}

and hence it will play a key role in the analysis
of
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
. More detailed properties of
\mathcal{C}_{z}
that are needed for the study of
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
when
\Sigma
is Lipschitz smooth are given in Section
5.1
and when
\Sigma
is
C^{\infty}
-smooth in Section
6
.

Using the quasi boundary triples and generalized boundary triples from Section
4.2

we study the self-adjointness and the spectrum of
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
for non-critical interaction strengths in the case that
\Sigma
is a Lipschitz smooth closed and compact curve in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
or surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
When we fix the smoothness index
s=\tfrac{1}{2}
then

\{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}),\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}
in (
1.13
) becomes a generalized
boundary triple and
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
can be rigorously defined as in (
1.14
)
acting as the unperturbed Dirac operator in
\Omega_{\pm}
, while its domain consists of those functions
f
that have
H^{1/2}
-smoothness in
\Omega_{\pm}
, such that
(\alpha\cdot\nabla)f_{\pm}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
in the distributional sense, and that satisfy (
1.10
). We remark that one can not expect a better Sobolev regularity in the domain of
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
, as, e.g.,
{\rm dom\,}A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}\subset H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
, as it is known for special geometries that this does not hold; cf.
[
70
, Theorem 1.2 (ii)]
.
In Section
5.1
we derive conditions on
\eta,\tau,\lambda
under those we can describe self-adjoint realizations of
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
, see Theorem
5.6
for details.
While we can not show the self-adjointness for all non-critical interaction strengths, the above mentioned conditions generalize known ones from
[
27
]
and include important cases as purely Lorentz scalar and non-critical purely anomalous magnetic interactions and the non-critical confinement case. For at least
C^{1}
-smooth
\Sigma
our conditions reduce to all non-critical interaction strengths in (
1.16
). Moreover, we show under the above mentioned conditions that the essential spectrum of
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
is

\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda})=(-\infty,-m]\cup[m,\infty)

and that there are at most finitely many discrete eigenvalues in
(-m,m)
.
Eventually, we study in Section
5.2
the confinement case with non-critical and critical interaction strengths in more detail. In particular, we describe self-adjoint realizations and study the spectral properties for all combinations of the coupling constants in the confinement case for arbitrary bounded Lipschitz smooth curves in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Since graphene is a hexagonal lattice, the associated differential operators have to be studied on non-smooth domains and thus, our results also contribute to the study of graphene and similar materials.

In order to handle critical interaction strengths as well, we construct in Section
4.3
in a similar way as in
[
12
,
15
,
37
]
an ordinary boundary triple under the more general assumption of a Lipschitz smooth interaction support. The description of
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
via this ordinary boundary triple is not as natural as via the above mentioned quasi or generalized boundary triple and the corresponding parameter is an unbounded operator involving also the boundary integral operator
\mathcal{C}_{z}
for
z=0
. To show the self-adjointness and study the spectral properties of
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
also for critical interaction strengths even a more detailed analysis of
\mathcal{C}_{z}
is necessary.
For this we have to assume additionally that
\Sigma
is
C^{\infty}
-smooth. In this case we show in Theorem
6.2
for all interaction strengths the self-adjointness of
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
. In particular, it turns out that in the critical case there is a loss of Sobolev regularity in the domain of
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
: while in the non-critical case it is contained in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
, which is for smooth
\Sigma
better than for Lipschitz smooth interaction supports as discussed above, one has
{\rm dom\,}A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}\not\subset H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
for any
s>0
in the critical case.
While the spectral properties of
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
are known in the non-critical case from the considerations for less smooth
\Sigma
, we study them in the critical case in Theorem
6.8
. There we prove that also for critical interaction strengths involving all three parameters
\eta,\tau,\lambda
the point
-m\frac{\tau}{\eta}
may belong to the essential spectrum of
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
. While the latter is always true in dimension two, an additional geometric assumption is required in dimension three in the present paper
(we also refer to
[
28
]
for a very recent related result on additional intervals in the essential spectrum
for the case
\lambda=0
).
This is another step forward in the analysis of
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
for general critical interaction strengths, which were only analyzed before when
\lambda=0
or when
\eta=\tau=0
and
\lambda^{2}=4
.

1.7.
Structure of the paper

In Section
2
we briefly recall the definition
of boundary triples and their Weyl functions from abstract extension theory
and provide some related results that will be needed in the further course.
As a warm up, we treat one-dimensional Dirac operators in Section
3
. In Section
4
we construct quasi and ordinary boundary triples and characterize the necessary function spaces and integral operators. Then, in Section
5.1

-interactions on Lipschitz hypersurfaces in the case of non-critical interaction strengths and discuss in Section
5.2
the confinement case. Finally, in Section
6
, we study critical interaction strengths in the case of smooth curves and surfaces as interaction supports.

2.
Ordinary, generalized, and quasi boundary triples

In this section we briefly recall basic definitions of ordinary, generalized, and quasi boundary triples, and some related techniques
in extension and spectral theory of symmetric and self-adjoint operators in Hilbert spaces.
The concepts will be presented such that they can be applied directly to
Dirac operators with singular interactions in the next sections.
We refer the reader to
[
11
,
19
,
20
,
32
,
45
,
46
,
61
]
for more details on boundary triple techniques.

Throughout this section
\mathcal{H}
denotes a complex Hilbert space with inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathcal{H}}

and
S
is a densely defined closed symmetric operator with adjoint
S^{*}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
T
be a linear operator in
\mathcal{H}
such that
\overline{T}=S^{*}
. A triple

\{\mathcal{G},\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}
consisting of
a Hilbert space
\mathcal{G}

and linear mappings
\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}:{\rm dom\,}T\rightarrow\mathcal{G}

is called a
quasi boundary triple
for
S^{*}
if the following holds:

(i)

For all
f,g\in{\rm dom\,}T
the abstract Green’s identity

(Tf,g)_{\mathcal{H}}-(f,Tg)_{\mathcal{H}}=(\Gamma_{1}f,\Gamma_{0}g)_{\mathcal{G}}-(\Gamma_{0}f,\Gamma_{1}g)_{\mathcal{G}}

is true.

(ii)

The range of
\Gamma=(\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1})
is dense in
\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}
.

(iii)

The restriction
A_{0}:=T\upharpoonright{{\rm ker\,}\,}\Gamma_{0}
is a self-adjoint operator in
\mathcal{H}
.

If (i) and (iii) hold, and the mapping
\Gamma_{0}:{\rm dom\,}T\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
is surjective, then

\{\mathcal{G},\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}
is called
generalized boundary triple
; if
(i) and (iii) hold, and the mapping

\Gamma=(\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}):{\rm dom\,}T\rightarrow\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}

is surjective, then

\{\mathcal{G},\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}
is called
ordinary boundary triple
.

We remark that the above (non-standard) definition of generalized and ordinary boundary triples is equivalent to the
usual one given in, e.g.,
[
11
,
32
,
45
,
46
,
61
]
,
see
[
19
, Corollaries 3.2 and 3.7]
.
In particular, if
\{\mathcal{G},\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}
is an ordinary boundary triple,
then
T=S^{*}
. Moreover, each ordinary boundary triple is a generalized and quasi boundary triple, and each generalized boundary triple is a quasi boundary triple; cf.
[
19
, Corollary 3.7]
.
Note that a quasi boundary triple, generalized boundary triple, or ordinary boundary triple for
S^{*}
exists if and only if
the defect numbers
{\rm dim\,}{{\rm ker\,}\,}(S^{*}\pm i)
coincide, i.e. if and only if
S
admits self-adjoint extensions in
\mathcal{H}
.
Moreover, the operator
T
in Definition
2.1
is in general
not unique.

Next, we recall the definition of the
\gamma
-field and the Weyl function associated with the quasi
boundary triple
\{\mathcal{G},\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}
.
These mappings will allow us to describe spectral properties of self-adjoint extensions of
S
.
Let
A_{0}=T\upharpoonright{{\rm ker\,}\,}\Gamma_{0}
. Then the direct sum decomposition

(2.1)

{\rm dom\,}T={\rm dom\,}A_{0}\dot{+}{{\rm ker\,}\,}(T-z)={{\rm ker\,}\,}\Gamma_{0}\dot{+}{{\rm ker\,}\,}(T-z),\qquad z\in\rho(A_{0}),

holds.
The definition of the
\gamma
-field and Weyl function for quasi boundary triples
is in accordance with the one for ordinary and generalized boundary triples in
[
45
,
46
]
.

Definition 2.2
.

Assume that
T
is a linear operator in
\mathcal{H}
satisfying
\overline{T}=S^{*}
and let

\{\mathcal{G},\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}
be a quasi boundary triple for
S^{*}
.
Then the corresponding
\gamma
-field
\gamma
and Weyl function
M
are defined by

\rho(A_{0})\ni z\mapsto\gamma(z)=\bigl(\Gamma_{0}\upharpoonright{{\rm ker\,}\,}(T-z)\bigr)^{-1}

and

\rho(A_{0})\ni z\mapsto M(z)=\Gamma_{1}\bigl(\Gamma_{0}\upharpoonright{{\rm ker\,}\,}(T-z)\bigr)^{-1},

respectively.

From (
2.1
) we get that the
\gamma
-field is well defined and that

{\rm ran\,}\gamma(z)={{\rm ker\,}\,}(T-z)
holds for all
z\in\rho(A_{0})
.
Moreover,
{\rm dom\,}\gamma(z)={\rm ran\,}\Gamma_{0}
is dense in
\mathcal{G}

by Definition
2.1
.
With the help of the abstract Green’s identity in Definition
2.1
(i)
one verifies that

(2.2)

\gamma(z)^{*}=\Gamma_{1}(A_{0}-\overline{z})^{-1},\qquad z\in\rho(A_{0});

this is a bounded and everywhere defined operator from
\mathcal{H}
to
\mathcal{G}
. Therefore,

\gamma(z)
is a (in general not everywhere defined) bounded operator; cf.

[
19
, Proposition 2.6]
or
[
20
, Proposition 6.13]
. If
\{\mathcal{G},\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}

is a generalized or ordinary boundary triple, then
\gamma(z)
is automatically bounded and everywhere defined.

Next, we state some useful properties of the Weyl function
M
corresponding to the quasi boundary triple

\{\mathcal{G},\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}
; see, e.g,
[
19
, Proposition 2.6]
for proofs of these statements.
For any
z\in\rho(A_{0})
the operator
M(z)
is densely defined in

\mathcal{G}
with
{\rm dom\,}M(z)={\rm ran\,}\Gamma_{0}
and

{\rm ran\,}M(z)\subset{\rm ran\,}\Gamma_{1}
. Next, one has
{\rm ran\,}\Gamma_{0}\subset{\rm dom\,}M(z)^{*}
and
for all
z,\mu\in\rho(A_{0})
and
\varphi\in{\rm ran\,}\Gamma_{0}
the relation

(2.3)

M(z)\varphi-M(\mu)^{*}\varphi=(z-\overline{\mu})\gamma(\mu)^{*}\gamma(z)\varphi

holds.
Therefore, we see that
M(z)\subset M(\overline{z})^{*}
for any
z\in\rho(A_{0})
and hence

M(z)
is a closable, but in general unbounded linear operator in

\mathcal{G}
. If
\{\mathcal{G},\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}

is a generalized or ordinary boundary triple, then

M(z)
is bounded and everywhere defined.

In the main part of the paper we are going to use ordinary boundary triples, generalized boundary triples, quasi boundary triples,
and their Weyl functions to define and study self-adjoint extensions of the underlying symmetry
S
.
Let again
T
be a linear operator in
\mathcal{H}
such that
\overline{T}=S^{*}
,
let
\{\mathcal{G},\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}
be a quasi boundary triple for
S^{*}
, and
let
\vartheta
be a linear operator (or relation) in
\mathcal{G}
. Then we define the extension
A_{\vartheta}
of
S
by

(2.4)

A_{\vartheta}=T\upharpoonright{{\rm ker\,}\,}(\Gamma_{1}-\vartheta\Gamma_{0}),

i.e.
f\in{\rm dom\,}T
belongs to
{\rm dom\,}A_{\vartheta}
if and only if
f
satisfies

\Gamma_{1}f=\vartheta\Gamma_{0}f
.
If
\vartheta
is a symmetric operator in
\mathcal{G}
, then Green’s identity implies

(2.5)

(A_{\vartheta}f,g)_{\mathcal{H}}-(f,A_{\vartheta}g)_{\mathcal{H}}=(\vartheta\Gamma_{0}f,\Gamma_{0}g)_{\mathcal{G}}-(\Gamma_{0}f,\vartheta\Gamma_{0}g)_{\mathcal{G}}=0

for all
f,g\in{\rm dom\,}A_{\vartheta}
and hence the extension
A_{\vartheta}
is symmetric in
\mathcal{H}
.

Of course, one is mostly interested in the self-adjointness of
A_{\vartheta}
. If
\{\mathcal{G},\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}
is an ordinary boundary triple, then
A_{\vartheta}
is self-adjoint in
\mathcal{H}
if and only if

\vartheta
is self-adjoint in
\mathcal{G}
; cf. Theorem
2.3
below.
However, if
\{\mathcal{G},\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}
is a generalized or a quasi boundary triple, then the self-adjointness of
\vartheta
does, in general, not imply the self-adjointness of
A_{\vartheta}
, or vice versa. However, the following theorem, where we also state an abstract version of the Birman-Schwinger principle in item (i) and a
Krein-type resolvent formula for canonical extensions
A_{\vartheta}
in assertion (iii), will allow us to give conditions for the self-adjointness of
A_{\vartheta}
;
for the proof we refer to
[
11
, Theorems 2.1.3 and 2.6.5]
,
[
19
, Theorem 2.8]
, and
[
20
, Theorem 6.16]
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
T
be a linear operator in
\mathcal{H}
satisfying
\overline{T}=S^{*}
, let
\{\mathcal{G},\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}

be a quasi boundary triple for

In this section, we investigate one-dimensional Dirac operators with electrostatic, Lorentz scalar, and anomalous magnetic

Let

and
\Omega_{-}:=(-\infty,0)

where

In what follows we will make use of the orthogonal decomposition
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}};{\mathbb{C}}^{2})=L^{2}((0,\infty);{\mathbb{C}}^{2})\oplus L^{2}((-\infty,0);{\mathbb{C}}^{2})

Following the usual construction of self-adjoint realizations of the expression in (

\begin{split}Sf&:=\left(-i\sigma_{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}f_{+}+m\sigma_{3}f_{+}\right)\oplus\left(-i\sigma_{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}f_{-}+m\sigma_{3}f_{-}\right),\\
{\rm dom\,}S&:=H^{1}_{0}\left((0,\infty);{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\right)\oplus H^{1}_{0}((-\infty,0);{\mathbb{C}}^{2}).\end{split}

It can be shown that the adjoint operator
S^{\ast}

In the next step, we want to introduce the expression in (

to a suitable operator domain, which is characterized by imposing the coupling conditions (

\Gamma_{0}f=-i\sigma_{1}\big(f(0+)-f(0-)\big)\quad\text{ and }\quad\Gamma_{1}f=\frac{1}{2}\big(f(0+)+f(0-)\big),

In the following proposition we investigate the maps
\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}
. In order to formulate the result, recall that
k(z)
and

,

Proposition 3.1
.

The triple
\{{\mathbb{C}}^{2},\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}
is an ordinary boundary triple for

. The value of the associated
\gamma

by

\left[\gamma(z)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\xi_{1}\\
\xi_{2}\\
\end{array}\right)\right](x)=\frac{i}{2}e^{ik(z)|x|}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\zeta(z)&{\text{\rm sgn\,}}(x)\\
{\text{\rm sgn\,}}(x)&\zeta(z)^{-1}\\
\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\xi_{1}\\
\xi_{2}\\
\end{array}\right),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

and the Weyl function is

\rho(A_{0})\ni z\mapsto M(z):=\frac{i}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\zeta(z)&0\\
0&\zeta(z)^{-1}\\
\end{array}\right).

Note that the application of
\gamma(z)
can be viewed as the matrix vector product of
G_{z,1}(x)
in (
1.19
) and the vector
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
, while the matrix representing

consists of the columns resulting from applying

to the columns of

. Similar operators will play a role in the higher dimensional considerations in the next sections.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Integration by parts and a straightforward computation show that the abstract Green’s identity in Definition
2.1
(i) is valid. To show that
(\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}):{\rm dom\,}S^{*}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{4}
is surjective, consider for
(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{4}
the function

f(x)=\frac{i}{2}\begin{pmatrix}c_{2}\\
c_{1}\\
\end{pmatrix}{\text{\rm sgn\,}}(x)\,e^{-|x|}+\begin{pmatrix}c_{3}\\
c_{4}\\
\end{pmatrix}e^{-|x|},\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Then

,
\Gamma_{0}f=(c_{1},c_{2})
and
\Gamma_{1}f=(c_{3},c_{4})
and hence,
(\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1})
is surjective. Finally, to show that Definition
2.1
(iii) holds, notice that the restriction

is the self-adjoint free Dirac operator defined in (

\{\mathbb{C}^{2},\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}
is an ordinary boundary triple for
S^{\ast}
.

It remains to show the claimed formulas for the
\gamma
-field and the Weyl function. Consider the functions

f_{1}(x)=\frac{i}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\zeta(z)\\
{\text{\rm sgn\,}}(x)\\
\end{array}\right)e^{ik(z)|x|}\quad\text{and}\quad f_{2}(x)=\frac{i}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\text{\rm sgn\,}}(x)\\
\zeta(z)^{-1}\\
\end{array}\right)e^{ik(z)|x|}.

These functions form a basis of

,

, and

\left[\gamma(z)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\xi_{1}\\
\xi_{2}\\
\end{array}\right)\right](x)=\xi_{1}f_{1}(x)+\xi_{2}f_{2}(x)=\frac{i}{2}e^{ik(z)|x|}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\zeta(z)&{\text{\rm sgn\,}}(x)\\

\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\xi_{1}\\
\xi_{2}\\
\end{array}\right),

which is the claimed expression for

. Moreover, we have

\xi_{2}\\

\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\xi_{1}\\
\xi_{2}\\
\end{array}\right),

∎

Theorem 3.2
.

For any
\eta,\tau,\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
the operator

defined in (

) is self-adjoint in

and for all

and

\begin{split}(&A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}-z)^{-1}f=(A_{0}-z)^{-1}f\\
&~~~-\left(g_{z,1},\overline{f}\right)_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}};{\mathbb{C}}^{2})}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\zeta(z)\\
{\text{\rm sgn\,}}(\cdot)\\
\end{array}\right)e^{ik(z)|\cdot|}-\left(g_{z,2},\overline{f}\right)_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}};{\mathbb{C}}^{2})}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\text{\rm sgn\,}}(\cdot)\\

\end{array}\right)e^{ik(z)|\cdot|},\end{split}

where

and

.
Moreover, the following holds:

(i)

\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda})=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(A_{0})=\left(-\infty,-m\right]\cup\left[m,\infty\right)

(ii)

Set

and assume

(a)

If

, then

.

(b)

If

, then exactly those points

(3.4)

Proof.

First, since the matrix
P_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}=\eta I_{2}+\tau\sigma_{3}-\lambda\sigma_{2}
is self-adjoint, Theorem

implies that the operator

defined by (

) is self-adjoint in

. Moreover, by Theorem

(iii) we have for

and

(A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}-z)^{-1}f=(A_{0}-z)^{-1}f-\gamma(z)(I_{2}+P_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}M(z))^{-1}P_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}\gamma(\overline{z})^{\ast}f.

After a simple calculation using the expressions for
\gamma(z)
and

in Proposition
3.1
one obtains the claimed resolvent formula. Statement (i) follows from the fact that both

and

are self-adjoint extensions of the operator

, which has the finite defect indices
(2,2)
.

It remains to show the claims about the discrete spectrum. For

we have
\sigma_{\rm ess}(A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda})=\mathbb{R}
and therefore,
\sigma_{\rm disc}(A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda})=\emptyset
. Hence, we assume

in the following. Note that (i) and Theorem

(i) imply that

\frac{d}{4}-1=\frac{m\tau+z\eta}{\sqrt{m^{2}-z^{2}}}.

In this section we use similar boundary mappings as in Section

one obtains a generalized or quasi boundary triple instead of an ordinary boundary triple; cf. Section

we introduce an ordinary boundary triple that can be used to study Dirac operators with singular interactions, but with non-obvious and unbounded parameters. Before we can define the boundary triples, some preliminaries related to function spaces and trace theorems are needed. For smooth surfaces similar boundary triples and Sobolev spaces were used in
[

,

,

,

]
and
[

,

,

]
, respectively, see also

]
for the Lipschitz case.

Let

be the space dimension and set
N:=2^{[(q+1)/2]}
, where
[\cdot]
is the Gauss bracket. Consequently, we have
N=2
for
q=2
and
N=4
for
q=3
. Let
\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{q}
be the

anti-commuting
\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
-valued Dirac matrices introduced in (
1.17
)–(

Throughout this section, let
\Omega_{+}\subset\mathbb{R}^{q}
,

, be a bounded Lipschitz domain, set
\Omega_{-}:=\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{+}}
and

. We denote by

the unit normal vector field at

that is pointing outwards of

. In the following we will often denote the restriction of a function

defined on

as a two component vector

4.1.
Sobolev spaces for Dirac operators and related trace theorems

Define for
s\in[0,1]
the space

H^{s}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N}):=\bigl\{f\in H^{s}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N}):(\alpha\cdot\nabla)f\in L^{2}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})\bigr\},

where the derivatives are understood in the distributional sense and
H^{s}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
is the standard
L^{2}
-based Sobolev space of order
s
of
\mathbb{C}^{N}
-valued functions,
and endow it with the norm

\|f\|_{H^{s}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})}^{2}:=\|f\|_{H^{s}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})}^{2}+\|(\alpha\cdot\nabla)f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})}^{2}.

One can show with standard techniques that
H^{s}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
is a Hilbert space and that
C_{0}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega_{\pm}};\mathbb{C}^{N})
is dense in
H^{s}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
; cf.
[
24
, Lemma 2.1]
,
[
12
, Lemma 3.2]
, or
[
79
, Proposition 2.12]
for similar arguments. Moreover, with the help of the Fourier transform one finds that
H^{s}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})=H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})
for any
s\in[0,1]
. We shall use the notation

H^{s}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})=H^{s}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{+};\mathbb{C}^{N})\oplus H^{s}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{-};\mathbb{C}^{N}).

Using
[
71
, Chapter 1, Theorem 14.3]
it is not difficult to see that the interpolation property

(4.1)

\big[H^{1}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N}),H^{0}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})\big]_{1-s}=H^{s}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})

holds for any
s\in[0,1]
.
In the following lemma we state a trace theorem for
H^{s}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
for
s\geq\frac{1}{2}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

For
s\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
the map
C_{0}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega_{\pm}};\mathbb{C}^{N})\ni f\mapsto f|_{\Sigma}
admits a unique continuous extension
\gamma_{D}^{\pm}:H^{s}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})\rightarrow H^{s-1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
.

Proof.

For
s\in(\frac{1}{2},1]
the claim follows from the classical trace theorem
[
76
, Theorem 3.38]
, as
H^{s}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
is continuously embedded in
H^{s}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
. It remains to verify the claim for
s=\frac{1}{2}
. Consider for
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
the Hilbert space

(4.2)

H^{s_{1},s_{2}}_{\Delta}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N}):=\bigl\{f\in H^{s_{1}}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N}):\Delta f\in H^{s_{2}}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})\bigr\}

endowed with the norm

\|f\|^{2}_{H^{s_{1},s_{2}}_{\Delta}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})}:=\|f\|_{H^{s_{1}}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})}^{2}+\|\Delta f\|_{H^{s_{2}}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})}^{2}.

It follows from
[
59
, Lemma 3.1]
that there exists a continuous trace map from
H^{1/2,-1}_{\Delta}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
to
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
.
Since (
1.3
) implies
(\alpha\cdot\nabla)^{2}=\Delta I_{N}
in the distributional sense,
H^{1/2}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
is continuously embedded in
H^{1/2,-1}_{\Delta}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
. This yields the claim also for
s=\frac{1}{2}
.
∎

Using Lemma
4.1
, that
\pm\nu
is the unit normal vector field pointing outwards of
\Omega_{\pm}
, and the fact that
C_{0}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega_{\pm}};\mathbb{C}^{N})
is dense in
H^{s}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
one can show for all
f,g\in H^{s}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
,
s\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
, the following integration by parts formula:

(4.3)

\int_{\Omega_{\pm}}i(\alpha\cdot\nabla)f\cdot\overline{g}\,\textup{d}x=\pm\int_{\Sigma}i(\alpha\cdot\nu)\gamma_{D}^{\pm}f\cdot\overline{\gamma_{D}^{\pm}g}\,\textup{d}\sigma+\int_{\Omega_{\pm}}f\cdot\overline{i(\alpha\cdot\nabla)g}\,\textup{d}x.

In the construction of boundary triples for Dirac operators with singular interactions some families of integral operators related to the fundamental solution
G_{z,q}
,
q=2,3
, given in (
1.19
) are required.
We introduce for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus((-\infty,-m]\cup[m,\infty))
the potential operator
\Phi_{z}:L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})
by

(4.4)

\Phi_{z}\varphi(x):=\int_{\Sigma}G_{z,q}(x-y)\varphi(y)\textup{d}\sigma(y),\quad\varphi\in L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}),~x\in\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma,

and the strongly singular boundary integral operator
\mathcal{C}_{z}:L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\rightarrow L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
acting as

(4.5)

\mathcal{C}_{z}\varphi(x):=\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{\Sigma\setminus B(x,\varepsilon)}G_{z,q}(x-y)\varphi(y)\textup{d}\sigma(y),\quad\varphi\in L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}),~x\in\Sigma,

where
B(x,\varepsilon)
is the ball of radius
\varepsilon
centered at
x
.
Both operators
\Phi_{z}
and
\mathcal{C}_{z}
are well defined and bounded, see
[
4
, Lemma 2.1 and Lemma 3.3]
for
q=3
and
z=0
; in the other cases this can be shown with the same arguments. Moreover, for
z\in(-m,m)
the operator
\mathcal{C}_{z}
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
. The operator
\mathcal{C}_{z}
also satisfies the formula

(4.6)

-4\big(\mathcal{C}_{z}(\alpha\cdot\nu)\big)^{2}=I_{N},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\big((-\infty,-m]\cup[m,\infty)\big).

This result can be found in
[
5
, Lemma 2.2]
for
z\in(-m,m)
in the three-dimensional setting and can be shown in the same way in the other cases. Formula (
4.6
) shows that
\mathcal{C}_{z}
is bijective and that it has the bounded inverse

(4.7)

(\mathcal{C}_{z})^{-1}=-4(\alpha\cdot\nu)\mathcal{C}_{z}(\alpha\cdot\nu),\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\big((-\infty,-m]\cup[m,\infty)\big).

In the next lemma we improve the mapping properties of
\Phi_{z}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
z\in\rho(A_{0})
the operator
\Phi_{z}
gives rise to a bounded map

\Phi_{z}:L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\rightarrow H^{1/2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}).

Proof.

Let
\gamma_{D}:H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})\rightarrow H^{1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
be the Dirichlet trace operator. First, Fubini’s theorem implies that

(4.8)

(\Phi_{z}\varphi,f)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})}=\big(\varphi,\gamma_{D}(A_{0}-\overline{z})^{-1}f\big)_{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}

holds for all
\varphi\in L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
and
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})
.
In the following we denote by
-\Delta
the free Laplace operator defined on
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q})
. Then, it is not difficult to see for
\mu\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[0,\infty)
that
\gamma_{D}(-\Delta-\overline{\mu})^{-1}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q})\rightarrow L^{2}(\Sigma)
is bounded. Hence, we can define the single layer potential

\textup{SL}(\mu):=\big(\gamma_{D}(-\Delta-\overline{\mu})^{-1}\big)^{*}:L^{2}(\Sigma)\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q}).

It is known that the single layer potential gives rise to a bounded operator
\textup{SL}(\mu):L^{2}(\Sigma)\rightarrow H^{3/2,0}_{\Delta}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma)
, where
H^{3/2,0}_{\Delta}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma)
is defined as in (
4.2
); cf.
[
58
, Equation (2.127)]
. Using (
1.6
), we find with (
4.3
) for
\varphi\in L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
and any test function
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})\subset C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})
that

\begin{split}\big((-i(\alpha\cdot\nabla)&+m\alpha_{0}+zI_{N})(\textup{SL}(z^{2}-m^{2})\varphi)_{\pm},f\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})}\\
&=\big(\textup{SL}(z^{2}-m^{2})\varphi,(A_{0}+\overline{z})f\big)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})}\\
&=\big(\varphi,\gamma_{D}(A_{0}-\overline{z})^{-1}(A_{0}+\overline{z})^{-1}(A_{0}+\overline{z})f\big)_{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}\\
&=\big(\varphi,\gamma_{D}(A_{0}-\overline{z})^{-1}f\big)_{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}=\big((\Phi_{z}\varphi)_{\pm},f\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})},\\
\end{split}

where (
4.8
) was used in the last step. Since this holds for all
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
, we conclude that

(4.9)

\Phi_{z}\varphi=(-i\alpha\cdot\nabla+m\alpha_{0}+zI_{N})\textup{SL}(z^{2}-m^{2})\varphi\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma

holds. Therefore, the mapping properties of
\textup{SL}(z^{2}-m^{2})
mentioned above and
(\alpha\cdot\nabla)^{2}=\Delta
imply the claim of this lemma.
∎

We note that for
\varphi\in L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
the trace of
\Phi_{z}\varphi
, which is well defined by Lemma
4.1
and Lemma
4.2
, is given by

(4.10)

\gamma_{D}^{\pm}(\Phi_{z}\varphi)_{\pm}=\mp\frac{i}{2}(\alpha\cdot\nu)\varphi+\mathcal{C}_{z}\varphi;

this can be shown in the same way as in
[
4
, Lemma 3.3]
or
[
15
, Proposition 3.4]
.

Finally, in order to further characterize the mapping properties of
\Phi_{z}
and
\mathcal{C}_{z}
in the next section, we need another type of Sobolev spaces. Let
\mathbb{U}:\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
be a measurable function such that
\mathbb{U}(x)
is a unitary matrix for any
x\in\Sigma
. Introduce for
t\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
the space

(4.11)

H^{t}_{\mathbb{U}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}):=\big\{\varphi\in L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}):\mathbb{U}\varphi\in H^{t}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\big\},

where
H^{t}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
denotes the standard Sobolev space on
\Sigma
of
\mathbb{C}^{N}
-valued functions. Moreover, we endow
H^{t}_{\mathbb{U}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
with the natural scalar product

(\varphi,\psi)_{H^{t}_{\mathbb{U}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}:=(\mathbb{U}\varphi,\mathbb{U}\psi)_{H^{t}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})},\quad\varphi,\psi\in H^{t}_{\mathbb{U}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}).

Note that
H^{0}_{\mathbb{U}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})=L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
.
By definition, for any
t\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
the multiplication by
\mathbb{U}
is a unitary map from
H^{t}_{\mathbb{U}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
to
H^{t}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
, and the multiplication operator associated with the measurable function

\mathbb{U}^{*}:\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
,
x\mapsto\mathbb{U}(x)^{*}
,
is a unitary map from
H^{t}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
to
H^{t}_{\mathbb{U}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
.

For
t\in[-\frac{1}{2},0]
we also make use of the anti-dual spaces

(4.12)

H^{t}_{\mathbb{U}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}):=\big(H^{-t}_{\mathbb{U}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\big)^{\prime}.

Since for any
t\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
the map
\mathbb{U}^{*}:H^{t}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\rightarrow H^{t}_{\mathbb{U}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
is unitary with
(\mathbb{U}^{*})^{-1}=\mathbb{U}
, its anti-dual map, that is denoted by
\mathbb{U}
as well, also provides a unitary map from
H^{-t}_{\mathbb{U}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
to
H^{-t}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
; its action is, by definition, constituted by

\big\langle\mathbb{U}\varphi_{1},\psi_{1}\big\rangle_{H^{-t}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\times H^{t}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}=\big\langle\varphi_{1},\mathbb{U}^{*}\psi_{1}\big\rangle_{H^{-t}_{\mathbb{U}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\times H^{t}_{\mathbb{U}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}

for
\varphi_{1}\in H^{-t}_{\mathbb{U}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
and
\psi_{1}\in H^{t}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
. In a similar way one finds that
\mathbb{U}^{*}:H^{-t}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\rightarrow H^{-t}_{\mathbb{U}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
is unitary and the action is given by

\big\langle\mathbb{U}^{*}\varphi_{2},\psi_{2}\big\rangle_{H^{-t}_{\mathbb{U}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\times H^{t}_{\mathbb{U}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}=\big\langle\varphi_{2},\mathbb{U}\psi_{2}\big\rangle_{H^{-t}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\times H^{t}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}

for
\varphi_{2}\in H^{-t}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
and
\psi_{2}\in H^{t}_{\mathbb{U}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
.
Again, by using
[
71
, Chapter 1, Theorem 14.3]
it is not difficult to verify the interpolation property

(4.13)

\big[H^{1/2}_{\mathbb{U}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}),H^{0}_{\mathbb{U}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\big]_{1-t}=H^{t/2}_{\mathbb{U}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}),\qquad t\in[0,1],

and with duality one also gets

(4.14)

\big[H^{0}_{\mathbb{U}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}),H^{-1/2}_{\mathbb{U}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\big]_{1-t}=H^{(t-1)/2}_{\mathbb{U}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}),\qquad t\in[0,1].

Mostly, the above construction is applied for
\mathbb{U}=\alpha\cdot\nu
, and then we will write

H_{\alpha}^{t}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}):=H_{\alpha\cdot\nu}^{t}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}).

Since
\mathbb{U}=\alpha\cdot\nu
is self-adjoint, we get for any
t\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]
that the maps

(4.15)

\alpha\cdot\nu:H^{t}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\rightarrow H^{t}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha\cdot\nu:H^{t}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\rightarrow H^{t}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})

are unitary.
If
\Sigma
is
C^{1,t+\varepsilon}
-smooth for some
\varepsilon>0
, then
H^{t}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})=H^{t}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
, cf.
[
14
, Lemma A.2]
.

4.2.
Quasi boundary triples and generalized boundary triples for Dirac operators with singular interactions

In this subsection we follow ideas from Section
3
and introduce a family of quasi boundary triples for Dirac operators; similar constructions
can also be found in
[
7
,
12
]
.

We introduce for
s\in[0,1]
the operators
T^{(s)}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})
by

(4.16)

\begin{split}T^{(s)}f&:=(-i(\alpha\cdot\nabla)+m\alpha_{0})f_{+}\oplus(-i(\alpha\cdot\nabla)+m\alpha_{0})f_{-},\\
{\rm dom\,}T^{(s)}&:=H^{s}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{+};\mathbb{C}^{N})\oplus H^{s}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{-};\mathbb{C}^{N})=H^{s}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}),\end{split}

and
S:=T^{(s)}\upharpoonright H^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
, which is given more explicitly by

Sf=(-i(\alpha\cdot\nabla)+m\alpha_{0})f,\qquad{\rm dom\,}S=H^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}).

The operator
S
is densely defined, closed, and symmetric. Using standard arguments and distributional derivatives one verifies that

(4.17)

S^{*}=T^{(0)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad(T^{(0)})^{*}=S;

cf.
[
12
, Proposition 3.1]
for a similar argument.
For
s\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
we introduce the mappings
\Gamma_{0}^{(s)},\Gamma_{1}^{(s)}:{\rm dom\,}T^{(s)}\rightarrow L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
by

(4.18)

\Gamma_{0}^{(s)}f:=i(\alpha\cdot\nu)(\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{+}-\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{-})\quad\text{and}\quad\Gamma_{1}^{(s)}f:=\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{+}+\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{-}).

Observe that
\Gamma_{0}^{(s)}
and
\Gamma_{1}^{(s)}
are both well defined due to Lemma
4.1
.

In the following theorem we show that the mappings
\Gamma_{0}^{(s)}
and
\Gamma_{1}^{(s)}
in (
4.18
) give rise to a quasi boundary triple for
S^{*}
and we compute the associated
\gamma
-field and Weyl function. Recall that
A_{0}
is the free Dirac operator defined in (
1.4
), that
\Phi_{z}
and
\mathcal{C}_{z}
are the mappings introduced in (
4.4
) and (
4.5
), respectively, and that
H^{s}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
is the space defined in (
4.11
) for
\mathbb{U}=\alpha\cdot\nu
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
s\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
. Then the following holds:

(i)

The triple
\big\{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}),\Gamma_{0}^{(s)},\Gamma_{1}^{(s)}\big\}
is a quasi boundary triple for
S^{*}=\overline{T^{(s)}}
with
T^{(s)}\upharpoonright{{\rm ker\,}\,}\Gamma_{0}^{(s)}=A_{0}
, and one has

(4.19)

{\rm ran\,}\Gamma_{0}^{(s)}=H^{s-1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}).

In particular,
\big\{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}),\Gamma_{0}^{(1/2)},\Gamma_{1}^{(1/2)}\big\}
is a generalized boundary triple.

(ii)

For
z\in\rho(A_{0})=\mathbb{C}\setminus((-\infty,-m]\cup[m,\infty))
the values
\gamma^{(s)}(z)
of the
\gamma
-field
are given by

\begin{split}\gamma^{(s)}(z)=\Phi_{z}\upharpoonright H^{s-1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}).\end{split}

Each
\gamma^{(s)}(z)
is a densely defined bounded operator
from the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})
and
an everywhere defined bounded operator from
H^{s-1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
to

H^{s}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
. In particular,
\gamma^{(1/2)}(z)
is a bounded and everywhere defined operator from
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})
.
Moreover,

\gamma^{(s)}(z)^{*}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})\rightarrow L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})

is compact.

(iii)

For
z\in\rho(A_{0})=\mathbb{C}\setminus((-\infty,-m]\cup[m,\infty))
the values
M^{(s)}(z)
of the Weyl function
are given by

\begin{split}M^{(s)}(z)=\mathcal{C}_{z}\upharpoonright H^{s-1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}).\end{split}

Each
M^{(s)}(z)
is a densely defined bounded operator in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
and a bounded everywhere defined operator from
H^{s-1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
to
H^{s-1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
. In particular,
M^{(1/2)}(z)
is a bounded and everywhere defined operator in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
.

Proof.

(i) Let
s\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
be fixed. First, we show that
\big\{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}),\Gamma_{0}^{(s)},\Gamma_{1}^{(s)}\big\}
is a quasi boundary triple. For this we note that
\overline{T^{(s)}}=T^{(0)}=S^{*}
holds, as the set
C_{0}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega_{+}};\mathbb{C}^{N})\oplus C_{0}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega_{-}};\mathbb{C}^{N})\subset{\rm dom\,}T^{(s)}
is dense in
H^{0}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
, while the norm in
H^{0}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
and the graph norm induced by
T^{(0)}
are equivalent; cf. the discussion at the beginning of Section
4.1
. With (
4.17
) this yields
\overline{T^{(s)}}=T^{(0)}=S^{*}
.

Let us verify the abstract Green’s identity in Definition
2.1
(i). For this consider

f=f_{+}\oplus f_{-},\,g=g_{+}\oplus g_{-}\in{\rm dom\,}T^{(s)}=H^{s}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{+};\mathbb{C}^{N})\oplus H^{s}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{-};\mathbb{C}^{N}).

Then integration by parts (
4.3
) applied in
\Omega_{\pm}
yields

\begin{split}\big((-i(\alpha\cdot\nabla)+m\alpha_{0})f_{\pm},g_{\pm}\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})}-\big(f_{\pm},(-i(\alpha\cdot\nabla)+m\alpha_{0})g_{\pm}\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})}~~&\\
=\pm\big(-i(\alpha\cdot\nu)\gamma_{D}^{\pm}f_{\pm},\gamma_{D}^{\pm}g_{\pm}\big)_{L^{2}\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}&.\end{split}

By adding these two formulas for
\Omega_{+}
and
\Omega_{-}
and using

\begin{split}\big(\Gamma_{1}^{(s)}f&,\Gamma_{0}^{(s)}g\big)_{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}-\big(\Gamma_{0}^{(s)}f,\Gamma_{1}^{(s)}g\big)_{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}\\
&=\left(\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{+}+\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{-}),i(\alpha\cdot\nu)(\gamma_{D}^{+}g_{+}-\gamma_{D}^{-}g_{-})\right)_{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}\\
&\qquad-\left(i(\alpha\cdot\nu)(\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{+}-\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{-}),\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{D}^{+}g_{+}+\gamma_{D}^{-}g_{-})\right)_{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}\\
&=\big(-i(\alpha\cdot\nu)\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{+},\gamma_{D}^{+}g_{+}\big)_{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}-\big(-i(\alpha\cdot\nu)\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{-},\gamma_{D}^{-}g_{-}\big)_{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})},\end{split}

one arrives at the abstract Green’s identity.

Next, we verify

(4.20)

A_{0}=T^{(s)}\upharpoonright{{\rm ker\,}\,}\Gamma_{0}^{(s)}.

In fact, the inclusion
A_{0}\subset T^{(s)}\upharpoonright{{\rm ker\,}\,}\Gamma_{0}^{(s)}
in (
4.20
) is clear.
On the other hand, the abstract Green’s identity shown above implies that
T^{(s)}\upharpoonright{{\rm ker\,}\,}\Gamma_{0}^{(s)}
is symmetric. As the free Dirac operator
A_{0}
is self-adjoint we obtain (
4.20
) and hence

T^{(s)}\upharpoonright{{\rm ker\,}\,}\Gamma_{0}^{(s)}
is self-adjoint.

It remains to prove that
{\rm ran\,}(\Gamma_{0}^{(s)},\Gamma_{1}^{(s)})
is dense in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\times L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
. For this, we prove

(4.21)

{\rm ran\,}(\Gamma_{0}^{(s)}\upharpoonright{{\rm ker\,}\,}\Gamma_{1}^{(s)})=H^{1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})

and

(4.22)

{\rm ran\,}(\Gamma_{1}^{(s)}\upharpoonright{{\rm ker\,}\,}\Gamma_{0}^{(s)})=H^{1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}).

To establish the inclusion “
\subset
” in (
4.21
) we note that any
f\in{{\rm ker\,}\,}\Gamma_{1}^{(s)}
satisfies
\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{+}=-\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{-}
, which yields that
f_{+}\oplus(-f_{-})\in{{\rm ker\,}\,}\Gamma_{0}^{(s)}={\rm dom\,}A_{0}=H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})
and thus,
f\in H^{1}(\Omega_{+};\mathbb{C}^{N})\oplus H^{1}(\Omega_{-};\mathbb{C}^{N})
. Therefore, the claimed inclusion follows from the definition of
\Gamma_{0}^{(s)}
. For the converse inclusion “
\supset
” let
\varphi\in H^{1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
. Choose
f_{\pm}\in H^{1}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
such that
\gamma_{D}^{\pm}f_{\pm}=\mp\frac{i}{2}(\alpha\cdot\nu)\varphi
. Then
f\in{{\rm ker\,}\,}\Gamma_{1}^{(s)}
and
\Gamma_{0}^{(s)}f=\varphi
, which shows the second inclusion in (
4.21
).
To verify (
4.22
) note that the inclusion “
\subset
” follows from
{{\rm ker\,}\,}\Gamma_{0}^{(s)}=H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})
and the definition of
\Gamma_{1}^{(s)}
. For the converse inclusion “
\supset
” let
\varphi\in H^{1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
. Choose
f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})
such that
\gamma_{D}f=\varphi
. Then
f\in{{\rm ker\,}\,}\Gamma_{0}^{(s)}
and
\Gamma_{1}^{(s)}f=\varphi
, which shows the other inclusion in (
4.22
).

Hence, we have shown that
\big\{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}),\Gamma_{0}^{(s)},\Gamma_{1}^{(s)}\big\}
is indeed a quasi boundary triple for all
s\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
. Thus, except for formula (
4.19
) assertion (i) has been shown. Equation (
4.19
) will be proved together with items (ii) and (iii).

Proof of (ii) and (iii): We verify that
\gamma^{(s)}(z)^{*}
is compact for all
s
and all
z\in\rho(A_{0})
. For this purpose, recall that formula (
2.2
) implies
\gamma^{(s)}(z)^{*}=\Gamma_{1}^{(s)}(A_{0}-\overline{z})^{-1}
. Since
(A_{0}-\overline{z})^{-1}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})\rightarrow H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})
is bounded, we see that
\gamma^{(s)}(z)^{*}
is actually independent of
s
and furthermore, that
\gamma^{(s)}(z)^{*}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})\rightarrow H^{1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
is also bounded. Since

is compactly embedded in

, the claimed compactness of

follows.

The remaining assertions will be proved in three separate steps.

Step 1.
Let

. Consider for

the function

. Therefore,

) for

. Moreover, as

in (

) is a fundamental solution for the Dirac equation the definition of

shows that

\big(T^{(1/2)}-z\big)f_{z}=0\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma.

Hence,
\gamma^{(1/2)}(z)=\Phi_{z}
. Finally, using the definition of

and (

and thus,

is bounded in

Step 2.
Let

. As

is a restriction of the triple for

\gamma^{(1)}(z)=\gamma^{(1/2)}(z)\upharpoonright{\rm ran\,}\Gamma_{0}^{(1)}=\Phi_{z}\upharpoonright H^{1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})

and

M^{(1)}(z)=M^{(1/2)}(z)\upharpoonright{\rm ran\,}\Gamma_{0}^{(1)}=\mathcal{C}_{z}\upharpoonright H^{1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}).

Using the closed graph theorem and the fact that

and

Step 3.
Let

and the trace theorem shows that

is bounded. Thus, all claims are proved.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
z\in\rho(A_{0})=\mathbb{C}\setminus((-\infty,-m]\cup[m,\infty))
. Then the following holds:

(i)

The map
\Phi_{z}

The map
\mathcal{C}_{z}

and

(4.23)

\begin{split}\big\langle\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{z}\varphi,\psi\big\rangle_{H^{-1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\times H^{1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}=\big\langle\varphi,\mathcal{C}_{\overline{z}}\psi\big\rangle_{H^{-1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\times H^{1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}\end{split}

and
\psi\in H^{1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
.

(iii)

The map

(iv)

For all

the operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{z_{1}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{z_{2}}:H^{-1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\rightarrow H^{1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
is bounded.

Proof.

(i) Since the map
\gamma^{(1)}(z)^{*}=\Gamma^{(1)}_{1}(A_{0}-\overline{z})^{-1}=\gamma_{D}(A_{0}-\overline{z})^{-1}
, cf. (
2.2
), is bounded from

to

, we can define its anti-dual

\widetilde{\Phi}_{z}:=(\gamma^{(1)}(z)^{*})^{\prime}:H^{-1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N}).

As
{\rm ran\,}\gamma^{(1)}(z)^{*}=H^{1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
is closed, by the closed range theorem the same is true for
{\rm ran\,}\widetilde{\Phi}_{z}
and this together with (

) implies that

{\rm ran\,}\widetilde{\Phi}_{z}=\big({{\rm ker\,}\,}\gamma^{(1)}(z)^{*}\big)^{\bot}=\big({\rm ran\,}(S-\overline{z})\big)^{\bot}={{\rm ker\,}\,}(T^{(0)}-z).

Since the norms in

are equivalent on

has the claimed mapping properties. Furthermore, we have for

and

\begin{split}\big(\widetilde{\Phi}_{z}\varphi,f\big)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})}&=\langle\varphi,\gamma^{(1)}(z)^{*}f\rangle_{H^{-1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\times H^{1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}\\
&=(\varphi,\Phi_{z}^{*}f)_{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}=(\Phi_{z}\varphi,f)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})},\end{split}

which shows that
\widetilde{\Phi}_{z}
is indeed an extension of
\Phi_{z}
. Eventually, as
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
is dense in
H^{-1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
, there can only be one continuous extension
\widetilde{\Phi}_{z}
of
\Phi_{z}
defined on
H^{-1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
.

(ii) Using the result from Theorem
4.3
(iii) for
s=1

\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{z}:=(\mathcal{C}_{\overline{z}})^{\prime}:H^{-1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\rightarrow H^{-1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}).

Then, by definition the relation (
4.23

Using this and (

and

\begin{split}\big\langle\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{z}\varphi,\psi\big\rangle_{H^{-1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\times H^{1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}&=\big\langle\varphi,\mathcal{C}_{\overline{z}}\psi\big\rangle_{H^{-1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\times H^{1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}\\

which shows that

(iii) By (
4.10
) we know that

\gamma_{D}^{\pm}(\Phi_{z}\varphi)_{\pm}=\mp\frac{i}{2}(\alpha\cdot\nu)\varphi+\mathcal{C}_{z}\varphi

holds for all

(iv) Using (

we see

\mathcal{C}_{z_{1}}-\mathcal{C}_{z_{2}}=(z_{1}-z_{2})\Phi_{\overline{z_{2}}}^{*}\Phi_{z_{1}}.

Taking the mapping properties of

and

∎

4.3.
An ordinary boundary triple for Dirac operators with singular interactions

In this subsection we introduce an ordinary boundary triple that is suitable for the investigation of Dirac operators with singular potentials in the so-called critical case; cf. Section
6
. For that, the operator
A_{\infty}
defined by

(4.27)

\begin{split}A_{\infty}f&=(-i(\alpha\cdot\nabla)+m\alpha_{0})f_{+}\oplus(-i(\alpha\cdot\nabla)+m\alpha_{0})f_{-},\\
{\rm dom\,}A_{\infty}&=\big\{f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}):\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{+}=-\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{-}\big\},\end{split}

will play an important role. The basic properties of
A_{\infty}
are stated in the following lemma:

Lemma 4.7
.

The operator
A_{\infty}
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})
and its spectrum is given by
\sigma(A_{\infty})=(-\infty,-m]\cup[m,\infty)
.

Proof.

Consider in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})
the operator
U
given by

Uf=f_{+}\oplus(-f_{-}),\qquad f=f_{+}\oplus f_{-}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N}).

Then it is not difficult to see that
U
is unitary and self-adjoint and
A_{\infty}=UA_{0}U
. Hence,
A_{\infty}
is self-adjoint and
\sigma(A_{\infty})=\sigma(A_{0})=(-\infty,-m]\cup[m,\infty)
.
∎

Now, we are prepared to introduce the desired ordinary boundary triple. Let
V:\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
be a measurable function such that
V(x)
is a unitary matrix for any
x\in\Sigma
and let
H_{(\alpha\cdot\nu)V^{*}}^{t}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
,
t\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]
, be the space defined in (
4.11
) and (
4.12
) for
\mathbb{U}=(\alpha\cdot\nu)V^{*}
. We remark that depending on the type of the singular interaction different choices of
V
are convenient and hence, we keep it in a general form here. In particular, when one is interested in combinations of electrostatic and Lorentz scalar interactions, then
V=I_{N}
is often used, while the choice of
V
in (
6.4
) below is convenient when also anomalous magnetic interactions are considered.

By construction of
H_{(\alpha\cdot\nu)V^{*}}^{t}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
the map
V:H_{\alpha}^{t}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\rightarrow H_{(\alpha\cdot\nu)V^{*}}^{t}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
is bijective.
Next, let
\Lambda
be a self-adjoint operator in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
which can be viewed for any
s\in[-\frac{1}{2},0]
as an isomorphism from
H^{s+1/2}_{(\alpha\cdot\nu)V^{*}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
to
H^{s}_{(\alpha\cdot\nu)V^{*}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
. Note that this choice of
\Lambda
implies that

(4.28)

\langle f,g\rangle_{H^{-1/2}_{(\alpha\cdot\nu)V^{*}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\times H^{1/2}_{(\alpha\cdot\nu)V^{*}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}=(\Lambda^{-1}f,\Lambda g)_{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}

holds for all
f\in H^{-1/2}_{(\alpha\cdot\nu)V^{*}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
and
g\in H^{1/2}_{(\alpha\cdot\nu)V^{*}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
.

In the statement of the next theorem, the Dirichlet trace operators
\gamma_{D}^{\pm}
are understood in the sense of Corollary
4.6
. Recall that
T^{(0)}
is defined in (
4.16
) for
s=0
and that
\widetilde{\Phi}_{z}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{z}
are the extensions of
\Phi_{z}
and
\mathcal{C}_{z}
from Proposition
4.4
, respectively. For simplicity, we assume that
m>0
in the theorem below; the case
m=0
is commented in Remark
4.9
.

Theorem 4.8
.

Let

. Define the maps
\Upsilon_{0},\Upsilon_{1}:H^{0}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\rightarrow L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})

(4.29)

\begin{split}\Upsilon_{0}f&:=\frac{1}{2}\Lambda^{-1}V(\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{+}+\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{-}),\\
\Upsilon_{1}f&:=-\Lambda\big(iV(\alpha\cdot\nu)(\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{+}-\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{-})+2V(\alpha\cdot\nu)\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\alpha\cdot\nu)(\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{+}+\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{-})\big).\end{split}

Then

is an ordinary boundary triple for
T^{(0)}
with
T^{(0)}\upharpoonright{{\rm ker\,}\,}\Upsilon_{0}=A_{\infty}
. Moreover, the
\gamma
-field
\beta
and the Weyl function
\mathcal{M}
associated with this triple defined on

\rho(A_{\infty})\ni z\mapsto\beta(z)=-4\widetilde{\Phi}_{z}(\alpha\cdot\nu)\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{z}(\alpha\cdot\nu)V^{*}\Lambda

and

\rho(A_{\infty})\ni z\mapsto\mathcal{M}(z)=4\Lambda V(\alpha\cdot\nu)(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{z}-\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{0})(\alpha\cdot\nu)V^{*}\Lambda.

We remark that it is not obvious that all products in the definition of

are well defined. This will become more clear in the alternative representation of
\Upsilon_{1}
in (

(iii) and that

(4.30)

\Upsilon_{0}f=\Lambda^{-1}V\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{0}\xi.

Similarly, employing Proposition
4.4
and
-4((\alpha\cdot\nu)\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{0})^{2}=I_{N}

(4.31)

\begin{split}\Upsilon_{1}f&=-\Lambda\big(iV(\alpha\cdot\nu)(\gamma_{D}^{+}(f_{\infty}+\widetilde{\Phi}_{0}\xi)_{+}-\gamma_{D}^{-}(f_{\infty}+\widetilde{\Phi}_{0}\xi)_{-})\\
&\qquad\qquad+2V(\alpha\cdot\nu)\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\alpha\cdot\nu)(\gamma_{D}^{+}(f_{\infty}+\widetilde{\Phi}_{0}\xi)_{+}+\gamma_{D}^{-}(f_{\infty}+\widetilde{\Phi}_{0}\xi)_{-})\big)\\
&=-\Lambda\big(iV(\alpha\cdot\nu)(\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{\infty,+}-\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{\infty,-}-i(\alpha\cdot\nu)\xi)+4V((\alpha\cdot\nu)\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{0})^{2}\xi)\big)\\
&=-\Lambda iV(\alpha\cdot\nu)(\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{\infty,+}-\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{\infty,-}).\end{split}

Now we are prepared to prove the abstract Green’s identity. Fix
f=f_{\infty}+\widetilde{\Phi}_{0}\xi,g=g_{\infty}+\widetilde{\Phi}_{0}\zeta\in{\rm dom\,}T^{(0)}
with
f_{\infty},g_{\infty}\in{\rm dom\,}A_{\infty}
and
\xi,\zeta\in H^{-1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
. Using that
\widetilde{\Phi}_{0}\xi,\widetilde{\Phi}_{0}\zeta\in{{\rm ker\,}\,}T^{(0)}
, see Proposition
4.4
(i), and that
A_{\infty}
is self-adjoint we get

\begin{split}(T^{(0)}f&,g)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})}-(f,T^{(0)}g)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})}\\
&=(A_{\infty}f_{\infty},\widetilde{\Phi}_{0}\zeta)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})}-(\widetilde{\Phi}_{0}\xi,A_{\infty}g_{\infty})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})}\\
&=\left\langle-i(\alpha\cdot\nu)\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{\infty,+},-\frac{i}{2}(\alpha\cdot\nu)\zeta+\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{0}\zeta\right\rangle_{H^{1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\times H^{-1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}\\
&\qquad\qquad+\left\langle i(\alpha\cdot\nu)\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{\infty,-},\frac{i}{2}(\alpha\cdot\nu)\zeta+\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{0}\zeta\right\rangle_{H^{1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\times H^{-1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}\\
&\qquad\qquad-\left\langle-\frac{i}{2}(\alpha\cdot\nu)\xi+\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{0}\xi,-i(\alpha\cdot\nu)\gamma_{D}^{+}g_{\infty,+}\right\rangle_{H^{-1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\times H^{1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}\\
&\qquad\qquad-\left\langle\frac{i}{2}(\alpha\cdot\nu)\xi+\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{0}\xi,i(\alpha\cdot\nu)\gamma_{D}^{-}g_{\infty,-}\right\rangle_{H^{-1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\times H^{1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})},\end{split}

where (
4.26
) in
\Omega_{+}
and
\Omega_{-}
and Proposition
4.4
(iii) were applied in the last step.
Since
f_{\infty},g_{\infty}\in{\rm dom\,}A_{\infty}
satisfy
\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{\infty,+}=-\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{\infty,-}
and
\gamma_{D}^{+}g_{\infty,+}=-\gamma_{D}^{-}g_{\infty,-}
, we find now with (
4.30
), (
4.31
), and (
4.28
) that

(4.32)

\begin{split}(T^{(0)}f&,g)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})}-(f,T^{(0)}g)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})}\\
&=\big\langle-i(\alpha\cdot\nu)(\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{\infty,+}-\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{\infty,-}),\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{0}\zeta\big\rangle_{H^{1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\times H^{-1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}\\
&\qquad\qquad-\big\langle\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{0}\xi,-i(\alpha\cdot\nu)(\gamma_{D}^{+}g_{\infty,+}-\gamma_{D}^{-}g_{\infty,-})\big\rangle_{H^{-1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\times H^{1/2}_{\alpha}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}\\
&=\big\langle-iV(\alpha\cdot\nu)(\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{\infty,+}-\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{\infty,-}),V\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{0}\zeta\big\rangle_{H^{1/2}_{(\alpha\cdot\nu)V^{*}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\times H^{-1/2}_{(\alpha\cdot\nu)V^{*}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}\\
&\quad-\big\langle V\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{0}\xi,-iV(\alpha\cdot\nu)(\gamma_{D}^{+}g_{\infty,+}-\gamma_{D}^{-}g_{\infty,-})\big\rangle_{H^{-1/2}_{(\alpha\cdot\nu)V^{*}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\times H^{1/2}_{(\alpha\cdot\nu)V^{*}}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}\\
&=(\Upsilon_{1}f,\Upsilon_{0}g)_{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})}-(\Upsilon_{0}f,\Upsilon_{1}g)_{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})},\end{split}

which is the abstract Green’s identity.

Next, it follows from Green’s identity (
4.32
) that
T^{(0)}\upharpoonright{{\rm ker\,}\,}\Upsilon_{0}
is symmetric. Moreover, one directly sees that
A_{\infty}\subset T^{(0)}\upharpoonright{{\rm ker\,}\,}\Upsilon_{0}
. Since

is self-adjoint by Lemma

.

Next, we show that

is surjective. Denote by
E_{\pm}:H^{1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\rightarrow H^{1}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
the bounded extension operator defined by
\gamma_{D}^{\pm}(E_{\pm}\varphi)=\varphi
. Consider for arbitrary

the function

\begin{split}f&=\frac{1}{2}\big((E_{+}i(\alpha\cdot\nu)V^{*}\Lambda^{-1}\zeta)\oplus(-E_{-}i(\alpha\cdot\nu)V^{*}\Lambda^{-1}\zeta)\big)-4\widetilde{\Phi}_{0}(\alpha\cdot\nu)\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\alpha\cdot\nu)V^{*}\Lambda\xi\\
&\in{\rm dom\,}A_{\infty}+{{\rm ker\,}\,}T^{(0)}={\rm dom\,}T^{(0)}.\end{split}

Then, with (
4.30

4.31

and

. Hence,

.

It remains to show the claimed formulas for the
\gamma
-field and the Weyl function. First, define for

f_{z}:=-4\widetilde{\Phi}_{z}(\alpha\cdot\nu)\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{z}(\alpha\cdot\nu)V^{*}\Lambda\varphi.

Then, by Proposition
4.4
(i) one has that

. Moreover, Proposition
4.4
(iii) and (

which shows the claimed formula for the
\gamma
-field
\beta(z)
. Furthermore, with the help of Proposition

(iii) and (
4.24

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}(z)\varphi&=\Upsilon_{1}\beta(z)\varphi\\
&=-\Lambda\big(iV(\alpha\cdot\nu)(\gamma_{D}^{+}(\beta(z)\varphi)_{+}-\gamma_{D}^{-}(\beta(z)\varphi)_{-})\\
&\qquad\qquad+2V(\alpha\cdot\nu)\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\alpha\cdot\nu)(\gamma_{D}^{+}(\beta(z)\varphi)_{+}+\gamma_{D}^{-}(\beta(z)\varphi)_{-})\big)\\
&=-\Lambda\big(-4iV(\alpha\cdot\nu)(-i(\alpha\cdot\nu)(\alpha\cdot\nu)\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{z}(\alpha\cdot\nu)V^{*}\Lambda\varphi)\\
&\qquad\qquad-16V(\alpha\cdot\nu)\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\alpha\cdot\nu)(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{z}(\alpha\cdot\nu))^{2}V^{*}\Lambda\varphi\big)\\

Thus, all claims are shown.
∎

5.1.
Spectral analysis of Dirac operators with singular interactions on boundaries of compact Lipschitz domains

This subsection is devoted to the analysis of the self-adjointness and spectral properties of the symmetric operator

.
For this purpose we investigate

; cf. Theorems
2.4
and
4.3
. Thus, we start by proving preliminary results on

, the main results on

are then proved in Theorem
5.6
.

Recall that
A_{0}
is the free Dirac operator defined in (
1.4
) and that
\mathcal{C}_{z}
,
z\in\rho(A_{0})
, is given by (
4.5
). We introduce the operator
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}:L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})\to L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
acting as

(5.3)

\begin{split}\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}\varphi(x)&:=\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{\Sigma\setminus B(x,\varepsilon)}\frac{i\left(\alpha\cdot(x-y)\right)}{2^{q-1}\pi|x-y|^{q}}f(y)\,d\sigma(y),\quad\varphi\in L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}),~x\in\Sigma.\end{split}

Then, the following holds:

Lemma 5.1
.

The operator
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}
defined by (
5.3
) is well defined and bounded, and for all
z\in\rho(A_{0})
the following holds:

(i)

\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}-\mathcal{C}_{z}
is compact in
L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
.

(ii)

\alpha_{0}\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}+\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}\alpha_{0}=0
. In particular
\alpha_{0}\mathcal{C}_{z}+\mathcal{C}_{z}\alpha_{0}
is compact in
L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
.

(iii)

\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}
is self-adjoint.

(iv)

\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|)\subset\left[\frac{1}{4\|\mathcal{C}_{z}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})\to L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})}},\|\mathcal{C}_{z}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})\to L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})}\right]
.

Proof.

First, we prove that
R_{z}:=\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}-\mathcal{C}_{z}
is a compact operator in
L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
. We prove this assertion for
q=3
, the proof for
q=2
follows the same lines using the representation of
G_{z,2}
in
[
15
, Lemma 3.2]
.

By (
1.19
) and (
5.3
) the integral kernel of
R_{z}
is given for
x,y\in\Sigma
,
x\neq y
, by

\begin{split}r_{z}(x-y)=&\frac{i\left(\alpha\cdot(x-y)\right)}{4\pi|x-y|^{3}}\left(1-e^{ik(z)|x-y|}\right)\\
&-\left(zI_{4}+m\alpha_{0}+k(z)\frac{\left(\alpha\cdot(x-y)\right)}{|x-y|}\right)\frac{1}{4\pi|x-y|}e^{ik(z)|x-y|}.\end{split}

It is not difficult to see that
|r_{z}(x-y)|=\mathcal{O}(|x-y|^{-1})
. Hence, one can prove with the same arguments as in
[
56
, (3.11) Proposition]
that
R_{z}
is a compact operator in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
. Since
\mathcal{C}_{z}
is bounded in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
, see (
4.5
) and the text below, we obtain that
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}
is well defined and bounded and that assertion (i) is true.

Assertion (ii) is a direct consequence of the anti-commutation relations of the Dirac matrices in (
1.3
) and item (i). Next, let us show the self-adjointness of
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}
. Since the relation
(r_{z}(x-y))^{*}=r_{\overline{z}}(y-x)
holds for
x\neq y\in\Sigma
, one has
(R_{z})^{*}=R_{\overline{z}}
. Hence, by (
2.3
) and Theorem
4.3
(iii) we get

(\mathcal{D}_{\alpha})^{*}=\left(\mathcal{C}_{z}+R_{{z}}\right)^{*}=\mathcal{C}_{\overline{z}}+R_{{\overline{z}}}=\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}.

Finally, we prove assertion (iv). Since
R_{z}
and
R_{\overline{z}}
are compact in
L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
, the inclusion

\begin{split}\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}^{2})&=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(\mathcal{C}_{z}\mathcal{C}_{\overline{z}})=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(\mathcal{C}_{z}\mathcal{C}_{z}^{*})\\
&\subset\sigma(\mathcal{C}_{z}\mathcal{C}_{z}^{*})\subset\left[\|\mathcal{C}_{z}^{-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})\rightarrow L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})}^{-2},\|\mathcal{C}_{z}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})\rightarrow L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})}^{2}\right]\end{split}

holds. By (
4.7
) we have

\begin{split}\|\mathcal{C}_{z}^{-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})\rightarrow L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})}&=4\|(\alpha\cdot\nu)\mathcal{C}_{z}(\alpha\cdot\nu)\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})\rightarrow L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})}\\
&=4\|\mathcal{C}_{z}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})\rightarrow L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})}.\end{split}

Taking the definition of the modulus of a self-adjoint operator into account, one concludes the claim of item (iv) from the last two displayed formulas.
∎

In the next lemma we show another useful connection of the operators
\mathcal{C}_{z}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}
. In the following we denote by
\chi_{\mathcal{S}}
the characteristic function of a measurable set
{\mathcal{S}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\omega_{{\rm max\,}}:={\rm max\,}\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|)
,
\omega_{{\rm min\,}}:={\rm min\,}\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|)
, and define the function
f_{\alpha}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
by

\begin{split}f_{\alpha}(\omega)&:=\omega_{\rm min\,}\chi_{[0,\omega_{\rm min\,})}(\omega)+\omega\chi_{[\omega_{\rm min\,},\omega_{\rm max\,}]}(\omega)+\omega_{\rm max\,}\chi_{(\omega_{\rm max\,},\infty)}(\omega).\end{split}

Then, for any
z\in\rho(A_{0})
there exists a self-adjoint unitary operator
U
which is independent of
z
and commutes with
f_{\alpha}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|)
and a compact operator
\mathcal{K}_{z}
such that
\mathcal{C}_{z}=Uf_{\alpha}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|)+\mathcal{K}_{z}
.

Remark 5.3
.

The definition of
f_{\alpha}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|)
and assertion (iv) of Lemma
5.1
imply
\omega_{\rm min\,}>0
and for all
g\in L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
that

\omega_{\rm min\,}\|g\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})}^{2}\leq\left(f_{\alpha}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|)g,g\right)_{L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})}\leq\omega_{\rm max\,}\|g\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})}^{2}

holds. In particular,
\sigma(f_{\alpha}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|))\subset[\omega_{\rm min\,},\omega_{\rm max\,}]
.

Proof of Lemma
5.2
.

Define the functions
f_{\alpha,1}(\omega):=(\omega-\omega_{\rm min\,})\chi_{[0,\omega_{\rm min\,})}(\omega)
and
f_{\alpha,2}(\omega):=(\omega-\omega_{\rm max\,})\chi_{(\omega_{\rm max\,},\infty)}(\omega)
.
Then, for any
\omega\geq 0
the equality

\omega=f_{\alpha}(\omega)+f_{\alpha,1}(\omega)+f_{\alpha,2}(\omega)

holds, which implies

|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|=f_{\alpha}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|)+f_{\alpha,1}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|)+f_{\alpha,2}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|).

Next, we show that
f_{\alpha,1}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|)
is compact. We denote the elements of the set
\sigma_{\mathrm{disc}}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|)
below
\omega_{{\rm min\,}}
by
0\leq\omega_{1}<\omega_{2}<\dots
. This sequence is either finite or it converges to
\omega_{{\rm min\,}}
; if it is finite, we extend it by
\omega_{{\rm min\,}}
to an infinite sequence. Define for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
the functions

f_{\alpha,1}^{(n)}(\omega):=(\omega-\omega_{{\rm min\,}})\chi_{[0,\omega_{n})}(\omega).

Then, the operators
f_{\alpha,1}^{(n)}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|)
are finite rank operators. Moreover, one has

\|f_{\alpha,1}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|)-f_{\alpha,1}^{(n)}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|)\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})\to L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})}\leq\omega_{{\rm min\,}}-\omega_{n}.

Hence,
f_{\alpha,1}^{(n)}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|)
converges to
f_{\alpha,1}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|)
with respect to the operator norm and therefore
f_{\alpha,1}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|)
is compact. In the same way one gets that
f_{\alpha,2}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|)
is compact. Furthermore, if we set
U:={\text{\rm sgn\,}}(\mathcal{D}_{\alpha})
, with the convention
{\text{\rm sgn\,}}(0)=1
, we obtain

\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}=U|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|=Uf_{\alpha}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|)+U(f_{\alpha,1}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|)+f_{\alpha,2}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|)).

Taking the considerations from above into account, we see with Lemma
5.1
(i) that
\mathcal{K}_{z}:=U(f_{\alpha,1}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|)+f_{\alpha,2}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|))-\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}+\mathcal{C}_{z}
is compact, which implies then the claimed result.
∎

The next lemma is devoted to the injectivity of the maps
I_{N}+P_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}\mathcal{C}_{z}
and
I_{N}+\mathcal{C}_{z}P_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
, where
P_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
is the matrix-valued function defined in (

):

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\eta,\tau,\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}
. Then,
I_{N}+P_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}\mathcal{C}_{z}
as well as
I_{N}+\mathcal{C}_{z}P_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
is injective in
L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
.

Proof.

Throughout the proof, let
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
. First, we show that
I_{N}+P_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}\mathcal{C}_{z}
is injective. Recall that
\mathcal{C}_{z}=M^{(1/2)}(z)
, where
M^{(1/2)}
is the Weyl function associated with the generalized boundary triple
\{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}),\Gamma_{0}^{(1/2)},\Gamma_{1}^{(1/2)}\}
; cf. Theorem
4.3
. Since the operator
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
in (
5.2
) is symmetric, we have
z\notin\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}(A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda})
. By Theorem
2.4
(i) this implies

in the form

\begin{split}I_{N}+\mathcal{C}_{z}P_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}&=\mathcal{C}_{z}\left(-4(\alpha\cdot\nu)\mathcal{C}_{z}(\alpha\cdot\nu)+P_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}\right)\\
&=-4\mathcal{C}_{z}(\alpha\cdot\nu)(\mathcal{C}_{z}-P_{\frac{\eta}{4},-\frac{\tau}{4},-\frac{\lambda}{4}})(\alpha\cdot\nu).\end{split}

In the following proposition we state conditions under which

,
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
, is surjective as well. Recall that
\omega_{{\rm min\,}}:={\rm min\,}\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|)

\omega_{{\rm max\,}}:={\rm max\,}\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|)
.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
\eta,\tau,\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
and let

such that one of the following conditions holds:

(i)

(ii)

\lambda\neq 0

(iii)

(iv)

\eta=\tau=0
and
\lambda^{2}\neq 4
.

(v)

|\eta|+|\tau|+|\lambda|<\frac{1}{\omega_{{\rm max\,}}}
.

(vi)

|\eta|+|\tau|+|\lambda|\neq 0
and
\frac{|d|}{|\eta|+|\tau|+|\lambda|}>4\omega_{{\rm max\,}}

Then, for any

is bijective in
L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
.

We remark that in items (i) and (ii) in the previous proposition the interval
[\omega_{{\rm min\,}},\omega_{{\rm max\,}}]
can be replaced by the (in general smaller) set
\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(|\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}|)
, cf. (
5.7
), (
5.9
), and Lemma

.

Proof of Proposition
5.5
.

By Lemma
5.4
the operator
I_{N}+P_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}\mathcal{C}_{z}
is injective. To show its surjectivity we study in all cases a product of the form

(5.4)

with a suitable injective and bounded operator
L
in

is surjective as well, which yields its bijectivity. Therefore, also

is surjective, which implies the claim. Hence, it suffices to show in all cases that for a suitable injective and bounded operator
L

is a Fredholm operator with index zero.

(i) Set
L=(I_{N}-\mathcal{C}_{z}P_{\eta,-\tau,-\lambda})

5.4

\begin{split}F&=I_{N}-d\mathcal{C}_{z}^{2}+i\lambda(\mathcal{C}_{z}(\alpha\cdot\nu)\alpha_{0}+(\alpha\cdot\nu)\alpha_{0}\mathcal{C}_{z})+\tau(\alpha_{0}\mathcal{C}_{z}+\mathcal{C}_{z}\alpha_{0})\\

5.1

[

, Lemma 3.2 and Remark 2.4]
. Moreover, as

\omega_{{\rm max\,}}\leq\|\mathcal{C}_{z}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{4})\to L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{4})}

holds for any
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus((-\infty,-m]\cup[m,\infty))
, cf. Lemma
5.1
(iv), we get
\omega_{{\rm max\,}}\leq M_{+}
. Hence, assumption (i) implies the assumption in Proposition
5.5
(v), while (ii) yields the assumption in Proposition
5.5
(vi). In particular, Theorem
5.6
can be applied.

To show the claim about the discrete spectrum, which is, by Theorem
5.6
, contained in
(-m,m)
, we fix
z\in(-m,m)
in the following.
Let us start with (i). The definition of
P_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
in (
5.1
) and the triangle inequality imply

\|P_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}\mathcal{C}_{z}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{4})\to L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{4})}\leq\big(|\eta|+|\tau|+|\lambda|\big)\|\mathcal{C}_{z}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{4})\to L^{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{C}}^{4})}<1.

Hence,
-1\notin\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}(P_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}\mathcal{C}_{z})
, which yields with Theorem
5.6
(iii) that
z\notin\sigma_{\mathrm{disc}}(A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda})
.

Next, we turn to (ii). Due to our assumptions, we can write

I_{4}+P_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}\mathcal{C}_{z}=P_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}(\alpha\cdot\nu)\big(I_{4}+P_{-\frac{4\eta}{d},-\frac{4\tau}{d},-\frac{4\lambda}{d}}\mathcal{C}_{z}\big)(\alpha\cdot\nu)\mathcal{C}_{z};

cf. (
5.11
).
Now, the assumptions in (ii) and the result from (i) show that
I_{4}+P_{-\frac{4\eta}{d},-\frac{4\tau}{d},-\frac{4\lambda}{d}}\mathcal{C}_{z}
is injective. Moreover, the other terms in the last displayed formula are injective. Hence,
0\notin\sigma_{\textup{p}}(I_{4}+P_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}\mathcal{C}_{z})
, which implies with Theorem
5.6
(iii) that
z\notin\sigma_{\textup{disc}}(A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda})
.
∎

5.2.
Confinement

In this section, we show that for some special combinations of interaction strengths the operator
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
introduced in (
5.2
) decomposes into two Dirac operators acting in
L^{2}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
with boundary conditions. From a physical point of view, this means that a particle that is located in one of the domains
\Omega_{\pm}
will remain in
\Omega_{\pm}
for all times, i.e.
\Sigma
is impenetrable for the particle. Moreover, in all situations in the confinement case we describe self-adjoint realizations of
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
, which is a novelty when all three parameters
\eta,\tau,\lambda
are present, and we can do that in the situation that
\Sigma
is only Lipschitz smooth.
The phenomenon of confinement was already observed in the case that
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a sphere in
[
48
]
and then for
C^{2}
-smooth surfaces
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
in various situations in
[
5
,
8
,
14
,
26
,
27
]
and for
C^{\infty}
-smooth
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
in
[
15
,
37
]
.
Recall that
P_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
is defined by (
5.1
).
The starting point is the following observation:

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
\eta,\tau,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, set
d=\eta^{2}-\tau^{2}-\lambda^{2}
, and let
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
be defined as in (
5.2
). Then, the following holds:

(i)

If
d\neq-4
, then there exists a matrix-valued function
Q_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}:\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
depending on the parameters
\eta,\tau,\lambda
such that
Q_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
is pointwise invertible and
f\in{\rm dom\,}A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
if and only if
f\in H^{1/2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
and

(5.16)

\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{+}=Q_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{-}.

(ii)

If
d=-4
, then
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}=A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}^{+}\oplus A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}^{-}
, where
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}^{\pm}
are the operators acting in
L^{2}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
given by

(5.17)

\begin{split}A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}^{\pm}f&=\big(-i(\alpha\cdot\nabla)+m\alpha_{0}\big)f,\\
{\rm dom\,}A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}^{\pm}&=\big\{f\in H_{\alpha}^{1/2}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N}):\big(2I_{N}\mp i(\alpha\cdot\nu)P_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}\big)\gamma_{D}^{\pm}f=0\big\}.\end{split}

Proof.

Define the function
R:\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
by

R:=\frac{i}{2}(\alpha\cdot\nu)P_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}=\frac{i}{2}(\alpha\cdot\nu)\big(\eta I_{N}+\tau\alpha_{0}+\lambda i(\alpha\cdot\nu)\alpha_{0}\big).

Then, the transmission condition for
f\in{\rm dom\,}A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
is equivalent to

(5.18)

(I_{N}-R)\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{+}=(I_{N}+R)\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{-}.

With the anti-commutation relations (
1.3
) it is not difficult to see that
R^{2}=-\frac{d}{4}I_{N}
. Hence, if
d\neq-4
, then
I_{N}\pm R
is invertible and (
5.18
) is equivalent to

\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{+}=(I_{N}-R)^{-1}(I_{N}+R)\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{-}=\frac{4}{d+4}(I_{N}+R)^{2}\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{-},

which is (
5.16
) with
Q_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}=\frac{4}{d+4}(I_{N}+R)^{2}
and shows item (i).

If
d=-4
, then
(I_{N}\mp R)(I_{N}\pm R)=0
. Hence, if
f\in{\rm dom\,}A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
, then

(I_{N}\mp R)^{2}\gamma_{D}^{\pm}f_{\pm}=2\left(I_{N}\mp R\right)\gamma_{D}^{\pm}f_{\pm}=0.

On the other hand, if
f_{\pm}\in{\rm dom\,}A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}^{\pm}
, then clearly (
5.18
) holds, as the left and the right hand side in (
5.18
) are zero, and thus,
f_{+}\oplus f_{-}\in{\rm dom\,}A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
. This finishes the proof of item (ii).
∎

Remark 5.12
.

Assume that
\eta,\tau,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
are such that
d=\eta^{2}-\tau^{2}-\lambda^{2}=-4
. Let
\nu=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{q})
, define the function
\mathcal{N}:\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{N/2\times N/2}
by

\mathcal{N}=\begin{cases}\nu_{1}+i\nu_{2},&q=2,\\
\sigma\cdot\nu,&q=3,\end{cases}

and note that

\alpha\cdot\nu=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathcal{N}^{*}\\
\mathcal{N}&0\end{pmatrix}

holds.
Then, the boundary conditions for
f\in{\rm dom\,}A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}^{\pm}
can be rewritten as

(5.19)

0=\big(2I_{N}\pm\lambda\alpha_{0}\mp i(\alpha\cdot\nu)(\eta I_{N}+\tau\alpha_{0})\big)\gamma_{D}^{\pm}f=\begin{pmatrix}(2\pm\lambda)I_{N/2}&\mp i(\eta-\tau)\mathcal{N}^{*}\\
\mp i(\eta+\tau)\mathcal{N}&(2\mp\lambda)I_{N/2}\end{pmatrix}\gamma_{D}^{\pm}f,

i.e.
f=(f_{1},f_{2})\in{\rm dom\,}A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}^{\pm}
with
f_{1},f_{2}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
if and only if
(f_{1},f_{2})\in H^{1/2}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
and

(5.20)

\begin{split}(2\pm\lambda)\gamma_{D}^{\pm}f_{1}\mp i(\eta-\tau)\mathcal{N}^{*}\gamma_{D}^{\pm}f_{2}&=0,\\
\mp i(\eta+\tau)\mathcal{N}\gamma_{D}^{\pm}f_{1}+(2\mp\lambda)\gamma_{D}^{\pm}f_{2}&=0.\end{split}

If
\lambda^{2}\neq 4
, then
d=-4
implies
\eta^{2}\neq\tau^{2}
and hence
d=-4
and
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}^{*}=I_{N/2}
yield that the two equations in (
5.20
) are equivalent to each other. Thus, in the latter case, one of the equations in (
5.20
) is sufficient to check if
f\in H^{1/2}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
belongs to
{\rm dom\,}A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}^{\pm}
.

In the following two propositions we discuss the (essential) self-adjointness of
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
in the confinement case. First, we discuss the non-critical case, i.e. when
(\frac{d}{4}-1)^{2}-\lambda^{2}\neq 0
. Using
d=-4
, we see that the condition for being non-critical is, in the confinement case, equivalent to
\lambda^{2}\neq 4
. We note that the Dirac operators with the same boundary conditions as
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}^{\pm}
were studied in the non-critical case in space dimension
q=2
in
[
24
,
81
]
and in dimension
q=3
in
[
14
]
.

Proposition 5.13
.

Let
\eta,\tau,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that
d=-4
and
\lambda^{2}\neq 4
and let
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}^{\pm}
be defined by (
5.17
). Then
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}^{\pm}
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
and the following is true:

(i)

\sigma(A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}^{+})
is purely discrete.

(ii)

\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}^{-})=(-\infty,-m]\cup[m,\infty)
and
\sigma_{\textup{disc}}(A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}^{-})
is finite.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.5
(iii) and Theorem
5.6
the operator
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})
,
\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda})=(-\infty,-m]\cup[m,\infty)
, and
\sigma_{\textup{disc}}(A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda})
is finite. Taking Lemma
5.11
into account, this can only be true if
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}^{\pm}
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
and, since
{\rm dom\,}A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}^{+}\subset H^{1/2}(\Omega_{+};\mathbb{C}^{N})
is compactly embedded in
L^{2}(\Omega_{+};\mathbb{C}^{N})
, if items (i) and (ii) are true.
∎

In the following proposition we discuss the case of confinement
d=-4
, when the interaction strengths
\eta,\tau,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
are critical, i.e. when
\lambda^{2}=4
. Note that the latter two conditions imply
\eta^{2}=\tau^{2}
.

Proposition 5.14
.

Let
\eta,\tau,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that
d=-4
and
\lambda^{2}=4
and let
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}^{\pm}
be defined by (
5.17
). Then the following holds:

(i)

For
\eta=\tau=0
and
\lambda^{2}=4
the operators
A_{0,0,\lambda}^{\pm}
are essentially
self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
and the following is true:

(a)

-{\text{\rm sgn\,}}(\lambda)m
is an eigenvalue of
\overline{A_{0,0,\lambda}^{+}}
with infinite multiplicity and
\sigma(\overline{A_{0,0,\lambda}^{+}})\setminus\{-{\text{\rm sgn\,}}(\lambda)m\}
is purely discrete.

(b)

\sigma(\overline{A_{0,0,\lambda}^{-}})=(-\infty,-m]\cup[m,\infty)
.

(ii)

For
\eta^{2}=\tau^{2}\neq 0
and
\lambda^{2}=4
one has
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}^{{\text{\rm sgn\,}}(\eta\tau\lambda)}=A_{0,0,\lambda}^{{\text{\rm sgn\,}}(\eta\tau\lambda)}
and there exist
\hat{\eta},\hat{\tau},\hat{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\hat{d}:=\hat{\eta}^{2}-\hat{\tau}^{2}-\hat{\lambda}^{2}=-4
,
(\frac{\hat{d}}{4}-1)^{2}\neq\hat{\lambda}^{2}
, and
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}^{-{\text{\rm sgn\,}}(\eta\tau\lambda)}=A_{\hat{\eta},\hat{\tau},\hat{\lambda}}^{-{\text{\rm sgn\,}}(\eta\tau\lambda)}
.

We remark that the operators
\overline{A_{0,0,2}^{\pm}}
and
\overline{A_{0,0,-2}^{\pm}}
are Dirac operators with
zigzag
type boundary conditions and their spectra without
\pm m
are closely related to the spectra of the Dirichlet Laplacians in
\Omega_{\pm}
. We refer to
[
50
,
65
,
87
]
for studies on Dirac operators with zigzag type boundary conditions.
It is remarkable that in the situation
\lambda^{2}=4
and
\eta^{2}=\lambda^{2}\neq 0
one of the operators
\overline{A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}^{\pm}}
is a Dirac operator with zigzag type boundary conditions, while the other one is a Dirac operator with boundary conditions, as they are treated in Proposition
5.13
.
In particular, the spectral properties of
\overline{A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}^{\pm}}
in the critical confinement case can be understood with the help of the Dirac operator with zigzag boundary conditions in Proposition
5.14
(i) and Proposition
5.13
and in all cases

is essentially self-adjoint.

Proof of Proposition
5.14
.

Throughout the proof, we often use for
f\in L^{2}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N})
the notation
f=(f_{1},f_{2})
with
f_{1},f_{2}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{\pm};\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
.
We sketch the proof of item (i) for
\lambda=2
, for details see also
[
65
,
87
]
or
[
37
, Theorem 2.4]
; the proof for
\lambda=-2
follows the same lines.
We make use of the differential expressions that are given by

(5.21)

\mathcal{D}:=\begin{cases}-i(\partial_{1}+i\partial_{2}),&q=2,\\
-i(\sigma\cdot\nabla),&q=3,\end{cases}\qquad\mathcal{D}^{*}:=\begin{cases}-i(\partial_{1}-i\partial_{2}),&q=2,\\
-i(\sigma\cdot\nabla),&q=3.\end{cases}

With the help of these expressions we can write

(5.22)

-i(\alpha\cdot\nabla)+m\alpha_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}mI_{N/2}&\mathcal{D}^{*}\\
\mathcal{D}&-mI_{N/2}\end{pmatrix}.

By (
5.20
) a function
f=(f_{1},f_{2})\in H^{1/2}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{+};\mathbb{C}^{N})
belongs to
{\rm dom\,}A_{0,0,2}^{+}
if and only if

(5.23)

\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{1}=0,

while there are no boundary conditions for
f_{2}
.
Let
f=(f_{1},f_{2})\in{\rm dom\,}A_{0,0,2}^{+}
. Then it follows from
f\in H^{1/2}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{+};\mathbb{C}^{N})
and (
5.22
) that
f_{1},f_{2}\in H^{1/2}(\Omega_{+};\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
and
\mathcal{D}f_{1},\mathcal{D}^{*}f_{2}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{+};\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
. We claim that one even has
f_{1}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{+};\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
.
To see this, define the function
g_{+}:=(f_{1},0)
. Since
\mathcal{D}f_{1}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{+};\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
, it follows from (
5.22
) that
g_{+}\in H^{1/2}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{+};\mathbb{C}^{N})
and hence,
g:=g_{+}\oplus 0\in H^{1/2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
. Since
f\in{\rm dom\,}A_{0,0,2}^{+}
, we have
\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{1}=0
and thus,
g\in{{\rm ker\,}\,}\Gamma_{0}^{(1/2)}={\rm dom\,}A_{0}=H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})
, cf. Theorem
4.3
. Hence,
f_{1}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{+};\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
and we conclude that

(5.24)

{\rm dom\,}A_{0,0,2}^{+}=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{+};\mathbb{C}^{N/2})\oplus\{f\in H^{1/2}(\Omega_{+};\mathbb{C}^{N/2}):\mathcal{D}^{*}f\in L^{2}(\Omega_{+};\mathbb{C}^{N/2})\}.

Recall that
\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}^{*}
are given by (
5.21
) and define the operator

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}f&=\mathcal{D}f,\quad{\rm dom\,}\mathcal{T}=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{+};\mathbb{C}^{N/2}).\end{split}

It is not difficult to see that
\mathcal{T}
is closed and that its adjoint is given by

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}^{*}f&=\mathcal{D}^{*}f,\quad{\rm dom\,}\mathcal{T}^{*}=\{f\in L^{2}(\Omega_{+};\mathbb{C}^{N/2}):\mathcal{D}^{*}f\in L^{2}(\Omega_{+};\mathbb{C}^{N/2})\}.\end{split}

Let
P
be the projection in
L^{2}(\Omega_{+};\mathbb{C}^{N})
defined for
f=(f_{1},f_{2})\in L^{2}(\Omega_{+};\mathbb{C}^{N})
by
P(f_{1},f_{2})=(0,f_{2})
. Then
{\rm dom\,}\mathcal{T}^{*}\simeq PH^{0}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{+};\mathbb{C}^{N})

and thus, as
C_{0}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega_{+}};\mathbb{C}^{N})
is dense in
H_{\alpha}^{0}(\Omega_{+};\mathbb{C}^{N})
, also
C_{0}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega_{+}};\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
is dense in
{\rm dom\,}\mathcal{T}^{*}
with respect to the graph norm. Therefore, we conclude from (
5.22
) and (
5.24
) that

\overline{A_{0,0,2}^{+}}=\begin{pmatrix}mI_{N/2}&\mathcal{T}^{*}\\
\mathcal{T}&-mI_{N/2}\end{pmatrix}.

In particular,
\overline{A_{0,0,2}^{+}}
has a supersymmetric structure, cf.
[
65
, Appendix A]
, and thus
\overline{A_{0,0,2}^{+}}
is self-adjoint. Next, choose
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
x_{1}-ix_{2}-(c_{1}-ic_{2})\neq 0
for all
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{q})\in\Omega_{+}
, and define for
n>2
the functions

(5.25)

f_{n}(x):=(x_{1}-ix_{2}-c_{1}+ic_{2})^{-n}e_{N},\quad x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{q})\in\Omega_{+},

where
e_{N}
is the
N
-th canonical basis vector in
\mathbb{C}^{N}
. Then, a direct calculation shows that
f_{n}\in{{\rm ker\,}\,}(\overline{A_{0,0,2}^{+}}+m)
, i.e.
-m
is an eigenvalue of
\overline{A_{0,0,2}^{+}}
with infinite multiplicity. Finally, one shows in a similar way as in
[
65
, Theorem 3.4]
with the help of
[
65
, Proposition A.2]
that
\sigma(\overline{A_{0,0,2}^{+}})=\{-m\}\cup\{\pm\sqrt{m^{2}+z}:z\in\sigma(-\Delta_{D}^{\Omega_{+}})\}
, where
-\Delta_{D}^{\Omega_{+}}
is the Dirichlet Laplacian in
\Omega_{+}
, and hence, as
\Omega_{+}
is a bounded Lipschitz domain,
\sigma(\overline{A_{0,0,2}^{+}})\setminus\{-m\}
is purely discrete; cf.
[
50
, Theorem 2.1 and Remark 2.3]
as well. Hence, item (i) (a) is shown.

To see the claims of assertion (i) (b), note that the boundary conditions for
A_{0,0,2}^{-}
are
2(I_{N}-\alpha_{0})\gamma_{D}^{-}f=0
. Therefore, one can conclude all claims with similar arguments as above; cf.
[
65
, Theorem 3.4]
for details in dimension three. In particular, as
\Omega_{-}
is an exterior domain it follows from
[
50
, Theorem 2.1]
that
\sigma(\overline{A_{0,0,2}^{-}})=\{m\}\cup\{\pm\sqrt{m^{2}+z}:z\in\sigma(-\Delta_{D}^{\Omega_{-}})\}=(-\infty,-m]\cup[m,\infty)
.

Let us prove item (ii). In this case, we have
\lambda^{2}=4
and
\eta^{2}=\tau^{2}
and thus, the boundary conditions in (
5.20
) are equivalent to

(5.26)

\begin{split}2(1\pm\textup{sgn}\,\lambda)\gamma_{D}^{\pm}f_{1}\mp i\eta(1-\textup{sgn}\,(\eta\tau))\mathcal{N}^{*}\gamma_{D}^{\pm}f_{2}&=0,\\
\mp i\eta(1+\textup{sgn}\,(\eta\tau))\mathcal{N}\gamma_{D}^{\pm}f_{1}+2(1\mp\textup{sgn}\,\lambda)\gamma_{D}^{\pm}f_{2}&=0.\end{split}

In the following, we consider
\eta=\tau\neq 0
and
\lambda=2
; in the other cases the proof is similar. In this situation
the boundary conditions for
A_{\eta,\eta,2}^{+}
are
\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{1}=0
, while there are no boundary conditions for

. Hence, we conclude

, cf. (

).

Moreover, by (

) the boundary conditions for

are

(5.27)

2i\mathcal{N}\eta\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{1}+4\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{2}=0.

Choose
\hat{\eta},\hat{\tau},\hat{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\eta=2\frac{\hat{\eta}+\hat{\tau}}{2+\hat{\lambda}}
,
\hat{d}:=\hat{\eta}^{2}-\hat{\tau}^{2}-\hat{\lambda}^{2}=-4
, and
\hat{\lambda}^{2}\neq 4
. Then, with the help of (

) it is not difficult to see that (

) is equivalent to the boundary conditions for

. This finishes the proof of this proposition.
∎

Finally, we remark that in dimension two one has that

is an eigenvalue of

of infinite multiplicity. Indeed, let

. Then, in a similar way as in (

, and shows via a direct calculation that

, cf.

, Theorem 2.4]
. Similarly, one gets that

is an eigenvalue of

of infinite multiplicity. However, this argument fails in dimension

.

Throughout this section let

In a similar way as in (
5.2
) we define for
\eta,\tau,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
the operator

(6.1)

\begin{split}B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}f&=\big(-i(\alpha\cdot\nabla)+m\alpha_{0}\big)f_{+}\oplus\big(-i(\alpha\cdot\nabla)+m\alpha_{0}\big)f_{-},\\
{\rm dom\,}B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}&=\left\{f\in H^{0}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}):i(\alpha\cdot\nu)(\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{+}-\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{-})\right.\\
&\hskip 130.0pt\left.+P_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{+}+\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{-})=0\right\},\end{split}

where
\gamma_{D}^{\pm}
are the Dirichlet trace operators defined in Corollary
4.6
and
P_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
is given by (
5.1
). Note that the operator
B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
is defined on a larger set as
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
in (
5.2
). However, it will turn out that in the non-critical case
\lambda^{2}\neq(\frac{d}{4}-1)^{2}
, which includes all combinations of interaction strengths treated in Section
5.1
, one always has
{\rm dom\,}B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}\subset H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
and hence,
A_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}=B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
; cf. Theorem
6.2
.

In order to show the self-adjointness and to study the spectral properties of
B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
for all values of
\eta,\tau,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
we make use of the ordinary boundary triple
\{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}),\Upsilon_{0},\Upsilon_{1}\}
from Theorem
4.8
and the associated notations.
For the operators
\Lambda
and
V
appearing in the boundary triple in Theorem
4.8
we make specific choices. We will introduce
\Lambda
as an operator acting on scalar-valued functions, but we will identify it with
\Lambda I_{M}
which acts on vector-valued functions for various values of
M\in\mathbb{N}
.
In space dimension
q=2
, we write
\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\simeq[0,1)
, and denote by
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{T})
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T})
the sets of all
1
-periodic test functions and distributions in
\mathbb{R}
, respectively, by
\ell
the length of
\Sigma
, by
\gamma:[0,\ell]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
an arc length parametrization of
\Sigma
, and we introduce the mappings
e_{n}(t)=e^{2\pi int}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, and

U:\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Sigma)\rightarrow\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T})
by

\langle Uf,\varphi\rangle_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T})\times\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{T})}:=\big\langle f,\ell^{-1}\varphi(\ell^{-1}\gamma^{-1}(\cdot))\big\rangle_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Sigma)\times\mathcal{D}(\Sigma)},\quad\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{T}),

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{X^{\prime}\times X}
denotes the sesquilinear duality product in
X^{\prime}\times X
for a Hilbert space
X
.
Then we define for a constant
c_{\Lambda}>0
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
the map

(6.2)

\Lambda^{t}u:=U^{-1}\left(\frac{4\pi}{\ell}\right)^{t/2}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(c_{\Lambda}+|n|)^{t/2}\langle Uu,e_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T})\times\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{T})}e_{n},\qquad u\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Sigma).

If
q=3
, we denote by
\mathcal{S}
the single layer boundary integral operator associated with
-\Delta+1
that is acting on
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C})
by

\mathcal{S}\varphi(x)=\int_{\Sigma}\frac{e^{-|x-y|}}{4\pi|x-y|}\varphi(y)d\sigma(y),\quad x\in\Sigma.

It is known that for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
this map can be extended to a bounded operator
\mathcal{S}:H^{s}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C})\rightarrow H^{s+1}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C})
; cf.
[
76
, Theorem 7.2]
. Define now with
c_{\Lambda}>0

(6.3)

\Lambda:=(\mathcal{S}^{-1}+c_{\Lambda})^{1/2}.

In both cases
q\in\{2,3\}
the operator
\Lambda
gives rise to a bijective operator from
H^{s}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C})
to
H^{s-1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C})
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
, see
[
66
]
for details. Moreover, the realization of
\Lambda
in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C})
is self-adjoint with
{\rm dom\,}\Lambda=H^{1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C})
, one has
\Lambda\geq c_{\Lambda}^{1/2}
, and, since
\Lambda^{-1}
is compact by Rellich’s embedding theorem, this realization in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C})
satisfies
\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(\Lambda)=\emptyset
.

Next, the map
V
appearing in the definition of the boundary triple in Theorem
4.8
is defined, depending on the space dimension, by

(6.4)

V=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-i(\nu_{1}-i\nu_{2})\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{for }q=2\text{ and}\quad V=\begin{pmatrix}I_{2}&0\\
0&-i(\sigma\cdot\nu)\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{for }q=3.

Moreover, we introduce the matrix
B\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
by

B:=\begin{pmatrix}(\eta+\tau)I_{N/2}&\lambda I_{N/2}\\
\lambda I_{N/2}&(\eta-\tau)I_{N/2}\end{pmatrix}.

Then, we have
P_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}=V^{*}BV
.
Define in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
the operator
\Theta
by

(6.5)

\begin{split}\Theta\varphi&=\Lambda\big(B-4V(\alpha\cdot\nu)\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\alpha\cdot\nu)V^{*}\big)\Lambda\varphi,\\
{\rm dom\,}\Theta&=\big\{\varphi\in L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}):\big(B-4V(\alpha\cdot\nu)\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\alpha\cdot\nu)V^{*}\big)\Lambda\varphi\in H^{1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\big\},\end{split}

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
is defined as in Proposition
4.4
.
Using the relations
P_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}=V^{*}BV
and

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{+}+\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{-})&=V^{*}\Lambda\Upsilon_{0}f,\\
i(\alpha\cdot\nu)(\gamma_{D}^{+}f_{+}-\gamma_{D}^{-}f_{-})&=-V^{*}\Lambda^{-1}\Upsilon_{1}f-4(\alpha\cdot\nu)\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\alpha\cdot\nu)V^{*}\Lambda\Upsilon_{0}f,\end{split}

it is not difficult to see that the transmission conditions in the definition of
B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
are equivalent to
\Upsilon_{1}f-\Theta\Upsilon_{0}f=0
, i.e. we have

(6.6)

{\rm dom\,}B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}={\rm dom\,}T^{(0)}\upharpoonright{{\rm ker\,}\,}(\Upsilon_{1}-\Theta\Upsilon_{0}).

Therefore, to understand the properties of
B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
, in view of Theorem
2.3
, it suffices to study the associated parameter
\Theta
.
In order to show the self-adjointness of
\Theta
, a detailed analysis of the involved integral operators is required. Define the operator
\mathcal{R}
by

(6.7)

\mathcal{R}\varphi(x)=\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{\Sigma\setminus B(x,\varepsilon)}r(x,y)\varphi(y)d\sigma(y),\qquad\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2}),

where the integral kernel
r
is given by

r(x,y)=\begin{cases}-\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{\nu_{1}(y)+i\nu_{2}(y)}{x_{1}+ix_{2}-(y_{1}+iy_{2})},&\text{ if }q=2,\\
-\frac{\sigma\cdot(x-y)}{\pi|x-y|^{3}}(\sigma\cdot\nu(y)),&\text{ if }q=3.\end{cases}

Note that for
q=2
the operator
\mathcal{R}
is a multiple of the Cauchy transform, while for
q=3
the map
\mathcal{R}
is closely related to the Riesz transform. We define also the formal adjoint of
\mathcal{R}
w.r.t. the inner product in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
by

\mathcal{R}^{*}\varphi(x)=\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{\Sigma\setminus B(x,\varepsilon)}r(y,x)^{*}\varphi(y)d\sigma(y).

For any
s\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]
one can show that
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}^{*}
give rise to bounded operators in
H^{s}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
that satisfy for all
\varphi\in H^{s}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
and
\psi\in H^{-s}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
the relation

\langle\mathcal{R}\varphi,\psi\rangle_{H^{s}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})\times H^{-s}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})}=\langle\varphi,\mathcal{R}^{*}\psi\rangle_{H^{s}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})\times H^{-s}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})},

see
[
66
]
, and we denote their realizations in
H^{-1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
by
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*}
, respectively. Then we have the following result
[
66
]
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}^{*}
be defined as above and let
\mathcal{M}(z)
,
z\in(-m,m)
, be the Weyl function associated with the boundary triple in Theorem
4.8
. Then the following holds:

(i)

There exists a self-adjoint and compact operator
\mathcal{K}_{1}
in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
such that

\Theta-\mathcal{M}(z)=\Lambda\begin{pmatrix}(\eta+\tau)I_{N/2}&\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*}\\
\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}&(\eta-\tau)I_{N/2}\end{pmatrix}\Lambda-4\begin{pmatrix}(z-m)I_{N/2}&0\\
0&(z+m)I_{N/2}\end{pmatrix}+\mathcal{K}_{1}.

(ii)

The operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*}:H^{-1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})\rightarrow H^{1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
is bounded.

(iii)

One has
\mathcal{R}^{2}=4I_{N/2}
. In particular, this implies that

\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*}-4I_{N/2}=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}(\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}):H^{-1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})\rightarrow H^{1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})

is bounded.

(iv)

There exist a compact operator
\mathcal{K}_{2}:H^{-1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})\rightarrow H^{1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
and closed subspaces
\mathcal{H}_{\pm}
of
H^{-1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
satisfying
{\rm dim\,}\mathcal{H}_{+}={\rm dim\,}\mathcal{H}_{-}
,
H^{-1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})=\mathcal{H}_{+}\dot{+}\mathcal{H}_{-}
, and
\mathcal{H}_{\pm}\not\subset H^{s}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
for any
s>-\frac{1}{2}
such that the realization of
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*}
in the space
H^{-1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
can be written as

\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*}=4P_{+}-4P_{-}+\mathcal{K}_{2},

where
P_{\pm}
is the projection onto
\mathcal{H}_{\pm}
. Moreover, for any
\varphi\in H^{1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
one has
P_{\pm}\varphi\in H^{1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
.

Now, we are prepared to prove the self-adjointness of
B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
. We remark that the result in the non-critical case
(\frac{d}{4}-1)^{2}-\lambda^{2}\neq 0
has been obtained in space dimension
q=2
with a different proof in
[
37
]
.

Theorem 6.2
.

The operator
B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})
and the following holds:

(i)

If
(\frac{d}{4}-1)^{2}-\lambda^{2}\neq 0
, then
{\rm dom\,}B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}\subset H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
.

(ii)

If
(\frac{d}{4}-1)^{2}-\lambda^{2}=0
, then
{\rm dom\,}B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}\not\subset H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
for all
s>0
.

Proof.

In view of Theorem
2.3
and (
6.6
) the operator
B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q};\mathbb{C}^{N})
if and only if
\Theta
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
. Using Proposition
6.1
and
\mathcal{M}(0)=0
we see that this is the case if and only if the operator

\begin{split}\Xi\varphi&=\Lambda\begin{pmatrix}(\eta+\tau)I_{N/2}&\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*}\\
\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}&(\eta-\tau)I_{N/2}\end{pmatrix}\Lambda\varphi,\\
{\rm dom\,}\Xi&=\left\{\varphi\in L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}):\Lambda\begin{pmatrix}(\eta+\tau)I_{N/2}&\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*}\\
\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}&(\eta-\tau)I_{N/2}\end{pmatrix}\Lambda\varphi\in L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\right\},\end{split}

is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
, as
\Theta-\Xi
is a bounded and self-adjoint operator in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
. We are going to prove the self-adjointness of
\Xi
for
\eta+\tau\neq 0
, the other cases will be commented at the end of the proof. We will make use of the restriction

(6.8)

\Xi_{1}:=\Xi\upharpoonright H^{1}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}).

Using standard arguments as, e.g., in Step 1 and Step 3 in the proof of
[
12
, Lemma 5.4]
, it is not difficult to show that
\Xi_{1}
is symmetric,
\Xi_{1}^{*}\subset\Xi
, and that
\Xi
is closed. So in order to show the self-adjointness of
\Xi
, it suffices to prove
\Xi\subset\overline{\Xi_{1}}
. For that, we consider the Schur complements associated with
\Xi
and
\Xi_{1}
, respectively. Recall that we assume
\eta+\tau\neq 0
. We claim that the representation

(6.9)

\begin{split}\Xi=\begin{pmatrix}I_{N/2}&0\\
(\eta+\tau)^{-1}\Lambda(\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}})\Lambda^{-1}&I_{N/2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}(\eta+\tau)\Lambda^{2}&0\\
0&\mathfrak{s}\end{pmatrix}\qquad\qquad\qquad&\\
\begin{pmatrix}I_{N/2}&(\eta+\tau)^{-1}\Lambda^{-1}(\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*})\Lambda\\
0&I_{N/2}\end{pmatrix}&\end{split}

with the maximal realization of the Schur complement

\begin{split}\mathfrak{s}\varphi&=(\eta-\tau)\Lambda^{2}-(\eta+\tau)^{-1}\Lambda(\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}})(\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*})\Lambda\varphi,\\
{\rm dom\,}\mathfrak{s}&=\big\{\varphi\in L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2}):\\
&\qquad\big((\eta-\tau)\Lambda^{2}-(\eta+\tau)^{-1}\Lambda(\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}})(\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*})\Lambda\big)\varphi\in L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})\big\},\end{split}

holds and that the domain of
\Xi
can be characterized as

(6.10)

\begin{split}{\rm dom\,}\Xi=\big\{\varphi=&(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})\in L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}):\varphi_{2}\in{\rm dom\,}\mathfrak{s},\\
&\varphi_{1}+(\eta+\tau)^{-1}\Lambda^{-1}(\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*})\Lambda\varphi_{2}\in H^{1}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})\big\}.\end{split}

Indeed, the right hand side in (
6.10
) is contained in
{\rm dom\,}\Xi
, as the application of the product on the right hand side in (
6.9
) on elements in this set gives an element in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
and coincides with the application of
\Xi
. On the other hand, as the first factor on the right hand side in (
6.9
) is a bijective mapping in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
and
{\rm dom\,}\Lambda^{2}=H^{1}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
, also the second inclusion in (
6.10
) holds.
Using Proposition
6.1
we see that

(6.11)

\begin{split}\mathfrak{s}&=(\eta+\tau)^{-1}\Lambda\big((\eta^{2}-\tau^{2}-\lambda^{2})I_{N/2}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*}-\lambda(\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*})\big)\Lambda\\
&=(\eta+\tau)^{-1}\Lambda\big((\eta^{2}-\tau^{2}-\lambda^{2}-4)I_{N/2}-4\lambda(P_{+}-P_{-})\big)\Lambda+\mathcal{A}\\
&=(\eta+\tau)^{-1}\Lambda\big((d-4-4\lambda)P_{+}+(d-4+4\lambda)P_{-}\big)\Lambda+\mathcal{A},\end{split}

where
\mathcal{A}
is a bounded operator in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
and
P_{\pm}
are the projections onto the spaces
\mathcal{H}_{\pm}
in Proposition
6.1
(iv). Under the assumption in (i) the last equation implies for
\varphi=(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})\in{\rm dom\,}\Xi
that

(d-4-4\lambda)P_{+}\Lambda\varphi_{2}+(d-4+4\lambda)P_{-}\Lambda\varphi_{2}\in H^{1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2}).

Applying
P_{\pm}
, which is by Proposition
6.1
a map in
H^{1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
, to this equation yields
P_{\pm}\Lambda\varphi_{2}\in H^{1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
, which gives finally
\varphi_{2}\in H^{1}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
. Taking the mapping properties of
\Lambda
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*}
into account, the second condition in (
6.10
) shows for
\varphi=(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})\in{\rm dom\,}\Xi
that
\varphi_{1}\in H^{1}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
. Hence, we obtain
{\rm dom\,}\Xi=H^{1}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
and thus,
\Xi=\Xi_{1}
.

On the other hand, under the assumption in (ii) at least one of the terms with
P_{\pm}
in the last line in (
6.11
) is zero. For instance, if
\lambda=\frac{d}{4}-1
, then there is no condition on
P_{+}\Lambda\varphi
in
{\rm dom\,}\mathfrak{s}
, i.e. any
\varphi\in L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
with
P_{-}\Lambda\varphi\in H^{1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
belongs to
{\rm dom\,}\mathfrak{s}
. Since
\mathcal{H}_{+}\not\subset H^{s}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
for any
s>-\frac{1}{2}
by Proposition
6.1
(iv), this implies that
{\rm dom\,}\mathfrak{s}\not\subset H^{s}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
for any
s>0
and thus, also
{\rm dom\,}\Xi\not\subset H^{s}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
for any
s>0
. Clearly, a similar statement holds, if
\lambda=-\frac{d}{4}+1
.

To see now that
\Xi\subset\overline{\Xi_{1}}
also under the assumption in (ii), we use
[
91
, Theorem 2.2.14]
, which implies that

\begin{split}{\rm dom\,}\overline{\Xi_{1}}=\big\{\varphi=(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})&\in L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}):\varphi_{2}\in{\rm dom\,}\overline{\mathfrak{s}_{1}},\\
&\varphi_{1}+(\eta+\tau)^{-1}\Lambda^{-1}(\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*})\Lambda\varphi_{2}\in H^{1}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})\big\},\end{split}

where
\mathfrak{s}_{1}
is the Schur complement associated with
\Xi_{1}
and given by
\mathfrak{s}_{1}=\mathfrak{s}\upharpoonright H^{1}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
.
Suppose that
\lambda=\frac{d}{4}-1
; the arguments for
\lambda=-\frac{d}{4}+1
follow the same lines.
Let
\varphi\in{\rm dom\,}\mathfrak{s}
and choose
\varphi_{n}\subset H^{1}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
such that
P_{+}\Lambda\varphi_{n}
converge to
P_{+}\Lambda\varphi
in
H^{-1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
and
P_{-}\Lambda\varphi_{n}=P_{-}\Lambda\varphi
. Then,
\varphi_{n}\rightarrow\varphi
in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
and by (
6.11
) also
\mathfrak{s}_{1}\varphi_{n}\rightarrow\mathfrak{s}\varphi
. Hence,
\varphi\in{\rm dom\,}\overline{\mathfrak{s}_{1}}
and
\overline{\mathfrak{s}_{1}}\varphi=\mathfrak{s}\varphi
which shows that also in this case
\mathfrak{s}\subset\overline{\mathfrak{s}_{1}}
and then with (
6.10
) that
\Xi\subset\overline{\Xi_{1}}
. Hence, in all cases
\Xi
and thus also
\Theta
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
, which implies with Theorem
2.3
the self-adjointness of
B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
.

It remains to see the statements on the Sobolev regularity in
{\rm dom\,}B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
. For this purpose, we use that
\beta(z)\varphi\in H^{s}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
if and only if
\varphi\in H^{s}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
holds for any
s\in[0,1]
, where
\beta(z)
denotes the
\gamma
-field associated with the boundary triple
\{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}),\Upsilon_{0},\Upsilon_{1}\}
, cf. Theorem
4.8
. Indeed, if
\varphi\in H^{s}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
, then the mapping properties of
\Phi_{z}
and
\mathcal{C}_{z}
in Theorem
4.3
, Proposition
4.4
, and Corollary
4.5
, and
\Lambda
imply that

\beta(z)\varphi=-4\widetilde{\Phi}_{z}(\alpha\cdot\nu)\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{z}(\alpha\cdot\nu)V^{*}\Lambda\varphi\in H^{s}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}).

Conversely, if
\beta(z)\varphi\in H^{s}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
, then the mapping properties of the trace operator in Lemma
4.1
and Corollary
4.6
and Proposition
4.4
(iii) show that

\gamma_{D}^{+}(\beta(z)\varphi)_{+}+\gamma_{D}^{-}(\beta(z)\varphi)_{-}=-8(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{z}(\alpha\cdot\nu))^{2}V^{*}\Lambda\varphi=2V^{*}\Lambda\varphi\in H^{s-1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}).

This implies that
\Lambda\varphi\in H^{s-1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
, i.e.
\varphi\in H^{s}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
.

Recall that for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
the formula

(6.12)

(B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}-z)^{-1}=(A_{\infty}-z)^{-1}+\beta(z)\big(\Theta-\mathcal{M}(z)\big)^{-1}\beta(\overline{z})^{*}

holds, see Theorem
2.3
. Hence, under the assumption in item (i) we conclude that
{\rm dom\,}A_{\infty}\subset H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
and
{\rm dom\,}\Theta=H^{1}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
imply

{\rm dom\,}B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}\subset{\rm dom\,}A_{\infty}+\beta(z)\big(H^{1}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})\big)\subset H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N}).

With similar arguments we see that, under the assumption of statement (ii), the relation
{\rm dom\,}\Theta\not\subset H^{s}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
implies
{\rm dom\,}B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}\not\subset H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
for any
s>0
. This finishes the proof of the theorem in the case
\eta+\tau\neq 0
.

If
\eta=-\tau\neq 0
, then a similar strategy as in the case
\eta+\tau\neq 0
leads to the conclusion of the claimed results. Indeed, in this case one can make a similar construction as in (
6.9
), but with the Schur complement being in the upper left corner in the operator matrix in the middle of the product in (
6.9
) instead of the lower right corner.

Finally, assume
\eta=\tau=0
and
\lambda\neq 0
. Using Proposition
6.1
(iv) one sees that
\Xi
can be written as

\begin{split}\Xi&=\Lambda\begin{pmatrix}0&\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*}\\
\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}&0\end{pmatrix}\Lambda\\
&=\Lambda\begin{pmatrix}0&\lambda I_{N/2}+\frac{1}{2}(\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*})+\frac{1}{2}(\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}})\\
\lambda I_{N/2}+\frac{1}{2}(\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*})+\frac{1}{2}(\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*})&0\end{pmatrix}\Lambda\\
&=\Lambda\begin{pmatrix}0&(\lambda+2)P_{+}+(\lambda-2)P_{-}\\
(\lambda+2)P_{+}+(\lambda-2)P_{-}&0\end{pmatrix}\Lambda+\mathcal{B},\end{split}

where
\mathcal{B}
is a bounded operator in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
.
The representation in the third line of the last equation and Proposition
6.1
show as above that for
\lambda\neq\pm 2
one has
{\rm dom\,}\Xi=H^{1}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
and thus,
\Xi=\Xi_{1}
is self-adjoint.
On the other hand, for
\lambda=\pm 2
one has no restrictions for one of the spaces
\mathcal{H}_{\pm}
, which implies with a similar argument as before in the case
\eta+\tau\neq 0
that
\overline{\Xi_{1}}=\Xi
is again self-adjoint, but
{\rm dom\,}\Xi\not\subset H^{s}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N})
for any
s>0
.
Moreover, one finds in a similar way as above with the help of Krein’s resolvent formula in (
6.12
) the claimed results about the Sobolev regularity in
{\rm dom\,}B_{0,0,\lambda}
. This finishes the proof of this theorem.
∎

where

To show item (i) let

\begin{split}(B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}-z)^{-1}&=(A_{\infty}-z)^{-1}+\beta(z)(\Theta-\mathcal{M}(z))^{-1}\beta(\overline{z})^{*}\\

To proceed, we note that the operator

which is the claimed formula.

Item (ii) is a consequence of the Birman-Schwinger principle in Theorem

In the following we will study the essential spectrum of

in the critical case.
In the critical confinement case, i.e. when

.
In this situation, we will see that there may be an additional point in the essential spectrum of

\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}
and

defined in (

\mathcal{H}_{\pm}

Proposition 6.7
.

For the operators
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}},\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*}
and the choice of
\Lambda
in (
6.2
)–(
6.3
) the following holds:

(i)

For
q=2
, the operator
\Lambda(\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*}-4I_{1})\Lambda
is compact in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C})
, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
one has
\Lambda^{n}P_{\pm}=P_{\pm}\Lambda^{n}
, and
P_{\pm}
are orthogonal projections in
H^{s}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C})
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

(ii)

For
q=3
, assume that there exists an open set
\Sigma_{0}\subset\Sigma
such that
\Sigma_{0}
is contained in a plane. Then, there exists a sequence
(\varphi_{n})\subset L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{2})
such that
\|\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{2})}=1
,
\varphi_{n}
converges weakly to zero, and

\Lambda(\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*}-4I_{2})\Lambda\varphi_{n}\rightarrow 0
in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{2})
. It is possible to choose this sequence such that
(\Lambda\varphi_{n})\subset\mathcal{H}_{\pm}
.

(iii)

The operator
\Lambda^{-2}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\Lambda^{-2}:H^{-1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})\rightarrow H^{1/2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
is compact.

Now, we are prepared to state and prove the following result about the essential spectrum of
B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda}
:

Theorem 6.8
.

Let
\eta,\tau,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
be such that
(\frac{d}{4}-1)^{2}-\lambda^{2}=0
and
d\neq-4
. Then, the following holds:

(i)

If
q=2
, then
\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda})=(-\infty,-m]\cup\big\{-\frac{\tau}{\eta}m\big\}\cup[m,\infty)
.

(ii)

If
q=3
, assume that there exists an open set
\Sigma_{0}\subset\Sigma
such that
\Sigma_{0}
is contained in a plane. Then
(-\infty,-m]\cup\big\{-\frac{\tau}{\eta}m\big\}\cup[m,\infty)\subset\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda})
.

We also refer to
[
28
]
for a very recent result in a similar spirit, where the three-dimensional Dirac operator with a combination of electrostatic and Lorentz-scalar
\delta
-shell interactions supported on the boundary of a bounded
C^{\infty}
-domain was considered in the critical case (i.e. when
\eta,\tau\in\mathbb{R}
with
\eta^{2}-\tau^{2}=4
and
\lambda=0
). With the help of pseudodifferential techniques, it was shown there that a whole new interval of essential spectrum centered at
-\frac{\tau}{\eta}m
appears in this situation. In particular, in this special case the inclusion in Theorem
6.8
(ii) is in general not an equality.

Proof of Theorem
6.8
.

We note first that with the same singular sequences as in the proof of
[
12
, Theorem 5.7 (i)]
one shows that
(-\infty,-m]\cup[m,\infty)\subset\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda})
. Hence, it remains to study
\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda})\cap(-m,m)
.

We use that
z\in\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda})\cap(-m,m)
if and only if
0\in\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(\Theta-\mathcal{M}(z))
, cf. Proposition
6.5
(iii). By Proposition
6.1
the latter is true if and only if
0
belongs to the essential spectrum of

(6.16)

\hat{\Xi}=\Lambda\begin{pmatrix}(\eta+\tau)I_{N/2}&\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*}\\
\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}&(\eta-\tau)I_{N/2}\end{pmatrix}\Lambda-4\begin{pmatrix}(z-m)I_{N/2}&0\\
0&(z+m)I_{N/2}\end{pmatrix}

defined on its maximal domain
{\rm dom\,}\hat{\Xi}={\rm dom\,}\Theta
, as
\Theta-\hat{\Xi}
is a compact and self-adjoint operator that does not influence the essential spectrum. We note that the assumptions
(\frac{d}{4}-1)^{2}-\lambda^{2}=0
and
d\neq-4
imply that
|\eta|\neq|\tau|
, hence, one can choose the constant
c_{\Lambda}
in (
6.2
)–(
6.3
) sufficiently large such that
0\in\rho((\eta+\tau)\Lambda^{2}-4(z-m)I_{N/2})
. Then, it follows from
[
91
, Theorem 2.4.6]
that
0\in\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(\hat{\Xi})
if and only if
0\in\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(\hat{\mathfrak{s}})
, where
\hat{\mathfrak{s}}
is the Schur complement associated with
\hat{\Xi}
given by

\begin{split}\hat{\mathfrak{s}}&=(\eta-\tau)\Lambda^{2}-4(z+m)I_{N/2}\\
&\qquad-\Lambda(\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}})\Lambda\big((\eta+\tau)\Lambda^{2}-4(z-m)I_{N/2}\big)^{-1}\Lambda(\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*})\Lambda,\\
{\rm dom\,}\widehat{\mathfrak{s}}&=\big\{\varphi\in L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2}):(\eta-\tau)\Lambda^{2}\varphi-4(z+m)\varphi\\
&\qquad-\Lambda(\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}})\Lambda\big((\eta+\tau)\Lambda^{2}-4(z-m)I_{N/2}\big)^{-1}\Lambda(\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*})\Lambda\varphi\\
&\qquad\qquad\in L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})\big\}.\end{split}

By iterating the resolvent identity twice, one finds for
w\in\rho(\Lambda^{2})
that

(\Lambda^{2}-w)^{-1}=\Lambda^{-2}+w\Lambda^{-2}(\Lambda^{2}-w)^{-1}=\Lambda^{-2}+w\Lambda^{-4}+w^{2}\Lambda^{-3}(\Lambda^{2}-w)^{-1}\Lambda^{-1}

holds. Applying this formula with
w=4(z-m)/(\eta+\tau)
, we find that

\begin{split}\hat{\mathfrak{s}}=(\eta-\tau)\Lambda^{2}-4(z+m)I_{N/2}-\frac{1}{\eta+\tau}\Lambda(\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}})(\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*})\Lambda&\\
-4\frac{z-m}{(\eta+\tau)^{2}}\Lambda(\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}})\Lambda^{-2}(\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*})\Lambda+\mathcal{K}_{1}&,\end{split}

where

\mathcal{K}_{1}=-16\frac{(z-m)^{2}}{(\eta+\tau)^{3}}\Lambda(\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}})\Lambda^{-2}\big(\Lambda^{2}-4(z-m)(\eta+\tau)^{-1}\big)^{-1}(\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*})\Lambda

is due to the mapping properties of
\Lambda
specified at the beginning of this section bounded from
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
to
H^{1}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
and hence, by Rellich’s embedding theorem compact in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
. Taking Propositions
6.1
(iv) and
6.7
(iii) into account and making a similar calculation as in (
6.11
), there exist compact operators
\mathcal{K}_{2},\mathcal{K}_{3}
in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
such that we can rewrite

\begin{split}\hat{\mathfrak{s}}&=\frac{1}{\eta+\tau}\Lambda((d-4)I_{N/2}-4\lambda(P_{+}-P_{-}))\Lambda+\frac{1}{\eta+\tau}\Lambda(4I_{N/2}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*})\Lambda\\
&\qquad-4(z+m)I_{N/2}-4\frac{z-m}{(\eta+\tau)^{2}}\Lambda^{-1}(\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}})(\lambda I_{N/2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*})\Lambda+\mathcal{K}_{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{\eta+\tau}\Lambda\big((d-4)I_{N/2}-4\lambda(P_{+}-P_{-})\big)\Lambda+\frac{1}{\eta+\tau}\Lambda(4I_{N/2}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*})\Lambda\\
&\qquad-4(z+m)I_{N/2}-4\frac{z-m}{(\eta+\tau)^{2}}\Lambda^{-1}\big((\lambda^{2}+4)I_{N/2}+4\lambda(P_{+}-P_{-})\big)\Lambda+\mathcal{K}_{3}.\end{split}

In the following, we consider the case when
4\lambda=d-4
, the case
4\lambda=4-d
can be handled similarly. For
4\lambda=d-4
, the expression for
\hat{\mathfrak{s}}
simplifies to

(6.17)

\begin{split}\hat{\mathfrak{s}}&=\frac{2}{\eta+\tau}\Lambda(d-4)P_{-}\Lambda-8\frac{\eta}{\eta+\tau}\Lambda^{-1}\left(z+\frac{\tau}{\eta}m\right)P_{+}\Lambda+\frac{1}{\eta+\tau}\Lambda(4I_{N/2}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*})\Lambda\\
&\qquad-4\Lambda^{-1}\left(z+m+\frac{z-m}{(\eta+\tau)^{2}}(\lambda^{2}-d+8)\right)P_{-}\Lambda+\mathcal{K}_{3}.\end{split}

Now, there exists a sequence
(\varphi_{n})
with
(\Lambda\varphi_{n})\subset\mathcal{H}_{+}
such that
\|\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})}=1
,
\varphi_{n}
converges weakly to zero, and

\Lambda(\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*}-4)\Lambda\varphi_{n}\rightarrow 0
in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C}^{N/2})
; for
q=3
this is stated in Proposition
6.7
(ii), while for
q=2
this is true as by Proposition
6.7
(i)
\Lambda(\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*}-4)\Lambda
is compact in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C})
and compact operators turn any weakly convergent sequence into a strongly convergent one. With the help of this sequence, we see that
0\in\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(\hat{\mathfrak{s}})
, if
z=-\frac{\tau}{\eta}m
, which shows that
0\in\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(\Theta-\mathcal{M}(-\frac{\tau}{\eta}m))
and hence,
-\frac{\tau}{\eta}m\in\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda})
. In particular, for
q=3
all claims are shown.

Let
q=2
, then it remains to show that
\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda})\cap(-m,m)=\{-\frac{\tau}{\eta}m\}
.
Assume that this is not the case. Then following the arguments at the beginning of this proof there is
z\in(-m,m)\setminus\{-\frac{\tau}{\eta}m\}
such that
0\in\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(\hat{\mathfrak{s}})
. Thus, there exists a singular Weyl sequence, i.e. there exists a sequence
(\varphi_{n})\subset{\rm dom\,}\hat{\mathfrak{s}}
such that
\|\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C})}=1
,
\varphi_{n}
converges weakly to zero, and
\hat{\mathfrak{s}}\varphi_{n}\rightarrow 0
. Consider first the case
4\lambda=d-4\neq 0
.
Since
\Lambda(4I_{1}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{*})\Lambda
is compact in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C})
,
P_{\pm}\Lambda=\Lambda P_{\pm}
, and
P_{\pm}
are orthogonal projections, see Proposition
6.7
(i),
the representation of
\hat{\mathfrak{s}}
in (
6.17
) can be simplified to

\begin{split}\hat{\mathfrak{s}}&=-8\frac{\eta}{\eta+\tau}\left(z+\frac{\tau}{\eta}m\right)P_{+}\\
&\qquad+P_{-}\left(\frac{2d-8}{\eta+\tau}\Lambda^{2}-4\left(z+m+\frac{z-m}{(\eta+\tau)^{2}}(\lambda^{2}-d+8)\right)\right)P_{-}+\mathcal{K}_{4},\end{split}

where
\mathcal{K}_{4}
is a compact operator in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C})
. Since
P_{\pm}
are orthogonal projections in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C})
by Proposition
6.7
(i), these maps are bounded in
L^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{C})
, and thus, an application of
P_{+}
to last displayed formula shows that
P_{+}\varphi_{n}\rightarrow 0
. Hence,
(P_{-}\varphi_{n})
is a singular sequence for

\begin{split}\frac{2d-8}{\eta+\tau}\Lambda^{2}-4\left(z+m+\frac{z-m}{(\eta+\tau)^{2}}(\lambda^{2}-d+8)\right)+\mathcal{K}_{4}.\end{split}

However, the definition of
\Lambda
in (
6.2
) implies that the essential spectrum of
\Lambda^{2}
is empty, which is finally a contradiction. This finishes the proof of this theorem in the case
4\lambda=d-4\neq 0
. The case
4\lambda=-d+4\neq 0
can be handled similarly. Eventually, if
4\lambda=d-4=0
, then the expression in (
6.17
) can be simplified to

\begin{split}\hat{\mathfrak{s}}&=-8\frac{\eta}{\eta+\tau}\left(z+\frac{\tau}{\eta}m\right)+\mathcal{K}_{4},\end{split}

and hence,
z=-\frac{\tau}{\eta}m
is the only point with
0\in\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(\widehat{\mathfrak{s}})
. Therefore, we have shown in all cases that
z=-\frac{\tau}{\eta}m
is the only possible point such that
0\in\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(\widehat{\mathfrak{s}})
, i.e.
\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(B_{\eta,\tau,\lambda})\cap(-m,m)=\{-\frac{\tau}{\eta}m\}
.
∎
