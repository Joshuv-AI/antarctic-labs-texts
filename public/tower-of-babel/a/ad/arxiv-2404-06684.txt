Beyond trees: the metric geometry of subsets of weighted Hamming cubes
Ian Doust, Anthony Weston

Abstract
. Associated to any finite metric space are a large number of objects and quantities which provide some degree of structural or geometric information about the space. In this paper we show that in the setting of subsets of weighted Hamming cubes there are unexpected relationships between many of these quantities. We obtain in particular formulas for the determinant of the distance matrix, the M M -constant and the cofactor sum for such spaces. In general, these types of results offer valuable insights into the combinatorial optimization of certain constrained quadratic forms on finite metric spaces. A key focus in this context are embedding properties of negative type metrics, which play a prominent role in addressing important questions like the sparsest cut problem in graph theory. The current work extends previous results for unweighted metric trees, and more generally, for subsets of standard Hamming cubes, as well as results for weighted metric trees. Finally we consider polygonal equalities in these spaces, giving a complete description of the nontrivial 1 1 -polygonal equalities that can arise in weighted Hamming cubes.

1.
Introduction

It is now over half a century since Graham and Pollak’s surprising discovery
[
15
]
that the determinant of the distance matrix for an unweighted metric tree on
n+1
vertices is always
(-1)^{n}n2^{n-1}
, independent of the actual structure of the tree. Since then many authors have explored how this fact might be generalized. One natural direction is to look at what happens if one adds weights to the edges of the tree. On the other hand, since every unweighted tree can be isometrically embedded in the vertices of a Hamming cube with the Hamming metric, one might also look at more general subsets of such
cubes. This current paper combines and extends the work in
both of these directions. In a completely new line of enquiry,
we examine and classify the nontrivial
1
-polygonal
equalities that can arise in weighted Hamming cubes.
We then use this classification to provide a surprising
link between the rank of the distance matrix and the
linear algebraic properties of subsets of these spaces.

Apart from the determinant of the distance matrix
D_{T}
,
a number of other quantities have been found to dependent only on
n
, the number of edges of such a tree, and not on the tree structure.

(i)

In
[
7

it was shown
that the sum of the entries in the inverse of

is

\frac{2}{n}
.

(ii)

Graham, Hoffman and Hosoya
[

]
showed that the cofactor sum

equals

.

(iii)

Doust and Wolf
[

showed that the

-constant of the tree is always
\frac{n}{2}

Some of these quantities are closely connected to the metric geometry of these spaces, particularly the embedding notions
of negative type and generalized roundness.
Underlying many of these ideas is what Linial
[
21
]
termed
the geometrization of combinatorics; the idea that information
about combinatorial objects can be gleaned from looking at them
from a geometric perspective.
In Section

known extensions of the above results (i) – (iii).

The setting for the current paper is what we shall call weighted Hamming cubes.
Given a list of positive weights

It is easy to verify that any

-point weighted tree can be isometrically embedded (algorithmically) in such a cube and that the embedded points form an affinely independent subset of

A major component in developing the theory here will be the following extension of results of Doust and Weston
[

]
,

Suppose that

In Section

in weighted Hamming cubes.

Kelleher et al.

Our proof of this equivalence depends, in an essential way, on

We then proceed to give a complete description of the nontrivial

2.
Background and preliminary
observations

Given a finite metric space
(X,d)
with elements
x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
we shall denote the

distance matrix
of
X
by
D_{X}=(d(x_{i},x_{j}))_{i,j}
. Given
p>0
, we shall let
D_{X,p}
, or simply
D_{p}
, denote the
p
-distance matrix
of
X
,

D_{p}=(d(x_{i},x_{i})^{p})_{i,j}
.
Although the concepts which we shall now introduce are applicable for general metric spaces, to simplify the definitions and exposition, we shall restrict ourselves to the setting of finite metric spaces. Further, we shall always assume that the spaces are nontrivial, in the sense that they have at least two elements.

The usual inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
will be denoted
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.
Throughout we shall use
\boldsymbol{1}
to denote a vector of an appropriate size whose entries are all
1
. Thus, if
A
is any
n\times n
matrix, the sum of the entries of
A
can be written as
\langle A\boldsymbol{1},\boldsymbol{1}\rangle
. The
cofactor sum
of
A
is the quantity

{\mathop{\mathrm{cof}}}(A)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(-1)^{i+j}\det(A_{i|j})

where
A_{i|j}
is the submatrix of
A
obtained by deleting the
i
th row and the
j
th column.

2.1.
p
-negative type and generalized roundness

The concept of
p
-negative type has its roots in embedding questions.

Let
F_{0}
denote the hyperplane of appropriate dimension of all vectors
\boldsymbol{\xi}
such that
\langle\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{1}\rangle=0
; that is, the set of all column vectors whose components sum to zero.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(X,d)
be a finite metric space with
m+1
points
\{x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\}

and suppose that
p\geq 0
.
We say that
(X,d)
is of
p
-negative type
if

(2.1)

\langle D_{p}\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle=\sum_{i,j}d(x_{i},x_{j})^{p}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\leq 0

for all
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi_{0},\dots,\xi_{m})^{T}\in F_{0}
.

If strict inequality holds in (
2.1
) except when
\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{0}
we say that
(X,d)
is of
strict
p
-negative type
.

The classical result of Schoenberg
[
28
]
is that
(X,d)
may be isometrically embedded in a Hilbert space if and only if it is of
2
-negative type. Spaces of
1
-negative type, often simply referred to as spaces of negative type (or quasihypermetric spaces; see
[
24
]
), have arisen is many areas. An important example is in the study of the sparsest cut problem in graph theory. See, for instance,
[
5
,
1
]
.

Of particular importance in the current study are the following facts due to
Schoenberg
[
28
]
and Hjorth et al.
[
17
]
,
respectively.

(i)

Every subset of
\ell^{1}
is of
1
-negative type.

(ii)

Every finite metric tree is of strict

1
-negative type.

Doust and Weston
[
8
]
introduced an enhanced measure of the
p
-negative type inequality (
2.1
). The

p
-negative type gap
for a finite metric space

(X,d)
of
p
-negative type
is the largest non-negative constant
\Gamma_{p}(X)
such that

(2.2)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{p}(X)\left\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\|_{1}^{2}+2\langle D_{p}\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 0

for all
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in F_{0}
. The main result of
[
8
]
was that if
(X,d)
is an
(m+1)
-point metric tree with edge weights
[w_{1},\dots,w_{m}]
, then

(2.3)

\Gamma_{1}(X)=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}^{-1}\right)^{-1},

which again is independent of the structure of the
tree and independent of which weight is assigned to which edge.

The concept of generalized roundness was introduced by Enflo

[
12
]
in the 1960s as a tool to study universal uniform
embedding spaces.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and suppose that
p\geq 0
. We say that
(X,d)
has
generalized roundness
p
if

(2.4)

\sum_{i<j}d(a_{i},a_{j})^{p}+\sum_{i<j}d(b_{i},b_{j})^{p}\leq\sum_{i,j}d(a_{i},b_{j})^{p}

for all finite lists of elements
[a_{1},\dots,a_{N}]
and
[b_{1},\dots,b_{N}]
of
X
.

It is worth stressing that the elements of the lists in this definition do not need to be distinct. One can however assume that the two lists are disjoint.

It was only much later that Lennard, Tonge and Weston
[
19
]
showed that the notions of
p
-negative type and generalized
roundness
p
are in fact equivalent in any metric space.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and suppose

p\geq 0
. Then:

(i)

(X,d)
is of
p
-negative type if and only if
(X,d)
has generalized roundness
p
.

(ii)

The set of all values
p
for which
(X,d)
has
generalized roundness
p
is always an interval of the form

[0,\wp]
, with
\wp\geq 0
, or
[0,\infty)
.

It is important to note in the statement of Theorem

2.3
(ii) that it is possible that
\wp=0
. In
other words, there exist (necessarily infinite) metric spaces
that do not have generalized roundness
p
for any
p>0
.
The first such example was constructed by Enflo
[
12
]
.

Given a metric space
(X,d)
we let

\wp(X,d)=\sup\{p\thinspace:\thinspace\text{$(X,d)$ has generalized roundness $p$}\}.

In settings where the metric on
X
is entirely clear, we may
write
\wp(X)
instead of
\wp(X,d)
.
By Theorem
2.3
(ii), if
\wp(X,d)
is finite, then
the underlying supremum is clearly attained.
Depending on the context,
\wp(X,d)
is known as either the
maximal generalized roundness
, or the
supremal

p
-negative type
of
(X,d)
. Sánchez
[
27
]
, following work of Wolf
[
30
]
, gave an important characterization of
\wp(X,d)
which links this value to two other quantities which will appear in this study.

Theorem 2.4
.

Suppose that
(X,d)
is a finite metric space and that
\wp(X,d)
is finite. Then

\wp(X,d)=\min\{p\geq 0\thinspace:\thinspace\det(D_{p})=0\text{ {\rm or} }\langle D_{p}^{-1}\boldsymbol{1},\boldsymbol{1}\rangle=0\}.

In relation to Theorem
2.4
we note
that for any
metric space
(X,d)
, finite or otherwise,

\wp(X,d)=\infty
if and only if
d
is an ultrametric. This result
is due to Faver et al.
[
13
]
.

Li and Weston
[
20
]
showed that for any finite metric space,

(2.5)

\{p\thinspace:\thinspace\text{$(X,d)$ is of strict $p$-negative type}\}=[0,\wp(X,d)).

It follows immediately from Sánchez’s formula that if
(X,d)
is a finite metric space of strict
1
-negative type, then
D_{X}
is invertible. In general, the converse implication fails.

Example 2.5
.

Let
(X,d)
be a 5 point metric space with distance matrix

D_{X}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\alpha&\alpha&1&1\\
\alpha&0&\alpha&1&1\\
\alpha&\alpha&0&1&1\\
1&1&1&0&\alpha\\
1&1&1&\alpha&0\end{pmatrix}

with
\alpha=12/7
.
A calculation shows that

\det(D_{p})=6\alpha^{3p}-2\alpha^{5p}.

This has a first positive zero at

p_{0}=\frac{\log 3}{2\log\alpha}>1.

On the other hand,

\langle D_{p}^{-1}\boldsymbol{1},\boldsymbol{1}\rangle=\frac{7\alpha^{p}-12}{2\big(\alpha^{2p}-3\bigr)},

which clearly has a single zero at
p_{1}=1
. Thus
(X,d)
has supremal
p
-negative type
1
(and hence is not is of strict
1
-negative type), but

D_{X}
is invertible.

However, as we shall see in the next subsection,
this behaviour cannot occur
in the setting of subsets of
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1})
.

2.2.
The
M
-constant

The
M
-constant of a compact metric space was introduced by Alexander and Stolarsky
[
2
]
, and further investigated by Nickolas and Wolf (see
[
24
]
), who showed that it contains useful information about embedding of the space into Euclidean space.
If
(X,d)=(\{x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\},d)
is a
finite metric space with distance matrix
D_{X}
, then

M(X)=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in F_{1}}\langle D_{X}\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\in(0,\infty],

where
F_{1}=\{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}\thinspace:\thinspace\langle\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{1}\rangle=1\}
.

We recall here a number of results which link this quantity to the concept of strict
1
-negative type and to properties of the distance matrix
D_{X}
.

Proposition 2.6
.

[
10
, Theorem 3.2]

Suppose that
(X,d)
is an
(m+1)
-point metric space of
1
-negative type with distance matrix
D_{X}
. Then there exists
{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
such that
D_{X}{\boldsymbol{b}}=\boldsymbol{1}
and
\langle{\boldsymbol{b}},\boldsymbol{1}\rangle\geq 0
. Further

(i)

The value of
\langle{\boldsymbol{b}},\boldsymbol{1}\rangle
is independent of
{\boldsymbol{b}}
.

(ii)

M(X)<\infty
if and only if
\langle{\boldsymbol{b}},\boldsymbol{1}\rangle>0
. In this case

M(X)=\langle{\boldsymbol{b}},\boldsymbol{1}\rangle^{-1}
.

Proposition 2.7
.

[
10
, Theorem 3.3]

Suppose that
(X,d)
is a finite metric space of strict
1
-negative type with distance matrix
D_{X}
. Then

(i)

D_{X}
is invertible.

(ii)

M(X)<\infty
.

(iii)

\displaystyle M(X)=\frac{1}{\langle D_{X}^{-1}\boldsymbol{1},\boldsymbol{1}\rangle}
.

In general
M(X)
can be infinite, but as observed by Nickolas and Wolf
[
23
]
, this can not happen for finite subsets of
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1})
.

Proposition 2.8
.

[
23
, Theorem 4.7]

If
X
is an
m+1
point subset of
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1})
,
then
M(X)\leq\frac{m+1}{4}\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}(X)
.

Theorem 2.9
.

Suppose that
(X,d)
is a finite subset of
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1})
with distance matrix
D_{X}
. Then
D_{X}
is invertible if and only if
(X,d)
is of strict

1
-negative type.

Proof.

To prove the nontrivial implication, suppose that
D_{X}
is invertible, but that
(X,d)
is not of strict
1
-negative type.
Since
(X,d)\subseteq(\mathbb{R}^{n},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1})
, it is certainly of
1
-negative type, we must have
\wp(X,d)=1
. Theorem
2.4
then implies that
\langle D_{X}^{-1}\boldsymbol{1},\boldsymbol{1}\rangle=0
.
Taking
{\boldsymbol{b}}=D_{X}^{-1}\boldsymbol{1}
in Proposition
2.6
, this implies that
M(X)=\infty
. But by Proposition
2.8
,
M(X)
is finite, which gives the desired contradiction.

\Box

2.3.
Polygonal equalities

In studying a question of Bernius and Blanchard, Elsner et al.
[
11
]
characterized when one obtains the ‘polygonal equality’

(2.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{i<j}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\right\|_{1}+\sum_{i<j}\left\|{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\right\|_{1}

for finite subsets
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and

\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}

of
L_{1}(\Omega,\mu)
.

Motivated by this and some of the equivalent formulations of generalized roundness,
Kelleher et al.
[
18
, Definition 3.20]
introduced the
concept of a nontrivial
p
-polygonal equality in a
general metric space.

Definition 2.11
.

Suppose that
(X,d)
is a metric space and that
p\geq 0
. A
p
-polygonal equality
in
(X,d)
is an equality of the form

(2.7)

\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{j=1}^{t}m_{i}n_{j}d(x_{i},y_{j})^{p}=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}\leq s}m_{i_{1}}m_{i_{2}}d(x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}})^{p}+\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\leq t}n_{j_{1}}n_{j_{2}}d(y_{j_{1}},y_{j_{2}})^{p}

for some (not necessarily distinct)
x_{1},\dots,x_{s},y_{1},\dots,y_{t}\in X
and real weights
m_{1},\dots,m_{s}
,
n_{1},\dots,n_{t}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{s}m_{i}=\sum_{i=j}^{t}n_{j}
.

In order for this concept to be useful, one needs to eliminate ‘trivial’ examples of such equalities. For the purpose of considering condition (7) in Theorem
1.1
it is not necessary that we give the original definition of a

nontrivial
p
-polygonal equality
.
(The interested reader is referred to
[
18
, Definition 3.20]

or
[
9
, Theorem 2.3]
) for details.) Rather we shall work with the
following characterization which applies in the category
of finite metric spaces.

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
(X,d)
be a finite metric space and suppose that
p\geq 0
. Then the following are equivalent.

(i)

(X,d)
admits a nontrivial
p
-polygonal equality.

(ii)

\langle D_{p}\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle=0
for some nonzero
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in F_{0}
.

Remark 2.13
.

Every nontrivial
p
-polygonal equality in the sense of

[
18
, Definition 3.20]
can be algorithmically
reduced to an identity of the form
\langle D_{X,p}\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle=0

and this may therefore be considered the standard form for
the polygonal equality. It is sufficient therefore in order
to understand all of the nontrivial
p
-polygonal equalities
in a space to provide a description of all such identities

\langle D_{X,p}\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle=0
which are admitted in the space.

An implicit consideration of nontrivial
p
-polygonal
equalities (without naming them) was already prominent
in Li and Weston
[
20
]
. Subsequently, Weston
[
29
]

classified the nontrivial
p
-polygonal equalities in certain
subsets of
L_{p}
-spaces and used this to show that any
two-valued Schauder basis of
L_{p}
must have strict

p
-negative type.

Building off of work in
[
18
]
, the authors have
recently shown that conditions (3) and (7) of Theorem

1.1
are equivalent in all metric spaces.

Theorem 2.14
.

[
9
, Theorem 1.3]

Suppose that
(X,d)
is a metric space. Then
(X,d)
is of strict
p
-negative type if and only if
(X,d)
does not admit any nontrivial
p
-polygonal equalities.

From (
2.5
) we immediately get that if
(X,d)
is a finite metric space with
\wp(X)
finite, then

\{p>0\thinspace:\thinspace\text{$(X,d)$ admits a nontrivial $p$-polygonal equality}\}=[\wp(X),\infty).

It is worth noting that if
(X,d)
is a finite subset of
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1})
with
\wp(X)=1
then it is easy to find nontrivial
1
-polygonal equalities. In this case
(X,d)
is not of strict
1
-negative type, so by Theorem
2.9
,
D_{X}
is not invertible. Sánchez
[
27
, Corollary 2.5]
showed that then
\ker(D_{X})\subseteq F_{0}
and so any nonzero
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\ker(D_{X})
gives a nontrivial
1
-polygonal equality.

2.4.
Known results for weighted trees

The literature contains several formulas for the quantities
\det(D_{X})

and
{\mathop{\mathrm{cof}}}(D_{X})
for weighted trees, extending the results noted in the introduction.

Bapat, Kirkland and Neumann
[
3
]
extended Graham and Pollak’s result on the determinant of the distance matrix, giving a formula which is again independent of the structure of the tree. Related work can be found in
[
31
]
.

Theorem 2.15
.

[
3
, Corollary 2.5]

Suppose that
T
is an
(n+1)
-point metric tree with edge weights
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}
. Then

(2.8)

3.
Weighted and unweighted Hamming cubes

Let
H_{n}
denote the usual (unweighted) Hamming cube,
H_{n}=\{0,1\}^{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, equipped with the
\ell^{1}
metric. Given a weight list
\mathfrak{W}=[w_{1},\dots,w_{n}]
, the diagonal matrix
W=\mathrm{diag}(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})
acts as an invertible map from
H_{n}
onto
H_{\mathfrak{W}}
. Given
X\subseteq H_{\mathfrak{W}}
, let
{\hat{X}}=W^{-1}X
be the inverse image of
X
in
H_{n}
. Clearly
{\hat{X}}
is affinely independent if and only if
X
is.

Any
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in H_{\mathfrak{W}}
can be written as

{\boldsymbol{x}}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}w_{k}\,b_{k}\,{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}

where
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}
is the
k
th standard basis vector and
b_{k}\in\{0,1\}
. We shall denote the inverse image of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
under
W
by
{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}
. That is,
{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}b_{k}\,{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}\in H_{n}
.

It is worth observing that for all
k
, the reflection map under which
w_{k}\,b_{k}\,{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}
, the
k
th coordinate of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
, is replaced by
w_{k}\,(1-b_{k})\,{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}
is an isometry of
H_{\mathfrak{W}}
. In particular applying one or more of these reflections does not affect the distance matrix of a subset
X\subseteq H_{\mathfrak{W}}
, nor whether the set is affinely independent. We may therefore, when convenient, assume that any subset
X=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},\dots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{m}\}\subseteq H_{\mathfrak{W}}
has
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}=\boldsymbol{0}
.

In what follows we shall make use of a weighted inner product. If
{\boldsymbol{x}}=\sum_{k}w_{k}\,b_{k}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}
and
{\boldsymbol{y}}=\sum_{k}w_{k}\,b^{\prime}_{k}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}
, then we define

\langle{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle_{W}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}w_{k}\,b_{k}\,b_{k}^{\prime}.

For all
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in H_{\mathfrak{W}}
we have
\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{1}=\langle{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle_{W}
.

Our first step is to show that
D_{X}
is invertible if and only if
D_{{\hat{X}}}
is invertible.

Suppose then that
X=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}=\boldsymbol{0},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{m}\}\subseteq H_{\mathfrak{W}}
. Let
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{X}=(\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}\right\|_{1},\dots,\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{m}\right\|_{1})^{T}
be the vector of norms of the nonzero elements of
X
. The above inner product determines a Gram matrix for
X
:

G_{W}=\bigl(\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\rangle_{W}\bigr)_{i,j=1}^{m}.

Lemma 2.1 from
[
7
]
extends to the weighted setting.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
X=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}=\boldsymbol{0},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{m}\}
be a subset of
H_{\mathfrak{W}}
. Then

\det(D_{X})=(-1)^{m-1}2^{m-1}\det\begin{pmatrix}0&{\boldsymbol{u}}_{X}^{T}\\
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{X}&G_{W}\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

Beginning with the distance matrix
D_{X}
, perform the elementary rows operations of first subtracting the first row from each of the other rows, and then subtracting the first column of that matrix from each of the other columns.

If we write
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}w_{k}\,b_{i,k}\,{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}
, then a small calculation shows that the
(i,j)
th entry of the resulting matrix
D_{1}
is

\begin{cases}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i-1}\right\|_{1},&\text{if $j=0$},\\
\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j-1}\right\|_{1},&\text{if $i=0$},\\
\sum_{k=1}^{n}w_{k}\bigl(|b_{i-1,k}-b_{j-1,k}|-b_{i-1,k}-b_{j-1,k}\bigr),&\text{if $1\leq i,j\leq m$.}\end{cases}

Since each
b_{i,k}\in\{0,1\}
one can readily verify that

w_{k}\bigl(|b_{i-1,k}-b_{j-1,k}|-b_{i-1,k}-b_{j-1,k}\bigr)=-2w_{k}\,b_{i-1,k}\,b_{j-1,k}

and so

\sum_{k=1}^{n}w_{k}\bigl(|b_{i-1,k}-b_{j-1,k}|-b_{i-1,k}-b_{j-1,k}\bigr)=-2\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i-1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j-1}\rangle_{W}.

Thus

D_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&{\boldsymbol{u}}_{X}^{T}\\
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{X}&-2\,G_{W}\end{pmatrix}

and so

\det(D_{X})=\det(D_{1})=(-2)^{m-1}\det\begin{pmatrix}0&{\boldsymbol{u}}_{X}^{T}\\
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{X}&G_{W}\end{pmatrix}.

(X,d)=(\{x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\},d)

(6.1)

\displaystyle\langle D_{X}\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle

\displaystyle=-2\Bigl\|\sum_{i}\xi_{i}\iota(x_{i})\Bigr\|_{2}^{2}.

Proof.

Let

\langle D_{X}\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle=\langle D_{\iota(X),2}\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle

Theorem 6.5
.

Suppose that
(X=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},\dots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{m}\},d)
is a metric subspace of
H_{\mathfrak{W}}
. Then the following conditions are equivalent for each
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in F_{0}
.

(i)

\sum_{k=0}^{m}\xi_{k}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{0}
.

(ii)

\langle D_{X}\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle=0
.

(iii)

D_{X}\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{0}
.

Proof.

If we let
\iota
denote the natural
S
-embedding of
H_{\mathfrak{W}}

into
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
D_{X}=D_{\iota(X),2}
.
Furthermore, as a map from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\iota
is linear and invertible. Now let
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in F_{0}

be given and apply Lemma
6.1
to conclude that

\langle D_{X}\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle=\langle D_{\iota(X),2}\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle=-2\Bigl\|\sum\limits_{i}\xi_{i}\iota({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\Bigr\|_{2}^{2}=-2\Bigl\|\iota\biggl(\sum\limits_{i}\xi_{i}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\biggr)\Bigr\|_{2}^{2}.

Thus
\langle D_{X}\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle=0
if and only if

\sum\limits_{i}\xi_{i}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}=0
.

Suppose now that
\sum\limits_{i}\xi_{i}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}=0
. For
0\leq i\leq m
and
1\leq k\leq n
let
x_{i,k}=w_{k}b_{i,k}
denote the
k
th component of
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
. Then, for
1\leq k\leq n

\sum_{j=0}^{m}x_{j,k}\,\xi_{j}=\sum_{j=0}^{m}w_{k}\,b_{j,k}\,\xi_{j}=0.

Using the properties of binary digits as in the proof of Theorem
3.4
, we see that if
0\leq i\leq m
,
the
i
th component of
D_{X}\boldsymbol{\xi}
is given by

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\right\|_{1}\xi_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}w_{k}|b_{i,k}-b_{j,k}|\,\xi_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m}\left(\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}w_{k}\big(b_{i,k}+b_{j,k}-2b_{i,k}b_{j,k}\big)\right)\xi_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m}\xi_{j}\left(\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}w_{k}b_{i,k}\right)+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\left(\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m}w_{k}\,b_{j,k}\,\xi_{j}\right)-2\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\left(b_{i,k}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m}w_{k}\,b_{j,k}\,\xi_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

As this is true for all
i
we conclude
that
D_{X}\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{0}
.

Clearly if
D_{X}\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{0}
then
\langle D_{X}\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle=0
so this completes the proof.

Corollary 6.6
.

Suppose that
(X,d)
is a
metric subspace of
H_{\mathfrak{W}}
. Then

\ker(D_{X})=\mathcal{F}_{0}(X)
.

Proof.

By Theorem
6.5
, we have
\mathcal{F}_{0}(X)\subseteq\ker(D_{X})
. Since obviously

\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{0}\in\mathcal{F}_{0}(X)
, it follows
that
\ker(D_{X})=\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
if and only if

\mathcal{F}_{0}(X)=\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
.

Now suppose that
\ker(D_{X})
is nontrivial.
In other words, assume that
\det(D_{X})=0
.
Then
X
is affinely dependent by Theorem

3.4
. This entails that
\wp(X)=1

by Theorem
4.1
.
Hence
\ker(D_{X})\subseteq F_{0}
by Sánchez

[
27
, Corollary 2.5 (i)]
. In particular,
each
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\ker(D_{X})
satisfies

\sum_{k=0}^{m}\xi_{k}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{0}
by Theorem

3.4
. So, in fact,

\ker(D_{X})\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{0}(X)
.
This completes the proof.

Remark 6.7
.

In the setting of Corollary
6.6
, if
\wp(X)=1
,
then
\det(D_{X})=0
(and so
\ker(D_{X})

(in that order). In particular, each nonzero

\boldsymbol{\xi}\not\in F_{0}

Example 6.8
.

Let the set

X=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{3}\}=\left\{\binom{0}{0},\binom{1}{0},\binom{0}{2},\binom{1}{2}\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}

be endowed with the
\ell^{1}
metric.
To see that one can’t drop the

\boldsymbol{\xi}\in F_{0}

\boldsymbol{\xi}=(1,0,0,0)^{T}

\sum_{k}\xi_{k}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{0}

\boldsymbol{\xi}=(1,-1,0,1)^{T}

and

Example 6.9
.

Theorem
6.5
is dependent on the structure of

and does not extend to more general subsets of

.
To see this let the set

be endowed with the

The kernel of

is
\mathrm{span}((1,-1,-1,1,0)^{T})\subseteq F_{0}

\boldsymbol{\xi}=(2,-2,-1,0,1)^{T}

for which

and
\langle D_{X}\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\neq 0
.

Example 6.10
.

If
p>1
the set of
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in F_{0}
for which
\langle D_{p}\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle=0
is in general not a linear subspace. Let
X
be the star graph on 4 vertices,

X=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\\
0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\right\}\subseteq H_{3}.

It is known that
\wp(X)=\frac{\ln 3}{\ln 2}\approx 1.585
. Taking
p=2>\wp(X)
one finds that
\langle D_{2}\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle=0
if and only if

\boldsymbol{\xi}=(-2s-2t\pm 2\sqrt{st},s,t,s+t\mp 2\sqrt{st})^{T}

with

, which does not form a subspace of

\mathbb{R}^{4}
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
, we saw that
D_{X}
is invertible if and only if

is affinely independent. This can be rephrased as saying that
{\mathop{\mathrm{rank}}}(D_{X})=m+1

Theorem 6.11
.

Suppose that
X=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{m}\}\subseteq H_{\mathfrak{W}}
with the usual
\ell^{1}
metric. Then

(6.2)

\mathrm{rank}(D_{X})=\mathrm{dim}\left(\mathrm{span}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},\dots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{m}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})\right)+1.

Proof.

Both sides of (
6.2
) are invariant under the reflection maps considered in Section
3
, so without loss of generality we may assume that
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}=\boldsymbol{0}
.
Let
A
be the
n\times(m+1)
matrix whose
j
th column is
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}
so that the right-hand side of (
6.2
) is
{\mathop{\mathrm{rank}}}(A)+1
.
Since
D_{X}
and
A
both have
m+1
columns, it will suffice then to show that
{\mathop{\mathrm{nullity}}}(D_{X})+1={\mathop{\mathrm{nullity}}}(A)
. Since the first standard basis vector
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}
lies in
\ker(A)
we can write
\ker(A)=\mathrm{span}({\boldsymbol{e}}_{1})\oplus\mathcal{N}
where
\mathcal{N}=\{\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi_{0},\dots,\xi_{m})^{T}\in\ker(A)\thinspace:\thinspace\xi_{0}=0\}
.

For
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\ker(D_{X})
let
U\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{\xi}-\langle\boldsymbol{\xi},{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}\rangle{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}=(0,\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{m})^{T}
. Then, by
Theorem
6.5
,

AU\boldsymbol{\xi}=A\boldsymbol{\xi}-\langle\boldsymbol{\xi},{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}\rangle A{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}=\boldsymbol{0}

and so
U\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{N}
. Conversely, for
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{N}
let
V\boldsymbol{\xi}=\left(-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}\right){\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\xi}=\bigl(-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i},\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{m}\bigr)^{T}
. Then

V\boldsymbol{\xi}\in F_{0}
and

AV\boldsymbol{\xi}=\left(-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}\right)A{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}-A\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{0}

so applying Theorem
6.5
again we see that
D_{X}V\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{0}
.

It is easy to check that
VU
and
UV
are just the identity maps on
\ker(D_{X})
and
\mathcal{N}
and so these two vector spaces are isomorphic. Thus
{\mathop{\mathrm{nullity}}}(A)=1+\dim(\mathcal{N})=1+{\mathop{\mathrm{nullity}}}(D_{X})
, as required.

7.
Final remarks and open problems

We have seen that many of the implications included in Theorem
1.1
are in fact valid in greater generality than the setting of weighted Hamming cubes, so it is natural to ask whether one might be able to relax the hypothesis in that theorem.
One might ask, for example, whether the theorem is valid for finite subsets of
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1})
.

Noting that conditions (3), (4), (5), (6) and (7) are equivalent in any finite metric spaces, our aim in this section is to clarify the relationships between conditions (1), (2), (3) in wider classes of spaces than weighted Hamming cubes, namely, finite subsets of
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1})
, finite spaces of
1
-negative type, and general finite metric spaces. We shall examine here each of the 3 pairs of conditions. (Of course, Condition (1) only makes sense in the first of these wider classes.)

7.1.
(2) invertibility of
D_{X}
and (3) strict
1
-negative type

We saw in Theorem
2.9
that these two conditions are equivalent for finite subsets of
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1})
. Indeed it follows from Sánchez’s formula that (3) implies (2) in any finite metric space. On the other hand, Example
2.5
shows that there are spaces of
1
-negative type in which Condition (2) does not imply Condition (3).

7.2.
(1) Affine independence and (3) strict
1
-negative type

That Condition (1) implies Condition (3) for finite subsets of
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1})
is
[
18
, Corollary 4.10]
. To see that the converse implication fails for such spaces, consider

X=\left\{\binom{0}{0},\binom{1}{0},\binom{1}{2},\binom{2}{2}\right\}\subseteq(\mathbb{R}^{2},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1}).

Then
X
is certainly affinely dependent but

D_{X}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&3&4\\
1&0&2&3\\
3&2&0&1\\
4&3&1&0\end{pmatrix}

which is invertible. Further,
\langle D_{X}^{-1}\boldsymbol{1},\boldsymbol{1}\rangle=\frac{506}{4727}>0
. Noting that
(X,d)
is of
1
-negative type,
by Sánchez’s formula, we see that the supremal
p
-negative type can’t be
1
, and so
(X,d)
is of strict
1
-negative type. That is, this space satisfies Condition (3), but not
Condition (1).

7.3.
(1) Affine independence and (2) invertibility of
D_{X}

Since Conditions (2) and (3) are equivalent for finite subsets of
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1})
it follows from the statements in Subsection
7.2
that Condition (1) implies Condition (2) in this setting, but that that converse implication fails.

There remain a few questions which the authors have been unable to answer. If
(X,d)
is an
(n+1)
-point metric tree with integer edge weights
\mathfrak{W}=[w_{1},\dots,w_{n}]
, then (from
2.3
) the
1
-negative type gap satisfies

\frac{\prod_{k=1}^{n}w_{k}}{\Gamma_{1}(X)}=\Bigl(\prod_{k=1}^{n}w_{k}\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{n}w_{k}^{-1}\Bigr)

which is always an integer whose value clearly only depends on the weights and not the structure of the tree. The right-hand side of this equation is half the sum of the
(n-1)
-dimensional volumes of the faces of
H_{\mathfrak{W}}
and so

\frac{\Gamma_{1}(X)}{2}=\frac{\text{Volume of $H_{\mathfrak{W}}$}}{\text{Volume of faces of $H_{\mathfrak{W}}$}}.

Numerical experiments indicate that if
X
is an affinely independent
(n+1)
-point subset of
H_{\mathfrak{W}}
and the weights are positive integers then (using the notation from Section
3
)

\frac{\bigl(\prod_{k=1}^{n}w_{k}\bigr)\det({\hat{B}})^{2}}{\Gamma_{1}(X)}

is also always an integer (and one needs the determinant factor for this to be true). It would be interesting to attach some meaning to this quantity and thereby obtain a geometric formula for the
1
-negative type gap in this setting.

In Section
2
we recalled that if
(X,d)
is
a finite metric space with
\wp(X)
finite, then

(7.1)

\displaystyle\{p>0\thinspace:\thinspace\text{$(X,d)$ admits a nontrivial $p$-polygonal equality}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[\wp(X),\infty).

In Section
6
we showed that a subset
X
of
a weighted Hamming cube
H_{\mathfrak{W}}

admits a nontrivial
1
-polygonal equality
if and only if
X
is affinely dependent. For each
p>1
it
remains an open problem to characterize the subsets of
H_{\mathfrak{W}}

that admit a nontrivial
p
-polygonal equality.
By
7.1
and Theorem
6.5
,
if
p>1
and if
X\subseteq H_{\mathfrak{W}}
is
affinely dependent, then
X
admits a nontrivial
p
-polygonal
equality. The converse will also be true if
p>1
is sufficiently
close to
1
. This is simply because
H_{\mathfrak{W}}
has only finitely
many
subsets. There will also exist a smallest
p\in(1,\infty)

such that every non-ultrametric metric subspace of
H_{\mathfrak{W}}

admits a nontrivial
p
-polygonal equality. Necessarily,
this value of
p
will equal
\wp(X)
for some set

X\subseteq H_{\mathfrak{W}}
.

More generally, if we let
\mathcal{X}_{p}
denote the set
of subsets of
H_{\mathfrak{W}}
that admit a nontrivial
p
-polygonal
equality, there will exist finitely many real numbers

1=p_{0}<p_{1}<\dots<p_{l}
such that

\mathcal{X}_{p_{0}},\ldots,\mathcal{X}_{p_{l}}
are
pairwise distinct sets and

\mathcal{X}_{p}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{X}_{p_{k-1}}&\text{ if }p\in[p_{k-1},p_{k})\\
\mathcal{X}_{p_{l}}&\text{ if }p\in[p_{l},\infty).\end{cases}

By
7.1
, we will have

\mathcal{X}_{p_{0}}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{p_{1}}\subset\ldots\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{p_{l}}
, with all inclusions strict.
The determination of the real numbers
p_{1},\ldots,p_{l}

and characterization of the corresponding sets

\mathcal{X}_{p_{1}},\ldots,\mathcal{X}_{p_{l}}

are open problems, the resolution of which would provide
deep insights into the metric geometry of weighted Hamming
cubes.
