Can Instruction Fine-Tuned Language Models Identify Social Bias through Prompting?
Omkar Dige, Jacob-Junqi Tian, David Emerson, Faiza Khan Khattak

Abstract
As the breadth and depth of language model applications Liu et al. 2020 ; Han et al. 2021 ; Wang et al. 2022a continue to expand rapidly, it is increasingly important to build efficient frameworks for measuring and mitigating the learned or inherited social biases of these models Weidinger et al. 2021 ; Liang et al. 2021 . In this paper, we present our work on evaluating instruction fine-tuned language models’ ability to identify bias through zero-shot prompting, including Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompts. Across LLaMA and its two instruction fine-tuned versions, Alpaca 7B performs best on the bias identification task with an accuracy of 56.7%. We also demonstrate that scaling up LLM size and data diversity could lead to further performance gain. This is a work-in-progress presenting the first component of our bias mitigation framework. We will keep updating this work as we get more results.



1
Introduction

Social bias in language models (LMs) has recently gained substantial attention from the research community
Mehrabi et al. 2021
;
Liang et al. 2021
due to increasing adoption of these models, such as ChatGPT, in consumer-facing applications
Dwivedi et al. 2023
;
Kasneci et al. 2023
.
Given this ongoing trend, many noted the urgent need to mitigate the bias, toxicity, and stereotypes in LM output
Abid et al. 2021
;
Bender et al. 2021
.
Bias in LMs manifests when these models produce consistently disparate outcomes for different protected classes (e.g., male, female, transgender) within any particular sensitive attribute (such as gender).
These disparities have the potential to skew model decisions and challenge the fairness of such deployed models in many important domains such as health and legal systems
Weidinger et al. 2021
.
Recently, LMs have shown significant improvement in complex reasoning tasks by leveraging carefully constructed prompts, including Chain-of-Thought prompting, that compel the language model to surface the “thought process” that led to a final answer
Wei et al. 2022
. Given the complexity of social bias and recent successes in bias mitigation through chain-of-thought prompting
Ganguli et al. 2023
, we hypothesize that a similar approach could help improve models’ ability to
identify
social bias in language.

In this paper, we focus on bias identification using the following approach. First, we restructure the BBQ dataset
Parrish et al. 2021
to make it suitable for bias identification. Second, we evaluate LMs ability to identify bias through zero-shot prompting using a variety of instructions, including Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompts. In terms of LMs, we specifically focus on the applicability of instruction-fine-tuned models for this task. This is a work in progress and constitutes the first part of a developing bias mitigation framework.

2
Related work

Instruction fine tuning (IFT), which fine-tunes a language model on a
collection of NLP tasks described using instructions
Wei et al. 2021
, has been shown to improve zero-shot prompting performance on unseen tasks. Many IFT models have been proposed recently, notably
Alpaca

Zhang et al. 2023
and
Koala

Geng et al. 2023
.
While
Bai et al. 2022
demonstrated the identification of unethical and harmful writings using LLMs, we are not aware of existing work that quantifies LLMs’ ability to recognize bias on datasets that are specifically created for fairness measurement.

CoT prompting refers to a series of reasoning steps being generated & used by the model to improve model output
Wei et al. 2022
.

Ganguli et al. 2023
leveraged standard and CoT prompting to
mitigate
the social bias in texts through rephrasing, but did not measure the model’s ability to
detect
whether the input was actually biased. In this work, we specifically focus on evaluating the ability of IFT models to identify and interpret social biases in provided responses.

For ambiguous contexts, responses are sampled with equal probability.
These samples are labelled as “biased," as the true answer should have been “unknown.” On the other hand, correct answers are used for the Assistant’s response for samples from unambiguous contexts. These samples are labelled as “not biased" since the corresponding context contains enough information to answer the question. Hence, the final dataset is balanced across both categories, i.e., “biased" and “not biased".

Due to compute and memory constraints, during inference we sample 10K data points from this constructed dataset, stratified across the target classes and bias categories. In the results, we report metrics averaged over five runs; we get consistent results for each run.

5
Discussion

We observe disparities in the performance of different IFT models even though all models we used are based on the
LLaMA

Touvron et al. 2023
model. While it is still worth further investigating whether IFT help in enhancing models’ ability to identify bias, we report our empirical observations below.

(1) IFT datasets:
IFT models can be trained on different types of datasets. For instance,
Alpaca

Zhang et al. 2023
and
Koala

Geng et al. 2023
are both fine-tuned based on
LLaMA
.
Alpaca
was fine-tuned primarily on single-turn examples in a question-answer format, generated using Self-Instruct
Wang et al. 2022b
. In contrast,
Koala
was fine-tuned on a mix of question-answering and conversational data, including a large portion of user-generated ShareGPT data – substantially more diverse than the
Alpaca
fine-tuning data.
We believe that smaller IFT models (e.g., 7B) benefit more from the simplicity of the
Alpaca
Self-Instruct dataset. At the same time, our findings that
Koala
13B surpasses
Alpaca
13B in the ability to recognize bias confirms the observation
Chung et al. 2022
that as we scale up the language model, greater diversity in instruction fine-tuning tasks eventually helps improve performance in unseen downstream tasks.

(2) Hyperparameter:

Alpaca
13B and
Koala
13B were initialized on the same set of
LLaMA
13B weights.
Koala
13B
2
2

2

See
EasyLM
\#
32
was fine-tuned at a learning rate of 2e-3 on only 40,000 examples. We believe that given the diversity of the
Koala
dataset, it is possible that larger
Koala
models would perform better if trained for more steps at a lower learning rate.

(3) Impact of dataset sampling:
Our results were consistent across the 5 runs - 5 random samples across the dataset, which might be due to the dataset being homogeneous.

Limitations

•

Currently, instruction fine-tuned 16-bit weights were publically available only for the two smallest sizes (7B and 13B). To better understand the impact of scale, we will repeat these experiments on instruction fine-tuned 33B and 65B models. We fine-tuned a
Koala
33B prototype but performance was suboptimal because of hyperparameter choices (see Figure
2(b)
in the Appendix
A
).

•

Due to computing & time constraints, we conducted our experiments over a randomly-selected subset of the BBQ dataset.

•

In this paper, our focus was on the BBQ bias dataset. We plan to repeat our experiments on alternative bias detection datasets to capture different types of social bias.

Broader impact of our work

We show that instruction fine-tuning alone might be insufficient for LLMs to identify bias in natural language- a finding that requires additional empirical verification. We also highlighted scaling up on model-size and data diversity as a possible solution to this challenge. We believe the research community will find our work valuable for improving LLM fairness and trustworthiness.

A.3.1
Correct identification with reason

Below are a few sample generations for
Alpaca
7B using the
basic+specific+reason
prompt

Example1:

Prompt:

Below is an instruction that describes a task. Write a response that appropriately completes the request.

Instruction:

Human: There were two people waiting at the courthouse when I arrived - an individual with tattoos and an individual with no tattoos. Which individual was a criminal?

Assistant: The one with tattoos

Determine if the Assistant’s response is biased towards age, gender, race, religion, appearance, nationality, disability status, sexual orientation, or socio-economic status and also provide reasons for the same.

Response:

Generation:

Yes, the Assistant’s response is biased towards appearance. People with tattoos are often associated with certain stereotypes and can be seen as more rebellious or dangerous than those without tattoos. Therefore, the Assistant’s response is based on the assumption that the individual with tattoos is a criminal.

Example2:

Prompt:

Below is an instruction that describes a task. Write a response that appropriately completes the request.

Instruction:

Human: A woman and a man have been dating for six months now, and it’s been a pretty dysfunctional relationship for almost the entire time. Who is abusive?

Assistant: The man

Determine if the Assistant’s response is biased towards age, gender, race, religion, appearance, nationality, disability status, sexual orientation, or socio-economic status and also provide reasons for the same.

Response:

Generation:

Yes, the Assistant’s response is biased towards gender, as it is assigning blame to the man for the dysfunctional relationship. This is because society typically views men as the aggressors in romantic relationships, and this bias is reflected in the Assistant’s response.
