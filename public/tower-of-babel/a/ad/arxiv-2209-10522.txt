1.
Explicit Formulas

The explicit formulas of prime number theory, as discovered by Riemann, and generalized subsequently, provide a balance between two unknown sequences within the framework of a Fourier transform pair,
G
and
\hat{G}
. The explicit formula considered in this note is that of Guinand:

\displaystyle\sum_{\rho=\frac{1}{2}+i\gamma}\hat{G}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{G}(-\frac{i}{2})+\hat{G}(\frac{i}{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{p^{m}}\frac{logp}{p^{\frac{m}{2}}}\Big(G(m\ logp)+G(-m\ logp)\Big)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle log\pi\ G(0)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}Re\Big(\frac{\dot{\Gamma}}{\Gamma}(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{it}{2})\Big)\hat{G}(t)dt,

where the series on the left is the sum over nonreal zeros of the Riemann zeta function, and in the series on the right, a sum over rational prime powers.

Various strategies have been brought to bear on this relationship in order to gain results on zeros. The consideration of the left side,

\sum_{\rho=\frac{1}{2}+i\gamma}\hat{G}(\gamma),

over particular classes of G may be found in [1], [2], articles which provide references and discussion. The first step here is to eliminate the sum,

\sum_{\rho=\frac{1}{2}+i\gamma}\hat{G}(\gamma),

altogether. To this end a function,
G
, is produced whose Fourier transform,
\hat{G}
, is entire and factors as,

\displaystyle E(s)\zeta(s)=\hat{G}(\frac{s-\frac{1}{2}}{i}),

so that

\displaystyle\hat{G}(\gamma)=0

for all nonreal zeros,
\rho=\frac{1}{2}+i\gamma
, of
\zeta(s)
. (The
\gamma
are not assumed to be real.) In this case an explicit formula,

\displaystyle\sum_{p^{m}}\frac{logp}{p^{\frac{m}{2}}}\Big(G(m\ logp)+G(-m\ logp)\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V(G)

\displaystyle V(G)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{G}(-\frac{i}{2})+\hat{G}(\frac{i}{2})

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle log\pi\ G(0)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{\dot{\Gamma}}{\Gamma}(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{it}{2})+\frac{\dot{\Gamma}}{\Gamma}(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{it}{2})\Big)\hat{G}(t)dt,

exists, and a sum on prime powers is thereby expressed in terms of quantities with no apparent connection with the zeros of the Riemann zeta function. It is readily checked that the equation,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{\sqrt{n}}\Big(G(log(x_{n})+G(-log(x_{n})\Big)=V(G),

does not identify the prime powers; i. e.,
x_{n}=n
. To pinpoint the prime powers as a unique solution, it would be expected that infinitely many equations would be needed. Given the one pair,
G(v)
and
\hat{G}(t)
, the obvious candidates for further
G
are convolutions, the translations,
G(v+c)

e^{-ict}\hat{G}(t)
. In a coming section, we use the particular translates,
[c=logm:m=1,2,3,\cdots]
, obtaining an infinite system of equations,

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Lambda(n)f(m,n)=V(m),

indexed by the natural numbers,
[m=1,2,\cdots]
. The entries of the column vector,

V=[V(m)]_{m=1}^{\infty},

are the values of the
m
-th translate,
V(G(v+logm))
. The objective would be a unique solution vector,

\Lambda=[\Lambda(n)]_{n=1}^{\infty},

of

T\Lambda=V,

T=[f(m,n)]_{m,n=1}^{\infty},

with
\Lambda(n)
being the von Mangoldt function. The entries,

-th row and

are essentially special values of the modular function,

\lambda(\tau)=\Big(\frac{\theta_{2}(\tau)}{\theta_{3}(\tau)}\Big)^{4}.

These are numbers from finite algebraic extensions of the rational field,
Q

T
guaranteed a unique solution. Alternatively, instead of creating equations from the translates of one
G

,
with

since
\zeta(s)
is a factor of all such
\zeta_{L}

\hat{G}_{L}(\frac{s-\frac{1}{2}}{i})
existed. The new ingredient is a whole new set of unrelated

\displaystyle\frac{s(s+1)}{32\pi^{2}\sqrt{2}}(2^{\frac{s}{2}}-2^{-\frac{s}{2}})(2^{\frac{s-1}{2}}-2^{-\frac{s-1}{2}})\zeta^{*}(s)\zeta^{*}(s+1)

Proof.

As before, let

Then

with the multiplicative coefficients,

\displaystyle c(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}(k),\qquad(k,2)=1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-3\qquad\qquad k=2^{m},\ 1\leq m.

With

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{2w-1}(\theta_{4}^{4}(e^{-\pi y})-1)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-8\pi^{-2w}\Gamma(2w)\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c(k)k^{-2w}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-8\pi^{-2w}\Gamma(2w)\sum_{(k,2)=1}\Big(1-3\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}2^{-2mw}\Big)c(k)k^{-2w}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-8\pi^{-2w}\Gamma(2w)(1-2^{2-2w})\zeta(2w-1)(1-2^{-(2w-1)})\zeta(2w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-8\pi^{-(s+1)}\Gamma(s+1)(1-2^{1-s})\zeta(s)(1-2^{-s})\zeta(s+1),

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{2w-1}(\theta_{4}^{4}(e^{-\pi y})-1)dy

\displaystyle-\frac{4}{2w}\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{2w}\frac{\dot{\theta}_{4}}{\theta_{4}}(e^{-\pi y})\theta_{4}^{4}(e^{-\pi y})dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{4}{2w}\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{2w}\frac{\dot{\theta}_{4}}{\theta_{4}}(e^{-\pi y})y^{-2}\theta_{2}^{4}(e^{-\frac{\pi}{y}})dy

\displaystyle\frac{8(32)\pi}{s+1}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{d|j}a(d)b(\frac{j}{d})\Big(\frac{j}{d^{2}}\Big)^{\frac{s}{2}}K(s,2\pi\sqrt{j}).

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{itv}\Big[\frac{1}{t-i(2n+\frac{1}{2})}-\frac{1}{t+i(2n+\frac{1}{2})}\Big]dt=\text{Min}[e^{v(2n+1)},e^{-v(2n+1)}].

(5)

Write (2) as a double integral,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{4n+1}{(2n+\frac{1}{2})^{2}+t^{2}}\hat{G}(t)dt=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{4n+1}{(2n+\frac{1}{2})^{2}+t^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{itv}G(v)dvdt,

substitute (4), interchange the order of integration, and use (5) to obtain for (2) the sums,

\displaystyle-\sum_{n=0}^{N}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{v(2n+\frac{1}{2})}G(v)dv-\sum_{n=0}^{N}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-v(2n+\frac{1}{2})}G(v)dv,

which equals,

\displaystyle-\sum_{n=0}^{N}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{v(2n+\frac{1}{2})}G(-v)dv-\sum_{n=0}^{N}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-v(2n+\frac{1}{2})}G(v)dv.

(6)

Combining (3) and (6), and letting
N\longrightarrow\infty
, then,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}Re\Big(\frac{\dot{\Gamma}}{\Gamma}(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{it}{2})\Big)\hat{G}(t)dt=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big[2e^{-2v}G(0)-e^{-\frac{v}{2}}(G(-v)+G(v))\Big]\frac{dv}{1-e^{-2v}}-\gamma G(0).

The expression in the square brackets is

\displaystyle J(e^{v})=2e^{-2v}\alpha(1)\beta(1)-e^{-v}\alpha(e^{-v})\beta(e^{v})-\alpha(e^{v})\beta(e^{-v}),

so that, as stated in the lemma,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}Re\Big(\frac{\dot{\Gamma}}{\Gamma}(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{it}{2})\Big)\hat{G}(t)dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{J(e^{v})}{1-e^{-2v}}dv-\gamma\alpha(1)\beta(1).

Changing variables,

\displaystyle v=logu\qquad dv=\frac{du}{u},

we have,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{J(e^{v})}{1-e^{-2v}}dv

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{2u^{-2}\alpha(1)\beta(1)-u^{-1}\alpha(u^{-1})\beta(u)-\alpha(u)\beta(u^{-1})}{1-u^{-2}}\frac{du}{u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{2u^{2}\alpha(1)\beta(1)-u\alpha(u)\beta(u^{-1})-\alpha(u^{-1})\beta(u)}{1-u^{2}}\frac{du}{u}

∎

The term,

\displaystyle V(\infty)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle log\pi\ G(0)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{\dot{\Gamma}}{\Gamma}(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{it}{2})+\frac{\dot{\Gamma}}{\Gamma}(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{it}{2})\Big)\hat{G}(t)dt,

on the right side of the prime power equation comes from the Archimedian factor in
Z(s)
, a component of the functional equation on which the Guinand formula rests, and might as well be labelled that way. It plays the role of a known term in the equations, but a difficult expression, containing the values of
\beta(e^{-\pi y})
in an integral. An effort was made to evaluate it as a discrete expression of special values of
\beta
.

5.
Introducing a Variable
x>0

Continuous translates,
G(v+logx),x>0,
of
G
are introduced, from which a discrete infinite collection,
x=m,m=1,2,\cdots
is selected to create an infinite set of prime power equations. For
x>0
, it is readily verified that,

\displaystyle G(v+logx)=\sqrt{x}e^{\frac{v}{2}}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a(k)e^{-\pi kxe^{v}}\Big)\Big(\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}b(l)e^{-\frac{\pi l}{x}e^{-v}}\Big),

so that

\displaystyle G(mlogp+logx)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{x}p^{\frac{m}{2}}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a(k)e^{-\pi kxp^{m}}\Big)\Big(\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}b(l)e^{-\frac{\pi l}{xp^{m}}}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{x}p^{\frac{m}{2}}\alpha(xp^{m})\beta(\frac{1}{xp^{m}})

\displaystyle G(-mlogp+logx)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{x}p^{-\frac{m}{2}}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a(k)e^{-\pi kxp^{-m}}\Big)\Big(\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}b(l)e^{-\frac{\pi lp^{m}}{x}}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{x}p^{-\frac{m}{2}}\alpha(\frac{x}{p^{m}})\beta(\frac{p^{m}}{x}).

With the variable,
x
, in all the terms, the modified prime power equation is now,

Lemma 6
.

\displaystyle\sqrt{x}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Lambda(n)\ \alpha(xn)\beta(\frac{1}{xn})+\sqrt{x}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n}\alpha(\frac{x}{n})\beta(\frac{n}{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}\hat{G}(\frac{i}{2})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sqrt{x}\hat{G}(-\frac{i}{2})

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sqrt{x}\ log\pi\ \alpha(x)\beta(\frac{1}{x})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sqrt{x}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{J(e^{v},x)}{1-e^{-2v}}dv-\gamma\sqrt{x}\ \alpha(x)\beta(\frac{1}{x}),

where

\displaystyle J(e^{v},x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2e^{-2v}\alpha(x)\beta(\frac{1}{x})-e^{-v}\alpha(xe^{-v})\beta(\frac{e^{v}}{x})-\alpha(xe^{v})\beta(\frac{e^{-v}}{x}).

In order to remove
\hat{G}(\frac{i}{2})
and
\hat{G}(-\frac{i}{2})
from the prime power equation, note that, given a relation,

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{x}g(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a\sqrt{x}+\frac{b}{\sqrt{x}}+h(x),

as exists in our prime power formula, then the operation,

f(x)+f(\frac{1}{x})-f(1)(\sqrt{x}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}})

eliminates

a\sqrt{x}+\frac{b}{\sqrt{x}}.

This operation removes
\hat{G}[\frac{i}{2}]
and
\hat{G}[-\frac{i}{2}]
. The resulting prime power formula would depend only on
\alpha
and
\beta
, but as the situation stands, there is no evident advantage in taking this step, and the right side,
V(G)
, is left with the two Bessel terms.

6.
Solving for Prime Powers

Writing the prime power equation as,

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f(x,n)\Lambda(n)=V(x)\qquad x>0,

and replacing
x
with
m=1,2,\cdots
, in turn, there results a sequence of equations,

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f(m,n)\Lambda(n)=V(m),

indexed by the natural numbers,
[m=1,2,\cdots]
. With the pair of column vectors,

\Lambda=[\Lambda(n)]_{n=1}^{\infty},

and,

V=[V(m)]_{m=1}^{\infty},

the linear system of equations is put in the form,

T\Lambda=V.

The entries of

T=[f(m,n)]_{m,n=1}^{\infty},

are

f(m,n)=\alpha(mn)\beta(\frac{1}{mn})+\frac{1}{n}\alpha(\frac{m}{n})\beta(\frac{n}{m}),

where the square root,
\sqrt{m}
, was put into the
V
vector. The objective is a solution,

\Lambda=T^{-1}V.

From any angle this does not look promising.

7.
The Invertibility of
T

Firstly, the possible inverse,
T^{-1}
, cannot be approached as a limit of inverses of upper left hand
n\times n
cutouts of
T
. These are not approximations to T. The diagonal entries of
T
are

f(n,n)=\frac{\alpha(1)\beta(1)}{n}+\epsilon(n)\qquad\epsilon(n)\longrightarrow 0.

Since
\alpha
and
\beta
are positive,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=n+1}^{2n-1}f(n,k)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=n+1}^{2n}\frac{1}{k}\alpha\Big(\frac{n}{k+1}\Big)\beta\Big(\frac{k+1}{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle log2\ \alpha(1)\beta(2).

Thus
T
is not diagonally dominant, in fact, as shown, far from it. And the arguments used to establish the existence of an inverse for infinite systems, which rely on finite dimensional (upper left) approximations to
T
, cannot be applied.

Note that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=n+1}^{2n-1}f(n,k)\Lambda(k)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=n+1}^{2n}\frac{\Lambda(k)}{k}\alpha\Big(\frac{n}{k+1}\Big)\beta\Big(\frac{k+1}{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle log2\ \alpha(1)\beta(2),

so the action of the
n
th row of the finite dimensional,
n\times n
, upper left truncation,
T_{n}
, of
T
, on
\Lambda
differs from the action of the
n
th row of
T
on
\Lambda
by a constant for all
n
.

8.
The Matrix T

Secondly, assuming an inverse,
T^{-1}
, finding it contains the known complications of finding an inverse in the finite dimensional case. Even matrices with simple entries might have an inverse that relies on a highly sophisticated combinatorial identity. Basically, they’re works of art. Consider what there is to encounter in the entries of
T
. Since

\displaystyle\alpha(u)=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}2^{l}\beta(2^{l}u),

the matrix,
T
, depends on
\beta
alone, and consists of sums and products of values,

\beta(e^{-\pi r})\qquad r\in Q^{+},

which are, to within a factor of
\frac{1}{16}
, equal to

\theta_{2}^{4}(\tau)\qquad q=e^{i\pi\tau},\tau=ir.

For
r=N
, a positive integer,

\theta_{2}^{4}(N\tau)=\Big(\frac{\theta_{3}(N\tau)}{\theta_{3}(\tau)}\Big)^{4}\lambda(N\tau)\theta_{3}^{4}(\tau),

and the factor,

F(N\tau)=\Big(\frac{\theta_{3}(N\tau)}{\theta_{3}(\tau)}\Big)^{4}\lambda(N\tau),

is algebraic at
\tau=i
. With
N=p
, a prime, this follows from three results. First, that

\frac{\theta_{3}(p\tau)}{\theta_{3}(\tau)}

is an algebraic function of
\lambda(\tau)
; second, that
\lambda(p\tau)
is an algebraic function of
\lambda(\tau)
; and, third, that
\lambda(i)=\frac{1}{2}
. The values of
F(Ni)
are then algebraic functions of
\lambda(i)
, being compositions indexed on the prime factors of
N
. For rational
r=\frac{N}{M}>0
,
F(ri)
is algebraic, arguing as before with
\tau=\frac{i}{M}
, using the relation,

\lambda(\frac{i}{M})=1-\lambda(iM).

Finally,

\displaystyle\theta_{3}(i)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{\frac{1}{4}}}{\Gamma(\frac{3}{4})}.

Thus, the values,
\theta_{2}^{4}(ri)
, the building blocks of the entries of
T
, are algebraic multiples of
\theta_{3}^{4}(i)
.

9.
Supposing
T^{-1}

Write,

\Lambda=T^{-1}V.

Let

U=[u_{mn}]_{m,n=1}^{\infty}

\displaystyle u_{mn}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1\qquad n\leq m

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\qquad n>m

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\qquad n=m=p^{k},

then

U\Lambda=\Psi_{0},

where
\Psi_{0}
is the column vector,

[\Psi_{0}(N)]_{N=1}^{\infty},

with

\Psi_{0}(N)=\sum_{n\leq N}u_{Nn}\Lambda(n).

Having reached this point, it would then seem that technique is within reach to reconcile this formula with the explicit,

\Psi_{0}(N)=N-\sum_{\rho}\frac{N^{\rho}}{\rho}-\frac{\dot{\zeta}}{\zeta}(0)-\frac{1}{2}log(1-N^{-2})\qquad N>1,

and identify the zeros of the zeta function originating in the structure of
T
. The point here is that a mystery has simply been recast in a slightly novel setting. None of the mystery has been eliminated. The unknown,
T^{-1}
, was therefore an expected stopping point, though a
T^{-1}
might be investigated in the context of function fields, where the (norms of) primes have an internal structure,

p=q^{e(p)}\qquad e(p)\in Z^{+}.

With

e_{n}=\Big|[p:e(p)=n]\Big|,

the sequence,

N_{k}=\sum_{d|k}de_{d}

satisfies a finite linear recursion over the integers. Its characteristic polynomial produces the zeros of a zeta function. If

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{logp}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{T_{mn}^{-1}V(n)}\qquad m=m(p),

is the internal structure in rational primes, then a connection over
m(p)
might be discoverable. It could replace the finite recursion, and the zeta zeros would come out of that connection.

10.
Other
G

\hat{G}

As we saw in Lemma 1, a
G

\theta_{4}^{4}

\displaystyle\frac{s(s+1)}{32\pi^{2}\sqrt{2}}(2^{\frac{s}{2}}-2^{-\frac{s}{2}})(2^{\frac{s-1}{2}}-2^{-\frac{s-1}{2}})\zeta^{*}(s)\zeta^{*}(s+1)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{d|j}a(d)b(\frac{j}{d})\Big(\frac{j}{d^{2}}\Big)^{\frac{s}{2}}K[s,2\pi\sqrt{j}].

G

\displaystyle\frac{s(s+2)(s+3)}{256\pi^{3}\sqrt{2}}2^{\frac{s}{2}}(2^{\frac{s-1}{2}}-2^{-\frac{s-1}{2}})\zeta^{*}(s)\zeta^{*}(s+3)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{d|j}a(d)B(\frac{j}{d})\Big(\frac{j}{d^{2}}\Big)^{\frac{s}{2}}K[s,2\pi\sqrt{j}].

This paper could have been written with a

\theta_{3}

\displaystyle E_{L}(s)\zeta_{L}(s)=\hat{G}_{L}(\frac{s-\frac{1}{2}}{i}).

For example, the form producing

for

\displaystyle L=Q(\sqrt{-1})

\displaystyle\sum_{m,n=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\pi\tau(m^{2}+n^{2})},

but

\displaystyle\sum_{m,n=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}(-1)^{n}e^{i\pi\tau(m^{2}+n^{2})},

The field,

\displaystyle L=Q(\sqrt{-2})

\displaystyle\sum_{m,n=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\pi\tau(m^{2}+2n^{2})},

\displaystyle 1-4^{1-s},
