Hamiltonian mechanics of “magnetic” solitons in two-component Bose-Einstein condensates
A. M. Kamchatnov

Abstract
We consider motion of a “magnetic” soliton in two-component condensates along a non-uniform and time-dependent backgrounds in framework of the Hamiltonian mechanics. Our approach is based on generalization of Stokes’ remark that soliton’s velocity is related with its inverse half-width by the dispersion law for linear waves continued to the region of complex wave numbers. We obtain expressions for the canonical momentum and the Hamiltonian as functions of soliton’s velocity and transform the Hamilton equations to the Newton-like equation. The theory is illustrated by several examples of concrete soliton’s dynamics.

I
Introduction

The idea that solitons behave under action of smooth enough external forces similar to
point-like particles of classical mechanics is well known. In particular, a number of
perturbation methods is based on this qualitative picture
kaup-76
;
km-77
;
km-78
;
ka-81

(see also Refs.
km-89
;
og-2000
and references therein) and these theories are confirmed
by numerical and real experiments. The standard perturbation theory is quite involved in
case of dark solitons propagating along a non-uniform background
ky-94
;
ba-2000
;
anhf-11

when separation of the soliton’s dynamics from dynamics of the surrounding background
is not obvious: a moving soliton generates a ‘shelf’ in the background and this leads to an
essential back reaction on the soliton’s motion. For example, the frequency of oscillations
of a dark soliton in a Bose-Einstein condensate (BEC) confined in a harmonic trap with the
frequency
\omega_{0}
is equal to
\omega_{0}/\sqrt{2}
whereas the center of gravity of BEC still
oscillates with the trap frequency
ba-2000
. This means that the soliton’s motion
generates a counterflow in the surrounding condensate which results in an essential
redistribution of its mass. In this sense, motion of dark solitons can be likened
kp-04

to motion of quasi-particles in solid-state physics where dynamical properties of
quasi-particles are determined by their interaction with the surrounding matter. As a result,
derivation of equations of motion of such solitonic quasi-particles by the standard
perturbation theory is, generally speaking, quite a difficult problem.

A simpler method has been recently suggested in Ref.
ks-23a
. It is based on the
assumption that if the nonlinear wave equation is Hamiltonian, then its reduction to the
case of the soliton’s motion along a smooth time-dependent background remains also Hamiltonian.
The condition that the soliton’s dynamics is Hamiltonian, combined with old Stokes’ remark

stokes
that the soliton’s velocity is related with its inverse half-width by the
dispersion relation for linear waves, allows one to find expressions for the canonical momentum
and the Hamiltonian of the point-like soliton. The method was applied to the KdV
ks-23a
,
NLS
ik-22
;
kamch-23
, DNLS
ks-24
equations and analytical results were confirmed by
numerical solutions for concrete situations.

In this paper, we will obtain the equations of motion for so-called “magnetic” solitons
in a two-component BEC. Such soliton solutions were obtained in Ref.
qps-16
in the
limit of small difference between the inter- and intra-components interaction. In this case,
the BEC components move in opposite directions without transfer of the total density,
so the dynamics is completely determined by the counterflow of the BEC components. We
obtain the expressions for the canonical momentum and the Hamiltonian of the soliton
dynamics and transform the Hamilton equations to the Newton-like equation for soliton’s
motion. Since the dynamics is determined by the gradients of the background flow, we
assume that the Newton equation remains valid when these gradients are resulted from
the background dynamics with account of external potentials. The theory is illustrated
by concrete examples of the magnetic soliton dynamics.

II
Basic equations

A two-component condensate is described by the system of Gross-Pitaevskii equations

\begin{split}&i\psi_{1,t}+\frac{1}{2}\psi_{1,xx}-(\alpha_{1}|\psi_{1}|^{2}+\alpha_{2}|\psi_{2}|^{2})\psi_{1}=V_{1}\psi_{1},\\
&i\psi_{2,t}+\frac{1}{2}\psi_{2,xx}-(\alpha_{2}|\psi_{1}|^{2}+\alpha_{1}|\psi_{2}|^{2})\psi_{2}=V_{2}\psi_{2},\end{split}

(1)

where
\psi_{1,2}
are wave functions of two components,
\alpha_{1}
is the constant of
interaction between atoms in each component, and
\alpha_{2}
is the constant of interaction
between atoms which belong to different components. We assume that

\alpha_{1}>\alpha_{2}>0,

(2)

what corresponds to the repulsive interaction between atoms and the BEC is a mixture of
the components which do not separate from each other in space (see, e.g.,
ps-16
).

V_{1}
and
V_{2}
are the external (trap) potentials acting on the first and the second
component, correspondingly.

It is convenient to introduce pseudospin variables (see, e.g.,
mueller

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\psi_{1}\\
\psi_{2}\\
\end{array}\right)=\sqrt{\rho}\,e^{i\Phi/2}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\,e^{-i\phi/2}\,e^{-i\mu_{1}t}\\
\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,e^{i\phi/2}\,e^{-i\mu_{2}t}\\
\end{array}\right),

(3)

where
\rho_{1}=\rho\cos^{2}(\theta/2),\rho_{2}=\rho\sin^{2}(\theta/2)
are the densities of the
components, so that
\rho=\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}
is the total density of BEC. The functions

\Phi
and
\phi

motions of the components; consequently the components’ velocities are equal to

v_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(\Phi_{x}-\phi_{x}),\qquad v_{2}=\frac{1}{2}(\Phi_{x}+\phi_{x}).

(4)

In the Thomas-Fermi approximations, Eqs. (
1
) give the stationary distributions of
the densities of the components confined in a trap with the potentials
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
,

\begin{split}\rho_{1}=\rho_{1,0}(x)=\frac{\alpha_{1}(\mu_{1}-V_{1}(x))-\alpha_{2}(\mu_{2}-V_{2}(x))}{\alpha_{1}^{2}-\alpha_{2}^{2}}),\\
\rho_{2}=\rho_{2,0}(x)=\frac{\alpha_{1}(\mu_{2}-V_{2}(x))-\alpha_{2}(\mu_{1}-V_{1}(x))}{\alpha_{1}^{2}-\alpha_{2}^{2}};\end{split}

(5)

then the total density is equal to

\rho=\rho_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}(\mu-V_{1}(x)-V_{2}(x)),

Substitution of Eqs. (

&\Phi_{t}+\frac{\rho_{x}^{2}}{4\rho^{2}}-\frac{\rho_{xx}}{2\rho}-\frac{\cot\theta}{2\rho}(\rho\,\theta_{x})_{x}+\frac{1}{4}(\Phi_{x}^{2}+\phi_{x}^{2}+\theta_{x}^{2})+(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})\rho=-(V_{1}+V_{2}),\\
&\theta_{t}+\frac{1}{2\rho}(\phi_{x}\rho\sin\theta)_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\Phi_{x}\theta_{x}=0,\\
&\phi_{t}-\frac{1}{2\rho\sin\theta}(\rho\,\theta_{x})_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\Phi_{x}\phi_{x}-(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})\rho\cos\theta=V_{1}-V_{2}.\end{split}

We are interested in dynamics of “magnetic” solitons of relative motion of the components

of the

III
Dispersion relation of linear waves, dispersionless limit and soliton solution

Let a small-amplitude harmonic wave propagate along a uniform condensate with constant
values of
w=w_{0},v=v_{0}
and zero external potentials
V_{1}=V_{2}=0
. We linearize Eqs. (
14
)
with respect to small deviations
w^{\prime}=w-w_{0},v^{\prime}=v-v_{0}
from this uniform state and obtain

\begin{split}&w^{\prime}_{t}+2v_{0}w_{0}w^{\prime}_{x}-(1-w_{0}^{2})v^{\prime}_{x}=0,\\
&v^{\prime}_{t}-(\widetilde{\rho}-v_{0}^{2})w^{\prime}_{x}+\frac{w^{\prime}_{xxx}}{1-w_{0}^{2}}+2v_{0}w_{0}v^{\prime}_{x}=0.\end{split}

(15)

This system readily gives the dispersion relation for harmonic waves
w^{\prime},v^{\prime}\propto\exp[i(kx-\omega t)]
:

\omega(k)=k\left(2v_{0}w_{0}\pm\sqrt{(\widetilde{\rho}-v_{0}^{2})(1-w_{0}^{2})+k^{2}}\right).

(16)

By definition we have
|w_{0}|<1
, so the uniform state is modulationally unstable for large
enough values of the relative velocity,
|v_{0}|>\sqrt{\widetilde{\rho}}
, of the components. We will
confine ourselves to the modulationally stable situations with
|v_{0}|<\sqrt{\widetilde{\rho}}
.

Even for zero potentials
V_{1}=V_{2}=0
the background state can be non-uniform and time-dependent,
if a large-scale wave propagates through BEC, so that the wave variables are give by the functions

w=w(x,t)
and
v=v(x,t)
. If the characteristic size of these distributions is much greater
than the soliton’s width
\sim\kappa^{-1}
, then we can neglect the dispersion term in the second
equation (
14
) and arrive at the equations of the dispersionless (hydrodynamic)
approximation

w_{t}-[v(1-w^{2})]_{x}=0,\qquad v_{t}-[w(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})]_{x}=0.

(17)

These are Ovsyannikov equations
ovs-79
derived long ago for internal waves in
two-fluid systems. Characteristic velocities of this system are equal to

v_{\pm}=2vw\pm\sqrt{(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})(1-w^{2})}.

(18)

Eqs. (
17
) can be cast to the diagonal Riemann form

\frac{\partial r_{+}}{\partial t}+v_{+}\frac{\partial r_{+}}{\partial x}=0,\qquad\frac{\partial r_{-}}{\partial t}+v_{-}\frac{\partial r_{-}}{\partial x}=0

(19)

for the Riemann invariants

r_{\pm}=vw\pm\sqrt{(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})(1-w^{2})}.

(20)

The characteristic velocities are expressed in terms of the Riemann invariants by
simple formulas

v_{+}=\frac{1}{2}(3r_{+}+r_{-}),\qquad v_{-}=\frac{1}{2}(r_{+}+3r_{-}).

(21)

The simplest soliton solution of Eqs. (
14
) (with
V_{1}=V_{2}=0
) was found in
Ref.
qps-16
and it corresponds to zero background relative velocity,
v_{0}=0
,
and equal background densities of the components,
w_{0}=0
, far from the soliton.
The general soliton solution with arbitrary values of the background parameters

v_{0},w_{0}
was obtained in Ref.
ikcp-17
. We don’t need here the explicit form
of this solution. Some necessary most important for us
relations can be found without knowledge of the full solution. For example, according
to the old remark of Stokes
stokes
, the exponentially small soliton’s tails

\propto\exp[\pm\kappa(x-Vt)]
,
V
being the soliton’s velocity, and the small-amplitude
harmonic waves
\propto\exp[i(kx-\omega t)]
obey the same linearized system (
15
).
Consequently, the replacement
k\to i\kappa
transforms the harmonic wave to the
soliton’s tail and the phase velocity
\omega/k
of a harmonic wave transforms to the
soliton’s velocity,

V=\frac{\omega(i\kappa)}{i\kappa}.

(22)

As a result, we obtain the expression for the soliton’s velocity

V=2vw\pm\sqrt{(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})(1-w^{2})-\kappa^{2}},

(23)

where
v,w
are the local values of the large-scale wave variables obeying the
dispersionless equations (
17
) and
\kappa
is the soliton’s inverse half-width.

Another relationship

\kappa^{2}=(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})(1-w^{2})-(q-vw)^{2}

(24)

(
q
is a constant parameter) can be obtained using the fact that in completely integrable
system (
14
) (with
V_{1}=V_{2}=0
) the carrier wave number of a wave packet propagating
along a large-scale wave is only a function of the local values of
v
and
w
given by
the formula (see
ks-24b
)

k^{2}=(q-vw)^{2}-(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})(1-w^{2}),

(25)

where
q
is an integration constant determined by the values of
k
at some initial point.
Again the Stokes transformation yields the formula (
24
). Combining Eqs. (
24
)
and (
25
), we obtain (for the choice of the upper sign and supposing
q-vw>0
)

V=\frac{dx}{dt}=vw+q,

(26)

where
x=x(t)
is the soliton’s path.

It is instructive to derive this relation by another method based on the analysis of the
Whitham modulation equations at their soliton limit. It was used in Refs.

;
seh-21

for consideration of soliton-mean flow interaction and we generalize here this approach.
Periodic solutions of the system (
14
) with
V_{1}=V_{2}=0
are parameterized by four
constant parameters
r_{i},

i=1,2,3,4,
which in a modulated wave become slow functions
of
x
and
t
(see
ikcp-17
). Their slow evolution is governed by the Whitham
modulation equations

\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial t}+v_{i}\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial x}=0,\quad i=1,2,3,4.

(27)

At the soliton limit we have
r_{2}=r_{3}
and these equations reduce to

\frac{\partial r_{1}}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{2}(3r_{1}+r_{4})\frac{\partial r_{1}}{\partial x}=0,\qquad\frac{\partial r_{2}}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{2}(r_{1}+2r_{2}+r_{4})\frac{\partial r_{2}}{\partial x}=0,\qquad\frac{\partial r_{4}}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{2}(r_{1}+3r_{4})\frac{\partial r_{4}}{\partial x}=0,

(28)

and the phase velocity of the periodic solution becomes the soliton’s velocity equal to

V=\frac{1}{2}(r_{1}+2r_{2}+r_{4}).

(29)

Consequently, the soliton’s velocity changes along its path according to the equation

\frac{dV}{dt}=\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}+V\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}=\frac{1}{2}\left[(r_{2}-r_{1})\frac{\partial r_{1}}{\partial x}-(r_{4}-r_{2})\frac{\partial r_{4}}{\partial x}\right].

(30)

The first and third Eqs. (

19
), that is they
describe evolution of the large-scale wave at the soliton’s location and we can identify

, where

are defined bt Eqs. (

into Eq. (

\frac{dV}{dt}=(1-3w^{2})vv_{x}+(\widetilde{\rho}-3v^{2})vv_{x}+V(vw)_{x}=(vw)_{t}+V(vw)_{x}=\frac{d(vw)}{dt}.

(31)

Integration of this equation gives at once Eq. (
26
).

IV
Hamilton equations

In derivation of the canonical momentum and Hamiltonian, we still assume that the background wave
obeys the dispersionless equations (
17
) with
\widetilde{\rho}=\mathrm{const}
. It is convenient
to introduce a new variable
\varphi
instead of
\kappa
,

\kappa=\sqrt{(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})(1-w^{2})}\sin\varphi.

(32)

Then Eq. (
23
) takes the form

V=2vw+\sqrt{(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})(1-w^{2})}\cos\varphi

and substitution of this expression into Eq. (

vw+\sqrt{(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})(1-w^{2})}\cos\varphi=q.

We will need a useful formula which follows from this identity. To obtain it, we differentiate
(
34
) along the soliton’s path
x=x(t)
, so that
d/dt=\partial/\partial t+V\partial/\partial x
, and
eliminate
\partial v/\partial t
,

with the use of Eqs. (
17

simplifications yield the necessary formula

\frac{d\varphi}{dt}=-\left[\sqrt{(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})(1-w^{2})}\right]_{x}\sin\varphi.

Now we interpret Eq. (
33

\frac{dx}{dt}=2vw+\sqrt{(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})(1-w^{2})}\cos\varphi=\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}\right)_{x}

(36)

and assume that the momentum has the form

p=(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})(1-w^{2})f(\varphi),

(37)

where
f(\varphi)
is an unknown function. Integration of Eq. (
36
) gives

H=2vwp+\left[(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})(1-w^{2})\right]^{3/2}\int\cos\varphi f^{\prime}(\varphi)d\varphi.

(38)

To find

, we turn to another Hamilton equation

\frac{dp}{dt}=-\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}\right)_{p}.

(39)

Substitution of Eqs. (
37
) and (
38
) gives after evident simplifications

\begin{split}&\left[(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})(1-w^{2})\right]_{x}\sqrt{(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})(1-w^{2})}f\cos\varphi+(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})(1-w^{2})f^{\prime}\frac{d\varphi}{dt}\\
&=-\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})(1-w^{2})}\left[(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})(1-w^{2})\right]_{x}\int\cos\varphi f^{\prime}d\varphi-\left[(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})(1-w^{2})\right]^{3/2}\cos\varphi f^{\prime}\left(\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x}\right)_{p}.\end{split}

(40)

Differentiation of Eq. (
37

for constant

\left(\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x}\right)_{p}=-\frac{f\left[(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})(1-w^{2})\right]_{x}}{f^{\prime}(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})(1-w^{2})}.

We substitute Eqs. (

which can be solved at once to give

where

is an integration constant. Its arbitrariness corresponds to the invariance of the
Hamilton equations with respect to multiplication of

and

by the same constant factor.
We choose

to simplify some further formulas. Then Eqs. (

) and (

yield after exclusion of

V
Newton equation

In Hamiltonian mechanics, we have to solve Eq. (
44
) with respect to
\dot{x}

and to substitute this function of
x
and
p
into Eq. (
45
). Then the
Hamilton equations (
36
) and (
39
) govern the soliton’s motion.
It is impossible to perform
such an elimination of
\dot{x}
explicitly, therefore it is more convenient to
pass from the Hamilton equations to the Newton-like equation for the soliton’s
acceleration
\ddot{x}
. Actually, this transformation was already put in
practice for the NLS soliton in Ref.
ik-22
and we can use the formulas
obtained there for writing the Newton equation for the magnetic soliton with help of
the substitutions (
47
). The Newton equation for the NLS soliton reads
ik-22

2\ddot{x}=n_{x}+(u+\dot{x})u_{x}+2u_{t}+\frac{n_{t}+(nu)_{x}}{\sqrt{n-(\dot{x}-u)^{2}}}\arccos\frac{\dot{x}-u}{\sqrt{n}}.

(48)

Consequently, the substitutions (
47
) yield the Newton equation for the
magnetic soliton in the following form

\begin{split}&2\ddot{x}=\left[(1-w^{2})(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})\right]_{x}+2(2vw+\dot{x})(vw)_{x}+4(vw)_{t}\\
&+\frac{\left[(1-w^{2})(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})\right]_{t}+\left[2(1-w^{2})(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})vw\right]_{x}}{2\sqrt{(1-w^{2})(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})-(\dot{x}-2vw)^{2}}}\arccos\frac{\dot{x}-2vw}{\sqrt{(1-w^{2})(\widetilde{\rho}-v^{2})}}.\end{split}

(49)

As one can see, in this theory the soliton’s dynamic is governed by the gradients of
the background distributions in agreement with our assumption that the soliton’s

VI
Conclusion

The method used in this paper is based on the generalization of Stokes’
remark
stokes
that soliton’s tails propagate with soliton’s velocity and
at the same time satisfy the linearized equations for small-amplitude waves. Therefore, the
soliton’s velocity is related with the soliton’s inverse half-width by the dispersion relation
continued to the region of complex wave numbers. Validity of this remark is confirmed by
particular soliton solutions of a number of concrete nonlinear wave equations. It remains
also correct for solitons propagating along non-uniform background, as one can see from
the coincidence of the equation for soliton’s motion with the corresponding results
obtained in the soliton limit of the Whitham modulation equations in completely integrable
cases when the Whitham equations are available in the Riemann diagonal form. Extension
of the obtained by this method equations for solitons motion to non-integrable situations
allows one to reproduce some known results and to obtain the new ones.

This approach is not limited to the problem of solitons motion. Another its interesting
application is to the quasi-classical method of calculation of velocities of solitons
produced from an intensive initial pulse
kamch-23b
. In completely integrable cases,
it easily reproduces the known results following from the WKB method applied to the
spectral problem associated with the concrete equation in framework of the inverse
scattering transform method. What is more important, it can be applied to not completely
integrable equations and gives the results which agree very well with the results of
numerical simulations.

At last, we should mention that our approach allowed us to connect the condition of
preservation of Hamiltonian mechanics of propagation of high-frequency wave packets
along a smooth large-scale dispersionless flow with the quasi-classical limit of the
Lax pair for equations integrable in framework of AKNS scheme
kamch-23b
;
ks-24b
.
This observation sheds new light on conditions of complete integrability of nonlinear
wave equations.

One may hope that the suggested method can lead to many other interesting results.
