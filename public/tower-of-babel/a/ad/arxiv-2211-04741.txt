𝖧𝖺𝗋𝗉𝗈𝖼𝗋𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗌 \mathsf{Harpocrates} : Privacy-Preserving and Immutable Audit Log for Sensitive Data Operations
Mohit Bhasi Thazhath, Jan Michalak, Thang Hoang

Abstract
The audit log is a crucial component to monitor fine-grained operations over sensitive data (e.g., personal, health) for security inspection and assurance. Since such data operations can be highly sensitive, it is vital to ensure that the audit log achieves not only validity and immutability, but also confidentiality against active threats to standard data regulations (e.g., HIPAA) compliance. Despite its critical needs, state-of-the-art privacy-preserving audit log schemes (e.g., Ghostor (NSDI ’20), Calypso (VLDB ’19)) do not fully obtain a high level of privacy, integrity, and immutability simultaneously, in which certain information (e.g., user identities) is still leaked in the log. In this paper, we propose 𝖧𝖺𝗋𝗉𝗈𝖼𝗋𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗌 \mathsf{Harpocrates} , a new privacy-preserving and immutable audit log scheme. 𝖧𝖺𝗋𝗉𝗈𝖼𝗋𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗌 \mathsf{Harpocrates} permits data store, share, and access operations to be recorded in the audit log without leaking sensitive information (e.g., data identifier, user identity), while permitting the validity of data operations to be publicly verifiable. 𝖧𝖺𝗋𝗉𝗈𝖼𝗋𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗌 \mathsf{Harpocrates} makes use of blockchain techniques to achieve immutability and avoid a single point of failure, while cryptographic zero-knowledge proofs are harnessed for confidentiality and public verifiability. We analyze the security of our proposed technique and prove that it achieves non-malleability and indistinguishability. We fully implemented 𝖧𝖺𝗋𝗉𝗈𝖼𝗋𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗌 \mathsf{Harpocrates} and evaluated its performance on a real blockchain system (i.e., Hyperledger Fabric) deployed on a commodity platform (i.e., Amazon EC2). Experimental results demonstrated that 𝖧𝖺𝗋𝗉𝗈𝖼𝗋𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗌 \mathsf{Harpocrates} is highly scalable and achieves practical performance.

Introduction

Remote data storage systems have become predominant in the past decade due to the growth of cloud computing.
Cloud Service Providers (CSPs) have dedicated resources to store data, and have developed a suite of services to support it such as AWS S3, Azure Blobs, GCP Buckets.
The widespread use of cloud data storage has made it easy to share/access remote data.

Given that CSPs generally maintain a vast amount of user data,
many of which can be highly sensitive (e.g., personal, health),

A commitment scheme is

Threat and Security Models.

We assume participants do not trust each other, i.e., they can behave maliciously toward the records created by other participants.
The adversary is curious about the record content, e.g., who is the owner of a particular data, whom the data is shared with or accessed by, and what data is being shared or accessed.
The adversary may also attempt to modify the record to compromise validity or to gain unauthorized access.
We aim to achieve integrity and confidentiality properties for the audit log via
record non-malleability
and
audit log indistinguishability
as follows.

\mathcal{S}

Assign Ownership Protocol

\mathcal{S}
performs an assign ownership operation on receiving a
\mathsf{tk}_{\mathsf{str}}
.

\mathcal{S}
decrypts
\mathsf{tk}_{\mathsf{str}}
to identify which data needs to be assigned.
A serial number
\mathsf{sn}
is generated using the seed
\rho
(
Figure 3
, line
14
) in the store record.

\mathsf{sn}
uses a collision-resistant
\mathsf{PRF}
function to ensure that only one assignment is created for the corresponding store operation.
Without this,
\mathcal{S}
can use a single
\mathsf{tk}_{\mathsf{str}}
to maliciously perform assign operations to multiple data owners.
Subsequently,
\mathcal{S}
generates a commitment
\mathsf{cm}_{\mathsf{own}}\leftarrow\mathsf{Com}_{r^{\prime}}(\mathsf{pk}_{\mathsf{addr}}^{\mathcal{O}}||v^{\prime},\mathsf{pp})
(
Figure 3
, line
15
) to represent ownership assignment.
To successfully create an assign record,
\mathcal{S}
generates a proof of ownership
\pi_{\mathsf{own}}
(
Figure 3
, line
21
).
Verification of the proof shows that
\mathcal{S}
received a store request for the corresponding data.
The proof is generated by the following NP statements –

NP-statements for Data Owner Assignment.

Given an assign owner instance
x=(\mathsf{rt}_{\mathsf{str}},\mathsf{sn},\mathsf{cm}_{\mathsf{own}},h)
, the witness
w=(\mathsf{path}_{\mathsf{str}},\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{adr}}^{\mathcal{S}},\rho,r,v,\mathsf{pk}_{\mathsf{adr}}^{\mathcal{S}},r^{\prime},v^{\prime},\mathsf{pk}_{\mathsf{adr}}^{\mathcal{O}},h_{\mathsf{sig}})
is a valid witness for
x
if the following holds –

•

Commitment
\mathsf{cm}_{\mathsf{str}}
appears in the Merkle tree
\mathsf{rt}_{\mathsf{str}}
, i.e.,
\mathsf{rt}_{\mathsf{str}}=H(\dots(H(H(\mathsf{cm}_{\mathsf{str}})||\mathsf{path}_{\mathsf{str}}^{d})||\mathsf{path}_{\mathsf{str}}^{d-1})||\dots||\mathsf{path}_{\mathsf{str}}^{1}))
where
d
is the height of the Merkle tree and
\mathsf{path}_{\mathsf{str}}^{i}
represents the sibling node at height
i
.

•

Commitments
\mathsf{cm}_{\mathsf{str}}
and
\mathsf{cm}_{\mathsf{own}}
are well-formed.

•

Serial number
\mathsf{sn}
is computed correctly, i.e.,
\mathsf{sn}=\mathsf{PRF}(\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{adr}}^{\mathcal{S}}||\rho)

•

Values committed to
\mathsf{cm}_{\mathsf{str}}
and
\mathsf{cm}_{\mathsf{own}}
are the same.

•

The address secret key
\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{adr}}^{\mathcal{S}}
ties
h_{\mathsf{sig}}
to
h
.

We utilize Merkle trees to prove set membership i.e, the statement to prove
\mathsf{cm}_{\mathsf{str}}
appears in
\mathsf{rt}_{\mathsf{str}}
.
The tree is stored locally by the blockchain nodes and only the root is added to the record.
This is because we want to capture the state of the tree when the particular record was created.

\mathcal{S}
then creates an encrypted ownership token
\mathsf{tk}_{\mathsf{own}}
(
Figure 3
, line
22
).
Finally,
\mathcal{S}
signs record
\mathsf{tx}_{\mathsf{own}}
, using a one-time signing key, and appends it to the distributed audit log.

\mathcal{S}
also sends
\mathsf{tk}_{\mathsf{own}}
to
\mathcal{O}
.

VI
Implementation

We implemented
\mathsf{Harpocrates}
in Rust with 3,100 lines of code.
We used Hyperledger Fabric
[
25
]
to set up the audit log as the distributed ledger.
Each data operation was implemented as Hyperledger chaincode (equivalent to smart contracts) in Go.
For the signature scheme, we implemented Schnorr signature scheme
[
22
]
.
For the asymmetric encryption, we implemented ECIES with AES-GCM as the underlying symmetric encryption scheme
[
24
]
.
For the commitment scheme, we used Pedersen commitment
[
26
]
.
We implemented the Merkle trees with Poseidon hash function as it can efficiently compute hashes of two elements at once
[
27
]
.
We implemented MiMC as the primary hash function as well as PRF due to its low multiplicative complexity
[
23
]
.
Finally, our implementation made use of Bulletproofs
[
20
]
as the back-end zero-knowledge proof due to its small proof size and transparent setup.

We prove/verify statements in zero-knowledge for each data operation as follows –

Store.

We implemented a circuit with one Pedersen commitment opening gadget
\mathsf{PedG}
(based on
[
28
]
) to show knowledge of openings of a commitment and one hash pre-image gadget
\mathsf{HshG}
(based on
[
23
]
) to show knowledge of a pre-image of a MiMC hash.

Assign.

We implemented a circuit with one
\mathsf{MemG}
, two
\mathsf{PedG}
, and two
\mathsf{HshG}
. Here,
\mathsf{MemG}
is a Merkle tree based proof of membership gadget.

Share.

We implemented a circuit with one range gadget
\mathsf{RgeG}
, one
\mathsf{HshG}
, one
\mathsf{MemG}
, and two
\mathsf{PedG}
gadgets.
\mathsf{RgeG}
(based on
[
20
]
) is used to show knowledge that a value (
\mathsf{ts}
in our case) is within a given range.

Access.

We
implemented a circuit with
\mathsf{RgeG}
, one
\mathsf{HshG}
, one
\mathsf{MemG}
, and two
\mathsf{PedG}
gadgets.

Hardware.

We used 50 AWS EC2

instances to deploy a Hyperledger Fabric blockchain network.
Each instance had 4GB vRAM with a 2-core 3.1 GHz CPU (Intel Xeon) and network bandwidth of 4.3 Gbps.

We used standard parameters to achieve 128-bit security.
We used standard Poseidon Hash parameters for the Merkle tree including width

, full rounds

and partial rounds

We used MiMC hash function with rate

For the ECIES encryption scheme, our implementation used a 256-bit public and secret key.
We used Ed25519 curve of order

Circuit Complexity.

Recording store operations incur the least circuit complexity, compared with other operations such as assign, share, and access.
This is because performing a store operation only invokes one
\mathsf{PedG}
and one
\mathsf{HshG}

The constraint system of

Assign operation incurs a little higher circuit complexity than share and access because of an extra
\mathsf{HshG}

\mathsf{sn}

\mathsf{ts}

Processing Latency.

The processing latency depends on circuit complexity, especially the number of multiplication gates (
|M|
).
Store operation incurs a small latency compared with other operations because it does not require checking set membership, which attributes to large circuit complexity.
We include the Common Reference String (CRS) loading delay that is needed for the backend zero-knowledge proof (i.e., Bulletproofs), which takes around
1.5
seconds (s) in total.
Without CRS, the proving and verification time for store operation is 0.35 s and 85 milliseconds (ms), respectively, which are about 60 times lesser than assign, share, and access.
This matches their difference in
\lvert M\rvert
.

Memory Usage.

The memory usage also depends on the circuit complexity to prove each data operation.
The CRS component takes approximately
32
MB.
Proving incurs more memory usage than verification, due to the space needed to store the witness.

Bandwidth.

Since Bulletproof incurs a polylogarithmic proof size, the bandwidth overhead for each data operation is small.
The proof size produced by Bulletproofs is
2\lceil\log_{2}(|M|)\rceil+13
group elements.
For store,
|M|=644
so its proof contains
2\lceil\log_{2}(644)\rceil+13=33
group elements, which results in a total of
32\cdot 33=1,056
bytes (B).
For assign, share, and access operations,
|M|\approx 37,000
and, their proof size is 1,440 B.

VIII
Related Work

Centralized Audit Log.

Traditional data sharing/access schemes record data operations directly on the server that stores the data
[
29
,
30
]
.
Such records are needed for audit purposes, where a third-party auditor determines whether any unauthorized accesses have been made in the system
[
1
,
31
]
.
However, with the introduction of regulations like HIPAA a
secure logging
mechanism
[
10
]
is needed.
Secure logging primitives
[
11
,
32
]
rely on a trusted author whose signature is considered the root of trust for integrity or requires analyzing the entirety of the audit log to detect tampering due to the privacy-preserving nature of the records.
Alternatively, prior works
[
33
,
12
]
use secure hardware to achieve immutable and privacy-preserving records.
Since the audit log is maintained by the centralized server,
it is vulnerable to a single point of failure,
where the corrupted server can compromise
the confidentiality and integrity of the audit log.

Decentralized Audit Log.

Several works have proposed a decentralized audit log to address the single point of failure vulnerabilities.
These schemes were designed for medical data
[
3
,
13
]
, supply chain
[
15
,
16
]
, and financial applications
[
34
,
35
]
.
Some schemes permit tamper resistance and access control to data objects being logged
[
36
,
37
]
.
However, these approaches do not hide the user identities (e.g., sender/receiver).
Several works attempted to achieve either full anonymity
[
18
]
or partially anonymous
[
19
]
.
There are several schemes that permit the validity of audit log to be verified publicly
[
17
]
or privately
[
18
,
19
]
.
Private validity verifiability requires every user in the system to participate and prove the integrity of their records during the audit phase.

IX
Conclusion

We proposed
\mathsf{Harpocrates}
, a privacy-preserving and immutable audit log scheme.

\mathsf{Harpocrates}
achieved immutability and resistance to single point of failure by leveraging blockchain, and achieved public verifiability of record validity using zero-knowledge proofs.
We fully implemented
\mathsf{Harpocrates}
and evaluated its performance by using Hyperledger Fabric and Amazon EC2.
