A Determinantal Identity for the Permanent of a Rank 2 Matrix
Adam W. Marcus

Abstract
We prove an identity relating the permanent of a rank 2 2 matrix and the determinants of its Hadamard powers. When viewed in the right way, the resulting formula looks strikingly similar to an identity of Carlitz and Levine, suggesting the possibility that these are actually special cases of some more general identity (or class of identities) connecting permanents and determinants. The proof combines some basic facts from the theory of symmetric functions with an application of a famous theorem of Binet and Cauchy in linear algebra.



1
Introduction

The relationship between the
determinant
function, which maps a square
matrix
A
to

\det\left[A\right]=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}(-1)^{|\sigma|}\prod_{i=1}^{n}A(i,\sigma(i)),

and the
permanent
function, which maps a square matrix
B
to

~\mathrm{per}\left[B\right]=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}B(i,\sigma(i)),

is an important open problem in complexity theory.
Despite having similar forms, the computation of the determinant can be done
efficiently (due to, among other things, its predictable behavior with respect
to Gaussian
elimination) while the computation of the permanent is thought to be
significantly harder.
One of the more successful approaches to relating these two functions, known as

geometric complexity theory
, is to look for formulas of the type

~\mathrm{per}\left[M\right]=\det\left[M^{\prime}\right],

(1)

where
M
is a square matrix and
M^{\prime}
is a (typically much larger) square
matrix formed from affine combinations of the entries of
M
.
(The size of the matrix
M^{\prime}
that is needed for
(
1
) to hold can then be related to other measures of complexity
— we refer the reader to
[
3
]
for more details.)

For matrices of a specific form, however, it is sometimes possible to find a
formula that expresses the permanent of a matrix as the determinant of
other matrices
of the same size
.
One notable example of this is a result of Borchardt
[
2
]
that was
later generalized by Cayley
[
5
]
and then further generalized by
Carlitz and Levine
[
4
]
.
For a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with entries
m(i,j)
and integer
p
,
let
M_{p}
be the matrix with

M_{p}(i,j)=m(i,j)^{p}.

In this notation, the main result of
[
4
]
is the following theorem:

Theorem 1

(Carlitz–Levine)
.

For a rank 2 matrix
M
with no zero entries,

\det\left[M_{-2}\right]=\det\left[M_{-1}\right]~\mathrm{per}\left[M_{-1}\right].

(2)

The proof in
[
4
]
is elementary, using little more than the
definitions and some facts concerning the cycle structure of permutations.
The goal of this article is to prove a formula that has an intriguingly
similar form to (
2
) but for seemingly quite different reasons.
Our main result, Theorem
9
, states that for matrices
M
with rank
at most
2
,

(n!)^{2}\det\left[M_{n}\right]=(n^{n})\det\left[M_{n-1}\right]~\mathrm{per}\left[M_{1}\right].

(3)

The proof will use a combination of tools from the theory of polynomials and a
theorem of Binet and Cauchy on the minors of a product of matrices.

1.1
Motivation.

While the restriction to matrices of rank at most 2 may seem overly simplistic,
it should be noted that the permanents of such matrices appear naturally in the
context of binary operations that are symmetric in both arguments (or BOSBAs).
The author came across them, for example, during an investigation of the
characteristic polynomials of random matrices.
More specifically, let
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be Hermitian
matrices, and
consider
the (random) polynomial

r(x)=\det\left[xI-A-Q^{T}BQ\right],

where
Q
is an orthogonal matrix drawn uniformly from
\mathcal{O}_{n}
(the
group of
n\times n
orthogonal matrices) with respect to the Haar measure.

It is not hard to see that the value of
r(x)
is independent of the
eigenvectors of
B
and therefore must be a symmetric function of the
eigenvalues of
B
.
Conjugating by
Q
, one can see that the same is true for
A
as well.
That is, when viewed as a function of the eigenvalues of
A
(as the first
argument) and the eigenvalues of
B
(as the second argument),
r(x)
is a
BOSBA.
Furthermore, it can be shown that the expected value of this polynomial is the
permanent of a rank 2 matrix:

\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left\{\det\left[xI-A-Q^{T}BQ\right]\right\}=\frac{1}{n!}~\mathrm{per}\left[\{x-\lambda_{i}(A)-\lambda_{j}(B)\}_{i,j=1}^{n}\right],

where
\lambda_{i}(A)
denotes the eigenvalues of
A
(and similarly for
B
).

Expected characteristic polynomials of this type play an important role in the
field of
finite free probability
which has contributed to a
number of recent theoretical
[
7
,
9
]
and algorithmic
[
10
,
12
]
advances.
Since permanents are, by nature, often more difficult to work with than
determinants (they are not multiplicative, for example), the hope is that a
formula like
(
3
) could have uses (though,
admittedly, the author has not found any yet — see
Section
4
).

2
Preliminaries

We will use the customary notation that
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and that

\binom{[n]}{k}
denotes the collection of subsets of
[n]
size
k
.
For a permutation
\sigma
, we write
|\sigma|
to denote the number of cycles
in its cycle decomposition.
For a vector
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
, and a set
S\subseteq[n]
, we will write

\vec{x}^{S}:=\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}\and\sigma(S)=\{\sigma(i):i\in S\}.

In particular, given a matrix
M
and sets
I,J
, we will write
M(I,J)
to
denote the submatrix of
M
formed by the rows in
I
and columns in
J
.

2.1
Symmetric and alternating polynomials

A polynomial
p\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
is said to be
symmetric
if

p(x_{1},\dots x_{i},x_{i+1},\dots,x_{n})=p(x_{1},\dots x_{i+1},x_{i},\dots,x_{n})

and
alternating
if

p(x_{1},\dots x_{i},x_{i+1},\dots,x_{n})=-p(x_{1},\dots x_{i+1},x_{i},\dots,x_{n})

for all transpositions
(i,i+1)
.
Since the set of transpositions generates the symmetric group, equivalent
definitions are (for symmetric polynomials)

p(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=p(x_{\pi(1)},\dots,x_{\pi(n)})

and (for alternating polynomials)

p(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=(-1)^{|\pi|}p(x_{\pi(1)},\dots,x_{\pi(n)})

(4)

for all
\pi\in S_{n}
.
In particular, (
4
) implies that any alternating polynomial
p

must be
0
whenever
x_{i}=x_{j}
for some
i\neq j
.

Examples of symmetric polynomials are the
elementary symmetric polynomials

e_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\begin{cases}1&\text{for $k=0$,}\\
\sum\limits_{S\in\binom{[n]}{k}}\vec{x}^{S}&\text{for $1\leq k\leq n$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

and the
power sum polynomials

p_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{k}.

One example of an alternating polynomial is the
Vandermonde polynomial

\Delta(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\prod_{i<j}(x_{j}-x_{i}).

(5)

Furthermore, it is easy to see that the Vandermonde polynomial is an essential
part of any alternating polynomial:

Lemma 2
.

For all alternating polynomials
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
, there exists a symmetric
polynomial
t(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
such that

f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\Delta(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})t(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}).

Proof.

For distinct
y_{2},\dots,y_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, (
4
) implies that the
univariate polynomial

g(x)=f(x,y_{2},\dots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}[x]

satisfies
g(y_{k})=0
for each
k=2,\dots,n
.
Hence
(x-y_{k})
must be a factor of
g
and so
(x_{1}-x_{k})
must be a factor
of
f
.
Since this is true for all
k
and all
i
(not just
i=1
), every polynomial
of the form
(x_{i}-x_{k})
must be a factor of
f
, and so

f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\Delta(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})t(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

(6)

for some polynomial
t
.
It remains to show that
t
is symmetric.
To do so, for each
\sigma\in S_{n}
, we can apply (
6
) to
(
4
) to get

\Delta(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})t(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=(-1)^{|\sigma|}\Delta(x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma(n)})t(x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma(n)}).

Since
\Delta
is, itself, an alternating polynomial, we have

\Delta(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=(-1)^{|\sigma|}\Delta(x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma(n)})

and so canceling both sides results in

t(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=t(x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma(n)})

as needed.
∎

2.2
Linear algebra

The only tool we will need from linear algebra is a famous theorem of Binet and
Cauchy regarding the minors of products of matrices.
The
(I,J)
-minor
of a matrix
M
is defined as

\left|M\right|_{I,J}=\det\left[M(I,J)\right].

The following theorem is attributed to Binet and Cauchy (independently)

[
11
]
.

Theorem 3

(Cauchy–Binet)
.

Let
m,n,p
and
k
be positive integers for which
k\leq\min\{m,n,p\}
.
Then for all
m\times n
matrices
A
and
n\times p
matrices
B
and all
sets
I,J
with
|I|=|J|=k
, we have

\left|AB\right|_{I,J}=\sum_{K\in\binom{[n]}{k}}\left|A\right|_{I,K}\left|B\right|_{K,J}

(7)

For those unfamiliar with Theorem
3
, we hope to convey some
appreciation for it by pointing out that (
7
) simultaneously
generalizes two fundamental formulas from linear algebra that (a priori) have
no
obvious relation to each other: the formula for matrix multiplication (the case
when
k=1
), and the product formula for determinants (the case when

m=n=p=k
).

3
The main theorem

Our approach will be to first prove (
3
) in a special case
(Corollary
8
) and to then extend that result to the full theorem.
We start by finding an expansion for the permanent of certain matrices in terms
of the elementary symmetric polynomials:

Lemma 4
.

For vectors
\vec{u},\vec{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, if
A
is the
n\times n
matrix with

A(i,j)=1+u_{i}v_{j},

then

~\mathrm{per}\left[A\right]=\sum_{k=0}^{n}k!(n-k)!e_{k}(\vec{u})e_{k}(\vec{v}).

Proof.

By definition, we have

~\mathrm{per}\left[A\right]=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+u_{i}v_{\sigma(i)})

where for each
\sigma
, we have

\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+u_{i}v_{\sigma(i)})=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\vec{u}^{S}\vec{v}^{\,\sigma(S)}.

For fixed
S
with
|S|=k
, as
\sigma
ranges over all permutations,

\sigma(S)
will range over all sets
T\in\binom{[n]}{k}
and any
\sigma^{\prime}

for which
\sigma^{\prime}(S)=\sigma(S)
will give the same term.
As there are a total of
k!(n-k)!
such permutations, we have

~\mathrm{per}\left[A\right]=\sum_{k=0}^{n}k!(n-k)!\sum_{S\in\binom{[n]}{k}}\sum_{T\in\binom{[n]}{k}}\vec{u}^{S}\vec{v}^{T}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}k!(n-k)!e_{k}(\vec{u})e_{k}(\vec{v})

as claimed.
∎

For a vector
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
\{Q_{\vec{x}}^{k}\}_{k=0}^{n}
be the
collection of
n\times n
matrices with entries

{Q_{\vec{x}}^{k}}(i,j)=\begin{cases}x_{j}^{i}&\text{if $i>k$,}\\
x_{j}^{i-1}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(8)

For example, when
\vec{x}=(a,b,c)
, we have

{Q_{\vec{x}}^{0}}=\begin{bmatrix}a&b&c\\
a^{2}&b^{2}&c^{2}\\
a^{3}&b^{3}&c^{3}\end{bmatrix},~{Q_{\vec{x}}^{1}}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\
a^{2}&b^{2}&c^{2}\\
a^{3}&b^{3}&c^{3}\end{bmatrix},~\dots~,~{Q_{\vec{x}}^{3}}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\
a&b&c\\
a^{2}&b^{2}&c^{2}\end{bmatrix}.

Lemma 5
.

For any vector
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\det\left[{Q_{\vec{x}}^{k}}\right]=e_{n-k}(\vec{x})\Delta(\vec{x}).

Proof.

It should be clear from the properties of determinants that each function

f_{k}(\vec{x}):=\det\left[{Q_{\vec{x}}^{k}}\right]

is an alternating polynomial.
Hence, by Lemma
2
, we have that

f_{k}(\vec{x})=t_{k}(\vec{x})\Delta(\vec{x})

for some symmetric function
t_{k}
, so it remains to show that
t_{k}=e_{k}
.

We start by considering degrees.
Note that each
f_{k}(\vec{x})
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree

\binom{n+1}{2}-k
and that
\Delta(\vec{x})
is a homogeneous
polynomial of degree
\binom{n}{2}
.
Hence
t_{k}(\vec{x})
must be a homogeneous polynomial of degree
n-k
.
Now note that the degree of each
x_{i}
in
f_{k}(\vec{x})
is at
most
n
, whereas the the degree of each
x_{i}
in
\Delta(\vec{x})
is

n-1
.
Hence the degree of each
x_{i}
in
t_{k}(\vec{x})
is at most
1
.

The only symmetric polynomials that satisfy these constraints are multiples of
the elementary symmetric polynomials, and so we must have

f_{k}(\vec{x})=w_{k}e_{n-k}(\vec{x})\Delta(\vec{x})

for some constant
w_{k}
.
However, one can check that we must have
w_{k}=1
by looking at the monomial
formed by the product of the diagonal entries in
{Q_{\vec{x}}^{k}}
and
seeing that it is positive.
∎

We will now use Lemma
5
to prove two corollaries.
The first corollary appears in the solution to Problem 293(a) in
[
6
]

(and we suspect
the authors were aware of Lemma
5
though they did not explicitly
state it).

Corollary 6
.

For vectors
\vec{u},\vec{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, if
B
is the
n\times n

matrix with entries

B(i,j)=(1+u_{i}v_{j})^{n-1},

then

\det\left[B\right]=\Delta(\vec{u})\Delta(\vec{v})\left(\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{j}\right).

Proof.

Let
Y
be the
n\times n
diagonal matrix with entries
Y(i,i)=\binom{n-1}{i-1}
.
It is easy to check that

B={Q_{\vec{u}}^{n}}^{\intercal}Y{Q_{\vec{v}}^{n}}

and so by the product rule for the determinant, we have

\det\left[B\right]=\det\left[{Q_{\vec{u}}^{n}}\right]\det\left[Y\right]\det\left[{Q_{\vec{v}}^{n}}\right].

Lemma
5
then completes the proof.
∎

The second corollary is similar in spirit to the first one, but requires the
added machinery of Theorem
3
.

Corollary 7
.

For vectors
\vec{u},\vec{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, if
C
is the
n\times n
matrix with
entries

C(i,j)=(1+u_{i}v_{j})^{n},

then

\det\left[C\right]=\Delta(\vec{u})\Delta(\vec{v})\left(\prod_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\right)\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{e_{k}(\vec{u})e_{k}(\vec{v})}{\binom{n}{k}}.

Proof.

Let
\widehat{Q}_{\vec{x}}
be the
n\times(n+1)
matrix with entries

\widehat{Q}_{\vec{x}}(i,j)=x_{i}^{j-1}

and let
Z
be the
(n+1)\times(n+1)
diagonal matrix with
Z(i,i)=\binom{n}{i-1}
.
Then we have
C=\widehat{Q}_{\vec{u}}Z\widehat{Q}_{\vec{v}}^{\intercal}

and so we can use Cauchy–Binet (twice) to expand
\det\left[C\right]
:

\displaystyle\det\left[C\right]

\displaystyle=\left|\widehat{Q}_{\vec{u}}Z\widehat{Q}_{\vec{v}}^{\intercal}\right|_{[n],[n]}=\sum_{K\in\binom{[n+1]}{n}}\left|\widehat{Q}_{\vec{u}}\right|_{[n],K}\left|Z\widehat{Q}_{\vec{v}}^{\intercal}\right|_{K,[n]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K,L\in\binom{[n+1]}{n}}\left|\widehat{Q}_{\vec{u}}\right|_{[n],K}\left|Z\right|_{K,L}\left|\widehat{Q}_{\vec{v}}^{\intercal}\right|_{L,[n]}.

(9)

To simplify (
9
), we note that since
Z
is diagonal,

\left|Z\right|_{K,L}=0
unless

K=L
.
Furthermore, note that the complement of each set
K
contains
a single element.
When that element is
p\in[n+1]
, we have

\left|\widehat{Q}_{\vec{x}}\right|_{[n],K}=\left|{Q_{\vec{x}}^{p}}\right|\and\left|Z\right|_{K,K}=\frac{\det\left[Z\right]}{\binom{n}{p-1}}.

The result then follows from Lemma
5
.

∎

Putting the three corollaries together gives us the following:

Corollary 8
.

For vectors
\vec{u},\vec{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, if
A,B,C
are
n\times n
matrices
with entries

A(i,j)=1+u_{i}v_{j},\quad B(i,j)=(1+u_{i}v_{j})^{n-1},\and C(i,j)=(1+u_{i}v_{j})^{n},

then

(n!)^{2}\det\left[C\right]=(n^{n})\det\left[B\right]~\mathrm{per}\left[A\right].

Proof.

Combining Lemma
4
and
Corollary
7
gives

n!\det\left[C\right]=\Delta(\vec{u})\Delta(\vec{v})\left(\prod_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\right)~\mathrm{per}\left[A\right],

where we have

\prod_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{n}{j}\binom{n-1}{j-1}=\frac{n^{n}}{n!}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\binom{n-1}{j-1}=\frac{n^{n}}{n!}\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{j}.

Plugging in Corollary
6
gives the result.
∎

Finally, we extend Corollary
8
to the case of any rank
2
matrix.

Theorem 9
.

Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be any rank
2
matrix and let
X_{n-1},X_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be the matrices with

X_{n-1}(i,j)=X(i,j)^{n-1}\and X_{n}(i,j)=X(i,j)^{n}.

Then

(n!)^{2}\det\left[X_{n}\right]=(n^{n})\det\left[X_{n-1}\right]~\mathrm{per}\left[X\right].

Proof.

First note that if we let
\vec{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the vector with
\vec{1}(k)=1

for all
k
, then Corollary
8
proves the theorem in the case that

X=\vec{u}\otimes\vec{v}+\vec{1}\otimes\vec{1}.

Now let
Y=\vec{u}\otimes\vec{v}+\vec{w}\otimes\vec{x}
for general
\vec{w},\vec{x}
.
The expansion of
\det\left[Y_{n}\right]
in terms of monomials has the form

\det\left[Y_{n}\right]=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{n},j_{1},\dots,j_{n}}c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{n},j_{1},\dots,j_{n}}\prod_{k}u_{k}^{i_{k}}w_{k}^{n-i_{k}}v_{k}^{j_{k}}x_{k}^{n-j_{k}}

(10)

where the
c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{n},j_{1},\dots,j_{n}}
are constants.
Similarly,
\det\left[Y_{n-1}\right]~\mathrm{per}\left[Y\right]
has an expansion

\det\left[Y_{n-1}\right]~\mathrm{per}\left[Y\right]=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{n},j_{1},\dots,j_{n}}\widehat{c}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{n},j_{1},\dots,j_{n}}\prod_{k}u_{k}^{i_{k}}w_{k}^{n-i_{k}}v_{k}^{j_{k}}x_{k}^{n-j_{k}}

(11)

for some constants
\widehat{c}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{n},j_{1},\dots,j_{n}}
.
Plugging in
\vec{x}=\vec{1}
and
\vec{w}=\vec{1}
, however, does not cause any of the
coefficients to combine.
That is,

\det\left[X_{n}\right]=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{n},j_{1},\dots,j_{n}}c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{n},j_{1},\dots,j_{n}}\prod_{k}u_{k}^{i_{k}}v_{k}^{j_{k}}

and

\det\left[X_{n-1}\right]~\mathrm{per}\left[X\right]=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{n},j_{1},\dots,j_{n}}\widehat{c}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{n},j_{1},\dots,j_{n}}\prod_{k}u_{k}^{i_{k}}v_{k}^{j_{k}},

and so by Corollary
8
, we have

(n!)^{2}c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{n},j_{1},\dots,j_{n}}=(n^{n})\widehat{c}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{n},j_{1},\dots,j_{n}}

for all indices
i_{1},\dots,i_{n}
and
j_{1},\dots,j_{n}
.
Plugging this into (
10
) and (
11
) implies equality for
Y
.
∎

4
Conclusion

It is worth noting that Theorem
9
does not seem to be useful
algorithmically.
For the purpose of computing permanents, it tends to be slower than the
algorithm of Barvinok
[
1
]
and also runs into stability issues
whenever the two determinants approach
0
(in particular, when the original
matrix
X
is actually rank 1).

Returning to the motivation discussed in Section
1
, the
author discovered Theorem
9
in a (failed) attempt to prove the
following conjecture, which would have useful implications in the
field of finite free probability:

Conjecture 10
.

For any matrix
T
with rank at most
2
,

~\mathrm{per}\left[\begin{matrix}T&T\\
T&T\end{matrix}\right]\leq\binom{2n}{n}~\mathrm{per}\left[T\right]^{2}.

A proof is known in the case that the entries of
T
are all
positive, but the general case seems harder.

4.1
Further research

There are two obvious directions for extending Theorem
9
,
both of which would be quite
interesting.
First, the structural similarity between (
2
) and (
3
)
suggests that a larger class of formulas of the type

c(n)\det[M_{i}]=\det\left[M_{j}\right]~\mathrm{per}\left[M_{k}\right]

could exist for matrices
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
of a certain type (in
our case, rank 2).
Second, one can ask if there is a determinantal formula similar to
(
3
) that is capable of computing the permanent of a rank
3
matrix.
In this regard, it is worth mentioning that the author’s original proof of
Theorem
9
used a formula of Jacobi that relates the determinants
of the
{Q_{\vec{x}}^{k}}
matrices defined in (
8
) to
Schur
polynomials

[
8
]
.
The author opted for the current presentation, which was suggested by an
anonymous referee, due to its elegance.
For the purposes of extension, however, we mention the connection to Schur
polynomials as a possible approach.

A final (but far more speculative) research direction lies in the fundamental
relationship between the permanent and determinant functions.
In particular, one can ask whether different models for geometric complexity
theory could lead to new and interesting results.
Equations (
2
) and (
3
) suggest two possible
generalizations.
First, one could attempt to satisfy (
1
) with a matrix
M^{\prime}

whose entries are
polynomials
in the
entries of
M
(instead of just affine combinations).
Second, one could try to replace (
1
) with an equation of the
form

\det\left[M^{\prime\prime}\right]~\mathrm{per}\left[M\right]=\det\left[M^{\prime}\right],

where both
M^{\prime\prime}
and
M^{\prime}
are affine (or polynomial) combinations of the
entries of
M
.
It is unclear what the immediate implications of either generalization would
be, but given the central importance of complexity theory in computer science,
one would expect that any new results of this type would be quite interesting.
