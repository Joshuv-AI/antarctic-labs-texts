Abstract
In this chapter we provide an introduction to fractional dissipative partial differential equations (PDEs) with a focus on trying to understand their dynamics. The class of PDEs we focus on are reaction-diffusion equations but we also provide an outlook on closely related classes of PDEs. To simplify the exposition, we only discuss the cases of fractional time derivatives and fractional space derivatives in the PDE separately. As our main tools, we describe analytical as well as numerical methods, which are generically necessary to study nonlinear dynamics. We start with the analytical study of steady states and local linear stability for fractional time derivatives. Then we extend this view to a global perspective and consider time-fractional PDEs and gradient flows. Next, we continue to steady states, linear stability analysis and bifurcations for space-fractional PDEs. As a final analytical consideration we discuss existence and stability of traveling waves for space-fractional PDEs. In the last parts, we provide numerical discretization schemes for fractional (dissipative) PDEs and we utilize these techniques within numerical continuation in applied examples of fractional reaction-diffusion PDEs. We conclude with a brief summary and outlook on open questions in the field.

Introduction

Reaction-diffusion partial differential equations (PDEs) are a crucial modeling tool appearing in all the natural sciences, engineering and beyond
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. In particular, reaction-diffusion PDEs are relevant at very small length scales up to very large macroscopic length scales. The existence, uniqueness and regularity theory for large classes of reaction-diffusion PDEs is quite classical by now and many aspects are well-understood
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
. Yet, asking for a more precise description of the qualitative and quantitative dynamics of quite small classes of reaction-diffusion PDEs, a lot of questions appear that have led to very active research areas. There are few absolutely complete and comprehensive results about the dynamics of reaction-diffusion PDEs. This is expected as such a dynamical classification does not even exist for nonlinear ODEs formed by the reaction terms. Yet, for the case with enough dissipativity, there is a well-established strategy that can be applied. First, one uses dissipativity to establish boundedness of solutions, often obtaining the existence of a (finite-dimensional) attractor as a consequence of global dissipation
[
9
,
10
]
. Unfortunately, just having the existence of an attractor yields relatively little information on the precise dynamics, so one then proceeds to study finer information via invariant sets and patterns
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
. A first step in this direction is the analysis of steady states. Often one starts with homogeneous steady states and then proceeds to more complex steady states using complementary methods, e.g., bifurcation theory
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
and variational methods
[
17
]
. Once steady states are found, one continues to investigate their local linear stability via classical methods such as spectral theory
[
18
]
. A next step is the investigation of non-stationary patterns such as traveling waves
[
12
]
, which are effectively invariant steady sets in a moving frame. Again, one then studies the stability of traveling waves
[
19
,
20
]
. To cross-validate and globally extend dynamical results, it is often imperative to combine analytical approaches with numerical techniques, which are based upon spatial discretization combined with temporal simulation or numerical continuation methods
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
.

To illustrate this classical paradigm, let us consider a typical example given by the PDE

\partial_{t}u=\partial_{x}^{2}u+u(p_{1}-u)(u-p_{2})=:\partial_{x}^{2}u+f(u;p),\quad u_{0}(x)=u(0,x),

(1)

where
u=u(t,x)
,
x\in\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}
,
t\geq 0
, and
p=(p_{1},p_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
are parameters. The reaction-diffusion PDE (
1
) has many different names in different fields: Fisher equation with Allee effect
[
3
]
, Allen-Cahn equation
[
24
]
, Chafee-Infante equation
[
25
]
, Nagumo equation
[
26
]
, real Ginzburg-Landau equation
[
27
]
, Schlögl model
[
28
]
, and (overdamped)
\Phi^{4}
model
[
29
]
. This clearly demonstrates the importance of (
1
) as a key example, or even as a normal form
[
30
]
, for many dynamical phenomena. One easily checks that (
1
) is dissipative due to the
-u^{3}
term in the nonlinearity
[
9
,
10
]
. For the case of a bounded domain
\Omega=(a,b)
,
a<b
, one obtains a finite-dimensional attractor
[
31
,
32
,
10
,
9
]
. For concreteness, let us consider homogeneous Neumann boundary data

\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(a)=0=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(b).

(2)

In this case, we immediately spot the three homogeneous equilibria
u_{*,0}(x)\equiv 0
,
u_{*,1}=p_{1}
,
u_{*,2}=p_{2}
. The standard configuration for the Allen-Cahn equation is to fix
p_{2}=-p_{1}
and set
p_{1}^{2}=:p_{AC}
so that the nonlinearity becomes
p_{AC}u-u^{3}
. Even in this simple case, the homogeneous state
u_{*,0}
undergoes bifurcations as
p_{AC}
is increased generating non-homogenous/patterned steady states in the process
[
11
,
15
,
12
]
. Note that linear stability of any steady state on a
\Omega=(a,b)
reduces to the study of the eigenvalue problem

({\textnormal{D}}_{u}f)(u_{*,\cdot};p)U=\lambda U,\qquad U=U(x),

where
{\textnormal{D}}_{u}
denotes the Fréchet derivative of
f
on a suitable function space
\mathcal{X}
, e.g., one could consider
\mathcal{X}=H^{1}(\Omega)=W^{1,2}(\Omega)
. Computation and visualization of the non-homogeneous steady states is possible locally near bifurcation points. Globally, the
situation is already very difficult and it helps to employ numerical tools
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
. A natural step is to spatially discretize the steady state problem, e.g., using finite elements, finite differences or a spectral Galerkin method. Then one can apply path-following techniques to generate global bifurcation diagrams
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
.

For the case of an unbounded domain
\Omega=\mathbb{R}
and the standard Nagumo parameter choices
p_{1}=1
and
p_{2}=p_{Nag}\in(0,1)
, it is well-known that the PDE (
1
) has traveling wave solutions
[
33
,
19
,
12
]
of the form

u(t,x)=u(x-st)=u(\zeta)

(3)

for a wave speed
s=s(p_{Nag})
. In fact, the ansatz (
3
) just results in a second-order ODE. One can prove that this ODE has a parametric family of heteroclinic orbits corresponding to traveling front solutions and a parametric family of homoclinic orbits corresponding to traveling pulse solutions. One can then prove using linearization and Sturm-Liouville theory that the traveling front is linearly (and in fact also nonlinearly) stable, while the traveling pulse is already linearly unstable
[
33
,
19
,
12
]
. To visualize the traveling wave dynamics, one option is to just combine spatial discretization on a very
large domain
(a,b)
with
a\ll-1
and
b\gg 1
with a time discretization. Alternatively, one could freeze the wave by using a suitable reference frame
[
34
]
.

Although the theory of (
1
) is very detailed, an important question for applications is its robustness. This not only means proving stability results in phase space but also refers to structural stability of the model itself, i.e., if the PDE class is changed and/or extended, do dynamical phenomena persist or do new effects appear? Before answering this question, we have to consider the input from applications. Which extensions to reaction-diffusion PDEs do occur in practice? One answer is provided by going back to the microscopic derivation of diffusion. In particular, the Laplacian
\Delta
as well as the associated heat operator
\partial_{t}-\Delta
can arise from taking a macroscopic limit of a random walk described by a stochastic process
z(t)
. If the stochastic process satisfies a power law for the mean-squared displacement

\mathbb{E}[z(t)^{2}]\sim 2\kappa_{\alpha}t^{\alpha},

then one easily calculates that a standard random walk has the exponent
\alpha=1
. The case
\alpha=1
could also be called “classical” diffusion. Classical diffusion is a Markovian and a Gaussian/Wiener process. Note that
\kappa_{1}=:\sigma
becomes the diffusion constant of the mean-field limit diffusion equation

\partial_{t}u=\sigma\Delta u

for the probability density
u(t,x)
of the random walker to be at position
x
at time
t
. Yet, if
\alpha>1
, then the behavior is super-diffusive (or “ballistic”), while
\alpha\in(0,1)
gives subdiffusive dynamics. The cases
\alpha\neq 1
are also called anomalous diffusion
[
35
]
and lead to different macroscopic operators beyond the standard heat equation; we will define these fractional differential operators, as well as several additional ones, in Section
2
. Once all these additional fractional operators arising from various applications have been collected, we can try to proceed to study dissipative fractional PDEs along the same lines as the classical example (
1
) presented above.

The remaining part of this review is structured as follows. In Section
2
we fix the notation we use for this chapter. In Section
3
we study the extension of steady states and linear stability with a focus on fractional time derivatives. In Section
4
we take a global perspective and consider time-fractional reaction-diffusion equations and their associated gradient flows. In Section
5
we briefly recap linear stability analysis and bifurcations for space-fractional derivatives. In Section
6
, we survey results for traveling waves for space-fractional PDEs. In Section
7
, we give an overview for numerical discretization schemes for fractional (dissipative) PDEs, while in Section
8
, we show how these techniques can be embedded into numerical continuation in practical examples of fractional reaction-diffusion PDEs arising in applications. In Section
9
, we provide a brief summary and outlook towards interesting open problems.

We stress that our review provides only a partial snapshot of the field of dynamics of fractional dissipative PDEs. It is probably already impossible to cover the field comprehensively as it has grown so quickly in recent years. Yet we hope that this chapter makes it much easier to find an entry point to the results and techniques in the field, even beyond the results we mention here. Furthermore, we believe that the snapshot of results discussed here clearly indicates that the general approach to study dissipative reaction-diffusion PDEs can be lifted from classical to fractional derivatives. Yet, the techniques and phenomena are often substantially different, which makes it an exciting area to explore.

2.1
Time-Fractional Derivative(s)

First, we define the Riemann-Liouville fractional integral and derivative for the subdiffusion range
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Let
f(t)\in\mathnormal{L}^{1}(0,T)
for any
T>0
. The fractional Riemann-Liouville integral of order
\alpha
is defined as

(\mathfrak{D}_{0,t}^{-\alpha}f)(t):=(k_{\alpha}\ast f)(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad\alpha\in(0,1),

(4)

where the (Laplace) convolution kernel is
k_{\alpha}(t):=t^{\alpha-1}/\Gamma(\alpha)
, and
\Gamma(\alpha)
is the Gamma function. We can also generalize the time dependence of the integrals yielding the natural definitions

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}^{-\alpha}_{t_{0},t}f(t):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,\qquad\mathfrak{D}^{-\alpha}_{t,T}f(t):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{t}^{T}(s-t)^{\alpha-1}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Notably, for
\alpha=1
we get
(\mathfrak{D}_{0,t}^{-\alpha}f)(t)=\int_{0}^{t}f(s)\mathrm{d}s
. For
f:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
the Riemann-Liouville fractional derivative of order
\alpha
is defined as

(\mathfrak{D}_{0,t}^{\alpha}f)(t):=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(k_{1-\alpha}\ast f)(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad\alpha\in(0,1),

(5)

with the obvious generalizations to other endpoints
t_{0}
,
T
, which is then reflected in the notation by adding suitable indices. Notably, the Riemann-Liouville fractional derivative is nonzero on constants,

(\mathfrak{D}_{0,t}^{\alpha}1)(t)=k_{1-\alpha}(t),

which tends to zero as
t^{-\alpha}
for
t\to\infty
and is unbounded for
t\to 0
. At
\alpha=0
,
\mathfrak{D}_{0,t}^{\alpha}
is the identity operator, i.e.,
(\mathfrak{D}_{0,t}^{\alpha}f)(t)=f(t)
, while for
\alpha=1
,
(\mathfrak{D}_{0,t}^{\alpha}f)(t)
formally yields
(\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}t)\left(\delta\ast f\right)(t)=f^{\prime}(t)
with Dirac delta distribution
\delta(t)
and
{}^{\prime}:=\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}t
. A simple sufficient condition for the existence of the Riemann-Liouville fractional derivative is as follows.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
36
, Lemma 2.2]
)
.

Let
f(t)\in\mathnormal{AC}([0,T])
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Then
(\mathfrak{D}_{0,t}^{\alpha}f)(t)
exists almost everywhere. Moreover
(\mathfrak{D}_{0,t}^{\alpha}f)(t)\in\mathnormal{L}^{p}(0,T)
,
1\leq p<1/\alpha
, and

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{D}_{0,t}^{\alpha}f)(t)=(k_{1-\alpha}\ast f^{\prime})(t)+k_{1-\alpha}(t)f(0).

Here
\mathnormal{AC}([0,T])
is the space of functions
f
which are absolutely continuous on
[0,T]
, i.e.,
f(t)=c+\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\mathrm{d}s
for some
g\in\mathnormal{L}^{1}(0,T)
and constant
c
. This lemma motivates the definition of the (Djrbashian-)Caputo fractional derivative. For
f\in\mathnormal{AC}([0,T])
the
(Djrbashian-)Caputo fractional derivative
of order
\alpha
is defined as

(\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}_{0,t}^{\alpha}f)(t):=(k_{1-\alpha}\ast f^{\prime})(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}f^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad\alpha\in(0,1).

(6)

Hence, for sufficiently smooth
f:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
, Lemma
2.1
gives a well-known relation between Riemann-Liouville and Caputo derivatives

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}_{0,t}^{\alpha}f(t)=\mathfrak{D}_{0,t}^{\alpha}(f(t)-f(0)).

One can extend these definitions also beyond the case
\alpha\in(0,1)
to any
\alpha>0
. Define
m:=\lceil\alpha\rceil\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
for
\alpha\not\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and
m:=\alpha+1
for
\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
.
Then, the Riemann-Liouville derivatives are given by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}^{\alpha}_{0,t}f(t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\mathrm{d}^{m}}{\mathrm{d}t^{m}}\mathfrak{D}^{-(m-\alpha)}_{0,t}f(t),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}^{\alpha}_{t,T}f(t)

\displaystyle:=(-1)^{m}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{m}}{\mathrm{d}t^{m}}\mathfrak{D}^{-(m-\alpha)}_{t,T}f(t).

The left and right Caputo derivatives are given by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{\tilde{D}}^{\alpha}_{0,t}f(t)

\displaystyle:=\mathfrak{D}^{-(m-\alpha)}_{0,t}f^{(m)}(t),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{\tilde{D}}^{\alpha}_{t,T}f(t)

\displaystyle:=(-1)^{m}\mathfrak{D}^{-(m-\alpha)}_{t,T}f^{(m)}(t),

where one requires sufficient regularity, in terms of a generalization of Lemma
2.1
to ensure existence of the derivatives. The Riemann-Liouville and the Caputo fractional derivatives are related to each other: For sufficiently smooth
f
, one has

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}^{\alpha}_{0,t}f(t)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{\tilde{D}}^{\alpha}_{0,t}u+\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\frac{f^{(k)}(0)(t-0)^{k-\alpha}}{\Gamma(k+1-\alpha)}.

2.2
Space-Fractional Derivative(s)

We start with the case of the spatial domain
\Omega=\mathbb{R}
. In experiments asymmetric superdiffusive behavior has been observed in many applications
[
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
,
41
]
(see also the references in
[
42
,
43
]
and
[
44
, Part 3]
). Using continuous time random walks (CTRW) with an asymmetric distribution of jump lengths allows one to derive evolution equations with asymmetric Lévy operators
[
45
, §3.3.3]
. In this context, a very general class of space-fractional derivatives is derived microscopically from Lévy operators. A subclass of Lévy operators are the Riesz–Feller operators, which are the infinitesimal generators of stable Lévy processes; see
[
46
, §14]
and
[
47
]
. In the one-dimensional setting, Riesz–Feller operators can be characterized by two parameters: the index of stability
\gamma
and another parameter
\theta
measuring the asymmetry. Riesz–Feller operators can be defined as Fourier multiplier operators

\mathcal{F}[\operatorname{D}_{\theta}^{\gamma}u](\xi)=\psi^{\gamma}_{\theta}(\xi)\ \mathcal{F}[u](\xi),\qquad\xi\in\mathbb{R},

with
\mathcal{F}
denoting the Fourier transform and symbol

\psi^{\gamma}_{\theta}(\xi)=-|\xi|^{\gamma}\exp\left[{\textnormal{i}}(\mathrm{sgn}(\xi))\theta\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right]\quad\text{ for }\,0<\gamma\leq 2\,\text{ and }\,|\theta|\leq\min\{\gamma,2-\gamma\}.

We will require enough regularity for
u=u(x)
with
x\in\mathbb{R}
so that we can define its Fourier transform. Special cases for Riesz-Feller operators are the second order derivative
\operatorname{D}_{0}^{2}=\partial^{2}_{x}{}
, the (negative) fractional Laplacians
\operatorname{D}_{0}^{\gamma}=-(-\partial^{2}_{x})^{\gamma/2}
for
0<\gamma\leq 2
and
\theta=0
(which are the only symmetric Riesz–Feller operators), and Weyl fractional derivatives
\operatorname{D}_{2-\gamma}^{\gamma}
for
0<\gamma<2
and
\theta=2-\gamma
(which are extremely asymmetric Riesz–Feller operators).

For the higher-dimensional setting
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we will only need the (positive) fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta_{x})^{\gamma/2}
,
\gamma\in(0,2)
. In full space fractional Laplacians
(-\Delta_{x})^{\gamma/2}
can be defined in various equivalent ways, see, e.g.,
[
48
]
:
For example, again as Fourier multiplier operators

\mathcal{F}[(-\Delta_{x})^{\gamma/2}u](\xi)=|\xi|^{\gamma}\ \mathcal{F}[u](\xi),\qquad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

with symbol
|\xi|^{\gamma}
. Or, we can define them as singular integral operators in two different ways:

(-\Delta_{x})^{\gamma/2}u(x):=c_{d,\gamma}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,{\frac{-u(x+y)-u(x-y)+2u(x)}{2|y|^{d+\gamma}}}\,\,\operatorname{d}\!y\,=c_{d,\gamma}P.V.\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{d+\gamma}}\,dy,

(7)

where P.V. indicates that a principal value integral is considered, and the normalizing constant

c_{d,\gamma}:=\frac{2^{\gamma}\Gamma\big(\tfrac{d+\gamma}{2}\big)}{\pi^{d/2}\big|\Gamma\big(-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\big)\big|}

(8)

is such that the singular integral operators have the Fourier symbol
|\xi|^{\gamma}
. On bounded domains, the situation becomes more complicated and one has to carefully distinguish different definitions for the fractional Laplacian, e.g., the spectral fractional Laplacian (SFL) and the integral fractional Laplacian (IFL). These operators coincide on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, but they differ when restricted to bounded domains
\Omega
. To define the Dirichlet spectral fractional Laplacian let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain and let
(\phi_{i},\lambda_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}\times{\mathbb{R}}
be the eigenpairs of

\Delta\phi=\lambda\phi\quad\mbox{ in $\Omega$},\quad\phi|_{\partial\Omega}=0

(9)

with the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}\rightarrow-\infty
sorted in descending order and normalized such that
\|\phi_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1
,
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Note that
(\phi_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
forms an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\Omega)
. The (positive) Dirichlet spectral fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{\gamma/2}
is defined as the operator
u\mapsto(-\Delta)^{\gamma/2}u(x):=\sum_{i}(-\lambda_{i})^{\gamma/2}(u,\phi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\phi_{i}(x)
. Note that
\gamma=0
reproduces the identity operator and
\gamma=2
the standard Laplacian. The operator is an isomorphism
\widetilde{H}^{\gamma/2}(\Omega)\rightarrow H^{-\gamma/2}(\Omega):=(\widetilde{H}^{\gamma/2}(\Omega))^{\prime}
, where for
\beta\in[0,1]
the fractional Sobolev space

\widetilde{H}^{\beta}(\Omega)
is the closure of
C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
under the norm

\|u\|_{\widetilde{H}^{\beta}(\Omega)}:=\left(\sum_{i}(-\lambda_{i})^{\beta}|(u,\phi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(10)

(See
[
49
, Chap. 3]
for alternative, equivalent norms on
\widetilde{H}^{\beta}(\Omega)
.) The Dirichlet spectral fractional Laplacian is based on Dirichlet boundary conditions in (
9
). Replacing the Dirichlet conditions in (
9
) by Neumann boundary conditions leads to a similar operator, the Neumann spectral fractional Laplacian. Throughout the text we will abbreviate

\Delta^{\gamma/2}:=-(-\Delta)^{\gamma/2}

for the integral and the spectral fractional (both Dirichlet and Neumann) Laplacian. Note that this is consistent with the 1D operator
\operatorname{D}_{0}^{\gamma}
introduced earlier.

Remark
.

In the literature there is sometimes confusion regarding the signs of “the” fractional Laplacian. This issue already arises for the classical Laplacian as some authors prefer to have it as “negative” operator while others prefer a “positive” operator. This is just a matter of sign conventions. In dynamical systems it is more common to have the classical Laplacian with nonpositive eigenvalues to indicate that there is decay for the heat semigroup in accordance with the principle that stability is associated with negative real parts for elements in the spectrum. So we will adopt this convention here, which explains our sign conventions.

For further general references regarding fractional calculus, we refer to
[
50
,
36
]
, which cover the univariate case in significant detail. We refer to
[
44
]
for a good survey of further theoretical and numerical aspects of fractional differential operators.

3
Steady States & Linear Stability: Time Fractional PDEs

In case of subdiffusion
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the waiting time probability density function from the continuous time random walk modelling is non-exponential and yields a non-Markovian process with memory. Arguing via Fourier and Laplace transforms, e.g.,
[
35
,
51
,
52
]
, one derives what we refer to here as the subdiffusion equation for
u=u(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
t>0
,

\partial_{t}u=\mathfrak{D}_{0,t}^{1-\alpha}\sigma\partial_{x}^{2}u,

(11)

with diffusion coefficient
\sigma
and time-fractional Riemann-Liouville derivative
\mathfrak{D}_{0,t}^{1-\alpha}
. This non-local convolution operator in time with power law kernel entails that the system has memory. Acting by the inverse of
\mathfrak{D}_{0,t}^{1-\alpha}
on (
11
) yields the distinct but mathematically equivalent form
[
53
]

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}_{0,t}^{\alpha}u=\sigma\partial_{x}^{2}u,

(12)

with the Caputo fractional derivative
\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}_{0,t}^{\alpha}
. However, care has to be taken in the functional analytic interpretation of (
11
) and (
12
), in particular regarding continuity of solutions at
t=0

[
52
]
. Indeed, replacing the left hand side of (
12
) with
\mathfrak{D}_{0,t}^{\alpha}(u-u_{0})
gives an alternative form, which requires less smoothness of
u
with
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)

[
35
,
54
]
. The Cauchy problems of (
11
) and (
12
) are also equivalent to an integro-differential equation that was first investigated via Mellin transforms
[
55
]
, which we do not discuss further here; more details on the comparisons can be found in
[
56
]
. In all cases, the integral memory means that equations with subdiffusion cannot be viewed as dynamical systems on the phase space of the natural initial condition
u(0,x)
. Starting at time
t>0
requires suitably prescribing
u(s,x)
on the elapsed time interval
0\leq s\leq t
, so that such equations may be viewed as equations with monotonically increasing delay. Moreover, as in general for non-autonomous equations, we expect that solutions can cross the initial state and stability properties are not necessarily determined by the spectrum of a time-independent linear operator.

3.1
The Linear Subdiffusion Equation

In order to highlight essential differences between classical diffusion and subdiffusion we consider the basic subdiffusion equation in the Riemann-Liouville form (
11
). Compared to the heat equation, it is more subtle to see that solutions of (
11
) remain positive, and to determine the growth and decay properties. We briefly outline some aspects and refer to, e.g.,
[
35
]
for further details and different perspectives involving the Fox-function. The Green’s function of (
11
) has the Fourier transform
[
35
, eq. 49]

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\Phi(t,q)=E_{\alpha}(-\sigma q^{2}t^{\alpha}),

(13)

where
E_{\alpha}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{n}/\Gamma(1+n\alpha)
is the Mittag-Leffler function, and
q
the Fourier-wavenumber. The function (
13
) has the asymptotic behavior
[
57
, eq. 20]

\displaystyle E_{\alpha}(-\sigma q^{2}t^{\alpha})\sim\begin{cases}\exp(-\frac{\sigma q^{2}t^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)}),&t\ll(\sigma q^{2})^{1/\alpha},\\
(\sigma q^{2}t^{\alpha}\Gamma(1-\alpha))^{-1},&t\gg(\sigma q^{2})^{1/\alpha}.\end{cases}

(14)

Here we see an effect of memory for nonzero wavenumber: exponential decay for relatively short times, and algebraic decay for long times, here with power
-\alpha
. The separation of these depends in particular on the wavenumber. The algebraic decay effectively replaces the exponential decay of Fourier modes for the classical heat equation, and illustrates that linear stability for processes with subdiffusion has a different character from that for classical diffusion. On the one hand, this behavior is also foreshadowed in Fourier-Laplace space, for which we view the right-hand side of (
11
) realized as an operator with space-time domain
\mathnormal{AC}([0,T];\mathnormal{H}^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
into
\mathnormal{L}^{1}(0,T;\mathnormal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
. With the Laplace transformation
\mathfrak{L}
we obtain

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{L}\mathcal{F}{\Phi})(s,q)=(s+\sigma q^{2}s^{1-\alpha})^{-1},\quad{\textnormal{Re}}(s)>0,

(15)

where
s\in\mathbb{C}
is the Laplace space variable. Indeed, one expects that the branch point of (
15
) at the origin creates algebraic decay in the inverse Laplace transform. This analysis will be the topic of Section
3.3
for reaction subdiffusion models and directly relates to decay in fractional linear ODE. This is always algebraic with rate
-\alpha
, in contrast to classical ODE, where decay is exponential for eigenvalues with negative real parts, cf.
[
58
,
59
]
. On the other hand, in
x
-space a scaling analysis shows that the parabolic scaling of the classical heat equation is replaced in (
11
) by a fractional self-similar scaling: if
u(t,x)
solves (

) then so does

for
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
. Based on the similarity variable

a series expansion of the Green’s function can be found (e.g.
[

, eq. 46]
,
[
60
, eq. 4.23]
),

\Phi(t,x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\sigma t^{\alpha}}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!\Gamma\left(1-\alpha(n+1)/2\right)}\left(\frac{|x|}{\sqrt{\sigma t^{\alpha}}}\right)^{n},\quad t>0.

(16)

Via this form one can find that

has an expression in terms of the Wright function
[
61
]
and is therefore positive
[
60
]
and decays algebraically locally uniformly in

for

with power
-\alpha/2

[
62
,
63
,
64
]
. In addition, we remark that, using the method of separation of variables, it was shown in
[
65
]
that solutions to the Cauchy problem (

) with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition decay pointwise algebraically with power

for

, i.e., faster than on the unbounded domain, which was also found to be optimal in the more general case (

) below. A more detailed discussion of the Mittag-Leffler and Wright functions, their estimates and relation to fractional calculus can be found in
[
52
]
; we also refer to
[

,
60
,
67
]
for further details of the Wright functions serving as the Green’s functions to various (space-)time fractional diffusion equations.

We also briefly outline some aspects of the subdiffusion equation in the Caputo form (

). The formulation via
\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}_{0,t}^{\alpha}
motivates to consider a more general form of time fractional evolution equations with a convolution
(k\ast v)(t)=\int_{0}^{t}k(t-s)v(s)ds
,
t\geq 0
for a kernel

, generalizing (
12
) to

k\ast\partial_{t}u=\sigma\partial_{x}^{2}u.

(17)

The theory for Cauchy-problems in (
12
) and (

) is by now quite well developed and an excellent introduction is provided by
[
52
]
. For (

) we particularly refer to
[
68
]
and

, which provide decay estimates on bounded and unbounded domains, respectively. For instance, in the subdiffusion case with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition the decay estimate of solutions has a different character, and exponential decay of Fourier modes is replaced by algebraic decay with rate

, consistent with the separable solutions to (

); moreover,
[

]
proves

-decay with a fixed rate

for any space dimension. On unbounded domains, the character of algebraic decay persists, but the

-decay depends on the spatial dimension

: the rate is slower than that on the bounded domain for

and is fixed at

for

, cf.
[
69
]
. Hence, the decay rate on the bounded domain provides a lower bound for the case of the unbounded domain in any space dimension. In contrast to the classical diffusion, the subdiffusion is so slow that increasing the spatial dimensions (particularly for

) and restricting to bounded domains does not increase the decay rate. We also refer to
[
54
]
for various analytical aspects of time-fractional diffusion equations. In addition, to the subdiffusion equation, various examples of kernels

can be found in
[
68
]
. Lastly, we remark that the subdiffusion equations of the forms (

3.2
Subdiffusion-Reaction Models

Modelling additional reactions and also the extension to systems is a subtle task that can lead to rather different results. Most approaches come as subdiffusion-limited (S) or activation-limited (A) reactions.

S: Subdiffusion limited.

In the more straightforward and broadly studied class (S), either the non-local operator acts on the diffusion terms as well as the reaction terms
[
70
,
71
,
72
,
73
,
74
,
75
]
, or one simply adds an extra source or sink to the right hand side of (
12
)
[
76
]
, so that in the scalar case we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u=\mathfrak{D}_{0,t}^{1-\alpha}[\sigma\partial_{x}^{2}u+f(u)]\quad\text{or}\quad\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}_{0,t}^{\alpha}u=\sigma\partial_{x}^{2}u+f(u),

(18)

with
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. Here the reaction is “anomalous” even without subdiffusive transport
\sigma=0
and for spatially uniform states. Analogously, adding a nonlinear source term to (
17
) gives rise to a time fractional evolution equation

k\ast\partial_{t}u=\sigma\partial_{x}^{2}u+f(u).

(19)

The theory for Cauchy-problems in (
18
) is well developed (mostly on bounded domains) and an excellent introduction is provided by
[
52
]
, for which we also refer to the numerous references in this active field. The initial-boundary value problem of (
19
) and even more general semilinear diffusion cases have been considered in
[
76
,
77
]
, which provide well-posedness and stability theory. The existence problem of steady states in (
18
) and (
19
) is exactly the same as for classical reaction-diffusion systems, although the stability properties may differ. For instance, the linear stability principle has a different character, and exponential decay in the stable case is replaced by algebraic decay with rate
-\alpha
. Indeed, (
18
) for linear
f(u)=bu
and for a Fourier mode
\tilde{q}
such that
b<\sigma\tilde{q}^{2}
has the same form as the subdiffusion equation (
11
) for the Fourier mode
q=\sqrt{\tilde{q}^{2}-b/\sigma}
, consistent with the power
-\alpha
in (
14
). There are numerous studies of asymptotic stability for equations of the form (
19
); we refer to
[
76
,
77
]
as abstract studies and the references therein. In addition, some recent studies concern the asymptotic stability for modified forms of (
19
), e.g., with fractional Laplacian
[
78
]
or nonlinearity involving infinite delays
[
79
]
. Another difference in stability properties is that spatially constant steady states tend to be spectrally more stable against non-steady perturbations in the presence of subdiffusion of class (S) than classical diffusion, e.g.
[
58
,
59
,
80
]
. We refer to
[
59
]
for a recent account of stability in fractional ODE.

Both aspects, i.e., non-expoential decay and (de)stabilizing effect, also arise for the less considered class (A) on which we focus in the following. Here the reaction kinetics is not affected by subdiffusive transport, i.e., any nonlocal time-dependent operator acts on the diffusion terms only
[
81
,
70
,
82
,
83
,
75
]
. Within this class of models we distinguish the two cases of added source/sink (A
add
) and linear creation/annihilation (A
lin
) as detailed next.

A
add
: subdiffusion with added source/sink.

Akin to classical reaction-diffusion models, following
[
81
]
, in this case one simply adds an extra source or sink to the right-hand side of (
11
). More generally, for a possibly nonlinear reaction term
f(u)
one thus obtains

\partial_{t}u=\sigma\mathfrak{D}_{0,t}^{1-\alpha}\partial_{x}^{2}u+f(u).

(20)

While for the classical diffusion at
\alpha=1
the usual maximum principle holds, the Green’s function for (
20
) with negative linear reaction dynamics, e.g.,
f(u)=-u
, partially takes negative values, cf.
[
70
]
. Heuristically, the reason is that the sink can remove particles much faster than they can be transported from other positions. Hence, (
20
) is not a model for an absolute density
u
, see also
[
84
]
. However, when
u
represents a density perturbation from a saturated strictly positive state this phenomenon is acceptable, at least for short times. Within (
20
) this would concern
u\approx u_{*}
, where
u_{*}>0
solves
f(u_{*})=0
. A corresponding ad hoc model, cf.
[
85
]
, for multiple species is

\partial_{t}{U}=\Sigma\mathfrak{D}_{0,t}^{1-\alpha}\partial_{x}^{2}{U}+{F}({U}),

(21)

where
{U}={U}(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the vector of the perturbation densities,
\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is the diffusion matrix with positive entries, and
{F}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a possibly nonlinear function that models the reaction kinetics.

A
lin
: subdiffusion with linear creation/annihilation.

In this case, the addition or removal of particles through (linear) reactions occurs only during the waiting time
[
70
]
and the resulting scalar equation with reaction rate
a
reads

\partial_{t}u=\sigma e^{at}\mathfrak{D}_{0,t}^{1-\alpha}(e^{-at}\partial_{x}^{2}u)+au.

From the corresponding integral form one can infer that this model preserves positivity of solutions since the amount of removed particles is less than the amount of existing particles. For multiple species and reaction rate matrix
{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
the model

\partial_{t}{U}=\Sigma e^{{A}t}\mathfrak{D}_{0,t}^{1-\alpha}(e^{-{A}t}\partial_{x}^{2}{U})+{A}{U},

(22)

has been proposed in
[
86
,
84
]
, and rigorously derived in
[
87
]
. Since the matrix exponentials are suitable for linear reactions only, the form of an appropriate model for nonlinear reactions is not clear. We also refer to
[
84
]
for a discussion of the modelling of reaction subdiffusion equations.

3.3
Spectra, Growth and Decay

The Fourier-Laplace transform can be employed also for systems to derive dispersion relations of temporal and spatial modes, analogous to the classical reaction diffusion systems. We illustrate decay and growth in subdiffusion from this viewpoint. In contrast to classical diffusion, the fractional powers in the dispersion relation yield branch points, and we already alluded to the related algebraic decay in the subdiffusion equation in Section
3.1
. Also generally, the branch points strongly impact the decay laws determined by an inverse Laplace transform, and moreover the decay properties of models (A
add
) and (A
lin
) differ significantly, although the models are equal in absence of reactions. In addition, the solutions to the dispersion relation to the left of the most unstable branch point in the complex plane depend on the choice of branch cut, and following
[
64
]
we therefore refer to these solutions as pseudo-spectrum; when there is no ambiguity in this section we refer to all solutions of dispersion relations (in the admissible domain) as pseudo-spectrum. Non-canonical branch cuts admit to relate pseudo-spectrum to regular spectrum of the classical diffusion equations through the limit
\alpha\to 1
. For illustration, we consider two-component systems and start with classical diffusion, i.e., a system

\partial_{t}{U}=\Sigma\partial_{x}^{2}{U}+{F}({U}),

(23)

where
{U}={U}(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the vector of density of the species with
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
t>0
,
{F}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
represents the reaction kinetics, and
\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(1,\sigma)
is the diagonal matrix of positive constant diffusion coefficients. Here the first diffusion coefficient is normalized to
1
by a suitable scaling of
x
so that
\sigma
is in fact the ratio of diffusion coefficients. Suppose that (
23
) possesses a spatially homogeneous steady state
{U}_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, i.e.,
{F}({U}_{*})=0
. The linearization of (
23
) about
{U}_{*}
is given by

\partial_{t}{U}=\Sigma\partial_{x}^{2}{U}+{A}{U},\quad{A}:=\left({\textnormal{D}}_{U}{F}({U}_{*})\right)=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}\\
a_{3}&a_{4}\end{pmatrix}.

(24)

The right-hand side of (
24
) defines the linear operator
\mathcal{L}:=\Sigma\partial_{x}^{2}+{A}
, with eigenvalue problem

\mathcal{L}{U}=\Sigma\partial_{x}^{2}{U}+{A}{U}=s{U}.

Upon Fourier transforming
x\in\mathbb{R}
to Fourier-wavenumbers
q\in\mathbb{R}
, the eigenvalue problem becomes

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{F}{U})=-q^{2}\Sigma(\mathcal{F}{U})+{A}(\mathcal{F}{U})=s(\mathcal{F}{U}),

whose solvability condition as a matrix eigenvalue problem yields the dispersion relation

D_{{\rm reg}}(s,q^{2}):=\det(s{I}+q^{2}\Sigma-{A})=(s+q^{2}-a_{1})(s+\sigma q^{2}-a_{4})-a_{2}a_{3}=0.

(25)

The solution set
\Lambda_{\rm reg}:=\{s\in\mathbb{C}:D_{{\rm reg}}(s,q^{2})=0\text{ for a }q\in\mathbb{R}\}
defines in particular the
\mathnormal{L}^{2}
-spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
with domain
(\mathnormal{H}^{2}(\mathbb{R}))^{2}
, e.g.,
[
88
]
, which we refer to as regular spectrum. The homogeneous steady state
{U}_{*}
is called strictly spectrally stable (unstable) if
\max({\textnormal{Re}}(\Lambda_{\rm reg}))<0
(
>0
). It is then also linearly and nonlinearly stable (unstable) for (
24
) and (
23
), respectively
[
88
]
.

Dispersion relation for A
add
:

Proceeding in the same way for (
21
) gives

(s{I}+s^{1-\alpha}q^{2}\Sigma-{A})\,\mathfrak{L}\mathcal{F}{U}=\mathcal{F}{U}_{0},\quad{\textnormal{Re}}(s)>0,\ q\in\mathbb{R},

with the constraint on
s
due to the branch point of
s^{1-\alpha}
at the origin. Hence, the analogue of (
25
) is the dispersion relation on
\Omega_{0}^{+}:=\{s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}:\arg(s)\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)\}
given by

\displaystyle D_{\rm add}(s,q^{2})

\displaystyle:=\det(s{I}+s^{1-\alpha}q^{2}\Sigma-{A})=(s+s^{1-\alpha}q^{2}-a_{1})(s+s^{1-\alpha}\sigma q^{2}-a_{4})-a_{2}a_{3}=0.

For any given
q\in\mathbb{R}
we can extend the domain of
D_{\rm add}
on the principal branch such that the values on positive reals are preserved. Due to the discrete solution set, we can choose
\theta_{1}(q)\in(0,\pi/2)
such that there is no solution of the dispersion relation on the branch cut

\mathfrak{B}_{0}^{\theta_{1}(q)}:=\{s\in\mathbb{C}:{\textnormal{Im}}(s)/{\textnormal{Re}}(s)=\tan(\theta_{1}(q)),\,{\textnormal{Re}}(s)<0\},

i.e.,
D_{\rm add}(s,q^{2})\neq 0
for
s\in\mathfrak{B}_{0}^{\theta_{1}(q)}
. For simplicity we suppress the dependence of
\theta_{1}
on
q
, and obtain the extended domain of
D_{\rm add}
as

\Omega_{0}:=\{s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}:\arg(s)\in(-\pi+\theta_{1},\pi+\theta_{1}),\,\theta_{1}\in(0,\pi/2)\}.

The pseudo-spectrum
\Lambda_{\rm add}
is the corresponding set of roots of
D_{\rm add}
on
\Omega_{0}
.

Dispersion relation for A
lin
:

For (
22
) we follow
[
86
,
64
,
87
]
and make the simplifying assumption that
{A}
is diagonalizable, so that with the eigenvalues
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
of
{A}
, the coordinate change

{P}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{2}&a_{2}\\
\mu_{1}-a_{1}&\mu_{2}-a_{1}\end{pmatrix},\quad{P}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{a_{1}-\mu_{2}}{a_{2}(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})}&\frac{1}{\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}}\\
-\frac{a_{1}-\mu_{1}}{a_{2}(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})}&-\frac{1}{\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}}\end{pmatrix}

gives
\bar{{A}}:={P}^{-1}{A}{P}=\mathrm{diag}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
. We order the eigenvalues so that
{\textnormal{Re}}(\mu_{1})\geq{\textnormal{Re}}(\mu_{2})
. Setting
{U}={P}{W}
turns (
22
) into

\partial_{t}{W}=\bar{\Sigma}e^{\bar{{A}}t}\mathfrak{D}_{0,t}^{1-\alpha}(e^{-\bar{{A}}t}\partial_{x}^{2}{W})+\bar{{A}}{W},\quad\bar{\Sigma}=\begin{pmatrix}d_{1}&d_{2}\\
d_{3}&d_{4}\end{pmatrix}:={P}^{-1}\Sigma{P}.

Fourier-Laplace transforming then gives the dispersion relation

\begin{split}D_{\rm lin}(s,q^{2}):=\ &\det(s{I}-\bar{{A}}+(s{I}-\bar{{A}})^{1-\alpha}\bar{\Sigma}q^{2})=\det((s{I}-\bar{{A}})^{1-\alpha})\det((s{I}-\bar{{A}})^{\alpha}+\bar{\Sigma}q^{2})\\
=\ &(s-\mu_{1})^{1-\alpha}(s-\mu_{2})^{1-\alpha}\big(((s-\mu_{1})^{\alpha}+d_{1}q^{2})((s-\mu_{2})^{\alpha}+d_{4}q^{2})-d_{2}d_{3}q^{4}\big)=0,\end{split}

where
{\textnormal{Re}}(s-\mu_{1}),\,{\textnormal{Re}}(s-\mu_{2})>0
. As above, we may extend the domain to
s\in\Omega_{\rm lin}\subset\mathbb{C}
by choosing branch cuts that connect
\infty
to the finite branch points
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
, respectively. It turns out, cf.
[
64
]
, that
s=\infty
is a branch point of
(s-\mu_{1})^{\alpha}(s-\mu_{2})^{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in(0,1/2)\cup(1/2,1)
; for
\alpha=1/2
we can think of
\infty
as an intermediate point on the branch cut connecting
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
. For simplicity, we assume real
\mu_{1}>\mu_{2}
; the general case can be found in
[
64
]
. In order to keep non-integer powers of positive reals on the positive real axis, we then choose branch cuts
\mathfrak{B}_{\mu_{j}}^{\theta_{j}}
,
j=1,2
, such that

\Omega_{\rm lin}:=\{s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\}:\arg(s-\mu_{j})\in(-\pi+\theta_{j},\pi+\theta_{j}),\ \theta_{j}\in(0,\pi/2),\ j=1,2\}.

As for (A
add
), given
q\in\mathbb{R}
we select
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
such that the solutions of
D_{\rm lin}(\cdot,q^{2})=0
do not lie on the branch cuts. The pseudo-spectrum
\Lambda_{\rm lin}
is the corresponding set of roots of
D_{\rm lin}
on
\Omega_{\rm lin}
.

Remark
.

We illustrate the special nature of rational
1-\alpha=n/m\in\mathbb{Q}
for the non-trivial factor of
D_{\rm lin}
,

\displaystyle D_{{\rm lin}2}(s,q^{2})

\displaystyle:=\big((s-\mu_{1})^{\alpha}+d_{1}q^{2}\big)\big((s-\mu_{2})^{\alpha}+d_{4}q^{2}\big)-d_{2}d_{3}q^{4}=0,\quad s\in\Omega_{\rm lin}.

In the rational case it can be recast as a polynomial with respect to the two variables

,

, of degree

\tilde{D}_{{\rm lin}2}(z_{1},z_{2},q^{2})=(z_{1}^{m-n}+d_{1}q^{2})(z_{2}^{m-n}+d_{4}q^{2})-d_{2}d_{3}q^{4}.

By resultant theory, the polynomial system

has a finite number of roots

, which gives a finite number of roots

for

, including multiplicities.

3.4
Turing-type instability

Concerning the onset of instability, models (

occurs with a unique finite wavenumber

, while

has eigenvalues with strictly negative real parts, i.e.,

via the well known instability condition

, e.g.,
[

]
. Turing instability for class (S) is well studied: roughly speaking, the subdiffusion does not have an effect on the steady instability threshold; we refer to
[

,

,
80

for further details. However, this is not the case for models of class (A).

A

For (

is Turing unstable, then the spectrum for all

is unstable. Somewhat counterintuitively, this means that, in terms of the diffusion ratio

\sigma_{\text{add}}
for (A

for any

the grow rate at

for some numerical explorations.

A

For (

, cf.

4
Gradient Flows: Time Fractional PDEs

In the last section, we have seen how to analyze dissipative subdiffusion equations locally by focusing on spectra and bifurcations. In this section, we describe the natural complementary view on this class of differential equations via global gradient flow techniques. Gradient flows have been vigorously studied in the field of nonlinear PDEs. An abstract form of gradient flows in an appropriate space
{\mathcal{X}}
(e.g., Hilbert and Banach spaces) reads,

\partial_{t}u(t)=-\nabla E(u(t))\ \mbox{ for }\ t>0,\quad u(0)=u_{0},

(26)

where
\partial_{t}=\text{\rm d}/\text{\rm d}t
,
u:[0,\infty)\to{\mathcal{X}}
is a gradient flow emanating from an initial datum
u_{0}
and
\nabla E
denotes a functional derivative in a proper sense (e.g., Fréchet derivative and subdifferential) of an energy (or entropy) functional
E:{\mathcal{X}}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}:=[-\infty,\infty]
. There are many parabolic equations (systems), e.g., Cahn-Hilliard system, Allen-Cahn equation, nonlinear diffusion equations such as porous medium and fast diffusion equations, many of phase transition models, which admit variational structures of gradient flow type. One of crucial features of gradient flows is the decrease (in time) of the energy
t\mapsto E(u(t))
. Moreover, any elements of the
\omega
-limit set of each gradient flow are equilibria, i.e., critical points of the functional
E
. On the other hand, the non-emptiness of the
\omega
-limit set is nontrivial, and moreover, even if it is nonempty, it is rather delicate whether or not the
\omega
-limit set is singleton.

This section is concerned with a fractional gradient flow, which is a variant of gradient flows with time-fractional derivatives instead of the classical one, say

\partial_{t}^{\alpha}(u-u_{0})(t)=-\nabla E(u(t))\ \mbox{ for }\ t>0,

(27)

where
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\partial_{t}^{\alpha}:=\mathfrak{D}_{0,t}^{\alpha}
is a shorthand notation for the Riemann-Liouville derivative throughout this section.

4.1
Brief History

The study of fractional gradient flows may date back to the 1970s, when the following nonlinear Volterra equation was studied extensively:

u(t)+(a*\xi)(t)=g(t),\quad\xi(t)\in A(u(t)),\quad t>0,

(28)

where

is a (possibly multivalued) nonlinear operator in a Hilbert or Banach space
{\mathcal{X}}
,

is given and

yields (

which can also be regarded as a nonlocal (in time) perturbation problem of a (local) nonlinear evolution equation. We also refer the reader to
[

99

100

4.2
Abstract Theory for Fractional Gradient Flows

In this subsection, we consider a fractional gradient flow of subdifferential type in a Hilbert space. To this end, we set up a couple of notions: Let
H
be a real Hilbert space and let
\phi:H\to(-\infty,+\infty]
be a proper (i.e.,
\phi\not\equiv+\infty
) lower-semicontinuous convex functional with
effective domain

D(\phi):=\{w\in H\colon\phi(w)<+\infty\}
. Then the subdifferential operator
\partial\phi:H\to 2^{H}
(here
2^{H}
denotes the power set of
H
) is defined by

\displaystyle\partial\phi(w)=\{\xi\in H\colon\phi(v)-\phi(w)\geq(\xi,v-w)_{H}\ \mbox{ for }v\in D(\phi)\}\ \mbox{ for }w\in D(\phi)

with domain
D(\partial\phi):=\{w\in D(\phi)\colon\partial\phi(w)\neq\emptyset\}
. The subdifferential is a generalized notion of the Fréchet derivative for convex functionals, and it is necessary to establish an abstract theory which is applicable to PDEs having gradient flow structures. The Cauchy problem for a fractional gradient flow reads,

-\partial_{t}^{\alpha}(u-u_{0})(t)\in\partial\phi(u(t))\ \mbox{ for }\ t>0,

(32)

where
u_{0}\in D(\phi)
is a prescribed initial datum. We recall the Riemann-Liouville kernel

k_{\beta}(t):=\frac{t^{\beta-1}}{\Gamma(\beta)}

of order
\beta\in(0,1)
and
\partial_{t}^{\alpha}(u-u_{0})=\mathfrak{D}_{0,t}^{\alpha}(u-u_{0})=\partial_{t}[k_{1-\alpha}*(u-u_{0})]
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
(see also (
4
)). We are then concerned with strong solutions to (
32
) defined as

Definition 4.1

(Strong solution)
.

For
0<T<\infty
, a function
u\in L^{2}(0,T;H)
is called a strong solution to (
32
) on
[0,T]
if the following conditions are all satisfied:

(i)

k_{1-\alpha}*(u-u_{0})\in W^{1,2}(0,T;H)
and
[k_{1-\alpha}*(u-u_{0})](0)=0
,

(ii)

u(t)\in D(\partial\phi)
for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
and

-\partial_{t}^{\alpha}(u-u_{0})(t)\in\partial\phi(u(t))

for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
.

Moreover, a function
u\in L^{2}_{\rm loc}([0,\infty);H)
is called a global strong solution to (
32
) if for every

the restriction
u|_{[0,T]}
of

to
[0,T]
is a strong solution to (
32
) on
[0,T]
.

Define the space
H^{\alpha,2}_{0}(0,T;H)
by

H^{\alpha,2}_{0}(0,T;H)=\{w|_{[0,T]}\colon w\in H^{\alpha,2}(\mathbb{R};H)\ \mbox{ and }\ \mathrm{supp}\,w\subset[0,\infty)\},

where
H^{\alpha,2}(\mathbb{R};H)\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R};H)
denotes the Bessel potential function space of order
\alpha
. Then the main result of this section is stated as follows:

Theorem 4.2

(
[
121
, Theorem 2.3, Corollary 2.7]
)
.

For every
u_{0}\in D(\phi)
, the Cauchy problem admits a unique global strong solution
u\in L^{2}_{\rm loc}([0,\infty);H)
such that the following holds true
:

(i)

Set
\mathcal{F}(t):=\left[k_{1-\alpha}*\left(\phi(u(\cdot))-\phi(u_{0})\right)\right](t)
for

. Then the function

is continuous on

, and it holds that

\displaystyle\int^{t}_{s}\left\|\partial_{t}^{\alpha}(u-u_{0})(\tau)\right\|_{H}^{2}\,\text{\rm d}\tau+\mathcal{F}(t)\leq\mathcal{F}(s)

for all
0\leq s\leq t<\infty
.

(ii)

It holds that
u\in H^{\alpha,2}_{0}(0,T;H)
for
0<T<\infty
, and moreover,

u\in\begin{cases}L^{\infty}(0,T;H)&\mbox{ if }\ \alpha\in(0,1/2],\\
C([0,T];H)&\mbox{ if }\ \alpha>1/2\end{cases}

(33)

for
0<T<\infty
. In particular, if
\alpha>1/2
, then the initial condition

holds true in a classical sense.

(iii)

It holds that
\phi(u(\cdot))\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty)
and
k_{\alpha}*\|\partial_{t}^{\alpha}(u-u_{0})\|_{H}^{2}\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty)
, and moreover,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(k_{\alpha}*\|\partial_{t}^{\alpha}(u-u_{0})\|_{H}^{2}\right)(t)+\phi(u(t))\leq\phi(u_{0})

for a.e.

(iv)

Let

be a global strong solution to (

. Then it holds that

\displaystyle\|u_{1}(t)-u_{2}(t)\|_{H}\leq\|u_{0,1}-u_{0,2}\|_{H}

Remark
.

(i)

Even for

, one can still construct a function satisfying all the requirements except

for strong solutions to (

(ii)

From the theorem above, we know that
\phi(u(t))\leq\phi(u_{0})
for a.e.

; however, it may still be open to question whether

is nonincreasing or not. On the other hand, the nonlocal quantity
\mathcal{F}(t)
is surely continuous and nonincreasing in

(iii)

The assertions except (iv) of Theorem
4.2
can also be extended to

\phi:H\to(-\infty,+\infty]

We briefly review two key ingredients of the proof for Theorem

[

\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(0,T;H)
and define an operator

by

\displaystyle D(\mathcal{B})

\displaystyle=\{w\in\mathcal{H}\colon k_{1-\alpha}*w\in W^{1,2}(0,T;H),\ (k_{1-\alpha}*w)(0)=0\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(u)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\left(k_{1-\alpha}*w\right)\ \mbox{ for }\ w\in D(\mathcal{B}).

Then

4.4
Towards Dynamics of Fractional Gradient Flows

Recall that due to the nonlocal nature of fractional derivatives in time, one cannot expect a classical semigroup property of the solution operator
S(t):u_{0}\mapsto u(t)
associated with the fractional gradient flow (
32
). On the other hand, we may think of a trajectory approach as follows: We denote

I:=\left\{[0,t]\colon t\geq 0\right\}

and

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Sol}=D(\phi)\cup\{u:[0,t]\to D(\phi)\colon u\mbox{ is a strong solution of \eqref{cp} on }[0,t]

\displaystyle\mbox{for some initial datum }u_{0}\in D(\phi)\mbox{ and }t>0\}.

Moreover, we define an operator
U:I\times D(\phi)\to\mathfrak{Sol}
by

U([0,t],u_{0}):=\begin{cases}u_{0}&\mbox{ if }\ t=0,\\
u&\mbox{ if }\ t>0,\end{cases}

where
u
denotes the strong solution of (
32
) on
[0,t]
with the initial datum
u_{0}
, and furthermore, we generate a map
\overline{U([0,t],u_{0})}
from
(-\infty,0]
into
D(\phi)
by

\overline{U([0,t],u_{0})}(\cdot):=\begin{cases}U([0,t],u_{0})(\cdot+t)&\mbox{ if }\ \cdot\in[-t,0],\\
u_{0}&\mbox{ if }\ \cdot\not\in[-t,0]\end{cases}

for
t>0
and
\overline{U(\{0\},u_{0})}(\cdot)\equiv u_{0}
for
t=0
. Define a phase space
\mathfrak{X}
by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{X}=\left\{\overline{U([0,t],u_{0})}\colon u_{0}\in D(\phi),\ t\geq 0\right\}.

For each
\tau\geq 0
, we define an evolution operator
T_{\tau}:\mathfrak{X}\to\mathfrak{X}
by

T_{\tau}(\overline{U([0,t],u_{0})})=\overline{U([0,t+\tau],u_{0})}

for each
\overline{U([0,t],u_{0})}\in\mathfrak{X}
. Then we immediately observe that

T_{0}(\overline{U([0,t],u_{0})})=\overline{U([0,t],u_{0})},

and moreover, for
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\geq 0
, we find that

\displaystyle{T_{\tau_{2}}\circ T_{\tau_{1}}(\overline{U([0,t],u_{0})})}

\displaystyle=T_{\tau_{2}}(\overline{U([0,t+\tau_{1}],u_{0})})

\displaystyle=\overline{U([0,t+\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}],u_{0})}=T_{\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}}(\overline{U([0,t],u_{0})}).

Hence the semigroup property
T_{\tau_{1}}\circ T_{\tau_{2}}=T_{\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}}
holds true. Furthermore, we can also define a functional
\mathcal{E}:\mathfrak{X}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\overline{U([0,t],u_{0})})

\displaystyle:=\left[k_{1-\alpha}*\big(\phi(U([0,t],u_{0})(\cdot))-\phi(u_{0})\big)\right](t)

\displaystyle=\left[k_{1-\alpha}*\big(\phi(u(\cdot))-\phi(u_{0})\big)\right](t),

which is decreasing in

. Therefore we obtain

\mathcal{E}(\overline{U([0,t],u_{0})})\leq\mathcal{E}(\overline{U([0,s],u_{0})})

if

. That is,
\mathcal{E}
is a Lyapunov functional for the dynamical system
(\mathfrak{X},(T_{\tau})_{\tau\geq 0})
.

5
Steady States & Linear Stability: Space Fractional PDEs

In Section
3
, we have presented spectra and bifurcations for dissipative subdiffusion equations. In this section, we focus on one-dimensional space-fractional reaction–diffusion problems. The space-fractional case is technically a bit easier to deal with as one still studies Markovian dynamics and the spectral theory for the space-fractional Laplacian is a bit more classical, or it is even encoded immediately in its definition for the spectral fractional Laplacian (SFL) as introduced in Section
2.2
. We proceed to study the effect of the fractional derivative order on the spectra, steady states and bifurcations, also considering three well-known PDEs in which the standard Laplacian is replaced by a spectral fractional Laplacian. A generic fractional reaction–diffusion system can be formulated as

\partial_{t}u=-D(-\Delta)^{\gamma/2}{u}+F({u};p)=:D\Delta^{\gamma/2}{u}+F({u};p),

(42)

where we recall the shorthand notation

\Delta^{\gamma/2}:=-(-\Delta)^{\gamma/2}

for the fractional diffusion operator with fractional order
\gamma\in(0,2)
, considered on a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and with suitable boundary conditions specified below for each example. As for classical diffusion, a key role in this context is played by the eigenvalues of the fractional diffusion operator. A natural goal, similar to Section
3
, is to study patterns and we are going to give a brief summary of the spectral properties for different definitions and analytical stability results for bifurcations and Turing instability.

5.1
Spectra for Space-fractional PDEs

As discussed in Section
2
, the several definitions of the fractional Laplacian in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are all equivalent in that setting. This is not the case on a bounded domain, so different definitions also have different spectral properties.

Regarding its spectral properties, the spectral fractional Laplacian of fractional order
\gamma
turns out to be the simplest case, as the eigenvalues and the eigenfunctions are the
(\gamma/2)
-power of the eigenvalues of the Laplacian and the very same eigenfunctions of the Laplacian, respectively. In particular, we recall the spectral definition of the fractional Laplacian
\Delta^{\gamma/2}

[
124
]
(cf. Section
2.2
)

\Delta^{\gamma/2}u(x)=-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(-\lambda_{j})^{\gamma/2}u_{j}\phi_{j}(x),\qquad u_{j}:=\langle u,\phi_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}_{\Omega}}=\int_{\Omega}u\phi_{j}~\textnormal{d}x

(43)

where
\lambda_{j}
and
\phi_{j}
the eigenvalues and the normalized eigenfunctions, respectively, of the standard Laplacian
\Delta
on
\Omega
, i.e., the solutions of the eigenvalue problem
\Delta\phi_{j}=\lambda_{j}\phi_{j}
with specific boundary conditions
[
125
]
. For the one-dimensional case
\Omega=(0,L)
the Dirichlet spectral fractional Laplacian has eigenpairs

(\phi_{j},\tilde{\lambda}_{j})=(\sin\left({j\pi x}/{L}\right),-\left({j\pi}/{L}\right)^{\gamma}),\quad j\geq 1,

(44)

while the Neumann spectral fractional Laplacian has eigenpairs

(\phi_{j},\tilde{\lambda}_{j})=(\cos\left({j\pi x}/{L}\right),-\left({j\pi}/{L}\right)^{\gamma}),\quad j\geq 0.

(45)

For other definitions, as for instance the integral fractional Laplacian, obtaining results on the spectral properties is not as straightforward as for the spectral definition. In
[
126
]
, the differences between these two operators, namely the spectral and the integral definitions, have been emphasized by studying their spectral properties. In particular, with respect to Dirichlet boundary conditions the first eigenvalue of the integral fractional Laplacian is strictly less than that of the spectral one. We refer also to
[
127
,
128
,
129
]
for more detailed studies.

5.2
Analytical Results for Turing Bifurcations

Pattern formation in reaction–diffusion systems with fractional diffusion has been investigated in different contexts: predator–prey interactions
[

[

However, the interplay between the fractional order and the other parameters can be nontrivial. In particular it is not obvious if the presence of the fractional operator leads to a common effect in this class of problems or it depends on the particular structure of the equation (nonlinear part). Considering the spectral definition, the modification of the classical Turing instability analysis is quite straightforward, since the fractional order enters as exponent of the eigenvalues in the diffusion matrix (hence in the dispersion relation). With other definitions, the first step is to obtain the corresponding linearized problem. To this end, the Fourier transformed

Remark
.

Subdiffusion limited reaction-diffusion equations like (
18
), as any typical non-autonomous equation, do not support (non-trivial) traveling wave solutions of the form (
3
) with nonzero speed.
For example, consider the subdiffusion limited reaction-diffusion equation in (
18
) with
(Djrbashian-)Caputo fractional derivative

\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}_{0,t}^{\alpha}
of order
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
Computing the (Djrbashian-)Caputo fractional derivative with respect to
t\geq 0
of the traveling wave ansatz
u(t,x)=u(x-st)=u(\zeta)
with
\zeta=x-st
and
s<0
yields

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}_{0,t}^{\alpha}\big[u(x-st)\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\sigma)^{-\alpha}\big(\partial_{\sigma}u(x-s\sigma)\big)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\sigma)^{-\alpha}\big(-su^{\prime}(x-s\sigma)\big)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

and using
\xi:=x-s\sigma
,
\zeta=x-st
,
\delta:=\zeta-\xi
,

\displaystyle=\frac{(-s)^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{x}^{x-st}\frac{u^{\prime}(\xi)}{(x-st-\xi)^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}\xi=\frac{(-s)^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{x}^{\zeta}\frac{u^{\prime}(\xi)}{(\zeta-\xi)^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle=\frac{(-s)^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{-st}\frac{u^{\prime}(\zeta-\delta)}{\delta^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}\delta.

The integral is rewritten using the traveling wave variables
\zeta
and
\xi
, but still depends either on the spatial variable
x
or on the time variable
t
.
Therefore, a subdiffusion limited reaction-diffusion equation (
18
) with Caputo fractional derivative can only support traveling wave solutions of the form
u(t,x)=u(x-st)
that are either stationary (
s=0
) or steady states (if
s\neq 0
).
However, in the natural long-term memory limit (for fixed
\zeta
),

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}_{0,t}^{\alpha}\big[u(x-st)\big]

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{(-s)^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{-st}\frac{u^{\prime}(\zeta-\delta)}{\delta^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}\delta=\frac{(-s)^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{\prime}(\zeta-\delta)}{\delta^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}\delta

\displaystyle=\frac{(-s)^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{-\infty}^{\zeta}\frac{u^{\prime}(\delta)}{(\zeta-\delta)^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}\delta,

the traveling wave equation of (
18
) turns into an integro-differential equation of the form,

\frac{(-s)^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{-\infty}^{\zeta}\frac{u^{\prime}(\delta)}{(\zeta-\delta)^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}\delta=\sigma\partial_{\zeta}^{2}u+f(u),\qquad\zeta\in\mathbb{R},

(54)

cf.
[
158
,
157
]
. For the subdiffusive Allen-Cahn equation, a formal analysis of bistable fronts in
[
158
]
predicts algebraic decay in one of the tails due to memory effects. For the time-fractional Fisher–KPP equation (
18
) with Caputo derivative
\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}_{0,t}^{\alpha}
, the existence of invading (i.e., monostable) fronts satisfying (
54
) has been proven in
[
159
]
(for complementary results see
[
160
,
161
]
). Some numerical simulations (see e.g.,
[
159
, §2]
) suggest that solutions of the initial value problem for (
18
) with initial datum given by a Heaviside step function converge to traveling wave solutions of (
54
).
However, we are not aware of an analytical proof of the convergence of solutions of (
18
) to traveling wave fronts of (
54
) for
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

In the remaining part of this section, we are going to focus on the existence of traveling wave solutions for reaction-diffusion equations with anomalous diffusion of the form

\partial_{t}{u}=\mathcal{L}_{Levy}u+f(u)\,,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,,\quad t\in(0,\infty)\,,

(55)

where
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is a nonlinear function and
\mathcal{L}_{Levy}
is a Lévy operator (i.e., the generator of a Lévy process).

6.1
Traveling Wave Solutions in One-Dimensional Settings

A particularly important subclass arises in the Riesz-Feller case leading to evolution equations

[

modeling symmetric super-diffusion

6.1.1
Approximation of the Diffusion Operator

Fractional Laplacians
\operatorname{D}_{0}^{\gamma}
can be approximated by a family of convolution operators
J_{\epsilon}\ast\phi-\phi
with
J_{\epsilon}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
such that
J_{\epsilon}\ast\phi-\phi\longrightarrow\operatorname{D}_{0}^{\gamma}\phi
for
\epsilon\to 0
in a suitable sense. The associated (traveling wave) equations
-s\phi^{\prime}=J_{\epsilon}\ast\phi-\phi+f(\phi)
exhibit for all sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
a unique monotone solution
(\phi_{\epsilon},s_{\epsilon})
with
\phi_{\epsilon}^{\prime}>0
see
[
175
,
176
]
. The limit
\phi=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\phi_{\epsilon}
exists and yields the profile
\phi
of a traveling front solution of (
61
).

6.1.2
Using an Extension Problem

The operator square root of the Laplacian
\operatorname{D}_{0}^{1/2}
can be obtained from the harmonic extension problem to the upper half space as the operator that maps the Dirichlet boundary condition to the Neumann condition. For fractional Laplacians and other integro-differential operators, a similar characterization is available
[
177
,
178
]
(see also Section
7.2.2
and the related discussion of the SFL in (
89
)). From those characterizations, properties of these integro-differential equations can be derived from purely local arguments in the
extension problems
[
177
]
. For example, the stationary traveling wave problem

0=\operatorname{D}_{0}^{\gamma}w+f(w)\quad\text{ in }\,\mathbb{R}

(63)

for
\gamma\in(0,2)
and a bistable function
f
can be related to a nonlinear boundary value problem for a partial differential equation. This allows one to prove that stationary traveling front solutions
w(x)=\phi(x)
of (
63
) exist if and only if
f
is bistable with a balanced potential; and are unique (up to translations) under the additional assumption
f^{\prime}(u_{\pm})<0
, see
[
165
, Theorem 2.4]
. Moreover, the asymptotic behavior of the profile of traveling front solutions can be analyzed, see
[
165
, Theorem 2.7]
.

6.1.3
Minimizers of a Nonlocal Energy Functional

Consider the nonlocal energy functional

\mathcal{E}(w,\Omega):=\mathcal{K}(w,\Omega)+\int_{\Omega}\,{F(w(x))}\,\,\operatorname{d}\!x\,

(64)

where
\mathcal{K}(w,\Omega)
is (related to) the squared Gagliardo–Sobolev–Slobodeckij
H^{s}
semi-norm of
w
, and
F
is a double-well potential with
F(u_{\pm})=0
. In the one-dimensional setting, stationary traveling front solutions
w(x)=\phi(x)
of (
63
) are local minimizers of the functional
\mathcal{E}(w,\mathbb{R})
. For a bistable function
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
with balanced potential, there exists a unique (up to translations) nontrivial global minimizer

of (
64
). This minimizer

is a traveling front solution of (
63
), and is unique (up to translations) in the class of monotone solutions of (

[
168

6.1.4
Homotopy Arguments

In
[
165
,
168
]
, the existence of

traveling front solutions of (

6.1.5
Semigroup Approach and Squeezing Method using Sub- and Super-solutions

For reaction-diffusion equations supporting a comparison principle, the squeezing method using sub- and supersolutions allows one to prove the existence, uniqueness and local asymptotic stability with exponential decay of traveling wave solutions. The squeezing method was formulated—first—for a class of nonlinear (nonlocal) evolution equations
[

including (

\partial_{t}{u}=J\ast u-u+f(u)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\,{J(x-y)\ u(y,t)}\,\,\operatorname{d}\!y\,-u(t,x)+f(u(t,x))\,,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}\,,\quad t>0\,,

[

6.2
Traveling Wave Solutions in Multi-Dimensional Settings

Next, we discuss the existence of traveling wave solutions for reaction-diffusion equations (
55
) with bistable function
f
in the multi-dimensional setting. Consider the classical bistable reaction-diffusion equation

\partial_{t}{u}=\Delta u+f(u)\,,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,,\quad t\in(0,\infty)\,,

(66)

and bistable reaction function
f
satisfying (
57
). Here, we briefly discuss the theory of traveling front solutions to (
66
) and refer to
[
181
,
182
,
183
]
for further information. Planar traveling waves in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are of the form

u(t,x):=\phi(x\cdot e-st)

(67)

for some vector
e\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\|e\|=1
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
. If a planar traveling wave (
67
) is a solution of (
66
) then its profile
\phi
satisfies a second-order ODE

\phi^{\prime\prime}+s\phi^{\prime}(\zeta)+f(\phi(\zeta))=0\qquad\text{for }\zeta\in\mathbb{R},

(68)

just like in the one-dimensional setting. A planar traveling front is a planar traveling wave (
67
) which satisfies
\lim_{\zeta\to\pm\infty}\phi(\zeta)=u_{\pm}
for some
u_{\pm}\in\mathbb{R}
. The existence of planar traveling fronts of (
66
) and the properties of its profile
\phi
and speed
s
follow again from the analysis of ODE (
68
), see
[
163
,
164
]
. For bistable reaction function
f
satisfying (
57
),
u_{-}=u_{3}
and
u_{+}=u_{1}
, there exists a monotone profile
\phi\in\mathbb{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and a unique speed
s
satisfying (
60
). The level sets of a planar traveling front at level
c\in\mathbb{R}
and time
t\geq 0
are

\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ u(t,x)=c\}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ x\cdot e=\phi^{-1}(c)+st\},

(69)

i.e., the level sets are parallel hyperplanes which are orthogonal to the direction of propagation
e
. Hence, they are called planar. Moreover, planar traveling fronts are only unique up to shifts. In particular, these traveling fronts are invariant in the moving coordinate frame with speed
s
in the direction
e
. In the one-dimensional setting, bistable traveling fronts are asymptotically stable. In the multi-dimensional setting, the stability w.r.t. perturbations of the initial data is more subtle, but see, e.g.,
[
184
,
185
,
186
,
187
,
183
,
188
]
. Robustness/stability of traveling front solutions w.r.t. perturbations of the nonlinear bistable reaction function has been studied also
[
189
]
.

For classical bistable reaction-diffusion equations (
66
) in the multi-dimensional setting, other kinds of traveling front solutions exist, see the reviews in e.g.
[
182
,
183
]
: For example, traveling fronts with non-planar level sets exist such as (i) (axisymmetric) conical-shape, (ii) (non-axisymmetric) pyramidal shape, and (iii) exponential/parabolic shape
[
190
]
, to name a few examples. Again, these traveling wave solutions (resp. their profiles) are invariant in a moving coordinate frame with constant speed.

The notion of transition fronts has been introduced to encompass all traveling front solutions which converge to
u_{\pm}
far away from their level sets uniformly in time
[
191
,
182
]
. There exist transition fronts of the classical bistable reaction-diffusion equation (
66
) which are not invariant in any moving coordinate frame with constant speed
[

. Thus, traveling fronts are a (strict) subset of transition fronts. Nonetheless, for (bistable) transition fronts a
global mean speed
can be defined which is independent of the shape of level sets of the transition fronts, see
[
182
]
.

7
Discretization and Simulation: Fractional PDEs

Eventually, the analytical/pen-and-paper methods discussed in the previous sections will reach their limits for fractional dissipative PDEs similarly to the case of standard differential operators. Hence, we have to develop numerical schemes to understand in more detail the dynamics we are interested in. Two aspects make the numerical treatment of fractional (partial) differential equations more challenging than that of standard (partial) differential equations. First, fractional differential operators are inherently nonlocal operators which brings about increased computational complexity due to fully populated matrices or the necessity to track the history in time-dependent problems. Second, solutions to fractional differential equations typically exhibit singularities even for smooth input data, which often lowers convergence rates, unless specialized techniques or locally refined meshes are employed.

7.1
Numerical Methods for Fractional Operators in 1D

Several classes of numerical methods for fractional differential operators have been proposed in the univariate setting. They can be broadly divided into 2 groups: the first group is related to discretizing the operator in the style of a finite difference method, which leads to methods with a time-marching flavor whereas the second class are Galerkin/collocation methods, which typically involve solving a non-trivial linear system of equations. Detailed discussions can be found in
[
204
,
205
]
and in particular the excellent monograph
[
206
]
to which we will frequently refer. The simplest model of a time-fractional differential equation (FODE) takes the form

D^{\alpha}_{0,t}u+\lambda u=f(t),\quad\mbox{ $t\in(0,T)$},

(75)

where
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
D^{\alpha}_{0,t}
is either the Riemann-Liouville or the Caputo fractional differential operators introduced in Section
2.1
and
\alpha\not\in{\mathbb{N}}
. More complex time-fractional PDEs (FPDE) of the form

D^{\alpha}_{0,t}u(t,x)+Au(x)=f(t,x)\quad\mbox{ $t\in(0,T),\quad x\in\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}$}

(76)

for spatial operators
A
can successfully be treated numerically by combining separate discretizations of
D^{\alpha}_{0,t}u
and
Au
in a way analogous to the procedure in classical time-dependent problems, viz., the famous “method of lines” in which first a spatial discretization is applied and then the resulting (fractional) ODE system is discretized or the well-known “Rothe method”/“method of transverse lines” in which first the temporal problem is discretized and afterwards the resulting spatial problems are solved numerically. In both approaches for (
76
), it is useful to have a good understanding of discretizations for the scalar case (
75
). Indeed, a diagonalization of the operator
A
(or its discretization) leads to problems of the form (
75
) so that (
75
) may be thought of as a simple model for (
76
). In this section, we will not discuss the discretization of the spatial operator
A
, and we will mostly restrict ourselves to the case
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

Remark
.

A hallmark of solutions of (
75
) (and thus also (
76
)) is that they have a (weak) singularity at the initial time
t=0

[
50
]
,
[
207
]
; see also the remarks in Section
4
. Consider the case of Caputo derivatives and
\alpha\in(0,1)
. For smooth
f
and
\lambda=0
, solutions of (
75
) can be expected to have the form
u(t)=t^{\alpha}\tilde{u}(t)+c
for
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
and smooth
\tilde{u}
since a particular solution of
\mathfrak{\tilde{D}}^{\alpha}_{0,t}u=t^{\beta}
is given by
u(t)=\frac{\Gamma(\beta+1)}{\Gamma(\beta+\alpha+1)}t^{\alpha+\beta}
,
[
207
, Ex. 1]
. For
\lambda\neq 0
, the solution structure is more involved in that the behavior
O(t^{\alpha})
is only the leading order behavior as

. For example, the function
u(t)=E_{\alpha,1}(-\lambda t^{\alpha})
with the standard (entire) Mittag-Leffler function

solves the homogeneous equation
\mathfrak{\tilde{D}}^{\alpha}_{0,t}u+\lambda u=0

[

for

.

7.1.1
The L1 Scheme and Related Methods

The definitions of
\mathfrak{D}^{\alpha}_{0,t}
and
\mathfrak{\tilde{D}}^{\alpha}_{0,t}
may motivate various approximations. For example, for
\alpha\in(0,1)
a discretization of
\mathfrak{\tilde{D}}^{\alpha}_{0,t}u
in the points
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}
may be taken to be

\displaystyle\mathfrak{\tilde{D}}^{\alpha}_{0,t}u(t_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{\tau=0}^{t_{n}}(t_{n}-\tau)^{-\alpha}u^{\prime}(s)\,d\tau\approx\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\int_{\tau=t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}(t_{n}-\tau)^{-\alpha}\frac{u(t_{k+1})-u(t_{k})}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\,d\tau.

(77)

This approximation is known as the L1 method. For the case of equidistant points
t_{k}=k{\Delta t}
the sum takes the form of a (discrete) convolution:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{\tilde{D}}^{\alpha}_{0,t}u(t_{n})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}b_{n-k-1}\left[u(t_{k+1})-u(t_{k})\right],\qquad b_{k}:=\frac{{\Delta t}^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\left[(k+1)^{1-\alpha}-k^{1-\alpha}\right].

(78)

A numerical method involving
\mathfrak{\tilde{D}}^{\alpha}_{0,t}
is obtained by replacing
u(t_{k})
with approximations
u_{k}
. When solving numerically a FODE of the form (
75
) with initial condition
u(0)=u_{0}
, the discretization (
78
) of
\mathfrak{\tilde{D}}^{\alpha}_{0,t}
leads to a numerical method that has the form of a time-stepping scheme, i.e., the approximations
u_{1}
,
u_{2},\ldots
are determined in turn. In contrast to classical ODEs, the cost of computing
u_{n}
is not
O(n)
but
O(n^{2})
due to the “memory” effect, i.e., the nonlocal nature of the fractional derivative. It is possible to reduce the cost to from
O(n^{2})
to
O(n\log n)
(see Section
7.1.3
below).

Remark
.

The L1 scheme is obtained in (
77
) by replacing
u
by its piecewise linear interpolant
\Pi_{1}u
. For
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the L2-scheme is obtained by approximating
u
by the linear interpolant on the first interval
(0,t_{1})
and on the interval
(t_{j-1},t_{j})
by the quadratic polynomial
\Pi_{2}u
interpolating in three consecutive nodes
t_{j-1}
,
t_{j}
,
t_{j+1}
, and by the quadratic interpolant in the nodes
t_{n-2}
,
t_{n-1}
,
t_{n}
for the last interval
(t_{n-1},t_{n})
,
[
206
, Chap. 4]
. Likewise, an approximation of the Caputo derivative for
\alpha>1
can be based on locally higher order polynomial interpolation,
[
205
, Sec. 3]
. A variant, which includes the fractional form of the Crank-Nicolson scheme (if
\theta=1/2
), is known as Alikhanov’s method (also known as
L2-L1_{\sigma}
) and approximates the fractional derivative at intermediate points
t_{j+\theta}=\theta t_{j}+(1-\theta)t_{j+1}
using again polynomial approximations on the intervals
(t_{j},t_{j+1})
and the terminal interval
(t_{n},t_{n+\theta})
, see
[
206
, Sec. 4.1]
,
[
205
, Sec. 3]
.

The L1 scheme has a truncation error
O({\Delta t}^{2-\alpha})
for
smooth

u
,
[
206
, Lem. 4.2]
; however, since by Remark
Remark
the solution of FODEs typically have at least a (weak) singularity near the initial point
t=0
, lower convergence rates are observed,
[
208
]
. For example, for a semidiscrete analysis of (
76
) with an elliptic operator
A
on uniform temporal meshes
[
206
, Sec. 5.1]
shows convergence
O({\Delta t})
in the discrete
\ell^{\infty}
norm (see, e.g.,
[
209
,
208
]
for the fully discrete error analysis). For smooth right-hand sides
f
in (
75
) or (
76
), better convergence rates can be obtained using one of the following two devices. The first one is to use the “corrected L1 scheme”, which is based on a uniform mesh and modifies the initial 2 steps of the time-marching formulation; for example, errors
O(({\Delta t}/t_{n})^{2-\alpha})
are then recovered for the case of (
76
) and an elliptic operator
A
,
[
206
, Thm. 5.3]
. The second device is to resort to graded meshes better to resolve the singularity at

. Currently successful techniques to obtain error estimates for the L1 scheme and related ones on uniform and non-uniform meshes include two strategies: The first one uses new variants of Gronwall’s Lemma
[

that are adapted to FODEs and originate from earlier work on Volterra integral equations,
[

. The second technique exploits that the L1 scheme is associated with an M-matrix and obtains estimates via suitably constructed barrier functions,
[
206
, Thm. 6.6]
.

7.1.2
Convolution Quadrature

The temporal fractional differential operators have a form that makes them amenable to convolution quadrature (CQ), going back to
[
211
,
212
]
; see also
[
213
]
and
[
206
, Chap. 3]
for an overview. CQ realizes numerically operators of the form (we follow the notation introduced by Lubich)

(K(\partial_{t})u)(t):=\int_{s=0}^{t}k(t-s)u(\tau)\,d\tau

(79)

in that good approximations at uniformly spaced points
t_{n}=nh
are obtained in the form

K(\partial^{\Delta t}_{t})u(t_{n})=\sum_{j=0}^{n}w_{n-j}u(t_{j})

for suitable weights
w_{j}
(more below). Important properties of CQ are:

a)

determining the weights
w_{j}
requires only the Laplace transform of
k
;

b)

CQ include the standard (numerical) differentiation and integration operators;

c)

CQ has the structure of an algebra under composition, which greatly simplifies the development of methods for operators that are the composition of, e.g., an integral operator of the form (
79
) and a differentiation operator as it appears in the definition of the operators
\mathfrak{\tilde{D}}^{\alpha}_{0,t}
and
\mathfrak{D}^{\alpha}_{0,t}
.

Example 7.1
.

(Derivation of CQ)

Several ways to motivate the form of CQ are given in
[
213
, Sec. 2]
. One way, taken from
[
211
]
, derives CQ from ODE solvers (here: multistep methods), representing the kernel in terms of the inverse Laplace transform, and using the so-called Z-transform. In more detail but proceeding purely formally, we express
k
in terms of the inverse Laplace transform and write with the Laplace transform
K={\mathfrak{L}}k
of the kernel
k

\displaystyle y(t)

\displaystyle:=(K(\partial_{t})u)(t)=\int_{\tau=0}^{t}k(t-\tau)u(\tau)\,d\tau=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{s\in\sigma+{\rm i}{\mathbb{R}}}K(s)\int_{\tau=0}^{t}e^{s(t-\tau)}u(s)\,d\tau\,ds

(80)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{s\in\sigma+{\rm i}{\mathbb{R}}}K(s)v(t;s)\,ds,

where the function
t\mapsto v(t;s)
solves the initial value problem (IVP)
v^{\prime}(t;s)=sv(t;s)+u(t)
,
v(0,s)=0
(variation of constants formula). Next, this IVP is solved with a numerical method to get an approximation
v_{n}(s)\approx v(t_{n};s)
, which leads to the approximation
y(t_{n})\approx y_{n}:=(2\pi{\rm i})^{-1}\int_{s\in\sigma+{\rm i}{\mathbb{R}}}K(s)v_{n}(s)\,ds
. To compute
v_{n}(s)
, one may employ a multistep method with
k
-steps (determined by the coefficients
\alpha_{i}
,
\beta_{i}
), which takes the form

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\Delta t}}\sum_{j=n-k}^{n}\alpha_{n-j}v_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=n-k}^{n}\beta_{n-j}(sv_{j}+u(t_{j})),

where the required additional initial data
v_{j}
,
j=-k+1,\ldots,-1
are taken to be zero. The values
v_{j}
can be computed recursively. A closed form of the sought values
v_{j}
is obtained with the aid of the Z-transform, which maps a sequence
(a_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
to a formal power series
{\mathcal{A}}(z):=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}z^{i}
. With the transforms
\alpha(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\alpha_{i}z^{i}
,
\beta(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\beta z^{i}
,
{\mathcal{V}}(z):=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}v_{i}z^{i}
,
{\mathcal{U}}(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}u(t_{i})z^{i}
, one arrives at

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\Delta t}}\alpha(z){\mathcal{V}}(z)

\displaystyle=\beta(z)\left(s{\mathcal{V}}(z)+{\mathcal{U}}(z)\right).

Solving for
{\mathcal{V}}
gives with the generating function
\delta(z):=\alpha(z)/\beta(z)
of the multistep method

\displaystyle{\mathcal{V}}(z)

\displaystyle=(\delta(z)/{\Delta t}-s)^{-1}{\mathcal{U}}(z).

Hence, the Z-transform
{\mathcal{Y}}(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}y_{i}z^{i}
satisfies

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Y}}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{s\in\sigma+{\rm i}{\mathbb{R}}}K(s)(\delta(z)/{\Delta t}-s)^{-1}\,ds\,{\mathcal{U}}(z)=K(\delta(z)/{\Delta t}){\mathcal{U}}(z).

Expanding

\displaystyle K(\delta(z)/{\Delta t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\omega_{j}z^{j}

and recognizing that the power series of
K(\delta(z)/{\Delta t}){\mathcal{U}}(z)
involves the convolution of the sequences
(\omega_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
and
(u(t_{j}))_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
gives the desired relation

\displaystyle y_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\omega_{n-i}u(t_{i}),\qquad n=0,1,\ldots

(81)

for the Convolution Quadrature.

The formal calculation in Example
7.1
shows how to design different CQ methods by choosing the function
\delta
, which in turn is given by the choice of the underlying ODE solver. To formulate the method, only the Laplace transform
K
of the kernel
k
is required. Even for rather general
K
, the weights
\omega_{j}
can be obtained numerically by applying the trapezoidal rule to the Cauchy integral formula for derivatives,
[
213
, Sec. 3.1]
. The underlying ODE solver (e.g., the multistep method in Example
7.1
) is often required to be A-stable. This stipulation derives from the observation that the domain of holomorphy of
K
often contains the whole right half-plane (e.g., the function
K(s)=s^{\alpha}
,
\alpha\not\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, which is only defined on the slit plane
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(-\infty,0]
) and the observation that for A-stable ODE solvers, the function
\delta
maps the complex unit ball
B_{1}(0)\subset{\mathbb{C}}
to a subset of the right half-plane
\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\,|\,\operatorname{Re}z>0\}
. However, the requirement of
A
-stability of the underlying ODE solver depends on the problem under consideration; for example, for (
76
) with
\alpha\in(0,1)
and an elliptic operator
A
weaker stability requirements suffice,
[
211
]
,
[
206
, Sec. 3.1]
,
[
213
, Sec. 8]
.

For an application of CQ to fractional derivatives, one notes that the Laplace transform
K_{\alpha}(s)=\int_{t=0}^{\infty}t^{\alpha}e^{-st}\,dt
of the kernel
k(t)=t^{\alpha}
is explicitly given by
K_{\alpha}(s)=\Gamma(1+\alpha)s^{-(1+\alpha)}
. The Laplace transform of the differentiation operator is simply
s
. Hence, for
u
with
u(0)=0
, the Laplace transform of
\mathfrak{\tilde{D}}^{\alpha}_{0,t}u
is given by
s^{\alpha}{\mathfrak{L}}u(s)
. That is, for
\alpha\in(0,1)
the CQ realization of the Caputo derivative for functions vanishing at
t=0
can be done with the kernel
K_{\alpha}(s)
. Since the Riemann-Liouville and Caputo derivatives coincide for such functions, the same CQ techniques can be employed. There is a extensive convergence theory for CQ based on uniform meshes, see, e.g.,
[
213
, Secs. 2.5, 5.3]
. Generally speaking, for sufficiently smooth function
u
, the difference
y(t_{n})-y_{n}
of the values (
80
) and (
81
) is
O({\Delta t}^{p})
if

a)

the order of the underlying ODE solver is
p
and

b)

u
vanishes to sufficient order at
t=0
.

Concerning the requirement
a)
: if one has to base CQ on an A-stable ODE solver, then multistep methods are limited to order
p=2
by Dahlquist’s barrier. However, CQ can also be based on Runge-Kutta methods,
[
214
]
and arbitrary order A-stable Runge-Kutta methods such as the Radau IIA formulas are available,
[
213
, Sec. 5]
. In the context of FODEs and FPDEs, requirement
b)
is typically a more limiting restriction as the solution
u
of the FODE/FPDE cannot be expected to vanish to high order at
t=0
. Order reduction is then visible. One remedy is to resort to a “corrected” CQ akin to that discussed above for the L1 method. For general CQ methods, see, e.g,
[
215
]
and
[
206
, Sec. 3.2]
for the case (
76
) of an elliptic
A
. An alternative remedy is to base the CQ on non-uniform meshes that are refined towards
t=0
, see
[
216
]
for an recent account on such CQ methods. Finally, we point to
[
217
]
for
a posteriori
error estimates for CQ on general meshes.

7.1.3
Fast Methods

At first sight, the evaluation of fractional differential operators in the nodes
t_{0},\ldots,t_{n}
leads to complexity
O(n^{2})
due to the time-stepping nature; alternatively, the computation can be viewed as a matrix-vector multiplication with a lower triangular matrix. For uniform point distributions, the convolution structure leads to Toeplitz matrices and algorithms with
O(n\log n)
complexity using FFT techniques are available for Toeplitz matrices, see
[
213
, Sec. 3.3]
for such algorithms in the context of CQ. Especially in the context of FPDEs, which involves additionally discretizations of spatial operators, various “fast” methodologies have emerged that exploit the fact that the pertinent kernel
k(s)=s^{\alpha}
is smooth with a singularity only at the origin. As such, compression techniques developed for integral operators (IO) such as fast multipole techniques
[
218
]
, wavelet compression,
[
219
]
and
{\mathcal{H}}
-matrices,
[
220
]
that lead to polylogarithmic-linear complexity
O(n\log^{\beta}n)
(for some
\beta\geq 0
) for storage and matrix-vector multiplication are, in principle, available. The broad
{\mathcal{H}}
-matrix format consists of blockwise low-rank matrices, and, in the context of IOs, blockwise low-rank approximation are obtained by approximating the kernel function by, e.g., piecewise polynomials. Therefore,
{\mathcal{H}}
-matrix compression is well-suited for Galerkin or collocation discretizations of IOs on both uniform and nonuniform meshes; see, e.g.,
[
220
, Sec.10.6]
for a discussion of the case of Volterra IOs.
{\mathcal{H}}
-matrices come with an (approximate) arithmetic that includes addition, multiplication, and inverse of matrices,
[
220
]
.

In the context of fractional differential operators, a more popular technique to effectively realize a compression is to approximate the kernel by a (short) sum of exponentials (SOE) instead of piecewise polynomials. A detailed analysis is given in
[
206
, Sec. 4.2]
for the L1; see also the survey
[
221
]
for related schemes. To illustrate the main point, consider the L1 scheme, i.e., the realization of
\int_{s=0}^{t_{n}}k(t_{n}-s)(\Pi_{1}u)(s)\,ds
, where
\Pi_{1}u
is the piecewise linear interpolant of
u
on a uniform mesh with nodes
t_{j}=j{\Delta t}
. One assumes that the kernel
k(s)
admits the approximation
k(s)\approx\sum_{\ell=0}^{N_{k}}\omega_{\ell}e^{-s_{\ell}s}
for some small
N_{k}
and suitable
\omega_{\ell}
,
s_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{R}}
; the smoothness of
k
away from
s=0
ensures that this can be achieved for the argument
s
bounded away from
0
. Since this approximation is poor for
s
close to
0
, the following approximation is based on a decomposition into a “history” part and a “local” part:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t_{n}}k(t_{n}-s)\Pi_{1}u(s)\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t_{n-1}}k(t_{n}-s)\Pi_{1}u(s)\,ds+\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}k(t_{n}-s)\Pi_{1}u(s)\,ds.

The “local” part
\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}k(t_{n}-s)\Pi_{1}u(s)\,ds
is evaluated exactly and requires only knowledge of the values
u(t_{n})
and
u(t_{n-1})
. The “history” part
\int_{0}^{t_{n-1}}k(t_{n}-s)\Pi_{1}u(s)\,ds
is realized efficiently with a recursion for the
N_{k}
terms
H_{\ell}(t_{n}):=\int_{0}^{t_{n}}e^{-s_{\ell}(t_{n}-s)}\Pi_{1}u(s)\,ds
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t_{n-1}}k(t_{n}-s)\Pi_{1}u(s)\,ds

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{\ell=0}^{N_{k}}\omega_{\ell}\int_{0}^{t_{n-1}}e^{-s_{\ell}(t_{n}-s)}\Pi_{1}u(s)\,ds=\sum_{\ell=0}^{N_{k}}\omega_{\ell}e^{-{\Delta t}s_{\ell}}H_{\ell}(t_{n-1}),

and the update formula for the
N_{k}
terms
H_{\ell}(t_{n})
is given by

\displaystyle H_{\ell}(t_{n})

\displaystyle=H_{\ell}(t_{n-1})+e^{-s_{\ell}{\Delta t}}\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}e^{-s_{\ell}(t_{n})-s}\Pi_{1}u(s)\,ds,

which again involves only knowledge of the values
u(t_{n-1})
and
u(t_{n})
and can be done explicitly. In the context of FODEs, the values
u(t_{j})
are approximated by values
u_{j}
and the initial value
u_{0}
is prescribed. Correspondingly, one sets
H_{\ell}(t_{0})=0
. The computational complexity of algorithms based on SOE hinges on the number
N_{k}
of terms in the approximation to be small. By
[
206
, Thm. 4.2]
one has
N_{k}=O(|\log(\varepsilon{\Delta t})|^{2})
for a prescribed accuracy
\varepsilon
so that for accuracy requirement
\varepsilon=({\Delta t})^{\beta}
(
\beta>0
) one arrives at complexity
O(n\log^{2}n)
.

A method somewhat similar to the technique of using sums of exponentials is the “fast and oblivious” CQ of
[
222
]
. It is a CQ that is applicable if the Laplace transform
K
of the kernel
k
is sectorial, i.e., it is holomorphic on a sector
\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\,|\,|\operatorname{arg}z|<\pi/2+\delta\}
for some
\delta>0
and satisfies certain decay conditions as
|z|\rightarrow\infty
. A typical representative of this setting are operators of the form
\mathfrak{\tilde{D}}^{\alpha}_{0,t}u+Au
where
A
is an elliptic spatial operator. Fast and oblivious CQ also reduces the cost to
O(n\log^{\beta}n)
(
\beta\geq 0
) by introducing a few auxiliary variables that track the history part in an agglomerated fashion (with controllable error); see
[
213
, Sec. 8.2]
and the related
[
206
, Sec. 3.5]
,
[
223
]
,
[
224
]
for details.

7.1.4
Spectral Galerkin Methods for FODEs

The techniques described in Sections
7.1.1
,
7.1.2
have the form of time-marching schemes. A different approach is obtained by spectral techniques, which come in variants such as spectral Galerkin, spectral Petrov-Galerkin, and spectral collocation methods. Starting points concerning the vast literature can be found in the contributions
[
225
]
and
[
226
]
of the handbook
[

]
,the monograph
[

]
as well as
[

. See also
[

,

,

,

]
and references therein. While we discuss high order spectral Galerkin methods, Galerkin methods can also be formulated for fixed order methods similar to the SEM discussed below.

Consider (
75

\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\lambda>0
. The classical Spectral Method seeks to approximate the solution from the space
{\mathcal{P}}_{N}:=\operatorname{span}\{t^{i}\,|\,i=0,\ldots,N\}
of polynomials of degree
N

The first class, associated with the names of polyfractonomials

(ii)

The second class of functions are “Müntz polynomials”
\{\pi(t^{\beta})\,|\,\pi\in{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\}

[
232
]
.

(iii)

The third class are the recent “Log orthogonal polynomials”
\{t^{\beta}\pi(\log t)\,|\,\pi\in{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\}
for chosen
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
[
228
]
,
[
206
, Sec. 12.2]

7.2.1
Numerical Methods for the Spectral Fractional Laplacian

Let

Rational Approximations

Very good discussions of various methods based on rational approximations of matrix functions are given in
[
240
]
and
[
241
]
, which we follow here. A rather intuitive approach to treat (

and work with powers of the resulting system matrix. For example, as described in
[

by replacing the continuous eigenpairs

with basis

with the symmetric stiffness matrix

and the symmetric mass matrix

with entries

with entries

(87)

(

8
Numerical Continuation: Fractional PDEs

In addition to theoretical investigations, it is also useful to look at the bifurcation structure of steady states to understand how solutions behave far from the homogeneous state or critical points when a parameter is varied. Moreover, by varying further parameters, the bifurcation structure itself also changes, which could reveal the effect of crucial quantities on the onset of non-trivial steady states. To compute the bifurcation structure of stationary steady states, a spatial discretization is usually combined with numerical continuation techniques
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
, i.e., one wants to combine spatial discretization methods from Section
7
with analytical bifurcation formulas
[
15
,
16
]
. For classical reaction–diffusion systems on bounded domains (and
d=1,2,3
), the global structure can be computed numerically with, for instance, the advanced continuation/bifurcation software
pde2path

[
23
]
. Recently, it has also been used beyond its standard setting, namely to treat cross-diffusion systems
[
281
,
282
]
, optimal control problems
[
283
]
, network dynamics
[
284
]
, and differential geometric PDEs in parametric form
[
285
]
.

It is also quite natural to extend the class of problems treated by
pde2path
to fractional diffusion PDEs to exploit the software continuation capabilities and routines. In fact, it allows for branch point and Hopf point continuation, continuation of relative equilibria (e.g., traveling waves and rotating waves), branch switching from periodic orbits (Hopf pitchfork/transcritical bifurcation, and period doubling). Therefore, in
[
148
]
, new capabilities for non-standard diffusion problems involving the fractional Laplacian in the spectral definition on 1D domains have been developed and tested for three important benchmark problems in the non-local setting, aiming at better understanding the effect of fractional diffusion on the steady state bifurcation structure of generic fractional reaction–diffusion systems.

In the following, we show how to implement a discretization of the spectral fractional Laplacian and its embedding within the numerical continuation package
pde2path
, and we demonstrate results for the Swift–Hohenberg equation in the non-local setting (
48
) obtained thanks to the new continuation capabilities
[
148
]
.

K_{tot}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}N\times n_{p}N}
and the vector
F_{tot}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}N}
correspond to the finite element discretization of the diffusion and reaction terms (and of the boundary conditions) from equations (
42
). To this end, in
[
148
]
the Balakrishnan formula representation
[
290
,
48
]
in the version appearing in
[
291
, formula (4), p.260]
has been discretized via a sinc quadrature for the integral as well as a finite element discretization in space; see also Section
7.2
in particular (
92
) and the discussion following it. This method has been proposed in
[
292
,
238
]
for the inverse formula (namely for the inverse fractional Laplacian representation,
\Delta^{-\gamma/2}u
), while in
[
148
]
this method has been adapted to the direct formula (
96
), that is, for
\gamma\in(0,2)
and
u\in\widetilde{H}^{\gamma/2}(\Omega)

\Delta^{\gamma/2}u(x)=\frac{\sin(\gamma\pi/2)}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Delta(\nu I-\Delta)^{-1}u(x)\nu^{\gamma/2-1}\textnormal{d}\nu.

(96)

Here are the steps leading to the discretization. Thanks to the change of variable
\nu={\textnormal{e}}^{\eta}
(
\textnormal{d}\nu={\textnormal{e}}^{\eta}\textnormal{d}\eta
) in equation (
96
) and then approximating the integral using sinc quadrature
[
293
]
one obtains

\displaystyle\Delta^{\gamma/2}u(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin(\gamma\pi/2)}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}({\textnormal{e}}^{\eta}I-\Delta)^{-1}\Delta u(x){\textnormal{e}}^{\eta\gamma/2}~\textnormal{d}\eta

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\sin(\gamma\pi/2)}{\pi}\kappa\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}{\textnormal{e}}^{\kappa l\gamma/2}({\textnormal{e}}^{\kappa l}I-\Delta)^{-1}\Delta u(x),

where
\kappa
is the stepsize parameter coming from the sinc quadrature.
Choosing suitable values of
n_{+}
and
n_{-}
for the truncation, the sum is approximated by

\Delta^{\gamma/2}u(x)\approx\frac{\sin(\gamma\pi/2)}{\pi}\kappa\sum_{l=-n_{-}}^{n_{+}}{\textnormal{e}}^{\kappa l\gamma/2}v_{l}(x),

(97)

where
v_{l}\in\mathbb{U}(\mathcal{\tau})
is the solution of the Galerkin variational problem

{\textnormal{e}}^{\kappa l}\int_{\Omega}v_{l}w+\int_{\Omega}\nabla v_{l}\cdot\nabla w=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla w\qquad\forall w\in\mathbb{U}(\mathcal{\tau})

and
\mathbb{U}(\mathcal{\tau})
is the finite element space satisfying homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions.
The variational problem is then equivalent to the matrix formulation

({\textnormal{e}}^{\kappa l}M+K)\mathtt{v_{l}}=K\mathtt{u},

(98)

where
M
and
K
are the classical FEM mass and stiffness matrices respectively, and
\mathtt{v_{l}}
denotes the node vector for
v_{l}
and can be obtained as the solution of the linear system (
98
). Therefore, the discretization requires solving
n_{-}+n_{+}+1
independent linear systems to obtain an approximation of
\Delta^{\gamma/2}u
.

However, to interface the fractional Laplacian via a discretization of the Balakrishnan formula with the
pde2path
setup, a matrix approximation of the operator
\Delta^{\gamma/2}
itself is needed. This matrix, denoted by
K_{\gamma}
to emphasize its dependence on the fractional order
\gamma
, can be built column by column by
testing
the proposed approximation method on the standard basis of Lagrangian
\mathbb{P}_{1}
elements. Finally, once the matrix
K_{\gamma}
has been built, the standard stiffness matrix
K
has to be replaced by
-MK_{\gamma}
in the expression of
K_{tot}
in the Matlab routines relevant to the algebraic system of the form (
95
). We refer to
[
148
]
for the detailed description of the implementation in
pde2path
, the Matlab code and the choice of the quantities
\kappa,\,n_{+},\,n_{-}
. In addition, the Matlab code is also freely available in the GitHub folder
[
294
]
.

8.2
The Fractional Swift–Hohenberg Equation

Thanks to the new capabilities of the continuation software, it is possible to investigate the effect of the fractional order
\gamma
on the steady state bifurcation structure of benchmark problems. As in Section
5
, we consider as a case study the fractional Swift–Hohenberg equation with competing cubic–quintic nonlinearities (
48
) and homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. For the classical Swift–Hohenberg equation (obtained with
\gamma=2
), it is known that bifurcation diagrams exhibit the so-called homoclinic snaking
[
295
,
296
,
297
]
. A non-trivial branch of stationary solutions bifurcates from a branch point and, for a suitable range of the main bifurcation parameter, undergoes several fold bifurcations leading to multi-stability of the solutions along the branch that are often important localized patterns. Looking at the bifurcation diagram, this branch presents the characteristic snaking-type structure. Recently, in
[
148
]
, also the bifurcation structure of the Swift–Hohenberg equation with nonlocal reaction terms (but also other well-known PDEs) has been investigated with a focus on understanding how the snaking scenario changes under the nonlocal influence (namely decreasing the fractional power
\gamma
) as already carried out for nonlocal reaction terms in
[
155
]
. As a first step, the Swift–Hohenberg equation can be converted into a system of second-order PDEs by substituting
(u_{1},u_{2})=(u,\Delta^{\gamma/2}u)
in equation (
48
). The resulting system is

\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\partial_{t}\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}\\
u_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-\Delta^{\gamma/2}u_{2}-2u_{2}-(1-p)u_{1}+qu_{1}^{3}-u_{1}^{5}\\
\Delta^{\gamma/2}u_{1}-u_{2}\end{pmatrix},

which fits the
pde2path
extended framework. As in
[
148
]
, we consider equation (
48
) defined on a bounded interval
\Omega=(-5\pi,5\pi)\subset\mathbb{R}
of length
L=10\pi
together with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions
u(-5\pi)=u(5\pi)=0
and
\Delta^{\gamma/2}u(-5\pi)=\Delta^{\gamma/2}u(5\pi)=0
. In the system two parameters are involved: we fix the parameter
q=2
, while parameter
p
is taken as the bifurcation parameter. For the space discretization, we choose the mesh size
h=0.04
, corresponding to
n_{p}=786
mesh points. The quantities
\kappa
,
n_{+}
and
n_{-}
in the quadrature approximation (
97
) of the fractional Laplacian have been chosen in
[
148
]
following
[
292
, Corollary 2]
where a similar discretization method was used. In detail, the value of
\kappa
is related to the mesh size
h
and the values
n_{+}
and
n_{-}
are chosen proportional to
1/\kappa^{2}
as follows

\kappa=\frac{1}{|\log(h)|},\qquad n_{+}\coloneqq\left\lceil\frac{\pi^{2}}{4(1-\gamma/2)\kappa^{2}}\right\rceil,\qquad n_{-}\coloneqq\left\lceil\frac{\pi^{2}}{2\gamma\kappa^{2}}\right\rceil.

It is then possible to investigate some of the effects of super-diffusion on the steady state bifurcation structure and solution profiles. We highlight here the main effects that were observed for the bifurcation points on the homogeneous branch, the snaking branch in the bifurcation structure, and the solution profile.

Solution profile.
Finally, it has been observed that, for a fixed value of the bifurcation parameter, the non-homogeneous stationary solutions tend to have flatter peaks or valleys as the fractional order of the Laplacian decreases. This effect has been named as “sharpening the teeth” of the spatial solution profiles. In Figure
4
, solution profiles located on the first (blue) branch bifurcating from the homogenous branch at
p=4
for different values of the fractional order
\gamma
(see Figure
3
) are shown. Whereas the solution profiles for
\gamma=1.8
and
\gamma=1.4
resemble the basic periodic pattern specific to the branch (i.e., to the eigenfunction associated to the corresonding mode), we clearly see that at
\gamma=1
the profile significantly “flattens” towards the maximum of oscillation. The same happens to the solution profile along the upper part of the snaking branch for
\gamma=0.6
, as illustrated in Figure
5
. Moving up the snaking branch, namely increasing the parameter
p
, the oscillations in the solution profile significantly enlarge while the peaks and valleys are squared off.

Moreover, in
[
148
]
it has been shown that all these effects are also common to the fractional Allen–Cahn equation and the fractional Schnakenberg system, contributing to a better understanding of the effects of the fractional Laplacian on the steady solutions of generic reaction–diffusion systems on bounded domains. Overall, there are currently still no additional detailed numerical continuation case studies available for various fractional dissipative PDEs but we anticipate this research direction to naturally grow in the coming years.

9
Conclusion & Outlook

In this chapter, we have summarized several important research directions for fractional dissipative PDEs. As mentioned in the introduction, it is impossible at this point to provide a completely comprehensive overview of the area as it has grown so rapidly in recent years. Yet, even in a relatively condensed format, several conclusions for fractional dissipative PDEs have become apparent. First, the general dynamics approach known for classical dissipative PDEs can be transferred in many cases. One starts to analyze the invariant sets, studies their local stability via spectral theory, gets a more global analytical view via gradient/Lyapunov-function structures, repeats the strategy for moving patterns, and finally transitions to numerical discretization and numerical continuation to fill out the dynamical picture. Second, the technical approach often differs significantly. Many methods from classical derivative calculus are not available for fractional derivatives. This often necessitates very significant technical extensions, e.g., for transform methods, spectral theory, energy estimates, bifurcation theory, sub/super-solutions, new integral discretizations, and updated numerical continuation packages. For some problems, entirely new methods have been employed such as converting by extension back to local derivatives. Third, once methods are available, one notices often significant quantitative changes of the dynamics. This includes, e.g., changes for the spectrum, space-time asymptotics, bifurcation points, numerical cost/accuracy or spatial structure of patterns. Yet, there are also numerous phenomena from classical dissipative PDEs that are qualitatively robust. This is re-assuring as one wants physical dissipative systems to behave robustly under small structural perturbations of the model.

Currently, the field of fractional dissipative PDEs still poses immense challenges as only the dynamics of some basic phenomena have been understood, so we can only outline a few possible future directions here: (I) We have mostly focused on purely time-fractional or purely space-fractional PDEs with Riemann-Liouville, Caputo and Riesz–Feller derivatives. Although there is a good motivation for this viewpoint regarding diffusive processes, it is certainly plausible for applications to directly think in terms of space-time modelling leading to nonlocal operators in space and time. For example, for subdiffusive processes on complex networks, one expects the mean-field limit PDEs to have nonlocal operators in space and time, potentially even new coupled operators beyond current standard examples. (II) Since we have mostly focused on low-dimensional, or even scalar, problems, the analysis of the existence and stability theory for steady states was still somewhat tractable. Once spatial multi-component systems are considered, spectral problems are likely going to become much more complicated, which is an effect already well-known for classical dissipative PDEs. (III) The last issue also connects to a topic we have not covered here concerning invariant/inertial manifolds for fractional dissipative PDEs. We conjecture that existing techniques will transfer once suitable spectral gap conditions are satisfied but verifying these conditions could be much more challenging as already indicated in our discussion of spectral theory for time-fractional PDEs. (IV) On a global level, the theory of nonlocal gradient flows is extremely active currently in multiple directions, so we anticipate that fractional PDEs will be embedded within these efforts as key benchmark examples. (V) One the level of pattern formation, we only touched upon traveling waves, which have a relatively simple (translation) symmetry group and upon Turing patterns generated at local bifurcation points. Yet, for classical reaction-diffusion PDEs, there are many other phenomena of key interest such as chaos, chimeras, modulated patterns, and spirals, just to name a few. It is likely that fractional derivatives can fundamentally alter the mechanisms for these in analogy to pattern formation results observed when one transitions from lattice or all-to-all network models to intermediate-range nonlocal coupling. (VI) From a numerical perspective, some excellent discretization schemes do exist but we are still very far from having practical software packages available that would allow for a seamless use of these schemes to study fractional nonlinear dynamics. Hence, a major software development effort in this direction would be highly welcome. (VII) Lastly, the PDEs we focused on had relatively simple polynomial nonlinearities and mostly semilinear structures providing a connection to several classical parabolic PDEs. Yet, many complex systems applications are bound to lead us far beyond these mathematical base cases.
