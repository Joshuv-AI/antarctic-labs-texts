Laplace-Beltrami spectrum of ellipsoids that are close to spheres and analytic perturbation theory
Suresh Eswarathasan, Theodore Kolokolnikov

Abstract
. We study the spectrum of the Laplace Beltrami operator on ellipsoids. For ellipsoids that are close to the sphere, we use analytic perturbation theory to estimate the eigenvalues up to two orders. We show that for biaxial ellipsoids sufficiently close to the sphere, the first L 2 L^{2} eigenvalues have multiplicity at most two, and characterize those that are simple. For the triaxial ellipsoids sufficiently close to the sphere that are not biaxial, we show that at least the first sixteen eigenvalues are all simple. We also give the results of various numerical experiments, including comparisons to our results from the analytic perturbation theory, and approximations for the eigenvalues of ellipsoids that degenerate into infinite cylinders or two-dimensional disks. We propose a conjecture on the exact number of nodal domains of near-sphere ellipsoids.

Introduction

This article aims to compute the Laplace-Beltrami spectrum (and its
multiplicities) of a class of 2-dimensional ellipsoids in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

through analytic perturbation theory
[
18
]
, more specifically

We describe our main results first before providing

Main results

1.2.
Some motivations

From the point of view of classical mechanics, ellipsoids
E_{a,b,c}
form one of the oldest known examples of integrable systems, themselves holding a venerable place in the subject. Their quantum analogues have been intensively studied in the last forty years, with numerous contributions arising from a beautiful mixture of symplectic geometry and WKB approximations, the connection being exploited by microlocal/semiclassical analysis. We survey some related and microlocally-oriented results in the next section.

We emphasize that the spectrum of geometric spaces with large symmetry groups has been explicitly computed
[
23
]
with a partial list being compact rank-one symmetric spaces (CROSSes), certain projective spaces, Steifel manifolds, and Grassmannians. However, it appears that not much is known for manifolds lacking large symmetry groups like biaxial and triaxial ellipsoids let alone their multiplicities. While microlocal analysis has addressed the approximation of the eigenvalues in the semiclassical limit, we have not found any literature providing bounds on multiplicity.

The study of Laplace’s equation on
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
using ellipsoidal-type coordinate systems is well-developed and is centered around the analysis of the Lamé equation. In fact, the word “ellipsoidal harmonics” has been attached to a variety of families of functions including for eigenfunctions on
E_{a,b,c}
. See the treatise of Dassios
[
7
]
for a survey and in particular Chapter 4.4 for a brief treatment on product-form eigenfunctions on
E_{a,b,c}
.

We make note that the
use of analytic perturbation theory allows for both accurate approximations (as confirmed by our numerics in Section
5.1
) and multiplicity calculation
. Furthermore, it provides us the opportunity to bypass the use of Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization rules and the computation of subprincipal symbols, per microlocal analysis, which are two highly powerful but technical concepts. In this vein, the contents of our article appear to be novel.

The question of what is
\rm{spec}(-\Delta_{g})
has played a prominent role in recent years in data analysis. For example, in
[
19
]
eigenvalues of the Laplace–Beltrami operator were used to extract “fingerprints” which characterize surfaces and solid objects. In
[
2
,
4
]
, these eigenvalues (and their corresponding eigenfunctions) were used for dimensionality reduction and data representation.

We close our motivations section by briefly discussing the concept of shape DNA in shape matching. By “shape DNA”, we mean the first
N
elements of
\rm{spec}(-\Delta_{g})
for
(M,g)
. Our results can be encompassed as the computation of this shape DNA for ellipsoids that are close to spheres. Note that shape DNA plays a crucial role in the representation of data sets, itself being usefuk in copyright protection and database retrieval. For more applications, see
[
19
]
.

1.3.
Related results from semiclassics

There are a number of relevant results from the semiclassical analysis literature that require discussion. We begin with the work of Pankratova
[
16
]
. In this article, the author uses a special ellipsoidal coordinate system and the so-called parabolic-equation method (in the spirit of Babich and Lazutkin) to compute high-frequency asymptotics for eigenvalues arising only from product-form eigenfunctions of
-\Delta_{g}
.

Three works of greatest relevance to Theorems
2
and
35
are those of Sjöstrand
[
22
]
, Colin de Verdère-Parisse
[
6
]
, and Toth
[
24
]
, each of which we describe in detail. First, the work of Toth
[
24
]
not only proves the quantum integrability of
E_{a,b,c}
(i.e. the existence of a second quantum Hamiltonian on
E_{a,b,c}
that commutes with
\Delta_{g}
) but also formulates an interesting conjecture: the joint spectrum for these quantum Hamiltonians is encoded by a second-order complex ODE with automorphic boundary conditions.

An asymptotic description of eigenvalues for semiclassical Schrödinger operators whose potentials satisfy a non-degeneracy condition is given in the work of Sjöstrand
[
22
]
. In the case of biaxial 2-dimensional ellipsoids, one can reduce to a Mathieu-type operator
A(\hbar)
on the non-
S^{1}
-invariant axis and then use quantum Birkhoff normal forms to read off formulas for the energies in a fixed window (in our setting, this corresponds to the low-energy spectrum with the constraints that
m^{2}\approx l^{2}
) but to order
\hbar
. This leads us to the more geometric work of Colin de Verdière-Parisse.

The articles
[
5
,
6
]
investigate Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization rules in the presence of singularities generated by select classes of quantum Hamiltonians. The spectrum of ellipsoids is studied as an application by Colin de Verdière-Vu Ngoc and depends strongly on some previous work of Colin de Verdière-Parisse in one dimension. The work
[
6
]
determines that the energies of a certain class of 1-dimensional semiclassical Schrödinger operators
P(\hbar)
in a fixed window can be explicitly deduced from solving for the coefficients
a_{j}
in the equation
e^{\frac{\sum_{j}a_{j}\hbar^{j}}{\hbar}}=1
for small enough
h
. Following this reasoning in the case of biaxial 2-dimensional ellipsoids should allow one to reproduce the multiplicity information given in Theorem
2
. To perform this calculation however, it appears one needs to push the quantum Birkhoff normal forms to greater precision via the calculation of
P(\hbar)
’s subprincipal terms. In some sense, our work proceeds in this direction albeit through the lens of analytic perturbation theory. This is one avenue in which our Theorems
2
and
4
are new.

In closing this discussion, we point the connections between our method and high-frequency quasimode constructions in semiclassical
analysis. If we write out the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
E_{a,b,c}
as
-\hbar^{2}\left(\Delta_{g}+\varepsilon A_{1}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})\right)
, it becomes
clear that we are utilizing an additional small parameter
\varepsilon
whilst bounding
\hbar
away from zero to write quasimodes on
S^{2}
. In fact, we are
computing the quasifrequencies for
-\Delta_{g}
but not in a high-frequency regime. This naturally results in our Theorems
2
and

4
not being descriptive of high frequencies but at the upshot of being descriptive for multiplicities.

1.4.
Outline of the paper

Our main tool is the theory of perturbations, a sharply defined set of ideas that is described for instance in the classic applied mathematics
text of Hinch
[
11
]
and in a more pure, theoretical fashion in the treatise of Kato
[
12
]
.
While there exists a number of sources
from pure mathematics rigorizing asymptotics, including
[
12
]
, there appear to be much fewer sources demonstrating the analyticity of eigenvalues
for analytic families of metrics. In this paper, we utilize a combination of results from Rellich’s perturbation theory notes (unfortuntaely, now
discontinued) from the Courant Mathematical Institute
[
18
]
and an article of Bando-Urakawa
[
1
]
on eigenvalues for certain families of
Laplace-Beltrami operators. In fact, for the benefit of easier reading, Section
2
is dedicated to an appreciable reproduction of useful
results from
[
1
]
along with some alternate proofs coming from Rellich’s Courant notes.

Once we have explained our theoretical tools, particularly in Theorem
6
, Sections
4
and
4
are dedicated to explicit calculations with the coordinate representations of
-\Delta_{g}
on
E_{a,b,c}
and the ultraspherical harmonic basis on
L^{2}(S^{2})
. Section
3
utilizes the symmetries of
E_{a,b,b}
to reduce our eigenvalue calculation problem to one for analytic families of ordinary differential equations, allowing us to apply the theory of Sturm-Liouville equations as well as make deductions on multiplicities. The triaxial ellipsoid
E_{a,b,c}
is the most difficult computationally, so Section
4
and the appendix are focused in this direction. The lack of rotation-invariance (in other words, an invariant

-action on
E_{a,b,c}
) obstruct most simplifications hence requiring a more in-depth analysis.

Our final section, namely Section
5
, is focused on verifying the accuracy of our analytic methods via numerics. A combination of MATLAB
calculations as well as Laplace-Beltrami eigenfunction approximations on surfaces, as generated by code of Macdonald-Brandman-Rooth
[
14
]
, are provided
for comparisons: these demonstrate that our analytic results are in fact accurate up to a designated, yet still high, order. We also provide some simulations
that address the shapes of regions where eigenfunctions are non-zero (which go by the moniker of “nodal domains” in the spectral geometry community) on
different ellipsoids
E_{a,b,c}
.

2.1.
Main tools and Rellich’s Theorem

The proof of this Theorem
8
hinges upon the aforementioned robust and clever result of Rellich
[
18
]
and an auxilliary lemma about linear differential operators whose coefficients have an analytic dependence on a small parameter. First, we start with a definition for the notion of real-analytic operators:

Definition
9

(Real-analytic families of operators)
.

For
\varepsilon>0
, let
A(\varepsilon)\in\mathscr{L}(H^{s_{1}}(M),\newline
H^{s_{0}}(M))
, the Banach space of bounded operators from
H^{s_{1}}(M)
to
H^{s_{0}}(M)
. We say
A(\varepsilon)
is
real-analytic in
\varepsilon
if there exists a
A_{i}\in\mathscr{L}(H^{s_{1}},H^{s_{0}})
with the property that
A(\varepsilon)=\sum_{i}\varepsilon^{i}A_{i}
and a sequence of constants
\{a_{i}\}_{i}
where
\|A_{i}\|\leq a_{i}
such that

\|A(\varepsilon)\|\leq\sum_{n}\varepsilon^{n}a_{n}<\infty.

In fact, the definition goes both ways: starting off with the series expansion and finiteness of its norm, that
\mathcal{L}
is Banach gives us that
A(\varepsilon)
is in fact an element of
\mathscr{L}(H^{s_{1}},H^{s_{0}})
. We now give a technical lemma that is useful for analysis in coordinate charts:

Lemma
10
.

Let
U\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a coordinate chart on
M
. Let
L_{\varepsilon}
be family of differential operators on
M
which can locally be expressed in
U
as

L_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}a_{\alpha}(\varepsilon,x)D_{x}^{\alpha}

where each
a_{\alpha}
has a real-analytic dependence on
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}(U)
, uniformly for
x\in U
. Then the family of bounded operators
L_{\varepsilon}:H^{m}(M)\rightarrow L^{2}(M)
is real-analytic.

Proof.

We leave the proof as an easy exercise for the interested reader.
∎

It is important to note that in local coordinates
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
on
M
, the coefficients of the Laplace-Beltrami operator are simply products of functions which are themselves analytic in
\varepsilon
thanks to our analyticity assumption on
g_{\varepsilon}
(this assumption itself implying analyticity for the coefficients of
g^{-1}
, which appear in the local coordinate expressions of
\Delta_{g_{\varepsilon}}
, thanks to the analyticity of
\det^{-1}(g)
and the adjugate matrix of
g
.)

Finally, we arrive at our main technical results in the theoretical portion of this article:

Theorem
11

(Rellich’s Theorem)
.

Let
I
be an interval containing 0. Let
s_{1}>s_{0}\geq 0
be integers. Let
A_{\varepsilon}
be a real-analytic family of bounded operators mapping from
H^{s_{1}}
to
H^{s_{0}}
with
A_{0}=:A
. Assume that

(1)

each operator
A_{\varepsilon}
,
\varepsilon\in I
, is self-adjoint with domain
H^{s_{1}}
but with respect to the inner product
\langle\,,\,\rangle_{s_{0}}
. In other words,
A_{\varepsilon}
is a densely defined unbounded operator on
H^{s_{0}}(M)
and has
D(A_{\varepsilon})=D(A_{\varepsilon}^{*})
,

(2)

A_{0}
is a positive operator on its domain, and

(3)

\Lambda
is an eigenvalue of
A_{0}
with multiplicity
l
that is also isolated in the spectrum, that is there exists
\delta(\Lambda)>0
such that
spec(A)\cap[-\delta+\Lambda,\delta+\Lambda]=\{\Lambda\}
.

Then there exists
I^{\prime}\subset I
containing 0,
l
real-analytic families of eigenvalues
\{\Lambda_{m}(\varepsilon)\}_{m=1}^{l}
and eigenvectors
\{\psi_{m}(\varepsilon)\}_{m=1}^{l}
of
A_{\varepsilon}
for
\varepsilon\in I^{\prime}
such that

•

\Lambda_{j}(0)=\Lambda
, for
m\in\{1,\dots,l\}
,

•

\{\psi_{m}(\varepsilon)\}_{m=1}^{l}
is orthonormal with respect to the inner product
\langle,\rangle_{s_{0}}
for all
\varepsilon\in I^{\prime}
, and moreover,

•

given any
d_{1},d_{2}<\delta
, there exists
I^{\prime\prime}\subset I^{\prime}
such that for all
\varepsilon\in I^{\prime\prime}
,
\mbox{spec}(A_{\varepsilon})\cap[-d_{1}+\Lambda,d_{2}+\Lambda]=\{\Lambda_{m}(\varepsilon)\}_{m=1}^{l}
.

A similar statement can be found in the classic texts of Kato
[
12
]
and Riesz-Nagy
[
17
]
. Note also that a priori, our intervals
I^{\prime}
and
I^{\prime\prime}
depend on
\Lambda
therefore making this result inherently non-uniform across the entirety of
\mbox{spec}(A_{0})
.

In fact, by applying Theorem
11
to each element of the spectrum of
A
below a fixed threshold
L
say, and carefully choosing the intervals
I^{\prime\prime}
we then immediately have the

Corollary
12
.

Given
L>0
, there exists
\varepsilon_{0}(L)
such that
\mbox{spec}(A(\varepsilon))\cap[0,L]
consists entirely of analytic eigenvalues as described in Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem
8
using Lemma
10
and Theorem
11
.

The proof is almost immediate after considering the following well-known isometry between
L^{2}(M,g_{\varepsilon})
and
L^{2}(M,g)
which we give in local-coordinate form:

U_{\varepsilon}(f)(x)=\sqrt{\frac{\det g(x)}{\det g_{\varepsilon}(x)}}\,f(x).

Thanks to the analyticity of
\sqrt{\frac{\det g(x)}{\det g_{\varepsilon}(x)}}
, it follows immediately that
U_{\varepsilon}\Delta_{g_{\varepsilon}}U_{\varepsilon}^{-1}+I
satisfies the hypotheses of Lemma
10
and is a bounded family of operators from
H^{2}(M,g_{\varepsilon})
to
L^{2}(M,g)
where
s_{1}=2
and
s_{0}=0
. Furthermore, these operators are densely defined on
L^{2}(M)
, self-adjoint, and positive, thus satisfying the hypotheses of Theorem
11
.

We conclude the proof by noting that for
\tilde{\psi}_{m}(\varepsilon)
an eigenvector of
U_{\varepsilon}\Delta_{g_{\varepsilon}}U_{\varepsilon}^{-1}+I
as per the conclusions of Theorem
11
,
\psi_{m}(\varepsilon):=U_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\tilde{\psi}_{m}(\varepsilon)
gives us the desired eigenvectors corresponding to
g_{\varepsilon}
. Hence,

\Lambda_{m}(\varepsilon)=\langle\left(U_{\varepsilon}\Delta_{g_{\varepsilon}}U_{\varepsilon}^{-1}+I\right)\tilde{\psi}_{m}(\varepsilon),\tilde{\psi}_{m}(\varepsilon)\rangle_{g_{0}}

which itself admits an power series expansion therefore verifying the analyticity. The last step is to just shift the spectrum by -1.
∎

2.2.
Proof of Theorem
11
: Some technical statements

The idea behind the proof of Rellich’s Theorem is both natural and computational in nature, however there are a number of moving parts that we must carefully identify in a top-down format. Throughout this section, we consider assumptions (1)-(3) in the statement of Theorem
11
.

For the sake of simplicity, as our operators of interest are themselves Laplace-Beltrami operators corresponding to perturbed metrics
g_{\varepsilon}
, we assume that
A_{\varepsilon}
admits a discrete, non-negative spectrum and that
\mbox{Dom}(A_{\varepsilon})=\mbox{Dom}(A_{0})=H^{2}(M,dV_{g_{0}})
. Now, set
B_{\varepsilon}:=A_{0}-A_{\varepsilon}
and
\mu(\varepsilon):=\Lambda-\Lambda(\varepsilon)
where
A_{\varepsilon}\psi(\varepsilon)=\Lambda(\varepsilon)\psi(\varepsilon)
. This leads us to the following series of simple lemmas:

Lemma
13

(Restriction and matrix identity)
.

Let
\varphi_{\Lambda}
be a eigenvector of eigenvalue
\Lambda
and
\psi(\varepsilon)
an eigenvector of
A_{\varepsilon}
. We have

\displaystyle(A_{0}-\Lambda)\psi(\varepsilon)=(B_{\varepsilon}-\mu(\varepsilon))\psi(\varepsilon)

and hence
\langle(A-\Lambda)\varphi_{\Lambda},\psi(\varepsilon)\rangle=\langle\varphi_{\Lambda},(B_{\varepsilon}-\mu(\varepsilon))\psi(\varepsilon)\rangle=0
by self-adjointness with domain
H^{2}
.

Lemma
14

(Pseudo inverses)
.

Let
A
be our self-adjoint, unbounded operator on
L^{2}(M)
and
\delta
be as in the hypotheses Theorem
11
. There exists
R\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2})\cap\mathscr{L}(H^{2})
such that

•

R\,\Pi_{E_{\Lambda}}=0
.

•

R\,(A-\Lambda)=(A-\Lambda)\,R=I-\Pi_{E_{\Lambda}}

Proof.

We invoke spectral calculus for unbounded operators and denote by
E_{\sigma}
the spectral measure for
A
(which is in our case discrete). Then we can set

R=\int_{0}^{\Lambda-\delta}\frac{1}{\sigma-\Lambda}\,dE_{\sigma}+\int_{\Lambda+\delta}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sigma-\Lambda}\,dE_{\sigma}.

The boundedness follows from spectral multiplier taking values less than
\delta^{-1}
on the spectrum.
∎

Notice that for
\psi(\varepsilon)
an eigenfunction of
A_{\varepsilon}
, we have the orthogonal decomposition
\psi(\varepsilon)=\overline{\psi}(t)+R(B_{\varepsilon}-\mu(\varepsilon))\psi(\varepsilon)
where
\overline{\psi}(t)\in E_{\Lambda}
; this follows immediately from a combination of Lemma
13
and Lemma
14
. Iterating this expression, if we have a convergent operator series, allows us to express
\psi(\varepsilon)
completely in terms of data coming from
E_{\Lambda}
. This notion motivates the main idea of Rellich’s proof as seen through the following natural generalization of this series.

Lemma
15

(Neumann series for
\psi(\varepsilon)
)
.

Let
\mu
be a free parameter and
B_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{i=1}\varepsilon^{i}A_{i}
be analytic in
\varepsilon
in the sense of Definition
9
, with each of its terms
A_{i}
having norm
\|A_{i}\|_{op}\leq c_{0}
where
c_{0}>0
is fixed. Consider
\overline{\psi}\in E_{\Lambda}
and set
S(\varepsilon)=R\circ(B_{\varepsilon}-\mu)
with
\|R\|=r_{0}
.

Then there exists
\varepsilon_{0},\mu_{0}
small enough such that for
|\varepsilon|<\varepsilon_{0}
and
|\mu|<\mu_{0}
, we have that

\psi(\varepsilon)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}S(\varepsilon)^{n}(\overline{\psi}(\varepsilon))

(16)

exists in
L^{2}
.

This element
\psi(\varepsilon)
will be shown to be our desired eigenfunction for
A_{\varepsilon}
. It should be noted that the expression for this putative eigenfunction involves only information from a fixed eigenspace for
A_{0}
, namely
E_{\Lambda}
. Also, the reason for having only a single number
c_{0}
bounding the norms of our operator-valued coefficients is because we only deal with 2nd-order differential operators on
M
, therefore yielding only 2nd-order operators with uniformly bounded real coefficients.

Proof of Lemma
15
.

We only need to verify that
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}S(t)^{n}
has a small norm for
t
sufficiently small. Hence, we must further bound
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\|R\circ(B_{\varepsilon}-\mu)\|^{n}
, which in turn leads us to bounding
\|R\circ(B_{\varepsilon}-\mu(\varepsilon))\|^{n}\leq\|R\|^{n}\cdot\|(B_{\varepsilon}-\mu(\varepsilon))\|^{n}
. If we choose
t_{0},\mu_{0}>0
small enough so that

\|R\circ(B_{\varepsilon}-\mu(\varepsilon))\|\leq r_{0}\left(\mu_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{i}c_{0}\right)<1,

we then have a convergent Neumann series. This completes our proof.
∎

Remark 17
.

In our case of Laplace-Beltrami operators arising as perturbations from that on
S^{2}
, we can take
r_{0}=1
as the spectral gaps
\delta
can be taken to be as greater than or equal to
2
always.

2.3.
Proof of Theorem
11
: Weierstrass factorization and final steps

Let us massage our series representation for the putative eigenfunction
\psi(\varepsilon)
in equation (
16
) a bit further, under the assumption that
\mu(\varepsilon)
is sufficiently small. To provide some motivation, let
E_{\Lambda}=\mbox{span}\{\varphi_{\Lambda,m}\}_{m=1}^{l}
and suppose that
\psi(\varepsilon)
is
actually
an eigenvector
A_{\varepsilon}
. Then we know
\Pi_{E_{\Lambda}}\psi(\varepsilon)=\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{l}c_{m^{\prime}}(\varepsilon)\varphi_{\Lambda,m^{\prime}}
for some values
c_{m^{\prime}}(\varepsilon)
; plugging in this linear combination into the series representation of
R(B_{\varepsilon}-\mu)
with the identity in Lemma
13
, gives

\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{l}c_{m^{\prime}}(\varepsilon)\langle\varphi_{\Lambda,m},(B_{\varepsilon}-\mu(\varepsilon))\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}S^{n}\varphi_{\Lambda,m^{\prime}}\rangle=0

(18)

for each
m=1,\dots,l
.

This set of equations (
18
) s commonly referred to as the “first solvability conditions” in asymptotics. Rellich’s idea was to remove the dependence of
\mu
on
\varepsilon
and treat it as an independent variable. If we can solve these equations in
c_{m^{\prime}}
for sufficiently small
\mu
, then the theory of zeroes of analytic functions return our desired eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

We label
v_{m,m^{\prime}}(\varepsilon):=\langle\varphi_{\Lambda,m},(B_{\varepsilon}-\mu)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}S^{n}\varphi_{\Lambda,m^{\prime}}\rangle
; note that
v_{m,m^{\prime}}(\varepsilon)=\overline{v_{m^{\prime},m}(\varepsilon)}
and hence the corresponding
l\times l
matrix is Hermitian. We lift these functions into
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{\mu,\varepsilon}
and consider the resulting determinant of

F(\mu,\varepsilon):=\det\left(v_{m,m^{\prime}}(\varepsilon)\right)_{m,m^{\prime}}

(19)

For
|\varepsilon|<\varepsilon_{0}
and
|\mu|<\mu_{0}
, we know that
F(\mu,\varepsilon)
is an analytic function in
\mu
and
\varepsilon
. Notice that in the special case of
\mu=\widetilde{\mu}(\varepsilon)=\Lambda-\Lambda(\varepsilon)
, and if
|\mu(\varepsilon)|<\mu_{0}
, then
F(\mu(\varepsilon),\varepsilon)=0
thus showing the existence of non-zero projection coefficients of
\overline{\psi}(t)
.

It is through the Weierstrass Preparation Theorem and that we can show the existence of such sufficiently small
\mu(\varepsilon)
, and therefore
\Lambda(\varepsilon)
, as encapsulated by the following proposition:

Proposition
20
.

Consider the analytic function
F(\mu,\varepsilon)
defined in (
19
). We have that
F(\mu,0)=\mu^{l}
and therefore

F(\mu,\varepsilon)=\left(\mu^{l}+p_{l-1}(\varepsilon)\mu^{l-1}+\dots+p_{1}(\varepsilon)\mu+p_{0}(\varepsilon)\right)\times E(\mu,\varepsilon)

where each
p_{i}(\varepsilon)
is analytic in
\varepsilon
and
E(\mu,\varepsilon)
is a non-vanishing analytic function in a rectangle
I(\mu_{0})
.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of the Malgrange-Weierstrass Preparation Theorem with the derivative conditions applied in
\mu
. For its rigorous statement, see the treatise by Guillemin-Golubitsky
[
10
, Chapter 4]
.
∎

Proof that Proposition
20
implies Theorem
11
.

Take
\varepsilon_{0}
and
\mu_{0}
small enough to satisfy the hypotheses of Proposition
20
. Thanks to the corresponding matrix for the determinant
F
being Hermitian,
F
is itself real and we have a complete factorization of

\left(\mu^{l}+p_{l-1}(\varepsilon)\mu^{l-1}+\dots+p_{1}(\varepsilon)\mu+p_{0}(\varepsilon)\right)

into monomials of the form
\mu-\widetilde{\mu}_{m^{\prime}}(\varepsilon)
for
m^{\prime}=1,\dots,l
where
|\mu-\widetilde{\mu}_{m^{\prime}}(\varepsilon)|\in[0,\mu_{0})
and
\varepsilon\in[0,\varepsilon_{0})
. By analyticity of
F(\mu,\varepsilon)
, it follows that
\widetilde{\mu}_{m}(\varepsilon)
is analytic for all
m^{\prime}
.

Notice that
F(\tilde{\mu}_{m}(\varepsilon),\varepsilon)=0
, implying there exists non-trivial solution vectors
\overrightarrow{c}(\varepsilon)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{l}
where

\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{l}c_{m^{\prime}}(t)v_{m,m^{\prime}}(\varepsilon)=0

(21)

for all
m=1,\dots,l
. With this, we are ready to show
\psi(\varepsilon)
as defined in Lemma
15
, with
\mu=\tilde{\mu}_{m}(\varepsilon)
and
\overline{\psi}(\varepsilon)
having the coefficients
\overrightarrow{c}(\varepsilon)
, is an eigenfunction of
A_{\varepsilon}
of eigenvalue
\Lambda_{m}(\varepsilon)=\Lambda+\tilde{\mu}_{m}(\varepsilon)
.

We have

\displaystyle(A_{0}-\Lambda)\psi(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{(A_{0}-\Lambda)R(B_{\varepsilon}-\tilde{\mu}_{m}(t))}_{\mbox{orthogonality}}\psi(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{(B_{\varepsilon}-\tilde{\mu}_{m}(\varepsilon))\psi(\varepsilon)-\Pi_{E_{\Lambda}}\left((B_{\varepsilon}-\tilde{\mu}_{m}(\varepsilon))\psi(\varepsilon)\right)}_{\mbox{Lemma \ref{lem:pseudo_inv}}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{(B_{\varepsilon}-\tilde{\mu}_{m}(\varepsilon))\psi(\varepsilon)-\sum_{m=1}^{l}\left(\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{l}c_{m^{\prime}}(\varepsilon)\langle\varphi_{\Lambda,m},(B_{\varepsilon}-\tilde{\mu}(\varepsilon))\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}S^{n}\varphi_{\Lambda,m^{\prime}}\rangle\right)\varphi_{\Lambda,m}}_{\mbox{Lemma \ref{lem:neumann}}}

\displaystyle=(B_{\varepsilon}-\tilde{\mu}_{m}(\varepsilon))\psi(\varepsilon)

with the last equation following from the implications of
F(\tilde{\mu}_{m}(\varepsilon),\varepsilon)=0
namely (
21
). Therefore,
A_{\varepsilon}\psi(\varepsilon)=\left(\Lambda+\tilde{\mu}_{m}(\varepsilon)\right)\psi(\varepsilon)
, and finally yielding the eigenfunction we aimed for. Since
A(\varepsilon)
is self-adjoint and
\Lambda
is real, so is
\mu(\varepsilon)
. By positivity of

, we know that
\Lambda+\tilde{\mu}(\varepsilon)\geq 0
and
|\tilde{\mu}(\varepsilon)|\leq\delta
. Orthonormality follows from executing the Gram-Schmit procedure.

To prove the final part of the theorem, let
\Lambda^{\prime}=\Lambda+(d_{2}-d_{1})
. Then the operator norm of
\|\left(A_{0}+(2\delta)\Pi_{E_{\Lambda}}\right)-\Lambda^{\prime}\|_{op}
is bounded below, as
A_{0}+(2\delta)\Pi_{E_{\Lambda}}
has no spectrum in

, and this continues to be the case in the interval
[-d_{1}+\Lambda,d_{2}+\Lambda]
for parameters
|t|<t_{1}(\Lambda,\delta)
where
\varepsilon_{1}\leq\varepsilon_{0}
thanks to analyticity which in turn provides continuity of our operator norms in

. This completes our proof.
∎

3.
Biaxial Ellipsoids in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

3.1.
Coordinate calcuations and reduced equations

Let
\varphi\in(0,\pi)
and
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
. A natural set of ellipsoidal coordinates are
(a\sin\varphi\cos\theta,a\sin\varphi\cos\theta,b\cos\varphi)
. We don’t make any assumptions on the sizes of
a
or
b
yet. The induced metric in these coordinates is of the form

\displaystyle g_{11}=a^{2}\cos^{2}\varphi+b^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi,g_{12}=g_{21}=0,g_{22}=a^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi.

Notice that the functions
g_{11}
and
g_{22}
are, respectively, the squared chord length for the ellipse in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
given by
x^{2}/a^{2}+z^{2}/b^{2}
squared and the squared radius of the
S^{1}
cross section of our ellipsoid

Therefore the corresponding Laplace-Beltrami operator takes the form

\Delta_{g_{0}}=(a^{2}\cos^{2}\varphi+b^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi)^{-1}\partial_{\varphi\varphi}+(a^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi)^{-1}\partial_{\theta\theta}+h(\varphi)\partial_{\varphi},

(22)

where
h(\varphi)=\sqrt{\det(g)}^{-1}\partial_{\varphi}(\sqrt{\det(g)}(a^{2}\cos^{2}\varphi+b^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi)^{-1})
. In the upcoming sections, we set
a=1+\alpha\varepsilon
and
b=1+\beta\varepsilon
where
\alpha,\beta\neq 0
independent in
\varepsilon
. For
\varepsilon_{0}=1/2
, the metric
g_{\varepsilon}
for

is analytic and admits a finite polynomial expansion in
\varepsilon
. Hence, Theorem
6
applies and we can perform calculations using analytic series in
\varepsilon

\varepsilon_{0}
.

Thanks to the natural

action on

, basic representation theory (see for instance Terras’ treatise
[
23
]
) tells us that

has a basis consisting of separable eigenfunctions of the form
u(\varphi)e^{im\theta}
where

. Plugging this ansatz into the Laplace-Beltrami operator and performing the standard calculations leads to the following separated equations written in Sturm-Liouville form:

\frac{\left(\frac{a\sin\varphi}{\sqrt{a^{2}\cos^{2}\varphi+b^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi}}\partial_{\varphi}\left(\frac{a\sin\varphi}{\sqrt{a^{2}\cos^{2}\varphi+b^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi}}\partial_{\varphi}\ \right)+\Lambda a^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi\right)u}{u}=m^{2}=-\frac{\partial_{\theta\theta}f}{f}

where
f(\theta)=e^{im\theta}
. Thus, the factor
u
satisfies the following reduced equation

\left(\frac{a\sin\varphi}{\sqrt{a^{2}\cos^{2}\varphi+b^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi}}\partial_{\varphi}\left(\frac{a\sin\varphi}{\sqrt{a^{2}\cos^{2}\varphi+b^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi}}\partial_{\varphi}\ \right)+\Lambda a^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi\right)u-m^{2}u=0,

(23)

exhibiting some dependence on the integral parameter

; in the upcoming calculations, we incorporate the parameter
a^{2}
into
\Lambda
and abuse notation by calling the new eigenvalue
\Lambda(\varepsilon)
. We will use these equations to compute approximations for the purported basis of

.

3.2.
Deriving the first solvability condition

The theory of 2nd-order ODEs tells us that the eigenvalues
\Lambda(\varepsilon)
, for each
m
, are simple. Thanks to the term
m^{2}
and
therefore being able to use
\pm m
to generate potentially different
eigenfunctions in
\varphi
, we know that we can write each solution in
\varphi

as either
u_{m}^{+}
or
u_{-m}
: it is of these
u_{\pm m}
that we take
the expansion and in turn approximate
\Lambda^{2}(\varepsilon)
, which we
see has multiplicity at least
2
for
m\neq 0
and multiplicity at least 1
when
m=0
.

Theorem
11
holds for the Legendre equation on
[-1,1]
. Hence
we know that given
l(l+1)\in spec(S^{2})
, there exists
\varepsilon_{0}(S^{2},l(l+1))
such that the following expansions are valid:

\displaystyle\Lambda(\varepsilon)=l(l+1)+\varepsilon\Lambda_{1}+\varepsilon^{2}\Lambda_{2}+\dots

\displaystyle u_{\pm m}(\varphi,\varepsilon)=u_{0}(\varphi)+\varepsilon u_{1}(\varphi)+\varepsilon^{2}u_{2}(\varphi)+\dots.

We make note that we are abusing notation by using
\Lambda_{1}
to represent the first-order coefficient of
\Lambda
; as we have fixed the eigenvalue
\Lambda
, this use is unambiguous.

The simplicity of the Sturm-Liouville spectrum is used when writing the
order 0 (in
\varepsilon
) term for
u_{\pm m}
. To these expansions, we apply

A_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\varphi}\left(\frac{\sin\varphi}{\sqrt{a^{2}\cos^{2}\varphi+b^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi}}\partial_{\varphi}\left(\frac{\sin\varphi}{\sqrt{a^{2}\cos^{2}\varphi+b^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi}}\partial_{\varphi}\right)-\frac{m^{2}}{a^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi}\right)

and work out the formal series for

A_{\varepsilon}u_{\pm m}(\varepsilon)=-\Lambda(\varepsilon)u_{\pm m}(\varepsilon).

We expand
A_{\varepsilon}
in
\varepsilon
to obtain

A_{\varepsilon}=A_{0}+\varepsilon A_{1}+O(\varepsilon^{2})

where the big-O notation means that the “implicit” object
is a 2nd-order differential operator, and with

\displaystyle A_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{\varphi\varphi}+\cot\varphi\partial_{\varphi}-\frac{m^{2}}{\sin^{2}\varphi}

(24)

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\alpha-\beta)\left(2\sin^{2}\varphi\partial_{\varphi\varphi}+2\sin(2\varphi)\partial_{\varphi}\right)-2\alpha A_{0}

(25)

A gathering of the 0th-order and 1st-order terms in
\varepsilon
yield the
following two equations:

\displaystyle A_{0}u_{0}

\displaystyle=-l(l+1)u_{0},\mbox{ and }

(26)

\displaystyle A_{0}u_{1}+l(l+1)u_{1}

\displaystyle=-A_{1}u_{0}-\Lambda_{1}u_{0}.

(27)

The solution to (
26
) is given by the spherical harmonics
u_{0}=c_{0}P_{l}^{m}(\cos\varphi)
where
c_{0}
is any constant.

The operator
A_{0}
is self-adjoint with respect to the measure
\sin\varphi d\varphi
, whose corresponding inner product we denote by
\langle,\rangle
.
Taking the product of both sides of (
27
) with respect to
u_{0}
we
then obtain:
\left\langle u_{0},A_{0}u_{1}+l(l+1)u_{1}\right\rangle=\left\langle u_{1},A_{0}u_{0}+l(l+1)u_{0}\right\rangle=0,
so that
-\langle u_{0},A_{1}u_{0}\rangle-\Lambda_{1}\langle u_{0},u_{0}\rangle=0

or

\Lambda_{1}=\frac{-\langle u_{0},A_{1}u_{0}\rangle}{\langle u_{0},u_{0}\rangle}.

(28)

Next we compute,

A_{1}u_{0}=\left(\alpha-\beta\right)\left\{\left(\left(-l\left(l+1\right)2\sin^{2}\varphi+2m^{2}\right)u_{0}+2\sin\varphi\cos\varphi\partial_{\varphi}u_{0}\right)\right\}+2\alpha l\left(l+1\right)u_{0}

where we used the Legendre equation (
26
) to rewrite
\partial_{\varphi\varphi}u_{0}=\left(-\cot\varphi\partial_{\varphi}+\frac{m^{2}}{\sin^{2}\varphi}-l(l+1)\right)u_{0}.
Therefore (
28
) yields the formula

\Lambda_{1}=\left(\beta-\alpha\right)\frac{\int\left\{\left(-2l\left(l+1\right)\sin^{2}\varphi+2m^{2}\right)u_{0}+2\sin\left(\varphi\right)\cos\varphi\partial_{\varphi}u_{0}\right\}u_{0}\sin\varphi d\varphi}{\int_{0}^{\pi}u_{0}^{2}\sin\varphi d\varphi.}-2\alpha l\left(l+1\right).

Changing variables
\cos\varphi=t,
we then obtain

\Lambda_{1}=\left(\beta-\alpha\right)\frac{\int_{-1}^{1}\left\{\left(-2l\left(l+1\right)\left(1-t^{2}\right)+2m^{2}\right)P^{2}(t)-2\left(1-t^{2}\right)tP^{\prime}(t)P(t)\right\}dt}{\int_{0}^{\pi}P(t)^{2}dt.}-2\alpha l\left(l+1\right)

(29)

where
P(t)=\frac{\partial^{l+m}}{dt^{(l+m)}}\left[\left(1-t^{l}\right)^{m}\right].
Integrating by parts, we rewrite:

\int_{-1}^{1}2\left(1-t^{2}\right)tP^{\prime}P=-\int_{-1}^{1}\left(1-3t^{2}\right)P^{2}

so that (
29
) becomes

\Lambda_{1}=\left(\beta-\alpha\right)\left\{\left(-2l\left(l+1\right)+2m^{2}+1\right)+(2l\left(l+1\right)-3)\frac{\int_{-1}^{1}t^{2}P^{2}dt}{\int_{0}^{\pi}P^{2}dt.}\right\}-2\alpha l\left(l+1\right)

Finally, the ratio
\frac{\int_{-1}^{1}t^{2}P^{2}dt}{\int_{-1}^{1}P^{2}}=\frac{2l^{2}-2m^{2}+2l-1}{\left(2l+3\right)\left(2l-1\right)}
is
evaluated in Appendix
6
. This yields formula (
3
).

The multiplicity statement in Theorem
2
follows easily from here
after taking into account that for each
l(l+1)
, we get a single analytic
eigenvalue for
m=0
and get a double analytic eigenvalue for each
m=\pm 1,\dots,\pm l
. Now apply Corollary
12
which implies that
the multiplicities cannot be higher than 2.

4.

\mathbb{R}^{3}

4.1.
Coordinate calculations

We now pursue the calculation of eigenvalues for triaxial ellipsoids. For
organizational reasons, let us write
\Delta_{g}
in coordinates. In

\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we take the coordinates
(a\cos\varphi\cos\theta,b\cos\varphi\sin\theta,c\sin\varphi)
where
\varphi\in(0,\pi)
and
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
.
This leads us the following expression of
\Delta_{g}
as follows:

\Delta_{g}=A\partial_{\varphi\varphi}+B\partial_{\varphi\theta}+C\partial_{\theta\theta}+E\partial_{\varphi}+F\partial_{\theta}

where

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle:=\frac{g_{22}}{D},\ \ B:=-2\frac{g_{12}}{D},\ \ C:=\frac{g_{11}}{D},

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle:=D^{-1/2}\left(\left(g_{22}D^{-1/2}\right)_{\varphi}-\left(g_{12}D^{-1/2}\right)_{\theta}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial_{\varphi}g_{22}}{D}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{g_{22}}{D^{2}}\partial_{\varphi}D-\frac{\partial_{\theta}g_{12}}{D}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{g_{12}}{D^{2}}\partial_{\theta}D

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle:=D^{-1/2}\left(\left(g_{11}D^{-1/2}\right)_{\theta}-\left(g_{12}D^{-1/2}\right)_{\varphi}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial_{\theta}g_{11}}{D}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{g_{11}}{D^{2}}\partial_{\theta}D-\frac{\partial_{\varphi}g_{12}}{D}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{g_{12}}{D^{2}}\partial_{\varphi}D

where

\displaystyle D(\theta,\varphi)

\displaystyle:=g_{11}g_{22}-g_{12}g_{21}

\displaystyle g_{11}(\theta,\varphi)

\displaystyle:=\cos^{2}\varphi\left(a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta+b^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\right)+c^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi

\displaystyle g_{22}(\theta,\varphi)

\displaystyle:=\sin^{2}\varphi\left(a^{2}\sin^{2}\theta+b^{2}\cos^{2}\theta\right)

\displaystyle g_{12}(\theta,\varphi)

\displaystyle:=g_{21}=\left(b^{2}-a^{2}\right)\frac{\sin\left(2\varphi\right)\sin\left(2\theta\right)}{4}

4.2.
Deriving the first solvability condition

We want our triaxial ellipsoid to be a small perturbation of
S^{2}
, so we
set

a:=1+\alpha\varepsilon,\ \ \ b:=1+\beta\varepsilon,\ \ c:=1+\gamma\varepsilon

Note that our metric coefficients
g_{ij}
are analytic and non-vanishing in

\varepsilon
, therefore making the inverse metric
g^{ij}

6
.

Thus, we can carefully massage
\Delta_{g}
into an analytic

\Delta_{g}=A_{0}+\varepsilon A_{1}+\varepsilon^{2}A_{2}(\varepsilon)

where

A_{0}=\partial_{\varphi\varphi}+\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\varphi}\partial_{\theta\theta}+\frac{\cos\varphi}{\sin\varphi}\partial_{\varphi}

and

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle:=\left[\left(2\beta-2\alpha\right)\cos^{2}\theta\cos^{2}\varphi+\left(2\gamma-2\beta\right)\cos^{2}\varphi-2\gamma\right]\partial_{\varphi\varphi}+{\frac{\left(\left(2\alpha-2\beta\right)\cos^{2}\theta-2\alpha\right)}{\sin^{2}\varphi}}\partial_{\theta\theta}

\displaystyle+4\left(\alpha-\beta\right){\frac{\cos\varphi\sin\theta\cos\theta}{\sin\varphi}}\partial_{\varphi\theta}+4\left(\beta-\alpha\right){\frac{\sin\theta\cos\theta}{\sin^{2}\varphi}}\partial_{\theta}

\displaystyle+4{\left[\left(\left(\beta-\alpha\right)\left(\cos\left(\theta\right)\right)^{2}-\beta+\gamma\right)\cos^{2}\varphi+\frac{3}{2}\left(\alpha-\beta\right)\cos^{2}\theta-\frac{\alpha}{2}+\beta-\gamma\right]}\frac{\cos\varphi}{\sin\varphi}\partial_{\varphi}.

Now, we set

to be a solution of

\Delta_{g}u=-\Lambda(\varepsilon)u.

Expanding similarly as in Section

thanks to Theorem

, we are can again write the following two analytic series
in
\varepsilon:

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=u_{0}+\varepsilon u_{1}+\ldots

\displaystyle\Lambda(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=l(l+1)+\varepsilon\Lambda_{1}+\ldots

Gathering terms in
\varepsilon
yields the 0th-order equation of

A_{0}u_{0}=-l(l+1)u_{0}

for

and whose solution is given by

\displaystyle u_{0}

\displaystyle=C_{0}v_{0}(\theta,\varphi)+\sum_{m=1}^{l}C_{m}v_{m}(\theta,\varphi)+D_{m}w_{m}(\theta,\varphi),\ \ \ \text{where}

\displaystyle v_{m}

\displaystyle=\cos(m\theta)P_{l}^{m}(\cos(\varphi));\ \ w_{m}=\sin(m\theta)P_{l}^{m}(\cos(\varphi));

the set
\{w_{0},\{w_{m},v_{m}\}_{m=1}^{l}\}
form a basis for the space of
spherical harmonics with eigenvalue

with the
P_{l}^{m}

P_{l}^{m}(t)=A_{l,m}\left(1-t^{2}\right)^{m/2}\frac{\partial^{m+l}}{\partial t^{m+l}}\left[\left(1-t^{2}\right)^{l}\right].

The normalization constants
A_{l,m}
are chosen so that

\left\langle v_{m},v_{m}\right\rangle=\left\langle w_{m},w_{m}\right\rangle=1,

(30)

where the inner product

, that is

\left\langle v,w\right\rangle:=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}v(\theta,\varphi)w(\theta,\varphi)\sin\varphi d\varphi d\theta.

At order

A_{0}u_{1}+l(l+1)u_{1}=-\Lambda_{1}u_{0}-A_{1}u_{0}.

(31)

Note that
A_{0}
is self-adjoint with respect to the inner product

\langle,\rangle

\displaystyle\left\langle v_{k},u_{0}\right\rangle\Lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=-\left\langle v_{k},A_{1}u_{0}\right\rangle,\ \ \ k=0\ldots l,

\displaystyle\left\langle w_{k},u_{0}\right\rangle\Lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=-\left\langle w_{k},A_{1}u_{0}\right\rangle,\ \ \ k=1\ldots l.

Thanks to orthonormality, we have that
\left\langle v_{k}u_{0}\right\rangle=C_{k}
and
\left\langle w_{k}u_{0}\right\rangle=D_{k}.

Thus, in contrast to the biaxial case, the first solvability equation returns
a more complicated system of linear equations to solve. This eigenvalue
problem problem for

can be read off as

M\overline{V}=\Lambda_{1}\overline{V}

where

is the

2l+1
column vector containing coefficients

and

We now seek to simplify the “matrix elements”

4.3.
Expanding into Fourier modes

To execute this simplification of the quantities
\left\langle v_{m},A_{1}u_{0}\right\rangle
, we expand in terms of Fourier modes on
S^{1}
in the variable

\theta
. For simplicity of notation, set

. We
find that

A_{1}\left(\cos(m\theta)P\right)=g_{m-}(\varphi)\cos\left(\left(m-2\right)\theta\right)+g_{m}(\varphi)\cos\left(m\theta\right)+g_{m+}(\varphi)\cos\left(\left(m+2\right)\theta\right)

(33a)

and

A_{1}\left(\sin(m\theta)P\right)=g_{m-}(\varphi)\sin\left(\left(m-2\right)\theta\right)+g_{m}(\varphi)\sin\left(m\theta\right)+g_{m+}(\varphi)\sin\left(\left(m+2\right)\theta\right)

(33b)

and

\displaystyle g_{m-}(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2}\left(l\left(l+1\right)\sin^{2}\varphi+\left(-l(l+1)-m^{2}\right)+\frac{2m(m-1)}{\sin^{2}\varphi}\right)P

\displaystyle+\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2}\left({-\cos\varphi\sin\varphi+}\frac{2(m-1)\cos\varphi}{\sin\varphi}\right)P_{\varphi},

(34a)

\displaystyle g_{m+}(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2}\left(l\left(l+1\right)\sin^{2}\varphi+\left(-l(l+1)-m^{2}\right)+\frac{2m(m+1)}{\sin^{2}\varphi}\right)P

\displaystyle+\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2}\left({-\cos\varphi\sin\varphi+}\frac{2(-m-1)\cos\varphi}{\sin\varphi}\right)P_{\varphi},

(34b)

\displaystyle g_{m}(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\left(\left(\alpha+\beta-2\gamma\right){m}^{2}+l\left(l+1\right)2\gamma+\left(\alpha+\beta-2\gamma\right)l(l+1)\cos^{2}\varphi\right)P+

\displaystyle\left(\alpha+\beta-2\gamma\right)\sin\left(\varphi\right)\cos\left(\varphi\right)P_{\varphi}.

(34c)

In above expressions, we have used the standard Legendre ODE (albeit with

in order to

In total, each quantity

\displaystyle\left\langle v_{m},A_{1}u_{0}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle v_{m},A_{1}v_{m}\right\rangle C_{m}+\left\langle v_{m},A_{1}v_{m+2}\right\rangle C_{m+2}+\left\langle v_{m},A_{1}v_{m-2}\right\rangle C_{m-2}

n

n
.
