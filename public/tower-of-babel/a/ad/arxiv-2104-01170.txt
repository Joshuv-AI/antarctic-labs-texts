Characterization of the dissipative mappings and their application to perturbations of dissipative-Hamiltonian systems
Mohit Kumar BaghelNicolas Gillis, Punit Sharma

Abstract
In this paper, we find necessary and sufficient conditions to identify pairs of matrices X X and Y Y for which there exists Δ ∈ ℂ n , n \Delta\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,n} such that Δ + Δ ∗ \Delta+\Delta^{*} is positive semidefinite and Δ ​ X = Y \Delta X=Y . Such a Δ \Delta is called a dissipative mapping taking X X to Y Y . We also provide two different characterizations for the set of all dissipative mappings, and use them to characterize the unique dissipative mapping with minimal Frobenius norm. The minimal-norm dissipative mapping is then used to determine the distance to asymptotic instability for dissipative-Hamiltonian systems under general structure-preserving perturbations. We illustrate our results over some numerical examples and compare them with those of Mehl, Mehrmann and Sharma (Stability Radii for Linear Hamiltonian Systems with Dissipation Under Structure-Preserving Perturbations, SIAM J. Mat. Anal. Appl. 37 (4): 1625-1654, 2016).

Introduction

In this paper, we consider the dissipative mapping problem.
Given
X,Y\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,m}
, the dissipative mapping problem can be divided in three subproblems:

1)

Existence
: find necessary and sufficient conditions on matrices
X
and
Y
for the existence of matrices
\Delta\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,n}
such that
\Delta+\Delta^{*}
is positive semidefinite and
\Delta X=Y
.

2)

Characterization
: characterize all such dissipative matrices taking
X
to
Y
.

3)

Minimal norm
: characterize all solutions to the dissipative mapping problem that have minimal norm. In this paper, we focus on the Frobenius norm, which is the standard in the literature.

The dissipative mapping problem belongs to a wider class of mapping problems:
for
X,Y\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,m}
, the structured mapping problems require to find
\Delta\in\mathcal{S}\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}^{n,n}
such that
\Delta X=Y
, where
\mathcal{S}
stands for the structure of the mapping.
The mapping problems have been extensively studied in
[
12
]
and
[
1
,
2
]
for the structures that are associated with orthosymmetric scalar products.
These include symmetric, skew-symmetric, Hermitian, skew-Hermitian, Hamiltonian, skew-Hamiltonian, Hermitian, skew-Hermitian, persymmetric, and per-skew symmetric matrices. If
\mathcal{S}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n,n}
, then it is the real structured mapping problem. In particular, for given
X,Y\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,m}
, the real dissipative mapping problem is to find
\Delta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n,n}
such that
\Delta+\Delta^{T}
is positive semidefinite and
\Delta X=Y
.

The structured mapping problems occur and are useful in solving various distance problems related to structured matrices and matrix
polynomials, see for example
[
5
,
6
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
and the references therein.
A mapping problem closely related to the dissipative mapping is the positive semidefinite (PSD) mapping, when
\mathcal{S}
is the cone of Hermitian positive semidefinite matrices.
The PSD mapping problem has been recently solved and used in
[
16
,
17
]
to derive formulas for structured distances to instability for linear time-invariant dissipative Hamiltonian (DH) systems.
DH systems have the form

\dot{x}(t)=(J-R)Qx(t),

(1.1)

where
x(t)
is the state vector at time
t
,

J^{*}=-J\in\mathbb{F}^{n,n}
,

R^{*}=R\in\mathbb{F}^{n,n}
is positive semidefinite and is referred to as the dissipation matrix, and
Q=Q^{*}\in\mathbb{F}^{n,n}
is positive definite and describes the energy of the system via the function
x\mapsto x^{*}Qx
. When the coefficient matrices in (
1.1
) are complex, that is,.
\mathbb{F}={\mathbb{C}}
, then it is called a complex DH system, and when the coefficient matrices in (
1.1
) are real, that is,
\mathbb{F}={\mathbb{R}}
, then it is called a real DH system.
Such systems are special cases of port-Hamiltonian systems; see for example
[
8
,
20
,
21
,
16
,
17
]
.
A linear time-invariant (LTI) control system
\dot{x}=Ax
with
A\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,n}
is called
asymptotically stable
around its equilibrium point at the origin if
(i) for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\delta_{1}>0

such that if
\|x(t_{0})\|<\delta_{1}
, then
\|x(t)\|<\epsilon
, for all
t>t_{0}
, and
(ii) there exists
\delta_{2}>0
such that if
\|x(t_{0})\|<\delta_{2}
, then
x(t)\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
. It is called
stable
if only (i) holds. An equivalent algebraic characterization of stability is given in terms of spectral conditions on
A
: the system
\dot{x}=Ax
is called stable if all eigenvalues of the matrix
A
are in the closed left half of the complex plane and those on the imaginary axis are semisimple. It is asymptotically stable if all eigenvalues of
A
are in the open left half of the complex plane.

Any LTI stable system can be represented in the form (
1.1
) of a DH system
[
7
]
. DH systems are always stable and remain stable as long as the perturbations preserve the DH structure. Still, they may have eigenvalues on the imaginary axis, i.e., they are not necessarily asymptotically stable. Therefore it is useful to know the
distance to asymptotic instability
for DH systems
[
16
,
17
]
defined as the smallest norm of the perturbation that makes system lose its asymptotic stability.
Studying this question is an essential topic in many applications, like in power system and circuit simulation, see, e.g.,
[
14
,
15
,
13
]
, and multi-body systems, see, e.g.,
[
9
,
22
]
.
This is also useful in some specific applications,
e.g., the analysis of disk brake squeal
[
16
, Example 1.1]
, of mass-spring-damper dynamical systems
[
17
, Example 4.1]
, and of circuit simulation and power system modelling
[
16
, Example 1.2]
, where the interest is in studying the stability or the instability under perturbation of the matrices

J
,
R
and
Q
. In the DH modelling of physical systems, the matrix
J
describes the energy flux among the system’s energy storage elements, and
R
represents the damping effects in the system. Thus perturbing
J

and
R
together, or only one at a time, is of particular interest.

In
[
16
]
, the authors derived various distances to asymptotic instability for DH systems (
1.1
) while focusing on perturbations that affect only one matrix from
\{J,R,Q\}
at a time.
Similarly in
[
17
]
, the authors derived real distances to instability for real DH systems while perturbing only the dissipation matrix
R
.
The framework suggested in
[
16
]
depends heavily on reformulating the instability radius problem in terms of an equivalent problem of minimizing the generalized Rayleigh quotient of two positive semidefinite matrices. This reformulation was achieved using minimal norm skew-Hermitian mappings in case only
J
is perturbed,
and PSD mappings in case only
R
or
Q
is perturbed.
However, we note that the framework suggested in
[
16
]
does not work if we allow perturbations in the DH system that affect more than one matrix from
\{J,R,Q\}
at a time.
Analyzing the stability of the system (
1.1
) when more than one matrix from
\{J,R,Q\}
is perturbed is one of the main motivations of our work.

In this paper, we focus on perturbations of DH systems that affect both
J
and
R
simultaneously. More precisely, we find (see Section
5
) that the minimal-norm solution to the dissipative mapping problem can be a necessary tool in computing the structured distance to asymptotic instability for DH systems (
1.1
) when both
J
and
R
are simultaneously perturbed.

Contributions and outline of the paper

In Section 2, we present some preliminary results that will be needed to solve the
dissipative mapping problem. For the solutions to the
dissipative mapping problem, we present two different characterizations in terms of three matrix variables
K,G,Z
with symmetry and semidefinite structures. Both characterizations have an advantage over each other. The first characterization (see Section 3) results in a straightforward computation of the minimal-norm solutions to the dissipative mapping problem, but the matrix variables
K,G,Z
are highly constrained. We also derive necessary and sufficient conditions for solving the real dissipative mapping problem and compute solutions that are of minimal Frobenius norm.
The second characterization (see Section 4) has the advantage of having a simple form in terms of matrix variables
K,G
, and
Z
. On the other hand, it is unclear how to find minimal-norm solutions via this second characterization.
The minimal-norm dissipative mapping is used in Section 5 for studying the structured distance to instability for DH systems (
1.1
) for simultaneous perturbations of
J
and
R
.
In Section 6, we present numerical examples comparing these distances to instability with those of
[
16
]
where perturbations affect only one of
the matrices
J
,
R
, or
Q
.

3
First characterization of dissipative mappings

In this section, we derive the first characterization of dissipative mappings.
This characterization allows us to find the minimal Frobenius norm solutions to
the dissipative mapping problem which is shown to be unique. This minimal-norm solution will turn out to be a necessary tool in computing the structured stability radius in Section
5
. For given
X\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,m}
and
Y\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,m}
, we define the set of dissipative mappings from
X
to
Y
as follows

\mathbb{S}(X,Y):=\{\Delta\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,n}~:~\Delta+\Delta^{*}\succeq 0,~\Delta X=Y\}.

We will need the following lemma in characterizing the set of all solutions to the dissipative mapping problem.

Lemma 3.1

Let
X,Y\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,m}
. Suppose that
\text{{\rm rank}}(X)=p
and consider the reduced singular value decomposition
X=U_{1}\Sigma_{1}{V}_{1}^{*}
with

U_{1}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,p}
,
\Sigma_{1}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{p,p}
and
V_{1}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{m,p}
. If
X^{*}Y+Y^{*}X\succeq 0
, then
U_{1}^{*}\left(YX^{\dagger}+{(YX^{\dagger})}^{*}\right)U_{1}\succeq 0
.

Proof
. Since
X=U_{1}\Sigma_{1}{V}_{1}^{*}
, we have
X^{\dagger}=V_{1}\Sigma_{1}^{-1}U_{1}^{*}
. Thus

\displaystyle X^{*}Y+Y^{*}X\succeq 0

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle V_{1}\Sigma_{1}U_{1}^{*}Y+Y^{*}U_{1}\Sigma_{1}V_{1}^{*}\succeq 0

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle V_{1}^{*}\left(V_{1}\Sigma_{1}U_{1}^{*}Y+Y^{*}U_{1}\Sigma_{1}V_{1}^{*}\right)V_{1}\succeq 0\hskip 10.00002pt(\because\,\text{using\,Lemma}\,\ref{psdsimilar})

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}U_{1}^{*}YV_{1}+V_{1}^{*}Y^{*}U_{1}\Sigma_{1}\succeq 0\hskip 42.67912pt(\because V_{1}^{*}V_{1}=I_{p})

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}^{-1}\left(\Sigma_{1}U_{1}^{*}YV_{1}+V_{1}^{*}Y^{*}U_{1}\Sigma_{1}\right)\Sigma_{1}^{-1}\succeq 0

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle U_{1}^{*}YV_{1}\Sigma_{1}^{-1}+\Sigma_{1}^{-1}V_{1}^{*}Y^{*}U_{1}\succeq 0

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle U_{1}^{*}\left(YV_{1}\Sigma_{1}^{-1}U_{1}^{*}+U_{1}\Sigma_{1}^{-1}V_{1}^{*}Y^{*}\right)U_{1}\succeq 0

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle U_{1}^{*}\left(YX^{\dagger}+{(YX^{\dagger})}^{*}\right)U_{1}\succeq 0,

which completes the proof.

Remark 4.2

The assumption that
X^{*}Y+Y^{*}X
is invertible
is not necessary for the existence of a dissipative mapping which only requires

to be positive semidefinite; see Theorem
3.2
.
This assumption is more of a technical need for the second characterization of dissipative mappings in Theorem
4.1
. However,
there are situations, such as computing the eigenpair backward error for DH matrices, where one has to find dissipative mappings taking
X\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,r}
to
Y\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,r}
such that
X^{*}Y+Y^{*}X
is invertible.
For example, consider a DH matrix
J-R
with
Q=I_{n}
,
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
which is not an eigenvalue of
J-R
,
and
\hat{X}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,r}
with
\text{rank}(\hat{X})=r
. Then the structured eigenpair backward error for the DH matrix
J-R
,
for making
\lambda
an eigenvalue of multiplicity
r
and the columns of
\hat{X}
as the corresponding eigenvectors, is the smallest norm of the perturbations
\Delta_{J}-\Delta_{R}
such that

(J-\Delta_{J})+(R-\Delta_{R})
is a DH matrix and

((J-\Delta_{J})+(R-\Delta_{R}))\hat{X}=\lambda\hat{X}
. By Lemma
2.7
, the latter condition is equivalent to finding a dissipative mapping
\Delta
such that
\Delta\hat{X}=\hat{Y}
, where
\hat{Y}=(\lambda I_{n}-(J-R))\hat{X}
.
Such a dissipative mapping exists if and only if
\hat{X}^{*}\hat{Y}+\hat{Y}^{*}\hat{X}=\hat{X}^{*}(\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}{(\lambda)}I_{n}+R)\hat{X}\succeq 0
. Hence if
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}{(\lambda)}>0
, then
\hat{X}^{*}\hat{Y}+\hat{Y}^{*}\hat{X}\succ 0
since
R\succeq 0
.

A result analogous to Corollary
3.3
for the vector case (when

), where the conditions on the vectors

and

, is stated below.

Corollary 4.3

Let
x,y\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus{\{0\}}
.
Then
\mathbb{S}(x,y)\neq\emptyset
if and only if
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}{(x^{*}y)}\geq 0
.
Moreover, if
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}{(x^{*}y)}>0
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}(x,y)=\{H+\widetilde{H}(K,G,Z)\mid K,G,Z\in\mathbb{C}^{n,n},K\succeq 0,~G^{*}=-G\},

where
H
and
\widetilde{H}
are respectively defined as

H:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{yx^{*}}{\|x\|^{2}}-\frac{xy^{*}}{\|x\|^{2}}\right)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\{yy^{*}+(x^{*}y)\frac{yx^{*}}{\|x\|^{2}}+(y^{*}x)\frac{xy^{*}}{\|x\|^{2}}+(|x^{*}y|^{2})\frac{xx^{*}}{\|x\|^{4}}\right\}

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}(K,G,Z):=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\mathcal{P}_{x}K\mathcal{P}_{x}+\mathcal{P}_{x}G\mathcal{P}_{x}+(\alpha(y^{*}x)+1)xx^{\dagger}Z\mathcal{P}_{x}+(\alpha(x^{*}y)-1)\mathcal{P}_{x}Z^{*}xx^{\dagger}

\displaystyle+\alpha\mathcal{P}_{x}Z^{*}xy^{*}+\alpha yx^{*}Z\mathcal{P}_{x}+\alpha\mathcal{P}_{x}Z^{*}xx^{*}Z\mathcal{P}_{x}\Big),

where

.

5
Structured distance to asymptotic instability for DH systems

In this section, we study the distance to asymptotic instability for DH systems

\dot{x}=(J-R)Qx
defined in (

).
DH systems are always stable, but they are not necessarily asymptotically stable.
In

, the authors have obtained

R

Q

17

\hat{\Delta}+\hat{\Delta}^{*}\succeq 0
. Set
\hat{\Delta}_{R}=-\frac{\hat{\Delta}+\hat{\Delta}^{*}}{2}

and
\hat{\Delta}_{J}=\frac{\hat{\Delta}-\hat{\Delta}^{*}}{2}
. Then
\hat{\Delta}_{R}\preceq 0
. Thus, if
R+B\hat{\Delta}_{R}B^{*}\succeq 0
, then
clearly
(\hat{\Delta}_{J},\hat{\Delta}_{R})\in S_{d}^{{\mathbb{C}}}(J,R;B)
, and

{\|\hat{\Delta}_{J}\|}_{F}^{2}+{\|\hat{\Delta}_{R}\|}_{F}^{2}={\|\hat{\Delta}\|}_{F}^{2}=\frac{2{\|B^{\dagger}(iwI_{n}-(J-R)Q)\hat{x}\|}^{2}}{{\|B^{*}Q\hat{x}\|}^{2}}-\frac{|\hat{x}^{*}Q(iwI_{n}-(J-R)Q)\hat{x}|^{2}}{{\|B^{*}Q\hat{x}\|}^{4}}.

This show the equality in (
5.15
) and hence in (
5.14
), i.e.,

\eta_{{\mathbb{C}}}^{S_{d}}(J,R;B,iw)^{2}=\inf_{x\in\Omega}\left\{\frac{2{\|B^{\dagger}(iwI_{n}-(J-R)Q)x\|}^{2}}{{\|B^{*}Qx\|}^{2}}-\frac{|x^{*}Q(iwI_{n}-(J-R)Q)x|^{2}}{{\|B^{*}Qx\|}^{4}}\right\}.

(5.16)

Now suppose
k=\text{dim}(\Omega)
and let columns of
U\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,k}
forms an orthonormal basis for
\Omega
. Then
x(\neq 0)\in\Omega
if and only if

x=U\alpha
for some
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}^{k}\setminus\{0\}
. Using this in (
5.16
), we obtain

\eta_{{\mathbb{C}}}^{S_{d}}(J,R;B,iw)^{2}=\inf_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}^{k}\setminus\{0\}}\left\{\frac{2{\|B^{\dagger}(iwI_{n}-(J-R)Q)U\alpha\|}^{2}}{{\|B^{*}QU\alpha\|}^{2}}-\frac{|\alpha^{*}U^{*}Q(iwI_{n}-(J-R)Q)U\alpha|^{2}}{{\|B^{*}QU\alpha\|}^{4}}\right\}.

(5.17)

Note that
B^{*}QU
is a full rank matrix because if suppose
B^{*}QU\alpha=0
for some
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}^{k}\setminus\{0\}
. This implies that

0=B\hat{\Delta}B^{*}QU\alpha=(iwI_{n}-(J-R)Q)U\alpha,

so that
iw\in\Lambda((J-R)Q)
which is a contradiction. Thus the matrix
U^{*}QBB^{*}QU
is positive definite. Let
U^{*}QBB^{*}QU=WW^{*}
, where
W
is the unique Cholesky factor of
U^{*}QBB^{*}QU
, then by using

y=W^{*}\alpha
in (
5.17
), we have

\displaystyle\hskip-270.30118pt\eta_{{\mathbb{C}}}^{S_{d}}(J,R;B,iw)^{2}=

\displaystyle\inf_{y\in{\mathbb{C}}^{k}\setminus\{0\}}\left\{\frac{2{\|B^{\dagger}(iwI_{n}-(J-R)Q)UW^{-*}y\|}^{2}}{{\|y\|}^{2}}-\frac{|y^{*}W^{-1}U^{*}Q(iwI_{n}-(J-R)Q)UW^{-*}y|^{2}}{{\|y\|}^{4}}\right\}.

(5.18)

In view of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, note that for every
y\in{\mathbb{C}}^{k}\setminus\{0\}
we have

\displaystyle\frac{2{\|B^{\dagger}(iwI_{n}-(J-R)Q)UW^{-*}y\|}^{2}}{{\|y\|}^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{2{\|B^{\dagger}(iwI_{n}-(J-R)Q)UW^{-*}y\|}^{2}}{{\|y\|}^{2}}

\displaystyle-\frac{|y^{*}W^{-1}U^{*}Q(iwI_{n}-(J-R)Q)UW^{-*}y|^{2}}{{\|y\|}^{4}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{{\|B^{\dagger}(iwI_{n}-(J-R)Q)UW^{-*}y\|}^{2}}{{\|y\|}^{2}}.

Using this in (
5.1
) yields

\displaystyle 2\inf_{y\in{\mathbb{C}}^{k}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{{\|B^{\dagger}(iwI_{n}-(J-R)Q)UW^{-*}y\|}^{2}}{{\|y\|}^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\eta_{{\mathbb{C}}}^{S_{d}}(J,R;B,iw)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\inf_{y\in{\mathbb{C}}^{k}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{{\|B^{\dagger}(iwI_{n}-(J-R)Q)UW^{-*}y\|}^{2}}{{\|y\|}^{2}},

and hence

\sqrt{2}\,\sigma_{\min}\left(B^{\dagger}(iwI_{n}-(J-R)Q)UW^{-*}\right)\geq\eta_{{\mathbb{C}}}^{S_{d}}(J,R;B,iw)\geq\sigma_{\min}\left(B^{\dagger}(iwI_{n}-(J-R)Q)UW^{-*}\right).

This completes the proof.

By using Theorem
5.4
in Theorem
5.2
, we obtain a lower bound for the structured stability radius
r_{{\mathbb{C}}}^{S_{d}}(J,R;B)
as follows.

Consider a real LTI DH system of the form

(5.20)

Let us first state a result from

Theorem 5.7
(

]

We have

\mu_{{\mathbb{R}},2}(M)=\inf_{\gamma\in(0,1]}\sigma_{2}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}{M}&-\gamma\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}{M}\\

\Delta
that attains the value of

Let us now prove a result bounding
\mu_{{\mathbb{R}},F}(M)
using
\mu_{{\mathbb{R}},2}(M)

Theorem 5.8

We have

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mu_{{\mathbb{R}},2}(M)\leq\mu_{{\mathbb{R}},F}(M)\leq\mu_{{\mathbb{R}},2}(M).

(5.21)

Proof
. By definition,

\displaystyle\mu_{{\mathbb{R}},2}(M)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\left\{{\|\Delta\|}_{2}:~\Delta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m,p},~\text{det}(I_{m}-\Delta M)=0\right\}

(5.22)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\inf\left\{{\|\Delta\|}_{F}:~\Delta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m,p},~\text{det}(I_{m}-\Delta M)=0\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu_{{\mathbb{R}},F}(M)^{-1},

since for any
\Delta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m,p}
, we have
{\|\Delta\|}_{2}\leq{\|\Delta\|}_{F}
. Now suppose
\hat{\Delta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m,p}
such that

is of rank at most two. This implies that

and

, which yields that

\mu_{{\mathbb{R}},F}(M)^{-1}\leq\sqrt{2}{\|\hat{\Delta}\|}_{2}=\mu_{{\mathbb{R}},2}(M).

(5.23)

Hence from (
5.22
) and (
5.23
),
we obtain (
5.21
).

As an application of the above theorem we bound the real unstructured distance

Conclusion

In this paper, we have
derived necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of the dissipative mappings taking
X\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,k}
to
Y\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,k}
,
charactered the set of dissipative mappings,
and
found the minimal Frobenius norm solution.
We have then applied these results to DH systems. In particular, we have used
dissipative mappings to derive bounds for the structured distance to asymptotic instability for both complex and real DH systems (
1.1
) when both
J
and
R
are subject to perturbations.

The bounds computed in this paper involve solving a difficult non-convex optimization problem. Possible future works include more sophisticated methods to compute (
5.10
), and
to study other applications of the dissipative mappings.
