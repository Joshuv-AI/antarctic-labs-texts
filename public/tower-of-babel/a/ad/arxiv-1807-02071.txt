General thermodynamic equilibrium with axial chemical potential for the free Dirac field
M. Buzzegoli, and F. Becattini

Abstract
We calculate the constitutive equations of the stress-energy tensor and the currents of the free massless Dirac field at thermodynamic equilibrium with acceleration and rotation and a conserved axial charge by using the density operator approach. We carry out an expansion in thermal vorticity to the second order with finite axial chemical potential μ A \mu_{A} . The obtained coefficients of the expansion are expressed as correlators of angular momenta and boost operators with the currents. We confirm previous observations that the axial chemical potential induces non-vanishing components of the stress-energy tensor at first order in thermal vorticity due to breaking of parity invariance of the density operator with μ A ≠ 0 \mu_{A}\neq 0 . The appearance of these components might play an important role in chiral hydrodynamics.



1
Introduction

The physics of chiral fluids has drawn remarkable attention lately. Much of the interest is
driven by the quest of the Chiral Magnetic Effect (CME) in relativistic heavy ion
collisions
Kharzeev:2015znc
. Besides, the discovery of a finite global polarization of the

\Lambda
hyperons at the Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider (RHIC)
STAR:2017ckg

in agreement with the predictions of local thermodynamic equilibrium
Becattini:2007sr

has demonstrated that the Quark-Gluon Plasma (QGP) has sufficient local vorticity and
acceleration to induce polarization of produced particles. Because of the relation between spin
and chirality, this phenomenon ought to have a close connection with the physics of CME.

Most of the studies of the chiral fluids were aimed at determining both thermodynamic
equilibrium and non-equilibrium coefficients (either dissipative or non-dissipative
according to the classification of ref.
Haehl:2014zda
) within different theoretical
frameworks: in relativistic hydrodynamics
Neiman:2010zi
;
Pu:2010as
;
with Kubo Formulae in holography
Landsteiner:2011iq
;
Landsteiner:2012kd
;
in finite temperature field theory
Chowdhury:2015pba
;
Golkar:2012kb
;
in chiral kinetic theory
Chen:2015gta
;
Hidaka:2017auj
;
Abbasi:2017tea
;
Huang:2018wdl
;
and in effective field theory
Glorioso:2017lcn
;
Huang:2017pqe
.

Thermodynamic equilibrium coefficients include - amongst others - the well known proportionality
coefficient between vector current and magnetic field in the CME, originally derived
in ref.
Fukushima:2008xe
assuming equilibrium. In fact, the systematic
derivations of all constitutive relations at thermodynamic equilibrium, which is non-dissipative
by construction, can be carried out with a functional approach
Kovtun2017
;
Kovtun2018
,
with finite temperature field thoery
Ambrus:2014uqa

or with the covariant density operator approach
Becattini:2014yxa
. The latter was used to derive the constitutive relations of the stress-energy tensor in presence of acceleration and vorticity.

In this paper, we will take this approach to study the thermodynamic equilibrium of a chiral
fluid endowed with an axial chemical potential (i.e. a non-vanishing mean axial charge) including
acceleration and vorticity and we will present explicit calculation for the free massless Dirac
field. We will see that the presence of a non-vanishing axial charge is responsible for additional
parity-breaking terms in the constitutive equations of the stress-energy tensor and currents,
which would not be otherwise present. These terms can be very important for the physics of CME
and particularly for the the implementations of chiral magnetohydrodynamics. Indeed, general thermodynamic
equilibrium relations are the fundamental building blocks of hydrodynamics once they are
promoted to space-time dependent functions. Particularly, we will see that the axial chemical
potential entails the appearance of new terms which are first-order in the gradient expansion.

\hbar=c=k_{B}=1

The Minkowskian metric tensor is

2
Thermodynamic equilibrium and chirality

In this section we will summarize the density operator approach and the inclusion of
a conserved axial charge.

The covariant density operator formalism has been described in detail elsewhere, here
we will briefly summarize it. The most general equilibrium density operator can be obtained
by maximizing the total entropy
S=-{\rm tr}(\widehat{\rho}\log\widehat{\rho}\,)
with the constraints of given
energy, momentum and charge densities over some spacelike hypersurface
\Sigma

weert
;
Becattini:2014yxa
;
Hayata:2015lga
and can be written as
Zubarev
;
weert
;
Becattini:2012tc
, see also
Becattini:2015nva
;
Hongo:2016mqm
,

{\widehat{\rho}}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}}\exp\left[-\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\mu}\left({\widehat{T}}^{\mu\nu}(x)\beta_{\nu}(x)-\sum_{i}\zeta_{i}(x){\widehat{j}}^{\mu}_{i}(x)\right)\right],

(1)

where
{\widehat{T}}^{\mu\nu}
and
{\widehat{j}}^{\mu}_{i}
are the symmetric stress-energy tensor and some conserved currents
operators,
\beta^{\mu}
is the four-temperature vector such that
T=1/\sqrt{\beta^{2}}

is the proper comoving temperature and
\zeta_{i}
are the ratio of comoving chemical potentials and
the temperature
\zeta_{i}=\mu_{i}/T
. The Eq. (
1
) describes a
local
thermal equilibrium;
to obtain a
global
equilibrium distribution the operator (
1
) is required to be time independent, implying that the integrand is divergenceless. This is possible
if
Becattini:2012tc
all
\zeta_{i}
are constants and the four-temperature
\beta

is a Killing vector field:

\nabla_{\mu}\zeta_{i}=0,\qquad\nabla_{\mu}\beta_{\nu}+\nabla_{\nu}\beta_{\mu}=0.

(2)

The general solution of Eq. (
2
) in Minkowsky space-time is

\zeta_{i}=\text{constant,}\qquad\beta_{\mu}=b_{\mu}+\varpi_{\mu\nu}x^{\nu},

(3)

where
b
is a constant four-vector and
\varpi
a constant antisymmetric tensor, dubbed
thermal vorticity
,
that accounting for Eq. (
2
) can be expressed as an exterior derivative of the
\beta
field

\varpi_{\mu\nu}=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{\mu}\beta_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}\beta_{\mu}\right).

(4)

Recalling the definition of four-momentum operator
\widehat{P}
, conserved charges
\widehat{Q}_{i}

{\widehat{P}}^{\mu}=\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\lambda}\,{\widehat{T}}^{\lambda\mu},\qquad{\widehat{Q}}_{i}=\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\lambda}\,{\widehat{j}}^{\lambda}_{i}

and generators of Lorentz transformation
{\widehat{J}}

{\widehat{J}}^{\mu\nu}=\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\lambda}\left(x^{\mu}{\widehat{T}}^{\lambda\nu}-x^{\nu}{\widehat{T}}^{\lambda\mu}\right),

(5)

the form (
3
) allows to write the density operator (
1
) at global equilibrium as

{\widehat{\rho}}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}}\exp\Big[-b_{\mu}{\widehat{P}}^{\mu}+\frac{1}{2}\varpi_{\mu\nu}{\widehat{J}}^{\mu\nu}+\sum_{i}\zeta_{i}\,{\widehat{Q}}_{i}\Big].

The state characterized by this density operator is a generalization of global equilibrium, and since the parameters are globally defined we will refer to this condition as
general global equilibrium
.

As conserved currents, one usually have electric and baryon currents. However, for massless
fermions there is one additional conserved current, the axial current
{\widehat{j}}_{A}
, such that:

\nabla_{\mu}{\widehat{j}}_{A}^{\,\mu}=0

(6)

which has an associated conserved axial charge

{\widehat{Q}}_{A}=\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\lambda}\,{\widehat{j}}^{\lambda}_{\textup{A}}

(7)

where
\Sigma
is an arbitrary space-like hypersurface. Thus, for a system of massless fermions, one would
deal with a general global thermodynamic equilibrium of this sort:

{\widehat{\rho}}_{\textup{GE}}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{\textup{GE}}}\exp\Big[-b_{\mu}{\widehat{P}}^{\mu}+\frac{1}{2}\varpi_{\mu\nu}{\widehat{J}}^{\mu\nu}+\zeta\,{\widehat{Q}}+\zeta_{\textup{A}}\,{\widehat{Q}}_{\textup{A}}\Big].

(8)

In fact, the axial current is not conserved in presence of interactions because of the well known
axial anomaly and the above continuity equation gets modified into (for QED):

\nabla_{\mu}{\widehat{j}}_{A}=CF^{\mu\nu}\tilde{F}_{\mu\nu}\,.

(9)

Nevertheless, in presence of an external field such that the right hand side of (
9
)
vanishes, the axial charge is still constant and thermodynamic equilibrium is possible.
If, on the other hand, we deal with dynamical gauge fields, the right hand side can be written
as the divergence of the so-called Chern-Simons current:

K^{\mu}=C\,\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}A_{\nu}F_{\rho\sigma}

and its integral (the helicity, for the EM field) can be embodied in the definition
of a gauge-invariant overall conserved axial charge including both fermion and gauge boson
contribution
Boyarsky:2015faa
. In both the above cases, the density operator (
8
) is perfectly
meaningful.

In this work, we will confine ourselves to the simplest case of free fermions, so that
(
6
) is fulfilled. Thus, the obtained relations will not include contributions
from possible external gauge fields, yet they will be very useful as a leading order
expressions for a chiral fluid.

As a final remark, it is important to stress that
\beta
must be a time-like vector
for the local mean values to be physical (see next Section). In general, for
\varpi\neq 0

there is a 3D boundary in space-time separating a region where
\beta
is space-like;
for an analysis of the effect of the boundary inside this region on rotating fermions
see
Ambrus:2015lfr
.
In the physical region, the four-temperature vector
\beta
defines a four-velocity
u

of the fluid - the
\beta

frame

Becattini:2014yxa
or thermodynamic
frame
Kovtun2017
-

u^{\mu}(x)=\frac{\beta^{\mu}(x)}{\sqrt{\beta^{2}}}.

(10)

As we will see, this frame is the most appropriate for thermodynamic equilibrium.

3
Mean value of local operators

Our goal is to calculate the mean values of physical quantities at thermodynamic equilibrium
with the density operator
{\widehat{\rho}}
in Eq. (
8
):

\langle\widehat{O}(x)\rangle={\rm tr}\left({\widehat{\rho}}\,\widehat{O}(x)\right)_{\textup{ren}}.

For this purpose, we will use the method already employed
in refs.
Becattini:2015nva
;
Buzzegoli:2017cqy
.

The first step is to shift the angular momentum operator at
x
by using the translational operator

\widehat{\sf T}(x)=\exp[\,\mathrm{i}x\cdot{\widehat{P}}\,]
:

\begin{split}\widehat{J}^{\mu\nu}_{x}\equiv&\widehat{\sf T}(x)\widehat{J}^{\mu\nu}\widehat{\sf T}^{-1}(x)=\widehat{J}^{\mu\nu}-x^{\mu}\widehat{P}^{\nu}+x^{\nu}\widehat{P}^{\mu}\\
&=\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\lambda}\left[(y^{\mu}-x^{\mu})\widehat{T}^{\lambda\nu}(y)-(y^{\nu}-x^{\nu})\widehat{T}^{\lambda\mu}(y)\right].\end{split}

(11)

Thanks to this definition and Eq. (
3
) we can rewrite the density
operator (
8
) as

{\widehat{\rho}}_{\textup{GE}}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}}\exp\Big[-\beta(x)\cdot{\widehat{P}}+\frac{1}{2}\varpi:{\widehat{J}}_{x}+\zeta\,{\widehat{Q}}+\zeta_{\textup{A}}\,{\widehat{Q}}_{\textup{A}}\Big].

(12)

The second step is to make an expansion in thermal vorticity. Indeed, thermal vorticity (
4
)
is adimensional in natural units and, under most circumstances (e.g. in
relativistic heavy ion collisions) is
\ll 1
. An expansion of (
12
) in
\varpi

will result in a good approximation of the mean values. At the leading order one has:

\begin{split}\langle\widehat{O}(x)\rangle_{\textup{GE}}&\equiv{\rm tr}\left({\widehat{\rho}}_{\textup{GE}}\,\widehat{O}(x)\right)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{\textup{GE}}}{\rm tr}\left(\exp\Big[-b_{\mu}{\widehat{P}}^{\mu}+\frac{1}{2}\varpi_{\mu\nu}{\widehat{J}}^{\mu\nu}+\zeta\,{\widehat{Q}}+\zeta_{\textup{A}}\,{\widehat{Q}}_{\textup{A}}\Big]\,\widehat{O}(x)\right)\\
&\simeq\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{\beta(x)}}{\rm tr}\left(\exp\Big[-\beta(x)_{\mu}{\widehat{P}}^{\mu}+\zeta\,{\widehat{Q}}+\zeta_{\textup{A}}\,{\widehat{Q}}_{\textup{A}}\Big]\,\widehat{O}(x)\right)\equiv\langle\widehat{O}(x)\rangle_{\beta(x)}=\langle\widehat{O}(0)\rangle_{\beta(x)},\end{split}

where in the last step we have taken advantage of the translational invariance of the homogeneous
operator
\propto\exp[-\beta\cdot{\widehat{P}}]
. The above expression shows that - as expected - for small
deviations from homogeneous equilibrium, the mean value is well approximated by the
homogeneous equilibrium value with four-temperature equal to its value in the same point
x

where the operator is evaluated.

The corrections to the leading order term are obtained by using the expansion based on a path
ordered method developed in ref.
weert
, in our case along the fluid velocity
u
(
10
). This method is also appropriate for Euclidean space-time. The resulting expression is given
in terms of connected correlators:

\begin{split}\langle\widehat{O}\rangle_{c}&=\langle\widehat{O}\rangle\\
\langle\widehat{J}\widehat{O}\rangle_{c}&=\langle\widehat{J}\widehat{O}\rangle-\langle\widehat{J}\rangle\langle\widehat{O}\rangle\\
\langle\widehat{J}_{1}\widehat{J}_{2}\widehat{O}\rangle_{c}&=\langle\widehat{J}_{1}\widehat{J}_{2}\widehat{O}\rangle-\langle\widehat{J}_{1}\rangle\langle\widehat{J}_{2}\widehat{O}\rangle_{c}-\langle\widehat{J}_{2}\rangle\langle\widehat{J}_{1}\widehat{O}\rangle_{c}-\langle\widehat{O}\rangle\langle\widehat{J}_{1}\widehat{J}_{2}\rangle_{c}-\langle J_{1}\rangle\langle J_{2}\rangle\langle\widehat{O}\rangle\\
&\vdots\end{split}

weighted with the homogeneous statistical operator

\langle\widehat{O}\,\rangle_{\beta(x)}\equiv\frac{{\rm tr}\left[\exp\left(-\beta(x)\cdot{\widehat{P}}+\zeta{\widehat{Q}}+\zeta_{\textup{A}}{\widehat{Q}}_{\textup{A}}\right)\widehat{O}\,\right]}{{\rm tr}\left[\exp\left(-\beta(x)\cdot{\widehat{P}}+\zeta{\widehat{Q}}+\zeta_{\textup{A}}{\widehat{Q}}_{\textup{A}}\right)\right]}.

(13)

At the second order in thermal vorticity we obtain
Buzzegoli:2017cqy

\begin{split}\langle\widehat{O}(x)\rangle=&\langle\widehat{O}(0)\rangle_{\beta(x)}+\frac{\varpi_{\mu\nu}}{2|\beta|}\int_{0}^{|\beta|}{\rm d}\tau\langle{\rm T}_{\tau}\left(\widehat{J}^{\mu\nu}_{-\mathrm{i}\tau u}\widehat{O}(0)\right)\rangle_{\beta(x),c}\\
&+\frac{\varpi_{\mu\nu}\varpi_{\rho\sigma}}{8|\beta|^{2}}\int_{0}^{|\beta|}{\rm d}\tau_{1}{\rm d}\tau_{2}\langle{\rm T}_{\tau}\left(\widehat{J}^{\mu\nu}_{-\mathrm{i}\tau_{1}u}\widehat{J}^{\rho\sigma}_{-\mathrm{i}\tau_{2}u}\widehat{O}(0)\right)\rangle_{\beta(x),c}+\mathcal{O}(\varpi^{3}),\end{split}

(14)

where
{\rm T}_{\tau}\left(\cdots\right)
is the
{\rm T}_{\tau}
-ordered product defined as

{\rm T}_{\tau}\big(\widehat{O}_{1}(\tau_{1})\widehat{O}_{2}(\tau_{2})\cdots\widehat{O}_{N}(\tau_{N})\big)\equiv\widehat{O}_{p_{1}}(\tau_{p_{1}})\widehat{O}_{p_{2}}(\tau_{p_{2}})\cdots\widehat{O}_{p_{N}}(\tau_{p_{N}})

with

\displaystyle p\mathrel{:}\{1,2,\dots,N\}\to\{1,2,\dots,N\}

\displaystyle\tau_{p_{1}}\leq\tau_{p_{2}}\leq\cdots\leq\tau_{p_{N}}.

Thermal vorticity can be decomposed into two space-like fields

At global equilibrium, when

(15)

Furthermore, it is useful to define another four-vector:

\gamma_{\mu}=\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}w^{\nu}\alpha^{\rho}u^{\sigma}=(\alpha\cdot\varpi)^{\lambda}\Delta_{\lambda\mu},

(16)

where

(17)

The operators
{\widehat{J}}
and

are the generators of rotations and boosts with respect

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\varpi_{\mu\nu}\widehat{J}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=-\alpha^{\rho}\widehat{K}_{\rho}-w^{\rho}\widehat{J}_{\rho}

\displaystyle\varpi_{\mu\nu}\varpi_{\rho\sigma}\widehat{J}^{\mu\nu}\widehat{J}^{\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle=2\alpha^{\mu}\alpha^{\nu}\{\widehat{K}_{\mu},\,\widehat{K}_{\nu}\}+2w^{\mu}w^{\nu}\{\widehat{J}_{\mu},\,\widehat{J}_{\nu}\}+4\alpha^{\mu}w^{\nu}\{\widehat{K}_{\mu},\,\widehat{J}_{\nu}\}

whence the mean value expansion (

\begin{split}\langle\widehat{O}(x)\rangle=&\,\langle\widehat{O}(0)\rangle_{\beta(x)}-\frac{\alpha_{\rho}}{|\beta|}\int_{0}^{|\beta|}{\rm d}\tau\,\langle{\rm T}_{\tau}\left(\widehat{K}^{\rho}_{-\mathrm{i}\tau u}\widehat{O}(0)\right)\rangle_{\beta(x),c}\\
&-\frac{w_{\rho}}{|\beta|}\int_{0}^{|\beta|}{\rm d}\tau\,\langle{\rm T}_{\tau}\left(\widehat{J}^{\rho}_{-\mathrm{i}\tau u}\widehat{O}(0)\right)\rangle_{\beta(x),c}\\
&+\frac{\alpha_{\rho}\alpha_{\sigma}}{2|\beta|^{2}}\int_{0}^{|\beta|}{\rm d}\tau_{1}{\rm d}\tau_{2}\,\langle{\rm T}_{\tau}\left(\widehat{K}^{\rho}_{-\mathrm{i}\tau_{1}u}\widehat{K}^{\sigma}_{-\mathrm{i}\tau_{2}u}\widehat{O}(0)\right)\rangle_{\beta(x),c}\\

or, after defining the correlators

\begin{split}\langle\langle\widehat{K}^{\rho_{1}}\cdots\widehat{K}^{\rho_{n}}\widehat{J}^{\sigma_{1}}\cdots\widehat{J}^{\sigma_{m}}\widehat{O}\rangle\rangle\equiv&\int_{0}^{|\beta|}\frac{{\rm d}\tau_{1}\cdots{\rm d}\tau_{n+m}}{|\beta|^{n+m}}\times\\
&\times\langle{\rm T}_{\tau}\left(\widehat{K}^{\rho_{1}}_{-\mathrm{i}\tau_{1}u}\cdots\widehat{K}^{\rho_{n}}_{-\mathrm{i}\tau_{n}u}\widehat{J}^{\sigma_{1}}_{-\mathrm{i}\tau_{n+1}u}\cdots\widehat{J}^{\sigma_{m}}_{-\mathrm{i}\tau_{n+m}u}\widehat{O}(0)\right)\rangle_{\beta(x),c}\end{split}

(18)

can be written as:

\begin{split}\langle\widehat{O}(x)\rangle=&\langle\widehat{O}(x)\rangle_{\beta(x)}-\alpha_{\rho}\langle\langle\,\widehat{K}^{\rho}\widehat{O}\,\rangle\rangle-w_{\rho}\langle\langle\,\widehat{J}^{\rho}\widehat{O}\,\rangle\rangle+\frac{\alpha_{\rho}\alpha_{\sigma}}{2}\langle\langle\,\widehat{K}^{\rho}\widehat{K}^{\sigma}\widehat{O}\,\rangle\rangle\\

4
Chiral thermodynamic coefficients

In this section, we identify and determine the thermodynamic coefficients up to second order
in thermal vorticity for the stress energy tensor as well as vector and axial currents.
We have seen in the previous section, that the mean values of local observables can be expanded
in thermal vorticity (
19
) and that each term involves a coefficient expressed
as connected correlator between the observables and the Lorentz group generators (
18
),
caluculated with the homogeneous statistical operator
{\widehat{\rho}}_{\textup{h}}
(
13
)

{\widehat{\rho}}_{\textup{h}}=\frac{1}{{\mathcal{Z}}_{\textup{h}}}\exp\left(-\beta(x)\cdot{\widehat{P}}+\zeta{\widehat{Q}}+\zeta_{\textup{A}}{\widehat{Q}}_{\textup{A}}\right).

(20)

This operator features several symmetries: translations, rotations in the hyperplane perpendicular
to
\beta(x)
, and time-reversal
\mathsf{T}
in the
\beta(x)
rest frame. However, as
{\widehat{Q}}

is odd under charge conjugation
\mathsf{C}
and
{\widehat{Q}}_{A}
is odd under parity
\mathsf{P}
(see Table
1
),
{\widehat{\rho}}_{\textup{h}}
is not invariant under
\mathsf{C}
and
\mathsf{P}
.

The symmetries of the density operator have direct consequences on the value of the aforementioned
correlators and, more generally, on mean values of operators. For instance, time-reversal invariance
of
{\widehat{\rho}}_{\textup{h}}
implies that any mean value of
\langle\widehat{O}(x)\rangle_{\beta(x)}
, such as the ones
in (
18
), vanishes if the operator
\widehat{O}(x)
is odd under time-reversal:

\mathsf{T}\widehat{O}\mathsf{T}=-\widehat{O}.

Similarly, rotational invariance implies that correlators in (
18
) will be non-vanishing
only if the involved operators transform like the same irreducible vectors of the same representations
of the rotation group. To study the impact of symmetries on the correlators (
18
) it
is convenient to redefine:

\widehat{K}^{\rho_{1}}\cdots\widehat{K}^{\rho_{n}}\widehat{J}^{\sigma_{1}}\cdots\widehat{J}^{\sigma_{m}}=\widehat{A}

and to decompose both
\widehat{A}
and
\widehat{O}
in irreducible components under rotation: scalar, vector,
symmetric traceless tensor, etc. The only non-vanishing correlators will be those matching corresponding
components of the same kind
and
with the same transformation property under time-reversal.

In the homogeneous equilibrium density operator (
20
), the term
\mu_{A}{\widehat{Q}}_{A}
is
the only responsible for parity breaking, if the Hamiltionian is parity invariant. For this
reason, all correlators (
18
) in which
\widehat{A}
and
\widehat{O}
transform with different
sign under parity will be proportional to odd powers of
\mu_{A}
, because:

{\rm tr}(\,{\widehat{\rho}}_{h}\,\widehat{A}\,\widehat{O}\,)={\rm tr}(\,\mathsf{P}\,{\widehat{\rho}}_{h}\,\widehat{A}\,\widehat{O}\,\mathsf{P}^{-1})={\rm tr}(\,{\widehat{\rho}}_{h}(-\mu_{A})\mathsf{P}\widehat{A}\,\widehat{O}\,\mathsf{P}^{-1})=-{\rm tr}(\,{\widehat{\rho}}_{h}(-\mu_{A})\widehat{A}\,\widehat{O}\,).

These correlators will be henceforth dubbed as

5
Discussion:
\beta
vs Landau frame in chiral hydrodynamics

As an interlude preceding the explicit calculation of the thermodynamic coefficients,
it is worth discussing the question of the frame, which is relevant for the hydrodynamics of chiral
fluids, that can be defined in general as fluids where the axial current is relevant. As has been
mentioned, the four-velocity in our formulae has been defined starting from the four-temperature
vector
\beta
which is a Killing vector in global equilibrium. This frame is called
\beta
or
thermodynamic frame
Becattini:2014yxa
;
Kovtun2017
and, as it can be seen from Eq. (
24
)
it does not coincide with Landau frame, where
u
is by definition an eigenvector of the (symmetrized)
stress-energy tensor. However, unlike in the non-chiral case, where the difference between Landau frame
velocity and
\beta
frame velocity shows up only at second order in the gradients of
u
or
\beta

owing to the terms in
u^{\nu}\gamma^{\mu}

Becattini:2015nva
, see also
Ambrus:2017opa

for an in-depth analysis; here the difference between
the two velocities shows up at first order in the gradients because of the chiral terms in
u^{\nu}w^{\mu}

in (
24
) (see Appendix
A
for the transformation equations).

This is especially important for chiral hydrodynamics because it implies that one cannot formulate
it at first order without taking these equilibrium non-dissipative terms in
u^{\nu}w^{\mu}
into
account. Even if one tried to eliminate them in the stress-energy tensor by going to the Landau frame,
they would reappear in the constitutive equations of the axial current at first order in the
gradients; this is just what happens, see Appendix
A
. For chiral-magneto-hydrodynamics,
with dynamical electro-magnetic fields, the transition to Landau frame would be even more complicated
because a change of the velocity definition implies a change of definition in the comoving electric
field.

In conclusion, the
\beta
frame appears as the most appropriate to write down hydrodynamic equations,
as advocated in
Becattini:2014yxa
;
Kovtun2017
.

6
Free massless Dirac field

We are now going to calculate the coefficients for the massless free Dirac field at finite temperature,
vector and axial chemical potential. The basic quantity to compute is the grand-canonical partition
function
{\mathcal{Z}}
:

{\mathcal{Z}}={\rm tr}\left[{\rm e}^{-\beta(\widehat{H}-\mu\widehat{Q}-\mu_{\textup{A}}\widehat{Q}_{\textup{A}})}\right].

For the non interacting massless Dirac field described by the Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\left(\bar{\sf{\Psi}}\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}{\sf{\Psi}}-(\partial_{\mu}\bar{\sf{\Psi}})\gamma^{\mu}{\sf{\Psi}}\right)

the standard vector and axial currents read:

\displaystyle\widehat{j}_{\textup{V}}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\bar{{\sf{\Psi}}}\gamma^{\mu}{\sf{\Psi}},\quad\hphantom{\gamma_{5}}\partial_{\mu}\widehat{j}_{\textup{V}}^{\mu}=0,

\displaystyle=\bar{{\sf{\Psi}}}\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{5}{\sf{\Psi}},\quad\partial_{\mu}\widehat{j}_{\textup{A}}^{\mu}=0,

with the corresponding conserved vector and axial charges (

Euclidean spacetime

\Psi(|\beta|,\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{x}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{x}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{x}$}})=-\Psi(0,\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{x}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{x}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{x}$}})
and Euclidean
gamma matrices

, fulfilling the relation

P^{\pm}=(p_{n}\pm\mathrm{i}\mu,{\bm{p}}),\quad\quad X=(\tau,{\bm{x}}),\quad\quad\sumint_{\{P\}}=\frac{1}{|\beta|}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\int\frac{{\rm d}^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}},

(37)

where

\begin{split}C(X,Y)&=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}X_{1}X_{2}\to X\\
Y_{1}Y_{2}\to Y\end{subarray}}{\mathcal{D}_{\mu\nu}(\partial_{X_{1}},\partial_{X_{2}})}_{ab}{\mathcal{O}_{\alpha\dots\beta}(\partial_{Y_{1}},\partial_{Y_{2}})}_{cd}\langle{\rm T}_{\tau}\bar{{\sf{\Psi}}}(X_{1})_{a}{\sf{\Psi}}(Y_{2})_{d}\rangle\langle{\rm T}_{\tau}{\sf{\Psi}}(X_{2})_{b}\bar{{\sf{\Psi}}}(Y_{1})_{c}\rangle\\
&=-\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}X_{1}X_{2}\to X\\
Y_{1}Y_{2}\to Y\end{subarray}}{\rm tr}\left[{\mathcal{D}_{\mu\nu}(\partial_{X_{1}},\partial_{X_{2}})}\langle{\rm T}_{\tau}{\sf{\Psi}}(X_{2})\bar{{\sf{\Psi}}}(Y_{1})\rangle{\mathcal{O}_{\alpha\dots\beta}(\partial_{Y_{1}},\partial_{Y_{2}})}\langle{\rm T}_{\tau}{\sf{\Psi}}(Y_{2})\bar{{\sf{\Psi}}}(X_{1})\rangle\right].\end{split}

Now, we express fermionic propagator in Fourier space as in (
38

\displaystyle\langle{\rm T}_{\tau}{\sf{\Psi}}(X_{2})_{a}\bar{{\sf{\Psi}}}(Y_{1})_{b}\rangle=\sum_{\chi}\sumint_{\{P\}}\frac{{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}P^{+}_{\chi}\cdot(X_{2}-Y_{1})}}{{P^{+}_{\chi}}^{2}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\chi}G(P^{+}_{\chi})\right)_{ab},

\displaystyle\langle{\rm T}_{\tau}{\sf{\Psi}}(Y_{2})_{a}\bar{{\sf{\Psi}}}(X_{1})_{b}\rangle=\sum_{\chi^{\prime}}\sumint_{\{Q\}}\frac{{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}Q^{+}_{\chi^{\prime}}\cdot(Y_{2}-X_{1})}}{{Q^{+}_{\chi^{\prime}}}^{2}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\chi^{\prime}}G(Q^{+}_{\chi^{\prime}})\right)_{ab},

where, as previously,
P^{+}_{\textup{R/L}}=(p_{n}+\mathrm{i}\mu_{\textup{R/L}},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{p}$}})
and
Q^{+}_{\textup{R/L}}=(q_{m}+\mathrm{i}\mu_{\textup{R/L}},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{q}$}})
.
The derivatives, appearing in the point-splitted operators
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{O}
, act on the propagators and are readily obtained:

\displaystyle\partial_{X}\langle{\rm T}_{\tau}{\sf{\Psi}}(X)_{a}\bar{{\sf{\Psi}}}(Y)_{b}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\chi}\sumint_{\{P\}}\mathrm{i}P^{+}_{\chi}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}P^{+}_{\chi}\cdot(X-Y)}\frac{\left(\mathbb{P}_{\chi}G(P^{+}_{\chi})\right)_{ab}}{{P^{+}_{\chi}}^{2}};

\displaystyle\partial_{Y}\langle{\rm T}_{\tau}{\sf{\Psi}}(X)_{a}\bar{{\sf{\Psi}}}(Y)_{b}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\chi}\sumint_{\{P\}}(-\mathrm{i}P^{+}_{\chi}){\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}P^{+}_{\chi}\cdot(X-Y)}\frac{\left(\mathbb{P}_{\chi}G(P^{+}_{\chi})\right)_{ab}}{{P^{+}_{\chi}}^{2}}.

At this point, we take these derivatives, we send
X_{1},X_{2}\to X
and
Y_{1},Y_{2}\to Y
and we rename
Q
into
-Q
; note that

Q^{+}_{\chi^{\prime}}=(q_{m}+\mathrm{i}\mu_{\chi^{\prime}},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{q}$}})
goes to
-Q^{-}_{\chi^{\prime}}=(\mathrm{i}\mu_{\chi^{\prime}}-q_{m},-\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{q}$}})
.
At the end, the correlator

becomes

C(X,Y)=-\sum_{\chi}\sumint_{\{P,Q\}}\frac{{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}(P^{+}_{\chi}+Q^{-}_{\chi^{\prime}})(X-Y)}}{{P^{+}_{\chi}}^{2}\,{Q^{-}_{\chi}}^{2}}\,\,F_{\chi}(P^{+},Q^{-}),

where we defined

\begin{split}F_{\chi}(P,Q)\equiv{\rm tr}\left[\mathbb{P}_{\chi}G(-Q_{\chi})\mathcal{D}_{\mu\nu}(\mathrm{i}Q_{\chi},\mathrm{i}P_{\chi})G(P_{\chi}){\mathcal{O}_{\alpha\dots\beta}(-\mathrm{i}P_{\chi},-\mathrm{i}Q_{\chi})}\right].\end{split}

The trace is to be carried out over spinorial indices by using the Euclidean
\tilde{\gamma}

matrices properties:

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\left(\tilde{\gamma}_{\mu}\tilde{\gamma}_{\nu}\right)

\displaystyle=4\,\delta_{\mu\nu},

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\left(\tilde{\gamma}_{\mu_{1}}\dots\tilde{\gamma}_{\mu_{2n+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\left(\tilde{\gamma}_{k}\tilde{\gamma}_{\lambda}\tilde{\gamma}_{\mu}\tilde{\gamma}_{\nu}\gamma_{5}\right)

\displaystyle=4\epsilon^{k\lambda\mu\nu},

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\left(\tilde{\gamma}_{k}\tilde{\gamma}_{\lambda}\tilde{\gamma}_{\mu}\tilde{\gamma}_{\nu}\right)

\displaystyle=4\,\delta_{k\lambda}\delta_{\mu\nu}-4\,\delta_{k\mu}\delta_{\lambda\nu}+4\,\delta_{k\nu}\delta_{\lambda\mu}.

However, all the operators
\widehat{O}
that will be considered are analytic functions in the four-momentum

Q
and
P
; as a result, the function
F_{\chi}
will also be analytic in those variables. This
enables us to analytically entail the sum over the Matsubara frequencies.

Before carrying out the thermal sum, we send
Y
to
0
, as dictated by (
40
),
and separate the momentum integral from the frequency sum in
\sumint
(
37
)

C(\tau,\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{x}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{x}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{x}$}})=C(X,0)=(-1)\sum_{\chi}\int\frac{{\rm d}^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\frac{{\rm d}^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{p}$}}+\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{q}$}})\cdot\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{x}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{x}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{x}$}}}\,S_{\chi}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{p}$}},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{q}$}},\tau),

where we have defined

S_{\chi}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{p}$}},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{q}$}},\tau)\equiv\frac{1}{|\beta|}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|\beta|}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}(p_{n}+\mathrm{i}\mu_{\chi})\tau}}{(p_{n}+\mathrm{i}\mu_{\chi})^{2}+p^{2}}\frac{{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}(q_{m}-\mathrm{i}\mu_{\chi})\tau}}{(q_{m}-\mathrm{i}\mu_{\chi})^{2}+q^{2}}F_{\chi}(P^{+},Q^{-}).

As stated previously,
F_{\chi}
is an analytic function, hence the sum over frequencies can be carried
out using the formula:

\frac{1}{|\beta|}\sum_{\{\omega_{n}\}}\frac{(\omega_{n}\pm\mathrm{i}\mu)^{k}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}(\omega_{n}\pm\mathrm{i}\mu)\tau}}{(\omega_{n}\pm\mathrm{i}\mu)^{2}+\omega^{2}}=\frac{1}{2\omega}\left[(\mathrm{i}\omega)^{k}{\rm e}^{-\omega\tau}(1-n_{\textup{F}}(\omega\mp\mu))-(-\mathrm{i}\omega)^{k}{\rm e}^{\omega\tau}n_{\textup{F}}(\omega\pm\mu)\right],

(41)

where
\omega
could be the modulus of
p^{2}
or of
q^{2}
and
n_{\textup{F}}
is the Fermi-Dirac distribution function:

n_{\textup{F}}(\omega)=\frac{1}{{\rm e}^{|\beta|\omega}+1}.

Introducing the notation

\tilde{P}(\pm)=(\pm\mathrm{i}p,\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{p}$}}),\quad p^{\pm}_{\chi}=p\pm\mu_{\chi}

(42)

and similarly for
\tilde{Q}(\pm)
, and taking advantage of Eq. (
41
), we have

\begin{split}S_{\chi}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{p}$}},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{q}$}},\tau)=\frac{1}{4pq}\Bigl\{&F_{\chi}\left(\tilde{P}(+),\tilde{Q}(+)\right){\rm e}^{-(p^{-}_{\chi}+q^{+}_{\chi})\tau}\big[1-n_{\textup{F}}(p-\mu_{\chi})\big]\big[1-n_{\textup{F}}(q+\mu_{\chi})\big]+\\
&-F_{\chi}\left(\tilde{P}(+),\tilde{Q}(-)\right){\rm e}^{(-p^{-}_{\chi}+q^{-}_{\chi})\tau}\big[1-n_{\textup{F}}(p-\mu_{\chi})\big]n_{\textup{F}}(q-\mu_{\chi})+\\
&-F_{\chi}\left(\tilde{P}(-),\tilde{Q}(+)\right){\rm e}^{(p^{+}_{\chi}-q^{+}_{\chi})\tau}n_{\textup{F}}(p+\mu_{\chi})\big[1-n_{\textup{F}}(q+\mu_{\chi})\big]+\\
&+F_{\chi}\left(\tilde{P}(-),\tilde{Q}(-)\right){\rm e}^{(p^{+}_{\chi}+q^{-}_{\chi})\tau}n_{\textup{F}}(p+\mu_{\chi})n_{\textup{F}}(q-\mu_{\chi})\Bigr\}.\end{split}

Note that we have added and subtracted the chemical potential in the exponential in such a way
that the corresponding energy couples with the relating argument of the Fermi distribution function

n_{\textup{F}}
. This feature will be used later on.

Finally, putting together and integrating by parts, we can write the coefficient (
40
) as:

\begin{split}C_{\mu\nu\alpha\dots\beta i}&=\int_{0}^{|\beta|}\frac{{\rm d}\tau}{|\beta|}\int{\rm d}^{3}x\,x_{i}\,C(\tau,\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{x}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{x}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{x}$}})\\
&=-\mathrm{i}\sum_{\chi}\int_{0}^{|\beta|}\frac{{\rm d}\tau}{|\beta|}\int\frac{{\rm d}^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\left[\frac{\partial S_{\chi}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{p}$}},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{q}$}},\tau)}{\partial q^{i}}\right]_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{q}$}}=-\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{p}$}}},\end{split}

that is an integral over time and momenta of a known function.
We can simplify this expression further with the following steps:

•

after the evaluation of
F_{\chi}(P,Q)
calculate
S_{\chi}
;

•

then derive
S_{\chi}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{p}$}},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{q}$}},\tau)
and replace
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{q}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{q}$}}\to-\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{p}$}}

•

integrate over solid angle
\Omega

first and over
\tau
thereafter;

•

express all exponential factors with the Fermi distribution function using

{\rm e}^{|\beta|E}=\frac{1}{n_{\textup{F}}(E)}-1,\quad E=p\pm\mu_{\textup{R,L}};

•

express the derivative of
n_{\textup{F}}(E)
in terms of its powers

\displaystyle n_{\textup{F}}^{\prime}(E)

\displaystyle=-|\beta|n_{\textup{F}}^{2}(E){\rm e}^{|\beta|E}=-|\beta|n_{\textup{F}}(E)+|\beta|n_{\textup{F}}^{2}(E),

\displaystyle n_{\textup{F}}^{\prime\prime}(E)

\displaystyle=|\beta|^{2}n_{\textup{F}}(E)-3|\beta|^{2}n_{\textup{F}}^{2}(E)+2|\beta|^{2}n_{\textup{F}}^{3}(E),

\displaystyle n_{\textup{F}}^{\prime\prime\prime}(E)

\displaystyle=6|\beta|^{3}n_{\textup{F}}^{4}(E)-12|\beta|^{3}n_{\textup{F}}^{3}(E)+7|\beta|^{3}n_{\textup{F}}^{2}(E)-|\beta|^{3}n_{\textup{F}}(E);

•

after the due simplifications, express powers of

in terms of its derivative:

\displaystyle n_{\textup{F}}^{4}(E)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6|\beta|^{3}}n_{\textup{F}}^{\prime\prime\prime}(E)+2n_{\textup{F}}^{3}(E)-\frac{7}{6}n_{\textup{F}}^{2}(E)+\frac{1}{6}n_{\textup{F}}(E),

\displaystyle n_{\textup{F}}^{3}(E)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2|\beta|^{2}}n_{\textup{F}}^{\prime\prime}(E)+\frac{3}{2}n_{\textup{F}}^{2}(E)-\frac{1}{2}n_{\textup{F}}(E),

\displaystyle n_{\textup{F}}^{2}(E)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\beta|}n_{\textup{F}}^{\prime}(E)+n_{\textup{F}}(E),

in this way a simple expression made only of

and its derivative is obtained;

•

finally, integrate by part

when possible;

•

sum up all the pieces with different
\chi

This procedure always leads to expressions of the form

where

6.2
Second order correlators

A second order correlator of the perturbative expansion (
23
) is the mean value
of the local operator
\widehat{O}_{\alpha\dots\beta}
with two Lorentz generators. We will follow
similar steps as those used for the first order correlators.
We start by writing the second order generic correlator (
23
) in Euclidean space-time as

C_{\mu\nu|\gamma\delta|\alpha\dots\beta|ij}=\mskip-3.0mu\int_{0}^{|\beta|}\mskip-3.0mu\frac{{\rm d}\tau_{1}}{|\beta|}\mskip-3.0mu\int_{0}^{|\beta|}\mskip-3.0mu\frac{{\rm d}\tau_{2}}{|\beta|}\mskip-3.0mu\int{\rm d}^{3}x\mskip-3.0mu\int\mskip-3.0mu{\rm d}^{3}y\,\langle{\rm T}_{\tau}\left(\widehat{T}_{\mu\nu}(X)\,\widehat{T}_{\gamma\delta}(Y)\,\widehat{O}_{\alpha\dots\beta}(0)\right)\rangle_{\textup{$T$,c}}\,x_{i}y_{j},

(44)

where
X=(\tau_{1},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{x}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{x}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{x}$}})
,
Y=(\tau_{2},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{y}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{y}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{y}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{y}$}})
and the connected correlator is given by

\langle\widehat{A}\,\widehat{B}\,\widehat{C}\rangle_{\textup{c}}=\langle\widehat{A}\,\widehat{B}\,\widehat{C}\rangle-\langle\widehat{A}\rangle\langle\widehat{B}\,\widehat{C}\rangle-\langle\widehat{B}\rangle\langle\widehat{A}\,\widehat{C}\rangle-\langle\widehat{C}\rangle\langle\widehat{A}\,\widehat{B}\rangle+2\langle\widehat{A}\rangle\langle\widehat{B}\rangle\langle\widehat{C}\rangle.

Again, using the point splitting procedure and taking advantage of the Wick’s theorem, we can
split the mean value of (
44
) in two parts

C(X,Y,Z)\equiv\langle{\rm T}_{\tau}\left(\widehat{T}_{\mu\nu}(X)\,\widehat{T}_{\gamma\delta}(Y)\,\widehat{O}_{\alpha\dots\beta}(Z)\right)\rangle_{\textup{$T$,c}}=C_{1}+C_{2},

(45)

where
Z=(\tau_{2},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{z}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{z}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{z}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{z}$}})
and

\displaystyle C_{1}=

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}X_{1}X_{2}\to X\\
Y_{1}Y_{2}\to Y\\
Z_{1}Z_{2}\to Z\end{subarray}}(-1){\rm tr}\left[\mathcal{D}_{\mu\nu}(\partial_{X_{1}},\partial_{X_{2}})\langle{\rm T}_{\tau}{\sf{\Psi}}(X_{2})\bar{{\sf{\Psi}}}(Z_{1})\rangle\mathcal{O}_{\alpha\dots\beta}(\partial_{Z_{1}},\partial_{Z_{2}})\times\right.

\displaystyle\hphantom{\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}X_1 X_2\to X\\
Y_1 Y_2\to Y \\
Z_1 Z_2\to Z\end{subarray}} (-1) {\rm tr}\Big[}\left.\times\langle{\rm T}_{\tau}{\sf{\Psi}}(Z_{2})\bar{{\sf{\Psi}}}(Y_{1})\rangle\mathcal{D}_{\gamma\delta}(\partial_{Y_{1}},\partial_{Y_{2}})\langle{\rm T}_{\tau}{\sf{\Psi}}(Y_{2})\bar{{\sf{\Psi}}}(X_{1})\rangle\right],

\displaystyle C_{2}=

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}X_{1}X_{2}\to X\\
Y_{1}Y_{2}\to Y\\
Z_{1}Z_{2}\to Z\end{subarray}}(-1){\rm tr}\left[\mathcal{D}_{\mu\nu}(\partial_{X_{1}},\partial_{X_{2}})\langle{\rm T}_{\tau}{\sf{\Psi}}(X_{2})\bar{{\sf{\Psi}}}(Y_{1})\rangle\mathcal{D}_{\gamma\delta}(\partial_{Y_{1}},\partial_{Y_{2}})\times\right.

\displaystyle\hphantom{\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}X_1 X_2\to X\\
Y_1 Y_2\to Y \\
Z_1 Z_2\to Z\end{subarray}} (-1) {\rm tr}\Big[}\left.\times\langle{\rm T}_{\tau}{\sf{\Psi}}(Y_{2})\bar{{\sf{\Psi}}}(Z_{1})\rangle\mathcal{O}_{\alpha\dots\beta}(\partial_{Z_{1}},\partial_{Z_{2}})\langle{\rm T}_{\tau}{\sf{\Psi}}(Z_{2})\bar{{\sf{\Psi}}}(X_{1})\rangle\right].

Following similar procedure of first order correlators, we express the two point function in Fourier space (
38
)
and we can show that
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are equal to

\begin{split}C_{1}&=(-1)\sum_{\chi}\sumint_{\{P,Q,K\}}\frac{{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}(P^{+}_{\chi}+K^{-}_{\chi})X}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}(Q^{-}_{\chi}-K^{-}_{\chi})Y}{\rm e}^{-\mathrm{i}(P^{+}_{\chi}+Q^{-}_{\chi})Z}}{{P^{+}_{\chi}}^{2}{Q^{-}_{\chi}}^{2}{K^{-}_{\chi}}^{2}}\,F_{1\,\chi}(P,Q,K),\\
C_{2}&=(-1)\sum_{\chi}\sumint_{\{P,Q,K\}}\frac{{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}(P^{-}_{\chi}+K^{+}_{\chi})X}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}(Q^{+}_{\chi}-K^{+}_{\chi})Y}{\rm e}^{-\mathrm{i}(P^{-}_{\chi}+Q^{+}_{\chi})Z}}{{P^{-}_{\chi}}^{2}{Q^{+}_{\chi}}^{2}{K^{+}_{\chi}}^{2}}F_{2\,\chi}(P,Q,K),\end{split}

where we denoted the momenta as
P^{+}_{\chi}=(p_{n}+\mathrm{i}\mu_{\chi},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{p}$}})
,

,

,
and we defined the trace functions as:

\begin{split}F_{1\,\chi}(P,Q,K)=&{\rm tr}\Big[\mathbb{P}_{\chi}G(-Q^{-}_{\chi})\mathcal{D}_{\gamma\delta}(\mathrm{i}Q^{-}_{\chi},-\mathrm{i}K^{-}_{\chi})G(-K^{-}_{\chi})\mathcal{D}_{\mu\nu}(\mathrm{i}K^{-}_{\chi},\mathrm{i}P^{+}_{\chi})\times\\
&\hphantom{{\rm tr}\Big[}\times G(P^{+}_{\chi})\mathcal{O}_{\alpha\dots\beta}(-\mathrm{i}P^{+}_{\chi},-\mathrm{i}Q^{-}_{\chi})\Big],\\

&\hphantom{{\rm tr}\Big[}\times G(Q^{+}_{\chi})\mathcal{O}_{\alpha\dots\beta}(-\mathrm{i}Q^{+}_{\chi},-\mathrm{i}P^{-}_{\chi})\Big].\end{split}

Once the form of
\mathcal{O}
is explicitly given, these two trace functions are computed thanks to the well known gamma matrix traces.
In general, we can say that as far as

is an analytic function of the derivative operator

, the two

and
F_{2}
are analytic functions on the four-momenta

,
Q
and
K
.

Now, we send
Z
to
0
in Eq. (
45
), as required by (

),
and we separate the momenta integrals from the frequencies sums

C(\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{x}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{x}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{x}$}},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{y}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{y}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{y}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{y}$}})\equiv C(X,Y,0)=-\sum_{\chi}\int\frac{{\rm d}^{3}p\,{\rm d}^{3}q\,{\rm d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{9}}\,{\rm 7
Discussion and summary

Many of the coefficients presented here have been obtained with different methods.
The expectation values of the stress-energy tensor and the currents for free massless
Dirac field evaluated here are in agreement with the exact results in the case of pure
rotation and vanishing chemical potentials of
Ambrus:2014uqa
.
The CVE and AVE have been discussed at length over the past decade
(see
Kharzeev:2015znc
;
Landsteiner:2016led
;
Jensen:2012kj
), and several calculations of

W^{\textup{V}}
(
48
) and
W^{\textup{A}}
(
49
) were presented in literature.

As it was mentioned in Section
4.2
, the AVE does not vanish for
\zeta_{A}=\mu_{A}/T=0
, as it is apparent from eq. (
49
). This means that the AVE does not
need chiral imbalance to show up and persists for a perfectly chirally balanced system.
We interpret this feature - at least in the form in eq. (
49
) - as an evidence
that AVE is not related to the anomalous axial current divergence. As it was found by
A. Vilenkin
Vilenkin:1979ui
;
Vilenkin:1980zv
;
Vilenkin:1980ft
and lately highlighted
in ref.
Buzzegoli:2017cqy
, an axial current proportional to vorticity arises
because it is allowed by the symmetry of the density operator (this was also recognized
in
Son:2009tf
) with rotation and, indeed, the very same expression (
49
)
is found for free massless fermions, without coupling to either external or dynamical
gauge fields which make up the anomalous divergence of the axial current.
We understand that this statement goes against the conventional interpretation in
literature, and yet some other authors have lately cast doubts about the anomalous origin
of the AVE
Kalaydzhyan:2014bfa
;
Flachi:2017vlp
. We hope that our viewpoint will contribute to clarify the point.

As far as the stress-energy tensor is concerned, the consequence of parity breaking induced
by
\zeta_{A}
, is an energy flux
q^{\mu}
along the vorticity
w^{\mu}
, see Eq. (
24
):

q^{\mu}=\Delta^{\mu}_{\,\,\rho}\,u_{\sigma}\,\langle{\widehat{T}}^{\sigma\rho}\rangle=\mathbb{W}_{2}\,w^{\mu}.

To our knowledge, this kind of coefficient was obtained first by
Vilenkin:1979ui
as
an energy flux resulting from neutrinos emitted by a rotating black hole; indeed, our result
of
\mathbb{W}_{2}^{\textup{Can}}
for a free Dirac field (
47
) perfectly agrees
with the one in
Vilenkin:1979ui
(there is a factor
\frac{1}{2}
of a difference
because Vilenkin considered only left-handed neutrinos). Lately, the same result for this
coefficient was obtained by
Landsteiner:2011iq
;
Landsteiner:2012kd
using holographic
techniques, by
Chen:2015gta
;
Hidaka:2017auj
;
Abbasi:2017tea
in chiral kinetic theory and in
Landsteiner:2012kd
;
Chowdhury:2015pba
evaluating Kubo formulae in finite temperature
field theory.

It is also worth noting that thermodynamic equilibria with vorticity imply different mean
values for the canonical and symmetric stress-energy tensor
Becattini:2011ev
;
Becattini:2012pp
.
This is seen here for the coefficients
\mathbb{A},\,\mathbb{W}_{1},\,\mathbb{W}_{2},\,G_{1}
and
G_{2}
.
Particularly, the coefficient
\mathbb{A}
vanishes if the stress-energy tensor is symmetric
but not for the canonical.

The second order coefficients
N_{\alpha}^{\textup{A}},\,N_{w}^{\textup{A}}
and
G^{\textup{A}}
, see
eqs (
33
) and (
34
) are indeed newly obtained and they appear
as quantum corrections of the axial current in presence of acceleration and rotation. They
are the axial counterpart of the second-order equilibrium corrections of the vector current discussed in
Buzzegoli:2017cqy
.

N_{\alpha}^{\textup{A}},\,N_{w}^{\textup{A}}
are corrections along the fluid velocity and hence modify
the axial charge density, while
G^{\textup{A}}
yields a chiral flow along the four-vector

\gamma^{\mu}
defined in Eq. (
16
).

To summarize, we have studied global thermodynamic equilibrium with acceleration
and vorticity with axial charge chemical potential inducing a macroscopic parity
breaking. We carried out an expansion of the stress-energy tensor at the second
order in the thermal vorticity tensor, including acceleration and vorticity and
obtained the constitutive equations at equilibrium of the stress-energy tensor as
well as of the vector and axial currents. These equations may be phenomenologically
relevant for the physics of the Quark Gluon Plasma and especially for its hydrodynamic
modelling in presence of chirality imbalance.

Appendix A
From
\beta
to Landau Frame

In this work we choose the fluid velocity
u
in the direction of the Killing
\beta
four-vector.
This choice corresponds to the
\beta
or thermodynamic frame. While this is a natural frame for
the generalized equilibrium, this is not the most common choice in literature and it is not the
frame typically used in numerical codes. It is then of interest to translate the results of this
paper into the Landau frame.

To accomplish this task, it suffices to establish the relation between the
\beta
-frame velocity

u
and the Landau frame velocity
u_{\textup{L}}
.
By definition, the Landau velocity
u_{\textup{L}}
is the eigenvector of the stress energy tensor:

u_{{\rm L}\mu}T^{\mu\nu}=\rho_{\textup{L}}u_{\textup{L}}^{\nu}

where
\rho_{\textup{L}}
is both the eigenvalue and the energy density in the Landau frame.
Being a four-vector, the Landau velocity
u_{\textup{L}}
can be expressed in terms of the tetrad

\{u,\alpha,w,\gamma\}
defined in Eq.s (
15
) and (
16
):

u_{\textup{L}}^{\mu}=a\,u^{\mu}+b\,\frac{w^{\mu}}{|w|}+c\,\frac{\gamma^{\mu}}{|\gamma|}+d\,\frac{\alpha^{\mu}}{|\alpha|}\,,

(50)

where
|w|=\sqrt{-w^{2}},\,|\gamma|=\sqrt{-\gamma^{2}},\,|\alpha|=\sqrt{-\alpha^{2}}

and
a,b,c
and
d
are four unknown constants such that
u_{\textup{L}}^{\mu}u_{{\rm L}\mu}=1
, i.e.

a^{2}-b^{2}-2bd\frac{\alpha\cdot w}{|\alpha||w|}-c^{2}-d^{2}=1.

Furthermore, since for a vanishing thermal vorticity we expect that the two thermodynamic frames
coincide, for
\varpi=0
we must have that
a=1
and
\rho_{\textup{L}}=\rho
.
This means that the leading term of
a
and
\rho_{\textup{L}}
is zeroth order in thermal vorticity.

Now, taking the stress-energy tensor mean value at the second order expansion on thermal vorticity

\begin{split}T^{\mu\nu}=&\,\mathbb{A}\,\epsilon^{\mu\nu\kappa\lambda}\alpha_{\kappa}u_{\lambda}+\mathbb{W}_{1}w^{\mu}u^{\nu}+\mathbb{W}_{2}w^{\nu}u^{\mu}\\
&+(\rho-\alpha^{2}U_{\alpha}-w^{2}U_{w})u^{\mu}u^{\nu}-(p-\alpha^{2}D_{\alpha}-w^{2}D_{w})\Delta^{\mu\nu}\\
&+A\,\alpha^{\mu}\alpha^{\nu}+Ww^{\mu}w^{\nu}+G_{1}u^{\mu}\gamma^{\nu}+G_{2}u^{\nu}\gamma^{\mu}+\mathcal{O}(\varpi^{3})\end{split}

and contracting it with the landau velocity (
50
), we obtain

\begin{split}u_{{\rm L}\mu}T^{\mu\nu}=&\left(a\,\rho_{\textup{eff}}-b\,\mathbb{W}_{1}|w|+d\,\mathbb{W}_{1}\frac{\alpha\cdot w}{|\alpha|}\right)u^{\nu}\\
&+\left(a\,\mathbb{W}_{2}-b\,p_{\textup{eff}}\frac{1}{|w|}-b\,W|w|+c\,\mathbb{A}\frac{|\alpha|^{2}}{|\gamma|}+d\,W\frac{\alpha\cdot w}{|\alpha|}\right)w^{\nu}+\\
&+\left(a\,G_{1}-b\,\mathbb{A}\frac{1}{|w|}-c\,p_{\textup{eff}}\frac{1}{|\gamma|}\right)\gamma^{\nu}+\\
&+\left(b\,A\frac{\alpha\cdot w}{|w|}+c\,\mathbb{A}\frac{\alpha\cdot w}{|\gamma|}-d\,p_{\textup{eff}}\frac{1}{|\alpha|}-d\,A|\alpha|\right)\alpha^{\nu}+\mathcal{O}(\varpi^{3}),\end{split}

where for simplicity we defined
\rho_{\textup{eff}}\equiv\rho-\alpha^{2}U_{\alpha}-w^{2}U_{w}
and

p_{\textup{eff}}\equiv p-\alpha^{2}D_{\alpha}-w^{2}D_{w}
.
Equating this expression to
\rho_{\textup{L}}u_{\textup{L}}^{\nu}
, with
u_{\textup{L}}
given by (
50
),
we obtain the five equations in five unknown variables
(a,b,c,d,\rho_{\textup{L}})
that
diagonalize the stress energy tensor (at second order in thermal vorticity):

\begin{split}a\,\rho_{\textup{eff}}-b\,\mathbb{W}_{1}|w|+d\,\mathbb{W}_{1}\frac{\alpha\cdot w}{|\alpha|}&=a\,\rho_{\textup{L}}\\
a\,\mathbb{W}_{2}-b\,p_{\textup{eff}}\frac{1}{|w|}-b\,W|w|+c\,\mathbb{A}\frac{|\alpha|^{2}}{|\gamma|}+d\,W\frac{\alpha\cdot w}{|\alpha|}&=b\,\rho_{\textup{L}}\,\frac{1}{|w|}\\
a\,G_{1}-b\,\mathbb{A}\frac{1}{|w|}-c\,p_{\textup{eff}}\frac{1}{|\gamma|}&=c\,\rho_{\textup{L}}\frac{1}{|\gamma|}\\
b\,A\frac{\alpha\cdot w}{|w|}+c\,\mathbb{A}\frac{\alpha\cdot w}{|\gamma|}-d\,p_{\textup{eff}}\frac{1}{|\alpha|}-d\,A|\alpha|&=d\,\rho_{\textup{L}}\frac{1}{|\alpha|}\\
a^{2}-b^{2}-2bd\frac{\alpha\cdot w}{|\alpha||w|}-c^{2}-d^{2}&=1.\end{split}

(51)

To solve it, we can simplify the equations furtherer.
Indeed, we can write the first Eq. of (
51
) as

\frac{b}{a}=\frac{\rho_{\textup{eff}}-\rho_{\textup{L}}}{\mathbb{W}_{1}}\frac{1}{|w|}+\frac{d}{a}\frac{\alpha\cdot w}{|\alpha||w|}.

(52)

In the same way, we can write the third Eq. of (
51
) isolating
b/a
:

\frac{b}{a}=\frac{G_{1}}{\mathbb{A}}|w|-\frac{c}{a}\frac{\rho_{\textup{L}}+p_{\textup{eff}}}{\mathbb{A}}\frac{|w|}{|\gamma|};

hence, equating the previous two, we obtain

\frac{c}{a}=\frac{\mathbb{A}}{\rho_{\textup{L}}+p_{\textup{eff}}}\left[\frac{G_{1}}{A}|\gamma|+\frac{\rho_{\textup{eff}}-\rho_{\textup{L}}}{\mathbb{W}_{1}}\frac{|\gamma|}{|w|^{2}}+\frac{d}{a}\frac{(\alpha\cdot w)|\gamma|}{|\alpha||w|^{2}}\right].

(53)

From Eq. (
53
) we conclude that
c
is at least first order in
\varpi
.
While, from the fourth Eq. of (
51
) we have

d=\frac{\mathbb{A}}{\rho_{\textup{L}}+p_{\textup{eff}}+A|\alpha|^{2}}(b\,|\gamma|+c\,|w|)\frac{\alpha\cdot w}{|w||\gamma|}|\alpha|,

(54)

showing that
d
is at least second order in thermal vorticity.

Since we are interested in second order solution and
d
is already second order,
the Eq. (
52
) gives

\frac{b}{a}=\frac{\rho_{\textup{eff}}-\rho_{\textup{L}}}{\mathbb{W}_{1}}\frac{1}{|w|}+\mathcal{O}(\varpi^{3}),

(55)

while the Eq. (
53
) becomes

\frac{c}{a}=\frac{\mathbb{A}}{\rho_{\textup{L}}+p_{\textup{eff}}}\left[\frac{G_{1}}{A}|\gamma|+\frac{\rho_{\textup{eff}}-\rho_{\textup{L}}}{\mathbb{W}_{1}}\frac{|\gamma|}{|w|^{2}}\right]+\mathcal{O}(\varpi^{3}).

(56)

We can also note that in the second equation of (
51
) the term in
d
is higher order
and can be omitted, as a consequence, after the replacement of Eq.s (
55
) and (
56
), the second equation of (
51
) becomes a third grade equation for
\rho_{\textup{L}}
:

\mathbb{W}_{1}\mathbb{W}_{2}(\rho_{\textup{L}}+p_{\textup{eff}})|w|^{2}+(\rho_{\textup{L}}+p_{\textup{eff}})(\rho_{\textup{L}}-\rho_{\textup{eff}})(\rho_{\textup{L}}+p_{\textup{eff}}+W|w|^{2})+\mathbb{W}_{1}G_{1}A|w|^{2}|\alpha|^{2}+\mathbb{A}(\rho_{\textup{eff}}-\rho_{\textup{L}})|\alpha|^{2}=0.

We find that the only solution of this Eq. reproducing
\rho_{\textup{L}}=\rho
for
\varpi=0
is:

\rho_{\textup{L}}=\rho_{\textup{eff}}-\frac{\mathbb{W}_{1}\mathbb{W}_{2}}{\rho+p}|w|^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\varpi^{4}).

Replacing this solution in (
55
), we obtain

\frac{b}{a}=\frac{\mathbb{W}_{2}}{\rho+p}|w|+\mathcal{O}(\varpi^{3})

and replacing it in (
56
) gives

\frac{c}{a}=\frac{G_{1}(\rho+p)+\mathbb{A}\mathbb{W}_{2}}{(\rho+p)^{2}}|\gamma|+\mathcal{O}(\varpi^{3}).

From these two, we learn that
b
is actually first order on thermal vorticity and
c
is second
order, thus from (
54
) it follows that
d
is in fact third order and can be set to zero.
Consequently, the last equation to solve is
a^{2}-b^{2}-c^{2}=1
, that gives

a=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-(b/a)^{2}-(c/a)^{2}}}=1+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathbb{W}_{2}^{2}}{(\rho+p)^{2}}|w|^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\varpi^{3}).

In conclusion, we found that the relation between the Landau fluid velocity
u_{\textup{L}}

and the
\beta
-frame fluid velocity
u
is

u_{\textup{L}}=\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathbb{W}_{2}^{2}}{(\rho+p)^{2}}|w|^{2}\right)u+\frac{\mathbb{W}_{2}}{\rho+p}w+\frac{G_{1}(\rho+p)+\mathbb{A}\mathbb{W}_{2}}{(\rho+p)^{2}}\gamma+\mathcal{O}(\varpi^{3})

or, reverting it,
u
is given by

u=\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathbb{W}_{2}^{2}}{(\rho+p)^{2}}|w|^{2}\right)u_{\textup{L}}-\frac{\mathbb{W}_{2}}{\rho+p}w-\frac{G_{1}(\rho+p)+\mathbb{A}\mathbb{W}_{2}}{(\rho+p)^{2}}\gamma+\mathcal{O}(\varpi^{3}).

(57)

This transformation at first order in vorticity and the following relation between CVE conductivity
in Landau frame with
\mathbb{W}_{2}
and
W^{\textup{V}}
were also pointed out in
Landsteiner:2012kd
.

57
), we can express the stress-energy tensor

24
) in the Landau frame:

\begin{split}T^{\mu\nu}=&\,\mathbb{A}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\kappa\lambda}\alpha_{\kappa}u_{{\rm L}\lambda}-\frac{\mathbb{A}\mathbb{W}_{2}}{\rho+p}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\kappa\lambda}\alpha_{\kappa}w_{\lambda}+(\mathbb{W}_{1}-\mathbb{W}_{2})u_{\textup{L}}^{\nu}w^{\mu}-p_{\textup{eff}}\,\Delta_{\textup{L}}^{\mu\nu}\\
