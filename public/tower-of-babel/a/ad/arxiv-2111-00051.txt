A unified approach to q q MZVs
Benjamin Brindle

Abstract




Abstract.
This survey gives a self-contained introduction to
q
-analogues of multiple zeta values (
q
MZVs). For this, we consider most common models of
q
MZVs in a unified setup going back to Bachmann and Kühn, such as a related quasi-shuffle product each. Also, we give distinguished translations between several models. As another unified approach to
q
MZVs, we introduce the concept of marked partitions.

Multiple zeta values;

1
Introduction

The
multiple zeta value
of an
admissible index

\textbf{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
, i.e.,
k_{1}\geq 2,\,k_{i}\geq 1
is

\displaystyle\zeta(\textbf{k}):=\zeta(k_{1},\dots,k_{r}):=\sum\limits_{m_{1}>\dots>m_{r}>0}\frac{1}{m_{1}^{k_{1}}}\dots\frac{1}{m_{r}^{k_{r}}},

where we set
\zeta(\emptyset):=1
. We say that
\operatorname{wt}(\textbf{k}):=k_{1}+\dots+k_{r}\text{ is the \emph{weight} and}

0pt(\textbf{k}):=r\text{ is the \emph{depth} of {k}.}
For well-definedness see e.g.
[
Ba6
, Prop. 1.4]
.

MZVs have representation as iterated (
Kontsevich
) integrals:

\displaystyle\zeta(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})=\int\limits_{1>t_{1}>\dots>t_{k}>0}\omega_{1}(t_{1})\dots\omega_{k}(t_{k}),

where
k:=k_{1}+\dots+k_{r}
and

\displaystyle\omega_{i}(t):=\begin{cases}\frac{dt}{1-t},\ &\text{if }i\in\{k_{1},k_{1}+k_{2},\dots,k_{1}+\dots+k_{r}\},\\
\frac{dt}{t},\ &\text{else.}\end{cases}

The product of MZVs is a rational weighted sum of MZVs again. There are two important representations, the one coming from the iterated sums (so-called
stuffle product
), the other from iterated integrals (
shuffle product
). In particular, we get
\mathbb{Q}
-linear relations among MZVs, so-called double shuffle relations. After some regularization, conjecturally, these relations generate all
\mathbb{Q}
-linear relations among MZVs, which is why stuffle and shuffle product are of great interest.

For a more algebraic understanding, we need
quasi-shuffle algebras
(introduced by Hoffman,
[
Hof
]
). These are algebras, where the product is in general a
\mathbb{Q}
-bilinear map
\ast_{\diamond}
on
\mathbb{Q}\left\langle A\right\rangle
-
A
a set,
\diamond
an associative and commutative product on
\mathbb{Q}A
- such that

\displaystyle\textbf{1}\ast_{\diamond}w=w\ast_{\diamond}\textbf{1}:=w,

\displaystyle au\ast_{\diamond}bv:=a(u\ast_{\diamond}bv)+b(au\ast_{\diamond}v)+(a\diamond b)(u\ast_{\diamond}v)

for any
a,b\in\mathbb{Q}A
and
u,v,w\in\mathbb{Q}\left\langle A\right\rangle
. The elements in
A
are called
letters
, monoids in
\mathbb{Q}\left\langle A\right\rangle

words
.

Defining the free non-commutative algebra of two letters,
\mathfrak{h}:=\mathbb{Q}\left\langle x_{0},x_{1}\right\rangle
(
{\mathbf{1}}
the empty word) and its subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}^{0}:=\mathbb{Q}\textbf{1}\oplus x_{0}\mathfrak{h}x_{1}
, then we can view
\zeta
as evaluation map
\mathfrak{h}^{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
via
{\mathbf{1}}\mapsto 1
,

\displaystyle z_{k_{1}}\dots z_{k_{r}}\longmapsto\zeta(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})

and
\mathbb{Q}
-linear continuation with the abbreviation
z_{k}:=x_{0}^{k-1}x_{1}
.

On
\mathfrak{h}^{0}
we consider two particular quasi-shuffle products: First, consider on
\mathbb{Q}A
with

A=\{z_{k}=x_{0}^{k-1}x_{1}\,|\,k\geq 1\}
the diamond product
z_{m}\diamond z_{n}:=z_{m+n}
. Then the induced quasi-shuffle product
\ast
on
\mathbb{Q}\left\langle A\right\rangle
is called
stuffle product
(see e.g.
[
Ba6
, Def. 2.11]
). It is closed under restriction to
\mathfrak{h}^{0}
.

Second, we consider the
shuffle product
(see e.g.
[
Ba6
, Def. 2.8]
): For
A=\{x_{0},x_{1}\}
, i.e.,
\mathbb{Q}\left\langle A\right\rangle=\mathfrak{h}
, and the diamond constant
0
, the induced quasi-shuffle product
\shuffle
on
\mathfrak{h}
is called the
shuffle product
, which also closed under restriction to
\mathfrak{h}^{0}
.

They are important to us since both,
\zeta:(\mathfrak{h}^{0},\ast)\rightarrow(\mathbb{R},\cdot)
and
\zeta:(\mathfrak{h}^{0},\shuffle)\rightarrow(\mathbb{R},\cdot)
, are algebra homomorphisms (
[
IKZ
, Prop. 1]
), the first embodying the product of MZVs obtained from iterated sums, the second the one obtained from iterated integrals.

With the integral representation, one proves the duality relations, which are distinguished linear relations among MZVs. One famous example is the equality
\zeta(2,1)=\zeta(3)
. Conjecturally, they should be implied by the (extended) double shuffle relations, but a proof for this is not known so far.

Theorem 1.1

(Duality,
[
Za1
, §9]
)
.

For every
w\in\mathfrak{h}^{0}
we have

\displaystyle(\zeta\circ\tau)(w)=\zeta(w),

where
\tau
is the anti-automorphism on
\mathfrak{h}
defined via
\tau(x_{i}):=x_{1-i}
for
i\in\{0,1\}
.

In the following and main section of this survey, we give a unified approach to
q
-analogues of multiple zeta values. They are important in the theory of MZVs on the one hand because of their algebraic structure and, on the other hand, since they can be viewed as holomorphic functions in the unit disc giving connections to quasi-modular forms. There exist several models of
q
MZVs, which are often introduced in different ways. One of the goals of the survey is to unify them in the following sense. We introduce
“general”

q
MZVs and we will see that every model we consider is just of specific shape of these general
q
MZVs.

Definition 1.2

(
q\mathrm{MZV}
,
[
BK2
]
)
.

(i)

Define for
r\geq 0
,
k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 0
and polynomials
Q_{1}\in X\mathbb{Q}[X]
,
Q_{2},\dots,Q_{r}\in\mathbb{Q}[X]
with
\deg(Q_{j})\leq k_{j}
for all
j

\displaystyle\zeta_{q}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r}):=\sum\limits_{m_{1}>\dots>m_{r}>0}\frac{Q_{1}(q^{m_{1}})}{(1-q^{m_{1}})^{k_{1}}}\cdots\frac{Q_{r}(q^{m_{r}})}{(1-q^{m_{r}})^{k_{r}}}\in\mathbb{Q}\llbracket q\rrbracket,

with
\zeta_{q}(\emptyset,\emptyset):=1
, where
q
is a formal variable.

(ii)

Furthermore, we define the space

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\mathcal{Z}_{q}:=\left\langle\zeta_{q}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r})\,|\,r\geq 0,\,k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 0,\,Q_{1}\in X\mathbb{Q}[X],\,\deg(Q_{j})\leq k_{j}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{Q}}.

Furthermore, for every model we describe the subspace of
\mathcal{Z}_{q}
that it spans and give a shuffle product that it satisfies. Since it is sometimes useful (like for proving that Schlesinger–Zudilin duality and the partitions relation are equivalent; see
[
Bri
, Thm. 3.22]
), we give distinguished translations of several models into others.

These subspaces then are considered in more detail in Section 3, where we refer to the picture at the end for a brief overview.

Section 4 yields a combinatorial view to
q
MZVs. Every
q
MZV is a
q
-series in particular, and the coefficient of
q^{N}
in a
q
MZV is a natural multiple of distinguished partitions of
N
. Interesting is now that for lots of models - like the Schlesinger–Zudilin model, Bradley–Zhao model, bi-brackets - these multiplicities can be interpreted as coloring of certain rows or columns in the corresponding Young diagram. This leads to the new concept of
marked partitions
and has particularly application for visulization of certain theorems. For example, Schlesinger–Zudilin duality is obtained by just transposing the Young diagram inclusive the markings. Essentially for the whole section is the following:

Theorem 1.3

(
[
Bri
, Thm. 4.7.]
)
.

A
q
-series
S
is in
\mathcal{Z}_{q}
iff there exists
f=(f_{r})_{r\geq 0}
with

f_{r}\in\mathbb{Q}[X_{1},\dots,X_{r},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{r}]
for
r\geq 1
and
f_{0}\in\mathbb{Q}
such that

(i)

f_{r}\equiv 0
for all but finite many
r
,

(ii)

S=f_{0}+\sum\limits_{N\geq 1}\left(\sum\limits_{r\geq 1}\sum\limits_{((\textbf{m},\textbf{n}))\in\mathcal{P}_{r}(N)}f_{r}(m_{1},\dots,m_{r},n_{1},\dots,n_{r})\right)q^{N}.

2
Models of
q
MZVs

On the one hand,
q
-analogues of multiple zeta values are important in the theory of MZVs because of their algebraic structure and, on the other hand, since they can be viewed as holomorphic functions in the unit disc giving connections to quasi-modular forms.

We begin with considering general modified
q
-analogues of MZVs (Def.
1.2
), such as their well-definedness and connection to MZVs. Furthermore, we get here a first time in contact with
\mathcal{Z}_{q}
, the
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra of
q
MZVs. A natural question is which elements generate this algebra, which leads to different models of modified
q
MZVs. Every model of
q
MZVs contains at least one algebraic aspect of MZVs: Schlesinger–Zudilin’s model inherits the stuffle product, and Bradley–Zhao’s model the duality of MZVs. Also interesting is Bachmann’s model since it gives a deep connection to modular forms that play an important role in the theory of MZVs as already Gangl, Kaneko, and Zagier (in
[
GKZ
]
) and Broadhurst and Kreimer (in
[
BK
]
) have shown. For more details about the various models, we refer to the original works
[
Bra
]
,
[
Zh1
]
,
[
Sch
]
,
[
Zu2
]
,
[
Ba2
]
,
[
Tak
]
,
[
OOZ
]
,
[
Oko
]
, such as to
[
Zh2
]
, where the author gives an overview of the models and their history.

In general, a
q
-analogue of an object is a modified object in an additional variable
q
(often a series in (complex)
q
with
|q|<1
) that returns the original object in the limit
q\rightarrow 1
, taken on the real axis from the left.

For example, a
q
-analogue of a natural number
n
is

\displaystyle[n]_{q}:=\frac{1-q^{n}}{1-q}=1+q+q^{2}+\dots+q^{n-1}.

Modified

q
-analogues of MZVs are
q
-series that return a multiple zeta value if we multiply the
q
-series first with a power of
(1-q)
(most times,
(1-q)^{\operatorname{wt}(\textbf{k})}
) and then take the limit
q\rightarrow 1
. This is convenient since we can avoid the additional power-of-
(1-q)
-factors without losing the structure we want to consider; furthermore, the spaces spanned by these objects become
\mathbb{Q}
-algebras as we will see, while the spaces spanned by non-modified objects become
\mathbb{Q}(1-q)
-algebras. We consider only modified
q
MZVs, why we avoid the word
“modified”
in the following.

Remark 2.1

(to Def.
1.2
)
.

(i)

The condition
Q_{1}\in X\mathbb{Q}[X]
is necessary for well-definedness.

(ii)

Note that
\mathcal{Z}_{q}
does not contain all modified
q
-analogues of MZVs inevitably. For example, it is not clear yet whether the modified
q
MZVs introduced by Shen and Qin (
[
SQ
]
) are in
\mathcal{Z}_{q}
.

We use notation from
[
BK2
]
, where the authors introduce important subspaces of
\mathcal{Z}_{q}
:

Definition 2.2
.

Define for
d\geq 0

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{q,d}:=

\displaystyle\left\langle\zeta_{q}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r})\in\mathcal{Z}_{q}|\,r\geq 0,\,k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 1,\deg(Q_{j})\leq k_{j}-d\right\rangle_{\mathbb{Q}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}^{\circ}_{q,d}:=

\displaystyle\left\langle\zeta_{q}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r})\in\mathcal{Z}_{q,d}|\,r\geq 0,\,k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 1,Q_{j}\in X\mathbb{Q}[X]\right\rangle_{\mathbb{Q}}

with the abbreviation
\mathcal{Z}^{\circ}_{q}:=\mathcal{Z}^{\circ}_{q,0}
.

Remark 2.3
.

Naively, we could think of

as

of

in accordance with the definition of weight for MZVs. But this is not well-defined since for example

\displaystyle\zeta_{q}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r})=\zeta_{q}(k_{1}+1,\dots,k_{r};(1-X)Q_{1},Q_{2}\dots,Q_{r}).

Hence, we need another notion of weight. Such one we will consider e.g. for bi-brackets (Def.
2.33
).

Remark 2.4
.

(i)

The spaces
\mathcal{Z}_{q}

(
[

]
,
[

(ii)

The spaces
\mathcal{Z}_{q,1}
and

(
[

]

[

Proposition 2.5

(
[
Bri
, Prop. A.32]
)
.

(i)

For
q\in\mathbb{C},\,|q|<1
, every
q
MZV converges and can be viewed as holomorphic function inside the unit disk or upper half plane if
q=e^{2\pi i\tau}
with
\tau\in\mathbb{H}
.

(ii)

For
k_{1}\geq 2,\,k_{2},\dots,k_{r}\geq 1
, the limit

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{q\rightarrow 1^{-}}(1-q)^{k_{1}+\dots+k_{r}}\zeta_{q}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r})=\zeta_{q}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})\prod\limits_{j=1}^{r}Q_{j}(1),

exists and is obtained by interchanging limit and summation due to absolute convergence on
[0,1)
.

Proof.

For (i) we show the convergence of

\displaystyle(1-q)^{k_{1}+\dots+k_{r}}\zeta_{q}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r})

for all
k_{1}\geq 1,\,k_{2},\dots,k_{r}\geq 0
and for
|q|<1
which is equivalent to (i) because of
q\neq 1
.

Therefore, fix some
q
with
|q|<1
,
r\geq 1
(for
r=0
there is nothing to do),
k_{1}\geq 1,\,k_{2},\dots,k_{r}\geq 0
and polynomials
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r}
in accordance to the definition of
q
MZVs.

We can write every
Q_{j}
in the form

\displaystyle Q_{j}=Q_{j}^{+}-Q_{j}^{-},

where
Q_{j}^{+}
and
Q_{j}^{-}
are polynomials having coefficients
\geq 0
and the same properties as
Q_{j}
(i.e.,
\deg\leq k_{j},\,Q_{1}^{+},Q_{1}^{-}\in X\mathbb{Q}[X]
). Thus,
\zeta_{q}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r})
is a finite rational linear combination of
q
MZVs where the corresponding polynomials have non-negative coefficients, which is the reason why we can assume in the following w.l.o.g. that the
Q_{j}
all have non-negative coefficients.

Then we have

\displaystyle 0\leq|Q_{j}(q^{m})|\leq Q_{j}(0)+Q_{j}(1)|q|^{m}

for all
|q|<1
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

For
j\neq 1
, we use
|q|<1
and that the coefficients of
Q_{j}
are all non-negative which gives us furthermore

\displaystyle 0\leq|Q_{j}(q^{m})|\leq Q_{j}(0)+Q_{j}(1)=:c_{j}.

Since
Q_{1}(0)=0
, we have for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle 0\leq|Q_{1}(q^{m})|\leq Q_{1}(1)|q|^{m}.

Now, since

\displaystyle\Bigg|\frac{(1-q)^{k}}{(1-q^{m})^{k}}\Bigg|=\Bigg|\frac{1}{(1+q+\dots+q^{m-1})^{k}}\Bigg|\leq C_{q,k}

for all
m
with an appropriate constant
C_{q,m}\in\mathbb{R}
(note that
\lim\limits_{m\rightarrow\infty}\Big|\frac{(1-q)^{k}}{(1-q^{m})^{k}}\Big|=|(1-q)|^{k}
exists since
|q|<1
), we get for all tuples
(m_{1},\dots,m_{r})
of natural numbers

\displaystyle 0\leq|1-q|^{k_{1}+\dots+k_{r}}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{r}\frac{|Q_{j}(q^{m_{j}})|}{|1-q^{m_{j}}|^{k_{j}}}\leq C|q|^{m_{1}}

with
C:=Q_{1}(1)c_{2}\cdots c_{r}C_{q,k_{1}}\cdots C_{q,k_{r}}
.

For fixed
0\leq s<1
, the map
x\mapsto s^{x}
decreases with exponential growth in
x
. Hence, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there is a constant
M_{n}
(depends on
|q|
) such that

\displaystyle|q|^{m}\leq M_{n}\frac{1}{m^{n}}

for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, for
n=r+1
, we obtain

\displaystyle 0\leq\,

\displaystyle|1-q|^{k_{1}+\dots+k_{r}}|\zeta_{q}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r})|\leq CM_{r+1}\sum\limits_{m_{1}>\dots>m_{r}>0}\frac{1}{m_{1}^{r+1}}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle CM_{r+1}\sum\limits_{m_{1}>0}\binom{m_{1}-1}{r-1}\frac{1}{m_{1}^{r+1}}\leq CM_{r+1}\sum\limits_{m>0}\frac{1}{m^{2}}<\infty

because of
\binom{m-1}{r-1}=\frac{(m-1)(m-r+1)}{(r-1)!}\leq m^{r-1}
and the well-known fact that the Basler sum converges.

In particular, the defining sum of
(1-q)^{k_{1}+\dots+k_{r}}\zeta_{q}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r})
converges for every
|q|<1
, which proves (i).

For the proof of (ii), we remark that on the intervall
[0,1)
our sum converges absolutely since there, we can choose
C_{q,k}=1
for all
q
and
k\geq 1
, i.e., independent of
q
. Due to this uniform convergence, limit and summation can be interchanged. Now, for all
k\geq 0,\,m\in\mathbb{N}
and
1\leq j\leq r
,

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{q\rightarrow 1}Q_{j}(q^{m})=Q_{j}(1),\quad\lim\limits_{q\rightarrow 1}\frac{(1-q)^{k}}{(1-q^{m})^{k}}=\lim\limits_{q\rightarrow 1}\left(\frac{1}{1+q+\dots+q^{m-1}}\right)^{k}=\frac{1}{m^{k}},

which is why we get for fixed
k_{1}\geq 2,\,k_{2},\dots,k_{r}\geq 1
and with (i)

\displaystyle\zeta_{q}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})\prod\limits_{j=1}^{r}Q_{j}(1)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m_{1}>\dots>m_{r}>0}\lim\limits_{q\rightarrow 1}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{r}\frac{(1-q)^{k_{j}}Q_{j}(q^{m_{j}})}{(1-q^{m_{j}})^{k_{j}}}

∎

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{q\rightarrow 1}(1-q)^{k_{1}+\dots+k_{r}}\zeta_{q}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r}).

A similar proof for the proposition with stronger assumption for (i) is given in
[
BK1
, Lem. 6.6]
.

Often, we talk about
\mathcal{Z}_{q}
as algebra. We justify this in the following as we see that we can give
\mathcal{Z}_{q}
a structure such that it becomes a quasi-shuffle algebra.

Definition 2.6
.

Consider the alphabet

A_{Z}:=\{{\textstyle{\binom{k}{Q}}}\,|\,Q\in\mathbb{Q}[X],\,k\in\mathbb{N},\,\deg(Q)\leq k\}.

Define on
\mathbb{Q}A_{Z}
the commutative and associative product
\diamond:\mathbb{Q}A_{Z}\otimes\mathbb{Q}A_{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}A_{Z}
by
\mathbb{Q}
-bilinearity,
w\diamond{\mathbf{1}}:={\mathbf{1}}\diamond w:=w
for all
w\in\mathbb{Q}A_{Z}
and

\displaystyle\left(\binom{k_{1}}{Q_{1}},\binom{k_{2}}{Q_{2}}\right)\longmapsto\binom{k_{1}+k_{2}}{Q_{1}\cdot Q_{2}}.

Set
M:=A_{Z}^{\circ}\mathbb{Q}\left\langle A_{Z}\right\rangle+\mathbb{Q}
, where
A_{Z}^{\circ}:=\{{\textstyle{\binom{k}{Q}}}:Q\in X\mathbb{Q}[X],\,k\in\mathbb{N},\,\deg(Q)\leq k\}
, and let
\ast
be the induced quasi-shuffle product on
M
. It turns out that
\zeta_{q}
becomes an algebra homomorphism:

Proposition 2.7
.

The evaluation map
\zeta_{q}:M\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{q}
, defined via

\displaystyle\binom{k_{1}}{Q_{1}}\cdots\binom{k_{r}}{Q_{r}}\longmapsto\zeta_{q}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r}),

extended to
M
by
\mathbb{Q}
-linearity, is an algebra homomorphism, i.e., for all
u,v\in M
we have

\displaystyle\zeta_{q}(u\ast v)=\zeta_{q}(u)\zeta_{q}(v).

Proof.

This follows from multiplication of
q
MZVs, represented as iterated sums.
∎

Remark 2.8

(
[

)

The subspaces
\mathcal{Z}_{q,d},\mathcal{Z}_{q,d}^{\circ}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}_{q}

Define two free non-commutative algebras of two letters,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}:=\,\mathbb{Q}\left\langle x_{0},x_{1}\right\rangle,\quad\mathfrak{K}:=\,\mathbb{Q}\left\langle p,y\right\rangle

Monomials in the two
“letters”

“words”
. They form a
\mathbb{Q}
-basis of
\mathfrak{h}

\mathfrak{K}

2.1
Schlesinger–Zudilin model

One of the most natural questions is how bases or at least generating systems of the
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space
\mathcal{Z}_{q}
look like and whether there are interesting subspaces we should consider.

The probably most natural looking generating system when writing the elements of

[

(i)

(ii)

\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{SZ}}(\emptyset):=1
and for

\displaystyle\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{SZ}}(\mathbf{k}):=\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{SZ}}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r}):=\zeta_{q}\left(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};X^{k_{1}},\dots,X^{k_{r}}\right)=\sum\limits_{m_{1}>\dots>m_{r}>0}\frac{q^{m_{1}k_{1}}}{(1-q^{m_{1}})^{k_{1}}}\dots\frac{q^{m_{r}k_{r}}}{(1-q^{m_{r}})^{k_{r}}}.

We introduced an extended version due to Ebrahimi-Fard, Manchon, and Singer (cf.
[

]
). In the original model, due to Schlesinger and Zudilin, only indices with

were allowed.

Remark 2.11
.

(i)

Note that if one of the indices is 0 in an SZ-

for some

. Therefore, it is often useful to distinguish between zero and non-zero indices.

(ii)

An index

(iii)

The name of the SZ-model is attributed not only to Schlesinger, although his publication (

(with

On closer inspection, we see that this is almost

\displaystyle\zeta_{q^{-1}}^{\mathrm{SZ^{\prime}}}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})=(-1)^{k_{1}+\dots+k_{r}}\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{SZ}}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r}).

Further details of the history of SZ-

(iv)

For some applications as translation/duality in the OOZ-model (Thm.

As for MZVs and MZSVs, every SZ-
q
MZSV is a finite sum of SZ-
q
MZVs.

Proposition 2.12
.

SZ-

MZVs span
\mathcal{Z}_{q}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{q}=\left\langle\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{SZ}}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})\,\middle|\,r\geq 0,\,k_{1}\geq 1,\,k_{i}\geq 0\right\rangle_{\mathbb{Q}}.

Proof.

The proof is obtained from the fact that every expression

In particular, the SZ-model is closed under the operator

(i).

Definition 2.13

(SZ-stuffle product)
.

(i)

Define
u_{k}:=p^{k}y\in\mathfrak{K}
for all
k\geq 0
.

(ii)

Consider on
\mathfrak{K}

(b)

and

Remark at this point that
\mathfrak{K}^{1}
is generated by the words starting in some

Definition 2.14

Identifying

\displaystyle\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{SZ}}\,:\,\mathfrak{K}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}\llbracket q\rrbracket,\qquad u_{k_{1}}\dots u_{k_{r}}\mapsto\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{SZ}}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})

to

by

Interesting is that

an algebra homomorphism on

.

The map

is an algebra homomorphism on

\displaystyle\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{SZ}}(v)\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{SZ}}(w)=\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{SZ}}(v\ast_{\mathrm{SZ}}w).

Proof.

The statement follows directly from the definition of SZ-
q
MZVs as iterated sums.
∎

We can consider SZ-
q

Definition 2.16

(i)

Define recursively the SZ-

(b)

(c)

Theorem 2.18

(
[
Bri
, Thm. 2.9]
)
.

Define for every

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{X_1,\dots,X_r}{Y_1,\dots,Y_r}:=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 1\\
d_{1},\dots,d_{r}\geq 0\end{subarray}}\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{SZ}}\left(k_{1},\{0\}^{d_{1}},\dots,k_{r},\{0\}^{d_{r}}\right)X_{1}^{k_{1}-1}\dots X_{r}^{k_{r}-1}Y_{1}^{d_{1}}\dots Y_{r}^{d_{r}}.

Then, for every

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{X_1,\dots,X_r}{Y_1,\dots,Y_r}=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}>\dots>m_{r}>0\\
n_{1},\dots,n_{r}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{r}(1+X_{j})^{n_{j}-1}(1+Y_{j})^{m_{j}-m_{j+1}-1}q^{m_{j}n_{j}}.

We need the binomial theorem and the following for all

\displaystyle\#\{n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}\,\big|\,M_{1}>n_{1}>\dots>n_{\ell}>M_{2}\}=\binom{M_{1}-M_{2}-1}{\ell}.

With this, we get

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{X_1,\dots,X_r}{Y_1,\dots,Y_r}=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 1\\
d_{1},\dots,d_{r}\geq 0\end{subarray}}\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{SZ}}\left(k_{1},\{0\}^{d_{1}},\dots,k_{r},\{0\}^{d_{r}}\right)X_{1}^{k_{1}-1}\dots X_{r}^{k_{r}-1}Y_{1}^{d_{1}}\dots Y_{r}^{d_{r}}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 1\\
d_{1},\dots,d_{r}\geq 0\end{subarray}}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}>n_{1}>\dots>n_{d_{1}}>m_{2}\\
>\dots>0\end{subarray}}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{r}\frac{q^{m_{j}k_{j}}}{(1-q^{m_{j}})^{k_{j}}}X_{1}^{k_{1}-1}\dots X_{r}^{k_{r}-1}Y_{1}^{d_{1}}\dots Y_{r}^{d_{\ell}}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 1\\
d_{1},\dots,d_{r}\geq 0\end{subarray}}\sum\limits_{m_{1}>\dots>m_{r}>0}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{r}\binom{m_{j}-m_{j+1}-1}{d_{j}}\frac{q^{m_{j}k_{j}}}{(1-q^{m_{j}})^{k_{j}}}X_{1}^{k_{1}-1}\dots X_{r}^{k_{r}-1}Y_{1}^{d_{1}}\dots Y_{r}^{d_{r}}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}>\dots>m_{r}>0\\
n_{1},\dots,n_{r}>0\end{subarray}}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{r}\left[\sum\limits_{k_{j}\geq 1,d_{j}\geq 0}\binom{m_{j}-m_{j+1}-1}{d_{j}}\binom{n_{j}-1}{k_{j}-1}X_{j}^{k_{j}-1}Y_{j}^{d_{j}}q^{m_{j}n_{j}}\right]

∎

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}>\dots>m_{r}>0\\
n_{1},\dots,n_{r}>0\end{subarray}}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{r}(1+X_{j})^{n_{j}-1}(1+Y_{j})^{m_{j}-m_{j+1}-1}q^{m_{j}n_{j}}.

\displaystyle\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{SZ}}\circ\tilde{\tau}=\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{SZ}}.

Remark 2.20

(Comparison)
.

The SZ-model is a very elegant model of

MZVs since it satisfies the

That was also the reason why we got a generating series we can work with. But there is more: The stuffle product for the SZ-model induces together with SZ-duality the shuffle product of MZVs (cf.
[

, for details see
[

2.1.1
SZ-shuffle on Rota–Baxter algebras

Most quasi-shuffle products in different models can be defined on so-called Rota–Baxter algebras such that
q
MZVs in the responding model is just a special value of iterated such operators. Exemplary, we do this here for the SZ-model. For other models, see
[
Sin
]
.

Definition 2.21

(
Rota–Baxter operator
)
.

Let
C
be a ring,
\lambda\in C
, and
\mathcal{A}
a
C
-algebra.

A
Rota–Baxter operator
(RBO)
R
of weight
\lambda
on
\mathcal{A}
over
C
is a
C
-module endomorphism of
\mathcal{A}
such that

(2.2)

\displaystyle R(x)R(y)=R(xR(y))+R(R(x)y)+\lambda R(xy)

for all
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
.

Furhermore, a
Rota–Baxter
\mathcal{C}
-algebra
(RBA) is a pair
(\mathcal{A},R)
with a
C
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
and a RBO (of some weight
\lambda
) on
\mathcal{A}
over
C
.

Example 2.22
.

Consider
t\mathbb{Q}\llbracket t,q\rrbracket
, the vector space of formal power series
f(t)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}t^{n}
with
a_{n}\in\mathbb{Q}\llbracket q\rrbracket
and
\inf\{n\in\mathbb{N}\,|\,a_{n}\neq 0\}
. It can be viewed as a
\mathbb{Q}\llbracket q\rrbracket
-algebra. We will denote it by
\mathcal{A}_{q}
.
Then a RBO of weight 1 is the operator
P_{q}:\mathcal{A}_{q}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}_{q}
, defined via

\displaystyle P_{q}[f](t):=\,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n>0}f(q^{n}t).

In the following, let
T:\mathcal{A}_{q}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}_{q}
be the operator
t\mapsto\frac{t}{1-t}
. Then we see that every SZ-
q
MZV is just the value at
t=1
of a concatination of Operators
P_{q}
and
T
:

Proposition 2.23

(
[
Sin
, Prop. 5.2]
)
.

For every
r\geq 1
and
k_{1}\geq 1,k_{2},\dots,k_{r}\geq 0
we have:

\displaystyle P_{q}^{k_{1}}\left[TP_{q}^{k_{2}}\left[T\dots P_{q}^{k_{r}}[T]\dots\right]\right](t)=\sum\limits_{m_{1}>\dots>m_{r}>0}t^{m_{1}}\frac{q^{m_{1}k_{1}}}{(1-q^{m_{1}})^{k_{1}}}\dots\frac{q^{m_{r}k_{r}}}{(1-q^{m_{r}})^{k_{r}}},

i.e., in particular
P_{q}^{k_{1}}\left[TP_{q}^{k_{2}}\left[T\dots P_{q}^{k_{r}}[T]\dots\right]\right](1)=\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{SZ}}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})
.

That
\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{SZ}}
is an algebra homomorphism on
(\mathfrak{K}^{1},\shuffle_{SZ})
follows now by the more general statement, saying that
\phi_{q}:\mathfrak{K}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}_{q}
, defined through

\displaystyle p^{k_{1}}y\cdots p^{k_{r}}y\longmapsto P_{q}^{k_{1}}\left[TP_{q}^{k_{2}}\left[T\cdots P_{q}^{k_{r}}[T]\cdots\right]\right]

is an algebra homomorphism. This follows from obtaining that the letter
p
corresponds to
P_{q}
and
y
to
T
. Then (ii) of the defining property of
\shuffle_{\mathrm{SZ}}
(if
y
is in one of both sides of the product leading, then we may pull it out) corresponds to the fact that by multiplication in
\mathcal{A}_{q}
we may pull out some factor (since
\mathcal{A}_{q}
is commutative), especially a factor
\frac{t}{1-t}
, corresponding to
T
. Analogously, we can clarify that defining property (iii) of
\shuffle_{\mathrm{SZ}}
corresponds to the RHS of the definition of an RBO of weight 1, (
2.2
). But now, the LHS of (
2.2
) will give the desired product in
\mathcal{A}_{q}
, making
\phi_{q}
to an algebra homomorphism.

2.2
Bradley–Zhao model

In depth one, the BZ-model of
q
MZVs was first considered by Kaneko, Kurokawa, and Wakayama in 2002,
[
KKW
]
. The general model was then introduced by Zhao in 2003 (
[
Zh1
]
) and independent of Zhao by Bradley in 2004 (
[
Bra
]
), clarifying its name. BZ-
q
MZVs satisfy the same duality as MZVs which is why this model plays an important role in the context of
q
MZVs.

Definition 2.24

(
Bradley–Zhao-
q
MZVs
)
.

For every admissible index
\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})
we define

\displaystyle\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{BZ}}(\mathbf{k}):=

\displaystyle\,\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{BZ}}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r}):=\,\zeta_{q}\left(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};X^{k_{1}-1},\dots,X^{k_{r}-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{m_{1}>\dots>m_{r}>0}\frac{q^{m_{1}(k_{1}-1)}}{(1-q^{m_{1}})^{k_{1}}}\cdots\frac{q^{m_{r}(k_{r}-1)}}{(1-q^{m_{r}})^{k_{r}}}.

In contrast to the SZ-model, BZ-
q
MZVs span only a subspace of
\mathcal{Z}_{q}
:

Proposition 2.25
.

The span of the BZ-model is
\mathcal{Z}_{q,1}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{q,1}=\left\langle\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{BZ}}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})\,|\,r\geq 0,\,k_{1}\geq 2,\,k_{i}\geq 1\right\rangle_{\mathbb{Q}}.

Proof.

Every BZ-
q
MZV is by definition an element of
\mathcal{Z}_{q,1}
. That also every element of
\mathcal{Z}_{q,1}
can be written as rational linear combination, follows from the identity

\displaystyle\frac{X^{s}}{(1-X)^{n}}=\frac{X^{s}}{(1-X)^{s+1}}\left(1+\frac{X}{1-X}\right)^{n-s+1}

for all
0\leq s<n
and since the RHS is a rational linear combination of expressions
\frac{X^{k-1}}{(1-X)^{k}}
.
∎

Note that
\mathcal{Z}_{q,1}
is a proper subspace of
\mathcal{Z}_{q}
since
\zeta_{q}(1;X)\in\mathcal{Z}_{q}
, e.g., can not be written in terms of BZ-
q
MZVs. This fact can be proven with arguments similar to
[
BK1
, Thm. 2.14 (ii)]
.

BZ-
q
MZVs satisfy a quasi-shuffle product, in analogy to the stuffle product of MZVs since it is induced by multiplication of iterated sums:

Definition 2.26
.

(i)

We can define
\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{BZ}}
also as map
\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{BZ}}\,:\,\mathfrak{h}^{0}\rightarrow\,\mathcal{Z}_{q}
, defined via

\displaystyle z_{k_{1}}\dots z_{k_{r}}\longmapsto\,\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{BZ}}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})

by
\mathbb{Q}
-linear continuation and
\mathbf{1}\mapsto 1
.

(ii)

Define on
\mathbb{Q}\{z_{k}:\,k\in\mathbb{N}\}
the commutative and associative product
\diamond_{BZ}
via

\displaystyle z_{k_{1}}\diamond_{BZ}z_{k_{2}}:=z_{k_{1}+k_{2}}+z_{k_{1}+k_{2}-1}

for all
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 1
and
\mathbf{1}\diamond_{BZ}w:=w\diamond_{BZ}\mathbf{1}:=w
for all
w\in\mathfrak{h}^{0}
. Let be
\ast_{BZ}
the induced quasi-shuffle product on
\mathfrak{h}^{1}
.

Notice that
\mathfrak{h}^{0}\subset\mathfrak{h}^{1}
is closed under
\ast_{BZ}
. We have the following:

Proposition 2.27
.

On
(\mathfrak{h}^{0},\ast_{BZ})
,
\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{BZ}}
is an algebra homomorphism, i.e., for all

we have

\displaystyle\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{BZ}}(u\ast_{BZ}v)=\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{BZ}}(u)\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{BZ}}(v).

Proof.

This is elementary calculation using the product of iterated sums and the fact

∎

\displaystyle\frac{q^{m(k-2)}}{(1-q^{m})^{k}}=\frac{q^{m(k-2)}}{(1-q^{m})^{k-1}}+\frac{q^{m(k-1)}}{(1-q^{m})^{k}}.

Define for the following the abbreviation

\displaystyle f_{\ell}(X,Y):=\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{X,0,\dots,0}{Y,\dots,Y}

for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, where both rows have
\ell
entries. Furthermore, we identify
(f_{\ell_{1}},\dots,f_{\ell_{r}})
,
\ell_{j},r\in\mathbb{N}
, with the concatenation of

.

Theorem 2.28

(
[
Bri
, Thm. 2.13]
)
.

Define for
r\geq 1
the generating series of BZ-

\displaystyle\,\mathfrak{b}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{X_1,\dots,X_r}{Y_1,\dots,Y_r}=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 1\\

Then, we have for every
r\geq 1

\displaystyle\,\mathfrak{b}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{X_1,\dots,X_r}{Y_1,\dots,Y_r}=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{r}\geq 1\\
\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{r}\in\{0,1\}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{r-(\delta_{1}+\dots+\delta_{r})}\mathfrak{s}\left(f_{\ell_{1}}(\delta_{1}X_{1},Y_{1}),\dots,f_{\ell_{r}}(\delta_{r}X_{r},Y_{r})\right)\prod\limits_{j=1}^{r}(1+\delta_{j}X_{j})Y_{j}^{\ell_{j}-1}.

We give a proof for clarity reasons only for

\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{X}{Y}=\sum\limits_{\ell\geq 1,\,\delta\in\{0,1\}}(-1)^{1-\delta}\mathfrak{s}\left(f_{\ell}(\delta X,Y)\right)(1+\delta X)Y^{\ell}.

Using the definition of
\mathfrak{b}
and
\mathfrak{s}
as generating series of BZ- resp. SZ-

in each summand resp.

additionally in the last sum)

\displaystyle\,\sum\limits_{k,\,d\geq 1}\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{BZ}}\left(k+1,\{1\}^{d-1}\right)X^{k}Y^{d}

\displaystyle=

\sum\limits_{g_{1}=0}^{F_{\mathbf{h},\mathbf{g}}^{\mathbf{f}}(1)}

F_{\mathbf{h},\mathbf{g}}^{\mathbf{f}}(1)=f_{1}
).

•

Independent of the above choice, the
g_{1}
chosen
b_{i}^{j}
can coincide. The ways, how this is possible, are: they coincide all (
l_{1}=1
), they can coincide in two different ways (
l_{1}=2
) and so on or they are all different

. The number of ways how they can coincide gives the multinomial coefficient. If
g_{1}=0
, nothing coincides (
l_{1}=0
) and there is exact one possibility, which clarifies the Kronecker-delta
\delta_{g_{1}=0}
and so the
\sum\limits_{\ell_{2}=0}^{g_{1}}
-sum is declared.

•

Now, there are
f_{1}-g_{1}
of the

of the first item left, i.e., they are all at most
m_{2}
. Hence,
0
upto
f_{1}-g_{1}
of them can equal
m_{2}
. For every such number,
h_{1}
, there are
\binom{f_{1}-g_{1}}{h_{1}}
possibilities, which of them are equal
m_{2}
. This clarifies the
\sum\limits_{h_{1}=0}^{H_{\mathbf{h},\mathbf{g}}^{\mathbf{f}}(1)}
-sum (note that
H_{\mathbf{h},\mathbf{g}}^{\mathbf{f}}(1)=f_{1}-g_{1}
).

•

Now,
f_{1}-g_{1}-h_{1}
of the
b_{i}^{j}
of the first item are left and all are less than
m_{2}
.

•

So we can do every of the above three steps (i)-(iii) again, now with
m_{2}
and
m_{3}
instead of
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
(and
F_{\mathbf{h},\mathbf{g}}^{\mathbf{f}}(2)=f_{2}+(f_{1}-g_{1}-h_{1})
instead of
F_{\mathbf{h},\mathbf{g}}^{\mathbf{f}}(1)
for the number of
b_{i}^{j}
’s less than
m_{2}
).

•

We are now done since we get as remaining part of the formula (after ordering the indices and setting
m_{r+1}:=0
as usual) the sum

∎

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{j}>a_{1}^{j}>\dots>a_{\ell_{j}}^{j}>m_{j+1}\\
1\leq j\leq r\end{subarray}}\frac{q^{m_{1}p_{1}}}{(1-q^{m_{1}})^{p_{1}}}\cdots\frac{q^{m_{r}p_{r}}}{(1-q^{m_{r}})^{p_{r}}}=\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{SZ}}\left(p_{1},\{0\}^{\ell_{1}},\dots,p_{r},\{0\}^{\ell_{r}}\right).

Remark 2.45
.

Zudilin’s model of
q
MZVs,
multiple
q
-zeta brackets
, is closely related to bi-brackets (
[
Zu2
]
for details). They are defined for
k_{1},\dots,k_{r},\,d_{1},\dots,d_{r}\geq 1
(
r\geq 0
) as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Z}_{q}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,\dots,k_r}{d_1,\dots,d_r}:=c\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\dots,m_{r}>0\\
n_{1},\dots,n_{r}>0\end{subarray}}m_{1}^{d_{1}-1}n_{1}^{k_{1}-1}\cdots m_{r}^{d_{r}-1}n_{r}^{k_{r}-1}q^{(m_{1}+\dots+m_{r})n_{1}+\dots+m_{r}n_{r}}

with

.

This model is also of interest because of the duality relation,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Z}_{q}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,\dots,k_r}{d_1,\dots,d_r}=\mathfrak{Z}_{q}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{d_r,\dots,d_1}{k_r,\dots,k_1},

which is exactly the partition relation for bi-brackets (
[
Zu2
, Prop. 4]
).

We consider this model not in more detail since it is
“more or less”
Bachmann’s model of bi-brackets, and the connection to bi-brackets is also studied very well in
[
Zu2
]
.

Remark 2.46

(Comparison)
.

Bi-brackets are very good for connecting
q
MZVs direct with quasi-modular forms. Furthermore, this model gives with the partition relation another view onto SZ-duality since both are equivalent (cf.
[
Bri
, Thm. 3.22]
). However, explicit calculations are usually complicated in this model because the explicit representation of Eulerian polynomials is not very nice.

.

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{q}=\left\langle\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{TBZ}}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})\,\middle|\,r\geq 0,\,k_{i}\in\overline{\mathbb{N}},\,k_{1}\neq 1\right\rangle_{\mathbb{Q}}.

Proof.

The TBZ-model spans the same space as the SZ-model (Prop.

Definition 2.49
.

(i)

\displaystyle z_{k_{1}}\diamond_{TBZ}z_{k_{2}}:=z_{k_{1}+k_{2}}+z_{k_{1}+k_{2}-1},\quad z_{k}\diamond_{TBZ}z_{\overline{1}}:=z_{\overline{1}}\diamond_{TBZ}z_{k}:=z_{k+1},\quad z_{\overline{1}}\diamond_{TBZ}z_{\overline{1}}:=z_{2}-z_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\left(\sum\limits_{m_{1}=m_{2}=m_{3}>0}+\sum\limits_{m_{1}>m_{2}=m_{3}>0}+\sum\limits_{m_{1}=m_{2}>m_{3}>0}+\sum\limits_{m_{1}>m_{2}>m_{3}>0}\right)\frac{q^{m_{1}}}{1-q^{m_{1}}}\frac{q^{m_{2}}}{1-q^{m_{2}}}

\displaystyle\,\sum\limits_{m_{1}\geq m_{2}\geq m_{3}>0}\frac{q^{m_{1}}}{1-q^{m_{1}}}\frac{q^{m_{2}}}{1-q^{m_{2}}}=\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{SZ},\star}(1,1,0)=\zeta_{q}^{\mathrm{SZ},\star}(\tilde{\tau}(w)).
