Van Hove singularities in the density of states of a chaotic dynamical system
Bryn Davies

Abstract
We show that the statistics of chaotic systems can be predicted by constructing an associated sequence of periodic differential operators and computing their densities of states. For such operators, the density of states is well understood and can be computed straightforwardly, often yielding explicit formulas. As a case study, we investigate a nonlinear recursion relation that maps naturally onto a family of periodic operators generated by a Fibonacci tiling rule. This correspondence enables us to derive an explicit formula for the limiting statistics of the chaotic system and to demonstrate that the clustering near to critical values is equivalent to the van Hove singularities in the operators’ densities of states.

Introduction

Chaotic systems have been long-standing objects of fascination. Although the long-term behaviour of individual orbits is hard to predict, in no small part due to the sensitivity to initial conditions, the collective behaviour of many orbits can often be captured in a statistical sense by understanding convergence to invariant measures. Then, using probabilistic methods, it is often possible to make precise statements about the likelihood of a future state
[
1
,
2
]
. Although this is most commonly achieved by computing the density of states through exploiting ergodic properties and finding invariant measures, other methods exist, such as understanding a system’s statistics by reformulating the problem in terms of random matrix theory
[
3
,
4
]
.

In this work, we demonstrate a new approach to predicting the statistics of a chaotic system, by constructing a corresponding sequence of periodic differential operators and computing their associated densities of states. This ambition is similar in spirit to moment problems, where the aim is to find a linear operator whose moments generate the values of the recursion relation
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
. However, in this case our approach will have the significant benefit of being able to exploit the well-developed spectral theory for periodic differential operators
[
8
]
. This yields predictions of the system’s statistics including, as in the case study considered here, explicit formulas.

The link between dynamical systems and spectral properties of one-dimensional differential operators is well known. It has facilitated many breakthroughs on the spectral theory of operators with exotic and non-periodic coefficients. For example, much of the spectral theory for quasi-periodic operators is based on reformulating problems as dynamical systems
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
and random systems are often studied using Lyapunov functions to characterise decay rates and mean free paths
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
. In this work, we will show that this relationship is also useful in the other direction, as reformulating a dynamical system as a sequence of differential operators allows well-known formulas for their densities of states to be used to predict the statistics.

We will take as a case study a nonlinear recursion relation which can be related to a sequence of periodic operators generated by a Fibonacci tiling rule. The recursion relation is

IV
Periodic operators

Consider the differential eigenvalue problem

\mathcal{H}_{L}u=\lambda u,\qquad\mathcal{H}_{L}=\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}+V(x),

(6)

where
V:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is some piecewise smooth and
L
-periodic potential function. A key tool for understanding the solution space of (
6
) is the
monodromy matrix
. Given the basis functions
\phi(x,\lambda)
and
\psi(x,\lambda)
, which are defined as solutions to (
6
) satisfying the conditions
\phi(0,\lambda)=\partial_{x}\psi(0,\lambda)=1
and
\partial_{x}\phi(0,\lambda)=\psi(0,\lambda)=0
, the monodromy matrix
M_{L}
is defined as

M_{L}(\lambda):=\begin{pmatrix}\phi(L,\lambda)&\psi(L,\lambda)\\
\partial_{x}\phi(L,\lambda)&\partial_{x}\psi(L,\lambda)\end{pmatrix}.

(7)

M_{L}(\lambda)\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
is a square matrix with determinant equal to 1.

The crucial information about the spectrum of the differential operator
\mathcal{H}_{L}
is given by the eigenvalues of
M_{L}
, which are in turn determined by its trace. As a result, the trace of the monodromy matrix

\Delta_{L}(\lambda):=\mathrm{tr}\,M_{L}(\lambda)=\phi(L,\lambda)+\partial_{x}\psi(L,\lambda),

(8)

will be the key quantity arising from the analysis of these operators. It is often known as the
discriminant
of the operator
\mathcal{H}_{L}
. The dispersion relation of the differential eigenvalue problem (
6
) is

\det\left(M_{L}(\lambda)-e^{\mathrm{i}kL}I\right)=0,

(9)

where
k
is the Bloch momentum. Using the fact that
\det(M_{L})=1
, this is equivalent to

2\cos(kL)=\Delta_{L}(\lambda),

(10)

from which it is clear that (
9
) admits a real-valued solution
\lambda(k)
for real
k
if and only if
\Delta_{L}(\lambda)\in[-2,2]
. The function
k\mapsto\lambda(k)
of solutions is many valued and the branches are the
spectral band functions

\lambda_{j}(k)
depicted, for example, in Fig.
1
(a).

The reappearance of the interval
[-2,2]
in the condition
\Delta_{L}(\lambda)\in[-2,2]
for solutions to the dispersion relation (
10
) is not a coincidence. Our objective now is to build a sequence of differential operators whose discriminants satisfy the recursion relation (
1
). Then, the appropriate formula for the dependence of
x_{2}
on
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
will follow from the choice of
V
in (
6
) (and the associated monodromy matrix).

A sequence of differential operators whose discriminants satisfy the recursion relation (
1
) can be built using a
Fibonacci tiling rule
, based on insight from
[
22
]
. In this rule, we will define a sequence of periodic potentials
V_{n}(x)
,
n=0,1,2,\dots
iteratively. Suppose that we have two initial potentials
V_{0}(x)
and
V_{1}(x)
. Then, we can form a new periodic potential whose unit cells is the combination of the unit cells from
V_{0}(x)
and
V_{1}(x)
side by side, as depicted in Fig.
6
. If we repeat the pattern of forming new periodic unit cells by combining the previous two, then we are applying a
Fibonacci tiling rule
. This can, equivalently, be viewed as a two-letter substitution rule given by
A\to AB
and
B\to A

[
16
,
17
]
. When initiated with the starting value of
\mathcal{F}_{1}=A
(or, equivalently,
\mathcal{F}_{0}=B
), this gives the sequence of words shown in Fig.
6
. We use this to construct periodic potentials by assigning the value of
V(x)
on each section of unit size according to the corresponding letter in the tiling rule. The length of each unit cell in the sequence follows the Fibonacci sequence. The link with (
1
) was first discovered by
[
22
]
and is straightforward to show from the observation that monodromy matrices for periodic systems combined in this way are related by
M_{F_{n}}=M_{F_{n-2}}M_{F_{n-1}}
. Rearranging this to give
M_{F_{n-2}}^{-1}=M_{F_{n-1}}M_{F_{n}}^{-1}
and adding it to
M_{F_{n+1}}=M_{F_{n-1}}M_{F_{n}}
gives

M_{F_{n+1}}+M_{F_{n-2}}^{-1}=M_{F_{n-1}}M_{F_{n}}+M_{F_{n-1}}M_{F_{n}}^{-1}.

(11)

Taking the trace and using the fact that
\mathrm{tr}(M_{F_{n-1}}M_{F_{n}})+\mathrm{tr}(M_{F_{n-1}}M_{F_{n}}^{-1})=\mathrm{tr}(M_{F_{n-1}})\mathrm{tr}(M_{F_{n}})
gives (
1
), with
x_{n}=\mathrm{tr}(M_{F_{n}})
. Now that we have constructed a sequence of differential operators that satisfies the dynamical system of interest, we can use their densities of states to predict the statistics.

V
Canonical example

Before computing density of states, we pause to examine some specific examples of dependencies (of
x_{2}
on
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
) that allow the system to be related to a sequence of periodic operators. The formula (
5
) arises by considering the canonical example of constant potentials
V_{0}(x)=A>0
and
V_{1}(x)=B>0
. These are, trivially, 1-periodic and their monodromy matrices are given by

M_{F_{j}}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\omega_{j}&\frac{1}{\omega_{j}}\sin\omega_{j}\\
-\omega_{j}\sin\omega_{j}&\cos\omega_{j}\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{where }j=0,1,

(12)

with
\omega_{0}=\sqrt{B-\lambda}
and
\omega_{1}=\sqrt{A-\lambda}
. Here
F_{0}=1
and
F_{1}=1
are taken as the first two (nonzero) Fibonacci numbers. The associated discriminants are given by

\Delta_{F_{0}}=2\cos\omega_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad\Delta_{F_{1}}=2\cos\omega_{1}.

(13)

Suppose we form a new material which is 2-periodic and alternates between the values
A
and
B
on each unit-sized section (as sketched in Fig.
6
). We can compute the monodromy matrix of this 2-periodic system through the product
M_{F_{2}}=M_{F_{0}}M_{F_{1}}
. Taking the trace of
M_{F_{2}}
gives the discriminant

\Delta_{F_{2}}=2\cos\omega_{0}\cos\omega_{1}-\big(\tfrac{\omega_{0}}{\omega_{1}}+\tfrac{\omega_{1}}{\omega_{0}}\big)\sin\omega_{0}\sin\omega_{1}.

(14)

This is the formula that was specified in (
5
) to fix the dependence of
x_{2}
on
x_{1}
and
x_{0}
. Thus, by choosing this relationship between the initial values, it is guaranteed that
x_{2}=\Delta_{F_{2}}(\lambda)
.

There are many other formulas that would similarly allow for the orbits of (
1
) to be related to a sequence of periodic operators. Indeed, any choice for
V_{0}
and
V_{1}
would yield such a formula (although it may not admit a concise analytic expression). An example that uses sinusoidal potentials to yield a formula in terms of Mathieu functions is derived in Appendix
C
.

VI
Density of states

The density of states for a periodic differential operator is well studied. The density
D(\lambda)
quantifies the number of states per unit energy. For an infinitely periodic system it is typically defined in terms of the limit of finite systems with arbitrarily large size
[
8
]
. At a given value
\lambda
, the density of states is the reciprocal of the slope of the spectral band function at that point:

D(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}k}\lambda_{j}|_{\lambda_{j}=\lambda}\right|^{-1},

(15)

That there can only be at most one point on the dispersion curves where
\lambda_{j}=\lambda
is a property of one-dimensional systems
[
32
]
.

From (
15
), it is apparent that singularities will occur whenever a band function
k\mapsto\lambda_{j}(k)
has zero slope. These are known as
van Hove singularities

[
31
]
. In general, for one-dimensional operators this will happen at the edges of the reduced Brillouin zone. This can be seen by differentiating the dispersion relation (
10
) with respect to
k
to give

-2L\sin(kL)=\frac{\mathrm{d}\Delta_{L}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\frac{\mathrm{d}\lambda}{\mathrm{d}k},

(16)

from which it is clear that
\mathrm{d}\lambda/\mathrm{d}k=0
if and only if
k\in\{0,\pm\pi/L\}
(whenever
\mathrm{d}\Delta_{L}/\mathrm{d}\lambda
is non-zero).

Since the values of the discriminants associated to eigenstates of the periodic operators can be related to the orbits of the recursion relation (
1
), we would like to use similar tools to characterise its statistics. We can use our understanding of the map
\lambda\mapsto\Delta_{L}
to identify the value of the discriminant at each point on one of the spectral band functions
\lambda_{j}
. In light of this, the integrated density of states (IDS)
\nu(\Delta)
associated to the value of the discriminant can be written as

\nu(\Delta)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mu\{k\in\mathcal{B}:\Delta_{L}(\lambda_{j}(k))<\Delta\},

(17)

where
\mu
is the normalised Haar measure
(2\pi)^{-1}\mathrm{d}k
. Since the band functions are monotonic,
\Delta_{L}
is a well-defined function of
k\in\mathcal{B}
and the dependence on
\lambda_{j}
in (
17
) can be dropped. Hence, (
17
) can be simplified to

\nu(\Delta)=\mu\{k\in\mathcal{B}:\Delta_{L}(k)<\Delta\},

(18)

for
-2\leq\Delta\leq 2
.

The density of states can be calculated as the derivative of the IDS (
18
). This gives

D(\Delta)=\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\Delta}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\Delta_{L}}{\mathrm{d}k}\right|^{-1}.

(19)

Differentiating the dispersion relation
\Delta_{L}=2\cos kL
with respect to
k
gives

\frac{\mathrm{d}\Delta_{L}}{\mathrm{d}k}=-2L\sin kL=-L\sqrt{4-4\cos^{2}kL}=-L\sqrt{4-\Delta_{L}^{2}}.

Substituting this into (
19
) and normalising to give a density function on the interval
(-2,2)
gives a formula for the density of states:

D(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{4-x^{2}}},

(20)

for
-2<x<2
. This density function is plotted (dashed line) in Fig.
5
(d) where we see excellent agreement with the limiting statistics of the chaotic dynamical system.

VII
Conclusions

This work showed that constructing a sequence of periodic differential operators associated to a nonlinear recursion relation provides an avenue for deriving an explicit formula for the limiting statistics. For the example studied here, this formula was shown to predict the statistics from numerical experiments accurately. This result also offers an explanation for the clustering of states of the chaotic recursion relation near to the identifiable critical points at
x=\pm 2
, as it illuminates an equivalence with Van Hove singularities in the densities of states of the associated operators.

We envisage that this strategy could be extended to other systems in future work. It was recently shown that a sequence of periodic operators can be constructed in association with the chaotic logistic map
[
33
]
. As a result, the familiar singularities in the well-known invariant measure of that system
[
34
,
35
]
are also equivalent to van Hove singularities. Other straightforward generalisations could also be generated easily. For example, the method could be generalised easily to the nonlinear recursion relations associated to the
generalised
Fibonacci tiling rules
2
2

2

See
[
36
]
for details of the generalised Fibonacci tiling rules,
[
37
,
38
,
39
]
for the basic spectral properties of the associated operators and
[
19
]
for analysis of the escape criteria.
. However, generalising this work to a wider set of nonlinear recursion relation is a challenging open question for future study.

then
|x_{n}|>2
for all
n\geq N
and
|x_{n}|\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

It is easy to see that

\displaystyle|x_{N+3}|

\displaystyle\geq|x_{N+2}||x_{N+1}|-|x_{N}|

\displaystyle\geq|x_{N+2}||x_{N}|-|x_{N}|

\displaystyle=|x_{N+2}|(|x_{N}|-1)+(|x_{N+2}|-|x_{N}|).

By hypothesis,
|x_{N+2}|>|x_{N}|
meaning that
|x_{N+3}|>|x_{N+2}|(|x_{N}|-1),

where
(|x_{N}|-1)>1
. Proceeding by induction, it can be shown that

|x_{N+2+n}|>|x_{N+2}|(|x_{N}|-1)^{n},

(21)

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{>0}
, from which it is clear that
|x_{n}|\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.
∎

Appendix B
Derivation of
f_{X_{3}}

In this appendix, the probability density function
f_{X_{3}}
given in (
3
) is calculated, where
X_{3}=X_{2}X_{1}-X_{0}
with
X_{0}
,
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
being independent and identically distributed random variables, drawn from uniform distributions on the interval
[-2,2]
.

First, the density function of the product
X_{1}X_{2}
can be calculated using elementary properties of conditional probability. In particular, it holds that

f_{X_{2}X_{1}}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{X_{1}}(y)f_{X_{2}}(x/y)\frac{1}{|y|}\,\mathrm{d}y.

(22)

Here,
f_{X_{1}}(y)=1/4
for
|y|\leq 2
and is zero otherwise. Similarly,
f_{X_{2}}(x/y)=1/4
for
|x/y|<2
. Hence, the integrand in (
22
) is non-zero when
|x|/2\leq|y|\leq 2
. Using symmetry of the integrand, this leads to

\displaystyle f_{X_{2}X_{1}}(x)

\displaystyle=2\int_{|x|/2}^{2}\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{y}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\left(\log 2-\log\frac{|x|}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{8}\log\left(\frac{|x|}{4}\right),

(23)

for
-4\leq x\leq 4
and vanishes outside of this interval.

Since
X_{2}X_{1}
and
X_{0}
are independent, the density function of their difference
X_{3}=X_{2}X_{1}-X_{0}
is given by the convolution of
f_{X_{2}X_{1}}
and
f_{X_{0}}
:

\displaystyle f_{X_{3}}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{X_{2}X_{1}}(x-z)f_{X_{0}}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{32}\int_{-2}^{2}\log\left(\frac{|x-z|}{4}\right)\chi_{(-4,4)}(x-z)\,\mathrm{d}z.

If
x,z\in[-2,2]
, then
|x-z|\leq 4
, so
\chi_{(-4,4)}(x-z)=1
. Thus, for
-2\leq x\leq 2
it holds that

f_{X_{3}}(x)=-\frac{1}{32}\int_{-2}^{2}\log\left(\frac{|x-z|}{4}\right)\,\mathrm{d}z.

(24)

Partitioning the integral into
[-2,x]
and
[x,2]
gives

f_{X_{3}}(x)=-\frac{1}{32}\int_{-2}^{x}\log\left(\tfrac{x-z}{4}\right)\,\mathrm{d}z-\frac{1}{32}\int_{x}^{2}\log\left(\tfrac{z-x}{4}\right)\,\mathrm{d}z,

where each of the two integrals can be solved using integration by parts to obtain the formula stated in (
3
):

f_{X_{3}}(x)=\frac{x-2}{32}\log\left(\frac{2-x}{4}\right)-\frac{x+2}{32}\log\left(\frac{x+2}{4}\right)+\frac{1}{8},

for
-2\leq x\leq 2
.

Appendix C
Mathieu functions

Another reasonable choice of potential function is piecewise sinusoidal on each of the two unit cells to be tiled. Consider, for example, the
\pi
-periodic potential

V_{q}(x)=\alpha-2q\cos(2x),

(25)

for
\alpha,q\in\mathbb{R}
. Then the monodromy matrix can be written in terms of the Mathieu functions
C(a,q,x)
and
S(a,q,x)
, which are defined as the even and odd solutions to the Mathieu equation
u^{\prime\prime}(x)+(a-2q\cos(2x))u(x)=0
(see e.g.

[
40
]
for their properties). By the symmetry of the equation, we have that
C^{\prime}(a,q,0)=0
and
S(a,q,0)=0
, so we can choose the normalisation to be such that they are exactly the basis functions:
\phi=C
and
\psi=S
. Then, the monodromy matrix is given by

M_{\pi}^{(q)}(\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}C(\alpha-\lambda,q,\pi)&S(\alpha-\lambda,q,\pi)\\
C^{\prime}(\alpha-\lambda,q,\pi)&S^{\prime}(\alpha-\lambda,q,\pi)\end{pmatrix}\\

(26)

where
C^{\prime}
and
S^{\prime}
denote the derivatives with respect to the position variable
x
.

Given two initial values
x_{0},x_{1}\in[-2,2]
, we need to find
\lambda
,
\alpha
,
q_{0}
and
q_{1}
such that

x_{n}=\mathrm{tr}\left(M_{\pi}^{(q_{n})}(\lambda)\right)=C(\alpha{-}\lambda,q_{n},\pi)+S^{\prime}(\alpha{-}\lambda,q_{n},\pi).

This is always possible, not least since the range of this function is larger than
[-2,2]
. Then, the required formula for
x_{2}
is

\displaystyle x_{2}=\mathrm{tr}\left(M_{\pi}^{(q_{1})}(\lambda)M_{\pi}^{(q_{0})}(\lambda)\right)

(27)

\displaystyle=C(q_{1})C(q_{0}){+}S(q_{1})C^{\prime}(q_{0}){+}C^{\prime}(q_{1})S(q_{0}){+}S^{\prime}(q_{1})S^{\prime}(q_{0}),

where we have written
C(q_{n}):=C(\alpha-\lambda,q_{n},\pi)
, and similarly for
S
, for brevity.

We can apply the Fibonacci tiling rule to two unit cells corresponding to
q=q_{0}
and
q=q_{1}
. Then, the successive transfer matrices will satisfy the properties explored above, meaning that their traces satisfy the recursion relation (
1
) and the density of states converges to the distribution (
20
).

Appendix D
Distribution of
x_{2}

This appendix studies the distribution of the random variable
X_{2}
, given by

X_{2}=2\cos\Omega_{0}\cos\Omega_{1}-\left(\frac{\Omega_{0}}{\Omega_{1}}+\frac{\Omega_{1}}{\Omega_{0}}\right)\sin\Omega_{0}\sin\Omega_{1},

(28)

where
\Omega_{0}
and
\Omega_{1}
are given by

\Omega_{0}=\arccos(X_{0}/2),\quad\Omega_{1}=\arccos(X_{1}/2),

(29)

with
X_{0}
and
X_{1}
being independent and identically distributed random variables, obeying uniform distributions on the interval
[-2,2]
. It will not be possible to derive an explicit formula for the distribution of
X_{2}
, but the features of its distribution (shown in Fig.
7
(e)) can be better understood by examining each term in (
28
).

The first term of (
28
) can be understood easily, since
2\cos\Omega_{0}\cos\Omega_{1}=\frac{1}{2}X_{0}X_{1}
. Thus, it is possible to rescale the density function (
23
) to see that
2\cos\Omega_{0}\cos\Omega_{1}
has probability density function
f_{2\cos\Omega_{0}\cos\Omega_{1}}
given by

f_{2\cos\Omega_{0}\cos\Omega_{1}}(x)=-\frac{1}{4}\log\left(\frac{|x|}{2}\right),

(30)

for
-2\leq x\leq 2
, vanishing outside of this interval. This distribution is shown in Fig.
7
(a), where the histogram shows
10^{6}
independent realisations of
2\cos\Omega_{0}\cos\Omega_{1}
and the dotted line shows the density function (
30
).

Figure 7
:
The distributions of the first few terms of the recursion relation (
1
) (and their key components). In (a), (b) and (c) the analytically computed distributions from (
30
), (
36
) and (
45
), respectively, are shown in dashed lines. In (e), (f) and (g) the critical thresholds
\pm 2
are marked by vertical dashed lines. In each case,
10^{5}
independent realisations of the initial conditions are used.

To understand the second term of (
28
), the first step is to compute the distribution of
\sin\Omega_{0}\sin\Omega_{1}
. If
X_{i}
is uniformly distributed on
[-2,2]
, then
\Omega_{i}=\arccos(X_{i}/2)
has the probability density function

f_{\Omega_{i}}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2}\sin\omega,

(31)

for
0\leq\omega\leq\pi
and vanishes outside of this interval. Subsequently, the random variable
\sin(\Omega_{i})
has the probability density function

f_{\sin(\Omega_{i})}(y)=\tan(\arcsin y)=\frac{y}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}}},

(32)

for
0\leq y\leq 1
, which vanishes outside of this interval. The product
\sin\Omega_{0}\sin\Omega_{1}
can be handled by computing the cumulative distribution function

P(\sin\Omega_{0}\sin\Omega_{1}\leq x)=\int_{0}^{1}P(y\sin\Omega_{0}\leq x)\frac{y}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}y.

If
x/y<1
then
P(\sin\Omega_{0}\leq x/y)=1-\sqrt{1-x^{2}/y^{2}}
. Conversely, if
x/y>1
then
P(\sin\Omega_{0}\leq x/y)=1
. Hence, the integral above can be partitioned into the intervals
[0,x]
and
[x,1]
to see that

P(\sin\Omega_{0}\sin\Omega_{1}\leq x)=1-\int_{x}^{1}\frac{\sqrt{y^{2}-x^{2}}}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}y.

(33)

To compute the density function, this expression should be differentiated with respect to
x
, for which the Leibniz integral rule can be used to see that

f_{\sin\Omega_{0}\sin\Omega_{1}}(x)=\int_{x}^{1}\frac{x}{\sqrt{y^{2}-x^{2}}\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}y.

(34)

This integral can be expressed in terms of the complete elliptic integral of the first kind, which is the function
K:(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
defined as
[
40
]

K(k)=\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-k^{2}\sin^{2}t}}\,\mathrm{d}t.

(35)

Making an appropriate substitution and change of variables in the integral (
34
) gives that

f_{\sin\Omega_{0}\sin\Omega_{1}}(x)=xK(1-x^{2}).

(36)

This distribution is shown in Fig.
7
(b), where the histogram shows
10^{6}
independent realisations of
\sin\Omega_{0}\sin\Omega_{1}
and the dotted line is the the density function (
36
).

The missing piece needed to understand the distribution of
X_{2}
is the density function of
\Omega_{0}/\Omega_{1}+\Omega_{1}/\Omega_{0}
. Given two independent random variables with density given by (
31
), their ratio has distribution

P\left(\frac{\Omega_{0}}{\Omega_{1}}\leq x\right)=\int_{0}^{\pi}P(\Omega_{0}\leq xy)\frac{1}{2}\sin y\,\mathrm{d}y,

(37)

where
x>0
. If
0<\pi/x<y
then
P(\Omega_{0}\leq xy)=1
. Conversely if
0<y<\pi/x
then

P(\Omega_{0}\leq xy)=\int_{0}^{xy}\frac{1}{2}\sin t\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\cos xy.

(38)

If
x<1
then it must be the case that
y<\pi<\pi/x
so it can be calculated directly that

\displaystyle P\left(\frac{\Omega_{0}}{\Omega_{1}}\leq x\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\cos xy\right)\frac{1}{2}\sin y\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1+\cos\pi x}{4(x^{2}-1)},

(39)

which can be differentiated to find that
\Omega_{0}/\Omega_{1}
has probability density function given by

f_{\Omega_{0}/\Omega_{1}}(x)=-\frac{\pi(x^{2}-1)\sin\pi x+2x(1+\cos\pi x)}{4(x^{2}-1)^{2}},

for
0<x<1
. For
x>1
, the integral in (
37
) can be handled by partitioning it into the intervals
[0,\pi/x]
and
[\pi/x,\pi]
:

\displaystyle P\left(\frac{\Omega_{0}}{\Omega_{1}}\leq x\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi/x}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\cos xy\right)\frac{1}{2}\sin y\,\mathrm{d}y

(40)

\displaystyle+\int_{\pi/x}^{\pi}\frac{1}{2}\sin y\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{3+\cos\frac{\pi}{x}}{4}+\frac{1+\cos\frac{\pi}{x}}{4(x^{2}-1)},

which can be differentiated to obtain that

f_{\Omega_{0}/\Omega_{1}}(x)=\frac{\pi(x^{2}-1)\sin\frac{\pi}{x}-2x(1+\cos\frac{\pi}{x})}{4(x^{2}-1)^{2}},

(41)

for
x>1
. Subsequently, the distribution of
\Omega_{0}/\Omega_{1}+\Omega_{1}/\Omega_{0}
can be found by applying the transformation
x\mapsto x+1/x
. This has a global minimum for
x>0
at
x=1
so it holds that

P\left(\frac{\Omega_{0}}{\Omega_{1}}+\frac{\Omega_{1}}{\Omega_{0}}\leq 2\right)=0.

(42)

Meanwhile, for any
x\geq 2
, it holds that

\displaystyle P\left(\frac{\Omega_{0}}{\Omega_{1}}+\frac{\Omega_{1}}{\Omega_{0}}\leq x\right)

\displaystyle=P\left(\frac{x-\sqrt{x^{2}-4}}{2}\leq\frac{\Omega_{0}}{\Omega_{1}}\leq\frac{x+\sqrt{x^{2}-4}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=P\left(\frac{\Omega_{0}}{\Omega_{1}}\leq\frac{x+\sqrt{x^{2}-4}}{2}\right)-P\left(\frac{\Omega_{0}}{\Omega_{1}}\leq\frac{x-\sqrt{x^{2}-4}}{2}\right).

(43)

Using (
39
) and (
40
), this becomes

\begin{split}P\left(\frac{\Omega_{0}}{\Omega_{1}}+\frac{\Omega_{1}}{\Omega_{0}}\leq x\right)&=\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{(x+\sqrt{x^{2}-4})^{2}-4}\left(1+\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{x+\sqrt{x^{2}-4}}\right)\right)\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{4}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{x+\sqrt{x^{2}-4}}\right)-\frac{1}{(x-\sqrt{x^{2}-4})^{2}-4}\left(1+\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}(x-\sqrt{x^{2}-4})\right)\right)\end{split}

(44)

which can be differentiated to obtain the probability density function

\begin{split}f_{\Omega_{0}/\Omega_{1}+\Omega_{1}/\Omega_{0}}(x)&=\frac{1}{4(x^{2}-4)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\bigg[-4-2\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}(x-\sqrt{x^{2}-4})\right)\\
&\quad-2\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{x+\sqrt{x^{2}-4}}\right)+\pi\sqrt{x^{2}-4}\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}(x-\sqrt{x^{2}-4})\right)\\
&\qquad+\pi\sqrt{x^{2}-4}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{x+\sqrt{x^{2}-4}}\right)\bigg].\end{split}

(45)

The distribution (
45
) is shown with the dashed line in Fig.
7
(c), where it is compared to a histogram showing
10^{6}
independent realisations of
\Omega_{0}/\Omega_{1}+\Omega_{1}/\Omega_{0}
.

The distribution of
X_{2}
could now be obtained by a similar approach, however it is not reasonable to expect that explicit analytic expressions will be possible. Nevertheless, the density functions calculated above are already sufficient to yield some intuition on the qualitative properties of the distribution of
X_{2}
, which is shown in Fig.
7
(e).

First, consider the distribution of
(\Omega_{0}/\Omega_{1}+\Omega_{1}/\Omega_{0})\sin\Omega_{0}\sin\Omega_{1}
. This variable must fall in the interval
[0,2]
since it holds that
0\leq(x/y+y/x)\sin x\sin y\leq 2
for
0\leq x,y\leq\pi
. Further, this function attains its maximal value of 2 when
x=y=\pi/2
. Both
\Omega_{0}
and
\Omega_{1}
are drawn from distributions with sinusoidal density functions (
31
) which attain maxima at
\pi/2
. This explains why values tend to cluster around the upper end of the interval
[0,2]
, as shown in Fig.
7
(d). The sharpness of this peak can be understood by examining the sharp peaks in the densities of
\sin\Omega_{i}
(
32
) and
\Omega_{0}/\Omega_{1}+\Omega_{1}/\Omega_{0}
(
45
): the values of
\sin\Omega_{0}
and
\sin\Omega_{1}
are strongly clustered around 1 and the values of
\Omega_{0}/\Omega_{1}+\Omega_{1}/\Omega_{0}
are strongly clustered around 2, leading to a peak in the density of their product close to 2.

The distribution of
X_{2}
, shown in Fig.
7
(e), is the difference between the distributions of
2\cos\Omega_{0}\cos\Omega_{1}
in Fig.
7
(a) and of
(\Omega_{0}/\Omega_{1}+\Omega_{1}/\Omega_{0})\sin\Omega_{0}\sin\Omega_{1}
in Fig.
7
(d). In this case, there is strong clustering of values around -2. This can be understood from the fact that the density function of
2\cos\Omega_{0}\cos\Omega_{1}
in (
30
) has a singularity at 0 and the distribution of
(\Omega_{0}/\Omega_{1}+\Omega_{1}/\Omega_{0})\sin\Omega_{0}\sin\Omega_{1}
in (
45
) shows clustering around 2. This clustering of values of
X_{2}
around -2 is the first hint of the van Hove singularities studied in this work emerging. Examining the distributions of
X_{3}
and
X_{4}
in Fig.
7
(f) and Fig.
7
(g), the singularities around
\pm 2
are increasingly clear. As shown in Fig.
5
, as
n
becomes large the distribution of
X_{n}
confined to the interval
[-2,2]
loses its asymmetry and the clustering of values around
\pm 2
converges to singularities in the density of states.
