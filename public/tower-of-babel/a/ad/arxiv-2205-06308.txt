An Equivalence Principle for the Spectrum of Random Inner-Product Kernel Matrices with Polynomial Scalings
Yue M. Lu and Horng-Tzer Yau

Abstract
We investigate random matrices whose entries are obtained by applying a nonlinear kernel function to pairwise inner products between n n independent data vectors, drawn uniformly from the unit sphere in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} . This study is motivated by applications in machine learning and statistics, where these kernel random matrices and their spectral properties play significant roles. We establish the weak limit of the empirical spectral distribution of these matrices in a polynomial scaling regime, where d , n → ∞ d,n\to\infty such that n / d ℓ → n/d^{\ell}\to\ratio , for some fixed ℓ ∈ ℕ \ell\in\mathbb{N} and ∈ ( 0 , ∞ ) \ratio\in(0,\infty) . Our findings generalize an earlier result by Cheng and Singer, who examined the same model in the linear scaling regime (with ℓ = 1 \ell=1 ). Our work reveals an equivalence principle: the spectrum of the random kernel matrix is asymptotically equivalent to that of a simpler matrix model, constructed as a linear combination of a (shifted) Wishart matrix and an independent matrix sampled from the Gaussian orthogonal ensemble. The aspect ratio of the Wishart matrix and the coefficients of the linear combination are determined by ℓ \ell and the expansion of the kernel function in the orthogonal Hermite polynomial basis. Consequently, the limiting spectrum of the random kernel matrix can be characterized as the free additive convolution between a Marchenko-Pastur law and a semicircle law. We also extend our results to cases with data vectors sampled from isotropic Gaussian distributions instead of spherical distributions.



1
Introduction

Let
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a set of independent data vectors drawn uniformly from the unit sphere
\mathcal{S}^{(d-1)}
. Consider a random matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with entries

A_{ij}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\,f_{d}(\sqrt{d}\,\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}_{j})&\text{if }i\neq j\\
0&\text{if }i=j\end{cases},

(1.1)

where
f_{d}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is a (nonlinear) “kernel” function. Its empirical spectral distribution (ESD) is denoted by

\varrho_{A}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\lambda_{i}}(x)

(1.2)

where
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
are the eigenvalues of
A
, and
\delta_{\lambda}
denotes the counting measure. In this work, we establish the weak-limit of the ESD
\varrho_{A}
when
d,n\to\infty
such that
n/d^{\ell}\to\ratio\in(0,\infty)
, for some fixed
\ratio\in\mathbb{R}
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
.

Our study of this model is motivated by recent problems in machine learning, statistics, and signal processing, where random matrices like (
1.1
) and their spectral properties play crucial roles. Examples include kernel methods (such as kernel-PCA
[
37
]
and kernel-SVM
[
36
]
), covariance thresholding procedures
[
5
,
12
]
, nonlinear dimension reduction
[
3
]
, and probabilistic matrix factorization
[
32
]
. Moreover, the closely-related non-Hermitian version of (
1.1
), where
A_{ij}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}f_{d}(\sqrt{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\boldsymbol{y}_{j})
for two sets of vectors
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\}_{i\leq n}
and
\{\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\}_{j\leq p}
, appears in the random feature model
[
35
,
28
,
22
,
34
]
, an interesting theoretical model for large random neural networks.

In this work, we examine a general high-dimensional polynomial scaling regime, where the number of samples
n
can grow to infinity proportional to
d^{\ell}
, for some positive integer
\ell
. The linear regime, with
\ell=1
, was previously studied by
Cheng and Singer 2013
, who showed that the ESD of
A
converges to a deterministic limit distribution, with its Stieltjes transform characterized as the solution to a cubic equation (see Section
3
for further discussions on related work in the literature). Interestingly,
Fan and Montanari 2017
observed that the limiting ESD of
A
(as determined by the aforementioned cubic equation) is equivalent to that of a simpler matrix model, which consists of the linear combination of a (shifted) Wishart matrix and an independent matrix sampled from the Gaussian orthogonal ensemble (GOE). This observation leads to an intriguing interpretation: the
nonlinear
kernel
f_{d}
is asymptotically equivalent to a
linear
and
noisy
transformation.

The main contribution of this work is to demonstrate that the aforementioned characterization, derived under the linear scaling regime, represents a special case of a more general
equivalence principle
. This principle holds when
n/d^{\ell}\to\ratio\in(0,\infty)
for any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and under mild conditions on the kernel
f_{d}
. Specifically, we establish in Theorem
2
that the ESD of
A
is asymptotically equivalent to that of

B=\frac{\mu_{\ell}}{\sqrt{nN_{\ell}}}(W^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}W-N_{\ell}I)+\gamma_{\ell}H

where
W\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\ell}\times n}
is an i.i.d. standard Gaussian matrix with aspect ratio
N_{\ell}/n\to 1/(\ratio\ell!)
, and
H
is a GOE matrix independent of
W
. Both constants
\mu_{\ell}
and
\gamma_{\ell}
depend on
\ell
and can be determined by expanding
f_{d}
in the orthogonal Hermite polynomial basis. As a direct consequence of this equivalence principle, the limiting ESD of
A
can be characterized simply as a free additive convolution between a (shifted) Marchenko-Pastur (MP) law and a semicircle law.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we begin by examining the special case of polynomial kernel functions, with the equivalence principle formalized in Theorem
1
and a heuristic explanation provided in Section
2.2
. We address the case of more general nonlinear functions in Section
2.3
, stating the corresponding asymptotic characterizations in Theorem
2
. Section
2.4
presents several numerical experiments to illustrate our theory, while Section
3
discusses related work in the literature. We dedicate Section
4
to the proof of Theorem
1
and gather auxiliary results in the appendix. In Section
5
, we extend our findings to cases where data vectors are sampled from the isotropic Gaussian distribution rather than the spherical distribution. Finally, we conclude the paper in Section
6
, discussing potential extensions of our results and open problems.

1.1
Notation

Before delving into the technical details, we first establish some notations employed throughout this paper.

Common sets
:
\mathbb{N}
represents the set of positive integers, and
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\left\{0\right\}
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
[n]\coloneqq\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}
, and
(n)_{k}\coloneqq n(n-1)\ldots(n-k+1)
denotes the falling factorial. For
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathds{1}_{ij}
represents the Kronecker delta function,
i.e.
,
\mathds{1}_{ij}=1
if
i=j
and
\mathds{1}_{ij}=0
otherwise. The data vectors’ dimension is denoted by
d
. The unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is expressed as
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
. Throughout the paper, we consistently use
z
to represent a complex number in the upper-half plane
\mathbb{C}_{+}
, and we define

\eta\coloneqq\mathfrak{Im}(z)>0.

Additionally, we assume that
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathfrak{Re}(z)\right\rvert}\leq\tau^{-1}
and
\tau\leq\eta\leq\tau^{-1}
, for some global constant
\tau>0
.

Probability distributions
:
\mathsf{Unif}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})
denotes the uniform probability measure on
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
. For two independent vectors
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})
, we define

\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}\coloneqq\mathsf{Law}(\sqrt{d}\,\boldsymbol{x}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{y}).

(1.3)

Due to the rotational symmetry of
\mathsf{Unif}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})
, we also have
\sqrt{d}\,\boldsymbol{x}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\sim\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}
, where
\boldsymbol{e}_{1}=(1,0,\ldots,0)
. Given a random variable
X
, its
L^{p}
norm is denoted by
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert X\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}\coloneqq(\mathbb{E}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert X\right\rvert}^{p})^{1/p}
. We say a symmetric random matrix is drawn from the GOE if its law is equivalent to that of
(F+F^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})/\sqrt{2n}
, where
F
is an
n\times n
matrix with i.i.d. standard normal entries.

Stochastic order notation
: In our proof, we utilize a concept of high-probability bounds known as
stochastic domination
. This notion, first introduced in
[
17
,
16
]
, provides a convenient way to account for low-probability exceptional events where some bounds may not hold. Consider two families of nonnegative random variables:

X=\big(X^{(d)}(u)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}d\in\mathbb{N},u\in U^{(d)}\big),\quad Y=\big(Y^{(d)}(u)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}d\in\mathbb{N},u\in U^{(d)}\big),

where
U^{(d)}
is a possibly
d
-dependent parameter set. We say that
X
is
stochastically dominated
by
Y
, uniformly in
u
, if for every (small)
\varepsilon>0
and (large)
D>0
we have

\sup_{u\in U^{(d)}}\mathbb{P}[X^{(d)}(u)>d^{\varepsilon}Y^{(d)}(u)]\leq d^{-D}

for sufficiently large
d\geq d_{0}(\varepsilon,D)
. If
X
is stochastically dominated by
Y
, uniformly in
u
, we use the notation
X\prec Y
. Moreover, if for some complex family
X
we have
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert X\right\rvert}\prec Y
, we also write
X=\mathcal{O}_{\prec}(Y)
. This stochastic order notation should not be mistaken for the conventional big
\mathcal{O}
notation, which we will also use in this paper: for two
deterministic
sequences
X^{(d)}
and
Y^{(d)}
, we write
X=\mathcal{O}_{\alpha}(Y)
, with some parameter
\alpha
, if
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert X\right\rvert}\leq C(\alpha)Y
for all sufficiently large
d
. Here, the constant
C(\alpha)
may depend on
\alpha
.

Vectors and matrices:
For a vector
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, its
\ell_{2}
norm is denoted by
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert}
. For a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}
and
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{F}}
denote the operator (spectral) norm and the Frobenius norm of
A
, respectively. Additionally,
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A\right\rVert}_{\infty}\coloneqq\max_{i,j\in[n]}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert A(i,j)\right\rvert}
denotes the entry-wise
\ell_{\infty}
norm. We use
\boldsymbol{1}
to denote
(1,1,\ldots,1)
, and
I
is an identity matrix. Their dimensions can be inferred from the context. The trace of
A
is written as
\tr(A)
. Lastly, for an
n\times n
Hermitian matrix
A
, the Stieltjes transform of its empirical spectral distribution is
s_{A}(z)\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\tr(A-zI)^{-1}
. Here, the matrix
(A-zI)^{-1}
is the resolvent of
A
.

2
An Asymptotic Equivalence Principle

2.1
Polynomial Kernels

We begin by examining the special case when the kernel function
f_{d}
in (
1.1
) is a degree-
L
polynomial, for some
L\in\mathbb{N}
. (The case of more general nonlinear functions is addressed in Section
2.3
.) For reasons that will be clarified later (see Section
2.2
), we expand
f_{d}
in the following form:

f_{d}(x)=\sum_{0\leq k\leq L}\mu_{k}q_{k}^{(d)}(x)

(2.1)

where
\left\{\mu_{k}\right\}_{k}
is a set of expansion coefficients, and
q_{k}^{(d)}(x)
denotes the
k
th Gegenbauer polynomial
[
11
,
14
]
. The Gegenbauer polynomials, also known as the ultraspherical polynomials in the literature
[
24
]
, form a set of orthogonal polynomial basis with respect to the probability measure
\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}
defined in (
1.3
). Specifically, we have
\mathsf{deg}\,q_{k}^{(d)}(x)=k
and

\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}}\big[q_{i}^{(d)}(\xi)q_{j}^{(d)}(\xi)\big]=\mathds{1}_{ij},

(2.2)

where
\mathds{1}_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta.

The coefficients of these polynomials can be determined by performing the Gram-Schmidt procedure on the monomial basis
\left\{0,1,x,x^{2},\ldots\right\}
and by using the explicit formula for the moments of
\xi
[see (
D.5
)]. The first few polynomials in the sequence are

q_{0}^{(d)}(x)=1,\qquad q_{1}^{(d)}(x)=x,\qquad q_{2}^{(d)}(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\frac{d+2}{d-1}}(x^{2}-1),

(2.3)

and

\qquad q_{3}^{(d)}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\sqrt{\frac{d+4}{d-1}}\left(\frac{d+2}{d}x^{3}-3x\right).

(2.4)

Note that the coefficients of the Gegenbauer polynomials depend on the dimension
d
. To simplify the notation, we will suppress this dependence by writing throughout the paper

q_{k}(x)\coloneqq q_{k}^{(d)}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{q}_{k}(x)\coloneqq q_{k}^{(d-1)}(x).

(2.5)

In Appendix
A
, we compile a list of properties of Gegenbauer polynomials that will be used in our analysis.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, define a matrix
A_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that

(A_{k})_{ij}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\,q_{k}(\sqrt{d}\,\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}_{j})&\text{if }i\neq j\\
0&\text{if }i=j\end{cases},

(2.6)

where
\left\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right\}_{i\in[n]}
is a collection of independent vectors drawn from
\mathsf{Unif}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})
. Considering (
2.1
), we can express the inner-product kernel matrix in (
1.1
) as

A=\sum_{k=0}^{L}\mu_{k}A_{k}=\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1}+\sum_{k=\ell}^{L}\Big)\mu_{k}A_{k},

(2.7)

where
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
\ell\leq L
. The main result of this paper is to establish an asymptotic
equivalence principle
for the empirical spectral distribution (ESD) of
A
: when
n=\ratio d^{\ell}+o(d^{\ell})
for some
\ratio>0
, the ESD of
A
is asymptotically equivalent to that of a matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, defined as

B\coloneqq\sum_{k=\ell}^{L}\mu_{k}B_{k}.

(2.8)

The first component in the sum in (
2.8
) is a (shifted) Wishart matrix, constructed as follows:

B_{\ell}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{nN_{\ell}}}W^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}W-\sqrt{\frac{N_{\ell}}{n}}I,

(2.9)

where
W\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\ell}\times n}
is an i.i.d. Gaussian matrix with
N_{\ell}\coloneqq d^{\ell}/\ell!+o(d^{\ell})
. For each
k>\ell
,
B_{k}
is an
n\times n
GOE matrix. Furthermore,
B_{\ell},B_{\ell+1},\ldots,B_{L}
are jointly
independent
.

For
z\in C_{+}
, let
s_{A}(z)
and
s_{B}(z)
denote the Stieltjes transforms of the ESDs of
A
and
B
, respectively. The following theorem formalizes the asymptotic equivalence of the matrix models
A
and
B
.

Theorem 1
.

Fix
\ratio,\tau>0
, two positive integers
\ell\leq L
, and a set of coefficients
\left\{\mu_{k}\right\}_{0\leq k\leq L}
. Suppose that
n/d^{\ell}=\ratio+\mathcal{O}(d^{-1/2})
, and
z=E+\mathrm{i}\eta
with
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert E\right\rvert}\leq\tau^{-1}
and
\tau\leq\eta\leq\tau^{-1}
. Then, almost surely as
d\to\infty
, we have
s_{A}(z)\to m(z)
and
s_{B}(z)\to m(z)
, where
m(z)
is the unique solution in
C_{+}
to the equation:

m(z)\Big(z+\frac{\gamma_{a}m(z)}{1+\gamma_{b}m(z)}+\gamma_{c}m(z)\Big)+1=0.

(2.10)

Here,
\gamma_{a}\coloneqq\mu_{\ell}^{2}
,
\gamma_{b}\coloneqq\mu_{\ell}\sqrt{\ell!\ratio}
, and
\gamma_{c}\coloneqq\sum_{\ell+1\leq k\leq L}\mu_{k}^{2}
. Moreover, for every
\varepsilon>0
and
D>0
, we have

\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s_{A}(z)-m(z)\right\rvert}\geq d^{-(1/2-\varepsilon)}\Big)\leq d^{-D}

(2.11)

for all sufficiently large
d
.

Remark 1
.

As
B
is the linear combination of a (shifted) Wishart matrix and independent GOE matrices, its limiting eigenvalue density is given by an additive free convolution of a Marchenko-Pastur law with a semicircle law. Explicit formulas for the limiting density function

\varrho_{\mathrm{fc}}(x)\coloneqq\lim_{\eta\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{\pi}\mathfrak{Im}[m(x+i\eta)]

(2.12)

can be found in
[
9
, Appendix A]
. As a consequence of Theorem
1
, the spectral distribution of
A
converges weakly almost surely to the same limit
\rho_{\mathrm{fc}}(E)
as
d\to\infty
.

Remark 2
.

It is important to note that the components of
A
associated with indices
k<\ell
do not play any role in the equivalent model given by
B
in (
2.8
). This is due to the fact that the “low-order” components
\left\{A_{k}\right\}_{k<\ell}
are essentially low-rank matrices with ranks on the order of
\mathcal{O}(d^{\ell-1})
. As a result, they do not contribute to the limiting spectrum density of the
n\times n
matrix when
n\asymp d^{\ell}
. For a more detailed explanation, see Lemma
2
.

2.2
A Heuristic Explanation of the Equivalence Principle

The equivalence principle stated above has a simple heuristic explanation. To understand this, let us first revisit a crucial property of Gegenbauer polynomials. Given any
\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
,

q_{k}(\sqrt{d}\,\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}_{j})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}\sum_{a\in[N_{k}]}Y_{k,a}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})Y_{k,a}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}),

(2.13)

where
\left\{Y_{k,a}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\right\}_{a\in[N_{k}]}
represents a collection of orthonormal degree-
k
spherical harmonics associated with
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
, and

N_{k}=\frac{d^{k}}{k!}(1+\mathcal{O}(d^{-1}))

(2.14)

denotes the cardinality of the set. For a comprehensive explanation and the exact expression for
N_{k}
, we refer the reader to Appendix
A
. The above identity is valuable because it allows us to “linearize” the term
q_{k}(\sqrt{d}\,\boldsymbol{x}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{y})
, transforming it into an inner product of two
N_{k}
-dimensional vectors comprised of spherical harmonics.

Using (
2.13
) and another identity,
q_{k}(\sqrt{d})=\sqrt{N_{k}}
, we can express the matrices
\left\{A_{k}\right\}
in a factorized form:

A_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{nN_{k}}}S_{k}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}S_{k}-\sqrt{\frac{N_{k}}{n}}\,I,

(2.15)

where
S_{k}
is an
N_{k}\times n
matrix with entries consisting of the spherical harmonics, that is,

(S_{k})_{ai}=Y_{k,a}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}),\qquad\text{ for }a\in[N_{k}],i\in[n]

(2.16)

and
\left\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right\}_{i\in[n]}
are the data vectors in the definition given by (
2.6
). By design, the
i
th column of
S_{k}
depends solely on
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
. Consequently, due to the independence of
\left\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right\}_{i}
, the columns of
S_{k}
are entirely independent. While the entries within each column are indeed dependent, the orthonormality of the spherical harmonics (refer to (
A.4
) in Appendix
A
) confirms that these entries are uncorrelated random variables with unit variances. Thus, the columns of
S_{k}
are independent and isotropic random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{N_{k}}
.

Heuristically, if we replace the entries of
S_{k}
with i.i.d. standard Gaussians, we can expect the limiting ESD of
A_{k}
to be characterized by the Marchenko-Pastur (MP) law with an aspect ratio parameter

\phi_{k}\coloneqq N_{k}/n.

(2.17)

For
k=\ell
, this intuition underlies the equivalent model
B_{\ell}
constructed in (
2.9
). (Refer to Appendix
C
for a review of the relevant properties of the MP law.) Note that, since we set the diagonal entries of
A_{k}
to zero, there is an additional shift (by
\sqrt{\phi_{k}}
) of the eigenvalues in (
2.9
).

According to the heuristic arguments above, the limiting ESD of
A_{k}
for every
k
should be characterized by the MP law. However, in our equivalent model given by
B
, the higher order components,
i.e.
,
B_{k}
with
k>\ell
, are associated with the semicircle law. This discrepancy can be explained by examining the density function associated with the (shifted) MP law. By setting
\phi=\phi_{k}
and
t=1/\sqrt{\phi_{k}}
in (
C.6
), we obtain

\varrho_{k}(x)\dif x\coloneqq\frac{\sqrt{\big[(2+x-1/\sqrt{\phi_{k}}\,)(2-x+1/\sqrt{\phi_{k}}\,)\big]_{+}}}{2\pi(1+x/\sqrt{\phi_{k}})}\,\dif x+(1-\phi_{k})_{+}\,\delta(x+\sqrt{\phi_{k}})\dif x.

(2.18)

This density function is entirely determined by the aspect ratio parameter
\phi_{k}
defined in (
2.17
). Recall the expression for
N_{k}
in (
2.14
) and that
n=\ratio d^{\ell}+o(d^{\ell})
. For
k=\ell
, we have
\phi_{k}\to(\ell!\ratio)^{-1}
as
d\to\infty
. In this case, (
2.18
) corresponds to an MP law with an aspect ratio of size
\mathcal{O}(1)
. However, for
k>\ell
, we have
\phi_{k}\asymp d^{k-\ell}
, and thus
1/\sqrt{\phi_{k}}=\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{d})
. In this situation, the MP law in (
2.18
) degenerates to the standard semicircle law

\varrho_{\mathrm{sc}}(x)\dif x\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{(4-x^{2})_{+}}\;\dif x.

(2.19)

Theorem
1
rigorously establishes the heuristic arguments mentioned above. In addition, it shows that, despite the apparent statistical dependence among
\left\{A_{k}\right\}_{k\geq\ell}
, they can be treated as a collection of asymptotically independent matrices. As a result, the self-consistent equation in (
2.10
) corresponds to the additive free convolution of an MP law with a semicircle law. To heuristically derive (
2.10
), we can employ (
2.15
) and (
2.16
) to express:

A_{ij}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{0\leq k\leq L}t_{k}\sum_{a\in[N_{k}]}Y_{k,a}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})Y_{k,a}(\boldsymbol{y}_{j})-\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{0\leq k\leq L}t_{k}N_{k}\Big)I,\qquad t_{k}\coloneqq\frac{\mu_{k}\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}.

(2.20)

Notice that this expression has the form of the matrix presented in (
C.3
) (in Appendix
C
). By further assuming that the family
{Y_{k,a}(\boldsymbol{x})}
consists of not just uncorrelated but indeed independent random variables,
A
adheres to the MP law in (
C.4
), meaning its limiting Stieltjes transform, denoted by
m(z)
, satisfies the equation:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}

\displaystyle=-z-\frac{1}{n}\Big[\sum_{k<\ell}+\sum_{k=\ell}+\sum_{k>\ell}\Big]\frac{N_{k}t_{k}^{2}m}{1+t_{k}m}

\displaystyle=-z-\Big[\sum_{k<\ell}+\sum_{k=\ell}+\sum_{k>\ell}\Big]\frac{\mu_{k}^{2}m}{1+\mu_{k}\sqrt{n/N_{k}}m}

\displaystyle=-z-\frac{\mu_{\ell}^{2}m}{1+\mu_{\ell}\sqrt{\ell!\ratio}\,m}-\Big(\sum_{k>\ell}\mu_{k}^{2}\Big)m+\mathcal{O}(d^{-1/2}),

(2.21)

where in the last step we have used the fact that
n/N_{\ell}\to\ratio\ell!
,
n/N_{k}\to\infty
for
k<\ell
and
n/N_{k}\to 0
for
k>\ell
. Observe that, after dropping the
\mathcal{O}(d^{-1/2})
error term, (
2.21
) is exactly the self-consistent equation in (
2.10
).

2.3
General Nonlinear Kernels

In this subsection, we extend the equivalence principle established in Theorem
1
to encompass cases where the kernel
f_{d}
in (
1.1
) is a general function, going beyond merely polynomials.

As our analysis will involve the expansion of
f_{d}
using normalized Hermite polynomials, we first revisit the definition of these orthogonal polynomials: For
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the
k
th Hermite polynomial, denoted by
h_{k}(x)
, has degree
k
. Furthermore, for

3
Related Work

In this section, we discuss several related lines of work in the literature.

Random kernel matrices
: Among the earlier studies on the spectrum of random kernel matrices,
Koltchinskii and Giné 2000
considered a setting where the data vectors
\left\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right\}
are sampled from a fixed manifold in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. They demonstrated that, when
n\to\infty
with
d
fixed, the spectrum of the kernel matrix converges to that of an integral operator on the manifold. The high-dimensional setting, where
n,d\to\infty
with
n/d\to\ratio\in(0,\infty)
, was first investigated by
El Karoui 2010
. This work examined a scaling in which the matrix elements
A_{ij}
are
f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
. When the data vectors
\left\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right\}
are independently sampled from
\mathsf{Unif}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})
, the typical size of
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
is
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{d})
for
i\neq j
. As a result, the kernel matrix only depends on the properties of the kernel function
f(x)
in a local neighborhood near
x=0
. Indeed,
El Karoui 2010
showed that, under mild local smoothness assumptions on
f(x)
, the kernel matrix is asymptotically equivalent to a simpler matrix obtained via a first-order Taylor expansion. Our work, as well as that of
[
9
]
, considers a different scaling: the presence of the
\sqrt{d}
factor in (
1.1
) means that the kernel function
f_{d}
is applied to values of size
\mathcal{O}(1)
rather than
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{d})
. Consequently, the spectrum of the matrix in (
1.1
) depends on the global properties of the kernel function.

The linear asymptotic regime of the model in (
1.1
) was first investigated by
Cheng and Singer 2013
, who established the weak limit of the ESD of the kernel matrix. The idea of “decorrelation” by expanding the nonlinear kernel function using an orthogonal polynomial basis was first introduced in that work and plays a significant role in our paper. The results of
[
9
]
were obtained for data vectors sampled from isotropic Gaussian and spherical distributions.
Do and Vu 2013
extended these results to other distributions (such as the Bernoulli case). The spectral norm of the kernel matrix was explored by
Fan and Montanari 2017
, who also made the observation that the limiting distribution obtained in
[
9
]
is the free additive convolution of an MP law and a semicircle law.

Asymptotics with polynomial scaling
: Studies of high-dimensional statistical problems often focus on the linear asymptotic regime. In random matrix theory, the more general polynomial asymptotic regime was investigated by
Bloemendal et al. 2015
, who demonstrated the local MP law for sample covariance matrices
X^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}X
, where
X
is an
N\times n
matrix with
\log N\asymp\log n
. In that work, the matrix
X
is assumed to have independent entries. Although the kernel matrix in our work can also be written in the form of a (generalized) sample covariance matrix [see (
2.15
) and (
2.20
)], the matrix entries in our problem consist of spherical harmonics, which are uncorrelated but dependent random variables. As a result, the technical approach of
[
7
]
cannot be directly applied here. In the context of kernel methods, exact asymptotics in the polynomial scaling regime were examined in
Opper and Urbanczik 2001
and
Bordelon et al. 2021
using nonrigorous statistical physics methods. The heuristics employed by these authors were similar to the scheme outlined in Section
2.2
, specifically, treating the uncorrelated spherical harmonics as if they were independent standard normal random variables.

Ghorbani et al. 2020
explored the high-dimensional limit of kernel regression and several related models under a scaling where
d^{\ell+\delta}\leq n\leq d^{\ell+1-\delta}
for some (small)
\delta>0
. In this context, the number of samples is assumed to fall between two consecutive integer powers of
d
. This contrasts with our work, in which we assume
n/d^{\ell}\to\kappa\in(0,\infty)
. Upon completing this paper, we became aware of an independent work by
Misiakiewicz 2022
that examines the limit of kernel ridge regression in the exact polynomial asymptotic regime. One of the main results of that study shows that the limiting spectrum of the kernel matrix follows the MP law when the kernel function
f_{d}
is the
\ell
th Gegenbauer polynomial. This finding corresponds to a specific case in our equivalence principle, where the matrix
A
in (
2.7
) comprises solely the component
\mu_{\ell}A_{\ell}
. In our model, the inclusion of high-order components
\left\{A_{k}\right\}_{k>\ell}
leads to more general limiting distributions (the free convolution between the MP law and the semicircle law) and additional technical challenges in the proof.

Non-Hermitian ensembles and random feature models
: Lastly, we note that it is possible to extend the current study to a non-Hermitian version of (
1.1
). In this case, one would investigate an
n\times p
matrix, with entries given by

A_{ij}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}f_{d}(\sqrt{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\boldsymbol{y}_{j}),

where
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\}_{i\leq n}
and
\{\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\}_{j\leq p}
represent two collections of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This type of matrix appears in the random feature model
[
35
,
28
,
22
,
34
]
, an interesting theoretical model for large random neural networks. For such non-Hermitian matrices, an asymptotic Gaussian equivalence phenomenon, analogous to the equivalence principle obtained in this study, have been demonstrated under the linear scaling regime, when
n,d
and
p
grow to infinity at fixed ratios
[
30
,
19
,
21
,
29
,
23
]
. We conjecture that similar equivalence principles may exist under the polynomial scaling regime.

(4.5)

Observe that the left-hand side of (
4.4
) has exactly the same functional form as the left-hand side of (
2.10
). The error estimate in (
4.5
) then implies that the Stieltjes transform
s(z)
approximately satisfies the nonlinear equation in (
2.10
). By analyzing the uniqueness and stability of the solution
m(z)
to this nonlinear equation (see Proposition
9
in Appendix
G
), we can then conclude that
s(z)\approx m(z)
.

4.2
Schur Complement: Reparameterization

We devote this and the next subsections to establishing the estimate in (
4.3
). Observe that, by construction, different coordinates of
A
are statistically exchangeable. Thus, we only need to show (
4.3
) for a single index
i\in[n]
. We choose to do so for
i=n
.

Recall from (
2.7
) and (
2.6
) that

A_{na}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{\ell\leq k\leq L}\mu_{k}q_{k}(\sqrt{d}\,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}_{a}),

(4.6)

and the entries of the minor
A^{[n]}
are of the form

A^{[n]}_{ab}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{\ell\leq k\leq L}\mu_{k}q_{k}(\sqrt{d}\,\boldsymbol{x}_{a}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}_{b})\mathds{1}_{a\neq b},\qquad\text{for }a,b\in[n-1],

(4.7)

where
\mathds{1}_{a\neq b}\coloneqq 1-\mathds{1}_{ab}
. As a reminder to the reader: due to Lemma
2
, we assume that
A
contains no lower-order components,
i.e.
,
\mu_{k}=0
for
0\leq k<\ell
.

The random variables
\{A_{na}\}
and
\{A^{[n]}_{ab}\}
are weakly correlated. To make this correlation explicit, we use the following reparameterization. For every
a\in[n-1]
, let

\xi_{a}\coloneqq\sqrt{d}\,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}_{a}\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{a}\coloneqq(1-\xi_{a}^{2}/d)^{-1/2}R_{n}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}_{a},

(4.8)

where
R_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(d-1)}
is a matrix whose columns are unit-norm vectors orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
. We can now rewrite (
4.6
) as

A_{na}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{\ell\leq k\leq L}\mu_{k}q_{k}(\xi_{a}).

(4.9)

By definition,
(R_{n}R_{n}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}+\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\boldsymbol{x}_{n}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})=I
, and hence
\boldsymbol{x}_{a}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}_{b}=\boldsymbol{x}_{a}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(R_{n}R_{n}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}+\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\boldsymbol{x}_{n}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})\boldsymbol{x}_{b}
for
a,b\in[n-1]
. We can then verify that

\sqrt{d}\,\boldsymbol{x}_{a}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}_{b}=r(\xi_{a})r(\xi_{b})\sqrt{d-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{a}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{b}+\xi_{a}\xi_{b}/\sqrt{d}
, where

r(\xi)\coloneqq(1-1/d)^{-1/4}(1-\xi^{2}/d)^{1/2}.

(4.10)

This allows us to rewrite (
4.7
) as

A^{[n]}_{ab}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{\ell\leq k\leq L}\mu_{k}q_{k}\Big(r(\xi_{a})r(\xi_{b})\sqrt{d-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{a}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{b}+\xi_{a}\xi_{b}/\sqrt{d}\Big)\mathds{1}_{a\neq b}.

(4.11)

The advantage of the new parameterizations in (
4.9
) and (
4.11
) is that the families
\left\{\xi_{a}\right\}
and
\left\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{a}\right\}
are independent, as we show below.

Lemma 3
.

\left\{\xi_{a}\right\}_{a<n}
is an i.i.d. family with
\xi_{a}\sim\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}
, where
\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}
is the probability measure defined in (
1.3
).
\left\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{a}\right\}_{a<n}
is an i.i.d. family of random vectors with
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{a}\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathcal{S}^{d-2})
. Moreover,
\left\{\xi_{a}\right\}
and
\left\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{a}\right\}
are mutually independent.

Proof.

Fix
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
, and let
E=[\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\,R_{n}]
. By construction,
E
is an orthogonal matrix. Define

\boldsymbol{v}_{a}=E^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}_{a},\quad 1\leq a\leq n-1.

As
\boldsymbol{x}_{a}\sim_{\text{i.i.d.}}\mathsf{Unif}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})
and
E
is independent of
\left\{\boldsymbol{x}_{a}\right\}_{a<n}
, we can conclude from the orthogonal invariant property of the spherical distribution that
\boldsymbol{v}_{a}\sim_{\text{i.i.d.}}\mathsf{Unif}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})
. The statement of the lemma then follows from the observation that

\xi_{a}=\sqrt{d}\,\boldsymbol{e}_{1}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{v}_{a}\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{a}=\frac{\boldsymbol{v}_{a,\setminus 1}}{\|{\boldsymbol{v}_{a,\setminus 1}}\|},

where
\boldsymbol{v}_{a,\setminus 1}
is the
(d-1)
-dimensional vector obtained from
\boldsymbol{v}_{a}
after removing its first element. Then
\xi_{a}\sim\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}
. Meanwhile,
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{a}
is uniformly distributed over
\mathcal{S}^{d-2}
, and is independent of
\xi_{a}
. Note that the above derivations are done for fixed
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
(i.e. when conditioned on
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
). However, since the distributions of
\left\{\xi_{a}\right\}
and
\left\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{a}\right\}
are invariant to the choice of
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
, we can conclude that
\left\{\xi_{a}\right\}
and
\left\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{a}\right\}
are indeed independent of
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
.
∎

It is easy to check that
\max_{a}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\xi_{a}\right\rvert}=\mathcal{O}_{\prec}(1)
[see (
D.43
)], and thus
\xi_{a}\xi_{b}/\sqrt{d}=\mathcal{O}_{\prec}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}})
. It is then natural to consider a Taylor expansion of the right-hand side of (
4.11
) to try to bring the term
\xi_{a}\xi_{b}/\sqrt{d}
out of the polynomial
q_{k}(\cdot)
. In fact, a more careful analysis will give us the following expansion formula, which plays an important role in our proof. First, we need to introduce a set of functions: for each
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, let

\mathcal{E}_{t}=\Bigg\{\sum_{0\leq\alpha,\beta\leq t}c_{\alpha\beta}(d)\frac{q_{\alpha}(\xi_{1})q_{\beta}(\xi_{2})}{d^{\max\left\{\alpha,\beta\right\}/2+1}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}c_{\alpha\beta}(d)=\mathcal{O}_{t}(1)\text{ for }0\leq\alpha,\beta\leq t\Bigg\}.

(4.12)

In what follows, we will use
\mathcal{E}_{t}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
to denote
any
function in
\mathcal{E}_{t}
. The exact form of
\mathcal{E}_{t}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
can change from one expression to another.

Proposition 1
.

For each
k=0,1,2,\ldots,L
, we have

q_{k}\Big(r(\xi_{1})r(\xi_{2})x+\xi_{1}\xi_{2}/\sqrt{d}\Big)=\sum_{t=0}^{k}\widetilde{q}_{k-t}(x)r^{k-t}(\xi_{1})r^{k-t}(\xi_{2})\Big[\sqrt{(k)_{t}}\frac{q_{t}(\xi_{1})q_{t}(\xi_{2})}{d^{t/2}}+\mathcal{E}_{t}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\Big],

(4.13)

where
(k)_{t}=k!/(k-t)!
denotes the falling factorial, and
\widetilde{q}_{k}(x)
is the
k
th Gegenbauer polynomial in dimension
d-1
as defined in (
2.5
).

Proof.

See Appendix
B
.
∎

For the first term on the right side of (
4.13
) with
t=0
, we define an
(n-1)\times(n-1)
matrix
\widetilde{A}_{k}
, whose entries are given by:

(\widetilde{A}_{k})_{ab}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\widetilde{q}_{k}(\sqrt{d-1}\,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{a}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{b})\,\mathds{1}_{a\neq b},\quad\text{for }a,b\in[n-1].

(4.14)

We write their linear combination as

\widetilde{A}\coloneqq\sum_{\ell\leq k\leq L}\mu_{k}\widetilde{A}_{k}.

(4.15)

For the first term on the right side of (
4.13
) with
t=k
, we define the vector

\boldsymbol{v}_{k}(\xi)\coloneqq(q_{k}(\xi_{1}),q_{k}(\xi_{2}),\ldots q_{k}(\xi_{n-1}))^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}.

(4.16)

Using the expansion in (
4.13
), we can now write the matrix in (
4.11
) as

A^{[n]}=\widetilde{A}+\frac{\mu_{\ell}}{\sqrt{nd^{\ell}/\ell!}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}(\xi)\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}(\xi)^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}+\sum_{1\leq i\leq 5}\Delta_{i},

(4.17)

where
\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{5}
are matrices defined as follows: for
a,b\in[n-1]
,

\displaystyle({\Delta}_{1})_{ab}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\mu_{\ell}q_{\ell}^{2}(\xi_{a})}{\sqrt{nd^{\ell}/\ell!}}\delta_{ab},

(4.18a)

\displaystyle({\Delta}_{2})_{ab}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\leq k\leq L}\mu_{k}\sum_{0\leq t\leq k}\sqrt{(k)_{t}}\cdot(\widetilde{A}_{k-t})_{ab}(r^{k-t}(\xi_{a})r^{k-t}(\xi_{b})-1)\frac{q_{t}(\xi_{a})q_{t}(\xi_{b})}{d^{t/2}},

(4.18b)

\displaystyle({\Delta}_{3})_{ab}

\displaystyle=\mu_{\ell}\sum_{1\leq t\leq\ell-1}\sqrt{(\ell)_{t}}\cdot(\widetilde{A}_{\ell-t})_{ab}\frac{q_{t}(\xi_{a})q_{t}(\xi_{b})}{d^{t/2}},

(4.18c)

\displaystyle({\Delta}_{4})_{ab}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell<k\leq L}\mu_{k}\sum_{1\leq t\leq k}\sqrt{(k)_{t}}\cdot(\widetilde{A}_{k-t})_{ab}\frac{q_{t}(\xi_{a})q_{t}(\xi_{b})}{d^{t/2}},

(4.18d)

\displaystyle({\Delta}_{5})_{ab}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\leq k\leq L}\mu_{k}\sum_{0\leq t\leq k}(\widetilde{A}_{k-t})_{ab}\cdot r^{k-t}(\xi_{a})r^{k-t}(\xi_{b})\mathcal{E}_{t}(\xi_{a},\xi_{b}).

(4.18e)

Here
{\Delta}_{2}
represents the error incurred when replacing
r^{k-t}(\xi_{a})r^{k-t}(\xi_{b})
by
1
; the error terms for
k=\ell
with
t\geq 1
are collected in
{\Delta}_{3}
; the error terms for
\ell<k\leq L
are collected in
{\Delta}_{4}
; lastly, all the terms involving
\mathcal{E}
are collected in
{\Delta}_{5}
.

Later, we will demonstrate that the matrices
\left\{\Delta_{i}\right\}
can all be considered as small noise terms that become negligible as
d\to\infty
. Moreover, by the construction of
\widetilde{A}_{k}
in (
4.14
) and by Lemma
3
, both
\widetilde{A}
and its resolvent
\widetilde{G}(z)\coloneqq(\widetilde{A}-zI)^{-1}
are independent of
\left\{\xi_{a}\right\}
. These observations lead us to consider the following expansion.

Lemma 4
.

Let
G^{[n]}(z)
and
\widetilde{G}(z)
denote the resolvents of
A^{[n]}
and
\widetilde{A}
, respectively. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{a,b\neq n}A_{na}G^{[n]}_{ab}(z)A_{nb}=\sum_{\ell\leq\alpha,\beta\leq L}\mu_{\alpha}\mu_{\beta}\chi_{\alpha\beta}(z)-\theta(z)\sum_{\ell\leq\beta\leq L}\mu_{\beta}\chi_{\ell\beta}(z)

(4.19)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Big(\sum_{\ell\leq\alpha\leq L}\mu_{\alpha}\boldsymbol{v}_{\alpha}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\xi)-\theta(z)\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\xi)\Big)\widetilde{G}(z)\Big(\sum_{1\leq c\leq 5}\Delta_{c}\Big)G^{[n]}(z)\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{\ell\leq\beta\leq L}\mu_{\beta}\boldsymbol{v}_{\beta}(\xi)\Big),

where, for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}
,
\boldsymbol{v}_{\alpha}(\xi)
is the vector defined in (
4.16
),

\displaystyle\chi_{\alpha\beta}(z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{v}_{\alpha}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\xi)\widetilde{G}(z)\boldsymbol{v}_{\beta}(\xi),

(4.20)

and

\displaystyle\theta(z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\mu_{\ell}\sum_{\ell\leq\alpha\leq L}\mu_{\alpha}\chi_{\alpha\ell}}{\sqrt{d^{\ell}/(\ell!n)}+\mu_{\ell}\chi_{\ell\ell}}.

(4.21)

Proof.

We start by recalling the simple resolvent identity in (
F.6
). Applying this identity on the two matrices
A^{[n]}
and
\widetilde{A}+\frac{\mu_{\ell}\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}(\xi)\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}(\xi)^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}}{\sqrt{nd^{\ell}/\ell!}}
, and by using (
4.17
), we get

\displaystyle G^{[n]}(z)

\displaystyle=\Big(\widetilde{A}+\frac{\mu_{\ell}\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}(\xi)\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}(\xi)^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}}{\sqrt{nd^{\ell}/\ell!}}-zI\Big)^{-1}\Big(I-\sum_{1\leq i\leq 5}\Delta_{i}G^{[n]}(z)\Big)

\displaystyle=\Big(\widetilde{G}(z)-\frac{\mu_{\ell}\widetilde{G}(z)\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}(\xi)\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\xi)\widetilde{G}(z)}{\sqrt{nd^{\ell}/\ell!}+\mu_{\ell}\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\xi)\widetilde{G}(z)\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}(\xi)}\Big)\Big(I-\sum_{1\leq i\leq 5}\Delta_{i}G^{[n]}(z)\Big),

(4.22)

where the second equality follows from the Woodbury matrix identity.
By (
4.9
) and (
4.16
),

A_{na}={\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}}\sum_{\ell\leq k\leq L}\mu_{k}[\boldsymbol{v}_{k}(\xi)]_{a}.

Substituting this into (
4.22
) then leads to the desired result.
∎

4.3
Schur Complement: the Leading Term

Next, we demonstrate that the right-hand side of (
4.19
) can indeed be separated into a leading term, whose form is provided in (
4.3
), and a small, random error term.

Proposition 2
.

Let
A=\sum_{\ell\leq k\leq L}\mu_{k}A_{k}
, and
i\in[n]
. Under the same conditions of Theorem
1
, we have

\sum_{a,b\neq i}A_{ia}G^{[i]}_{ab}(z)A_{ib}=\Big[\frac{\gamma_{a}s(z)}{1+\gamma_{b}s(z)}+\gamma_{c}s(z)\Big]+\mathcal{O}_{\prec}(d^{-1/2}),

(4.23)

where
s(z)
is the Stieltjes transform of the spectrum of
A
.

To prepare for the proof of Proposition
2
, we first establish several intermediate results.

Lemma 5
.

For
1\leq\alpha,\beta\leq L
, we have

\chi_{\alpha\beta}(z)=s(z)\delta_{\alpha\beta}+\mathcal{O}_{\prec}\Big(\frac{1}{\eta^{2}\sqrt{d}}\Big).

(4.24)

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{s}(z)\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\tr\widetilde{G}
and
s^{[n]}(z)\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\tr G^{[n]}
be the Stieltjes transforms of
\widetilde{G}
and
G^{[n]}
, respectively. We will establish (
4.24
) in three steps, by showing (a)
\chi_{\alpha\beta}(z)\approx\widetilde{s}(z)\delta_{\alpha\beta}
; (b)
\widetilde{s}(z)\approx s^{[n]}(z)
; and (c)
s^{[n]}(z)\approx s(z)
.

For step (a), we first recall the Ward identity (
F.3
), which gives us

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{1\leq a,b<n}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\widetilde{G}_{ab}\right\rvert}^{2}=\frac{\mathfrak{Im}(\widetilde{s}(z))}{\eta}\leq\frac{1}{\eta^{2}},

where the inequality is due to (
F.4
). By the definitions presented in (
4.16
) and (
4.20
),

\chi_{\alpha\beta}(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{a,b}[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\widetilde{G}_{ab}(z)]q_{\alpha}(\xi_{a})q_{\beta}(\xi_{b}).

Applying (
D.64b
) in Proposition
7
with
\Psi_{c}=1/{\eta}
, we have

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\chi_{\alpha\beta}(z)-\widetilde{s}(z)\delta_{\alpha\beta}\right\rvert}\prec\frac{1}{\eta\sqrt{n}}.

(4.25)

For step (b), we use the expansion formula in (
4.17
). By (
F.8
), (
F.7
), and the triangular inequality,

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s^{[n]}(z)-\widetilde{s}(z)\right\rvert}\leq\frac{C}{n\eta}+\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{5}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Delta_{i}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{F}}}{\sqrt{n}\eta^{2}}\leq\frac{C}{n\eta}+\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\eta^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{5}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Delta_{i}\right\rVert}_{\infty},

(4.26)

where
C
is an absolute constant. Next, we bound the entry-wise
\ell_{\infty}
norm of
\left\{\Delta_{i}\right\}
, whose constructions are given in (
4.18a
) – (
4.18e
). Our derivations will frequently use the following property of stochastic domination, which can be verified by a simple union bound: If
X_{1}\prec Y_{1}
and
X_{2}\prec Y_{2}
, then

X_{1}X_{2}\prec Y_{1}Y_{2}\quad\text{and}\quad X_{1}+X_{2}\prec Y_{1}+Y_{2}.

(4.27)

By (
D.43
), and the property stated above, we have
\max_{a}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert q_{k}(\xi_{a})\right\rvert}^{2}\prec 1
. It follows that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Delta_{1}\right\rVert}_{\infty}\prec\frac{1}{\sqrt{nd^{\ell}}}
. Similarly, by using (
D.44
), we can easily verify that

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Delta_{3}\right\rVert}_{\infty}\prec\frac{1}{\sqrt{nd}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Delta_{4}\right\rVert}_{\infty}\prec\frac{1}{\sqrt{nd}}.

To bound
\Delta_{5}
, we first check from the definition in (
4.12
) that
\max_{a,b\in[n-1]}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathcal{E}_{t}(\xi_{a},\xi_{b})\right\rvert}\prec 1/d
. Moreover, by its construction in (
4.10
), the function
r(\xi)\leq 1+\mathcal{O}(1/d)
for all
\xi
. Combining these estimates then gives us
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Delta_{5}\right\rVert}_{\infty}\prec\frac{1}{d\sqrt{n}}
.

The matrix
\Delta_{2}
in (
4.18b
) requires some additional care, as it contains terms with index
t=0
. First write

r^{k-t}(\xi_{a})r^{k-t}(\xi_{b})-1=(r^{k-t}(\xi_{a})-1)(r^{k-t}(\xi_{b})-1)+(r^{k-t}(\xi_{a})-1)+(r^{k-t}(\xi_{b})-1).

(4.28)

Applying the high-probability bound (
D.45
) and the property in (
4.27
), we get

\max_{a,b}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert r^{k-t}(\xi_{a})r^{k-t}(\xi_{b})-1\right\rvert}\prec 1/d,

which then implies that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Delta_{2}\right\rVert}_{\infty}\prec\frac{1}{d\sqrt{n}}
. Substituting these bounds on
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Delta_{c}\right\rVert}_{\infty}
for
1\leq c\leq 5
into (
4.26
) then leads to

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s^{[n]}(z)-\widetilde{s}(z)\right\rvert}\prec\frac{1}{\eta^{2}\sqrt{d}}.

(4.29)

Now we move to step (c), where we compare
s^{[n]}(z)
with
s(z)
. By the interlacing properties of the eigenvalues of
A^{[n]}
and
A
, we have the following standard result (see
[
16
, Lemma 7.5]
for a proof):

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s^{[n]}(z)-s(z)\right\rvert}\leq\frac{C}{n\eta},

(4.30)

where
C
is an absolute constant. Finally, the statement of the lemma can be obtained by applying the triangular inequality and by using the estimates in (
4.25
), (
4.29
), and (
4.30
).
∎

Next, we show that the terms involving
\left\{\Delta_{c}\right\}_{1\leq c\leq 5}
on the right-hand side of (
4.19
) are small. This is done in the following two lemmas.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\alpha,\beta,k
be integers such that
0\leq\alpha,\beta,k\leq L
and
\alpha\neq\beta
. We have

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{\alpha}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\xi)\widetilde{G}(z)\Lambda_{\beta}(\xi)\widetilde{A}_{k}\right\rVert}\prec\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\eta},

(4.31)

where
\Lambda_{\beta}(\xi)
denotes a diagonal matrix defined as

\Lambda_{\beta}(\xi)\coloneqq\diag\left\{q_{\beta}(\xi_{1}),q_{\beta}(\xi_{2}),\ldots,q_{\beta}(\xi_{n-1})\right\}.

(4.32)

Proof.

Write
u\coloneqq\boldsymbol{v}_{\alpha}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\xi)\widetilde{G}(z)\Lambda_{\beta}(\xi)\widetilde{A}_{k}
. This is a vector with
n-1
entries. In our proof, we will show that

\max_{a\in[n-1]}\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert u_{a}\right\rvert}\prec{1}/{\eta},

(4.33)

of which (
4.31
) is then an immediate consequence. For each
a\in[n-1]
, define a matrix

C_{a}\coloneqq\widetilde{G}(z)\diag\big\{(\widetilde{A}_{k})_{1a},(\widetilde{A}_{k})_{2a},\ldots,(\widetilde{A}_{k})_{n-1,a}\big\}.

One can verify that

u_{a}=\boldsymbol{v}_{\alpha}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\xi)C_{a}\boldsymbol{v}_{\beta}(\xi).

We start by proving (
4.33
) for the special case when
\alpha>\beta=0
. Recall that
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}(\xi)=\boldsymbol{1}
. We then have
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert C_{a}\boldsymbol{v}_{0}(\xi)\right\rVert}\leq\|\widetilde{G}\|_{\mathsf{op}}\|\widetilde{A}_{k}\|_{\infty}\sqrt{n}\prec 1/\eta
, where the second step uses (
F.1
) and (
D.44
). Since
C_{a}
is independent of
\boldsymbol{v}_{\alpha}(\xi)
, we can apply (
D.64a
) in Proposition
7
with
\Psi_{b}=1/\eta
to get

u_{a}=\boldsymbol{v}_{\alpha}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\xi)C_{a}\boldsymbol{v}_{0}(\xi)\prec 1/\eta.

(4.34)

By the same arguments,
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\xi)C_{a}\boldsymbol{v}_{\beta}(\xi)\prec 1/\eta
for
\beta>0
. In what follows, we assume
1\leq\alpha,\beta\leq L
with
\alpha\neq\beta
. Using the Ward identity (
F.3
) in the second step, and applying (
F.4
), (
D.44
) in the third step, we get

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert C_{a}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{F}}\leq\|\widetilde{G}\|_{\mathsf{F}}\|\widetilde{A}_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\sqrt{n\cdot\mathfrak{Im}\,\widetilde{s}(z)}}{\sqrt{\eta}}\|\widetilde{A}_{k}\|_{\infty}\prec\frac{1}{\eta}.

By appealing to (
D.64b
) in Proposition
7
with
\Psi_{c}=1/\eta
, we have

u_{a}=\boldsymbol{v}_{\alpha}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\xi)C_{a}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}(\xi)\prec 1/\eta.

(4.35)

One can check that the high-probability bounds in (
4.34
) and (
4.35
) are both uniform in
a\in[n-1]
. (To see this, note that we always bound
\max_{i}|(\widetilde{A}_{k})_{ia}|
via
\|\widetilde{A}_{k}\|_{\infty}
in the above derivations.) Applying the union bound over

then gives us (
4.33
).
∎

Lemma 7
.

For
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}
with
\ell\leq\alpha,\beta\leq L
, and for

The Isotropic Gaussian Model

In this section, we show that the results stated in Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
still hold if the data vectors are sampled from the i.i.d. isotropic Gaussian distribution instead of the spherical distribution. Specifically, let
\boldsymbol{g}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{g}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a set of independent vectors drawn from
\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}/d)
. Consider a random matrix
J\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with entries

J_{ij}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\,f_{d}(\sqrt{d}\,\boldsymbol{g}_{i}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{g}_{j})&\text{if }i\neq j\\
0&\text{if }i=j\end{cases},

(5.1)

where
f_{d}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is a (nonlinear) kernel function. We show that the weak limit of the empirical spectral distribution of
J
is characterized by the same equivalence principle given in Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
.

Our approach is based on a simple coupling method, which allows us to define
J
in the same probability space as the matrix
A
in (
1.1
) and to write
J
as a perturbation of
A
. To that end, we rewrite
\boldsymbol{g}_{i}
, for each
i\in[n]
, as

\boldsymbol{g}_{i}=\nu_{i}\boldsymbol{x}_{i},

(5.2)

where
\nu_{i}\coloneqq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\boldsymbol{g}_{i}\right\rVert}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{g}_{i}/\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\boldsymbol{g}_{i}\right\rVert}
. By the rotational invariance of the isotropic Gaussian distribution,
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})
, and
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
is independent of
\nu_{i}
. In our subsequent discussions, we will often use the following standard concentration result (see,
e.g.
,
[
39
, Theorem 3.1.1]
): for any

,

\mathbb{P}(\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\nu_{i}-1\right\rvert}\geq t)\leq 2e^{-cdt^{2}},

(5.3)

where
c>0
is some absolute constant. In other words,

In concluding this section, we highlight an important caveat concerning the results presented above. While changing the data distribution from spherical to isotropic Gaussian does not alter the weak limit of the empirical spectral distribution of the kernel matrix, the Gaussian model may indeed introduce additional spike eigenvalues that are absent in the original spherical case. 6
Summary and Discussions

Our main results, Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
, establish the weak limit of the spectrum for random inner-product kernel matrices in the polynomial scaling regime, where
d,n\to\infty
such that
n/d^{\ell}\to\kappa\in(0,\infty)
for a fixed
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. The central insight of this work is an asymptotic equivalence principle, which asserts that the limiting spectral distribution of the kernel matrix corresponds to that of a linear combination of a (shifted) Wishart matrix and an independent GOE matrix. Consequently, the limiting distribution is characterized by a free additive convolution between an MP law and a semicircle law.

We have established our results only for data vectors sampled from spherical or isotropic Gaussian distributions. In fact, Theorem
1
is expected to hold (with certain modifications in error bounds) for more general data distributions exhibiting reasonably fast decay at infinity. It is also worth noting that the error bounds in Theorem
1
are not optimal. Moreover, we currently constrain the imaginary part of the spectral parameter
z=E+i\eta
to
\eta>\tau
for some constant
\tau
. Although our proof approach can accommodate
\eta\geq d^{-c}
for some small constant
c
with more careful book-keeping, this is still far from the regime
\eta\sim n^{-1+\epsilon}
required to reach individual eigenvalue locations.

Extending Theorem
1
to encompass the entire range
n^{-1+\epsilon}\leq\eta\leq 1
may prove challenging due to the presence of multiscale structures in the eigenvalues. These structures emerge because the matrix
A
in (
2.7
) is a sum of component matrices
\left\{A_{k}\right\}
across different scales. As a result, accurately characterizing individual eigenvalues, including extremal ones, poses an interesting open problem. Related issues, such as eigenvector statistics and eigenvalue universality, also hinge on addressing this matter. The potential for multiscale structures sets the nonlinear model apart from standard mean field models like Wigner matrices or sample covariance matrices
[
16
]
. We aim to tackle some of these questions in future papers.

In this appendix, we collect a few useful properties of the (normalized) Gegenbauer polynomials

Three-term recurrence relation
: For each

, we have

xq_{k}(x)=a_{k}q_{k+1}(x)+a_{k-1}q_{k-1}(x),

(A.1)

(A.2)

Spherical harmonics and the addition theorem
: We recall that a spherical harmonic of degree
k
is a harmonic and homogeneous polynomial of degree
k

d

\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
. The set of all degree
k

N_{k}\coloneqq\frac{2k+d-2}{k}{{k+d-3}\choose{k-1}}=\frac{d^{k}}{k!}(1+\mathcal{O}(1/d)).

(A.3)

Using the Gram-Schmidt procedure, we can construct an orthonormal set of spherical harmonics. Let
Y_{k,i}(\boldsymbol{x})
, for
i\in[N_{k}]
, denote the
i
th degree-
k

The Gengenbauer polynomials and spherical harmonics are deeply connected. In particular, we have the following identity, which can be viewed as a high-dimensional generalization of the classical addition theorem

]
: for any

q_{k}(\sqrt{d}\,\boldsymbol{x}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}\sum_{a\in[N_{k}]}Y_{k,a}(\boldsymbol{x})Y_{k,a}(\boldsymbol{y}).

(A.5)

In addition, for the special case of
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{y}

q_{k}(\sqrt{d})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}\sum_{a\in[N_{k}]}Y_{k,a}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sqrt{N_{k}}.

(A.6)

The identity in (

Using (

), we can rewrite the matrix

) as

A_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{nN_{k}}}S_{k}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}S_{k}-\sqrt{\frac{N_{k}}{n}}\,I,

(A.7)

where

is a

matrix whose entries are the spherical harmonics,

(S_{k})_{ai}=Y_{k,a}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}),

(A.8)

and
\left\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right\}_{i\in[n]}

).

\mu_{i}=\mathcal{O}(1),\nu_{i}=\mathcal{O}(1)
are some fixed expansion coefficients. It follows that

\frac{\xi_{1}\xi_{2}}{\sqrt{d}}\mathcal{E}_{m}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{a+1}\sum_{j=0}^{b+1}\mu_{i}\nu_{j}q_{i}(\xi_{1})q_{j}(\xi_{2})}{d^{\max\left\{a+1,b+1\right\}/2+1}}=\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{a+1}\sum_{j=0}^{b+1}({\mu_{i}\nu_{j}}/{d^{\pi_{ij}}})q_{i}(\xi_{1})q_{j}(\xi_{2})}{d^{\max\left\{i,j\right\}/2+1}},

where
\pi_{ij}=\max\left\{a+1,b+1\right\}/2-\max\left\{i,j\right\}/2\geq 0
. Thus, we
\frac{\xi_{1}\xi_{2}}{\sqrt{d}}\mathcal{E}_{m}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\in\mathcal{E}_{m+1}
.

The properties stated in (
B.1c
) can be proved similarly. Consider the function in (
B.2
). We have
\xi_{1}^{2}q_{a}(\xi_{1})=\sum_{i=0}^{a+2}\mu_{i}q_{i}(\xi_{1})
for some expansion coefficients
\mu_{i}=\mathcal{O}(1)
. It follows that

({\xi_{1}^{2}}/{d})\mathcal{E}_{m}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{a+2}\frac{\mu_{i}}{d^{\pi_{i}}}q_{i}(\xi_{1})q_{b}(\xi_{2})}{d^{\max\left\{i,b\right\}/2+1}},

, we have

. That
(\xi_{2}^{2}/d)\mathcal{E}_{m}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\in\mathcal{E}_{m+2}
follows from analogous arguments.

Recall the definition of
r(\xi)
in (
4.10
). We have

\displaystyle r^{2}(\xi_{1})r^{2}(\xi_{2})\mathcal{E}_{m}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})

\displaystyle=(1-1/d)^{-1}(1-\xi_{1}^{2}/d)(1-\xi_{2}^{2}/d)\mathcal{E}_{m}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})

\displaystyle=(1+\mathcal{O}(1/d))\Big(1+(\xi_{1}\xi_{2}/\sqrt{d})^{2}/d-\xi_{1}^{2}/d-\xi_{2}^{2}/d\Big)\mathcal{E}_{m}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2}).

(B.3)

Note that
\mathcal{E}_{m}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\in\mathcal{E}_{m+2}
by (
B.1a

B.1b
) twice, we have
(\xi_{1}\xi_{2}/\sqrt{d})^{2}\mathcal{E}_{m}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\in\mathcal{E}_{m+2}

\mathcal{E}_{m+2}

∎

Proof.

Appendix C
Review of the Marchenko-Pastur Law

In this appendix, we review some basic properties of the Marchenko-Pastur (MP) law that will be used in our work. Let
X
be an
M\times n
matrix whose entries
X_{\mu i}
are independent complex-valued random variables satisfying

\mathbb{E}X_{\mu i}=0\qquad\mathbb{E}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert X_{\mu i}\right\rvert}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}.

(C.1)

Additionally,
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\sqrt{n}X_{\mu i}\right\rvert}
has a sufficient number of bounded moments. We also assume that
M
and
n
satisfy the bounds

n^{1/C}\leq M\leq n^{C}

(C.2)

for some positive constant
C
, and define the aspect ratio

\phi\coloneqq\phi_{n}=\frac{M}{n},

which may depend on
n
. Now consider an
n\times n
matrix

R=X^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\Sigma X-\frac{\tr\Sigma}{n}I,

(C.3)

where
\Sigma
is a diagonal
M\times M
matrix with matrix elements

\sigma_{i}
on the diagonal. Note that the subtraction by
\frac{\tr\Sigma}{n}I
in (
C.3
) makes sure that the diagonal elements of
R
are approximately equal to 0. We do this to match the construction in (
1.1
).

Assuming that the law of the diagonal entries of
\Sigma
is given by a probability law
\pi_{M}=M^{-1}\sum\delta_{\sigma_{i}}\to\pi
. The MP law asserts that the limiting Stieltjes transform
of the eigenvalues of
R
, denoted by
m(z)
, satisfies the self-consistent equation

\frac{1}{m}=-z-\phi\int\frac{x^{2}m}{1+xm}\pi(\dif x).

(C.4)

See,
e.g.
, eq. (2.10) in
Knowles and Yin 2016
. (Notice that our equation is slightly different due to a constant shift of the eigenvalues).
In the special case of
\Sigma=tI
and
R=tX^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}X-t\phi I
, the above formula reduces to

\frac{1}{m}=-z-\phi\frac{t^{2}m}{1+tm}.

(C.5)

The limiting eigenvalue density associated with (
C.5
) is given by

\varrho_{\phi,t}(x)\dif x\coloneqq\frac{\sqrt{\big[(2\sqrt{\phi}+x/t-1)(2\sqrt{\phi}-x/t+1)\big]_{+}}}{2\pi\sign(t)[x+t\phi]}\,\dif x+(1-\phi)_{+}\,\delta(x+t\phi)\dif x.

(C.6)

As a side note, we remark that a different convention was used in
Bloemendal et al. 2014
, where
Y
is an
M\times n
matrix whose entries
Y_{i\mu}
are independent random variables satisfying

\mathbb{E}Y_{i\mu}\;=\;0\,,\;\mathbb{E}Y_{i\mu}^{2}\;=\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{nM}}
.
One can check easily that
\varrho_{tH+r}(x)\dif x=\varrho_{H}\big(\frac{x-r}{t}\big)\frac{\dif x}{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}}
where
\varrho_{H}
denotes the density of a symmetric matrix
H
.
Hence
R=t[\sqrt{\phi}Y^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}Y-\phi I]
and we have
\varrho_{R}(x)\dif x={\varrho_{Y^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}Y}(\frac{x}{t\sqrt{\phi}}+\sqrt{\phi})}/({\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}\sqrt{\phi}})\dif x
.
Thus our formula is identical to the one that appeared in
[
6
, eq. (3.3)]
.

Appendix D
Moment Bounds and Concentration Inequalities

In this appendix, we derive several moment and concentration inequalities that will be used in our proof.

Lemma 11
.

Let
\xi\sim\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}
, and
q_{k}(x)
be the
k
th Gegenbauer polynomial for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. For each
p\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}=(\mathbb{E}\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert}^{p})^{1/p}\leq 2\sqrt{p}

(D.1)

and

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert q_{k}(\xi)\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}\leq C(k,p)

(D.2)

where
C(k,p)
is some constant that only depends on
k
and
p
.

Remark 6
.

Both the probability measure
\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}
and the Gegenbauer polynomials
q_{k}(x)
depend on the dimension
d
. However, the upper bounds in (
D.1
) and (
D.2
) hold uniformly for all
d
.

Proof.

The probability distribution of
\xi
is given by

w_{d}(\xi)=\begin{cases}\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{\sqrt{d\pi}\,\Gamma((d-1)/2)}(1-\xi^{2}/d)^{\frac{d-3}{2}}&\text{if }\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert}\leq\sqrt{d}\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(D.3)

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the gamma function. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert}^{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\,\Gamma(d/2)}{\sqrt{d\pi}\,\Gamma((d-1)/2)}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{d}}\xi^{p}(1-\xi^{2}/d)^{\frac{d-3}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\xi

(D.4)

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{p/2}\,\Gamma(d/2)\,\Gamma((p+1)/2)}{\Gamma((d+p)/2)\,\Gamma(1/2)}

(D.5)

\displaystyle\leq(4p)^{p/2},

(D.6)

where the second line uses the relationship between the beta function and the gamma function
[
1
]
, and the last line is obtained by applying Stirling’s formula for the gamma function
[
25
]
.

Next, we show (
D.2
). Two special cases are easy: for
k=0
, we have
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert q_{0}(\xi)\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}=1
; for
p=1
,
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert q_{k}(\xi)\right\rVert}_{L^{1}}\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert q_{k}(\xi)\right\rVert}_{L^{2}}=1
. Thus, in what follows we assume
k\geq 1
and
p\geq 2
. Denote by
c_{k}
the leading coefficient of the polynomial
q_{k}(\xi)
. Since
q_{k}(\xi)-c_{k}\xi^{k}
is a polynomial of degree
k-1
, it can be written as linear combination of the lower order Gegenbauer polynomials,
i.e.
,

q_{k}(\xi)=c_{k}\xi^{k}+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}b_{k,i}q_{i}(\xi),

where
\left\{b_{k,i}\right\}_{i}
are the expansion coefficients. By using the orthogonality of the Gegenbauer polynomials (see (
2.2
)), we have, for
0\leq i\leq k-1
,

0=\mathbb{E}[q_{k}(\xi)q_{i}(\xi)]=c_{k}\mathbb{E}[\xi^{k}q_{i}(\xi)]+b_{k,i},

and thus

q_{k}(\xi)=c_{k}\xi^{k}-c_{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}[\xi^{k}q_{i}(\xi)]q_{i}(\xi).

From the triangular inequality,

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert q_{k}(\xi)\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}\leq c_{k}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\xi^{k}\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}+c_{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathbb{E}[\xi^{k}q_{i}(\xi)]\right\rvert}\cdot\mathinner{\!\left\lVert q_{i}(\xi)\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}.

(D.7)

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathbb{E}[\xi^{k}q_{i}(\xi)]\right\rvert}\leq[\mathbb{E}\,\xi^{2k}\,\mathbb{E}\,q_{i}^{2}(\xi)]^{1/2}\leq(8k)^{k/2},

(D.8)

where the last inequality is due to (
D.1
) and the fact that
\mathbb{E}q_{i}^{2}(\xi)=1
. In addition,
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\xi^{k}\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}\leq(4pk)^{k/2}
by (
D.1
). Substituting these two bounds into (
D.7
) then gives us, for
p\geq 2
,

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert q_{k}(\xi)\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}\leq c_{k}(4pk)^{k/2}\Big(1+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert q_{i}(\xi)\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}\Big).

(D.9)

We now provide a bound for
c_{k}
, the leading coefficient of
q_{k}(\xi)
. Recall from (
2.3
) that
c_{1}=1
and
c_{2}=(1/\sqrt{2})\sqrt{(d+2)/(d-1)}
. A general formula for
c_{k}
can be obtained by examining the three-term recurrence relation in (
A.1
), which gives us
c_{k+1}=c_{k}/a_{k}
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
. This implies that
c_{k}=(a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{k-1})^{-1}
, and from the explicit formula for
a_{k}
in (
A.2
),

c_{k}=\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\Big(\frac{1+(2/d)i}{1+i}\Big)^{1/2}\Big(1+\frac{i}{i+d-2}\Big)^{1/2}\leq 2^{k/2}

(D.10)

for all
d\geq 2
. Using this estimate in (
D.9
) leads to

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert q_{k}(\xi)\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}\leq(8pk)^{k/2}\Big(1+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert q_{i}(\xi)\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}\Big),\qquad\text{for all }k\in\mathbb{N}.

Starting from
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert q_{0}(\xi)\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}=1
, we can apply the above bound recursively to verify (
D.2
).
∎

In the discussion of the equivalence principle for general nonlinear kernel functions in Section
2.3
and Section
5
, we examine three closely-related probability distributions: the standard normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, the probability measure
\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}
as defined in (
1.3
), and the distribution of the random variable

\breve{\xi}_{d}\coloneqq\sqrt{d}\boldsymbol{x}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{y},

(D.11)

where
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are two independent random vectors sampled from

. It can be readily verified that, as

Lemma 12
.

Let
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be a function. If the following conditions hold: (a)
\int f^{2}(x)w(x)\dif x<\infty
, and (b) there exist positive constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
such that
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert f(x)\right\rvert}<c_{1}e^{c_{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x\right\rvert}}
when
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x\right\rvert}\geq c_{3}
, then

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{2}(x)\mathinner{\!\left\lvert w(x)-w_{d}(x)\right\rvert}\dif x\xrightarrow{d\to\infty}0

(D.12)

and

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{2}(x)\mathinner{\!\left\lvert w(x)-\breve{w}_{d}(x)\right\rvert}\dif x\xrightarrow{d\to\infty}0.

(D.13)

Here,
w(x)
denotes the probability density function of
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
,
w_{d}(x)
denotes the density function of the probability measure
\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}
as defined in (
1.3
), and
\breve{w}_{d}(x)
represents the probability density function of the random variable
\breve{\xi}_{d}
defined in (
D.11
).

Proof.

We begin with (
D.12
). Let
M
be a constant in the interval
[c_{3},\sqrt{d/2}]
. We split the integration in (
D.12
) into two parts:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{2}(x)\mathinner{\!\left\lvert w(x)-w_{d}(x)\right\rvert}\dif x=\Big(\int_{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x\right\rvert}\leq M}+\int_{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x\right\rvert}\geq M}\Big)f^{2}(x)\mathinner{\!\left\lvert w(x)-w_{d}(x)\right\rvert}\dif x

(D.14)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\sup_{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x\right\rvert}\leq M}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert 1-w_{d}(x)/w(x)\right\rvert}\cdot\int_{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x\right\rvert}\leq M}f^{2}(x)w(x)\dif x+2c_{1}^{2}\int_{x\geq M}e^{2c_{2}x}[w(x)+w_{d}(x)]\dif x.

(D.15)

In reaching the second step, we have used the condition that
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert f(x)\right\rvert}<c_{1}e^{c_{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x\right\rvert}}
for
x\geq M
, and we have also used the property that the densities
w(x)
and
w_{d}(x)
are both even functions.

A closed-form expression of
w_{d}(x)
can be found in (
D.3
). By applying Stirling’s formula for the gamma function
[
25
]
and Taylor’s expansion for
\log(1-x^{2}/d)
, it is straightforward to verify that
w_{d}(x)/w(x)=[1+\mathcal{O}(1/d)]e^{3x^{2}/(2d)}
over the interval
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x\right\rvert}\leq M
, where
M
is some constant satisfying
M<\sqrt{d/2}
. Consequently, we have

\sup_{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x\right\rvert}\leq M}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert 1-w_{d}(x)/w(x)\right\rvert}\xrightarrow{d\to\infty}0.

(D.16)

Once more, using the closed-form expression in (
D.3
), we can directly confirm that
w_{d}(x)\leq[e^{3/2}+\mathcal{O}(1/d)]w(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Therefore, the second term on the right-hand side of (
D.15
) can be bounded as follows:

\int_{x\geq M}e^{2c_{2}x}[w(x)+w_{d}(x)]\dif x\leq\mathcal{O}(1)\int_{x\geq M}e^{2c_{2}x}w(x)\dif x\leq\mathcal{O}(1)e^{-M^{2}/4},

(D.17)

where the final inequality follows from the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality and standard Gaussian tail bounds. By assumption,
\int f^{2}(x)w(x)\dif x<\infty
. Upon substituting (
D.16
) and (
D.17
) into (
D.15
), we have

\limsup_{d\to\infty}\int f^{2}(x)\mathinner{\!\left\lvert w(x)-w_{d}(x)\right\rvert}\dif x\leq\mathcal{O}(1)e^{-M^{2}/4}.

(D.18)

Since the constant
M
can be chosen arbitrarily, we have then demonstrated (
D.12
).

Next, we verify the expression in (
D.13
). Let
\varphi(t)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[e^{\mathrm{i}t\breve{\xi}_{d}}]
represent the characteristic function of the random variable
\breve{\xi}_{d}
defined in (
D.11
). It is straightforward to verify that
\varphi(t)=(\mathbb{E}[e^{\mathrm{i}t\sqrt{d}x_{1}y_{1}}])^{d}=(1+t^{2}/d)^{-d/2}
. By applying the inversion formula for characteristic functions, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lvert w(x)-\breve{w}_{d}(x)\right\rvert}\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\mathrm{i}tx}(e^{-t^{2}/2}-(1+t^{2}/d)^{-d/2})\dif t\right\rvert}

(D.19)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert e^{-t^{2}/2}-(1+t^{2}/d)^{-d/2}\right\rvert}\dif t

(D.20)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}\leq\sqrt{4\log d}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert(1+t^{2}/d)^{-d/2}-e^{-t^{2}/2}\right\rvert}\dif t+\frac{1}{\pi}\Big(\int_{\sqrt{4\log d}\leq t\leq\sqrt{d}}+\int_{t\geq\sqrt{d}}\Big)(1+t^{2}/d)^{-d/2}\dif t.

(D.21)

In reaching the final step, we have used the inequality
\log(1+t^{2}/d)\leq t^{2}/d
, which then implies that
(1+t^{2}/d)^{-d/2}\geq e^{-t^{2}/2}
. It is straightforward to verify via Taylor’s expansion that

\sup_{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}\leq\sqrt{4\log d}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert e^{t^{2}/2}(1+t^{2}/d)^{-d/2}-1\right\rvert}=\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\log^{2}d}{d}\Big).

(D.22)

We then have:

\int_{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}\leq\sqrt{4\log d}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert(1+t^{2}/d)^{-d/2}-e^{-t^{2}/2}\right\rvert}\dif t=\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\log^{2}d}{d}\Big).

(D.23)

To bound the second integral on the right-hand side of (
D.21
), we use the inequality
\log(1+t^{2}/d)\geq(\log 2)t^{2}/d
for
0\leq t\leq\sqrt{d}
. It follows that

\int_{\sqrt{4\log d}\leq t\leq\sqrt{d}}(1+t^{2}/d)^{-d/2}\dif t\leq\int_{\sqrt{4\log d}\leq t\leq\sqrt{d}}e^{-(\log 2)t^{2}/2}\dif t=\mathcal{O}(d^{-1}),

(D.24)

where the final step uses standard Gaussian tail bounds. For the third integral on the right-hand side of (
D.21
), we obtain

\int_{t\geq\sqrt{d}}(1+t^{2}/d)^{-d/2}\dif t=\sqrt{d}\int_{1}^{\infty}(1+t^{2})^{-d/2}\dif t\leq\sqrt{d}\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{-d}\dif t=\mathcal{O}(d^{-1/2}).

(D.25)

By combining the bounds (
D.22
), (
D.24
), and (
D.25
) for the three integrals, we can establish that

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert w(x)-\breve{w}_{d}(x)\right\rvert}=\mathcal{O}(d^{-1/2}).

(D.26)

Let
M_{d}
be a positive number that depends on
d
. We divide the integral in (
D.13
) into two parts, following a similar approach as in (
D.15
). This results in:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f^{2}(x)\mathinner{\!\left\lvert w(x)-\breve{w}_{d}(x)\right\rvert}\dif x

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x\right\rvert}\leq M_{d}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert 1-\breve{w}_{d}(x)/w(x)\right\rvert}\cdot\int_{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x\right\rvert}\leq M_{d}}f^{2}(x)w(x)\dif x

(D.27)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+2c_{1}^{2}\int_{x\geq M_{d}}e^{2c_{2}x}w(x)\dif x+2c_{1}^{2}\int_{x\geq M_{d}}e^{2c_{2}x}\breve{w}_{d}(x)\dif x

(D.28)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}(d^{-1/2})e^{M_{d}^{2}/2}+\mathcal{O}(1)e^{-M_{d}^{2}/4}+2c_{1}^{2}\int_{x\geq M_{d}}e^{2c_{2}x}\breve{w}_{d}(x)\dif x,

(D.29)

where in obtaining the last inequality, we have utilized (
D.26
), the assumption that
\int f^{2}(x)w(x)\dif x<\infty
, and the estimate provided in (
D.17
) to bound the first two terms on the right-hand side of (
D.27
). By applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have:

\int_{x\geq M_{d}}e^{2c_{2}x}\breve{w}_{d}(x)\dif x\leq\big[\mathbb{E}e^{4c_{2}\breve{\xi}_{d}}\cdot\mathbb{P}(\breve{\xi}_{d}\geq M_{d})\big]^{1/2}\leq\big[\mathbb{E}e^{4c_{2}\breve{\xi}_{d}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\breve{\xi}_{d}^{2}\big]^{1/2}/M_{d}=\mathcal{O}(M_{d}^{-1}),

(D.30)

with the final step due to the identities
\mathbb{E}e^{4c_{2}\breve{\xi}_{d}}=(1-16c_{2}^{2}/d)^{-d/2}
and
\mathbb{E}\breve{\xi}_{d}^{2}=1
. The desired result in (
D.13
) then follows by substituting (
D.30
) into (
D.29
), and setting
M_{d}=\sqrt{\log d}/2
.
∎

In the following discussion, we derive several useful moment and high probability bounds for linear and quadratic functions of independent random variables. We first recall the following estimates, whose proof can be found in
[
16
, Lemma 7.8, Lemma 7.9]
.

Lemma 13
.

Let
(X_{i})_{i\in[n]}
and
(Y_{j})_{j\in[m]}
be independent families of random variables, and
(b_{i})_{i\in[n]}
and
(c_{ij})_{i\in[n],j\in[m]}
be deterministic complex-valued coefficients; here
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose that all entries of
(X_{i})
and
(Y_{j})
are independent and satisfy

\mathbb{E}X=\mathbb{E}Y=0,\qquad\mathbb{E}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert X\right\rvert}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert Y\right\rvert}^{2}=1,\qquad\mathinner{\!\left\lVert X\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}\leq\kappa_{p},\qquad\mathinner{\!\left\lVert Y\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}\leq\gamma_{p},

(D.31)

for all
p\in\mathbb{N}
and some constants
\kappa_{p},\gamma_{p}
. Then, we have

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\textstyle\sum_{i}b_{i}X_{i}\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}\leq(Cp)^{1/2}\kappa_{p}\Big(\sum_{i}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert b_{i}\right\rvert}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

(D.32a)

and

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\textstyle\sum_{i,j}c_{ij}X_{i}Y_{j}\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}\leq Cp\kappa_{p}\gamma_{p}\Big(\sum_{i,j}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert c_{ij}\right\rvert}^{2}\Big)^{1/2},

(D.32b)

where
C
is an absolute constant.

Proposition 4
.

Let
(c_{ij})_{i,j\in[n]}
be a deterministic matrix with complex-valued coefficients, and
(\xi_{i})_{i\in[n]}
be an independent family of random variables with
\xi_{i}\sim\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}
. For every
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\textstyle\sum_{i,j}c_{ij}q_{a}(\xi_{i})q_{b}(\xi_{j})-(\sum_{i}c_{ii})\delta_{ab}\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}\leq C(a,b,p)\big(\textstyle\sum_{i,j}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert c_{ij}\right\rvert}^{2}\big)^{1/2},

(D.33)

where
C(a,b,p)
is a quantity that depends on
a
,
b
, and
p
, but not on
n
.

Proof.

By the triangular inequality, we have

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\textstyle\sum_{i,j}c_{ij}q_{a}(\xi_{i})q_{b}(\xi_{j})-(\sum_{i}c_{ii})\delta_{ab}\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\textstyle\sum_{i\neq j}c_{ij}q_{a}(\xi_{i})q_{b}(\xi_{j})\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}+\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\textstyle\sum_{i}c_{ii}\big(q_{a}(\xi_{i})q_{b}(\xi_{i})-\delta_{ab}\big)\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}.

(D.34)

To bound the first term on the right-hand side, we use the standard decoupling technique. Observe that, for every
i\neq j
, the following identity holds:

1=\frac{1}{Z_{n}}\sum_{I\subset[n]}\mathds{1}(i\in I)\mathds{1}(j\in I^{c}),

(D.35)

where the sum ranges over all subsets of
[n]
, and
Z_{n}=2^{n-2}
. Applying this identity and the triangular inequality allows us to write

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\textstyle\sum_{i\neq j}c_{ij}q_{a}(\xi_{i})q_{b}(\xi_{j})\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}\leq\frac{1}{Z_{n}}\sum_{I\subset[n]}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\textstyle\sum_{i\in I,j\in I^{c}}c_{ij}q_{a}(\xi_{i})q_{b}(\xi_{j})\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}.

(D.36)

Note that the families of random variables
\left\{q_{a}(\xi_{i})\right\}_{i\in I}
and
\left\{q_{b}(\xi_{j})\right\}_{j\in I^{c}}
have mean-zero independent components with unit variances; they are also mutually independent. We can then apply (
D.32b
) to bound each term in the sum in (
D.36
). Moreover, the sum involves a total of
2^{n}-2
terms. Thus,

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\textstyle\sum_{i\neq j}c_{ij}q_{a}(\xi_{i})q_{b}(\xi_{j})\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}\leq 4Cp\mathinner{\!\left\lVert q_{a}(\xi)\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert q_{b}(\xi)\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}\Big(\sum_{i\neq j}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert c_{ij}\right\rvert}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

(D.37)

Now we bound the second term on the right-hand side of (
D.34
). Write
X_{i}=q_{a}(\xi_{i})q_{b}(\xi_{i})-\delta_{ab}
and
\sigma=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert X_{i}\right\rVert}_{L^{2}}
. Then
(X_{i}/\sigma)_{i}
is a family of independent random variables that satisfy the condition of Lemma
13
. Using (
D.32a
) gives us

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\textstyle\sum_{i}c_{ii}\big(q_{a}(\xi_{i})q_{b}(\xi_{i})-\delta_{ab}\big)\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle=\sigma\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\textstyle\sum_{i}c_{ii}(X_{i}/\sigma)\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}

(D.38)

\displaystyle\leq(CP)^{1/2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert X_{i}\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}\big(\textstyle\sum_{i}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert c_{ii}\right\rvert}^{2}\big)^{1/2}.

(D.39)

By the triangular inequality (in the first line) and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (in the third line),

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert X_{i}\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert q_{a}(\xi_{i})q_{b}(\xi_{i})\right\rVert}_{L^{p}}+1

(D.40)

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{E}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert q_{a}(\xi_{i})\right\rvert}^{p}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert q_{b}(\xi_{i})\right\rvert}^{p})^{1/p}+1

(D.41)

\displaystyle\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert q_{a}(\xi_{i})\right\rVert}_{L^{2p}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert q_{b}(\xi_{i})\right\rVert}_{L^{2p}}+1.

(D.42)

Substituting (
D.37
) and (
D.42
) into (
D.34
), and by applying the moment bound in (
D.2
), we complete the proof of the statement in (
D.33
).
∎

The moment estimates obtained above can be easily turned into high probability bounds, as follows.

Proposition 5
.

Suppose
n=\mathcal{O}(d^{\ell})
for some fixed
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
(\xi_{i})_{i\in[n]}
be an independent family of random variables with
\xi_{i}\sim\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}
, and let
A_{k}
be the matrix defined in (
2.6
). For each fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we have the bounds

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[n]}\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert q_{k}(\xi_{i})\right\rvert}

\displaystyle\prec 1,

(D.43)

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A_{k}\right\rVert}_{\infty}

\displaystyle\prec\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},

(D.44)

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[n]}\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert r^{k}(\xi_{i})-1\right\rvert}

\displaystyle\prec\frac{1}{d},

(D.45)

where
r(\xi)
is the function defined in (
4.10
).

Proof.

For any
\epsilon>0
and
p\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\textstyle\max_{i}\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert q_{k}(\xi_{i})\right\rvert}\geq d^{\epsilon})

\displaystyle\leq n\,\mathbb{P}(\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert q_{k}(\xi)\right\rvert}\geq d^{\epsilon})

(D.46)

\displaystyle\leq Cd^{\ell}\,\mathbb{P}(\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert q_{k}(\xi)\right\rvert}^{p}\geq d^{\epsilon p})

(D.47)

\displaystyle\leq Cd^{\ell-\epsilon p}C(k,p),

(D.48)

where the last step is by the Markov inequality and by the moment bound in (
D.2
). For any
D>0
, we can always find
p=p(\epsilon,D)
such that
Cd^{\ell-\epsilon p}C(k,p)<d^{-D}
for all sufficiently large
d
. Since
\epsilon
and
D
were arbitrary, we have verified (
D.43
). To show (
D.44
), observe that the law of
(A_{k})_{ij}
, for any
i\neq j
, is equal to that of
q_{k}(\xi)/\sqrt{n}
with
\xi\sim\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}
. By (
D.43
), we have
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert(A_{k})_{ij}\right\rvert}\prec 1/\sqrt{n}
, uniformly over
\left\{i,j\in[n]\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i\neq j\right\}
. Applying the union bound over
\mathcal{O}(d^{2\ell})
entries of
A_{k}
then yields (
D.44
).

Now we prove (
D.45
). The case of
k=0
is trivial, so we assume
k\geq 1
. From the definition in (
4.10
),
r(\xi)=(1-\xi^{2}/d)^{1/2}+\mathcal{O}(1/d)
, and thus

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lvert r(\xi)-1\right\rvert}

\displaystyle\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lvert 1-(1-\xi^{2}/d)^{1/2}\right\rvert}+\mathcal{O}(1/d)

(D.49)

\displaystyle\leq\xi^{2}/d+\mathcal{O}(1/d).

(D.50)

Combining this deterministic bound with the high-probability bound in (
D.43
), we have that, for
\xi_{i}\sim\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}
,

r(\xi_{i})-1=\mathcal{O}_{\prec}(1/d).

(D.51)

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert r^{k}(\xi_{i})-1\right\rvert}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{k}{k\choose{t}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert r(\xi_{i})-1\right\rvert}^{t}.

Applying (
D.51
) and the union bound then allows us to conclude (
D.45
).
∎

Proposition 6
.

Suppose that
n=\mathcal{O}(d^{\ell})
for some fixed
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
(\xi_{i})_{i\in[n]}
be an independent family of random variables with
\xi_{i}\sim\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}
. For every
k,t\in N_{0}
, and every function
\mathcal{E}_{t}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
from the function class defined in (
4.12
), we can write

r^{k}(\xi_{1})r^{k}(\xi_{2})\mathcal{E}_{t}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=f_{1}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})+f_{2}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2}),

(D.52)

where the two functions
f_{1},f_{2}
are such that

f_{1}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\in\mathcal{E}_{t+2\ell}

(D.53)

and

\max_{a,b\in[n]}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert f_{2}(\xi_{a},\xi_{b})\right\rvert}\prec\frac{1}{d\sqrt{n}}.

(D.54)

Proof.

The special case when
k=0
is trivial, as we can simply choose
f_{1}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=\mathcal{E}_{t}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
and
f_{2}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=0
. In what follows we assume
k\geq 1
. For any
\nu\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the
\nu
-th order Taylor expansion of the function
(1-x)^{k/2}
around
x=0
. This allows us to write

(1-x)^{k/2}=P_{\nu}(x)+R_{\nu}(x),

(D.55)

where

P_{\nu}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{\nu-1}\frac{(-1)^{i}(k/2)_{i}}{i!}x^{i},

(D.56)

and
R_{\nu}(x)
is the remainder term. (In (
D.56
),
(k/2)_{i}=(k/2)(k/2-1)\ldots(k/2-i+1)
denotes the falling factorial.) For any
0\leq x\leq 1
, we can verify from the Lagrange form of the the remainder that

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert R_{\nu}(x)\right\rvert}\leq(k/2)_{\nu}\max\left\{(1-x)^{(k/2)_{\nu+1}},1\right\}x^{\nu}.

(D.57)

Now recall the definition of
r(\xi)
in (
4.10
). By using (
D.55
), we can indeed decompose
r^{k}(\xi_{1})r^{k}(\xi_{2})\mathcal{E}_{t}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
into the form of (
D.52
), where

f_{1}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=(1-1/d)^{-k/2}P_{\nu}(\xi_{1}^{2}/d)P_{\nu}(\xi_{2}^{2}/d)\mathcal{E}_{t}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})

and

f_{2}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=(1-1/d)^{-k/2}\big[R_{\nu}(\xi_{1}^{2}/d)P_{\nu}(\xi_{2}^{2}/d)+P_{\nu}(\xi_{1}^{2}/d)R_{\nu}(\xi_{2}^{2}/d)+R_{\nu}(\xi_{1}^{2}/d)R_{\nu}(\xi_{2}^{2}/d)\big]\mathcal{E}_{t}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2}).

Note that
(1-1/d)^{-k/2}=1+\mathcal{O}(1/d)
. For an independent family of random variables
\left\{\xi_{a}\right\}_{a\in[n]}
with
\xi\sim\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}
, we can use (
D.43
) to verify that
\max_{a}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert P_{\nu}(\xi_{a}^{2}/d)\right\rvert}\prec 1
. Similarly, by the definition in (
4.12
) and (
D.43
), we have
\max_{a,b}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathcal{E}_{t}(\xi_{a},\xi_{b})\right\rvert}\prec 1/d
. It follows from these estimates that

\displaystyle\max_{a,b}\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert f_{2}(\xi_{a},\xi_{b})\right\rvert}\prec\frac{1}{d}(1+\max_{a}|{R_{\nu}(\xi_{a}^{2}/d)}|)\max_{a}|{R_{\nu}(\xi_{a}^{2}/d)}|.

(D.58)

Next, we establish a high-probability upper bound for
\max_{a}|{R_{\nu}(\xi_{a}^{2}/d)}|
. For any
a\in[n]
,
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, and
D>0
, and for sufficiently large
d
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|{R_{\nu}(\xi_{a}^{2}/d)}|\geq d^{\epsilon-\nu})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}((1-\xi_{a}^{2}/d)^{(k/2)_{\nu+1}}\xi_{a}^{2\nu}\geq d^{\epsilon/2})+\mathbb{P}(\xi_{a}^{2\nu}\geq d^{\epsilon/2})

(D.59)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}((1-\xi_{a}^{2}/d)^{(k/2)_{\nu+1}}\xi_{a}^{2\nu}\geq d^{\epsilon/2},\xi_{a}^{2}<d/2)+\mathbb{P}(\xi_{a}^{2}\geq d/2)+\mathbb{P}(\xi_{a}^{2\nu}\geq d^{\epsilon/2})

(D.60)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\xi_{a}^{2\nu}\geq\min\{2^{(k/2)_{\nu+1}},1\}d^{\epsilon/2})+\mathbb{P}(\xi_{a}^{2}\geq d/2)+\mathbb{P}(\xi_{a}^{2\nu}\geq d^{\epsilon/2})

(D.61)

\displaystyle\leq d^{-D}.

(D.62)

In the first step, we use the deterministic bound in (
D.57
) and the fact that
(k/2)_{\nu}<d^{\epsilon/2}
for all sufficiently large
d
. The last step follows from the property that
\xi_{a}=\mathcal{O}_{\prec}(1)
. Since the above inequality is uniform in
a\in[n]
, we have
\max_{a}|{R_{\nu}(\xi_{a}^{2}/d)}|\prec d^{-\nu}
. Substituting this bound into (
D.58
) and choosing
\nu=\lceil\ell/2\rceil
, we then reach the statement in (
D.54
).

To show (
D.53
), we use the definition of the Taylor polynomial in (
D.56
), which gives us

f_{1}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=(1-1/d)^{-k/2}\sum_{0\leq i,j\leq\nu-1}c_{i}c_{j}(\xi_{1}^{2}/d)^{i}(\xi_{2}^{2}/d)^{j}\mathcal{E}_{t}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2}),

where
\left\{c_{i}\right\}
are some fixed coefficients. The statement in (
D.53
) then follows from a repeated application of the property in (
B.1c
).
∎

Proposition 7
.

Let
(b_{i})_{i\in[n]}
,
(c_{ij})_{i,j\in[n]}
and
(\xi_{i})_{i\in[n]}
be three independent families of random variables. Moreover,
(\xi_{i})
is i.i.d., with
\xi_{i}\sim\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}
. Suppose that

\big(\textstyle\sum_{i}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert b_{i}\right\rvert}^{2}\big)^{1/2}\prec\Psi_{b}\quad\text{and}\quad\big(\textstyle\sum_{i,j}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert c_{ij}\right\rvert}^{2}\big)^{1/2}\prec\Psi_{c}

(D.63)

for some random variables
\Psi_{b}
and
\Psi_{c}
. Then, for every fixed
L\in\mathbb{N}
, and any
a,b\in[L]
, we have

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\textstyle\sum_{i}b_{i}q_{a}(\xi_{i})\right\rvert}\prec\Psi_{b},

(D.64a)

and

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\textstyle\sum_{i,j}c_{ij}q_{a}(\xi_{i})q_{b}(\xi_{j})-(\sum_{i}c_{ii})\delta_{ab}\right\rvert}\prec\Psi_{c}.

(D.64b)

Proof.

For any
\epsilon>0
,
D>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\textstyle\sum_{i,j}c_{ij}q_{a}(\xi_{i})q_{b}(\xi_{j})-(\sum_{i}c_{ii})\delta_{ab}\right\rvert}\geq d^{\epsilon}\Psi_{c}\Big)

(D.65)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\textstyle\sum_{i,j}c_{ij}q_{a}(\xi_{i})q_{b}(\xi_{j})-(\sum_{i}c_{ii})\delta_{ab}\right\rvert}\geq d^{\epsilon}\Psi_{c}\text{ and }\big(\textstyle\sum_{i,j}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert c_{ij}\right\rvert}^{2}\big)^{1/2}\leq d^{\epsilon/2}\Psi_{c}\Big)

(D.66)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\mathbb{P}\Big(\big(\textstyle\sum_{i,j}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert c_{ij}\right\rvert}^{2}\big)^{1/2}>d^{\epsilon/2}\Psi_{c}\Big)

(D.67)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\textstyle\sum_{i,j}c_{ij}q_{a}(\xi_{i})q_{b}(\xi_{j})-(\sum_{i}c_{ii})\delta_{ab}\right\rvert}\geq d^{\epsilon/2}\Big(\textstyle\sum_{i,j}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert c_{ij}\right\rvert}^{2}\big)^{1/2}\Big)+d^{-(D+1)}

(D.68)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\big(\frac{C(a,b,p)}{d^{\epsilon/2}}\Big)^{p}+d^{-(D+1)}.

(D.69)

In the second step, we have used the definition of the stochastic dominance inequality (
D.63
) with parameters
\epsilon/2
and
D+1
. The last step follows from the Markov inequality and the moment bound (
D.33
). For any
D>0
, there is a large enough
p
such that
\epsilon p/2\geq D+1
. Thus, the right-hand side can be bounded by
d^{-D}
for all sufficiently large
d
. This then established the bound in (
D.64b
).

The proof of (
D.64a
) follows exactly the same arguments. The only difference is that, instead of using the moment bound in (
D.33
), we appeal to the bound in (
D.32a
) and the moment estimate of
q_{a}(\xi)
given in (
D.2
) when applying the Markov inequality. We omit the details.
∎

Appendix E
The Operator Norms of
\left\{A_{k}\right\}

In this appendix, we present some high probability bounds for the operator norms of the matrices
\left\{A_{k}\right\}
defined in (
2.6
).

E.1
The Low-Order Components

We start with the low-order components,
i.e.
, those
A_{k}
with
0\leq k<\ell
.

Lemma 14
.

Suppose that
n=\alpha d^{\ell}+o(d^{\ell})
for some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha>0
. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
k<\ell
, let
\lambda_{1}(A_{k})\geq\lambda_{2}(A_{k})\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{n}(A_{k})
denote the eigenvalues of
A_{k}
in non-increasing order. For all sufficiently large
d
, we have

\max_{i\in[N_{k}]}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\lambda_{i}(A_{k})-\sqrt{n/N_{k}}\right\rvert}=\mathcal{O}_{\prec}(1)

(E.1)

and

\lambda_{i}(A_{k})=-\sqrt{N_{k}/{n}}\qquad\text{for all }i>N_{k}.

(E.2)

Remark 7
.

Recall from (
A.3
) that
N_{k}={d^{k}}/{k!}+\mathcal{O}(d^{k-1})
. Thus, the condition
k<\ell
ensures that
N_{k}\ll n
for all sufficiently large
d
. The lemma asserts that the spectrum of
A_{k}
can be separated into two distinct clusters: the top
N_{k}
eigenvalues reside within an interval of width
\mathcal{O}_{\prec}(1)
, centered around
\sqrt{n/N_{k}}=\sqrt{k!\ratio d^{\ell-k}}(1+o(1))
; meanwhile, the remaining
n-N_{k}
eigenvalues are all equal to
\sqrt{N_{k}/n}={o}(1)
.

Proof.

Consider the factorized representation of
A_{k}
in (
A.7
). Observe that the positive-semidefinite matrix
Y_{k}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}Y_{k}
is rank-deficient. In fact,
\rank(Y_{k}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}Y_{k})=N_{k}\ll n
, which then immediately gives us (
E.2
).

Next, we study the top
N_{k}
eigenvalues of
A_{k}
. In what follows, we use
\lambda_{i}(\boldsymbol{M})
to represent the
i
th largest eigenvalue of any symmetric matrix
\boldsymbol{M}
. By (
A.7
), and for each
i\in[N_{k}]
, we have

\lambda_{i}(A_{k})+\sqrt{N_{k}/n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{nN_{k}}}\lambda_{i}(S_{k}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}S_{k})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{nN_{k}}}\lambda_{i}(S_{k}S_{k}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}).

It follows that

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\lambda_{i}(A_{k})-\sqrt{n/N_{k}}\right\rvert}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{nN_{k}}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\lambda_{i}(S_{k}S_{k}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}-nI)\right\rvert}+\sqrt{N_{k}/n}

and thus

\max_{i\in[N_{k}]}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\lambda_{i}(A_{k})-\sqrt{n/N_{k}}\right\rvert}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{nN_{k}}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert S_{k}S_{k}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}-nI\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}+\mathcal{O}(d^{-(\ell-k)/2}).

(E.3)

The case of
k=0
is straightforward:
S_{0}=(1,1,\ldots,1)
represents a constant row vector of length
n
, and thus
S_{0}S_{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}-n=0
. For
1\leq k<\ell
, we bound the operator norm of
S_{k}S_{k}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}-nI
by using the matrix Bernstein inequality. To that end, we first define a sequence of matrices
\left\{\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}=\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}-I\right\}_{i\in[n]}
where

\boldsymbol{u}_{i}=(Y_{k,1}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}),Y_{k,2}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}),\ldots,Y_{k,N_{k}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}))

is an
N_{k}
-dimensional random vector comprising of spherical harmonics. One can verify from the definition of
S_{k}
in (
A.8
) that

S_{k}S^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}_{k}-nI=\sum_{i\in[n]}\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}.

(E.4)

Since the data vectors
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\sim_{\text{iid}}\mathsf{Unif}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})
, the matrices
\left\{\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}\right\}
are independent. By (
A.4
),
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
is an isotropic random vector and thus
\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}=\boldsymbol{0}
. Moreover, (
A.6
) implies that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\right\rVert}=\sqrt{N_{k}}
. It follows that

K\coloneqq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}=N_{k}-1

(E.5)

and

\sigma^{2}\coloneqq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}^{2}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{E}[(N_{k}-2)\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}+I]\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}=n(N_{k}-1).

(E.6)

For every
t>0
, the standard matrix Bernstein inequality
[
38
]
gives us

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{nN_{k}}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n]}\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}\geq t\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2N_{k}\exp\Big(-\frac{nN_{k}t^{2}/2}{\sigma^{2}+K\sqrt{nN_{k}}(t/3)}\Big)

(E.7)

\displaystyle\leq 2N_{k}\exp\Big(-\frac{t^{2}/2}{1+\sqrt{N_{k}/n}(t/3)}\Big),

(E.8)

where to reach the second inequality we have used the expressions in (
E.5
) and (
E.6
). For every
0<\epsilon<1/2
, let
t=d^{\epsilon}
. Since
N_{k}=\mathcal{O}(d^{k})
with
k<\ell
, we have
\sqrt{N_{k}/n}(t/3)<1
for all sufficiently large
d
. It follows that, for every
D>0
,

\mathbb{P}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{nN_{k}}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n]}\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}\geq t\Big)\leq d^{-D}

for sufficiently large
d
, and thus
\frac{1}{\sqrt{nN_{k}}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n]}\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}=\mathcal{O}_{\prec}(1)
. The statement (
E.1
) of the lemma then follows from (
E.4
) and (
E.3
).
∎

E.2
Moment Calculations for the High-Order Components

Next, we bound the operator norms of the high-order matrices,
i.e.
, those
A_{k}
with
k\geq\ell
. The approach taken in our proof of Lemma
14
, which is based on the matrix Bernstein inequality, can be modified to show that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A_{\ell}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}=\mathcal{O}_{\prec}(1)
, but it does not seem to yield useful results for cases when
k>\ell
. In what follows, we control the operator norm of
A_{k}
by using the standard moment method. Observe that, for each
\epsilon>0
and
p\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\,\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A_{k}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}\geq d^{\epsilon})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\,\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A_{k}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}^{2p}\geq d^{2\epsilon p})

(E.9)

\displaystyle\leq d^{-2\epsilon p}\,\mathbb{E}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A_{k}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}^{2p}

(E.10)

\displaystyle\leq d^{-2\epsilon p}\,\mathbb{E}\tr(A_{k}^{2p}).

(E.11)

Thus, to control the probability on the left-hand side, it suffices to bound
\mathbb{E}\tr(A_{k}^{2p})
for a sufficiently large integer
p
. Bounds for the moments have been previously studied in the literature.
Cheng and Singer 2013
provides a bound for the 4th moment
\mathbb{E}\tr(A_{k}^{4})
. For general
p\in\mathbb{N}
,
Ghorbani et al. 2020
showed that

\mathbb{E}\tr(A_{k}^{2p})\leq(Cp)^{3p}n(1+d^{p(k-\ell)+\ell-2k})

(E.12)

for some positive constant
C
. By substituting (
E.12
) into (
E.11
), we observe that, when
k>\ell
, this moment bound is not sufficient to make (
E.11
) smaller than
d^{-D}
for every
D>0
. As a result, we cannot use (
E.12
) to show
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A_{k}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}\prec 1
. In this work, we adapt the moment calculations from
[
20
]
and derive a new bound for
\mathbb{E}\tr(A_{k}^{2P})
that is tight up to its leading order (see Lemma
17
).

As in
[
20
]
, our moment calculations hinge on the following property of Gegenbauer polynomials.

Lemma 15
.

Let
\boldsymbol{z}\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})
. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and any deterministic vectors
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}
in
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}}q_{k}(\sqrt{d}\boldsymbol{x}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{z})q_{k}(\sqrt{d}\boldsymbol{z}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}q_{k}(\sqrt{d}\boldsymbol{x}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{y}).

(E.13)

Proof.

Using (
A.5
), we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}}q_{k}(\sqrt{d}\boldsymbol{x}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{z})q_{k}(\sqrt{d}\boldsymbol{z}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{a,b\in[N_{k}]}Y_{k,a}(\boldsymbol{x})Y_{k,b}(\boldsymbol{y})\big[\mathbb{E}Y_{k,a}(\boldsymbol{z})Y_{k,b}(\boldsymbol{z})\big]

(E.14)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{a,b\in[N_{k}]}Y_{k,a}(\boldsymbol{x})Y_{k,b}(\boldsymbol{y})\delta_{ab}\qquad[\text{by }\eqref{eq:sh_ortho}]

(E.15)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{a\in[N_{k}]}Y_{k,a}(\boldsymbol{x})Y_{k,a}(\boldsymbol{y}).

(E.16)

Applying (
A.5
) one more time then gives us (
E.13
).
∎

Lemma 16
.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\sim_{\text{iid}}\mathsf{Unif}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
i,j\in[n]
, write

Q_{ij}\coloneqq q_{k}(\sqrt{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}).

(E.17)

Then, for every length
2p
sequence of indices
\boldsymbol{i}=[i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{2p}]\in[n]^{2p}
, we have

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathbb{E}\,Q_{i_{1}i_{2}}Q_{i_{2}i_{3}}\ldots Q_{i_{2p-1}i_{2p}}Q_{i_{2p}i_{1}}\right\rvert}\leq[\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}}q_{k}^{2p}(\xi)]N_{k}^{p-\nu(\boldsymbol{i})+1},

(E.18)

where
\nu(\boldsymbol{i})\coloneqq\#\left\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{2p}\right\}
is the number of distinct indices in
\boldsymbol{i}
, and
\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}
is the probability measure defined in (
1.3
).

Remark 8
.

This lemma is essentially a restatement of
[
20
, Lemma 3]
, after one adjusts for the different definitions and scalings used in that work and this paper. For the sake of readability and completeness, we present the results in a form that is more convenient for our subsequent arguments, and provide a slightly simplified proof below.

Proof.

We can view the product
Q_{i_{1}i_{2}}Q_{i_{2}i_{3}}\ldots Q_{i_{2p-1}i_{2p}}Q_{i_{2p}i_{1}}
graphically, as a length
2p
cycle on the vertex set
[n]
. First, consider two special cases: (I)
\nu(\boldsymbol{i})=1
,
i.e.
, all the indices are identical. Recall from (
A.6
) that

Q_{ii}=\sqrt{N_{k}}\quad\text{for all }i.

(E.19)

Thus

\mathbb{E}Q_{i_{1}i_{2}}Q_{i_{2}i_{3}}\ldots Q_{i_{2p},i_{1}}=Q_{i_{1}i_{1}}^{2p}=N_{k}^{p}.

(E.20)

(II)
\nu(\boldsymbol{i})=2p
,
i.e.
, all the indices are distinct. Mapping the indices to the canonical set
[2p]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Q_{12}Q_{23}Q_{34}\ldots Q_{2p,1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{3},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{2p}}\Big([\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{2}}Q_{12}Q_{23}]Q_{34}\ldots Q_{2p,1}\Big)

(E.21)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{3},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{2p}}(Q_{13}Q_{34}\ldots Q_{2p,1}),

(E.22)

where the last step follows from (
E.13
).
The above reduction procedure can be applied repeatedly: at each step, it reduces the length of the cycle by
1
and produces an extra factor of
1/\sqrt{N_{k}}
. Doing this for
2p-1
times then gives us

\mathbb{E}Q_{12}Q_{23}\ldots Q_{2p,1}=N_{k}^{-(2p-1)/2}\mathbb{E}Q_{11}=N_{k}^{1-p},

(E.23)

where the last equality is due to (
E.19
). Note that, since the Gegenbauer polynomials are normalized [recall (
2.2
)], we have

\mathbb{E}q_{k}^{2p}(\xi)\geq(\mathbb{E}q_{k}^{2}(\xi))^{p}=1.

(E.24)

Thus, with (
E.20
) and (
E.23
), we have verified the bound in (
E.18
) for the cases of
\nu(\boldsymbol{i})=1
and
\nu(\boldsymbol{i})=2p
, respectively.

In fact, the procedures leading to (
E.20
) and (
E.23
) are special cases of a general and systematic reduction process. Suppose we are given a length
t
cycle on indices
\boldsymbol{i}=(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{t})
. To simplify the notation, define

M(\boldsymbol{i})=\mathbb{E}\,Q_{i_{1}i_{2}}Q_{i_{2}i_{3}}\ldots Q_{i_{t-1}i_{t}}Q_{i_{t}i_{1}}.

(E.25)

We now introduce two reduction mappings, each of which converts a length
t
cycle to a length
(t-1)
cycle.

Type-A reduction
: Given an index sequence
\boldsymbol{i}=(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{t})
for
t\geq 2
. Suppose there exists at least one
j\in[t]
such that
i_{j}\neq i_{a}
for all
a\in[t]\setminus\left\{j\right\}
. In other words, the index
i_{j}
appears exactly once in the cycle. (It is possible that there are more than one such “singleton” indices in the sequence, in which case we will choose any one of them.) We remove
i_{j}
from the original sequence
\boldsymbol{i}
, and call the resulting length
t-1
sequence
R_{A}(\boldsymbol{i})
. More precisely,

R_{A}(\boldsymbol{i})=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{j-1},i_{j+1},\ldots,i_{t})

where the indices
j-1
and
j+1
are interpreted modulo
[t]
. Using the same conditioning technique that leads to (
E.22
), we can easily verify that

M(\boldsymbol{i})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}M(R_{A}(\boldsymbol{i})).

(E.26)

Thus, a type-A reduction step
R_{A}(\boldsymbol{i})
reduces the cycle length by 1 and contributes a factor of
1/\sqrt{N_{k}}
.

Type-B reduction
: Given an index sequence
\boldsymbol{i}=(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{t})
for
t\geq 2
. Suppose there exists at least one
j\in[t]
such that
i_{j+1}=i_{j}
, where
j+1
is to be interpreted modulo
[t]
. (If more than one such indices exist, we choose any one of them.) We define

R_{B}(\boldsymbol{i})=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{j-1},i_{j+1},\ldots,i_{t})

as a length
t-1
sequence obtained by removing
i_{j}
from
\boldsymbol{i}
. By (
E.19
), we must have

M(\boldsymbol{i})=\sqrt{N_{k}}M(R_{B}(\boldsymbol{i})).

(E.27)

Thus, a type-B reduction step
R_{B}(\boldsymbol{i})
reduces the cycle length by 1 and contributes a factor of
\sqrt{N_{k}}
.

Given an index sequence
\boldsymbol{i}=(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{t})
, we can simplify it by iteratively using the Type-A and Type-B reductions, until it cannot be further simplified. Clearly, this process stops after a finite number of steps. Let
\bar{\boldsymbol{i}}=(\bar{i}_{1},\bar{i}_{2},\ldots,\bar{i}_{{t}(\bar{\boldsymbol{i}})})
denote the final outcome of the iterative reduction process.

Example 1
.

As an illustration, let us consider two concrete index sequences and demonstrate how they can be simplified though the above process.

(a)

\boldsymbol{i}=(1,1,2,2,2,3)
: We can start by using a Type-A step to remove the “singleton” 3. Then, the “redundant” copies of
1
and
2
can be removed by applying the Type-B step three times. This then gives us a shorter sequence
(1,2)
, in which both
1
and
2
are singletons. Removing
1
by a Type-A step gives us the final output
\bar{\boldsymbol{i}}=(2)
.

(b)

\boldsymbol{i}=(1,2,1,2,2,3)
: Removing the “singleton” 3 (via Type-A reduction) and one redundant copy of
2
(via Type-B reduction), we get
\bar{\boldsymbol{i}}=(1,2,1,2)
, which cannot be further simplified.

By (
E.26
) and (
E.27
), we must have

M(\boldsymbol{i})=N_{k}^{(n_{B}-n_{A})/2}M(\bar{\boldsymbol{i}}),

(E.28)

where
n_{A}
(resp.
n_{B}
) is the total number of Type-A (resp. Type-B) steps used in the reduction process that leads to the final outcome
\bar{\boldsymbol{i}}
. Denote by
t(\boldsymbol{i})
and
\nu(\boldsymbol{i})
the length and number of unique indices in
\boldsymbol{i}
, respectively. We also define
t(\bar{\boldsymbol{i}})
and
\nu(\bar{\boldsymbol{i}})
in the same way for the simplified sequence
\bar{\boldsymbol{i}}
. By construction, we must have

n_{A}+n_{B}=t(\boldsymbol{i})-t(\bar{\boldsymbol{i}}).

One can also check that

n_{A}=\nu(\boldsymbol{i})-\nu(\bar{\boldsymbol{i}}).

Substituting these two identities into (
E.28
) then gives us

M(\boldsymbol{i})=N_{k}^{(t(\boldsymbol{i})-2\nu(\boldsymbol{i})+2\nu(\bar{\boldsymbol{i}})-t(\bar{\boldsymbol{i}}))/2}M(\bar{\boldsymbol{i}}),

(E.29)

Again, by examining the constructions of the Type-A and Type-B reduction steps, we can conclude that there can only be two possibilities for the final output
\bar{\boldsymbol{i}}
:

Case 1
:
t(\bar{\boldsymbol{i}})=\nu(\bar{\boldsymbol{i}})=1
,
i.e.
,
\bar{i}=(\bar{i}_{1})
is a length 1 cycle. Recall from (
E.19
) that
M(\bar{\boldsymbol{i}})=\sqrt{N_{k}}
. It then follows from (
E.29
) that

M(\boldsymbol{i})=N_{k}^{(t(\boldsymbol{i})-2\nu(\boldsymbol{i})+2)/2}.

By applying this identity to a sequence of length
t(\boldsymbol{i})=2p
and recalling (
E.24
), we reach the statement in (
E.18
).

Case 2
: The only other possibility is for
\bar{\boldsymbol{i}}
to contain at least two unique indices,
i.e.
,
\nu(\bar{\boldsymbol{i}})\geq 2
. Moreover, every unique index must appear at least twice in the cycle, as otherwise the sequence
\bar{\boldsymbol{i}}
can be further simplified by a Type-A step. These two conditions imply
t(\bar{\boldsymbol{i}})\geq 2\nu(\bar{\boldsymbol{i}})
, in which case (
E.28
) gives us

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert M(\boldsymbol{i})\right\rvert}\leq N_{k}^{(t(\boldsymbol{i})-2\nu(\boldsymbol{i}))/2}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert M(\bar{\boldsymbol{i}})\right\rvert}.

(E.30)

Moreover, consecutive indices in
\bar{\boldsymbol{i}}=(\bar{i}_{1},\bar{i}_{2},\ldots,\bar{i}_{t(\bar{\boldsymbol{i}})})
cannot have repetitions,
i.e.
,
\bar{i}_{j}\neq\bar{i}_{j+1}
for all
j\in[t(\bar{\boldsymbol{i}})]
with
j+1
to be interpreted as modulo
t(\bar{\boldsymbol{i}})
. (If this were not true, then
\bar{\boldsymbol{i}}
would not be the final output as it could be further simplified by a Type-B step.) This condition allows us to apply Hölder’s inequality to get

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert M(\bar{\boldsymbol{i}})\right\rvert}=\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathbb{E}Q_{\bar{i}_{1}\bar{i}_{2}}Q_{\bar{i}_{2}\bar{i}_{3}}\ldots Q_{\bar{i}_{t(\bar{\boldsymbol{i}})}i_{1}}\right\rvert}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert q_{k}(\xi)\right\rvert}^{t(\bar{\boldsymbol{i}})}.

(E.31)

Now consider a sequence
\boldsymbol{i}
with length
t(\boldsymbol{i})=2p
. Since
t(\bar{\boldsymbol{i}})\leq 2p
, we can use Hölder’s inequality to further bound the right-hand side as

\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert q_{k}(\xi)\right\rvert}^{t(\bar{\boldsymbol{i}})}\leq[\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}}q_{k}^{2p}(\xi)]^{t(\bar{\boldsymbol{i}})/(2p)}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}}q_{k}^{2p}(\xi),

(E.32)

where the last step follows from (
E.24
),
i.e.
,
\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}}q_{k}^{2p}\geq 1
. By substituting (
E.31
) and (
E.32
) into (
E.30
), we can verify the bound in (
E.18
).
∎

Lemma 17
.

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
p\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\tr(A_{k}^{2p})\leq n(2p)^{2p-2}\big[p/n+\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}({n}/{N_{k}})^{i}\big]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}}q_{k}^{2p}(\xi).

(E.33)

Proof.

Using the shorthand notation in (
E.17
), we can write
A_{k}(i,j)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}Q_{ij}\cdot(1-\delta_{ij})
for
i,j\in[n]
. Let

\mathcal{C}\coloneqq\left\{\boldsymbol{i}=(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{2p})\in[n]^{2p}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i_{j}\neq i_{j+1}\text{ for all }j\in[2p-1]\text{ and }i_{1}\neq i_{2p}\right\}

denote the set of all length
2p
cycles in which no two consecutive indices are equal. We then have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\tr(A_{k}^{2p})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{p}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathbb{E}\,Q_{i_{1}i_{2}}Q_{i_{2}i_{3}}\ldots Q_{i_{2p-1}i_{2p}}Q_{i_{2p}i_{1}}

(E.34)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{p}}\Big(\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}M(\boldsymbol{i})+\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{2}}M(\boldsymbol{i})+\ldots+\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{2p}}M(\boldsymbol{i})\Big),

(E.35)

where, for each
a\in[2p]
,

\mathcal{C}_{a}\coloneqq\mathcal{C}\cap\left\{\boldsymbol{i}\in[n]^{2p}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\nu(\boldsymbol{i})=a\right\}

and
M(\boldsymbol{i})=\mathbb{E}\,Q_{i_{1}i_{2}}Q_{i_{2}i_{3}}\ldots Q_{i_{2p-1}i_{2p}}Q_{i_{2p}i_{1}}
. The terms
M(\boldsymbol{i})
in (
E.35
) can be bounded in two different ways. First, recall that, for any
i_{j}\neq i_{j+1}
, we have
Q_{i_{j}i_{j+1}}\sim q_{k}(\xi)
with
\xi\sim\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}
. Thus, for every
\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathcal{C}
, we can apply Hölder’s inequality to get

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert M(\boldsymbol{i})\right\rvert}\leq\mathbb{E}q_{k}^{2p}(\xi).

(E.36)

On the other hand, for each
a\in[2p]
, Lemma
16
gives us

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert M(\boldsymbol{i})\right\rvert}\leq\mathbb{E}q_{k}^{2p}(\xi)N_{k}^{p-a+1}\quad\text{for all }\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{a}.

(E.37)

We use (
E.36
) to bound the
M(\boldsymbol{i})
terms in
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{p+1}
and use (
E.37
) for the
M(\boldsymbol{i})
terms in
\mathcal{C}_{p+2},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{2p}
. It then follows from (
E.35
) that

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathbb{E}\tr(A_{k}^{2p})\right\rvert}\leq\frac{1}{n^{p}}\Big(\sum_{a=1}^{p+1}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathcal{C}_{a}\right\rvert}+\sum_{a=p+2}^{2p}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathcal{C}_{a}\right\rvert}N_{k}^{p-a+1}\Big)\mathbb{E}q_{k}^{2p}(\xi),

(E.38)

where
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathcal{C}_{a}\right\rvert}
denotes the cardinality of
\mathcal{C}_{a}
. For each
a\in[2p]
, we have

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathcal{C}_{a}\right\rvert}\leq a^{2p-2}n(n-1)\ldots(n-a+1)\leq(2p)^{2p-2}n^{a}.

This is a crude bound, but it is sufficient for our purpose. Substituting this bound into (
E.38
) give us

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathbb{E}\tr(A_{k}^{2p})\right\rvert}\leq(2p)^{2p-2}\Big(\sum_{a=1}^{p+1}n^{a-p}+n\sum_{a=p+2}^{2p}({n}/{N_{k}})^{a-p-1}\Big)\mathbb{E}q_{k}^{2p}(\xi),

which then implies (
E.33
).
∎

E.3
Bounds on the Operator Norms

Proposition 8
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
1
, we have, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A_{k}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}\prec d^{\max\{\ell-k,0\}/2}.

(E.39)

Proof.

The statement of the proposition means that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A_{k}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}\prec 1
for all the high-order components with
k\geq\ell
. In contrast, for the low-order components,
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A_{k}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}\prec d^{(\ell-k)/2}
when
0\leq k<\ell
.

To show (
E.39
) for the case of
k\geq\ell
, suppose that we are given an arbitrary
\epsilon>0
. Substituting (
E.33
) into (
E.11
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\,\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A_{k}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}\geq d^{\epsilon})

\displaystyle\leq d^{-2\epsilon p}\,n(2p)^{2p-2}\big[p/n+\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}({n}/{N_{k}})^{i}\big]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathsf{s}_{d}^{1}}q_{k}^{2p}(\xi)

(E.40)

Recall that
n=\alpha d^{\ell}+o(d^{\ell})
and
k\geq\ell
. In addition, we have
N_{k}=\frac{d^{k}}{k!}(1+\mathcal{O}(1/d))
. Thus, for sufficiently large
d
, we have, by Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}(\,\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A_{k}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}\geq d^{\epsilon})\leq C(\alpha,k,p)d^{\ell-2\epsilon p},

where
C(\alpha,k,p)
is some constant that depends on
\alpha,k,p
but not on
d
. For any
D>0
, choose a
p
such that
\ell-2\epsilon p<-D-1
. It follows that

\mathbb{P}(\,\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A_{k}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}\geq d^{\epsilon})\leq d^{-D}

for all sufficiently large
d
.

Next, we consider the low-order components, with
0\leq k<\ell
. Lemma
14
implies that

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}\leq\sqrt{n/N_{k}}+\mathcal{O}_{\prec}(1)\prec d^{(\ell-k)/2},

(E.41)

where the second step uses the fact that
n=\alpha n^{\ell}+o(n^{\ell})
and
N_{k}=d^{k}/k!(1+\mathcal{O}(1/d))
.
∎

Appendix F
Perturbations of Resolvents and Stieltjes Transforms

In this appendix, we recall some standard properties of the resolvent and the Stieltjes transform of Hermitian matrices. Let
H
be an
n\times n
Hermitian matrix. Its resolvent is denoted by
G(z)\coloneqq(H-zI)^{-1}
, where
z\in\mathbb{C}
is the spectral parameter with
\eta=\mathfrak{Im}[z]>0
. From the trivial inequality

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\frac{1}{\lambda-z}\right\rvert}\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\qquad\text{for all }\lambda\in\mathbb{R},

one can easily verify the following bounds on the operator and the entry-wise
\ell_{\infty}
norms of
G(z)
:

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert G(z)\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\eta}

(F.1)

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert G(z)\right\rVert}_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\eta}.

(F.2)

In our proof, we will also need the
Ward identity

[
16
, Lemma 8.3]
:

\sum_{ij}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert G_{ij}(z)\right\rvert}^{2}=\frac{\mathfrak{Im}(\tr G(z))}{\eta}.

(F.3)

Let
s(z)
be the Stieltjes transform of the empirical spectral distribution of
H
. Since
s(z)=\frac{1}{n}\tr G(z)
, (
F.2
) immediately implies the pointwise bound

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s(z)\right\rvert}\leq\frac{1}{\eta}.

(F.4)

One can also easily check that

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s(z_{1})-s(z_{2})\right\rvert}\leq\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert z_{1}-z_{2}\right\rvert}}{\mathfrak{Im}(z_{1})\mathfrak{Im}(z_{2})}.

(F.5)

Both the resolvent and the Stieltjes transform are stable with respect to matrix perturbations. In our proof of Theorem
1
, we will use the following standard perturbation estimates.

Lemma 18
.

Let
H_{1},H_{2}
be
n\times n
Hermitian matrices, and
G_{1}(z),G_{2}(z)
their resolvents. Then,

G_{1}(z)=G_{2}(z)-G_{2}(z)(H_{1}-H_{2})G_{1}(z).

(F.6)

Moreover, for the Stieltjes transforms of
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s_{1}(z)-s_{2}(z)\right\rvert}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert H_{1}-H_{2}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{F}}}{\sqrt{n}\,\eta^{2}}\leq\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert H_{1}-H_{2}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}}{\eta^{2}}

(F.7)

and

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s_{1}(z)-s_{2}(z)\right\rvert}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\rank(H_{1}-H_{2})}{n\cdot\eta},

(F.8)

where
C
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

The formula in (
F.6
) can be easily verified by using the identities
I=(H_{1}-zI)G_{1}=(H_{2}-zI+H_{1}-H2)G_{1}
and
G_{2}(H_{2}-zI)=I
. Applying (
F.6
) gives us

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s_{1}(z)-s_{2}(z)\right\rvert}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\tr[G_{2}(z)(H_{1}-H_{2})G_{1}(z)]\right\rvert}

(F.9)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\tr[G_{1}(z)G_{2}(z)(H_{1}-H_{2})]\right\rvert}

(F.10)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert G_{1}(z)G_{2}(z)\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{F}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert H_{1}-H_{2}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{F}},

(F.11)

where the last step uses the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. By using (
F.1
) in the last step, we have

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert G_{1}(z)G_{2}(z)\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{F}}\leq\sqrt{n}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert G_{1}(z)G_{2}(z)\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}\leq\sqrt{n}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert G_{1}(z)\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert G_{1}(z)\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\eta^{2}}.

Substituting this bound into (
F.11
) then leads to the first inequality in (
F.7
). The second inequality in (
F.7
) then follows immediately from the fact that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert H_{1}-H_{2}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{F}}\leq\sqrt{n}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert H_{1}-H_{2}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}
.

Next, we show (
F.8
). First consider the special case when
\rank(H_{1}-H_{2})=1
. As the eigenvalues are invariant under unitary transforms, we can assume without loss of generality that

H_{1}-H_{2}=a\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}},

(F.12)

for some
a\neq 0
. Let
H_{1}^{[1]}
(resp.
H_{2}^{[1]}
) denote the
(n-1)\times(n-1)
minor matrix obtained by removing the first column and row of
H_{1}
(resp.
H_{2}
). By (
F.12
),
H_{1}^{[1]}=H_{2}^{[1]}
. Let
s^{[1]}(z)
denote the Stieltjes transform of the eigenvalues of
H_{1}^{[1]}
. We have

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s_{1}(z)-s_{2}(z)\right\rvert}\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s_{1}(z)-s^{[1]}(z)\right\rvert}+\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s^{[1]}(z)-s_{2}(z)\right\rvert}\leq\frac{C}{n\cdot\eta},

(F.13)

where the second step uses
[
16
, Lemma 7.5]
, and
C
is an absolute constant. For the case when
\rank(H_{1}-H_{2})>1
, we can always write
s_{1}(z)-s_{2}(z)
as a telescoping sum of rank-one perturbations. The inequality in (
F.8
) can then be obtained by applying the triangular inequality and by using the result for the rank-one case.
∎

Proof of Lemma
2
.

By using the factorized representation of
A_{k}
in (
A.7
), we can write the low-order term as

A_{\mathrm{LO}}=\sum_{0\leq k<\ell}\frac{\mu_{k}}{\sqrt{nN_{k}}}S_{k}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}S_{k}-\sigma_{d}I,

(F.14)

where
S_{k}
is an
N_{k}\times n
matrix, with
N_{k}=\mathcal{O}(d^{k})
, and
\sigma_{d}\coloneqq\sum_{0\leq k<\ell}\mu_{k}\sqrt{N_{k}/n}
. Since
n\asymp d^{\ell}
, we have
\sigma_{d}=\mathcal{O}(d^{-1/2})
and that

\rank(A_{\mathrm{LO}}+\sigma_{d}I)\leq\sum_{0\leq k<\ell}N_{k}=\mathcal{O}(d^{\ell-1}).

Write
\breve{A}\coloneqq A+\sigma_{d}I=(A_{\mathrm{LO}}+\sigma_{d}I)+A_{\mathrm{HI}}
, whose Stieltjes transform is denoted by
\breve{s}(z)
. By the triangular inequality,

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s(z)-\hat{s}(z)\right\rvert}

\displaystyle\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s(z)-\breve{s}(z)\right\rvert}+\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\breve{s}(z)-\hat{s}(z)\right\rvert}

(F.16)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sigma_{d}}{\eta^{2}}+\frac{C\rank(\breve{A}-A_{\mathrm{HI}})}{n\cdot\eta},

(F.17)

where the last step follows from the perturbation inequalities in (
F.7
) and (
F.8
). By substituting the estimates
\sigma_{d}=\mathcal{O}(d^{-1/2})
and (

We conclude this appendix by establishing a general comparison inequality that will be utilized in our proofs for both Theorem
2
and Theorem
3
. Given that the former focuses on data vectors sampled from the spherical distribution, while the latter pertains to isotropic Gaussian data vectors, we present a general model in the following lemma that can accommodate both scenarios.

Lemma 19
.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a set of independent and identically distributed vectors sampled from a probability distribution
\pi
. Consider a random matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with entries

A_{ij}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\,f_{d}(\sqrt{d}\,\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}_{j})&\text{if }i\neq j\\
0&\text{if }i=j\end{cases},

(F.18)

where
f_{d}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is a function. Let
\hat{A}
be another matrix constructed in the same way as in (
F.18
), but with
f_{d}
replaced by a different function
\hat{f}_{d}
. For
z\in C_{+}
with
\eta=\mathfrak{Im}[z]
, let
s(z)
and
\hat{s}(z)
denote the Stieltjes transforms of
A
and
\hat{A}
, respectively. Then, we have

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s(z)-\hat{s}(z)\right\rvert}\leq\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}\big[\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}}\big(f_{d}(\sqrt{d}\,\boldsymbol{x}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{y})-\hat{f}_{d}(\sqrt{d}\,\boldsymbol{x}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{y})\big)^{2}\big]^{1/2}+\mathcal{O}_{\prec}\Big(\frac{1}{\eta\sqrt{n}}\Big),

(F.19)

where
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are two independent vectors sampled from the distribution
\pi
.

Proof.

By the triangular inequality,

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s(z)-\hat{s}(z)\right\rvert}

\displaystyle\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathbb{E}s(z)-\mathbb{E}\hat{s}(z)\right\rvert}+\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s(z)-\mathbb{E}s(z)\right\rvert}+\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\hat{s}(z)-\mathbb{E}\hat{s}(z)\right\rvert}

(F.20)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s(z)-\hat{s}(z)\right\rvert}+\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s(z)-\mathbb{E}s(z)\right\rvert}+\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\hat{s}(z)-\mathbb{E}\hat{s}(z)\right\rvert}.

(F.21)

Next, we bound each term on the right-hand side. For the first term, we apply the perturbation estimate (
F.7
) in Lemma
18
and Hölder’s inequality, which result in the following expression:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s(z)-\hat{s}(z)\right\rvert}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\eta^{2}}\mathbb{E}\|A-\hat{A}\|_{\mathrm{F}}

(F.22)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\eta^{2}}(\mathbb{E}\|A-\hat{A}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2})^{1/2}

(F.23)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}\big[\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}}\big(f_{d}(\sqrt{d}\boldsymbol{x}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{y})-\hat{f}_{d}(\sqrt{d}\boldsymbol{x}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{y})\big)^{2}\big]^{1/2}.

(F.24)

To reach the last step, we have used the fact that, for any
i,j\in[n]
with
i\neq j
, the probability distribution of
\sqrt{n}(A_{ij}-\hat{A}_{ij})
equals to that of
f_{d}(\sqrt{d}\,\boldsymbol{x}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{y})-\hat{f}_{d}(\sqrt{d}\,\boldsymbol{x}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{y})
, for two independent vectors
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}
sampled from the distribution
\pi
.

The second and the third terms on the right-hand side of (
F.21
) can be bounded by standard concentration arguments in random matrix theory. One approach is to view
s(z)
and
\hat{s}(z)
, for fixed
z
, as functions of the independent vectors
\left\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right\}_{i\in[n]}
. It is straightforward to verify that these functions have bounded variations. This then allows one to apply McDiarmid’s inequality to bound the deviation of
s(z)
and
\hat{s}(z)
from their corresponding expectations. Another approach, as used in
[
9
]
, is to apply the Burkholder’s inequality
[
2
, Lemma 2.12]
to directly bound the moments of the difference between
s(z),\hat{s}(z)
and their corresponding expectations. By
[
9
, Lemma 2.4]
, for any
p\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathbb{E}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s(z)-\mathbb{E}s(z)\right\rvert}^{2p}\leq\frac{C_{p}}{\eta^{2p}n^{p}},

where
C_{p}
is a constant that depends on
p
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, applying Markov’s inequality gives us

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s(z)-\mathbb{E}s(z)\right\rvert}\geq\frac{d^{\varepsilon}}{\eta\sqrt{n}}\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s(z)-\mathbb{E}s(z)\right\rvert}^{2p}\geq\frac{d^{2\varepsilon p}}{\eta^{2p}n^{p}}\big)\leq C_{p}d^{-2\varepsilon p}.

(F.25)

For any
D>0
, we can always find a
p
such that the right-hand side of the above inequality is less than

for all sufficiently large
d
. It follows that

s(z)=\mathbb{E}s(z)+\mathcal{O}_{\prec}\Big(\frac{1}{\eta\sqrt{n}}\Big).

(F.26)

Using the same steps, we can also show:

\hat{s}(z)=\mathbb{E}\hat{s}(z)+\mathcal{O}_{\prec}\Big(\frac{1}{\eta\sqrt{n}}\Big).

(F.27)

By substituting (
F.24
), (
F.26
), and (
F.27
) into (
F.21
), we complete the proof.
∎

Appendix G
Stability Analysis of the Self-Consistent Equation

In this appendix, we present a stability analysis of the self-consistent equation in (
2.10
) satisfied by the limiting Stieltjes transform. We start by rewriting (
2.10
) as

\frac{1}{m}=-z-\frac{\gamma_{a}m}{1+\gamma_{b}m}-\gamma_{c}m.

(G.1)

Now suppose
s\in C_{+}
is an approximate solution to (
G.1
) such that

\frac{1}{s}=-z-\frac{\gamma_{a}s}{1+\gamma_{b}s}-\gamma_{c}s+\omega,

(G.2)

with some error term
\omega
. We show in the following proposition that
s\approx m
as long as the error
\omega
is small.

Proposition 9
.

For any
z\in C_{+}
, there exists a unique solution
m\in C_{+}
to (
G.1
). Moreover, let
s\in C_{+}
be an approximate solution to (
2.10
) in the sense of (
G.2
), with the error term satisfying

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\omega\right\rvert}\leq\frac{\eta}{2},\qquad\eta=\mathfrak{Im}[z].

(G.3)

We have

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s-m\right\rvert}\leq\frac{4\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\omega\right\rvert}}{\eta^{2}}.

(G.4)

Proof.

The existence and uniqueness of the solution
m\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
is a well-known result. One way to establish this is to observe that
(
G.1
)
is just the free additive convolution of the Marchenko-Pastur law and the semicircle law. See,
e.g.
,
[
4
]
for more details. An alternative approach, as used in
[
9
, Lemma A.1]
, is to rewrite (
G.1
) as a third-degree polynomial in
m
and to analyze the roots of that polynomial.

Next, we establish the stability bound in (
G.4
). Recall the family of MP density functions given in (
C.6
), with two shape parameters
\phi
and
t
. In what follows, we set
\phi=\gamma_{a}/\gamma_{b}^{2}
and
t=\gamma_{b}
, and write
\varrho_{\mathrm{MP}}\coloneqq\varrho_{\phi,t}
. The Stieltjes transform of
\varrho_{\mathrm{MP}}
is specified as the unique solution to (
C.5
).
Substituting our particular choices of
\phi
and
t
into (
C.5
), and by the uniqueness of the solution, we obtain the following characterization: for any
z,\Gamma\in C_{+}
,

\frac{1}{\Gamma}=-z-\frac{\gamma_{a}\Gamma}{1+\gamma_{b}\Gamma}\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad\Gamma=\int\frac{\varrho_{\mathrm{MP}}(\dif x)}{x-z}.

(G.5)

Using (
G.5
) and (
G.1
), we can now write the exact solution

as

m=\int\frac{\varrho_{\mathrm{MP}}(\dif x)}{x-z-\gamma_{c}m}.

(G.6)

By the assumption in (
G.3
),
\mathfrak{Im}[z+\gamma_{c}s-\omega]\geq\eta/2
. Thus, it follows from (
G.5
) and (
G.2
) that

s=\int\frac{\varrho_{\mathrm{MP}}(\dif x)}{x-z-\gamma_{c}s+\omega}.

(G.7)

Computing the imaginary part of the equation for

, we get

\mathfrak{Im}[m]=\int\frac{\eta+\gamma_{c}\mathfrak{Im}[m]}{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x-z-\gamma_{c}m\right\rvert}^{2}}\,\varrho_{\mathrm{MP}}(\dif x),

(G.8)

which also gives us the trivial bound
\mathfrak{Im}[m]\leq 1/\eta
. Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Im}[s]

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\eta+\gamma_{c}\mathfrak{Im}[s]-\mathfrak{Im}[\omega]}{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x-z-\gamma_{c}s+\omega\right\rvert}^{2}}\,\varrho_{\mathrm{MP}}(\dif x)

(G.9)

\displaystyle\geq\int\frac{\eta/2+\gamma_{c}\mathfrak{Im}[s]}{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x-z-\gamma_{c}s+\omega\right\rvert}^{2}}\,\varrho_{\mathrm{MP}}(\dif x),

(G.10)

where the second step uses (

G.3

\mathfrak{Im}[s]\leq 2/\eta

\displaystyle(s-m)\Big[1-\int\frac{\gamma_{c}\,\varrho_{\mathrm{MP}}(\dif x)}{(x-z-\gamma_{c}m)(x-z-\gamma_{c}s+\omega)}\Big]=\int\frac{-\omega\,\varrho_{\mathrm{MP}}(\dif x)}{(x-z-\gamma_{c}m)(x-z-\gamma_{c}s+\omega)}.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in the first step, and by (

G.10

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\int\frac{\varrho_{\mathrm{MP}}(\dif x)}{(x-z-\gamma_{c}m)(x-z-\gamma_{c}s+\omega)}\right\rvert}

\displaystyle\leq\Big[\int\frac{\varrho_{\mathrm{MP}}(\dif x)}{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x-z-\gamma_{c}m\right\rvert}^{2}}\int\frac{\varrho_{\mathrm{MP}}(\dif x)}{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x-z-\gamma_{c}s+\omega\right\rvert}^{2}}\Big]^{1/2}

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert 1-\int\frac{\gamma_{c}\,\varrho_{\mathrm{MP}}(\dif x)}{(x-z-m)(x-z-s+\omega)}\right\rvert}\geq 1-\frac{\gamma_{c}}{\eta^{2}/4+\gamma_{c}}=\frac{\eta^{2}}{\eta^{2}+4\gamma_{c}}.

(G.15)

Substituting (
