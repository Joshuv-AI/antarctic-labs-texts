Fibration structure for Gromov h-principle
Koji Yamazaki

Abstract
The h-principle is a powerful tool for obtaining solutions to partial differential inequalities and partial differential equations. Gromov discovered the h-principle for the general partial differential relations to generalize the results of Hirsch and Smale. In his book, Gromov generalizes his theorem and discusses the sheaf theoretic h-principle , in which an object called a flexible sheaf plays an important role. We show that a flexible sheaf can be interpreted as a fibrant object with respect to a model structure.

Introduction

The
h-principle
is a powerful tool for obtaining solutions to partial differential inequalities and partial differential equations.
Hirsch
[
12
]
and Smale
[
24
,
23
]
discovered the h-principle for the immersions, and Phillips
[
19
]
discovered the h-principle for the submersion.
Gromov
[
8
]
discovered
Gromov h-principle
, the h-principle for the general partial differential relations, to generalize the above results.
The proof of
Gromov h-principle
is favoured in modern times via
Holonomic Approximation Theorem
by Y. M. Eliashberg and N. M. Mishachev
[
5
,
4
]
.
Their proof is rather elementary, as they consider a space of functions to contain the Whitney topology.
Before that, Gromov himself has refined his proofs.

In his book, Gromov
[
9
]
generalizes his theorem and discusses the
sheaf theoretic h-principle
, in which an object called the
flexible sheaf
plays an important role.
This proof is rather abstract.
For this reason, the proof by Eliashberg and Mishachev is more prefered to see the concrete images.
However, the abstract proof by Gromov also has some advantages
(cf. Section 0.5).

We, therefore, wish to develop further the abstract proof by Gromov himself.
We first clarify what a flexible sheaf is.
We show that a flexible sheaf can be interpreted as a fibrant object with respect to a model structure.

0.1
What’s h-principle?

Consider the following partial differential inequality
R
:

f(x,y,\frac{\partial y}{\partial x},\cdots,\frac{\partial^{r}y}{\partial x^{r}})>0.

(
R
)

In analysis, we often detect a weak solution and show later that it is a strict solution.
In differential topology, we detect a formal solution and transform it into a strict solution by homotopy.

A formal solution
is a family of continuous maps
s_{0}(x),s_{1}(x),\cdots,s_{r}(x)
such that

f(x,s_{0}(x),s_{1}(x),\cdots,s_{r}(x))>0.

We say that the partial differential inequality
R
satisfies the
h-principle
or the
homotopy principle
if any formal solution can be homotopically transformed into a strict solution.
We say that it satisfies the
parametric h-principle
or the
parametric homotopy principle
if the inclusion map
\operatorname{Sol}(R)\hookrightarrow\Gamma(R)
is a weak homotopy equivalence where
\operatorname{Sol}(R)
is the space of strict solutions of
R
and
\Gamma(R)
is the space of formal solutions of
R
.

0.2
Sheaf theoretic h-principle

A
quasitopological space
is a generalization of a topological space, whose homotopy groups can be defined
(cf. Section 1).

qTop
is the category of quasitopological spaces.

qTop
has a model structure
(cf. Section 1.2).

Let
B
be a topological space.
A
continuous sheaf
on
B
is a sheaf on
B
with value in
qTop
.

{\bf Sh}(B;{\bf qTop})
is the category of continuous sheaves on
B
.
For example, the spaces of solutions to a partial differential relation give a continuous sheaf.
A continuous sheaf
\mathcal{F}
has the
sheaf of formal sections

\mathcal{F}^{\ast}
and the
diagonal map

\Delta:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}^{\ast}

(cf. Section 2.3).
We say that
\mathcal{F}
satisfies the
(sheaf theoretic) parametric h-principle
if the
\Delta
is a sectionwise weak equivalence.
The following fundamental theorem is the basis of Gromov theory.

Theorem 0.1

(
Gromov
[
9
, page 76]
)
.

Any flexible sheaf on a polyhedron satisfies the parametric h-principle.

This theorem follows immediately from the following two lemmas.

Lemma 0.2

(
Gromov
[
9
, page 76]
)
.

Let
B
be a polyhedron, and let
\mathcal{F}
be a continuous sheaf on
B
.

(1)

The diagonal morphism
\Delta:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}^{\ast}
is a stalkwise weak equivalence.

(2)

The sheaf
\mathcal{F}^{\ast}
of formal sections is flexible.

Lemma 0.3

(
Gromov
[
9
, page 77]
)
.

Let
B
be a polyhedron, and let
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
be flexble sheaves on
B
.

f:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
is a sectionwise weak equivalence if
f
is a stalkwise weak equivalence.

The above Lemma
0.2
holds in more general settings
(cf. Lemma
2.10
and Lemma
4.5
).

0.3
Main results

We want to understand a flexible sheaf as a fibrant object.
Furthermore, we hope to understand
\Delta:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}^{\ast}
as a fibrant replacement of
\mathcal{F}
by Lemma
0.2
.

Question
.

Is there a model structure on
{\bf Sh}(B;{\bf qTop})
such that the followings hold?

•

A morphism
f
in
{\bf Sh}(B;{\bf qTop})
is a weak equivalence

\Leftrightarrow

f
is a stalkwise weak equivalence.

•

An object
\mathcal{F}
in
{\bf Sh}(B;{\bf qTop})
is fibrant

\Leftrightarrow

\mathcal{F}
is flexible.

In this paper, we give two answers.
The first result is that
{\bf Sh}(B;{\bf qTop})
can be embedded in a model category.

Theorem 3.1
.

There exists a right proper model category
{\bf PSh}_{\ast}(\tilde{\mathscr{U}}_{B};{\bf qTop})
and a fully faithful and left exact embedding
\iota:{\bf Sh}(B;{\bf qTop})\rightarrow{\bf PSh}_{\ast}(\tilde{\mathscr{U}}_{B};{\bf qTop})
such that the followings hold.

Let
f:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
be a morphism in
{\bf Sh}(B;{\bf qTop})
.

•

\iota(f)
is a weak equivalence

\Leftrightarrow

f
is a stalkwise weak equivalence
(i.e.
f_{x}:\mathcal{F}_{x}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}_{x}
is a weak equivalence for any
x\in B
).

•

\iota(f)
is a fibration

\Leftrightarrow

f
is a flexible extension
(cf. Definition
2.6
).

In particular, for a continuous sheaf
\mathcal{F}
on
B
,

•

\iota(\mathcal{F})
is fibrant

\Leftrightarrow

\mathcal{F}
is flexible
(cf. Definition
2.6
).

Unfortunately,
{\bf Sh}(B;{\bf qTop})
itself cannot be expected to have cofibratons.
Therefore,
{\bf Sh}(B;{\bf qTop})
itself cannot be expected to have a model structure.
However,
{\bf Sh}(B;{\bf qTop})
has fibrations and weak equivalences.
An
ABC (pre)fibration structure
is known as a categorical structure equipped with fibrations and weak equivalences
(cf.
[
22
]
or Section 4).
The second result is that
{\bf Sh}(B;{\bf qTop})
has an ABC prefibration structure.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
B
be a strongly locally contractible normal Hausdorff space.
There exists an ABC prefibration structure on
{\bf Sh}(B;{\bf qTop})
such that the followings hold.

Let
f:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
be a morphism in
{\bf Sh}(B;{\bf qTop})
.

•

f
is a weak equivalence

\Leftrightarrow

f
is a stalkwise weak equivalence
(i.e.
f_{x}:\mathcal{F}_{x}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}_{x}
is a weak equivalence for any
x\in B
).

•

f
is a fibration

\Leftrightarrow

f
is a flexible extension
(cf. Definition
2.6
).

In particular, for a continuous sheaf
\mathcal{F}
on
B
,

•

\mathcal{F}
is fibrant

\Leftrightarrow

\mathcal{F}
is flexible
(cf. Definition
2.6
).

The proof of the first result is almost purely category-theoretic.
On the other hand, the proof of the second result reflects geometric considerations by Gromov.
Most of the axioms of the ABC prefibration category
{\bf Sh}(B;{\bf qTop})
follow from Theorem
3.1
.
However, only the proof of the axiom of the existence of a decomposition needs a generalisation of Lemma
0.2

(cf. Lemma
2.10
and Lemma
4.5
).

0.4
History of h-principles

In the Cousin problem solution, Oka
[
18
]
showed that the solvability of certain complex analytic problems can be attributed to the solvability of topological problems.
This is what is now called
Oka principle
, and it can be said that the germ of the philosophy of the h-principle already existed here.

For a complex manifold
X
, let
\mathcal{O}_{X}^{\ast}
be the sheaf of non-zero holomorphic functions everywhere, and let
\mathcal{C}_{X}^{\ast}
be the sheaf of non-zero continuous functions everywhere.

Oka principle
can be stated in the following format.

Theorem 0.4

(Oka
[
18
]
; cf.
[
6
]
)
.

Let
X
be a Stein manifold.
Then, the map
H^{1}(X;\mathcal{O}_{X}^{\ast})\rightarrow H^{1}(X;\mathcal{C}_{X}^{\ast})
induced by the inclusion
\mathcal{O}_{X}^{\ast}\subset\mathcal{C}_{X}^{\ast}
is an isomorphism.

The sheaf
\mathcal{O}_{X}^{\ast}
(resp.
\mathcal{C}_{X}^{\ast}
) is regarded as the sheaf of holomorphic (resp. continuous) functions valued in
\mathbb{C}^{\ast}(\cong GL_{1}(\mathbb{C}))
.
The homology group
H^{1}(X;\mathcal{O}_{X}^{\ast})
(resp.
H^{1}(X;\mathcal{C}_{X}^{\ast})
) is regarded as the set of isomorphism classes of holomorphic (resp. continuous) complex line bundles over
X
.
Then, the above theorem can be rephrased as follow.

Corollary 0.5
.

Let
X
be a Stein manifold.
For any
C^{0}
-almost complex line bundle, there exists only one holomorphic complex line bundle which is isomorphic to it, up to holomorphic isomorphic.

Because
GL_{1}(\mathbb{C})
is abelian, extensive skills in sheaf cohomologies can be used to prove
Oka principle
.
On the other hand, the general
GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is not abelian.
Grauert showed that the same statement still holds true for
GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
in general.

Theorem 0.6

(Grauert
[
7
]
; cf.
[
6
]
)
.

Let
X
be a Stein manifold.
For any
C^{0}
-almost complex vector bundle, there exists only one holomorphic complex vector bundle which is isomorphic to it, up to holomorphic isomorphic.

I do not know whether
Oka principle
inspired Nash’s work, but the idea of attributing the solvability of analytic problems to the solvability of topological problems reappears in the proof of
Nash embedding theorems

[
17
]
.
This had a direct impact on the later work of Hirsch
[
12
]
and Smale
[
24
,
23
]
, and the prototype of the h-principle was completed as the h-principle for immersions of manifolds.
This is now known as
Hirsch-Smale h-principle
.

Let
M
and
N
be manifolds.
A
formal immersion

M\rightarrow N
is a pair
(f,\tilde{f})
of a continuous map
f:M\rightarrow N
and a continuous bundle map
\tilde{f}:TM\rightarrow TN
over
f
such that each
\tilde{f}:T_{x}M\rightarrow T_{f(x)}N
is injective.
Let
\operatorname{Imm}(M,N)
be the set of immersions
M\looparrowright N
.
Let
\operatorname{Imm^{f}}(M,N)
be the set of formal immersions
M\rightarrow N
.

Hirsch-Smale h-principle
is stated in the following format.

Theorem 0.7

(Hirsch
[
12
]
, Smale
[
24
,
23
]
)
.

Let
M
and
N
be manifolds.
If we have an inequality
\operatorname{dim}(M)<\operatorname{dim}(N)
, then the inclusion map
\operatorname{Imm}(M,N)\subset\operatorname{Imm^{f}}(M,N)
is a weak homotopy equivalence.

A similar theorem on submersions is given by Phillips.
Let
M
and
N
be manifolds.
A
formal submersion

M\rightarrow N
is a pair
(f,\tilde{f})
of a continuous map
f:M\rightarrow N
and a continuous bundle map
\tilde{f}:TM\rightarrow TN
over
f
such that each
\tilde{f}:T_{x}M\rightarrow T_{f(x)}N
is surjective.
Let
\operatorname{Sub}(M,N)
be the set of submersions
M\rightarrow N
.
Let
\operatorname{Sub^{f}}(M,N)
be the set of formal submersions
M\rightarrow N
.

Phillips h-principle
is stated in the following format.

Theorem 0.8

(Phillips
[
19
]
)
.

Let
M
and
N
be manifolds.
If
M
is an open manifold, then the inclusion map
\operatorname{Sub}(M,N)\subset\operatorname{Sub^{f}}(M,N)
is a weak homotopy equivalence.

Let
B
be a smooth manifold.
For each open subset
U,V(\subset B)
, we define

\operatorname{Diff}_{B}(U,V):=\{\mbox{A diffeomorphism }U\rightarrow V\}.

\operatorname{Diff}_{B}
is a groupoid called a
pseudogroup
.

Let
p:E\rightarrow B
be a smooth fibre bundle.
Let
J^{r}p:J^{r}E\rightarrow B
be the
r
-jet bundle of
p
.
A
partial differential relation
is a subbundle of the
r
-jet bundle
J^{r}p
.
A partial differential relation
R
is
open
if the subbundle
R
is an open subset of
J^{r}p
.
For each open subset
U,V(\subset B)
, we define

\operatorname{Diff}_{E/B}(U,V):=\{\mbox{A diffeomorphism }\hat{\phi}:U\rightarrow V\,|\,p\circ\hat{\phi}=\phi\circ p\,(^{\exists}\phi:p(U)\rightarrow p(V))\}.

\operatorname{Diff}_{E/B}
is a groupoid.
The map
\hat{\phi}\mapsto\phi
defines a groupoid morphism
\operatorname{Diff}_{E/B}\rightarrow\operatorname{Diff}_{B}
.
A fibre bundle
E\rightarrow B
is
natural
if the groupoid morphism
\operatorname{Diff}_{E/B}\rightarrow\operatorname{Diff}_{B}
has a section
\operatorname{Diff}_{B}\rightarrow\operatorname{Diff}_{E/B}
.

A partial differential relation
R
on a natural fibre bundle
E\rightarrow B
is
\operatorname{Diff}_{B}
-invariant
if a section
\operatorname{Diff}_{B}\rightarrow\operatorname{Diff}_{E/B}
as above preserves
R
.

Gromov h-principle
is stated in the following format.

Theorem 0.9

(Gromov
[
8
]
; cf.
[
4
,
9
]
)
.

Let
p:E\rightarrow B
be a smooth natural fibre bundle.
Let
R
be an open,
\operatorname{Diff}_{B}
-invariant partial differential relation on
p
.
If
M
is an open manifold,
R
satisfies the parametric h-principle.

0.5
Advantages of the sheaf theoretic h-principle

Hirsch-Smale h-principle
holds even if the domain manifold
M
is not open.
To show this, it is necessary to discuss the
microextension trick
.
One advantage of the
sheaf theoretic h-principle
is that we can state the argument for the
microextension trick
as the
microextension theorem
.
Another advantage is that it has the potential to be applied to geometries (e.g. metric spaces) other than smooth manifolds.
In this section, another advantage, which is different from the two above, is discussed in more detail.
The advantage is that it has the potential for more generalizations, such as including
Haefliger h-principle

[
11
]
and
Oka-Grauert principle

[
18
,
7
]
.

Any integrable distribution can be realised locally as the kernel of a submersion.
On the basis of this observation, Phillips
[
20
,
21
]
was thinking of applying
Phillips h-principle
to foliations.
Haefliger
[
11
]
took this a step further and gave a classification of homotopy classes of integrable distributions.

Haefliger’s idea is that any integrable distribution can be expressed as a kernel of submersion into a “universal
q
-dimensional manifold”
\Gamma^{q}
.
Here, the object
\Gamma^{q}
is the étale groupoid corresponding to the pseudogroup
\operatorname{Diff}_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}
consisting of local diffeomorphisms on
\mathbb{R}^{q}
.

Haefliger h-principle
is stated in the following format.

Theorem 0.10

(Haefliger
[
11
]
)
.

Let
M
be a smooth manifold.
If
M
is an open manifold, there exists a bijection between the following.

1.

The integrable homotopy classes of integrable distribution on
M
whose corank is
q
.

2.

The integrable homotopy classes of the continuous bundle map
TM\rightarrow N\Gamma^{q}
such that it is surjective on each fibre, where
N\Gamma^{q}
is the classifying space of the tangent bundle
T\Gamma^{q}
of
\Gamma^{q}
.

Haefliger h-principle
means that “
\pi_{0}(\operatorname{Sub}(M,\Gamma^{q}))\rightarrow\pi_{0}(\operatorname{Sub^{f}}(M,\Gamma^{q}))
is bijection”.

Let
X
and
Y
be complex manifolds.
Let
\mathcal{O}(X,Y)
be the set of holomorphic maps
X\rightarrow Y
.
Let
\mathcal{C}(X,Y)
be the set of continuous maps
X\rightarrow Y
.
The following properties are important in the Oka-Grauert Theory.

Theorem 0.11

(
cf.
[
6
, Corollary 5.5.6]
)
.

Let
X
be a Stein manifold, and let
Y
be an Oka manifold
(e.g. complex Lie group).
Then,
\mathcal{O}(X,Y)\subset\mathcal{C}(X,Y)
is a weak homotopy equivalence.

A complex vector bundle on
X
corresponds one-to-one to a
GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
-structure on
X
.
In fact, a
G
-structure on
X
is a
generalized map

X\rightarrow G
, where
G
is any Lie groupoid.
We consider a Lie group to be a Lie groupoid whose object space is a singleton.

Oka-Grauert principle
(Theorem
0.6
) means that “
\pi_{0}(\mathcal{O}(X,GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})))\rightarrow\pi_{0}(\mathcal{C}(X,GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})))
is bijection”.

In order to unify
Haefliger h-principle
and
Oka-Grauert principle
into
Gromov h-principle
, we need to develop the
stack theoretic h-principle
.
To approach this dream, first, need to develop the
sheaf theoretic h-principle
.

1
Quasitopological spaces

Some different topologies can be admitted into the space of maps, for example, the
Whitney topology
.
However, if we admit the quotient topology into the stalk, the topologies will be collapsed.
Here we want to deal with the germs while keeping the local information.
The solution to this problem is a
quasitopological space
.
The discussion in Section 1.1.4 solves this problem.

The idea of a quasitopological space is credited to Spanier and Whithead
[
26
]
.
Spanier
[
25
]
has written a paper on quasitopological spaces.
A quasitopological space dealt with in this paper is not exactly the same as the one they defined, but it is almost similar.

In this paper, a quasitopological space is a sheaf on a certain small site.
(For more information on sites and sheaves, see
[
15
]
.)
The category of quasitopological spaces has a model structure.

1.1
Quasitopological spaces

We will define a quasitopological space and check its properties in this subsection.
The discussion in Section 1.1.4 is often used when we have a stalkwise discussion on continuous sheaves.
The discussion in Section 1.1.5 implies that finite polyhedra (e.g. simplexes, horns, their boundaries, etc.) can be treated in the same way as topological spaces.

1.1.1
Prequasitopological spaces

Let
Sing
\Delta
be a small category defined as follows.

•

An object of

\Delta
is a closed subset of a simplex
\Delta^{n}
.

•

A morphism of

Definition 1.1
.

A

is a presheaf on the small category
Sing
\Delta
.
A morphism between prequasitopological spaces is a morphism between presheaves.

pqTop
is the category of prequasitopological spaces.

The category

is the category
{\bf PSh}({\bf Sing\Delta})

This category is complete and cocomplete and Cartesian closed.
For a prequasitopological space

the set to which

is mapped by a functor

Example 1.2
.

For a topological space

, we define a prequasitopological space

as the following:

\operatorname{Sing^{q}}(X)[S]:=\{\mbox{continuous map }S\rightarrow X\}.

The construction

In particular, if

If

Each element

We often equate

1.1.4
Filtered colimit trick

A limit on the category
qTop
can be calculated objectwise.
(i.e. For any small diagram
X_{\lambda}
in
qTop
and any object
S
in
Sing
\Delta

(\displaystyle\lim_{\longleftarrow}X_{\lambda})[S]\cong\displaystyle\lim_{\longleftarrow}(X_{\lambda}[S])

A colimit cannot be calculated in the same way in general.
However, because the covering on the site

\Delta

Proposition 1.6
.

For any closed subset
S
of a simplex, the evaluation functor
\operatorname{ev}_{S}:{\bf qTop}\rightarrow{\bf Set};X\mapsto X[S]
preserves any filtered colimit.

Proof.

Take any closed subset
S

X_{\lambda}
(
\lambda\in\Lambda
) in
qTop
.
We will show that the following canonical map is an isomorphism:

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\longrightarrow\\
\lambda\end{subarray}}(X_{\lambda}[S])\rightarrow(\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\longrightarrow\\
\lambda\end{subarray}}X_{\lambda})[S].

(1)

For any index
\lambda\in\Lambda
and any finite closed covering
\{S_{i}\}_{i}
of
S
, the following diagram is an equalizer:

X_{\lambda}[S]\rightarrow\prod_{i}X_{\lambda}[S_{i}]\rightrightarrows\prod_{i,j}X_{\lambda}[S_{i}\cap S_{j}].

Filtered colimits commute with finite limits in

(cf.
[
16
, Chapter 9, Section 2]
).
Then, the following diagram is an equalizer:

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\longrightarrow\\

.
∎

Definition A.13

(Cartesian closed model categories)
.

A
Cartesian closed model category
is a model category that is Cartesian closed, satisfying any (i.e. all) of the following equivalent conditions:

•

For any two cofibrations
f
and
f^{\prime}
,
f\triangledown f^{\prime}
is a cofibration.
In addition, if
f
or
f^{\prime}
is acyclic, then
f\triangledown f^{\prime}
is also acyclic.

•

For any cofibration
f
and any fibration
g
,
g^{\#f}
is a fibration.
In addition, if
f
or
g
is acyclic, then
g^{\#f}
is also acyclic.

Proposition A.14
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a model category which is Cartesian closed.
Suppose that
\mathscr{C}
is a cofibrantly generated model category such that a set
I

J

The following conditions are equivalent.

1.

\mathscr{C}
is a Cartesian closed model category.

2.

For any two elements
f,f^{\prime}\in I
,
f\triangledown f^{\prime}
is a cofibration.
For any element
f\in I
and any element
f^{\prime}\in J
,
f\triangledown f^{\prime}
is an acyclic cofibration.

Proof.

1.

\Rightarrow

2.
is obvious.
We will show
2.

1.
.

By
2.
, For any two elements
f,f^{\prime}\in I
and any acyclic fibration
g
, we have the relation
f\triangledown f^{\prime}\pitchfork g
.
This is equivalent to the relation
f\pitchfork g^{\#f^{\prime}}
.
This means that
g^{\#f^{\prime}}
is an acyclic fibration.
Then, for any cofibration
f

f\pitchfork g^{\#f^{\prime}}
.
This is equivalent to the relation
f\triangledown f^{\prime}\pitchfork g
.
In the same way,
f^{\prime}
can be any cofibration.
Therefore, for any two cofibrations
f
and
f^{\prime}

f\triangledown f^{\prime}

We discuss the same for any

and

g
.
Then, for any cofibration
f

f\triangledown f^{\prime}

\mathscr{C}
