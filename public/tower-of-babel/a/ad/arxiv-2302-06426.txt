Linguistic ambiguity analysis in ChatGPT
Miguel Ortega-Martín, Óscar García-Sierra, Alfonso Ardoiz, Jorge Álvarez, Juan Carlos Armenteros, Adrián Alonso

Abstract
Linguistic ambiguity is and has always been one of the main challenges in Natural Language Processing (NLP) systems. Modern Transformer architectures like BERT, T5 or more recently InstructGPT have achieved some impressive improvements in many NLP fields, but there is still plenty of work to do. Motivated by the uproar caused by ChatGPT, in this paper we provide an introduction to linguistic ambiguity, its varieties and their relevance in modern NLP, and perform an extensive empiric analysis. ChatGPT strengths and weaknesses are revealed, as well as strategies to get the most of this model.



1
Introduction

Sometimes on a day-to-day basis humans struggle to solve linguistic ambiguity, so we must accept that maybe machines may also face difficulties alike. As
Jurafsky and Martin 2020
state at the start of one the most important Natural Language Processing books, most of NLP tasks could be seen as a disambiguation task in any of the six levels in which linguistic is built: phonetics, morphology, syntax, semantics, pragmatics and discourse. In the example sentence given by
Jurafsky and Martin 2020
, "I made her duck", we can encounter as much as five different senses, which would require disambiguating in most of the six previous levels. In this work we will focus on three of them, how they are dealt with in modern NLP systems and what room is still there for improvement.

This work contains a general review of ambiguity in linguistics (section 2); a review of how modern NLP systems handle ambiguity problems (section 3); an introduction to ChatGPT (section 4); an empirical analysis of different types of linguistic ambiguity with ChatGPT (section 5); the conclusions (section 6); the limitations of our work (section 7); and the Ethics Statements (section 8). Our contributions in this paper are the following:

•

review of current challenges of linguistic ambiguity in NLP.

•

analysis of the linguistic weaknesses and strengths of the ChatGPT model related to linguistic ambiguity.

2
Linguistic Ambiguity

Typically, three types of linguistic ambiguity are reviewed when analysing modern NLP systems: lexical, syntactic and semantic. More complex levels, like pragmatics and discourse, also present ambiguities, but those are nowadays still way to far from being solved.

We will focus on these three basic levels as they gather the main challenges faced by modern NLP models. However, sometimes the borders among levels are diffuse, and more than one kind of ambiguity could be applied to a given sentence. For instance, the lexical level also include ambiguities that happen in the phonetic level, and lexical and syntactic ambiguities mean a change of meaning and therefore can also be seen as semantic ambiguities.

2.1
Lexical Ambiguity

Homonymy and polysemy are the two main representations of lexical ambiguity. Homonymy refers to those cases in which two words with different etymology and meaning end up having similar spellings or pronunciations. In the typical sentence "I went to the bank" the word "bank" can be a financial business or the area next to a river. Without further context, if we ask anyone "What bank refers to", its answer should be that the sentence is ambiguous.

As an additional note, we can talk about homophones (two words are read the same, but could be written differently) and homographs (two words are written and read the same).

On the other hand, polysemy occurs in those situations in which the same word can have several meanings. For example, the various meanings that the verb "to serve" has in the dictionary: "serve time in prison", "serve a tea", etc.

In conclusion, in homonymy, there are two words that have come to the same form. In polysemy, there is one word that has reached multiple meanings.

2.2
Syntactic Ambiguity

This ambiguity does not come from a word, but from groups of words and their relationships. Also known as amphibology, syntactic ambiguity is due to an ambiguous sentence structure, in which a chunk or group of words in the sentence generates more than one interpretation of the whole sentence. A typical case of syntactic ambiguity is a type of structure like "Mary saw John with a telescope", in which the prepositional phrase "with a telescope" could imply that Mary used a telescope to see John, or that John was carrying a telescope when Mary saw him.

2.3
Semantic Ambiguity

In the semantic ambiguity cases, a lack of context leads to more than one interpretation of a sentence. In the sentence "My mother and my sister were sad after she shouted at her" without further context, we cannot disambiguate to whom the pronouns "she" and "her" refer to. In other cases the lack of context could be bigger. In the sentence "She loves me" we do not have any clue of who "she" could refer to. Irony, sarcasm or metaphors are other cases hard to infer, since humans need knowledge of the world and language itself to understand any hidden meaning.

3
Ambiguity in NLP

Ambiguity is a common problem that cannot be solved by the majority of Language Models (LMs) used in NLP. Most of the time the main problem is the lack of context. From Information Retrieval (IR) and Question Answering (QA) to Text Summarization or Grammatical Error Correction systems
(
Jusoh 2018
)
, training Word-sense disambiguation (WSD) systems and knowledge bases (KB) are some of the techniques traditionally used
Bano 2015
.

3.1
Lexical Ambiguity in NLP

Traditionally, without further Part-of-Speech tagging, it was not possible to disambiguate homonym words in NLP tasks. For example, in the sentence "How does a bear bear with the pain?", it would be hard to differentiate the first, a noun, from the second, a verb. Static embeddings
(
Mikolov et al. 2013
)
suffered this same problem, those two "bear" words would only have one same embedding. Yet modern contextual embeddings
(
Liu et al. 2020
)
used in Transformers models made a huge advance as the embedding of these same words with different use would effectively be different. Same happens with polysemy: given two meaning of a word like the verb "to serve", they will have different contexts and, therefore, different embeddings.

This can be seen in the Entity Linking
1
1

1

https://paperswithcode.com/task/entity-linking
(EL) task, which is a common task where we need to disambiguate among personal names. For instance, in the sentence "Paris Hilton visited Paris" both "Paris" need to be considered as different entities and matched to their corresponding id from a KB.

3.2
Syntactic Ambiguity in NLP

Syntactic ambiguity plays also a key role in NLP. For instance, dependency tagging is a common preprocessing technique in which every token of an input is given a syntactic tag with its corresponding root. Given an ambiguous syntactic structure, inferring one or another interpretation could have enormous relevance in latter tasks.

SpaCy
2
2

2

https://spacy.io/
is a state-of-the-art Python library for NLP tasks, and Dependency tagging is among its capabilities. For the previous syntactically ambiguous sentence, "Mary saw John with a telescope", an spaCy Language Model (LM) for English
3
3

3

en_core_web_lg model
labels the "telescope" as a complement of the subject, "Mary". In this case, having one or another interpretation will highly rely on how those structures were labelled in the training set of the model. If we introduce more context in the sentence (for instance, "Mary saw John with a telescope in his hand.") the ambiguity could be solved by a human being. However, as a proof of how complex is ambiguity still nowadays for NLP systems, even the best spaCy LM for English labels "telescope" as a complement of "Mary" in this sentence in which it is clearly a complement of "John". As stated before, mistakes like this, that could be due to the quality of the training data, could have a huge impact in latter tasks. Models rely on the quality of the training data and may also lead to confirmation bias due statistical relevance.

ChatGPT

Large Language Models (LLMs) have taken over NLP. The current trend among them is to align their performance with the user intents. In 2022 OpenAI firstly released IntructGPT
Ouyang et al. 2022
, a fine-tuned model of GPT-3
Brown et al. 2020
with supervised learning and 1.3 billion parameter, in which human feedback plays a key role. At the end of 2022 OpenAI released a sibling model of IntructGPT, ChatGPT
4
4

4

https://openai.com/blog/chatgpt/
, another fine-tuned version of GPT-3, with 175 millions of parameters and trained to interact with users and receive feedback from them.

In these models, prompting
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
and human feedback play a huge role. A prompt is a piece of text used in the input that aims to reduce its context as it improves the quality of the output.

Some work has quickly appeared

5

https://medium.com/geekculture/i-spent-14-days-testing-chatgpt-here-are-3-ways-it-can-improve-your-everyday-life-30852a349ad1
focusing on writing, automatizing, code generation, common knowledge question and reasoning. But, to the best of our knowledge, linguistic ambiguity has not been addressed yet.

Thanks to our experiments we have noted that by default it is preferable for these conversational models to give an answer, even if it is not of sufficient quality, rather than none at all. This can be applied to cases of poorly formulated or incomprehensible questions or to situations in which the model is not really capable of answering a well-formulated question.

5
Experimental set-up and analysis

To evaluate how ChatGPT deals with ambiguity, we have carried some experiments to measure its capabilities. At first, the only public dataset on ambiguity was considered: Sentence-Ambiguity on Big-BENCH
(
Srivastava et al. 2022
)
. However, the type of ambiguity this test deals with, discourse level ambiguity, was not of interest to us. It was therefore necessary to create new tests to assess ambiguity at the lexical, syntactic and semantic levels.

To do so, we input some ambiguous sentences via prompting and analyse ChatGPT outputs. In some occasions some queries required a change to another ChatGPT season, since we have realised that model’s cache memory helps it to agree with the user in some situations without further reasoning. Appendixes contain all the sentences used in our empiric analysis.

The main question for each sentence is if ChatGPT detects the presence or absence of ambiguity, but each kind of ambiguity will have its own particular target. In some cases, in addition to the initial ones, we have tried some other prompts that would allow us to go further in how ChatGPT works in general and to comprehend its relation with linguistic ambiguity in particular. For instance, in the case of homonymy it is relevant to ask if both words are the same, and in the case of polysemy, an special "definition classification" task is performed.

5.1
Lexical Ambiguity

5.1.1
Homonymy

Homonym research is focused on homographs (two words that are written and read the same) since, although homophones have a huge interest for Speech-to-Text tasks, they are not written the same, so it does not make sense to analyse them with a text-only system like ChatGPT. Our experiment is designed as the following:

•

We compile a list of sentences (Appendix A.1) and ask the model to label them as ambiguous or non-ambiguous with the following prompt:
Is the sentence "[sentence]" ambiguous?

•

Then we ask the model if the homonyms from a given sentence have the same meaning using the following prompt:
What does every occurrence of the word "[word]" mean?

•

Finally, in some cases we modified the original prompt to check any improvements in the outputs.

5.1.2
Polysemy

In this case, we use a sample of 10 lemmas (Appendix B). For each one of them:

•

We input a non-ambiguous sentence and tried to label it as ambiguous or non-ambiguous with the following prompt:
Is the sentence "[sentence]" ambiguous?

•

Then we design a meaning classification task, where we input two senses/definitions of the lemma and perform a classification task among those senses in the previous sentence. The prompt model is:
These are two definitions of "[lemma]": [DEF1, DEF2]. Which of the definitions appear in the sentence "[sentence]"?

•

Also, for each sentence we ask ChatGPT to guess the meaning of the polysemous word, but without giving the definitions in the input, as in the second experiment. The prompt model is:
In the sentence "[sentence]" which is the definition of "[lemma]"?

•

In fourth place we create a collection of clearly ambiguous sentences using those lemmas, and try to label them as ambiguous or non-ambiguous with the following prompt:
Is the sentence "[sentence]" ambiguous?

•

Finally, in the last experiment we try to confuse ChatGPT using erroneous pairs of lemma-definitions.

Appendix B.2 contains all the inputs and outputs from this section.

In the first experiment we want to test whether ChatGPT recognises the concept of ambiguity, and whether it is able to detect it in simple sentences. The result is that ChatGPT correctly detects non-ambiguity in all of the ten sentences. Since a classification task must be done on them, all sentences are suppose not to be ambiguous, so ChatGPT does a great job by not detecting ambiguity.

For the second experiment we design a meaning classification task, where for each of the ten lemmas we input two senses/definitions of each and one sentence to perform a classification task among those senses. Definitions and sentences come from Cambridge Dictionary

For this second test, ChatGPT correctly classifies nine out of ten sentences. It only fails to classify the verb "to cheat", as seen in query
1
from Appendix B.2. However, this can be solved with another better prompt in which the definitions are removed (query
2
from Appendix B.2). In this case, less context is better. In order to go further with this idea, in the third experiment we check what happens if no definitions context is given. In the other nine sentences, ChatGPT easily gives a correct definition for all the nine polysemous words.

As it was done in the case of homonymy, it’s important to test clearly ambiguous examples. Sentences like "I saw the mouse on the table" and "I saw the mouse on the desktop" could have one predominant interpretation, but it is not either 100% clear for humans. However, ChatGPT does not see any ambiguity, and clearly associates the first one with the animal and the second one with the electronic device. Given how easily the model previously detected polysemy, it is shocking that it does not detect it here.

Same happens in the sentence "I spent the morning in the bank", where ChatGPT clearly identifies the first definition. In our opinion, there is not enough context and they should be flagged for ambiguity.

Following the idea of trying to improve prompting, if we ask ChatGPT if those two sentences with the term "mouse" are ambiguous, it labels them as ambiguous, and alludes to the need of more context in order to disambiguate. One plausible cause for this could be the use of a prompt with the word "ambiguous" but, again, a lack of determinism can be noted.

Another interesting experiment we have conducted is trying to confuse ChatGPT using erroneous pairs of lemma-definitions. Following the previous prompt model in which we input the two definition, what would happen if those are wrong in some way? A first test is using wrong definitions of the lemma that appears in the sentence, and there ChatGPT detects our trap (query

In conclusion, ChatGPT is remarkably good at classifying meaning of polysemous words in non-ambiguous sentences, even when the definitions are not in the context. In ambiguous sentences ChatGPT does not seem to perform as good as for homonymy, but results improve with better prompting.

5.3
Semantic Ambiguity

Although semantic ambiguity relates in literature with homonymy and polysemy, in this section we will focus on co-reference resolution. The following experiments have been done for every sentence from Appendix D.1:

•

We analyse the ambiguity of the sentence with the following prompt:

•

We explore other specific prompts to analyse the reference of the ambiguous pronouns.

In first place we ask the model if the sentence "My mother and my sister were sad after she shouted at her" is ambiguous. As seen in query

from Appendix D.2, The explanation is great and fully captures the logic behind co-reference resolution. Then, if we go further and ask "who shouted at who?", ChatGPT again detects the ambiguity of the pronouns "she" and "her" (query

Another specific case of co-reference is gender disambiguation. Here ambiguity can be useful to explore stereotypical gender bias
(
Blodgett et al. 2020
)
in language models. In the example sentences proposed by
Zhao et al. 2018
"The physicist hired the secretary because he was overwhelmed with work." and "The physicist hired the secretary because she was overwhelmed with work.", systems must be able to make correct linking predictions in pro-stereotypical scenarios (…) and anti-stereotypical scenarios"
(
Zhao et al. 2018
)
. These pro-stereotypical and anti-stereotypical scenarios came from data from US Department of Labor statistics.

We ask the model if they are ambiguous (queries
3
and
4
). ChatGPT correctly labels them as non-ambiguous. Then we ask about the gender of both people in each sentence, because this must be determined by the pronoun of the subordinate sentence, and not by statistical stereotypes. This means that even if traditionally a gender is more prone to a given job, in these sentence the pronoun makes this sentence non-ambiguous, so there is no need to draw upon that "statistical knowledge". Grammar should be above that. But this does not happen, and ChatGPT wrongly interprets it in query
5
from Appendix D.3.

This means that even in a situation where there is no ambiguity, ChatGPT puts gender bias over grammar. This could naively mean that ChatGPT is misunderstanding grammar, but, as expected, if we change the gender of the pronouns, ChatGPT suddenly does not fail, as seen in query

In conclusion, in the semantic field ChatGPT does some amazing job detecting ambiguous pronouns in co-reference resolution situations, but disappointingly fails putting gender bias over grammar in situations where this grammar makes a sentence non-ambiguous.

5.5
Results

Table 1 contains the results of the ambiguity detection queries. In our data samples ChatGPT achieves an accuracy of 0.6061 and an F1 of 0.48.

For the case of ambiguity detection in sentences with homonyms, False Positives represents the higher amount, although this was due to the detection of polysemy in some of the homonyms, and ChatGPT did well apart from that. In the case of polysemy ChatGPT correctly detected all negative examples, but failed to detect the positive ones. The model did well when classifying definitions with or without context, and also performed great with erroneous queries.

Syntactic ambiguity appears to be harder to detect, but in the end, with the help of good prompting and reinforcement learning, ChatGPT did well at detection of disambiguation. In semantics, ChatGPT performed perfectly in the detection of ambiguities. Apart from that, it has some bright sports (co-reference resolution) and some weaknesses (puts gender bias over grammar in some non-ambiguous situations). In the generation task ChatGPT did well, but also revealed some of its worse issues: the lack of systematicity. Lastly, it should also be pointed that in most of the cases ChatGPT brilliantly alludes to lack of context as the key factor in disambiguation.

6
Conclusions

Transformer-based LLMs have achieved enormous advances in NLP, where disambiguation has always been one of the main problems. Models like ChatGPT continue to improve these issues, but there is still plenty of work to do.

We have provided an introduction to linguistic ambiguity, its varieties and their relevance in NLP, and performed an extend empiric analysis of how ChatGPT treats them. This analysis revealed some of the model strengths and weaknesses. We also provide the data used in the experiments, and most of the model outputs as well.

We consider this a promising starting point towards the use of ChatGPT and generative models in disambiguation tasks. Probably by the moment this paper is read some of the flaws of the model have improved and we have to move on to another defects.

7
Limitations

It should be noted that this work is an empiric analysis. We understand that the main limitation of our work is that ChatGPT is a living model that evolves in time, eager to keep on learning and able to track a conversation (not only a prompt). Therefore, some of the data used here and the results produced with them may vary in the future as the model evolves. It should also be pointed that this is not an open-source model that in some occasions suffers from overload. Furthermore, it is relevant to note that experiments have been carried out for a high-resource language like English, so the results may be different for languages with other linguistic features or fewer resources.

8
Ethics statement

We are aware of the possibilities that models like ChatGPT can imply for both industry and academic research in the future. Our intention here is to contribute to a better understanding and development of NLP in general and this model in particular, in order to improve its performance, but always promoting a responsible use.
