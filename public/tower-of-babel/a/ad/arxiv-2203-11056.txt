Mass and width of an unstable particle
Scott Willenbrock

Abstract




Abstract
: We show that the mass and width of an unstable particle are precisely defined by the pole in the complex energy plane,
\mu=m-(i/2)\Gamma
, by identifying the width,
\Gamma
, with the particle’s decay rate and the mass,
m
, with the oscillatory frequency. We find that the physical
Z
boson mass lies 26 MeV below its quoted value, while the physical
W
boson mass lies 20 MeV below. We also clarify the various Breit-Wigner formulae that are used to describe a resonance.

The squared mass of a stable particle is equal to the pole in the particle’s propagator. For an unstable particle the pole is complex, and is related to both the mass and the width of the particle. In this paper we make this relation precise.

For simplicity we consider a complex scalar field; the calculation also applies to the transverse part of a massive vector field, such as the
W
and
Z
bosons. We study the propagator, which in a free field theory is the amplitude for a particle to be created at the origin and destroyed at spacetime point
x
(or an antiparticle to be created at
x
and destroyed at the origin),

\langle 0|T\phi(x)\phi^{\dagger}(0)|0\rangle=\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{ie^{-ip\cdot x}}{p^{2}-m^{2}+i\epsilon}\;.

(1)

The squared mass of the particle is the pole in the momentum-space propagator.

We now include interactions to all orders in perturbation theory.
Consider the self-energy corrections to the momentum-space propagator from the diagrams in Fig. 1, where the shaded circle denotes the bare one-particle-irreducible self-energy to all orders in perturbation theory,
i\Pi(p^{2})
.
Summing the series of diagrams gives

\displaystyle\frac{i}{p^{2}-m_{B}^{2}+i\epsilon}\left(1-\frac{\Pi(p^{2})}{p^{2}-m_{B}^{2}+i\epsilon}+\cdots\right)

(2)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{p^{2}-m_{B}^{2}+\Pi(p^{2})+i\epsilon}

(3)

where
m_{B}
is the bare mass.
The pole of the full momentum-space propagator,
\mu^{2}
, is the solution to the equation

\mu^{2}-m_{B}^{2}+\Pi(\mu^{2})=0\;.

(4)

Combining Eqs. (
3
) and (
4
) yields

\langle 0|T\phi_{B}(x)\phi_{B}^{\dagger}(0)|0\rangle=\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{ie^{-ip\cdot x}}{p^{2}-\mu^{2}+\Pi(p^{2})-\Pi(\mu^{2})+i\epsilon}

(5)

As usual, we renormalize the fields such that the residue of the pole is the same as in the free field theory (see Sec. 10.3 of Ref.
[
1
]
). We define the renormalized fields
\phi_{R}=Z^{-1/2}\phi_{B}
,
\phi_{R}^{\dagger}=Z^{-1/2}\phi_{B}^{\dagger}
; note that
\phi_{B}^{\dagger}
is renormalized by the same factor as
\phi_{B}
, rather than by the complex-conjugate factor (see Sec. 4 of Ref.
[
2
]
). The full propagator for the renormalized fields is thus

\langle 0|T\phi_{R}(x)\phi_{R}^{\dagger}(0)|0\rangle=\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{iZ^{-1}e^{-ip\cdot x}}{p^{2}-\mu^{2}+\Pi(p^{2})-\Pi(\mu^{2})+i\epsilon}

(6)

where

Z^{-1}=1+\Pi^{\prime}(\mu^{2})\;.

(7)

If the particle is unstable
\Pi(\mu^{2})
is complex, and hence, from Eq. (
4
),
\mu^{2}
is complex. The pole position is gauge invariant
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
and infrared safe
[
4
,
5
]
. The fact that the pole is complex is a fundamental feature of an unstable particle, and will be used in the next section to derive the mass and width.

Width and Mass

The width of an unstable state, whether it be a fundamental particle or a composite, is generally understood to be the decay rate of the particle, that is,
\Gamma=1/\tau
, where
\tau
is the lifetime. This provides an unambiguous definition of
\Gamma
, and is used in all branches of physics.

The scattering amplitude of a process with an intermediate unstable particle acquires a complex pole from the propagator,

S\sim\frac{1}{p^{2}-\mu^{2}}\;.

(8)

Going to the rest frame of the unstable particle (we consider a general frame in Appendix A), we can rewrite Eq. (
8
) as

S\sim\frac{1}{p_{0}^{2}-\mu^{2}}=\frac{1}{(p_{0}-\mu)(p_{0}+\mu)}\;.

(9)

To find the time dependence of the scattering amplitude, we Fourier transform from energy to time
[
6
]
:

S\sim\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dp_{0}\;\frac{e^{-ip_{0}t}}{(p_{0}-\mu)(p_{0}+\mu)}\;.

(10)

For
t>0
we can close the contour in the lower half complex
p_{0}
plane, as shown in Fig. 2, since the integral along the large semicircle is exponentially damped. Using the residue theorem, we pick up the contribution of the pole at
p_{0}=\mu
,

S\sim e^{-i\mu t}=e^{-i{\rm Re}\,\mu t}e^{{\rm Im}\,\mu t}\;.

(11)

The decay probability is given by the square of the scattering amplitude,

|S|^{2}\sim e^{2{\rm Im}\,\mu t}\;.

(12)

This corresponds to exponential decay with
2{\rm Im}\,\mu=-\Gamma
. Hence we learn that
{\rm Im}\,\mu=-\Gamma/2
.

For
t<0
we close the contour in the upper half complex
p_{0}
plane and pick up the contribution of the pole at
p_{0}=-\mu
. This pole is associated with antiparticle propagation. The residue theorem gives
S\sim e^{i{\rm Re}\,\mu t}e^{-{\rm Im}\,\mu t}
, and hence
|S|^{2}\sim e^{\Gamma t}
. This also corresponds to exponential decay since
t
is negative, and proves that the particle and antiparticle have the same lifetime.

The oscillatory frequency of the state is dictated by the particle energy, which in the rest frame is just its mass. Hence (from Eq. (
11
))
{\rm Re}\,\mu=m
, and we conclude that

\boxed{\mu=m-\frac{i}{2}\Gamma}\;.

(13)

The same result has been obtained in Ref.
[
7
]
by different means, also using
\Gamma=1/\tau
. Eq. (
13
) is also used to define the mass and width of hadronic resonances (see Sec. 50 of Ref.
[
8
]
), and is used, in matrix form, in the analysis of neutral meson oscillations (see Sec. 13 of Ref.
[
8
]
). Eq. (
13
) provides an unambiguous decomposition of
\mu
into physically meaningful quantities. Note that nowhere did we make a nonrelativistic approximation.

Returning to Eq. (
4
), and using Eq. (
13
), we find

{\rm Im}\,\mu^{2}=-m\Gamma=-{\rm Im}\,\Pi(\mu^{2})

(14)

or

\Gamma=\frac{1}{m}{\rm Im}\,\Pi(\mu^{2})\;.

(15)

This is an implicit formula for the decay rate, since
\mu=m-(i/2)\Gamma
, and can be solved perturbatively.

The exponential decay of an unstable particle follows from the complex pole in the particle’s propagator. The propagator also has branch-point singularities, which we discuss in Appendix B. These singularities do not affect the exponential decay of the particle.

Some authors, going back to the original papers on the subject
[
9
,
10
]
, have expressed the opinion that the definition of the mass (and width) of an unstable particle is inherently ambiguous due to the uncertainty principle. A typical argument is that the energy-time uncertainty principle,
\Delta E\Delta t\geq 1
, restricts the accuracy with which the mass can be defined by
\Delta m\sim\Delta E\geq 1/\Delta t=1/\tau=\Gamma
. This is faulty logic, as
\Delta E
corresponds not to the uncertainty in the definition of the mass, but to the spread of energies that an unstable particle is produced with,
i.e.
, the width. There is no uncertainty in the pole position in the complex energy plane, nor in the mass, which is the real part of the pole position. The same is true of the width.

Resonance formulae

In the energy region near the pole at
p_{0}=\mu
, the amplitude of Eq. (
9
) can be approximated by neglecting the antiparticle pole at
p_{0}=-\mu
,

S\sim\frac{1}{E-\mu}

(16)

where
E=p_{0}
.
The cross section in the resonance region is thus approximately

|S|^{2}\sim\frac{1}{(E-m)^{2}+\Gamma^{2}/4}

(17)

which is the well-known Breit-Wigner formula
[
11
]
. The resonance shape has a full width at half maximum of
\Gamma
, which is why
\Gamma
is called the width. A similar formula may also be obtained from nonrelativistic quantum mechanics, such as in the original paper
[
11
]
.

Another way to approximate the cross section in the resonance region is to start from Eq. (
8
). Making the approximation, valid for
\Gamma^{2}\ll m^{2}
,

\mu^{2}=\left(m-\frac{i}{2}\Gamma\right)^{2}\approx m^{2}-im\Gamma

(18)

gives the propagator

S\sim\frac{1}{p^{2}-m^{2}+im\Gamma}

(19)

and hence the cross section

|S|^{2}\sim\frac{1}{(p^{2}-m^{2})^{2}+m^{2}\Gamma^{2}}\;.

(20)

This is often referred to as a relativistic Breit-Wigner formula. The relativistic invariance follows from the inclusion of both particle and antiparticle poles.

The moral is that both versions of the Breit-Wigner formula, Eqs. (
17
) and (
20
), are approximations, and should be treated thusly. In addition, the formula
\mu^{2}=m^{2}-im\Gamma
[see Eq. (
18
)], which has gained widespread currency, is also an approximation.
The precise definition of the mass and width of an unstable particle is
\mu=m-(i/2)\Gamma
[Eq. (
13
)].

Inserting Eq. (
13
) into Eq. (
8
) without making any approximations gives the propagator

S\sim\frac{1}{p^{2}-\left(m-\frac{i}{2}\Gamma\right)^{2}}

(21)

and hence the cross section

|S|^{2}\sim\frac{1}{(p^{2}-m^{2}+\Gamma^{2}/4)^{2}+m^{2}\Gamma^{2}}\;.

(22)

It may seem unconventional to include the
\Gamma^{2}/4
term in the denominator of the above expression, but its presence follows from the precise definition of mass and width provided by Eq. (
13
). Eq. (
22
) is the relativistic Breit-Wigner formula, Eq. (
20
), but without taking the limit
\Gamma^{2}\ll m^{2}
.

Weak boson masses

The
Z
and
W
boson masses have been extracted from experiment by using the resonance formula

|S|^{2}\sim\frac{1}{(p^{2}-M^{2})^{2}+(p^{2}{\it\Gamma}/M)^{2}}

(23)

which corresponds to none of the Breit-Wigner formulae, Eqs. (
17
),(
20
), and (
22
). This formula is based on the propagator

\frac{1}{p^{2}-M^{2}+ip^{2}{\it\Gamma}/M}

(24)

which has a troubled history. It was once thought that the mass of an unstable particle could be defined by

M^{2}-m_{B}^{2}+{\rm Re}\,\Pi(M^{2})=0\;.

(25)

in contrast to Eq. (
4
). Combining Eqs. (
3
) and (
25
) yields the momentum-space propagator

\frac{1}{p^{2}-M^{2}+\Pi(p^{2})-{\rm Re}\,\Pi(M^{2})}\approx\frac{1}{p^{2}-M^{2}+i{\rm Im}\,\Pi(p^{2})}\;

(26)

For a weak boson coupled to massless fermions, the leading-order approximation gives
{\rm Im}\,\Pi(p^{2})=p^{2}{\it\Gamma}/M
, where
{\it\Gamma}
is evaluated at tree level. Inserting this into Eq. (
26
) gives the propagator of Eq. (
24
). This is sometimes called the on-shell scheme for unstable particles.

The trouble with this scheme are not the aforementioned approximations, which can be improved; it is that the mass
M
is gauge dependent
[
12
]
and thus unphysical. Despite this, the parameterization of the resonance region of Eq. (
23
) has persisted, although it has no good theoretical justification.

Despite appearances, the propagator of Eq. (
24
) is equivalent to that of Eq. (
21
), and gives exactly the same resonance shape. Both propagators have simple poles, and by equating the poles we can find the exact relation between the parameters
M
and
{\it\Gamma}
and the physical mass and width of the particle,

m=M\left(\frac{r+1}{2r^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\approx M\left(1-\frac{3}{8}\left(\frac{{\it\Gamma}}{M}\right)^{2}\right)

(27)

\Gamma=2M\left(\frac{r-1}{2r^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\approx{\it\Gamma}\left(1-\frac{5}{8}\left(\frac{{\it\Gamma}}{M}\right)^{2}\right)

(28)

where

r=\left(1+\left(\frac{{\it\Gamma}}{M}\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\;.

(29)

The world-average values
M_{Z}=91.1876\pm 0.0021\;{\rm GeV}
and
{\it\Gamma}_{Z}=2.4952\pm 0.0023\;{\rm GeV}

[
8
]
can be used to derive the physical
Z
boson mass and width from Eqs. (
27
) and (
28
),

\displaystyle m_{Z}=91.1620\pm 0.0021\;{\rm GeV}

(30)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{Z}=2.4940\pm 0.0023\;{\rm GeV}\;.

(31)

The physical
Z
boson mass is about 26 MeV less than the parameter
M_{Z}
, a result we derived long ago
[
13
]
; this is about ten times greater than the uncertainty in the mass. The
Z
boson width is the same as the parameter
{\it\Gamma}_{Z}
within the uncertainty. This yields a
Z
boson lifetime of
\tau_{Z}=2.6391\pm 0.0024\times 10^{-25}\;{\rm s}
.

The world-average values
M_{W}=80.379\pm 0.012\;{\rm GeV}
and
{\it\Gamma}_{W}=2.085\pm 0.042\;{\rm GeV}

[
8
]
yield the physical
W
boson mass and width

\displaystyle m_{W}=80.359\pm 0.012\;{\rm GeV}

(32)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{W}=2.084\pm 0.042\;{\rm GeV}\;.

(33)

The physical
W
boson mass is about 20 MeV less than the parameter
M_{W}
, which is nearly twice the uncertainty in the mass. The
W
boson width is the same as the parameter
{\it\Gamma}_{W}
within the uncertainty, and yields
\tau_{W}=3.158\pm 0.064\times 10^{-25}\;{\rm s}
.

Going forward, it would be simpler to use the propagator of Eq. (
21
) to model the resonance region directly in terms of the physical mass and width, rather than the poorly justified propagator of Eq. (
24
).

The top-quark mass and width are also extracted from experiment using the parameterization of Eq. (
23
). The world-average values are
M_{t}=172.76\pm 0.30\;{\rm GeV}
and
{\it\Gamma}_{t}=1.42+0.19-0.15\;{\rm GeV}

[
8
]
. The width is sufficiently narrow that these values are equal to the physical top quark mass and width well within the uncertainties. In addition, the physical top quark mass is ambiguous by an amount of order
\Lambda_{QCD}\sim 200\;{\rm MeV}

[
14
]
, just like any quark.

The Higgs boson width is expected to be so narrow (
\sim 4
MeV) that the difference between the physical mass and the parameter
M_{H}
is negligible.

Discussion

The relation
\mu=m-(i/2)\Gamma
[Eq. (
13
)] is used to define the mass and width of hadronic resonances (see Sec. 50 of Ref.
[
8
]
) and is also used, in matrix form, in the analysis of neutral meson oscillations (see Sec. 13 of Ref.
[
8
]
). In the electroweak sector, an alternative decomposition of the pole position in terms of mass and width,
\mu^{2}=m^{2}-im\Gamma
[Eq. (
18
)], has gained widespread currency. How did this come to pass? Up until the discovery of the
W
and
Z
bosons, all particles that decayed via the electroweak interactions had widths that were many orders of magnitude less than their mass; the muon, for example, has
\Gamma_{\mu}/m_{\mu}=3\times 10^{-17}
. The formula
\mu^{2}=m^{2}-im\Gamma
is therefore an excellent approximation to
\mu=m-(i/2)\Gamma
. Over time it was forgotten that
\mu^{2}=m^{2}-im\Gamma
is an approximation, and it was taken to be exact. This approximation is sufficiently accurate for all particles that decay via the electroweak interaction except for the
W
and
Z
bosons, where
\Gamma_{Z}^{2}/m_{Z}^{2}\sim\Gamma_{W}^{2}/m_{W}^{2}\sim 0.0007
is greater than the accuracy of the measurements of the masses (
\Delta m_{Z}/m_{Z}\sim 0.00002
,
\Delta m_{W}/m_{W}\sim 0.0002
).

The expression
\mu^{2}=m^{2}-im\Gamma
is often used as an intermediate step for the
W
and
Z
boson masses in precision electroweak analyses
[
15
]
. One could even view this as an alternative definition of
m
and
\Gamma

[
16
]
. There are several shortcomings to this argument. If we define
m
and
\Gamma
this way, then
\Gamma
is not the decay rate and
m
is not the oscillation frequency. We have a perfectly good way of defining mass and width precisely,
\mu=m-(i/2)\Gamma
, so why would we want to introduce an alternative definition? Why would we introduce an alternative definition for the
W
and
Z
bosons but not for hadronic resonances? And why this particular alternative definition, whose only motivation is that it is approximately equal to the precise definition? Indeed, the current standard definition of the
W
and
Z
boson masses is yet another alternative definition,
\mu^{2}=M^{2}/(1+i\frac{\it\Gamma}{M})
, where
M
and
\it\Gamma
are related to
m
and
\Gamma
in a complicated way [see Eqs. (
27
) and (
28
)]. These alternative definitions should be regarded as useful parameters, not as the fundamental mass and width of the
W
and
Z
bosons. Just as the electron has a unique mass, so do the
W
and
Z
bosons.

Our goal in physics is not only to make precise measurements, but to also be precise about our concepts. With
\mu=m-(i/2)\Gamma
as the definition of the mass and width, the time dependence of an unstable particle at rest is given by

e^{-imt}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\Gamma t}

(34)

as desired, while any other definition leads to a bizarre time dependence. For example, the definition
\mu^{2}=m^{2}-im\Gamma
yields the time dependence

e^{-i\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}m\left(1+\sqrt{1+\Gamma^{2}/m^{2}}\right)^{1/2}t}e^{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Gamma\left(1+\sqrt{1+\Gamma^{2}/m^{2}}\right)^{-1/2}t}\;.

(35)

With this definition it is evident that
\Gamma
is not the decay rate and
m
is not the mass. An even more bizarre time dependence is arrived at using the current standard definition of the
W
and
Z
boson masses,

e^{-i\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}M\left(1+\sqrt{1+\it\Gamma^{2}/M^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\left(1+\it\Gamma^{2}/M^{2}\right)^{-1/2}t}e^{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\it M\left(\sqrt{1+\it\Gamma^{2}/M^{2}}-1\right)^{1/2}\left(1+\it\Gamma^{2}/M^{2}\right)^{-1/2}t}\;,

(36)

again demonstrating that
\it\Gamma
is not the decay rate and
M
is not the mass.
