Propagation of dark solitons of DNLS equation along a large-scale background Journal: Wave Motion
A. M. Kamchatnov, D. V. Shaykin

Abstract
We study dynamics of dark solitons in the theory of the DNLS equation by the method based on imposing the condition that this dynamics must be Hamiltonian. Combining this condition with Stokes’ remark that relationships for harmonic linear waves and small-amplitude soliton tails satisfy the same linearized equations, so the corresponding solutions can be converted one into the other by replacement of the packet’s wave number k k by i ​ κ i\kappa , κ \kappa being the soliton’s inverse half-width, we find the Hamiltonian and the canonical momentum of the soliton’s motion. The Hamilton equations are reduced to the Newton equation whose solutions for some typical situations are compared with exact numerical solutions of the DNLS equation.

1
Introduction

In situations when the soliton’s width is much smaller than the typical length of the
background wave along which the soliton propagates, one can introduce with good enough
accuracy the soliton’s coordinate
x(t)
and describe its propagation as a motion of a
point-like particle through a non-uniform and varying with time surrounding. In this
case, evolution of the background wave is governed by the equations of dispersionless
(hydrodynamic) approximation independently of the soliton’s motion. However, the soliton’s
motion cannot be separated from the background wave evolution: this motion causes a
counterflow around the soliton and such a counterflow changes drastically the soliton’s
dynamics. Well-known examples of this back reaction on the soliton’s motion are the
formation of shelves behind KdV solitons propagating along shallow water with uneven
bottom (see, e.g.,
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
) and change of the frequency of
oscillations of a dark soliton in a Bose-Einstein condensate confined in a harmonic
trap (see, e.g.,
[
5
,
6
]
).

So far, the counterflow effects were studied by different forms of perturbation analysis.
It was recently noticed
[
7
]
, that if the nonlinear wave equation under consideration
can be written in a Hamiltonian form and one assumes that the reduction of the wave evolution
to the soliton’s motion along the large-scale background wave remains Hamiltonian, then such
an analysis can
be considerably simplified. In fact, it is well known that completely integrable equations
are Hamiltonian (see, e.g.,
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
), but their Hamiltonian dynamics
is formulated in terms of the scattering data or related variables in the inverse scattering
transform method. Since a large-scale background wave can be represented as a collection of
a large number of long waves associated with the continuous spectrum of the linear spectral problem,
the assumption that the soliton’s particle-like dynamics remains Hamiltonian seems very
natural. This idea was recently applied to dynamics of KdV
[
7
]
and NLS
[
11
]

solitons where it was also generalized to some non-integrable situations. In this paper, our
goal is to apply this method to propagation of the DNLS soliton.

The DNLS equation

i\psi_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\psi_{xx}-i(|\psi|^{2}\psi)_{x}=0

(1)

has applications to propagation of the nonlinear Alfvén waves in magnetized plasma
(see, e.g.,
[
12
]
and references therein) and of ultrashort optical pulses in nonlinear
fibers (see, e.g.,
[
13
]
). Complete integrability of this equation was established in
Ref.
[
14
]
. We will not use here this fact explicitly (except for the assumption
that the soliton’s dynamics is Hamiltonian) and we will obtain first the Hamilton equations
which govern the soliton’s motion along a non-uniform and time-dependent background wave.
Then we derive the Newton equation which is more convenient for applications and, at last,
compare our analytical theory with numerical solutions of Eq. (
1
) for the initial
conditions corresponding to propagation of solitons along the large-scale rarefaction waves or
non-uniform profiles created by an external potential.

2
Basic equations

Substitution

\psi(x,t)=\sqrt{\rho(x,t)}\exp\left(i\int^{x}u(x^{\prime},t)dx^{\prime}\right)

(2)

and separation of real and imaginary parts transform Eq. (
1
) to the hydrodynamic-like form

\begin{split}&\rho_{t}+\left[\rho\left(u-\frac{3}{2}\rho\right)\right]_{x}=0,\\
&u_{t}+uu_{x}-(\rho u)_{x}+\left(\frac{\rho_{x}^{2}}{8\rho^{2}}-\frac{\rho_{xx}}{4\rho}\right)_{x}=0,\end{split}

(3)

for real variables
\rho
and
u
which can be interpreted as “density” and “flow velocity”,
correspondingly. This system has two important limits.

First, we can consider small-amplitude waves propagating along a uniform background with
constant values of
\rho=\rho_{0}
and
u=u_{0}
. Then linearization of Eqs. (
2
) with
respect to small deviations
\rho^{\prime}=\rho-\rho_{0}
,
u^{\prime}=u-u_{0}
leads after standard calculations
to the dispersion relation for the harmonic waves
\rho^{\prime},u^{\prime}\propto\exp[i(kx-\omega t)]
:

\omega(k)=k\left[u_{0}-2\rho_{0}\pm\sqrt{\rho_{0}(\rho_{0}-u_{0})+k^{2}/4}\right].

(4)

As we see, the uniform state
(\rho_{0},u_{0})
becomes modulationally unstable for
\rho_{0}<u_{0}
,
so we will only confine ourselves to the modulationally stable situations with
\rho_{0}>u_{0}
.

Second, we can consider large-scale waves with typical wavelength much greater than the
dispersion size
O(1)
in our non-dimensional variables,
|\rho_{x}/\rho|,|u_{x}/u|\ll 1
.
Then we can neglect the dispersive term in Eqs. (
3
) and arrive at the dispersionless
equations

\rho_{t}+\left[\rho\left(u-\frac{3}{2}\rho\right)\right]_{x}=0,\quad u_{t}+uu_{x}-(\rho u)_{x}=0.

(5)

The characteristic velocities of this system are equal to

v_{\pm}=u-2\rho\pm\sqrt{\rho(\rho-u)}.

(6)

Naturally, they are real for
\rho>u
and coincide with the phase velocities

\left.\omega/k\right|_{k\to 0}
of linear waves with the dispersion relation (
4
)
in the long wavelength limit. As follows from the condition
u<\rho
, they both are
negative,
v_{-}<v_{+}<0
. Eqs. (
5
) can be cast to the Riemann diagonal form

\frac{\partial r_{\pm}}{\partial t}+v_{\pm}\frac{\partial r_{\pm}}{\partial x}=0.

(7)

for the Riemann invariants

r_{\pm}=\frac{u}{2}-\rho\pm\sqrt{\rho(\rho-u)},

(8)

and the velocities (
6
) are expressed in terms of Riemann invariants by the formulas

v_{+}=\frac{3}{2}r_{+}+\frac{1}{2}r_{-},\qquad v_{-}=\frac{1}{2}r_{+}+\frac{3}{2}r_{-}.

(9)

If some solution of Eqs. (
7
) is found, then the physical variables are given
by the expressions

\rho=\frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{-r_{+}}\pm\sqrt{-r_{-}})^{2},\qquad u=\pm 2\sqrt{r_{+}r_{-}}.

(10)

These equations describe evolution of the background wave.

3
DNLS soliton

Soliton solutions of the DNLS equation (
1
) were found in Refs.
[
12
,
14
,
15
]
.
Here we reproduce this solution in a convenient for us form.

If we look for the traveling wave solution of Eqs. (
3
) in the form
\rho=\rho(\xi)
,

u=u(\xi)
,
\xi=x-Vt
,
V
being the phase velocity of the wave, then Eqs. (
3
) becomes
a pair of ordinary differential equations which can be easily integrated to give

u(\xi)=\frac{A}{\rho(\xi)}+V+\frac{3}{2}\rho.

(11)

\begin{split}\rho_{\xi}^{2}(\xi)=&-\mathcal{R}(\rho)=-\big[\rho^{4}+4V\rho^{3}\\
&+4(V^{2}-A+B)\rho^{2}-4C\rho+4A^{2}\big],\end{split}

(12)

where
A,B,C
are the integration constants. The solution is parameterized more conveniently
by the zeroes
\nu_{i}
,
i=1,2,3,4,
of the polynomial
\mathcal{R}(\rho)
,

\mathcal{R}(\rho)=\prod_{i=1}^{4}(\rho-\nu_{i})=\rho^{4}-s_{1}\rho^{3}+s_{2}\rho^{2}-s_{3}\rho+s_{4},

(13)

where

\begin{split}&s_{1}=\sum_{i}\nu_{i}=-4V,\\
&s_{2}=\sum_{i<j}\nu_{i}\nu_{j}=4(V^{2}-A+B),\\
&s_{3}=\sum_{i<j<k}\nu_{i}\nu_{j}\nu_{k}=4C,\\
&s_{4}=\nu_{1}\nu_{2}\nu_{3}\nu_{4}=4A^{2}.\end{split}

(14)

We assume that
\nu_{i}
are ordered according to

\nu_{1}\leq\nu_{2}\leq\nu_{3}\leq\nu_{4}.

(15)

The soliton solutions appear when two middle zeroes coincide,
\nu_{2}=\nu_{3}=\rho_{0}
, and then they
are equal to the background density
\rho_{0}
of the uniform state along which the soliton
propagates. If
\rho
varies within the interval
\nu_{1}\leq\rho\leq\nu_{2}=\rho_{0}
, then we obtain a
dark soliton; if within the interval
\nu_{2}=\rho_{0}\leq\rho\leq\nu_{4}
, then the soliton is bright.
To be definite, we confine ourselves to the case of dark solitons with
\nu_{1}\leq\rho\leq\nu_{2}=\rho_{0}
.
The standard calculation yields the solution of Eq. (
12
) in the form

\rho=\rho_{0}-\frac{(\rho_{0}-\nu_{1})(\nu_{4}-\rho_{0})}{(\nu_{4}-\nu_{1})\cosh^{2}\theta-(\rho_{0}-\nu_{1})},

(16)

where
\theta=\frac{1}{2}\kappa\xi
and

\kappa=\sqrt{(\rho_{0}-\nu_{1})(\nu_{4}-\rho_{0})}

(17)

is the inverse half-width of the soliton.

Let us express
\nu_{1},\nu_{4}
in terms of the values

(\rho_{0},u_{0})
of the parameters of the flow at infinity. Eq. (
11
) and the first
Eq. (
14
) give

\nu_{1}+\nu_{4}=-4V-2\rho_{0},\quad\nu_{1}\nu_{4}=(2u_{0}-3\rho_{0}-2V)^{2},

so we get

\begin{split}&\nu_{1}=-2V-\rho_{0}-\sqrt{(\rho_{0}-u_{0})(u_{0}-2\rho_{0}-2V)},\\
&\nu_{4}=-2V-\rho_{0}+\sqrt{(\rho_{0}-u_{0})(u_{0}-2\rho_{0}-2V)}.\end{split}

(18)

Substitution of these values into Eq. (
17
) yields

\kappa^{2}=4\left[\rho_{0}(\rho_{0}-u_{0})-(V+2\rho_{0}-u_{0})^{2}\right].

(19)

Whence, the soliton velocity
V
is related with the inverse half-width
\kappa
by the formula

V=u_{0}-2\rho_{0}\pm\sqrt{\rho_{0}(\rho_{0}-u_{0})-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{4}}.

(20)

Comparison with Eq. (
4
) shows that the soliton’s velocity is related with the dispersion
law of linear waves by the expression

V=\frac{\omega(i\kappa)}{i\kappa}.

(21)

This is a particular case of the general Stokes’ remark
[
16
]
(see also
[
17
,
18
]
)
that both linear waves
\propto\exp[i(kx-\omega t)]
and soliton’s tails
\propto\exp[\pm\kappa(x-Vt)]

are described by the linearized equations, so the phase velocity
\omega(k)/k
transforms to the
soliton’s velocity by the replacement
k\to i\kappa
.

If we rewrite Eq. (
19
) in the form

\begin{split}\kappa^{2}&=4\left[\sqrt{\rho_{0}(\rho_{0}-u_{0})}-2\rho_{0}+u_{0}-V\right]\\
&\,\,\,\,\,\times\left[\sqrt{\rho_{0}(\rho_{0}-u_{0})}+2\rho_{0}-u_{0}+V\right],\end{split}

(22)

we find at once that the soliton’s velocity
V
must have the value between the dispersionless
characteristic velocities (
5
),

v_{-}<V<v_{+},

(23)

that is it is always negative. The parameters
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{4}
are always positive since
positive density at the center of soliton is equal to
\rho_{min}=\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{4}>\nu_{1}
.
One can easily find that at the edges of the interval (
23
) the parameters
\nu_{1}

and
\nu_{4}
are equal to

\begin{split}&\nu_{1}(v_{-})=\rho_{0},\\
&\nu_{1}(v_{+})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\rho_{0},&\hbox{for}\quad u_{0}<0,\\
5\rho_{0}-4u_{0}\\
-4\sqrt{\rho_{0}(\rho_{0}-u_{0})},&\hbox{for}\quad 0<u_{0}<\rho_{0};\end{array}\right.\end{split}

(24)

and

\begin{split}&\nu_{4}(v_{-})=5\rho_{0}-4u_{0}+4\sqrt{\rho_{0}(\rho_{0}-u_{0})},\\
&\nu_{4}(v_{+})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}5\rho_{0}-4u_{0}\\
-4\sqrt{\rho_{0}(\rho_{0}-u_{0})},&\hbox{for}\quad u_{0}<0,\\
\rho_{0},&\hbox{for}\quad 0<u_{0}<\rho_{0}.\end{array}\right.\end{split}

(25)

\nu_{1}(V)
and
\nu_{4}(V)
are shown in Fig.

\nu_{1}(V)

(26)

One can easily find that this point is located inside the interval (

),

V_{m}\leq v_{+}(u_{0})
, for

has its maximal

we should

\begin{split}\mathrm{(A)}:\quad u_{0}-\frac{3}{2}\rho_{0}<V<v_{+},\quad V+\frac{3}{2}\rho_{0}-u_{0}>0,\\
\mathrm{(B)}:\quad v_{-}<V<u_{0}-\frac{3}{2}\rho_{0},\quad V+\frac{3}{2}\rho_{0}-u_{0}<0.\end{split}

(27)

If
\frac{3}{4}\rho_{0}<u_{0}<\rho_{0}
, then we get a single interval

\mathrm{(C)}:\quad v_{-}<V<v_{+},\quad V+\frac{3}{2}\rho_{0}-u_{0}<0.

(28)

\psi=\sqrt{\rho}\exp(i\phi)
can be found by integration
of Eq. (

), that is

\phi=\int\left(\frac{\rho_{0}\sqrt{\nu_{1}\nu_{4}}}{\rho}+V+\frac{3}{2}\rho\right)d\xi,

\begin{split}\phi_{A}=u_{0}\xi&-\arctan\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\sqrt{\frac{\nu_{4}}{\nu_{1}}}\tanh\frac{\kappa\xi}{2}\right)\\

where

(31)

Obviously,

\begin{split}&\Delta\phi_{A}=-2\arctan\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\sqrt{\frac{\nu_{4}}{\nu_{1}}}\right)-6\arctan\frac{1}{\gamma}+2\pi,\\

,

,

, tends to zero non-uniformly almost everywhere except a

. So one can consider

.

4
Hamilton equations for soliton motion

We assume that soliton’s width is much smaller than the characteristic background wavelength.
Therefore, we can calculate the soliton’s Hamiltonian
H
and the canonical momentum
p
under
assumption that the background variables
\rho,u
have their values
\rho(x,t),u(x,t)
at the
location
x(t)
of the soliton at the moment
t
. Then Eq. (
20
) can be written as one
of the Hamilton equations

\frac{dx}{dt}=u-2\rho+\sqrt{\rho(\rho-u)-\kappa^{2}/4}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}.

(34)

It is convenient to introduce the variable
\phi
instead of
\kappa
,

\kappa=2\sqrt{\rho(\rho-u)}\sin\phi,

(35)

so that

\frac{dx}{dt}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}=u-2\rho+\sqrt{\rho(\rho-u)}\cos\phi.

(36)

Another important relation follows from preservation of the Poincaré-Cartan integral
invariant by the dispersionless flow (
5
) (see Refs.
[
7
,
11
]
). This
means that in the completely integrable equations, as the DNLS equation, the wave number
k

of a short wavelength packet propagating along large-scale background waves is only a function
of the local values of
\rho
and
u
,
k=k(\rho,u)
, which satisfies in the DNLS case the
equations
[
19
]

\begin{split}&\frac{\partial k^{2}}{\partial\rho}=4\left[\sqrt{k^{2}+4\rho(\rho-u)}-(2\rho-u)\right],\\
&\frac{\partial k^{2}}{\partial u}=-2\left[\sqrt{k^{2}+4\rho(\rho-u)}-2\rho\right],\end{split}

(37)

with the solution

k^{2}=(q+2\rho-u)^{2}-4\rho(\rho-u),

(38)

where
q
is an integration constant determined by the value of
k
at some fixed point in
the
(\rho,u)
-plane. Then, using the Stokes reasoning (see Eq. (
21
) and the text below it),
we find that the inverse half-width
\kappa
is related with the local background variables by
the formula

\kappa^{2}=4\rho(\rho-u)-(q+2\rho-u)^{2}.

(39)

Its substitution into Eq. (
34
) with account of Eq. (
36
) give the integral of motion

u/2-\rho+\sqrt{\rho(\rho-u)}\cos\phi=q/2,

(40)

where we assumed for definiteness that
q+2\rho-u>0
. Differentiation of this identity along
soliton’s path yields

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\frac{du}{dt}-\frac{d\rho}{dt}-\sqrt{\rho(\rho-u)}\sin\phi\frac{d\phi}{dt}\\
&+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\rho(\rho-u)}}\left[(2\rho-u)\frac{d\rho}{dt}-\rho\frac{du}{dt}\right]=0,\end{split}

where
d/dt=\partial/\partial t+V\partial/\partial x
. We eliminate
\rho_{t}
and
u_{t}
with help of Eqs. (
5
)
and find after elementary transformations the formula

\frac{d\phi}{dt}=-\left(\sqrt{\rho(\rho-u)}\right)_{x}\sin\phi.

(41)

Now we can turn to derivation of the Hamiltonian
H
and the canonical momentum
p
for soliton’s
motion. We look for the momentum in the form

p=\rho(\rho-u)f(\phi),

(42)

where
f(\phi)
is unknown function. Then integration of Eq. (
36
) with respect to
p
gives

H=(u-2\rho)p+[\rho(\rho-u)]^{3/2}\int\cos\phi\,f^{\prime}d\phi.

(43)

To find
f(\phi)
, we use another Hamilton equation

\frac{dp}{dt}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}.

(44)

Substitution of Eqs. (
42
) and (
43
) with the use of Eq. (
41
) leads to the equation

f^{\prime}\sin\phi=3\int^{\phi}\cos\phi\,f^{\prime}d\phi,

(45)

which can be easily integrated to give

f=C(\phi-\sin\phi\cos\phi),

(46)

where
C
is an integration constant which appearance reflects invariance of the Hamilton equations
with respect to multiplication of
p
and
H
by the same constant factor. We choose it equal to

C=2
and express
\phi
in terms of
\dot{x}=dx/dt=V
and the local variables
\rho=\rho(x,t)
,

u=u(x,t)
with help of Eq. (
36
). As a result, we obtain

\begin{split}&p=2\rho(\rho-u)\arccos\frac{\dot{x}+2\rho-u}{\sqrt{\rho(\rho-u)}}\\
&-2(\dot{x}+2\rho-u)\sqrt{\rho(\rho-u)-(\dot{x}+2\rho-u)^{2}},\end{split}

(47)

and

H=(u-2\rho)p+\frac{4}{3}\left[\rho(\rho-u)-(\dot{x}+2\rho-u)^{2}\right]^{3/2}.

(48)

It is worth noticing that these expressions reduce to similar expressions for the NLS soliton
by means of replacements
\rho(\rho-u)\to\rho_{NLS}
,
u-2\rho\to u_{NLS}
(see
[
11
]
).

For practical applications, it is convenient to derive the Newton equation for the
soliton’s motion.

5
Newton equation

In Hamiltonian mechanics,
\dot{x}=\dot{x}(x,p)
, but it is impossible to solve explicitly Eq. (
47
)
with respect to
\dot{x}
. Therefore, it is more convenient to transform the Hamilton equation
(
44
) to the Newton equation for the soliton’s acceleration
\ddot{x}
. Differentiation
of Eq. (
47
) with respect to time
t
gives after obvious simplifications

\begin{split}&\frac{dp}{dt}=2\left[(2\rho-u)\frac{d\rho}{dt}-\rho\frac{du}{dt}\right]\arccos\frac{\dot{x}+2\rho-u}{\sqrt{\rho(\rho-u)}}\\
&-4\left(\ddot{x}+2\frac{d\rho}{dt}-\frac{du}{dt}\right)\sqrt{\rho(\rho-u)-(\dot{x}+2\rho-u)^{2}}.\end{split}

(49)

The right-hand side of Eq. (
44
) reads

\begin{split}-\left.\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}\right|_{p}=&-(u_{x}-2\rho_{x})p-\left.\frac{\partial H^{(0)}}{\partial\rho}\right|_{\dot{x}}\rho_{x}\\
&-\left.\frac{\partial H^{(0)}}{\partial u}\right|_{\dot{x}}u_{x}-\frac{\partial H^{(0)}}{\partial\dot{x}}\left.\frac{\partial\dot{x}}{\partial x}\right|_{p},\end{split}

(50)

where
H^{(0)}=\frac{4}{3}\left[\rho(\rho-u)-(\dot{x}+2\rho-u)^{2}\right]^{3/2}
. The derivative

\left.\frac{\partial\dot{x}}{\partial x}\right|_{p}
is to be obtained by differentiation of Eq. (
47
)
with respect to
x
at constant
p
and this gives

\begin{split}\left.\frac{\partial\dot{x}}{\partial x}\right|_{p}=&\frac{(\rho^{2}-\rho u)_{x}}{2\sqrt{\rho(\rho-u)-(\dot{x}+2\rho-u)^{2}}}\\
&\times\arccos\frac{\dot{x}+2\rho-u}{\sqrt{\rho(\rho-u)}}+u_{x}-2\rho_{x}.\end{split}

(51)

The other derivatives are trivial. Substitution of all the derivatives into Eq. (
50
)
followed by equating the result to Eq. (
49
) and elimination of
\rho_{t}
and
u_{t}

with the use of the dispersionless equations

\begin{split}&\rho_{t}+\left[\rho\left(u-\frac{3}{2}\rho\right)\right]_{x}=0,\\
&u_{t}+uu_{x}-(\rho u)_{x}=-U_{x},\end{split}

(52)

where
U(x)
is the potential of external forces, yield after some simplifications the Newton equation

\begin{split}&2\ddot{x}=\frac{\rho U_{x}}{\sqrt{\rho(\rho-u)-(\dot{x}+2\rho-u)^{2}}}\\
&\times\arccos\frac{\dot{x}+2\rho-u}{\sqrt{\rho(\rho-u)}}+\frac{d}{dt}(2\rho-u)-U_{x}.\end{split}

(53)

If there is no external potential, then the equation

2\ddot{x}=\frac{d}{dt}(2\rho-u)

(54)

gives at once the integral of motion

2\dot{x}-2\rho+u=\mathrm{const}.

(55)

Actually, it follows easily from Eqs. (
36
) and (
40
) written for the case
of a uniform background without external forces. This equation is convenient for finding the
soliton’s paths for motion of solitons along rarefaction waves and other large-scale waves
evolving without influence of external forces. Its generalization (
53
) is derived
under the assumption that non-uniformity of the background produced by the external potential
is more essential than change of the soliton’s energy caused by its deformation: in our
derivation of the Hamiltonian we supposed that the soliton keeps its form in slightly
non-uniform background.

\rho

(see Eq. (

r_{+}=0

r_{-}=-2\rho

v_{-}=-3\rho

v_{-}=x/t
and this gives the distribution

\rho(x,t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\rho_{L},&\quad x<-3\rho_{L}t,\\
-\frac{x}{3t},&\quad-3\rho_{L}t<x<-3\rho_{R}t,\\
\rho_{R},&\quad x>-3\rho_{R}t,\end{array}\right.

(56)

We assume that the left edge of the rarefaction wave propagates to the left with velocity

v_{L}=-3\rho_{L}
faster, than the initial soliton velocity
V_{0}

(57)

(59)

As one can see, interaction of the soliton with the rarefaction wave leads to quite a considerable
effect and our analytical theory agrees very well with the numerical solution of the DNLS
equation.

6
Conclusion

The problem of soliton’s motion along non-uniform and time-dependent background, especially
in presence of external forces, is very difficult because of back reaction of the
counterflow caused by a moving soliton in the large-scale background wave. Previously,
different versions of the perturbation theory were developed for solving this problem,
but they were quite complicated and therefore applied to a very limited number of equations
(mainly, KdV and NLS equations). We suggested in Ref.
[
7
]
a new approach.
Although it is basically also perturbative, many difficulties are removed by imposing
the condition that the equations of soliton’s motion must be Hamiltonian. Combining
this condition with Stokes’ remark that some relationships for harmonic linear wave
can be converted into the relationships for solitons by replacement of the packet’s wave
number
k
by
i\kappa
,
\kappa
being the soliton’s inverse half-width, we reduce
derivation of the Hamiltonian and the canonical momentum of the soliton’s motion to a
straightforward calculation. The resulting Hamilton equations can be transformed to
the Newton equation where the role of the external potential can be taken into account
by its inclusion into the equations of the dispersionless (hydrodynamic) flow. The
effectiveness of this method was demonstrated in Ref.
[
11
]
for the NLS dark
soliton, where the results of Ref.
[
21
]
were easily reproduced. In the present
paper, we applied this method to the DNLS soliton which has quite a nontrivial dynamics
without reflection symmetry. Validity of our approach is confirmed by its good agreement
with exact numerical solutions of the DNLS equation. We believe that this approach can
find many other applications.
