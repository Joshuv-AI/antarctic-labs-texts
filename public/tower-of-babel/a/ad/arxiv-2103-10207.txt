Abstract
Petri games are a multi-player game model for the synthesis problem in distributed systems, i.e., the automatic generation of local controllers. The model represents causal memory of the players, which are tokens on a Petri net and divided into two teams: the controllable system and the uncontrollable environment. For one environment player and a bounded number of system players, the problem of solving Petri games can be reduced to that of solving Büchi games. High-level Petri games are a concise representation of ordinary Petri games. Symmetries, derived from a high-level representation, can be exploited to significantly reduce the state space in the corresponding Büchi game. We present a new construction for solving high-level Petri games. It involves the definition of a unique, canonical representation of the reduced Büchi game. This allows us to translate a strategy in the Büchi game directly into a strategy in the Petri game. An implementation applied on six structurally different benchmark families shows in most cases a performance increase for larger state spaces.

Introduction

Whether telecommunication networks,
electronic banking, or the world wide web,

distributed systems

are all around us and are becoming
increasingly more widespread.
Though an entire system may appear as one unit,
the local controllers in a network often act autonomously on only incomplete information
to avoid constant communication.
These independent agents must behave correctly under all
possible uncontrollable behavior of the environment.

Synthesis

[
7
]

by automatically generating correct ones
from a given specification

In case of a single process in the underlying model,

(e.g.,
[
3
]
,

[
25
]
).
Due to the incomplete information in systems with
multiple processes progressing on their individual rate,

asynchronous distributed systems

2.1
P/T Petri Nets

A (marked P/T)
Petri net
is a tuple

\mathsf{N}=(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{T},\mathsf{F},\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{0}})
,
with the disjoint sets of
places

\mathsf{P}

and
transitions

\mathsf{T}
,
a
flow function

\mathsf{F}:(\mathsf{P}\times\mathsf{T})\cup(\mathsf{T}\times\mathsf{P})\to\mathbb{N}
,
and an
initial marking

\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{0}}
,
where a
marking
is a multi-set
\mathsf{M}:\mathsf{P}\to\mathbb{N}

that indicates the number of tokens on each place.

\mathsf{F}(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})=n>0
means there is an
arc
of
weight

n

from node
\mathsf{x}
to
\mathsf{y}
describing the flow of tokens in the net.
A transition
\mathsf{t}\in\mathsf{T}
is
enabled
in a marking
\mathsf{M}

if
\forall\mathsf{p}\in\mathsf{P}:\mathsf{F}(\mathsf{p},\mathsf{t})\leq\mathsf{M}(\mathsf{p})
.
If
\mathsf{t}
is enabled then
\mathsf{t}
can
fire
in
\mathsf{M}
,
leading to a new marking
\mathsf{M}^{\prime}
calculated by

\forall\mathsf{p}\in\mathsf{P}:\mathsf{M}^{\prime}(\mathsf{p})=\mathsf{M}(\mathsf{p})-\mathsf{F}(\mathsf{p},\mathsf{t})+\mathsf{F}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{p})
.
This is denoted by
\mathsf{M}[\mathsf{t}\rangle\mathsf{M}^{\prime}
.

\mathsf{N}
is called
safe
if for all markings
\mathsf{M}
that can be reached from
\mathsf{M}_{0}

by firing a sequence of transitions we have
\forall\mathsf{p}\in\mathsf{P}:\mathsf{M}(\mathsf{p})\leq 1
.
For each transition
\mathsf{t}\in\mathsf{T}
we define the
pre-
and
postset
of
\mathsf{t}
as the multi-sets
\mathit{pre}(\mathsf{t})=\mathsf{F}(\cdot,\mathsf{t})

and
\mathit{post}(\mathsf{t})=\mathsf{F}(\mathsf{t},\cdot)
over
\mathsf{P}
.

An example for a Petri net can be seen in Fig.

Ignoring the different shades and potential double borders for now,

Dots represent the number of tokens on each place in the initial marking of the net.
The flow is depicted as weighted arcs between places and transitions.

2.2
P/T Petri Games

Petri games are an extension of Petri nets to incomplete information games between two teams of players:
the controllable system vs. the uncontrollable environment.
The tokens on places in a Petri net represent the individual players.
The place a player resides on determines their team membership.
Particularly, a player can switch teams.
For that, the places are divided into system places and environment places.
A play of the game is a concurrent execution of transitions in the net.
During a play, the
knowledge
of each player is represented by their
causal history
,
i.e., all visited places and used transitions to reach to current place.
Players enrich this local knowledge when synchronizing in a joint transition.
Then the complete knowledge of all participating players are exchanged.
Based on this, players allow or forbid transitions in their postset.
A transition can only fire if every player in its preset allows the execution.
The system players in a Petri game win a play if they satisfy a safety-condition,
given by a designated set of bad places they must not reach.

Formally, a (P/T)
Petri game
is a tuple

\mathsf{G}=(\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{S}},\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{E}},\mathsf{T},\mathsf{F},\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{0}},\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{B}})
,
with a set of
system places

\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{S}}
,
a set of
environment places

\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{E}}
,
and a set of
bad places

\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{B}}\subseteq\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{S}}
.
The set of all places is denoted by
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{S}}\dot{\cup}\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{E}}
,
and
\mathsf{T},\mathsf{F},\mathsf{M}_{0}
are the remaining components of a
Petri net
\mathsf{N}=(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{T},\mathsf{F},\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{0}})
,
called the
underlying net
of
\mathsf{G}
.
We consider only Petri games with finitely many places and transitions.

In Fig.
2(b)
, a Petri game is depicted.
We just introduced the underlying net of the game.
The system places are shaded gray, the environment places are white.
Bad places are marked by a double border.
This Petri game is the P/T-version of the high-level Petri game described in the introduction.
The three tokens/system players residing on
\mathit{Sys}_{i}

represent the computers.
The environment player residing on
\mathit{Env}
makes their decision
which computer should host a server by taking a transition
\mathit{d}_{i}
.
The system players can then get informed of the decision and react accordingly
as described above.

A
strategy
for the system players
in a Petri game
\mathsf{G}

can be formally expressed
as a
sub-process
of the

unfolding

[
10
]
:
in the unfolding of a Petri net,
every loop is unrolled and every backward
branching place is expanded by
duplicating the place,
so that every transition
represents the unique occurrence
of a transition during an execution of the net.
The causal dependencies in
\mathsf{G}

(and thus, the knowledge of the players)
are naturally represented in its unfolding,
which is the unfolding of the
underlying net with system-,
environment-, and bad places
marked correspondingly.

A strategy is obtained from the unfolding
by deleting some of the branches
that are under control of the system players.
This sub-process has to meet three
conditions:
(i) The strategy must be
deadlock-free
,
to avoid trivial solutions;
it must allow the possibility to continue,
whenever the system can proceed.
Otherwise the system players could win with the respect to
the safety objective (bad places) if they decide to do nothing.
(ii) The system players must act in a
deterministic
way,
i.e., in no reachable marking of the strategy two
transitions involving the same system player are enabled.
(iii)
Justified refusal:

if a transition is not in the strategy,
then the reason is that a system player
in its preset forbids all occurrences
of this transition in the strategy.
Thus, no pure environment decisions are restricted,
and system players can only allow or forbid a
transition of the original net, based on only their knowledge.
In a
winning
strategy, the system players cannot reach bad places.

The remaining branches in the unfolding are cut off in the strategy.
The other two cases (after firing

) are analogous.

2.3
High-Level Petri Nets

While in P/T Petri nets only tokens can reside on places,
in high-level Petri nets each place is equipped with a
type

that describes the form of data (also called
colors
) the place can hold.
Instead of weights, each arc between a place
p
and a transition
t
is
equipped with an
expression
, indicating which of these colors are taken from
or laid on
p
when firing
t
.
Additionally, each transition
t
is equipped with a
guard

that restricts when
t
can fire.

Formally, a
high-level
Petri net is a tuple

\mathit{N}=(\mathit{P},\mathit{T},\mathit{F},\mathit{ty},\mathit{e},\mathit{g},\mathit{M}_{0})
,
with a set of
places

\mathit{P}
,
a set of
transitions

\mathit{T}
satisfying
\mathit{P}\cap\mathit{T}=\emptyset
,
a
flow relation

\mathit{F}\subseteq(\mathit{P}\times\mathit{T})\cup(\mathit{T}\times\mathit{P})
,
a
type function

\mathit{ty}
from
\mathit{P}

such that for each place
p
,
\mathit{ty}(p)
is the set of colors that can lie on
p
,
a mapping
\mathit{e}
that, for every transition
t
, assigns to each arc
(p,t)
(or
(t,p)
)
in
\mathit{F}
an expression
\mathit{e}(p,t)

(or
\mathit{e}(t,p)
) indicating which colors are
withdrawn from
p
(or laid on
p
) when
t
is fired,
a
guard function

\mathit{g}
that equips each transition
t

with a Boolean expression
\mathit{g}(t)
,
an
initial marking

\mathit{M}_{0}
,
where a
marking
in
\mathit{N}
is a function

\mathit{M}
with domain
\mathit{P}

indicating what colors reside on each place,
i.e.,
\forall p\in\mathit{P}:\mathit{M}(p)\in[\mathit{ty}(p)\to\mathbb{N}]
.

Fig.
2(a)
a high-level Petri net is depicted.
As in the P/T case, we ignore the different shadings and borders of places for now.
The types of the places can be deducted from the surrounding arcs.
For example, the place
\mathit{E}
has the type
\mathit{ty}(\mathit{E})=C_{2}=\{\bullet\}
,
and the place
\mathit{A}
has the type
\mathit{ty}(\mathit{A})=C_{1}\times C_{1}
.
Each arc is equipped with an expression, e.g.,
\mathit{e}(\mathit{Sys},\mathit{a})=y
,
and
\mathit{e}(\mathit{a},\mathit{A})=(y,x)
.
In the given net, all guards of transitions are
true
and therefore not depicted.

Typically, expressions and guards will contain variables.
A
mode
(or valuation)
v
of a transition
t\in\mathit{T}

assigns to each variable
x

occurring in
\mathit{g}(t)
, or
any expression
\mathit{e}(p,t)
or
\mathit{e}(t,p)
,
a value
v(x)
.
The set
\mathit{Val}(t)
contains all modes of
t
.
Each
v\in\mathit{Val}(t)
assigns a Boolean value, denoted by
v(t)
, to
g(t)
,
and to each arc expression
e(p,t)
or
e(t,p)
a multi-set over
\mathit{ty}(p)
,
denoted by
v(p,t)
or
v(t,p)
.
A transition
t
is
enabled in a mode

in a marking
\mathit{M}

if
v(t)=\mathit{true}
and for each arc
(p,t)\in\mathit{F}
and every
c\in\mathit{ty}(p)

we have
v(p,t)(c)\leq\mathit{M}(p)(c)
.
The marking
\mathit{M}^{\prime}
reached by firing
t
in mode
v
from
\mathit{M}

(denoted by

3
Canonical Representations of Symbolic Decision Sets

In this paper, we investigate for a given high-level Petri game
\mathit{G}
with one environment player
whether the system players in
\mathsf{L}(\mathit{G})
have a winning strategy (and possibly generate one).
This problem is solved via a reduction to a symbolic two-player Büchi game
\mathcal{G}(\mathit{G})^{c}
.
The general idea of this reduction is, as in
[
13
]
,
to equip the markings of the Petri game with a set of transitions for each system player (called
commitment sets
)
which allows the players to fix their next move.
In the generated Büchi game, only a subset of all interleavings is taken into account, in the way that
the moves of the environment player are delayed until no system player can progress
without interacting with the environment.
By that, each system player gets informed about the environment’s last position
during their next move.
This means that in every state, every system player
knows the current position of the environment or learns it in the next step,
before determining their next move.
Thus, the system players can be considered to be completely informed about the game.
This is only possible due to the existence of only one environment player.
For more environment players such interleavings would not
ensure that each system player is informed (or gets informed in their next move)
about all environment positions.
The nodes of the game are called
decision sets
.
In
[
18
]
,
symmetries in the Petri net are exploited to define
equivalence classes of decision sets, called
symbolic decision sets
.
These are used to create an equivalent, but significantly smaller, Büchi game.

In this section we introduce the new canonical representations of symbolic decision sets
which serve as nodes for the new Büchi game.
We transfer relations between and properties of (symbolic) decision sets to the established canonical representations.
We start by recalling the definitions of symmetries in Petri nets
[
33
]

and of (symbolic) decision sets
[
18
]
.

From now on we consider high-level Petri games
\mathit{G}

representing a safe P/T Petri game
\mathsf{L}(\mathit{G})

that has one environment player,
a bounded number of system players with a safety objective,
and where the system cannot proceed infinitely without the environment.

3.2
Symbolic Decision Sets

A
decision set
is a set

D\subseteq\mathsf{P}\!\times\!(\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{T})\cup\top)
.
An element

\mathsf{C}\subseteq\mathit{post}(\mathsf{p})

\mathsf{p}

who allows all transitions in
\mathsf{C}
to fire.

\mathsf{C}
is then called a
commitment set
.
An element

indicates the player on place
\mathsf{p}

has to

The step of this decision is called
\top
-resolution

In a

\top
-symbol in a decision set

is replaced with a suitable commitment set.
This relation is denoted by
D[\top\rangle D^{\prime}
.
If there are no
\top
-symbols in

,
a transition
\mathsf{t}
is

\forall\mathsf{p}\in\mathit{pre}(\mathsf{t})\ \exists(\mathsf{p},\mathsf{C})\in D:\mathsf{t}\in\mathsf{C}
,
i.e., there is a token on every place in
\mathit{pre}(\mathsf{t})
(as for markings) and additionally,

\mathsf{t}
is in every commitment set of such a token.
In the process of firing an enabled transition, the tokens are moved accordingly to the flow
\mathsf{F}
.
The moved or generated tokens on system places are then equipped with a
\top
-symbol,
while the tokens on environment places allow
all
transitions
that they are involved in.
This relation is denoted by
D[\mathsf{t}\rangle D^{\prime}
.
The

is given by

D_{0}=\{(\mathsf{p},\{\mathsf{t}\in\mathsf{T}\,|\,\mathsf{p}\in\mathit{pre}(\mathsf{t})\})\,|\,\mathsf{p}\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{E}}\cap\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{0}}\}\cup\{(\mathsf{p},\top)\,|\,\mathsf{p}\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{S}}\cap\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{0}}\}

i.e., the environment in the initial marking allows all possible transitions,
the system players still have to choose a commitment set.

3.3
Canonical Representations

In order to exploit symmetries to reduce the size of the state space,
one aims to consider only one representative of each of the equivalence classes
induced by the symmetries.
This can be done either by checking whether a newly generated state
is equivalent to any already generated one, or
by transforming each newly generated state into an equivalent,
canonical representative.
In
[
18
]
we consider the former approach.
The nodes of the symbolic Büchi game are symbolic decision sets.
In the construction, an arbitrary representative
\overline{D}

is chosen for each of these equivalence classes.
This means, when reaching a new node
D^{\prime}
,
we must apply every symmetry
s
to test whether there
already is a representative
\overline{D^{\prime}}=s(D^{\prime})
,
or whether
D^{\prime}
is in a new symbolic decision set.

In this section, we now aim at the second approach and
define the new
canonical representations
of symbolic decision sets.
For that, we first define
dynamic representations
,
and then show how to construct a canonical one.
We use these instead of (arbitrary representatives of)
symbolic decision sets in the construction of
the symbolic Büchi game in Sec.
4
.

Dynamic Representations

A dynamic representation is an abstract description of a symbolic decision set.
It consists of
dynamic subclasses of variables, and a dynamic decision set
where these dynamic subclasses replace explicit colors.
Any (valid) assignment of values to the variables in the dynamic
subclasses results in a decision set in the equivalence class.

Formally, a
dynamic representation

is a tuple
{R}=(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})
,
with the set of
dynamic subclasses

\mathcal{C}=\{Z_{i}^{j}\,|\,1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq m_{i}\}

for natural numbers
m_{i}
, and
a
dynamic decision set

\mathcal{D}
.
A dynamic subclass
Z_{i}^{j}
contains a finite number of variables with values in
C_{i}
.
Each
Z_{i}^{j}
has a cardinality
|Z_{i}^{j}|

that indicates the number of variables.
In total, there are as many variables with values in
C_{i}
as there are colors, i.e.,
\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}|Z_{i}^{j}|=|C_{i}|
.
An
assignment

\mathit{va}:\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}C_{i}\to\mathcal{C}
is
valid
if it respects the cardinality of dynamic subclasses, i.e.,

{|\{c\in C_{i}\,|\,\mathit{va}(c)=Z_{i}^{j}\}|}=|Z_{i}^{j}|
.
Every valid assignment of colors
c\in C_{i}
to the
Z_{i}^{j}
,
1\leq j\leq m_{i}
, gives a partition of
C_{i}
.
A dynamic decision set is the same as a decision set,
with dynamic subclasses replacing explicit colors.
For every decision set
D
in a symbolic decision set
with dynamic representation
{R}=(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})
,
there is a valid assignment
\mathit{va}_{D}

such that
D=\mathit{va}_{D}^{-1}(\mathcal{D})
.
In general, there are several dynamic representations of a symbolic decision set.

3.5
Properties of Canonical Representations

The goal is to use canonical representations instead of
individual decision sets or (arbitrary representatives of) symbolic decision sets as nodes in a two player game.
The edges
({R},{R}^{\prime})
in this game are built from relations

{R}[t.Z\rangle{R}^{\prime}
and
{R}[\top\rangle{R}^{\prime}
,
depending on the properties of
{R}
.
For example, if
{R}
describes nondeterministic situations
in the Petri game, then the edges from
{R}
are built in such a way that
player 0 (representing the system) cannot win the game from there.
In this section, we define the relevant properties of canonical representations.

In
[
13
]
, the following properties of a decision set
D
are defined.
Let
\mathsf{M}(D)=\{\mathsf{p}\in\mathsf{P}\,|\,(\mathsf{p},\top)\in D\lor\exists\mathsf{C}\subseteq\mathsf{T}:(\mathsf{p},\mathsf{C})\in D\}
be the
underlying marking
of
D
.

D
is
environment-dependent
iff

\neg D[\top\rangle
, i.e.,
there is no
\top
symbol in any tuple in
D
,
and
\forall\mathsf{t}\in\mathsf{T}:D[\mathsf{t}\rangle\Rightarrow\mathit{pre}(\mathsf{t})\cap\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{E}}\neq\emptyset
,
i.e., all enabled transitions have an environment place in their preset.

D

contains a bad place
iff
\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{B}}\cap\mathsf{M}(D)\neq\emptyset
.

D
is a
deadlock
iff

\neg D[\top\rangle
,
and
\exists\mathsf{t}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{T}:\mathsf{M}(D)[\mathsf{t}^{\prime}\rangle\land\forall\mathsf{t}\in\mathsf{T}:\neg D[\mathsf{t}\rangle
, i.e.,
there is a transition that is enabled in the underlying marking, but the system forbids all enabled transitions.

D
is
terminating
iff
\forall\mathsf{t}\in\mathsf{T}:\neg\mathsf{M}(D)[\mathsf{t}\rangle
.

D
is
nondeterministic
iff

\exists\mathsf{t}_{1},\mathsf{t}_{2}:\mathsf{t}_{1}\neq\mathsf{t}_{2}\land\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{S}}\cap\mathit{pre}(\mathsf{t}_{1})\cap\mathit{pre}(\mathsf{t}_{2})\neq\emptyset\land D[\mathsf{t}_{1}\rangle\land D[\mathsf{t}_{2}\rangle
,
i.e., two separate transitions sharing a system place in their presets
both are enabled.

In
[
18
]
,
we showed that all
decision sets in one equivalence class
share the same of the properties defined above.
Thus, we say a symbolic decision set has one of the above properties
iff its individual members have the respective property.

We now define these properties for canonical representations.
Since we do not want to consider individual decision sets,
we do that on the level of dynamic representations.
Let
{R}=(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})
be a canonical representation.

{R}
is
environment-dependent
iff

\neg{R}[\top\rangle
, i.e.,
there is no
\top
symbol in any tuple in
\mathcal{D}
,
and
\forall t.Z:{R}[t.Z\rangle\Rightarrow\exists p\in\mathit{P}_{E}:(p,t)\in\mathit{F}
, and

{R}

contains a bad place
iff

\exists p\in\mathit{P}_{B}\,\exists X:(p.X,\top)\in{R}\lor\exists\mathsf{C}:(p.X,\mathsf{C})\in\mathcal{D}
.
Both these properties are rather analogous to the respective property of decision sets.
For termination and deadlocks, we introduce
for the given
{R}

the representation
{R}_{\mathit{all}}

with the same dynamic subclasses, and the dynamic decision set
where every player has all possible transitions
t.Z

in their commitment set.
Since then all transitions that could fire in the underlying marking are enabled,
this substitutes for
\mathsf{M}(D)
.
We say
{R}
is a
deadlock
iff

\neg{R}[\top\rangle
,
and
\exists t^{\prime}.Z^{\prime}:{R}_{\mathit{all}}[t^{\prime}.Z^{\prime}\rangle\land\forall t.Z:{R}[t.Z\rangle
.
Analogously,
{R}
is
terminating

iff
\forall t.Z:\neg{R}_{\mathit{all}}[t.Z\rangle
.
For nondeterminism we have to consider two cases.
The first one is analogous to the property for individual decision sets.

\mathit{ndet}_{1}({R})=\exists t.Z,t^{\prime}.Z^{\prime}:t.Z\neq t^{\prime}.Z^{\prime}\land\exists p\in\mathit{P}_{S}\,\exists X,\mathsf{C}:(p.X,\mathsf{C})\in\mathcal{D}\land t.Z,t^{\prime}.Z^{\prime}\in\mathsf{C}\land{R}[t,Z\rangle\land{R}[t^{\prime}.Z^{\prime}\rangle
.
The second case considers the situation that
two instances

of one
t.Z
can both fire with a shared system place in their preset.

\mathit{ndet}_{2}({R})=\exists t.Z\,\exists p\in\mathit{P}_{S}\,\exists X,\mathsf{C}:(p.X,\mathsf{C})\in\mathcal{D}\land t.Z\in\mathsf{C}\land\exists Z_{i}^{j,k}\in Z:|Z_{i}^{j}|>1\land{R}[t,Z\rangle
.
Finally,
{R}
is
nondeterministic
iff
\mathit{ndet}_{1}({R})\lor\mathit{ndet}_{2}({R})
.

Corollary 2

The properties of a symbolic decision set and
its canonical representation coincide.

For the proof, Theorem
3.2
is applied
to the properties of individual decision sets.

4.1
The Symbolic Two-Player Game

We reduce a Petri game
\mathsf{G}

with high-level representation
\mathit{G}

to a two-player Büchi game
\mathcal{G}(\mathit{G})^{c}
.
The goal is to directly create a strategy
\sigma

for the system players in
\mathsf{G}

from a strategy
f
for player 0 in
\mathcal{G}(\mathit{G})^{c}
.
Recall that a Petri game strategy must be deadlock-free, deterministic, and
satisfy the justified refusal condition.
Additionally, to be winning, it must not contain bad places.

The nodes in
\mathcal{G}(\mathit{G})^{c}
are canonical representations of symbolic decision sets,
equivalence classes of situations in the Petri game.
The properties of canonical representations defined in Sec.
3.5

characterize these situations.
These properties are used in the construction of the game.
As in
[
13
,
18
]
,
the environment in the game
\mathcal{G}(\mathit{G})^{c}
only moves when
the system players cannot continue alone.
Thus, they get informed
of the environment’s decisions in their next steps
and the system can therefore be considered as completely informed.
Bad situations (nondeterminism, deadlocks, tokens on bad places)
result in player 0 not winning.
If player 0 can avoid these situations and always win the game,
this strategy can be translated into a winning strategy for the system players in the Petri game.

The
symbolic two-player Büchi game
with canonical representations

Conclusions and Outlook

We presented a new construction for the synthesis of distributed systems modeled by
high-level Petri games with one environment player, an arbitrary number of system players,
and a safety objective.
The main idea is the reduction to a
symbolic two-player Büchi game,
in which the nodes are equivalence classes
of symmetric situations in the Petri game.
This leads to a significant reduction of the state space.
The novelty of this construction is to obtain the reduction
by introducing canonical representations.
To this end, a theoretically cheaper construction of the Büchi game
can be obtained depending on the input system.
Additionally, the representations now allow to skip
the inflated generation of an explicit Büchi game strategy
and to directly generate a Petri game strategy
from the symbolic Büchi game strategy.
Our implementation, applied on six structurally different benchmark families,
shows in general a performance gain in favor of the canonical representatives
for larger state spaces.

In future work, we plan to integrate the algorithms in AdamWEB
[
19
]
,
a web interface
6
6

6

http://adam.informatik.uni-oldenburg.de/
for the synthesis of distributed systems,
to allow for an easy insight in the symbolic games and strategies.
Furthermore, we want to continue our investigation on the benefits
of canonical representations, e.g., to
directly generate high-level representations of Petri game strategies that match the
given high-level Petri game.

Appendix 0.A
Proofs

In this appendix, we give the proofs of the lemmas and theorems in the paper.
In the main body
we omitted the partition of basic color classes into so called
static subclasses

C_{i}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n_{i}}C_{i,q}

in a symmetric net
\mathit{N}
.
The proofs will take this more general case into account.

The symmetries are restricted because they must respect the partition,
i.e.,
\forall s\in\xi_{\mathit{N}}:s_{i}(C_{i,q})=C_{i,q}
.
A representation
{R}=(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})

of a symbolic decision set
is equipped with a function
\mathit{stat}
that assigns each dynamic subclass

Z_{i}^{j}\in\mathcal{C}
a static subclass
C_{i,q}
,
i.e.,
\mathit{stat}(Z_{i}^{j})=q\in\{1,\dots,n_{i}\}
.
The function satisfies
j<k\Rightarrow\mathit{stat}(Z_{i}^{j})<\mathit{stat}(Z_{i}^{k})

(i.e., the dynamic subclasses are grouped by assigned static subclass).
With the condition

\forall i,q:\sum_{\mathit{stat}(Z_{i}^{j})=q}|Z_{i}^{j}|=|C_{i,q}|
,
each valid assignment of values to the variables in the dynamic subclasses
gives a partition of the static subclasses
C_{i,q}
.
A valid assignment is a function

\mathit{va}:\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}C_{i}\to\mathcal{C}

that maps colors
c\in C_{i}
to dynamic subclasses
Z_{i}^{j}
and is
consistent with the static subclasses (function
\mathit{stat}
),
and cardinality of colors and dynamic subclasses.

A permutation of the dynamic subclasses is
a tuple
\rho=(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{n})

of permutations on
\{Z_{i}^{j}\,|\,1\leq j\leq n_{i}\}

that respects the function
\mathit{stat}
,
i.e.,
\mathit{stat}(Z_{i}^{j})=\mathit{stat}(\rho_{i}(Z_{i}^{j}))
.
We apply a permutation to a representation
{R}=(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})

by keeping
\mathcal{C}
and replacing every occurrence of
Z_{i}^{j}

in
\mathcal{D}
by
\rho_{i}(Z_{i}^{j})
,
and changing the cardinality
|Z_{i}^{j}|
accordingly.
The function
\mathit{stat}
does not change.
We denote the new representation by
\rho({R})
.

Lemma
1
states that minimal representations are unique up to a permutation.
In the general case, minimal representations contain no two subclasses

Z_{i}^{j},Z_{i}^{k}

such that

\mathit{stat}(Z_{i}^{j})=\mathit{stat}(Z_{i}^{j})
and
\operatorname{con}(Z_{i}^{j})=\operatorname{con}(Z_{i}^{k})

(such dynamic subclasses can be merged).

Proof
(Lemma
1
)

It follows directly from the definition that
if
{R}
is minimal, so is
\rho({R})
for any permutation
\rho
.
Let now
{R}
and
{R}^{\prime}
be two minimal representations
of the same symbolic decision set.
We show there is a permutation between
{R}
and
{R}^{\prime}
.
There exist two corresponding representations

{R}_{p}
and
{R}_{p}^{\prime}
,
where all dynamic subclasses are split into
dynamic subclasses of cardinality
1
.
This means that
{R}
can be reached from

{R}_{p}
by merging all subclasses

Z_{i}^{j_{1}},\dots,Z_{i}^{j_{N}}

such that each pair

Z_{i}^{j_{k}},Z_{i}^{j_{\ell}}

satisfies

\mathit{stat}(Z_{i}^{j_{k}})=\mathit{stat}(Z_{i}^{j_{\ell}})\land\operatorname{con}(Z_{i}^{j_{k}})=\operatorname{con}(Z_{i}^{j_{\ell}})
,
and analogously for
{R}^{\prime}
and
{R}_{p}^{\prime}
.
All representations of a fixed symbolic decision set
that only contain dynamic subclasses of cardinality
1

can trivially transformed into each other by a permutation.
Furthermore, we can pick a permutation
\rho

from
{R}_{p}
to
{R}_{p}^{\prime}
such that
if
Z_{i}^{j_{k}}
and
Z_{i}^{j_{\ell}}

are merged in
{R}_{p}
, then
\rho(Z_{i}^{j_{k}})

and
\rho(Z_{i}^{j_{\ell}})
are merged in
{R}_{p}^{\prime}
.
This implies a corresponding permutation between

{R}
and
{R}^{\prime}
.

Now that we proved that minimal representations
are unique up to a permutation, the next task is to prove that ordering
a minimal representation makes it unique,
and we can therefore speak of a canonical representation.
This is formally stated in Theorem
3.1
.
To prove the theorem, we introduce Lemma

.
It states that if two representations are ordered
and equivalent (i.e., can be reached from another via a permutation),
then they have the same symbolic decision set
\mathcal{D}
.

Lemma 2

Let
{R}_{1}=(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{D}_{1})
and
{R}_{2}=(\mathcal{C}_{2},\mathcal{D}_{2})

be two ordered representations of the
same symbolic decision set, and

\rho
be a permutation
such that
{R}_{2}=\rho({R}_{1})

\mathcal{D}_{1}=\mathcal{D}_{2}
.

Proof
(Lemma

)

We denote the matrix of a representation

by
\mathit{mat}_{i}

Since
{R}_{1}
is ordered,

\rho
cannot transform
\mathit{mat}_{1}

into a (lexicographically) smaller matrix.
Aiming a contradiction, assume that
\rho
transforms
\mathit{mat}_{1}
into a bigger matrix
\rho(\mathit{mat}_{1})

Since
\rho(\mathit{mat}_{1})=\mathit{mat}_{2}

this would mean that
\rho^{-1}
transforms
\mathit{mat}_{2}

into a smaller matrix, implying that

is not ordered. Contradiction.
Therefore,
\mathit{mat}_{1}=\rho(\mathit{mat}_{1})=\mathit{mat}_{2}

Since

is just another presentation of
\mathcal{D}_{i}
,
we follow
\mathcal{D}_{1}=\mathcal{D}_{2}
.

Now we are equipped with all tools to prove

Proof
(Theorem

The statement follows

and

Let
{R}_{1}=(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{D}_{1})

and

Lemma

gives us that there

\rho

.
Then we have by

that
\mathcal{D}_{1}=\mathcal{D}_{2}
.
This means for all

(1)
First, we must define concurrency and conflicts in Petri nets.
Consider a Petri net

\mathsf{N}=(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{T},\mathsf{F},\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{0}})

and two nodes
\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y}\in\mathsf{P}\cup\mathsf{T}
.
We write
\mathsf{x}<\mathsf{y}
, and call
\mathsf{x}

causal predecessor
of
\mathsf{y}
,
if
\mathsf{x}\mathsf{F}^{+}\mathsf{y}
.
Additionally, we write
\mathsf{x}\leq\mathsf{y}
if
\mathsf{x}<\mathsf{y}
or
\mathsf{x}=\mathsf{y}
,
and call
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}

causally related
if

\mathsf{x}\leq\mathsf{y}
or
\mathsf{y}\leq\mathsf{x}
.
The nodes
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
are said to be
in conflict
,
denoted by
\mathsf{x}\sharp\mathsf{y}
, if

\exists\mathsf{p}\in\mathsf{P}\,\exists\mathsf{t_{1}}\neq\mathsf{t_{2}}\in\mathit{post}(p):\mathsf{t_{1}}\leq\mathsf{x}\land\mathsf{t_{2}}\leq\mathsf{y}
.
Two nodes are called
concurrent
,
if they are neither in conflict, nor causally related.
A set
\mathsf{X}\subseteq\mathsf{P}\cup\mathsf{T}

is called concurrent, if each two elements in
\mathsf{X}
are concurrent.

\mathsf{N}
is called an
occurrence net
if:
i) every place has at most one transition in its preset, i.e.,

\forall\mathsf{p}\in\mathsf{P}:|\mathit{pre}(\mathsf{p})|\leq 1
;
ii) no transition is in self conflict, i.e.,

\forall{\mathsf{t}\in\mathsf{T}:}\neg(\mathsf{t}\sharp\mathsf{t})
;
iii) no node is its own causal predecessor, i.e.,

\forall\mathsf{x}\in\mathsf{P}\cup\mathsf{T}:{\neg(\mathsf{x}<\mathsf{x})}
;
iv) the initial marking contains exactly the places with no transition in their preset, i.e.,

\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{0}}=\{\mathsf{p}\in\mathsf{P}\,|\,\mathit{pre}(\mathsf{p})=\emptyset\}
.
An occurrence net is called a
causal net

if additionally
v) from every place there is at most one outgoing transition, i.e.,

\forall\mathsf{p}\in\mathsf{P}:|\mathit{post}(\mathsf{p})|\leq 1
.

For a superscripted net
\mathsf{N}^{x}
,
we implicitly also equip its components with
the superscript, i.e.,

\mathsf{N}^{x}=(\mathsf{P}^{x},\mathsf{T}^{x},\mathsf{F}^{x},\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{0}}^{x})
,
as well as the corresponding pre- and postset function

\mathit{pre}^{x},\mathit{post}^{x}
.
Let
\mathsf{N}
and
\mathsf{N}^{\prime}
be two Petri nets.
A function
h:\mathsf{P}\cup\mathsf{T}\to\mathsf{P}^{\prime}\cup\mathsf{T}^{\prime}

is called a
Petri net homomorphism
, if:
i) it maps places and transitions in
\mathsf{N}
into the corresponding sets in
\mathsf{N}^{\prime}
, i.e.,

\forall\mathsf{p}\in\mathsf{P}:{h(\mathsf{p})\in\mathsf{P}^{\prime}}\land\forall{\mathsf{t}\in\mathsf{T}}:h(\mathsf{t})\in\mathsf{T}^{\prime}
;
ii) it maps the pre- and postset correspondingly, i.e.,

\forall{\mathsf{t}\in\mathsf{T}}:\mathit{pre}^{\prime}(h(\mathsf{t}))=h(\mathit{pre}(\mathsf{t}))\land\mathit{post}^{\prime}(h(\mathsf{t}))=h(\mathit{post}(\mathsf{t}))
.
The homomorphism is called
initial
if additionally
iii) it maps the initial marking of
\mathsf{N}
to the initial marking of
\mathsf{N}^{\prime}
,
i.e.,
h(\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{0}})=\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{0}}^{\prime}
.

A(n
initial
)
branching process

\beta=(\mathsf{N}^{U},\lambda^{U})
of
\mathsf{N}

consists of an occurrence net
\mathsf{N}^{U}
and a(n initial)
homomorphism
\lambda^{U}:\mathsf{P}^{U}\cup\mathsf{T}^{U}\to\mathsf{P}\cup\mathsf{T}
that
is injective on transitions with same preset, i.e.,

\forall\mathsf{\mathsf{t_{1}}},\mathsf{t_{2}}\in\mathsf{T}^{U}:(\mathit{pre}^{U}(\mathsf{t_{1}})=\mathit{pre}^{U}(\mathsf{t_{2}})\land\lambda^{U}(\mathsf{t_{1}})=\lambda^{U}(\mathsf{t_{2}}))\Rightarrow\mathsf{t_{1}}=\mathsf{t_{2}}
.
If
\beta_{R}=(\mathsf{N}^{R},\rho)
is an initial
branching process of
\mathsf{N}
with a causal net
\mathsf{N}^{R}
,

\beta_{R}
is called an
initial (concurrent) run of
\mathsf{N}
.
A run formalizes a single concurrent execution of the net.
For two branching processes

\beta=(\mathsf{N}^{U},\lambda^{U})
and
\beta^{\prime}=({\mathsf{N}^{V}},{\lambda^{V}})

we call
\beta
a
subprocess
of
\beta^{\prime}
, if
i)
\mathsf{N}^{U}
is a
subnet
of
{\mathsf{N}^{V}}
, i.e.,

\mathsf{P}^{U}\subseteq{\mathsf{P}^{V}},\mathsf{T}^{U}\subseteq{\mathsf{T}^{V}},\mathsf{F}^{U}\subseteq{\mathsf{F}^{V}},\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{0}}^{U}={\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{0}}^{V}}
;
ii)
{\lambda^{V}}
acts on
\mathsf{P}^{U}\cup\mathsf{T}^{U}

as
\lambda^{U}
does, i.e.,

{\lambda^{V}}|_{\mathsf{P}^{U}\cup\mathsf{T}^{U}}=\lambda^{U}
.

A branching process
\beta=(\mathsf{N}^{U},\lambda^{U})

is called an
unfolding of
\mathsf{N}
, if
for every transition that can occur in the net,
there is a transition in the unfolding with corresponding label, i.e.,

\forall\mathsf{t}\in\mathsf{T}\,\forall X\subseteq\mathsf{P}^{U}:X\text{ concurrent}\land\mathit{pre}(\mathsf{t})=\lambda^{U}(X)\Rightarrow\exists\mathsf{t}^{U}\in\mathsf{T}^{U}:\lambda^{U}(\mathsf{t}^{U})=\mathsf{t}\land\mathit{pre}^{U}(\mathsf{t}^{U})=X
.
The unfolding of a net is unique up to isomorphism.
The unfolding of a Petri game

\mathsf{G}=(\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{S}},\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{E}},\mathsf{T},\mathsf{F},\mathsf{M}_{0},\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{B}})

with underlying net
\mathsf{N}
is the unfolding of
\mathsf{N}
,
where the distinction of system-, environment-, and bad places
is lifted to the branching process:

\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{S}}^{U}=\{\mathsf{p}\in\mathsf{P}^{U}\,|\,\lambda^{U}(\mathsf{p})\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{S}}\}
,

\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{E}}^{U}=\{\mathsf{p}\in\mathsf{P}^{U}\,|\,\lambda^{U}(\mathsf{p})\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{E}}\}
,
and

\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{B}}^{U}=\{\mathsf{p}\in\mathsf{P}^{U}\,|\,{\lambda^{U}(\mathsf{p})\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{B}}}\}
.

A
strategy
for the system players in
\mathsf{G}

is a subprocess
\sigma=(\mathsf{N}^{\sigma},\lambda^{\sigma})

of the unfolding of
\mathsf{G}
satisfying the following conditions:

Justified refusal:

If a transition is forbidden in the strategy,
then a system player in its preset uniformly forbids all occurrences in the strategy, i.e.,

\forall\mathsf{t}\in\mathsf{T}^{U}:({\mathsf{t}\notin\mathsf{T}^{\sigma}}\land\mathit{pre}^{U}(t)\subseteq\mathsf{P}^{\sigma})\Rightarrow(\exists\mathsf{p}\in\mathit{pre}^{U}(\mathsf{t})\cap\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{S}}^{\sigma}\,\forall\mathsf{t^{\prime}}\in\mathit{post}^{U}(\mathsf{p}):\lambda^{U}(\mathsf{t^{\prime}})=\lambda^{U}(\mathsf{t})\Rightarrow\mathsf{t^{\prime}}\notin\mathsf{T}^{\sigma})
.
This also implies that no pure environment transition is forbidden.

Determinism:

In no reachable marking (cut) in the strategy does a
system player allow two transitions in his postset
that are both enabled, i.e.,

\forall\mathsf{p}\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{S}}^{\sigma}\ \forall\mathsf{M}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathsf{N}^{\sigma}):\mathsf{p}\in\mathsf{M}\Rightarrow\exists^{\leq 1}\mathsf{t}\in\mathit{post}^{\sigma}(\mathsf{p}):\mathsf{M}[\mathsf{t}\rangle
. The set
\mathcal{R}(\mathsf{N}^{\sigma})

are all reachable markings in the strategy.

Deadlock freedom:

Whenever the system can proceed in
\mathsf{G}
,
the strategy must also give the possibility to continue, i.e.,

\forall\mathsf{M}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathsf{N}^{\sigma}):(\exists\mathsf{t}\in\mathsf{T}^{U}:\mathsf{M}[\mathsf{t}\rangle)\Rightarrow{\exists\mathsf{t^{\prime}}\in\mathsf{T}^{\sigma}:\mathsf{M}[\mathsf{t^{\prime}}\rangle}
.

An initial concurrent run
\pi=(\mathsf{N}^{R},\rho)
of
the underlying net
\mathsf{N}
of a Petri game
\mathsf{G}

is called a
play
in
\mathsf{G}
.
The play
\pi

conforms
to
\sigma

if it is a subprocess of
\sigma
.
The system players win
\pi
if
\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{B}}^{R}=\emptyset
,
otherwise the environment players win
\pi
.
The strategy
\sigma
is called
winning
,
if all plays that conform to
\sigma

are won by the system players.
This is equivalent to
\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{B}}^{\sigma}=\emptyset
.

(2a)
We give more in-depth details
on the generation of a Petri game strategy
\sigma

from a Büchi game strategy
f
.
Since the edges in
\mathcal{G}(\mathit{G})^{c}
are built from relations
{R}[\top\rangle{R}^{\prime}

and
{R}[t.Z\rangle{R}^{\prime}
between canonical representations
{R},{R}^{\prime}
,
we first have to explain these relations more formally
then in the main body of the paper.

With the canonical representations
of symbolic decision sets,
we must also adapt the relations between these objects,
namely the transition firing and the
\top
-resolution.
For that, we now introduce
symbolic versions of these relations.
In the
symbolic transition firing
,
we consider
symbolic instances

of a transition
t
, where
the parameters are assigned
elements in
dynamic subclasses
instead of specific colors
in basic color classes.
For a representation
{R}

we group the dynamic subclasses by

\forall 1\leq i\leq n:\mathcal{C}_{i}=\{Z_{i}^{j}\,|\,1\leq j\leq m_{i}\}
.
For a transition
t\in\mathit{T}

we then define
\mathit{Val}_{R}(t)=\mathcal{C}_{1}^{t_{1}}\!\times\!\cdots\!\times\!\mathcal{C}_{n}^{t_{n}}

(analogously to
\mathit{Val}(t)=C_{1}^{t_{1}}\!\times\!\cdots\!\times\!C_{n}^{t_{n}}
).
The instance of the

x
-th parameter
of type
\mathcal{C}_{i}

in
\mathit{Val}_{R}(t)

can be specified by a pair

(\varphi_{i}(x),\psi_{i}(x))=(j,k)
,
meaning that the parameter represents
the
k
-th
(arbitrarily chosen)
element of
Z_{i}^{j}
.
Since we maximally have

|Z_{i}^{j}|

different instances of
Z_{i}^{j}
,

k<|Z_{i}^{j}|
must hold.
Furthermore,
k
must be
less than the number of parameters
instanced to
Z_{i}^{j}
.
A
symbolic mode

[\varphi,\psi]
of
t

is defined by two families of functions

\varphi=\{\varphi_{i}:\{1,\dots,t_{i}\}\to\mathbb{N}^{+}\}

and

\psi=\{\psi_{i}:\{1,\dots,t_{i}\}\to\mathbb{N}^{+}\}

satisfying the conditions above.
We denote
Z^{[\varphi,\psi]}=((Z_{i}^{\varphi_{i}(x),\psi_{i}(x)})_{x=1}^{t_{i}})_{i=1}^{n}
,
and
t.Z^{[\varphi,\psi]}

is called a
symbolic instance of

t
.

To fire a symbolic instance
t.Z^{[\varphi,\psi]}

from a representation
{R}=(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})
,
we have to
split
the dynamic subclasses
Z_{i}^{j}

such that the
(by
\varphi_{i}
and
\psi_{i}
)
instantiated elements are represented by
new subclasses
Z_{i}^{j,k}
of
cardinality
1
.
The possibly remaining (non-instantiated) elements
are collected in the additional subclass
Z_{i}^{j,0}
.
The new dynamic decision set
can be naturally derived from
\mathcal{D}
: since the subclasses
Z_{i}^{j}

are split into
\{Z_{i}^{j,k}\}_{k}
,
every tuple
(p.Z,\mathsf{C})\in\mathcal{D}

in which the original subclasses appeared
is replaced by all possible tuples containing the new
subclasses instead.
This
split representation
is denoted by
{R}[\varphi,\psi]
.

A symbolic instance
t.Z^{[\varphi,\psi]}

can be
fired
from the representation

{R}[\varphi,\psi]=:{R}_{s}=(\mathcal{C}_{s},\mathcal{D}_{s})

analogously to the ordinary case
D[t.c\rangle

with the dynamic subclasses
Z_{i}^{j,k}
replacing
the explicit colors
c\in C_{i}
in guards and arc expressions.
This leads to a new representation
{R}_{s}^{\prime}
with same
dynamic subclasses
\mathcal{C}_{s}^{\prime}=\mathcal{C}_{s}

and the dynamic decision set
\mathcal{D}_{s}^{\prime}
that results from firing
t.Z^{[\varphi,\psi]}

from
\mathcal{D}_{s}
(w.r.t the flow function
\mathsf{F}
).
Finally, the canonical representation
{R}^{\prime}
of
{R}_{s}^{\prime}
is found.
The symbolic firing is denoted by
{R}[t.Z^{[\varphi,\psi]}
and
can be described by

{R}\xrightarrow{\text{splitting w.r.t }[\varphi,\psi]}{R}_{s}\xrightarrow{\text{firing }t.Z^{[\varphi,\psi]}}{R}_{s}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\text{canon. rep.}}{R}^{\prime}.

The symbolic instances of a transition
t

form a partition of the regular instances
t.c
,
c\in\mathit{Val}(t)
.

The idea of
symbolic
\top
-resolution

is similar to the
symbolic firing.
A canonical representation which
contains a tuple
(p.Z,\top)

is partitioned into a finer
representation.
The symbol
\top

gets resolved as before,
and finally, the new canonical representation is built.
Partitioning
is a more general case of splitting.
While in the
{R}[\varphi,\psi]
only
dynamic subclasses of cardinality
1

are split off, the
partitions

{R}_{p}

of a representation
{R}

are exactly the representations from
which
{R}
can be reached by merging dynamic subclasses.

This gives, for a canonical representation
{R}
,
that
{R}[\top\rangle
iff

\exists(p.Z,\top)\in\mathcal{D}
,
and
{R}[\top\rangle{R}^{\prime}

iff there is a partition
{R}_{p}

of
{R}

such that
{R}^{\prime}
is
the canonical representation of
{R}_{p}^{\prime}
.
In
{R}_{p}^{\prime}
the dynamic subclasses are copied from

{R}_{p}
, and in
\mathcal{D}_{p}^{\prime}

every
(p.Z,\top)\in\mathcal{D}_{p}
chooses
a new commitment set
\mathsf{C}
.
The symbolic
\top
-resolution is denoted by
{R}[\top\rangle{R}^{\prime}
and can be described by

{R}\xrightarrow{\text{\text{partitioning}}}{R}_{p}\xrightarrow{\top\text{-resolution}}{R}_{p}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\text{canon. rep.}}{R}^{\prime}.

(2b)
Now we describe the algorithm:
Initially, the strategy
\sigma

contains places corresponding to the
initial marking
\mathsf{M}_{0}

in the Petri game, i.e.,
places labeled with
p.c

for every
p.c\in\mathsf{M}_{0}
,
each with a token on them.
They constitute the initial marking of
\sigma
.
Every node
r
in
T_{f}
,
labeled with
{R}=(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})
,
is now associated to a set
\mathit{K}_{r}
of
cuts
–
reachable markings in the strategy/unfolding.
The set
\mathit{K}_{r_{0}}
,
associated to the root
r_{0}
, contains only
the cut
\kappa_{0}
consisting of
the places in the
initial marking described above.
Every cut
\kappa
is equipped with
a valid assignment
\mathit{va}_{\kappa}

that maps
\kappa
to
places in the dynamic decision set

\mathcal{D}
.
Every edge
(r,r^{\prime})
in
T_{f}

corresponds to an edge
({R},{R}^{\prime})

in
\mathcal{G}(\mathit{G})^{c}
, and the edges there again can correspond to
either relations
{R}[t.Z^{[\varphi,\psi]}\rangle{R}^{\prime}

or
{R}[\top\rangle{R}^{\prime}
.

Suppose in the breadth-first traversal of
T_{f}

we reach a node
r
with label
{R}

and set of associated cuts
\mathit{K}_{r}
.
Further suppose there is an edge in
T_{f}

from
r
to a node
r^{\prime}
labeled with
{R}^{\prime}
.
From there, we distinguish three cases:

1) The edge
(r,r^{\prime})
in
T_{f}

corresponds to a relation
{R}[\top\rangle{R}^{\prime}
in
\mathit{G}
.
As we know, this can be depicted as

{R}\xrightarrow{\text{\text{partition }}\phi}{R}_{p}\xrightarrow{\top\text{-resolution}}{R}_{p}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\text{canon. rep. }}{R}^{\prime}
.
We now describe how
\mathit{K}_{r}
changes in these three steps.
In the first step, the partition
{R}_{p}
, there is a
family
\phi
of functions

\phi_{i}:({\mathcal{C}_{p}})_{i}\to\mathcal{C}_{i}

describing the partition.
For every
(\kappa,\mathit{va}_{\kappa})\in\mathit{K}_{r}
,
we change the assignment to a function

\mathit{va}_{\kappa}^{\prime}:\bigcup_{i}C_{i}\to\mathcal{C}_{p}

such that

\mathit{va}_{\kappa}=\phi\circ\mathit{va}_{\kappa}^{\prime}
.
In the second step, the resolution of
\top
,
the set
\mathit{K}_{r}
does not change
(and
we now denote
\mathit{va}_{\kappa}^{\prime}:\bigcup_{i}C_{i}\to\mathcal{C}_{p}^{\prime}
) .
For the third step (the canonical representation)
there again is a partition function

\phi^{\prime}:\mathcal{C}_{p}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
.
We again change the assignment
for every cut
\kappa
to

\mathit{va}_{\kappa}^{\prime\prime}=\phi^{\prime}\circ\mathit{va}_{\kappa}^{\prime}
.
Finally we assign to node
r^{\prime}
the set

\mathit{K}_{r^{\prime}}=\{(\kappa,\mathit{va}_{\kappa}^{\prime\prime})\,|\,(\kappa,\mathit{va}_{\kappa})\in\mathit{K}_{r}\}
.

2) The edge
(r,r^{\prime})
in
T_{f}

corresponds to a relation
{R}[t.Z^{[\varphi,\psi]}\rangle{R}^{\prime}

in
\mathit{G}
and
t
is an system transition.
The relation can be described by

{R}\xrightarrow{\text{splitting w.r.t }[\varphi,\psi]}{R}_{s}\xrightarrow{\text{firing }t.Z^{[\varphi,\psi]}}{R}_{s}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\text{canon. rep. }}{R}^{\prime}
.
Since the first step (splitting) is a special case of partition,
we proceed as in the first case, and get the same cuts
\kappa

with altered assignments

\mathit{va}_{\kappa}^{\prime}:\bigcup_{i}C_{i}\to\mathcal{C}_{s}
.
Since after the process of splitting,
the dynamic subclasses appearing in

Z^{[\varphi,\psi]}

are all of cardinality
1
, there is for each cut
\kappa

only one mode
v_{\kappa}=((c_{i,j})_{j=1}^{t_{i}})_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathit{Val}(t)

such that
\mathit{va}_{\kappa}(v_{\kappa})=Z^{[\varphi,\psi]}
.
For every cut
\kappa
we add a transition
with label
t.d
to the strategy,
the places labeled with
p.c
from
\kappa

in its preset such that
c\in v(p,t)
,
and we add places labeled with
p^{\prime}.c^{\prime}
to the
strategy in the postset of the transition if

c^{\prime}\in v(t,p^{\prime})
.
We then delete the places in the preset
of the new transition from
\kappa
and add the places
in the postset, to get the cut
\kappa^{\prime}
with
the same assignment
\mathit{va}_{\kappa^{\prime}}=\mathit{va}_{\kappa}^{\prime}:\bigcup_{i}C_{i}\to\mathcal{C}_{s}^{\prime}
.
In the third step (canonical representation) we can
again proceed as in the first case, which results in

\mathit{K}_{r^{\prime}}=\{(\kappa^{\prime},\mathit{va}_{\kappa}^{\prime\prime})\,|\,(\kappa,\mathit{va}_{\kappa})\in\mathit{K}_{r}\}
.

3) The edge
(r,r^{\prime})
in
T_{f}

corresponds to a relation
{R}[t.Z^{[\varphi,\psi]}\rangle{R}^{\prime}

in
\mathit{G}
and
t
is an environment transition.
In this case, we proceed exactly as in the second case,
but with one crucial change in the first step
(splitting w.r.t
[\varphi,\psi]
):
instead of choosing
one
function

\mathit{va}_{\kappa}^{\prime}:\bigcup_{i}C_{i}\to\bigcup_{i}{R}_{p}.\hat{C}_{i}

such that

\mathit{va}_{\kappa}=\phi\circ\mathit{va}_{\kappa}^{\prime}
,
we add a pair
(\kappa,\mathit{va}_{\kappa}^{\prime})
for

all
such assignments and all corresponding

t.Z^{[\varphi,\psi]}
.
The rest of the procedure remains the same.

(3)
Finally, we prove that
the algorithm
is correct.
This proof works analogous to

[
13
]
,
where Finkbeiner and Olderog consider the case of P/T Petri games.
The crucial difference is that we associate multiple
cuts in
\sigma
to a node in the tree
T_{f}
.
We have to prove that the generated strategy
\sigma

is satisfying the conditions justified refusal,
determinism and deadlock freedom defined above.
Then,
\sigma
is
winning
,
since
f
does not reach
representations that contain a bad place.

Justified refusal:

Assume there is a transition
\mathsf{t}
in the unfolding
that is not in the strategy, but the preset
of the transition is (so
\mathsf{t}
would be enabled in the strategy).
Let
t.c=\lambda(\mathsf{t})
.
Then, the reason for
\mathsf{t}
’s absence is that
there is a system place
\mathsf{p}
in the preset of
\mathsf{t}

such that on all traversals that reach a representation
{R}=(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})

and a cut with valid assignment
(\kappa,\mathit{va}_{\kappa})

such that
\mathsf{t}
is enabled in
\kappa
,

\mathcal{D}
forbids
\mathit{va}_{\kappa}(\lambda(\mathsf{t}))=t.\mathit{va}_{\kappa}(c)
,
and hence also no transition
\mathsf{\mathsf{t}^{\prime}}

with
\lambda(\mathsf{t^{\prime}})=\lambda(\mathsf{t})

(implying
\mathit{va}_{\kappa}(\lambda(\mathsf{t}))=\mathit{va}_{\kappa}(\lambda(\mathsf{\mathsf{t}^{\prime}}))
)
is in the strategy.

Deadlock freedom:

The generated strategy is deadlock free because
in every branch in the tree
T_{f}

either infinitely many transitions are fired,
or at some point it loops
in a terminating state.
The strategy
f
does not contain representations that are deadlocks.

Determinism:

Aiming a contradiction, assume that there
are two transitions
\mathsf{t_{1}}
and
\mathsf{t_{2}}

in the strategy that have a shared system place
\mathsf{p}
in their preset.
and are both enabled at the same cut
\kappa
in the strategy.

Case 1.

Suppose that during the construction of
\sigma
,

\kappa
is encountered as a cut
(\kappa,\mathit{va}_{\kappa})\in\mathit{K}_{r}

associated to node
r
labeled with representation
{R}=(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})
.
Then
{R}
is nondeterministic. Contradiction (the strategy does not contain nondeterministic representations).

Case 2.

Suppose there exist two cuts
(\kappa_{1},\mathit{va}_{\kappa_{1}})\in\mathit{K}_{r_{1}}

and
(\kappa_{2},\mathit{va}_{\kappa_{2}})\in\mathit{K}_{r_{2}}

such that during the construction of
\sigma
,
first
\mathsf{t_{1}}
was introduced at
\kappa_{1}

and then
\mathsf{t_{2}}
was introduced at
\kappa_{2}
,
with
\mathit{pre}(\mathsf{t_{i}})\subseteq\kappa_{i}
but
\mathit{pre}(\mathsf{t_{1}})\nsubseteq\kappa_{2}

and
\mathit{pre}(\mathsf{t_{2}})\nsubseteq\kappa_{1}
.
We again distinguish two cases.

Case 2a
concerns the situation that

r_{2}
is
below

r_{1}
in
T_{f}
.
Suppose in the Büchi game the symbolic instances

t_{1}.Z^{[\varphi^{1},\psi^{1}]},\dots,t_{n}.Z^{[\varphi^{n},\psi^{n}]}
,
with
n\geq 1
, are fired between
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
,
corresponding to a sequence

\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{0}^{\prime}[\mathsf{\mathsf{t}_{1}^{\prime}}\rangle\dots[\mathsf{\mathsf{t}_{n}^{\prime}}\rangle\kappa_{n}^{\prime}=\kappa_{2}
.
If this sequence removes a token from a place
\mathsf{p}\in\mathit{pre}(\mathsf{\mathsf{t}_{1}})

and puts it on
\mathit{pre}(\mathsf{\mathsf{t}_{2}})
then place
\mathit{pre}(\mathsf{\mathsf{t}_{1}})
and
\mathit{pre}(\mathsf{\mathsf{t}_{2}})
are in conflict, therefore there cannot exist a cut
\kappa
in which both
\mathsf{\mathsf{t}_{1}}
and
\mathsf{\mathsf{t}_{2}}
are enabled. Contradiction.
Thus, a transition that takes a token from a place in
\mathit{pre}(\mathsf{\mathsf{t}_{1}})

puts it outside of
\mathit{pre}(\mathsf{\mathsf{t}_{2}})
.
Consider the first transition
\mathsf{\mathsf{t}_{j}}
that removes a token from
\mathit{pre}(\mathsf{\mathsf{t}_{1}})
.
Then the representation
{R}=(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})
,
label of the node
r
that introduces
\kappa_{j-1}

is nondeterministic, as two instances

t.Z^{[\varphi,\psi]}
and
t_{j}.Z^{[\varphi^{i},\psi^{i}]}
,
corresponding to

\mathsf{\mathsf{t}_{1}}
and
\mathsf{\mathsf{t}_{j}}

are enabled in
\mathcal{D}
.
Then proceed as in Case 1.

Case 2b
concerns the situation that
r_{1}
and
r_{2}

are on different branches in
T_{f}
.
Let
r
be their last common ancestor.
Let
\mathsf{p}
be the environment place contained in the
cut
(\kappa,\mathit{va}_{\kappa})\in\mathit{K}_{r}

that such that
\kappa\leq\kappa_{i}
.
Let
\mathsf{\mathsf{t}_{i}^{\prime}}
be the transition
from
\mathsf{p}
in the path from
r
to
r_{i}
.
Then all transitions added in branches after
\mathsf{t_{1}^{\prime}}

are in conflict with all transitions added in branches after
\mathsf{t_{2}^{\prime}}
.
In particular,
\mathsf{t_{1}}
and
\mathsf{t_{2}}
are in conflict,
and therefore cannot be enabled at the same marking. Contradiction.
