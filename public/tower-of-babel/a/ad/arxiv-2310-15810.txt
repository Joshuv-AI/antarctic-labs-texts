Cutoff for the Glauber-Exclusion process in the full high-temperature regime: an information percolation approach
Hong-Quan Tran

Abstract
The Glauber-Exclusion process is a superposition of a Glauber dynamics and the Symmetric Simple Exclusion Process (SSEP) on the lattice. The model was shown to admit a reaction-diffusion equation as the hydrodynamic limit. In this article, we define a notion of temperature regimes via the reaction function in the equation and prove cutoff in the full high-temperature regime for the attractive model in dimensions 1 1 and 2 2 with periodic boundary condition. Our results show that the equation in the hydrodynamic limit reflects the mixing behavior of the large but finite system. Besides, cutoff is proved under the lack of reversibility and an explicit formula for the invariant measure. We also provide the spectral gap and prove pre-cutoff in all dimensions. Our proof involves a new interpretation of attractiveness, the information percolation framework introduced by Lubetzky and Sly, anti-concentration of simple random walk on the lattice, and a coupling inspired by excursion theory. We hope that this approach can find new applications in the future.

Introduction

The Glauber-Exclusion process, introduced in
[
9
,

, is a superposition of a Glauber dynamics and an appropriately accelerated symmetric simple exclusion process (SSEP) on a lattice. The model is also called Glauber-Kawasaki dynamics in
[
11
]
. Here, we use the term Glauber-Exclusion as in
[
60
]
. The model can be described as follows.

Notations.

Fix a number
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. For any
L\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, let
\Lambda^{d}_{L}:=(\mathbb{Z}/L\mathbb{Z})^{d}
be the torus of side-length
L
in dimension
d
. For any finite set
\Omega
, we call the elements of
\{-1,1\}^{\Omega}
the spin configurations on
\Omega
, and we denote by
\leq
the usual coordinate-wise order on
\{-1,1\}^{\Omega}
. Our state space is
\mathcal{X}:=\{-1,1\}^{\Lambda^{d}_{L}}
, the set of all spin configurations on
\Lambda^{d}_{L}
. For any
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
u,u^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{d}_{L}
, we denote by
x^{u\leftrightarrow u^{\prime}}
the configuration obtained from
x
by exchanging the values at two sites
u
and
u^{\prime}
, and
x^{u,1}
(resp.
x^{u,-1}
) the configuration obtained from
x
by replacing the value at site
u
by
1
(resp.
-1
). We also denote by
\exp(\theta)
the exponential distribution with parameter
\theta
, i.e. the distribution with density
\mathds{1}_{\left\{s>0\right\}}\theta e^{-\theta s}\mathrm{d}s
.

The generator of the symmetric simple exclusion process (SSEP)
\mathcal{L}_{E}
acts on an observable
\varphi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
by

(\mathcal{L}_{E}\varphi)(x):=\sum_{u\sim u^{\prime}}\left(\varphi(x^{u\leftrightarrow u^{\prime}})-\varphi(x)\right),

(1)

where the sum is taken over all pairs of neighbors
\{u,u^{\prime}\}
on the lattice. This means that
\mathcal{L}_{E}
exchanges the contents of two neighboring sites
u,u^{\prime}
at rate
1
.

To define the Glauber dynamics, we first need the following definition.

Flip-rate function.

Throughout the paper, we fix a number
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and a function
c:\{-1,1\}^{B(0,m)}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, where
B(0,m)
is the subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
consisting of all sites of distance at most
m
from
0
. We call this function
c
the
local flip-rate function
.

For any
L
large enough, we identify
B(0,m)
with the subset of
\Lambda^{d}_{L}
consisting of all sites of distance at most
m
from site
0
. The function
c
extends to a
global flip-rate function

\hat{c}:\Lambda^{d}_{L}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
as follows.

1.

\hat{c}(0,\cdot)
is local and given by
c
:

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathcal{X},\;\hat{c}(0,x):=c\left(x|_{B(0,m)}\right).

2.

\hat{c}
is invariant by translation:

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathcal{X},u\in\Lambda^{d}_{L},\;\hat{c}(u,x):=\hat{c}(0,x_{u+\cdot}),

where

\displaystyle x_{u+\cdot}(u^{\prime}):=x(u+u^{\prime}),\;\forall x\in\mathcal{X},\,u,u^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{d}_{L}.

The generator
\mathcal{L}_{G}
of the Glauber dynamics associated with the local flip-rate function
c(\cdot)
acts on an observable
\varphi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
by

(\mathcal{L}_{G}\varphi)(x):=\sum_{u\in\Lambda^{d}_{L}}\hat{c}(u,x)(\varphi(x^{u,-x(u)})-\varphi(x)).

(2)

This means that
\mathcal{L}_{G}
flips the spin on site
u
of configuration
x
at rate
\hat{c}(u,x)
, for any
(u,x)\in\Lambda^{d}_{L}\times\mathcal{X}
.
We suppose that the Glauber dynamics is attractive.

Hypothesis 1

(Attractiveness)
.

For any
x,y\in\{-1,1\}^{B(0,m)}
such that
x\leq y
,

\displaystyle c(x)

\displaystyle\geq c(y)\text{ if }x(0)=y(0)=1,

(3)

\displaystyle c(x)

\displaystyle\leq c(y)\text{ if }x(0)=y(0)=-1.

(4)

Hypothesis
1
implies that, for any
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
such that
x\leq y
, for any
u\in\Lambda^{d}_{L}
,

\displaystyle\hat{c}(u,x)

\displaystyle\geq\hat{c}(u,y)\text{ if }x(u)=y(u)=1,

(5)

\displaystyle\hat{c}(u,x)

\displaystyle\leq\hat{c}(u,y)\text{ if }x(u)=y(u)=-1.

(6)

These conditions are necessary and sufficient to construct a Markovian coupling of two processes whose generators are
\mathcal{L}_{G}
that preserves order. More precisely, suppose that
(Y^{1}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(Y^{2}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
are two Markov processes with generator
\mathcal{L}_{G}
starting at
y_{1},y_{2}
, respectively. Then there is a Markovian coupling of
(Y^{1}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(Y^{2}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
such that, almost surely,

y_{1}\leq y_{2}\Rightarrow\forall t\geq 0,\;Y^{1}_{t}\leq Y^{2}_{t}.

See Theorem 4.11, p.143 in
[
38
]
for more details. When such an order-preserving coupling exists, we say that the process
Y
is attractive.

Although we will work with a general function
c(\cdot)
, we usually use the following example considered by De Masi
et al
in
[
10
]
to illustrate our method.

Example 1

(Example of De Masi
et al
)
.

The model considered is in dimension one, i.e.
d=1
, with the local flip-rate function
c
given by

\forall x\in\{-1,1\}^{\{1,0,-1\}},\;c(x):=1-\gamma x(0)(x(1)+x(-1))+\gamma^{2}x(1)x(-1),

for some
\gamma\in[0,1]
.
This corresponds to the global flip-rate function
\hat{c}
given by

\forall x\in\mathcal{X},u\in\Lambda_{L},\;c(u,x):=1-\gamma x(u)(x(u+1)+x(u-1))+\gamma^{2}x(u+1)x(u-1).

The
Glauber-Exclusion process
is a continuous time Markov process
\textbf{X}=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, taking values in
\mathcal{X}
, whose generator is

\mathcal{L}_{GE}:=L^{2}\mathcal{L}_{E}+\mathcal{L}_{G}.

(7)

This is a superposition of a Glauber dynamics and a SSEP accelerated by a diffusive factor
L^{2}
.

For
\rho\in[-1,1]
, we denote by
\mathfrak{R}_{\rho}
the Rademacher probability measure on
\{-1,1\}
with average
\rho
. More precisely,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{\rho}(1)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1+\rho}{2},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{\rho}(-1)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1-\rho}{2}.

Reaction function.

We denote by
\nu_{\rho}
the product measure of
\mathfrak{R}_{\rho}
on
\{-1,1\}^{B(0,m)}
. We define the polynomial
R(\cdot):[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle\forall\rho\in[-1,1],\;R(\rho):=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\rho}}\left[-2\xi_{0}c(\xi)\right],

where
\xi=(\xi_{u})_{u\in B(0,m)}\sim\nu_{\rho}
. We call
R(\cdot)
the
reaction function
. The reason behind this name will be explained in the next paragraph.

The hydrodynamic limit.

Originally, De Masi, Ferrari, and Lebowitz introduced the model in
[
9
,
10
]
for the infinite lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and proved that the hydrodynamic limit of the model is a reaction-diffusion equation. We recall here the analogous result obtained by Kipnis, Olla, and Varadhan
[
30
]
for our case in a finite box. Let the density field on
(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{d}
at time
t
be defined by

\mu_{t}^{L}(\mathrm{d}u)=\dfrac{1}{L^{d}}\sum_{u\in\Lambda^{d}_{L}}X_{t}(u)\delta_{u/L}(\mathrm{d}u)\;\;\text{on }(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{d}.

Then, when
L
grows to infinity, under appropriate convergence conditions on the initial data, the density field converges weakly to the solution
\rho:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{d}\to[-1,1]
of the following reaction-diffusion equation:

\dfrac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}=\Delta\rho+R(\rho),

(8)

where
\Delta
is the Laplacian in
(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{d}
, and
R(\cdot)
is the function defined above. Equation (
8
) explains the name reaction function of
R(\cdot)
. The intuition behind this result is as follows. Applying the generator
\mathcal{L}_{GE}
to the function
\varphi:x\mapsto x(u)
, we see that

\mathcal{L}\varphi(x)=\left(L^{2}\sum_{u^{\prime}\sim u}(x(u^{\prime})-x(u))\right)-2x(u)\hat{c}(u,x).

Therefore,

\dfrac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t}(u)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}\varphi(X_{t})\right]=\left(L^{2}\sum_{u^{\prime}\sim u}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t}(u^{\prime})-X_{t}(u)\right]\right)-2\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t}(u)\hat{c}(u,X_{t})\right].

Suppose that
\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t}(u)\right]
is well approximated by
\rho(t,u/L)
for some smooth function
\rho
, for any site
u
and any fixed time
t
. Then

\displaystyle L^{2}\sum_{u^{\prime}\sim u}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t}(u^{\prime})-X_{t}(u)\right]\approx L^{2}\sum_{u^{\prime}\sim u}\left(\rho(t,u^{\prime}/L)-\rho(t,u/L)\right)\approx\Delta\rho(t,u/L).

Moreover, the local law around a site
u
at any fixed time
t
is believed to be close to that of a product of
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t}(u)\right]}
, a phenomenon known as
local equilibrium
, see Subchapter
1.2
and Chapter 3 in
[
29
]
for more details. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t}(u)\hat{c}(u,X_{t})\right]\approx\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t}(u)\right]}}\left[\xi_{0}c(\xi)\right]=R\left(\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t}(u)\right]\right)\approx R(\rho(t,u/L)).

These intuitive arguments indicate that
\rho(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfies equation (
8
). Showing the convergence amounts to verifying the above intuition rigorously.

Based on this result, we define the
temperature regimes
as follows.

Temperature regimes.

•

High-temperature regime
: The system is at high temperature if the reaction function
R(\cdot)
has a unique root
\rho_{*}
in
[-1,1]
, and
R^{\prime}(\rho_{*})<0
.

•

Critical-temperature regime
: The system is at critical temperature if the reaction function
R(\cdot)
has a unique root
\rho_{*}
in
[-1,1]
, and
R^{\prime}(\rho_{*})=0
.

•

Low-temperature regime
: The system is at low temperature if the reaction function
R(\cdot)
has at least two roots in
[-1,1]
.

Explanation for the temperature regimes.

Let us explain why we define the temperature regimes for the model as above. Consider the equation (
8
) with the initial condition
\rho(0,\cdot)
constant in space, say
\rho(0,\cdot)=\rho_{0}
for some
\rho_{0}\in[-1,1]
. We then see that
\rho(t,\cdot)
must also be constant in space, so we can safely denote by
\rho(t)
the value of the function
\rho
at time
t
. The equation
(
8
)
then simply becomes an ODE:

\begin{cases}\rho^{\prime}=R(\rho),\\
\rho(0)=\rho_{0}.\end{cases}

(9)

Though simple, the solution of
(
9
)
can exhibit very different behaviors, depending on
R(\cdot)
. Consider the flip-rate function in Example
1
. Then
R(\rho)=-2(1-2\gamma)\rho-2\gamma^{2}\rho^{3}
. One can verify that the system is at high temperature if
0\leq\gamma<1/2
, at critical temperature if
\gamma=1/2
, and at low temperature if
1/2<\gamma\leq 1
.

the phase diagrams of the ODE (
9
) for two different values of
\gamma
.

Let
\rho_{+}(\cdot)
and
\rho_{-}(\cdot)
denote the solutions of the ODE (
9
) with initial conditions
\rho_{+}(0):=1
and
\rho_{-}(0):=-1
. Viewing the diagram, we see that, in the first case, for any initial condition

, the solution of ODE (

\left|\rho(t)-\rho_{*}\right|\lesssim e^{R^{\prime}(\rho_{*})t}
. In the second case,

has 3 roots

In this paper, we study the model in the high-temperature regime.

Hypothesis 2

(High temperature)
.

•

R(\rho)
admits a unique root
\rho_{*}
in
[-1,1]
,

•

R^{\prime}(\rho_{*})<0
.

The primary purpose of this paper is to study the mixing times of the system in the high-temperature regime. We begin by explaining some terminologies.

The mixing times and the spectral gap.

We use the notation
\mathbb{P}_{x}\left[\cdot\right]
for the law of the process
X
starting from
x
. The strict positivity of
c(\cdot)
ensures that the generator
\mathcal{L}^{GE}
is irreducible. This implies that there exists a unique invariant probability distribution
\pi
, and the distribution of the process converges to
\pi
when the time tends to infinity:

\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X},\;\mathbb{P}_{x}\left[X_{t}=y\right]\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}\pi(y).

(10)

This convergence is often measured by the total variation distance,
\textrm{d}_{\textsc{tv}}\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
, defined by

\textrm{d}_{\textsc{tv}}\left(\mu,\nu\right)=\max_{A\subset\mathcal{X}}|\mu(A)-\nu(A)|,

(11)

for any two probability distributions
\mu,\nu
on
\mathcal{X}
. The speed of convergence is quantified by the
mixing-times
:

\textrm{t}_{\textsc{mix}}(\epsilon):=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\textrm{d}_{\textsc{tv}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{x}\left[X_{t}\in\cdot\right],\pi\right)\leq\epsilon\right\},\;\;\epsilon\in(0,1).

(12)

The smallest real part of the non-zero eigenvalues of
-\mathcal{L}_{GE}
, also called the
spectral gap
of
\mathcal{L}^{GE}
, denoted by
gap
, governs the asymptotic speed of convergence to equilibrium:

\texttt{gap}=\lim_{t\to\infty}-\dfrac{1}{t}\log\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\textrm{d}_{\textsc{tv}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{x}\left[X_{t}\in\cdot\right],\pi\right).

(13)

The cutoff phenomenon.

Note that the mixing times and the spectral gap depend on the side length
L
of the system, though we have kept this dependency implicit to lighten the notation. We say that the system exhibits
cutoff
if in the limit where
L
tends to infinity, the asymptotic behavior of
\textrm{t}_{\textsc{mix}}(\epsilon)
does not depend on the precision
\epsilon
anymore:

\forall\epsilon\in(0,1)\;\text{fixed},\dfrac{\textrm{t}_{\textsc{mix}}(\epsilon)}{\textrm{t}_{\textsc{mix}}(1/4)}\xrightarrow[L\to\infty]{}1.

(14)

We refer the readers to the works
[
2
,
12
,
13
]
for the discovery of the cutoff phenomenon and to the book
[
37
]
for an introduction to the subject.

Conjectured universal behaviors.

A stochastic spin system, with an appropriate notion of temperature, under an appropriate time scale, is conjectured to exhibit the following behaviors.

•

At high temperatures, the system mixes fast. The inverse-spectral-gap
\texttt{gap}^{-1}
is
\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
, the mixing times are logarithmic in the size of the lattice, and cutoff occurs.

•

At critical temperatures, the inverse gap is (sub)-polynomial in the size of the lattice, and so are the mixing times.

•

At low temperatures, the inverse gap is exponential in the size of the lattice, and so is the mixing time, and there is no cutoff.

See, for example,
[
24
,
35
,
65
]
for the conjectures.

Results

The first main result of the paper is the following.

Theorem 1

(Cutoff in dimensions
1
and
2
)
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1)
be fixed. For
d\in\{1,2\}
, there exists a constant
\kappa
such that

\dfrac{\log|\Lambda^{d}_{L}|}{2|R^{\prime}(\rho_{*})|}-\kappa\log\log L\leq\textrm{t}_{\textsc{mix}}(\epsilon)\leq\dfrac{\log|\Lambda^{d}_{L}|}{2|R^{\prime}(\rho_{*})|}+\kappa\log\log L.

(15)

Remark 1
.

In our setup, the dynamics is not reversible, and no explicit formula for the invariant measure
\pi
is available, except when
c
is constant. In fact, in
[
20
]
, Gabrielli
et al
showed that the system is reversible if and only if the function
c(\cdot)
is of the following form

c(x)=(a_{1}+a_{2}x(0))h(x),

(16)

where
h(x)
is independent of
x(0)
, and in this case
\pi
is a product of i.i.d. Rademacher. To the best of our knowledge, it is also the only case that an explicit formula for
\pi
is available. However, one can show that if a function
c(\cdot)
satisfies the reversibility condition (
16
), then it is attractive if and only if it is a constant function.

Our second main result is about the spectral gap of the system.

Theorem 2

(Spectral gap)
.

For any dimension
d
,

\liminf_{L\to\infty}\texttt{gap}\geq\dfrac{1}{|R(\rho_{*})|}.

(17)

Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
together confirm the conjectured behaviors of the system in the full high-temperature regime in dimensions
1
and
2
. Furthermore, they connect the mixing behaviors of the Glauber-Exclusion process with the behaviors of the solutions of the ODE (
9
).

Our third result investigates the mixing times in higher dimensions.

Theorem 3

(Pre-cutoff in higher dimensions)
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1)
be fixed. For any
d\geq 3
, there exists a constant
\kappa
such that

\dfrac{1}{d}\dfrac{\log|\Lambda^{d}_{L}|}{|R^{\prime}(\rho_{*})|}-\kappa\leq\textrm{t}_{\textsc{mix}}(\epsilon)\leq\dfrac{\log|\Lambda^{d}_{L}|}{|R^{\prime}(\rho_{*})|}+\kappa\sqrt{\log L}.

(18)

Theorem
3
says that the magnitude of the mixing times in all dimensions is
\log L
. Furthermore, the pre-factor in front of
\log L
is bounded between two constants independent of the precision
\epsilon
. This phenomenon is known as
pre-cutoff
; see Chapter 18 in
[
37
]
for more details. Establishing cutoff for the process in dimensions
d\geq 3
remains an open problem. We believe that neither the lower nor the upper bounds in Theorem
3
is optimal. Furthermore, our proof of the upper bound in dimensions
1
and
2
relies on the fact that the simple random walks in dimensions
1
or
2
is recurrent, which is no longer valid for higher dimensions. We do not know if this is just a coincidence.
The Interchange Process, a Markov process closely related to the Exclusion process, is conjectured to behave somehow differently in dimensions
d\geq 3
compared to
d\in\{1,2\}
in
[
62
]
. This fact is verified for all dimensions
d\geq 5
in
[
18
]
.
We do not know whether this phase transition in infinite volume is connected to a change of mixing behavior in finite volume.

Nevertheless, the upper bound in Theorem
3
is already better than that in
[
60
]
. In
[
60
]
, the authors impose the assumption that the derivative
R^{\prime}
of
R
is uniformly negative, i.e.
\max_{\rho\in[-1,1]}R^{\prime}(\rho)<0,

and prove the upper bound
\dfrac{\log|\Lambda^{d}_{L}|}{\left|\max_{\rho\in[-1,1]}R^{\prime}(\rho)\right|}
on the mixing time. Hence, our assumption is weaker, and the pre-factor in front of
\log L
is also sharper.

Example 2
.

Let
\theta
be a strictly positive number. In dimension one, we consider the local flip-rate function
c(\cdot):\{-1,1\}^{\{-1,0,1\}}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
defined by

\forall x\in\{-1,1\}^{\{-1,0,1\}},\;c(x):=\theta+2\times\mathds{1}_{\left\{x(0)=1,\;x(1)=-1\right\}}.

(19)

The corresponding global flip-rate function
\hat{c}
is given by

\hat{c}(u,x):=\theta+2\times\mathds{1}_{\left\{x(u)=1,\;x(u+1)=-1\right\}}.

(20)

It is not hard to see that the function
c(\cdot)
satisfies Hypothesis
1
. The corresponding reaction function is

R(\rho)=\rho^{2}-2\theta\rho-1.

(21)

By direct computation, one can show that
R(\rho)
has a unique root in the interval
[-1,1]
:

\rho_{*}=\theta-\sqrt{\theta^{2}+1},

and moreover,

R^{\prime}(\rho_{*})<0.

However, if
0<\theta<1
, then
\forall\rho\in(\theta,1],\,R^{\prime}(\rho)>0
. This means that
R(\rho)
satisfies our conditions but does not satisfy the condition in
[
60
]
.

Structure of the proof.

In Section
3
, we interpret the attractiveness in a more intuitive way. We also discuss some properties of monotone boolean functions, which will be used later in our proof. In Section
4
, we construct the dual coupling, which will subsequently be used in Section
5
to prove Theorem
2
, Theorem
3
, and the lower bound in Theorem
1
. Finally, in Section
6
, we prove the upper bound in Theorem
1
.
For simplicity, we will assume that

. Nevertheless, many ingredients are valid for other dimensions, and we will comment on how to adapt the proof to higher dimensions when necessary.

3
Monotone boolean functions and attractiveness

This section aims to discuss some properties of monotone boolean functions and to prove Proposition
7
, which allows us to interpret the attractiveness more intuitively.

3.1
Monotone boolean functions

Definition 4

(Boolean function)
.

A
boolean function
is a function from some hypercube
\{-1,1\}^{n},\,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, into
\{-1,1\}
. A boolean function
f
is increasing if
x\leq y\Rightarrow f(x)\leq f(y)
, for any
x,y\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
.

Definition 5

(Pivotal set)
.

For an increasing boolean function
f
on
\{-1,1\}^{n}
, its
pivotal set
is given by

\mathsf{Piv}\left(f\right):=\{j\in[n]:\exists x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\{-1,1\}^{n}\text{ such that }f(x^{j,1})=1;\;f(x^{j,-1})=-1\}.

(22)

In particular, to evaluate the value of an increasing boolean function
f
on a spin configuration
x
, we do not need to know every coordinate of
x
but only the coordinates in
\mathsf{Piv}\left(f\right)
. The following lemma is elementary but very useful in our proof.

Lemma 6

(Pivotal sets of monotone boolean functions)
.

For any increasing boolean function
f
on
\{-1,1\}^{n}
,

\mathsf{Piv}\left(f\right)\neq\O\iff f(1,\dots,1)=1\text{ and }f(-1,\dots,-1)=-1.

In particular, for any increasing boolean function
f
on
\{-1,1\}
,

|\mathsf{Piv}\left(f\right)|=\dfrac{f(1)-f(-1)}{2}.

3.2
Interpretation of attractiveness

We canonically identify the functions on
\mathcal{X}
which depend only on the coordinates in
B(0,m)
with the functions on
\{-1,1\}^{B(0,m)}
. We now give a more intuitive interpretation of the attractiveness.

Proposition 7

(Interpretation of attractiveness)
.

There exist
q\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and increasing boolean functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{q}
on
\{-1,1\}^{B(0,m)}
and positive numbers
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{q}
, such that

\forall x\in\{-1,1\}^{B(0,m)},\;c(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{q}\lambda_{i}\mathds{1}_{\left\{f_{i}(x)=-x(0)\right\}}.

(23)

In particular, for any function
\varphi
on
\mathcal{X}
,

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathcal{X},\;\mathcal{L}_{G}\varphi(x)=\sum_{u\in\Lambda_{L}}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\lambda_{i}\left(\varphi(x^{u,f_{i}(x_{u+\cdot})})-\varphi(x)\right).

(24)

Moreover, we can choose
f_{1}\equiv 1,\;f_{2}\equiv-1
and
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
strictly positive.

Interpretation.

Proposition
7
is of independent interest. It implies that the technical conditions (
5
), (
6
) for the attractiveness of the Glauber dynamics can be replaced by a more intuitive interpretation: for each site
u
,
x(u)
is replaced by
f_{i}(x_{u+\cdot})
at rate
\lambda_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq q
. The monotonicity of
(f_{i})_{1\leq i\leq q}
clearly implies that the system is attractive. In fact, for two processes
(Y^{1}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(Y^{2}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
whose generator is
\mathcal{L}_{G}
, one can construct a coupling of them by updating the same sites simultaneously using the same functions. This coupling preserves the order thanks to the monotonicity of
(f_{i})_{1\leq i\leq q}
. Besides, the updates given by deterministic functions
f_{1}\equiv 1
or
f_{2}\equiv-1
can be carried out without looking at the spins of any sites. We say that these updates are
oblivious
. We will see that oblivious updates play a crucial role in our proof.

Illustration by Example
1
.

For the flip-rate function considered in Example
1
, one can verify that the following functions and rates satisfy Proposition
7
. For any
x=(x(-1),x(0),x(1))\in\{-1,1\}^{\{-1,0,1\}}
,

\displaystyle f_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=1,\;

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)^{2},

\displaystyle f_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=-1,\;

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)^{2},

\displaystyle f_{3}(x)

\displaystyle=\text{sgn}(x(-1)+x(0)+x(1)),\;

\displaystyle\lambda_{3}

\displaystyle=4\gamma^{2},

\displaystyle f_{4}(x)

\displaystyle=x(-1),\;

\displaystyle\lambda_{4}

\displaystyle=2(\gamma-\gamma^{2}),

\displaystyle f_{5}(x)

\displaystyle=x(1),\;

\displaystyle\lambda_{5}

\displaystyle=2(\gamma-\gamma^{2}).

Here
sgn
denotes the sign function:
\text{sgn}(s)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $s>0$},\\
0&\text{if $s=0$},\\
-1&\text{if $s<0$}.\end{cases}

We first need the following lemma to prove Proposition
7
.

Lemma 8

(Decomposition of monotone functions)
.

Let
\Omega
be a finite set with a partial order
\prec
. Let
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be an increasing function, meaning that
x\prec y\Rightarrow f(x)\leq f(y)
. Then there exist
q\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and increasing (not boolean) functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{q}:\Omega\to\{0,1\}
and positive numbers
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{q}
such that

\displaystyle f(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{q}\lambda_{i}f_{i}(\cdot).

(25)

Moreover, we can choose
f_{1}(\cdot)=\mathds{1}_{\left\{f(\cdot)>0\right\}}
and
\lambda_{1}=\mathds{1}_{\left\{f\not\equiv 0\right\}}\min\limits_{\{f(\cdot)>0\}}f
.

Lemma
8
can be proved by induction on the size of the support of the function
f
. We omit the proof here. Now we prove Proposition
7
.

Proof of Proposition
7
.

First we prove that (
23
) implies (
24
). Indeed, if (
23
) is true, then by definition of
\hat{c}(\cdot)
, for any
u
in
\Lambda_{L}
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\hat{c}(u,x)=\sum_{i=1}^{q}\lambda_{i}\mathds{1}_{\left\{f_{i}(x_{u+\cdot})=-x(u)\right\}}.

(26)

Therefore, for any function
\varphi
on
\mathcal{X}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{G}\varphi(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in\Lambda_{L}}\hat{c}(u,x)(\varphi(x^{u,-x(u)})-\varphi(x))

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in\Lambda_{L}}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\lambda_{i}\mathds{1}_{\left\{f_{i}(x_{u+\cdot})=-x(u)\right\}}(\varphi(x^{u,-x(u)})-\varphi(x))

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in\Lambda_{L}}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\lambda_{i}\mathds{1}_{\left\{f_{i}(x_{u+\cdot})=-x(u)\right\}}(\varphi(x^{u,f_{i}(x_{u+\cdot})})-\varphi(x))

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in\Lambda_{L}}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\lambda_{i}(\varphi(x^{u,f_{i}(x_{u+\cdot})})-\varphi(x)),

where the second equality is due to (

), the third equality is because
x^{u,f_{i}(x_{u+\cdot})}=x^{u,-x(u)}
whenever
f_{i}(x_{u+\cdot})=-x(u)
, and the last equality is because
\varphi(x^{u,f_{i}(x_{u+\cdot})})-\varphi(x)=0
whenever
f_{i}(x_{u+\cdot})=x(u)
. It remains to show (
23
).

Let
n:=\left|B(0,m)\right|-1.
We identify the set
{B(0,m)}
with
\{0,1,\dots,n\}
such that the site
0
is identified with number
0
.
Then (
23
) is reformulated as follows: there exist increasing boolean functions
\tilde{f}_{1},\dots,\tilde{f}_{q}
on
\{-1,1\}^{n+1}
and positive numbers
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{q}
such that, for any
\xi_{0},\dots,\xi_{n}\in\{-1,1\}

c(\xi_{0},\dots,\xi_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{q}\lambda_{i}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\tilde{f}_{i}(\xi_{0},\dots,\xi_{n})=-\xi_{0}\right\}}.

(27)

Note that Hypothesis
1
implies that
c
is a positive function on
\{-1,1\}^{n+1}
such that
c(-1,\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})
is increasing and
c(1,\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})
is decreasing as functions of
(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})
.

We can now apply Lemma
8
to the function

to conclude that there exist increasing functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{q_{1}}
from
\{-1,1\}^{n}
to
\{0,1\}
and positive numbers
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{q_{1}}
, for some
q_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, such that, for any
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}\in\{-1,1\}
,

c(-1,\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{q_{1}}\lambda_{i}f_{i}(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}).

(28)

Moreover, we can choose

\displaystyle f_{1}(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{\left\{c(-1,\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})>0\right\}}\equiv 1,

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=\min_{\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}\in\{-1,1\}}c(-1,\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})>0.

Here, we have used the fact that
c(\cdot)
only takes strictly positive values.
We then define the boolean functions
(\tilde{f}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq q_{1}}
on
\{-1,1\}^{n+1}
by

\tilde{f}_{i}(\xi_{0},\dots\xi_{n})=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }\xi_{0}=1,\\
2f_{i}(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})-1&\text{ if }\xi_{0}=-1.\end{cases}

It is easy to see that
\tilde{f}_{i}
is increasing for any

.
Note also that
\tilde{f}_{1}\equiv 1
.

Let
\varphi_{1}:\{-1,1\}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be defined by, for any
\xi_{0},\dots,\xi_{n}\in\{-1,1\}
,

\varphi_{1}(\xi_{0},\dots,\xi_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{q_{1}}\lambda_{i}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\tilde{f}_{i}(\xi_{0},\dots,\xi_{n})=-\xi_{0}\right\}}.

Noting that
\mathds{1}_{\left\{\tilde{f}_{i}(1,\xi_{1}\dots,\xi_{n})=-1\right\}}=0
and
\mathds{1}_{\left\{\tilde{f}_{i}(-1,\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})=1\right\}}=\mathds{1}_{\left\{f_{i}(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})=1\right\}}=f_{i}(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}(1,\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}(-1,\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})

\displaystyle=c(-1,\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}).

Similarly
c(1,\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})
is a positive decreasing function of
(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})
. We can still apply Lemma
8
, by replacing the relation
\leq
by
\geq
, to conclude that there exist decreasing functions (increasing functions w.r.t relation
\geq

and positive numbers
\lambda^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\lambda^{\prime}_{q_{2}}
such that

c(1,\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{q_{2}}\lambda^{\prime}_{i}g_{i}(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}).

(29)

By a similar argument, we claim that the boolean functions
(\tilde{f}_{q_{1}+i})_{1\leq i\leq q_{2}}
defined by

\tilde{f}_{q_{1}+i}(\xi_{0},\dots\xi_{n})=\begin{cases}1-2g_{i}(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})&\text{ if }\xi_{0}=1,\\

are increasing. Similarly, we can choose
\lambda^{\prime}_{1}>0
and

. Hence
\tilde{f}_{q_{1}+1}\equiv-1
.
Similarly, we define
\varphi_{2}:\{-1,1\}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
by, for all
\xi_{0},\dots,\xi_{n}\in\{-1,1\}

\varphi_{2}(\xi_{0},\dots,\xi_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{q_{2}}\lambda^{\prime}_{i}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\tilde{f}_{q_{1}+i}(\xi_{0},\dots,\xi_{n})=-\xi_{0}\right\}}.

\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle\varphi_{2}(1,\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})

\displaystyle=c(1,\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}),

\displaystyle\varphi_{2}(-1,\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})

\displaystyle=0.

Hence

c=\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2}.

We can set
\lambda_{q+i}=\lambda^{\prime}_{i},\;1\leq i\leq q_{2}
to write

c(\xi_{0},\dots,\xi_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{q_{1}+q_{2}}\lambda_{i}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\tilde{f}_{i}(\xi_{0},\dots,\xi_{n})=-\xi_{0}\right\}}.

(30)

We can renumber these functions and their coefficients such that
\tilde{f}_{1}\equiv 1,\,\tilde{f}_{2}\equiv-1
. So we have established (
27
). This finishes our proof.

4
Dual coupling

4.1
Graphical Construction

We call
\Lambda_{L}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
the
space-time slab
, where each slice
\Lambda_{L}\times\{t\}
is equipped with the graph structure of
\Lambda_{L}
. We identify
\Lambda_{L}
with the subset
\{0,\dots,L-1\}
of
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.

We introduce a notation essential to our proof.

A collection of marks.

A collection of marks
\mathcal{C}
is a pair
(\mathcal{C}^{\text{exclusion}},\mathcal{C}^{\text{glauber}})
, where
\mathcal{C}^{\text{exclusion}}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\text{glauber}}
are subsets of
\Lambda_{L}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
[q]\times\Lambda_{L}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
, respectively, that satisfy the following conditions.

•

There is at most one mark at a time:

\displaystyle\forall t>0,\,\left|\mathcal{C}^{\text{exclusion}}\cap\Lambda_{L}\times\{t\}\right|+\left|\mathcal{C}^{\text{glauber}}\cap[q]\times\Lambda_{L}\times\{t\}\right|\leq 1.

•

\mathcal{C}
is locally finite in time: for any
0<t_{1}<t_{2}
,

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{C}^{\text{exclusion}}\cap\Lambda_{L}\times[t_{1},t_{2}]\right|+\left|\mathcal{C}^{\text{glauber}}\cap[q]\times\Lambda_{L}\times[t_{1},t_{2}]\right|<\infty.

In other words,
\mathcal{C}
can be identified with a counting measure on
(\Lambda_{L}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})\cup([q]\times\Lambda_{L}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})
whose projection onto
\mathbb{R}_{+}
has no multiple point and is locally finite. We call an element
(u,t)\in\mathcal{C}^{\text{exclusion}}
the exclusion mark on the edge
(u,u+1)
at time
t
and an element
(i,u,t)\in\mathcal{C}^{\text{glauber}}
the Glauber mark of type
i
on site
u
at time
t
.

Effect of a collection of marks.

Given a collection of marks
\mathcal{C}
and a configuration
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
. The process
(X^{x_{0}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is defined recursively according to the following rules.

•

X^{x_{0}}_{0}=x_{0}
(
x_{0}
is the initial configuration).

•

The process
(X^{x_{0}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is piecewise constant and can only jump when a mark appears.

•

When we see an exclusion mark on the edge
(u,u+1)
, we make the transition
x\mapsto x^{u\leftrightarrow u+1}
(exchange the spins at sites
u
and
u+1
).

•

When we see a Glauber mark of type
i
at site
u
, we make the transition
x\mapsto x^{u,f_{i}(x_{u+\cdot})}
(update site
u
using the function
f_{i}
).

Roughly speaking, the marks tell us how the process
(X^{x_{0}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is updated. We have kept the dependency on
\mathcal{C}
implicit to lighten the notation.
The Glauber-Exclusion process associated with the generator
\mathcal{L}_{GE}
can be constructed as follows.

Graphical Construction 1.

Let the background process

\Xi=\left((\Xi^{\text{exclusion}}_{u})_{0\leq u\leq L-1},(\Xi^{\text{Glauber}}_{i,u})_{1\leq i\leq q,\,0\leq u\leq L-1}\right)

(33)

be defined as follows.
(\Xi^{\text{exclusion}}_{u})_{0\leq u\leq L-1},(\Xi^{\text{Glauber}}_{i,u})_{1\leq i\leq q,\,0\leq u\leq L-1}
are independent homogeneous Poisson processes, and

•

\Xi^{\text{exclusion}}_{u}
is of intensity
L^{2}
,
0\leq u\leq L-1
,

•

\Xi^{\text{Glauber}}_{i,u}
is of intensity
\lambda_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq q,\,0\leq u\leq L-1
.

Almost surely, a realization of
\Xi
naturally defines a collection of marks
\mathcal{C}
as follows. For any
(i,u,t)\in[q]\times\Lambda_{L}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

•

(u,t)\in\mathcal{C}^{\text{exclusion}}
iff the process
\Xi^{\text{exclusion}}_{u}
jumps at time
t
,

•

(i,u,t)\in\mathcal{C}^{\text{glauber}}
iff the process
\Xi^{\text{Glauber}}_{i,u}
jumps at time
t
.

Then the process
(X^{x_{0}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
constructed by the rules above is a Markov process with generator
\mathcal{L}^{GE}
starting from configuration
x_{0}
. An illustration is given in Figure
2
, where each exclusion mark is naturally drawn as a left-right arrow on the corresponding edge, and each Glauber mark is drawn as
\times
on the corresponding site, with the corresponding update function next to it.

Remark 3
.

In the future, we may construct the (random) collection of marks
\mathcal{C}
differently, but as long as it has the same distribution as the one given by
\Xi
, the process
(X^{x_{0}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
constructed by the above rules is still a Markov process associated with generator
\mathcal{L}^{GE}
starting at
x_{0}
.

The alphabet.

Let
\mathcal{A}:=\bigcup\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}_{+}\times B(0,m)^{i})
be the set of all words beginning with an element in
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
followed by elements in
B(0,m)
. We define a lexicographical order on
\mathcal{A}
using the usual orders on
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
B(0,m)
. For any word
\mathsf{w}\in\mathcal{A}
, we call the elements of
\{\mathsf{w}\}\times B(0,m)
the children of
\mathsf{w}
. The alphabet
\mathcal{A}
will be used to label the branching processes that naturally arise when we study the
update history
of our process.

Update history.

Consider a collection of marks
\mathcal{C}
and the resulting process

2.

\mathsf{w}

\mathcal{C}_{his}(E)
when we reverse the time interval
[0,t]

\mathcal{C}^{bw}_{his}(E)

•

For any particle
\mathsf{w}
reaching time

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}_{\mathsf{w}}:=x(u).

For any particle
\mathsf{w}
not reaching time

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}_{\mathsf{w}}:=f_{i}((\mathfrak{s}_{\mathsf{w}^{\prime}})_{\mathsf{w}^{\prime}\in\{\mathsf{w}\}\times B(0,m)}).

\displaystyle\forall u\in E,\,\mathfrak{s}_{u}=X^{x}_{t}(u),

4.2
The branching exclusion process (BEP)

We fix a subset
E
of
\Lambda_{L}
in this subsection.
We claim that the history itself, viewed backward in time, is a Markov process, called the
branching exclusion process
(BEP), defined as follows.

Definition of the BEP.

We consider the following way of putting marks and particles in the space-time slab. We go forward in time.

1.

At time
0
, at each site of
E
, we put a particle labeled by the corresponding element of
\mathcal{A}
. Each particle is associated with
q
Poisson clocks, called the Glauber clocks of type
1,\dots,q
, of intensity
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{q}
, respectively. All the Glauber clocks are independent.

2.

Each edge of the lattice is associated with a Poisson clock of intensity
L^{2}
, called the exclusion clock. The exclusion clocks are independent and independent of the Glauber clocks. Each time an exclusion clock rings, we put an exclusion mark on the corresponding edge.

3.

The particles follow the exclusion marks until one Glauber clock rings, say the clock of type
i
of particle
\mathsf{w}
at time
s
. Suppose that
\mathsf{w}
is at site
u
at time
s-
. Then we put a Glauber mark of type
i
on site
u
at time
s
. After that, we remove particle
\mathsf{w}
and add new particles to all sites in
B(u,m)
, canonically labeled by the children of
\mathsf{w}
. We think of this as the particle
\mathsf{w}
splits into its neighbor sites. Each particle born on a non-occupied site is given
q
independent Glauber clocks, independent of all exclusion clocks and all Glauber clocks of other particles. Each particle born on an occupied site “moves together” with the particles already there. The particles moving together are called a group of particles, and they are associated with the same
q
Glauber clocks. All the particles then continue to evolve with similar rules.

The BEP starting from
E
consists of two parts
(\Psi_{BEP}(E),\mathcal{C}_{BEP}(E))
, where

•

\Psi_{BEP}(E)
is the collection of trajectories of all the particles, i.e. the map associating each point
(u,t)
of the space-time slab with the set of particles occupying the site
u
at time
t
.

•

\mathcal{C}_{BEP}(E)
is the set consisting of all the marks on the trajectories of all the particles constructed above.

Remark 4
.

Knowing
E
, the pair
(\Psi_{BEP}(E),\mathcal{C}_{BEP}(E))
is determined by
\mathcal{C}_{BEP}(E)
.

We say that a particle
\mathsf{w}
is
alive
at time
t
if it was born before time
t
and has not been removed by time
t
. Let
W_{t}
be the set consisting of the particles with the smallest labels in each group of particles alive at time
t
, for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. A spin configuration on
W_{t}
is identified with a spin configuration on the set of all particles alive at time
t
, where the particles in a group are given the same spin.

Spins of particles in BEP.

Each spin configuration
\mathfrak{s}
on
W_{t}
is extended to
\bigcup_{0\leq s\leq t}W_{s}
by the following rules. For any
\mathsf{w}\notin W_{t}
,
\mathsf{w}
must be removed at some point in
[0,t]
by a Glauber mark, say of type
i
. Then

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}_{\mathsf{w}}:=f_{i}\left((\mathfrak{s}_{\mathsf{w}^{\prime}})_{\mathsf{w}^{\prime}\in\{\mathsf{w}\}\times B(0,m)}\right).

This allows us to extend the spin configuration to all the particles alive at some point in the time interval
[0,t]
.

By the homogeneity of the Poisson processes, we can see that
\mathcal{C}_{BEP}(E)
and
\mathcal{C}_{his}^{bw}(E)
have the same distribution. In fact, we can show that their first marks have the same distribution, then conditionally on their first marks, their second marks have the same distribution, etc. This leads to the following result.

Proposition 10

(History is BEP)
.

Let
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Consider the BEP starting from
E
and the history of
E
at time
t
given by a collection of mark generated by
\Xi
. Then the point processes
\mathcal{C}_{BEP}(E)
and
\mathcal{C}_{his}^{bw}(E)
have the same distribution. In particular, for some
x\in\mathcal{X}
, if we define the spin configuration
\mathfrak{s}
on
W_{t}
by

\displaystyle\forall\mathsf{w}\in W_{t},\;\mathfrak{s}_{\mathsf{w}}:=x(u),\text{ if $\mathsf{w}$ is at site $u$ at time $t$},

then

X^{x}_{t}(E)\overset{d}{=}(\mathfrak{s}_{u})_{u\in E}.

(34)

Example 3
.

Figure
2
also illustrates the BEP starting from
E=\{1,2\}
. The time axis upward is the one of the Glauber-Exclusion process, and the one downward is the one of the BEP.

The idea of looking at the history backward in time is not new. It is known as
duality
in the context of interacting particle systems. For our case, the BEP was already introduced by De Masi
et al
in the original paper
[
10
]
(for the proof of Proposition
10
, see Theorem 3.1 in
[
10
]
). The primary motivation is that it makes the study of correlation functions easier. To study the
k-
points correlation function, we only need to follow the history of
k
particles backward instead of realizing the whole process forward. This idea also relates to the famous “coupling from the past” algorithm in
[
53
]
and is also an essential ingredient of the information percolation framework in
[
42
]
. Proposition
10
says that the history itself can be viewed as a Markov process (the BEP). This will be useful for the construction of our coupling.

We introduce some further necessary notations.

Update functions.

By construction, for any particle
\mathsf{w}
alive at some point in the time interval
[0,t]
, there exists an increasing boolean function
F_{\mathsf{w},t}
on
\{-1,1\}^{W_{t}}
such that for any spin configuration
\mathfrak{s}
on
W_{t}
, its extension to
\bigcup_{0\leq s\leq t}W_{s}
satisfies

\mathfrak{s}_{\mathsf{w}}=F_{\mathsf{w},t}((\mathfrak{s}_{\mathsf{w}^{\prime}})_{\mathsf{w}^{\prime}\in W_{t}}).

(35)

The function
F_{\mathsf{w},t}
describes the dependence of the spin of
\mathsf{w}
on the spins of the particles alive at time
t
.

Pivotal sets of the update functions.

For any
t\geq 0
, for any particle
\mathsf{w}
that is alive at some time
s<t
, we denote by

\mathsf{Piv}\left(\mathsf{w},t\right):=\mathsf{Piv}\left(F_{\mathsf{w},t}\right).

(36)

We write
\mathsf{Piv}\left(E,t\right)
for
\bigcup_{\mathsf{w}\in E}\mathsf{Piv}\left(\mathsf{w},t\right)
.

Observation 11
.

For any
s<t
,

\mathsf{Piv}\left(\mathsf{w},t\right)\subset\bigcup_{\mathsf{w}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Piv}\left(\mathsf{w},s\right)}\mathsf{Piv}\left(\mathsf{w}^{\prime},t\right).

(37)

Update times of the pivotal set.

Let the sequence
(T_{j})_{j\geq 0}
be defined recursively, as follows.

\displaystyle T_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle T_{j+1}

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{t\geq T_{j}:\text{a Glauber clock of a particle in $\mathsf{Piv}\left(E,T_{j}\right)$ rings at time $t$}\right\}.

Observation 12
.

(\mathsf{Piv}\left(E,t\right))_{t\geq 0}
is piece-wise constant and only jumps at the times
(T_{j})_{j>0}
. The same conclusion is true for
(F_{\mathsf{w},t})_{t\geq 0}
, for any
\mathsf{w}\in E
.

Information percolation.

We now briefly explain the idea that we borrow from
[
42
]
, which will be made more precise later. First, to determine the spin of
\mathsf{w}\in E
, we do not need to follow the whole history, but only the part that is “essential”, say
(\mathsf{Piv}\left(\mathsf{w},s\right))_{0\leq s\leq t}
. In particular, if
\mathsf{Piv}\left(\mathsf{w},s\right)=\O
for some
s<t
, then the update function
F_{\mathsf{w},s}
degenerates, i.e. becomes a constant function. Then, we can determine
\mathfrak{s}_{\mathsf{w}}
without tracking further the history in the past. Second, we regard
(\mathsf{Piv}\left(\mathsf{w},t\right))_{t\geq 0}
as a percolation cluster in the space-time slab. Surprisingly, we prove in Lemma
17
below that the information percolation cluster defined this way is “subcritical” in the full high-temperature regime. This subcriticality means that the update functions quickly degenerate, so the spins at time
t
quickly become independent of those at time
0
as
t
grows to infinity, and the system mixes quickly.

4.3
The idealized branching process (IBP)

We will compare the BEP with its idealized version, the
idealized branching process
(IBP). The IBP starting from a particle
\mathsf{w}\in\mathcal{A}
is defined as follows.

•

Initially,
\mathsf{w}
has
q
independent Poisson clocks, of intensities
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{q}
, called the Glauber clocks of type
1,\dots,q
respectively.

•

Each time a clock of a particle
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}
rings, we remove
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}
and add the children of
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}
into the set of particles. Each of them is given
q
independent Glauber clocks, and the clocks of different particles are independent.

The IBP starting from
\mathsf{w}
is a pair
(\Psi_{IBP},\mathcal{C}_{IBP})
, where

•

\Psi_{IBP}
is the process associating each time
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
with the set of particles alive at time
t
, i.e. the set of particles born before time
t
and not having been removed by time
t
.

•

\mathcal{C}_{IBP}
is the counting measure on
[q]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
that tracks the type of the Glauber clocks that ring.

Remark 5
.

In the IBP, the particles no longer live on the lattice. All the particles are different, and all their clocks are independent. In particular, it is a dimension-free object, i.e. it does not depend on the size
L
of the system.

For any finite subset
E
of
\mathcal{A}
such that for all
\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2}\in E
,
\mathsf{w}_{1}
is not a descendant of
\mathsf{w}_{2}
, the IBP starting from
E
consists of
|E|
independent IBPs starting from particles labeled by the elements of
E
.

Spins and update functions of the IBP.

Let
\widetilde{W}_{t}
be the set of particles of the IBP alive at time
t
. Similarly to what we have done for the BEP, for any spin configuration
\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}
on
\widetilde{W}_{t}
, we extend
\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}
to
\bigcup_{0\leq s\leq t}\widetilde{W}_{t}
by defining for each
\mathsf{w}\notin\widetilde{W}_{t}
,

\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}_{\mathsf{w}}:=f_{i}((\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}_{\mathsf{w}^{\prime}})_{\mathsf{w}^{\prime}\in\{\mathsf{w}\}\times B(0,m)}).

We can extend
\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}
to all particles alive at some time
s<t
with this procedure. In particular, we can define the spin of
\mathsf{w}
. We define the update functions
\tilde{F}_{\mathsf{w},t}
, the pivotal sets
(\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(\tilde{F}_{\mathsf{w},t}))_{t\geq 0}
, and the update times
(\tilde{T}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
analogously as for the BEP.

4.4
The coupling

Let
E
be a subset of
\Lambda_{L}
. We will construct a coupling between the BEP and the IBP starting from
E
.
Let there be one exclusion clock at each edge of the lattice and an infinite number of Glauber clocks of types
1,\dots,q
. All of them are independent. We will use these clocks to construct the two processes.
We say that the coupling is
succesful
until time
t
if
\mathsf{Piv}\left(E,t\right)=\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(E,t)
, and for each
\mathsf{w}\in\mathsf{Piv}\left(E,t\right)
, the Glauber clocks associated with it at time
t
are the same in the BEP and the IBP. We now construct the coupling, which should be thought of as embedding the IBP in the space-time slab
\Lambda_{L}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

1.

Whenever an exclusion clock rings, we put an exclusion mark at the corresponding edge. When a particle of the BEP meets an exclusion mark, it jumps to the other endpoint of the edge.

2.

At time
0
, each particle
\mathsf{w}\in E
is associated with the same
q
Glauber clocks in the BEP and the IBP.

3.

For each particle, the set of clocks associated with it stays unchanged in the interval
(T_{j},T_{j+1}]
, where
(T_{j})_{j\geq 0}
are the update times of
\mathsf{Piv}\left(E,\cdot\right)
.

4.

Suppose that the coupling is still successful until time
T_{j}
. Then
T_{j+1}
corresponds to the ring of a Glauber clock of some particle
\mathsf{w}\in\mathsf{Piv}\left(E,T_{j}\right)
. Note that
T_{j+1}=\tilde{T}_{j+1}
because the same clock of
\mathsf{w}
rings in the IBP. Suppose that
\mathsf{w}
is at site
u
at time
T_{j+1}-
. Then one of two cases can happen.

(a)

Case 1
: At time
T_{j+1}-
, there is no particle in
B(u,m)
except
\mathsf{w}
. This means that the children of
\mathsf{w}
are born on sites not occupied by other particles in the pivotal set. Then we update
\mathsf{Piv}\left(E,T_{j+1}\right)
and
\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(E,\tilde{T}_{j+1})
: we let the particles in
\mathsf{Piv}\left(E,T_{j+1}-\right)\setminus\{\mathsf{w}\}
keep their clocks (in IBP and BEP), and we remove from
\mathsf{Piv}\left(E,T_{j+1}-\right)
and
\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(E,\tilde{T}_{j+1}-)
the particles whose spins are no longer necessary to determine the spins atop, and then we associate the children of
\mathsf{w}
in BEP and IBP with the same Glauber clocks. Note that
\mathsf{Piv}\left(E,T_{j+1}\right)\subset(\mathsf{Piv}\left(E,T_{j+1}-\right)\setminus\{\mathsf{w}\})\cup(\{\mathsf{w}\}\times B(0,m))
. In this case, the coupling is still successful until time
T_{j+1}
.

(b)

Case 2
: At time
T_{j+1}-
, there is some particles in
B(u,m)
other than
\mathsf{w}
. Then from time
T_{j+1}
, all the particles in the two pivotal sets are given different Poisson clocks. The coupling becomes unsuccessful since then.

We say that the coupling is successful until infinity if it is successful until
t
for any finite
t
.
The interest in the above coupling is the following.

Observation 13

(Spins atop coincide)
.

If the coupling is successful until time
t
, and if
\mathfrak{s}_{\mathsf{w}}=\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}_{\mathsf{w}},\;\forall\mathsf{w}\in\mathsf{Piv}\left(E,t\right)
, then
\mathfrak{s}_{\mathsf{w}}=\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}_{\mathsf{w}},\;\forall\mathsf{w}\in E
.

We will show that the coupling is successful until infinity with high probability.

Proposition 14

(The coupling is successful w.h.p)
.

Let
E
be an arbitrary subset of
\Lambda_{L}
. Consider the coupling of the BEP and the IBP starting from
E
described above. Then

\mathbb{P}\left[\text{The coupling is not successful until infinity}\right]\leq\beta\dfrac{|E|^{2}}{L},

(38)

for some constant
\beta
.

The motivation for the coupling is as follows. We are inspired by the local equilibrium phenomenon, so we try to couple the BEP and the IBP so that when we generate the spins of the particles at time
t
in the two processes according to the same distribution (assuming that those sets are the same), then the spins atop are the same. If this is the case, as the spins atop of the IBP are independent, the spins atop of the BEP are also independent. The naive coupling is to match every particle in the two processes. However, the total number of particles increases exponentially fast, so we can not expect the naive coupling to work until the mixing times. Nevertheless, it is
not necessary
to match the evolution of every particle, but only the ones that truly influence the spins atop, say
\mathsf{Piv}\left(E,\cdot\right)
.

The idea to look only at
(\mathsf{Piv}\left(E,t\right))_{t\geq 0}
ultimately comes from
[
42
]
(in this paper, the authors call it the update support function, see Subsection
2.2
in
[
42
]
). In Subsection
4.5
, we analyze the IBP, and in particular, we prove that
\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(\cdot)
behaves like a subcritical Galton Watson process. In Subsection
4.6
, we use this to prove Proposition
14
.

4.6

Interchange process IP(2).

The interchange process IP(2) on
\Lambda^{d}_{L}
, whose edges have conductance

, is a couple of random walk

is associated with an independent Poisson clock of intensity
L^{2}
. Whenever a clock rings, if
\mathcal{U}_{1}
(or
\mathcal{U}_{2}
) is at one endpoint of the corresponding edge, it jumps to the other endpoint.

From now on, we denote by

the shortest-path distance on the lattice. We have the following result.

\mathcal{O}_{\theta,k}(\log L/L^{2})&\text{if $d=2$},\\
\mathcal{O}_{\theta,k}(1/L^{2})&\text{if $d\geq 3$}.\end{cases}

To lighten the notation, we write

At time
t
, a clock of one of the two particles rings, and then one of the children is born on the site occupied by the other particle.

In short, two particles in
\mathsf{Piv}\left(\cdot\right)
have an interaction if one splits into the site occupied by the other. For example, in Figure
2
,
\mathsf{w}
and
\mathsf{w}^{\prime}
have an interaction at time
s_{3}
. By definition, the coupling is successful up to time
t
if there is no interaction up to time
t
. Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\text{The coupling is not successful until infinity}\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\text{There is an interaction}\right].

We only need to prove

\mathbb{P}\left[\text{There is an interaction}\right]\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(\tilde{T}_{j})\right]}{L}\right).

(57)

Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\text{There is an interaction}\right]=\sum_{\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2}}\mathbb{P}\left[\text{The first interaction is between $\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2}$}\right].

Recall the definition of the update times
(T_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
of the pivotal set. Let
\mathcal{E}(j,\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2})
be the event that
T_{j}
is the first time such that both
\mathsf{w}_{1}
and
\mathsf{w}_{2}
are in the pivotal set.
Let
\mathcal{E}(j)
be the event that there is no interaction up to time
T_{j}
, and let
\mathcal{E}(\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2})
be the event that there is an interaction between
\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2}
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\text{The first interaction is of $\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2}$}\right]\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{E}(j,\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2})\cap\mathcal{E}(j)\cap\mathcal{E}(\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2})\right].

Let
\mathcal{F}_{j}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the Poisson processes up to time
T_{j}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{E}(j,\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2})\cap\mathcal{E}(j)\cap\mathcal{E}(\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}(j,\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2})}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}(j)}\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{E}(\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2})\big|\mathcal{F}_{j}\right]\right]

Let
\tau
be the time such that a Glauber clock of
\mathsf{w}_{1}
or
\mathsf{w}_{2}
rings counted from
T_{j}
:

\displaystyle\tau:=\inf\left\{t>0:\text{a Glauber clock of $\mathsf{w}_{1}$ or $\mathsf{w}_{2}$ rings at time $T_{j}+t$}\right\}.

Conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{j}
, on the event
\mathcal{E}(j,\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2})\cap\mathcal{E}(j)
, from time
T_{j}
onwards,
\mathsf{w}_{1}
and
\mathsf{w}_{2}
move as an IP(2) on the lattice, until one of their Glauber clocks rings at time
\tau\sim\exp(2\lambda)
. To have an interaction, the distance between
\mathsf{w}_{1}
and
\mathsf{w}_{2}
at time
\tau
must be smaller than
m
. Therefore, by Lemma
21
, conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{j}
, on the event
\mathcal{E}(j,\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2})\cap\mathcal{E}(j)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{E}(\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2})\big|\mathcal{F}_{j}\right]\leq\beta/L,

for some constant
\beta
.
We deduce that,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\text{There is an interaction}\right]\leq\sum_{\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{\beta}{L}\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{E}(j,\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2})\cap\mathcal{E}(j)\right].

Note that on the event
\mathcal{E}(j,\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2})
, either
\mathsf{w}_{1}
or
\mathsf{w}_{2}
(or both of them) is born at time
T_{j}
. We denote by
\mathcal{E}(j,\mathsf{w}_{i})
the event that
\mathsf{w}_{i}
is born at time
T_{j}
,
i\in\{1,2\}
. Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2}}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}(j,\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2})}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}(j)}\leq\sum_{i\in\{1,2\}}\sum_{\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2}}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}(j,\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2})}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}(j)}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}(j,\mathsf{w}_{i})}.

Moreover, note also that on the event
\mathcal{E}(j,\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2})
,
\mathsf{w}_{2}\in\mathsf{Piv}\left(T_{j}\right)
. Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2}}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}(j,\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2})}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}(j)}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}(j,\mathsf{w}_{1})}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2}}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\mathsf{w}_{2}\in\mathsf{Piv}\left(T_{j}\right)\right\}}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}(j,\mathsf{w}_{1})}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}(j)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathsf{w}_{1}}\left|\mathsf{Piv}\left(T_{j}\right)\right|\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}(j,\mathsf{w}_{1})}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}(j)}

\displaystyle\leq(2m+1)\left|\mathsf{Piv}\left(T_{j}\right)\right|\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}(j)},

where all these inequalities are true almost surely. The last inequality is because at most
(2m+1)
particles are born at time
T_{j}
.
Note that, on the event
\mathcal{E}(j)
,
\mathsf{Piv}\left(T_{j}\right)=\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(\tilde{T}_{j})
. This implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2}}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}(j,\mathsf{w}_{1},\mathsf{w}_{2})}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}(j)}\leq 2(2m+1)\left|\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(\tilde{T}_{j})\right|.

Taking expectation, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\text{There is an interaction}\right]\leq 2(2m+1)\dfrac{\beta}{L}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(\tilde{T}_{j})\right],

which finishes the proof.
∎

We finish this section by proving Lemma
23
. The left-hand side is the sum of the expected sizes of the process
\left|\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(\cdot)\right|
at its update times. The intuition is that the inequality is true if we replace
\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(\cdot)
by a subcritical Galton-Watson process. The only problem is that
\left|\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(\cdot)\right|
is not Markovian. Nevertheless, it is “subcritical”, and the idea is to enlarge the process
\left|\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(\cdot)\right|
to make it Markovian and then adapt the proof of the subcritical Galton-Watson case.

Proof of Lemma
23
.

Let
t_{0}
be a number such that
\psi(t_{0})<1
, which exists thanks to Lemma
18
. We rewrite the left-hand side as

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(\tilde{T}_{j})\right|\mathds{1}_{\left\{it_{0}\leq\tilde{T}_{j}<(i+1)t_{0}\right\}}\right].

Let the sequence
(B_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
be defined recursively as follows.

\displaystyle B_{0}

\displaystyle:=\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(0),

\displaystyle B_{i}

\displaystyle:=\bigcup_{\mathsf{w}\in B_{i-1}}\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(\mathsf{w},it_{0}),\,\forall i\geq 1.

It is not hard to see that
\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(it_{0})\subset B_{i},\forall u\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, because given the IBP up to time
it_{0}
and the spins of the particles in
B_{i}
, by definition of
B_{i}
, we can recursively determine the spins of the particles in
B_{i-1}
, and so on, up to time
0
. It is also not hard to see that the sequence
(|B_{i}|)_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
is a subcritical Galton-Watson process, by its definition and the definition of
t_{0}
.

Let
\kappa
be the constant in Lemma
19
. We claim that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left|\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(\tilde{T}_{j})\right|\mathds{1}_{\left\{it_{0}\leq\tilde{T}_{j}<(i+1)t_{0}\right\}}\right]\leq\dfrac{e^{\kappa t_{0}}}{2m}\mathbb{E}\left[|B_{i}|^{2}\right].

To see this, consider the IBP starting from
B_{i}
(at time
it_{0}
) up to time
(i+1)t_{0}
. We denote this process by
\texttt{Gw}_{i}
. Let
(\mathcal{T}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
be the update times of
\texttt{Gw}_{i}
. Then
\texttt{Gw}_{i}(it_{0})=B_{i}
, and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left|\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(\tilde{T}_{j})\right|\mathds{1}_{\left\{it_{0}\leq\tilde{T}_{j}<(i+1)t_{0}\right\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left|\texttt{Gw}_{i}(\mathcal{T}_{j})\mathds{1}_{\left\{it_{0}\leq\mathcal{T}_{j}<(i+1)t_{0}\right\}}\right|\right].

This is because
\texttt{Gw}_{i}
can be thought of as an enlargement of
\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(\cdot)
in the time interval
[it_{0},(i+1)t_{0}]
, where we first enlarge the set
\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(it_{0})
to
B_{i}
, and then we do not remove any particle of the branching process starting from
B_{i}
.

Note that the size of
\texttt{Gw}_{i}(\cdot)
increases almost surely, hence

\displaystyle\left|\texttt{Gw}_{i}(\mathcal{T}_{j})\mathds{1}_{\left\{it_{0}\leq\mathcal{T}_{j}<(i+1)t_{0}\right\}}\right|\leq\left|\texttt{Gw}_{i}((i+1)t_{0})\right|.

Moreover, at each update time
\mathcal{T}_{j}
, the size of
\texttt{Gw}_{i}
increases by 2m, and hence

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\mathds{1}_{\left\{it_{0}\leq\mathcal{T}_{j}<(i+1)t_{0}\right\}}\leq\dfrac{\left|\texttt{Gw}_{i}((i+1)t_{0})\right|}{2m}.

This implies

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left|\texttt{Gw}_{i}(\mathcal{T}_{j})\mathds{1}_{\left\{it_{0}\leq\mathcal{T}_{j}<(i+1)t_{0}\right\}}\right|\leq\dfrac{\left|\texttt{Gw}_{i}((i+1)t_{0})\right|^{2}}{2m}.

By Lemma
19
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\texttt{Gw}_{i}((i+1)t_{0})\right|^{2}\right]\leq e^{\kappa t_{0}}\mathbb{E}\left[|B_{i}|^{2}\right].

This proves the claim. Now, we can take the sum over
i
to conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left|\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(\tilde{T}_{j})\right|\right]\leq\dfrac{e^{\kappa t_{0}}}{2m}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[|B_{i}|^{2}\right].

Since
(|B_{i}|)_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
is a subcritical Galton-Watson process, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[|B_{i}|^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|B_{0}|^{2}\right]\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(|E|^{2}\right).

This finishes the proof.
∎

Proposition 24

(Replacement lemma)
.

Suppose that
X_{0}\sim\mathfrak{R}_{\rho_{0}}^{\otimes\Lambda_{L}}
, for some
\rho_{0}\in(-1,1)
.
Then, for any subset
E\subset\Lambda_{L}
,

\sup_{t\geq 0}\textrm{d}_{\textsc{tv}}\left(\text{Law}\left(X_{t}(E)\right),\mathfrak{R}_{\rho(t)}^{\otimes|E|}\right)\leq\dfrac{\beta|E|^{2}}{L},

(58)

where
\rho(\cdot)
is the solution of equation (
9
), and
\beta
is the constant in Proposition
14
.

Remark 8
.

The proposition above is of independent interest. It shows that the joint distribution of
o\left(\sqrt{L}\right)
arbitrary sites is close to that of a product of i.i.d. Rademacher. This provides sharp estimates on the correlation functions, allowing us to derive the correct lower bound on the mixing times using distinguishing statistics.

Proposition
24
is just a direct corollary of Proposition
14
.

Proof.

Consider the coupling of BEP and IBP starting from
E
in Subsection
4.4
. We generate the spins at time
t
by
\mathfrak{s}(W_{t})\sim\mathfrak{R}_{\rho_{0}}^{\otimes W_{t}}
and
\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}(\widetilde{W}_{t})\sim\mathfrak{R}_{\rho_{0}}^{\otimes\widetilde{W}_{t}}
. Conditionally on the success of the coupling until time
t
, we couple the spins on
\mathsf{Piv}\left(E,t\right)
and
\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(E,t)
such that
\mathfrak{s}_{\mathsf{w}}=\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}_{\mathsf{w}},\;\forall\mathsf{w}\in\mathsf{Piv}\left(E,t\right)
. Then Observation
13
implies that

\mathfrak{s}_{\mathsf{w}}=\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}_{\mathsf{w}},\;\forall\mathsf{w}\in E.

Recall that, by Proposition
10
,

\displaystyle X_{t}(E)

\displaystyle\overset{d}{=}(\mathfrak{s}_{\mathsf{w}})_{\mathsf{w}\in E},

and by Lemma
15
,

\displaystyle(\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}_{\mathsf{w}})_{\mathsf{w}\in E}

\displaystyle\overset{d}{=}\mathfrak{R}_{\rho(t)}^{\otimes E}.

Hence

\displaystyle\textrm{d}_{\textsc{tv}}\left(\text{Law}\left(X_{t}(E)\right),\mathfrak{R}_{\rho(t)}^{\otimes|E|}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[(\mathfrak{s}_{\mathsf{w}})_{\mathsf{w}\in E}\neq(\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}_{\mathsf{w}})_{\mathsf{w}\in E}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\text{The coupling is not successful until time $t$}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{\beta|E|^{2}}{L}.

This finishes our proof.
∎

Corollary 25

(1-point and 2-point correlation functions)
.

Under the hypothesis of Proposition
24
, for any
u,v\in\Lambda_{L}
, for any
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t}(u)\right]-\rho(t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\beta/L,

(59)

\displaystyle\left|\textrm{Cov}\left(X_{t}(u),X_{t}(v)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 12\beta/L.

(60)

Proof.

Let
u,v\in\Lambda_{L}
, and let
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}
be i.i.d. Rademacher
\mathfrak{R}_{\rho(t)}
. Let
\beta
be the constant in Proposition
14
.

1.

Applying Proposition
24
with
E:=\{u\}
, we deduce that there exists a coupling of
X_{t}(u)
and
\xi_{1}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[X_{t}(u)\neq\xi_{1}\right]\leq\dfrac{\beta}{L}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t}(u)\right]-\rho(t)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t}(u)-\xi_{1}\right]\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}\left[(X_{t}(u)-\xi_{1})\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{t}(u)\neq\xi_{1}\right\}}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X_{t}(u)-\xi_{1}\right|\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{t}(u)\neq\xi_{1}\right\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{P}\left[X_{t}(u)\neq\xi_{1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\beta/L.

2.

Similarly, applying Proposition
24
with
E:=\{u,v\}
, we deduce that there exists a coupling of
(X_{t}(u),X_{t}(v))
and
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
such that if we let
\mathcal{E}:=\{(X_{t}(u),X_{t}(v))\neq(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\}
, then
\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{E}\right]\leq\dfrac{4\beta}{L}
. Hence

\displaystyle\left|\textrm{Cov}\left(X_{t}(u),X_{t}(v)\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t}(u)X_{t}(v)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t}(u)\right]\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t}(v)\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t}(u)X_{t}(v)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{1}\xi_{2}\right]\right|+\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{1}\xi_{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t}(u)\right]\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t}(v)\right]\right|.

The first term in the last expression is

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[(X_{t}(u)X_{t}(v)-\xi_{1}\xi_{2})\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}}\right]\right|\leq 2\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{E}\right]\leq 8\beta/L.

The second term does not exceed

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{1}\right]\right|\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t}(v)\right]\right|+\left|\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t}(v)\right]\right|\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{1}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t}(u)\right]\right|\leq 2\beta/L+2\beta/L=4\beta/L.

This finishes our proof.
∎

We are ready to prove the lower bound on the mixing times. We denote by
(X^{+}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
the Glauber-Exclusion process starting from all-plus and recall that
\rho_{+}(\cdot)
is the solution of (
9
) with the initial condition
\rho_{+}(0)=1
.

Proof of the lower bound.

The proof uses the classical distinguishing statistic method (see Section
7.3
in
[
37
]
). Our distinguishing statistic is the total magnetization
\sum\limits_{u\in\Lambda_{L}}x(u)
.
By abuse of notation, let
X_{\infty}\sim\pi
(so that for any initial configuration,
X_{t}\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{d}X_{\infty}
). Let
\beta
be defined as in Proposition
14
. From (
59
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{u\in\Lambda_{L}}X^{+}_{t}(u)\right]-L\rho_{+}(t)\right|\leq 2\beta,

and by letting
t\to\infty
,

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{u\in\Lambda_{L}}X_{\infty}(u)\right]-L\rho_{*}\right|\leq 2\beta.

From (
60
), we see that

\displaystyle\textrm{Var}\left[\sum_{u\in\Lambda_{L}}X^{+}_{t}(u)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in\Lambda_{L}}\textrm{Var}\left[X^{+}_{t}(u)\right]+\sum_{u\neq v}\textrm{Cov}\left(X^{+}_{t}(u),X^{+}_{t}(v)\right)

\displaystyle\leq L+L(L-1)\times 12\beta/L

\displaystyle<(12\beta+1)L.

By letting
t
tend to infinity, we get

\textrm{Var}\left[\sum_{u\in\Lambda_{L}}X_{\infty}(u)\right]\leq(12\beta+1)L.

So by Proposition 7.9 in
[
37
]
,

\displaystyle\textrm{d}_{\textsc{tv}}\left(\mathbb{P}\left[X^{+}_{t}\in\cdot\right],\pi\right)\geq 1-8\dfrac{\max\left\{\textrm{Var}\left[\sum\limits_{u\in\Lambda_{L}}X^{+}_{t}(u)\right],\textrm{Var}\left[\sum\limits_{u\in\Lambda_{L}}X_{\infty}(u)\right]\right\}}{\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\sum\limits_{u\in\Lambda_{L}}X^{+}_{t}(u)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum\limits_{u\in\Lambda_{L}}X_{\infty}(u)\right]\right)^{2}}.

Let
t:=\dfrac{\log L}{-2R(\rho_{*})}-\kappa
for some constant
\kappa
that we will choose later. Let
\kappa_{1}
be a constant satisfying Lemma
18
. Then

\displaystyle\rho_{+}(t)-\rho_{*}

\displaystyle\geq e^{R(\rho_{*})t-\kappa_{1}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{e^{-R(\rho_{*})\kappa-\kappa_{1}}}{\sqrt{L}}.

Therefore, when
L
is large enough,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{u\in\Lambda_{L}}X^{+}_{t}(u)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{u\in\Lambda_{L}}X_{\infty}(u)\right]

\displaystyle\geq L(\rho_{+}(t)-\rho_{*})-4\beta

\displaystyle=\sqrt{L}e^{-R(\rho_{*})\kappa-\kappa_{1}}-4\beta.

\displaystyle>\dfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{L}e^{-R(\rho_{*})\kappa-\kappa_{1}}.

Therefore,

8\dfrac{\max\left\{\textrm{Var}\left[\sum_{u\in\Lambda_{L}}X^{+}_{t}(u)\right],\textrm{Var}\left[\sum_{u\in\Lambda_{L}}X_{\infty}(u)\right]\right\}}{\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{u\in\Lambda_{L}}X^{+}_{t}(u)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{u\in\Lambda_{L}}X_{\infty}(u)\right]\right)^{2}}<\dfrac{32(12\beta+1)}{e^{-2R(\rho_{*})\kappa-2\kappa_{1}}}.

Note that as
R^{\prime}(\rho_{*})<0
, for
\kappa
large enough, the last expression is smaller than
\epsilon
. Hence

\textrm{d}_{\textsc{tv}}\left(\mathbb{P}\left[X^{+}_{t}\in\cdot\right],\pi\right)\geq 1-\epsilon,

and therefore,

\displaystyle\textrm{t}_{\textsc{mix}}(\epsilon)>t=\dfrac{\log L}{-2R(\rho_{*})}-\kappa,

which finishes our proof.
∎

Now we prove Theorem
2
and the upper bound in Theorem
3
. First, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 26
.

Consider a BEP starting with one particle
\mathsf{w}
. Then, there exist constants
\kappa
and
\beta
such that for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathsf{Piv}\left(\mathsf{w},t\right)\right|\right]\leq\psi(t)+e^{\kappa t}\dfrac{\beta}{\sqrt{L}}.

Proof of Theorem
2
and upper bound in Theorem
3
.

Let
x\in\mathcal{X}
be an arbitrary configuration. We want to compare
\mathbb{P}_{x}\left[X_{t}\in\cdot\right]
and
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left[X_{t}\in\cdot\right]
for some
t>0
. We use the same BEP  starting from
E=\Lambda_{L}
to generate
X^{x}_{t}
and
X^{\pi}_{t}
, the processes starting from
x
and
\pi
. Let
\mathsf{w}\in\Lambda_{L}
be arbitrary. We see that

\displaystyle\textrm{d}_{\textsc{tv}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{x}\left[X_{t}\in\cdot\right],\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left[X_{t}\in\cdot\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[X^{x}_{t}\neq X^{\pi}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{u\in\Lambda_{L}}\mathbb{P}\left[X^{x}_{t}(u)\neq X^{\pi}_{t}(u)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{u\in\Lambda_{L}}\mathbb{P}\left[\mathsf{Piv}\left(u,t\right)\neq\O\right]

\displaystyle=L\mathbb{P}\left[\mathsf{Piv}\left(\mathsf{w},t\right)\neq\O\right]

\displaystyle\leq L\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathsf{Piv}\left(\mathsf{w},t\right)\right|\right].

By Observation
11
, it is not hard to see that,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathsf{Piv}\left(\mathsf{w},mt\right)\right|\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathsf{Piv}\left(\mathsf{w},t\right)\right|\right]^{m}.

Hence, with the constant
\kappa
and
\beta
as in Lemma
26
,

\textrm{d}_{\textsc{tv}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{x}\left[X_{mt}\in\cdot\right],\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left[X_{mt}\in\cdot\right]\right)\leq L\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathsf{Piv}\left(\mathsf{w},mt\right)\right|\right]\leq L\left(\psi(t)+e^{\kappa t}\dfrac{\beta}{\sqrt{L}}\right)^{m}.

(61)

Provided that
\left(\psi(t)+e^{\kappa t}\dfrac{\beta}{\sqrt{L}}\right)<1
,
we can take
m=\left\lceil\dfrac{\log L-\log\epsilon}{-\log\left(\psi(t)+e^{\kappa t}\dfrac{\beta}{\sqrt{L}}\right)}\right\rceil

to make the right-hand side smaller than
\epsilon
. This implies that

\textrm{t}_{\textsc{mix}}(x;\epsilon)\leq mt\leq\left(\dfrac{\log L-\log\epsilon}{-\log\left(\psi(t)+e^{\kappa t}\dfrac{\beta}{\sqrt{L}}\right)}+1\right)t,

for any
t
such that
\left(\psi(t)+e^{\kappa t}\dfrac{\beta}{\sqrt{L}}\right)<1
. It remains to choose a suitable
t
that leads to our results.
We will choose
t
such that
e^{\kappa t}\dfrac{\beta}{\sqrt{L}}\ll\psi(t)L^{-1/3}
. This will imply

\displaystyle\psi(t)+e^{\kappa t}\dfrac{\beta}{\sqrt{L}}\leq\psi(t)(1+L^{-1/3}).

Hence, with
\kappa_{1}
the constant in Lemma
18
, we have

\displaystyle\log\left(\psi(t)+e^{\kappa t}\dfrac{\beta}{\sqrt{L}}\right)\leq\log\psi(t)+\log(1+L^{-1/3})\leq R(\rho_{*})t+\kappa_{1}+L^{-1/3}.

Therefore, if
R(\rho_{*})t+\kappa_{1}+L^{-1/3}<0
,

\displaystyle\textrm{t}_{\textsc{mix}}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{\log L-\log\epsilon}{-(R(\rho_{*})t+\kappa_{1}+L^{-1/3})}t+t

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\log L-\log\epsilon}{-(R(\rho_{*})+\kappa_{1}/t+1/(tL^{1/3})}+t

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\log L}{-R(\rho_{*})}+\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{\log L}{t}\right)+t.

We can take
t=\sqrt{\log L}
, which satisfies all the conditions we impose on
t
, to finish the proof of the upper bound in Theorem
3
for
d=1
. The proof for higher dimensions is similar.

It remains to prove Theorem
2
. Recall that

gap

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}-\dfrac{1}{t}\log\max_{x\in\Omega}\textrm{d}_{\textsc{tv}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{x}\left[X_{t}\in\cdot\right],\pi\right).

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to\infty}-\dfrac{1}{mt}\log\max_{x\in\Omega}\textrm{d}_{\textsc{tv}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{x}\left[X_{mt}\in\cdot\right],\pi\right).

On the other hand, equation (
61
) implies that, for any
t\geq 0
,
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,

\log\max_{x\in\Omega}\textrm{d}_{\textsc{tv}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{x}\left[X_{mt}\in\cdot\right],\pi\right)\leq\log L+m\log\left(\psi(t)+e^{\kappa t}\dfrac{\beta}{\sqrt{L}}\right).

Therefore,

\texttt{gap}\geq\lim_{m\to\infty}-\dfrac{1}{mt}\left(\log L+m\log\left(\psi(t)+e^{\kappa t}\dfrac{\beta}{\sqrt{L}}\right)\right)=-\dfrac{1}{t}\log\left(\psi(t)+e^{\kappa t}\dfrac{\beta}{\sqrt{L}}\right).

This implies

\displaystyle\liminf_{L\to\infty}\texttt{gap}\geq-\dfrac{1}{t}\log\psi(t)\geq-\dfrac{1}{t}(R(\rho_{*})t+\kappa_{1})=-R(\rho_{*})-\kappa_{1}/t.

Note that this inequality is true for any positive
t
. Letting
t
tend to infinity, we obtain the conclusion of Theorem
2
.
∎

Now we prove Lemma
26
.

Proof of Lemma
26
.

We use the coupling of BEP and IBP starting from one particle
\mathsf{w}
. We write
\mathsf{Piv}\left(t\right)
for
\mathsf{Piv}\left(\mathsf{w},t\right)
and
\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(t)
for
\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(\mathsf{w},t)
. Let
\mathcal{E}
denote the event that the coupling is successful until time
t
. Then, on
\mathcal{E}
, we have

\left|\mathsf{Piv}\left(t\right)\right|=\left|\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(t)\right|.

Let
\beta_{1}
be as in Proposition
14
. Then
\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{E}^{C}\right]\leq\beta_{1}/L
, where
\mathcal{E}^{C}
denotes the complement of
E
. Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathsf{Piv}\left(t\right)|\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathsf{Piv}\left(t\right)|(\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}}+\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}^{C}})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathsf{Piv}\left(t\right)|\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathsf{Piv}\left(t\right)|\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}^{C}}\right].

Note that

\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathsf{Piv}\left(t\right)|\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[|\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(t)|\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|\widetilde{\mathsf{Piv}}(t)|\right]=\psi(t).

Let
\kappa_{1}
be the constant in Lemma
19
. Note also that

\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathsf{Piv}\left(t\right)|\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}^{C}}\right]^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|W_{t}|\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}^{C}}\right]^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|W_{t}|^{2}\right]\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{E}^{C}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|\widetilde{W}_{t}|^{2}\right]\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{E}^{C}\right]\leq e^{\kappa_{1}t}\times\dfrac{\beta_{1}}{L},

where we have used

for the first inequality, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for the second inequality, Lemma

for the third inequality, and Lemma

for the last inequality.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathsf{Piv}\left(t\right)|\right]\leq\psi(t)+e^{\kappa_{1}t/2}\times\dfrac{\sqrt{\beta_{1}}}{\sqrt{L}}.

We can take

and

to finish the proof.
∎

6
Upper bound

In this section, we prove the upper bound in Theorem
1
. For simplicity, we only give the proof for
d=1
, but we will comment on how to adapt the proof for
d=2
.

Recall that in the decomposition of the local flip-rate function
c(\cdot)
in Proposition
7
,
f_{1}\equiv 1,\,f_{2}\equiv-1
, so the updates given by
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
are called
oblivious
because they can be carried out without looking at the spins of any sites. These updates will play a special role in our proof. It is more convenient to merge two oblivious deterministic updates into a single oblivious random update as follows. We allow the collection of marks to contain another type of mark: the refresh marks, which can appear on the sites in the space-time slab. We adapt the definition of the collection of marks accordingly.
From now on, let

\bar{\rho}:=\dfrac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}}.

(62)

We will use the following graphical construction.

Graphical Construction 2.

Let the background process
\Xi
be defined as follows.

\Xi=\left((\Xi^{\text{exclusion}}_{u})_{0\leq u\leq L-1},(\Xi^{\text{refresh}}_{u})_{0\leq u\leq L-1},(\Xi^{\text{Glauber}}_{i,u})_{3\leq i\leq q,\,0\leq u\leq L-1}\right),

(63)

where
(\Xi^{\text{exclusion}}_{u})_{0\leq u\leq L-1},(\Xi^{\text{refresh}}_{u})_{0\leq u\leq L-1},(\Xi^{\text{Glauber}}_{i,u})_{3\leq i\leq q,\,0\leq u\leq L-1}
are independent homogeneous Poisson processes, and

•

\Xi^{\text{exclusion}}_{u}
is of intensity
L^{2}
,
0\leq u\leq L-1
,

•

\Xi^{\text{refresh}}_{u}
is of intensity
\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}
,
0\leq u\leq L-1
,

•

\Xi^{\text{Glauber}}_{i,u}
is of intensity
\lambda_{i}
,
3\leq i\leq q,\,0\leq u\leq L-1
.

The process
\Xi
naturally defines a collection of marks
\mathcal{C}
consisting of exclusion, refresh, and Glauber marks as follows.

•

Whenever
\Xi^{\text{exclusion}}_{u}
jumps, we put an exclusion mark on the edge
(u,u+1)
.

•

Whenever
\Xi^{\text{refresh}}_{u}
jumps, we put a refresh mark at site
u
.

•

Whenever
\Xi^{\text{Glauber}}_{i,u}
jumps, we put a Glauber mark of type
i
at site
u
.

Given the collection of marks
\mathcal{C}
, an infinite sequence of independent Rademacher variables
\mathfrak{R}_{\bar{\rho}}
, and an initial condition
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
, we construct the process, which we always denote by
(X^{x_{0}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, as follows.

•

The process
(X^{x_{0}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a piecewise constant process starting from
x_{0}
and can only jump when a mark appears.

•

When we see an exclusion mark, say on the edge
(u,u+1)
, we make the transition
x\mapsto x^{u\leftrightarrow u+1}
(exchange the spins of sites
u
and
u+1
).

•

When we see a refresh mark, say at site
u
, we replace the
u-th
coordinate of
X
by an independent
\mathfrak{R}_{\bar{\rho}}
(randomize the spin at site
u
).

•

When we see a Glauber mark, say of type
i
at site
u
, we make the transition
x\mapsto x^{u,f_{i}(x_{u+\cdot})}
(update site
u
using the function
f_{i}
).

Then, when the collection of marks
\mathcal{C}
is generated by
\Xi
and the Rademacher variables
\mathfrak{R}_{\bar{\rho}}
are independent of
\Xi
, the process
(X^{x_{0}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a Markov process with the generator
\mathcal{L}_{GE}
starting from
x_{0}
.

The grand coupling.

We can use the same Poisson process
\Xi
and the same independent Rademacher variables
\mathfrak{R}_{\bar{\rho}}
to construct the Glauber-Exclusion process from any configuration
x\in\mathcal{X}
. It is not hard to see that this coupling preserves order, i.e. ,
x\leq x^{\prime}\Rightarrow\forall t\geq 0,\,X^{x}_{t}\leq X^{x^{\prime}}_{t}
.

Correlation marks associated with Glauber marks.

It is convenient to associate each Glauber mark with a set of points as follows. Whenever we see a Glauber mark at site
u
, we put on each site in
B(u,m)
a mark, and we call those marks the correlation marks associated to the aforementioned Glauber mark. These correlation marks indicate that the spins of those sites are (possibly) used in the corresponding Glauber updates.

We introduce another Markov process
Z
, which we call the
independent-site process
, constructed as follows.

Independent-site process.

From the collection of marks generated by
\Xi
, let
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the process taking values in
\mathcal{Z}:=\{0,1\}^{\Lambda_{L}}
constructed as follows. For any initial configuration
z_{0}\in\mathcal{Z}
,

•

The process
(Z^{z_{0}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a piecewise constant process starting from
z_{0}
and can only jump when a mark appears.

•

When we see an exclusion mark, say on the edge
(u,u+1)
, we make the transition
z\mapsto z^{u\leftrightarrow u+1}
.

•

When we see a refresh mark, say at site
u
, we make the transition
z\mapsto z^{u,1}
.

•

When we see a Glauber mark, say at site
u
, we remove all the sites with the associated correlation marks from
Z
, i.e. we make the transition
z\mapsto z\setminus B(u,m)
.

Here we have identified the elements of
\{0,1\}^{\Lambda_{L}}
with the subsets of
\Lambda_{L}
. As its name suggests, the process
Z
tracks the positions of independent sites in
X
conditionally on
\Xi
. Roughly speaking, when a site is resampled according to an independent Rademacher
\mathfrak{R}_{\bar{\rho}}
due to a refresh mark, it becomes independent of all other sites and hence is added to
Z
. On the other hand, when a site
u
is updated due to a Glauber mark, it creates a correlation with its neighbors, and all these neighbors are removed from
Z
. By abuse of notation, for any
z\subset\Lambda_{L}
, we denote by
\mathbb{P}_{z}\left[\cdot\right]
the distribution of the process
Z
starting from
z
.

The grand coupling for
Z
.

We can use the same background process
\Xi
to construct the process
Z
from any configuration
z\subset\Lambda_{L}
.

Remark 10

(Attractiveness of the process
Z
)
.

For any
z_{1},z_{2}\subset\Lambda_{L}
such that
z_{1}\leq z_{2}
, if
Z^{z_{1}}
and
Z^{z_{2}}
are constructed using the grand coupling, then almost surely,

\displaystyle\forall t\geq 0,\;Z^{z_{1}}_{t}\leq Z^{z_{2}}_{t}.

Red, blue, and green regions.

Let the red, blue, and green regions be defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Blue}(t)

\displaystyle=Z^{\O}_{t},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Red}(t)

\displaystyle=\{u\in\Lambda_{L}|X^{+}_{t}(u)\neq X^{-}_{t}(u)\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Green}(t)

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{L}\setminus\left(\mathrm{Red}(t)\cup\mathrm{Blue}(t)\right).

Here,
(X^{+}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(X^{-}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote the process
X
starting from configurations all-plus and all-minus, respectively.
To lighten the notation, we write
X^{x}_{t}(\mathrm{Blue})
for
X^{x}_{t}(\mathrm{Blue}(t))
, and similarly for
\mathrm{Red}
, and
\mathrm{Green}
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{t}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\Xi([0,t])
and
\left(X_{s}^{x}(\Lambda_{L}\setminus\mathrm{Blue}(s)\right)_{x\in\mathcal{X},0\leq s\leq t}
.
The interest in introducing red, blue, and green regions is the following.

Observation 27
.

By construction, almost surely, conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{t}
, for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
X^{x}_{t}(\mathrm{Blue})\sim\mathfrak{R}_{\bar{\rho}}^{\otimes{\mathrm{Blue}(t)}}
and is independent of
X^{x}_{t}(\mathrm{Red}\cup\mathrm{Green})
.

From now on, for any subset
E\subset\Lambda_{L}
, we will use the shorthand notation
\nu_{E}:=\mathfrak{R}_{\bar{\rho}}^{\otimes E}
.
The meanings of the red, blue, and green regions above are as follows: the red region
\mathrm{Red}
is the set of disagreements of
X^{+}
and
X^{-}
.
Conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{t}
, the spins in the blue region
\mathrm{Blue}
have the product measure
\nu_{\mathrm{Blue}(t)}
, independent of the spins on
\mathrm{Red}\cup\mathrm{Green}
, and the green region
\mathrm{Green}
is the set of sites whose spins may present correlation but independent of the initial configuration. More precisely, notice that, as
X^{+}_{t}(\mathrm{Green})=X^{-}_{t}(\mathrm{Green})
, by monotonicity, in the grand coupling, the spins on
\mathrm{Green}
do not depend on the initial configuration anymore:

X^{+}_{t}(\mathrm{Green})=X^{-}_{t}(\mathrm{Green})=X^{x}_{t}(\mathrm{Green}),\;\forall x\in\mathcal{X}.

Therefore, we can safely denote it by
X_{t}(\mathrm{Green})
. Note also that
X_{t}(\mathrm{Green})
and
X^{x}_{t}(\mathrm{Red}),\,x\in\mathcal{X}
, are
\mathcal{F}_{t}
measurable.

The following lemma summarizes the discussion above.

Lemma 28

(The conditional law)
.

Almost surely, for any
t\geq 0
, conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{t}
,

X^{x}_{t}\sim\nu_{\mathrm{Blue}(t)}\otimes\delta_{X^{x}_{t}(\mathrm{Red})}\otimes\delta_{X_{t}(\mathrm{Green})},

(64)

where
\delta_{X_{t}(\mathrm{Green})}
(resp.
\delta_{X^{x}_{t}(\mathrm{Red})}
) is the Dirac measure on
\{-1,1\}^{\mathrm{Green}(t)}
(resp.
\{-1,1\}^{\mathrm{Red}(t)}
) which gives all mass to
X_{t}(\mathrm{Green})
(resp.
X^{x}_{t}(\mathrm{Red})
).

6.1
Proof of the upper bound

First, note that, by the definition of
\mathrm{Red}
,

\displaystyle\left|\mathrm{Red}(t)\right|=\dfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{u\in\Lambda_{L}}\left(X^{+}_{t}(u)-X^{-}_{t}(u)\right)\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right].

lead to the following lemma.

Lemma 29

(Decay of the red region)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathrm{Red}(t)\right|\right]=\dfrac{L}{2}(\rho^{+}(t)-\rho^{-}(t))+\mathcal{O}\left(1\right).

(65)

For any
\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}

Lemma 30

(BAD is rarely visited)

There exist constants

(66)

Proposition 31

(“Adjacent” measures quickly become close)
.

Let
z\subset\Lambda_{L}
, let

, and let

be an arbitrary spin configuration on
\Lambda_{L}\setminus(z\cup\{u\})
. Let
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}
be the two probability distributions on

where

which gives all mass to

. Let
\beta_{2}
be as in Lemma

such that

\left\|\mathbb{P}_{\pi_{1}}\left[X_{1}\in\cdot\right]-\mathbb{P}_{\pi_{2}}\left[X_{1}\in\cdot\right]\right\|_{TV}\leq\kappa\left(\dfrac{\log L}{\sqrt{L}}+\sqrt{\mathbb{P}_{z\cup\{u\}}\left[\exists s\in[0,1]:Z_{s}\in\mathrm{BAD}_{\beta_{2}}\right]}\right).

(68)

The same result holds if we replace
\pi_{1}
by
\delta^{u}_{-1}\otimes\nu_{z}\otimes\delta_{y}
.

says that very quickly, one cannot distinguish two processes starting from the measure of the form

and its perturbation

: the total variation distance drop from

to

•

When a particle meets an exclusion mark, it jumps to the other endpoint of the corresponding edge.

•

When a particle, say
\mathsf{w}

.

•

When a particle, say
\mathsf{w}

•

If a particle

With those rules, we can construct

It is more convenient to generate the history in the forward direction. Let

takings values in

as follows.

•

.

•

Whenever

1.

Put an exclusion mark on the edge
(u,u+1)
,

2.

E_{s}:=E_{s-}^{u\leftrightarrow u+1}
.

•

Whenever

jumps,

, say at time

–

.

–

(u-\left|E_{s-}\right|)
-th site in

. Then
E_{s}:=E_{s-}
.

•

Whenever
\Xi^{\text{Glauber,*}}_{i,u}
jumps, say at time

,

–

th site in

By the homogeneity of the Poisson processes

viewed backward in time. In particular, the process

Here, we have rearranged the Poisson clocks so that the first clocks in

indicate the refresh of the sites in

, and the first clocks in
\Xi^{\text{Glauber,*}}
indicate the Glauber updates of the sites in the neighbors of the process

. This makes our computation easier. More precisely, let
\mathcal{G}_{s}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\Xi^{*}
up to time

. Let
\mathcal{T}_{0}=0
and
(\tau_{i},\mathcal{T}_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be defined recursively as follows.

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{i+1}

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{t>\mathcal{T}_{i}:\,\text{a clock among $(\Xi^{\text{refresh,*}}_{u})_{1\leq u\leq\left|E_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}\right|},(\Xi^{\text{Glauber,*}}_{u,i})_{3\leq i\leq q,1\leq u\leq(2m+1)\left|E_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}\right|}$ rings at time $t$}\right\}

\displaystyle\tau_{i+1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{i+1}-\mathcal{T}_{i}.

Then we have the following:

1.

Conditionally on
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}
,
\tau_{i+1}\sim\exp\left((\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})\left|E_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}\right|+(\lambda-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})(2m+1)\left|E_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}\right|\right)
.

2.

\left|E_{s}\right|=\left|E_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}\right|,\,\forall s\in[\mathcal{T}_{i},\mathcal{T}_{i+1});\;\left|E_{\mathcal{T}_{i+1}}\right|\geq\left|E_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}\right|-(2m+1),

and hence

\left|E_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}\right|\geq|E|-i(2m+1).

This is because at most

sites are removed each time a Glauber mark appears.

3.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\text{the ring at $\mathcal{T}_{i+1}$ is among $\Xi^{\text{refresh,*}}_{1},\dots,\Xi^{\text{refresh,*}}_{\left|E_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}\right|}$}\big|\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}\right]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}}{(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})+(\lambda-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})(2m+1)}

\displaystyle=:1-\alpha.

(80)

Based on the arguments above,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[Z^{\O}_{t}\cap E=\O\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[\text{For any $\mathcal{T}_{i}\leq t$, the ring at $\mathcal{T}_{i}$ is among $\Xi^{\text{Glauber,*}}$}\right].

Note that, for any

,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\text{For any $\mathcal{T}_{i}\leq t$, the ring at $\mathcal{T}_{i}$ is among $\Xi^{\text{Glauber,*}}$}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{T}_{l}>t\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{T}_{l}\leq t,\text{the ring at $\mathcal{T}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{T}_{l}$ are among $\Xi^{\text{Glauber,*}}$}\right].

(81)

By equation (
3

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{T}_{l}\leq t,\text{the ring at $\mathcal{T}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{T}_{l}$ are among $\Xi^{\text{Glauber,*}}$}\right]\leq\alpha^{l}.

(82)

We can choose
l=10\left\lceil\dfrac{\log L}{-\log\alpha}\right\rceil
to make this term smaller than
\dfrac{1}{L^{10}}
. The bound for the first term on the right side of (

) is a consequence of the concentration of the sum
\tau_{1}+\dots+\tau_{l}
.
Let
\beta:=(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})+(\lambda-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})(2m+1)
. By Claims
1
and
2
, conditionally on
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}
,
\tau_{i+1}
is stochastically dominated by
\exp\left(\beta(\left|E\right|-i(2m+1)\right)
.
Set
\theta:=(2m+1)l
, then provided that
|E|>\theta+\dfrac{2\theta}{\beta}
,

\dfrac{\beta(|E|-(i-1)(2m+1))}{\beta(|E|-(i-1)(2m+1))-\theta}\leq 2,\,\forall 1\leq i\leq l.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\theta\mathcal{T}_{l}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\theta(\mathcal{T}_{l-1}+\tau_{l})}|\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}_{l-1}}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\theta\mathcal{T}_{l-1}}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\tau_{l}}|\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}_{l-1}}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\theta\mathcal{T}_{l-1}}\times\dfrac{\beta(|E|-(l-1)(2m+1))}{\beta(|E|-(l-1)(2m+1))-\theta}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\theta\mathcal{T}_{l-1}}\right].

By induction, we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\theta\mathcal{T}_{l}}\right]\leq 2^{l}.

Hence, by Chernoff’s bound,

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{T}_{l}>t\right]\leq e^{-\theta t}2^{l}=(2e^{-(2m+1)t})^{l}.

With

chosen as above, there exists
\beta_{3}
such that when

, the last expression is smaller than

. We finish the proof by choosing
\beta_{2}
such that
\beta_{2}\log L\geq\theta+\theta/\beta
, which clearly exists when
\theta=(2m+1)l,\;l=10\left\lceil\dfrac{\log L}{-\log q}\right\rceil
.
∎

(Perturbation of a product measure)

Proof of Lemma

u_{1}\preceq u_{2}
if there exists a permutation
\sigma
of
[d]
such that
\forall i\in[d],u_{1}(i)\preceq u_{2}(\sigma(i))
. We will also need the following result.

Lemma 50

(Distance between
2
walks in an
IP(2)
is bigger than that between 2 independent SRWs)
.

Consider the lattice
\Lambda^{d}_{L}
where every edge has conductance
L^{2}
. Let
(\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2})
be an IP(2) and
(\check{\mathcal{U}}_{1},\check{\mathcal{U}}_{2})
be a pair of independent SRWs on
\Lambda^{d}_{L}
. There is a Markovian coupling of
(\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2})
and
(\check{\mathcal{U}}_{1},\check{\mathcal{U}}_{2})
such that if the initial condition
(u_{1},u_{2},\check{u}_{1},\check{u}_{2})
satisfies
(\check{u}_{2}-\check{u}_{1})\preceq(u_{2}-u_{1})
, then almost surely,

\displaystyle\forall t\geq 0,\,(\check{\mathcal{U}}_{2}(t)-\check{\mathcal{U}}_{1}(t))\preceq(\mathcal{U}_{2}(t)-\mathcal{U}_{1}(t)),

and in particular,

\displaystyle\mathsf{dist}\left(\check{\mathcal{U}}_{2}(t),\check{\mathcal{U}}_{1}(t)\right)\leq\mathsf{dist}\left(\mathcal{U}_{2}(t),\mathcal{U}_{1}(t)\right).

Clearly, Lemma
49
and Lemma
50
together imply Lemma
21
.

Proof of Lemma
21
.

Let
u_{1},u_{2}\in\Lambda_{L}
. Consider the coupling of the IP(2)
(\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2})
and a pair of independent SRWs
(\check{\mathcal{U}}_{1},\check{\mathcal{U}}_{2})
, both starting from
u_{1},u_{2}
, independent of
\zeta
, that satisfies Lemma
50
. Then, by Lemma
50
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\mathsf{dist}\left(\mathcal{U}_{1}(\zeta),\mathcal{U}_{2}(\zeta)\right)\leq k\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\mathsf{dist}\left(\check{\mathcal{U}}_{1}(\zeta),\check{\mathcal{U}}_{2}(\zeta)\right)\leq k\right].

This and Lemma

lead to what we want.

.

Proof of Lemma
49
.

We denote by
\check{\mathcal{U}}_{1}(t,i)
the
i
-th coordinate of
\check{\mathcal{U}}_{1}(t)
, and similarly for
\check{\mathcal{U}}_{2}
. We see that
(\check{\mathcal{U}}_{1}(\cdot,i)-\check{\mathcal{U}}_{2}(\cdot,i))_{1\leq i\leq d}
are
d
independent SRWs on
\Lambda_{L}
, where every edge has conductance
2L^{2}
. Hence, for any
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\mathsf{dist}\left(\mathcal{U}_{1}(t),\mathcal{U}_{2}(t)\right)\leq k\right]

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{P}\left[\left(\check{\mathcal{U}}_{1}(t,i)-\check{\mathcal{U}}_{2}(t,i)\right)\in\{-k,\dots,k\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left((2k+1)\max_{u\in\Lambda_{L}}\mathbb{P}\left[\check{\mathcal{U}}_{1}(t,i)-\check{\mathcal{U}}_{2}(t,i)=u\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}_{k}\left(\dfrac{1}{L^{d}}\left(\dfrac{1}{t^{d/2}}+1\right)\right).

Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\mathsf{dist}\left(\check{\mathcal{U}}_{1}(\zeta),\check{\mathcal{U}}_{2}(\zeta)\right)\leq k\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[\mathsf{dist}\left(\check{\mathcal{U}}_{1}(t),\check{\mathcal{U}}_{2}(t)\right)\leq k\right]\theta e^{-\theta t}\mathrm{d}t.

We split the integral above into three intervals
[0,1/L^{2}],\,[1/L^{2},1],\,[1,\infty)
. First, we estimate the integral on
[0,1/L^{2}]
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1/L^{2}}\mathbb{P}\left[\mathsf{dist}\left(\check{\mathcal{U}}_{1}(t),\check{\mathcal{U}}_{2}(t)\right)\leq k\right]\theta e^{-\theta t}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1/L^{2}}\theta e^{-\theta t}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\theta/L^{2}.

Now we estimate the integral on
[1,\infty)
:

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[\mathsf{dist}\left(\check{\mathcal{U}}_{1}(t),\check{\mathcal{U}}_{2}(t)\right)\leq k\right]\theta e^{-\theta t}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}_{k}\left(\int_{1}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{L^{d}}\left(\dfrac{1}{t^{d/2}}+1\right)\theta e^{-\theta t}\mathrm{d}t\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}_{k}\left(\dfrac{1}{L^{d}}\int_{1}^{\infty}\theta e^{-\theta t}\mathrm{d}t\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}_{k}\left(\dfrac{1}{L^{d}}\right).

Finally, we estimate the integral on
[1/L^{2},1]
:

\displaystyle\int_{1/L^{2}}^{1}\mathbb{P}\left[\mathsf{dist}\left(\check{\mathcal{U}}_{1}(t),\check{\mathcal{U}}_{2}(t)\right)\leq k\right]\theta e^{-\theta t}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}_{k}\left(\int_{1/L^{2}}^{1}\dfrac{1}{L^{d}}\left(\dfrac{1}{t^{d/2}}+1\right)\theta e^{-\theta t}\mathrm{d}t\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}_{k}\left(\dfrac{\theta}{L^{d}}\int_{1/L^{2}}^{1}\dfrac{1}{t^{d/2}}\mathrm{d}t\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}_{k}\left(\dfrac{\theta}{L}\left(2\sqrt{t}\Big|^{1}_{1/L^{2}}\right)\right)=\mathcal{O}_{k}\left(\dfrac{\theta}{L}\right)&\text{if $d=1$},\\
\mathcal{O}_{k}\left(\dfrac{\theta}{L^{2}}\left(\log t\Big|^{1}_{1/L^{2}}\right)\right)=\mathcal{O}_{k}\left(\dfrac{\theta\log L}{L^{2}}\right)&\text{if $d=2$},\\
\mathcal{O}_{k}\left(\dfrac{\theta}{L^{d}}\left(\dfrac{t^{1-d/2}}{1-d/2}\Bigg|^{1}_{1/L^{2}}\right)\right)=\mathcal{O}_{k}\left(\dfrac{\theta}{L^{2}}\right)&\text{if $d\geq 3$}.\end{cases}

The estimates above lead to what we want.
∎

Now we prove Lemma
50
.

Proof of Lemma
50
.

The intuition is simple: an IP(2) evolves exactly like
2
independent SRWs when the two particles are not next to each other. The distance between 2 walks in IP(2) is forbidden to jump to
0
, while the distance between two independent SRWs can. This makes the distance between 2 particles in
IP(2)
always bigger than that of
2
independent SRWs in distribution.

More formally, note that
Y:=(\mathcal{U}_{1}-\mathcal{U}_{2})
and
\tilde{Y}:=(\check{\mathcal{U}}_{1}-\check{\mathcal{U}}_{2})
are also Markov processes. We only have to construct a coupling of
Y
and
\tilde{Y}
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
(resp.
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
) be the generator of
Y
(resp
\tilde{Y}
). Let
\mathfrak{e}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{e}_{d}
be the unit vectors in
\Lambda^{d}_{L}
.
Then

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(u,u^{\prime})=\begin{cases}2L^{2}&\text{ if $u^{\prime}=u\pm\mathfrak{e}_{i}$ for some $i\in[d]$},\\
0&\text{ if not},\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(u,u^{\prime})=\begin{cases}2L^{2}&\text{ if $u^{\prime}=u\pm\mathfrak{e}_{i}$ for some $i\in[d]$ and $u^{\prime}\neq 0$},\\
L^{2}&\text{ if $(u,u^{\prime})\in\{(\pm\mathfrak{e}_{i},\mp\mathfrak{e}_{i})|i\in[d]\}$},\\
0&\text{ if not},\end{cases}

To construct the coupling kernel
\mathcal{L}^{coupling}
, we only need to construct the jump rates from each configuration
(u,\check{u})
such that
u\preceq\check{u}
. Without loss of generality, we can suppose that
\mathsf{dist}\left(u(i),0\right)\leq\mathsf{dist}\left(\check{u}(i),0\right),\,\forall i\in[d]
. The kernel is as follows. For any coordinate
i
,

•

If
\mathsf{dist}\left(u(i),0\right)<\mathsf{dist}\left(\check{u}(i),0\right)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{coupling}((u,\check{u}),(u\pm\mathfrak{e}_{i},\check{u}))=\mathcal{L}^{coupling}((u,\check{u}),(u,\check{u}\pm\mathfrak{e}_{i}))=2L^{2}.

•

If
\mathsf{dist}\left(u(i),0\right)=\mathsf{dist}\left(\check{u}(i),0\right)
, then
u(i)=\pm\check{u}(i)
, and therefore there exists
\xi\in\{-1,1\}
such that
\mathsf{dist}\left(u(i)\pm 1,0\right)=\mathsf{dist}\left(\check{u}(i)\pm\xi,0\right)
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{coupling}((u,\check{u}),(u\pm\mathfrak{e}_{i},\check{u}\pm\xi\mathfrak{e}_{i}))=2L^{2}\mathds{1}_{\left\{u\pm e_{i}\neq 0\right\}}.

If
u\in\{\pm\mathfrak{e}_{i}\}
, then we necessarily have
\check{u}\in\{\pm\mathfrak{e}_{i}\}
, and in this case,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{coupling}((u,\check{u}),(-u,\check{u}))

\displaystyle=L^{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{coupling}((u,\check{u}),(u,0))

\displaystyle=2L^{2}.

For any
(u^{\prime},\check{u}^{\prime})\neq(u,\check{u})
not listed in the cases above,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{coupling}((u,\check{u}),(u^{\prime},\check{u}^{\prime}))=0.

We can verify that this gives us a coupling kernel for
(Y,\tilde{Y})
that preserves the relation
\preceq
. This finishes our proof.
∎
