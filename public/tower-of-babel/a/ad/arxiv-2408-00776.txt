Contact-conditioned learning of locomotion policies
Michal Ciebielski, Majid Khadiv

Abstract
Locomotion is realized through making and breaking contact. State-of-the-art constrained nonlinear model predictive controllers (NMPC) generate whole-body trajectories for a given contact sequence. However, these approaches are computationally expensive at run-time. Hence it is desirable to offload some of this computation to an offline phase. In this paper, we hypothesize that conditioning a learned policy on the locations and timings of contact is a suitable representation for learning a single policy that can generate multiple gaits (contact sequences). In this way, we can build a single generalist policy to realize different gaited and non-gaited locomotion skills and the transitions among them. Our extensive simulation results demonstrate the validity of our hypothesis for learning multiple gaits for a biped robot.

INTRODUCTION

The dominant approaches in the control of legged robots which have demonstrated significant improvements in recent years are model-based control, in particular (nonlinear) model predictive control (MPC)

,

, and learning-based control, especially (deep) reinforcement learning (RL)
[

. Each of these approaches presents different strengths and weaknesses. MPC techniques are notably versatile, meaning that given adequate perception and task information, they can easily adapt to new tasks and environments. Furthermore, they can take the constraints of the robot and environment into account to generate safe behaviors. However, they are sensitive to unexpected contact events and tend to be computationally expensive at run-time.

(Deep) RL-based approaches, on the other hand, can simply include uncertainties during training using domain randomization
[
8
]
which leads to robust behaviors. Furthermore, since the training happens purely offline in simulation, the run-time computation is at a minimum, i.e., inference of a small neural network. One may directly learn the policies in sensor space, including measurement noise and uncertainties in the final policy
[
9
,
10
]
. However, RL-based methods need millions of samples to train a single task and there is normally no transfer of knowledge to a new task. Finally, the reward shaping and domain randomization are performed manually, resulting in an extremely laborious and inefficient trial-and-error process.

As there are complementary features in optimal control and reinforcement learning, there has been a direction of research attempting to combine these two methods to address their weaknesses. For instance,
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
proposed to approximate the solution space of MPC to reduce online computation. The main drawback of these approaches is that they can at best be as robust as MPC policy. However, it is difficult to make MPC robust to contact event uncertainty
[
14
,
15
]
. Another approach has been to guide the exploration of RL by providing trajectories from model-based trajectory optimization techniques
[
16
,
17
]
, which has shown highly robust motions on a wide range of environments.
This seems to be one of the most successful approaches in combining optimal control and reinforcement learning for locomotion.

To make the above approaches more general, it is essential to have a generalist low-level policy that can realize different gaited and non-gaited contact plans. One notable effort in this regard is
[
18
]
which learns multiple gaits for a quadruped robot using a single policy. This work uses a mixture-of-expert policy network where each expert learns one single gait (mode of the hybrid system). This means that the approach requires an expert for each gait, hence it does not scale to the case of non-gaited motions where there are many combinations of hybrid modes. Contrary to
[
18
]
,
in this paper, we propose a new way of representing goals to the low-level policy that is general among a wide range of motions of a legged system. Specifically, we condition the policy on the location and time of the next contact(s) for the end-effectors which provides a general representation for locomotion and perhaps loco-manipulation. We benchmark the performance and robustness of contact-conditioned policies against the velocity-conditioned ones and show that on top of being a general representation, conditioning policies on contact switches result in improved robustness and performance.

The main contribution of the paper is as follows:

•

We propose a novel goal representation to learn low-level locomotion policies. This representation is general enough that can be used for learning any type of gaited or non-gaited locomotion behaviors through a single policy network. In particular, we present learning of a wide range of walking and running gaits for a biped robot and switching between them by using an MPC controller as the expert.

•

Through an extensive set of simulation experiments on a biped robot, We show that this representation is more desirable than the traditional velocity-based policies. In particular, we compare this representation with the common velocity conditioning used in the literature for both MPC

Importantly, we show that the contact-conditioned policy outperforms the velocity-conditioned one when the policy is tested out of the distribution of the training data.

II
Preliminaries

In this section, first, we explain behavioral cloning that is used in the paper to learn policies from an MPC controller
[
13
]
. Then, we give an overview of the expert policy that is used in the paper to clone different policies. Note that our results are independent of these frameworks and should apply to any other approach to learning a hierarchy of policies.

II-A

Behavioral clonning

Behavioral cloning (BC) is the simplest form of imitation learning through which the collected data from an expert is used to train a policy using supervised learning
[
22
]
. While several extensions of online algorithms have been proposed to eliminate the compounding error and distribution shift
[
23
,
24
]
, BC is still used for many applications due to its simplicity and ease of implementation. The main reason behind this choice in this work is that contrary to online learning and RL algorithms, the distribution of data remains the same for the comparison which allows us to focus mainly on the choice of goal representation.

In BC process, first, a dataset
D
comprised of observed states
s
and expert actions
a
is collected offline. Then, these samples are treated as if they were independent and identically distributed (i.i.d) and supervised learning is carried out to approximate a policy
\pi
that replicates the action of the expert at any given state. The training objective is minimizing the errors in mimicking an expert’s actions
\pi=\arg\max_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{D}[\log\pi(a\mid s)]
, simply minimizing the empirical risk as in supervised learning.

II-B

Expert Controller

We use the MPC formulation in
[
25
]
that extends the formulation in
[
26
,
27
]
to a unified framework for walking and running of bipedal robots. The software is also open-source and available on
Github
. Due to its simplicity of formulation, the MPC can solve for both the step location and timing of the next step at 1 KHz. This makes the MPC highly reactive to external disturbances and changes of commands (desired gait and motion), which are the main requirements for learning a policy in this work. The generated foot trajectories from the MPC are mapped to the joint torques using task-space inverse dynamics. We would like to again emphasize that our results in this paper are independent of the choice of the MPC and are valid for any other expert controller.

III
Method

In this section, we detail our framework and the implementation details. First, we detail the different possibilities for goal representation and explain our proposed conditioning on the next contact locations and timings. Then, we present the implementation details and evaluation metrics used to benchmark our results with the alternative representations.

III-A

Goal Representation

The classical approach to represent the desired behavior for learning locomotion policies is an average desired velocity
[
28
,
5
]
. In this approach, the idea is to simply give the desired direction and speed of motion to the robot, and the policy generates whole-body actions that result in kinematically and dynamically feasible motions. The average desired velocity is then modulated by a high-level policy to perform a desired task like object manipulation
[
21
]
. While this approach is simple and intuitive, we argue that it is limiting in many cases. For instance, if the robot can move with different types of gaits, the desired velocity is an ambiguous representation, as the robot would be confused about which gait to select at each state (multi-modality). To address this, one can argue that we can always have the gait type as input
[
18
]
, and by conditioning also on the velocity, the robot could uniquely decide the actions. While we use this approach as a baseline, it is clear that this cannot scale to the general non-gaited locomotion behaviors, as the number of inputs to the policy increases substantially. Secondly, we argue that simply conditioning on the gait type does not exploit the shared structure between different gaits through contact. Hence, in this paper, we present a new representation of goals in the policy.

For both locomotion and manipulation problems, making and breaking contact with the world is critical. To structure a policy that can be generalized to most locomotion and manipulation problems, we propose to condition the policy on a set of new contact locations and times for some end-effectors (see Fig.

). This way of representing the policy is more flexible than the velocity-conditioned policy. For instance, for moving in highly constrained environments such as stepping stones
[

, the high-level policy needs to have access to choose the contact locations.

Specifically, our proposed conditions, namely contact goals
g_{cc}
, conditions the policy on the next desired contact position
p_{k+1}
and time remaining to that contact
t_{rem}
for each end-effector. One can potentially increase the number of contact switches to the future to avoid myopic behaviors, e.g., two contact goals
g_{tcc}
which are the next two desired contact positions and timings. We benchmark this conditioning to the standard desired velocity goals
g_{vc}
which are the velocity
v_{d_{i}}
and the type of gait
G_{i}
.

IV
Results

In this section, we present the results of the simulation experiments of policies conditioned on the next contact switches and velocity-conditioned policies. First, we describe how the evaluation data was collected and then, we compare the failure rates and survivability of the policies. After that, we evaluate how well the policies track the desired velocities and contacts. Lastly, we evaluate the performance of the policies on similar metrics but with desired behaviors out of the distribution of the training data.

IV-A

Evaluation Setup

In order to compare the effect of different conditioning variables in the multi-gait setting, each of the policies was rolled out with the same distribution of velocities, durations and gaits
(v_{d},T_{d},G)
. In the experiments the following questions are answered: which goal conditioning contains a richer representation for policy learning? How well do the policies track the desired velocity and desired contact locations? How often do the trained policies fail? What amount of data do the policies require to perform well?

To answer these questions a set of 3 policies was trained with different amounts of data, namely with 25, 50, 100, 200, and 400 episodes. These policies were then tested in three sets of evaluations, the first which we will call single batch consisted of an episode with one desired velocity, duration and gait, the second one which we will call triple batch, consisted of an episode with three velocities, durations and gaits, and in the third evaluation the desired velocity was modified to sample from velocity data not seen in the training. Each episode had a total simulation time of between 1 and 9 seconds and the data was collected at 1 kHz. Results from these evaluations are presented in the following sections.

V
Discussion

In this study, we explored a novel goal representation for low level locomotion policies. As a baseline we compared to a typical desired velocity conditioned policy to see how a policy which was conditioned on contact could perform. In the experiments on in-distribution data, the biggest differences could be seen in the fail rate and survivability, particularly in the higher data regime. This supports our hypothesis that the contact-conditioned policy is better able to exploit the shared structure between different gaits.

While the performance of the contact conditioned-policy is important, the primary focus should be on its potential to be used for various gaited and non-gaited behaviors. When examining the performance of policies on previously unseen velocities, it becomes evident that velocity can be viewed as the task objective. The contact-conditioned policy, which is not directly conditioned on velocity but rather on the positions and times remaining until the respective contacts, provides a more generic representation. This generic nature potentially allows it to generalize more effectively across various tasks. In contrast, the velocity-conditioned policy tends to overfit to the specific task of achieving only the desired velocities which were seen during training. Therefore, within a hierarchical policy framework, the contact-conditioned policy begins to approximate a generic low-level controller that can be utilized with various contact planners.

Regarding the two-contact policy, it can be considered a simple heuristic. Using the nominal step duration and desired velocity to find the next desired contact location may cause contradictory data to be introduced into the training dataset, because when recovering from disturbances this heuristic is unlikely to be correct. The failure rate graph indicates that while this policy yields early rewards in low-data regimes, the information it provides is of lower quality. As the amount of training data increases, this policy’s performance diminishes, eventually being outperformed by the single contact-conditioned policy. An interesting follow-up would be to condition the policy on two high-fidelity contacts, rather than relying on a simple heuristic.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a novel goal conditioning of low-level policies for legged locomotion. Contrary to traditional conditioning on the desired average velocity, we proposed to represent the goal as a set of locations and timings of contacts in the future. Through an extensive set of simulation experiments on the walking and running of a biped robot, we have shown that representing desired behavior as next contact switches can improve the performance and robustness in both in-distribution and out-of-distribution scenarios. In particular, the latter has shown that conditioning the policy on contact switches can be a rich goal representation that can help generalize to unseen cases in training.

In the future, we plan to train a single policy that can realize any desired contact sequences. Furthermore, we are interested in testing the hypothesis on quadrupedal locomotion as well as humanoid loco-manipulation tasks. Finally, real-world experiments are foreseeable in a near future.
