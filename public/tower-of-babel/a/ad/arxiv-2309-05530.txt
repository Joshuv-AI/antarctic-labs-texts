Stable C 1 C^{1} -conforming finite element methods for a class of nonlinear fourth-order evolution equations
Agus L. Soenjaya, Thanh Tran

Abstract
. We propose some finite element schemes to solve a class of fourth-order nonlinear PDEs, which include the vector-valued Landau–Lifshitz–Baryakhtar equation, the Swift–Hohenberg equation, and various Cahn–Hilliard-type equations with source and convection terms, among others. The proposed numerical methods include a spatially semi-discrete scheme and two linearised fully-discrete C 1 C^{1} -conforming schemes utilising a semi-implicit Euler method and a semi-implicit BDF method. We show that these numerical schemes are stable in ℍ 2 \mathbb{H}^{2} . Error analysis is performed which shows optimal convergence rates in each scheme. Numerical experiments corroborate our theoretical results.



1.
Introduction

This paper aims to develop and analyse some fully-discrete
C^{1}
-conforming finite element methods to solve a class of nonlinear fourth-order evolution PDEs with diffusion, convection, as well as nonlinear precession and source terms. The proposed numerical methods would be applicable to some concrete nonlinear models arising in micromagnetics, materials science, and population dynamics. These include the vector-valued Landau–Lifshitz–Baryakhtar equation, the scalar-valued Swift–Hohenberg equation, and various convective Cahn–Hilliard-type equations with source term, among others.

We now describe the problem which is discussed in this paper. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
d\in\{1,2,3\}
, be a bounded open domain with boundary
\partial\Omega
, and let
t\in[0,T]
be the time variable. Let
\boldsymbol{u}(t):\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
m=1
or
3
, be the unknown (scalar-valued or vector-valued) function which satisfies

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}{\partial t}-\beta_{1}\Delta\boldsymbol{u}+\beta_{2}\Delta^{2}\boldsymbol{u}=\beta_{3}(1-|\boldsymbol{u}|^{2})\boldsymbol{u}-\beta_{4}\boldsymbol{u}\times\Delta\boldsymbol{u}\quad&\\[4.30554pt]
\hskip 117.0pt+\beta_{5}\Delta(|\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}\boldsymbol{u})+\beta_{6}(\boldsymbol{j}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}\quad&\text{for $(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in(0,T)\times\Omega$,}\\[4.30554pt]
\boldsymbol{u}(0,\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{u_{0}}(\boldsymbol{x})\quad&\text{for }\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega,\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle{\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}{\partial\boldsymbol{n}}=\boldsymbol{0}},\;\displaystyle{\frac{\partial(\Delta\boldsymbol{u})}{\partial\boldsymbol{n}}=\boldsymbol{0}}\quad&\text{for }(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in(0,T)\times\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Here,
\beta_{2}
and
\beta_{3}
are positive constants of physical significance, while the constants
\beta_{4},\beta_{5},\beta_{6}
are non-negative. The constant
\beta_{1}
can be positive or negative, but without loss of generality it is taken as positive here. The symbol
\times
denotes the cross product. We set
\beta_{4}=0
when
\boldsymbol{u}
is scalar-valued. The vector field
\boldsymbol{j}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a given current density. For physical reason, we assume that the current density
\boldsymbol{j}
is divergence-free and is tangential to the boundary, i.e.
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{j}=0
on
\Omega
and
\boldsymbol{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0
on
\partial\Omega
. The Neumann boundary condition is set for the above problem, but similar argument will also work for the Dirichlet boundary condition.

In (
1.1
),
-\Delta\boldsymbol{u}
and

\Delta^{2}\boldsymbol{u}
represent (homogeneous) linear diffusion,
\Delta(|\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}\boldsymbol{u})
corresponds to nonlinear diffusion,
\boldsymbol{u}\times\Delta\boldsymbol{u}

represents a form of precession, while the term
(1-|\boldsymbol{u}|^{2})\boldsymbol{u}
describes interaction between the source and cubic absorption terms. The term
(\boldsymbol{j}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}
is a convective term arising from a given current density
\boldsymbol{j}
. Some combinations of these features are widespread across various systems of interest in physics, chemistry, and biology, which we elaborate in the following paragraphs.

When
m=3
, i.e.
\boldsymbol{u}
is vector-valued, the problem (
1.1
) includes the following:

(i)

The case
\beta_{6}=0
is the Landau–Lifshitz–Baryakhtar equation in the theory of micromagnetics (see
[
4
,
5
,
44
,
45
,
49
]
for physical motivations). When
\beta_{6}\neq 0
, this is the model with spin-torque considered in
[
50
]
.

(ii)

The case
\beta_{5}=\beta_{6}=0
is a regularisation of the Landau–Lifshitz–Bloch equation, whose stochastic version is analysed in
[
28
]
.

When
m=1
and
\beta_{4}=0
, i.e.
\boldsymbol{u}
is scalar-valued, the present model includes the following:

(i)

The case
\beta_{1}<0
and
\beta_{5}=0
gives the Swift–Hohenberg equation
[
47
]
, which describes the effects of thermal fluctuations on the convective instability
[
20
]
. A system of such equations is also considered in
[
6
]
.

(ii)

The case
\beta_{1}\geq 0
and
\beta_{5}=0
gives a fourth-order reaction-diffusion-convection equation, which could be used to model anomalous bi-flux diffusion
[
7
,
16
,
32
]
.

(iii)

The case
\beta_{3}=0
gives the Cahn–Hilliard equation with convection
[
13
,
23
,
34
]
, which is used to study phase separation in a mixture. A system of such equations is also considered in
[
15
]
. The case
\beta_{3}\neq 0
gives the Cahn–Hilliard equation with mass source
[
25
,
35
]
or the Allen–Cahn/Cahn–Hilliard equation
[
33
]
.

(iv)

The case
\beta_{6}=0
gives a generalised diffusion model for growth and dispersal in a multi-species population
[
19
,
38
]
.

The wide applicability of (
1.1
) to model various physical phenomena motivates the numerical analysis of the system as done in the present paper.
To the best of our knowledge, numerical scheme to approximate
the solution of the problem (
1.1
) in its generality do not exist yet in the literature. While numerical methods to separately solve the scalar-valued Swift–Hohenberg equation
[
39
,
40
]
, the fourth order bi-flux reaction diffusion equation
[
32
]
, the extended Fisher–Kolmogorov equation
[
31
]
, and the Cahn–Hilliard equation
[
14
,
24
,
34
]
are present in the literature, none of these is sufficiently general to include (
1.1
). Related to this, a conforming method for a stochastic counterpart of (
1.1
) with
\beta_{5}=\beta_{6}=0
is analysed in
[
28
]
, where convergence in probability of the scheme is shown. For the general case considered in the present paper (where all the coefficients of the equation (
1.1
) are nonzero), more careful analysis needs to be done to obtain an optimal order of convergence.

Since (
1.1
) is a fourth-order equation, a conforming finite element method
requires
C^{1}
-continuous elements. In this paper, we propose two fully discrete

C^{1}
-conforming finite element schemes to solve (
1.1
) for
d=1,2,3
, namely, a semi-implicit Euler scheme and a semi-implicit BDF scheme.
While the case
d=3
is also analysed in this paper for completeness, we admit that it is very restrictive in practice since a
C^{1}
-conforming finite element on tetrahedron requires piecewise polynomials of degree at least 9, thus computationally infeasible. Therefore for
d=3
, non-conforming methods should be used instead. A mixed finite element method specifically developed to solve only the Landau–Lifshitz–Baryakhtar equation in mixed formulation is studied in a separate paper
[
43
]
, where a certain nonlinear scheme is proposed.

It is noted that the schemes proposed in this paper are linear, in that one only needs to solve a system of linear equations at each time step. This is an advantage, for instance in micromagnetic simulations as argued by
[
46
]
. These methods are unconditionally stable in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
for
d=1
and
2
(in the sense that no restriction of the time-step size in terms of the spatial mesh size is needed, and without assuming quasi-uniformity of the triangulation), and conditionally stable for
d=3
. We then prove rigorously that the numerical schemes converge to the exact solution in
\mathbb{H}^{\beta}
norm (for
\beta=0
,
1
,
2
) at the optimal order. This is done by developing an appropriate elliptic projection which is adapted to the problem and performing careful analysis to estimate the nonlinear terms. In particular, these linearised finite element schemes offer simple methods to solve various nonlinear vector-valued or scalar-valued problems mentioned previously.

While such conforming methods could be rather expensive computationally
due to the
C^{1}
-continuity requirement across element boundaries, this feature
leads to an enhanced accuracy and a higher order of convergence in capturing information about solutions and their gradients compared to the non-conforming methods
[
51
]
. In general, one also achieves a better stability property compared to the non-conforming approximations. With a tremendous growth in computational power during the last decade, it is now relatively easier to implement a
C^{1}
-conforming finite element to approximate the solution of a fourth-order nonlinear PDE in 1D or 2D, with an added advantage that these proposed schemes are linear. Without trying to be exhaustive, we mention several recent papers which discuss the analysis and implementation of
C^{1}
-conforming finite element schemes to approximate the solution to fourth-order nonlinear equations, including the KdV–Rosenau equation
[
2
]
, the Rosenau-RLW equation
[
3
]
, the extended Fisher–Kolmogorov equation
[
31
]
, a fourth-order optimal control problem
[
12
]
, quasi-geostrophic equation
[
26
,
27
]
, and the von Kármán equation
[
36
]
. However, the problems discussed in those papers are scalar-valued and have different nonlinearities from ours.

This paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
, some notations
and preliminary results used in this paper are discussed. In Section

3
, error estimates for the semi-discrete approximation are
established. Section
4
and
5
discuss fully
discrete schemes based on semi-implicit Euler and BDF2 methods, respectively. Numerical results
are presented in Section
6
. Finally, a lemma concerning a regularity result for an auxiliary equation is discussed in Section
7
, while some commonly used results are presented in the appendix. For ease of presentation, we will assume that
\boldsymbol{u}
is vector-valued in the analysis (
m=3
), noting that the same argument will also work when
\boldsymbol{u}
is scalar-valued.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notations

We begin by stating some function spaces used in this paper. The function space
\mathbb{L}^{p}:=\mathbb{L}^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
denotes the space of
p
-th integrable functions taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\mathbb{W}^{m,r}:=\mathbb{W}^{m,r}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
denotes the Sobolev space of
functions on
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for
d=1,2,3
, taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. As usual, we write
\mathbb{H}^{m}:=\mathbb{W}^{m,2}
.
If
X
is a Banach space, the spaces
L^{p}(0,T;X)
and
W^{m,r}(0,T;X)
denote respectively the Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces of functions on
(0,T)
taking values in
X
. The space
C([0,T];X)
denotes the space of continuous function on
[0,T]
taking values in
X
. For brevity, we will write
L^{p}(\mathbb{W}^{m,r}):=L^{p}(0,T;\mathbb{W}^{m,r})
and
L^{p}(\mathbb{L}^{q}):=L^{p}(0,T;\mathbb{L}^{q})
.

Throughout this paper, we denote the scalar product in a Hilbert space
H
by
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{H}
and its corresponding norm by
\left\|{\cdot}\right\|_{H}
. The scalar product of
\mathbb{L}^{2}
vector-valued functions taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the scalar product of
\mathbb{L}^{2}
matrix-valued functions taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
will both be denoted by
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}
.

We also define the function spaces

(2.1)

\mathbb{H}_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{2}:=\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}\,:\,\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}{\partial\boldsymbol{n}}=0\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{H}_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{4}:=\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{H}^{4}\,:\,\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}{\partial\boldsymbol{n}}=\frac{\partial(\Delta\boldsymbol{v})}{\partial\boldsymbol{n}}=0\right\}.

We assume sufficient conditions on the smoothness of the boundary of
\Omega

such that the following hold:

(2.2)

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{H}_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{2},

(2.3)

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{3}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\left\|{\nabla\Delta\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{H}_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{2}\cap\mathbb{H}^{3},

(2.4)

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{4}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\left\|{\Delta^{2}\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{H}_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{4}.

The above are true for
C^{4}
-domains; see e.g.
[
44
, Lemma 3.3]
.
For domains with less smooth boundaries, it
is known, for instance, that the
\mathbb{H}^{2}
-regularity
result (
2.2
) is guaranteed to
hold for any convex Lipschitz domains
[
29
, Section 3.2]
. The

\mathbb{H}^{3}
-regularity result (
2.3
) holds for convex polygonal
domains, see
[
9
]
,
[
29
, Section 5.1]
. The

\mathbb{H}^{4}
-regularity result (
2.4
) can be guaranteed for convex polygonal domains satisfying certain interior angle conditions; see
[
9
,
21
]
,

[
29
, Section 7.3]
and references therein for more details.

Finally, the constant
C
denotes a
generic constant which can take different values at different occurrences. If
the dependence of
C
on some variable, e.g.
T
, is highlighted, we will write

C(T)
. The notation
A\lesssim B
means
A\leq CB
, where the specific form of
the constant
C
is not important to clarify.

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}\odot\nabla\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{k}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{k}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{k+1}},

(2.23)

\displaystyle\left\|{(\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{v})\odot\nabla\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{k}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{k}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{k}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{k+1}},

where
C=C(\Omega,k,d)
, but is independent of
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}
.

Proof.

The first inequality (
2.9
) is a direct consequence
of integration by parts and Hölder’s inequality. The inequality
(
2.10
) is a result of Young’s inequality.

To prove (
2.11
), we first deduce from
Hölder’s inequality

\displaystyle\left\|{\varphi\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,p}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\varphi\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}}+\left\|{\nabla(\varphi\boldsymbol{v})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}}\leq\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{L^{q_{1}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{r_{1}}}+\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{L^{q_{1}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,r_{1}}}+\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{W^{1,q_{2}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{r_{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{L^{q_{1}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,r_{1}}}+\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{W^{1,q_{2}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{r_{2}}}.

By symmetry, we obtain (
2.11
).
Inequalities (
2.12
),
(
2.13
), and (
2.14
)
can be proved in exactly the same manner.

Next, we prove (
4
). By Hölder’s inequality,

\displaystyle\left\|{(\boldsymbol{u}\odot\boldsymbol{v})\circledast\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,p}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{(\boldsymbol{u}\odot\boldsymbol{v})\circledast\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}}+\left\|{\nabla\big((\boldsymbol{u}\odot\boldsymbol{v})\circledast\boldsymbol{w}\big)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{q_{1}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{r_{1}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{s_{1}}}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,q_{1}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{r_{1}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{s_{1}}}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{q_{2}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,r_{2}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{s_{2}}}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{q_{3}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{r_{3}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,s_{3}}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,q_{1}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{r_{1}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{s_{1}}}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{q_{2}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,r_{2}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{s_{2}}}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{q_{3}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{r_{3}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,s_{3}}}.

Inequality (
4
) then follows by symmetry.
The proof of (
5
) can be done in
exactly the same manner.

We will prove (
5
) next. By Hölder’s inequality,

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{w})}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,p}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Big(\left\|{(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\partial_{i}\boldsymbol{w})\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}}+\left\|{\nabla\big((\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\partial_{i}\boldsymbol{w})\boldsymbol{u}\big)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Big(\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{q_{1}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{r_{1}}}\left\|{\partial_{i}\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{s_{1}}}+\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{r_{2}}}\left\|{\partial_{i}\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{s_{2}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{q_{2}}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{r_{3}}}\left\|{\nabla\partial_{i}\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{s_{3}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{q_{3}}}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{r_{1}}}\left\|{\partial_{i}\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{s_{1}}}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{q_{1}}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,q_{1}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{r_{1}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,s_{1}}}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{q_{2}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,r_{2}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,s_{2}}}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{q_{3}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{r_{3}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{2,s_{3}}}.

Therefore, (
5
) follows by symmetry.
Next, let
\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})
and
\boldsymbol{w}=(w_{1},w_{2},w_{3})
. Then

\displaystyle\left\|{\varphi\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{k}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left\|{\varphi w_{i}}\right\|_{H^{k}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{3}C\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{H^{k}}\left\|{w_{i}}\right\|_{H^{k}}\leq C\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{H^{k}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{k}},

where we used the fact that

(2.24)

\displaystyle\left\|{\varphi\psi}\right\|_{H^{k}}\leq C\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{H^{k}}\left\|{\psi}\right\|_{H^{k}}

for scalar-valued functions
\varphi
and
\psi
, if
k>d/2

[
1
]
. Moreover, using (
2.24
) again,

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{k}}\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}\left\|{v_{i}w_{j}}\right\|_{H^{k}}\lesssim\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}\left\|{v_{i}}\right\|_{H^{k}}\left\|{w_{j}}\right\|_{H^{k}}\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left\|{v_{i}}\right\|_{H^{k}}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{3}\left\|{w_{j}}\right\|_{H^{k}}\right)\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{k}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{k}},

and similarly we also have
\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{k}}\leq C\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{k}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{k}}
, thus proving (
2.19
). Furthermore, (
2.20
) follows by repeatedly applying (
2.19
).

Next, by (
2.18
) we have

\displaystyle\left\|{\varphi\nabla\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{k}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\|{\varphi\partial_{i}\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{k}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}C\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{H^{k}}\left\|{\partial_{i}\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{k}}\leq C\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{H^{k}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{k+1}},

proving (
2.21
).
Inequality (
2.22
) can be shown in the same manner.

Finally, (
2.23
) follows from (
2.19
) and (
2.22
). This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

To facilitate the proof of our error analysis, we need several multilinear forms defined in the following lemmas.

Lemma 2.3
.

Given
\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbb{H}_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{2}
, let
\cal{B}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\eta};\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot):\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{H}^{1}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\cal{C}(\boldsymbol{\eta};\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot):\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{H}^{1}\to\mathbb{R}
be respectively defined by

\displaystyle\cal{B}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\eta};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle:=\beta_{3}\left\langle(\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta})\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\beta_{5}\left\langle(\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta})\nabla\boldsymbol{v},\nabla\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

(2.25)

\displaystyle\quad\;+\beta_{5}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{v}),\nabla\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\beta_{5}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{v}),\nabla\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

and

(2.26)

\displaystyle\cal{C}(\boldsymbol{\eta};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}):=-\beta_{4}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\eta}\times\nabla\boldsymbol{v},\nabla\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}.

The following statements hold true:

(i)

The map
\cal{B}
is symmetric with respect to the third and fourth variable.

(ii)

For all
\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{H}^{1}
,

(2.27)

\displaystyle|\cal{B}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\eta};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})|

\displaystyle\leq(\beta_{3}+3\beta_{5})\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}},

(2.28)

\displaystyle|\cal{C}(\boldsymbol{\eta};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})|

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{4}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}.

(iii)

Moreover, if
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{H}_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{2}
and
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{H}^{1}
, then

\displaystyle|\cal{B}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\eta};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big(\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,4}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,4}}\Big)\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,4}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

(2.29)

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}},

(2.30)

\displaystyle|\cal{B}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\eta};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}},

and

(2.31)

\displaystyle|\cal{C}(\boldsymbol{\eta};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,4}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,4}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}},

(2.32)

\displaystyle|\cal{C}(\boldsymbol{\eta};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}},

where the constants depend only on
\beta_{3},\beta_{4},\beta_{5}
, and
\Omega
.

Proof.

We can write

\displaystyle\cal{B}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\eta};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=\beta_{3}\left\langle(\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta})\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\beta_{5}\left\langle(\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta})\nabla\boldsymbol{w},\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\beta_{5}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{w}),\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\beta_{5}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{w}),\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\cal{B}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\eta};\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{v}).

Thus,
\cal{B}
is symmetric in terms of
\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{w}
.
By Hölder’s inequality and the Sobolev embedding
\mathbb{H}^{2}\subset\mathbb{L}^{\infty}
, we have

\displaystyle\big|\cal{B}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\eta};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})\big|

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}},

\displaystyle\big|\cal{C}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\eta};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})\big|

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}},

showing (
2.27
) and (
2.28
), respectively. Next, performing integration by parts (and noting the boundary terms vanish since
\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{H}_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{2}
), and applying Hölder’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\cal{B}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\eta};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=\beta_{3}\left\langle(\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta})\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}-\beta_{5}\left\langle\nabla\cdot\big((\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta})\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\big),\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad-\beta_{5}\left\langle\nabla\cdot\big(\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{v})\big),\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}-\beta_{5}\left\langle\nabla\cdot\big(\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{v})\big),\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\cal{C}(\boldsymbol{\eta};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=\beta_{4}\left\langle\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol{\eta}\times\nabla\boldsymbol{v}),\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}.

We can estimate each term on the right-hand side as follows. For the first term in
\cal{B}
, by Hölder’s inequality and Sobolev embedding,

\displaystyle\big|\beta_{3}\left\langle(\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta})\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\big|\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}.

For the second term, by (
5
), similarly we have

\displaystyle\big|\beta_{5}\left\langle\nabla\cdot\big((\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta})\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\big),\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\big|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{(\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta})\nabla\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,4}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,4}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,4}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,4}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}.

The third and the fourth terms in
\cal{B}
can be estimated in a similar way by using (
5
).
For the functional
\mathcal{C}
, by Hölder’s inequality and (
2.14
), we have

\displaystyle\big|\beta_{4}\left\langle\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol{\eta}\times\nabla\boldsymbol{v}),\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\big|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}\times\nabla\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

(2.33)

\displaystyle\lesssim\big(\left\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}+\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,4}}\big)\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}.

This concludes the proof of (
iii
) and (
2.31
). The proof of (
2.30
) and (
2.32
) then follows by the Sobolev embedding
\mathbb{H}^{2}\subset\mathbb{L}^{\infty}\cap\mathbb{W}^{1,4}
, completing the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Given
\alpha>0
and
\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathbb{H}_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{2}
, let
\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot):\mathbb{H}_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{2}\times\mathbb{H}_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by

(2.34)

\displaystyle\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle:=\cal{A}_{0}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})+\cal{B}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})+\cal{C}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}),

where
\cal{B}
and
\cal{C}
are defined by (
2.3
)
and (
2.26
), respectively, and
\cal{A}_{0}
is defined by

\displaystyle\cal{A}_{0}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle:=\alpha\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\beta_{1}\left\langle\nabla\boldsymbol{v},\nabla\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\beta_{2}\left\langle\Delta\boldsymbol{v},\Delta\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}.

Then
\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\cdot,\cdot)
is bounded, i.e., there exists
\kappa>0
depending only on
\alpha,\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{5}
, and

\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
, such that

(2.35)

|\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})|\leq\kappa\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{H}_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{2}.

Moreover,
\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\cdot,\cdot)
is coercive, i.e.,

(2.36)

\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})\geq\mu\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{H}_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{2},

for all
\alpha>0
if
\beta_{1}\geq 0
, and for all
\alpha>\beta_{1}^{2}/\beta_{2}

if
\beta_{1}<0
. The constant
\mu
depends
on
\alpha
,
\beta_{1}
,
\beta_{2}
, and
\Omega
, but is independent
of
\boldsymbol{\phi}
.

Proof.

For any
\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{H}_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{2}
, by Hölder’s inequality, (
2.27
), (
2.28
), and Sobolev embedding, we have

\displaystyle|\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})|

\displaystyle\leq\left|{\cal{A}_{0}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})}\right|+\left|{\cal{B}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})}\right|+\left|{\cal{C}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\big(\alpha+|\beta_{1}|+\beta_{2}+\beta_{3}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}+\beta_{4}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}+3\beta_{5}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}\big)\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}

\displaystyle=:\kappa\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}},

for any
\alpha>0
, thus showing (
2.35
).

On the other hand, for any
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{H}_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{2}
, noting
(
2.9
) we deduce

\displaystyle\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\alpha\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\beta_{1}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\beta_{2}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\beta_{3}\left\|{|\boldsymbol{\phi}||\boldsymbol{v}|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

(2.37)

\displaystyle\quad+\beta_{5}\left\|{|\boldsymbol{\phi}||\nabla\boldsymbol{v}|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+2\beta_{5}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2},

so that

(2.38)

\displaystyle\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})\geq\alpha\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\beta_{1}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\beta_{2}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

If
\beta_{1}
is non-negative, then for any
\alpha>0
we have (
2.36
)
with
\mu=\min\{\alpha,\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\}
.
If
\beta_{1}
is negative, we derive from (
2.38
)
and (
2.9
) that

\displaystyle\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-|\beta_{1}|\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\beta_{2}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{3\alpha}{4}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\Big(\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}-\frac{|\beta_{1}|}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\Big)^{2}+\Big(\beta_{2}-\frac{\beta_{1}^{2}}{\alpha}\Big)\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\mu^{\ast}\Big(\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\Big)\geq\mu^{\ast}C\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2},

where
\mu^{\ast}=\min\{3\alpha/4,\beta_{2}-\beta_{1}^{2}/\alpha\}
is positive if

\alpha>0
is sufficiently large, and the constant
C
is given
by (
2.2
). Inequality (
2.36
) then follows with

\mu=C\mu^{\ast}
.
∎

satisfies

(2.42)

\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{r+1})}+\left\|{\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{r+1})}+\left\|{\partial_{t}^{2}\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{1})}+\left\|{\partial_{t}^{3}\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{L}^{2})}\leq K.

These assumptions are needed so that, among other things, the optimal order approximation properties (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) of our finite element spaces are satisfied.

3.
Semi-discrete Galerkin Approximation

Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be a shape-regular triangulation of
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

into intervals (in 1D), triangles (in 2D), or tetrahedra (in 3D) with maximal mesh-size
h
. We
introduce a
C^{1}
-conforming finite element space
\mathbb{V}_{h}\subset\mathbb{H}_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{2}\cap C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
which consists of

C^{1}
-
P_{r}
elements (
C^{1}
-piecewise polynomials of degree
r
) defined on
\mathcal{T}_{h}
. For example, in 1D
we take
\mathbb{V}_{h}
to be the space of
C^{1}
-
P_{3}
Hermite finite elements
[
18
]
, whereas we use the Hsieh–Clough–Tocher
C^{1}
-
P_{3}
macroelements in 2D
[
17
,
37
]
, and the Ženíšek
C^{1}
-
P_{9}
elements in 3D
[
48
,
52
]
.

Some essential approximation properties for our finite element spaces
[
17
,
18
,
22
,
52
]
are described as follows. Due to shape regularity of the triangulation, the
following optimal order
\mathbb{H}^{\beta}
-approximation property for this
C^{1}
-conforming finite element holds: There exists a constant
C
independent of
h
and
\boldsymbol{v}
such that
for any
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{H}^{r+1}
, with
0\leq\beta\leq 2
,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\inf_{\boldsymbol{\chi}\in{\mathbb{V}}_{h}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{\chi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{\beta}}\leq Ch^{r+1-\beta}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{r+1}}.

Furthermore, there exists a nodal interpolation operator
\mathcal{I}_{h}:\mathbb{H}^{r+1}\to\mathbb{V}_{h}
that satisfies

(3.2)

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}-\mathcal{I}_{h}(\boldsymbol{v})}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{\beta}}\leq Ch^{r+1-\beta}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathbb{H}^{r+1}},

such that (see
[
11
, Section 4.4]
) for
p\in[1,\infty)
and
m\in[0,2]
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\left\|{\cal{I}_{h}(\boldsymbol{v})}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{m,p}}\leq C\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{m,p}},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{W}^{m,p}.

At some parts of the paper (which will be clearly indicated), we will also assume that the triangulation is quasi-uniform so that the inverse estimates hold, see
[
11
, Theorem 4.5.11]
.

A semi-discrete Galerkin approximation to problem (
2.39
) is defined as
follows: Find
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{V}_{h}
such that for all

t\in[0,T]
and
\boldsymbol{\chi}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\left\langle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t),\boldsymbol{\chi}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t);\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t),\boldsymbol{\chi})-(\alpha+\beta_{3})\left\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t),\boldsymbol{\chi}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}-\beta_{6}\left\langle(\boldsymbol{j}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t),\boldsymbol{\chi}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}=0,

and
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(0)=\boldsymbol{u}_{0,h}
, where
\boldsymbol{u}_{0,h}
is an approximation of

\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
in
\mathbb{V}_{h}
, say
\boldsymbol{u}_{0,h}=\mathcal{I}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})
, so
that

(3.5)

\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0,h}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{\beta}}\leq Ch^{r+1-\beta}.

This system of ordinary differential equations (
3.4
) has a unique solution
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}

defined on the interval
[0,t_{h}]\subseteq[0,T]
by the Cauchy–Lipschitz
theorem; see
[
44
]
. We will prove several stability results, which will be used to ensure
that the semi-discrete solution
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
can be continued globally to
[0,T]

for any
T>0
when
d=1
or
d=2
with any
\boldsymbol{u}_{0,h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
, or when
d=3
with sufficiently small
\boldsymbol{u}_{0,h}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
h>0
and
T>0
be given. For all
t\in[0,T]
and initial data
\boldsymbol{u}_{0,h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
,

(3.6)

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}\mathrm{d}s\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(0)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2},

where the constant depends on
T
, but is independent of
h
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of
[
44
, Proposition 3.6]
and is omitted.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
h>0
and
T>0
be given. Then the following statements hold true.

(i)

If
d=1
or
d=2
, for all
t\in[0,T]
and initial data
\boldsymbol{u}_{0,h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}+\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(s)}{\partial s}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(0)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2},

where the constant depends on
T
, but is independent of
h
.

(ii)

If
d=3
, then (
3.7
) holds for all
t\in[0,T]
when
\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(0)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\lesssim T^{-2/3}
.

Proof.

Taking
\boldsymbol{\chi}=\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
in (
3.4
) gives

\displaystyle\left\|{\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{\beta_{1}}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\beta_{2}}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\beta_{3}}{4}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}+\beta_{4}\left\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\times\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle+\beta_{5}\left\langle\nabla(|\boldsymbol{u}_{h}|^{2}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}),\partial_{t}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}=\frac{\beta_{3}}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\beta_{6}\left\langle(\boldsymbol{j}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

Similar argument as in
[
44
, Proposition 3.6]
then yields

\displaystyle\left\|{\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|{|\boldsymbol{u}_{h}||\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}|}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

(3.8)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}.

It remains to bound the last two terms on the right-hand side of (
3
).

Case 1:
d=1
. The Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality gives

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}},

\displaystyle\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{3}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}.

Inserting these into (
3
), using the Sobolev embedding,
and integrating over
(0,t)
yield

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1+\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{4}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\Big)\mathrm{d}s

(3.9)

\displaystyle\lesssim 1+\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{3}+\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{4}\Big)\mathrm{d}s,

where in the last step we used (
2.2
) and (
2.9
). Noting that
\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{2})}\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(0)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}
by
Proposition
3.1
, we have the required result by invoking the Gronwall inequality.

Case 2:
d=2
. The Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality gives

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}},

\displaystyle\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}.

Inserting these into (
3
), using the Sobolev embedding,
and integrating over
(0,t)
yield

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1+\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{3}+\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{3}\Big)\mathrm{d}s

(3.10)

\displaystyle\lesssim 1+\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{3}+\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{4}\Big)\mathrm{d}s,

where in the last step we used (
2.2
) and (
2.9
). Invoking the Gronwall inequality (and noting Proposition
3.1
) yields the required result.

Case 3:
d=3
. The Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality gives

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{1/2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{3/2},

\displaystyle\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{1/2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{7/2}.

Inserting these into (
3
), using the Sobolev embedding,
and integrating over
(0,t)
yield

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

(3.11)

\displaystyle\lesssim 1+\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{5}\mathrm{d}s\lesssim 1+\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{5}\mathrm{d}s,

where in the last step we used (
2.2
). Hence, by the Bihari–Gronwall’s inequality (Theorem
8.1
), the result follows for
t\in[0,T]
if
\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(0)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\lesssim T^{-2/3}
.
This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

To estimate the error in the semi-discrete approximation, we write the error at any time
t\in[0,T]
as a sum of two terms:

(3.12)

\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)-\boldsymbol{u}(t)=\boldsymbol{\theta}(t)+\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)

where

(3.13)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}(t):=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}(t)-\boldsymbol{u}(t)\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\theta}(t):=\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}(t),

with
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}(t)
being the elliptic projection of the exact
solution
\boldsymbol{u}(t)
defined by

(3.14)

\displaystyle\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{u}(t);\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}(t)-\boldsymbol{u}(t),\boldsymbol{\chi})=0\quad\text{for all }\boldsymbol{\chi}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}.

Note that the elliptic projection of
\boldsymbol{u}(t)
exists by the Lax–Milgram
theorem. We can now prove some estimates for this projection.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}
be defined by (
3.14
),
\boldsymbol{u}
be the solution of (
2.39
) which
satisfies (
2.41
), and

\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}(t)-\boldsymbol{u}(t)
as defined in
(
3.13
). Then for any
t\in[0,T]
,

(3.15)

\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+h\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+h^{2}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\leq Ch^{r+1}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{r+1})},

and

(3.16)

\left\|{\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+h\left\|{\nabla\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+h^{2}\left\|{\Delta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\leq Ch^{r+1}\left\|{\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{r+1})}.

The constant
C
depends on
\kappa,\mu
, and
T
, where
\kappa
and
\mu
were defined in (
2.35
) and (
2.36
), respectively.

Proof.

For all
\boldsymbol{\chi}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
, by the coercivity and the boundedness of
\cal{A}
, and (
3.14
),

\displaystyle\mu\left\|{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}(t)-\boldsymbol{u}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{u}(t);\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}(t)-\boldsymbol{u}(t),\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}(t)-\boldsymbol{u}(t))

\displaystyle=\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{u}(t);\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}(t)-\boldsymbol{u}(t),\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}(t)-\boldsymbol{\chi})-\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{u}(t);\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}(t)-\boldsymbol{u}(t),\boldsymbol{u}(t)-\boldsymbol{\chi})

\displaystyle\leq\kappa\left\|{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}(t)-\boldsymbol{u}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(t)-\boldsymbol{\chi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}.

Therefore, by (
3.1
),

(3.17)

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}=\left\|{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}(t)-\boldsymbol{u}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\leq\inf_{\boldsymbol{\chi}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(t)-\boldsymbol{\chi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\leq Ch^{r-1}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{r+1}}.

To prove the
\mathbb{L}^{2}
-estimate, we use the Aubin–Nitsche duality argument.
For each
\boldsymbol{u}(t)\in\mathbb{H}^{4}\cap\mathbb{H}_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{2}
, let
\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)\in\mathbb{H}^{4}\cap\mathbb{H}_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{2}
satisfy

(3.18)

\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{u}(t);\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\psi}(t))=\left\langle\boldsymbol{\rho}(t),\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\quad\text{for all }\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}.

For any
t\in[0,T]
, such
\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)
exists by Lemma
7.1
under the assumed conditions on
\Omega
. Moreover, by (
7.2
),

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{4}}\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}.

Therefore, taking
\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)
in (
3.18
) and noting (
3.14
),
we have for all
\boldsymbol{\chi}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
,

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{u}(t);\boldsymbol{\rho}(t),\boldsymbol{\psi}(t))=\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{u}(t);\boldsymbol{\rho}(t),\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)-\boldsymbol{\chi})

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\inf_{\boldsymbol{\chi}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)-\boldsymbol{\chi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\leq Ch^{r+1}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{r+1}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}},

where in the last step we used (
3.17
) and (
3.1
). This implies

\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\leq Ch^{r+1}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{r+1}}\leq Ch^{r+1}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{r+1})}.

The estimate (
3.15
) then follows by using (
2.9
).

We now prove (
3.16
). For ease of presentation, the dependence
of the functions on
t
will sometimes be omitted.
Note that by the coercivity of
\cal{A}
and the definition of
\boldsymbol{\rho}
,

(3.19)

\displaystyle\mu\left\|{\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}\leq\cal{A}\big(\boldsymbol{u};\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho},\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho}\big)=\cal{A}\big(\boldsymbol{u};\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho},\cal{I}_{h}(\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho})\big)+\cal{A}\big(\boldsymbol{u};\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho},\cal{I}_{h}(\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u})-\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}\big),

where we used
\cal{I}_{h}(\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho})=\partial_{t}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}-\cal{I}_{h}(\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u})
.
We now estimate each term on the last line.
To this end, noting the time derivative of (
3.14
) and (
2.34
), we
differentiate equation (
3.14
) with respect to
t
to have,
for all
\boldsymbol{\chi}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
,

(3.20)

\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{u};\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\chi})+2\cal{B}(\boldsymbol{u},\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\chi})+\cal{C}(\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\chi})=0.

Thus, for the first term on the right-hand side of (
3.19
), by the boundedness of
\cal{B}
,
\cal{C}
, and
\mathcal{I}_{h}
we have

\displaystyle\big|\cal{A}\big(\boldsymbol{u};\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho},\cal{I}_{h}(\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho})\big)\big|

\displaystyle\lesssim\big|\cal{B}\big(\boldsymbol{u};\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{\rho},\cal{I}_{h}(\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho})\big)\big|+\big|\cal{C}\big(\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{\rho},\cal{I}_{h}(\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho})\big)\big|

(3.21)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\left\|{\cal{I}_{h}(\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho})}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\lesssim h^{r-1}\left\|{\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{r+1}},

where in the last step we used (
3.15
). For the second
term on the right-hand side of (
3.19
), by the boundedness
of
\cal{A}
(see (
2.35
)), the assumption on the exact
solution
\boldsymbol{u}
(see (
2.41
)), and (
3.2
), we
have

\displaystyle\big|\cal{A}\big(\boldsymbol{u};\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho},\cal{I}_{h}(\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u})-\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}\big)\big|

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|{\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\left\|{\cal{I}_{h}(\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u})-\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\lesssim h^{r-1}\left\|{\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\left\|{\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{4}}.

This, together with (
3.19
) and (
3
), gives

(3.22)

\displaystyle\left\|{\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\leq Ch^{r-1}\left\|{\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{r+1})},

where
C=C(\kappa,\mu,T)
.

For the estimate of
\left\|{\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}
, we use duality
argument as before. For each
\boldsymbol{u}(t)\in\mathbb{H}^{4}\cap\mathbb{H}_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{2}
, let
\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)\in\mathbb{H}^{4}\cap\mathbb{H}_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{2}
satisfy

(3.23)

\displaystyle\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{u}(t);\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\psi}(t))=\left\langle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho}(t),\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\quad\text{for all }\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}.

The existence of
\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)
is conferred by Lemma
7.1
. Moreover,
by (
7.2
),

(3.24)

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{4}}\lesssim\left\|{\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}},

where the constant is independent of
t
but depends on
T
.
Taking
\boldsymbol{\xi}=\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho}
in (
3.23
), we have

(3.25)

\displaystyle\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{u}(t);\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\psi}(t))=\left\|{\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

Consequently, equations (
3.25
) and (
3.20
) yield

\displaystyle\left\|{\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{u};\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\psi}-\boldsymbol{\chi})-2\cal{B}(\boldsymbol{u},\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\chi})-\cal{C}(\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\chi})

), and of

The corresponding estimate for

4.
Time Discretisation by the Linearised Euler Method

We shall now consider a discretisation in the time variable. In the sequel,
let
k
be the time step and
\boldsymbol{U}^{n}
be the approximation in
\mathbb{V}_{h}
of

\boldsymbol{u}(t)
at time
t=t_{n}:=nk
,
n=0,1,2,\ldots
. We denote
\boldsymbol{u}^{n}:=\boldsymbol{u}(t_{n})
and define, for any discrete vector-valued function
\boldsymbol{v}^{n}
,

\delta\boldsymbol{v}^{n}:=\frac{\boldsymbol{v}^{n}-\boldsymbol{v}^{n-1}}{k},\quad n=1,2,3,\ldots.

Note that under the
assumption (
2.41
), we have

(4.1)

\left\|{\delta\boldsymbol{u}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}}=\left\|{\frac{1}{k}\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}}\leq C,\quad 1\leq p\leq\infty,\ n=1,2,3,\ldots.

We will describe a time discretisation scheme using a semi-implicit (linearised) Euler method as follows. We start with
\boldsymbol{U}^{0}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}(0)\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
where
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}(0)
is
defined by (
3.14
). For
t_{n}\in[0,T]
, given
\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
, define
\boldsymbol{U}^{n}
by (recalling the definition of
\cal{A(\cdot\,;\cdot\,,\cdot)}
and
\alpha

in (
2.34
))

(4.2)

\displaystyle\left\langle\delta\boldsymbol{U}^{n},\boldsymbol{\chi}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1};\boldsymbol{U}^{n},\boldsymbol{\chi})-(\alpha+\beta_{3})\left\langle\boldsymbol{U}^{n},\boldsymbol{\chi}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}-\beta_{6}\left\langle(\boldsymbol{j}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{U}^{n},\boldsymbol{\chi}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}=0,\quad\boldsymbol{\chi}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}.

Note that this fully discrete scheme is linear at each time step.

Before analysing the above scheme, we first clarify the notion of stability that is used subsequently.
Since equation (
1.1
) is not a gradient flow, we will adopt the definition of stability for a fully discrete scheme given in
[
42
]
.

Definition 4.1
.

A discrete time-stepping scheme is
unconditionally stable
in the
\mathbb{H}^{\beta}
norm if for any
k\leq\nu
, where
\nu
depends only on the coefficients of the equation,

(4.3)

\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{\beta}}\leq C,\quad\forall n\in\{1,2,\ldots,\lfloor T/k\rfloor\}.

The constant
C
is independent of
n
and
k
.

The method is
conditionally stable
if (
4.3
) holds under a restriction on
k
in terms of
h
.

First, we show that the above scheme is well-defined. For the stability estimate, we need to
introduce the following constant which is defined from the coefficients in (
1.1
):

(4.4)

\lambda:=\begin{cases}2\beta_{1}/\left(\beta_{6}^{2}+2\beta_{1}\beta_{3}\right)&\quad\text{if $\beta_{1}\geq 0$},\\
4\beta_{2}/\left(\beta_{1}^{2}+\beta_{6}^{2}+4\beta_{2}\beta_{3}\right)&\quad\text{if
$\beta_{1}<0$}.\end{cases}

Proposition 4.2
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, given
\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
and

k<\lambda
,
there exists a unique
\boldsymbol{U}^{n}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
that solves the fully
discrete scheme (
4.2
).

Proof.

For each
\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
, define a bilinear form
\cal{S}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot):\mathbb{V}_{h}\times\mathbb{V}_{h}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle\cal{S}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}):=(1-k(\alpha+\beta_{3}))\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}-k\beta_{6}\left\langle(\boldsymbol{j}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+k\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}).

Note that this bilinear form is bounded. Moreover, it follows from
Lemma
2.4
that there exists
\alpha>0

such that
\cal{A}
is coercive and
k<1/(\alpha+\beta_{3}+\beta_{6})
, if
k<\lambda
.
Hence,
\cal{S}
is
\mathbb{V}_{h}
-coercive.
Now, equation (
4.2
) is equivalent to

\cal{S}(\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1};\boldsymbol{U}^{n},\boldsymbol{\chi})=\left\langle\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1},\boldsymbol{\chi}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\quad\text{for all }\boldsymbol{\chi}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}.

Therefore, given
\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
, the proposition follows from the
Lax–Milgram lemma.
∎

Next, we show that the scheme (
4.2
) is unconditionally
stable in
\mathbb{L}^{2}
for
k
satisfying

(4.5)

k<\lambda/2.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
T>0
be given and let
\boldsymbol{U}^{n}
be defined by (
4.2
) with initial data
\boldsymbol{U}^{0}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
.
Then for
k
satisfying (
4.5
) and
n\in\{1,2,\ldots,\lfloor T/k\rfloor\}
,

(4.6)

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\sum_{m=1}^{n}\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{m}-\boldsymbol{U}^{m-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+k\sum_{m=1}^{n}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{U}^{m}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2},

where the constant depends on
T
, but is independent of
n
and
k
.

Proof.

Taking
\boldsymbol{\chi}=\boldsymbol{U}^{n}
in (
4.2
), and using the
identity

(4.7)

2\boldsymbol{a}\cdot(\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{b})=|\boldsymbol{a}|^{2}-|\boldsymbol{b}|^{2}+|\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{b}|^{2},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

we have (after multiplying by
k
)

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}-\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+2\beta_{2}k\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+2\beta_{3}k\left\|{|\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}||\boldsymbol{U}^{n}|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+4\beta_{5}k\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+2\beta_{5}k\left\|{|\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}||\nabla\boldsymbol{U}^{n}|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=-2\beta_{1}k\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+2\beta_{3}k\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+2\beta_{6}k\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}.

If
\beta_{1}\geq 0
, then we have

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}-\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\beta_{1}k\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+2\beta_{2}k\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq\left(\frac{\beta_{6}^{2}}{\beta_{1}}+2\beta_{3}\right)k\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

Otherwise, if

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}-\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+2\beta_{2}k\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq k(\beta_{1}^{2}/2\beta_{2})\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\beta_{2}k\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+2\beta_{3}k\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+k(\beta_{6}^{2}/2\beta_{2})\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\beta_{2}k\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\beta_{1}^{2}+\beta_{6}^{2}}{2\beta_{2}}+2\beta_{3}\right)k\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\beta_{2}k\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

In any case, this implies (after changing the index
n
to

and rearranging the equation)

5.
Time Discretisation by the Linearised BDF2 Method

To obtain higher order accuracy in time, we shall consider the BDF2 (second order backward differentiation formula) time discretisation scheme. First, for any discrete vector-valued function
\boldsymbol{v}^{n}
, define

\cal{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}:=\delta\boldsymbol{v}^{n}+\frac{k}{2}\delta^{2}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}=\delta\boldsymbol{v}^{n}+\frac{1}{2}(\delta\boldsymbol{v}^{n}-\delta\boldsymbol{v}^{n-1})=\frac{3\boldsymbol{v}^{n}-4\boldsymbol{v}^{n-1}+\boldsymbol{v}^{n-2}}{2k},\quad n=2,3,\ldots,

where
\delta^{2}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}=\delta(\delta\boldsymbol{v}^{n})
.
Under the assumption (
2.42
), by using (
4.1
), we have for all

p\in[1,\infty]
and
n=1,2,\ldots
,

(5.1)

\displaystyle\left\|{\cal{D}\boldsymbol{u}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}}=\left\|{\delta\boldsymbol{u}^{n}+\frac{1}{2}(\delta\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\delta\boldsymbol{u}^{n-1})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}}\leq\frac{3}{2}\left\|{\delta\boldsymbol{u}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\delta\boldsymbol{u}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}}\leq C.

We start with
\boldsymbol{U}^{0}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}(0)\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
. Since the BDF2
method we are going to introduce, see
(
5.4
)
below, is a two-step method, we need to define
\boldsymbol{U}^{1}
by employing one time step of the Crank–Nicolson method to maintain a second-order accuracy in time (or by one time step of the semi-implicit Euler method in (
4.2
) with very small step-size). More precisely,

(5.2)

\left\langle\delta\boldsymbol{U}^{1},\boldsymbol{\chi}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\cal{A}\left(\overline{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}};\overline{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}},\boldsymbol{\chi}\right)-(\alpha+\beta_{3})\left\langle\overline{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}},\boldsymbol{\chi}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}-\beta_{6}\left\langle(\boldsymbol{j}\cdot\nabla)\overline{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}},\boldsymbol{\chi}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}=0,\quad\boldsymbol{\chi}\in\mathbb{V}_{h},

where
\overline{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}:=(\boldsymbol{U}^{0}+\boldsymbol{U}^{1})/2
. It is straightforward to verify (using a method similar to the one in the previous section) that

(5.3)

\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{\beta}}\leq C(h^{r+1-\beta}+k^{2}),\quad\beta=0,1,2.

Next, for
n\geq 2
be such that
t_{n}\in[0,T]
, we
compute
\boldsymbol{U}^{n}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
from
\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}
and
\boldsymbol{U}^{n-2}
by

(5.4)

\displaystyle\left\langle\cal{D}\boldsymbol{U}^{n},\boldsymbol{\chi}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1};\boldsymbol{U}^{n},\boldsymbol{\chi})-(\alpha+\beta_{3})\left\langle\boldsymbol{U}^{n},\boldsymbol{\chi}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}-\beta_{6}\left\langle(\boldsymbol{j}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{U}^{n},\boldsymbol{\chi}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}=0,\quad\boldsymbol{\chi}\in\mathbb{V}_{h},

which is a linear scheme.
We now show that the scheme (
5.4
) is well-defined.

Proposition 5.1
.

For
n\geq 2
, given
\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}
and
\boldsymbol{U}^{n-2}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
and
k<3\lambda/2
(where
\lambda
was defined in (
4.4
)),
there exists a unique
\boldsymbol{U}^{n}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
that solves the fully
discrete scheme (
5.4
).

Proof.

For each
\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
, define a bilinear form
\cal{F}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{V}_{h}\times\mathbb{V}_{h}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\cal{F}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}):=(3-2k(\alpha+\beta_{3}))\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}-2k\beta_{6}\left\langle(\boldsymbol{j}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+2k\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}).

Equation (
5.4
) is then equivalent to

\cal{F}(\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1};\boldsymbol{U}^{n},\boldsymbol{\chi})=\left\langle 4\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}-\boldsymbol{U}^{n-2},\boldsymbol{\chi}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}.

Therefore, the result follows by the same argument as in Proposition
4.2
.
∎

Next, we show that the scheme (
5.4
) is unconditionally stable in
\mathbb{L}^{2}
for
k
satisfying (
4.5
). An important identity that will often be used is

(5.5)

2\boldsymbol{a}\cdot(3\boldsymbol{a}-4\boldsymbol{b}+\boldsymbol{c})=|\boldsymbol{a}|^{2}-|\boldsymbol{b}|^{2}+|2\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{b}|^{2}-|2\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{c}|^{2}+|\boldsymbol{a}-2\boldsymbol{b}+\boldsymbol{c}|^{2},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
T>0
be given and let
\boldsymbol{U}^{n}
be defined by (
5.4
) with initial data
\boldsymbol{U}^{0}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
.
Then for
k
satisfying (
4.5
) and
n\in\{1,2,\ldots,\lfloor T/k\rfloor\}
,

(5.6)

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\sum_{m=1}^{n}\left\|{2\boldsymbol{U}^{m}-\boldsymbol{U}^{m-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+k\sum_{m=1}^{n}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{U}^{m}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2},

where the constant depends on
T
, but is independent of
n
and
k
.

Proof.

First, taking
\boldsymbol{\chi}=\overline{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}
in (
5.2
) and applying the same argument as in Proposition
4.3
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+2\beta_{2}k\left\|{\Delta\overline{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+2\beta_{3}k\left\|{\overline{\boldsymbol{U}}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}+2\beta_{5}k\left\|{\left|{\overline{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}}\right|\left|{\nabla\overline{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}}\right|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+4\beta_{5}k\left\|{\overline{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}\cdot\nabla\overline{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2k}{\lambda}\left\|{\overline{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\beta_{2}k\left\|{\Delta\overline{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2},

where
\lambda
was defined in (
4.4
). Therefore, for
k
satisfying (
4.5
),

\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq C\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

Next, taking
\boldsymbol{\chi}=\boldsymbol{U}^{n}
in (
5.4
) and using (
5.5
),
we have (after multiplying by
k
)

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{2\boldsymbol{U}^{n}-\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-\left\|{2\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}-\boldsymbol{U}^{n-2}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}-2\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}+\boldsymbol{U}^{n-2}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\;+2\beta_{2}k\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+2\beta_{3}k\left\|{|\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}||\boldsymbol{U}^{n}|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+4\beta_{5}k\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+2\beta_{5}k\left\|{|\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}||\nabla\boldsymbol{U}^{n}|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=-2\beta_{1}k\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+2\beta_{3}k\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+2\beta_{6}k\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}.

Similar argument as in the proof of Proposition
4.3
then yields the result.
∎

Next, we show that the method is also stable in the
\mathbb{H}^{2}
norm (in the sense of Definition
4.1
). Analogously to (
4.10
) and (
4.11
), we have by (
5.1
) and (
4.10
),

(5.7)

\displaystyle\left\|{\cal{D}\boldsymbol{\rho}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\leq\frac{3}{2}\left\|{\delta\boldsymbol{\rho}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\delta\boldsymbol{\rho}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\leq Ch^{r+1},

and that by Taylor’s theorem

(5.8)

\displaystyle\left\|{\cal{D}\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\leq Ck\int_{t_{n-2}}^{t_{n}}\left\|{\partial_{t}^{3}\boldsymbol{u}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq Ck^{2},

where
C
depends on
\kappa,\mu,T
, and
K
, with
K
as defined in (
2.42
).

By using Lemma
4.4
, we have the following result, which is analogous to Proposition
4.5
.

Proposition 5.3
.

Assume that
\boldsymbol{u}

satisfies (
2.42
). Then for
h,k>0
with
k
satisfying (
4.5
), and for any
n\in\{1,2,\ldots,\lfloor T/k\rfloor\}
,

(5.9)

\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\leq C(h^{r+1}+k^{2}).

Proof.

Similar calculations as in (
4
) (replacing
\delta
there by
\cal{D}
) shows that for all

\boldsymbol{\chi}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
,

\displaystyle\left\langle\cal{D}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n},\boldsymbol{\chi}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n},\boldsymbol{\chi})

\displaystyle=(\alpha+\beta_{3})\left\langle\boldsymbol{U}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}^{n},\boldsymbol{\chi}\right\rangle-\beta_{6}\left\langle(\boldsymbol{U}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}^{n})\otimes\boldsymbol{j},\nabla\boldsymbol{\chi}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}-\left\langle\cal{D}\boldsymbol{\rho}^{n},\boldsymbol{\chi}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

(5.10)

\displaystyle\quad-\left\langle\cal{D}\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}^{n},\boldsymbol{\chi}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}-\Big(\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1};\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{\chi})-\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n};\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{\chi})\Big).

Now, take
\boldsymbol{\chi}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n}
. Using the identity (
5.5
) and applying similar argument as in the proof of Proposition
4.5
yield

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2k}\left(\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2k}\left(\left\|{2\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-\left\|{2\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n-1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n-2}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2k}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n}-2\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n-1}+\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n-2}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\mu\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\big|(\alpha+\beta_{3})\left\langle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n}+\boldsymbol{\rho}^{n},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\big|+\big|\beta_{6}\left\langle(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n}+\boldsymbol{\rho}^{n})\otimes\boldsymbol{j},\nabla\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\big|+\big|\left\langle\cal{D}\boldsymbol{\rho}^{n},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\big|

\displaystyle\quad+\big|\left\langle\cal{D}\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}^{n},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\big|+\big|\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1};\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n})-\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n};\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n})\big|

\displaystyle\leq C\big(1+\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}\big)h^{2(r+1)}+Ck^{2}+C\left\|{2\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+C\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+C\epsilon\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}+C\epsilon\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}

for any
\epsilon>0
, where in the last step we used (
5.7
) and (
5.8
). Choosing
\epsilon=\mu/2C
, then multiplying by
k
and summing the inequalities over
m\in\{2,3,\ldots,n\}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{2\boldsymbol{\theta}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+Ch^{2(r+1)}\left(1+k\sum_{m=2}^{n}\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{m-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}\right)+Ck^{4}+\sum_{m=2}^{n}Ck\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{m-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

Since
\boldsymbol{U}^{0}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}(0)
and (
5.3
) hold, by the discrete Gronwall inequality (noting the stability of
\boldsymbol{U}^{n}
in Proposition
5.2
) we have

(5.11)

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq C(h^{2(r+1)}+k^{4}),

where
C
depends on
\kappa,\mu,T
, and
K
(with
K
as defined in (
2.42
)). Inequality (
4.23
) then follows.
∎

Next, we discuss the stability of the semi-implicit BDF2 scheme in the
\mathbb{H}^{2}
norm, first in the case where the triangulation is quasi-uniform.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
T>0
be given and
\boldsymbol{U}^{n}
be defined by (
5.4
). Assume that the triangulation
\cal{T}_{h}
is quasi-uniform. If
k=O(h)
, then for any initial data
\boldsymbol{U}^{0}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
and
n\in\{1,2,\ldots,\lfloor T/k\rfloor\}
,

(5.12)

\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\leq C\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}},

where
C
depends on
T
, but is independent of
n
and
k
.

Proof.

The identity
\boldsymbol{U}^{n}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n}+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{n+1}
, (
5.9
), (
3.26
), and the inverse estimates give

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}+\left\|{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{n+1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\leq Ch^{-2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+C\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\lesssim(1+k^{2}h^{-2})\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}

if we assume
k=O(h)
, as required.
∎

In the following, we show that without assuming quasi-uniformity of
\cal{T}_{h}
, the energy
and the
\mathbb{H}^{2}
norm of the discrete solution
\boldsymbol{U}^{n}
remain bounded, analogously to Proposition
4.7
. To do this, we need the identity

(5.13)

\displaystyle 4\cal{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{v}^{n}=4\left|{\delta\boldsymbol{v}^{n}}\right|^{2}+\left(\left|{\delta\boldsymbol{v}^{n}}\right|^{2}-\left|{\delta\boldsymbol{v}^{n-1}}\right|^{2}\right)+\left|{\delta\boldsymbol{v}^{n}-\delta\boldsymbol{v}^{n-1}}\right|^{2}.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
T>0
be given and
\boldsymbol{U}^{n}
be defined by
(
5.4
) with initial data
\boldsymbol{U}^{0}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
.

(i)

If
d\leq 2
, then for sufficiently small
k

and
n\in\{1,2,\ldots,\lfloor T/k\rfloor\}
,

(5.14)

\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}\leq B\exp\left(C\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\right),

where
C
depends on

T
, while
B
depends on
\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
and
\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
, but both
C
and
B
are independent of
n
and
k
.

(ii)

If
d=3
, then

(5.15)

\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}\leq C\left(C_{0}\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{-3}-t_{M}\right)^{-\frac{2}{3}},

for
n\in\{1,2,\ldots,M\}
, where

(5.16)

\displaystyle M:=\max\big\{n:\;n<\lfloor T/k\rfloor,\;nk\leq C_{0}\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{-3},\;k\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{m}-\boldsymbol{U}^{m-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{3}\leq C_{0},\;\forall m\leq n\big\}.

Here
C_{0}
depends on
C_{0}^{\prime}
, the constant given by the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality;
see (
4.30
). The constant
C
depends on
t_{M}:=Mk\leq T
.

Proof.

First, we aim to show that

(5.17)

k\left\|{\delta\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq B,

for a constant
B
depending on
\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
and
\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
.
To this end, we take
\boldsymbol{\chi}=\delta\boldsymbol{U}^{1}
in (
5.2
), then apply (
2.30
) and Young’s inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\left\|{\delta\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\beta_{1}}{2k}\big(\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{U}^{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\big)+\frac{\beta_{2}}{2k}\big(\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{U}^{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{3}\left|{\left\langle\overline{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}},\delta\boldsymbol{U}^{1}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}}\right|+\beta_{6}\left|{\left\langle(\boldsymbol{j}\cdot\nabla)\overline{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}},\delta\boldsymbol{U}^{1}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}}\right|+\left|{\cal{B}(\overline{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}},\overline{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}};\overline{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}},\delta\boldsymbol{U}^{1})}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|{\overline{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}+\epsilon\left\|{\delta\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left(1+\left\|{\overline{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\right)\left\|{\overline{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}\left\|{\delta\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1+\left\|{\overline{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{6}+\epsilon\left\|{\delta\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

for any
\epsilon>0
. Choosing
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small, then rearranging and multiplying by

give

k\left\|{\delta\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}+k\left(1+\left\|{\overline{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{6}\right)\leq B,

where
B
is a constant depending on
\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
and
\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
, thus showing (
5.17
).

Next, by taking
\boldsymbol{\chi}=\delta\boldsymbol{U}^{n}
in (
5.4
)
and using (
2.34
), we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\cal{D}\boldsymbol{U}^{n},\delta\boldsymbol{U}^{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\beta_{1}\left\langle\nabla\boldsymbol{U}^{n},\nabla\delta\boldsymbol{U}^{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\beta_{2}\left\langle\Delta\boldsymbol{U}^{n},\Delta\delta\boldsymbol{U}^{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\beta_{3}\left\langle\boldsymbol{U}^{n},\delta\boldsymbol{U}^{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\beta_{6}\left\langle(\boldsymbol{j}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{U}^{n},\delta\boldsymbol{U}^{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}-\cal{B}(\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1},\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1};\boldsymbol{U}^{n},\delta\boldsymbol{U}^{n})-\cal{C}(\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1};\boldsymbol{U}^{n},\delta\boldsymbol{U}^{n}).

1

\displaystyle\left\|{\delta\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{U}}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left(\left\|{\delta\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-\left\|{\delta\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{4}\left\|{\delta\boldsymbol{U}^{n}-\delta\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2k}\left(\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{U}^{n}-\nabla\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}+\epsilon\left\|{\delta\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\big(1+\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\big)\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,4}}^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,4}}^{2}+\big(1+\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{4}\big)\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}.

For the case

and
2
, summing over

and multiplying by

(noting (

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}

6.
Numerical Experiments

We perform numerical simulations for the semi-implicit Euler scheme using the open-source package
FreeFEM

[
30
]
to verify its order of convergence experimentally. These numerical experiments were done using the Hsieh–Clough–Tocher (HCT) element in the domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
t\in[0,T]
.
Since the exact solution of the equation is not known, we use extrapolation to empirically verify the order of convergence. Let
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
be the finite element solution with spatial step size
h
and a very small fixed time step size. For
s=0,1,2,
and each
h>0
, define the extrapolated spatial order of convergence in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{s})
-norm:

\displaystyle\text{rate}_{\mathbb{H}^{s}}:=\log_{2}\left[\frac{\left\|{\boldsymbol{e}_{2h}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{s})}}{\left\|{\boldsymbol{e}_{h}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{s})}}\right],\text{ where }\left\|{\boldsymbol{e}_{h}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{s})}:=\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h/2}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{s})}.

The order of convergence in the
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{L}^{2})
-norm,
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{1})
and
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
-norms are found to be
4,3
, and
2
, respectively, confirming the theoretical results in Theorem

7.
A Regularity Lemma

The following lemma was used in the proof of error estimate for the semi-discrete scheme in Section
3
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a domain such that the elliptic regularity properties (
2.2
), (
2.3
), (
2.4
) hold, and let
T>0
. Assume that the exact solution
\boldsymbol{u}
of (
1.1
)
belongs to
L^{\infty}(0,T;{H}_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{2})
. For any

\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in{L}^{2}
and for each
t\in[0,T]
, there exists

\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)\in{H}^{4}\cap{H}_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{2}
such that

(7.1)

\displaystyle\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{u}(t);\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\psi}(t))=\left\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rangle_{{L}^{2}},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\xi}\in{H}^{2}.

Moreover,

(7.2)

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)}\right\|_{{H}^{4}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\right\|_{{L}^{2}},

where the constant depends on
T
and
\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}({H}^{2})}
(but is independent of
t
).

Proof.

We use the Faedo–Galerkin method.
Let
\{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
denote an orthonormal basis of
{L}^{2}

consisting of eigenfunctions for
-\Delta
such that

\displaystyle-\Delta\boldsymbol{e}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\ \text{ in $\Omega$}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{e}_{i}}{\partial\boldsymbol{n}}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \text{ on }\partial\Omega,

where
\lambda_{i}>0
are the eigenvalues of
-\Delta
, associated with

\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
. By elliptic regularity, for
j=2,3
,

\displaystyle\Delta^{j}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}=\lambda_{i}^{j}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\ \text{ in $\Omega$}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial(\Delta^{j-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{i})}{\partial\boldsymbol{n}}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \text{ on }\partial\Omega.

Let
{S}_{n}:=\text{span}\{\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{e}_{n}\}
and
\Pi_{n}:{L}^{2}\to{S}_{n}
be the orthogonal projection defined by

\displaystyle\left\langle\Pi_{n}\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\phi}\right\rangle_{{L}^{2}}=\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\phi}\right\rangle_{{L}^{2}}\quad\text{for all }\boldsymbol{\phi}\in{S}_{n}\text{ and all }\boldsymbol{v}\in{L}^{2}.

Note that
\Pi_{n}
is self-adjoint and satisfies

(7.3)

\left\|{\Pi_{n}\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{{L}^{2}}\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{{L}^{2}}\quad\text{for all }\boldsymbol{v}\in{L}^{2}.

Given
\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in{L}^{2}
, for each
t\in[0,T]
,
the Faedo–Galerkin method seeks to approximate the solution to (
7.1
) by
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}(t)\in{S}_{n}
satisfying the equation

(7.4)

\displaystyle\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{u}(t);\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}(t),\boldsymbol{\xi})=\left\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rangle_{{L}^{2}}\;\text{ for all }\boldsymbol{\xi}\in{S}_{n}.

The existence of such
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}(t)
follows by a standard argument and the
Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem. We will need the following bound on

\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}(t)
, the proof of which is deferred till the end of this proof:

(7.5)

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}(t)}\right\|_{{H}^{4}}\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\right\|_{{L}^{2}}.

It follows from (
7.5
) and (
7.6
), and the Banach–Alaoglu theorem that for each
t\in[0,T]
, there is a subsequence, which is still denoted by
\{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}(t)\}
, such that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}(t)

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)\quad\text{weakly in }{H}^{4}.

Since
{H}^{4}
is compactly embedded into
{H}^{3}
, a further subsequence then satisfies

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}(t)\to\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)\quad\text{strongly in }{H}^{3}.

Note that Sobolev embedding gives
{H}^{3}(\Omega)\subset\boldsymbol{C}^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
, and thus in particular

\partial\boldsymbol{\psi}/\partial\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0}
on
\partial\Omega
. Standard
argument then shows
\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)
satisfies the equation (
7.1
), while (
7.2
) follows from (
7.5
).

We now prove (
7.5
).
Firstly, by taking
\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}
in (
7.4
), and using (
2.36
), we have

\displaystyle\mu\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{{H}^{2}}^{2}\leq\cal{A}(\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n})=\left\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}\right\rangle_{{L}^{2}}\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\right\|_{{L}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{{L}^{2}},

which implies

(7.6)

\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{{H}^{2}}\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\right\|_{{L}^{2}}.

Next, by taking
\boldsymbol{\xi}=\Delta^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}
in (
7.4
) and integrating by parts the terms involving
\alpha,\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
, we have (after rearranging the terms)

\displaystyle\alpha\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{{L}^{2}}^{2}+\beta_{2}\left\|{\Delta^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}\left\langle\Delta\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n},\Delta^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}\right\rangle_{{L}^{2}}-\cal{B}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n},\Delta^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n})-\cal{C}(\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n},\Delta^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n})+\left\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi},\Delta^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}\right\rangle_{{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq|\beta_{1}|\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{{L}^{2}}\left\|{\Delta^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{{L}^{2}}+\big|\cal{B}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n},\Delta^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n})\big|+\big|\cal{C}(\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n},\Delta^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n})\big|+\left\|{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\right\|_{{L}^{2}}\left\|{\Delta^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{{L}^{2}}

(7.7)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{{L}^{2}}\left\|{\Delta^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{{L}^{2}}+\left(1+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{{H}^{2}}^{2}\right)\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi_{n}}}\right\|_{{H}^{2}}\left\|{\Delta^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{{L}^{2}}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\right\|_{{L}^{2}}\left\|{\Delta^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{{L}^{2}}.

We deduce from (
7
) that

\displaystyle\left\|{\Delta^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}(t)}\right\|_{{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}(t)}\right\|_{{H}^{2}}\left\|{\Delta^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}(t)}\right\|_{{L}^{2}}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\right\|_{{L}^{2}}\left\|{\Delta^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}(t)}\right\|_{{L}^{2}},

which implies

\left\|{\Delta^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}(t)}\right\|_{{L}^{2}}\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\right\|_{{L}^{2}}.

With assumption (
2.4
), this and (
7.6
)
imply the required estimate, completing the proof of the lemma.
∎

We collect here a few results which are used in this paper.

Theorem 8.1

(Bihari–Gronwall’s inequality
[
8
,
10
]
)
.

Let
f
be a non-decreasing continuous function which is non-negative on
[0,\infty)
such that
\int_{1}^{\infty}1/f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty
. Let
F
be the anti-derivative of
-1/f
which vanishes at
\infty
. Let
y
be a non-negative continuous function and let
g
be a locally integrable non-negative function
[0,\infty)
. Suppose that there exists a
y_{0}>0
such that for all
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle y(t)\leq y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}f(y(s))\,\mathrm{d}s.

Then for any
T<T^{*}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\leq T}y(t)\leq F^{-1}\left(F\left(y_{0}+\int_{0}^{T}g(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\right)-T\right),

where
T^{*}
is the unique solution of the equation

\displaystyle T^{*}=F\left(y_{0}+\int_{0}^{T^{*}}g(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\right).

Theorem 8.2

(Discrete form of Bihari–Gronwall’s inequality
[
41
]
)
.

Let
\varphi
be a strictly increasing non-negative function on
{R}_{+}
with
\varphi(+\infty)=+\infty
and
\psi
be non-decreasing non-negative function on
{R}_{+}
. Let
c\geq 0
be a real constant, and let
v_{n},f_{n}
be non-negative sequences. If the inequality

\varphi(v_{n})\leq c+\sum_{s=0}^{n-1}f_{s}\,\psi(v_{s})

holds, then for all
n\leq M
,

v_{n}\leq\varphi^{-1}\left[G^{-1}\left(G(c)+\sum_{s=0}^{n-1}f_{s}\right)\right],

where
G^{-1}
and
\varphi^{-1}
are the inverse functions of
G
and
\varphi
, respectively, and

G(z):=\int_{z_{0}}^{z}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{\psi(\varphi^{-1}(s))},\quad z\geq z_{0}>0.

Here,
M
is chosen so that if
n\leq M
, then

G(c)+\sum_{s=0}^{n-1}f_{s}\in\text{dom}(G^{-1}).
