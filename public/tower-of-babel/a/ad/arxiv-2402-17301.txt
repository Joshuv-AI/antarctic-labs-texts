A Computational Tsirelson’s Theorem for the Value of Compiled XOR Games
David Cui, Giulio Malavolta, Arthur Mehta, Anand Natarajan, Connor Paddock, Simon Schmidt, Michael Walter, Tina Zhang

Abstract
Nonlocal games are a foundational tool for understanding entanglement and constructing quantum protocols in settings with multiple spatially separated quantum devices. In this work, we continue the study initiated by Kalai et al. (STOC ’23) of compiled nonlocal games, played between a classical verifier and a single cryptographically limited quantum device. Our main result is that the compiler proposed by Kalai et al. is sound for any two-player XOR game. A celebrated theorem of Tsirelson shows that for XOR games, the quantum value is exactly given by a semidefinite program, and we obtain our result by showing that the SDP upper bound holds for the compiled game up to a negligible error arising from the compilation. This answers a question raised by Natarajan and Zhang (FOCS ’23), who showed soundness for the specific case of the CHSH game. Using our techniques, we obtain several additional results, including (1) tight bounds on the compiled value of parallel-repeated XOR games, (2) operator self-testing statements for any compiled XOR game, and (3) a “nice” sum-of-squares certificate for any XOR game, from which operator rigidity is manifest.



1
Introduction

In a nonlocal game, a polynomially-bounded classical verifier interacts with multiple non-communicating provers. A set of predetermined rules governs whether the provers win or lose the game. The
classical value
of a nonlocal game is the highest winning probability that any classical (or unentangled) provers can achieve, whereas the
quantum value
is the highest winning probability that any quantum (potentially entangled) provers can achieve. Although the study of nonlocal games originated with Bell’s theorem
[
Bel64
]
, they have become central in the study of quantum complexity theory through their connection with multiprover interactive proofs
[
CHTW04
]
. This connection has led to
many protocols for efficiently certifying classical and quantum computations in the two-prover setting
[
RUV13
,
CGJV20
,
Gri17
,
JNV+21
]
.

A central question in the nonlocal game paradigm is whether the techniques and results can be translated to a model where a verifier interacts with a
single
prover.
The single-prover setting is theoretically appealing and practically motivated since a non-communication assumption between provers can be challenging to enforce.
Recently, Kalai et al.
[
KLVY23
]
proposed a generic procedure that compiles
any

k
-player one-round nonlocal game into a multi-round single-prover protocol, using a quantum homomorphic encryption scheme
[
Mah20
,
Bra18
]
.
In the same work, the authors showed that the compilation process preserves the classical value of the nonlocal game.
Shortly after, Natarajan and Zhang
[
NZ23
]
proved that the same compiler preserves the quantum value of the
2
-player CHSH game
[
CHSH69
]
. This was sufficient to achieve efficient delegation of BQP computations, however, their work left open the question of whether the Kalai et al. compiler preserved the quantum value for a more general class of games.

A natural generalization of the CHSH game is the class of two-player XOR games
[
CHTW04
]
, where the winning predicate depends on the XOR of the players’ single-bit answers. These games have a foundational place in the theory of nonlocal games thanks to an influential theorem of Tsirelson
[
Tsi80
,
Tsi87
]
, which exactly characterizes their optimal quantum strategies in terms of a semidefinite program. This makes XOR games one of the few classes of nonlocal games where the optimal quantum strategies are well understood, and finding the quantum value is computationally tractable. As such, they are a convenient stepping stone towards understanding more general families of nonlocal games.

1.1
Results

Our main result is that the quantum value of any two-player XOR game is preserved by the compilation procedure of
[
KLVY23
]
, resolving a question posed in
[
NZ23
]
. We give an informal statement of our result; a more detailed statement can be found in
Theorem

3.1
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be any XOR game and
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}}
the corresponding compiled game. Then any prover running in time polynomial in the security parameter
\lambda
wins
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}}
with probability at most
\omega_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G})+\mathrm{negl}(\lambda)
, where
\omega_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G})
is the quantum value of the original nonlocal game
\mathcal{G}
.

The negligible factors in
Theorem

1.1
come from the fact that the compiled game assumes the existence of quantum homomorphic encryption, whose security is computational and depends on chosen parameters. It is well-known that quantum homomorphic encryption can be constructed from the hardness of the standard learning with errors (LWE) problem
[
Mah18
,
Bra18
]
, and thus our theorem holds up to the same assumption. A useful consequence of this result is that we can apply the extensive literature on XOR games to give new examples of well-behaved compiled games. For instance, we obtain the following new results as almost immediate corollaries of the proof of our main theorem:

•

Self-testing for all XOR games
Using a result from Slofstra
[
Slo11
]
, we show that every compiled XOR game
self-tests some properties of the provers’ measurement operators: in particular, for each XOR game there is a set of relations between the Alice and Bob operators that are approximately satisfied for any near-optimal strategy for the compiled game (
Theorem

3.6
). As a special case, we recover the self-test for anticommutation in
[
NZ23
]
.

•

Parallel repetition
Building on
[
CSUU08
]
, we show that the quantum value of compiled
parallel repeated
XOR games satisfies perfect exponential decay in the number of parallel repetitions (
Theorem

3.11
). This is the first parallel repetition theorem for compiled games.

In addition, we obtain a self-testing result for the compiled Magic Square game, which is not an XOR game but has self-testing properties that are similarly convenient for applications.

•

Self-testing for the Magic Square game:
We show that the compilation of the Magic Square game of Mermin and Peres
[
Mer90
,
Per90
,
Ara02
]
is a self-test for a pair of anticommuting operators (
Theorem

5.7
). This mirrors the statement in the two-prover settings
[
WBMS16
]
.

1.2
Technical Outline

Non-local games and the
[
KLVY23
]
compiler.

We recall the basic setup of a nonlocal game. Before the interaction, Alice and Bob prepare a shared and possibly entangled state
|\psi\rangle
. Once this is done, they are separated and cannot communicate. The verifier samples a pair of challenges (or questions)
(x,y)
from a known distribution, and sends
x
to Alice and
y
to Bob. The players measure their respective quantum observables
A_{x}
and
B_{y}
on their respective shares of the state
|\psi\rangle
, and return outcomes
a,b
to the verifier. The verifier decides whether the players win or lose by evaluating a deterministic predicate
V(x,y,a,b)\in\{0,1\}
.

The KLVY compiler converts any nonlocal game into an interaction between the verifier and a single computationally-bounded prover, the latter of which sequentially emulates the roles of Alice and Bob. The setup of the compiled game is as follows:

1.

The prover prepares an initial state
|\psi\rangle
. There is
no
assumption that this state lives in a bipartite Hilbert space. Then the verifier generates a key pair for a quantum fully homomorphic encryption scheme and sends the public key to the prover.

2.

The verifier samples a pair of challenges
(x,y)
from a known distribution. It sends the encryption
c=\mathrm{Enc}(x)
of the first challenge to the prover. The prover, acting first as Alice, measures the state and returns the outcome
\alpha
. In the honest case, this measurement is the homomorphic execution of measuring the observable
A_{x}
with respect to
|\psi\rangle
.

3.

The verifier sends
y
to the prover in the clear (i.e. unencrypted). The prover, now acting as Bob, measures an observable
B_{y}
on its residual state and returns the outcome
b
. There is
no
assumption that this observable commutes with the earlier “Alice” measurement.

4.

The verifier decides if the prover wins based on the predicate
V(x,y,\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha),b)
.

It is easy to show that the compiled value is always
at least
the nonlocal value, as any nonlocal strategy can be implemented using the homomorphic encryption scheme. It is far less obvious how to establish upper bounds on the compiled value. This is because all known
general
techniques for upper-bounding the value of a nonlocal game make essential use of the spatial separation between the two provers. Nevertheless, we are able to show that the two specific techniques used by
[
NZ23
]
to analyze the compiled CHSH game both generalize to all compiled XOR games.
We believe the two approaches are complementary and improve our understanding of compiled nonlocal games from dual perspectives.

The macroscopic locality approach.

Our first approach generalizes the “macroscopic locality” proof of soundness of
[
NZ23
]
for the CHSH game, which is based on arguments contained in
[
Roh14
]
. One can use the IND-CPA security of the homomorphic encryption to upper-bound the maximum possible probability with which any efficiently measurable Bob operator can guess the value of Alice’s question
x
. Intuitively, this allows us to establish that Bob’s strategy cannot depend, in any detectable way, on Alice’s question. Applying Jensen’s inequality to Bob’s operator, we can then bound, up to a negligible function, the bias of every quantum strategy for the compiled XOR game by the bias of some
vector
strategy for the
nonlocal
XOR game. These vector strategies can be thought of as a vector solution to the semidefinite program which computes the bias of the XOR game.
By Tsirelson’s theorem, every such vector strategy gives rise to a quantum strategy for the nonlocal XOR game, yielding the desired bound on the winning probability for the compiled game in terms of the optimal nonlocal value of the original XOR game.
As a corollary, we obtain a one-sided rigidity or “self-testing” statement for any compiled XOR game: For any near-optimal strategy, it must hold that
A_{x}|\psi\rangle\approx\sum_{y}c_{xy}B_{y}A_{x}|\psi\rangle
for explicit coefficients
c_{xy}
.

The sum of squares approach.

A more abstract approach to upper-bounding the quantum value of a nonlocal game is to associate with a game a formal polynomial
p
, whose variables are identified with the observables
A_{x}
and
B_{y}
so that
\langle\psi|p(A_{x},B_{y})|\psi\rangle
is precisely the winning probability of the strategy. (For example, in the case of CHSH, the polynomial is
p(A_{x},B_{y})=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{8}(A_{0}B_{0}+A_{0}B_{1}+A_{1}B_{0}-A_{1}B_{1})
, and it can be easily verified that
\langle\psi|p(A_{x},B_{y})|\psi\rangle
captures the winning probability of the strategy
(\{A_{x}\},\{B_{y}\},|\psi\rangle)
for the CHSH game. Moreover, to prove that the quantum value of a game is at most
\omega
, it suffices to give a sum-of-squares (SOS) certificate demonstrating that
\omega-p(A_{x},B_{y})
is a positive operator whenever
p
is evaluated on variables that corresponds to valid observables. Such a certificate consists of a decomposition of
\omega-p(A_{x},B_{y})
as a positive operator

\omega-p(A_{x},B_{y})=\sum_{i}q_{i}(A_{x},B_{y})^{*}q_{i}(A_{x},B_{y})+g(A_{x},B_{y}),

where the
q_{i}
’s and
g
are polynomials, and
g
is a polynomial that evaluates to zero whenever the variables are valid observables. In particular,
g
lies in the ideal generated by a certain collection of polynomial constraints on the variables
A_{x}
and
B_{y}
, including the commutator constraint
[A_{x},B_{y}]=0
for all
x,y
, which formally represents the spatial separation of the players.

The reason why such an SOS certificate suffices to upper bound the game value in the nonlocal case is that (1) taking the expectation
\langle\psi|\omega-p(A_{x},B_{y})|\psi\rangle
maps positive operators to positive numbers, and (2)
\langle\psi|p(A_{x},B_{y})|\psi\rangle
is equal to the winning probability of strategy
S=(\{A_{x},B_{y}\},|\psi\rangle)
. Property (1) gives that evaluating the expectation on
\omega-p(A_{x},B_{y})
yields a positive number, and property (2) says that this positive number is equal to
\omega-p_{win}
, where
p_{win}
is the winning probability of the strategy
S=(\{A_{x},B_{y}\},|\psi\rangle)
. Therefore, in the nonlocal case, taking the expectation plays the role of what is often known as a “pseudo-expectation” in the SOS literature.

In general, an SOS certificate of the aforementioned form does not automatically yield an upper bound on the compiled value. The reason is that there is no longer an obvious choice of pseudo-expectation operator which will satisfy the requisite properties. In particular, the game value can no longer be described by a sum of quantities that look like
\langle\psi|A_{x}B_{y}|\psi\rangle
, as we no longer have the guarantee
[A_{x},B_{y}]=0
afforded to us before by the spatial separation, meaning that the
g
polynomial may not be zero when evaluated on the “valid” strategies for the compiled game. In
[
NZ23
]
, a choice of pseudo-expectation operator was carefully defined so that the pseudo-expectation of
g(A_{x},B_{y})
was zero by fiat, and (weaker versions of) properties (1) and (2) could be argued by reduction to cryptographic security properties. However, the fact that the measurement operators used by Alice in the compiled game return encrypted outcomes was an obstacle to carrying out these cryptographic reductions because the security only holds with respect to efficient distinguishers.
In
[
NZ23
]
, this obstacle was overcome in the CHSH game because the SOS certificate for this game has a particularly simple dependence on the
A_{x}
observables.
Building on a work of Slofstra
[
Slo11
]
, we show that
any
XOR game has a “nice” SOS certificate (in which each
q_{i}
has degree
1
and depends on at most one
A_{x}
variable) and that these “nice” SOS certificates yield bounds on the compiled value as well as the quantum value.

1.3
Further directions

The most immediate question raised by our work is to determine how far our techniques can extend beyond XOR games: for instance, can we find “nice” SOS decompositions, of higher degree, for all nonlocal games? Another more conceptual question is to determine the “correct” notion of rigidity in the compiled setting: unlike in the nonlocal case, our results are not symmetric between between Alice and Bob.

1.4
Related work

During the completion of this work, we became aware that the self-testing result for the Magic Square game was independently obtained separately in unpublished works by Thomas Vidick, and by Fermi Ma and Alex Lombardi, using similar techniques to ours.

Preliminaries

2.1
Cryptography

We adopt several definitions from
[
NZ23
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
QPT (quantum polynomial time) algorithm
is a logspace-uniform family of quantum circuits with size polynomial in the number of input qubits and in the security parameter.
If the circuits are unitary then we call this a
(unitary) QPT circuit
.
A POVM
\{M_{\beta}\}
is called
QPT-measurable
is there is a QPT circuit such that measuring some output qubits and post-processing gives rise to the same probabilities as the POVM.
A binary observable
B
is called
QPT-measurable
if this is the case for the corresponding projective POVM.
This is equivalent (by uncomputation) to demanding that
B
interpreted as a unitary can be realized by a QPT circuit.

Here we follow
[
NZ23
]
in considering security against uniform adversaries, and indeed all of our reductions are uniform.
We remark that we can also define security against non-uniform adversaries to obtain a stronger conclusion at the cost of relying on a stronger cryptographic assumption (specifically, QHE secure against non-uniform adversaries, which is quite standard in cryptography).
We now recall the notion of quantum homomorphic encryption (QHE).

Definition 2.2
.

A
quantum homomorphic encryption scheme

\mathrm{QHE}=(\mathrm{Gen},\mathrm{Enc},\mathrm{Eval},\mathrm{Dec})

\mathcal{C}
is defined as a tuple of algorithms with the following syntax.

•

\mathrm{Gen}
is a PPT algorithm that takes as input the security parameter
1^{\lambda}
and returns a secret key
\mathrm{sk}
.

•

\mathrm{Enc}
is a PPT algorithm that takes as input a secret key
\mathrm{sk}
and a classical input
x
, and outputs a classical ciphertext
c
.

•

\mathrm{Eval}
is a QPT algorithm that takes as input a tuple a classical description of a quantum circuit
C:\mathcal{H}\times(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}\to(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes m}
, a quantum register with Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, and a ciphertext
c
, and outputs a ciphertext
\tilde{c}
.
If
C
has classical output, we require that
\mathrm{Eval}
also has classical output.

•

\mathrm{Dec}
is a QPT algorithm that takes as input a secret key
\mathrm{sk}
and ciphertext
c
, and outputs a state
|\psi\rangle
.
Additionally, if
c
is a classical ciphertext, the decryption algorithm deterministically outputs a classical string
y
.

We require that the scheme satisfies the following properties.

•

Correctness with Auxiliary Input:

For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
, any quantum circuit
C:\mathcal{H}_{A}\times(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}\to\{0,1\}^{*}
with classical output, any state

, the following experiments output states with negligible trace distance:

–

Game 1:
Start with
(x,|\psi\rangle_{AB})
.
Apply the circuit
C
, obtaining the the classical string
y
.
Return
y
and register
B
.

–

Game 2:
Start with a key
\mathrm{sk}\leftarrow\mathrm{Gen}(1^{\lambda})
,
c\in\mathrm{Enc}(\mathrm{sk},x)
, and
|\psi\rangle_{AB}
.
Apply
\mathrm{Eval}

C
, register
A
, and ciphertext
c
to obtain
\tilde{c}
.
Compute
\tilde{y}\leftarrow\mathrm{Dec}(\mathrm{sk},\tilde{c})
.
Return
\tilde{y}
and register
B
.

•

CPA Security:
For all pairs of messages
(x_{0},x_{1})
and any QPT adversary
\mathcal{A}
it holds that

\left|\Pr\left[1=\mathcal{A}(c_{0})^{\mathrm{Enc}(\mathrm{sk},\cdot)}\middle|\begin{array}[]{l}\mathrm{sk}\leftarrow\mathrm{Gen}(1^{\lambda})\\

.

In a slight abuse of notation, we often write expressions such as
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}f(\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha))

2.2
Non-Local Games

Definition 2.4
.

A
nonlocal game

\mathcal{G}
is a tuple
(I_{A},I_{B},O_{A},O_{B},\pi,V)
consisting of finite sets
I_{A}
and
I_{B}
of inputs for Alice and Bob, respectively, finite sets
O_{A}
and
O_{B}
of outputs for Alice and Bob, respectively, a probability distribution of the inputs
\pi:I_{A}\times I_{B}\to[0,1]
, and a verification function
V:I_{A}\times I_{B}\times O_{A}\times O_{B}\to\{0,1\}
.

A nonlocal game is played by a verifier and two provers, Alice and Bob.
In the game, the verifier samples a pair
(x,y)\leftarrow\pi
and sends
x
to Alice and
y
to Bob.
Alice and Bob respond with
a\in O_{A}
and
b\in O_{B}
, respectively.
They win if
V(x,y,a,b)=1
.
The players are not allowed to communicate during the game, but they can agree on a strategy beforehand.
Their goal is to maximize their winning probability.
If we do not specify otherwise, the distribution
\pi
will be the uniform distribution.

Definition 2.5
.

A
quantum (tensor) strategy

S
for a nonlocal game
\mathcal{G}=(I_{A},I_{B},O_{A},O_{B},\pi,V)
is a tuple
S=(\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{B},|\psi\rangle,\{A_{xa}\},\{B_{yb}\})
, consisting of finite dimensionals Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
, a bipartite state
|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
, PVMs
\{A_{xa}\}_{a\in O_{A}}
acting on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
for each
x\in I_{A}
for Alice and PVMs
\{B_{yb}\}_{b\in O_{B}}
acting on
\mathcal{H}_{B}
for each
y\in I_{B}
for Bob. Often we will drop the Hilbert spaces, and just write
S=(|\psi\rangle,\{A_{xa}\},\{B_{yb}\})
.

Here we restrict without loss of generality to pure states and projective measurements (PVMs).
For a strategy
S=(|\psi\rangle,\{A_{xa}\},\{B_{yb}\})
, the probability of Alice and Bob answering
a,b
when obtaining
x,y
is given by
p(a,b|x,y)=\langle\psi|A_{xa}\otimes B_{yb}|\psi\rangle
. Therefore, the
winning probability
of a quantum strategy
S
for the nonlocal game
\mathcal{G}
is given by

\displaystyle\omega_{q}(S,\mathcal{G})=\sum_{x,y}\pi(x,y)\sum_{a,b}V(x,y,a,b)p(a,b|x,y)=\sum_{x,y}\pi(x,y)\sum_{a,b}V(x,y,a,b)\langle\psi|A_{xa}\otimes B_{yb}|\psi\rangle.

For a nonlocal game
\mathcal{G}
, we define the
quantum value

\omega_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G})=\mathrm{sup}_{S}\omega_{q}(S,\mathcal{G})
to be the supremum over all quantum tensor strategies compatible with
\mathcal{G}
. A strategy
S
is called
\varepsilon
-optimal
for a game
\mathcal{G}
, if it holds
\omega_{q}(S,\mathcal{G})\geq\omega_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G})-\varepsilon
. In the case of
\varepsilon=0
, i.e.
\omega_{q}(S,\mathcal{G})=\omega_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G})
, we usually omit
\varepsilon
and call the strategy
optimal
.

The tensor-product structure is a way of mathematically representing the locality of the players employing a quantum strategy in a nonlocal game. However, there is a more general way to model this nonlocality mathematically.

Definition 2.6
.

A
commuting operator strategy

\mathcal{S}
for a nonlocal game
\mathcal{G}=(I_{A},I_{B},O_{A},O_{B},\pi,V)
is a tuple
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{H},|\psi\rangle,\{A_{xa}\},\{B_{yb}\})
, consisting of a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, a state
|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
, and two collections of mutually commuting PVMs
\{A_{xa}\}_{a\in O_{A}}
acting on
\mathcal{H}
for each
x\in I_{A}
for Alice and PVMs
\{B_{yb}\}_{b\in O_{B}}
acting on
\mathcal{H}
for each
y\in I_{B}
for Bob, i.e.
[A_{xa},B_{yb}]=0
for all
a,b,x,y\in O_{A}\times O_{B}\times I_{A}\times I_{B}
. Like for quantum strategies, we will often omit the Hilbert space and write
\mathcal{S}=(|\psi\rangle,\{A_{xa}\},\{B_{yb}\})
for a commuting operator strategy.

We can also define the commuting operator (also known as the quantum commuting) value of a nonlocal game
\omega_{qc}^{*}(\mathcal{G})=\sup_{S}\omega_{qc}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{G})
to be the supremum over all commuting operator strategies
S
compatible with
\mathcal{G}
. It is not hard to see that every quantum (tensor) strategy is a commuting operator strategy. The converse holds if we restrict our commuting operator strategies to be finite dimensional (i.e.
\mathcal{H}
is finite dimensional). However, there are examples of nonlocal games
\mathcal{G}
for which there is a perfect (wins with probability
1
) commuting operators strategy but no perfect tensor-product strategy, see for example
[
Slo16
]
.

2.3
XOR Games

Definition 2.7
.

An
XOR game
is a nonlocal game for which
O_{A}=O_{B}=\{0,1\}
and
V(x,y,a,b)=\frac{1}{2}(1+(-1)^{g(x,y)}(-1)^{a+b})
for some function
g:I_{A}\times I_{B}\to\{0,1\}
. An XOR game is completely described by the
cost matrix

G=(G_{xy})_{x\in I_{A},y\in I_{B}}
, where
G_{xy}=(-1)^{g(x,y)}\pi(x,y).
We will say that
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}}
is a
compiled XOR game
if it is a compiled game for an XOR game
\mathcal{G}
.

Given a commuting operator strategy for an XOR game
\mathcal{S}=(|\psi\rangle,\{A_{xa}\},\{B_{yb}\})
we can derive an expression for the commuting operator
bias
of the strategy
\beta_{qc}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{G})
in terms of
\pm 1
-valued observables (i.e. self-adjoint unitaries) as follows:

\displaystyle 2\omega_{qc}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{G})-1

\displaystyle=2\left[\sum_{x,y}\pi(x,y)\sum_{a,b}V(a,b,x,y)\langle\psi|A_{xa}B_{yb}|\psi\rangle\right]-1

(2.1)

\displaystyle=2\left[\sum_{x,y}\pi(x,y)\sum_{a,b}\frac{1}{2}(1+(-1)^{f(x,y)+a+b})\langle\psi|A_{xa}B_{yb}|\psi\rangle\right]-1

(2.2)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}\pi(x,y)\sum_{a,b}(-1)^{f(x,y)+a+b}\langle\psi|A_{xa}B_{yb}|\psi\rangle

(2.3)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}\pi(x,y)(-1)^{f(x,y)}\langle\psi|\left(\sum_{a}(-1)^{a}A_{xa}\right)\left(\sum_{b}(-1)^{b}B_{yb}\right)|\psi\rangle

(2.4)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}G_{xy}\langle\psi|A_{x}B_{y}|\psi\rangle

(2.5)

\displaystyle=\beta_{qc}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{G}),

(2.6)

where
A_{x}=A_{x0}-A_{x1}
and
B_{y}=B_{y0}-B_{y1}
are the
\pm 1
-valued (binary) observables for all
x,y\in I_{A}\times I_{B}
. Note that this construction is reversible, and thus strategies consisting of these
\pm 1
-valued observables are equivalent to commuting operator strategies for
\mathcal{G}
.
With this in mind, we define the
optimal commuting operator bias
of
\mathcal{G}
to be
\beta_{qc}^{*}(\mathcal{G})=2\omega_{qc}^{*}(\mathcal{G})-1
. Since all quantum strategies are commuting operator strategies we can similarly define the quantum
bias

\beta_{q}(S,\mathcal{G})=2\omega_{q}(S,\mathcal{G})-1
. We define the
optimal quantum bias
of
\mathcal{G}
to be
\beta_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G})=2\omega_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G})-1
.

We shall now present some of the relevant structural properties we will need about XOR games.

Definition 2.8
.

For an XOR game
\mathcal{G}
, a
vector strategy

\mathcal{V}
is a Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and a collection of real unit vectors
\left\{u_{x}:x\in I_{A}\right\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\left\{v_{y}:y\in I_{B}\right\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The
vector bias
for
\mathcal{G}
is given by

\beta(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{G})=\sum_{x,y}G_{xy}\langle u_{x}|v_{y}\rangle.

(2.7)

The optimal vector bias is denoted by
\beta^{*}_{vec}(\mathcal{G})=\sup_{\mathcal{V}}\beta(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{G})
, and
\mathcal{V}
is an optimal vector strategy if
\beta_{vec}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{G})=\beta^{*}_{vec}(\mathcal{G})
.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
S=\{|\psi\rangle,\{A_{x}\},\{B_{y}\}\}
be a quantum strategy for an XOR game
\mathcal{G}
. We define the
row biases

\{r_{x,S}\in\mathbb{R}:x\in I_{A}\}
, where each

r_{x,S}=\sum_{y}G_{xy}\langle\psi|A_{x}B_{y}|\psi\rangle.

We similarly define the
column biases

\{c_{y,S}\in\mathbb{R}:y\in I_{B}\}
, where each

c_{y,S}=\sum_{x}G_{xy}\langle\psi|A_{x}B_{y}|\psi\rangle.

These bias quantities play an important role in the following theorem:

Theorem 2.10

(
[
Slo11
,
Tsi87
]
)
.

For any XOR game
\mathcal{G}
, the following hold:

1.

All optimal quantum strategies
S
have the same bias
\beta_{q}(S)
, row biases
\{r_{x,S}\}
and column biases
\{c_{y,S}\}
, henceforth denoted
\beta
,
\{r_{x}:x\in I_{A}\}
, and
\{c_{y}:y\in I_{B}\}
respectively. All optimal row and column biases are positive.

2.

In any optimal quantum commuting operator strategy, we have that

A_{x}|\psi\rangle=\hat{B}_{x}|\psi\rangle:=\frac{1}{r_{x}}\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}|\psi\rangle,

for all
x\in I_{A}
.

3.

The optimal quantum bias
\beta^{\ast}_{q}(\mathcal{G})
is the solution to the following primal and dual semi-definite programs:

\begin{aligned} (P):\quad\mathop{\mathrm{max}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{x},v_{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\\
\|u_{x}\|=\|v_{y}\|=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{x\in I_{A},y\in I_{B}}G_{xy}\langle u_{x}|v_{y}\rangle\end{aligned}\qquad\quad\begin{aligned} (D):\quad\min_{\{r_{x}\},\{c_{y}\}}&\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x}r_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y}c_{y}:\\
&\begin{pmatrix}\Delta(\mathbf{r})&-G^{T}\\
-G&\Delta(\mathbf{c})\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0,\end{aligned}

where
\Delta(\mathbf{r})
is the
|I_{A}|\times|I_{A}|
diagonal matrix with the values
r_{x}
along the diagonal, and likewise
\Delta(\mathbf{c})
is the
|I_{B}|\times|I_{B}|
diagonal matrix with the values
c_{y}
along the diagonal. Furthermore, the optimal row and column biases are the solutions to the dual semi-definite program.

Proof.

The first two items are Theorem 3.1 and Corollary 3.2 of
[
Slo11
]
, together with the fact that any optimal quantum strategy is an optimal vector strategy, which follows from
Corollary

2.11
. The formulation of the primal in (3) is a direct consequence of
Corollary

2.11
, and the duality of the semi-definite programs is shown in the proof of Theorem 3.1 of
[
Slo11
]
.
∎

Corollary 2.11
.

Let
\beta\in[-1,1]

\mathcal{G}

1.

\mathcal{G}
has an optimal quantum strategy
S
with
\beta_{q}(S,\mathcal{G})=\beta
.

2.

\mathcal{G}
has an optimal commuting operator strategy

with

3.

2.4
Compiled Games

Definition 2.12
.

A
compiled game

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}}
consists of a nonlocal game
\mathcal{G}
and a quantum homomorphic encryption scheme
\mathrm{QHE}=(\mathrm{Gen},\mathrm{Enc},\mathrm{Eval},\mathrm{Dec})
. However, unlike a standard nonlocal game, it is played by a verifier and a single prover. The behaviour of the interaction is described as follows:

1.

The verifier samples
(x,y)\leftarrow\pi
,
\mathrm{sk}\leftarrow\mathrm{Gen}(1^{\lambda})
, and
c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(\mathrm{sk},x)
. The verifier sends
c
to the prover.

2.

The prover replies with some classical ciphertext
\alpha
.

3.

The verifier sends
y
(in the clear) to the prover.

4.

The prover replies with some classical message
b
.

5.

The verifier computes
a:=\mathrm{Dec}(\mathrm{sk},\alpha)
and accepts if and only if
a\in O_{A}
,
b\in O_{B}
, and
V(a,b,x,y)=1
.

Definition 2.13
.

A
quantum strategy for a compiled game
is a tuple
(\mathcal{H},|\psi\rangle,\{A_{c\alpha}\},\{B_{yb}\})
consisting of a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, an efficiently
(in QPT) preparable state
|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
, operators
A_{c\alpha}=U_{c\alpha}P_{c\alpha}
acting on
\mathcal{H}
, where
U_{c\alpha}
is a QPT-measurable unitary and
\{P_{c\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}
(where
\Lambda
is the set of outcomes), is a QPT-measurable PVM for all
c\in\mathrm{Enc}(sk,x)
,
x\in I_{A}
, as well as QPT-measurable PVMs
\{B_{yb}\}_{b\in O_{B}}
acting on
\mathcal{H}
for all
y\in I_{A}
.

For convenience, we let
|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle:=A_{c\alpha}|\psi\rangle
be the
unnormalized post-measurement state
after Step 2 in
Definition

2.12
. For a strategy for the compiled game
S=(|\psi\rangle,\{A_{c\alpha}\},\{B_{yb}\})
, the probability of Alice and Bob answering
a,b
after being given
x,y
is denoted

\displaystyle p(a,b|x,y)=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha;\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)=a}\langle\psi|(A_{c\alpha})^{*}B_{yb}A_{c\alpha}|\psi\rangle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha;\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)=a}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|B_{yb}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle

It follows that the
winning probability
of the quantum strategy
S
for the compiled game
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}}
is given by

\displaystyle\omega_{q}(S,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}})=\sum_{x,y}\pi(x,y)\sum_{a,b}V(x,y,a,b)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha;\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)=a}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|B_{yb}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle.

(2.8)

Theorem 2.14
.

(
[
KLVY23
, Theorem 3.2]
)
If
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}}
is a compiled nonlocal game with underlying nonlocal game
\mathcal{G}
. Then, there exists a compiled quantum strategy
S
for
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}}
and a negligible function
\eta(\lambda)
such that

\displaystyle\omega_{q}(S,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}})\geq\omega_{q}(\mathcal{G})-\eta(\lambda),

where

depends on

.

2.5
Block encodings

Given binary observables that are efficiently measurable, we will also be interested in measuring their linear combinations and products in order to infer relations.
To this end, we use block encodings as in
[
NZ23
]
.
See
[
GSLW19
]
for a general overview of this notion.
We only define it for square matrices:

Definition 2.15
.

A
block encoding
of a matrix
H
on
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
is a unitary
U
on
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes(m+n)}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\displaystyle tH=\left\lparen\langle 0|^{\otimes m}\otimes I\right\rparen U\left\lparen|0\rangle^{\otimes m}\otimes I\right\rparen,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad U=\begin{pmatrix}tH&*\\
*&*\end{pmatrix}

for some
t>0
called the
scale factor
of the block encoding.
(Note that any unitary is trivially block encodable with
m=0
and
t=1
.)
A block encoding is called

if

is implemented by a QPT circuit.

The below lemmas summarize and generalize the discussion in
[
NZ23
]
(cf. Lemmas 11–13 therein).

Lemma 2.16
.

If
B=B_{+}-B_{-}
is a QPT-measurable binary observable, with projections

, then

interpreted as a unitary can be implemented by a QPT circuit (in particular, it is a QPT-implementable block encoding of itself, with scale factor 1).

Lemma 2.17
.

For any fixed

, the following holds.
If
H_{1},\dots,H_{k}
are operators with QPT-implementable block encodings, each with scale factor
\Theta(1)
, then
H=\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{j}H_{j}
also has a QPT-implementable block encoding with scale factor
\Theta(1)
.

Lemma

2.17
follows from
[
GSLW19
, Lemma 29]
.
We note that it can be extended to
k=\operatorname{poly}(\lambda)
many block encodings (if the vector

can be prepared efficiently).

Lemma 2.18
.

If
H
and
H^{\prime}
are operators with QPT-implementable block encodings, each with scale factor
\Theta(1)
, then their product
HH^{\prime}
also has a QPT-implementable block encoding with scale factor
\Theta(1)
.

3
The macroscopic locality approach

In this section, we will show that for the well-known class of nonlocal games known as XOR games, the quantum value coincides with the quantum value of its compiled version. In particular, we establish the following result:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be any XOR game and
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}}
the corresponding compiled game.
For every strategy
S
for
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}}
, there exists a negligible function
\eta(\lambda)
such that the following holds:

\displaystyle\omega_{q}(S,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}})\leq\omega_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G})+\eta(\lambda).

As a corollary, we will deduce a self-testing and a parallel repetition theorem for compiled XOR games. Just as we did for ordinary nonlocal games, we define the quantum bias of a compiled game as
\beta_{q}(S,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}})=2\omega_{q}(S,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}})-1
and
\beta_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}})=2\omega_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}})-1
.​​​

3.1
The quantum value of compiled XOR games

In order to show that the quantum value of a compiled XOR game coincides with the quantum value of the associated nonlocal game, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an XOR game, with cost matrix
G=(G_{xy})_{x\in I_{A},y\in I_{B}}
, and let
V\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{B}\times I_{B}}
be a positive semidefinite matrix such that
V_{yy}=1
for all
y\in I_{B}
.
Then there exist unit vectors
u_{x},v_{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for all
x\in I_{A}
,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{s,y\in I_{B}}G_{xs}G_{xy}V_{sy}}=\sum_{y\in I_{B}}G_{xy}\langle u_{x}|v_{y}\rangle.

(3.1)

Proof.

Since the matrix
V\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{B}\times I_{B}}
is positive semi-definite, there exists
d\in\mathbb{N}
and vectors
v_{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
y\in I_{B}
such that
V_{sy}=\langle v_{s}|v_{y}\rangle
for all
s,y\in I_{B}
.
Each
v_{y}
is a unit vector, since we have
\lVert v_{y}\rVert^{2}=\langle v_{y}|v_{y}\rangle=V_{yy}=1
by assumption.
Next, for every
x\in I_{A}
, define unit vectors

u_{x}=\frac{1}{\|\sum_{s\in I_{B}}G_{xs}v_{s}\|}\sum_{s\in I_{B}}G_{xs}v_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in I_{B}}G_{xy}\langle u_{x}|v_{y}\rangle=\frac{1}{\|\sum_{s\in I_{B}}G_{xs}v_{s}\|}\sum_{s,y\in I_{B}}G_{xs}G_{xy}V_{sy}=\sqrt{\sum_{s,y\in I_{B}}G_{xs}G_{xy}V_{sy}},

since
\|\sum_{s\in I_{B}}G_{xs}v_{s}\|^{2}=\sum_{s,y\in I_{B}}G_{xs}G_{xy}V_{sy}
.
Thus we have established
Eq.

3.1
.
∎

The next lemma is proven in the same way as
[
NZ23
, Lemma 26]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}}
be a compiled XOR game and
S=(|\psi\rangle,\{A_{c\alpha}\},\{B_{yb}\})
a quantum strategy.
For every
x\in I_{A}

\displaystyle\left|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\left(\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\right)|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\right|^{2}\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\left(\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\right)^{2}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle,

where
G=(G_{xy})
is the cost matrix of the underlying XOR game,
|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle=A_{c\alpha}|\psi\rangle
are the post-measurement states after Step 2 of the protocol, and the
B_{y}:=B_{y0}-B_{y1}
are the (binary) observables corresponding to the PVMs
\{B_{yb}\}_{b}
.

Proof.

Applying Jensen’s inequality to the function
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
x\mapsto x^{2}
, we obtain the three inequalities

\displaystyle(\mathbb{E}(X))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}(X^{2})

\displaystyle\text{for real-valued random variables }X,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{k}p_{k}x_{k}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k}p_{k}x_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle\text{for }x_{k}\in\mathbb{R},p_{k}\geq 0\text{ and }\sum_{k}p_{k}=1,

(3.3)

\displaystyle(\langle\psi|O|\psi\rangle)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\langle\psi|O^{2}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle\text{for quantum states }|\psi\rangle\text{ and observables }O.

(3.4)

Define
\mathrm{B}_{x}=\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}
. From
Eq.

3.2
, we deduce

\displaystyle\left|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\mathrm{B}_{x}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\right|^{2}\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\left(\sum_{\alpha}(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\mathrm{B}_{x}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\right)^{2}.

Note that the post-measurement states
|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle
are not normalized and it holds
\sum_{\alpha}\||\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\|^{2}=1
. Therefore, using
Eq.

3.3
, we get

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\left(\sum_{\alpha}(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\mathrm{B}_{x}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\left(\sum_{\alpha}\||\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\|^{2}(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)}\frac{\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\mathrm{B}_{x}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle}{\||\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\|^{2}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\||\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\|^{2}\left(\frac{\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\mathrm{B}_{x}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle}{\||\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\|^{2}}\right)^{2}.

Since
\frac{|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle}{\||\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\|}
is a quantum state, we finally infer

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\||\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\|^{2}\left(\frac{\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\mathrm{B}_{x}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle}{\||\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\|^{2}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\||\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\|^{2}\frac{\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\mathrm{B}_{x}^{2}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle}{\||\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\mathrm{B}_{x}^{2}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle

from
Eq.

3.4
.
∎

We are now ready to prove the main result of this section.

Proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

Let
S=(|\psi\rangle,\{A_{c\alpha}\},\{B_{yb}\})
be an arbitrary quantum strategy for
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}}
.
By
Eq.

2.8
and the definition of the bias, it holds that

\displaystyle\beta_{q}(S,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}G_{xy}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|B_{y}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\left(\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\right)|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle.

By
Lemma

3.3
, we get

\displaystyle\beta_{q}(S,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x}\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\left(\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\right)^{2}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle}.

(3.5)

Now fix any
x_{0}\in I_{A}
.
For each
x\in I_{A}
, the operator
\mathcal{B}_{x}=\left(\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\right)^{2}
has a QPT-implementable block encoding with scale factor
\Theta(1)
by

2.16
,
2.17
and
2.18
.
Moreover,
\lVert\mathcal{B}_{x}\rVert=\lVert\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\rVert^{2}\leq\lparen\sum_{y}\pi(x,y)\rparen^{2}\leq 1
.
Then, using
Lemma

2.21
with the distributions
D_{1}=\delta_{x}
and
D_{2}=\delta_{x_{0}}
over Alice’s questions, we see that there exists a negligible function
\eta_{x}(\lambda)\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle\left|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\mathcal{B}_{x}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x_{0})}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\mathcal{B}_{x}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\right|\leq\eta_{x}(\lambda),

and hence we can upper bound
Eq.

3.5
as follows:

\displaystyle\beta_{q}(S,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x}\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\mathcal{B}_{x}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x_{0})}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\mathcal{B}_{x_{0}}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle}+\sum_{x\neq x_{0}}\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\mathcal{B}_{x}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x_{0})}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\mathcal{B}_{x_{0}}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle}+\sum_{x\neq x_{0}}\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x_{0})}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\mathcal{B}_{x}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle+\eta_{x}(\lambda)}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x_{0})}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\mathcal{B}_{x_{0}}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle}+\sum_{x\neq x_{0}}\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x_{0})}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\mathcal{B}_{x}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle}+\sum_{x\neq x_{0}}\sqrt{\eta_{x}(\lambda)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x_{0})}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\mathcal{B}_{x}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle}+\eta^{\prime}(\lambda),

(3.6)

where the last inequality is due to
\sqrt{s+t}\leq\sqrt{s}+\sqrt{t}
, which holds for all
s,t\geq 0
, and where we introduced the negligible function
\eta^{\prime}(\lambda):=\sum_{x\neq x_{0}}\sqrt{\eta_{x}(\lambda)}
.
Finally, we observe that if we define a matrix
V\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{B}\times I_{B}}
with entries
V_{sy}:=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x_{0})}\sum_{\alpha}\frac{1}{2}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|B_{s}B_{y}+B_{y}B_{s}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle
, then there exists a vector strategy
\{u_{x},v_{y}\}
such that, for all
x\in I_{A}
,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x_{0})}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\mathcal{B}_{x}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle}=\sqrt{\sum_{s,y}G_{xs}G_{xy}V_{sy}}=\sum_{y}G_{xy}\langle u_{x}|v_{y}\rangle.

(3.7)

This follows from
Lemma

3.2
, because
V\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{B}\times I_{B}}
is positive semidefinite (it is a nonnegative linear combination of Gram matrices) and also satisfies
V_{yy}=1
.
As a consequence of this and Tsirelson’s theorem (
Theorem

2.10
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x_{0})}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\mathcal{B}_{x}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle}=\sum_{x,y}G_{xy}\langle u_{x}|v_{y}\rangle\leq\beta_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G}).

(3.8)

Thus we have proved that

\displaystyle\beta_{q}(S,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}})\leq\beta_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G})+\eta^{\prime}(\lambda).

By definition of the biases, we get
\omega_{q}(S,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}})\leq\omega_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G})+\frac{1}{2}\eta^{\prime}(\lambda)
.
This finishes the proof.
∎

Together,
Theorems

2.14
and
3.1
imply that the “asymptotic” quantum value of a compiled XOR game is equal to the quantum value of the underlying XOR game.
That is, if
S
is any quantum strategy for the compiled game, then

\displaystyle\limsup_{\lambda\to\infty}\omega_{q}(S,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}})\leq w_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G}),

and conversely, there always exists a strategy such that

\displaystyle\liminf_{\lambda\to\infty}\omega_{q}(S,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}})\geq w_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G}).

3.2
Self-testing results for compiled XOR games

We now give a self-testing type result which allows us to extract partial information about a quantum strategy if the winning probability is near-optimal.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an XOR game with cost matrix
G
.
Recall there exist
row biases

r_{x}\geq 0
for every
x\in I_{A}
such that
r_{x}=\sum_{y}G_{xy}\langle u_{x}|v_{y}\rangle
for every optimal vector strategy
\{u_{x},v_{y}\}
.
Slofstra showed that these row (and column) biases are robust in the sense that for an
\varepsilon
-optimal vector strategy, it holds that

\displaystyle\left(r_{x}-\sum_{y}G_{xy}\langle u_{x}|v_{y}\rangle\right)^{2}\leq 2(|I_{A}|+|I_{B}|)\beta_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G})\varepsilon,

(3.9)

see
[
Slo11
, Section 3]
.
Note that the quantity
\varepsilon
refers to the winning probability, whereas in
[
Slo11
, Section 3]
, the notion of
\varepsilon
-optimality is defined with respect to the bias.

Slofstra furthermore defined the
solution algebra

\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{G})
to be the universal
C^{*}
-algebra generated by self-adjoint elements
Y_{y}
for
y\in I_{B}
, subject to the relations:

\displaystyle Y_{y}^{2}=\mathds{1}\text{ for all }y\in I_{B}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left(\sum_{y\in I_{B}}G_{xy}Y_{y}\right)^{2}=r_{x}^{2}\mathds{1}\text{ for all }x\in I_{A}.

We will see that in an
\varepsilon
-optimal quantum strategy of the compiled XOR game, the Bob operators fulfill similar relations (
Theorem

3.6
).
To this end we make the following definition, which is justified by the discussion at the end of the preceding section.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an XOR game and
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}}
the corresponding compiled game.
We say that a quantum strategy
S
for
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}}
is
\varepsilon
-optimal
if (for sufficiently large
\lambda
)

\displaystyle\omega_{q}(S,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}})\geq\omega_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G})-\varepsilon.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an XOR game with cost matrix
G
. If
S=(|\psi\rangle,\{A_{c\alpha}\},\{B_{yb}\})
is an
\varepsilon
-optimal quantum strategy for
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}}
.
Then there exists a negligible function
\eta^{\prime}(\lambda)
such that for every
z\in I_{A}
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(z)}\sum_{\alpha}\|\left\lparen r_{z}\mathds{1}-(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)}\sum_{y}G_{zy}B_{y}\right\rparen|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\|^{2}\leq 2\big\lparen\left\lparen\lvert I_{A}\rvert+\lvert I_{B}\rvert\right\rparen\,\beta_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G})+1\big\rparen\varepsilon^{\prime},

where
\varepsilon^{\prime}=2\varepsilon+\eta^{\prime}(\lambda)
.

Proof.

Since
S
is
\varepsilon
-optimal, and using
Eqs.

3.6
and
3.8
in the proof of
Theorem

3.1
(with
x_{0}=z
), we see that

\displaystyle\beta_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G})-2\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{q}^{*}(S,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in I_{A}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\left(\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\right)|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x\in I_{A}}\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\left(\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\right)^{2}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x\in I_{A},y\in I_{B}}G_{xy}\langle u_{x}|v_{y}\rangle+\eta^{\prime}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\leq\beta^{*}_{q}(\mathcal{G})+\eta^{\prime}(\lambda),

(3.10)

where
\{u_{x},v_{y}\}
is a vector strategy (depending on the choice of
z
) such that, for all
x\in I_{A}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in I_{B}}G_{xy}\langle u_{x}|v_{y}\rangle=\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(z)}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\left(\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\right)^{2}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle}.

(3.11)

From this we can draw two conclusions:

First, since the second inequality in
Eq.

3.10
holds for each
x\in I_{A}
(by
Lemma

3.3
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\left(\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\right)^{2}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle}\quad-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\left(\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\right)|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\leq\varepsilon^{\prime},

where
\varepsilon^{\prime}:=2\varepsilon+\eta^{\prime}(\lambda)
.

From this, it follows that

\displaystyle\quad\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\|\left\lparen r_{x}-(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)}\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\right\rparen|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\|^{2}

\displaystyle=r_{x}^{2}-2r_{x}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\left\lparen\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\right\rparen|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\left(\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\right)^{2}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq r_{x}^{2}-2r_{x}\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\left(\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\right)^{2}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle}+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\left(\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\right)^{2}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle+2r_{x}\varepsilon^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq\left(r_{x}-\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\left(\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\right)^{2}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle}\right)^{2}+2\varepsilon^{\prime},

(3.12)

where we used that
r_{x}\leq 1
in the last step.

Second, from
Eq.

3.10
we see that the vector strategy
\{u_{x},v_{y}\}
is
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-optimal.
Thus it follows from
Eqs.

3.11
and
3.9
that

\displaystyle\left\lparen r_{x}-\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(z)}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi_{c\alpha}|\left(\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\right)^{2}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle}\right\rparen^{2}=\left\lparen r_{x}-\sum_{y}G_{xy}\langle u_{x}|v_{y}\rangle\right\rparen^{2}\leq 2(|I_{A}|+|I_{B}|)\beta_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G})\varepsilon^{\prime},

for all
x\in I_{A}
. The lemma follows from this and
Eq.

3.12
for
x=z
.
∎

The next theorem shows that in a near-optimal strategy, Bob’s operators nearly satisfy the relations of the solution algebra
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{G})
with respect to a certain expectation and state-dependent norm.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an XOR game with cost matrix
G
. If
S=(|\psi\rangle,\{A_{c\alpha}\},\{B_{yb}\})
is an
\varepsilon
-optimal quantum strategy for
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}}
,
then there exists a negligible function
\eta^{\prime}(\lambda)
such that for every
x\in I_{A}

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\|\left(r_{x}^{2}-\left(\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\right)^{2}\right)|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\|^{2}\leq C_{1}\varepsilon^{\prime},

where
\varepsilon^{\prime}=2\varepsilon+\eta^{\prime}(\lambda)
and

C_{1}=4\Big\lparen\left\lparen|I_{A}|+|I_{B}|\right\rparen\,\beta_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G})+1\Big\rparen\left\lparen r_{x}+\left\lparen\sum_{y}|G_{xy}|\right\rparen^{2}\right\rparen\leq 8\Big\lparen\left\lparen|I_{A}|+|I_{B}|\right\rparen\,\beta_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G})+1\Big\rparen

is a constant that depends only on the XOR game.

Proof.

Using the triangle inequality,
(x+y)^{2}\leq 2x^{2}+2y^{2}
and
\|B_{y}\|\leq 1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\|

\displaystyle\left(r_{x}^{2}\mathds{1}-\left(\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\right)^{2}\right)|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\|r_{x}^{2}\mathds{1}-(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)}r_{x}\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+2\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\|(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)}r_{x}\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}-\left(\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\right)^{2}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2r_{x}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\|r_{x}\mathds{1}-(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)}\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+2\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\|\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\|^{2}\|(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)}r_{x}\mathds{1}-\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(r_{x}+\left(\sum_{y}|G_{xy}|\right)^{2}\right)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\|r_{x}\mathds{1}-(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)}\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\|^{2}.

We deduce

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\|\left(r_{x}^{2}\mathds{1}-\left(\sum_{y}G_{xy}B_{y}\right)^{2}\right)|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\|^{2}\leq 2\Big\lparen(|I_{A}|+|I_{B}|)\beta_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G})+1\Big\rparen 2\left(r_{x}+\left(\sum_{y}|G_{xy}|\right)^{2}\right)\varepsilon^{\prime}

from
Lemma

3.5
.
∎

The previous theorem recovers the self-test for anticommuting operators in the CHSH game: Given the CHSH game with a uniform input distribution, we recall

\displaystyle|I_{A}|=|I_{B}|=2,\,G_{xy}=\frac{1}{4}(-1)^{xy},\,r_{x}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}\,\text{ and }\,\beta_{q}^{*}(CHSH)=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}.

Then,
Theorem

3.6
yields

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\|\left(B_{0}B_{1}+B_{1}B_{0}\right)|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\|^{2}\leq 16(2\sqrt{2}+1)(\sqrt{2}+1)(2\varepsilon+\sqrt{|\eta^{\prime}(\lambda)|})

for every
\varepsilon
-optimal quantum strategy
S=(|\psi\rangle,\{A_{c\alpha}\},\{B_{yb}\})
for the compiled CHSH game.

3.3
Parallel repetition of XOR games

We show an upper bound on the quantum value of the compiled parallel repetition of XOR games.
Throughout this section, we will treat the number of parallel repetitions
n
as fixed (in other words, as a constant in the security parameter).
However, we remark that a similar analysis can be used to establish the soundness of any polynomial number
n
of parallel repetitions, at the cost of a sub-exponential security guarantee for the QHE.
A similar tradeoff was also shown in
[
KLVY23
]
.

Definition 3.7
.

The
parallel repetition

\wedge_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{G}_{i}
of
n
nonlocal games
\mathcal{G}_{i}=(I_{A}^{i},I_{B}^{i},O_{A}^{i},O_{B}^{i},\pi_{i},V_{i})
is defined as the nonlocal game with input sets
I_{A}=\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}I_{A}^{i}
and
I_{B}=\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}I_{B}^{i}
, output sets
O_{A}=\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}O_{A}^{i}
and
O_{B}=\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}O_{B}^{i}
, probability distribution
\pi=\otimes_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i}
, and verification function
V(a,b,x,y)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}V_{i}(a_{i},b_{i},x_{i},y_{i})
.

While the conjunction of XOR games is not an XOR game, we can make the following definition.
We use the notation
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
.

Definition 3.8
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{i}
be XOR games with cost matrices
G_{i}
for
i\in[n]
, and let
M\subseteq[n]
.
Then the
sum (modulo two)

\oplus_{i\in M}\mathcal{G}_{i}
is defined as the XOR game with the cost matrix
G=\otimes_{i\in M}G_{i}
, i.e.
G_{xy}=\prod_{i\in M}(G_{i})_{x_{i}y_{i}}
for
x=(x_{i})_{i\in M}
and
y=(y_{i})_{i\in M}
.

We first discuss the results of
[
CSUU08
]
for nonlocal games.
Clearly, any quantum strategy
S
for
\wedge_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{G}_{i}
determines one for
\oplus_{i\in M}\mathcal{G}_{i}
, for any subset
M\subseteq[n]
, as follows:
Alice and Bob first proceed as in strategy
S
to obtain
a,b\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. They then output
\oplus_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}
and
\oplus_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}
, respectively.
Then one has the following relation between the quantum value of the parallel repetition and the bias of the sum games:

Lemma 3.9

(
[
CSUU08
, Lemma 8]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{i}
be XOR games for
i\in[n]
.
Then it holds for any strategy
S
for
\wedge_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{G}_{i}
that

\displaystyle\omega_{q}(S,\wedge_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{G}_{i})=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{M\subseteq[n]}\beta_{q}(S_{M},\oplus_{i\in M}\mathcal{G}_{i}).

It follows from this and the nontrivial result that the bias of XOR games is multiplicative with respect to taking sums
[
CSUU08
, Theorem 1]
that one has the upper bound

\displaystyle\omega_{q}^{*}(\wedge_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{G}_{i})\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{M\subseteq[n]}\beta_{q}^{*}(\oplus_{i\in M}\mathcal{G}_{i})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{i}),

(3.13)

The converse inequality holds for any nonlocal game, since the players can always play optimal strategies for the individual games
G_{i}
in parallel.
This establishes the parallel repetition theorem for XOR games
[
CSUU08
, Theorem 2]
:

\displaystyle\omega_{q}^{*}(\wedge_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{G}_{i})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{i}).

We now use a similar argument to obtain a repetition theorem in the compiled setting.
As above, we first argue that any quantum strategy
S
for the compiled parallel repetition
(\wedge_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{G}_{i})_{\mathrm{comp}}
determines a quantum strategy
S_{M}
for the compile sum games
(\oplus_{i\in M}\mathcal{G}_{i})_{\mathrm{comp}}
, for any subset
M\subseteq[n]
.
To this end, we modify step 2 such that if
S
were to output
\alpha
,
S_{M}
instead applies
\mathrm{Eval}
to
\alpha
to compute
\{0,1\}^{n}\ni a\mapsto\oplus_{i\in M}a_{i}
on the encrypted data and returns the new ciphertext
\alpha^{\prime}
.
1
1

1

Here we assume that the QHE scheme allows for more than one homomorphic evaluation, i.e., after given an evaluated ciphertext one can keep evaluating homomorphically any circuit of one’s choice. This property is satisfied by all known QHE schemes. We omit a formal definition of this property and we refer the reader to
[
GHV10
]
for details.

We also modify step 4 such that if
S
were to output
b\in\{0,1\}^{n}
,
S_{M}
instead outputs
\oplus_{i\in M}b_{i}
.
Then the following lemma can be proved similarly as
[
CSUU08
, Lemma 8]
above:

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{i}
be XOR games for
i\in[n]
.
Then it holds for any strategy
S
for
(\wedge_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{G}_{i})_{\mathrm{comp}}
that

\displaystyle\omega_{q}(S,(\wedge_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{G}_{i})_{\mathrm{comp}})=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{M\subseteq[n]}\beta_{q}(S_{M},(\oplus_{i\in M}\mathcal{G}_{i})_{\mathrm{comp}}).

By combining the preceding with
Theorem

3.1
, we can deduce the following parallel repetition for compiled XOR games.

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{i}
be XOR games for
i\in[n]
.
Then for any strategy
S
for
(\wedge_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{G}_{i})_{\mathrm{comp}}
there exists a negligible function
\eta(\lambda)
such that the following holds:

\displaystyle\omega_{q}^{*}(S,(\wedge_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{G}_{i})_{\mathrm{comp}})\leq\left\lparen\prod_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{i})\right\rparen+\eta(\lambda).

Conversely, there exists a quantum strategy
S
and a negligible function
\eta(\lambda)
such that

\displaystyle\omega_{q}^{*}(S,(\wedge_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{G}_{i})_{\mathrm{comp}})\geq\left\lparen\prod_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{i})\right\rparen-\eta(\lambda).

Proof.

By
Lemma

3.10
, we have

\displaystyle\omega_{q}(S,(\wedge_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{G}_{i})_{\mathrm{comp}})=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{M\subseteq[n]}\beta_{q}(S_{M},(\oplus_{i\in M}\mathcal{G}_{i})_{\mathrm{comp}}).

Since each
\oplus_{i\in M}\mathcal{G}_{i}
is an XOR game, and remembering the relation between the bias and the winning probability, we can apply
Theorem

3.1
and deduce from it that there exist negligible functions
\eta_{M}(\lambda)
such that

\displaystyle\beta_{q}(S_{M},(\oplus_{i\in M}\mathcal{G}_{i})_{\mathrm{comp}})\leq\beta^{*}_{q}(\oplus_{i\in M}\mathcal{G}_{i})+\eta_{M}(\lambda),

Using this and
Eq.

3.13
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\omega_{q}(S,(\wedge_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{G}_{i})_{\mathrm{comp}})\leq\left\lparen\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{M\subseteq[n]}\beta^{*}_{q}(\oplus_{i\in M}\mathcal{G}_{i})\right\rparen+\eta(\lambda)=\left\lparen\prod_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{i})\right\rparen+\eta(\lambda),

where
\eta:=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{M\subseteq[n]}\eta_{M}
is a negligible function.
This establishes the first claim.

The second follows at once from
Theorem

2.14
and the inequality
\omega_{q}^{*}(S,\wedge_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{G}_{i})\geq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{q}^{*}(S,\mathcal{G}_{i})
which as mentioned above holds for any nonlocal game.
∎

4
The SOS approach to bounding the compiled value

In this section, we provide an alternative proof of

4.1
SOS’s and bounding the value of nonlocal games

We briefly review the sum of squares (SOS) approach to bounding the commuting operator value
\omega^{*}_{qc}(\mathcal{G})
for a nonlocal game
\mathcal{G}
. The starting point is an abstract characterization of a Bell scenario. In particular, we model a two-party (non-communicating) measurement scenario with

\mathcal{A}_{n,m}:=\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{Z}^{*n}_{m}\times\mathbb{Z}^{*n}_{m}]

\mathcal{A}
for the scenario algebra.

2

2

If we let
a_{x}
and

to be the order

\mathcal{A}

\mathcal{A}
in terms of the orthogonal spectral projections
a^{x}_{a}
and
b^{y}_{b}

a^{x}_{a}=\frac{1}{m}\sum^{m-1}_{k=0}(\omega^{-a}a_{x})^{k},\qquad b^{y}_{b}=\frac{1}{m}\sum^{m-1}_{k=0}(\omega^{-b}b_{y})^{k}.

(4.1)

We briefly recall some more definitions. An element

is a (hermitian) square if

for some

. Moreover, we say an element of
\mathcal{A}
is a sum-of-squares (SOS) if it can be written as a sum of (hermitian) squares in
\mathcal{A}
. A linear functional
f:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is said to be
positive
if
f(x^{*}x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathcal{A}
. In this setting,
3
3

3

Here we are viewing the group algebra
\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{Z}^{*n}_{m}\times\mathbb{Z}^{*n}_{m}]
as a semi-pre-
C^{*}
-algebra, with the positive cone generated by sums of squares as described in
[
Oza13
]
. The corresponding universal
C^{*}
algebra is canonically isomorphic to
C^{*}(\mathbb{Z}^{*n}_{m})\otimes_{max}C^{*}(\mathbb{Z}^{*n}_{m})
.
an
expectation
on
\mathcal{A}
(also called an abstract state) is a linear functional
\E:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, that is both positive and unital (i.e
\E(\mathds{1})=1
). Every commuting operator strategy
\mathcal{S}=(|\psi\rangle,\{A_{xa}\},\{B_{yb}\})
determines an expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}}:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
. The expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}}
is determined by first applying the
*
-representation mapping
a_{xa}\mapsto A_{xa}
and
b^{y}_{b}\mapsto B_{yb}
, where the inner-product of the resulting operators with the state
|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
is then taken in the appropriate Hilbert space. Conversely, the GNS theorem for
*
-algebras
[
Sch20
,
Oza13
]
shows that every expectation
\E:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
gives rise to a commuting operator strategy
\mathcal{S}
.

Given a nonlocal game
\mathcal{G}
, the associated
game polynomial

p_{\mathcal{G}}:=\sum_{a,b,x,y}\pi(x,y)V(x,y,a,b)a^{x}_{a}b^{y}_{b},

(4.2)

is an element of the scenario algebra
\mathcal{A}
. Hence, we can realize the commuting operator value
\omega^{*}_{qc}(\mathcal{G})
as the supremum of
p_{\mathcal{G}}
over all expectations on
\mathcal{A}
. Furthermore, if
\nu\mathds{1}-p_{\mathcal{G}}
is expressible as an SOS then
\nu\geq\omega^{*}_{qc}(\mathcal{G})
. For all strict upper bounds
\nu>\omega^{*}_{qc}(\mathcal{G})
one can find an SOS decomposition for
\nu-p_{\mathcal{G}}

[
Oza13
]
. For the exact value
\nu=\omega^{*}_{qc}(\mathcal{G})
, it remains uncertain whether an SOS decomposition always exists
[
MSZ23
]
.

For some families of nonlocal games, it is more convenient to consider the
bias polynomial

h_{\mathcal{G}}
, rather than the game polynomial. For instance, in the binary output case, the bias polynomial is expressed in terms of the order 2 observables
a_{x}
and
b_{y}
. The supremum over expectations of
h_{\mathcal{G}}
is the commuting operator bias
\beta^{*}_{qc}(\mathcal{G})
, and obtaining a sum of squares decomposition for
\beta\mathds{1}-h_{\mathcal{G}}
certifies that
\beta
as an upper bound
\beta\geq\beta^{*}_{qc}\geq\beta^{*}_{q}
. An instance of this will be seen in
Section

4.3
, where we describe the bias polynomial of an XOR game
\mathcal{G}
and find associated SOS decomposition’s of
\beta\mathds{1}-h_{\mathcal{G}}
.

4.2
Bounding the compiled value using cryptographic pseudo-expectations

The SOS method for establishing an upper-bound on
\omega^{*}_{qc}(\mathcal{G})
relies on the correspondence between commuting operator strategies
\mathcal{S}
and expectations
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}}
on the scenario algebra
\mathcal{A}
. Despite not having a correspondence between quantum strategies for compiled games
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}}
and expectations, we will see that certain SOS decompositions for
h_{\mathcal{G}}
can be used to bound the compiled value.

To illustrate this approach suppose we have a sum-of-squares decomposition
\beta\mathds{1}-h_{\mathcal{G}}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}r_{i}^{\ast}r_{i}
and let
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathcal{A}
denote the subspace of
\mathcal{A}
that contains
\mathds{1}
and
r_{i}^{\ast}r_{i}
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
. Furthermore, suppose we have a function
\tilde{\E}:\mathcal{T}\to\mathbb{C}
, with the following properties:

1.

\tilde{\E}[\cdot]
is linear,

2.

\tilde{\E}[\mathds{1}]=1
,

3.

\tilde{\E}[h_{\mathcal{G}}]=\beta_{q}(S,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}})
, and

4.

For all
i\in\mathcal{I}
,
\tilde{\E}[r_{i}^{\ast}r_{i}]\geq-\delta
, for some
\delta\geq 0
.

Then,

\beta-\beta_{q}(S,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}})=\tilde{\E}\left[\beta\mathds{1}-h_{\mathcal{G}}\right]=\tilde{\E}\left[\sum_{i}r_{i}^{\ast}r_{i}\right]=\sum_{i}\tilde{\E}[r_{i}^{\ast}r_{i}]\geq-|\mathcal{I}|\delta,

giving us an upper-bound for
\beta_{q}(S,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}})
.

For every strategy for the compiled game
S
, we can define a “pseudo-expectation”
\tilde{\E}_{S}:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
that satisfies properties (1)-(3) and (4) with negligible
\delta
. In
Section

4.3
, we use this approach to bound the compiled value of all XOR games.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
S=(|\psi\rangle,\{A_{c\alpha}\},\{B_{yb}\})
be a quantum strategy for the compiled game. The
(degree 2) pseudo-expectation induced by
S
, is the function
\tilde{\E}_{S}
defined on all degree
2
monomials in
\mathcal{A}
by

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\left[a_{x}a_{z}\right]

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,\text{ if $x=z$}\\
0,\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

(4.3)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\left[a_{x}b_{y}\right]=\tilde{\E}_{S}\left[b_{y}a_{x}\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)}\langle\psi|(A_{c\alpha})^{*}B_{y}A_{c\alpha}|\psi\rangle

(4.4)

\displaystyle\tilde{\E}_{S}\left[b_{y}b_{w}\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{x}\E_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi|(A_{c\alpha})^{*}B_{y}B_{w}A_{c\alpha}|\psi\rangle,

(4.5)

for all
x,z\in I_{A}
and
y,w\in I_{B}
.

Remark 4.2
.

We note that the distribution over
x
in
Eq.

4.5
can be replaced with any efficiently sampleable distribution and the results below would still hold.

From the definition, we immediately obtain that
\tilde{\E}_{S}[\cdot]
is linear and unital. We next show that it is non-negative up to some negligible function on certain squares terms.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\tilde{\E}_{S}[\cdot]
be the degree 2 pseudo-expectation induced by a strategy for the compiled game
S
. Then

\tilde{\E}_{S}\left[\left(\sum_{y}\gamma_{y}b_{y}\right)^{2}\right]\geq 0,

where
\gamma_{y}\in\mathbb{R}
,
and there exists a negligible function of the security parameter
\lambda
such that

\tilde{\E}_{S}\left[\left(a_{x}-\sum_{y}\gamma_{x,y}b_{y}\right)^{2}\right]\geq-\eta(\lambda),

where
\gamma_{x,y}\in\mathbb{R}
. Note that the order of the quantifiers imply the negligible function
\eta
may depend on
S
.

Proof.

Let us fix a strategy for the compiled game
S=(|\psi\rangle,\{A_{c\alpha}\},\{B_{yb}\})
. To show the non-negativity of the first family of square terms we write

\displaystyle\tilde{\E}\left[\left(\sum_{y}\gamma_{y}b_{y}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{y,w}\gamma_{y}\gamma_{w}\tilde{\E}\left[b_{y}b_{w}\right]

(4.6)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y,w}\gamma_{y}\gamma_{w}\E_{x}\E_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi|(A_{c\alpha})^{\ast}B_{y}B_{w}A_{c\alpha}|\psi\rangle

(4.7)

\displaystyle=\E_{x}\E_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi|(A_{c\alpha})^{\ast}\left(\sum_{y,w}\gamma_{y}\gamma_{w}B_{y}B_{w}\right)A_{c\alpha}|\psi\rangle

(4.8)

\displaystyle=\E_{x}\E_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi|(A_{c\alpha})^{\ast}\left(\sum_{y}\gamma_{y}B_{y}\right)^{\ast}\left(\sum_{y}\gamma_{y}B_{y}\right)A_{c\alpha}|\psi\rangle

(4.9)

\displaystyle=\E_{x}\E_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi|\left(\left(\sum_{y}\gamma_{y}B_{y}\right)A_{c\alpha}\right)^{\ast}\left(\left(\sum_{y}\gamma_{y}B_{y}\right)A_{c\alpha}\right)|\psi\rangle

(4.10)

\displaystyle=\E_{x}\E_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\left\lVert\left(\sum_{y}\gamma_{y}B_{y}\right)|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\right\rVert^{2}\geq 0.

(4.11)

The proof for the second type of square terms depends on the security of the QHE scheme. Let
\mu_{x}
be the distribution on
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
generated by the following procedure:

1.

Sample a uniformly random encoding
c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)
.

2.

Measure
|\psi\rangle
with the observable
A_{c}
, obtaining outcome
\alpha
which is then decrypted as
a=(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)}
.

3.

Measure the post-measurement state with the observable
\hat{B}_{x}:=\sum_{y}\gamma_{x,y}B_{y}
, obtaining some outcome
b\in\mathbb{R}
.

Let
\hat{B}_{x}=\sum_{b}b\hat{B}_{xb}
be the spectral decomposition of
\hat{B}_{x}
. The expectation of
(a-b)^{2}
over
\mu_{x}
is then given by

\displaystyle\E_{\mu_{x}}\left[(a-b)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{b}\langle\psi|(A_{c\alpha})^{\ast}\hat{B}_{xb}A_{c\alpha}|\psi\rangle(a-b)^{2}

\displaystyle=\E_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{b}\langle\psi|(A_{c\alpha})^{\ast}\hat{B}_{xb}A_{c\alpha}|\psi\rangle(1-2(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)}b+b^{2})

\displaystyle=1-2\E_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha,b}\langle\psi|(A_{c\alpha})^{\ast}\hat{B}_{xb}A_{c\alpha}|\psi\rangle(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)}b+\E_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha,b}\langle\psi|(A_{c\alpha})^{\ast}\hat{B}_{xb}A_{c\alpha}|\psi\rangle b^{2}

\displaystyle=1-2\E_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)}\langle\psi|(A_{c\alpha})^{\ast}\hat{B}_{x}A_{c\alpha}|\psi\rangle+\E_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi|(A_{c\alpha})^{\ast}\hat{B}_{x}^{2}A_{c\alpha}|\psi\rangle.

Note that
\hat{B}_{x}
and
\hat{B}_{x}^{2}
have QPT-implementable block encodings with scale factor
\Theta(1)
by
Lemmas

2.16
,
2.17
and
2.18
. So by
Lemma

2.21
, there exists a negligible function
\eta(\lambda)
such that

\left|\E_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi|(A_{c\alpha})^{*}\hat{B}_{x}^{2}A_{c\alpha}|\psi\rangle-\E_{x^{\prime}}\E_{c^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x^{\prime})}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi|(A_{c^{\prime}\alpha})^{*}\hat{B}_{x}^{2}A_{c^{\prime}\alpha}|\psi\rangle\right|\leq\eta(\lambda).

(4.12)

Now, if we expand

\displaystyle\tilde{\E}\left[\left(a_{x}-\sum_{y}\gamma_{x,y}b_{y}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\tilde{\E}\left[a_{x}^{2}\right]-2\sum_{y}\gamma_{x,y}\tilde{\E}\left[a_{x}b_{y}\right]+\sum_{y,w}\gamma_{x,y}\gamma_{x,w}\tilde{\E}\left[b_{y}b_{w}\right]

\displaystyle=1-2\sum_{y}\gamma_{x,y}\E_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)}\langle\psi|(A_{c\alpha})^{*}B_{y}A_{c\alpha}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\sum_{y,w}\gamma_{xy}\gamma_{xw}\E_{x^{\prime}}\E_{c^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x^{\prime})}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi|(A_{c^{\prime}\alpha})^{*}B_{y}B_{w}A_{c^{\prime}\alpha}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=1-2\E_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(a)}\langle\psi|(A_{c\alpha})^{\ast}\left(\sum_{y}\gamma_{x,y}B_{y}\right)A_{c\alpha}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\E_{x^{\prime}}\E_{c^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x^{\prime})}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi|(A_{c^{\prime}\alpha})^{\ast}\left(\sum_{y}\gamma_{xy}B_{y}\right)^{2}A_{c^{\prime}\alpha}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=1-2\E_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(a)}\langle\psi|(A_{c\alpha})^{\ast}\hat{B}_{x}A_{c\alpha}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\E_{x^{\prime}}\E_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x^{\prime})}\sum_{\alpha}\langle\psi|(A_{c^{\prime}\alpha})^{\ast}\hat{B}_{x}^{2}A_{c^{\prime}\alpha}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\E_{\mu_{x}}\left[(a-b)^{2}\right]-\eta(\lambda)

\displaystyle\geq-\eta(\lambda),

where the second to last inequality follows from
Eq.

4.12
, completing the proof.
∎

Definition 4.4
.

We call a sum-of-squares decomposition
nice
if it is a non-negative linear combination of terms of the form given in
Theorem

4.3
.

Corollary 4.5
.

Suppose that nonlocal game
\mathcal{G}
has a nice sum-of-squares decomposition for
\beta\mathds{1}-h_{\mathcal{G}}
, then for any strategy for the compiled game
S
there exists a negligible function
\eta(\lambda)
of the security parameter such that

\beta_{q}(S,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}})\leq\beta+\eta(\lambda).

4.3
A nice SOS certificate for any XOR game

In this section, we exhibit a nice SOS decomposition for
\beta^{\ast}_{q}(\mathcal{G})\mathds{1}-h_{\mathcal{G}}
for every XOR game
\mathcal{G}
, where
\beta^{\ast}_{q}(\mathcal{G})
is the optimal quantum bias for
\mathcal{G}
and
h_{\mathcal{G}}
is the bias polynomial associated with
\mathcal{G}
. This, in a sense, is the dual view of Tsirelson’s theorem.

Recall that an XOR game
\mathcal{G}
is described by its cost matrix
G=(G_{xy})_{x\in I_{A},y\in I_{B}}
. The bias polynomial is expressed as

h_{\mathcal{G}}=\sum_{x\in I_{A},y\in I_{B}}G_{xy}a_{x}b_{y}\in\mathcal{A}.

Theorem 4.6
.

For any XOR game
\mathcal{G}
and any quantum strategy
S
for the compiled game,

\tilde{\mathbb{E}}_{S}[h_{\mathcal{G}}]=\beta_{q}(S,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}}).

Proof.

Fix a quantum strategy
S=(|\psi\rangle,\left\{A_{c\alpha}\right\},\left\{B_{yb}\right\})
, then

\displaystyle\tilde{\E}_{S}\left[h_{\mathcal{G}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in I_{A},y\in I_{B}}G_{xy}\tilde{\E}_{S}\left[a_{x}b_{y}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in I_{A},y\in I_{B}}G_{xy}\E_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha}(-1)^{\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)}\langle\psi|(A_{c\alpha})^{\ast}B_{y}A_{c\alpha}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in I_{A},y\in I_{B}}G_{xy}\sum_{a,b}\E_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha;\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)=a}(-1)^{a+b}\langle\psi|(A_{c\alpha})^{\ast}B_{yb}A_{c\alpha}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in I_{A},y\in I_{B}}\pi(x,y)\sum_{a,b}c_{xy}(-1)^{a+b}\E_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(x)}\sum_{\alpha;\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)=a}\langle\psi|(A_{c\alpha})^{\ast}B_{yb}A_{c\alpha}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=2\omega_{q}(S,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{comp}})-1.

∎

Let
\beta^{\ast}_{q}(\mathcal{G})
be the optimal quantum bias for
\mathcal{G}
. By
Theorem

2.10
for any optimal quantum strategy
S
for the XOR game
\mathcal{G}
the corresponding row and column biases
r_{x,S}
and
c_{y,S}
will be the same, which we simply denote as
r_{x}
and
c_{y}
.
For each
x\in I_{A}
we then define the following element of the scenario algebra,

\hat{b}_{x}:=\frac{1}{r_{x}}\sum_{y\in I_{B}}G_{xy}b_{y}.

We begin by expanding the following expression

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in I_{A}}\frac{r_{x}}{2}\left(a_{x}-\hat{b}_{x}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\beta\mathds{1}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in I_{A}}r_{x}\hat{b}_{x}^{2}-\sum_{x\in I_{A}}r_{x}a_{x}\hat{b}_{x}

\displaystyle=\beta\mathds{1}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in I_{A}}r_{x}\left(\hat{b}_{x}^{2}-\mathds{1}\right)-\sum_{x\in I_{A},y\in I_{B}}G_{xy}a_{x}b_{y}

\displaystyle=\beta\mathds{1}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in I_{A}}r_{x}\left(\hat{b}_{x}^{2}-\mathds{1}\right)-h_{\mathcal{G}}.

If we show that the negative of
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in I_{A}}r_{x}\left(\hat{b}_{x}^{2}-\mathds{1}\right)
has a sum-of-squares decomposition, then we would be done. Towards this,

-\sum_{x\in I_{A}}r_{x}\left(\hat{b}_{x}^{2}-\mathds{1}\right)=\beta\mathds{1}-\sum_{x\in I_{A}}r_{x}\hat{b}_{x}^{2}=\sum_{y\in I_{B}}c_{y}b_{y}^{2}-\sum_{x\in I_{A}}r_{x}\hat{b}_{x}^{2}

(4.13)

Now, let
\mathbf{b}
and
\mathbf{\hat{b}}
be the column vectors of
\{b_{y}:y\in I_{B}\}
and
\{\hat{b}_{x}:x\in I_{A}\}
, respectively. Similarly, we let
\mathbf{r}
and
\mathbf{c}
denote the vectors of
\left\{r_{x}\right\}
and
\left\{c_{y}\right\}
, respectively. With this, we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in I_{A}}r_{x}\hat{b}_{x}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\hat{b}}^{\top}\Delta(\mathbf{r})\mathbf{\hat{b}}

\displaystyle=\left(\Delta(\mathbf{r})^{-1}G\mathbf{b}\right)^{\top}\Delta(\mathbf{r})\left(\Delta(\mathbf{r})^{-1}G\mathbf{b}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{b}^{\top}G^{\top}\Delta(\mathbf{r})^{-1}G\mathbf{b}.

Putting this all together we obtain,

\sum_{y\in I_{B}}c_{y}b_{y}^{2}-\sum_{x\in I_{A}}r_{x}\hat{b}_{x}^{2}=\mathbf{b}^{\top}\Delta(\mathbf{c})\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{b}^{\top}G^{\top}\Delta(\mathbf{r})^{-1}G\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{b}^{\top}\left(\Delta(\mathbf{c})-G^{\top}\Delta(\mathbf{r})^{-1}G\right)\mathbf{b}.

By
Theorem

2.10
,

\begin{pmatrix}\Delta(\mathbf{r})&-G^{\top}\\
-G&\Delta(\mathbf{c})\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0.

In addition, the matrix
\Delta(\mathbf{r})
is invertible since each
r_{x}
is strictly positive. Hence, by the Schur complement condition for positivity, we obtain

M:=\Delta(\mathbf{c})-G^{\top}\Delta(\mathbf{r})^{-1}G\succeq 0.

Lastly, taking the spectral decomposition
M=\sum_{y\in I_{B}}\lambda_{y}\mathbf{v}_{y}\mathbf{v}_{y}^{\ast}
gives us the sum-of-squares decomposition

\sum_{y\in I_{B}}c_{y}b_{y}^{2}-\sum_{x\in I_{A}}r_{x}\hat{b}_{x}^{2}=\sum_{y\in I_{B}}\lambda_{y}\left(\sum_{w\in I_{B}}v_{yw}b_{w}\right)^{2},

where
\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{y}\mathbf{v}_{y}^{\ast}\mathbf{b}=\left(\sum_{w}v_{yw}b_{w}\right)^{2}
, for each
y\in I_{B}
. We summarize with the following theorem:

Theorem 4.7
.

For any XOR game
\mathcal{G}
, there is a sum-of-squares certificate for the optimal quantum bias
\beta
of the form

\displaystyle\beta\mathds{1}-h_{\mathcal{G}}=\sum_{x\in I_{A}}\frac{r_{x}}{2}\left(a_{x}-\hat{b}_{x}\right)^{2}+\sum_{y\in I_{B}}\frac{\lambda_{y}}{2}\left(\sum_{w\in I_{B}}v_{yw}b_{w}\right)^{2},

(4.14)

where each
v_{yw}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda_{y}
are non-negative for all
y,w\in I_{B}
.

Theorem

4.7
allows us to upper-bound any strategy for the compiled game for any XOR game in terms of the optimal quantum bias and the security parameter of the compilation scheme. Hence, we obtain our second proof of
Theorem

3.1
, stated in
Section

3
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

From
Corollary

4.5
and
Theorem

4.7
, we obtain the desired result.
∎

5
Magic square game

Consider the following set of equations

\displaystyle x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}=0\quad(r_{1}),

\displaystyle x_{1}+x_{4}+x_{7}=0\quad(c_{1}),

\displaystyle x_{4}+x_{5}+x_{6}=0\quad(r_{2}),

\displaystyle x_{2}+x_{5}+x_{8}=0\quad(c_{2}),

(5.1)

\displaystyle x_{7}+x_{8}+x_{9}=0\quad(r_{3}),

\displaystyle x_{3}+x_{6}+x_{9}=1\quad(c_{3}).

In the nonlocal
magic square game

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{MS}}
, the referee sends the index
i\in[6]
of one of the six equations to Alice, and the index
j\in S_{i}
of one of three variables in Alice’s equation to Bob.
Here,
S_{i}\subseteq[9]
denotes the index of the variables that appear in the
i
th equation.
That is,
S_{i}=\{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\}
if the
i
th equation contains variables
x_{s_{1}},x_{s_{2}},x_{s_{3}}
.
Alice answers with
\{0,1\}
-assignment
a=(a_{s_{1}},a_{s_{2}},a_{s_{3}})
and Bob answers with an
\{0,1\}
-assignment
b
to his variable
x_{j}
.
The players win the game, Alice’s assignments satisfy her equation and if two players’ assignments coincide in the common variable
x_{j}
.

The magic square game has a perfect quantum strategy, that is,
\omega_{q}^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{MS}})=1
.
Hence the same is true for the compiled game by
Theorem

2.14
.

Corollary 5.1
.

There exists a quantum strategy
S
for the compiled game
(\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{MS}})_{\mathrm{comp}}
and a negligible function
\eta(\lambda)
such that

\omega_{q}(S,(\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{MS}})_{\mathrm{comp}})\geq 1-\eta(\lambda).

In the remainder of this section, we want to prove that in any almost perfect quantum strategy for the compiled magic square game, we can find almost anticommuting Bob operators.
We first make a definition.

Definition 5.2
.

We call a quantum strategy
S
for
(\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{MS}})_{\mathrm{comp}}

\varepsilon
-perfect
if (for sufficiently large
\lambda
)

\displaystyle\omega_{q}(S,(\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{MS}})_{\mathrm{comp}})\geq 1-\varepsilon.

Note that for any
\varepsilon
-perfect quantum strategy
S=(|\psi\rangle,\{A_{c\alpha}\},\{B_{jb}\})
of the compiled magic square game, it holds

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b,i,j;\\
V(a,b,i,j)=0\end{subarray}}p(a,b|i,j)\leq 18\varepsilon,

where
p(a,b|i,j)
denotes the probability that the prover returns (an encryption of)
a
and
b
when given as questions (the encryption of)
i
and
j
.
That is, if the
i
th equation be of the form
x_{s_{1}}+x_{s_{2}}+x_{s_{3}}=d_{i}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\leftarrow\mathrm{Enc}(i)}\Bigg(\sum_{j\in S_{i}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a;\\
a_{s_{1}}+a_{s_{2}}+a_{s_{3}}=d_{i}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha;\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)=a}\|B_{j(a_{j}\oplus 1)}|\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a;\\
a_{s_{1}}+a_{s_{2}}+a_{s_{3}}\neq d_{i}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\alpha;\mathrm{Dec}(\alpha)=a}\||\psi_{c\alpha}\rangle\|^{2}\Bigg)\leq 18\varepsilon,

(5.2)

since
V(a,a_{j}\oplus 1,i,j)=0
and
V(a,b,i,j)=0
for all
b
if
a_{s_{1}}+a_{s_{2}}+a_{s_{3}}\neq d_{i}
.

We now prove a number of technical lemmas.
Define
B_{j}:=B_{j0}-B_{j1}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
