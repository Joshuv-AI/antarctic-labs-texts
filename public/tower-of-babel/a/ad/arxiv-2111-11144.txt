A Complete Axiom System for 1-Free Kleene Star Expressions under Bisimilarity: An Elementary Proof
Allan van Hulst

Abstract
Grabmayer and Fokkink recently presented a finite and complete axiomatization for 1-free process terms over the binary Kleene star under bismilarity equivalence (proceedings of LICS 2020, preprint available). A different and considerably simpler proof is detailed in this paper. This result, albeit still somewhat technical, only relies on induction and normal forms and is therefore also much closer to a potential rewriting algorithm. In addition, a complete verification in the Coq proof assistant of all results in this work is provided, but correctness does not depend upon any computer-assisted methodology.

Introduction

A completeness proof for a 1-free process theory modulo bisimilarity was recently
presented in
[
6
]

https://www.cs.vu.nl/~wanf/publications.html

Being somewhat daunted by the complexity of this proof provided a powerful incentive
to search for a simpler solution, which is presented in this work. This different and
considerably simpler proof only uses induction and normal forms, and has been additionally
verified by means of the Coq proof assistant.

This paper is an intermediate step in a research line towards the resolution of a question
originally posed in
[
7
]
: does there exist a finite and complete
axiomatization for the unary Kleene star under bisimilarity equivalence? This problem
is usually considered in the context of process theories including the constants 0
(deadlock), 1 (empty process) and the operators + (non-deterministic choice) and

\cdot
(sequential composition). Milner himself suggested that solving this problem
may involve a considerable effort
[
7
]
. It is clear that the question remains
unanswered to this day.

Earlier attempts in this direction include a completeness proof in absence of both
the constants 0 and 1 in
[
3
]
, and a variant where every Kleene star appears
as
p^{*}\cdot 0
in
[
4
]
. Completeness proofs for simpler theories (e.g.
without the Kleene star) can be found in any process algebra handbook (cf.
[
1
]
).

The new approach in
[
6
]
deviates from the rather syntactic treatment in earlier
works and instead takes the more semantic avenue of using process charts. This toolset is
applied to prove completeness for a 1-free process theory over the binary Kleene star
modulo bisimilarity. While novel and innovative, this results in a quite complex proof.

This paper presents a two-fold approach. First, it is shown that every process term
has a bisimulant in a normal form and of non-increased star nesting depth. In essence,
our normalization requirement conforms to the following congruence property:
if term
p
reduces to both
t
and
u
in one or more steps then bisimilarity of

t
and
u
is a consequence of bisimilarity of
t\cdot p\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0ptq
and
u\cdot p\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0ptq
. The second part of the proof applies induction towards the star
nesting depth and proves equality under the condition that one operand is normalized,
which is sufficient due to symmetry and transitivity.

This paper is set up in self-contained form. No claim is made that the supporting Coq
code is the most neat or elegant reflection of such a proof in formalized mathematics,
it just serves as an additional layer of verification.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section
2

contains a number of basic definitions and section
3
concerns
the soundness of the axiom system. These basic preliminaries are then followed
by three sections in which the the completeness proof is built from the ground up.
Section
4
defines a summation operator and proves some basic
results. Normal forms of terms are defined and shown to be derivable under
bisimilarity in section
5
. These results are then integrated
into a completeness proof in section
6
. The formalization of
the theory in the Coq proof assistant is detailed in section
7
,
which mainly serves to make this material more accessible to readers who are less
familiar with such techniques. A short concluding section
8

refines Milner’s completeness problem to a more detailed conjecture.

Definitions

We will mostly follow standard nomenclature and definitions in process algebra and
propose the books
[
1
]
and
[
5
]
as general reference texts. Throughout
this work, we will assume
A
to be a set of
actions
. There is no requirement that

A
is finite, as this proof concerns the completeness of closed terms only. Actions form
the elementary operations in the set of process terms
T
defined inductively as

T\mathbin{::=}\,\,0\mid A\mid T+T\mid T\cdot T\mid T\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0ptT.

In the process algebra
T
the constant
0
expresses deadlock (i.e.
a process exhibiting no behavior). Every action
a\in A
induces a step

a
to the special termination symbol
\surd
as its sole behavior (note
that
\surd
is not part of the algebra
T
). The operation
p+q

models a (possibly non-deterministic) choice between
p
and
q
, while the
sequential composition
p\cdot q
denotes the concatenation of the behaviors
of
p
and
q
. The binary Kleene star
p\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0ptq
models zero or more
iterations of
p
, possibly followed by
q
. Sequential composition is assumed
to be right-associative, while the other operators associate to the left. The
Kleene star binds stronger than sequential composition, which in turn binds
stronger than plus.

Process terms exhibit behavior defined as taking actions resulting in
either a new process term or the termination symbol
\surd
. We set

V=T\cup\{\surd\}
and define a relation
\longrightarrow\subseteq V\times A\times V
to formally capture this behavior. Assume that

p,q,p^{\prime},q^{\prime}\in T
,
v\in V
, and
a\in A
in the set of derivation rules listed
below, using the notation
p\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}q
for
(p,a,q)\in\longrightarrow
.

\displaystyle\frac{}{a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}\surd}

\displaystyle\frac{p\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}v}{p+q\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}v}

\displaystyle\frac{q\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}v}{p+q\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}v}

\displaystyle\frac{p\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}p^{\prime}}{p\cdot q\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}p^{\prime}\cdot q}

\displaystyle\frac{p\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}\surd}{p\cdot q\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}q}

\displaystyle\frac{p\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}p^{\prime}}{p\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0ptq\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}p^{\prime}\cdot p\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0ptq}

Bisimilarity is a coinductively defined relationship which relates process
terms in a lock-step fashion. The proof in
[
6
]
defines bisimilarity
in terms of process charts. As these constructs are not used in this
proof, we will employ a definition in terms of
V
.

Elements
u,v\in V
are bisimilar (notation
u\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}v
) if
there exists a relation
R\subseteq V\times V
such that

(u,v)\in R
and for all
(x,y)\in R
the following are satisfied

1.

x=\surd
if and only if
y=\surd
,

2.

for all
x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}x^{\prime}
there exists a
y^{\prime}\in V
such that

y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}y^{\prime}
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in R
, and

3.

for all
y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}y^{\prime}
there exists an
x^{\prime}\in V
such that

x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}x^{\prime}
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in R
.

Examples of pairs of bisimilar terms include
(a\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0ptb)\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0ptc\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}(a+b)\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0ptc

and
(a+aa)\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0pt0\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}a\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0pt0
, but the terms

a\cdot b+a\cdot c
and
a\cdot(b+c)
are not bisimilar.

(B2)

For all

3
Soundness

We briefly consider soundness of the theory to fulfill the objective
of being self-contained and to state a simple lemma which serves as a
very useful building block in the remainder of the proof.

Lemma 1
.

For all
p,q\in T
such that

1.

for all
p\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}p^{\prime}
there exists a
q\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}q^{\prime}
such that
p^{\prime}\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}q^{\prime}
and

2.

for all
q\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}q^{\prime}
there exists a
p\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}p^{\prime}
such that
p^{\prime}\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}q^{\prime}
,

it holds that
p\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}q
.

Soundness of the axiom RSP is neither deep nor straightforward.

Lemma 2
.

If
p\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}q\cdot p+r
then
p\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}q\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0ptr
, for all
p,q,r\in T
.

Proof.

Assume
R
is the witnessing relation for
p\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}q\cdot p+r
. It is
straightforward to show that the transitive closure
\overline{R}

of
R
is again a bisimulation. If
R^{\prime}
is defined as:

R^{\prime}=\{(p,q\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0ptr)\}\cup\overline{R}\cup\{(p^{\prime},q^{\prime}\cdot q\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0ptr)\mid(p^{\prime},q^{\prime}\cdot p)\in\overline{R}\}\cup\{(p^{\prime},q\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0ptr)\mid(p^{\prime},p)\in\overline{R}\}

then
R^{\prime}
can be chosen as a witnessing relation for
p\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}q\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0ptr
.
∎

Soundness of the theory is required as a lemma in the completeness proof.

Lemma 3
.

For all
p,q\in T
such that
p=q
it holds that
p\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}q
.

Proof.

Assume that
p=q
and apply induction towards the derivation tree of
p=q
.
Most of the cases can be resolved easily via Lemma
1
.
Soundness for the axioms B5 and BKS2 is only slightly more complicated.
Soundness for the axiom RSP is shown in Lemma
2
.
∎

4
Summation

Expressing process terms as sums forms a crucial step between algebraic
and semantic reasoning in the succeeding proofs. For example, the proof
of Lemma
5
becomes much easier once we are able to obtain
such sums under equality.

For finite sets
N\subseteq A\times V
we define the summation operator

\sigma:\{N\subseteq A\times V\}\rightarrow T
recursively by setting

\sigma(\emptyset)=0
and

\sigma(\{(a,u)\}\cup N)=\sigma(N)+\begin{cases}a&\textrm{if}\,\,u\,\,\textrm{equals}\,\,\surd\\
a\cdot u&\textrm{if}\,\,u\in T\\
\end{cases}

where the expression
\{(a,u)\}\cup N
refers to the disjoint union
(i.e.
(a,u)\not\in N
).

Lemma 4
.

For all
p\in T
there exists an
N\subseteq A\times V

such that
p=\sigma(N)
and for all
(a,u)\in A\times V
it holds
that
p\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}u
if and only if
(a,u)\in N
.

Proof.

Apply induction towards the structure of
p
. In case
p\equiv 0
, choose
\emptyset

for
N
. If
p\equiv a
for some
a\in A
, choose
\{(a,\surd)\}
for
N
. For the case

p\equiv p_{1}+p_{2}
, observe that in general
\sigma(N_{1}\cup N_{2})=\sigma(N_{1})+\sigma(N_{2})

always holds.

If
p\equiv p_{1}\cdot p_{2}
, first use induction to obtain
N_{1}
such that

p_{1}=\sigma(N_{1})
. Then, apply the following projection to each element

(a,u)\in N_{1}
: (1) if
u
equals
\surd
then project to
(a,p_{2})
, (2)
if
u\in T
then project to
(a,u\cdot p_{2})
and name the result
N^{\prime}
.
Subsequently, we have
p_{1}\cdot p_{2}=\sigma(N^{\prime})
using B5.

For the case
p\equiv p_{1}\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0ptp_{2}
, use BKS1 to rewrite as
p_{1}\cdot p_{1}\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0ptp_{2}+p_{2}
and treat the part
p_{1}\cdot p_{1}\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0ptp_{2}
in
the same way as for
p\equiv p_{1}\cdot p_{2}
.
∎

We apply Lemma
4
to obtain a useful intermediate result
stated in Lemma
5
, which is very similar in form to Lemma

1
. We define the predicate
\mathrm{teq}\,(u,v)
for all
u,v\in V
as shown below, where
\mathrm{teq}
stands for termination-or-(axiomatically)equal.

\mathrm{teq}\,(\surd,\surd)=\mathit{true}\qquad\mathrm{teq}\,(p,q)\iff p=q\,\,\textrm{if}\,\,p,q\in T

and define
\mathrm{teq}\,(u,v)
as
\mathit{false}
in all other cases.

Lemma 5
.

For all
p,q\in T
such that

1.

for all
p\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}p^{\prime}
there exists a
q\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}q^{\prime}
such that
\mathrm{teq}\,(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})
and

2.

for all
q\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}q^{\prime}
there exists a
p\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}p^{\prime}
such that
\mathrm{teq}\,(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})
,

it holds that
p=q
.

Proof.

Assume
p=\sigma(M)
and
q=\sigma(N)
for
M,N\subseteq A\times V

as derived by Lemma
4
. Rewrite as
p=\sigma(M)=\sigma(M)+\sigma(N)=\sigma(N)=q
and solve the two equalities
\sigma(M)=\sigma(M)+\sigma(N)
and

\sigma(N)=\sigma(M)+\sigma(N)
separately by induction towards the size of the
set that appears once in the respective equality.
∎

5
Normalization

In the remainder, let
p\longrightarrow^{*}q
denote the fact that
p
reduces to

q
in zero or more steps for
q\in T
. Similarly, we define
p\longrightarrow^{+}q

representing a reduction in one or more steps for
q\in T
.

The core of this completeness proof uses the result that terms in
T
can
be normalized under bisimilarity such that for every subterm
r\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0pts
and
all reductions
r\longrightarrow^{+}p
and
r\longrightarrow^{+}q
such that

p\cdot r\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0pts\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}q\cdot r\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0pts
it holds that
p\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}q
.
The precise meaning of ’subterm’ will become more clear in a short while. We require
a predicate
\mathrm{congr}
to express (a premise for) this congruence property:

\mathrm{congr}\,(p,q)\iff\textrm{for all}\,\,p\longrightarrow^{+}t:\,\,t\cdot q\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}q\,\,\textrm{does not hold}.

Informally, if there exists a
p\longrightarrow^{+}t
such that

t\cdot q\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}q
then
\mathrm{congr}\,(p,q)
is false, and true if no such

p\longrightarrow^{+}t
exists. We may now prove a crucial lemma.

Lemma 6
.

If
p\cdot r\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}q\cdot r
and
\mathrm{congr}\,(p,r)
and
\mathrm{congr}\,(q,r)

then
p\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}q
, for all
p,q,r\in T
.

Proof.

Assume
R
is a witnessing relation for
p\cdot r\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}q\cdot r

and define
R^{\prime}
as follows:

R^{\prime}=\{(\surd,\surd)\}\cup\{(p,q)\}\cup\{(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})\mid(p^{\prime}\cdot r,q^{\prime}\cdot r)\in R,\,p\longrightarrow^{+}p^{\prime},\,q\longrightarrow^{+}q^{\prime}\}

Assume there exists
(p^{\prime}\cdot r,q^{\prime}\cdot r)\in R
such that
(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})\in R^{\prime}
for some

p^{\prime},q^{\prime}\in T
. If there exists a step
p^{\prime}\cdot r\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}p^{\prime\prime}\cdot r
for
p^{\prime\prime}\in T

and a step
q^{\prime}\cdot r\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}r
(i.e. when
q^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}\surd
) such that

p^{\prime\prime}\cdot r\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}r
then this contradicts
\mathrm{congr}\,(p,r)
. The argument is
symmetric.
∎

The congruence property may be used to recursively define a normal form,
thereby making the notion of subterm with regard to the congruence property
more precise. Two remarks are important now.

First, suppose we have a term
(p\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0ptq)\cdot(r\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0pts)
then
two properties are desired:

1.

for
p\longrightarrow^{+}t
and
p\longrightarrow^{+}u
we have:

t\cdot p\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0ptq\cdot r\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0pts\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}u\cdot p\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0ptq\cdot r\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0pts

implies
t\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}u
and

2.

for
r\longrightarrow^{+}x
and
r\longrightarrow^{+}y
we have:

x\cdot r\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0pts\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}y\cdot r\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0pts
implies
x\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}y
.

Therefore, the cases for
p\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0ptq
and
r\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0pts
cannot be
separated into two conditions relying solely on
p\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0ptq
and

r\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0pts
. This is resolved by using a binary predicate to express
the fact that a term is normalized.

In general, the process algebra
T
does not have a neutral element under sequential
composition. This necessitates the definition of two slightly different
predicates for normal forms. Although this makes the proof a slightly more
difficult, this does not present a fundamental complication.

and
\mathrm{nf}

as shown below. Note that there is no mutual dependence between

\iff

\mathit{true}

\iff

\mathit{true}

\mathrm{nfmult}\,(p_{1},p_{1}\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0ptp_{2}\cdot q)\,\,\textrm{and}\,\,\mathrm{nfmult}\,(p_{2},q)\,\,\textrm{and}

\mathit{true}

\mathit{true}

6
Completeness

We will use the normal form obtained under bisimilarity in section
5

to finish the completeness proof. This requires some administrative work but is not very deep.
Clearly the implication
p\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}q\Rightarrow p=q
is our proof obligation. The
completeness proof uses induction towards
d(p)
. Lemma
12
can
be used to derive an

such that

and such that

\mathrm{nf}\,(r)
holds. By transitivity, these two equalities may be solved separately. This
mainly comes down to two steps where deriving axiomatic equality is mostly done by
invocations of Lemma
5
.

1.

Using Lemma
5
and induction to reduce to an equality of a form
similar to
t\cdot p\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0ptq=r\mathbin{{}^{\scalebox{1.1}{$*$}}}\hskip-2.0pts

and

Using

6

-induction hypothesis for completeness with

7
Formalization

All the results in this paper have been formalized and verified using
version 8.4pl4 of the Coq proof-assistant (
[
2
]
). The Coq code
is available at

https://github.com/allanvanhulst/mscs/

We import Coq-libraries for lists, maximality natural numbers, and for Presburger arithmetic.

Require Import List.
Require Import Max.
Require Import Omega.

We then encode the law of the excluded middle (LEM) as an additional
axiom. The addition of this axiom is consistent with Coq (cf.
[
2
]
).

Axiom LEM : forall (P : Prop), P \/ ~ P.

The set of actions
A
is a parameter for the theory
T
.

Parameter A : Set.

Inductive T :=
| zero : T
| act : A -> T
| plus : T -> T -> T
| mult : T -> T -> T
| star : T -> T -> T.

Notations for 0, plus, multiplication and the binary Kleene-star are
subsequently introduced. The Unicode character
\cdot
is used for
sequential composition. Note that non-ASCII characters are valid
within the Coq proof assistant but may render in different ways on
different platforms. Moreover, the operators for plus and Kleene-star
are overloaded and assigned a new meaning with regard to
T
here. This
is perfectly valid in Coq but due to parsing limitations, one cannot
re-assign associativity or precedence to an already existing operator.
However, this is not a problem here.

Notation "0" := zero.
Notation "p + q" := (plus p q).
Notation "p · q" := (mult p q) (at level 45, right associativity).
Notation "p * q" := (star p q).

We then define the set
V
as an inductive construction
for either
\surd
(i.e.
term
) or the constructor

\texttt{emb}:T\rightarrow V
.

Inductive V :=
| term : V
| emb : T -> V.

The transition relation
\longrightarrow\subseteq V\times A\times V
is then defined as a ternary predicate
having a corresponding notation
p -(a)-> q
.

Inductive step : V -> A -> V -> Prop :=
| step_act : forall (a : A), step (emb (act a)) a term
| step_plus_left : forall (p q : T) (v : V) (a : A),
step (emb p) a v -> step (emb (p + q)) a v
| step_plus_right : forall (p q : T) (v : V) (a : A),
step (emb q) a v -> step (emb (p + q)) a v
| step_mult_left : forall (p q p’ : T) (a : A),
step (emb p) a (emb p’) -> step (emb (p · q)) a (emb (p’ · q))
| step_mult_right : forall (p q : T) (a : A),
step (emb p) a term -> step (emb (p · q)) a (emb q)
| step_star_left : forall (p q p’ : T) (a : A),
step (emb p) a (emb p’) -> step (emb (p * q)) a (emb (p’ · (p * q)))
| step_star_term : forall (p q : T) (a : A),
step (emb p) a term -> step (emb (p * q)) a (emb (p * q))
| step_star_right : forall (p q : T) (a : A) (v : V),
step (emb q) a v -> step (emb (p * q)) a v.

Notation "p ’-(’ a ’)->’ q" := (step p a q) (at level 30).

Bisimilarity then directly follows the definition in this paper.

Definition bisim (u v : V) : Prop := exists (R : V -> V -> Prop),
R u v /\ forall (x y : V), R x y ->
(x = term <-> y = term) /\
(forall (a : A) (x’ : V), x -(a)-> x’ ->
exists (y’ : V), y -(a)-> y’ /\ R x’ y’) /\
(forall (a : A) (y’ : V), y -(a)-> y’ ->
exists (x’ : V), x -(a)-> x’ /\ R x’ y’).

The simplest way to encode the axioms in Coq while having the most
possible certainty that no interference with the internal Coq-axioms
takes place is to inductively define axiomatic equality as a binary
predicate. This makes the proof somewhat cumbersome. For instance,
every instance where transitivity or symmetry of the axiom system is
applied needs to be specified explicitly. Essentially, we are encoding
the derivation of axiomatic equality as a proof-tree here.

Inductive ax : T -> T -> Prop :=
| refl : forall (x : T), ax x x
| symm : forall (x y : T), ax x y -> ax y x
| trans : forall (x y z : T), ax x y -> ax y z -> ax x z
| comp_plus : forall (w x y z : T), ax w y -> ax x z -> ax (w + x) (y + z)
| comp_mult : forall (w x y z : T), ax w y -> ax x z -> ax (w · x) (y · z)
| comp_star : forall (w x y z : T), ax w y -> ax x z -> ax (w * x) (y * z)
| B1 : forall (x y : T), ax (x + y) (y + x)
| B2 : forall (x y z : T), ax ((x + y) + z) (x + (y + z))
| B3 : forall (x : T), ax (x + x) x
| B4 : forall (x y z : T), ax ((x + y) · z) (x · z + y · z)
| B5 : forall (x y z : T), ax ((x · y) · z) (x · (y · z))
| B6 : forall (x : T), ax (x + 0) x
| B7 : forall (x : T), ax (0 · x) 0
| BKS1 : forall (x y : T), ax (x · (x * y) + y) (x * y)
| BKS2 : forall (x y z : T), ax ((x * y) · z) (x * (y · z))
| RSP : forall (x y z : T), ax x (y · x + z) -> ax x (y * z).

The notation
<=>
is used for bisimilarity, and the
notation
==
is used for axiomatic equality.

Notation "u ’<=>’ v" := (bisim u v) (at level 25).
Notation "p ’==’ q" := (ax p q) (at level 25).

This is all the Coq-code required to express the completeness theorem
at the very end of the Coq-proof.

Theorem completeness : forall (p q : T), emb p <=> emb q -> p == q.

The Coq-proof in its present form consists of 3959 lines of code,
and is divided into 73 lemmas and one theorem. Note that most of the
lemmas are very simple. An example is shown below.

Lemma step_plus_fmt : forall (p q : T) (a : A) (u : V),
emb (p + q) -(a)-> u -> emb p -(a)-> u \/ emb q -(a)-> u.
Proof.
intros p q a u H ; inversion H ; auto.
Qed.

Conclusions

Compared to the proof method proposed in
[
6
]
, the
completeness result presented in this work is simpler, although a rather
technical treatment was essentially unavoidable. The formalization in the
Coq proof assistant ensures guaranteed correctness of the result. The method
is not very distant to an algorithmic rewriting procedure and an actual
implementation may be constructed by reasonable effort.

The theory and axiom system originally considered by Milner
[
7
]
allows for a
congruence property to be formulated in a very similar way as was done here.
Again, we require that for all
p\longrightarrow^{+}t
and
p\longrightarrow^{+}u

we have
t\cdot p^{*}\cdot q\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}u\cdot p^{*}\cdot q
implies
t\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}u
.
This follows if for all
p\longrightarrow^{+}t
the following two conditions are
satisfied

1.

if
t\cdot p^{*}\cdot q\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}(t+1)\cdot p^{*}\cdot q
then
t\downarrow
and

2.

if
t\downarrow
and
t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}t^{\prime}
then there does not exist a step

p^{*}\cdot q\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}r
such that
t^{\prime}\cdot p^{*}\cdot q\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}r
.

It is not unlikely that the difficulty in obtaining such normal forms lies
almost entirely in ensuring that the first of these two conditions is met.
Assume that the predicate
\mathrm{nfmult}
now expresses this new congruence
property recursively and further assume that the theory
T
now has the unary
Kleene star and 1. Resolving the following conjecture may be the key to finding
a solution to Milner’s question.

Conjecture 1
.

For
p\in T
there exists a
q\in T
such that

p\mathbin{\underline{\leftrightarrow}}q
and
\mathrm{nfmult}\,(q,1)
and
d(q)\leq d(p)
.

At this point, it cannot be excluded that Conjecture
1
can
be resolved using a proof method similar to the one applied in this paper.
