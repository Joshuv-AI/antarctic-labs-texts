Mathematical models for nonlinear ultrasound contrast imaging with microbubbles
Vanja Nikolić, Teresa Rauscher

Abstract
Ultrasound contrast imaging is a specialized imaging technique that applies microbubble contrast agents to traditional medical sonography, providing real-time visualization of blood flow and vessels. Gas-filled microbubbles are injected into the body, where they undergo compression and rarefaction and interact nonlinearly with the ultrasound waves. Therefore, the propagation of sound through a bubbly liquid is a strongly nonlinear problem that can be modeled by a nonlinear acoustic wave equation for the propagation of the pressure waves coupled via the source terms to a nonlinear ordinary differential equation of Rayleigh–Plesset type for the bubble dynamics. In this work, we first derive a hierarchy of such coupled models based on constitutive laws. We then focus on the coupling of Westervelt’s acoustic equation to Rayleigh–Plesset type equations, where we rigorously show the existence of solutions locally in time under suitable conditions on the initial pressure-microbubble data and final time. Thirdly, we devise and discuss numerical experiments on both single-bubble dynamics and the interaction of microbubbles with ultrasound waves.

Introduction

Ultrasound imaging is a crucial tool in medical diagnostics due to its non-invasive nature, real-time capability, and versatility. However, it has limitations in terms of precision. To address this issue, contrast agents based on microbubbles were introduced. These agents consist of microbubbles with a gas core encapsulated by a shell that resonates and produces echoes when exposed to ultrasound waves. This technique, known as
ultrasound contrast imaging
, shows strong nonlinear effects due to the high-frequency pressure waves in the tissue, the interaction of sound waves with the bubbles, and the behavior of the microbubbles themselves. The bubbles can form, expand, or collapse – a process known as acoustic cavitation. Acoustic cavitation can be non-inertial, involving stable periodic oscillations, or inertial, involving rapid growth and violent collapse of the bubbles. To minimize potential tissue damage, inertial cavitation is typically avoided. Properly managing pressure in the focal region is critical to optimizing the imaging process, that is, ensuring clear images while maintaining safety and effectiveness in ultrasound contrast imaging technologies. In particular, monitoring the nonlinear effects can help maintain the balance between achieving high-quality imaging and minimizing unwanted cavitation effects.

With this motivation in mind, in this work we investigate the complex nonlinear problem of ultrasound contrast imaging from a mathematical side, focusing on non-inertial cavitation.
The problem of modeling and simulation of ultrasound contrast agents has recently gained attention; see, for example,
[

4
]
and the references contained therein.
Our main contributions in this regard are threefold. They pertain to (i) the derivation of the coupled nonlinear models of ultrasound-microbubble interactions, (ii) their rigorous mathematical analysis, and (iii) their numerical
study

The models, derived in Section
2
starting from constitutive laws, are based on a volume coupling of a PDE for the propagation of ultrasound waves with an ODE for the microbubble dynamics. Among them is the system based on the damped Westervelt’s equation for the acoustic pressure
p=p(x,t)
in bubbly media. This wave equation is given by

\displaystyle((1+2k(x)p)p_{t})_{t}-c^{2}\Delta p-b\Delta p_{t}=c^{2}\eta v_{tt},

(2)

where

R

is capable of capturing prominent nonlinear effects in acoustic wave propagation, such as the steepening of the wave front and generation of higher harmonics. In (
2

b
the sound diffusivity, and the coefficient
\eta
is related to the number of microbubbles per unit volume. The nonlinearity coefficient

is allowed to vary in space, as relevant for imaging applications such as
acoustic nonlinearity tomography

[
16

15
]

When coupling (

These equations are derived under the assumptions that the bubble size is much smaller than the pressure wavelength, which, in turn, means we may assume that microbubbles remain spherical as they oscillate. The equations describe the variation of the bubble radius

\displaystyle\rho_{l0}\left[RR_{tt}+\tfrac{3}{2}R_{t}^{2}\right]=\,p_{\textup{int}}-p_{\textup{ext}},

2
Derivation of nonlinear acoustic models in bubbly media

In this section, we derive a hierarchy of second-order wave equations for the propagation of sound through a bubbly media starting from a governing system of equations and then couple them to models of microbubble dynamics. To this end, we introduce the following quantities, which are decomposed into their mean and alternating part denoted by
\cdot_{0}
and
\cdot^{\prime}
, respectively:

\displaystyle\text{density of the mixture:}

\displaystyle\rho=\rho_{0}+\rho^{\prime},

\displaystyle\text{density of the liquid:}

\displaystyle\rho_{l}=\rho_{l0}+\rho_{l}^{\prime},

(4)

\displaystyle\text{pressure of the liquid:}

\displaystyle p=p_{0}+p^{\prime},

\displaystyle\text{gas volume fraction:}

\displaystyle\mathcal{m}=\mathcal{m}_{0}+\mathcal{m}^{\prime},

\displaystyle\text{bubble number density:}

\displaystyle n=n_{0}+n^{\prime},

\displaystyle\text{bubble radius:}

\displaystyle R=R_{0}+R^{\prime},

\displaystyle\text{liquid and gas velocity:}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}.

In our setting the bubbles are assumed small and densely distributed such that they determine the properties of a continuum mixture of density
\rho
. The volume concentration of microbubbles is assumed to be uniform. As
\mathcal{m}
is the gas volume fraction, that is the fraction of unit volume of mixture occupied by microbubbles, we have

\mathcal{m}=nv\ \text{ with }\ v=\frac{4}{3}\pi R^{3},

(5)

where
n
is the bubble number density, or more precisely the concentration of bubbles per unit volume, and
R
the bubble radius. Then the continuum density is given by

\rho=(1-\mathcal{m})\rho_{l}+\mathcal{m}\rho_{g},

(6)

where
\rho_{l}
and
\rho_{g}
are the liquid and gas densities in a suspension of gas bubbles in liquid, respectively. We assume that the mean pressure
p_{0}
is the same in bubbles and liquid and that all bubbles have the same equilibrium radius
R_{0}
.

2.1
Approach in the derivation

In the upcoming derivation, we adopt the approach of Crighton described in
[
7
]
, following Lighthill’s scheme introduced in
[
22
]
and elaborated by Blackstock in
[
3
]
; see also
[
11
, Ch. 5]
. According to this approach, we distinguish three categories of contributions:

•

First order. First-order contributions are terms that are linear with respect to the fluctuating quantities
(\cdot)^{\prime}
and do not relate to dissipative effects.

•

Second order. Second-order contributions are linear dissipative terms and terms that are quadratic with respect to fluctuating quantities.

•

Higher order. All remaining terms are considered to be of higher order.

The fundamental rule in Lighthill’s approximation scheme is that one should keep first- and second-order terms while higher order contributions are neglected. A further approximation rule that we make use of below is the
substitution corollary
that permits us to substitute each term with second- or higher-order terms with its first-order approximation. In addition, we assume in this section that the fluctuating quantities are zero at initial time.

Derivation of acoustic equations in bubbly liquids

The acoustic field is fully described by the conservation of mass for the mixture

\rho_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\boldsymbol{u})=0,

(7)

denotes the parameter of nonlinearity and

2.2.4
Nonlinear acoustic equations for bubbly fluids

To arrive at bubbly media counterparts of classical models of nonlinear acoustics, we now consider the case that the bubble number density is constant in the equilibrium state, such that
n=n_{0}
and hence
\mathcal{m}=n_{0}v
.
Additionally, we assume that the mean volume fraction occupied by gas is negligible
(\mathcal{m}_{0}\approx 0)
, allowing us to approximate
\rho_{0}\approx\rho_{l0}
.

\bullet

Nonlinear in
v^{\prime}
. Considering all terms up to second order for
p^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{m}^{\prime}=n_{0}v
, we can reduce (
37
) to

\frac{1}{c^{2}}p^{\prime}_{tt}\negthickspace-\negmedspace\Delta p^{\prime}\negthickspace-\negmedspace\frac{b}{c^{2}}\Delta p^{\prime}_{t}\negthickspace-\negmedspace\frac{1}{\rho_{0}c^{4}}\tfrac{B}{2A}(p^{\prime 2})_{tt}\negthickspace-\negmedspace\frac{\rho_{0}}{c^{2}}(\boldsymbol{u}\negthinspace\cdot\negthinspace\boldsymbol{u})_{tt}\negthickspace=\rho_{0}n_{0}v^{\prime}_{tt}\negmedspace+\negmedspace n_{0}v^{\prime}\Delta p^{\prime}\negthickspace+\negmedspace\frac{\rho_{0}n_{0}^{2}}{2}(v^{\prime 2})_{tt},

(38)

where
b=\frac{\gamma}{\rho_{0}}\left(\frac{1}{c_{v}}-\frac{1}{c_{p}}\right)
is the sound diffusivity.
This is an inhomogeneous version of the Kuznetsov equation
[
18
]
with nonlinear coupling terms on the right-hand side. With the commonly used approximation

\|\boldsymbol{u}\|=\sqrt{\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}}\approx\left|\tfrac{1}{c\rho_{0}}p^{\prime}\right|

(valid if the propagation distance is much larger than a wavelength), we can reduce equation (
38
) to the inhomogeneous Westervelt equation given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c^{2}}p^{\prime}_{tt}-\Delta p^{\prime}-\frac{b}{c^{2}}\Delta p^{\prime}_{t}-\frac{\beta}{\rho_{0}c^{4}}(p^{\prime 2})_{tt}=\rho_{0}n_{0}v^{\prime}_{tt}+n_{0}v^{\prime}\Delta p^{\prime}+\frac{\rho_{0}n_{0}^{2}}{2}(v^{\prime 2})_{tt}

(39)

with
\beta=\frac{B}{2A}+1
.

\bullet

Linear in
v^{\prime}
. Retaining only first-order terms for
\mathcal{m}=n_{0}v
permits further reduction of equation (
37
) to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c^{2}}p^{\prime}_{tt}-\Delta p^{\prime}-\frac{b}{c^{2}}\Delta p^{\prime}_{t}-\frac{1}{\rho_{0}c^{4}}\tfrac{B}{2A}(p^{\prime 2})_{tt}-\frac{\rho_{0}}{c^{2}}(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{u})_{tt}=\rho_{0}n_{0}v^{\prime}_{tt}.

(40)

Equation (
40
) is an inhomogeneous Kuznetsov equation with the right-hand side terms that are linear in
v^{\prime}
. By reasoning as before, we obtain the following version of the inhomogeneous Westervelt equation:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c^{2}}p^{\prime}_{tt}-\Delta p^{\prime}-\frac{b}{c^{2}}\Delta p^{\prime}_{t}-\frac{\beta}{\rho_{0}c^{4}}(p^{\prime 2})_{tt}=\rho_{0}n_{0}v^{\prime}_{tt},

(41)

which will be at the focal point of our theoretical and numerical investigations.
Observe that equation (
41
) has been derived by taking into account all terms up to second order for
p^{\prime}
but retaining only terms that are linear in
v^{\prime}
.

2.3
Coupling to microbubble dynamics

Figure 1
:
Single-microbubble dynamics; adapted from
[
20
]

Solving equation (
41
) requires an additional relation between the sound pressure and the microbubble volume and we therefore couple it with a Rayleigh–Plesset-type equation in terms of the bubble radius
R
together with the volume-radius relation
v=\frac{4}{3}\pi R^{3}
. As already mentioned, many different models exist for describing the oscillating dynamics of microbubbles. We provide an overview here and refer to, e.g., the book
[
5
]
and the review papers
[
31
,
9
]
for further details.

Starting from

\rho_{l0}\left[RR_{tt}+\tfrac{3}{2}R_{t}^{2}\right]=\,p_{\textup{int}}-p_{\textup{ext}}

(42)

where we recall that
\rho_{l0}
is the mean mass density of the liquid and
p_{\textup{int}}
the internal pressure, the simplest Rayleigh–Plesset equation is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{l0}\left[RR_{tt}+\tfrac{3}{2}R_{t}^{2}\right]=p_{\textup{b}}-4\mu\frac{R_{t}}{R}-p^{\prime},

(43)

where
p_{\textup{b}}=p_{\textup{v}}-p_{\textup{stat}}
,
p_{\textup{v}}
is the vapor pressure,
p_{\textup{stat}}
is the static ambient pressure, and
\mu
is the viscosity of the medium; see Figure
1
.
A modified equation is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{l0}\left[RR_{tt}+\tfrac{3}{2}R_{t}^{2}\right]=p_{\textup{b}}-4\mu\dfrac{R_{t}}{R}-\dfrac{2\sigma}{R}-p^{\prime},

(44)

where
\sigma
is the surface tension between the liquid and vapor/gas at the bubble wall.

A further extension of the ODE arises from taking bubble contents into considerations. A generalized Rayleigh–Plesset equation, also known as the RPNNP (which stands for Rayleigh–Plesset–Noltingk–Neppiras–Poritsky) equation is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{l0}\left[RR_{tt}+\tfrac{3}{2}R_{t}^{2}\right]=\,p_{\textup{b}}-4\mu\dfrac{R_{t}}{R}-\dfrac{2\sigma}{R}+p_{\textup{pgn}}\left(\dfrac{R_{0}}{R}\right)^{3\kappa}-p^{\prime};

(45)

see
[
30
,
28
]
. Here
p_{\textup{pgn}}
is the (constant) gas pressure inside the bubble at rest given by
p_{\textup{pgn}}=\frac{2\sigma}{R_{0}}-p_{\textup{b}};

see
[
20
]
. Furthermore,
R_{0}
in (
45
) is the equilibrium radius, i.e., the radius of a bubble at rest and
\kappa\geq 1
is the adiabatic index.

\bullet

Incorporating acoustic radiation
. Other versions of microbubble models arise from considering damping of the bubble dynamics by the sound radiated by the bubble. A model that incorporates sound radiation is given by (see
[
31
]
)

\displaystyle\rho_{l0}\left[RR_{tt}+\tfrac{3}{2}R_{t}^{2}\right]=\,p_{\textup{b}}-4\mu\dfrac{R_{t}}{R}-\dfrac{2\sigma}{R}+p_{\textup{pgn}}\left(\dfrac{R_{0}}{R}\right)^{3\kappa}\left(1-3\kappa\dfrac{R_{t}}{c}\right)-p^{\prime}.

(46)

\bullet

Coated-bubble dynamics
. The coating of bubbles is known to stabilize them and prolong their existence; see, e.g.,
[
31
]
. In this case, the size-dependent effective surface tension can given by
\sigma(R)=\chi\bigl(\tfrac{R^{2}}{R_{0}^{2}}-1\bigr)
,
where
\chi
is the shell elasticity. The dynamics of microbubbles with thin shells can then be described by

\displaystyle\rho_{l0}\left[RR_{tt}+\tfrac{3}{2}R_{t}^{2}\right]=\,p_{\textup{b}}-4\mu\frac{R_{t}}{R}-\frac{2\sigma(R)}{R}+p_{\textup{pgn}}\left(\frac{R_{0}}{R}\right)^{3\kappa}\left(1-3\kappa\frac{R_{t}}{c}\right)-4\kappa_{s}\frac{R_{t}}{R^{2}}-p^{\prime}

(47)

with
p_{\textup{pgn}}=\frac{2\sigma_{0}}{R_{0}}-p_{\textup{b}}
, where
\sigma_{0}
is the surface tension of the bubble at rest and
\kappa_{s}
is the surface dilatational viscosity of the shell; see
[
31
, eq. (8)]
.

Generalized ODE
. Going forward, in the analysis, we assume
\rho_{0}\approx\rho_{l0}
and consider the following mathematically generalized equation for the dynamics of microbubbles:

\displaystyle\rho_{0}\left[RR_{tt}+\tfrac{3}{2}R_{t}^{2}\right]\begin{multlined}=h_{0}(R,R_{t})-p^{\prime},\end{multlined}

(48)

where we impose local Lipschitz continuity on the function
h_{0}
; see Theorem
2
for details. The assumptions allow having

\displaystyle h_{0}(R,R_{t})=\begin{multlined}p_{\textup{b}}-\frac{4\mu}{R}R_{t}-\frac{2\sigma(R)}{R}+p_{\textup{pgn}}\left(\frac{R_{0}}{R}\right)^{3\kappa}{\left(1-3\kappa_{0}\frac{R_{t}}{c}\right)}{-4\kappa_{s}\dfrac{R_{t}}{R^{2}}}\end{multlined}

(49)

with
\sigma
constant or
\sigma(R)=\chi\left(\tfrac{R^{2}}{R_{0}^{2}}-1\right)
and
\kappa_{s}
,
\kappa_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
so that equation (
46
) (with
\kappa_{0}=\kappa
) and equation (
45
) (with
\kappa_{0}=\kappa_{s}=0
) are covered by our theoretical results. The ODE will then be coupled to the damped Westervelt equation for the pressure, using the fact that
v^{\prime}_{tt}=v_{tt}
to arrive at the right-hand side. When considering this coupled system,
R=R(x,t)
is a function of both time and space. Thus, the equation governing the dynamics of microbubbles is an ODE defined pointwise in space.

3
Analysis of the Westervelt–Rayleigh–Plesset system

In this section, we analyze the Westervelt–Rayleigh–Plesset system. For simplicity of notation, we drop
(\cdot)^{\prime}
when denoting fluctuating pressure quantities and use
p
instead of
p^{\prime}
, but we keep the notation
R=R_{0}+R^{\prime}
for the total bubble radius. We consider the following initial boundary-value problem:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &((1+2k(x)p)p_{t})_{t}-c^{2}\Delta p-b\Delta p_{t}=\xi(R^{3})_{tt}\quad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\[2.84526pt]
&(p,p_{t})_{|t=0}=(p_{0},p_{1}),\quad p_{|\partial\Omega}=0,\\[2.84526pt]
&\rho_{0}\left[RR_{tt}+\tfrac{3}{2}R_{t}^{2}\right]\begin{multlined}=h_{0}(R,R_{t})-p\qquad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),\end{multlined}\\[2.84526pt]
&(R,R_{t})_{|t=0}=(R_{0},R_{1}),\end{aligned}\right.

(50)

with
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
(corresponding to
\frac{4}{3}\pi c^{2}\eta
in the derivation).
The aim of this section is to determine the conditions on the initial pressure-microbubble data under which (
50
) has a unique solution.

Notation

Below we use
x\lesssim y
to denote
x\leq Cy
, where
C>0
is a generic positive constant which may depend on the final time in such a manner that it tends to
+\infty
as
T\rightarrow+\infty
. We use the notation
{H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)}=\,H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
.
When denoting norms in Bochner spaces, we omit the temporal domain; for example,
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}(L^{q}(\Omega))}
denotes the norm in
L^{p}(0,T;L^{q}(\Omega))
.

We recall first a well-posedness result for the Westervelt equation for non-bubbly media with strong attenuation, which is needed to set up the analysis of the coupled problem. This form of Westervelt’s equation has been extensively studied in the mathematical literature; see, e.g.,
[
13
,
25
,
14
,
16
]
and the references provided therein. One of the key aspects of its analysis is ensuring that the factor
1+2kp
next to the second time derivative of the pressure stays positive (i.e., that it does not degenerate), which can be achieved through sufficiently small (and smooth) data.

Proposition 1
.

Assume that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{1,2,3\}
is a bounded and
C^{1,1}
-regular domain. Let
c
,
b>0
and
k\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Furthermore, let
(p_{0},p_{1})\in{H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)}\times H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and
f\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
.
There exists data size
\delta=\delta(T)>0
, such that if

\|p_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+\|p_{1}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|f\|_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq\delta,

(51)

then there is a unique solution of

\left\{\begin{aligned} &((1+2k(x)p)p_{t})_{t}-c^{2}\Delta p-b\Delta p_{t}=f(x,t)\quad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
&p_{|\partial\Omega}=0,\quad(p,p_{t})_{|t=0}=(p_{0},p_{1})\end{aligned}\right.

(52)

in

{X}_{p}=L^{\infty}(0,T;{H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)})\cap W^{1,\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))\cap H^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
,
such that
1+2kp\geq\gamma>0\ \text{ in }\ \Omega\times(0,T)

for some
\gamma>0
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\|p\|^{2}_{{X}_{p}}\lesssim\|p_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+\|p_{1}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega)))}.

(53)

Proof.

The proof follows analogously to that of
[
16
, Proposition 1]
; the only difference is that here smallness of data is imposed instead of the smallness of
\|k\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
. We thus omit the details.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
1
, we can define the solution mapping

\mathcal{S}:L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\rightarrow{X}_{p},\quad\text{ such that }\mathcal{S}(f)=p,

(54)

which will be employed in the analysis of the coupled problem. We will furthermore exploit Lipschitz continuity of this mapping in the following sense. Let
f_{1}
,
f_{2}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
and denote
p^{(1)}=\mathcal{S}(f_{1})
and
p^{(2)}=\mathcal{S}(f_{2})
. By Proposition
1
,

\|p^{(1)}\|_{{X}_{p}},\,\|p^{(2)}\|_{{X}_{p}}\leq\mathcal{C}

(55)

for some
\mathcal{C>0}
. Then it can be shown (analogously to the proof of contractivity in
[
16
, Proposition 1]
) that

\displaystyle\|p^{(1)}-p^{(2)}\|_{{X}_{p}}\leq C(T,\mathcal{C})\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))};

(56)

for completeness, we provide the details in Appendix
A.1
.

We approach the analysis of the Westervelt–Rayleigh–Plesset system by employing Banach’s fixed-point theorem. To this end, we linearize the Rayleigh–Plesset equation, in the general spirit of
[
32
,
2
]
, while eliminating
p
via the mapping
\mathcal{S}
. More precisely, to analyze (
50
) under the assumptions of Proposition
1
, we set up the fixed-point mapping
\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{B}_{R}\ni R^{*}\mapsto R
,
where
R
solves

\left\{\begin{aligned} &R_{tt}=h(R^{*},R^{*}_{t},\mathcal{S}(f(R^{*})))\\
&(R,R_{t})|_{t=0}=(R_{0},R_{1}),\end{aligned}\right.

(57)

with
f(R^{*})=\xi\left((R^{*})^{3}\right)_{tt}
and the function
h
given by

h(R^{*},R^{*}_{t},\mathcal{S}(f(R^{*})))=\frac{1}{R^{*}}\bigl(-\tfrac{3}{2}(R^{*}_{t})^{2}+\tfrac{1}{\rho_{0}}h_{0}(R^{*},R^{*}_{t})-\tfrac{1}{\rho_{0}}\mathcal{S}(f(R^{*}))\bigr).

(58)

Above,
R^{*}
is taken from the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{R}\negthinspace=\negthinspace\big\{\negthinspace R^{*}\negthickspace\in\negthinspace C^{2}([0,T];L^{\infty}(\Omega))\negthinspace:

\displaystyle\|R^{*}_{tt}\|_{C(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\negthickspace\leq\negthinspace M,

(59)

\displaystyle\|R^{*}\|_{C(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\negthickspace+\negthinspace\|R^{*}_{t}\|_{C(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\negthickspace\leq m,

\displaystyle\|R^{*}-R_{0}\|_{C(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\leq\varepsilon_{0},\,(R^{*},R^{*}_{t})_{|t=0}=(R_{0},R_{1})\big\}.

Above,
\varepsilon_{0}>0
is small enough and will be set by the upcoming proof, together with small enough
m>0
and an adequately calibrated
M>0
. We note that the
\varepsilon_{0}
condition is there to ensure the positivity of
R
, provided
R_{0}
is positive.

We make the following assumptions on the function
h_{0}
in (
58
). We assume that for any
R^{*}\in\mathcal{B}_{R}
, and for any
R^{(1),*}
,
R^{(2),*}\in\mathcal{B}_{R}
,

\displaystyle\|h_{0}(R^{*},R^{*}_{t})\|_{C(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\leq C

\displaystyle(m,\varepsilon_{0})

(60)

\displaystyle\|h_{0}(R^{(1),*},R^{(1),*}_{t})-h_{0}(R^{(2),*},R^{(2),*}_{t})\|_{C(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|R^{(1),*}-R^{(2),*}\|_{C^{1}(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}

(61)

We note that function
h_{0}
in (
49
) satisfies these assumptions; for completeness, we provide the proof in Appendix
A.1
.

Theorem 2
.

Assume that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
d\in\{2,3\}
, is a bounded and
C^{1,1}
-regular domain. Furthermore, assume that
c
,
b>0
,
\rho_{0}>0
,
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, and
k\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, and let
(p_{0},p_{1})\in{H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)}\times H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),\,(R_{0},R_{1})\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\times L^{\infty}(\Omega)
,
where

R_{0}(x)\geq\underline{R}_{0}>0\ \quad\text{a.e. in }\Omega.

(62)

Let assumptions (
60
) and (
61
) on the function
h_{0}
hold. Then there exist pressure data size
\delta_{p}>0
, bubble data size
\delta_{R}
,
and final time
\tilde{T}
,
such that if

\|p_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+\|p_{1}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\delta_{p},\quad\|R_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\|R_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\delta_{R},\ \text{ and }\ T\leq\tilde{T},

(63)

there is a unique
(p,R)\in{X}_{p}\times\mathcal{B}_{R}
that solves (
50
).

Proof.

As announced, we carry out the proof by relying on the Banach fixed-point theorem. Note that
\mathcal{B}_{R}\neq\emptyset
since the solution of

\displaystyle R_{tt}=0,\quad(R,R_{t})|_{t=0}=(R_{0},R_{1})

(64)

belongs to
\mathcal{B}_{R}
as long as the final time and
\delta_{R}
are small enough.

Let
R^{*}\in\mathcal{B}_{R}
. For small enough
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}(\underline{R}_{0})
,
R^{*}
is bounded and positive:

0<\underline{R}:=\underline{R}_{0}-\varepsilon_{0}\leq R_{0}-\varepsilon_{0}\leq R^{*}(x,t)\leq\|R_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\varepsilon_{0}:=\overline{R}

(65)

a.e. in
\Omega\times(0,T)
.
Toward checking the assumptions of Proposition
1
, we note that

\displaystyle\|f(R^{*})\|_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}\negthickspace\leq

\displaystyle 3\xi\|R^{*}\|^{2}_{C(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\negthickspace\sqrt{T}\|R^{*}_{tt}\|_{C(L^{2}(\Omega))}\negthickspace+\negthinspace 6\xi\|R^{*}\negthinspace\|_{C(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\negthickspace\sqrt{T}\|R^{*}_{t}\|^{2}_{C(L^{2}(\Omega))}

(66)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{T}C(\Omega)(M+m)m^{2}.

Therefore, the smallness condition (
51
) can be fulfilled by making
\delta_{p}
and
T
small enough so that

\delta_{p}+\sqrt{T}C(\Omega)(M+m)m^{2}\leq\delta
.
Thus, by Prop.
1
, the mapping
\mathcal{S}
is well-defined (and, in turn, problem (
57
)) and we have
p=\mathcal{S}(f(R^{*}))
. The remainder of the proof is dedicated to showing that
\mathcal{T}
is a strictly contractive self-mapping.

The self-mapping property
:
From (
57
), we have the following bound:

\displaystyle\|R_{tt}\|_{C(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\leq\,\|h(R^{*},R^{*}_{t},\mathcal{S}(f(R^{*})))\|_{C(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}.

(67)

To estimate the
h
term further, we use the lower bound for
R^{*}
established in (
65
):

\displaystyle\|h(R^{*},R^{*}_{t},\mathcal{S}(f(R^{*})))\|_{C(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\negthickspace\leq\begin{multlined}\negthickspace\tfrac{1}{\overline{R}}\bigl\|\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}\tfrac{3}{2}(R^{*}_{t})^{2}\negthickspace+\tfrac{1}{\rho_{0}}h_{0}(R^{*}\negthickspace,R^{*}_{t})-\tfrac{1}{\rho_{0}}\mathcal{S}(f(R^{*}))\bigr\|_{C(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\end{multlined}.

(68)

On account of the fact that
R^{*}\in\mathcal{B}_{R}
and assumption (
60
) made on
h_{0}
, we then have

\displaystyle\|h(R^{*}\negthickspace,R^{*}_{t}\negthickspace,\mathcal{S}(f(R^{*})))\|_{C(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\negthinspace\lesssim\negthinspace\|R^{*}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}\negthickspace+\negthinspace C(m,\varepsilon_{0})\negthinspace+\negthinspace\|\mathcal{S}(f(R^{*}))\|_{C(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}.

(69)

To estimate the
\mathcal{S}
term further, we employ Proposition
1
and the embedding
C([0,T];L^{\infty}(\Omega))\hookrightarrow{X}_{p}
:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{S}(f(R^{*}))\|_{C(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\leq

\displaystyle C(\Omega)\|\mathcal{S}(f(R^{*}))\|_{{X}_{p}}

(70)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(\Omega,T)\left(\|p_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+\|p_{1}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|f(R^{*})\|_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}\right).

Then taking into account the bound on
f
derived in (
66
) yields

\|\mathcal{S}(f(R^{*}))\|_{C(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\leq C(\Omega,T)\bigl(\delta_{p}+\sqrt{T}(M+m)m^{2}\bigr).

(71)

Therefore, by employing (
71
) in (
69
) and then (
67
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\|R_{tt}\|_{C(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\lesssim\,1+\delta_{p}+m^{2}+\sqrt{T}(M+m)m^{2}\leq\,M,

(72)

provided
M>0
is large enough and
T>0
small enough relative to it. Next, since

\displaystyle R=

\displaystyle R_{0}+R_{1}t+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}h(R^{*},R^{*}_{t},\mathcal{S}(f(R^{*})))(\tilde{s})\,\textup{d}\tilde{s}\textup{d}s,

(73)

we find that

\displaystyle\|R\|_{C(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}+\|R_{t}\|_{C(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}

(74)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|R_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+(1+T)\|R_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+(T+T^{2})\|h(R^{*},R^{*}_{t},\mathcal{S}(f(R^{*})))\|_{C(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}(1+T)\delta_{R}+(T+T^{2})\bigl[1+m^{2}+\delta_{p}+\sqrt{T}(M+m)m^{2}\bigr],\end{multlined}

which can be made smaller than
m
by decreasing
\delta_{R}
and
T
. To conclude that
R\in\mathcal{B}_{R}
, it remains to estimate
R-R_{0}
. To this end, the expression for
R
in (
73
) yields

\displaystyle\|R-R_{0}\|_{C(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\leq T\|R_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+T^{2}\|h(R^{*},R^{*}_{t},\mathcal{S}(f(R^{*}))\|_{C(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}.

(75)

Therefore, reducing
T
allows us to conclude that
\|R-R_{0}\|_{C(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\negthickspace\leq\varepsilon_{0}
,
and,
R\in\mathcal{B}_{R}
.

Strict contractivity
: To prove strict contractivity of
\mathcal{T}
, we take
R^{(1),*}
,
R^{(2),*}\in\mathcal{B}_{R}
and note that the difference
{\overline{\mathcal{R}}}=\mathcal{T}(R^{(1),*})-\mathcal{T}(R^{(2),*})
solves

\displaystyle{\overline{\mathcal{R}}}_{tt}=

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}h(R^{(1),*},R^{(1),*}_{t},\mathcal{S}(f(R^{(1),*})))-h(R^{(2),*},R^{(2),*}_{t},\mathcal{S}(f(R^{(2),*})))\end{multlined}

(76)

with zero initial conditions. Thus we immediately have

\displaystyle\|{\overline{\mathcal{R}}}\|_{C^{2}(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\negthickspace\lesssim\negthickspace\|h(R^{(1),*}\negthickspace,R^{(1),*}_{t}\negthickspace,\mathcal{S}(f(R^{(1),*})))-h(R^{(2),*}\negthickspace,R^{(2),*}_{t}\negthickspace,\mathcal{S}(f(R^{(2),*})))\|_{C(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}

(77)

and with
{\overline{\mathcal{R}}}^{*}=R^{(1),*}-R^{(2),*}
we can rewrite the difference of
h
terms as follows:

\displaystyle h(R^{(1),*},R^{(1),*}_{t},\mathcal{S}(f(R^{(1),*})))-h(R^{(2),*},R^{(2),*}_{t},\mathcal{S}(f(R^{(2),*})))

(78)

\displaystyle=

4.3
The influence of ultrasound on microbubbles

We next present numerical results for the Westervelt–Rayleigh–Plesset model. To somewhat simplify the computational complexity, we set the right-hand source term in the Westervelt equation to zero (that is, we take
\xi=0

When it comes to the rigorous numerical analysis of the Westervelt equation, its finite element semi-discretization in space is best understood theoretically.

To achieve focusing, we construct a rectangular domain with one curved side, where the waves are excited such that they focus at the center of the domain. The Neumann conditions are given by
\frac{\partial p}{\partial n}=A_{p}\sin(2\pi f_{p}t)
on the curved part of the boundary of the computational domain, and set to zero elsewhere. The amplitude of the acoustic boundary excitation is taken to be
A_{p}=0.1
MPa/m and the frequency

[
1
]
, with Gmsh
[
10

used for meshing. The simulations are based on using continuous linear finite elements for spatial discretization and a predictor-corrector Newmark scheme for time integration, following the algorithm in
[
12

. The integration parameters in the Newmark scheme are set to

and the time step is taken to be

5
Outlook

In this work, we have explored the mathematical modeling and analysis of ultrasound contrast imaging, with a focus on the nonlinear effects in ultrasound propagation and interactions between microbubbles and pressure waves. Adapting the mathematical analysis to the setting of Neumann or absorbing acoustic boundary conditions in future reseach is important for modeling more realistic scenarios, where one should avoid non-physical reflections of sound waves. Furthermore, extending the analysis to address the refined wave-ODE models derived in Section
2
, such as (
38
), would provide deeper insights into the complex interactions between acoustic waves and microbubble dynamics. From a numerical perspective, addressing the fully coupled problem (
50
) with
\xi\neq 0
, where both wave propagation and microbubble dynamics are solved simultaneously, would be a significant step forward in capturing the complete range of physical behaviors.
This setting introduces additional computational complexity, particularly due to the need for much finer time steps in discretizing the ODE compared to the wave equation, which already need to be relatively small in the nonlinear acoustic setting. Techniques such as multirate time stepping and operator splitting methods could be explored here to manage differing time scales in the wave and ODE dynamics. Furthermore, rigorous numerical analysis of the schemes used in the present work and those developed in the future would provide a deeper understanding of the simulated problems.

A.2.1

We next adapt the derived models by introducing fractional derivatives following

[

(100)

where
0\leq n-1<y<n

A.2.2
Analysis of the system

In the following, we consider the Westervelt–Rayleigh–Plesset system with time-fractional acoustic attenuation given by

\left\{\begin{aligned} &((1+2k(x)p)p_{t})_{t}-c^{2}\Delta p-b\tau^{\alpha-1}\Delta\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}p=\xi(R^{3})_{tt}\quad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\[2.84526pt]
&(p,p_{t})_{|t=0}=(p_{0},p_{1}),\quad p_{|\partial\Omega}=0,\\[2.84526pt]
&\rho_{0}\left[RR_{tt}+\frac{3}{2}R_{t}^{2}\right]\begin{multlined}=h_{0}(R,R_{t})-p\qquad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),\end{multlined}\\[2.84526pt]
&(R,R_{t})_{|t=0}=(R_{0},R_{1}),\end{aligned}\right.

with
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
(corresponding to
\frac{4}{3}\pi c^{2}\eta
in the derivation) and
\alpha\in(0,1)

106

\tau=1
. We recall first a well-posedness result from
[
15
