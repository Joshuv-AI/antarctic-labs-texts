Robust Point Matching with Distance Profiles
YoonHaeng Hur, Yuehaw Khoo

Abstract
We show the outlier robustness and noise stability of practical matching procedures based on distance profiles. Although the idea of matching points based on invariants like distance profiles has a long history in the literature, there has been little understanding of the theoretical properties of such procedures, especially in the presence of outliers and noise. We provide a theoretical analysis showing that under certain probabilistic settings, the proposed matching procedure is successful with high probability even in the presence of outliers and noise. We demonstrate the performance of the proposed method using a real data example and provide simulation studies to complement the theoretical findings. Lastly, we extend the concept of distance profiles to the abstract setting and connect the proposed matching procedure to the Gromov-Wasserstein distance and its lower bound, with a new sample complexity result derived based on the properties of distance profiles. This paper contributes to the literature by providing theoretical underpinnings of the matching procedures based on invariants like distance profiles, which have been widely used in practice but have rarely been analyzed theoretically.

Introduction

Matching is one of the most fundamental tasks of modern data science, which can be found in numerous applications. For instance, the goal of shape correspondence problems
[
37
]
in computer graphics is to match corresponding parts of different geometric shapes, often obtained by 3D scanners or imaging equipment used for Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) or Computed Tomography (CT). Graph matching aims to find corresponding nodes between two or more graphs representing structured data, such as neuronal connectivity in human brains
[
35
]
or social networks
[
6
]
.

A formal mathematical framework for matching
[
26
]
conceptualizes objects as metric measure spaces, which are essentially probability measures defined on suitable metric spaces. In practice, of course, objects are not fully available and thus approximated by suitable point clouds, say,
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
and
\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}\}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, respectively, and the practical goal is to match these point clouds. Quadratic matching is one of the most standard approaches to this problem, which formulates and solves a quadratic program based on pairwise distances
\{\|X_{i}-X_{\ell}\|_{2}\}_{i,\ell=1}^{n}
and
\{\|Y_{j}-Y_{\ell}\|_{2}\}_{j,\ell=1}^{m}
. When
n=m
, quadratic matching finds a one-to-one correspondence that aligns the pairwise distances as closely as possible, which is formulated as a Quadratic Assignment Problem (QAP)
[
21
,
24
]
. When
n\neq m
, the notion of correspondence is relaxed to soft assignments or couplings, leading to the formulation of the Gromov-Wasserstein distance
[
26
]
, which has been increasingly used in applications, such as word alignment
[
2
]
, statistical inference
[
7
,
41
]
, and generative modeling
[
9
,
19
]
. On the practical side, however, quadratic matching requires solving a quadratic program that is NP-hard in general, and thus needs to rely on several methods, such as convex relaxation
[
1
]
, semidefinite relaxation
[
42
,
20
]
, spectral relaxation
[
23
]
, or entropic regularization
[
32
,
34
,
31
]
.

Matching procedures based on the pairwise distances
\{\|X_{i}-X_{\ell}\|_{2}\}_{i,\ell=1}^{n},\{\|Y_{j}-Y_{\ell}\|_{2}\}_{j,\ell=1}^{m}
are invariant to rigid transformations, which is desirable in practice. For instance, in imaging problems, the images of the objects of interest are often obtained from different angles and thus need to be matched up to rigid transformations. However, such procedures are difficult to analyze theoretically and the literature has been mostly studying theoretical guarantees for other matching procedures using
\|X_{i}-Y_{j}\|_{2}
’s, which aim to match the points based on the proximity.
[
11
,
22
,
40
]
establish theoretical results on recovering the ground truth matching for the procedures based on
\|X_{i}-Y_{j}\|_{2}
’s under some noise, and
[
18
,
28
,
17
]
show matching guarantees in the presence of outliers. Although Theorem 2 of
[
40
]
concerns a procedure using the pairwise distances
\{\|X_{i}-X_{\ell}\|_{2}\}_{i,\ell=1}^{n}
and
\{\|Y_{j}-Y_{\ell}\|_{2}\}_{j,\ell=1}^{m}
, it requires solving a nontrivial minimization over permutations, which is computationally challenging, and the result does not consider the presence of outliers.

Motivated by the above challenges, this paper theoretically establishes outlier robustness and noise stability of a matching procedure based on the distances
\{\|X_{i}-X_{\ell}\|_{2}\}_{i,\ell=1}^{n}
and
\{\|Y_{j}-Y_{\ell}\|_{2}\}_{j,\ell=1}^{m}
, which is easily implementable in practice. The proposed procedure associates each point with the set of distances from it to other points, namely,
X_{i}
and
Y_{j}
are associated with the sets of distances to the other points
\{\|X_{i}-X_{\ell}\|_{2}\}_{\ell=1}^{n}
and
\{\|Y_{j}-Y_{\ell}\|_{2}\}_{\ell=1}^{m}
, which we call the distance profiles of
X_{i}
and
Y_{j}
, respectively. The matching procedure is to find for each
X_{i}
, the point
Y_{j}
having the most similar distance profile to that of
X_{i}
, where the similarity is measured by a suitable quantity between the sets
\{\|X_{i}-X_{\ell}\|_{2}\}_{\ell=1}^{n}
and
\{\|Y_{j}-Y_{\ell}\|_{2}\}_{\ell=1}^{m}
. The idea of associating a point with suitable invariants based on the distances to other points from it has a long history in the object matching literature, which includes shape contexts
[
4
,
33
]
, integral invariants
[
25
]
, invariant histograms
[
8
]
, and the local distribution of distances
[
26
]
. However, theoretical results on robustness of such methods are rarely found in the literature. In the context of random graph matching,
[
12
,
13
]
propose and analyze a degree profile matching procedure based on the adjacency matrices
(A_{i\ell})_{i,\ell=1}^{n}
and
(B_{j\ell})_{j,\ell=1}^{n}
of certain correlated random graphs. However, their analysis focuses on random graph models, such as Erdös-Rényi and correlated Wigner models, where the edges are independently generated, namely,
(A_{i\ell})_{i,\ell=1}^{n}
are mutually independent, which is substantially different from our setting where the distances
\{\|X_{i}-X_{\ell}\|_{2}\}_{i,\ell=1}^{n}
are not mutually independent.

Distance Profile Matching

We first describe the distance profile matching procedure that we propose to study. As mentioned earlier, we build on the principle of matching points using invariants like distance profiles, which has a long history in the literature

While there are many different procedures incorporating this idea, we are particularly inspired by a simple and easily implementable procedure called degree profile matching
[

First, notice that Algorithm

Outlier Robustness of Distance Profile Matching

This section analyzes the outlier robustness of the distance profile matching procedure described in Algorithm
1
. As we have seen in the previous section, Algorithm
1
is designed to match the points corresponding to the same region of the objects, while ignoring or arbitrarily matching the points from the outlier parts. In order to theoretically analyze such outlier robustness, we consider appropriate model assumptions on the underlying objects
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
that capture the nature of the outliers. Motivated by several practical considerations, we model
\mu,\nu
as follows.

Assumption 1
.

Suppose that
\mu
and
\nu
are mixture distributions consisting of
t
and
s
components, respectively, defined as follows: for some
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{t})\in\Delta_{t}
and
(q_{1},\ldots,q_{s})\in\Delta_{s}
,

\mu=\sum_{k=1}^{t}p_{k}N(\theta_{k},\sigma_{k}^{2}I_{d})\quad\text{and}\quad\nu=\sum_{k=1}^{s}q_{k}N(\eta_{k},\tau_{k}^{2}I_{d}),

For some
K\leq\min\{t,s\}
, we assume that the first
K
components of
\mu
and
\nu
are identical, that is,
p_{k}=q_{k}
,
\theta_{k}=\eta_{k}
, and
\sigma_{k}=\tau_{k}
for
k\in[K]
.

Assumption
1
is equivalent to letting
\mu=\lambda\rho+(1-\lambda)\mu_{o}
and
\nu=\lambda\rho+(1-\lambda)\nu_{o}
, where the common part

is the mixture consisting of the first

are the mixtures of the remaining components. The motivation behind Assumption

is that the objects

are composed of several regions as we have seen from the example in Section

The points corresponding to the same region are to be matched, while we ignore matching the points from the outlier components.

In general cases with
\Gamma>0
, where the point clouds are samples, the Wasserstein-1 distances (
1.1
) deviate from (
2.4
) due to the noise and sampling error. Therefore, the condition (
2.6
) needs to be modified to reflect this deviation, and characterizing this probabilistic deviation leads to the right-hand side of (
2.3
).

Based on the above insights, we prove Theorem
1
as follows.

Proof.

We analyze the deviation of (
1.1
) from (
2.4
), namely, for any pair
(i,j)\in[n]\times[m]
, we derive an upper bound on the deviation
|W(i,j)-\overline{W}(t(i),s(j))|
. To this end, we define an intermediate quantity
W^{\prime}(i,j)
as follows: for any
(i,j)\in[n]\times[m]
,

\begin{split}W^{\prime}(i,j)&:=W_{1}\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\delta_{\|\theta_{t(i)}-\theta_{t(\ell)}\|_{2}},\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\delta_{\|\eta_{s(j)}-\eta_{s(\ell)}\|_{2}}\bigg)\\
&=W_{1}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{n_{k}}{n}\delta_{\|\theta_{t(i)}-\theta_{k}\|_{2}},\sum_{k=1}^{s}\frac{m_{k}}{m}\delta_{\|\eta_{s(j)}-\eta_{k}\|_{2}}\bigg),\end{split}

where we define
n_{k}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}1\{t(\ell)=k\}
and
m_{k}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}1\{s(\ell)=k\}
. We first derive an upper bound on
|W^{\prime}(i,j)-\overline{W}(t(i),s(j))|
for any
(i,j)\in[n]\times[m]
. By the triangle inequality of
W_{1}
, we have

\begin{split}&|W^{\prime}(i,j)-\overline{W}(t(i),s(j))|\\
&\leq\underbrace{W_{1}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{n_{k}}{n}\delta_{\|\theta_{t(i)}-\theta_{k}\|_{2}},\sum_{k=1}^{t}p_{k}\delta_{\|\theta_{t(i)}-\theta_{k}\|_{2}}\bigg)}_{=:(*)}+\underbrace{W_{1}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{s}\frac{m_{k}}{m}\delta_{\|\eta_{s(j)}-\eta_{k}\|_{2}},\sum_{k=1}^{s}q_{k}\delta_{\|\eta_{s(j)}-\eta_{k}\|_{2}}\bigg)}_{=:(**)}.\end{split}

For each
\alpha\in[K]
, order
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{t}
by the distance to
\theta_{\alpha}
, namely, find
k_{\alpha}\colon[t]\to[t]
such that
\|\theta_{\alpha}-\theta_{k_{\alpha}(1)}\|_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\|\theta_{\alpha}-\theta_{k_{\alpha}(t)}\|_{2}
. Then, by Lemma
2
, we have

\begin{split}(*)&\leq\max_{\alpha\in[K]}W_{1}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{n_{k}}{n}\delta_{\|\theta_{\alpha}-\theta_{k}\|_{2}},\sum_{k=1}^{t}p_{k}\delta_{\|\theta_{\alpha}-\theta_{k}\|_{2}}\bigg)\\
&\leq\max_{\alpha\in[K]}\|\theta_{\alpha}-\theta_{k_{\alpha}(t)}\|_{2}\cdot\max_{j\in[t-1]}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{j}\left(\frac{n_{k_{\alpha}(k)}}{n}-p_{k_{\alpha}(k)}\right)\right|\\
&\leq R\cdot\max_{\alpha\in[K]}\max_{j\in[t-1]}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{j}\left(\frac{n_{k_{\alpha}(k)}}{n}-p_{k_{\alpha}(k)}\right)\right|=:R\cdot Z_{1}.\end{split}

Similarly, for each
\beta\in[K]
, order
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{s}
by the distance to
\eta_{\beta}
, namely, find
\ell_{\beta}\colon[s]\to[s]
such that
\|\eta_{\beta}-\eta_{\ell_{\beta}(1)}\|_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\|\eta_{\beta}-\eta_{\ell_{\beta}(s)}\|_{2}
. Then, by Lemma
2
, we have

\begin{split}(**)&\leq\max_{\beta\in[K]}W_{1}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{s}\frac{m_{k}}{m}\delta_{\|\eta_{\beta}-\eta_{k}\|_{2}},\sum_{k=1}^{s}q_{k}\delta_{\|\eta_{\beta}-\eta_{k}\|_{2}}\bigg)\\
&\leq\max_{\beta\in[K]}\|\eta_{\beta}-\eta_{\ell_{\beta}(s)}\|_{2}\cdot\max_{j\in[s-1]}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{j}\left(\frac{m_{\ell_{\beta}(k)}}{m}-q_{\ell_{\beta}(k)}\right)\right|\\
&\leq R\cdot\max_{\beta\in[K]}\max_{j\in[s-1]}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{j}\left(\frac{m_{\ell_{\beta}(k)}}{m}-q_{\ell_{\beta}(k)}\right)\right|=:R\cdot Z_{2}.\end{split}

Hence,
|W^{\prime}(i,j)-\overline{W}(t(i),s(j))|\leq R\cdot(Z_{1}+Z_{2})
. Next, we bound
|W(i,j)-W^{\prime}(i,j)|
for any
(i,j)\in[n]\times[m]
. By the triangle inequality again, we have

\begin{split}&|W(i,j)-W^{\prime}(i,j)|\\
&\leq W_{1}\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\delta_{\|X_{i}-X_{\ell}\|_{2}},\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\delta_{\|\theta_{t(i)}-\theta_{t(\ell)}\|_{2}}\bigg)+W_{1}\bigg(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\delta_{\|Y_{j}-Y_{\ell}\|_{2}},\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\delta_{\|\eta_{s(j)}-\eta_{s(\ell)}\|_{2}}\bigg).\end{split}

Meanwhile, notice that
X_{i}=\theta_{t(i)}+\xi_{i}
and
Y_{j}=\eta_{s(j)}+\zeta_{j}
for any
(i,j)\in[n]\times[m]
, where
\xi_{i}\,|\,t(i)\sim N(0,\sigma_{t(i)}^{2}I_{d})
and
\zeta_{j}\,|\,s(j)\sim N(0,\tau_{s(j)}^{2}I_{d})
are independent. Recall that the Wasserstein-1 distance between two sets of points in
\mathbb{R}
with the same cardinality satisfies the following:

W_{1}\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\delta_{\|X_{i}-X_{\ell}\|_{2}},\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\delta_{\|\theta_{t(i)}-\theta_{t(\ell)}\|_{2}}\bigg)\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\left|\|X_{i}-X_{\ell}\|_{2}-\|\theta_{t(i)}-\theta_{t(\ell)}\|_{2}\right|.

From
|\|X_{i}-X_{\ell}\|_{2}-\|\theta_{t(i)}-\theta_{t(\ell)}\|_{2}=|\|\theta_{t(i)}-\theta_{t(\ell)}+\xi_{i}-\xi_{\ell}\|_{2}-\|\theta_{t(i)}-\theta_{t(\ell)}\|_{2}|\leq\|\xi_{i}-\xi_{\ell}\|_{2}
, we have

W_{1}\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\delta_{\|X_{i}-X_{\ell}\|_{2}},\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\delta_{\|\theta_{t(i)}-\theta_{t(\ell)}\|_{2}}\bigg)\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\|\xi_{i}-\xi_{\ell}\|_{2}\leq\max_{i,\ell\in[n]}\|\xi_{i}-\xi_{\ell}\|_{2}=:U.

Similarly,

W_{1}\bigg(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\delta_{\|Y_{j}-Y_{\ell}\|_{2}},\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\delta_{\|\eta_{s(j)}-\eta_{s(\ell)}\|_{2}}\bigg)\leq\max_{j,\ell\in[m]}\|\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{\ell}\|_{2}=:V.

Hence,
|W(i,j)-W^{\prime}(i,j)|\leq U+V
and
|W(i,j)-\overline{W}(t(i),s(j))|\leq R(Z_{1}+Z_{2})+U+V
. Now, fix
i\in[n]
such that
t(i)\in[K]
. Then,

\begin{split}&\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[m]\\
s(j)\neq t(i)\end{subarray}}W(i,j)-\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[m]\\
s(j)=t(i)\end{subarray}}W(i,j)\\
&\geq\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[m]\\
s(j)\neq t(i)\end{subarray}}\overline{W}(t(i),s(j))-\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[m]\\
s(j)=t(i)\end{subarray}}\overline{W}(t(i),s(j))-2R(Z_{1}+Z_{2})-2(U+V)\\
&\geq\min_{\beta\in[s]\backslash\{t(i)\}}\overline{W}(t(i),\beta)-\overline{W}(t(i),t(i))-2R(Z_{1}+Z_{2})-2(U+V)\\
&\geq\underbrace{\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in[K]\\
\beta\in[s]\backslash\{\alpha\}\end{subarray}}\overline{W}(\alpha,\beta)-R\cdot\bigg(1-\sum_{k\in[K]}p_{k}\bigg)}_{=:\Omega}-2R(Z_{1}+Z_{2})-2(U+V),\end{split}

(2.7)

where the last inequality follows from Lemma
1
. Therefore,
2R(Z_{1}+Z_{2})+2(U+V)<\Omega
implies that
s(\pi(i))=t(i)
for any
i\in[n]
such that
t(i)\in[K]
. We show that
\mathbb{P}(E)\leq\delta
, where
E
is the event
(2R(Z_{1}+Z_{2})+2(U+V)\geq\Omega)
. By Lemma
3
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{1}\geq\frac{\Omega}{8R}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2K(t-1)\exp\left(-\frac{2n\Omega^{2}}{(8R)^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{2}\geq\frac{\Omega}{8R}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2K(s-1)\exp\left(-\frac{2m\Omega^{2}}{(8R)^{2}}\right).

Meanwhile, for
i\neq\ell
, note that we have
\xi_{i}-\xi_{\ell}\,|\,t(i),t(\ell)\sim N(0,(\sigma_{t(i)}^{2}+\sigma_{t(\ell)}^{2})I_{d})
, which implies
\|\xi_{i}-\xi_{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}\,|\,t(i),t(\ell)\sim(\sigma_{t(i)}^{2}+\sigma_{t(\ell)}^{2})\chi^{2}(d)
. Hence, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i,\ell\in[n]}\|\xi_{i}-\xi_{\ell}\|_{2}\geq\frac{\Omega}{8}\right)\leq\sum_{i\neq\ell}\mathbb{P}\left(\|\xi_{i}-\xi_{\ell}\|_{2}\geq\frac{\Omega}{8}\right)\leq n^{2}\mathbb{P}\left(\chi^{2}(d)\geq\frac{(\Omega/8)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right).

As
\Omega^{2}\geq 256d\cdot\max_{k\in[t]}\sigma_{k}^{2}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i,\ell\in[n]}\|\xi_{i}-\xi_{\ell}\|_{2}\geq\frac{\Omega}{8}\right)\leq n^{2}\mathbb{P}\left(\chi^{2}(d)\geq\frac{(\Omega/8)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\leq n^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{(\Omega/8)^{2}}{32\sigma^{2}}\right).

Similarly, as
\Omega^{2}\geq 256d\cdot\max_{k\in[s]}\tau_{k}^{2}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{j,\ell\in[m]}\|\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{\ell}\|_{2}\geq\frac{\Omega}{8}\right)\leq m^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{(\Omega/8)^{2}}{32\tau^{2}}\right).

Putting all together, we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(E)&\leq\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{1}\geq\frac{\Omega}{4R}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{2}\geq\frac{\Omega}{4R}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(U\geq\frac{\Omega}{8}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(V\geq\frac{\Omega}{8}\right)\\
&\leq 2K(t+s-2)\exp\left(-\frac{2(n\wedge m)\Omega^{2}}{(8R)^{2}}\right)+2(n\vee m)^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{(\Omega/8)^{2}}{32\Gamma^{2}}\right)\\
&\leq\frac{\delta}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}=\delta,\end{split}

where the last inequality follows from (
2.3
). Hence, we have (
2.2
).
∎

Remark 1
.

From the proof of Theorem
1
, one may have noticed that we can replace the left-hand side of (
2.3

\Omega_{o}:=\min_{\alpha\in[K]}\left(\min_{\beta\in[s]\backslash\{\alpha\}}\overline{W}(\alpha,\beta)-\overline{W}(\alpha,\alpha)\right).

To see this, it suffices to observe that we can replace
\Omega
in the last line of (

\Omega_{o}
, and the rest of the proof follows the same with
\Omega_{o}

.

Lemma 1
.

For the centers
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{t}
and
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{s}
in Assumption
1
, define
\overline{W}
as in (
2.4
). Then, for any
k\in[K]
, we have

\overline{W}(\alpha,\alpha)\leq\bigg(1-\sum_{k\in[K]}p_{k}\bigg)\cdot\max_{(\alpha,\beta)\in[t]\times[s]}\|\theta_{\alpha}-\eta_{\beta}\|_{2}.

(2.8)

Proof.

The Wasserstein-1 distance on
\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R})
can be written as the
L^{1}
distance between cumulative distribution functions. For
\alpha\in[K]
, we have

\begin{split}\overline{W}(\alpha,\alpha)&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{t}p_{k}1\{\|\theta_{\alpha}-\theta_{k}\|_{2}\leq z\}-\sum_{k=1}^{s}q_{k}1\{\|\eta_{\alpha}-\eta_{k}\|_{2}\leq z\}\right|\,\mathrm{d}z\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\sum_{k\in[t]\backslash[K]}p_{k}1\{\|\theta_{\alpha}-\theta_{k}\|_{2}\leq z\}-\sum_{k\in[s]\backslash[K]}q_{k}1\{\|\theta_{\alpha}-\eta_{k}\|_{2}\leq z\}\right|\,\mathrm{d}z\\
&=(1-\lambda)W_{1}\left(\sum_{k\in[t]\backslash[K]}\tfrac{p_{k}}{1-\lambda}\delta_{\|\theta_{\alpha}-\theta_{k}\|_{2}},\sum_{k\in[s]\backslash[K]}\tfrac{q_{k}}{1-\lambda}\delta_{\|\theta_{\alpha}-\eta_{k}\|_{2}}\right),\end{split}

where
\lambda=\sum_{k\in[K]}p_{k}
. Since
|\|\theta_{\alpha}-\theta_{k}\|_{2}-\|\theta_{\alpha}-\eta_{\ell}\|_{2}|\leq\|\theta_{k}-\eta_{\ell}\|_{2}
for any
k\in[t]\backslash[K]
and
\ell\in[s]\backslash[K]
, one can deduce that (
2.8
) holds.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Let
z_{1}\leq\cdots\leq z_{t}
be any real numbers. For any
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{t}),(q_{1},\ldots,q_{t})\in\Delta_{t}
, we have

W_{1}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{t}p_{k}\delta_{z_{k}},\sum_{k=1}^{t}q_{k}\delta_{z_{k}}\bigg)\leq(z_{t}-z_{1})\cdot\max_{j\in[t-1]}\bigg|\sum_{k=1}^{j}(p_{k}-q_{k})\bigg|.

(2.9)

Proof.

Notice that

W_{1}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{t}p_{k}\delta_{z_{k}},\sum_{k=1}^{t}q_{k}\delta_{z_{k}}\bigg)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg|\sum_{k=1}^{t}(p_{k}-q_{k})1\{z_{k}\leq z\}\bigg|\,\mathrm{d}z=\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}\bigg|\sum_{k=1}^{j}(p_{k}-q_{k})\bigg|\cdot(z_{j+1}-z_{j}),

where the last equality follows from

\bigg|\sum_{k=1}^{t}(p_{k}-q_{k})1\{z_{k}\leq z\}\bigg|=\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}\bigg|\sum_{k=1}^{j}(p_{k}-q_{k})\bigg|\cdot 1\{z_{j}\leq z<z_{j+1}\}\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{R}.

Therefore, we have (
2.9
).

Necessary Noise Level for the Matching Guarantee

In Theorem
1
, one can intuitively notice that the noise level, denoted as
\Gamma=\max_{k\in[t]}\sigma_{k}\vee\max_{k\in[s]}\tau_{k}
, must be small enough to ensure the matching guarantee. While its desirable scale in relation to the sample size is transparent from the analysis, one may be curious about its magnitude compared to the ambient dimension
d
. From the first term on the right-hand side of (
2.3
), one can see that
\Gamma
has to be of order
d^{-1/2}
. At first, such a dimension-dependence may look like a strong condition, it is in fact a minimal condition given that both sides of (
2.3
) scale with typical Euclidean distances in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. See Figure
2
. Each Gaussian component essentially corresponds to a ball of radius
\Gamma\sqrt{d}
, which has to be well-separated from the other balls to ensure that it is a distinct component. Accordingly, it is natural to have
\Gamma<\frac{\|c_{1}-c_{2}\|_{2}}{\sqrt{d}}
for any distinct
c_{1},c_{2}\in\{\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{t},\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{s}\}
. This explains that
\Gamma=O(d^{-1/2})
implied by (
2.3
) is a minimal condition for the matching guarantee.

\Gamma\sqrt{d}
. All of them are contained in a larger ball of diameter
R
.

Remark 2
.

So far, we have focused on the matching of the points from the common part, namely, the first

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in[K]\\

(2.10)

we can show that

\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[n]\\
t(i)\in[K]\end{subarray}}W(i,\pi(i))<\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[n]\\
t(i)\notin[K]\end{subarray}}W(i,\pi(i))

(2.11)

holds with probability at least
1-\delta
. This means that if the threshold
\rho
in Algorithm

is chosen between the two quantities in (

), the output

Noise Stability of Assignment-Based Matching

This section provides an additional noise stability result of the matching procedure that seeks an assignment. In the proof of Theorem
1
, we have seen that
W(i,j)\approx W^{\prime}(i,j)
which was chiefly based on the fact that the deviation of
X_{i}
from its center
\theta_{t(i)}
due to the noise is sufficiently small. The main principle is that the matching procedure based on distance profiles is robust to the noise added to the points. In this section, we separate this noise stability property from the previous analysis and analyze it in the context of assignment-based matching.

To this end, we consider two sets of locations
\{\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}\}
and
\{\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n}\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and assume
\eta_{\pi^{\ast}(i)}=T(\theta_{i})
for
i\in[n]
, where
T\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an unknown rigid transformation and
\pi^{\ast}
is an unknown permutation on
[n]
representing the correspondence, say,
\theta_{i}
and
\eta_{\pi^{\ast}(i)}
are to be matched. Unlike the previous model in Section
2
, we assume there is a ground truth one-to-one correspondence
\pi^{\ast}
between two sets of locations, which we want to recover from the following perturbed locations:

X_{i}=\theta_{i}+\xi_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad Y_{i}=\eta_{i}+\zeta_{i},

where
\xi_{i}\sim N(0,\sigma_{i}^{2}I_{d})
and
\zeta_{i}\sim N(0,\tau_{i}^{2}I_{d})
are independent noise variables.
3
3

3

Technically, one may think of this model as the previous model in Section
2
with
t=s=K
, namely, no outliers, and with the sampling procedure that draws only one point from each component, rather than randomly drawing from
\mu,\nu
.
For instance, for
d=3
, we can think of
X_{i}
’s and
Y_{i}
’s as the coordinates obtained by scanning some objects of interest but from different angles, say, frontal and lateral views, where the noise is added to the ground truth coordinates
\theta_{i}
’s and
\eta_{i}
’s during the scanning process. As the goal of this model is to recover the permutation
\pi^{\ast}
, we obtain a permutation from the following assignment-based matching procedure: defining
W(i,j)
as in (
1.1
),

\hat{\pi}=\argmin_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W(i,\pi(i)),

which is a linear assignment problem that can be solved efficiently by polynomial-time algorithms. As discussed in Section
2
, several conditions determine the success of the matching. Again, the configuration of the locations
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n},\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n}
is crucial for the matching to be well-defined. To this end, we suppose the following configuration condition:

\Phi:=\min_{i\neq j}W_{1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\delta_{\|\theta_{i}-\theta_{k}\|_{2}},\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\delta_{\|\theta_{j}-\theta_{k}\|_{2}}\right)>0.

(3.1)

The condition (
3.1
) ensures that
\pi^{\ast}
is the unique optimal matching in the following sense:

\pi^{\ast}=\argmin_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\delta_{\|\theta_{i}-\theta_{k}\|_{2}},\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\delta_{\|\eta_{\pi(i)}-\eta_{k}\|_{2}}\right)=:\argmin_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\mathcal{Q}(\pi).

To see this, note that
\mathcal{Q}(\pi^{\ast})=0
, while
\mathcal{Q}(\pi)>0
for
\pi\neq\pi^{\ast}
as
\Phi>0
. The following theorem shows that the obtained permutation
\hat{\pi}
recovers
\pi^{\ast}
with high probability given this condition (
3.1
) and the variances
\sigma_{i}
’s and
\tau_{i}
’s are sufficiently small.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that the locations
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n},\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy

\eta_{\pi^{\ast}(i)}=T(\theta_{i})\quad\forall i\in[n],

(3.2)

for some unknown rigid transformation
T\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and some unknown permutation
\pi^{\ast}
on
[n]
. Now, suppose we have the following noisy locations:

X_{i}=\theta_{i}+\xi_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad Y_{i}=\eta_{i}+\zeta_{i},

(3.3)

where
\xi_{i}\sim N(0,\sigma_{i}^{2}I_{d})
and
\zeta_{i}\sim N(0,\tau_{i}^{2}I_{d})
are independent noise variables. Let
\hat{\pi}
be the permutation obtained from the following assignment-based matching procedure:

\hat{\pi}=\argmin_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{i},\hat{\nu}_{\pi(i)}),

(3.4)

where

\hat{\mu}_{i}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\delta_{\|X_{i}-X_{\ell}\|_{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{\nu}_{i}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\delta_{\|Y_{i}-Y_{\ell}\|_{2}}\quad\forall i\in[n].

Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, we have

\mathbb{P}(\hat{\pi}=\pi^{\ast})\geq 1-\delta

if

\sigma^{2}\vee\tau^{2}\leq\frac{\Phi^{2}}{64(d\vee 8\log(2n^{2}/\delta))},

(3.5)

where
\sigma=\max_{i\in[n]}\sigma_{i}
,
\tau=\max_{i\in[n]}\tau_{i}
, and
\Phi
is defined in (
3.1
).

Proof.

For the proof, we may assume that
\pi^{\ast}
is the identity without loss of generality. First, letting
Q(\pi)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{i},\hat{\nu}_{\pi(i)})
, observe that

\left(\hat{\pi}\neq\pi^{\ast}\right)\subset\bigcup_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{n}\backslash\{\pi^{\ast}\}}\left(Q(\pi^{\ast})\geq Q(\pi)\right).

The event
Q(\pi^{\ast})\geq Q(\pi)
is equivalent to

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{i},\hat{\nu}_{i})-W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{i},\hat{\nu}_{\pi(i)})\right)=\sum_{i:\pi(i)\neq i}\left(W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{i},\hat{\nu}_{i})-W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{i},\hat{\nu}_{\pi(i)})\right)\geq 0,

which is contained in the following events:

\bigcup_{i:\pi(i)\neq i}\left(W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{i},\hat{\nu}_{i})\geq W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{i},\hat{\nu}_{\pi(i)})\right)\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\bigcup_{j\neq i}\left(W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{i},\hat{\nu}_{i})\geq W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{i},\hat{\nu}_{j})\right)

Therefore,

\left(\hat{\pi}\neq\pi^{\ast}\right)\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\bigcup_{j\neq i}\left(W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{i},\hat{\nu}_{i})\geq W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{i},\hat{\nu}_{j})\right).

(3.6)

Now, recall that we can write a rigid transformation
T\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
T(\theta)=R\theta+b
for some orthonormal matrix
R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and some vector
b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Hence, we have

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\delta_{\|X_{i}-X_{\ell}\|_{2}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\delta_{\|\theta_{i}-\theta_{\ell}+\xi_{i}-\xi_{\ell}\|_{2}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\delta_{\|\theta_{i}-\theta_{\ell}+\xi_{i\ell}\|_{2}},

\displaystyle\hat{\nu}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\delta_{\|Y_{i}-Y_{\ell}\|_{2}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\delta_{\|R(\theta_{i}-\theta_{\ell})+\zeta_{i}-\zeta_{\ell}\|_{2}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\delta_{\|\theta_{i}-\theta_{\ell}+\zeta_{i\ell}\|_{2}},

where we let
\xi_{i\ell}=\xi_{i}-\xi_{\ell}
and
\zeta_{i\ell}=R^{\top}(\zeta_{i}-\zeta_{\ell})
. Next, recall that the Wasserstein-1 distance between two sets of points in
\mathbb{R}
with the same cardinality satisfies the following:

W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{i},\hat{\nu}_{j})=\min_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\left|\|\theta_{i}-\theta_{\ell}+\xi_{i\ell}\|_{2}-\|\theta_{j}-\theta_{\pi(\ell)}+\zeta_{j\pi(\ell)}\|_{2}\right|.

Note that

\begin{split}&\left|\left|\|\theta_{i}-\theta_{\ell}+\xi_{i\ell}\|_{2}-\|\theta_{j}-\theta_{\pi(\ell)}+\zeta_{j\pi(\ell)}\|_{2}\right|-\left|\|\theta_{i}-\theta_{\ell}\|_{2}-\|\theta_{j}-\theta_{\pi(\ell)}\|_{2}\right|\right|\\
&\leq\left|\|\theta_{i}-\theta_{\ell}+\xi_{i\ell}\|_{2}-\|\theta_{i}-\theta_{\ell}\|_{2}\right|+\left|\|\theta_{j}-\theta_{\pi(\ell)}\|_{2}-\|\theta_{j}-\theta_{\pi(\ell)}+\zeta_{j\pi(\ell)}\|_{2}\right|\\
&\leq\|\xi_{i\ell}\|_{2}+\|\zeta_{j\pi(\ell)}\|_{2}\\
&\leq(U+V),\end{split}

where we define
U=\max_{i,\ell\in[n]}\|\xi_{i\ell}\|_{2}
and
V=\max_{j,\ell\in[n]}\|\zeta_{j\ell}\|_{2}
. As

W_{1}(\mu_{i},\mu_{j})=\min_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\left|\|\theta_{i}-\theta_{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\theta_{j}-\theta_{\pi(\ell)}\|_{2}^{2}\right|,

we conclude that

|W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{i},\hat{\nu}_{j})-W_{1}(\mu_{i},\mu_{j})|\leq(U+V)\quad\forall i,j\in[n].

Therefore, if we can find
i,j\in[n]
such that
i\neq j
and
W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{i},\hat{\nu}_{i})\geq W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{i},\hat{\nu}_{j})
, we must have

(U+V)\geq W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{i},\hat{\nu}_{i})\geq W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{i},\hat{\nu}_{j})\geq W_{1}(\mu_{i},\mu_{j})-(U+V)\geq\Phi-(U+V).

Accordingly, by (
3.6
), we have

\mathbb{P}(\hat{\pi}\neq\pi^{\ast})\leq\mathbb{P}(2(U+V)\geq\Phi)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(U\geq\frac{\Phi}{4}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(V\geq\frac{\Phi}{4}\right).

Since
\xi_{i}-\xi_{\ell}\sim N(0,(\sigma_{i}^{2}+\sigma_{\ell}^{2})I_{d})
and thus
\|\xi_{i\ell}\|_{2}^{2}\sim(\sigma_{i}^{2}+\sigma_{\ell}^{2})\chi^{2}(d)
for
i\neq\ell
, one can deduce that

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i,\ell\in[n]}\|\xi_{i\ell}\|_{2}\geq\frac{\Phi}{4}\right)\leq\sum_{i\neq\ell}\mathbb{P}\left(\|\xi_{i\ell}\|_{2}\geq\frac{\Phi}{4}\right)\leq n^{2}\mathbb{P}\left(\chi^{2}(d)\geq\frac{\Phi^{2}}{32\sigma^{2}}\right).

By Lemma
4
and (
3.5
), we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i,\ell\in[n]}\|\xi_{i\ell}\|_{2}\geq\frac{\Phi}{4}\right)\leq n^{2}\mathbb{P}\left(\chi^{2}(d)\geq\frac{\Phi^{2}}{32\sigma^{2}}\right)\leq n^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{\Phi^{2}}{512\sigma^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{2}.

Similarly, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{j,\ell\in[n]}\|\zeta_{j\ell}\|_{2}\geq\frac{\Phi}{4}\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{2}.

Therefore, we have
\mathbb{P}(\hat{\pi}\neq\pi^{\ast})\leq\delta
.
∎

For the case where there is no rigid transformation, namely,
T
is the identity map, the noise stability guarantee in the above model is well-studied in the literature, known as the feature matching problem. In this case, the simplest approach is to solve the discrete optimal transport problem
[
30
]
using the squared Euclidean distances, namely,

\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{OT}}=\argmin_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|X_{i}-Y_{\pi(i)}\|_{2}^{2}.

(3.7)

It is well-studied
[
11
,
18
]
that such a procedure is indeed guaranteed to recover
\pi^{\ast}
with high probability if the separation among
\theta_{i}
’s is large enough compared to the noise level
\sigma
. However, in the presence of an unknown rigid transformation
T
, such a procedure is not guaranteed to succeed, while
\hat{\pi}
obtained in Theorem
2
is invariant to rigid transformations as we proved. We demonstrate this point in Section
5
.

4
Connection to the Gromov-Wasserstein Distance and Its Lower Bound

As briefly mentioned in Section
1
, quadratic matching and its generalization, the Gromov-Wasserstein (GW) distance, are widely used for matching tasks in various fields. Their computational difficulty, however, has been major obstacles for practical applications, which has led to the development of various approximation and relaxation schemes. One of such methods, proposed as a tractable lower bound to the GW distance by
[
26
]
, is closely related to the distance profile matching. Despite its favorable analytic properties, its usage has been limited to the approximation of the GW distance
[
26
,
10
]
. In this section, we reveal the connection between the distance profiles and this lower bound, suggesting the potential of this lower bound as a matching method. While the full analysis of matching performance of this lower bound is beyond the scope of this paper, we analyze its statistical complexity that also shows its practical benefit.

4.1
Gromov-Wasserstein Distance, Lower Bound, and Distance Profiles

To establish the connection between the distance profiles and the lower bound, we formally extend the definition of distance profiles to abstract metric measure spaces. We start by recalling the definitions of metric measure spaces, couplings, and the Gromov-Wasserstein distance
[
26
]
.

Definition 1
.

We call
(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}},\mu)
a metric measure space if
\mu
is a Borel probability measure on a complete and separable metric space
(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}})
.

Definition 2
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}},\mu)
and
(\mathcal{Y},d_{\mathcal{Y}},\nu)
be two metric measure spaces. We call a Borel probability measure
\gamma
on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
a coupling if
\gamma(A\times\mathcal{Y})=\mu(A)
and
\gamma(\mathcal{X}\times B)=\nu(B)
for any Borel sets
A\subset\mathcal{X}
and
B\subset\mathcal{Y}
. We denote the set of all couplings by
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
.

Definition 3
.

The Gromov-Wasserstein distance of order
p\geq 1
between two metric measure spaces
(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}},\mu)
and
(\mathcal{Y},d_{\mathcal{Y}},\nu)
is defined as follows:

\mathrm{GW}_{p}(\mu,\nu):=\left(\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{2}}|d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,x^{\prime})-d_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,y^{\prime})|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)\mathrm{d}\gamma(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\right)^{1/p}.

(4.1)

The Gromov-Wasserstein (GW) distance generalizes the quadratic matching based on pairwise distances
\{\|X_{i}-X_{\ell}\|_{2}\}_{i,\ell=1}^{n}
and
\{\|Y_{j}-Y_{\ell}\|_{2}\}_{j,\ell=1}^{m}
to abstract metric measure spaces. Similarly, we can generalize the distance profiles of the point clouds in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to those of metric measure spaces.

Definition 4
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}},\mu)
be a metric measure space. For
x\in\mathcal{X}
, let
\mu_{x}
be the probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
that represents the distribution of
d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,X)
, where
X
is a
\mathcal{X}
-valued random variable whose law is
\mu
. We call
\mu_{x}
the distance profile of
\mu
at point
x
.

Given a complete and separable metric space
(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}})
, suppose we have a point cloud
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
on
\mathcal{X}
. Recall that the empirical measure associated with the points
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
is
\hat{\mu}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
. Then,
(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}},\hat{\mu})
is a metric measure space, and the distance profile of
\hat{\mu}
at
x\in\mathcal{X}
is
\hat{\mu}_{x}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\delta_{d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,X_{\ell})}
. This fully generalizes the distance profiles that we use in Algorithm
1
; the distance profile of
X_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the previous sections is the set of distances
\{\|X_{i}-X_{\ell}\|_{2}\}_{\ell=1}^{n}
, which is equivalent to the probability measure
\hat{\mu}_{X_{i}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\delta_{\|X_{i}-X_{\ell}\|_{2}}
. Analogously, letting
\hat{\nu}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\delta_{Y_{j}}
be the empirical measure associated with the points
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}
, we can see that the distance profile of
Y_{j}
is equivalent to
\hat{\nu}_{Y_{j}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\delta_{\|Y_{j}-Y_{\ell}\|_{2}}
. Then,
W(i,j)
in (
1.1
) is exactly the Wasserstein-1 distance between the distance profiles
\hat{\mu}_{X_{i}}
and
\hat{\nu}_{Y_{j}}
.

[
26
]
considers the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of
\mu_{x}
in Definition
4
and calls the collection of all such CDFs indexed by
x\in\mathcal{X}
the local distribution of distances. Using this,
[
26
]
defines the following.

Definition 5
.

The third lower bound to
\mathrm{GW}_{p}
between metric measure spaces
(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}},\mu)
and
(\mathcal{Y},d_{\mathcal{Y}},\nu)
is defined as follows:

\mathfrak{T}_{p}(\mu,\nu):=\left(\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}W_{p}^{p}(\mu_{x},\nu_{y})\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)\right)^{1/p}.

[
26
]
proves that
\mathfrak{T}_{p}\leq\mathrm{GW}_{p}
and establishes metric properties of
\mathfrak{T}_{p}
. In fact,
[
26
]
also proposes other lower bounds to
\mathrm{GW}_{p}
, called the first and second lower bounds, which turned out to be looser than the third lower bound as shown in
[
27
]
, making the third lower bound the most desirable one to approximate the GW distance. Computational advantage of
\mathfrak{T}_{p}
over
\mathrm{GW}_{p}
is clear, as
\mathfrak{T}_{p}
can be computed by solving a linear program, while
\mathrm{GW}_{p}
requires solving a non-convex quadratic program. To see this, consider the point clouds
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
and
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}
on complete and separable metric spaces
(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}})
and
(\mathcal{Y},d_{\mathcal{Y}})
, with
\hat{\mu}
and
\hat{\nu}
denoting their empirical measures, respectively. In this case,

\mathrm{GW}_{p}^{p}(\hat{\mu},\hat{\nu})=\min_{\gamma\in\Pi_{n,m}}\sum_{i,k=1}^{n}\sum_{j,\ell=1}^{m}|d_{\mathcal{X}}(X_{i},X_{k})-d_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y_{j},Y_{\ell})|^{p}\gamma_{ij}\gamma_{k\ell},

(4.2)

where
\Pi_{n,m}=\{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n\times m}:\sum_{j=1}^{m}\gamma_{ij}=\frac{1}{n}~\forall i\in[n]~\text{and}~\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{ij}=\frac{1}{m}~\forall j\in[m]\}
is the set of all couplings between
\hat{\mu}
and
\hat{\nu}
, given as nonnegative matrices of which the sum of each row (resp. column) is
\frac{1}{n}
(resp.
\frac{1}{m}
). The objective function of (
4.2
) is quadratic in the variable
\gamma
. Meanwhile,

\mathfrak{T}_{p}^{p}(\hat{\mu},\hat{\nu})=\min_{\gamma\in\Pi_{n,m}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}W_{p}^{p}(\hat{\mu}_{X_{i}},\hat{\nu}_{Y_{j}})\gamma_{ij}.

(4.3)

Unlike (
4.2
), the minimization (
4.3
) is a linear program that is solvable by polynomial-time algorithms.

Now, we can see that both
\mathfrak{T}_{p}(\hat{\mu},\hat{\nu})
and Algorithm
1
with
\mathcal{D}=W_{p}^{p}
are matching procedures that find correspondences based on
W_{p}^{p}(\hat{\mu}_{X_{i}},\hat{\nu}_{j})
for all
i\in[n]
and
j\in[m]
. Algorithm
1
allows some points to be matched with multiple points or to be excluded from matching, while
\mathfrak{T}_{p}
requires all the
X_{i}
’s (resp.
Y_{j}
’s) to participate in the matching equally, as enforced by the constraints of
\Pi_{n,m}
in (
4.3
), namely, the sum of each row (resp. column) of any coupling is
\frac{1}{n}
(resp.
\frac{1}{m}
). Also, when
n=m
, one can see that the assignment-based distance profile matching in Section
3
is equivalent to (
4.3
) with
p=1
from the fact that a linear program over
n\Pi_{n,n}
, which is the Birkhoff polytope, is equivalent to the linear assignment problem.

Matching Guarantee of the Third Lower Bound

We conclude this section by discussing the potential of
\mathfrak{T}_{p}
as a matching method in connection with the matching guarantees shown in the previous sections. From the above observation, we can see that the matching guarantee of the assignment-based matching in Section
3
directly translates to the matching guarantee of the procedure based on
\mathfrak{T}_{p}
when
n=m
, indicating the noise stability of the third lower bound. When
n\neq m
, however,
\mathfrak{T}_{p}
finds a coupling that may not explicitly assign to each
X_{i}
its corresponding
Y_{j}
. Algorithm
1
can be thought of as a modification of
\mathfrak{T}_{p}
to allow for establishing explicit correspondences in a flexible yet provably robust way as shown in Section
2
. A natural question is whether a similar guarantee can be directly established for the coupling found by
\mathfrak{T}_{p}
, which would advocate its usage as a matching method. While we do not study this question in this paper, we analyze the statistical complexity in terms of the dimensionality of computing
\mathfrak{T}_{p}
since a low variance is a necessary condition for the matching using
\mathfrak{T}_{p}
to be practically useful.

4.2
Statistical Complexity of the Third Lower Bound

In Section
2
, to model the point clouds approximating underlying objects, we have adopted a viewpoint that the point clouds
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
and
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}
are independent samples from some probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
, respectively. We have seen that Algorithm
1
and
\mathfrak{T}_{1}(\hat{\mu},\hat{\nu})
are procedures that compare the quantities
W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{X_{i}},\hat{\nu}_{j})
’s, which are the sample versions of the Wasserstein-1 distances between the distance profiles at the population level involving
\mu
and
\nu
. Using the properties of distances profiles, we can analyze the statistical complexity of
\mathfrak{T}_{1}(\hat{\mu},\hat{\nu})
in terms of the sample sizes
n
and
m
. Concretely, we analyze the rate of convergence of the average deviation of
\mathfrak{T}_{1}(\hat{\mu},\hat{\nu})
from its population version
\mathfrak{T}_{1}(\mu,\nu)
, which is given by

\mathbb{E}|\mathfrak{T}_{1}(\mu,\nu)-\mathfrak{T}_{1}(\hat{\mu},\hat{\nu})|.

By the metric properties of
\mathfrak{T}_{1}
, we have
\mathbb{E}|\mathfrak{T}_{1}(\mu,\nu)-\mathfrak{T}_{1}(\hat{\mu},\hat{\nu})|\leq\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{T}_{1}(\mu,\hat{\mu})+\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{T}_{1}(\nu,\hat{\nu})
. Hence, it suffices to analyze the rate of convergence of
\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{T}_{1}(\mu,\hat{\mu})
. To this end, first note that

\begin{split}\mathfrak{T}_{1}(\mu,\hat{\mu})&=\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\hat{\mu})}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}W_{1}(\mu_{x},\hat{\mu}_{x^{\prime}})\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,x^{\prime})\\
&\leq\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\hat{\mu})}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}\left(W_{1}(\mu_{x},\mu_{x^{\prime}})+W_{1}(\mu_{x^{\prime}},\hat{\mu}_{x^{\prime}})\right)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,x^{\prime})\\
&\leq\underbrace{\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\hat{\mu})}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}W_{1}(\mu_{x},\mu_{x^{\prime}})\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,x^{\prime})}_{(*)}+\underbrace{\int_{\mathcal{X}}W_{1}(\mu_{x^{\prime}},\hat{\mu}_{x^{\prime}})\,\mathrm{d}\hat{\mu}(x^{\prime})}_{(**)}.\end{split}

(4.4)

It turns out that the expectation of (**) enjoys a fast convergence rate. The idea is that

\mathbb{E}(**)=\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{1}(\mu_{X_{i}},\hat{\mu}_{X_{i}})\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}W_{1}(\mu_{x},\hat{\mu}_{x})\right),

(4.5)

where the last term can be shown to converge at the rate of
O(n^{-1/2})
by Theorem
4
which we present later in this section.

Unfortunately, the rate of convergence of the expectation of (*) can be as slow as that of
\mathbb{E}W_{1}(\mu,\hat{\mu})
, which is known
[
14
,
16
]
to be
O(n^{-1/d})
for
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. To see this, notice that (*)
\leq W_{1}(\mu,\hat{\mu})
as
W_{1}(\mu_{x},\mu_{x^{\prime}})\leq d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,x^{\prime})
by Proposition
2
. That said, when
\mu
has a certain structure, we can derive a faster rate of convergence for (*). For instance, if
\mu
is a mixture of Gaussian distributions as in Section
2
(or, any rotationally invariant distributions), the effective dimension of
\mu
is the number of the components, which can be much smaller than the ambient dimension
d
. The key is that
W_{1}(\mu_{x},\mu_{x^{\prime}})
, the distance between distance profiles, captures this structure and thus is determined by the effective dimension, rather than the ambient dimension.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose
\mu
is a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
given as a mixture of rotationally invariant distributions, namely,
\mu=\sum_{k=1}^{t}p_{k}\mu_{k}
, where
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{t})\in\Delta_{t}
, and
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{t}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
are rotationally invariant with respect to the centers
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, respectively. Then, for any
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

W_{1}(\mu_{x},\mu_{x^{\prime}})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{t}p_{k}\left|\|x-\theta_{k}\|_{2}-\|x^{\prime}-\theta_{k}\|_{2}\right|.

(4.6)

Proof.

By definition, one can check that
\mu_{x}
is also given as the mixture of the distance profiles
(\mu_{k})_{x}
’s, namely,
\mu_{x}=\sum_{k=1}^{t}p_{k}(\mu_{k})_{x}
. Then,

W_{1}(\mu_{x},\mu_{x^{\prime}})=W_{1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{t}p_{k}(\mu_{k})_{x},\sum_{k=1}^{t}p_{k}(\mu_{k})_{x^{\prime}}\right)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{t}p_{k}W_{1}((\mu_{k})_{x},(\mu_{k})_{x^{\prime}}),

(4.7)

where the inequality follows from the fact that if
\gamma_{k}\in\Pi((\mu_{k})_{x},(\mu_{k})_{x^{\prime}})
is a coupling between
(\mu_{k})_{x}
and
(\mu_{k})_{x^{\prime}}
, the convex combination
\sum_{k=1}^{t}p_{k}\gamma_{k}
is a valid coupling between
\mu_{x}
and
\mu_{x^{\prime}}
. As
\mu_{k}
is rotationally invariant with respect to the center
\theta_{k}
, we can deduce that
(\mu_{k})_{x}=(\mu_{k})_{\theta_{k}+\|x-\theta_{k}\|_{2}u}
for any
u
on the unit sphere. Hence,

\begin{split}W_{1}((\mu_{k})_{x},(\mu_{k})_{x^{\prime}})&=W_{1}\left((\mu_{k})_{\theta_{k}+\|x-\theta_{k}\|_{2}u},(\mu_{k})_{\theta_{k}+\|x^{\prime}-\theta_{k}\|_{2}u}\right)\\
&\leq\left\|\theta_{k}+\|x-\theta_{k}\|_{2}u-\theta_{k}-\|x^{\prime}-\theta_{k}\|_{2}u\right\|_{2}\\
&=\left|\|x-\theta_{k}\|_{2}-\|x^{\prime}-\theta_{k}\|_{2}\right|,\end{split}

(4.8)

where the inequality uses Proposition
2
. Combining (
4.7
) and (
4.8
), we obtain (
4.6
).
∎

Theorem 3
.

Suppose
\mu
is a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
given as a mixture of rotationally invariant distributions, namely,
\mu=\sum_{k=1}^{t}p_{k}\mu_{k}
, where
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{t})\in\Delta_{t}
, and
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{t}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
are rotationally invariant with respect to the centers
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, respectively. Let
\hat{\mu}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
be the empirical measure based on
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
that are i.i.d. from
\mu
. Then,

\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{T}_{1}(\mu,\hat{\mu})\leq O(n^{-1/t})+C\sqrt{\frac{d+1}{n}},

where
C
is an absolute constant that is independent of
n
,
d
, and
\mu
.

Proof.

By (
4.4
), (
4.5
), and Theorem
4
, it suffices to show that
\mathbb{E}(*)\leq O(n^{-1/t})
. Define
\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{t}
by letting
\Phi(x)=\left(\sqrt{p_{1}}\|x-\theta_{1}\|_{2},\ldots,\sqrt{p_{t}}\|x-\theta_{t}\|_{2}\right)
. From Proposition
1
, we have

W_{1}(\mu_{x},\mu_{x^{\prime}})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{t}p_{k}\left|\|x-\theta_{k}\|_{2}-\|x^{\prime}-\theta_{k}\|_{2}\right|\leq\|\Phi(x)-\Phi(x^{\prime})\|_{2},

where the last inequality follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. For any coupling
\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\hat{\mu})
, note that
(\Phi\times\Phi)_{\#}\gamma\in\Pi(\Phi_{\#}\mu,\Phi_{\#}\hat{\mu})
, where
\Phi\times\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{t}\times\mathbb{R}^{t}
is a map defined by
(\Phi\times\Phi)(x,x^{\prime})=(\Phi(x),\Phi(x^{\prime}))
. Conversely, for any coupling
\rho\in\Pi(\Phi_{\#}\mu,\Phi_{\#}\hat{\mu})
, we can find a coupling
\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\hat{\mu})
such that
\rho=(\Phi\times\Phi)_{\#}\gamma
by Lemma 3.12 of
[
3
]
. In summary,

\Pi(\Phi_{\#}\mu,\Phi_{\#}\hat{\mu})=\{(\Phi\times\Phi)_{\#}\gamma:\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\hat{\mu})\}.

(4.9)

Therefore,

\begin{split}\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\hat{\mu})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}W_{1}(\mu_{x},\mu_{x^{\prime}})\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,x^{\prime})&\leq\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\hat{\mu})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\Phi(x)-\Phi(x^{\prime})\|_{2}\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,x^{\prime})\\
&=\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\hat{\mu})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{t}\times\mathbb{R}^{t}}\|z-z^{\prime}\|_{2}\,\mathrm{d}(\Phi\times\Phi)_{\#}\gamma(z,z^{\prime})\\
&=\inf_{\rho\in\Pi(\Phi_{\#}\mu,\Phi_{\#}\hat{\mu})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{t}\times\mathbb{R}^{t}}\|z-z^{\prime}\|_{2}\,\mathrm{d}\rho(z,z^{\prime})\\
&=W_{1}(\Phi_{\#}\mu,\Phi_{\#}\hat{\mu}),\end{split}

where the first equality follows from the change of variables, and the second equality is due to (
4.9
). As
\Phi_{\#}\hat{\mu}
is equivalent to the empirical measure of
\Phi_{\#}\mu
which is defined on
\mathbb{R}^{t}
, we conclude from the standard results
[
14
,
16
]
that
\mathbb{E}(*)\leq W_{1}(\Phi_{\#}\mu,\Phi_{\#}\hat{\mu})\leq O(n^{-1/t})
.
∎

We have used (
4.5
) to bound (**) defined in (
4.4
) by
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}W_{1}(\mu_{x},\hat{\mu}_{x})
. For each fixed
x\in\mathcal{X}
, as
\mu_{x}
is a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
, one can deduce from the standard results
[
14
,
16
]
that
W_{1}(\mu_{x},\hat{\mu}_{x})
enjoys a fast rate of convergence in terms of the sample size
n
, which is
O(n^{-1/2})
. Theorem
4
shows that the rate of convergence of
\mathbb{E}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}W_{1}(\mu_{x},\hat{\mu}_{x})
is as fast as that of
\mathbb{E}W_{1}(\mu_{x},\hat{\mu}_{x})
for fixed
x\in\mathcal{X}
, that is,
O(n^{-1/2})
. Theorem
4
considers the Euclidean case, which can be extended to general metric measure spaces; we provide this extension in Appendix
A.3
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}},\mu)
be a metric measure space and
\hat{\mu}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
be the empirical measure based on
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
that are i.i.d. from
\mu
. Suppose
\mathcal{X}
is a closed subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
d_{\mathcal{X}}
is the standard Euclidean distance. Then,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}W_{1}(\mu_{x},\hat{\mu}_{x})\right]\leq C\sqrt{\frac{d+1}{n}},

(4.10)

where
C
is an absolute constant that is independent of
n
,
d
, and
\mu
.

Proof.

For
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
r\in\mathbb{R}
, let
\phi_{x,r}(\cdot)=1\{d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,\cdot)\leq r\}
, which is a function from
\mathcal{X}
to
\{0,1\}
. Let
F_{x}
be the cumulative distribution function of
\mu_{x}
. Then,
F_{x}(r)=\mu\phi_{x,r}
by definition. Similarly, letting
\hat{F}_{x}
be the cumulative distribution function of
\hat{\mu}_{x}
, we have
\hat{F}_{x}(r)=\hat{\mu}\phi_{x,r}
. Recalling that
W_{1}
between two probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
is the
L_{1}
distance between their cumulative distribution functions, we have

W_{1}(\mu_{x},\hat{\mu}_{x})=\int_{0}^{\infty}|F_{x}(r)-\hat{F}_{x}(r)|\,\mathrm{d}r=\int_{0}^{\infty}|\mu\phi_{x,r}-\hat{\mu}\phi_{x,r}|\,\mathrm{d}r\leq\sup_{\phi\in\mathcal{F}_{x}}|\mu\phi-\hat{\mu}\phi|,

where
\mathcal{F}_{x}:=\{\phi_{x,r}:r\geq 0\}
for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Therefore,

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}W_{1}(\mu_{x},\hat{\mu}_{x})\leq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sup_{\phi\in\mathcal{F}_{x}}|\mu\phi-\hat{\mu}\phi|=\sup_{\phi\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}}|\mu\phi-\hat{\mu}\phi|,

where
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}:=\bigcup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{F}_{x}=\{\phi_{x,r}:x\in\mathcal{X},r\geq 0\}
. By Theorem 8.23 of
[
38
]
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}W_{1}(\mu_{x},\hat{\mu}_{x})\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\phi\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}}|\mu\phi-\hat{\mu}\phi|\right]\leq C\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{vc}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}})}{n}}.

(4.11)

where
C
is an absolute constant that is independent of
x
,
n
,
\mathcal{X}
, and
\mu
. Here,
\mathrm{vc}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}})
is the Vapnik-Chervonenkis (VC) dimension of the function class
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}
. As
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, this is upper bounded by the VC dimension of the set of all closed Euclidean balls in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which is
d+1
; see
[
15
]
. Hence, we obtain (
4.10
).
∎

Lastly, we provide the analytic property of the distance between distance profiles used throughout this section.

Proposition 2
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}},\mu)
be a metric measure space. Then,
W_{1}(\mu_{x},\mu_{x^{\prime}})\leq d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,x^{\prime})
for
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
.

Proof.

Define a map
S\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
by letting
S(\cdot)=(d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,\cdot),d_{\mathcal{X}}(x^{\prime},\cdot))
. Then, one can verify that
S_{\#}\mu
is a coupling of
\mu_{x}
and
\mu_{x^{\prime}}
. Therefore,

\begin{split}W_{1}(\mu_{x},\mu_{x^{\prime}})&=\inf_{\rho\in\Pi(\mu_{x},\mu_{x^{\prime}})}\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}|z_{1}-z_{2}|\,\mathrm{d}\rho(z_{1},z_{2})\\
&\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}|z_{1}-z_{2}|\,\mathrm{d}S_{\#}\mu(z_{1},z_{2})\\
&=\int_{\mathcal{X}}|d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,\bar{x})-d_{\mathcal{X}}(x^{\prime},\bar{x})|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\bar{x})\\
&\leq d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,x^{\prime}),\end{split}

where the last inequality follows from the triangle inequality of
d_{\mathcal{X}}
.
∎

6
Conclusion

We have proposed a matching procedure based on distance profiles, which is easily implementable without having to solve nontrivial optimization problems over permutations. Most importantly, we have shown that the proposed method is provably robust to outliers and noise under some model assumptions that we believe capture essential aspects of empirical applications. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work that provides theoretical guarantees for the robustness of a matching procedure based on distance profiles in the context of object matching. Also, we have extended the concept of distance profiles to the abstract setting and connected the proposed matching procedure to the Gromov-Wasserstein distance and its lower bound, with a new sample complexity result derived based on the properties of distance profiles. We have also demonstrated the empirical performance of the proposed method through real data and simulations.

We suggest several directions for future research. On the theoretical side, it would be interesting to consider other types of matching guarantees in Theorem
1
. As mentioned in Section
5
, the matching accuracy (
5.1
) may be practically more useful. Similarly, for Theorem
2
, it would be interesting to consider the probability that
\hat{\pi}
is close to
\pi^{\ast}
under a certain discrepancy, such as the Hamming distance, instead of the probability of
\hat{\pi}=\pi^{\ast}
. On the practical side, it would be interesting to delve deeper into the procedure of finding inliers, that is, the points from the common part of two objects. In Remark
2
, though we have noted that the threshold
\rho
can be chosen to identify the inliers at least in theory, developing a practical method applicable to real data is very challenging in general. Lastly, building on the sample complexity result in Section
4
, we may analyze the outlier robustness of the third lower bound in general cases. We leave these directions for future research.

A.3
Extension of Theorem
4
to General Metric Measure Spaces

From (
4.11
) in the proof of Theorem
4
, one can deduce that Theorem
4
can be extended to any metric measure space
(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}},\mu)
by replacing
d+1
in (
4.10
) with the VC dimension of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}
defined in the proof, which is the VC dimension of the set of all balls in
\mathcal{X}
. Unfortunately, controlling the VC dimension of the set of all balls in a general metric measure space is highly nontrivial. A more straightforward quantity is the
\varepsilon
-covering number
\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon,\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}})
, namely, the smallest possible number of
\varepsilon
-balls to cover
\mathcal{X}
. The following theorem derives an upper bound using the covering number for a general bounded metric measure space
(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}},\mu)
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}},\mu)
be a metric measure space and
\hat{\mu}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
be the empirical measure based on
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
that are i.i.d. from
\mu
. Suppose
\Delta=\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,x^{\prime})<\infty
and the support of
\mu
is exactly
\mathcal{X}
. Let
\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon,\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}})
be the
\varepsilon
-covering number of
\mathcal{X}
under
d_{\mathcal{X}}
. Then,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}W_{1}(\mu_{x},\hat{\mu}_{x})\right]\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{0}^{\Delta/4}\sqrt{\frac{\Delta}{\varepsilon}+\log\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon,\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}})}\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon,

(A.1)

where
C
is an absolute constant that is independent of
n
,
\mathcal{X}
, and
\mu
.

Proof.

By the Kantorovich-Rubinstein theorem (Theorem 1.14 of
[
39
]
), we have

W_{1}(\mu_{x},\hat{\mu}_{x})=\sup_{f\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}}\left(\mu_{x}f-\hat{\mu}_{x}f\right),

where
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}=\left\{f\colon[0,\Delta]\to\mathbb{R}\,|\,f(0)=0~\text{and}~f~\text{is}~1\text{-Lipschitz}\right\}
. Here, we have used the fact that the supports of
\mu_{x}
and
\hat{\mu}_{x}
are contained in the interval
[0,\Delta]
for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Hence,

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}W_{1}(\mu_{x},\hat{\mu}_{x})=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sup_{f\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}}\left(\mu_{x}f-\hat{\mu}_{x}f\right)=\sup_{\phi\in\mathcal{F}}\left(\mu\phi-\hat{\mu}\phi\right),

where
\mathcal{F}=\{f\circ d_{\mathcal{X},x}:f\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1},x\in\mathcal{X}\}
which is a function class from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathbb{R}
. Here,
d_{\mathcal{X},x}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by
d_{\mathcal{X},x}(\cdot)=d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,\cdot)
. Using symmetrization and Dudley’s chaining argument (see Chapter 8 of
[
38
]
), one can derive the following:

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}W_{1}(\mu_{x},\hat{\mu}_{x})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\phi\in\mathcal{F}}\left(\mu\phi-\hat{\mu}\phi\right)\right]\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{0}^{\Delta/2}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon,\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}})}\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon,

(A.2)

where
\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon,\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}})
denotes the
\varepsilon
-covering number of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}}
, namely, the sup norm defined by
\|\phi\|_{\mathcal{X}}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|\phi(x)|
for any
\phi\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. Here, we are using the fact that
\sup_{\phi\in\mathcal{F}}\|\phi\|_{\mathcal{X}}\leq\Delta
since
\sup_{z\in[0,\Delta]}|f(z)|\leq\Delta
for any
f\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}
by Lipschitzness. Now, noticing that
\mathcal{F}
is a function class consisting of composite functions, we upper bound
\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon,\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}})
by the product of the
\varepsilon
-covering number of
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}
with respect to the sup norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
on
[0,\Delta]
, that is,
\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup_{z\in[0,\Delta]}|f(z)|
, and the
\varepsilon
-covering number of
\mathcal{X}
with respect to
d_{\mathcal{X}}
. Concretely, we claim

\mathcal{N}(2\varepsilon,\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}})\leq\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon,\mathrm{Lip}_{1},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})\times\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon,\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}}).

(A.3)

To see this, let
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{\varepsilon}\subset\mathrm{Lip}_{1}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\varepsilon}\subset\mathcal{X}
be
\varepsilon
-coverings of
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}
and
\mathcal{X}
, respectively. For
f\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we can find
f^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{\varepsilon}
and
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}^{\varepsilon}
such that
\|f-f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon
and
d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,x^{\prime})\leq\varepsilon
. Then,

\begin{split}\|f\circ d_{\mathcal{X},x}-f^{\prime}\circ d_{\mathcal{X},x^{\prime}}\|_{\mathcal{X}}&\leq\|f\circ d_{\mathcal{X},x}-f\circ d_{\mathcal{X},x^{\prime}}\|_{\mathcal{X}}+\|f\circ d_{\mathcal{X},x^{\prime}}-f^{\prime}\circ d_{\mathcal{X},x^{\prime}}\|_{\mathcal{X}}\\
&\leq\|d_{\mathcal{X},x}-d_{\mathcal{X},x^{\prime}}\|_{\mathcal{X}}+\|f-f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\\
&\leq d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,x^{\prime})+\varepsilon\\
&\leq 2\varepsilon.\end{split}

Hence, we obtain (
A.3
). The standard upper bound on the covering number of
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}
(see Exercise 8.2.7 of
[
38
]

\log\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon,\mathrm{Lip}_{1},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})\leq\frac{c\Delta}{\varepsilon},

(A.4)

where

is some absolute constant. Combining (
