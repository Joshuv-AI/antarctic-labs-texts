Axially Symmetric Willmore Minimizers with Prescribed Isoperimetric Ratio
Jan-Henrik Metsch

Abstract
We establish the existence and smoothness of minimizers of the Willmore energy among axially symmetric surfaces of spherical type with prescribed isoperimetric ratio. Afterwards, we study the behavior of these minimizers as the isoperimetric ratio tends to zero.

Introduction

The isoperimetric ratio
\mathcal{I}(f)
of a smooth embedding
f:\mathbb{S}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
is defined as

\mathcal{I}[f]:=6\sqrt{\pi}\frac{V[f]}{A[f]^{\frac{3}{2}}},

where
A[f]
and
V[f]
denote the surface area of
f
and the volume enclosed by
f
, respectively. By the classical isoperimetric inequality

V[f]\leq\frac{1}{6\sqrt{\pi}}A[f]^{\frac{3}{2}}

we have
\mathcal{I}(f)\in(0,1]
with
\mathcal{I}(f)=1
occurring only for round spheres. For
\sigma\in(0,1]
, Schygulla
[
22
]
introduces the problem of minimizing the Willmore energy

\mathcal{W}[f]:=\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}H[f]^{2}d\mu_{f}

under all embeddings
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{3})
with
\mathcal{I}[f]=\sigma
. This problem is motivated by the models for the shape of blood cells by Canham
[
2
]
and Helfrich
[
8
]
, who independently proposed to model the bending energy of a blood cell by a quadratic expression in its curvature. Helfrich’s article
[
8
]
also motivates the isoperimetric constraint: He prescribes the area and the volume. However, as the Willmore energy is invariant under scaling, these two constraints reduce to prescribing the isoperimetric ratio. By Hopf’s theorem, the constraint of prescribed isoperimetric ratio
\mathcal{I}[f]=\sigma
is nondegenerate for
\sigma<1
so that by Lagrange’s theorem, the minimizers satisfy the Euler-Lagrange equation

\Delta_{g}H+\frac{1}{2}H(H^{2}-4K)=\Lambda\sigma\left(\frac{1}{V[f]}-\frac{3H}{2A[f]}\right),

(1.1)

where
H
and
K
denote the mean and the Gauß curvature of
f
respectively. In this article, we investigate the axially symmetric case. For
\sigma\in(0,1]
, we introduce

\mathcal{S}_{\sigma}:=\set{f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{3})\textrm{ embedding }}{\ \mathcal{I}[f]=\sigma\textrm{ and }f\circ T=f\textrm{ for all }T\in\operatorname{SO}(2)}.

Additionally, we let
\beta(\sigma):=\inf_{f\in\mathcal{S}_{\sigma}}\mathcal{W}[f]
. A convenient characterization of the surfaces in
\mathcal{S}_{\sigma}
is achieved using their
profile curves
. Indeed, after translation, every
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\sigma}
is of the form

f_{\gamma}:[0,1]\times\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3},\ f_{\gamma}(s,\omega):=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{1}(s)\omega\\
\gamma_{2}(s)\end{bmatrix}

(1.2)

where
\gamma\in\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}:=\set{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}\ }{\ \mathcal{I}[f_{\gamma}]=\sigma}
and
\mathcal{P}
denotes the set of admissible profile curves

\mathcal{P}:=\left\{\gamma\in C^{\infty}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{2})\ \big|\ \begin{array}[]{l}\gamma_{1}(0)=\gamma_{1}(1)=0\textrm{ and }\gamma_{1}(t)>0\textrm{ for all }t\in(0,1)\end{array}\right\}.

Conversely, using Equation (
1.2
), any
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}
can be assigned a surface of spherical type in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
so that we can identify
f_{\gamma}
and
\gamma
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For all
\sigma\in(0,1]
, there exists
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\sigma}
such that
\mathcal{W}[f]=\beta(\sigma)
. Moreover,
\beta(\sigma)<8\pi
for all
\sigma\in(0,1]
and
\beta(\sigma)\rightarrow 8\pi
as
\sigma\rightarrow 0^{+}
.

The proof of
1.1
is split into three steps. First, the existence of a weak minimizing profile
\gamma\in W^{1,2}((0,1))\cap W^{2,2}_{\operatorname{loc}}((0,1))
is established using the direct method of the calculus of variations. The arguments used here follow those executed by Choski and Venernoni in
[
4
]
. Next, the smoothness of
\gamma
on
(0,1)
– away from the axis of rotation – is established using elliptic regularity arguments.
To study regularity at the axis of rotation, a careful analysis of the local graph representation

\set{(r\omega,u(r))\ }{\ r\in[0,r_{0}),\ \omega\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}

of
f_{\gamma}
near the axis of rotation is required. For the unconstrained Willmore equation, such analysis has been carried out in
[
3
]
by Chen and Li. We adapt their analysis to prove

u(r)=\lambda r^{2}\ln(r)+C^{2}([0,r_{0})).

(1.3)

The parameter
\lambda
is a residue, as has been studied by Kuwert and Schätzle in
[
14
]
and cannot be eliminated by ODE arguments alone, as the Euler-Lagrange equation (
1.1
) allows for singular solutions of the form in Equation (
1.3
).

After proving the existence of smooth minimizers, we investigate their behavior in the limit
\sigma\rightarrow 0^{+}
. This has already been done by Schygulla
[
22
]
. He proved that as
\sigma\rightarrow 0^{+}
, his minimizers converge to a double sphere in the sense of measures. In
[
12
]
, this result was later refined by Kuwert and Li, who prove that asymptotically, Schygulla’s minimizers look like two spheres joined by a catenoidal neck. We prove that our axially symmetric minimizers display the same behavior. Restricting to the class

\mathcal{F}^{0+}_{\sigma}:=\left\{\gamma\in\mathcal{F}_{\sigma},\ \bigg|\ \gamma(0)=(0,0),\ A[f_{\gamma}]=1\textrm{ and }\int_{0}^{1}\gamma_{2}(t)dt\geq 0\right\},

we eliminate the invariance of the problem under vertical translation, scaling and reflection – thereby out ruling the trivial obstructions to convergence. Characterizing the solutions to the Euler-Lagrange equation in the limit
\sigma\rightarrow 0^{+}
we then establish:

Theorem 1.2

(Convergence to a Double Sphere)
.

Let
(\sigma_{k})\subset(0,1]
be a sequence of isoperimetric ratios
\sigma_{k}\rightarrow 0^{+}
and
(\gamma^{(k)})_{k}
be a sequence of profile curves
\gamma^{(k)}\in\mathcal{F}^{0+}_{\sigma_{k}}
satisfying
\beta(\sigma_{k})=\mathcal{W}[f_{\gamma^{(k)}}]
. Then as
k\rightarrow\infty
,

\gamma^{(k)}(t)\rightarrow\kappa(t):=(0,R)+R(|\sin(2\pi t)|,-\cos(2\pi t))\hskip 14.22636pt\textrm{with }R:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{8\pi}}

in
C^{\infty}([0,1]\backslash\{\frac{1}{2}\})
and in
W^{1,2}((0,1))
.

Since
\kappa
is not smooth at
t=\frac{1}{2}
, it is clear that the curves
\gamma^{(k)}
have to develop some form of singularity near
t=\frac{1}{2}
. We prove that near
t=\frac{1}{2}
, the profile curves solve approximately the equation
H=0
. Using a suitable blow-up argument, we then establish the following theorem:

Theorem 1.3

(Formation of a Catenoidal Neck)
.

Let
\sigma_{k}\rightarrow 0^{+}
and
\gamma^{(k)}\in\mathcal{F}^{0+}_{\sigma_{k}}
satisfy
\mathcal{W}[f_{\gamma^{(k)}}]=\beta(\sigma_{k})
. There exist
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and
\rho>0
such that the following statements hold:

(1)

For
k\geq k_{0}
there exists a unique
\tau_{k}\in[\frac{1}{8},\frac{7}{8}]
such that
\gamma_{1}^{(k)}(\tau_{k})=\epsilon_{k}:=\inf_{[\frac{1}{8},\frac{7}{8}]}\gamma_{1}^{(k)}
.

(2)

\dot{\gamma}_{1}^{(k)}<0
on
[\tau_{k}-\rho,\tau_{k})
and
\dot{\gamma}_{1}^{(k)}>0
on
(\tau_{k},\tau_{k}+\rho]
.

(3)

The parameters from
(1)
satisfy
\epsilon_{k}\rightarrow 0^{+}
and
\tau_{k}\rightarrow\frac{1}{2}
.

(4)

After potentially putting

\hat{\gamma}^{(k)}(t):=\left(\gamma_{1}^{(k)}(1-t),\ \gamma_{2}^{(k)}(1-t)-\gamma_{2}^{(k)}(1)\right)
,
it can be assumed that
\dot{\gamma}_{2}^{(k)}(\tau_{k})\leq 0
for all
k\geq k_{0}
and the following convergence is in
C^{\infty}_{\operatorname{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
:

\frac{1}{\epsilon_{k}}\left(\gamma^{(k)}(\tau_{k}+\epsilon_{k}t)-(0,\gamma_{2}^{(k)}(\tau_{k}))\right)\rightarrow\left(\sqrt{1+L_{*}^{2}t^{2}},-\operatorname{asinh}(L_{*}t)\right)\hskip 14.22636pt\textrm{with }L_{*}:=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}

General Notation

For

If

Immersions

Let
f:\mathbb{S}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a sufficiently regular immersion and
n
denote a regular unit normal vector field along
f
. The first and second fundamental forms are respectively denoted by

g_{ij}:=\langle\partial_{i}f,\partial_{j}f\rangle\hskip 14.22636pt\textrm{and}\hskip 14.22636pth_{ij}:=\langle\partial_{ij}f,n\rangle.

The surfaces measure on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
induced by
f
is denoted by
\mu_{f}
. In local coordinates
d\mu_{f}=\sqrt{\det g}dx
. Let
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
denote the principal curvatures of
f
– that is, the eigenvalues of the Weingarten operator
g^{-1}h
. The mean curvature
H
and the Gauß curvature
K
are given by

H=k_{1}+k_{2}\hskip 14.22636pt\textrm{and}\hskip 14.22636ptK=k_{1}k_{2}.

For example, considering the inclusion
\imath:\mathbb{S}^{2}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
with the interior normal field
n=-\imath
, we get
H[f]=2
and
K[f]=1
.

Functionals

Let
f:\mathbb{S}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a sufficiently regular immersion. The Willmore energy of
f
is defined as

\mathcal{W}[f]:=\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}H[f]^{2}d\mu_{f}.

(2.1)

For any of the two choices of continuous normal vector field
n
along
f
, the surface area, the volume and the isoperimetric ratio of
f
are respectively defined as

A[f]:=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}d\mu_{f},\hskip 14.22636ptV[f]:=\left|\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}f_{2}\langle n,e_{2}\rangle d\mu_{f}\right|\hskip 14.22636pt\textrm{and}\hskip 14.22636pt\mathcal{I}[f]:=6\sqrt{\pi}\frac{V[f]}{A[f]^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

(2.2)

To justify the definition of volume, we have the following lemma:

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{3})
be an embedding. Then there exists a bounded
C^{1}
-domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
\partial\Omega=f(\mathbb{S}^{2})
and which satisfies
|\Omega|=V[f]
.

Proof.

The existence of
\Omega
is due to the Jordan-Brouwer separation theorem (see e.g.
[
16
]
, Corollary 5.24). Let
n_{\operatorname{int}}
denote the interior unit normal along
\partial\Omega
. Since
\Omega\in C^{1}
, we can use Gauß’s theorem to compute

|\Omega|=-\int_{\partial\Omega}x_{2}\langle n_{\operatorname{int}},e_{2}\rangle dS=-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}f_{2}\langle n_{\operatorname{int}},e_{2}\rangle d\mu_{f}.

This establishes Equation (
2.2
) when
n
in the interior normal and thereby also for the exterior normal
n_{\operatorname{ext}}=-n_{\operatorname{int}}
.
∎

\Phi:(-\epsilon_{0},\epsilon_{0})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}

\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}\mathcal{W}[\Phi(\epsilon)]=\int_{\Sigma}W[f]\langle\Phi^{\prime}(0),n\rangle d\mu_{f}+\int_{\partial\Sigma}\omega(\eta)dS_{f}.

(2.3)

In this formula,
\eta

\displaystyle W[f]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta_{g}H+\frac{1}{2}H(H^{2}-4K)\right),

(2.4)

\displaystyle\omega(\eta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\varphi\frac{\partial H}{\partial\eta}-H\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial\eta}-\frac{1}{2}H^{2}\langle Df\eta,DfX\rangle\right).

Direct Method of the Calculus of Variations

In this section, we employ the direct method of the calculus of variations to obtain an axially symmetric minimizer of the Willmore energy with prescribed isoperimetric ratio
\sigma
. The analysis follows with only minor modifications, the arguments in
[
4
]
.

3.1
Functional Space

We first define a suitable space of profile curves.

Definition 3.1

(Admissible Profile Curves)
.

A curve
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}):[0,1]\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{2}
is called an
admissible profile curve
if:

(1)

\gamma\in C^{1}([0,1])\cap W^{2,2}_{\operatorname{loc}}((0,1))
.

(2)

\gamma_{1}(0)=\gamma_{1}(1)=0
and
\gamma_{1}(t)>0
for all
t\in(0,1)
.

(3)

\gamma
is parameterized proportional to arc length.

(4)

\Sigma_{\gamma}
has curvature bounded in
L^{2}(\mu_{\Sigma_{\gamma}})
. That is

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}k_{1}^{2}+k_{2}^{2}d\mu_{\Sigma_{\gamma}}=\int_{0}^{1}(k_{1}^{2}+k_{2}^{2})2\pi|\dot{\gamma}|\gamma_{1}dt<\infty.

We denote the set of all admissible profile curves by
\mathcal{P}
.

We wish to deduce the existence of a curve
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}
that minimizes the Willmore energy under all curves in
\mathcal{P}
with prescribed isoperimetric ratio. Given
\sigma\in(0,1)
we put

\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}:=\left\{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}\ \bigg|\ A[\gamma]=1\hskip 5.69046pt\textrm{and}\hskip 5.69046ptV[\gamma]=\frac{\sigma}{6\sqrt{\pi}}\right\}\hskip 14.22636pt\textrm{and}\hskip 14.22636pt\beta(\sigma):=\inf\set{\mathcal{W}[\gamma]\ }{\ \gamma\in\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}}.

Note that in the definition of
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
, we prescribe the area and the volume of
\gamma
. However, as both the Willmore energy
\mathcal{W}
and the isoperimetric ratio
\mathcal{I}
are invariant under scaling, we have

\beta(\sigma)=\inf\set{\mathcal{W}[\gamma]\ }{\ \gamma\in\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}}=\inf\set{\mathcal{W}[\gamma]\ }{\ \gamma\in\mathcal{P}\textrm{ with }\mathcal{I}[\gamma]=\sigma}.

(3.1)

Unfortunately, the space
\mathcal{P}
does not have sufficient compactness properties and we require a weakened notation of admissible profile curves.

Definition 3.2

(Weak Profile Curves)
.

We say that
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{2}
is a
weak profile curve
if:

(1)

\gamma\in W^{1,2}((0,1))
(so in particular

Theorem 4.1
.

For

Our main tool for improving the regularity of

Definition 4.2

(Generalized Profile Curves)

We say that

\Sigma_{a}
and construct a surface
\Sigma_{a}^{\epsilon}
by replacing the graphs of
u^{\pm}
with the graphs of
u_{\pm}+\epsilon\varphi_{\pm}
with small
\epsilon>0
. Consequently
\mathcal{W}[\Sigma_{a}^{\epsilon}]<8\pi-\epsilon c_{0}
for small
\epsilon>0
. Additionally,
\Sigma_{a}^{\epsilon}
can be parameterized over
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and is axially symmetric. Finally, we note
\mathcal{I}[\Sigma_{a}]\rightarrow 0
, as
a\rightarrow 0^{+}
. Given
\sigma\in(0,1)
we can therefore choose
a
and
\epsilon
so small such that simultaneously

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}[\Sigma_{a}^{\epsilon}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{W}[\Sigma_{a}]-\epsilon c_{0}=8\pi-\epsilon c_{0},

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}[\Sigma_{a}^{\epsilon}]

\displaystyle<\sigma.

Approximating
\Sigma_{a}^{\epsilon}
with smooth surfaces, we deduce that there exists a smooth, axially symmetric embedding
F_{0}:\mathbb{S}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
\mathcal{W}[F_{0}]<8\pi
and
\mathcal{I}[F_{0}]<\sigma
. Theorem 5.2 in
[
14
]
allows us to deduce that the Willmore flow
F(t)
with initial datum
F(0)=F_{0}
exists for all times and converges to a sphere. As
F_{0}
is axially symmetric and the solution to the Willmore flow is unique (Proposition 1.1 in
[
13
]
), it follows that
F(t)
is axially symmetric for all
t\geq 0
. Since
\mathcal{I}[F(0)]<\sigma
and
\mathcal{I}[F(t)]\rightarrow 1
as
t\rightarrow\infty
, there exists a time
t_{0}>0
such that
\mathcal{I}[F(t_{0})]=\sigma
. Finally, by definitions of
\beta(\sigma)
and the dissipation of Willmore energy along the Willmore flow, we get

\beta(\sigma)\leq\mathcal{W}[F(t_{0})]\leq\mathcal{W}[F(0)]<8\pi.

∎

A.5
The Monotonicity Formula

Let
\Sigma
be a smooth
2
-dimensional manifold without boundary,
f\in C^{1}(\Sigma,\mathbb{R}^{3})
be an immersion and
\mu_{\Sigma}
denote the surface measure on
\Sigma
that is induced by
f
. For
p\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the
muliplicity
of
p
is defined as

\theta^{2}_{f}(p):=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\mu_{\Sigma}(B_{r}(p))}{\pi r^{2}}.

(A.10)

If
\Sigma
is compact, this expression is well-defined and

\theta^{2}_{f}(p)=|\set{x\in\Sigma\ }{\ f(x)=p}|.

(A.11)

The following result is due Simon
[
24
]
. Its applicability in the present setting has essentially been established by Kuwert and Schätzle
[
14
]
, Appendix A.

Lemma A.2

(Monotonicity Formula)
.

Let
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}\cap C^{\infty}((0,1),\mathcal{H}^{2})
. The surface
f_{\gamma}:[0,1]\times[0,2\pi)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
as defined in Equation (
2.11
) is
C^{1}
-immersed. Putting
\theta^{2}(p):=\theta^{2}_{f_{\gamma}}(p)
, we have

\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\left|\frac{1}{4}\vec{H}+\frac{(f_{\gamma}(x)-p)^{\perp}}{|f_{\gamma}(x)-p|^{2}}\right|d\mu_{\Sigma_{\gamma}}(x)\leq\frac{1}{4\pi}\mathcal{W}[f_{\gamma}]-\theta^{2}(p).

Here
\vec{H}
is the almost everywhere defined mean curvature vector of
f_{\gamma}
and
(f_{\gamma}(x)-p)^{\perp}
is the component of
f_{\gamma}(x)-p
that is orthogonal to
Df_{\gamma}(x)(T_{x}\mathbb{S}^{2})
.

Proof.

We put
f:=f_{\gamma}
and
\mu:=\mu_{\Sigma_{\gamma}}
. We use the monotonicity formula proved in Appendix A of
[
14
]
. We may apply their results as the mean curvature
H
is in
L^{2}(\mu)
. Indeed, by the definition of the class
\mathcal{P}
(see Definition
3.1
)

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}H^{2}d\mu\leq 2\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}k_{1}^{2}+k_{2}^{2}d\mu<\infty.

Using Equation (A3) from
[
14
]
, we get

\displaystyle\sigma^{-2}\mu(B_{\sigma}(x))+\int_{f^{-1}(B_{\rho}(p)\backslash B_{\sigma}(p))}\left|\frac{1}{4}\vec{H}+\frac{(f(x)-p)^{\perp}}{|f(x)-p|^{2}}\right|^{2}d\mu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho^{-2}\mu(B_{\rho}(x))+\frac{1}{16}\int_{f^{-1}(B_{\rho}(p)\backslash B_{\sigma}(p))}\vec{H}^{2}d\mu+R_{p,\rho}-R_{p,\sigma}

(A.12)

where for
\alpha=\sigma,\rho

R_{p,\alpha}:=\frac{1}{2\alpha^{2}}\int_{f^{-1}(B_{\alpha}(p))}\langle(x-p),\vec{H}\rangle d\mu.

The Equation after (A6) in
[
14
]
implies
R_{p,\sigma}\rightarrow 0
as
\sigma\rightarrow 0^{+}
. We note that
\mu(B_{\rho}(p))\leq A[f]<\infty
by definition of the class
\mathcal{P}
. Hence
\rho^{-2}\mu_{\Sigma}(B_{\rho}(p))\rightarrow 0
for
\rho\rightarrow\infty
. So Equation (A13) in
[
14
]
implies
\lim_{\rho\rightarrow\infty}R_{p,\rho}=0
.
Letting
\rho\rightarrow\infty
and
\sigma\rightarrow 0^{+}
in Equation (
A.12
) then gives

\liminf_{\sigma\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left[\sigma^{-2}\mu_{\Sigma}(B_{\sigma}(x))+\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}\backslash f^{-1}(B_{\sigma}(p))}\left|\frac{1}{4}\vec{H}+\frac{(f(x)-p)^{\perp}}{|f(x)-p|^{2}}\right|^{2}d\mu\right]\leq\frac{1}{4}\mathcal{W}[f].

The lemma follows by using Fatou’s lemma and Equation (
A.10
).
∎

Lemma A.3
.

Let
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}\cap C^{\infty}((0,1))
and denote by
\vec{H}
the almost everywhere defined mean curvature vector of
f_{\gamma}
. Suppose that there exists
p\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that

\frac{1}{4}\vec{H}(s)=-\frac{(f_{\gamma}(s,\theta)-p)^{\perp}}{|f_{\gamma}(s,\theta)-p|^{2}}.

(A.13)

Then
\Sigma_{\gamma}
is either a sphere or the inversion of a scaled and vertically translated catenoid. In particular, only the second case is possible if
\mathcal{W}[\Sigma_{\gamma}]=8\pi
.

Proof.

By vertical translation of
\gamma
and rotational invariance, we can assume
p=Re_{1}
for some
R\geq 0
. Let
L:=L[\gamma]
. Using Equation (
2.13
), we compute

\displaystyle(f_{\gamma}(s,\theta)-Re_{1})\cdot n(s,\theta)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{1}\cos\theta-R\\
\gamma_{1}\sin\theta\\
\gamma_{2}\end{bmatrix}\cdot\frac{1}{L}\begin{bmatrix}-\dot{\gamma}_{2}\cos\theta\\
-\dot{\gamma}_{2}\sin\theta\\
\dot{\gamma}_{1}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{-\gamma_{1}\dot{\gamma}_{2}+\dot{\gamma}_{1}\gamma_{2}+R\dot{\gamma}_{2}\cos\theta}{L}.

A quick computation shows
|f_{\gamma}(s,\theta)-Re_{1}|^{2}=\gamma_{1}^{2}+\gamma_{2}^{2}+R^{2}-2R\gamma_{1}\cos\theta
. Inserting into Equation (
A.13
), we get

\frac{1}{4}H(s)\left(\gamma_{1}^{2}+\gamma_{2}^{2}+R^{2}-2R\gamma_{1}\cos\theta\right)=-\frac{-\gamma_{1}\dot{\gamma}_{2}+\dot{\gamma}_{1}\gamma_{2}+R\dot{\gamma}_{2}\cos\theta}{L}.

(A.14)

The case
R>0

Comparing the coefficients of
\cos\theta
in Equation (
A.14
)and using
R\neq 0
we derive

\frac{\dot{\gamma}_{2}}{L}=\frac{1}{2}H(s)\gamma_{1}(s)
. By using Equation (
2.20
), we get

\frac{2\dot{\gamma}_{2}^{2}}{L}=H\gamma_{1}\dot{\gamma}_{2}=-\frac{\gamma_{1}\ddot{\gamma}_{1}}{L}+\frac{\dot{\gamma}_{2}^{2}}{L}.

We use
\dot{\gamma}_{2}^{2}=L^{2}-\dot{\gamma}_{1}^{2}
and get
-\ddot{\gamma}_{1}\gamma_{1}=\dot{\gamma}_{2}^{2}=L^{2}-\dot{\gamma}_{1}^{2}
. Next, we put
x(t):=\gamma_{1}(\frac{t}{L})
.
x
satisfies the following problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} \ddot{x}x&=\dot{x}^{2}-1,\\
x(0)&=x(L)=0,\\
\dot{x}(0)&=1,\\
x(t)&>0\hskip 14.22636pt\textrm{for }t\in(0,L).\end{aligned}\right.

(A.15)

Since
|\dot{\gamma}(t)|\leq L
we have
|\dot{x}|\leq 1
. Suppose that there exists
t_{0}\in(0,L)
such that
\dot{x}(t_{0})^{2}=1
. Then by the Picard-Lindelöff theorem
x(t)=x(t_{0})\pm(t-t_{0})
. This, however, contradicts
x(0)=x(L)=0
. So
x^{2}(t)<1
for all
t\in(0,L)
and hence the following computation is admissible for
t\in(0,L)
:

-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\ln(1-\dot{x}^{2})=\frac{\dot{x}\ddot{x}}{1-\dot{x}^{2}}=-\frac{\dot{x}}{x}=-\frac{d}{dt}\ln(x)

Integrating and subsequent exponentiation proves that there exists a positive constant
K
such that

1-\dot{x}^{2}=Kx^{2}
. Differentiating this identity gives
\dot{x}(\ddot{x}+Kx)=0
Since
\dot{x}(0)=1
we have
\dot{x}\neq 0
for small times
t\in[0,t_{0}]
and get

x(t)=a\sin(\sqrt{K}t)+b\cos(\sqrt{K}t)\hskip 14.22636pt\textrm{for }t\in[0,t_{0}].

As
x(t)>0
, Equation (
A.15
) and the Picard-Lindelöff theorem imply that this formula is valid not only on
[0,t_{0}]
but for all
t\in[0,L]
. Since
x(0)=x(L)=0
we get
b=0
and
\sqrt{K}=L^{-1}n\pi
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, as
x>0
on
(0,L)
and
\dot{x}(0)=1
, we get

x(t)=\frac{L}{\pi}\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{L}t\right)\hskip 14.22636pt\textrm{for all $t\in[0,L]$}.

Let
y(t):=\gamma_{2}(\frac{t}{L})
. As
\dot{y}^{2}=1-\dot{x}^{2}=\sin^{2}(\frac{\pi}{L}t)
, we have
\dot{y}(t)=\pm\sin(\frac{\pi}{L}t)
and hence

y(t)=y(0)\mp\frac{L}{\pi}\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{L}t\right).

The formulas for
x(t)
and
y(t)
imply that
\gamma
parameterises a circle of radius
\frac{L}{\pi}
with center at
(0,y(0))
. In particular
\mathcal{W}[\gamma]=4\pi
.

The Case
R=0

Let
I:\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\set{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\set{0}
,
I(x):=\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}
. Then
I^{*}\delta=\frac{1}{|x|^{4}}\delta
and

H[I\circ f_{\gamma}]=H[f_{\gamma},I^{*}\delta]=|f_{\gamma}|^{2}H[f_{\gamma}]+4\langle f_{\gamma},n\rangle=0.

This shows that the surface defined by the profile curve
\Gamma(s):=\frac{\gamma(s)}{|\gamma(s)|^{2}}
is a minimal surface. Let
\alpha
denote the maximal solution to the problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{\alpha}(t)&=\frac{|\gamma(\alpha(t))|^{2}}{L},\\
\alpha(0)&=\frac{1}{2}.\end{aligned}\right.

We define
\Gamma^{*}(t):=\Gamma(\alpha(t))
and compute

|\dot{\Gamma}^{*}(s)|=\left|\left(\frac{\dot{\gamma}}{|\gamma|^{2}}-2\frac{\langle\gamma,\dot{\gamma}\rangle}{|\gamma|^{4}}\gamma\right)\dot{\alpha}\right|=1.

Consequently,
\Gamma^{*}
is parameterized by arc length and satisfies
H[\Gamma^{*}]=0
. Following the arguments after Equation (
6.6
) we get
\Gamma_{1}^{*}(t)=\sqrt{a+bt+t^{2}}
and
\dot{\Gamma}_{2}^{*}(t)\Gamma_{1}^{*}(t)=c
for constants
a>0
and
b,c\in\mathbb{R}
. By potentially redefining
\Gamma^{*}(t)\rightarrow\Gamma^{*}(-t)
we may further assume
b\geq 0
. Since
|\dot{\Gamma}^{*}|=1
we have

1=(\dot{\Gamma}_{1}^{*})^{2}+(\dot{\Gamma}_{2}^{*})^{2}=\frac{c^{2}+(t+\frac{b}{2})^{2}}{(\Gamma_{1}^{*})^{2}}\hskip 14.22636pt\textrm{and hence}\hskip 14.22636ptt^{2}+bt+a=(t+\frac{b}{2})^{2}+c^{2}.

If
c=0
we deduce
\Gamma^{*}(t)=(t+\frac{b}{2},\Gamma_{2}^{*}(0))
for
t\geq-\frac{b}{2}
. Hence
\Sigma_{\Gamma^{*}}
is part of a a plane
P
orthogonal to the
x_{3}
-axis and consequently
\Sigma_{\gamma}
is part of a sphere or, if
0\in P
, part of
P
. The second case is impossible. Indeed, if
\gamma(s)=(\gamma_{1}(s),c_{0})
for some
c_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, we get
L=|\dot{\gamma}|=|\dot{\gamma}_{1}|
. Since
\gamma_{1}(0)=0
and
\gamma_{1}(t)\geq 0
for all
t\in[0,1]
we get
\gamma_{1}(t)=Lt
, which contradicts
\gamma_{1}(1)=0
. So, if
c=0
,
\Sigma_{\gamma}
is part of a sphere and by the definition of the class
\mathcal{P}
, it then follows that
\Sigma_{\gamma}
is a sphere and hence
\mathcal{W}[\Sigma_{\gamma}]=4\pi
. If
c\neq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}^{*}(t)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left(t+\frac{b}{2}\right)^{2}+c^{2}},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}^{*}(t)

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{2}^{*}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{cdt}{\sqrt{(t+\frac{b}{2})^{2}+c^{2}}}=\Gamma_{2}^{*}(0)-|c|\operatorname{asinh}(\frac{b}{2c})+|c|\operatorname{asinh}(\frac{t}{c}+\frac{b}{2c}).

This is the profile curve of a scaled and vertically translated catenoid. Hence,
\Sigma_{\gamma}
is the inversion of a scaled and vertically translated catenoid and
\mathcal{W}[\Sigma_{\gamma}]=8\pi
.
∎

B.1
Lower Semi Continuity

The proof of Equations (

) and (

) is the consequence of two lemmas. First, we establish the following:

Lemma B.1
.

Let
\rho_{n},\rho\in C^{0}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0})
and
f_{n},f:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be measurable such that
\rho_{n}\rightarrow\rho
in
C^{0}([0,1])
and
f_{n}\rho_{n}\rightarrow f\rho
weakly in
L^{1}((0,1))
. Further assume
f^{2}\rho\in L^{1}((0,1))
and
\|f_{n}^{2}\rho_{n}\|_{L^{1}((0,1))}\leq C<\infty
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

\int_{0}^{1}f^{2}(t)\rho(t)dt\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{1}f_{n}^{2}(t)\rho_{n}(t)dt.

All integrals are well-defined since
f^{2}\rho\geq 0
and
f_{n}^{2}\rho_{n}\geq 0
.

Proof.

For
R>0
we put
\phi_{R}:=f1_{|f|\leq R}\in L^{\infty}((0,1))
. Using
f_{n}\rho_{n}\rightarrow f\rho
weakly in
L^{1}((0,1))
and
\rho_{n}\rightarrow\rho
in
C^{0}([0,1])
, we estimate

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\phi_{R}(t)\rho(t)dt=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{1}f_{n}(t)\phi_{R}(t)\rho_{n}(t)dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{1}f_{n}(t)^{2}\rho_{n}(t)dt\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{R}(t)^{2}\rho_{n}(t)dt\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{1}f_{n}(t)^{2}\rho_{n}(t)dt\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{R}(t)^{2}\rho(t)dt\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Note that
\phi_{R}\rightarrow f
pointwise and
|\phi_{R}|\leq|f|
. As
|f|^{2}\rho\in L^{1}((0,1))
we can let

and get the claimed estimate.
∎

.

Let

f:[\alpha,\beta]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
we put

V_{\alpha}^{\beta}(f):=\sup\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}|f(t_{i+1})-f(t_{i})|\ \bigg|\ N\in\mathbb{N}\textrm{ and }\alpha=t_{0}<t_{1}<...<t_{N-1}<t_{N}=\beta\right\}.

f
is said to have
bounded variation
if
V_{\alpha}^{\beta}(f)<\infty
. If additionally
f\in L^{1}((\alpha,\beta))
, we define

\|f\|_{\operatorname{BV}((\alpha,\beta))}:=\|f\|_{L^{1}((\alpha,\beta))}+V_{\alpha}^{\beta}(f)
.
The space of all
f
with
\|f\|_{\operatorname{BV}((\alpha,\beta))}<\infty
is denoted by
\operatorname{BV}((\alpha,\beta))
.

We stress that to define
V_{\alpha}^{\beta}(f)
, we consider a function
f:[\alpha,\beta]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and not the equivalence class of all functions
\tilde{f}
such that
\tilde{f}=f
almost everywhere. We require the following theorem. For a proof, we refer to Theorem 2.35 and Proposition 2.38 in
[
15
]
.

Theorem B.5

(Helly’s Selection Theorem)
.

Let
(f_{n})
be a sequence of measurable functions
f_{n}:[\alpha,\beta]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
c\in[\alpha,\beta]
such that
|f_{n}(c)|+V_{\alpha}^{\beta}(f_{n})\leq C<\infty
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, there exists a subsequence
f_{n_{a}}
and a measurable function
f:[\alpha,\beta]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
f_{n_{a}}\rightarrow f
pointwise and

V_{\alpha}^{\beta}(f)\leq\liminf_{a\rightarrow\infty}V_{\alpha}^{\beta}(f_{n_{a}}).

We establish the following corollary:

Corollary B.6
.

Let
(f_{n})\subset\operatorname{BV}((\alpha,\beta))
be a sequence such that
\|f_{n}\|_{\operatorname{BV}((\alpha,\beta))}\leq C<\infty
. Then, there exist
f\in\operatorname{BV}((\alpha,\beta))
and a subsequence
(f_{n_{a}})
such that
f_{n_{a}}\rightarrow f
pointwise and in
L^{1}((\alpha,\beta))
.

Proof.

We first claim that
f_{n}(\alpha)
is bounded. Once this is shown, we can use Helly’s selection theorem to get a subsequence
f_{n_{a}}
such that
f_{n_{a}}\rightarrow f
pointwise. For
x\in(\alpha,\beta)
we estimate

\displaystyle|f_{n_{a}}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq|f_{n_{a}}(\alpha)|+|f_{n_{a}}(\alpha)-f_{n_{a}}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq|f_{n_{a}}(\alpha)|+|f_{n_{a}}(\alpha)-f_{n_{a}}(x)|+|f_{n_{a}}(x)-f_{n_{a}}(\beta)|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{a\geq 0}\left(|f_{n_{a}}(\alpha)|+V_{\alpha}^{\beta}(f_{n_{a}})\right).

Therefore
f_{n_{a}}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}((\alpha,\beta))
and hence
f\in L^{\infty}((\alpha,\beta))
. Using Lebesgue’s theorem, we obtain

0=\lim_{a\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}|f(t)-f_{n_{a}}(t)|dt.

To prove that
f_{n}(\alpha)
is bounded, let
x_{n}\in[\alpha,\beta]
such that
|f_{n}(x_{n})|<\inf_{[\alpha,\beta]}|f_{n}|+1
. We have the estimate

C\geq\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}|f_{n}(x)|dx\geq\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}|f_{n}(x_{n})|-1dx=|f_{n}(x_{n})|-(\beta-\alpha).

So,
f_{n}(x_{n})
is bounded and we can estimate

|f_{n}(\alpha)|\leq|f_{n}(x_{n})|+|f_{n}(x_{n})-f_{n}(\alpha)|+|f_{n}(\beta)-f_{n}(x_{n})|\leq|f_{n}(x_{n})|+V_{\alpha}^{\beta}(f_{n})\leq C.

∎

Let

be open. Then there exist at most countably many and uniquely determined disjoint intervals

Definition B.7

By a usual diagonal argument, we get the following corollary from Corollary

Appendix C
General Regularity Theory

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}
be an open interval and
\gamma\in W^{1,\infty}(U,\mathcal{H}^{2})\cap W^{2,2}(U,\mathcal{H}^{2})
. For
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
we define the functional

I(\phi):=\alpha\int_{0}^{1}\gamma_{1}^{2}(t)\dot{\phi}_{2}(t)+2\gamma_{1}(t)\dot{\gamma}_{2}(t)\phi_{1}(t)dt+\beta\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma}(t)|\phi_{1}(t)+\frac{\gamma_{1}(t)\langle\dot{\phi}(t),\dot{\gamma}(t)\rangle}{|\dot{\gamma}(t)|}dt.

(C.1)

We assume that
\gamma
satisfies the equation

\delta\mathcal{W}[\gamma]\phi=I(\phi)\hskip 14.22636pt\textrm{for all }\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(U).

(C.2)

The goal of this section is to prove the following regularity theorem:

Theorem C.1

(Regularity Theorem)
.

Let
\gamma\in W^{1,\infty}(U,\mathcal{H}^{2})\cap W^{2,2}(U,\mathcal{H}^{2})
satisfy Equation (
C.2
). Further, we assume that
|\dot{\gamma}(t)|=L>0
and

\displaystyle|\alpha|+|\beta|+L+L^{-1}+\|\gamma\|_{C^{0}(U)}+\|\ddot{\gamma}\|_{L^{2}(U)}+\mathcal{W}[\gamma]\leq M,

(C.3)

\displaystyle\kappa(\gamma;U):=\inf_{t\in U}\gamma_{1}(t)>0.

(C.4)

Then
\gamma\in C^{\infty}(U)
and
\|\gamma\|_{C^{m}(U)}\leq C(M,\kappa(\gamma;U),U,m)
. The constant is increasing in the first and decreasing in the second slot.

The proof is split into several lemmas. We remark that Eichmann and Grunau have carried out similar arguments in
[
7
]
for the Willmore equation without the isoperimetric Lagrange multiplier.

Lemma C.2

Under the assumptions of Theorem

\displaystyle C\|\phi\|_{C^{1}}.

(C.7)

We recall that
H=k_{1}+k_{2}
. Using the formulas for
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
from Equation (

) it is easy to derive the following equations:

\displaystyle\delta k_{1}\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{\ddot{\phi}_{2}\dot{\gamma}_{1}+\ddot{\gamma}_{2}\dot{\phi}_{1}-\ddot{\phi}_{1}\dot{\gamma}_{2}-\ddot{\gamma}_{1}\dot{\phi}_{2}}{L^{3}}-3\frac{\ddot{\gamma}_{2}\dot{\gamma}_{1}-\ddot{\gamma}_{1}\dot{\gamma}_{2}}{L^{4}}\frac{\langle\dot{\gamma},\dot{\phi}\rangle}{L}

(C.8)

\displaystyle\delta k_{2}\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{\dot{\phi}_{2}}{\gamma_{1}L}-\frac{\dot{\gamma}_{2}}{\gamma_{1}^{2}L}\phi_{1}-\frac{\dot{\gamma}_{2}}{\gamma_{1}L^{2}}\frac{\langle\dot{\gamma},\dot{\phi}\rangle}{L}

(C.9)

Next, we estimate the terms in
\delta H\phi
that do not contain
\ddot{\phi}
. Using
|\dot{\gamma}|=L
and Estimate (
C.3
) we get

|\delta k_{2}\phi|\leq C\|\phi\|_{C^{1}}\hskip 14.22636pt\textrm{and}\hskip 14.22636pt\left|\frac{\ddot{\gamma}_{2}\dot{\phi}_{1}-\ddot{\gamma}_{1}\dot{\phi}_{2}}{L^{3}}-3\frac{\ddot{\gamma}_{2}\dot{\gamma}_{1}-\ddot{\gamma}_{1}\dot{\gamma}_{2}}{L^{4}}\frac{\langle\dot{\gamma},\dot{\phi}\rangle}{L}\right|\leq C\|\phi\|_{C^{1}}|\ddot{\gamma}|.

(C.10)

Using Equation (
C.6
) and inserting Estimates (
C.7
) and (
C.10
) yields

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\tau_{1}}^{\tau_{2}}H(\ddot{\phi}_{2}\dot{\gamma}_{1}-\ddot{\phi}_{1}\dot{\gamma}_{2})\gamma_{1}|\dot{\gamma}|dt\right|\leq

\displaystyle C\|\phi\|_{C^{1}}\left[1+\int_{\tau_{1}}^{\tau_{2}}|H|\gamma_{1}|\dot{\gamma}_{1}|dt+\int_{\tau_{1}}^{\tau_{2}}|H|\ |\ddot{\gamma}|\gamma_{1}|\dot{\gamma}_{1}|dt\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\|\phi\|_{C^{1}}(1+\|\ddot{\gamma}\|_{L^{2}{((\tau_{1},\tau_{2}))}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\|\phi\|_{C^{1}}.

(C.11)

Here we have first used the Hölder inequality and the bound on the Willmore energy from Estimate (
C.3
).

Estimate Auxiliary Terms

For the moment, we abbreviate
\xi:=\chi(a+bt)
. We use Estimate (
C.3
) to estimate

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\tau_{1}}^{\tau_{2}}H(\ddot{\xi}_{2}\dot{\gamma}_{1}-\ddot{\xi}_{1}\dot{\gamma}_{2})\gamma_{1}|\dot{\gamma}|dt\right|\leq 2\|\ddot{\xi}\|_{C^{0}}\int_{\tau_{1}}^{\tau_{2}}|H|\ |\dot{\gamma}|^{2}\gamma_{1}dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\|\ddot{\xi}\|_{C^{0}}\sqrt{\mathcal{W}[\gamma]}\leq C\|\xi\|_{C^{2}}.

(C.12)

We wish to use this estimate to replace
\phi
in Estimate (
C.11
) with
\zeta
. To do so, we recall
\phi=\zeta-\chi(a+bt)
. Using
\ddot{\zeta}=\varphi
, Estimate (
C.12

we get

\left|\int_{\tau_{1}}^{\tau_{2}}H(\varphi_{2}\dot{\gamma}_{1}-\varphi_{1}\dot{\gamma}_{2})\gamma_{1}|\dot{\gamma}|dt\right|\leq C\|\varphi\|_{L^{1}((0,1))}.

(C.13)

In particular, choosing
\varphi=(\varphi_{1},0)
we find that for all
\varphi_{1}\in C^{\infty}_{0}((\tau_{1},\tau_{2}))
we have

\left|\int_{\tau_{1}}^{\tau_{2}}H\varphi_{1}\dot{\gamma}_{2}\gamma_{1}|\dot{\gamma}|dt\right|\leq C\|\varphi_{1}\|_{L^{1}((\tau_{1},\tau_{2}))}.

(C.14)

Regularity Improvement

Estimate (
C.14
) allows us to extend the integral expression
\int_{\tau_{1}}^{\tau_{2}}H\dot{\gamma}_{2}\gamma_{1}|\dot{\gamma}|\varphi_{1}dt
to a linear and continuous functional
\Lambda:L^{1}((\tau_{1},\tau_{2}))\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. As
(L^{1}((\tau_{1},\tau_{2})))^{\prime}=L^{\infty}((\tau_{1},\tau_{2}))
we may deduce

H\dot{\gamma}_{2}\gamma_{1}|\dot{\gamma}|\in L^{\infty}((\tau_{1},\tau_{2}))

\|H\dot{\gamma}_{2}\gamma_{1}|\dot{\gamma}|\ \|_{L^{\infty}((\tau_{1},\tau_{2}))}\leq C.

As
\gamma_{1}\geq\kappa(\gamma;U)
on

and

we obtain
\|H\dot{\gamma}_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}((\tau_{1},\tau_{2}))}\leq C
. Using
\varphi=(0,\varphi_{2})
one can derive a similar estimate for

. Combining both estimates, we get

\|H\|_{L^{\infty}((\tau_{1},\tau_{2}))}=\frac{\|H\dot{\gamma}\|_{L^{\infty}((\tau_{1},\tau_{2}))}}{L}\leq\frac{1}{L}(\|H\dot{\gamma}_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}((\tau_{1},\tau_{2}))}+\|H\dot{\gamma}_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}((\tau_{1},\tau_{2}))})\leq C.

Using the identities from Equation (
2.20
), we deduce
\gamma\in W^{2,\infty}((\tau_{1},\tau_{2}))
and the estimate
\|\ddot{\gamma}\|_{L^{\infty}((\tau_{1},\tau_{2}))}\leq C
.
∎

Lemma C.3
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
C.1

\gamma\in W^{3,\infty}(U)
,
H\in W^{1,\infty}(U)
and

\|\gamma\|_{W^{3,\infty}(U)}+\|H\|_{W^{1,\infty}(U)}\leq C(M,\kappa(\gamma;U),U).

Proof..

Let again
U=(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
. As in the proof of Lemma
C.2

C
always refers to a constant depending on
M
,
\kappa(\gamma;U)
and
U
. Also, the rest of the proof is very similar to the proof of Lemma
C.2
and we only sketch the parts that need to be altered. First, the test function has to get modified. Given
\varphi=(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})\in C_{0}^{\infty}((\tau_{1},\tau_{2}),\mathbb{R}^{2})
we put

\zeta(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\varphi(r)dr.

Let
[r_{1},r_{2}]=\operatorname{supp}\varphi
. Then
\zeta(t)=0
for
t\in[\tau_{1},r_{1}]
and
\zeta(t)=a
when
t\in[r_{2},\tau_{2}]
where

a=\int_{\tau_{1}}^{\tau_{2}}\varphi(r)dr.

We choose the same smooth function
\chi
as in the proof of Lemma
C.2
and put
\phi(t):=\zeta(t)-a\chi(t)
. It is easy to see that
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(U)
and that
\|\phi\|_{C^{0}}\leq C\|\varphi\|_{L^{1}}
and
\|\dot{\phi}\|_{L^{1}}\leq C\|\varphi\|_{L^{1}}
.
Testing the weak Euler-Lagrange equation with
\phi
as we did in Equation (
C.6
) and using the estimates derived in Lemma
C.2
we get

\left|\int_{\tau_{1}}^{\tau_{2}}\hskip-8.5359ptH\left(\delta H\phi\right)\gamma_{1}|\dot{\gamma}|dt\right|\leq C\left[\int_{\tau_{1}}^{\tau_{2}}\hskip-8.5359ptH^{2}\left(|\dot{\gamma}|\ |\phi_{1}|+\gamma_{1}|\dot{\phi}|\right)dt+|I(\phi)|\right]\leq C\|\phi\|_{W^{1,1}(U)}.

(C.15)

Next, we take care of the terms in
\delta H
that do not contain
\ddot{\phi}
. Recalling Equations (
C.8
) and (
C.9
) and using the result of Lemma
C.2
, we estimate

\displaystyle\left|\delta H\phi-\frac{\ddot{\phi}_{2}\dot{\gamma}_{1}-\ddot{\phi}_{1}\dot{\gamma}_{2}}{L^{3}}\right|

\displaystyle=|\delta k_{2}\phi|+\left|\frac{\ddot{\gamma}_{2}\dot{\phi}_{1}-\ddot{\gamma}_{1}\dot{\phi}_{2}}{L^{3}}-3\frac{\ddot{\gamma}_{2}\dot{\gamma}_{1}-\ddot{\gamma}_{1}\dot{\gamma}_{2}}{L^{4}}\frac{\langle\dot{\gamma},\dot{\phi}\rangle}{L}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left(|\phi|+|\dot{\phi}|\right).

(C.16)

Inserting into Estimate (
C.15
) and using the estimate for
\|H\|_{L^{\infty}((\tau_{1},\tau_{2}))}
from Lemma
C.2
, we get

\left|\int_{\tau_{1}}^{\tau_{2}}H(\ddot{\phi}_{2}\dot{\gamma}_{1}-\ddot{\phi}_{1}\dot{\gamma}_{2})\gamma_{1}|\dot{\gamma}|dt\right|\leq C\|\phi\|_{W^{1,1}(U)}.

As in the proof of Lemma
C.2
, we now insert the formula for
\phi
and estimate the auxiliary terms to get

\left|\int_{\tau_{1}}^{\tau_{2}}H(\dot{\varphi}_{2}\dot{\gamma}_{1}-\dot{\varphi}_{1}\dot{\gamma}_{2})\gamma_{1}|\dot{\gamma}|dt\right|\leq C\|\phi\|_{W^{1,1,}}\leq C\|\varphi\|_{L^{1}}.

(C.17)

Now we argue similarly to as we did in the final step of the proof of Lemma
C.2
. First we take
\varphi=(\varphi_{1},0)
and consider the densely defined linear operator

\Lambda:L^{1}((\tau_{1},\tau_{2}))\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\hskip 14.22636pt\Lambda(\varphi_{1}):=\int_{\tau_{1}}^{\tau_{2}}H\dot{\varphi}_{1}\dot{\gamma}_{2}\gamma_{1}|\dot{\gamma}|dt.

Estimate (
C.17

. Now, by the Riesz representation theorem (see e.g.
[
9
]
, Theorem 19.2), there exists a function

\Lambda(\varphi)=\int_{\tau_{1}}^{\tau_{2}}h\varphi dt
. Going back to

\int_{\tau_{1}}^{\tau_{2}}h\varphi_{1}dt=\int_{\tau_{1}}^{\tau_{2}}H\gamma_{1}\dot{\gamma}_{2}|\dot{\gamma}|\dot{\varphi}_{1}dt\hskip 14.22636pt\textrm{for all }\varphi_{1}\in C^{\infty}_{0}((\tau_{1},\tau_{2})).

Thus

is weakly differentiable and Estimate (
C.17
) implies

\|(H\gamma_{1}\dot{\gamma}_{2}|\dot{\gamma}|)^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}((\tau_{1},\tau_{2}))}\leq C.

Using the results from Lemma
C.2
as well as

we obtain

instead, we can derive a similar estimate for

. The lemma follows as in the proof of Lemma
C.2
by using the identities in Equation (
2.20

∎

Lemma C.4
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
C.1

,
H\in W^{k,\infty(U)}

,
\kappa(\gamma;U)
and

and this time also

C.3
. Therefore it suffices to prove the following implication for

\left\{\begin{aligned} &H\in W^{k,\infty}(U)\\

Testing the weak version of the Euler-Lagrange equation with

Appendix D
Calculus Lemmas

Lemma D.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
u\in C^{1}((0,1))\cap C^{0}([0,1))
,
h\in C^{n}([0,1)
and

\frac{(xu(x))^{\prime}}{x}=h(x).

Then
u\in C^{n+1}([0,1))
.

Proof..

First, we consider the case
n=0
. We have

xu(x)=0\cdot u(0)+\int_{0}^{x}th(t)dt.

(D.1)

So, for
x\in(0,1)
we get

u^{\prime}(x)=\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{x}th(t)dt\right]=h(x)-\frac{1}{x^{2}}\int_{0}^{x}th(t)dt.

We claim that
u^{\prime}(x)\rightarrow\frac{1}{2}h(0)
as
x\rightarrow 0
. Indeed

\frac{1}{x^{2}}\int_{0}^{x}h(t)tdt=\int_{0}^{1}sh(sx)ds\rightarrow h(0)\int_{0}^{1}sds=\frac{1}{2}h(0).

Now we consider the case
n>0
. Rewriting Equation (
D.1
) we get

u(x)=x\int_{0}^{1}h(tx)tdt

and deduce
u\in C^{n}([0,1))
. Next, compute

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(x)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}th(tx)dt+x\int_{0}^{1}t^{2}h^{\prime}(tx)dt

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}th(tx)dt+x\left[\frac{t^{2}h(tx)}{x}\bigg|_{t=0}^{t=1}-\int_{0}^{1}2t\frac{h(tx)}{x}dt\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h(x)-\int_{0}^{1}th(tx)dt.

Clearly, this last formula is
C^{n}([0,1])
, which proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma D.2
.

Let
r_{0}>0
,
f\in C^{0}([0,r_{0}])\cap C^{1}((0,r_{0}])
and
w\in C^{2}([0,r_{0}])
such that

w^{\prime\prime}(r)+\left(\frac{w}{r}\right)^{\prime}=f(r).

Then
|w^{\prime\prime}(r)|\leq\frac{4}{3}\sup_{0\leq\rho\leq r}|f(\rho)|
and consequently
|w^{\prime}(r)|\leq|w^{\prime}(r_{0})|+\frac{4}{3}r_{0}\sup_{0\leq\rho\leq r_{0}}|f(\rho)|
.

Proof..

We compute

\left(\frac{(wr)^{\prime}}{r}\right)^{\prime}=\left(w^{\prime}+\frac{w}{r}\right)^{\prime}=f.

Integrating this equation from
r>0
to
r_{0}
shows

-\frac{(w(r)r)^{\prime}}{r}+w^{\prime}(r_{0})+\frac{w(r_{0})}{r_{0}}=\int_{r}^{r_{0}}f(s)ds=\int_{0}^{r_{0}}f(s)ds-\int_{0}^{r}f(s)ds.

We put
c_{0}:=w^{\prime}(r_{0})+\frac{w(r_{0})}{r_{0}}-\int_{0}^{r_{0}}f(s)ds
. Then

\frac{(rw(r))^{\prime}}{r}=c_{0}+\int_{0}^{r}f(s)ds.

We multiply by
r
. Another integration from
r
to
r_{0}
shows

\displaystyle r_{0}w(r_{0})-rw(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}c_{0}(r_{0}^{2}-r^{2})+\int_{r}^{r_{0}}x\int_{0}^{x}f(s)dsdx

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}c_{0}r^{2}+\frac{1}{2}c_{0}r_{0}^{2}+\int_{0}^{r_{0}}x\int_{0}^{x}f(s)dsdx-\int_{0}^{r}x\int_{0}^{x}f(s)dsdx.

This shows that there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

rw(r)=c_{1}r^{2}+c_{2}+\int_{0}^{r}\int_{0}^{x}xf(s)dsdx.

Taking
r=0
shows
c_{2}=0
. Next, we substitute
x=ry
and obtain

w(r)=c_{1}r+r\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{ry}yf(s)dsdy.

We compute two
r
-derivatives.

\displaystyle w^{\prime\prime}(r)=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\int_{0}^{yr}yf(s)dsdy+r\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}\int_{0}^{ry}yf(s)dsdy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{1}y^{2}f(yr)dy+r\int_{0}^{1}y^{3}f^{\prime}(yr)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{1}y^{2}f(yr)dy+\int_{0}^{1}y^{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}f(yr)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{1}y^{2}f(yr)dy+y^{3}f(yr)\bigg|_{y=0}^{y=1}-\int_{0}^{1}3y^{2}f(yr)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{1}y^{2}f(yr)dy+f(r)

Consequently

|w^{\prime\prime}(r)|\leq\left(1+\int_{0}^{1}y^{2}dy\right)\sup_{0\leq\rho\leq r}|f(\rho)|=\frac{4}{3}\sup_{0\leq\rho\leq r}|f(\rho)|.

∎

Lemma D.3
.

Let
f\in C^{1}((0,\rho))
such that
f^{\prime}\in L^{1}((0,\rho))
. Then
f\in C^{0}([0,\rho])
.

Proof..

We prove continuity at
x=0
. The case
x=\rho
works similarly. Let
x<y\in(0,\rho]
. Then

|f(x)-f(y)|\leq\int_{x}^{y}|f^{\prime}(t)|dt\leq\int_{0}^{y}|f^{\prime}(t)|dt.

This implies that
f(\epsilon_{n})
is Cauchy for any
\epsilon_{n}\rightarrow 0^{+}
and hence
f(0):=\lim_{x\rightarrow 0^{+}}f(x)
is well-defined. To see that
f
is continuous at
x=0
, we use
f^{\prime}\in L^{1}((0,1))
to estimate

|f(0)-f(y)|\leq\int_{0}^{y}|f^{\prime}(t)|dt\rightarrow 0,\hskip 14.22636pt\textrm{as $y\rightarrow 0^{+}$.}

∎
